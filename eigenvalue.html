Eigenvalue (λ)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Operator theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 51
A scalar λ is an eigenvalue of a linear operator A:V→V when
Av=λv
(54)
for some nonzero vector v, called an eigenvector. The scalar must belong to the field over which V is a vector space. Geometrically, a real eigenvalue tells us what A does along a special line: every vector on that line is multiplied by the same number. The eigenvector gives the line and the eigenvalue gives its scaling.
For a real eigenvalue, λ>0 preserves the orientation along the line, λ<0 reverses it, and λ=0 collapses the line to zero. The length is multiplied by the modulus ∣λ∣, so ∣λ∣>1 stretches and 0<∣λ∣<1 contracts. For example, the diagonal matrix diag(2,−1/2,0) doubles one coordinate direction, reverses and halves another, and annihilates the third. Its eigenvalues are 2,−1/2,0; the last direction lies in its kernel.
For a finite square matrix, the eigenvalues are the roots of a polynomial pA​, its characteristic polynomial:
pA​(λ)=det(λI−A)=0.
(55)
Here I is the identity matrix. The reason is that Av=λv means (A−λI)v=0 with v=0: the shifted matrix must have a nontrivial kernel, equivalently a zero determinant. The worked symmetric matrix example in eigenvector shows both stages: find the eigenvalues, then find their eigenspaces.
Eigenvalues are especially useful for repeated application of a linear map. If Av=λv, then
Anv=λnv(n≥0).
(56)
One multiplication proves the next step, so a matrix power becomes an ordinary power on each eigenvector. This explains growth, decay, and alternating signs along these directions. Gilbert Strang develops this connection, and its extension to the matrix exponential, in his lecture "Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors" (MIT 18.065, 2018).[ref]
A documented historical connection is C. G. J. Jacobi's "Über ein leichtes Verfahren die in der Theorie der Säcularstörungen vorkommenden Gleichungen numerisch aufzulösen" (1846, Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik 30, pp. 51–94). Its opening discusses symmetric matrices arising from small oscillations, with a common unknown subtracted from every diagonal entry. In modern notation this is (A−λI)v=0; Jacobi seeks the parameter and the ratios of the components of v. This is evidence of an early physical and computational setting for the eigenvalue problem, without assigning its discovery to a single person.[ref][ref]
