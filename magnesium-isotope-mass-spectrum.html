Magnesium isotope mass spectrum

 Home Chemistry Mass spectrometry
OurBigBook.com Words: 419
For a modern chosen example, the National Institute of Standards and Technology lists neutral magnesium atomic masses 23.985041697u, 24.985836976u, and 25.982592968u, with reference isotopic abundances 0.7899, 0.1000, and 0.1101. They describe the isotopes with mass numbers 24, 25, and 26, respectively.[ref] These modern inputs are not a reconstruction of historical observations.
Use the dimensionless mass-to-charge ratio coordinate x=(m/u)/z. For the illustration only, approximate ion masses by the neutral atomic masses. The singly charged positions are then near 23.98504, 24.98584, and 25.98259; the doubly charged companions are near 11.99252, 12.49292, and 12.99130. The neutral-to-ion correction already given above is essential at higher accuracy. A peak near x=12 alone need not identify a chemical element: another ion with a different electric charge can occupy the same mass-to-charge ratio interval.
For the narrow singly charged peak at x≈24.98584, the chosen full width at half maximum Δx=0.12 gives mass resolving power R≈208.2; widening to Δx=1.20 gives R≈20.82. Adjacent isotope spacings here are approximately one unit in x. Peaks are visibly separated in the first chosen profile and overlap strongly in the second. A small uncertainty in a fitted peak centre would not by itself make the overlapping components resolved.
Even integrated peak areas need a response model. For isotope fraction fi​, let ηi,z​ collect the probability of ionization into electric charge state z, transmission through the analyzer, and the detection response. In a linear instrument with overall exposure factor C, the component area is
Ai,z​=Cfi​ηi,z​.
(31)
Equal ηi,z​ at a fixed z makes normalized areas equal the fractions; otherwise it does not. In the chosen example, doubling only the mass-25 response changes the normalized areas to 0.71809, 0.18182, and 0.10009, although the neutral sample is unchanged. Photographic darkening introduces another possible nonlinearity: neither line darkness nor peak height is a universal measure of neutral isotopic abundance. Calibration of position and calibration of intensity answer different questions. Matched standards, a known electric charge assignment, and a response correction make isotope analysis useful; a visually impressive set of peaks alone does not supply those controls.
