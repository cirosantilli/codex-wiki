Crystal X-ray spectrometer

 ... Electromagnetic radiation X-ray X-ray tube Characteristic X-ray X-ray emission spectroscopy Siegbahn precision X-ray wavelength spectroscopy
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 4
A crystal X-ray spectrometer selects a narrow wavelength interval by diffraction at known interplanar spacing d and Bragg angle θ. For fixed diffraction order n, a known d lets an angular measurement determine λ=2dsinθ/n. This reverses the crystallography problem of finding d from a known wavelength.
Siegbahn's laboratory developed X-ray tubes and evacuated spectrographs for longer wavelengths. Rotating the crystal selected reflections; photographic plates recorded positions. Opposite-side exposures measured angles on both sides of the direct beam. Later tube instruments mounted a beam-defining slit opposite the plate holder. A separate double instrument compared two reflected beams with two ionization chambers. These were different instruments, not one universal assembly.[ref]
The Nobel biography describes constant-temperature spectrographs and a precision angular scale read by diametrically opposed microscopes.[ref] Temperature control matters because thermal expansion changes d; an accurately read angle cannot compensate for an unknown interplanar spacing.
The schematic separates preparation, selection and readout. An X-ray tube target supplies Characteristic X-rays with a possible bremsstrahlung background. A collimator limits incident directions; evacuation reduces air X-ray attenuation. Rotating the crystal brings different wavelengths into reflection. A photographic plate stores line positions over an exposure, whereas an ionization chamber can measure collected electric charge at successive angles. Neither readout alone establishes absolute wavelength: an angular zero, angular-scale calibration, diffraction order, d and any required refraction correction enter the inference. Narrower slits reduce accepted angular spread but also reduce signal; a weak line can become harder to locate despite a narrower instrumental response.
A control reported in Siegbahn's lecture photographed a line system with copper-sulphate and calcium-sulphate crystals. Irregular relative shifts around copper's characteristic frequency supported anomalous dispersion; the arrangement excluded differing absorption across that frequency as the explanation.[ref] A finer angular scale alone cannot remove this physical refraction correction.
