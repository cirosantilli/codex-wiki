Infimal convolution (E□F)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Convex optimization Convex analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 3
An infimal convolution is the cheapest way to split a prescribed total between two costs. Suppose two activities contribute u and v to a required total x, costing f(u) and g(v). Their combined cost is optimized over all splits u+v=x, rather than charging both activities for the entire total.
For proper extended-real functions f,g:X→R∪{+∞} on a real vector space X, define
h(x)=(f□g)(x)=infu∈X​{f(u)+g(x−u)}=infu+v=x​{f(u)+g(v)}.
(40)
The definition does not require convexity. An infinite cost forbids that contribution, as with an indicator functional of a constraint set. If no split has two finite costs, set h(x)=+∞; if the costs of admissible splits are unbounded below, h(x)=−∞. Thus proper inputs need not produce a proper extended-real function. The notation E□F denotes the same operation. See Parikh and Boyd, Proximal Algorithms, §3.1 for the standard convex analysis definition and its connection to the Moreau envelope.
An infimum is a best lower bound, whereas a minimum is a value realized by some split. Writing min in place of inf requires an attaining pair (u∗​,v∗​). For convex functions, mixing two nearly optimal splits and applying convexity to each cost proves that their infimal convolution is convex whenever it is proper; one must use nearly optimal splits because exact minimizers need not exist. Its convex conjugate is f∗+g∗ whenever the extended real number arithmetic is well defined; see conjugate of an infimal convolution for the precise identity. This turns the optimization over splits into addition in the conjugate variables.
Two quadratic costs: share the load according to its price. Let X=R and let the convex quadratic functions be
f(u)=2a​u2,g(v)=2b​v2,a,b>0.
(41)
For fixed x, eliminate v=x−u. The derivative of the total cost is au−b(x−u), so equal marginal costs give
u∗​=a+bb​x,v∗​=a+ba​x,au∗​=bv∗​.
(42)
The cheaper activity receives more of a positive total: when a<b and x>0, u∗​>v∗​. Completing the square gives an exact certificate for every competing split:
2a​u2+2b​(x−u)2=2(a+b)ab​x2+2a+b​(u−a+bb​x)2.
(43)
Consequently the infimum is a unique minimum, and
(f□g)(x)=21​a+bab​x2.
(44)
The effective quadratic coefficient is c=ab/(a+b), with 1/c=1/a+1/b. It is smaller than either a or b: allowing both activities reduces the cost compared with assigning everything to either one. If a=b, each receives half the total and c=a/2. This is a simple quadratic optimization problem whose answer preserves the form of the original costs.
Smoothing a corner by splitting its cost. Take the absolute value function f(u)=∣u∣ and the convex quadratic function g(v)=v2/(2t), with t>0. This infimal convolution is the Moreau envelope of the absolute value function:
u∗​=sgn(x)max(∣x∣−t,0),v∗​=x−u∗​,(f□g)(x)={x2/(2t),∣x∣−t/2,​∣x∣≤t,∣x∣>t.​
(45)
Here sgn is the sign function, with sgn(0)=0. The optimal u∗​ is soft thresholding. In the central region, the quadratic function carries the whole total; outside it, v∗​ stays at tsgn(x) and the absolute value function carries the excess. At t=1 the result is the Huber loss in the normalization x2/2 near zero and ∣x∣−1/2 outside. Its derivative is x for ∣x∣≤1 and sgn(x) outside, so the corner becomes a continuous change of slope. Parikh and Boyd's [ref] uses twice this normalization for the Huber loss; the explicit formula here fixes the convention. This is also the one-dimensional case of the existing Moreau envelope of the Euclidean norm.
