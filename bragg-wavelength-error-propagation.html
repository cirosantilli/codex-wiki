Bragg wavelength error propagation (δλ/λ≃δd/d+cotθδθ)

OurBigBook.com Words: 218
Differentiate Bragg's law at fixed correctly assigned diffraction order:
nλ=2dsinθ,ndλ=2sinθdd+2dcosθdθ.
(12)
Divide the second equality by the first. For small signed changes,
λδλ​≃dδd​+cotθδθ.
(13)
The Bragg angle error δθ must be in radians. An error quoted for detector deflection 2θ must first be halved. The expression is a first-order Taylor expansion; an unknown diffraction order is a discrete assignment error, not an infinitesimal perturbation.
For error bounds, add magnitudes. For independent zero-mean random errors with standard deviations σd​,σθ​, variance addition instead gives
(λσλ​​)2≃(dσd​​)2+cot2θσθ2​.
(14)
Correlated errors require a covariance term. A shared calibration bias does not become independent merely because many line positions are measured.
With a chosen angular error 10 arcseconds, δθ=4.84814×10−5rad, the angular contribution is 8.3972×10−5 at 30∘ and 1.7646×10−5 at 70∘. Larger Bragg angles reduce this contribution because cotθ decreases. For a nearby spectral doublet, the inverse relation is Δθ≃tanθΔλ/λ: a fixed fractional separation spreads farther apart angularly at large Bragg angle.
This does not permit unlimited improvement. The geometry requires nλ≤2d; a desired wavelength might not have an accessible large-angle reflection with the chosen crystal. Higher diffraction orders can reach larger Bragg angles but their strengths depend on the crystal lattice structure factor. Finite crystal response, slit widths and source size remain relevant. An error in d, including uncorrected thermal expansion, remains even when the angular term is small. Very near 90∘, the leading angular derivative vanishes and the second-order Taylor expansion matters.
