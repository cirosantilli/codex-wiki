A quasar is a luminous active galactic nucleus powered by gas accretion onto a supermassive black hole. The hot disc produces much of the optical/ultraviolet continuum; surrounding gas gives broad and narrow emission lines, while some systems have relativistic jets. A small emitting region is inferred from rapid variability, R≲cΔtobs​/(1+z) when beaming is absent. Sustaining a high luminosity from such a compact region favours accretion over ordinary stellar power. For approximately spherical, ionized hydrogen with electron-scattering opacity,
LE​=σT​4πGMmp​c​≃1.26×1031M⊙​M​W.
(19)
Comparing luminosity with this Eddington scale constrains mass only after assumptions about anisotropy, opacity and accretion state. The emitted power obeys L=ηM˙c2; high binding-energy efficiency makes growth of a massive compact accretor energetically plausible.
Broad-line reverberation mapping estimates a gas radius from a continuum-to-line time lag, R∼cτ, and combines it with line velocity width to infer M∼fvir​Rv2/G. Geometry, inclination and nongravitational gas motions enter the factor fvir​. Independent evidence for compact massive nuclei comes from resolved stellar or gas orbits in nearby galaxies. Andrea M. Ghez and collaborators' Galactic-centre stellar-orbit study fitted a common central potential and distance, demonstrating a compact dark mass of order four million solar masses; the Galactic centre is a low-luminosity example rather than a quasar.[ref]
quasars are an accretion phase of massive nuclear black holes; luminosity, variability and dynamical evidence constrain different aspects of that interpretation. A finite-resolution dark-mass measurement by itself is not a direct image of an event horizon.
