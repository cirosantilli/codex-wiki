Take overdots in this question to mean differentiation with respect to the orbiting particle's proper time τ. Write μ=GM/c2, x=r/μ and ℓ=hc/(GM), with h the specific angular momentum. Then the dimensionless timelike effective potential is
u(x)=c2U​=−x1​+2x2ℓ2​−x3ℓ2​.
(1)
The last term is the relativistic correction to the Newtonian centrifugal-plus-gravitational potential. At large r the two agree. Newtonian angular momentum gives an infinite inner centrifugal barrier for nonzero h; the relativistic correction lowers that barrier and permits plunge. The exterior problem starts at r=2μ, where U=−c2/2; extrapolating the potential towards r=0 is not a static exterior description.
Circular orbits obey
U′=0⟺GMr2−h2r+3μh2=0,h2=r−3μGMr2​.
(2)
There are two extrema when h2>12(GM/c)2: an inner maximum representing an unstable circular orbit and an outer minimum representing a stable one. At equality they merge at r=6μ; below it neither exists. More explicitly, evaluate the curvature at a circular orbit:
U′′∣circ​=r3(r−3μ)GM(r−6μ)​.
(3)
Timelike circular orbits require r>3μ. They are radially stable for r>6μ, so the marginally stable inner limit is rISCO​=6GM/c2. The Newtonian potential instead has one stable minimum at r=h2/(GM), without an innermost stable circular orbit.
For the Schwarzschild circular-orbit local speed in the question, γ is the Lorentz factor relative to a local Schwarzschild static observer, not dt/dτ. That observer's clock obeys dT=1−2μ/r​dt. Since the particle passes with local speed v, rdϕ/dτ=γv. Circular normalization gives
c2(rϕ˙​)2​=r−3μμ​,γ2=1+c2(rϕ˙​)2​=r−3μr−2μ​,c2v2​=r−2μμ​.
(4)
Thus v=c/2 at the innermost stable circular orbit. Using Schwarzschild time in place of the local clock would give a different speed.
For the conserved particle energy measured at infinity, substitute h2 in the circular energy equation. Setting y=μ/r gives
k2=1−2y+1−3yy(1−2y)​=1−3y(1−2y)2​,E=mc21−3μ/r​1−2μ/r​​.
(5)
Take the positive root for a future-directed exterior orbit. It approaches mc2−GMm/(2r) at large radius. The marginally bound circular Schwarzschild orbit solves k=1, giving y(4y−1)=0 and hence rmb​=4GM/c2 at finite radius. It is unstable; strictly bound circular orbits have r>4μ, with E<mc2. The phrase smallest bound orbit here denotes this marginal circular limit, not a prohibition on a bound-energy trajectory plunging below it.
At the innermost stable circular orbit,
EISCO​=98​​mc2≃0.94281mc2​,η=1−98​​≃0.0572​.
(6)
A radiatively efficient thin disc with a zero-torque inner boundary condition can therefore release about 5.7% of the accreted rest energy before the plunge. energy advection, stress at the inner edge and photon capture can alter the escaping luminosity, so this is a model efficiency rather than a universal emitted fraction.
For the Kerr ISCO spin limits of an equatorial prograde disc around a rapidly rotating Kerr black hole, the innermost stable circular orbit moves inward and its specific energy decreases. In the ideal extremal limit its Boyer-Lindquist radius tends to μ, E/(mc2)→1/3​, and the corresponding binding-energy efficiency tends to 42.3%. Retrograde rotation has the opposite trend: the extremal limit is rISCO​=9μ, E/(mc2)=5/(33​), giving about 3.8%. The prograde limiting coordinate radius does not identify a massive orbit with the null horizon generator.[ref]
Finally t˙=k/(1−2μ/r)=1/1−3μ/r​. Dividing ϕ˙​=h/r2 by this gives
Ω∞​=dtdϕ​=r3GM​​​.
(7)
Its algebraic form is exactly Newtonian, but r is the Schwarzschild spacetime areal radius and t Schwarzschild time, normalized at infinity. Local clock rates, orbit stability and binding energy remain relativistic. With GM⊙​≃1.3271×1020m3s−2,
fISCO,∞​=2π63/2G(10M⊙​)c3​≃0.220kHz​,fmb​fISCO​​=(64​)3/2≃0.544​.
(8)
The marginally bound circular-orbit frequency is about 0.404kHz. These Schwarzschild ISCO orbital frequencies and marginally bound frequencies are rotation frequencies per distant stationary observer's time, as intended by using dϕ/dt. A static observer at finite radius ro​ instead divides both frequencies by 1−2μ/ro​​, leaving their ratio unchanged. Identifying an observed quasi-periodic oscillation with the innermost stable circular orbit orbital frequency is an additional astrophysical assumption.

a OurBigBook.com
A donor star can feed a compact companion through Roche-lobe overflow near the inner Lagrange point, or through capture of a stellar wind. The transferred gas usually carries angular momentum; when its circularization radius exceeds the accretor's effective inner radius it forms an accretion disc. Outward angular momentum transport permits inward mass flow, while dissipation converts gravitational binding energy into heat and radiation. For a compact object with a surface L∼GMM˙/R; for a black hole write L=ηM˙c2, with efficiency determined by the flow and inner boundary. Optically thick disc regions emit a sum of thermal radiation spectrum components; a hot optically thin accretion corona can inverse-Compton scatter softer photons into a harder X-ray component. A black hole has no material surface on which to radiate a stellar boundary layer.
An explicit example is the wind-fed black-hole binary Cygnus X-1. Its massive stellar companion supplies gas, and the accretion flow produces strong X-ray emission.[ref] Compact-object evidence comes from orbital dynamics rather than X-ray brightness alone. The single-lined binary mass function constrains the unseen mass; inclination, donor properties and distance refine it. Jerome A. Orosz and collaborators combined optical photometry, radial velocities, rotation and a distance constraint to obtain a black-hole mass of 14.8±1.0M⊙​ in their 2011 analysis. The inference depends on the stellar and orbital model, and that historical value should not be confused with a latest measurement.[ref]
Binary accretion converts orbital mass transfer into compact gravitational-energy release; a dynamical mass measurement identifies the accretor more securely than luminosity alone.

A positive cosmological constant contributes a constant vacuum energy density and negative pressure. In the time-unit convention of Question 2,
ρΛ​=8πGΛ​,pΛ​=−ρΛ​c2,RR¨​=−34πG​(ρ+c23p​)+3Λ​.
(18)
It therefore causes accelerating expansion once its negative-pressure effect outweighs the decelerating components. Its density stays constant while matter dilutes, so a small early contribution can dominate late. For an isolated positive-vacuum component the scale factor grows exponentially. The cosmological constant problem is the mismatch between the small inferred vacuum density and generic quantum-field vacuum-energy expectations, rather than a failure of the background solution itself.
The key direct expansion evidence came from Type Ia supernova distance-redshift measurements. After light-curve and colour standardization, distant events were fainter than expected in simple decelerating matter-only models, indicating larger luminosity distances. Riess and collaborators' 1998 analysis and Perlmutter and collaborators' independent 42-supernova study favoured an accelerating low-matter-density cosmology with a positive vacuum contribution. Dust, source evolution, selection effects, photometric calibration and peculiar velocities must be controlled: faintness alone is not a model-independent measurement of Λ.[ref][ref]
cosmic microwave background acoustic geometry and independent matter-density constraints help break the supernova parameter degeneracy: nearly flat geometry with substantially less than critical matter requires another component within the FLRW model. large-scale structure of the universe and distance measurements provide further consistency tests. The combined evidence supports a dark-energy contribution compatible with a constant; accelerating expansion by itself does not prove exactly constant vacuum energy or rule out every alternative gravity model.

A quasar is a luminous active galactic nucleus powered by gas accretion onto a supermassive black hole. The hot disc produces much of the optical/ultraviolet continuum; surrounding gas gives broad and narrow emission lines, while some systems have relativistic jets. A small emitting region is inferred from rapid variability, R≲cΔtobs​/(1+z) when beaming is absent. Sustaining a high luminosity from such a compact region favours accretion over ordinary stellar power. For approximately spherical, ionized hydrogen with electron-scattering opacity,
LE​=σT​4πGMmp​c​≃1.26×1031M⊙​M​W.
(19)
Comparing luminosity with this Eddington scale constrains mass only after assumptions about anisotropy, opacity and accretion state. The emitted power obeys L=ηM˙c2; high binding-energy efficiency makes growth of a massive compact accretor energetically plausible.
Broad-line reverberation mapping estimates a gas radius from a continuum-to-line time lag, R∼cτ, and combines it with line velocity width to infer M∼fvir​Rv2/G. Geometry, inclination and nongravitational gas motions enter the factor fvir​. Independent evidence for compact massive nuclei comes from resolved stellar or gas orbits in nearby galaxies. Andrea M. Ghez and collaborators' Galactic-centre stellar-orbit study fitted a common central potential and distance, demonstrating a compact dark mass of order four million solar masses; the Galactic centre is a low-luminosity example rather than a quasar.[ref]
quasars are an accretion phase of massive nuclear black holes; luminosity, variability and dynamical evidence constrain different aspects of that interpretation. A finite-resolution dark-mass measurement by itself is not a direct image of an event horizon.

The weak equivalence principle says that freely falling test bodies with identical initial conditions follow the same trajectory independently of composition, when nongravitational forces and appreciable self-gravity are negligible. In Newtonian language it implies universal gravitational-to-inertial mass ratio. The Einstein equivalence principle additionally requires local Lorentz invariance and local position invariance of nongravitational experiments: a sufficiently small freely falling laboratory realizes special relativity. The strong equivalence principle extends universality to gravitational experiments and self-gravitating bodies.
A falling elevator makes the local content clear. Uniform gravitational acceleration can be removed along a freely falling worldline by a suitable frame, but curvature cannot generally be removed over an extended region. Nearby geodesics have relative acceleration
Dτ2D2ξμ​=−Rμανβ​uαξνuβ
(20)
for the displayed Riemann-sign convention. tidal forces therefore remain detectable even in free fall. Equivalence motivates a metric description and gravitational clock shifts, but does not uniquely select Einstein's field equations.
Tests compare accelerations of different materials, frequencies of clocks at different gravitational potentials, or the response of self-gravitating bodies. lunar laser ranging times pulses reflected by lunar retroreflectors and fits Earth-Moon motion in the Sun's field; a differential free-fall violation would add a characteristic periodic range perturbation. The fit must also model tides, station and reflector geometry and propagation delays, with solar-radiation pressure and reflector thermal effects checked as possible mimics. Williams and collaborators' 2012 analysis is a concrete example of this method, published after this examination.[ref]
Equivalence is a local universality statement; tidal curvature and the detailed gravitational field equations are additional physics.

A galaxy cluster deflects light from background galaxies according to its projected gravitational field. In a thin gravitational lens approximation,
β=θ−α(θ),κ=Σcrit​Σ​,Σcrit​=4πGDl​Dls​c2Ds​​.
(21)
The distances are angular-diameter distances; only mass in the appropriate foreground/background geometry lenses the chosen source this way. Near critical curves, strong gravitational lensing produces highly magnified arcs and multiple images of the same source. Farther out, weak gravitational lensing produces small coherent shear distortions, inferred statistically because individual galaxies have unknown intrinsic ellipticities. The measured reduced shear is g=γ/(1−κ). Transparent lensing obeys surface-brightness conservation in gravitational lensing at fixed source redshift while changing solid angle and integrated flux.
Strong-lensing image positions and source redshifts constrain the central projected mass; weak shear extends the map to larger radii. Multiple source planes help constrain geometry and degeneracies, but the mass-sheet degeneracy illustrates why image shapes alone do not uniquely fix absolute mass. Point-spread-function anisotropy, selection, shape calibration, cluster-member contamination, source redshift errors and unrelated line-of-sight structure require controls. Lensing does not require hydrostatic equilibrium, making it valuable for disturbed clusters, although interpretation still depends on the gravitational model.
An instructive observation is the merging Bullet Cluster: weak-lensing mass concentrations approximately follow the collisionless galaxies while the dominant visible baryonic plasma, traced by X-rays, is displaced. Clowe and collaborators combined ground-based and Hubble images for lensing reconstruction and compared them with the gas distribution, providing evidence for mass beyond the observed baryons.[ref]
Cluster lensing maps total projected gravitating mass and magnifies background sources; it does not by itself identify the microscopic dark-matter particle.
