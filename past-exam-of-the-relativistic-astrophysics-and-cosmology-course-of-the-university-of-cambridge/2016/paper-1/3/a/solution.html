Astrophysical jets are directed plasma outflows whose bulk energy and momentum remain concentrated near an axis over distances much larger than the launch region. They occur around accreting black holes and neutron stars and in forming stellar systems, with very different characteristic speeds and scales. An accretion reservoir supplies binding energy; a rotating black hole can additionally provide spin energy through electromagnetic stresses. An observed jet therefore does not uniquely identify either a black hole or a particular launch mechanism.
Magnetic fields couple the rotating engine to the outflow. Gas tied to suitable open field lines can be accelerated centrifugally from a disc; a large-scale poloidal field threading a rotating hole can support outward electromagnetic energy flux. Farther out, magnetic stresses, hoop stresses and external pressure can aid collimation. Pressure-driven acceleration, magnetic-to-kinetic conversion and entrainment depend on loading and environment. A simple stationary axisymmetric picture does not guarantee stability against kinks, mixing or disruption.
The emission commonly contains synchrotron radiation from relativistic electrons spiraling in magnetic fields. Its broad continuum and linear polarization constrain the particles and ordered field component; inverse Compton scattering can extend radiation to higher photon energies. Compact knots can be shocks or moving patterns rather than individual material parcels. Interaction with the surroundings creates shocks, hotspots, cocoons and radio lobes, transporting energy far beyond the central emission region.

Light-travel delays also produce apparent superluminal motion. In a source time interval Δt, the transverse displacement is βcΔtsinθ, but the observer receives the two signals separated by Δt(1−βcosθ). Therefore
βapp​=1−βcosθβsinθ​.​

The maximum is Γβ, at cosθ=β. An apparent speed above c violates no local speed limit. The receding jet is deboosted, so an intrinsically two-sided source may look one-sided; absorption and environmental differences can also contribute.
Jets feed back on their host systems by depositing mechanical energy, inflating cavities, accelerating particles and modifying surrounding gas. Radio luminosity measures only part of that budget; unseen ions, magnetic fields, radiative losses and work on the environment all matter. Connecting the compact engine to a large lobe therefore requires both relativistic emission physics and the dynamics of its surroundings.
