Supernovae are explosive stellar events with different energy sources and progenitors; a remnant is the expanding ejecta and shocked environment left afterward. Their spectra classify the observed event, while explosion mechanisms give a related but distinct physical classification. Lack of hydrogen absorption defines TypeI spectroscopically; TypeII shows hydrogen. TypeIa typically shows silicon features, while stripped-envelope core-collapse events can be TypeIb or Ic.
In core collapse, an evolved massive star's central core loses support as nuclear processing ceases to supply stable pressure support, electron captures reduce electron pressure and photodisintegration consumes energy. Collapse can form a neutron star or black hole. A shock launched near core bounce loses energy, so a successful explosion requires additional processes, often modeled through neutrino heating aided by multidimensional flow; some collapses fail to eject a bright ordinary supernova. The compact remnant and neutrino emission distinguish this channel physically from a thermonuclear disruption.
TypeIa explosions involve thermonuclear burning of a carbon-oxygen white dwarf in a binary-related evolutionary channel. Both near-Chandrasekhar and sub-Chandrasekhar routes are studied; no single progenitor path is forced by the spectral classification alone. Radioactive nickel and cobalt decay can power much of the later optical emission. Empirical light-curve standardization makes these events useful distance indicators, but dust, population differences and calibration must be controlled; they are not intrinsically identical candles.

Thermal X-ray emission traces shocked plasma, optical lines trace cooling gas, and radio synchrotron emission traces relativistic electrons and magnetic fields. Shocks can accelerate cosmic rays and alter the energy budget. Ejecta abundances, a surviving compact object, pulsar-powered nebulae and the remnant's dynamics help reconstruct the explosion, but present morphology alone does not uniquely identify the progenitor.
Supernovae enrich the interstellar medium, stir and heat galactic gas and can drive outflows or trigger local compression. Their feedback couples small stellar scales to galaxy evolution. Quantifying that effect requires accounting for cooling and the collective environment rather than assigning all explosion energy to a permanently retained galactic reservoir.
