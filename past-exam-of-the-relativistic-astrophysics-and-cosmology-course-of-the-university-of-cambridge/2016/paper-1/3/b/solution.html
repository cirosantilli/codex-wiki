The mapping's Jacobian determines area magnification and parity. Its determinant can vanish on critical curves, whose source-plane images are caustics; a source near a caustic can produce highly stretched arcs or changing image multiplicity. In geometric optics, gravity is achromatic and preserves surface brightness after the usual redshift factors, while changing the apparent solid angle and total flux. Frequency-dependent source size, extinction and plasma propagation can modify an observed broadband image without changing this basic gravitational law.

Statistical shear correlations probe projected matter structure and cosmological growth; intrinsic alignments, shape noise and selection require control. Compact masses can instead produce microlensing magnification changes when individual image separations are unresolved.
Lensing weighs both luminous and dark gravitating matter and can reveal mass concentrations without requiring hydrostatic equilibrium. It is nevertheless a projected and model-dependent inference: the mass-sheet degeneracy, line-of-sight structure and uncertain source redshifts can mimic changes in inferred mass or distance. Strong, weak and microlensing therefore constrain different scales and complement dynamical, photometric and cosmological measurements rather than automatically recovering a unique three-dimensional mass map.
