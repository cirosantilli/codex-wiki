A radio pulsar is a rapidly rotating, magnetized neutron star whose emission beam periodically crosses the line of sight. Collapse of a massive stellar core can leave such a compact remnant. Angular-momentum conservation during contraction helps explain rapid rotation, while amplified magnetic fields and a plasma-filled magnetosphere enable particle acceleration and beamed emission. The pulses are not repeated stellar contractions. Not every newborn neutron star is seen as a radio pulsar, because emission, beaming and observational selection matter.

As the star spins down, the available accelerating potential falls. If particle acceleration and pair production can no longer sustain the radio-emitting magnetosphere, the object crosses a model-dependent pulsar death line and ceases to be detectable as an ordinary radio pulsar. It still exists as a cooling neutron star; “death” refers to the emission state, not disappearance of the star. The death boundary is not a precisely universal spin period.
In a surviving binary, later Roche-lobe overflow from a companion can create an accretion disc. Transferred angular momentum spins the old neutron star up, and the accretion luminosity can produce an X-ray binary. Long accretion episodes can produce millisecond rotation, commonly accompanied by a smaller inferred external magnetic field. After mass transfer declines, the radio pulsar can switch on again as a recycled pulsar, often with a white dwarf companion. Other binary histories can leave a second neutron star, an ablated companion or an isolated recycled object; there is no single obligatory evolutionary sequence.
A direct observational bridge is the coherent approximately 2.49 ms X-ray pulsation discovered with RXTE in SAX J1808.4-3658, an accretion-powered binary. It demonstrated an accreting neutron star rotating at a rate appropriate to recycled millisecond radio pulsars.[ref] Timing analysis must correct orbital Doppler shifts, telescope and solar-system timing, and instrumental aliases. Radio dispersion, sky coverage and beaming selection affect demographic inferences. The sequence is rotational pulsar spin-down, possible loss of radio activity, and possible accretion-driven recycling—not a necessary path for every neutron star.
