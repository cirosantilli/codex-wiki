Prisoner's dilemma (T>R>P>S)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Game theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 12
Two people can each have a clear incentive to choose an outcome that leaves both worse off. The prisoner's dilemma isolates this tension between individual best responses and Pareto efficiency. For example, two partners can each save effort by withholding a promised contribution, yet both would prefer a successful joint project to mutual withholding. The model applies when each person's payoffs really have the ordering below; the names “cooperate” and “defect” alone do not establish a prisoner's dilemma.
In the symmetric two-player normal-form game, each player chooses cooperation C or defection D simultaneously, without observing the other's current choice. The four payoffs are the mutual-cooperation reward R, mutual-defection punishment P, sole-defector temptation T, and sole-cooperator payoff S, with T>R>P>S. Defection is a strictly dominant strategy: it beats cooperation against either opposing pure strategy. This definition and the resulting one-shot Nash equilibrium are discussed in Steven Kuhn, Prisoner's Dilemma, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 1.
Take the concrete payoffs T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0. Each entry below gives the row player's payoff followed by the column player's payoff:
CD​C(3,3)(5,0)​D(0,5)(1,1)​​
(2)
For the row player, switching from C to D improves the payoff from 3 to 5 against C, and from 0 to 1 against D. The column player has the same comparisons, so D is a strictly dominant strategy for each. Even against a mixed strategy choosing C with probability q, the expected payoff advantage is 2q+(1−q)=1+q>0. Thus cooperation cannot appear with positive probability in a best response, and (D,D) is the unique one-shot Nash equilibrium, including mixed strategies.
At (D,D), a unilateral deviation to C lowers the deviator's payoff from 1 to 0. A joint change to (C,C) instead raises both payoffs from 1 to 3: mutual cooperation Pareto dominates mutual defection, making the Nash equilibrium fail Pareto efficiency. A Nash equilibrium tests unilateral deviations, so it need not maximize shared gains. This is the useful lesson: improving the available outcome may require changing incentives, rather than telling each participant to choose a better best response.
The additional inequality 2R≥T+S says that mutual cooperation gives each player at least as much average payoff as equal-time alternation between the two asymmetric outcomes. It is a further assumption, not a consequence of T>R>P>S. Here it holds strictly: 6>5, and alternating exploitation gives each player average payoff 5/2<3. This comparison concerns equal-time averages, not every discounted sequence. See Kuhn, section 3, on the additional cardinal-payoff condition.
