Radius of convergence (R)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Sequence and series Power series
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 12
The radius of convergence of ∑n≥0​an​(z−c)n is the unique R∈[0,∞] separating interior absolute convergence from exterior divergence. It converges absolutely when ∣z−c∣<R and diverges when ∣z−c∣>R. At the centre it always has the finite value a0​, since all positive powers vanish. The boundary convergence of a power series is a separate question.
For 0<R<∞, the interior is a disk in the complex plane and an open interval (c−R,c+R) on the real line. A power series converges uniformly on every smaller closed disk ∣z−c∣≤r<R. This gives stable polynomial approximation over a whole compact region, rather than a separate limiting calculation at each point. See Jiří Lebl, Basic Analysis, §11.3.2: convergence of power series.
Four examples reveal what R measures:
  • ∑n≥0​zn has R=1, since its coefficients have constant magnitude one.
  • ∑n≥1​zn/n also has R=1: a factor 1/n changes boundary behaviour without changing the limiting coefficient-root growth.
  • ∑n≥0​zn/n! has R=∞. The ratio test gives successive term ratio ∣z∣/(n+1)→0 for every fixed z, producing the exponential function.
  • ∑n≥0​n!zn has R=0. For any z=0, successive term magnitudes have ratio (n+1)∣z∣→∞, so the terms do not tend to zero.
The Cauchy-Hadamard theorem computes R directly from the coefficients. The attractive feature is that one growth statistic decides convergence throughout the interior and exterior, including sparse series with many zero coefficients. Finite changes to the coefficients do not alter the radius, though they can change the sum.
