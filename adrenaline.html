Adrenaline

 Home Biology Signal transduction Hormone
OurBigBook.com Words: 581 Articles: 1
Adrenaline, also called epinephrine, is an endogenous catecholamine messenger: the body makes it and can release it into blood as a hormone. The adrenal medulla, the inner region of an adrenal gland, secretes adrenaline in response to sympathetic nervous system stimulation. A circulating hormone broadcasts a signal, but each receiving biological cell interprets it through its own receptors and intracellular machinery. Adrenaline activates both alpha-adrenergic receptors and beta-adrenergic receptors; it does not command one identical response everywhere.[ref][ref]
Fuel mobilization is a useful example. A beta-adrenergic receptor can increase adenylyl cyclase activity through a stimulatory G protein, increasing the intracellular second messenger cAMP. Protein kinase A responds to cAMP; a phosphorylation relay through phosphorylase kinase activates glycogen phosphorylase, favoring glycogenolysis. Here adrenaline is the extracellular message, cAMP is the intracellular message, and glycogen is the reserve being mobilized. These are three different roles.[ref][ref][ref]
The two reserves provide different material. Glycogenolysis draws on glycogen; lipolysis breaks down stored triglycerides, releasing fatty acids and glycerol. A skeletal muscle biological cell's own reserve and fuel released by adipose tissue are different supplies. Increasing a signal cannot mobilize an empty reserve: receptor activation changes the use of available material rather than creating fuel. The adrenergic adipocyte assay below gives direct human evidence for the lipolysis branch.[ref]
Receptor context can even oppose this branch. In the human adipocyte preparation below, beta-adrenergic receptors promoted cAMP accumulation, whereas alpha-2 adrenergic receptors opposed it. Alpha-1 adrenergic receptors were associated with a different phospholipid readout. The balance of these responses matters more than the name of the messenger alone.[ref]
This separation is useful when explaining signal transduction: ask which receptor is present, which relay it controls, which reserve or target is available, and which response was actually measured. Receptor binding occupancy, second messenger production and final fuel release are distinct observations. A stronger cAMP signal need not imply an equally large change in every downstream process; substrate availability and the rest of the receiving biological cell still matter.
The extract-transfer experiment under second messenger supplies the earlier experimental separation between hormone reception and an intracellular intermediary. Its existing account includes the preparations and controls. The complementary adrenergic adipocyte assay asks a different question: can the same adrenaline stimulus engage opposing receptors within a human preparation?
