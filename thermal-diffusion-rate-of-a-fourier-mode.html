Thermal diffusion rate of a Fourier mode (β=ξk2)

 ... Statistical physics Thermodynamics Heat Thermal conduction Thermal diffusivity Thermal diffusion time
OurBigBook.com Words: 249
With thermal diffusivity ξ, a Fourier mode of angular wavenumber k decays at rate β=ξk2. To avoid confusing this k with thermal conductivity, use α=ξ and angular wavenumber K below: the same rate is β=αK2, with units s−1.
Consider an insulated bar 0≤x≤L with constant thermal diffusivity, no sources and no heat flux through either end or the sides. An original initial temperature profile and its exact evolution are
T(x,0)=T+A0​cosLnπx​,T(x,t)=T+A0​e−αKn2​tcosLnπx​,Kn​=Lnπ​,n=1,2,….
(27)
The cosine has zero temperature gradient at both ends, satisfying the insulated Neumann boundary conditions. Its spatial mean is zero, so conservation of energy leaves the mean temperature T unchanged while the nonuniform part disappears. A short derivation substitutes T=T+A(t)cos(Kn​x) into the heat equation: the second spatial derivative supplies −Kn2​, hence A′=−αKn2​A. The exponential decay time is τn​=1/(αKn2​); doubling angular wavenumber quarters this time. If cycles-per-length spatial frequency fx​ is used instead, K=2πfx​ and the rate is 4π2αfx2​.
For the two model materials in thermal diffusivity and L=0.010m, τ1,A​≈5.07s and τ1,B​≈20.3s. After 10s, the remaining amplitude fractions are approximately 0.139 and 0.610. A narrow hot-and-cold pattern disappears faster than a broad one because its temperature curvature, and hence the imbalance in conductive heat flux, is larger.
