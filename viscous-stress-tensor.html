Viscous stress tensor (σvisc​=2μD)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics Dynamic viscosity
OurBigBook.com Words: 470 Articles: 1
The viscous stress tensor is the part of the Cauchy stress tensor associated with deformation rates. For an isotropic Newtonian fluid in incompressible flow,
σ=−pI+σvisc​,σvisc​=2μD,D=21​[∇u+(∇u)T],trD=∇⋅u=0.
(7)
Here p is pressure, I is the identity matrix, μ is dynamic viscosity, and D is the strain-rate tensor. The convention is (∇u)ij​=∂j​ui​; T denotes transpose and tr denotes trace. Across a plane with unit normal vector n, the viscous traction is σvisc​n. The tensor makes this rule work for any plane orientation. [ref]
In the worked Couette flow, u=(Uy/h,0,0), so
D=2hU​​010​100​000​​,σvisc​=​020​200​000​​Pa.
(8)
In particular, Dxy​=10s−1 while the shear rate is 20s−1; the factor 2 in 2μD recovers τxy​=2Pa. On the upper face of a fluid slice, n=ey​ gives viscous traction +2ex​Pa; on the lower face it is −2ex​Pa. Constant stress can therefore transmit a force between the plates even though its divergence, and the net viscous force on each slice, vanish.
Compare planar extension u=(ax,−ay,0) with solid-body rotation u=(−Ωy,Ωx,0):
Dextension​=​a00​0−a0​000​​,∇urotation​=​0Ω0​−Ω00​000​​,Drotation​=0.
(9)
Extension changes lengths and produces viscous normal stresses ±2μa. Rotation preserves distances and gives zero viscous stress, despite its nonzero velocity gradient and vorticity 2Ωez​. For a=Ω=1s−1 and μ=0.10Pas, extension gives σvisc,xx​=0.20Pa and σvisc,yy​=−0.20Pa; rotation gives zero. A rotating fluid may still have a pressure gradient supplying its centripetal acceleration.
For an isotropic Newtonian fluid in three-dimensional compressible flow, separate shape change from volume change:
σvisc​=2μ(D−3∇⋅u​I)+ζ(∇⋅u)I.
(10)
The first term is deviatoric stress, with zero trace; ζ is volume viscosity, also called bulk viscosity. For uniform expansion u=bx, D=bI and ∇⋅u=3b, so the shear contribution vanishes and the viscous stress is 3ζbI. Thus dynamic viscosity and volume viscosity describe distinct responses, and incompressible flow removes only the volume-change term. [ref]
