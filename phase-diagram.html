Phase diagram

 ... Statistical physics Thermodynamics Thermodynamic potential Thermodynamic free energy Gibbs free energy Phase coexistence curve
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 20
A phase diagram partitions a space of thermodynamic variables into regions occupied by different equilibrium thermodynamic phases and records the phase coexistence curves between them. For a pure, one-component substance, a pressure–temperature phase diagram gives one point for each pair (T,p). In a single-thermodynamic phase region both controls can vary independently. On a two-thermodynamic phase phase coexistence curve, they obey one relation; at a triple point, both are fixed. These statements assume bulk thermodynamic equilibrium, fixed composition and no additional applied fields.[ref]
At fixed temperature and pressure, the stable bulk thermodynamic phase minimizes Gibbs free energy for a fixed amount of substance. For a pure substance this is a comparison of chemical potentials on a common normalization. Two candidate thermodynamic phases coexist where μα​(T,p)=μβ​(T,p) and neither is exceeded in stability by a third thermodynamic phase. Differentiating their equality gives the Clapeyron equation. Thus a phase coexistence curve is a locus of equal stability, rather than a trajectory that every heated sample must follow.[ref]

They specify sublimation, liquid–gas phase coexistence and melting boundaries for the picture. They are not an equation of state, do not predict latent heat or mass density, and do not supply a quantitative critical theory. In particular, the melting slope is merely an illustrative choice.
For example, at fixed π=3 the melting boundary is at θ=1.2 and the liquid–gas boundary is at θ=2. Heating from (θ,π)=(1.6,3) to (3.6,3) therefore starts in liquid, crosses phase coexistence at (2,3), and ends in gas. In an equilibrium conversion at that crossing, added heat can change the phase fraction at fixed temperature through latent heat. The pressure–temperature point alone does not specify how much of each thermodynamic phase is present: for that one needs an additional amount or volume constraint.
Now connect exactly the same endpoints by raising π from 3 to 6 at θ=1.6, moving to θ=3.6 at π=6, then lowering π to 3. The first leg stays on the liquid side because θmelt​ ranges only from 1.2 to 1.5 and πvap​(1.6)=2.2<3. The second leg lies above the entire liquid–gas boundary, whose largest drawn pressure coordinate is 5; the third lies beyond its largest temperature coordinate, 3. No leg crosses a first-order phase transition boundary. The middle leg passes through a supercritical fluid region, with θ>3 and π>5, and the final leg returns to a gaslike state continuously.
Avoiding a first-order phase transition does not mean that no heat or work is exchanged. Along the detour, mass density, enthalpy and heat capacity may change substantially but continuously. The liquid-gas critical point itself has singular behavior, so the detour goes around it rather than through it. This connected fluid region is the striking lesson of a phase diagram: a familiar change from liquid to gas need not involve boiling across a sharp boundary.
An equilibrium phase diagram also does not predict how rapidly a conversion occurs. Metastability and a barrier described by classical nucleation theory can delay the observed phase transition. Nor should a binary-composition phase diagram be read as the same picture: its coexistence regions and lever rule encode composition and phase fractions, and can occupy an area even though pure-substance two-thermodynamic phase coexistence occupies a curve in the pressure–temperature plane.
