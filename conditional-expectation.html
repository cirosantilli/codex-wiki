Conditional expectation (E[X∣G])

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Measure theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 17
The conditional expectation is the average of a random variable that can be predicted using specified information. It is itself a random variable: the average appropriate to one observed group may differ from that appropriate to another group. The sigma-algebra G records which events can be distinguished with the available information.
For a real integrable random variable X on a probability space (Ω,F,P) and a sub-sigma-algebra G, the conditional expectation m=E[X∣G] is the almost surely unique integrable random variable that is measurable with respect to G and satisfies
∫A​mdP=∫A​XdPfor every A∈G.
(19)
Its average therefore agrees with that of X on every distinguishable event. The integral characterization handles discrete observations, continuous observations, and information that is not given by a single numerical value.[ref]
If the information tells us which member of a finite measurable partition A1​,…,Ak​ contains the outcome, then on every member of positive probability,
m=P(Ai​)E[X1Ai​​]​on Ai​.
(20)
Here 1Ai​​ is an indicator function. Zero-probability members do not determine a unique value, which is why uniqueness is only almost surely. For square-integrable X, this same conditional mean is the best predictor among G-measurable random variables under mean squared error; the conditional variance describes its remaining error. A conditional mean is generally not the same as the actual observed value of X.[ref]
