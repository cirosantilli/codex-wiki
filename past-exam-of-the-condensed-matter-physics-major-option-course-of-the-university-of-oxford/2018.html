A phonon is one quantum of a normal vibrational mode of a crystal. In the harmonic approximation, each mode with wavevector q, branch index s and angular frequency ωs​(q) is an oscillator with energies ℏωs​(n+1/2). A phonon changes the occupation n by one; it is not a particular atom moving through the solid.
For a three-dimensional primitive cell containing r atoms there are 3r branches. Three acoustic branches approach zero frequency as q→0: at zero wavevector they represent rigid translations, with all basis atoms displaced together. Their small-wavevector frequencies usually grow linearly with wavevector. The remaining 3r−3 optical branches have internal relative motion of basis atoms and generally nonzero zone-centre frequencies. The names reflect their coupling to radiation in suitable polar crystals, not a rule that every optical mode must be infrared active. Acoustic and optical branches can each have longitudinal or transverse polarizations.

At the zone centre, the dynamical matrix commutes with the point-group representation on displacements. An irreducible representation of dimension two enforces a doublet; distinct one-dimensional representations need not have equal frequencies. The tetragonal point group D4h​ has two-dimensional Eg​ and Eu​ representations, so optical modes transforming that way are doubly degenerate at the centre. Atomic positions and the displacement representation would be needed to assign every optical branch to a particular representation; the point-group label alone is not a complete mode decomposition.
For propagation along the tetragonal fourfold axis, the two transverse acoustic polarizations transform together and have equal sound speeds and frequencies. For example ωTA,x​=ωTA,y​≃vT​∣qz​∣, whereas the longitudinal speed is independent. An optical doublet may split away from the centre along a direction whose little group has only one-dimensional representations. It need not remain degenerate at every nearby wavevector.
All irreducible representations of D2h​ are one-dimensional, so the orthorhombic point group enforces no analogous optical doublet or equality of transverse sound speeds. The three acoustic frequencies are nevertheless all zero at the exact centre in both crystals, because of translations; that common zero is not evidence that their slopes are equal. Accidental equalities remain possible. For a polar crystal, a direction-dependent macroscopic electric field also distinguishes longitudinal from transverse infrared-active limits; an analytic zone-centre symmetry classification alone does not remove LO–TO splitting.

At a valley K, γ(K)=1+2cos(2π/3)=0. For h-BN the inequivalent sublattice energies therefore leave the direct two-band gap
Eg​=∣ϵB​−ϵN​∣.​
(18)

Near a valley, ∣γ(K+q)∣≃(3​a/2)∣q∣: graphene has approximately linear, gapless Dirac cones while h-BN has gapped band edges. The model panel in the original figure in solution displays this distinction.
First, undoped h-BN is an electrical insulator with thermally suppressed carriers, whereas neutral graphene is a zero-gap semimetal whose transport is readily changed by gating. Disorder, temperature and contacts still affect its measured conductivity. Second, h-BN suppresses interband absorption below its large gap and is transparent over much of the visible range, whereas graphene permits low-energy interband absorption unless occupations block it. A quantitative optical threshold in real h-BN also involves additional bands and strong excitonic effects; a two-orbital model should not be presented as a numerical prediction of its complete optical spectrum.

Angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy illuminates a surface with photons of known energy and measures the kinetic energy and emission direction of the escaping electrons. Referencing the initial binding energy to the Fermi level gives
EB​=hνlight​−Φ−Ekin​,k∥​=ℏ2me​Ekin​​​sinθ.​
(19)
Here Φ is the sample/analyser work-function reference appropriate to the calibrated energy scale, and θ is the emission angle from the surface normal. Parallel crystal momentum is conserved modulo a surface reciprocal lattice vector; measuring two angular coordinates maps the two in-plane momentum components.
Sweeping angle and energy traces the occupied spectral weight and its dispersion. In a quasi-two-dimensional sheet, no detailed free-electron model of the perpendicular final-state momentum is required to map the in-plane bands. Peaks reflect the occupied single-particle spectral function, multiplied by Fermi occupation and photoemission matrix elements and broadened by finite lifetimes and resolution. Ordinary photoemission does not directly measure empty conduction bands; a gap may need complementary information. A clean surface, vacuum and charge control are especially important for an insulating specimen.

For type I, a magnetic unit cell uses the magnetic Bravais translations (0,a,a), (a,0,a) and (a,a,0). They form an FCC lattice with conventional cubic parameter 2a. A two-spin basis is an up spin at (0,0,0) and a down spin at (a,0,0), or fractional conventional coordinates (0,0,0) and (1/2,0,0). Every translation preserves the parity of nx​+ny​+nz​.
At Q=(2π/a)(h,k,l), the FCC translation sum allows h,k,l all integers or all half-odd-integers. The spin basis contributes S[1−e2πih], which removes the integer set and leaves
type I: (h,k,l)=(n+21​,m+21​,p+21​).​
(23)
Equivalently the spin field is S(−1)nx​+ny​+nz​, so magnetic Bragg peaks are nuclear reciprocal vectors shifted by (1/2,1/2,1/2).
For type II with parallel spins along z, use (a,a,0), (−a,a,0) and (0,0,a) as magnetic translations: a primitive tetragonal cell with side lengths 2​a,2​a,a, containing an up spin at zero and a down spin at (a,0,0). Below TN​, a small perpendicular field produces canting of the two ordered sublattices. In the isotropic two-sublattice mean-field model, the induced component is B⊥​/λ: the reduced ordered moment at higher temperature tilts more easily and the two effects cancel. Thus χ⊥​ remains constant until the transition.
A field parallel to the maintained ordering axis instead increases the magnitude of one sublattice magnetization and decreases the other. At zero temperature saturated localized spins have essentially no such amplitude response in this model, so χ∥​→0. Thermal population changes make it easier to redistribute the longitudinal moments as temperature rises, giving the growing longitudinal susceptibility that meets χ⊥​ at TN​. These directions presume the domain or a weak anisotropy fixes the ordered axis; in a perfectly isotropic freely rotating sample the Néel vector can reorient.
Above TN​ there is no chosen magnetic axis, so the response is isotropic. Antiferromagnetic correlations oppose uniform magnetization, giving the mean-field Curie–Weiss form
χ(T)=T+TN​C​,C=3kB​a3μ0​(gμB​)2S(S+1)​,kB​TN​=2J1​S(S+1).​
(28)
At TN​ it equals C/(2TN​)=μ0​/λ, consistent with the transverse branch; at large temperature it decreases as 1/T. A negative Weiss temperature characterizes this uniform antiferromagnetic response, rather than a ferromagnetic divergence at positive TN​. The illustrative mean-field curves in solution make these limits visible.

In Raman scattering, the photon transfers ℏω=ℏ(ωi​−ωs​) and q=ki​−ks​ to a collective excitation. Changing scattering geometry selects different q and probes its dispersion.
The open circles rise and then flatten near 45meV: they trace the lower transverse phonon polariton branch. It is strongly dispersive on its photon-like side, then becomes predominantly TO-phonon-like and approaches the TO energy at larger wavevector. The open triangles stay near 50meV and are the nearly nondispersive longitudinal optical phonon. Longitudinal motion creates its own polarization field and is shifted above the transverse resonance. It does not hybridize with a propagating transverse photon in the same way, and it should not be misidentified as the upper transverse polariton branch, which rises with wavevector. Raman tensor and polarization selection rules determine which excitation is visible.

In weak-coupling BCS theory, a useful scale is
kB​Tc​≃1.14ℏωD​exp[−N(0)Veff​1​].​
(38)
Here the phonon spectrum sets the attractive-interaction cutoff, N(0) is the consistently defined Fermi-level density of states and Veff​ the effective pairing attraction after repulsion is accounted for. Light hydrogen supports high vibrational frequencies; strong electron–phonon coupling and favourable metallic density of states can raise Tc​. Pressure changes bonding, phase stability, electronic structure and phonon frequencies. It is not simply an independent multiplier in this expression. In a real strongly coupled hydride, Eliashberg theory uses the full spectral function and a screened Coulomb pseudopotential; the elementary exponential is a mechanism guide rather than a controlled quantitative fit to 203K.
Measure a four-terminal resistance that falls below the instrumental bound, and test that a magnetic field suppresses the transition. Independently detect diamagnetic screening and, where feasible, field-cooled flux expulsion or an AC susceptibility signal with controlled cell background. A resistance drop alone can reflect contact changes or a filamentary path; a consistent magnetic response is important evidence for superconductivity. A reproducible isotope shift upon replacing hydrogen by deuterium tests the involvement of lattice motion, although it is not by itself proof of superconductivity.
Extreme-pressure samples are microscopic and enclosed in a diamond-anvil cell. Reliable separate electrical contacts, homogeneous pressure, pressure calibration during cooling, chemical stability and the tiny magnetic signal relative to the anvils and gasket are practical difficulties. A sufficiently clean two-dimensional electron gas at low temperature in a perpendicular magnetic field has quantized orbital Landau levels. In the integer quantum Hall regime, the longitudinal resistivity is nearly zero while the Hall resistance has plateaux
∣Rxy​∣=pe2h​,p∈N.​
(46)
The Hall sign depends on carrier charge and lead convention. Fractional plateaux can occur in strongly interacting states, but the following single-particle counting and calibration use the integer effect.
One resolved spin branch of a Landau level has one state per flux quantum h/e: in area A, its guiding-centre degeneracy is AeBz​/h for positive Bz​. Defining the nominal filling factor as the number of electrons per available orbital state gives
ν=AeBz​/hNS​A​,NS​=hνeBz​​.​
(47)
The same count follows in Landau gauge: a 2π/Ly​ increment in ky​ moves the orbit centre by 2πℓB2​/Ly​, giving A/(2πℓB2​)=AeBz​/h states. Include each spin branch separately; an unresolved spin-degenerate orbital level contains twice this count, and common plateau indices are then even.

Therefore NS​=peBz​/h is not an exact identity across an entire plateau. The plateau resistance realizes the von Klitzing constant RK​=h/e2≃25812.8Ω, divided by a known integer. A four-terminal Hall measurement with negligible longitudinal voltage and contacts in the quantized regime supplies a reproducible resistance reference, which can be compared to other resistors using precision bridges. A perfectly clean two-dimensional system has sharply degenerate Landau levels and integer filling only at isolated fields for fixed density. Real disorder broadens each level into a band, with states localized in the tails and extended states near its centre. When the chemical potential moves through a localized region, changing field can change the occupation of those localized states without changing the number of conducting extended channels. The Hall conductance then remains pe2/h and the Hall voltage at fixed current remains Ix​h/(pe2) over a finite field interval.
In the same regime, separated edge channels and localized bulk states suppress dissipative backscattering, so ρxx​ is very small. At a transition the chemical potential reaches the next band of extended states; longitudinal conduction rises and the Hall conductance changes to another integer. This is a mobility-gap explanation: a plateau does not require the density of states itself to vanish, nor does it require the carrier density to grow linearly with field. Low temperature, sufficient mobility and suitable contacts are needed to resolve it.
