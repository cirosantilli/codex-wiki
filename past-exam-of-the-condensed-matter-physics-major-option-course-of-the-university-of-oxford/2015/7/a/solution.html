A Frenkel exciton is a tightly bound electron–hole excitation localized on approximately one molecular or ionic unit. Its radius is comparable to the lattice spacing, its exciton binding energy can be large, and transport is described through transfer of the local excitation between units. Molecular solids such as crystalline anthracene provide a natural example: the optical excitation is largely associated with an individual molecule, so a broad parabolic-band hydrogen model is not the most useful starting point.
A Wannier-Mott exciton extends over many unit cells. It is described approximately by a conduction electron and a valence hole with effective masses and a dielectric-screened Coulomb attraction. Its binding is usually smaller, and its levels resemble a hydrogenic series. gallium arsenide is a suitable example because its direct-gap band-edge carriers have light effective masses and substantial dielectric dielectric screening, supporting spatially extended excitons. Localization length and dielectric screening, rather than whether a material happens to be called a semiconductor, distinguish the two limiting pictures. Real materials can lie between the limits, so neither family is a universal sharp classification.
