Away from an absorption edge, the scalar atomic scattering factor is the Fourier transform of an approximately spherical, real atomic electron density. Opposite points contribute conjugate phases, so their sum is real. In Thomson scattering the electronic response is also effectively in phase with the driving field in this nonabsorbing approximation. Near an absorption edge, resonantly driven electronic excitations introduce a phase lag and absorption. The atomic scattering factor becomes f0​+f′+if′′, with f′′ describing the absorptive component and f′ its dispersive companion. The sign of the imaginary part depends on the time and scattering-phase conventions; here it is the source's +if′′ convention. This discussion concerns scalar charge scattering, without magnetic or tensor resonant contributions.

Use fractional coordinates and the structure factor convention
F(h)=∑j​fj​e2πih⋅rj​,I(h)∝∣F(h)∣2.
(1)
For real atomic scattering factors, F(−h)=F(h)∗, immediately proving Friedel's law. If an inversion centre is chosen as origin, each site at rj​ has an identical partner at −rj​, with the same possibly complex atomic scattering factor. Each pair contributes 2fj​cos(2πh⋅rj​); sites fixed by inversion modulo a lattice translation also have even phase factors at reciprocal-lattice vectors. Hence
F(−h)=F(h),I(−h)=I(h)​
(2)
even for scalar anomalous X-ray scattering in a centrosymmetric structure. This last equality is an equality of amplitudes, not generally complex conjugacy.

Write Fx​(h)=fx​Sx​(h,k,l), where the four-site orbit has structure factor
Sx​(h,k,l)=​e2πix(h+k+l)+(−1)h+le2πix(−h−k+l)+(−1)k+le2πix(−h+k−l)+(−1)h+ke2πix(h−k−l).​

Under spatial inversion the electric polarization, a polar vector, changes from P to −P. Neumann's principle requires an equilibrium property of a phase retaining inversion symmetry to be unchanged. Thus P=−P, forcing P=0: a centrosymmetric phase cannot itself be ferroelectric. A transition can, of course, remove that symmetry.
For an inversion-symmetric parent phase the Landau theory free energy satisfies F(P)=F(−P). Every polynomial term of odd total degree changes sign and must have zero coefficient. Restricting to one fixed direction gives an even scalar expansion in P.

For the full prism point group, a threefold rotation eliminates any invariant basal component of a polar vector, while the horizontal mirror eliminates its axial component. Therefore the phase preserving all of D3h​ is nonpolar, despite lacking inversion. A polar phase would have to break some of these symmetries.
The indicated six vertex directions comprise three basal directions and their horizontal-mirror partners. Crucially, this orbit does not contain the negatives of those vectors. Choose z along the prism and x toward one basal vertex. The cubic polynomial
I3​=Px​(Px2​−3Py2​)
(9)
is invariant under the prism's rotations and mirrors. Along a vertex direction P=Pn with a nonzero basal component, it is proportional to P3, so odd scalar terms are symmetry-allowed. Along the prism axis the horizontal mirror instead sends Pz​↦−Pz​, forcing an even free energy. Merely omitting inversion is insufficient to decide either polarity or the allowed invariants.

For a partially filled 3d shell, electron–electron Coulomb repulsion and Hund's rules first favour particular total spin and orbital terms. The crystal field splitting is substantial because these orbitals overlap the ligand environment; it normally exceeds the spin-orbit coupling and often quenches the orbital moment. Competition between the splitting and the electron pairing energy determines high-spin versus low-spin filling. A common hierarchy is Coulomb term energies, then crystal-field and Hund-exchange scales, then spin–orbit and weak-field Zeeman energies; the relative sizes of crystal-field splitting and pairing are material-dependent. Hyperfine coupling, if the nucleus carries spin, adds a smaller splitting. An isolated ion has no inter-ion exchange ordering field.

For a partially filled 4f shell, the outer 5s and 5p shells shield the localized orbitals from the ligands. Coulomb interactions establish L,S through Hund's rules, and spin-orbit coupling usually exceeds the much smaller crystal field splitting. First form a ground J multiplet using J=L+S, then split that multiplet by the crystal environment. The orbital contribution is therefore generally retained, and the g-factor is a multiplet Landé factor rather than an almost spin-only value. Weak external Zeeman and hyperfine interactions can subsequently resolve the remaining states. Exceptions with J=0 or unusual valence require separate treatment.

The tetrahedral crystal field splitting places the two e orbitals below the three t2​ orbitals. In the weak-field, high-spin limit the occupancy is e2t21​, with S=3/2. Its lowest cubic term is 4T1​: an orbital triplet, before spin degeneracy is counted. In the hypothetical sufficiently strong-field limit the occupancy is e3, with S=1/2 and an orbital doublet 2E. This latter limit requires overcoming pairing/Hund energy; ordinary tetrahedral oxide complexes generally remain high-spin.
Both orbital degeneracies permit a Jahn-Teller effect, so a regular tetrahedron is not a stable static equilibrium in the elementary adiabatic treatment. The sign of a particular distortion requires an electron–lattice coupling model, not just the degeneracy count. To make that dependence explicit, let Q>0 stretch z while contracting x,y. In a leading point-charge quadrupole model write the shifts
orbitalδϵ​dxy​−2gQ​dxz​gQ​dyz​gQ​dz2​2gQ​dx2−y2​−2gQ​​(g>0).
(16)
In the high-spin configuration the two e shifts cancel. For Q>0 the remaining electron selects dxy​, gaining −2gQ; for Q<0 it selects the dxz​,dyz​ doublet, gaining gQ. Adding KQ2/2 gives a tetragonally elongated minimum at Q=2g/K, with energy gain 2g2/K, versus only g2/(2K) along the compressed branch. The elongated branch has an orbital singlet. This is the usual elementary elongation argument; quantitative coupling includes higher crystal-field multipoles and configuration mixing.
For the strong-field e3 configuration the lower e orbital is doubly occupied. The shift of the three electrons is −2g∣Q∣, so the same model has both an elongated and a compressed minimum, Q=±2g/K, with equal stabilization. Elongation favours double occupation of dx2−y2​; compression favours double occupation of dz2​. This tetrahedral doublet distortion-sign ambiguity prevents a unique choice from the supplied data. For example, a small symmetry-allowed cubic elastic invariant AQθ​(Qθ2​−3Qϵ2​) selects opposite tetragonal signs when A changes sign, while leaving the regular-site electronic configuration unchanged. Higher couplings or host constraints are needed to choose. Dynamic motion between equivalent minima may also restore an average regular shape without stabilizing the undistorted adiabatic configuration.
Once a static axial distortion removes the orbital degeneracy, second-order spin-orbit coupling produces a time-reversal-even quadratic spin Hamiltonian operator. The residual tetragonal symmetry requires equal x,y coefficients. Dropping a multiple of S(S+1) leaves Han​=DSz2​ for the weak-field S=3/2 state. The sign and magnitude of D depend on excited-state splittings. For the strong-field S=1/2 Kramers doublet, Sz2​=1/4 is constant: this term cannot cause zero-field splitting, although the Zeeman g-factor can be anisotropic.

For competing exchanges on a body-centred cubic antiferromagnet, use the Heisenberg model convention H=∑⟨ij⟩​Jij​Si​⋅Sj​, with each bond counted once, dimensionless spins, and positive antiferromagnetic J. There are eight nearest and six next-nearest neighbours. Bond counting in the two classical collinear structures gives
NEI​​=S2(−4J1​+3J2​),NEII​​=−3J2​S2.
(22)
For type II the eight J1​ contributions cancel, while all six J2​ bonds are antiparallel. Their crossing yields c=2/3. One can also exclude a lower spiral within the classical bilinear model: writing ui​=cos(qi​a/2) gives
J(q)=8J1​ux​uy​uz​+4J2​(ux2​+uy2​+uz2​)−6J2​.
(23)
For a favourable sign of the product, ux2​+uy2​+uz2​≥3∣ux​uy​uz​∣2/3≥3∣ux​uy​uz​∣. Hence when J2​≥2J1​/3 its minimum is at ux​=uy​=uz​=0, the type-II ordering vectors. When J2​≤2J1​/3, compare with the type-I value and use 3−∑ui2​≤3(1−∣ux​uy​uz​∣) to obtain the complementary bound. Thus
J2​/J1​>2/3 ⟹ classical type-II order​.
(24)
This is a classical/mean-field energy comparison; an exact quantum ground state is not generally a collinear product state.
For type I a magnetic cell is the simple cube of side a, with opposite moments at 0 and a(1,1,1)/2. Its magnetic structure factor is proportional to 1−eiπ(h+k+l). Allowed magnetic reflections have integer h,k,l with h+k+l odd. The first two families are (100) and (111), with symmetry-equivalent members understood.
For type II choose magnetic primitive translations a(1,1,0), a(1,0,1) and a(0,1,1), enclosing four spins in volume 2a3. Equivalently, a side-2a cube is a convenient nonprimitive magnetic cell. On each original simple-cubic sublattice the moment at an is proportional to (−1)nx​+ny​+nz​. Accordingly,
(h,k,l)=(p+1/2,q+1/2,r+1/2),p,q,r∈Z.
(25)
The translated body-centre basis gives 1+eiπ(h+k+l)=1±i, so there is no further extinction. The first two families are (1/2,1/2,1/2) and (3/2,1/2,1/2). The neutron polarization factor may additionally suppress a reflection for a particular moment direction; the stated rules are the positional selection rules.
Each sublattice has number density nA​=1/a3 and saturation magnetization Ms​=gμB​S/a3. Replacing neighbouring spins by their averages makes their exchange energy equivalent to a molecular magnetic field. Its minus sign expresses antiferromagnetic exchange, not an externally applied field. Since ⟨SB​⟩=MB​/(nA​gμB​),
λAB​=g2μB2​8J1​a3​,λAA​=g2μB2​6J2​a3​​.
(26)
For MB​=−MA​, Bmf​=(λAB​−λAA​)MA​. Linearizing the Brillouin magnetization formula gives
kB​TN​=3S(S+1)​(8J1​−6J2​)​.
(27)
The g-factor cancels because exchange is specified in energy units. A convention with 2JSi​⋅Sj​ doubles these exchange energies and TN​.
Let m=MA​/Ms​, A=(S+1)/(3S) and let bB​>0 denote the cubic coefficient of the Brillouin magnetization formula. The argument is y=(TN​/T)m/A. Thus m=Ay−bB​y3 implies
m2=bB​A3​(TN​T​)3(TTN​​−1)∼bB​A3​TN​TN​−T​.
(28)
Consequently β=1/2​ in mean-field theory. The positive branch falls from saturation at zero temperature to zero at TN​ and remains zero above it. An elastic magnetic neutron-reflection intensity scales as I∝MA2​; a log–log fit against TN​−T has slope 2β. Background subtraction and a suitable critical interval are needed. Fluctuations can change the measured exponent from its mean-field value; uniform bulk magnetization vanishes for a compensated antiferromagnet and is not itself this order parameter.

For a periodic single-electron Hamiltonian operator, Bloch's theorem gives
ψbk​(r)=eik⋅rubk​(r),ubk​(r+R)=ubk​(r),ψbk​(r+R)=eik⋅Rψbk​(r).
(29)
Two bands at the same k have the same translation eigenvalues but different cell-periodic eigenfunctions, generally different energies and different orbital/nodal character. They may be chosen orthogonal even at a band degeneracy. The basis choices are treated in the two scoped branches below.
For a slowly varying external potential U(r), the semiclassical wavepacket Hamiltonian is ϵb​(k)+U(r). Hamilton's equation for the conjugate variables r,ℏk gives ℏk˙=−∇U=F. Equivalently the electric perturbation changes the translation phase at the rate qE⋅R/ℏ. This is the single-band acceleration theorem, requiring a slowly varying field and negligible interband tunnelling/scattering; it refers to crystal momentum, not free-space mechanical momentum.
Let e>0 and K=eE0​/(ℏω). Integrating from the specified initial condition k(0)=0 gives, with the first field alone,
kx​=K(cosωt−1),ky​=0​.
(30)
With both components switched on,
kx​=K(cosωt−1),ky​=−Ksinωt​.
(31)
This is a circle centred at (−K,0), not at the band minimum. Since vi​=ℏki​/mi∗​, integration gives

In a frame moving with that drift, the oscillation is an ellipse with semiaxis ratio my∗​/mx∗​≫1. A closed real-space ellipse would require different initial momentum. Indeed, after one period x−x0​=−2πeE0​/(mx∗​ω2)=0, an explicit check against that interpretation.

ϵ−ϵΓ​=2ω2e2E02​​[mx∗​(1−cosθ)2​+my∗​sin2θ​].

The conduction and valence dispersions are two cones touching at the Dirac point, with slopes ±ℏvF​. Here k is measured relative to one valley centre. Each cone has a density of states proportional to ∣E∣, which vanishes at the touching energy. Gate-induced carriers satisfy approximately n=Cg​(Vg​−VD​)/e per unit area. Negative and positive offsets from VD​ produce holes and electrons, respectively. Away from neutrality a simple charge carrier mobility model gives σ≃eμ∣n∣, so resistance decreases on either side of a maximum near VD​. Disorder, electron–hole puddles, contacts and quantum transport round the peak; the cone dispersion alone fixes neither its height nor its width, nor does it require exact electron–hole symmetry in a real device.
A direct derivation of the magnetic spectrum starts from the single-valley Dirac Hamiltonian operator H=vF​(σx​πx​+σy​πy​). Introduce oscillator operators using the magnetic length ℓB​=ℏ/(e∣B∣)​. Up to exchanging sublattices or valleys,
H=ℓB​2​ℏvF​​(0a†​a0​),[a,a†]=1.
(37)
Squaring gives adjacent oscillator number operators, hence
Ej​=sgn(j)vF​2eℏ∣B∣∣j∣​,j∈Z,E0​=0​.
(38)
The positive expression printed in the source describes the electron branch only; the negative-energy partners must also be included. The same square-root dependence follows from quantizing circular orbit area, with the Dirac Berry phase cancelling the usual half-integer offset.
Each spin and valley contributes Ae∣B∣/h states in area A. Including two spins and two valleys gives NLL​=4Ae∣B∣/h, or areal degeneracy 4e∣B∣/h. The zero-energy level is half-filled at charge neutrality: the carrier density counts departures from that state.
For n=1016 m−2, the filling factor and fourfold graphene Hall sequence are
ν=eBnh​=B41.3567 T​,νN​=4(N+1/2)=2,6,10,14,….
(39)
In a plateau, ρxx​≃0 and ∣ρxy​∣=h/(νN​e2). The Hall sign reverses between electron and hole doping; the sketch uses its magnitude to avoid tensor-index sign conventions. Representative plateau-centre fields are 20.68, 6.893, 4.136 and 2.954 T for ν=2,6,10,14. Between plateaux, crossing extended states in broadened levels gives longitudinal peaks and Hall steps. Their ideal filling-centre positions have ν=4j, namely
Bj​=j10.3392 T​,j=1,2,3,….​
(40)
Thus peaks near 10.34, 5.17 and 3.45 T separate the first prominent plateaux as field increases. Many weak oscillations accumulate toward zero field and are damped there. Finite-width plateaux require localized states between extended-state energies; an exactly clean Landau spectrum does not by itself prescribe plateau widths. The illustration uses an explicitly schematic broadening, not inferred experimental widths.
At 6.5 T, ν=6.36, close to the ν=6 plateau. With vF​=106 ms−1, E1​=92.5 meV and E2​=130.8 meV. In BCS theory, an electron distorts the positively charged lattice, and the retarded response can attract another electron. Within a phonon-energy shell around the Fermi surface, the effective attraction can overcome the screened Coulomb repulsion and destabilize the normal Fermi sea toward Cooper pairs. In conventional elemental superconductors the pairs predominantly join time-reversed states of opposite momentum and spin, forming a spin singlet.
In the weak-coupling approximation,
kB​Tc​≃1.14ℏωD​exp[−1/(N(0)Veff​)]​.
(49)
Here N(0) is the appropriate single-spin density of states and Veff​>0 the effective attractive interaction in a consistent normalization. Both the phonon scale and the exponential coupling matter. A large Fermi-level density of states, often associated with partially filled narrow d bands, can raise Tc​; weak coupling or a small density of states can suppress it even in a good conductor. With force constants and coupling fixed, ωD​∝M−1/2 gives the conventional isotope trend. This is not a universal monotonic ranking of elements by atomic mass: phonon stiffness, electron–phonon matrix elements, Coulomb effects and magnetism vary between elements. Magnetic pair breaking helps explain why some elements do not follow a naive density-of-states expectation.

The competition between magnetic screening over the London penetration depth λ and condensate recovery over the superconducting coherence length ξ is measured by the Ginzburg-Landau parameter κ=λ/ξ. In the single-component local theory the boundary is κ=1/2​. For κ<1/2​, a normal–superconducting interface has positive energy: a type-I superconductor favours macroscopic regions and a thermodynamic critical field Hc​. Demagnetizing fields may create an intermediate state of normal and superconducting domains.
For κ>1/2​ the interface energy is negative. A type-II superconductor admits flux vortices between the lower and upper critical fields, with normal-like cores of size ξ and circulating screening currents over λ. Each singly quantized vortex carries Φ0​=h/(2e); a vortex lattice and pinning-controlled irreversible response can occur. Pairing strength, carrier density, Fermi velocity and impurity scattering affect these lengths; Tc​ alone does not determine the type. The stated boundary is the standard Ginzburg–Landau result near Tc​.
