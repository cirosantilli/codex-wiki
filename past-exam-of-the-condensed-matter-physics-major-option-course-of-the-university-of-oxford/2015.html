A screw axis combines rotation about a line with translation parallel to that line. An np​ screw rotates through 2π/n and advances by p/n of the lattice repeat along the axis; a 21​ screw advances by half a repeat after a half-turn. The combined operation, rather than either component separately, must map every atomic species onto equivalent sites.
A glide plane combines reflection in a plane with a fractional translation parallel to it. Reflecting alone need not preserve the crystal. A crystallographic point symmetry is an isometry with a fixed point, such as a rotation, reflection, inversion, or rotoinversion. Point operations have no irreducible translational advance: a screw or a glide with nonzero fractional advance cannot be described merely as a rotation or mirror at a different origin. A crystal's point group records the rotational parts of its space-group operations, whereas the full space group retains their translations.

Take the square projection cell 0≤x,y≤1 and label the heights rather than erasing them in projection. The Fe positions appear at the cell corners and at (1/2,1/2), both with z=0. The Se projections are (1/2,0) at height +η and (0,1/2) at height −η, together with their periodic copies on opposite edges. These are distinct height classes even though the observer looks down the z axis.
The corner Fe sites together count as one atom per cell, the central Fe as one, and each edge-shared Se projection contributes half at each of two equivalent edges. The count is therefore two Fe and two Se per primitive tetragonal lattice cell. The Fe sublattice has its shorter nearest-neighbor directions along the projected cell diagonals. The distinction between those directions and the conventional tetragonal axes matters for the distortion in d.

A representative half-turn-plus-translation operation about an axis parallel to x is
gx​:(x,y,z)↦(x+21​,21​−y,−z).
(1)
It rotates about the line y=1/4,z=0 and translates by a/2 along x. It exchanges the two Fe sites, and maps (1/2,0,η) onto (0,1/2,−η) modulo a lattice translation. Likewise,
gy​:(x,y,z)↦(21​−x,y+21​,−z)
(2)
is a 21​ screw about x=1/4,z=0. Including translated equivalents gives the principal-axis screw lines
[100]:[010]:​y=41​+2m​, z=2n​,x=41​+2m​, z=2n​,​m,n∈Z.​
(3)
Thus the cell contains lines at the quarter and three-quarter coordinates, in both the z=0 and z=1/2 levels.
For completeness, translated diagonal twofold operations also produce diagonal 21​ screw lines. The operations (x,y,z)↦(y+1,x,−z) and (x,y,z)↦(1−y,−x,−z) preserve the same basis. Their axial translations are (1/2,1/2,0) and (1/2,−1/2,0), half the primitive repeats along the corresponding diagonal directions. Their lines are
[110]:[11ˉ0]:​x−y=21​+m, z=2n​,x+y=21​+m, z=2n​.​​
(4)
Diagonal lines with integer x∓y instead support pure twofold rotations. Listing only rotational parts modulo primitive translations would hide these shifted screw lines. There is no fractional screw advance along z: every Fe site has integer z, so a symmetry taking the Fe layer into itself requires an integer vertical translation. Proper fourfold axes parallel to z are therefore not 41​, 42​, or 43​ screws in this structure. All these statements can be checked directly on the four supplied coordinates without choosing an alternative tabulated origin.[ref]

Choose orthorhombic basal axes along the tetragonal diagonals, A=a+b and B=a−b, and allow their lengths to differ slightly. Two orthorhombic twins interchange A and B. This corresponds to a small shear of the original tetragonal basal cell, or to unequal nearest-neighbor Fe–Fe distances along its two diagonal directions; it is not simply stretching the original tetragonal x and y axes unequally.
The tetragonal indices map to H=h+k, K=h−k. Thus tetragonal 110 becomes orthorhombic 200 in one twin and 020 in the rotated twin. Their spacings are A/2 and B/2, producing the two peaks. Equal populations of the symmetry-related twins explain their equal intensities.
In contrast, tetragonal 200 becomes orthorhombic 220. Its inverse spacing squared is 4/A2+4/B2, invariant under exchanging A and B. It therefore does not split between those twins, exactly as observed. The diffraction facts determine the distortion's orientation and twin equivalence, but not its numerical strain amplitude without the measured peak separation.

A neutron can exchange both energy and crystal momentum with a lattice vibration. Determine its incoming and outgoing wave vectors ki​,kf​ from neutron wavelengths and directions. Its nonrelativistic kinetic energy is En​=ℏ2kn2​/(2mn​), so the measured transfers are
Q=ki​−kf​,ℏω=2mn​ℏ2​(ki2​−kf2​).​
(11)
For phonon creation, momentum conservation reads Q=G+q and the neutron loses energy ℏων​(q). For phonon annihilation the corresponding phonon energy is gained by the neutron. Reduce Q by a reciprocal lattice vector G to obtain the phonon wave vector, and trace a branch by repeating this measurement at several reduced wave vectors.
A triple-axis spectrometer provides a suitable arrangement: a monochromator selects the incident wavelength, the oriented sample defines the momentum transfer through the scattering angles, and an analyzer crystal selects the final wavelength before detection. A time-of-flight spectrometer instead uses pulse timing and flight paths to obtain the energy change. In either case instrumental energy and angular resolution must be separated from intrinsic line widths. Nuclear scattering from atomic displacements couples neutrons to phonons; the one-phonon intensity contains a polarization factor involving Q⋅eν​, atomic scattering lengths, and thermal occupation factors. Intensities can therefore differ even at equivalent phonon frequencies.

This follows because the extra translation (1/2,1/2,1/2) must have phase one for a reciprocal lattice vector. In the k=0 section the condition is h+l even. The reciprocal steps are
a∗=0.3532π​=17.799 nm−1,c∗=1.052π​=5.984 nm−1.​

There are three acoustic branches, not two. For propagation parallel to the tetragonal c axis, one branch is a longitudinal wave, polarized parallel to c. The other branches are transverse waves whose polarizations lie along the equivalent basal directions. The fourfold symmetry about c makes those transverse modes degenerate along that axis.
Consequently at a generic nonzero wave vector along c there are only two distinct acoustic frequencies: one longitudinal frequency and one doubly degenerate transverse frequency. At the zone center all acoustic frequencies go to zero as required by uniform translation invariance. The degeneracy would generally lift if propagation moved away from the symmetry axis or the basal tetragonal symmetry were broken.

κph,a​≃31​CV​vg2​τ=0.72 Wm−1K−1.​
(17)

The calculation is not a complete thermal-transport average. Hund's rules maximizes total spin within the partially filled shell: parallel spins require an antisymmetric spatial wavefunction, reducing Coulomb overlap through exchange. Among terms with that spin, the second rule selects the largest total orbital angular momentum. The third rule, from spin-orbit coupling within the selected term, gives J=∣L−S∣ below half filling and J=L+S above half filling, with the half-filled case treated directly.
For seven 4f electrons, put one parallel-spin electron in each ml​=−3,−2,−1,0,1,2,3 orbital. Their orbital projections sum to zero and form the half-filled L=0 shell; all seven spins add. Thus
S=27​,L=0,J=27​,2S+1LJ​=8S7/2​.​
(18)
There is no ambiguity from applying the below- or above-half-filled formula because L=0. The absence of a first-order orbital moment is important for the weak magnetic anisotropy discussed in c. These are isolated-ion term rules; a crystal field or strong covalency can alter other ions' orbital behavior, but the half-filled Eu2+ shell is unusually robust.

Eu2+ has the half-filled 4f7 term with L=0. First-order orbital magnetism is therefore quenched already at the ion level, making the dominant moment approximately spin-only. Spin-orbit-induced magnetocrystalline anisotropy arises only through weaker admixtures and other small interactions; magnetic dipole interactions can also contribute. This explains why a cubic anisotropy term is allowed by symmetry but numerically weak.
An appropriate insulating local-moment model is a nearly isotropic Heisenberg model,
H=−⟨i,j⟩∑​Jij​Si​⋅Sj​+Hweak anisotropy​+Hdipolar​,S=7/2,​
(25)
with dominant effective ferromagnetic couplings in the sign convention Jij​>0. The Eu sublattice in rocksalt EuO is face-centered cubic; nearest- and further-neighbor effective exchanges can be retained as needed. Because the solid is not metallic, an itinerant-electron Stoner model or a generic free-carrier RKKY interaction mechanism is not the natural starting point. Exchange can still arise through virtual hopping and ligand-mediated processes in an insulator; it does not require mobile dc carriers.

The microscopic electric interaction is spin independent to leading order, but the Pauli exclusion principle ties the spatial symmetry of an electronic state to its spin symmetry. Different spin combinations therefore have different Coulomb and kinetic energies. Projecting those energies onto localized low-energy moments often gives an approximately rotationally invariant bilinear exchange Hamiltonian operator −JS1​⋅S2​. For spin one half, the spin singlet state and triplet state energies are distinguished by S1​⋅S2​=−3/4 and 1/4, so any isotropic two-level spin singlet state–triplet state splitting can be written in this form plus a constant. More general spins or orbital degeneracy can require extra interactions.
An antiferromagnetic oxide commonly has superexchange through a ligand such as oxygen. Virtual hopping between half-filled metal orbitals lowers the energy of antiparallel spins more strongly because the corresponding intermediate doubly occupied configurations are allowed by the Pauli exclusion principle. The simple one-orbital Hubbard model limit gives +4t2S1​⋅S2​/U, equivalent to negative J in the question's minus-sign convention. Bond angles and orbital occupancy can change the sign in more complicated oxides, so superexchange is not universally antiferromagnetic irrespective of geometry.

In both collinear trial states, a spin and its second neighbor are parallel. The next-nearest-neighbor energy is therefore −J2​S2 per spin in either case. Making J2​ negative makes that contribution positive and raises both energies.
A negative J2​ prefers opposite second-neighbor spins. Neither all-parallel order nor the simple alternating nearest-neighbor pattern satisfies that preference: in the latter, alternating twice returns to the original direction. This competition is a source of frustration when the nearest-neighbor interaction favors one of those collinear patterns, and allows a noncollinear twist to lower the energy. Merely calling J2​ antiferromagnetic does not mean that the nearest-neighbor alternating state satisfies it.

A parabolic isotropic band has group velocity v=ℏk/m∗ and semiclassical force ℏk˙=−e(E+v×B) for electrons with charge −e, where e>0. In the relaxation-time approximation the nonequilibrium drift velocity loses momentum at rate 1/τ. Combining these gives
m∗(v˙+v/τ)=−eE−ev×B.​
(31)
The relaxation term represents the average effect of scattering rather than a force on every carrier between collisions. Linear response, an approximately energy-independent m∗ and τ near the Fermi surface, and wavelengths long enough for a local bulk response are implicit. The equilibrium random velocities average to zero and do not themselves contribute a drift current.

At zero magnetic field the two circular conductivities coincide, so an isotropic material has no magnetically induced circular birefringence. In the collisionless Drude model range the dielectric function is ϵ=ϵ∞​−ωp2​/ω2. In a low-carrier-density semiconductor use a thin, cold sample in a transmission spectrometer. A Frenkel exciton is a tightly bound electron–hole excitation localized on approximately one molecular or ionic unit. Its radius is comparable to the lattice spacing, its exciton binding energy can be large, and transport is described through transfer of the local excitation between units. Molecular solids such as crystalline anthracene provide a natural example: the optical excitation is largely associated with an individual molecule, so a broad parabolic-band hydrogen model is not the most useful starting point.
A Wannier-Mott exciton extends over many unit cells. It is described approximately by a conduction electron and a valence hole with effective masses and a dielectric-screened Coulomb attraction. Its binding is usually smaller, and its levels resemble a hydrogenic series. gallium arsenide is a suitable example because its direct-gap band-edge carriers have light effective masses and substantial dielectric dielectric screening, supporting spatially extended excitons. Localization length and dielectric screening, rather than whether a material happens to be called a semiconductor, distinguish the two limiting pictures. Real materials can lie between the limits, so neither family is a universal sharp classification.
