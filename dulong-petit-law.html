Dulong-Petit law

 ... Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Heat Heat capacity Heat capacity at constant volume
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 1
In the classical high-temperature limit, each independent harmonic oscillator contributes kB​ to the heat capacity at constant volume, where kB​ is the Boltzmann constant. A three-dimensional crystalline solid with N chemical atoms has 3N vibrational normal modes in the bulk harmonic approximation. Its vibrational internal energy and heat capacity at constant volume therefore obey
Uvib​=U0​+3NkB​T,CV​=(∂T∂Uvib​​)V,N​=3NkB​.
(56)
Here U0​ is independent of temperature at fixed volume. Thus the Dulong-Petit law predicts CV​≃3NkB​ for a real solid in this regime. Per mole of chemical atoms, rather than per mole of a compound's formula units,
CV,mol​⟶3R≃24.9434Jmol−1K−1,R=NA​kB​,
(57)
with molar gas constant R and Avogadro constant NA​. A formula unit containing r chemical atoms consequently has a classical vibrational prediction of 3rR per mole of formula units.
For example, in this ideal classical model, one mole of chemical atoms heated at fixed volume from 300 to 600K gains vibrational internal energy
ΔUvib​=3R(600−300)K≃7483.02J.
(58)
The heat capacity at constant volume stays the same even though the thermal part of the internal energy doubles. These temperatures illustrate the stipulated classical model; they do not assert that every real solid is classical at 300K.
The proof outline is the equipartition theorem: one classical harmonic oscillator has a quadratic kinetic energy and a quadratic potential energy, each with mean kB​T/2. Their sum is kB​T, whose derivative with respect to temperature is kB​. Summing over 3N normal modes gives 3NkB​. The classical result does not require equal angular frequencies: each harmonic normal mode contributes the same amount once it is classical. Overall rigid motions give only a finite-size correction to the bulk count. This independence from the individual angular frequencies makes the Dulong-Petit law useful as a quick estimate of a solid's heating requirement.
Experiments on freely expanding solids often measure the heat capacity at constant pressure CP​, whereas the harmonic prediction is for the heat capacity at constant volume CV​. In a smooth single thermodynamic phase of a simple compressible material, the difference between constant-pressure and constant-volume heat capacities is
CP​−CV​=κT​TVα2​,
(59)
where α=V−1(∂V/∂T)P​ is the volumetric coefficient of thermal expansion and κT​=−V−1(∂V/∂P)T​ is the isothermal compressibility. Using measured CP​ as an approximation to CV​ requires this correction to be small compared with CV​. [ref]
High temperature here means kB​T≫ℏω for the relevant vibrational normal modes, with reduced Planck constant ℏ and mode angular frequency ω. At low temperature, excitation across a quantum energy spacing is suppressed: modes with ℏω≫kB​T freeze out thermally, and the classical equipartition theorem fails. The Einstein model of a solid makes this loss of vibrational heat capacity explicit. The Dulong-Petit law is therefore an ideal high-temperature vibrational limit, not a statement that all solids have the same heat capacity at every temperature. Other contributions and departures from the harmonic approximation can matter even at high temperature. [ref]
Historically, Petit and Dulong's 1819 experiments compared the specific heat capacities of several elemental solids and found an approximately constant product with their relative atomic weights. Their original empirical regularity came before the classical harmonic explanation; it suggested that counting chemical atoms reveals a simpler pattern than comparing equal masses. [ref]
