Cartesian basis under rotation ([p]U​=QTp)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Cartesian basis
OurBigBook.com Words: 197
Rotate the standard basis through 30∘ using the rotation matrix whose columns are the new basis vectors:
u=(3​/21/2​),v=(−1/23​/2​),Q=[u v]=(3​/21/2​−1/23​/2​),U=(u,v).
(2)
Here u⋅v=0 and u⋅u=v⋅v=1, so Q is an orthogonal matrix with QTQ=I, where T denotes transpose and I is the identity matrix. For the same vector p=(2,1)T, its coordinate vector in this Cartesian basis is
[p]U​=QTp=(p⋅up⋅v​)=(3​+1/2−1+3​/2​)=(αβ​).
(3)
Thus α≈2.232 and β≈−0.134: travel forwards along u and a little backwards along v to reconstruct p=αu+βv. The orthogonal projections onto the two axes give these signed coordinates because the measuring vectors are both perpendicular and of unit length.
The squared norm remains
α2+β2=(3​+1/2)2+(−1+3​/2)2=(13/4+3​)+(7/4−3​)=5=22+12.
(4)
This is useful when choosing axes aligned with a geometric feature: the coordinates change but distances and dot products retain the same sum-of-products formula. The two directions of the change of basis must be kept straight: Q reconstructs the vector in old coordinates, p=Q[p]U​, and QT extracts its new coordinates. Applying Q to the old coordinate vector p instead would actively rotate the vector.
A short proof explains both formulas. If p=Qc, multiplication by QT gives c=QTp. Then ∥p∥2=cTQTQc=cTc. Perpendicular unit-length columns make the matrix inverse equal the transpose; this is the computational convenience supplied by a Cartesian basis.
