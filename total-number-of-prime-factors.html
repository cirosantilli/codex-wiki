Total number of prime factors (Ω(n))

The total number of prime factors Ω(n) counts every occurrence of a prime factor in the prime factorization of a positive integer n. Equivalently, it adds the prime valuations:
n=p1a1​​⋯prar​​⟹Ω(n)=a1​+⋯+ar​=∑p∣n​vp​(n).
(7)
One may also write Ω(n)=∑p​vp​(n) over all prime numbers, since only finitely many prime valuations are nonzero. The empty sum gives Ω(1)=0. By contrast, the prime omega function has ω(n)=r and counts distinct prime factors. [ref]
Expanding 72=23⋅32 into 2⋅2⋅2⋅3⋅3 shows Ω(72)=3+2=5, whereas ω(72)=2 and rad(72)=6. The same calculation for the comparison examples gives

(8)
The total number of prime factors agrees for 72 and 32, although their prime supports of an integer differ. Meanwhile 72 and 36 have the same radical of an integer and prime omega function, but different values of the total number of prime factors. These examples show exactly which information each arithmetic function forgets.
The total number of prime factors is a completely additive arithmetic function:
Ω(ab)=Ω(a)+Ω(b)(a,b≥1).
(9)
The proof is that multiplication adds each prime valuation, vp​(ab)=vp​(a)+vp​(b), and summing preserves that equality. No coprimality condition is needed. For example, 12=22⋅3 and 18=2⋅32 both have total number of prime factors three; their product 216=23⋅33 has six. By comparison, the prime omega function is only an additive arithmetic function: here ω(216)=2=4=ω(12)+ω(18).
There is a useful way to see the total number of prime factors as a count of elementary building blocks: any expression of n as a product of prime numbers has exactly Ω(n) factors, by the fundamental theorem of arithmetic. Equivalently, every factorization tree for n>1 has Ω(n) leaves. Repeated prime factors increase this count without introducing new kinds of prime number. The difference is the existing excess prime-factor multiplicity:
Ω(n)−ω(n)=∑p∣n​(vp​(n)−1)≥0.
(10)
Every summand is nonnegative, so Ω(n)=ω(n) if and only if n is a squarefree integer, including n=1. In particular, a prime power pk with k≥1 has Ω(pk)=k, ω(pk)=1 and rad(pk)=p.
Historically, Hardy and Ramanujan's 1917 paper explicitly separated the prime omega function from the total number of prime factors, writing them as f(n) and F(n), respectively. Their example 23⋅32⋅5 has counts three and six. That distinction let them compare two measures of how an integer is built from prime factors. [ref]
