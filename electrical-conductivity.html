Electrical conductivity (σ)

 ... Electromagnetism Electric charge Electric current Electrical resistance and conductance Electrical resistance Ohm's law
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 34
Electrical conductivity σ measures the local response of current density to electric field. In the rest frame of an isotropic Ohmic conductor, the local form of Ohm's law is
J=σE,[σ]=V/mA/m2​=(Ωm)−1.
(32)
Larger positive electrical conductivity gives larger current density at the same electric field. It is a bulk material coefficient, distinct from the geometry-dependent electrical conductance of a whole component and from a sheet's surface conductivity. Electrical resistivity is ρel​=1/σ in this scalar model. [ref].
The local and whole-wire forms fit together particularly neatly. For uniform current in a straight homogeneous wire, with length L, wire cross-sectional area A, constant electrical conductivity and terminal electric potential difference V, the axial electric field has magnitude E=V/L. Thus
I=jA=σEA=LσA​V,R=σAL​.
(33)
For the defined electrical resistance example σ=5×105(Ωm)−1, L=1m, A=10−6m2 and V=5V, the local values are E=5V/m and j=2.5×106A/m2; integrating gives I=2.5A. One material law therefore supplies both a spatial description and a terminal relation.
The scalar form requires isotropy. In an anisotropic material the response can instead be a tensor, Ji​=∑j​σij​Ej​, and current density need not be parallel to electric field; uniaxial conductivity tensor is one existing example. These statements concern the material's rest frame. A moving electrical conductor instead uses the moving-conductor Ohm law.
