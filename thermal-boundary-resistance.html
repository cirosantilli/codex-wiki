Thermal boundary resistance (rB​=ΔT/q)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Temperature Cryogenics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 3
Thermal boundary resistance describes a temperature drop between the two sides of a material interface carrying heat flux. Efficient thermal conduction within each material does not guarantee efficient heat transfer between them. The solid–helium case is Kapitza resistance; the broader concept also includes solid–solid interfaces. [ref]
Let Ts​ and Tl​ be the temperatures extrapolated to the interface from its solid and liquid sides, each treated as internally in thermal equilibrium. For uniform normal heat flux q>0 through area A, define the finite-drop, area-specific thermal resistance and the resistance of the whole interface by
ΔT=Ts​−Tl​,Q˙​=Aq,rB​=qΔT​[Km2W−1],RB​=Q˙​ΔT​=ArB​​[KW−1].
(32)
The macroscopic jump represents an unresolved interfacial region; it is not a claim that one point has two equilibrium temperatures. At nonzero heat flux, the two sides are outside mutual thermal equilibrium. A measured difference between distant thermometers also contains bulk temperature gradients, which must be measured or bounded before assigning all of it to the interface.
An energy balance explains why a temperature jump can coexist with continuous heat flux. For a stationary interfacial region with no local work or heat source,
dtdUint​​=Aqin​−Aqout​.
(33)
The first law of thermodynamics therefore requires qin​=qout​=q in a steady state. It does not require Ts​=Tl​. For a heated sample with electrical power P=VI, an independently determined outward parasitic loss Pleak​ and no other inputs,
dtdUs​​=P−Pleak​−Aq,q=AP−Pleak​​in a steady state.
(34)
Here V is the heater's electric potential difference and I its electric current. Subtracting a lead loss means subtracting power before dividing by area; q is not the total heating power. Unknown losses and stored internal energy prevent that simple steady inference.
For a one-dimensional comparison, put an interface between two slabs with the same area A, positive constant thermal conductivities ks​,kl​ and lengths Ls​,Ll​. Neglect side losses. Integrating Fourier's law across each slab gives
TH​−Ts​=qks​Ls​​,Tl​−TC​=qkl​Ll​​,TH​−TC​=q(ks​Ls​​+kl​Ll​​)+(Ts​−Tl​).
(35)
Thus, for a linear interface with constant rB​, the series result is
q=Ls​/ks​+rB​+Ll​/kl​TH​−TC​​,Rtotal​=A1​(ks​Ls​​+rB​+kl​Ll​​).
(36)
These slab formulas are a chosen thermal conduction model, not a bulk transport law for moving liquid helium. If the interface law is nonlinear, substitute its own relation between q and Ts​−Tl​ into the preceding balance; a single constant resistance cannot describe every load. Increasing bulk thermal conductivity has diminishing benefit when the interfacial drop dominates. Increasing usable contact area reduces the whole-interface thermal resistance if the local interface law and the other conditions remain fixed.
