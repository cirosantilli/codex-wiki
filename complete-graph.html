Complete graph (Kn​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Graph theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 12
A complete graph is a simple graph in which every two distinct vertices are joined by an edge. The finite complete graph on n vertices is denoted Kn​. Here the edges are undirected, and self-loops are excluded. The usual pictures below use n≥1: K1​ is a single vertex, K2​ a single edge, and K3​ a triangle in a graph.[ref]
Completeness describes all the possible pairs, rather than a drawing style. Different layouts can represent the same graph through a graph isomorphism. Crossing lines away from the drawn vertices do not add new vertices or edges.
There are (2n​)=n(n−1)/2 unordered pairs of distinct vertices, so this is the edge count of a complete graph. Each vertex has vertex degree n−1. The degree-sum formula gives the same count: 2∣E∣=n(n−1). These formulas make a complete graph the upper extreme for edge counts among simple graphs of a fixed size.
This is useful when modelling pairwise relationships. If every pair of objects needs its own direct connection, ten vertices require 45 edges, while twenty require 190. Adding one more vertex to Kn​ introduces n further edges. This counts a specified direct-link model; it does not require every communication system to implement every relationship with a separate physical connection.
A proper vertex coloring of Kn​ must give every vertex a different color, so its chromatic number is n. For n≥1, its independence number is 1: two distinct vertices already have an edge between them. Thus complete graphs provide immediate test cases for graph coloring and independent sets.
