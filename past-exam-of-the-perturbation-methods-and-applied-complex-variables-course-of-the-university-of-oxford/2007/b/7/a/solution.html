The integral construction requires differentiation under the integral and vanishing boundary terms. With those assumptions, integration by parts gives
w′′+zw=[g(ζ)ezζ]∂Γ​+∫Γ​(ζ2g−g′)ezζdζ.
(33)
The standard construction therefore sets g′=ζ2g, giving g=Ceζ3/3. Convergent contours run between rays where ℜζ3<0: convenient central rays have arguments −π/3, π/3 and π. Two choices are the oriented paths
Γ1​:∞e−iπ/3⟶0⟶∞eiπ/3,Γ2​:∞eiπ/3⟶0⟶−∞.
(34)
The cubic exponential dominates the linear one at both ends. To verify independence directly, put A=∫0∞​e−r3/3dr>0 and B=∫0∞​re−r3/3dr>0. At z=0 the value and derivative of the integral along rays θ1​,θ2​ are A(eiθ2​−eiθ1​) and B(e2iθ2​−e2iθ1​). For the two contours above their derivative-to-value ratios are respectively B/A and (B/A)(eiπ/3−1), which differ. Hence their Wronskian is nonzero.
For the second equation multiply by z before integrating by parts:
z(w′′+w)+w′=[(1+ζ2)gezζ]∂Γ​−∫Γ​((1+ζ2)g′+ζg)ezζdζ.
(35)
Setting the residual weight to zero gives g=C(1+ζ2)−1/2. One contour is the segment from −i to i, with the square root positive at ζ=0. Its integral is a nonzero multiple of J0​(z): substituting ζ=it gives i∫−11​eizt(1−t2)−1/2dt, an entire function nonzero at zero.
A second contour starts at i and follows ζ=i−t, 0≤t<∞, choosing a continuous branch along the path. For ℜz>0 it converges at infinity; at the branch endpoint its integrable singularity is t−1/2. In both cases the boundary term contains 1+ζ2​ and vanishes at branch endpoints. The second integral has a nonzero logarithmic divergence as z→0 through positive real values, because its large-t integrand is a nonzero multiple of e−zt/t. It is therefore independent of the entire first solution; analytic continuation supplies solutions elsewhere away from the equation's singular point.
Strictly, nonzero w and an arbitrary contour alone do not prove the printed necessity for g. Necessity also needs injectivity of the chosen Laplace representation, while the vanishing-integrand argument above is the usual sufficient construction. For a concrete counterexample to unconditional necessity in the Bessel case, take a positively oriented circle ∣ζ∣=R>1, choose the branch (1+ζ2)−1/2∼1/ζ outside the cut [−i,i], and add one to that weight. The added entire integrand integrates to zero. Expanding the original weight on this circle gives integral 2πiJ0​(z), which is nonzero and solves the equation, although the modified weight is not a constant multiple of the printed one. The standard weights and contours above still give the requested independent solutions.
