For x>0, the real and imaginary parts of the exponent divided by x are
Re(it3)=v3−3u2v=−r3sin3θ,Im(it3)=u3−3uv2=r3cos3θ.

t=(1+is)1/3,s∈R,
(2)

Deform the finite interval into the ray t=eiπ/6r, 0≤r<∞, followed by the reverse of the descent branch from one. The integrand is entire, so Cauchy's integral theorem permits the deformation.

Set x=δX and write y(x)=Y(X). The equation becomes
δ2ϵ​Y′′+δα−1XαY′+Y=0.

A dominant balance must compare all three coefficients, rather than just the two derivative terms.
For −1<α<1, balancing diffusion and drift gives δ=ϵ1/(α+1). Their common coefficient tends to infinity, so the reaction term is smaller and the leading inner equation is
Y′′+XαY′=0,Y′=Cexp[−Xα+1/(α+1)].
(11)
This derivative is integrable at infinity, allowing an ordinary localized endpoint adjustment. Thus the thickness is ϵ1/(α+1) in this range.
For α=1, δ=ϵ​ makes all three terms comparable. Keep all of them:
Y′′+XY′+Y=0.
(12)
One decaying solution is e−X2/2, and reduction of order gives the other. This is a square-root-thickness layer with all terms retained.
For α>1, the first variation scale is δ=ϵ​. Keep diffusion and reaction, and drop drift at this scale:
Y′′+Y=0.
(13)
This leading motion is oscillatory, not a monotone decaying boundary-layer profile. To describe localization one must also resolve the slower damping envelope. Removing the first derivative in the original equation by
y=exp[−2ϵ(α+1)xα+1​]v
(14)
gives
v′′+[ϵ1​−2ϵαxα−1​−4ϵ2x2α​]v=0.
(15)
Near the endpoint the wavelength is O(ϵ​), while the envelope changes on x=O(ϵ1/(α+1)). Hence an oscillatory endpoint region is possible, but it has distinct wavelength and envelope scales; calling the diffusion-drift balance alone its leading equation would incorrectly discard the reaction term. This generalizes the oscillatory endpoint with a quadratically vanishing drift.
For α≤−1, the diffusion-drift balance would require δα+1=ϵ, which gives no shrinking positive power scale. At every shrinking scale the singular drift dominates diffusion, so no regular finite-amplitude diffusion boundary layer of the preceding kind occurs. The coefficient itself is singular at zero; singular endpoint solutions are a different issue from a bounded boundary-layer correction.

ii OurBigBook.com
Put p=y′. The dynamical system is
y′=p,p′=ϵp−ey​.
(16)
The nullclines are p=0 and p=ey. There are no equilibria, since they never intersect. To the right in the (y,p) plane means p>0, and to the left means p<0. The arrows point upwards above p=ey and downwards below it.
Near the repelling critical manifold p=ey, an orbit can move on the slow x=O(1) scale, with y′≃ey. More precisely the slow trajectory has p=ey+ϵe2y+⋯. Away from this narrow region, p changes rapidly on x=O(ϵ); an O(1) change of y in a fast layer has p=O(ϵ−1).
For example, take a fixed A<−log2. A trajectory starting at y=A can shadow the slow curve, then depart upwards so that y rapidly increases to zero at the right endpoint. This gives a terminal outflow boundary layer at x=1, of width O(ϵ). For −log2<A<0, the outer trajectory reaches a positive value near one, and the terminal adjustment is downwards: the orbit turns below the nullcline, crosses p=0, and returns to y=0. The boundary layer is still at x=1. These directions follow from the phase portrait and agree with the matched solution below.

Write the complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow as w=ϕ+iψ, and the complex velocity as q=w′=u−iv. The lower solid wall has ψ=0. The upper solid wall and its continuing free streamline have constant ψ equal to the far-field volume flux per unit depth, namely a. Consequently the potential domain is the infinite strip
0<Imw<a.​
(32)
The points A,E correspond to ϕ→−∞, B,C to ϕ→+∞, and D is w=ia by the chosen potential normalization.
In the hodograph plane, the horizontal wall has q real positive, the vertical wall has q imaginary positive because the physical velocity is downward, and the fluid free boundary has ∣q∣=1. The upstream reservoir velocity tends to zero, the outgoing velocity is one, and the velocity at D is i. Thus the hodograph is the first-quadrant unit quarter disk, with A,E at q=0, B,C at q=1, and D at q=i.

Orient the contour so that the plus side is on the left; for a positively oriented simple closed contour this is its interior. If ζ(s) is a smooth arclength parametrization with ζ(0)=t, define the Cauchy principal value by deleting a symmetric interval ∣s∣<η and taking η↓0. This pairs the two singular sides at equal local arclength.
Near t, write f(ζ)=f(t)+[f(ζ)−f(t)]. The second term divided by ζ−t is regular because f is analytic there. For the constant term, indent the contour on either side of t by a small semicircle. Its limiting contribution to the Cauchy integral is ±f(t)/2, while the remaining contour tends to its principal value. This proves the Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem:
w±​(t)=±21​f(t)+2πi1​PV∫Γ​ζ−tf(ζ)​dζ.
(42)
In particular w+​−w−​=f and w+​+w−​=g for the given integral equation.
Define W=w inside and W=−w outside. Its jump is W+​−W−​=w+​+w−​=g, and it vanishes at infinity. Therefore
W(z)=2πi1​∫Γ​t−zg(t)​dt.
(43)
Indeed, the difference between the two sides has no jump, extends analytically across the smooth contour, and vanishes at infinity; Liouville's theorem makes it zero. Applying the Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem again,
f=W+​+W−​=πi1​PV∫Γ​t−zg(t)​dt​.
(44)
Thus the normalized closed-contour singular integral operator is its own inverse.

Extend f to be zero for x<0. Define h(x)=0 for x>0 and
h(x)=∫0∞​K(x−t)f(t)dt(x<0).
(48)
Then the full-line equation is K∗f=f+h. Use the Fourier transform convention
fˉ​(k)=∫0∞​eikxf(x)dx,hˉ(k)=∫−∞0​eikxh(x)dx.
(49)
Under the corresponding growth bounds, the positive-support transform is analytic above a horizontal line and the negative-support transform below a horizontal line. If both functions have suitable subexponential growth, the basic domains are respectively the upper and lower half-planes; exponential decay can enlarge these to overlapping strips. The convolution theorem gives (Kˉ−1)fˉ​=hˉ.
The Wiener-Hopf method factors Kˉ−1 into functions analytic and nonzero in the respective domains. After multiplicative or additive separation, the two sides continue to one entire function, whose growth determines a polynomial by Liouville's theorem. Real-axis zeros must be assigned consistently with the inversion contour, rather than divided out without specifying a side.
For K=e−∣x∣, direct integration gives Kˉ=2/(1+k2), so
1+k21−k2​fˉ​=hˉ.
(50)

k+ik2−1​fˉ​=−(k−i)hˉ.
(51)

fˉ​=Ck2−1k+i​,hˉ=−k−iC​.
