Then the full-line equation is K∗f=f+h. Use the Fourier transform convention
fˉ​(k)=∫0∞​eikxf(x)dx,hˉ(k)=∫−∞0​eikxh(x)dx.

Under the corresponding growth bounds, the positive-support transform is analytic above a horizontal line and the negative-support transform below a horizontal line. If both functions have suitable subexponential growth, the basic domains are respectively the upper and lower half-planes; exponential decay can enlarge these to overlapping strips. The convolution theorem gives (Kˉ−1)fˉ​=hˉ.
The Wiener-Hopf method factors Kˉ−1 into functions analytic and nonzero in the respective domains. After multiplicative or additive separation, the two sides continue to one entire function, whose growth determines a polynomial by Liouville's theorem. Real-axis zeros must be assigned consistently with the inversion contour, rather than divided out without specifying a side.

This is the transform, on Imk>0, of
f(x)=A(cosx+sinx),x>0.​
(54)
Equivalently, Fourier inversion closed below the inversion line for x>0 picks up the two real poles with clockwise orientation.
As a direct check, let G(x)=∫0∞​e−∣x−t∣f(t)dt. Since (d2/dx2)e−∣x∣=e−∣x∣−2δ0​, the equation G=f gives f′′=−f. At zero, differentiation of the integral gives G′(0)=G(0), so f′(0)=f(0); this sets the sine and cosine coefficients equal. Direct substitution, or the transforms above, then verifies the full integral equation, not merely its differentiated form.
