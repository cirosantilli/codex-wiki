By June 2022, important principles of reading acquisition were established, including the need to learn written word forms and to understand the language they express. The major unanswered questions concerned how these skills become efficient and integrated, why children respond differently to experience and instruction, and how experimental gains become sustained comprehension and participation. Useful future research should target those uncertainties with explicit competing predictions rather than reopen every instructional question as if no evidence existed.
One question concerns what is learned from a successful encounter with a new written word. Does the child acquire an item-specific spelling, a reusable mapping, a contextual guess, or some combination? David L. Share's delayed learning experiments motivate the distinction, while artificial orthography work shows that strong item learning and generalisation need not move together. Future studies should therefore distinguish recognition of practised items from transfer to novel combinations and later retention, rather than use one immediate naming test as a complete index of learning.[ref] [ref]
A specific experiment could randomise unfamiliar but pronounceable items to matched exposure schedules varying the consistency of a spelling–sound relation. Hold the number of encounters, visual complexity and oral vocabulary knowledge constant. Assess trained spellings against homophonic alternatives after a delay, and test untrained recombinations that require the relevant mapping. Eye tracking during text reading could establish where the new form attracts attention, while comprehension questions verify meaningful engagement. The experiment should specify whether its prediction concerns initial learning, retention or generalisation.
A second question is how the distribution of teaching examples interacts with the learner's existing knowledge. Keith S. Apfelbaum and colleagues found that varied irrelevant consonants helped first graders learn vowel mappings. It remains necessary to identify when variation helps, whether its effects depend on prior decoding and how long the advantage lasts. A multisite classroom experiment could independently vary relevant consistency and irrelevant variation while matching total teaching time and opportunities to respond.[ref]
Pretraining measures should be collected before randomisation, and interaction tests powered prospectively. A significant benefit in one subgroup and a nonsignificant effect in another does not demonstrate different treatment effects. Instructional fidelity and actual exposure need observation because a teacher may adapt a nominally identical activity in different ways. Broader spelling, unfamiliar-word reading and later passage comprehension would distinguish a useful instructional principle from a narrow training-task effect.
A third question is how best to coordinate word-level and language-level support. Margaret J. Snowling and colleagues' longitudinal profiles show that dyslexia, DLD and their co-occurrence can lead to comprehension difficulty through different constraints. The unresolved question is not whether either dimension matters, but which combination, timing and intensity of support helps a specified child. Diagnostic categories are too coarse to substitute for a measured profile.[ref]
A factorial teaching trial could retain adequate core reading instruction for everyone and independently randomise additional decoding and oral language components, with contact and resource use measured. Include children across the skill distribution rather than only extreme, mutually exclusive categories. Prespecify primary outcomes for word reading, language and comprehension, with delayed follow-up. A subsequent adaptive stage could randomise a well-defined change for children not responding, instead of attributing improvement after an uncontrolled adjustment to personalisation itself.
The trial would also address a fourth question: what constitutes meaningful transfer? Janice M. Keenan and colleagues showed that comprehension assessments differ in their reliance on component skills. An intervention can look more successful simply because its outcome task resembles training. Future evaluations need independent passages, several assessment formats and checks that the construct retains a comparable meaning across time and groups. Function in the classroom and the child's experience should accompany test scores, without treating either as a substitute for accurate measurement.[ref]
A fifth uncertainty concerns how findings generalise across writing systems, language backgrounds and educational environments. Artificial-script work offers experimental control, but Joanne S. H. Taylor and colleagues' instruction study used adults learning small novel vocabularies. It cannot establish the full trajectory of a multilingual child encountering an unfamiliar language of instruction. Coordinated cross-language studies should define the mappings and language experiences that differ, rather than merely compare national averages.[ref]
Recruitment should include varied socioeconomic circumstances and children whose language development differs from the majority sample. Assessment needs to separate opportunity to learn from persistent difficulty and to examine the validity of the measures in each context. Shared protocols and culturally appropriate materials can coexist. The aim is to test which mechanisms generalise and which depend on script, vocabulary or teaching, not presume that every observed group difference reveals an intrinsic deficit.
Computational models can sharpen all these questions. A model should learn from a specified developmental input, make predictions for held-out items and trajectories, and face comparison with alternatives that use different representations or learning rules. Matching average skilled-reader accuracy is weaker than predicting the effect of an experimental change in input. Parameters should not be allowed to absorb every individual difference without independent constraints; failure should inform revision rather than invite unrestricted refitting.
Finally, the timescale of research must match the educational claim. Short experiments identify candidate processes, longitudinal cohorts reveal ordering and trajectories, and controlled implementation studies assess delivery and lasting benefit. Preregistration, transparent exclusions, appropriate handling of school clustering and missing follow-up, and independent replication make the chain more credible. The central unanswered problem is how to connect the child's particular learning history and profile with an instructionally manipulable process that produces durable, generalisable gains.
