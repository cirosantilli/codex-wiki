Two-path attosecond phase reconstruction (Sq​(τ)=Cq​+Dq​cos(2ωτ−Δϕq​+δq​))

An original two-amplitude model makes the timing inference explicit. Use the spectral phase convention of the coherent harmonic pulse-train model, and define positive delay τ to retard the reference electric field. Let the amplitudes leading to the same final photoelectron state be
M−​(τ)=b−​ei(ϕq−1​+ωτ+η−​),M+​(τ)=b+​ei(ϕq+1​−ωτ+η+​),
(40)
with real b±​≥0. Squaring their sum gives
Sq​(τ)=b−2​+b+2​+2b−​b+​cos(2ωτ−Δϕq​+δq​),Δϕq​=ϕq+1​−ϕq−1​,δq​=η−​−η+​.
(41)
The 2ω oscillation comes from opposite signs of the reference photon's phase. For Δϕq​=2ωt0​ and δq​=0, the signal peaks at τ=t0​ modulo T/2. A fitted phase contains the target contribution δq​; setting it to zero is an assumption, not an observation. In an experiment, unobserved final channels can add as probabilities, so the displayed formula is one effective-channel idealization.
For a chosen example, impose Δϕq​=0.80 radians and δq​=0.15 radians at 800nm. The observed oscillation has argument 2ωτ−0.65. Its peak shift is 0.1380fs, while the corrected finite-difference group delay is Δϕq​/(2ω)=0.1699fs. Ignoring the target contribution would bias the inferred delay by −31.9 attoseconds. These stipulated phases demonstrate a reconstruction error; they are not atomic calculations or measured delays.
Recovering all neighboring phase differences determines the component spectral phases up to an overall phase and the chosen timing origin, provided the oscillation phases are consistently unwrapped. Known component amplitudes then give a temporal field by a finite inverse Fourier transform. This is why measuring the optical spectrum alone is insufficient: it omits the very information that decides when constructive wave interference occurs.
