Use the condensate wavefunction convention ∫∣ψ∣2d3r=N, so n(r)=∣ψ(r)∣2 is the number density and ψ/N​ is a normalized one-particle orbital. The contact interaction strength is U0​=4πℏ2a/m for dilute atoms with s-wave scattering length a. The chemical potential μ=∂E/∂N fixes the stationary time dependence e−iμt/ℏ and enforces normalization when minimizing the energy. The Gross–Pitaevskii equation follows from an energy functional that contains the pair-counting factor 1/2:
E[ψ]=∫[2mℏ2​∣∇ψ∣2+V∣ψ∣2+2U0​​∣ψ∣4]d3r.
(1)
Varying E−μ∫∣ψ∣2d3r gives the stationary Gross–Pitaevskii equation; the interaction contribution to that equation is U0​nψ, without the factor 1/2.
For the broadening discussed here assume repulsive interactions, a>0. The Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate neglects the gradient kinetic energy compared with the trapping and interaction energies. At fixed spatial scale, increasing NU0​ increases the mean-field potential. Expansion then reduces the kinetic energy further: for a cloud radius R, its scale per atom is ℏ2/(mR2), whereas the trap energy scale is mω2R2. Their ratio decreases as (a0​/R)4. In the weak-interaction limit the kinetic energy instead balances confinement, giving the Gaussian wave packet ground state of the quantum harmonic oscillator.
The spherical Thomas–Fermi condensate has
nTF​(r)=U0​μ−21​mω2r2​(0≤r≤R),nTF​(r)=0(r>R),μ=21​mω2R2.
(2)
Integrating this density gives N=8πμR3/(15U0​). Thus
R=a0​(15Na/a0​)1/5,μTF​=2ℏω​(15Na/a0​)2/5.​
(3)
At fixed N, stronger repulsion produces a wider, lower wavefunction: the Gaussian wave packet crosses over towards the square root of an inverted parabola, with an almost flat central region and a steeper outer drop. The density is the inverted parabola; the wavefunction is its square root. The exact wavefunction remains smooth and has a tail, since kinetic energy cannot be neglected near the Thomas–Fermi condensate edge rounding region. The healing length must be small compared with the cloud size in the bulk. Attractive interactions would not follow this large-N expansion argument.
The original plot compares the noninteracting endpoint with two normalized repulsive spherical Thomas–Fermi condensate profiles. The latter are asymptotic shapes, rather than exact intermediate solutions of the Gross–Pitaevskii equation. Each has the same integrated atom number; χ=Na/a0​ labels the illustrative interaction strengths.
Choose A real and positive. The Gaussian integral gives
N=A2π3/2a03​,A=π3/4a03/2​N​​,μ0​=23​ℏω,E0​=23​Nℏω.​
(4)
The three zero-point energies add for each atom. The ideal-gas chemical potential is a one-atom energy; the total energy includes all N atoms.
For weak interactions, the first-order correction in perturbation theory is the energy functional of the contact interaction evaluated on the unperturbed Gaussian wave packet. The integral needed is
∫∣ψ0​∣4d3r=π3a06​N2​(2πa02​​)3/2=(2π)3/2a03​N2​.
(5)
Consequently the Gaussian interaction energy of a trapped condensate is
EI​=2(2π)3/2a03​N2U0​​,ℏωEI​​=2π​a0​N2a​.​
(6)
Here ℏω=ℏ2/(ma02​). These are the mean-field expressions consistent with the supplied equation and ∫∣ψ∣2=N. Exact first-order perturbation theory for precisely N atoms in one orbital replaces N2 by N(N−1) through the number of distinct pairs; this distinction is negligible in the large-N regime of the crossover estimate.
To judge the ideal-gas approximation, compare the interaction energy with the total ideal energy, or compare the interaction energy per atom with ℏω:
E0​EI​​=32π​2​a0​Na​.
(7)
Thus the crossover from Gaussian to Thomas–Fermi condensate is governed by χ=Na/a0​: perturbation theory is reliable for χ≪1, while the regime of the Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate requires χ≫1. The characteristic crossover number is Nc​∼a0​/a; there is no sharp critical atom number here. If one specifically defines crossover by EI​=E0​ using the weak-interaction expression, it gives Nc​=(32π​/2)a0​/a. That prefactor is a convention-dependent estimate extrapolated to the edge of perturbation theory, not a phase transition. Comparing EI​ itself to a single ℏω would incorrectly omit a factor of N.
For the numerical estimate use m≃87×1.66054×10−27kg and ℏ≃1.05457×10−34Js. Then
a0​=m(2π×50s−1)ℏ​​≃1.52μm,Nc​∼aa0​​≃3.0×102 atoms.​
(8)
The particular equality EI​=E0​ would instead give about 1.15×103 atoms. These quantify different choices within a smooth crossover; a well-developed spherical Thomas–Fermi condensate profile requires going substantially beyond the characteristic scale.
