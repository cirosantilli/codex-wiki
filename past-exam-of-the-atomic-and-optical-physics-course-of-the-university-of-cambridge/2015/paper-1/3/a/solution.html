In the ideal point-nucleus Dirac-Coulomb problem, hydrogen levels depend on n and j, so 2S1/2​ and 2P1/2​ are degenerate despite their different orbital angular momenta. The Lamb shift removes this degeneracy: the 2S1/2​ level lies about h×1.06GHz above 2P1/2​, an energy of roughly 4.4μeV. This is distinct from the much larger 2P3/2​−2P1/2​ fine-structure splitting.
quantum electrodynamics changes the electron's interaction with the Coulomb field. electron self-energy and vacuum polarization give state-dependent radiative corrections; a complete prediction also keeps specified recoil and nuclear-size contributions. An S wave samples the nucleus differently from a P wave, so equal n,j no longer ensures equal energy. A leading-log estimate for an nS radiative contribution scales as α(Zα)4me​c2ln[(Zα)−2]/n3, with a numerical prefactor and further state-dependent terms. This is a scale estimate, not a replacement for renormalized bound-state QED. The measurable shift is a difference relative to a specified reference Hamiltonian, rather than an unregulated absolute vacuum energy.[ref]
The original microwave experiment used a tungsten oven to dissociate hydrogen, an electron beam to populate metastable 2S atoms, and electron ejection from a metal target to measure surviving metastable atoms. Microwaves transferred population to rapidly decaying 2P, reducing detector current. At a fixed microwave frequency, interruption of the radiation and a scan of static magnetic field revealed Zeeman-tuned resonances. Comparing resonance locations with the Dirac-level prediction showed the S level shifted upward. Field calibration and homogeneity, excessive microwave intensity, unresolved hyperfine structure and stray-electric-field quenching limit the measurement; the signal is not direct absorption by a large equilibrium excited population.[ref]
