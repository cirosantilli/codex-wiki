In the moving-obstacle test of atomic superfluidity, a blue-detuned focused laser repelled atoms in a sodium condensate and was scanned at different speeds. After a fixed exposure, time-of-flight absorption imaging measured the reduction of condensate fraction as a heating indicator. Low-speed heating was small, with a pronounced onset near 1.6mm/s, below the peak-density sound speed near 6.2mm/s. A stationary-beam control showed no heating; above-transition clouds constrained heating through the noncondensed fraction. Changing scan frequency and amplitude at common obstacle velocity helped separate a flow threshold from a particular trap resonance. Density inhomogeneity, obstacle geometry and vortex nucleation limit identification of the observed onset with a unique homogeneous Landau velocity.[ref]
A second experiment stirred rubidium-87 in a magnetic trap by rotating a small transverse anisotropy generated with a far-detuned attractive laser. After stirring, the cloud expanded for 27ms and absorption imaging along the rotation axis revealed vortex cores. Below a threshold no cores appeared; faster rotation produced one or several. Ramping the stirrer down before release reduced additional excitation, and waiting after removing it tested vortex survival. Beam-centre stability and residual trap anisotropy are important controls; an isolated density dip without the rotation and persistence evidence would be less conclusive. This stirred-condensate vortex experiment is a response to rotation, distinct from merely imaging the appearance of a condensate.[ref]
