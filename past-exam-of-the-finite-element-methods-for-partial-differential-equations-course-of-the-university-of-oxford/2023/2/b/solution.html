Use the Sobolev space with a partial Dirichlet condition
V={v∈H1(Ω):Tv=0 almost everywhere on ΓD​},​
(23)
where T is the Sobolev trace operator. The restricted Sobolev trace operator is continuous into L2(ΓD​); its kernel is consequently a closed vector subspace of H1(Ω). Thus V, with the first-order Sobolev norm, is a Hilbert space. The endpoint convention for ΓD​ and ΓN​ does not affect this definition: finitely many boundary points have zero boundary length. It would be inappropriate to impose pointwise boundary values on arbitrary H1 functions in two dimensions.
For a smooth solution, Green's first identity gives
∫Ω​(−Δu+u)v=∫Ω​(∇u⋅∇v+uv)−∫∂Ω​∂n​uTv.
(24)
The boundary term vanishes because Tv=0 on ΓD​ and the normal derivative is zero on ΓN​. This suggests the weak formulation
u∈V,a(u,v)=ℓ(v)(v∈V),a(p,v)=(∇p,∇v)0​+(p,v)0​,ℓ(v)=(f,v)0​.​
(25)
Here a is exactly the first-order Sobolev inner product. In particular the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
∣a(p,v)∣≤∥p∥1​∥v∥1​,a(v,v)=∥v∥12​,∣ℓ(v)∣≤∥f∥0​∥v∥1​.
(26)
Thus a is a bounded bilinear form and a coercive bilinear form, both with constant one. The Lax-Milgram theorem proves existence and uniqueness, together with
∥u∥1​≤∥f∥0​.​
(27)
The positive reaction term already controls the entire Sobolev norm, so no Poincaré inequality is needed here. The zero Neumann boundary condition is the natural part of the weak formulation; it is not an additional restriction on the test space. For this weak solution, −Δu+u=f holds as a distributional identity, and the zero normal derivative on ΓN​ is understood through the corresponding Green's first identity.
