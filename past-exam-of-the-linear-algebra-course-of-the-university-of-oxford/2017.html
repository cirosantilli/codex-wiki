Part 1(b)(iii) needs a separable polynomial hypothesis over a general field; its intended case, a counterexample to the unrestricted claim, and the corrected general result are distinguished in that solution. This paper uses the characteristic polynomial convention χT​(x)=det(T−xI), which differs by (−1)dimV from the monic convention.

i OurBigBook.com
For a linear transformation T of a finite-dimensional vector space over a field F, its minimal polynomial mT​ is the unique monic polynomial of least degree satisfying mT​(T)=0. There is at least one nonzero polynomial with this property because the powers I,T,T2,… lie in the finite-dimensional vector space of linear maps V→V. Dividing any other such polynomial by mT​ leaves a remainder of smaller degree that also vanishes at T, so that remainder must be zero. This also proves uniqueness.
If C represents T in any basis, the paper's characteristic polynomial is
χT​(x)=det(C−xI).​
(1)
It is independent of the basis, since changing the basis replaces C−xI by a similar matrix, preserving its determinant. Its degree is dimV and its leading coefficient is (−1)dimV; mT​ is always monic.

The Cayley-Hamilton theorem states
χT​(T)=0.​
(2)
Let d=dimV. Extend the field to an algebraically closed field and choose a basis in which the matrix of T is an upper triangular matrix, as permitted. Write its diagonal entries as λ1​,…,λd​ and put Vi​=span(e1​,…,ei​). Each Vi​ is an invariant subspace, and
(T−λi​I)Vi​⊆Vi−1​.
(3)
Apply T−λd​I first, then T−λd−1​I, and continue. The resulting product sends Vd​ into V0​={0}. These factors are commuting operators, so
∏i=1d​(T−λi​I)=0.
(4)
The determinant of an upper triangular matrix gives χT​(x)=(−1)d∏i​(x−λi​). Thus χT​(T)=0 after the field extension. Its entries were in the original field, whose embedding is injective, so they were already zero there. The zero-dimensional case is immediate as well.

The displayed matrix is the transpose convention for a companion matrix. Expanding det(xI−A) along its last row gives
det(xI−A)=xn+αn−1​xn−1+⋯+α1​x+α0​=f(x).
(5)
Indeed, the last entry contributes (x+αn−1​)xn−1 and the entry in column j<n contributes αj−1​xj−1; the cofactor signs cancel those from the superdiagonal entries −1. Consequently
χT​(x)=(−1)nf(x).​
(6)
The Cayley-Hamilton theorem gives f(A)=0, so mT​ divides f. To exclude a smaller minimal polynomial, let e1​,…,en​ be the standard basis. The first n−1 rows of A show
e1T​Aj=ej+1T​(0≤j<n).
(7)
If ∑j=0n−1​cj​Aj=0, multiplying on the left by e1T​ gives ∑j​cj​ej+1T​=0, so every coefficient is zero. No nonzero polynomial of degree below n vanishes at A. Since f and mT​ are monic polynomials,
mT​=f.​
(8)

Write B=D+K, where D has A in each diagonal block and zero elsewhere, while K has I immediately above the diagonal and zero elsewhere. These block matrices satisfy DK=KD: either product has A immediately above the diagonal. Also, Ki has I on its ith superdiagonal and is zero elsewhere.
The binomial theorem for commuting matrices therefore yields
Bj=∑r=0j​(rj​)Dj−rKr.
(9)
Only r=i can contribute to block (k,k+i), provided that block lies in the matrix. Hence
(Bj)k,k+i​={0,(ij​)Aj−i,​j<i,j≥i.​​
(10)
Thus βi,j​=(ij​), interpreted as an element of the field. The formula remains valid in positive field characteristic: some of these coefficients can then vanish. This possibility matters in the next part.

Solution

iii OurBigBook.com
Write f=mT​. Part (ii) identifies the first superdiagonal block exactly:
(f(B))k,k+1​=f′(A),​
(11)
where f′ is the formal derivative. Thus the candidate is g=f′. If the irreducible polynomial f has f′=0, its polynomial degree makes f and f′ coprime. Equivalently, f is a separable polynomial. This includes every irreducible polynomial over a field of characteristic zero, and every irreducible polynomial over a perfect field.
Under this separability hypothesis, Bézout's identity for polynomials gives a(x)f(x)+b(x)f′(x)=1. Evaluating at A proves that C=f′(A) is an invertible matrix. Moreover, f(B) has zero diagonal blocks, because f(A)=0. It is therefore a nilpotent matrix with f(B)N=0. The only product of N−1 strictly upward block steps that reaches block (1,N) uses the first superdiagonal every time, so
(f(B)N−1)1,N​=CN−1=0.
(12)
For N=1, the corresponding assertion is simply f(B)0=I=0. The block upper triangular matrix B has
χU​(x)=det(B−xI)=det(A−xI)N=(−1)nNf(x)N.
(13)
The minimal polynomial divides this characteristic polynomial. Since f is an irreducible polynomial, it must be a power of f; the exact nilpotency index just found forces
mU​=fN,χU​=(−1)nNfNif f is separable.​
(14)
The arbitrary-field qualification is necessary. In the rational function field F=F2​(t), take f(x)=x2−t. This is an irreducible polynomial: a square of a rational function has even order of vanishing at t=0, while t has odd order. Its formal derivative is zero. With N=2,
A=(0t​10​),B=(A0​IA​),B2=tI4​.
(15)
The off-diagonal block in f(B) is zero, and mU​=f, rather than f2. No alternative polynomial g coprime to f could represent that block: g(A)=0 would imply mT​=f∣g. Thus the printed coprimality claim fails as stated over a general field.
There is also a precise general replacement. In characteristic p>0, let q=pr be the largest power for which f(x)=h(xq), so that h′=0; set q=1 in characteristic zero. Irreducibility of f implies that h is an irreducible polynomial, so h and h′ are coprime. The binomial theorem in characteristic p gives Bq=Dq+Kq. Expanding h(Dq+Kq), with the coefficients equivalently supplied by the Hasse derivatives, gives
f(B)=KqH,
(16)
where H commutes with K and is a block upper triangular matrix whose diagonal blocks are h′(Aq). These blocks are invertible by Bézout's identity for polynomials, since h(Aq)=f(A)=0. Thus H is invertible, and
f(B)s=KqsHs=0⟺qs≥N.
(17)
The same characteristic polynomial and divisibility argument now prove the corrected arbitrary-field answer:
mU​(x)=f(x)⌈N/q⌉,χU​(x)=(−1)nNf(x)N.​
(18)
This is the minimal polynomial of a companion block chain; q=1 recovers the intended separable case.

The annihilator of a vector subspace is
U∘={ϕ∈V′:ϕ(u)=0 for every u∈U}.
(27)
Choose a basis e1​,…,er​ of U and extend it to a basis e1​,…,ed​ of V. In the dual basis, precisely fr+1​,…,fd​ span U∘. Therefore
dimU∘=dimV−dimU.​
(28)
The canonical restriction linear transformation
ρ:V′⟶U′,ρ(ϕ)=ϕ∣U​
(29)
has kernel of a linear map U∘. It is surjective: extend any linear functional on U by assigning arbitrary values, for instance zero, to the additional basis vectors. The First isomorphism theorem for vector spaces gives the explicit linear isomorphism
V′/U∘ ⟶U′,ϕ+U∘⟼ϕ∣U​.​
(30)
It is natural because the displayed map itself makes no basis choice; a basis was used only to prove surjectivity. In particular, U′ is canonically a quotient vector space of V′, rather than canonically a subspace of V′.

If ϕ=T′ψ and v∈kerT, then ϕ(v)=ψ(Tv)=0. Thus imT′⊆(kerT)∘, using the annihilator of a vector subspace.
Conversely, let ϕ vanish on the kernel of a linear map kerT. Define a linear functional on imT by ψ0​(Tv)=ϕ(v). This is well-defined: Tv=Tw implies v−w∈kerT, so ϕ(v)=ϕ(w). Extend ψ0​ to a linear functional ψ on W, using the extension argument from part (i). Then T′ψ=ϕ, proving
imT′=(kerT)∘.​
(31)
The dimension formula from part (i) and the rank-nullity theorem now give
rankT′=dim(kerT)∘=dimV−dimkerT=rankT.
(32)
To deduce the eigenvalue statement, apply this equality of rank of a linear map to T−λI. Its dual map is T′−λI, so their kernel of a linear map dimensions agree. Thus the same λ are eigenvalues, with the same geometric multiplicities.
For algebraic multiplicities as well, part (a)(iii) gives
((T−λI)r)′=(T′−λI)r,
(33)
so equality of rank applies to every power. Hence
dimker(T−λI)r=dimker(T′−λI)r.
(34)
After extending to a field where the characteristic polynomials split, take r≥dimV. These kernel of a linear map spaces are the generalized eigenspaces; the primary decomposition theorem for linear maps identifies their dimensions with the algebraic multiplicities. The same proof works over the original field for every eigenvalue in it. We obtain
T and T′ have the same nonzero eigenvalues, with both kinds of multiplicity equal.​
(35)
In fact the argument also includes the zero eigenvalue.

Use the convention that the inner product is conjugate-linear in its first argument and linear in its second, as in part (b). The adjoint operator is characterized by
⟨Tv,w⟩=⟨v,T∗w⟩(v,w∈V).​
(36)
Existence and uniqueness follow from the finite-dimensional representation of linear functionals by the inner product. More concretely, choose an orthonormal basis ei​ and let aij​=⟨ei​,Tej​⟩. Then
⟨ei​,T∗ej​⟩=⟨Tei​,ej​⟩=aji​​,
(37)
which defines T∗ uniquely and verifies the required identity by expansion in that basis. Thus its matrix is the conjugate transpose, proved here rather than assumed. A self-adjoint operator satisfies T∗=T, equivalently ⟨Tv,w⟩=⟨v,Tw⟩ for every pair of vectors.

ii OurBigBook.com
Proceed by induction on dimV. The result is immediate for dimension zero. In positive dimension, the characteristic polynomial has a root over the complex numbers, so there is a nonzero eigenvector v with eigenvalue λ. Normalize it to have norm one. Self-adjointness gives
λ⟨v,v⟩=⟨Tv,v⟩=⟨v,Tv⟩=λ⟨v,v⟩,
(38)
so λ is real.
The orthogonal complement v⊥ is an invariant subspace: if w⊥v, then
⟨v,Tw⟩=⟨Tv,w⟩=λ⟨v,w⟩=0.
(39)
The restriction of T to v⊥ is again self-adjoint, since the same inner product identity holds on that subspace. By induction it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Adjoining v gives
an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of T.​
(40)
The existence of the first eigenvector is the essential use of the complex numbers in this proof of the finite-dimensional spectral theorem.

The integral is conjugate-linear in f, linear in g, and conjugate-symmetric, by the corresponding properties of the integral and of complex conjugation. Also,
⟨f,f⟩=∫−∞∞​∣f(x)∣2e−x2dx≥0.

If a polynomial f is not zero, there is a real point x0​ with f(x0​)=0: a nonzero polynomial cannot vanish at every real point. By continuity, ∣f∣ has a positive lower bound on a sufficiently small closed interval about x0​. The continuous function e−x2 also has a positive lower bound there. The integral over this interval is strictly positive. Hence
⟨f,f⟩=0 ⟺ f=0.​

Differentiate the expression from the source before applying any finite-dimensional result. The exponential factors cancel:
Tf=f′′−2xf′−2f.​
(46)
This is a polynomial whenever f is a polynomial, and its polynomial degree is at most that of f. Differentiation and multiplication are linear, so T is a linear transformation V→V, and every Vn​ is an invariant subspace.
For self-adjointness, put w(x)=e−x2. Then w′=−2xw and wTf=(wf′)′−2wf. Integration by parts therefore gives
⟨Tf,g⟩​=∫−∞∞​((wf′​)′−2wf​)gdx=−∫−∞∞​wf′​g′dx−2∫−∞∞​wf​gdx=⟨f,Tg⟩.​
(47)
The discarded endpoint terms are wf′​g and wf​g′. They vanish at both infinities because exponential decay dominates every polynomial. The same decay justifies the intermediate integrals. Consequently
T(Vn​)⊆Vn​,⟨Tf,g⟩=⟨f,Tg⟩(f,g∈V).​
(48)
Here the question's self-adjointness is in the sense of an adjoint on an inner product space: T itself represents the adjoint on the polynomial space. Each finite-dimensional restriction T∣Vn​​ is a self-adjoint operator. No choice of operator domain in a completed Hilbert space is needed for this argument. The resulting Gaussian polynomial operator will produce Hermite polynomials in part (iv).

Construct compatible choices in the nested spaces, rather than choosing unrelated orthonormal bases for different Vn​. Set V−1​={0} and define
Ln​=Vn​∩Vn−1⊥​.
(51)
The orthogonal complement is taken for the inner product from part (i). Since dimVn​=n+1 and dimVn−1​=n, the space Ln​ has dimension one and Vn​=Vn−1​⊕Ln​.
It is an invariant subspace. Indeed, if v∈Ln​ and u∈Vn−1​, part (ii) gives
⟨u,Tv⟩=⟨Tu,v⟩=0,
(52)
because Tu∈Vn−1​. Also Tv∈Vn​. Hence Tv∈Ln​. Any nonzero vector in this one-dimensional space is consequently an eigenvector. Choose a unit vector gn​∈Ln​. It has polynomial degree exactly n: membership in Vn−1​ would make it orthogonal to itself and thus zero. Part (iii) now supplies its eigenvalue.
For m<n, gm​∈Vn−1​ and gn​⊥Vn−1​, so the choices are pairwise orthogonal. We have proved
⟨gm​,gn​⟩=δmn​,deggn​=n,Tgn​=−2(n+1)gn​(n≥0).​
(53)
An explicit compatible construction is the Gram-Schmidt process on 1,x,x2,…:
rn​=xn−∑j=0n−1​⟨gj​,xn⟩gj​,gn​=⟨rn​,rn​⟩​rn​​.
(54)
The leading term of rn​ is xn, so it is never zero. This fixes the positive real leading coefficient and yields the normalized Hermite polynomials. For example, the Gaussian integral gives
g0​=π−1/4,g1​=2​π−1/4x,g2​=2​π1/42x2−1​.
(55)
Every polynomial is a finite linear combination of these eigenvectors, since g0​,…,gn​ form an orthonormal basis of Vn​ for each n.
