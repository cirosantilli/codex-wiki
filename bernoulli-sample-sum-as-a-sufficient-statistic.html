Bernoulli sample sum as a sufficient statistic (T=∑i=1n​Xi​)

Let n be fixed and known, and let X1​,…,Xn​ be independent and identically distributed Bernoulli random variables with common success probability p. Their joint probability mass function, on the binary sample space {0,1}n, is
Pp​(X=x)=∏i=1n​pxi​(1−p)1−xi​=pt(1−p)n−t,t=T(x)=∑i=1n​xi​.
(62)
There are (tn​) arrangements with t successes, so T has the binomial distribution. For 0<p<1 and t∈{0,…,n}, its exact conditional full-data probability mass function is
Pp​(X=x∣T=t)=(tn​)pt(1−p)n−tpt(1−p)n−t1{T(x)=t}​​=(tn​)1{T(x)=t}​​.
(63)
This conditional distribution is uniform over arrangements, with no dependence on p: the sum is a sufficient statistic. [ref]
For n=4, the group sizes are 1,4,6,4,1 and the within-group conditional probabilities are respectively 1,1/4,1/6,1/4,1. For example, T=2 leaves exactly 0011,0101,0110,1001,1010,1100, each with conditional probability 1/6. At p=1/4, each such sequence has unconditional probability 9/256 and the group has probability 54/256. At p=1/2, these values are 1/16 and 6/16; at p=3/4 they return to 9/256 and 54/256. The conditional weights stay 1/6 in all three cases.
At the boundary p=0, only T=0 has positive probability; at p=1, only T=n does. Dividing by Pp​(T=t) on the other groups would divide by zero. Nevertheless, the same uniform-on-arrangements Markov kernel is a valid common version for the entire family 0≤p≤1: it gives the correct point mass on the one observed group at each boundary and assigns permissible versions on its unobserved groups. Define the joint probability mass function there by the original product of the one-trial Bernoulli probability mass functions, or use the limiting convention 00=1 in the compact formula.
The sample mean T/n is an unbiased estimator of p and retains the sufficient statistic because n is known. By contrast, X1​ is also an unbiased estimator of p but, for n≥2, is not sufficient: the conditional success probability of the independent X2​ given X1​ is still p. Thus unbiasedness does not imply sufficiency.
Sufficiency also does not imply completeness of a statistic. For n≥2, the full-data sufficient statistic X admits the nonzero function g(X)=X1​−X2​ with expectation zero for every p. Nor does minimality imply completeness of a statistic: for n=2 and the restricted statistical model p∈{1/4,3/4}, T is a minimal sufficient statistic since the likelihood ratio P3/4​(X=x)/P1/4​(X=x)=32t−2 distinguishes its three values. Yet g(0)=3,g(1)=−5,g(2)=3 has expectation (27−30+3)/16=0 at both parameter values and is never zero. Changing the parameter family can change completeness of a statistic even while preserving sufficiency.
