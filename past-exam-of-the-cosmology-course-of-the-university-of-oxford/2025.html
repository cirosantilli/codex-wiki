The Helmholtz decomposition separates the peculiar velocity into a gradient field and a transverse vector field:
v=∇V+v⊥​,∇⋅v⊥​=0.
(24)
With decaying or periodic spatial boundary conditions this is equivalently the Fourier transform projection, for k=0,
v⊥i​(k)=(δij​−k2ki​kj​​)vj​(k).
(25)
The transverse projection operator annihilates a gradient, because (δij​−ki​kj​/k2)kj​=0. Both forces in the linear cosmological Euler equation are gradients, with spatially homogeneous coefficients. Applying the transverse projection operator therefore gives
v˙⊥​+Hv⊥​=0.

v⊥​(x,t)=a(t)a(ti​)​v⊥​(x,ti​).​
(27)
At this order the scalar pressure and gravitational forces cannot generate vorticity; an initially vanishing transverse vector field remains zero. Its nonzero initial value is damped by cosmic expansion.

The cosmic microwave background consists of relic photons from the early hot, nearly thermal photon-baryon fluid. Before cosmological recombination, abundant free electrons repeatedly scatter photons by Thomson scattering. Their mean free path λγ​=(ne​σT​)−1 is short and their Thomson scattering rate ne​σT​c is much greater than the Hubble parameter. An earlier photon therefore cannot stream directly to us: subsequent scattering repeatedly changes its direction.
As cosmic expansion cools the plasma to about T≃3000K, electrons and protons combine into neutral hydrogen. The free-electron abundance drops rapidly, the scattering optical depth to the observer becomes of order one, and photon decoupling occurs. Since the cosmic microwave background temperature scales as T=T0​(1+z), with T0​≃2.725K, this occurs at z≃1100. The small baryon-to-photon ratio delays neutralization to well below 13.6eV/kB​: the high-energy tail contains enough photons to ionize hydrogen until the temperature is much lower than the Hydrogen binding energy expressed as a temperature. The observed cosmic microwave background is primarily this pre-existing radiation undergoing its last scattering; its nearly blackbody spectrum is then preserved by cosmological redshift.
For the requested estimate take a flat matter-dominated universe, a(t)=(t/t0​)2/3. Restore c in distances. The comoving particle horizon at cosmological recombination and the comoving radial distance to the Cosmic microwave background last-scattering surface are
rhor,∗​=c∫0t∗​​a(t)dt​=3ct0​a∗​​,χ∗​=c∫t∗​t0​​a(t)dt​=3ct0​(1−a∗​​).
(45)
The proper particle horizon is a∗​rhor,∗​=3ct∗​ and the angular diameter distance is DA​=a∗​χ∗​. Therefore the angular particle-horizon size in a power-law universe, taking one horizon radius as the transverse length, is
θhor​≃χ∗​rhor,∗​​=1+z∗​​−11​≃0.0311rad≃1.78∘.​
(46)
The angular diameter of a patch of that radius is about 3.56∘. The factor of two is a radius-versus-diameter convention; either gives a horizon scale of only a few degrees. This is a particle horizon, rather than the smaller sound horizon. Including early radiation in cosmology changes the numerical estimate but preserves its order of magnitude.
The horizon problem is that the cosmic microwave background has almost the same temperature across the whole sky, including pairs of regions whose past light cones do not overlap in this noninflating model. Local thermal equilibration cannot explain that observation within this history. A sufficiently long period of cosmic inflation makes the comoving Hubble radius decrease: a region once small enough to share a causal past can expand to encompass our entire observed Cosmic microwave background last-scattering surface. After reheating, it can be homogeneous across many later particle horizon patches. The matter-only estimate integrates from a decelerating Big Bang; extending the earlier history by cosmic inflation changes that causal conclusion.

The four contributions have distinct physical origins. The intrinsic photon temperature perturbation is δγ​/4: a local blackbody radiation energy density satisfies ργ​∝T4, so its first-order fractional perturbation is four times the fractional temperature perturbation. The endpoint cosmological Newtonian potential term ψ∗​ is the gravitational redshift between the emitting plasma and the observer; escaping a negative potential well lowers the measured photon energy. Together with the intrinsic contribution it produces the ordinary Sachs-Wolfe effect. The term −n^⋅v∗​ is the Doppler CMB anisotropy, caused by the plasma's line-of-sight peculiar velocity at last scattering, with the source's direction convention.
Finally, 2∫dχψ′ is the Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect. A photon passing through a static potential well gains energy on entry and loses it on exit, with no net shift. When the potential evolves while the photon crosses, the two shifts fail to cancel. The factor two is the sum of the temporal and spatial scalar-potential contributions when anisotropic stress is negligible, so the two potentials are equal. The prime is a partial derivative at fixed comoving coordinate; it is not the total derivative along the light ray.
For a low-cosmological redshift galaxy sample, the intrinsic, endpoint-potential and last-scattering Doppler CMB anisotropy terms are localized near z≃1100, whereas the galaxy density contrast is measured much nearer us. In the Limber approximation their radial kernels have no overlap, so their leading cross correlations vanish. The late Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect samples the same nearby evolving potentials as the galaxies, giving the desired late-time CMB-matter cross correlation. The early Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect near matter-radiation equality is likewise radially separated.
This is the leading linear, separated-kernel description intended here. The primary Cosmic microwave background anisotropy and late density contrast share primordial initial conditions, so their exact large-angle correlation need not be identically zero outside the Limber approximation. Additional secondary effects, such as the Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect, can also correlate with galaxies in real maps; they are absent from the stipulated linear expression.
