Exact polynomial steps for a delay equation (y′(t)=ay(t−1),ϕ=1)

OurBigBook.com Words: 306
Use the Malthusian delay differential equation with time measured in units of its constant delay:
y′(t)=ay(t−1),y(t)=1(−1≤t≤0).
(8)
Here a is a real constant. On the first interval y(t−1)=1, hence y(t)=1+at. On the next interval the delayed input is 1+a(t−1), already known, so the method of steps for a delay differential equation gives
y(t)=⎩⎨⎧​1+at,1+at+2a2​(t−1)2,1+at+2a2​(t−1)2+6a3​(t−2)3,​0≤t≤1,1≤t≤2,2≤t≤3.​
(9)
Each polynomial integrates the preceding interval's delayed polynomial, and its integration constant ensures continuity. At a=1, the step endpoints are y(1)=2, y(2)=7/2, and y(3)=37/6. At t=3/2, the value is 21/8, but its derivative is the earlier value y(1/2)=3/2. This is the geometric distinction from the ordinary differential equation y′=y, whose derivative would equal its current value.
More generally, for any integer n≥0,
y(t)=1+∑k=1n+1​k!ak​(t−k+1)k,n≤t≤n+1.
(10)
At an integer join the newly introduced term vanishes. Inside a step, differentiation lowers each power by one and gives exactly a times the preceding step evaluated at t−1. Together with y(0)=1, this verifies the formula by mathematical induction; it is an exact construction, not a truncated power series.
The history and the new equation need not prescribe matching derivatives at the starting time; their mismatch can reappear later in higher derivatives.[ref] In this example, y′(0−)=0 and y′(0+)=a. For a=0, y′ is continuous at t=1 but y′′ jumps there by a2; at t=2, the first jump is in y′′′, of size a3. The solution of a differential equation is therefore continuously differentiable for positive time, although it need not have continuous derivatives of every order at step boundaries.
