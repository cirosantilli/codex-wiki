Asymptotic equivalence (an​∼bn​⟺an​/bn​→1)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Asymptotic analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 7
For functions with g nonzero sufficiently near a specified limit,
f∼g⟺gf​⟶1.​
(17)
The same definition applies to sequences an​∼bn​ as n→∞. Equivalently, (f−g)/g→0: replacing f by g has a relative error tending to zero, and the difference is o(g) in little o notation. The limit and reference scale are essential parts of the statement.
For example, n2+n∼n2 because (n2+n)/n2=1+1/n→1. Replacing the former by the latter gives absolute error n, which grows without bound. Thus asymptotic equivalence controls a ratio, rather than requiring the absolute error to vanish. This distinction is crucial when approximating a rapidly growing factorial with Stirling's formula.
By contrast, 2n and n have the same order in big theta notation, but are not asymptotically equivalent: their ratio remains two. For positive functions, asymptotic equivalence gives both-sided constant bounds eventually, whereas constant bounds alone do not recover the leading coefficient.
For a local example with real h, the exponential function obeys eh−1∼h as h→0. The Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder gives
eh−1=h+2eξ​h2,∣ξ∣≤∣h∣.
(18)
Thus for ∣h∣≤1,
∣eh−1−h∣≤2e​h2.
(19)
This states eh−1=h+O(h2) in big O notation, and division by h shows that the relative difference measured against h vanishes. The diagram uses positive h; the bound is valid on both sides of zero. At h=0.1, the absolute error is approximately 0.00517092 and the relative difference against h is approximately 0.0517092.
