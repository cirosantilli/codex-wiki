Electromagnetic induction is the production of an electromotive force by changing magnetic flux or by moving a conductor through a magnetic field. With the loop and surface orientations related by the right-hand rule, Faraday's law of induction is
E=∮(E+v×B)⋅dℓ=−dtd​∫B⋅dS.​
(71)
Here v is the local wire velocity. For a stationary loop, the induced electric field can have a nonzero closed-path integral; its differential form is ∇×E=−∂B/∂t. For a straight rod moving perpendicularly to itself and to a uniform magnetic field, the motional electromotive force has magnitude Bℓv.
The minus sign expresses Lenz's law: the induced electric current produces a magnetic field that opposes the change in linked magnetic flux. Pulling a conducting loop through a magnetic field therefore requires work against an electromagnetic resisting force; that work supplies the resulting heat or stored energy.
For an inductor with constant self-inductance, E=−LdI/dt and its stored magnetic energy is LI2/2. A changing electric current in one coil can induce an electromotive force in another through mutual inductance, the operating principle of a electrical transformer. These examples connect the flux law, the direction of the response and conservation of energy.
