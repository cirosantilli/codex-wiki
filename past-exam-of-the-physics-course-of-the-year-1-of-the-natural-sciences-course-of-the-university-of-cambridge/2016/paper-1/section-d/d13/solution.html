Snell's law is n1​sinθ1​=n2​sinθ2​, with angles measured from the interface normal. The Huygens-Fresnel principle explains it by constructing the transmitted wavefront from secondary wavelets: during the same time interval, distances travelled in the two media are proportional to c/n1​ and c/n2​. The geometry of the advancing plane wavefront then gives sinθ1​/sinθ2​=(c/n1​)/(c/n2​)=n2​/n1​.
For incidence from index n into vacuum, the transmitted angle is α=arcsin(nsinθ). The ray bends away from the normal, so its positive deviation is
δ(θ,n)=arcsin(nsinθ)−θ.​
(53)
For 0<θ<θc​=arcsin(1/n),
∂θ∂δ​=1−n2sin2θ​ncosθ​−1>0.
(54)
Thus the graph rises monotonically from zero to a finite endpoint with a vertical tangent:
θc​=arcsin(1/n),δmax​=2π​−arcsin(1/n).​
(55)
At the endpoint the transmitted ray grazes the interface. Beyond the critical angle for total internal reflection, no propagating transmitted ray exists and total internal reflection occurs.
Inside a prism, the two ray angles with the face normals sum to its apex angle. For the equilateral cross-section, they are θ and π/3−θ. The entry deviation equals the corresponding exit deviation under ray reversal. Adding the two changes of direction therefore gives
Δ(θ)=δ(θ,n)+δ(π/3−θ,n).​
(56)
This is symmetric about θ=π/6. Symmetry alone gives a stationary point; to establish that it is a minimum, differentiate once more:
∂θ2∂2δ​=(1−n2sin2θ)3/2n(n2−1)sinθ​>0
(57)
within the transmitted-ray interval. The sum is therefore strictly convex, and
θmin​=6π​,Δmin​=2arcsin(n/2)−3π​.​
(58)
These expressions require 1<n<2 for a non-grazing path through both faces; n=2 is a grazing limit, and n>2 admits no such two-face transmitted path. More explicitly, both internal angles must be at most θc​, which is possible only if 2θc​≥π/3.
Atmospheric hexagonal ice crystals can present pairs of faces with a 60∘ angle, acting as small prisms. Their orientations turn the minimum-deviation cone around the Moon into an optical halo. No transmitted ray of this family is deflected less than the minimum deviation of a prism. Moreover, near the minimum, Δ−Δmin​ is quadratic in the departure from the symmetric ray, so a range of incident directions is compressed into a small outgoing angular range. This caustic makes the inner rim sharp and bright, with the ray density decreasing farther outside. The common 22-degree halo has an angular radius near 22∘ about either the Sun or Moon, hence a diameter near 44∘.[ref]
