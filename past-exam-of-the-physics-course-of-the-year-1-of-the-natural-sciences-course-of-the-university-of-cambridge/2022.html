Choose three mutually perpendicular axes through the same point, and label coordinates x,y,z. The moments of inertia of any rigid body are
Ix​=∫(y2+z2)dm,Iy​=∫(z2+x2)dm,Iz​=∫(x2+y2)dm.
(16)
Subtracting gives
Ix​+Iy​−Iz​=2∫z2dm≥0.
(17)
For a thin planar lamina lying in z=0, the right-hand side vanishes, proving the perpendicular axis theorem:
Iz​=Ix​+Iy​.​
(18)
The two in-plane axes and the perpendicular axis must intersect at one point; this need not be the centre of mass. For an extended three-dimensional mass distribution, the equality generally fails. The general moment-of-inertia triangle inequalities are
Ix​+Iy​≥Iz​,Iy​+Iz​≥Ix​,Iz​+Ix​≥Iy​,​
(19)
with equality in the first precisely when all mass lies in the plane perpendicular to its z axis, and cyclically for the others. None of these identities requires principal axes.

V(t)=Re(Veiωt),I(t)=Re(Ieiωt).

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is the energy eigenvalue equation Hψ=Eψ for a particle of mass m. The Hamiltonian operator is the sum of the kinetic operator −(ℏ2/2m)d2/dx2 and the prescribed potential energy V(x). Here ℏ is the reduced Planck constant. A corresponding stationary state is Ψ(x,t)=ψ(x)e−iEt/ℏ. The Born rule identifies ∣Ψ∣2=∣ψ∣2 as probability density for a state with wavefunction normalization ∫∣Ψ∣2dx=1; integrating it over an interval gives a position probability. A plane wave scattering state is not a normalizable wavefunction on the whole line, but its probability currents give the reflection and transmission fractions of an incident wave packet.
There is a notation discrepancy in the sources: the original PDF prints an unbarred h2 in the kinetic term, whereas the local TeX uses ℏ2. The standard physical equation uses the reduced Planck constant ℏ=h/(2π), where h is the Planck constant; that convention is used below. Reading the printed h literally as the Planck constant would scale all lengths in this model by 2π relative to the standard answers, but would leave the amplitude ratios unchanged.
For E>V0​, both sides allow oscillatory wavefunctions. A classical particle always crosses the potential step and slows because its kinetic energy falls from E to E−V0​. In quantum mechanics there is generally both reflection and transmission even though the particle is above the step. Define the positive wavenumbers
k=ℏ2mE​​,q=ℏ2m(E−V0​)​​.
(62)
With no wave incident from the right, write
ψ(x)={Aeikx+Be−ikx,Ceiqx,​x≤0,x>0.​
(63)
A finite potential step with constant mass makes both the wavefunction and its first derivative continuous. The two boundary conditions at x=0 are
A+B=C,k(A−B)=qC.
(64)
Solving gives the reflected and transmitted wavefunctions
ψref​(x)=Ak+qk−q​e−ikx,ψtr​(x)=Ak+q2k​eiqx.​
(65)
For the specified electron energies, q/k=10/50​=1/5​, so the relative probability amplitudes are
AB​=5​+15​−1​=0.381966,AC​=5​+125​​=1.381966.​

A scanning tunneling microscope brings a tip and surface, both electrical conductors, close enough to leave only a small vacuum gap. An applied electric potential difference makes occupied electron states on one side able to feed unoccupied states on the other. Although the gap is a classically forbidden potential barrier, quantum tunnelling through its finite width produces an electric current.
For an approximate rectangular potential barrier of gap width d, the transmission probability, and thus the electric current at fixed small bias, varies approximately as e−2κd. This strong distance dependence makes the device sensitive to changes in separation on the scale of a chemical atom. Feedback control moves the tip vertically to keep the electric current constant during a scan; the required height changes form an image of the surface, with contrast also depending on its electronic states. The finite gap permits transmitted probability current, whereas the semi-infinite step in part (b) does not.

Read the original diagram as a clockwise arc carrying electric current from its upper endpoint to its lower endpoint, with tangent semi-infinite leads extending to the right. Their crossing is insulated, so there is no extra junction. Choose +z out of the page. At the centre the arc produces a negative axial magnetic field, and each straight lead a positive one, by the right-hand rule.
For the arc, every element is at distance R, with dl perpendicular to its displacement to the centre. The Biot-Savart law therefore gives
Bz,arc​=−4πRμ0​I​∫−θ/2θ/2​dϕ=−4πRμ0​Iθ​.

To calculate magnetic-field cancellation of an arc and tangent leads away from the plane, put a2=R2+h2. Each point on the arc is distance a from (0,0,h), and the axial component of dl×s still has magnitude R2dϕ. Thus its axial magnetic field is
Bz,arc​(h)=−4π(R2+h2)3/2μ0​IθR2​.​
(81)
For either tangent lead the axial numerator remains Rds, while the cubed distance is (R2+h2+s2)3/2. Hence
Bz,lead​(h)=4πμ0​I​∫0∞​(a2+s2)3/2Rds​=4π(R2+h2)μ0​IR​.​
(82)
Adding both leads gives
Bz​(h)=4πRμ0​I​[1+(h/R)22​−[1+(h/R)2]3/2θ​].​
(83)
For ϵ=h/R≪1, a binomial series yields
Bz​(h)=4πRμ0​I​[(2−θ)+(−2+23​θ)ϵ2+O(ϵ4)].
(84)
The exact condition for cancellation of the axial component is
θ(h)=21+ϵ2​,θ(h)=2+R2h2​+O(R4h4​)in radians.​
(85)
For example, keeping θ=2 would leave a small positive axial magnetic field proportional to h2/R2. Both contributions weaken with height, but the arc factor is (1+ϵ2)−3/2 whereas the lead factor is (1+ϵ2)−1. The arc therefore loses a greater fraction of its axial magnetic field. Increasing its angle compensates for this loss. Physically, the arc is all at the same finite radius, whereas distant elements of the semi-infinite leads undergo a smaller fractional change in distance on moving a little out of the plane.
The cancellation here concerns Bz​ only; off the plane it does not assert that every component of the magnetic field vanishes. The small-height result also stays within the tangent-lead geometry shown in the original paper.
