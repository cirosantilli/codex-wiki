A scanning tunneling microscope brings a tip and surface, both electrical conductors, close enough to leave only a small vacuum gap. An applied electric potential difference makes occupied electron states on one side able to feed unoccupied states on the other. Although the gap is a classically forbidden potential barrier, quantum tunnelling through its finite width produces an electric current.
For an approximate rectangular potential barrier of gap width d, the transmission probability, and thus the electric current at fixed small bias, varies approximately as e−2κd. This strong distance dependence makes the device sensitive to changes in separation on the scale of a chemical atom. Feedback control moves the tip vertically to keep the electric current constant during a scan; the required height changes form an image of the surface, with contrast also depending on its electronic states. The finite gap permits transmitted probability current, whereas the semi-infinite step in part (b) does not.
