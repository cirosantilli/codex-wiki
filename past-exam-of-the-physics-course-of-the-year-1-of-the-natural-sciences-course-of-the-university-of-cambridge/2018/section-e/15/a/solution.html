An electric dipole is a localized pair of opposite electric charges, or more generally a neutral electric charge distribution with nonzero first electric moment. For charges −q and +q separated by displacement d directed from negative to positive, the electric dipole moment is
p=qd,[p]=Cm.​
(84)
A neutral molecule can have a permanent electric dipole moment when its positive and negative charge centres do not coincide. An applied electric field can also produce an induced electric dipole moment by distorting an initially nonpolar electric charge distribution.
At distance r≫∣d∣ in vacuum, expand the difference of the two point-charge potentials to first order in the separation. The leading electric potential is
Φ(r)=4πε0​r2p⋅r​.
(85)
The monopole term cancels because the net electric charge is zero. Taking E=−∇Φ gives the electric dipole field
E(r)=4πε0​r33(p⋅r)r−p​.​
(86)
On the dipole axis its magnitude is 2p/(4πε0​r3) and its direction is along p; in the equatorial plane the magnitude is p/(4πε0​r3) and the field points opposite to p. The r−3 decay is faster than the r−2 field of an isolated point charge.
In a uniform external electric field, the opposite forces on the two charges cancel in their resultant but form a couple. Its torque is
τ=p×E,∣τ∣=pEsinθ.
(87)
For a fixed permanent electric dipole moment, the interaction potential energy is
U=−p⋅E=−pEcosθ.​
(88)
Differentiating with respect to orientation recovers the torque. Parallel alignment minimizes U and is stable against a small rotation; antiparallel alignment maximizes U and is unstable. Rotating a fixed dipole slowly from parallel to antiparallel requires external work 2pE.
A nonuniform electrostatic electric field can also exert a net force. Expanding the two charge forces gives F≃(p⋅∇)E, which equals ∇(p⋅E) for a fixed dipole in a curl-free electrostatic field. Thus a suitably aligned dipole is attracted toward stronger field. An induced electric dipole moment requires care in computing energy: when p=αE for constant isotropic electric polarizability, building the dipole costs energy and the effective interaction is U=−αE2/2, rather than substituting directly into the fixed-dipole formula. These field, torque and energy effects explain molecular orientation and electric polarization.
