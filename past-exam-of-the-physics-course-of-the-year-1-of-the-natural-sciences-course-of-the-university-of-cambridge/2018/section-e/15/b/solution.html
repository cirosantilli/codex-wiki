A conservative vector field has path-independent work: the integral ∫AB​F⋅dr depends only on its endpoints. Equivalently, the line integral around every closed path in its domain vanishes. One can then define a single-valued potential energy U by
U(B)−U(A)=−∫AB​F⋅dr,F=−∇U,WA→B​=U(A)−U(B).​
(89)
The arbitrary additive constant in U has no effect on the force. A useful zero is often chosen at infinity for a localized attractive interaction.
A differentiable conservative force has ∇×F=0 because the curl of a gradient vanishes. Conversely, a smooth curl-free field on a simply connected domain admits a global scalar potential. The domain condition matters: on the punctured plane, F=(C/r)θ has zero curl away from the missing origin but closed-loop work 2πC around it. It is not globally conservative there. The existence of a local potential does not establish a single-valued potential throughout a domain with holes.
The work-energy theorem gives ΔT=W for kinetic energy T. If all forces are conservative and U has no explicit time dependence, then
ΔT=−ΔU,T+U=constant.​
(90)
This is conservation of mechanical energy. In one dimension, an equilibrium occurs at dU/dx=0; a local minimum with U′′>0 is stable, while a maximum with U′′<0 is unstable. Expanding about a minimum gives U≃U0​+U′′(x0​)(x−x0​)2/2, producing simple harmonic motion with ω2=U′′(x0​)/m.
Examples include the spring force Fx​=−kx with U=kx2/2, the near-surface gravitational force −mgz with U=mgz, and the central Newtonian gravitational field with U=−GMm/r. An electrostatic force on a fixed electric charge q has U=qΦ, where Φ is the electric potential.
Sliding friction provides a contrasting physical test: traversing a closed path still dissipates mechanical energy, since the frictional force opposes the local motion on both outward and return legs. Its work depends on path length and cannot be represented by a position-only U. Total energy can remain conserved when the resulting internal energy is included. Also, even if F=−∇U(r,t) at each instant, explicit time dependence gives d(T+U)/dt=∂U/∂t; a static conservative-field argument alone does not guarantee constant mechanical energy in that situation.
