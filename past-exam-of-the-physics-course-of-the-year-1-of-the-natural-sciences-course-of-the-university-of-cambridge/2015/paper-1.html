Symmetry is needed to take the field outside the surface integral; Gauss's law alone does not make an arbitrary charge distribution spherical.
For gravity the corresponding relations are ∮S​g⋅dS=−4πGMenc​ and ∇⋅g=−4πGρm​. A spherical mass has exterior field g=−GMr^/r2. Inside a uniform sphere, Menc​(r)=4πρm​r3/3, so g=−(4πGρm​/3)rr^​. Inside an empty spherical shell the enclosed mass is zero and symmetry gives zero field. These examples distinguish an inverse-square exterior field from a field inside distributed matter.
The law also treats other symmetries, but their field-distance dependence differs: an infinitely long line charge has cylindrical rather than spherical spreading and a 1/r field. Flux conservation and geometry must both be used, rather than assuming every Gaussian surface leads to an inverse-square answer.

A time-independent conservative force defines a conservative vector field on a domain when the work between two points is independent of path, equivalently when every closed-path work integral vanishes. Define a potential energy U by F=−∇U​ and U(B)−U(A)=−∫AB​F⋅dr. The additive constant is arbitrary; only differences affect work. A vanishing curl is necessary, and is sufficient on a simply connected domain under the usual smoothness assumptions. Local zero curl by itself need not guarantee a single-valued global potential on a domain with holes.
For a test mass outside a point or spherical mass, U=−GMm/r with zero at infinity reproduces the inward gravitational force. Near a surface with approximately uniform downward gravity, U=mgz when z increases upward. A linear spring has U=kx2/2. In electrostatics the electric potential is energy per unit charge, so U=qV and E=−∇V; potential and potential energy have different units and roles.
For motion under a time-independent conservative force, d(K+U)/dt=0. nonconservative forces change the mechanical energy by their work, and an explicitly time-dependent potential can exchange energy with the external agency changing it. A time-varying magnetic field can give a circulating electric field, so not every electric field is electrostatic or conservative. Conversely, zero magnetic work on a charge does not turn a velocity-dependent magnetic force into an ordinary position-potential force.

For a test particle outside a spherical central mass, the gravitational force is central, so angular momentum is conserved and the orbit is planar. With specific angular momentum h=r2θ˙ and specific orbital energy ϵ=r˙2/2+h2/(2r2)−GM/r, the radial effective potential combines attraction with the centrifugal barrier. Its nonradial solutions are conic sections:
r(θ)=1+ecos(θ−θ0​)p​,p=GMh2​,e2=1+(GM)22ϵh2​.​
(45)
For nonzero h, negative energy gives an ellipse with 0≤e<1, zero energy a parabolic Kepler orbit and positive energy a hyperbolic Kepler orbit. The circle is the special case e=0. Exactly radial motion is a separate zero-angular-momentum case and may hit the central body; a mathematical point-mass trajectory is not automatically a physically noncolliding orbit.
Conservation of h gives constant areal velocity h/2, so equal areas are swept in equal times. For a bound ellipse of semimajor axis a, ϵ=−GM/(2a) and T2=4π2a3/(GM). A circular orbit has speed GM/r​, while the minimum local escape speed is 2GM/r​, obtained by setting the total energy to zero. These are the test-mass two-body results: a comparable second mass requires the relative-coordinate gravitational parameter G(M+m), and nonspherical structure or drag can perturb the conic and its conserved quantities.

In SI units, with microscopic charge density ρe​ and current density J, Maxwell's equations can be written
∇⋅E=ε0​ρe​​,∇⋅B=0,∇×E=−∂t∂B​,∇×B=μ0​J+μ0​ε0​∂t∂E​.​
(46)
The first relates electric flux to charge. The second states that the net magnetic flux through a closed surface is zero. Faraday's law relates electric circulation to changing magnetic flux. The Ampère-Maxwell equation includes both conduction current and displacement current; the latter is essential to time-dependent fields and local charge conservation.
For fixed contours and spanning surfaces, the integral curl equations are ∮E⋅dl=−dΦB​/dt and ∮B⋅dl=μ0​Ienc​+μ0​ε0​dΦE​/dt. For a moving material contour the first must include the motional force term; its total emf is ∮(E+vloop​×B)⋅dl. This prevents confusing a changing circuit area with a partial time derivative of a static magnetic field.
Taking the divergence of the Ampère-Maxwell equation and using electric Gauss's law gives ∂t​ρe​+∇⋅J=0. In a source-free vacuum, combining the two curl equations gives ∇2E−μ0​ε0​∂t2​E=0, and similarly for B, predicting electromagnetic waves of speed c=1/μ0​ε0​​. The Lorentz force law then specifies how these fields act on a charge. Macroscopic matter descriptions may instead use D and H with free sources and constitutive relations; those should not be silently mixed with the microscopic-source equations above.
