A distant external charge polarizes the neutral atom. Its electric field displaces the positive and negative charge distributions relative to one another, giving an induced dipole p=αEext​ with static electric polarizability α>0. The field is stronger on the nearer side, so the opposite-charge attraction exceeds the like-charge repulsion. This is charge-induced dipole attraction, not a net monopole charge on the atom.
Quantitatively, for external charge Q=±e at separation r, the polarization energy is U=−αEext2​/2, hence
U(r)=−2(4πϵ0​)2r4αQ2​,Fr​=−drdU​=−(4πϵ0​)2r52αQ2​.​
(61)
Here negative Fr​ means decreasing separation. Reversing the external charge reverses the induced dipole but leaves this leading long-range force attractive and of the same magnitude. The factor one half accounts for the energy cost of inducing the dipole.
The supplied charge density describes the unperturbed atom. Adding its electron field to the nucleus field leaves Eatom​=ee−2r/a0​(r−2+2/(a0​r)+2/a02​)/(4πϵ0​), an exponentially small residual because the cloud has an infinite tail. A completely frozen density would give only that tiny sign-dependent force. Allowing polarization is essential to obtain the dominant attractive power-law force for either sign of external charge when r≫a0​.
