In electrostatic equilibrium a conductor has E=0 throughout its bulk and constant electric potential on each connected component. Otherwise free charge would continue moving. Gauss's law then excludes bulk excess charge; it resides on surfaces. Just outside a conductor the tangential field vanishes and the normal jump is
Eout​=ϵ0​σ​n​
(57)
for a vacuum interface with outward normal n. Surface charge rearranges in an external field to enforce these boundary conditions. Greater curvature often concentrates charge, but the actual distribution is a global boundary-value problem.
An empty closed cavity inside a conductor has constant potential and zero field, even in an external electrostatic field. If a charge q is placed in the cavity, a Gaussian surface in the metal shows that the cavity wall carries total induced charge −q; its detailed distribution depends on geometry. An initially neutral isolated conductor then has +q on its exterior surface. A grounded conductor can exchange charge with Earth, so this isolation condition no longer applies.
A useful example is a charge q at height d above an infinite grounded plane. In the physical half-space, the method of images replaces the induced charges by an image −q at height −d, giving the attractive force q2/(16πϵ0​d2). The image is a mathematical construction, not an actual charge behind the conductor. Equipotential surfaces and charge conservation determine conductor fields together.
