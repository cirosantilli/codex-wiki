The field is stronger on the nearer side, so the opposite-charge attraction exceeds the like-charge repulsion. This is charge-induced dipole attraction, not a net monopole charge on the atom.

(61)

A completely frozen density would give only that tiny sign-dependent force. A charge q moving nonrelativistically in prescribed fields obeys the Lorentz force law
mdtdv​=q(E+v×B).​
(62)
The magnetic part is perpendicular to velocity, so d(mv2/2)/dt=qE⋅v: the electric field can change kinetic energy, while the magnetic field alone changes direction without doing work.
In a uniform electric field with no magnetic field, acceleration is constant: v=v0​+(qE/m)t and r=r0​+v0​t+qEt2/(2m). The trajectory is a parabola when there is initial motion perpendicular to the field, and a line when motion is purely along it. Reversing the charge reverses the electric acceleration.
In a uniform magnetic field with no electric field, resolve velocity into parallel and perpendicular components. The parallel component is constant; the perpendicular component rotates at the cyclotron frequency
ωc​=m∣q∣B​,rL​=∣q∣Bmv⊥​​.​
(63)

A position-dependent force is a conservative force when its work depends only on endpoints. Equivalently, its work around any closed allowed path vanishes, and one can define a potential energy U satisfying
F=−∇U,WA→B​=U(A)−U(B).​
(65)
The potential is defined up to an additive constant. A vanishing curl, ∇×F=0, is a local test; it guarantees a global single-valued potential on a simply connected region with suitable regularity. Holes or excluded singularities require checking the global path condition.
The work-energy theorem gives dK/dt=F⋅v. For a static potential this implies d(K+U)/dt=0. If U depends explicitly on time, then instead d(K+U)/dt=∂U/∂t under that force alone. Friction and other nonconservative forces add their own power and can convert mechanical energy into heat.
Examples include the gravitational potential energy −GMm/r outside a spherical mass and the electrostatic energy U=qV, where E=−∇V. For a point source charge Q, U=qQ/(4πϵ0​r), giving attraction or repulsion through the sign of qQ. A spring has U=kx2/2 and force −kx. An equilibrium has ∇U=0; a strict local minimum is stable against small displacements when the quadratic curvature is positive. Expanding about that minimum explains the harmonic restoring force used in many small-oscillation models.
Magnetic force on a moving point charge does no work, but it is velocity-dependent and is not generally a position-only force −∇U. Likewise a time-varying magnetic flux induces a circulating electric field with ∮E⋅dl=−dΦB​/dt, so that electric field cannot globally be represented solely by an electrostatic scalar potential. These distinctions prevent treating every field force as conservative simply because an energy description exists for the complete system.

For steady currents in vacuum, the Biot-Savart law gives the magnetic field by summing oriented current elements. With R=r−r′ from the source to the observation point,
B(r)=4πμ0​​∫R3J(r′)×R​d3r′.​
(66)
For a thin wire it becomes B=(μ0​I/4π)∫dl′×R/R2. The cross product fixes the direction by the right-hand rule. Fields from different steady circuits add by superposition; the entire current path, including return wires where relevant, belongs in the calculation.
An infinite straight wire gives circular field lines of magnitude B=μ0​I/(2πs)​ at perpendicular distance s. This follows by integrating the transverse component of each element along the wire. A circular loop of radius a has cancelling transverse components on its axis, leaving
Bz​(z)=2(a2+z2)3/2μ0​Ia2​,Bz​(0)=2aμ0​I​.​
(67)
Multiple coincident turns multiply the field by their number. Far from the loop this is the field of a magnetic dipole of moment μ=Iπa2n. Integrating over many loops gives the approximately uniform interior field B≃μ0​nI of a long solenoid, with n turns per unit length. Finite coils have fringing fields, and appropriately spaced coil pairs can produce a more uniform measurement region.
The law is useful for predicting fields of wires and coils where direct symmetry is insufficient, designing electromagnets and estimating stray fields. It is a magnetostatic law: steady charge flow satisfies charge continuity. An instantaneous application to rapidly changing currents neglects propagation and radiation; such problems require the time-dependent Maxwell's equations and retarded fields. A quasistatic approximation can be valid when circuit dimensions are small compared with the light-travel distance during the variation timescale.
