Union by rank (rank(r))

 Home Computer science Data structure Disjoint-set data structure Disjoint-set forest
OurBigBook.com Words: 294
Union by rank keeps a nonnegative integer rank, initially zero, for each member. For each input, obtain its root of a tree using the disjoint-set find operation, then compare the resulting ranks. Attach the lower-rank root of a tree below the higher-rank root of a tree, leaving both ranks unchanged. When the two disjoint-set representatives differ but their ranks are equal, choose either survivor and increase its rank by exactly one. A redundant disjoint-set union operation changes no ranks. Path compression also changes no ranks. The surviving root's rank bounds the height of its rooted tree, measured in edges; after path compression it need not equal that height.[ref]
In our example, let the first argument's root of a tree survive equal-rank ties. The first two merges create roots 2,4, each of rank 1. The third gives parent[4]=2 and increases the rank of 2 to 2. The fourth creates root 6 of rank 1. The fifth attaches 6 below 2 without increasing any rank. Before any subsequent compression queries, the indexed arrays are

The redundant call on 1,2 leaves these arrays unchanged. In particular, the disjoint-set representative of 1 is 2, although 1 is the minimum member of its block of a set partition.
Two short arguments explain the rank invariant. First, a root of rank r has at least 2r members in its block of a set partition: rank zero starts with one, and increasing rank combines two disjoint blocks of a set partition with at least 2r members each. Unequal-rank linking only adds members. Thus every rank is at most ⌊log2​n⌋ for n stored members, using the floor function and logarithm. Second, height is initially zero; unequal-rank linking adds at most one to the lower height, while equal-rank linking raises the available bound by one. Path compression only shortens routes. These arguments prove a height bound, not the stronger amortized analysis below.
