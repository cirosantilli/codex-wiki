A state function is determined by the equilibrium state, rather than the path used to reach it. Its differential is exact and its change around a closed thermodynamic cycle is zero. Internal energy, entropy and enthalpy are examples; transferred heat and work are not state functions.
For slow expansion in contact with a large thermal reservoir, the fixed temperature of that reservoir maintains an isothermal process. For a fixed amount of ideal gas, internal energy and enthalpy depend only on temperature, so
T=constant,U=constant,H=constant.​
(1)
The ideal gas law also gives pV=constant. For a reversible expansion the absorbed heat equals the work done by the gas, while its entropy increases by nmol​Rln(V2​/V1​); constant internal energy does not mean zero heat transfer.

With a thermally insulating boundary, δQ=0. If the slow piston motion is frictionless and reversible, it is an isentropic process:
S=constant.​
(2)
The first law of thermodynamics gives dU=−pdV, so expansion lowers the internal energy and temperature. For constant heat capacity ratio γ, pVγ and TVγ−1 are constant combinations of state functions. Insulation alone does not guarantee constant entropy: an irreversible adiabatic process can produce entropy. The conclusion uses the intended reversible, quasistatic piston idealization.

For steady insulated flow through a porous plug, neglect changes of bulk kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy, and allow no shaft work. Each amount of gas brings flow work p1​V1​ upstream and delivers p2​V2​ downstream. The steady energy conservation balance is
U1​+p1​V1​=U2​+p2​V2​,H1​=H2​.​
(3)
Thus enthalpy is conserved in this Joule-Thomson effect. For an ideal gas, H=H(T), so T1​=T2​ and U1​=U2​ as well. The entropy increases:
ΔS=nmol​Rlnp2​p1​​>0.​
(4)
The porous-plug process is irreversible despite being slow and insulated; it is not the reversible expansion of the preceding part.

In the one-mole Van der Waals equation, b>0 represents excluded volume: the volume available to molecular motion is V−b, rather than V. Subtracting b in the denominator therefore increases the repulsive contribution to pressure. The a>0 term represents intermolecular attraction, which reduces the outward momentum flux at a wall and hence appears as the negative correction −a/V2. The two minus signs do not mean that both physical effects lower pressure.
For a reversible isothermal process along a homogeneous gas branch with V2​>V1​>b, the work done by the gas is
Wby​=∫V1​V2​​(V−bRT​−V2a​)dV=RTlnV1​−bV2​−b​+a(V2​1​−V1​1​).​
(5)
To find the internal energy change without guessing a caloric law, combine dU=TdS−pdV with the Helmholtz free energy Maxwell relation (∂S/∂V)T​=(∂p/∂T)V​. This gives
(∂V∂U​)T​=T(∂T∂p​)V​−p=V2a​.
(6)
Therefore
ΔU=a(V1​1​−V2​1​)>0.​
(7)
The attractive contribution −a/V becomes less negative as the gas expands: separating attracting particles requires energy. This differs from the ideal gas result ΔU=0 at fixed temperature. The required heat is Q=ΔU+Wby​=RTln[(V2​−b)/(V1​−b)]. Below the critical temperature, an equilibrium path crossing a liquid–gas coexistence region needs the equilibrium coexistence construction rather than the unstable homogeneous Van der Waals isotherm.

The Clapeyron equation for molar latent heat L is dp/dT=L/[T(Vvap​−Vliq​)]. Neglect the liquid molar volume, treat the vapor as an ideal gas, and approximate L as constant over the interval. Then
dTdlnp​≃RT2L​,lnpl​ph​​=RL​(Tl​1​−Th​1​).
(8)
Using one atmosphere as 101325Pa gives
L≃1/1.2−1/4.2Rln(101325/133)​=92.7Jmol−1.​
(9)
This is the effective molar enthalpy of vaporization inferred from the Integrated Clausius-Clapeyron relation. If L varies with temperature, the inferred value is weighted by 1/T2, not an unweighted arithmetic mean. Real helium is not exactly an ideal gas over the whole interval, and its liquid changes superfluid state within it, so this is an estimate under the stated approximations rather than a precise temperature-independent material constant.

A magnetic field favors alignment of the magnetic moments of a paramagnet, reducing the number of substantially occupied magnetic configurations at fixed temperature. Its magnetic entropy therefore decreases as the field magnitude increases, explaining the negative sign of (∂S/∂B)T​=−αB/T2.
Choose the magnetic Helmholtz free energy convention dF=−SdT−mdB. Equality of its mixed partial derivatives gives the magnetic Maxwell relation
(∂T∂m​)B​=(∂B∂S​)T​=−T2αB​.
(10)
The magnetic moment inferred from an entropy derivative, integrated at fixed B, is
m(T,B)=TαB​+m0​(B).​
(11)
The given entropy derivative alone cannot determine the temperature-independent function m0​(B). With the usual independent-paramagnet condition m→0 as T→∞ at fixed B, m0​(B)=0, giving Curie's law, m=αB/T. The sketch uses that additional condition. The inverse-T expression applies in the weak-field regime; its divergence cannot describe the actual low-temperature saturation of a finite set of magnetic moments.
For reversible quasistatic thermal isolation, dS=0. With positive field-dependent heat capacity CB​=T(∂S/∂T)B​,
0=TCB​​dT−T2αB​dB,dT=CB​TαB​dB.​
(12)
Reducing a positive B therefore lowers T: this is adiabatic demagnetization. Equivalently, reducing B2 cools for either field sign. Reduced alignment would raise magnetic entropy at fixed T; cooling compensates so total entropy remains constant. Insulation without reversibility would allow additional entropy production and need not follow this adiabat exactly.

Assume a stationary temperature profile, spatially uniform mass density and radioactive heat generation rate, constant thermal conductivity, isotropic solid thermal conduction, and blackbody emission into effectively cold surroundings. The volumetric source is
qv​=ρh=4.375×10−7Wm−3.
(13)
Total radiogenic power equals the surface blackbody radiation loss. The Stefan–Boltzmann law gives
34πR3​qv​=4πR2σSB​TS4​,TS​=(3σSB​qv​R​)1/4=22.52K.​
(14)
For steady radiogenic heating of a conducting sphere, the interior heat equation is
r21​drd​(r2κdrdT​)+qv​=0.
(15)
Regularity at the centre removes the 1/r2 integration term and yields dT/dr=−qv​r/(3κ). Matching T(R)=TS​ gives
T(r)=TS​+6κqv​​(R2−r2),TC​=TS​+6κqv​R2​=371.4K.​
(16)
The high central temperature is compatible with a cold surface because finite thermal conductivity requires a large temperature gradient to carry the accumulated interior heat. A nonzero background radiation temperature Tbg​ replaces TS4​ by TS4​−Tbg4​ in the surface balance. The calculation neglects convection, stored transient heat, changing radioactive abundances, phase changes and any substantial external irradiation.

1=N∫0∞​e−αxdx=αN​,N=α,⟨x⟩=α∫0∞​xe−αxdx=α1​.​

It is a Rayleigh distribution with scale σ=1/2α​. Its derivative is g′(y)=2αe−αy2(1−2αy2), so it rises from zero, has one maximum, and then decays:

Let the gas have a small bulk velocity ux​(y), an approximately isotropic local thermal velocity distribution, and a mean free path λ short compared with the flow-gradient scale. A molecule transports the mean streamwise momentum acquired near its previous collision. To first order, its nonequilibrium momentum relative to the destination layer is approximately −mτc​vy​dux​/dy. The resulting transverse momentum flux is
Πxy​≃−nmτc​⟨vy2​⟩dydux​​.
(21)
Thus the dynamic viscosity is η≃ρτc​⟨vy2​⟩. With isotropy, a representative collision time τc​∼λ/⟨v⟩, and a chosen thermal speed average, this has the form
η=Kρ⟨v⟩λ.​
(22)
The dimensionless K depends on how speeds and collision times are averaged; a simple one-speed estimate gives K=1/3. Assumptions include dilute binary collisions, a locally equilibrated distribution, weak shear, and negligible boundary slip. The kinetic theory identity p=ρ⟨v2⟩/3 then gives
ηp​=3K⟨v⟩2⟨v2⟩​λ⟨v⟩​.
(23)
For the Maxwell speed distribution, ⟨v2⟩/⟨v⟩2=3π/8, so
ηp​=8Kπ​νcoll​,νcoll​≃λ⟨v⟩​.​
(24)
This explains both the units of inverse time and the relation to molecular collision frequency, up to the averaging convention.
In the cylinder apparatus, distinguish the vector mean molecular velocity, ⟨v⟩=u, from the mean thermal speed ⟨∣v−u∣⟩. For a slow nearly isothermal Couette flow, the thermal speed is approximately position-independent, whereas the bulk mean is azimuthal and changes from the local rotating-wall speed to zero at the fixed wall. In the side gap, with y measured radially from the inner wall,
uφ​(y)≃ωR(1−hy​),0≤y≤h.
(25)
Across either end gap, at cylindrical radius s, the corresponding profile is uφ​(s,z)≃ωs(1−z/h), with z measured away from the moving end face. The no-slip boundary condition and small h/R,h/H underlie these linear profiles.
The side-wall shear stress has magnitude ηωR/h, producing resisting torque 2πηωR3H/h. The original diagram also has a gap at both circular end faces. At either face, the stress is ηωs/h and its ring contribution is
dτend​=s(hηωs​)2πsds,τend​=2hπηωR4​.
(26)
Adding the side and both ends gives the end-face torque in narrow-gap cylindrical viscometry:
τ=Cηω,C=hπ​(2R3H+R4).​
(27)
The motor supplies the opposite of this viscous resisting torque. Corner flow and the small axle area are neglected. Retaining only the cylindrical wall would omit the πR4/h term shown by the actual closed-ended geometry.
For hard-sphere Ar atoms of collision diameter dAr​, λ=1/(2​nπdAr2​) and ρ=nmAr​. Therefore
dAr​=[2​πηKmAr​⟨v⟩​]1/2,⟨v⟩=πmAr​8kB​T​​.
(28)
Using mAr​=40mu​, T=273.15K and the simple estimate K=1/3 gives
⟨v⟩≃380ms−1,dAr​≃3.0×10−10m.​
(29)
This is a kinetic estimate with a model-dependent numerical prefactor, rather than a precise atomic size measurement. Since the density cancels and the hard-sphere collision diameter is taken constant, the prediction is η∝T​. The collision cross-section of a real atom can vary with speed, so its exact temperature dependence need not follow this power law.

For a reversible adiabatic process of an ideal gas, δQ=0 and dU=−pdV. At fixed composition and constant sample heat capacity at constant volume CV​,
CV​dT=−Vnmol​RT​dV,dlnT=−CV​nmol​R​dlnV.
(30)
Mayer's relation gives nmol​R=Cp​−CV​, so TVγ−1 is constant and
pVγ=constant,γ=CV​Cp​​.​
(31)
The finite-path power law requires approximately constant heat capacities as well as reversibility. When rotational or vibrational populations change appreciably, use ∫CV​(T)dT/T+nmol​RlnV=constant instead; the local isentropic pressure exponent remains γ(T).
For an intact heteronuclear diatomic gas, quantum excitation progressively activates molecular motion. With rotational and vibrational modes frozen, the three translational degrees of freedom give CV,m​=3R/2 and γ=5/3. Two active rotations add R, giving CV,m​=5R/2 and γ=7/5. One active harmonic vibration contributes a further R, through kinetic and potential terms, giving CV,m​=7R/2 and γ=9/7.
For molecular moment of inertia I=μred​re2​, the rigid rotor levels are EJ​=ℏ2J(J+1)/(2I), so the rotational excitation scale is
Θrot​=2IkB​ℏ2​.​
(32)
The first gap corresponds to 2Θrot​, so the crossover occurs at temperatures of that order. For bond force constant ks​ and reduced molecular mass μred​, the vibrational scale is
Θvib​=kB​ℏωvib​​=kB​ℏ​μred​ks​​​.​
(33)
Typically Θvib​≫Θrot​. Real gases may condense at the low-temperature end or dissociate and excite electronic states at the high-temperature end; the three plateau values describe the ideal intact-molecule model.
For the piston apparatus, mechanical equilibrium requires p0​A=pair​A+mg, hence p0​=pair​+mg/A. Let x be a small upward displacement, so V=V0​+Ax. The local isentropic compressibility gives
p−p0​≃−V0​γp0​A​x,mx¨=A(p−p0​)=−V0​γp0​A2​x.
(34)
Consequently the adiabatic gas-spring piston period is
τ=2πγB​​,B=p0​A2mV0​​.​
(35)
This neglects friction, gas inertia, leakage and dissipation, with internal equilibration much faster than the oscillation and external thermal equilibration much slower. The small oscillation probes γ locally; it need not keep γ constant over a large temperature interval.
With pair​=101325Pa, the supplied dimensions give p0​=101351.4Pa and B=2.20566×10−5s2. Thus
γNO​=(0.0251s)24π2B​=1.382,γHe​=(0.0229s)24π2B​=1.660.​
(36)
Helium is close to the monatomic ideal gas value 5/3; NO is close to the diatomic gas value 7/5, with rotations active and vibration largely frozen. The numbers are approximate experimental inferences, rather than exact rational values.

For a single-particle state of energy ϵ, put z=eμ/(kB​T). The equilibrium mean occupancies are
nˉMB​=ze−ϵ/(kB​T),nˉFD​=z−1eϵ/(kB​T)+11​,nˉBE​=z−1eϵ/(kB​T)−11​.​
(37)
The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution is the dilute classical limit. The Fermi-Dirac distribution applies to identical fermions with at most one particle in each complete single-particle state by the Pauli exclusion principle. The Bose-Einstein distribution applies to identical bosons with no such upper occupation bound. The chemical potential fixes total particle number; these are mean state occupancies, not three separately normalized single-particle probability densities. For a number-conserving ideal Bose gas, μ cannot exceed the lowest single-particle energy.
Take k to be a wavevector and count periodic-box states with cell volume (2π)3/V in momentum space, parameterized by k=p/ℏ. Write gs​ for internal degeneracy. The number of states in a spherical shell is gs​V4πk2dk/(2π)3. Since ϵ=αk,
g(ϵ)=2π2α3gs​V​ϵ2,ϵ≥0.​
(38)
Thus the one-particle canonical partition function is
Z1​=∫0∞​g(ϵ)e−βϵdϵ=2π2α3gs​V​β32​=aVTb,a=π2α3gs​kB3​​,b=3.​
(39)
Here β=1/(kB​T), gs​=1 for one nondegenerate branch, and gs​=2 for the spin-half case below. The integral uses ∫0∞​u2e−udu=2. For classical indistinguishable particles, ZN​=Z1N​/N!, giving
U=−∂β​lnZN​=3NkB​T,p=kB​T(∂V∂lnZN​​)T,N​=VNkB​T​.​
(40)
The factor three in the energy arises from the linear dispersion relation and three-dimensional state counting; the usual nonrelativistic quadratic-dispersion value 3NkB​T/2 does not apply.
For spin-half fermions at T=0, all states up to the Fermi energy are occupied. With gs​=2,
N=3π2α3VϵF3​​,ϵF​=α(3π2n)1/3,n=VN​.
(41)
Integrating the occupied energy states gives
U0​=43​NϵF​=43​α(3π2)1/3N4/3V−1/3,p0​=−(∂V∂U0​​)N​=41​nϵF​=3VU0​​.​
(42)
These are the corresponding ultrarelativistic ideal Fermi gas formulae with general slope α. The nonzero ground-state internal energy and degeneracy pressure reflect the Pauli exclusion principle: particles cannot all occupy the lowest-momentum state. Formally extrapolating the classical equations gives U,p→0, but Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics is not valid at fixed positive n as T→0.
At large T, the number equation gives z≃N/Z1​≪1, so expanding the Fermi-Dirac distribution recovers the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. A convenient crossover scale is the Fermi temperature,
T0​∼TF​=kB​α​(3π2n)1/3.​
(43)
For T≫TF​, both gases have p≃nkB​T. At fixed n, the Fermi curve begins at p0​=nϵF​/4, has zero initial slope, lies above the classical line, and approaches it at high temperature. For example its low-temperature expansion is p/(nϵF​)=1/4+(π2/6)(T/TF​)2+⋯. The plot evaluates the finite-temperature occupation integrals at fixed number density.
If the source's symbol k is interpreted literally as momentum rather than a wavevector, the state-space cell is h3/V. In that convention replace α in the wavevector-based coefficients and energy scales above by ℏα. The dimensionless thermodynamics and the relation p=U/(3V) are unchanged.

The Second law of thermodynamics states that total entropy of an isolated composite system cannot decrease. For a cyclic heat engine exchanging heat only with reservoirs at Th​>Tc​>0, it gives −Qh​/Th​+Qc​/Tc​≥0. Since W=Qh​−Qc​,
η=Qh​W​≤1−Th​Tc​​=ηmax​.​
(44)
The bound is attained by a reversible heat engine: no friction, no finite-temperature-difference heat exchange and no other entropy production. A Carnot cycle provides an implementation with two reversible isotherms and two reversible adiabats. All temperatures in these ratios are absolute.
Let nmol​ be the fixed gas amount, r=V2​/V1​>1, ΔT=Th​−Tc​, and let CV​ denote the heat capacity of the whole sample. First take the unregenerated Stirling cycle, so that heat rejected during isochoric cooling is not stored and returned during isochoric heating. The reversible quasistatic gas work is
W12​=nmol​RTh​lnr,W23​=0,W34​=−nmol​RTc​lnr,W41​=0,
(45)
while the heat inputs are Q12​=nmol​RTh​lnr and Q41​=CV​ΔT. Hence
W=nmol​RΔTlnr,η=nmol​RTh​lnr+CV​ΔTnmol​RΔTlnr​<1−Th​Tc​​.​
(46)
The strict inequality follows because CV​ΔT>0 adds heat input without additional work. Quasistatic motion alone does not make heat exchange with a single hot or cold reservoir reversible while the gas temperature changes on an isochore. Indeed, using those two reservoirs gives total external entropy production CV​ΔT(1/Tc​−1/Th​)>0 per cycle.
The strict comparison requires the no-regeneration assumption. A perfect reversible regenerator stores CV​ΔT during 2→3 and returns it during 4→1; only the isothermal heat transfers then enter the external heat budget, giving η=1−Tc​/Th​ exactly. For example, perfect regeneration with Th​=600K, Tc​=300K and r=2 gives η=1/2=ηmax​. Thus the four gas paths by themselves do not enforce an efficiency strictly below the Carnot bound.
For the gas itself, the four entropy changes are
ΔS12​=nmol​Rlnr,ΔS23​=CV​lnTh​Tc​​,ΔS34​=−nmol​Rlnr,ΔS41​=CV​lnTc​Th​​.​
(47)
Expansion increases the available positional states; compression reverses that change. Isochoric cooling reduces thermal populations and isochoric heating restores them. The sum is zero because entropy is a state function. This does not imply zero total entropy production in the gas plus external reservoirs.
Taking S1​ as reference, the isotherms are horizontal on a T–S diagram. The isochore through state 1 obeys S−S1​=CV​ln(T/Th​), and the isochore through state 2 is shifted right by nmol​Rlnr. For the internally reversible quasistatic gas paths, δQ=TdS, so
∮TdS=Qnet​=W.​
(48)
The enclosed area is the net work per engine cycle, with a clockwise orientation. It is not the total heat input.
For the refrigerator, the stated rates give the cooling coefficient of performance C=Q˙​c​/P=1.9/0.1=19. But the absolute reservoir temperatures are Tc​=277.15K and Th​=293.15K, so Carnot's theorem requires
C≤Th​−Tc​Tc​​=17.3219.​
(49)
At 0.1kW input the largest allowed cooling rate is only 1.7322kW. Equivalently the stated 2.0kW heat rejection gives the reservoir entropy rate
293.15K2.0kW​−277.15K1.9kW​=−0.03305WK−1<0,
(50)
a direct violation of the Second law of thermodynamics for this cyclic two-reservoir device.
The unregenerated neon model makes the algebraic obstruction explicit. With CV​/(nmol​R)=3/2, reversing the cycle gives
C=nmol​RΔTlnrnmol​RTc​lnr−CV​ΔT​=ΔTTc​​−2lnr3​.
(51)
Setting this to 19 produces lnr=−0.89385, or r=0.4091, contradicting the required expansion ratio r>1. Perfect regeneration can raise performance only to the Carnot bound and does not fix the problem. There is no physically admissible V2​/V1​ for the stated data.
