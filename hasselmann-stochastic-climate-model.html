Hasselmann stochastic climate model (CT˙=F−λT+η)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Geophysics Climate change Climate model
OurBigBook.com Words: 504
The Hasselmann stochastic climate model represents slowly responding climate variables driven by fast weather fluctuations as well as external forcing. Klaus Hasselmann's 1976 paper separates fast and slow variables, retains fluctuations lost by simple averaging, and explains why stabilizing climate feedbacks are needed to prevent indefinitely growing variability. Its general framework is broader than a single-temperature equation.[ref]
Consider an original scalar specialization for a spatially averaged temperature anomaly T, with an effective ocean mixed layer heat capacity per unit area C:
CdtdT​=F(t)−λT+η(t),C>0,λ>0.
(12)
Here F is imposed radiative forcing, λ is the net restoring coefficient after the chosen climate feedbacks, and η represents random net heat flux from unresolved weather. The anomaly energy per unit area is CT, so its tendency equals the net input. The weather term represents exchange with omitted degrees of freedom; it does not assert that an isolated ocean creates energy. F and η have units Wm−2, and λ has units Wm−2K−1.
First set η=0, start from T(0)=0, and switch to constant F0​>0. The integrating factor eλt/C gives
T(t)=λF0​​(1−e−t/τ),τ=λC​.
(13)
For C=2.0×108Jm−2K−1, λ=2.0Wm−2K−1 and F0​=4.0Wm−2, the relaxation time is 108s, about 3.17 years using 365.25 days per year. At t=τ, T=2(1−e−1)K≃1.264K, with eventual warming 2K. Doubling C doubles the response time but leaves this equilibrium unchanged. This separates thermal inertia from climate sensitivity.
Now set F=0 and idealize the weather forcing as Gaussian white noise with
Eη(t)=0,E[η(t)η(t′)]=2Dδ(t−t′),D>0.
(14)
The Dirac delta function has inverse-time units, so D has units (Wm−2)2s. The rigorous stochastic differential equation is
dT=−Cλ​Tdt+C2D​​dWt​,
(15)
where Wt​ is Brownian motion. This is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. In stationary initialization its variance and autocovariance function are
v∞​=CλD​,RT​(u)=v∞​e−∣u∣/τ.
(16)
Define the two-sided angular-frequency power spectral density by RT​(u)=(2π)−1∫−∞∞​ST​(ω)eiωudω. The Fourier transform of the exponential autocovariance function, or the squared response to a sinusoidal input, gives
ST​(ω)=λ2+C2ω22D​,ST​(0)ST​(ω)​=1+(ωτ)21​.
(17)
Thus fast fluctuations are suppressed; the climate output is red over the frequency range above the corner ∣ω∣=1/τ, with a low-frequency plateau. This is a colored noise spectrum without a periodic driver. The corner is fc​=1/(2πτ) in cycles per second, not 1/τ. The continuous white input is only an approximation on times longer than weather's correlation time.
For the same C,λ and D=108(Wm−2)2s, v∞​=0.25K2 and the stationary standard deviation is 0.50K. From fixed T(0)=0, the variance grows as v∞​(1−e−2t/τ), even when the forced expected value is predictable. Integrating ST​/(2π) over all angular frequencies returns D/(Cλ), checking the normalization independently. If λ=0 the unforced variance instead grows as 2Dt/C2; if λ<0, deviations amplify. Neither case has the stationary spectrum above.
A red spectrum alone cannot establish attribution of recent climate change: internal variability, changing forcing and measurement effects must be distinguished. This scalar model supplies a calculable baseline for variability and persistence, not a universal spectrum for all climate timescales or a full attribution analysis.
