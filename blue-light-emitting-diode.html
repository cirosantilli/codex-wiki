Blue light-emitting diode

OurBigBook.com Words: 626 Articles: 1
Blue transitions motivate wide-bandgap semiconductors, but a large band gap alone cannot guarantee efficient emission. Gallium nitride has a direct band gap; indium gallium nitride composition tunes the transition energy.[ref]
For an emitted photon, with frequency ν and vacuum wavelength λ,
Eγ​=hν=λhc​,λ=Eγ​hc​,
(11)
where h is the Planck constant and c the vacuum speed of light. Multiplying energy by wavelength gives hc=1239.841984eVnm. Thus chosen wavelengths 450nm and 630nm correspond to 2.75520eV and 1.96800eV: blue requires more energy per photon than red. A stipulated transition energy 3.40eV instead gives 364.659nm, in the ultraviolet. These are conversions, not measured emission lines.
For near-edge emission, Eγ​≈Eg​ estimates the photon energy from the emitting material's band gap. Quantum well confinement or impurity transitions can change it. The 1994 blue light-emitting diode used zinc-doped indium gallium nitride, emitting below its near-edge transition.[ref]
The measured forward terminal electric potential difference Vf​ is also not fixed by Eg​/q, where q is the elementary charge. It depends on electric current, temperature, contacts and series electrical resistance. Even the simple Shockley diode equation with series electrical resistance has Vf​=Vj​+IRs​, whereas an optical transition does not acquire an extra energy qIRs​ merely because the contact drop increases. Consequently, extracting a band gap from one terminal-voltage reading is not justified.
