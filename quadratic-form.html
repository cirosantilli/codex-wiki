Quadratic form (Q(av)=a2Q(v))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 64
A quadratic form on a vector space over a field F is a function Q:V→F satisfying Q(av)=a2Q(v) whose polar bilinear form of a quadratic form BQ​(v,w)=Q(v+w)−Q(v)−Q(w) is a bilinear form. In finite dimension, it is represented in coordinates by a homogeneous degree-two polynomial, or by Q(v)=vTAv with a suitable matrix A.
When the characteristic is not two, one may choose A to be a symmetric matrix and recover it from BQ​/2. In characteristic two, the symmetric-matrix restriction would lose examples: Q(x,y)=xy has an upper-triangular representing matrix (00​10​), while vTAv for a symmetric matrix has no xy cross term. Its polar form is BQ​((x,y),(u,v))=xv+yu, an alternating bilinear form. The distinction matters when studying quadratic forms over finite fields.[ref]

Level sets of a positive semidefinite quadratic form (xTAx=E,A≥0)

Words: 91
Quadratic form OurBigBook.com
For a real symmetric matrix A with nonnegative eigenvalues, the finite-dimensional spectral theorem changes the equation to ∑i​λi​qi2​=E in an orthonormal eigenbasis. If E>0 and A has positive rank, the positive-eigenvalue coordinates form an ellipsoid, while the zero-eigenvalue coordinates are arbitrary. The level set is therefore a cylinder over that ellipsoid, with axis directions given by the kernel of a linear map of A. For rank two in three dimensions, it is an elliptic cylinder. At E=0 the level set is exactly the kernel of a linear map; for E<0 it is empty. This distinguishes a positive semidefinite matrix from a positive-definite matrix, whose positive level sets are bounded ellipsoids.

Polar bilinear form of a quadratic form (BQ​(x,y)=Q(x+y)−Q(x)−Q(y))

The displayed bilinear form is the polar form of a quadratic form. In odd characteristic of a field, Q(x)=BQ​(x,x)/2 recovers the quadratic form; in characteristic of a field two, different forms can have the same alternating bilinear form. Over F2​, two such forms differ by a linear map to the field, giving the affine parametrization of binary quadratic refinements.

Two-transitive binary quadratic-form actions (Sp2m​(2)↷Q±(B))

Words: 78
Binary quadratic refinement OurBigBook.com
The symplectic group over a finite field is transitive on each of the two quadratic types. The stabilizer of a refinement is its orthogonal group over a finite field. In the displayed parametrization, other refinements of the same type correspond to nonzero singular vectors, and refinements of opposite type to nonsingular vectors. Witt's lemma makes the orthogonal stabilizer transitive on each of these vector sets. Thus both type actions are two-transitive for m≥2, and a minus stabilizer is transitive on the plus orbit. For m=1 the minus orbit is a singleton.

Principal-axis reduction of a quadric (x0​=−H−1q)

Words: 65
Quadratic form OurBigBook.com
Write a real quadratic polynomial as xTHx+2qTx+c, with H symmetric. If H is invertible, translation by x0​=−H−1q removes the linear term. An orthonormal eigenbasis then makes the remaining quadratic form diagonal. Eigenvalue signs and the translated constant determine the real geometry, such as an ellipsoid or a one-sheet hyperboloid. A singular H requires separate treatment of linear terms in its null directions; a center need not exist.

Matrix congruence (C=PTAP)

Words: 118 Articles: 1
Quadratic form OurBigBook.com
Two square matrices A,C are congruent when C=PTAP for an invertible matrix P. This is the transformation of the matrix of a bilinear form under change of basis, and also transforms a quadratic form under an invertible linear map on its coordinates. The matrices need not be symmetric. It differs from matrix similarity, which uses P−1AP for a linear operator. For real symmetric matrices, completing the square diagonalizes the corresponding quadratic form by matrix congruence.

Positive definiteness of the first form is essential to this construction.

Isotropy of nondegenerate ternary quadratic forms over finite fields

Words: 158 Articles: 1
Ternary quadratic form OurBigBook.com
Over a finite field of odd cardinality q, a diagonal nondegenerate ternary quadratic form Ax2+By2+Cz2 has a zero with z=1: the sets {Ax2} and {−C−By2} both have (q+1)/2 elements and must intersect. This gives a nonzero nonsingular zero. Over a local integer ring with these coefficients units, the Hensel lemma lifts it to an isotropic vector in the fraction field.

Local isotropy of the five seven thirteen form (5x2+7y2+13z2)

Words: 98
Isotropy of nondegenerate ternary quadratic forms over finite fields OurBigBook.com
This ternary quadratic form has a nonzero zero over Qp​ exactly when p=7. Away from 2,5,7,13, the finite-field square-set intersection proof supplies a nonsingular residue zero, and Hensel lemma lifts it. At 5 use (0,1,1) modulo 5; at 13 use (3,1,0). At 2 hold x=2,z=1 and apply the strong form of Hensel lemma to 7Y2+33 at Y=1: the function and derivative have valuations 3 and 1. At 7, the equation modulo 7 forces x=z=0 because 5 is not a square; reduction after dividing by 7 then forces y=0. Any normalized integral nonzero solution would therefore have all coordinates divisible by 7, a contradiction.

Hyperbolic plane (quadratic form, q(xe+yf)=xy)

Quadratic form OurBigBook.com
A hyperbolic plane is a two-dimensional vector space with a quadratic form and a basis e,f satisfying q(e)=q(f)=0 and B(e,f)=1, where B is the polar bilinear form of a quadratic form. Equivalently, q(xe+yf)=xy. This definition also applies in characteristic two. Over the real numbers, the identity xy=((x+y)/2)2−((x−y)/2)2 gives one positive and one negative square, hence signature zero. The signature statement is specific to real quadratic forms.

(1)

Cholesky decomposition (A=LL∗)

Words: 602 Articles: 3
Positive-definite matrix OurBigBook.com
Every real symmetric matrix that is a positive-definite matrix has a unique Cholesky decomposition A=LLT, where L is a lower triangular matrix with positive diagonal entries.[ref] For a complex Hermitian positive-definite matrix, the corresponding factorization is A=LL∗, with L∗ the conjugate transpose and the diagonal of L positive and real.[ref] The factorization turns a linear system into two triangular systems.
For example, matching entries in A=LLT gives
A=(42​25​),L=(ℓ11​ℓ21​​0ℓ22​​),ℓ112​=4,ℓ11​ℓ21​=2,ℓ212​+ℓ222​=5.
(8)
Choose the positive square roots in order: ℓ11​=2, ℓ21​=1 and ℓ22​=2. Therefore
A=(21​02​)(20​12​).​
(9)
The second radicand, 5−12=4, is the scalar Schur complement remaining after eliminating the first variable. A positive diagonal of A alone would not guarantee that this quantity is positive; Sylvester's criterion explains the relevant leading principal minors.
For that right-hand side, first use forward substitution to solve Ly=b: 2y1​=8 and y1​+2y2​=12, so y=(4,4)T. Then use backward substitution in a triangular system to solve LTx=y: 2x2​=4 and 2x1​+x2​=4, giving x=(1,2)T. No matrix inverse is required. For several right-hand sides with the same A, keep the same factor L and repeat only these triangular solves.
Cholesky decomposition as a sum of squares (uTAu=∥LTu∥22​)
Words: 186
Cholesky decomposition OurBigBook.com
For any real vector u, a Cholesky decomposition gives the quadratic form identity
uTAu=uTLLTu=(LTu)T(LTu)=∥LTu∥22​.
(10)
Since L is an invertible matrix, this squared Euclidean norm is positive whenever u=0. The factorization thus certifies positive definiteness and gives coordinates in which the quadratic form is a sum of ordinary squares. For the matrix above,
4u12​+4u1​u2​+5u22​=(2u1​+u2​)2+(2u2​)2.
(11)
The level set uTAu=1 is an ellipse; the linear map z=LTu sends it to the unit circle. The points u=(1/2,0)T and u=(−1/4,1/2)T go to (1,0)T and (0,1)T, respectively.
The same factor works in the reverse direction for Gaussian random vectors. If Z has independent unit-variance, zero-mean Gaussian random variables as coordinates, X=LZ has covariance matrix LLT=A. In this example X1​=2Z1​ and X2​=Z1​+2Z2​: their variances are 4 and 5, while their covariance is 2. This is a concrete way to generate specified correlations from independent draws.
A triangular factor is not generally a square root of a matrix in the sense B2=A. Here L2=(44​04​)=A. The Cholesky decomposition uses the product with the transpose, which is also what makes the quadratic form a squared Euclidean norm.
Cholesky decomposition and geodetic adjustment
Words: 195
Cholesky decomposition OurBigBook.com
André-Louis Cholesky developed his calculation in the setting of geodetic adjustment: measured angles and lengths must satisfy geometric relations, while corrections should be small. The manuscript reproduced in Claude Brezinski's study is titled Sur la résolution numérique des systèmes d’équations linéaires and ends with the date 2 December 1910 at Vincennes. The manuscript reports a 56-equation calculation adjusting altitudes in an Algerian triangulation network. Its date establishes when this written account existed, rather than an exact invention date.[ref]
A related algebraic reason for the method's usefulness is that normal equations involve a Gram matrix. If a real matrix M has linearly independent columns, then
vTMTMv=∥Mv∥22​>0(v=0),
(12)
so MTM is a positive-definite matrix. That gives the structure needed by a Cholesky decomposition. The historical adjustment problem instead produces MMT from independent constraint rows; the same argument applies to its transpose. In numerical least squares, explicitly forming normal equations squares the condition number of a full-column-rank design matrix; a QR decomposition can therefore be preferable for accuracy. Indeed, the extreme singular values give κ2​(MTM)=σmax​(M)2/σmin​(M)2=κ2​(M)2. The factorization remains useful when a positive-definite matrix is already the natural input.

Sylvester's criterion (Δ1​,…,Δn​>0)

Words: 168
Positive-definite matrix OurBigBook.com
A Hermitian matrix A is a positive-definite matrix exactly when its leading principal minors
Δk​=detA1:k,1:k​,k=1,…,n,
(13)
are all positive. The leading principal minor uses the first k rows and the first k columns in the same ordering. For the two-by-two Cholesky decomposition example, Δ1​=4 and Δ2​=4⋅5−22=16, so the criterion proves positive definiteness without calculating eigenvalues.
The successive pivot elements before taking square roots are Δk​/Δk−1​, with Δ0​=1. Here they are 4 and 16/4=4. In general, a leading triangular matrix factor has determinant equal to the product of its diagonal entries, so
Δk​=∏j=1k​ℓjj2​.
(14)
Conversely, positive leading principal minors make each successive Schur complement pivot element positive, allowing the Cholesky decomposition to be constructed. The identity uTAu=∥LTu∥22​ then proves positive definiteness; for complex matrices, replace the transposes by conjugate transposes. This gives a short inductive proof of the criterion.
For B=(12​21​), the first minor is positive but detB=−3. The second Cholesky radicand would be 1−22=−3, and v=(1,−1)T directly gives vTBv=−2<0. Thus positive diagonal entries are insufficient. Replacing “positive” by “nonnegative” in just the leading-minor test is also insufficient for positive semidefiniteness: diag(0,−1) has leading minors 0,0, but its quadratic form is negative on (0,1)T.

Criterion for membership in a diagonal basis

Sylvester's law of inertia OurBigBook.com
A vector v can be included in a diagonal basis for a real quadratic form precisely when v=0 and either its quadratic value is nonzero or it lies in the radical of a bilinear form. Isotropic dimension bound from real inertia (dimU≤min(p,q))

For a nondegenerate real quadratic form of inertia (p,q), projection of a totally isotropic subspace onto either its positive or negative coordinate subspace is injective: a vector with one projection zero would have strictly one-signed quadratic value unless it vanished. Common zero of complex quadratic forms (n≥2d)

Any d quadratic forms on Cn have a common nonzero zero when n≥2d. Choose a totally isotropic subspace for the first form, of dimension at least ⌊n/2⌋.
