Unimodular matrix (U∈GLn​(Z))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 673 Articles: 3
A unimodular matrix is a square integer matrix whose determinant is 1 or −1. The decisive property is that its inverse matrix also has integer entries: multiplication by U gives a bijection of the integer lattice Zn onto itself. Thus an invertible linear map can preserve not only real coordinates but the requirement that every coordinate is an integer. These matrices form the integral general linear group.[ref]
For an original two-dimensional example, take
U=(10​11​),U−1=(10​−11​).
(29)
Its determinant is one, and it maps (a,b) to (a+b,b). Conversely, the integer point (x,y) comes from (x−y,y). For instance, (3,2) has new coordinates (1,2) relative to the columns (1,0) and (1,1). No integer point disappears, although the basis vectors and their fundamental cell change shape.
This is useful whenever coordinates must remain integral. A unimodular change of lattice basis can give a shorter or more convenient description without changing the objects represented. Smith normal form and Hermite normal form use controlled integer operations to simplify integer matrices; ordinary Gaussian elimination may divide by numbers that destroy integrality. The geometric freedom is substantial: a sheared basis may look completely different while describing exactly the same infinite set of integer points.
A determinant of −1 is allowed too. Swapping two basis columns reverses orientation while preserving the integer lattice. The condition is therefore preservation of the complete integer structure, rather than a requirement to preserve orientation.
