Point spread function (PSF)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Physical optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 23
A point spread function is an imaging system's response to an optical point source. Here it means the distribution of image light intensity: an ideal point object becomes a finite spot, often with faint rings, because of diffraction. An unresolved star and a sufficiently small bead emitting fluorescence are practical approximations to an optical point source. Their image tells us how nearby object details will overlap, and reveals blurring from optical aberrations, defocus, and, for an optical telescope, astronomical seeing.
A useful convention separates shape from throughput. At a fixed image plane, let p(x)≥0 be the point spread function with ∫R2​p(x)d2x=1. Its units are inverse area. A point delivering detected radiant flux F at image position x0​ produces light intensity Fp(x−x0​); this point spread function normalization makes the integral over the image equal to F. A profile divided by its own peak is convenient for comparing widths, but is a different point spread function normalization and does not by itself compare total radiant flux.
In incoherent imaging, different object points have negligible mutual spatial optical coherence over the measurement, so their image light intensities add. If the response also obeys translation invariance over the chosen field of view, the expected continuous image is the convolution
I(x)=τ∫R2​O(u)p(x−u)d2u=τ(O∗p)(x).
(1)
Here O is the object brightness distribution expressed in image-scaled coordinates, with magnification already included, and τ is constant transmission. For O(u)=F0​δ(u−u0​), where δ is the two-dimensional Dirac delta function, this gives I=τF0​p(x−u0​). A changing response across the field of view instead requires a position-dependent integral kernel p(x;u); one measured point spread function is then only a local calibration.
In coherent imaging, the superposition principle applies to the complex wave amplitudes. With amplitude spread function h, the corresponding ideal model is
Eimage​=h∗Eobject​,Iimage​=∣Eimage​∣2,p=∫R2​∣h∣2d2x∣h∣2​.
(2)
Thus ∣h∗E∣2 generally differs from ∣h∣2∗∣E∣2: coherent contributions produce wave interference terms that depend on their relative wave phases. Their time average vanishes for mutually incoherent emitters, which explains the intensity convolution. Partial optical coherence requires the correlations between object points as well.[ref]
The figure is an original diffraction model, not measured data. All panels have the same total radiant flux: each member of a pair contributes half the single-source radiant flux. Dashed curves show the separate light intensity contributions; the solid curve is their sum. The point spread functions have tails, so even sources satisfying the Rayleigh criterion overlap. As separation decreases, the image changes continuously; there is no abrupt disappearance of all information at one separation.
A motivating measured example is Measurement of point-spread function (PSF) for confocal fluorescence microscopy by InCheon Song and others (2005). A 543.8nm laser, delivered through an optical fiber, excited fluorescent beads on a slide through a 100×, numerical aperture 1.3 oil-immersion objective. A moving stage scanned the bead; emitted light returned through the objective, an emission filter and a pinhole to a photodiode. The readout was fluorescence light intensity against scanned position. Under the same setup, nominal bead diameters 0.17, 0.5, and 1μm produced progressively broader measured spots. The size comparison controls for the finite object: the measured response is the convolution of bead emission with the effective point spread function, rather than a pure point response. Inverting this convolution model gave a response compared with the theoretical one. The smaller bead gave a weaker signal relative to noise; uncertainty in actual bead diameter also affected the restored width. These are limits of the calibration. In confocal microscopy, excitation and pinhole detection jointly determine the effective point spread function; this experiment does not measure the ideal widefield Airy disk plotted above.[ref]
For a new calibration, useful additional controls are to measure detector background, avoid saturating the photodetector, repeat the scan to check stability, and compare isolated beads at several positions in the field of view. Record wavelength, numerical aperture, pinhole setting, sampling interval and optical focus. These are practical recommendations, not extra measurements claimed for the published experiment.

Airy disk (θ1​≃1.22λ/D)

Words: 998 Articles: 3
Point spread function OurBigBook.com
The Airy disk is the central bright region, up to the first dark ring, in the scalar diffraction pattern of a uniformly illuminated, unobscured circular aperture. The whole pattern includes the weaker surrounding rings; its radius is distinct from its full width at half maximum.
For a circular optical pupil of diameter D under normal, uniform monochromatic plane wave illumination, Fraunhofer diffraction gives
I0​I(θ)​=P(u)=[u2J1​(u)​]2,u=λπDsinθ​,P(0)=1.
(3)
Here I0​ is the on-axis light intensity, θ is the angle from the optical axis, λ is the wavelength in the propagation medium, and J1​ is the order-one Bessel function of the first kind. The paraxial approximation replaces sinθ by θ, measured in radians. The first positive zero j1,1​=3.831705970… gives
sinθ1​=πj1,1​​Dλ​,θ1​≃1.219669891Dλ​≃1.22Dλ​.
(4)
This is the angular radius of the Airy disk; the angular diameter is twice as large. The Airy disk derivation explains the origin of the Bessel function of the first kind and of the numerical coefficient.[ref]
The image and both radial curves show the same calculated light intensity, divided by its peak. The image's logarithmic scale makes the weak rings visible; its colour is a display choice. The linear curve shows how small the ring peaks are compared with the centre. A dark ring marks a zero of wave amplitude, produced by cancellation across the optical pupil; it is not the edge of all transmitted light. The first Airy disk contains about 83.78% of the total radiant flux, as calculated in Airy disk normalization and encircled energy.
For an original design example, take an ideal optical lens in air with focal length f=100mm, circular optical pupil diameter D=5.00mm and wavelength λ=550nm. In the focal plane, the radial coordinate r≃fθ gives
θ1​≃1.34164×10−4rad,r1​≃1.219669891Dλf​=13.4164μm.
(5)
The first-zero diameter is 26.8327μm. Solving P(u1/2​)=1/2 gives u1/2​=1.616339948…, so the diameter measured by full width at half maximum is only
w1/2​=π2u1/2​​Dλf​≃1.028993970Dλf​=11.3189μm.
(6)
Thus a quoted “spot diameter” is incomplete without its definition. Doubling D halves both widths at fixed wavelength and focal length; increasing magnification alone changes the image scale without improving object-space discrimination.
The Airy disk supplies a calculable reference for a point spread function, allowing diffraction to be distinguished from defocus and optical aberrations. Its beauty is that one circular boundary determines an entire sequence of bright and dark rings through a Fourier transform. The ideal formula assumes an unobscured pupil, uniform illumination, ideal optical focus, a single wavelength, and a scalar description with small output angles. A blocked centre, nonuniform pupil transmission or a significant pupil wave phase error changes the response. High numerical aperture may require polarization of an electromagnetic wave and a vector field description. The circular-aperture diffraction laboratory experiment tests the far-field pattern directly; the Rayleigh criterion uses its first zero to define a two-source convention.

Airy disk derivation (A(u)=2J1​(u)/u)

Words: 108
Airy disk OurBigBook.com
Write a=D/2 and wavenumber k=2π/λ. The pupil Fourier transform, normalized by pupil area, gives the Fraunhofer diffraction wave amplitude. In pupil polar coordinates,
A(u)=πa21​∫0a​∫02π​e−ikρsinθcosϕρdϕdρ=2∫01​tJ0​(ut)dt,u=kasinθ.
(7)
The angular average is J0​ because J0​(z)=(2π)−1∫02π​e−izcosϕdϕ. The Bessel function of the first kind identity d{zJ1​(z)}/dz=zJ0​(z) then reduces the radial integral to A(u)=2J1​(u)/u. Taking the squared magnitude yields the Airy disk light intensity profile.[ref]
The apparent division by zero is removable. The power series
J1​(u)=2u​−16u3​+384u5​+O(u7)
(8)
gives A(u)=1−u2/8+u4/192+O(u6) and P(u)=1−u2/4+5u4/192+O(u6). At the centre, every pupil contribution has the same wave phase, so their normalized sum is one. The first dark ring instead corresponds to J1​(u)=0 with u>0, rather than to the removable zero of J1​ at the origin.

Airy disk normalization and encircled energy (E(U)=1−J0​(U)2−J1​(U)2)

Words: 166
Airy disk OurBigBook.com
Peak normalization P(0)=1 does not make the integral of a point spread function equal to one. In the paraxial approximation to the focal plane, define b=πD/(λf) and u=br. The unit-integral point spread function normalization is
p(r)=4πb2​P(br),∫R2​p(r)d2r=1.
(9)
Here p has units of inverse area; multiplying by the detected radiant flux gives image-plane irradiance. Its encircled energy, meaning the fraction inside radius R=U/b, is
E(U)=∫0U/b​2πrp(r)dr=21​∫0U​uP(u)du=1−J0​(U)2−J1​(U)2.
(10)
This is an integral over area, so its radial weight is u. An unweighted integral of a line through the centre is a different quantity.
A short proof uses the Bessel function of the first kind identities J0′​=−J1​ and J1′​=J0​−J1​/u. They give
dUd​(1−J0​(U)2−J1​(U)2)=U2J1​(U)2​=2U​P(U).
(11)
Both expressions for E vanish at zero. Since these Bessel functions of the first kind tend to zero as U→∞, E(∞)=1, establishing the normalization. At the first dark ring,
E(j1,1​)=1−J0​(j1,1​)2≃0.837784869.
(12)
A circular detector region ending at the first zero therefore misses about 16.22% of the ideal radiant flux, even though the surrounding rings have low peak light intensity. This matters when converting measured counts into total source brightness or choosing an image aperture for a photodetector.

Circular-aperture diffraction laboratory experiment

Words: 328
Airy disk OurBigBook.com
An actual teaching setup is described in Laboratory Exercise No. 4: Diffraction, MIT 6.237, Fall 2024, for MIT's 6.237 Modern Optics Project Laboratory, Fall 2024. Preparation includes predicting the aperture-size dependence and the distinction between Fraunhofer diffraction and Fresnel diffraction. A helium–neon laser supplies 632.8nm light; the far-field setup uses a planar incident wavefront, a diffracting object and a distant screen, with beam expansion when needed. Figure 1 also marks an optional CCD camera. Exercise 4.7 asks students to sketch the circular-aperture pattern, measure the Airy disk diameter between correctly identified boundaries, and infer the aperture size. No measured values are supplied. As a regime comparison, exercise 4.9 varies the circular opening's position near a microscope objective optical lens, observes near-field patterns and compares them with the far-field case. This tests whether a change in propagation geometry changes the pattern. The coherent illumination measures one aperture's response, rather than the discrimination of two mutually incoherent optical point sources.[ref]
For a quantitative interpretation, let L be the aperture-to-screen distance and dspot​ the first-zero diameter on the screen. In the paraxial approximation, the independent calculation is
dspot​≃2Lθ1​=2πj1,1​​DλL​,D≃2πj1,1​​dspot​λL​.
(13)
For an explicitly hypothetical measurement, L=1.80m and dspot​=5.60mm at 632.8nm would imply D≃0.496mm. The scale D2/λ≃0.389m is smaller than L, but only by a factor of about 4.6; this alone gives limited confidence in a high-precision Fraunhofer diffraction approximation. It is not a reported MIT measurement.
Additional recommended controls are to keep wavelength fixed, record L, centre and uniformly illuminate the aperture, and repeat with another D. Compare dspot​/L, which should vary inversely with D. A camera readout should include blocked-beam background and avoid saturation. A scaled repetition at larger L tests residual Fresnel diffraction; nonuniform illumination, optical aberrations, detector sampling and uncertainty in locating a dark ring limit the inferred D. The screen pattern's apparent bright edge depends on display brightness and is not a substitute for the first-zero boundary.

Optical transfer function (H(ν)=p​(ν))

Point spread function OurBigBook.com
The optical transfer function is the Fourier transform of the normalized intensity point spread function for incoherent imaging with translation invariance, using spatial frequency in cycles per unit length. It has H(0)=1. For an ideal circular pupil it is the normalized pupil autocorrelation, with cutoff 2NA/λ0​ in object coordinates: no overlap remains when two pupil copies are shifted apart by more than one pupil diameter.[ref]

Diffraction-limited system

Words: 1k Articles: 5
Point spread function OurBigBook.com
A diffraction-limited system has an optical response close to the ideal diffraction response for its specified optical pupil, wavelength and optical focus. Improving the optical aberrations further makes little difference to that response. This describes the optics: a poorly sampled or noisy detector can still deliver an image with much worse usable diffraction-limited resolution.
The optical pupil determines which wave amplitudes and wave phases contribute to an image point. In a scalar, paraxial approximation at the focal plane of an optical lens in air, write the pupil transmission as T(u,v)eiϕ(u,v), after removing the ideal focusing phase. The amplitude spread function is proportional to its scaled Fourier transform:
h(x,y)∝∬T(u,v)eiϕ(u,v)exp[−λf2πi​(ux+vy)]dudv.
(14)
Here (u,v) are pupil coordinates, (x,y) are image coordinates, f is focal length, and λ is the wavelength in air. For a uniformly transmitting, unobscured circular optical pupil at ideal optical focus, the result is an Airy disk with surrounding rings. A blocked centre, support structures, or nonuniform transmission changes the ideal point spread function; “diffraction limited” does not specify one universal spot shape. The existing Fourier model of a Lyot coronagraph illustrates how pupil and focal masks deliberately alter this response.[ref]
Optical aberrations add pupil wave phase errors. Defocus contributes a quadratic pupil wave phase in the paraxial approximation, redistributing light intensity from the best-focused spot. The Strehl ratio compares the peak with the ideal response for the same pupil and total radiant flux. A broad optical spectrum produces a weighted sum of wavelength-dependent point spread functions; chromatic aberration can additionally change optical focus with wavelength. Thus the operating conditions belong with any claim that a system is diffraction limited.

Numerical aperture (NA=nsinα)

Words: 39
Diffraction-limited system OurBigBook.com
The numerical aperture is nsinα, where n is the refractive index of the medium between object and objective, and α is the half-angle of the admitted cone of light. Specify object-side or image-side numerical aperture; the microscope convention usually means the object side.[ref]

Diffraction-limited resolution (θ∼λ/D)

Words: 1k Articles: 3
Diffraction-limited system OurBigBook.com
Diffraction-limited resolution is a scale for distinguishing details with a specified ideal diffraction response and a specified criterion. For two equally bright, mutually incoherent optical point sources imaged through a uniformly transmitting, unobscured circular optical pupil, the Rayleigh criterion places the centre of one Airy disk at the first zero of the other. At small angles in air this separation is
θR​=πj1,1​​Dλ​≃1.22Dλ​,j1,1​≃3.831706.
(15)
Here D is pupil diameter, λ is wavelength in air, and j1,1​ is the first positive zero of the order-one Bessel function of the first kind. This is the radius to the first zero, not the diameter of the central spot. Fraunhofer diffraction gives the radial peak-normalized light intensity
P(q)=[q2J1​(q)​]2,q=λπDθ​,P(0)=1.
(16)
The removable value at zero follows from J1​(q)∼q/2. The pupil Fourier transform reduces to a radial integral by circular symmetry, which produces the Bessel function of the first kind; its first zero supplies the factor 1.22.[ref]
In the usual scalar circular-pupil approximation for a fluorescence microscope, the corresponding object-plane Rayleigh criterion is
rR​=2πj1,1​​NAλ0​​≃0.61NAλ0​​,q=λ0​2πNAr​.
(17)
Here λ0​ is the vacuum emission wavelength, and the object-side numerical aperture already includes the medium's refractive index. Do not divide λ0​ by n again. For small cone angles in air, NA≃D/(2f) and image-plane radius fθR​ agrees with this form. The assumptions include a single wavelength, ideal optical focus, and mutually incoherent emitters. High numerical aperture can require a vector description with polarization of an electromagnetic wave and emitter orientation; confocal microscopy has its own excitation-and-detection response. The same 0.61 formula therefore does not describe every microscope or illumination method.[ref]
In this original model, λ0​=550nm and numerical apertures 0.25 and 0.50 give first-zero radii 1.342μm and 0.671μm. Each point spread function is divided by its own peak to compare shape. The pupil axes are transverse wave vector components multiplied by λ0​/(2π), so pupil radius equals numerical aperture. A wider pupil accepts a wider range of transverse wave vectors, producing a narrower image. Increasing magnification alone enlarges the same spot and does not reduce its object-plane radius.
The Rayleigh criterion is a useful visual convention, not an absolute information barrier. For equal incoherent sources with total detected radiant flux F and separation vector s, the ideal image is
I(x)=2F​[p(x−s/2)+p(x+s/2)].
(18)
Below the Rayleigh criterion this still differs from Fp(x); for small s, symmetry cancels the linear change and the first change is quadratic in separation. Estimating two unknown source positions is then increasingly sensitive to noise, background, relative brightness and uncertainty in the point spread function. Localizing one isolated source and recovering an arbitrary extended object are different inference tasks. Measurements that separate optical modes can also extract separation information differently from direct image light intensity measurement.[ref]
There remains a real limit for arbitrary images in this model: the optical transfer function is zero above the transmitted spatial frequency band. Its cutoff for scalar incoherent imaging is νc​=2NA/λ0​ in object-plane cycles per unit length. The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem therefore suggests object-referred detector pixel pitch ppix​≤λ0​/(4NA), with finer sampling providing margin. Finite detector pixels also integrate over their area, suppressing contrast; coarse sampling can cause aliasing. Finer sampling cannot alone recover spatial frequencies removed by the optics, and Fourier deconvolution amplifies noise where the optical transfer function is small. Photon shot noise, read noise, optical aberrations and defocus can all worsen practical discrimination. A useful resolution claim therefore reports the object model, criterion, wavelength, numerical aperture, sampling and signal-to-noise ratio in photon counting.

Rayleigh criterion (θR​=1.22λ/D)

Words: 549 Articles: 1
Diffraction-limited resolution OurBigBook.com
The Rayleigh criterion calls two equal, mutually incoherent optical point sources just resolved when one circular-pupil Airy disk's centre coincides with the other's first zero. It is a specified discrimination convention, distinct from full width at half maximum and from precision in estimating one source's position.
For this circular optical pupil, the Rayleigh criterion uses angular separation sR​=θ1​≃1.22λ/D, or sR​=r1​≃1.22λf/D in the paraxial approximation to the focal plane. The point spread functions still overlap.[ref]
For the Airy disk design example with D=5.00mm, f=100mm and λ=550nm, the angular convention gives sR​=1.34164×10−4rad, or about 27.67 arcseconds, and the image-plane separation is 13.4164μm. Two equally bright, mutually incoherent optical point sources at separations 0.7, 1 and 1.5 times this scale would have image separations 9.391, 13.416 and 20.125μm respectively. These calculated cases are compared in incoherent two-source Airy profile.
The Rayleigh criterion is useful because it ties an easily recognized two-peak convention to a predictable optical scale. It does not assert that all information vanishes below that separation. Inferring two unknown positions also depends on photon shot noise, background, unequal brightness and uncertainty in the point spread function; locating a single isolated source is a different task. A theoretical analysis shows that alternative optical measurements can estimate the separation of two incoherent optical point sources below this scale.[ref]
The mutual optical coherence condition is essential. In incoherent imaging, time-averaged light intensities add. In coherent imaging, wave amplitudes add before squaring, leaving a wave interference term controlled by relative wave phase. Two holes illuminated by the same coherent laser therefore do not implement the equal-incoherent-source convention merely because their individual patterns resemble Airy disks. The coherent single-aperture laboratory test establishes the response of each aperture; it does not establish the pair's mutual incoherence.
Incoherent two-source Airy profile (Qs​(x)=21​{P(b(x−s/2))+P(b(x+s/2))})
Words: 279
Rayleigh criterion OurBigBook.com
Let x measure position along the line joining two mutually incoherent optical point sources in the focal plane, with equal detected radiant fluxes and separation s. Extend the even Airy disk profile P(u) to signed u, and put b=πD/(λf) as in Airy disk normalization and encircled energy. Relative to the peak of one source carrying the pair's total radiant flux, their summed light intensity is
Qs​(x)=21​P(b(x−s/2))+21​P(b(x+s/2)),bsR​=j1,1​.
(19)
The two-dimensional point spread functions underlying these line profiles all have the same total radiant flux. The line-profile integrals themselves need not equal one.
The dashed curves show individual contributions; the solid curve is their sum. All panels use the same brightness scale, and the vertical markers indicate the true source centres. At s=sR​, the other source contributes zero at each centre, so QsR​​(±sR​/2)=1/2. At the midpoint,
QsR​​(0)=P(j1,1​/2)≃0.367515831.
(20)
The midpoint is therefore about 26.50% below the source-centre peak. This is a calculation for this particular Airy disk and equal-incoherent-source convention, not a universal experimental threshold. At 0.7sR​ the two central peaks merge; at 1.5sR​ the valley is deeper. Changing separation continuously changes the profile, without a discontinuity at the Rayleigh criterion.
To see how information survives when the central peaks merge, set z=bx and δ=bs. A symmetric Taylor expansion gives
Qs​(x)=P(z)+8δ2​P′′(z)+O(δ4).
(21)
The linear terms cancel because the source positions are symmetric. At the midpoint, the Airy disk derivation gives Qs​(0)=1−δ2/16+O(δ4). Thus a close pair's shape differs from that of one source, but only quadratically at leading order. Detecting this small change becomes sensitive to noise and response calibration. For unequal flux fractions α and 1−α, the intensity instead becomes αP(b(x−s/2))+(1−α)P(b(x+s/2)); its valley and peak positions need not match the equal-source example.

Spatial resolution element (Δϑ∼λ/D)

Words: 50
Diffraction-limited resolution OurBigBook.com
A spatial resolution element is one independently resolved angular or spatial scale under a specified resolution criterion. For an ideal telescope of diameter D, its angular scale is of order λ/D and its angular area of order (λ/D)2. A photodetector detector pixel or spaxel can be smaller than a resolution element without adding independent spatial information.

The Strehl ratio compares the peak of an observed point spread function with the ideal diffraction-limited system peak for the same pupil and total flux. It measures how strongly wavefront errors redistribute light away from the central peak.
