Take
D(Δ/2)=Cc∞​(Rd).​
(4)
Part (i) proves that Brownian motion solves this martingale problem. For the converse, let X be any continuous adapted process satisfying this martingale problem and X0​=0.
Set τn​=inf{t≥0:∣Xt​∣≥n}. Choose a smooth cutoff function χn​ equal to one on a neighbourhood of the closed ball of radius n. Apply the martingale problem to χn​(x)xi​ and χn​(x)xi​xj​, and stop at τn​. Inside that ball, their Laplace operators are respectively 0 and 2δij​. The optional sampling theorem therefore shows that
Xt∧τn​i​,Xt∧τn​i​Xt∧τn​j​−δij​(t∧τn​)
(5)
are martingales. Continuity makes τn​↑∞ almost surely. Hence every Xi is a continuous local martingale. The Itô product rule and the fact that a continuous finite-variation local martingale is constant identify its quadratic covariations:
[Xi,Xj]t​=δij​t.
(6)
Here δij​ is the Kronecker delta.
The Lévy characterization of multidimensional Brownian motion now applies. To see explicitly why it identifies the entire law, for ξ∈Rd the Itô formula makes
Et​(ξ)=exp(iξ⋅Xt​+21​∣ξ∣2t)
(7)
a complex local martingale. Its absolute value on [0,T] is at most e∣ξ∣2T/2, so the bounded local martingale criterion makes it a true martingale there. For 0≤s≤t,
E[eiξ⋅(Xt​−Xs​)∣Fs​]=e−∣ξ∣2(t−s)/2.​
(8)
This conditional characteristic function identifies Xt​−Xs​ as having a centered multivariate normal distribution with covariance matrix (t−s)I, independent of Fs​. Together with continuity and X0​=0, these are exactly the defining properties of standard d-dimensional Brownian motion. Thus the martingale problem for Brownian motion has a unique law.

The existence assertion is false under the hypotheses printed in the source. No measurability of θ is specified. Even requiring θ to be a Borel measurable function does not suffice: a coefficient can be positive at every point while approaching zero too rapidly near the starting point. This is the obstruction described by pointwise positivity does not ensure weak existence.
Here is a counterexample in dimension one, with Θ=1:
θ(0)=1,θ(x)=min(x2,1)(x=0).
(9)
Suppose that a solution to the martingale problem with domain Cc∞​(R) existed. The cutoff argument from part (a)(ii), now with L=θΔ, makes X a continuous local martingale with
[X]t​=2∫0t​θ(Xs​)ds≤2t.
(10)
The criterion for a continuous local martingale with bounded quadratic variation makes it a square-integrable martingale, and the Itô isometry gives EXt2​=E[X]t​.
Apply the occupation-times formula with the indicator of the singleton {0}. Since that singleton has zero Lebesgue measure,
0=∫0t​1{Xs​=0}​d[X]s​=2∫0t​1{Xs​=0}​ds.
(11)
Thus X spends zero ordinary time at zero. Away from zero, θ(x)≤x2, so
0≤EXt2​=2E∫0t​θ(Xs​)ds≤2∫0t​EXs2​ds.
(12)
The Gronwall inequality forces EXt2​=0 for every t. Continuity then forces X≡0 almost surely, contradicting either the zero occupation time just proved or [X]t​=2t for that constant path. Consequently
0<θ≤Θ, even Borel measurable, does not ensure existence.​
(13)
For completeness, the intended construction works if θ is a Borel measurable function and bounded away from zero on every compact set. In particular, it works for continuous strictly positive θ. Let B be standard d-dimensional Brownian motion, and define a clock and its inverse by
Au​=∫0u​2θ(Br​)dr​,τt​=inf{u≥0:Au​>t},Xt​=Bτt​​.
(14)
The Brownian path on each finite interval has compact range, so the added hypothesis makes Au​ finite. The clock is continuous and strictly increasing, and Au​≥u/(2Θ), so Au​→∞. Its inverse is continuous and satisfies τt​≤2Θt. Each τt​ is a stopping time for the Brownian filtration.
For f∈Cc∞​(Rd), the optional sampling theorem applied at these bounded random times shows that
f(Bτt​​)−21​∫0τt​​Δf(Br​)dr
(15)
is a true martingale in the filtration Gt​=Fτt​B​. The inverse-clock substitution dτt​=2θ(Xt​)dt, valid almost everywhere, rewrites it as
f(Xt​)−∫0t​θ(Xs​)Δf(Xs​)ds.
(16)
This proves the Brownian time change for a variable diffusion speed:
Xt​=BA−1(t)​ solves the corrected martingale problem.​
(17)
The factor 2 in the clock is necessary because standard Brownian motion has infinitesimal generator Δ/2.

A sufficient condition is that x↦2θ(x)​ is globally Lipschitz. A more familiar, stronger condition implying this is that θ is globally Lipschitz and θ≥ε>0. Both conditions repair the existence issue in part (i).
To connect this to the suggested stochastic differential equation, apply the coordinate and coordinate-product cutoff tests to any solution of the martingale problem. As before they give
[Xi,Xj]t​=2δij​∫0t​θ(Xs​)ds.
(18)
Define, using stochastic integrals against the coordinates of X,
Wti​=∫0t​2θ(Xs​)​1​dXsi​.
(19)
These integrals exist even without a global positive lower bound: their quadratic variations are exactly t. Indeed,
[Wi,Wj]t​=∫0t​2θ(Xs​)1​d[Xi,Xj]s​=δij​t.
(20)
The Lévy characterization of multidimensional Brownian motion identifies W as standard d-dimensional Brownian motion. By associativity of stochastic integration,
dXt​=2θ(Xt​)​dWt​,X0​=0.​
(21)
The diffusion matrix 2θ(x)​I is globally Lipschitz under the stated condition, and boundedness of θ gives bounded growth. The global existence theorem for stochastic differential equations with Lipschitz coefficients gives a unique strong solution of a stochastic differential equation and uniqueness in law. Every solution to the martingale problem has just been represented as a weak solution of a stochastic differential equation of this form, so they all have the same law. Conversely, the Itô formula applied to any f∈Cc∞​ gives a true martingale, since its stochastic integrand is bounded. Thus the martingale problem is well posed under this sufficient condition.

Write Nt​=Mt​−M0​ and Vt​=⟨M⟩t​=[N]t​. The Dambis-Dubins-Schwarz theorem supplies a standard Brownian motion W such that

P(sup0≤u≤R​Wu​≥a)=2P(WR​≥a)⟶1(R→∞).

Write the diffusion and drift coefficients as
σ(x)=1+x21​,b(x)=−2(1+x2)21​.
(44)
Both are continuously differentiable, with
σ′(x)=−(1+x2)22x​,b′(x)=(1+x2)32x​.
(45)
These derivatives are bounded. For example 2∣x∣≤1+x2 bounds both absolute values by one. The mean value theorem therefore makes both coefficients globally Lipschitz. They are also bounded, so satisfy the linear growth condition for an SDE.
The global existence theorem for stochastic differential equations with Lipschitz coefficients consequently gives
a global strong solution, unique up to indistinguishability.​
(46)
In particular, there is no finite explosion time. The solution has continuous paths, so all subsequent level-hitting and stopping arguments apply.
