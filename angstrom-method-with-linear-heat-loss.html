Ångström method with linear heat loss (α=ω/(2ab))

Side losses need not destroy the method. Let θ be the small temperature disturbance about a steady background in surroundings at fixed temperature. For a rod of cross-sectional area S and perimeter P, a uniform real surface heat transfer coefficient hs​ produces a loss per unit volume of Hθ, with H=hs​P/S. Assume constant k,ρ,cp​,H, a cross-section nearly uniform in temperature, and no distributed heat source. The balance becomes
ρcp​∂t​θ=k∂x2​θ−Hθ,∂t​θ=α∂x2​θ−λθ,λ=ρcp​H​≥0.
(18)
Here H has units Wm−3K−1, while λ has units s−1. This is a distributed version of Newton's law of cooling; a constant linearized contribution from thermal radiation may be included over a small temperature range. It does not represent arbitrary time-dependent coupling to a surrounding material with its own thermal storage.
For the decaying exponential branch, the phasor equation gives
γ2=αλ+iω​,a2−b2=αλ​,2ab=αω​,
(19)
so that
a=2αλ2+ω2​+λ​​,b=2αλ2+ω2​−λ​​.
(20)
Real positive side loss increases a and decreases b at fixed angular frequency and thermal diffusivity. Their product eliminates λ, yielding the combined inference
α=2abω​=2log(A1​/A2​)ΔϕωΔx2​=2log(A1​/A2​)ΔtΔx2​​,Δt=ωΔϕ​.
(21)
This result requires that attenuation and lag belong to the same exponential complex wavenumber at the same frequency, and that the loss term is real. It does not generally survive a superposition caused by a nearby finite end, spatially varying coefficients, transverse temperature gradients, or unequal sensor delays. The inferred λ=α(a2−b2) supplies an additional model check; a negative value signals inconsistency with this passive-loss model rather than negative physical cooling.
