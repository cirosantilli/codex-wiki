Tracking error (e(t)=r(t)−ym​(t))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Control theory Closed-loop control
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 1
The tracking error used by the controller is the signed difference
e(t)=r(t)−ym​(t),
(13)
between the reference signal and the measured output. A positive tracking error means the readout is below the desired value; a negative tracking error means it is above. With exact measurement ym​=y, r=1 and y=1.1 give e=−0.1. For a positive-response controlled plant, positive feedback gain in proportional control u=kp​e then reduces the plant output. The sign of the controller must also account for the sign of the controlled plant's response: a positive command that reduces the plant output requires a different sign choice. Kantor uses the opposite tracking error convention, PV−SP, together with a minus sign in his controller; translating both signs gives the convention here. [ref]
Distinguish the controller's tracking error from the physical plant output discrepancy ε=r−y. If the readout has constant sensor bias b, then
ym​=y+b,e=ε−b,ε=e+b.
(14)
All four quantities r,y,ym​,e have the same units under dimensional analysis; a sensor bias is expressed in those units too. A readout that is too high by 0.2∘C can make e=0 when the physical temperature is 0.2∘C below the goal. Integral action integrates the tracking error it receives; it cannot infer this missing calibration information from that tracking error alone. Thermometer calibration against an independent reference addresses a different problem from tuning a feedback gain.
A motivating apparatus/model comparison is Park, Martin, Kelly and Hedengren's temperature-control laboratory: two TIP31C heaters with attached TMP36GZ sensors and heat sinks, an Arduino, a power supply, and a USB-connected computer. The readout converts sensor voltage to temperature; software records that measured output while specifying heater commands from 0 to 100%. For the single-heater test, one command changes in steps and the other heater stays off. Parameters are fitted to one run, then a different command sequence tests predictions without feeding measured temperatures into the simulation. The authors observe that their richer thermal model predicts the response better than the approximation with a single time constant and a delayed control input, especially after command changes. The real sensor has its own thermal lag. They also repeat the same command profile at different ambient temperatures and find changes in the response, motivating ambient-condition reporting. This experiment measures a command-to-readout response, not independently the exact heater temperature or delivered power. [ref]
The lesson for tracking error is to record three traces during a tracking test: the reference signal, the measured output, and the applied control input. Their first difference is the tracking error; a model prediction minus a readout is instead a model discrepancy. In a proposed comparison of proportional control and proportional-integral control, keep the initial temperature, ambient conditions, heater limits, and target changes fixed; run the second heater separately as a known control disturbance. Independent thermometer calibration is needed to assess physical accuracy. These are suggested controls, not additional experiments claimed for the paper. A sensor's lag, measurement noise, actuator saturation, and changed ambient conditions can all invalidate conclusions drawn from the ideal first-order model.
