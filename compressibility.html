Compressibility (κX​=−V−1(∂V/∂P)X,N​)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 755 Articles: 6
Compressibility measures how readily a sample's volume decreases when its pressure increases. For a simple compressible system with fixed particle number N and composition, specify the constraint X:
κX​=−V1​(∂P∂V​)X,N​,VdV​=−κX​dP(X,N fixed).
(35)
Here P denotes pressure, also written p elsewhere. The minus sign makes compressibility positive on a mechanically stable equilibrium branch. Its units are inverse pressure, Pa−1; its reciprocal KX​=1/κX​ is the constrained bulk modulus, measured in pascal units. Holding temperature fixed gives isothermal compressibility; holding entropy fixed gives isentropic compressibility κS​ along an isentropic process. The latter describes a reversible adiabatic compression, since an irreversible adiabatic process can increase entropy. [ref]
For an ideal gas, the ideal gas law PV=NkB​T and the reversible ideal-gas adiabat give, at the same equilibrium state,
κT​=P1​,κS​=γP1​,γ=CV​CP​​>1.
(36)
Here CP​ and CV​ are the whole sample's heat capacity at constant pressure and heat capacity at constant volume, and γ is the heat capacity ratio. Constant γ is required for the finite-path formula PVγ=constant. Reversible adiabatic compression raises the temperature, making the pressure rise more steeply than in an isothermal compression. Thus keeping entropy fixed makes the gas harder to compress locally. For a classical monatomic gas at 100kPa, γ=5/3, so κT​=10−5Pa−1 and κS​=6×10−6Pa−1; the corresponding values of the bulk modulus are 100kPa and 166.67kPa.
For a smooth, stable single thermodynamic phase at positive temperature, Maxwell relations also imply
κS​κT​​=CV​CP​​,κT​−κS​=CP​TVα2​,
(37)
where α is the coefficient of thermal expansion. The ideal gas result is one example of this broader link between mechanical and thermal responses. The equality κT​=κS​ is possible when α=0 and the other responses remain finite. These formulas compare equilibrium partial derivatives, rather than arbitrary fast or dissipative compression histories. [ref]
