Apparent depth (dpar​=(nobs​/nobj​)d)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Refraction
OurBigBook.com Words: 390
A submerged object looks shallower because its emerging light rays bend away from the surface normal vector. Their backward extensions give an approximate virtual image nearer the surface. For an object at perpendicular depth d in a medium of refractive index nobj​, viewed through a flat boundary from a medium of refractive index nobs​, the near-normal paraxial approximation gives dpar​=(nobs​/nobj​)d. Thus the model water depth 12cm appears approximately 9cm from air. This is a bundle-of-light rays statement, not a deflection of the axial light ray.[ref]
For general transmitted light rays, specify what “depth” means. Put the object at (0,−d) below a horizontal boundary z=0. A light ray reaches the boundary at (x,0), with object-side angle θobj​ and observer-side angle θobs​, both measured from the upward normal vector. For x>0, its backward extension meets the object's normal line x=0 at z=−daxis​, where
x=dtanθobj​,nobj​sinθobj​=nobs​sinθobs​,daxis​=tanθobs​x​=dnobj​cosθobj​nobs​cosθobs​​.
(17)
This axis-intercept depth is exact for that one transmitted light ray in the stated model. It tends to dpar​ as the angles tend to zero; at exactly zero the quotient is interpreted as a limit. These elementary tangent, sine and cosine relations show precisely where the small-angle approximation enters.

At appreciable obliquity these depths differ, so the full outgoing bundle has no single exact virtual image on the object's normal line. An oblique eye samples a narrow bundle; its local apparent position also depends on that bundle and cannot be inferred by declaring daxis​ to be a universal image depth. A plane refracting boundary gives approximate image formation near normal incidence, with departures at larger angles. The construction assumes a flat surface, uniform transparent media with isotropy, and a propagating transmitted light ray; it does not extend to total internal reflection.
