L-infinity norm (∥f∥∞​=esssupμ​∣f∣)

On a measure space of positive total measure, the L-infinity norm of an essentially bounded measurable function is ∥f∥∞​=esssupμ​∣f∣. The absolute value matters: a constant function f=−3 has L-infinity norm three, even though its essential supremum is −3. The norm belongs to the equivalence class in Lp space; different representatives equal almost everywhere give the same answer. [ref].
For the function g in essential infimum and essential supremum, ∥g∥∞​=1 under Lebesgue measure, whereas its supremum norm is four. Replacing the exceptional values by the ordinary values x leaves every finite Lp norm unchanged as well: ∥g∥p​=(∫01​xpdx)1/p=(p+1)−1/p. This tends to one as p→∞. That agreement explains why the endpoint with exponent p=∞ uses an essential bound rather than a pointwise bound.
A finite probability space makes the distinction between zero and small measure explicit. Let f(a)=1, f(b)=3, and f(c)=10. Assign weights μ(a)=1/2−ε, μ(b)=1/2, μ(c)=ε, where 0≤ε<1/2. For 1≤p<∞,
∥f∥p​=((1/2−ε)+21​3p+ε10p)1/p.

When ε=0, the singleton {c} is a null set and ∥f∥∞​=3. For every ε>0, however small, ∥f∥∞​=10. For example, ε=0.001 gives ∥f∥1​=2.009 and ∥f∥2​=5.099​, yet the large-p Lp norms approach ten. The ordinary pointwise supremum norm is ten in both cases.
Why can a rare value dominate? For a bounded function on a probability space, an almost everywhere bound ∣f∣≤M gives ∥f∥p​≤M. If 0<t<∥f∥∞​, the set At​={∣f∣>t} has positive probability, so ∥f∥p​≥tμ(At​)1/p. As p→∞, the factor μ(At​)1/p tends to one. These two bounds squeeze the limit to ∥f∥∞​. The probability measure assumption keeps total measure finite; this explanation does not assert the same finite-p integrability on arbitrary infinite-measure spaces.
