Use the continuous convention 0log2​(0/q)=0. Let S={r:pr​>0}. Applying the allowed logarithm inequality to qr​/pr​ for r∈S gives
−d(p,q)=∑r∈S​pr​log2​pr​qr​​≤c∑r∈S​(qr​−pr​)=c(∑r∈S​qr​−1)≤0.
(1)
Hence d(p,q)≥0. Equality forces ∑r∈S​qr​=1. Since every qr​ is strictly positive, this makes S the entire alphabet. Equality in every weighted logarithm inequality then forces qr​/pr​=1 for every r. Conversely p=q makes every summand zero. Thus
d(p,q)≥0,d(p,q)=0⟺p=q.​
(2)
This is Gibbs inequality for relative entropy, with zero probabilities in the first argument handled explicitly.

The given logarithmic moment is
ℓ=n(n+1)2​∑r=1n​rlog2​r,
(9)
where the r=1 term is zero. It is attained by the positive distribution
qr​=n(n+1)2r​,
(10)
whose probabilities sum to one. Write A=2/[n(n+1)], so log2​qr​=log2​A+log2​r. For any distribution p with the required logarithmic moment,
d(p,q)=−H(p)−log2​A−ℓ=H(q)−H(p).
(11)
Consequently the unique maximizer and its entropy are
pZ​(r)=n(n+1)2r​,H(Z)max​=log2​2n(n+1)​−ℓ.​
(12)
The increasing weights are consistent with the prescribed moment; they need not be a decreasing power law.

Write wi​=(Aq)i​. For each row put Si​={j:aij​>0}; the positive row entries αi​=(aij​)j∈Si​​ form a probability distribution because the row sum is one. When wi​>0, define the probability distribution

(13)

i:wi​>0∑​wi​d(ri​,αi​)​=i,j∑​aij​qj​log2​wi​qj​​=j∑​qj​log2​qj​i∑​aij​−i∑​wi​log2​wi​=H(w)−H(q).​

H(W)−H(V)=i:wi​>0∑​wi​d(ri​,αi​)≥0.​

A binary code assigns a finite binary word ck​ to each source symbol xk​. In the ordinary unframed convention these codewords are nonempty. It is uniquely decodable when concatenation gives an injective map from finite symbol strings to bit strings:
ci1​​⋯cir​​=cj1​​⋯cjs​​⟹r=s and it​=jt​ for all t.​
(16)
The decoder can recover the entire symbol sequence without being told where codewords end. This is stronger than merely assigning distinct words to individual symbols.

Solution
Words: 68
ii OurBigBook.com
An instantaneous code can decode each symbol as soon as its final bit arrives, without waiting for later bits. Equivalently it is prefix-free: no codeword is a proper prefix of another, and different symbols receive different codewords.
For nonempty codewords, decoding greedily from the start of the bit string identifies the first codeword unambiguously; repeating identifies the whole sequence. Thus an instantaneous code is uniquely decodable under the ordinary nonempty-word convention.

The codeword lengths are integers. For necessity, take L=maxk​lk​ and view codewords as nodes of the binary tree. A codeword of length lk​ has 2L−lk​ descendant words at depth L. Prefix-freeness makes these descendant sets disjoint, so
∑k​2L−lk​≤2L,hence∑k​2−lk​≤1.
(17)
For sufficiency, reorder the lengths nondecreasingly. Assign words successively. At depth lk​, words extending previously assigned codewords number
∑i<k​2lk​−li​.
(18)
The asserted inequality, even after dropping the later nonnegative terms, gives
∑i<k​2lk​−li​+1≤2lk​.
(19)
Thus at least one depth-lk​ word does not extend an earlier word. Choose it. Since lengths are nondecreasing, it cannot be a proper prefix of a shorter earlier word, and equal-length duplicates have been excluded. Induction constructs the required code. Therefore Kraft inequality is necessary and sufficient:
a binary prefix code with these lengths exists⟺k∑​2−lk​≤1.​
(20)

First consider any instantaneous code. Put K0​=∑k​2−lk​≤1 and qk​=2−lk​/K0​. Part 1(a) gives
0≤d(p,q)=−H(X)+EL+log2​K0​,
(21)
so EL≥H(X)−log2​K0​≥H(X).
For the construction, take the Shannon lengths lk​=⌈−log2​pk​⌉. Then 2−lk​≤pk​, so Kraft's condition holds. Also lk​<−log2​pk​+1 for every k. Averaging gives
H(X)≤EL<H(X)+1.​
(22)
For a nontrivial alphabet all pk​<1, so these lengths are positive and the ordinary code convention applies. For a deterministic one-symbol source, the usual zero-bit convention uses l1​=0 when the number of source symbols is externally known. If strictly nonempty codewords and arbitrary unframed message lengths are required instead, that degenerate case has EL≥1 and cannot satisfy the printed strict upper bound. This convention issue does not affect sources with at least two symbols.

Let Fk​=∑r<k​pr​ and let Jk​=[Fk​,Fk​+pk​) be the probability cell of symbol k. Its midpoint is Gk​=Fk​+pk​/2. Use the binary expansion terminating in zeros at a dyadic rational, so truncation to lk​ bits gives
ak​=2−lk​⌊2lk​Gk​⌋.
(23)
The chosen codeword corresponds to the dyadic interval Dk​=[ak​,ak​+2−lk​). It contains Gk​, and its width satisfies 2−lk​≤pk​/2. Consequently
ak​>Gk​−2−lk​≥Fk​,ak​+2−lk​≤Gk​+2−lk​≤Fk​+pk​.
(24)
Thus Dk​⊆Jk​. The probability cells are disjoint, so the code intervals are disjoint. If one binary codeword were a prefix of another, its dyadic interval would contain the other's interval, contradicting this disjointness. Hence the code is instantaneous.
Finally, lk​=⌈−log2​pk​⌉+1<−log2​pk​+2. Multiplying by pk​ and summing yields
EL<H(X)+2.​
(25)
This is the Shannon-Fano-Elias code: the extra bit makes the dyadic interval narrow enough to remain inside the cell around its midpoint.

For finite-valued random variables, their mutual information is
I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)−H(X,Y)=H(Y)−H(Y∣X)=H(X)−H(X∣Y).​
(26)
Equivalently,
I(X;Y)=∑x,y​p(x,y)log2​pX​(x)pY​(y)p(x,y)​,
(27)
with zero joint-probability terms omitted. It is the relative entropy of the joint law from the product of its marginals and is nonnegative, with equality exactly when the two variables are independent.

A discrete memoryless channel has finite input and output alphabets and fixed conditional probabilities Mij​=P(Y=j∣X=i), whose rows sum to one. Memorylessness means that for successive uses, conditional on the input sequence, the output probabilities factor as the product of these one-use conditional probabilities.
Its information capacity in bits per use is
C=pX​max​I(X;Y)=pX​max​[H(Y)−i∑​pX​(i)H(Mi⋅​)].​
(28)
The maximization varies the input distribution while keeping the channel matrix fixed. The output distribution is pX​M in the row-vector convention.

Let B be the selected block, and write α=P(B=1). Because both the input and output alphabets are disjoint across blocks, B is determined by either the input or the output. The mutual-information chain rule therefore gives
I(X;Y)=H(B)+I(X;Y∣B)≤h2​(α)+αC1​+(1−α)C2​.
(29)
For any fixed α, equality is achievable by using a capacity-achieving distribution inside each block. It remains to maximize this binary expression.
Put S=2C1​+2C2​ and r=2C1​/S. A direct expansion of binary relative entropy yields
h2​(α)+αC1​+(1−α)C2​=log2​S−d((α,1−α),(r,1−r)).
(30)
Part 1(a) makes the maximum log2​S, uniquely at α=r. Hence
C=log2​(2C1​+2C2​),P(B=i)=2C1​+2C2​2Ci​​.​
(31)
The block choice itself communicates information, in addition to any information sent within the selected block.
This is capacity of a disjoint sum of channels.

There are three distinguishable output groups: columns {1,2} can arise only from input one; column {3} can arise from inputs two or three, whose rows are identical; and columns {4,5} can arise only from input four.
Each group has zero internal channel capacity. A block with one input cannot carry a variable input choice, and the middle block has identical output laws for both of its inputs. Combining the three disjoint zero-capacity blocks therefore gives
CN​=log2​(1+1+1)=log2​3 bits per use.​
(32)
Equivalently, the output identifies exactly which of these three groups was used, so mutual information is the entropy of that group label. It is maximized when all group probabilities are one third. The capacity-achieving input distributions are precisely
p1​=p4​=31​,p2​+p3​=31​.
(33)
Splitting the middle probability arbitrarily cannot communicate an extra distinction because its two channel rows agree.

For any chosen input distribution, the chain rule and nonnegativity of conditional mutual information give
I(U;V,W)=I(U;V)+I(U;W∣V)≥I(U;V),
(34)
and likewise I(U;V,W)≥I(U;W). Choosing an input distribution attaining either marginal capacity proves K≥max{K1​,K2​}.
For the upper bound, conditional independence implies H(V,W∣U)=H(V∣U)+H(W∣U), while entropy subadditivity gives H(V,W)≤H(V)+H(W). Therefore
I(U;V,W)​=H(V,W)−H(V,W∣U)≤H(V)+H(W)−H(V∣U)−H(W∣U)=I(U;V)+I(U;W)≤K1​+K2​.​
(35)
Maximizing over the input law gives
max{K1​,K2​}≤K≤K1​+K2​.​
(36)
More precisely, under the conditional-independence assumption,
I(U;V,W)=I(U;V)+I(U;W)−I(V;W).
(37)
The outputs may be dependent through the common input, and this final term measures the information they share rather than add independently.
This is information from two conditionally independent observations.
