Lyapunov moment inequality (∥Z∥r​≤∥Z∥s​(0<r<s))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability inequality
OurBigBook.com Words: 349
The Lyapunov moment inequality says that higher positive absolute moments give no smaller absolute moment sizes on a probability space. For a real random variable Z and 0<r<s,
(E∣Z∣r)1/r≤(E∣Z∣s)1/s,​
(1)
allowing an infinite value. For r≥1 the left expression is an Lp norm; below one it is a quasi-norm, so the usual triangle inequality terminology needs that distinction. The comparison uses a probability measure of total mass one; arbitrary unnormalized measure spaces need a factor depending on their total mass.
For a nonnegative count, the first moment size is its expected value, while the second is the square root of its second moment. Both ten-trial counts in second moment method have first moment size one. The independent count has second moment size 1.9​≈1.37840, whereas the shared-outcome count N=10B has second moment size 10​≈3.16228. The second moment distinguishes models with the same average because its square increases the influence of rare large counts.
More generally, for N=10B with a Bernoulli random variable B of parameter 1/10,
(ENr)1/r=10(1/10)1/r.
(2)
It rises from one at r=1 toward ten as r→∞. Replacing 1/10 by a parameter 0<q≤1 gives 10q1/r, which decreases toward zero for a fixed positive exponent when q↓0, making the rare-event and large-exponent limits different questions. For a scaled Rademacher random variable taking values −2 and two with equal probabilities, every absolute moment size is exactly two: increasing the exponent does not distinguish signs when the absolute value is constant.
For finite E∣Z∣s, apply the reversed Jensen's inequality to the strictly concave function tr/s and the nonnegative random variable ∣Z∣s:
E∣Z∣r≤(E∣Z∣s)r/s.
(3)
Taking the positive rth root proves the claim. Equality in this finite case holds exactly when ∣Z∣ is constant almost surely. If the higher moment is infinite, the upper bound is infinite and remains valid. Exponent zero is excluded from the statement; logarithmic or geometric limiting summaries require separately stated conventions, especially when Z can vanish.
