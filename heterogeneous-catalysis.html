Heterogeneous catalysis

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Catalysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 4
In heterogeneous catalysis, the catalyst and reactants occupy different thermodynamic phases; a solid surface can furnish the catalytic route.
For example, specify dissolved hydrogen peroxide as the reactant, water as the solvent, and solid manganese dioxide as the catalyst. The solid and liquid chemical solution are separate thermodynamic phases. A dispersion that looks thoroughly mixed still contains those separate phases; mixing distributes contact, rather than making the solid molecularly dissolved. The manganese dioxide peroxide contact demonstration makes the arrival of the liquid at the solid tangible.
A useful surface-route picture is approach from the liquid, adsorption at a catalytic surface site, conversion through surface-bound reaction intermediates, and release of reaction products. [ref] This sequence organizes possible roles; it is not a verified sequence of elementary reactions for every catalyst, or for the particular manganese dioxide demonstration. The net chemical equation supplies neither the binding geometry nor the identity of the working catalytic surface site.
This contact requirement makes surface area and diffusion practically important. More exposed solid can provide more catalytic surface sites, whereas diffusion through the surrounding liquid can limit their supply of reactants. Changing stirring or particle size can therefore change the observed reaction rate without identifying the microscopic rate constant. A recoverable solid is attractive for reuse, but separation alone does not establish retained activity: compare with catalyst deactivation.
