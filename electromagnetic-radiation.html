Electromagnetic radiation

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism
OurBigBook.com Words: 8k Articles: 59
Electromagnetic radiation is a propagating disturbance of the electric field and magnetic field that carries energy and momentum away from its source. It can be a sustained electromagnetic wave or a finite wave packet: periodic oscillation is not required. In vacuum the disturbance travels at the speed of light. A receiver responds to fields arriving now from an earlier source event, as expressed by retarded time. This is how electrical activity at one place can deliver energy and information elsewhere without a connecting wire.
Stored electromagnetic energy alone does not establish outgoing electromagnetic radiation. A stationary electric charge in electrostatics, or a steady localized electric current in magnetostatics, can store electromagnetic energy with zero Poynting vector. Static combinations can have circulating energy flux without net outward power. Near a changing source, reactive energy can return later. The near and far field distinction separates these contributions from outgoing radiation zone fields.[ref]
A single traveling vacuum plane electromagnetic wave obeying the Maxwell equations has B=k×E/c. Its Poynting vector gives the energy density and flux of a plane electromagnetic wave:
S=μ0​E×B​=ϵ0​c∣E∣2k,I=⟨S⟩⋅k=2cϵ0​E02​​.
(1)
Here I is wave intensity; ϵ0​ and μ0​ are vacuum permittivity and vacuum permeability. For a monochromatic wave with linear polarization and peak amplitude E0​, squared cosine averages to 1/2. Pulses require the instantaneous Poynting vector; arbitrary incident-and-reflected superpositions need not obey this relation.[ref]
The electromagnetic momentum density is g=S/c2. Completely absorbing a unidirectional vacuum pulse of energy U transfers momentum U/c; see radiation pressure on a perfectly absorbing surface. A calculated 1.0mJ pulse transfers 3.34×10−12kgm/s. Symmetric emission can lose energy without net recoil because opposite momenta cancel.[ref]
The direction of emission matters as much as its total power. For an oscillating electric dipole moment along z, the angular distribution of electric dipole radiation is strongest across the axis and vanishes along it. Its origin is geometric: the electric dipole radiation field is the transverse projection of the second time derivative of the electric dipole moment, so its magnitude contains sinθ. Squaring this electric field to obtain wave intensity gives sin2θ. The electric-dipole approximation for radiation requires a source much smaller than its wavelength and observation in the radiation zone. [ref]
For example, the power per solid angle at θ=30∘ is one quarter of its equatorial value, while the electric field amplitude is one half. To normalize the angular distribution to total power, use spherical coordinates with dΩ=sinθdθdϕ:
∫sin2θdΩ=2π∫0π​sin3θdθ=38π​,P1​dΩdP​=8π3​sin2θ.
(2)
The integral follows by substituting x=cosθ, giving ∫−11​(1−x2)dx=4/3. This is a probability-like normalization over direction, with units of inverse steradian; it does not make the dipole an isotropic radiator. The same angular integral underlies the Larmor formula for nonrelativistic accelerated electric charge.
Detection requires the incident electromagnetic field to do something observable. An antenna develops an electrical response; a thermopile converts absorbed energy into a thermal and then electrical signal; a photodetector can respond through photon absorption. Hertz spark transmission and detection experiment makes this conversion visible as a tiny spark. Detector orientation, sensitivity, and surrounding reflections all matter: failure to see a signal does not prove that no electromagnetic radiation reached the receiver.

Radiation spectrum

Words: 116 Articles: 2
Electromagnetic radiation OurBigBook.com
The distribution of emitted or received electromagnetic power, radiative flux or specific intensity across frequency or wavelength. The spectral quantity and interval convention must be specified: Fν​ and Fλ​ differ by the change-of-variable factor, while νFν​ gives energy flux per logarithmic frequency interval. This physical spectrum is distinct from the spectrum of a linear operator or a spectrum in topology.

Power-law spectrum (Fν​∝ν−α)

Words: 60 Articles: 1
Radiation spectrum OurBigBook.com
A radiation spectrum whose spectral flux density follows a power law over a stated frequency range. For Fν​∝ν−α, the spectral energy distribution satisfies νFν​∝ν1−α. Thus α=1 gives equal energy flux in equal logarithmic frequency intervals. A spectral break invalidates extrapolation with a single index.

Radiation spectral index (α=−dlogFν​/dlogν)

Words: 26

The local logarithmic slope with the displayed sign convention. Other fields sometimes use the opposite sign, so an index should be accompanied by its defining power-law spectrum.

Radiation reaction

Words: 308 Articles: 3
Electromagnetic radiation OurBigBook.com
Radiation reaction is the influence on a source's motion of its own electromagnetic field, including the balance of radiated energy and momentum with changes of the near field. For sufficiently weak radiation loss, an averaged power balance can give the leading damping law without solving the full self-field dynamics. Replacing a constant-frequency radiation formula by its instantaneous slowly varying value requires an additional time-scale assumption; it is not an exact treatment of arbitrarily rapid motion changes.

Stationary no-runaway response and preacceleration

Vacuum frequency-wavelength relation (νλ=c)

Words: 111
Electromagnetic radiation OurBigBook.com
A monochromatic wave in vacuum has frequency ν and wavelength λ related by νλ=c, where c is the speed of light. During one oscillation period T=1/ν, a constant-wave phase surface travels one wavelength, so λ=cT. Equivalently, the plane electromagnetic wave relation ω=ck with angular frequency ω=2πν and wavenumber k=2π/λ gives the same result.
As a calculated example, a vacuum radio wave with ν=300MHz=3.00×108Hz has λ=0.999308m using c=299792458m/s. Doubling frequency halves wavelength, independently of wave amplitude and wave intensity. The frequency spectrum of a pulse contains multiple frequencies; the relation applies to each vacuum component, rather than assigning one exact wavelength to the whole pulse. In a homogeneous medium replace c by the appropriate phase velocity; the vacuum equation does not assert that all media have the same wavelength at a given frequency.

Outgoing radiation pulse (Sr​(r,t)=Pemit​(t−r/c)/(4πr2))

Words: 360
Electromagnetic radiation OurBigBook.com
An outgoing radiation pulse carries a finite amount of electromagnetic energy through successively larger enclosing spheres. In vacuum, far from a localized source and without absorption or additional reflections, let Pemit​(t) be the emitted power. The retarded time at radius r is s=t−r/c. Define Sr​ as the angular average of the radial Poynting vector over the sphere, not a cycle average:
Sr​(r,t)=4πr2Pemit​(t−r/c)​,u(r,t)=cSr​(r,t)​,drdU​=4πr2u=cPemit​(t−r/c)​.
(5)
Here u is the angularly averaged energy density in the leading radiation zone approximation. Multiplying by shell area removes geometric dilution: the energy per unit radial thickness translates outward without losing its shape. These angular averages do not assume that an actual electric dipole emits equally in all directions. Nor do they specify an exact spherically symmetric transverse solution of the Maxwell equations.
For an explicitly stipulated model, emit
Pemit​(s)=P0​g(s/τ),g(q)={sin2(πq),0,​0≤q≤1,otherwise,​U=∫Pemit​(s)ds=2P0​τ​.
(6)
After emission ends, t>τ, the pulse lies between r=c(t−τ) and r=ct, provided this shell is in the radiation zone. Its radial thickness remains cτ. Substituting s=t−r/c in the shell integral ∫4πr2udr gives U=P0​τ/2 at every such time. The same substitution in the time integral of 4πr2Sr​ at any enclosing sphere gives that same total crossing energy. This is conservation of energy in two different views.
Along any fixed direction with nonzero outgoing power, compare the same part of the pulse at two radii, hence at different arrival times. The leading electric field amplitude falls as 1/r, while its squared wave intensity falls as 1/r2. At 2r the field is one half and the energy flux one quarter; the signal arrives r/c later. A small fixed area receiver therefore intercepts less energy at greater distance even though every enclosing sphere receives the same total pulse energy. The inverse-square law expresses spreading over area, rather than destruction of energy. Close to the source, the neglected near and far field terms prevent extrapolating these formulas down to r=0.

Hertz spark transmission and detection experiment

Words: 259
Electromagnetic radiation OurBigBook.com
In his 1888 reflection experiments, Heinrich Hertz excited a straight conductor with a central spark gap driven by an induction coil. The receiver was a wire circle of 35cm radius with an adjustable small spark gap; tiny sparks were observed in darkness. There was no connecting transmission wire. [ref]
He removed hanging metal fixtures, installed a 4m by 2m zinc reflecting sheet, and moved and rotated the receiver. Spark strength alternated with position and gap orientation, supporting specular reflection and wave interference. He checked observations from farther away because the observer's body perturbed the response. Changing oscillator dimensions changed the spatial pattern. [ref]
These tests supported propagating electromagnetic waves, rather than mere detection of nearby induction. They did not establish unlimited reception distance. His wire-versus-air wave interference experiment addressed finite velocity; he later judged its quantitative velocity comparison doubtful because of room effects. [ref]
The physical inference combines transmission with a spatially structured response to a reflected disturbance. In an ideal monochromatic incident-and-reflected model, standing wave nodes are separated by λ/2; combining a reliable wavelength with independently known frequency gives phase velocity v=νλ. A spark is a threshold event, however: its presence or length is not a calibrated wave intensity or a direct arrival-time measurement. The actual loop also responds to its orientation and finite size, so the simple standing-wave model is an explanation of the test's logic, rather than a reconstruction of Hertz's measured points. Poynting theorem explains how source energy can reach and drive the receiver's discharge.

X-ray

Words: 5k Articles: 25
Electromagnetic radiation OurBigBook.com
X-rays are high-frequency electromagnetic radiation, conventionally with wavelengths of roughly 0.01 to 10nm. The precise wavelength convention varies with context. Hot accreting gas and energetic electronic processes can emit X-rays, whose short wavelengths make them useful probes of compact objects and electron shells.
For a photon, E=hν=hc/λ, where h is the Planck constant and c is the speed of light. As a calculated example, λ=0.10nm gives E≃12.4keV, using hc≃1.23984keVnm. The same wavelength can describe different production mechanisms: bremsstrahlung produces a continuous frequency spectrum, whereas Characteristic X-rays arise from discrete electronic changes in chemical atoms. Measuring transmitted X-rays gives radiography; measuring their frequency spectrum gives spectroscopy.

Röntgen X-ray discovery experiment

Words: 242
X-ray OurBigBook.com
Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen's 1895 first communication describes an induction coil driving an evacuated gas-discharge tube, enclosed in black cardboard in darkness. A paper screen coated with barium platinocyanide showed fluorescence even with its uncoated side facing the tube, visible two metres away. The covering excluded ordinary illumination.[ref]
He interposed materials: a thousand-page book transmitted; 1.5mm lead was practically opaque. A stepped stack of foil produced a photographic plate record. Different materials were not equivalent at equal density and thickness. Photographic plates checked visual observations while remaining covered against ordinary illumination.[ref]
Section 14 lists photographs of hand bones, wire on a bobbin, boxed weights, an enclosed compass, and a lead-painted door. It does not identify the hand's owner. Magnetic deflection moved the cathode ray impact point and thus the source, without bending the emerging X-rays. Their nature remained unsettled.[ref]
The observations belong to 1895; the contemporary English translation appeared in Nature on 23 January 1896.[ref] The experimental logic separates production, transmission and detection. A visibly opaque covering can transmit X-rays; fluorescence then converts deposited energy into visible light. A shadow measures a difference between paths through an object, rather than a direct view of its surface. Neither a screen's brightness nor photographic darkness alone measures an absolute X-ray energy or establishes the X-ray attenuation law below.

Bothe-Geiger coincidence experiment

Words: 871 Articles: 1
X-ray OurBigBook.com
In 1924–1925, Walther Bothe and Hans Geiger tested event-level Compton scattering correlations against the statistical conservation proposed by BKS theory.[ref]
Needle Geiger counters shared a wall. X-rays passed beside it through hydrogen; recoil electrons registered in one counter and scattered photons triggered electron release in the other. Photographic records resolved approximately 10−4s.[ref]
The coincidence excess matched paired-emission expectations. Bothe distinguishes a later directional test from this timing experiment.[ref]
The two channels have different physical roles. The first responds to the local recoil electron's ionization. The second converts a scattered photon into an electron response, as in the photoelectric effect. A large counter signal is an amplified indication of an interaction, not a direct measurement of the incoming photon's energy. The detection efficiency and accepted directions determine how many genuine partners can be seen. Consequently, failure to record every partner is compatible with paired production.
The relevant distinction is between an average balance and a balance in each event. For an initially stationary free electron, Compton recoil kinematics gives
E=E′+Te​,cE​n^=cE′​n^′+pe​.
(7)
Here E,E′ are incident and scattered photon energies, Te​ is recoil kinetic energy, and pe​ is electron momentum. These are paired constraints, not just equations between averages over unrelated detections. Summing correctly paired events preserves the equations; throwing away the pair identities loses information. Two samples can have identical separate energy distributions while differing in their joint probability distribution.
The experiment therefore supplied evidence for the event association required by photon recoil, rather than weighing both outgoing energies in every event. A statistically established excess above accidental coincidences matters; one near-simultaneous pair cannot establish the association. It also cannot by itself settle every question about wave-particle duality.
Preparation, readout and background checks address different failure modes. The direct beam must avoid the second counter, and the counter boundary must prevent a recoil electron from masquerading as its own photon partner. Separate-channel rates constrain accidental coincidences; common disturbances or electrical coupling could instead create a correlated instrumental background. A useful diagnostic is to displace one record in time: under a stationary process assumption, unrelated-pair expectations persist while prompt partners separate. This is the explanatory control modeled below, not an assertion that Bothe performed that particular record-shifting procedure. A background estimate and a statistical comparison are essential to the inference.
The existing coincidence circuit article explains electronic selection, and accidental coincidence derives the accepted-window background. Here pairing can be understood directly from the two recorded time sequences, without imposing a modern detector trigger on the historical readout.

For an original dilute, stationary model with observation time T, neglecting end effects and detector dead time, the expected pair density is
dℓdE[N]​=T[re​rγ​+Sf(ℓ)],∫−∞∞​f(ℓ)dℓ=1.
(8)
This counts all pairs of records, rather than at most one detector trigger per electron. It uses independent unrelated detections for the flat term and an added same-event contribution. A normal distribution for timing spread is a chosen approximation, not a claim about the historical instrument. Intrinsic emission delay, counter response and readout uncertainty can all affect the observed probability density function.
Define the accepted lag interval by ∣ℓ∣<w/2: w is its full width. Integrating the flat baseline gives the existing accidental coincidence expectation Tre​rγ​w. The additional area is TS∫−w/2w/2​f(ℓ)dℓ. Moving a window of the same full width away from the peak tests the baseline under the model's stationarity assumption. It does not remove correlated instrumental backgrounds or changing rates automatically.
For the lag model, choose T=1000s, re​=2s−1, rγ​=0.5s−1, S=0.1s−1 and timing standard deviation σ=1ms. A full width w=2ms gives 2 expected accidental coincidences and 100erf(1/2​)≃68.27 accepted genuine pairs, using the error function. The total is approximately 70.27, whereas the displaced interval from 4 to 6ms has approximately 2.003 expected pairs. A finite sample fluctuates around these expectations; these values are not measured counts or a significance calculation.
The right panel isolates the information lost by retaining singles totals alone. In a separate idealized model, stipulate M=106 independent 1ms bins, with Bernoulli distribution indicators X,Y for electron and photon detection in a bin. Set pe​=Pr(X=1)=0.002 and pγ​=Pr(Y=1)=0.0005. Independence predicts Pr(X=1,Y=1)=pe​pγ​=10−6, hence one expected double detection. Alternatively choose
Pr(X=1,Y=1)=0.000101,Pr(X=1,Y=0)=0.001899,Pr(X=0,Y=1)=0.000399,Pr(X=0,Y=0)=0.997601.
(9)
These nonnegative probabilities sum to one and preserve both marginal probabilities, yet predict 101 double detections in expectation. Their covariance is 0.000101−pe​pγ​=0.000100. This is an exact joint probability distribution illustrating pairing; it does not model the timing spread in the left panel or reconstruct either historical hypothesis quantitatively. Its lesson is why two singles totals cannot decide whether the observed events share a cause.

X-ray tube

Words: 3k Articles: 12
X-ray OurBigBook.com
An X-ray tube accelerates electrons from a negative cathode to a positive target anode. A heated cathode supplies electrons by thermionic emission; the evacuated envelope limits collisions before the target. This differs from the gas-discharge tube in the Röntgen X-ray discovery experiment.[ref]
In bremsstrahlung, an electron changes velocity in the target's electric fields, emitting a photon while losing kinetic energy. Different encounters allow a continuous range of transferred energies. This is distinct from the discrete electronic transitions producing Characteristic X-rays.
Changing the accelerating voltage changes the available kinetic energy per electron and the X-ray tube cutoff. Changing the number of arriving electrons changes the number of opportunities for photon emission. These are distinct controls: a larger electric current does not by itself give each electron more energy. For an original idealized budget, V=50kV and tube electric current i=0.20mA give electrical power Vi=10W and arrival rate i/e≃1.25×1015s−1, where e is the elementary charge. This is input power, not emitted X-ray power; obtaining the latter requires a conversion efficiency.

X-ray tube cutoff (λmin​=hc/(eV))

Words: 133
X-ray tube OurBigBook.com
For an electron starting with negligible kinetic energy and accelerated through a constant positive electric potential difference V, electrical work supplies K=eV. In the ideal single-electron bremsstrahlung model, conservation of energy bounds any one emitted photon:
Eγ​=hν=λhc​≤eV,νmax​=heV​,λmin​=eVhc​.
(10)
The endpoint corresponds to essentially all available kinetic energy going into one photon. It is a limiting bound, not a claim that every electron produces an endpoint photon. Neglect target recoil, initial kinetic energy, and losses before the emitting interaction. If the accelerating voltage varies, its largest value sets the corresponding ideal upper endpoint; a mean voltage does not substitute for that value.
For a calculated constant-V example, 50kV gives Emax​=50keV and λmin​=0.0248nm. Doubling V halves λmin​. Producing a 0.0200nm photon requires at least V=hc/(eλ)≃62.0kV in this model. A continuous frequency spectrum spans lower photon energies; the cutoff calculation predicts neither its shape nor the efficiency of photon emission.

Characteristic X-ray

Words: 2k Articles: 10
X-ray tube OurBigBook.com
A Characteristic X-ray comes from an electronic transition filling an inner electron shell vacancy. The emitted photon carries the difference between atomic energies. Vacancy creation by direct electron impact requires kinetic energy above the shell's binding energy, exceeding the line energy. Higher tube voltage need not move the spectral line.[ref]
An original stipulated shell model illustrates the distinction without using measured atomic data. Let positive binding energies be BK​=9.0keV and BL​=1.2keV, ignoring relaxation corrections. Filling a K-shell vacancy from the L shell releases
EKL​=BK​−BL​=7.8keV,λKL​=EKL​hc​≃0.159nm.
(11)
An 8.5kV tube can energetically emit a 7.8keV bremsstrahlung photon, yet cannot directly remove this K-shell electron. A 12kV tube can create that vacancy by electron impact; if radiative filling occurs, the line is still 7.8keV, not 12keV. This argument concerns direct electron impact and an initially unexcited target; it does not describe every possible excitation route.
X-ray emission spectroscopy
Words: 2k Articles: 8
Characteristic X-ray OurBigBook.com
X-ray emission spectroscopy studies the frequency spectrum of emitted X-rays. The spectral line positions identify electronic energy differences; their strengths also depend on excitation, photon absorption inside the sample, geometry and detector response. An X-ray tube may excite a sample with electrons, while X-ray fluorescence uses incident photons. The distinction matters when choosing what the experiment measures: identifying a chemical element from a line position is different from determining its abundance from a line strength.
Barkla characteristic X-ray experiment
Words: 421
X-ray emission spectroscopy OurBigBook.com
Charles Glover Barkla's 1911 paper describes irradiating a target with an X-ray tube and observing secondary X-rays using an electroscope. For soft Characteristic X-rays from tin, he used a soft primary beam and placed the electroscope close to the target to limit loss in air. Successive aluminium absorbers changed the gas-ionization signal. Thick absorbers suppressed the soft component; a carbon radiator supplied a comparison for the surviving scattered component. Subtracting this estimated contribution left approximately equal fractional losses through successive sheets, evidence for a nearly homogeneous component. Changing primary-beam hardness could suppress excitation of the more penetrating characteristic component.[ref]
An electroscope reads loss of stored electric charge; ionization in its detecting gas enables discharge. An ionization chamber with an electrometer instead measures the collected electric charge or electric current. These are integrating readouts: neither directly assigns an energy to each arriving photon. The diagram separates these functions without claiming a surviving apparatus drawing.
The K and L components were distinguished by their penetrating power and dependence on the emitting chemical element. Barkla's Nobel lecture, delivered on 3 June 1920 for the 1917 prize, explains that absorber-defined K radiation had seemed homogeneous, whereas later diffraction experiments resolved neighbouring spectral lines in both K and L groups. Absorber tests therefore distinguished groups without resolving every line.[ref]
Henry Gwyn Jeffreys Moseley subsequently related the frequencies of corresponding spectral lines to atomic number. The approximate relation ν​=a(Z−b), with series-dependent constants a,b, is Moseley's law; its discovery should be distinguished from Barkla's identification of characteristic secondary radiation.[ref]
A continuous incident frequency spectrum can contribute a continuous scattered background alongside discrete Characteristic X-rays. Here “scattered” identifies a production process, not a universal spectral shape: scattering of a narrow incident line need not make a continuum. In particular, the characteristic component is photon emission after vacancy creation, rather than simply the primary beam redirected. Source hardness, target substitution and absorber thickness probe different parts of that distinction. A surviving signal alone establishes neither an additional electron shell nor a fully resolved spectral line.
Siegbahn precision X-ray wavelength spectroscopy
Words: 1k Articles: 5
X-ray emission spectroscopy OurBigBook.com
Precision X-ray emission spectroscopy turns closely spaced spectral lines into quantitative constraints on atomic energy levels. Bragg's law makes a crystal an angular ruler for wavelength: resolving nearby directions can reveal components hidden inside an apparently single line. Accurate line positions and the ability to separate two lines are related, but distinct, experimental achievements.
Karl Manne Georg Siegbahn received the 1924 Nobel Prize in Physics; his lecture was delivered on 11 December 1925.[ref]
His son Kai Siegbahn received the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physics for electron spectroscopy.[ref]
The Barkla characteristic X-ray experiment distinguished element-dependent K and L groups through absorption and excitation. Moseley's law related corresponding line frequencies across chemical elements to atomic number. Measuring the components within those groups asks a further question: which distinct atomic energy differences produce the neighbouring spectral lines?
Siegbahn's lecture organizes measured spectral lines into energy levels and resolved double levels.[ref] The payoff is a map of atomic energy differences as well as an element's position in the periodic table.
Crystal X-ray spectrometer
Words: 1k Articles: 4
Siegbahn precision X-ray wavelength spectroscopy OurBigBook.com
A crystal X-ray spectrometer selects a narrow wavelength interval by diffraction at known interplanar spacing d and Bragg angle θ. For fixed diffraction order n, a known d lets an angular measurement determine λ=2dsinθ/n. This reverses the crystallography problem of finding d from a known wavelength.
Siegbahn's laboratory developed X-ray tubes and evacuated spectrographs for longer wavelengths. Rotating the crystal selected reflections; photographic plates recorded positions. Opposite-side exposures measured angles on both sides of the direct beam. Later tube instruments mounted a beam-defining slit opposite the plate holder. A separate double instrument compared two reflected beams with two ionization chambers. These were different instruments, not one universal assembly.[ref]
The Nobel biography describes constant-temperature spectrographs and a precision angular scale read by diametrically opposed microscopes.[ref] Temperature control matters because thermal expansion changes d; an accurately read angle cannot compensate for an unknown interplanar spacing.
The schematic separates preparation, selection and readout. An X-ray tube target supplies Characteristic X-rays with a possible bremsstrahlung background. A collimator limits incident directions; evacuation reduces air X-ray attenuation. Rotating the crystal brings different wavelengths into reflection. A photographic plate stores line positions over an exposure, whereas an ionization chamber can measure collected electric charge at successive angles. Neither readout alone establishes absolute wavelength: an angular zero, angular-scale calibration, diffraction order, d and any required refraction correction enter the inference. Narrower slits reduce accepted angular spread but also reduce signal; a weak line can become harder to locate despite a narrower instrumental response.
A control reported in Siegbahn's lecture photographed a line system with copper-sulphate and calcium-sulphate crystals. Irregular relative shifts around copper's characteristic frequency supported anomalous dispersion; the arrangement excluded differing absorption across that frequency as the explanation.[ref] A finer angular scale alone cannot remove this physical refraction correction.

The Rayleigh criterion uses a first-zero convention for specified diffraction patterns. A Gaussian function has no finite zero, so its two-maxima threshold is not the Rayleigh criterion. Measurement resolution, the precision of an isolated line's centre and absolute wavelength accuracy should therefore be reported separately.

Rayleigh scattering

Words: 1k Articles: 9
Electromagnetic radiation OurBigBook.com
Rayleigh scattering is elastic scattering of electromagnetic radiation by particles much smaller than the incident wavelength. In the stationary particle approximation, the scattered electromagnetic radiation keeps the incident frequency: its direction changes, rather than its color. The incident electric field induces an oscillating electric dipole moment, which produces electric dipole radiation. This is a useful example of how a microscopic response can explain a familiar large-scale sight, why the sky is blue. [ref].
The smallness condition for Rayleigh scattering by a small sphere of radius a is ka≪1, where the wavenumber is k=2π/λ and λ is the surrounding medium's wavelength. For ordinary materials with modest refractive index, the incident electric field then varies little across the particle, making the electric-dipole approximation for radiation useful. For very large refractive index, also check the internal size ∣m∣ka≪1 before applying the simple electric dipole model. Once these conditions fail, use Mie scattering for a sphere instead of extrapolating the Rayleigh scattering wavelength dependence.
Historically, the 1871 paper [ref] connected the color and polarization of an electromagnetic wave of scattered skylight with particles smaller than the wavelength. Its dimensional analysis anticipates scattered light intensity proportional to particle volume squared and to λ−4. The original mechanical picture is historical; the modern explanation uses induced electric dipole radiation.

Why the sky is blue

Words: 168
Rayleigh scattering OurBigBook.com
In a clear terrestrial atmosphere, Rayleigh scattering by air molecules redirects some sunlight toward an observer looking away from the Sun. Shorter wavelengths are preferentially scattered, enriching the scattered light in blue compared with the incoming sunlight. Along the longer atmospheric path to the low Sun, more short-wavelength light is removed from the direct beam, helping produce red sunsets. [ref].
Violet light has an even shorter wavelength than blue light, so the Rayleigh scattering wavelength dependence alone cannot predict the perceived sky color. The incoming solar optical spectrum and the eye's differing response to wavelengths both matter; human vision is less sensitive to violet than to blue. [ref]. Multiple scattering and other atmospheric particles also affect the observed sky. Cloud droplets are generally too large for this simple Rayleigh scattering model: Mie scattering describes spherical droplets, with much weaker visible wavelength selectivity, while repeated scattering helps make clouds appear white. [ref].
