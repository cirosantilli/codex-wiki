Finite-layer model of Lippmann reflection (λB​=2ndcosθ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Lippmann plate
OurBigBook.com Words: 485
To isolate the readout mechanism, replace the processed photographic emulsion by N equally spaced, identical weak scattering planes, with separation d. Take the readout refractive index n to be real and independent of wavelength, and let θ be the internal angle from the plane normal. Adjacent specularly backscattered contributions differ in wave phase by 2kz​d=4πndcosθ/λ0​. Their geometric series gives
rN​=a∑j=0N−1​eijδ,δ=λ0​4πndcosθ​−2π,FN​=N2∣a∣2∣rN​∣2​=[Nsin(δ/2)sin(Nδ/2)​]2.
(53)
Here a is a small plane reflection wave amplitude, assumed constant in this illustrative band; FN​=1 at δ=0 by continuity. Since incident and reflected waves occupy the same external medium, ∣rN​∣2 is the model reflectance when transmission factors are absorbed into a. Require N∣a∣≪1 for the weak-scattering sum to remain self-consistent. Normalizing to FN​ discards absolute brightness; it does not predict a highly reflecting historical plate.
The first-order peak obeys
λB​=2ndcosθ.
(54)
This is Bragg's law with an angle measured from the normal, rather than the glancing Bragg angle. For external viewing angle α in air approximated by refractive index one, Snell's law gives nsinθ=sinα, hence
λB​(α)=2nd1−n2sin2α​​.
(55)
The expression describes specular readout: illumination must supply the matching incident direction. Tilting a plate under fixed narrow illumination can make the signal disappear as well as change its selected colour.
For the chosen n=1.5 and d=200nm, calculated peaks are 600nm at α=0, 565.69nm at 30∘, and 529.15nm at 45∘. Greater obliquity selects a shorter wavelength. Uniform shrinkage to d′=sd, with unchanged n, multiplies every peak wavelength by s: a chosen s=0.95 moves the normal peak to 570nm. More generally, λB′​/λB​=(n′/n)s at normal incidence. A change of thickness alone is insufficient if processing also changes the refractive index.
Finite N gives a finite spectral response. The nearest zeros satisfy δ=±2π/N, giving λ±​=λB​/(1±1/N). At the normal 600nm peak, N=30 gives neighbouring zeros at approximately 580.65 and 620.69nm; N=10 gives 545.45 and 666.67nm. Thus greater sampled depth narrows the central band in this model. The small-detuning distance from peak to a nearest zero is approximately λB​/N; it is not the full width at half maximum.
Real processed photographic emulsions have a continuous, irregular depth response rather than identical planes. Absorption, wavelength-dependent refractive index, unequal scattering strengths, a nonideal exposure reflector, changes during photographic processing and multiple scattering of light can alter both peak positions and shapes. A transfer calculation with measured material parameters would be needed for quantitative reflectance. The present model explains the phase-selection mechanism and the tradeoff between depth and spectral width; it cannot establish faithful reproduction of an arbitrary incident optical spectrum. Its useful lesson is that a photograph can record colour through sub-wavelength structure, while its eventual appearance still depends on illumination, viewing geometry and processing.
