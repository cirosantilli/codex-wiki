A broad component plus a narrow central component can have one local maximum while still displaying two distinct width scales. This is what “bimodal” means in this imaging context; it does not require two separated peaks. To isolate the population effect, construct an original normalized line profile
Gσ​(u)=2π​σe−u2/(2σ2)​,P(u)=(1−f0​)G1​(u)+f0​G0.20​(u).
(38)
Both terms are normal distributions; their integrals are one, so the component areas are 1−f0​ and f0​. With f0​=0.488, the narrow component has central height f0​/(2π​0.20)≃0.9734, while the broad component contributes (1−f0​)/2π​≃0.2043. A tall central feature therefore need not contain most of the particles. Holding these widths fixed deliberately isolates changing weights; it does not model how temperature, interactions or camera resolution determine actual widths.
For absorption imaging in the weak-probe regime with negligible saturation, the optical depth is OD(x,z)=−ln[I(x,z)/I0​(x,z)]=σabs​ncol​(x,z), where I0​ is the reference light intensity without atoms, I the transmitted light intensity, σabs​ an effective absorption cross section, and ncol​ the column density. Integrating ncol​ over both image coordinates gives the particle number; a central pixel height alone does not. Finite resolution can obscure a narrow component, and saturation invalidates this simple logarithmic calibration.
