Lattice enthalpy from solution calorimetry

A second indirect route makes the experimental distinction especially clear: a calorimeter can measure dissolution of a weighed salt into a known amount of water, but its final ions are hydrated, not separated gaseous ions. At compatible standard states and the same temperature,
ΔHsol∘​=Ldiss​+ΔHhyd,pair∘​,Ldiss​=ΔHsol∘​−ΔHhyd,pair∘​.
(9)
Here the combined hydration of ions term takes one mole each of gaseous Na+ and Cl− to the specified aqueous reference state. Its sum suffices: dissolution calorimetry alone cannot independently assign single-ion hydration of ions enthalpies, or determine Ldiss​ without another input. A finite-concentration measurement also requires dilution corrections before it can supply ΔHsol∘​.
In a simple constant-pressure solution calorimeter, an insulated vessel, stirrer and thermometer follow the temperature change after mixing. A calibrated effective heat capacity Ceff​ converts the corrected temperature change to heat. With no applied heating, negligible external heat transfer, and only pressure-volume work, the approximate molar result is
ΔHsol​≃−nCeff​ΔT​.
(10)
Here n is the amount of substance dissolved and Ceff​ includes the chemical solution and the vessel. Cooling means positive dissolution enthalpy, because the dissolving sample takes heat from its surroundings.[ref]
An actual reference measurement used potassium chloride, not sodium chloride: a platinum-lined calorimeter with an adiabatic shield and a quartz-oscillator thermometer. Because dissolution absorbed heat, measured electrical heating prevented loss of shield control; the readouts were electrical input and the vessel/shield temperature record. Stirring was checked visually in a dummy vessel. The thermometer was compared with a calibrated resistance thermometer, and electrical calibration bracketed the reaction. The reported result was +(17.584±0.017)kJmol−1 at 298.15K, for one mole of potassium chloride in 500 moles of water.[ref]
Checks on residual water, particle size and final concentration, and agreement with a second calorimeter, constrained systematic errors. Imperfect shield tracking required heat transfer corrections. This experiment measures an enthalpy of solution, not a direct lattice energy; the hydration and dilution bookkeeping still has to be supplied.
