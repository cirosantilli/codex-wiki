Write the increment function of the one-step method as Φ(x,v,h)=f(x+βh,v+βhf(x,v)). Its consistency error is
Tn​=hy(xn​+h)−y(xn​)​−Φ(xn​,y(xn​),h).
(1)
As h→0, the first term tends to y′(xn​) and the second tends to f(xn​,y(xn​))=y′(xn​) by continuity. Both limits are uniform along the exact-solution curve, a compact set: its small stage perturbations lie in a fixed compact neighborhood, so uniform continuity applies there. Thus maxn<N​∣Tn​∣→0, proving consistency for every β∈[0,1].

By the chain rule, y′′=fx​+fy​f along the exact solution. Applying Taylor's theorem to the exact increment and the two arguments of f gives
hy(x+h)−y(x)​=f(x,y(x))+2h​y′′(x)+O(h2),Φ(x,y(x),h)=f(x,y(x))+βh(fx​+fy​f)(x,y(x))+O(h2).
(2)
The assumed differentiability makes the remainders uniform on 0≤x≤1−h. Subtracting gives the consistency error
Tn​=h(21​−β)y′′(xn​)+O(h2).​
(3)
Here O denotes Big O notation. The general order of a numerical method is therefore one for β=1/2 and at least two for β=1/2. For example, on y′=y with unit initial value, yN​=(1+h+βh2)1/h. Expanding its logarithm gives yN​=e[1+(β−1/2)h+O(h2)], so the first-order term is actually nonzero when β=1/2.
To control accumulation, the mean value theorem and the bound on fy​ give
∣Φ(x,u,h)−Φ(x,v,h)∣≤L(1+βhL)∣u−v∣.
(4)
Put Kh​=L(1+βhL), qh​=1+hKh​ and en​=y(xn​)−yn​. The exact and numerical updates yield ∣en+1​∣≤qh​∣en​∣+h∣Tn​∣, with e0​=0. Iterating this recurrence, as in the Lipschitz one-step error bound, gives
∣en​∣≤Kh​qhn​−1​maxj<n​∣Tj​∣≤Kh​eKh​xn​−1​maxj<n​∣Tj​∣.
(5)
Because Kh​≤L(1+L) for h≤1, the prefactor is bounded independently of the mesh. When β=1/2, the consistency error is O(h2), hence the maximum global error is O(h2). This proves at least second-order numerical convergence of the explicit midpoint method.

For f(x,y)=y and β=1/2, the explicit midpoint method multiplies the current value by R=1+h+h2/2. Starting from one, induction gives
yn​=(1+h+2h2​)n.​
(6)
Let A=eh and B=R. The Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder for the exponential function gives
6h3​≤A−B≤6h3eh​,1≤B≤A.
(7)
The difference of powers identity gives An−Bn=(A−B)∑k=0n−1​An−1−kBk. Every summand lies between one and An−1. Since nh=xn​, the global error therefore satisfies
6h2xn​​≤y(xn​)−yn​≤6h2xn​exn​​.​
(8)
The same inequalities hold at n=0, where the error vanishes. At the fixed endpoint xN​=1, the lower bound h2/6 prevents any higher order of a numerical method, while the upper bound is eh2/6. Thus the convergence order is exactly two.

A constant-coefficient linear multistep method, normalized so that αk​=1, has the form
∑j=0k​αj​Yn+j​=h∑j=0k​βj​f(xn+j​,Yn+j​),xn​=x0​+nh.
(9)
Here Yn​ approximates the solution y(xn​) of the initial value problem, and Y0​,…,Yk−1​ are starting approximations. The coefficients are independent of n and h. If βk​=0, the update is explicit; otherwise it is an implicit time-stepping method, whose new value must satisfy the displayed equation.

Fix the following normalization of the local truncation error, also called the consistency error: insert a smooth exact solution into the linear multistep method and divide the residual by h,
Tn​(h)=h1​∑j=0k​αj​y(xn​+jh)−∑j=0k​βj​y′(xn​+jh).
(10)
Thus the unscaled defect is hTn​(h). Consistency of a numerical method means that Tn​(h)→0 uniformly over mesh points in each fixed interval for every sufficiently smooth exact solution. Write the characteristic polynomials of a linear multistep method as
ρ(z)=∑j=0k​αj​zj,σ(z)=∑j=0k​βj​zj.
(11)
By Taylor's theorem, consistency of a numerical method is equivalent to
∑j​αj​=0,∑j​jαj​=∑j​βj​,
(12)
or ρ(1)=0 and ρ′(1)=σ(1).
Second-order accuracy means Tn​(h)=O(h2) for smooth solutions, equivalently an unscaled local truncation error O(h3). In addition to the two consistency conditions, its order conditions require
∑j​j2αj​=2∑j​jβj​.
(13)
An order of a numerical method of exactly two additionally requires that the next order condition fails. These statements concern the local defect; a second-order global error requires stability and suitably accurate starting values as well.

For a constant-coefficient linear multistep method, zero-stability means uniform boundedness of the propagation of starting perturbations as h→0. Equivalently, every solution of the homogeneous linear recurrence
∑j=0k​αj​vn+j​=0
(14)
satisfies ∣vn​∣≤Kmax0≤j<k​∣vj​∣ for all n≥0, with K independent of n and the starting values. The equivalent root condition for a multistep method is that every root of a polynomial ζ of the degree-k polynomial ρ satisfies ∣ζ∣≤1, and every root on ∣ζ∣=1 is simple. Indeed, a root of multiplicity m produces recurrence modes nqζn, 0≤q<m: repeated roots strictly inside the disk are harmless, whereas roots outside it or repeated roots on its boundary produce unbounded modes.

The coefficients of this linear multistep method are α0​=b, α1​=−a, α2​=1 and β0​=β1​=0, β2​=c. Substitution into the order of a numerical method conditions gives
1−a+b=0,2−a−c=0,2−2a​−2c=0.
(15)
The second equation gives c=2−a; substituting this into the third gives −2+3a/2=0. Consequently there is exactly one second-order choice,
a=34​,b=31​,c=32​.​
(16)
The resulting scheme is the BDF2 method,
3Yn+2​−4Yn+1​+Yn​=2hf(xn+2​,Yn+2​).
(17)
For a C4 exact solution, Taylor's theorem in the original normalization gives the exact leading order defect
Tn​(h)​=hy(xn​+2h)−34​y(xn​+h)+31​y(xn​)​−32​y′(xn​+2h)=−92​h2y′′′(xn​)+O(h3).​
(18)
In particular the coefficient −2/9 is nonzero: the method has formal order exactly two, rather than three. Bounded third derivatives already suffice for the uniform estimate Tn​(h)=O(h2); the stronger smoothness above only makes the displayed remainder immediate. Multiplying the recurrence by 3/2 instead gives the common derivative normalization with leading defect −h2y′′′(xn​)/3.
The first of the characteristic polynomials of a linear multistep method is
ρ(z)=z2−34​z+31​=(z−1)(z−31​).
(19)
Both roots are simple and lie in the closed unit disk, so the root condition for a multistep method proves zero-stability.
The Dahlquist equivalence theorem states that, for fixed-coefficient linear multistep methods on the usual class of initial value problems with a right-hand side continuous in x and uniformly Lipschitz continuous in y, convergence of a numerical method for every such problem and every convergent set of starting values is equivalent to consistency of a numerical method and zero-stability. The statement is on fixed finite intervals where the exact solution exists, with implicit updates well defined. Its order estimate says that a zero-stable method with Tn​(h)=O(hp) and starting errors O(hp) has global error O(hp) under the corresponding solution smoothness and Lipschitz continuity assumptions; merely convergent starting values do not ensure the order-p rate.
For this application, sufficient assumptions are a unique solution y∈C3([x0​,x0​+T]), a uniform Lipschitz continuity bound L in y on a neighborhood of its graph, and the nearby solution branch of each implicit update. For sufficiently small h, the map
z⟼34​Yn+1​−31​Yn​+32​hf(xn+2​,z)
(20)
is a contraction mapping with factor at most 2hL/3<1 on a suitable small closed neighborhood. The Banach fixed-point theorem supplies local solvability of the nearby update. With starting errors
∣Y0​−y(x0​)∣+∣Y1​−y(x0​+h)∣=O(h2),
(21)
the order estimate gives
max0≤nh≤T​∣Yn​−y(xn​)∣≤CT​(∣Y0​−y(x0​)∣+∣Y1​−y(x0​+h)∣+maxn​∣Tn​(h)∣)=O(h2).
(22)
Thus the BDF2 method is second-order convergent under these standard hypotheses. Exact Y0​=y(x0​) together with one Euler method step for Y1​ already gives the required starting error when y′′ is bounded, since only one starting step is taken.
The question's assumption of continuity of f alone does not establish uniqueness, the needed solution smoothness, or this uniform convergence estimate. The added Lipschitz continuity condition is a sufficient hypothesis for the theorem used here; its failure does not imply that every particular initial value problem is nonunique or that its numerical approximations cannot converge.
