Distance from a point to a plane (D=∣n⋅p−d∣/∥n∥)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology
OurBigBook.com Words: 690 Articles: 3
The distance from a point to a plane is the shortest Euclidean distance from the given point to any point on the plane. For P={x:n⋅x=d} with nonzero normal vector n, a point p has
D=∥n∥∣n⋅p−d∣​.​
(41)
The numerator is the residual of the plane equation, not yet a length: its size depends on the chosen normal vector. Dividing by that vector's Euclidean norm removes this arbitrary scale.
For the original example P:2x−y+2z=3 and p=(4,1,2), the normal vector is n=(2,−1,2) and ∥n∥=3. The residual is 8−1+4−3=8, so the distance is 8/3. Using the equivalent plane equation −6x+3y−6z=−9 instead gives numerator 24 and Euclidean norm 9, hence the same 8/3. A point already on the plane has zero residual and zero distance.
Why is the perpendicular route shortest? Let q be the closest point on a plane and r any other point on it. The displacement p−q is orthogonal to q−r, so the Pythagorean theorem gives
∥p−r∥2=∥p−q∥2+∥q−r∥2≥∥p−q∥2.
(42)
Equality holds only for r=q. This is the closest-point principle of orthogonal decomposition, applied after a translation of an affine plane to its direction linear subspace.[ref]
The formula is useful whenever the deviation from a flat reference should be measured geometrically. A raw linear equation residual cannot be compared across arbitrary equation scalings; a perpendicular distance can. The same reasoning works for a hyperplane in any finite-dimensional Euclidean space, with the appropriate number of coordinates.
