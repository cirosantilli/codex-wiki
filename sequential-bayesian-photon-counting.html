Sequential Bayesian photon counting (pk​(n)=pk−1​(n)Lsk​​(n;ϕk​)/Zk​)

Consider an original balanced-readout model, rather than a fit to the experiment. Let n∈{0,…,Nmax​}, with a normalized prior probability p0​(n)≥0 and ∑n​p0​(n)=1. A detected chemical atom has outcome s∈{e,g}. Define the conditional probabilities
Le​(n;ϕ)=P(e∣n,ϕ)=21+Ccos(ϕ+nχ)​,Lg​(n;ϕ)=1−Le​(n;ϕ),0≤C≤1.
(6)
Here χ is the accumulated quantum phase per photon, ϕ the controlled analysis quantum phase, and C the fringe contrast. The two probabilities sum to one for every n. Actual calibration may also require an offset and asymmetric detection errors; C alone does not specify a quantum instrument.
Assume no photon number change during the record and fresh independently prepared atomic meters. For observed outcomes s1​,…,sk​ at recorded settings ϕ1​,…,ϕk​, the Born rule and Bayes' theorem give
Zk​=∑m=0Nmax​​pk−1​(m)Lsk​​(m;ϕk​),pk​(n)=Zk​pk−1​(n)Lsk​​(n;ϕk​)​=∑m=0Nmax​​p0​(m)∏j=1k​Lsj​​(m;ϕj​)p0​(n)∏j=1k​Lsj​​(n;ϕj​)​.
(7)
The posterior probability is defined only for Zk​>0. Summing its numerator over n proves normalization. This is also the update of diagonal elements of a field density operator under a quantum measurement; it does not assume that an initial quantum superposition was an unknown pre-existing integer. The observed settings are part of the record, especially when selected adaptively.
For an exact finite example take p0​=(1,1,1,1)/4, C=4/5, and χ=π/2. An e outcome at ϕ=0 has likelihood (9,5,1,5)/10, giving p1​=(9,5,1,5)/20. Another e at ϕ=−π/2 gives p2​=(9,9,1,1)/20. A subsequent g at ϕ=0 gives
p3​=681​(9,45,9,5),P(s1​=e,s2​=e,s3​=g)=20017​.
(8)
Thus three atomic records raise the posterior probability of n=1 from 1/4 to 45/68, while leaving finite uncertainty. No one of these outcomes identifies n with certainty.
The periodic likelihood function imposes an identifiability limit. If χ=2π/q for an integer q>0, then Ls​(n+q;ϕ)=Ls​(n;ϕ) for every setting: the posterior odds between n and n+q never change. Varying ϕ can resolve a cosine reflection ambiguity within a period, but cannot remove this exact period. Changing χ can supply additional information. A finite cutoff is therefore a physical assumption to justify, not evidence that excluded high photon numbers were absent. At C=0 no outcome changes the prior probability; at 0<C<1 neither binary outcome has an exactly zero likelihood.
The no-loss assumption also has a time limit. In an original zero-temperature loss model, let κ=1/Tc​. A Fock state ∣n⟩ loses its first photon at rate nκ, so its survival probability is exp(−nκt) and its mean lifetime is Tc​/n for n>0. Several records must be acquired within that lifetime to identify a plateau. When losses matter, first predict pk−​(n)=∑m​Tnm​(Δt)pk−1​(m) using a normalized transition matrix, then apply the atomic Bayesian inference update to pk−​. Thermal excitation requires upward transitions too. A delayed or broadened inference after a jump need not indicate that an atom caused the jump.
