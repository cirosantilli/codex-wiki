The Banach fixed-point theorem states that a contraction mapping T:X→X of a nonempty complete metric space (X,d) has a unique fixed point, and iterations of a map xn+1​=T(xn​) converge to it from every x0​∈X. Precisely, assume a single constant q∈[0,1) satisfies d(Tx,Ty)≤qd(x,y) for every x,y∈X.
Induction gives d(xn+1​,xn​)≤qnd(x1​,x0​). The triangle inequality and the geometric series therefore give, for m>n,
d(xm​,xn​)≤∑j=nm−1​qjd(x1​,x0​)≤1−qqn​d(x1​,x0​).
(1)
Thus (xn​) is a Cauchy sequence. By completeness it converges to some x∗​∈X. The contraction mapping is a continuous map, so Tx∗​=limn​Txn​=limn​xn+1​=x∗​. If y∗​ is another fixed point, then d(x∗​,y∗​)≤qd(x∗​,y∗​), forcing x∗​=y∗​. Letting m→∞ in the estimate also gives the contraction iteration error bound:
Tx∗​=x∗​,d(xn​,x∗​)≤1−qqn​d(x1​,x0​).​
(2)
If q=0, T is constant and the sequence reaches its unique fixed point after the first step; this also removes any ambiguity about 00 in the displayed estimate at n=0.

A retraction from a topological space X onto a subset Y is a continuous map r:X→Y satisfying r(y)=y for every y∈Y. Equivalently, r∘ι=idY​, where ι:Y↪X is the inclusion.
The no-retraction theorem for the finite-dimensional closed unit ball says
For n≥1, no retraction Bn⟶∂Bn exists.​
(5)
Indeed, if r were such a retraction, F(x)=−r(x) would be a continuous map from Bn into itself. The Brouwer fixed-point theorem would give x=F(x)=−r(x). Since ∣r(x)∣=1, x lies on the boundary, where r(x)=x. Thus x=−x, contradicting ∣x∣=1. Here the Brouwer fixed-point theorem asserts that every continuous map of a finite-dimensional closed unit ball into itself has a fixed point.

τ(x)=∣v∣2−a⋅v+(a⋅v)2+(1−∣a∣2)∣v∣2​​,r(x)=a+τ(x)(x−a).​
(6)

∣v∣2t2+2(a⋅v)t+∣a∣2−1=0.
(7)
Its constant term is negative, so it has exactly one positive root, namely τ(x). Since ∣a+v∣=∣x∣≤1, that positive root satisfies τ(x)≥1. Thus r(x)∈∂Bn for every x=a in the closed unit ball.

The retraction of a Hilbert ball onto its sphere exists. In the real Hilbert space ℓ2, let S(x0​,x1​,…)=(0,x0​,x1​,…) be the unilateral shift operator, and let e0​=(1,0,…). For x in the closed unit ball B, define
F(x)=1−∥x∥2​e0​+Sx.
(8)
The orthogonality of e0​ and Sx gives ∥F(x)∥2=1. Thus F is a continuous map into the unit sphere. It has no fixed point: F(x)=x would imply xj+1​=xj​ for every j≥0. A constant sequence in ℓ2 must be zero, whereas F(0)=e0​=0.
Put d=x−F(x), which is nonzero everywhere, and define
α(x)=∥x−F(x)∥22(1−⟨F(x),x⟩)​,r(x)=F(x)+α(x)(x−F(x)).​
(9)
This is a continuous map on the whole closed unit ball, including zero. To check its range, observe that ⟨F(x),d⟩=⟨F(x),x⟩−1, so
∥F(x)+αd∥2=1+2α(⟨F(x),x⟩−1)+α2∥d∥2=1.
(10)
If ∥x∥=1, then ∥x−F(x)∥2=2(1−⟨F(x),x⟩), hence α(x)=1 and r(x)=x. Therefore r:B→∂B is a retraction.
Geometrically this is the second intersection of the line from F(x) through x with the unit sphere. In fact
α(x)−1=∥x−F(x)∥21−∥x∥2​≥0.
(11)
The construction illustrates why the finite-dimensional hypothesis in the no-retraction theorem matters. The closed unit ball of ℓ2 is not a compact set in the norm topology.

For Banach spaces X,Y, a nonlinear compact operator T:X→Y is a continuous map that sends every bounded set to a relatively compact subset of Y: the closure of its image is a compact set. For a continuous map, the image condition is equivalent to requiring that every bounded sequence (xj​) has a subsequence along which T(xj​) converges in the norm topology of Y. For a linear operator, this is the usual compact operator definition; the continuity requirement is included explicitly here because the subsequent applications are nonlinear.
The compact-domain version of the Schauder fixed-point theorem says
K=∅ compact and convex in a Banach space,T:K→K continuous⟹Tu=u for some u∈K.​
(14)
A useful equivalent version replaces K by a nonempty closed bounded convex set C and assumes T:C→C is a continuous map with relatively compact image. This latter form will be used for the partial differential equation. Both forms assert existence of a fixed point, without asserting uniqueness.

We first prove the compact-domain Schauder fixed-point theorem. Let K be a nonempty compact convex set in a Banach space X, and let T:K→K be a continuous map. The Brouwer fixed-point theorem states that every continuous map of a finite-dimensional closed unit ball into itself has a fixed point. By the homeomorphism proved in part (a)(ii), it applies equally to any nonempty finite-dimensional compact convex set, including a singleton.
For ε>0, use the supplied Schauder approximation lemma to obtain a finite-dimensional linear subspace Lε​ and a continuous map Tε​:K→K∩Lε​ with ∥Tx−Tε​x∥≤ε. The set Kε​=K∩Lε​ is nonempty because it contains Tε​(K). It is a compact convex set, since a finite-dimensional linear subspace is closed. Restricting Tε​ to Kε​ and applying the Brouwer fixed-point theorem gives xε​=Tε​xε​, and thus
∥Txε​−xε​∥≤ε.
(20)
Take εj​=1/j. The compactness of K yields a convergent subsequence xεjm​​​→x∗​∈K. The continuity of T and the preceding estimate give
Tx∗​=x∗​.​
(21)
For completeness, this also proves the version on a nonempty closed bounded convex set C when T(C) is relatively compact. Let K=convT(C)⊂C. The closed convex hull of a compact set in a Banach space is compact. To verify the fact needed here, cover T(C) by finitely many balls of radius δ. Replacing every term of a convex combination by a centre changes it by at most δ in norm. The convex hull of those finitely many centres is compact in a finite-dimensional linear subspace, so it has a finite net of radius δ. Thus convT(C) is totally bounded. Its closure is complete and totally bounded, hence compact. Now T(K)⊂T(C)⊂K, so the compact-domain result supplies a fixed point in C.

A useful form of Minty's lemma is the following. Let X be a real Banach space, K⊂X a nonempty convex set, and A:K→X∗ a monotone operator that is hemicontinuous. Here operator monotonicity means ⟨Ax−Ay,x−y⟩≥0 for x,y∈K, and hemicontinuity means that t↦⟨A(x+t(y−x)),z⟩ is continuous on [0,1] for x,y∈K and z∈X. For u∈K,
⟨Au,v−u⟩≥0 ∀v∈K⟺⟨Av,v−u⟩≥0 ∀v∈K.​
(32)
The forward implication follows from operator monotonicity:
⟨Av,v−u⟩=⟨Au,v−u⟩+⟨Av−Au,v−u⟩≥0.
(33)
For the reverse implication, fix w∈K and take vt​=u+t(w−u)∈K for 0<t≤1. The assumed inequality gives
0≤⟨Avt​,vt​−u⟩=t⟨A(u+t(w−u)),w−u⟩.
(34)
Divide by t and send t↓0. Hemicontinuity gives ⟨Au,w−u⟩≥0, as required.
In the whole-space version, for a monotone operator A:X→X∗ that is hemicontinuous,
Au=f⟺⟨Av−f,v−u⟩≥0∀v∈X.​
(35)
Apply the just-proved result to A−f and K=X; the resulting inequality ⟨Au−f,v−u⟩≥0 for all v forces Au=f by choosing v=u±z. This is the variational inequality form of Minty's lemma.
