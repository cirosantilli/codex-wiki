In the scalar two-particle model, the fermionic exchange symmetry and bosonic exchange symmetry conditions are
Ψ(x2​,x1​)=−Ψ(x1​,x2​) for fermions,Ψ(x2​,x1​)=Ψ(x1​,x2​) for bosons.​
(1)
For particles with spin, exchange acts on all their labels; the stated coordinate-only model fixes the spatial symmetry sector. Define SΨ(x1​,x2​)=Ψ(x2​,x1​). Equal masses make the kinetic operator invariant under exchange, and the assumed potential is invariant too, so HS=SH. Therefore HΨ=EΨ implies H(SΨ)=E(SΨ). The components
ΨB​=21​(Ψ+SΨ),ΨF​=21​(Ψ−SΨ)
(2)
are respectively symmetric and antisymmetric eigenfunctions whenever nonzero. Their sum is the original nonzero quantum state, so at least one is nonzero. An eigenspace may contain both sectors if the energy is degenerate.
The normal modes of two harmonically coupled identical particles provide the separation. The coordinate transformation to y+​,y−​ is orthogonal, with absolute Jacobian one. Exchanging the particles leaves y+​ fixed and changes y−​ to −y−​. Hence bosonic quantum states are even and fermionic quantum states odd in the relative coordinate. The chain rule gives
∂x1​​=2​∂y+​​+∂y−​​​,∂x2​​=2​∂y+​​−∂y−​​​,∂x1​2​+∂x2​2​=∂y+​2​+∂y−​2​.
(3)
Also x12​+x22​=y+2​+y−2​ and (x1​−x2​)2=2y−2​. Combining the two trapping potentials with the interaction therefore gives the separated Hamiltonian operator
H=[−2mℏ2​∂y+​2​+21​mω2y+2​]+[−2mℏ2​∂y−​2​+21​mω2cos2αy−2​].
(4)
These are independent oscillators of frequencies ω and ωcosα. Both frequencies are positive in the stated range. Their product eigenstates have energies
En+​,n−​​=ℏω[n+​+21​+(n−​+21​)cosα],n+​,n−​=0,1,2,….​
(5)
The parity of an oscillator level is (−1)n. Consequently bosons allow even n−​ and fermions odd n−​, while n+​ is unrestricted.
The bosonic ground state uses n+​=n−​=0, and the fermionic ground state uses n+​=0,n−​=1. The oscillator length in the relative mode is
τ2=mωcosαℏ​=mωℏsecα​.​
(6)
Using the normalized Gaussian for each mode gives
ΦB​(y+​,y−​)=ψσ​(y+​)ψτ​(y−​),ΦF​(y+​,y−​)=ψσ​(y+​)ϕτ​(y−​).​
(7)
Indeed ϕτ​(y)=2​yψτ​(y)/τ by differentiating the Gaussian, so it is the normalized odd first excited state. The ground energies are EB​=ℏω(1+cosα)/2 and EF​=ℏω(1+3cosα)/2.
Choose normalized factors for a normalized product quantum state; this can always be done by reciprocal rescaling of its factors. Since the coordinate Jacobian has absolute value one,
E[f(y−​)]=∬f(y−​)∣ψ(y+​)∣2∣ϕ(y−​)∣2dy+​dy−​=∫f(y)∣ϕ(y)∣2dy.​
(8)
If the supplied factors are not individually normalized, the general expression has an additional factor ∥ψ∥2; choosing normalized factors is the convention in the displayed formula.
For the ground states, put ρ(y)=∣ψτ​(y)∣2=e−y2/τ2/(π​τ). Then ∣ϕτ​(y)∣2=2y2ρ(y)/τ2 and ρ′=−2yρ/τ2. Thus, for differentiable f with suitable Gaussian integrability and vanishing boundary terms,
EF​[f]=τ22​∫y2f(y)ρ(y)dy=−∫yf(y)ρ′(y)dy=∫(f(y)+yf′(y))ρ(y)dy.​
(9)
This is integration by parts, and every polynomial satisfies the required endpoint conditions. The relative-coordinate moments in even and odd oscillator ground states follow by substituting f(y)=yn:
EF​[y−n​]=(n+1)EB​[y−n​].​
(10)
For odd n, both expectations vanish by parity, so this is an equality of zeros rather than an increase in signed mean separation. The even moments show the physical difference. In particular EB​[y−2​]=τ2/2 and EF​[y−2​]=3τ2/2, so the fermionic root-mean-square particle separation is 3​ times the bosonic value. The fermionic probability density vanishes at coincidence y−​=0, expressing Pauli exclusion principle in this spatial symmetry sector. This is an exchange constraint on the wave function, not an additional classical force. As α approaches π/2, the relative oscillator softens and its width diverges; the excluded endpoint would no longer have this normalizable oscillator ground state.

Use an expansion for the eigenstate continuing the nondegenerate level, with intermediate normalization ⟨ψ0​,ψ(ϵ)⟩=1:
E(ϵ)=E0​+ϵE(1)+ϵ2E(2)+O(ϵ3),ψ(ϵ)=ψ0​+ϵχ+ϵ2η+⋯,⟨ψ0​,χ⟩=0.
(11)
Insert these expressions into the eigenvalue equation. At first order,
(H0​−E0​)χ=−(H′−E(1))ψ0​.
(12)
Taking the inner product with ψ0​ eliminates the left side by self-adjointness. Thus
E(1)=⟨ψ0​,H′ψ0​⟩,E(ϵ)=⟨ψ0​,Hψ0​⟩+O(ϵ2).​
(13)
For j=0, projection onto ψj​ instead gives
⟨ψj​,χ⟩=E0​−Ej​⟨ψj​,H′ψ0​⟩​.
(14)
At second order, projecting the eigenvalue equation onto ψ0​ gives E(2)=⟨ψ0​,H′χ⟩. Expand χ in the orthonormal energy basis and use self-adjointness of H′:
ϵ2E(2)=ϵ2j=0∑​E0​−Ej​∣⟨ψj​,H′ψ0​⟩∣2​.​
(15)
Nondegeneracy ensures the denominators for this level do not vanish; convergence and validity of the expansion are among the assumed perturbation-theory hypotheses.
For the cosine perturbation of the ground level in a one-dimensional box, write u=πx/a. The product-to-sum identity gives
cos(2u)sinu=21​(sin(3u)−sinu),
(16)
so, with the given common normalization of the sine eigenfunctions,
Vϕ1​=2ϵ​(ϕ3​−ϕ1​).​
(17)
The diagonal matrix element is −ϵ/2, and the only nonzero off-diagonal matrix element out of the ground state is ⟨ϕ3​,Vϕ1​⟩=ϵ/2. Let Ebox​=π2ℏ2/(2ma2). Since E3​=9Ebox​, the ground energy is
Eground​=Ebox​−2ϵ​+Ebox​−9Ebox​ϵ2/4​+O(ϵ3)=2ma2π2ℏ2​−2ϵ​−16π2ℏ2ma2ϵ2​+O(ϵ3).​
(18)
The second-order term is negative, as expected for a nondegenerate ground level: every excited-quantum state denominator is negative. The first-order shift is also negative for positive ϵ, because the ground-quantum state density is largest near the center, where this perturbing potential is negative.

Use the Dirac notation convention that the inner product is linear in the ket. Orthonormality of the computational basis gives
⟨k∣f⟩=∑ℓ∈ZN​​f(ℓ)⟨k∣ℓ⟩=f(k).
(19)
The finite geometric sum satisfies ∑k=0N−1​λrk=N if r=0 in ZN​, and zero otherwise: in the second case it equals (1−λrN)/(1−λr)=0. Therefore
⟨m∣n⟩=N1​∑k​λ(n−m)k=δmn​.
(20)
There are N such orthonormal vectors in an N-dimensional space, so they form a basis. The inverse expansion is verified by the same sum:
N​1​j∑​λ−jk∣j​⟩=N1​j,ℓ∑​λj(ℓ−k)∣ℓ⟩=∣k⟩.​
(21)
This is the unitary discrete Fourier transform and its inverse.
For the basis-copying isometry, the images ∣k⟩∣k⟩ are orthonormal, so C is a linear isometry, with C∗C=I. Its matrix elements give
⟨Ck∣∣f⟩∣g⟩=⟨k∣f⟩⟨k∣g⟩=f(k)g(k)=⟨k∣f.g⟩.
(22)
Applying the definition of the adjoint operator, the coefficient of ∣k⟩ in C∗∣f⟩∣g⟩ is exactly this expression. Thus
C∗∣f⟩∣g⟩=∣f.g⟩.​
(23)
Equivalently C∗∣k⟩∣ℓ⟩=δkℓ​∣k⟩. The adjoint of basis copying acts as pointwise multiplication of coefficient functions.
For the Fourier transform of a basis-copying isometry, the normalization matters:
fm​(k)fn​(k)=Nλ(m+n)k​=N​1​fm+n​(k).
(24)
Hence C∗∣m⟩∣n⟩=N−1/2∣m+n​⟩. Taking adjoint matrix elements, or expanding both output factors in the inverse Fourier basis, yields
C∣j​⟩=N​1​m+n=j∑​∣m⟩∣n⟩.​
(25)
The printed formula is missing the factor 1/N​. There are N mutually orthogonal terms in its sum, so without that factor its right side has norm N​, whereas its left side has norm one because C is an isometry. The formula above is the normalized identity consistent with all the preceding definitions. For N=1 the distinction disappears, but the no-cloning assertion is substantive for N≥2.
The normalized output is not ∣j​⟩∣j​⟩. Its coefficient matrix pairs every Fourier label m with j−m; tracing out either factor gives I/N. Thus it is a maximally entangled state, while two clones of a pure Fourier quantum state would have pure reduced quantum states. A copier for the computational basis therefore does not copy the Fourier basis.
More generally, suppose a linear isometry with a fixed blank input could clone every normalized quantum state. Its restriction to ∣ψ⟩∣blank⟩ would define a linear map D with D∣k⟩=∣k⟩∣k⟩ on every computational basis quantum state. Linearity forces D=C, which has just been shown to fail on Fourier quantum states. This proves the no-cloning theorem in the pure-quantum state isometric setting.
The inner-product proof makes the obstruction independent of the chosen basis. If an isometry cloned two quantum states with overlap s=⟨ψ∣ϕ⟩, preservation of inner products would give s=s2, or, allowing irrelevant output phases, ∣s∣=∣s∣2. Distinct nonorthogonal rays have 0<∣s∣<1, which is impossible. Even allowing an output environment does not help: unitary evolution would give ∣s∣=∣s∣2∣⟨eψ​∣eϕ​⟩∣≤∣s∣2, the same contradiction. Arbitrary unknown quantum states cannot be cloned, although any fixed orthonormal basis can be copied and a one-dimensional quantum state space is trivial.
