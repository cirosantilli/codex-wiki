A permutation π of identical particles multiplies a fermionic state by sgn(π); a bosonic state is invariant. Particle exchange keeps y+​ fixed and sends y−​ to −y−​. Consequently the spatial wave function is even in y−​ for the stated bosonic model and odd for the stated fermionic model. This assumes no additional spin variables in the supplied model; exchange of full particle states is the underlying rule.
The coordinate change is orthogonal, with unit Jacobian determinant, so the kinetic energy becomes
H=−2mℏ2​(∂y+​2​+∂y−​2​)
(1)
on the square ∣y+​∣,∣y−​∣<a with zero boundary values. Product states ψr​(y+​)ψs​(y−​) have energy
Er,s​=K(r2+s2),K=8ma2π2ℏ2​.
(2)
The one-dimensional eigenfunction is even for odd s and odd for even s. Bosons therefore allow odd s, whereas fermions require even s. The lowest bosonic pair is (r,s)=(1,1); its first excited pair is (2,1). The lowest fermionic pair is (1,2). Thus
EB,0​=2K=4ma2π2ℏ2​,EB,1​=EF,0​=5K=8ma25π2ℏ2​.​
(3)
The printed bosonic ground-energy claim omits the factor π2/4; the value above follows directly from the supplied one-dimensional spectrum and kinetic energy.
The separation is ∣x1​−x2​∣=2​∣y−​∣. The normalized y+​ factor integrates out. In the fermionic ground state, the relative-coordinate density is a−1sin2(πy−​/a), giving
⟨∣y−​∣⟩F​=a2​∫0a​ysin2(πy/a)dy=2a​.
(4)
For the bosonic ground state it is a−1cos2(πy−​/(2a)), giving
⟨∣y−​∣⟩B​=a2​∫0a​ycos2(πy/(2a))dy=a(21​−π22​).
(5)
Both integrals follow by replacing squared trigonometric functions by their constant and cosine parts and integrating once by parts. Hence
⟨∣x1​−x2​∣⟩F​=2​a​,⟨∣x1​−x2​∣⟩B​=2​a(21​−π22​),​
(6)
and the difference is 22​a/π2​. The odd relative state vanishes at coincidence, which increases its expected separation.

Let E=fH​(ψ). For any admissible variation η, differentiation with respect to a real parameter gives
dtd​fH​(ψ+tη)​t=0​=∥ψ∥22Re⟨η∣(H−E)ψ⟩​.
(7)
Stationarity for all variations, including their imaginary multiples, forces (H−E)ψ=0; conversely this eigenvector equation makes the derivative vanish. Thus stationary Rayleigh quotients are exactly eigenvectors, with their eigenvalues as the quotient values.
Put κ=e2/(4πϵ0​) and assume α>0. The squared norm of the radial trial function is 4π∫0∞​r2e−2αrdr=π/α3. Its kinetic expectation, computed through ∫∣∇ψ∣2, is ℏ2α2/(2m)=κaα2/2. Splitting the potential integral at R gives
⟨V⟩=−4κα3(R1​∫0R​r2e−2αrdr+∫R∞​re−2αrdr)=−Rκ​[1−(1+αR)e−2αR].
(8)
For example, the first integral is [1−e−2s(1+2s+2s2)]/(4α3) and the second is e−2s(R/(2α)+1/(4α2)), where s=αR. Therefore
fH​(ψα​)=κ[2aα2​−R1−(1+αR)e−2αR​].​
(9)
Its derivative is κ[aα−(1+2αR)e−2αR]. The actual stationary condition is consequently
aα=(1+2αR)e−2αR.​
(10)
The extra Coulomb factor in the printed stationary equation is an error: the common prefactor cancels on differentiation. The corrected equation is dimensionless on both sides. Its left side increases from zero, while its right side decreases from one, so it has one positive solution and gives the minimum in this trial family.
Let ε=R/a and y=aα. Expansion of (1+2s)e−2s gives 1−2s2+O(s3). Thus
aα=1−2ε2+O(ε3),α=a1​[1−2(R/a)2+O((R/a)3)].​
(11)
There is no linear correction in R/a. By the quantum variational principle, any normalized trial state gives an upper bound on the true ground energy. Choosing α=1/a already gives the exact bound
E0​(R)≤aκ​[21​−ε1−(1+ε)e−2ε​].​
(12)
Its small-radius expansion is
E0​(R)≤aκ​[−21​+32​ε2+O(ε3)].
(13)
Using the optimized α improves this bound starting at order ε4, and hence gives the same leading finite-size correction. The true energy is also at least the point-nucleus value −κ/(2a), since the finite-radius potential is nowhere more attractive.

A density operator is a positive self-adjoint trace-class operator with trace one. In finite dimensions this is a positive semidefinite Hermitian matrix with trρ=1. Its expectation of an observable is ⟨A⟩=tr(Aρ)​ whenever the expectation is defined.
In the source's square-root notation, take the unique positive square root W=ρ​. It has Hilbert-Schmidt norm one and satisfies WW∗=ρ, so
tr(Aρ)=tr(W∗AW).​
(14)
The standard vector purification is the associated vector ∣W⟩⟩=∑r,s​Wrs​∣ξr​⟩∣ηs​⟩ on a system and a chosen copy. Tracing out the copy gives ρ; measuring A⊗I gives the same trace formula. Thus the operator square root and the purified vector are two related objects, distinguished by this vectorization.
For a general coefficient matrix C, expansion of the expectation gives
⟨Ψ∣A⊗I∣Ψ⟩=∑r,t,s​crs​​Art​cts​=tr(ACC∗).
(15)
Thus ρ1​=CC∗​. Positivity is immediate from v∗CC∗v=∥C∗v∥2, and its trace is one by normalization of Ψ.
If C is nonsingular, it is square and ρ1​ is positive definite. Define
U=C−1ρ1​​.​
(16)
Then UU∗=C−1ρ1​(C∗)−1=I, which for a square matrix also implies U∗U=I. Thus U is unitary, ρ1​​=CU, and C=ρ1​​U∗.
In an eigenbasis of ρ1​, its square root is diag(pr​​). Hence crs​=pr​​Usr​​. Define
∣ζr​⟩=s∑​Usr​​∣ηs​⟩.​
(17)
Unitarity gives ⟨ζr​∣ζt​⟩=δrt​, and substitution gives the Schmidt decomposition
∣Ψ⟩=r∑​pr​​∣ξr​⟩∣ζr​⟩.​
(18)
For singular or rectangular C, the same conclusion follows on its support by a singular value decomposition; the stated nonsingular case needs only the unitary calculation above.
For the supplied Bell state, the requested matrices are
C=2​1​(0−1​10​),ρ1​=21​I,ρ1​​=2​1​I,U=(01​−10​).​
(19)
The Schmidt vectors can be chosen as ζ1​=η2​, ζ2​=−η1​, and the two coefficients are 1/2​. The square root's canonical vector purification is (∣ξ1​η1​⟩+∣ξ2​η2​⟩)/2​; the original Bell vector is a different purification of the same reduced state, related by a unitary on the second factor. This illustrates the unitary freedom of purification.
