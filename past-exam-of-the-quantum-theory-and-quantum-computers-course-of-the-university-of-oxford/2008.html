Let U(t)=e−iHt/ℏ and write ψS​(t)=U(t)ψ0​ in the Schrödinger picture, with observables AS​ constant unless they have an explicit time dependence. In the Heisenberg picture the state is ψH​=ψ0​ and
AH​(t)=U(t)∗AS​U(t),A˙H​=ℏi​[H,AH​]+U∗(∂t​AS​)U.​
(1)
Differentiate the two unitary factors to obtain the equation. Unitarity gives ⟨ψS​,AS​ψS​⟩=⟨ψ0​,AH​ψ0​⟩.
For the interaction picture choose a reference split H=H0​+V, put U0​=e−iH0​t/ℏ, and define ψI​=U0∗​ψS​, AI​=U0∗​AS​U0​. Then iℏψ˙​I​=VI​ψI​, VI​=U0∗​VU0​, while AI​ evolves with H0​ (and its explicit time dependence). Again
⟨ψI​,AI​ψI​⟩=⟨ψS​,AS​ψS​⟩=⟨ψ0​,AH​ψ0​⟩.​
(2)
These are different unitary allocations of the same dynamics between states and observables, so measured expectations agree.
The canonical commutation relation now gives
X˙t​=Pt​/m,P˙t​=−mω2Xt​−E,A˙t​=−iωAt​−E.​
(3)
Multiplication of the last equation by eiωt and integration gives
At​=e−iωtA0​−ωiE​(e−iωt−1).​
(4)
Since At∗​−At​=2imωXt​ and A0​ψ0​=0, both initial expectations of A0​,A0∗​ vanish. Therefore
⟨Xt​⟩=−mω2E​(1−cosωt).​
(5)
The printed positive displacement endpoint has the wrong sign for the Hamiltonian's positive EX term. The two turning values are 0 and −2E/(mω2); their ordering depends on the sign of E. The result oscillates about the displaced equilibrium −E/(mω2), exactly as the force −E predicts.

Choose an orthonormal eigenbasis H0​∣n⟩=En​∣n⟩ and the phase convention ⟨0∣ψ′⟩=0. Comparing coefficients of the eigenvalue equation at first order yields
(H0​−E0​)ψ′=−(H′−E′)∣0⟩.
(6)
Projecting onto ∣0⟩ and the other eigenvectors gives
E′=⟨0∣H′∣0⟩,ψ′=n=0∑​E0​−En​⟨n∣H′∣0⟩​∣n⟩.​
(7)
Projection of the next-order equation onto ∣0⟩ gives
E′′=⟨0∣H′∣ψ′⟩=n=0∑​E0​−En​∣⟨n∣H′∣0⟩∣2​.​
(8)
These are coefficients of the printed series, so E′′ denotes the quadratic coefficient rather than the second derivative itself. Without a phase convention an arbitrary purely imaginary multiple of ψ0​ can be added to the first vector correction. Continuous spectrum, if present, requires the corresponding spectral integral; the oscillator has a discrete complete eigenbasis.
Put K=mω2/2 and let g(x)=(2πσ2)−1/2e−x2/(2σ2) denote the ground-state density. Then H′=K(a2−x2)1[−a,a]​ and p=∫−aa​g(x)dx. Because g′=−xg/σ2, integration by parts yields
I2​:=∫−aa​x2gdx=σ2p−2aσ2g(a),I4​:=∫−aa​x4gdx=3σ4p−2aσ2(a2+3σ2)g(a).
(9)
Thus the two first-order shifts are
E0′​=K[(a2−σ2)p+2aσ2g(a)],E1′​=K[(a2−3σ2)p+6aσ2g(a)].​
(10)
The first is the supplied ground-state expression; the second follows because the excited-state density is x2g/σ2, so E1′​=K(a2I2​−I4​)/σ2.
Subtracting, and using Kσ2=ℏω/4, gives
E1′​−E0′​=−2ℏω​[p(a)−2ag(a)].​
(11)
The bracket is zero at a=0, tends to one at infinity, and has derivative 2a2g(a)/σ2≥0. Therefore the first-order predicted level gap is
E1app​−E0app​=ℏω[1−2λ​{p(a)−2ag(a)}].​
(12)
For every λ>2 it becomes negative for all sufficiently large a, crossing when p−2ag=2/λ. It is still positive at a=0: the stated conclusion must be read with the large-a regime, rather than for every interval width.
For λ>1 the inner potential curves downward, placing a central barrier between two symmetric minima at ±a. The even unperturbed ground state has its greatest density at the central barrier, while the odd first excited state has a node there and is less penalized. This explains the relative first-order shifts. A negative predicted gap signals failure of that perturbation approximation, not inversion of the exact lowest two states: the true one-dimensional ground state remains nodeless and even, with the odd state above it. At a high barrier the actual low levels instead form a close double-well pair. The apparent level crossing from a finite-interval oscillator perturbation is useful precisely as a diagnostic of the approximation's range.

A density operator is a positive self-adjoint trace-class operator with Trρ=1​. A finite expectation is ⟨A⟩ρ​=Tr(ρA)​. It represents a pure state exactly when ρ=∣v⟩⟨v∣ for a unit vector, equivalently ρ2=ρ or Trρ2=1.
For a finite-dimensional spectral decomposition ρ=∑j​pj​∣vj​⟩⟨vj​∣, introduce an auxiliary space with orthonormal vectors fj​ and use the enlarged space K=H⊗E. Define
Ωρ​=∑j​pj​​vj​⊗fj​,A=A⊗I.
(13)
Then ∥Ωρ​∥2=∑pj​=1 and ⟨Ωρ​,AΩρ​⟩=∑pj​⟨vj​,Avj​⟩=Tr(ρA). This purification shows that subsystem mixedness can arise from entanglement with an unobserved environment even when the total state is pure. Subsystem measurements alone cannot distinguish these two descriptions.
A nonzero pure bipartite vector is entangled when it cannot be written as a tensor product of two vectors; equivalently its Schmidt rank exceeds one. For mixed bipartite states, entanglement means absence of a convex decomposition into product density operators.
For the nonorthogonal expansion in the question, the unnormalized reduced operator is
S1​=j,k∑​⟨ηk​,ηj​⟩∣ξj​⟩⟨ξk​∣.​
(14)
Indeed ⟨Ψ,(A⊗I)Ψ⟩=∑j,k​⟨ξk​,Aξj​⟩⟨ηk​,ηj​⟩=Tr(AS1​). Positivity follows because ⟨v,S1​v⟩=∥∑j​⟨v,ξj​⟩ηj​∥2≥0, and TrS1​=∥Ψ∥2=:Z. Thus ρ1​=S1​/Z​ is a density operator; if Ψ is already normalized, Z=1. The Gram factors cannot be dropped merely because the vectors are called bases. For unnormalized Ψ, physical expectations likewise divide its raw quadratic form by Z.
For the Schmidt decomposition diagonalize S1​ in an orthonormal basis ξj​ with its positive eigenvalues cj2​. Expand Ψ=∑j​ξj​⊗wj​ with wj​=(⟨ξj​∣⊗I)Ψ. The reduced matrix gives ⟨wk​,wj​⟩=cj2​δjk​. Therefore ηj​=wj​/cj​ are orthonormal and
Ψ=j:cj​>0∑​cj​ξj​⊗ηj​.​
(15)
Zero-eigenvalue components vanish because their squared norms are zero. This proves the decomposition for an arbitrary nonzero vector; the zero vector uses the empty sum. For a unit vector ∑cj2​=1.
In the numerical example the coefficient matrix in the two product bases is C=(10​21​32​). Taking the first-subsystem trace yields
S2​=CTC=​123​258​3813​​.​
(16)
The vector (1,−2,1)T is in its nullspace, so rank is at most two; its leading 2×2 minor has determinant one, so rankS2​=2​. This is the rank of C and the Schmidt rank, proving entanglement. The displayed source ket has norm squared 19, and the displayed reduced matrix has trace 19. The physical density operator is ρ2​=S2​/19​; its nonzero eigenvalues are (19±337​)/38, since CCT=(148​85​). The raw matrix is correct for the unnormalized quadratic form actually printed.

Put d=2N and λ=e2πi/d, with nonnegative integer N so the register exists. The geometric sum satisfies
∑j=0d−1​λj(k−l)={d,0,​k=l(modd),k=l(modd).​
(17)
Consequently the matrix F+​ with entries d−1/2λjk has F+∗​F+​=I. This proves unitarity and
f(j)=d−1/2k=0∑d−1​λ−jkf​(k).​
(18)
For a binary output string j=∑r=1N​jr​2N−r, expansion of the printed tensor product gives coefficient
d−1/2∏r=1N​λ2N−rkjr​=d−1/2λkj.
(19)
Thus the printed product is exactly F+​∣k⟩. It is a correct product decomposition of a Fourier phase state, but the printed recovery identity has inconsistent signs:
k∑​f​(k)F+​∣k⟩=j∑​f(−jmodd)∣j⟩,​
(20)
by the square of the quantum Fourier transform. To recover f(j) instead, use F−​=F+∗​, replacing every product phase by its complex conjugate. Equivalently one may reverse the sign in the definition of f​. The discrepancy is visible for d≥4 by taking f concentrated at 1: the printed expression returns the basis vector at d−1. The recovery display also uses k as the summation variable on its right while its summand uses j; that dummy variable should be j.
For factorization first check gcd(3,91)=1. Successive residues are
30,31,…,36≡1,3,9,27,81,61,1(mod91).
(21)
The first return is r=6​. Its half power is 33=27, which is neither 1 nor −1 modulo 91. Therefore
gcd(27−1,91)=13,gcd(27+1,91)=7,91=7⋅13.​
(22)
In Shor's algorithm prepare a uniform exponent register of size Q=2n, conveniently 912≤Q<2⋅912, and reversibly compute 3xmod91 into a second register. Measuring that second register, or tracing it out, leaves periodic exponent correlations of period r. Conditional on one measured residue, the first register is a superposition over an arithmetic progression of exponents separated by r. The quantum Fourier transform converts this periodic structure into peaks near k/Q=s/r. Continued fractions recover s/r; when s is coprime to r, its denominator is r. Verify by modular exponentiation and repeat when the reduced denominator is insufficient. For this example Q=16384 is suitable, and useful peaks include those near Q/6 and 5Q/6. Either transform sign gives the same period information, so the source's inverse-sign error does not invalidate the order-finding mechanism.
The Part C inverse-transform display also prints j as its summation index while the summand uses k; that sum must be over k, as in the corrected inverse above.
