Let U(t)=e−iHt/ℏ and take an observable with no explicit time dependence. In the Schrödinger picture, ψS​(t)=U(t)ψ0​ and AS​=A. In the Heisenberg picture, ψH​=ψ0​ and AH​(t)=U(t)†AU(t). For the interaction picture choose a time-independent splitting H=H0​+V, put U0​=e−iH0​t/ℏ, and define
ψI​=U0†​ψS​,AI​=U0†​AU0​,VI​=U0†​VU0​.
(1)
Then iℏψ˙​I​=VI​ψI​ and A˙I​=(i/ℏ)[H0​,AI​]. Unitarity gives the identical expectation in all three descriptions:
⟨ψS​,AψS​⟩=⟨ψ0​,AH​ψ0​⟩=⟨ψI​,AI​ψI​⟩.​
(2)
Different choices of the split interpolate between pictures; H0​=H makes the state constant, while H0​=0 gives the Schrödinger description.
Differentiate U†AU to obtain the Heisenberg equation of motion
A˙H​=ℏi​[H,AH​].​
(3)
For an explicitly time-dependent observable there is additionally U†(∂t​A)U. Thus HH​=H is constant. In a normalized energy eigenstate, ⟨[H,AH​]⟩=E⟨AH​⟩−E⟨AH​⟩=0, so the expectation of its Heisenberg derivative vanishes whenever the products are defined.
For the harmonic oscillator, the canonical commutator gives
X˙=P/m,P˙=−mω2X,dtd​(XP)=P2/m−mω2X2.​
(4)
The last identity follows from the operator product rule without commuting X and P. Although XP is not itself self-adjoint, the same calculation can be applied to the self-adjoint dilation observable (XP+PX)/2 and gives the same derivative.
Stationarity of the three expectations gives ⟨P⟩=⟨X⟩=0 and ⟨P2⟩=m2ω2⟨X2⟩. Substitution into E=⟨P2⟩/(2m)+mω2⟨X2⟩/2 yields the quantum virial theorem
⟨2mP2​⟩=2E​=21​mω2⟨X2⟩.​
(5)
The vanishing means make the variances equal to the second moments, so
(ΔP)2=mE,(ΔX)2=mω2E​,ΔXΔP=ωE​=(n+21​)ℏ≥ℏ/2.​
(6)
This satisfies the position-momentum Heisenberg uncertainty relation, with equality only for the ground state.

For a nonzero trial vector use the Rayleigh quotient E(ψ)=⟨ψ,Hψ⟩/⟨ψ,ψ⟩. Its first variation in any allowed direction η is
δE=∥ψ∥22Re⟨η,(H−E)ψ⟩​.
(7)
Vanishing for all complex directions implies (H−E)ψ=0; an eigenvector conversely makes it vanish. The ground energy is the infimum of this quotient for a lower-bounded Hamiltonian, so minimizing over a trial family provides an upper bound to it, exact when that family contains a ground eigenvector.
Set a=⟨ϕ,Hϕ⟩, b=⟨ϕ,Iϕ⟩ and c=⟨ϕ,HIϕ⟩. All three are real: H,I are self-adjoint and commute. Also I†I=I2=1. Expansion of the numerator and norm gives
E(t)=1+t2+2tb(1+t2)a+2tc​.​
(8)
Differentiate the quotient, retaining only nonzero trial vectors:
E′(t)=(1+t2+2tb)22(c−ab)(1−t2)​.
(9)
Thus the stationarity equation is (t2−1)(c−ab)=0​. If c=ab, the quotient is identically a wherever defined. Otherwise t=±1 are the two extrema, with
E±​=1±ba±c​,E+​−E−​=1−b22(c−ab)​.​
(10)
In this nonconstant case ∣b∣<1, so both denominators are positive. If c−ab>0, the plus value is the maximum and the minus value the minimum; the assignments reverse when c−ab<0. One can also see global extremality by resolving the trial vector into the orthogonal I=±1 sectors: its energy is a weighted mean of their two fixed projected energies.
The null-vector exception matters. If ∣b∣=1, then Iϕ=±ϕ, the covariance c−ab vanishes and one of ϕ±Iϕ is zero. That value has no Rayleigh quotient and is not an extra stationary physical state.
For particle exchange, change of integration variables shows that Iϕ(x1​,x2​)=ϕ(x2​,x1​) is unitary and I2=1, hence self-adjoint. Exchanging coordinates interchanges the two kinetic derivatives and leaves the symmetric potential unchanged, so [I,H]=0. The vectors ϕ±Iϕ lie in its symmetric and antisymmetric sectors. Their normalized nonzero versions are respectively bosonic and fermionic spatial trial wavefunctions.
Because the energy of a general trial state is the weighted average of its sector energies, at least one nonzero symmetry projection has energy no greater than the original trial. Thus minimizing can be performed in a definite exchange-symmetry sector. This is a variational approximation within the trial subspace; it does not assert that projecting an arbitrary trial vector produces an exact eigenvector of H. For particles with a prescribed statistics sector one minimizes within that physical sector.

For classical events, A∖C⊂(A∖B)∪(B∖C): an outcome in A but not in C either fails B or belongs to B∖C. Monotonicity and the union bound prove the Fréchet-Bell inequality
P(A∖C)≤P(A∖B)+P(B∖C).
(11)
Malus' law gives transmission cos2γ for a photon polarized by the first filter, so its conditional rejection probability at the second is sin2γ​. Conditional and joint probabilities must be distinguished: P(A∖B)=P(A)P(Bc∣A).
For equal unbiased marginals P(A)=P(B)=1/2, the quantum two-setting rejection probabilities are 21​sin2(θA​−θB​). A classical common assignment of all three outcomes would therefore require sin2(θA​−θB​)+sin2(θB​−θC​)≥sin2(θA​−θC​). Choose directions 0,γ,2γ. Then
2sin2γ<sin2(2γ)when 0<γ<π/4.​
(12)
For example γ=π/6 gives 1/2<3/4. More generally sin2a+sin2b−sin2(a+b)=−2sinasinbcos(a+b). In a triangle whose side directions are these filters, the internal angle supplementary to a+b is obtuse exactly in this violation range. This specifies the angle convention behind the triangle description: with polarization axes regarded as unoriented lines, a claim that every sufficiently large physical axis separation violates the inequality would not be correct. The contradiction concerns a single classical joint model for all settings, not the validity of ordinary probability or a comparison of arbitrarily renormalized sequential experiments.
Use ∣0⟩=∣α0​⟩ and ∣1⟩=∣α1​⟩. Projecting the first photon with ∣0⟩⟨0∣ gives the unnormalized product vector c0​∣00⟩, with success probability ∣c0​∣2. Projection of the second onto ∣θ⟩=cosθ∣0⟩+sinθ∣1⟩ gives
c0​cosθ∣0⟩∣θ⟩,P(both transmissions)=∣c0​∣2cos2θ.​
(13)
Conditioned on success, normalize these vectors; a zero amplitude means that outcome never occurs. For the maximally entangled equal-amplitude pair, rotation of both real polarization bases gives the same correlations for arbitrary settings. Comparing the appropriately normalized coincidence/rejection frequencies at three setting pairs tests the inequality above. A general unequal-amplitude state need not reproduce the unbiased rotationally invariant probabilities, so the maximally entangled choice supplies the clean explicit example.
For swapping, label the four systems in order ξ1​,η1​,ξ2​,η2​ and let ∣Φ⟩η​=2−1/2∑j=01​∣ηj​ηj​⟩. Its overlap with ∣ηr​ηs​⟩ is δrs​/2​. Applying its rank-one projector to Alice's two systems gives
21​r,k∑​br2​∣ξr​⟩∣ηk​⟩∣ξr​⟩∣ηk​⟩.​
(14)
After regrouping the remote and local systems, this factorizes into [∑r​br2​∣ξr​ξr​⟩/2​]⊗∣Φ⟩η​. The selected outcome has probability p=21​∑r​∣br​∣4. Therefore the normalized conditional remote state is
∣χ⟩ξ​=∑r​∣br​∣4​∑r​br2​∣ξr​ξr​⟩​.​
(15)
It has Schmidt rank at least two whenever the original pair was entangled, since squaring a nonzero coefficient does not make it zero. Thus the two photons that never interacted become entangled conditional on Alice's joint measurement outcome. This is entanglement swapping. Alice is projecting onto a specified Bell state, not performing a universal test of whether an arbitrary unknown state is entangled. Averaging all her outcomes leaves the remote reduced density operator unchanged, so no faster-than-light signal is available without her classical outcome label.
