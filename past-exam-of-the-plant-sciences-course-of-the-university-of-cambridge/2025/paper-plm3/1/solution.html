The habitat occupied by the conspicuous plant is only part of the life cycle: successful reproduction also requires suitable conditions for its other generation, dispersal and fertilization. In alternation of generations, a multicellular haploid gametophyte alternates with a multicellular diploid sporophyte. The transitions in ploidy occur at meiosis and fertilization; growth within either generation is by mitosis:

Thus spores disperse and initiate a generation, whereas gametes fuse. A plant gametophyte normally produces gametes by mitosis, not by another reduction division. Retention and nourishment of the young plant embryo on the maternal gametophyte distinguishes the land-plant reproductive arrangement from simply releasing a naked zygote into the environment.
In bryophytes, the persistent, usually most conspicuous generation is the gametophyte. It bears antheridia producing motile sperm and archegonia containing eggs. Following fertilization, the sporophyte remains attached to the maternal gametophyte and obtains resources through that association. Although some sporophytes photosynthesize, this does not make them nutritionally independent. The unbranched reproductive axis terminates in a single sporangium; elevating it can improve spore dispersal without requiring the whole gametophyte to grow tall.
This organization helps explain both constraints and successes on land. bryophyte gametophytes lack the true xylem and phloem of vascular plants, and often depend strongly on surface water and short transport distances. rhizoids provide attachment and can contribute to uptake, but are not anatomically equivalent to a root. Their small stature and limited control of water loss restrict sustained activity during drying. However, many tolerate drying and resume activity after rehydration: desiccation tolerance and avoidance of drying are different strategies. Periodic metabolic inactivity can permit persistence in exposed habitats even where continuous growth would be impossible.
Reproduction imposes an additional bottleneck. Motile sperm need a water connection to reach an egg, so successful vegetative survival on a dry surface does not imply that fertilization can occur there at any time. Rain, dew and protected microsites can provide intermittent opportunities. Separation of male and female gametophytes can add a spatial limitation. Resistant spore walls containing sporopollenin protect a dispersal stage, but a dispersed spore must still germinate and establish, and the resulting gametophyte must reproduce. Some mosses and hornworts have stomata on their sporophytes; liverworts lack true stomata, and their air pores are not equivalent regulated valves. It is therefore misleading to assign all these lineages one identical water-control mechanism.
Early land-plant evolution was not a ladder in which living mosses became living ferns. The Rhynie chert preserves reproductive bodies interpreted as gametophytes with axial growth, conducting tissues and stomata, sometimes sharing structural characters with their associated sporophytes. These fossils document combinations unlike those of modern bryophytes and caution against projecting a tiny modern fern gametophyte onto every early lineage.[ref] Identification depends on reproductive structures and anatomy; resemblance alone cannot demonstrate that two isolated fossil bodies belonged to one life cycle. Branching sporophytes bearing several sporangia increased reproductive output per fertilization event, while the evolution of effective transport and support permitted greater independence from the maternal generation.
In most familiar ferns, the large leafy individual is an independently growing sporophyte, with a root system, xylem, phloem, a plant cuticle and stomata. These features support water acquisition, long-distance transport and regulated exchange, allowing larger stature and access to a broader range of terrestrial environments. The sporophyte produces spores in sporangia, often clustered into sori. In a typical homosporous fern, a spore grows into a small free-living gametophyte, commonly a photosynthetic prothallus. The young sporophyte initially develops on it but becomes independent. Therefore sporophyte dominance reduces one kind of dependence without eliminating the ecological requirements of the other generation.
The fern gametophyte commonly lacks the hydraulic and water-conservation capacities of the mature sporophyte; establishment, competition, drying and the water requirement for sperm movement can limit recruitment. Yet it need not be uniformly more drought-sensitive. Experimental drying and rehydration of tropical fern gametophytes revealed substantial variation in recovery, with differences associated with habitat and repeated drying cycles.[ref] Such assays need hydrated controls and checks of subsequent growth or reproduction: recovery of chlorophyll fluorescence alone is not completion of the life cycle. The two generations can exploit different microsites, so habitat limitation is not simply the intersection of two identical environmental requirements.
There are important alternatives within the fern life cycle. heterosporous water ferns produce distinct microspores and megaspores, with reduced male and female gametophytes developing within the spore walls. This offers protection and changes provisioning, but it does not supply the delivery mechanism of a pollen tube that can remove dependence on an external water film. Nor does adaptation to an aquatic habitat demonstrate freedom from water for fertilization. Differences among homospory, heterospory, mating systems and vegetative persistence mean that a single generalized prothallus cannot describe every fern.
Developmental genetics confirms that the two generations are distinct developmental programs rather than automatic visual consequences of chromosome number. Removing KNOX2 regulators in a moss produced gametophyte-like bodies from diploid embryos without intervening meiosis.[ref] This does not reconstruct the historical ancestor, but demonstrates a mechanism by which the deployment of generations can evolve. Across these lineages, increasing sporophyte autonomy broadened terrestrial opportunities, while establishment of the gametophyte and water-dependent fertilization continued to filter where complete sexual life cycles could occur.
