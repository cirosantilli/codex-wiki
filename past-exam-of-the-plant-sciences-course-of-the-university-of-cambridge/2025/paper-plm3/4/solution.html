The innovation is the integration of male and female reproductive organs on one developmentally coordinated, usually determinate shoot. Its origin is best understood as a reorganization of older reproductive programs, although the exact extinct precursor remains uncertain. A bisexual flower bears both stamens and carpels; this differs from monoecy, in which separate male and female structures occur on one individual. Many living angiosperms have unisexual flowers, so bisexuality is an inferred ancestral feature and a recurrently modified condition, not a definition satisfied by every living member.
The starting materials already existed in ancestral seed plants: microsporangia producing the male lineage, ovules retaining the female lineage, protective structures and reproductive meristems. Living gymnosperms provide comparisons of these functions, but no extant cone can simply be declared the direct ancestor of the flower. A bisexual organization must establish male and female organ identities in distinct positions, integrate their maturation and regulate the growth of a common reproductive axis. Enclosure of ovules within carpels, a receptive surface for pollen, and the elaboration of the perianth are related innovations, but their historical order cannot be inferred merely by listing their present-day functions.
Developmental genetics shows how a common axis can generate different reproductive organs. In the ABC model of flower development, overlapping regulatory activities specify different organ types. For the reproductive regions, B- and C-class activity together, with suitable cofactors, specifies stamen identity, while C-class activity without B-class activity specifies carpel identity. In Arabidopsis thaliana, the relevant programs involve APETALA3, PISTILLATA and AGAMOUS, with SEPALLATA activities required for normal specification of the inner organs. Higher-order loss of SEPALLATA function transformed their identities, providing experimental evidence that the spatial code depends on additional regulatory partners.[ref] The simple A/B/C lettering summarizes functional experiments in model plants; its exact domains and functions are not universal across all flowers.
Many of these regulators are MADS-box transcription factors acting in interacting complexes. gene duplication, divergence of protein interactions, changes in target genes and cis-regulatory evolution can therefore generate new combinations and spatial boundaries without creating male and female development from nothing. Comparative work found related B-class activity in gymnosperms, including experimental effects of a Gnetum regulator expressed in Arabidopsis thaliana.[ref] This supports continuity in reproductive regulatory machinery. Heterologous activity does not show that a gymnosperm gene had the same entire function in its native ancestor, or that the modern angiosperm network was already assembled.
One plausible developmental route is spatial subdivision of a previously unisexual axis. In an out-of-male hypothesis, restriction of B-class activity within an originally male reproductive structure allows a distal region with C-class but no B-class activity to express female identity, while the proximal region retains male identity. Conversely, recruitment of male activity into part of an initially female structure is developmentally conceivable. These are comparative models of changing organ identities; contemporary homeotic transformations demonstrate accessibility of a developmental outcome, not the sequence of events that actually occurred in an extinct lineage.
The mostly male theory makes a more specific historical proposal: an ancestral predominantly male structure acquired ectopic ovules and ultimately a bisexual organization. Its arguments combine morphology, fossils and hypotheses about reproductive regulators, including the history of LEAFY and NEEDLY homologues.[ref] It should not be equated with every model involving a shift in B-class expression. Nor does the loss or retention of one duplicated regulator uniquely determine the ancestral cone morphology: expression domains can evolve, gene histories can be difficult to resolve, and the relevant intermediates may be extinct. Another possibility is condensation and reorganization of an axis bearing separate reproductive units. Fossil anatomy is needed to discriminate organ-level and whole-axis homologies.
ancestral state reconstruction across living angiosperms supports a bisexual ancestral flower, while leaving uncertainty about several details of its organization.[ref] This concerns the last common ancestor of living angiosperms, not necessarily the very first transitional reproductive shoot on their stem lineage. Different characters need not have arisen simultaneously. Fossils can constrain plausible combinations and their ordering, whereas developmental experiments reveal which reorganizations can work. Neither line of evidence alone establishes a complete historical sequence.
The adaptive opportunities of integration are substantial but conditional. A shared perianth or floral reward can attract a visitor that both removes pollen and deposits it on receptive stigma surfaces. Spatially coordinated organs can make transfer more precise and reduce the construction cost of maintaining separate displays. Bisexuality also permits flexible allocation between male and female reproduction as resources or mating opportunities change. When cross-pollination is unreliable, some self-compatible flowers can obtain reproductive assurance through self-fertilization. That benefit depends on compatibility and timing, however: bearing both organ types does not itself guarantee selfing.
Integration also creates conflicts. Transfer of a plant's own pollen to its receptive surfaces can reduce outcross pollen export, block opportunities for outcrossing, or produce inbreeding depression. herkogamy separates organs spatially, dichogamy separates their maturation in time, and self-incompatibility can reject genetically unsuitable pollen. These mechanisms show how the benefits of a shared shoot can be retained while limiting self-fertilization; they need not all have accompanied its initial origin. Later transitions to separate sexes can be favored in other ecological or genetic settings.
Tests of adaptive significance should compare pollen export, receipt, seed set and offspring performance, rather than count visits alone. Manipulate organ position or timing while controlling display size and reward; compare cross-pollination and autonomous selfing treatments, with emasculation and hand-pollination controls where appropriate. A diversification analysis must also account for shared ancestry and other innovations: the coincidence of bisexual flowers and the success of angiosperms does not isolate bisexuality as the cause of their species richness. A defensible account combines inherited reproductive regulatory modules, new spatial coordination and conditional reproductive advantages, while treating the precise precursor and causal role in diversification as hypotheses.
