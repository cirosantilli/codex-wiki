Regression toward the mean and randomized comparisons

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Regression toward the mean
OurBigBook.com Words: 195
For causal inference, a before-and-after change among units selected by an extreme baseline reading combines any causal effect with the change expected from selection. In the synthetic model the selected-group mean falls by about 0.610 with no intervention at all. Calling that entire fall an intervention effect would attribute a predictable measurement error contribution to a change in the signal.
One comparison design selects all eligible units by the same rule, then uses randomization to assign an intervention or a comparison condition, with the same measurement times and instrument in both groups. To see the principle algebraically, let assignment A∈{0,1} be independent of (T,ε1​,ε2​) within the selected population, and let the intervention add a constant δ to the second reading: Y=T+ε2​+δA. Then
E[Y−X∣X>1,A=1]=−0.610054…+δ,E[Y−X∣X>1,A=0]=−0.610054….
(24)
Subtracting the two expected changes isolates δ under these assumptions. Both groups experience the same selection tendency in expectation. A finite experiment still has sampling uncertainty; randomization does not guarantee identical realized groups. Without randomization, the same threshold alone does not establish comparable latent signals or error processes. Different selection rules, selective follow-up, changing instruments, or common temporal changes also require explicit assumptions. This is a statistical inference principle about comparisons, not evidence that any particular intervention works.
