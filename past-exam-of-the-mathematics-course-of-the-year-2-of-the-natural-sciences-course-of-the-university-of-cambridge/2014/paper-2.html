The necessary boundary identity for a self-adjoint operator is
[p(f′∗g−f∗g′)]ab​=0for every admissible f,g.​
(2)
At regular endpoints, a standard full family of separated self-adjoint conditions is
αa​y(a)+βa​p(a)y′(a)=0,αb​y(b)+βb​p(b)y′(b)=0,​
(3)
with real coefficients and neither pair (αa​,βa​), (αb​,βb​) zero. For example, if βa​=0, both functions obey pf′=−αa​f/βa​ at a, so pf′∗g−pf∗g′=0 there. The cases βa​=0 and the endpoint b follow directly. These include Dirichlet boundary conditions, Neumann boundary conditions and Robin boundary conditions.
Separate vanishing at each endpoint is sufficient but not necessary. Coupled conditions such as y(b)=eiχy(a) and p(b)y′(b)=eiχp(a)y′(a), with real χ, make the two endpoint forms equal and hence their difference zero. More generally the allowed endpoint data must be a maximal subspace on which the boundary form vanishes; this ensures that the adjoint has the same domain. Merely imposing extra endpoint restrictions until the boundary term vanishes proves symmetry, but can overrestrict the domain and fail to give self-adjointness.

For a constant orthogonal Cartesian change of coordinates xi′​=Qij​xj​, the tensor component transformation law for order n is
Ti1​⋯in​′​=Qi1​j1​​⋯Qin​jn​​Tj1​⋯jn​​.​
(43)
Repeated indices are summed. In Cartesian orthonormal coordinates the metric identifies upper and lower indices, so the same orthogonal matrix acts on every index.
An isotropic tensor has the same numerical components after every proper orthogonal change of axes: Q⊗nT=T for every Q with QTQ=I and detQ=1. For example, δij​ is isotropic because Qik​Qjk​=δij​. Some conventions require invariance under all orthogonal transformations, including reflections; this stronger convention must be distinguished for odd-order tensors, notably the Levi-Civita symbol, which is invariant under proper rotations but changes sign under its ordinary three-index transformation for a reflection.

A group representation assigns each g∈G an invertible linear map D(g) on a fixed vector space, preserving multiplication:
D(gh)=D(g)D(h),D(e)=I.
(69)
Choosing a basis turns those maps into matrices. A faithful representation is injective, equivalently its kernel is {e}, so distinct elements have distinct matrices.
Two group representations on spaces of the same dimension are called equivalent representations if one fixed invertible change of basis conjugates every representing matrix: E(g)=S−1D(g)S for all g. Choosing a different S separately for each element does not establish equivalence.
The character of a representation is
χD​(g)=trD(g).​
(70)
The cyclic property of the trace makes it invariant under a change of basis and constant on conjugacy classes. Thus different characters at even one group element prove inequivalence.
