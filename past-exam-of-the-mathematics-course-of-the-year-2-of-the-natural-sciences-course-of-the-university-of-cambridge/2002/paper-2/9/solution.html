Conjugacy classes. For a∈G, its conjugacy class is
Cl(a)={gag−1:g∈G}.​
(66)
Every element belongs to its own conjugacy class, by taking g=e. To prove uniqueness, suppose x=gag−1=hbh−1 belongs to both Cl(a) and Cl(b). Then
b=(h−1g)a(h−1g)−1.
(67)
For every t∈G, this makes tbt−1 a conjugate of a as well. Hence Cl(b)⊆Cl(a), and reversing the argument gives the other inclusion. Two conjugacy classes that meet are therefore equal, proving that the classes partition G.
Abelian case. If G is an abelian group, then gag−1=agg−1=a for every g, so Cl(a)={a}​ for each a.
Centre. An element has a singleton conjugacy class precisely when gzg−1=z for every g, equivalently gz=zg for every g. Thus the definition in the paper agrees with the usual centre of a group:
Z(G)={z∈G:gz=zg for every g∈G}.
(68)
The identity lies in Z(G). If z,w∈Z(G), then for any g, gzw=zgw=zwg, so zw∈Z(G). Also gz=zg implies z−1g=gz−1, so z−1∈Z(G). This proves it is a subgroup. Any two of its elements commute because either one commutes with every element of G. Consequently Z(G) is an abelian subgroup of G​.
Square symmetry. Use the paper's notation D4​ for the eight-element dihedral group of a square. Let r be a quarter-turn and s a reflection, so r4=s2=e and srs=r−1. A rotation rj commutes with s exactly when rj=r−j, or 2j=0 modulo four. This selects e and r2. No reflection rjs commutes with r, because
(rjs)r=rj−1s,r(rjs)=rj+1s,
(69)
and rj−1=rj+1. Therefore
Z(D4​)={e,r2}={identity,half-turn}.​
(70)
