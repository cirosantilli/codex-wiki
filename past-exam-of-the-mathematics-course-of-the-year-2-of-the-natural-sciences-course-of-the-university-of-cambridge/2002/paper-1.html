Nilpotence and eigenvalues. If Av=λv with v=0, then Anv=λnv. Thus An=0 forces λ=0 for every eigenvalue. Conversely, if all n complex eigenvalues, counted with algebraic multiplicity, vanish, the monic characteristic polynomial is χA​(t)=tn. The Cayley-Hamilton theorem then gives An=0. Hence
An=0⟺spec(A)={0}.​
(20)
This is the criterion for a nilpotent matrix.
Symmetric case. Here a symmetric matrix is understood to be real. The spectral theorem gives A=QDQT with Q orthogonal and D real diagonal. The previous result makes every diagonal entry zero, so A=0. Alternatively, choose 2r≥n, so A2r=0. For every real vector x,
xTA2rx=​A2r−1x​2=0.
(21)
It follows that A2r−1=0. Repeat until A=0. This uses positivity of the quadratic form xTMTMx. The restriction to real symmetric, or complex Hermitian matrices, matters: a complex matrix satisfying only AT=A need not have this positivity.
The parameters and powers. The first two columns of T coincide, so detT=0 for all α,β. The sum of its principal two-by-two determinants, each a minor of a matrix, is
0+(β−1)+(β−α)=2β−α−1.

Together with trT=2+β, this gives
χT​(t)=t3−(2+β)t2+(2β−α−1)t.
(23)
For every eigenvalue to vanish both remaining coefficients must be zero. Therefore
β=−2,α=−5.​

Geometry of repeated application. For any parameters the image of a linear map lies in the span of a=(1,1,1)T and b=(1,α,β)T, since Tx=(x1​+x2​)a+x3​b. Thus it lies in a fixed plane through the origin, independently of the input. When a,b are independent its normal is a×b=(β−α,1−β,α−1); if they coincide, the image is a line and is still contained in a plane.
For α=−5,β=−2, put s=x1​+x2​ and t=x3​. Then
Tx=(s+t,s−5t,s−2t)T,x1′​+x2′​−2x3′​=0.

T2x=3(x1​+x2​−2x3​)(1,−1,0)T,T3x=0.​
(27)
Thus the image of a linear map contracts from a plane to a line and then to the origin under successive applications. The line from T2 is precisely the kernel of T.

The original PDF uses the conjugate transpose N†, rather than the transpose found in the transcription. The printed diagonalisation criterion is correct when diagonalisation means unitary diagonalization of a normal matrix. Since N†=A−iB,
NN†=A2+B2−i(AB−BA),N†N=A2+B2+i(AB−BA).
(31)
Consequently NN†=N†N if and only if AB=BA. By part (ii), this gives a common unitary matrix U and
U†NU=DA​+iDB​,
(32)
which is diagonal. Conversely, if N=UDU† for a diagonal D and a unitary matrix U, then
NN†=UDD†U†=UD†DU†=N†N.
(33)
Thus the precise conclusion is
N is unitarily diagonalizable⟺N is normal⟺AB=BA.​
(34)
If arbitrary invertible changes of basis are allowed, the printed assertion is false. For example,
N=(10​12​),A=(11/2​1/22​),B=(0i/2​−i/20​)
(35)

Length functional. On the unit sphere, write the position vector in spherical coordinates as
r(θ,ϕ)=(sinθcosϕ,sinθsinϕ,cosθ).
(82)
Its two coordinate tangent vectors satisfy rθ​⋅rϕ​=0, ∣rθ​∣=1 and ∣rϕ​∣=sinθ. Consequently the induced Riemannian metric is ds2=dθ2+sin2θdϕ2. On a segment for which θ increases and ϕ=ϕ(θ),
S=∫θ1​θ2​​1+sin2θ(ϕ′)2​dθ.​
(83)
Segments where θ is not monotone require another parameter or a subdivision. Endpoint turning points can be handled by a limit.
First integral. With F=1+sin2θ(ϕ′)2​, there is no explicit ϕ dependence. The Euler-Lagrange equation therefore says that
∂ϕ′∂F​=1+sin2θ(ϕ′)2​sin2θϕ′​=c
(84)
is constant. For c=0, put k=1/∣c∣ and solve:
ϕ′=sinθk2sin2θ−1​sgnc​,ϕ(θ)−ϕ(θ1​)=sgnc∫θ1​θ​sinϑk2sin2ϑ−1​dϑ​.​

The radicand must be nonnegative along the chosen segment. The printed expression with both limits fixed at θ1​,θ2​ describes a longitude difference between endpoints; a formula for ϕ(θ) needs the variable upper limit and the additive constant shown here. The case c=0 gives a meridian with constant longitude. The equator is also a stationary route, although it cannot be parameterized using changing θ.
Integration and geometric meaning. Put t=cotθ, so dθ=−dt/(1+t2) and
sinθk2sin2θ−1​=1+t2k2−1−t2​​.
(86)
For a nonconstant segment k>1, and writing K=k2−1​, the integral becomes
ϕ−ϕ0​=∓arcsinKcotθ​.
(87)

cotθ=Acosϕ+Bsinϕ.​

Multiplying by sinθ shows that the corresponding Cartesian coordinates satisfy z=Ax+By. Thus every such stationary route lies in a plane through the sphere's center: it is a great circle, the geodesic of the sphere. The meridians and equator are the limiting or separately parameterized cases of the same description.
Rome to Singapore. Use exactly the approximate coordinates supplied in the question: (θ,ϕ)=(π/4,π/12) at departure and (π/2,7π/12) at arrival. Inserting them into the preceding relation determines
cotθ=cos(ϕ−π/12),12π​≤ϕ≤127π​.​

In latitude ℓ=π/2−θ, the route is ℓ=arctan[cos(ϕ−π/12)]: it starts at 45∘ north with an initially eastward heading and then curves southward to the equator. The first-integral expression in θ has an integrable endpoint singularity at departure, where θ has a turning point; the longitude parameter is regular there.
For an explicit regular arc-length parametrization, let a=r(π/4,π/12) and b=r(π/2,7π/12). Their dot product is sin(π/4)cos(π/2)=0. Hence
r(s)=acoss+bsins,0≤s≤2π​,Smin​=2π​.​

Integrating gives length at least the endpoint angle π/2, which this route attains. For a sphere of radius R, the corresponding shortest distance is πR/2.
