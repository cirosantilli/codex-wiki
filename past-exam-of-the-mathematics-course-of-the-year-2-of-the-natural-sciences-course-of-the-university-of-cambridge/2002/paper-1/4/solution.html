Nilpotence and eigenvalues. If Av=λv with v=0, then Anv=λnv. Thus An=0 forces λ=0 for every eigenvalue. Conversely, if all n complex eigenvalues, counted with algebraic multiplicity, vanish, the monic characteristic polynomial is χA​(t)=tn. The Cayley-Hamilton theorem then gives An=0. Hence
An=0⟺spec(A)={0}.​
(20)
This is the criterion for a nilpotent matrix.
Symmetric case. Here a symmetric matrix is understood to be real. The spectral theorem gives A=QDQT with Q orthogonal and D real diagonal. The previous result makes every diagonal entry zero, so A=0. Alternatively, choose 2r≥n, so A2r=0. For every real vector x,
xTA2rx=​A2r−1x​2=0.
(21)
It follows that A2r−1=0. Repeat until A=0. This uses positivity of the quadratic form xTMTMx. The restriction to real symmetric, or complex Hermitian matrices, matters: a complex matrix satisfying only AT=A need not have this positivity.
The parameters and powers. The first two columns of T coincide, so detT=0 for all α,β. The sum of its principal two-by-two determinants, each a minor of a matrix, is
0+(β−1)+(β−α)=2β−α−1.
(22)
Together with trT=2+β, this gives
χT​(t)=t3−(2+β)t2+(2β−α−1)t.
(23)
For every eigenvalue to vanish both remaining coefficients must be zero. Therefore
β=−2,α=−5.​
(24)
Direct multiplication at these values gives
T=​111​111​1−5−2​​,T2=​3−30​3−30​−660​​,T3=0.​
(25)
Indeed the first row of T2 multiplied by T is 3row1​(T)+3row2​(T)−6row3​(T)=0, and the second row is its negative.
Geometry of repeated application. For any parameters the image of a linear map lies in the span of a=(1,1,1)T and b=(1,α,β)T, since Tx=(x1​+x2​)a+x3​b. Thus it lies in a fixed plane through the origin, independently of the input. When a,b are independent its normal is a×b=(β−α,1−β,α−1); if they coincide, the image is a line and is still contained in a plane.
For α=−5,β=−2, put s=x1​+x2​ and t=x3​. Then
Tx=(s+t,s−5t,s−2t)T,x1′​+x2′​−2x3′​=0.
(26)
The columns span this entire plane, so T has rank two. The next two steps are
T2x=3(x1​+x2​−2x3​)(1,−1,0)T,T3x=0.​
(27)
Thus the image of a linear map contracts from a plane to a line and then to the origin under successive applications. The line from T2 is precisely the kernel of T.
