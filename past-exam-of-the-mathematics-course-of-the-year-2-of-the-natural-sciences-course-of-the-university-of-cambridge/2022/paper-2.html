For a Cartesian change of components xi′​=Rij​xj​, a rank-n tensor transforms according to
Ti1​⋯in​′​=Ri1​j1​​⋯Rin​jn​​Tj1​⋯jn​​,​
(46)
with repeated indices summed. An isotropic tensor has unchanged components under every proper rotation matrix.
For rank two write T′=RTRT. Invariance under quarter-turns implies invariance under their squares, the coordinate half-turns
Rx​(π)=diag(1,−1,−1),Ry​(π)=diag(−1,1,−1).
(47)
Each off-diagonal entry changes sign under at least one of these, so all off-diagonal entries vanish. A quarter-turn about the third axis then interchanges the first two diagonal entries, making them equal; a quarter-turn about the first axis equates the other two. Therefore
Tij​=λδij​.​
(48)
Conversely R(λI)RT=λI for every rotation. This proves the isotropic second-rank tensor classification without assuming that the original tensor was symmetric. Also λ=Tkk​/3 is a scalar, since the trace is rotation-invariant.

Write G={e,a,b,c}. By Lagrange's theorem, the order of a group element divides four. Since no element has order four, all three nonidentity elements have order two. Thus every element is its own inverse. For any x,y∈G,

A cyclic group is generated by one element: G=⟨g⟩={gn:n∈Z}. The hypothesis needs the qualification no proper nontrivial subgroup: every nontrivial group has the proper trivial subgroup {e}, so the literal wording admits no examples.
Under that intended hypothesis, choose g=e. The subgroup ⟨g⟩ is nontrivial and must therefore be all of G, proving cyclicity. If g had infinite order, then ⟨g2⟩ would be nontrivial and proper, since g is not an even power of an infinite-order generator. Thus g has finite order N>1 and ∣G∣=N.
If N were composite, choose a divisor d with 1<d<N. Then ⟨gd⟩ would have order N/d, strictly between one and N, again contradicting the hypothesis. Consequently
G≅Cp​ for a prime p.​
(85)
Conversely, Lagrange's theorem shows that a group of prime order has only itself and its trivial subgroup. This proves the characterization of groups with no proper nontrivial subgroups, even without assuming finiteness at the outset.
