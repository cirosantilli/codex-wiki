A square matrix is a diagonalizable matrix over the chosen field when there are an invertible matrix P and a diagonal matrix D such that A=PDP−1. If vj​ is column j of P and Djj​=λj​, the identity AP=PD gives Avj​=λj​vj​. Invertibility makes these n columns linearly independent eigenvectors.
Conversely, put n linearly independent eigenvectors in the columns of P and their eigenvalues in D. Then P is invertible and AP=PD, giving
A is diagonalizable ⟺ A has an eigenbasis of n vectors.​
(32)
Repeated eigenvalues are allowed; the requirement is a complete eigenbasis, not distinctness of the eigenvalues.

The Cauchy-Riemann equations are ux​=vy​,uy​=−vx​​. They give the gradient identity
∇u⋅∇v=ux​vx​+uy​vy​=vy​vx​−vx​vy​=0.
(49)
At a regular intersection, where f′(z)=0, the two nonzero gradients are normal vectors to the two level sets, so their tangent directions are perpendicular. This is the orthogonality of regular real and imaginary level curves.
The regularity qualification is necessary: ∣∇u∣=∣∇v∣=∣f′∣, so at a critical point both normals vanish and the curves need not have unique tangents. For example f=z2 gives diagonal lines for u=0 and coordinate axes for v=0, all meeting at the origin. Thus the conclusion is right-angle intersection wherever f′(z)=0​, rather than an unrestricted claim at critical points.
