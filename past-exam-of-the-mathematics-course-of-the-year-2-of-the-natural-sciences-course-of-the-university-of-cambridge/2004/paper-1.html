A real orthogonal matrix satisfies QTQ=I; a complex unitary matrix satisfies U†U=I. Their columns are orthonormal in the corresponding real or complex inner product.
Adopt the convention that a complex inner product is linear in its second argument, conjugate symmetric, and positive definite: ⟨u,u⟩≥0, with equality exactly when u=0. Conjugate symmetry and second-argument linearity then imply antilinearity in the first:
⟨αu1​+βu2​,v⟩=⟨v,αu1​+βu2​⟩​=α⟨v,u1​⟩+β⟨v,u2​⟩​=αˉ⟨u1​,v⟩+βˉ​⟨u2​,v⟩.
(20)
For the specified real symmetric matrix use b=a2. Its upper block is the outer product (1,a)T(1,a), so it has one zero eigenvalue and one eigenvalue 1+a2. A complete orthonormal eigenbasis is
λ0​=0,v0​=1+a2​(−a,1,0)T​;λ+​=1+a2=1+b,v+​=1+a2​(1,a,0)T​;λc​=c,vc​=(0,0,1)T.​
(21)
If c=1+b, the last two eigenvalues coincide and their eigenspace is the span of v+​,vc​; every nonzero vector in that span is an eigenvector. Since c>0, the zero eigenspace remains one-dimensional.

An ordinary point of the normalized second-order equation is a point where both coefficient functions p(x) and q(x) are analytic in a neighborhood. Ordinary continuity is enough for a local existence theorem, but analyticity is the condition used for a convergent local power series solution.

The Wronskian is W=y1​y2′​−y1′​y2​. If constants A,B obey Ay1​+By2​=0, differentiating gives Ay1′​+By2′​=0. At any point where W=0, these form an invertible two-by-two system, forcing A=B=0. Thus the two solutions are linearly independent.
For the given equation, the normalized coefficients are p=(1−x)/(2x) and q=−1/(4x), so zero is a regular singular point. Substitute a Frobenius series y=xr∑n=0∞​an​xn, a0​=0. The lowest power gives the indicial equation
2r(2r−1)=0,r=0, 21​.​

(23)

an+1​=2(n+r+1)an​​.​

y1​=n=0∑∞​2nn!xn​=ex/2,y2​=x1/2n=0∑∞​(2n+1)!!xn​=x​(1+3x​+15x2​+105x3​+⋯).​

Homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions py′=0 are another choice removing the boundary terms. Put N[y]=∫ab​(py′2+qy2)dx, D[y]=∫ab​wy2dx>0, and I=N/D. At a stationary function let λ=I[y]. The lowest eigenvalue minimizes the Rayleigh quotient under the usual self-adjoint, lower-bounded hypotheses; higher eigenvalues are stationary values with orthogonality constraints when minimizing successively.
The hydrogen equation becomes −(r2ψ′)′−2rψ=2Er2ψ. Its radial energy quotient is therefore
