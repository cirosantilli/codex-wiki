For a separated harmonic function R(r)Θ(θ) in polar coordinates, single-valuedness forces integer angular frequencies. The radial factors for frequency m≥1 are rm,r−m, while frequency zero has a constant and lnr. Finiteness at the origin excludes r−m and lnr.
The source's angular factor is cosθ in every term, as printed, rather than cos(nθ). The power-law angular mode for the planar Poisson equation gives
Δ[rn+2cosθ]=[(n+2)2−1]rncosθ=(n+1)(n+3)rncosθ.
(1)
Hence, assuming the series permits termwise differentiation, the general finite, single-valued solution is
ϕ=C+m=1∑∞​rm[Am​cos(mθ)+Bm​sin(mθ)]+cosθn=0∑∞​(n+1)(n+3)αn​rn+2​.​
(2)
The arbitrary harmonic series must itself converge to a finite function on the prescribed disk. If α0​=0, the source has a direction-dependent limit at the origin; the displayed potential still extends finitely there, but the equation at that point is understood weakly rather than as a smooth classical source.
For the special source, a particular potential is r3cosθ/8. Its boundary cosine term is canceled by −a2rcosθ/8, and the boundary sine term is supplied by (r/a)2sin2θ. The resulting Dirichlet boundary conditions has solution
ϕ(r,θ)=8r(r2−a2)​cosθ+a2r2​sin2θ.​
(3)
It is finite at zero, has the required boundary values, and its Laplacian is rcosθ.

The far radial derivative selects the growing frequency-one term Urcosθ. The matching decaying harmonic is Bcosθ/r. The no-penetration condition gives ∂r​ϕ=(U−B/r2)cosθ=0 at r=a, so B=Ua2. Thus the potential flow around a circular cylinder is

For a complex derivative to exist, the difference quotient must agree along real and imaginary increments. These give ux​+ivx​ and vy​−iuy​, respectively. Equating their real and imaginary parts gives the Cauchy-Riemann equations
ux​=vy​,uy​=−vx​.​
(12)
For g(z)=zRez, the components are u=x2 and v=xy. The first equation requires 2x=x, so x=0; the second requires 0=−y, so y=0. Thus zero is the only possible differentiability point. Directly,
hg(h)−g(0)​=Reh⟶0,
(13)
so g′(0)=0​. The function is differentiable at that isolated point but is not holomorphic on any neighborhood of it.

The residue theorem states that a positively oriented simple closed contour enclosing isolated poles and none on the contour has integral 2πi times the sum of their residues, provided the function is holomorphic elsewhere inside. Near a simple zero of ψ, ψ(z)=ψ′(a)(z−a)+O((z−a)2). Hence
Resz=a​ψ(z)ϕ(z)​=z→alim​ψ(z)(z−a)ϕ(z)​=ψ′(a)ϕ(a)​.​
(17)
For cotz=cosz/sinz, this gives Resz=kπ​cotz=1​ for every integer k.
To evaluate the real integral, integrate F(z)=Logz/(z2+a2) around an upper semicircle, with a small upper semicircular indentation excluding zero. Use the branch real on the positive axis and with argument between zero and π in the upper half-plane. The large arc tends to zero as O(lnR/R) and the small arc as O(ϵ∣lnϵ∣). The only enclosed pole is ia, whose contribution is
2πiResz=ia​F=aπ​(lna+2iπ​).
(18)
On the negative real axis the upper boundary value is Log(−x)=lnx+iπ. The two real segments therefore contribute
2I+iπ∫0∞​x2+a2dx​=2I+2aiπ2​.
(19)
Comparing real parts yields
I=∫0∞​x2+a2lnx​dx=2aπlna​,I∣a=1​=0.​
(20)
The integral is absolutely convergent at both zero and infinity, so no principal-value interpretation is needed.

For causal functions the convolution is (f∗g)(t)=∫0t​f(τ)g(t−τ)dτ. In a half-plane of absolute convergence, changing variables u=t−τ in its Laplace transform and using Fubini's theorem gives
L(f∗g)(s)=∫0∞​∫0∞​e−s(u+τ)f(τ)g(u)dudτ=F(s)G(s).
(21)
This proves the convolution theorem for Laplace transforms. Transforming the differential equation gives (s2+1)Y(s)=sy(0)+y′(0)+F(s). The direct transform pairs L(cost)=s/(s2+1) and L(sint)=1/(s2+1) then give
y(t)=y(0)cost+y′(0)sint+∫0t​f(τ)sin(t−τ)dτ.​
(22)
For a continuous forcing, call the last term w(t). The Leibniz integral rule gives w′=∫0t​f(τ)cos(t−τ)dτ and w′′=f(t)−w(t). Also w(0)=w′(0)=0. Thus both the original equation and the initial values hold.
For an impulse at positive time T, the Dirac delta evaluates the convolution, yielding
y(t)=y(0)cost+y′(0)sint+H(t−T)sin(t−T).​
(23)
Here H is the Heaviside step function. The displacement remains continuous at T, while its derivative jumps upward by one, exactly as required by integrating the differential equation across the unit impulse.

An isotropic tensor has unchanged components under every rotation of the Cartesian axes. Under proper rotations in three dimensions, rank one has no nonzero isotropic tensor, rank two has Tij​=Aδij​​, and rank three has Tijk​=Bϵijk​​. Here δ is the Kronecker delta and ϵ the Levi-Civita symbol. If invariance under reflections is also required for a true tensor, the rank-three case vanishes; ϵ is then a pseudotensor.

A normal mode has all coordinates following one fixed displacement pattern with a common time factor. Its normal frequency is that factor's oscillation frequency; normal mode coordinates are amplitudes in a basis of such patterns, making the linear equations independent. A zero-frequency mode can instead have constant velocity along a symmetry direction.
For the unit circle, each angular displacement equals its corresponding arc displacement. The kinetic energy and change in potential energy are
T=2m​(θ˙12​+θ˙22​+μθ˙32​),V−V0​=2k​[(θ2​−θ1​)2+(θ3​−θ2​)2+(θ1​−θ3​)2].
(28)
Any common spring prestress changes only the constant V0​, because the sum of the three arc lengths is fixed. The Euler-Lagrange equations give
mθ¨1​+k(2θ1​−θ2​−θ3​)=0,mθ¨2​+k(2θ2​−θ1​−θ3​)=0,μmθ¨3​+k(2θ3​−θ1​−θ2​)=0.
(29)
These are the normal modes of three beads connected by springs on a circular hoop, with third mass M=μm. For μ>0, their three mode vectors and squared angular frequencies are
v0​=(1,1,1), ω02​=0;vA​=(1,−1,0), ωA2​=m3k​;vS​=(1,1,−2/μ), ωS2​=mk​(1+μ2​).​
(30)
The zero mode rotates the entire configuration without stretching a spring. The antisymmetric mode moves the equal masses oppositely while leaving the third fixed. In the symmetric mode the equal masses move together against the third, with zero total angular momentum. These vectors are orthogonal in the mass inner product with matrix mdiag(1,1,μ).
Writing θ=Q0​v0​+QA​vA​+QS​vS​ gives independent equations Q¨​0​=0, Q¨​A​+ωA2​QA​=0, Q¨​S​+ωS2​QS​=0. In particular Q0​=A+Bt, retaining possible slow common rotation. For μ=1, the two positive frequencies coincide at 3k/m​; their two-dimensional space is all patterns with θ1​+θ2​+θ3​=0.

A right coset of H in G is Hg={hg:h∈H}. Right multiplication by g is a bijection H→Hg, with inverse x↦xg−1, so every right coset has ∣H∣ elements. The analogous map h↦gh proves the same assertion for left cosets.
Write the displayed matrices as M(a,b,c). Direct multiplication gives
M(a,b,c)M(A,B,C)=M(a+A,b+B+aC,c+C).
(31)
All new coordinates are integers, so the set is closed. Matrix multiplication is associative, the identity is M(0,0,0), and
M(a,b,c)−1=M(−a,ac−b,−c),​
(32)
which also has integer coordinates. Thus it is the Integer Heisenberg group.
The subset H={M(0,0,t):t∈Z} contains the identity, and multiplication and inversion add or negate t, so it is a subgroup. Its right cosets are
HM(a,b,c)={M(a,b,c+t):t∈Z}={M(a,b,s):s∈Z}.​
(33)
They are exactly the sets with fixed integer coordinates (a,b), as described by cosets of a coordinate subgroup of the integer Heisenberg group. The factor order HM is essential; multiplying by H on the other side would also change the upper-right coordinate by at.

Let r be a quarter-turn and s a reflection of the square. The dihedral group is generated by r,s with r4=s2=1 and srs=r−1, and its elements are 1,r,r2,r3,s,sr,sr2,sr3. Two generators are minimal because this group is not cyclic. Its subgroups of order two are ⟨r2⟩ and ⟨srj⟩ for j=0,1,2,3. Its subgroups of order four are ⟨r⟩, ⟨r2,s⟩ and ⟨r2,sr⟩.
A group homomorphism obeys ϕ(gh)=ϕ(g)ϕ(h) for all elements; a group isomorphism is a bijective group homomorphism. Take the rotation subgroup ⟨r⟩ and define
ϕ(rj)=ij∈Q,kerϕ={1}.​
(34)
The quaternion element i has order four, so the map is well defined modulo four, preserves products and is injective onto {1,i,−1,−i}. Surjectivity onto all eight quaternion elements is not required of a homomorphism.
A cyclic group is generated by the powers of one element. The final claim needs the usual qualification no nontrivial proper subgroup, and ∣G∣>1: the trivial subgroup is proper in every nontrivial group, even one of prime order. With that interpretation, if G=⟨g⟩ has prime order p, Lagrange's theorem permits only subgroup orders one and p. Conversely, if its order n>1 is composite, choose a divisor d with 1<d<n. The subgroup ⟨gd⟩ has order n/d, strictly between one and n. This proves the assertion described by groups with no proper nontrivial subgroups.

A group representation is a homomorphism D:G→GL(V) into invertible linear maps. It is a faithful representation when it is injective, equivalently when its kernel contains only the identity. A faithful real two-dimensional representation of the cyclic group of order four is
D(1)=I,D(i)=J=(01​−10​),D(−1)=−I,D(−i)=−J.​
(35)
These are rotations of the plane through 0,π/2,π,3π/2. Since J2=−I and J4=I, their multiplication agrees with the group, and all four matrices are distinct.
For any invertible S, set D(g)=SD(g)S−1. Then
D(g)D(h)=SD(g)S−1SD(h)S−1=SD(gh)S−1=D(gh),
(36)
so this also is a group representation. The character of a representation is χD​(g)=trD(g). For the displayed quarter-turn representation, its values on 1,i,−1,−i are 2,0,−2,0.
