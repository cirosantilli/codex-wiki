If Av=λv for a nonzero eigenvector, then A2v=λ2v. Since A2=A, λ(λ−1)=0: every eigenvalue is zero or one. Let m be the number of ones, counted with algebraic multiplicity. The trace is m and the determinant is the product of these eigenvalues. Therefore

Every output y=Ax is fixed by a second application:
Ay=A2x=Ax=y.​

The first term lies in the image of a linear map A and the second in its kernel. Their intersection is zero, because a vector in the image is fixed by A whereas a vector in the kernel is killed by A. Spectrum in three dimensions. Let Q be any real three-by-three orthogonal matrix. For a complex eigenvector v, ∥Qv∥=∥v∥ implies ∣λ∣=1. Classification and existence. For y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=0, an ordinary point x0​ is one where p,q are analytic. There are two independent analytic power series solutions, with arbitrary initial values y(x0​),y′(x0​). A regular singular point is a singular point where (x−x0​)p(x) and (x−x0​)2q(x) are analytic. The Frobenius method then constructs solutions beginning with (x−x0​)σ, where σ solves the indicial equation. At least one Frobenius solution exists; two such solutions exist without logarithms when the two exponents differ by a noninteger. Repeated exponents or an integer difference can require a logarithmic second solution.
For this equation,

(43)
Both are analytic at zero, while p,q themselves have poles there. Thus zero is a regular singular point.

The boundary conditions must make this boundary form zero for every admissible pair. Vertical lines are the additional solutions, obtained as a limiting case or directly from the constant horizontal optical momentum. Horizontal displacement. Let the ray enter at (x0​,1), travelling to the right and downward at angle 0<θ<π/2 to the vertical. The refractive index is continuous at y=1, so there is no instantaneous angular jump at entry. The tangent has (dx/ds,dy/ds)=(sinθ,−cosθ); hence y′(x0​)=−cotθ. The first integral gives k=sinθ, or R=cscθ. The circle derivative y′=−(x−c)/y then fixes c=x0​−cotθ.
As y↓0, the rightward ray approaches x=c+R. The total limiting horizontal distance is therefore
D=R−cotθ=cscθ−cotθ=tan2θ​.​
(74)
For vertical incidence, D=0 by continuity. For a leftward ray the displacement has the opposite sign, with the same magnitude. Since tan(θ/2) increases on [0,π/2],
D≤1,Dmax​=1 in the grazing-incidence limit θ→π/2.​
(75)
If entry is required to have a strictly downward component, this is a supremum rather than an attained maximum. A horizontal tangent at entry gives the limiting unit-radius arc, which immediately bends downward in the medium.
The lower boundary is a limit, rather than a point reached in finite optical travel time. On a nonvertical circular ray, ds/dy=R/R2−y2​→1 as y↓0. Thus the optical path length contains a divergent integral proportional to ∫dy/y. The horizontal distance stays finite even though propagation toward y=0 takes infinite time.

Local errors of the Euler schemes. Let h=Δt>0, and evaluate f and its partial derivatives at an exact starting point (y(t),t). Define
A=ft​+fy​f=y′′(t).
(76)
The exact Taylor expansion is y(t+h)=y+hf+21​h2A+O(h3). The forward Euler method retains only y+hf, so its one-step defect, exact minus computed value, is
δE​=2h2​A+O(h3)=O(h2).​
(77)
For the Backward Euler method, substitution of the exact solution into the scheme gives the residual
y(t+h)−y(t)−hf(y(t+h),t+h)=−2h2​A+O(h3),
(78)
because f(y(t+h),t+h)=f+hA+O(h2). Under local smoothness, solving the implicit step changes this residual by a factor 1+O(h), so the one-step defect has the same leading term:
δBE​=−2h2​A+O(h3)=O(h2).​
(79)
These are unnormalized local truncation errors, as in the question. A convention dividing by h instead gives O(h). Both schemes have first-order global error under the usual smoothness and stability hypotheses.
Euler stability. For the Dahlquist test equation y′=−Λy, put z=hΛ, with Rez>0. The numerical amplification factors are
RE​(z)=1−z,RBE​(z)=1+z1​.​
(80)
Strict decay requires ∣R∣<1; the boundary ∣R∣=1 gives bounded but generally nondecaying values. For Forward Euler method,
∣1−hΛ∣<1⟺0<h<∣Λ∣22ReΛ​.
(81)
For positive real Λ, this reduces to 0<hΛ<2. The absolute stability region in the z plane is the disk ∣z−1∣≤1. For Backward Euler method, ∣1+z∣>1 whenever Rez>0, so it decays for every step size. It is A-stable and also L-stable, since RBE​(z)→0 for large z in this half-plane. Here z has the opposite sign from the alternative convention z=hλ for y′=λy.
Predictor-corrector accuracy. The printed two-stage construction is Heun's method, equivalently
yn+1​=yn​+2h​[f(yn​,tn​)+f(yn​+hf(yn​,tn​),tn​+h)].
(82)
Although the second stage resembles a Backward Euler method evaluation, it uses the predicted value, so the combined method is explicit. Expand that stage around the exact starting point, putting
B=ftt​+2ffty​+f2fyy​.
(83)
Then
f(y+hf,t+h)=f+hA+2h2​B+O(h3),
(84)
and the numerical step is y+hf+21​h2A+41​h3B+O(h4). Along the exact solution, y′′′=B+fy​A, so comparison with the exact Taylor expansion gives
δH​=h3(6fy​A​−12B​)+O(h4)=O(h3).​
(85)
Thus the local defect improves by one power of h, giving second-order global error under the usual hypotheses. For the linear test equation the defect reduces to −h3Λ3y/6+O(h4).
Predictor-corrector stability. Applied to the test equation, the predictor is y(1)=(1−z)yn​ and the second value is y(2)=(1−z+z2)yn​. Their average gives
