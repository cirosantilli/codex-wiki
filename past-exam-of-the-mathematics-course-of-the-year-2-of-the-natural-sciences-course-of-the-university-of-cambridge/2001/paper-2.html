For the nonconstant boundary data, use separation of variables in polar coordinates and put ϕ=R(r)cos2θ. Laplace's equation reduces to

Green identity. With n the outward unit normal, the product rule gives ∇⋅(u∇v)=u∇2v+∇u⋅∇v. Integrating over V and applying the divergence theorem yields Green's first identity:

Uniqueness for nonzero mass. Suppose two real solutions have the same prescribed boundary data, and let w be their difference. The spatial equation called the Klein-Gordon equation in the question is the modified Helmholtz equation, so ∇2w=m2w. With u=v=w, Green's first identity becomes
∫V​(∣∇w∣2+m2w2)dV=∫S​w∂n​wdS.
(6)
For identical Dirichlet boundary conditions, w=0 on S; for identical Neumann boundary conditions, ∂n​w=0 there. Either makes the right-hand side zero. When m is real and nonzero, both integrands on the left are nonnegative, forcing w=0. Thus
For m=0, either type of boundary data determines at most one solution.​
(7)
This is the required uniqueness assertion; existence is understood for admissible smooth data on a suitable bounded region. Complex-valued solutions obey the same result by treating their real and imaginary parts separately.
Zero mass. For m=0, the argument gives ∇w=0, hence w=C on the connected region. Dirichlet boundary conditions still force C=0, but Neumann boundary conditions leave an arbitrary additive constant:

Energy minimization. Let η=w−v for the two functions in the last part. They share their boundary values, so η=0 on S. Since v is a harmonic function, Green's first identity gives
∫V​∇v⋅∇ηdV=∫S​η∂n​vdS−∫V​η∇2vdV=0.

Cosets and Lagrange. A left coset is gH={gh:h∈H}. The left cosets of a subgroup partition a group, each with ∣H∣ elements. Lagrange's theorem states ∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣​ for a finite group, so every subgroup order divides the group order.
The 64-element group. Work in the commutative ring R=Z/4Z, which has zero divisors. Denote the displayed lower triangular matrix by A(a,b,c), where a,b,c are its three entries below the diagonal. Direct matrix multiplication gives
A(a,b,c)A(d,e,f)=A(a+d,b+e+cd,c+f).​
(49)
All arithmetic is modulo four, so the set is closed. Associativity comes from matrix multiplication, the identity element is A(0,0,0), and
A(a,b,c)−1=A(−a,ca−b,−c).​
(50)
The formula works in either multiplication order and requires no division. Hence these matrices form a group of order ∣G∣=43=64​. This is the lower triangular version of the Heisenberg group over a commutative ring.
The abelian subgroup. If a=c, write h(a,b)=A(a,b,a). Then
h(a,b)h(d,e)=h(a+d,b+e+ad)=h(d,e)h(a,b).
(51)
The identity is h(0,0) and the inverse is h(−a,a2−b), proving an abelian subgroup. Its two independent entries give ∣H∣=16​ and [G:H]=4​, in accordance with Lagrange's theorem. This is the abelian plane subgroup of the Heisenberg group modulo four.
More explicitly, A(a,b,c)↦a−c is a surjective group homomorphism to the additive group of R, with kernel H. The four left cosets are exactly the four sets with fixed a−c=t, t=0,1,2,3; representatives are A(t,0,0). Thus H is also a normal subgroup.
Matrices squaring to the identity. The product formula gives
A(a,b,c)2=A(2a,2b+ca,2c).
(52)
The first and third coordinates vanish only when a,c∈{0,2}. Then ca=0 modulo four, so the middle coordinate vanishes exactly when b∈{0,2}. Therefore the complete set is
K={A(a,b,c):a,b,c∈{0,2}},∣K∣=8.​
(53)
It is a subgroup: multiplying two such elements keeps all three entries even, and their cross term cd vanishes modulo four, so the product simply adds the three even coordinates. The identity is included and each element is its own inverse. In fact K is isomorphic to the direct product of groups C2​×C2​×C2​. The seven nonidentity elements are involutions.
Finally, the last subset in the PDF is defined by a=0=b, with no restriction on c. It has 3⋅3⋅4=36 elements, which cannot be a subgroup because 36 does not divide 64. It also excludes the identity element. Thus

Representations. A complex group representation is a group homomorphism ρ:G→GL(W) to the invertible linear maps on a vector space. It is an irreducible representation when W=0 and the only invariant subspaces are 0 and W.

(67)
Hence the fourth-order centered second derivative satisfies
D4,h​u(x)=12h2−u(x+2h)+16u(x+h)−30u(x)+16u(x−h)−u(x−2h)​=u′′−90h4​u(6)+O(h6).​
(68)
Rearranging proves the requested u′′=D4,h​u+O(h4), including its leading error coefficient.
Explicit diffusion scheme. Take xj​=jh, h=1/N, tn​=nτ, and let Ujn​ approximate ψ(xj​,tn​). Combining the forward Euler method in time with D4,h​ in space gives
Ujn+1​=Ujn​+12h2τ​(−Uj+2n​+16Uj+1n​−30Ujn​+16Uj−1n​−Uj−2n​),Uj0​=f(jh).​
(69)
To check the error, insert the exact solution. The heat equation gives ψt​=ψxx​, while the time Taylor expansion and the spatial formula give the one-step defect
ψ(xj​,tn​+τ)−ψ(xj​,tn​)−τD4,h​ψ(xj​,tn​)=2τ2​ψtt​+90τh4​ψxxxxxx​+O(τ3+τh6).
(70)
Thus its unnormalized local truncation error is
O(τ2)+O(τh4),​
(71)
as requested. Dividing this defect by τ gives the normalized residual O(τ)+O(h4); these are distinct error conventions.
Boundary implementation. The centered stencil requires two ghost values at each end. An even-reflection closure for Neumann diffusion imposes
U−1n​=U1n​,U−2n​=U2n​,UN+1n​=UN−1n​,UN+2n​=UN−2n​.​
(72)
The reflected first centered difference at either endpoint is zero. Apply the same evolution formula at the endpoints after substituting these ghost values; for example,
U0n+1​=U0n​+12h2τ​(−2U2n​+32U1n​−30U0n​).
(73)
The ghost values also close the stencil at j=1 and j=N−1. They are determined auxiliary values, not new physical data. For a smooth solution compatible with the Neumann boundary conditions, differentiating those conditions in time and using the heat equation gives ψxxx​=ψxxxxx​=⋯=0 at the endpoints. Thus even reflection preserves the smoothness needed for fourth-order spatial accuracy. If the initial data fail the required higher compatibility conditions, a uniform smooth error expansion through t=0 cannot be assumed.
For completeness, the reflected spatial operator has cosine eigenvectors cos(ℓπj/N) and eigenvalues
λℓ​=−h24​sin22Nℓπ​(1+31​sin22Nℓπ​).
(74)
The most negative one is −16/(3h2). Requiring ∣1+τλℓ​∣≤1 gives the forward Euler method stability restriction τ/h2≤3/8​ in the natural trapezoid-weighted discrete norm. A fourth-order spatial stencil does not remove the explicit diffusion time-step restriction.
Implicit alternative. The Crank-Nicolson diffusion scheme using the same spatial operator and boundary closure is
(I−2τ​D4,h​)Un+1=(I+2τ​D4,h​)Un.​
(75)
It has second-order time accuracy and fourth-order spatial accuracy for smooth compatible solutions; its one-step defect is O(τ3)+O(τh4). Each spatial eigenvalue λ≤0 has amplification factor (1+τλ/2)/(1−τλ/2) of magnitude at most one, so it removes the explicit restriction for this boundary closure.
The price is solving a banded linear system at every step; the same matrix factorization can be reused for fixed h and τ. Large time steps still reduce accuracy. Moreover, the amplification factor tends to −1 as τ∣λ∣→∞, so short spatial wavelengths can alternate sign with little numerical damping. Thus unconditional linear stability does not guarantee a monotone or well-resolved transient. Boundary accuracy and initial compatibility remain necessary with either time discretization.
