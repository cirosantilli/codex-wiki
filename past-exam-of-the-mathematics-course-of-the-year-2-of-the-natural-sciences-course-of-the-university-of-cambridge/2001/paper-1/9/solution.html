Variational formulation. Take real p,q,w with p,w>0 in the interval, and impose homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, or other suitable conditions that cancel the boundary terms. The functional
J[y]=∫ab​[p(x)(y′)2+q(x)y2−λw(x)y2]dx
(61)
has Euler-Lagrange equation
dxd​(2py′)−2(q−λw)y=0,
(62)
which is equivalent to −(py′)′+qy−λwy=0. The same construction is constrained stationarity of the energy ∫[p(y′)2+qy2] subject to ∫wy2=1, with λ a Lagrange multiplier. For fixed zero endpoint values, the variation vanishes at both ends; homogeneous zero-flux conditions also cancel the boundary term [2py′δy]ab​.
Multiplying the differential equation by y and using integration by parts gives the Rayleigh quotient
R[y]=∫ab​wy2dx∫ab​[p(y′)2+qy2]dx​.​
(63)
For a nonzero admissible trial function, R[y]≥λ0​ by the Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle. The Rayleigh-Ritz method chooses trial functions satisfying the endpoint conditions, often with adjustable coefficients, and minimizes this quotient over that trial family. Expanding y=∑cn​yn​ in a normalized eigenbasis shows why the lower bound holds: R[y]=∑λn​cn2​/∑cn2​, a weighted average of eigenvalues no smaller than the least one.
Radial quadratic trial. The radial problem becomes
−(r2ψ′)′=λr2ψ,ψ(1)=0,ψ′(0)=0,
(64)
where the printed endpoint notation y refers to ψ. Thus p=w=r2 and q=0. At the origin the vanishing r2 and regularity of ψ make the boundary term vanish; the weight must be retained in both numerator and denominator.
For a quadratic ψ=A+Br+Cr2, the derivative condition sets B=0 and the outer condition gives C=−A. Overall amplitude cancels in the Rayleigh quotient, so take ψ​=1−r2. Then
∫01​r2(ψ​′)2dr=4∫01​r4dr=54​,
(65)
while
∫01​r2ψ​2dr=∫01​(r2−2r4+r6)dr=31​−52​+71​=1058​.
(66)
Consequently
ψ​(r)=1−r2,λ0​=8/1054/5​=221​=10.5.​
(67)
Why the estimate is higher. For a regular radial function put u=rψ. Then u(0)=u(1)=0, and direct differentiation transforms the equation to u′′+λu=0. Its first mode is u=sin(πr), corresponding to ψ=sin(πr)/r with its continuous value at the origin. Thus λ0​=π2. The quadratic trial is not proportional to that Spherical Bessel function, so it has nonzero components in higher modes. Its Rayleigh quotient is therefore strictly larger than π2, in agreement with 21/2>π2.
