For a vacuum electric potential, put Φ=R(r)Y(θ) in Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates. Multiplication by r2/(RY) separates the radial and angular terms. With separation constant ℓ(ℓ+1) and x=cosθ, the angular Legendre differential equation is
dxd​[(1−x2)dxdPℓ​​]+ℓ(ℓ+1)Pℓ​=0.​
(1)
Regularity at both polar axes selects nonnegative integers ℓ and the Legendre polynomials. The radial equation is (r2R′)′−ℓ(ℓ+1)R=0, whose independent solutions are rℓ and r−ℓ−1. Hence the angularly regular axisymmetric vacuum solution is
Φ(r,θ)=ℓ=0∑∞​(Aℓ​rℓ+Bℓ​r−ℓ−1)Pℓ​(cosθ).​
(2)
The radial domain and its boundary conditions determine which terms are admissible.
For the given charge density, only P1​(cosθ)=cosθ is forced. Write Φ=f(r)cosθ. The interior radial Poisson equation is f′′+2f′/r−2f/r2=−4. The identity for the Poisson equation with a spherical harmonic source gives the particular solution fp​=−r2, since the radial operator takes r2 to four. Finiteness at zero and decay at infinity therefore give
Φin​=(Ar−r2)cosθ,Φout​=Br−2cosθ.
(3)
Continuity of the electric potential and its radial derivative at a requires Aa−a2=B/a2 and A−2a=−2B/a3. Solving gives A=4a/3, B=a4/3, so
Φ(r,θ)=⎩⎨⎧​(34​ar−r2)cosθ,3r2a4​cosθ,​0≤r≤a,r≥a.​​
(4)
All other angular modes have zero coefficients after imposing regularity, matching and decay. At the origin the interior expression is (4a/3)z−rz, which extends with a continuous gradient and value zero. The angular charge density has no unique value exactly at that point; Poisson's equation holds classically for r>0 and as a weak solution through the origin. There is no additional point charge.

Expand the divergence of Φ∇Ψ−Ψ∇Φ. The cross terms cancel, leaving ΦΔΨ−ΨΔΦ. The divergence theorem gives Green's second identity,
∫V​(ΦΔΨ−ΨΔΦ)dV=∫S​(Φ∂n​Ψ−Ψ∂n​Φ)dS.
(5)
Use the Green function convention Δx​G(x,x0​)=δ(x−x0​). Integrating over a bounded domain containing x0​ yields ∫S​∂n​GdS=1​. Thus zero Neumann boundary data would be incompatible with this unit Dirac delta source. A suitable Neumann Green function with constant boundary flux instead has ∂n​G=1/∣S∣, where ∣S∣ is the boundary area. Substituting Ψ=G, ΔΦ=σ, ∂n​Φ=f gives
Φ(x0​)=∫V​GσdV−∫S​GfdS+∣S∣1​∫S​ΦdS.​
(6)
The last term is an undetermined constant. The data must obey the Neumann Poisson problem compatibility condition ∫V​σdV=∫S​fdS, obtained by integrating the original equation. Under this condition an additive constant in G cancels from the first two terms.
For the upper half-space, reflect the source to x0∗​=(x0,1​,x0,2​,−x0,3​). The method of images with equal-sign sources gives
G(x,x0​)=−4π1​(∣x−x0​∣1​+∣x−x0∗​∣1​).​
(7)
The reflected singularity lies outside the domain; on z=0 the two normal derivatives cancel, so ∂n​G=−∂z​G=0. Its total unit flux passes through a large hemisphere at infinity, where G∼−1/(2πR). This is why the bounded-domain condition on the boundary flux cannot be applied to the infinite plane alone.
With zero interior source and the given point boundary source, the boundary representation is Φ(x0​)=−G((0,0,0),x0​). The terms at infinity vanish for this resulting 1/R field. Therefore
Φ(x,y,z)=2πx2+y2+z2​1​,z>0.​
(8)
To check the singular boundary condition, −∂z​Φ=z/[2π(x2+y2+z2)3/2] is the Poisson kernel for the upper half-space. Its integral over a horizontal plane is one and its mass concentrates at the origin as z↓0, giving ∂n​Φ=δ(x)δ(y) in the distributional sense. This is the Neumann point source on a half-space boundary.

Choose the square root of one minus z squared with f(0)=1: explicitly f(z)=1−z​1+z​ with principal square roots. It is single valued and holomorphic away from the two specified exterior real branch cuts. If q(x)=x2−1​>0 for ∣x∣>1, its boundary values are
x>1x<−1​f(x+i0)−iq(x)+iq(x)​f(x−i0)+iq(x)−iq(x)​​.​
(9)
For example, above the right cut 1−z approaches the negative real axis from below, selecting −ix−1​; above the left cut 1+z approaches it from above, selecting +i−x−1​. Continuing once around either branch point changes the sign, which explains the need for cuts.
Apply the residue theorem to F(z)=1/[zf(z)] on a large disk slit along both cuts, with small arcs excluding ±1. The only enclosed pole is at zero, and its residue is 1/f(0)=1. On the positively oriented boundary of the slit domain, the upper right lip runs from one to R, and the lower right lip returns from R to one. The jump in F gives their combined integral as 2iIR​, where IR​=∫1R​dx/[xx2−1​]. The left lips similarly give 2iIR​ after the substitution x=−t.
The small arcs contribute O(ε​) because the branch singularities have order −1/2. The large arc contributes O(R−1) because F(z)=O(∣z∣−2). Taking limits therefore yields 4iI=2πi, and hence
I=2π​.​

Compute the complex derivative along horizontal and vertical increments. Along the real direction, f′=ux​+ivx​; along the imaginary direction, f′=(uy​+ivy​)/i=vy​−iuy​. Equality gives the Cauchy-Riemann equations
ux​=vy​,uy​=−vx​.​
(11)
The real and imaginary parts of a holomorphic function are smooth. Equality of their mixed partial derivatives then gives uxx​+uyy​=vyx​−vxy​=0 and vxx​+vyy​=−uyx​+uxy​=0, proving that both are harmonic functions.
For the first real part, ux​=2x and uy​=−2y. The Cauchy-Riemann equations require vy​=2x and vx​=2y, so its harmonic conjugate is v=2xy+C. Therefore
f(z)=z2+iC,C∈R.​
(12)
For the second real part, u=x/(x2+y2) is the real part of 1/z. Its harmonic conjugate is −y/(x2+y2)+C, giving
f(z)=z1​+iC,C∈R,z=0.​
(13)
These are the most general answers on a connected open domain: the difference of two candidates has zero real part, so the Cauchy-Riemann equations make its imaginary part constant. The second function cannot extend holomorphically through zero.

The first two vectors displace every particle identically in the horizontal or vertical direction. Every relative displacement is zero, so every spring has zero extension to first order. The third vector is (yi​,−xi​) evaluated at the equilibrium points, which is an infinitesimal clockwise rotation. For any edge the relative displacement is perpendicular to that edge, again giving zero extension.
These are the rigid-motion zero modes of a spring framework: two translations and one infinitesimal rotation. Each has Kq=0, so ω=0​. In the linearized dynamics its amplitude has the form A+Bt, expressing free rigid motion rather than a restoring oscillation. Exact finite rigid rotations also preserve every spring length; the mode vector is their first-order tangent.

A group is a nonempty set with a closed binary operation that is associative, has an identity element, and gives every element an inverse element. A subgroup is a subset that is itself a group under the inherited operation. The left coset of H represented by g is gH={gh:h∈H}; right cosets are defined analogously. Left cosets partition a finite group, and h↦gh is a bijection from H to gH. Therefore ∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣, proving Lagrange's theorem and the required divisibility.
For the given matrices, put a=A and c=AB=iI. Since B=iA, the generators commute; a2=I, c4=I, and the generated elements are
G={I,c,c2,c3,a,ca,c2a,c3a},B=ca.
(33)
They are distinct, and (cras)(cr′as′)=cr+r′as+s′, with exponents reduced modulo four and two. The Cayley table is
Icc2c3acac2ac3a​IIcc2c3acac2ac3a​ccc2c3Icac2ac3aa​c2c2c3Icc2ac3aaca​c3c3Icc2c3aacac2a​aacac2ac3aIcc2c3​cacac2ac3aacc2c3I​c2ac2ac3aacac2c3Ic​c3ac3aacac2ac3Icc2​​
(34)
The table proves closure and identifies I as the identity. The inverse element of cras is c−ras, and associativity follows from matrix multiplication. Thus this is the direct product of cyclic groups of orders four and two, an abelian group of order eight.
Its subgroups are exactly
{I},{I,c2},{I,a},{I,c2a},
(35)
{I,c,c2,c3},{I,ca,c2,c3a},{I,c2,a,c2a},G.
(36)
To see the list is complete, Lagrange's theorem leaves orders one, two, four and eight. There are exactly three elements of order two, giving the three order-two subgroups. An order-four subgroup containing an element of order four must be one of the two cyclic groups displayed; without such an element it must be the four-element set of elements whose squares are I. This is the enumeration in subgroups of C4 times C2.
For example choose H={I,c,c2,c3}. A complete set of cosets is H,aH={a,ca,c2a,c3a}​. Since G is Abelian, the left and right cosets coincide.

A group homomorphism h:G→F preserves multiplication: h(g1​g2​)=h(g1​)h(g2​). It sends the identity element to the identity and inverse elements to inverse elements. Its group kernel is K={g∈G:h(g)=eF​}. This contains eG​ and is closed under multiplication and inverse elements, so it is a subgroup. For k∈K and g∈G,
h(gkg−1)=h(g)eF​h(g)−1=eF​.
(37)
Thus gKg−1⊆K; applying the same argument with g−1 gives equality. Hence the group kernel is a normal subgroup.
Let S2​={e,τ} with τ=(12). The parity of a permutation defines the desired map from S3​:
h(e)=h((123))=h((132))=e,h((12))=h((13))=h((23))=τ.​
(38)
The sign of a permutation is multiplicative, so parity adds modulo two under composition, exactly matching τ2=e. Thus this is a group homomorphism; it is surjective because a transposition maps to τ. Its group kernel is

(39)
This is the alternating group on three objects, a normal subgroup of index two in S3​.

Write the symmetry group of a square as D4​=⟨r,s∣r4=s2=e, srs=r−1⟩, with r a counterclockwise quarter-turn and s reflection across the horizontal axis. The conjugacy classes of the symmetry group of a square are
{e},{r2},{r,r3},{s,r2s},{rs,r3s}.​
(40)
Here r2 is the half-turn and r3 the clockwise quarter-turn. The two members of the fourth class reflect across the horizontal and vertical axes. Those of the fifth reflect across y=x and y=−x. The identity and half-turn are central elements, giving singleton classes. The relations srs=r3, rsr−1=r2s, and r(rs)r−1=r3s show that each displayed pair consists of conjugate elements. More generally, conjugating rks by r changes k to k+2, and conjugating by s changes k to −k; parity is preserved, so the two reflection pairs cannot merge.
In a one-dimensional representation, the relation srs=r−1 forces the image of r to equal its inverse. Thus r and s can be assigned independent signs α,β∈{1,−1}. These four choices give four irreducible characters. The ordinary two-dimensional action on the plane gives one more character: a rotation through θ has trace 2cosθ, and a reflection has trace zero. Therefore the character table of the symmetry group of a square is
class sizeA1​A2​B1​B2​E​e111112​r211111−2​{r,r3}211−1−10​{s,r2s}21−11−10​{rs,r3s}21−1−110​​.
(41)
For instance the last row has squared character norm (4+4)/8=1, so the planar representation is irreducible; its inner product with each one-dimensional row is zero. All five rows obey character orthogonality. Their squared dimensions sum to 1+1+1+1+4=8, so they exhaust the irreducible representations of this order-eight group.
