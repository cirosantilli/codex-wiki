For a>0, the positive constant-u surfaces in elliptic cylindrical coordinates satisfy
a2cosh2ux2​+a2sinh2uy2​=1.
(1)
They are confocal elliptic cylinders with focal lines x=±a,y=0. For a generic fixed v, eliminating u gives
a2cos2vx2​−a2sin2vy2​=1.
(2)
Because u≥0, a fixed v selects the half-branch with the signs of x and y fixed by cosv and sinv. These are hyperbolic cylinders. The degenerate u=0 surface is y=0, ∣x∣≤a, extended in z. At v=0,π the cross-sections are the two exterior horizontal rays; at v=π/2,3π/2 they are vertical half-axes.
Differentiate the position vector to get the coordinate tangent vectors
ru​=(asinhucosv,acoshusinv,0),rv​=(−acoshusinv,asinhucosv,0),rz​=(0,0,1).
(3)
Their pairwise dot products vanish. Their squared lengths are a2(sinh2u+sin2v), a2(sinh2u+sin2v) and one. This proves orthogonality and gives the scale factors of orthogonal coordinates:
hu​=hv​=h=asinh2u+sin2v​,hz​=1.​
(4)
The Riemannian metric is ds2=h2(du2+dv2)+dz2. Away from the focal lines, divide the coordinate tangent vectors by their lengths to obtain the local orthonormal unit vectors eu​,ev​,ez​. For physical vector field components Au​,Av​,Az​, the formulas for vector calculus in elliptic cylindrical coordinates are
∇⋅A=h2∂u​(hAu​)+∂v​(hAv​)+∂z​(h2Az​)​,​
(5)
∇×A=h∂v​Az​−∂z​(hAv​)​eu​+h∂z​(hAu​)−∂u​Az​​ev​+h2∂u​(hAv​)−∂v​(hAu​)​ez​.​
(6)
The gradient of a scalar function has components (ϕu​/h,ϕv​/h,ϕz​). Apply the divergence formula to this gradient; Laplace's equation becomes
a2(sinh2u+sin2v)ϕuu​+ϕvv​​+ϕzz​=0.​
(7)
The formulas apply where h>0; the focal lines require a limit or another coordinate chart.

The spectral theorem gives a real orthonormal eigenbasis v1​,…,vn​ for a real symmetric matrix A. Distinct eigenspaces are orthogonal; within a repeated eigenspace, choose an orthonormal basis, for example by Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization. Put these eigenvectors in the columns of T. Then T−1=TT and
T−1AT=diag(λ1​,…,λn​).
(18)
For a change of variables y=Sx, choose S=TT​. Since x=Ty, the quadratic form becomes xTAx=∑i​λi​yi2​; the λi​ are precisely the eigenvalues of A.
In the numerical case, the off-diagonal entries are half the cross-term coefficients, so
A=​113​11−3​3−3−3​​.
(19)
A direct eigenvector calculation gives the unit eigenvectors (1,1,0)/2​, (1,−1,1)/3​, (1,−1,−2)/6​, with eigenvalues two, three and minus six. They are pairwise orthogonal. Therefore the required orthogonal diagonalization is
y1​=2​x1​+x2​​,y2​=3​x1​−x2​+x3​​,y3​=6​x1​−x2​−2x3​​,​
(20)
and the transformed quadratic form is 2y12​+3y22​−6y32​​.

If Hv=λv for a Hermitian matrix and a nonzero eigenvector, then v†Hv=λv†v. The scalar on the left equals its complex conjugate because H†=H, while v†v>0. Thus λ is real. If also Hw=μw, then
μv†w=v†Hw=(Hv)†w=λv†w.
(21)
Consequently distinct eigenvalues force v†w=0, proving orthogonality of their eigenvectors.
For a unitary matrix, U†U=I preserves the norm induced by the standard Hermitian inner product. If Uv=ζv with v=0, then ∥v∥2=∥Uv∥2=∣ζ∣2∥v∥2, so ∣ζ∣=1​.

I=U†U=(A−iB)(A+iB)=A2+B2+i(AB−BA).
(22)

A2+B2=I,AB=BA.​
(23)

Let Av=αv. The commutation relation gives A(Bv)=B(Av)=αBv, so B preserves the eigenspace of A with eigenvalue α. Because it is a nondegenerate eigenvalue, the eigenspace is one-dimensional, and Bv=βv for some real β. This includes Bv=0. Thus the eigenvectors of A form a common eigenbasis, and
Uv=(α+iβ)v.
(24)
Writing the corresponding unit-modulus eigenvalue of U as ζ=eiθ gives
α=Reζ=cosθ,β=Imζ=sinθ.​
(25)
The pairing refers to the same common eigenvector; independent reorderings of the two real spectra would lose this information. The simultaneous diagonalization can also be constructed without requiring simple eigenvalues by diagonalizing B within each eigenspace of A.

For the normalized second-order linear differential equation y′′+P(z)y′+Q(z)y=0, an ordinary point z0​ has analytic P,Q near z0​. At a regular singular point, which is not ordinary, (z−z0​)P(z) and (z−z0​)2Q(z) are analytic. Ordinary points admit two independent convergent power series solutions. Regular singular points instead call for the Frobenius method, which permits leading powers and, in some cases, logarithms.
Here zero is an ordinary point. Substitute y=∑n≥0​an​zn into the Hermite differential equation and equate coefficients:
(n+2)(n+1)an+2​+(λ−2n)an​=0,an+2​=(n+2)(n+1)2n−λ​an​.​
(26)
The even and odd initial coefficients are independent, giving
y=a0​[1−2λ​z2+24λ(λ−4)​z4+⋯]+a1​[z+62−λ​z3+120(2−λ)(6−λ)​z5+⋯].
(27)
These are the power series solutions of the Hermite differential equation, with the present parameter equal to twice the parameter in that article. The ratio of successive terms within each nonterminating parity is asymptotically 2∣z∣2/n, so both series have infinite radius of convergence.
For a nonzero polynomial of degree m, its highest coefficient in −2zy′+λy forces λ=2m. Conversely at that value the matching parity terminates. Hence
λ=2m,m=0,1,2,….​
(28)
The polynomial solutions are scalar multiples of the Hermite polynomials. Two examples are λ=0: y=1;λ=2: y=z​; direct substitution verifies both. The other independent solution generally remains nonpolynomial.

Multiplication by x2 puts the eigenvalue problem into Sturm-Liouville form, −(x2y′)′=λx2y, with weight x2. If u,v correspond to distinct eigenvalues λ,μ, subtract their multiplied equations and integrate to get
(λ−μ)∫0π​x2uvdx=[x2(uv′−vu′)]0π​=0.
(29)
The upper boundary term vanishes by the Dirichlet boundary condition. For a bounded solution near zero, x2y′ has a limit because (x2y′)′=−λx2y is integrable. A nonzero limit would force a 1/x divergence in y, so that limit is zero; integration then gives y′=O(x). The lower boundary term therefore also vanishes, proving the weighted orthogonality.
Set z=xy. For x>0, y′′+2y′/x=z′′/x, and finiteness of y(0) gives z(0)=0. Thus z′′+λz=0 with zero values at zero and π. The radial Dirichlet spectrum in three dimensions gives
λn​=n2,yn​(x)=xsinnx​,n=1,2,…,yn​(0)=n.​
(30)
The zero and negative eigenvalues yield only the zero solution under these boundary conditions. Also ∫0π​x2yn2​dx=π/2.
For the forced equation, the same substitution gives z′′−βz=x. The Fourier sine series of x on (0,π) has coefficients
bn​=π2​∫0π​xsinnxdx=n2(−1)n+1​.
(31)
Expanding z=∑cn​sinnx gives −(n2+β)cn​=bn​, hence
y(x)=2n=1∑∞​n(n2+β)(−1)n​xsinnx​.​
(32)
The value at zero is defined by a limit. In fact ∣sinnx/x∣≤n, so uniform convergence holds even there, by comparison with 2∑(n2+β)−1. An independent check comes from solving the constant-coefficient equation directly:
y(x)=−β1​+βxsinh(πβ​)πsinh(β​x)​.
(33)
It satisfies both endpoint conditions and the forcing for x>0, and its finite origin limit agrees with the series. For β>0 the homogeneous boundary value problem has no nonzero solution, so the solution is unique.

Substitution of y=xp into the homogeneous Euler-Cauchy equation gives (p(p−1)+p−1)xp−2=(p2−1)xp−2, so p=1,−1​. Use the solution u(x)=x that vanishes at zero and v(x)=1/x that decays at infinity. Their Wronskian is uv′−u′v=−2/x. The half-line Green function for the first cylindrical harmonic is therefore
G(x,s)={−x/2,−s2/(2x),​0<x≤s,0<s≤x.​​
(34)
It is continuous at x=s, its first derivative has jump 1/2−(−1/2)=1, and it solves the homogeneous equation elsewhere. Thus Lx​G=δ(x−s) for integration with ordinary measure ds. The boundary conditions hold for each fixed s>0.
Integrating against the forcing gives
y(x)=−2x1​∫0x​s2e−sds−2x​∫x∞​e−sds.
(35)
Since the two integrals are 2−(x2+2x+2)e−x and e−x, respectively,
y(x)=x(x+1)e−x−1​(x>0),y(0)=0.​
(36)
A Taylor expansion gives y=−x/2+O(x2) near zero, while y∼−1/x at infinity. This verifies the two endpoint limits; direct differentiation verifies Ly=e−x on x>0. The differential expression itself is singular at zero, so the endpoint condition is imposed by continuity. No nonzero combination of x and 1/x satisfies both homogeneous endpoint conditions, proving uniqueness.

Vary r by r+εη with η(a)=η(b)=0. The first variation of the functional is
δI1​=∫ab​(F1,r​η+F1,r′​η′)dz=∫ab​(F1,r​−dzd​F1,r′​)ηdz.
(37)
The boundary term from integration by parts is zero. The fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations therefore gives F1,r​−dF1,r′​/dz=0​. For I2​, independently vary r and θ with zero endpoint variations; the two Euler-Lagrange equations are
F2,r​−dzd​F2,r′​=0,F2,θ​−dzd​F2,θ′​=0.​
(38)
For the optical path, Fermat principle makes ∫μds stationary. In cylindrical coordinates its integrand is L=μ(r,θ)s, with s=1+r′2+r2θ′2​. There is no explicit z dependence. The Beltrami identity for several dependent variables gives
L−r′Lr′​−θ′Lθ′​=μs−sμ(r′2+r2θ′2)​=sμ​=constant.
(39)
The conserved quantity is the axial component of the optical momentum of a ray. As μ>0, squaring and inverting yields
μ21+r′2+r2θ′2​=constant.​
(40)
Now suppose μ=μ(r) and seek a path of radius R>0. The angle is a cyclic coordinate, so Lθ′​=μR2θ′/1+R2θ′2​ is conserved. At fixed radius this is strictly increasing in θ′, forcing θ′=ω constant. The radial Euler-Lagrange equation reduces to
μ′(R)(1+R2ω2)+μ(R)Rω2=0.
(41)
Consequently the helical rays in a radially graded refractive index have
r(z)=R,θ(z)=θ0​+ωz,ω2=−R[μ(R)+Rμ′(R)]μ′(R)​.​
(42)
A real nonzero helix exists at such a radius when 0<−Rμ′(R)/μ(R)<1, and its helical pitch is 2π/∣ω∣. If μ′(R)=0, the solution is instead a straight axial light ray. Thus radial dependence permits constant-radius rays for suitable profiles; an arbitrary radial profile need not support a nonzero real helix. This qualification is necessary, for example when the refractive index increases with radius.

Choose homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, y(a)=y(b)=0, and nonzero admissible functions with finite F and positive G. Variations obey the same zero endpoint values. At a stationary Rayleigh quotient, set λ=F[y]/G[y]. Then
0=GδΛ=δF−λδG=2[py′η]ab​+2∫ab​[−(py′)′+qy−λwy]ηdx.
(43)
The boundary term vanishes, and the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations gives the Sturm-Liouville equation −(py′)′+qy=λwy. Thus stationary functions are eigenfunctions. Conversely, multiply that equation by y and integrate by parts to obtain F=λG; its first variation then vanishes by the same calculation. This proves that stationary values are the corresponding eigenvalues. Homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions also make the displayed boundary terms vanish; general Robin boundary conditions can require a boundary contribution to the quadratic form.
For p=1, q=0, w=x on (0,π), the polynomial trial function y=x(π−x) satisfies the endpoint conditions. Its Rayleigh quotient uses
F=∫0π​(π−2x)2dx=3π3​,G=∫0π​x3(π−x)2dx=60π6​,
(44)
so the polynomial estimate is λpoly​=20/π3≃0.64503​. The trigonometric trial function y=sinx gives
F=2π​,G=∫0π​xsin2xdx=4π2​,
(45)
and hence λtrig​=2/π≃0.63662​. Both are upper bounds on the lowest eigenvalue, by the Rayleigh-Ritz method; neither trial is an exact eigenfunction, so the inequalities are strict.
Their closeness has a useful explanation beyond their similar single-arch shapes. Both trial functions are symmetric under x↦π−x, forcing ∫0π​xy2dx=(π/2)∫0π​y2dx. The reflection-symmetric trials for a linearly weighted Rayleigh quotient therefore reduce this problem to a constant multiple of the ordinary Dirichlet energy quotient. The sine is the best trial within this symmetric class, and the polynomial's value is only a factor 10/π2≃1.0132 larger. The unrestricted lowest eigenfunction can be asymmetric because the weight x is asymmetric.
