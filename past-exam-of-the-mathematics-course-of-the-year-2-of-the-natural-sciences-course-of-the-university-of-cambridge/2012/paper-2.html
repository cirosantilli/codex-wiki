The group order ∣G∣ is the number of elements of the finite group. A normal subgroup H≤G satisfies gHg−1=H for every g∈G, equivalently its left and right cosets agree.
For the square, let R be counterclockwise rotation by π/2, and s reflection in the horizontal axis. The dihedral group is
D4​={I,R,R2,R3,s,Rs,R2s,R3s},R4=s2=I,sRs=R−1.
(45)
The first four elements rotate by 0,90,180,270 degrees. The other four are Euclidean reflections in the horizontal axis, the line y=x, the vertical axis and the line y=−x, respectively, for a square centred at the origin with sides parallel to the axes.
The eight nontrivial proper subgroups are
{I,R2},{I,Rjs}(j=0,1,2,3),⟨R⟩={I,R,R2,R3},{I,R2,s,R2s},{I,R2,Rs,R3s}.​​
(46)
To check completeness, Lagrange's theorem allows proper orders one, two and four. The order-two subgroups are generated by the five involutions displayed. An order-four subgroup containing R or R3 is the rotation subgroup. Otherwise it must contain reflections; their product is a rotation, which must be R2, so its reflections differ by that half-turn, giving the two listed Klein subgroups. The trivial subgroup {I} is an additional proper subgroup. Thus the PDF's count of eight uses “proper” to exclude the trivial subgroup; with the usual inclusive definition there are nine proper subgroups.
A normal subgroup of order two is central: conjugation fixes its identity and must also fix its only other member. The half-turn R2 commutes with every square symmetry, whereas the reflection subgroups are exchanged by conjugation. Hence the unique order-two normal subgroup is
{I,R2}.​
(47)
For the general polygon, write Dn​={Rj,Rjs:0≤j<n} with Rn=s2=I and sRs=R−1. A rotation Rj commutes with s exactly when Rj=R−j, or 2j≡0(modn). A reflection cannot commute with R when n≥3, because
R(Rjs)R−1=Rj+2s=Rjs.
(48)
Therefore the center of a group here is {I} for odd n, and {I,Rn/2} for even n. The order-two normal subgroups correspond exactly to nonidentity central involutions, proving
{no order-two normal subgroup,{I,Rn/2} is the unique one,​n odd,n even.​​
(49)
This agrees with the involutions in an even dihedral group and uses the convention ∣Dn​∣=2n throughout.

A group homomorphism is a multiplication-preserving map ϕ:G1​→G2​, so ϕ(gh)=ϕ(g)ϕ(h). A group isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism; its inverse is automatically multiplication-preserving as well.
For the real general linear group, the identity matrix has nonzero determinant. If A,B have nonzero determinants, multiplicativity of the determinant gives det(AB)=detAdetB=0. Associativity is inherited from matrix multiplication, and every such matrix has an inverse, with detA−1=1/detA=0. Hence the given set GL(n,R) is a group.

when both factors are special orthogonal. Moreover A−1=AT is again orthogonal and has determinant one. Thus SO(n) is a subgroup. The orientation-preserving general linear group is also a subgroup: the identity has positive determinant, products preserve positivity, and inversion replaces the determinant by its positive reciprocal.

(ab​cd​)=AT=A−1=(d−c​−ba​).

The conjugacy class of g∈G is {hgh−1:h∈G}. An irreducible representation acts on a nonzero vector space with no nonzero proper subspace invariant under every group element.
In the quaternion group, 1 and −1 commute with all elements, so each forms its own conjugacy class. The element i commutes with ±1,±i, while conjugation by j or k changes it to −i. Thus its class is {i,−i}. The same argument for j,k gives all five classes:
{1},{−1},{i,−i},{j,−j},{k,−k}.​
(55)
Their sizes sum to eight. Equivalently each noncentral element has a four-element centralizer and hence a two-element class.
Use the following finite-group representation theorems over C: the number of inequivalent irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes, and their dimensions obey the sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions, ∑dj2​=∣G∣. Thus there are five irreducibles, with ∑j=15​dj2​=8. Starting with five dimensions equal to one uses five of those eight units; increasing one dimension from one to two uses exactly the remaining three, while any larger increase would exceed eight. Therefore
five complex irreducible representations, with dimensions 1,1,1,1,2.​
(56)
These are the dimensions underlying the character table of the quaternion group. The scalar field matters: the quaternionic irreducible becomes four-dimensional over the reals. The class-count and dimension-sum statements used here have their standard complex meaning.
