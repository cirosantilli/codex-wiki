For real positive weight w, the weighted inner product is
⟨y1​,y2​⟩w​=∫ab​y1​(x)∗y2​(x)w(x)dx.​
(1)
The functions must have finite weighted norm; the conjugate is omitted for real functions. Multiplying the differential equation by xe−x gives
(xe−xy′)′+ne−xy=0.
(2)
Thus the Sturm-Liouville form has p=xe−x, q=0 and w=e−x. With the positive-sign spectral convention define
Ay=−w1​(py′)′=−xy′′+(x−1)y′,Ay=ny.​
(3)
The existing Laguerre differential operator on polynomials uses the opposite operator L=−A, with eigenvalues −n; keeping this sign convention explicit avoids confusing the two descriptions.
For admissible f,g, integration by parts gives
⟨f,Ag⟩w​−⟨Af,g⟩w​=[xe−x(f′∗g−f∗g′)]0∞​.
(4)
The boundary form must vanish for every pair in the self-adjoint domain. In the usual regular-at-zero Laguerre realization one imposes limx↓0​xe−xy′=0, excluding the logarithmic branch; at infinity one uses the weighted square-integrable domain with vanishing boundary form. A condition y(∞)=0 would be inappropriate for polynomial eigenfunctions. For any pair of polynomials, the factor x makes the boundary form zero at zero, while the exponential beats all polynomial growth at infinity. These functions also have finite weighted norms.
Self-adjointness yields (n−m)⟨ym​,yn​⟩w​=0, giving orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions:
∫0∞​e−xym​(x)∗yn​(x)dx=0(m=n).​
(5)
For the specified polynomials, direct differentiation gives Au=u and Av=3v. Hence their eigenvalues in the displayed convention are 1 and 3, respectively, or −1 and −3 for L=−A.
To find the quadratic, put h=ax2+bx+c. The equation xh′′+(1−x)h′+2h=0 reduces to (4a+b)x+(b+2c)=0. Thus b=−4a, c=2a. Since h(1)=−a=1,
h(x)=−x2+4x−2.​
(6)
These are scalar multiples of the Laguerre polynomials of degrees one, three and two.
For n=0, the first integral is xe−xy′=C, so all solutions with the prescribed interior normalization are
y(x)=1+C∫x0​x​tet​dt.
(7)
Two independent choices are
y1​(x)=1,y2​(x)=1+∫x0​x​tet​dt.​
(8)
They both equal one at x0​, and their Wronskian is ex/x=0. For sufficiently large x, the last unit interval contributes at least ex−1/x, proving that y2​→∞. These are the zero-eigenvalue solutions of the Laguerre differential equation, with the second expressible through the exponential integral. It also has a logarithmic singularity at zero and is not an admissible polynomial or weighted square-integrable eigenfunction. An interior value alone does not enforce the endpoint conditions of the spectral problem.
