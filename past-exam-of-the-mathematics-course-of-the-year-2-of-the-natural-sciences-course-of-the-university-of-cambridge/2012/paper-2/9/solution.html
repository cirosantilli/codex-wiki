A group homomorphism is a multiplication-preserving map ϕ:G1​→G2​, so ϕ(gh)=ϕ(g)ϕ(h). A group isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism; its inverse is automatically multiplication-preserving as well.
For the real general linear group, the identity matrix has nonzero determinant. If A,B have nonzero determinants, multiplicativity of the determinant gives det(AB)=detAdetB=0. Associativity is inherited from matrix multiplication, and every such matrix has an inverse, with detA−1=1/detA=0. Hence the given set GL(n,R) is a group.
For the special orthogonal group, the identity is included and
(AB)(AB)T=A(BBT)AT=I,det(AB)=1
(50)
when both factors are special orthogonal. Moreover A−1=AT is again orthogonal and has determinant one. Thus SO(n) is a subgroup. The orientation-preserving general linear group is also a subgroup: the identity has positive determinant, products preserve positivity, and inversion replaces the determinant by its positive reciprocal.
Now take an element (ac​bd​) of the special orthogonal group in two dimensions. Orthogonality and determinant one imply
(ab​cd​)=AT=A−1=(d−c​−ba​).
(51)
Hence d=a, b=−c, and the unit length of the first column gives a2+c2=1. There is an angle θ, unique modulo 2π, such that a=cosθ, c=sinθ. Therefore
A=R(θ)=(cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​).​
(52)
Define the map from the circle group by
ϕ:U(1)⟶SO(2),a+ic⟼(ac​−ca​).​
(53)
If z=a+ic and w=p+iq, matrix multiplication gives ϕ(z)ϕ(w)=(ap−cqaq+cp​−(aq+cp)ap−cq​)=ϕ(zw). Thus it is a homomorphism. The entries determine a,c uniquely, proving injectivity, and the parametrization proves surjectivity. This establishes
SO(2)≅U(1).​
(54)
The use of the unit complex number, rather than an unrestricted real angle, removes the 2π ambiguity: angles add modulo 2π, exactly as unit complex numbers multiply.
