The intersection H∩K contains the identity element. If a,b∈H∩K, then ab−1 belongs to both H and K, so it belongs to their intersection. The subgroup test proves that H∩K is a subgroup of G, with the inherited operation. Explicitly, closure and inverses come from this test, and associativity is inherited from G.
Label the vertices of the equilateral triangle 1,2,3 counterclockwise. For the dihedral group of order six, let r=(123) be the counterclockwise rotation by 120∘, and let s=(23) be reflection in the line through vertex 1 and the opposite side's midpoint. Choose
I=e,A=r,B=r2,C=s,D=rs,E=r2s.
(61)
Then B rotates by 240∘. The three Euclidean reflections C,D,E fix vertices 1,3,2 respectively. Products act on a vertex from right to left. The relations r3=s2=e and sr=r−1s give
(risϵ)(rjsη)=ri+(−1)ϵjsϵ+η,
(62)
where exponents of r are reduced modulo three and those of s modulo two. This constructs the entire Cayley table:
IABCDE​IIABCDE​AABIECD​BBIADEC​CCDEIAB​DDECBIA​EECDABI​​.
(63)
For example AC=D whereas CA=E, so the group is nonabelian.
A subgroup of order two is generated by its unique nonidentity element, which must have order two. The rotations A,B have order three, and each reflection has order two. Thus there are exactly
⟨C⟩={I,C},⟨D⟩={I,D},⟨E⟩={I,E}.​
(64)
None is a normal subgroup. Conjugation by A sends the reflection rjs to rj+2s, cycling the three reflections. For example ACA−1=E∈/⟨C⟩. Each of the other two subgroups fails the same conjugation test.
Finally, take two distinct such subgroups. For instance their union is ⟨C⟩∪⟨D⟩={I,C,D}, but CD=B lies outside it. Thus it is not closed under the inherited group operation and is not a group. The distinctness is necessary: the union of a subgroup with itself is that subgroup.
