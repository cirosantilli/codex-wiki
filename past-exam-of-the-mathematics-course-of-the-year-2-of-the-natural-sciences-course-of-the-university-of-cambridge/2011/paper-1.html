(1)
Put ω=∇×v. Using the Levi-Civita symbol, the Kronecker delta contraction and the product rule,
[∇×(v×ω)]i​​=∂j​(vi​ωj​−vj​ωi​)=ωj​∂j​vi​−vj​∂j​ωi​−ωi​∂j​vj​+vi​∂j​ωj​.​
(2)
The last term vanishes because the divergence of a curl is zero: the symmetric mixed derivatives contract against an antisymmetric symbol. Thus the curl of a cross product identity becomes
∇×(v×ω)=(ω⋅∇)v−(v⋅∇)ω−ω(∇⋅v).​
(3)
For orthogonal coordinates qi​ with position vector r(q1​,q2​,q3​), the scale factors of orthogonal coordinates and local unit vectors are
hi​=​∂qi​∂r​​,ei​=hi​1​∂qi​∂r​,dr=∑i​hi​ei​dqi​.

Orthogonality gives ds2=∑i​hi2​dqi2​, and right-handedness fixes e1​×e2​=e3​. In cylindrical coordinates, r=(ρcosϕ,ρsinϕ,z), so
hρ​=1,hϕ​=ρ,hz​=1,eρ​=(cosϕ,sinϕ,0),eϕ​=(−sinϕ,cosϕ,0),ez​=(0,0,1).​
(5)
The radial vector points away from the axis; the azimuthal vector is tangent in the direction of increasing ϕ; ez​ is the Cartesian positive z direction. The azimuthal basis is undefined on the coordinate axis itself.
For the axial velocity profile, the cylindrical coordinates formula gives
ω=∇×u=−∂ρ​uz​eϕ​=2u0​ρeϕ​.​
(6)
The associated cross product is
u×ω=−2u02​ρ(1−ρ2)eρ​.​
(7)
This field has only a radial component depending on ρ, so its curl is zero. Also ∇⋅u=∂z​uz​=0. The identity above therefore yields
(ω⋅∇)u=(u⋅∇)ω.​
(8)
In fact both directional derivatives vanish: the first differentiates uz​ez​ only in the azimuthal direction, and the second differentiates ω only in the z direction. All physical vector fields extend smoothly through ρ=0, despite the coordinate-basis singularity.

A square matrix is invertible precisely when its determinant is nonzero. Equivalent conditions are full rank, a trivial kernel, and zero not being an eigenvalue.
For a lower triangular matrix, the first row has only the possibly nonzero entry A11​. A Laplace expansion along that row gives detA=A11​detA(1), where the remaining principal submatrix is again lower triangular. Induction from the one-by-one case yields
detA=j=1∏n​Ajj​.​
(27)
This proof includes singular triangular matrices; it makes no division by diagonal entries.
For the diagonal example, put N=n(n+1)/2. Its determinant is b1+2+⋯+n=bN, and invertibility requires b=0. Write the complex number as b=reiθ with r>0. A nonzero purely imaginary determinant requires cos(Nθ)=0. Thus
b=rexp[n(n+1)(2m+1)πi​],r>0,m∈Z.​
(28)
Equivalently bN∈iR∖{0}; the angle is understood modulo 2π.
For skew-Hermitian matrices, reversing products under the conjugate transpose gives (CD)†=D†C†=DC. Consequently
CD is skew-Hermitian ⟺ DC=−CD ⟺ CD+DC=0.​
(29)
The latter condition is vanishing of the anticommutator. A universal choice for the last request is
α=−1,CD−DC=[C,D].​
(30)
Indeed (CD−DC)†=DC−CD=−(CD−DC), so the commutator of two skew-Hermitian matrices is skew-Hermitian.

Over the chosen scalar field, an n-by-n matrix is diagonalizable if and only if it has n linearly independent eigenvectors. Repeated eigenvalues are allowed provided all eigenspaces together supply a basis.
For the given matrix, the characteristic polynomial is λ2−4λ−5=(λ−5)(λ+1). One may choose
λ1​=5,V1​=(51​),λ2​=−1,V2​=(−11​).​
(31)
Taking these as columns gives the change of basis
P=(51​−11​),P−1=61​(1−1​15​),P−1AP=(50​0−1​).​
(32)
This is not a rotation matrix. The PDF's terminology cannot hold literally: A is real and nonsymmetric, whereas a real matrix orthogonally similar to a real diagonal matrix is symmetric. Also V1​⋅V2​=−4=0, so scaling these eigenvectors cannot make an orthonormal rotation basis. A general invertible similarity is the valid construction.
Let D=P−1AP. Inserting PP−1 between successive factors gives An=PDnP−1, and hence
P−1AnP=diag(5n,(−1)n).​
(33)
To write An=αn​A+βn​I, match its action on the two eigenspaces:
5αn​+βn​=5n,−αn​+βn​=(−1)n.
(34)
Solving gives the matrix powers for a diagonalizable matrix with two eigenvalues
An=65n+(−1)n+1​A+65n+5(−1)n​I,n≥0.​
(35)
The cases n=0,1 recover I,A, respectively. Equivalently the spectral projections of a diagonalizable matrix are (A+I)/6 and (5I−A)/6, and the power multiplies them by 5n and (−1)n.

The Cauchy-Riemann equations are ux​=vy​ and uy​=−vx​. A holomorphic function is smooth, so equality of mixed partial derivatives gives
Δu=(vy​)x​−(vx​)y​=0,Δv=−(uy​)x​+(ux​)y​=0.​
(36)
Thus its real and imaginary parts are harmonic functions.
An entire function is holomorphic at every finite point of the complex plane. The globally convergent power series for the exponential implies that ez and e−z are entire. Their difference therefore gives
sinhz=2ez−e−z​,(sinhz)′=coshz
(37)
at every complex z, proving that the hyperbolic sine is entire. For powers, the base case f1​(z)=z has derivative one everywhere. If zn is entire with derivative nzn−1, the product rule gives (zn+1)′=zn+znzn−1=(n+1)zn everywhere. Induction proves that every positive integer power is entire.
A function has a pole of order n at z0​ when it is holomorphic in a punctured neighbourhood and g(z)=(z−z0​)nf(z) extends holomorphically to z0​ with g(z0​)=0. An isolated essential singularity is neither a removable singularity nor a pole; equivalently its Laurent series has infinitely many nonzero negative-power terms. In particular
e1/z=∑m=0∞​m!z−m​,0<∣z∣<∞,
(38)
has an essential singularity at zero.
For fN​, the Laurent series about infinity is
fN​(z)=m=0∑∞​m!zN−m​=zN+zN−1+2!zN−2​+⋯+N!1​+(N+1)!z−1​+⋯.​
(39)
To classify the point correctly, use w=1/z. Then fN​(1/w)=w−New, and ew is holomorphic with value one at zero. Therefore
fN​ has a pole of order N at infinity.​
(40)
The pole at infinity is compatible with an essential singularity at the finite point zero. Negative powers of z are regular powers of the local coordinate w near infinity, so they do not make that point essential.

Write a second-order linear ordinary differential equation in monic form y′′+P(x)y′+Q(x)y=0. At a finite point x0​, an ordinary point has analytic P,Q. A singular point is a regular singular point if (x−x0​)P and (x−x0​)2Q extend analytically there. A singular point failing this condition is an irregular singular point. The point at infinity is classified by the reciprocal coordinate t=1/x.
For the Laguerre differential equation, P=1/x−1 and Q=ν/x. Thus xP=1−x and x2Q=νx are analytic at zero, while P itself is singular: zero is regular singular. Put Y(t)=y(1/t). Since y′=−t2Y′ and y′′=t4Y′′+2t3Y′, the transformed equation is
Y′′+(t1​+t21​)Y′+t3ν​Y=0.
(41)
Here tPt​=1+1/t is not analytic at zero, so infinity is an irregular singular point, for every real ν.
For the Frobenius solution, the lowest power xσ−1 has coefficient a0​σ2. Hence the indicial equation is σ2=0, and equating the remaining powers gives
(k+1+σ)2ak+1​+(ν−k−σ)ak​=0.
(42)
With the repeated root σ=0, the Frobenius series for the Laguerre differential equation obeys
ak+1​=(k+1)2k−ν​ak​.​
(43)
If the series does not terminate, its consecutive term ratio is asymptotic to x/k, so the ratio test gives infinite radius of convergence. It is an entire function, even though the differential equation has a singular coefficient at zero. The repeated indicial root yields only one analytic Frobenius branch; an independent solution generally has a logarithmic term there.
For ν=n≥0, the factor k−n stops the recurrence at degree n. With a0​=1 its coefficients are ak​=(−1)k(kn​)/k!, giving the Laguerre polynomials
L0​=1,L1​=1−x,L2​=1−2x+2x2​,L3​=1−3x+23x2​−6x3​.​
(44)
The same termination argument includes n=0, as required for L0​ despite the earlier reference to positive integers.

δ(F−μG)​=2∫0π​[u′η′+(ϵx−μ)uη]dx=2∫0π​[−u′′+(ϵx−μ)u]ηdx.​

For ϵ=0, zero and negative eigenvalues yield no nonzero solution with both Dirichlet boundary conditions. Positive eigenvalues have solutions u=Asin(λ​x) after the left condition, and the right condition gives λ​=n, n≥1. Hence the lowest eigenpair is
