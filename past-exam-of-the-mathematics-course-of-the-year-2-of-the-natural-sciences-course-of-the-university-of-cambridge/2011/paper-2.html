For two real eigenfunctions, multiply the equation for yn​ by ym​ and the equation for ym​ by yn​, then subtract. The q terms cancel. The resulting Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator is
[p(ym​yn′​−yn​ym′​)]′+(λn​−λm​)rym​yn​=0.
(1)
Integrating first over [ϵ,R] and then taking the endpoint limits gives
(λn​−λm​)∫0∞​rym​yn​dx=−[p(ym​yn′​−yn​ym′​)]0∞​.
(2)
The needed assumptions are that the improper integral exists and that the displayed boundary expression tends to zero at both endpoints. Bounded ym​,yn​ and their first derivatives are sufficient because p→0, but are stronger than necessary. Merely knowing that p→0 does not control arbitrarily growing eigenfunctions. With the vanishing boundary form and distinct eigenvalues, division by λn​−λm​ proves orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions:
∫0∞​r(x)ym​(x)yn​(x)dx=0(m=n).​
(3)
For complex eigenfunctions in a real self-adjoint differential operator problem, use ym​​ instead, giving the corresponding weighted inner product.
Multiplying the particular equation by e−x puts it in Sturm-Liouville form:
(xe−xy′)′+λe−xy=0.

Consequently
p(x)=xe−x,q(x)=0,r(x)=e−x.​

The Dirichlet uniqueness for the Poisson equation follows from a positive integral. Suppose Ta​,Tb​ satisfy the same data, and put w=Ta​−Tb​. Then w is a harmonic function in V and vanishes on S. Green's first identity, applied with both arguments w, gives
∫V​∣∇w∣2dV=∫S​w∂n​wdS−∫V​wΔwdV=0.
(9)
Thus ∇w=0, and w is constant on the connected body. Its zero boundary value makes that constant zero. This proves at most one temperature field; it does not require an independent existence construction. As usual, the argument assumes a bounded body and sufficient regularity for Green's first identity, or the corresponding finite-energy weak formulation.
For the composite body, let w−​ and w+​ be the differences in V1​ and V2​. They are harmonic functions, have a common interface value w, and obey
α∂n​w+​=β∂n​w−​,w+​∣S2​​=0.
(10)
The unit normal vector n points out of V1​; the outward normal of V2​ on its inner boundary is −n. Keeping that sign in Green's first identity gives
E1​:=∫V1​​∣∇w−​∣2dV=∫S1​​w∂n​w−​dS,E2​:=∫V2​​∣∇w+​∣2dV=−∫S1​​w∂n​w+​dS.
(11)
The outer boundary term in E2​ is zero. Weight these equations by the positive coefficients that match the flux condition:
βE1​+αE2​=∫S1​​w(β∂n​w−​−α∂n​w+​)dS=0.​
(12)
Both Dirichlet energies must vanish. Hence both differences are constants; the outer data set the constant in V2​ to zero, and interface continuity sets the constant in V1​ to zero. This establishes uniqueness for a harmonic transmission problem. Physically β and α play the roles of the inner and outer thermal conductivities; continuity of temperature and normal heat flux is what cancels the interface contribution.

For the two insulating walls, successive Euclidean reflections in x=b and x=−b generate sources of strength q at (2nb,0) for every integer n. Only n=0 lies inside the physical strip. This is the Neumann image lattice for a point source in a strip. Set

The group order of a finite group is its number of elements, ∣G∣. The order of a group element g is the least positive integer r with gr=e. Such an r exists in a finite group because two powers must coincide, and cancellation then gives a positive power equal to the identity element.
For the direct product of groups, the componentwise operation is closed because each factor is closed. Associativity holds in each coordinate:
[(g,h)(g′,h′)](g′′,h′′)=(gg′g′′,hh′h′′)=(g,h)[(g′,h′)(g′′,h′′)].
(56)
The identity element is (e1​,e2​), and the inverse element of (g,h) is (g−1,h−1). Thus all group axioms hold. For finite factors, the multiplication principle gives
∣G1​×G2​∣=∣G1​∣∣G2​∣.​
(57)
For Z2​×Z2​, write E=(e,e), u=(w,e), v=(e,w), z=(w,w), with w2=e. Its Cayley table, with the row element multiplying the column element, is
Euvz​EEuvz​uuEzv​vvzEu​zzvuE​​.
(58)
This is the Klein four-group: every nonidentity element has order two.
In the cyclic group Z4​=⟨a⟩, the set {e,a2} is closed because (a2)2=e, and both elements have inverses in the set. It is therefore a proper subgroup of order two. By Lagrange's theorem, a proper subgroup has order one or two. An order-two subgroup must be generated by an element of order two. Neither a nor a3 has order two, whereas a2 does. Hence
the proper subgroups of Z4​ are {e} and {e,a2}.​
(59)
The PDF's claim needs “nontrivial”: {e,a2} is the only nontrivial proper subgroup, but {e} is also a proper subgroup under the usual definition.
A group isomorphism preserves element orders: ϕ(g)r=e if and only if gr=e, using the homomorphism property and injectivity. Since Z4​ has elements of order four and the Klein four-group does not,
Z2​×Z2​≅Z4​.​
(60)

These verify the listed matrices and the defining relations, with R(e)=I2​. To check every product explicitly through a single calculation, write elements in the unique form xϵyj, with ϵ=0,1 and j=0,1,2. The relations give
(xϵyj)(xηyk)=xϵ+ηy(−1)ηj+k.
(68)
The displayed matrices obey YjX=XY−j, so their product has exactly the same form:
(XϵYj)(XηYk)=Xϵ+ηY(−1)ηj+k=R((xϵyj)(xηyk)).
(69)
All exponents are reduced modulo two or three respectively. Thus this verifies all 36 products, and R is a two-dimensional complex group representation. It is given to be an irreducible representation.
For a one-dimensional complex group representation U, let u=U(x) and v=U(y), which are nonzero complex numbers. The relations require
u2=1,v3=1,vu=uv2.
(70)
Since scalar multiplication commutes and u=0, the last relation gives v=v2, hence v=1. Then u=±1. The choice u=1 is the trivial representation; the unique nontrivial choice is the sign representation:
U(e)=U(y)=U(y2)=1,U(x)=U(xy)=U(xy2)=−1.​
(71)
Its group kernel is
kerU={e,y,y2}.​
(72)
A faithful representation is injective and has trivial kernel. Here the kernel has three elements, so U is not faithful.
Both one-dimensional representations are irreducible representations, and they are inequivalent to each other and to the given two-dimensional one. The sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions gives
12+12+22=6=∣S3​∣.
(73)
These exhaust all complex irreducible representations. Their number equals the number of conjugacy classes, so there are three conjugacy classes. Explicitly they are
{e},{y,y2},{x,xy,xy2}.​
(74)
The last two sets consist of the 3-cycles and transpositions respectively.
