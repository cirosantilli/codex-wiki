Reflect the source across the equatorial plane and subtract the two ball kernels. This gives the Dirichlet Green function inside a hemisphere Ghemi​(x;x0​)=GB​(x;x0​)−GB​(x;x0−​). It is zero on the spherical boundary because each ball kernel is zero there, and on the plane because reflection makes their values equal.
For a point charge e, Poisson's equation in SI units is ΔV=−eδ(x−x0​)/ε0​, so V=−eGhemi​/ε0​. Writing x0∗−​ for the reflected inverse point gives
V(x)=4πε0​e​[∣x−x0​∣1​−∣x−x0−​∣1​−∣x−x0∗​∣a/r0​​+∣x−x0∗−​∣a/r0​​].​

At a point of complex differentiability, the complex derivative is independent of the direction of approach. Taking increments along the real axis gives f′=ux​+ivx​; taking imaginary increments gives f′=vy​−iuy​. Equality of their real parts and imaginary parts proves the Cauchy-Riemann equations ux​=vy​ and uy​=−vx​.
For the specified real part, observe that z2ez has exactly that real part. Equivalently, integrating vy​=ux​ and checking vx​=−uy​ gives
v=ex[(x2−y2)siny+2xycosy]+C.
(9)
Thus
f(z)=z2ez+iC,C∈R.​
(10)
The freedom is only a constant imaginary part: the difference of two such holomorphic functions has zero real part, so its Cauchy-Riemann equations make it constant on each connected domain.

The square-root factor gives an algebraic branch point at z=0. The logarithm is locally analytic there but has value an odd multiple of iπ, which is nonzero, so it cannot cancel that branching. The zero and pole of (z−1)/(z+1) give logarithmic branch points at z=1 and z=−1, respectively. Thus the finite singularities are
z=0,1,−1(all branch points; no finite poles).​
(11)
Infinity is also a branch point. On the logarithm branch tending to zero there, log[(z−1)/(z+1)]=−2/z+O(z−3), so the product behaves as −2z−1/2. Other logarithm branches add 2πikz​ and retain branching. A chosen branch cut joins or separates these branch points; its ordinary interior points are not additional branch points of the underlying multivalued function.

The residue theorem states that the contour integral of a meromorphic function around a positively oriented simple closed contour equals 2πi times the sum of its enclosed residues, provided there are no singularities on the contour. The roots of 1+zn are zk​=e(2k+1)iπ/n, k=0,…,n−1. They are simple poles, with residues 1/(nzkn−1​)=−zk​/n.
Use the sector contour of angle 2π/n, running outward along the positive axis and inward along the rotated ray. It encloses only z0​=eiπ/n. The large-arc contribution is O(R1−n), hence vanishes. With J=∫0∞​dx/(1+xn), the two ray contributions give
(1−e2πi/n)J=−n2πi​eiπ/n.
(13)
Since 1−e2iα=−2ieiαsinα,
J=nsin(π/n)π​.​
(14)
For the second integral, use zn/2/(1+zn) on a branch continuous across the sector away from zero, with a small inner arc of radius ϵ. Its large arc is O(R1−n/2) and its small arc is O(ϵ1+n/2), both vanishing for n≥3. On the rotated ray, zn/2 changes by eiπ=−1, so the two radial contributions add rather than subtract. The enclosed residue is z01−n/2​/n=−ieiπ/n/n. Consequently
(1+e2πi/n)I=n2π​eiπ/n,I=ncos(π/n)π​​.

Both answers are instances of the Mellin integral of a reciprocal binomial. The direct contour argument also tracks the branch needed when n is odd.

A two-index Cartesian tensor has components transforming as Tij′​=Rik​Rjl​Tkl​ under an orthogonal change of orthonormal basis. The tensor order here counts indices, rather than the matrix rank. Its scalar, symmetric-traceless and axial tensor decomposition follows by setting

For a conservative system near a stable equilibrium, the quadratic Lagrangian has the form L=21​q˙​TMq˙​−21​qTKq, with symmetric mass matrix M and stiffness matrix K. A normal mode q=veiωt satisfies the generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations (K−ω2M)v=0, so the frequency equation is det(K−ω2M)=0.
To quadratic order, vertical velocities contribute only fourth-order corrections to the kinetic energy, leaving T=(x˙2+y˙​2+z˙2)/2. A string of length ℓ with small horizontal difference d shortens its vertical projection by d2/(2ℓ). The upper segment carries all three masses, the middle segment two, and the lower segment one. V=3g2(3a)x2​+2g2(2b)(y−x)2​+g2c(z−y)2​=2g​[ax2​+b(x−y)2​+c(y−z)2​].

Lagrange's theorem states that the group order of a finite group divides the group order; more precisely ∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣.
If every element squares to the identity element, every element is its own inverse element, and ab=(ab)−1=b−1a−1=ba. Thus a group of exponent two is abelian. For distinct nonidentity a,b, the four elements 1,a,b,ab are distinct: for example ab=1 would imply a=b, and ab=a would imply b=1. Products remain in this set because a,b commute and square to one. Thus they form a Klein four-group subgroup of order four.
Now let ∣G∣=2p with odd prime number p. By Lagrange's theorem, every element order is one of 1,2,p,2p. If an element has order 2p, its square has order p. If no element had order p, all nonidentity elements would therefore have order two. There are at least two distinct such elements, producing a subgroup of order four. This contradicts 4∤2p. Hence there is an element of order p.

A group homomorphism satisfies φ(g1​g2​)=φ(g1​)φ(g2​); a bijective one is a group isomorphism. Its kernel of a group homomorphism is K={g:φ(g)=1H​}. It is a subgroup, and for k∈K, g∈G,
φ(gkg−1)=φ(g)φ(k)φ(g)−1=1H​.
(27)
Thus gKg−1⊆K, and applying the same argument to g−1 gives equality. Therefore K is a normal subgroup.
Write Bn​=(10​n1​). Direct matrix multiplication gives Bn​Bm​=Bn+m​, B0​=I and Bn−1​=B−n​. Consequently these matrices form the integer shear group, a subgroup of GL(2,R). It is an infinite cyclic group, generated by B1​, since B1n​=Bn​ and no nonzero integer power is the identity.
The map n↦Bn​ is a bijective group homomorphism from the additive group of integers, proving the required group isomorphism. There are proper subgroups: for example {B2n​:n∈Z} omits B1​. In fact every subgroup is {Bmn​:n∈Z} for an integer m≥0, by the classification of subgroups of an infinite cyclic group; m=0 gives the trivial subgroup and m=1 the whole group.
