On the sphere, constant θ gives a latitude circle of radius asinθ and height acosθ. Constant ϕ gives a meridian semicircle in a vertical plane through the z axis. The orthonormal basis of spherical coordinates is
er​=(sinθcosϕ,sinθsinϕ,cosθ),eθ​=(cosθcosϕ,cosθsinϕ,−sinθ),eϕ​=(−sinϕ,cosϕ,0).
(1)
Here er​ points outward, eθ​ along increasing polar angle, and eϕ​ along increasing azimuth. At either pole the radial unit vector is unambiguous, but the two tangential coordinate vectors depend on the undefined longitude. The figure shows their directions away from the poles.
This coordinate ambiguity does not make a vector field singular if its tangential coefficient vanishes suitably. In Cartesian coordinates,
A(+)=r(r+z)(−y,x,0)​,A(−)=r(r−z)(y,−x,0)​.
(2)
The first is smooth at the north pole and zero there; the second is smooth and zero at the south pole. Their actual singular axes are the opposite ones. These are the regular patches of a two-patch vector potential for a radial monopole.

A complex 3×3 matrix is diagonalizable exactly when it has a basis of three linearly independent eigenvectors. If all its eigenvalues are one, a diagonalization would have D=I and hence A=PIP−1=I. Therefore the specified matrix, which is not a scalar identity matrix, is not diagonalizable.
For the displayed real symmetric matrix, choose the orthogonal matrix
Q=​1/2​1/2​0​1/2​−1/2​0​001​​.
(24)
Its columns form an orthonormal eigenbasis, and
QTAQ=diag(1,3,3).​
(25)
In the orthogonal coordinates u=(x+y)/2​, v=(x−y)/2​, the quadric surface has equation u2+3v2+3z2=1. It is a prolate spheroid, with long semiaxis one in direction (1,1,0) and two equal short semiaxes 1/3​. The figure gives its principal-axis sketch.
A diagonalizable matrix can certainly have exactly two distinct eigenvalues: diag(1,1,2) is already diagonal and has an eigenbasis despite its repeated eigenvalue.

A Hermitian matrix satisfies A†=AT=A. If Av=λv with v=0, then v†Av=λv†v is real because its conjugate equals v†A†v=v†Av. Since v†v>0, its eigenvalue λ is real. If Aw=μw with μ=λ, then
μv†w=v†Aw=(Av)†w=λv†w,
(26)
so v†w=0: distinct eigenspaces are orthogonal for the standard complex inner product.
The converse is false. For example (10​12​) has the two real eigenvalues one and two but is not a Hermitian matrix. It can be embedded as a block in a 3×3 example if desired.
For Hermitian A,B, (AB)†=B†A†=BA. Thus AB is Hermitian exactly when AB=BA, equivalently when their commutator vanishes. Also (AB+BA)†=AB+BA, so a value that works for every such pair is
c=1.​
(27)
This sum is their anticommutator.

For f=u+iv holomorphic in z=x+iy, the Cauchy-Riemann equations are ux​=vy​ and uy​=−vx​. Therefore the normal vectors to the two level curves obey
∇u⋅∇v=ux​vx​+uy​vy​=ux​(−uy​)+uy​ux​=0.

For an isolated pole of order N≥1, the Laurent coefficients must satisfy
a−N​=0,an​=0(n<−N).​

Then the principal part of a Laurent series is a finite sum with lowest power −N. On a punctured neighborhood of the center, an essential singularity occurs exactly when infinitely many negative-power Laurent coefficients are nonzero. If all negative-power coefficients vanish, the singularity is removable; if finitely many are nonzero, the singularity is a pole. The punctured-neighborhood assumption matters: an annulus separated from its center cannot by itself classify the function there.

Termwise differentiation of the convergent Frobenius series and collection of powers in the Bessel differential equation gives the lowest coefficient (σ2−ν2)a0​, the next coefficient [(σ+1)2−ν2]a1​, and, for n≥2, the coefficient [(σ+n)2−ν2]an​+an−2​. Since a0​=0, the stated choices σ=±ν, a1​=0 and the recurrence make all coefficients vanish.
For ν=1/2, the two resulting even-coefficient series are
σ=21​:a0​x1/2(1−3!x2​+5!x4​−⋯)=a0​x​sinx​,
(36)
and
σ=−21​:a0​x−1/2(1−2!x2​+4!x4​−⋯)=a0​x​cosx​.
(37)
These are linearly independent on x>0 and give the Bessel functions of order one half, up to normalization. The specified condition a1​=0 is sufficient; in the negative-exponent branch at this particular order the next coefficient equation alone would allow a1​ to be free, adding a multiple of the positive-exponent solution.
For the Legendre differential equation, substitution gives the full conditions
σ(σ−1)=0,σ(σ+1)a1​=0,an+2​=(σ+n+2)(σ+n+1)(σ+n)(σ+n+1)−λ(λ+1)​an​(n≥0).​
(38)
Thus σ=0 permits arbitrary a1​, while σ=1 requires a1​=0. This Frobenius recurrence for the Legendre equation separates the even and odd series. The nearest singular points of the differential equation are ±1, so the series about zero has radius at least one, and the generic nonterminating solutions have radius exactly one. Particular cancellations can give a larger radius.
Termination occurs when the degree reaches a nonnegative integer N with λ(λ+1)=N(N+1). For λ≥0 this gives
λ=N∈{0,1,2,…}.​
(39)
Choose the even branch for even N or the odd branch for odd N to obtain a Legendre polynomial. The recurrence then terminates and gives a solution for all real x, with infinite radius of convergence. A nonzero entire solution at other λ≥0 is impossible: each nonterminating parity has coefficient ratio tending to one in the variable x2, and so radius one.

Take fixed-endpoint variations yε​=y+εη with η(a)=η(b)=0. The first variation is
δI=∫ab​(fy​η+fy′​η′)dx=∫ab​(fy​−dxd​fy′​)ηdx.
(40)
The boundary term vanishes by the endpoint conditions. The fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations gives the Euler-Lagrange equation fy​−d(fy′​)/dx=0. Along its solutions,
dxd​(f−y′fy′​)=fx​+y′(fy​−dxd​fy′​)=fx​.
(41)
When fx​=0, this proves the Beltrami identity f−y′fy′​=constant.
For the surface z=F(y), the line element is ds2=dx2+[1+F′(y)2]dy2. The arc-length integrand for a graph y(x) is L=1+[1+F′(y)2]y′2​. It has no explicit x dependence and satisfies L−y′Ly′​=1/L. Thus [1+F′(y)2]y′2 is constant. Choosing the fixed direction along a smooth nonconstant segment and integrating yields
∫y1+F′(s)2​ds=ax+b.​
(42)
The same result follows by defining the transverse arc coordinate u(y)=∫y1+F′(s)2​ds, which changes the metric to ds2=dx2+du2. Its geodesics are straight lines in (x,u) coordinates. This proves the formula for geodesics on a generalized cylinder and shows it gives locally shortest paths. The additional straight lines x=constant in the unrolled plane are also geodesics, but cannot be represented as graphs y(x). Classical differential-geometric statements assume the surface has sufficient smoothness; the arc-length coordinate itself only requires the displayed integral to be well defined.
