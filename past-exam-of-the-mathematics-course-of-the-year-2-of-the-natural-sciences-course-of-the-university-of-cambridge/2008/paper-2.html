A normal subgroup H satisfies gHg−1=H for every g∈G, equivalently gH=Hg. A left coset is gH={gh:h∈H} and a right coset is Hg={hg:h∈H}.
If ∣G∣=2∣H∣, then for g∈H both cosets equal H. For g∈/H, neither coset intersects H: an intersection would imply g∈H. Both have ∣H∣ elements, because multiplication by g is a bijection. They therefore both equal G∖H, proving gH=Hg in this case too. This establishes that an index-two subgroup is normal.
For Lagrange's theorem, left cosets partition G. Indeed if g1​h1​=g2​h2​, then g1​H=g2​H by multiplying by elements of H; otherwise the cosets are disjoint. Every coset has ∣H∣ elements, so if there are [G:H] cosets,
∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣.​
(25)
The group order of H thus divides the group order of G. Normality is not needed for this divisibility statement.

θ(gkg−1)=θ(g)θ(k)θ(g)−1=1H​,
(26)
so gKg−1⊆K. Applying the same inclusion with g−1 gives equality, proving that K is a normal subgroup.
The map gK↦θ(g) is well defined, multiplicative and injective, with image imθ. Thus the first isomorphism theorem gives
G/K≅imθ≤H.​
(27)
The quotient is isomorphic to all of H precisely when the original homomorphism is surjective.
For the general linear group, the determinant gives a homomorphism det:GL(n,R)→R×, since det(AB)=detAdetB. Its kernel is the special linear group SL(n,R), so that subgroup is normal. Every nonzero real t is the determinant of diag(t,1,…,1) for n≥1, making the map surjective. Therefore
GL(n,R)/SL(n,R)≅R×,​
