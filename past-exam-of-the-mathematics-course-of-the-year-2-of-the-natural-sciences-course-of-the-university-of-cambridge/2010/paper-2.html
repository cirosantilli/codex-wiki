Use separation of variables, Φ=F(r)H(θ), in the Laplace equation in polar coordinates. Dividing by FH/r2 gives
Fr(rF′)′​=−HH′′​=n2.

Single-valued 2π-periodic angular factors have integer n≥0. For n≥1, they are cosnθ,sinnθ, and the radial equation r2F′′+rF′−n2F=0 has solutions rn,r−n. The zero angular mode gives F=a0​+b0​logr. These separated products give the requested solutions; arbitrary sums may use independent coefficients for each radial-angular combination.
Boundedness at the origin removes the logarithm and all negative radial powers. The boundary function is even, so its sine Fourier coefficients vanish. With the convention f(θ)=a0​+∑n≥1​an​cosnθ, its Fourier coefficients are
a0​=2π1​∫−ππ​f(θ)dθ=2C​,an​=π1​∫−ππ​f(θ)cosnθdθ=πn2C​sin2nπ​.

Φin​=2C​+πC​arctan(1−(r/R)22(r/R)cosθ​),r<R.

At the two jumps, the boundary interpretation matters. At every continuity point the radial limit equals the prescribed value. ab=c=c−1=(ab)−1=b−1a−1=ba.
(66)
The other products follow: ac=ca=b, bc=cb=a, and a2=b2=c2=e. Thus the second possibility is the Klein four-group. The two groups are not isomorphic because only C4​ has an element of order four. This proves the order-four case of the classification of groups of order p squared.
A group homomorphism Φ:G→H obeys Φ(gh)=Φ(g)Φ(h). It maps identity to identity and inverses to inverses. Its group kernel K={g:Φ(g)=eH​} contains eG​ and is closed under ab−1, so the subgroup test makes it a subgroup. For k∈K and g∈G,
Φ(gkg−1)=Φ(g)eH​Φ(g)−1=eH​.
(67)
Thus gKg−1⊆K; applying the same argument to g−1 gives equality. Hence K is a normal subgroup.
Define Φ:G/K→H by Φ(gK)=Φ(g). If gK=hK, then h−1g∈K, giving Φ(g)=Φ(h), so this is well-defined. Coset multiplication shows it is a group homomorphism. Its kernel is just the identity coset, since Φ(g)=eH​ means g∈K. It is therefore injective, and the assumed surjectivity of Φ makes it surjective as well. This proves the first isomorphism theorem here:
G/K≅H.​
(68)
In the quaternion group, the element −1=i2=j2=k2 commutes with each of i,j,k, because an element commutes with its square. It then commutes with their negatives too, and with ±1. The set N={1,−1} is a subgroup because (−1)2=1, and it is central, hence a normal subgroup.
The four quotient cosets are N,iN,jN,kN. They square to N, and (iN)(jN)=kN. Reversed products have the same coset: for example (ij)−1=k−1=−k, while j−1i−1=(−j)(−i)=ji, so ji=−k. The same calculation gives kj=−i and ik=−j. After signs are identified, these are precisely the products in the Klein four-group. Define
Φ(±1)=e,Φ(±i)=a,Φ(±j)=b,Φ(±k)=c.​
(69)
This preserves all products, is surjective, and has kernel exactly N. The first isomorphism theorem therefore gives
Q8​/{±1}≅V.​
(70)
This is the quaternion group modulo its centre: the reverse-product relations also show that none of ±i,±j,±k is central, so Z(Q8​)=N.

Two elements are conjugate group elements if h=xgx−1 for some x∈G. The conjugacy class of g is {xgx−1:x∈G}. Conjugacy is an equivalence relation: it is reflexive using x=e, symmetric by replacing x with x−1, and transitive because
k=yhy−1,h=xgx−1⟹k=(yx)g(yx)−1.
(71)
Thus the classes partition the group: every element belongs to its own class, and any two classes that intersect are equal. This proves that every element belongs to exactly one class.
If G is Abelian, then xgx−1=g, so every class is a singleton. In general the elements whose classes are singletons are precisely those commuting with every group element. Thus the center of a group is
Z(G)={z∈G:zg=gz for every g∈G}.
(72)
It contains e. If a,b∈Z(G), then ab−1 commutes with every g, since b−1 does and a does. The subgroup test therefore proves that Z(G) is a subgroup. Any two of its elements commute with each other, so it is an abelian subgroup.
For the square centred at the origin with vertices (±1,±1), put

A group representation on a vector space W is a group homomorphism ρ:G→GL(W): ρ(gh)=ρ(g)ρ(h) and ρ(e)=I. An invariant subspace U obeys ρ(g)U⊆U for every g. A nonzero representation is irreducible if its only invariant subspaces are 0 and W. It is a faithful representation if it is injective, equivalently if its group kernel is {e}. Its character is χρ​(g)=trρ(g). For inequivalent complex irreducible representations of a finite group, character orthogonality is
∣G∣1​g∈G∑​χα​(g)​χβ​(g)=δαβ​.​
(76)
Use right-to-left composition of permutations and assign the matrix labels consistently by
I=e,A=(123),B=(132),C=(13),D=(12),E=(23).
(77)
The resulting Cayley table, with row multiplying column, is
IABCDE​IIABCDE​AABIDEC​BBIAECD​CCEDIBA​DDCEAIB​EEDCBAI​​.
(78)
Conjugation relabels the entries of a cycle, preserving its length. Thus the conjugacy classes are
{I},{A,B},{C,D,E}.​
(79)
Define the permutation representation by Pσ​ej​=eσ(j)​, or (Pσ​)ij​=δi,σ(j)​. Applying it to a basis vector proves Pσ​Pτ​=Pστ​. The six assignments above produce exactly the listed matrices. They are distinct, and Pσ​=I forces σ(j)=j for every j, so the representation is faithful.
Every permutation fixes (1,1,1), hence fixes every vector (c,c,c). Therefore V0​ is a one-dimensional invariant subspace carrying the trivial representation. Its orthogonal complement is
V0⊥​={(v1​,v2​,v3​):v1​+v2​+v3​=0},
(80)
since the inner product with (c,c,c) is c(v1​+v2​+v3​). Permuting coordinates preserves their sum, so this plane is invariant too. Every real vector has the unique decomposition
v=3v1​+v2​+v3​​(1,1,1)+[v−3v1​+v2​+v3​​(1,1,1)].
(81)
Thus the permutation representation is the direct sum of group representations on the line and the plane.
To find the eigenvectors of A, note that A(v1​,v2​,v3​)=(v3​,v1​,v2​). With ζ=e2πi/3, its three eigenvalues and eigenspaces are
1ζζ2​span(1,1,1)span(1,ζ2,ζ)span(1,ζ,ζ2)​.​
(82)
The only real eigenvalue is one, whose eigenline is V0​, so A has no nonzero real eigenvector in V0⊥​. A proper nonzero real invariant subspace of that two-dimensional plane would be a line, giving such an eigenvector. Hence the plane representation is irreducible over R.
For the later complex dimension-count theorem one should also establish irreducibility over C; real irreducibility alone would not imply it in general. In the complexified plane, the only candidate A-invariant lines are its ζ and ζ2 eigenlines. But C exchanges them:
C(1,ζ2,ζ)=ζ(1,ζ,ζ2),C(1,ζ,ζ2)=ζ2(1,ζ2,ζ).
(83)
Neither is invariant under the whole group, proving complex irreducibility as well. The required decomposition is therefore
P≅1⊕W,dimW=2,​
(84)
where W is the standard representation of the symmetric group for three letters. Its character is obtained by subtracting one from the number of fixed coordinates: on the three classes it is (2,−1,0).
Finally, the sign representation gives a second one-dimensional irreducible, with value +1 on I,A,B and −1 on the transpositions. These are exactly the two one-dimensional possibilities: if r=A,s=C, a one-dimensional representation sends them to commuting nonzero scalars v,u. The relations r3=s2=e and srs=r−1 imply v3=u2=1 and v=v−1. Hence v=1 and u=±1, the trivial and sign choices. Together with the two-dimensional W, these give
12+12+22=6=∣Σ3​∣.
