Away from the axis, cylindrical coordinates put the field in the line vortex form
v=2πρκ​eϕ​.
(5)
The only potentially nonzero component of its curl is
(∇×v)z​=ρ1​∂ρ∂​(ρvϕ​)=ρ1​∂ρ∂​2πκ​=0.
(6)
The other components vanish because there is no axial dependence and no radial or axial velocity. Thus the curl is zero for ρ>0; the field is singular on the axis, so a classical curl there is not defined.
For a spanning surface avoiding that axis, the smoothness hypotheses of Stokes theorem hold and give ∮C​v⋅dr=0. If the enclosed planar region contains the axis, the singularity prevents applying that theorem directly. Excise a small disk about the origin. The resulting annulus has zero curl, so its outer circulation equals the counterclockwise circulation on the small circle:
∮C​v⋅dr=∫02π​2πρκ​ρdϕ=κ.​
(7)
This sign assumes positive, counterclockwise orientation. Clockwise orientation gives −κ; more generally the value is κ times the winding number about the origin. A curve through the origin is excluded because its integral is singular. In the distribution interpretation the missing vorticity is concentrated on the axis as κδ(x)δ(y)ez​, explaining the nonzero circulation despite zero classical curl away from it.

Expand the radial modified Helmholtz equation as Ly=−y′′−2y′/x+y. Setting y=z/x gives Ly=(−z′′+z)/x, so
y(x)=xAex+Be−x​.

The first has limit one at zero. Their Wronskian is uv′−u′v=−x−2. Since the highest-derivative coefficient of L is −1, the Green function must have first-derivative jump −1. The radial modified Helmholtz Green function on the half-line is

An eigenvalue λ and associated eigenvector e=0 satisfy Ae=λe. The characteristic polynomial det(A−λI) has degree equal to the positive matrix size. The fundamental theorem of algebra gives a complex root, at which A−λI is singular and therefore has a nontrivial kernel. Hence every nonempty square complex matrix has an eigenvector.
A nondiagonalizable example is the Jordan block (00​10​): its only eigenvalue is zero, and its eigenspace is one-dimensional. A Hermitian matrix satisfies A†=A and has an orthonormal eigenbasis by the spectral theorem. One proof finds an eigenvector, notes that its orthogonal complement is invariant, and repeats. Its eigenvalues are real because
λe†e=e†Ae=e†Ae=λe†e,
(26)
where e†e>0.
With all eigenvalues negative, A is invertible. Its same complete eigenbasis gives
A−1ei​=λi−1​ei​,Amei​=λim​ei​(m=1,2,…).​
(27)
Some powered eigenvalues can coincide, but the original eigenvectors remain a complete basis. Writing A=Udiag(λi​)U†, the convergent matrix exponential series gives
∫0∞​etAdt=Udiag(∫0∞​etλi​dt)U†=Udiag(−λi−1​)U†.
(28)
Thus the matrix inverse from a decaying matrix exponential is
∫0∞​etAdt=−A−1,A−1=−∫0∞​etAdt.​
(29)
The identity printed in the original PDF is missing a minus sign. Already for the one-by-one matrix A=(−1) its integral is 1, whereas its inverse is −1. Equivalently, integrating d(etA)/dt=AetA gives A∫0∞​etAdt=−I, confirming the correction without diagonalization.

For a harmonic function, put a=uxx​ and b=uxy​=uyx​. Since uyy​=−uxx​, its Hessian matrix is
D2u=(ab​b−a​),detD2u=−a2−b2.
(43)
A nonzero determinant is therefore negative, and the two real Hessian eigenvalues are ±a2+b2​. At a stationary point the quadratic term of the Taylor expansion has both signs. Consequently every such nondegenerate stationary point is a saddle point. This is the nondegenerate stationary points of a planar harmonic function criterion. The same argument applies to v, whose stationary points coincide with those of u. A zero Hessian determinant is outside this second-order classification.

In monic form the Chebyshev differential equation has P(x)=−x/(1−x2) and Q(x)=p2/(1−x2). Both are analytic near zero, so it is an ordinary point. At x=1, (x−1)P=x/(x+1) and (x−1)2Q=−p2(x−1)/(x+1) are analytic; at x=−1, (x+1)P=x/(x−1) and (x+1)2Q=−p2(x+1)/(x−1) are analytic. Thus both endpoints are regular singular points.
Insert yq​=∑n≥0​an​xn+q and equate coefficients. The recurrence relation is
an+2​=(n+q+2)(n+q+1)(n+q)2−p2​an​,q=0,1.​
(47)
Explicitly the factors are (n2−p2)/[(n+2)(n+1)] for q=0 and [(n+1)2−p2]/[(n+3)(n+2)] for q=1. The lowest equation for q=1 forces a1​=0. For q=0, one may choose a1​=0 to select an even solution, independent of the odd q=1 solution. The leading coefficient a0​ is free in either chosen parity branch.
For a nonterminating branch the consecutive parity-term ratio tends to x2. Hence the ratio test gives convergence for ∣x∣<1, and radius of convergence one. Terminating branches are polynomials with infinite radius. For p=m≥0 an integer, choose q of the same parity as m: the recurrence stops once n+q=m, producing a degree-m Chebyshev polynomial. Normalization at 1 gives all nonzero coefficients through degree three:
T0​=1,T1​=x,T2​=2x2−1,T3​=4x3−3x.​
(48)
Set Y(θ)=y(cosθ). Direct differentiation gives Y′′=sin2θy′′−cosθy′, so the equation becomes Y′′+m2Y=0. For m>0, Y=Acos(mθ)+Bsin(mθ). A polynomial y has finite derivative at x=1, and therefore Y′(0)=−sin0y′(1)=0, forcing B=0. Also Y(0)=Tm​(1)=1, so A=1. For m=0, the transformed solution is affine in θ and the same derivative condition removes its linear term. Thus
Tm​(x)=cos(marccosx),−1≤x≤1,m=0,1,2,….​
(49)
The endpoint values follow by continuity. The finite-derivative condition is what excludes the independent nonpolynomial branch, rather than normalization alone.
