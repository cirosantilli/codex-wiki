A matrix is diagonalizable if some invertible S makes S−1AS diagonal, equivalently if it has a basis of eigenvectors. If Ap=0 and Av=λv for v=0, then 0=Apv=λpv, so every eigenvalue is zero. There is at least one zero eigenvalue: otherwise A and hence Ap would be invertible. A diagonalizable nilpotent matrix has an all-zero diagonal form and therefore is zero itself.
For the specified matrices, direct determinant expansion gives
det(λI−B)=λ3,det(λI−C)=λ(λ−6)(λ+6).​
(14)
The Cayley-Hamilton theorem gives B3=0. Its entry B12​=−2 is nonzero for every real α, so B is not diagonalizable for any α. The matrix C has three distinct real eigenvalues and is therefore diagonalizable over R. For an explicit check, take columns (−1,0,1)T, (2,−3,1)T, (2,3,1)T in S; then S−1CS=diag(−6,0,6).
