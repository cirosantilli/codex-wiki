For a Cartesian tensor under a proper orthogonal change R, the rank-n law is Ti1​⋯in​′​=Ri1​j1​​⋯Rin​jn​​Tj1​⋯jn​​, with repeated indices summed. An isotropic tensor has T′=T for every such rotation.
For rank two, T=RTRT. A half-turn about a coordinate axis, obtained by applying a quarter-turn twice, reverses each off-diagonal component with exactly one index on that axis. Invariance forces every off-diagonal entry to vanish. A quarter-turn about the third axis exchanges T11​ and T22​; quarter-turns about the other axes equate them with T33​. Thus the isotropic second-rank tensor is
Tij​=λδij​.​
(15)
Conversely R(λI)RT=λI, proving sufficiency.

For small oscillations with quadratic kinetic and potential energies, a normal mode has a fixed spatial amplitude pattern and one angular normal frequency. Its amplitude is a normal coordinate; choosing all modes diagonalizes the two quadratic forms and decouples their equations. A zero frequency describes a free symmetry motion.
Let qj​=R[θj​−jπ/2] be the small signed arc displacements, with cyclic indices j=0,1,2,3. The springs follow arcs, so their extensions are qj+1​−qj​. The Lagrangian of the normal modes of a four-bead spring ring is
L=2m​j=0∑3​q˙​j2​−2k​j=0∑3​(qj+1​−qj​)2.​
(20)
The stiffness matrix divided by k is the cycle graph Laplacian, with diagonal entries two, adjacent entries minus one, and zero opposite entries. An orthonormal basis of mode vectors and their frequencies is
u0​=21​(1,1,1,1),ω0​=0;u1​=2​1​(1,0,−1,0),u2​=2​1​(0,1,0,−1),ω1,2​=2k/m​;u3​=21​(1,−1,1,−1),ω3​=2k/m​.​
(21)
The normal mode coordinates are Qα​=uα​⋅q, with equations Q¨​α​+ωα2​Qα​=0. The zero mode rotates all beads together without stretching a spring and has Q0​=A+Bt. In each of the two degenerate middle modes, opposite beads have opposite signed arc displacements while the other pair remains stationary. Any fixed linear combination is another mode of that frequency. The last mode alternates the signed displacement of adjacent beads. The radius cancels from the frequencies because arc length supplies the same factor R2 to the angular kinetic and spring energies.

A group has a closed binary operation, associativity, an identity, and an inverse for each element. For a finite group, Lagrange's theorem states ∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣ for each subgroup H, so ∣H∣ divides ∣G∣.
The order of a group element g is the least positive n with gn=e, or infinity if none exists. In a finite group the powers form the cyclic subgroup ⟨g⟩ of that order; Lagrange's theorem proves ord(g)∣∣G∣. If ∣G∣=p is prime and g=e, its order must be p. Thus g generates the whole group, proving G≅Cp​.
If every nonidentity element has order two, every element is its own inverse. Then ab=(ab)−1=b−1a−1=ba. This proves that a group of exponent two is abelian.

i OurBigBook.com
In a group of order six, element orders divide six. If there were no element of order three or six, every nonidentity element would have order two, making the group abelian by the preceding argument. Choose a=e and b∈/{e,a}. Then {e,a,b,ab} is a subgroup of order four: its elements are distinct, it is closed because a,b commute and square to e, and each element is its own inverse. This contradicts Lagrange's theorem, since four does not divide six. Hence an element of order three or six exists.

Suppose x,y have order three but x=y,y2. Their cyclic subgroups have trivial intersection: any common nonidentity element would generate both subgroups and force x=y or y2. The nine products xiyj, 0≤i,j<3, are distinct. Indeed xiyj=xkyℓ implies xi−k=yℓ−j in that intersection, so i=k and j=ℓ modulo three. Nine distinct elements cannot fit in a group of order six, proving the claim without assuming commutativity.

A normal subgroup H satisfies gHg−1=H for every g∈G. A group homomorphism preserves multiplication, Φ(ab)=Φ(a)Φ(b). It follows that Φ(e1​)=e2​ and Φ(g−1)=Φ(g)−1.
The kernel of a group homomorphism K={g:Φ(g)=e2​} contains the identity and satisfies ab−1∈K for a,b∈K, so is a subgroup. For k∈K, Φ(gkg−1)=Φ(g)e2​Φ(g)−1=e2​. Thus gKg−1⊆K, and applying the same inclusion to g−1 gives equality. Hence the kernel is a normal subgroup.
The product f=∏i<j​(xi​−xj​) is a Vandermonde determinant up to a fixed overall sign. A transposition exchanging xp​,xq​ reverses their mutual factor. Every other factor involving one of these variables is paired with the corresponding factor involving the other; those paired products are unchanged. Therefore
f⟼−funder every transposition.​
(23)
If a permutation has decompositions into r and s transpositions, they act as (−1)rf and (−1)sf. This polynomial is nonzero, for example at pairwise distinct real inputs, so the signs must agree. Thus all decompositions have the same parity, and an even decomposition forces every decomposition to be even. This defines the sign of a permutation; its kernel is the alternating group, linking the parity argument to the normal-kernel result above.

An invariant subspace W satisfies D(g)W⊆W for every g∈G; applying the inverse gives equality. The representation is irreducible when the only invariant subspaces are 0 and the full space.
The inverse and conjugate transpose each reverse multiplication order, so their combination preserves it:
D2​(gh)=[D1​(h−1)D1​(g−1)]†=D1​(g−1)†D1​(h−1)†=D2​(g)D2​(h).
(24)
Its values are invertible, so this is the conjugate contragredient representation.
If W is invariant under D2​, take w∈W and v∈W⊥. For the standard Hermitian inner product,
⟨w,D1​(g)v⟩=⟨D1​(g)†w,v⟩=⟨D2​(g−1)w,v⟩=0,
(25)
since D2​(g−1)w∈W. Hence D1​(g)v∈W⊥, proving its invariance. If D1​ is irreducible, W⊥ is either zero or the whole space; finite-dimensional orthogonal complementation makes W the whole space or zero, respectively. Thus D2​ is irreducible whenever D1​ is irreducible.
