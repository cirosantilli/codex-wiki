For the hyperbolic parametrization of oblate spheroidal coordinates, the three coordinate tangent vectors are

A matrix is diagonalizable if some invertible S makes S−1AS diagonal, equivalently if it has a basis of eigenvectors. If Ap=0 and Av=λv for v=0, then 0=Apv=λpv, so every eigenvalue is zero. There is at least one zero eigenvalue: otherwise A and hence Ap would be invertible. A diagonalizable nilpotent matrix has an all-zero diagonal form and therefore is zero itself.
For the specified matrices, direct determinant expansion gives
det(λI−B)=λ3,det(λI−C)=λ(λ−6)(λ+6).​
(14)
The Cayley-Hamilton theorem gives B3=0. Its entry B12​=−2 is nonzero for every real α, so B is not diagonalizable for any α. The matrix C has three distinct real eigenvalues and is therefore diagonalizable over R. For an explicit check, take columns (−1,0,1)T, (2,−3,1)T, (2,3,1)T in S; then S−1CS=diag(−6,0,6).

For the normalized second-order equation y′′+P(x)y′+Q(x)y=0, an ordinary point has analytic P,Q. A regular singular point is a singular point where (x−x0​)P and (x−x0​)2Q extend analytically through x0​. The two finite singular points here are 0 and 1/2. At zero, the respective limiting coefficients are 1/2,0; at 1/2, they are −1,0. Thus both are regular singular points, with indicial roots 0,1/2 and 0,2, respectively.

For the zero initial data, the Green function of an undamped oscillator is G0​(x,s)=sin[k(x−s)]/k for 0≤s≤x, and zero for s>x. It is continuous at the source and its first derivative jumps by one. The resulting convolution is
y(x)=2∫0x​sin[k(x−s)]cos(ks)ds=xsinkx.​

Apply the Euler-Lagrange equation to the integrand of F−λG, namely py′2−qy2−λry2. With fixed zero endpoint values, it gives (py′)′+qy+λry=0. Multiplying by y and using integration by parts gives F=λG, so each stationary Rayleigh quotient F/G equals its eigenvalue.
In the half-line problem, p=x, q=−x, r=1, so the quotient is R[y]=∫0∞​x(y′2+y2)dx/∫0∞​y2dx. For y=e−βx, β>0, its numerator is (β2+1)/(4β2) and denominator 1/(2β). Hence R=(β+β−1)/2, minimized at β=1:
Rexponential​=1.​
(24)
For the compactly supported linear trial, A>0, the numerator is A2/2+A4/12 and denominator A3/3. Thus R=3/(2A)+A/4, minimized at A=6​, giving
Rlinear​=26​​≃1.225.​
(25)
The derivative jump at the cutoff is allowed in the quadratic-form trial space of the Rayleigh-Ritz method: the trial is continuous and has finite form energy.
The exponential ground state of a linearly weighted Sturm-Liouville problem proves which estimate is best. Vanishing endpoint terms give
∫0∞​x(y′2+y2)dx=∫0∞​y2dx+∫0∞​x(y′+y)2dx≥∫0∞​y2dx.
(26)
Equality occurs for y=Ce−x, which also satisfies the differential equation with λ=1. Thus the exponential trial gives the exact smallest eigenvalue, whereas the linear trial gives a larger upper bound. Finiteness at the singular endpoint supplies the regular branch with xyy′→0; all the displayed trials have the required vanishing boundary terms.
