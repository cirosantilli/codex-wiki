For a subgroup H of a finite group G, distinct left cosets are disjoint and partition G. Each coset gH has exactly ∣H∣ elements because h↦gh is a bijection. Counting the partition proves Lagrange's theorem:
∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣.​
(28)
A cyclic group is generated by a single element. In a group of order four, an element of order four generates the whole group, giving C4​. Otherwise all three nonidentity elements have order two. Choose two of them a,b; cancellation makes ab=c the third. Since c−1=c, (ab)−1=b−1a−1=ba=ab. The remaining products then give the Klein four-group. This proves that there are precisely two groups of order four up to isomorphism, distinguished by whether an element of order four exists.
For the square, take r to be counterclockwise rotation by 90∘ and s reflection in the horizontal axis. They satisfy r4=s2=e and sr=r−1s. The dihedral group here is D4​ of order eight, with elements e,r,r2,r3,s,rs,r2s,r3s. Both generators are necessary: one generator would give a cyclic, hence abelian, group, while r and s do not commute. In the following Cayley table, the row acts after the column; the formula (rasb)(rcsd)=ra+(−1)bcsb+d, with exponents reduced modulo four and two, computes every entry.

The largest cyclic subgroup is ⟨r⟩={e,r,r2,r3}, of order four. Every reflection has order two and there is no element of order eight. The other subgroups of order four are
H0​={e,r2,s,r2s},H1​={e,r2,rs,r3s}.​
(30)
Both are Klein four-groups, generated by the commuting involutions r2 and one reflection. For completeness, any order-four subgroup containing r or r3 equals ⟨r⟩. Otherwise it contains a reflection f; the product of two distinct reflections is a nonidentity rotation, which must be r2 if odd powers of r are excluded. This forces the subgroup to be {e,r2,f,r2f}, giving exactly the two displayed choices. It is the square case of the classification of subgroups of a dihedral group.
