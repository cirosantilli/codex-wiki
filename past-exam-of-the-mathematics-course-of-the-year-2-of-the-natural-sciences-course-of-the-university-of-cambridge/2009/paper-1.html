Use separation of variables u=X(x)T(t). The fixed-end Dirichlet boundary conditions select Xn​=sin(nx), n≥1, and each mode satisfies Tn′′​+2hTn′​+n2Tn​=0. In the physical resistance range 0≤h<1, all normal modes are underdamped, with ωn​=n2−h2​>0. The modal factor normalized by Tn​(0)=1, Tn′​(0)=0 is
Tn​(t)=e−ht[cos(ωn​t)+ωn​h​sin(ωn​t)].
(6)
Consequently the linearly damped string has solution
u(x,t)=n=1∑∞​bn​sin(nx)e−ht[cos(ωn​t)+ωn​h​sin(ωn​t)],bn​=π2​∫0π​f(x)sin(nx)dx.​
(7)
The coefficients are those of the initial Fourier sine series. Differentiating each normalized modal factor at zero confirms that the string starts from rest. Suitable initial data give convergence in the string's energy space; the piecewise linear profile here need not give a twice differentiable solution along every characteristic from its corner.
For the specified tent profile, integrating its two linear pieces by parts gives
∫0π​f(x)sin(nx)dx=n22​sin2nπ​.
(8)
For example the first piece gives −πcos(nπ/2)/(2n)+sin(nπ/2)/n2, and the second gives the opposite cosine term and the same sine term. Hence
bn​=πn24​sin2nπ​,b2m​=0,b2m+1​=π(2m+1)24(−1)m​.​

The homogeneous Cauchy-Euler equation has solutions x and x−1. Choose yL​=x, which vanishes at zero, and yR​=x−x−1, which vanishes at one. Their Wronskian is W(s)=yL​(s)yR′​(s)−yL′​(s)yR​(s)=2/s. For a Green function integrated against the unweighted measure ds, continuity at x=s and the jump condition s2[Gx​]s−s+​=1 give
G(x,s)=⎩⎨⎧​2x​(1−s−2),21​(x−x−1),​0<x≤s<1,0<s≤x<1.​​
(10)
It satisfies Lx​G=δ(x−s) for L=x2∂x2​+x∂x​−1. Therefore the candidate solution is
u(x)=2x−x−1​∫0x​f(s)ds+2x​∫x1​(1−s−2)f(s)ds.​
(11)
The leading coefficient vanishes at zero, so the endpoint condition needs care. If f extends continuously to [0,1], this expression has limit −f(0) as x↓0. Indeed x−1∫0x​f(s)ds→f(0) and x∫x1​f(s)s−2ds→f(0), while the remaining terms vanish. Thus the continuous zero-endpoint problem in this class requires f(0)=0. With that compatibility condition the formula gives both boundary values and is unique: a difference of two bounded solutions is Ax+B/x, whose two endpoint conditions force both constants to vanish. For incompatible forcing such as f≡1, the bounded solution with u(1)=0 is x−1, which cannot also vanish at zero. Pointwise vanishing of G(x,s) for fixed s does not justify interchanging the singular endpoint limit with its integral. This issue is treated in Green kernel and endpoint compatibility for a singular Euler equation.
For the supplied compatible forcing f(s)=s, the integrals are x2/2 and (1−x2)/2+logx. Their polynomial pieces cancel, giving
u(x)=2xlogx​.​
(12)
Direct differentiation gives Lu=x, and xlogx→0 at zero. Its derivative diverges there, which is allowed because the boundary condition requires the value, not a finite derivative.

The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices supplies an orthonormal basis of real eigenvectors. Put these in the columns of Q, so QTQ=I and QTAQ=diag(λ1​,…,λn​). In the question's coordinate convention choose O=QT and y=Ox. Since x=OTy,
xTAx=yTOAOTy=∑i=1n​λi​yi2​.
(16)
Reorder the eigenvalues with m positive, ℓ−m negative and n−ℓ zero. For nonzero eigenvalues set zi​=∣λi​∣​yi​, and retain the remaining coordinates as zi​=yi​. This is an invertible real transformation z=DOx, where D is the diagonal rescaling; the combined matrix L=DO gives z=Lx. It gives
xTAx=∑i=1m​zi2​−∑i=m+1ℓ​zi2​,
(17)
with the zero coordinates absent. The general allowed range is 0≤m≤ℓ≤n. The printed restriction m≥1 is not valid for every real symmetric matrix: a negative-definite or zero form has no positive square. The invariance of these counts is Sylvester's law of inertia.
For the specified form, a particularly simple change is
z=Lx,L=​1/21/20​1/2−1/21​1/2−1/2−1​​,x1​x2​+x1​x3​=z12​−z22​.​
(18)
Subtracting the first two squared coordinates verifies the identity directly, and detL=1 proves invertibility. Thus m=1, ℓ=2 and there is one zero direction. The final rescaling need not remain orthogonal.

A square complex matrix U is a unitary matrix when U†U=I, so it preserves the Euclidean norm. If Uv=μv with v=0, then ∥v∥=∥Uv∥=∣μ∣∥v∥, giving ∣μ∣=1. For a Hermitian matrix A=A† and Aej​=λj​ej​,
λj​=ej†​ej​ej†​Aej​​∈R,
(19)
because the numerator equals its complex conjugate. Hence no eigenvalue of A+iI is zero, proving it is invertible.
For V=(A−iI)(A+iI)−1, the Hermitian conjugate is V†=(A−iI)−1(A+iI). The factors and their inverses commute, since they are rational functions of A. Thus V†V=I. Its action on the same eigenvectors is
Vej​=λj​+iλj​−i​ej​.​
(20)
These eigenvalues have modulus one. This is the Cayley transform of a Hermitian matrix in the convention that avoids the unitary eigenvalue one.
For the inverse direction, U†=U−1 gives
(I−U−1)−1=−(I−U)−1U,I+U−1=U−1(I+U).
(21)
Taking the conjugate transpose of B=i(I+U)(I−U)−1 therefore yields
B†=−i(I−U−1)−1(I+U−1)=i(I−U)−1(I+U)=B.
(22)
The last equality uses commutativity of the two factors. This proves that the inverse Cayley transform of a unitary matrix without eigenvalue one is Hermitian. Solving for U gives U=(B−iI)(B+iI)−1. An orthonormal eigenbasis of B therefore diagonalizes U, with diagonal entries (bj​−i)/(bj​+i).
The result extends even when a unitary matrix has eigenvalue one: choose ∣α∣=1 so that αU does not. There are only finitely many forbidden phases, since the matrix has finitely many eigenvalues. Diagonalizing αU also diagonalizes U in the same basis. Thus every unitary matrix is unitarily diagonalizable.

For z=reiθ=0, fix one argument θ. Every value of the complex logarithm is logr+i(θ+2πk), k∈Z. By complex exponentiation, zz=exp[z(logr+i(θ+2πk))]. Taking the real part of its exponent gives all possible moduli:
∣zz∣=exp[rcosθlogr−rsinθ(θ+2πk)],k∈Z.​
(27)
Equivalently these are rReze−Imz(θ+2πk). If z is real and nonzero the modulus is the single value rRez; otherwise the values form a geometric progression with ratio e−2πImz. Changing the initially chosen argument just reindexes the same set. The logarithmic definition excludes z=0. This is the modulus of a complex self-power.

For a nonzero solution on a branch where the real power is differentiable, set v=u1−α. Multiplying the Bernoulli differential equation by (1−α)u−α gives the linear equation
v′+(1−α)f(x)v=(1−α)g(x).
(28)
It can be solved by an integrating factor. For the specified cubic equation take α=3, f=g=x, so v=u−2 satisfies v′−2xv=−2x. Its integrating factor is e−x2, giving (ve−x2)′=−2xe−x2 and hence v=1+Cex2. Thus all nonzero real solutions are
u(x)=±1+Cex2​1​,​
(29)
on connected intervals where the denominator is positive, with a fixed sign on each interval. Also u≡0 solves the original equation and must be restored because the substitution excluded it. The choices C=0 give the constant solutions u=±1. Smoothness of the original right-hand side gives uniqueness, so a nonzero solution cannot cross through the zero solution at a finite regular point.

Introduce a Lagrange multiplier for the constraint G=1 and vary F−λG with y↦y+ϵη, where η(a)=η(b)=0. The first variation is
δ(F−λG)=2∫ab​[py′η′+(q−λw)yη]dx=2∫ab​[−(py′)′+qy−λwy]ηdx.
(32)
The fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations gives the Sturm-Liouville equation −(py′)′+qy=λwy. Multiplying by y, integrating by parts and using the endpoint conditions gives F=λG. Therefore the normalization implies λ=F. Assume the coefficient and function regularity needed for these variations and integrations; the condition w>0 makes the constraint derivative nonzero at a normalized function.
For the radial problem, set σ=rψ. A direct differentiation gives σ′′=rψ′′+2ψ′, so
−σ′′=Eσ,σ(0)=σ(1)=0.
(33)
Finite ψ(0) supplies the first boundary value. The Rayleigh quotient is R[σ]=∫01​σ′2dr/∫01​σ2dr. For the two trials, σ1​=r(1−r) and σ2​=r(1−r2), the numerator-denominator pairs are (1/3,1/30) and (4/5,8/105), respectively. Hence
E1​=10,E2​=221​.​
(34)
The exact eigenvalues are n2π2, so Emin​=π2≃9.86960. Both trials give upper bounds by the Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle, and the first estimate is more accurate, since it is smaller. Trial admissibility uses the Dirichlet energy form for σ; the first trial's nonzero radial derivative at the origin does not invalidate this variational calculation.
To improve, add independent trial functions and minimize the quotient over their span. Here merely combining the two supplied trials gives no improvement: their two-dimensional stiffness and mass matrices are
K=(1/31/2​1/24/5​),M=(1/301/20​1/208/105​),det(K−EM)=25200(E−10)(E−42)​.
(35)
The lowest Ritz value is still ten. Instead add the symmetric quartic τ=r2(1−r)2. On the span of σ1​,τ, the generalized eigenvalue problem has
K=(1/31/15​1/152/105​),M=(1/301/140​1/1401/630​).
(36)
Its determinant is (E2−112E+1008)/529200, so the least root gives
Eimproved​=56−4133​≃9.86974962.​
(37)
This is strictly better than both original estimates. The symmetry obstruction in a polynomial Rayleigh-Ritz trial space explains why the cubic addition failed while the quartic helps. It is also the shifted-interval version of the polynomial Rayleigh-Ritz trial space for a one-dimensional box.

At a regular constrained extremum, the method of Lagrange multipliers makes I+λJ stationary for a constant λ. For an endpoint-vanishing variation η, integration by parts gives
δ(I+λJ)=∫x1​x2​​[fy​+λgy​−dxd​(fy′​+λgy′​)]ηdx.
(38)
The fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations therefore yields
dxd​(fy′​+λgy′​)−(fy​+λgy​)=0.​
(39)
This Euler-Lagrange equation must be supplemented by the two endpoint conditions and the prescribed integral constraint, which determine the constants and multiplier. Existence of the normal multiplier assumes that the first variation of J is not identically zero. If that regularity fails, the general multiplier equation uses αI+λJ, with (α,λ)=(0,0); one cannot automatically set α=1.

Write the speed as v=x˙2+y˙​2​>0 and take
L=21​(xy˙​−yx˙)+λv.
(40)
The two Euler-Lagrange equations are
y˙​−λdtd​(vx˙​)=0,−x˙−λdtd​(vy˙​​)=0.​
(41)
For example Lx​=y˙​/2 and Lx˙​=−y/2+λx˙/v, yielding the first equation. Integrating once gives constants c1​,c2​ with λx˙/v=y+c1​ and λy˙​/v=−x+c2​. The case λ=0 would force both velocities to vanish, contrary to a regular curve. Squaring and adding therefore gives
(x−c2​)2+(y+c1​)2=λ2.​
(42)
Thus every regular stationary curve is a circle, whose center is set by the integration constants. A simple closed traversal of length L has radius L/(2π) and positive enclosed area L2/(4π). The planar isoperimetric inequality proves that this stationary candidate is the global maximum; the necessary variational equations alone would not prove that inequality. The stationary curves of the planar isoperimetric problem give this argument independently of the chosen parametrization.
