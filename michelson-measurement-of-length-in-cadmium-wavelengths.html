Michelson measurement of length in cadmium wavelengths

 ... Optics Physical optics Optical coherence First-order optical coherence Temporal optical coherence Michelson interferometer
OurBigBook.com Words: 253
Albert Abraham Michelson's 1907 lecture, Recent Advances in Spectroscopy, pp. 171–172, connects counting interference fringes with a reproducible length standard. Broad or multiple spectral lines obscured fringes at large path differences; red cadmium emission supplied a suitable source. His 1907 Nobel Prize in Physics recognized optical precision instruments and their spectroscopy and metrology applications.[ref]
His authored account of the metre comparison at Sèvres describes a heated, evacuated cadmium discharge tube and a spectrometer selecting one spectral line. A movable reference mirror was compared with pairs of silvered parallel surfaces defining intermediate standards. Counted interference fringes established a short interval; repeated comparisons extended it to a decimetre and then a metre. White-light fringes located surface coincidence, while monochromatic fringes supplied counts and fractional orders. A thermally shielding box, firm pier, calibrated translation screw and tested thermometers controlled drafts, vibration, travel and temperature. Uniform temperature mattered in comparisons; the final standard required an absolute temperature reading. Optical microscopes made the final comparison to the marked metre bar, limiting the whole result.[ref]
This is a measurement chain, rather than one enormous unchecked fringe count: short intervals establish whole orders; fractional-order readings refine each longer comparison. The optical coherence envelope helps locate white-light coincidence, whereas the carrier phase difference measures small departures from it. Repetition checks counting mistakes, but agreement alone cannot exclude shared systematic errors. The general lesson of metrology is to preserve the link from the optical ruler through each transfer and control to the final length.
