In a source-free vacuum, the Maxwell equations in SI are
∇⋅E=0,∇⋅B=0,∇×E=−∂t∂B​,∇×B=μ0​ϵ0​∂t∂E​,
(1)
where ϵ0​ and μ0​ are the vacuum permittivity and vacuum permeability. Take the curl of the last equation, substitute Faraday's law of induction, and use the curl of the curl identity:
∇×(∇×B)=∇(∇⋅B)−∇2B=−μ0​ϵ0​∂t2∂2B​.
(2)
The zero divergence therefore gives the wave equation
∇2B=c21​∂t2∂2B​,c=μ0​ϵ0​​1​.​
(3)
For a propagating plane wave, write B=Re[B0​ei(k⋅r−ωt)]. The wave equation gives ω2=c2∣k∣2, and Gauss's law for magnetism gives k⋅B0​=0. Thus the oscillating magnetic field is a transverse wave: it is perpendicular to the propagation wavevector. A static background magnetic field, if present, is separate from this propagating component.

Consequently a spherical Gaussian surface inside the metal encloses zero net electric charge, so the inner surface carries −q. For the uniform space charge in a grounded spherical cavity, grounding sets the metal's electric potential to zero. With no external sources and zero electric potential at infinity, the exterior electric potential is also zero by the uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential. The original shell electric charge is no longer fixed: electric charge can flow to or from earth.
Inside the cavity, Gauss's law gives E=ρrr^/(3ϵ0​). Integrating the electric field inward from the zero boundary electric potential gives
V(r)=∫rr1​​3ϵ0​ρs​ds=6ϵ0​ρ(r12​−r2)​,V(0)=6ϵ0​ρr12​​.​
(6)
The inner surface carries −Qρ​, where Qρ​=4πρr13​/3, and the outer surface carries zero net electric charge. Only the cavity contributes to the final electrostatic energy:
U=2ϵ0​​∫∣E∣2d3r=2ϵ0​​4π∫0r1​​(3ϵ0​ρr​)2r2dr,U=45ϵ0​2πρ2r15​​=40πϵ0​r1​Qρ2​​.​

Write the uniform surface charge density on the annulus as σ=Q/[π(b2−a2)], and let x be signed distance along its axis. A ring of radius r and width dr contains electric charge dq=2πσrdr. Its transverse electric field components cancel in pairs, and its electric potential is dV=dq/[4πϵ0​x2+r2​]. Setting V(∞)=0 and integrating gives the axial field of a uniformly charged annulus:
V(x)=2ϵ0​σ​(x2+b2​−x2+a2​),​
(20)
E(x)=−dxdV​x^=2ϵ0​σx​(x2+a2​1​−x2+b2​1​)x^.​
(21)
At x=0, each element's electric field cancels that of the diametrically opposite element, so E=0. There is no surface charge at the centre because a>0; the axis electric field is continuous there.
For positive Q and x>0, differentiate the signed axial electric field:
dxdEx​​=2ϵ0​σ​[(x2+a2)3/2a2​−(x2+b2)3/2b2​].
(22)
Its stationary condition is a4/3(x2+b2)=b4/3(x2+a2). Factoring with A=a2/3 and B=b2/3 gives
xmax2​=a2/3+b2/3a4/3b4/3​,xmax​=a2/3+b2/3​(ab)2/3​.​
(23)
The derivative starts positive, has exactly one positive zero and is negative at large x, so this is the positive-side maximum. For either sign of Q, the electric field magnitude has equal maxima at x=±xmax​. As b→a, the thin-ring limit is xmax​→a/2​.

For a steady filamentary electric current I, the Biot-Savart law is
B(r)=4πμ0​​∫∣r−r′∣3Idℓ′×(r−r′)​.​

Here r is the observation position, r′ the source position, dℓ′ points in the direction of positive electric current, and μ0​ is the vacuum permeability. A circular loop has centre magnetic field μ0​I/(2r): the cross product has magnitude rdℓ′ and a constant axial direction, and ∫dℓ′=2πr.

The method of images replaces a conductor's induced surface charge by suitable fictitious image charges located outside the physical region in which the electric field is sought. Choose them so that the combined electric potential satisfies the specified conductor boundary values, has the required behaviour at infinity, and has the correct physical sources. The uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential then identifies this constructed electric potential with the physical solution in that region.
For an infinite grounded conducting plane at z=0, a point charge q at (x,y,z) with z>0 has an image charge −q at (x,y,−z). Distances to the pair are equal on the plane, so their electric potentials cancel there. This construction determines the electric field above the plane; below it the actual conductor has constant electric potential and zero electric field, rather than the fictitious image's continuation.

Use the electric dipole above a grounded conducting plane approximation, valid for d≪a. Let z^ point upward, and choose p=p(sinθx^+cosθz^). Reflecting the two constituent point charges and reversing their signs produces an image electric dipole moment
p′=p(−sinθx^+cosθz^)
(32)
at height −a. Thus the parallel electric dipole moment reverses and the normal one is unchanged. At the real electric dipole, the separation from its image is 2az^, giving the image electric field
Eim​=4πϵ0​(2a)31​[3(p′⋅z^)z^−p′]=32πϵ0​a3p​(sinθx^+2cosθz^).
(33)
The physical torque is τ=p×Eim​, so
τ=−32πϵ0​a3p2sinθcosθ​y^​=−64πϵ0​a3p2sin2θ​y^​.​
(34)
For 0<θ<π/2, its direction reduces θ, tending to align the electric dipole with the upward normal. In general it favours either normal orientation; a perfectly parallel orientation has zero torque but is unstable to tilting.
The conductor-response potential energy includes one half of the real-image interaction:
U=−21​p⋅Eim​=−64πϵ0​a3p2(1+cos2θ)​.
(35)
This factor accounts for the induced surface charge changing as the physical source is assembled. Differentiation with respect to height at fixed orientation gives
F=−∂a∂U​z^=−64πϵ0​a43p2(1+cos2θ)​z^.​
(36)
There is no horizontal force, since translating the entire arrangement parallel to the plane leaves U unchanged. For example, the normal and parallel orientations give respective downward force magnitudes 3p2/(32πϵ0​a4) and 3p2/(64πϵ0​a4).
A single physical point charge induces total plane electric charge −q. One can verify this by integrating its induced surface charge density, −qh/[2π(ϱ2+h2)3/2], over the plane. The two physical electric charges therefore induce opposite totals, giving
Qplane​=−(+q)−(−q)=0.​
(37)
The local surface charge density is generally nonzero. The zero total holds for the finite neutral pair as well as its ideal electric dipole limit.

Fermat's principle states that the travel time, or equivalently the optical path length ∫nds in a stationary medium, is stationary under small variations of the path with endpoints held fixed. It need not be a global minimum.
Let a light ray join a point at height h1​ above a plane interface to a point at depth h2​ below it, with horizontal separation D. If the crossing point is horizontally ξ from the first endpoint, its optical path length is
L(ξ)=n1​h12​+ξ2​+n2​h22​+(D−ξ)2​.
(38)
Setting its derivative to zero gives
h12​+ξ2​n1​ξ​=h22​+(D−ξ)2​n2​(D−ξ)​.
(39)
The two geometric ratios are the sines of the incident and refracted angles, measured from the interface normal vector. Hence Snell's law is
n1​sinθ1​=n2​sinθ2​.​
(40)

The paraxial approximation restricts light rays to small slopes relative to the optical axis, so sinβ≃tanβ≃β and geometric lengths can be expanded to second order in height. Treat the source's optical lens as a thin lens located at x=0, with ∣y∣≪u,∣v∣ and small surface slopes.
For positive real conjugate distances u,v, the collapsed-plane geometric path has length
S(y)=u2+y2​+v2+y2​≃u+v+2y2​(u1​+v1​).
(41)
To thin-element accuracy, a segment s(y)=s0​−αy2 of this path lies inside the optical lens, replacing the same length of air. The geometric air path is S−s, the material path is s, and the optical path length and travel time are therefore
L(y)=S(y)+(n−1)s(y),ct(y)≃u+v+(n−1)s0​+y2[2u1​+2v1​−(n−1)α].
(42)
This distinguishes geometric length from optical path length; adding ns to S without subtracting the replaced air would count that segment twice.
For a nonaxial light ray, Fermat's principle gives dL/dy=0 and hence
u1​+v1​=2(n−1)α.
(43)
The focal length is obtained with the incident object at infinity:
f1​=2(n−1)α,f=2(n−1)α1​.​
(44)
The constant thickness s0​ supplies a common delay but does not change the leading focal length. Since α has units of inverse length, this formula has the correct dimensions. These are thin lens results; paraxial approximation alone does not remove finite-thickness corrections in a general thick lens.
