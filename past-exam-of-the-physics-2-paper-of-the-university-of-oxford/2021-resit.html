The real image is upside down. A converging optical lens produces negative transverse magnification on the screen: a point above the optical axis sends a central light ray through the optical lens centre to a point below the optical axis. The same happens in the horizontal direction. Viewed directly from behind, the screen therefore shows a picture rotated through 180∘ relative to the scene.

The image is upright vertically when viewed down from behind, but left and right are interchanged. To track the image formation, let +x point forwards and +z upwards. Incoming light travels along −x. The 45∘ flip plane mirror sends it along +z. Before this reflection, the optical lens has inverted the scene's top into the −z direction; the law of reflection maps that direction into +x on the horizontal screen. The screen's far edge is the top of the picture for someone looking down from behind. The horizontal inversion from the optical lens remains, since this plane mirror does not change the lateral coordinate.

c OurBigBook.com
The minimum is two additional plane mirrors, making three including the flip plane mirror. Their reflecting planes can meet at 45∘ in the vertical section, as in a pentaprism. For example, in the xz section take the first added reflecting plane at 67.5∘ to +x and the second at 112.5∘. The upward beam first travels diagonally forwards and upwards, then horizontally backwards towards an eye looking forwards. The two reflections together rotate directions through 90∘: the propagation direction +z becomes −x, while the screen's top direction +x becomes +z at the eye. Thus the picture remains upright vertically.
One additional plane mirror would also send +z into −x, but it would send the top direction +x into −z, making the image upside down. This proves minimality for ordinary plane mirrors. The three-reflection arrangement still reverses left and right; correcting that additionally requires a roof arrangement or another suitable image-erecting system. Vertical erection alone is what is asked here.

Use a converging thin lens as a magnifying glass, with the screen at or just inside its front focal plane. It presents an upright virtual image at infinity or at the eye's near point, respectively. Let the near point distance be D; the conventional numerical assumption is D=250mm, which is not specified in the paper.
Under the usual approximation that the eye is close to the eyepiece, interpreting 50mm as the focal length f gives the familiar angular magnifications
M∞​=fD​=5,Mnear​=1+fD​=6(f=50mm, D=250mm).​
(8)
The first is for a relaxed eye with the screen at the front focal plane. For the second, the virtual image is at signed distance v=−D and the thin lens equation gives screen distance u=fD/(D+f)=41.7mm. Thus the often intended maximum 6 requires a screen-to-eye distance of approximately 41.7mm, rather than exactly 50mm.
If 50mm instead fixes the physical screen-to-eye separation s, an eye adjacent to the optical lens sees the angular magnification
M=sD​=5(s=50mm, D=250mm).​
(9)
This is achieved by f=s for relaxed viewing, or by f=sD/(D−s)=62.5mm for a virtual image at the near point. The latter follows from 1/f=1/s−1/D.
More generally, the source does not specify the eyepiece-to-eye distance either. If that distance is e, then u=s−e and a virtual image at the near point has transverse magnification (D−e)/u. Its angular magnification relative to unaided viewing at D is
M=s−eD−e​.
(10)
For example, s=50mm and e=25mm give M=9, with u=25mm and f=28.125mm. This is a concrete counterexample to a universal maximum of 5 at fixed screen-to-eye separation. In ideal geometrical optics, e→s− allows arbitrarily large M with an arbitrarily short focal length; practical aperture and optical aberrations constrain this. A unique numerical maximum therefore needs the conventional near-eye eyepiece geometry and near point assumption. The conventional f=50mm answer is 6, with those assumptions stated explicitly.

Let U>0 be the drop in electric potential along the positive electric charge's path. By conservation of energy, qU=mv2/2, so the nonrelativistic exit speed is v=2qU/m​. Apply a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to the incident velocity and to the desired orbital plane. The magnetic Lorentz force is perpendicular to the velocity, does no work, and supplies the centripetal force:
qvB=Rmv2​,R=qBmv​=B1​q/m2U​​.​
(11)
For example, v=vx and B=Bz give initial deflection along −y for positive q. Reversing the magnetic field reverses the bending. A uniform electric field alone would give constant acceleration and a parabolic path, not uniform circular motion. The displayed result assumes qU≪mc2; the exact relativistic momentum relation would give R=q2U2/c2+2mqU​/(qB).

In the parallel LC circuit both components have the same voltage phasor. Adding their current phasors by Kirchhoff's current law gives the total electrical admittance:
Z1​=jωL1​+jωC=j(ωC−ωL1​).

In SI units, the Maxwell equations in vacuum with prescribed charge density ρ and current density J are
∇⋅E=ϵ0​ρ​,∇⋅B=0,∇×E=−∂t​B,∇×B=μ0​J+μ0​ϵ0​∂t​E.
(17)
When ρ=0 and J=0, take the curl of the third equation and substitute the fourth. Using the curl of the curl identity and ∇⋅E=0 gives the electromagnetic wave equation:
∇×(∇×E)=−∇2E=−μ0​ϵ0​∂t2​E,∇2E−c21​∂t2​E=0,c=(μ0​ϵ0​)−1/2.​
(18)
For example, E=E0​xcos(kz−ωt), B=(E0​/c)y​cos(kz−ωt) is a plane electromagnetic wave provided ω=ck. The electric field, magnetic field and propagation direction are mutually perpendicular.
The Maxwell equations are linear, so the superposition principle applies to fields. However, the Poynting vector is quadratic:
⟨S⟩=μ0​1​⟨E1​×B1​+E2​×B2​+E1​×B2​+E2​×B1​⟩.
(19)
One may add the average wave intensities only when the cross terms vanish under the actual detector average. For co-propagating plane electromagnetic waves of parallel linear polarization, averaging over optical cycles but potentially resolving the difference frequency gives
I(t)=I1​+I2​+2I1​I2​​cos[(ω1​−ω2​)t+ϕ].
(20)
If the detector integrates for T, the last term acquires sinc[(ω1​−ω2​)T/2], with sinc function convention sincx=sinx/x, evaluated at the midpoint phase. Thus T≫1/∣f1​−f2​∣ suppresses beats, and the infinite-time average adds the wave intensities exactly for distinct fixed frequencies. Random mutually uncorrelated wave phases also remove the cross terms in an ensemble average; orthogonal linear polarizations remove them for co-propagating waves in polarization-insensitive detection. Merely having different frequencies does not remove a resolved beat signal: two nearly equal frequencies with fixed relative wave phase are a concrete counterexample. For different propagation directions, the relevant addition is of average Poynting vectors, or their components through the chosen detector surface.

Use the principle of dark-field microscopy: block the unscattered beam where it is concentrated, while transmitting most light scattering at nonzero angles. All direct light rays of the collimated illumination are parallel, so the thin lens focuses them to one point in its back focal plane. Put a small opaque circular stop centred on that point, one focal length behind the optical lens. In the real image geometry above this is 11.719mm behind the optical lens, well before the real image at 187.5mm.
A light ray leaving object coordinate y with a small initial direction θ reaches the optical lens at y+uθ. The thin lens changes its direction to θ−(y+uθ)/f. At the back focal plane its coordinate is therefore
yf​=y+uθ+f(θ−fy+uθ​)=fθ.
(26)
Thus direct illumination with θ=0 is concentrated at the centre, whereas scattered light at different directions is distributed away from it, independently of y. A stop of radius rs​ removes approximately ∣θ∣≲rs​/f; choose it just large enough to cover the direct beam's finite diffraction spot, illumination divergence and alignment errors. Scattered light inside that small angular cone is also lost, so negligible loss requires most useful scattered light to lie outside it. A stop in the object's real image plane instead removes object detail and does not generally separate scattered and direct light. For the virtual image interpretation, the same physical back focal plane filtering requires a downstream observation arrangement that actually passes through this plane.

The Huygens-Fresnel principle treats every point of an advancing wavefront as a source of secondary spherical waves; their forward envelope constructs the next wavefront. Consider two points where an oblique plane wavefront meets a plane interface a distance AB apart. After its first point reaches A, let time t elapse until the second reaches B. In medium 1, the incident travel distance is v1​t=ABsini. Meanwhile, a secondary wave from A has travelled distance v2​t in medium 2. The tangent from B to that secondary wavefront gives v2​t=ABsinr. Therefore
sinrsini​=v2​v1​​=n1​n2​​,n1​sini=n2​sinr.​
(45)
Here i,r are the angle of incidence and angle of refraction, measured from the interface normal vector, and nj​=c/vj​ are the refractive indices at the common frequency. This is Snell's law.

A simple converging optical lens bends different wavelengths through different angles because of optical dispersion. For a fixed shape in air, the lensmaker's equation gives 1/f∝n(λ)−1: the larger short-wavelength refractive index gives a shorter focal length. Different colours therefore form sharp images at different axial positions, causing longitudinal chromatic aberration, blur and coloured fringes in a white-light image. Off the optical axis, colour-dependent transverse magnification can also give lateral chromatic aberration. An achromatic doublet combines suitable optical glasses and curvatures to reduce this wavelength dependence.

B(x)=2Lμ0​NI​[a2+x2​x​+a2+(L−x)2​L−x​].
(56)
The source's acute endpoint angles satisfy cosα=x/a2+x2​ and cosβ=(L−x)/a2+(L−x)2​. Hence
B=2Lμ0​NI​(cosα+cosβ).​
(57)
Although the paper calls this a “flux”, B is magnetic flux density, measured in teslas. Magnetic flux is an surface integral ΦB​=∫B⋅dA, measured in webers, and cannot be assigned to a point. For example, two differently sized small disks at the same point have the same limiting B but different magnetic fluxes, approximately B times their normal areas. The displayed plus sign uses the internal-point angle convention shown in the source.
