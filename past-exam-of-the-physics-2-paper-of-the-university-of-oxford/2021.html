A large electrical conductor between the point charges provides electrostatic shielding: one must not retain their original mutual Coulomb force through an ideal infinite plate. In the usual broad-plate approximation, let σ denote the unperturbed surface charge density on each face. The electrostatic boundary condition gives an outward electric field E0​=σ/ϵ0​. Neglecting local electrostatic induction, the rod's tension then satisfies mgtanϕ=QE0​, giving
ϕ≃tan−1(ϵ0​mgQσ​)=1.98∘.​
(3)
There are two physical qualifications. First, if σ means the total charge per projected plate area, shared equally between the two faces, then E0​=σ/(2ϵ0​) and the same approximation gives ϕ≃0.989∘. The factor of two is a convention about the specified surface charge density, not a different Gauss's law.
Second, a point charge near an electrical conductor makes its surface charge density nonuniform. For an infinite plane with prescribed background electric field E0​, the method of images adds an attractive force kQ2/(4x2), where x=lsinϕ. The more accurate equilibrium equation is
mgtanϕ=QE0​−16πϵ0​l2sin2ϕQ2​.
(4)
Its larger, stable root is approximately 1.96∘ for the per-face convention, or 0.877∘ for the total-area convention. This ideal-plane refinement is not an exact answer for an unspecified finite plate: its dimensions and the meaning of the quoted surface charge density would be needed. In particular, adding a uniform-sheet electric field to the original mutual repulsion would describe a different, nonshielding model.

In vacuum, Maxwell's equations in differential form are
∇⋅E=ϵ0​ρ​,∇⋅B=0,∇×E=−∂t∂B​,∇×B=μ0​J+μ0​ϵ0​∂t∂E​.
(10)
Here ρ is charge density, J is electric current density, E is electric field, and B is magnetic flux density. In a source-free region, taking the curl of the third equation and using ∇×(∇×E)=∇(∇⋅E)−∇2E gives the wave equation
∇2E=μ0​ϵ0​∂t2∂2E​.
(11)
The same argument gives the wave equation for B. A concrete family satisfying all four Maxwell's equations is
E=xF(z−ct),B=cy​​F(z−ct),c=μ0​ϵ0​​1​.​
(12)
The function F may be any twice differentiable profile. These are transverse travelling waves; reverse the magnetic field direction for propagation in the opposite direction. Because the source-free Maxwell's equations are homogeneous linear differential equations, any finite linear combination of such paired electric fields and magnetic fields is again a solution. Infinite superpositions also work whenever the required derivatives converge. A solution of the component wave equations alone must still satisfy the divergence and mutual-curl constraints.

Use the following arrangement: an inverting operational amplifier with input resistor Ra​ and feedback resistor Rf​, followed by an op amp integrator with input resistor Ri​ and feedback capacitor Ci​. Ground both non-inverting inputs. Each ideal operational amplifier has infinite input impedance, so its inputs draw no electric current; negative feedback makes the inverting input a virtual ground.
Kirchhoff's current law at the first summing junction gives V1​=−(Rf​/Ra​)Vin​. At the second summing junction it gives V1​/Ri​+Ci​dVout​/dt=0. Consequently the two inversions cancel:
Vout​(t)=Vout​(t0​)+Ra​Ri​Ci​Rf​​∫t0​t​Vin​(t′)dt′.​
(20)
The initial output voltage records the initial capacitor charge. A reset switch across Ci​ sets it to zero. The formula holds while both stages remain within their supply rails; a persistent input voltage otherwise produces amplifier saturation.

Faraday's law of induction states that the induced electromotive force around an oriented closed circuit equals the negative rate of change of linked magnetic flux. For a stationary circuit,
E=∮E⋅dℓ=−dtdΦB​​,ΦB​=∫A​B⋅dA.​
(50)
Here E is electric field, B is magnetic flux density, and the oriented surface A has the circuit as its boundary. For N identical series turns, replace ΦB​ by the total flux linkage NΦB​.
