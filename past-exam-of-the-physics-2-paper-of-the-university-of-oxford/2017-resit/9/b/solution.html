The paraxial approximation restricts light rays to small slopes relative to the optical axis, so sinβ≃tanβ≃β and geometric lengths can be expanded to second order in height. Treat the source's optical lens as a thin lens located at x=0, with ∣y∣≪u,∣v∣ and small surface slopes.
For positive real conjugate distances u,v, the collapsed-plane geometric path has length
S(y)=u2+y2​+v2+y2​≃u+v+2y2​(u1​+v1​).
(41)
To thin-element accuracy, a segment s(y)=s0​−αy2 of this path lies inside the optical lens, replacing the same length of air. The geometric air path is S−s, the material path is s, and the optical path length and travel time are therefore
L(y)=S(y)+(n−1)s(y),ct(y)≃u+v+(n−1)s0​+y2[2u1​+2v1​−(n−1)α].
(42)
This distinguishes geometric length from optical path length; adding ns to S without subtracting the replaced air would count that segment twice.
For a nonaxial light ray, Fermat's principle gives dL/dy=0 and hence
u1​+v1​=2(n−1)α.
(43)
The focal length is obtained with the incident object at infinity:
f1​=2(n−1)α,f=2(n−1)α1​.​
(44)
The constant thickness s0​ supplies a common delay but does not change the leading focal length. Since α has units of inverse length, this formula has the correct dimensions. These are thin lens results; paraxial approximation alone does not remove finite-thickness corrections in a general thick lens.
