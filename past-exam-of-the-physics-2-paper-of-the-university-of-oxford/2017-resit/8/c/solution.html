Use the electric dipole above a grounded conducting plane approximation, valid for d≪a. Let z^ point upward, and choose p=p(sinθx^+cosθz^). Reflecting the two constituent point charges and reversing their signs produces an image electric dipole moment
p′=p(−sinθx^+cosθz^)
(32)
at height −a. Thus the parallel electric dipole moment reverses and the normal one is unchanged. At the real electric dipole, the separation from its image is 2az^, giving the image electric field
Eim​=4πϵ0​(2a)31​[3(p′⋅z^)z^−p′]=32πϵ0​a3p​(sinθx^+2cosθz^).
(33)
The physical torque is τ=p×Eim​, so
τ=−32πϵ0​a3p2sinθcosθ​y^​=−64πϵ0​a3p2sin2θ​y^​.​
(34)
For 0<θ<π/2, its direction reduces θ, tending to align the electric dipole with the upward normal. In general it favours either normal orientation; a perfectly parallel orientation has zero torque but is unstable to tilting.
The conductor-response potential energy includes one half of the real-image interaction:
U=−21​p⋅Eim​=−64πϵ0​a3p2(1+cos2θ)​.
(35)
This factor accounts for the induced surface charge changing as the physical source is assembled. Differentiation with respect to height at fixed orientation gives
F=−∂a∂U​z^=−64πϵ0​a43p2(1+cos2θ)​z^.​
(36)
There is no horizontal force, since translating the entire arrangement parallel to the plane leaves U unchanged. For example, the normal and parallel orientations give respective downward force magnitudes 3p2/(32πϵ0​a4) and 3p2/(64πϵ0​a4).
A single physical point charge induces total plane electric charge −q. One can verify this by integrating its induced surface charge density, −qh/[2π(ϱ2+h2)3/2], over the plane. The two physical electric charges therefore induce opposite totals, giving
Qplane​=−(+q)−(−q)=0.​
(37)
The local surface charge density is generally nonzero. The zero total holds for the finite neutral pair as well as its ideal electric dipole limit.
