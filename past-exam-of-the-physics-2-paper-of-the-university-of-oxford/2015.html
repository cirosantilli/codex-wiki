Take the electric potential to vanish at infinity. The distance from the centre to each point charge is half the diagonal of a polygon of the square, namely a/2​. The superposition principle for the electric potential therefore gives, for four charges +q,
V=4πϵ0​1​a/2​4q​=πϵ0​a2​q​.​
(1)
Strictly, specifying only the magnitude of each electric charge leaves its sign undetermined. For signed charges qj​, the general answer is V=2​∑j=14​qj​/(4πϵ0​a). For example, two positive and two negative charges of equal magnitude give zero electric potential at the centre. The boxed result uses the usual interpretation that all four charges have the same positive sign.

Gauss's law states that the outward electric flux through a closed surface is the enclosed electric charge divided by the vacuum permittivity:
∮S​E⋅dS=ϵ0​Qenc​​,∇⋅E=ϵ0​ρ​.
(2)
In electrostatics, the electric field is the negative gradient of the electrostatic potential, E=−∇V. Substitution into the differential equation form of Gauss's law gives Poisson's equation ∇2V=−ρ/ϵ0​. Consequently, in a region containing no charge density,
∇2V=0,​
(3)
which is Laplace's equation. Charges on the bounding conductors enter through the boundary conditions; they need not vanish for Laplace's equation to hold inside the empty gap.

Neglect fringing field near the edges of the parallel-plate capacitor. Put the plates at z=0,d, with prescribed electric potentials VA​,VB​. Translational symmetry parallel to a plane makes the electrostatic potential independent of the coordinates parallel to the plates. Thus Laplace's equation reduces to d2V/dz2=0. Its general solution is V=A+Bz, and the two boundary conditions determine both constants:
V(z)=VA​+dVB​−VA​​z,E=dVA​−VB​​z^.​
(4)
The electric field is uniform and points toward decreasing electric potential. No plate voltages are specified, so a numerical electric potential cannot be fixed; choosing one plate as the reference only fixes the arbitrary additive constant.

Use the geometric cross-section shown in the source: the outer cylinder has radius 2a and centre O, and the circular cavity has radius a and centre C=ax^. Let the electric current be in the +z direction. The conducting cross-sectional area is 3πa2, so the uniform electric current density is J=Iz^/(3πa2).
To obtain the exterior magnetic field of a wire with an eccentric cylindrical cavity, apply the superposition principle to a full cylinder of electric current density J centred at O and a second cylinder of electric current density −J centred at C. Their electric currents are 4I/3 and −I/3. This reproduces the actual electric current density everywhere, including zero in the cavity. Outside a long circular cylinder, Ampère's law gives B=μ0​Icyl​z^×r/(2πr2). Every point outside the outer boundary is outside both component cylinders. Consequently, writing r=xx^+yy^​,
B(x,y)=6πμ0​I​z^×(r24r​−∣r−ax^∣2r−ax^​),r≥2a.​
(5)
In Cartesian coordinates, the same magnetic flux density is
Bx​=6πμ0​I​(−x2+y24y​+(x−a)2+y2y​),By​=6πμ0​I​(x2+y24x​−(x−a)2+y2x−a​),Bz​=0.
(6)
The cavity breaks rotational symmetry. Thus the magnetic flux density is generally neither constant in magnitude nor tangential on a circle centred at O; replacing its line integral by 2πrB would be unjustified. Its line integral is nevertheless μ0​I, as Ampère's law requires. At distances r≫a, the leading term becomes μ0​Iϕ^​/(2πr), with corrections of order μ0​Ia/r2.

Choose the entry point as (0,0), the initial velocity as vx^, and the slit as (d,a), with a,d>0. A magnetic field perpendicular to the page changes the direction of the velocity, while the Lorentz force does no work. For positive q, an upward initial force requires the magnetic flux density to point into the page. The resulting circular motion has centre (0,r), since its initial tangent is horizontal.
The slit must lie on the circle, giving d2+(a−r)2=r2 and hence r=(d2+a2)/(2a). Equating the centripetal force to the magnetic part of the Lorentz force, mv2/r=∣q∣vB, gives
∣B∣=∣q∣(d2+a2)2mva​,B=−q(d2+a2)2mva​z^.​
(7)
A negative electric charge requires the opposite direction of magnetic flux density. These expressions assume nonrelativistic motion and negligible energy loss through electromagnetic radiation.
For an opaque screen, reaching the slit must be the first encounter with x=d. The velocity there has vx​/v=(d2−a2)/(d2+a2), so passage from left to right requires a<d, as in the drawn arrangement. At a=d the trajectory merely touches the screen. If a>d, the same circle first meets the screen at height d2/a<a, where the particle is blocked; its later passage through (d,a) does not solve the opaque-screen problem. This qualification distinguishes a geometric circle through two points from an accessible slit trajectory.

Take the divergence of the proposed Ampère's law. Since the divergence of a curl vanishes identically, it implies ∇⋅J=0. However, local charge conservation requires the continuity equation
∇⋅J=−∂t∂ρ​.
(8)
For example, electric current entering a charging capacitor accumulates electric charge on its plates, so the charge density is time dependent. The proposed equation cannot describe that process.
From Gauss's law, ∇⋅E=ρ/ϵ0​. Thus ∇⋅(ϵ0​∂t​E)=∂t​ρ, which supplies precisely the missing divergence. Adding the displacement current density gives the Ampère-Maxwell equation:
JD​=ϵ0​∂t∂E​,∇×B=μ0​(J+ϵ0​∂t∂E​).​
(9)
Its divergence now yields the correct continuity equation. Through a fixed surface, the associated displacement current is ID​=ϵ0​dΦE​/dt, where ΦE​ is the electric flux. In a vacuum capacitor gap, this equals the conduction electric current charging the plates, even though no charges cross the gap. Charge conservation fixes the required divergence of the correction; by itself it does not exclude adding a further divergence-free vector field.

LQ¨​+RQ˙​+CQ​=0.
(10)
The initial conditions are Q(0)=Q0​ and Q˙​(0)=0: the open switch initially prevents electric current, and finite inductance prevents an instantaneous jump in it. Put γ=R/(2L) and ω02​=1/(LC). The two characteristic roots are s=−γ±γ2−ω02​​. An oscillatory electric current requires a nonzero imaginary part, hence
R2<C4L​,orR<2CL​​,ωd​=LC1​−4L2R2​​.​

Use Fraunhofer diffraction, appropriate to observation in the far field or in the focal plane of a lens, and uniform illumination across the slit. At observation angle θ, points separated by transverse coordinate u acquire a phase difference kusinθ, where k=2π/λ is the wavenumber. The superposition principle for the coherent wave amplitudes gives
A(θ)∝∫−w/2w/2​eikusinθdu=wβsinβ​,β=λπwsinθ​.
(13)
The intensity is proportional to the squared modulus of the wave amplitude, so
I(θ)=I(0)(βsinβ​)2.​
(14)
The limit at β=0 is one. The noncentral zeros satisfy wsinθ=mλ for nonzero integers m for which the angle is physically allowed. In the Rayleigh criterion, two mutually incoherent point images are just resolved when the central maximum of one diffraction pattern lies at the first minimum of the other. For single-slit diffraction this gives
ΔθR​=arcsin(wλ​)≃wλ​(w≫λ).​
(15)
The exact statement here takes one image on the slit normal; for nearby paraxial images the angular resolution is λ/w. The first propagating minimum exists only for w≥λ. The numerical factor 1.22 belongs to a circular aperture and does not apply to this slit.

For a linear element in sinusoidal steady state, the complex impedance is the ratio of the voltage phasor to the electric current phasor, Z=V/I. Use the convention that the physical signal is the real part of its phasor times eiωt. Then time differentiation becomes multiplication by iω.
For a resistor, Ohm's law gives V=RI. For a capacitor, I=CV˙ gives I=iωCV. For an inductor, V=LI˙ gives V=iωLI. Hence
ZR​=R,ZC​=iωC1​=−ωCi​,ZL​=iωL.​
(16)
The imaginary part encodes the relative phase of electric current and voltage: electric current leads voltage in an ideal capacitor and lags it in an ideal inductor.

The electric dipole moment is p=2aqz^. By the superposition principle, the exact electric potential is
V(r)=4πϵ0​q​[r2+a2−2arcosθ​1​−r2+a2+2arcosθ​1​].

First use the usual idealization of a grounded infinite conducting plane at z=0, with the real point charge +q at (0,0,d). The method of images replaces the metal, for purposes of calculating the exterior electric field, by an image charge −q at (0,0,−d). The exact electric potential in z>0 is
V(ρ,z)=4πϵ0​q​[ρ2+(z−d)2​1​−ρ2+(z+d)2​1​].
(27)
It vanishes on the plane, and the uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential identifies it with the physical exterior solution. The tangential electric field cancels on the plane, while the normal components add:
E(ρ,0+)=−2πϵ0​(ρ2+d2)3/2qd​z^.​
(28)
The electric field inside the conductor is zero, so the electrostatic boundary condition gives σ=ϵ0​Ez​(0+). Integrating the surface charge density over the facing plane yields
Qface​=∫0∞​2π(ρ2+d2)3/2−qd​2πρdρ=−qd[−ρ2+d2​1​]0∞​,Qface​=−q.​
(29)
The exact image expression is essential near the charge: using the far-field electric dipole approximation at ρ comparable with d would be invalid.
Electrical boundary condition. The source does not specify grounding or the initial total electric charge of the slab. The boxed total is the grounded infinite-plane answer and the large-plane approximation to the charge on its nearby face. An initially neutral, finite, electrically isolated slab retains zero total electric charge; an approximately −q accumulation near the real charge is then balanced by approximately +q on its remote face and edges. This gives a concrete counterexample to interpreting −q as the unconditional total charge of every large metal slab. The local image formula assumes the infinite-plane limit with no applied background electric field.

Faraday's law of induction states that the induced electromotive force around a material electrical circuit equals the negative rate of change of its linked magnetic flux:
E=∮C(t)​(E+v×B)⋅dℓ=−dtd​∫S(t)​B⋅dS.​
(30)
The surface orientation and direction around its boundary are related by the right-hand rule. Lenz's law supplies the physical meaning of the minus sign: the induced electric current produces a magnetic field opposing the change in linked magnetic flux. For a stationary contour the local form is ∇×E=−∂t​B; for this moving wire, the magnetic Lorentz force term is also needed in the electromotive force.

R=Aw​4aρc​​=πdw2​16ρc​a​.

Use dF=Idℓ×B for the magnetic Lorentz force on each wire segment. Let +x point right, +y upward and +z out of the page, so B=−Bz^. With clockwise electric current, the four magnetic forces are
Ftop​=IaBy^​,Fbottom​=0,Fright​=IBhx^,Fleft​=−IBhx^.​

a OurBigBook.com
Follow the branch of a light ray that undergoes one internal specular reflection in the spherical drop. At entry, refraction turns its direction by α−β. The internal specular reflection turns it by π−2β, because the radius is the normal vector and the incidence and reflection angles are both β. At exit the second refraction contributes another α−β. Thus the total deviation angle is
δ=2(α−β)+(π−2β)=π+2α−4β.​
(36)
Snell's law at entry gives sinα=nsinβ, taking the surrounding air's refractive index as one. The same internal angle at successive encounters follows from the isosceles triangles formed by each chord and the two radii. Internal specular reflection here need not be total internal reflection; the transmitted branch at that encounter does not belong to the one-reflection trajectory.

Regard the refraction angle as a function of the angle of incidence, β=arcsin(sinα/n). Implicit differentiation of Snell's law gives β′=cosα/(ncosβ). Differentiating the deviation angle gives δ′=2−4β′. An interior stationary point therefore satisfies
cosα=2n​cosβ,A=2.​
(37)
The stationary deviation of a primary rainbow ray is a minimum: differentiating cosα=ncosββ′ again and using Snell's law gives β′′=tanβ((β′)2−1). At the stationary point, β′=1/2, and hence δ′′=−4β′′=3tanβ>0 for a positive interior refraction angle. This minimum is responsible for the concentration of emerging light rays near the primary rainbow direction.

1−n2sin2β=cos2α=4n2​cos2β=4n2​(1−sin2β).

Solution

Words: 141
d OurBigBook.com
With the Sun behind the observer and raindrops ahead, the one-reflection branch sends many light rays toward directions close to the minimum deviation angle. Nearby values of the angle of incidence produce nearly the same outgoing direction there, forming a wave caustic. For water the resulting cone is approximately 42∘ from the antisolar direction. The observer sees the intersecting drops as a circular rainbow, or the part of that circle visible above the ground.
Normal dispersion makes the refractive index larger for violet than for red light. At the minimum, increasing the refractive index increases the deviation angle, and therefore decreases the viewing angle π−δmin​. Red is on the outer edge of the primary rainbow, violet on the inner edge. The internal specular reflection is partial: with n=4/3, the relevant β≃40.2∘ is below the critical angle arcsin(1/n)≃48.6∘. Diffraction and the finite angular size of the Sun smooth the geometrical concentration; the physical intensity does not actually become infinite.
