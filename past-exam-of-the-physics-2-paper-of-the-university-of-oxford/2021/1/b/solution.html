A large electrical conductor between the point charges provides electrostatic shielding: one must not retain their original mutual Coulomb force through an ideal infinite plate. In the usual broad-plate approximation, let σ denote the unperturbed surface charge density on each face. The electrostatic boundary condition gives an outward electric field E0​=σ/ϵ0​. Neglecting local electrostatic induction, the rod's tension then satisfies mgtanϕ=QE0​, giving
ϕ≃tan−1(ϵ0​mgQσ​)=1.98∘.​
(3)
There are two physical qualifications. First, if σ means the total charge per projected plate area, shared equally between the two faces, then E0​=σ/(2ϵ0​) and the same approximation gives ϕ≃0.989∘. The factor of two is a convention about the specified surface charge density, not a different Gauss's law.
Second, a point charge near an electrical conductor makes its surface charge density nonuniform. For an infinite plane with prescribed background electric field E0​, the method of images adds an attractive force kQ2/(4x2), where x=lsinϕ. The more accurate equilibrium equation is
mgtanϕ=QE0​−16πϵ0​l2sin2ϕQ2​.
(4)
Its larger, stable root is approximately 1.96∘ for the per-face convention, or 0.877∘ for the total-area convention. This ideal-plane refinement is not an exact answer for an unspecified finite plate: its dimensions and the meaning of the quoted surface charge density would be needed. In particular, adding a uniform-sheet electric field to the original mutual repulsion would describe a different, nonshielding model.
