Approximate the atmosphere by concentric spherical layers of refractive index. At every boundary, Snell's law conserves nrsinψ, where ψ is the angle between the light ray and the radius. Straight propagation between boundaries conserves the same quantity because rsinψ is the distance of that line from the origin. In the smooth limit this is the spherical ray invariant
p=n(r)rsinψ.
(25)
At the surface, a light ray apparently on the horizon is tangent, so p=ns​R. In vacuum far from the Earth, n=1, and the straight incident line has distance p from the centre. Removing the atmosphere while retaining that incident line gives minimum altitude
H=p−R=(ns​−1)R=0.0003(6400km)=1.92km.​
(26)
The detailed variation of refractive index with altitude changes the local curvature of the light ray, but not the conserved p. This conclusion assumes a spherically stratified atmosphere and a light ray which actually connects the surface to space; nonspherical structure or a trapping layer would require separate analysis.

Write T for transit time and ℓ(y)=t0​−αy2 for lens thickness to avoid using t for both. In the thin lens approximation, the two geometrical distances to the lens plane are a2+d2​ and b2+d2​. Their sum counts the lens segment as vacuum. Replacing that segment by material of refractive index n adds (n−1)ℓ(d) to the optical path length. Thus, to the retained thin-element accuracy,
cT=a2+d2​+b2+d2​+(n−1)(t0​−αd2).
(62)
Using the Taylor expansion 1+u​=1+u/2+O(u2) for d≪a,b gives
T=c1​[a(1+2a2d2​)+b(1+2b2d2​)+(n−1)(t0​−αd2)].​

The omitted geometrical terms include O(d4/a3+d4/b3) in optical path length, together with finite-thickness corrections discarded by the thin lens and paraxial approximations. The addition is n−1, because the underlying distance already includes propagation across the thickness once in vacuum.

Fermat's principle makes the optical path length, or transit time in a stationary medium, stationary under small variations of the light ray with fixed endpoints. It need not be a global minimum. In the result of part (a), the coefficient of d2 is
2a1​+2b1​−α(n−1).
(64)
For a family of paraxial light rays from the object to its image, stationarity with respect to d requires this coefficient to vanish. Therefore the thin lens equation is
a1​+b1​=2α(n−1)=f1​.
(65)
For a distant object, a→∞, and the image position b=f defines the focal length. Hence
f=2α(n−1)1​.​
(66)
The axial member d=0 is stationary by symmetry for arbitrary b; requiring the neighbouring non-axial light rays to meet the same image is what determines b.

In surrounding refractive index n′, the geometrical segments contribute n′[a2+d2​+b2+d2​] to the optical path length, while replacement by the lens adds (n−n′)ℓ(d). Fermat's principle therefore gives
n′(a1​+b1​)=2α(n−n′).
(67)
The physical focal length measured in the surrounding medium is
f(n′)=2α(n−n′)n′​=2α(n/n′−1)1​.​
(68)
For α>0 and n>1, increasing n′ from 1 towards n makes the positive focal length grow monotonically from its vacuum value to infinity: df/dn′=n/[2α(n−n′)2]>0. At n′=n, there is no refraction at the boundary, so the element has zero optical power and no finite optical focus. If n′>n, its optical power changes sign and the same shape becomes diverging.

Both elements diverge, but their physical focal lengths differ by a factor n for n>1. For example, n=1.5 makes the void's focal length magnitude 1.5 times that of the concave glass element.
