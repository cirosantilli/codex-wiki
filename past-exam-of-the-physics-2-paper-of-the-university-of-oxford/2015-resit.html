Take the electric potential to vanish at infinity and work at r>0. For a positive point charge q in vacuum,
V(r)=4πϵ0​rq​,E(r)=4πϵ0​r2q​r^​.
(1)
The field is radial and outward. Since a radial scalar function has gradient ∇V=(dV/dr)r^,
−∇V=−(−4πϵ0​r2q​)r^=E.
(2)
Thus the two expressions have the required vector relationship. Adding an arbitrary constant to V would leave the electric field unchanged; the chosen reference at infinity fixes that constant. The point-charge singularity itself is excluded from the ordinary derivative calculation.

Let ψ=kx−ωt and use bold B for the magnetic flux density, to distinguish it from the scalar amplitude B in Ez​. For a plane electromagnetic wave travelling along +x, Faraday's law gives the wave-associated field
B=ωk​x^×E.
(3)
Hence
Bx​=0,By​=−ωkB​cosψ,Bz​=ωkA​sinψ​.
(4)
For an explicit component check, ∇×E=(0,kBsinψ,kAcosψ), while differentiating the displayed field gives −∂B/∂t equal to that vector. The field is transverse and also satisfies ∇⋅B=0.
In vacuum, Ampère-Maxwell equation compatibility requires ω/k=c, so each factor k/ω is 1/c. More generally it is the inverse phase velocity on a suitable homogeneous-medium plane-wave branch. A time-independent background field could be added without changing the electric wave's Faraday equation; the requested propagating-wave field takes that independent background to be zero. Finally, E×B=(k/ω)(Ey2​+Ez2​)x^ confirms the propagation direction and the relative signs.

For a fixed number N of identical bulbs, first approximate each as a resistor of electrical resistance R connected to a fixed ideal battery electric potential difference V. A series circuit has electrical resistance NR, while a parallel circuit has electrical resistance R/N. Thus
Pseries​=NRV2​,Pparallel​=RNV2​,Pseries​Pparallel​​=N2​.
(5)
Parallel connection therefore maximizes dissipation in this model: every bulb receives the full source electric potential difference instead of sharing it. For N=1 the two arrangements coincide.
An actual incandescent light bulb changes electrical resistance as its filament heats, so a single constant R need not describe both operating points. The same qualitative conclusion holds for identical stable bulbs with increasing current-voltage function i(U): parallel operation gives Pp​=NVi(V), whereas symmetric series operation gives Ps​=Vi(V/N). The former is larger for N>1. This comparison assumes a fixed collection and an ideal source as specified; an unlimited number of parallel bulbs on a zero-internal-resistance ideal battery would have no finite total-power maximum.

The magnetostatic Ampère's law is ∮B⋅dℓ=μ0​Ienc​, equivalently ∇×B=μ0​J in vacuum. Let the wire radius be a and take positive electric current along its axis. Cylindrical symmetry makes B azimuthal and constant in magnitude on a radius-r circular path, so 2πrB=μ0​Ienc​.
Uniform electric current density gives J=I/(πa2). Inside the wire, Ienc​=Jπr2=Ir2/a2; outside, the full electric current is enclosed. Therefore
B(r)=⎩⎨⎧​2πa2μ0​Ir​ϕ^​,2πrμ0​I​ϕ^​,​0≤r≤a,r≥a.​​
(6)
The direction is set by the right-hand rule around the electric current. The magnitude starts at zero on the axis, rises linearly to μ0​I/(2πa) at the surface and then falls as 1/r. The two expressions are continuous at r=a. The long-wire approximation neglects end effects and any high-frequency redistribution of electric current.

Let R1​<R2​ and put charge per unit length +λq​ on the inner shell and −λq​ on the facing outer conductor. For a long coaxial Gaussian surface of radius r in the gap, Gauss's law gives
E(r)2πrL=ϵ0​λq​L​,E(r)=2πϵ0​rλq​​.
(7)
The electric potential difference between the shells is
V1​−V2​=∫R1​R2​​E(r)dr=2πϵ0​λq​​logR1​R2​​.
(8)
With total inner charge Q=λq​L, the capacitance is
C=log(R2​/R1​)2πϵ0​L​​.
(9)
This assumes a vacuum or approximately air-filled gap and negligible fringing at the ends. A uniform linear dielectric replaces ϵ0​ by its permittivity. The expression depends on the shell-to-shell potential difference, not on the arbitrary common potential reference.

For an ideal inductor of constant self-inductance L, the passive-sign electric potential difference is VL​=LdI/dt. The supplied power is VL​I, so increasing the electric current quasistatically from zero stores
U=∫VL​Idt=∫0I​LI′dI′=21​LI2​.
(10)
Any electrical resistance would additionally dissipate energy as heat; it is not included in this stored magnetic energy.
For a long vacuum solenoid with N turns, axial length ℓ and cross-sectional area A, Ampère's law gives B=μ0​(N/ℓ)I. Its flux linkage is NΦ=NBA, so
L=INΦ​=ℓμ0​N2A​.
(11)
The nearly uniform field occupies volume Aℓ, with negligible external-field energy in the long-solenoid approximation. Dividing its stored energy by this volume yields
uB​=AℓU​=21​μ0​(ℓN​)2I2=2μ0​B2​​.
(12)
The vacuum and linear-inductance assumptions matter; a nonlinear magnetic material requires the appropriate field-energy integral rather than simply substituting a varying inductance into LI2/2.

Fermat principle states that the optical path length ∫nds, equivalently the travel time in the nondispersive stationary isotropic ray model, is stationary for the actual light ray between fixed endpoints. It need not be a minimum in every optical system.
For two homogeneous media separated by y=0, place the fixed endpoints at (0,h1​) and (D,−h2​), with positive heights. If the ray crosses at (x,0), its optical path is
L(x)=n1​x2+h12​​+n2​(D−x)2+h22​​.
(13)
Stationarity with respect to the crossing point gives
dxdL​=n1​x2+h12​​x​−n2​(D−x)2+h22​​D−x​=0.
(14)
The two fractions are sinθ1​ and sinθ2​, with angles measured from the interface normal. Thus
n1​sinθ1​=n2​sinθ2​​,
(15)
which is Snell's law. Dividing L by the vacuum light speed gives the corresponding travel time; minimizing geometric distance without refractive-index weighting would incorrectly predict a straight ray through unlike media.

Use nonrelativistic dynamics, take B>0 along +y, and neglect radiation. The Lorentz force qv×B initially points along +z. It does no work, so the speed stays v and the trajectory inside the field is circular, with
r=qBmv​,Ω=mqB​.
(16)
Starting from the entry point, the orbit can be parametrized as x=rsinθ, z=r(1−cosθ), where θ=Ωt. Its largest possible x before turning back is r. Therefore it enters the strict region x>b exactly when
r>b,B<qbmv​​.
(17)
For that case, the first boundary crossing satisfies sinθb​=b/r. The velocity components are vx​=vcosθb​ and vz​=vsinθb​, so the outgoing angle above the +x direction is
θb​=sin−1(mvqBb​),0≤θb​<2π​​.
(18)
The zero-field limit is straight motion. At r=b the particle only touches the far boundary tangentially and does not enter x>b; for r<b it turns around before reaching that boundary and eventually returns through the entrance side. The equality case must therefore not be included in a crossing condition.

The electric field does work qEd on the initially stationary particle. Thus the entry speed into the magnetic region is
v0​=m2qEd​​.
(19)
In the source geometry the magnetic field is perpendicular to the page, and the particle enters perpendicular to the straight interface. Its return to that interface occurs after a semicircle. The separation between exit and return points is its orbit diameter, so the required separation d gives r=d/2. Consequently
∣B∣=qd2mv0​​=qd8mE​​​.
(20)
For the drawn field into the page and a positive charge entering upwards, the trajectory bends left. Its speed is constant while in the magnetic field, so the time there is the semicircle length divided by v0​:
tB​=v0​πr​=2v0​πd​=π8qEmd​​=q∣B∣πm​​.
(21)
The last form is half the cyclotron period and independently checks the geometric calculation.

Label the capacitor's left terminal a and right terminal b, with the battery's negative node at zero. The source diagram has 2R from a to ground, R from b to ground, and a source-side resistor R feeding a from electric potential difference V. The capacitor electric current I2​ flows from a to b; I1​ flows through 2R.
With the capacitor removed, Va​=2V/3 and Vb​=0, so the Thévenin equivalent seen by the capacitor has Vth​=2V/3. Suppress the ideal electric potential difference source to find its electrical resistance: the left terminal sees R∥2R=2R/3 to ground and the right sees R, giving Rth​=5R/3 between the terminals.
The initially uncharged capacitor electric potential difference VC​=Va​−Vb​ therefore obeys
Rth​CdtdVC​​+VC​=Vth​,VC​(0)=0.
(22)
Solving gives VC​=(2V/3)(1−e−t/τ), with τ=Rth​C. The capacitor electric current is I2​=CV˙C​, hence
I2​(t)=5R2V​e−t/τ,A=5R2V​,τ=35RC​​.
(23)
At t=0+, the short-like uncharged capacitor makes Va​=Vb​=2V/5, so I2​=2V/(5R) directly from the right resistor. At long times the capacitor carries no direct electric current. These endpoint checks agree with the exponential solution.

The differential vacuum Gauss's law is
∇⋅E=ρvol​/ϵ0​​.
(27)
Integrate over a volume Ω with closed boundary S. The divergence theorem converts the left side into outward electric flux:
∮S​E⋅n^dS=∫Ω​∇⋅EdV=ϵ0​1​∫Ω​ρvol​dV=ϵ0​Qenc​​​.
(28)
This is the integral form. Surface charge can be treated by a thin-layer limit or by the corresponding jump in the normal field; the enclosed charge then includes the charged sheet. The surface orientation must be outward, and the field entering the integral is the total field, not merely the field of the enclosed charges alone.

Choose z=0 at the sheet, positive z upwards, so the uniformly charged slab occupies −d<z<0. Use the usual isolated-plane superposition convention, with no imposed uniform background field. The slab alone produces +ρd/(2ϵ0​)z^ above it, while the negatively charged sheet produces −σ/(2ϵ0​)z^ there. Consequently
E(z>0)=2ϵ0​ρd−σ​z^​.
(29)
Since ρd>σ, the field is upward, away from the net positive charge distribution, with magnitude (ρd−σ)/(2ϵ0​). The equal halves in the separate infinite-sheet fields follow from their reflection symmetry before superposition.

Below the slab, its own field is downward, −ρd/(2ϵ0​)z^. The negative sheet attracts a positive test charge upwards and contributes +σ/(2ϵ0​)z^. Adding the fields gives
E(z<−d)=−2ϵ0​ρd−σ​z^​.

Inside the uniformly charged slab, its own electric field is ρ(z+d/2)z^/ϵ0​: it vanishes at the slab's midplane and has derivative ρ/ϵ0​. Everywhere below the negative sheet, that sheet adds +σz^/(2ϵ0​). Therefore
E(−d<z<0)=2ϵ0​ρ(2z+d)+σ​z^​.
(31)
Its magnitude is the absolute value of this scalar coefficient. The field changes direction at
z0​=−21​(d+ρσ​)​,−d<z0​<−d/2.
(32)
For z<z0​ it points downward; for z>z0​ it points upward, towards the negative sheet. Thus it is neither uniform nor directed upwards throughout the slab. The derivative and the field at the lower slab boundary also agree with Gauss's law and the previously found outside field.

The signed normal-field profile is constant and negative below z=−d, rises linearly through the slab, jumps down at the negative sheet, then remains constant and positive above. More explicitly,
Ez​(−d−)=Ez​(−d+)=2ϵ0​σ−ρd​,Ez​(0−)=2ϵ0​ρd+σ​,Ez​(0+)=2ϵ0​ρd−σ​.

Ez​(0+)−Ez​(0−)=−ϵ0​σ​​,

exactly the negative-sheet boundary condition. The ideal sheet itself has distinct one-sided fields rather than one common field value.

For two identical narrow slits under coherent normal illumination, with λ the wavelength in the propagation medium, let Is​ be the intensity contributed by either slit alone in the relevant angular range. At far-field angle θ, the path difference is Δ=ssinθ and the phase difference is 2πΔ/λ. Adding the two complex amplitudes gives
I(θ)=2Is​[1+cos(λ2πssinθ​)]=4Is​cos2(λπssinθ​)​.
(35)
For a screen perpendicular to the optical axis, tanθ=z/d. Thus the angular fringe factor uses sinθ=z/d2+z2​; in the paraxial region ∣z∣≪d it reduces to
I(z)≃4Is​cos2(λdπsz​),Δz=sλd​​.
(36)
The supplied large-distance condition gives the Fraunhofer diffraction regime for the slit separation; the further replacement sinθ≃z/d is a separate small-angle approximation. An unspecified finite slit width would add a single-slit envelope, and Is​ can then vary with angle. The formula above gives the interference factor in the ideal narrow-slit, equal-amplitude model; no absolute source intensity is supplied.

An ideal focusing lens maps propagation angle to position in its back focal plane, with z≃fθ for paraxial light rays. The two-slit relative phase remains 2πssinθ/λ, so the focal-plane interference pattern has the same form with propagation distance replaced by focal length:
If​(z)=Imax,f​cos2(λfπsz​),Δzf​=sλf​​.
(37)
The new peak intensity Imax,f​ accounts for focusing and need not equal the earlier screen peak. The lens forms the angular Fraunhofer pattern at a finite focal-plane distance, so a long free propagation distance is no longer needed to obtain that angular pattern. The displayed position formula assumes the same narrow-slit and paraxial approximations.

c OurBigBook.com
Write λ0​ for the vacuum wavelength. Initially the identical air-filled boxes add equal optical paths. When the refractive index in one box falls from nair​ to n(P), the extra path in the box that remains full is
Δ(P)=l[nair​−n(P)].
(38)
Identical fixed window contributions cancel. At the screen centre the geometric slit-path difference is zero, so, neglecting unequal transmission changes,
I(0,P)=Imax​cos2(λ0​πl[nair​−n(P)]​).
(39)
Each extra optical path of one wavelength produces one full intensity period and one new maximum. Taking the stated count in the usual sense of 20 completed fringe periods during evacuation, excluding the initial maximum, and using n(0)=1 for vacuum gives
N=λ0​l(nair​−1)​,nair​=1+lNλ0​​​.
(40)
Taking N=20, vacuum wavelength λ0​≃460 nm and l=30 mm,
nair​−1≃3.07×10−4,nair​≃1.00031​.
(41)
If the quoted 460 nm instead denotes the wavelength λair​ in ambient air, use λ0​=nair​λair​ and the exact relation nair​=[1−Nλair​/l]−1. It gives 1.00030676, differing by less than 10−7 from the vacuum-wavelength estimate, well below one-fringe counting resolution.
The count convention matters: 20 maxima including both the initial and final maxima span only 19 periods, giving approximately 1.000291. If the final fractional fringe is not measured, an integer count alone has a resolution of order λ0​/l≃1.53×10−5 in refractive index. The standard 20-period estimate should therefore not be interpreted as an arbitrarily precise index measurement.
