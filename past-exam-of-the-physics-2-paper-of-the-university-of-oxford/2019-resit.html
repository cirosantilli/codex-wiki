For one wire, Ampère's circuital law on a concentric circle of radius r gives
2πrB(r)=μ0​I,B(r)=2πrμ0​I​.
(1)
The other wire experiences the Lorentz force on its electric current, dF=Idℓ×B. Its direction is perpendicular to this magnetic field, so the force per unit length is
LF​=2πrμ0​I2​.​
(2)
The right-hand rule gives attraction for parallel electric currents in the same direction and repulsion for opposite directions.
For the upper wire, the upward magnetic force per unit length balances its weight per unit length, λg, where g is the gravitational acceleration. Therefore
req​=2πλgμ0​I2​.​
(3)
This assumes the lower wire is held fixed and both wires remain straight. Within the vertical-motion model, increasing the separation weakens the upward magnetic force, so this equilibrium is vertically restoring.

Interpret the coordinates as a plane in ordinary three-dimensional electrostatics. Set ke​=1/(4πϵ0​), where ϵ0​ is the vacuum permittivity. The work required to bring the second point charge in quasistatically is its electric charge times the first charge's electric potential:
U=−dke​Q2​.​
(4)
The individual point-charge self-energies are excluded; this is the electrostatic potential energy of point charges relative to infinite separation.
By the superposition principle, for r=(x,y) and d=(d,0) the electric field is
E(r)=ke​Q(∣r∣3r​−∣r−d∣3r−d​).
(5)
At the first position both contributions point toward positive x, whereas at the second they oppose one another. Thus
E(d/3,0)=4d245ke​Q​x^,E(−d/3,0)=−16d2135ke​Q​x^.​
(6)
At (d/2,d) the two distances are 5​d/2. The vertical electric field components cancel and the horizontal components add:
E(d/2,d)=55​d28ke​Q​x^.​
(7)
Dimensional convention. The source's description of space as two-dimensional cannot literally mean two-dimensional electrostatics with the same inverse-square formula. In a genuinely two-dimensional Gauss's law, the circumference grows as r, giving an electric field proportional to 1/r and a logarithmic electric potential. For example, integrating 1/r from d to infinity diverges, so the same finite reference potential energy at infinite separation does not exist. The displayed results use the conventional three-dimensional Coulomb's law restricted to the stated plane.

For this space-charge-filled parallel-plate capacitor, place the first plate at x=0 and the second at x=d=xo​, ignoring fringing field of a capacitor. With vacuum permittivity between the plates and fixed charge density ρ, Poisson's equation and the prescribed boundary electric potentials give
dx2d2V​=−ϵ0​ρ​,V(0)=V1​,V(d)=V2​.
(10)
Integrating twice and imposing both values yields
V(x)=V1​+dV2​−V1​​x+2ϵ0​ρ​x(d−x).​
(11)
The electric field is minus the gradient of the electric potential, hence
Ex​(x)=dV1​−V2​​+ϵ0​ρ​(x−2d​),∣E(x)∣=​dV1​−V2​​+ϵ0​ρ​(x−2d​)​.​
(12)
Thus the electric potential is a parabola, concave downward for ρ>0 and upward for ρ<0. The signed electric field is a straight line. Its magnitude has a V-shaped corner if its zero
x∗​=2d​−ρdϵ0​(V1​−V2​)​
(13)
lies inside the gap; otherwise its magnitude is a single straight segment. An interior zero requires ∣V1​−V2​∣<∣ρ∣d2/(2ϵ0​). At that point the electric potential has its interior extremum. For ρ=0, these reduce to a linear electric potential and a uniform electric field.

Let vC​ denote the capacitor voltage with the same polarity as the applied voltage. By Kirchhoff's voltage law, Ohm's law and I=CdvC​/dt,
RCdtdvC​​+vC​=V,vC​(0)=0.
(14)
Solving this first-order differential equation gives the RC low-pass step response
vC​(t)=V(1−e−t/τ),τ=RC=0.050s.​
(15)
The capacitor voltage is continuous at switching because an instantaneous finite jump would require an impulsive electric current.
For the square-wave response of an RC low-pass filter, take equal positive and negative half-periods. Let A=10V, T=1/f, h=T/2 and α=e−h/τ. After the transient, symmetry makes the capacitor voltage alternate between −vp​ and +vp​. Over the positive half-period,
vC​(t)=A−(A+vp​)e−t/τ,0≤t<h.
(16)
Requiring vC​(h)=vp​ gives
vp​=A1+α1−α​=Atanh(4τT​).​
(17)
The next half-period is the sign-reversed copy. These exact expressions explain the requested sketches:
  • At 1kHz, h/τ=0.01. The voltage barely changes in each half-period, so the output is nearly triangular about zero, with extrema about ±0.0500V. Its small-signal slope is approximately ±A/τ=±200Vs−1.
  • At 10Hz, h=τ. Clearly curved exponential rises and falls reach only ±4.621V; the output never settles to either input level.
  • At 0.1Hz, h=100τ. The output closely follows the input plateaux at ±10V, joined by continuous exponential transitions with time constant 0.050s.

Faraday's law of induction states that the oriented electromotive force around a closed material circuit is minus the rate of change of its linked magnetic flux:
E=∮C(t)​(E+u×B)⋅dℓ=−dtd​∫S(t)​B⋅dS.
(24)
The surface orientation and circuit direction obey the right-hand rule. The minus sign expresses Lenz's law: the induced electric current, when allowed to flow, produces a magnetic field opposing the change of linked magnetic flux. For a stationary circuit this reduces to ∮E⋅dℓ=−dΦB​/dt.
The source's “Lorenz force” is the Lorentz force, q(E+v×B). A wire element of cross-section Ac​ contains nAc​dℓ carriers of charge q with drift velocity vd​. Adding their magnetic forces and using I=nqAc​vd​ gives
dF=Idℓ×B,F=IL×B​
(25)
for a straight wire in a uniform magnetic field. The length vector follows conventional electric current, irrespective of the carrier sign.
For the unlabelled upward-motion request, define t^=(cosϕ,0,−sinϕ) down the slope, b^=(0,1,0) across the rails and n^=(sinϕ,0,cosϕ) out of their plane. Choose the vertical magnetic field as B=Bz^. An electric current I across the bar in direction −b^ gives a horizontal magnetic force −IBsx^. The other forces are weight −mgz^ and the rail normal force Nn^. Uniform upward velocity requires
mgsinϕ−IBscosϕ=0,N−mgcosϕ−IBssinϕ=0.
(26)
Consequently
I=Bsmgtanϕ​,FB​=cosϕmgsinϕ​,N=cosϕmg​.​
(27)
Each symmetric rail supplies half of N. The electric current must make IL×B point horizontally toward the uphill side; for the coordinates just defined it flows along −b^. Reversing the vertical magnetic field reverses this current direction. The external supply must maintain that electric current against the motional electromotive force during ascent. For cosϕ=0, no finite horizontal magnetic force can support uniform motion along the vertical rails.

In SI units, Maxwell's equations in vacuum, allowing charge density ρ and electric current density J, are
∇⋅E∇×E​=ρ/ϵ0​,=−∂t​B,​∇⋅B∇×B​=0,=μ0​J+μ0​ϵ0​∂t​E.​​
(35)
The source-free specialization sets ρ=0 and J=0; it cannot describe the charged objects at their locations. Applying the divergence theorem to the first equation gives Gauss's law:
∮∂Ω​E⋅dS=∫Ω​∇⋅Ed3x=ϵ0​Qenc​​.
(36)
For an isolated point charge q1​, spherical symmetry makes the electric field radial and constant in magnitude on a concentric sphere. Therefore 4πr2Er​=q1​/ϵ0​. Multiplying by a second point charge gives Coulomb's law,
E=4πϵ0​r2q1​​r^,F2​=4πϵ0​r2q1​q2​​r^.​
(37)
The force points away from the first charge for like signs and toward it for opposite signs.
For the spherical capacitor, a Gaussian sphere between the shells encloses q, so Er​=q/(4πϵ0​r2) for a<r<b. Integrating the electric field inward from the outer shell yields
V(r)−V(b)=−∫br​4πϵ0​s2q​ds=4πϵ0​q​(r1​−b1​).
(38)
An arbitrary constant V(b) remains unless a reference is specified. The capacitance is determined by the potential difference:
C=V(a)−V(b)q​=4πϵ0​b−aab​.​
(39)
For the subsequent charging operation, take the initial outer shell to be earthed, or interpret the stated V as the inner-to-outer voltage with the outer reference zero. Isolating the inner shell fixes its electric charge at q=CV. Removing the outer shell leaves an isolated conducting sphere. Its electric field and electric potential, now with zero at infinity, are
E(r)={0,qr^/(4πϵ0​r2),​r<a,r>a,​V(r)=4πϵ0​rq​(r≥a).
(40)
Consequently
Vsurface​=b−abV​,Vold outer position​=b−aaV​.​
(41)
These changes of electric potential are compatible with constant electric charge. Without the initial reference convention the absolute stated V does not determine q=CV: for example, if the outer shell were initially isolated and neutral, the absolute inner electric potential would instead be q/(4πϵ0​a).
Finally take the connected spheres to be sufficiently far apart that the long wire's capacitance and their mutual influence can be neglected. Their common electric potential requires
aqa​​=2aq2a​​,qa​+q2a​=q,
(42)
so qa​=q/3 and q2a​=2q/3. The magnitudes of the surface electric fields are
Ea​=12πϵ0​a2q​,E2a​=24πϵ0​a2q​,Ea​:E2a​=2:1.​
(43)
This is the usual widely separated-sphere approximation. A wire enforces an equipotential, but alone does not make this ratio exact for arbitrary arrangements. As a concrete counterexample, if the second shell enclosed the first concentrically and they were connected, all net electric charge would reside on the exterior and the inner sphere's surface electric field would be zero.

In the Huygens-Fresnel principle construction, after a time Δt the incident disturbance advances v1​Δt while the secondary disturbance in the second medium advances v2​Δt. The two right triangles share the segment along the interface. Hence
sinθ2​sinθ1​​=v2​v1​​=n1​n2​​,θ2​=arcsin(n2​n1​​sinθ1​).​
(44)
Here both angles are measured from the normal vector, and vj​=c/nj​ are phase velocities. This is Snell's law. For n1​>n2​, no real transmitted angle exists when
θ1​>θc​=arcsin(n2​/n1​).​
(45)
This is total internal reflection; equality is the limiting grazing geometry. The statement concerns an ideal transparent interface, not material absorption or losses at a nearby second boundary.
Let β be the internal angle to the optical axis in the straight step-index optical fiber. At its flat entrance, Snell's law gives sinθi​=n1​sinβ. At the side wall a meridional ray has angle of incidence π/2−β. The limiting total internal reflection condition is cosβ=n2​/n1​. Thus the optical fiber acceptance angle satisfies
sinθi,max​=n12​−n22​​≡NA,θi,max​=arcsinn12​−n22​​.​
(46)
The numerical aperture expression assumes the square root is at most one for air; otherwise all forward launch angles are admitted by this ideal ray test. Strictly confined meridional rays lie inside the limiting cone.
For n1​=1.48 and n2​=1.46, NA=0.242487 and θi,max​=14.0334∘. A light ray at internal angle β travels distance L/cosβ along a guide of axial length L. In the nondispersive approximation its travel time is t=n1​L/(ccosβ). Therefore
tmax​thalf​​=cn2​n12​L​=5.00438μs,=c1−sin2(θi,max​/2)/n12​​n1​L​=4.95365μs,​Δt≃50.7ns.​
(47)
Here L=1000m and c=299792458ms−1; using 3.00×108ms−1 gives 5.001μs and 4.950μs. The halving applies to the external launch angle, not to β. For actual short pulses the group refractive index ng​ replaces the prefactor n1​ in t=ng​L/(ccosβ); the source supplies no optical dispersion data. The difference calculated here is geometrical intermodal dispersion in a nondispersive geometrical optics model.
Bending lowers the outer-wall angle of incidence, allowing a previously guided light ray to escape into the cladding. To calculate a definite meridional ray bend threshold, continue the on-axis launch assumption and take a meridional ray in the plane of the circular bend. Its initial internal angle to the centreline tangent obeys sinβ=sin10∘/n1​. Its straight path has distance bray​=Rcosβ from the bend centre. At the outer wall of radius R+r, elementary right-triangle geometry gives
siniout​=R+rRcosβ​.
(48)
At the loss threshold, this equals n2​/n1​. Solving for the centreline radius gives
Rc​=n1​cosβ−n2​n2​r​=n12​−sin210∘​−n2​n2​r​=14.93mm.​
(49)
For this launch, R<Rc​ violates the total internal reflection condition at the outer wall; R=Rc​ is the limiting case. A real optical fiber has gradual bend loss governed by its electromagnetic wave modes, rather than a universal abrupt cutoff.
Launch dependence. The final sentence does not separately specify the entry position or orientation of the bend relative to the incident light ray. For a planar launch displaced by u from the centreline toward the outer wall, replace the numerator of siniout​ by (R+u)cosβ. For example, a launch at the inner edge, u=−r, instead gives
Rc,inner​=rn1​cosβ−n2​n1​cosβ+n2​​=29.96mm.
(50)
Thus 14.93mm is the result for the stated on-axis continuation; it is not an entry-independent threshold. No missing figure is needed for these explicitly specified ray models.

Use phasors with time dependence eiωt and electrical impedance Z=V/I. The element laws V=IR, I=CdV/dt and V=LdI/dt give
ZR​=R,ZC​=iωC1​=−ωCi​,ZL​=iωL.​
(51)
A complex number records both amplitude and phase, turning the linear differential equations of a sinusoidally driven electrical circuit into algebraic equations. A resistor has in-phase voltage and electric current and dissipates average power. For an ideal capacitor, the electric current leads the voltage by 90∘; for an ideal inductor, it lags by 90∘. Their imaginary electrical impedances express reversible energy storage and return, with zero net average power over a period. Using e−iωt would conjugate the displayed reactive signs without changing predictions.
In the illustrated Maxwell bridge, the left and right vertices are the supply terminals and the upper and lower vertices are detector terminals. Insert a sensitive AC null detector between the latter and drive the former with a sinusoidal source. The upper-left arm is Zx​=Rx​+iωLx​, the upper-right arm is R3​, the lower-left arm is R2​, and the lower-right arm is the parallel combination
Zp​=(R1​1​+iωC)−1.
(52)
At a null no detector electric current flows, so the two voltage dividers give
Zx​+R3​R3​​=R2​+Zp​Zp​​,Zx​Zp​=R2​R3​.
(53)
Multiplying by the parallel-arm electrical admittance yields
Rx​+iωLx​=R2​R3​(R1​1​+iωC).
(54)
Equating real and imaginary parts therefore gives
Rx​=R1​R2​R3​​,Lx​=CR2​R3​.​
(55)
Two independent adjustments are needed to annul the in-phase and quadrature detector signals. For example, change R2​ to set Lx​ and R1​ to set Rx​, iterating until the detector reads zero. Alternatively, with fixed R2​,R3​, independently vary R1​ and C.
The Maxwell bridge compares an unknown lossy inductor with a stable standard capacitor, avoiding the need for an accurately known reference inductor. The ideal balance equations contain no frequency, and a null measurement need not rely on absolute detector calibration. In practice choose a suitable frequency below the coil's self-resonance, where the series Rx​,Lx​ model and the standard capacitor remain accurate.
If the initial setting is far from balance, the bridge as drawn needs adjustable components and a detector, not merely a more precise readout. Fit resistance boxes or switched ranges for R1​ and a ratio arm, and a selectable standard C. Choose ranges around
R2​R3​=CLx​​,R1​=CRx​Lx​​.
(56)
Then approach the null with coarse adjustments followed by finer ones. A large required R1​ can be brought into range by increasing C and decreasing R2​R3​ proportionally. Start with reduced excitation or reduced detector sensitivity to avoid overload, then increase sensitivity near the null. Changing frequency alone cannot repair an impossible component range, since the ideal balance conditions are frequency-independent. Stray capacitance and lead electrical resistance set practical accuracy limits and may require shielding and short connections.
