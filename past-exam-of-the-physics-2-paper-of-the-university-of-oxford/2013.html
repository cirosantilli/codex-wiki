1 OurBigBook.com
Newton's second law applies separately to each body's total force: zero force on q1​ means zero acceleration of q1​, while a nonzero force on a free q2​ gives nonzero acceleration of q2​. It does not assert that those two forces must be equal and opposite.
The conducting shell is a third interacting body. Its induced surface charges shield the cavity from the external charge, while charges on its outer surface can still exert a force on q2​. Reaction forces act on the shell and, if it is held fixed, on its supports. In the electrostatic limit the force balance of the complete isolated charge–shell system is F1​+F2​+Fshell​=0. Thus the given situation is consistent with both Newton's second law and Newton's third law once the shell is included.
Shielding removes the cavity field due to external charges; it does not generally remove the force of the inner surface charges induced by an off-centre q1​. Zero total force on q1​ is an additional condition, satisfied for example when it is at the centre of a spherical cavity.

The electric field is force per unit positive stationary test charge, in the limit that the test charge does not disturb the source:
FE​=qE.​
(1)
The magnetic flux density is defined by the velocity-dependent part of the Lorentz force, FB​=qv×B. Equivalently, a straight current element in a uniform field experiences
F=Iℓ×B,​
(2)
which fixes its magnitude and right-hand-rule direction by force measurements with different current orientations.

For a point charge qj​, Coulomb's law gives Ej​=qj​r^/(4πϵ0​r2). The flux through an oriented surface element is
Ej​⋅dS=4πϵ0​qj​​r2r^⋅dS​=4πϵ0​qj​​dΩ.
(3)
Here dΩ is its signed solid angle seen from the charge. A closed surface subtends total oriented solid angle 4π for a point inside and zero for a point outside. Thus the flux of each inside charge is qj​/ϵ0​ and each outside charge has zero net flux. By superposition,
∮S​E⋅dS=ϵ0​1​j inside∑​qj​=ϵ0​qenclosed​​.​
(4)
This is Gauss's law; a surface passing through a point-charge singularity is excluded.

In classical Maxwell's equations, electric charge is a source or sink of the electric field, so in vacuum ∇⋅E=ρ/ϵ0​ can be nonzero. There is no magnetic-charge source in the same theory: Gauss's law for magnetism is ∇⋅B=0. Magnetic flux entering a closed surface equals the flux leaving it. A magnetic dipole has both polarities; it is not an isolated magnetic monopole.

Let J1​,J2​ be the single-slit intensities at the two wavelengths. At a screen position y, the path difference is approximately Δ=dsinθ, with sinθ=y/D2+y2​. Fields from the two slits interfere at each wavelength, while a detector averaging over the optical beat period adds the intensities of the two distinct wavelengths:
I(y)=4J1​cos2(λ1​πΔ​)+4J2​cos2(λ2​πΔ​).​
(5)
For equal single-slit intensities J1​=J2​=J, define σˉ=(1/λ1​+1/λ2​)/2 and Δσ=1/λ2​−1/λ1​. Then
I=4J[1+cos(πΔσΔ)cos(2πσˉΔ)].
(6)
The first cosine gives the interference visibility envelope. Near the centre one may further replace Δ by dy/D. The formula neglects unspecified single-slit envelopes and the slow change of single-slit intensity across the screen.

For the centred screen, ∣y∣≤0.30 m. Retaining the screen-angle geometry gives
Δmax​=0.502+0.302​d(0.30)​=5.145μm,λˉ=σˉ1​=512.20nm.
(7)
There are about σˉΔmax​=10.045 carrier orders on each side. With equal spectral intensities, direct extrema of the intensity formula give
21 bright fringes: the central maximum and ten on each side.​
(8)
Their contrast declines toward the edges: the first equal-intensity visibility zero occurs at Δ=1/(2Δσ)=5.25μm, just beyond the screen's 5.145μm limit. Edge visibility is only about 0.031, so fewer fringes may be discernible at a practical contrast threshold.
The screen reaches ∣y∣/D=0.6, where a paraxial conversion of its full width is inaccurate. Also, wavelength-independent detector sensitivity does not itself establish equal source intensities. The boxed count specifies the usual equal-intensity idealization; without spectral weights and a visibility threshold, a unique experimental count is not fixed.

Take B as reference and define positive source current I upward into the upper internal node; the source symbol does not specify an arrow, so this definition fixes its sign. With A open, no current flows through the upper series resistor. The shunt resistor therefore has voltage IR, and the battery's longer plate is on the right, adding +V toward A. Thus
VTh​=VA​−VB​=IR+V.​
(9)
Deactivate the independent sources: open the ideal current source and short the ideal voltage source. The two resistors then lie in series between A and B, giving
RTh​=2R.​
(10)
The Thévenin equivalent is a source IR+V in series with 2R, positive toward A. If I is instead defined downward, replace I by −I; the resistance is unchanged.

The ideal voltage source drives a series L–R–C branch and a separate resistor R in parallel. At ω0​=1/LC​, the series branch is purely resistive, so its peak current is Ip​=V0​/R. Its stored energies are
UL​=21​LIp2​sin2ω0​t,UC​=21​LIp2​cos2ω0​t.
(11)
Their sum is constant in sinusoidal steady state, with value Umax​=LV02​/(2R2). Each resistor dissipates average power V02​/(2R), including the resistor directly across the source. Over one cycle,
Wdiss​=ω0​2π​RV02​​,Wdiss​Umax​​=4πRω0​L​=4πR1​CL​​.​
(12)
Omitting the parallel resistor would double this ratio. The expression concerns the driven steady state, not a switch-on transient.

The upper positive plate gives a downward field E=V/a, while B points into the page. For a rightward electron, the electric force is upward and the magnetic force downward. Its initial speed is
v0​=me​2(1eV)​​=5.931×105ms−1,BE​=0.0337(1)20000​=5.935×105ms−1.
(13)
These are equal to the precision intended by the rounded plate spacing: the arrangement is a velocity selector. Accordingly,
θexit​≃0.​
(14)
It is not appropriate to calculate a large electric deflection and ignore magnetic bending. For completeness, if all printed values are treated literally, let vd​=E/B, u=v0​−vd​, and ωc​=eB/me​. The exact nonrelativistic crossed-field motion is
x=vd​t+ωc​u​sinωc​t,y=ωc​u​(cosωc​t−1),
(15)
with vx​=vd​+ucosωc​t and vy​=−usinωc​t. At the exit solve x(te​)=0.50 m, then use θ=atan2(vy​,vx​). The small rounding mismatch produces nanometre-scale oscillations, with ∣θ∣≤sin−1(∣u∣/vd​)≃6.32×10−4 rad. Its precise oscillatory exit angle is not robust to the supplied rounding.

2 OurBigBook.com
In uniform fields, a rigid neutral electric dipole with both charges moving at the selected common velocity has zero net translational force. To leading nonrelativistic order its torque is τ=p×(E+v×B), which also vanishes at the velocity-selector condition. Thus it continues undeflected and, initially nonrotating, does not start rotating in the ideal balanced fields. An unbalanced effective field could exert a torque; the dipole orientation is not otherwise specified.

Ri​Vi​​+Rf​Vo​​=0,Vi​Vo​​=−Ri​Rf​​,∣Av​∣=Ri​Rf​​.​

Fermat's principle says that the travel time, or equivalently the optical path length, is stationary under small path variations at fixed endpoints. For a flat interface, let the ray cross at horizontal coordinate x, with source and detector at heights h1​,h2​ and horizontal separation D. Apart from 1/c, its travel time is
L(x)=n1​h12​+x2​+n2​h22​+(D−x)2​.
(21)
Setting dL/dx=0 gives
h12​+x2​n1​x​=h22​+(D−x)2​n2​(D−x)​,n1​sini=n2​sinr.​
(22)
The angles are measured to the normal. This is Snell's law; “least time” is more generally a stationarity condition.

Take a horizontal cross-section in units of R. The eye is at E=(2​,0), and the tadpole swims along the perpendicular diameter T=(0,y) shown in the source. Since glass and water have the same red-light index, their internal interface causes no red-light refraction. Write an exit point on the circular wall as P=(cosϕ,sinϕ). An unobstructed outward ray to the eye has ∣ϕ∣≤π/4.
For ϕ≥0, let e be the magnitude of the emerging ray's angle to the outward normal and i its internal angle. Geometry and Snell's law give
sine=3−22​cosϕ​2​sinϕ​,i=sin−1(1.33sine​).
(23)
The outgoing internal ray makes angle β=ϕ−i to +x. Its backward continuation intersects the swim diameter at
y(ϕ)=sinϕ−cosϕtan(ϕ−i).
(24)
The directly visible positive endpoint is the largest such intercept, including all single-pass refracted rays. Maximizing this one-variable expression gives ϕ∗​≃38.07∘ and y∗​≃0.75744. By symmetry the visible length is 2Ry∗​, so
visible fraction≃0.757(about three quarters).​
(25)
Checking only the grazing exit at ϕ=45∘ gives 2​/[1+1.332−1​]≃0.75350, a close estimate but a slightly smaller fraction. The exact limiting ray occurs before that grazing ray, where two refracted paths merge at a caustic. This is a geometrical, single-pass visibility boundary; finite pupil size and detection thresholds would smooth the transition.

The result need not be the same. Optical dispersion changes the refractive index with wavelength. Under normal dispersion, the blue-light index is larger, changing the refracted rays and generally reducing the visible fraction in the one-interface model. The blue indices of both water and glass are not specified; they may also differ from each other, introducing refraction at their interface. Thus no unique numerical blue-light fraction follows from the red-light value alone.

Let the column radius be b=A/π​, and J=I0​/A be its uniform axial current density. Treating it as concentric shells, only the current at radii below r contributes to B(r): a surrounding shell has zero interior field. Thus

Take x∈[−s/2,s/2] along the tilt, with local separation h(x)=a+2δx/s. Since s≫a and the tilt is small, each narrow strip is a local parallel-plate capacitor at the same voltage V. Its surface charge densities are approximately

Isolation fixes charge, not voltage: Q=C0​V0​. The electrostatic energy is therefore U(t)=Q2/[2C(δ(t))]. Inverting the small-tilt capacitance gives
U(t)=2aϵ0​s2V02​​[1−3a2δ02​​sin2ω0​t+O(a4δ04​​)].​
