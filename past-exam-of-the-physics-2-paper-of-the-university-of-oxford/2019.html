The method of images replaces electrical conductors by fictitious image charges outside the physical region. Choose the image charges so that the physical electric charges and their images produce the prescribed constant electric potential on every conducting boundary. The uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential then identifies this constructed electric potential with the physical solution in the charge-containing region. Several conducting planes can require several, or infinitely many, image charges; a single reflected image is sufficient for one infinite plane.
Put the plane at z=0 and the point charge at (0,0,d). For z>0, use an image charge −q at (0,0,−d):
ϕ(x,y,z)=4πϵ0​q​[x2+y2+(z−d)2​1​−x2+y2+(z+d)2​1​].
(3)
This vanishes on the plane. An arbitrary constant can be added if a different reference electric potential is chosen. Excluding the point charge's own electric field, Coulomb's law gives
F=−16πϵ0​d2q2​z^.​
(4)
The separation from the image charge is 2d, and the force is attractive for either sign of q. No imposed external electric field is included.

RQR​​=2RQ2R​​,QR​+Q2R​=Q,QR​≃Q/3,Q2R​≃2Q/3.​

For completeness, the exact result for ideal externally tangent conducting spheres can be obtained by the method of images. Let their radii be a,b, their centre separation D=a+b, and their common electric potential V. Begin with central image charges 4πϵ0​Va and 4πϵ0​Vb. A wirewound resistor uses a metal alloy chosen for stable electrical resistivity. Compared with a typical carbon composition resistor, it can offer tighter resistor tolerance, a smaller temperature coefficient of resistance, lower excess electrical noise, and greater continuous electrical dissipation when its construction provides an adequate heat path. These are design-dependent advantages, rather than guarantees for every alloy or package.
The winding has parasitic inductance and interturn capacitance. Its electrical impedance therefore ceases to be a nearly real electrical resistance at sufficiently high frequencies; an approximate model is a series circuit R+iωL shunted by a small parasitic capacitance. A resonance can occur, making a wirewound resistor unsuitable where a nearly frequency-independent electrical resistance is required. Noninductive winding patterns reduce this problem. For comparable small-signal duties it is usually larger and more expensive. A carbon composition resistor is compact and has relatively small parasitic inductance, but commonly has poorer stability, larger resistor tolerance, and more excess electrical noise.

The passive RC low-pass filter is simple, inexpensive, requires no power supply, and has no operational amplifier saturation or finite bandwidth limitation. It offers no voltage gain; its output impedance is finite, and both source electrical resistance and loading can change the observed frequency response. Its resistor also produces thermal noise.
The active inverting low-pass amplifier provides a low-frequency voltage gain of magnitude five and, within the usable bandwidth, a small output impedance, so it can drive a load with much less change in frequency response. The input electrical resistance is approximately R1​=1kΩ and may itself load the source. It needs a power supply and adds electrical noise and offset; finite operational amplifier gain, bandwidth, slew rate, available output swing, and output electric current limit the ideal formula. Its inversion must also suit the application. The best choice depends on required voltage gain, source and load electrical impedances, and the signal's frequency and amplitude.

A mercury-vapor lamp is an extended, spontaneously emitting source, so practical interference requires spatial filtering and spectral selection. A narrow illuminated source slit gives sufficient spatial coherence; selecting one strong spectral line improves temporal coherence and avoids overlapping differently spaced interference fringes.
In the classical double-slit experiment, both apertures are illuminated by the same narrow source. The arrangement is symmetric, permits direct measurement and adjustment of slit separation, and has equal optical materials in the two paths. Its main drawbacks are low transmitted intensity through narrow apertures, the need for a well-made closely spaced slit pair and alignment, and the finite single-slit diffraction envelope. A broad source washes out interference fringes.
Lloyd's mirror uses the direct wave from a narrow source and the reflected wave, which appears to come from its virtual image behind a plane mirror. It requires only one physical source slit; the mirror can be adjusted to make a small effective source separation without manufacturing a double aperture. Only the direct-reflected overlap region shows interference fringes. Finite mirror size, alignment, surface quality, unequal intensities and the polarization-dependent reflection coefficient can reduce visibility. For the usual grazing reflection from glass, the reflected electric wave acquires approximately a π phase shift, so the zero path-difference position is dark rather than the bright central fringe of the symmetric classical double-slit experiment. Both arrangements still require adequate spatial coherence and temporal coherence.
