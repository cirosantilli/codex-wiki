With the Sun behind the observer and raindrops ahead, the one-reflection branch sends many light rays toward directions close to the minimum deviation angle. Nearby values of the angle of incidence produce nearly the same outgoing direction there, forming a wave caustic. For water the resulting cone is approximately 42∘ from the antisolar direction. The observer sees the intersecting drops as a circular rainbow, or the part of that circle visible above the ground.
Normal dispersion makes the refractive index larger for violet than for red light. At the minimum, increasing the refractive index increases the deviation angle, and therefore decreases the viewing angle π−δmin​. Red is on the outer edge of the primary rainbow, violet on the inner edge. The internal specular reflection is partial: with n=4/3, the relevant β≃40.2∘ is below the critical angle arcsin(1/n)≃48.6∘. Diffraction and the finite angular size of the Sun smooth the geometrical concentration; the physical intensity does not actually become infinite.
