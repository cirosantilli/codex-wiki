Sequence convergence in the cofinite topology

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Topology Cofinite topology
OurBigBook.com Words: 237
By convergence of a sequence in a topological space, xn​→a means that every open neighbourhood of a contains all sufficiently late terms. In the cofinite topology, this is equivalent to the condition that, for every b=a, the value b occurs only finitely often. Necessity uses the open neighbourhood X∖{b}; sufficiency chooses an index beyond every last occurrence of the finitely many points excluded by a given open neighbourhood.
Consequently a sequence with distinct terms converges to every point of X. For the original choice xn​=n on X=N>0​, take the two open neighbourhoods
U1​=X∖{2,5,8}∋1,U2​=X∖{1,3,7}∋2.
(2)
All terms with n≥9 lie in U1​ and all terms with n≥8 lie in U2​. More generally, each finite excluded set has a last visit, so xn​→1 and xn​→2, and in fact xn​→a for every a∈X. No numerical distance is tending to zero here; the open sets specify the meaning of convergence.
Distinct terms are sufficient, not necessary. The sequence 1,1,2,2,3,3,… also converges to every point, since each value occurs only twice. By contrast, the constant sequence xn​=1 converges only to 1: an open neighbourhood of any a=1 can exclude 1. The alternating sequence 1,2,1,2,… converges to no point, since some value different from any proposed limit occurs infinitely often.
