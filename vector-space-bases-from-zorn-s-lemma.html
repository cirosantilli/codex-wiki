Vector space bases from Zorn's lemma (∃B⊆V: B is a basis of V)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Set theory Zorn's lemma
OurBigBook.com Words: 267
Every vector space V over a field F has a basis, assuming the axiom of choice. More generally, any linearly independent subset A⊆V extends to a basis. This is an algebraic Hamel basis: each representation uses a finite linear combination, even when the basis is infinite.[ref]
Use the poset of linearly independent subsets containing A:
PA​={S⊆V:A⊆S, S is linearly independent over F},S≤T⟺S⊆T.
(23)
It is a set, being a subset of the power set of V, and is nonempty because A∈PA​.
A short proof outline supplies both essential steps:
  • For a nonempty chain in a partial order C⊆PA​, put U=⋃S∈C​S. Any finite linear relation in U lies in one member of C: among finitely many containing members, one contains the rest. Its linear independence makes every coefficient zero. Thus U∈PA​ is an upper bound. For the empty chain in a partial order, use A; its empty union need not contain A.
  • Zorn's lemma gives a maximal element B. If a vector v lies outside its linear span, then B∪{v} is linearly independent: a linear relation with nonzero coefficient on v would express v in that linear span. This contradicts maximality, so B is a spanning set and hence a basis.[ref]
Taking A=∅ proves existence, including V={0}, whose basis is the empty set. The chain in a partial order need not be indexed by the natural numbers: only the finitely many members needed for a particular linear relation are compared. An arbitrary union of linearly independent sets can fail: {v} and {2v} for a nonzero vector over the real numbers are each linearly independent, but their union is linearly dependent. The nesting of a chain in a partial order is what prevents this failure.
