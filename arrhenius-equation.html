Arrhenius equation (k(T)=Aexp[−Ea​/(RT)])

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Activation energy
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 5
The Arrhenius equation models the temperature dependence of a rate constant by
k(T)=Aexp(−RTEa​​),A>0,T>0.
(7)
Here A is the pre-exponential factor, Ea​ the activation energy, R the molar gas constant, and T the absolute thermodynamic temperature. In its original form, A and Ea​ are held constant over the range being modeled. It is an empirical relationship, not a universal rule for every reaction mechanism. See the Arrhenius equation.
Eliminating A between two positive temperatures gives the useful prediction
lnk(T1​)k(T2​)​=REa​​(T1​1​−T2​1​).
(8)
For Ea​>0, warming increases k. The logarithmic sensitivity is dln(k/kref​)/dT=Ea​/(RT2), so there is no universal doubling per ten degrees. The same 10K rise from 300 to 310K gives a factor 1.67749 for Ea​=40kJmol−1, versus 2.81398 for 80kJmol−1.
The useful simplification is that a strong change in reaction rate becomes a straight-line relation after changing variables. Measurements at several temperatures can estimate the slope and test whether one pair of constants describes the chosen range. Curvature can indicate a failure of that approximation; fitting a line does not uniquely establish a microscopic reaction mechanism.
Svante Arrhenius's 1889 paper on sucrose conversion through catalysis by a chemical acid compared the strong observed temperature dependence with the much smaller increase in collision frequency. He discussed van't Hoff's earlier temperature relations and proposed a small population of hypothetical active sucrose, replenished from ordinary sucrose, to interpret the dependence. The inspected translated excerpt derives an inverse-temperature relation in its equations (6)–(7) and then applies it to the reaction rate. That historical hypothesis is distinct from a modern transition state description. See the On the Reaction Velocity of the Inversion of Cane Sugar by Acids. The excerpt omits parts of the original paper; it does not provide a complete original apparatus description or dataset.
The pre-exponential factor must have the same units as the rate constant, since the exponential function supplies a dimensionless quantity. For a rate law v=k[A]m, both A and k have units (molL−1)1−ms−1. Thus a first-order reaction uses s−1, whereas a second-order reaction uses Lmol−1s−1. Calling A a frequency factor does not give it frequency units for every order of reaction. Its value includes the physical and geometrical factors allowed by the chosen reaction mechanism; equal activation energies need not mean equal reaction rates.
To read the blue Arrhenius plot, define the dimensionless quantity X=1000K/T and y=ln[k/k(300K)]. Its numerical slope is mX​=−60000/(1000R)≈−7.21634, giving Ea​=−(1000K)RmX​=60kJmol−1. The slope against 1/T, by contrast, is −7216.34K. The orange tangent at 300K gives Ea,app​≈64.9887kJmol−1; its finite-interval slope between 280 and 340K gives about 65.1227kJmol−1. The distinction between a local derivative and a fitted interval matters even with perfectly noise-free synthetic evaluations.
Two measured positive rate constants give a two-point estimate
Ea,fit​=R1/T1​−1/T2​ln[k(T2​)/k(T1​)]​.
(9)
Several temperatures let linear regression test the constant-parameter approximation: inspect the regression residuals, keep the same rate law and composition convention, and account for uncertainty in both k and T. A straight line over a short range supports a useful local approximation; it does not uniquely identify a reaction mechanism. A fixed change of units or of kref​ shifts the intercept without changing the inferred activation energy.[ref]
