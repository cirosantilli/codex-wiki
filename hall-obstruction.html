Hall obstruction (∣N(S)∣<∣S∣)

 ... Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Graph theory Matching (graph theory) Hall's marriage theorem Hall condition
OurBigBook.com Words: 209
A Hall obstruction is a left subset S with fewer distinct neighbours than vertices. At most ∣N(S)∣ members of S can be matched, so at least ∣S∣−∣N(S)∣ must remain unmatched. The pigeonhole principle proves failure without enumerating every proposed assignment.
For a stronger counterexample than an isolated vertex, take L={a,b,c,d} and R={1,2,3,4} with
N(a)=N(b)=N(c)={1,2},N(d)={3,4}.
(13)
Every vertex has positive degree of a vertex, the sides have equal cardinality, and ∣N(L)∣=∣L∣=4. Every two-vertex left subset also passes the Hall condition. Nevertheless S={a,b,c} has only N(S)={1,2}, so 2<3 rules out matching saturation of L.
All sixteen left subsets can be checked by writing k=∣S∩{a,b,c}∣ and ε=1 if d∈S, otherwise 0. Then
∣S∣=k+ε,∣N(S)∣=21{k>0}​+2ε.
(14)
Only k=3,ε=0 violates the Hall condition. Every matching in a graph uses at most two edges incident to {a,b,c} and at most one incident to d, so the matching number is three. There are exactly twelve size-three matchings in a graph: choose two of a,b,c in three ways, assign them to 1,2 in two ways, and choose 3 or 4 for d. Thus the obstruction identifies the bottleneck: adding availability only for d cannot help; allowing c to use slot 3 permits {a1,b2,c3,d4}, a perfect matching.
