Thermal diffusivity (κ)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Heat Thermal conduction
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 5
Thermal diffusivity measures how rapidly thermal conduction smooths a temperature difference inside a material. Thermal conductivity measures the heat flux driven by a specified temperature gradient; thermal diffusivity also accounts for the heat capacity that must be warmed. A material can carry substantial heat yet change temperature slowly because it stores substantial internal energy per kelvin.
Write α=κ for thermal diffusivity, reserving k for thermal conductivity. In a homogeneous isotropic material,
α=ρck​=Cvol​k​,[α]=Jm−3K−1Wm−1K−1​=m2s−1.
(131)
Here ρ is mass density, c is specific heat capacity for the relevant mechanical constraint, and Cvol​=ρc is volumetric heat capacity. The familiar k/(ρcp​) uses specific heat capacity at constant pressure. For ordinary solids with negligible thermal expansion, the difference between the two constrained specific heat capacities is often neglected. A rigid fixed-volume model instead uses specific heat capacity at constant volume; a compressible moving fluid requires the accompanying work and transport terms. Thus the constraint is part of the approximation, not a choice made solely from the units. [ref]
Assume a stationary material in the continuum approximation, local thermal equilibration on times shorter than those resolved, no internal heating, no phase transition, and negligible convection and mechanical work. Combining conservation of energy with Fourier's law gives the one-dimensional balance
ρc∂t∂T​=−∂x∂q​=∂x∂​(k∂x∂T​).
(132)
If k,ρ,c are constant over the region and temperature excursion, this becomes the heat equation ∂t​T=α∂x2​T. If k varies, retain the differentiated product; replacing it with k∂x2​T loses a term. Anisotropic materials need a thermal conductivity tensor. The scalar constant-α examples below deliberately exclude these complications. [ref]
For an original comparison, stipulate two model materials with equal thermal conductivity k=2Wm−1K−1 and equal mass density ρ=1000kgm−3, but specific heat capacities cA​=1000 and cB​=4000Jkg−1K−1. Then
αA​=2×10−6m2s−1,αB​=5×10−7m2s−1.
(133)
At the same temperature gradient, both have the same instantaneous heat flux by Fourier's law. At the same initial temperature profile and boundary conditions, material B takes four times as long to smooth a given spatial pattern. With fixed end temperatures, identical geometry and no sources, both eventually have the same steady profile and heat flux. The larger heat capacity changes the transient, not this steady state. These are stipulated parameters, not measurements of named substances.
