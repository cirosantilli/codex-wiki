The partial derivatives are gx​=sinx and gy​=y, so all stationary points are (nπ,0) for integers n. Their Hessian matrix is diag(cosx,1). At even multiples of π it is a positive-definite matrix, whereas at odd multiples it has one positive and one negative eigenvalue. Consequently
(2nπ,0): strict minima, g=0;((2n+1)π,0): saddle points, g=2.​

Use cylindrical coordinates x=ρcosϕ, y=ρsinϕ. The velocity field becomes
v=αzρϕ​,vρ​=vz​=0.​
(96)
For each fixed height, particles follow concentric circular streamlines around the vertical axis. At z>0 the motion is anticlockwise viewed from above, while at z<0 it is clockwise. Within a horizontal plane the angular velocity is αz, independent of radius, and the speed is α∣z∣ρ. Both the plane z=0 and the vertical axis are stationary. The rotation rate varies with height, so the entire liquid is not one rigidly rotating body.

For an oriented surface S′ with compatible boundary direction, the Stokes theorem states
∮∂S′​v⋅dr=∫S′​(∇×v)⋅ndS.​
(103)
Let H denote the removed patch and S′=S∖H the remaining cylinder. Keep the outward orientation inherited from the closed cylinder. Everywhere on the removed curved patch, the normal curl component is −αR, so its flux is −αRA, where A is its actual curved area. Part (d) then gives
∫S′​(∇×v)⋅ndS=0−(−αRA)=αRA.
(104)
Consequently the circulation around a hole in an oriented surface is
∮∂S′​v⋅dr=αRA.​
(105)
The positive direction here is induced by the remaining outward-oriented surface; locally it runs clockwise around the hole when viewed from outside. If the edge is instead traversed positively around the removed patch, the direction reverses and the answer is −αRA. The question does not specify an edge direction, so the sign must be accompanied by this convention. The result depends only on the removed curved area, not on its shape. If the patch has several boundary components, use the sum of their induced line integrals. Since [α]=1/(length⋅time), the result has the units of a velocity line integral, length squared per time.
