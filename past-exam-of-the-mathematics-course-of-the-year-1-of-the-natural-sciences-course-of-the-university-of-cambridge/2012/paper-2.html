Write the kernel as K(s,t)=(4πt)−1/2e−s2/(4t), with t>0. Its partial derivatives are
Kt​=(−2t1​+4t2s2​)K,Kss​=(−2t1​+4t2s2​)K.
(67)
Thus the heat kernel itself satisfies Kt​=Kss​. The proposed convolution consequently gives
ut​(x,t)=∫R​φ(ξ)Kt​(x−ξ,t)dξ=∫R​φ(ξ)Kss​(x−ξ,t)dξ=uxx​(x,t).
(68)
This verifies the heat equation. The parameter is positive time; the real integral formula is not defined at negative time.
The passage of derivatives through the integral needs justification. A sufficient condition is the Gaussian-weighted absolute integrability in heat-kernel convergence for Gaussian-integrable initial data; it holds, in particular, for data of polynomial growth. On compact positive-time intervals strictly below an integrable later time, the differentiated kernels have an integrable Gaussian bound, so differentiation under the integral sign applies.
Even if the PDF's assumed convergence is read as ordinary conditional convergence, one can justify the same calculation. Choose a later time T>t and let H(ξ) be a primitive of φ(ξ)e−ξ2/(4T). Convergence at spatial point zero and time T makes H bounded on both tails. Put q(x,ξ,t)=K(x−ξ,t)eξ2/(4T). This function, its spatial and time derivatives, and their ξ-derivatives decay as Gaussians when t<T. Integration by parts rewrites the convolution as −∫Hqξ​dξ, with vanishing endpoint terms. Boundedness of H now permits differentiation under this absolutely convergent integral; since qt​=qxx​, the same equation follows. Thus one need not silently equate conditional and absolute convergence.
