After j choices, let Cj​ be the number of forward moves. The two unwrapped positions are Cj​ and F−(j−Cj​), whose gap is F−j. It follows that the first subsequent meeting occurs exactly at step F, regardless of the order of the choices. Its position is the number of forward moves in that block.

pnmeet​=(nF​)pnqF−n,0≤n≤F.​

First take 1≤n≤F−1. The forward object reaches n at its nth move, while the reverse object reaches that label at its (F−n)th move. If the forward object wins after k reverse moves, then 0≤k≤F−n−1. The final choice is forward; among the preceding n+k−1 choices, exactly k are reverse. By the negative binomial stopping argument, this case has probability (kn−1+k​)pnqk.
Sum these mutually exclusive cases and equate the result to part (e), using parts (b) and (d). This proves the success-failure stopping identity here:
∑k=0F−n−1​(kn−1+k​)pnqk=(nF−1​)pnqF−n+∑k=n+1F​(kF​)pkqF−k.
(28)
The right side splits the same race into a special meeting at n and all meetings beyond n; the left side splits it according to the number of reverse moves at the winning time.
At n=F, both sides are zero with an empty sum and (FF−1​)=0. At n=0, the event has probability one; the right side equals ∑k=0F​(kF​)pkqF−k=1. The printed left side includes (0−1​) and is outside the usual nonnegative-upper-index combinatorial definition. With the explicit generalized convention (0−1​)=1, its k=0 term is one and all k≥1 terms vanish, so the identity also holds at that endpoint. Without this convention the displayed sum is undefined there; the zero-target race must instead be stated separately. All identities extend to p=0,1 by their polynomial forms.
