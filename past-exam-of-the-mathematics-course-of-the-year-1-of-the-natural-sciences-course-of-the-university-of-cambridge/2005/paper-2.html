A differentiable gradient field must have equal mixed derivatives. Here ∂Fy​/∂z=3z2 but ∂Fz​/∂y=−x, which are not identically equal on any open region. Equivalently,
∇×F=(−x−3z2, x+y−3z2, 0).
(1)
Thus no scalar potential exists for this field on an open region. Vanishing of the curl on a special lower-dimensional set would not supply a three-dimensional potential.

Write F=rr−γ−1. For r>0, direct Cartesian differentiation gives the divergence and curl of a radial vector field:
∂i​(xi​r−γ−1)=3r−γ−1−(γ+1)r−γ−3xi​xi​=(2−γ)r−γ−1.
(4)
The volume element in spherical coordinates therefore gives, for γ=2,
∫V​∇⋅FdV=4π(2−γ)∫ϵR​r1−γdr=4π(R2−γ−ϵ2−γ).​
(5)
For the direct surface integral, the outer sphere has outward normal +n and contributes 4πR2−γ. The inner boundary's outward normal relative to the shell is −n, so it contributes −4πϵ2−γ. Hence
∫S​F⋅dA=4π(R2−γ−ϵ2−γ).​
(6)
For γ=2, the field has zero ordinary divergence everywhere in this shell. The separate surface contributions are 4π and −4π, so both full integrals are zero. This radial power-law flux through a spherical shell was obtained by direct differentiation and integration; the excluded origin is never part of the volume.

The Lagrange multiplier equations are 2x=2λx, −2y=2λy, together with x2+y2=1. If x=0, then λ=1 and y=0; if y=0, then λ=−1 and x=0. Thus

Let w>0 denote the stated cost per area of the cylindrical wall. Charge for the curved wall and cap as specified; no floor cost is supplied. The volume and cost are V=πr2h+2πr3/3 and C=2πwrh+4πwr2. To minimize cost at fixed volume, apply a Lagrange multiplier to C−λ(πr2h+2πr3/3−V). Differentiation with respect to h gives λ=2w/r. The r derivative then gives 2πwh+8πwr=λ(2πrh+2πr2), reducing to h=2r. Thus the optimal cylinder and hemispherical cap with unequal surface costs has
r=(8π3V​)1/3,h=2(8π3V​)1/3.​

The required Legendre transform is the Helmholtz free energy, F=U−TS​ up to an additive constant. Its differential is dF=dU−TdS−SdT=−SdT−pdV, so FT​∣V​=−S and FV​∣T​=−p. Equality of mixed partial derivatives for a smooth thermodynamic state gives the Maxwell relation
(∂V∂S​)T​=(∂T∂p​)V​.​
(23)
Next use the total differential dS=ST​∣V​dT+SV​∣T​dV. Along a fixed-pressure path, divide by dT to get ST​∣p​=ST​∣V​+SV​∣T​VT​∣p​. Substitute the Maxwell relation to obtain
(∂T∂S​)p​−(∂T∂S​)V​=(∂T∂p​)V​(∂T∂V​)p​.​
(24)
Multiplying by T gives the usual difference between the heat capacity at constant pressure and the heat capacity at constant volume.

At fixed entropy, the total differential in coordinates (T,p) is 0=ST​∣p​dT+Sp​∣T​dp. Dividing by dT along that path gives ST​∣p​=−Sp​∣T​pT​∣S​. The same argument in coordinates (T,V) gives ST​∣V​=−SV​∣T​VT​∣S​. These are the two required intermediate identities.
To prove the final result without cancelling a possibly vanishing thermal-expansion derivative, write a=ST​∣V​, b=SV​∣T​, c=pT​∣V​, d=pV​∣T​. In a regular thermodynamic region with a,d=0, fixed pressure gives VT​∣p​=−c/d, while fixed entropy gives TV​∣S​=−b/a. The chain rule then gives ST​∣p​=a−bc/d and pV​∣S​=d−bc/a. Both cross-products equal ad−bc, proving
(∂T∂S​)p​(∂V∂p​)T​=(∂V∂p​)S​(∂T∂S​)V​.​
(25)
The heat-capacity and compressibility ratio identity rewrites this as Cp​/CV​=κT​/κS​: keeping entropy fixed rather than temperature fixed changes the mechanical stiffness by the same factor as the two thermal responses.

A real normal matrix satisfies MMT=MTM. For a symmetric matrix, MT=M and both products equal M2. For an antisymmetric matrix, MT=−M and both equal −M2. For an orthogonal matrix, both products are the identity. Thus each of the three classes consists of normal matrices.
