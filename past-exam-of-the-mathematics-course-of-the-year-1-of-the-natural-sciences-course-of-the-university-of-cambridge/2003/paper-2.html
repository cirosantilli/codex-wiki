With normal vector n=(−1,−2,2), a plane through A=(2,−1,1) has equation n⋅(r−A)=0. As n⋅A=2, its plane equation is
−x−2y+2z=2.​
(1)
The distance from a point to a plane is the absolute signed offset divided by the Euclidean norm of its normal vector. Hence the Euclidean distance from the origin is 2/3​, since ∣n∣=3. The closest point is 2n/9=(−2,−4,4)/9, whose Euclidean distance from the origin is 2/3 and which satisfies the plane equation.

For an odd integrable function, both f(x) and f(x)cosnx are odd. Their integrals over [−π,π] therefore vanish, so the constant and cosine Fourier coefficients are zero. For f(x)=sinax, the remaining Fourier sine coefficients are
bn​=π2​∫0π​sinaxsinnxdx=π1​[a−nsinπ(a−n)​−a+nsinπ(a+n)​]=π(n2−a2)2(−1)n+1nsinπa​.
(4)
The product-to-sum identity in the first step reduces the integral to two cosines. Here a is real and noninteger, so no denominator vanishes. Consequently the Fourier series of a noninteger-frequency sine is
sinax=n=1∑∞​π(n2−a2)2(−1)n+1nsinπa​sinnx,−π<x<π.​

For a bounded volume V with piecewise smooth closed boundary ∂V, oriented by the outward unit normal vector, and a continuously differentiable vector field on a neighborhood of V, the divergence theorem states
∫V​∇⋅FdV=∫∂V​F⋅ndS.
(9)
For an oriented piecewise smooth surface S with piecewise smooth boundary C=∂S, and a continuously differentiable vector field on a neighborhood of S, Stokes theorem states
∫S​(∇×F)⋅ndS=∮C​F⋅dr.
(10)
The boundary orientation follows the right-hand rule relative to the chosen normal vector. Reversing that orientation reverses both sides of the latter identity.

For h=x2/a2+y2/b2+z2−1, the gradient ∇h=2(x/a2,y/b2,z) is a normal vector to the ellipsoid. The outward unit normal vector is (x/a2,y/b2,z)/x2/a4+y2/b4+z2​. The conditions a,b>2​ ensure that z=1−x2/a2−y2/b2​>0 everywhere on the rectangular projection.
The dot product F⋅∇h=2xy(1/b2−1/a2) vanishes pointwise when a=b. In that case F has no normal component, so the surface flux is zero. For unequal axes, the given vector surface element gives
Φ=(b21​−a21​)∫01​∫01​1−x2/a2−y2/b2​xy​dydx.
(19)
Put u=x2/a2, v=y2/b2. Then xydxdy=a2b2dudv/4, and
Φ=4a2−b2​∫01/a2​∫01/b2​(1−u−v)−1/2dvdu.
(20)
Two elementary integrations now give
Φ=3a2−b2​[1−(1−a21​)3/2−(1−b21​)3/2+(1−a21​−b21​)3/2].​
(21)
This is the rotational flux through an ellipsoidal graph patch with unit vertical semiaxis and unit projection lengths. It also tends continuously to zero when a=b, agreeing with the pointwise argument.

At fixed particle number, the differential of the internal energy identifies T=(∂U/∂S)V​ and p=−(∂U/∂V)S​. Equality of continuous mixed partial derivatives gives the first Maxwell relation:
(∂V∂T​)S​=∂V∂S∂2U​=−(∂S∂p​)V​.​
(24)
For the enthalpy, dH=dU+pdV+Vdp=TdS+Vdp. Thus T=(∂H/∂S)p​, V=(∂H/∂p)S​, and the same argument gives the enthalpy Maxwell relation
(∂p∂T​)S​=∂p∂S∂2H​=(∂S∂V​)p​.​
(25)
The smoothness and independent thermodynamic coordinates are understood within a single phase.

b OurBigBook.com
Differentiate H=U+pV at fixed pressure and subtract the heat capacity at constant volume:
Cp​−CV​=(∂T∂U​)p​+p(∂T∂V​)p​−(∂T∂U​)V​.
(26)
Now use U=U(T,V) and the multivariable chain rule along a constant-pressure path:
(∂T∂U​)p​=(∂T∂U​)V​+(∂V∂U​)T​(∂T∂V​)p​.
(27)
Substitution cancels the fixed-volume derivative and gives the volume derivative form of the heat-capacity difference,
Cp​−CV​=[(∂V∂U​)T​+p](∂T∂V​)p​.​
(28)
For the ideal gas, the first term in brackets vanishes and V=NkT/p, so (∂V/∂T)p​=Nk/p. Consequently Cp​−CV​=Nk​ for the entire gas, with fixed particle number N. The extra heat capacity accounts for expansion work at constant pressure.

Away from the origin, the chain rule gives rx​=x/r and ry​=y/r. Differentiating once more yields the two-dimensional case of the Hessian of the Euclidean norm:
rx​=rx​,ry​=ry​,rxx​=r3y2​,ryy​=r3x2​,rxy​=−r3xy​.​

These partial derivatives are smooth wherever r>0, so Clairaut's theorem gives ryx​=rxy​=−xy/r3​. None of these formulas supplies a derivative at the origin: even the restriction r(x,0)=∣x∣ has no two-sided derivative there.

Write c=cosθ, s=sinθ. Since θ is constant, the multivariable chain rule gives ∂x​=c∂u​−s∂v​ and ∂y​=s∂u​+c∂v​. Applying these operators twice to a twice continuously differentiable function gives
gxx​=c2guu​−2csguv​+s2gvv​,gyy​=s2guu​+2csguv​+c2gvv​.
(30)
The mixed terms cancel and c2+s2=1, so
gxx​+gyy​=guu​+gvv​.​
(31)
This is rotational invariance of the Laplacian: an orthogonal change of coordinates preserves the trace of the Hessian matrix. A nonconstant angle would introduce additional derivatives and is not covered by this constant rotation calculation.

Expand u=3x2−2xy+3y2. The Lagrange multiplier equations for u−λv are (6−2λ)x−2y=0 and −2x+6y+λ=0, together with the constraint y=x2−1/4. The first two imply y=(3−λ)x and (6λ−16)x=λ. The value λ=8/3 would make the latter equation inconsistent, so division is allowed:
x=6λ−16λ​,y=6λ−16λ(3−λ)​.​
(35)
Insert these into x2−y−1/4=0 and multiply by (6λ−16)2. Simplification gives 2(3λ3−21λ2+48λ−32)=0, hence
3λ3−21λ2+48λ−32=0.​
(36)
The gradient of the constraint (2x,−1) is never zero, so the Lagrange multiplier condition is necessary at every constrained extremum. To establish a global minimum, parametrize the whole parabola by y=x2−1/4. The restricted function becomes
q(x)=3x4−2x3+23​x2+21​x+163​.
(37)
It is coercive: q(x)→∞ as ∣x∣→∞, so it attains a global minimum. Moreover q′′(x)=36(x−1/6)2+2>0, making it strictly convex and ensuring that this minimum is unique. This proves more than the permitted assumption of one real multiplier root.
The level sets of u are nested ellipses centered at the origin. In the rotated coordinates p=(x+y)/2​, q=(x−y)/2​, they have equation 2p2+4q2=constant, with the longer axis along x=y. The smallest one meeting the parabola is tangent to it at the unique minimizing point, approximately (x,y)=(−0.12654,−0.23399), where u≃0.15307 and λ≃1.15085.
