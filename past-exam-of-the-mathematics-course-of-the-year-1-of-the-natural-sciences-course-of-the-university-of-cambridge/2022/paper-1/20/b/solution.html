First suppose α>0. Differentiation under the integral sign, justified on compact positive parameter intervals by an integrable bound, gives
f′(α)=∫0∞​1+x2ln(α2+x2)​dx+2α2∫0∞​(x2+α2)(x2+1)dx​.
(98)
The first integral is f(α)/α. For α=1, use the partial fraction decomposition
(x2+α2)(x2+1)1​=1−α21​(x2+α21​−x2+11​).
(99)
The standard inverse tangent antiderivatives then give
∫0∞​(x2+α2)(x2+1)dx​=1−α21​(2απ​−2π​)=2α(1+α)π​.
(100)
The same value holds at α=1 by continuity, or by direct integration. Hence the proposed first-order linear differential equation is valid on the positive parameter range:
f′(α)−αf(α)​=1+απα​,α>0.​
(101)
Divide by α to recognise a quotient rule derivative:
(αf(α)​)′=1+απ​,f(α)=α[πln(1+α)+C].
(102)
To fix C, the value f(0)=0 alone is insufficient, since every displayed solution tends to zero there. Instead consider
limα→0+​αf(α)​=∫0∞​1+x2ln(x2)​dx.
(103)
This limit follows from the dominated convergence theorem: for 0<α≤1 and 0<x<1, the logarithm's absolute value is bounded by 2∣lnx∣+ln2; for x≥1, it is at most ln(1+x2). After division by 1+x2, these are integrable. The limiting improper integral is absolutely convergent, and the substitution rule x=1/t changes it into its own negative. Its value is therefore zero, giving C=0. Thus
∫0∞​1+x2αln(α2+x2)​dx=παln(1+α),α>0.​
(104)
The question calls α a real parameter, but this last formula and the proposed differential equation do not hold on the entire real axis. The defining integral is odd in α, because its logarithm depends on α2. Applying the positive-parameter result to ∣α∣ therefore gives the correct extension
f(α)=παln(1+∣α∣),α∈R,​
(105)
with f(0)=0. In particular, for α<0, the right-hand side of the differential equation must be −πα/(1−α); for every α=0 the unified expression is π∣α∣/(1+∣α∣). The logarithmic quadratic parameter integral therefore provides the requested identity on its valid range and the corrected answer elsewhere.
