Enthalpy (H=U+pV)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 20
Enthalpy is the state function obtained by adding pressure times volume to internal energy:
H=U+pV.
(1)
Here U denotes internal energy, also written E in some references. Like internal energy, enthalpy is measured in joules and is an extensive variable. Its definition packages the internal energy change and expansion work into one useful quantity for fixed-pressure calculations. It does not define a body's stored heat. [ref]
For a fixed amount and composition of a simple compressible system, the relation between neighboring equilibrium states is
dH=TdS+Vdp.
(2)
Here T is absolute temperature and S is entropy. Thus entropy and pressure are the natural variables of this thermodynamic potential, with partial derivatives T=(∂H/∂S)p​ and V=(∂H/∂p)S​. Fixed composition matters: changing composition adds further terms. The formula follows in one line from the equilibrium first law of thermodynamics identity:
dH=dU+pdV+Vdp=(TdS−pdV)+pdV+Vdp=TdS+Vdp.
(3)
The cancellation of pdV is the reason enthalpy replaces volume with pressure as a natural variable. This identity concerns equilibrium states; it does not identify TdS with actual irreversible heat transfer. [ref]
For a closed system undergoing a quasistatic process at constant pressure, require fixed amount and composition, only pressure-volume work, no friction, and unchanged bulk kinetic energy and potential energy. Then the actual first law of thermodynamics balance gives
δQ=dU+pdV=dH−Vdp,dp=0 ⟹ Qp​=ΔH.
(4)
The symbol Qp​ means transferred heat for this fixed-pressure process. If the piston example in heat additionally meets these conditions, its 20kJ of incoming heat equals ΔH, while ΔU=15kJ and pΔV=W=5kJ. The example shows exactly what enthalpy adds to internal energy: the expansion work required at the imposed pressure.
Within a single thermodynamic phase, heat capacity at constant pressure gives
Cp​=(∂T∂H​)p​,ΔH=∫T1​T2​​Cp​(T)dT
(5)
with the amount and composition fixed. Constant Cp​ gives ΔH=Cp​ΔT; dividing by mass gives specific heat capacity at constant pressure. At equilibrium melting, enthalpy can instead change through latent heat while temperature stays fixed. The sensible heat example therefore adds two single-thermodynamic phase enthalpy changes and one latent heat contribution.
The usefulness of enthalpy lies in matching the imposed constraint: a fixed-pressure experiment naturally reports ΔH, while fixed-volume heating with no other work reports ΔU. Neither equality is universal. Additional work or changing pressure alters the relation between supplied heat and ΔH; irreversible entropy production also prevents identifying actual δQ with TdS. The separate Joule-Thomson coefficient branch uses enthalpy to describe throttling under its own assumptions.

Stagnation enthalpy (h0​=h+u2/2)

Words: 42
Enthalpy OurBigBook.com
The sum of specific enthalpy and macroscopic kinetic energy per unit mass. In a steady adiabatic flow without shaft work, its change is determined by the change in external potential energy. At essentially constant external potential energy, it is conserved through a stationary nonradiating shock wave even though entropy increases.

Enthalpy of mixing (ΔHmix​)

Words: 29
Enthalpy OurBigBook.com
The mixture enthalpy minus the sum of the unmixed component enthalpies at the same temperature and pressure. Its sign does not alone determine miscibility, which depends on the full Gibbs free energy.

Reaction enthalpy (Δr​H)

Words: 17
Enthalpy OurBigBook.com
The enthalpy change per specified extent of a reaction. Scaling or reversing a chemical equation scales or reverses this quantity.

Molar enthalpy (hm​=H/n)

Words: 32
Enthalpy OurBigBook.com
Molar enthalpy is enthalpy per amount of substance n, measured in Jmol−1. With um​=U/n and molar volume vm​=V/n, hm​=um​+pvm​. Its fixed-pressure temperature derivative is the corresponding molar heat capacity Cp,m​, at fixed composition.

Isenthalpic process

Words: 37
Enthalpy OurBigBook.com
An isenthalpic process preserves enthalpy for a fixed amount of material, or preserves specific enthalpy between inlet and outlet when comparing a flowing fluid of fixed composition. It need not preserve temperature or entropy. An insulated throttle satisfying the steady-flow energy balance assumptions is an important example.

Steady-flow energy balance (Q˙​−W˙s​=m˙Δ(h+w2/2+gz))

Words: 220
Enthalpy OurBigBook.com
For steady flow through a fixed control volume with one inlet and one outlet, the first law of thermodynamics and conservation of mass give
Q˙​−W˙s​=m˙[h2​−h1​+2w22​−w12​​+g(z2​−z1​)].
(6)
Here m˙ is the mass flow rate, Q˙​ incoming heat per unit time, W˙s​ outgoing non-flow work per unit time, w the flow speed, z elevation and g gravitational acceleration. The specific enthalpy h=u+p/ρ includes both the transported internal energy per unit mass u and the pressure work needed to push volume through the boundary; ρ is mass density. The inlet supplies that pressure work, while the outlet consumes it to displace the downstream fluid.[ref]
For an insulated stationary throttle, require negligible heat transfer, no shaft or other non-flow work, and negligible changes in bulk kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy. Then h2​=h1​. With unchanged composition this is also equality of molar enthalpy. The upstream and downstream states must admit equilibrium pressure, temperature and property values, although the constriction itself is irreversible and can be far from thermodynamic equilibrium. Start-up storage of energy in the walls and fluid, heat leakage, or a large outlet speed invalidates the reduced balance. A nozzle can convert specific enthalpy into bulk kinetic energy even when it is insulated.
The same accounting for a fixed transported amount is U2​−U1​=p1​V1​−p2​V2​, hence U1​+p1​V1​=U2​+p2​V2​. This explains why dropping the two pressure work terms would give the wrong conserved quantity.[ref]

Joule-Thomson effect

Words: 433 Articles: 1
Enthalpy OurBigBook.com
The Joule-Thomson effect is the temperature response of a fluid flowing from higher to lower pressure through a valve or a plug of porous medium, when the throttle is insulated and the other steady-flow energy balance assumptions hold. Its equilibrium endpoints have equal specific enthalpy. The Joule-Thomson coefficient determines whether a small drop cools or warms; cooling is not universal.
A useful contrast is between three insulated expansions. In free expansion of a gas-only closed system into a vacuum, no external work occurs and U2​=U1​; an ideal gas returns to its initial equilibrium temperature. In a throttle, continuous flow carries pressure work across the boundary and preserves specific enthalpy under the stated assumptions; an ideal gas again has no endpoint temperature change. In a reversible adiabatic expansion of a loaded closed system, the gas supplies work, loses internal energy and cools, even if it is an ideal gas. That process preserves entropy; throttling generally produces entropy. For a small constant-enthalpy step, dH=TdS+Vdp=0 gives dS=−Vdp/T>0 when dp<0 and T,V>0. This equilibrium comparison computes endpoint entropy differences without supplying a reversible path through the plug.
The effect is useful in refrigerators because an appropriately prepared real gas can cool while passing through a simple valve. The inlet must lie in a suitable positive-Joule-Thomson coefficient region. The Joule-Thomson inversion curve makes that requirement precise; a pressure drop by itself is not a guarantee of refrigeration.

Measuring the Joule-Thomson effect with a porous plug

Words: 201
Joule-Thomson effect OurBigBook.com
The 2024 operating instructions for PHYWE's 04361-00 apparatus describe a frit between two gas chambers. A copper-coil heat exchanger first restores inlet temperature after cylinder-regulator cooling. An upstream manometer reads gauge pressure; the outlet stays at atmospheric pressure. Two NiCr-Ni thermocouples sit 2–5 mm from the frit. The digital meter's difference mode reports 0.01K precision.[ref]
The manual specifies at most 1bar gauge pressure, one hour of apparatus acclimatization, half an hour of meter warm-up, about 1.5 minutes of flow settling, then 10–15 seconds of averaging. It identifies a stagnant-gas zeroing error that shifts the fitted intercept and notes a maximum nitrogen signal of about 0.3K in this setup.[ref]
Define x=p1​−p2​>0 and y=T1​−T2​, so a small-drop fit is y≃μJT​x+c. A constant sensor offset c leaves the slope unchanged; pressure-dependent drift or heat leakage can alter it. The observable is the differential temperature slope across the plug, not cylinder-outlet cooling. Display precision alone does not establish accuracy or prove an isenthalpic process. This low-pressure, room-temperature apparatus does not map a complete Joule-Thomson inversion curve.

Joule-Thomson coefficient (μ=(∂T/∂p)H​)

Words: 846 Articles: 4
Enthalpy OurBigBook.com
The Joule-Thomson coefficient is the local temperature response to changing pressure at fixed enthalpy and fixed amount and composition:
μJT​=(∂p∂T​)H​,[μJT​]=KPa−1.
(7)
It describes the equilibrium endpoints of a Joule-Thomson effect experiment meeting the steady-flow energy balance assumptions. With Δp=p2​−p1​<0, a sufficiently small drop gives ΔT≃μJT​Δp. Thus μJT​>0 means cooling, μJT​<0 warming, and μJT​=0 no first-order change. At zero coefficient, a finite drop can still change temperature. For a chosen illustrative local value μJT​=0.20Kbar−1 and Δp=−0.50bar, the linear prediction is ΔT=−0.10K; these are stipulated numbers, not measured gas properties.
For a fixed-composition simple compressible system, the measurable form is
μJT​=Cp​T(∂V/∂T)p​−V​=Cp​V​(Tα−1).
(8)
Here Cp​ is the whole sample's heat capacity at constant pressure and α its volumetric coefficient of thermal expansion; alternatively use molar volume and molar heat capacity consistently. An ideal gas has α=1/T, so μJT​=0. Its enthalpy depends only on temperature; positive heat capacity at constant pressure then makes any such finite throttle preserve temperature. A real gas can have either sign. For Cp​>0, compare Tα with one: the thermal expansion response decides the sign, not insulation alone.[ref]
A short derivation uses dH=TdS+Vdp and the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂p)T​=−(∂V/∂T)p​:
dH=Cp​dT+[V−T(∂T∂V​)p​]dp.
(9)
Setting dH=0 gives the coefficient. These are equilibrium state function identities, not a claim of a reversible thermodynamic process inside the throttle. For a finite drop, solve the endpoint enthalpy equality or integrate
T2​−T1​=∫p1​p2​​μJT​(T(p),p)dp
(10)
along the constant-enthalpy equilibrium curve. The coefficient can change sign during that integration, as the Van der Waals throttling model illustrates.

Joule-Thomson inversion curve (μJT​(T,p)=0)

Words: 95 Articles: 1
Joule-Thomson coefficient OurBigBook.com
The Joule-Thomson inversion curve is the set of equilibrium temperature–pressure states where the Joule-Thomson coefficient vanishes. For positive heat capacity at constant pressure, its condition is Tα=1, with α the coefficient of thermal expansion. The curve separates locally cooling and warming throttle regions. A finite isenthalpic process can cross it, so the inlet sign alone does not fix the total temperature change. An ideal gas has zero coefficient everywhere, rather than an isolated dividing curve.

Joule-Thomson inversion temperature (Tinv​(p))

Words: 36
Joule-Thomson inversion curve OurBigBook.com
A Joule-Thomson inversion temperature is a temperature at which the Joule-Thomson coefficient is zero at a specified pressure. There can be more than one such temperature at that pressure; distinguish the upper and lower branches from a maximum or a dilute-gas limiting value.

Van der Waals throttling model

Words: 492
Joule-Thomson coefficient OurBigBook.com
A hypothetical Van der Waals equation model illustrates positive and negative Joule-Thomson coefficient regions and a finite throttle that first warms, then cools. Use the molar convention already distinguished in Van der Waals equation:
p=vm​−bm​RT​−vm2​am​​,um​=CV,m​T−vm​am​​,CV,m​=23​R.
(11)
Here vm​ is molar volume, R the molar gas constant, um​ internal energy per mole, and CV,m​ the stipulated constant molar heat capacity at fixed volume. The second equation is an additional caloric assumption: an equation of state alone does not specify the temperature dependence of internal energy. It satisfies the equilibrium identity (∂um​/∂vm​)T​=T(∂p/∂T)vm​​−p=am​/vm2​. The arbitrary energy reference is set to zero.
Define dimensionless variables r=vm​/bm​, θ=Rbm​T/am​, π=bm2​p/am​ and η=bm​hm​/am​, where hm​ is molar enthalpy. These differ from the critical-value scaling in Van der Waals law of corresponding states. The throttle calculation reduces to
π=r−1θ​−r21​,η=23​θ+πr−r1​,θ(r;η)=3/2+r/(r−1)η+2/r​.
(12)
Thus a prescribed inlet fixes η, and solving the first and last equations at the outlet pressure gives the final temperature. For an inlet (θ1​,π1​)=(0.8,0.12) and outlet π2​=0.025, the model gives θ2​≃0.752287, a decrease of 0.047713. The inlet (2.2,0.12) instead gives θ2​≃2.205786, an increase of 0.005786. The purple inlet (1.45,0.30) initially warms but ends at θ2​≃1.437585 for the same outlet pressure. These are reduced toy values, not kelvin readings or experimental evidence. No physical gas parameters were fitted.
For a short inversion derivation, implicit differentiation of the Van der Waals equation at fixed pressure gives
(∂T∂vm​​)p​=RT/(vm​−bm​)2−2am​/vm3​R/(vm​−bm​)​.
(13)
Insert this into T(∂vm​/∂T)p​=vm​ and substitute back into the equation of state. The Joule-Thomson inversion curve is
Tinv​=Rbm​2am​​(1−vm​bm​​)2,θinv​=2(1−1/r)2,πinv​=r22r−3​.
(14)
Positive inversion pressure requires r>3/2. Its maximum pressure is π=1/3 at r=3, while the upper dilute limit is θinv​→2. Using Van der Waals critical point values, the latter is Tinv,max​/Tc​=27/4. The inversion locus depends on the equation of state; the chosen molar heat capacity additionally determines how much temperature changes along each finite throttle.[ref]
The displayed region has θ>8/27, above the model's critical temperature, and uses only r>1, p>0, (∂p/∂vm​)T​<0 and positive Cp,m​. Subcritical calculations require attention to phase coexistence, metastability and the Maxwell construction; an unstable homogeneous continuation is not an equilibrium throttle endpoint. Even on a stable branch, this mean-field theory with constant CV,m​ is a qualitative model, not an accurate inversion map for a particular real gas. Phase transitions, changing composition, appreciable heat leakage or bulk kinetic energy changes require a different property calculation or steady-flow energy balance. At phase coexistence, the smooth single-thermodynamic phase coefficient formula cannot replace a balance involving the phase fraction.

Specific enthalpy (h)

Words: 80 Articles: 1
Enthalpy OurBigBook.com
Specific enthalpy is enthalpy per unit mass. For a simple compressible fluid of fixed composition, the first law of thermodynamics gives
dh=Tds+ρdp​,
(15)
where T is temperature, s is specific entropy, p is pressure, and ρ is mass density. Along an isentropic flow, dh=dp/ρ. For a perfect gas with constant specific-heat ratio γ, h=γp/[(γ−1)ρ].

Polytropic enthalpy (Q=(n+1)p/ρ)

Words: 35
Specific enthalpy OurBigBook.com
For p=Kρ1+1/n with K,n>0, the specific enthalpy relative to vacuum is Q=(n+1)Kρ1/n=(n+1)p/ρ. It obeys ∇Q=∇p/ρ and nDt​Q=−Q∇⋅u. The vacuum reference is fixed when these equations are combined with the polytropic equation of state.

Heat capacity at constant pressure (Cp​=(∂H/∂T)p​)

Words: 228 Articles: 3
Enthalpy OurBigBook.com
For a fixed-composition closed system, the heat capacity at fixed pressure is Cp​=(∂H/∂T)p​, with enthalpy H. Under the quasistatic process assumptions in heat capacity, only pressure-volume work is permitted, so dp=0 gives δQ=dH=Cp​dT. Thus supplied heat is Q=ΔH, or Q=Cp​ΔT within a single thermodynamic phase when Cp​ is constant. For an ideal gas, the expansion work explains why this exceeds the required heat at fixed volume for the same temperature increase. Its per-unit-mass counterpart is specific heat capacity at constant pressure.

Difference between constant-pressure and constant-volume heat capacities (CP​−CV​)

Heat capacity at constant pressure OurBigBook.com
For a simple compressible system,
CP​−CV​=T(∂T∂V​)P​(∂T∂P​)V​.
(16)
This follows by expressing the entropy as a function of temperature and volume and using a Maxwell relation.

Heat-capacity difference and mechanical stability (Cp​−CV​=TVαV2​/κT​≥0)

Words: 59
Difference between constant-pressure and constant-volume heat capacities OurBigBook.com
For a fixed-composition simple compressible system, a Maxwell relation gives Cp​−CV​=T(∂p/∂T)V​(∂V/∂T)p​. At fixed volume, the differential equation of state gives (∂p/∂T)V​=αV​/κT​, proving the displayed result. Positive absolute temperature and positive isothermal compressibility imply a nonnegative difference. It vanishes when thermal expansion does, so strict inequality requires a nonzero expansion coefficient. This stability argument includes nonideal internal-energy changes, unlike a purely ideal-gas expansion-work explanation.

Volume derivative form of the heat-capacity difference (Cp​−CV​=[(∂U/∂V)T​+p](∂V/∂T)p​)

Words: 71
Difference between constant-pressure and constant-volume heat capacities OurBigBook.com
For a fixed-composition simple compressible thermodynamic system with smooth equations of state, write the internal energy as U(T,V) and the enthalpy as H=U+pV. Differentiating H at constant pressure and applying the multivariable chain rule gives
Cp​=(∂T∂U​)p​+p(∂T∂V​)p​,(∂T∂U​)p​=(∂T∂U​)V​+(∂V∂U​)T​(∂T∂V​)p​.
(17)
Subtracting the heat capacity at constant volume proves the displayed identity. For an ideal gas, (∂U/∂V)T​=0 and (∂V/∂T)p​=NkB​/p, so Cp​−CV​=NkB​. For a nonideal material the internal energy change with volume contributes as well as expansion work.
