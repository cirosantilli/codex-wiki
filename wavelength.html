Wavelength (λ)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Partial differential equation Wave equation Wavenumber
OurBigBook.com Words: 939 Articles: 4
The wavelength λ is the spatial period of a monochromatic wave, measured at a fixed time along the wavevector. For a plane wave with a sinusoid as its profile, it is the distance between consecutive wave crests, or between any consecutive points with the same wave phase modulo 2π:
λ=∣k∣2π​=fvp​​.
(56)
Here k uses angular wavenumber, f is ordinary frequency, and vp​ is phase speed. A distance between arbitrary zero crossings need not be a wavelength: consecutive zeros of a sinusoid are only λ/2 apart, with opposite slopes. In a standing wave, consecutive nodes of a standing wave are also λ/2 apart; λ refers to the constituent travelling waves, even though the nodes of a standing wave do not travel.
Across a stationary interface between stationary linear media, the incident, reflected and transmitted monochromatic waves have the same frequency. The boundary conditions must hold at every time: terms oscillating with different angular frequencies cannot cancel throughout the entire time record. Each medium's dispersion relation then determines its own angular wavenumber and wavelength, so
f1​=f2​,λ1​λ2​​=vp,1​vp,2​​.
(57)
For electromagnetic radiation in a transparent medium with refractive index n, vp​=c0​/n and λ=λ0​/n, where c0​ is the vacuum speed of light. A synthetic example with λ0​=600nm and n=1.5 gives λ=400nm at the same frequency. This conservation argument assumes time-independent linear material response and a stationary interface in the chosen frame. A moving interface can produce a Doppler effect; time-dependent or nonlinear response can change or generate frequencies.[ref]
A broad frequency spectrum generally supplies many component wavelengths, rather than one wavelength for an entire pulse. Even two tones have both their individual wavelengths and a different overall repetition distance: the 2- and 8-cycle-per-metre sum in spatial sampling of a wave repeats after 0.50m, but contains the finer 0.125m structure. A narrow wave packet can have an approximate carrier wavelength; the wave envelope propagates approximately at group velocity, determined by the slope of the dispersion relation. Substituting that group velocity into λ=vp​/f generally gives the wrong carrier wavelength. Where the medium varies slowly, local wavelength gives a more useful description.
