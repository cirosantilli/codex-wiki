State of matter

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 824 Articles: 22
A state of matter is a broad category of material behavior. A solid resists a sustained change of shape, a liquid flows while remaining difficult to compress, and a gas expands to fill its container. These distinctions are useful first descriptions, but they do not enumerate all thermodynamic phases. Plasma adds collective behavior of charged particles, while liquid crystals show that flow and structural order need not disappear together.
Distinguish three questions: what broad state of matter does the material resemble, which thermodynamic phase is present, and what are its temperature, pressure and other state variables? Two samples at different temperatures can be in the same liquid thermodynamic phase. Conversely, crystal polymorphism gives different solid thermodynamic phases of the same substance, distinguished by their crystal structures. A phase transition between them need not change the broad state-of-matter category.
For an imagined substance with crystalline forms A and B, melting A produces a solid–liquid phase transition, while converting A to B produces a solid–solid phase transition. A sample containing A and B has two thermodynamic phases even though all its material is solid. Cutting one uniform crystal into two pieces does not create a second thermodynamic phase: count distinct equilibrium forms, rather than pieces or grains. This distinction matters when applying the phase rule.
A phase diagram locates stable thermodynamic phases using temperature and pressure. Its liquid–gas phase coexistence curve ends at a liquid-gas critical point, around which a path connects liquid and gas without a first-order phase transition. Broad categories can therefore remain useful without a sharp boundary.[ref]
