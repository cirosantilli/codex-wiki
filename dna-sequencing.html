DNA sequencing

 Home Biology Genetics Molecular genetics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 9
DNA sequencing determines the order of nucleotide bases in DNA. Sequence data can test molecular rearrangements, as in the functional plastid-to-nucleus gene transfer experiment, or support phylogenetics after a suitable sequence alignment.
An ordered nucleotide sequence answers a different question from fragment size. The eight-nucleotide strands ACGTTAGC and AACCGGTT have equal lengths and identical counts of adenine, cytosine, guanine and thymine, yet different orders. A length-compatible gel band therefore cannot identify either strand by itself. Sanger sequencing makes length informative by deliberately coupling each stopping position to the identity of its last added nucleotide.
That coupling is the beautiful experimental idea: convert an invisible order into a collection of ordered endpoints. Sanger ladder reading recovers the newly synthesized strand; base pairing and the reverse complement then connect that readout to the original template. The distinction also matters when checking a mutation: a reliable local nucleotide sequence supports a particular molecular change, while its effect on a phenotype requires additional evidence.
