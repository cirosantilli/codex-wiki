Modular arithmetic (Z/mZ)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 14
Modular arithmetic performs addition and multiplication on integers while remembering only their remainders for a fixed positive integer modulus m. The relation a≡b(modm) means that m divides a−b: the two integers lie in the same congruence class. For example, −3, 2 and 7 represent one class modulo 5. Choosing the least nonnegative residue gives exactly m representatives, 0,1,…,m−1, even though each congruence class contains infinitely many integers.
A clock supplies a familiar example. Represent the twelve positions by 0,1,…,11, with 0 at the position usually labelled twelve. Starting at ten and moving seven positions gives
10+7=17≡5(mod12).
(129)
The integer congruence records the final position; it discards the number of complete revolutions. Adding another twelve changes the elapsed count while preserving the position. The picture also shows a single congruence class modulo five spread along the number line.
The useful surprise is that discarding this information still preserves the arithmetic. If a≡a′ and b≡b′ modulo m, then a+b≡a′+b′ and ab≡a′b′ modulo m. Thus a large calculation can reduce its intermediate integers after every step. For instance,
10+7≡5(mod12),10⋅7≡10(mod12),102≡4(mod12).
(130)
Replacing 10 by −2 gives the same answers with smaller intermediate values. The congruence classes with these operations form the quotient ring Z/mZ; their addition alone forms a cyclic group. This passage from integers to a finite arithmetic system is the setting for modular exponentiation, primality testing and the RSA cryptosystem. [ref]
Multiplicative cancellation needs extra care. Modulo six, 2⋅1≡2⋅4, but 1≡4: multiplication by two has merged distinct congruence classes. Cancelling a factor while retaining the integer modulus is justified when that factor has a modular multiplicative inverse, equivalently when it is coprime to the integer modulus. A linear congruence can consequently have no solutions, one solution class, or several solution classes. The Chinese remainder theorem supplies a complementary operation: splitting a calculation across coprime integer moduli and reconstructing its class modulo their product.
Small remainders can also certify impossibility. A perfect square has residue zero or one modulo four, as checking the four congruence classes shows. Therefore an integer equation x2=4k+2 has no solution. The converse direction needs care: a permitted residue does not guarantee an integer solution, since 5≡1(mod4) but five is not a perfect square. Such local tests provide necessary conditions for Diophantine equations without replacing the full problem.
In Disquisitiones arithmeticae (1801), Carl Friedrich Gauss defines integer congruence in article 1 and adopts the symbol ≡ in article 2. Article 12 derives divisibility tests from powers of ten and explains checking arithmetic by its remainders. These passages show a systematic treatment of the same idea; they are not a claim that remainders were first used in 1801. [ref]
