Mixture measurements and the Gibbs-Duhem constraint

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Gibbs-Duhem equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 316
A concrete motivating experiment is the acetone–chloroform mixture in Truman State University's Liquid-Vapor Equilibrium of a Binary System, Procedure and Figure 1. A two-neck flask feeds a distillation head with a water-cooled laboratory condenser, condensate sampling arm and temperature probe. Starting with one pure liquid, the procedure adds the other component successively and waits for stable temperature with returning condensate. Small paired samples from the flask and condensate are read on an Abbé refractometer; a tabulated refractive index–composition relation gives the two compositions. Controls include three-point thermometer calibration, corrected atmospheric pressure readings, and agreement of the initial pure-liquid and condensate refractive indices with the reference value. Small withdrawals and little retained condensate reduce disturbance of composition. Sample evaporation can bias the readout. The inferred paired mole fractions, temperature and pressure describe vapor-liquid equilibrium, subject to the conversion from mass fraction to mole fraction and to representative sampling. This teaching protocol reports no measured results of a particular student run.[ref]
How does such a measurement expose activity coefficients? Let xi​ and yi​ be the mole fractions in the liquid and gas thermodynamic phases, respectively, and let pi∗​(T) be the pure-component vapor pressure. Assume the gas thermodynamic phase is an ideal gas mixture and neglect the change of the pure-liquid reference chemical potential between its saturation pressure and the experimental pressure. Equality of each component's chemical potential between the two thermodynamic phases then gives
RTln(xi​γi​)=RTlnpi∗​(T)yi​p​,γi​≃xi​pi∗​(T)yi​p​.
(98)
This turns the paired composition readings into estimates of the activity coefficients. The approximation needs refinement when the gas departs appreciably from ideal gas behavior or the neglected liquid pressure effect matters.
The apparatus measures a boiling-composition series in which temperature changes as the mixture changes. Its readings therefore cannot be inserted directly into the fixed-temperature, fixed-pressure activity coefficient constraint. They motivate the more general task: test whether the measured mixture properties can arise from a common Gibbs free energy while accounting for changing state variables. A failure can reveal incompatible property fits or experimental bias; a pass supplies a consistency check, not proof that every reading is correct.
