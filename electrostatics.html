Electrostatics

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism
OurBigBook.com Words: 6k Articles: 43
Electrostatics studies time-independent electric charge distributions and their electric fields. In vacuum, its local equations in SI units are
∇⋅E=ϵ0​ρ​,∇×E=0,E=−∇Φ,∇2Φ=−ϵ0​ρ​.
(173)
Here ρ is charge density, ϵ0​ is vacuum permittivity, and Φ is electric potential. Gauss's law supplies the divergence equation; the static form of Faraday's law of induction supplies the zero curl. The Poisson equation becomes Laplace's equation where there is no electric charge. Prescribed electric charges give electric fields through Coulomb's law and the superposition principle; mobile electric charges on an electrical conductor instead require solving for their distribution together with the electric potential. [ref].
The useful distinction is between prescribed sources and prescribed boundary conditions. On a grounded electrical conductor, an external connection fixes its electric potential relative to an electrical reference potential and allows electric charge to enter or leave. On an isolated electrical conductor, total electric charge is fixed and its constant electric potential is an unknown. A neutral isolated electrical conductor can therefore have a nonzero local surface charge density through electrostatic induction. Equal electric potentials also do not imply equal electric charges: charge sharing between distant connected spheres gives a simple geometric example. [ref].
Electrostatics is useful because a spatial boundary value problem replaces the need to follow every moving charge carrier during charge relaxation. It describes the settled state of a capacitor and explains electrostatic shielding of sensitive equipment. Its assumptions must match the apparatus: stationary boundaries and sources, negligible time variation of the magnetic field, and enough time for charge relaxation. A metal carrying a maintained electric current through finite electrical resistance generally needs a nonzero internal electric field by Ohm's law; it is not a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium. Static macroscopic electric charge also does not mean that every Electron is motionless. [ref].
