Early two-stage electron microscope experiment

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Electron optics Electron microscope
OurBigBook.com Words: 225
Ernst Ruska and Max Knoll developed electron optics while improving the writing spot of a cathode-ray oscilloscope.[ref] A structured object tests image formation more strongly than a single bright spot: each feature must retain its own image position.
On 7 April 1931, a cold cathode source and two short magnetic coils imaged aperture grids at 50kV. A platinum grid preceded the first coil; a bronze grid preceded the second. The fluorescent screen showed one-stage grid images and the first grid reimaged through both stages. Comparing these patterns tested successive image formation, not merely beam concentration. Heating could destroy even metal grids. This prototype did not establish resolution beyond an optical microscope.[ref]
In 1933, Ruska measured 3mm focal lengths at 75kV with a pole-piece lens, then built an instrument giving 12000-fold magnification.[ref] TU Berlin dates images surpassing conventional optical microscope resolution to 1933.[ref]
A fluorescent screen converts the spatial electron distribution into visible light.[ref] A collector reporting only total electric current would discard that spatial pattern. In a two-stage image relay, the first lens makes an intermediate real image; the second treats that image as its object.
