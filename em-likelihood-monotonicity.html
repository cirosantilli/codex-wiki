EM likelihood monotonicity

OurBigBook.com Words: 173
For Q(θ∣θ0​)=Eθ0​​[logpθ​(Y,Z)∣Y], the Jensen inequality gives
logpθ​(Y)−logpθ0​​(Y)≥Q(θ∣θ0​)−Q(θ0​∣θ0​).
(12)
Thus any expectation-maximization algorithm M-step that increases Q cannot decrease the observed-data likelihood function.[ref]
For a short proof, assume common support and finite expressions, and keep the E-step conditional distribution qt​(z)=pθ(t)​(z∣y) fixed. The concavity of the logarithm and Jensen's inequality give
ℓ(θ)=logEqt​​[qt​(Z)pθ​(y,Z)​]≥Eqt​​[logpθ​(y,Z)−logqt​(Z)]=F(qt​,θ).
(13)
This evidence lower bound equals Q(θ∣θ(t))−Eqt​​logqt​(Z). At θ=θ(t), the ratio inside the expectation is the constant pθ(t)​(y), so equality holds. During the M-step, the second term is constant; increasing Q raises a lower bound that touches the current log-likelihood. Hence
ℓ(θ(t+1))≥F(qt​,θ(t+1))≥F(qt​,θ(t))=ℓ(θ(t)).
(14)
Increasing likelihood values alone do not establish convergence of the statistical parameters or attainment of a global maximum. In a differentiable finite mixture model, the gradient of ℓ equals the candidate-parameter gradient of Q evaluated at the current fit. Thus an interior fixed point of an exact M-step is a stationary point, which need not be a local maximum; the Identical-mean EM trap is an explicit counterexample. Small changes in log-likelihood can also occur before the means have settled, so inspecting both is useful.
