Debye T-cubed test (CV,mlattice​≃βT3)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Heat Heat capacity
OurBigBook.com Words: 623
The Debye T-cubed test asks whether a bulk three-dimensional crystal's lattice molar heat capacity approaches a cubic law well below its Debye temperature TD​. It tests the low-frequency acoustic phonon branches in the Debye model, rather than asserting that every contribution to a real sample's heat capacity is cubic. Peter Debye's continuum description makes a striking connection: a thermal measurement can reveal the low-frequency vibrational density of states without resolving individual vibrations.[ref]
For a monatomic bulk model, the Debye model has vibrational density of states g(ω)=9Nω2/ωD3​ up to the Debye frequency ωD​, containing 3N normal modes. With x=ℏω/(kB​T), the thermal vibrational internal energy becomes
Ulattice​=9NkB​T(TD​T​)3∫0TD​/T​ex−1x3​dx.
(46)
The reduced Planck constant is ℏ, the Boltzmann constant is kB​, and N counts chemical atoms. For T≪TD​, replace the upper limit by infinity. The integral is π4/15, as in the Stefan–Boltzmann law derivation. Differentiating at fixed volume gives, per mole of chemical atoms,
CV,mlattice​≃βT3,β=5TD3​12π4R​,[β]=Jmol−1K−4,
(47)
where R is the molar gas constant. The cubic exponent comes from three-dimensional acoustic phonon branches with linear low-wavevector dispersion relations. The coefficient encodes their propagation speeds. Formula-unit normalization in a compound requires its number of chemical atoms per formula unit; do not silently substitute moles of formula units for moles of chemical atoms.
For a simple metal in its nonsuperconducting state, well below both Debye temperature and electron Fermi temperature, include electronic heat capacity:
Cp,m​(T)≃γT+βT3,TCp,m​​≃γ+βT2.
(48)
This approximation also requires negligible difference between constant-pressure and constant-volume heat capacities at the measurement precision, and no significant magnetic, nuclear or Schottky anomaly contribution in the fit interval. Thus linear regression of Cp,m​/T against T2 separates an electronic intercept from a lattice slope. For an insulating crystal with no relevant low-energy magnetic excitations, the electronic term can instead be negligible; a superconductivity transition changes the electronic response.[ref]
After subtracting calorimeter addenda, Brando's second gold sample, 72.6mg, gave a linear Cp,m​/T versus T2 plot in the nonsuperconducting state. The reported electronic intercept was 7.0×10−4Jmol−1K−2; deviations from reference heat capacities were below 2%.[ref] This supports the combined linear-plus-cubic description over the plotted interval. Because the platform subtraction used reference gold, it is also an instrument validation with a shared material standard, rather than a wholly independent determination of the Debye model from an uncalibrated instrument.
For a worked original inference, stipulate values of y=Cp,m​/T equal to (0.001100,0.002300,0.004300)Jmol−1K−2 at T=(1,2,3)K. Assign independent u(y)=0.000020Jmol−1K−2 and negligible temperature error. Equal-weight linear regression on x=T2 gives
γ=(7.00±0.20)×10−4Jmol−1K−2,β=(4.000±0.035)×10−4Jmol−1K−4.
(49)
The points sit exactly on the stipulated line; these standard uncertainties come from the assigned input precision, not an estimate from zero residual scatter. With R=8.314462618Jmol−1K−1, inversion gives
TD​=(5β12π4R​)1/3=169.38K,TD​u(TD​)​=3βu(β)​,TD​=(169.4±0.5)K.
(50)
At 2K, the electronic and lattice molar heat capacities are respectively 0.0014 and 0.0032Jmol−1K−1. Their equality occurs at γ/β​=1.323K: even in this clean model, the total heat capacity approaches a linear, rather than cubic, law as T tends to zero in the normal state.
A straight line over a narrow interval is a useful diagnostic, not a universal proof. Inspect residuals and repeat the fit over several upper and lower temperature cutoffs; curvature can signal higher lattice terms, an unremoved Schottky anomaly, or a faulty calorimeter addenda subtraction. Correlated thermometer calibration and sample-amount errors affect both axes and can dominate the illustrative linear regression uncertainty. Low-dimensional samples, additional low-energy excitations and proximity to a phase transition need other models. The test is valuable precisely because its failures can reveal physics beyond the bulk acoustic approximation.
