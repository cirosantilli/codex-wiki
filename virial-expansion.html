Virial expansion

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 8
The virial expansion describes the first departures from the ideal gas law as a power series in number density. For a homogeneous, single-species gas in three dimensions at thermal equilibrium, write n=N/V, with V the volume, and define the dimensionless compressibility factor Z by
Z=nkB​Tp​=1+B2​(T)n+B3​(T)n2+⋯,p=nkB​T[1+B2​(T)n+B3​(T)n2+⋯].
(41)
Here p is absolute pressure, T is absolute thermodynamic temperature, and kB​ is the Boltzmann constant. The second virial coefficient determines the initial slope away from Z=1; higher coefficients describe further corrections. The symbol Z here is an equation of state ratio, not a canonical partition function. [ref]
The left panel sketches the measurement principle; the right panel uses deliberately chosen polynomials, Z=1+bx+6x2 with x=nℓ3 and b=±2, where ℓ is a reference length. These curves are not measured data. Their initial slopes have opposite signs, but both bend because of the quadratic term. Using a finite secant from x=0 to x=h gives (Z(h)−1)/h=b+6h, rather than b: reducing the fitting range reduces this particular truncation bias. This algebra explains why measuring one nonideal pressure is insufficient to extract a reliable second virial coefficient.
Waxman and Hastings's 1971 Burnett experiment used sample and expansion chambers in a thermostatic bath, with a valve between them and a pressure transducer linked to a piston gauge. They measured successive isothermal expansions: evacuate the expansion chamber, open the valve, record the equilibrated pressure, isolate and evacuate that chamber again, then repeat. This avoids separately measuring the absolute gas amount and chamber volumes. For carbon dioxide at 50∘C, their measurements below 35 bar gave a molar second virial coefficient of approximately −102.2cm3mol−1. [ref]
An idealized reduction makes the signal explicit. Let q=(VA​+VB​)/VA​ be the calibrated chamber volume ratio, pi​ the pre-expansion pressure, and pf​ the post-expansion pressure. Particle conservation during one valve opening gives nf​=ni​/q. At the same temperature, division of the two equations of state yields
pf​pi​​=qZ(ni​/q,T)Z(ni​,T)​=q+(q−1)kB​TB2​(T)​pi​+O(pi2​).
(42)
Thus the intercept determines q, while the dilute-limit slope determines the second virial coefficient. In the actual experiment, helium runs calibrated the cell constant and chamber deformation corrections were included. Agreement of the carbon dioxide low-pressure intercept with the helium calibration, and the absence of an anomalous low-pressure upturn, checked for gas adsorption on chamber walls. [ref]
For a limited dilute-gas fit, hold temperature fixed, allow thermal equilibrium after every expansion, calibrate absolute pressure and the chamber ratio, and check leaks and sample composition. Repeat the analysis with a smaller upper pressure cutoff and an added curvature term; compare the resulting second virial coefficients with their uncertainties. Too wide a range mixes in higher coefficients; too narrow a range makes the nonideal signal hard to distinguish from instrument error. The displayed chamber equation neglects deformation, dead spaces and adsorption, so it is a teaching reduction rather than the complete experimental analysis.
Units matter. With number density in m−3, B2​ has units m3 and B3​ has units m6. For molar density c=n/NA​ in molm−3, the same equation of state is
Z=cRTp​=1+B2,m​c+B3,m​c2+⋯,B2,m​=NA​B2​,B3,m​=NA2​B3​.
(43)
Here NA​ is the Avogadro constant and R=NA​kB​ is the molar gas constant; the molar coefficients have units m3mol−1 and m6mol−2. A coefficient tabulated per mole must therefore multiply molar density, not number density.
For the classical formulas below, use a nondegenerate gas with thermal de Broglie wavelength λth​ satisfying nλth3​≪1, negligible quantum corrections to relative motion, and a stable, sufficiently short-ranged pair potential. Diluteness must also control the omitted interaction terms: ∣B2​n∣≪1 alone is not enough when positive and negative contributions cancel. A finite truncation is a local low-number density approximation, not an equation of state for a dense liquid or across a phase transition. [ref] [ref]
