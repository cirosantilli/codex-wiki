Heat (Q)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 6k Articles: 50
Heat is energy transferred across a thermodynamic system's boundary because of a temperature difference. It is measured in joules, like work and internal energy. A hot cup transfers heat to cooler surroundings; its temperature describes its state, while the transferred heat describes a process. The cup stores internal energy, rather than a substance called heat. [ref]
For a closed system with unchanged bulk kinetic energy and potential energy, choose Q>0 for heat entering and W>0 for work done by the system. The first law of thermodynamics gives
ΔU=Q−W,dU=δQ−δW.
(123)
Here U is the stored internal energy. For example, a sealed piston cylinder receiving 20kJ of heat and delivering 5kJ of work gains 15kJ of internal energy. A closed system retains its matter while exchanging energy; it need not be an isolated system. [ref]
Unlike internal energy, heat is not a state function: different paths between the same equilibrium states can transfer different Q, with compensating differences in work. The symbol δQ records this dependence on the path; there is no stored quantity whose difference is always Q. The two paths in first law of thermodynamics illustrate this directly. Supplied heat can raise temperature as sensible heat, change a phase fraction through latent heat, or help supply expansion work. Measuring temperature alone therefore does not determine transferred heat.
For a reversible thermodynamic process, δQrev​=TdS, where T is absolute temperature and S is entropy. This identification requires reversibility: an irreversible adiabatic process has Q=0 and can nevertheless increase entropy. Likewise Q=ΔH, with enthalpy H, requires the particular fixed-pressure conditions in enthalpy. These distinctions make heat useful as a precise boundary-transfer quantity in heat engines, refrigerators and heating calculations.
Historically, James Prescott Joule's paddle experiments showed that stirring water with input work could raise its temperature, helping establish conservation of energy. The lesson is still useful: a temperature rise can result from work even without incoming heat. [ref]
