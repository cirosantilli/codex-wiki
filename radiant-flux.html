Radiant flux (Φ=dQ/dt)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Radiometry
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 4
Radiant flux is the power carried by electromagnetic radiation: radiation energy transferred per unit time, measured in watts, W=Js−1. Specify what is counted: all radiation emitted by a source, radiation within one beam, or radiation intercepted by a photodetector. Writing Q(t) for the cumulative energy crossing the specified boundary gives
Φe​(t)=dtdQ​,Φe​=t2​−t1​Q(t2​)−Q(t1​)​,Q(t2​)−Q(t1​)=∫t1​t2​​Φe​(t)dt.
(1)
The first expression is an instantaneous derivative; the second is an average over the stated time interval. If 0.020J arrives during 0.010s, the mean radiant flux is 2W. The photodetector's absorbed power equals this incident radiant flux only if it absorbs all of the incident radiation.
Keeping radiant flux fixed while widening a beam reduces its irradiance, the incident power per receiving area. Radiance additionally resolves the propagation direction and has units Wm−2sr−1; an optical camera can collect different radiant fluxes from sources with the same radiance but different apparent solid angles. Luminous flux, measured in lumens, weights the optical spectrum by the human eye's standardized response instead of counting energy equally at every wavelength. Equal radiant fluxes need not give equal luminous fluxes.[ref]
Radiant power also differs from the optical power of an optical lens, which is reciprocal focal length in dioptres. Likewise, astronomical radiative flux is power per area. Stating the units prevents these shared words from hiding different physical quantities.
