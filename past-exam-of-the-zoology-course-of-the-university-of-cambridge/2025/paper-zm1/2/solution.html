The transition places vertebrate origins within a prolonged assembly of animal body plans, ecological interactions and preservational opportunities. It does not identify one boundary at which a complete modern vertebrate suddenly appeared. Three questions must be separated: when the relevant lineages diverged, when diagnostic features evolved, and when those features became recoverable as fossils.
The Ediacaran precedes the Cambrian, whose beginning is approximately 539 million years ago. Ediacaran communities already contained macroscopic organisms and evidence of animal movement and feeding. Kimberella, for example, has been interpreted from its body organization as a mollusc-like bilaterian; its relevance is that substantial animal differentiation predates the boundary, rather than that it is a vertebrate ancestor.[ref] trace fossils record activities that may otherwise leave no body remains. Increasingly elaborate movement and sediment disturbance reveal ecological capacities, but an isolated burrow does not identify a notochord, a head or a particular branch of the animal phylogenetic tree.
Across the transition, animal skeletons, predation, burrowing and water-column communities became more prominent. Their appearance changes both biology and observation. Resistant skeletons improve preservation, while bioturbation can destroy the sedimentary surfaces that had preserved some earlier soft-bodied communities. Different preservational windows reveal different subsets of life. An apparent replacement in the fossil record can therefore combine real ecological turnover with taphonomic bias. One should compare anatomy and environments across several deposits rather than treating all Ediacaran absences as biological absences.
The Cambrian exceptional deposits provide positive evidence. Haikouichthys and Myllokunmingia from Chengjiang reveal small fish-like animals with combinations of axial, muscular and branchial structures interpreted as early vertebrate organization.[ref] Metaspriggina from the Burgess Shale adds evidence from a distinct assemblage, including prominent eyes, segmented musculature, organized into myomeres, and serial branchial supports.[ref] These are more informative than an undifferentiated claim that “fish existed”: they allow particular characters to be mapped onto alternative phylogenetic trees.
A plausible sequence involves a swimming chordate organization with a notochord and segmental musculature, followed by elaboration of anterior sensory and feeding systems along the vertebrate lineage. Nevertheless, fossil compressions can obscure boundaries or superimpose unrelated structures. Confidence should come from repeated specimens, bilateral agreement, positional relationships and combinations of characters, rather than resemblance of one dark patch to an organ. A structure absent from a compressed specimen may be unpreserved. Nor does a plausible early vertebrate have to be a member of the living vertebrate crown group, still less a direct ancestor of a living species. A stem group can contain extinct side branches showing different combinations of ancestral and derived states.
Living organisms constrain the reconstruction without forming a ladder. Molecular analyses place tunicates, rather than cephalochordates, as the closest living relatives of vertebrates.[ref] A modern lancelet is consequently a comparative relative with its own history, not an unchanged stage through which vertebrates passed. The same warning applies to living hagfish and lampreys. Their anatomy can reveal inherited features and secondary specializations; simplicity alone cannot polarize a character.
The New Head hypothesis connects cranial sensory and feeding innovations with neural crest and ectodermal placodes. This is a developmental explanation for assembling a more elaborate head. Experiments on living chordates can test where cells originate, which structures they form and which regulatory interactions are needed. The fossil presence of eyes or branchial supports cannot directly demonstrate the embryonic migration of neural crest. Conversely, a conserved regulatory gene alone does not establish that a complete modern head existed in an Ediacaran ancestor. Developmental, molecular and anatomical evidence address complementary parts of the argument.[ref]
Ecological context helps explain why these capacities might diversify. More active swimming, prey detection and food processing can create opportunities and selective pressures for improved sensory and locomotor coordination. molecular oxygen availability constrains the metabolic activities that can be sustained. Erik A. Sperling and colleagues compared modern low-oxygen animal communities and related the scarcity of carnivory at low oxygen levels to hypotheses about Cambrian ecological expansion.[ref] This motivates a mechanism; environmental correlation alone neither dates the vertebrate ancestor nor establishes oxygenation as a sufficient single cause.
The useful conclusion is a constrained historical sequence, with explicit gaps. Ediacaran evidence establishes antecedent animal complexity; Cambrian exceptional preservation documents recognizable early vertebrate combinations; comparative phylogenetics and evolutionary developmental biology test their placement and assembly. molecular clocks can estimate earlier divergences, but their dependence on rates and fossil calibration prevents them from turning every absence into a precise origin date.
