Southern fossils have changed the geography, timing and anatomical character of mammaliaform evolution, while exposing how strongly a northern fossil record had shaped earlier expectations. Their importance is not that every southern lineage arose in the south. A dated occurrence establishes presence and a minimum age; an evolutionary origin requires an argument from phylogenetics and biogeography. Nor do modern continental outlines reproduce the connections of Gondwana.
A particularly consequential example is Ambondro mahabo, represented by a jaw fragment with lower teeth from Middle Jurassic Madagascar, approximately 167±2 Ma. The teeth possess a basined heel associated with the crushing component of tribosphenic molars, which combine shearing and crushing in a single occlusal system. Its age extended the then-known record of this dental construction substantially backwards and challenged a simple northern origin inferred from better-sampled northern localities. The material nevertheless consists of a very restricted part of the animal, and the matching upper dentition was not directly observed. Assigning the entire skeleton, physiology or reproductive mode from these teeth would go beyond the evidence. [ref]
The discovery also changed the character of the phylogenetic question. One hypothesis groups several southern forms in Australosphenida, associated with the ancestry of monotremes, and proposes that a tribosphenic-like construction arose independently of northern Boreosphenida, including therians. In that interpretation a highly functional tooth arrangement is convergent evolution, not a unique certificate of therian ancestry. A competing analysis supports a single origin of tribospheny and rearranges the relationships of the relevant southern taxa. [ref] [ref] The important lesson is not to declare one historical topology established by the striking tooth alone. The choice depends on which cusps and basins are judged instances of biological homology, character coding, inclusion of other anatomical regions, and the taxon sample. Nor does a Middle Jurassic tribosphenic tooth by itself date the split between living marsupials and placental mammals.
South American monotreme material further undermined the equation of present distribution with evolutionary birthplace. Early Paleocene Monotrematum, approximately 60–65 Ma, has teeth with affinities to the platypus lineage. Living monotremes are restricted to Australasia, but that restriction cannot be projected unchanged into the past. [ref] South America, Antarctica and Australia offered changing opportunities for connection, fragmentation and movement. A South American occurrence is compatible with a geographically extensive southern history; it does not uniquely establish the direction of dispersal or prove that Monotrematum was the direct ancestor of the living platypus. The distinction between a crown group and related members of its stem group is essential when attaching such fossils to divergence-time estimates.
Latest Cretaceous Madagascar has contributed more than isolated tooth surprises. The cranium of Vintana, around 70 Ma, supplied extensive new characters for the formerly tooth-dominated Gondwanatheria. Its robust feeding apparatus and unusual cranial combination permit tests of relationships and ecology that were unavailable from a few teeth. Yet a mosaic of familiar and unusual features is precisely the situation in which a single apparently diagnostic character can mislead. The placement of gondwanatherians therefore needs broad anatomical and taxonomic comparison, including other extinct groups, rather than assignment to a living order by superficial resemblance. [ref]
The approximately 66 Ma skeleton of Adalatherium expanded that evidence into the postcranial skeleton. It documents a relatively large, several-kilogram non-therian mammal with an unusual combination of cranial, dental, vertebral and limb features. This contradicts the caricature that all Mesozoic mammaliaforms were anatomically interchangeable tiny insect-eaters. Madagascar's long isolation provides a plausible context for its evolutionary distinctiveness, but the claim that isolation caused a particular feature remains an explanatory hypothesis. One needs comparative island and continental samples to distinguish an island effect from inherited lineage-specific anatomy. [ref]
Australia's early Eocene Djarthia, approximately 55 Ma, addresses a different issue: the relationships and movement of marsupials. Its ankle material supports a position close to the base of Australidelphia, a group containing the living Australian orders and the South American Microbiotheria. This is stronger evidence than assuming that every small Australian jaw belongs inside a modern Australian family. [ref] It is compatible with a history involving South America and Antarctica, but a branching order is not a map of a single migration. An extinct distribution can be extensive, intermediate populations can disappear, and the oldest sampled fossil need not occur near the place where a lineage originated. Also, the older and more inclusive Metatheria must not be confused with the living marsupial crown group.
Together these discoveries change how large-scale history is tested. First, expanding southern sampling can overturn a supposed absence that arose from collecting intensity, exposure of suitable rocks or preservation. taphonomy and the concentration of many early records on durable teeth must be modelled before comparing continental diversity. Second, new skeletons can change the inferred phylogenetic tree, and therefore whether a tooth adaptation counts as one origin or several. Third, fossil calibration constrains the minimum age of a lineage, with explicit uncertainty in both geological age and phylogenetic assignment. Fourth, continental drift changes which dispersal routes were physically plausible. Shared distributions across former Gondwana may reflect vicariance, later dispersal, or a mixture; the geological separation must be compared with a defensible lineage age rather than used as its automatic explanation.
From the Jurassic through the Palaeogene, these independent discoveries have cumulatively changed both the anatomical evidence and its interpretation. They reveal a more diverse southern mammaliaform history and force explicit tests of convergence, missing data and geographic sampling, rather than replacing an assumed northern centre with an equally assumed southern centre.
