A node-based name selects a common ancestor from included reference taxa; a branch-based name selects a lineage by including one reference and excluding another. Both designate a clade, so both include every descendant of the selected ancestor. The distinction concerns the rule locating that ancestor, not whether descendants are included. Neither requires its members to share a particular modern appearance.[ref]
For a node-based clade definition, choose internal phylogenetic specifiers A and B. Let m(A,B) be their last common ancestor, and let D(v) include ancestor v and all its descendants. Then the named group is
N(A,B)=D(m(A,B)).
(1)
It is the smallest clade containing both references. For a branch-based clade definition, choose an internal reference A and an external reference Z. The named group is the largest clade containing A but excluding Z: organisms sharing a more recent common ancestor with A than with Z. On a rooted phylogenetic tree, this selects the A-side lineage after its separation from the lineage containing Z. These are also called minimum-clade definitions and maximum-clade definitions. The names describe inclusion constraints, not distances measured from branch lengths.[ref]
An informative avian node example is crown Aves, here anchored to living ostrich and sparrow lineages. Their last common ancestor is the ancestor of living Palaeognathae and Neognathae; the name includes that ancestor and all descendants, including extinct descendants within either branch. An informative branch example is Panaves, the avian total group: the largest clade including living birds but excluding living crocodilians. With standard archosaur relationships it includes Aves, extinct avian stem relatives, nonavian dinosaurs and pterosaurs. The external reference is decisive: the definition asks which side of the bird–crocodilian split an organism occupies, not whether it flies. These crown and total usages were distinguished explicitly in the avian nomenclatural analysis by Jacques A. Gauthier and Kevin de Queiroz.[ref]
Consider a simplified cladogram in which the crocodilian lineage separates first, followed on the avian side by pterosaurs, successive nonavian dinosaur branches, Archaeopteryx, other extinct avian branches and finally the living-bird crown divergence. Archaeopteryx belongs to Panaves but lies outside crown Aves. A fossil on the palaeognath branch after the palaeognath–neognath divergence belongs to both. A crocodilian belongs to neither. Finding a still earlier bird-line fossil would extend the known fossil history of Panaves without automatically changing the age or known membership of crown Aves. This example is schematic: it illustrates how definitions operate and is not a claim that any sampled fossil is the direct ancestor of another.
Two distinctions prevent mistakes. First, a node-based clade definition need not define a crown group: internal references can be extinct, as in a deliberately broader Archaeopteryx-inclusive node definition. Likewise, a branch-based clade definition need not define an entire total group; using a nonavian theropod dinosaur as the excluded reference selects a much narrower avian-lineage branch than excluding crocodilians. Second, a stem group is not synonymous with the branch-defined total group. The total includes the crown; the stem alone excludes it and normally forms a succession of extinct branches.
The definition must therefore accompany an ambiguous name. Historical uses of Aves have included the living-bird crown and more inclusive fossil assemblages; Avialae has also received differing definitions. Discovery of feathers in a nonavian theropod dinosaur changes the inferred distribution of a phylogenetic character, but does not by itself place the animal inside crown Aves. Conversely, loss of flight does not remove a crown descendant from its clade. A name supplies an ancestry rule; anatomical and molecular evidence supply the tree on which that rule is applied. Uncertain fossil placement produces uncertain membership even when the definition is precise.
