A node-based name selects a common ancestor from included reference taxa; a branch-based name selects a lineage by including one reference and excluding another. Both designate a clade, so both include every descendant of the selected ancestor. The distinction concerns the rule locating that ancestor, not whether descendants are included. Neither requires its members to share a particular modern appearance.[ref]
For a node-based clade definition, choose internal phylogenetic specifiers A and B. Let m(A,B) be their last common ancestor, and let D(v) include ancestor v and all its descendants. Then the named group is
N(A,B)=D(m(A,B)).
(1)
It is the smallest clade containing both references. For a branch-based clade definition, choose an internal reference A and an external reference Z. The named group is the largest clade containing A but excluding Z: organisms sharing a more recent common ancestor with A than with Z. On a rooted phylogenetic tree, this selects the A-side lineage after its separation from the lineage containing Z. These are also called minimum-clade definitions and maximum-clade definitions. The names describe inclusion constraints, not distances measured from branch lengths.[ref]
An informative avian node example is crown Aves, here anchored to living ostrich and sparrow lineages. Their last common ancestor is the ancestor of living Palaeognathae and Neognathae; the name includes that ancestor and all descendants, including extinct descendants within either branch. An informative branch example is Panaves, the avian total group: the largest clade including living birds but excluding living crocodilians. With standard archosaur relationships it includes Aves, extinct avian stem relatives, nonavian dinosaurs and pterosaurs. The external reference is decisive: the definition asks which side of the bird–crocodilian split an organism occupies, not whether it flies. These crown and total usages were distinguished explicitly in the avian nomenclatural analysis by Jacques A. Gauthier and Kevin de Queiroz.[ref]
Consider a simplified cladogram in which the crocodilian lineage separates first, followed on the avian side by pterosaurs, successive nonavian dinosaur branches, Archaeopteryx, other extinct avian branches and finally the living-bird crown divergence. Archaeopteryx belongs to Panaves but lies outside crown Aves. A fossil on the palaeognath branch after the palaeognath–neognath divergence belongs to both. A crocodilian belongs to neither. Finding a still earlier bird-line fossil would extend the known fossil history of Panaves without automatically changing the age or known membership of crown Aves. This example is schematic: it illustrates how definitions operate and is not a claim that any sampled fossil is the direct ancestor of another.
Two distinctions prevent mistakes. First, a node-based clade definition need not define a crown group: internal references can be extinct, as in a deliberately broader Archaeopteryx-inclusive node definition. Likewise, a branch-based clade definition need not define an entire total group; using a nonavian theropod dinosaur as the excluded reference selects a much narrower avian-lineage branch than excluding crocodilians. Second, a stem group is not synonymous with the branch-defined total group. The total includes the crown; the stem alone excludes it and normally forms a succession of extinct branches.
The definition must therefore accompany an ambiguous name. Historical uses of Aves have included the living-bird crown and more inclusive fossil assemblages; Avialae has also received differing definitions. Discovery of feathers in a nonavian theropod dinosaur changes the inferred distribution of a phylogenetic character, but does not by itself place the animal inside crown Aves. Conversely, loss of flight does not remove a crown descendant from its clade. A name supplies an ancestry rule; anatomical and molecular evidence supply the tree on which that rule is applied. Uncertain fossil placement produces uncertain membership even when the definition is precise.

The terms describe contrasting palatal architectures; their literal “old” and “new” implications do not establish which architecture was ancestral. A palaeognathous palate has extensive bracing of the palatal apparatus against the skull base, involving the vomer, pterygoid bone, palatine bone and prominent basipterygoid processes. In a neognathous palate, reduced bracing and a more mobile pterygoid–palatine connection permit greater relative movement. These are arrangements of joints and contacts, not simply differences in lower-jaw shape. The distinction concerns the structural basis of cranial kinesis, movement within the skull beyond ordinary opening of the lower jaw.[ref]
Living Palaeognathae, including ostriches and tinamous, and living Neognathae, including landfowl, waterfowl and other birds, form the deepest two surviving branches of crown Aves. Historically, the heavily braced condition was commonly interpreted as retention of a primitive nonavian configuration, followed by evolution of a more mobile palate in Neognathae. That interpretation combined resemblance to outgroups with the misleading intuition that a lineage branching early must preserve the ancestral anatomy. A sister lineage has had just as much time to change; its living members are not ancestors of the other branch.
The difficulty is establishing character polarity when delicate palatal elements are absent, crushed or wrongly identified in fossils near the crown divergence. Comparisons of the vomer across living and extinct birds documented meaningful differences in contact geometry but also substantial variation and repeated reduction. The 2019 analysis by Han Hu and colleagues emphasized how incomplete contacts restrict inferences about extinct cranial kinesis. A single robust-looking element cannot establish an immobile skull, and one potentially mobile joint cannot establish the full range of movement. Constraints elsewhere in the kinetic chain remain relevant.[ref]
The Ichthyornis material described in 2018 helped separate components of the supposedly modern avian head. Three-dimensionally preserved skull elements combined a small beak at the jaw tips and retained teeth with a reconstructed kinetic apparatus, while other cranial regions remained markedly dinosaurian. Thus tooth loss, beak expansion and acquisition of a mobile feeding apparatus did not form one indivisible evolutionary event. This example of mosaic evolution also weakens the argument that a toothed stem bird must have a fully rigid, palaeognath-like palate. Its importance is the combination of characters, rather than an inference that every modern kinetic mechanism was already identical.[ref]
The strongest recent change came from Janavis finalidens, a toothed bird from approximately 66.7 million years ago, described in 2022. micro-computed tomography exposed a well-preserved pterygoid bone within the specimen. Its articular construction and comparative shape resemble those of Galloanserae, supporting a mobile pterygoid–palatine connection. The animal was recovered outside the living-bird crown group, close to Ichthyornis, rather than inside Neognathae. Consequently, anatomy previously treated as a neognath innovation occurred on an earlier branch. On that reconstruction, mobility evolved before crown Aves, and the tightly integrated palaeognathous palate is a subsequent specialization of the palaeognath lineage.[ref]
The inference is best understood by separating observation, placement and reconstruction. The observation is the morphology of a fossil pterygoid bone. Its placement outside crown Aves comes from a wider analysis of phylogenetic characters using phylogenetics. The inference that the crown ancestor had a relatively mobile palate combines those findings with distributions of phylogenetic characters in living groups. None alone proves the entire history. Convergence in the fossil lineage remains a logical alternative; it would require a different pattern of character acquisition from ancestral mobility followed by increased bracing. Distinguishing these alternatives requires additional palatal characters and fossil sampling, not the age of the specimen alone. A 66.7-million-year-old stem bird need not predate the crown ancestor chronologically: stem and crown lineages can coexist.
Functional and developmental caution also matters. fossils generally lack ligaments, muscles and direct measurements of movement. Comparative joint geometry can support potential mobility, but cannot yield exact excursions or feeding performance without further assumptions. The palaeognathous palate should not be reduced to total skull immobility, nor every neognathous palate to one uniform kinetic mechanism. The defensible revision is that important neognath-like palatal features originated before the living-bird crown split, making palaeognath specializations a plausible derived condition. The two living clades remain valid; what changes is the inferred history of the anatomical states used to describe them.

The frog skeleton evolved through mosaic evolution: reorganization of the head and pelvis preceded completion of the compact, tailless, long-limbed jumping apparatus. Its principal transformations are shortening and stiffening of the axial skeleton, conversion of the posterior body into a sacrum–pelvis–urostyle system, and specialization of the appendicular skeleton for propulsion and landing. These are connected mechanically, but need not have evolved simultaneously or initially served the same function.
In the derived adult frog, the trunk is short, most species having eight or nine presacral vertebrae, and the ribs are strongly reduced or absent. The adult tail no longer consists of a long freely jointed series. Instead, the urostyle forms a posterior axial rod from caudal vertebral and associated developmental components. This increases the integration of the posterior body with the pelvic girdle. The ilium has an elongated, anteriorly directed shaft, placing much of the pelvic apparatus posterior to its sacral connection. Changes in the sacroiliac joint and adjacent musculature permit transmission of bilateral hindlimb thrust to the trunk. The urostyle is therefore more than a tail that simply shrank: it is part of a reorganized force-transmission system.
The limbs change in both proportion and construction. Elongate hindlimbs increase the possible extension stroke, and elongated proximal ankle elements, the tibiale and fibulare, add another effective segment to the propulsive chain. Union of tibia and fibula into a tibiofibula and of radius bone and ulna into a radioulna stiffens the lower limb segments. The forelimbs and pectoral girdle participate in support and absorption of landing forces, while the frog head is typically broad, short and flattened, with reduction and fusion of cranial elements, including integration of frontal and parietal components into a frontoparietal bone. These are generalizations about the characteristic skeleton, not a claim that every living frog is an obligate jumper: swimming, burrowing, climbing and walking lineages modify the inherited apparatus.
The decisive stem example is Triadobatrachus massinoti, from the Early Triassic of Madagascar, about 250 million years ago. It belongs to the frog total group, Salientia, outside crown Anura. Its phylogenetic importance is the association of recognizable frog-lineage features with retained ancestral construction. An anteriorly elongated ilium and frog-like cranial organization were already present, while a short series of separate caudal vertebrae persisted. The forearm and lower-leg pairs were not united into the modern radioulna and tibiofibula, and the hindlimbs lacked the full proportional specialization of powerful living jumpers. Thus pelvic reorganization did not require that all the later limb and tail modifications had already evolved.
The 2016 micro-computed tomography redescription is especially important because it revises the evidence rather than merely repeating an iconic reconstruction. It recognized 15 presacral vertebrae, with an atlas vertebra–axis vertebra complex, replacing the traditional interpretation of 14 with a divided atlas. Its trunk was therefore substantially less abbreviated in vertebral count than that of a typical living frog. Reconstructions under alternative amphibian relationships suggested that some proportional trunk reduction may already have occurred before the frog–salamander split, preceding the iliac elongation and tail shortening seen in this fossil. That inference depends on the comparative tree and model; vertebral number and actual trunk proportions are different characters. A count alone cannot demonstrate exactly how flexibly the animal moved.[ref]
Locomotor inference requires an additional test. In 2016, Andrés I. Lires and colleagues compared major limb-bone lengths across 188 living limbed amphibians and lizards, relating their proportions to observed locomotor categories while considering shared ancestry. Triadobatrachus massinoti was better supported as using salamander-like lateral undulatory locomotion than as a specialized jumper. This is evidence against assuming that each frog-like skeletal feature originated as an adaptation for saltation. The limitations are that bone proportions are proxies for performance, living comparison sets may omit extinct combinations, and soft tissues and direct behaviour remain unknown. Failure to support specialized jumping does not exclude every short hop or vigorous hindlimb movement.[ref]
Comparison with Prosalirus bitis, from the Early Jurassic, provides a later anatomical checkpoint. Its caudopelvic construction includes a urostyle, rod-like iliac organization and elongate hindlimbs, and was interpreted as supporting jumping through changes in pelvic orientation and iliosacral mobility. This comparison reinforces a sequence in which early frog-lineage pelvic and cranial modifications preceded completion of the more specialized locomotor system. It does not turn the named fossils into a direct ancestor–descendant chain, nor require every phylogenetic character to have followed exactly the same history in every branch.[ref]
Triadobatrachus massinoti separates frog ancestry from fully developed frog jumping. It documents a combination that comparison of living frogs and salamanders alone could not predict reliably. The appropriate evolutionary explanation allows early changes in body support or movement to become exaptations for later saltation, followed by stronger specialization of the limbs and caudopelvic apparatus. Its retained tail and incompletely fused appendicular elements demonstrate staged assembly, while the revised vertebral interpretation shows why the ordering must remain anchored to observed anatomy.

taphonomy studies the transformation of biological remains from death or production into the material ultimately recovered and interpreted. It explains how a living organism or community becomes an incomplete geological sample. For shed structures or trace fossils, production of the evidence need not involve the death of the producer. Its relevance therefore extends beyond asking whether a skeleton was buried intact.
Three primary process classes are decay; physical disarticulation and transport; and burial alteration, including diagenetic mineralization. The boundaries are operational and the mechanisms interact: post-mortem decay can continue after burial, and previously buried remains can be reworked and transported again. biostratinomy groups events before final burial, whereas diagenesis groups subsequent physical and chemical changes. Those are stages or settings, rather than three mutually exclusive mechanisms.
First, post-mortem decay transforms tissues by autolysis, microbial decomposition and associated chemical reactions. Soft tissues, connective tissues and different phylogenetic characters disappear at different rates. The important result is not simply that hard parts survive longer: a partly decayed organism can acquire a misleading anatomical combination. Experiments on amphioxus and lamprey larvae followed recognizable characters through decomposition. Derived diagnostic features tended to disappear before some more general chordate features, producing a directional bias. Coding every unobserved diagnostic character as ancestrally absent can push a genuinely derived fossil toward an earlier position on a phylogenetic tree, the effect called stemward slippage.[ref]
Experiments on adult hagfish and lampreys extended the comparison beyond larvae. They documented transformations as well as disappearance, so a residual stain or collapsed structure should not automatically be identified as an intact organ. Replicate specimens sampled through time are needed because destructive observations cannot follow every internal character in the same individual. For a new experimental test, temperature, water chemistry, microbial conditions and starting condition should be recorded and, where tested, varied independently. A laboratory sequence supplies constraints on interpretations; it is not a universal clock assigning an exact post-mortem duration to every fossil.[ref]
Second, disarticulation, fragmentation and taphonomic transport alter which elements occur together and where they accumulate. Loss of ligaments releases bones, while currents or scavengers can move them. Shape, density, size, articulation and orientation influence transportability, so a concentration of robust elements need not represent the most abundant living species or the original place of death. Flow experiments and comparison of element representation, as developed in Michael R. Voorhies's vertebrate assemblage study, provide a basis for testing such alternatives. A useful comparison measures elements under specified flow and sediment conditions, with articulated versus disarticulated material distinguished. hydraulic sorting cannot be transferred unchanged between all animals and environments.[ref]
The consequences include mixing habitats and ages. An apparently coherent bone bed may combine local and transported animals; reworking of fossils can place older remains in younger sediment; time averaging can combine successive communities into one assemblage. None implies that every fossil assemblage is hopelessly mixed. Articulation, abrasion, orientation, size distributions, sedimentary context and independent age information can instead delimit the plausible history. Abrasion alone is an imperfect transport-distance meter because material properties and the transport medium also matter.
Third, burial changes both preservation and geometry. Rapid isolation can reduce scavenging, but burial does not halt all decomposition. sedimentary compaction flattens and fractures remains; dissolution removes mineralized tissues; precipitation can infill pores or replicate tissues; fossil recrystallization can erase original microscopic detail. A fossil can therefore retain an excellent outline but lack its original chemistry, or preserve histology while its overall shape has been distorted. Mineralized replacement is not the only route to fossilization, and surviving original hard parts need not have undergone complete replacement.
Experiments by Derek E. G. Briggs and Amanda J. Kear illustrate constructive as well as destructive diagenesis. Decaying shrimps developed diagenetic mineralization by calcium phosphate preserving muscle detail, particularly in oxygen-restricted systems, with the animal itself supplying phosphate. diagenetic mineralization began on a timescale of weeks. This demonstrates a mechanism by which decay-associated chemistry can stabilize fine structures before they disappear. It does not show that low oxygen guarantees preservation: chemical availability, acidity, tissue composition and the relative rates of destruction and precipitation still control the outcome.[ref]
These mechanisms are indispensable for reconstructing the history of life. A fossil's first occurrence is a minimum observation of a lineage, not automatically its evolutionary origin. A sudden appearance of soft-bodied organisms can reflect a new window of exceptional preservation, while disappearance of a phylogenetic character can reflect its destruction rather than evolutionary loss. Relative fossil abundance reflects both biological abundance and the probabilities of production, preservation, exposure and recovery. Comparisons of diversity through time must therefore consider changes in environments, preservational pathways and sampling. The fossil record is filtered evidence whose reliability can be tested; taphonomy identifies which biological conclusions that evidence can support.

Living cartilaginous fishes are specialized descendants, not a surviving anatomical stage before the origin of bony vertebrates. fossil discoveries have shifted several apparently “bony-fish” features toward the ancestry of all living jawed vertebrates. Their absence in living Chondrichthyes can consequently represent character loss, while other shark features remain ancestral or are new specializations. The change is a revision of character polarity, not a claim that every aspect of shark anatomy is reduced.
A simple comparison of living sharks with Osteichthyes can suggest a one-way sequence: an ancestor with a cartilaginous internal skeleton, followed by origin of extensive bone on the bony-vertebrate branch. That inference is insecure because the two living sister lineages can both have changed, and living jawless outgroups can also have lost structures. The fossil record samples extinct combinations near the relevant divergences. To determine whether a structure is a gain or loss, one must place those combinations on a rooted phylogenetic tree and distinguish anatomical absence from poor preservation.
The early gnathostome problem involves four familiar assemblages: placoderms, acanthodians, Chondrichthyes and Osteichthyes. Treating all four as separate complete clades can conceal transitions occurring along the stems of surviving groups. In particular, many newer analyses place acanthodians as successive branches on the chondrichthyan stem. Their name “spiny sharks” is not itself evidence for that placement; cranial, scale, fin and other characters must support it. If this topology is correct, the large difference between living sharks and early armoured jawed fishes includes a history of reduction within the cartilaginous-fish lineage.
The 2012 redescription of the Acanthodes braincase illustrates why anatomical interpretation and tree position must be kept separate. It revealed a mixture of conditions previously assigned to different living groups and argued for shark-like aspects of the last common ancestor of modern jawed vertebrates. Its analysis still placed Acanthodes on the osteichthyan stem, rather than treating every acanthodian as a stem chondrichthyan. Thus a resemblance initially interpreted as an osteichthyan specialization could instead be an ancestral feature retained in more than one lineage. Later hypotheses should not be projected retrospectively onto that study.[ref]
Entelognathus primordialis, described in 2013 from the Silurian of China, supplied a particularly important association: an armoured placoderm-like body and skull with large marginal jaw bones identifiable as premaxilla, maxilla and dentary. Such bones had been treated as characteristic innovations of Osteichthyes. Placement of this animal near the top of the jawed-vertebrate stem, together with recovery of acanthodians on the chondrichthyan stem, instead supported a macromeric, extensively ossified dermal ancestor. On that reconstruction, the small-scale-dominated covering and absence of those large jaw bones in living Chondrichthyes reflect reduction and reorganization rather than simple retention of a primitively unossified condition.[ref]
Janusiscus schultzei, described in 2015 from the Early Devonian, reinforced the point using a different anatomical region. Its combination of a bony dermal skull roof and informative internal cranial conditions supported a position outside crown gnathostomes. Braincase anatomy therefore did not align neatly with the traditional picture of a primitive shark head versus a derived bony-fish head. Together, these fossils move apparently osteichthyan-like states closer to the common ancestry of both surviving groups. Their significance comes from character combinations and placement, not from choosing whichever living animal looks most similar.[ref]
A separate question concerns internal skeletal tissue. dermal bone, perichondral bone, endochondral bone and calcified cartilage must not be conflated. endochondral bone forms during replacement of a cartilaginous template; perichondral bone forms around it; calcified cartilage remains a different tissue despite being mineralized. Therefore a bony dermal skull roof alone cannot establish endochondral ossification in the same animal.
The 2020 description of Minjinia turgenensis, from the Early Devonian of Mongolia, addressed that distinction. micro-computed tomography revealed extensive internal trabecular tissue in the braincase, interpreted as endochondral bone, rather than just an external bony shell. Its recovery close to the crown divergence permits endochondral ossification to be reconstructed before the separation of Chondrichthyes and Osteichthyes, followed by loss or marked suppression along the chondrichthyan lineage. This conclusion depends on identification of the tissue and the animal's position; the absence of a more complete skeleton and uncertainty among early jawed-vertebrate trees constrain how strongly it can be generalized.[ref]
The resulting inference is conditional but testable. If well-supported stem relatives and the bony-vertebrate branch possess a particular homologous tissue, while living cartilaginous fishes lack it, an ancestral presence followed by loss can fit better than several independent origins. Moving a crucial fossil to another branch, changing a tissue identification or allowing different transition rates can change the ancestral reconstruction. maximum parsimony is useful but is not proof that losses and gains have equal biological probability. Character coding should record unknown preservation as unknown, rather than use an unobserved delicate feature as evidence of absence.
The major revision is from a ladder of increasing ossification to a branching history of gains, retention and losses. It concerns particular tissues and anatomical elements: reduction of large dermal bones need not coincide exactly with loss of endochondral ossification. fossils reveal that apparently simple modern anatomy can be secondarily simplified, and that major living-group differences may reflect transformations on both sides of a divergence. Continued uncertainty over placoderm relationships and individual tissue homologies defines the comparisons still needed; it does not restore living sharks as unchanged proxies for the jawed-vertebrate ancestor.

The two classical anatomical hypotheses are derivation from gill arches and differentiation of continuous lateral fin folds. Developmental co-option supplies an additional explanation for shared patterning mechanisms. The histories of the shoulder support, appendage blade and developmental programme need not be identical. Evaluating them separately allows evidence that initially appears contradictory to describe different components of the same evolutionary transition.
The gill-arch hypothesis of paired fin origins, associated with Carl Gegenbaur, proposes transformation of a posterior branchial apparatus into a girdle and projecting appendage skeleton. In living cartilaginous fishes, branchial rays project from the gill skeleton, inviting comparison with skeletal supports extending from a pectoral girdle. The historical archipterygium interpretation treated these as evidence for a gill-to-fin transformation. Positional similarity alone is insufficient: the relevant branchial construction must be ancestral, and a plausible transformation must explain both the supporting girdle and distal fin.
Developmental experiments provide stronger evidence for shared mechanisms than simple adult resemblance. Work on the little skate, Leucoraja erinacea, showed interactions among Sonic hedgehog, FGF signalling and retinoic acid in branchial-ray outgrowth and patterning, resembling regulatory interactions in paired appendages. The crucial inference is reuse of a spatial patterning system; expression of the same molecule by itself would be weaker evidence. Functional perturbation helps distinguish an active requirement from a coincidental expression domain.[ref]
A 2016 study used timed inhibition of Hedgehog signalling with cyclopamine, lineage labelling and measurements of cell proliferation in skate embryos. Earlier inhibition disrupted branchial-ray polarity, while later inhibition reduced their production or expansion. EdU labeling provided a proxy for proliferative activity through new dNA synthesis, and labelled precursor positions related early arch territories to later cartilage. Solvent-treated controls are necessary because handling and drug vehicle can alter development; stage matching and general growth measurements are needed to distinguish pattern-specific effects from generalized retardation. These results support parallel roles for the signal in axial organization and progenitor expansion. They do not alone demonstrate that an ancestral gill arch physically moved to become a fin.[ref]
The fossil argument for a pharyngeal contribution was strengthened before this examination by the 2023 analysis of Kolymaspis sibirica. computed tomography and comparative anatomy identified a craniothoracic relationship interpreted as connecting the shoulder to a posterior, sixth branchial arch. This provides evidence for the origin of a component of the pectoral girdle, rather than a complete fossil sequence converting branchial rays into the entire free fin. Homology of the articulation and the reconstruction of the head–trunk boundary remain important assumptions. A pharyngeal contribution to the girdle does not require a wholly pharyngeal origin for every appendicular tissue.[ref]
The lateral fin-fold hypothesis proposes that the two localized pairs of fins arose through differentiation and regional restriction of continuous bilateral folds along the body wall. It predicts possible precursors extending through intervening pectoral and pelvic regions, with increased regional differentiation and locomotor control later. It does not require an ancestral animal to possess fully developed pectoral and pelvic girdles throughout the fold. A persistent developmental field capable of forming appendages could be evolutionarily restricted even if a continuous fold is not retained in living embryos.
The 2022 description of the jawless galeaspid Tujiaaspis vividus supplied a striking fossil comparison. Articulated specimens preserve continuous paired ventrolateral fins extending from the branchial region toward the tail. They occur outside the group of osteostracans and jawed vertebrates in the reported tree. Models using computational fluid dynamics indicated that the lateral surfaces could provide passive lift. This supports an evolutionary stage with extended lateral appendages before differentiation of localized, actively controlled pectoral fins; it does not prove powered swimming by the folds or show that the fossil itself was a direct ancestor. Surface reconstruction, swimming conditions and the assumed body geometry constrain the functional model.[ref]
Other jawless stem relatives sharpen the proposed sequence. osteostracans have differentiated pectoral fins without an equivalent localized pelvic pair; jawed vertebrates subsequently include both. This distribution argues against assuming simultaneous origin of the two appendage pairs. In a fin-fold interpretation, anterior differentiation can precede restriction of the remaining competent body-wall region to a pelvic position. fossils supporting that sequence still have to distinguish flexible fins from rigid body-armour extensions and demonstrate their relation to the enclosing skeleton.
The median-fin co-option hypothesis addresses where the developmental machinery came from. median fins are evolutionarily older than the paired appendages. Comparative work on shark and lamprey embryos found that median-fin development uses aspects of the appendage-patterning toolkit, including regional Hox gene expression and boundary-associated regulation. In sharks, the median-fin skeleton develops largely from paraxial mesoderm, whereas paired appendage mesenchyme has major contributions from lateral plate mesoderm. Similar patterning despite different tissue sources supports redeployment of an older regulatory programme. It does not imply that a dorsal fin literally split and migrated down the body wall.[ref]
This is the distinction between deep homology of mechanisms and a claim of whole-organ biological homology. Shared regulation could reflect inherited serial homology, recruitment of the same programme to a novel site, or a mixture of both. Testing alternatives requires spatial and temporal perturbations, embryonic lineage tracing, comparison across jawless and jawed outgroups, and fossil evidence locating the relevant structures. Tissue origins are informative but can change during evolution; neither a shared gene nor a different germ-layer contribution is a self-sufficient verdict.
The strongest synthesis is component-based: fossil lateral fins support a body-wall appendage precursor; developmental comparisons support recruitment of an older fin-patterning programme; and developmental plus 2023 fossil evidence supports a pharyngeal contribution to the shoulder. The remaining problem is to establish how those components were assembled and when regional pectoral and pelvic control evolved. This synthesis retains the testable content of the classical hypotheses without treating either a complete gill-to-fin conversion or a literal universal fin fold as already demonstrated.
