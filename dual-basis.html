Dual basis (ei∗​(ej​)=δij​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Dual space
OurBigBook.com Words: 278 Articles: 2
For a basis e1​,…,en​ of a finite-dimensional vector space V, its dual basis consists of linear functionals e1∗​,…,en∗​ satisfying
ei∗​(ej​)=δij​.
(6)
The Kronecker delta means that each rule gives one on its own basis vector and zero on the others. These rules extract coordinates:
v=∑i=1n​ei∗​(v)ei​.
(7)
Applying ei∗​ to a linear combination removes every term except the ith. Consequently the dual basis is a basis of the dual space, and every linear functional has expansion φ=∑i​φ(ei​)ei∗​. [ref].
Coordinate extraction works for a nonorthogonal basis too. With b1​=(1,0) and b2​=(1,1), the dual basis is
b1∗​(x,y)=x−y,b2∗​(x,y)=y.
(8)
The checks b1∗​(b1​)=1, b1∗​(b2​)=0, b2∗​(b1​)=0, and b2∗​(b2​)=1 fix the two rules. For v=(3,1) they give coordinates (2,1), so v=2b1​+b2​. The usual dot products with b1​,b2​ instead give 3,4 and do not extract these coordinates: simple projection formulas depend on an orthonormal basis.
The same idea explains polynomial interpolation. On polynomials of polynomial degree at most n, evaluation at distinct interpolation nodes x0​,…,xn​ gives the dual basis to the Lagrange cardinal polynomials, because ℓi​(xj​)=δij​. Samples are coordinates in this basis, so the reconstruction p=∑i​p(xi​)ℓi​ is a dual-basis expansion. Differentiation weights from nodal evaluations and factorial monomial basis for polynomial differentiation apply the same principle to different measurements.
