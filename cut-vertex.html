Cut vertex (k(G−v)>k(G))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Graph theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 3
A cut vertex, also called an articulation point, is a vertex v of an undirected graph whose deletion increases the number of connected components of a graph. Write k(G) for that count, including isolated vertices, and G−v for the graph obtained by removing v and every incident edge. Then the test is k(G−v)>k(G), even when the original graph is disconnected. By contrast, deleting a bridge in a graph removes just one edge and keeps both endpoint vertices. These are different failure models. [ref]
First take two triangles in a graph, ABC and ADE, sharing only the vertex A. This simple graph has
V={A,B,C,D,E},E={AB,AC,BC,AD,AE,DE}.
(12)
Here XY abbreviates the unordered pair {X,Y}. Removing A leaves the two connected components of a graph {B,C} and {D,E}, so k(G−A)=2>1=k(G). Removing any other vertex leaves a connected graph. Thus A is the only cut vertex. Every edge lies on a triangle in a graph, so deleting any single edge still leaves a graph path between its endpoints along the other two edges. There are no bridges in a graph.

Now take two disjoint triangles in a graph, ABC and DEF, joined by the edge CD:
V={A,B,C,D,E,F},E={AB,AC,BC,CD,DE,DF,EF}.
(13)
Deleting only CD preserves all six vertices and leaves connected components of a graph {A,B,C} and {D,E,F}: CD is the only bridge in a graph. Deleting C also removes AC and BC, leaving connected components of a graph {A,B} and {D,E,F}. Deleting D instead leaves {A,B,C} and {E,F}. Thus C,D are exactly the cut vertices.

For a disconnected example, adjoin an isolated vertex Z to the first graph. Now k(G)=2, deleting A gives k(G−A)=3, and deleting Z gives k(G−Z)=1. The original graph was already disconnected, but A is still a cut vertex; Z is not. The empty graph has count zero, so deleting the only vertex of a one-vertex graph does not create a cut vertex. Nor is either endpoint of a single isolated edge a cut vertex, although that edge is a bridge in a graph.
In a communications network, a cut vertex models a router whose failure separates surviving routers. The shared-A example shows why having an alternative graph path around each individual edge does not protect against failure of the common router. An additional edge from B to D removes this particular vulnerability: after deleting A, B,D still join the two sides. A bridge in a graph instead models a vulnerable link. This distinction tells us whether to add another cable between existing devices or a route avoiding an indispensable device; it describes connectivity, rather than traffic capacity or failure probabilities.
The beautiful structural consequence is that a graph splits into biconnected components joined at cut vertices. Within each nontrivial robust piece, a single vertex failure cannot separate the other vertices; between pieces, the shared cut vertices reveal the bottlenecks. Also, a Hamiltonian graph has no cut vertex: deleting any vertex from its Hamilton cycle leaves a Hamiltonian path through all remaining vertices.
Historically, Tarjan's June 1972 paper developed depth-first search as a tool for finding biconnected components in time linear in the numbers of vertices and edges. [ref] In his 1983 recollection, Tarjan credited Hopcroft with the original linear-time biconnected component algorithm during their collaboration beginning in summer 1970, and described the published version as a refinement. The historical lesson is how a carefully organized traversal turns many separate deletion experiments into one computation. [ref]
