Using the usual everywhere sign-valued representatives, for every n≥1 we have
Y0​=Xn​∏j=1n​Yj​.
(5)
The right side is measurable with respect to σ(Y,Tn​). Therefore Y0​ is measurable with respect to their intersection G.
Write T=⋂n​Tn​. The Kolmogorov zero-one law applies to the independent random variables (Xn​), so every event C∈T has probability zero or one. Also Y0​ is independent of Y: this follows first for finite intersections of sign events from the given independence, then for their generated sigma-algebra by part (a).
The sets D∩C with D∈Y and C∈T form a pi-system generating H. If P(C)=0, the event D∩C has probability zero and is independent of every event in σ(Y0​). If P(C)=1, it differs from D by a null set, so the same independence follows from that of Y0​ and Y. Another application of part (a) proves
Y0​ is G-measurable,σ(Y0​) and H are independent.​
(6)
In particular H cannot contain the nontrivial event {Y0​=1}, whereas G does. Thus adjoining a sigma-algebra need not commute with a decreasing intersection.
There is a small version qualification if the assumptions specify only the probability distributions, allowing exceptional values other than signs. Literal measurability of an arbitrary version of Y0​ need not follow without choosing sign-valued representatives or adjoining the ambient null sets. All the independence conclusions are unaffected.

The sigma-algebra generated by a random variable X is the smallest sigma-algebra on Ω making X a measurable function into (R,B(R)). Equivalently, it is the intersection of all sigma-algebras containing the inverse images X−1(C) of Borel sets C.
Let D={X−1(C):C∈B(R)}. This collection is already a sigma-algebra: X−1(R)=Ω,
(X−1(C))c=X−1(Cc),⋃j=1∞​X−1(Cj​)=X−1(⋃j=1∞​Cj​).
(15)
The right-hand sets are in D, since the Borel sigma-algebra is closed under the indicated operations. Any sigma-algebra making X measurable must contain every member of D. Conversely D makes X measurable by definition. Hence
σ(X)={X−1(C):C∈B(R)}.​
(16)
This equality holds exactly as a collection of sets, without completing the sigma-algebra.

In particular the argument does not assume that a function obtained by scalar clipping is a convex function or a concave function.

Let G=T−1(F)={T−1(A):A∈F}. Inverse images commute with complements and countable unions, so this is a sub-sigma-algebra of F. The random variable X∘T is G-measurable.
The assumption P∘T−1=P says that T is a measure-preserving transformation. Consequently X∘T has the same probability distribution as X and is integrable. It also implies E[W∘T]=E[W] for every integrable random variable W, first for indicator functions, then for simple functions, and then by positive and negative approximation.
To identify a conditional expectation, take any A∈F. The assumed identity with the bounded random variable Z=1A​ gives
E[X1T−1(A)​]​=E[X(1A​∘T)]=E[X1A​]=E[(X1A​)∘T]=E[(X∘T)1T−1(A)​].​
(58)
Every member of G has the displayed form T−1(A), so this verifies all defining conditional expectation identities. Therefore
E[X∣G]=X∘Talmost surely.
(59)
