Let m=EX. First, m lies in the open interval I. For example, if I has a finite lower endpoint ℓ, then X−ℓ>0 almost surely, and its expected value is strictly positive; hence m>ℓ. The corresponding argument at a finite upper endpoint gives the other strict inequality. Integrability ensures m is finite when either endpoint is infinite.
The tangent affine function at m gives the supporting inequality for a differentiable convex function. Under the stipulated condition f′′>0, contact with that tangent occurs only at m: define
h(t)=f(t)−f(m)−f′(m)(t−m).
(18)
Then h(m)=0, and h(t)>0 for every t=m. One direct proof uses h′(t)=f′(t)−f′(m): f′ is strictly increasing, so h decreases towards m on the left and increases away from m on the right. Therefore
E[f(X)]=f(m)+E[h(X)]≥f(m),
(19)
with equality exactly when h(X)=0 almost surely, equivalently when X=m almost surely. Conversely, an almost surely constant X=m plainly gives equality. This proves the strict Jensen equality criterion.
No extra assumption that f(X) is an integrable random variable is needed to interpret this argument. Its negative part is bounded by the absolute value of the integrable random variable f(m)+f′(m)(X−m), since h(X)≥0. Thus its expected value is defined in (−∞,+∞]; if it equals the finite number f(m), then E[h(X)]=0 and the required integrability follows automatically. Equality holds if and only if X=EX almost surely.

The product measure μ⊗ν is the unique probability measure on the sigma-algebra generated by measurable rectangles for which
(μ⊗ν)(C×D)=μ(C)ν(D)for all C,D∈B(R).​
(39)
Here the printed product notation B(R)×B(R) denotes the generated product sigma-algebra, conventionally written B(R)⊗B(R); it is not just the collection of rectangles. Existence is the product measure theorem for finite measures, which are in particular sigma-finite measures. Uniqueness follows because the rectangles form a pi-system generating the product sigma-algebra, and two finite measures agreeing there agree everywhere by the Dynkin lemma.[ref]

supn​E[∣Mn∧τ​∣1{∣Mn∧τ​∣>K}​]≤E[H1{H>K}​]⟶0.
(55)
The last limit follows from the dominated convergence theorem. This is precisely uniform integrability. It also gives Mn∧τ​→Mτ​=∣Sτ​∣−Lτ​ with convergence in L1, and therefore EMτ​=0, consistently with the preceding answer. A common bound on the individual expected values alone would not have sufficed; the integrable dominating random variable is what supplies control of every tail.
