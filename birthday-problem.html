Birthday problem (Pn,m​=1−∏j=0n−1​(1−j/m))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics

The birthday problem asks for the probability that at least two members of a group share a birthday. Use the explicit model of independent random variables taking m labels with a uniform distribution on a finite set. Let m≥1 and n≥0 be integers. For calendar examples below, stipulate m=365 equally likely labels and omit leap days. For 0≤n≤m, the probability of at least one matching pair is
Pn,m​=1−mnm(m−1)⋯(m−n+1)​=1−j=0∏n−1​(1−mj​).​
(8)
At n=0, the empty product is one and P0,m​=0. At n>m, a repeat is certain by the pigeonhole principle. Count the complement to obtain the formula: the first label has m choices, then a distinct second label has m−1, and so forth, out of mn equally likely label sequences.[ref]
For the stipulated calendar, P22,365​≈47.57% and P23,365​≈50.73%. Only 23 people suffice to put the probability above one half. There are (223​)=253 possible pairs, each with matching probability 1/365. The many comparison opportunities explain why a small group can contain a repeat.
The distinction also matters for hash collisions. If independently generated outputs of a hash function are uniformly distributed over m possible labels, the same counting formula governs repeats. The birthday square-root scale explains why looking for any repeated output requires far fewer labels than trying to match one predetermined output. The uniform-independent model is an assumption about those outputs; the birthday problem does not itself establish that a particular implementation meets it.
