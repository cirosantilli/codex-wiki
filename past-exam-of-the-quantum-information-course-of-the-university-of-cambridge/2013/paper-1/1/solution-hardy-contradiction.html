In a deterministic local hidden-variable model, write C+(R,λ) or D+(I,λ) without an argument for the remote splitter setting. That omission is the parameter independence assumption: the local response for a fixed complete hidden state does not change when the other wing changes its apparatus. Measurement independence also requires the hidden-state ensemble to be independent of the freely selected settings, allowing the same λ assignments to be compared across configurations.
The zero probabilities derived above then constrain those assignments, apart from sets of hidden states of zero measure:
  • Case RR requires C+(R,λ)C−(R,λ)=0.
  • Case IR requires D+(I,λ)=1⇒C−(R,λ)=1.
  • Case RI requires D−(I,λ)=1⇒C+(R,λ)=1.
For a trial with D+(I,λ)D−(I,λ)=1, the last two implications force C+(R,λ)=C−(R,λ)=1, contradicting the first condition. Thus these deterministic local assignments predict zero probability for the double-D event, whereas quantum theory predicts 1/16. This is Hardy's paradox, an inequality-free ideal contradiction. With imperfect apparatus the zero-event conditions become small nonzero rates and require a statistical inequality rather than declaring a contradiction from one imperfect detection.[ref]
The logical contradiction combines counterfactual hidden-variable assignments; it does not assign simultaneously observed path properties to a quantum state. Nor does it allow a message to be sent by changing a distant splitter: the observable local marginal obeys quantum no-signalling. For example, fixing the plus-side splitter to I gives P(D+)=1/8 for either minus-side setting in the results above.
