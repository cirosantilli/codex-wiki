Consistent histories assigns probabilities to suitably compatible sequences of quantum alternatives without making an external measuring apparatus fundamental. At successive times choose complete orthogonal sets of projection operators. In the Heisenberg picture, a history has class operator Cα​=Pαn​​(tn​)⋯Pα1​​(t1​). For an initial density operator ρ, its decoherence functional is
D(α,β)=tr(Cα​ρCβ†​).
(15)
A common sufficient consistency condition is D(α,β)=0 for α=β. Then p(α)=D(α,α) is nonnegative and coarse-grained alternatives have additive probabilities: the interference cross-terms vanish. The weaker requirement on real cross-terms can suffice for individual probability sum rules. Predictions must stay within one compatible framework; mutually incompatible history families cannot simply be combined into one classical account.
The many-worlds interpretation instead treats unitary quantum evolution as universally valid and interprets the correlated outcome branches as components of one global state. A measurement interaction gives
(c0​∣0⟩+c1​∣1⟩)∣Aready​⟩∣Eready​⟩⟶c0​∣0⟩∣A0​⟩∣E0​⟩+c1​∣1⟩∣A1​⟩∣E1​⟩.
(16)
When the environment records become nearly orthogonal, quantum decoherence suppresses observable interference between stable apparatus records. Each relative state contains an observer correlated with a definite record; no additional physical collapse is postulated. Branch structure is an emergent approximation, rather than a unique literal splitting at every arbitrary choice of basis.
Both approaches use the ordinary unitary formalism and decoherence, but their interpretations of histories and branches differ. Decoherence alone does not prove that one unique branch is selected, nor does the unitary equation by itself settle every assumption needed to justify Born rule probabilities. Neither interpretation predicts ordinary interference experiments incorrectly merely by interpreting the same state differently.
