In quantum teleportation, Alice transfers an arbitrary unknown qubit ∣χ⟩=α∣0⟩+β∣1⟩ using one shared Bell pair and two bits of classical communication. Define ∣βmn​⟩=(I⊗XnZm)∣Φ+⟩, with m,n∈{0,1}. Regrouping the initial three-qubit state into this Bell basis gives

Alice's Bell-basis measurement selects each label with probability 1/4. She sends (m,n) to Bob, who applies the inverse ZmXn, restoring ∣χ⟩. The correction depends on the measurement label, not on the unknown amplitudes. Before the message, Bob's average state is I/2, independent of the input; quantum no-signalling therefore prevents superluminal communication. The shared pair is consumed and Alice does not retain an independent copy, in agreement with the no-cloning theorem.
The Bouwmeester quantum teleportation experiment used photon polarization, a down-conversion entangled pair, a beam-splitter Bell-state analyzer and coincidence-conditioned output polarization measurements. Temporal indistinguishability of the two analyzer photons was essential. Its successful selected Bell outcome demonstrated the state transfer, rather than a deterministic implementation of all four outcomes.[ref]
The general experimental tasks are to distribute entanglement, prepare and synchronize the input, perform a sufficiently discriminating joint measurement, communicate the label and apply or account for the correction. A demonstration must test nonorthogonal input states, not merely transfer a known classical basis label. Finite efficiency, accidental coincidences, distinguishability and phase drift reduce success probability or teleportation fidelity. Postselected verification, a heralded usable output and unconditional deterministic teleportation are distinct experimental claims.
