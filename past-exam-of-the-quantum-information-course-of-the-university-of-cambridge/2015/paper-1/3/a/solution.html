A bipartite pure state is entangled when it is not a product state. Its Schmidt decomposition is ∣ψ⟩=∑i​λi​​∣iA​iB​⟩, where λi​≥0 and ∑i​λi​=1. More than one nonzero coefficient means entanglement. The two reduced density matrices have the same nonzero spectrum, and their Von Neumann entropy −∑i​λi​log2​λi​ is the pure-state entanglement entropy. A maximally entangled pair of qubits has one bit of this entropy.
For mixed states, classical correlations are not sufficient: a separable quantum state is a convex mixture ∑j​pj​ρA(j)​⊗ρB(j)​, and entanglement means that no such decomposition exists. A reduced entropy alone no longer measures mixed-state entanglement. An entanglement witness or a suitable separability criterion can detect it.
Local unitary transformations preserve the Schmidt spectrum. More general local operations and classical communication can discard entanglement but cannot freely create it from a separable input. Nielsen's pure-state conversion theorem gives the exact deterministic criterion: ∣ψ⟩→∣ϕ⟩ is possible precisely when λψ≺λϕ. In decreasing order, every partial sum for the input is no larger than the corresponding output sum, with equal totals. The direction matters: a uniform spectrum can be converted to a concentrated one; a product state cannot deterministically become a Bell pair.
A probabilistic local filter can concentrate a partially entangled pair. For ∣ψ⟩=λ​∣00⟩+1−λ​∣11⟩ with 1/2≤λ<1, Alice's successful operator F=diag((1−λ)/λ​,1) produces equal amplitudes. The outcome is a Bell pair with probability 2(1−λ); a complementary operator supplies a complete measurement. Failure is heralded, so there is no deterministic creation of a more entangled pair. This illustrates entanglement concentration.
Many-copy entanglement distillation and entanglement dilution connect noisy or nonuniform resources with standard Bell pairs. Entanglement enables quantum teleportation and other protocols, but classical communication is still required; the no-communication theorem excludes message transmission merely by choosing a distant local measurement.
