An eigenvalue of an observable in a known corresponding eigenstate is a simple example satisfying the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen criterion of reality: its value is predictable with certainty. For example, a spin angular momentum component in a prepared spin-up eigenstate has a definite predicted value. For a distant system, Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen invoked locality: a measurement performed on a sufficiently separated, noninteracting partner should not disturb that system. In the relativistic version here, the measurement events have spacelike separation.
The Pauli matrices obey [σz​,σx​]=2iσy​, so
[Sz​,Sx​]=iℏSy​=0.​
(1)
Their nondegenerate eigenstates are incompatible: an Sz​ eigenstate has equal probabilities for the two Sx​ outcomes, and conversely. Thus no single quantum state has both components sharply determined. The Robertson uncertainty relation gives ΔSz​ΔSx​≥ℏ∣⟨Sy​⟩∣/2, but its right-hand side may be zero; that alone is not a proof that simultaneous zero uncertainties exist. The absence of a common eigenstate supplies the stronger conclusion for this spin-half pair.
For the specified Bell state, Bob obtains +ℏ/2 or −ℏ/2 with probability 1/2 when he measures Sz​. Conditional on his result, Alice obtains the same sign with certainty if she measures Sz​. Under the EPR locality premise, this certainty supplies an element of reality for her Sz​; it is a conditional prediction after Bob learns his own random result, not a premeasurement prediction of its sign.
The normalized Sx​ eigenstates are ∣+⟩=(∣0⟩+∣1⟩)/2​ and ∣−⟩=(∣0⟩−∣1⟩)/2​. Expanding their tensor products gives
∣++⟩+∣−−⟩=∣00⟩+∣11⟩.
(2)
Consequently the same Bell state has equal, perfectly correlated Sx​ outcomes. Bob's two outcomes again each have probability 1/2, and the same locality-and-certainty argument applies separately to Alice's Sx​.
The disputed step is to combine predictions conditional on two incompatible choices into two simultaneous, measurement-independent pre-existing values. Quantum theory provides the two conditional ensembles, not a joint sharp assignment of Sx​ and Sz​. This is quantum steering. Bob cannot measure both incompatible observables sharply on the same member of the pair. Nor does conditional steering allow signalling: ignoring his result leaves Alice's reduced density matrix equal to I/2 for either measurement choice, by the no-communication theorem.
To test the stronger claim of a local hidden-variable theory, Alice needs several correlation settings, not just perfect agreement along the two common axes. For the CHSH inequality, choose observables with outcomes ±1:
A0​=σz​,A1​=σx​,B0​=2​σz​+σx​​,B1​=2​σz​−σx​​.
(3)
In the given Bell state, ⟨σz​⊗σz​⟩=⟨σx​⊗σx​⟩=1 and the crossed correlations vanish. Hence
S=⟨A0​B0​⟩+⟨A0​B1​⟩+⟨A1​B0​⟩−⟨A1​B1​⟩=22​,​
(4)
exceeding the local bound ∣S∣≤2. The bound assumes Bell local causality and measurement independence, with the appropriate treatment of sampling and settings.
Experimental Bell tests support violations rather than that local assignment. The Aspect switching experiment changed analyzer settings during separated photon flights, while leaving sampling and switching limitations. By the time of this examination, the Delft electron-spin Bell test had used electron spins at stations 1.3km apart, efficient spin readout and rapid random basis choices. The combination addressed both detection and locality loopholes, providing evidence against the tested local model class.[ref]
This does not prove that every conceivable notion of reality is false, or that noncommutation alone rules out every hidden-variable theory. A nonlocal theory can evade the local premise. What Alice can challenge is Bob's claim that locality, measurement-independent simultaneous values and all observed correlations can be retained together. An ordinary measurement disturbance at Bob's lab is not by itself a local explanation of Alice's conditional ensemble.
