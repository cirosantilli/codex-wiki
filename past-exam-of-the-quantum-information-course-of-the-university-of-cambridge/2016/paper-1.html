An eigenvalue of an observable in a known corresponding eigenstate is a simple example satisfying the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen criterion of reality: its value is predictable with certainty. For example, a spin angular momentum component in a prepared spin-up eigenstate has a definite predicted value. For a distant system, Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen invoked locality: a measurement performed on a sufficiently separated, noninteracting partner should not disturb that system. In the relativistic version here, the measurement events have spacelike separation.
The Pauli matrices obey [σz​,σx​]=2iσy​, so
[Sz​,Sx​]=iℏSy​=0.​
(1)
Their nondegenerate eigenstates are incompatible: an Sz​ eigenstate has equal probabilities for the two Sx​ outcomes, and conversely. Thus no single quantum state has both components sharply determined. The Robertson uncertainty relation gives ΔSz​ΔSx​≥ℏ∣⟨Sy​⟩∣/2, but its right-hand side may be zero; that alone is not a proof that simultaneous zero uncertainties exist. The absence of a common eigenstate supplies the stronger conclusion for this spin-half pair.
For the specified Bell state, Bob obtains +ℏ/2 or −ℏ/2 with probability 1/2 when he measures Sz​. Conditional on his result, Alice obtains the same sign with certainty if she measures Sz​. Under the EPR locality premise, this certainty supplies an element of reality for her Sz​; it is a conditional prediction after Bob learns his own random result, not a premeasurement prediction of its sign.
The normalized Sx​ eigenstates are ∣+⟩=(∣0⟩+∣1⟩)/2​ and ∣−⟩=(∣0⟩−∣1⟩)/2​. Expanding their tensor products gives
∣++⟩+∣−−⟩=∣00⟩+∣11⟩.
(2)
Consequently the same Bell state has equal, perfectly correlated Sx​ outcomes. Bob's two outcomes again each have probability 1/2, and the same locality-and-certainty argument applies separately to Alice's Sx​.
The disputed step is to combine predictions conditional on two incompatible choices into two simultaneous, measurement-independent pre-existing values. Quantum theory provides the two conditional ensembles, not a joint sharp assignment of Sx​ and Sz​. This is quantum steering. Bob cannot measure both incompatible observables sharply on the same member of the pair. Nor does conditional steering allow signalling: ignoring his result leaves Alice's reduced density matrix equal to I/2 for either measurement choice, by the no-communication theorem.
To test the stronger claim of a local hidden-variable theory, Alice needs several correlation settings, not just perfect agreement along the two common axes. For the CHSH inequality, choose observables with outcomes ±1:
A0​=σz​,A1​=σx​,B0​=2​σz​+σx​​,B1​=2​σz​−σx​​.
(3)
In the given Bell state, ⟨σz​⊗σz​⟩=⟨σx​⊗σx​⟩=1 and the crossed correlations vanish. Hence
S=⟨A0​B0​⟩+⟨A0​B1​⟩+⟨A1​B0​⟩−⟨A1​B1​⟩=22​,​
(4)
exceeding the local bound ∣S∣≤2. The bound assumes Bell local causality and measurement independence, with the appropriate treatment of sampling and settings.
Experimental Bell tests support violations rather than that local assignment. The Aspect switching experiment changed analyzer settings during separated photon flights, while leaving sampling and switching limitations. By the time of this examination, the Delft electron-spin Bell test had used electron spins at stations 1.3km apart, efficient spin readout and rapid random basis choices. The combination addressed both detection and locality loopholes, providing evidence against the tested local model class.[ref]
This does not prove that every conceivable notion of reality is false, or that noncommutation alone rules out every hidden-variable theory. A nonlocal theory can evade the local premise. What Alice can challenge is Bob's claim that locality, measurement-independent simultaneous values and all observed correlations can be retained together. An ordinary measurement disturbance at Bob's lab is not by itself a local explanation of Alice's conditional ensemble.

By Nielsen's pure-state conversion theorem, deterministic LOCC conversion requires the input vector of squared Schmidt coefficients to be majorized by the output vector. Entanglement can be discarded by local operations but cannot increase deterministically for these pure inputs. Probabilistic concentration is a separate question and is allowed below.[ref]
There is a dimensional qualification in the proposed measurement. Write P=∣0A′​⟩⟨0A′​∣+∣1A′​⟩⟨1A′​∣. The two supplied measurement operators obey
M1†​M1​+M2†​M2​=P.

Thus their effects form a POVM on the occupied support, where P is the identity. Alice's full space is three-dimensional, so these two operators alone do not sum to I3​. A full-space completion is M3​=∣2A′​⟩⟨2A′​∣. It has zero probability on either input, so it changes none of the requested outcomes. Strictly, the POVM consists of the effects Er​=Mr†​Mr​; the specified Mr​ additionally fix a quantum instrument and therefore the conditional states.

The diagonal success operator diag(λ1​/λ0​​,1) instead gives the standard single-copy entanglement concentration probability 2λ1​. Postselection can increase entanglement in the retained branch, while the outcome-averaged entanglement cannot increase under LOCC.

A weak measurement couples an observable only weakly to a meter, so the pointer distributions associated with its different eigenvalues overlap strongly. One trial supplies little information and a small disturbance; an ensemble is needed to estimate a small mean shift. It is not an exact determination of a previously hidden eigenvalue with no disturbance.
For pointer position q and conjugate momentum p, a short von Neumann measurement interaction has unitary U=exp(−igA⊗p/ℏ). An eigenvalue a translates the pointer by ga. The weak regime requires those translations to be small relative to the pointer width. Without postselection the first-order mean displacement is g⟨A⟩ for an initially uncorrelated, suitably centered meter.
If the system is prepared in ∣i⟩ and postselected in ∣f⟩, the unnormalized meter state is
⟨f∣U∣i⟩∣φ⟩≃⟨f∣i⟩(1−ℏig​Aw​p)∣φ⟩,Aw​=⟨f∣i⟩⟨f∣A∣i⟩​.​
(15)
For a real centered Gaussian pointer with no position–momentum correlation, the conditional position shift is gReAw​; the imaginary part affects the conjugate momentum distribution. The weak value can be complex or have a real part outside the observable's eigenvalue interval, because it is a conditional transition-amplitude ratio, not an eigenvalue.
An example apparatus uses photon polarization as the two-level system, a weak polarization-dependent transverse displacement as the interaction, and an output polarizer for postselection. Repeated position detections estimate the selected beam's displacement. Nearly orthogonal selection can make the conditional shift large, but also reduces the accepted sample and eventually invalidates the first-order expansion. The original weak-value proposal emphasized preselection, a weak coupling and postselection, rather than an anomalous result from an ordinary sharp spin measurement.[ref]
Weak coupling limits quantum measurement backaction per trial; it does not abolish the information–disturbance tradeoff. Repeating weak observations can accumulate substantial backaction, and discarding rejected trials cannot automatically improve the information available per prepared system. Conditional pointer amplification and a statistically improved parameter estimate are distinct claims.

Quantum teleportation transfers an unknown qubit state using a previously shared Bell pair, local operations and two classical bits. It requires no description of the unknown complex amplitudes and produces no extra copy.
Let the input be ∣χ⟩S​=α∣0⟩+β∣1⟩, and let Alice and Bob share ∣Φ+⟩AB​. Alice applies a controlled-NOT gate from S to A, then a Hadamard gate to S. Expanding the circuit gives the Bell-basis teleportation identity in computational-output form:
∣χ⟩S​∣Φ+⟩AB​⟼21​∑a,b∈{0,1}​∣a⟩S​∣b⟩A​XbZa∣χ⟩B​.
(16)
Her projective measurement yields each bit pair (a,b) with probability 1/4. She sends the two classical bits, and Bob applies ZaXb to recover ∣χ⟩. The order is the inverse of the displayed conditional operator; interchanging these Pauli corrections changes only an irrelevant global phase when both bits are one.
Before receiving the bits, Bob has
ρB​=41​∑a,b​XbZa∣χ⟩⟨χ∣ZaXb=2I​.
(17)
Thus teleportation respects the no-communication theorem and cannot transmit the usable unknown state faster than the classical message. Alice's input has been measured, and the shared entanglement resource has been consumed, in agreement with the no-cloning theorem. The protocol transfers the state, not the material particle initially carrying it.
A physical implementation must distribute and preserve an entangled pair, implement a Bell-basis measurement and transmit its result to a controllable output system. Imperfect entanglement, lost outcomes and noisy corrections reduce quantum fidelity; a heralded probabilistic experiment must report its success probability separately from conditional fidelity. Ideal deterministic teleportation assumes every Bell outcome can be identified and corrected.

Quantum error correction encodes a logical qubit into a larger Hilbert space, diagnoses a specified error family with an error syndrome, and reverses it without learning the protected logical amplitudes. It must preserve arbitrary superpositions, not just distinguish two classical codewords.
The bit-flip repetition code encodes
α∣0⟩+β∣1⟩⟼α∣000⟩+β∣111⟩.
(18)
Measure the commuting stabilizer generators Z1​Z2​ and Z2​Z3​ using ancillary qubits. Their outcomes reveal the parity relations, with the three-qubit bit-flip syndrome table
errorIX1​X2​X3​​(Z1​Z2​,Z2​Z3​)(+1,+1)(−1,+1)(−1,−1)(+1,−1)​recoveryIX1​X2​X3​​​​
(19)
Both codewords have the same parity checks, so the syndrome does not reveal α or β. For example, the X2​ error produces α∣010⟩+β∣101⟩; applying X2​ restores the encoded superposition. A coherent combination of correctable errors is also recoverable after the syndrome is recorded in an ancilla, by linearity.
This three-qubit code corrects one bit flip, not every one-qubit error. A physical Z1​ changes the relative logical phase without a nontrivial bit-flip syndrome. General single-qubit noise is a linear combination of I,X,Y,Z; protecting arbitrary single-qubit errors therefore needs a code such as the Shor code, the Steane code or the five-qubit code, with the corresponding enlarged syndrome structure.
The general exact-recovery criterion is the Knill--Laflamme condition. For code projector P and error operators Er​, there must be constants Crs​ such that
PEr†​Es​P=Crs​Pfor every r,s.​
(20)
It expresses that the syndrome information can depend on the error but not on the logical state. Encoding entangles several physical qubits; it does not create independent copies of an unknown input, so it respects the no-cloning theorem. Fault-tolerant implementations must also prevent noisy syndrome extraction and recovery gates from spreading an initially correctable error into an uncorrectable one.
