Evanescent probability current (J=mℏ​Im(ψ∗ψ′))

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics Potential barrier Quantum tunnelling
OurBigBook.com Words: 157
A nonzero probability density inside a barrier does not by itself imply transport. In one dimension, the probability current of a wavefunction is J=(ℏ/m)Im(ψ∗ψ′). A single evanescent wave Ce−κx has ψ∗ψ′=−κ∣C∣2e−2κx, a real quantity, hence J=0. This is what happens on the inaccessible side of a potential barrier extending to infinity with E<V0​.[ref]
For the finite-barrier wavefunction Aeκx+Be−κx, the cross terms instead give
J=m2ℏκ​Im(B∗A).
(79)
The relative quantum phase of the two probability amplitudes permits a nonzero, spatially constant probability current, even though neither exponential function separately carries it. The probability continuity equation gives ∂x​J=0 for this stationary state. Matching the wavefunction and its derivative also makes J continuous at both faces, so
Jinc​=mℏk​,Jref​=−mℏk​∣r∣2,Jtr​=mℏk​∣t∣2,R+T=1.
(80)
Here R=∣r∣2 is the quantum reflection probability, and T=Jtr​/Jinc​ is the transmission probability. The net exterior probability current equals the interior probability current; it is not lost as the wavefunction decreases. A physical incident wave packet supplies finite total probability, while the stationary state describes the energy component used to compute its transmitted flux.
