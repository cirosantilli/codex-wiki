Free expansion

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 3
Free expansion occurs when a gas spreads into an initially evacuated region without driving an external load. The standard idealization uses a rigid, insulated vessel: initially a partition confines the gas to volume V1​, while the remaining compartment contains vacuum. Open a passage and wait for thermodynamic equilibrium; the same fixed amount of substance n now occupies the larger accessible volume V2​. The outer vessel never changes size. This simple release separates three questions: what energy is conserved, whether temperature changes, and why entropy increases. [ref]
Choose the gas as the closed system, neglect heat exchange with the walls, and assume negligible changes in bulk kinetic energy and potential energy between the equilibrium endpoints. Insulation gives Qin​=0; no moving outer boundary or external load receives work, so Wout​=0. The first law of thermodynamics therefore gives
ΔU=Qin​−Wout​=0.
(26)
The gas can develop internal motion during the release; this does not supply external work. The intermediate state need not have one equilibrium pressure or temperature, so there is no actual quasistatic process curve p(V) to integrate. In particular, substituting the equilibrium ideal gas law into ∫pdV would describe a different process. [ref]
For an ideal gas, internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature. With positive heat capacity at constant volume, U(T2​)=U(T1​) implies T2​=T1​, and the ideal gas law gives p2​/p1​=V1​/V2​. Doubling the accessible volume thus halves the final pressure without changing the final temperature. This is an endpoint statement; it does not assert a spatially uniform, constant temperature during the rush. Insulation alone does not imply constant temperature: a reversible adiabatic expansion cools an ideal gas because it supplies work. [ref]
For a real gas, U(T,V) can depend on volume as well as temperature, so unchanged internal energy need not mean unchanged temperature. For a fixed-composition simple compressible system in a single thermodynamic phase, equilibrium identities using a Maxwell relation give
dU=CV​dT+[T(∂T∂p​)V​−p]dV,(∂V∂T​)U​=CV​p−T(∂p/∂T)V​​.
(27)
Here CV​ is the whole sample's heat capacity at constant volume. The sign depends on the equation of state; for an ideal gas the numerator vanishes. For a finite free expansion, solve U(T2​,V2​)=U(T1​,V1​), or integrate this slope along equilibrium states of equal internal energy; that mathematical curve does not describe the actual release. [ref]
Free expansion is useful because it tests how internal energy depends on number density while eliminating external expansion work. Its beautiful surprise is that an ideal gas can finish with the same temperature and internal energy but greater entropy: a thermometer alone cannot diagnose reversibility. See entropy change in free expansion for the calculation and temperature sensitivity of a free-expansion experiment for the experimental qualification. The continuous-flow throttling associated with the Joule-Thomson coefficient has a different balance: under its usual assumptions it preserves enthalpy, whereas the gas-only idealization here preserves internal energy. [ref]
