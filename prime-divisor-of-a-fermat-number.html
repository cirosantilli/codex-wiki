Prime divisor of a Fermat number (ordp​(2)=2n+1)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory Fermat number
OurBigBook.com Words: 259 Articles: 1
For a prime number p, the exact criterion is
p∣Fn​⟺ordp​(2)=2n+1,n≥0,
(8)
where p must be different from two and ordp​(2) is the multiplicative order of two modulo p. If p∣Fn​, then 22n≡−1(modp): the order divides 2n+1 but not 2n, so it is exactly 2n+1. Conversely, that order makes 22n a square root of unity different from one in the prime field Fp​; it must be −1, because (x−1)(x+1)=0 has no other roots in a field.
For example, modulo seventeen the powers of two are 2,4,8,16,15,13,9,1. Their first return to one occurs after eight steps. The half-way value is 16=−1(mod17), proving that 17∣F2​. Likewise, ord641​(2)=64 identifies 641 as a prime factor of F5​. These are statements about divisibility, not evidence that the entire Fermat number is prime.
The multiplicative order always divides p−1, by Fermat's little theorem, so p≡1(mod2n+1). In particular, for n≥1 every prime divisor of a Fermat number is one modulo four. That weaker modular congruence is insufficient: thirteen is one modulo four, but ord13​(2)=12, which is not a power of two. It divides no Fermat number at any index. The exact order criterion explains the whole family, instead of testing finitely many terms.
