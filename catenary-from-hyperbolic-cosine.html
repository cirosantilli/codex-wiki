Catenary from hyperbolic cosine (y=acosh(x/a),a>0)

 ... Analysis Real analysis Calculus Exponential function Hyperbolic function Hyperbolic cosine
OurBigBook.com Words: 323
A catenary is the static shape of an ideal flexible, inextensible chain hanging under its own gravitational weight in a uniform gravitational field, with uniform linear mass density along its arc length. With the lowest point above x=0, one convenient vertical origin gives
y(x)=acosh(x/a),a>0.
(42)
Here a has units of length. The supports and the chain's arc length determine the scale and position; horizontal and vertical shifts give the general catenary. [ref]
For a=1, the three points at x=−1,0,1 have heights cosh1≈1.543081, 1, and cosh1≈1.543081. If heights should instead be measured from the lowest point, subtract a:
yshifted​(x)=a(cosh(x/a)−1).
(43)
The same three heights are then approximately 0.543081, 0, and 0.543081. The shift changes the vertical origin and preserves the catenary shape.
The scale also measures tension: if H>0 is its constant horizontal component, μ is the linear mass density, and g is the magnitude of gravitational acceleration, then a=H/(μg). A short force balance explains this. For the portion from the lowest point to x≥0, with arc length s(x), vertical tension balances its gravitational weight and horizontal tension gives
Hy′(x)=μgs(x),s′(x)=1+y′(x)2​.
(44)
Applying differentiation yields ay′′=1+y′2​. The proposed hyperbolic cosine solves this differential equation, because y′=sinh(x/a) and ay′′=cosh(x/a)=1+sinh2(x/a)​. This is the mechanism behind the shape, under the stated ideal chain assumptions.
The same identity simplifies the arc length between symmetric supports at x=±b:
L=∫−bb​1+y′(x)2​dx=∫−bb​cosh(x/a)dx=2asinh(b/a).
(45)
For a=b=1, L≈2.350402, exceeding the horizontal span of two. A larger a at fixed b gives a shallower sag: the Taylor series gives a(cosh(x/a)−1)=x2/(2a)+x4/(24a3)+⋯. Thus a parabola is a local approximation, while the exact ideal chain shape is a catenary. The useful connection is that the same hyperbolic Pythagorean identity controls its coordinates, force balance, and arc length.
