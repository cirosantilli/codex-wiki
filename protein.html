Protein

 Home Biology
OurBigBook.com Words: 11k Articles: 96
A protein is a biological molecule made of one or more polypeptide chains: ordered amino acid residues joined by peptide bonds. Biological translation assembles a chain in the order specified by messenger RNA codons. Protein folding then brings parts of that chain into spatial relationships that support its function. See Cooper, The Cell: protein composition and structure. These processes can overlap in a biological cell; the sequence-to-shape explanation does not require waiting until biological translation finishes before any folding begins. See Cooper, The Cell: protein folding and processing.
The useful connection is from information to physical work. A protein's protein primary structure records an order; its protein tertiary structure can bring distant amino acid residues together at an active site. An enzyme uses such an arrangement to recognize a substrate and accelerate a chemical reaction. Other proteins serve as receptors, membrane transport proteins or structural material. For example, actin assembles into filaments whose mechanical properties can be described by biopolymer mechanics. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: protein function and binding.
Order matters beyond composition. Let A stand for alanine and M for methionine, and read from the N-terminus to the C-terminus. The illustrative strings AMAMAMAMAMAM and AAAAAAMMMMMM each contain exactly six A and six M amino acid residues. Each has 12−1=11 peptide bonds when made into one unbranched polypeptide chain. In the first string all eleven neighboring pairs have different letters; in the second only the pair at positions 6 and 7 does. A composition histogram therefore loses information about which amino acid residues are neighbors. Their arrangements can affect protein folding and function; these short invented strings neither specify a known functional protein nor establish what shape either would adopt.
Protein structure is a moving arrangement, not a permanently rigid outline. Substrate binding can change an enzyme's shape through induced fit, and a functional protein can include flexible regions. Sequence, surroundings and interacting molecules must therefore be considered together. See Alberts et al.: binding, conformational changes and protein function.
