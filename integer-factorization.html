Integer factorization (N=∏p​pvp​(N))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 10
Integer factorization seeks the prime factorization of a positive integer N. For N>1, this means identifying its prime factors and their multiplicities; one has the empty prime factorization. Finding one proper divisor 1<d<N is useful progress, but need not finish the job: factor d and N/d further until all factors are prime numbers.
For example, 77=7⋅11 is a semiprime, whereas 231=3⋅7⋅11 has three prime factors. Primality testing asks whether a proposed integer is prime; it need not return a factor when the answer is no. Trial division is a direct factoring method: try division by small prime numbers until a divisor is found. More elaborate methods obtain a proper divisor through a greatest common divisor of N with a specially constructed integer.
The beautiful trick in congruence of squares is that two integers with equal squares modulo N can reveal different factors through their sum and difference. In the worked N=77 example, 202≡132(mod77) gives gcd(20−13,77)=7 and gcd(20+13,77)=11. Computing those greatest common divisors uses the Euclidean algorithm; it does not require trying every potential factor. A factor base supplies a systematic way to construct useful equal-square relations. [ref].
This connection is useful in cryptography because recovering the factors of an RSA cryptosystem modulus defeats the factoring assumption behind its construction. That application concerns large, carefully chosen inputs; the small examples here explain the arithmetic mechanism and are not benchmarks or estimates of present cryptographic security.
