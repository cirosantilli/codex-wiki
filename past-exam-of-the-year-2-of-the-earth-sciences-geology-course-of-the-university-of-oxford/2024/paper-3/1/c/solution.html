The water supports only P waves. At oblique incidence the seabed can convert a P wave into an SV-wave in the sediment, and the return SV-wave can convert back to a P wave in the water. Further seismic mode conversion can occur at the basement. Thus the down-going and up-going sediment legs can independently be P or S. The S entries refer to SV-waves; a fluid P wave source in this two-dimensional isotropic geometry does not excite the decoupled SH-wave sector.

For example, the unconverted P branch gives 2(50+20)tan36.870∘=105 m. The earliest geometrically permitted basement P seismic head wave is the doubly converted path with sediment S legs, at approximately 90 m. The corresponding basement S family first appears at approximately 297 m. These limiting distances describe existence of ray branches; a finite detectable wave amplitude also depends on conversion efficiency and acquisition noise.
There is no separate finite-distance P head wave at the water–sediment interface: Vp,w​=Vp,s​=1500 ms−1, so the nominal critical angle is 90∘, and a ray from a source 50 m above the interface cannot reach it at finite horizontal distance. Nor can the slower sediment S speed, 900 ms−1, support a seismic head wave launched from water: it would require sinθw​=1500/900>1. A seismic reflection still exists at the seabed because the densities, hence the acoustic impedances, differ.
