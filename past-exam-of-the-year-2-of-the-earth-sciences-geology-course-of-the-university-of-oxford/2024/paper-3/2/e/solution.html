Assume that the intrusion has a positive magnetic susceptibility contrast, that induced magnetization dominates its remanent magnetization, and that the local present-day geomagnetic field points roughly northward and downward, as in this northern mid-latitude setting. Write
ΔM≃ΔχH0​,ΔT=∣B0​+b∣−∣B0​∣≃B0​⋅b,

where b is the anomalous magnetic field and the last expression assumes ∣b∣≪∣B0​∣. A total-field magnetic anomaly measures a field projection, so it need not have the same sign everywhere above a high-susceptibility body.[ref]
The 100 km east–west extent makes an approximately two-dimensional, east–west-striking body a useful model away from its ends. On a profile drawn north on the left, south on the right, the expected normally induced response has a subsidiary negative lobe on the northern side and a stronger positive lobe over the body, displaced towards the south. The northward horizontal field component makes the anomaly asymmetric; its downward component produces the dominant positive response. The field falls back towards the regional value away from the intrusion. Broad bodies can have edge shoulders or a broad central high, so the sketch represents a qualitative shape rather than one uniquely specified curve.
An exactly vertical inducing field would remove this north–south skew for a symmetric induced body. Real magnetic inclination and magnetic declination should be taken from a suitable geomagnetic field model for the survey date and location; no exact local value is supplied here. Strong oblique or reversed remanent magnetization can shift or reverse the lobes, and heterogeneous magnetic susceptibility, body dip and three-dimensional end effects can alter the profile. Higher magnetic susceptibility alone does not determine a unique amplitude or polarity pattern without these qualifications.
