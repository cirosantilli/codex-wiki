For the two negative gravity anomalies in the actual Figure 1, examine where the steepest flanks occur within the total fall into the trough. The solid upper intrusion profile has maximum slope relatively low on its flanks, close to the flat trough; its body widens downwards. The lower basin profile has maximum slope relatively high on its flanks, near the shoulders; its body narrows downwards. The dashed gradient traces and arrows mark these transitions. The signed derivative changes sign on opposite sides of a symmetric trough; a plot of gradient magnitude instead gives two positive peaks.
A shallow edge controls a relatively abrupt local change, whereas deeper geophysical density contrast spreads its effect over greater horizontal distance. Downward-narrowing geometry leaves relatively more of the anomaly associated with the shallow broad part, placing the strong flank change nearer the outer shoulders. Downward-widening geometry adds a broader deep contribution beneath the narrow outcrop, shifting the inflection towards the lower part of the trough. Thus the location of a flank's maximum slope relative to its shoulder and minimum is more useful here than the shared −40 mGal peak alone.
A symmetric constant-density body with vertical sides gives a symmetric negative trough with paired rounded flanks; for a broad shallow body the main slope changes occur approximately halfway down those flanks. It is intermediate between the two end members, rather than a triangular anomaly or a rectangular discontinuity. Exact fractional inflection heights depend on width, depth, burial and rounding. This qualitative inference assumes approximately comparable simple bodies, uniform geophysical density contrast, and a properly removed regional field. Gravity inversion is non-unique: lateral density changes, multiple bodies, and a buried top can imitate changes of wall slope. Use the seismic reflection constraints to test the interpretation.
