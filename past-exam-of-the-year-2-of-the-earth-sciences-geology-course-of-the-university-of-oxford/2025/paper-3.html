Let Sg​ be the fraction of the pore space occupied by gas, and let ϕ be the porosity. At fixed porosity and unchanged rock framework, the bulk density is
ρ=(1−ϕ)ρs​+ϕ[(1−Sg​)ρw​+Sg​ρg​].
(1)
Replacing water with much less dense gas therefore decreases the bulk density approximately linearly, as in Figure 1. The liquid and gas densities here are those at reservoir conditions.
A pore fluid has negligible static shear modulus, so the rock framework chiefly controls the effective shear modulus μ. If μ changes little, Vs​=μ/ρ​ increases gently as the density falls. This does not imply that S waves travel through the pore gas itself: their restoring force comes from the connected solid framework.
The P wave speed depends on both bulk modulus and shear modulus:
Vp​=ρκ+4μ/3​​.
(2)
Gas is much more compressible than water. Even a small connected gas fraction can strongly reduce the effective fluid bulk modulus, approximately through the compliance average κf−1​≃(1−Sg​)/κw​+Sg​/κg​ for a pressure-equilibrated mixture. The saturated rock's effective bulk modulus consequently falls rapidly at first, overwhelming the smaller density reduction and causing the sharp initial fall in Vp​. Once the fluid is already very compliant, further gas replacement changes the stiffness less; the continuing density decrease can then make Vp​ rise again. Figure 1 assumes a particular saturation response: patchy fluids, changing effective pressure, and frequency-dependent fluid motion can alter it. The plotted Vs​ is only nearly constant, so the plot should not be interpreted as an exact fixed-μ constitutive model.

Rj​=Zj2​+Zj1​Zj2​−Zj1​​,Zpj​=ρj​Vpj​,Zsj​=ρj​Vsj​.

Here acoustic impedance is the P wave impedance; ρVs​ is the corresponding shear-wave impedance. Positive R preserves the chosen stress-wave seismic reflection polarity; negative R reverses it. These are amplitude coefficients, not reflected energy fractions. At normal incidence in the ideal isotropic model there is no P-to-S mode conversion. The cap-rock impedances are
Zp1​=2.25×106,Zs1​=0.900×106kgm−2s−1.
(4)
The following are approximate readings of the supplied Figure 1 PNG, checked against the matching source PDF, rather than exact tabulated parameters. At zero gas, use ρ≃1.75 gcm−3, Vp​≃1.75 kms−1, and Vs​≃0.60 kms−1; at full gas use ρ≃1.25 gcm−3, Vp​≃1.15 kms−1, and Vs​≃0.65 kms−1. Both end members change seismic reflection polarity, but the P wave change is much larger: approximately +0.15 to −0.22, compared with +0.08 to −0.05 for S waves. Thus a calibrated P wave survey is better for distinguishing these two saturation end members and should give a stronger reflection from the fully gas-filled reservoir. A negative P wave reflection relative to the water-filled prediction is a useful gas indicator in this stipulated model. In a real seismic reflection survey, lithology, porosity, effective pressure, and seismic wavelet phase also affect the response, so a bright or reversed reflection alone does not prove the presence of gas.

A proton-precession magnetometer measures the scalar magnitude F=∣B∣ of the local magnetic field. Its sensor contains a proton-rich liquid, such as a hydrocarbon or suitable water-based fluid. A strong current through a coil briefly polarizes the nuclear magnetic moments, producing a small net magnetization. The polarizing field is directed sufficiently across the local magnetic field to leave a transverse component when the current is switched off.
The protons then undergo Larmor precession about the ambient magnetic field. Their rotating net magnetization induces a decaying alternating voltage in a receiving coil. Measuring the frequency determines the field:
ω=γp​F,f=2πγp​​F,2πγp​​≃42.58 MHzT−1.
(11)
For example, 50 μT gives about 2.13 kHz. The known proton gyromagnetic ratio provides an absolute frequency-to-field scale; this instrument does not measure the field's direction. Strong spatial gradients cause different parts of the liquid to precess at different rates and shorten the usable signal, which matters very close to a buried steel object.[ref]

b OurBigBook.com
Lay out a surveyed grid extending beyond the possible burial area into unaffected ground. Use closely spaced parallel traverses with measurements along each line; spacing should resolve a car-scale magnetic anomaly, with a finer grid over any candidate. For a shallow car, a pilot survey with roughly metre-scale spacing is a sensible starting point, refined according to anomaly width rather than treated as a universal prescription. Record position, time, sensor height, repeat readings and any nearby interference. Cross-lines and repeat stations check reproducibility. Maintain a constant sensor height on a nonmagnetic staff and remove magnetic items from the operator; map fences, gates, pipes, buildings and vehicles before interpreting an anomaly.
Operate a synchronized stationary base-station magnetometer in quiet ground while the rover surveys, or make frequent base returns if a second instrument is unavailable. A total-field magnetic anomaly is the corrected field magnitude minus a specified background:
ΔT(x)=Fcorrected​(x)−Fbackground​(x).
(12)
For a field-sized target, a local background estimated away from the car is often sufficient. A larger regional survey may subtract the International Geomagnetic Reference Field and an independently justified regional trend. Do not remove the car's broad anomaly by overfitting that trend.
Correct rover observations for time-varying geomagnetic field using the base record, for example Fc​(x,t)=Fr​(x,t)−[Fb​(t)−Fb​(t0​)]. Check instrument offsets and tie-line differences, reject disturbed or poor-quality readings, and allow for differing sensor heights. Preserve localized target signals when interpolating a contour map or comparing with a magnetic dipole model. There are no compulsory gravity-style free-air or Bouguer gravity anomaly reductions for this magnetic search.
Postpone surveying during a geomagnetic storm or rapid irregular magnetic variation, when base correction may be inadequate. Avoid periods of moving farm machinery or electrical interference, and recognize that strong permanent cultural interference or highly magnetic host rock may make this particular method unsuitable. Ordinary cloud cover or darkness does not prevent a magnetic survey.[ref]

Strong geological magnetic anomalies generally occur in rocks containing abundant strongly magnetic iron oxides, especially magnetite and titanomagnetite. Many mafic rocks, including basalt and gabbro, and some ultramafic rocks have large signatures; magnetite-rich iron formations can be exceptionally strong, while serpentinization can create additional magnetite. Actual magnetic susceptibility and remanent magnetization, rather than the rock name alone, determine the response. Sedimentary and felsic rocks are often weaker but have important exceptions.
Magnetite, titanomagnetite and maghemite exhibit strong ferrimagnetism; some pyrrhotite is also strongly magnetic. Hematite is generally much weaker in induced magnetic susceptibility, although it can carry stable remanent magnetization. Quartz and calcite have very small, usually negative, susceptibilities, and many ordinary silicates have only weak paramagnetism. A steel car exhibits strong ferromagnetism and can therefore stand out against a weakly magnetic host, but its shape and pre-existing remanent magnetization complicate the signal.[ref]

Use compression-positive principal stresses σ1​≥σ2​≥σ3​. The two-dimensional Mohr's circle shown is the σ1​–σ3​ circle: its horizontal axis is compressive normal stress σn​, its vertical axis signed shear stress τ, its centre is C=(σ1​+σ3​)/2, and its radius is R=(σ1​−σ3​)/2. The intercepts (σ1​,0) and (σ3​,0) represent principal stress planes, on which shear stress vanishes. The top and bottom points have ∣τ∣=R, the maximum shear stress.
In the rock sketch, θ is the counterclockwise angle from the σ1​ direction to the plane's unit normal vector n=(cosθ,sinθ). Choose the clockwise unit tangent t=(sinθ,−cosθ) and define τ=t⋅σcn, with σc=diag(σ1​,σ3​) compression positive. Thus a positive θ produces a positive 2θ rotation on the plotted circle. The actual traction on the positive-normal face is −σcn=−σn​n−τt. Reversing the tangent convention reverses the plotted shear sign, not the physical prediction.
In three dimensions there are circles for each pair of principal stresses. The largest is the σ1​–σ3​ circle; a general three-dimensional plane requires all three principal stresses and its normal, rather than a single angle on this circle.

For a plane whose normal lies in the σ1​–σ3​ plane, rotate through 2θ from (σ1​,0) in the stated sign convention. The circle point gives
σn​=C+Rcos2θ=σ1​cos2θ+σ3​sin2θ,τ=Rsin2θ.​
(17)
The normal-to-plane angle is essential: if the angle supplied is between the plane itself and σ1​, use its complement for θ. The principal stress planes have zero shear stress, and planes at 45∘ to those principal stress planes have maximum ∣τ∣.
For an arbitrary plane in a fully three-dimensional stress field, let ni​ be its direction cosines in the principal stress basis. Then
σn​=∑i=13​σi​ni2​,∣τ∣=∑i=13​σi2​ni2​−σn2​​.
(18)
These follow by resolving the traction into its normal and tangential components; the compression-positive tensor is the negative of the usual tension-positive Cauchy stress tensor. The family of Mohr circles visualizes the possible combinations, but the two-dimensional 2θ rule alone does not specify an arbitrary three-dimensional orientation.[ref]

Draw a failure envelope in the (σn′​,τ) plane. A useful brittle shear-failure approximation is the Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion
∣τ∣=c+μf​σn′​,μf​=tanφ,
(19)
where c is cohesion, φ the internal friction angle, and σn′​ the effective compressive normal stress. A circle wholly inside the envelope is below this ideal failure threshold. Increasing the differential stress expands it until it first touches the envelope: the tangent points identify the failing plane orientations. For a linear envelope, tangency implies
R=C′sinφ+ccosφ,σ1′​=σ3′​1−sinφ1+sinφ​+1−sinφ2ccosφ​.
(20)
With the angle convention above, the upper tangent point is at 2θ=90∘+φ. Its plane therefore makes 45∘−φ/2 with the maximum-compression direction; there is a conjugate plane on the other side. For φ=30∘, this is 30∘, rather than the 45∘ plane of maximum shear stress. An existing weak fault can reactivate first, with its own lower cohesion and friction, and need not have this optimum new-fracture orientation.
For tensile brittle fracture, add a tensile strength cutoff: failure occurs when σ3′​≤−T0​, where T0​>0. The opening fracture is approximately perpendicular to the minimum-compression direction. Real rock envelopes curve, especially at small confinement; a straight shear envelope must not be extrapolated as an exact tensile law. High confinement can instead favor distributed or ductile deformation, so merely drawing a larger circle does not establish that brittle failure is the active mechanism.[ref]

Pore fluid pressure P reduces the compressive load carried across grain contacts or an accessible fault. In the usual elementary approximation,
σ′=σc−PI,σi′​=σi​−P,σn′​=σn​−P,τ′=τ.
(21)
At fixed total stress, increasing P translates the entire Mohr circle left by P without changing its radius or the differential stress. The circle therefore approaches the frictional failure envelope even though the applied shear has not increased. Equivalently, in total-stress coordinates the strength on an existing plane is c+μf​(σn​−P), reduced by μf​P. For a known orientation and constant frictional parameters, the ideal critical pressure is
Pcrit​=σn​−μf​∣τ∣−c​,
(22)
provided the plane is in contact and sliding failure is reached before tensile opening.
For the diagram's illustrative σ1​=80, σ3​=20, c=10 MPa and μf​=0.6, the dry circle is below failure. The freely oriented tangency threshold is about P=8.4 MPa; the 15 MPa circle is already beyond that threshold and shows the loading tendency, not a stable post-failure state. If P grows enough that σ3​−P≤−T0​, fluid pressure can instead open a tensile fracture. A porous-continuum effective stress law may use σi′​=σi​−αP with a coefficient α different from one. Also, changing fluid pressure can change total stress through constrained deformation; pure horizontal translation assumes those total stresses remain fixed.[ref]

Fault slip must taper toward zero at the tips of an isolated finite fault, because its two sides are still joined beyond the tips. Accumulating more central displacement at a fixed short length steepens the displacement gradient and loads the surrounding rock, especially near the tips. In a simple elastic scaling, a fault of length L and characteristic displacement D requires a characteristic driving shear stress of order
Δτ∼μLD​,
(23)
where μ is the shear modulus and the omitted coefficient depends on fault geometry. The host's finite strength prevents this gradient increasing indefinitely. Tip damage, new cracking and linkage with neighboring segments allow the fault system to lengthen and accommodate more displacement without unbounded tip loading. Self-similar growth at roughly comparable driving stress consequently gives the approximate fault displacement-length scaling D∝L; displacement also commonly increases toward the interior of a fault segment.[ref]
The word “must” describes this mechanical limitation over sustained growth. It is not a rule that every earthquake or every increment of accumulated fault slip lengthens the mapped trace: strong barriers, interactions and early establishment of fault length can permit substantial later displacement at nearly constant length.

A blind reverse fault can transfer displacement into folding above its buried tip. Look for an asymmetric growing anticline, often with a steep forelimb and gentler backlimb, aligned with the inferred fault strike. A fault-propagation fold is one possible geometry; the fold alone does not uniquely establish the fault's depth or shape. Exposed older geological strata may be more steeply tilted than younger deposits, while growth strata thin and onlap toward an uplifting crest or fan in dip as deformation accumulates. Progressive unconformities and warped young deposits help distinguish active folding from an inherited fold.
In the landscape, seek an elongate uplifted ridge, warped or tilted river terraces and alluvial fans, localized fold scarps, incision across the rising structure, and drainage deflection or abandoned channels. Compare landforms of known relative ages: increasing deformation with age records repeated motion. Opposite fold limbs may show contrasting uplift and tilting, while active axial surfaces can concentrate deformation into narrow belts even without the main fault reaching the surface. Combine geological mapping and dating with seismic reflection or evidence from geodesy where available; an erosional ridge alone is not proof of a buried active reverse fault.[ref]

Interferometric synthetic-aperture radar compares the phase of coherent radar returns acquired before and after the earthquake from closely repeated viewing geometry. After image registration and removal of orbital and topographic phase, the residual interferometric phase constrains relative ground displacement along the radar line of sight. Defining uLOS​ positive toward the satellite and choosing the interferogram's phase order accordingly,
Δϕdef​=λ4π​uLOS​(mod2π).
(24)
One complete colour cycle therefore represents λ/2 of relative line-of-sight displacement. Reversing the image order reverses the displayed phase sign. This observes a projection of both horizontal and vertical motion, not uplift alone.[ref]
If the main reverse rupture reaches the surface, coherent areas on opposite sides can show a displacement step: fringes become offset, truncated or abruptly juxtaposed along a trace. A narrow belt can lose interferometric coherence where cracking, intense gradients or changed scatterers prevent reliable phase comparison. If the rupture remains blind, a simple elastic model instead predicts a continuous surface displacement field, often a broad asymmetric lobe with continuous curved or closed fringes and a smooth displacement gradient across the projected tip.
These are diagnostic tendencies, not a unique test. An ordinary wrapped-phase boundary is just a 2π colour reset and is not itself a rupture. A step of an integer number of cycles can be hidden in wrapped phase; loss of interferometric coherence can hide either kind of near-field deformation. Vegetation, atmosphere and viewing direction also affect the pattern, and a displacement perpendicular to the radar line of sight is poorly sensed. Inspect coherence and unwrapped displacement, compare ascending and descending views where possible, and test a fault model against field observations. A sharp fold scarp above a blind rupture or a secondary shallow fault can also create localized gradients, so smooth fringes do not prove a buried tip and broken fringes alone do not prove a surface-breaking main rupture.

For a surface-breaking reverse earthquake, expect a fault scarp or disrupted belt with the hanging wall displaced upward and commonly over the footwall. Small thrust faults, splays and backthrusts may repeat or override shallow deposits. Shortened soil, roads or other markers may buckle, while soft sediment can form pressure ridges and compression folds. Near the scarp, gravitational collapse and local bending can also produce tensile fissures and small normal faults; a reverse event need not create compression everywhere at the surface.
For a blind reverse rupture, expect coherent uplift and warping, newly tilted surfaces, or a fold scarp without a throughgoing primary fault offset. Small flexural-slip surfaces along bedding, secondary thrust faults or backthrusts, and opening cracks or small normal faults over the bent outer arc of the anticline can accompany the folding. Deformation may be distributed or localized at fold hinges. Thus a blind main fault does not imply an entirely unbroken ground surface, and secondary cracking must be distinguished from the principal rupture by its geometry and displacement pattern. Shaking-triggered landslides or soil liquefaction can accompany either case and should not automatically be mapped as primary tectonic rupture.[ref]

A Sun-synchronous orbit maintains approximately constant orientation relative to the mean Sun, so corresponding passes over a location occur at nearly the same local solar time. It is usually a nearly polar, slightly retrograde low Earth orbit. Earth rotates beneath the successive tracks, allowing broad coverage; the satellite is not stationary over a location and does not necessarily revisit that location every day.
It is attained by selecting altitude, orbital eccentricity and orbital inclination so that nodal precession caused by Earth's equatorial bulge matches the mean apparent annual motion of the Sun, about 360∘ per year eastward. To leading order,
Ω˙=−23​J2​n(a(1−e2)RE​​)2cosi.
(25)

  • (III): x=−5.515, y>8.955, the nepheline–albite boundary above the intersection.
The opposite extension of each line is metastable because the third mineral has lower thermodynamic potential there. All three minerals can coexist at the intersection in this fixed-temperature, fixed-pressure activity model.

Equalities give the stable coexistence boundaries described above. In particular, a point between the two descending line extensions is not automatically a fourth field: only the lower line borders the stable corundum region on each side of the intersection. The free-energy comparisons, rather than the appearance of the complete straight-line sketch, select the stable mineral.
