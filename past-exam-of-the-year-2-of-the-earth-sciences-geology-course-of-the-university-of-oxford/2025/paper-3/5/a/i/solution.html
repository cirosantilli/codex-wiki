A Sun-synchronous orbit maintains approximately constant orientation relative to the mean Sun, so corresponding passes over a location occur at nearly the same local solar time. It is usually a nearly polar, slightly retrograde low Earth orbit. Earth rotates beneath the successive tracks, allowing broad coverage; the satellite is not stationary over a location and does not necessarily revisit that location every day.
It is attained by selecting altitude, orbital eccentricity and orbital inclination so that nodal precession caused by Earth's equatorial bulge matches the mean apparent annual motion of the Sun, about 360∘ per year eastward. To leading order,

Here a is the semi-major axis, e the orbital eccentricity, i the orbital inclination, n=GME​/a3​ the mean motion, and J2​ Earth's dimensionless quadrupole coefficient. A retrograde inclination has cosi<0, giving the required positive precession; typical imaging altitudes yield inclinations around 97∘–99∘. The launch establishes the required orbital plane, and subsequent orbit maintenance limits drift.
This geometry suits repeated optical and multispectral imaging of land, vegetation and oceans, because comparable local time reduces illumination and shadow differences. Landsat and Sentinel-2 are examples. Radar missions such as Sentinel-1 also use this orbit for regular global sampling even though their illumination is supplied by the radar. Constant local time helps comparison but does not remove seasonal changes in solar elevation.[ref]
