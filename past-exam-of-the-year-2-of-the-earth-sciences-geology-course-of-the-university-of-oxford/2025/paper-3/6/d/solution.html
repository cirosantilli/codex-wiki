The photic zone is the illuminated layer supporting photosynthesis; its euphotic depth is conventionally associated with about 1% of surface photosynthetically active radiation, integrated over approximately 400–700 nm. This is a useful convention rather than a universal organism-specific compensation depth. Spectral filtering can be represented locally by
Ed​(z,λ)=Ed​(0,λ)exp[−∫0z​Kd​(ζ,λ)dζ],
(30)
where Kd​ is the wavelength-dependent diffuse attenuation coefficient. It includes effects of absorption and scattering and is not simply equal to the absorption coefficient a.
In clear, low-chlorophyll water, pure water removes red light strongly, while weak blue pigment and CDOM absorption permits blue light, typically around 450–490 nm, to penetrate furthest. The deepest usable light is consequently dim and blue-rich, and the euphotic zone can extend relatively deep. At high pigment concentrations, phytoplankton pigments preferentially remove blue and red light; associated detritus or CDOM can remove still more blue. The remaining transmission window commonly shifts toward blue-green or green wavelengths, roughly 500–570 nm, and the total light falls off faster, so the euphotic depth is shallower. Exact window and depth depend on the full particle/dissolved mixture, not chlorophyll alone. The diagram normalizes the two deep spectra separately to show their different shapes, not equal absolute light at a common depth.
This confines photosynthetic growth to a thinner illuminated interval in high-chlorophyll water and allows a deeper potential habitat in clear low-chlorophyll water, subject also to nutrient supply, mixing and the organism's compensation requirement. Cells near the base benefit from low-light acclimation and pigment systems that capture the remaining wavelengths. In blue-rich deep ocean water, blue-absorbing pigments and some low-light-adapted picoplankton can be favored; where the surviving light is greener, groups with effective green-absorbing accessory pigments, such as certain phycobilins, gain an advantage. A pigment appears coloured by light it fails to absorb, so green-absorbing accessory pigments need not themselves look green. There is no universal deep species determined by chlorophyll concentration alone. A deep chlorophyll maximum can partly reflect increased pigment per cell rather than a proportional maximum in carbon biomass.[ref]
