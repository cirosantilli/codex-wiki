The P wave source is normally the more useful depth source for the end members and for the stated approximately 50% gas case, because the predicted ∣Rp​∣ exceeds ∣Rs​∣, making the reservoir-top arrival easier to identify, assuming comparable source and recording quality. For a horizontal reflector and vertical two-way travel time t entirely through the homogeneous cap rock,
h=2Vp1​tp​​=750tp​ m,
(9)
or h=Vs1​ts​/2=300ts​ m for an identified S wave arrival, with times in seconds. The reservoir's own wave speed is not the overburden wave speed used in this conversion. Nonzero-offset data require moveout correction and an appropriate overburden velocity model; no numerical depth can be obtained without a measured time.
At approximately 50% gas, Figure 1 gives ρ≃1.5 gcm−3, Vp​≃1.05 kms−1, and Vs​≃0.62–0.63 kms−1. Thus
Zp2​≃1.58×106,Rp​≃−0.18,Zs2​≃(0.93–0.945)×106,Rs​≃+0.02.
(10)
The S wave reflector is then weak because its impedance is nearly matched to the cap rock. An exact impedance match, ρ2​Vs2​=ρ1​Vs1​, gives Rs​=0: a reservoir top can exist without producing an observable normal-incidence S wave reflection. Rounding the 50% Vs​ to 0.60 would suggest exactly that cancellation, but the plotted line sits slightly higher. The continuous Zs​ curve crosses the cap-rock value at a somewhat greater saturation; the coarse figure does not justify a precise percentage.
There is also a smaller-saturation P wave impedance match: Rp​ changes sign between zero and modest gas saturation, so it must pass through zero. Thus neither source guarantees a detectable reflector at every saturation. Joint P wave and S wave observations help distinguish a weak impedance contrast from an absent reservoir; a slower S wave speed alone is no reason to prefer it when its reflected amplitude is near zero.
