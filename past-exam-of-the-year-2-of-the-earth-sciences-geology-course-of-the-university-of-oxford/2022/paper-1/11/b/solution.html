Four documented examples, each tied to its actual context, are:
  • Geographic range: widespread marine bivalve genera survived the end-Cretaceous event better than geographically restricted genera. Spatial spread can reduce exposure to some geographically heterogeneous stresses, although this does not guarantee survival of a global perturbation.[ref]
  • Body size: large-bodied mammals were disproportionately lost during the late Quaternary. The global analysis documents size selectivity across continents and trophic groups; this is not evidence that large body size always raises extinction risk in every geological interval.[ref]
  • Physiological buffering and respiratory capacity: poor capacity to tolerate elevated carbon dioxide and associated oxygen stress has been linked to higher vulnerability among end-Permian marine groups. This is a paleophysiological interpretation informed by living relatives and fossil traits, rather than a direct measurement of a fossil animal’s tolerance.[ref]
  • Sexual dimorphism as a proxy for male reproductive investment: greater male–female carapace differences in fossil cytheroid ostracods were associated with higher estimated extinction rates. The measured fossil trait is dimorphism; interpreting it as investment requires the biological correspondence observed in living relatives.[ref]
These examples demonstrate event- and group-dependent selectivity. They do not define one universal vulnerability ranking: the bivalve study, for instance, did not find body-size selectivity at the same end-Cretaceous genus-level event.
