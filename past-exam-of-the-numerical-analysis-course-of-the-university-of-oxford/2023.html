For a complex n×n matrix B=(bij​), put
Ri​=∑j=i​∣bij​∣,Di​={z∈C:∣z−bii​∣≤Ri​}.
(1)
The Gershgorin circle theorem states that
spec(B)⊆i=1⋃n​Di​.​
(2)
To prove it, let Bv=λv with v=0, and choose i for which ∣vi​∣=maxj​∣vj​∣>0. The ith coordinate of the eigenvalue equation gives
(λ−bii​)vi​=∑j=i​bij​vj​.
(3)
The triangle inequality, followed by division by ∣vi​∣, gives
∣λ−bii​∣≤∑j=i​∣bij​∣∣vi​∣∣vj​∣​≤∑j=i​∣bij​∣=Ri​.
(4)
Thus λ∈Di​, proving the assertion for every eigenvalue.
The second theorem, Gershgorin eigenvalue counting, says that if a union of k indexed Gershgorin discs is disjoint from the remaining n−k Gershgorin discs, that union contains exactly k eigenvalues, counted with algebraic multiplicity. In particular,
An isolated Gershgorin disc contains exactly one simple eigenvalue.​
(5)
Discs that touch are not disjoint; overlapping Gershgorin discs cannot individually be assigned one eigenvalue each.

The power iteration starts from v0​=0, normally scaled to unit Euclidean norm, and repeats
wk+1​=Avk​,vk+1​=∥wk+1​∥2​wk+1​​.​
(6)
Normalization prevents the rapidly growing or decaying magnitudes of Akv0​ from obscuring its direction. The iteration requires wk+1​=0.
Because A is a diagonalizable matrix, choose an eigenbasis u1​,…,un​, with corresponding eigenvalues λ1​=λ,λ2​,…,λn​, and expand v0​=∑j​cj​uj​. First suppose the dominant eigenvalue is simple and c1​=0. Then
Akv0​=c1​λk(u1​+∑j=2n​c1​cj​​(λλj​​)kuj​).
(7)
For
q=maxj≥2​​λλj​​​<1,
(8)
the sum in parentheses after u1​ is O(qk) in Big O notation. Consequently the power iteration direction error is O(qk): the iterates approach the line spanned by u1​. For a real matrix, a uniquely dominant eigenvalue is real, since otherwise its complex conjugate would have the same modulus. If λ>0, vk​ approaches one unit eigenvector; if λ<0, successive vk​ alternate signs. In either case,
σ∈{−1,1}min​​vk​−σ∥u1​∥2​u1​​​2​=O(qk).​
(9)
This is the sign and phase in power iteration qualification. Also, the necessary assumption is a nonzero coefficient c1​ in the eigenbasis, rather than merely a nonzero ordinary inner product with u1​: those tests can differ for a nonsymmetric matrix. If c1​=0, the method cannot generate the missing component.
If the dominant value λ has a higher-dimensional eigenspace, the same argument replaces c1​u1​ by the nonzero sum of all starting components in that eigenspace. The limiting direction is then that of this sum, which is still an eigenvector for λ. The usual algorithm assumes λ=0; a strict dominance gap guarantees this whenever another eigenvalue is present.
An estimate retaining the sign of λ is the Rayleigh quotient
λk​=vkT​vk​vkT​Avk​​⟶λ.​
(10)
Continuity of this quotient at a nonzero eigenvector proves the limit even when A is a nonsymmetric matrix. Alternatively, (Avk​)i​/(vk​)i​→λ for a coordinate whose limiting eigenvector component is nonzero. The scaling factor ∥Avk​∥2​ alone tends only to ∣λ∣.

Choose a shift μ which is not an eigenvalue. Inverse iteration applies power iteration to B=(A−μI)−1, implemented by a linear system solve rather than by forming an explicit matrix inverse:
(A−μI)wk+1​=vk​,vk+1​=∥wk+1​∥2​wk+1​​.​
(11)
A single LU decomposition of A−μI can be reused for all the solves at this fixed shift. An eigenpair Auj​=λj​uj​ transforms into
Buj​=λj​−μ1​uj​.
(12)
Thus an eigenvalue λ∗​ uniquely closest to μ becomes the dominant eigenvalue of B. For a diagonalizable matrix, the argument in the preceding subpart gives convergence of diagonalizable fixed-shift inverse iteration, provided the starting coefficient in the target eigenspace is nonzero, with power iteration direction error O(qμk​), where
qμ​=minj:λj​=λ∗​​∣λj​−μ∣∣λ∗​−μ∣​<1.​
(13)
For any prescribed simple eigenvalue, taking a sufficiently close but unequal shift makes the numerator small while the denominator stays close to its positive separation from the remaining eigenvalues. This makes qμ​ small and gives very fast convergence per iteration. The Rayleigh quotient of vk​ estimates λ∗​ directly. If βk​ estimates the associated eigenvalue of B, the corresponding estimate is μ+βk−1​.
The selection principle also applies when the other eigenvalues have nontrivial blocks in the Jordan normal form: their contributions acquire powers of k, multiplying strictly smaller geometric factors. A simple uniquely closest eigenvalue still attracts the direction when the Riesz projection of v0​ onto its generalized eigenspace is nonzero. For nonreal target eigenvalues, allow complex shifts and arithmetic; a real shift cannot distinguish a conjugate pair of equal distances. Setting μ=λ∗​ makes the solve singular, and an exactly zero starting target component prevents convergence to that target in exact arithmetic. When λ∗​<μ is real, account for the sign behavior of fixed-shift inverse iteration.

The four row Gershgorin discs have centres 9,3,−4,−5 and radii 1,2,2,1, respectively. Their real-axis intersections are
[8,10],[1,5],[−6,−2],[−6,−4].
(14)
None of the Gershgorin discs contains zero, so the Gershgorin circle theorem gives
0∈/spec(A),A is nonsingular.​
(15)
The discs centred at 9 and 3 are each isolated. By Gershgorin eigenvalue counting, they contain exactly one eigenvalue apiece, and both eigenvalues are simple. Each must be real: the characteristic polynomial is real, so a nonreal root would bring a distinct conjugate root into the same disc. Denote these eigenvalues by β∈[8,10] and α∈[1,5]. The remaining two eigenvalues lie in the union of the negative-centred discs.
For the shift 4, the distances satisfy
∣α−4∣≤3,∣β−4∣≥4,∣γ−4∣≥6
(16)
for either of the other eigenvalues γ, since its real part is at most −2. Thus α is uniquely closest to 4. Once 4 is checked not to be an eigenvalue, the natural full target-disc interval enclosure and a conservative asymptotic direction factor are
[a,b]=[1,5],q4​≤43​.​
(17)
As always, inverse iteration requires a nonzero initial target component. The following calculation verifies the shift and gives sharper information.
For the tridiagonal matrix, let pj​(t) be the determinant of the leading j×j block of tI−A. Expansion along the last row gives
p0​(t)p2​(t)p3​(t)p4​(t)​=1,=(t−3)(t−9)+1=t2−12t+28,=(t+4)p2​(t)−p1​(t)=t3−8t2−21t+121,=(t+5)p3​(t)−p2​(t)=t4−3t3−62t2+28t+577.​p1​(t)=t−9,
(18)
In particular, detA=577, p4​(4)=−239 and p4​(5)=−583. Both shifted linear systems are nonsingular. Further exact evaluations are
tp4​(t)​−6121​−5−113​−4−79​−397​3103​4−239​8−607​9181.​​
(19)
The intermediate value theorem gives a real root in each of (−6,−5), (−4,−3), (3,4) and (8,9). As p4​ has polynomial degree four, these are all its roots and each is simple. In particular, A is a diagonalizable matrix, α∈(3,4) and β∈(8,9). The closest competitor to α at the shift 4 is β, so this refinement yields
α∈[3,4],q4​=β−44−α​<41​.​
(20)
For the shift 5, the original Gershgorin discs only give ∣α−5∣≤4 and ∣β−5∣≥3; those bounds do not decide which is closer. The refined interval enclosures do:
1<5−α<2,3<β−5<4,∣γ−5∣>8
(21)
for the negative eigenvalues. Therefore the answer for this particular matrix is yes:
μ=5:λ∗​=α∈[3,4],q5​=β−55−α​<32​.​
(22)
Both shifts select the same eigenvector line and alternate signs because α<μ. The factor bounds concern asymptotic directional convergence, rather than a universal error reduction from the first iterate for a nonsymmetric matrix. The wider interval [1,5] remains a valid full-disc enclosure for both shifts; [3,4] records the stronger information obtained from the characteristic polynomial.

For the distinct interpolation nodes, define the Lagrange cardinal polynomials
Li​(x)=∏0≤j≤nj=i​​xi​−xj​x−xj​​,i=0,…,n.
(23)
Every denominator is nonzero, each Li​ has polynomial degree at most n, and Li​(xj​)=δij​. Hence the Lagrange interpolation polynomial
pn​(x)=i=0∑n​fi​Li​(x)​
(24)
belongs to Πn​ and has pn​(xj​)=fj​. This proves existence.
If pn​ and p​n​ both fit the data, their difference has polynomial degree at most n but vanishes at the n+1 distinct interpolation nodes. Repeated application of the factor theorem implies that ∏j=0n​(x−xj​) divides that difference. A nonzero polynomial of degree at most n cannot have such a divisor of degree n+1. Thus pn​−p​n​=0, proving uniqueness. When n=0, the empty product defining L0​ is 1, so the argument includes the constant case.

The monomial xk lies in Πn​ and already fits the data. Uniqueness of polynomial interpolation therefore gives
pn​(x)=xk.​
(25)
Apply this result with a nonnegative integer m≤n. Its Lagrange interpolation representation is the polynomial identity
xm=∑i=0n​xim​∏j=i​(xi​−xj​)∏j=i​(x−xj​)​.
(26)
The numerator in each summand is a monic polynomial of degree n, so the coefficient of xn on the right is exactly ∑i​xim​/∏j=i​(xi​−xj​). The coefficient on the left is zero for m<n and one for m=n. Comparing coefficients proves the Lagrange leading-coefficient identity
i=0∑n​∏j=i​(xi​−xj​)xim​​={0,1,​0≤m<n,m=n.​​
(27)
The intended exponent range is m≥0: arbitrary negative exponents would not give this polynomial identity. For m=0, xi0​ denotes the value of the constant polynomial 1, including at a zero interpolation node.

Clearing the denominators in the rational interpolation conditions gives
j=0∑m​aj​xij​+fi​j=0∑ℓ−1​bj​xij​=fi​xiℓ​,i=0,…,n.​
(28)
These are n+1 linear equations for (m+1)+ℓ=n+1 unknowns. The ith row of the coefficient matrix, in the order a0​,…,am​,b0​,…,bℓ−1​, is
(1,xi​,…,xim​, fi​,fi​xi​,…,fi​xiℓ−1​).
(29)
This rational interpolation linearization is equivalent to the original fraction conditions only if r(xi​)=0 at every interpolation node. If r(xi​)=0, the cleared equation merely forces q(xi​)=0, leaving the fraction undefined.
For fi​=xim+1​, the coefficient row becomes
(1,xi​,…,xim​,xim+1​,…,xim+ℓ​)=(1,xi​,…,xin​).
(30)
This is the Vandermonde matrix, whose determinant is ∏0≤i<j≤n​(xj​−xi​)=0. Equivalently, a vector in its null space would define a degree-at-most-n polynomial vanishing at all n+1 distinct interpolation nodes, which is zero by the preceding uniqueness proof. Thus the cleared linear system has exactly one solution.
There is also a constructive description of that solution. Define the monic polynomial
W(x)=∏i=0n​(x−xi​).
(31)
The polynomial xm+1r(x)−q(x) is monic of degree n+1 and vanishes at every interpolation node. It must therefore equal W(x). By polynomial division, write uniquely
W(x)=xm+1R(x)+S(x),degS≤m.
(32)
The quotient R is monic of degree ℓ, and
r(x)=R(x),q(x)=−S(x)​
(33)
is the unique coefficient solution. This does not assert that R is nonzero at every interpolation node.
For a counterexample with m=0, ℓ=1, choose x0​=0,x1​=1 and the specified data fi​=xi​, so f0​=0,f1​=1. The cleared equations reduce to
a0​=0,a0​+b0​=1,
(34)
giving the unique solution q(x)=0, r(x)=x−1. But at x1​=1 the quotient is 0/0. Indeed, any genuine rational interpolant of this prescribed form would need r(0)=0; then q(0)/r(0)=0 forces the constant numerator to be zero. At a nonzero denominator its value at 1 would also be zero, never one. Cancelling the common factor in the cleared candidate would merely give the identically zero rational function, which still fails the data. Hence
(x0​,f0​)=(0,0), (x1​,f1​)=(1,1):unique cleared solution, but no rational interpolant.​
(35)

Integrate the ordinary differential equation across one step, using the fundamental theorem of calculus:
y(xn+1​)−y(xn​)=∫xn​xn​+h​f(x,y(x))dx.
(36)
The midpoint quadrature rule approximates the integral by hf(xn​+h/2,y(xn​+h/2)). Approximate the unknown midpoint state by the midpoint value of linear interpolation between the endpoint states:
y(xn​+h/2)≈2yn​+yn+1​​.
(37)
Combining these approximations gives the implicit midpoint rule
yn+1​=yn​+hf(xn​+2h​,2yn​+yn+1​​),y0​=α.​
(38)
It is an implicit time-stepping method because its new state occurs inside the evaluation of f. An equivalent derivation uses a straight-line approximation on the step and imposes the ordinary differential equation at its midpoint, a one-stage collocation method.

Work on a fixed finite interval [a,a+T]. Write Yn​=y(xn​), t=xn​+h/2, and use the paper's normalized local truncation error
τn​=hYn+1​−Yn​​−f(t,2Yn+1​+Yn​​).
(39)
Assume y has bounded continuous derivatives through order three on this interval, with ∥y′′∥≤M2​ and ∥y′′′∥≤M3​. Assume also that the Jacobian matrix fy​ has operator norm at most L on the segments joining y(t) to the endpoint averages. For a scalar equation this last hypothesis is simply ∣∂f/∂y∣≤L there.
Two symmetric estimates separate the errors. By Taylor's theorem applied to y′ about t, the linear term integrates to zero on [−h/2,h/2], giving
​hYn+1​−Yn​​−y′(t)​​=​h1​∫−h/2h/2​(y′(t+s)−y′(t))ds​≤2hM3​​∫−h/2h/2​s2ds=24M3​h2​.​
(40)
Likewise the two linear terms in the endpoint expansions cancel, and their second-order remainders give
​2Yn+1​+Yn​​−y(t)​≤8M2​h2​.
(41)
The derivative bound on f gives a Lipschitz condition in its second variable. Since y′(t)=f(t,y(t)), the triangle inequality now yields
∥τn​∥≤(24M3​​+8LM2​​)h2=Kh2.​
(42)
The constant is independent of n and h as long as the steps remain in the fixed interval. This implicit midpoint consistency bound proves numerical consistency of order two. The corresponding unnormalized residual is hτn​=O(h3); the two powers conventionally called local truncation error differ because the paper divides by the step size. If local error instead means the error of an exactly solved numerical step started at Yn​, the small-step Lipschitz condition gives the bound h∥τn​∥/(1−hL/2)=O(h3) as well.

Let f be continuous in its first variable and uniformly Lipschitz continuous in its second variable, with constant L≥0. Retain the finite-interval smoothness assumptions and the bound ∥τn​∥≤Kh2 from the preceding subpart. We first establish solvability of each implicit step. For a proposed starting state u, the map
Gu​(v)=u+hf(xn​+2h​,2u+v​)
(43)
has Lipschitz constant hL/2. If hL/2<1, the contraction mapping theorem on the complete state space gives a unique next state v=Ψn,h​(u).
To compute a useful Lipschitz constant for the method, introduce its increment function by
Ψn,h​(u)=u+hΦn,h​(u),Φn,h​(u)=f(xn​+2h​,u+2h​Φn,h​(u)).
(44)
For two starting states u,w,
∥Φn,h​(u)−Φn,h​(w)∥≤L∥u−w∥+2hL​∥Φn,h​(u)−Φn,h​(w)∥.
(45)
Rearranging gives the implicit midpoint Lipschitz estimate
Lip(Φn,h​)≤1−hL/2L​,Lip(Ψn,h​)≤Rh​:=1−hL/21+hL/2​.​
(46)
Thus, for example, hL≤1 gives an increment Lipschitz constant at most 2L, uniformly as h→0.
For an explicit global error estimate, set en​=Yn​−yn​. Subtract the numerical formula from the exact-value identity containing hτn​:
en+1​=en​+h[f(xn​+2h​,2Yn​+Yn+1​​)−f(xn​+2h​,2yn​+yn+1​​)]+hτn​.
(47)
The Lipschitz condition and the triangle inequality imply
(1−hL/2)∥en+1​∥≤(1+hL/2)∥en​∥+h∥τn​∥,
(48)
and therefore
∥en+1​∥≤Rh​∥en​∥+1−hL/2Kh3​.
(49)
Iterating this inequality is the elementary discrete Gronwall inequality argument. For L>0, summing the geometric series and using Rh​−1=hL/(1−hL/2) gives
∥en​∥≤Rhn​∥e0​∥+LKh2​(Rhn​−1).
(50)
For hL≤1, Rh​=1+hL/(1−hL/2)≤1+2hL≤e2hL. If nh≤T, this proves
nh≤Tmax​∥en​∥≤e2LT∥e0​∥+LK​(e2LT−1)h2.​
(51)
With the exact initial value, e0​=0, the global error is O(h2) and tends uniformly to zero on every such finite interval. If L=0, the recurrence instead gives ∥en​∥≤∥e0​∥+KTh2, with the same conclusion. Starting values merely converging to the exact initial value also suffice for numerical convergence; second-order accuracy requires their errors to be O(h2). The result concerns exact implicit solves and convergence on fixed finite intervals, not uniform accuracy over an unbounded time interval.

Applying the implicit midpoint rule to the simple harmonic oscillator system gives
yn+1​−yn​=2h​(zn+1​+zn​),zn+1​−zn​=−2h​(yn+1​+yn​).
(52)
Factor the change in the quadratic form y2+z2 and substitute these equations:
​(yn+12​+zn+12​)−(yn2​+zn2​)=(yn+1​−yn​)(yn+1​+yn​)+(zn+1​−zn​)(zn+1​+zn​)=2h​(zn+1​+zn​)(yn+1​+yn​)−2h​(yn+1​+yn​)(zn+1​+zn​)=0.​
(53)
Thus every step preserves the first integral
yn+12​+zn+12​=yn2​+zn2​=y02​+z02​.​
(54)
For completeness, the implicit equations are solvable for every real h: putting s=h/2, their coefficient matrix has determinant 1+s2>0 and the update is
(yn+1​zn+1​​)=1+s21​(1−s2−2s​2s1−s2​)(yn​zn​​).
(55)
This is an orthogonal matrix, as follows from (1−s2)2+4s2=(1+s2)2. It therefore preserves the Euclidean norm, explaining geometrically the exact energy conservation. This is a concrete instance of Runge-Kutta conservation of quadratic invariants. Preservation of the circle does not assert that the numerical and exact solutions have the same phase at a fixed nonzero step size.
