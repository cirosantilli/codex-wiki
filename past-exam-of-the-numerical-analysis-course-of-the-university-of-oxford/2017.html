For each interpolation node define the Lagrange cardinal polynomial
ℓj​(x)=∏0≤k≤nk=j​​xj​−xk​x−xk​​,0≤j≤n.
(1)
All denominators are nonzero because the interpolation nodes are distinct. Each polynomial has polynomial degree at most n, and ℓj​(xi​)=δij​, where δij​ is the Kronecker delta. Consequently the linear combination
pn​(x)=∑j=0n​fj​ℓj​(x)
(2)
belongs to the vector space of univariate polynomials Πn​ and has the required values. This proves existence of the Lagrange interpolation polynomial.
For uniqueness, suppose pn​ and rn​ both work. Their difference is a polynomial of polynomial degree at most n with n+1 distinct roots of a polynomial. The root bound for a polynomial forces pn​−rn​ to be the zero polynomial.
The seven supplied values follow fi​=i2+1. The quadratic polynomial x2+1 belongs to Π6​ and takes all seven values, so uniqueness gives
p6​(x)=x2+1.​
(3)
The subscript bounds the polynomial degree; it does not require polynomial degree exactly six.

w′(xk​)=∏j=k​(xk​−xj​)=0,ℓk​(x)=(x−xk​)w′(xk​)w(x)​=w(x)x−xk​wk​​.

(5)
Apply the same Lagrange interpolation formula to the constant polynomial one. Since its interpolant is itself,
1=∑k=0n​ℓk​(x)=w(x)∑k=0n​x−xk​wk​​.
(6)

pn​(x)=k=0∑n​wk​/(x−xk​)k=0∑n​wk​fk​/(x−xk​)​.​
(7)
The denominator is exactly 1/w(x), so is nonzero away from the interpolation nodes. At x=xj​, multiply numerator and denominator by x−xj​ and take the limit: their values tend to wj​fj​ and wj​, respectively. The removable singularity therefore has value fj​; in numerical evaluation return the stored value directly at an interpolation node.

A Newton-Cotes quadrature integrates a Lagrange interpolation polynomial at equally spaced quadrature nodes. For the Newton-Cotes closed quadrature, the endpoints are included: write xj​=a+jh, h=(b−a)/n, and use
∫ab​f(x)dx ≈ ∫ab​pn​(x)dx=∑j=0n​Wj​f(xj​),Wj​=∫ab​ℓj​(x)dx.
(8)
These quadrature weights depend on the quadrature nodes, not on the sampled function. Open Newton-Cotes quadrature uses equally spaced interior quadrature nodes instead.
For Simpson's rule, use n=2 and set x=a+ht, so 0≤t≤2. The Lagrange cardinal polynomials become
L0​(t)=2(t−1)(t−2)​,L1​(t)=t(2−t),L2​(t)=2t(t−1)​.
(9)
Integrating these quadratic polynomials gives 1/3, 4/3, 1/3, respectively. The change of variable contributes h, so
∫ab​f(x)dx ≈ 3h​[f(a)+4f(a+h)+f(b)]=6b−a​[f(a)+4f(2a+b​)+f(b)].​
(10)
Its degree of precision of a quadrature rule is three: it integrates every quadratic polynomial by construction, and also the cubic nodal polynomial (x−a)(x−a−h)(x−b), whose integral vanishes by odd symmetry about a+h. Any cubic polynomial differs from its quadratic polynomial interpolant by a constant multiple of this nodal polynomial.

Gaussian elimination uses elementary row operations to remove entries below successive pivot elements, reducing a linear system to one with an upper triangular matrix. Back substitution then solves that system. It also computes a reusable matrix factorization, so additional right-hand sides can be handled by forward substitution and back substitution without repeating the elimination.
For elimination without row exchanges, let A(1)=A. At step k, provided the pivot element akk(k)​ is nonzero, form the elimination multipliers
mik​=akk(k)​aik(k)​​,i=k+1,…,n,
(20)
and perform rowi​←rowi​−mik​rowk​, applying the same operation to the right-hand side. More explicitly the resulting entries satisfy aij(k+1)​=aij(k)​−mik​akj(k)​ for i>k, j≥k.
Let Ek​=I−∑i>k​mik​ei​ekT​, where ei​ are the standard basis vectors. Then
U=En−1​⋯E1​A,A=LU,L=E1−1​⋯En−1−1​.
(21)
The matrix L is a unit lower triangular matrix storing the elimination multipliers, while U is an upper triangular matrix:
L=​1m21​⋮mn1​​01⋮mn2​​⋯⋯⋱⋯​00⋮1​​,U=​u11​0⋮0​u12​u22​⋮0​⋯⋯⋱⋯​u1n​u2n​⋮unn​​​.
(22)
Solving Ax=b becomes Ly=b followed by Ux=y. If a required pivot element vanishes, an unpermuted LU decomposition can fail even for an invertible matrix. Row exchanges, typically selected by partial pivoting, instead produce PA=LU with a permutation matrix P; then solve Ly=Pb, Ux=y.

Since every ujj​>0, the diagonal matrix D is invertible. Set
U=D−1U,uij​=uii​uij​​.
(23)
The division is by the diagonal entry of row i. Thus U is an upper triangular matrix with diagonal entries one and A=LDU.
To use symmetry without assuming the conclusion, multiply by the matrix inverse of L and its transpose:
M=L−1AL−T=DUL−T.
(24)
The matrix M is symmetric because A is a symmetric matrix. It is also an upper triangular matrix, since every factor on the right is an upper triangular matrix. A symmetric upper triangular matrix is diagonal. Its diagonal is exactly D, because U and L−T have diagonal entries one. Hence M=D, giving
A=LDLT,U=LT.​
(25)
This is the LDL decomposition.
Take the positive square roots of the diagonal entries:
D1/2=diag(u11​​,…,unn​​),L=LD1/2.

A linear spline is a continuous function whose restriction to every interval [xi−1​,xi​] is a polynomial of polynomial degree at most one. With hi​=xi​−xi−1​ and ℓi​=ℓ(xi​), linear interpolation gives
ℓ(x)=hi​xi​−x​ℓi−1​+hi​x−xi−1​​ℓi​,xi−1​≤x≤xi​.
(36)
Integrating the two linear polynomials and adding the contributions gives
∫x0​xn​​ℓ(x)dx=i=1∑n​2hi​​(ℓi−1​+ℓi​).​
(37)
This is the composite trapezoidal rule applied to the nodal values, and it is exact for the linear spline itself, including nonuniform spline knots.
A cubic spline on this partition of an interval is a function S∈C2[x0​,xn​] that restricts to a polynomial of polynomial degree at most three on every interval [xi−1​,xi​]. Thus its second derivative is continuous and piecewise of polynomial degree at most one: S′′ is a linear spline on the same spline knots. Its third derivative is constant on each open interval and can jump across the spline knots; its fourth derivative vanishes inside each open interval.

Put E=T−S. Matching interpolation data and endpoint derivatives gives
E(xi​)=0(0≤i≤n),E′(x0​)=E′(xn​)=0.
(38)
The endpoint slope constraints make S a clamped cubic spline. The decisive step in its minimum roughness property of a clamped cubic spline is the orthogonality identity
∫x0​xn​​S′′(x)E′′(x)dx=0.
(39)
Prove it separately on each interval. Two applications of integration by parts, using the vanishing fourth derivative of the cubic piece, give
∫xi−1​xi​​S′′E′′dx=[S′′E′]xi−1​xi​​−[S′′′E]xi−1​xi​​=[S′′E′]xi−1​xi​​.
(40)
The second boundary term is zero because E vanishes at both endpoints of this interval. Sum over i. At each interior spline knot, the two values of S′′E′ agree because both factors are continuous, so the contributions cancel. The remaining outer boundary terms vanish because E′ is zero at both outer endpoints. This proves the identity.
Expanding the square now gives the stronger exact equality
∫x0​xn​​(T′′)2dx=∫x0​xn​​(S′′)2dx+∫x0​xn​​(T′′−S′′)2dx.​
(41)
The last integral is nonnegative, proving the required inequality. It measures the excess second derivative roughness penalty of T over S.
The argument applies directly to twice continuously differentiable competitors. More generally it holds when T′ is an absolutely continuous function and T′′ is square-integrable, the usual finite-energy class for this second derivative roughness penalty. Mere existence of pointwise second derivatives does not by itself guarantee that all displayed integrals exist; the variational statement is understood for admissible competitors with this regularity.

Let R be a second cubic spline matching the same values and endpoint derivatives. The minimum roughness property of a clamped cubic spline applied with S as the minimizing cubic spline gives J(R)≥J(S), where J(u)=∫x0​xn​​(u′′)2dx. Applying the same argument with their roles exchanged gives J(S)≥J(R). Hence equality holds, and the exact identity from part (i) yields
∫x0​xn​​(R′′−S′′)2dx=0.
(42)
The integrand is continuous and nonnegative. If it were positive somewhere, it would have a positive integral over a small interval. It follows that R′′=S′′ everywhere. Consequently R−S is an affine function. Its value and derivative vanish at x0​, so it is identically zero. Thus R=S, proving uniqueness of the clamped cubic spline with the prescribed data. Existence was assumed in the question; this argument establishes the remaining uniqueness claim.

Fix i, write α=xi−1​, β=xi​, and let E=T−S on this interval. The Hermite interpolation conditions give both
E(α)=E(β)=0,E′(α)=E′(β)=0.
(43)
Two applications of integration by parts therefore give
∫αβ​ET(4)dx=[ET′′′−E′T′′]αβ​+∫αβ​E′′T′′dx=∫αβ​E′′T′′dx.
(44)
On this single cubic piece, the same calculation also gives
∫αβ​E′′S′′dx=[E′S′′−ES′′′]αβ​+∫αβ​ES(4)dx=0.
(45)
Here S(4)=0 inside the interval, and all four endpoint values of E,E′ are zero. Since T′′=E′′+S′′, we obtain the exact identity
∫xi−1​xi​​(T−S)T(4)dx=∫xi−1​xi​​(T′′−S′′)2dx≥0.​
(46)
This is an integral inequality, with no assertion that the integrand on the left has one sign pointwise. The stronger nodal derivative conditions make the boundary terms vanish on each interval separately; part (i) only needed their cancellation after summing.
The stated Hermite interpolating polynomial exists uniquely in Π2n+1​. Indeed, a polynomial in the kernel of a linear map of the value-and-derivative data map has a double root of a polynomial at each of n+1 distinct interpolation nodes. A nonzero such polynomial would have polynomial degree at least 2n+2, which is impossible. The map is therefore an injective function and a linear map between vector spaces of the same finite dimension of a vector space, 2n+2, and hence is a linear isomorphism. No higher smoothness of the original function f is required to construct it.
An explicit illustration uses interpolation nodes 0,1,2, values 0,1,0, and zero derivatives at every interpolation node. The clamped cubic spline is S(x)=3x2−2x3 on [0,1] and its reflection on [1,2]. Its Hermite interpolating polynomial is the quartic polynomial T(x)=x2(2−x)2. On [0,1], T−S=x2(1−x)2, and the corresponding reflected formula holds on [1,2]. Direct integration gives J(S)=24, J(T)=128/5, and J(T−S)=8/5, exactly as the energy identity predicts.
