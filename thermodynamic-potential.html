Natural variables of a thermodynamic potential

Words: 51
Thermodynamic potential OurBigBook.com
Natural variables are the independent variables appearing in the differential that directly identifies a potential's conjugate quantities. For U(S,V,N), dU=TdS−pdV+μdN. Legendre transforms exchange a variable for its conjugate: F=U−TS has natural variables T,V,N, and G=F+pV has T,p,N. Equilibrium minimum principles require the corresponding environmental constraints and fixed conserved amounts.

Exergy (A−Aeq​)

Words: 202 Articles: 2
Thermodynamic potential OurBigBook.com
For a closed system with fixed composition in an environment at temperature TR​>0 and pressure pR​, define A=U+pR​V−TR​S, where U is internal energy, V is volume and S is entropy. The exergy is this availability relative to the equilibrium reference state, A−Aeq​. When the combined system and environment are isolated except for the specified exchanges, ΔStot​=−ΔA/TR​, so the Second law of thermodynamics makes A decrease toward its constrained minimum. Away from equilibrium, the environmental TR​,pR​ must not be replaced by the system's instantaneous values.

Availability curvature and equilibrium fluctuations (⟨(x−x0​)2⟩≃kB​TR​/A′′(x0​))

Words: 124 Articles: 1
Exergy OurBigBook.com
After integrating out other variables, let A(x) include the constrained entropy and the measure appropriate to the chosen coordinate x. Its equilibrium probability density function is proportional to e−A(x)/(kB​TR​). Near a stable interior minimum, A(x)=A(x0​)+A′′(x0​)(x−x0​)2/2+⋯ with A′′(x0​)>0. The resulting normal distribution has variance kB​TR​/A′′(x0​). This is the leading Gaussian fluctuation approximation; a coordinate-dependent measure contributes to the effective A, and higher-order terms matter near a vanishing curvature.

Volume fluctuations of a small fluid parcel (⟨δV2⟩=kB​TκT​Vˉ)

Words: 63
Availability curvature and equilibrium fluctuations OurBigBook.com
For a material parcel of fixed amount inside a much larger thermal and mechanical environment, availability is A(V)=F(V,T)+pbath​V. Since FV​=−p, its curvature is AVV​=−pV​=1/(VκT​). The Gaussian fluctuation approximation gives ⟨δV2⟩=kB​TκT​Vˉ. This describes parcel-volume fluctuations, not the geometric volume of a fixed observation box or a globally rigid vessel. A fixed spatial box instead has number fluctuations related to the same compressibility.

Thermodynamic free energy

Words: 5k Articles: 61
Thermodynamic potential OurBigBook.com
A free energy is a thermodynamic potential that compares possible states under specified environmental constraints. For a closed system of fixed composition, treated as a simple compressible system with only pressure-volume work, stable thermodynamic equilibrium minimizes Helmholtz free energy at fixed temperature and volume, or Gibbs free energy at fixed temperature and pressure. The choice follows the experiment: a rigid sealed vessel and a sealed cylinder with a freely moving loaded piston impose different constraints. Both may exchange heat with a thermal reservoir. See W. C. Carter, MIT thermodynamics notes, sections 2-1.14 and 2-1.19.
The useful insight is that internal energy alone cannot rank these alternatives: exchanging heat also changes the surroundings' entropy. Subtracting TS packages that accounting into a property of the chosen system. Adding pV additionally accounts for displacing the surroundings at fixed pressure. A free energy is a state function; supplied heat describes a transfer during a process. Under the stated constraints, supplied heat is Q=ΔU at fixed volume or Q=ΔH at fixed pressure, whereas neither ΔF nor ΔG generally equals Q.
A lower free energy identifies a thermodynamic preference, without specifying a timescale. A barrier can trap a system in a local minimum, producing metastability although a lower global minimum exists. The enzyme example illustrates the corresponding distinction for chemical reactions: changing the route can change the reaction rate while preserving the endpoint Gibbs free energy difference and chemical equilibrium.

Free-energy functional (A[m])

Words: 49
Thermodynamic free energy OurBigBook.com
A free-energy functional assigns a free energy to an entire spatial order parameter configuration rather than only to one uniform value. In Landau-Ginzburg theory, a local derivative expansion gives A[m]=∫dDx{κ(∇m)2/2+V(m)}, with appropriate boundary terms and regular backgrounds. The Landau approximation minimizes this functional; a fluctuating statistical field theory instead integrates its Boltzmann weight over configurations. These procedures need not give identical critical exponents.

Helmholtz free energy (F)

Words: 273
Thermodynamic free energy OurBigBook.com
The Helmholtz free energy is
F=U−TS,
(1)
where U is internal energy, also denoted E elsewhere here, T is absolute temperature, and S is entropy. For the closed system of fixed composition and only pressure-volume work described above, spontaneous relaxation at fixed temperature and volume cannot increase F. Stable thermodynamic equilibrium is a global minimum over the permitted alternatives, including different thermodynamic phases. A rigid vessel fixes the total volume; it does not require each coexisting thermodynamic phase to retain its own volume.
For a constructed comparison at 300K, suppose one candidate has 1.5kJ more internal energy but 6JK−1 more entropy than another at the same temperature and volume. Its difference is
ΔF=1500−300(6)=−300J.
(2)
Thus the candidate with higher internal energy has lower Helmholtz free energy. This is the practical reason for retaining the entropy term when selecting a state.
For equilibrium states within a smooth branch of fixed composition, the first law of thermodynamics gives dU=TdS−pdV, hence
dF=−SdT−pdV,S=−(∂T∂F​)V​,p=−(∂V∂F​)T​.
(3)
These derivatives extract entropy and pressure; the differential is not an evolution equation for relaxation. If chemical species amounts ni​ vary, add ∑i​μi​dni​, with molar chemical potentials μi​. A closed system can still change chemical species amounts through chemical reactions; the fixed-composition simplification then needs the conserved-inventory constraints of chemical equilibrium. See MIT 5.60, Lecture 14, fundamental equations and chemical potentials.
The minimum principle has a short explanation. At fixed volume, no boundary work occurs, so Q=ΔU. A thermal reservoir at positive temperature T loses entropy Q/T. Consequently
ΔStotal​=ΔS−TΔU​=−TΔF​≥0,
(4)
by the Second law of thermodynamics. Here both endpoint states share the imposed temperature. See MIT 5.60, Lecture 12, criteria for spontaneous change.
The microscopic counterpart is F=−kB​TlnZ in the canonical ensemble, with Boltzmann constant kB​ and partition function Z. The partition function examples compute the balance between internal energy and entropy; the chemical equilibrium example already displays an ideal-mixture Helmholtz free energy minimum.

Gibbs free energy (G)

Words: 5k Articles: 58
Thermodynamic free energy OurBigBook.com
The Gibbs free energy is
G=U−TS+pV=H−TS,H=U+pV,
(5)
where H is enthalpy. For a closed system of fixed composition, with only pressure-volume work, spontaneous relaxation at fixed temperature and pressure cannot increase G; stable thermodynamic equilibrium minimizes it over the allowed states. A loaded piston can move while maintaining the imposed pressure, so the enthalpy term includes the displacement cost. Along a smooth equilibrium branch of fixed composition,
dG=−SdT+Vdp.
(6)
Thus its temperature slope at fixed pressure is −S, while its pressure slope at fixed temperature is V. See MIT 5.60, Lecture 13, Gibbs free energy and its derivatives.
Consider two illustrative thermodynamic phases, A and B, of the same substance at one fixed pressure. Write B minus A, using quantities per mole:
ΔH=12kJmol−1,ΔS=40Jmol−1K−1=0.040kJmol−1K−1.
(7)
Treat these differences as approximately constant over a limited temperature window. Then
kJmol−1ΔG(T)​=12−0.040KT​,T∗​=ΔSΔH​=300K.
(8)
At 280K, ΔG=+0.8kJmol−1, so A is preferred. At 320K, ΔG=−0.8kJmol−1, so B is preferred. At 300K, both have equal molar Gibbs free energy, permitting phase coexistence in this bulk model. B's greater entropy outweighs its greater enthalpy above the crossing.
Each plotted point compares alternatives at the same temperature; moving along the curve changes the imposed temperature. The model predicts preference, not instantaneous conversion: nucleation barriers can sustain metastability. Real enthalpy and entropy differences generally vary with temperature through heat capacities, bending this line outside the approximation.
At fixed pressure, the first law of thermodynamics gives Q=ΔH under the stated work restriction, so the same thermal reservoir accounting yields ΔStotal​=−ΔG/T≥0. Variable composition restores ∑i​μi​dni​ to dG; constrained Gibbs minimization for chemical equilibrium handles those permitted changes.
The historical foundation is Gibbs's [ref]. Its equations (87) and (91) introduce the combinations now called Helmholtz free energy and Gibbs free energy, connecting entropy criteria, chemical potentials and phase coexistence within one framework.

Unconstrained particle exchange requires the reservoir chemical potentials to be included; minimizing G over arbitrary particle number is a different problem.

The standard free-energy change for moving a species between specified phases or solvents. For a consistently standardized equilibrium distribution coefficient, ΔG∘=−RTlnK. Pure-solid lattice dissolution is a separate thermodynamic step.

For the equation of state V=NkB​T/p−NA(T), integrate (∂G/∂p)T,N​=V along an isotherm. This gives the displayed Gibbs free energy difference. Its negative temperature derivative at fixed endpoint pressures gives ΔS=−NkB​ln(p1​/p0​)+NA′(T)(p1​−p0​). The physically admissible branch must retain positive volume.

Gibbs free energy of an ideal-gas mixture

Words: 85
Gibbs free energy OurBigBook.com
For a single-phase ideal gas mixture with molar amounts Ni​, common temperature T, and pressure P, extensivity and ideal mixing give
G=∑i​Ni​μi​=N∑i​xi​[μi∘​(T)+RTlog(P∘xi​P​)].
(9)
Here N=∑i​Ni​, xi​ is the mole fraction, R is the molar gas constant, and P∘ is the standard pressure. Integrating ∂μi​/∂P=RT/P and including the ideal mixing entropy proves μi​=μi∘​+RTlog(xi​P/P∘). Reactions can change N, so fractions alone do not set the extensive Gibbs free energy. Its composition Hessian is RT(δij​/Ni​−1/N); it is positive semidefinite by the weighted Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Thermochemical equilibrium

Chemical-potential balance for a reaction OurBigBook.com
Thermochemical equilibrium requires every chemical reaction to have zero Gibbs free-energy change. For ideal reactants, the equilibrium constant is K=exp[−Δr​G∘/(RT)] after standard-state activity factors are included.
Constrained Gibbs minimization for chemical equilibrium
Words: 67
Thermochemical equilibrium OurBigBook.com
At fixed temperature and pressure, a closed reacting system reaches thermochemical equilibrium by minimizing Gibbs free energy over nonnegative species amounts subject to conserved elemental abundances. For an allowed stoichiometric vector ν, an interior minimum has ∑i​νi​μi​=0. More generally the chemical potential vector lies in the row span of the elemental conservation matrix at positive concentrations, with the appropriate one-sided inequalities for absent species. The first law of thermodynamics gives the thermodynamic differential, while the Second law of thermodynamics supplies the minimization direction.

Kelvin equation (ln(p/psat​)≃2Γvl​/(RkB​T))
Words: 132 Articles: 1
Phase coexistence curve OurBigBook.com
In the ideal-vapor, incompressible-liquid capillarity approximation, a spherical droplet of radius R has the displayed equilibrium vapor-pressure relation. Here vl​ is liquid volume per molecule, Γ is surface tension, and psat​ is the flat-interface saturation pressure at the same temperature. The Young–Laplace equation raises liquid pressure by 2Γ/R, increasing its chemical potential by approximately 2Γvl​/R. Matching this with the ideal-vapor chemical-potential increase kB​Tln(p/psat​) proves the formula. The liquid contribution from the vapor-pressure change itself is neglected relative to its curvature pressure, and bulk properties are taken independent of radius. A convex small drop therefore requires a higher ambient vapor pressure than a flat interface.

Triple point
Words: 557 Articles: 1
Phase coexistence curve OurBigBook.com
A triple point is a temperature–pressure pair at which three distinct bulk thermodynamic phases of a one-component substance coexist in thermodynamic equilibrium. In the familiar solid–liquid–gas case, their three phase coexistence curves meet. Other combinations, such as two crystal polymorphs and a liquid, also qualify: “triple” counts thermodynamic phases, rather than states-of-matter categories.
For the same substance, the chemical potential in each thermodynamic phase must agree:
μs​(Tt​,pt​)=μl​(Tt​,pt​)=μg​(Tt​,pt​).
(12)
There are two independent equality conditions for two controls, temperature and pressure. Generically they select an isolated pair (Tt​,pt​); the third pairwise equality follows from the first two. Equivalently, the phase rule gives F=1−3+2=0. This counts independent intensive controls, not phase fractions: changing the amounts of the three thermodynamic phases need not change the ideal bulk temperature and pressure, provided all three remain present. Different sets of crystal polymorphs can give different triple points for the same substance.[ref]
At a liquid-gas critical point, the two fluid thermodynamic phases instead merge, and their mass density difference and latent heat vanish. It is the endpoint of the liquid–gas phase coexistence curve, rather than a meeting of three distinct thermodynamic phases. The regular independent-equality count used for a triple point cannot explain this merger. A triple point can retain finite mass density and entropy differences between all three thermodynamic phases.[ref]
Triple-point cell
Words: 349
Triple point OurBigBook.com
A triple-point cell turns phase coexistence into a reproducible reference for thermometer calibration. The NIST water-cell demonstration uses a sealed glass vessel containing water of specified isotopic composition and an open central thermometer well. Cooling this well with solid carbon dioxide and alcohol freezes an ice mantle while leaving liquid water and its vapor. The coolant is outside the sealed sample; the sample's vapor space does not communicate with room air.[ref]
In the actual NIST cell-comparison experiment, newly frozen mantles were compared with an aged reference mantle in a controlled maintenance bath. A warm aluminium rod melted a thin inner layer around the well, allowing the mantle to rotate freely and establishing an ice–water interface close to the sensor. A standard platinum resistance thermometer and a resistance bridge measured the electrical resistance ratio to a calibrated, temperature-controlled reference resistor. Readings at two currents permitted extrapolation to zero electrical power, correcting self-heating; measured immersion depths supplied hydrostatic pressure corrections. Comparing preparations over time tested stabilization rather than assuming freshly frozen ice was ready.[ref]
The inference is a reproducible fixed-point response for the thermometer, not a direct reading of three chemical potentials. The reference cell anchors the comparison; a water point alone does not determine a thermometer's response at every other temperature. The ideal F=0 argument excludes impurity and isotope variation, finite interface effects and unintended heat transfer; real cells also require adequate immersion and stable electrical references. Repeatability is evidence that these effects are controlled, not a reason to omit their uncertainty.
Since 20 May 2019 the kelvin has been defined through a fixed numerical value of the Boltzmann constant. The thermodynamic temperature of the water triple point is consequently a measured quantity with uncertainty, rather than an exact value imposed by the kelvin definition. Its continued use as an assigned reference on the International Temperature Scale of 1990 is a separate convention.[ref][ref]

Phase diagram
Words: 1k Articles: 16
Phase coexistence curve OurBigBook.com
A phase diagram partitions a space of thermodynamic variables into regions occupied by different equilibrium thermodynamic phases and records the phase coexistence curves between them. For a pure, one-component substance, a pressure–temperature phase diagram gives one point for each pair (T,p). In a single-thermodynamic phase region both controls can vary independently. On a two-thermodynamic phase phase coexistence curve, they obey one relation; at a triple point, both are fixed. These statements assume bulk thermodynamic equilibrium, fixed composition and no additional applied fields.[ref]
At fixed temperature and pressure, the stable bulk thermodynamic phase minimizes Gibbs free energy for a fixed amount of substance. For a pure substance this is a comparison of chemical potentials on a common normalization. Two candidate thermodynamic phases coexist where μα​(T,p)=μβ​(T,p) and neither is exceeded in stability by a third thermodynamic phase. Differentiating their equality gives the Clapeyron equation. Thus a phase coexistence curve is a locus of equal stability, rather than a trajectory that every heated sample must follow.[ref]
The following original cartoon uses dimensionless positive coordinates θ=T/T∗​ and π=p/p∗​, with arbitrary reference scales T∗​,p∗​. It represents no particular substance and contains no measured data. Stipulate a triple point at (1,1), a liquid-gas critical point at (3,5), and these drawing rules:
πsub​(θ)πvap​(θ)θmelt​(π)​=eθ−1,=2θ−1,=1+0.1(π−1),​​0<θ≤1,1≤θ≤3,π≥1.​
(13)
They specify sublimation, liquid–gas phase coexistence and melting boundaries for the picture. They are not an equation of state, do not predict latent heat or mass density, and do not supply a quantitative critical theory. In particular, the melting slope is merely an illustrative choice.
For example, at fixed π=3 the melting boundary is at θ=1.2 and the liquid–gas boundary is at θ=2. Heating from (θ,π)=(1.6,3) to (3.6,3) therefore starts in liquid, crosses phase coexistence at (2,3), and ends in gas. In an equilibrium conversion at that crossing, added heat can change the phase fraction at fixed temperature through latent heat. The pressure–temperature point alone does not specify how much of each thermodynamic phase is present: for that one needs an additional amount or volume constraint.
Now connect exactly the same endpoints by raising π from 3 to 6 at θ=1.6, moving to θ=3.6 at π=6, then lowering π to 3. The first leg stays on the liquid side because θmelt​ ranges only from 1.2 to 1.5 and πvap​(1.6)=2.2<3. The second leg lies above the entire liquid–gas boundary, whose largest drawn pressure coordinate is 5; the third lies beyond its largest temperature coordinate, 3. No leg crosses a first-order phase transition boundary. The middle leg passes through a supercritical fluid region, with θ>3 and π>5, and the final leg returns to a gaslike state continuously.
Avoiding a first-order phase transition does not mean that no heat or work is exchanged. Along the detour, mass density, enthalpy and heat capacity may change substantially but continuously. The liquid-gas critical point itself has singular behavior, so the detour goes around it rather than through it. This connected fluid region is the striking lesson of a phase diagram: a familiar change from liquid to gas need not involve boiling across a sharp boundary.
An equilibrium phase diagram also does not predict how rapidly a conversion occurs. Metastability and a barrier described by classical nucleation theory can delay the observed phase transition. Nor should a binary-composition phase diagram be read as the same picture: its coexistence regions and lever rule encode composition and phase fractions, and can occupy an area even though pure-substance two-thermodynamic phase coexistence occupies a curve in the pressure–temperature plane.

Phase rule (F=C−P+2)
Words: 149
Phase diagram OurBigBook.com
The phase rule counts independent intensive controls compatible with coexistence of P distinct bulk thermodynamic phases made from C independent components:
F=C−P+2.
(14)
Here P counts thermodynamic phases, not pressure, and F does not count phase fractions. For the nonreacting one-component case, C=1 and F=3−P: a single thermodynamic phase occupies a region with F=2, two-thermodynamic phase coexistence a curve with F=1, and a triple point an isolated point with F=0. Fixing pressure externally removes one adjustable control wherever that constraint is independent.[ref]
For an elementary count without chemical reactions, take C chemical species. Each thermodynamic phase has C−1 independent composition fractions, since they sum to one. Shared temperature and pressure add two controls, giving 2+P(C−1). Equality of each species' chemical potential between one reference thermodynamic phase and the other P−1 thermodynamic phases supplies C(P−1) constraints. Subtraction gives 2+P(C−1)−C(P−1)=C−P+2. This argument assumes the constraints are independent and treats interfaces as negligible; chemical reactions, external fields or critical mergers require appropriate qualifications.

A two-solid microstructure formed by the coupled transformation of liquid at a eutectic point. Lamellae and rods are common morphologies. A hypoeutectic alloy can contain both primary crystals and a eutectic microstructure; those microconstituents are not the same accounting categories as its total phase fractions.

The fraction of an alloy that solidifies through the eutectic reaction, equal to the remaining liquid fraction immediately before that reaction in a simple equilibrium solidification path. It differs from the total fraction of either solid phase, and later precipitation can change the phase inventory.

At a first-order phase transition, the Gibbs free energy is continuous while at least one first derivative, such as entropy or volume, jumps. A nonzero entropy jump produces latent heat L=TΔS.
Two-phase scalar coexistence endpoint conditions (V′=V′′=V(3)=0,V(4)>0)
Words: 107
First-order phase transition OurBigBook.com
A smooth scalar Landau free energy can end a two-phase phase coexistence curve by merging two minima and their intervening maximum. At a generic stable endpoint, its first three derivatives vanish and its fourth derivative is positive. A field shift and two mixed control parameters reduce the local form to a quartic Landau free energy; the order-parameter jump then vanishes and the magnetic susceptibility diverges. This conclusion assumes there is no competing third phase and that the endpoint lies inside the control-parameter domain. It is not a general theorem about every first-order line: a sextic potential with fixed negative quartic coefficient has a symmetry-related ordered coexistence line that ends at a triple point with a finite jump.

Latent heat (L=TΔS=ΔH)
Words: 2k Articles: 12
Phase coexistence curve OurBigBook.com
Latent heat is the heat absorbed or released in a first-order phase transition through its entropy jump. For an equilibrium conversion from thermodynamic phase α to thermodynamic phase β at fixed pressure, evaluate both thermodynamic phases at the same temperature and pressure on their phase coexistence curve:
L=Ttr​(Sβ​−Sα​)=Hβ​−Hα​.
(16)
Use one normalization throughout: L, S and H may all be per mole, giving L in Jmol−1, or all per unit mass, giving L in Jkg−1. Here S is entropy and H is enthalpy on that basis. Equal Gibbs free energies of the coexisting thermodynamic phases give ΔH=Ttr​ΔS. Identifying this enthalpy difference with supplied heat assumes a closed system and an equilibrium conversion with only pressure-volume work. [ref]
For a pure substance with positive latent heat per unit mass L, let x be the phase fraction of the higher-enthalpy thermodynamic phase, measured by mass. While both thermodynamic phases coexist at the fixed pressure,
Q=mL(x2​−x1​),T=Ttr​.
(17)
Adding heat changes x rather than the temperature; this creates the horizontal portion of a heating curve. Once x=1, additional heat raises the temperature according to specific heat capacity at constant pressure. Reversing the conversion releases heat. The plateau represents changing enthalpy, rather than a finite single-thermodynamic phase heat capacity satisfying Q=Cp​ΔT. [ref]
For a constructed fixed-pressure model, take m=1kg, initial temperature 280K, transition temperature Ttr​=300K, solid specific heat capacity at constant pressure cs​=2kJkg−1K−1, latent heat L=100kJkg−1, and liquid specific heat capacity at constant pressure cl​=3kJkg−1K−1. These are stipulated model parameters, not empirical properties of a named material. Heating the solid to 300K takes 40kJ of sensible heat; converting it fully to liquid takes another 100kJ of heat; heating the liquid to 320K takes 60kJ. The total is 200kJ. After a total heat input of 90kJ, x=(90−40)/100=1/2 and the temperature is still 300K. The corresponding per-unit-mass entropy jump is L/Ttr​=31​kJkg−1K−1.
A continuous phase transition has no latent heat. Its heat capacity can have a jump or a singularity without a finite interval in which added heat converts coexisting thermodynamic phases at a constant temperature. A heating plateau therefore requires the stated phase coexistence conditions; it is not a feature of every phase transition. [ref]

Clausius-Clapeyron relation (dp/dT=L/(TΔv))
Words: 1k Articles: 9
Latent heat OurBigBook.com
The Clapeyron equation connects the slope of a phase coexistence curve to the latent heat and the change in volume during a first-order phase transition. A steep boundary can indicate a large latent heat, a small change in volume, or both. This lets a measured boundary constrain caloric properties and predicts how changing pressure shifts a melting temperature.
For a pure substance in bulk phase coexistence, label the initial and final thermodynamic phases α and β. Both have the same absolute temperature T, pressure p, and chemical potential. With s, h, and v denoting entropy, enthalpy, and volume on one common basis, define Δs=sβ​−sα​, Δh=hβ​−hα​, and Δv=vβ​−vα​. Then
dTdp​=ΔvΔs​=TΔvL​​,L=Δh=TΔs.
(18)
The derivative follows the phase coexistence curve; it is not a partial derivative within one thermodynamic phase. Use molar volume vm​ with molar latent heat Lm​ in Jmol−1, or specific volume v with latent heat L in Jkg−1. Both give dp/dT in PaK−1. For molar mass M, Lm​=ML and Δvm​=MΔv, so the predicted slope is independent of the chosen basis. This formulation assumes a simple compressible system, fixed composition, and negligible interface contributions; a curved interface can instead require the Young–Laplace equation.
For positive latent heat in the stated conversion direction, the sign of the slope is the sign of Δv. Converting liquid to gas normally increases volume and gives a positive slope. The negative melting-point slope of water illustrates the opposite sign. At a thermodynamic critical point, the coexisting gas and liquid merge: both L and Δv vanish, so their ratio needs a limiting analysis rather than substitution of zero into the Clapeyron equation.[ref]
A short derivation uses the molar Gibbs free energy gm​, equal to the chemical potential for a pure substance. Equality persists along the boundary, so dgm,α​=dgm,β​ with dgm​=−sm​dT+vm​dp. Therefore −Δsm​dT+Δvm​dp=0. Equality of gm​=hm​−Tsm​ also gives Δhm​=TΔsm​. Eliminating Δsm​ yields the Clapeyron equation.

Floating ice with submerged volume fraction f has density fρl​ by Archimedes' principle. Its volume change on melting is therefore vl​−vs​=(1−1/f)/ρl​<0. An enthalpy cycle gives Lf​=Ls​−Lv​ for quantities consistently expressed per mass at the same temperature. Treating these latent heats and specific volumes as constant and integrating the Clausius-Clapeyron relation gives the displayed pressure estimate. Using a vaporization latent heat extrapolated from a different temperature carries that approximation into the inferred fusion heat.

Ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation (dlnp/dT≃Lm​/(RT2))
Words: 447 Articles: 3
Clausius-Clapeyron relation OurBigBook.com
For liquid–gas phase coexistence, make two distinct approximations: the gas obeys the ideal gas law, vm,g​=RT/p, and its molar volume greatly exceeds the liquid molar volume, vm,g​≫vm,l​. Here R is the molar gas constant and p is the saturation pressure of the pure substance. Substitution into the Clapeyron equation gives
dTdp​≃RT2Lm​(T)p​,dTdln(p/p∗​)​≃RT2Lm​(T)​,
(19)
where p∗​>0 is any fixed reference pressure, making the logarithm dimensionless. Constant latent heat is a further approximation, needed for the elementary Integrated Clausius-Clapeyron relation below.[ref]
The neglected liquid molar volume has a transparent error estimate. If the gas is ideal but ϵ=vm,l​/vm,g​ is retained, then
(dTdp​)full​=RT2(1−ϵ)Lm​p​,(dp/dT)full​(dp/dT)approx​​=1−ϵ.
(20)
Thus ignoring the liquid molar volume underestimates this slope by a relative amount ϵ. This estimate does not bound errors from treating a real gas as ideal or from freezing Lm​(T). Near a thermodynamic critical point, the gas and liquid molar volumes approach each other and these dilute-vapor assumptions fail.
Integrated Clausius-Clapeyron relation (ln(p2​/p1​)≃−(Lm​/R)(1/T2​−1/T1​))
Words: 309 Articles: 2
Ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation OurBigBook.com
If the molar latent heat Lm​ is approximately constant on the chosen temperature interval, the ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation integrates to
lnp1​p2​​≃−RLm​​(T2​1​−T1​1​),p(T)≃p1​exp[RLm​​(T1​1​−T1​)].
(21)
For Lm​>0, higher absolute temperature gives higher saturation pressure. A plot of ln(p/p1​) against inverse temperature 1/T is a straight line of slope −Lm​/R. One reference pair (T1​,p1​) fixes its intercept. On a per-unit-mass basis replace Lm​/R by L/Rs​, with Rs​=R/M and M the molar mass; using L in Jkg−1 with the molar R would give incompatible units.
If only the constant-latent heat approximation is relaxed, the same ideal-vapor model instead gives
lnp1​p2​​≃R1​∫T1​T2​​T2Lm​(T)​dT.
(22)
Consequently an apparent straight-line fit across a broad temperature range need not represent a single material constant. The simple exponential also approaches a finite value as T→∞; this is a property of the stipulated formula, not a prediction that liquid–gas phase coexistence extends beyond a thermodynamic critical point.

Lm​(T)≃−Rd(1/T)dln(p/p∗​)​.

In the stipulated vapor-pressure calculation, the slope is −4811.16K, which recovers 40.0kJmol−1. Saturation-pressure equilibrium measurement
Words: 211
Clausius-Clapeyron relation OurBigBook.com
A concrete experiment turns the phase coexistence curve into pairs of temperature and saturation pressure. A 1969 paper reports water measurements made in 1942 and 1947–1949 using a boiler and precision mercury manometer. Platinum resistance thermometers read the boiler temperature through a resistance bridge. The pressure-transfer line passed through an ice-water condenser and a dry-ice trap; helium filled connecting sections. Corrected mercury column height gave the pressure at the thermometer coils, including hydrostatic pressure contributions from water vapor, helium, mercury, and mercury vapor.[ref]
Controls included normal/reversed-lead readings, readings at two thermometer electric currents extrapolated to zero current to remove self-heating, repeated bridge calibration, and daily triple point thermometer calibration checks. Cellar temperature measurements corrected fluid-column and gauge-block thermal expansion. One measurement series was excluded because a trapped gas bubble was suspected of corrupting the mercury head. The paper adjusted measurements to round temperatures using previously tabulated slopes; those adjusted values therefore are not independent measurements of those slopes.[ref]
The thermodynamic inference requires liquid and gas in phase coexistence, a well-defined common temperature, and a pressure corrected to that same location. Leaks, contamination, or a temperature gradient can spoil that interpretation. A separate calorimetry measurement of latent heat, together with the two specific volumes, allows a test of the full Clapeyron equation. Converting a measured phase coexistence curve slope directly into latent heat instead assumes that equation; it is not an independent test.
Common-pressure melting-point slope (dTm​/dp=−Tm​(1/ρs​−1/ρl​)/L)

Clausius-Clapeyron relation OurBigBook.com
When the solid and liquid have the same pressure, equality of chemical potentials implies −(sl​−ss​)dT+(1/ρl​−1/ρs​)dp=0. With latent heat of fusion L=Tm​(sl​−ss​), this gives the displayed slope. If solid is less dense than liquid, raising the common pressure depresses the melting temperature. Tm​ is absolute temperature. This coexistence-curve derivative differs from a thermomolecular pressure difference between equal-density phases.

These are explicitly stipulated rounded inputs, not measurements made here. The specific volume change on melting is
Δv=ρl​1​−ρs​1​=−9.0513×10−5m3kg−1.
(25)
The common-pressure melting-point slope therefore gives
dpdTm​​=LTm​Δv​=−7.4022×10−8KPa−1≃−0.07402KMPa−1.
(26)
A small 1MPa increase in the common pressure thus lowers the melting temperature by about 0.074K in this local model. Positive latent heat does not force a positive phase coexistence curve slope: ice contracts on conversion to liquid water. Increasing pressure favors the smaller specific volume.
