Equatorial coordinate system

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 8
An equatorial coordinate system labels a viewing direction on the celestial sphere by right ascension α, increasing eastward around the reference celestial equator, and declination δ, increasing northward from that celestial equator. These two angles locate a direction, without specifying the object's Euclidean distance. In the traditional convention, zero right ascension points toward the reference equinox, the northward crossing of the Sun's annual path through the celestial equator. An hour circle through the direction supplies the north-south arc on which declination is measured. [ref].
For a concrete construction, choose Cartesian coordinates with x pointing toward the reference equinox, z toward the north reference celestial pole, and positive y given by the cross product of the positive z and x directions. The direction's unit vector is
n=(cosδcosα, cosδsinα, sinδ).
(11)
This is a spherical coordinate system with polar angle π/2−δ and no radial coordinate. The trigonometric functions require consistent angular units: convert right ascension in hours to radians before evaluating them. The synthetic direction α=4h=60∘, δ=30∘ gives n=(3​/4,3/4,1/2); its squared norm is 3/16+9/16+1/4=1. A change of reference axes applies a rotation to this unit vector before recovering the two angles. Away from the celestial poles, recover right ascension using the quadrant-aware atan2(ny​,nx​) reduced modulo 2π, and declination using arcsinnz​; a plain arctangent of ny​/nx​ loses the quadrant. Adding a common offset to every right ascension describes only the special case of a rotation about the same polar axis.
The reference specification matters. For an equatorial coordinate system tied to the Earth, precession changes the reference celestial equator and reference equinox, and nutation distinguishes their mean and true versions. The date specifying those axes is distinct from the astronomical epoch at which a catalogue's source directions apply: advancing a star's proper motion and changing reference axes are separate operations. A modern catalogue may instead use fixed reference frame axes, aligned approximately with the traditional J2000.0 axes, without a moving reference equinox. Thus a pair of numbers labelled merely "J2000" does not settle every convention. State the reference frame, the astronomical epoch, and whether a direction is catalogued or apparent. [ref].
During one night's elementary fixed-direction approximation, a star's right ascension and declination remain constant while its altitude, azimuth and hour angle change because the Earth turns. In angular units, with Θ the local sidereal time, west-positive hour angle H obeys
H=Θ−α(mod2π).
(12)
At upper meridian transit, H=0 and the local sidereal time equals the transiting direction's right ascension. Pair apparent sidereal time with apparent right ascension referred to the true celestial equator and reference equinox of date; mean sidereal time refers to the mean reference equinox. Raw fixed-frame catalogue coordinates require conversion before precision pointing. [ref].
This separation makes the equatorial coordinate system useful: a catalogue can identify a target independently of the observer's current astronomical horizon, while local sidereal time tells the observer which hour circle is on the celestial meridian. Precision observations also account for proper motion, stellar parallax, aberration of light and atmospheric refraction; the fixed-direction construction above omits those effects. A reference-axis rotation preserves angles between directions, whereas changing the observer or correcting atmospheric refraction is a different operation. [ref].
