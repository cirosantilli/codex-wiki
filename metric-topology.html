Metric topology

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Topological analysis Metric space
OurBigBook.com Words: 137
The metric topology consists of the sets U such that, for every x∈U, some open ball Bd​(x,r) lies in U. Thus open sets describe which sufficiently small changes remain inside a region; they do not specify the numerical size of those changes.
For v∈R2, the L1 norm, Euclidean norm, and maximum norm satisfy
∥v∥∞​≤∥v∥2​≤∥v∥1​≤2​∥v∥2​≤2∥v∥∞​.
(6)
These bounds make them equivalent norms. For example, B∞​(a,r/2)⊆B1​(a,r)⊆B∞​(a,r): every open ball contains a sufficiently small open ball for either other metric. Hence all three define the same open sets, convergent sequences, and continuous maps. More generally, any two norms on a real or complex finite-dimensional vector space are equivalent norms; see H. A. Priestley, Functional Analysis I, §§0.5–0.7 and Theorem 4.3.
The metric topology therefore does not determine numerical distances. Multiplying a metric by 100 preserves its open sets while multiplying every distance by 100. Nor does the general equivalence of metrics require constant-factor bounds: that stronger comparison holds for the three norms used here.
