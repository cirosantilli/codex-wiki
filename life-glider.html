Life glider (F4(G)=G+(1,1))

 Home Computer science Conway Game of Life
OurBigBook.com Words: 288
The Life glider is a five-cell pattern that recreates its shape after four generations, shifted one cell along each coordinate. Use x increasing rightward and y increasing downward, as in the figure, and start from the live set
G0​={(1,0),(2,1),(0,2),(1,2),(2,2)}.
(18)
Every cell outside G0​ starts dead. Applying the Conway's Game of Life rule, rather than moving the drawing by hand, gives:
For an exact check of every frame, the intermediate live sets are
G1​G2​G3​G4​​={(0,1),(2,1),(1,2),(2,2),(1,3)},={(2,1),(0,2),(2,2),(1,3),(2,3)},={(1,1),(2,2),(3,2),(1,3),(2,3)},={(2,1),(3,2),(1,3),(2,3),(3,3)}=G0​+(1,1).​
(19)
Here G0​+(1,1) means add (1,1) to each ordered pair in G0​. In the first step, the live cells (1,0) and (0,2) have respectively one and one live neighbors, so die; the other three have respectively three, three and two and survive. Exactly (0,1) and (1,3) are born, with three live neighbors each. This derives G1​ and explains how motion can occur while individual cells merely switch states.
Because the rule uses the same relative neighbors at every position, applying the global update function and applying a translation commute. Thus Gt+4​=Gt​+(1,1) for every generation t; the four-step calculation establishes continued motion on the infinite board. The average displacement is (1/4,1/4) cells per generation, or Euclidean distance 2​/4 per generation. The live-cell count happens to stay five in these frames, but the rule does not conserve live-cell count in general: a single isolated live cell dies immediately. The Life glider persists indefinitely in an otherwise empty infinite board; encounters with other patterns or a finite implementation's edge can change that conclusion.
