Laplace distribution (Laplace(μ,b),b>0)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability distribution Continuous probability distribution
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 6
The Laplace distribution, also called the double exponential distribution, is a location-scale family with probability density function
f(x;μ,b)=2b1​exp(−b∣x−μ∣​),x∈R,μ∈R,b>0.
(76)
The location μ is its mean, median and mode; b controls its spread. In the centred rate convention, the same law has μ=0 and θ=1/b>0, giving f(x)=θe−θ∣x∣/2. Keeping track of whether a parameter is a scale or its reciprocal prevents reversing the effect of increasing it. [ref]
The two halves of the probability density function are reflected exponential decay curves, each with total probability 1/2. Their meeting point has a sharp corner: the left and right derivatives are 1/(2b2) and −1/(2b2). This finite peak is not a positive probability mass at μ. Indeed
P(∣X−μ∣≤ε)=1−e−ε/b⟶0(ε↓0),
(77)
so P(X=μ)=0, just as for any absolutely continuous probability distribution.
The figure compares a Laplace distribution with μ=0, b=1 against a normal distribution with mean zero and standard deviation σ=2​: both have variance 2. The right panel uses a logarithmic scale for the two-sided tail probability P(∣X∣>r). Its straight Laplace distribution curve comes from e−r; the normal distribution tail falls faster. Thus values far from the center can be more plausible under a Laplace distribution even after matching the variance. These are theoretical curves, not measured frequencies. The tails still have exponential decay; this is not a heavy-tailed distribution in the convention requiring every positive exponential moment to diverge.
Integrating either half of the probability density function gives the cumulative distribution function
F(x)={21​e(x−μ)/b,1−21​e−(x−μ)/b,​x<μ,x≥μ.​
(78)
In particular F(μ)=1/2. The basic moments are
E[X]=μ,E∣X−μ∣=b,Var(X)=2b2,SD(X)=2​b.
(79)
Thus the scale is the expected value of the absolute distance from the center, and is not the standard deviation. [ref] A short way to obtain the moments is to note that R=∣X−μ∣ has an exponential distribution with rate 1/b: P(R>r)=e−r/b for r≥0. Integrating gives ER=b and ER2=2b2; the symmetric distribution gives the centered first moment zero.
