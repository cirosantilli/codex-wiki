Let t1​,…,tm​ be the distinct roots in (0,1) at which ϕr​ changes sign, and put q(x)=∏j=1m​(x−tj​). Both ϕr​ and q change sign at each such point, while roots of even multiplicity do not change sign. Consequently ϕr​q has one constant sign on (0,1) except at its zeros, and is not identically zero. Its integral cannot vanish.
If m<r, however, orthogonality of the monic orthogonal polynomial ϕr​ to every polynomial of lower degree gives (ϕr​,q)=0, a contradiction. Hence m≥r. Since degϕr​=r, there must be exactly r such roots, each of multiplicity one. They exhaust the degree, leaving no complex, exterior, or endpoint roots. Thus all roots are simple and lie strictly in (0,1), which proves the requested weaker inclusion in [0,1]. This is the sign-change proof of the zeros of orthogonal polynomials theorem.

The source's requested formula is obtained by the two stated endpoint matching conditions within its restricted family, rather than by full Padé matching. At k=1 both reduce to x. By definition, a best uniform approximation p∗∈Pn​ minimizes ∥u−p∥∞​ over p∈Pn​. Write E=∥u−p∗∥∞​. The equioscillation theorem says that, if E>0, optimality is equivalent to the existence of n+2 ordered points with signed errors
u(ξj​)−p∗(ξj​)=σ(−1)jE,σ∈{1,−1}.
(26)
The absolute-value bars on the left of the printed formula must be omitted for this alternating-sign statement. A nonnegative absolute value cannot equal a negative −E.
For sufficiency, suppose a polynomial p had strictly smaller error. At the alternating points, p−p∗ must have the same sign as u−p∗, since otherwise the new error would have magnitude at least E. The difference polynomial therefore changes sign in all n+1 successive gaps and has at least n+1 distinct roots, impossible for a nonzero element of Pn​.
For necessity, suppose that no n+2 alternating extremal points exist. On the compact extremal set K={x:∣u(x)−p∗(x)∣=E}, group the signs of the error into consecutive blocks. The positive and negative extremal sets are disjoint compact sets, so sign changes are separated by positive distances; the number of blocks is finite. The absence of an n+2 alternating selection means there are at most n+1 blocks. Place a root in each gap between successive blocks and choose the overall sign of their product q to agree with the error on K. Then degq≤n and q is nonzero throughout K.
By compactness and continuity, on a neighborhood of K the signs still agree and ∣q∣ is bounded below. A sufficiently small positive η reduces the error there when p∗ is replaced by p∗+ηq. On the compact complement of this neighborhood the old error is below E by a fixed positive margin, and choosing η smaller preserves that margin. This gives a strictly better approximation, a contradiction. If E=0, the target already lies in Pn​ and optimality is immediate.
For u(x)=x1/3, the chord is x. Concavity gives x1/3−x≥0, with maximum at x∗​=1/(33​) and maximum value 2/(33​). Shift the chord up by half that amount. The errors at 0,x∗​,1 are respectively −E,+E,−E, so
p1∗​(x)=x+33​1​,E=33​1​.​
(27)

Use the standard meaning of a cubic spline: a C2 function that is cubic on each unit interval. If it vanishes on both exterior intervals, continuity of the function and its first two derivatives gives zero endpoint jets at zero and at k.
Such a spline can be represented through its third-derivative jumps as
ϕ(x)=∑j=0k​cj​(x−j)+3​.
(28)
For x≥k, this must be the zero polynomial. Expanding in powers of x yields the four moment conditions
∑j=0k​cj​jm=0,m=0,1,2,3.
(29)
If k≤3, the columns of this Vandermonde system are linearly independent, so every cj​=0. No nonzero spline has such short support.
For k=4, a nonzero null vector is (1,−4,6,−4,1), since the fourth finite difference annihilates every cubic. Thus the Cardinal cubic B-spline is
ϕ(x)=61​[x+3​−4(x−1)+3​+6(x−2)+3​−4(x−3)+3​+(x−4)+3​].​
(30)
Its knot values at 0,1,2,3,4 are 0,1/6,2/3,1/6,0, whose sum is one. It is nonzero and supported on [0,4], proving the minimal value k=4. Equivalently, on [0,1] it is x3/6, on [1,2] it is [−3x3+12x2−12x+4]/6, and the other half follows by symmetry ϕ(4−x)=ϕ(x).

Work in the usual bounded Lipschitz, polygonally triangulated setting in which Sobolev traces and conforming meshes are defined. Choose an H1 lift G of the smooth boundary data. Multiplying the Poisson equation by v∈H01​(Ω) and integrating by parts gives the weak formulation
∫Ω​∇u⋅∇v=∫Ω​fv,u∈G+H01​(Ω).
(31)
The test function has zero trace, so the boundary flux term vanishes. This formulation only needs first weak derivatives.
Let Vh​ be the conforming finite element space of continuous functions affine on each triangle, and Vh0​ its zero-boundary subspace. Choose a nodal interpolation Gh​ of the boundary lift and seek uh​∈Gh​+Vh0​ satisfying the same identity for every vh​∈Vh0​. General smooth boundary data need not be exactly representable by boundary piecewise linear functions; this is the usual discrete boundary approximation.
With nodal shape functions φj​, interior coefficients Uj​ satisfy
∑j∈I​Aij​Uj​=Fi​−∑j∈B​Aij​gj​,Aij​=∫Ω​∇φi​⋅∇φj​,Fi​=∫Ω​fφi​.
(32)
Here I and B index interior and boundary vertices. Finite element assembly sums the triangle contributions.
Take a translated lower triangle with local vertices (0,0),(h,0),(h,h). Its local functions are N1​=1−x/h, N2​=(x−y)/h, N3​=y/h. Their gradients are constant and the area is h2/2, giving the stiffness matrix
A(T)=21​​1−10​−12−1​0−11​​.​
(33)
Translations preserve this matrix, while rotations or a different local vertex order permute its entries. The coupling across the hypotenuse is zero.
Each interior grid vertex belongs to six triangles. Summation gives diagonal value 4 and coupling −1 to each of its four horizontal and vertical neighbors. Both positive-diagonal edge couplings vanish. Thus, before prescribed boundary values are removed, an interior equation has the five-point stencil
4Uij​−Ui−1,j​−Ui+1,j​−Ui,j−1​−Ui,j+1​=Fij​.​
(34)
There is no h−2 factor in the assembled variational matrix; that factor belongs to a differently normalized finite-difference equation.
If h=1/N and m=N−1, lexicographic ordering makes the Dirichlet-reduced matrix block tridiagonal with diagonal blocks tridiag(−1,4,−1) and neighboring blocks −I. A row with all four neighboring nodes unknown has five nonzeros: 4 once and −1 four times. Rows adjacent to the boundary have fewer unknown-neighbor entries; their exact count is 1+1i>1​+1i<m​+1j>1​+1j<m​. Removed boundary contributions appear on the right, rather than as new nonzeros.

The Sobolev space H1(b,c) consists of L2 functions with a weak first derivative in L2, with norm
∥v∥H12​=∫bc​(∣v∣2+∣v′∣2)dx.
(35)
In one dimension these functions have absolutely continuous representatives and well-defined endpoint Sobolev traces. Put
V={v∈H1(b,c):v(b)=0}.
(36)
This is a closed subspace, hence a Hilbert space. Both trial and test functions belong to V; one does not impose a derivative boundary condition in this space.
Integrate the differential equation against v∈V:
∫bc​fv=∫bc​exu′v′dx−ecu′(c)v(c).
(37)
The printed condition uses the ordinary derivative, u′(c)=−u(c), so the weak form is
a(u,v)=∫bc​exu′v′dx+ecu(c)v(c),ℓ(v)=∫bc​fvdx.​
(38)
Seek u∈V with a(u,v)=ℓ(v) for all v∈V. The coefficient ec in the endpoint term must be retained: replacing the ordinary derivative with the conormal flux would change the boundary problem.
Let L=c−b. The Poincaré inequality on V can be stated as ∥v∥L2​≤L∥v′∥L2​. Also ∣v(c)∣≤L​∥v′∥L2​. Consequently
a(v,v)≥eb∥v′∥L22​≥α∥v∥H12​,α=1+L2eb​>0,
(39)
proving coercivity. By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the trace bound,
∣a(u,v)∣≤ec(1+L)∥u∥H1​∥v∥H1​.
(40)
Thus continuity holds with M=ec(1+L). The load is continuous if f∈L2, and the Lax-Milgram theorem gives a unique weak solution.
Choose a finite-dimensional conforming subspace Vh​⊂V, such as continuous piecewise linear functions vanishing at b, and solve a(uh​,vh​)=ℓ(vh​) for every vh​∈Vh​. Subtracting the weak equations gives Galerkin orthogonality a(u−uh​,vh​)=0.
Céa's lemma states, for a continuous coercive bilinear form with constants M,α on a Hilbert space,
∥u−uh​∥H1​≤αM​vh​∈Vh​inf​∥u−vh​∥H1​.​
(41)
To prove it, put e=u−uh​ and use orthogonality to write a(e,e)=a(e,u−vh​) for any vh​∈Vh​. Coercivity and continuity give α∥e∥2≤M∥e∥∥u−vh​∥. If e=0 the claim is immediate; otherwise cancel its norm and take the infimum. Any approximation theorem making the infimum tend to zero under mesh refinement therefore gives strong convergence uh​→u in H1.
Finally, a is symmetric and positive definite on V. It is an inner product, so ∥v∥a​=a(v,v)​ is an energy norm on all of V: homogeneity and definiteness are immediate, and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for this inner product gives the triangle inequality. No smaller space is required. In this norm the continuity and coercivity constants are one. More precisely, Galerkin orthogonality gives
∥u−vh​∥a2​=∥u−uh​∥a2​+∥uh​−vh​∥a2​,
(42)
so
∥u−uh​∥a​=vh​∈Vh​min​∥u−vh​∥a​.​
(43)
The finite element solution is the exact best energy-norm approximation.

Take linearly independent basis functions φi​∈H01​(0,1) and write uh​(x,t)=∑j​Uj​(t)φj​(x). The weak formulation of the heat equation, tested against φi​, is
∫01​∂t​uh​φi​=−∫01​uh′​φi′​.
(44)
Thus the semidiscrete finite element heat equation is
MU˙=KU,Mij​=∫01​φi​φj​,Kij​=−∫01​φi′​φj′​.​
(45)
The vector U contains the basis coefficients. A natural initial vector G is the L2 projection of the initial data: MG=b, where bi​=∫01​gφi​, and U(0)=G. Other consistent initial approximations can be specified, but exact representation of an arbitrary g in the trial space is not implicit.
For a nonzero coefficient vector a, the function vh​=∑i​ai​φi​ is nonzero by basis independence. Hence aTMa=∥vh​∥L22​>0, proving that the mass matrix is symmetric positive definite. Also aTKa=−∥vh′​∥L22​<0: if the derivative vanished, vh​ would be a constant, and its zero endpoint traces would make it zero. Therefore K is symmetric negative definite, not merely semidefinite, for these homogeneous Dirichlet conditions.
The squared spatial norm is UTMU. Differentiating and using the semidiscrete system gives the energy identity
dtd​∥uh​(t)∥L22​=2UTMU˙=2UTKU=−2∥uh′​(t)∥L22​≤0.
(46)
Using the trapezoidal rule in time gives the Crank-Nicolson method
(M−21​ΔtK)Uk+1=(M+21​ΔtK)Uk.​
(47)
The left matrix is positive definite for every Δt>0, so every step exists uniquely. To prove stability, set d=Uk+1−Uk and s=Uk+1+Uk. The update says Md=(Δt/2)Ks. Multiplying by sT gives
(Uk+1)TMUk+1−(Uk)TMUk=2Δt​sTKs≤0.
(48)
Consequently
∥uhk+1​∥L2​≤∥uhk​∥L2​for every Δt>0.​
(49)
The same inequality applied to the difference of two discrete solutions proves nonamplification of initial perturbations. This is unconditional stability in the mass-matrix norm, exactly the spatial L2 norm of the finite element functions.
