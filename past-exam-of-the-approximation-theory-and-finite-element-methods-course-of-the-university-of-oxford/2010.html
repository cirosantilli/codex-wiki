A cubic spline that vanishes for x≤0 and is twice continuously differentiable can be written using truncated power functions as s(x)=∑j=0k​aj​(x−jh)+3​. Vanishing for x≥kh imposes the four moment equations ∑j​aj​jr=0 for r=0,1,2,3. Their Vandermonde matrix has rank min(4,k+1), so a nonzero coefficient vector first exists at k=4​.
A normalized compact cubic cardinal B-spline is
B3​(x/h)=61​j=0∑4​(−1)j(j4​)(x/h−j)+3​.​
(4)
It has nodal values 0,1/6,2/3,1/6,0 at 0,h,2h,3h,4h, so their sum is one. The alternating binomial coefficients meet the four moment equations, and the factor 1/6 fixes the requested normalization.
For interpolation on [−1,1], centre the translates as βj​(x)=B3​(x/h−j+2), with j=−n−1,…,n+1. Their restrictions form a basis for the cubic C2 spline space on these knots. Write S=∑j​cj​βj​. The 2n+1 interpolation equations are
6cr−1​+4cr​+cr+1​​=u(rh),r=−n,…,n.​
(5)
The recurrence determines all remaining coefficients after choosing c−n−1​,c−n​, giving exactly two free parameters. The two natural cubic spline conditions become
c−n−1​−2c−n​+c−n+1​=0,cn−1​−2cn​+cn+1​=0,​
(6)
because S′′(rh)=h−2(cr−1​−2cr​+cr+1​).
For uniqueness, take the difference s of two candidates. It vanishes at every knot and has s′′(±1)=0. Integrating by parts twice on each cubic piece gives ∫−11​(s′′)2=0: terms involving s vanish at the knots, and those involving s′′s′ cancel at internal knots and vanish at the endpoints. Thus s′′=0; the remaining affine function vanishes at both endpoints and is zero. The square linear system for the coefficients is therefore nonsingular, giving existence as well as uniqueness of the natural cubic spline interpolant.

Here the exchange point lies between adjacent reference points and has the same, positive error sign as the point it replaces. Hence the new set remains spatially ordered, and the old error at its points alternates in sign. Its magnitudes are ∣h0​∣ everywhere except at η, where M=u(η)−q(η)>∣h0​∣.
Set wr​=∏j=r​(zr​−zj​)−1. Apply the order-(n+1) divided difference to q and v; both vanish because their degrees are at most n. In the new alternation equations, all products (−1)rwr​ have the same sign. Thus the single-point exchange in polynomial minimax approximation gives
∣h1​∣=∣h0​∣+∑r​∣wr​∣∣ws​∣​(M−∣h0​∣)>∣h0​∣.​
(16)
The Remez algorithm repeatedly solves for the reference polynomial and common signed height, locates a larger error extreme, and performs an order-preserving exchange. The reference height bounds the optimum from below and the global error maximum bounds it from above. Their gap provides an optimality stopping certificate. A general implementation also needs the endpoint exchange rule when the new extreme lies outside the reference set.

For a line p(x)=ax+b, the error ex−ax−b is strictly convex. Its two endpoint maxima must agree, giving a=e−1. Its unique interior minimum is at c=loga∈(0,1). Equating its magnitude to the endpoint error gives
p1∗​(x)=ax+21+a−aloga​,E1​=21−a+aloga​,a=e−1.​
(17)
The errors at 0,c,1 are respectively E1​,−E1​,E1​. The equioscillation theorem therefore certifies this best uniform approximation directly.
For degree above one, subtracting a general polynomial does not preserve convexity of the error. The required n+2 alternating extreme points include additional unknown interior points, and their derivative and equal-height equations form a coupled nonlinear problem. The simple endpoint-and-single-minimum calculation therefore does not extend as a direct higher-degree recipe.

The first-order Sobolev space is H1(Ω)={v∈L2(Ω):∂1​v,∂2​v∈L2(Ω)}, with derivatives understood as weak derivatives and norm squared ∫Ω​(v2+∣∇v∣2).
The printed normal boundary condition must be distinguished from a conormal condition. Take K symmetric positive definite, as required for the symmetric matrix requested later. Integration by parts gives
∫Ω​∇v⋅K∇u−∫∂Ω​vn⋅K∇u=∫Ω​fv.
(18)
The natural boundary term contains the conormal derivative n⋅K∇u, not the ordinary normal derivative ∂n​u. On a smooth boundary, writing t for its unit tangent, the literal condition ∂n​u=0 leaves the term b∂t​u, where b=n⋅Kt. Its weak form therefore retains a boundary pairing:
∫Ω​∇v⋅K∇u−⟨b∂t​(Tru),Trv⟩=∫Ω​fv.
(19)
Here the tangential derivative of the Sobolev trace can be interpreted in H−1/2 and paired against H1/2 on a smooth boundary. This form is generally nonsymmetric; the requested usual symmetric energy construction does not follow from the literal boundary condition.
For an explicit failure of the claimed mean-zero compatibility, take the unit disk, K=diag(2,1) and u=(x2−y2)[1−(x2+y2)/2]. It has ∂n​u=0 on the circle, but
f=−∇⋅K∇u=−2+12x2−6y2,∫Ω​f=−2π​=0.
(20)
Thus replacing the printed normal condition by a zero conormal flux is not harmless.
For the intended zero-conormal derivative problem, the requested variational and finite element conclusions are as follows. Seek u∈H1(Ω) with
a(u,v):=∫Ω​∇v⋅K∇u=ℓ(v):=∫Ω​fv(v∈H1(Ω)).​
(21)
Putting v=1 gives ∫Ω​f=0. On a connected domain this condition permits a solution unique modulo constants; the Poincare-Wirtinger inequality gives coercivity after fixing the mean.
The energy is J(v)=21​a(v,v)−ℓ(v). A minimizer satisfies dtd​J(u+tv)∣t=0​=a(u,v)−ℓ(v)=0. Conversely, a weak solution satisfies
J(v)−J(u)=21​a(v−u,v−u)≥0,
(22)
so it is a minimizer. Testing by u gives the minimum value
Jmin​=−21​ℓ(u)=−21​a(u,u).​
(23)
This value is independent of the arbitrary additive constant when ∫f=0. If the compatibility fails, constant shifts make J unbounded below.
Write uh​=∑j​cj​ϕj​. The Galerkin method gives
Ac=F,Aij​=∫Ω​∇ϕi​⋅K∇ϕj​,Fi​=∫Ω​fϕi​.​
(24)
Symmetry of K gives AT=A. For vh​=∑j​zj​ϕj​, zTAz=∫Ω​∇vh​⋅K∇vh​≥0. Equality forces vh​ constant by positive definiteness and connectedness. Since the basis sums to one, the coefficient vector 1=(1,…,1)T represents that constant, giving
kerA=span(1),1TF=∫Ω​f=0.​
(25)
Thus the stiffness matrix is symmetric positive semidefinite and singular; the displayed compatibility condition is necessary and sufficient for the finite system. Positive definiteness of K controls every gradient direction, preventing additional zero-energy directions and supplying ellipticity. A degenerate coefficient can introduce further kernel modes, while an indefinite one destroys the minimizing-energy argument.

Take the affine trial space V1​={v∈H1(a,b):v(a)=1} and the test space V0​={v∈H1(a,b):v(a)=0}. Multiplication by v∈V0​ and integration by parts incorporate the right Neumann boundary condition as a load:
u∈V1​,∫ab​u′v′dx=∫ab​fvdx+2v(b)(v∈V0​).​
(26)
For the continuous piecewise-quadratic trial space, impose the left value exactly and use the same equation for every piecewise-quadratic test function vanishing at a. With a basis (ψj​) of that test space and uh​=1+∑j​cj​ψj​, the finite element equations are
j∑​(∫ab​ψj′​ψi′​dx)cj​=∫ab​fψi​dx+2ψi​(b).​
(27)
The Endpoint Poincaré inequality, reflected about the interval midpoint, makes the derivative norm a norm on V0​. Galerkin orthogonality then gives the exact best-approximation identity: for every admissible vh​,
∥u′−vh′​∥22​=∥u′−uh′​∥22​+∥uh′​−vh′​∥22​.
(28)
Therefore uh​ minimizes the derivative error over all admissible piecewise-quadratic functions.
To prove the requested error bound, assume f∈H1(a,b). Then u∈H3(a,b) and u′′′=−f′. We need an elementary Poincare-Wirtinger inequality on each element I of length hI​: if w∈H1(I) has mean zero, its continuous representative has a zero somewhere, and the fundamental theorem of calculus and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality give ∥w∥L2(I)​≤hI​∥w′∥L2(I)​.
Choose an affine function qI​ whose slope is the average of u′′ on I and whose average equals the average of u′. Then w=u′−qI​ and w′=u′′−qI′​ both have mean zero. Applying the just-proved bound twice yields
∥u′−qI​∥L2(I)​≤hI2​∥u′′′∥L2(I)​.

Define a quadratic interpolant on I by integrating qI​ from the true left-endpoint value. The equal-average condition ensures that it reaches the true right-endpoint value, so these pieces join to an admissible continuous piecewise-quadratic function Ih​u. Sum the squared estimates and use the best-approximation property:
∥u′−uh′​∥2​≤∥u′−(Ih​u)′∥2​≤h2∥u′′′∥2​=h2∥f′∥2​.​

The bilinear square finite element contains the terms 1,x,y,xy. In anticlockwise corner order beginning at (0,0), its shape functions are
N1​=(1−x/h)(1−y/h),N2​=(x/h)(1−y/h),N3​=xy/h2,N4​=(1−x/h)(y/h).
(33)
The mass matrix has entries Mij​=∫0h​∫0h​Ni​Nj​dxdy. Each product separates into one-dimensional factors, with ∫01​(1−s)2ds=∫01​s2ds=1/3 and ∫01​s(1−s)ds=1/6. Diagonal, adjacent-corner and opposite-corner entries are respectively h2/9, h2/18, h2/36. Thus
ME​=36h2​​4212​2421​1242​2124​​.​
(34)
Number global grid nodes by k=i+(n+1)j, with xi​=ih, yj​=jh and 0≤i,j≤n. For an interior node, four elements contribute to its diagonal, two to each axial neighbour and one to each diagonal neighbour. Its global row therefore has the nine-point stencil
36h2​​141​4164​141​​.​
(35)
In the stated ordering the axial entries occur at columns k±1 and k±(n+1); the four diagonal ones occur at k±(n+1)±1. No boundary degrees of freedom have been removed.
For general valid quadrilaterals, use an isoparametric finite element. Map the unit square to an element by F(ξ,η)=∑i=14​xi​Ni​(ξ,η) and define physical shape functions Ni​∘F−1. Require a one-to-one nondegenerate map and compatible common edges. On a shared edge the map and the trace of each shape function are linear between the same endpoints; the neighbouring traces therefore agree. The assembled functions are continuous and lie in H1, giving a conforming finite element space. On a non-parallelogram the pulled-back functions are generally not bilinear polynomials in the physical coordinates themselves.
