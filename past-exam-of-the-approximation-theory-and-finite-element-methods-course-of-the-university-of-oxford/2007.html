Assume A is nonempty, as is necessary for an approximating point to exist. The reverse triangle inequality gives
∣d(u,q)−d(u,r)∣≤d(q,r).
(1)
Thus the distance from the fixed point u is a continuous, indeed Lipschitz continuous, real-valued function on A. A continuous function on a nonempty compact set attains its minimum, so some p∈A satisfies
d(u,p)=q∈Amin​d(u,q)=q∈Ainf​d(u,q).​
(2)
If empty compact sets are allowed, the printed statement needs this nonemptiness qualification.

The vector subspace contains zero, whose approximation error is ∥u∥. For any q with ∥q∥>2∥u∥, the triangle inequality gives ∥u−q∥≥∥q∥−∥u∥>∥u∥, so such points cannot improve on zero.
Restrict to K={q∈A:∥q∥≤2∥u∥+1}. This is nonempty, closed and bounded in a finite-dimensional normed space, hence compact. The continuous function q↦∥u−q∥ attains a minimum on K by part (a). Outside K its value exceeds the error at zero, so that minimizer is best on all of A:
∥u−p∥≤∥u−q∥(q∈A).​
(3)
Completeness of the ambient space is unnecessary; compactness is used only in the finite-dimensional vector subspace.

c OurBigBook.com
The geometry of a strictly convex normed space proves uniqueness whenever a best approximation exists. If distinct p1​,p2​ both have best error E>0, then their two residuals have norm E. The defining midpoint inequality gives
​u−2p1​+p2​​​=​2(u−p1​)+(u−p2​)​​<E,
(4)
contradicting best approximation because the midpoint lies in A. If E=0, both approximants equal u.
The printed existence assertion is false for an arbitrary vector subspace. For a concrete counterexample take B=ℓ2 with its strictly convex Hilbert norm, let A=c00​ be its finite-support sequences, and take u=(2−j)j≥1​. Truncations in A tend to u, so the best-error infimum is zero, but u∈/A and that infimum is not attained. If the finite-dimensional assumption of part (b) is retained, it supplies existence and the argument above supplies uniqueness.
For the estimate, suppose Y is a projection operator onto A, as needed for a finite operator norm. Since Yp=p,
u−Yu=(u−p)−Y(u−p).
(5)
The triangle inequality and operator norm give
∥u−Yu∥≤(1+∥Y∥)∥u−p∥=(1+∥Y∥)E.​
(6)
The estimate actually holds without attainment: apply it with any q∈A instead of p and take the infimum. Its proof applies in every normed space. This is strict convexity separates uniqueness from existence in best approximation.

Use the usual approximation-space convention Pn​={p:degp≤n}. The source's phrase “degree n” must include lower degrees for its later space and interpolation statements; the consequence of a literal exact-degree reading is addressed in part (d).
Order the alternating points as x0​<⋯<xn+1​. If q had strictly smaller error than En​, the values
(q−p)(xj​)=(u−p)(xj​)−(u−q)(xj​)
(12)
would have the alternating signs of (u−p)(xj​): the second term has magnitude strictly less than En​. The intermediate value theorem would then give at least n+1 distinct zeros of q−p, one in each gap. A nonzero polynomial of degree at most n cannot have that many zeros. Thus no such q exists, proving
∥u−p∥∞​≤∥u−q∥∞​(q∈Pn​).​
(13)
The remainder of the equioscillation theorem says that a best uniform approximating polynomial exists, is unique, and is characterized by this n+2-point alternating extreme-error condition. For positive error, alternation is necessary as well as sufficient. If u itself lies in Pn​, the unique best polynomial is u with zero error.

If (u−p,q)=0 for all q∈A, then for any a∈A the difference p−a lies in A and Pythagorean theorem gives
∥u−a∥22​=∥(u−p)+(p−a)∥22​=∥u−p∥22​+∥p−a∥22​≥∥u−p∥22​.
(22)
Thus p is best.
Conversely, if p is best, then for every q∈A the real quadratic
∥u−p−tq∥22​=∥u−p∥22​−2t(u−p,q)+t2∥q∥22​
(23)
has its minimum at t=0. Its linear coefficient must vanish, giving (u−p,q)=0. Hence best approximation is equivalent to orthogonality of the residual to the approximation space. The weight is positive almost everywhere, so this is a genuine positive-definite inner product on continuous functions.

a(u,v)=∫ab​(u′v′+x2uv)dx+u(a)v(a)+u(b)v(b),ℓ(v)=∫ab​fvdx+Av(a)+Bv(b).

(Δtuhm+1​−uhm​​,vh​)+((uhm+1​)′,vh′​)+(uhm+1​,vh​)=0(vh​∈Vh​).​
(53)
This applies Backward Euler method to diffusion and reaction together. The step bilinear form is coercive, so each step has a unique solution.

Test with vh​=uhm+1​ and apply 2(a−b,a)=∥a∥2−∥b∥2+∥a−b∥2. The exact energy identity is
2Δt∥uhm+1​∥22​−∥uhm​∥22​+∥uhm+1​−uhm​∥22​​+∥(uhm+1​)′∥22​+∥uhm+1​∥22​=0.
(54)
The printed identity omits the last reaction contribution while explicitly describing its last term as the H1 seminorm. It requires the full H1 norm instead of that seminorm. With the printed seminorm its left side equals −∥uhm+1​∥22​, rather than zero. The source reaction term forces this correction.
All terms other than the difference of successive squared norms are nonnegative. Consequently
∥uhm​∥2​≤∥uh0​∥2​≤∥u0​∥2​.​
(55)
The second inequality uses contractivity of the initial orthogonal projection. No restriction relates Δt to h, giving unconditional stability. The retained reaction term strengthens dissipation.

The matrices are symmetric, and VTAV=∥vh​∥22​+Δt(∥vh′​∥22​+∥vh​∥22​)>0 for nonzero V, proving positive definiteness.
