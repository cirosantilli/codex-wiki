Cathode-ray attenuation with finite detector acceptance (U(x)=e−(a+b)x,W(x)=e−ax)

An original toy model separates two ways to lose a central cathode ray signal. Let x be forward depth into a uniform gas. Normalize the entering electron population to one. Write U(x) for the fraction still in a narrow forward channel, and D(x) for the fraction diverted into a wider collection channel. Assume constant removal rate a per unit depth in both channels, and constant transfer rate b from U to D, with no return. Removal means that an electron stops or is trapped before reaching the collecting plane; it does not mean that its electric charge disappears. The wide ideal collector accepts all surviving members of both channels. This is a stipulated two-population differential equation model, not a general law of electron transport:
dxdU​=−(a+b)U,dxdD​=bU−aD,U(0)=1,D(0)=0.
(29)
The first equation gives exponential decay. Adding the equations gives W′=−aW for W=U+D, hence
U(x)=e−(a+b)x,W(x)=e−ax,D(x)=e−ax(1−e−bx).
(30)
These formulas also give a conservation check: U+D+(1−W)=1. The fraction 1−W has been removed before the plane, while D remains present but misses the narrow collector. An exponential fit to the narrow signal therefore measures a+b, not the removal rate a by itself. This is an identifiability issue: one declining signal cannot distinguish the two unknown rates.
For a worked example, choose a=0.15cm−1 and b=0.35cm−1. These are invented teaching inputs, not measured coefficients for air or Lenard's apparatus. At x=2.00cm,
U=e−1≃0.3679,W=e−0.3≃0.7408,D≃0.3729,1−W≃0.2592.
(31)
Thus about 63.2% misses the narrow collector, although only about 25.9% has been removed in this model. Treating the whole narrow-signal deficit as stopping would confuse redistribution with removal.
Suppose a calibrated readout is proportional to arriving electron number with the same response for either channel. Choose an empty-path signal R0​=1020 arbitrary units, of which B=20 is background; the two collection geometries have the same calibrated reference. The calculated readings at 2cm are Rn​=20+1000e−1≃387.88 and Rw​=20+1000e−0.3≃760.82. After background subtraction, define Tj​=(Rj​−B)/(R0​−B) for each geometry. Then the original inverse problem has the solution
a=−xlnTw​​=0.15cm−1,b=−xlnTn​​−a=0.35cm−1.
(32)
A single narrow reading would instead allow every nonnegative pair with a+b=0.50cm−1. Using the raw ratios without subtracting B gives an erroneous narrow rate of about 0.4834cm−1. In a real fluorescent screen, the assumed response must be tested: deposited energy and conversion to light need not remain constant as electrons slow. Repeating the comparison at several depths tests the constant slopes, although straight slopes alone do not establish the microscopic mechanism.
Actual electrons change kinetic energy and direction. Electron stopping power S(K)=−dK/ds>0 describes mean kinetic energy loss per travelled path length s; collisional losses include ionization, while radiative losses include bremsstrahlung. The continuous slowing-down approximation range integrates reciprocal total electron stopping power, neglecting fluctuations. It approximates travelled path length, not forward penetration depth.[ref]
RCSDA​(K0​)=∫0K0​​S(K)dK​.
(33)
As a separate original limiting example, stipulate a straight path, constant S=2keVcm−1, and entering kinetic energy K0​=6keV. Then K(s)=6keV−Ss until stopping, and RCSDA​=3cm. In that deterministic model every entering electron reaches each depth below 3cm and none crosses a plane beyond it. There is no exponential population tail. Real fluctuations and electron scattering make the situation more complicated, but the example already proves that continuous energy loss does not imply constant-rate removal.
The window also changes the entering population: source kinetic energy before the foil need not equal K0​ just outside it. For an electron initially at rest, an ideal static accelerating electric potential difference V>0 supplies electrical work eV, where e is the elementary charge. If losses before and within the foil total ΔK≥0, a transmitted electron enters the observation chamber with K0​=eV−ΔK≤eV. A screen's brightness determines neither V nor ΔK. These formulas specify a modern ideal work-energy theorem argument; no numerical historical voltage or foil thickness is inferred from the diagram.
