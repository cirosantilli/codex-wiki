Replicator equation (x˙i​=xi​[(Ax)i​−xTAx])

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Game theory Evolutionary game theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k
The replicator equation says that a strategy grows in frequency when its expected payoff exceeds the population's current mean payoff. It is an ordinary differential equation for a composition, rather than for total population size. The subtraction of the mean is what makes relative success matter: even a type growing in absolute number can shrink as a fraction if other types grow faster.
Let xi​(t) be the frequency of pure strategy i, with n available pure strategies and
x∈Δn​={x∈Rn:xi​≥0, ∑i=1n​xi​=1}.
(8)
In a symmetric bimatrix game, let the payoff matrix A have entry aij​ equal to the payoff to strategy i against j. The other player's payoff in that encounter is aji​: symmetry between player roles does not require A=AT. Uniform random encounters give the expected payoffs and their population mean
fi​(x)=(Ax)i​=∑j​aij​xj​,fˉ​(x)=∑i​xi​fi​(x)=xTAx.
(9)
After choosing the time unit so that the growth-rate scale is one, the continuous-time replicator equation is
x˙i​=xi​(fi​(x)−fˉ​(x))=xi​((Ax)i​−xTAx),i=1,…,n.
(10)
Here a payoff is measured on a scale proportional to net growth per unit time, not merely as an arbitrary preference ranking. More generally one can specify locally Lipschitz frequency-dependent payoffs fi​(x) directly; the same equation with fˉ​=∑i​xi​fi​ then defines a unique frequency trajectory. The linear pairwise model and its reproductive interpretation are given in Taylor and Jonker (1978), sections 2–3; the formulas here also follow by the following direct normalization.
Suppose the number Ni​ of type i obeys N˙i​=Ni​[b(t)+fi​(x)], where the baseline b(t) is identical for all types. Set N=∑i​Ni​>0 and xi​=Ni​/N. The quotient rule gives
N˙=N[b(t)+fˉ​(x)],x˙i​=N2N˙i​N−Ni​N˙​=xi​(fi​−fˉ​).
(11)
Thus the common baseline cancels. Adding the same constant to every entry of A leaves the replicator equation unchanged; multiplying A by a positive constant changes only the speed along trajectories. The same payoff matrix can therefore describe frequency evolution at different growth-rate baselines and time scales.
The probability simplex is invariant in forward time. On ∑i​xi​=1,
∑i​x˙i​=∑i​xi​fi​−fˉ​∑i​xi​=0.
(12)
Normalization alone would not exclude negative frequencies. Nonnegativity follows as well: along a solution,
xi​(t)=xi​(0)exp(∫0t​[fi​(x(s))−fˉ​(x(s))]ds).
(13)
An absent type remains absent, and a positive frequency remains positive at every finite time. For the fixed payoff matrix model, bounded rates on the compact probability simplex give global forward solutions. Frequencies can still tend to zero as t→∞. Each face is invariant, so this model has no spontaneous introduction of new pure strategies; mutation or outside introductions would require additional terms. For two present types the logarithm of their frequency ratio satisfies dtd​log(xi​/xj​)=fi​−fj​, directly expressing the accumulated payoff advantage.
For two pure strategies, write the composition as (x,1−x), where x is the frequency of strategy 1, and let
A=(ac​bd​),f1​=ax+b(1−x),f2​=cx+d(1−x).
(14)
Their payoff difference and the resulting scalar ordinary differential equation are
Δf(x)=f1​−f2​=(b−d)+(a−b−c+d)x,x˙=F(x)=x(1−x)Δf(x).
(15)
In the interior 0<x<1, the sign of the payoff difference determines the direction of motion. The endpoints are always equilibrium points, and an additional interior equilibrium point exists precisely when the affine payoff difference has a zero strictly between them. At a simple interior zero x∗​, F′(x∗​)=x∗​(1−x∗​)Δf′(x∗​): a negative value gives asymptotic stability, a positive value gives instability. At the endpoints F′(0)=b−d and F′(1)=c−a, with stability understood relative to [0,1]. A zero derivative requires examining the actual flow rather than this strict sign test.
For an original coordination game, imagine two conventions that reward matching, with convention 1 giving the larger matching reward. Choose
Acoord​=(30​02​),f1​=3x,f2​=2(1−x),x˙=x(1−x)(5x−2).
(16)
The crossing is x∗​=2/5. Below it, convention 1 loses frequency; above it, convention 1 gains frequency. The endpoint equilibrium points 0 and 1 have one-sided asymptotic stability, while 2/5 is unstable: the corresponding derivatives are −2,−3 and 6/5, respectively. Every initial 0<x(0)<2/5 tends to 0, every initial 2/5<x(0)<1 tends to 1, and exactly x(0)=2/5 stays there. Even the convention with the larger matching reward can disappear if it starts below the threshold. This is bistability, not selection of a universally best convention.
The compositions giving a symmetric Nash equilibrium in this coordination game are the two pure conventions and the mixed composition (2/5,3/5). Both pure conventions are evolutionarily stable strategies because a mutant convention has strictly lower payoff against the pure resident. The mixed symmetric Nash equilibrium is not an evolutionarily stable strategy: against that resident both conventions tie, but after a pure convention-1 mutant is introduced its payoff advantage is 5(x−2/5)>0. Equal payoffs at a symmetric Nash equilibrium need not provide resistance to invasion or asymptotic stability.
For an original coexistence model, choose payoffs making each convention advantageous when sufficiently rare:
Acoex​=(12​30​),f1​=3−2x,f2​=2x,x˙=x(1−x)(3−4x).
(17)
Here x∗​=3/4 attracts every initial composition with both types present: x˙>0 below it and x˙<0 above it. The interior derivative is F′(3/4)=−3/4, while F′(0)=3 and F′(1)=1 make both endpoints unstable to introduction of the other type. The endpoints nevertheless remain stationary if the other type is exactly absent; the attraction statement excludes those boundary initial states.
The sole symmetric Nash equilibrium in this coexistence model is p=(3/4,1/4). It is also an evolutionarily stable strategy. For any different mixed strategy q=(y,1−y), the first comparison in the two-condition criterion for evolutionary stability ties because Ap=(3/2,3/2). Its second comparison is strictly positive:
pTAq−qTAq=(p−q)TAq=(3/4−y)(3−4y)=4(y−3/4)2>0.
(18)
Thus restoring frequency feedback can protect a mixture even though all present types earn equal payoffs at the mixture. Coexistence here means different individuals using different pure strategies; it does not require every individual to randomize with a mixed strategy.
Frequency evolution need not increase the mean payoff. In the coexistence example fˉ​=5x−4x2, whose maximum occurs at x=5/8, whereas the attracting composition is 3/4. For 5/8<x<3/4, the frequency x increases while dtd​fˉ​=(5−8x)x˙<0. Individual payoff advantages drive the equation; maximizing collective mean payoff is a different optimization problem.
More generally, a rest point only equalizes payoffs among present types: xi​>0 implies (Ax)i​=xTAx. A symmetric Nash equilibrium additionally requires (Ax)i​≤xTAx for every absent type. All vertices are rest points even when an absent type could invade. For finite symmetric bimatrix games, a rest point with Lyapunov stability on the full probability simplex is a symmetric Nash equilibrium; an interior trajectory that converges has a symmetric Nash equilibrium as its limit. These are conditional results, not a convergence theorem for every trajectory. See Cressman and Tao (2014), Theorem 1.
The rock paper scissors payoff matrix gives an explicit obstruction to universal convergence:
A=​01−1​−101​1−10​​.
(19)
Since AT=−A, xTAx=0, and in the interior dtd​log(x1​x2​x3​)=∑i​(Ax)i​=0. Thus x1​x2​x3​ is conserved. Its maximum on the probability simplex occurs only at (1/3,1/3,1/3), the unique symmetric Nash equilibrium, so an interior trajectory starting elsewhere cannot converge to that composition. Every other interior product level is a closed curve without a rest point, giving a periodic orbit. The existence of these cycles is also discussed in Cressman and Tao (2014), Example 1. The useful lesson is to inspect the specified payoff feedback and dynamical system, rather than infer convergence from the existence of an evolutionarily stable strategy or a Nash equilibrium.
