Primary visual cortex (V1)

 Home Biology Neuroscience
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 11
The primary visual cortex, often abbreviated V1, is an early cortical stage of visual processing. Its neurons have receptive fields: changing a stimulus in a particular part of the visible scene can change their spike trains. Orientation selectivity adds a further distinction: at the same location, a properly aligned bar can evoke a strong response while a rotated bar evokes little. Simple cells make the dependence on local position and visual contrast especially clear. See the original experiments under orientation selectivity and simple cell.
In their 1962 study, David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel recorded from forty cats under thiopental anesthesia, maintained light anesthesia while monitoring cortical electrical activity, and used succinylcholine to stabilize the eyes. White-light patterns were projected onto a screen 1.5m away. Extracellular single-unit recording with tungsten electrodes sampled individual units; receptive fields were mapped separately for each eye. This connected controlled stimulus geometry to individual spike trains. [ref]
These acute, anesthetized-cat recordings do not directly measure human perception or responses during awake behavior. The useful idea is a local description of structure: a population can distinguish where a feature lies, which way it is aligned, and which side is brighter. The signed spatial filter model of a simple cell turns that idea into a computable example. One neuron's preferred stimulus does not establish recognition of an entire object. Likewise, orientation selectivity in one neuron and an orderly map of preferences across primary visual cortex are different findings: rodents can have strong orientation selectivity without the orderly arrangement typical of some other species. [ref] The existing correlation-based development of visual cortical maps and orientation-map self-organization sections describe hypotheses about how organization develops.
