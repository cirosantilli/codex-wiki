Electric field line (dr/ds∥E)

Words: 85
Electric field OurBigBook.com
An oriented curve whose tangent points along the local electric field. Where the field is nonzero, choosing arc length s gives dr/ds=E/∣E∣. These curves visualize the direction of the force on a positive test charge; they are not generally particle trajectories, since a particle can have inertia and initial velocity. In electrostatics, they are normal to equipotential surfaces and cannot form a closed loop in a region with a single-valued potential that strictly decreases along the line. A field null can separate families of lines.

Electric flux (ΦE​=∫S​E⋅ndS)

Words: 608
Electric field OurBigBook.com
Electric flux measures how much electric field crosses a chosen oriented surface, with a sign distinguishing the two crossing directions. A strong electric field running along a surface contributes nothing to its electric flux: only the normal component counts. This lets Gauss's law turn a potentially complicated electric field into one number recording the net enclosed electric charge.
For a surface S with chosen unit normal n, the electric flux is the surface integral
ΦE​(S)=∫S​E⋅ndA=∫S​E⋅dS,dS=ndA.
(1)
Here dA is positive surface area, while dS is the vector area element. The dot product makes electric flux a signed scalar, rather than a vector like the electric field. Its SI unit is Nm2/C: electric field measured using the newton per coulomb, multiplied by surface area in square metres. Reversing the orientation of a surface replaces n by −n and reverses the electric flux. Calling it electric flux does not imply that electric charge is moving through the surface. [ref].
For an open patch in a plane, with surface area A and a constant electric field, the unit normal is constant and the surface integral simplifies to
ΦE​=E⋅(An)=EAcosθ,E=∣E∣.
(2)
The angle θ is between the electric field and the chosen unit normal, not between the electric field and the plane. The cosine describes the signed projected surface area seen by the electric field. Thus electric flux is positive for 0≤θ<90∘, zero at 90∘, and negative for 90∘<θ≤180∘. [ref].
For example, take E=100N/C and A=0.02m2, so EA=2Nm2/C. Three choices of angle give
ΦE​(0∘)ΦE​(60∘)ΦE​(120∘)​=2cos0∘=2Nm2/C,=2cos60∘=1Nm2/C,=2cos120∘=−1Nm2/C.​
(3)
Keeping the 60∘ patch fixed and reversing its unit normal gives 120∘ and changes +1 to −1Nm2/C. The electric field and physical patch have not changed. If the electric field is instead tangent to the patch, its dot product with the unit normal vanishes: electric flux is zero even though E=100N/C.
For a closed surface enclosing a volume V, choose the outward unit normal. Gauss's law gives
ΦE​(∂V)=∮∂V​E⋅ndA=ε0​Qenclosed​​.
(4)
Here ε0​ is the vacuum permittivity, and Qenclosed​ is the signed total electric charge inside. The electric field in the surface integral includes all sources, including external electric charges. Assume no ideal point charge lies on the integration surface. Zero enclosed electric charge implies zero total electric flux; it does not imply zero electric field everywhere on the closed surface or inside it. Positive and negative contributions to the surface integral can cancel. [ref].
To see the cancellation with sources inside, place point charges +q at (−a,0,0) and −q at (a,0,0), where q>0, a=0.30m, and both lie inside the sphere of radius R=1m centered at the origin. This separated pair has the electric dipole geometry. Its enclosed electric charge is q−q=0, so its total outward electric flux is zero. At the point P=(0,0,R) on the sphere, Coulomb's law and the superposition principle give the individual electric fields
E+​(P)=(R2+a2)3/2kq(a,0,R)​,E−​(P)=(R2+a2)3/2kq(a,0,−R)​,k=4πε0​1​.
(5)
The z components cancel and the positive x components add:
E(P)=(R2+a2)3/22kqa​x≈0.527244R2kq​x=0.
(6)
For the stipulated approximation k=8.99×109Nm2/C2 and q=1nC, this is about 4.740N/C along positive x. At P, the outward unit normal is z, so this nonzero electric field is tangent to the sphere. At other points of the sphere, its normal component can be positive or negative. The source-field formula applies away from the two point charges, where the electric field has zero divergence; it is singular at the sources. [ref].
The diagram shows streamlines of the three-dimensional electric field in the section y=0. The circle is a cross-section of the sphere, not the integration surface: Gauss's law uses the entire three-dimensional surface integral. A two-dimensional line integral of outward field around that circle would be a different quantity, with units Nm/C rather than the electric flux units Nm2/C.

Electric potential (Φ)

Words: 4k Articles: 28
Electric field OurBigBook.com
In electrostatics, the electric potential Φ tells us the interaction potential energy per unit test electric charge in the electric field of prescribed sources. Its most directly useful quantity is an electric potential difference: how much work per unit electric charge an external agent must supply to move that test electric charge slowly between two points. This replaces the bookkeeping of a vector force along a path with the difference of two scalar values. The test electric charge must be small enough not to rearrange the sources appreciably.
For motion from A to B, write ΔΦ=Φ(B)−Φ(A). With fixed sources and only the electric force doing work,
ΔΦ=−∫AB​E⋅dl,ΔU=qΔΦ,Wfield​=−qΔΦ,ΔK=−ΔU.
(7)
Here U is interaction potential energy, K is kinetic energy, and the line integral uses the electric field produced by the other electric charges. For slow transport with unchanged kinetic energy, the external work is Wext​=ΔU, so ΔΦ=Wext​/q for nonzero q. This external work has the opposite sign to the work done by the electric field. The SI unit is the volt,
1V=1J/C.
(8)
A volt measures energy per unit electric charge, not energy itself. For example, crossing an electric potential difference of +5V changes the potential energy of q=+2nC by +10nJ, but changes that of q=−2nC by −10nJ. Both test electric charges encounter the same electric potential difference; their opposite signs reverse the energy change.[ref]
The electrostatic potential is a scalar function of position. The electric field is a conservative vector field, and all three components follow from the negative gradient of the electric potential:
E=−∇Φ,Ex​=−∂x∂Φ​,Ey​=−∂y∂Φ​,Ez​=−∂z∂Φ​.
(9)
The electric field points toward decreasing electric potential. A positive test electric charge feels electric force in that direction; a negative test electric charge feels electric force in the opposite direction, since F=qE. A freely accelerated negative test electric charge therefore moves toward higher electric potential while lowering its potential energy U=qΦ.[ref]
Only differences fix physical electrostatic potentials: adding the same constant C0​ everywhere, Φ′=Φ+C0​, changes neither the gradient nor any electric potential difference. Choosing an electrical reference potential is this elementary gauge transformation. Setting zero at infinity is convenient for finitely many localized point charges; it is not available for an ideal uniform electric field extending to infinity. There we can choose a finite reference point. Assigning zero at ground is a convention, whereas physically grounding an electrical conductor also allows exchange of electric charge to impose a boundary value. Zero electric potential at a point never by itself implies zero electric field.[ref]
For a fixed point charge Q at the origin in vacuum, choose zero at infinity. Coulomb's law gives
Φ(r)=rkQ​,E(r)=r2kQ​r,k=4πε0​1​,r>0,
(10)
where ε0​ is the vacuum permittivity. Use the stipulated approximation k=8.99×109Nm2/C2 and Q=+2nC. At r=0.20m,
Φ=89.9V,Er​=449.5N/C.
(11)
A test electric charge q=−3nC has U=−2.697×10−7J and radial electric force Fr​=−1.3485×10−6N, directed inward. Moving it inward from rA​=0.20m to rB​=0.10m raises the electric potential from 89.9 to 179.8V but lowers its potential energy:
ΔΦ=+89.9V,ΔU=−2.697×10−7J,Wfield​=+2.697×10−7J.
(12)
This is the Coulomb potential energy calculation using electric potentials. The source's singular self-field at r=0 is excluded; when finding the force on a source point charge, use only the electric field of the other sources.[ref]
For a uniform electric field E=300V/mx, choose Φ(0)=12V. Then
Φ(x)=12V−(300V/m)x,ΔΦ=−E⋅(rB​−rA​).
(13)
Moving from xA​=0 to xB​=0.04m gives ΔΦ=−12V. For q=+2nC the change in potential energy is −24nJ and the electric field does +24nJ of work. For q=−2nC, the same prescribed displacement gives ΔU=+24nJ: the external agent must supply that work for slow transport. This uniform electric field approximates the interior of a parallel-plate capacitor sufficiently far from the edges; the fringing field of a capacitor limits the approximation.[ref]
An equipotential is a level set Φ=constant. On a smooth equipotential with nonzero gradient, every tangent displacement has dΦ=∇Φ⋅dl=0, so the electric field is normal to the equipotential. Thus moving a test electric charge along an equipotential requires no net electrical work. Around a point charge, the equipotentials are spheres; the circles in the diagram are their sections, not an assumption of two-dimensional Coulomb's law. A uniform electric field has parallel plane equipotentials. For contours separated by the same small electric potential difference, smaller normal separation means a stronger electric field, approximately ∣E∣≈∣ΔΦ∣/Δn. At a point where ∇Φ=0, the electric field is zero and has no direction to call normal.[ref]
The superposition principle adds electric potentials as signed scalars. For finitely many stationary point charges qj​ at rj​ in vacuum,
Φ(r)=k∑j​∣r−rj​∣qj​​,E(r)=k∑j​qj​∣r−rj​∣3r−rj​​.
(14)
For example, the electric dipole with +q at (a,0,0) and −q at (−a,0,0) has zero electric potential everywhere in the midplane x=0, yet its electric field at the midpoint is E=−(2kq/a2)x. With q=2nC and a=0.10m, this is −3596N/Cx. At (2a,0,0), the same model gives Φ=2kq/(3a)≈119.867V and Ex​=8kq/(9a2)≈1598.222N/C. Cancellation of electric potentials is quite different from cancellation of vector electric fields.
A motivating measurement is conductive-paper electric potential mapping. A documented teaching experiment uses slightly conductive paper on an insulating board, two circular electrodes in highly conducting ink, a steady supply, and a digital voltmeter. Connect the supply to the two electrodes, keep the voltmeter common lead on the negative electrode, and use its other lead as a movable probe. Adjust the supply using the measured electric potential difference between electrodes; the published procedure uses 10V. The observable is the voltmeter reading Φ(P)−Φ(COM) at the probe position P. Locate several points reading 2V, record their positions on a separate map, and join them into an equipotential; repeat at other readings. Lift the probe between contacts so it does not damage the paper. This procedure makes the electrical reference potential concrete: every reading compares two contacts.[ref]
From neighboring readings infer an electric field component by a finite difference, Ex​≈−[Φ(x+h)−Φ(x)]/h. Reconstruct two in-plane components with displacements along two independent directions. Alternatively, draw the electric field normal to mapped equipotentials, toward lower electric potential. The second diagram illustrates the same measurement principle with bar electrodes spanning a uniform sheet, insulating top and bottom edges, and a synthetic model Φ(x)=(50V/m)x. The model readings 4.0V at x=0.08m and 5.0V at x=0.10m imply Ex​=−50V/m. These values are calculated illustrations, not reported experimental data. The documented circular-electrode experiment similarly estimates the electric field from readings at successive centimetre marks.[ref]
Useful controls include checking the voltmeter zero, checking that the electric potential difference along each painted electrode is negligible, and repeating reference readings to detect supply drift. Reversing the supply should reverse the signs relative to the same reference contact. Contact resistance, leakage electric currents, and touching the sheet can distort the map. A voltmeter has finite input electrical resistance and draws a small electric current; voltmeter loading changes the quantity being measured if that electrical resistance is insufficiently large relative to the sheet's effective source electrical resistance.[ref][ref]
The paper carries a steady electric current, so this is a planar analogue rather than a direct measurement of isolated point charges in three-dimensional vacuum. For constant electrical conductivity, Ohm's law and steady charge conservation give j=−σ∇Φ and ∇⋅j=0, hence the two-dimensional Laplace equation away from electrodes. Insulating sheet edges impose zero normal electric current, while finite electrodes fix boundary values. This derivation explains both the analogy and its limits: inhomogeneous paper, finite boundaries, and finite electrodes can change the contours. One should not fit the three-dimensional point charge formula kQ/r to a planar map without testing that model. Small probe spacing resolves spatial variation better, but amplifies uncertainty in the inferred finite difference. The published laboratory instructions explicitly ask students to assess whether their proposed field fit is appropriate.[ref][ref]
The scalar description has a crucial limit. Faraday's law of induction for a stationary closed path Γ says
∮Γ​E⋅dl=−dtdΦB​​,∇×E=−∂t∂B​,
(15)
where ΦB​ is magnetic flux. Nonzero changing magnetic flux gives nonzero circulation, incompatible with a single-valued electric potential alone. More generally, a scalar electric potential and a magnetic vector potential together give
E=−∇Φ−∂t∂A​,B=∇×A.
(16)
Consequently, in the presence of induction, two voltmeter lead routes between the same contacts can produce different readings if the enclosed magnetic flux changes. This follows by subtracting the two path line integrals; it is a deduction from Faraday's law of induction, not a claimed outcome of the paper experiment. The steady setup and negligible induction justify interpreting its readings as electric potential differences. Merely introducing a scalar electric potential that depends on time cannot supply a nonzero curl of the electric field.[ref]
A short mathematical justification connects the statements above. Along a smooth path, the chain rule gives dΦ=∇Φ⋅dl. Integrating and using E=−∇Φ yields the endpoint formula by the fundamental theorem for line integrals, proving path independence. Conversely, vanishing circulation around every closed path lets us define Φ(P)=−∫P0​P​E⋅dl independently of the route, with Φ(P0​)=0. Zero curl is a local test; for a continuously differentiable curl-free vector field, a simply connected open region suffices for a global potential. The explicit finite-source formula already gives a global electric potential away from its source points.[ref]

Voltmeter loading

Voltmeter loading is the change in an electric potential difference caused by the electric current drawn by the measuring voltmeter.
For a loaded voltage divider in steady direct current, a voltmeter connected across the lower resistor places its finite input electrical resistance Rm​ in parallel with the lower arm's electrical resistance R2​ and any intended electrical load of electrical resistance RL​. With positive, fixed electrical resistances, an upper resistor of electrical resistance R1​, and an ideal voltage source supplying electric potential difference Vin​, Kirchhoff's current law and Ohm's law give
R1​Vin​−Vm​​=R2​Vm​​+RL​Vm​​+Rm​Vm​​,Rp​=(R2​1​+RL​1​+Rm​1​)−1,Vm​=Vin​R1​+Rp​Rp​​.
(18)
Here Vm​ is the connected circuit's electric potential difference across the voltmeter; omit 1/RL​ if there is no intended electrical load. The parallel connection and need for large input electrical resistance are standard voltmeter operating principles.[ref]
Voltmeter loading is a bias relative to the target electrical circuit with the voltmeter absent and any intended electrical load still present. Voltage divider loading error instead compares the attached-load output with the wholly unloaded output. An accurate readout of Vm​, supported by measurement calibration, can still differ from either target: calibration accuracy concerns the indication of the connected electric potential difference, whereas voltmeter loading changes that electric potential difference itself.
