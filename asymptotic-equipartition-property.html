Asymptotic equipartition property (−n−1log2​P(Xn)→H(X1​))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Information theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 11
The asymptotic equipartition property explains why a long message from a source can usually be described using its information entropy per symbol. Individual messages can have very different probabilities, but the messages carrying almost all the total probability have similar logarithmic costs per symbol. The Bernoulli typical-set example makes the distinction between an individual message and a large collection explicit.
For independent and identically distributed random variables X1​,X2​,… on a finite alphabet, let p be their probability mass function and H=E[−log2​p(X1​)]. Then
−n1​log2​P(X1​,…,Xn​)⟶Halmost surely.​
(170)
Here P(X1​,…,Xn​) means the probability mass of the realized word, evaluated under the source law. The product rule for independent random variables turns its information content into a sum:
−n1​log2​P(X1​,…,Xn​)=n1​∑j=1n​[−log2​p(Xj​)].
(171)
The strong law of large numbers gives the limit. Zero-probability symbols do not occur; on the positive-probability support of a finite alphabet, these information costs are finite. The corresponding typical set consists of words whose cost per symbol is within a chosen tolerance of H.
For a strictly stationary process with a finite alphabet, the constant-limit extension requires ergodicity. The Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem then replaces the one-symbol information entropy with the information rate. Stationarity without ergodicity in an information source shows why stationarity alone is insufficient.[ref]
