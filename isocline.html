Isocline (f(x,y)=k)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Differential equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 312
For the first-order ordinary differential equation y′=f(x,y) defined on an open subset D of the plane, an isocline of slope k is the level set
{(x,y)∈D:f(x,y)=k}.
(1)
Here k is a constant. At every point of this isocline, the direction field prescribes a tangent vector proportional to (1,k) for a solution of a differential equation. It does not generally prescribe the tangent line of the isocline itself. The level set can have several components or fail to be a smooth curve, and points outside D are excluded.
For example, take y′=x−y on the whole plane. Its isoclines are the parallel straight lines
x−y=k,y=x−k.
(2)
Each line has slope 1, while the direction field along it has slope k. Thus the isocline y=x carries horizontal segments even though the line itself rises. A function graph of a solution of a differential equation follows the segments, rather than following this line. Only the isocline with k=1, namely y=x−1, is also a solution of a differential equation.[ref]
The first-order linear differential equation has the exact family
y(x)=x−1+Ae−x,y′(x)=1−Ae−x=x−y(x).
(3)
The initial condition y(x0​)=y0​ selects A=(y0​−x0​+1)ex0​ and hence a unique solution of a differential equation for every real x. The picture shows y(0)=−1,0,1,2. For A=0 the solution of a differential equation stays on the k=1 isocline; for other A it moves through different isoclines. These formulas check the pictured trajectories by direct differentiation, independently of how the direction field was sampled.
The zero-slope isocline f(x,y)=0 is called a nullcline. Below the nullcline y=x in this example, y′>0; above it, y′<0. For A>0, the solution of a differential equation meets it at x=logA, where y′=0 and y′′=1, a strict local minimum. The nullcline is therefore a useful guide to turning points, although it is not itself a solution of a differential equation. In the equivalent planar autonomous differential equation x˙=1, y˙​=x−y, it is the nullcline for the second component; there is no equilibrium point, because the first component never vanishes.
This makes isoclines useful even when no explicit solution of a differential equation is available: organize a direction field by common slopes, read where function graphs rise or fall, and identify possible turning points on nullclines.
