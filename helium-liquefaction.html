Helium liquefaction

 ... Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Temperature Cryogenics
OurBigBook.com Words: 824 Articles: 3
Helium liquefaction converts helium gas into a collectable liquid. It creates a bath for investigating material properties at very low temperatures, including superconductivity. The central question is how the incoming specific enthalpy compares with the specific enthalpies of the outgoing liquid and vapor. The liquefaction enthalpy budget makes that comparison explicit; a temperature decrease by itself does not specify a liquid yield.
In a Joule-Thomson effect cooling stage, a throttling pressure drop approximately preserves enthalpy under the assumptions of the steady-flow energy balance. For an ideal gas, specific enthalpy depends only on temperature: with positive specific heat capacity at constant pressure, equal specific enthalpy means equal temperature. Thus its Joule-Thomson coefficient is zero. An ideal gas also supplies no liquid branch. Cooling by throttling depends on real gas properties; expansion is not an automatic source of refrigeration.
For a local drop dp<0, dT=μJT​dp cools only where the Joule-Thomson coefficient is positive. Precooling must bring the real gas into a useful cooling region, with enough cooling to overcome unwanted heat transfer. A finite expansion can cross a Joule-Thomson inversion curve; its final state requires the full enthalpy balance, including phase coexistence if liquid forms. Kamerlingh Onnes's 1908 helium liquefaction experiment combined external precooling with regeneration to reach that regime. The Joule-Thomson effect article develops the throttling identities and their assumptions.
