Wavelet polynomial annihilation (cψ​(p;a,b)=0(degp<q))

For a>0 and b∈R, define the shifted, scaled analyzing function and its coefficient by
ψa,b​(x)=a−1/2ψ(ax−b​),cψ​(f;a,b)=∫R​f(x)ψa,b​(x)​dx=a​∫R​f(b+au)ψ(u)​du.
(32)
Assume the product is integrable; for example this holds for a locally integrable function f and a bounded analyzing function with compact support. The bar is complex conjugation. This normalization preserves the L2 norm of the analyzing function. If p is a polynomial of degree of a polynomial less than q, then p(b+au) is a sum of monomials ur with r<q. Linearity of integration and the vanishing moments give cψ​(p;a,b)=0 at every position and scale. Nonzero polynomials on the whole real line are not square-integrable, so this cancellation is an integral pairing; it is not a claim that these polynomials are vectors of L2(R).
Here is a quantitative reason that smooth regions yield small details. Let ψ have compact support and q vanishing moments, and suppose f is q times continuously differentiable on an interval containing b and b+asuppψ. Subtract its degree-(q−1) Taylor polynomial at b. If ∣f(q)∣≤M on that interval, the Taylor remainder bound gives
∣cψ​(f;a,b)∣≤q!M​aq+1/2∫R​∣u∣q∣ψ(u)∣du.
(33)
The factor aq comes from the Taylor remainder; a1/2 comes from the coefficient normalization. Thus the analyzing function measures the departure from the local polynomial trend, rather than the height of f itself. The wavelet coefficient decay for Hölder functions uses the same argument with a Hölder-Taylor remainder bound. These estimates concern regularity of f on the analyzing function's entire support of a function; extra vanishing moments alone do not make ψ smooth or establish an orthonormal wavelet construction.[ref]
