Wald's equation (E[SN​]=E[X1​]E[N])

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 905 Articles: 4
Wald's equation turns a random sum into a product of two expected values: the average contribution per step and the average number of steps. Its surprising feature is that the number of steps may depend on what has already happened. A procedure can stop on its first successful Bernoulli trial, for example, while the identity still accounts for its total cost. The crucial restriction is that the decision to take the next step cannot use that step's unobserved outcome.
Let X1​,X2​,… be real independent and identically distributed random variables with E∣X1​∣<∞, let μ=E[X1​], and put S0​=0 and Sn​=∑i=1n​Xi​. Suppose N is a nonnegative integer-valued stopping time for the natural filtration Fn​=σ(X1​,…,Xn​), with E[N]<∞. Then SN​ is an integrable random variable and
E[SN​]=μE[N].​
(54)
These hypotheses allow statistical dependence between N and the increments; they do not allow access to future increments. The finite-expected value requirement is stronger than stopping almost surely.[ref]
For the proof idea, count step i only on {N≥i}. This event is determined by the first i−1 observations, so its indicator function is independent of Xi​. The Proof of Wald's equation by stopping indicators combines this observation with the tail-sum formula for expectation. The same calculation with ∣Xi​∣ gives E∑i=1N​∣Xi​∣=E∣X1​∣E[N]<∞, which justifies interchanging the expected value and the infinite series. This is why the result survives a data-dependent end point without assuming an independent count.
The identity is useful in randomized algorithms and repeated sampling: one can translate an average amount of work into an average number of attempts, or conversely, without first finding the full probability distribution of the total.
