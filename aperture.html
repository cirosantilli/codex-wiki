Aperture

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 595 Articles: 3
An aperture is an opening that admits light into or through an optical instrument. Its shape and size select the transmitted light rays; an aperture alone does not bend them as an optical lens does. The aperture stop is the opening that most restricts the bundle from an on-axis object point. The entrance pupil is the image of that aperture stop formed by the preceding optical lens elements, viewed from the object side. Consequently the effective collecting diameter D is the entrance pupil diameter, which can differ from the physical hole's diameter. For example, preceding optical lens elements that enlarge a 10mm aperture stop by a factor of two give D=20mm; measuring the physical hole alone would misidentify the focal ratio. [ref].
For an unobscured circular entrance pupil under a uniform beam normal to its plane, the collecting area and admitted radiant flux are

where Epupil​ is incident irradiance. Doubling D quadruples admitted radiant flux. Obstructions, nonuniform illumination and losses after the entrance pupil require the actual illuminated area and transmission to be included. The same D also sets an angular diffraction-limited resolution scale λ/D at wavelength λ. Collection and diffraction therefore answer different questions from field of view: an aperture selects the bundle from each object point, while the receiving plane's extent helps select which object directions appear in the image.
NASA/JPL's published pinhole demonstration uses two white cards, foil taped over a window in one card, and a small hole pierced in the foil. Sunlight passes through the hole onto the second card; the projected image is viewed on that card. The activity describes a larger image when the card separation increases, improved visibility when the receiving card is shaded, and a separate image from each of several holes. These are qualitative observations of image formation, rather than calibrated measurements of radiant flux or diffraction. [ref].
A proposed quantitative extension replaces sunlight by a steadily illuminated asymmetric target and puts the foil and a translucent receiving screen in a dark box. Record target distance u, hole-to-screen distance b, hole diameter d, image height, orientation and edge blur. Measure screen brightness with a fixed photodetector, or record the screen with an optical camera whose integration time and gain stay fixed. Exchange centred foils of measured d at fixed u,b; then vary b with the same foil. Geometrical optics predicts an inverted image with transverse magnification m=−b/u and a point's geometric blur diameter d(1+b/u). Thus a larger hole admits more radiant flux without changing the central image scale, whereas increasing b enlarges the image. These are predictions to test, not reported data. Keep target illumination and detector position fixed, subtract a hole-covered dark reading, repeat the original foil to check drift, and avoid clipping the detector response. Foil thickness and ragged edges can block oblique light rays; at sufficiently small d, diffraction invalidates the claim that ever smaller holes always sharpen the image. A lensless optical camera has no optical lens focal length: b is a propagation distance, not that focal length.
