Kullback-Leibler divergence (DKL​(P∥Q))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics f-divergence
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 21
The Kullback-Leibler divergence, also called relative entropy, compares two probability distributions in a specified order. In DKL​(P∥Q), observations follow P and the comparison probability distribution is Q: the first argument supplies the averaging weights. For probability mass functions pi​,qi​ on a finite alphabet,
DKL​(P∥Q)=∑i​pi​logqi​pi​​=EP​[logq(X)p(X)​].
(43)
Here X is a random variable with probability distribution P. For probability density functions relative to the same measure μ, replace the sum by ∫plog(p/q)dμ. These formulas use natural logarithms unless stated otherwise. The expected value is under P, even though Q appears in the denominator. [ref]
Take P=(0.75,0.25) and Q=(0.5,0.5) on two outcomes. Direct calculation gives
DKL​(P∥Q)DKL​(Q∥P)​=0.75log(1.5)+0.25log(0.5)≈0.130812,=0.5log(2/3)+0.5log2≈0.143841.​
(44)
The second outcome contributes negatively to the first sum: assigning it more probability than P does makes its individual contribution negative. Nevertheless, relative entropy nonnegativity guarantees a nonnegative total. Reversing the arguments changes both the likelihood ratio and the averaging probabilities; taking an absolute value inside the logarithm would define a different quantity.
The units depend on the logarithm: natural logarithms give nats, whereas base-two logarithms give bits. Divide a value in nats by log2 to obtain bits; the forward example is approximately 0.188722 bits. Changing the base rescales every Kullback-Leibler divergence by the same positive factor. Since the two calculated directions differ, Kullback-Leibler divergence is asymmetric and cannot be a metric. It also need not satisfy the triangle inequality. [ref]
The operational meaning is an expected penalty for using the wrong probability distribution. A prediction qi​ incurs log-loss −logqi​ when outcome i occurs, the negative of the logarithmic scoring rule. Under P, its expected value is the cross-entropy H(P,Q)=−∑i​pi​logqi​. Predicting with P itself incurs information entropy H(P)=−∑i​pi​logpi​, so
H(P,Q)−H(P)=DKL​(P∥Q).
(45)
Thus Kullback-Leibler divergence is exactly excess expected log-loss, whenever these separate quantities are finite. [ref]
For lossless source coding, ideal codeword lengths −log2​qi​ similarly give excess expected length DKL​(P∥Q) in bits per symbol. Actual prefix codes require integer codeword lengths; Shannon's source coding theorem explains how long blocks approach ideal per-symbol lengths. The interpretation concerns an average, not a fractional physical bit attached to every outcome. In our example, ideal coding with Q costs one bit per symbol, while H(P)≈0.811278 bits; the difference is exactly the computed 0.188722 bits. [ref]
Zeros require explicit conventions. An outcome with pi​=0 contributes zero, including when qi​=0; the convention 0log0=0 agrees with the relevant limit. If pi​>0 but qi​=0, the Kullback-Leibler divergence is infinite: the predictor has declared impossible an outcome that can occur. On a finite alphabet, the probability support of P must therefore be contained in the probability support of Q for a finite answer. For example, P=(1,0) and Q=(0.5,0.5) give D(P∥Q)=log2, but D(Q∥P)=+∞. For general probability measures, failure of absolute continuity of measures P≪Q likewise gives infinity. Even P≪Q does not guarantee a finite integral. [ref]
To see how confidently wrong predictions are punished, hold the true Bernoulli distribution parameter at p=0.75 and vary the predicted parameter q. Its binary relative entropy is
d(0.75,q)=0.75logq0.75​+0.25log1−q0.25​.
(46)
It vanishes at q=0.75, equals approximately 2.894055 nats at q=0.01, and equals 0.596495 nats at q=0.99. Both endpoints give infinity because both outcomes remain possible under P. Predicting almost no successes is especially costly because successes actually occur three quarters of the time. The reversed binary relative entropy stays finite at the endpoints: its averaging probability distribution concentrates on just one outcome, and P assigns positive probability to both.
For model selection, minimizing expected log-loss ranks candidate statistical models by D(P∥Q), since H(P) is common to them. The true probability distribution is usually unknown, so one estimates predictive performance using fresh observations or cross-validation. Fitting and assessing on the same data can reward overfitting; a small fitted Kullback-Leibler divergence alone does not establish useful prediction.
In variational inference, a tractable trial probability distribution R commonly minimizes D(R∥Π) against a target posterior distribution Π. Maximizing the evidence lower bound accomplishes this without evaluating the target's normalization constant. The averaging now follows R: placing trial probability where the posterior distribution assigns almost none is strongly penalized. The chosen direction and trial family therefore matter. [ref]
These comparisons do not by themselves establish causal inference. Two statistical models can reproduce the same observed probability distribution while predicting different responses to an intervention. Choosing the smaller Kullback-Leibler divergence therefore needs additional assumptions or experimental evidence before supporting a causal claim. [ref]
The original reference is S. Kullback and R. A. Leibler's 1951 paper [ref], published in The Annals of Mathematical Statistics 22(1), pages 79–86. Its title connects this comparison with sufficient statistics: the historical setting was statistical information, before today's widespread applications in prediction and variational inference.
