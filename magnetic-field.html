Magnetic field (B)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism
OurBigBook.com Words: 5k Articles: 58
A magnetic field is a vector field B(r,t) whose local value determines the magnetic part of the Lorentz force on a body with electric charge q and velocity v. In SI units,
FB​=qv×B,∣FB​∣=∣q∣vBsinα,
(1)
where v is the speed, B=∣B∣, and α is the angle between the velocity and the magnetic field. The cross product makes a nonzero magnetic force perpendicular to both the velocity and the magnetic field. The right-hand rule gives its direction for positive electric charge; negative electric charge reverses it. [ref].
The SI unit of magnetic field is the tesla:
1T=(1C)(1ms−1)1N​.
(2)
For a stipulated example in SI units, take electric charge q=2C, velocity v=(3,0,0)ms−1, and magnetic field B=(0,0,4)T. The cross product gives magnetic force FB​=(0,−24,0)N. With the same speed directed along +z, the magnetic force is zero: motion parallel to a magnetic field produces no magnetic acceleration.
Magnetic field lines visualize the direction of a magnetic field by being tangent to it at each point. They are not generally the path of a body with electric charge: in a uniform magnetic field along +z, the magnetic field lines are straight while transverse velocity produces circular motion, and adding parallel velocity produces helical motion in a uniform magnetic field. The worked Lorentz force example below illustrates this distinction.
A magnetic field need not be static. Magnetostatics is the restricted case with no dependence on time; an electromagnetic wave has changing electric field and magnetic field even in vacuum. A changing magnetic field is coupled to the electric field through Faraday's law of induction. [ref]. The useful separation is between local bending, described by the Lorentz force, and the amount of magnetic field crossing a surface, described by magnetic flux.

Magnetic field strength (H)

Words: 67
Magnetic field OurBigBook.com
In macroscopic electromagnetism, magnetic field strength is H=B/μ0​−M, where B is magnetic flux density and M is magnetization. In SI units H is measured in Am−1, while B is measured in tesla units. In a linear isotropic medium B=μH, where μ is magnetic permeability. The macroscopic Ampère-Maxwell equation writes its curl in terms of free current and displacement current. The electromagnetic Poynting vector is E×H in this convention.

Solenoid (B=μ0​nI)

Words: 2k Articles: 8
Magnetic field OurBigBook.com
A solenoid is a coil of electrical conductor, usually wire wound into a helix around a circular cylinder. The same electric current I flows through its successive turns. Each turn contributes a magnetic field; the superposition principle makes their axial contributions reinforce inside the coil. Curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the electric current around the turns: the thumb gives the interior magnetic field direction by the right-hand rule. Throughout this branch, N is the total number of turns, ℓ is the coil length, and n=N/ℓ is the solenoid turn density. [ref].
The attraction of a long solenoid is a controllable, nearly uniform magnetic field over an interior working region: change the electric current to tune its strength, or reverse the electric current to reverse its direction. This makes it a useful setting for studying the Lorentz force, measuring magnetic flux, and constructing an inductor. Its geometry turns the many contributions from individual turns into the simple long-solenoid approximation. A real finite coil has end fields and leakage; its exterior magnetic field is not zero. The on-axis magnetic field of a finite solenoid below quantifies that difference.
Historically, the coil helped connect magnetic fields to electric currents. In his memoir on the mathematical theory of electrodynamic phenomena, Ampère modeled the interactions of magnets using the ends of solenoids in place of microscopic magnetic constituents. This was a route toward explaining magnetism by electric currents, rather than a modern microscopic account of magnetization. [ref]. The historical setting is documented by the [ref].

Solenoid turn density (n=N/ℓ)

Words: 135
Solenoid OurBigBook.com
The solenoid turn density n counts turns per unit axial length in a uniformly wound solenoid. The total turn count N is dimensionless, whereas n has units m−1. A 1000-turn coil of length 0.50m has n=2000m−1; an axial interval of length s=0.010m then contains ns=20 turns. The effective circumferential surface current density in the closely spaced circular-turn model is K=nI, with units A/m. [ref].
For a single-layer helix, the axial advance per turn is approximately 1/n. The circular-turn model neglects this advance and the associated axial component of electric current, as well as the detailed magnetic field close to individual wires. The number of turns around the whole coil is always N=nℓ here; it must not be substituted for n in B=μ0​nI.

Long-solenoid approximation (Bin​=μ0​nIz,Bout​=0)

Words: 369
Solenoid OurBigBook.com
In magnetostatics, an ideal infinitely long solenoid is a cylindrical sheet of circumferential surface current density K=nI. It has negligible winding advance, no magnetic core, and no imposed external magnetic field. Choose positive electric current to produce magnetic field along +z. In this ideal model the interior magnetic field is uniform and axial, B=μ0​nI, and the exterior magnetic field is zero; μ0​ is the vacuum permeability. For a finite, closely wound air-core coil, the same result is an approximation well inside the coil, several radii from either end. Air is treated as having the magnetic permeability of vacuum. [ref].
For a short proof, the translational and rotational symmetries of the ideal cylindrical current sheet, together with the Biot-Savart law, give an axial magnetic field. Ampère's law on rectangles wholly outside makes that axial exterior value independent of radius; the condition of no imposed magnetic field makes it zero at infinity and hence everywhere outside. Rectangles wholly inside similarly show that the interior value is independent of radius. Now choose an Amperian loop with axial length s, one long side inside and the other outside. The transverse sides are perpendicular to the magnetic field, so their contributions to the line integral vanish. The loop crosses ns turns of the winding, giving
∮Γ​B⋅dl=Bs=μ0​(ns)I,B=μ0​nI.
(6)
Here s is the integration-path length, not the whole coil length ℓ. An Amperian loop surrounding both sides of the winding encloses equal and opposite electric currents; zero net circulation alone would not prove zero magnetic field at every exterior point.
For the 1000-turn, 0.50m coil with electric current I=0.50A, the long-solenoid approximation gives
B≈(1.256637×10−6)(2000)(0.50)T=1.256637×10−3T≈1.257mT.
(7)
This illustrative calculation uses vacuum permeability μ0​≈1.256637×10−6H/m, a rounded value, not an exact constant. Since the 2019 redefinition of SI, vacuum permeability is determined experimentally.[ref] Doubling I doubles the ideal magnetic field; doubling N at fixed ℓ does the same. Increasing ℓ while keeping N fixed decreases n and hence the ideal magnetic field.

On-axis magnetic field of a finite solenoid (Bz​(z))

Words: 375
Solenoid OurBigBook.com
Model a finite solenoid as a continuum of coaxial turns shaped as circles of radius a>0, uniformly distributed over length ℓ with solenoid turn density n=N/ℓ. Put its centre at z=0, so its ends are z=±ℓ/2. With the same electric current orientation as in the long-solenoid approximation, its axial magnetic field is
Bz​(z)=2μ0​nI​[a2+(z+ℓ/2)2​z+ℓ/2​−a2+(z−ℓ/2)2​z−ℓ/2​].
(8)
This formula applies only on the axis, where symmetry cancels the transverse magnetic field. It does not describe points off the axis. It is exact for the continuum circular-turn model, which neglects winding thickness, discrete wire spacing, and helical advance. [ref].
The derivation is an especially direct use of the superposition principle. The on-axis magnetic field of a circular current loop at source position z′ is μ0​Ia2/[2(a2+(z−z′)2)3/2]. There are ndz′ turns in an axial slice, so
Bz​(z)=2μ0​nIa2​∫−ℓ/2ℓ/2​[a2+(z−z′)2]3/2dz′​.
(9)
Substitute u=z−z′ and use the antiderivative u/[a2a2+u2​] to obtain the displayed expression. If the centre is instead at z=z0​, replace every z in that expression by z−z0​.
At the centre and at either end, respectively,
μ0​nIBz​(0)​=ℓ2+4a2​ℓ​,μ0​nIBz​(ℓ/2)​=2ℓ2+a2​ℓ​.
(10)
For aspect ratios ℓ/a=2,6,20, the centre ratios are approximately 0.7071,0.9487,0.9950. The end ratio tends to 1/2 as ℓ/a becomes large, even though the centre ratio tends to 1. Thus a long coil can have an excellent central magnetic field while the ends still differ strongly from that value. Convergence to the long-solenoid approximation is not uniform across the end region.
For a concrete geometry, give the earlier coil radius a=0.025m, so ℓ/a=20. Its central magnetic field is approximately 1.2504mT, its end value is 0.6275mT, and at z=ℓ its axial magnetic field is still 0.00277mT. The last point is outside the coil: finite length does not permit the zero-exterior conclusion of the infinite model. Off the axis, the magnetic field lines bend around to form the return field. A central axial value alone also does not determine the total magnetic flux through every turn of a short coil.

Solenoid inductance (L≈μ0​N2A/ℓ)

Words: 396 Articles: 1
Solenoid OurBigBook.com
For a long vacuum-core or air-core solenoid with fixed geometry and linear magnetic response, neglecting end effects gives self-inductance
L≈ℓμ0​N2A​=μ0​n2Aℓ,A=πa2.
(11)
Here N is the total number of turns, n=N/ℓ is the solenoid turn density, and A is the interior cross-sectional area. The reason for the square of N is physical: more turns produce more magnetic field, and more turns link the resulting magnetic flux. Approximately equal magnetic flux Φ≈BA through each turn gives magnetic flux linkage Λ≈NΦ≈μ0​N2AI/ℓ; division by the electric current yields L. [ref].
For the same N=1000, ℓ=0.50m, and a=0.025m, the area is A=π(0.025)2m2≈1.9635×10−3m2. The long-coil estimate is
L≈0.50(1.256637×10−6)(1000)2π(0.025)2​H≈4.9348mH.
(12)
At I=0.50A, each turn links approximately 2.4674×10−6Wb and the total magnetic flux linkage is approximately 2.4674×10−3Wb. Their ratio to I is the stated self-inductance. A real finite coil need not give precisely these values: the axial centre correction above is not by itself a correction formula for inductance, which depends on magnetic flux across all the turns.
At fixed A and ℓ, doubling N quadruples L. At fixed solenoid turn density n and area A, doubling ℓ doubles both N and L. These are different changes to the geometry, so there is no contradiction between L∝1/ℓ at fixed N and L∝ℓ at fixed n. A magnetic core requires its own magnetic permeability model; nonlinear magnetization can make a constant-L description inadequate.

Stored energy of a solenoid (U=21​LI2)

Words: 165
Solenoid inductance OurBigBook.com
The magnetic energy stored in a linear solenoid of constant self-inductance L is
U=21​LI2,U≈2μ0​B2​Aℓ
(13)
in the long-solenoid approximation. The first formula is an inductor relation; the second identifies its storage with magnetic energy in the interior magnetic field, under the vacuum-core approximation. This agreement joins the electrical circuit description to the spatial magnetic field description. The expression B2/(2μ0​) is the vacuum magnetic energy density. [ref].
A short derivation starts from the terminal electric potential difference vL​=LdI/dt, with positive electric current entering the terminal at higher potential. The absorbed power is vL​I, and integrating from zero electric current gives
U=∫0I​Lidi=21​LI2.
(14)
The result describes recoverable storage in the inductor; Joule heating in the wire is a separate expenditure of energy. [ref].
For the long-coil example, L≈4.9348mH and I=0.50A give U≈6.1685×10−4J=0.61685mJ. Substituting the ideal B≈1.256637mT and volume Aℓ≈9.8175×10−4m3 into B2Aℓ/(2μ0​) gives the same value. Doubling electric current quadruples stored magnetic energy. In a real coil, reducing electric current can return this energy to an electrical circuit, or release it as Joule heating in a closed RL circuit.

Meissner effect

Words: 2k Articles: 9

The Meissner effect is the equilibrium expulsion of a sufficiently weak, static magnetic field from the bulk of a material exhibiting superconductivity. Expulsion includes a magnetic field that was already present before cooling: zero electrical resistance alone does not predict this. The Field-cooled Meissner experiment tests that distinction. The assumptions matter: Finite-thickness Meissner screening can leave an appreciable magnetic field throughout a small sample, and the Limits of Meissner screening distinguish quantum vortex penetration and retained magnetic flux from this equilibrium state.

Meissner effect discovery

Words: 407
Meissner effect OurBigBook.com
The 1933 report Ein neuer Effekt bei Eintritt der Supraleitfähigkeit by W. Meissner and R. Ochsenfeld describes cooling tin and lead cylinders in a fixed transverse magnetic field. Two parallel cylinders were about 140mm long and 3mm thick, separated by 1.5mm. A roughly 10mm rotating search coil between them drove a ballistic galvanometer. At about 5G, the reported below/above-transition induction ratios were 1.70 for tin single crystals and 1.77 for polycrystalline lead. The increased gap magnetic field indicates redistribution outside the cylinders, rather than an unchanged frozen field. Coil-winding geometry and noncircular tin specimens limited comparison with the ideal prediction. [ref]
Their second arrangement used a lead tube, about 130mm long with 3mm outer and 2mm inner diameters. A rotating search coil could sit inside or alongside it. The bore retained magnetic flux after removing the applied magnetic field; the coil nearly filled the bore, preventing a test of interior field uniformity. A hole containing magnetic field is distinct from magnetic field penetrating the surrounding material exhibiting superconductivity. [ref]
The modern Field-cooled Meissner experiment provides a complementary readout. MIT's probe I places a sample in a brass holder, with an 810-turn test coil of 7.1mm inner diameter, 10.5mm outer diameter and 12.3mm length, inside a longer solenoid. A nearby silicon-diode thermometer tracks cooling by helium gas. With lead still normal, the guide applies about 200mA to the solenoid and connects the test coil to a current-integrator box and oscilloscope. Cooling produces a transient; warming gives the opposite sign. Switching off the solenoid while the sample is normal checks the sign. [ref]
The inferred change is in linked magnetic flux, not a direct scan of B(z). For a stationary test coil the pulse obeys ∫Vdt=−ΔΨ, where Ψ is its magnetic flux linkage. An integrator or AC-coupled oscilloscope needs its transfer response accounted for before extracting this pulse area. Keep the source electric current fixed during cooling, monitor temperature, and compare warming and cooling slowly enough to reduce sample–thermometer lag. An AC inductance change alone can reflect changed normal-state electrical conductivity; the fixed-field expulsion test distinguishes that alternative. [ref] Neither this coil readout nor the historical gap-field measurement resolves the microscopic London penetration depth by itself.

Limits of Meissner screening

Words: 175
Meissner effect OurBigBook.com
For a bulk type-II superconductor with negligible demagnetizing field, raising the applied magnetic field intensity past the lower critical value Hc1​ favors a superconducting mixed state containing quantum vortex lines; superconductivity survives until the upper critical value Hc2​. The magnetic field extends around each vortex over a penetration scale, not solely inside its core. Thus a nonzero average magnetic flux density can coexist with superconductivity. Below Hc1​, a type-II superconductor also supports a Meissner state; a vortex-free state above the equilibrium entry field may instead be metastable. [ref]
Real preparation histories may leave quantum vortex lines held in place by material inhomogeneities, retaining magnetic flux and producing hysteresis. This flux pinning restricts access to equilibrium: it is different from the arbitrary frozen interior magnetic field of the ideal perfect conductor model. Harvard's vortex-imaging work illustrates strong pinning to bulk disorder in a type-II superconductor. [ref] Separately, flux conservation in a superconducting loop and fluxoid and flux quantization in a superconductor explain why a hole can retain magnetic flux. Neither bore retention, vortex penetration nor weak screening in a thin sample should be silently identified with the equilibrium Planar London screening calculation.

(31)
