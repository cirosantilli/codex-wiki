Thomson electron transmission diffraction experiment

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics Wave function Matter wave
OurBigBook.com Words: 363
Alongside the 1927 Davisson–Germer experiment, George Paget Thomson developed an independent transmission test. Alexander Reid first used celluloid; Thomson used thin metal films. A narrow electron beam crossed a film about 10−6cm thick, reaching a photographic plate perpendicular to the beam. Rings appeared. Accelerating voltages of 20–60kV set the de Broglie wavelengths; metal structures known from X-ray diffraction allowed quantitative comparison. A downstream magnetic field deflected ring-forming electrons like those passing through holes, identifying the carriers as electrons rather than X-rays. Celluloid's uncertain structure limited its quantitative interpretation.[ref]
The Davisson–Germer experiment uses crystal reflection and angular electric current scans; this transmission camera records a spatial pattern behind the specimen. Both probe interference in electron diffraction, with different specimen preparations and readouts. A developed photographic plate records accumulated detections, not a direct image of the wavefunction.
Why rings? In an ideal film containing many small crystals with uniformly distributed orientations, a family of lattice planes with spacing d can satisfy Bragg's law at every azimuth about the incident beam. First-order elastic scattering then forms a cone of opening half-angle 2θ, where 2dsinθ=λ. A flat detector a distance L downstream cuts this cone in a circle:
R=Ltan(2θ)=Ltan[2arcsin(2dλ​)]≃dLλ​.
(8)
The last expression requires small scattering angles. It explains a useful control: hold the specimen and camera distance fixed, increase accelerating voltage, and the ring radii should shrink with the electron accelerating-voltage wavelength. It predicts positions, not intensities: preferred crystal orientations, film thickness and repeated quantum scattering can alter a real pattern.
For a chosen example, take L=0.200m and d=0.200nm. The exact relativistic electron accelerating-voltage wavelength gives λ=8.589pm at 20kV and 4.176pm at 80kV. The corresponding exact ring radii are 8.594mm and 4.176mm. These are calculated examples with chosen spacings and camera distance, not Thomson's measurements. The higher voltage lies outside the range quoted from his lecture, deliberately illustrating a prediction rather than reconstructing his data.
