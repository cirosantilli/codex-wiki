Mutagenesis

Deliberate or naturally induced generation of sequence changes. In experimental protein evolution the method determines the distribution of variants available for testing.

Mutation load

The number or distribution of sequence changes per variant in a mutagenized library. Higher load explores more distant combinations while commonly reducing functional recovery.

Saturation mutagenesis

Retrotransposon insertion

Insertion of a copied mobile element through an RNA intermediate. Orthologous insertion patterns can inform phylogenetic relationships, with lineage sorting and insertion-site properties considered.

Ultraconserved element

A genomic region showing exceptionally strong sequence conservation across the compared lineages. Conserved cores can anchor sequence capture, while associated variable regions contribute phylogenetic information.

Relationship between corresponding genes separated by lineage divergence, distinguished from relationships created by gene duplication. Correct copy assignment matters when interpreting comparative expression or inferring a gene tree.

Tumour mutational burden

The ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution rates after accounting for relevant mutation opportunities. Somatic-cancer analyses need background models appropriate to somatic mutation processes rather than raw event ratios.

Modification or design of a protein to change its properties. Rational design, library construction and directed evolution offer complementary ways to test sequence–function relationships.

Design of a protein catalyst using computational models of structure and chemistry. Experimental screening and directed evolution test and improve the proposed catalytic arrangement.

Off-target effect

An unintended effect outside the nominated molecular target. Independent reagents, verified target changes and functional rescue help distinguish target-specific consequences from these effects.

Gene knockout

Words: 31
Genetic engineering OurBigBook.com
A genetic intervention eliminating functional output of a gene. A targeted insertion or deletion is not by itself proof of knockout: remaining alleles, reading frame and functional protein must be checked.

CRISPR genome editing

Words: 204 Articles: 8
Genetic engineering OurBigBook.com
Targeted alteration of a genome using a guide-directed CRISPR system. Independent targeting reagents, verification of the resulting alleles and functional rescue help distinguish an intended effect from unrelated damage.

CRISPR locus labelling

Words: 24
CRISPR genome editing OurBigBook.com
Sequence-targeted fluorescent localization of chromosomal sites using a nuclease-inactive targeting system. Colocalization with a protein is evidence of shared location, not necessarily direct molecular contact.

CRISPR-Cas9

Words: 18
CRISPR genome editing OurBigBook.com
A guide-directed bacterial DNA-recognition and cleavage system. Catalytically inactive dCas9 retains targeting functions used in imaging and transcriptional regulation.

CRISPR activation

Words: 21
CRISPR genome editing OurBigBook.com
Guide-directed recruitment of transcriptional activation machinery to increase endogenous gene expression. Unlike a promoter-replacing knock-in, it need not change the target sequence.

CRISPR interference

Words: 22
CRISPR genome editing OurBigBook.com
Guide-directed reduction of gene expression using nuclease-inactive DNA targeting. In mammalian cells, dCas9 fused to a repressor can reduce transcription without requiring a DNA break.

Cas9

A guide-directed DNA nuclease. Target recognition depends on a guide RNA and an appropriate protospacer adjacent motif; cleavage is followed by cellular repair, so cleavage alone does not specify the final allele.
dCas9

Cas9 OurBigBook.com
A catalytically inactive form of Cas9 retaining guide-directed DNA binding. Recruitment of regulatory domains makes it a platform for CRISPR interference and CRISPR activation.

Protospacer adjacent motif

Words: 22
CRISPR genome editing OurBigBook.com
A short target-adjacent DNA motif recognized by a CRISPR-associated nuclease. The motif requirement depends on the nuclease; the commonly used Cas9 from Streptococcus pyogenes recognizes NGG.

Guide RNA

Words: 23
CRISPR genome editing OurBigBook.com
An RNA molecule whose complementary sequence directs a targeting complex to a nucleic-acid sequence. In CRISPR genome editing, guide complementarity and a compatible protospacer adjacent motif jointly constrain targeting.

Optogenetics

Words: 28
Genetic engineering OurBigBook.com
Optogenetics uses genetically introduced light-responsive components to perturb or monitor biological processes with spatial and temporal control. Light-only, construct-only and expression-matched controls help distinguish the intended response from phototoxicity.

Site-directed mutagenesis

Words: 20
Molecular genetics OurBigBook.com
site-directed mutagenesis deliberately changes a specified sequence position. Functional interpretation requires distinguishing a local mechanistic effect from altered abundance, localization or protein folding.

De novo gene birth

Words: 35
Molecular genetics OurBigBook.com
Origin of a new coding gene from ancestrally non-coding DNA, rather than divergence from a previously coding copy. Failure to detect a distant relative is insufficient evidence: comparative genomic context and evidence of ancestral coding status matter.

Neofunctionalization

Words: 26
Molecular genetics OurBigBook.com
Acquisition of a new function by a gene copy, classically following gene duplication. The other copy can retain an ancestral function while changes accumulate in the retained duplicate.

Subfunctionalization

Words: 24
Molecular genetics OurBigBook.com
Partitioning of ancestral functions or expression domains between duplicated genes. Each copy can retain a subset of deployments that a single ancestral copy performed.

Gene duplication

Words: 151 Articles: 7
Molecular genetics OurBigBook.com
Creation of an additional inherited copy of a gene. Copies can be lost, retain shared functions, undergo subfunctionalization, or undergo neofunctionalization; duplication alone does not guarantee a useful novelty.

Whole-genome duplication

Duplication of the full genomic complement. gene loss, subfunctionalization and neofunctionalization can obscure the original duplicated organization.

Two-round vertebrate genome duplication hypothesis

The hypothesis that two early large-scale duplication rounds contributed to the multiple paralogous genomic regions of jawed vertebrates. Hox clusters are one informative set of loci; a single family does not by itself establish genome-wide duplication.

Tandem gene duplication

Creation of adjacent copies of a gene or genomic segment. Subsequent divergence can produce a local gene family while preserving aspects of ancestral order.

Sanger sequencing determines a DNA nucleotide sequence by extending a common sequencing primer and occasionally incorporating a dideoxynucleoside triphosphate. A normal deoxynucleoside triphosphate already has hydrogen in place of a 2′ hydroxy group; it retains the 3′ hydroxy group. A dideoxynucleoside triphosphate lacks that 3′ hydroxy group too. It can join the growing strand because the preceding nucleotide supplies the reacting 3′ hydroxy group. Once incorporated, its own 3′ end cannot form the next phosphodiester bond. Thus the blocked step is the next addition, not attachment of the terminator itself.[ref]
In the historical four-reaction format, each tube receives copies of the same template, the same sequencing primer, a DNA polymerase and all four ordinary deoxynucleoside triphosphates. The tubes differ in their terminating substrate: ddATP, ddCTP, ddGTP or ddTTP. In the A reaction, ordinary dATP can allow copying to continue past one A position, whereas incorporation of ddATP stops another copy there. Competition produces a family of endpoints, rather than every strand stopping at the first A. Adjusting substrate proportions makes later endpoints detectable. A lane identifies the terminating nucleobase in the new strand, not the complementary template letter.[ref]
The readout requires gel electrophoresis capable of separating single-stranded DNA fragments differing by one nucleotide. Historical denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis suppressed strand folding and resolved these adjacent lengths; radioactive labels were detected by autoradiography. An ordinary agarose gel electrophoresis comparison with a 100-base pair DNA ladder does not supply that resolution. Four separate reaction lanes carry the identities in this format.[ref]

PCR thermal cycle

Words: 1k Articles: 3

A PCR thermal cycle separates three jobs. During PCR denaturation, heating separates the paired DNA strands without intentionally cutting their phosphodiester bonds. During PCR annealing, cooling permits base pairing between PCR primers and templates. During PCR extension, the chosen interval supports DNA polymerase synthesis from the paired 3′ ends. Each new nucleotide is supplied as a deoxynucleoside triphosphate; its incorporation lengthens the strand. The DNA polymerase is a catalyst, while these nucleotides are consumed substrates.[ref]
Taq polymerase withstands repeated high-temperature strand separation better than a heat-sensitive DNA polymerase. Its extension activity still depends on temperature, buffer solution and magnesium ions. Heat resistance does not mean constant activity at every stage: the separation temperature serves the DNA, whereas the extension temperature serves the copying enzyme. As one institutional example, Addgene's Basic PCR Program specifies 94∘C separation, 55∘C primer binding and 72∘C extension. These are example settings, not universal properties of all PCR primers or DNA polymerases.[ref]
For timing arithmetic alone, those stipulated holds give 20+25+45=90s per PCR thermal cycle, and three cycles give 270s. Adding an assumed 90s initial separation and 180s final extension gives 540s=9min of holds. A real thermal cycler also takes time to move between temperatures. The schedule tells us when conditions favour PCR denaturation, PCR annealing or PCR extension; it does not measure the fraction of DNA copied.

Words: 6k Articles: 223
Molecular genetics OurBigBook.com
Gene expression uses a gene's information to produce functional RNA or a protein. Transcription makes RNA from a DNA template; for a protein-coding gene, biological translation then uses messenger RNA to specify an amino acid chain. Many genes instead produce functional RNA, including transfer RNA and ribosomal RNA, without a translated protein product. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition (2002): from DNA to RNA.
Follow the chosen coding strand 5′-ATGGCTTAA-3′: transcription of its complementary template gives 5′-AUGGCUUAA-3′ in messenger RNA. In the stated reading frame, its three codons are AUG, GCU and UAA: methionine, alanine and a stop codon. Thus nine nucleotides specify two amino acid residues followed by termination, not three residues. This is an illustrative coding segment embedded in suitable expression and initiation context, not a claim that an isolated nine-nucleotide RNA is sufficient to initiate biological translation.
The distinction between copying and use explains how biological cells can preserve the same DNA yet produce different products. DNA replication duplicates the record; gene expression selects and uses parts of it. Transcription does not use all DNA at once, and gene expression can be controlled at several steps, including transcription, RNA processing and biological translation. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: regulation of gene expression. Transcription is synthesis of RNA using one DNA strand as a template by an RNA polymerase. During elongation the RNA polymerase reads that template 3′→5′ while extending RNA 5′→3′. Only the selected region is transcribed; other regions need not be active, and the template strand can differ between genes. See Alberts et al.: RNA polymerase and selection of transcribed DNA regions.
For the displayed example, align these three nucleotide sequences in the same left-to-right positions:
coding DNA:template DNA:RNA product:​5′-ATGGCTTAA-3′,3′-TACCGAATT-5′,5′-AUGGCUUAA-3′.​

The first template triplet TAC selects AUG: T pairs with A, A with U, and C with G. The next CGA selects GCU, and ATT selects UAA. The coding strand therefore matches the RNA lettering except for T versus U, whereas the template is complementary. Reading that template in its own 5′→3′ direction gives 5′-TTAAGCCAT-3′; orientation cannot be discarded. The gene expression diagram keeps all three rows aligned. This correspondence makes nucleotide sequences interpretable, but replacing T by U is valid only after identifying the coding strand. Moreover, RNA may be processed after transcription, so the initial product and mature messenger RNA need not have identical uninterrupted nucleotide sequences. Words: 2k Articles: 45
Gene expression OurBigBook.com
Biological translation uses a ribosome to read messenger RNA codons 5′→3′ and assemble the corresponding amino acid chain. Transfer RNA adaptors match codons through base pairing and carry the amino acids; the genetic code supplies the correspondence. The chain grows from the N-terminus toward the C-terminus, by adding each new residue at the C-terminus. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: ribosomes, transfer RNA and protein synthesis.
In the gene expression diagram, read 5′-AUG GCU UAA-3′ in the chosen reading frame. Under the standard genetic code, AUG specifies methionine and GCU specifies alanine; UAA is a stop codon. The result before any subsequent processing is the two-residue chain N–methionine–alanine–C, followed by termination. The stop codon contributes zero amino acids. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: the standard genetic code. This example assumes appropriate initiation at the displayed AUG in a suitable context in a eukaryote; the diagram displays the coding segment, not a complete independently translatable messenger RNA.
Initiation establishes the starting position and reading frame, using a ribosome, an initiating transfer RNA and additional factors. At a standard stop codon, a release factor promotes release of the chain rather than delivery of another amino acid by transfer RNA. See Clark, Douglas and Choi: initiation and termination of translation. A short two-residue example illustrates decoding, not the size or folding of a typical functional protein.
The useful connection is between two different alphabets: nucleotides specify the order of amino acids through the genetic code. Base pairing helps select transfer RNA, while the ribosome catalyzes joining the delivered amino acids. The messenger RNA remains a template rather than being consumed as the chain's building material. Separating template, adaptor and product makes clear why transcription and biological translation are distinct mechanisms.

A reading frame specifies how consecutive nucleotides are grouped into codons. Starting at position 1 of AUGGCUUAA gives AUG / GCU / UAA; starting at position 2 gives UGG / CUU with AA left over. The letters are unchanged but the groups differ. A possible grouping alone does not establish an actual biological translation initiation site.
For the twelve-nucleotide example, number positions from the 5′ end. The three possible forward reading frames start at positions 1, 2 and 3:
1:2:3:​AUGGCUUUCUGA,UGGCUUUCU(GA),GGCUUUCUG(A).​

Parentheses enclose incomplete trailing groups. The second and third rows do not assert initiation of biological translation in a biological cell. A chosen start fixes which positions belong together; the ribosome then advances through consecutive codons rather than searching each three-letter window independently.

Frameshift mutation
Words: 139
Reading frame OurBigBook.com
A frameshift mutation changes downstream grouping into codons through insertion or deletion of a number of nucleotides not divisible by three. In the constructed messenger RNA example, insert C immediately after AUG while retaining the same initiation position: AUGCGCUUUCUG(A). The first codon is preserved, but the original downstream groups GCU, UUC and UGA disappear. The displayed fragment now has no complete standard stop codon; what happens later depends on its continuation.
The short proof is positional: every downstream nucleotide moves by k positions after an insertion of length k. Boundaries recur every three positions, so they retain the original phase exactly when k is divisible by three. An insertion of three nucleotides can still change the peptide, but it does not shift the downstream reading frame. This distinction helps interpret genetic variants without predicting a particular phenotype from grouping alone.[ref]
Degeneracy of the genetic code
Words: 119
Genetic code OurBigBook.com
Degeneracy of the genetic code means that distinct codons can specify the same amino acid. For example, UUU and UUC both specify phenylalanine in the standard genetic code.[ref] In the original AUG GCU UUC UGA example, changing UUC to UUU preserves the predicted amino acid order. This illustrates redundancy of assignment, not equality of every possible effect on gene expression.
Degeneracy of the genetic code differs from ambiguity: multiple inputs share one output, whereas an ambiguous assignment would give a single codon more than one meaning in the same specified decoding context. The standard genetic code table has one listed assignment per triplet. Alternative genetic codes, including those used in some mitochondria, and engineered decoding contexts must be specified separately; the standard table is not a universal statement about all organelles or synthetic systems.
Nirenberg and Matthaei experiment
Words: 134
Genetic code OurBigBook.com
In 1961, Marshall Warren Nirenberg and J. Heinrich Matthaei ground washed Escherichia coli and used centrifugation to prepare a cell-free extract. Reactions at 35∘C combined ribosomes, soluble components, ATP regeneration and phenylalanine carrying a 14C isotope label, with or without polyuridylic acid, an RNA containing only U. After incubation, trichloroacetic acid precipitation, washing and counting radioactive decay detected label in insoluble material. Added polyuridylic acid strongly increased incorporation; removing ribosomes or ATP regeneration suppressed it. Product characterization supported a phenylalanine polypeptide.[ref]
The inference connects an imposed RNA template to a specific amino acid product. Yet repeated U alone cannot distinguish one-, two- or three-letter coding units: every possible group still contains only U. Neither triplet length nor the complete genetic code follows.[ref]
