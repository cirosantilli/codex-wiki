An isomorphic embedding of normed vector spaces is a bounded linear operator that is injective and whose inverse function on its image is bounded. Equivalently, T is bounded below: there is c>0 such that
c∥x∥≤∥Tx∥(x∈X).​
(1)
Indeed this inequality gives injectivity and ∥T−1z∥≤c−1∥z∥ for z∈T(X); the converse follows by applying the boundedness of T−1 to Tx. Together with ∥Tx∥≤∥T∥∥x∥, it makes T an isomorphic embedding onto its image with the inherited norm. The image is necessarily closed when X is a Banach space; that extra conclusion requires completeness.

A quotient operator is a surjective bounded linear operator that is an open map. For a linear map, being an open map is equivalent to the existence of c>0 such that
BY​(0,c)⊆T(BX​(0,1)).
(2)
The inclusion gives a neighbourhood of Tx inside the image of every sufficiently small ball about x, by translation and scaling. Conversely, an open map sends BX​(0,1) to a neighbourhood of zero. A linear open map onto Y is automatically surjective, since its image contains a ball and is a linear subspace.
The Banach-space adjoint is T∗g=g∘T. For g∈Y∗, the ball inclusion gives
∥T∗g∥=sup∥x∥<1​∣g(Tx)∣≥sup∥y∥<c​∣g(y)∣=c∥g∥.
(3)
Also ∥T∗g∥≤∥T∥∥g∥. Thus the Banach-space adjoint is an isomorphic embedding, with lower bound c.

The series ∑xn​ converges in the Banach space X because ∑∥xn​∥<∑δn=(1−δ)−1. Write its sum as x. The continuity of T and the residual estimate give

The real Hahn-Banach separation theorem says that if C is a nonempty open convex set in a real normed vector space and y∈/C, there is a nonzero continuous linear functional g such that g(y)≥supz∈C​g(z).
Put δ=1−1/M∈(0,1). The prescribed set is open because it is a union of translates of an open ball, and it is convex because both summands are convex. If ∥y∥<1 and y∈/C, Hahn-Banach separation gives a nonzero g∈Y∗ with
g(y)≥supz∈C​g(z)=∥T∗g∥+δ∥g∥≥(M−1+δ)∥g∥=∥g∥.
(7)
The displayed supremum is a sum of independent suprema; the symmetry of the unit balls identifies them with the respective operator norms. But g(y)≤∥g∥∥y∥<∥g∥, a contradiction. Hence y=Tx+r for some ∥x∥≤1 and ∥r∥<δ.
To obtain the strict bound on x required in the approximation hypothesis, choose t<1 sufficiently close to 1. Then ∥tx∥<1 and
∥T(tx)−y∥≤∥r∥+(1−t)∥Tx∥<δ.
(8)
Thus the approximation condition holds with exactly δ=1−1/M. When X is a Banach space, the preceding successive approximation for quotient operators proves T is a quotient operator. Combining this with the Banach-space adjoint estimate proves
T is a quotient operator⟺T∗ is an isomorphic embedding.​
(9)

The Baire category theorem states that a nonempty complete metric space cannot be a countable union of closed nowhere dense sets; equivalently, every countable intersection of open dense sets is dense.
Let En​=span{e1​,…,en​} in the space of finitely supported sequences. Each En​ is a closed linear subspace for every norm, since a finite-dimensional subspace is closed. Hence
Fn​=T−1(En​)
(10)
is a closed linear subspace of the Banach space X. Every finitely supported sequence lies in some En​, so X=⋃n≥1​Fn​. By the Baire category theorem, some FN​ has nonempty interior.
A linear subspace with nonempty interior is the whole space: translate a ball in it to zero and scale any vector into that ball. Thus FN​=X, giving T(X)⊆EN​ and
rankT≤N<∞.​
(11)
The proof uses no particular choice of norm on the space of finitely supported sequences.

The compact set S(Bc0​​) is bounded, so S is a bounded linear operator. For each g∈Y∗, the continuous linear functional g attains its maximum on that compact set. Symmetry of S(Bc0​​) implies that maximum is ∥S∗g∥, so S∗g attains its norm on Bc0​​. By norm attainment on the vanishing sequence space,
S∗(Y∗)⊆c00​⊂ℓ1​.

The weak topology σ(X,X∗) is the coarsest topology making every f∈X∗ continuous. Its basic neighbourhoods impose finitely many inequalities ∣fj​(x−x0​)∣<ε. The weak-star topology σ(X∗,X) is the coarsest topology making each evaluation f↦f(x) continuous, for x∈X.
The canonical embedding into the bidual is
JX​x∈X∗∗,(JX​x)(f)=f(x).
(18)
It is linear and ∥JX​x∥≤∥x∥. For x=0, the Hahn-Banach theorem supplies a norming functional f with ∥f∥=1 and f(x)=∥x∥, proving equality. Thus JX​ is an isometric embedding. The coordinates defining the weak-star topology on X∗∗, restricted to JX​(X), are precisely JX​x↦f(x) for f∈X∗. These are exactly the coordinates defining the weak topology on X, so JX​ is also a topological embedding for these topologies.
A normed vector space is a reflexive space if JX​ is onto X∗∗. Such a space is automatically a Banach space, since it is isometric to the complete continuous dual space X∗∗. The Banach-Alaoglu theorem states that the closed unit ball of a continuous dual space is compact for its weak-star topology. The Goldstine theorem states that JX​(BX​) is dense in the weak-star topology in BX∗∗​. Consequently
X is reflexive⟺BX​ is weakly compact.​
(19)
If JX​ is onto, its weak-to-weak-star homeomorphism carries BX​ onto the Banach-Alaoglu theorem's compact ball BX∗∗​. Conversely, if BX​ is weakly compact, JX​(BX​) is compact and hence closed in the Hausdorff weak-star topology. Its density from the Goldstine theorem makes it all of BX∗∗​, and scaling gives surjectivity of JX​.
Now suppose X is separable and nonzero. Choose a countable dense set (un​) in its unit sphere and, using the Hahn-Banach theorem, choose norming functionals fn​ with ∥fn​∥=1 and fn​(un​)=1. These continuous linear functionals separate points: for x=0, choose ∥un​−x/∥x∥∥<1/2 to get fn​(x)>∥x∥/2. Define
d(x,x′)=∑n≥1​min{2−n,∣fn​(x−x′)∣}.
(20)
The series converges uniformly, separates distinct points, and obeys the triangle inequality because min(c,a+b)≤min(c,a)+min(c,b). Thus d is a metric. Its topology is coarser than the weak topology: a finite set of coordinate controls makes the finite initial sum small, while the remaining sum is at most ∑n>N​2−n=2−N. On a weakly compact set K, the identity (K,weak)→(K,d) is therefore a continuous bijection from a compact space into a Hausdorff space, hence a homeomorphism. This proves that K is metrizable in its weak topology, an instance of countable separating family metrizes a weakly compact set.
If X is also a reflexive space, its closed unit ball is weakly compact and therefore metrizable by this construction. Compactness in a metric space implies sequential compactness, so every sequence in BX​ has a weakly convergent subsequence. Scaling proves the claim for every bounded sequence.
For the converse, assume every bounded sequence in X has a weakly convergent subsequence. Use the same metric d on BX​. A weak limit of points of BX​ remains in BX​: ∣f(x)∣≤1 for every ∥f∥≤1, and the Hahn-Banach theorem recovers ∥x∥≤1. Thus every sequence in BX​ has a subsequence convergent in d to a point of BX​, so (BX​,d) is sequentially compact and hence compact.
We must still recover the entire weak topology, rather than just the selected coordinates. Suppose xn​→x in d but not weakly. There is a neighbourhood in the weak topology U of x and a subsequence outside U. By hypothesis that subsequence has a further subsequence converging weakly to y∈BX​. It then converges in d to y, so uniqueness of metric limits gives y=x, contradicting eventual membership in U. Hence convergence in d implies weak convergence. Every f∈X∗ is therefore sequentially continuous on (BX​,d) and hence continuous, since the domain is a metric space. The weak topology and d-topology on BX​ coincide. The ball is weakly compact, so the criterion above proves
X separable:X reflexive⟺every bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence.​
(21)
The zero space satisfies all statements directly. The converse did not assume completeness, nor use the full Eberlein-Šmulian theorem.

A unital C-star algebra is a complex Banach algebra A with a multiplicative identity 1A​ and a conjugate-linear involution a↦a∗ satisfying (ab)∗=b∗a∗ and the C-star identity ∥a∗a∥=∥a∥2. A C-star isomorphism Φ:A→B is a bijective complex-linear map preserving products and the involution. It carries 1A​ to 1B​ and is automatically isometric.
For a commutative unital C-star algebra, let Ω be its maximal ideal space, equivalently the space of Gelfand characters, with the weak-star topology. A Gelfand character has χ(1)=1 and ∥χ∥=1. The equations χ(ab)=χ(a)χ(b) and χ(1)=1 define a closed in the weak-star topology subset of the dual unit ball. The Banach-Alaoglu theorem makes Ω a compact Hausdorff space. Maximal ideals give characters by the Gelfand-Mazur theorem, and the Banach algebra spectrum satisfies σ(a)={χ(a):χ∈Ω}.
The Gelfand transform is a(χ)=χ(a). It preserves products, constants and the involution; the latter uses the fact that characters of a C-star algebra respect the involution. Every element of a commutative C-star algebra is normal, so the C-star identity and the spectral radius formula give
∥a∥∞​=supχ∈Ω​∣χ(a)∣=r(a)=∥a∥.
(22)
Thus its image is a closed self-adjoint subalgebra of C(Ω), contains the constants, and separates characters. The complex Stone-Weierstrass theorem makes that image dense and therefore all of C(Ω). This is the Commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem.
For the convergent sequence space c, coordinatewise multiplication satisfies ∥αβ∥∞​≤∥α∥∞​∥β∥∞​, and coordinatewise complex conjugation is an involution. The constant sequence 1 is the identity, and
∥α∗α∥∞​=supn​∣αn​∣2=∥α∥∞2​.
(23)
It is complete: a uniform limit of convergent sequences is convergent, so c is a closed linear subspace of the Banach space ℓ∞​. Hence it is a commutative unital C-star algebra.
Take
Ω=N∪{∞},the one-point compactification of discrete N.​
(24)
Every finite point is isolated, and the neighbourhoods of ∞ contain all but finitely many integers. This is a compact Hausdorff space: an open cover has a set containing ∞, leaving only finitely many points to cover. Define
Φ(α)(n)=αn​,Φ(α)(∞)=limn→∞​αn​.
(25)
Continuity at ∞ is exactly convergence of the sequence. Thus Φ is onto C(Ω), and pointwise operations show it is a C-star isomorphism with ∥Φα∥∞​=∥α∥∞​. Explicitly, these are all Gelfand characters: each χ(en​) is 0 or 1 and at most one is 1. If χ(en​)=1, multiplication by en​ gives χ(α)=αn​. If all vanish, density of finitely supported sequences in l-p in c0​ gives χ∣c0​​=0, so χ(α)=limαn​.

For a self-adjoint C-star element a, the following are three equivalent characterizations of a Positive element of a C-star algebra:
​σ(a)⊆[0,∞);∥a−∥a∥1A​∥≤∥a∥;there is t>0 with ∥a−t1A​∥≤t.​​
(26)
If a=b2 with b=b∗, the spectral mapping theorem gives nonnegative spectrum. Conversely, the continuous functional calculus applied to λ​ on σ(a) gives a self-adjoint C-star element b with b2=a. For real σ(a),
∥a−t1A​∥=maxλ∈σ(a)​∣λ−t∣.
(27)
Thus the third condition means σ(a)⊆[0,2t], while the first implies the second by taking the interval [0,∥a∥]. The second implies the third with t=∥a∥ if a=0; if a=0, any t>0 works. This proves every equivalence.
The positive cone of a C-star algebra contains zero and is closed under multiplication by nonnegative real scalars. To verify addition without assuming that positive elements commute, put r=∥a∥, s=∥b∥ for a,b≥0. Then
∥(a+b)−(r+s)1A​∥≤∥a−r1A​∥+∥b−s1A​∥≤r+s.
(28)
If r+s>0, the third characterization makes a+b positive; otherwise both are zero. Hence it is a convex cone. If a and −a are positive, σ(a)={0} and a=0 by the self-adjoint spectral norm formula; therefore the cone is pointed and defines a partial order on Asa​ by h≤k if k−h∈A+​. It is generating because the continuous functional calculus gives
∣h∣=(h2)1/2,h±​=21​(∣h∣±h)≥0,h=h+​−h−​.
(29)
For a≥0, the spectral mapping theorem yields
∥a∥≤1⟺σ(a)⊆[0,1]⟺1A​−a≥0⟺0≤a≤1A​.​
(30)
Finally, let a∈M2​(C) be Hermitian, and write τ=tra, d=deta. Its two eigenvalues are real. Both are nonnegative exactly when their sum τ and product d are nonnegative. The corresponding sum and product for I−a are 2−τ and 1−τ+d. Therefore the necessary and sufficient conditions for a positive contraction are
0≤τ≤2,d≥0,1−τ+d≥0.​
(31)
Indeed, nonnegative sum and product force two real numbers to be nonnegative, including the zero-product cases. Applying this to a and I−a places both eigenvalues in [0,1].
