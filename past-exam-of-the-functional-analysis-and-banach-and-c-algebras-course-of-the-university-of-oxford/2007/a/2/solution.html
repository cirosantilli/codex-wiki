Regard the spaces as real and set u=Reϕ. Its real norm equals ∥ϕ∥, since rotating any test vector makes its functional value real and nonnegative without changing its norm. By the allowed real Hahn-Banach theorem extend u to a real-linear v:X→R with that norm, and define ψ(x)=v(x)−iv(ix). Real linearity gives ψ(ix)=iψ(x), so ψ is complex linear. On Y this formula recovers ϕ. Also Reψ=v; rotating a test vector again gives ∣ψ(x)∣≤∥v∥∥x∥. The reverse norm inequality follows by restriction to Y, proving a norm-preserving complex extension.
We use two standard consequences: a point outside a closed subspace can be separated by a bounded functional annihilating that subspace, and the norm of a vector is the supremum of its pairings with dual unit-ball functionals. If ψ∈X∗ annihilates Y, the scalar holomorphic function ψ∘f vanishes at the prescribed sequence tending to zero. Its identity theorem makes it identically zero. Separation then gives f(z)∈Y for every z∈D.
For ϕ∈Y∗ extend to X∗ with the same norm and put F=ϕ∘f. Then F is holomorphic with ∣F∣≤∥ϕ∥. For the understood nonzero increments h,k, the Cauchy integral formula on ∣ζ∣=r gives

Taking absolute values, bounding the contour length by 2πr, and each denominator below by r−∣z∣, r−∣z∣−∣h∣, r−∣z∣−∣k∣ proves exactly the stated estimate.
Fix z,r and restrict ∣h∣,∣k∣<(r−∣z∣)/2. Taking the supremum over ∥ϕ∥≤1 bounds the norm of the difference of the two Y-valued difference quotients by a fixed constant times ∣h−k∣. They are therefore Cauchy as h→0. Since Y is a closed subspace of a Banach space, it is complete. Consequently the derivative exists as a norm limit in Y. The labelled (a)-(c) are the simultaneous hypotheses, not separate claims to prove.
