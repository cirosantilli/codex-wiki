i OurBigBook.com
The quotient norm is
∥x+Y∥X/Y​=y∈Yinf​∥x−y∥.​
(1)
Changing the representative does not change the infimum. The triangle inequality and absolute homogeneity follow from those of the original norm. Vanishing means x belongs to the closure of Y, hence to Y because the subspace is closed. Thus this is a norm.
Use the absolutely convergent series criterion for completeness. If ∑∥ξn​∥X/Y​<∞, choose representatives zn​ of ξn​ satisfying ∥zn​∥≤∥ξn​∥X/Y​+2−n. The series ∑zn​ converges in the Banach space X to some z. Since the quotient map is contractive, ∑ξn​ converges to z+Y. Consequently the quotient is a Banach space.

A set is totally bounded if for every ε>0 it can be covered by finitely many open balls of radius ε.
Here is the needed separated-sequence argument, including the relevant Riesz lemma. For a proper closed subspace Y, choose z∈/Y, put d=dist(z,Y)>0, and choose y∈Y with ∥z−y∥<2d. Then w=(z−y)/∥z−y∥ is a unit vector at distance greater than 1/2 from Y. Starting with one unit vector, apply this to the span of the vectors already chosen. These spans are closed and proper because they are finite-dimensional while X is infinite-dimensional. The resulting unit vectors satisfy ∥wm​−wn​∥>1/2 for m=n. A ball of radius 1/4 contains at most one of them. Hence the closed unit ball is not totally bounded.

Take S=T(BX​). A bounded linear operator is compact exactly when the closure of this set is compact. In a complete metric space this is equivalent to its being totally bounded.
If T is compact, choose a finite ε-net z1​,…,zm​ for T(BX​) and let Y be their span. Then dist(Tx,Y)<ε for ∥x∥≤1, giving ∥πY​T∥≤ε.
Conversely, fix δ>0 and take Y with ∥πY​T∥≤δ/4. For every x∈BX​ choose yx​∈Y with ∥Tx−yx​∥<δ/2. These approximants satisfy ∥yx​∥≤∥T∥+δ/2, so form a bounded subset of a finite-dimensional space and have a finite δ/2-net. The same centers give a finite δ-net for T(BX​). Thus it is totally bounded and T is compact. In particular
T compact⟺∀ε>0 ∃dimY<∞: ∥πY​T∥≤ε.​

The natural canonical embedding into the bidual is JX​x(f)=f(x). It is linear and ∥JX​x∥≤∥x∥. A norming functional from the Hahn-Banach theorem gives the reverse inequality, so it is an isometry. A reflexive Banach space is one for which this canonical embedding is onto.
A nonreflexive example is c0​. Its dual is ℓ1 and its bidual is ℓ∞; the canonical map is the natural inclusion, which misses the constant sequence one. This also shows why merely having an isometric embedding in the bidual is insufficient.

An infinite-dimensional reflexive example is ℓ2, by the Riesz representation theorem.
The weak topology on X is the coarsest topology making every f∈X∗ continuous. The weak-star topology on X∗ is the coarsest making every evaluation f↦f(x), x∈X, continuous. In each case basic neighborhoods impose finitely many evaluation inequalities. The Banach-Alaoglu theorem states that the closed unit ball of a dual space is compact for its weak-star topology. The separation theorem needed here says: if K is a nonempty weak-star compact convex subset of a dual space E∗ and z∈/K, then some e∈E strictly separates them,
Rez(e)>supk∈K​Rek(e).
(3)
The real-part convention covers complex spaces; for real spaces omit it.
Let K be the weak-star closure of JX​(BX​) inside BX∗∗​. This is compact by Banach-Alaoglu, convex, and nonempty. If z∈BX∗∗​∖K, separation in the dual pair (X∗∗,X∗) gives f∈X∗ with
Rez(f)>supx∈BX​​Ref(x)=∥f∥.
(4)
But ∣z(f)∣≤∥z∥∥f∥≤∥f∥, a contradiction. Therefore K=BX∗∗​, proving the required Goldstine theorem density.
If X is reflexive, JX​ identifies its weak topology with the weak-star topology of X∗∗ and carries BX​ onto BX∗∗​, so Banach-Alaoglu gives weak compactness. Conversely, if BX​ is weakly compact, its image is weak-star compact, hence closed in the Hausdorff bidual topology. Density then forces JX​(BX​)=BX∗∗​. Scaling gives surjectivity on the entire bidual. Thus
BX​ weakly compact⟺X reflexive.​
(5)

The Baire category theorem says that a nonempty complete metric space is not a countable union of closed sets with empty interior; equivalently, every countable intersection of open dense sets is dense. To prove the latter, start with any nonempty open set V and open dense sets Gn​. Choose closed balls of positive radii with
B1​⊂V∩G1​,Bn+1​⊂Bn​∩Gn+1​,rn​<2−n.
(6)
Each choice is possible by density and openness. Their centers form a Cauchy sequence; its limit exists by completeness and belongs to every closed ball, hence to V∩⋂Gn​. This proves density and the theorem.
The Uniform boundedness principle says that a pointwise bounded family T of bounded linear maps from a Banach space X to a normed space Z has uniformly bounded operator norms. Put Em​={x:supT∈T​∥Tx∥≤m}. These sets are closed and cover X. Baire gives an open ball B(x0​,r)⊂Em​. For ∥h∥<r, the triangle inequality applied to T(x0​+h)−Tx0​ gives ∥Th∥≤2m. Taking h=(r/2)u for unit u proves supT​∥T∥≤4m/r.
For the particular expansion, uniqueness makes all coefficient maps and partial-sum maps linear. Since Pk​x→x, the norms ∥Pk​x∥ form a bounded sequence with limit ∥x∥. Also, with P0​=0,
∣φn​(x)∣=∥φn​(x)xn​∥=∥(Pn​−Pn−1​)x∥≤2supk​∥Pk​x∥.
(7)
This proves both preliminary bounds. The supremum therefore defines a finite norm ∥⋅∥′, with ∥x∥≤∥x∥′.
To prove (i) implies (ii), the identity map from (X,∥⋅∥′) to (X,∥⋅∥) is a bounded linear bijection. If (i) holds both spaces are Banach, so the bounded inverse theorem makes its inverse bounded: ∥x∥′≤C∥x∥. This is precisely (ii).

(vi) implies (iv) without assuming beforehand that any coordinate functional is continuous. Define the everywhere-defined linear map
Φ:X→ℓ∞,Φ(x)=(φn​(x))n≥1​.
(10)
The preliminary coordinate bound ensures it really takes values in ℓ∞. Condition (vi) states exactly that its graph is sequentially closed: yr​→x and Φ(yr​)→β in the supremum norm imply Φ(x)=β. The product is a metric space, so sequentially closed means closed. The closed graph theorem therefore makes Φ bounded. Every coordinate projection on ℓ∞ is bounded, hence so is each φn​.
Together with the preceding implications this proves all six conditions are equivalent. No continuity of the coordinate functionals was assumed during the preliminary bounds.

A character of an algebra is a nonzero multiplicative complex-linear map χ:A→C. A maximal proper ideal is a proper ideal contained in no larger proper ideal. For a character, χ(1)=1 and χ(a)∈σ(a): otherwise a−χ(a)1 would be invertible although its character value is zero. The spectral bound gives ∣χ(a)∣≤∥a∥, so the character is continuous. It is onto, and its kernel is a closed maximal ideal because A/kerχ≅C.
Conversely let M be maximal. Its closure is an ideal and remains proper: otherwise an element of M would lie within distance one of the unit, hence would be invertible by the Neumann series, forcing 1∈M. Maximality then gives M=M. The Banach algebra A/M is a field. For any b in this quotient choose λ∈σ(b), using nonemptiness of the complex spectrum. In a field only zero is noninvertible, so b−λ1=0. Hence every quotient element is a scalar multiple of the unit. The quotient map therefore determines a character χM​, uniquely normalized by χM​(1)=1. These constructions are inverse:
Δ(A)⟷M(A),χ⟼kerχ.​
(11)
For the convolution algebra, write ej​ for the sequence supported at j with value one. Then ej​ek​=ej+k​, e0​=1, and e1​e−1​=1. If z=χ(e1​), continuity and ∥e±1​∥1​=1 imply ∣z∣≤1 and ∣z−1∣≤1. Thus ∣z∣=1, so uniquely z=eit for 0≤t<2π, and χ(ej​)=eijt for every integer j. Absolute convergence in ℓ1 now gives
χt​(α)=j∈Z∑​αj​eijt.​
(12)
Conversely each displayed map is bounded, nonzero, and multiplicative, since the absolutely convergent double sum can be rearranged along k+l=j. Thus these are all characters, and all maximal proper ideals are
Mt​={α∈ℓ1(Z):j∑​αj​eijt=0},0≤t<2π.​
(13)
They are distinct because the character is uniquely recovered from its kernel and its value on the unit.

An element is a Positive element of a C-star algebra if it is self-adjoint and its spectrum lies in [0,∞), equivalently if a=b∗b for some b.
For a normal a, the continuous functional calculus is the unique unital isometric *-isomorphism C(σ(a))→C∗(1,a) sending the coordinate function z to a. It respects sums, products, conjugation and the unit, satisfies ∥f(a)∥=maxσ(a)​∣f∣, and has σ(f(a))=f(σ(a)). For self-adjoint a it preserves the order of real-valued functions.
The piecewise definition of gn​ is consistent for n≥2. At n=1 the printed branches conflict, even assigning both zero and one at t=1/2. All the requested limit and face arguments use only n≥2; discarding this first index resolves that harmless initial-index defect without altering any limit.
For n≥2, interpolate linearly from (1/(2n),0) to (1/n,g(1/n)), and from (1−1/n,g(1−1/n)) to (1−1/(2n),1). On the middle interval the error is zero. On the left both g and gn​ lie between zero and g(1/n); on the right both lie between g(1−1/n) and one. Thus
∥g−gn​∥∞​≤max{g(1/n),1−g(1−1/n)}⟶0
(15)
by continuity at the endpoints.
Put δ=1/(2n). To prove the bounds on hn​, it suffices to show 0≤t−δgn​(t)≤1−δ. For t≤δ, gn​=0; for t≥1−δ, gn​=1. On the remaining interval, δ≤t≤1−δ and 0≤gn​≤1, so both inequalities follow. Consequently gn​ and hn​ are positive contractions.
For the face assertion, write r=∥a∥. Since a∈[0,1A​] and 0∈/F, 0<r≤1. If r<1, then a=r(a/r)+(1−r)0, with both endpoints in [0,1A​]. The face property would force 0∈F, a contradiction. Thus ∥a∥=1. Its spectrum contains one by positivity and the norm formula, and contains zero because it is noninvertible.
Applying functional calculus to the scalar identity gives
a=δgn​(a)+(1−δ)hn​(a),gn​(a),hn​(a)∈[0,1A​].
(16)
Since 0<δ<1 and a∈F, the face property gives gn​(a)∈F. Isometry of functional calculus gives gn​(a)→g(a) in norm; closedness of F therefore proves
∥a∥=1,g(a)∈F.​
(17)

For a bounded linear functional on a unital C*-algebra, the state criterion is
x(1A​)=∥x∥=1.​
(18)
Equivalently it is positive and takes the unit to one.
In the Gelfand–Naimark–Segal construction, set Nx​={b:x(b∗b)=0}. This is a left ideal. Give A/Nx​ the inner product ⟨[b],[c]⟩=x(c∗b), linear in the first variable, and complete it to a Hilbert space Hx​. Left multiplication gives the bounded *-representation πx​(a)[b]=[ab]. The vector ξ=[1A​] is cyclic and satisfies x(a)=⟨πx​(a)ξ,ξ⟩.
The Hermitian matrix ρ has eigenvalues λ±​=1/2±∣α∣ and trace one. In an orthonormal eigenbasis v+​,v−​,
xρ​(a)=λ+​⟨av+​,v+​⟩+λ−​⟨av−​,v−​⟩.
(19)
If ∣α∣≤1/2, both weights are nonnegative and sum to one, so this functional is positive and normalized, hence a state. If ∣α∣>1/2, evaluating on the positive rank-one projection onto the negative-eigenvalue eigenspace gives a negative value, so it is not a state. Therefore
xρ​ is a state⟺∣α∣≤1/2.​
(20)
To obtain the representation dimension, cyclicity of trace gives
xρ​(b∗b)=Tr(ρb∗b)=∥bρ1/2∥HS2​.
(21)
Thus A/Nxρ​​ identifies isometrically with the matrix space {bρ1/2:b∈M2​(C)}, with left multiplication as the representation. If r=rankρ, this space consists, after a unitary change of basis, of arbitrary first r columns and zero remaining columns. Its complex dimension is 2r. It is already complete because it is finite-dimensional. Rank is one at ∣α∣=1/2 and two at ∣α∣<1/2, proving
dimHxρ​​=2 at ∣α∣=1/2,dimHxρ​​=4 at ∣α∣<1/2.​
(22)
