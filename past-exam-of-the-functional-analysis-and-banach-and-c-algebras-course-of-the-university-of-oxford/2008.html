A subset is totally bounded if for every ε>0 it has a finite cover by balls of radius ε. If its closure is compact, the cover by all such balls has a finite subcover, proving total boundedness.
Conversely, cover S by finitely many balls of radius 2−r at stage r. Starting with all indices, at least one ball contains infinitely many of the currently indexed terms. Retain those indices as σr​. These sets are infinite and nested, and two terms indexed by σr​ have distance less than 21−r. Choose increasing nr​∈σr​; then (xnr​​) is Cauchy, because all its terms after stage r lie in that same ball. If M is complete it converges. To cover sequences in the closure as well, approximate their nth terms by points of S at distance less than 1/n and apply this argument. The corresponding subsequence in the closure has the same limit. Sequential compactness therefore proves total boundedness implies relative compactness in a complete ambient space.
The Arzelà-Ascoli theorem says a subset of C[0,1] is relatively compact exactly when it is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous. Pointwise boundedness together with equicontinuity is an equivalent version here. In the specified derivative-bounded family, the mean-value estimate gives ∣f(t)−f(s)∣≤∣t−s∣, uniformly over the family, while ∥f∥∞​≤1. Thus this family is relatively compact.
For V, the image of the unit ball has sup norm at most one and derivative f of sup norm at most one; it lies in that family, so V is compact.
For W, take fn​=n1[0,1/n]​, each of L1 norm one. Then Wfn​(t)=min(nt,1). Every subsequence converges pointwise to zero at t=0 and to one at every t>0. This limit is discontinuous, so no subsequence converges uniformly to a continuous function. Thus W is not compact. The same integration formula has different compactness properties because the domain unit balls impose different controls.

A sublinear functional on a real vector space satisfies p(x+y)≤p(x)+p(y) and p(tx)=tp(x) for every t≥0. The dominated real Hahn-Banach theorem says that a linear functional on a subspace satisfying f≤p extends to a linear functional on the whole space still dominated by p.
The limit-superior functional is finite on bounded sequences, positively homogeneous, and subadditive, since for every tail sup(αn​+βn​)≤supαn​+supβn​. Passing to the tail limits proves sublinearity. On the subspace c of convergent real sequences, the ordinary limit is linear and equals p. Hahn-Banach therefore extends it to φ on ℓ∞ with
φ(x)≤nlimsup​αn​.​

The weak-star topology on X∗ is the coarsest topology making every evaluation ϕ↦ϕ(x) continuous. Finite intersections of evaluation inequalities form a neighborhood basis. Distinct functionals differ on some x, so disjoint disks around their evaluation values give disjoint neighborhoods; thus the topology is Hausdorff. The Banach-Alaoglu theorem states that the closed unit ball of X∗ is compact in this topology.
If f∈X∗∗ is weak-star continuous, some x1​,…,xn​ and ε>0 make ∣f(ϕ)∣<1 whenever ∣ϕ(xi​)∣<ε for every i. If all evaluations vanish, every scalar multiple of ϕ lies in this neighborhood, forcing f(ϕ)=0. Thus f vanishes on the kernel of the map E(ϕ)=(ϕ(x1​),…,ϕ(xn​)). It factors linearly through the finite-dimensional range of E. Extending that factor algebraically to Kn gives scalars ai​ with
f(ϕ)=∑i​ai​ϕ(xi​)=ϕ(∑i​ai​xi​).
(6)
Consequently f=JX​x for x=∑i​ai​xi​.
For the two induced topologies, let B be the common norm-closed unit ball and write τj​=σ(Y,Xj​). Under either topology B is compact by Banach-Alaoglu, and the ambient space is Hausdorff. If the two topologies are comparable, the identity map from B with the finer topology to B with the coarser one is a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space. Such a map is a homeomorphism, since it carries closed compact sets to closed sets. Thus in the comparable case the topologies agree on B, giving alternative (a).

The open mapping theorem says that a bounded surjective linear map T:E→F between Banach spaces is open. Here is a proof using only Baire and completeness. Surjectivity gives F=⋃n≥1​T(nBE​), hence also a cover by their closures. Baire makes one such closure contain a ball B(y0​,r). Taking differences gives B(0,r)⊆T(2nBE​)​. Scaling yields some δ>0 with
B(0,δ)⊆T(BE​)​.
(9)
If ∥y∥<δ/2, recursively use scaled versions of this inclusion to choose xk​ with ∥xk​∥≤2−k and residual ∥y−T∑j=1k​xj​∥<δ2−k−1. The sum converges in E to an element of norm at most one, whose image is y. Thus B(0,δ/2)⊆T(BE​), and scaling gives B(0,δ/4)⊆T(21​BE​)⊆T(BE​). Translating and scaling proves openness on every open set. This proof does not use the closed graph or inverse mapping theorem.
Apply it to the evaluation map E:X→ℓ∞, Ef=(f(zn​))n​. It is bounded with norm at most one and onto by hypothesis. Hence its image of the unit ball contains a ball about zero, yielding a constant c and, for every α, an interpolant g with
g(zn​)=αn​,∥g∥∞​≤c∥α∥∞​.​
(10)
For completeness, scale a ball of radius η inside E(BX​) by 2∥α∥∞​/η to obtain c=2/η; zero data use g=0.
For each n, interpolate the data which are one at index n and zero elsewhere, obtaining gn​ of norm at most c. The supplied factorization property gives gn​=kn​hn​ with ∥kn​∥∞​=∥gn​∥∞​≤c. Evaluation at zn​ gives 1=∣kn​(zn​)hn​(zn​)∣≤c∣hn​(zn​)∣, so

For the real lattice version of the Stone-Weierstrass theorem, let f∈CR​(Ω) and ε>0. For any x,y∈Ω, separation of points and constants give hxy​∈L matching f at both x and y: take an affine combination of a separating function, and for x=y take a constant. Fix x. The open sets where hxy​>f−ε cover Ω as y varies. Compactness gives finitely many such functions; their maximum gx​ belongs to the lattice L, satisfies gx​>f−ε everywhere, and has gx​(x)=f(x). The open sets where gx​<f+ε now cover Ω as x varies. Choose finitely many and take their minimum h∈L. Then f−ε<h<f+ε everywhere. Thus L is uniformly dense in the real continuous functions.
For the complex *-algebra, its real subalgebra AR​ of Hermitian C-star elements contains the real and imaginary parts of every member. It separates points, because at least one of those parts distinguishes any pair distinguished by a complex function. Its uniform closure is a unital real subalgebra and is a lattice by the allowed result. The real theorem makes this closure all of CR​(Ω). Approximating the real and imaginary parts separately proves A is uniformly dense in C(Ω).
On [0,1], the algebra of polynomials in t2 is unital, closed under conjugation, and separates points because t↦t2 is injective there. Choose p(t2) within ε/3 of a(t), and correct its endpoints by
b(t)=p(t2)+[a(0)−p(0)](1−t2)+[a(1)−p(1)]t2.
(12)
This is still a polynomial in t2 and has exactly the requested endpoint values. The correction has absolute value at most ε/3, because it is a convex combination of two errors of that size. Therefore
b(0)=a(0),b(1)=a(1),∥a−b∥∞​<2ε/3<ε.​
(13)

The spectrum of an element is σA​(a)={λ∈C:a−λ1A​ is not invertible in A}. A Normal element of a C-star algebra satisfies a∗a=aa∗.
For a Hermitian C-star element h, the C-star identity gives ∥h2∥=∥h∥2. For normal a, commutation gives
∥a2∥2=∥(a∗)2a2∥=∥(a∗a)2∥=∥a∗a∥2=∥a∥4.
(14)
All powers remain normal, so induction yields ∥a2k∥=∥a∥2k. Taking the spectral-radius limit along these powers gives
νA​(a)=∥a∥for normal a.​
(15)
Let φ:A→B be a C-star homomorphism, without assuming continuity or preservation of the ambient unit. Put p=φ(1A​), a projection in a C-star algebra. If p=0, then φ=0. Otherwise its image lies in the unital corner pBp and it is unital as a map into that corner. An inverse in A maps to an inverse in the corner, so σpBp​(φ(b))⊆σA​(b). Apply this to the normal element b=a∗a and use the C-star identity in both algebras:
∥φ(a)∥2=∥φ(a∗a)∥=νpBp​(φ(a∗a))≤νA​(a∗a)=∥a∗a∥=∥a∥2.
(16)
Thus every C-star homomorphism is norm non-increasing, including nonunital maps.

A Unitary element of a C-star algebra satisfies u∗u=uu∗=1. A projection in a C-star algebra satisfies p=p∗=p2.
If q=u∗u is a projection in a C-star algebra, the C-star identity gives ∥u(1−q)∥2=∥(1−q)q(1−q)∥=0. Hence u=uq=uu∗u. Conversely uu∗u=u implies (u∗u)2=u∗u, and u∗u is a Hermitian C-star element, so it is a projection in a C-star algebra. Taking adjoints of the triple-product equality gives the corresponding equality for u∗, proving
u partial isometry⟺uu∗u=u⟺u∗ partial isometry.​
(17)
For nonzero u, let p=uu∗ and q=u∗u. These are projections, u=pu=uq, and ∥u∥=1. The rectangular corner pAq is closed because it is the range of the bounded idempotent map a↦paq.
Define Θ:pAq→qAq by Θ(a)=u∗a. It lands in the corner, and its inverse is b↦ub. The identities uu∗a=pa=a and u∗ub=qb=b verify both compositions. Both maps are contractive because ∥u∥=∥u∗∥=1, so Θ is an isometry. For the proposed multiplication and involution,
Θ(au∗b)=Θ(a)Θ(b),Θ(ua∗u)=qa∗u=a∗u=Θ(a)∗.
(18)
Thus the operations correspond exactly to the ordinary operations in the closed C-star algebra qAq. This proves associativity, closure, all involution identities and the C*-norm identity at once. Moreover Θ(u)=q, so the unit of Au​ is u. If u is unitary, p=q=1, and the map a↦u∗a is an onto isometric *-isomorphism from Au​ to A.

First suppose x is a positive linear functional, without assuming boundedness. Every Hermitian C-star element h is a difference of positive elements, since ∥h∥1±h≥0. Thus x(h) is real. For a Hermitian C-star element h of norm at most one, −1≤h≤1 gives ∣x(h)∣≤x(1).
For arbitrary a, choose a scalar ζ of modulus one so that ζx(a)=∣x(a)∣. The real part of ζa is a Hermitian C-star element and has norm at most ∥a∥. Since x is real on Hermitian C-star elements, it respects real parts, giving
∣x(a)∣=x(Re(ζa))≤x(1)∥a∥.
(19)
This proves boundedness and ∥x∥≤x(1). Evaluation at the unit gives the reverse inequality, so positivity implies ∥x∥=x(1).
Conversely let x be bounded with x(1)=∥x∥=c≥0. If c=0, x=0 is positive. Otherwise normalize to c=1. For a Hermitian C-star element h and real t, the C-star identity gives
∣1+itx(h)∣≤∥1+ith∥≤1+t2∥h∥2​.
(20)
Writing x(h)=a+ib and squaring yields −2tb+t2(a2+b2)≤t2∥h∥2. Taking t→0 through positive and negative values forces b=0. Hence x is real on Hermitian C-star elements. If 0≤a≤1, then 1−a has norm at most one, so x(1−a)≤1 and x(a)≥0. Scaling proves nonnegativity on every positive element, in particular every b∗b. Therefore
