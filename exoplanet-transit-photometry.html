Exoplanet transit photometry

Exoplanet transit photometry measures the decrease in a star's observed radiative flux during an exoplanet transit. Define F0​ as the unobscured star's observed radiative flux and F(t) as the value at time t. For a dark, opaque exoplanet crossing a uniformly bright circular stellar disc, a fully superimposed planetary disc blocks exactly the fraction
δ=F0​F0​−Ffull​​=πR⋆2​πRp2​​=(R⋆​Rp​​)2.
(16)
Here Rp​ and R⋆​ are the planet and star radii. This exoplanet transit depth determines a radius ratio, Rp​/R⋆​=δ​; an absolute planet radius requires an independently estimated stellar radius. For Rp​/R⋆​=0.10, δ=0.010: a one-percent dip, with Ffull​/F0​=0.990. Halving the radius ratio to 0.05 reduces the exoplanet transit depth to 0.0025, a quarter as much.
For the central exoplanet transit illustrated below, the transit impact parameter is b=0. Write k=Rp​/R⋆​<1 and let z be the projected centre-to-centre separation in units of R⋆​. The light curve is F/F0​=1−A(z)/π, where A(z) is the overlap area of circles of radii 1 and k. There is no overlap when z≥1+k; the planetary disc is fully inside when z≤1−k, giving A=πk2. Between those limits, the overlap area grows or shrinks continuously. Thus even a uniform star has curved ingress and egress, with a flat bottom only during full overlap. The illustration uses approximately constant transverse velocity over the short crossing, so z=∣ut∣/R⋆​ with mid-transit at t=0. Analytic models also handle limb darkening. [ref].
The simple exoplanet transit depth formula assumes constant stellar brightness, no limb darkening or starspots, negligible light from the exoplanet, and no transit dilution by other light. It describes full overlap in a non-grazing exoplanet transit, requiring b<1−k for a full-overlap interval. A grazing exoplanet transit never hides the whole planetary disc against the star, and limb darkening weights different blocked areas differently. Repeating dips are valuable evidence, but eclipsing binary stars and starspots can imitate parts of the signal, so confirmation and joint modelling matter.
