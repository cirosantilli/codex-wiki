Wavefront

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Physical optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 6
A wavefront connects points with the same wave phase at one specified time. It is a surface, rather than the trajectory of a particular piece of light. For a scalar monochromatic wave written as u(r,t)=A(r)cosΦ(r,t), a wavefront is a connected level set of the phase function Φ; different surfaces can share the same wave phase modulo 2π. A plane wave has planar wavefronts. An outward wave from an ideal point source in a uniform medium has spheres as wavefronts, while an optical lens can turn nearly planar wavefronts into converging ones. The wavefront describes wave phase, not wave amplitude: drawing it does not say how bright the beam is. [ref]
The relation to light rays becomes precise in a stationary medium with isotropy, negligible loss and a real positive scalar refractive index n(r,ω). In the geometrical optics limit, spatial changes in the refractive index and wave amplitude are slow on the local wavelength scale. For a single smooth propagation branch, write
Φ(r,t)=k0​S(r,ω)−ωt+ϕ0​,k0​=cω​,∣∇S∣2=n2.
(60)
Here ω>0 is the angular frequency, c is the vacuum speed of light, S has units of length, and the last relation is the optical eikonal equation. The local wavevector is k=∇Φ=k0​∇S. Its direction is normal to a smooth wavefront, since a gradient is normal to its level sets. A forward light ray has unit tangent vector dr/ds=∇S/n, so it follows this normal under the stated assumptions. Along that light ray, dS/ds=∇S⋅(dr/ds)=n: the change in S is the optical path length. This is the short geometric argument linking wavefronts, light rays and optical path length. [ref]
The diagram shows incident and transmitted branches separately, with the reflected branch omitted. Its stipulated refractive indices are n1​=1 and n2​=3/2, and its incident angle is 30∘ to the interface normal. At a stationary interface, the frequency and tangential wavevector agree on the two sides: Snell's law gives sinθ2​=1/3, hence θ2​≈19.4712∘. The wavefronts turn while their normal spacing decreases from λ0​ to 2λ0​/3, where λ0​ is the vacuum wavelength. More explicitly, with x normal to the interface and y tangential, take S1​=3​x/2+y/2 for x<0 and S2​=2​x+y/2 for x>0. These functions agree at x=0; the drawn wavefronts are Sj​=mλ0​ at t=0 with ϕ0​=0. This is an exact piecewise plane wave phase construction for the stipulated transparent media, not measured material data. [ref]
The normal-to-wavefront rule requires its assumptions. In a medium with anisotropy, the wavevector remains normal to a smooth wavefront, but the group velocity and energy flux need not follow it. [ref] Near a wave caustic, where multiple light rays meet, one smooth phase function may no longer describe the field; diffraction and the full wave amplitude matter. A wavefront is also not generally a surface of equal pulse arrival time. In a medium with optical dispersion, a monochromatic wave phase moves with the phase velocity, while a sufficiently narrow wave packet follows the group velocity. Even identical optical path lengths at one frequency can give different group delays. The usual equal-travel-time picture needs a common launch reference and a nondispersive ray model; an arbitrary broadband disturbance has no single monochromatic wavefront.[ref]
