Strong law of large numbers (Xn​a.s.​μ)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Convergence of random variables Almost sure convergence
OurBigBook.com Words: 461 Articles: 2
Let X1​,X2​,… be real independent and identically distributed random variables with E∣X1​∣<∞, and let μ=E[X1​] be their expected value. Their sample mean satisfies
Xn​=n1​∑j=1n​Xj​a.s.​μ.
(2)
This is almost sure convergence: with probability one, an entire infinite sequence of observations has averages that eventually stay within every prescribed positive tolerance of μ. The variance need not be finite. The strong law of large numbers implies the weak law of large numbers, which asserts convergence in probability at each increasing sample size.
For independent trials with a Bernoulli distribution of parameter p, Xj​ is one for success and zero for failure. Then Xn​ is the observed success proportion, and the strong law of large numbers says Xn​→p almost surely. This turns a theoretical probability into a quantity one can estimate by repetition. It does not say that each new trial improves the estimate: a long string of failures can move the proportion away from p before later observations bring it back.
The strong law of large numbers gives the limiting location of the sample mean, while the central limit theorem describes its fluctuations when the variance is finite and positive. The two assertions can hold together:
Xn​a.s.​μ,σn​(Xn​−μ)​d​N(0,1).
(3)
The first error shrinks to zero; the second has been enlarged enough to retain a nontrivial probability distribution. The second assertion is convergence in distribution, not a claim that the enlarged error along one sequence approaches a particular normal random variable.
The assumptions make a practical difference. If every observation repeats the same nonconstant Bernoulli random variable, Xj​=X1​, then Xn​=X1​ for all n: identical probability distributions alone do not make repeated observations informative. For independent observations with a Standard Cauchy distribution, the Cauchy sample mean has an unchanged distribution, so it does not concentrate around a constant. Its expected value is undefined, and E∣X1​∣=∞; neither example contradicts the stated strong law of large numbers.
A brief proof outline in the case of finite variance uses the Kolmogorov maximal inequality for the centered partial sums. The probability that their maximum up to 2m exceeds ε2m is at most σ2/(ε22m). These bounds are summable, so the Borel-Cantelli lemma controls all sufficiently large such blocks and hence every intermediate sample size. The general case E∣X1​∣<∞ requires truncation to control large observations; the argument using finite variance alone does not prove that full version.
