Bounded monotone sequences have no universal convergence rate

 ... Real analysis Sequence and series Sequence Monotone sequence Bounded monotone sequence theorem Error bounds for bounded monotone sequences
OurBigBook.com Words: 238
Even restricting to increasing sequences in [0,1] with limit of a sequence one gives no common stopping index. For any positive integer K, define
an​={0,1−2−(n−K),​n≤K,n>K.​
(66)
This is an increasing bounded sequence converging to one, but its absolute error is one through index K. Since K can exceed any proposed index, the bounded monotone sequence theorem alone cannot say when the absolute error will fall below 1/2. A zero difference between two successive terms is also insufficient: the initial plateau eventually ends.
There is no universal eventual rate either. Let (rn​) be any proposed positive nonincreasing sequence tending to zero, with rn​≤1. Set
an​=1−rn​​.
(67)
Then (an​) is increasing, lies in [0,1], and converges to one, but for every sufficiently large n its absolute error satisfies 1−an​=rn​​>rn​. Indeed, rn​​/rn​=1/rn​​→∞, so no fixed constant multiple of rn​ bounds the absolute error eventually. If a proposed rate is not nonincreasing, replace it, after a finite initial segment if necessary, by the decreasing envelope sn​=sup{rk​:k≥n}; this also tends to zero, and the same construction defeats the original rate. This is an original construction, rather than a quantitative claim attributed to the qualitative theorem.
For a concrete slow example, an​=1−1/log(n+2) increases to one, where log is the natural logarithm. Its absolute error is 1/log(n+2), so making it less than ε requires n+2>exp(1/ε), using the exponential function. At ε=0.1, this first happens at n=22025. The reciprocal and geometric examples in increasing and decreasing approximations to a common limit meet the same tolerance at n=11 and n=4, respectively. All three obey the same bounded monotone sequence theorem.
