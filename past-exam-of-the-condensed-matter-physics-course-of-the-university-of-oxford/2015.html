Take one conduction electron per atom and a three-dimensional free electron gas, with spin degeneracy two and electron mass me​. State counting inside the occupied Fermi sphere gives
n=(2π)32​34πkF3​​=3π2kF3​​.
(1)
The highest occupied energy at zero temperature is EF​=ℏ2kF2​/(2me​), so
TF​=2me​kB​ℏ2​(3π2n)2/3​.
(2)
This relates the atomic number density to the Fermi temperature only because monovalency and the free-electron dispersion have been assumed. A real energy band can require a different density of states and band effective mass.
For the isotropic Debye model, count three acoustic polarizations up to one spherical wavevector cutoff qD​. The number of vibrational modes per volume is
3(2π)31​34πqD3​​=3n,qD​=(6π2n)1/3.
(3)
With ω=cq and ℏωD​=kB​θD​,
θD​=kB​ℏc​(6π2n)1/3​.
(4)
The effective c is the Debye sound speed: matching the low-frequency mode density of one longitudinal and two transverse branches requires 3/c3=1/cL3​+2/cT3​. It is not generally the arithmetic average of their speeds. The cutoff preserves the mode count; it need not coincide with the actual nonspherical Brillouin zone boundary.

Pauli exclusion principle prevents most filled electronic states from changing occupation at T≪TF​. Only a shell of width of order kB​T around the Fermi energy participates. Since the density of states near that energy is of order n/EF​ per volume and energy, the fraction of active electrons is of order kB​T/EF​=T/TF​. Their typical change of energy is of order kB​T, so the thermal energy increment per volume scales as
Δue​∼nTF​T​kB​T.
(5)
Differentiating with respect to temperature gives the electronic heat capacity
Ce​=AnkB​TF​T​​.
(6)
The simple shell argument fixes the linear law, not its coefficient. A Sommerfeld expansion using the spin-summed free-electron density of states g(EF​)=3n/(2EF​) gives Ce​=(π2/3)g(EF​)kB2​T, hence A=π2/2, consistent with the supplied value. The result excludes strong band-structure or interaction corrections and requires a degenerate electron population.

The Debye model treats the crystal as an isotropic elastic continuum with independent harmonic vibrational modes. There are three acoustic polarizations, approximated by linear dispersions and a common effective speed. A cutoff is chosen to retain 3N modes for N atoms. The model neglects anharmonic interactions and the detailed high-wavevector dispersion. In a multi-atom primitive cell, optical branches require additional treatment; copper has one atom per primitive cell even though its conventional cubic cell contains four atoms.
A frequency interval contains, per volume,
gph​(ω)dω=3(2π)34πq2dq​=2π2c33ω2​dω=ωD3​9nω2​dω.
(7)

uph​=ωD3​9nℏ​∫0ωD​​eℏω/(kB​T)−1ω3dω​=9nkB​T(θD​T​)3∫0θD​/T​ex−1x3dx​.

The structure factor is the coherent scattering amplitude of the contents of a unit cell at a specified momentum transfer. Contributions from its atoms add with phases fixed by their positions. At a reciprocal lattice vector indexed by Miller indices, the crystal basis structure factor is
Shkl​=j=1∑N​fj​(Q)exp[2πi(hxj​+kyj​+lzj​)]​.
(15)
Changing every phase sign conjugates the amplitude for real scattering factors and leaves the intensity unchanged. The positions here are fractions of the chosen cell edges. An atomic basis of a crystal should not be confused with a set of primitive translation vectors.
In kinematic elastic scattering the diffraction intensity of a Bragg reflection is proportional to ∣Shkl​∣2, after including geometrical, polarization, thermal-displacement and experimental factors. Powder integrated intensities also include multiplicity. The many-cell interference factor selects reciprocal-lattice vectors satisfying the diffraction geometry; the unit-cell factor determines their relative amplitudes and additional extinctions. Ring positions determine spacings through Bragg's law, while intensity patterns and systematic absences constrain lattice centering and atomic arrangements. Intensity loses the overall complex phase, so structure determination is not a direct measurement of every individual atomic phase.
X-rays scatter from electron density, giving an atomic factor that changes with momentum transfer and chemical charge distribution. Neutrons scatter coherently from nuclei with bound coherent scattering lengths, which need not track atomic number; magnetic neutron scattering is an additional channel in magnetic materials. Their different elemental/isotopic contrasts make the two probes complementary.

Symmetry-related permutations and signs belong to the same powder ring, not additional rings. For Al, the basis contains one atom, S=Ffcc​fAl​. Thus

For the stated KCl basis,
SKCl​=Ffcc​[fK​+(−1)hfCl​].
(19)
The all-even reflections add the two atomic factors; all-odd reflections subtract them. Ionic K+ and Cl− each have 18 electrons, giving similar X-ray amplitudes. Their difference is much smaller than their sum, so the 111 and 311 rings are weak. Consequently the four strong rings are
KCl:200, 220, 222, 400​.
(20)
This is an accidental diffraction extinction from equal atomic scattering factors in the equal-factor limit, not an extra exact fcc selection rule. Isoelectronic ions can have different radial electron distributions, so real odd reflections need not vanish exactly. Even the cruder neutral-atom estimate fK​≃19, fCl​≃17 makes the odd/even basis-intensity ratio only (2/36)2≃0.0031, before other ring-intensity factors.
For Si's displaced identical basis atoms,
SSi​=Ffcc​fSi​[1+eiπ(h+k+l)/2].
(21)
For all-odd indices, the bracket is 1±i, with squared magnitude 2. For all-even indices it vanishes when h+k+l=2(mod4), and has squared magnitude 4 when the sum is a multiple of four. Thus 200 and 222 vanish, whereas 111, 220, 311 and 400 survive:
Si:111, 220, 311, 400​.
(22)
These are the basis extinctions of the ideal diamond cubic structure. Intensity strengths within each surviving set can still differ because of atomic factors, multiplicity and geometry.

Replacing X-rays by neutrons of the same wavelength keeps the geometric Bragg positions, but replaces electron-density amplitudes by nuclear coherent scattering lengths. For Al all sites still carry the same nonzero amplitude, so its six reflections remain. For Si the identical two-site basis still has the same destructive-interference conditions, so its four reflections remain. Isotopic randomness can add diffuse/incoherent scattering but does not create a new periodic basis with different systematic Bragg absences.
K and Cl, however, have different nuclear amplitudes despite their isoelectronic ionic electron clouds. For natural isotopic composition, bK​=3.67 fm and bCl​=9.577 fm. The all-odd/all-even basis-intensity ratio becomes
∣bK​+bCl​∣2∣bK​−bCl​∣2​=0.199,
(23)
which is no longer the strong X-ray cancellation. The two odd rings should therefore be observable with suitable sensitivity, giving
NAl​=6,NKCl​=6,NSi​=4​.
(24)
The numerical contrast uses the NIST bound coherent lengths.[ref] Counts here mean allowed and reasonably observable rings in the specified range, assuming sufficient angular coverage and resolution; no theory alone fixes an instrument's intensity threshold. These nonmagnetic examples use nuclear elastic scattering, not magnetic scattering.

A phonon is one quantum of a collective harmonic normal mode of a crystal. A mode labelled by wavevector and branch has energies Em​=ℏω(m+1/2), with bosonic occupation statistics. The crystal momentum ℏk is defined modulo a reciprocal lattice vector; it is not generally the mechanical momentum of one atom. The phonon dispersion relation assigns its frequency and polarization to the wavevector and branch.
Inelastic neutron scattering can measure this relation in a single crystal. Determine the incident neutron energy and wavevector, scatter at a chosen angle, and analyze the outgoing energy by time of flight or a spectrometer analyzer. Energy and crystal-momentum conservation give
Ei​−Ef​=±ℏωs​(k),ki​−kf​=G±k.
(25)
The signs distinguish phonon creation and annihilation with consistent momentum convention. Repeating measurements at different momentum transfers maps peaks in the dynamic scattering response into phonon branches. The measured intensity depends on occupation, scattering factors and the projection of the mode polarization along the momentum transfer; a weak or missing peak need not mean that the corresponding mode does not exist.

An intrinsic semiconductor has thermally generated conduction electrons and valence holes, with n=p=ni​ at equilibrium in the ideal undoped neutral material. Their abundance is controlled by its band gap, temperature and band density of states.
An extrinsic semiconductor has its mobile carrier populations substantially modified by electrically active impurities. A donor impurity can supply electrons, producing n-type material; an acceptor impurity can capture a valence electron and leave a mobile electron hole, producing p-type material. Compensation and incomplete ionization can modify these simple counts. At low temperature dopant freeze-out can occur; at sufficiently high temperature intrinsic pair production can overwhelm a fixed dopant density. Doping and the dominant transport regime are related but are not interchangeable descriptions at every temperature.

Assume nondegenerate three-dimensional parabolic band edges, a fixed gap and approximately temperature-independent density-of-states effective masses. Let μ be the chemical potential, Ec​ and Ev​ the band edges, and Nc​,Nv​ their effective density of states. Boltzmann occupation gives
n=Nc​e−(Ec​−μ)/(kB​T),p=Nv​e−(μ−Ev​)/(kB​T),Eg​=Ec​−Ev​.
(33)
For one spin-degenerate spherical conduction extremum, direct integration of gc​(E)∝E−Ec​​ gives
Nc​=2(2πℏ2mc∗​kB​T​)3/2,
(34)
and similarly for the valence band with its appropriate density-of-states mass. Valley and additional band degeneracies can be included in these prefactors. The T3/2 factor follows from the integral ∫0∞​ϵ​e−ϵ/(kB​T)dϵ∝T3/2.
Multiplication removes the chemical potential:
np=Nc​Nv​e−Eg​/(kB​T).
(35)
The semiconductor mass-action law combined with intrinsic charge neutrality, n=p, therefore gives
ni​=Nc​Nv​​e−Eg​/(2kB​T)=AT3/2e−Eg​/(2kB​T)​.
(36)
The intrinsic chemical potential is not necessarily exactly midgap: μi​=(Ec​+Ev​)/2+(kB​T/2)ln(Nv​/Nc​). Equal effective masses are unnecessary for the displayed carrier-density law. A changing gap, effective mass or degeneracy would make the prefactor or activation law temperature dependent.

The source's description of electrons as intrinsic silicon's majority carriers needs qualification. Intrinsic neutrality gives equal electron and hole concentrations, not an electron density majority. Electrons can nevertheless dominate conductivity and the Hall response because their mobility is larger. For two weak-field carriers,

The sign must become positive, so use p-type acceptor impurities, such as boron substituting for tetravalent silicon. Assume uncompensated, fully ionized acceptors of density NA​, nondegenerate carriers and equilibrium throughout the interval. Charge neutrality and mass action give
p−n=NA​,np=ni2​,p=2NA​+NA2​+4ni2​​​,n=pni2​​​.

NA​≫ni​(500)≃4.0×1020 m−3=4.0×1014 cm−3​.

The order-of-magnitude onset is NA​∼4×1020 m−3, but equality is not a flat plateau: at NA​=ni​(500), p/NA​=(1+5​)/2=1.618, changing the hole-only Hall coefficient by about 38 percent. The word approximately does not define a unique sharp minimum without a tolerance.
For NA​≫ni​, p≃NA​[1+(ni​/NA​)2], so the hole-only RH​≃1/(eNA​) varies by a fraction of order (ni​/NA​)2.
