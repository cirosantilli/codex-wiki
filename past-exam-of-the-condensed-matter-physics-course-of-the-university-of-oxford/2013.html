The sites form a face-centered cubic lattice decorated by an identical two-atom atomic basis of a crystal, at fractional displacements 0 and δ=(1/4,1/4,1/4). This is the diamond cubic structure; the eight atoms in its conventional cube are not eight independent Bravais-lattice points.
For identical silicon atomic scattering factors f, the conventional-cell structure factor separates into the FCC factor and the two-site motif factor:
Fhkl​=f[1+(−1)h+k+(−1)h+l+(−1)k+l][1+eiπ(h+k+l)/2].
(1)
The first factor vanishes unless h,k,l are all even or all odd. For all-even indices, the motif also vanishes if h+k+l=4r+2; it survives if the sum is divisible by four. All-odd indices survive, with motif modulus 2​. Thus (100) and (110) are absent by centering, (200) is absent by the diamond basis, and (111) and (220) are allowed.
For a cubic cell, dhkl​=a/h2+k2+l2​. At fixed X-ray wavelength, Bragg's law makes the smallest scattering angles correspond to the smallest allowed positive values of h2+k2+l2. These start with 3 and 8, so
first line {111},second line {220}.​
(2)
The braces denote whole powder families, including their symmetry-equivalent plane normals. The next family is {311}, with squared-index sum 11; using the FCC rule alone would incorrectly insert a (200) line before (220).

I220​I111​​≃12⋅648⋅32​=31​​

At fixed radiation wavelength, differentiating Bragg's law gives cotθdθ=−da/a. With constant linear thermal expansion coefficient αL​,
aΔa​≃αL​ΔT=0.0018,Δψ=2Δθ≃−2αL​ΔTtanθ.​
(9)

Δψ111​≃−0.0416∘,Δψ220​≃−0.0702∘.​
(10)
These are shifts of 2θ, not of the Bragg angle alone. As a finite-step check, using a′=a(1+0.0018) gives ψ′=2arcsin[sin(ψ/2)/(1.0018)]. It produces shifts −0.04151∘ and −0.07008∘, consistent with the first-order estimate. Diffraction thermal-expansion thermometry needs a stable monochromatic X-ray beam, controlled collimation, an accurately calibrated angular scale, and an X-ray-sensitive detector with enough counts to fit reproducible line centroids. Crystal monochromators or appropriate energy selection are relevant here; an apparatus for visible-light interference does not directly measure these X-ray peaks. Sample displacement and angular-zero drift should be checked with a suitable reference standard.
The first-order angular sensitivities are about 6.93×10−5 and 1.17×10−4 degrees per kelvin, respectively. A one-kelvin inference therefore requires comparable centroid stability, with high count statistics and control of systematic errors; a centroid can be fitted more precisely than the line width. Fractional wavelength drift also mimics lattice expansion, so the desired temperature precision constrains Δλ/λ relative to αL​ΔT.
The powder must be at a known uniform temperature and allowed to equilibrate. Mechanical stress, clamping, composition changes, oxidation and phase changes can also shift or broaden peaks. Accurate work requires a calibrated a(T), including the actual temperature dependence of the thermal expansion coefficient, rather than assuming the supplied constant describes every temperature exactly. Using both lines provides a consistency check on a common cubic expansion.

Put C=αq2/(4πϵ0​) with α=2ln2. Relative to a chosen positive ion, successive neighbours have alternating charge. The sum of its interactions with all other ions is −C/R. Summing this over all 2N ions would count every pair twice, so the total electrostatic energy is −NC/R, or −C/R per ion pair.
There are 2N nearest-neighbour bonds in a periodic chain of N pairs; boundary corrections are negligible for large N. If A/Rn is the energy of each such bond, the repulsive contribution per ion pair is 2A/Rn. Therefore the alternating-charge chain cohesive energy is
u(R)=NU​=−4πϵ0​Rαq2​+Rn2A​.​
(11)
The factor two in the short-range term counts two bonds per ion pair. If a repulsion parameter were instead defined as the whole pair's repulsive coefficient, it would equal 2A in this convention; changing that definition must not silently change the equilibrium formula. The absolute zero of energy is separated ions for a genuinely decaying repulsion.

For a repulsive short-range potential and a stable finite minimum, assume A>0 and integer n>1. Setting u′(R)=C/R2−2nA/Rn+1=0 gives
R0​=(C2nA​)1/(n−1)=(αq28πϵ0​nA​)1/(n−1).​
(12)
At this point 2A/R0n​=C/(nR0​), so
u0​=−R0​C​(1−n1​),u′′(R0​)=R03​(n−1)C​>0.​
(13)
Here u′′ denotes the second derivative of the energy u, not a displacement variable. The negative equilibrium energy is the cohesive energy per pair relative to infinite separation.
The qualifiers on A,n matter: an arbitrary real coefficient and integer exponent do not automatically describe stabilizing repulsion. For instance A<0,n>1 gives collapse as R→0, whereas n=1 gives u=(2A−C)/R and has no isolated finite equilibrium. The displayed minimum therefore applies to the physically repulsive, faster-than-Coulomb branch of the stated model.

For a one-dimensional free-electron gas of length L, allowed wave vectors have spacing 2π/L. Including two spin states and occupied wave vectors from −kF​ to +kF​,
Ne​=22πL​(2kF​),kF​=2πne​​,EF​=2me​ℏ2kF2​​=8me​π2ℏ2ne2​​.​
(14)
The ground-state kinetic energy is found by integrating rather than assigning every electron the Fermi energy:
Ke​=22πL​∫−kF​kF​​2me​ℏ2k2​dk=3πme​Lℏ2kF3​​=31​Ne​EF​.​
(15)
Charge neutrality gives Ne​=ZN because each positive ion has charge +Ze. Thus Ke​=ZNEF​/3. Since the ion separation is 2R, L≃2RN and ne​=Z/(2R). These are one-dimensional number densities and state counts; using a three-dimensional kF​∝n1/3 relation here would change the model. The results concern the ground-state Fermi-gas contribution, neglecting electron interactions beyond the supplied electrostatic energy.

Use the supplied effective electrostatic term and enforce charge neutrality while varying the chain length: R=Z/(2ne​), with N and Z fixed. The total energy per positive ion becomes
ε(ne​)=−4πϵ0​RαZ2e2​+3ZEF​​=−2πϵ0​αZe2​ne​+24me​Zπ2ℏ2​ne2​.
(16)
The positive quadratic term supplies degeneracy pressure against compression. Differentiating gives the neutral one-dimensional metal equilibrium density,
ne∗​=π3ϵ0​ℏ26αme​e2​=π2aB​24α​≃6.37×1010m−1,​
(17)
where aB​=4πϵ0​ℏ2/(me​e2) is the Bohr radius. The second derivative Zπ2ℏ2/(12me​)>0 verifies a minimum. Within this specified toy energy, the minimizing electron density is independent of Z; instead the equilibrium ion spacing is 2R∗=Z/ne∗​.
At the minimum the electrostatic contribution is minus twice the kinetic contribution, so ε∗​=−ZEF∗​/3. These follow from the given electrostatic approximation, not from a full self-consistent calculation of a real one-dimensional metal. In particular, additional Coulomb terms cannot be appended without the corresponding neutral-background subtraction that defines the supplied finite energy.

Let a be the equilibrium spacing of this monatomic chain and uj​ the displacement from ja. The right nearest-neighbour bond exerts K(uj+1​−uj​) and the left bond K(uj−1​−uj​). The two next-nearest-neighbour bonds similarly connect j to j±2. Thus
mu¨j​=K(uj+1​+uj−1​−2uj​)+G(uj+2​+uj−2​−2uj​).​
(18)
Every uniform displacement gives zero force, as translation symmetry requires. There is one displacement coordinate per primitive cell. Adding further-neighbour springs does not introduce a second atom or turn this into a diatomic-chain problem.

Substitute the traveling normal mode uj​=uexp[i(kja−ωt)]. Combining the opposite-neighbour phases gives
mω2=2K(1−coska)+2G(1−cos2ka),
(19)
so the positive-frequency branch is
ω0​(k)=2mKsin2(ka/2)+Gsin2(ka)​​,∣k∣≤π/a.​
(20)
For positive spring constants this is a stable acoustic branch. At long wavelengths,
ω02​≃ma2(K+4G)​k2,cs​=amK+4G​​.​
(21)
The next-nearest-neighbour bond spans twice the distance, hence contributes the factor four. Relative to G=0, positive G increases the sound speed by 1+4G/K​. The chain still has one acoustic branch and no optical branch.
The group speed near k=0 and the maximum frequency need not change in the same way. The zone-edge value is always ω0​(π/a)=2K/m​, because the next-nearest-neighbour sites then move together. For G>K/4 the frequency maximum moves inside the zone, with cos(ka)=−K/(4G); the small-k sound speed nevertheless keeps increasing.

For passive friction Γ>0, the time dependence of each spatial normal coordinate follows its damping regime:
  • If ω0​>γ, it is underdamped: uk​(t)=e−γt[Acos(Ωt)+Bsin(Ωt)], with Ω=ω02​−γ2​. The amplitude envelope has decay time 1/γ=2m/Γ; cycle-averaged energy decays as e−2γt.
  • If ω0​=γ, it is critically damped: uk​(t)=(A+Bt)e−γt.
  • If 0<ω0​<γ, it is overdamped: uk​(t)=Ae−[γ−γ2−ω02​​]t+Be−[γ+γ2−ω02​​]t. Both terms decay without oscillation, with two different rates.
For any fixed positive damping, sufficiently small nonzero k is overdamped because ω0​≃cs​∣k∣. The slow branch then has ω≃−i(mcs2​/Γ)k2, a diffusive long-wavelength relaxation. At exactly k=0, uniform translation has no restoring force, so u0​(t)=A+Be−Γt/m and the constant displacement need not decay. Thus saying every amplitude simply decays as e−Γt/(2m) is valid only for the underdamped envelope, not for all wave numbers.

In the SI linear-response convention M=χH​H, a diamagnetic contribution has negative susceptibility: the induced moment opposes the applied field. Orbital motion supplies a familiar microscopic mechanism, and closed-shell atoms can be diamagnetic even without permanent moments. Paramagnetism has positive susceptibility, with induced magnetization along the field; thermal alignment of permanent localized moments is one mechanism, while conduction-electron spin response is another.
Neither sign alone is a definition of ferromagnetic ordering. An ordinary paramagnetic phase has no spontaneous zero-field magnetization. A diamagnetic background can coexist with a larger paramagnetic contribution in the same specimen. Curie's inverse-temperature law describes an appropriate localized-moment model, not every possible paramagnet.

Linear Curie behavior fails gradually when the Zeeman energy of a substantial moment is comparable to thermal energy. Its appropriate scale is
gμB​JB∼kB​T,Bnl​∼gμB​JkB​T​.​
(28)
At T=300K, kB​T/μB​=446.6T. Thus Bnl​∼446.6/(gJ)T: about 223T for g=2,J=1, or 447T for a spin-one-half moment with g=2. Larger moments saturate at smaller fields. The source does not specify numerical g,J, so it determines this parametric scale rather than one universal room-temperature threshold. At sufficiently large field the moment tends toward gμB​J and cannot keep increasing linearly with B.

e OurBigBook.com
Room-temperature iron is ferromagnetic: interactions among its electronic moments produce spontaneous local order. The independent-ion Curie law therefore needs an interaction and an ordered-state description. In a Weiss mean-field approximation, replace the applied field by Heff​=H+ΛM. Above the ordering temperature, linearizing the single-ion response yields
M=TCχ​​(H+ΛM),χH​=T−TC​Cχ​​,TC​=ΛCχ​.​
(29)
This is the Curie–Weiss approximation on the paramagnetic side. Merely substituting room temperature below TC​ into that high-temperature formula would produce a negative response and is not a valid model of stable iron.
Below TC​, use the full nonlinear moment response to the effective field; it has nonzero zero-field solutions. On a chosen ordered branch the local small-field response has the form M≃Ms​(T)+χdiff​(T)H, rather than M∝H/T through the origin. Macroscopic magnetic domains and hysteresis further determine the measured net moment. Exchange interactions, arising from electronic quantum statistics and Coulomb correlations, supply the alignment energy; bare dipolar fields alone do not explain ordinary iron's strong ferromagnetism. A localized-ion Weiss model describes the qualitative ordering, while iron's itinerant electronic structure limits a literal independent-fixed-ion interpretation.
