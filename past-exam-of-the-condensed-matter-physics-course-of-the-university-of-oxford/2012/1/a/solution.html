The incident X-ray drives the electrons of an atom. Away from an absorption edge, and when the photon energy greatly exceeds the relevant electronic binding energies but is small compared with me​c2, each electron has approximately the same Thomson scattering amplitude. The much heavier nucleus makes a negligible contribution because its acceleration is much smaller.
For elastic scattering, the amplitudes from different bound electrons add coherently. In units of the single-electron amplitude, the atomic scattering factor is the Fourier transform of the bound-electron number density:
f(q)=∫ne​(r)eiq⋅rd3r,f(0)=Ne​.
(1)
Consequently, when the electron cloud is not well resolved by the scattering vector, its internal phase differences are small and
f≃Ne​,I∝Ne2​.​
(2)
This is an approximation to the coherent scattering amplitude, not an assertion that independently scattered intensities add as Ne​. At larger momentum transfer, destructive interference within the cloud reduces f; near absorption edges, the atomic scattering factor acquires dispersive corrections. Inelastic scattering is a separate contribution. In an ionic crystal, use the ion's bound-electron count rather than automatically using the neutral atom's atomic number.
