A Bravais lattice is an infinite periodic set of equivalent points R=n1​a1​+n2​a2​+n3​a3​, with integer ni​ and independent primitive translations ai​. It describes translation symmetry, without assigning a chemical species to a point. An atomic basis of a crystal is the motif of atoms, with their species and relative positions, repeated identically at every lattice point. The resulting crystal structure consists of the lattice decorated by that basis; the basis can contain several atoms even when the lattice is primitive.
A primitive unit cell tiles space under lattice translations and contains exactly one lattice point, counting shared boundary points fractionally. Its volume is ∣a1​⋅(a2​×a3​)∣. A conventional unit cell is chosen to display useful crystal symmetry and may contain several lattice points. Thus a centered conventional cell is not primitive, but the crystal still has a primitive cell. Neither cell size nor the number of dots drawn on its boundary should be confused with the number of atoms in the crystal basis.

b OurBigBook.com
Use fractional coordinates relative to conventional cubic vectors (ax^,ay^​,az^). A body-centered cubic lattice has representative points
(0,0,0),(21​,21​,21​),
(1)
so its conventional cell contains two lattice points: eight shared corners contribute one and the interior point contributes one. A face-centered cubic lattice has
(0,0,0),(0,21​,21​),(21​,0,21​),(21​,21​,0),
(2)
so its conventional cell contains four: one from the shared corners and three from six half-shared faces.
For N atoms at fractional positions (xj​,yj​,zj​), the geometric structure factor and the atomic-factor-weighted amplitude are respectively
Ghkl​=∑j=1N​e2πi(hxj​+kyj​+lzj​),Fhkl​=∑j=1N​fj​(G)e2πi(hxj​+kyj​+lzj​).
(3)
For identical atoms F=fG. Applying the conventional-cell lattice factors gives
GBCC​=1+(−1)h+k+l={2,0,​h+k+l even,h+k+l odd,​​
(4)
and
GFCC​=1+(−1)k+l+(−1)h+l+(−1)h+k={4,0,​h,k,l all even or all odd,mixed parity.​​
(5)
These are lattice selection rules; an additional nontrivial crystal basis structure factor can extinguish further allowed reflections.
Of the eight equally represented parity triples, four satisfy the BCC condition and two the FCC condition. Thus the fractions among integer index triples are 1/2 and 1/4, respectively. This reflects the two and four equivalent lattice points in the conventional cells: their reciprocal lattices occupy corresponding subsets of the conventional reciprocal grids. In primitive reciprocal coordinates every integer triple denotes a reciprocal-lattice vector; lattice-centering absences are then built into the grid rather than an extra rule. A multi-atom chemical basis can still cause genuine additional absences. If permutations means merely reordering one fixed triple, its allowedness never changes: the quoted fractions concern all index triples, not reordering a chosen (h,k,l).

After compression along z, let the original conventional edges be a,a,c with c=a/2​. This gives exactly the specified volume reduction. The compressed FCC points admit the alternative translation vectors
b1​=2a​(1,1,0),b2​=2a​(−1,1,0),b3​=(0,0,a/2​).
(6)
They are mutually perpendicular, have equal length b=a/2​, and form a right-handed cube. Besides its corners, the lattice has the point
2b1​+b2​+b3​​=(0,a/2,a/(22​)),
(7)
which is the compressed image of an original FCC face centre. All compressed FCC sites are generated by the cube's integer translations and this body-centre coset. Therefore
compressed FCC≡BCC with conventional edge b=a/2​.​
(8)
The old compressed tetragonal conventional cell has volume a3/2​ and four sites; the new BCC cube has half that volume and two sites. This verifies the same physical site density rather than silently losing atoms during the change of cell. The continuous uniaxial deformation is the Bain path, named for Edgar Bain.

The FCC parity rule forbids (100) and (110) and allows (200) and (111). To follow each same plane family through the deformation, use fractional coordinates (x,y,z) in the compressed old cell and (X,Y,Z) in the new cube. The preceding vectors give
x=(X−Y)/2,y=(X+Y)/2,z=Z.
(9)
Thus hx+ky+lz=m becomes HX+KY+LZ=m, with
(H,K,L)=(2h+k​,2k−h​,l).​
(10)
Crucially, this transformation retains the plane spacing and diffraction order, not just a normal direction:
Old FCC reflectionNew-cell reciprocal coordinatesStatus
(100)(21​,−21​,0)Not a new reciprocal-lattice vector; absent
(110)(1,0,0)Odd index sum; absent
(200)(1,−1,0)Even index sum; allowed
(111)(1,0,1)Even index sum; allowed
The two physically allowed reflections remain allowed, and the two absent ones remain absent. Their angles and spacings change under strain; their allowedness as tracked reciprocal vectors does not.
The half-integer entry is deliberate. It cannot be relabeled as the ordinary BCC (11ˉ0) reflection while claiming to track the same diffraction order. Multiplying its indices by two doubles the reciprocal vector and halves the spacing; that new allowed reflection is the image of old FCC (200), not old (100). If only an orientation label is wanted, the old (100) and (200) planes both have the new (11ˉ0) orientation, but are distinct orders. This is the distinction between diffraction indexing under a cell change and merely reducing a plane normal to the smallest integer ratio.

In the spin-degenerate free electron model, a monovalent metal has conduction-electron number density n. The density of wave-vector states is 1/(2π)3 per unit real-space volume, with two spin states at each wave vector. Filling a sphere at zero temperature gives
n=(2π)32​34πkF3​​=3π2kF3​​,TF​=kB​EF​​=2me​kB​ℏ2​(3π2n)2/3.​
(11)
For the Debye model, there are three acoustic polarizations and 3n modes per unit volume in total. Replacing their dispersions by ω=ck with the effective Debye sound speed, mode counting gives
3(2π)31​34πkD3​​=3n,kD​=(6π2n)1/3,θD​=kB​ℏc​(6π2n)1/3.​
(12)
The factor two in the electron count is spin, whereas the factor three in the phonon count is polarization; the two counts are not interchangeable. If branch speeds differ, the Debye effective average obeys c−3=(cl−3​+2ct−3​)/3 in the isotropic approximation.
Dividing gives TF​/θD​=λ(3π2n/2)1/3, where λ=ℏ/(2me​c). A monatomic FCC conventional cell contains four atoms, so n=4/a3. Therefore
θD​TF​​=(6π2)1/3aλ​.​
(13)
Here and below c is the speed of sound, not the speed of light. The Debye cutoff counts acoustic modes in a spherical approximation to the Brillouin zone; it is not an electron-state cutoff.

An electron losing energy to an acoustic phonon has, in a normal process, k′=kF​−kph​, with possible addition of a reciprocal-lattice vector for an Umklapp process. The energy transfer is at most of order ℏωD​=kB​θD​, much smaller than EF​=kB​TF​. Hence the magnitudes change very little even though the momentum direction can change substantially. At T∼θD​≪TF​, thermal vacancies near the Fermi surface allow emission into some lower-energy states; exactly at zero temperature emission into an occupied Fermi sea would be blocked by the Pauli exclusion principle.
Using k′=(1−δ)kF​ and the free-electron dispersion, energy conservation gives
2me​ℏ2kF2​​[1−(1−δ)2]=ℏckph​.
(17)
Since δ≪1, the bracket is 2δ+O(δ2). Rearranging yields
2δ1​kF​kph​​kF​1​≃2me​cℏ​=λ.​
(18)
Equivalently δ≃ℏckph​/(2EF​). For a Debye-cutoff phonon in the preceding model, δ≃θD​/(2TF​)=1/480=0.00208, confirming the approximation. Near-elastic scattering constrains the radial distance in wave-vector space, not the scattering angle alone.

The electron-acoustic recoil length compares electronic recoil with the acoustic quantum energy associated with the same momentum. For transferred wave number q,
Erecoil​(q)=2me​ℏ2q2​,Ephonon​(q)=ℏcq,Ephonon​Erecoil​​=qλ.​
(19)
The dispersions cross at q=1/λ; there the reduced electron de Broglie wavelength is λ and its free-particle group velocity is 2c. Thus λ is the length scale at which an electron's localization/recoil energy and a corresponding acoustic excitation energy become comparable. At qλ≫1 the electron recoil energy is large compared with the phonon energy; this explains the separation of electronic and acoustic energy scales when lattice wave numbers exceed 1/λ.
Formally it is half the Reduced Compton wavelength with the speed of light replaced by the speed of sound. That is an analogy of scales, not a relativistic electron mass or a pair-production threshold: sound does not replace the limiting speed in electron dynamics. Also it is a reduced-wavelength convention; an ordinary de Broglie wavelength 2π/q at the crossing is 2πλ. No scattering lifetime appears, so λ is not an electron mean free path.

An intrinsic semiconductor has its carrier populations set primarily by thermal excitation across the band gap; in the ideal pure, neutral case its conduction-electron and hole densities are equal, n=p=ni​. An extrinsic semiconductor has carrier populations significantly controlled by donor or acceptor impurities, producing predominantly n-type semiconductor or p-type semiconductor behavior. Chemical purity alone does not fix conductivity independently of temperature and gap size.
The positive charge carrier mobility is the magnitude of mean drift speed per unit weak electric field. For electrons ve​=−μe​E, for holes vh​=μh​E, and its SI unit is m2V−1s−1. In an isotropic relaxation-time model, μ=∣q∣τ/m∗.
A band effective mass characterizes how an electron wave packet accelerates in an energy band, rather than replacing its vacuum rest mass. In one dimension and its tensor generalization,
m∗1​=ℏ21​dk2d2E​,(M∗−1)ij​=ℏ21​∂ki​∂kj​∂2E​.​
(20)
These follow by differentiating vi​=ℏ−1∂E/∂ki​ and using ℏk˙j​=Fj​. Effective masses can be anisotropic and electron band curvature can be negative.

An electron hole is a missing electron in an otherwise almost filled valence band. A completely filled band has no net transport current: its velocities cancel over the Brillouin zone. Describing a few missing states as mobile excitations is therefore simpler than following almost every occupied electron state.
Near an isotropic valence maximum, write the electron dispersion as Ev​(k)=Ev​(0)−ℏ2k2/(2mh​) with mh​>0. The electron curvature mass is negative there. Removing that electron creates an excitation with momentum ℏkh​=−ℏk and energy measured upward from the filled reference,
Eh​(kh​)=Ev​(0)−Ev​(−kh​)=2mh​ℏ2kh2​​.
(21)
Thus
mh∗​=−me,valence∗​>0.​
(22)
The sign refers to the positive excitation energy of a missing electron near a maximum, not to negative vacuum mass. A general maximum gives the corresponding positive hole-curvature tensor.[ref]

Removing an electron of charge −e leaves charge +e relative to the filled-band reference. This also reproduces the current. If the missing electron state had velocity ve​(k), subtracting its contribution from the zero current of a filled band gives
Δj=−(−e)ve​(k)=+eve​(k).
(23)
The hole dispersion with kh​=−k gives vh​(kh​)=ve​(k), so
qh​=+e,Δj=qh​vh​.​
(24)
Likewise ℏk˙=−eE becomes ℏk˙h​=+eE. Positive hole charge and positive hole effective mass consistently describe drift along the electric field, while the valence electrons themselves drift through an almost full band. A hole is a quasiparticle, not a proton moving through the crystal.

In the simple transport model, μe​=eτe​/me∗​ and μh​=eτh​/mh∗​. Conduction bands in many common semiconductors have lighter transport masses than the dominant valence hole bands. For comparable momentum-relaxation times, this gives larger electron mobility. It is a common trend, not a theorem: band anisotropy, light and heavy hole populations and different scattering times can alter the comparison. The hole's mass is not the mass of an atom, and crystal translational symmetry does not make its mobility infinite.
For the intrinsic germanium sample, electrons and holes both contribute positive conventional conductivity:
σ=e(nμe​+pμh​)=eni​(μe​+μh​),σ=1/ρ=2Sm−1.
(25)
Therefore
n=p=ni​=(0.5)e(0.36+0.18)1​=2.31×1019m−3.​
(26)
This is the density of each species; the combined carrier number density is 2ni​. Assigning separate densities from the two mobilities would violate intrinsic charge neutrality.

nm​=(0.36×10−9)34​=8.57×1028m−3.

The SI volume magnetic susceptibility is the weak-field response χM​=∂M/∂H, or M/H for a linear isotropic sample with no spontaneous moment. It is dimensionless; a molar susceptibility would be a different quantity. For weak magnetization, B≃μ0​H. Neglect local-field and demagnetizing corrections in this independent-spin model.
A spin-1/2 ion has moment projections ±gμB​/2. The requested coefficient assumes a spin-only g≃2, giving projections ±μB​ and Zeeman energies ∓μB​B. With x=μB​B/(kB​T), the one-ion partition function and magnetization are
Z1​=ex+e−x=2coshx,M=NμB​ex+e−xex−e−x​=NμB​tanhx.
(32)
For ∣x∣≪1, tanhx≃x, so
χM​≃μ0​∂B∂M​=kB​TNμ0​μB2​​.​
(33)
Here N is a number density, as specified, rather than total ion number. The small-field condition is
μB​∣B∣≪kB​T,​
(34)
meaning the Zeeman splitting 2μB​B is small compared with the thermal scale up to an irrelevant order-one convention. It is not a temperature-independent absolute upper bound on B. For a different g value the coefficient contains (gμB​/2)2 instead of μB2​.

A point magnetic dipole μ produces
B(r)=4πr3μ0​​[3(μ⋅r^)r^−μ].
(35)
For ∣μ∣∼μB​ and neighbour separation r∼0.2nm, its characteristic magnitude is
Bdip​∼4πr3μ0​μB​​≃0.116T.​
(36)
The axial field is twice this scale; the exact orientation and crystal geometry are unspecified. The corresponding pair magnetic dipole interaction has energy scale Udip​∼μB​Bdip​≃1.08×10−24J, giving
Tdip​∼kB​Udip​​≃0.078K∼0.1K.​
(37)
An axial orientation changes this to about 0.16K. A real ordering temperature depends on a lattice sum, symmetry and fluctuations, so this is a nearest-neighbour energy estimate, not a complete ordering calculation. It establishes that ordinary dipolar coupling is far too weak to explain a thousand-kelvin ferromagnetic transition.

The inverse-susceptibility curve has a Curie–Weiss form above TC​ and vanishes on approach to it. The material can have spontaneous magnetization for T<TC​, including the zero-temperature limit, whereas for T>TC​ its zero-field equilibrium state is paramagnetic. At a continuous transition the spontaneous moment tends to zero at TC​; divergence of differential susceptibility there is not a finite saturated permanent moment.
A simple Weiss molecular field model takes the effective internal field intensity to be Heff​=H+ΛM, with positive coupling Λ. Let C be the independent-moment Curie constant. Linearizing the single-ion response on the high-temperature side gives
M=TC​(H+ΛM),χM​=T−TC​C​,TC​=ΛC,χM​1​=CT−TC​​.​
(38)
This explains the straight upper branch and its positive temperature intercept. At TC​ the zero-field linearized response diverges; finite field, finite size and critical fluctuations limit its use. For a spin-only two-state mean-field model the full equation is m=tanh[(TC​/T)m+μB​μ0​H/(kB​T)], with m=M/(NμB​); below TC​ it has nonzero zero-field solutions.
An ordering scale near 1000K corresponds to kB​TC​≃0.086eV, about four orders of magnitude larger than the preceding dipolar pair scale. Exchange interaction can supply such energies: the Pauli antisymmetry of overlapping electron states changes their spatial correlations and hence Coulomb and kinetic energies according to their spin configuration. An effective ferromagnetic coupling can favor parallel moments. It is not simply the classical magnetic field of a neighbouring dipole. For example, with the convention H=−J∑⟨ij⟩​Si​⋅Sj​ and J>0, the localized-spin mean-field scale is kB​TC​=zJS(S+1)/3; this illustrates why an atomic electronic interaction, rather than a bare dipolar field, can produce a high transition temperature.

A bulk ferromagnet generally separates into magnetic domains. Within each domain many moments align and the local magnetization can be large, but different domain orientations partially or almost completely cancel the vector sum measured for the entire specimen. This reduces its external stray field and magnetostatic energy. Formation of magnetic domain walls costs exchange and anisotropy energy, so the actual pattern balances these costs with the reduction in stray-field energy.
A strong applied field can move domain walls and rotate moments toward a nearly saturation magnetization. When the field is removed, pinning and the sample's magnetic history determine the remanent moment. Thus a small net moment does not show that local ferromagnetic order has disappeared, or that moments within each domain are all randomly oriented. At finite temperature, thermal spin fluctuations also reduce the local ordered moment below its zero-temperature maximum even in a single-domain specimen. These two reductions—between-domain cancellation and within-domain thermal reduction—should be distinguished.
