A Bravais lattice is a set of points generated by integer translations along three independent primitive lattice translation vectors:
R=n1​a1​+n2​a2​+n3​a3​,ni​∈Z.
(1)
Every lattice point has the same environment under lattice translations. A crystal structure consists of this Bravais lattice with an atomic basis of a crystal repeated at every lattice point; the set of atomic positions need not itself be a Bravais lattice.
The two positions in the conventional cubic cell of alpha-tungsten give a body-centred cubic lattice, with one tungsten atom per primitive cell and two per conventional cell. One possible set of primitive lattice translation vectors is
a1​=2a​(−1,1,1),a2​=2a​(1,−1,1),a3​=2a​(1,1,−1).​
(2)
Their primitive-cell volume is a3/2.

For the conventional body-centred cubic lattice, the crystal basis structure factor of identical atoms is
Fhkl​=fW​[1+eiπ(h+k+l)].
(3)
It vanishes for odd h+k+l and equals 2fW​ for even h+k+l. Thus the first powder diffraction ring is the {110} family, with interplanar spacing d110​=a/2​.
The measured deflection is the angle between incident and scattered rays, 2θ=40.26∘, so the Bragg angle is θ=20.13∘. Using first-order Bragg diffraction, 2dsinθ=λ, gives
a=2​sinθλ​=0.31683nm.​
(4)
The mass density follows from two atoms per conventional unit cell, with molar mass M=0.1838kgmol−1 and Avogadro constant NA​:
ρ=NA​a32M​=1.9194×104kgm−3≃19.19gcm−3.​
(5)
Using 40.26∘ as the Bragg angle would confuse θ with 2θ and give an incorrect lattice constant and mass density.

c OurBigBook.com
The beta phase has a primitive cubic lattice with an eight-atom atomic basis of a crystal, rather than a body-centred cubic lattice. A lattice translation must map the entire atomic arrangement onto itself. For example, the putative body-centering vector (1/2,1/2,1/2) maps (1/4,1/2,0) to (3/4,0,1/2) modulo integer coordinates, where the given atomic basis of a crystal has no atom. None of the seven nonzero basis positions generates a translation that preserves all eight sites. Therefore
Bravais lattice: primitive cubic; basis: eight atoms.​
(6)
The presence of atoms at both the origin and the body centre alone is insufficient to establish body-centering of the full crystal structure.

Initially approximate every tungsten atom by the same atomic scattering factor f. Pairing the quarter-coordinate sites in the crystal basis structure factor gives
fFhkl​​=1+(−1)h+k+l+2[(−1)kcos2πh​+(−1)hcos2πl​+(−1)lcos2πk​].
(7)
For cubic powder diffraction, increasing deflection corresponds to increasing h2+k2+l2, since dhkl​=a/h2+k2+l2​. The first possibilities are
Family | h2+k2+l2 | F/f \{100} | 1 | 0 \{110} | 2 | 0 \{111} | 3 | 0 \{200} | 4 | 4 \{210} | 5 | 4
Hence the first two strong rings have Miller indices
{200},{210}.​
(8)
The powder diffraction multiplicity is six for {200}, from three choices of nonzero axis and two signs. For {210} it is 3!22=24, from six permutations and four sign choices. If the atomic scattering factors and angular corrections are taken as approximately equal for these nearby rings, their integrated diffraction intensity obey
I200​:I210​≃6∣4f∣2:24∣4f∣2=1:4.​
(9)
This is a multiplicity-based estimate. An exact measured ratio also depends on the momentum-transfer dependence of the atomic scattering factor, the Lorentz-polarization factor, the Debye–Waller factor, absorption and instrument geometry. In particular, the source does not provide the beta-phase lattice constant or instrument corrections needed to calculate those angular factors. Peak heights additionally depend on peak widths; the estimate refers to integrated ring intensities.

The two body-centre/origin sites and the other six sites have different local environments. Their effective atomic scattering factors therefore need not be identical even in an elemental, chemically pure crystal. Write them as fA​ and fB​, respectively. The crystal basis structure factor becomes
Fhkl​=fA​[1+(−1)h+k+l]+2fB​[(−1)kcos2πh​+(−1)hcos2πl​+(−1)lcos2πk​].
(10)
For the low-angle families this gives
F100​=0,F110​=2(fA​−fB​),​F111​=0.
(11)
Thus {110} can produce a weak extra powder diffraction ring below {200}. This is an accidental diffraction extinction from equal atomic scattering factors: its cancellation in part d is exact only in the identical-atom-scattering approximation. Small differences in the bonding electron density, or site-dependent Debye–Waller factors, can leave a small nonzero amplitude. For instance, taking fA​=f+δf and fB​=f gives F110​=2δf while F200​=4f+2δf; the corresponding intensity suppression is quadratic in ∣δf/f∣. No impurity is required. The {100} and {111} cancellations remain for arbitrary scalar fA​,fB​ in this two-site-class model.

a OurBigBook.com
The chemical potential is the change in Helmholtz free energy when particles are added at fixed temperature and volume:
μ=(∂N∂F​)T,V​.​
(12)
Equivalently, dU=TdS−PdV+μdN identifies it as the derivative of internal energy at fixed entropy and volume. In equilibrium, systems that exchange particles have equal chemical potentials.
The Fermi energy is the zero-temperature chemical potential, or, for independent electrons, the highest occupied single-particle energy in the zero-temperature limit. Its numerical value depends on the chosen energy reference; the Fermi energy need not be measured from the bottom of a band unless that convention is specified.

Expand a single-electron wavefunction as ∣ψ⟩=∑n​ψn​∣n⟩. Project the Schrödinger equation onto ⟨n∣ to obtain
Eψn​=ϵ0​ψn​−tψn−1​−tψn+1​.
(13)
Translation symmetry suggests the Bloch state ψn​=N−1/2eikna on a periodic chain of N sites. Substitution gives
E(k)=ϵ0​−t(eika+e−ika)=ϵ0​−2tcos(ka).​
(14)
Take the conventional positive hopping t>0. The first Brillouin zone is −π/a≤k<π/a; the band has minimum ϵ0​−2t at k=0 and maximum ϵ0​+2t at the zone edges. Its width is 4t. A negative hopping interchanges the extrema but does not change the density of states after replacing t by ∣t∣.
There are N allowed wavevectors and two spin states per wavevector. For the total spin-summed density of states D(E), each interior band energy has two roots, ±k. Using the spacing 2π/(Na) gives
D(E)=2π2Na​∑k:E(k)=E​∣dE/dk∣1​=π4t2−(E−ϵ0​)2​2N​.
(15)
Thus the density of states per atom is
g(E)=⎩⎨⎧​π4t2−(E−ϵ0​)2​2​,0,​∣E−ϵ0​∣<2t,∣E−ϵ0​∣>2t.​​
(16)
It is symmetric about ϵ0​ and has integrable inverse-square-root Van Hove singularities at both band edges. Its integral over the band is two states per atom. The plot labels the dispersion extrema and both singular edges.

c OurBigBook.com
Monovalency gives one electron per atom, so the two-spin-state band is half filled. At zero temperature, occupying −kF​<k<kF​ gives 2(Na/2π)(2kF​)=N. Hence
kF​=2aπ​,EF​=E(kF​)=ϵ0​.​
(17)
Relative to the band minimum, the Fermi energy is 2t.
The density of states obeys g(ϵ0​+x)=g(ϵ0​−x). At μ=ϵ0​, the Fermi-Dirac distribution satisfies
f(ϵ0​+x)+f(ϵ0​−x)=1.
(18)
Pairing the energies above and below the band centre therefore leaves exactly half of the two available states per atom occupied, at every positive temperature. Increasing μ strictly increases the mean particle number, so this solution at fixed half filling is unique. Consequently
μ(T)=ϵ0​for every T in this isolated symmetric-band model.​
(19)
This particle-hole symmetry of a half-filled bipartite band argument uses the symmetric tight binding band and fixed half filling; it is not a general temperature-independence property of chemical potential in metals with additional bands or an asymmetric density of states.

The first Brillouin zone is the square
−aπ​≤kx​<aπ​,−aπ​≤ky​<aπ​.
(20)
At half filling, translation of the wavevector by Q=(π/a,π/a) exchanges the two band energies:
E(k+Q)−ϵ0​=−[E(k)−ϵ0​].
(21)
Thus the same energy-pair argument as in part c gives
μ(T)=ϵ0​.​
(22)
At zero temperature, the Fermi surface satisfies cos(kx​a)+cos(ky​a)=0. Within the square Brillouin zone this is the diamond
∣kx​∣+∣ky​∣=aπ​,​
(23)
whose vertices are (±π/a,0) and (0,±π/a). The occupied region is the diamond's interior, containing the band minimum at the zone centre. It has half the Brillouin zone area, consistent with one electron per atom and two spin states per wavevector.

The reciprocal primitive vectors are (2π/ax​,0) and (0,2π/ay​), so the first Brillouin zone is
∣kx​∣≤π/ax​,∣ky​∣≤π/ay​.​
(24)
It is wider in the kx​ direction because ax​<ay​.
The geometry alone does not fix the hopping amplitudes or a unique Fermi surface. One consistent tight-binding model is
E(k)=ϵ0​−2tx​cos(kx​ax​)−2ty​cos(ky​ay​),tx​>ty​>0,
(25)
representing larger hopping between the closer atoms. It remains a nearest-neighbour bipartite band at half filling, so its chemical potential is ϵ0​. Its Fermi surface consists of the two sheets
kx​=±ax​1​arccos[−tx​ty​​cos(ky​ay​)].​
(26)
These meet the top and bottom zone edges and continue periodically into the neighbouring zones. The right panel of the figure gives an explicit example with tx​=2ty​ and ax​=0.65ay​. The central region between the sheets is occupied and has half the zone area. If the hopping amplitudes instead remained equal, a geometrically stretched diamond would also satisfy the stated information. The metric inequality ax​<ay​ alone does not determine which of these shapes occurs.

A phonon is a quantum of a normal mode of Bravais lattice vibration. A harmonic mode of angular frequency ω has energy ℏω(n+1/2), and adding one phonon increases its energy by ℏω. The phonon carries crystal momentum ℏq, defined modulo a reciprocal lattice vector.
The first Brillouin zone is the Wigner-Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice about the origin: the set of wavevectors at least as close to the origin as to any other reciprocal lattice point. Its boundaries are reciprocal-space perpendicular bisectors. It gives one representative of each wavevector modulo reciprocal lattice translations.

Write the Morse potential relative to its minimum as V(a+x)=D[e−2x/ξ−2e−x/ξ]. Expanding one order beyond the harmonic term gives
V(a+x)=−D+ξ2D​x2−ξ3D​x3+O(x4)=−D+2κ​x2−2ξκ​x3+O(x4).​
(35)
The negative cubic coefficient means that stretching is softer than compression. Thermal motion therefore has positive mean displacement even though the most probable minimum is at x=0.
For thermal expansion of a weakly anharmonic bond, classical equipartition gives ⟨x2⟩0​=kB​T/κ. Vanishing mean restoring force, to first anharmonic order, requires
0=⟨V′(a+x)⟩=κ⟨x⟩−2ξ3κ​⟨x2⟩+⋯,⟨x⟩≃2κξ3kB​T​.
(36)
For a bond free to expand under negligible applied tension, L=⟨r⟩≃a+⟨x⟩. Its linear thermal expansion coefficient is therefore
α∼aκξkB​​≃3.9×10−5K−1.​
(37)
Keeping the leading perturbative numerical factor gives α≃3kB​/(2aκξ)=5.9×10−5K−1. The question only requires the scale, not that factor. The thermal expansion is positive; a purely harmonic approximation would give zero mean displacement and zero thermal expansion. The local Taylor expansion describes a bound bond and is not a globally stable cubic potential.

The small parameter controlling the local Taylor expansion is the typical bond displacement divided by ξ. In the classical regime, the root-mean-square displacement is xrms​≃kB​T/κ​. Thus
ξxrms​​≪1⟺T≪kB​κξ2​=5.80×103K.​
(38)
For example, the magnitude of the cubic term divided by the quadratic term is ∣x∣/ξ, and subsequent terms carry further powers of this ratio. A physically safe bound-bond regime also has kB​T≪D, or T≪2.90×103K. These are the same parametric scale, differing only by a factor two. The inequalities are strong: they do not imply that a truncated local expansion is accurate close to several thousand kelvin.
At very low temperature, replace the classical fluctuation estimate by the zero-point motion as discussed in part ii. Its bond displacement is small compared with ξ for the supplied parameters, so low temperature does not itself invalidate the local expansion. Actual melting, other neighbours and bond breaking are outside this one-dimensional bond model.

Quantum statistics matter when thermal energy is comparable with a characteristic vibrational level spacing. The chain's natural angular frequency is ω0​=κ/m​=5.7471×1012s−1, giving
TQ​∼kB​ℏω0​​=43.9K.​
(39)
Factors of order unity depend on which vibrational scale is used. The largest chain phonon angular frequency is 2ω0​, corresponding to 87.8K. An isolated two-atom bond has reduced mass m/2 and angular frequency 2​ω0​, corresponding to 62.1K. Thus quantum corrections become appreciable on the scale of several tens of kelvin, and all modes are classical only when T is comfortably above the largest relevant vibrational scale. Below it, high-frequency phonons are progressively frozen out and equipartition fails.
For the isolated bond, the zero-temperature root-mean-square relative displacement is
xzp​=2(m/2)(2​ω0​)ℏ​​≃0.0061nm≃0.073ξ.
(40)
It is small enough for a local bond expansion. The classical weakly anharmonic estimate in part d therefore has a useful parametric window
102K≪T≪a few 103K,​
(41)
where the lower condition means comfortably above the vibrational scale, rather than a sharp threshold. The model's upper bound concerns local anharmonicity and binding, not a prediction of the melting temperature.

A material with paramagnetism develops a magnetization parallel to a weak applied magnetic field intensity, with positive magnetic susceptibility and no spontaneous magnetization in its paramagnetic phase. Molecular oxygen is an example; it has unpaired electron spins.
A material with diamagnetism develops an induced magnetization opposing the applied magnetic field, with negative magnetic susceptibility. A closed-shell atom such as helium is an example; its orbital response is diamagnetic.
A ferromagnet has a spontaneously ordered magnetization below its Curie temperature, even in the limit of vanishing applied magnetic field. Iron below its Curie temperature is an example. Different magnetic domains can cancel the net moment of an unmagnetized bulk sample, despite nonzero local spontaneous magnetization.

An f shell has orbital quantum number l=3 and seven orbital states with ml​=−3,−2,…,3. The first of Hund's rules maximizes total spin subject to the Pauli exclusion principle. Seven electrons occupy the seven orbitals singly with parallel spins, giving
S=7×21​=27​.​
(42)
Their orbital projections sum to zero, so the half-filled shell also has total orbital angular momentum L=0, total J=7/2, and Landé g-factor gJ​=2. These further values identify the magnetic moment used below.

The specified two-direction constraint permits magnetic moments μz​=±μ, with
μ=gJ​SμB​=7μB​,
(43)
where μB​ is the Bohr magneton. There are four magnetic gadolinium ions per conventional face-centered cubic lattice cell; the closed-shell nitrogen ions add no local spin moment. Hence the magnetic-ion number density is
n=a34​=3.20×1028m−3.

Z1​=2cosh(kB​Tμμ0​Hz​​),⟨μz​⟩=μtanh(kB​Tμμ0​Hz​​).

Ferromagnetic interactions enhance the response above the ordering transition. The Curie–Weiss law improves the independent-spin approximation:
χ(T)≃T−ΘC​,T>Tc​,​
(47)
with positive Weiss temperature Θ and the same high-temperature coefficient C. In a simple mean-field theory, take Θ=Tc​=70K. More generally Θ is fitted and need not equal Tc​, and the simple pole does not give exact critical behaviour arbitrarily close to the transition.
At zero temperature, a selected ferromagnetic orientation has all magnetic moments aligned. Its saturation magnetization is
Ms​(0)=nμ=a34​(7μB​)=2.077×106Am−1.​
(48)
Equivalently, there are 28μB​ per conventional cell. The moment points along either allowed easy-axis direction at zero magnetic field. This is the local or single-domain spontaneous magnetization; a macroscopic multidomain sample can have smaller, even zero, net magnetization in the absence of a selecting field.

Choose the positive spontaneous branch for Hz​=0. Its magnetization starts at Ms​(0), decreases with temperature, and reaches zero continuously at Tc​. It remains zero for Tc​≤T<2Tc​. There is an equally valid negative branch below Tc​; domain averaging can instead give zero net moment.
A fixed small positive magnetic field intensity selects the positive branch. At zero temperature it still saturates at Ms​(0); at intermediate temperature it increases the magnetization relative to the zero-field branch. It rounds the zero-field transition and leaves a positive magnetization above Tc​, tending in the linear-response region to CHz​/(T−Θ). The magnetization therefore does not vanish at Tc​ in the nonzero-field curve.
For a concrete labelled sketch, the figure uses the illustrative mean-field theory equation
m=tanh(tm+h​),m=Ms​(0)Mz​​,t=Tc​T​,h=kB​Tc​μμ0​Hz​​.
(49)
It shows h=0 and a fixed small h=0.01. The curve is an illustrative equilibrium equation of state, not a determination of the precise critical exponents of GdN. At h>0 no symmetry-breaking transition remains along this temperature sweep.
