With the convention ai​⋅bj​=2πδij​, the reciprocal lattice of a face-centered cubic lattice is a body-centered cubic lattice. The reciprocal conventional cube is parallel to the direct conventional cube and has side 4π/a. In direct-cubic coordinates its points are
G=a2π​(h,k,l),h,k,l all even or all odd.​
(1)
The even triples supply the corners of that reciprocal cube and the odd triples its body centres. The atomic basis can change diffraction intensities through its structure factor; it does not change the reciprocal lattice determined by the translation lattice.

The cubic Miller indices give an interplanar spacing dhkl​=a/h2+k2+l2​. Equivalently, the reciprocal lattice vector normal to these lattice planes has length 2π/dhkl​. Hence
d111​=3​a​,G111​=a2π​(1,1,1).​
(2)
The eight {111} reciprocal lattice vectors are the nearest nonzero reciprocal points. Their perpendicular bisectors form the eight hexagonal faces of the first Brillouin zone, a truncated octahedron. For the positive (111) direction, that Bragg plane is k⋅G111​=∣G111​∣2/2, or kx​+ky​+kz​=3π/a. It is parallel to the direct (111) lattice planes. The six remaining square zone faces correspond to the {200} reciprocal points. The zone boundary is halfway to a reciprocal point, not the point itself.

The density of states g(E) counts one-electron states per energy interval, including both electron spin orientations. It may be a total or a per-volume quantity; U must have the matching normalization. The Fermi-Dirac distribution
f(E,T)=e(E−μ(T))/(kB​T)+11​
(12)
is the occupation probability of such a state. In the fixed-volume free electron model, the integration variable E and the density of states do not depend on T. The occupation depends explicitly on T and implicitly through the chemical potential μ(T) needed to preserve electron number. Thermal expansion or a changing electronic band structure would require additional effects outside this model.
At T≪TF​, only states within order kB​T of the Fermi energy change their occupation. There are order g(EF​)kB​T such states, each carrying an excitation energy of order kB​T. Thus U(T)−U(0)∝g(EF​)(kB​T)2 and the electronic heat capacity is proportional to g(EF​)kB2​T. Using the supplied coefficient,
CV​=γT,γ=3π2​kB2​g(EF​),U(T)−U(0)=21​γT2.​
(13)
The shell argument fixes the scaling, not the numerical coefficient; the controlled Sommerfeld expansion fixes that coefficient at fixed electron number. Filled states far below EF​ cannot accept arbitrary thermal excitations because of the Pauli exclusion principle.

Let gv​(E) denote the spin-summed density of states per volume. An electron moment with magnitude the Bohr magneton μB​ has Zeeman energies ∓μB​B. At low temperature, the two spin populations are
n±​=21​∫0μ±μB​B​gv​(E)dE,n+​−n−​=gv​(EF​)μB​B+O(B3).
(16)
The common chemical potential changes only at order B2, so it does not affect this linear imbalance. The magnetization is M=μB​(n+​−n−​)=μB2​gv​(EF​)B. Define SI volume magnetic susceptibility by M=χp​H and take the weak applied field as B≃μ0​H. Then Pauli paramagnetism gives
χp​=μ0​μB2​gv​(EF​)=2EF​3μ0​nμB2​​.​
(17)
The last equality uses the three-dimensional parabolic free electron model, gv​(EF​)=3n/(2EF​). This susceptibility is nearly independent of temperature for T≪TF​, unlike the Curie law for localized independent moments. The displayed result excludes orbital diamagnetism and interaction enhancements. If susceptibility instead denotes ∂M/∂B, omit μ0​; confusing that convention with ∂M/∂H changes the units.

Take two localized spin orientations with moments ±μB​ along the applied magnetic field. This assumes an electron-like spin g factor of two. Their energies are ∓μB​B, so the one-moment partition function is Z1​=2coshx, with x=μB​B/(kB​T). The thermal mean moment is kB​T∂B​lnZ1​=μB​tanhx, and the spin-one-half paramagnetic magnetization is
M=nμB​tanh(kB​TμB​B​).​
(23)
For ∣x∣≪1, M≃nμB2​B/(kB​T). With the same SI convention χ=∂M/∂H and B≃μ0​H,
χ=TC​,C=kB​nμ0​μB2​​.​
(24)
This Curie law is a zero-field or weak-field susceptibility. At arbitrary field the differential response is (C/T)sech2x, and M saturates at nμB​; extending the linear law to unlimited field would contradict that finite saturation magnetization.

In the Weiss molecular field approximation, the local response obeys
M=nμB​tanh[kB​TμB​(B+λμ0​M)​].
(25)
Linearize around M=B=0 in the stable phase of paramagnetism. Substitution of a gives
M=μ0​TC​(B+λμ0​M),(T−λC)M=μ0​C​B.
(26)
Hence the Curie–Weiss law and Weiss temperature are
χ=T−θC​,θ=λC=kB​λnμ0​μB2​​.​
(27)
For positive λ, θ is the mean-field theory Curie temperature: the zero-magnetization solution becomes unstable there, and below it a spontaneous solution appears. For negative coupling, a negative fitted Weiss temperature indicates predominantly opposing interactions, but this one-uniform-magnetization treatment does not determine an antiferromagnetic ordering pattern or its transition temperature. More generally the fitted Weiss temperature need not equal the observed ordering temperature.
For θ>0, the paramagnetic curve is positive for T>θ, diverges as T↓θ, and tends to C/T at high temperature; its inverse is the straight line (T−θ)/C. The expression is not the stable susceptibility below θ. For example substituting T=θ/2 would predict a negative response around the unstable M=0 branch. The nonlinear self-consistency equation must instead be expanded about the appropriate spontaneous magnetization.

Within the stipulated localized, one-moment-per-atom spin model, the number density follows directly from mass density and molar mass:
n=Mmol​ρNA​​=0.0586938908​NA​=9.1400×1028m−3.
(28)
The face-centered cubic lattice is consistent with four atoms per conventional unit cell; no additional factor four is needed once n has been obtained from the density. The Curie constant is C=0.71549K. Identifying the model's θ=λC with the specified Curie temperature gives
λ=0.71549K627K​=8.7632×102.​
(29)
At very low temperature, a fully ordered single domain has Ms​=nμB​=8.4764×105Am−1. Its Weiss molecular field is
Beff​=λμ0​Ms​=μB​kB​TC​​=9.3343×102T.​
(30)
The cancellation of n in the middle expression provides a useful check. The ordinary magnetostatic scale is only μ0​Ms​≃1.065T, smaller by the factor λ. Thus Beff​ is not a plausible ordinary self-generated dipolar magnetic field of the nickel moments. It represents the exchange interaction energy in field units, not an additional Maxwell field of that magnitude. This does not assert that such large externally generated fields are forbidden. Nickel is an itinerant ferromagnet; the imposed one-μB​-per-atom model is an estimate rather than its measured microscopic moment.

d OurBigBook.com
A macroscopic nickel specimen can split into magnetic domains, each with nonzero local spontaneous magnetization but with different orientations. Their vector contributions can cancel, giving an almost zero measured net magnetization below the Curie temperature. This reduces the external stray-field energy, at the expense of magnetic domain wall energy; the balance favours a multidomain state in many ordinary specimens.
An applied magnetic field can move magnetic domain walls and rotate the domain moments, revealing a large response and eventually saturation magnetization. Thus a zero average does not mean that each local moment has become thermally disordered or that the material is above its Curie temperature. Preparation, shape and domain-wall pinning influence remanence.

Assume thermal equilibrium, a three-dimensional nondegenerate semiconductor, one parabolic conduction extremum and one parabolic valence extremum, with two spin states in each. Use the supplied effective masses as scalar density-of-states parameters. With conduction energy E=Ec​+ε, state counting gives the per-volume density of states
Dc​(ε)=2π21​(ℏ22me∗​​)3/2ε​.
(31)
Since Ec​−μ≫kB​T, replace the Fermi-Dirac distribution by its Boltzmann factor. Using x=ε/(kB​T) and the supplied integral,
n=e−(Ec​−μ)/(kB​T)∫0∞​Dc​(ε)e−ε/(kB​T)dε=Nc​e−(Ec​−μ)/(kB​T),
(32)
Nc​=2(2πℏ2me∗​kB​T​)3/2=2(h22πme∗​kB​T​)3/2.
(33)
The electron hole occupation is 1−f(E) in the valence band. Applying the same integration below Ev​ gives
p=Nv​e−(μ−Ev​)/(kB​T),Nv​=2(h22πmh∗​kB​T​)3/2.
(34)
Their product cancels the chemical potential, giving the semiconductor mass-action law
np=4(h22πkB​​)3(me∗​mh∗​)3/2T3e−Eg​/(kB​T).​
(35)
Thus, for masses expressed in ordinary mass units,
A=4(h22πkB​​)3=2π3ℏ6kB3​​,B=23​,C=3.​
(36)
The labels B,C in this last box are exponents, unrelated to the magnetic field or Curie constant above. Valley degeneracy or multiple valence bands must be included in the effective density of states, or absorbed into suitable density-of-states effective masses, not counted twice. Real silicon has anisotropic multiple valleys, so its conductivity mass is not automatically its density-of-states mass; the numerical answers below implement the paper's supplied scalar-mass model.[ref]
The formula is not an identity for arbitrarily degenerate carriers. For a concrete counterexample, take μ−Ec​=10kB​T while holes remain dilute. Fermi occupation gives n/Nc​≃24.1, whereas the Boltzmann replacement would give e10≃22026; the predicted product then differs by about three orders of magnitude. Separate electron and hole quasi-Fermi levels out of equilibrium would also prevent the cancellation used above.

A substitutional phosphorus atom supplies one more valence electron than the silicon atom it replaces. In the shallow hydrogenic donor state model, the remaining positive impurity attracts that extra electron through the screened Coulomb potential
V(r)=−4πϵ0​ϵr​re2​.
(39)
Rescaling the hydrogen atom binding energy by the effective mass and dielectric screening gives the ionization energy
EDbind​=13.6057eVϵr2​me∗​/me​​=13.605711.720.26​eV=0.02584eV.​
(40)
The donor level lies at Ec​−EDbind​; its binding magnitude is positive. The associated effective Bohr radius of a hydrogenic donor is aB∗​=ϵr​(me​/me∗​)a0​≃2.38nm, consistent with a shallow state extended over several lattice spacings.
At 1000K, kB​T=0.08617eV, approximately 3.3 times this binding energy. Dilute donors are consequently expected to be largely ionized, rather than frozen out. The actual ionized fraction also depends on the chemical potential, available conduction states and donor charge-state degeneracy; comparing kB​T to the binding energy alone is not a normalized ionization probability. At sufficiently high temperature, intrinsic electron-hole production can dominate the total carriers even though donors are ionized. Band anisotropy and short-range impurity corrections limit the numerical hydrogenic estimate.

n=2Nd​+Nd2​+4ni2​​​,p=Nd​+Nd2​+4ni2​​2ni2​​.
(44)
Since Nd​≫ni​, the hole density is
p≃Nd​ni2​​=4.5816×1010m−3,n≃1.0000×1020m−3.​
(45)

Ec​−μ=kB​TlnnNc​​=0.269180eV,μ−Ev​=0.840820eV.​
(46)
