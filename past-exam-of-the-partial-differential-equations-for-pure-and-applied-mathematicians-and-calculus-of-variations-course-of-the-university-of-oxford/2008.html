Choose a smooth compactly supported mollifier of integral one. On every compact subset of Ω, and for sufficiently small ε, each translated mollifier is an allowed test, so the assumption gives w∗ρε​=0. The standard mollifier approximation theorem gives w∗ρε​→w in local L1. Thus w=0 almost everywhere.
For a multi-index α, the weak derivative v=Dαu is the locally integrable function satisfying
∫Ω​uDαϕ=(−1)∣α∣∫Ω​vϕ(ϕ∈C0∞​(Ω)).​
(4)
Two such functions have zero pairing difference against every test, so the first result proves uniqueness. A compactly supported smooth test on an open subset E extends by zero to one on Ω, proving restriction of the derivative to E.
Put w=Dβu. For every test ϕ the defining identity applied to Dαϕ gives
(−1)∣α∣∫wDαϕ=(−1)∣α∣+∣β∣∫uDα+βϕ.
(5)
Thus these two distributions are identical; one is represented by a locally integrable function if and only if the other is. Their weak derivatives then agree, proving Dα(Dβu)=Dα+βu with exactly the asserted existence equivalence.
On (0,2) the displayed piecewise affine function is continuous at one. Integration by parts separately on the two intervals shows its first weak derivative is one on (0,1) and minus one on (1,2); the first boundary terms cancel by continuity. The derivative of that step function is −2δ1​. This distribution is not represented by a locally integrable function: tests of height one supported in shrinking neighborhoods of one have pairing −2 with it, whereas their integrals against any fixed local L1 function tend to zero. Hence there is a first weak derivative but no locally integrable second weak derivative.

The Lax-Milgram theorem gives, for a bounded coercive bilinear form on a real Hilbert space and every bounded linear functional, a unique vector representing that functional through the form, with the inverse bound supplied by coercivity. The Fredholm alternative for a compact operator K says that I+μK is invertible if its kernel is zero; in general its range is closed and consists exactly of vectors orthogonal to ker(I+μK)∗ in the Hilbert-space case.
Uniform ellipticity gives ∑aij​ξi​ξj​≥θ∣ξ∣2 for some θ>0. Bounded coefficients make Bλ​ bounded on H1, and for λ<−γ,
Bλ​[u,u]≥θ∥Du∥22​+(−γ−λ)∥u∥22​≥min{θ,−γ−λ}∥u∥H12​.
(8)
The forcing v↦∫fv is bounded on H1. The Lax-Milgram theorem therefore gives a unique weak solution for every λ<−γ.
When c=0, choose the shift λ0​=−1 and let Kf be the unique solution of B[Kf,v]+∫(Kf)v=∫fv. Thus K=(L+I)−1 in the weak sense. The equation Lu=f is equivalent to u=K(f+u), or
(I−K)u=Kf.​
(9)
Conversely a solution in L2 of this operator equation lies in H1 because it equals K(f+u), and satisfies the variational equation. The assumed boundedness K:L2→H1, followed by the compact Rellich embedding H1↪L2, proves compactness of K as an L2 operator.
By the supplied kernel assumption, ker(I−K) consists of constants. Also K1=1. Since K is a self-adjoint operator, the Fredholm alternative condition is Kf⊥1, and
⟨Kf,1⟩=⟨f,K1⟩=∫Ω​f.
(10)
Therefore a weak solution exists exactly when the mean of f is zero, and solutions differ by a constant. On a disconnected domain the actual kernel would consist of constants on each component and the condition would apply separately on each; the question's given kernel assumption specifies the single-constant case.
For a general coefficient matrix, the stated variational definition encodes zero conormal flux (aDu)⋅n=0. It agrees with ordinary zero normal derivative only under additional conditions on a. The argument above uses the weak formulation explicitly defined in the paper, so does not require that stronger boundary identification.

A strictly convex function satisfies that inequality strictly whenever x=y and 0<t<1.
For necessity of the strict supporting-tangent property, convexity gives a nonnegative gap along a segment from z0​ to z. A global minimizer has I(uˉ)≤I(v) for every admissible v. Global minimality implies strong local minimality, which implies weak local minimality. The reverse implications are not valid in general.
Where fv​ exists, the strengthened Weierstrass condition says that for some δ>0, the excess
E(x,y,p,q)=f(x,y,q)−f(x,y,p)−fv​(x,y,p)(q−p)
(14)
is nonnegative for every x∈[a,b], every trial slope q∈R, and all y,p with ∣y−uˉ(x)∣<δ, ∣p−uˉ′(x)∣<δ. This is a neighborhood condition in height and reference slope, not just an inequality on the reference curve.
The classical Weierstrass sufficient condition, with the usual smooth integrand regularity in addition to continuity, states that an admissible C1 curve satisfying the Euler-Lagrange equation, a coercively positive second variation, and this strengthened condition is a strong local minimizer. A strict excess away from the reference slope gives the corresponding strict conclusion. The differentiability assumptions are part of that classical sufficient theorem; continuity alone does not define its first and second variations.

With coefficients evaluated along (x,uˉ,uˉ′), put p=fvv​, r=fuv​ and s=fuu​. The second variation is
q(ϕ)=δ2I(uˉ)[ϕ,ϕ]=∫ab​(pϕ′2+2rϕϕ′+sϕ2)dx.
(19)
The Jacobi equation in divergence form is −(pz′+rz)′+rz′+sz=0; if r is differentiable it is −(pz′)′+(s−r′)z=0. A conjugate point c>a admits a nonzero Jacobi solution vanishing at a,c.
There are two positivity conventions worth distinguishing. Strict pointwise positivity means q(ϕ)>0 for each nonzero fixed-endpoint variation. Coercive positivity means q(ϕ)≥c∫ϕ′2 with one c>0 for all such variations; the endpoint Poincaré estimate makes this equivalent to a positive lower bound in the full H1 norm. Under the regular Legendre hypothesis p>0, the necessary and sufficient Jacobi criterion is no conjugate point in (a,b]; in that regular case it gives both strict and coercive positivity. Equivalently the Jacobi equation has a solution positive on [a,b], and the square identity
q(ϕ)=∫ab​pz2[(ϕ/z)′]2dx
(20)
gives positivity and a uniform derivative lower bound by the positive minimum of z and boundedness of z,z′. Conversely a conjugate Jacobi field, extended by zero beyond its second zero, is a nonzero Lipschitz variation with q=0, contradicting either positivity convention.
Coercivity itself forces p>0: local high-frequency variations make the leading coefficient at least the coercivity constant. Mere strict positivity need not force it, as q(ϕ)=∫ϕ2 shows. If that weaker convention is intended without regularity, a complete criterion is p≥0, no conjugate point for every regularized form qη​=q+η∫ϕ′2 with η>0, and no nonzero zero-endpoint Lipschitz weak solution of the original Jacobi equation. The regularized criteria imply qη​≥0, hence q≥0 by passage to the limit. For a nonnegative quadratic form, q(ϕ)=0 forces all mixed terms with test variations to vanish, giving exactly the weak Jacobi kernel; excluding it gives strict positivity. Necessity follows in reverse. This specifies the degenerate case without imposing an unstated strict Legendre condition.
For weak local minimality, sufficient conditions are the Euler-Lagrange equation and a coercively positive second variation with the stated smoothness. Taylor's remainder is bounded by ε∥ϕ∥H12​ for small W1,∞ perturbations, so the positive quadratic term dominates it. These are the classical weak minimum sufficient conditions.
