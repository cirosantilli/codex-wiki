Weak convergence means ℓ(uj​)→ℓ(u) for every bounded linear functional ℓ∈X∗. Norm convergence implies this by ∣ℓ(uj​−u)∣≤∥ℓ∥∥uj​−u∥. The converse fails for coordinate unit vectors in ℓ2: their norms are one, but pairing with any square-summable sequence tends to zero, so they are weakly null.
For the subsequence assertion apply Gram-Schmidt process to a countable dense subset to choose a countable orthonormal basis (em​) of the separable Hilbert space and a bound M for the sequence. Repeated subsequence selection and diagonalization make every coefficient ⟨ujk​​,em​⟩ converge to a scalar am​. Bessel inequality gives ∑m=1N​∣am​∣2≤M2 for every N, so u=∑m​am​em​ exists in H with norm at most M. Pairings with finite linear combinations of the basis converge. Approximating any other test vector by such combinations and using the common norm bound proves ujk​​⇀u. The finite-dimensional case uses the same argument with a finite basis.
Apply this to every subsequence of the bounded L2 sequence. Any weakly convergent further subsequence has limit v with ∫ab​v=0, since interval indicators are L2 tests. The permitted fact gives v=0 almost everywhere. If the whole sequence failed to converge weakly to zero, some fixed test functional and subsequence would stay a positive distance from zero. Its weakly convergent further subsequence would then contradict the preceding conclusion. Thus the entire sequence converges weakly to zero in L2(0,1).

The first weak derivative Dj​u=v is characterized by ∫Ω​u∂j​ϕ=−∫Ω​vϕ for every ϕ∈Cc∞​(Ω). The fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations in the locally integrable form says that a function pairing to zero with every such test is zero almost everywhere. Applied to the difference of two candidate derivatives, it proves uniqueness. The Sobolev space consists of u∈Lp with all first weak derivatives in Lp, with norm
∥u∥W1,p​=(∥u∥pp​+j=1∑n​∥Dj​u∥pp​)1/p.​
(1)
For the one-dimensional tent, integration by parts on each side of one has cancelling junction terms because the function is continuous. Thus u′=1 on (0,1) and u′=−1 on (1,2), almost everywhere. Both it and its derivative are bounded, so it belongs to every stated Sobolev space; its displayed norm to the power p is 2/(p+1)+2.
For u=x1​/r in the punctured ball, ordinary differentiation gives
Dj​u=δ1j​/r−x1​xj​/r3.​
(2)
The function is bounded and ∣Dj​u∣≤2/r, so polar integration gives finite derivative Lp norms when ∫01​rn−1−pdr<∞, namely p<n. To verify these are weak derivatives across the origin, integrate by parts outside a ball of radius ε against a compactly supported smooth test. The inner boundary term is at most a fixed constant times εn−1, since u is bounded. It tends to zero for n>1. The volume terms converge by local integrability of the displayed derivative. Thus u∈W1,p(B) for 1≤p<n with exactly these derivatives. Its value at the origin can be chosen arbitrarily.

The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators used here concerns a compact self-adjoint operator: its nonzero eigenvalues are real, have finite multiplicity and can accumulate only at zero; the orthogonal complement of its kernel is the closed span of its orthonormal eigenvectors. Self-adjointness is essential for this eigenbasis statement.
Let Kf be the unique zero-parameter weak solution. Uniqueness gives linearity. Testing with Kf and using coercivity gives ∥Kf∥H1​≤ν−1∥f∥2​. The Rellich-Kondrachov theorem makes the embedding H01​(Ω)↪L2(Ω) compact on this bounded C1 domain, so K is compact on L2. Symmetry gives
⟨Kf,g⟩=B[Kg,Kf]=⟨f,Kg⟩.
(6)
It is self-adjoint and positive. It is injective: if Kf=0, the weak equation pairs f to zero with all H01​ tests, dense in L2, so f=0. Moreover ⟨Kf,f⟩=B[Kf,Kf]>0 for f=0.
The parameter equation is equivalent to u=K(f+λu), that is
u−λKu=Kf.​
(7)
A solution of this operator equation in L2 lies in H01​, because its right-side representation is K(f+λu), and the equivalence follows in both directions. For λ≤0, the form B[u,v]−λ⟨u,v⟩ remains coercive; the preceding minimization argument gives a unique weak solution.
The spectral theorem now supplies a complete orthonormal basis (ϕj​) of L2 with Kϕj​=κj​ϕj​, κj​>0, and κj​→0. The elliptic eigenvalues are λj​=1/κj​>0, with finite multiplicity and tending to infinity, and Lϕj​=λj​ϕj​ weakly. If λ is not one of them, then
u=j∑​λj​−λ⟨f,ϕj​⟩​ϕj​.​
(8)
This converges even in H01​: its squared energy norm is ∑j​λj​∣⟨f,ϕj​⟩∣2/∣λj​−λ∣2, finite because the multiplier is bounded over j. Partial sums solve the weak equation with the corresponding L2 projections of f. Passing to the limit proves the equation for every test; the same coefficients force uniqueness. Thus the expansion represents the unique weak solution away from the elliptic eigenvalues.

A global minimizer satisfies I(uˉ)≤I(v) for every admissible v.
A point of convexity s of a differentiable scalar function is one where its tangent supports the entire graph: g(q)≥g(s)+g′(s)(q−s) for every q∈R. The Weierstrass necessary condition for strong minimality, under its differentiability assumptions in the slope variable, is
f(x,uˉ,q)−f(x,uˉ,uˉ′)−fv​(x,uˉ,uˉ′)(q−uˉ′)≥0(q∈R)
(17)
for almost every x. In other words the reference slope is a point of convexity of the slope integrand. The later example is smooth, so these derivative-based conditions apply there.

Write g(v)=−cos(πv/2). For the stipulated 0<δ<1, the displayed tent is continuous at its junction, has zero endpoint values, and its slopes are δ and δ−4. At both slopes g′ has the same value c=(π/2)sin(πδ/2)>0, and g′′=(π2/4)cos(πv/2) is strictly positive on common sufficiently small neighborhoods of them. Taylor's theorem gives, for small ∥ϕ′∥∞​,
I(uδ+ϕ)−I(uδ)≥c∫01​ϕ′+c0​∫01​ϕ′2=c0​∫01​ϕ′2.
(18)
Thus uδ is a strict weak local minimizer of a variational integral. It is not a strong local minimizer of a variational integral: the global supporting-tangent requirement at either slope has nonzero slope c, whereas g is bounded. Letting the trial slope tend to positive infinity violates that requirement. The Weierstrass necessary condition therefore fails.
The opening parameter range δ∈(0,1) is essential. The subsequent phrase “for each δ>0” must be read within it; taken literally beyond that range it is false. At δ=2 the tent slopes are 2,−2, where g=1 and g′′<0, so this is not a weak minimum.

The De la Vallée-Poussin criterion characterizes uniform integrability by existence of a nonnegative increasing convex function Ψ with Ψ(s)/s→∞ and a uniform bound on ∫Ψ(∣vj​∣). On a finite interval this gives a weakly convergent subsequence in L1 by the Dunford-Pettis theorem.
After discarding a finite prefix of the minimizing sequence if needed, put H(t)=Φ(t)−M≥0. Its derivative sequence has ∫H(uj′​)≤C. The superlinear bound directly gives uniform integrability: for H(t)≥A∣t∣ outside [−RA​,RA​],
∫E​∣uj′​∣≤RA​∣E∣+C/A.
(19)
If the convex version of the criterion is used, choose increasing Rm​→∞ so H(t)≥m2∣t∣ for ∣t∣≥Rm​, and set Ψ(s)=∑m≥1​(s−Rm​)+​. This is locally a finite sum, convex and increasing. It is superlinear because for fixed N and s≥2RN​, Ψ(s)≥Ns/2. If exactly m terms are active, Ψ(s)≤ms≤H(t) for s=∣t∣, so its integrals have the same bound. The criterion therefore supplies ujk​′​⇀v in L1.
