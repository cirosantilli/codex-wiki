Discovery of 51 Pegasi b

 ... Branches of physics Astrophysics Exoplanet Exoplanet atmosphere Exoplanet atmospheric observatory Doppler spectroscopy
OurBigBook.com Words: 191
Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz began observing 51 Pegasi in September 1994 and revisited its repeating Doppler spectroscopy signal in 1995. Their circular fit used 35 measurements: orbital period 4.2293±0.0011 days, stellar radial-velocity semi-amplitude 59±3ms−1, and residual scatter 13ms−1 after epoch-dependent velocity offsets. Apparently stable comparison stars checked the instrumental zero point. Low chromospheric stellar activity and an inferred roughly 30-day stellar rotation period argued against starspots; light curves and the spectral line bisector tested stellar pulsation. These checks favoured orbital motion but had finite sensitivity. The reported Mp​sini=0.47±0.02 Jupiter masses was not a true mass. Stronger upper bounds used stellar-spin information and assumed spin–orbit alignment, limiting a nearly face-on binary star interpretation conditionally.[ref]
The hot Jupiter matters both for planetary migration and for how exoplanets are found. At fixed small companion mass, orbital inclination and orbital eccentricity, the stellar radial-velocity semi-amplitude scales as K∝P−1/3. Comparing chosen orbital periods of 4 days and 12 years gives an amplitude ratio of about 10.3: a close companion can be much easier to detect and complete many orbits during one observing season. Finding a giant planet close to its star also asks how its present orbit relates to where it formed. Doppler spectroscopy supplies a dynamical constraint; it does not by itself establish a formation pathway or measure the exoplanet's radius.
