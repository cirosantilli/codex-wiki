Hubble's law (d˙=H(t)d)

Hubble's law states that the instantaneous rate of change of proper distance in cosmology between comoving observers is proportional to that distance:
vrec​(t):=d˙(t)=H(t)d(t).
(37)
This is exact for fixed comoving distance χ in an FLRW metric: differentiating d=aχ gives d˙=a˙χ=(a˙/a)d. The dot uses cosmic time, and both distances are compared on spatial slices at the same cosmic time. The rate d˙ is not a relative velocity measured in a single inertial frame of special relativity. Thus the instantaneous relation is not an exact observed redshift-distance formula. The associated Hubble flow describes the ideal expansion; peculiar velocities describe departures from it.
For nearby sources, the observed redshift approximately satisfies
cz≃H0​d
(38)
when source and observer peculiar velocities are small compared with H0​d, additional gravitational redshift is negligible, and the changes in the scale factor and Hubble parameter during light travel are small. Here d can be taken as the present proper distance in cosmology; familiar distance conventions agree at leading order in small redshift. Briefly, a(te​)≃a(t0​)[1−H0​(t0​−te​)], while d≃c(t0​−te​), so cosmological redshift gives z≃H0​d/c. At greater redshift, use an expansion history and an explicit distance convention instead.
Stipulate the illustrative Hubble constant H0​=70kms−1Mpc−1 and speed of light c=299792.458kms−1. A Mpc is a million parsecs. The following are computed examples, not a catalogue of observed galaxies:
  • At d=10Mpc, H0​d=700kms−1 and the approximate cosmological redshift is z≃0.002335.
  • At d=100Mpc, H0​d=7000kms−1 and the approximate cosmological redshift is z≃0.02335.
  • At d=200Mpc, H0​d=14000kms−1 and the approximate cosmological redshift is z≃0.04670.
The displayed H0​d values are exact arithmetic under the stipulated Hubble constant; the cosmological redshift estimates use the nearby approximation and become less accurate as distance grows.
Hubble's law turns a nearby observed redshift into an approximate distance indicator and makes the uniformity of cosmic expansion tangible: twice the proper distance in cosmology means twice the instantaneous separation rate. Recentring the ideal Hubble flow on another comoving observer preserves the same proportionality, as illustrated under scale factor, so recession in every direction does not select a central observer.
In his 1929 PNAS paper, Edwin Hubble presented a relation between the distances and radial velocities of extragalactic objects. His fits gave coefficients of 465 and 513kms−1Mpc−1 for 24 individual objects and nine groups respectively, and he used about 500kms−1Mpc−1 as a rounded representative value. Those historical fits used the paper's distance estimates; they are distinct from the stipulated modern-scale illustration above. [ref].
