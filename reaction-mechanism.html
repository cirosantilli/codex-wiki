Reaction mechanism

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 17
A reaction mechanism describes how reactants become reaction products through specified elementary reactions. Adding the steps must recover the overall chemical equation; intermediate chemical species cancel. That accounting is necessary, but the proposed steps must also predict a rate law compatible with experiment. Several reaction mechanisms can pass the same test.[ref]
For example, the stipulated steps A+B⟶I and I⟶P sum to A+B⟶P. I is a reaction intermediate: it is formed and then consumed. The catalyst C in the existing catalytic cycle instead starts available, participates, and is regenerated; its bound form can be a reaction intermediate. Both can disappear from the summed chemical equation, so cancellation alone does not distinguish their roles. Cancellation is algebraic bookkeeping, not an assertion that every step finishes instantaneously.
The net chemical equation supplies consumption ratios, not a clock. For the stipulated two-step example, directional reaction rates r1​=k1​[A][B] and r2​=k2​[I] give d[I]/dt=r1​−r2​ and d[P]/dt=r2​. Initially [I]=0 can mean zero reaction product formation while A already disappears. In particular, −d[A]/dt=d[P]/dt requires negligible intermediate accumulation here. A single elementary reaction with the same net chemical equation would instead predict immediate formation at k[A][B]. Measuring both sides can reveal a distinction hidden by the net equation.
This makes reaction mechanisms useful for designing observations: follow a candidate reaction intermediate, measure individual reaction products, or vary one starting concentration at a time. The consecutive first-order reaction mechanism quantifies an intermediate's rise and fall; the parallel first-order reaction mechanism shows how identical reactant traces can conceal different reaction products. The acetone iodination rate-law experiment connects such reasoning to a real laboratory protocol. The existing reaction coordinate, potential energy surface, and energy profile describe configurations and barriers along a route; a concentration plotted against time describes a different observable.
