A psychometric function specifies the probability of a measured response as a physical stimulus variable changes. The response might be “present,” “clockwise,” or correct in a two-alternative forced-choice task. These are different dependent variables. Understanding the function's shape is necessary to distinguish sensory changes from decision, attention and measurement effects. A reported threshold without its task, response definition and performance criterion is incomplete. For example, a visual experiment could use calibrated display visual contrasts, timed two-interval presentations, fixation control and button choices; the observer’s choice supplies the dependent variable.
A smooth curve need not mean that a receptor has a smoothly varying conscious threshold. Consider a noisy internal response R=μ(x)+ε, with “yes” reported when R>c. Here c is a decision criterion, not necessarily a biological detection limit. If Fε​ is the noise cumulative distribution function,
P(yes∣x)=1−Fε​(c−μ(x)).
(1)
With Gaussian noise of standard deviation σ, symmetry of the normal distribution gives a Gaussian psychometric function
P(yes∣x)=Φ(σμ(x)−c​).
(2)
The sigmoid is therefore a distribution tail swept by a changing mean response. At low means, few responses exceed the criterion; near it, many trials switch category; at high means, almost all do. The derivative is ϕ((μ−c)/σ)μ′(x)/σ, so slope depends on both sensory gain and variability. Moving c shifts the curve without necessarily improving sensitivity. If noise vanished and the mean crossed the criterion sharply, this simple model would approach a step rather than a graded transition.
There is no universal sigmoid in every stimulus coordinate. A nonlinear transduction function changes the curve, and a logarithmic intensity axis changes its appearance. As a counterexample, a toy detector responding whenever at least one event occurs in a Poisson distribution with mean kx has P(yes∣x)=1−e−kx. This is concave on a linear positive-intensity axis. It follows immediately by subtracting the zero-event probability from one. It is a mathematical example, not evidence that human detection universally uses a one-event rule. Variation in attention or decision criterion across trials can broaden a measured curve even if the within-state sensory noise is unchanged.
Task conventions determine the endpoints. A useful psychometric function with guessing and lapses is
p(x)=γ+(1−γ−λ)Φ(βx−α​),β>0.
(3)
Here γ is the lower asymptote and λ the upper error asymptote. In balanced two-alternative forced-choice performance, γ is commonly fixed at 1/2. A yes/no response probability does not automatically have this lower bound. In this particular cumulative-Gaussian parameterization, p(α)=γ+(1−γ−λ)/2; thus α gives 75% correct only for γ=1/2 and λ=0. Other function families use different scale parameters. Nor is λ automatically the fraction of inattentive trials: if a fraction r of trials involves random two-choice guessing, those trials contribute an upper error probability r/2.
The relation to signal detection theory is equally important. Under independent equal-variance Gaussian signal and noise samples, define sensitivity index d′=(μs​−μn​)/σ. In an ideal two-interval comparison, the response difference has mean μs​−μn​ and variance 2σ2, giving
P(correct)=P(Rs​−Rn​>0)=Φ(d′/2​).
(4)
This Gaussian forced-choice sensitivity relation follows by standardizing the Gaussian difference. It is a specified decision model, not an identity for every forced-choice experiment. In a yes/no task, hits and false alarms are both needed: under the corresponding equal-variance model, d′=Φ−1(H)−Φ−1(F). A criterion change can increase both rates while leaving d′ unchanged. The phenomenological fit above need not have parameters identical to these internal-response parameters.
These distinctions affect experiments. Felix A. Wichmann and N. Jeremy Hill used simulation and statistical modeling to show that unmodeled stimulus-independent errors can bias threshold and slope estimates, even when lapses are uncommon.[ref] Ignoring high-intensity errors can make a function appear shallower and falsely suggest worse sensory precision. A broad group-average curve can likewise reflect a mixture of individually sharp functions with different locations.
At predetermined stimulus levels, independent trials give counts ki​∼Binomial(ni​,p(xi​)). The parameter-dependent log-likelihood is ∑i​[ki​logpi​+(ni​−ki​)log(1−pi​)]. Fitting this binomial likelihood, checking goodness of fit and quantifying uncertainty are preferable to drawing a curve through noisy proportions. Easy trials help constrain lapses; levels near the transition constrain location and slope. Randomization, stable calibration and checks for learning or fatigue help distinguish a stationary sensory relation from drift. Adaptive procedures require inference appropriate to their sampling design.
Thus the function is both a description and a testable model. Its shape determines what a threshold means, how an experiment should sample, and which claims about sensory mechanisms are warranted.
