Hearing requires estimating sound-producing events from noisy mixtures at two ears. It involves mechanical detection, neural coding, source segregation and recognition. A frequency analysis alone cannot identify who or what produced the sound.
The physical input is an acoustic pressure fluctuation. At one ear a simplified room model is
p(t)=∑j​(hj​∗sj​)(t)+n(t),
(5)
where source signals sj​ are filtered by propagation and reflection paths hj​, and n is noise. The convolution model assumes approximately linear, time-invariant propagation over the analysis interval; a moving source or listener can violate that simplification. This is a many-to-one inverse problem: different source configurations can produce similar ear signals. Distance, reverberation and head orientation alter a source's waveform. Pressure level is logarithmic: sound pressure level is Lp​=20log10​(prms​/p0​), with reference p0​=20μPa in air. A 100 dB difference corresponds to a pressure ratio 10100/20=105. The system must remain sensitive to weak signals while avoiding saturation over a large operating range.
The middle ear helps transfer airborne vibrations into cochlear fluids despite an acoustic impedance mismatch. Within the cochlea, the graded mechanics of the basilar membrane produce frequency-dependent traveling-wave responses: high frequencies peak nearer the base, low frequencies farther toward the apex. This tonotopy provides a distributed spectral representation. Deflection of sensory hair cells drives mechanotransduction; inner hair cells provide most afferent signaling, while outer hair cells contribute to mechanical amplification and tuning. Detection therefore depends on an active biological filter, not merely a passive bank of resonators.
A revealing experiment by Mario A. Ruggero and colleagues measured chinchilla basilar-membrane vibrations using the Mössbauer technique or Doppler laser velocimetry before and after a reversible intervention that reduced cochlear transduction currents. Responses near the characteristic frequency were reduced particularly strongly for weak sounds, with smaller changes at high levels and away from that frequency.[ref] This supports feedback-dependent cochlear gain. The intervention also affects other cochlear processes, so it does not by itself identify every cellular contribution or directly measure human loudness. The important methodological contrast is controlled sound input plus measured mechanical output and a reversible physiological perturbation.
The resulting neural code must preserve several attributes simultaneously. Place of excitation and neural phase locking can provide complementary frequency and temporal information; firing rates, adaptation and population responses encode level and envelopes. Individual neurons have limited dynamic ranges, and temporal precision has limits. Pitch is not simply the location of the strongest spectral component, and timbre depends on spectral shape and temporal development. Even physically, a signal containing harmonics at 200 and 300 Hz repeats every 10 ms although it contains no 100 Hz component: both components complete integer cycles in that interval. Recovering source periodicity is therefore different from selecting a component in a frequency spectrum. It does not follow that every such signal has one obligatory perceptual pitch.
Localization adds ambiguity. Ear separation yields interaural time differences; head shadow yields interaural level differences; outer-ear filtering supplies direction-dependent spectral cues. In an ideal far-field point-receiver model, Δt≃dsinθ/c, obtained by dividing the path-length difference by sound speed. Real head diffraction modifies this approximation. One time difference can be compatible with several spatial directions, and phase is ambiguous when several cycles fit the delay. Head-related transfer functions, head movements and environmental context help resolve these ambiguities. Reverberation also requires distinguishing useful early information from later reflections.
Competing sources create both energetic masking, in which the target is poorly represented against overlapping acoustic energy, and informational masking, involving source confusion or selection despite available acoustic cues. Common onsets, harmonic relations, coherent modulation, timbre and spatial cues help group components into auditory objects. The grouping must remain stable when streams overlap, but also update when a source changes. These are the problems of auditory scene analysis.
E. Colin Cherry's classic experiments used simultaneous spoken messages and separate messages delivered to the two ears. Asking listeners to repeat one message while ignoring the other made source selection experimentally measurable.[ref] Ear and voice differences can aid selection; the result does not show that unattended information is never processed, nor that real rooms provide the clean separation of headphone channels.
Finally, meaningful recognition requires tolerance of speaker, rate and context variation. Richard M. Warren removed a speech segment and substituted a cough-like sound. Listeners could hear the utterance as continuous and had difficulty locating the replacement, whereas a silent interruption was more readily detected.[ref] Phonemic restoration shows that recognition uses constraints beyond the momentary waveform; it does not mean missing acoustic information has been physically recovered. Successful auditory perception combines peripheral sensitivity with grouping and context-sensitive inference, while retaining the possibility of masking, ambiguity and systematic error.
