Conic section

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Euclidean geometry
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 20
A conic section is obtained by intersecting a plane with a double circular cone. Cuts away from the vertex produce an ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola; a perpendicular cut is the circle case of an ellipse. A cut through the vertex can instead produce a degenerate conic. One simple surface therefore connects several familiar plane curves.[ref]
Consider the original family
z2=x2+y2,z=1+mx.
(27)
Substituting the plane equation into the double circular cone equation gives
(1−m2)x2+y2−2mx−1=0.
(28)
Thus m=0 gives a circle, m=3/5 an ellipse, m=1 a parabola, and m=7/5 a hyperbola. The last cut meets both sheets of the double circular cone, producing its two branches. These parameters specify a mathematical family, rather than measurements of a physical object.
The Cartesian coordinate system descriptions of these curves are second-degree equations
Ax2+Bxy+Cy2+Dx+Ey+F=0.
(29)
A rotation and a translation expose their principal directions and offsets. The conic type discriminant identifies the ordinary nondegenerate real type, while a separate degenerate conic check prevents confusing two crossing straight lines with a hyperbola.
This is useful because a difficult-looking equation can be understood through a small set of geometric features: foci of a conic, directrices of a conic, eccentricity of a conic and the semi-major and semi-minor axes. The same ellipse can be traced by a constant-length string or parametrized by x=acost, y=bsint. These are different descriptions of one curve, with different advantages for construction and calculation.
