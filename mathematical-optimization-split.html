Mathematical optimization

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics
OurBigBook.com Words: 75k Articles: 1k
Mathematical optimization turns a preference into a precise question: among the allowed decisions, which gives the best outcome? A model separates the objective function, which measures quality, from the optimization constraints, which determine the feasible set. This language unifies finding a cheap plan for a transportation problem, efficient scheduling, or an accurate model in linear regression. The separation is useful because changing a preference and changing what is possible are different modeling decisions. See Boyd and Vandenberghe, introductory optimization models.
For an objective function f:F→R, a maximization problem asks for a feasible point z∗​ satisfying f(z∗​)≥f(z) for every z∈F; a minimization problem reverses the inequality. This is a global extremum. A local maximum or local minimum compares only nearby feasible points, so finding one does not settle the global question.
Consider allocating ten units between two complementary inputs:
maxxysubject to x+y=10,x,y≥0.
(1)
The budget constraint forces a tradeoff: an extra unit of x costs a unit of y. The best allocation is (5,5), with objective function value 25. Without the budget constraint, x=y=t gives xy=t2→∞: there is no global maximum. Constrained optimization can therefore have a finite answer even when the corresponding problem with fewer optimization constraints does not. Its beauty is that geometry, calculus, and an arithmetic-geometric mean inequality can certify the same answer.
