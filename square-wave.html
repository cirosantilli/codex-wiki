Square wave (sgn(sinθ)=π4​∑n≥1, n odd​nsinnθ​)

A square wave alternates between two constant levels for equal halves of a period. Choose a 2π-periodic function equal to 1 on (0,π) and −1 on (−π,0), with value zero at the jumps. Its odd function symmetry makes the mean and every cosine Fourier coefficient vanish. Directly integrating the two constant pieces gives
a0​=an​=0,bn​=πn2[1−(−1)n]​,f(x)∼π4​∑k=0∞​2k+1sin((2k+1)x)​.
(16)
Thus only odd sine Fourier harmonics occur, with amplitudes decreasing like the reciprocal of their frequency multiplier. Every finite Fourier partial sum is smooth, yet their pointwise limit has jumps. At those jumps the Fourier series converges to zero, the midpoint of the two levels, regardless of which value we assign to the original function there.
This example explains how smooth components can encode sharp transitions. Increasing the number of Fourier harmonics narrows the transition region, but also produces the persistent overshoot described by the Gibbs phenomenon. The illustrated square wave and coefficient calculation are mathematical constructions, not measurements of a physical device.
