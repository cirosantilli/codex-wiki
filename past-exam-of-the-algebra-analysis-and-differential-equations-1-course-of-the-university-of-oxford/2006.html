Yes. The matrix is a cyclic shift operator on three coordinates. Since e1​,Ae1​,A2e1​ are the three standard basis vectors, no polynomial of degree below three annihilates A. Also A3=I, so its minimal polynomial is X3−1. Over the complex numbers this has the distinct roots 1,ω,ω2, where ω=e2πi/3. The minimal polynomial criterion for diagonalizability therefore proves diagonalizability.
Explicitly, vλ​=(1,λ2,λ)T satisfies Avλ​=λvλ​ when λ3=1. These three eigenvectors form an eigenbasis. This is a special case of diagonalization of a cyclic shift over a field.

No. The same cyclic-vector argument gives minimal polynomial X3−1=(X−1)(X2+X+1) over the finite field F2​. The quadratic has no root in F2​: its value is one at both zero and one. Thus the minimal polynomial does not split into linear factors over the specified field, violating the minimal polynomial criterion for diagonalizability. It becomes diagonalizable over a suitable larger finite field, but that is a different question.

No. In characteristic three, X3−1=(X−1)3. This is still the minimal polynomial, because the three cyclic basis vectors remain independent. Its repeated factor violates the minimal polynomial criterion for diagonalizability. Equivalently, the only possible eigenvalue is one; a diagonalizable matrix with that sole eigenvalue would be the identity matrix, whereas this matrix is not the identity.

Construct the vectors inductively by the Gram-Schmidt process, proving its needed properties directly. Suppose w1​,…,wj−1​ form an orthonormal basis of Uj−1​, and put
rj​=uj​−∑k<j​⟨uj​,wk​⟩wk​.
(2)
For every i<j, taking an inner product with wi​ gives ⟨rj​,wi​⟩=0. Also rj​=0, because otherwise uj​ would lie in the linear span of its predecessors, contrary to linear independence. Define
wj​=rj​/∥rj​∥.​
(3)
It lies in Uj​, has unit norm, and is orthogonal to every ui​ with i<j, since those vectors lie in Uj−1​. Writing uj​=rj​+∑k<j​⟨uj​,wk​⟩wk​ gives ⟨wj​,uj​⟩=∥rj​∥>0. The same identity shows that w1​,…,wj​ span Uj​, completing the induction, including the empty sum at j=1.
For uniqueness, any candidate w∈Uj​ expands in the basis w1​,…,wj−1​,rj​. Orthogonality to every earlier ui​, hence to every earlier wi​, makes its first j−1 coefficients zero. Thus w=trj​. Unit norm forces t=±1/∥rj​∥, and ⟨w,uj​⟩>0 forces the positive sign. This proves uniqueness in the Gram-Schmidt process with positive normalization without assuming a projection theorem or any other major orthogonalization result.

The evaluation homomorphism R[X]→R, p↦p(0), is surjective and has kernel of a ring homomorphism J1​=(X). Hence J1​ is an ideal, and the first isomorphism theorem for rings gives R[X]/J1​≅R​. Since this quotient is a field, J1​ is maximal​.

A closed curve has γ(a)=γ(b). A simple curve has no repeated points apart from the permitted equality of its two endpoints when it is closed. A smooth curve, in the regular contour-integration convention, has a continuous nonzero tangent γ′; piecewise smooth contours are treated by adding the integrals over their smooth pieces. For a continuous integrand, its contour integral is defined by
∫γ​f(z)dz=∫ab​f(γ(t))γ′(t)dt.​
(5)
For the positively oriented circle, γ′(t)=ireit and (γ(t)−z0​)n=rneint. Direct parameter integration therefore gives
∫γ​(z−z0​)ndz=irn+1∫02π​ei(n+1)tdt={2πi,0,​n=−1,n=−1.​​
(6)
For n=−1, an elementary antiderivative is ei(n+1)t/[i(n+1)] and its endpoint values agree because n is an integer. For n=−1, the integrand of the parameter integral is the constant i. No integral theorem is needed.

A holomorphic function with f′(z)=0 at every point is locally a conformal map. Write a=z0​. Since ∣a∣<1 and ∣z∣<1, 1−az=0, and
f′(z)=(1−az)2∣a∣2−1​=0.​
(9)
Thus the specified Möbius transformation is conformal throughout the disk. Expanding the two squared absolute values gives
1−∣f(z)∣2=∣1−az∣2(1−∣a∣2)(1−∣z∣2)​>0,​
(10)
so f maps the open unit disk into itself. Solving w=(a−z)/(1−az) gives z=(a−w)/(1−aw)=f(w). Equivalently f(f(z))=z. Hence f is its own inverse, proving both injectivity and surjectivity on the disk. Nonvanishing derivative establishes the local angle-preserving property; the explicit inverse supplies the separate global bijection claim.

The fundamental theorem of calculus first gives y′(x)=1+∫0x​f(y(s))ds. Integrating again, or differentiating the proposed expression twice and checking its initial values, yields the Volterra integral equation
y(x)=x+∫0x​(x−s)f(y(s))ds.​
(11)
For f(y)=−y, the initial value problem is solved by y(x)=sinx​. Its Picard iteration constructs the sine series directly. The base approximation is y0​(x)=x. If
yn​(x)=∑k=0n​(−1)k(2k+1)!x2k+1​,
(12)
then elementary integration gives
∫0x​(x−s)(2k+1)!s2k+1​ds=(2k+3)!x2k+3​.
(13)
Substituting this finite sum in the next update, then shifting its index, proves the same formula with n+1 in place of n. This is the required mathematical induction. The resulting partial sums converge to sinx​ for every real x, and uniformly on every bounded interval: the absolute terms on ∣x∣≤R are bounded by the convergent series ∑k​R2k+1/(2k+1)!.
The printed update lists n=1,2,…, which would leave y1​ unspecified if read literally. The usual Picard iteration, and the requested induction from y0​, use the update starting at n=0; that intended convention is used here.

The characteristic equation of a partial differential equation for uxx​+uxy​=0 is (dy)2−dxdy=0. Thus the two characteristic curve families are y=constant,y−x=constant​. Set ξ=y and η=y−x. The chain rule gives ∂x​=−∂η​ and ∂y​=∂ξ​+∂η​, reducing the equation to
Uξη​=0.​
(16)
Integrating in the two independent characteristic coordinates gives U=F(ξ)+G(η), or u(x,y)=F(y)+G(y−x). At x=0, the value condition gives F(y)+G(y)=0, while the derivative condition gives −G′(y)=ey. Hence G(y)=−ey+C and F(y)=ey−C, so
u(x,y)=ey−ey−x.​
(17)
Direct differentiation gives ux​=ey−x, uxx​=−ey−x and uxy​=ey−x, verifying both the equation and the two data. The additive constants cancel in u, so they do not create different solutions.
