A self-adjoint operator satisfies ⟨Tx,y⟩=⟨x,Ty⟩ for every x,y. The finite-dimensional finite-dimensional spectral theorem gives an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors and real eigenvalues; eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. Consequently a matrix represents a self-adjoint transformation in some, possibly nonorthonormal, basis exactly when it is diagonalizable with real eigenvalues.
For A0​, the eigenvalues are 2,1,0, all distinct and real. Eigenvectors are e1​, e2​, and (0,−1,1)T. Thus A0​ can represent a self-adjoint transformation after choosing a suitable basis.
For A1​, the eigenvalue one has algebraic multiplicity two but its eigenspace has dimension one. The lower 2×2 block is a nontrivial Jordan block, so the matrix is not diagonalizable. Hence A1​ cannot represent a self-adjoint transformation in any basis.
For A2​, the eigenspace for two is spanned by e1​ and (0,1,1)T, and the eigenvalue-one eigenspace is spanned by e2​. These three vectors form an eigenbasis. Therefore A2​ can represent a self-adjoint transformation. To see sufficiency on the given inner product space, first define the real diagonal transformation in an orthonormal basis and then apply the inverse of a diagonalizing basis change to obtain the requested matrix. Symmetry of the displayed matrix itself would be required only for an orthonormal representing basis.
