The dual space is the vector space of all linear functionals on V:
V′=HomF​(V,F).​
(1)
Addition and scalar multiplication are defined pointwise. The annihilator of a vector subspace is
U0={f∈V′:f(u)=0 for every u∈U}.​
(2)
It is a vector subspace of V′: linear combinations of functionals vanishing on U also vanish on U.

First suppose E=E∗. For u∈U=imE, choose v with Ev=u. For w∈W=kerE,
⟨u,w⟩=⟨Ev,w⟩=⟨v,Ew⟩=0.

(10)

E=E∗⟺U=W⊥.​
(11)
This is the characterization of an orthogonal projection among projections along a complementary subspace.

A Euclidean domain is an integral domain R admitting a Euclidean function δ:R∖{0}→Z≥0​ such that division with a smaller remainder is possible: for every a∈R and nonzero b∈R, there are q,r∈R with
a=qb+r,r=0 or δ(r)<δ(b).​
(12)
Thus the Euclidean function measures progress in repeated division. Definitions which additionally normalize it to be nondecreasing under multiplication give the same class of Euclidean domains; the division property is the one needed below.

The quantitative Picard-Lindelöf theorem applies on a rectangle ∣x−x0​∣≤a, ∥Z−B∥∞​≤b. Suppose F is continuous there, bounded in maximum norm by M, and uniformly Lipschitz continuous in Z. Then the initial value problem Y′=F(x,Y), Y(x0​)=B, has a unique solution on
∣x−x0​∣≤h,h=min{a,b/M},​
(53)
with the usual interpretation b/M=∞ if M=0. The solution remains in the dependent-variable ball. Its endpoints are included when F is continuous on the closed rectangle, as here. Equivalently, existence first on every smaller interval and the derivative bound give extension to those endpoints.
For the given cube, x0​=0 and a=b=1. Thus the theorem gives h=min{1,1/M} once a bound M and a uniform Lipschitz constant have been established. The theorem does not require Lh<1: convergence of Picard iteration can be proved by factorial estimates or by using successive shorter intervals.

They disagree, for example at x=0, so the normal derivative is not continuous across the interface. The jump, defined as upper trace minus lower trace, is 2πcos(πx). It vanishes at the two interior points x=±1/2, but not identically on the interface.
This illustrates that continuous transmission need not preserve normal derivatives. In fact, at any point where these traces differ, the two-sided first derivative with respect to y does not exist. The resulting function is continuous and smooth separately on each open side, but is not globally C1. Consequently a globally classical C2 solution satisfying all these data cannot exist. The calculation in part (c) evaluates the smooth lower formula on the interface; it does not supply the missing cross-interface regularity.
