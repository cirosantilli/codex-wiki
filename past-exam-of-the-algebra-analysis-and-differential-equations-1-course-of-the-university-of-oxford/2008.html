The minimal polynomial mT​ is the unique monic polynomial of least degree with mT​(T)=0. Existence follows from a linear dependence among powers of T in the finite-dimensional vector space End(V); polynomial division proves uniqueness and that it divides every annihilating polynomial. The characteristic polynomial is χT​(t)=det(tI−[T]B​), independent of basis because a change of basis conjugates the matrix.
Write
Tnv=−a0​v−a1​Tv−⋯−an−1​Tn−1v.
(1)
In the cyclic basis, the companion matrix is
C=​010⋮0​0010​⋯⋯⋯⋱⋯​000⋮1​−a0​−a1​−a2​⋮−an−1​​​.
(2)
For q(t)=tn+an−1​tn−1+⋯+a0​, the displayed vector relation gives q(T)v=0. Polynomial evaluation commutes with T, so q(T)Tjv=Tjq(T)v=0 for every basis vector. Thus q(T)=0.
No nonzero polynomial of degree below n can annihilate T, since applying it to the cyclic vector v would contradict independence of v,Tv,…,Tn−1v. Hence mT​=q. Finally, direct expansion of det(tI−C), or induction along the last column, gives χT​(t)=tn+an−1​tn−1+⋯+a0​=q(t). Therefore
mT​=χT​=q.​
(3)
The argument computes the determinant and annihilator directly and does not invoke the Cayley-Hamilton theorem.

A projection is a linear operator E satisfying E2=E; it need not be an orthogonal projection without an inner product. Pairwise annihilation gives
P2=(∑i​Ei​)2=∑i​Ei2​+∑i=j​Ei​Ej​=∑i​Ei​=P.
(4)
Thus their sum is a projection.
The identities Ei​P=Ei​ imply T(I−P)=0. Since T is nonsingular, P=I. Also
TEi​=∑j​λj​Ej​Ei​=λi​Ei​,
(5)
so imEi​⊆Vi​. Conversely, if Tx=λi​x, applying Ej​ gives (λj​−λi​)Ej​x=0. For j=i the eigenvalues are distinct, hence Ej​x=0. Since P=I, x=Ei​x, proving imEi​=Vi​.
Every vector is ∑i​Ei​x. If ∑i​vi​=0 with vi​∈Vi​, applying Ej​ gives vj​=0. Thus
V=V1​⊕⋯⊕Vr​.​
(6)
These operators are the spectral projections of a diagonalizable matrix, expressed without choosing an eigenbasis.

For a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space, every eigenvalue is real and eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. The spectral theorem gives an orthonormal eigenbasis. Accordingly
V=V1​⊥⋯⊥Vr​,​
(7)
and the Ei​ are orthogonal projections satisfying Ei∗​=Ei​. Since T is nonsingular, none of its eigenvalues is zero. Its representing matrix in the orthonormal eigenbasis is real diagonal.

Reduction modulo the prime p preserves integer addition, multiplication, and one:
a+b​=aˉ+bˉ,ab=aˉbˉ.
(8)
Every congruence class has an integer representative, so the ring homomorphism is onto. Its kernel is exactly pZ=(p). The quotient is a field because every class not divisible by p has a multiplicative inverse by Bézout's identity.
For the coefficientwise extension to the polynomial ring, addition is preserved term by term. If f=∑i​ai​xi and g=∑j​bj​xj, the coefficient of xk in their product is ∑i+j=k​ai​bj​. Reducing it modulo p gives ∑i+j=k​aˉi​bˉj​, the coefficient in ϕ^​(f)ϕ^​(g). Thus multiplication and one are preserved as well. Lift each coefficient of a polynomial over Zp​ to an integer to prove surjectivity. The kernel is
kerϕ^​=pZ[x]=(p).​
(9)
Indeed, its elements are precisely the polynomials whose coefficients are all divisible by p.
In the binomial theorem expansion of (x−1)p, every intermediate coefficient (jp​) is divisible by p: the numerator contains the prime factor p, while j!(p−j)! does not for 1≤j<p. Hence
(x−1)p=xp+(−1)p=xp−1in Zp​[x].
(10)
For p=2, the last equality uses 1=−1 in characteristic two. This is an instance of the Frobenius endomorphism.

Write z=x+iy, so the real and imaginary components are u=x2 and v=y2. The Cauchy-Riemann equations require ux​=vy​, namely 2x=2y; the other equation is uy​=−vx​=0. The first relation fails off the line x=y, so the function is not holomorphic on C. It is complex differentiable at points of that line, but a line contains no open neighborhood on which the equations hold.

The reciprocal map is holomorphic for z=0, and the complex exponential is entire. Its denominator vanishes exactly when
e2πi/z=1⟺z−1∈Z.

All these points are excluded from U. The set U is open: it is the inverse image of C∖Z under the reciprocal map on C∖{0}. A reciprocal of a nonvanishing holomorphic function is holomorphic. Therefore f is holomorphic throughout U.

The set U has two open connected components, the left and right half-planes. On each the function is constant in a neighborhood of every point, so its complex derivative is zero everywhere. Thus f is holomorphic on U. A holomorphic function can take different constant values on different components of a disconnected domain.

At any point on the imaginary axis, approaching from the left gives limit −1, while the assigned value and the right-hand limit are 1. Thus the function is discontinuous on that axis. Complex differentiability implies continuity, so f is not holomorphic on C. It is holomorphic separately in the two open half-planes.

The hyperbolic cosine is entire and cosh0=1. Continuity makes it nonzero in a neighborhood of zero, so its reciprocal is holomorphic there and has a convergent Taylor series. Since cosh and its reciprocal are even, all odd coefficients vanish.
Multiply the proposed expansion by coshz:
(1+c2​z2+c4​z4+⋯)(1+21​z2+241​z4+⋯)=1.
(12)
Coefficient comparison gives c2​+1/2=0 and c4​+c2​/2+1/24=0, hence
c2​=−21​,c4​=245​.​
(13)
The zeros of coshz solve e2z=−1, so they are z=i(π/2+kπ), k∈Z. The nearest ones are ±iπ/2, and they are simple because sinh is nonzero there. The hyperbolic secant has genuine poles at these points and is holomorphic in the disk of radius π/2. The radius of convergence of its Taylor series is therefore
R=π/2.​
(14)

Let F(z)=eiλz/(z−i). For λ>0, close the interval [−R,R] by the upper semicircle, counterclockwise. Its only enclosed pole is z=i, with residue e−λ. The residue theorem gives
∫−RR​F(x)dx+∫ΓR+​​F(z)dz=2πie−λ.
(15)
On z=Reiθ, ∣eiλz∣=e−λRsinθ and ∣z−i∣≥R−1. Hence the arc is bounded by
R−1R​∫0π​e−λRsinθdθ⟶0
(16)
by the supplied estimate, with parameter λR.
For λ<0, close below, with clockwise orientation. The pole at i lies outside, and the exponential decays there. Parametrizing the lower arc yields the same bound with ∣λ∣R in place of λR. Its contribution again tends to zero, and no residue remains. Therefore
R→∞lim​∫−RR​x−ieiλx​dx={2πie−λ,0,​λ>0,λ<0.​​
(17)
This is the contour-decay mechanism behind Jordan's lemma; the pole contributes only when the decaying closure encloses it.

Rewrite the linear equation as (x2y)′=0. The initial value gives the unique solution
y(x)=x−2(x>0).​
(18)
The Picard iteration is its integral equation
yn+1​(x)=1−2∫1x​tyn​(t)​dt.
(19)
The proposed formula holds for n=0. If it holds at n, termwise integration of the finite sum and d(logt)/dt=1/t give
yn+1​(x)=1+∑k=0n​(k+1)!(−2logx)k+1​=∑k=0n+1​k!(−2logx)k​.
(20)
This proves the expression by induction. The exponential series then gives yn​(x)→e−2logx=x−2. Convergence is also uniform on every compact subinterval of (0,∞), since ∣logx∣ is bounded there and the exponential majorant series converges. This is Picard iteration for a simple-pole linear differential equation.

Write the principal part as e2yuxx​−uyy​. The characteristic curves satisfy
e2y(dy)2−(dx)2=0,
(21)
or dx/dy=±ey. Integration gives x+ey=constant and x−ey=constant. Thus take ξ=x+ey, η=x−ey. Their Jacobian determinant is −2ey=0, and their image is ξ>η.
For u(x,y)=U(ξ,η), the chain rule gives
uxx​=Uξξ​+2Uξη​+Uηη​,uy​=ey(Uξ​−Uη​),
(22)
uyy​=e2y(Uξξ​−2Uξη​+Uηη​)+ey(Uξ​−Uη​).
(23)
The first-derivative terms cancel exactly. The canonical form of a second-order partial differential equation is
4e2yUξη​=0,U=F(ξ)+G(η).
(24)
On y=0, F(x+1)+G(x−1)=x. Differentiating this condition in x and using uy​=1 gives
F′(x+1)+G′(x−1)=1,F′(x+1)−G′(x−1)=1.
(25)
Hence F′=1 and G′=0. Substitution into the value condition fixes their combined constant and yields
u(x,y)=x+ey−1.​
(26)
Direct substitution verifies the PDE and both initial data. The cancellation illustrates a hyperbolic equation reduced by an exponential characteristic coordinate.
