Let mT​ be the minimal polynomial. If Tv=λv with v=0, then 0=mT​(T)v=mT​(λ)v, so mT​(λ)=0. Conversely, if mT​(λ)=0, write mT​(X)=(X−λ)q(X). If T−λI were invertible, the identity (T−λI)q(T)=0 would imply q(T)=0, contradicting minimality of the degree. Thus T−λI has a nonzero kernel of a linear map, and λ is an eigenvalue. This proves the eigenvalue criterion using the minimal polynomial for roots in the field under consideration.

For affine polynomials f(t)=a+bt and g(t)=c+dt, direct integration gives ⟨f,g⟩=2ac+2bd/3. This is bilinear and symmetric, and ⟨f,f⟩=2a2+2b2/3>0 for every nonzero f. Thus it is an inner product.
The adjoint of differentiation on affine polynomials is determined by ⟨Df,g⟩=⟨f,D∗g⟩. Since Df=b, the left side is 2bc. Writing D∗g=A+Bt, the right side is 2aA+2bB/3. Equality for every a,b forces A=0, B=3c, hence
D∗(c+dt)=3ct.​
(3)
This is not simply negative differentiation: there are no endpoint conditions here that would remove the boundary term in an integration by parts argument.

An ideal is an additive subgroup of a ring closed under multiplication by arbitrary ring elements; in a potentially noncommutative ring, a two-sided ideal must absorb multiplication on both sides. For a ring homomorphism ϕ, its kernel of a ring homomorphism K={r:ϕ(r)=0} contains zero, is closed under subtraction, and satisfies ϕ(rk)=ϕ(r)ϕ(k)=0 and ϕ(kr)=0. Thus K is an ideal.
Define ϕ​:R/K→S by r+K↦ϕ(r). It is well-defined because two representatives differ by an element of K, preserves addition and multiplication, is surjective by hypothesis, and is injective because its zero class is precisely K. Therefore R/kerϕ≅S​, the first isomorphism theorem for rings.
Apply this to the evaluation homomorphism C[z]→C, p↦p(1). Constants prove surjectivity, and division by z−1 shows that its kernel of a ring homomorphism is (z−1). Hence C[z]/(z−1)≅C​.

A Möbius transformation has the form M(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d) with ad−bc=0, extended to the Riemann sphere by its limiting values at its pole and at infinity. If c=0, it sends −d/c to infinity and infinity to a/c; if c=0, infinity maps to infinity.
Represent M by the invertible matrix (ac​bd​), with nonzero scalar multiples representing the same map. Substitution shows that composition corresponds to matrix multiplication. The product determinant is the product of two nonzero determinants, so the composition is again a Möbius transformation. Solving w=M(z) gives
M−1(w)=a−cwdw−b​,​
(9)
with the same extended-value convention. This inverse proves that M is a bijection of the extended complex plane.

Since the exponential factor is positive, equilibria must have y=0 and x4=1. Thus (x,y)=(−1,0), (1,0)​. At either point the Jacobian matrix is (0−4x3​10​). At (−1,0) its eigenvalues are ±2, so this is a saddle point. At (1,0) they are ±2i: the linearization has a centre equilibrium.
The requested first-order approximation follows by putting x=1+u, y=v: u˙=v+O(∥(u,v)∥2) and v˙=−4u+O(∥(u,v)∥2). The linearized system conserves 4u2+v2, giving the approximate ellipses
4(x−1)2+y2=constant.​
(11)
However, the printed nonlinear system has a weak repelling focus at (1,0), rather than a centre. A centre in the linearization need not be a nonlinear centre. Here that distinction can be proved explicitly, without relying on the numerical drift in the figure.
Put X=2(x−1), Y=y, τ=2t and r2=X2+Y2. The weak focus of an exponential polynomial planar system has the local positive function
H=2X2+Y2​+127​X3+21​XY2+31​Y3+41​X4−163​X3Y+163​XY3.
(12)
Expanding the vector field through cubic order and differentiating gives
dτdH​=163​r4+O(r5)>0for sufficiently small r>0.​
(13)
The cubic correction cancels the cubic terms in the derivative of H, and the quartic correction leaves the positive radial term 3r4/16. Since H=r2/2+O(r3) is positive and the angular velocity stays near −1, small nonzero trajectories spiral outward. Equivalently the reversed system has a strict local Lyapunov function and an asymptotically stable equilibrium. In particular, a periodic orbit sufficiently near the equilibrium is impossible, because H would have to increase strictly and return to its starting value. This proves the failure of the printed centre assertion while retaining its correct linearized ellipse calculation.

The characteristic equations for a first-order quasilinear equation are x˙=x, y˙​=y, u˙=2u, with characteristic parameter s. Through the prescribed point they give
(x(s),y(s),u(s))=(x0​es,y0​es,u0​e2s).​
(14)
For a nonzero projected initial point, the characteristic projections are rays from the origin, not trajectories crossing through the origin to the opposite ray. If x0​=y0​=0, a classical solution graph requires u0​=0, because the original equation reduces there to 0=2u.
Parameterize the initial line by (x,y,u)=(a,1−a,a3). Along its characteristic, x+y=es>0, a=x/(x+y) and u=a3e2s. Therefore
u(x,y)=x+yx3​,x+y>0.​
(15)
Differentiation verifies the equation, and substitution of x+y=1 verifies the initial data. Characteristic reachability for radial transport shows why the domain is this positive half-plane: each point there lies on precisely one characteristic from the data line. Rays in x+y<0 never meet that line, so their values are not determined by these data. Allowing a negative scaling factor would incorrectly join two different characteristic rays through a degenerate origin.
