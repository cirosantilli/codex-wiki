The dual space consists of the linear functionals on the original vector space, with addition and scalar multiplication defined pointwise:
V′=HomF​(V,F).​
(1)
The dual map acts by function composition with T:
T′:V′⟶U′,T′(λ)=λ∘T.​
(2)
For u∈U, its value is (T′λ)(u)=λ(Tu). The composite is a linear functional because both T and λ are linear maps. Moreover T′(aλ+bμ)=aT′λ+bT′μ, so the dual map is itself a linear map. Its direction is reversed because a measurement on the output of T becomes a measurement on its input.

The annihilator of a vector subspace consists of the linear functionals that vanish on every element of that vector subspace:
W0={λ∈V′:λ(w)=0 for every w∈W},dimW0=dimV−dimW.​
(3)
For the dimension identity, choose a basis w1​,…,wk​ of W and use basis extension to obtain w1​,…,wn​ for V. A linear functional is determined by its values on these n basis vectors. Belonging to the annihilator of a vector subspace sets its first k values to zero and leaves its last n−k values arbitrary. Equivalently the last n−k elements of the dual basis form a basis of W0.

A diagonalizable matrix over the field F admits an invertible matrix P with entries in F such that
P−1AP=Dwith D a diagonal matrix over F.​
(9)
Equivalently, the vector space Fn has a basis of eigenvectors of A. Indeed, AP=PD says that each column of P is an eigenvector with the corresponding diagonal entry as its eigenvalue. Requiring the basis and eigenvalues to lie in F makes the choice of field essential.

A prime ideal is a proper ideal I=R such that ab∈I implies a∈I or b∈I. A maximal ideal is a proper ideal I=R such that there is no ideal strictly between I and R.
Every ideal of the integers has the form (n)=nZ for a unique nonnegative integer n. Its prime ideals and maximal ideals are respectively
{(0)}∪{(p):p prime},{(p):p prime}.​
(16)
The zero ideal is a prime ideal because the integers form an integral domain. For a prime number p, divisibility of ab by p forces divisibility of one factor, so (p) is a prime ideal. Also any ideal (d) containing (p) has d∣p, so it equals (p) or (1); thus (p) is a maximal ideal.
If n>1 is composite, write n=ab with 1<a,b<n. Then ab∈(n) while neither factor belongs to (n), so (n) is not a prime ideal. A prime number p∣n gives (n)⊊(p)⊊Z, so (n) is not a maximal ideal either. The zero ideal is not maximal since (0)⊊(2)⊊Z, and (1)=Z is not proper.

If the quotient ring R/I is an integral domain, then I=R, because an integral domain has 1=0. For ab∈I,
(a+I)(b+I)=0+I.
(17)
There are no nonzero zero divisors in an integral domain, so a+I=0+I or b+I=0+I. Thus a∈I or b∈I, proving I is a prime ideal.
If R/I is a field, again I is proper. Let J be an ideal properly containing I, and choose a∈J∖I. The nonzero element a+I has a multiplicative inverse b+I, so ab−1∈I⊂J. Since ab∈J, subtraction gives 1∈J, hence J=R. Therefore I is a maximal ideal. These arguments establish the relevant prime ideal quotient criterion and maximal ideal quotient criterion.

Set X=0 to define a surjective ring homomorphism
E:Q[X,Y]⟶Q[Y],E(P)=P(0,Y).
(18)
Its kernel of a ring homomorphism is exactly (X): the constant coefficient in X must vanish, so every remaining monomial is divisible by X. The first isomorphism theorem for rings therefore gives
Q[X,Y]/(X)≅Q[Y].​
(19)
The polynomial ring Q[Y] is an integral domain, because the product of two nonzero polynomials has nonzero leading coefficient. It is not a field: Y has no polynomial multiplicative inverse, since a nonzero product YQ(Y) has positive degree of a polynomial. Consequently (X) is prime and is not maximal. Concretely, (X)⊊(X,Y)⊊Q[X,Y] is a strict chain of ideals.

A subset Ω⊆Rn is an open set if every x∈Ω admits a radius r>0 such that the open ball
B(x,r)={y∈Rn:∣y−x∣<r}
(20)

Put F=Rn∖Ω. This is a closed set. First assume F is nonempty. The Heine-Borel theorem makes the nonempty bounded closed set K a compact set. By the preceding parts, x↦d(x,F) is a continuous function and is strictly positive at every x∈K, because K⊂Ω.
The extreme value theorem gives x∗​∈K attaining its minimum. Therefore
dist(K,F)=x∈Kmin​d(x,F)=d(x∗​,F)>0.​
(24)
The equality with the distance between two sets follows by taking the two infima successively. This is the principle that a compact subset of an open set has positive distance from its complement.
If Ω=Rn, its complement is empty. With the usual extended-real convention inf∅=+∞, the stated inequality holds in that case too.

A holomorphic function on the open set Ω is complex differentiable at every point of Ω: the limit
f′(z)=limh→0, h∈C​hf(z+h)−f(z)​
(25)
exists independently of the direction of approach. Write f(x+iy)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y). The Cauchy-Riemann equations are
ux​=vy​,uy​=−vx​.​
(26)
To prove necessity, approach first with real h, obtaining f′(z)=ux​+ivx​. Approach instead with h=it for real t, obtaining
f′(z)=i1​(uy​+ivy​)=vy​−iuy​.
(27)
Equality of real and imaginary parts yields the displayed Cauchy-Riemann equations.
A sufficient condition is that u and v have continuous first partial derivatives throughout Ω and satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations there. Indeed, real differentiability gives, for h=a+ib,
f(z+h)−f(z)=fx​a+fy​b+o(∣h∣).
(28)
The Cauchy-Riemann equations imply fy​=ifx​, so this is fx​h+o(∣h∣). Dividing by h proves complex differentiability and f′(z)=fx​(z) at every point. Merely having first partial derivatives and the equations at a point, without a suitable differentiability hypothesis, would not justify this argument.

A conformal map preserves oriented angles between intersecting smooth curves at each point, with a nonzero local scale factor. A sufficient criterion is that f be a holomorphic function on the domain and that f′(z)=0 everywhere there. Its first-order action then multiplies every tangent direction by the same nonzero complex number, combining a rotation and a dilation. A conformal bijection additionally requires injectivity and surjectivity onto the target.
For z=x+iy in the given half-strip,
w=eiz=e−y(cosx+isinx).
(33)
Thus 0<∣w∣<1 and 0<argw<π. Conversely every such w has a unique argument in (0,π) and unique radius in (0,1), giving x=argw, y=−log∣w∣. Hence
eiS=D+​={w:∣w∣<1, Imw>0}.​
(34)
The complex exponential is injective on S, and its derivative ieiz never vanishes.
On D+​, use the Joukowski map with a sign change,
J(w)=−(w+w1​).
(35)
Since
ImJ(w)=Imw(∣w∣21​−1)>0,
(36)
it maps into the upper half-plane. To prove injectivity, J(w1​)=J(w2​) gives
(w1​−w2​)(1−w1​w2​1​)=0.
(37)
Both moduli are below one, so w1​w2​=1 and w1​=w2​.
For surjectivity, take any ζ in the upper half-plane and solve w2+ζw+1=0. The two roots have product one. Neither can lie on the unit circle, since there −(w+w−1) is real. Exactly one root therefore has modulus below one. For this root, the displayed imaginary-part identity forces Imw>0, so it belongs to D+​. Finally J′(w)=w−2−1 has no zero in D+​. This proves the Joukowski map from the upper half-disc to the upper half-plane is a conformal bijection.
Composing gives the required conformal map
F(z)=J(eiz)=−eiz−e−iz=−2cosz: S⟶H.​
(38)
Its derivative 2sinz is nonzero on S, since the zeros of the complex sine are the real integer multiples of π.

For a second-order linear partial differential equation with principal part Auxx​+2Buxy​+Cuyy​, the discriminant is B2−AC. Here A=1, B=0, C=−x2, so
B2−AC=x2>0(x>0).​
(47)
The equation is therefore a hyperbolic partial differential equation throughout its domain. The lower-order term −ux​/x does not affect this classification.
The characteristic curves satisfy
A(dy)2−2Bdxdy+C(dx)2=0,dxdy​=±x.
(48)

ξ=x2−2y,η=x2+2y.
(49)
Their Jacobian determinant is 8x=0, so they define an invertible change of variables on x>0, with image ξ+η>0. Write u(x,y)=U(ξ,η). The chain rule gives
ux​uxx​uyy​​=2x(Uξ​+Uη​),=2(Uξ​+Uη​)+4x2(Uξξ​+2Uξη​+Uηη​),=4(Uξξ​−2Uξη​+Uηη​).​
