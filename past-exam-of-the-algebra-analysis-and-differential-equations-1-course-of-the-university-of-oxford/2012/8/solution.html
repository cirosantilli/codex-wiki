For a second-order linear partial differential equation with principal part Auxx​+2Buxy​+Cuyy​, the discriminant is B2−AC. Here A=1, B=0, C=−x2, so
B2−AC=x2>0(x>0).​
(47)
The equation is therefore a hyperbolic partial differential equation throughout its domain. The lower-order term −ux​/x does not affect this classification.
The characteristic curves satisfy
A(dy)2−2Bdxdy+C(dx)2=0,dxdy​=±x.
(48)
Integrating gives the two families y∓x2/2=constant. Choose coordinates constant along these families:
ξ=x2−2y,η=x2+2y.
(49)
Their Jacobian determinant is 8x=0, so they define an invertible change of variables on x>0, with image ξ+η>0. Write u(x,y)=U(ξ,η). The chain rule gives
ux​uxx​uyy​​=2x(Uξ​+Uη​),=2(Uξ​+Uη​)+4x2(Uξξ​+2Uξη​+Uηη​),=4(Uξξ​−2Uξη​+Uηη​).​
(50)
Both the pure second partial derivatives and the first partial derivatives cancel in the original equation, leaving
16x2Uξη​=0,Uξη​=0.​
(51)
This is the full canonical form of a second-order partial differential equation, including the effect of the lower-order term. Integrating shows Uξ​ is a function of ξ alone, hence
u(x,y)=f(x2−2y)+g(x2+2y),​
(52)
with arbitrary twice differentiable functions f,g. The representation is global on x>0: for each fixed ξ, the allowed η>−ξ form an interval, so Uξ​ is independent of η throughout that interval; after subtracting its antiderivative, the same reasoning in the other coordinate leaves only g(η). These are quadratic characteristic coordinates for a variable-speed wave equation.
For the data on x=1, set s=1−2y, so the other argument is 2−s. The two initial conditions become
f(s)+g(2−s)=1,2(f′(s)+g′(2−s))=2.
(53)
Differentiating the first equation with respect to s also gives f′(s)−g′(2−s)=0. Solving these two linear equations yields f′(s)=g′(2−s)=1/2. Since s ranges over all real numbers,
f(s)=2s​+C,g(t)=2t​−C.
(54)
Consequently
u(x,y)=x2.​
(55)
Indeed uxx​=2, uyy​=0, ux​=2x, so the equation and both initial conditions hold directly.
