Self-adjointness means ⟨Tv,w⟩=⟨v,Tw⟩ for all v,w∈V, or T=T∗. Take the inner product linear in its first variable. If Tv=λv with v=0, then
λ∥v∥2=⟨Tv,v⟩=⟨v,Tv⟩=λ∥v∥2.
(4)
Therefore every eigenvalue is real.
If U is invariant and w∈U⊥, then for every u∈U, Tu∈U and ⟨Tw,u⟩=⟨w,Tu⟩=0. Hence Tw∈U⊥, proving invariance of the orthogonal complement.
Induct on d=dimV. The zero-dimensional case is empty. For d>0, choose an eigenvector and normalize it to a unit vector e. Its span is invariant, so its orthogonal complement is invariant as just proved. The restriction of T to that complement is again self-adjoint. Its dimension is d−1, so induction supplies an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors there. Adjoining e gives an orthonormal eigenbasis of V. This proves the finite-dimensional spectral theorem without assuming it in the argument.

A ring is a set with an abelian-group addition, associative multiplication and both distributive laws; here we use rings with multiplicative identity. A field is a nonzero commutative ring in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse.
The positive integers form neither a ring nor a field. They do not contain an additive identity zero, and they also lack additive inverses.

An essential singularity is an isolated singularity which is neither removable nor a pole; equivalently its Laurent expansion has infinitely many nonzero negative-power terms.
For the final unnumbered classification, the only possible finite singularities are the zeros of the denominator, namely z=±i and z=kπ for integers k. At zero,
f(z)=(1+z2)(sinz/z)21​⟶1,
(8)
so zero is removable, with extension value one. At each nonzero kπ, sinz has a simple zero, the other denominator factor is nonzero and the numerator is nonzero. These points are poles of order two, with leading coefficient (kπ)2/((kπ)2+1).
At z=±i, the polynomial denominator has a simple zero. Since sin(±i)=±isinh1=0 and the numerator is −1, these are simple poles. There are no essential singularities on C and no other finite singularities.

u(x,y)=sin(kx)sin(ky)​
(21)
