The zero polynomial belongs to Iv​. If f,g∈Iv​, linearity gives (f−g)(T)v=0. If a∈K[X] and f∈Iv​, polynomial evaluation obeys (af)(T)=a(T)f(T), so (af)(T)v=0. Thus Iv​ is an ideal of the polynomial ring K[X].
The n+1 vectors v,Tv,…,Tnv are linearly dependent in the n-dimensional vector space. Their dependence supplies a nonzero polynomial of degree at most n in Iv​. Hence this ideal is nonzero. A polynomial ring over a field is a Euclidean domain under degree, and every ideal in a Euclidean domain is principal. It follows that Iv​=(mv​) for a nonzero polynomial mv​, which can be scaled uniquely to be monic.
For completeness, uniqueness follows because any two generators divide each other and hence differ by a nonzero constant; the monic normalization forces that constant to be one. Since mv​ divides the polynomial of degree at most n constructed above, its degree is at most n. The generator characterization gives
mv​∣f⟺f∈Iv​⟺f(T)v=0.​
(1)
This is the minimal polynomial of a vector under a linear operator. If v=0, the ideal is the whole ring and the unique monic generator is the constant polynomial one.

A self-adjoint operator satisfies ⟨Tx,y⟩=⟨x,Ty⟩ for every x,y. The finite-dimensional finite-dimensional spectral theorem gives an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors and real eigenvalues; eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. Consequently a matrix represents a self-adjoint transformation in some, possibly nonorthonormal, basis exactly when it is diagonalizable with real eigenvalues.
For A0​, the eigenvalues are 2,1,0, all distinct and real. Eigenvectors are e1​, e2​, and (0,−1,1)T. Thus A0​ can represent a self-adjoint transformation after choosing a suitable basis.
For A1​, the eigenvalue one has algebraic multiplicity two but its eigenspace has dimension one. The lower 2×2 block is a nontrivial Jordan block, so the matrix is not diagonalizable. Hence A1​ cannot represent a self-adjoint transformation in any basis.
For A2​, the eigenspace for two is spanned by e1​ and (0,1,1)T, and the eigenvalue-one eigenspace is spanned by e2​. These three vectors form an eigenbasis. Therefore A2​ can represent a self-adjoint transformation. To see sufficiency on the given inner product space, first define the real diagonal transformation in an orthonormal basis and then apply the inverse of a diagonalizing basis change to obtain the requested matrix. Symmetry of the displayed matrix itself would be required only for an orthonormal representing basis.

A cubic over a field is reducible exactly when it has a root in that field. For p(X)=X3+2X2+1 modulo five, direct evaluation gives
(p(0),p(1),p(2),p(3),p(4))=(1,4,2,1,2).
(2)
No value is zero, so p is irreducible in Z5​[X].
The integer polynomial in the second part reduces to precisely p, because −2008≡2, 2010≡0 and −2009≡1(mod5). It is monic, hence primitive, and its degree is preserved by reduction. The irreducibility by degree-preserving reduction modulo a prime criterion states that a primitive integer polynomial with irreducible reduction of the same degree is irreducible over the rationals. This follows from Gauss's lemma: a rational factorization would give integer factors whose leading coefficients remain nonzero modulo five, contradicting the irreducible reduction. Thus
X3−2008X2+2010X−2009 is irreducible in Q[X].​
(3)

The modulus map is continuous, the denominator 1+∣z∣ is positive everywhere, and sums, products and quotients with nonzero denominator preserve continuity. Hence f is continuous throughout C.
For g, ∣g(z)∣=∣z∣, so g(z)→0=g(0) as z→0. The chosen argument is continuous locally away from the negative real axis, making g continuous there as well. At a point −r, r>0, approach along z=reiθ. From θ↑π, g(z)→ir; from θ↓−π, g(z)→−ir. The value assigned on the cut is ir. Thus g is discontinuous at every strictly negative real point, and therefore is not a continuous map on the entire complex plane. Its radial factor is r, as printed, rather than the r​ of a square root.

For f=u+iv, the Cauchy-Riemann equations are ux​=vy​ and uy​=−vx​. Holomorphy implies these relations. Conversely, if the first partial derivatives exist and are continuous near a point, satisfying the equations there gives complex differentiability; satisfying them on an open set gives a holomorphic function on that set.
Let r=x2+y2​. On the cut plane, r+x>0: equality could occur only at a nonpositive real point, which is excluded. Thus u=(r+x)/2​ is strictly positive and v=y/(2u) is well defined and smooth. Since y2=(r−x)(r+x),
u2=2r+x​,v2=2r−x​,2uv=y.
(4)
Consequently
(u+iv)2=x+iy=z.​
(5)
One can verify holomorphy directly:
ux​=2ru​=vy​,uy​=2rv​=−vx​.
(6)
Here r>0 throughout the domain, so all partial derivatives are continuous. Therefore f is holomorphic, specifically the principal square root on the cut plane, with derivative f′(z)=1/[2f(z)].

The two bounding circles meet at zero and one, which the Möbius transformation sends to zero and infinity. They therefore become straight lines through zero. More explicitly write w=f(z), so z=w/(1+w). The two disk inequalities become
Re(ζ​w)>0,Re(ζw)>0.
(7)
Since ζ=1/2+i3​/2, these say Rew>3​∣Imw∣. Thus
f(R)={w=0:−π/6<argw<π/6}.​

The positive real axis belongs to the image because f(1/2)=1, fixing which sector is intended. Cubing maps this sector bijectively onto the right half-plane: its argument interval of width π/3 becomes (−π/2,π/2). The Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk v↦(v−1)/(v+1) then maps that half-plane bijectively onto the unit disk. Hence a holomorphic bijection is

Every denominator is nonzero on the corresponding domain, and the sector width ensures the power map is injective. This is the conformal map of a symmetric circular lens.

The characteristic equations for a transport equation are x˙=1, y˙​=2, z˙=−3z. Label an initial point by s∈[0,1] and choose characteristic time zero on the data segment. Integration gives
x=s+t,y=2t,z=ese−3t.
(12)
Thus s=x−y/2 and t=y/2, giving
z(x,y)=ex−2y,0≤x−y/2≤1.​
(13)
This is the full infinite strip between the parallel characteristic lines y=2x and y=2x−2. Both positive and negative characteristic times are allowed because the problem specifies no forward-time restriction. Each characteristic in this strip meets the data segment exactly once. Outside it, the general solution z=e−3y/2F(x−y/2) contains unrestricted values of F, so the data do not determine a unique solution there.

An equilibrium has y=−x/2 from the first equation. Substitution in the second gives −x(x2+3)/2=0, so the origin is the only real equilibrium. Its Jacobian matrix is
J=(1−2​2−1​),λ=±i3​.
(14)
Purely imaginary linear eigenvalues alone do not classify the nonlinear equilibrium. Use instead
H=x2+xy+y2+8x4​.
(15)
Its derivatives are Hx​=2x+y+x3/2 and Hy​=x+2y, so the vector field is (Hy​,−Hx​). The chain rule gives H˙=Hx​Hy​−Hy​Hx​=0. Therefore the nonstationary trajectories in the phase portrait lie on the level curves
x2+xy+y2+x4/8=C.​
(16)
The quadratic part is positive definite, with eigenvalues 1/2 and 3/2, so H has a strict local minimum at zero. Small positive level sets are smooth closed ovals surrounding it, and the vector field never vanishes on them. They are periodic orbits. Thus the origin is a nonlinear centre, Lyapunov stable but not asymptotically stable. The near-origin ellipses have their long axis along y=−x. At (x,0) with x>0, the velocity points downward, fixing clockwise orientation.
