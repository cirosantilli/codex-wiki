The material frame of a rod obeys the director kinematics of a rod
di′​=u×di​.
(1)
In the stress-free reference configuration, the constitutive equation gives u1​=0, u2​=u^, and u3​=0, since all three stiffnesses are positive. Thus u=u^d2​. Its cross product with d2​ vanishes, so
d2′​=u^d2​×d2​=0.​
(2)
Consequently this director is a fixed vector in space, even though the other two directors rotate along the elastic rod.

Zero applied end force and the force balance n′=0 imply n(S)=0 everywhere. The moment balance then reduces to m′=0.
Choose an untwisted straight configuration: all three directors are constant, r′=d3​, and u=0. The constitutive equation gives
m=−EI2​u^d2​,
(9)

(10)
Its sign opposes the positive intrinsic curvature of an elastic filament. If the initial position and frame from part (ii) are retained, this solution is simply r(S)=Se3​ with di​=ei​; the clamp supplies the opposite reaction moment at the other end.

Because the two elastic rods each have thickness a, their centrelines are separated by a along the common director d1​. The bonded geometry is
r+(S+)=r(S)+ad1​(S),S+=S.
(11)
Differentiate with respect to reference arc length. The first rod is an inextensible rod, and the printed director kinematics of a rod give
dS+dr+​=d3​+a(−ud3​)=(1−au)d3​.
(12)
For the physically admissible orientation-preserving configuration 1−au>0, the rods have the same unit tangent d3+​=d3​ and the same planar material frame of a rod. Therefore
α+=1−au.​
(13)
In particular, the second rod is shortened on the inside of a bend with u>0.
The bending strain of a rod is defined here by differentiation with respect to reference arc length. Since the reference labels match,
dS+dd3+​​=dSdd3​​=ud1​=u+d1+​,u+=u,β=1.​
(14)

(15)
Thus u/(1−au) is its signed spatial curvature, rather than the u+ appearing in the given elastic energy.

The first term is the bending energy of rod 1, whose preferred bending strain of a rod is zero. The second is the bending energy of rod 2 relative to its preferred intrinsic curvature of an elastic filament u^. The third is its stretching energy, with stretching stiffness kB2​ and stress-free stretch ratio α+=1. Each term is an energy per unit reference arc length before integration.
Substitute u+=u and α+−1=−au. The resulting quadratic function of u has density
q(u)=21​(B1​+B2​+ka2B2​)u2−B2​u^u+21​B2​u^2.
(16)
There are no derivatives of u or imposed end constraints in this minimum potential energy principle in elasticity. Since
q′(u)=(B1​+B2​+ka2B2​)u−B2​u^,q′′(u)=B1​+B2​+ka2B2​>0,
(17)
the unique pointwise minimizer is also the constant global minimizer:
u∗​=B1​+B2​+ka2B2​B2​u^​=B1​/B2​+1+ka2u^​.​
(18)
For example, completing the square gives
E[u]−E[u∗​]=2B1​+B2​+ka2B2​​∫0L​(u−u∗​)2dS≥0,
(19)
which also proves uniqueness, up to equality almost everywhere, without merely assuming a uniform curvature.
With k, a and u^ fixed, the two limits are
B1​/B2​→∞B1​/B2​→0​:u∗​→0,:u∗​→1+ka2u^​.​​
(20)
In the first limit, rod 1 dominates the bending energy and suppresses the bend. In the second, rod 1 offers negligible bending resistance, but following rod 2's preferred bending strain of a rod still shortens rod 2 through the bonded geometry. The competition between its bending energy and stretching energy leaves u∗​<u^ when u^>0 and ka2>0. Only when that stretching penalty also vanishes does u∗​→u^.
These formulae describe an admissible bonded elastic rod provided 1−au∗​>0. If parameters instead force au∗​≥1, the unconstrained quadratic minimizer is outside the kinematic regime of the model; the given energy does not itself enforce positive stretch ratio.

Use the cylindrical coordinate system basis er​,eφ​,ez​. The meridional unit tangent vector and outward unit normal vector consistent with the given geometry are
es​=cosθer​−sinθez​,n=sinθer​+cosθez​.
(21)
An surface of revolution has θ independent of φ. Consequently
∂s​es​=−θs​n,∂φ​eφ​=−er​,er​=cosθes​+sinθn.
(22)
Apply the product rule to the vector force balance:
∂s​(rts​es​)∂φ​(tφ​eφ​)​=(rs​ts​+r∂s​ts​)es​−rts​θs​n,=(∂φ​tφ​)eφ​−tφ​cosθes​−tφ​sinθn.​
(23)
Equating the three independent components to zero, and using rs​=cosθ, gives
es​eφ​n​:r∂s​ts​+(ts​−tφ​)cosθ=0,:∂φ​tφ​=0,:rP−rts​θs​−tφ​sinθ=0.​
(24)
Thus, away from the rotation axis where r>0, the scalar mechanical equilibrium equations are
P=ts​θs​+tφ​rsinθ​,∂s​ts​=rcosθ​(tφ​−ts​),∂φ​tφ​=0.​
(25)
The two geometric factors θs​ and sinθ/r are the signed meridional and azimuthal principal curvatures in this convention. The normal equation is the Young–Laplace equation generalized to unequal principal membrane tensions.

Take s=σ up to an irrelevant additive constant, r=a constant, z=z0​−σ, and θ=π/2. These satisfy the geometric relations and give the principal stretches
λs​=1,λφ​=a0​a​.
(26)
The constitutive equations therefore produce constant membrane tensions
ts​=fs​(1,a/a0​),tφ​=fφ​(1,a/a0​).
(27)
The tangential mechanical equilibrium equations hold automatically, while the normal equation reduces to
Pa=fφ​(1,a0​a​).​
(28)
Every positive root of this algebraic equation provides a cylindrical solution with no axial stretch ratio change. There is no further constraint on ts​ in the interior equations when end boundary conditions are discarded. In particular, a closed-end pressure balance would impose additional axial loading data that are absent here. Existence of a positive root for an arbitrary specified P depends on the given constitutive equation.

The geometric equations written in reference arc length are

Before contact with both walls, the elastic rod can grow freely, so its elastic stretch ratio is α=1 and its length is its stress-free grown length,
ℓ(t)=L0​γ(t)=L0​(1+ct).
(39)
The first contact occurs when ℓ(tc​)=L1​. For the intended growth case c>0,
tc​=cL1​/L0​−1​=cL0​L1​−L0​​.​
(40)
The condition L1​>L0​ ensures tc​>0. If c=0, contact never occurs; negative c describes shrinkage rather than the stated continuing growth.

Write λ=L1​/L0​>1. The one-dimensional multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient reads ℓ/L0​=αγ. Before contact, α=1; afterwards, the imposed fixed length gives
α(t)=1+ctλ​,t≥tc​.
(41)
Substituting in the linear constitutive equation yields
n(t)=⎩⎨⎧​0,Es​(1+ctλ​−1),​0≤t≤tc​,t>tc​.​​
(42)
Here negative axial stress means compression. The two branches agree at tc​ because 1+ctc​=λ. At late times,
α→0,n→−Es​.
(43)
Thus the law permits arbitrarily severe elastic shortening at a finite compressive stress. It is a small-strain approximation, appropriate near α=1, and is not a credible material law as α→0. The one-dimensional constraint rules out buckling in this particular model; the finite limiting stress is a defect of extrapolating the linear constitutive equation, even with that constraint retained.

In morphoelasticity, the deformation gradient factors as F=AG, where G accounts for local stress-free growth and A accounts for the subsequent elastic accommodation. Elastic incompressibility means that the latter preserves volume:
detA=1.
(46)
It does not forbid volume increase by growth: here detF=detG=γ.
For the homogeneous diagonal deformation, the two unloaded transverse directions are equivalent. Taking the transversely symmetric branch implicit in the paper's hint gives α1​=α2​>0. Set α3​=α>0. The determinant constraint is then
α12​α=1,
(47)
so
A=diag(α−1/2,α−1/2,α).​
(48)
Equality of the transverse stretches uses that symmetry assumption; elastic incompressibility alone would only give α1​α2​α3​=1. The total deformation gradient and the actual axial length are
F=diag(α−1/2,α−1/2,αγ),ℓ=αγ,
(49)
because the initial cuboid is a unit cube.

For the neo-Hookean solid, the axial Cauchy stress tensor under the unloaded lateral boundary conditions is
t3​=μ(α2−α−1).
(58)
With K,μ>0, the spring balance is equivalent, after multiplication by α>0, to the cubic equation
(Kγ+μ)α3−Kα2−μ=0.​
(59)
For a fixed γ≥1, write its left side as h(α). It obeys h(0)=−μ<0 and tends to +∞ as α→∞. Moreover
h′(α)=α[3(Kγ+μ)α−2K].
(60)
Thus h first decreases from a negative value and then increases strictly, crossing zero exactly once on α>0. For γ>1,
h(1)=K(γ−1)>0,h(1/γ)=μ(γ−3−1)<0,
(61)
so the unique equilibrium has 1/γ<α<1. The tissue is elastically compressed, while its actual length αγ exceeds one and compresses the spring.
To determine the large-growth limit without presuming a power law, solve for γ:
γ=α1​+Kμ​(α−3−1).
(62)
This is a strictly decreasing function of α>0, since its derivative is
−α−2−K3μ​α−4<0.
(63)
It tends to +∞ only as α→0+. Consequently unlimited growth forces α→0+, and
t3​=μ(α2−α−1)→−∞.​
(64)
More precisely, the dominant balance is Kγα3∼μ, giving
α∼(Kγμ​)1/3,t3​∼−μ2/3(Kγ)1/3.​
(65)
The surrounding spring softens the divergence relative to rigid confinement but does not bound the compressive stress. Both axial extension and lateral expansion help accommodate growth:
ℓ=αγ∼(Kμ​)1/3γ2/3,A=α−1∼(μK​)1/3γ1/3,
(66)
whose product is the total volume γ, as required by elastic incompressibility and the imposed growth tensor.
