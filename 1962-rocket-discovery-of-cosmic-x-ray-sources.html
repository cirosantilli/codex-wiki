1962 rocket discovery of cosmic X-ray sources

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Observational astronomy X-ray astronomy
OurBigBook.com Words: 316 Articles: 1
In 1962, Riccardo Giacconi and colleagues flew three Geiger counters to search for lunar X-ray fluorescence. Different mica thicknesses tested penetration; lampblack blocked ultraviolet. Each counter sat in an anticoincidence detector using a scintillation counter to reject cosmic rays. There was no mechanical collimator. Rotation scanned the sky; optical sensors supplied orientation.[ref]
Counts were sorted by azimuth; a faulty counter and tumbling descent data were excluded. A directional peak lay above a diffuse component. Window, altitude and angular responses tested X-rays against charged particles; the Moon's nondetection checked optical leakage. The authors argued for an origin outside the Solar system, while residual cosmic ray background was not directly measured and source size, position and spectrum remained assumption-dependent.[ref]
A simple original forward problem makes the distinction precise. Let ϕ be the scan azimuth, let g(ϕ) be a dimensionless directional response with maximum one, let F be a source's band-integrated photon arrival rate per unit area, let A0​ be its on-axis X-ray effective area, and let b be a constant accepted background rate. The expected rate is
r(ϕ)=FA0​g(ϕ)+b.
(46)
For a broad illustrative response g(ϕ)=max(0,cosϕ), the full width at half maximum is 120∘: cosϕ=1/2 at ϕ=±60∘. For a one-dimensional ideal collimator model g(ϕ)=max(0,1−∣ϕ∣/θc​), the full width at half maximum is θc​. Choosing θc​=20∘ narrows the response sixfold without focusing any additional photons. These are teaching response functions, not reconstructions of the rocket's window transmission or geometry. A narrower scan peak can reflect the instrument response even when the source is unresolved.
