PCR converts a selected DNA sequence into a detectable and manipulable quantity; different downstream measurements give it different uses. A pair of PCR primers defines the interval amplified by DNA polymerase. Repeated denaturation, annealing and extension produce approximately Nn​=N0​(1+E)n copies while PCR efficiency E remains constant. At ideal efficiency, twenty cycles give about a millionfold amplification; depletion and accumulating products eventually cause a PCR plateau. Specificity derives from both primer-binding sites and their orientation, and should be checked by product size, melting behaviour or sequencing.
One application is genotyping. A disease-associated substitution can be detected by amplifying the surrounding region and reading the sequence, probing with an allele-specific oligonucleotide, or digesting a restriction site changed by the variant. An early demonstration amplified the human beta-globin region for restriction-based diagnosis of sickle cell disease. Amplification increased sensitivity; the restriction pattern supplied the allele information.[ref] A negative disease-specific signal is interpretable only if a control locus amplifies, because poor extraction or an inhibitor can mimic absence of an allele. In forensic DNA profiling, several short tandem repeat loci are amplified and fragment lengths compared. A multilocus match supports identity statistically; it does not eliminate contamination, mixtures, allelic dropout or the need for appropriate population-frequency estimates.
For infectious agents, primers can detect a pathogen-specific DNA sequence. An RNA virus first requires reverse transcription, followed by polymerase chain reaction. In RT-qPCR, fluorescence is monitored during amplification, and a threshold cycle is related to starting RNA using calibrated standards and efficiency measurements. This also measures changes in cellular gene expression: compare a target transcript between conditions after suitable normalization. Include extraction controls, a no-template reaction and, for RNA measurements, a no-reverse-transcriptase control to detect genomic DNA contamination. A positive pathogen signal establishes the presence of target nucleic acid, not necessarily a viable organism or infectious virus. Likewise, RNA abundance is not a direct measurement of protein abundance or transcription rate, since RNA degradation also matters.
PCR is also a preparative method. Amplified coding sequences can be assembled into expression vectors; primer tails can add restriction sites, assembly overlaps, sequencing adaptors or an affinity tag. High-fidelity polymerase reduces unwanted substitutions, but the final clone should still be sequenced. Site-directed mutagenesis introduces a specified substitution using mutagenic primers, whereas error-prone PCR creates a library for directed evolution. Sequencing after selection distinguishes the intended change from other mutations. In chromatin immunoprecipitation, quantitative amplification of recovered loci can test enrichment of a DNA-associated protein, with input DNA and immunoprecipitation controls needed to interpret it.
These examples separate presence, identity, quantity and construction. Endpoint band intensity is a poor substitute for quantitative amplification measured before the PCR plateau; multiplex assays can also suffer unequal amplification. The appropriate answer therefore identifies what is amplified, what is measured, and which alternative explanation each control excludes.
