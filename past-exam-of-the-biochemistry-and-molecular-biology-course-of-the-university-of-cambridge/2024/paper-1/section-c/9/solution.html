Antennae collect photons; reaction centres convert excitation into charge separation; electron flow then generates reducing power and a proton gradient for carbon assimilation. Chlorophyll and accessory pigments in light-harvesting complexes broaden light absorption and transfer excitation towards a photosynthetic reaction centre. Excitation-energy transfer between pigments is distinct from transferring an electron along the electron-transport chain. The reaction centre couples excitation to separation of an electron from its donor, creating an oxidizing partner and a reduced acceptor before the excitation is lost as heat or fluorescence.
In the thylakoid membrane, photosystem II uses light excitation of its reaction centre to drive electron transfer to acceptors. Its oxidized donor is regenerated using electrons extracted from water by the oxygen-evolving complex, which contains a manganese–calcium cluster. Four oxidizing equivalents permit the net reaction
2H2​O⟶O2​+4H++4e−.

The protons are released on the lumenal side. Electrons pass through plastoquinone, the cytochrome b6f complex and plastocyanin to photosystem I. Plastoquinone takes up protons on reduction and contributes to their net delivery into the lumen through the cytochrome-b6f pathway. The two photosystems operate in series because excitation at one centre alone does not bridge the full energetic span from water oxidation to efficient nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate reduction.
Excitation in photosystem I enables electron transfer to ferredoxin; ferredoxin-NADP reductase generates NADPH on the stromal side. For the four electrons corresponding to one oxygen molecule, the ideal minimum is eight photochemical excitations, four at each photosystem, yielding two NADPH. Real light requirements are higher because not every absorbed photon produces productive chemistry. The proton-motive force drives ATP synthase as protons return from the lumen to the chloroplast stroma. Counterion movements often allow a large pH component to accumulate without an equally large membrane voltage. In a classical test, an imposed acid-to-base transition in isolated chloroplast material supported ATP formation in darkness, separating the gradient's role from the immediate presence of light.[ref] Appropriate controls compare transitions that produce a transmembrane gradient with treatments that dissipate it.
In the stroma, the Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to reduce fixed carbon and regenerate the carbon acceptor. RuBisCO adds carbon dioxide to ribulose bisphosphate; subsequent phosphorylation and reduction produce triose phosphate. Fixation of three carbon dioxide molecules to one net triose phosphate requires nine ATP and six NADPH in the standard accounting, with most intermediate carbon recycled to regenerate the acceptor. “Light-independent” chemistry is not physiologically independent of light: energy supply, stromal conditions and enzyme regulation coordinate it with photochemistry.
Linear electron flow need not exactly match the ATP/NADPH demand. Cyclic electron flow around photosystem I increases proton-gradient-driven ATP production without net NADPH generation or water oxidation. Excess excitation is dangerous; non-photochemical quenching, changes in antenna allocation and photosystem repair limit damage. The chloroplast combines efficient collection with regulated conversion and protection, rather than maximizing photon capture regardless of metabolic demand.
