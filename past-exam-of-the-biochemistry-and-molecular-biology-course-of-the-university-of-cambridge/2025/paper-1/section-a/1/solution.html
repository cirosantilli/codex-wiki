The useful product is a correctly identified, active and reproducible protein preparation, not merely a large expression band. I would first examine the coding sequence, protein sequence and available biological information to identify the likely mature chain, protein domains, signal peptides, transmembrane segments, disulfide bonds and required post-translational modifications. A predicted protein structure can guide construct boundaries, but cannot establish that the expressed product will fold or function. A eukaryotic genomic sequence containing introns generally needs replacement by an intron-free Complementary DNA or synthetic coding region for expression in Escherichia coli. I would verify the chosen isoform and full sequence, since a database annotation can be wrong.
The expression host follows from the biochemical requirements. Escherichia coli is inexpensive and convenient for many soluble proteins; yeast, insect or mammalian cells may be preferable for secreted proteins or essential glycosylation. A membrane protein also requires a suitable membrane environment during expression and an appropriate detergent or membrane mimic after extraction. I would preserve biologically necessary domains and modifications. Removing a flexible region to improve yield creates a different construct whose suitability must be justified by the intended analysis.
For a bacterial preparation, I would clone the coding region into an expression vector with a controllable promoter, a suitable ribosome-binding site, a selectable marker and a removable affinity tag. A terminal polyhistidine tag is a practical initial choice, provided the terminus is compatible with activity. Correct reading frame, both junctions and the complete insert must be checked by DNA sequencing. The T7 expression system offers strong inducible expression through a phage RNA polymerase; the original work established selective expression of cloned genes, but strong transcription alone does not guarantee soluble, active product.[ref]
I would screen small cultures before scale-up, varying induction strength, induction duration and temperature, and comparing total lysate with soluble and insoluble fractions by SDS-PAGE. Empty-vector and uninduced controls identify host bands and background expression; an immunoblot against the tag can help identify the product. Expression at lower temperature or a weaker rate can improve protein folding; persistent insolubility may require changing the construct, host or folding environment. An inclusion body is evidence of abundant insoluble material, not of correctly folded protein. Refolding may be possible, but its yield and recovered activity need independent validation.
Once a small-scale condition produces active soluble material, I would expand the culture or fermentation while monitoring growth and retaining the validated induction conditions. After harvesting, controlled lysis at low temperature, an appropriate buffer solution and protection against proteolysis preserve the product. Centrifugation removes debris; a membrane-associated target instead requires isolation and solubilization of the relevant fraction. I would use immobilized metal affinity chromatography for a polyhistidine-tagged construct, wash away nonspecific binders, and elute under conditions compatible with activity. Tag cleavage, a second affinity step to remove tag and tagged protease, and an orthogonal separation such as ion-exchange chromatography can improve purity. A final size-exclusion chromatography step removes aggregates and separates different assemblies. No single chromatographic peak proves identity or homogeneity.
At each stage I would record protein concentration, total recovered amount and a suitable activity or binding readout, rather than optimizing yield alone. Final checks would include SDS-PAGE, intact or peptide mass spectrometry, a native-size measurement and the expected biological activity. Buffer-only and host-purification controls test whether apparent activity comes from contaminants; comparing tagged and cleaved material tests tag interference. Small frozen aliquots should be used only after checking stability and freeze–thaw effects. Scale-up is justified by recovery of the desired molecular species with retained function, with any differences from the native protein documented.
