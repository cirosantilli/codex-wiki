Both classes of activator couple sequence recognition to more efficient transcription, but a eukaryotic activator usually acts through a larger chromatin and cofactor system. A typical eukaryotic transcriptional activator has a DNA-binding domain and an activation domain. The former positions it at a regulatory sequence; the latter recruits or regulates other components. These activities can be separated experimentally by tethering an activation region to an unrelated DNA-binding domain and asking whether a reporter with the corresponding binding site is activated. Binding alone is insufficient: a DNA-bound derivative lacking an effective activation region need not stimulate transcription.
In eukaryotes, the target DNA is packaged in chromatin. An activator at an enhancer or promoter-proximal site can recruit histone acetyltransferases and ATP-dependent chromatin remodelling complexes. Histone modification and nucleosome repositioning can increase access to regulatory and promoter sequences; acetylated residues can also recruit reader proteins. These are context-dependent routes, not a universal requirement that every activated gene first undergo the same histone change. Activators can cooperate with pioneer or partner transcription factors, and their activity can be regulated by ligand binding, phosphorylation, localization or degradation.
Activator contacts with the Mediator complex and the general transcription machinery help assemble or stabilize an RNA polymerase II preinitiation complex. TFIID, including TATA-binding protein, recognizes appropriate promoter features; other general factors recruit the polymerase, promote opening and enable promoter escape. Regulatory contacts may additionally influence release of paused polymerase and productive elongation. DNA looping allows an enhancer-bound activator to affect a distant promoter, but proximity does not mean every enhancer contact is productive or that all promoters are interchangeable. Biochemical reconstitution showed that a mediator fraction was required for activated transcription in vitro, providing evidence that activator–polymerase communication can require an intermediate rather than a direct DNA-to-polymerase contact.[ref]
The bacterial catabolite activator protein, also called cAMP receptor protein, is a simpler example of regulated recruitment. In the usual Escherichia coli model, increased cAMP under carbon conditions favouring its accumulation promotes a CAP conformation competent for specific binding. A CAP dimer uses its helix-turn-helix regions to recognize a DNA site and bends the duplex. At a class I CAP-dependent promoter such as the canonical lac arrangement, a site centred near −61.5 positions an activating surface to contact the C-terminal domain of the RNA polymerase alpha subunit. This stabilizes polymerase recruitment; the CAP–alpha-domain–DNA structure supplies a direct molecular basis for that contact.[ref]
CAP is not limited to that single geometry. At class II promoters, a site near −41.5 overlaps the promoter region and permits additional contacts with polymerase, affecting more than initial recruitment. Promoter position, orientation and other repressors therefore matter. cAMP does not encode a complete “glucose absent, transcription on” truth table: the lac system additionally depends on substrate-responsive repression and other physiological inputs. An activating protein can improve a limiting step without determining the entire expression pattern.
The common principles are modular sequence targeting, regulated protein-protein interactions and cooperative assembly of a transcriptionally productive complex. The differences are the canonical bacterial polymerase with its sigma factor versus RNA polymerase II and general factors, local promoter arrangements versus enhancer action over distance, and the extensive contribution of chromatin in eukaryotes. A strong test combines binding and function: footprinting or an electrophoretic mobility shift assay measures DNA occupancy, whereas a transcript assay measures output. An activation-surface mutant that retains DNA binding but loses transcriptional stimulation separates recruitment from recognition. In a reconstituted system, removing and restoring a required cofactor tests causality; in cells, changes in chromatin or reporter activity alone do not identify the direct protein contact.
