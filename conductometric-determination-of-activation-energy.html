Conductometric determination of activation energy

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Activation energy Arrhenius equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 378
A concrete laboratory route to an Arrhenius plot is to infer a rate constant at each controlled temperature from a changing electrical conductivity. The Technical University of Munich's Activation Energy of a First-Order Reaction supplies a teaching protocol, rather than a published set of measured rate constants.
The manual follows tertiary amylbromide conversion, which produces conducting ions. A measuring cell connects to a thermostat; an immersed electrical conductivity probe also measures solution temperature, and a magnetic stirrer mixes it. Use 80mL of 80% by volume ethanol in water, equilibrate, then add 0.5mL of tertiary amylbromide. Record the baseline before addition and set time zero at addition. Repeat with fresh mixtures at 25, 30, and 35∘C, each within ±0.5∘C; record for 30 minutes at the first two temperatures and until completion at the last. Clean the cell between runs and keep the added volume fixed. The completed 35∘C mixture supplies endpoint electrical conductivity measurements at the other temperatures: one endpoint reading cannot be used unchanged at every temperature.[ref]
To see what is inferred from the readout, let σ0​, σ(t), and σ∞​ be the baseline, current, and completed-mixture electrical conductivities at the same temperature. Assume their baseline-subtracted changes are proportional to converted concentration. Combining that measurement model with a first-order reaction gives
f(t)=σ∞​−σ0​σ∞​−σ(t)​=c0​c(t)​=e−kt,−lnf(t)=kt.
(120)
Thus the electrical conductivity increases toward an endpoint, while the remaining reactant fraction decreases. Fit −lnf against time to obtain k; then fit ln(k/kref​) against 1/T in kelvin to estimate an apparent activation energy. This baseline-aware derivation states the measurement assumptions explicitly; it does not infer a rate constant from the steepness of a raw conductivity trace alone.
The controls distinguish a faster chemical reaction from a larger instrumental response. Measure the actual solution temperature, retain the same solvent composition and initial reactant concentration, and compare endpoint and baseline at that temperature. As further diagnostic checks, repeat runs and inspect regression residuals for departure from a first-order reaction. These checks follow from the model: electrical conductivity also depends on how ions move, a baseline drift can mimic conversion, and a mixing delay can distort early points. Near completion, a small endpoint error produces a large relative error in f and its natural logarithm. A successful fit supports an apparent rate model for this solvent and interval; it does not directly image a transition state or identify a unique reaction mechanism. No measured k or Ea​ is supplied here.
