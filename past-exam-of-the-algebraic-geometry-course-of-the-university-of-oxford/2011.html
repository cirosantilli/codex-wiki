An affine variety in Akn​ is the common zero set of a collection of polynomials in k[x1​,…,xn​], with its Zariski topology. We use the convention of this question that such a variety need not be irreducible. Its vanishing ideal and coordinate ring are
I(X)={f:f(p)=0 for every p∈X},A(X)=k[x1​,…,xn​]/I(X).​
(1)
The quotient identifies polynomials that give the same function on X, so its elements are exactly polynomial functions on X.
For affine embeddings X⊆An and Y⊆Am, a morphism of affine varieties is a map F=(f1​,…,fm​) with fi​∈A(X) and image in Y. Pullback of polynomial functions defines the contravariant homomorphism
F#:A(Y)⟶A(X),[g]⟼g∘F.​
(2)
It is well defined because any polynomial vanishing on Y pulls back to zero on X, and it preserves addition, multiplication, one, and the constants in k.

Part (b) immediately gives I​⊆I(V(I)). For the reverse inclusion, let h∈I(V(I)). The case h=0 is immediate, so assume h=0. Noetherianity gives finite generators I=(f1​,…,fr​).
Use the Rabinowitsch trick: in the polynomial ring with one auxiliary variable, form
J=(f1​,…,fr​,1−th)⊆k[x1​,…,xn​,t].
(5)
Apply the assumed maximal-ideal description to polynomial rings, including this ring in n+1 variables. If J were proper, it would lie in the maximal ideal of some point (a,b). Then a∈V(I) and 1−bh(a)=0, impossible because h vanishes on V(I). Therefore J is the whole ring, and there is an identity
1=∑i=1r​Pi​(x,t)fi​(x)+Q(x,t)(1−th(x)).
(6)
Substitute t=h−1 in the localization Rh​. The last term vanishes; clearing the finitely many powers of h from the remaining denominators yields hN∈I for some N. Thus h∈I​ and
I(V(I))=I​.​
(7)
This proves the strong Hilbert Nullstellensatz from the polynomial-ring weak form by converting nonvanishing into an extra equation.

Here subvariety means a closed affine subvariety. Suppose first that F# is onto, and write X⊆An, Y⊆Am. Put J=kerF# and Z=VY​(J). Choose gi​∈A(Y) with F#gi​=xi​∣X​ and let G:Z→An have components gi​∣Z​.
If h∈I(X), then F#(h(g1​,…,gn​))=h∣X​=0, so h(g1​,…,gn​)∈J. It vanishes on Z, proving G(Z)⊆X. The chosen lifts give G∘F=idX​, and F(X)⊆Z by the definition of J.
Let the components of F be polynomial functions f1​,…,fm​ on X. For each target coordinate yj​, the element yj​−fj​(g1​,…,gn​) has zero pullback, hence belongs to J. It vanishes on Z, giving F∘G=idZ​. Both maps have polynomial components, so F is an isomorphism onto the closed subvariety Z.
Conversely, if F:X→Z⊆Y is an isomorphism onto a closed subvariety, restriction A(Y)→A(Z) is surjective, and the pullback A(Z)→A(X) of that isomorphism is an isomorphism. Their composition is F#, proving
F# onto⟺F is a closed embedding.​
(8)
The word closed matters: an isomorphism onto a merely locally closed subset need not induce a surjective coordinate-ring map. For example Gm​↪A1 induces k[t]↪k[t,t−1].

The parametrization lands on the curve because (t3)4=(t4)3. Its pullback has image
imF#=k[t3,t4]⊂k[t]=A(A1).
(9)
Every monomial in this subalgebra has exponent 3a+4b for nonnegative integers a,b. No such exponent is one, so no linear combination of these monomials is t. Thus F# is not surjective and F is not an isomorphism.
It is nevertheless bijective on points. Away from the origin, x=0 and t=y/x gives t3=x and t4=y using x4=y3; the origin comes only from t=0. The rational inverse cannot extend regularly at the origin: if t=P(t3,t4)/Q(t3,t4) with Q(0)=0, then tQ(t3,t4) has a nonzero degree-one term, whereas P(t3,t4) has none.
F is bijective, but its inverse is not regular at the cusp.​
(10)
This distinguishes a set-theoretic bijection from an isomorphism of varieties in every characteristic.
This is a bijective monomial cusp parametrization with nonregular inverse.

A projective variety is a Zariski-closed subset of projective space Pkn​, equivalently the common zero set of homogeneous polynomials. Homogeneity makes their vanishing independent of the nonzero scalar used to represent a projective point.
A morphism is a map locally given by regular coordinate functions in affine charts. Equivalently, near each point of a projective source embedded in Pn, a morphism to Pm can be written
[x]⟼[F0​(x):⋯:Fm​(x)]​
(11)
using homogeneous polynomials of a common degree that are not all zero on that neighbourhood. The expressions must agree on overlaps, and for a specified target variety their values must lie in it. A single global tuple is a sufficient description when available, but is not part of the general definition.

The Segre embedding is
σn,m​([x0​:⋯:xn​],[y0​:⋯:ym​])=[xi​yj​]0≤i≤n, 0≤j≤m​.​
(12)
Rescaling the two representatives multiplies every target coordinate by the same scalar, and at least one product is nonzero, so the map is well defined. In affine source charts its coordinate ratios are regular products, so it is a morphism.
Arrange target coordinates into a matrix Z=(zij​). Every image matrix has rank one and satisfies all homogeneous equations
zij​zkℓ​−ziℓ​zkj​=0.
(13)
Conversely, a nonzero matrix satisfying these equations has rank one. Choose zi0​j0​​=0 and set xi​=zij0​​, yj​=zi0​j​/zi0​j0​​. The minors imply zij​=xi​yj​. Therefore
imσn,m​=V(all 2×2 minors),​
(14)
a projective variety. The two projective factors are uniquely recoverable from a nonzero column and row. This also shows injectivity and supplies the local inverse formulas used in the next part.

c OurBigBook.com
Choose projective embeddings X⊆Pn and Y⊆Pm. Define their product as the Segre image of the set of pairs (x,y), with its induced variety structure.
It is closed in the Segre variety. To see this directly, for every homogeneous f∈I(X) impose the equations f(z0j​,…,znj​)=0 on every column, and for every homogeneous g∈I(Y) impose g(zi0​,…,zim​)=0 on every row. For a rank-one matrix zij​=xi​yj​, the column equations are yjdegf​f(x)=0. A nonzero yj​ therefore forces x∈X. The row equations similarly force y∈Y. Thus these homogeneous equations together with the minors cut out exactly X×Y, proving it is projective.
On a chart where zi0​j0​​=0, the projections have formulas
πX​([Z])=[z0j0​​:⋯:znj0​​],πY​([Z])=[zi0​0​:⋯:zi0​m​].
(15)
These homogeneous coordinate tuples are nonzero on the chart and agree on overlaps, so both projections are morphisms.
Given morphisms α:Z→X and β:Z→Y, define γ(z)=σn,m​(α(z),β(z)). Locally write their coordinates as [a0​:⋯:an​] and [b0​:⋯:bm​]. Then γ has coordinates [ai​bj​], which are regular with a nonzero coordinate near each point. Hence γ is a morphism and
πX​∘γ=α,πY​∘γ=β.​
(16)
The two projections determine the underlying pair uniquely, so this morphism is unique. This is the product universal property, and it makes the construction independent up to canonical isomorphism of the chosen embeddings.

Let S=k[x0​,…,xn​] and let B=S/I(X) be the homogeneous coordinate ring of a nonempty projective variety. Its Hilbert function is hX​(t)=dimk​Bt​. For all sufficiently large integers t, this function agrees with a polynomial PX​(t), the Hilbert polynomial.
The dimension r is the largest length of a strict chain of nonempty irreducible closed subvarieties of X. Equivalently r=dimB−1=degPX​. The degree of the embedded variety is the integer specified by the leading term
PX​(t)=r!degX​tr+lower-degree terms.​
(17)
Geometrically it is the number of intersection points with a general linear subspace of codimension r, counted with intersection multiplicities. The polynomial's degree is the dimension r; the variety's degree is the leading coefficient multiplied by r!. Keeping those two meanings separate is important in the next computation.

The homogeneous coordinate ring of the Segre image is the subalgebra k[xi​yj​]⊆k[x0​,…,xn​,y0​,…,ym​], grading each product xi​yj​ as degree one. Its degree-t part is precisely the space of polynomials bihomogeneous of bidegree (t,t).
Indeed, every product of t generators has those two degrees. Conversely, any monomial of bidegree (t,t) can pair its t individual x factors with its t individual y factors, expressing it as a product of generators. The distinct resulting monomials are linearly independent. Counting them gives
PX​(t)=(nt+n​)(mt+m​).​
(18)
In fact this agrees with the Hilbert function for every integer t≥0. Its leading coefficient is 1/(n!m!), so the two degree quantities are
degPX​=n+m,degX=n!m!(n+m)!​=(nn+m​).​
(19)
Thus the Segre variety has dimension n+m and embedded projective degree (nn+m​).
This gives the Segre Hilbert polynomial and degree directly from its graded monomials.

A quasi-projective variety is a locally closed subset of projective space: equivalently an open subset of some projective variety, with the induced algebraic variety structure. Affine varieties are examples, via the standard affine chart in projective space.
It is irreducible if it is nonempty and cannot be written as the union of two proper closed subsets. Equivalently, every two nonempty open subsets meet, or every nonempty open subset is dense. Closed and open are interpreted in the induced Zariski topology of X.

For a multiplicatively closed set S containing one, the localization of a ring S−1R consists of fractions r/s with r∈R,s∈S, under the equivalence
sr​=s′r′​⟺∃u∈S: u(rs′−r′s)=0.​
(20)
The factor u is needed when the ring has zero divisors. Addition and multiplication are
sr​+s′r′​=ss′rs′+r′s​,sr​s′r′​=ss′rr′​.
(21)
The canonical map sends r to r/1 and makes every element of S a unit. It is universal with this property: a ring homomorphism ϕ:R→T taking S into units extends uniquely by r/s↦ϕ(r)ϕ(s)−1. Usually zero is excluded from S; if it is included, this construction is the zero ring.

A regular function on U is a function f:U→k locally representable, in an affine chart of the ambient projective space, as a quotient a/b of polynomial functions with denominator nonzero on that neighbourhood. Equivalently it is locally a quotient of homogeneous forms of the same degree. The equal-degree condition makes the quotient independent of the chosen projective representative.
The ring OX,p​ is the ring of germs of such functions near p. Two representatives (U,f) and (V,g) define the same germ when they agree on some smaller neighbourhood of p. Addition and multiplication use a common neighbourhood. It is a local ring: its unique maximal ideal consists of germs vanishing at p, and any germ nonzero at p has a regular reciprocal on a smaller neighbourhood.
mp​={[f]p​:f(p)=0},OX,p​/mp​≅k.​
(22)

A morphism F:X→Y is a continuous map in the Zariski topologies such that for every open V⊆Y and every regular function h on V, the pullback h∘F is regular on F−1(V).
Equivalently, near every point, choose affine charts of source and target; the target coordinates of F must then be regular functions of the source coordinates. These local conditions agree on overlaps. In particular a morphism is defined at every point of its source, not merely on a dense open subset.

ii OurBigBook.com
A rational map from irreducible X to Y is an equivalence class of morphisms FU​:U→Y defined on nonempty open subsets U⊆X. Two representatives are equivalent if they agree on a nonempty open subset of their intersection.
Irreducibility makes all these nonempty opens dense and ensures their intersections are nonempty, so the equivalence expresses agreement at general points. A rational map need not extend across all of X, and it need not be dominant. An injective homomorphism of function fields additionally requires a dominant morphism of affine varieties, or the corresponding condition on a rational map.

Polynomial functions are regular, giving an injective map A(X)↪OX​(X); injectivity follows from quotienting by the full vanishing ideal. To prove surjectivity, let f be globally regular and consider the denominator ideal
J={g∈A(X):gf is a polynomial function on X}.
(23)
It is an ideal because sums and polynomial multiples preserve this property.
Fix p∈X. Locally f=a/b on a neighbourhood V of p, with b nonzero there. Principal affine opens form a basis, so choose h∈A(X) with h(p)=0 and D(h)⊆V. Then
(hb)f=ha
(24)
as functions on all of X: on D(h) it follows from the local fraction, and off D(h) both sides vanish because h=0. Hence hb∈J and (hb)(p)=0.
Thus J has no common zero on X. By the weak Nullstellensatz for A(X), every proper ideal lies in a maximal ideal belonging to a point of X; therefore J cannot be proper. We get 1∈J, so f itself is polynomial. This proves
OX​(X)≅A(X).​
(25)
The argument works for reducible affine varieties as well: it uses functions and denominator ideals rather than assuming a single fraction field.
This is the denominator ideal proof for global regular functions.

For any affine variety and point p, with mp​={f∈A(X):f(p)=0}, the standard localization description is
OX,p​≅A(X)mp​​.​
(26)
Also OX​(D(g))≅A(X)g​ for a principal open subset. One way to see the latter is to realize D(g) as the affine variety {(x,z):g(x)z=1}; its coordinate ring is A(X)[z]/(gz−1)=A(X)g​ and part (d) applies.
For this cusp use x=t3,y=t2, identifying A(X) with k[t2,t3] inside k(t). The parametrization gives exactly the relation x2=y3; its kernel is generated by this binomial. For example reduce a polynomial modulo x2−y3 to a(y)+xb(y); substitution has even powers from a(t2) and odd powers at least three from t3b(t2), so it can vanish only when both polynomials are zero.
Write g=x−y=t2(t−1). The open set corresponds to t=0,1, and
OX​(U)=k[t2,t3]t2(t−1)​.
(27)
To simplify this ring without assuming the normalization coordinate is present, observe that in A(X)
g2=(x−y)2=y(y2−2x+y).
(28)
Thus y becomes a unit after inverting g, with inverse (y2−2x+y)/g2. Consequently t=x/y belongs to the localization. Since g=t2(t−1) is a unit, both t and t−1 become units. Conversely every original generator lies in the corresponding two-point localization of k[t]. Hence
OX​(U)=k[t,t−1,(t−1)−1].​
(29)
Finally p=(1,1) corresponds to t=1. It lies outside U, so compute its local ring from X, at m=(x−1,y−1). The element y is a unit there, hence t=x/y belongs to A(X)m​. Every polynomial in t nonzero at one is then a unit. Conversely a denominator from A(X)∖m remains nonzero at t=1. These inclusions prove
OX,p​=(k[x,y]/(x2−y3))(x−1,y−1)​≅k[t](t−1)​.​
(30)
This smooth point has the ordinary one-variable local ring, even though the global curve has a cusp at its origin.
The localization calculation describes regular functions on a cusp with two points removed.
