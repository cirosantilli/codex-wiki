The velocity potential gives horizontal and vertical velocity components (ϕx​,ϕz​). The kinematic boundary condition at each fixed impermeable wall is zero normal fluid velocity, hence
ϕx​=0 at x=0,L,ϕz​=0 at z=−h​.
(1)
Let η(x,t) be the surface elevation. To first order in its amplitude, the kinematic boundary condition for a free-surface graph gives ηt​=ϕz​ at z=0. The dynamic boundary condition for an inviscid interface fixes the surface pressure to atmospheric pressure. The unsteady Bernoulli equation, after choosing the time-dependent gauge of ϕ, then gives ϕt​+gη=0 at z=0: the quadratic velocity term is second order in amplitude. Differentiate this last equation in time and eliminate ηt​ to obtain the linearized free-surface boundary conditions in combined form,
ϕtt​+gϕz​=0(z=0)​.
(2)

Apply separation of variables to Laplace's equation. The side-wall Neumann boundary conditions give horizontal factors cos(kn​x) with kn​=nπ/L. The bottom condition fixes the vertical factor to cosh(kn​(z+h)). Substitution into the surface condition in i gives the normal modes of surface gravity waves in a rectangular tank:
ϕn​=cos(kn​x)cosh(kn​(z+h))[An​cos(ωn​t)+Bn​sin(ωn​t)],ωn2​=gkn​tanh(kn​h),n≥1​.
(3)
The corresponding surface elevation follows from ηn​=−∂t​ϕn​(x,0,t)/g. The spatially uniform n=0 mode has zero frequency; its constant elevation changes total fluid volume, so it is absent when that volume is fixed. An additive constant in ϕ changes no fluid velocity.

Put λ=ω2/g and
Q=2Liωc​[(z+h)2−(L−x)2],G=f+Q.
(5)
This polynomial is a harmonic function: Qxx​+Qzz​=0. Also Qx​(0,z)=iωc, Qx​(L,z)=0 and Qz​(x,−h)=0. Thus G satisfies Laplace's equation with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions on both side walls and the bottom. Its Fourier cosine series consequently has the form
G(x,z)=2a0​​+n=1∑∞​an​cos(kn​x)cosh(kn​(z+h)),kn​=Lnπ​​.
(6)
The surface condition becomes Gz​−λG=H(x), where
H(x)=Liωc​[h−2λ​(h2−(L−x)2)].
(7)
For a nonzero forcing frequency away from the natural frequencies, the coefficients of the oscillating-wall forcing of a rectangular tank are
Hn​=L2​∫0L​H(x)cos(kn​x)dx,a0​=−λH0​​,an​=kn​sinh(kn​h)−λcosh(kn​h)Hn​​(n≥1).
(8)
Here k0​=0 in the definition of H0​. In fact Hn​=2iωcλ/(Lkn2​) for n≥1, since ∫0L​(L−x)2cos(kn​x)dx=2L/kn2​. The zero-mode coefficient ensures that the mean surface elevation is hc/L in complex amplitude, exactly the first-order volume conservation adjustment to the moving wall.

For the nth normal mode, the denominator just obtained is
kn​sinh(kn​h)−gω2​cosh(kn​h)=gcosh(kn​h)​(ωn2​−ω2).
(9)
Its forcing coefficient is nonzero for c=0 and ω>0. Therefore the periodic response grows like (ωn2​−ω2)−1 as ω approaches ωn​: this is resonance. At exact resonance there is no bounded periodic particular solution in the linear inviscid model; the resonant amplitude instead contains a secular term proportional to tsin(ωn​t) or tcos(ωn​t). Physical damping and nonlinear effects eventually matter. Away from resonance, undamped free oscillations can persist alongside the periodic particular solution.

Write the density and pressure as ρ0​(z)+ρ′(x,z,t) and p0​(z)+p′(x,z,t), respectively. The background satisfies hydrostatic balance, p0′​=−gρ0​, and ρ0′​/ρ0​=−α. Linearizing the Euler equations for an inviscid fluid, incompressibility and advection of density gives
ux​+wz​=0,ρ0​ut​=−px′​,ρ0​wt​=−pz′​−gρ′,ρt′​=αρ0​w.
(10)
Differentiate the horizontal equation in x and use incompressibility to obtain pxx′​=ρ0​wzt​. Differentiate the vertical equation in time and twice in x:
ρ0​wttxx​=−∂z​(ρ0​wztt​)−αgρ0​wxx​.
(11)
Dividing by ρ0​>0 gives the equation for internal waves in an exponentially stratified incompressible fluid,
∂t2​(∇2w−αwz​)+αgwxx​=0​.
(12)
The first-derivative term retains the variation of inertial density with height; no Boussinesq approximation has been made.

Write the cosine in i as two complex exponentials. On a ray x=vt, the oscillatory phases are kv±ω(k). The stationary phase method requires v=∓ω′(k). By ii, the largest absolute horizontal group velocity for the occupied n=1 mode is
t∣x∣​≤b1​N​=4π2+α2h2​2hαg​​​.
(21)
Inside this region there are stationary wavenumbers supporting appreciable wave packet contributions. Outside it no stationary wavenumber exists; oscillatory cancellation suppresses the large-time response. This explains "mainly confined": the initial Lorentzian profile already has nonzero algebraic tails, so the displayed region is not the exact support of the solution. Near its boundary the stationary points meet k=0 and the usual nondegenerate stationary-phase formula needs a transition approximation.

Take a unit-width control volume spanning the stationary hydraulic jump. Mass conservation makes the discharge q=hu constant. The horizontal momentum flux is ρhu2, and the integrated hydrostatic pressure force on a vertical cross-section is ∫0h​ρg(h−z)dz=ρgh2/2. Momentum conservation therefore gives
[hu]−+​=[hu2+gh2/2]−+​=0​.
(22)
These are the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for the shallow water equations in the stationary shock frame. With depths h1​=h2​ on the two sides, eliminating the velocity gives
q2=2g​h1​h2​(h1​+h2​).
(23)

Orient the flow so q>0, and let subscripts 1 and 2 denote upstream and downstream. The mechanical energy flux is ρqB, where B=u2/2+gh. Inserting the relation for q2 in i gives the hydraulic jump head loss,
B1​−B2​=2q2​(h12​1​−h22​1​)+g(h1​−h2​)=4h1​h2​g(h2​−h1​)3​.
(24)
An admissible jump dissipates mechanical energy, so ρq(B1​−B2​)>0. The denominator is positive, hence
h2​>h1​​.
(25)
The zero depth-change case is continuous flow. A depth-decreasing discontinuity would require an energy input at the jump; it is not the dissipative hydraulic jump selected by this model.

Let H be the depth behind the bore. The wall's kinematic boundary condition makes the velocity there U; ahead of the bore the depth and velocity are h0​ and zero. Across a rightward hydraulic bore moving at V, the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions are
V(H−h0​)=HU,VHU=HU2+2g​(H2−h02​).
(27)
The first gives R=H/h0​=V/(V−U)=v/(v−1)>1. Substituting into the second gives F2v=(R2−1)/2, where v=V/U and F=U/gh0​​. Hence the piston-driven shallow-water bore obeys
F2v2=2(v−1)2v(2v−1)​=1+2(v−1)3​+2(v−1)21​,v>1​.
(28)
The positive depth increase is consistent with the energy-loss condition in ii, now viewed in the bore's own shock frame.

For fixed F>0, the left side L(v)=F2v2 is a strictly increasing function on v>1, with limit F2 at 1 and unbounded growth. The right side R(v)=1+3/[2(v−1)]+1/[2(v−1)2] is a strictly decreasing function, since
R′(v)=−2(v−1)23​−(v−1)31​<0.
(29)
It tends to infinity as v↓1 and to one as v→∞. Their continuity and the intermediate value theorem give a crossing, and strict opposite monotonicity makes it unique. There is exactly one admissible bore speed V>U for every F>0.
The illustrated case is F=1; changing F>0 moves the increasing curve without changing the existence or uniqueness argument. The vertical asymptote at v=1 expresses the growing depth ratio H/h0​=v/(v−1).
