Schrödinger equation (iℏ∂t​ψ=Hψ)

 Home Physics Mathematical physics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 16
The Schrödinger equation governs the evolution of a quantum state between instances of measurement in quantum mechanics. For a particle of mass m>0 moving in a prescribed real potential energy V(r,t), its position wavefunction satisfies
iℏ∂t∂ψ​=H(t)ψ,H(t)=−2mℏ2​∇2+V(r,t),ℏ=2πh​.
(75)
Here h is the Planck constant, ∇2 is the Laplace operator, and the Hamiltonian operator combines kinetic energy with potential energy. Specify an initial wavefunction, an operator domain and any boundary conditions: the differential expression alone does not completely specify a physical model. The Born rule then turns the evolving probability amplitude into position probabilities through ρ=∣ψ∣2 and integration over a region.
This scalar Schrödinger operator describes nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, with relevant momenta much smaller than mc, where c is the speed of light. It omits the constant rest energy mc2, assumes fixed particle number, and suppresses active spin dynamics and coupling to a magnetic vector potential. A free electron with a fixed, uncoupled spin state can still use this scalar model; the Pauli equation supplies the additional spin components and magnetic coupling when needed. A prescribed real V describes a closed evolution model or an approximation in which the source of that potential energy is treated as external. Dissipation, particle creation and a general measurement in quantum mechanics need further modelling.
For a time-independent self-adjoint Hamiltonian operator, the spectral theorem defines unitary time evolution by
ψ(t)=e−iHt/ℏψ(0),∥ψ(t)∥2=∥ψ(0)∥2.
(76)
Initial states in the operator domain satisfy the differential equation in the Hilbert space sense; the unitary operator also evolves all other states in that Hilbert space. Time-dependent Hamiltonian operators need suitable regularity and domain hypotheses; their evolution is not generally the exponential of −itH(t)/ℏ.
The local reason for conservation is especially tangible. Multiply the Schrödinger equation by the complex conjugate ψ∗, subtract its conjugate multiplied by ψ, and use the fact that the real potential energy terms cancel. For smooth wavefunctions this gives the probability continuity equation
∂t​ρ+∇⋅j=0,j=mℏ​Im(ψ∗∇ψ).
(77)
The divergence theorem identifies the change in probability inside a region with minus the outward probability current. Decay at infinity, or appropriate probability-conserving boundary conditions, preserves wavefunction normalization. Conservation of total probability does not require a stationary probability density.
Its usefulness comes from being a linear partial differential equation: quantum superpositions of solutions are solutions, while different quantum phases can produce interference. For a fixed Hamiltonian operator, the time-independent Schrödinger equation finds the energy eigenstates; each then acquires just its own time-dependent quantum phase. One eigenvalue problem therefore organizes both allowed energies and the evolution of arbitrary quantum superpositions, with a continuous spectrum included when required. This is the attractive bridge between a spatial wave problem and a clock: energy differences determine the rate at which relative quantum phases change. It makes atomic spectral lines, quantum tunnelling and quantum scattering calculable from a specified model.
For an explicit example without confinement, take a free particle on the whole line, V=0, initially in a Gaussian wave packet with position standard deviation σ0​ and mean momentum ℏk0​:
ψ(x,0)=(2πσ02​)1/4e−x2/(4σ02​)+ik0​x​,ts​=ℏ2mσ02​​,τ=ts​t​,v0​=mℏk0​​.
(78)
Unlike a single plane wave, this wavefunction satisfies wavefunction normalization. A Fourier transform writes it as a quantum superposition of plane waves. Their dispersion relation is ω(k)=ℏk2/(2m), so each Fourier component gains the factor e−iℏk2t/(2m). Completing the square in the resulting Gaussian integral gives
ψ(x,t)=(2πσ02​)1/41+iτ​1​exp[−4σ02​(1+iτ)(x−v0​t)2​+ik0​x−2miℏk02​t​],
(79)
with the square root chosen continuously from t=0. Its position probability density and standard deviation are
ρ(x,t)=2π​σt​exp[−(x−v0​t)2/(2σt2​)]​,σt​=σ0​1+τ2​.
(80)
The centre moves with the group velocity ω′(k0​)=v0​ and the wave packet spreads because different wavenumbers have different group velocities. This is unitary wave dispersion, without a random force or loss of probability. Independently, the initial momentum standard deviation is Δp=ℏ/(2σ0​) and the initial position-momentum covariance vanishes. Free evolution gives x(t)=x(0)+p(0)t/m in the Heisenberg picture, hence
σt2​=σ02​+m2t2​(Δp)2=σ02​+4m2σ02​ℏ2t2​.
(81)
This independently checks the spreading law. The Gaussian integral of ρ is one at every time; the decreasing peak is exactly offset by increasing width.
As hypothetical preparation parameters, choose a free electron with σ0​=1nm and k0​=2nm−1. Using ℏ≃1.054571817×10−34Js and m≃9.1093837×10−31kg gives
ts​≃17.276fs,v0​≃2.31535×105ms−1,Δp≃5.27286×10−26kgms−1.
(82)
The rounded electron mass uses the 2022 adjustment.[ref] At t=ts​, the centre is 4nm and σt​=2​nm; at t=2ts​, they are 8nm and 5​nm. The mean kinetic energy stays
⟨H⟩=2mℏ2​(k02​+4σ02​1​)≃0.1619eV.
(83)
It includes both mean motion and the momentum spread. The mean speed is about 7.72×10−4c and the momentum spread corresponds to 1.93×10−4c, making the nonrelativistic model accurate for the appreciably occupied part of this ideal Gaussian distribution. Its mathematically unbounded tails are an idealization.
The complex width also explains where the probability goes. Write ψ=ρ​eiθ, where θ is the dimensionless quantum phase. Differentiating the exact solution gives
∂x​θ=k0​+2σ02​(1+τ2)τ(x−v0​t)​,ρj​=mℏ​∂x​θ=v0​+ts2​+t2t​(x−v0​t).
(84)
Relative to the moving centre, the probability current jrel​=j−v0​ρ points outward on both sides for t>0. This is a flux statement, not an additional assumption about individual particle trajectories. At t=ts​ and x−v0​t=±σ0​, the relative flow speeds jrel​/ρ are ±σ0​/(2ts​), about ±2.8942×104ms−1. The probability within a moving interval of half-width σ0​ falls from about 0.6827 initially to 0.5205 at ts​, exactly because of this outward probability current.
Historically, the 1926 Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem (Erste Mitteilung) by Erwin Schrödinger began with the stationary hydrogen atom problem. It distinguishes discrete negative energies from a positive continuous spectrum; it is not yet the general evolution equation displayed above.[ref] The opening of quantum mechanics discusses its original vibrating-string analogy.
In the 12 December 1933 lecture The fundamental idea of wave mechanics by Erwin Schrödinger, Erwin Schrödinger explains the move from independent rays to waves through diffraction, then applies that analogy to atomic physics. His closing pages explicitly acknowledge the difficulty of reconciling wave language with observed particle tracks. This retrospective physical argument motivates the model; it does not derive the modern Born rule from classical mechanics.[ref]
The dates separate proposal from measurement: the de Broglie wavelength belongs to the 1924 thesis, the Schrödinger equation to 1926, and the Davisson–Germer experiment's electron diffraction beams to 1927.[ref] That linked experiment describes the prepared nickel crystal, evacuated electron gun, angular collector scans, retarding-potential selection and heating controls. Its measured collector electric current tests wave-dependent scattering probabilities; it neither measures a wavefunction directly nor establishes this equation uniquely.
