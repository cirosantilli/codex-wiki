I would increase the supply of correctly matured translation elongation factor P in a bacterial production system whose target proteins contain EF-P-sensitive proline motifs. This is a concrete proposal to relieve a catalytic bottleneck while preserving the machinery that selects the correct aminoacyl-tRNA. Its benefit should be tested for the chosen products rather than asserted for every protein or organism.
The rationale is that adjacent prolines are difficult substrates for peptide-bond formation. EF-P binds the ribosome near the peptidyl-tRNA and assists productive peptide-bond formation at susceptible sequences. Lili K. Doerfel and colleagues used defined biological translation reactions and engineered substrates to connect EF-P with rapid synthesis through consecutive prolines; Susanne Ude and colleagues showed relief of polyproline-dependent stalling and physiological regulation of the bacterial protein CadC.[ref][ref] Increasing functional EF-P is therefore mechanistically different from weakening decoding discrimination or accelerating rejection-sensitive steps indiscriminately.
I would first establish the limiting step in a reconstituted bacterial biological translation system, holding the mRNA, ribosome concentration, initiation factors, charged tRNA supply and energy regeneration constant. Titrate correctly modified EF-P against the unmodified or inactive factor. For an Escherichia coli production strain, coordinate EF-P expression with its native maturation enzymes: increasing an inactive, insufficiently modified pool may add burden without relieving stalls. Introduce the change in a tunable manner and verify both EF-P abundance and its modification state by targeted mass spectrometry. This is an engineering proposal based on demonstrated factor function; the cited experiments do not establish that overexpression will improve every industrial production strain.
A simple throughput calculation makes the scope explicit. If a ribosome needs T0​ for ordinary elongation and termination, and an additional Ts​ at susceptible stalls, the completion capacity of R productive ribosomes is approximately
Jgood​=T0​+Ts​R​pfull​pcorrect​pfolded​.
(3)
This neglects initiation limitation, traffic and variation between ribosomes. As an illustrative calculation, reducing stall time from 20 s to 2 s when the remaining cycle takes 30 s raises the capacity by 50/32=1.56 at unchanged quality. A product without substantial EF-P-sensitive stalls would show little direct benefit. Moreover, extra ribosomes do not necessarily increase output if they collide behind a slow site; relief of the bottleneck may free ribosomes as well as accelerate individual chains.
Preserving error control requires more than measuring total protein. aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase editing controls charging identity, while ribosomal initial selection and kinetic proofreading help discriminate cognate from near-cognate tRNAs. These should remain intact. If an independent per-residue missense probability is ϵ for a chain of length L, the error-free fraction is approximately (1−ϵ)L; even a small per-residue deterioration can matter for a long protein. EF-P acts principally on difficult peptidyl transfer, but altered dwell times can still change ribosomal frameshifting, folding and quality control. The proposal promises a testable route to low-error acceleration, not an automatic absence of side effects.
The central controls would use the same target sequence, promoter and untranslated regions with or without added active EF-P. An inactive or maturation-defective factor tests whether catalytic assistance, rather than nonspecific protein overexpression, accounts for the gain. A motif-free reporter tests specificity. A proline-substitution reporter can test the mechanism, but cannot substitute for testing the unchanged production target because it changes the product itself. Measure growth and resource burden and include several EF-P doses: excessive factor supply may become inhibitory or wasteful. Use matched biological replicates and establish that mRNA abundance or stability has not changed enough to explain the result.
Measure elongation using time-resolved synthesis and normalized ribosome profiling, looking for relief at the predicted stall sites and reduced collisions. Profiling alone does not measure completed protein flux. Pulse–chase measurements and calibrated mass spectrometry of full-length products should establish completion, while an activity assay tests correct folding. fluorescence alone is inadequate because fluorophore maturation and degradation also affect signal. Separate assays should quantify missense incorporation, stop-codon readthrough and frameshifts, with positive error controls and detection limits. Mass-spectrometric error measurements require standards because erroneous and correct peptides need not ionize equally.
Finally, compare soluble active yield, not just synthesis speed: some pauses aid cotranslational folding, and relieving them can lower useful output. If substrate or charged tRNA supply becomes limiting, rebalance those components rather than weakening kinetic proofreading. A successful result is a reproducible increase in completed, correctly folded target molecules per unit time, with no material increase in independently measured error classes. Extension to other sequence bottlenecks would require their own mechanistic diagnosis.
