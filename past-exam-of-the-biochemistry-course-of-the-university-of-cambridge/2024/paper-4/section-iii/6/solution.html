Cytochrome P450s are versatile haem-containing oxidation enzymes that can install chemically difficult, highly selective functional groups. Photosynthetic microorganisms can supply their reducing equivalents and metabolic precursors, but useful production requires coordinated enzyme expression, electron delivery, substrate supply and product handling.
A Cytochrome P450 contains an iron–haem centre coordinated by a protein cysteine thiolate. The characteristic spectrum of its reduced carbon-monoxide complex gives the family its name. A common monooxygenation can be written
RH+O2​+2e−+2H+⟶ROH+H2​O.
(1)
One oxygen atom enters the product and the other is reduced to water. Electron transfer and protonation activate bound oxygen, generating a powerful oxidant such as P450 compound I. Hydrogen abstraction followed by oxygen rebound can hydroxylate a C–H bond, but the family also supports other reactions, including epoxidation and successive oxidation steps. The exact chemistry is enzyme- and substrate-dependent; not every P450 simply performs a single hydroxylation.
This selectivity is valuable for complex natural products. Closely related substrates can differ in the position and stereochemistry of oxygenation, and an enzyme can distinguish sites that are difficult to separate by ordinary chemical synthesis. Oxygenated terpenoid intermediates, drug precursors and other specialized metabolites are therefore attractive targets. The opportunity is selective chemistry, not a guarantee that every plant pathway will be cheap to reproduce in a microbial host.
In an oxygenic photosynthetic chassis, light absorption by photosystem II supports extraction of electrons from water. Transfer through the photosynthetic chain and photosystem I generates reduced ferredoxin, which can feed a compatible P450 directly or contribute to NADPH production for an appropriate reductase system. The distinction matters: native microsomal P450s often use a flavoprotein reductase, whereas engineered direct ferredoxin delivery can bypass that partner. Electrons must still reach the P450 at adequate rates and with correct coupling to substrate oxidation.
There is a clear experimental proof of principle. A plant CYP79A1 catalytic domain was fused to the photosystem-associated PsaM anchor in a cyanobacterium, positioning it near the thylakoid electron supply. Activity was observed in light-driven experiments. This demonstrates functional rerouting of photosynthetic reducing power into a plant P450 reaction, rather than establishing commercial production of a finished pharmaceutical.[ref]
A production design starts from the entire pathway. Supply a suitable scaffold from endogenous metabolism or introduced enzymes, express the required P450s with compatible membrane anchors and redox partners, and include any downstream transferases or other finishing enzymes. CYP79A1, for example, participates in the pathway to dhurrin; expressing it alone establishes one activity, not the completed multienzyme pathway. For a new high-value oxygenated terpenoid, one must similarly verify every intermediate rather than equating scaffold accumulation with final-product synthesis.
Several constraints can dominate performance. Plant membrane enzymes may fold poorly, insert in the wrong orientation or fail to acquire haem. An abundant P450 may remain inactive because its substrate or electron donor is limiting. Competition with carbon fixation and essential redox metabolism can make maximal electron diversion harmful to growth. Uncoupled oxygen activation can generate reactive oxygen species instead of product. Excess light may increase oxidative damage, while high cell density limits light penetration. Oxygen supply is required for ordinary P450 monooxygenation even though the host produces oxygen photosynthetically.
Optimization should therefore measure carbon flux, enzyme abundance, redox state and products together. Adjust expression ratios, redox-partner choice, subcellular localization and induction timing; relieve precursor bottlenecks without starving essential pathways; and consider export or product capture to limit toxicity. A bioreactor must balance mixing, illuminated surface area, gas exchange and biomass density.
Useful controls include dark incubation, a catalytic-site mutant, pathway-minus strains and perturbation of photosynthetic electron supply while monitoring cell viability. Mass spectrometry and authentic standards establish product identity; oxygen uptake or a fluorescent reporter alone does not. Finally report titre, productivity, carbon yield and stability over repeated cultivation. Light-powered enzyme activity is the biochemical entry point; reliable, selective and economically recoverable product formation is the engineering goal.
