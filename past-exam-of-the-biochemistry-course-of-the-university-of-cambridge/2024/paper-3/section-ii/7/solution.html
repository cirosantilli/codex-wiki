Yes: a microRNA can suppress tumour formation or progression by restraining an oncogenic programme, but that role must be demonstrated for a defined microRNA, tissue and genetic context. Reduced expression in a tumour or growth inhibition after massive microRNA mimic transfection is suggestive rather than sufficient evidence of an endogenous tumour-suppressor function.
microRNAs are short regulatory RNAs whose guide strands associate with Argonaute. Recognition through sequence complementarity, often involving a microRNA seed region and a target's 3′ untranslated region, can recruit translational repression and RNA decay machinery. Canonical precursor processing involves Drosha and Dicer, although not every microRNA follows every canonical step. A microRNA can affect many transcripts, and a transcript can receive input from several microRNAs. Therefore modest coordinated repression of several growth or survival factors can have a substantial phenotype even without complete elimination of one target. Suppression is a property of the regulatory network, not of an RNA being universally “anticancer”.
The let-7 family supplies a mechanistic example. Experiments connecting let-7-responsive sequences in Ras transcripts with regulation of Ras expression linked a developmental regulatory RNA to a conserved growth pathway.[ref] Reduced let-7 in lung cancer is compatible with loss of this restraint, but the inverse expression relationship alone would not show causation. Functional experiments restoring let-7 in relevant lung-cancer systems suppressed tumour development, providing a stronger test of tumour-suppressive capacity.[ref] Expression should still be compared with a physiological range, and one must distinguish preventing tumour initiation from treating an established tumour.
Genetic loss gives a complementary direction of evidence. The miR-15a and miR-16-1 cluster lies within a recurrently deleted region associated with chronic lymphocytic leukaemia. Deletion of the corresponding mouse regulatory region or the microRNA cluster increased B-cell proliferative abnormalities and produced clonal lymphoproliferative disease in a subset of animals.[ref] Comparing the smaller cluster deletion with a larger deletion matters because neighbouring genes can contribute to a human copy-number association. Disease after specific microRNA loss supports endogenous restraint more directly than an expression signature does; incomplete penetrance and latency imply cooperation and context rather than an automatic one-step conversion of every b cell.
The p53–miR-34 connection illustrates both strength and limitation of a proposed pathway. p53 activation can induce miR-34-family transcription, and ectopic miR-34 family can restrain proliferation or promote death in suitable biological cells, connecting a transcriptional tumour suppressor with post-transcriptional repression.[ref] Yet mice lacking all three miR-34 family members retained substantial p53-dependent arrest and apoptosis and did not show the predicted general increase in tumour susceptibility in the tested settings.[ref] This does not prove that miR-34 family cannot suppress a tumour in any setting. It shows that a gain-of-function phenotype and placement downstream of p53 do not establish that the microRNA family is an indispensable general mediator of p53 tumour suppression.
A convincing analysis combines several evidence types. Measure mature guide RNA in defined malignant and normal biological cell populations, distinguishing it from precursor abundance and changes in stromal composition. Determine whether deletion, promoter silencing or altered processing explains loss. Reduce or delete the endogenous microRNA and test initiation or progression in a suitable model, with restoration from a controlled expression system. Compare related family members to expose redundancy. Use seed-mutant controls and multiple reagents to distinguish sequence-specific regulation from delivery toxicity or innate immune activation; deleting an entire processing enzyme is not a specific test of one microRNA because it affects many regulatory RNAs.
To identify consequential targets, combine binding evidence, early RNA/protein responses and target-site tests. A 3′-region reporter whose seed-complementary site is mutated supports direct sequence-dependent regulation, but the decisive endogenous test is to edit the site or express a suitably controlled microRNA-resistant target and ask whether it rescues the phenotype. Failure of one target to rescue can reflect regulation of several cooperating targets rather than absence of specificity. Conversely, rescuing only bulk proliferation does not establish which target controls invasion or metastasis. Measure relevant tissue phenotypes and target protein levels, not merely a pathway enrichment score from predicted targets.
Tumour-suppressive activity also does not guarantee an effective therapy. Delivery must reach the relevant biological cells and release the RNA into the correct compartment without excessive exposure of normal tissues. A clinical miR-34a microRNA mimic study encountered serious immune-mediated toxicity, illustrating that an endogenous regulatory rationale does not establish safe systemic replacement.[ref] Target abundance, isoform-specific 3′ regions and competing pathways can change the effect of the same microRNA between tumours. microRNAs can be genuine tumour suppressors; the strongest evidence is specific endogenous loss, controlled restoration and mechanistic target tests, interpreted alongside genetic redundancy and tissue context.
