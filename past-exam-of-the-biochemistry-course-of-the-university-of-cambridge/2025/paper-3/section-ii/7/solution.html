Solid-tumour immunotherapy must overcome a chain of barriers: identify suitable targets, generate effective immune biological cells, deliver them into every relevant lesion, sustain their function and prevent escape without damaging normal biological tissue. Failure at any one step can limit treatment. immune checkpoint blockade, adoptive T-cell therapy and cancer vaccines address different parts of this chain and therefore face overlapping but non-identical challenges.
Successful treatment is possible, so the problem is not a universal inability of immunity to affect solid tumours. For example, a randomised trial in advanced melanoma found longer progression-free survival with expanded tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes than with ipilimumab. The regimen also included preparative lymphodepletion and subsequent interleukin-2, so benefit and toxicity belong to the treatment package rather than the transferred biological cells alone.[ref] A result in a selected melanoma population cannot simply be transferred to every solid-tumour lineage.
The first challenge is target quality. Many tumour-associated antigens are also present in essential normal biological tissues, whereas truly tumour-specific neoantigens can vary between patients and subclones. T-cell receptors recognise peptides presented by compatible HLA molecules, requiring appropriate processing and presentation. A mutation detected in DNA does not guarantee expression, processing or presentation of an immunogenic peptide. chimeric antigen receptors can recognise a cell-surface target without peptide–HLA presentation, but conventional designs cannot directly access most intracellular proteins. Thus broader target access, tumour coverage and specificity cannot all be assumed from the same targeting strategy.
Even a promising target may be heterogeneous. Cells expressing little antigen can escape a single-target therapy, and different metastases may present different targets or immune environments. Targeting several antigens could reduce escape but can also increase the normal-tissue recognition problem. Multi-region sampling and single-cell measurements improve coverage estimates; one positive biopsy is not proof that all clonogenic tumour biological cells are targetable. A relevant experiment tests killing over a measured range of target densities, including normal biological cells, rather than only a high-expression laboratory line.
Immune activation and entry are separate obstacles. dendritic cells must acquire and present antigen with appropriate activating signals, and responsive T cells must expand and reach the tumour. Abnormal vessels, inadequate chemokine matching and dense or reorganised extracellular matrix can prevent effective entry. A tumour may contain many lymphocytes restricted to the surrounding stroma, with few contacting malignant biological cells. Studies linking a stromal TGF-beta signalling programme to poor response also showed in mouse models that combined pathway and PD-L1 blockade improved intratumoural access and antitumour activity. The causal combination experiment strengthens an inference that human expression correlations alone could not establish.[ref]
Once inside, immune biological cells face hypoxia, nutrient competition, inhibitory metabolites and suppressive signals from regulatory T cells, tumour-associated macrophages and other stromal populations. Persistent antigen stimulation can establish T-cell exhaustion, with impaired function and a regulatory state that is not always reversed by blocking one inhibitory receptor. PD-1 or CTLA-4 blockade can release important restraints, but cannot reliably restore missing antigen presentation, compensate for absent tumour-reactive clones or abolish every suppressive pathway. Hence a high density of PD-L1 or a large tumour mutational burden can be informative without being a deterministic response predictor.
Tumours also evolve under therapy. Paired pretreatment and relapse samples from a small melanoma series identified acquired defects involving JAK1, JAK2 or beta-2-microglobulin. These connected resistance with impaired interferon-gamma signalling or MHC class I presentation, and functional assays helped interpret the genetic findings. They provide concrete routes by which a previously responsive tumour can escape; a selected four-patient series cannot estimate how often each mechanism explains resistance generally.[ref] Some escape routes are shared across therapies; others depend on whether recognition uses a peptide–HLA complex or a directly bound surface antigen.
Safety constrains how strongly these barriers can be attacked. immune checkpoint blockade can produce immune-related adverse events in normal organs, while transferred immune biological cells can produce severe inflammatory responses or recognise normal biological tissues bearing the target. An early ERBB2-directed chimeric antigen receptor study reported fatal toxicity; recognition of low-level target in lung epithelium was the authors' proposed explanation, rather than a definitively isolated causal mechanism. It demonstrates why high tumour expression and selective killing in a simple culture assay are insufficient safety evidence.[ref] Candidate safeguards include restricted receptor activation, controllable persistence and elimination switches, but each trades activity or durability against risk and requires direct validation.
There are practical challenges as well. Autologous biological cell therapy requires accessible biological tissue or biological cells, reproducible manufacture and expansion without losing effective function. A patient may deteriorate before the product is ready. Conditioning and supportive regimens add burdens; local delivery may improve access to one site while missing distant disease. Vaccines face time needed for priming and the possibility that newly activated biological cells still cannot enter or function within the tumour. Combining therapies is mechanistically attractive but can increase toxicity, cost and uncertainty about which component supplies benefit.
Evaluation must therefore match the proposed mechanism. Test antigen recognition with antigen-negative and normal-tissue controls, measure trafficking and spatial contact separately from killing, and monitor immune-cell persistence and function over time. Paired and multi-region biopsies can distinguish absent entry from failed recognition or evolved escape. Clinical trials need appropriate comparators, durable disease-control endpoints and toxicity reporting, not response rate alone. The central challenge is to make recognition, access and sustained function coincide across a heterogeneous evolving tumour while preserving normal biological tissue; no single biomarker or immune intervention guarantees all three.
