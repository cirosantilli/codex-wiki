A common transition into anaphase both releases chromosomes for separation and permits the spindle to organise cytokinesis; later safeguards delay final separation when segregation remains incomplete. Temporal coordination makes the events occur in the right order, while spatial coordination positions division between the separating chromosome sets. The two forms of control interact but are experimentally distinguishable.
S phase establishes sister chromatids and their association through cohesin. During mitosis, chromosome condensation and DNA topoisomerase II activity help prepare the copies for separation. Each sister's kinetochore should attach to the opposite spindle pole, producing biorientation. Aurora B contributes to error correction by destabilising inappropriate attachments. Attachment and tension affect this process, but the spindle checkpoint should not be described simply as a direct meter of equal tension on every chromosome.
Unattached kinetochores catalyse formation of the mitotic checkpoint complex, involving Mad2, BubR1, Bub3 and Cdc20. This inhibits productive anaphase-promoting complex activity toward key substrates. In a classic causal experiment, destruction of the last unattached kinetochore allowed anaphase to proceed, with chromosome-arm damage providing an important control. It was the inhibitory signalling structure, rather than the physical presence of one unaligned chromosome somewhere in the biological cell, that maintained the delay.[ref]
Once attachment-associated checkpoint signalling is silenced and inhibitory complexes are dismantled, anaphase-promoting complex with Cdc20 promotes ubiquitin-dependent destruction of securin and mitotic cyclin B. securin destruction permits separase activity, which cleaves the remaining cohesion-bearing kleisin subunit. Non-cleavable Scc1 blocked sister separation in budding yeast, directly linking regulated proteolysis to the loss of chromosome cohesion.[ref] Chromosome movement can then proceed through shortening of kinetochore-associated microtubules and separation of the spindle poles.
Destruction of cyclin B also lowers CDK1 activity. This changes the phosphorylation balance, allowing protein phosphatases to reverse mitotic modifications and permitting chromosome decondensation, nuclear reassembly and cytokinetic organisation. Thus regulated proteolysis produces two coordinated outputs: removal of a physical chromosome linkage and removal of the mitotic kinase state. Their sharing of a controller improves ordering, but it does not mean that cytokinesis physically waits for a sensor to count every chromosome at its destination.
For animal cytokinesis, overlapping microtubules of the central spindle and signals associated with astral microtubules specify an equatorial cortical region. centralspindlin recruits and regulates the guanine-nucleotide exchange factor ECT2, which promotes local RhoA activation. RhoA stimulates actin assembly and myosin II activity, organising the actomyosin ring that drives furrow ingression. This geometry aligns the division plane between the segregating chromosome masses. Spindle position can itself be set by cortical cell polarity cues, explaining how the same machinery supports either approximately symmetric or asymmetric division.
Depletion and localisation experiments distinguish the biochemical signal from a simple structural scaffold. Experiments in human biological cells showed that ECT2 is required for cortical RhoA and furrow assembly, and that different centralspindlin subunits have distinguishable consequences for these events. Loss of central-spindle localisation need not abolish every equatorial signal, because additional spatial inputs can persist.[ref] Accordingly, central-spindle disruption should be assessed alongside astral signals, spindle orientation and direct measurements of the cortical activity zone.
Furrow ingression leaves an intercellular bridge with a midbody. Final abscission involves ESCRT-III-dependent membrane-remodelling machinery and must be distinguished from the earlier contractile event. Chromatin trapped in the bridge can sustain Aurora B-dependent signalling and delay abscission. Human-cell experiments connected persistent chromosome bridges with continued kinase activity and delayed final separation, reducing the risk of unsuccessful division and furrow regression.[ref] This safeguard can provide time to resolve a problem; persistent bridges can still lead to breakage, regression or abnormal descendants.
A decisive experimental series would image labelled chromosomes, microtubules, cyclin B, active RhoA and the biological cell boundary in the same biological cells. Acute checkpoint inhibition predicts premature anaphase and increased segregation errors. Stabilised securin or cleavage-resistant cohesion should obstruct separation; persistent cyclin B should obstruct normal mitotic exit. Acute ECT2 loss can impair cytokinesis even when chromosome segregation proceeds, demonstrating separable downstream machinery. Use timed interventions after spindle assembly, independent depletion reagents, appropriately expressed rescue constructs and controls for global biological cell damage. Count chromosome allocation, furrow initiation, ingression and abscission separately; a binucleate endpoint does not reveal which step failed.
The checkpoint also has blind spots. A merotelic attachment, where one kinetochore binds microtubules from both poles, may evade an unattached-kinetochore signal and generate a lagging chromosome. Coordination is therefore layered rather than absolute: attachment surveillance, a shared proteolytic transition, spindle-directed cortical signalling and a late abscission checkpoint jointly reduce the chance of partitioning the genome incorrectly. The detailed geometry differs in organisms that divide with a cell plate or other cytokinetic mechanisms.
