Singular perturbation (ϵ→0+)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Differential equation Ordinary differential equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 5k Articles: 73
A singular perturbation is a parameter-dependent problem whose limiting problem changes qualitatively as the parameter tends to zero. In an ordinary differential equation, a small coefficient of the highest derivative can reduce the order of the limiting differential equation, preventing it from satisfying all the original boundary conditions. Different length or time scales often require different asymptotic expansions.[ref]
Consider the boundary value problem
ϵyϵ′′​+yϵ′​=0,0<x<1,yϵ​(0)=0,yϵ​(1)=1,ϵ>0.
(1)
This second-order differential equation is exactly solvable. Setting v=yϵ′​ gives the first-order linear differential equation ϵv′+v=0, so v=Ce−x/ϵ. One integration and the two boundary conditions give
yϵ​(x)=1−e−1/ϵ1−e−x/ϵ​.
(2)
Both boundary conditions hold for every positive ϵ. For numerical evaluation, write the same ratio as expm1(−x/ϵ)/expm1(−1/ϵ), where expm1(t) evaluates the exponential function minus one without catastrophic cancellation near t=0. Keep the denominator for finite ϵ: at ϵ=0.2 it is approximately 0.993262, rather than exactly one.
Setting ϵ=0 gives y0′​=0. The outer solution selected by the right boundary condition is therefore y0​=1, which fails the left boundary condition. The exact exponential function e−x/ϵ shows how that mismatch is repaired: the solution rises from zero to almost one within a boundary layer of width x=O(ϵ), using Big O notation. For each fixed x>0, yϵ​(x)→1, while yϵ​(0)=0 always. This pointwise convergence fails to be uniform convergence to the outer solution on [0,1], since sup0≤x≤1​∣yϵ​(x)−1∣=1 for every ϵ>0.
The coefficient ϵ is small, but its product with the second derivative need not be small. Direct differentiation gives
yϵ′​(x)=ϵ(1−e−1/ϵ)e−x/ϵ​,yϵ′′​(x)=−ϵ2(1−e−1/ϵ)e−x/ϵ​.
(3)
At x=ϵX with fixed X≥0, these derivatives grow as ϵ−1 and ϵ−2 respectively as ϵ→0+. Consequently ϵyϵ′′​ and yϵ′​ have exactly equal magnitudes and opposite signs. Even away from the boundary layer, their magnitudes remain equal in this example; both become exponentially small at each fixed positive x. The outer expansion neglects an exponentially small transient there, rather than establishing that one derivative term is smaller than the other everywhere.
To predict the width without knowing the exact solution, suppose an order-one change occurs over distance δ. The derivative scales are y′=O(δ−1) and y′′=O(δ−2). The dominant balance ϵ/δ2∼1/δ gives δ∼ϵ. The resulting inner variable X=x/ϵ makes the boundary layer visible at a fixed scale. This is the useful feature of singular perturbation: an apparently disappearing term identifies the missing structure when one measures its derivatives on the appropriate scale.

Beyond-all-orders boundary-value sensitivity

Words: 68
Singular perturbation OurBigBook.com
A homogeneous mode can be order one inside an interval and exponentially small at an endpoint. Adjusting an endpoint value beyond all algebraic orders can then change the interior solution by order one. In the nested layers at an essential singular endpoint example, the mode exp(−x/ϵ−2ϵ2/x) has this behavior: prescribed exact endpoint values give a unique solution for each fixed positive ϵ, but leading asymptotic endpoint data do not select a unique bounded family.

Adiabatic elimination

Singular perturbation OurBigBook.com
Adiabatic elimination replaces a rapidly relaxing variable by its instantaneous stable quasistatic value. If R˙=−Γ(t)R+P(t) with Γ>0, write R=R0​+δR, R0​=P/Γ. Then δR˙+ΓδR=−R˙0​. After the transient, δR≃−R˙0​/Γ when coefficients vary slowly compared with Γ−1. This gives both the leading reduction and its time-scale error; rapid relaxation alone does not justify an additional Taylor expansion in another parameter.

Matched asymptotic expansion

Words: 2k Articles: 32
Singular perturbation OurBigBook.com
A matched asymptotic expansion constructs approximations in regions with different distinguished limits and fixes their free constants by requiring agreement in an overlap region. The inner expansion resolves rapid variation; the outer expansion describes the surrounding region. Matching compares their common limiting behavior, rather than imposing distant boundary conditions directly on a local approximation.[ref]
For the singular perturbation problem ϵy′′+y′=0, y(0)=0, y(1)=1, the leading outer solution is y0​=1. The inner variable X=x/ϵ gives Y0′′​+Y0′​=0 and Y0​(0)=0, hence Y0​=A(1−e−X). In the overlap region ϵ≪x≪1, the inner variable tends to infinity while the outer coordinate tends to zero. The asymptotic matching condition is
limX→∞​Y0​(X)=limx→0+​y0​(x)=1,
(4)
so A=1. One concrete path through the overlap region is x=ϵ​: then x→0 and X=ϵ−1/2→∞. Matching selects the free constant that the left boundary condition alone could not determine.
Why choose the right boundary condition for the outer solution? If instead one selects y0​=0 from the left, a proposed right boundary layer with Z=(1−x)/ϵ satisfies U0′′​−U0′​=0. Its general solution is A+BeZ. Matching to zero as Z→∞ forces A=B=0, contradicting U0​(0)=1. The direction in which the exponential function decays determines which endpoint can carry the boundary layer; endpoint selection is part of the calculation.

Overlap region

Words: 61
Matched asymptotic expansion OurBigBook.com
An overlap region is a parameter-dependent range in which two neighboring asymptotic expansions have simultaneously ordered common limits. Between scales ϵ2 and ϵ, it is ϵ2≪x≪ϵ: the narrow coordinate tends to infinity while the wider coordinate tends to zero. Matching uses these limiting descriptions, not an assertion that either expansion is uniform at all values of the other's scaled variable.

dxd​=ϵ−1dXd​,ϵyϵ′′​+yϵ′​=ϵ−1(Yϵ′′​+Yϵ′​).
(6)
Thus both derivative terms survive in the inner differential equation Yϵ′′​+Yϵ′​=0. The exact domain is 0≤X≤1/ϵ, with
Yϵ​(X)=1−e−1/ϵ1−e−X​,Y0​(X)=1−e−X.

Outer expansion

Words: 148
Matched asymptotic expansion OurBigBook.com
An outer expansion describes the solution away from a thin or distant region resolved by an inner expansion. It is matched to that inner expansion in their common overlap region. Its range of validity must be specified: an approximation at each fixed positive x need not have uniform convergence down to an endpoint.
For the singular perturbation problem ϵy′′+y′=0, y(0)=0, y(1)=1, try the asymptotic expansion y∼y0​+ϵy1​+ϵ2y2​+⋯ at fixed x>0. Substitution gives y0′​=0 and yn′​=−yn−1′′​ for n≥1. Expanding the right boundary condition gives y0​(1)=1 and yn​(1)=0 for n≥1. Therefore y0​=1 and every higher coefficient vanishes. The missing boundary layer is exponentially small at each fixed positive x, invisible to all these powers of ϵ.
The exact solution supplies a quantitative limit. For every fixed a∈(0,1),
supa≤x≤1​∣yϵ​(x)−1∣=1−e−1/ϵe−a/ϵ−e−1/ϵ​≤1−e−1/ϵe−a/ϵ​⟶0.
(8)
This is uniform convergence on [a,1]. On [0,1] the same supremum norm of the error is exactly one. The inner expansion supplies the endpoint information that this outer expansion cannot encode.

Additive composite expansion

Words: 179
Matched asymptotic expansion OurBigBook.com
An additive composite expansion combines an inner expansion and an outer expansion by subtracting their common behavior in the overlap region:
ycomp​=yout​+yin​−yoverlap​.
(9)
The subtraction removes the part counted twice. Near the boundary layer, the outer expansion cancels the overlap contribution; away from the boundary layer, the inner expansion cancels it. An error estimate is needed to establish accuracy across the whole domain.[ref]
For the singular perturbation problem ϵy′′+y′=0, y(0)=0, y(1)=1, the outer solution is one, the leading inner expansion is 1−e−x/ϵ, and their overlap is one. Thus ycomp​=1−e−x/ϵ. With q=e−1/ϵ, comparison with the exact normalized solution gives
yϵ​(x)−ycomp​(x)=1−qq(1−e−x/ϵ)​,sup0≤x≤1​∣yϵ​−ycomp​∣=q.
(10)
The supremum norm error is therefore exponentially small on the entire interval, even though the outer solution alone has error one. This is an error bound for function values; errors in derivatives have additional powers of ϵ−1.
The composite satisfies the differential equation exactly but has right boundary condition ycomp​(1)=1−q. Dividing it by 1−q restores that boundary condition and recovers the exact solution. This example makes both the strength and the limit of matching explicit: it gives a global approximation, while finite-ϵ normalization can still carry information beyond every algebraic order.
