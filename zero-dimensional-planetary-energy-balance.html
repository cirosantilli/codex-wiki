Zero-dimensional planetary energy balance (CT˙=S(1−α)/4−σT4)

Consider an idealized planet that is a sphere of radius R, illuminated by parallel sunlight of irradiance S, measured on a plane perpendicular to the rays. Its intercepting projected area is πR2, whereas its total surface area is 4πR2. Therefore the incoming power averaged per unit total surface area is
4πR2SπR2​=4S​,Pabs​=(1−α)SπR2,
(6)
where α is the broadband Bond albedo. The factor four is a sphere geometry result, not an atmospheric transmission factor. Local sunlight still varies over the illuminated hemisphere and vanishes on the dark side.
Now stipulate a single spatially uniform temperature T, no atmosphere, no internal heating, and emissivity one for outgoing thermal radiation. This is a thermal blackbody model using the Stefan–Boltzmann law; the prescribed solar-band reflection is treated separately, so the body is not a perfect absorber at all wavelengths. Let C>0 be the effective heat capacity per unit surface area, in Jm−2K−1. Conservation of energy gives the ordinary differential equation
CdtdT​=F(T),F(T)=4S​[1−α(T)]−σT4.
(7)
Here F is net heating per unit surface area, in Wm−2, and σ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant. Use σ=5.670374419×10−8Wm−2K−4, rounded from the exact constant reported by NIST. [ref] The uniform temperature is a model assumption: generally the surface average of T4 differs from the fourth power of the surface-average temperature.
For fixed Bond albedo α, radiative equilibrium requires F(T∗​)=0, giving
T∗​=[4σS(1−α)​]1/4.
(8)

Doubling albedo here lowers the equilibrium temperature by 33.231K, rather than halving it: the absorbed fraction changes from 0.7 to 0.4, and thermal radiation scales as T4. These stipulated Bond albedos are whole-planet examples, not measurements of bare water and ice. The model predicts neither Earth's modern surface temperature nor its response to actual climate change; it omits the greenhouse effect, spatial heat transfer and seasons.
Each fixed-albedo equilibrium is a stable equilibrium: for T∗​>0, the derivative is F′(T∗​)=−4σT∗3​<0. A small temperature increase produces net cooling, and a small decrease produces net heating. This is the simplest expression of the stabilizing Planck feedback. Allowing albedo to fall with temperature adds a competing term, as in ice-ocean albedo feedback.
