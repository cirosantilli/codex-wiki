Slowly varying function (ℓ(ut)/ℓ(t)→1)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Regular variation
OurBigBook.com Words: 346
A positive measurable function ℓ is slowly varying at infinity if
ℓ(t)ℓ(ut)​⟶1(t→∞)
(41)
for every fixed u>0. It has regular variation of index zero. “Slowly” concerns relative change under multiplication of the argument; it does not mean that the function must be bounded, have a small derivative, or converge to a finite value.
Every positive constant is a slowly varying function. So are logt, 1/logt and (logt)a for any fixed real number a, on t>1. Indeed,
(logt)a(log(ut))a​=(1+logtlogu​)a⟶1.
(42)
The first grows without bound, the second tends to zero, and the constant does neither. Another unbounded example is ℓ(t)=elogt​:
logℓ(t)ℓ(ut)​=logt+logu​+logt​logu​⟶0.
(43)
This function can grow much faster than logt while retaining regular variation of index zero.
Even convergence is unnecessary. The bounded function
ℓ(t)=exp(sin(loglogt)),t>1,
(44)
oscillates between e−1 and e. It is nevertheless a slowly varying function: the change in the argument of the sine under t↦ut is
loglog(ut)−loglogt=log(1+logtlogu​)⟶0,
(45)
and the Lipschitz continuity of the sine makes the logarithm of the ratio tend to zero. The sequences tk​=exp(exp(π/2+2πk)) and sk​=exp(exp(3π/2+2πk)) give the two values e and e−1 for every nonnegative integer k, so there is no limit at infinity.
Contrast this with h(t)=2+sin(logt), which is positive and bounded but is not a slowly varying function. Fix u=eπ/2. Along tk​=e2πk its rescaling ratio is h(utk​)/h(tk​)=3/2; along sk​=eπ+2πk the ratio is 1/2. The persistent oscillations prevent even a limit for this one fixed rescaling. It is not regularly varying with any finite index either.
The useful distinction is whether a fixed proportional change of input eventually produces a negligible proportional change of output. A slowly varying function can therefore preserve the scaling exponent of a power law while changing its magnitude substantially. The decomposition in regular variation explains why a logarithmic factor can matter in numerical estimates without changing the limiting exponent.
