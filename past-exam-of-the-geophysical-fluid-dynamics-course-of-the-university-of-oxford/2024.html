Take x eastward, y northward and z upward. In a rotating reference frame, the horizontal Cauchy momentum equation is
DtDuh​​+fz×uh​=−ρ1​∇h​p+Fh​,f=2Ωsinϕ.
(1)
Here Fh​ represents other accelerations, such as friction, and f is the Coriolis parameter. When the Rossby number U/(∣f∣L) and the ratio 1/(∣f∣τ) are small, and friction is weak, both advective and local acceleration can be neglected. The Coriolis force then balances the pressure gradient force: this is geostrophic balance, with fv=px​/ρ and fu=−py​/ρ. The geostrophic wind runs along atmospheric isobars, with low pressure on its left in the northern hemisphere.
A rapid change of pressure need not bring the corresponding geostrophic wind into existence at once. An initially weak wind therefore leaves an inward pressure gradient force around the new low. For a smoothed anomaly of horizontal scale L=5×105 m, geostrophic balance would require
Ug​∼ρ∣f∣L∣p′∣​≃33 ms−1(ρ1.2 kgm−3​)(∣f∣10−4 s−1​).
(2)
For the illustrative midlatitude value ∣f∣=10−4 s−1, the implied Rossby number is already about 0.67, so curved-flow acceleration matters. With a small tropical value ∣f∣=10−5 s−1, the inferred speed is 333 ms−1 and the Rossby number about 67: the assumed geostrophic balance is untenable. Geostrophic adjustment redistributes the anomaly and emits internal gravity waves, leaving a weaker balanced component.
For a target speed U∗​=10 ms−1 on the same scale, the required scale estimate is
∣p′adj​∣∼ρ∣f∣LU∗​=6 hPa(1.2 kgm−3ρ​)(10−4 s−1∣f∣​).​
(3)
Thus 6 hPa, or 0.30 of the initial magnitude, is the midlatitude estimate; at the illustrative tropical ∣f∣=10−5 s−1 it is 0.6 hPa, or 0.030 of the initial magnitude. The paper gives no latitude, so there is no unique numerical reduction. At the equator itself one must use equatorial dynamics rather than divide by f=0. Even the tropical 10 ms−1 estimate has Rossby number 2, so it describes the scale permitted by the stated balance, rather than a controlled tropical geostrophic balance.
There are two qualifications to “unstable”. A literal top-hat pressure anomaly has a discontinuous edge, where no finite classical geostrophic wind can balance its distributional pressure gradient. The 500 km estimate assumes a smoothed edge; a thinner edge requires stronger winds. More generally a large low is not, by its amplitude alone, proved to have an exponentially growing instability. For a concrete counterexample to the claim that such an amplitude cannot be stationary, set p′(s)=−Pexp(−s2/a2), with cylindrical radius s, constant ρ and f>0. The cyclonic gradient wind
vθ​(s)=2−fs+f2s2+4sps​/ρ​​
(4)
is real and satisfies vθ2​/s+fvθ​=ps​/ρ for every P>0, with a regular limit at the centre. This is a stationary axisymmetric flow; no claim about its perturbation stability is needed. Here the physically justified conclusion is that a rapidly imposed anomaly without its balancing wind is unbalanced.
Small tropical Coriolis parameter allows only weak horizontal pressure gradients to coexist with ordinary large-scale winds. Hydrostatic balance connects the vertical variation of these pressure gradients to horizontal temperature gradients; equivalently the approximate thermal wind relation gives fuz​≃−(g/T)Ty​. For comparable vertical wind shear, small ∣f∣ means smaller ∣Ty​∣. Internal gravity waves can spread heating-induced buoyancy anomalies efficiently before rotation arrests the adjustment. This motivates the weak temperature gradient approximation for the tropical free troposphere; it does not require the land surface or atmospheric boundary layer to have uniform temperature.

Use a plane wave with phase kx+mz−ωt, and take N>0, k,m=0. The positive-frequency branch of the given hydrostatic dispersion relation is ω=N∣k∣/∣m∣. The wavevector is K=(k,m). Differentiating with respect to its components gives the group velocity, which carries the wave packet and its energy:
cg​=∇K​ω=(kω​,−mω​).​
(5)
The normal phase velocity, which follows a crest in its normal direction, is
cp​=k2+m2ω​(k,m).​
(6)
Consequently,
K⋅cg​=0,cp​⋅cg​=0,cg,x​cg,z​​=−mk​.
(7)
Thus the group velocity lies along the constant-phase lines z=−(k/m)x+constant, perpendicular to the phase velocity. For k,m>0, crests move upward and rightward, whereas energy moves downward and rightward. Choosing m<0 gives upward and rightward energy propagation and downward and rightward normal phase propagation. Conjugate or oppositely directed components supply the other outgoing directions from a localized source.
The speeds are
∣cg​∣=∣m∣N​1+m2k2​​,∣cp​∣=∣m∣k2+m2​N∣k∣​.​
(8)
The hydrostatic limit is ∣k/m∣≪1. In that limit energy propagates almost horizontally, with ∣cg,x​∣=N/∣m∣ and ∣cg,z​∣=N∣k∣/m2, while normal phase propagation is almost vertical and has speed approximately N∣k∣/m2. The horizontal speed at which a crest intersects a fixed height is ω/k=cg,x​; this is not the horizontal component of the normal phase velocity vector. For example, N=10−2 s−1, vertical wavelength 2π/∣m∣=10 km and horizontal wavelength 2π/∣k∣=1000 km give ∣cg,x​∣≃15.9 ms−1 and ∣cg,z​∣≃0.159 ms−1.
These are local speeds in a uniform medium, without a background current. The non-rotating approximation requires ∣f∣≪ω, while the hydrostatic approximation requires ω≪N. At lower frequencies, rotation changes the dispersion relation to that of inertia-gravity waves.

The equations describe the linear reduced gravity shallow water equations for a uniform upper layer over a deep, resting lower layer, or equivalently one separated internal vertical mode. The layer is shallow compared with its horizontal scales, permitting hydrostatic balance, and its horizontal velocity is independent of depth. The Boussinesq approximation retains the small density contrast through g′=g(ρ2​−ρ1​)/ρ0​>0. We neglect friction, external forcing, background currents, and quadratic perturbation terms. On an equatorial beta plane, f=βy with β>0, and the equatorial Kelvin wave branch imposes v=0. The meridional Cauchy momentum equation then reduces exactly within this linear model to a balance between the Coriolis force and pressure gradient force.
Strictly, the printed h must denote the thickness perturbation η, with total thickness h0​+η and ∣η∣≪h0​. A signed oscillation around zero cannot represent the total positive layer thickness. Define c=g′h0​​. For a nonzero zonal wavenumber k, the zonal Cauchy momentum equation and conservation of mass give
ωu=kg′η​,ωη​=kh0​u,ω2=c2k2.
(9)
On the eastward branch ω=ck, the wave polarization is u=(c/h0​)η. Meridional geostrophic balance gives
g′ηy​=−βyh0​c​η,ηηy​​=−cβy​,η∝exp(−2cβy2​).
(10)
The branch ω=−ck instead grows as exp(+βy2/(2c)) and is excluded by decay away from the equator. Matching the paper's Gaussian function therefore gives
Leq2​=β2c​,η=A0​e−y2/Leq2​cos(kx−ckt),u=h0​c​η,v=0.​
(11)
The usual equatorial deformation radius is Req​=c/β​, with the envelope written e−y2/(2Req2​). Thus the printed exponent uses Leq​=2​Req​. Calling that width a deformation radius is a convention; using the standard radius without the factor 1/2 would fail the printed meridional equation.
At an instant, alternating positive and negative thickness anomalies have their largest magnitudes on the equator and decay symmetrically away from it. Eastward arrows occupy the positive anomalies, westward arrows the negative anomalies; the velocity vanishes at the nodes and weakens away from the equator. The whole pattern travels eastward at speed c. Meridionally, fu=−g′ηy​ is geostrophic balance. For a positive anomaly north of the equator, the northward pressure gradient force balances the southward Coriolis force; both directions reverse south of the equator. Zonally, fv=0 and ut​=−g′ηx​: the zonal pressure gradient force accelerates the flow, so it is not in zonal geostrophic balance. On the equator the meridional terms both vanish smoothly.

Hold l,m,f0​,N fixed when differentiating the dispersion relation of Rossby waves, so that α2≥0 is independent of k. For k=0, stationary zonal phase requires
0=kω​=U−k2+α2β​,U=k2+α2β​.
(12)
The zonal group velocity measured relative to the ground is
cg,x​=∂k∂ω​=U+(k2+α2)2β(k2−α2)​.
(13)
Using the stationarity condition,
cg,x​=(k2+α2)22βk2​=k2+α22Uk2​>0.​
(14)
Here β=2Ωcosϕ/a>0 on the ordinary midlatitude beta plane, so the stationary Rossby wave requires an eastward background current and carries its wave packet eastward. If α>0, a real nonzero stationary k exists only for 0<U<β/α2. The restriction k=0 matters: a zonally uniform disturbance with k=0 has ω=0 identically, but its zonal phase velocity is undefined, so it is not a counterexample within the stated meaning of stationary waves.

Kelvin waves: weakened trade winds, or a westerly wind burst, excite a downwelling equatorial Kelvin wave. Its positive upper-layer thickness anomaly travels eastward and deepens the eastern Pacific thermocline. Upwelling then entrains warmer water, favouring eastern Pacific surface warming and the development of El Niño. This is a propagating adjustment of the thermocline, rather than transport of an unchanged parcel of warm water across the entire basin.[ref]
Gravity waves: changed tropical convection and its latent heat release force atmospheric internal gravity waves. Their rapid propagation spreads the associated pressure and buoyancy disturbances, allowing the tropical terrestrial atmosphere to adjust to the shifted heating and contributing to the divergent circulation and surface wind response coupled to the ocean. This is one atmospheric adjustment mechanism; it does not assert that all wind variability during a particular El Niño is caused by this wave family.
Rossby waves: wind forcing during El Niño also launches westward equatorial Rossby waves with a shoaling thermocline signal. Reflection at the western Pacific boundary can produce an eastward upwelling equatorial Kelvin wave, which shoals the eastern thermocline and promotes cooling. This supplies a delayed negative feedback that can help terminate the event.[ref] These examples describe mechanisms relevant to events such as 2023/24, rather than claiming an event-specific attribution from the exam data.

a OurBigBook.com
Use eastward x, northward y, constant layer depth H, constant reference density ρ0​, and a depth-independent horizontal velocity field (u,v). Assume a flat bottom and a rigid upper boundary, giving the two-dimensional incompressibility condition ux​+vy​=0. The traditional approximation retains only the vertical component of planetary rotation in the horizontal equations. Take the Coriolis parameter f=f(y), a constant linear drag rate r, and no other torques. The component Cauchy momentum equations are
Du−fv=−ρ0​px​​+ρ0​Hτs​​−ru,Dv+fu=−ρ0​py​​−rv,D=∂t​+u∂x​+v∂y​.
(15)
Define relative vorticity ξ=vx​−uy​. Apply ∂x​ to the second equation and subtract ∂y​ of the first. The pressure gradient force has zero curl because ρ0​ is constant and mixed partial derivatives commute. Direct expansion of the material-acceleration terms gives
∂x​(Dv)−∂y​(Du)=Dξ+(ux​+vy​)ξ=Dξ.
(16)
The curl of the rotation terms is
∂x​(fu)+∂y​(fv)=f(ux​+vy​)+vfy​=vfy​.
(17)
The imposed zonal wind stress contributes −τs,y​/(ρ0​H), and constant linear drag contributes −rξ. Hence
Dξ+vfy​=−ρ0​Hτs,y​​−rξ.
(18)
Since Df=vfy​, defining absolute vorticity ζ=ξ+f gives
(∂t​+u∂x​+v∂y​)ζ=−ρ0​H1​∂y∂τs​​−rξ.​
(19)
Without the incompressibility condition, the left-hand side would contain the stretching term ζ∇h​⋅u. Variable layer depth, variable density or spatially varying linear drag would introduce further terms. No small-Rossby number assumption was needed to obtain this constant-depth vorticity equation.

On a beta plane, f=f0​+βy, the preceding vorticity equation is
ξt​+uξx​+vξy​+βv=−ρ0​Hτs,y​​−rξ.
(20)
In the broad ocean interior, assume a statistically steady flow, weak advection of relative vorticity, and negligible friction compared with planetary-vorticity advection. The constant-depth, constant-density assumptions of 2(a) exclude topographic and baroclinic vorticity generation contributions. More precisely, require ∣ξt​∣,∣u⋅∇ξ∣,∣rξ∣≪∣βv∣. Then Sverdrup balance is
βv=ρ0​H(∇h​×τ)z​​=−ρ0​H1​dydτs​​.​
(21)
Small Rossby number and slow forcing help justify these conditions in the interior; they do not justify them inside an intense western boundary current. For fluctuations, the mean eddy contribution to relative-vorticity advection must also be negligible.
The depth-integrated northward volume flux per unit zonal distance is V=Hv. For a basin 0<x<L and wind stress independent of x, the northward interior volume flux is
T(y)=∫0L​Hvdx=−βρ0​L​dydτs​​.​
(22)
A negative value denotes southward transport. The cancellation of H is why this interior volume flux is insensitive to the chosen constant depth. In a closed, steady basin the net volume flux across a full latitude section is zero: a boundary return must compensate T. Thus the displayed formula cannot represent the total closed-basin transport including that return.
For a concrete circulation sketch, use a northern-hemisphere subtropical basin 0<y<B and
τs​(y)=−τ0​cos(πy/B),V(y)=−βρ0​Bπτ0​​sin(πy/B).
(23)
This represents westward trade winds at the southern edge and eastward westerlies at the northern edge. The negative wind stress curl drives southward interior flow. Define an ocean transport streamfunction by Hu=−Ψy​, Hv=Ψx​ and impose no normal flow at the eastern wall through Ψ(L,y)=0. Integrating Sverdrup balance westward gives
ΨI​=(x−L)V(y),HuI​=(L−x)Vy​(y),HvI​=V(y).
(24)
Since V=0 at y=0,B, this also gives no normal flow at the north and south walls. Interior water moves westward in the southern part, southward across the basin, and eastward in the northern part. A northward western boundary current completes the clockwise ocean gyre.
Pure Sverdrup balance cannot satisfy the western impermeability condition as well: ΨI​(0,y)=−LV(y) varies along that wall. Indeed imposing no normal flow on both east and west walls would require their constant streamfunction values to agree in this simply connected closed basin, whereas their difference here is the nonzero T(y). The shaded return in the sketch represents a boundary region where the interior approximation breaks down. It is supplied by friction in 2(c), rather than inferred from Sverdrup balance alone. A southern-hemisphere subtropical ocean gyre has the reflected, anticlockwise circulation.
For an order-of-magnitude estimate, use τ0​=0.1 Nm−2, ρ0​=103 kgm−3, β=2×10−11 m−1s−1, L=5×106 m and a wind stress variation scale ℓτ​=106 m. Then
∣T∣∼βρ0​ℓτ​Lτ0​​=2.5×107 m3s−1=25 Sv.​
(25)
Here 1 Sv=106 m3s−1 is a Sverdrup. The sinusoidal example has maximum ∣τs,y​∣=πτ0​/B; taking B=π×106 m makes it agree with the chosen variation scale. Other reasonable basin widths and wind profiles change the coefficient, but retain a transport of order tens of Sverdrups.

Let Vb​ be the northward speed in the western boundary current, whose zonal width δ is much less than its meridional scale. Its dominant relative vorticity is ξ≃vx​∼Vb​/δ. Neglecting local wind stress curl and relative-vorticity advection in the narrow return region, the linear stationary vorticity equation becomes
βv≃−rξ≃−rvx​.
(26)
Consequently
δ∼βr​,v(x,y)∝e−x/δ.​
(27)
This decay into the interior from the western wall explains why the Stommel boundary layer lies on the west. The sign also matters: a northward return has vx​<0, so its negative relative vorticity supplies a positive drag contribution −rξ to balance βv. Here r has units s−1; no extra factor of f0​ belongs in r/β with the paper's definition of linear drag.
For example, at fixed y the leading correction to the interior ocean transport streamfunction is
ΨB​=LV(y)e−x/δ,HvB​=∂x​ΨB​=−δLV(y)​e−x/δ.
(28)
It cancels the western value of ΨI​ and carries ∫0∞​HvB​dx=−LV=−T. Exponentially small eastern-wall corrections and the turning regions near the northern and southern walls do not affect the leading width estimate.
Using one year =3.156×107 s, the representative β of 2(b), and H=103 m gives
r=3.17×10−8 s−1,δ≃1.58×103 m=1.6 km.​
(29)
To return the 25 Sv interior volume flux, conservation of mass requires
Vb​∼Hδ∣T∣​≃15.8 ms−1.​
(30)
For the exponential profile this is the boundary speed associated with its effective transport width δ. It is much narrower and faster than the familiar large-scale western boundary currents, whose energetic cores span tens of kilometres and have speeds of order 1 ms−1. The simplified constant-depth linear drag model with a one-year decay time therefore does not give a realistic quantitative boundary structure. Lateral viscosity, eddies, stratification and inertia can all affect a real current.
The corresponding relative vorticity scale is
∣ξ∣∼δVb​​≃9.96×10−3 s−1,∣f0​∣∣ξ∣​∼100for ∣f0​∣∼10−4 s−1.​
(31)
Thus relative vorticity cannot be discarded relative to planetary vorticity in this predicted return. Even a more realistic 1 ms−1 across 50 km gives ∣ξ∣∼2×10−5 s−1, an appreciable fraction of ∣f0​∣.
Magnitude alone does not prove that its material advection is large: a perfectly parallel current v=v(x), u=0 has u⋅∇ξ=0 exactly. For a gyre return varying over meridional scale B, however, incompressibility gives u∼δVb​/B, and both generic contributions to u⋅∇ξ scale as Vb2​/(Bδ). Relative to βVb​, this is
βVb​∣u⋅∇ξ∣​∼βBδVb​​≃1.6×102(B=π×106 m).
(32)
Absent special cancellations, the assumed neglect of relative-vorticity advection is therefore not self-consistent for these parameters. Its natural inertial width is instead δI​∼VI​/β​, where VI​=∣T∣/(HL)≃5×10−3 ms−1; this gives about 16 km. That comparison diagnoses the missing inertia rather than supplying a complete nonlinear boundary-current solution.
