Treat pn​(t) as the probability of the complete coarse-grained state n at time t, with pn​≥0 and ∑n​pn​=1. In the column-vector convention of the master equation, Wnm​ for n=m is the transition rate from m to n. The short-time transition law is
P(n,t+dt∣m,t)=δnm​+Wnm​dt+o(dt).
(1)
Nonnegative transition probabilities and conservation of total probability require
Wnm​≥0 (n=m),n∑​Wnm​=0,Wmm​=−n=m∑​Wnm​≤0.​
(2)
For a finite state space these conditions define a continuous-time Markov chain. The Markov property means that, conditional on the present state, no further history affects the transition probabilities. A time-independent W additionally assumes time-homogeneous rates; the waiting time in a state is exponential, and multiple jumps in an infinitesimal interval contribute beyond first order. A reduced description that omits relevant slow variables can retain memory and need not obey this closed equation. Quantum coherences are likewise absent from this classical population model. For infinite state spaces one also needs conditions preventing infinitely many jumps in finite time. Neither equal forward/backward rates nor detailed balance is required merely to conserve probability.

Use states 0,A,B, where 0 denotes an empty molecule and the other states denote its single occupied site. The source arrows give 0→A at rate f1​, A→0 at rate f4​, A→B at rate f2​, and B→A at rate f3​. Thus

Each diagonal entry is the negative total escape rate from its column's state. In particular, the empty state cannot make a direct jump to B: the reservoir touches A alone. The negative sign of the exchange term and J>0 make this a Heisenberg ferromagnet. Each classical bond minimizes its energy when its two fixed-length spins are parallel. On the connected cubic lattice all ground states are therefore uniform:
Sj​=Sn,∣n∣=1,​
(11)
using length S for the classical large-spin convention. Any two orientations are related by a common spatial rotation of every spin. The global spin-rotation symmetry is SO(3), and a chosen direction retains its SO(2) subgroup of rotations about that axis.
Spontaneous symmetry breaking means selecting one member of this continuous set in the thermodynamic limit, for example by taking volume to infinity before sending an aligning field to zero. The zero-field Hamiltonian operator remains rotationally invariant even though the selected state is not. There are gapless transverse spin waves. Although two rotation generators are broken, an isotropic ferromagnet has one quadratic magnon branch, whose two transverse spin components form a conjugate dynamical pair. It is not necessary to invent two independent linear-dispersion branches from the number of broken generators. The long-wavelength dispersion is derived in d.

For an analytic, local Landau theory with independent global rotations of the magnetization vector, a uniform scalar potential can depend only on ρ=m⋅m. Keeping terms through fourth order in the field, dropping an additive constant, and retaining the leading isotropic positive gradient term gives the displayed quadratic-plus-quartic functional. The stiffness has been absorbed into the normalization of length and field. Higher powers and higher derivatives are neglected in this long-wavelength approximation.
The quartic truncation is stable at large ∣m∣ when u>0. The coefficient t changes sign at the mean-field critical point; close to it one commonly writes t∝T−Tc​. If u<0, higher stabilizing powers are essential, so the displayed truncation alone cannot determine a stable first-order phase diagram. For u=0 and t<0 it is likewise unbounded.
For u>0, spatially uniform stationary points obey (t+4uρ)m=0. The global minima are
m=0(t≥0),∣m∣2=−4ut​(t<0).​
(21)
The corresponding minimum free-energy density is zero and −t2/(16u), respectively. The disordered phase gives way continuously to a ferromagnet with an arbitrary direction of magnetization. At this mean-field transition the amplitude vanishes as (−t)1/2; long-wavelength critical fluctuations can modify mean-field exponents in three dimensions.[ref]

Assume u>0, as required for the stable quartic model, and retain the stated domain t>0. For the opposite-axis quadratic anisotropy of a vector order parameter, fix the magnitude ρ. Then the anisotropy lowers the energy most by putting all magnetization along component 1 if r<0 and component 2 if r>0. Component 3 has positive quadratic coefficient t and cannot lower the uniform minimum. Minimizing the remaining amplitude gives
regionr<−t−t≤r≤tr>t​uniform equilibrium magnetization(±−(t+r)/(4u)​,0,0)(0,0,0)(0,±(r−t)/(4u)​,0)​fmin​−(t+r)2/(16u)0−(t−r)2/(16u)​​​

To check stability, in the component-1 minimum the longitudinal curvature is −2(t+r)>0, while the two transverse curvatures are −2r>0 and −r>0. The component-2 case similarly has longitudinal curvature −2(t−r) and transverse curvatures 2r,r, all positive in its region. The disordered state is stable exactly when t±r≥0.
Both boundaries r=−t and r=t are continuous, second-order mean-field transitions: the selected component vanishes with a square root, and the first derivatives of the minimum free energy agree across the boundary. In the stipulated upper half-plane t>0, the two ordered phases are separated by a disordered wedge. There is no direct first-order boundary between them in that domain. Extrapolating a phase boundary through t<0 would answer a different, larger phase-diagram question.
