Saponification of triglycerides

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Hydrolysis Saponification
OurBigBook.com Words: 200
A triglyceride contains three ester linkages joining glycerol to fatty acid-derived acyl groups. Complete saponification therefore consumes three hydroxide equivalents per triglyceride, yielding one glycerol and three fatty acid carboxylates.[ref]
Writing the three hydrocarbon groups separately allows their compositions to differ:
​C3​H5​[OC(=O)R1​][OC(=O)R2​][OC(=O)R3​]+3OH−⟶C3​H5​(OH)3​+R1​COO−+R2​COO−+R3​COO−.​
(36)
This is the preceding ester chemical equation applied three times: each linkage supplies one carboxylate, and each formerly esterified oxygen on the glycerol scaffold gains one hydrogen. The two sides retain the same three Ri​ groups and have net electric charge −3.
Soap consists of sodium or potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids. Its carboxylate head is hydrophilic, while its long hydrocarbon tail is hydrophobic. This makes it an amphiphile and a surfactant: the tails can associate with grease while the heads remain exposed to water, helping disperse grease for rinsing. An acetate ion is also a carboxylate, but lacks the long hydrophobic tail that gives soap this cleaning role.[ref]
