Thermometer

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Temperature
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 5
A thermometer measures temperature through a physical response whose relation to temperature is known. Examples include the length change from thermal expansion, a change in electrical resistance, and the pressure of a gas thermometer. If the response function X=f(T) is an injective function over the working range, its measured value determines T=f−1(X) through the inverse function. Thermometer calibration establishes that relation for the particular instrument and measurement conditions. Two matching readings are meaningful only after calibration to a common scale and with the probes sampling comparable equilibrium states. [ref]
For a contact thermometer, the sensing part must reach thermal equilibrium with the region being measured. That region may differ from the rest of the object: an immersed probe near a heater can read a local temperature while the surrounding water remains cooler. A stable reading can also be a steady state set by competing heat transfer paths to the sample and the room. Good contact, adequate immersion, mixing where appropriate, and checks for spatial differences help establish which temperature the reading represents. These are measurement controls inferred from the thermal equilibrium requirement, not a guarantee that every flat trace is equilibrium.
A thermometer also changes the sample it touches. Consider an original toy calculation with an initially uniform sample at To​=350K, a probe at Tp​=300K, and constant whole-body heat capacities Co​=100JK−1 and Cp​=1JK−1. Suppose together they form an isolated system, with no work or phase transition and negligible contact energy. The first law of thermodynamics and thermal equilibrium give
Tf​=Co​+Cp​Co​To​+Cp​Tp​​=349.50495…K,Tf​−To​=Co​+Cp​Cp​​(Tp​−To​).
(79)
The final thermometer reading underestimates the sample's original temperature by about 0.495K, although it correctly measures their new common temperature. The disturbance becomes small when the participating probe heat capacity is small compared with the sample's. A large controlled heat bath is a different limit: it supplies or removes heat while approximately maintaining its own temperature.
Waiting time is a separate issue from disturbance. Under a toy Newton's law of cooling model for a uniform probe in a maintained heat bath, a constant contact conductance G gives
Cp​dtdTp​​=G(Tb​−Tp​),Tp​(t)=Tb​+[Tp​(0)−Tb​]e−t/τ,τ=GCp​​.
(80)
The time constant τ controls this exponential decay. For stipulated values Tb​=350K, Tp​(0)=300K and τ=5s, the probe reads about 331.61K after one time constant and 347.51K after three. These are calculated values, not an instrument specification or measured data. Real probes may have multiple relaxation times and additional heat transfer paths; choose an equilibration criterion from the required accuracy, rather than assuming that a fixed waiting time works universally.
