Isotope shift of a linear rotor

Changing an isotope changes a mass and therefore the moment of inertia. If the bond length is held fixed, B∝1/μ and every corresponding transition shifts by the same fractional amount. This leading inverse-reduced mass scaling is also used in the more complete diatomic formulation.[ref]
For a deliberately stipulated carbon monoxide example, take m12​=12u, m13​=13u, m16​=16u, u=1.66053906660×10−27kg, r=1.13×10−10m for both isotopic forms, and h=6.62607015×10−34Js. The integer masses are simplifying inputs, not accurate isotopic atomic masses; the shared bond length is an imposed model assumption. Calculation gives
μ12,16​μ13,16​​=748​u,=29208​u,​I12,16​I13,16​​≈1.45395×10−46kgm2,≈1.52080×10−46kgm2,​B12,16​B13,16​​≈57.7188GHz,≈55.1817GHz.​

The heavier form's calculated J=0→1 line is 110.363GHz rather than 115.438GHz, a decrease of about 4.396%. These are model predictions from the stated inputs, not published measured frequencies. An isotope changes the spacing as well as the first line; the shift is not a fixed additive offset for the whole spectrum.
