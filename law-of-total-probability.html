Law of total probability (P(A)=∑i​P(A∣Bi​)P(Bi​))

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability space Probability measure
OurBigBook.com Words: 861 Articles: 1
Let (Bi​) be a countable measurable partition of the sample space, with positive probabilities. For any event A, the law of total probability gives
P(A)=∑i​P(A∣Bi​)P(Bi​).
(17)
The pieces A∩Bi​ are disjoint events, so add their masses and use the definition of conditional probability. Zero-mass partition pieces can be omitted without changing the answer.
For the coin selection in probability, let B mean the 3/4-head coin was selected and let H mean the first toss was heads. Then P(H)=P(H∣B)P(B)+P(H∣Bc)P(Bc)=11/20. Each conditional answer describes one possible hidden situation; its weight describes how often that situation occurs under the model. This decomposition is useful whenever a simple conditional model sits behind a more complicated marginal probability distribution.
The law of total probability combines local conditional probabilities into one overall probability. The measurable partition must cover the sample space with pairwise disjoint events: every elementary outcome belongs to exactly one part. Its probabilities are the weights of a weighted mean, and they sum to one.[ref]
For an original constructed example, make a finite set of 1,000 items: 600 belong to B1​ and 400 to B2​. Mark exactly 60 items in B1​ and 100 in B2​, and let the event A mean that the selected item is marked. Select an item using the uniform distribution on a finite set. These are prescribed counts, so the resulting probabilities are exact properties of this construction:
P(B1​)P(B2​)​=1000600​=0.6,=1000400​=0.4,​P(A∣B1​)P(A∣B2​)​=60060​=0.1,=400100​=0.25.​
(18)
The conditional probability 0.1 uses only the 600 items in B1​ as its denominator. The probability of the intersection A∩B1​ uses all 1,000 items instead: 0.6×0.1=0.06. Similarly, P(A∩B2​)=0.4×0.25=0.10. Adding the two disjoint events gives
P(A)=0.6(0.1)+0.4(0.25)=0.06+0.10=0.16=100060+100​.
(19)
An unweighted arithmetic mean of the two conditional probabilities would give (0.1+0.25)/2=0.175. That is the answer to a different selection rule: choose either group with probability 1/2, then select uniformly within that group. Here the first group contains more items, so selecting uniformly among all items gives it weight 0.6. In fact, if its weight were w, the overall probability would be 0.1w+0.25(1−w)=0.25−0.15w. Moving the weight from 1/2 to 0.6 lowers the result by 0.015, explaining exactly why 0.175 is too large for the original model.
The same construction is a mixture distribution: first select a group with its mixture weight, then select an item within that group. Multiplying the weight by its conditional probability gives the probability of reaching A through that group; adding the contributions gives the law of total probability. For example, an item in B1​ has selection probability 0.6/600=1/1000, and an item in B2​ has selection probability 0.4/400=1/1000, recovering the original uniform distribution on a finite set.
No assumption of independent events is needed. In this construction P(A∣B1​)=0.1 differs from P(A)=0.16, so A and B1​ are not independent events. Also B1​ and B2​ are disjoint events with positive probabilities, so they cannot be independent events: their intersection has probability zero, whereas P(B1​)P(B2​)=0.24. The measurable partition supplies exclusive alternatives, not an independence assumption.
For a short proof, the intersection pieces A∩Bi​ have union A and are pairwise disjoint events. Apply countable additivity, then substitute the definition of conditional probability on every positive-mass part:
P(A)=∑i​P(A∩Bi​)=∑i:P(Bi​)>0​P(A∣Bi​)P(Bi​).
(20)
A zero-probability event Bi​ has P(A∩Bi​)=0. Omit it: writing P(A∣Bi​) would divide by zero. Countability ensures that the union of the omitted zero-probability events also has probability zero. Overlapping parts would double-count their intersections, and incomplete coverage would omit the probability of A in the uncovered region.[ref]
Reversing the question gives one compact application of Bayes' theorem. Among the 160 marked items, 100 belong to B2​, so its conditional probability after observing A is
P(B2​∣A)=P(A)P(A∩B2​)​=0.160.10​=160100​=0.625.
(21)
The law of total probability supplied the denominator by collecting every way that A can occur. Its usefulness lies in choosing a measurable partition where the conditional probabilities are simple, then combining them without losing the relative sizes of the alternatives.
