Stellar parallax (p, ϖ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics
OurBigBook.com Words: 576 Articles: 4
Stellar parallax is the apparent change in a star's viewing direction caused by the observer's changing position. Nearby stars shift more against a distant reference background. In an ideal circular Earth orbit, the conventional annual stellar parallax p uses a baseline of one astronomical unit, approximately the Earth-Sun distance. Two opposite observing positions, about six months apart, are separated by approximately two astronomical units. Their maximum angular separation is 2p, so p is half that excursion, not the full January-to-July shift. The observed excursion depends on the projected baseline and observing dates. [ref].

Thus p=0.05 arcseconds implies d≃20 parsecs, while the ideal six-month excursion is 0.10 arcseconds. Geometry supplies this distance without assuming a luminosity or a particular stellar atmosphere.
In practical astrometry, many observations separate the annual stellar parallax signal from proper motion, the star's own changing sky position. The Gaia DR3 catalogue reports stellar parallax and its uncertainty in milliarcseconds: 50mas=0.05 arcseconds. A catalogue angle is a fitted measurement with measurement error. [ref].
For a positive stellar parallax with small standard deviation σp​, first-order propagation gives σd​/d≃σp​/p. Thus p=50mas with σp​=1mas gives d≃20 parsecs with σd​≃0.4 parsecs, assuming systematic offsets are negligible. This linear estimate requires σp​≪p; a reciprocal is nonlinear and does not generally preserve symmetric uncertainty intervals.
