Biological cell

 Home Biology
OurBigBook.com Words: 28k Articles: 511
A biological cell is a membrane-bounded basic unit of living organization. In a cell-level experiment it can be an experimental unit, provided that treatment assignment and relevant interactions are handled at that level.
The shared architecture of cellular life combines a selective cell membrane, internal cytoplasm, inherited information in DNA, and ribosomes that assemble proteins. Metabolism supplies transformations of matter and energy needed for maintenance and growth. These components form a coupled system: a boundary retains useful molecules, enzymes organize chemical reactions, and inherited information supports rebuilding the machinery. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: prokaryotic cells.
A worked structural comparison: in a typical prokaryote, DNA occupies a region without a surrounding cell nucleus; in a typical nucleated eukaryote, much of the DNA lies inside a membrane-bounded cell nucleus. Both still have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes. The extra boundary compartmentalizes processes; it does not create a different basic principle of living organization. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: eukaryotic cells.
Shared architecture describes cellular life and its developmental machinery, rather than requiring every specialized biological cell to retain every component. A mature mammalian red blood cell, for example, lacks a cell nucleus, although it develops from nucleated precursors. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: components of blood. This distinction makes cellular unity compatible with striking specialization.

Lysosomal enzyme latency

Words: 581 Articles: 1
Lysosome OurBigBook.com
Lysosomal enzyme latency is the difference between the enzyme activity accessible to an added substrate before disruption of the surrounding membrane and the activity revealed after disruption. Low accessible activity can reflect separation of enzyme from substrate, rather than absence of enzyme. This is an assay-accessibility statement, not a claim that the intact lysosome is inactive in the living biological cell.[ref]
Let F be the blank-corrected initial rate measured with a membrane-preserving preparation and T>0 the rate after a treatment that exposes all assayable enzyme. Use matched amounts of starting material, pH, substrate and measurement time. Define the apparent latent fraction by
ℓ=1−TF​.
(7)
This definition requires more than a large treated signal. Disruption must preserve enzyme activity, and the treatment must not independently alter the readout. A soluble-enzyme comparison with and without treatment helps test direct activation or inhibition; an appropriate blank tests signal unrelated to enzyme-catalysed hydrolysis. These are controls for interpreting the constructed assay, rather than additional historical observations.
For a deliberately chosen example, take F=10 and T=100 arbitrary rate units. Then ℓ=0.90: ninety percent of the assayable activity was initially inaccessible. After hypothetical partial damage, F=25 with unchanged T=100 gives ℓ=0.75. The preparation has lost latency without gaining total assayable activity. These percentages describe activity accessibility; they do not count intact lysosomes. Unequal enzyme contents, partial leakage and mixed particle populations prevent that identification. If F>T, the formula is negative, flagging failure of this simple accessibility interpretation rather than a negative population of hidden enzymes.
To check the logic, divide the same uniformly mixed preparation into two equal portions. If dilution halves both measured rates, (F/2)/(T/2)=F/T, so ℓ is unchanged. In contrast, comparing an undiluted untreated portion with a diluted treated portion changes the ratio artificially. Normalization to the same amount of original material is therefore part of the definition, not an optional numerical correction.

Ribosome

A ribosome is the molecular machinery for biological translation: it reads messenger RNA and joins amino acids into a polypeptide that can become a protein. Both prokaryotes and eukaryotes have ribosomes. A ribosome consists of ribosomal RNA and proteins organized into small and large subunits, with no enclosing membrane. Ribosomal RNA contributes to both structure and the active site that forms peptide bonds; a ribosome is therefore not merely a collection of proteins. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 4.3: ribosome composition and Nissen et al., The structural basis of ribosome activity in peptide bond synthesis, Science 289, 920–930 (2000).
For a computed decoding example, choose the reading frame starting at the first displayed nucleotide of 5′-AUG GCU GAA-3′. Under the standard genetic code, AUG specifies methionine (Met), GCU specifies alanine (Ala), and GAA specifies a glutamic acid residue (Glu) in the growing polypeptide. Nine nucleotides divide into three codons, giving the ordered three-amino acid residue segment Met–Ala–Glu. The ribosome reads the messenger RNA 5′→3′, and the chain grows from its N-terminus toward its C-terminus. If synthesis begins at this AUG in an appropriate eukaryotic initiation context, these are the first three amino acid residues before any subsequent processing. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 15.1: standard codon assignments.
For this three-amino acid residue segment, joining the three delivered amino acids requires two peptide bonds, one between Met and Ala and another between Ala and Glu. There is no stop codon in the displayed nucleotide sequence, so this is a growing segment rather than a complete released protein. An isolated nine-nucleotide RNA is not claimed to supply all signals needed for initiation. This small calculation separates three jobs: the messenger RNA specifies order, transfer RNA delivers the amino acids, and the ribosome catalyzes their joining. Each new amino acid residue is added at the C-terminus. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 15.5: initiation and chain elongation.
The beauty of a ribosome is its reusable chemistry: the same machinery can assemble many different proteins by following different messenger RNA templates. The genetic code connects an alphabet of nucleotides to an alphabet of amino acids, while transfer RNA links recognition of the message to delivery of building material. Changing the message can therefore change the product without building a different ribosome for every protein.
In 1955 George E. Palade described small particles in the cytoplasm, both free and associated with membranes, in A small particulate component of the cytoplasm, Journal of Biophysical and Biochemical Cytology 1, 59–68. The paper documents the structural observations, rather than the full modern decoding mechanism. Complementary biochemical work by Hoagland, Stephenson, Scott, Hecht and Zamecnik described a soluble RNA intermediate in protein synthesis in 1958, helping establish the role now assigned to transfer RNA; see A soluble ribonucleic acid intermediate in protein synthesis, Journal of Biological Chemistry 231, 241–257. Together, structural observations and biochemical experiments connected visible cellular machinery with its working parts.
