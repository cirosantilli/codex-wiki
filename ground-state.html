Ground state

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 8
A ground state is a normalized energy eigenstate that attains the lowest possible energy of a quantum system. Precisely, let the Hamiltonian operator H be a self-adjoint operator bounded below on a Hilbert space, and write E∗​=infσ(H) for the infimum of its spectrum. A ground state is a unit vector ψ0​ in the operator domain D(H) satisfying
Hψ0​=E∗​ψ0​,∥ψ0​∥=1.
(52)
Thus a ground state exists exactly when the spectral bottom E∗​ is an energy eigenvalue. The lowest energy eigenvalue among some listed bound states is insufficient if other parts of the spectrum lie below it. The ground-state subspace collects all minimizing energy eigenstates; it can have ground-state degeneracy. The quantum variational principle characterizes E∗​ as the infimum of normalized energy expectation values. [ref].
For the one-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator, the ground state has energy ℏω/2 above the minimum of the potential energy. This zero-point energy is compatible with being the lowest allowed energy: a localized wavefunction cannot simultaneously have zero kinetic energy. For a free particle on the whole line, the spectral bottom is zero but is unattained by any normalizable wavefunction; see ground-state energy. A lower bound therefore need not describe a physically preparable energy eigenstate.
The ground state supplies a reference for excitation costs and for the quantum variational principle. A ground state is also a stationary state under a time-independent Hamiltonian operator: its quantum phase changes, while its position probability density stays fixed. A nonzero ground-state energy does not imply motion along a classical trajectory, and ground-state degeneracy means there need not be a unique lowest quantum state.
