Planck's law (Bν​(T), Bλ​(T))

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 13
Planck's law gives the spectral radiance of an ideal blackbody in thermal equilibrium at absolute temperature T>0. It predicts how much power is carried in each small frequency or wavelength interval, per projected area and per solid angle. The whole curve is determined by T: no material-specific parameter enters this ideal Planck function.
Writing ν for frequency in hertz and λ for wavelength in metres, the two forms of the Planck function are
Bν​(T)=c22hν3​ehν/(kB​T)−11​,Bλ​(T)=λ52hc2​ehc/(λkB​T)−11​.
(69)
Here h is the Planck constant, kB​ the Boltzmann constant, and c the speed of light in vacuum. Their exact values in the International System of Units are h=6.62607015×10−34Js, kB​=1.380649×10−23JK−1, and c=299792458ms−1. [ref]
The units matter: Bν​ is measured in Wm−2sr−1Hz−1, whereas Bλ​ is measured in Wm−2sr−1m−1. Both are spectral radiance, rather than radiant exitance or energy density. To report Bλ​ per micrometre, multiply its per-metre value by 10−6; a micrometre-wide interval is 10−6 metres wide.
For a computed example, at T=6000K and λ=0.500μm, the exponent is hc/(λkB​T)≈4.79592. Substitution into Planck's law gives Bλ​≈3.17569×1013Wm−2sr−1m−1, or 3.17569×107Wm−2sr−1 per micrometre. At the same wavelength, the illustrative temperatures 3000K and 4500K give 2.60268×105 and 6.37793×106 in the latter units. These are ideal predictions for stipulated temperatures.
The key change of variables is ν=c/λ. Corresponding positive intervals carry the same power, so Bλ​dλ=Bν​∣dν∣ and
Bλ​(T)=Bν​(T)​ν=c/λ​λ2c​.
(70)
Equal-width wavelength intervals have unequal widths in frequency; the factor c/λ2 compensates for this. Merely replacing the horizontal-axis labels of Bν​ by c/ν does not produce Bλ​. This explains the frequency and wavelength peaks of Planck's law.
A short derivation uses the Boltzmann distribution for a single electromagnetic mode with thermal energies nhν, n=0,1,…. Put q=e−hν/(kB​T). The geometric series gives Z=∑n≥0​qn=(1−q)−1 and mean thermal energy hνq/(1−q). The electromagnetic mode density in a box, including both independent polarization of an electromagnetic wave states of a photon, is 8πν2/c3 per volume per unit frequency. Multiplication gives spectral energy density uν​=8πhν3/[c3(ehν/(kB​T)−1)]; isotropy gives Bν​=cuν​/(4π). This counts thermal excitation energy, excluding zero-point energy. [ref]
Planck's law connects quantum mechanics to a measurable radiation curve. Its small-hν/(kB​T) limit is the Rayleigh-Jeans law, Bν​≈2kB​Tν2/c2; its large-hν/(kB​T) limit is the Wien approximation, Bν​≈(2hν3/c2)e−hν/(kB​T). The suppression at high frequency makes the total energy density finite. Integrating the curve gives the Stefan–Boltzmann law, while locating its peak gives Wien's displacement law. Thus one formula predicts both the spectral shape and the total emission.
