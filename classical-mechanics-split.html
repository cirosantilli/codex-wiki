Classical mechanics

 Home Physics Branches of physics
OurBigBook.com Words: 82k Articles: 1k
Classical mechanics describes the motion of bodies by assigning positions and velocities and specifying how their interactions change those quantities. A point mass is the simplest model: its size and internal structure are ignored, leaving a mass and a moving position. A rigid body retains orientation and rotational kinetic energy. Choosing a model means deciding which features affect the question being asked; a sliding block may be treated as a point mass, whereas predicting its rolling without slipping requires its moment of inertia.
Three complementary questions organize the subject. Newton's second law asks how the net force determines the instantaneous acceleration. The work-energy theorem asks how that force, acting through a displacement, changes kinetic energy. Conservation of mechanical energy asks which combinations of position and velocity are accessible when the interactions admit a time-independent potential energy and no other force supplies net power. These descriptions are compatible, but answer different questions: an energy calculation may find a maximum speed without first solving for position at every time.
The harmonic oscillator below makes this unity visible. A restoring force produces a repeating position trace; the corresponding phase plane has a closed ellipse; kinetic energy and potential energy exchange while their sum stays fixed. Lagrangian mechanics and Hamiltonian mechanics provide further ways to organize the same dynamics, especially when coordinates or constraints make individual forces awkward to track.
The scope of classical mechanics also matters. Newtonian mechanics approximates motion at speeds small compared with the speed of light; special relativity changes that description when this approximation fails. Quantum mechanics is needed for phenomena for which a classical assignment of simultaneous position and momentum is inadequate. These limitations do not prevent classical mechanics from being a useful predictive model within its regime. [ref]
