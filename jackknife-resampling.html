Jackknife resampling

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Statistical inference
OurBigBook.com Words: 645 Articles: 2
The delete-one form of jackknife resampling recalculates a statistic after deleting each observation in turn. It uses one observed statistical sample to investigate how sensitive an estimator is to its individual observations, and supplies both a jackknife variance estimator and jackknife bias correction. The calculations are deterministic once the data are fixed: no random draws are needed.
In the basic setting, X1​,…,Xn​ are independent and identically distributed random variables, with sample size n>1. Let x1​,…,xn​ be the observed values and θn​=tn​(x1​,…,xn​) a scalar estimator of an estimand θ. Apply the same estimation rule to each reduced statistical sample, using its new sample size n−1 wherever the rule requires it:
θ(−i)​=tn−1​(x1​,…,xi−1​,xi+1​,…,xn​),i=1,…,n,θJ​=n1​∑i=1n​θ(−i)​.
(1)
Every index is deleted exactly once; an unusual value is treated by the same rule as every other value. The mean θJ​ summarizes the deletion estimates. They overlap in n−2 observations, so they are generally not independent random variables representing fresh repetitions of the original experiment. Cosma Shalizi's [ref] explain this construction and its scaling.
For a concrete sample mean, use the data (1,2,3,4,10), whose full sample mean is x=4. The five deletion sample means, in observation order, are
(θ(−1)​,…,θ(−5)​)=(4.75,4.5,4.25,4,2.5),θJ​=4.
(2)
Deleting 1 raises the sample mean by 0.75, while deleting 10 lowers the sample mean by 1.5. Thus 10 has the largest deletion effect in this statistical sample. This is a descriptive sensitivity calculation, not a rule for removing that observation: the full sample mean remains 4.
Early literature includes M. H. Quenouille's [ref] and John W. Tukey's one-paragraph abstract [ref], within the issue's collected abstracts. Shalizi's [ref] discuss the later development of jackknife bias correction and the jackknife variance estimator.
