Stark effect

 Home Physics Mathematical physics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 12
An electric field shifts and can split the energy eigenvalues of an atom. This is the Stark effect; its hydrogen atom ground-state case is quadratic in a weak field.
The observable in Stark effect spectroscopy is a change in a spectral line, rather than a direct reading of an individual energy eigenvalue. If an upper energy eigenstate u emits a photon into a lower energy eigenstate l, the Planck constant h relates its frequency to the difference:
hν(E)=Eu​(E)−El​(E),hΔν=ΔEu​−ΔEl​.
(35)
Here E denotes the applied electric field component along a chosen axis. Both levels can move, and electric-dipole selection rules for hydrogen decide which pairs produce observable spectral lines. This makes Stark effect spectroscopy a probe of energy eigenstates and their electric dipole moments: the slope −∂E/∂E is the corresponding dipole component by the Hellmann–Feynman theorem. The comparison with Zeeman splitting is useful: there an applied magnetic field supplies the perturbation.
