Rabi molecular beam magnetic resonance experiment

OurBigBook.com Words: 587 Articles: 1
In 1938, Isidor Isaac Rabi, Jerrold Zacharias, Sidney Millman and Polykarp Kusch introduced molecular-beam magnetic resonance. Their 1939 account describes a heated oven, vacuum slits, inhomogeneous A/B magnetic fields with opposite gradients, and a uniform static C magnetic field. Conductors supplied transverse oscillatory radiofrequency (RF) fields. Transitions spoiled refocusing, reducing the tungsten-filament detector signal. Alignment compared A-only and B-only attenuation; C-field sweeps at fixed RF frequency limited oscillator pickup. Matching frequency/field ratios across compounds checked nuclear assignments. Magnetic field calibration limited absolute magnetic moments; weak intervening fields caused unwanted transitions.[ref]
The readout has a useful logical structure: A and the apertures admit trajectories correlated with a magnetic moment projection; B tests whether that projection has survived C. The two deflectors compensate for unchanged states, whereas a changed projection experiences a different force in B. A dip followed by recovery as the static magnetic field passes through resonance is therefore a population-sensitive signal. A comparison with RF absent or far from resonance is the natural baseline in this functional model; RF pickup, drift or background loss must not be mistaken for a state transition. A dip alone does not establish a pure initial quantum state or resolve an entire time-domain Rabi cycle.
This beam method measures transmitted particles after state-dependent deflection. The later bulk nuclear magnetic resonance methods of Felix Bloch and Edward Mills Purcell instead use a macroscopic sample and detect nuclear induction or RF absorption; NMR spectroscopy builds on that bulk setting. The common resonant coupling does not make their preparations or readouts interchangeable.[ref][ref]
