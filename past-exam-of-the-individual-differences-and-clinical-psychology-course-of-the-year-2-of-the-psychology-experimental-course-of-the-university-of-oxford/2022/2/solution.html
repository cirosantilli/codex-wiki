For an adult with established obsessive-compulsive disorder, the core psychological recommendation is cognitive behavioral therapy including exposure and response prevention; an SSRI is another established first-line option, with choice and combination guided by impairment, preference, previous response and clinical assessment. There is no single justified prescription from the diagnostic label alone. A recommendation should connect the maintaining process to an intervention and then examine randomized evidence, tolerability and generalizability.
Assessment first distinguishes recurrent obsessions and compulsions from ordinary unwanted thoughts, practical precautions and other causes of repetitive behavior. Compulsions may be covert mental reviewing or neutralizing rather than visible washing or checking. Relevant information includes time consumed, distress, interference, avoidance, insight, concurrent depression, current risk, physical consequences, family responses and previous treatment. A baseline measure such as the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale can track severity, but neither a score nor the content of an intrusive thought replaces an assessment of its meaning and associated behavior.
Paul Salkovskis's cognitive-behavioral formulation distinguishes an intrusion from its appraisal and the responses that can maintain it. An unwanted thought is interpreted as unusually significant or as creating inflated responsibility for preventing harm. Ritualizing, reassurance or avoidance briefly reduces distress. This negative reinforcement makes repetition more likely and prevents learning that the intrusion can pass without neutralization. Attempting perfect certainty can generate still more checking. The target is therefore the response to the intrusion and its appraisal, not a requirement to eliminate all unwanted thoughts before normal life can resume.[ref]
In exposure and response prevention, therapist and patient agree a hierarchy of relevant situations and feared predictions. Exposure brings the person into contact with an appropriate trigger; response prevention means refraining from the ritual, including covert neutralization and reassurance that would perform the same function. Tasks may be in vivo or, where appropriate, imaginal. For checking, the learning target is tolerating ordinary uncertainty without repeated checking, rather than obtaining an absolute guarantee that nothing bad can happen. Tasks are collaborative and concern ordinary, acceptable uncertainty; genuine hazards are not deliberately created.
The rationale includes both learning that distress can change without ritualizing and testing whether compulsions are necessary. Habituation can occur, but a reduction in fear within every session is not the sole criterion of learning. An inhibitory learning interpretation emphasizes a new association between the cue and a nonritualized outcome that competes with the old threat expectation. Varying situations and planning practice between sessions can support retrieval of that learning outside the therapy room. Cognitive procedures address overestimation of threat, responsibility and the assumed significance of thoughts; they should facilitate behavioral learning rather than become endless reassurance.[ref]
Randomized evidence supports this emphasis. Edna B. Foa and colleagues compared intensive exposure and ritual prevention, clomipramine, their combination and pill placebo in a trial with 122 adult entrants. All active conditions outperformed placebo, and exposure-based treatment performed better than clomipramine alone. Combining clomipramine with exposure did not clearly outperform exposure alone. This supports an effective psychological treatment, but does not establish that adding any medication is always useless: the comparison involved a particular drug, intensive expert-delivered exposure, and a particular treatment schedule. Medication could be blinded against placebo; psychological treatment could not be concealed from participants and therapists.[ref]
An SSRI can be offered when preferred, when psychological treatment is inaccessible or difficult to engage with, or as part of combined care. Medication requires prescribing oversight, discussion of adverse effects, interactions and discontinuation, and a sufficient monitored trial before declaring nonresponse. Its effectiveness does not prove that an individual has a simple serotonin deficiency: changing a signaling system is different from establishing the original cause of a disorder.
The pre-examination NICE framework used stepped care: lower-intensity CBT including ERP for suitable mild impairment, a choice of more intensive CBT including ERP or an SSRI for moderate impairment, and combined CBT/ERP plus an SSRI for severe impairment. Inadequate response warrants review of diagnosis, treatment adequacy, engagement and comorbidity, followed by an agreed change or combination; persistent difficulty calls for specialist multidisciplinary assessment. These are severity-sensitive alternatives, not a demand that every adult start with combined treatment or that severity can be inferred from one alarming obsession.[ref]
For persistent symptoms despite an adequate serotonin-reuptake inhibitor trial, Helen Blair Simpson and colleagues randomized 100 adults to added exposure and ritual prevention, risperidone or placebo while continuing medication. At eight weeks, exposure-based augmentation produced more clinical response and minimal symptoms than either pill condition; risperidone did not outperform placebo in this trial. This is strong evidence to consider adequate ERP before routine antipsychotic augmentation in comparable patients. It is not proof that antipsychotics never help any subgroup, nor evidence that the result automatically generalizes to untreated first presentations.[ref]
Practical delivery matters. Family accommodation can unintentionally maintain rituals; with agreement, carers can support ordinary activities without supplying repeated reassurance or completing compulsions. Outcomes should include functioning, valued activities and distress as well as symptom scores. Nonresponse should prompt examination of whether exposure actually occurred and whether subtle rituals continued, rather than blaming the person. A relapse plan identifies early warning signs and maintains independent practice. The best-supported recommendation is accessible, competent CBT/ERP, with an SSRI or combined treatment when indicated by assessment and preference, and structured review rather than automatic escalation.
