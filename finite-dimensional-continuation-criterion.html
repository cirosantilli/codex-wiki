Finite-dimensional continuation criterion (T+​<∞ ⟹ escape from every compact subset of U)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Differential equation Ordinary differential equation Picard-Lindelöf theorem
OurBigBook.com Words: 455
Under the Picard-Lindelöf theorem's hypotheses on U⊂R×Rd, extend a solution of a differential equation as far as possible, to a maximal interval (T−​,T+​). If T+​<∞, the time-state trajectory (t,y(t)) must eventually leave every compact subset of U. Equivalently, confinement near T+​ to one compact subset of U permits continuation past T+​. See Teschl, Section 2.6, Lemma 2.14 and Corollaries 2.15–2.16, pp. 52–53.
The domain restriction matters even for a bounded trajectory. Restrict y′=1, y(0)=0, to U=R×(−1,1). Then y(t)=t exists in this domain only for −1<t<1; at t=1/2,3/4,9/10 its values are 1/2,3/4,9/10. As t↑1, it approaches the excluded boundary point (1,1) while remaining bounded. Its trajectory cannot fit into any compact subset of U near that endpoint. This is escape from a finite domain, without finite-time blowup in norm. Enlarging the domain to R×R gives the same formula y(t)=t for every real t.
Conversely, y′=y, y(0)=1, stays in the compact set [0,T]×[1,eT] for every fixed T>0. Its growing exponential function is unbounded over the entire future, but has no finite-time blowup. At T=2, its largest value on [0,2] is only e2≈7.38905610. The relevant question is boundedness before each finite endpoint, rather than one bound valid forever. The tangent function in the diagram under the Picard-Lindelöf theorem illustrates the other possibility: its norm escapes every bound before T=π/2.
This criterion is useful because an estimate can secure continuation without an explicit formula. In a finite-dimensional vector space, a bounded closed ball is compact; a bounded state trajectory therefore supplies the needed compact set when its time-state closure is inside the domain. That final proviso prevents mistaking a domain boundary for finite-time blowup.
A common sufficient estimate comes from global Lipschitz continuity in the state. Assume U=I×Rd for an open time interval I, with f continuous and a Lipschitz constant L≥0 valid for all states on each compact time interval under consideration. For [t0​,T]⊂I, let B=supt0​≤s≤T​∥f(s,0)∥, finite by continuity. Then ∥f(t,y)∥≤B+L∥y∥, and the Gronwall inequality gives, for t0​≤t≤T wherever the solution already exists,
∥y(t)∥≤⎩⎨⎧​∥y0​∥eL(t−t0​)+LB​(eL(t−t0​)−1),∥y0​∥+B(t−t0​),​L>0,L=0.​
(52)
For instance, y′=y+1, y(0)=0, has L=B=1 and the exact solution y(t)=et−1, attaining this estimate; at t=1 the value is e−1≈1.71828183. Finite bounds of this kind give continuation throughout I, with the analogous estimate backwards in time. When I=R, this gives existence for all real times. If I has a finite endpoint, it gives no continuation beyond that prescribed domain. The underlying linear-growth criterion is [ref].
A brief explanation of the continuation mechanism suffices: within the protecting compact set, the continuous function f is bounded, say by M. The integral equation yields ∥y(t)−y(s)∥≤M∣t−s∣ near the finite endpoint, so y(t) has a limit y∗​. The protecting compact set puts (T+​,y∗​) inside U. Restarting the initial value problem there and using local uniqueness joins the trajectories.
