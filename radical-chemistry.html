Radical (chemistry)

 Home Chemistry
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 9
A radical has one or more unpaired Electrons. In a simple atomic orbital or molecular orbital occupancy picture, an unpaired Electron occupies a spatial orbital without an opposite-spin partner in that orbital. A dot such as H∙ marks this electronic property; it does not mean a missing Electron or positive electric charge. Free radicals can be neutral or charged. Terminology also has a boundary: metal ions exhibiting paramagnetism are not normally called free radicals just because they have unpaired Electrons.[ref]
Compare three species containing the same single proton. The hydrogen atom H∙ has one Electron in its 1s atomic orbital, and is a neutral radical. The hydrogen cation H+ has no Electrons, so no unpaired Electron. The hydrogen anion H− has two Electrons paired in its 1s atomic orbital. It is charged but is not a radical in its ground state. With Np​ protons, Ne​ Electrons, and positive elementary charge e, the independent electric charge count is
q=(Np​−Ne​)e;H+:q=+e,H∙:q=0,H−:q=−e.
(1)
The electron pair and electric charge questions require different information. An odd Ne​ ensures that not all Electrons can be paired; an even Ne​ alone does not ensure pairing, as the molecular orbital description of molecular oxygen illustrates.
The distinction matters whenever a chemical reaction mechanism uses dots and charge signs together. Homolysis gives one bonding Electron to each fragment, while heterolysis assigns both to one fragment. A radical chain reaction can pass an unpaired Electron from one species to another without generating net electric charge. Detection then exploits the unpaired Electron's spin and magnetic moment, rather than merely measuring whether the sample carries an electric charge.
