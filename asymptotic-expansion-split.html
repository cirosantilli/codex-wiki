Asymptotic expansion (f(z)∼∑k≥0​ak​ϕk​(z))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 61
An asymptotic expansion describes a function near a specified limit by successively smaller scales. Each fixed finite truncation has a remainder smaller than its last retained scale. The central question is how the approximation improves as the parameter approaches the limit; convergence of the entire infinite series at a fixed parameter is a separate question. For the inverse-power scale 1,z−1,z−2,…,
f(z)∼∑k=0∞​ak​z−k(z→+∞)
(1)
means that, for every fixed nonnegative integer m,
f(z)−∑k=0m​ak​z−k=o(z−m).
(2)
The little o notation makes the accuracy requirement precise. With expansions to all orders, this is also equivalent to an O(z−m−1) remainder after the mth term; the implied bound may depend on m.[ref]
For a quick convergent example, the finite geometric series identity gives
1+z−11​=1−z1​+z21​−⋯+zm(−1)m​+1+z−1(−1)m+1z−m−1​.
(3)
For z>1, both the asymptotic expansion as z→+∞ and the ordinary convergent geometric series describe this function. The exact remainder displays the required extra inverse power.
The more surprising example under a divergent factorial series can approximate a convergent integral has a convergent integral but a divergent series at every fixed positive z:
F(z)=∫0∞​1+t/ze−t​dt∼1−z1​+z22!​−z33!​+⋯.
(4)
A few terms can estimate F(10) well, even though adding sufficiently many terms eventually destroys the approximation. Optimal truncation explains where to stop.
This makes asymptotic expansions useful when an exact expression is a complicated integral or differential equation solution, but a parameter is very large or very small. The leading term reveals the scale; corrections reveal which effects matter next. The asymptotic sequence must be specified: integer powers, fractional powers, logarithms, and exponential functions can encode different kinds of behavior. A good finite approximation includes a remainder estimate or an independently checked range of use.
