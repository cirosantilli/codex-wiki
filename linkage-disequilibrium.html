Linkage disequilibrium (D=P(AB)−P(A)P(B))

 Home Biology Genetics Population genetics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 5
Linkage disequilibrium means that knowing an allele at one genetic locus changes the probability of an allele at another genetic locus in population haplotypes. It compares their joint probability distribution with the product of their marginal distributions. This is useful because a genetic marker can carry information about another genetic locus: tag SNPs exploit this redundancy, without establishing a causal genetic association.
Consider two genetic loci with alleles A,a and B,b. Sample one haplotype, rather than an unphased diploid genotype. Write its four haplotype frequencies as fAB​,fAb​,faB​,fab​, summing to one, and set the marginal allele frequencies to p=fAB​+fAb​ and q=fAB​+faB​. The linkage disequilibrium coefficient is
D=fAB​−pq=fAB​fab​−fAb​faB​.
(3)
The second expression follows by substituting fab​=1−fAB​−fAb​−faB​. The entire two-locus joint probability distribution is then
Aatotal​Bpq+D(1−p)q−Dq​bp(1−q)−D(1−p)(1−q)+D1−q​totalp1−p1​​
(4)
Every row and column sum is unchanged when D varies. Linkage equilibrium means D=0: all four haplotype frequencies equal products of allele frequencies, so the two allele indicators are independent random variables. For p>0, the conditional probability P(B∣A)=q+D/p shows directly how positive D enriches B among copies carrying A, while negative D depletes it. Positive D also enriches ab; negative D enriches Ab and aB. Swapping the labels A and a reverses the sign of D, so neither sign is intrinsically favorable.
For example, fix the allele frequencies at p=0.60,q=0.40. At linkage equilibrium, the haplotype frequencies in the order AB,Ab,aB,ab are (0.24,0.36,0.16,0.24). Positive linkage disequilibrium D=0.12 instead gives (0.36,0.24,0.04,0.36), with P(B∣A)=0.60; negative linkage disequilibrium D=−0.12 gives (0.12,0.48,0.28,0.12), with P(B∣A)=0.20. Counting each allele separately would miss the difference.
Nonnegative haplotype frequencies constrain D. Define the nonnegative bounds L=min(pq,(1−p)(1−q)) and U=min(p(1−q),(1−p)q). The four entries above give
−L≤D≤U,max(0,p+q−1)≤fAB​≤min(p,q).
(5)
Thus ∣D∣≤1/4 globally, but a particular pair of allele frequencies usually permits a smaller, asymmetric interval. The standardized linkage disequilibrium convention used here retains the sign:
D′=⎩⎨⎧​D/U0D/L​D>0,D=0,D<0,​−1≤D′≤1.
(6)
This convention requires 0<p,q<1; if either genetic locus has only one allele in the population, D=0 but normalized measures are undefined. Some presentations report only ∣D′∣. At D′=1 or −1, at least one of the four haplotypes has zero frequency; this alone does not prove that genetic recombination has never occurred.
For the indicator random variables X=1A​ and Y=1B​, their covariance is D, and their variances are p(1−p) and q(1−q). The allelic correlation measure is their squared correlation coefficient,
rLD2​=p(1−p)q(1−q)D2​,0≤rLD2​≤p(1−p)q(1−q)max(L2,U2)​≤1.
(7)
The subscript distinguishes it from the recombination fraction r in linkage-disequilibrium decay under recombination. Squaring loses the association's sign. In the illustrated positive and negative examples, rLD2​=0.25 in both, whereas D′=0.75 and −0.50, respectively. For another original example, take p=0.10,q=0.50,fAB​=0.10: then D=0.05,D′=1, yet rLD2​=1/9. Every A carries B, but most copies carrying B lack A. The frequency constraints and the distinction between these normalizations are analyzed in Mathematical properties of the r² measure of linkage disequilibrium by Jenna M. VanLiere and others (sections 2.1 and 2.5, pp. 131–132 and 135–136).
Genetic linkage describes transmission through meiosis; linkage disequilibrium describes a population's haplotype frequencies. Nearby genetic loci on a chromosome can be in linkage equilibrium. Conversely, mixing populations can create linkage disequilibrium even between genetic loci on different chromosomes. For example, mix equal numbers of independently sampled haplotypes from two populations, each internally at linkage equilibrium, with p=q=0.80 in one and p=q=0.20 in the other. The pooled allele frequencies are p=q=0.50, but fAB​=(0.64+0.04)/2=0.34, giving D=0.09. This is a consequence of population stratification, not a measurement of the recombination fraction.
Measured haplotype frequencies also depend on the sample. Finite sample size, related individuals, genetic-study ascertainment, genotyping error and uncertain haplotype estimation can change an estimate. A missing rare haplotype can give empirical ∣D′∣=1 without demonstrating its absence from the population. Report the sample definition and allele frequencies alongside the association measure; a nonzero sample estimate alone does not establish nonzero population linkage disequilibrium.
A concrete dataset is the 2005 A haplotype map of the human genome by The International HapMap Consortium. The International HapMap Consortium used genotyping platforms including Illumina BeadArray to obtain SNP genotype calls from 269 DNA samples across four collections: 30 parent–offspring trios from Ibadan, 30 trios from Utah, 45 unrelated individuals from Beijing and 44 from Tokyo. Parent–offspring trios and the PHASE algorithm supplied haplotype estimation, rather than direct observation of every chromosome. In Figure 7, 36 SNPs resolve seven haplotypes in a chromosome 2 region in the Utah panel; seven tag SNPs capture the 36 SNPs at rLD2​≥0.8. The readout demonstrates reusable allelic correlation, not causal effects.
Controls required over 80% complete calls, at most one discordance among five duplicate genotypes, at most one Mendelian segregation error per trio panel, and a Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium test with P>0.001; blind reassessment checked performance. Minor allele frequencies of at least 0.05 were targeted, the collections were not population-representative, and inferred haplotypes retain phase uncertainty. These limits restrict extrapolation of linkage disequilibrium.[ref]
