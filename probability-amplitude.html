Probability amplitude (aj​=⟨j∣ψ⟩)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics Wave function
OurBigBook.com Words: 977 Articles: 6
A probability amplitude is a complex number that describes the contribution of a pure state to a specified measurement outcome. For a normalized quantum state ∣ψ⟩ and a rank-one outcome associated with a normalized vector ∣j⟩, it is the inner product aj​=⟨j∣ψ⟩. The Born rule gives the corresponding probability pj​=∣aj​∣2=aj∗​aj​, using the complex conjugate. Thus an amplitude can be negative or complex, while a probability is real and nonnegative. A wavefunction is the analogous position amplitude: its squared modulus gives a probability density, which must be integrated over a region to obtain a probability. [ref].
The extra information is the quantum phase. If a=reiθ, its modulus r fixes ∣a∣2=r2, but its angle θ controls how it adds to another contribution. In the complex plane, picture amplitudes as arrows: add the arrows, then square the resulting length. For coherent contributions A and B to the same outcome,
∣A+B∣2=∣A∣2+∣B∣2+2Re(A∗B).
(96)
The last term is interference. Adding probabilities first loses it. Contributions with different relative quantum phases can reinforce or cancel even when their individual squared moduli are fixed.
For the qubit quantum superposition ∣ψϕ​⟩=(3​/2)∣0⟩+(eiϕ/2)∣1⟩, project onto the plus vector of the Hadamard basis. By linearity, the two contributions combine as
⟨+∣ψϕ​⟩=A22​3​​​​+B22​eiϕ​​​.
(97)
Here ∣A∣2=3/8 and ∣B∣2=1/8, while the interference term is (3​/4)cosϕ. At ϕ=0, the arrows align and the plus probability is 1/2+3​/4≃0.9330127. At ϕ=π, they oppose and that probability is 1/2−3​/4≃0.0669873; cancellation is incomplete because the lengths differ. At ϕ=π/2, the arrows are perpendicular and the cross term vanishes. The minus amplitude uses A−B, reversing the cross term. These are alternate projective measurements on identically prepared copies, rather than a sequence of measurements on one copy.
A global phase multiplies every contribution by the same factor eiγ, giving
∣⟨j∣eiγψ⟩∣2=∣eiγ⟨j∣ψ⟩∣2=∣⟨j∣ψ⟩∣2.
(98)
It therefore changes no outcome probability. Changing a relative quantum phase can change the angle between contributions and hence their interference. Keeping amplitudes until the last step makes quantum circuits and matter-wave interference calculable; discarding their angles prematurely discards physically testable information. The Born rule example compares two measurement bases explicitly.
