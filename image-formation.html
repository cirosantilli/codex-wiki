Image formation

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 7
Image formation assigns each object point a corresponding image point: outgoing light rays from that object point either converge there, giving a real image, or diverge as though they originated there, giving a virtual image. The requirement applies separately to each point. Bringing unrelated light rays together makes a bright patch, but does not preserve the object's pattern. This point-by-point correspondence explains how an optical camera records a scene. [ref].
Use the same convention as thin lens: place the optical lens at x=0, with incident light travelling to the right and a real object at x=−u, u>0. Write the image coordinate as x=v, so v>0 for a real image and v<0 for a virtual image. Heights ho​,hi​ are positive above the optical axis and negative below it. In air, within the paraxial approximation, a thin lens obeys
u1​+v1​=f1​,v=u−ffu​,m=ho​hi​​=−uv​.
(33)
Here f is the signed focal length, positive for a converging optical lens and negative for a diverging optical lens; the expression for v assumes u=f. Object distance u is positive even though the object's coordinate is negative. These coordinate rules describe transmission through the optical lens; a mirror reverses the outgoing direction, so do not identify its image distance with this same rightward coordinate. [ref].
The beauty of geometrical optics is that two convenient light rays can locate a point, while a whole admitted bundle forms its image. For a converging thin lens, an incident light ray parallel to the optical axis exits through the rear optical focus; a light ray through the centre continues undeviated. Real optical lens elements do not focus every admitted light ray exactly: optical aberrations and diffraction limit the point correspondence.
