Electrolysis

 Home Chemistry Electrochemistry
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 8
Electrolysis uses an external source of electrical work to drive redox chemical reactions at the electrodes of an electrolytic cell. Its characteristic application is forcing a chemical reaction in a direction that is nonspontaneous under the specified conditions: a discharging galvanic cell supplies electrical work, whereas electrolysis consumes electrical work. This makes electrolysis useful for producing otherwise difficult-to-obtain chemical substances, charging a rechargeable battery, and depositing a metal coating on a conducting object.[ref]
Always identify the anode by oxidation and the cathode by reduction. In ordinary driven two-terminal operation, the source withdraws electrons from the positive anode and delivers electrons to the negative cathode. In a discharging galvanic cell, the anode is negative and the cathode positive. Thus terminal signs change with operating mode, while the reaction definitions remain valid. Through the electrolyte, ions transport electric charge; they need not all be consumed at an electrode. The electrolyte and electrolytic cell articles explain this transport and its conventional current direction.
For a net chemical equation transferring z electrons per reaction event, the molar reaction Gibbs free energy obeys
Δr​G=−zFEchem​,
(6)
where F is the Faraday constant and Echem​ is the reversible cell electromotive force for the reaction in the written direction. A nonspontaneous direction has Δr​G>0 and Echem​<0 at fixed temperature and pressure. The ideal opposing voltage magnitude is Δr​G/(zF); sustaining a finite electric current generally needs additional drive because of electrical resistance and finite electrode reaction rates. This thermodynamic voltage concerns the actual composition, rather than necessarily standard conditions.[ref] Not every use of electrolysis increases bulk chemical Gibbs free energy: transferring copper between equivalent surfaces can have zero bulk Δr​G while the source still supplies dissipated electrical work. See the existing electrolytic cell example.
A particularly revealing experiment measures copper transfer instead of merely looking for a coating. Two separated copper electrodes stand in aqueous CuSO4​ electrolyte in a vessel, with insulated leads to a regulated direct-current power supply, a series ammeter and a switch. A balance reads each electrode's mass before and after a timed run. The Institute of Physics describes copper cells with ammeters, adjustable current and removable cathodes; its comparison uses three similar cells at 1A for ten minutes, 0.5A for ten minutes, and 0.5A for twenty minutes. The specified observation to test is that deposited copper mass is proportional to electric current times time. These are documented experimental settings, not measurements generated here.[ref]
The ideal half-reactions are
anode, oxidation:cathode, reduction:​Cu(s)⟶Cu2+(aq)+2e−,Cu2+(aq)+2e−⟶Cu(s).​
(7)
Each deposited copper chemical atom requires two electrons, so z=2. The anode loses copper and the cathode gains it; ideally their mass changes have equal magnitudes, and the bulk copper cation inventory stays constant. Local concentrations near the electrodes can nevertheless differ. The sulfate anions participate in ionic transport without appearing in these net half-reactions; electronic electric current in the wires becomes ionic electric current in the electrolyte through the two interfaces.[ref]
Clean, rinse, dry and identify the electrodes before their initial weighing. After interrupting the electric current, rinse away adhering electrolyte, dry again and weigh without rubbing off the deposit. KIT's university practical explicitly calls for distilled-water rinsing, careful drying and weighing both electrodes; it warns that loose copper grains can be lost during handling.[ref] Useful additional controls are repeated drying and weighing until the reading stabilizes, and a matched unpowered immersion followed by the same rinse/dry cycle. These controls test retained liquid, residual dissolved salts, handling loss and chemical changes unrelated to passed electric charge; they are proposed checks, not additional observations reported by KIT. Keep immersed electrode area, spacing, electrolyte composition and temperature comparable across runs, and keep the electrodes from touching.
Record the electric current as a function of time, together with the exact switch-on and switch-off times. Taking I(t)≥0 in the chosen deposition direction, the transported electric charge is
Q=∫t0​t1​​I(t)dt.
(8)
For constant electric current, Q=I(t1​−t0​); with sampled readings, use numerical integration rather than assuming that one ammeter reading remained constant. The two equal-charge Institute of Physics settings each give 600C, while its shorter 0.5A setting gives 300C. With MCu​=63.546gmol−1, the mass per amount of substance tabulated by KIT, and F≈96485.33212Cmol−1, ideal Faraday's laws of electrolysis predict
mCu​=2FMCu​Q​≈{0.09879g,0.19758g,​Q=300C,Q=600C.​
(9)
These are calculated predictions for complete conversion of the measured electric charge into retained copper, not experimental results. For a stable process, doubling the electric charge doubles the deposited mass; equal electric charge gives equal deposited mass even when the electric current and duration differ.
The balance reading is a test of the whole model. Competing reduction of water or other chemical species can divert electric current from copper deposition; transient electrode capacitance can also consume part of a short pulse without producing copper. A fragile deposit can detach even when its formation used the expected electric charge. Conversely, retained electrolyte or salts can increase an apparent mass gain. Comparing anode loss with cathode gain helps detect such problems but does not identify every cause. A slope below MCu​/(2F) alone cannot distinguish competing chemical reactions from lost deposit. Current efficiency measures the fraction of transported electric charge assigned to a specified electrode reaction, rather than the fraction of electrical energy stored chemically.[ref]
The beautiful connection is that an electrical measurement counts chemical events: integrating electric current and reading a balance provide two ways of following the same electron stoichiometry. This makes electrolysis useful for controlling coating quantity and for testing a charge-to-amount of substance relation. It does not determine coating uniformity, adhesion or the required voltage, which depend on transport and electrode chemical kinetics as well.
