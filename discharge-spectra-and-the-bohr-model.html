Discharge spectra and the Bohr model

 Home Physics Mathematical physics Hydrogen atom Bohr model
OurBigBook.com Words: 328
In On the Constitution of Atoms and Molecules, Part I (1913), pp. 8–9, Niels Bohr interprets existing hydrogen discharge and astronomical spectra. Electrical discharge in a vacuum tube removes electrons; recombination supplies spectral lines. This is a theoretical interpretation, without a reported spectrograph of his own.[ref]
An actual optical obstacle appears in Theodore Lyman's measurements: hydrogen discharge light entered an evacuated spectrograph directly through a slit, avoiding a transmitting window. A concave reflection diffraction grating dispersed the light onto photographic film. Pumping preserved the vacuum; a second slit supplied a known spectral displacement for wavelength calibration. Gas contamination and vacuum leaks limited the measurement. This apparatus resolved ultraviolet spectral lines; it did not image orbits.[ref]
For a separate original calibration exercise, suppose an idealized spectrograph returns synthetic vacuum line positions xn​=λn→2​+b for n=3,4,5,6, with chosen additive offset b=0.200nm. Assume its dispersion scale is already known and only this offset is uncertain. Compare the finite-mass prediction with the fixed-nucleus prediction λn(∞)​=(μ/me​)λn→2​. Calibrate each model using only the 3→2 line: b=x3​−λ3model​. Then hold that offset fixed and test the other lines with residuals
dn​=xn​−b−λnmodel​.
(18)
For the finite-mass model every dn​=0. The fixed-nucleus model has b=0.55733nm and residuals d4​=−0.09264nm, d5​=−0.12100nm, d6​=−0.13400nm. Algebraically dn​=(1−μ/me​)(λn​−λ3​): one additive calibration cannot absorb a multiplicative change of the wavelengths. Allowing an unknown multiplicative dispersion factor as well would make the two models degenerate in this exercise, so independent scale calibration matters.
