A distribution is a linear functional on the space of test functions that is continuous in its test-function topology. Equivalently, for every compact K⊂R there are an integer m and a constant CK​ such that ∣⟨T,ϕ⟩∣≤CK​max0≤j≤m​∥ϕ(j)∥∞​ whenever ϕ has support in K.
Linearity of Tn​ follows from linearity of the integral and derivative. Since the integration interval has length 2/n,
∣⟨Tn​,ϕ⟩∣≤2n​∫−1/n1/n​(∣ϕ∣+∣ϕ′∣)dx≤∥ϕ∥∞​+∥ϕ′∥∞​.
(1)
This is a test-function seminorm estimate of order one, valid for every K and even uniformly in n. It proves that each Tn​ is a distribution.

Lx​g(x,ξ)=δ(x−ξ),gx​(0,ξ)+g(0,ξ)=0,g(1,ξ)=0.​

Y′′+(4+λ)Y=0,Y(0)=Y(π)=0.

There is a normalization conflict in the source: the printed Rodrigues' formula gives the standard Generalized Laguerre polynomial Pn​=Ln(m)​ with Pn​(0)=(nn+m​), which is generally not one. The norm requested here corresponds to that Rodrigues normalization. A solution normalized to one at zero is Pn​=Pn​/(nn+m​).
Set w=xme−x and p=xm+1e−x. The equation becomes (pPn′​)′+nwPn​=0. Multiply the equations for Pn​,Pk​ by the opposite polynomial, subtract and integrate. The boundary term p(Pn′​Pk​−Pk′​Pn​) vanishes at zero and infinity, so
(n−k)∫0∞​wPn​Pk​dx=0.
(20)
This proves orthogonality for k=n under either normalization.
For the squared norm, use the printed Rodrigues' formula and integrate by parts n times:
∫0∞​wPn2​dx=n!1​∫0∞​Pn​Dn(xn+me−x)dx=n!(−1)n​∫0∞​Pn(n)​xn+me−xdx.
(21)
All boundary terms vanish: the required derivatives have a positive power of x at zero and exponential decay at infinity. The leading coefficient is (−1)n/n!, so Pn(n)​=(−1)n. The Gamma function integral gives
∫0∞​xme−xPn​(x)2dx=n!(n+m)!​.​
(22)
For the source's alternative unit-at-zero normalization the correct answer instead is
∫0∞​xme−xPn​(x)2dx=n!(nn+m​)2(n+m)!​.​
(23)
Both cannot equal the printed norm when m,n>0. This distinction is the normalization of generalized Laguerre polynomials.

For m=0 the two normalizations agree, and the Laguerre polynomials are orthonormal with weight e−x. Take real −1<h<1. Their expansion coefficients for the requested function are
cn​(h)=1−h1​∫0∞​e−x/(1−h)Ln​(x)dx.
(24)
Substitute Rodrigues' formula and integrate by parts n times. Differentiating e−xh/(1−h) transfers a factor [h/(1−h)]n after the two sign factors cancel. Therefore
cn​(h)=(1−h)n!1​(1−hh​)n∫0∞​xne−x/(1−h)dx=hn​.
(25)
The integral is n!(1−h)n+1 by the Gamma function. Boundary terms vanish throughout this range.
The resulting generating function for Laguerre polynomials is
1−he−xh/(1−h)​=n=0∑∞​hnLn​(x),∣h∣<1.​
(26)
One can also verify convergence and equality directly: Rodrigues' formula gives Ln​(x)=∑j=0n​(jn​)(−x)j/j!, and summing ∑n≥j​(jn​)hn=hj/(1−h)j+1 reduces the double series to the exponential on the left. The absolute-value double series converges for fixed x and ∣h∣<1, justifying the interchange; the formula then also holds for complex h in that disk.

For zero viscosity, the method of characteristics gives u=u0​(a)e−t and x=a+u0​(a)(1−e−t). On the sloping part of the initial profile, u0​(a)=−2a, so x=a(2e−t−1). While this map is increasing, inversion gives
u(x,t)=⎩⎨⎧​2e−t,−2e−t−12e−tx​,0,​x≤1−2e−t,1−2e−t<x≤0,x>0.​​
(27)
The sloping interval shrinks from width one to zero while its height decreases from two to one. Its spatial derivative is −2e−t/(2e−t−1) and diverges as 2e−t−1↓0. Thus the first shock formation by characteristic intersection occurs at
t∗​=log2,x∗​=0.​
(28)
For 0≤t<t∗​ the profile consists of a left plateau, a straight descending ramp ending at zero, and a right zero plateau. At t∗​ the ramp becomes a vertical jump. This is a damped Burgers equation; viscosity smooths that jump into a thin transition.

In the moving stretched coordinates, ∂t​=∂τ​−s′(τ)ε−1∂ξ​ and ∂x​=ε−1∂ξ​. Multiplying the damped Burgers equation by ε gives

[2u2​−s′u]−+​=0.​

Assume a classical solution continuous up to the relevant boundary. At an interior positive maximum, ut​=0 and uxx​≤0, hence Lu=ut​−uxx​+u>0, a contradiction. At a positive maximum on the top edge t=τ, away from the spatial endpoints, the left time derivative satisfies ut​≥0, while again uxx​≤0; the same contradiction applies. Thus a positive maximum can occur only on the initial or lateral parabolic boundary.
For two solutions with the same data, their difference w satisfies Lw=0 and vanishes on that parabolic boundary. The weak parabolic maximum principle prohibits a positive maximum of w and also of −w. Therefore w=0, proving at most one solution. The positive reaction term is what gives the immediate strict contradiction at a positive maximum.
