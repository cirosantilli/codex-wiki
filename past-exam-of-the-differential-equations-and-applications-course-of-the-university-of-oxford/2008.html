Use the Poincaré-Lindstedt method. Introduce θ=Ωt and x=x0​(θ)+ϵx1​(θ)+⋯. The leading equation gives ω0​=3 and x0​=A0​cosθ, after fixing the arbitrary phase. At order ϵ,
9(x1′′​+x1​)=6ω1​A0​cosθ+3A0​(A02​−1)sinθ+3A03​sin3θ.
(2)
Here 4cos2θsinθ=sinθ+sin3θ. Periodicity requires the two resonant coefficients to vanish. With A0​>0, this gives
A0​=1,ω0​=3,ω1​=0.​
(3)
Therefore the leading limit cycle is x(t)=cos(3t)+O(ϵ), with frequency 3+O(ϵ2). For example the nonresonant first correction contains −ϵsin(9t)/24; its homogeneous sine/cosine pieces depend on phase and amplitude conventions. The absence of a first-order frequency shift is a solvability condition, not an assumption.

Use the method of multiple scales with independent fast and slow variables t and τ=ϵt, and write x0​=R(τ)cosΘ, Θ=3t+Φ(τ). At the next order, x1,tt​+9x1​=−2x0,tτ​−(4x02​−1)x0,t​. Its resonant terms are

(4)

dτdR0​​=21​R0​(1−R02​),dτdΦ0​​=0.​
(5)

The amplitude decreases from four towards the stable value one, consistent with the selected limit cycle; the zero-amplitude state is unstable. Order-ϵ corrections enforce the exact initial derivative after differentiating the slow amplitude term.

Let L=D2+1 on the mixed endpoint domain y(0)=0, y′(1)=0 in L2(0,1). Integration by parts gives the boundary form
⟨z,Ly⟩−⟨Lz,y⟩=[zy′−z′y]01​.
(6)
At zero, y′(0) is free, forcing z(0)=0; at one, y(1) is free, forcing z′(1)=0. Therefore the formal adjoint expression and full adjoint endpoint conditions coincide with the original ones:
L∗=D2+1,z(0)=0,z′(1)=0.​
(7)
With the usual second-order operator domain this is a self-adjoint problem. The consequences for its eigenvalues and eigenfunctions follow below.

The square-root reduction of a quadratic eikonal equation explains why two initial normal derivatives are possible. Set F=p2+q2−4u. Charpit's equations are x˙=2p, y˙​=2q, u˙=2p2+2q2=8u, p˙​=4p, q˙​=4q. Parametrize the initial semicircle by (x0​,y0​)=(coss,sins), −π/2<s<π/2. Tangential compatibility gives −p0​sins+q0​coss=0, and F=0 gives p02​+q02​=4. Thus there are two normal-gradient choices, p0​=2εcoss, q0​=2εsins, ε=±1.
Integration gives the requested parametric solutions
xyu​=[1+ε(e4t−1)]coss,=[1+ε(e4t−1)]sins,=e8t,p=2εe4tcoss,q=2εe4tsins.​​
(22)
For ε=+1, r=x2+y2​=e4t, so u+​=r2 on the whole right half-plane. For ε=−1, before focusing at the origin, r=2−e4t, so u−​=(2−r)2 on x>0, 0<r<2. The origin is a caustic for this inward family. The circle r=2 is approached only as characteristic time tends to negative infinity, where u and its gradient vanish. It cannot be crossed by this initial characteristic chart. Thus the initial data alone permit two branches; each is locally unique only after its normal-gradient sign is chosen.
On the added half-line write x0​=a≤0, y0​=1, u0​=1. Tangential differentiation gives p0​=0 and q0​=2δ, δ=±1. Its characteristics give
x=a,y=1+δ(e4t−1),u=e8t.​
(23)
The plus branch is u=y2 on x≤0,y>0; the minus branch is u=(2−y)2 on x≤0,y<2. On the positive y-axis, the semicircle traces are y2 and (2−y)2, respectively. Therefore only matching sign choices are continuous throughout their common positive-axis coverage. Mixed choices agree merely at the initial junction y=1.
For the matching-plus branch the data-covered open domain is
D+​={x>0}∪{y>0},u+​={x2+y2,y2,​x>0,x≤0, y>0.​​
(24)
For the matching-minus branch it is
D−​={x>0, x2+y2<4}∪{x<0, y<2}∪{x=0, 0<y<2}.​
(25)
Its formula is (2−x2+y2​)2 in the right portion and (2−y)2 in the left portion. The origin is excluded. The negative y-axis must also be a cut: the two formulas have different traces there, so including it would destroy continuity. Both matching solutions are C1 across the included positive-axis seam; generally their second transverse derivatives differ, which is compatible with this first-order problem. Values beyond these characteristic charts can sometimes be continued, but are not selected by the initial data through a nonsingular characteristic graph.

With A invertible, write the system as ux​+Muy​=d, M=A−1B. Real distinct eigenvalues of M give strict hyperbolicity; a real complete eigenbasis gives the usual diagonalizable hyperbolic form. The characteristic curves satisfy dy/dx=λi​. Left eigenvectors form characteristic dependent combinations wi​=ℓi​u; for constant coefficients they obey separate transport equations. Characteristic coordinates are functions constant on those curves, such as y−λi​x in the constant case. For variable coefficients extra derivatives of the eigenvectors can couple the combinations; local characteristic directions still follow the real matrix pencil. If A is singular one first chooses a noncharacteristic coordinate direction with an invertible coefficient combination.
For the specified constants,
M=(5−6​3−4​),d=(3−2​).
(26)
Its speeds are 2 and −1, with left eigenvectors (2,1) and (1,1). Hence
w+​=2u+v,(w+​)x​+2(w+​)y​=4,w−​=u+v,(w−​)x​−(w−​)y​=1.
(27)
The transported Riemann invariants are R+​=2u+v−4x,R−​=u+v−x​, constant on y−2x=constant and y+x=constant, respectively. Initial data give w+​(0,s)=s−sin(s/2) and w−​(0,s)=−es, so
w+​=y+2x−sin(y/2−x),w−​=−ey+x+x.
(28)
Invert these two combinations:
u=ey+x+y+x−sin(y/2−x),v=−2ey+x−y+sin(y/2−x).​
(29)
Both characteristic feet must lie on the supplied positive initial half-line. Thus
y−2x>0,y+x>0,equivalently y>max(2x,−x).​
(30)
Outside this sector at least one invariant needs unsupplied initial data. The closed expressions give one possible extension there but are not uniquely forced by the question's initial half-line.

For fixed source (ξ,η)∈D, define the Dirichlet Green function by −Δx,y​G=δ(ξ,η)​ in D, G=0 on ∂D. It is harmonic away from the source and has singularity −(2π)−1log(x−ξ)2+(y−η)2​. The outward normal vector derivative is ∂n​G=n⋅∇x,y​G at a boundary point of arc-length parameter s.
Green's second identity gives ∫D​(uΔG−GΔu)=∫∂D​(u∂n​G−G∂n​u)ds. Substituting the two PDEs and the boundary data yields
−u(ξ,η)+∫D​fG=∫∂D​g∂n​G,u(ξ,η)=−∫∂D​g∂n​Gds+∫D​fGdxdy.​
(31)
The singular version follows by excising a small source disk and taking its radius to zero, or directly by the distributional identity.
For the upper half-plane use the opposite-sign image source at (ξ,−η):
G=4π1​log(x−ξ)2+(y−η)2(x−ξ)2+(y+η)2​.​
(32)
The image lies outside the domain, so it is harmonic inside; the true source has the required unit singularity, and the two distances agree at y=0, giving zero boundary value. On that boundary n=−ey​, so
∂n​G=−π[(s−ξ)2+η2]η​.
(33)
The assumed far-field decay makes the large semicircle contribution vanish. With f=0, the representation becomes
u(ξ,η)=πη​∫−∞∞​(ξ−s)2+η2g(s)​ds.​
(34)
Finally set s=ξ+ηtanθ, ds=ηsec2θdθ. The Jacobian matrix cancels the denominator, giving
u(ξ,η)=π1​∫−π/2π/2​g(ξ+ηtanθ)dθ.​
(35)
If g is even, the substitution s↦−s in the Poisson integral gives u(−ξ,η)=u(ξ,η). At η=0 this agrees with the continuous even boundary trace.

At a positive interior maximum, ux​=0, uxx​≤0, and ut​≥0 if the point is compared with its past. The PDE instead gives ut​≤−u3<0, a contradiction. Thus a positive global/past-strip maximum must come from the spatial boundary or initial-time limit. The far value is positive, so any attained global maximum is positive; the argument does not prohibit a negative local maximum in isolation.
For uniqueness put w=u1​−u2​. It satisfies
wt​=wxx​−2tx​wx​−aw,a=u12​+u1​u2​+u22​≥0.
(36)
The parabolic comparison principle with zero difference data gives w≤0 and then −w≤0, hence w=0. As usual on this noncompact singular-time domain, this uses an admissible comparison class in which the initial and far-field limits justify truncation; for example bounded/subquadratic differences on finite past strips with the corresponding uniform zero traces suffice. A barrier δ(1+x2+2t) controls spatial infinity, since ∂t​−∂x2​+[x/(2t)]∂x​+a applied to it is nonnegative. The printed fixed-point limits alone should not silently be substituted for all uniform/growth hypotheses of this comparison argument.
Use drift cancellation in a parabolic similarity reduction. For the similarity ansatz, derivative scaling gives
ut​=t−3/2(−f/2−βηf′),uxx​=t−1/2−2βf′′,−2tx​ux​=−t−3/2ηf′/2.
(37)
Thus β=1/2​, the drift terms cancel, and f′′=f3−f/2 with f(∞)=1/2​. Use the positive-equilibrium separatrix for a cubic profile equation. Multiply by f′ and use the limiting equilibrium to determine the integration constant:
21​(f′)2=41​f4−41​f2+161​,(f′)2=21​(f2−1/2)2.
(38)
The branch approaching the positive far equilibrium satisfies f′=(1/2−f2)/2​; the constant equilibrium is included as well. Separation gives
f(η)=2​1​1+Ae−η1−Ae−η​,u(x,t)=2t​1​1+Ae−x/t​1−Ae−x/t​​.​
(39)
For a finite boundary value at x=0 and no interior pole, take A>−1. Values −1<A<0 describe the corresponding coth-type approach from above; A=0 is constant, and positive A gives the tanh-type branch. The other first-integral sign cannot approach the positive equilibrium nontrivially as η→∞.
The requested deficit integral is, after x=t​η,
∫0∞​(1/2t​−u)dx=2​∫0∞​1+Ae−ηAe−η​dη=2​log(1+A).
(40)
Therefore A=1​, and the selected solution is u(x,t)=(2t)−1/2tanh[x/(2t​)]​.
