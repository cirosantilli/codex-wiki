A distribution is a continuous linear functional on the space of test functions D(R)=Cc∞​(R). A precise useful form of continuity is: for every compact set K⊂R there exist an integer m≥0 and a constant CK​ such that
∣⟨T,ϕ⟩∣≤CK​0≤j≤mmax​x∈Ksup​∣ϕ(j)(x)∣when suppϕ⊂K.​
(6)
Equivalently, if a sequence of test functions has supports in one fixed compact set and every derivative tends uniformly to zero, then its images under T tend to zero. Keeping a common compact support is part of the test function topology; mere pointwise convergence is insufficient.

Set t=logx, so 0<t<π, and write y(x)=x−2w(t). Direct differentiation of this Cauchy-Euler equation gives
x2y′′+5xy′+y=x−2(w′′−3w).
(10)
The eigenvalue equation is therefore −w′′=(λ−3)w with w(0)=w(π)=0. For λ−3≤0, the linear/hyperbolic solutions cannot satisfy both endpoint conditions except trivially. For λ−3>0, the sine solution vanishes at both endpoints exactly when its frequency is a positive integer. Thus
λk​=k2+3,yk​(x)=x−2sin(klogx),k=1,2,….​
(11)
Each associated eigenspace is one-dimensional.
The displayed differential operator is not self-adjoint for the unweighted inner product. Let A=x2∂x2​+5x∂x​+1. Integration by parts on compactly supported smooth functions gives its formal adjoint
A∗f=(x2f)′′−(5xf)′+f=x2f′′−xf′−2f,
(12)
which differs from A. One can see an explicit failure of symmetry without boundary terms: take a nonzero real compactly supported smooth f in the interval and g=xf. Then
⟨f,Ag⟩−⟨Af,g⟩=3∫1eπ​x(fg′−f′g)dx=3∫1eπ​xf2dx>0.
(13)
Both test functions obey the Dirichlet endpoint conditions. Changing the sign of A for the eigenproblem does not repair this failure. A different, weighted inner product with weight x3 does give the Sturm-Liouville form x3Ay=(x5y′)′+x3y, explaining why a real discrete spectrum is nevertheless natural.

Use the cosine difference identity to separate the kernel:
k(x,t)=cos2xcos2t+21​sin2xsin2t.
(14)
Thus L has image E=span{c,s}, where c(x)=cos2x, s(x)=sin2x. Their squared norms in L2(0,π) are π/2 and they are orthogonal. Consequently
Lc=(π/2)c,Ls=(π/4)s.

λ=π/2 with eigenfunctions Ac,λ=π/4 with eigenfunctions Bs,A,B=0.​

Every image of L is a linear combination acos2x+bsin2x. The function cosx is not such a combination. Indeed these functions are continuous, so equality almost everywhere would imply equality everywhere; evaluation at the endpoints would require simultaneously a=1 and a=−1. Therefore the forcing is outside the image and there is no solution. Orthogonality to the kernel is the equivalent image test for this self-adjoint finite-rank operator.

Rewrite the equation as (L−I)y=cosx. On E=span{cos2x,sin2x}, the operator L−I has eigenvalues π/2−1 and π/4−1, both nonzero. On E⊥ it equals −I. The orthogonal decomposition L2(0,π)=E⊕E⊥ therefore makes L−I bijective with bounded inverse. In particular there is exactly one solution for this forcing. This is also the finite-rank Fredholm alternative: 1 is not an eigenvalue of L, so adding the identity term removes the image obstruction present in Ly=f.

Normalize the Laguerre differential equation as y′′+P(x)y′+Q(x)y=0, where P(x)=1/x−1 and Q(x)=n/x. At x=0, the products xP=1−x and x2Q=nx are analytic. Thus zero is a regular singular point, with indicial equation
r(r−1)+r=r2=0,r1​=r2​=0.​
(18)
At x=1 the coefficients P,Q are analytic, so it is an ordinary point. No singular-point indicial equation is needed there.
For infinity put z=1/x and Y(z)=y(1/z). Then the equation becomes
Y′′+(z−1+z−2)Y′+nz−3Y=0.
(19)
At z=0, the coefficient z(z−1+z−2)=1+1/z is not analytic. Therefore infinity is an irregular singular point, including when n=0. A terminating polynomial solution does not change the classification of the differential equation.

F(x0​,y0​,u0​,p0​,q0​)=0,p0​x0′​+q0​y0′​=u0′​.​

For a smooth solution, the first equation is ux​+(uv)y​=0. Multiplying it by v and adding u times the second equation gives
(uv)x​+(uv2+21​u2)y​=0.
(37)
Thus the appropriate shallow water equations conservation variables and flux are
W=(uuv​),H(W)=(uvuv2+u2/2​).
(38)
Use this conservative extension to define a weak solution across y=F(x). A jump contributes −F′[W]δ(y−F(x)) to Wx​ and [H]δ(y−F(x)) to Hy​. Cancellation of the singular parts gives the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions
F′(x)([u][uv]​)=([uv][uv2+u2/2]​).​
(39)
Equivalently this follows by integrating each conservation law over a thin space-time control region crossing the discontinuity.
The conservative extension is essential here. Piecewise smoothness by itself does not imply any jump law. Moreover the smooth second equation can also be written vx​+(u+v2/2)y​=0; imposing that different conservation law distributionally would give a different shock condition. Weak conservation laws depend on conserved variables. The requested jump law specifically selects mass u and momentum uv, and this is the extension used below.
