Torricelli's law (v=2gh​)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 3
An ideal fluid issuing from a small hole a vertical depth h below a free surface has speed
v=2gh​.
(39)
Here h is the vertical distance from the free surface to the centre of the orifice, and g is the positive magnitude of gravitational acceleration. The ideal prediction assumes incompressible flow, the same ambient pressure at the free surface and emerging jet, negligible velocity of the reservoir's free surface, and negligible viscous dissipation and surface tension effects. For a falling free surface, a quasi-steady approximation additionally assumes that the local outflow adjusts much faster than h changes. It applies the Bernoulli equation at each instant, rather than claiming that the whole drainage is steady flow.[ref]
A short proof uses a streamline connecting the reservoir's free surface at elevation zs​ to the emerging jet at elevation zo​. With constant mass density ρ, negligible surface speed and no mechanical energy losses, the Bernoulli equation gives
ρpambient​​+gzs​=ρpambient​​+2v2​+gzo​,h=zs​−zo​.
(40)
Equal pressures cancel, leaving v2/2=gh: a unit mass gains the same kinetic energy as a body in free fall through height h. This makes Torricelli's law a useful first estimate before the detailed velocity field is known. At one quarter of the original head, ideal jet speed is one half as large; it is not proportional to head.
Far from the accelerating outlet region, the reservoir has approximately hydrostatic pressure pambient​+ρgh at the outlet's elevation. The emerging jet instead has approximately ambient pressure when surface tension is negligible. Do not assign that bulk hydrostatic pressure to the emerging jet as well as adding the height difference: that would count the available mechanical energy twice. A sealed reservoir, a submerged outlet or significant surface tension changes the pressure conditions and requires a different balance.
Actual volumetric flow rate is commonly represented by a discharge coefficient:
Q=Cd​a2gh​,Cd​=a2gh​Q​,
(41)
where a is the geometric area of the orifice. This definition compares volumetric flow rates, not just speeds. If a nearly uniform jet at its vena contracta has area aj​ and speed vj​, define the two separate ratios Cc​=aj​/a and Cv​=vj​/2gh​. Then Q=aj​vj​ gives Cd​=Cc​Cv​. A contracted jet can consequently have a speed close to 2gh​ while Cd​ is appreciably below one. No particular Cd​ is universal: calibrate it for the opening and operating range.
