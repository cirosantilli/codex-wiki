Three-step model of high harmonic generation

 ... Physics Branches of physics Optics Laser Attosecond physics High harmonic generation
OurBigBook.com Words: 178
For an original classical calculation of the return step, neglect the residual ion's Coulomb force after release. Take a spatially uniform driving electric field E(t)=E0​cosωt, an electron of electric charge −e and mass m, and zero initial position and velocity at ti​. With α=ωti​ and β=ωt, integrating Newton's second law gives
v(t)=−mωeE0​​(sinβ−sinα),x(t)=mω2eE0​​[cosβ−cosα+(β−α)sinα].
(34)
A return requires β>α and x(t)=0. Define the cycle-averaged free-electron quiver kinetic energy
Up​=4mω2e2E02​​,Kreturn​=2Up​(sinβ−sinα)2.
(35)
Searching the first-return solutions for release phases 0<α<π/2 gives a maximum near α=0.3134 and β=4.3990, with Kreturn​≃3.1731Up​. For a chosen ionization energy Ip​, recombination into the initial state can therefore emit a photon with energy up to approximately Ip​+3.17Up​ in this model. This is an upper return-energy scale, not a prediction of the emitted yield. It omits release probabilities, quantum interference, propagation, phase matching and depletion of the neutral gas.
The calculation explains why a long driving wavelength can increase the available return energy: at fixed E0​, Up​ grows as ω−2. It does not say that conversion efficiency improves. The electron must return with a probability amplitude for emission that survives the microscopic dynamics and adds coherently across the gas.
