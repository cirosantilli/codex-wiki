Inner product (⟨⋅,⋅⟩)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 46
An inner product assigns a scalar ⟨u,v⟩ to two vectors and supplies their lengths and orthogonality. Using the convention linear in the first argument, it satisfies
⟨au+bv,w⟩=a⟨u,w⟩+b⟨v,w⟩,⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩​,⟨v,v⟩>0(v=0).
(1)
The resulting norm is ∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩​. Over the real numbers, conjugation has no effect and the form is symmetric and bilinear. Over the complex numbers, it is sesquilinear; the convention linear in the second argument is also common, so formulas for coefficients must match the stated convention.
The Euclidean example is ⟨u,v⟩=∑i​ui​vi​​. For real u=(2,1) and v=(1,3) this gives ⟨u,v⟩=5, while ∥u∥2=5 and ∥v∥2=10. If r=(−1,2), then ⟨r,u⟩=0: perpendicularity becomes an algebraic equation. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives ∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥, and in a real inner product space it makes the angle formula cosθ=⟨u,v⟩/(∥u∥∥v∥) meaningful for nonzero vectors.
Different inner products express different measurements. The weighted example ⟨u,v⟩=4u1​v1​+u2​v2​ on R2 gives ∥(x,y)∥2=4x2+y2, so its unit-length vectors lie on an ellipse. For functions, an integral such as ⟨f,g⟩=∫fg​ supplies the geometry of the L2 space. This lets orthogonal projection and least-squares solutions apply to curves as well as coordinate lists.

Inner product space ((V,⟨⋅,⋅⟩))

Words: 63
Inner product OurBigBook.com
An inner product space is a real or complex vector space equipped with a positive-definite inner product. Over the real numbers the form is symmetric and bilinear; over the complex numbers it is conjugate symmetric and sesquilinear. It defines the norm ∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩​. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality proves the triangle inequality for this norm. A complete inner product space is a Hilbert space; every finite-dimensional inner product space is complete.

Trace orthogonality of symmetric and antisymmetric matrices (tr(SA)=0)

Let S be a real symmetric matrix and A a real antisymmetric matrix of the same size. Invariance of the trace under transpose and the cyclic trace identity give
tr(SA)=tr((SA)T)=tr(ATST)=−tr(AS)=−tr(SA).

Weighted inner product (⟨f,g⟩w​)

Words: 295 Articles: 2
Inner product OurBigBook.com
For a positive weight w, the weighted inner product of functions is ⟨f,g⟩w​=∫f​gw. It is the usual inner product in the L2 space for the measure w(x)dx. A Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion uses this weight to make its eigenfunctions orthogonal.
The weight specifies how different locations contribute to a squared error: ∥f−g∥w2​=∫∣f−g∣2w. More weight makes an error in that region more costly. The weighted Euclidean inner product makes the same choice for a finite list of coordinates.
For an explicit real-function example on [0,1], let w(x)=1+x and approximate f(x)=x by a constant function. The orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace spanned by the function 1 gives
c=⟨1,1⟩w​⟨x,1⟩w​​=∫01​(1+x)dx∫01​x(1+x)dx​=3/25/6​=95​.
(3)
The best unweighted constant is 1/2. Giving greater weight to larger x moves the best constant upward, so a weighting choice changes the approximation even though the allowed family of functions is unchanged.

Weighted Euclidean inner product (⟨u,v⟩M​=uTMv)

Words: 100
Weighted inner product OurBigBook.com
A symmetric positive-definite matrix M defines an inner product on real coordinate vectors by
⟨u,v⟩M​=uTMv,∥u∥M2​=uTMu.
(4)
For diagonal M, positive diagonal entries weight the squared coordinate errors. This is the finite-coordinate version of a weighted inner product; the positive-definite matrix example appears in Francis Narcowich, Inner Products and Norms, standard inner products, example 8.
For M=diag(4,1), the squared norm of (a,b)T is 4a2+b2. A first-coordinate error of one costs as much as a second-coordinate error of two. The unit sphere is the ellipse 4a2+b2=1, and orthogonality means 4u1​v1​+u2​v2​=0. Thus “closest” and “perpendicular” depend on the chosen inner product. The orthogonal projection residual example shows how this changes a best approximation.

Dot product (a⋅b)

Words: 644 Articles: 3
Inner product OurBigBook.com
A dot product measures how much two Euclidean vectors point along each other. This article uses real Euclidean vectors in Rn, with the usual Cartesian basis: multiply matching coordinates and add,
a⋅b=∑i=1n​ai​bi​,∥a∥=a⋅a​.
(5)
The result is a scalar, while ∥a∥ is the Euclidean norm. This real convention has no complex conjugation; for vectors over the complex numbers, use the convention specified in inner product.
For two nonzero Euclidean vectors, the same dot product records their angle 0≤θ≤π through the cosine:
a⋅b=∥a∥∥b∥cosθ,θ=arccos∥a∥∥b∥a⋅b​.
(6)
Here arccos is an inverse trigonometric function. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality guarantees that the normalized ratio lies in [−1,1]. The zero vector has dot product zero with every vector, but its angle is undefined: the ratio would divide by zero. To outline the geometric identity, expand ∥a−b∥2=∥a∥2+∥b∥2−2a⋅b and compare with the law of cosines.[ref]
Take a=(3,4) and c=(2,0). Their Euclidean norms are 5 and 2, and
a⋅c=3(2)+4(0)=6,cosθ=5⋅26​=53​,θ=arccos(3/5)≈53.1301∘.
(7)
The unit vector u=c/∥c∥=(1,0) selects the horizontal direction. The scalar projection a⋅u=3 says how far a reaches along that direction; its vector projection is 3u=(3,0). Thus a=(3,0)+(0,4) splits into an aligned part and an orthogonal projection residual.
For b=(4,−3), the dot product a⋅b=12−12=0 proves orthogonality; both Euclidean norms are 5. For d=(−2,0), a⋅d=−6: the scalar projection onto d/∥d∥=(−1,0) is −3, because a reaches against that chosen direction. Its angle with d is 126.8699∘.
Keeping a=(3,4) fixed and choosing b of Euclidean norm 2 gives a⋅b=10cosθ. At angles 0∘,60∘,90∘,120∘,180∘, the dot products are 10,5,0,−5,−10. Positive values mean partial alignment, zero means orthogonality, and negative values mean a component against the other direction.
This interpretation explains work: for a constant force F and a straight displacement s, W=F⋅s counts the force component along the motion. A sideways force contributes zero work, and an opposing force contributes negative work.[ref]

Orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace

Words: 907 Articles: 5
Inner product OurBigBook.com
A finite-dimensional linear subspace W of an inner product space has a unique closest vector to any given vector x. This is its orthogonal projection PW​x. If e1​,…,ed​ is an orthogonal basis of W, compute it one direction at a time:
PW​x=∑j=1d​⟨ej​,ej​⟩⟨x,ej​⟩​ej​.
(13)
The inner product convention here is linear in its first argument, including over the complex numbers. For an orthonormal basis, each denominator is one. When W consists only of the zero vector, the empty sum gives PW​x=0. The finite-dimensional formula is described in Francis Narcowich, Inner Products and Norms: minimization problems.
Take W=span{(1,2)T} and x=(3,1)T, using the dot product. With e=(1,2)T,
e⋅ex⋅e​=1+43+2​=1,PW​x=(1,2)T,r=x−PW​x=(2,−1)T.
(14)
The orthogonal projection residual has r⋅e=2−2=0. To verify the best approximation directly, every candidate in the line is te, and
∥x−te∥22​=(3−t)2+(1−2t)2=5(t−1)2+5.
(15)
The smallest squared Euclidean norm error is therefore 5, attained only at t=1. The diagram connects the perpendicular residual to this one-variable quadratic polynomial.
The general mechanism is the Pythagorean theorem in an inner-product space. For any w∈W, the vectors r=x−PW​x and PW​x−w are orthogonal, so
∥x−w∥2=∥x−PW​x∥2+∥PW​x−w∥2.
(16)
Moving away from PW​x inside W adds a nonnegative squared norm, which is zero only when w=PW​x. This proves both optimality and uniqueness.
The useful feature is separation of preparation from evaluation. Construct an orthonormal basis once with the Gram-Schmidt process, then obtain each new best approximation using inner products instead of a fresh optimization problem. When W is the column space of a matrix, this gives linear least squares; when W is spanned by orthogonal polynomials, it gives a least-squares polynomial in an orthogonal-polynomial basis. The ambient inner product space need not be a Hilbert space for this finite-dimensional construction. In an infinite-dimensional target linear subspace, existence requires additional hypotheses, supplied by orthogonal decomposition by a closed subspace.

An orthogonal projection residual is the difference r=x−PW​x between a vector and its orthogonal projection onto a linear subspace W. It is orthogonal to every vector in W, so ⟨r,w⟩=0 for all w∈W. The Gram-Schmidt process uses such residuals to remove directions already represented by earlier basis vectors.
This is an optimality condition as well as a geometric description. No permitted change within W can remove a further orthogonal component of r. In the preceding dot product example, r=(2,−1)T is perpendicular to the line spanned by (1,2)T.

(17)

Orthogonal projection matrix

Words: 361 Articles: 2

A matrix represents a linear projection when applying it twice has the same effect as applying it once. For an orthogonal projection with the standard Hermitian inner product on Cm, the additional condition is that the conjugate transpose equals the original matrix:
P2=P=P†.
(19)
Thus P is both idempotent and a Hermitian matrix. Over the real numbers this says P2=P=PT. Its image of a linear map is the retained linear subspace, while its kernel of a linear map is the orthogonal complement: vectors in the image are unchanged, and vectors in the kernel become zero.
For a matrix A with full column rank, projecting onto its column space gives
P=A(A†A)−1A†.
(20)
The invertibility of A†A follows from linear independence of the columns. If those columns are an orthonormal set, A†A=I and P=AA†. These formulas and the conditions P2=P=PT in the real case are developed in MIT 18.06SC, Projections onto Subspaces, pp. 1–3.
For the line spanned by e=(1,2)T,
P=eTeeeT​=51​(12​24​),P(3,1)T=(1,2)T.
(21)
In particular, Pe=e and P(2,−1)T=0. The eigenvalues are 1 on the retained direction and 0 on the removed direction. This exposes the meaning of an orthogonal projection as retaining selected components, rather than stretching them.
Symmetry refers to the specified inner product. In the real weighted setting, the formula becomes PM​=A(ATMA)−1ATM and satisfies PM2​=PM​ and PMT​M=MPM​. For the preceding weighted line example,
PM​=81​(48​24​).
(22)
It is not symmetric under the ordinary transpose, but it is an orthogonal projection for the weighted Euclidean inner product. The matrix criterion P=P† should therefore be used with the standard Hermitian inner product, not silently transferred to a different geometry.

Orthogonal projection of a circle between planes (b/a=∣n1​⋅n2​∣)

Under orthogonal projection from one plane onto another, a circle of radius R becomes an ellipse with semiaxes R and R∣cosα∣, where α is the angle between the planes' unit normal vectors. To prove this, choose one in-plane unit vector parallel to the planes' intersection and the other perpendicular to it. Parallel planes have a circular image, and perpendicular planes give the degenerate case of a line segment. An arbitrary center simply projects to the center of the image.

The Gram-Schmidt process turns a finite ordered family of linearly independent vectors v1​,…,vk​ in an inner product space over the real numbers into an orthonormal basis q1​,…,qk​ of their linear span. It keeps every successive linear span, so the first j new vectors describe exactly the same directions as the first j inputs. Its idea is geometric: remove the orthogonal projection onto directions already found, then divide the remaining vector by its norm. This makes a complicated basis easier to use without changing the vector subspace it describes.[ref]
Start with u1​=v1​ and q1​=v1​/∥v1​∥. At step j, use the previously obtained orthonormal set to define
uj​=vj​−∑i=1j−1​⟨vj​,qi​⟩qi​,qj​=∥uj​∥uj​​,∥uj​∥=⟨uj​,uj​⟩​.
(25)
The sum is the orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace Wj−1​=span(q1​,…,qj−1​), and uj​ is the orthogonal projection residual. Linear independence ensures uj​=0: otherwise vj​ would already be a linear combination of earlier inputs. If the input family is linearly dependent, use only the retained orthonormal vectors in the sum and omit an input whenever its remainder is the zero vector; do not divide by zero. The retained outputs still form an orthonormal basis of the original linear span, but their number can be smaller than k.
For a short verification, orthonormality makes every earlier inner product vanish:
⟨uj​,qℓ​⟩=⟨vj​,qℓ​⟩−∑i<j​⟨vj​,qi​⟩⟨qi​,qℓ​⟩=0(ℓ<j).
(26)
Also uj​ is vj​ minus a linear combination of earlier vectors, while vj​ is uj​ plus that same linear combination. These two inclusions, followed by division by a nonzero norm, give
span(q1​,…,qj​)=span(v1​,…,vj​).
(27)
The argument also works in an inner product space over the complex numbers with the existing inner product convention, linear in the first argument: the coefficients remain ⟨vj​,qi​⟩. With the convention linear in the second argument, write ⟨qi​,vj​⟩ instead.
