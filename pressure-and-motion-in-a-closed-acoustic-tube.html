Pressure and motion in a closed acoustic tube (p′=Pcos(kx)cos(ωt),u=Psin(kx)sin(ωt)/(ρ0​c))

Consider a uniform tube 0≤x≤L with rigid ends, constant undisturbed mass density ρ0​ and speed of sound c. Neglect losses and transverse structure. A free normal mode has
k=Lnπ​,ω=ck,n=1,2,…,
(1)
and a consistent choice of sound pressure, acoustic particle velocity and acoustic displacement is
p′(x,t)u(x,t)ξ(x,t)​=Pcos(kx)cos(ωt),=ρ0​cP​sin(kx)sin(ωt),=−ρ0​cωP​sin(kx)cos(ωt).​
(2)
Here P>0 is a peak sound pressure. Direct differentiation verifies ρ0​∂t​u=−∂x​p′ and ∂t​p′=−ρ0​c2∂x​u, the one-dimensional linear homentropic acoustic equations. The rigid ends enforce u=0 there. A driven experiment only approximates this free-mode geometry; the formulas do not predict a finite resonant amplitude for an undamped driven tube.
The maxima of sound pressure amplitude occur at kx=jπ, where acoustic particle velocity vanishes. The maxima of acoustic particle velocity amplitude instead occur at kx=(j+1/2)π, where sound pressure vanishes. The two families are displaced by λ/4, but each family repeats after λ/2. At a position where both are nonzero, the time dependence is also a quarter cycle apart. Using consecutive maxima of sound pressure amplitude therefore gives
c=2fd​,f=2πω​,d=2λ​.
(3)
The relation also applies to consecutive minima of sound pressure amplitude, provided the same family is used throughout. Mixing maxima and minima would halve the spacing and give an erroneous speed of sound.
The local acoustic energy density is p′2/(2ρ0​c2)+ρ0​u2/2. Averaging over one wave period gives P2/(4ρ0​c2), independent of x. Likewise ⟨p′u⟩=0: this ideal standing wave has no mean longitudinal acoustic energy flux, although its pressure and fluid velocity oscillate. Energy transport into a real lossy tube is a different question from the free-mode pattern.
