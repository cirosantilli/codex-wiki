Electromagnetic radiation

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism
OurBigBook.com Words: 8k Articles: 59
Electromagnetic radiation is a propagating disturbance of the electric field and magnetic field that carries energy and momentum away from its source. It can be a sustained electromagnetic wave or a finite wave packet: periodic oscillation is not required. In vacuum the disturbance travels at the speed of light. A receiver responds to fields arriving now from an earlier source event, as expressed by retarded time. This is how electrical activity at one place can deliver energy and information elsewhere without a connecting wire.
Stored electromagnetic energy alone does not establish outgoing electromagnetic radiation. A stationary electric charge in electrostatics, or a steady localized electric current in magnetostatics, can store electromagnetic energy with zero Poynting vector. Static combinations can have circulating energy flux without net outward power. Near a changing source, reactive energy can return later. The near and far field distinction separates these contributions from outgoing radiation zone fields.[ref]
A single traveling vacuum plane electromagnetic wave obeying the Maxwell equations has B=k×E/c. Its Poynting vector gives the energy density and flux of a plane electromagnetic wave:
S=μ0​E×B​=ϵ0​c∣E∣2k,I=⟨S⟩⋅k=2cϵ0​E02​​.
(1)
Here I is wave intensity; ϵ0​ and μ0​ are vacuum permittivity and vacuum permeability. For a monochromatic wave with linear polarization and peak amplitude E0​, squared cosine averages to 1/2. Pulses require the instantaneous Poynting vector; arbitrary incident-and-reflected superpositions need not obey this relation.[ref]
The electromagnetic momentum density is g=S/c2. Completely absorbing a unidirectional vacuum pulse of energy U transfers momentum U/c; see radiation pressure on a perfectly absorbing surface. A calculated 1.0mJ pulse transfers 3.34×10−12kgm/s. Symmetric emission can lose energy without net recoil because opposite momenta cancel.[ref]
The direction of emission matters as much as its total power. For an oscillating electric dipole moment along z, the angular distribution of electric dipole radiation is strongest across the axis and vanishes along it. Its origin is geometric: the electric dipole radiation field is the transverse projection of the second time derivative of the electric dipole moment, so its magnitude contains sinθ. Squaring this electric field to obtain wave intensity gives sin2θ. The electric-dipole approximation for radiation requires a source much smaller than its wavelength and observation in the radiation zone. [ref]
For example, the power per solid angle at θ=30∘ is one quarter of its equatorial value, while the electric field amplitude is one half. To normalize the angular distribution to total power, use spherical coordinates with dΩ=sinθdθdϕ:
∫sin2θdΩ=2π∫0π​sin3θdθ=38π​,P1​dΩdP​=8π3​sin2θ.
(2)
The integral follows by substituting x=cosθ, giving ∫−11​(1−x2)dx=4/3. This is a probability-like normalization over direction, with units of inverse steradian; it does not make the dipole an isotropic radiator. The same angular integral underlies the Larmor formula for nonrelativistic accelerated electric charge.
Detection requires the incident electromagnetic field to do something observable. An antenna develops an electrical response; a thermopile converts absorbed energy into a thermal and then electrical signal; a photodetector can respond through photon absorption. Hertz spark transmission and detection experiment makes this conversion visible as a tiny spark. Detector orientation, sensitivity, and surrounding reflections all matter: failure to see a signal does not prove that no electromagnetic radiation reached the receiver.
