Nuclear magnetic resonance

 Home Chemistry
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 84
Nuclear magnetic resonance detects the response of atomic nuclei with a magnetic moment to a radio frequency magnetic field in a steady magnetic field. Its striking possibility is to measure a nuclear property through an ordinary electrical signal from a bulk sample: an enormous number of tiny magnetic moments can produce a measurable change in a receiving circuit.
For a nucleus with spin angular momentum J, define its gyromagnetic ratio by μ=γJ. Throughout this article γ is angular, measured in radians per second per tesla; γ/(2π) is the corresponding value in cycles per second per tesla. With B0​=B0​z^, the Hamiltonian is H=−γB0​Jz​. Adjacent energy levels differ by
ΔE=ℏ∣γ∣B0​,ω0​=∣γ∣B0​,f0​=2π∣γ∣B0​​.
(1)
Thus a measured resonance frequency and a calibrated magnetic field determine ∣γ∣. Converting that into a nuclear magnetic moment also requires the spin quantum number: the maximum projection has magnitude ∣γ∣ℏI. This simple experiment does not by itself determine the sign of γ.
The Rabi molecular beam magnetic resonance experiment detects transitions through changes in a selected molecular beam. The bulk experiments below instead detect electrical consequences of nuclear magnetization in matter at ordinary density. A small Boltzmann distribution population imbalance is multiplied by a very large number of nuclei. For noninteracting spin one-half nuclei of number density n and positive γ, their equilibrium magnetization is
M0​=n2γℏ​tanh(2kB​TγℏB0​​).
(2)
This follows by weighting the two projections ±γℏ/2 with their Boltzmann distribution probabilities. It explains both the smallness of the bulk polarization at ordinary temperature and the value of many nuclei contributing to one receiving circuit.
NMR spectroscopy measures resonant responses of atomic nuclei to applied electromagnetic radiation in a magnetic field. In residual-group monitoring of polyurethane formation, distinguishable signal integrals supply a relative residual-group readout; overlap or very weak signals can limit that inference.

Bloch nuclear induction experiment

Words: 185
Nuclear magnetic resonance OurBigBook.com
In their 1946 letter, Felix Bloch, William Webster Hansen, and Martin Packard reported water at room temperature. A steady magnetic field along z and an oscillating field along x produced a signal in a pickup coil along y, displayed on an oscilloscope. Some samples included paramagnetic additives to accelerate thermal equilibrium. Resonance fields at two frequencies gave the same field-to-frequency ratio, consistent with the proton value.[ref]
The detailed paper describes glass water bulbs inside tuned receiving coils. Transmitter coils, shields and a conducting paddle controlled leakage. Field modulation at 60 Hz drove the horizontal oscilloscope sweep. A diode and audio amplification supplied the vertical trace. Vibration changed coupling: their largest difficulty. Coil tuning capacitors formed tank circuits. The actual receiver omitted the conceptual diagram's radiofrequency amplifier.[ref]
The orthogonal coil axes suppress direct transmitter pickup, but do not eliminate it. A field-dependent signal at the nuclear resonance must be distinguished from leakage and noise; merely detecting a radiofrequency voltage is insufficient. This is why the field sweep and the independently checked field-to-frequency ratio matter.

Purcell nuclear resonance absorption experiment

Words: 234
Nuclear magnetic resonance OurBigBook.com
Edward Mills Purcell, Henry Cutler Torrey, and Robert Vivian Pound placed paraffin wax in a capacitively loaded coaxial cavity near 30 MHz between magnet poles. Coupling loops supplied and extracted power; oscillating and steady fields were perpendicular. Output balanced against a generator reference fed amplification, detection and a microammeter. A slow field sweep revealed absorption above fluctuations at room temperature. Weak drive avoided population equalization. Calibration was rough; field inhomogeneity could contribute to linewidth.[ref]
These are driven experiments with continuous radiofrequency excitation and changing static field, not demonstrations of modern pulsed Fourier transform nuclear magnetic resonance. Bloch's theoretical paper explicitly distinguishes proposed free precession after a pulse from the forced precession actually studied.[ref]
An experimental failure need not mean absent nuclear magnetic moments. In the model below, excessive drive reduces the population imbalance through nuclear magnetic resonance saturation; waiting too briefly can leave insufficient polarization. Separately, ordinary electrical losses can cause heating without identifying a nuclear resonance. The absorption experiment measured a balanced electrical signal, rather than using heating as its readout. Useful diagnostic checks include moving off resonance, changing the frequency, checking field uniformity and reducing drive. These are general checks; the historical operations actually documented are specified above.

Bloch equations (M˙=γM×B−M⊥​/T2​−(Mz​−M0​)z^/T1​)

Words: 247 Articles: 2
Nuclear magnetic resonance OurBigBook.com
The Bloch equations describe an ensemble magnetization M with Larmor precession plus phenomenological recovery and decay:
dtdM​=γM×B−T2​Mx​x^+My​y^​​−T1​Mz​−M0​​z^.
(3)
Here T1​,T2​>0 are model parameters, not a microscopic explanation of relaxation. The precession term follows from the torque on a magnetic moment; the two dissipative terms prescribe exponential decay. Bloch explicitly introduced this macroscopic relaxation description as a simplifying approximation.[ref]
For positive γ and no transverse drive, Mx​+iMy​ rotates as e−iγB0​t: its signed rotation is negative about z, although the resonance angular frequency ω0​=γB0​ is positive. This distinction prevents a hidden sign change when choosing a rotating frame.

Longitudinal nuclear spin relaxation (T1​)

Words: 53
Bloch equations OurBigBook.com
With no transverse drive, the Bloch equations give
Mz​(t)=M0​+[Mz​(0)−M0​]e−t/T1​.
(4)
Longitudinal nuclear spin relaxation restores the population imbalance and thus the magnetization parallel to the static magnetic field. It corresponds to population relaxation for the two-level model. Starting from Mz​(0)=0, waiting T1​ recovers 1−e−1 of M0​, whereas waiting 5T1​ recovers about 99.3%. This is why repeated measurements require a recovery-time decision.

Transverse nuclear magnetization decay (T2​)

Words: 81
Bloch equations OurBigBook.com
Transverse relaxation describes loss of ensemble transverse magnetization. It must not automatically be identified with pure dephasing. In a simple completely positive two-level relaxation model,
T2​1​=2T1​1​+Tϕ​1​,
(5)
where Tϕ​ describes additional pure dephasing. Thus population recovery itself contributes to transverse decay. A spread of static magnetic fields can further cancel ensemble phases without the same microscopic relaxation; an observed free signal may have an effective T2∗​ rather than homogeneous T2​. The simple exponential examples below assume a uniform field and a single homogeneous T2​.

Free induction decay (S(t)=S0​e−t/T2​e−iΔt)

Words: 300
Nuclear magnetic resonance OurBigBook.com
Free induction decay is a receiving signal from freely precessing transverse magnetization after excitation ends. Faraday's law of induction gives coil voltage proportional to the time derivative of linked magnetic flux, with sign set by the winding. At ω0​T2​≫1 its envelope follows the transverse magnetization; a fixed receiver phase can absorb the derivative's near-quadrature shift. We define the calibrated complex signal below to represent magnetization, not raw coil voltage.
For an original teaching model, take positive γ, a circular drive B1​(cosωtx^−sinωty^​), and axes rotating at −ω about z. Define u+iv=eiωt(Mx​+iMy​), w=Mz​, Δ=ω0​−ω and ω1​=γB1​. The Bloch equations become
u˙=−u/T2​+Δv,v˙=−Δu−v/T2​+ω1​w,w˙=−ω1​v−(w−M0​)/T1​.
(6)
A linearly oscillating field of peak amplitude 2B1​ has a co-rotating component of amplitude B1​; neglecting its counter-rotating component requires ω1​≪ω0​ near resonance.
At resonance, a short pulse starting from (0,0,M0​) gives (u,v,w)=(0,M0​sinθ,M0​cosθ) with θ=γB1​τ, neglecting relaxation during duration τ. A receiver phase rotation can make a π/2 pulse's initial transverse signal real. After the pulse, direct integration gives
S(t)=S0​e−t/T2​e−iΔt,I(t)=ReS(t),Q(t)=ImS(t).
(7)
These are the two components extracted by quadrature demodulation. Differentiating verifies S˙=−(T2−1​+iΔ)S; taking its magnitude separates decay from detuning.
Choose rounded illustrative γ/(2π)=42.58MHz/T, B0​=0.200T, B1​=10μT, T1​=0.800s and T2​=0.120s. Then f0​=8.516MHz and the π/2 duration is τ=1/(4(γ/(2π))B1​)=0.587ms, short relative to both relaxation times. For receiver detuning Δ/(2π)=5Hz and S0​=1, S(0.050s)=−0.65924i and S(0.100s)=−0.43460. These are calculated examples, not 1946 measurements.

Continuous-wave nuclear magnetic resonance

Words: 238 Articles: 2
Nuclear magnetic resonance OurBigBook.com
For constant drive, set the three rotating frame derivatives to zero. Substitution first gives u=ΔT2​v, then w=M0​−ω1​T1​v, hence
v=1+Δ2T22​+sω1​T2​M0​​,s=ω12​T1​T2​.
(8)
The absorption is proportional to ω1​v. With s≪1, its frequency dependence is a Cauchy distribution profile, often called a Lorentzian. With appreciable s, the response broadens and w=M0​/(1+s) on resonance: nuclear magnetic resonance saturation limits the response. The short-pulse amplitude in the previous example is not a weak continuous drive; these are different operating regimes of the same equations.

Nuclear resonance linewidth (ΔfFWHM​=1/(πT2​))

Words: 141
Continuous-wave nuclear magnetic resonance OurBigBook.com
For weak drive in this homogeneous model, half maximum occurs at ∣Δ∣=1/T2​. The absorption full width at half maximum is therefore
ΔωFWHM​=T2​2​,ΔfFWHM​=πT2​1​,ΔBFWHM​=∣γ∣T2​2​.
(9)
The example gives 2.653Hz or 0.06230μT. At finite drive the model width is multiplied by 1+s​. Field inhomogeneity and unresolved components can also broaden an actual line, so its width alone does not determine pure dephasing.

∫0∞​e−ateiΩtdt=a−iΩ1​,Rea−iΩ1​=a2+Ω2a​.
(10)
This connects exponential memory to a Lorentzian absorptive line. It is a mathematical connection, not evidence that the 1946 teams performed pulsed Fourier transform spectroscopy. Instrumental signal bandwidth is a separate choice: narrowing a receiver band can reject thermal electrical noise, but too narrow a band distorts a changing signal. A sample linewidth and a receiver bandwidth are not interchangeable.

Spin echo (90∘−τ−180∘−τ)

Words: 1k Articles: 3
Nuclear magnetic resonance OurBigBook.com
A spin echo is a reappearance of transverse magnetization after a pulse sequence reverses an earlier spread of phases. An ensemble can have almost no net transverse magnetization while its components retain enough individual phase memory to reassemble. In the ideal Hahn echo below, a short 90° pulse prepares transverse magnetization, a 180° pulse follows after time τ, and the signal refocuses at t=2τ. The receiver observes freely precessing magnetization, as in free induction decay; no third driving pulse is needed at the echo time.
The useful distinction is between cancellation in an ensemble average and loss of recoverable phase memory. A static spread of Larmor precession frequencies can make free induction decay much faster than transverse nuclear magnetization decay. A spin echo can remove that static spread from the echo amplitude, allowing a more selective measurement of transverse relaxation. It does not in general reverse noise, population relaxation, or every interaction between spins.

Use the rotating frame and positive gyromagnetic ratio convention of free induction decay. Let Δ=ω0​−ω be each component's fixed detuning, and normalize its magnetization by the equilibrium value. An instantaneous resonant pulse along +u rotates through −π/2 about u, preparing m(0+)=(0,1,0). A second pulse along +v rotates through −π about v. These ideal pulses require a drive much stronger than the relevant detunings and negligible relaxation during either pulse. The corresponding rotation matrices, acting on column vectors, are

The pulse P sends mu​+imv​ to its negative complex conjugate, hence sends s to s∗. Direct multiplication gives

The phase accumulated before the pulse is reflected; the angular velocity during subsequent free evolution keeps its sign. At 2τ every fixed detuning has zero residual phase. Equivalently, PFΔ​(t)P=FΔ​(−t), so FΔ​(τ)PFΔ​(τ)=P. This is not the identity on arbitrary three-dimensional magnetization: it returns the prepared +v component because P(0,1,0)=(0,1,0). Increasing σ narrows the echo without changing S(2τ) in this ideal model. Hahn spin echo experiment

Words: 131
Spin echo OurBigBook.com
Erwin Louis Hahn's 1950 apparatus enclosed liquid samples in the coil of a tuned LC circuit, coupled to a gated radio transmitter and radio receiver. One transmitter quadrupled a crystal oscillator's 7.5 MHz to 30 MHz. A separate proton resonance regulator stabilized the static magnetic field.[ref]
Waiting for thermal equilibrium prepared nuclear polarization. Oscilloscope photographs compared glycerol proton echoes at different field homogeneity: echo width changed; echo time followed twice the pulse separation. Receiver saturation obscured early free induction decay; transmitter leakage required care. The delayed signal tested refocusing; diffusion could still attenuate it.[ref]
Hahn analyzed equal pulses as well as noting improved recovery with a second pulse twice as long; the ideal sequence above isolates that latter case.[ref]

Diffusion attenuation of a spin echo (S(2τ)=exp[−2τ/T2​−2Dγ2G2τ3/3])

A spin echo refocuses a nucleus's fixed detuning. A nucleus that moves through a magnetic field gradient can have different detunings during the two intervals. To see the distinction without assuming that all noise is reversible, let Δ(t) vary during free evolution. With the same pulse convention, its residual phase at 2τ is
Φ=∫0τ​Δ(t)dt−∫τ2τ​Δ(t)dt=∫02τ​y(t)Δ(t)dt,y(t)={+1,−1,​t<τ,t>τ.​
(14)
Static detuning cancels because ∫y(t)dt=0. A detuning Δa​ during the first interval and Δb​ during the second leaves Φ=τ(Δa​−Δb​), which need not vanish. The final component signal is eiΦ before relaxation; changing the overall receiver convention changes its conjugation but not its magnitude.
For an original quantitative model, take a constant magnetic field gradient G=dBz​/dx and unrestricted one-dimensional Brownian motion x(t)=x0​+2D​Wt​, where D is the diffusion coefficient and Wt​ is standard Brownian motion. Then Δ(t)=Δ0​+γGx(t). The constant terms cancel, and the Gaussian distribution of the remaining phase follows from the Brownian covariance kernel E[Ws​Wt​]=min(s,t). Writing Q(r)=∫r2τ​y(t)dt gives
Q(r)={−r,r−2τ,​0≤r≤τ,τ≤r≤2τ,​VarΦ=2Dγ2G2∫02τ​Q(r)2dr=34​Dγ2G2τ3.
(15)
The characteristic function of a zero-mean Gaussian distribution now supplies the extra factor E[eiΦ]=e−VarΦ/2, so
S(2τ)=exp[−T2​2τ​−32​Dγ2G2τ3].
(16)
Here γ is in radians per second per tesla, G in tesla per metre, and D in square metres per second: the extra exponent is dimensionless. The figure chooses γ/(2π)=42.58MHz/T, G=0.010T/m and D=2.0×10−9m2/s. With the earlier τ and T2​, diffusion reduces the echo from 0.60653 to about 0.32930. These are illustrative model parameters, not values extracted from Hahn's experiment.
This result assumes instantaneous perfect pulses, a constant magnetic field gradient, unrestricted diffusion with constant diffusion coefficient, and independent homogeneous transverse relaxation. Restricted motion, changing gradients, finite pulses and interactions between spins can require different attenuation laws. Even a fixed Hamiltonian is refocused only for the terms whose sign the pulse reverses: a simultaneous 180° rotation of two spins leaves their Jz,1​Jz,2​ product unchanged, because both factors change sign. A recovered echo therefore demonstrates a particular recoverable phase history, rather than reversal of every microscopic process.
