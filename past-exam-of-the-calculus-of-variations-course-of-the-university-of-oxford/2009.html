For fixed-endpoint variations, the second variation is
q(φ)=δ2I(uˉ)(φ,φ)=dt2d2​I(uˉ+tφ)​t=0​=∫ab​(pφ′2+2rφφ′+sφ2)dx,
(9)
with p=fvv​, r=fuv​, s=fuu​ evaluated along (x,uˉ,uˉ′). The Jacobi equation, written without differentiating r separately, is
−(pz′+rz)′+rz′+sz=0.​
(10)
When r is differentiable this is −(pz′)′+(s−r′)z=0.
Positive definiteness means q(φ)>0 for every nonzero admissible variation. Uniform or coercive positivity of a second variation is the stronger assertion that q(φ)≥c∫φ′2 for some fixed c>0. On a fixed-endpoint interval this is equivalent to a positive lower bound in the W1,2 norm.
The strict Legendre implication uses the uniform interpretation of positivity. If q≥c∫φ′2, localized rapidly oscillating variations show p(x)≥c: otherwise support them in an interval where p−c<0, so the derivative term of q−c∫φ′2 is negative of order frequency squared, overwhelming its mixed and zero-order terms. Continuity extends the bound to the endpoints, giving p>0 on [a,b]. Under mere positive definiteness this strict conclusion is false: f=u2 at uˉ=0 gives q=2∫φ2>0 but p=0. In that weaker convention only the ordinary Legendre necessary condition p≥0 follows. This distinction makes the implication precise.
Under the stated regular condition p>0, a conjugate point c∈(a,b] to a is an endpoint admitting a nonzero Jacobi solution with z(a)=z(c)=0. The disconjugacy form of the Jacobi positivity criterion says that no such point in (a,b] exists if and only if there is a Jacobi solution strictly positive on all of [a,b].
For the required identity, let that positive solution be z and write φ=zh. Set A=pzz′+rz2. The Jacobi equation gives
A′=pz′2+2rzz′+sz2.
(11)
Expanding the second-variation integrand therefore yields
pφ′2+2rφφ′+sφ2=pz2h′2+(Ah2)′.
(12)
For compactly supported φ, the boundary term vanishes, proving
δ2I(uˉ)(φ,φ)=∫ab​pz2[(zφ​)′]2dx.​
(13)
This is the Jacobi identity for the second variation. The divergence form is also appropriate under the original C1 regularity; with p>0, the Euler-Lagrange equation gives the usual further regularity along the extremal.

A weak local minimizer of a variational integral is an admissible uˉ for which some ρ>0 makes I(v)≥I(uˉ) whenever v∈A and ∥v−uˉ∥W1,∞​<ρ. Both the function and its derivative are required to be close.

A strong local minimizer of a variational integral is an admissible uˉ for which the same conclusion holds whenever ∥v−uˉ∥∞​<ρ. Every strong local minimizer is weak, because Sobolev closeness implies uniform closeness. A global minimizer is both, and the converse implication from weak to strong is not generally valid.
If the integrand is independent of u, a weak local minimizer has vanishing first variation:
∫ab​fv​(x,uˉ′(x))φ′(x)dx=0(φ∈C0∞​(a,b)).
(20)
The bounded function h(x)=fv​(x,uˉ′(x)) therefore has zero distributional derivative, so is constant almost everywhere. For an elementary test-function argument, any smooth compactly supported ψ of integral zero is the derivative of a compactly supported smooth φ. Hence ∫hψ=0; subtracting a fixed unit-integral test function from arbitrary tests shows that h agrees with one constant against every test. The fundamental lemma then gives
fv​(x,uˉ′(x))=calmost everywhere.​
(21)
This is the first integral for a variational integrand independent of the function value.
A point of convexity v0​ of a differentiable real function g has a globally supporting tangent: g(v)≥g(v0​)+g′(v0​)(v−v0​) for every v. The Weierstrass necessary condition for a strong minimum says that uˉ′(x) is such a point for v↦f(x,uˉ(x),v) for almost every x. Equivalently, the Weierstrass excess function is nonnegative at every trial slope.
