For the supporting inequality, assume first that F is convex and fix z,z0​. For 0<t≤1,
F(z0​+t(z−z0​))≤(1−t)F(z0​)+tF(z).
(1)
Subtract F(z0​), divide by t and let t↓0. Differentiability gives
F(z)≥F(z0​)+DF(z0​)⋅(z−z0​).​
(2)
Conversely assume this inequality for every pair. Put w=(1−t)z0​+tz and apply it with base point w to both z0​ and z. Multiply those two inequalities by 1−t and t and add. The gradient terms cancel, proving F(w)≤(1−t)F(z0​)+tF(z). Thus the supporting inequality characterizes a differentiable convex function.
For the integral inequality let zˉ=∣Ω∣−1∫Ω​u. Apply the supporting inequality at zˉ to u(x) and integrate. Its affine term has integral DF(zˉ)⋅(∫Ω​u−∣Ω∣zˉ)=0, yielding
F(∣Ω∣1​∫Ω​u)≤∣Ω∣1​∫Ω​F(u).​
(3)
This is Jensen's inequality. Since u is integrable, the supporting affine lower bound has integrable negative part, so the right side is well-defined as an extended real integral. If its positive part is infinite, the inequality remains valid.

If an admissible u attained the infimum zero, each nonnegative term of its integrand would vanish almost everywhere. In particular ∣u∣2011=0 almost everywhere, so u=0 in its Sobolev space and its weak derivative is zero. The variational terminology refers to the size of a neighborhood, rather than weak versus strong convergence in a Banach space. The definitions in the two numbered children give
global minimizer⟹strong local minimizer⟹weak local minimizer.​
(9)
The second implication holds because smallness in W1,∞ implies smallness in the uniform norm. Reverse implications need not hold.
For differentiable f in its slope variable, define the Weierstrass excess function
E(x,y,p,q)=f(x,y,q)−f(x,y,p)−fv​(x,y,p)(q−p).
(10)
The strengthened Weierstrass condition means that some δ>0 gives
E(x,y,p,q)≥0for all x∈[a,b], q∈R,∣y−u(x)∣<δ,∣p−u′(x)∣<δ.​
(11)
It requires support at nearby heights and nearby reference slopes, while the trial slope q is arbitrary. Requiring this only exactly on the curve is the unstrengthened necessary condition.
A standard Weierstrass sufficient condition assumes the usual C2 smoothness of f, u∈A∩C1, the weak Euler-Lagrange equation, a coercively positive second variation, and the strengthened condition above. Explicitly, for some c>0,
δ2I(u)[η,η]=∫ab​(fuu​η2+2fuv​ηη′+fvv​(η′)2)dx≥c∥η∥W1,22​
(12)
for zero-endpoint variations. Then u is a strong local minimizer. If the excess is strictly positive for q=p throughout the tube, the strong minimum is strict. The second-variation hypothesis supplies a neighboring field of extremals. Its slope p(x,y) gives the comparison identity
I(v)−I(u)=∫ab​E(x,v(x),p(x,v(x)),v′(x))dx≥0
(13)
for uniformly close admissible v. This explains how the condition controls competitors with large derivatives.

A scalar Tonelli existence theorem states: if f(x,y,p) is continuous, convex in p, and has a uniform superlinear lower bound f(x,y,p)≥Θ(∣p∣)−C with Θ(r)/r→∞, and at least one fixed-endpoint admissible absolutely continuous function has finite energy, then the energy attains its infimum in the fixed-endpoint W1,1 class. One may take Θ nonnegative and convex. The lower bound makes derivatives uniformly integrable; endpoint control then gives uniform compactness of a minimizing sequence, and convexity supplies the required lower semicontinuity.
For the specified functional, the integrand is continuous, nonnegative, and convex in the derivative, with
f(x,y,p)≥52∣p∣52​,∣p∣∣p∣52/52​⟶∞.
(21)
The affine function u0​(x)=2x−1 has the required endpoints and finite energy. Thus a global minimizer exists. In fact any finite-energy minimizer belongs to W1,52, since its derivative's 52nd power is integrable.
It is unique. The admissible class is convex, and the integrand is strictly convex in (y,p): ∣p∣52 and ∣y∣2011 are strictly convex, with the latter multiplied by ex>0. If two distinct admissible finite-energy functions minimized the energy, their midpoint would have strictly smaller energy. Distinct continuous representatives differ on a set of positive measure, so the strict integral inequality really applies. This contradiction proves uniqueness.

In one dimension, u∈W1,2 has an absolutely continuous bounded representative. For any smooth zero-endpoint test function η, the functions u+tη are admissible and arbitrarily close to u for small t. Local minimality forces the first variation to vanish. Differentiation under the integral is valid because u,η are bounded and the derivative terms are integrable, giving the weak Euler-Lagrange equation
∫01​(2u′η′+4xu3η)dx=0.​
(22)
In distributional form this says (2u′)′=4xu3. The fundamental theorem for weak derivatives, or the fundamental lemma applied after subtracting an antiderivative, gives a constant C with
u′(x)=C+2∫0x​tu(t)3dtalmost everywhere.​
(23)
This is the integrated Euler-Lagrange equation. The boundary difference u(1)−u(0)=2 determines
C=2−2∫01​(1−t)tu(t)3dt.
(24)
The weak identity extends to all zero-trace W1,2 test functions by density.
