Change-of-basis matrix ([z]old​=P[z]new​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Change of basis
OurBigBook.com Words: 156
Fix the convention: the columns of the change-of-basis matrix P are the new basis vectors written in the old basis. Thus P converts new coordinate vectors into old ones; its matrix inverse converts old coordinate vectors into new ones. For the preceding basis,
P=(11​1−1​),P−1=21​(11​1−1​),P−1(20​)=(11​).
(45)
Its determinant is −2, so P is an invertible matrix. Checking which direction P converts is essential: different books may call either P or P−1 the change-of-basis matrix.
For a linear operator, convert the input into the old basis, apply the old matrix, and convert the output back. Reading that sequence from right to left gives
Anew​=P−1Aold​P.
(46)
For example, let the old diagonal matrix stretch horizontal components by 2 and vertical components by 3. Then
Aold​=(20​03​),Anew​=(2.5−0.5​−0.52.5​),Anew​(11​)=(22​).
(47)
The output represents 2u+2v=(4,0), just as Aold​(2,0)T=(4,0)T. The matrices look different because their bases differ, but they encode the same linear operator. This relation is called matrix similarity; it preserves the underlying action.[ref]
