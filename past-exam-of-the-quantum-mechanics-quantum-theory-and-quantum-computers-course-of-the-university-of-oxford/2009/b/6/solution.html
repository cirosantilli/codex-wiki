For classical events, A∖C⊂(A∖B)∪(B∖C): an outcome in A but not in C either fails B or belongs to B∖C. Monotonicity and the union bound prove the Fréchet-Bell inequality
P(A∖C)≤P(A∖B)+P(B∖C).
(28)
Malus' law gives transmission cos2γ for a photon polarized by the first filter, so its conditional rejection probability at the second is sin2γ​. Conditional and joint probabilities must be distinguished: P(A∖B)=P(A)P(Bc∣A).
For equal unbiased marginals P(A)=P(B)=1/2, the quantum two-setting rejection probabilities are 21​sin2(θA​−θB​). A classical common assignment of all three outcomes would therefore require sin2(θA​−θB​)+sin2(θB​−θC​)≥sin2(θA​−θC​). Choose directions 0,γ,2γ. Then
2sin2γ<sin2(2γ)when 0<γ<π/4.​
(29)
For example γ=π/6 gives 1/2<3/4. More generally sin2a+sin2b−sin2(a+b)=−2sinasinbcos(a+b). In a triangle whose side directions are these filters, the internal angle supplementary to a+b is obtuse exactly in this violation range. This specifies the angle convention behind the triangle description: with polarization axes regarded as unoriented lines, a claim that every sufficiently large physical axis separation violates the inequality would not be correct. The contradiction concerns a single classical joint model for all settings, not the validity of ordinary probability or a comparison of arbitrarily renormalized sequential experiments.
Use ∣0⟩=∣α0​⟩ and ∣1⟩=∣α1​⟩. Projecting the first photon with ∣0⟩⟨0∣ gives the unnormalized product vector c0​∣00⟩, with success probability ∣c0​∣2. Projection of the second onto ∣θ⟩=cosθ∣0⟩+sinθ∣1⟩ gives
c0​cosθ∣0⟩∣θ⟩,P(both transmissions)=∣c0​∣2cos2θ.​
(30)
Conditioned on success, normalize these vectors; a zero amplitude means that outcome never occurs. For the maximally entangled equal-amplitude pair, rotation of both real polarization bases gives the same correlations for arbitrary settings. Comparing the appropriately normalized coincidence/rejection frequencies at three setting pairs tests the inequality above. A general unequal-amplitude state need not reproduce the unbiased rotationally invariant probabilities, so the maximally entangled choice supplies the clean explicit example.
For swapping, label the four systems in order ξ1​,η1​,ξ2​,η2​ and let ∣Φ⟩η​=2−1/2∑j=01​∣ηj​ηj​⟩. Its overlap with ∣ηr​ηs​⟩ is δrs​/2​. Applying its rank-one projector to Alice's two systems gives
21​r,k∑​br2​∣ξr​⟩∣ηk​⟩∣ξr​⟩∣ηk​⟩.​
(31)
After regrouping the remote and local systems, this factorizes into [∑r​br2​∣ξr​ξr​⟩/2​]⊗∣Φ⟩η​. The selected outcome has probability p=21​∑r​∣br​∣4. Therefore the normalized conditional remote state is
∣χ⟩ξ​=∑r​∣br​∣4​∑r​br2​∣ξr​ξr​⟩​.​
(32)
It has Schmidt rank at least two whenever the original pair was entangled, since squaring a nonzero coefficient does not make it zero. Thus the two photons that never interacted become entangled conditional on Alice's joint measurement outcome. This is entanglement swapping. Alice is projecting onto a specified Bell state, not performing a universal test of whether an arbitrary unknown state is entangled. Averaging all her outcomes leaves the remote reduced density operator unchanged, so no faster-than-light signal is available without her classical outcome label.
