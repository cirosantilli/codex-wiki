Limit inferior and limit superior (liminf, limsup)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Sequence and series Sequence
OurBigBook.com Words: 505 Articles: 1
The limit inferior and limit superior retain the lower and upper long-run behaviour of a real sequence, even when it has no ordinary limit of a sequence. For each tail of a sequence beginning at N, define
lN​=inf{an​:n≥N},uN​=sup{an​:n≥N}.
(52)
Discarding more initial terms raises or preserves the infimum and lowers or preserves the supremum. Hence (lN​) is nondecreasing and (uN​) is nonincreasing. Their limits exist in the extended real number line:
liminfn→∞​an​=limN→∞​lN​=supN​lN​,limsupn→∞​an​=limN→∞​uN​=infN​uN​.
(53)
For a bounded sequence, both values are finite and liminfan​≤limsupan​. These bounds use the entire infinite tail of a sequence, rather than the last few observed terms. [ref].
Take the original example an​=(−1)n(1+1/n), beginning −2,3/2,−4/3,5/4,…. The subsequence at even integers decreases toward one, while the subsequence at odd integers increases toward minus one. If eN​ is the first even integer at least N and oN​ is the first odd integer at least N, then
uN​=1+1/eN​,lN​=−1−1/oN​.
(54)
Thus liminfan​=−1 and limsupan​=1. The finite initial extreme a1​=−2 disappears from later lower bounds; neither long-run value is attained by any term of this example.
For a bounded sequence, the limit inferior is the smallest subsequential limit and the limit superior is the largest subsequential limit; both are reached by suitable convergent subsequences. Every other subsequential limit lies between them. In the example there are only two subsequential limits, −1 and 1: a convergent subsequence cannot retain infinitely many terms of both parities, because those two branches stay separated. The whole interval between the two bounds need not consist of subsequential limits. [ref].
A finite ordinary limit of a sequence exists exactly when the limit inferior and limit superior agree at the same finite value L. The bounds lN​≤aN​≤uN​ and the squeeze theorem prove the sufficient direction. For a bounded sequence, its tail diameter of a sequence is DN​=uN​−lN​, so the limiting gap is zero exactly when the Cauchy sequence condition holds. In the example the gap approaches two, not zero. The same framework allows infinite bounds: an​=n has both values +∞, whereas an​=(−1)nn has limit inferior −∞ and limit superior +∞. Equality at infinity describes escape rather than convergence to a real value. [ref].
