The quotient q(w)=ϕ(w)/w extends to a holomorphic function at zero with value one. Since ϕ is injective and vanishes at zero, q has no zero in the unit disc. A holomorphic logarithm ℓ exists there, with ℓ(0)=0. Define the odd square-root transform of a normalized univalent function by
h(z)=zexp[ℓ(z2)/2].
(3)
It satisfies h(z)2=ϕ(z2), h(0)=0, h′(0)=1 and h(−z)=−h(z). To check injectivity, h(z)=h(w) gives ϕ(z2)=ϕ(w2), hence z2=w2. If w=−z, oddness gives h(z)=0, so z=w=0. Thus the two arguments always coincide, and h∈S. Choosing the square root by its derivative at zero removes its sign ambiguity.

The symmetry rule for extremal length says that if a domain and its curve family are invariant under reflection, the supremum defining extremal length may be taken over reflection-invariant conformal metrics. More generally, for a conformal or anticonformal involution T, use metrics satisfying T∗ρ=ρ.
To see this, replace ρ by ρs​=(ρ+T∗ρ)/2. Every curve in the invariant family and its image have length at least Lρ​, so Lρs​​≥Lρ​. Convexity of the square and change of variables give Aρs​​≤Aρ​. Therefore symmetrizing cannot reduce the length-squared to area ratio.

Regard the upper half-disc, with cyclic boundary vertices −1,0,x,1, as a conformal quadrilateral. Map it to a rectangle of width w and height h, so the boundary arcs [−1,0] and [x,1] become its vertical sides. The rectangle formulas for the conformal modulus of a quadrilateral give
d=hw​,d∗​=wh​,dd∗​=1​.
(14)
The remaining two boundary arcs, (0,x) and the semicircle, become the horizontal sides. Thus these are precisely the dual joining families in the same quadrilateral.

d OurBigBook.com
Use the simply connected domain shown in the diagram. A Riemann mapping theorem map followed by an automorphism of the unit disk sends its marked points to 0 and x∈(0,1). Split this disc along its real diameter. Map the upper conformal quadrilateral from c to a rectangle [0,w]×[0,h], with [−1,0] and [x,1] as vertical sides and (0,x) as its bottom. The lower half is a second identical rectangle. The full disc is obtained by gluing the two rectangles along their two vertical sides and their bottom sides; the two top sides form the original outer boundary.
In this doubled rectangle extremal-length duality, loops enclosing both marked bottom corners have shortest flat length 2w. They must meet both vertical seams, and a closed curve's total horizontal travel is at least 2w; the doubled horizontal segments attain it. Crosscuts separating the two corners must meet the bottom seam, and run from outer boundary back to outer boundary. Their total vertical travel is at least 2h, attained by doubled vertical segments. The total flat area is 2wh.
These flat metrics are extremal, not merely lower bounds. For an arbitrary metric, average the lengths of the doubled horizontal segments over their height and apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to get Lρ​(Γ)2h≤2wAρ​. Averaging the doubled vertical segments gives Lρ​(Γ∗)2w≤2hAρ​. Consequently
λ(Γ)=h2w​=2d,λ(Γ∗)=w2h​=2d∗​,λ(Γ)λ(Γ∗)=4​.
(15)
The conformal metric on the doubled rectangles pulls back to an admissible metric on the original punctured domain; the two marked corner points have zero area. Here Γ∗ consists of crosscuts with endpoints on the outer boundary, as drawn. Boundedness alone, without the illustrated domain topology and the specified curve families, would not justify this construction.

For ∣z∣<r, the Schwarz integral on the unit disk, rescaled to radius r, is
g(z)=2π1​∫02π​u(reiθ)reiθ−zreiθ+z​dθ.
(22)
On each smaller closed disk ∣z∣≤s<r, its kernel and all its derivatives in z are uniformly bounded. Since the boundary values of the real harmonic function u are continuous on this circle, differentiation under the integral sign proves that g is holomorphic. Taking the real part gives the Poisson integral of those boundary values. It agrees with u by uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem on the radius-r disk. Hence g is holomorphic and Reg=u on ∣z∣<r. Its imaginary part is the harmonic conjugate normalized by Img(0)=0.
