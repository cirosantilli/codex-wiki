Mixing calorimetry (mx​cx​(Tx​−Tf​)=(mw​cw​+Ccal​)(Tf​−Tw​)+Qloss,out​)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Calorimetry
OurBigBook.com Words: 417
Mixing calorimetry determines heat capacities by bringing bodies at different temperatures into thermal contact. The striking feature is that the final temperature is a weighted arithmetic mean with heat capacities as weights, rather than weights given by mass. The vessel participates in the exchange even though it is not the sample being studied.
For a hot nonreacting sample of mass mx​, initial temperature Tx​ and unknown specific heat capacity at constant pressure cx​, let receiving water have mass mw​, initial temperature Tw​ and known specific heat capacity at constant pressure cw​. Let the vessel and immersed parts start at Tw​ and have combined heat capacity Ccal​, excluding that water. Assume a common final temperature Tf​, approximately constant pressure and heat capacities, no phase transition, no chemical reaction and negligible external nonexpansion work. For signed outward heat loss Qloss,out​, the combined enthalpy balance is
mx​cx​(Tf​−Tx​)+(mw​cw​+Ccal​)(Tf​−Tw​)=−Qloss,out​.
(2)
Hence
cx​=mx​(Tx​−Tf​)(mw​cw​+Ccal​)(Tf​−Tw​)+Qloss,out​​.
(3)
The loss is positive if the room receives net heat, negative if the room supplies it. If the vessel starts at a different temperature Tcal,i​, replace its warming term by Ccal​(Tf​−Tcal,i​); do not assume the vessel shared the water's initial temperature. For variable material properties, replace each mcΔT by the corresponding integral m∫cp​(T)dT and each vessel term by ∫Ccal​(T)dT. The inferred constant cx​ then represents an average over the sample's cooling interval.[ref]
For a synthetic no-loss example, mix 0.100kg of hot water at 80∘C with 0.200kg of cold water at 20∘C in a vessel initially at 20∘C. Stipulate cw​=4180Jkg−1K−1 and Ccal​=100JK−1. The enthalpy balance gives
Tf​=418+836+100(418)(80)+(836+100)(20)​∘C≈38.523∘C.
(4)
The hot water releases 17.337kJ: the cold water receives 15.485kJ and the vessel 1.852kJ. Omitting the vessel predicts 40∘C, despite perfect insulation. These are computed numbers from stipulated properties, not laboratory observations.
For an unknown-sample example using the same cold water and vessel, stipulate mx​=0.100kg, Tx​=80∘C and cx​=450Jkg−1K−1. With no loss, Tf​≈22.752∘C. Inverting the full balance returns 450Jkg−1K−1; ignoring vessel storage returns about 402Jkg−1K−1. With a positive outward loss, ignoring that loss also biases the inferred cx​ downward under these hot-sample conditions. A small water temperature rise makes sensor offsets and loss estimates relatively influential; propagation of uncertainty must include the measured temperatures, masses and calibrated vessel term.
