Bilinear form (B:V×V→F)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 6k Articles: 47
A bilinear form takes two vectors in a vector space V over a field F and returns a scalar in F. Its defining property is separate linearity: holding either input fixed gives a linear functional of the other. For scalars a,b and vectors u,v,w,
B(au+bv,w)B(u,av+bw)​=aB(u,w)+bB(v,w),=aB(u,v)+bB(u,w).​
(1)
This is a scalar-valued bilinear map; the chosen field is part of the definition.[ref]
Separate linearity does not mean that B is a linear map on the vector space V×V with componentwise vector addition and scalar multiplication. Over the real numbers, B(2v,2w)=4B(v,w), whereas a linear map on the pair would give 2B(v,w). The simplest example is B(x,y)=xy on the one-dimensional vector space R: fixing y=3 gives x↦3x, but doubling both x and y quadruples their product.
A bilinear form measures how two inputs interact. The dot product measures alignment; a symmetric bilinear form can describe a quadratic form; an alternating bilinear form can measure signed area. A nondegenerate bilinear form turns each vector into a unique linear functional and so connects geometry with the dual space. The same short definition therefore supports lengths, bilinear orthogonal complements, and symplectic vector spaces, depending on the additional properties imposed.
Over the complex numbers, a bilinear form is linear in both inputs without conjugating either scalar. A Hermitian form instead is a sesquilinear form and conjugates when its inputs are exchanged. For complex matrices, the adjoint A∗ used with Hermitian forms and matrix C-star algebras is the conjugate transpose, whereas a bilinear form uses the ordinary transpose AT. For example, B(z,w)=zw has B(i,i)=−1, while the Hermitian form H(z,w)=zw has H(i,i)=1.[ref]

Matrix trace pairing (φ(A,B)=tr(AB))

Words: 77
Bilinear form OurBigBook.com
On square matrices over a field, the trace of a product is a symmetric bilinear form. Pairing with the matrix unit Eji​ reads off entry aij​, proving nondegeneracy. For real matrices of size at least two it is not positive-definite: a planar rotation block squares to −I2​ and has self-pairing −2. On real diagonalizable matrices its self-pairing is the sum of the squares of the real eigenvalues. This matrix pairing differs from the trace pairing of a field extension.

Matrix of a bilinear form (Aij​=B(ei​,ej​),B(v,w)=vTAw)

Words: 173
Bilinear form OurBigBook.com
Choose an ordered basis (e1​,…,en​) of a finite-dimensional vector space. The matrix of a bilinear form records all pairings of basis vectors, with Aij​=B(ei​,ej​). Writing v=∑i​vi​ei​ and w=∑j​wj​ej​, separate linearity gives
B(v,w)=∑i,j​vi​wj​B(ei​,ej​)=∑i,j​vi​Aij​wj​=vTAw,
(2)
where the last expression uses the coordinate vectors of v,w. Conversely, every matrix A defines a bilinear form by this formula.[ref]
For a concrete bilinear form on R2, take
A=(10​21​),B(v,w)=v1​w1​+2v1​w2​+v2​w2​.
(3)
Then B((1,0),(0,1))=2 but B((0,1),(1,0))=0: a bilinear form need not be a symmetric bilinear form. For v=(2,1)T and w=(−1,3)T, first compute Aw=(5,3)T, then vTAw=2⋅5+1⋅3=13. Reversing the inputs gives Av=(4,1)T and wTAv=−4+3=−1.
Under change of basis, let the columns of an invertible matrix P be the new basis vectors in old coordinates. Thus v=Pv′ and w=Pw′, so
B(v,w)=(Pv′)TA(Pw′)=(v′)T(PTAP)w′,A′=PTAP.
(4)
This is matrix congruence, rather than the matrix similarity P−1AP for an endomorphism. Both inputs have changed coordinates, which explains the two factors of P. For the displayed A and P=(10​11​), direct multiplication gives A′=(11​34​); its (1,2) entry is B(e1​,e1​+e2​)=3.[ref]

Symmetric and alternating parts of a bilinear form (B=S+K)

Words: 111
Bilinear form OurBigBook.com
Over a field whose characteristic is not two, a bilinear form has a unique decomposition into a symmetric bilinear form S and an alternating bilinear form K:
S(v,w)=2B(v,w)+B(w,v)​,K(v,w)=2B(v,w)−B(w,v)​.
(5)
Adding and subtracting the equations B(v,w)=S(v,w)+K(v,w) and B(w,v)=S(v,w)−K(v,w) both proves uniqueness and gives these formulas. In matrix notation, S has matrix (A+AT)/2 and K has skew-symmetric matrix (A−AT)/2.[ref]
The diagonal values q(v)=B(v,v) alone do not determine a general bilinear form, because K(v,v)=0 for every vector. For the matrix of a bilinear form in the example above,
A=symmetric part(11​11​)​​+alternating part(0−1​10​)​​,q(x,y)=(x+y)2.
(6)
Thus two different bilinear forms, represented by A and its symmetric matrix part, have exactly the same quadratic form. To recover a bilinear form from its diagonal, one needs extra information such as the hypothesis that it is a symmetric bilinear form.

Symmetric bilinear form (B(v,w)=B(w,v))

Words: 2k Articles: 8
Bilinear form OurBigBook.com
A bilinear form B is symmetric when B(v,w)=B(w,v) for every pair of vectors v,w. Equivalently, its matrix of a bilinear form in any basis is a symmetric matrix, since Aij​=B(ei​,ej​)=B(ej​,ei​)=Aji​. Symmetry makes the relation B(v,w)=0 reciprocal, giving the bilinear orthogonal complement a geometric interpretation.
Over the real numbers, set q(v)=B(v,v). The polarization identity
B(v,w)=21​(q(v+w)−q(v)−q(w))
(7)
recovers the symmetric bilinear form from its quadratic form: expand q(v+w) and use symmetry to combine the two cross terms. For q(x,y)=x2−y2, take v=(2,1) and w=(1,3). Then q(v+w)=−7, q(v)=3, and q(w)=−8, so B(v,w)=(−7−3+8)/2=−1=2⋅1−1⋅3. The same formula works whenever the characteristic is not two.[ref]
Three real diagonal matrices show how much variety symmetry allows. The identity matrix diag(1,1) gives the positive-definite bilinear form q=x2+y2. The matrix diag(1,−1) gives q=x2−y2 and is nondegenerate despite the isotropic vector (1,1). The matrix diag(1,0) gives q=x2 and is a degenerate bilinear form with radical of a bilinear form span{(0,1)}. Their level sets make the distinction visible: a circle, two branches of a hyperbola, and two parallel lines for q=1.
The inertia of a bilinear form records the numbers of positive, negative, and zero directions after matrix congruence diagonalization. By Sylvester's law of inertia, these counts survive change of basis; the three examples have inertia of a bilinear form (2,0,0), (1,1,0), and (1,0,1). Historically, Sylvester stated this invariance in an 1852 paper on reducing real quadratic forms to sums of positive and negative squares. Ghys and Ranicki reproduce his statement and distinguish it from his subsequent 1853 proof.[ref]

Positive-definite bilinear form (B(v,v)>0(v=0))

Words: 1k Articles: 5
Symmetric bilinear form OurBigBook.com
A real symmetric bilinear form is positive-definite when B(v,v)>0 for every nonzero vector. It defines an inner product and the associated norm B(v,v)​. Symmetry is part of this definition: positive diagonal values by themselves do not imply symmetry.[ref]
The identity matrix A=diag(1,1) gives B(v,w)=v1​w1​+v2​w2​ and B(v,v)=v12​+v22​. For v=(3,4) this is 25, giving norm 5. A weighted example, A=diag(4,1), gives norm 4x2+y2​: the first coordinate costs twice as much in length. Such inner products let geometry assign different weights to different directions.
Every positive-definite bilinear form is nondegenerate: a vector in its radical of a bilinear form has B(v,v)=0, so positivity forces v=0. The converse fails, as diag(1,−1) shows. Even positivity of the associated quadratic form does not force a general bilinear form to be symmetric: A=(1−1​11​) has vTAv=x2+y2 but B(e1​,e2​)=1 and B(e2​,e1​)=−1.
The complex Killing form is not positive-definite on the entire complex Cartan subalgebra; its restriction to the Euclidean subspace of a Cartan subalgebra is a real positive-definite form.

Positive definiteness (q(v)>0(v=0))

Words: 1k Articles: 4
Positive-definite bilinear form OurBigBook.com
The strict positivity of a quadratic form on every nonzero vector. A real symmetric matrix has positive definiteness exactly when its associated quadratic form does, making it a positive-definite matrix. A Hermitian form is positive definite when its value on (v,v) is real and strictly positive for every nonzero vector.
Think of a positive-definite quadratic form as an energy cost for a displacement: every direction costs something, and zero cost identifies the origin. For example, the real symmetric matrix
A=(31​13​),q(x,y)=3x2+2xy+3y2=3(x+3y​)2+38​y2
(8)
is a positive-definite matrix because the two squares can vanish together only at (0,0). The mixed term couples the coordinates, but cannot overwhelm their positive contributions: q(1,1)=8 and q(1,−1)=4. The graph is a bowl, and its positive level sets are ellipses with axes along the eigenvectors, rather than along the original coordinates.
The real and complex settings require different symmetry assumptions. For a real symmetric matrix use q(x)=xTAx with x∈Rn; for a complex Hermitian matrix use q(z)=z∗Az with z∈Cn, where z∗ is the conjugate transpose. The latter expression is real, so strict positivity is meaningful. A complex quadratic form such as z12​+z22​ has no such positivity: it vanishes at (1,i) and takes negative real values at (i,0). A Hermitian form uses complex conjugation, which changes this example to ∣z1​∣2+∣z2​∣2.
For a real matrix M without a symmetry hypothesis, its quadratic form depends only on its symmetric part of a matrix:
xTMx=xT2M+MT​x.
(9)
Positive eigenvalues of M alone do not establish positive definiteness of this quadratic form. For example, M=(10​41​) has both eigenvalues equal to 1, yet q(1,−1)=1−4+1=−2. The relevant test is positivity of the symmetric part of a matrix, not the spectrum of an arbitrary matrix.[ref]
Two-by-two positive-definiteness test (a>0,ac−b2>0)
Words: 206
Positive definiteness OurBigBook.com
For a real symmetric matrix A=(ab​bc​), completing the square gives, when a=0,
q(x,y)=ax2+2bxy+cy2=a(x+ab​y)2+aac−b2​y2.
(10)
Thus A is a positive-definite matrix exactly when a>0 and detA=ac−b2>0. For necessity, test (1,0) first, then (−b/a,1); for sufficiency, the displayed two positive squares give the result. These are the first two leading principal minors in Sylvester's criterion. A positive determinant alone is insufficient: −I2​ has determinant 1 but is a negative-definite matrix.
The eigenvalues provide an equivalent test under the same real-symmetric hypothesis:
λ±​=2a+c±(a−c)2+4b2​​,A positive definite ⟺ λ+​>0 and λ−​>0.
(11)
The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices supplies coordinates in an orthonormal basis in which q=λ+​u2+λ−​v2. More generally, a complex Hermitian matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis and q(z)=∑j​λj​∣wj​∣2, so it is a Hermitian positive-definite matrix exactly when every eigenvalue is positive. This is a short proof outline: test the eigenvectors for necessity, and sum the positive squared absolute values for sufficiency.[ref]
For a real positive semidefinite matrix, the condition is instead q(x,y)≥0 for all vectors, equivalent here to a≥0, c≥0, and ac−b2≥0. If a>0, the same completing the square proves this; if a=0, nonnegativity forces b=0 and leaves cy2 with c≥0. Merely replacing the strict inequalities on the leading principal minors by nonstrict ones fails: diag(0,−1) has both leading principal minors zero yet gives q(0,1)=−1. An indefinite quadratic form takes both positive and negative values; its real symmetric matrix has eigenvalues of both signs.
Positive-definiteness threshold in a two-by-two family (At​=(3t​t3​),∣t∣<3)

Positive definiteness OurBigBook.com
Vary the coupling in the opening example. The eigenvectors e+​=(1,1)T/2​ and e−​=(1,−1)T/2​ form a fixed orthonormal eigenbasis, with eigenvalues 3+t and 3−t. Writing a vector as ue+​+ve−​ gives
qt​=3x2+2txy+3y2=(3+t)u2+(3−t)v2.
(12)
For ∣t∣<3, both directions cost energy and At​ is a positive-definite matrix. At t=3, the quadratic form is 6u2: the entire line spanned by e−​ has zero energy, and At​ is a singular positive semidefinite matrix. At t=−3, the flat line is instead spanned by e+​. For ∣t∣>3, the quadratic form is an indefinite quadratic form; one direction lowers the energy without bound as its displacement grows. At t=4, for instance, qt​(e+​)=7 while qt​(e−​)=−1.
The geometric transition is precise. For ∣t∣<3, qt​=1 is an ellipse. At t=3, it consists of the two parallel lines u=±1/6​, extending freely in the zero-energy direction. For t>3, it is a hyperbola. The boundary case retains nonnegative energy but loses both the bounded positive level sets and the unique zero-energy vector.
Ellipsoid level sets of a positive-definite quadratic form (q(x)=k>0,rj​=k/λj​​)
Words: 189
Positive definiteness OurBigBook.com
For a real positive-definite matrix A in finite dimension, write A=QΛQT by the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices. In the coordinates in an orthonormal basis w=QTx, the positive level set q(x)=k becomes
∑j=1n​λj​wj2​=k,∑j=1n​k/λj​wj2​​=1.
(13)
This is an ellipsoid, with semi-major and semi-minor axes in two dimensions forming an ellipse; each semi-axis has length rj​=k/λj​​ and direction given by the corresponding eigenvector. In the opening example, the shorter semi-axis is k​/2 along e+​, while the longer is k/2​ along e−​. A stiffer direction, meaning a larger eigenvalue, reaches the same energy at a smaller displacement. The ratio of longest to shortest semi-axis is λmax​/λmin​​, the square root of the spectral condition number of a positive-definite matrix.[ref]
Strict positivity matters because λmin​>0 gives
λmin​∥x∥2≤q(x)≤λmax​∥x∥2.
(14)
Hence a fixed positive level set cannot extend arbitrarily far. The Cholesky decomposition A=LLT gives another view: q(x)=∥LTx∥2, so the ellipsoid is the inverse image of a sphere under an invertible linear map. A singular positive semidefinite matrix has a nonzero kernel; adding a kernel vector preserves its quadratic form, so any nonempty positive level set is unbounded. These statements concern finite dimension; the positive definite symmetric operator entry explains why strict positivity alone need not give a uniform lower bound on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space.
Unique minimizer of a positive-definite quadratic function (f(x)=21​xTAx−bTx+c,x∗​=A−1b)
Words: 217
Positive definiteness OurBigBook.com
Let A be a real positive-definite matrix and minimize the displayed quadratic function over all of Rn. Its gradient is Ax−b, so its only stationary point solves the linear system Ax∗​=b. The matrix is an invertible matrix, making this solution exist and be unique. Completing the square around that solution proves the stronger global statement:
f(x)=c−21​bTA−1b+21​(x−x∗​)TA(x−x∗​),f(x)−f(x∗​)>0(x=x∗​).
(15)
The quadratic function is strictly convex, and its level sets above the minimum are translated ellipsoids. Positivity therefore turns solving a linear system into locating the bottom of a bowl.[ref]
For the opening matrix A=(31​13​), take b=(1,1)T and c=0. Then A−1=81​(3−1​−13​), x∗​=(1/4,1/4)T, and f(x∗​)=−1/4. Every displacement h raises the value by 21​hTAh; along the unit eigenvectors e+​ and e−​ the increases are respectively 2s2 and s2 for displacement se+​ or se−​.
For a singular positive semidefinite matrix, a solution of Ax=b is still a global minimizer, but adding any kernel vector gives another. If b is outside the image of a linear map defined by A, some kernel vector v has bTv=0, and f(sv)=c−sbTv is unbounded below. For an indefinite quadratic form, a negative eigenvalue gives a direction along which the quadratic term tends to −∞ regardless of b. Thus a stationary point alone does not establish a minimum. The same energy-difference identity extends to the quadratic variational principle for a symmetric positive operator, with additional existence hypotheses in infinite dimension.

On real n by n matrices with n≥2, split into traceless symmetric matrices, skew-symmetric matrices and scalar matrices. These summands are orthogonal for the displayed symmetric bilinear form. The form is positive on the first summand, negative on the second and negative on the scalar identity direction. Its inertia of a bilinear form is (n(n+1)/2−1,n(n−1)/2+1,0), its rank is n2 and its signature is n−2. Indeed tr(S2)=∥S∥F2​, tr(K2)=−∥K∥F2​ and ϕ(tI,tI)=−n(n−1)t2.

Witt group of a field (W(K))

Words: 49
Symmetric bilinear form OurBigBook.com
For a field K of characteristic different from two, W(K) is the group of nonsingular symmetric bilinear forms modulo metabolic forms, with orthogonal sum as addition. A metabolic form has a half-dimensional totally isotropic subspace. Such a form is equivalent to a sum of hyperbolic planes, hence represents zero.

Alternating bilinear form (B(v,v)=0)

Words: 190
Bilinear form OurBigBook.com
A bilinear form is alternating if B(v,v)=0 for every vector v. Expanding B(v+w,v+w)=0 gives B(v,w)+B(w,v)=0, hence B(v,w)=−B(w,v). If the characteristic is not two, the converse follows from B(v,v)=−B(v,v) by dividing by two. In characteristic two, the relation B(v,w)=−B(w,v) is just symmetry and does not force zero self-pairings: B(x,y)=xy on F2​ has B(1,1)=1.[ref]
The matrix
J=(0−1​10​),B(v,w)=vTJw=v1​w2​−v2​w1​=det(v1​v2​​w1​w2​​)
(16)
gives an alternating bilinear form over every field. Over the real numbers, its value is the signed area of the parallelogram with sides v,w: B((2,1),(1,3))=6−1=5, and swapping the sides gives −5. Every self-pairing is zero, yet detJ=1, so this bilinear form is nondegenerate. Directly, pairing (x,y) with (1,0) gives −y, and pairing it with (0,1) gives x; both vanish only when (x,y)=0.
In characteristic different from two, a skew-symmetric matrix of odd integer size has zero determinant, since detA=det(−AT)=(−1)ndetA. Thus an alternating bilinear form on a finite odd-dimensional vector space is a degenerate bilinear form. The stronger statement that the rank of an alternating bilinear form is even holds in all characteristics: splitting off a two-dimensional block with matrix J repeatedly proves it. A nondegenerate alternating bilinear form defines a symplectic vector space, the linear setting for signed area rather than squared length.

Orthogonal complement for a bilinear form (W⊥={v:B(w,v)=0 for all w∈W})

Words: 147 Articles: 1
Bilinear form OurBigBook.com
For a bilinear form on a finite-dimensional vector space, the displayed right bilinear orthogonal complement is a subspace. Nondegeneracy identifies the ambient space with its dual and gives dimW⊥=dimV−dimW. If the form is symmetric or alternating, orthogonality is reciprocal and (W⊥)⊥=W. For a general nonsymmetric form, left and right complements must be distinguished. A restricted symmetric or alternating form has radical of a bilinear form W∩W⊥; quotienting by that radical of a bilinear form gives a nondegenerate induced form.

Orthogonal complement dimension for a degenerate bilinear form (dimU+dimU⊥=dimV+dim(U∩kerΦ))

For a bilinear form B on a finite-dimensional vector space V, let Φ:V→V∗ send u to B(u,⋅). Under the bidual evaluation map, U⊥ is the annihilator of a vector subspace im(Φ∣U​), so dimU⊥=dimV−rank(Φ∣U​). The rank-nullity theorem on U now gives the displayed equality. In particular the sum of dimensions is at least dimV. If U∩U⊥=0, the dimension formula for a sum of subspaces forces V=U⊕U⊥. The exact formula records the extra dimensions contributed by the left radical kerΦ, and does not assume an inner product.

Isometry of a space with a form

Words: 150 Articles: 2
Bilinear form OurBigBook.com
A linear bijection between spaces carrying compatible bilinear forms, Hermitian forms or quadratic forms is an isometry of a space with a form when it preserves the specified form. Preserving the polar form alone need not preserve a quadratic form in characteristic two. Subspaces may have degenerate restricted forms even when the ambient form is nonsingular.

Anisotropic vector for a form

Words: 49

A vector is anisotropic for a quadratic form if Q(v)=0, and for a Hermitian form if h(v,v)=0. The complementary zero-value condition is isotropy. For a symmetric bilinear form in odd characteristic of a field, this agrees with the associated quadratic form. A nondegenerate alternating bilinear form has all self-pairings zero, so nondegeneracy must not be confused with the existence of anisotropic vectors.

Witt's theorem

Words: 59
Isometry of a space with a form OurBigBook.com
An isometry of a space with a form between subspaces of a finite-dimensional nonsingular classical space extends to an ambient isometry. This includes alternating bilinear forms in all characteristics, symmetric bilinear forms in characteristic different from two, Hermitian forms with nontrivial involution, and quadratic forms with nonsingular polar form. No nonsingularity of the restricted subspace is required. Bare nonalternating symmetric bilinear forms in characteristic two require additional hypotheses and must not be confused with quadratic spaces.

Bounded bilinear form (∣B(u,v)∣≤C∥u∥∥v∥)

Words: 619 Articles: 5
Bilinear form OurBigBook.com
A bilinear form B:X×X→R on a real normed vector space is bounded when one constant 0≤C<∞ satisfies
∣B(u,v)∣≤C∥u∥∥v∥(u,v∈X).
(17)
This is an upper bound on the magnitude of every pairing. It says nothing about the sign of B(v,v) and does not require a symmetric bilinear form. The chosen norm is part of the hypothesis. Boundedness is equivalent to joint continuity, as shown by the boundedness criterion for a bilinear form.[ref]
For example, on R2 with the Euclidean norm, the bilinear forms
B−​(u,v)=u1​v1​−u2​v2​,B0​(u,v)=u1​v1​
(18)
both satisfy the upper bound with C=1, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Yet their quadratic forms are B−​(v,v)=x2−y2 and B0​(v,v)=x2: the former takes negative values and the latter vanishes at the nonzero vector (0,1). Thus neither is a coercive bilinear form. The latter is also a degenerate bilinear form, whereas B−​ is nondegenerate. Boundedness controls size, not positivity or loss of directions.
Every bilinear form on a real or complex finite-dimensional vector space equipped with any norm is bounded. In coordinates, write B(u,v)=uTAv using its matrix of a bilinear form. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives ∣uTAv∣≤∥A∥2​∥u∥2​∥v∥2​, with ∥A∥2​ the spectral norm; finite-dimensional equivalence of norms transfers this to the chosen norm. The conclusion concerns each fixed norm and fixed bilinear form: it does not supply a single constant for an arbitrary family of matrices or changing dimensions.
On an infinite-dimensional vector space, the upper bound is an additional condition. Equip the finitely supported sequences c00​ with the norm inherited from the l2 sequence space and set
B(u,v)=∑n≥1​nun​vn​.
(19)
Each pairing is a finite sum, so this is a well-defined bilinear form. For the basis vector en​, however, ∥en​∥2​=1 and B(en​,en​)=n; no finite C works. It is even a coercive bilinear form with α=1, since ∑nvn2​≥∑vn2​. Consequently coercivity alone does not imply boundedness on a normed vector space. This example uses c00​, which is not a Hilbert space; it does not define this pairing on all of the l2 sequence space.
A bounded bilinear form defines a bounded linear operator T:X→X′ by (Tu)(v)=B(u,v), where X′ is the continuous dual space. Its operator norm is the smallest admissible C:
∥T∥=supu=0,v=0​∥u∥∥v∥∣B(u,v)∣​.
(20)
This converts an upper bound for pairings into an upper bound for a linear map taking an unknown vector to a linear functional.

A real bilinear form B on a normed vector space X is coercive when there is one constant α>0 such that
B(v,v)≥α∥v∥2(v∈X).
(21)
The lower bound uniformly controls every direction in the chosen norm. Unlike the upper bound defining a bounded bilinear form, it forces positive diagonal values at every nonzero vector. No symmetry is assumed. For complex Hilbert spaces, the usual analogue uses a sesquilinear form and ReB(v,v)≥α∥v∥2; it is not the same assertion for a complex bilinear form.
A concrete bounded and coercive example on R2, with the Euclidean norm, is
A=(2−1​13​),B(u,v)=uTAv.
(22)

(23)
The best lower constant is α=2, attained at (1,0). Nevertheless, B(e1​,e2​)=1 and B(e2​,e1​)=−1, so coercivity does not imply a symmetric bilinear form. The symmetric part of a matrix S=(A+AT)/2=diag(2,3) determines q, while the skew-symmetric matrix part changes mixed pairings without changing q. This is the symmetric and alternating parts of a bilinear form decomposition.[ref]
For the upper constant, direct multiplication gives ATA=(5−1​−110​). Its eigenvalues are (15±29​)/2, so the optimal upper constant, the spectral norm, is C=(15+29​)/2​. The two constants answer different questions: how large a mixed pairing can be, and how small a self-pairing can be relative to squared norm.
The plotted quadratic forms show the mechanism: x2−y2 descends in one direction, x2 leaves one direction uncontrolled, and 2x2+3y2 controls both. These are self-pairings; their level sets cannot reveal the skew-symmetric matrix part. Calling q a quadratic energy here refers to this diagnostic quantity, not to a claim that a nonsymmetric equation comes from minimizing an energy functional.

Indeed the quadratic form is continuous, and its positive minimum on the compact unit sphere supplies α. If B is also a symmetric bilinear form, this is positive definiteness. For general B, it is the symmetric and alternating parts of a bilinear form decomposition that isolates the positive part. Switching between equivalent norms preserves coercivity, but usually changes its constant.
The infinite-dimensional vector space distinction is explicit on the real l2 sequence space:
B(u,v)=∑n=1∞​nun​vn​​.
(24)

On the span of e1​,…,eN​, the best lower constant is 1/N: each finite restriction is coercive, but the control disappears as N grows. Strict positivity is therefore weaker than a uniform lower bound.
The practical payoff is a stability estimate for a weak formulation. (25)

Thus a positive lower constant limits the response to load errors. The upper bound and completeness of the Hilbert space enter the existence theorem; the estimate itself uses the lower bound and the load's dual norm.

Radical of a bilinear form (radB)

radB={v∈V:B(v,w)=0 for every w∈V}.
(27)

(28)

Symplectic vector space

Words: 1k Articles: 11

A symplectic vector space is a vector space equipped with a nondegenerate bilinear form ω that is an alternating bilinear form: ω(v,v)=0 for every vector v. This implies ω(v,w)=−ω(w,v). When the characteristic is two, that latter equation alone does not imply the alternating condition. A symplectic vector space of finite dimension has even dimension: splitting off a hyperbolic pair leaves a smaller nondegenerate bilinear form, and repeating gives a decomposition into two-dimensional planes.

A symplectic basis organizes a nondegenerate alternating bilinear form into independent pairs. Let (V,ω) be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field K, with ω a nondegenerate bilinear form satisfying ω(v,v)=0 for every vector v.
