An A-module is completely reducible if it is a direct sum of modules each of which is a simple module:
V=⨁j∈J​Sj​.
(1)
Here a simple module is nonzero and has no submodules other than zero and itself. This is precisely the definition of a semisimple module. The indexing set need not be finite unless V has finite dimension; the zero module is included as the empty direct sum of modules.

ii OurBigBook.com
Construct the sum successively, retaining a simple module only when it adds a new direct summand. Suppose S=V1​+⋯+Vj−1​ is already expressed as a direct sum of modules selected from these simple modules. The intersection S∩Vj​ is a submodule of the simple module Vj​, so it is either zero or all of Vj​. In the latter case S+Vj​=S. In the former case
S+Vj​=S⊕Vj​.
(2)
Starting with S=0 and proceeding by mathematical induction gives a direct sum of modules equal to V1​+⋯+Vn​. Thus the sum is a semisimple module. The argument proves the useful finite sum of simple submodules lemma without claiming that the originally listed submodules are themselves independent.

Since A is a nonzero associative algebra of finite dimension, it has a nonzero left ideal I of least positive dimension. Such a minimal left ideal is a simple module under left multiplication.
For a∈A, right multiplication defines the module homomorphism
Ra​:I⟶A,x⟼xa.
(3)
Its kernel is a submodule of I, so its image Ia is either zero or a simple module isomorphic to I. Each Ia is a left ideal, since c(xa)=(cx)a.
Consider
J=∑a∈A​Ia.
(4)
It is a left ideal. It is also stable under right multiplication, because (Ia)b=I(ab), so it is a two-sided ideal. Moreover I=I1⊆J, giving J=0. The assumption that A is a simple ring therefore forces J=A.
Only finitely many summands are needed: express each member of a finite basis of A as a finite sum of elements of the Ia, and collect the finitely many indices used. After discarding zero summands, A is a finite sum of simple modules. Part (a)(ii) makes its left regular module a semisimple module. This is exactly the definition of a semisimple algebra, proving that a finite-dimensional simple algebra is semisimple. The finite dimension assumption is essential to this assertion about a simple ring.

Use the left A-module structure c⋅(a⊗b)=ca⊗b on the tensor product of associative algebras. Expanding each b∈B in the basis b1​,…,bm​ shows that every tensor can be written
x=∑j=1m​aj​⊗bj​=∑j=1m​aj​⋅(1⊗bj​).
(5)
Thus the displayed family generates the module.
For uniqueness, let βj​:B→k be the corresponding dual basis, with βj​(bi​)=δij​. Applying the linear map idA​⊗βj​ to a linear relation ∑i​ai​⊗bi​=0 gives aj​=0. Hence the family is linearly independent in a module, and
A⊗k​B≅A⊕mas left A-modules.​
(6)
In particular 1⊗b1​,…,1⊗bm​ is a basis of a module, with coefficients uniquely in A.

ii OurBigBook.com
Write x∈Z(A⊗k​B) uniquely as x=∑j​aj​⊗bj​ using part (i). For each a∈A, the equality (a⊗1)x=x(a⊗1) becomes
∑j​(aaj​−aj​a)⊗bj​=0.
(7)
The coefficient uniqueness from part (i) gives aaj​=aj​a for every a, so each aj​ belongs to the center of an associative algebra Z(A). Consequently x∈Z(A)⊗k​B.
Choose a basis z1​,…,zt​ of the vector space Z(A) and rewrite x=∑i=1t​zi​⊗di​. For b∈B, commuting with 1⊗b gives
∑i​zi​⊗(bdi​−di​b)=0.
(8)
Extend the linearly independent family zi​ to a basis of A and apply its dual basis to the first factor. It follows that bdi​=di​b for every b, hence di​∈Z(B). This proves Z(A⊗k​B)⊆Z(A)⊗k​Z(B).
Conversely, if z∈Z(A) and w∈Z(B), then
(z⊗w)(a⊗b)=za⊗wb=az⊗bw=(a⊗b)(z⊗w).
(9)
The pure tensors a⊗b span the tensor product, so z⊗w is central, as is every sum of such pure tensors. Therefore the center of a tensor product of associative algebras is
Z(A⊗k​B)=Z(A)⊗k​Z(B).​
(10)
These tensor products are identified with their natural vector subspaces of A⊗k​B; extending bases justifies these embeddings over the field k.

Let J=0 be a two-sided ideal of A⊗k​B. Choose a nonzero x∈J of least possible two-factor tensor rank n, and take a shortest expression
x=∑i=1n​ai​⊗bi​.
(11)
Both families (ai​) and (bi​) are linearly independent: a linear dependence in either family would combine terms and shorten the expression. In particular a1​,b1​=0.
Because A is a simple ring, the two-sided ideal generated by a1​ is A. Thus there are finitely many uj​,vj​∈A with ∑j​uj​a1​vj​=1. Form
y=∑j​(uj​⊗1)x(vj​⊗1)=∑i=1n​ci​⊗bi​∈J,ci​=∑j​uj​ai​vj​,c1​=1.
(12)
Since the bi​ are linearly independent, applying a linear functional selecting b1​ shows y=0. Its two-factor tensor rank is at most n and hence, by the choice of x, exactly n. Consequently c1​=1,c2​,…,cn​ are linearly independent.
Similarly, simplicity of B gives finitely many pj​,qj​∈B with ∑j​pj​b1​qj​=1. Now
z=∑j​(1⊗pj​)y(1⊗qj​)=∑i=1n​ci​⊗di​∈J,di​=∑j​pj​bi​qj​,d1​=1.
(13)
The linear independence of the ci​ shows z=0, and minimality again makes its two-factor tensor rank exactly n. We have obtained
z=1⊗1+i=2∑n​ci​⊗di​∈J∖{0}.​
(14)
Both coefficient families in this expression are linearly independent. Retaining this additional property is the key to the next part. This is coefficient normalization in an ideal of a tensor product.

ii OurBigBook.com
Keep the nonzero z of minimal two-factor tensor rank constructed in part (i). For every a∈A, its commutator with a⊗1 lies in J and equals
(a⊗1)z−z(a⊗1)=∑i=2n​(aci​−ci​a)⊗di​.
(15)
If this tensor were nonzero, its two-factor tensor rank would be at most n−1, contradicting minimality. Thus it is zero. The linear independence of d2​,…,dn​ forces aci​=ci​a for every a and i≥2. Since Z(A)=k, each ci​ is a scalar multiple of 1.
But 1,c2​,…,cn​ are linearly independent. Hence n=1, so z=1⊗1∈J. A two-sided ideal containing the multiplicative identity is the entire ring; therefore J=A⊗k​B.
We have proved the stronger result that A⊗k​B is a simple ring. It has finite dimension (dimk​A)(dimk​B), so part (b) proves
A⊗k​B is a semisimple ring.​
(16)
Part (c)(ii) also gives Z(A⊗k​B)=k, so this proves that a tensor product of central simple algebras is again a central simple algebra.

The character formula for an induced representation is
χIndHG​W​(g)=∣H∣1​x∈Gx−1gx∈H​∑​χW​(x−1gx).​
(17)
Equivalently, extend the character χW​ by zero outside the subgroup H and sum over all x∈G. The factor 1/∣H∣ accounts for passing from elements of G to representatives of left cosets of H.

For each h∈gG∩H, count the solutions of x−1gx=h. Once a solution x0​ is fixed, every solution is uniquely cx0​ with c∈CG​(g), the centralizer of g. Thus each h contributes ∣CG​(g)∣χW​(h) to the sum in part (a).
The orbit-stabilizer theorem for the conjugation action gives ∣CG​(g)∣=∣G∣/∣gG∣. The intersection gG∩H is the disjoint union of the conjugacy classes h1H​,…,hℓH​, and the character χW​ is constant on each such conjugacy class. Hence
χV​(g)=∣H∣∣CG​(g)∣​∑h∈gG∩H​χW​(h)=∣H∣∣G∣​i=1∑ℓ​∣gG∣∣hiH​∣​χW​(hi​)​.
(18)
If the intersection is empty, the empty sum gives zero. This is the conjugacy-class formula for an induced character.

Order the conjugacy classes of the symmetric group S4​ by representatives 1,(12),(12)(34),(123),(1234); their sizes are 1,6,3,8,6. For a three-dimensional rotation through an angle θ, the eigenvalues are 1,eiθ,e−iθ, so the trace of a three-dimensional rotation is 1+2cosθ.
Under the identification with the rotational symmetry group of a cube, elements of order two give half-turns, elements of order three give turns through ±2π/3, and elements of order four give turns through ±π/2. A half-turn can have an axis through opposite edges or opposite faces, but both kinds have trace −1. Thus
representativeclass sizeχC​​113​(12)6−1​(12)(34)3−1​(123)80​(1234)61​​.​
(19)
Its character inner product with itself is
⟨χC​,χC​⟩S4​​=249+6+3+0+6​=1.
(20)
By character orthogonality, a nonzero complex representation has character inner product one with itself exactly when it is an irreducible representation. Therefore C is an irreducible representation of dimension three.
For comparison useful in part (e), the standard representation of the symmetric group has character equal to the number of fixed letters minus one, namely (3,1,−1,0,−1) in this order. Multiplication by the sign representation changes this character to (3,−1,−1,0,1). Thus C is the tensor product of the standard representation of the symmetric group with the sign representation.

The given permutations satisfy r4=s2=1 and srs=r−1. The eight elements rj,rjs for 0≤j<4 are distinct: s∈/⟨r⟩, whose only element of order two is r2=(13)(24). Thus H is the order-eight dihedral group, and we may use the matrices
R=σ(r)=(01​−10​),S=σ(s)=(10​0−1​).
(21)
They obey the same relations and describe a quarter-turn and a reflection of a square.
The eigenvalues of R over the complex numbers are i and −i, with respective eigenlines spanned by (1,−i) and (1,i). Since S interchanges these eigenlines, neither is an invariant subspace for H. Any proper nonzero subrepresentation of this two-dimensional complex representation would be a line invariant under both R and S, and an R-invariant line must be one of those eigenlines. None qualifies, so W is irreducible.
For the calculation in part (e), its character is
χW​(1)=2,χW​(r2)=−2,χW​(r)=χW​(r3)=χW​(rjs)=0(0≤j<4).
(22)
Indeed R2=−I, while a quarter-turn or a reflection has trace zero. The character inner product (22+(−2)2)/8=1 provides a second irreducibility check.

a OurBigBook.com
Let ρ afford the character χ, and let m be the order of a group element g. Then ρ(g)m=I, so its minimal polynomial divides Tm−1. Over the complex numbers this polynomial has distinct polynomial roots, since its formal derivative mTm−1 does not vanish at any of its polynomial roots. Thus ρ(g) is diagonalizable, with eigenvalues λ1​,…,λd​ that are roots of unity.
Each λj​ has absolute value one, so λj−1​=λj​​. The eigenvalues of ρ(g−1)=ρ(g)−1 are λj−1​, giving
χ(g−1)=j∑​λj−1​=j∑​λj​​=χ(g)​.​
(29)
This is the character inversion identity.

b OurBigBook.com
On the complex vector space CF(G) of class functions, define the linear operator
Tf(g)=f(g−1).
(30)
This is a complex-linear map and satisfies T2=I. It is well-defined on class functions, because inversion of conjugacy classes takes an entire conjugacy class K to another conjugacy class K−1.
Use first the basis consisting of the indicator functions 1K​ of the conjugacy classes. The linear operator T permutes this basis by 1K​↦1K−1​. A permutation matrix has trace equal to the number of fixed basis vectors, so
trT=#{K:K=K−1}.
(31)
The right-hand side counts the real conjugacy classes.
By character orthogonality and completeness, the irreducible characters are another basis of CF(G). Part (a) gives Tχ=χ​. The pointwise complex conjugate of an irreducible character is again an irreducible character: conjugate the representing matrices, and conjugate any hypothetical proper invariant subspace back to contradict irreducibility. Thus T also permutes this basis, and
trT=#{χ∈Irr(G):χ=χ​}.
(32)
Here χ=χ​ means exactly that χ is a real-valued character. Equality of the trace in these two bases proves
#{real-valued irreducible characters}=#{real conjugacy classes}.​
(33)
This is the real character and real conjugacy class counting theorem. The linear operator used in the proof is f↦f∘inv; on the irreducible character basis it agrees with pointwise complex conjugation, although pointwise complex conjugation itself is not a complex-linear map on all class functions.

Write N=⟨a⟩. The relation bab−1=a2 lets every element be expressed as aibj with 0≤i<7 and 0≤j<3. There are 21 such expressions, so the assumption ∣G∣=21 makes them distinct. In particular a has order seven, b has order three, and N is a normal subgroup. This is the nonabelian group of order 21, the semidirect product C7​⋊C3​ with the action given by multiplication by two modulo seven.
Conjugation by a fixes N, while conjugation by b sends ai to a2i. The nonidentity conjugacy classes inside N are therefore
K1​={a,a2,a4},K2​={a3,a5,a6}.
(34)
For the other elements, direct calculation gives
at(aibj)a−t=ai+(1−2j)tbj.
(35)
When j=1 or 2, the coefficient 1−2j is nonzero modulo seven, so letting t vary gives every member of the coset Nbj. These cosets cannot merge under conjugation, since G/N is an abelian group and conjugation acts trivially on that quotient group. Thus the five conjugacy classes have representatives and sizes
representativeclass size​11​a3​a33​b7​b27​​.
(36)
To find the linear characters, note that the commutator subgroup G′ contains bab−1a−1=a. The quotient group G/N≅C3​ is abelian, so G′⊆N; hence G′=N. The abelianization is C3​. With ω=e2πi/3, its three linear characters are
λj​(a)=1,λj​(b)=ωj,j=0,1,2.
(37)
This exhausts the linear characters because one-dimensional characters factor through the abelianization.
For the remaining irreducible characters, put ζ=e2πi/7 and take the linear characters θt​ of N defined by θt​(a)=ζt. The six nontrivial linear characters form two orbits under conjugation, with exponent sets {1,2,4} and {3,5,6}. Define the induced characters
ψ=IndNG​θ1​,ψ​=IndNG​θ3​.
(38)
For ai∈N, the character formula for an induced representation gives
ψ(ai)=ζi+ζ2i+ζ4i,ψ​(ai)=ζ3i+ζ5i+ζ6i.
(39)
Both vanish outside N, since no element outside a normal subgroup can be conjugate into it. This is induction from an abelian normal subgroup with a free character orbit. For completeness, its irreducibility here follows directly from Frobenius reciprocity and character orthogonality on the cyclic group N:
⟨ψ,ψ⟩G​=⟨θ1​,ResNG​ψ⟩N​=⟨θ1​,θ1​+θ2​+θ4​⟩N​=1.
(40)
The same calculation with θ3​,θ5​,θ6​ proves that ψ​ is an irreducible character.
Let
α=ζ+ζ2+ζ4,β=ζ3+ζ5+ζ6=α.
(41)
The sum of the nontrivial seventh roots of unity gives α+β=−1. Squaring and reducing exponents modulo seven gives
α2=α+2β=−α−2.
(42)
Thus α,β are the two polynomial roots of T2+T+2, and
α=2−1+i7​​,β=2−1−i7​​.
(43)
To fix the signs with our specified ζ, observe that Imα=sin(2π/7)+sin(3π/7)−sin(π/7)>0, using the increase of sine on (0,π/2). These Gaussian periods for seventh roots of unity make the answer explicit.
The resulting character table is
class sizeλ0​λ1​λ2​ψψ​​1111133​a3111αβ​a33111βα​b71ωω200​b271ω2ω00​​.​
(44)
The two degree-three irreducible characters are distinct because α=β. Together with the three linear characters, they exhaust the irreducible characters: the sum of squares of irreducible degrees is 3⋅12+2⋅32=21=∣G∣. Equivalently, we have five distinct irreducible characters for the five conjugacy classes.
As a check on part (b), inversion interchanges K1​ and K2​, and also interchanges the two conjugacy classes outside N. Only the identity conjugacy class is real. Correspondingly λ0​ is the only real-valued character among the irreducible characters; λ1​,λ2​ and ψ,ψ​ are two conjugate pairs.
