Transcription (biology)

 Home Biology Genetics Molecular genetics Gene expression
OurBigBook.com Words: 304 Articles: 2
Transcription is synthesis of RNA using one DNA strand as a template by an RNA polymerase. During elongation the RNA polymerase reads that template 3′→5′ while extending RNA 5′→3′. Only the selected region is transcribed; other regions need not be active, and the template strand can differ between genes. See Alberts et al.: RNA polymerase and selection of transcribed DNA regions.
For the displayed example, align these three nucleotide sequences in the same left-to-right positions:
coding DNA:template DNA:RNA product:​5′-ATGGCTTAA-3′,3′-TACCGAATT-5′,5′-AUGGCUUAA-3′.​
(104)
The first template triplet TAC selects AUG: T pairs with A, A with U, and C with G. The next CGA selects GCU, and ATT selects UAA. The coding strand therefore matches the RNA lettering except for T versus U, whereas the template is complementary. Reading that template in its own 5′→3′ direction gives 5′-TTAAGCCAT-3′; orientation cannot be discarded. The gene expression diagram keeps all three rows aligned. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: template and coding strands in transcription.
This correspondence makes nucleotide sequences interpretable, but replacing T by U is valid only after identifying the coding strand. Moreover, RNA may be processed after transcription, so the initial product and mature messenger RNA need not have identical uninterrupted nucleotide sequences. Transcription also makes functional RNA that will not undergo biological translation. See Alberts et al.: RNA processing and RNA as the final gene product.
