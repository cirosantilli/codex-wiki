Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem (xnj​​→ℓ)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Sequence and series
OurBigBook.com Words: 716 Articles: 6
Every bounded sequence of real numbers contains a subsequence with a finite limit of a sequence. If ∣xn​∣≤M for every positive integer n, there are integers n1​<n2​<⋯ and a real number ℓ such that
limj→∞​xnj​​=ℓ.
(37)
The increasing indices matter: a subsequence retains the original order and selects terms arbitrarily far along the original sequence. The theorem guarantees the existence of at least one such selection; it does not claim that the entire sequence converges or that the subsequential limit is unique.[ref]
For example, the bounded sequence xn​=(−1)n+1/(n+1) has two distinct subsequential limits:
x2j​=1+2j+11​⟶1,x2j−1​=−1+2j1​⟶−1.
(38)
The even terms form a convergent sequence, and so do the odd terms. The full sequence cannot have a limit of a sequence because its two subsequences have different limits. Its limit superior is 1 and its limit inferior is −1.
This is useful when a construction produces many candidate points but does not immediately produce a convergent sequence. For a continuous function on a nonempty closed bounded feasible region in a finite-dimensional Euclidean space, take a sequence whose objective values approach the infimum. A convergent subsequence supplies a candidate point; a closed subset retains that point, and continuity passes the objective values to the limit of a sequence. This is the mechanism behind attainment in the extreme value theorem. The diagonal subsequence argument extends the real-sequence theorem to finite-dimensional Euclidean space.
The theorem is named for Bernard Bolzano and Karl Weierstrass.[ref]
