Optimal fixed step for quadratic gradient descent (η∗​=2/(λmin​+λmax​))

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis Gradient descent Fixed-step gradient descent on a positive-definite quadratic
OurBigBook.com Words: 257
For a fixed symmetric matrix and positive-definite matrix A, what positive step size minimizes the largest coordinate error multiplier over all eigenvector directions? Let m=λmin​>0 and L=λmax​. Since ∣1−ηλ∣ is a convex function of λ, its maximum over the eigenvalues occurs at an endpoint:
ρ(η)=max{∣1−ηm∣,∣1−ηL∣}.
(9)
Balance the two extremes by solving 1−ηm=−(1−ηL):
η∗​=m+L2​,ρ∗​=L+mL−m​=κ+1κ−1​,κ=mL​.
(10)
Here κ is the Euclidean norm condition number of A. This balance is optimal: for η<η∗​ the endpoint 1−ηm exceeds ρ∗​, and for η>η∗​ the endpoint ηL−1 exceeds ρ∗​. The optimal worst-case energy factor is ρ∗2​, rather than ρ∗​. This optimizes a uniform one-step bound; a particular initial error supported on fewer eigenvectors can have a different best step size.
In the example m=1, L=9, so η∗​=0.2 and ρ∗2​=0.64. Both eigenvalue directions attain that energy factor, making the displayed history exact. The smaller step size 0.1 instead gives
Ek​=2(0.81)k+29​(0.01)k.
(11)
Its first step reaches E1​=1.665, below 4.16 for η=0.2, but subsequent progress is limited by the first direction's energy factor 0.81. By k=5, the energies are approximately 0.69736 and 0.69793, respectively; by k=6, the 0.2 iteration is ahead. A better first step need not yield a faster long-run history.
As the condition number grows, ρ∗​ approaches one: stability in the direction of the largest eigenvalue forces small movement along the smallest-eigenvalue direction. This is a precise reason to improve scaling or use a method such as the Conjugate gradient method, rather than merely increasing the step size. When m=L, choosing η=1/m eliminates every error component in one step.
