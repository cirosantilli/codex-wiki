Argon detection in a binary gas mixture (x=MA​/MN​−1ρ/ρN​−1​)

 Home Chemistry Chemical substance Chemical element Noble gas Argon
OurBigBook.com Words: 300
Consider an original simplified model containing only nitrogen and argon, with argon mole fraction x. Let their molar masses be MN​ and MA​. Adding the component masses gives the mixture's mean molar mass
M=(1−x)MN​+xMA​.
(9)
For an ideal gas mixture at the same pressure and temperature as a pure-nitrogen reference, the gas density ratio is
r=ρN​ρ​=MN​M​=1+x(MN​MA​​−1),x=MA​/MN​−1r−1​.​
(10)
The inversion requires known identities, the binary-mixture assumption and MA​=MN​. A density ratio cannot uniquely recover an arbitrary multicomponent mixture. Nor should x be interpreted as the argon fraction of untreated air: the model sample has only the two specified components.
Choose round model values MN​=28gmol−1 and MA​=40gmol−1. For x=0.010, the calculated relative density excess is
r−1=0.010(2840​−1)=0.00428571…≃0.429%.​
(11)
Thus a one-percent mole fraction does not mean a one-percent gas density change. In a chosen 2.00L globe at 100kPa and 300K, the ideal gas law gives approximately 2.24508g for pure nitrogen and 2.25471g for this mixture: only 9.62mg separates the weighings. These are model calculations with rounded molar masses, not historical measurements.
A direct control calculation explains why the filling conditions matter. At fixed pressure, pure nitrogen at
T′=1+0.00428571…300K​≃298.720K
(12)
has the same gas density as that mixture at 300K. A roughly 1.28K mismatch can therefore imitate the entire stipulated composition signal. For small changes, the ideal gas law gives
ρdρ​=pdp​+MdM​−TdT​.
(13)
An independently measured pressure and temperature, matched weighing procedure, and checks for residual vapour distinguish composition from these alternative causes. The model explains the need for controls; it does not supply the complete corrections for a real gas-weighing instrument.
