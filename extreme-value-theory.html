Extreme value theory

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 21
Extreme value theory studies limiting probability distributions of normalized sample maxima and related extremes. The standard limits for maxima include the Fréchet distribution, negative Weibull distribution, and Gumbel distribution.
The question is often practical: if a batch finishes only when its slowest task finishes, its completion time is a sample maximum. A typical individual delay or a sample mean does not answer that question. The central limit theorem describes suitably normalized sums and sample means under its assumptions; it does not give a limit law for sample maxima. Extreme value theory instead describes how the largest observation changes as the number of opportunities for a large observation grows.
For independent and identically distributed random variables X1​,X2​,… with distribution function F, define the sample maximum
Mn​=max1≤i≤n​Xi​.
(17)
The event Mn​≤x means that every observation is at most x, so independence gives
P(Mn​≤x)=∏i=1n​P(Xi​≤x)=F(x)n.
(18)
Extreme value theory asks whether there are constants an​>0 and cn​∈R for which (Mn​−cn​)/an​ has convergence in distribution to a non-degenerate probability distribution. Equivalently, F(cn​+an​z)n must tend to a limiting distribution function at its continuity points. The extremal types theorem says that any such limit belongs, up to a change of location and positive scale, to the Gumbel distribution, Fréchet distribution, or negative Weibull distribution. It does not guarantee that a limit exists for every F. [ref].
The maximum of independent exponential random variables gives a particularly transparent worked example: its center grows as (logn)/λ, and its fluctuations have a Gumbel distribution limit on the scale 1/λ. Here and below log denotes the natural logarithm. This normalization depends on the exponential distribution. Other survival functions can lead to the Fréchet distribution or negative Weibull distribution, with different constants an​,cn​; the Gumbel distribution and the exponential example's normalization are not universal.
Independence is essential to the product calculation. If instead X1​=X2​=⋯=X for one rate-λ exponential random variable, then Mn​=X for every n. Its distribution function stays 1−e−λx for x≥0 and zero below zero, and its median stays (log2)/λ. Perfect dependence creates no logarithmic growth: repeating the same random variable supplies no new chance of a larger value.
A historical starting point is R. A. Fisher and L. H. C. Tippett's 1928 paper on limiting laws of the largest or smallest sample member, published in volume 24, issue 2, pages 180–190 of the Proceedings of the Cambridge Philosophical Society. Its abstract studies the possible limiting forms and emphasizes that approach to a limit can be slow. [ref].
