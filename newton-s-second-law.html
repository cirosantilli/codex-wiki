Newton's second law (Fnet​=ma)

In an inertial frame, a point mass of constant mass m>0 obeys

(14)
The net force is the sum of all forces acting on that body; momentum, velocity and acceleration refer to the same reference frame. A force changes velocity, rather than sustaining it: zero net force implies constant velocity, including a nonzero one. Doubling the net force at fixed mass doubles acceleration; doubling mass at fixed net force halves it. [ref]
For example, an otherwise balanced block of mass 0.5kg receives a constant horizontal net force 2N for 0.5s. Its acceleration is 4m/s2, so its velocity changes by 2m/s. Equivalently, the impulse is FΔt=1Ns, and Δv=Δp/m. Starting from rest, its displacement during the pulse is a(Δt)2/2=0.5m. After the pulse, zero net force leaves it moving at constant velocity.
Applying the law therefore requires both a specification of the net force and initial conditions. In the harmonic oscillator example, the force is −kx, so Newton's second law becomes a differential equation for position. In an accelerating reference frame, the measured relative acceleration includes a contribution from the frame's own motion; the equation of motion in a rotating frame illustrates the extra terms required.
Constant mass is essential to the displayed ma form. A variable-mass system exchanging matter also exchanges momentum; its balance includes the entering or leaving matter's velocity. Simply differentiating mv and equating it to external force on that open body can omit this flux. The existing variable-mass system equation specifies the needed correction.
In the 1687 Principia, Newton's second law was stated in terms of change of motion proportional to impressed force, directed along that force. Its original statement is verbal; the derivative equation above is modern notation. [ref]
