Cell nucleus

 Home Biology Biological cell
OurBigBook.com Words: 411 Articles: 3
The cell nucleus is a membrane-bounded compartment characteristic of eukaryotic biological cells, containing most of the cellular DNA in a typical nucleated biological cell. Its double nuclear envelope separates this DNA from the surrounding cytoplasm. Most does not mean all: mitochondria and chloroplasts retain their own DNA. Nor does every specialized eukaryotic biological cell retain a cell nucleus: mature mammalian red blood cells have lost theirs. Prokaryotes organize their DNA in a nucleoid without a nuclear envelope, and still have ribosomes. See NHGRI: nucleus, [ref] and Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 40.2: mature mammalian red blood cells.
Follow one nuclear protein-coding gene: its DNA is used in transcription to make RNA; after the appropriate processing, messenger RNA is exported through a nuclear pore complex; a ribosome in the cytoplasm then uses it in biological translation to build an amino acid chain. The DNA remains in the cell nucleus. The RNA is a template, not the material converted into a protein. The ribosome diagram below follows this route; gene expression gives a complementary nucleotide sequence example. See Alberts et al.: selective RNA export and protein import.
This architecture makes gene expression interesting in a spatial way: nuclear transcription and cytoplasmic biological translation are separated by a selective boundary. A messenger RNA can be processed before export and use, while proteins needed for nuclear functions travel inward. Inside the cell nucleus, the cell nucleolus helps assemble ribosome subunits for export. Thus the compartment housing most DNA also helps build the machinery that interprets its messages elsewhere.
Robert Brown described and named the cell nucleus in his account of plant biological cells, read to the Linnean Society in 1831 and published in its Transactions in 1833. This historical observation identified a recurring structure, without establishing its later-understood role in DNA storage or gene expression. See Brown, On the Organs and Mode of Fecundation in Orchideæ and Asclepiadeæ, Transactions 16, 685–738 (1833). This is a documented contribution rather than a claim that no one had observed the structure earlier.
