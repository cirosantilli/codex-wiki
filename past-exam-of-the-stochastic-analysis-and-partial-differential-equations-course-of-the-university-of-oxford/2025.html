Let Z=(Zn​)n≥0​ be a Markov chain with transition kernel K, and let N be an independent Poisson process of constant rate r>0. A Pseudo-Poisson process is Xt​=ZNt​​. On the Banach space Bb​(E) of bounded Borel measurable functions, with the supremum norm, write
(Kf)(x)=∫E​f(y)K(x,dy),K0=I.
(1)
Its infinitesimal generator, transition semigroup, and resolvent operator are respectively
L=r(K−I),D(L)=Bb​(E),​
(2)
Pt​=etL=e−rtn=0∑∞​n!(rt)n​Kn,​
(3)
and, for λ>0,
Rλ​=∫0∞​e−λtPt​dt=λ+r1​n=0∑∞​(λ+rr​)nKn=(λI−L)−1.​
(4)
The transition semigroup averages Kn against the Poisson distribution of the number of updates. Since K is a Markov operator, ∥K∥≤1; both series converge in operator norm. Updates with Zn+1​=Zn​ are allowed and do not count as changes of the observed state. The degenerate rate r=0 gives L=0, Pt​=I, and Rλ​=I/λ.

The displayed identity requires a correction to the process construction in the source. In a piecewise-deterministic Markov process, the instantaneous hazard function is evaluated at the current flowing state Xu−​, and the destination is sampled from Ku​(Xu−​,dy). Keeping the jump times and destinations of the original Markov jump process Y instead evaluates both quantities at its last reset state Yu−​, which generally differs from Xu−​. The source PDF confirms that these original jump times and destinations were retained. Consequently its definition does not in general produce the asserted transition kernel, or even a Markov process in the state variable X alone.
For an explicit counterexample, take a(t,x)=1, α(x)=2+tanhx, y0​=0, and let every Kt​(x,dy) be the standard normal distribution, independently of t,x. These are smooth coefficients with bounded positive jump rate, so nonexplosion holds. In the literal construction, the first holding time has the exponential distribution of rate 2. Thus the probability distribution of Xt​ has an atom of mass e−2t at t. Paths with at least one jump contribute no atom there, because the reset has a normal density. The asserted identity instead assigns to this no-jump atom the mass
exp(−∫0t​(2+tanhu)du)=coshte−2t​,
(5)
which differs for every t>0.
Here is the full derivation for the intended piecewise-deterministic Markov process. Assume nonexplosion; smoothness alone does not guarantee it. Start at (s,x), let τ be the first jump after s, and set
z(u)=φ(s,x,u),H(u)=∫su​α(z(r))dr.
(6)
One precise construction draws an independent unit-rate exponential random variable E and takes τ=inf{u≥s:H(u)≥E}, with inf∅=∞. This implements a hazard function along the flow of a vector field, rather than a fixed exponential holding time at the last reset. Its survival probability and jump-time probability density function are
S(u)=Ps,x​(τ>u)=e−H(u),−∂u​S(u)=α(z(u))e−H(u).
(7)
Equivalently, S′=−α(z)S with S(s)=1. On {τ>t}, the terminal state is z(t). Conditional on τ=u, draw the new state y from Ku​(z(u),dy) and restart the same construction from (u,y). The Markov property and the tower property of conditional expectation therefore give the first-jump decomposition of a piecewise-deterministic Markov process
(Ps,t​f)(x)​=e−H(t)f(z(t))+∫st​α(z(u))e−H(u)∫R​(Pu,t​f)(y)Ku​(z(u),dy)du.​​
(8)
This proves the requested identity under the corrected construction, for every bounded Borel measurable function f. The two contributions partition according to whether the first jump occurs before t; later jumps are already contained in Pu,t​.
For comparison, the literal construction does have a different renewal identity when started at a reset with Xs​=Ys​=x. If Qs,t​ denotes only these reset-start operators, the same conditional expectation argument gives
(Qs,t​f)(x)=e−α(x)(t−s)f(φ(s,x,t))+∫st​α(x)e−α(x)(u−s)(Ku​Qu,t​f)(x)du.
(9)
These Qs,t​ need not be the transition operators conditional on Xs​=x at an arbitrary time: that observation does not specify the hidden reset state. This distinction explains exactly why replacing only the between-jump paths is insufficient.

The identity for the killed deterministic flow follows directly from the chain rule and the fundamental theorem of calculus, independently of the construction issue in part (b). For a continuously differentiable f, put z(t)=φ(s,x,t) and H(t)=∫st​α(φ(s,x,u))du. Then
∂t​(Ps,tα​f)(x)​=e−H(t)[f′(z(t))∂t​z(t)−α(z(t))f(z(t))]=e−H(t)[a(t,z(t))f′(z(t))−α(z(t))f(z(t))]=(Ps,tα​Ltα​f)(x).​
(10)
Hence, on such test functions,
∂t​Ps,tα​=Ps,tα​Ltα​,Ltα​f=a(t,⋅)f′−αf.​
(11)
The time subscript on Ltα​ is necessary because the vector field may depend on time; it is suppressed on the right-hand side of the source's displayed operator identity. The killed deterministic flow transports an observable along the flow of a vector field and multiplies it by the no-jump survival probability.

Use the corrected piecewise-deterministic Markov process from part (b). For a smooth test function f, its time-dependent infinitesimal generator is
(Lt​f)(x)=a(t,x)f′(x)+α(x)[∫R​f(y)mt​(x,y)dy−f(x)].
(12)
Indeed, over an interval of length h, deterministic motion contributes ha(t,x)f′(x), while a jump has probability hα(x)+o(h) and changes the conditional mean of f by Kt​f(x)−f(x). Terms involving both drift and a jump contribute only to the remainder. Thus Pt,t+h​f=f+hLt​f+o(h).
The Dynkin formula, or this infinitesimal expansion integrated against the current probability distribution, gives the weak evolution identity
dtd​∫R​f(x)pt​(x)dx=∫R​(Lt​f)(x)pt​(x)dx.
(13)
Take f∈Cc∞​(R), so integration by parts has no boundary contribution. Assuming the displayed jump integrals are finite, Fubini's theorem gives
∫R​a(t,x)f′(x)pt​(x)dx∫R​α(y)pt​(y)∫R​f(x)mt​(y,x)dxdy​=−∫R​f(x)∂x​(a(t,x)pt​(x))dx,=∫R​f(x)∫R​α(y)mt​(y,x)pt​(y)dydx.​
(14)
Consequently the forward equation of a piecewise-deterministic Markov process is
∂t​pt​(x)=(At​pt​)(x)​=−∂x​(a(t,x)pt​(x))−α(x)pt​(x)+∫R​α(y)mt​(y,x)pt​(y)dy.​​
(15)
Here At​=Lt∗​ is the formal adjoint operator acting on probability density functions. Its terms are transport, loss from x, and incoming mass from every y. In particular, the incoming rate is α(y), evaluated at the departure state. The operator denoted A in the source is generally time-dependent. Because ∫mt​(y,x)dx=1, the gain and loss integrals cancel; with vanishing transport flux at infinity, dtd​∫pt​=0.
The deterministic initial state in part (b) does not actually yield an everywhere smooth full density. For finite times the no-jump contribution is the positive measure
exp(−∫0t​α(φ(0,y0​,u))du)δφ(0,y0​,t)​,
(16)
where δz​ is the Dirac measure at z. Smooth jump destination probability density functions cannot remove this positive atom. The density equation above is therefore conditional on the extra density hypothesis, for example with a suitable random initial probability density function. For the specified fixed initial state, write νt​ for the whole probability measure and use instead
dtd​∫fdνt​=∫Lt​fdνt​,ν0​=δy0​​.​
(17)
This weak measure evolution includes both the atom and any continuous component. It becomes the displayed partial differential equation whenever νt​(dx)=pt​(x)dx with the assumed regularity. It does not describe the literal hidden-reset construction without the correction in part (b).

For the stochastic differential equation dXs​=b(s,Xs​)ds+Σ(s,Xs​)dBs​, the diffusion generator on smooth functions is
(Ls​g)(x)=∑j​bj​(s,x)∂j​g(x)+21​∑j,k​(ΣΣT)jk​(s,x)∂jk​g(x).
(18)
One form of the Feynman-Kac formula is the following. Suppose the stochastic differential equation is well posed and nonexplosive, c≥0 is bounded, the terminal data g and source h are bounded, and F is a classical solution bounded on finite time intervals of
∂t​F+Lt​F−cF+h=0,F(T,x)=g(x).
(19)
With the regularity needed for the Itô formula, the parabolic Feynman-Kac formula with a source states
F(t,x)=Et,x​[​e−∫tT​c(r,Xr​)drg(XT​)+∫tT​e−∫ts​c(r,Xr​)drh(s,Xs​)ds].​​
(20)
Here Et,x​ means expectation for the diffusion process started at Xt​=x. Under hypotheses ensuring existence of a classical solution, this representation also identifies that solution uniquely in the stated bounded class. In the remaining parts, c=h=0, so F(t,x)=Et,x​g(XT​) solves the terminal-value Kolmogorov backward equation Ft​+Lt​F=0. Complex terminal data are allowed by applying the real formula to their real part and imaginary part separately.

The diffusion generator is L=μ∂x​+21​σ2∂xx​. By the Feynman-Kac formula, the required conditional characteristic function solves
Ft​+μFx​+21​σ2Fxx​=0,F(T,x)=eiux.
(21)
Use separation of variables in the form F(t,x)=eiuxh(t). Since Fx​=iuF and Fxx​=−u2F, the temporal factor satisfies the ordinary differential equation
h′(t)+(iuμ−21​σ2u2)h(t)=0,h(T)=1.
(22)
Thus h(t)=exp[(iuμ−21​σ2u2)(T−t)], and
E[eiuXT​∣Xt​=x]=exp(iu[x+μ(T−t)]−21​u2σ2(T−t)).​
(23)
This is bounded in absolute value by one, so it lies in the uniqueness class of the Feynman-Kac formula. As an independent check, integrating the stochastic differential equation gives XT​=x+μ(T−t)+σ(BT​−Bt​). The independent Brownian increments have the normal distribution with mean zero and variance T−t, yielding exactly the same characteristic function. For σ=0, the conditional distribution is a point mass at x+μ(T−t), and the same boxed expression still applies.

Let E be the underlying Banach space. By the Laplace-transform formula for a semigroup resolvent,
Rλ​f=∫0∞​e−λsPs​fds,∥Rλ​∥≤∫0∞​e−λsds=λ1​.
(32)
These are Bochner integrals, defined for every f∈E and λ>0. The contraction semigroup bound and semigroup property give
Rλ​Rμ​f=∫0∞​∫0∞​e−λs−μtPs+t​fdtds.
(33)
Interchanging and regrouping these Bochner integrals is justified by the integrable bound e−λs−μt∥f∥, whose integral is ∥f∥/(λμ). For λ=μ, set v=s+t and evaluate the inner scalar integral:
Rλ​Rμ​f​=∫0∞​(∫0v​e−λs−μ(v−s)ds)Pv​fdv=μ−λ1​∫0∞​(e−λv−e−μv)Pv​fdv=μ−λRλ​f−Rμ​f​.​
(34)
Since f was arbitrary, the resolvent identity follows:
Rλ​−Rμ​=(μ−λ)Rλ​Rμ​.​
(35)
For λ=μ, both sides vanish, so that case holds as well.

A second-order nondivergence-form elliptic operator on a domain D⊆Rd has the form
Af(x)=∑j,k=1d​ajk​(x)∂jk​f(x)+∑j=1d​bj​(x)∂j​f(x)+c(x)f(x).
(38)
Only the symmetric part of a affects the Hessian matrix term, so one may take a(x) symmetric. The operator is a uniformly elliptic operator if there is a constant κ>0, independent of x, such that
j,k=1∑d​ajk​(x)ξj​ξk​≥κ∣ξ∣2(x∈D, ξ∈Rd).​
(39)
A common bounded-coefficient convention additionally requires an upper bound ∑ajk​(x)ξj​ξk​≤Λ∣ξ∣2 with Λ<∞. The lower bound says that the principal quadratic form cannot degenerate in any direction or at any location.
For an explicit stochastic example, standard d-dimensional Brownian motion has infinitesimal generator
A=21​Δ,ajk​=21​δjk​,b=0,c=0.​
(40)
Its principal quadratic form is ∣ξ∣2/2, so it is uniformly elliptic with κ=Λ=1/2. More generally, a sufficiently regular stochastic differential equation dXt​=b(Xt​)dt+Σ(Xt​)dBt​ has diffusion generator b⋅∇+21​(ΣΣT):D2; it is uniformly elliptic when ΣΣT has a uniformly positive lower bound. A conservative diffusion process has no zeroth-order term, since its infinitesimal generator annihilates constants.

The convergence is in the strong operator topology: for each fixed f∈E, one must prove ∥λRλ​f−f∥→0. First, the contraction semigroup bound gives
∥λRλ​f∥≤∫0∞​λe−λt∥Pt​f∥dt≤∥f∥.
(41)
Thus λRλ​ is a contraction. This is the useful scaling of the contraction estimate in the source's hint. Since ∫0∞​λe−λtdt=1,
λRλ​f−f=∫0∞​λe−λt(Pt​f−f)dt.
(42)
Fix ε>0. By strong continuity, choose δ>0 so that ∥Pt​f−f∥<ε for 0≤t≤δ. Split the integral there and use ∥Pt​f−f∥≤2∥f∥ for the tail:
∥λRλ​f−f∥​≤ε∫0δ​λe−λtdt+2∥f∥∫δ∞​λe−λtdt≤ε+2∥f∥e−λδ.​
(43)
Let λ→∞ and then ε↓0. Therefore
λRλ​f⟶f in Efor every f∈E.​
(44)
The distinction from operator norm convergence is essential. For example, the multiplication semigroup on ℓ2(N) defined by (Pt​f)n​=e−ntfn​ is a strongly continuous semigroup of contractions: coordinatewise convergence and dominated convergence applied to ∑n​∣fn​∣2 prove strong continuity. Its resolvent operator is (Rλ​f)n​=fn​/(λ+n), and
∥λRλ​−I∥=supn≥1​λ+nn​=1.
(45)
Thus the claimed convergence cannot in general be read as convergence in operator norm.
