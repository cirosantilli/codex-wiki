A pi-system is a collection of subsets closed under binary intersections: A,B∈I implies A∩B∈I. Induction then gives closure under every nonempty finite intersection. A pi-system need not contain complements or unions.

Two sigma-algebras are independent if every event in one is independent of every event in the other:
P(G∩H)=P(G)P(H)for all G∈G, H∈H.​
(6)
The same formula defines independence of the generating collections when restricted to their members.

Independence of the full sigma-algebras immediately gives independence of their generating pi-systems. For the converse, fix H∈H0​. On G, define finite measures
νH​(G)=P(G∩H),λH​(G)=P(G)P(H).
(7)
They have the same total mass P(H) and agree on G0​ by assumption. The supplied measure-uniqueness theorem makes them equal on G. Thus the independence formula holds for every G∈G and every H∈H0​.
Now fix such an arbitrary G. The measures H↦P(G∩H) and H↦P(G)P(H) on H have equal total mass and agree on H0​. A second application gives equality on all of H. This proves independence extended from generating pi-systems.

For each i, the collection of events {Xi​≤x}, together with the empty event, is a pi-system. Its generated sigma-algebra is σ(Xi​), because the half-lines generate the Borel sigma-algebra and inverse images preserve intersections and the sigma-algebra operations.
The displayed probability identity says precisely that these two pi-systems are independent. Part (ii) makes σ(X1​) and σ(X2​) independent, which is the definition of independent random variables. Conversely independence of the random variables applies to the half-line events and gives the identity. Thus the joint cumulative distribution function factors exactly when the variables are independent.

For events En​, the Borel-Cantelli first lemma states that ∑n​P(En​)<∞ implies P(En​ infinitely often)=0. Indeed,
P(⋃n≥m​En​)≤∑n≥m​P(En​)→0.

The optional stopping theorem says that for a martingale and bounded stopping times σ≤τ,
E[Mτ​∣Fσ​]=Mσ​,EMτ​=EM0​.​

Let T be the first completion time of the word. A fresh gambler arrives before every throw with capital one, betting it all on the successive symbols 1,2,1,2. Each correct bet multiplies capital by six and each wrong bet loses it, so every bet is fair. A gambler who completes the word keeps the winnings.
At time T, the gambler who bet on all four final throws has capital 64. The gambler who bet on the final two throws has correctly matched the suffix 12 and has capital 62. There are no other survivors: the only nonempty suffixes of 1212 that are also prefixes are 12 and 1212. Thus the total surviving capital is 64+62.
To justify the expectation identity, independent blocks of four throws each have probability 6−4 of equalling the pattern, so P(T>4k)≤(1−6−4)k and ET<∞. Total gambling capital minus the number of arriving unit stakes is a martingale with bounded increments before T. Optional stopping therefore equates expected terminal capital with expected total stakes:
ET=64+62=1332.​
(23)
This is the waiting time for a word in independent uniform symbols. The extra 62 reflects the overlapping occurrence of the prefix 12 at the word's end.

Write R=er, u=1.2 and d=0.9. In a one-step binomial options pricing model, absence of arbitrage requires d<R<u. If R≤d, borrowing to buy the stock gives a nonnegative gain in either state and a positive gain in at least one. If R≥u, shorting the stock and investing the proceeds does the same. Conversely, strict inequalities give the risk-neutral probability q=(R−d)/(u−d)∈(0,1), under which the discounted stock is a martingale; this excludes arbitrage at each node. Hence
log0.9<r<log1.2.​

For this stock-dependent payoff, replace the stock drift by r under a risk-neutral measure. Conditional lognormal moments then give, with τ=T−t,
Vt​=e−rτEQ[ST3​∣Ft​]=St3​e(2r+3σ2)τ.​
