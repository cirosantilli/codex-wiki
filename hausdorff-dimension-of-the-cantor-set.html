Hausdorff dimension of the Cantor set (dimH​C=log2/log3)

 ... Analysis Real analysis Measure theory Geometric measure theory Hausdorff measure Hausdorff dimension
OurBigBook.com Words: 272
The middle-third Cantor set has Hausdorff dimension
d=log3log2​≈0.63093.
(7)
Its Lebesgue measure is zero, yet 0<Hd(C)<∞. These assertions measure different aspects of size: interval length is too coarse to distinguish this set from a null set, while the critical fractional measuring rule captures its remaining structure.
At level n, the construction covers C by 2n intervals of diameter 3−n. Their total s-cost is
2n(3−n)s=(23−s)n.
(8)
For s>d it tends to zero, proving the upper dimension bound. At s=d it equals one, giving Hd(C)≤1. For s<d this particular cover's cost grows, but that alone would not prove a lower bound on Hausdorff measure.
For the matching lower bound, use the Cantor distribution, which assigns mass 2−n to each level-n Cantor cylinder. An interval of length r with 3−(n+1)<r≤3−n meets at most two level-n construction intervals, since their gaps are at least 3−n. Its mass is therefore at most
22−n=2(3−n)d<23drd=4rd.
(9)
Every covering set of diameter r in the line lies in an interval of length r, so any small cover must have total d-cost at least 1/4. This is the mass distribution principle in its interval form: 1/4≤Hd(C)≤1, sufficient to prove the claimed dimension. The constants are convenient bounds, rather than an assertion of the exact critical Hausdorff measure. In the diagram, the right panel is a finite-stage approximation to the Cantor function, showing how probability is shared while the middle third receives none.
The reusable pattern is to obtain an upper bound by constructing covers and a lower bound by distributing mass. The same strategy underlies Cartesian powers of the Cantor set and the dimension formula for a finite similarity iterated function system satisfying the open set condition.
