A left-primitive ideal is a two-sided ideal of the form
P=AnnR​(V)={r∈R:rV=0}
(1)
for a nonzero simple module V on the left. Equivalently, the quotient ring R/P has a faithful module that is a simple module, and is therefore a primitive ring.
To prove that P is a prime ideal of a noncommutative ring, let A,B be two-sided ideals with AB⊆P. Since B is two-sided, BV is a submodule of V: for r∈R, b∈B and v∈V, one has r(bv)=(rb)v∈BV. If B⊆P, then BV=0, so simplicity gives BV=V. Consequently
AV=A(BV)=(AB)V=0,
(2)
and A⊆P. Thus AB⊆P implies A⊆P or B⊆P. Also P=R, since 1 acts as the identity element on the nonzero module V. These are precisely the required prime ideal of a noncommutative ring conditions.

ii OurBigBook.com
The Jacobson radical is
J(R)=⋂L maximal left ideal​L=⋂V simple left module​AnnR​(V).
(3)
Here the second intersection ranges over isomorphism classes of nonzero simple modules. For the equality, every nonzero v in a simple module gives a surjective module homomorphism R→V, r↦rv, whose kernel is a maximal left ideal. Conversely, every maximal left ideal L gives the simple module R/L. This description by annihilators shows that J(R) is a two-sided ideal.
Let j∈J(R) and put a=1+j. If the left ideal Ra were proper, it would lie in a maximal left ideal L. Both a and j would belong to L, giving 1∈L, a contradiction. Therefore there is u∈R with ua=1.
A left inverse alone need not be a two-sided inverse in a general ring, so one more step is necessary. The equality u(1+j)=1 gives u=1−uj∈1+J(R). Applying the same argument to u supplies v∈R with vu=1. Then
v=v(ua)=(vu)a=a,
(4)
and hence au=vu=1. Thus
1+J(R)⊆R×.​
(5)
For the zero ring the conclusion holds directly; the maximal left ideal argument concerns the nonzero case.

Let L be any maximal left ideal. If I⊆L, maximality gives L+I=R, because the two-sided ideal I is in particular a left ideal. Thus 1=ℓ+i for some ℓ∈L and i∈I. But
ℓ=1−i∈1+I
(6)
is a unit by hypothesis. A proper left ideal cannot contain a unit, since left multiplication by its inverse would put 1 in the left ideal. This contradiction proves I⊆L for every maximal left ideal L, and therefore
I⊆J(R).​
(7)
Together with ii, this is the ideal form of the unit criterion for the Jacobson radical.

Assume A=0, as is implicit in the claimed conclusion. Suppose that a surjective ring homomorphism f:R→M2​(A) exists. Choose r1​,r2​∈R mapping to the matrix units E12​ and E21​. Applying f to the assumed relation gives integers c1​,…,cn​ such that
E22​=E21​E12​=∑k=1n​ck​E12k​E21​.
(8)
But E122​=0 and E12​E21​=E11​, so the right side is c1​E11​. Its (2,2) entry is zero, whereas the (2,2) entry of E22​ is 1A​=0. This contradiction proves that no such surjective ring homomorphism exists.
If the convention for rings allows A to be the zero ring, that case must be excluded from the assertion: M2​(0)=0, and the zero ring homomorphism is surjective. This is a convention issue in the printed question.

c OurBigBook.com
Put B=R/I. By the definition of a left-primitive ideal, there is a faithful module V that is a simple module on the left over B. The assumed relation passes to the quotient ring B, since any pair of elements of B can be lifted to R.
By Schur's lemma, the endomorphism ring EndB​(V) is a division ring: the kernel and image of a nonzero module endomorphism are submodules, so simplicity makes that module endomorphism both injective and surjective. Set
Δ=EndB​(V)op.
(9)
Evaluation makes V a right vector space over a division ring Δ, and the B-action is Δ-linear. The opposite ring in the definition ensures the correct order for this right action.
The Jacobson density theorem says that, for any finite Δ-linearly independent family v1​,…,vm​ and any w1​,…,wm​∈V, some b∈B satisfies bvi​=wi​ for every i.
Suppose that V contains two Δ-linearly independent vectors v,w. Applying the Jacobson density theorem twice, choose a,b∈B satisfying
av=0,aw=v,bv=w,bw=0.
(10)
The inherited relation gives
ba=∑k=1n​ck​akb
(11)
with integers ck​. Acting on w, the left side gives baw=bv=w, but every term on the right gives akbw=0. This contradicts w=0. Thus dimΔ​V=1.
The ring homomorphism
B⟶EndΔ​(V)
(12)
given by the action is injective because V is a faithful module. It is surjective because the Jacobson density theorem, applied to a single nonzero basis vector, realizes every Δ-linear endomorphism of this one-dimensional vector space over a division ring. Every nonzero such endomorphism is invertible. Hence
R/I≅EndΔ​(V) is a division ring.​
(13)
The use of the Jacobson density theorem here only prescribes actions on finitely many vectors; it does not assume that an arbitrary primitive ring is already a full endomorphism ring.

i OurBigBook.com
A multiplicative subset S is a left Ore set if for every s∈S and r∈R there are s′∈S and r′∈R with
s′r=r′s.​
(14)
Equivalently, the subsets Sr and Rs intersect for each such pair. This is the common-left-multiple condition needed for Ore localization with denominators on the left. In a noncommutative domain, no extra condition concerning annihilation by denominators is needed, since all members of S are regular elements of a ring.

ii OurBigBook.com
We first prove that any two nonzero principal left ideals Ra and Rb intersect nontrivially. Suppose instead that Ra∩Rb=0. The nonzero left ideals
Ra,Rab,Rab2,…
(15)
then form a direct sum. Indeed, take a finite relation
∑i=0m​ui​abi=0.
(16)
It implies
u0​a=−(∑i=1m​ui​abi−1)b∈Ra∩Rb=0.
(17)
As R is a noncommutative domain and a=0, this gives u0​=0. Right cancellation of the nonzero element b reduces the remaining relation to one of the same form with one fewer term. Repetition gives u1​=⋯=um​=0, proving the asserted direct sum.
Consequently its finite partial sums give a strictly ascending chain of left ideals, contradicting the ascending chain condition in a left Noetherian ring. Thus Ra∩Rb=0 whenever a,b=0. This is the left Noetherian domain Ore lemma.
Now take s∈S=R∖{0} and r∈R. If r=0, choose s′=1 and r′=0. Otherwise choose
0=z∈Rr∩Rs,z=s′r=r′s.
(18)
Since z=0, the coefficient s′ is nonzero and hence belongs to S. This proves the left Ore set condition in all cases.

View R^ as a noncommutative formal power series ring, and let ε:R^→Q take the coefficient of the empty word. This is a ring homomorphism, since the empty word has only the factorization 1⋅1. In particular the two-sided ideal I generated by x1​,x2​ lies in kerε.
In fact I=kerε. Every noncommutative formal power series with zero constant term can be partitioned according to the first letter of its words and written
h=x1​h1​+x2​h2​,h1​,h2​∈R^.
(21)
Both summands belong to the two-sided ideal I.
For a=1+h∈1+I, define the formal geometric series
g=∑k=0∞​(−h)k.
(22)
This defines an element of R^: since h has zero constant term, every word in hk has length at least k. Thus the coefficient of a fixed word of length d receives contributions only from 0≤k≤d, each a finite sum.
For each finite partial sum gN​=∑k=0N​(−h)k, associativity gives both identities
(1+h)gN​=gN​(1+h)=1−(−h)N+1.
(23)
In every fixed word degree the last term vanishes once N is large enough. It follows coefficient by coefficient that ag=ga=1. Hence
(1+h)−1=k=0∑∞​(−h)k(h∈I).​
(24)
The argument is formal and uses no analytic convergence. More generally, every noncommutative formal power series with a nonzero rational constant term is a unit, after factoring out that scalar. This is the unit criterion for noncommutative formal power series.

d OurBigBook.com
Take D=R^. We first check that this noncommutative formal power series ring is a noncommutative domain. For nonzero F,G∈D, let p,q be the smallest lengths of words with nonzero coefficients in F,G, respectively. Their degree-p and degree-q parts Fp​,Gq​ are nonzero homogeneous polynomials in the free associative algebra.
Choose words u,v with nonzero coefficients in Fp​,Gq​. In Fp​Gq​, the coefficient of uv is the product of those two coefficients: a word of length p+q has a unique splitting into a prefix of length p and a suffix of length q. This product is nonzero in Q. The degree-p+q part of FG is exactly Fp​Gq​, since all lower parts of F and G vanish. Thus FG=0, proving that D is a noncommutative domain.
By the defining property of the free associative algebra, there is a ring homomorphism
f:R⟶D,f(x1​)=1+x1​,f(x2​)=2+x2​.
(25)
The same substitution defines a ring automorphism of R, with inverse x1​↦x1​−1, x2​↦x2​−2. Composing this ring automorphism with the natural inclusion R↪D proves that f is injective.
Finally,
f(x1​+x2​)f(x1​)​=3+x1​+x2​,=1+x1​,​f(x1​−x2​)f(x2​)​=−1+x1​−x2​,=2+x2​.​
(26)
Their constant terms are 3,−1,1,2, respectively, all nonzero. By the unit criterion for noncommutative formal power series proved in c, all four elements are units. Therefore D and f have every required property. The embedding changes the images of the generators, so the failure of the original monomial left Ore set in b presents no obstruction.

Use the Weyl algebra convention [yi​,xj​]=δij​, with [xi​,xj​]=[yi​,yj​]=0, where the brackets are commutators. Its order filtration of a Weyl algebra is
Fj​R=∑∣β∣≤j​C[x1​,…,xn​]yβ(j≥0),Fj​R=0(j<0).
(27)
Thus xi​ has degree zero and yi​ degree one. By the ordered monomial basis of a Weyl algebra, the associated graded ring is
A=grR≅C[x1​,…,xn​,ξ1​,…,ξn​],degxi​=0,degξi​=1,
(28)
where ξi​ is the symbol of yi​.
The Rees ring of a filtered algebra is the graded ring
R=j≥0⨁​(Fj​R)tj⊆R[t],​
(29)
where t is a central indeterminate. Here it is generated by xi​, Yi​=yi​t, and t, with degrees 0,1,1, respectively, and relations
[Yi​,xj​]=δij​t,[xi​,xj​]=[Yi​,Yj​]=[t,xi​]=[t,Yi​]=0.
(30)
It interpolates between the associated graded ring and the original filtered ring:
R/(t)≅grR,R/(t−1)≅R.
(31)
A good filtration for the order filtration on M is an increasing exhaustive family of complex vector subspaces (Mj​)j∈Z​, zero for sufficiently negative j, with Fi​RMj​⊆Mi+j​, such that the associated graded module
grM=⨁j∈Z​Mj​/Mj−1​
(32)
is a finitely generated module over A. Equivalently, there are finitely many m1​,…,ms​ and integer shifts d1​,…,ds​ such that Mj​=∑ℓ=1s​(Fj−dℓ​​R)mℓ​. In particular, choose any finite generating family m1​,…,ms​ of M and put
Mj​=ℓ=1∑s​(Fj​R)mℓ​.​
(33)
This is a good filtration for the order filtration.
For M=0, its dimension of a Weyl algebra module for the order filtration is
d(M)=dimSuppA​(grM)=dimKrull​(A/AnnA​(grM)).​
(34)
Here support of a module, annihilator of a module, and Krull dimension are taken over the commutative polynomial ring A. The corresponding algebraic set is the order characteristic variety of a Weyl algebra module. This dimension is independent of the chosen good filtration for the order filtration; one may set d(0)=−∞.
The pieces Fj​R generally have infinite complex dimension, since they contain all polynomials in the xi​. Thus a polynomial-growth definition based directly on dimC​Mj​ would be inappropriate for this order filtration of a Weyl algebra.

b OurBigBook.com
For the submodule N⊆M, define its subspace filtration and the quotient filtration on the quotient module Q=M/N by
Nj​=N∩Mj​,Qj​=(Mj​+N)/N.
(35)
They are compatible filtrations of a module, since multiplication by Fi​R sends Nj​ into Ni+j​ and Qj​ into Qi+j​.
In each degree, inclusion and the quotient map induce
ιj​:Nj​/Nj−1​⟶Mj​/Mj−1​,πj​:Mj​/Mj−1​⟶Qj​/Qj−1​.
(36)
The first map is injective: if n∈Nj​ belongs to Mj−1​, then n∈N∩Mj−1​=Nj−1​. The second map is surjective by the definition of Qj​.
For exactness in the middle, a class m+Mj−1​ lies in kerπj​ precisely when m∈Mj​ and m+N∈Qj−1​. This means m=m′+n for m′∈Mj−1​ and n∈N. Since m,m′∈Mj​, one also has n∈Nj​, and
m+Mj−1​=n+Mj−1​∈imιj​.
(37)
Conversely, every class from Nj​ maps to zero in Qj​/Qj−1​. Taking the direct sum over all degrees gives the short exact sequence of graded modules
0⟶grN⟶grM⟶gr(M/N)⟶0.
(38)
The maps respect the grR-action because they come from module homomorphisms compatible with the filtrations of a module. Consequently
grM/grN≅gr(M/N)​
(39)
as grR-modules. This proves exactness of associated graded modules for induced filtrations.
Furthermore A=grR is a Noetherian ring, and grM is a finitely generated module. Its submodule grN and its quotient module gr(M/N) are therefore finitely generated modules. Hence the induced subspace filtration and quotient filtration are both good filtrations for the order filtration.

Use the quotient filtration induced from the order filtration of a Weyl algebra. The key step is to identify the entire associated graded module of the left ideal I, rather than merely take symbols of its displayed generators.
For every member xαyβ of the ordered monomial basis of a Weyl algebra, right multiplication by the pure y-monomials gives
(xαyβ)y13​y22​=xαyβ+(3,2,0,…,0),(xαyβ)y12​y33​=xαyβ+(2,0,3,0,…,0).
(40)
No commuting past an xi​ is necessary. It follows that I is exactly the complex linear span of the basis monomials satisfying either
β1​≥3, β2​≥2,orβ1​≥2, β3​≥3.
(41)
Intersecting this linear span with each filtration piece and then taking symbols therefore yields the monomial ideal
grI=K=(ξ13​ξ22​,ξ12​ξ33​)⊆A.
(42)
By b,
gr(R/I)≅A/K.
(43)
To determine its Krull dimension, compute the radical of an ideal:
K​=(ξ1​ξ2​,ξ1​ξ3​)=(ξ1​)∩(ξ2​,ξ3​).
(44)
Indeed, K lies in the displayed intersection, an intersection of prime ideals. Conversely, (ξ1​ξ2​)3∈K and (ξ1​ξ3​)3∈K, so both generators of that intersection lie in K​. The equality of the intersection with (ξ1​ξ2​,ξ1​ξ3​) follows by factoring out ξ1​ and reducing modulo (ξ2​,ξ3​).
The order characteristic variety of a Weyl algebra module in this case has two irreducible components:
V(K)={ξ1​=0} ∪ {ξ2​=ξ3​=0}.
(45)
They are coordinate affine spaces of dimensions 2n−1 and 2n−2, respectively, in the 2n coordinates x1​,…,xn​,ξ1​,…,ξn​. The Krull dimension is the larger of these component dimensions. Thus
d(R/I)=2n−1.​
(46)
In particular, two displayed generators do not force a drop of two in dimension: their common factor creates the component ξ1​=0 of dimension 2n−1.

In fact J=R, so the quotient module is zero. This requires an argument using left ideals: a left ideal is not generally closed under multiplication on the right.
Write x=x1​, y=y1​, so that yx−xy=1. The commutator product rule gives
yx2=x2y+2x,y2x2=x2y2+4xy+2.
(47)
Both y2x2 and x2y2 lie in J=Rx2+Ry2, so their difference
h=y2x2−x2y2=4xy+2
(48)
belongs to J. Multiplying on the left and subtracting another known element of the left ideal gives
yh−4xy2=4(yx−xy)y+2y=6y∈J.
(49)
Because the scalar 6 is invertible in C, it follows that y∈J, and hence xy∈J. Therefore h−4xy=2∈J, giving 1∈J. We conclude
R/J=0,dimC​(R/J)=0.​
(50)
Thus the answer is yes for every n≥1. All ideal operations in the proof use left multiplication. In particular, simply replacing J by the commutative ideal (x12​,ξ12​) of the associated graded ring would miss the lower-order consequences that force J=R.
