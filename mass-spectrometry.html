Mass spectrometry

 Home Chemistry
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 19
Mass spectrometry separates and detects ions according to mass-to-charge ratio. It can distinguish isotopes which belong to the same chemical element but have different atomic masses. The instrument measures ions: interpreting the result as a neutral atomic mass requires accounting for electron removal and its ionization energy. For a singly positive ion in its ground state, m+​=ma​−me​+I/c2, where me​ is the electron rest mass, I is the ionization energy, and c is the speed of light.
For a simple illustrative analyzer, positive ions of mass m and electric charge q=ze gain kinetic energy qV=mv2/2 across an electric potential difference V before entering a transverse uniform magnetic field B. The Lorentz force supplies centripetal acceleration, so
qvB=rmv2​,qm​=2VB2r2​.
(28)
At fixed V and B, the measured bending radius r separates mass-to-charge ratios, not masses independently of electric charge. This ideal model assumes negligible initial kinetic energy and speeds small compared with the speed of light; the historical focusing mass spectrograph used the geometry described below.
The experimental achievement is to turn invisible differences between individual ions into separated, calibrated positions. Aston velocity focusing explains how an early mass spectrograph sharpened its images; the velocity-selected magnetic mass analyzer provides an independent model whose equations can be checked directly. The magnesium isotope mass spectrum shows why position, width, and intensity carry different information.
