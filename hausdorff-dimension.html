Hausdorff dimension (dimH​E)

 ... Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Measure theory Geometric measure theory Hausdorff measure
OurBigBook.com Words: 760 Articles: 4
The Hausdorff dimension is the critical exponent at which Hausdorff measure changes from infinite to zero:
dimH​E=inf{s≥0:Hs(E)=0}=sup{s≥0:Hs(E)=∞},
(4)
with the standard endpoint convention for an empty supremum. This measures fine-scale geometric size rather than the number of coordinates used to describe the set. A line segment has Hausdorff dimension one; a square has dimension two; the middle-third Cantor set has dimension log2/log3≈0.63093.
Why can a dimension be fractional? At a scale reduced by a factor of three, the Cantor set needs twice as many construction intervals. The balance 2(1/3)d=1 gives d=log2/log3, between the growth rates of finitely many points and a whole interval. Making this argument rigorous requires a cover upper bound and the mass distribution principle lower bound, as in Hausdorff dimension of the Cantor set.
At the critical exponent itself, Hausdorff measure can be zero, finite and positive, or infinite; dimension does not specify that endpoint value. For example, finite sets and countably infinite sets both have dimension zero, although their counting measures differ. Dimension is also different from a finite-resolution estimate: box-counting dimension is another asymptotic definition, and fitting a scaling slope over a handful of observed scales does not establish either dimension.
The metric matters. Multiplying every distance by a fixed positive constant multiplies Hs by that constant to the power s and preserves Hausdorff dimension. A Lipschitz map cannot increase it, because mapped cover diameters increase by at most a fixed factor. A bi-Lipschitz map therefore preserves it by applying this fact in both directions. These properties make Hausdorff dimension useful for comparing geometric models while controlling how their measurements change.
