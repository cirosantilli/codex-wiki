Perfect matching

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Graph theory Matching (graph theory)
OurBigBook.com Words: 369 Articles: 3
A perfect matching is a matching in a graph that covers every vertex exactly once. Its edges have no shared endpoints, and every vertex is an endpoint of one selected edge. It is useful when a collection must be divided into disjoint pairs with no element left over.
In a bipartite graph with equally many vertices on its two sides, a perfect matching can assign every person one allowed job and every job one person. The example under permanent of a matrix has three people and three jobs, with exactly three valid assignments. The blue edges below show those three perfect matchings; a crossing in the drawing is not an extra vertex.
Every selected edge covers two vertices, so a graph with an odd integer number of vertices has no perfect matching. An even integer number is necessary but does not guarantee existence: an isolated vertex cannot be covered. A perfect matching also need not be unique. For example, the complete bipartite graph K2,2​ has two perfect matchings, corresponding to the two ways of assigning two people to two jobs.
Counting all perfect matchings is a different question from finding one. For a zero-one biadjacency matrix of a balanced bipartite graph, the count is its permanent of a matrix. The nonzero determinant criterion for a perfect matching supplies an existence test, but cancellation means that a zero determinant does not rule one out.
