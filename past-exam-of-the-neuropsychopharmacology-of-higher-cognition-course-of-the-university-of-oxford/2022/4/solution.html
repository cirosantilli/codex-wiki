Reward prediction error remains a strong account of particular phasic dopamine signals and their contribution to learning; it is inadequate as a universal description of every dopamine neuron, projection and timescale. The question is best answered by separating the computational hypothesis, measurements of naturally occurring activity, and evidence that manipulating an error-like signal changes later learning.
In temporal-difference learning, a prediction is updated using
δt​=rt+1​+γV(st+1​)−V(st​),V(st​)←V(st​)+αδt​,
(1)
where V(s) is the value function for state s, rt+1​ is the next reward, 0≤γ≤1 discounts later return, and 0<α≤1 is the update step size. The terminal value is zero. A reward prediction error is therefore not simply reward, pleasure or salience. Its sign depends on what was expected and when. An unexpected reward can generate a positive error; an accurately predicted reward can generate little error; omission of an expected reward can generate a negative error. A cue can produce an error when it changes the prediction of future return even though no immediate reward is delivered.
Primate recordings motivated this account because phasic responses changed with learning and reward expectation rather than responding identically to every reward. Linking these patterns to a quantitative learning rule provided predictions about cue responses and outcome omissions that a generic “reward neuron” label lacked. However, extracellular identification and population sampling matter, and the computational state representation is an assumption. A model fitted with a poor description of what the animal knows can misclassify a genuine expectation-related response as a theoretical failure.[ref]
Association-learning experiments make the interpretation more than a curve-fitting exercise. In blocking, an established cue already predicts the reward, reducing learning about an added cue. Reward-timed optogenetic activation of dopamine neurons could overcome this blocking, supplying an error-like teaching event despite the normally expected reward. The relevant endpoint was subsequent learning about a cue, not just movement during stimulation. This supports a causal teaching contribution but does not establish that artificially synchronized activation reproduces every feature of an endogenous code.[ref]
The negative side of the error also has intervention evidence. Brief inhibition of targeted dopamine neurons around reward processing produced later learning changes resembling those expected from a negative error, with behavioural controls addressing distraction or immediate aversion. This strengthens a bidirectional teaching account beyond sufficiency of excitation alone. The imposed pause still tests a particular population and task, and inhibition can affect processes other than the proposed scalar signal. Appropriate timing controls and later unstimulated testing are essential to the interpretation.[ref]
Population heterogeneity can refine the hypothesis rather than simply refute it. Distributional reinforcement-learning work proposed multiple channels differing in optimism and in their relative sensitivity to positive and negative errors. Mouse single-unit data showed systematic variation in response reversal points consistent with this account. One reward can then be better than expected for one channel and worse for another. The finding challenges a single shared scalar prediction broadcast identically by all cells, while retaining prediction-error-like computations. It does not make heterogeneity alone proof of a particular distributional algorithm.[ref]
Other heterogeneity is not readily summarized as reward error. Cell-resolved imaging during a mouse virtual-navigation task found dopamine-neuron signals related to sensory, motor and cognitive variables, with structured differences across neurons. Regression and task design can separate some predictors, but correlated movements and expectations limit unique interpretation. Such findings make a universal scalar account implausible; they do not eliminate the well-controlled reward-error results in other populations. Nor should every movement-related signal be re-labelled as error without an independently specified model that predicts it.[ref]
The measurement compartment is another limitation. A comparison of identified VTA cell spiking with nucleus-accumbens dopamine release in a common decision task found release dynamics related to evolving reward expectations without corresponding changes in recorded cell-body spiking. Local release regulation therefore matters: a terminal sensor and a somatic spike train need not report the same quantity. This is especially important for interpreting slower ramps or motivational effects as direct readings of a broadcast phasic error.[ref]
Further qualifications concern learning itself. An error depends on subjective reward, inferred state, uncertainty and task structure; it is not calculated from the experimenter’s reward schedule alone. Values informed by a model of the environment can also contribute, so identifying an error-like signal does not automatically prove exclusively model-free learning. Conversely, a very flexible state model can accommodate almost any observation unless its representation and parameters are constrained independently. Good tests manipulate expectation while matching outcome and movement, and compare explicit alternatives on held-out behaviour and neural data.
The account is thus still appropriate when stated at the right resolution: some temporally precise dopamine activity supplies expectation-dependent teaching signals, supported by both recordings and causal learning manipulations. A complete account of the dopamine system must additionally explain projection-specific coding, local transmitter dynamics, motivation and movement. The productive revision is a family of constrained computational and circuit hypotheses, rather than either one molecule–one number or abandonment of prediction error altogether.
