Potassium has a closed 18-electron core and one valence electron. In 3d2D5/2​ the orbital label specifies principal quantum number n=3 and single-electron orbital angular momentum l=2. The term letter D specifies total orbital angular momentum L=2, the superscript 2S+1=2 specifies S=1/2, and the subscript is total electronic angular momentum J=5/2. The 5p2P3/2​ state instead has n=5, l=L=1, the same spin, and J=3/2. The closed electron shell that forms the core contributes no net L or S in this alkali-like approximation. The dash identifies a pair of levels rather than by itself specifying the direction of emission.
The leading electric dipole transition rules for one active electron are
Δl=±1,ΔS=0,ΔJ=0,±1 except J=0↔0,ΔmJ​=0,±1.​
(1)
The electronic parity must change, since the electric dipole operator is odd under spatial reflection. The electric-dipole selection rule imposes no general restriction on changing n by more than one. The rule for magnetic sublevels depends on the radiation's polarization relative to the quantization axis: the q=0 component couples equal mJ​, whereas q=±1 supplies the other changes. In a many-electron description using LS coupling the total orbital rule is ΔL=0,±1, excluding L=0↔0; for a single active electron the stronger Δl=±1 applies. Thus the displayed D–P pair satisfies the leading rules. Relativistic mixing and higher-multipole transitions can permit weaker lines outside the approximation of electric dipole transitions.

Use the common ionization continuum consisting of a divalent calcium ion and a free electron as the upper reference, with the singly ionized calcium ground state 4s2S1/2​ at zero energy. The resonance doublet is
4p2P3/2​→4s2S1/2​ (393.4 nm),4p2P1/2​→4s2S1/2​ (396.9 nm).​
(2)
The higher-J P component is higher in energy because its emission to the common lower level has the shorter wavelength. The full series is np2P1/2,3/2​→4s2S1/2​. As n→∞, its two upper levels approach the same continuum, so the ground-state ionization energy is approximately hc/(104.4 nm)=11.88 eV.
The second doublet has a common upper S level and two lower P levels:
5s2S1/2​→4p2P3/2​ (373.7 nm),5s2S1/2​→4p2P1/2​ (370.6 nm).​
(3)
Both combinations put 5s2S1/2​ near 6.47 eV above the ground state. The series is ns2S1/2​→4p2P1/2,3/2​ with n≥5. Its two limits measure the ionization energy from each lower P level, giving slightly different limit wavelengths near 142 nm, not two unrelated continua. From the rounded wavelengths, E(4p1/2​)≃3.124 eV and E(4p3/2​)≃3.152 eV, leaving roughly 8.74 eV to the same continuum. The tabulated level ordering and labels independently agree with these assignments.[ref]
The quoted wavelengths and limits are rounded; air and vacuum conventions also matter for precision spectroscopy. They justify a labelled schematic and the approximate energy differences, not an artificial claim of exact closure at every printed decimal.

In the regime of weak-field Zeeman splitting, J,mJ​ remain good labels and ΔE=μB​gJ​mJ​B. For the 393.4 nm line, Ju​=3/2 in 4p2P3/2​ and Jl​=1/2 in 4s2S1/2​. The Landé g-factors are gu​=4/3 and gl​≃2. Apply ΔmJ​=0,±1 to the four upper and two lower sublevels. The allowed pairs (mu​,ml​) are
(3/2,1/2), (1/2,1/2), (−1/2,1/2), (1/2,−1/2), (−1/2,−1/2), (−3/2,−1/2).
(5)
Their frequency displacements in units of μB​B/h are respectively 1,−1/3,−5/3,5/3,1/3,−1, so none coincide. Looking perpendicular to the field permits both the π radiation from q=0 transitions and the σ radiation from q=±1 transitions. Consequently
six distinct components: two π and four σ.​
(6)
A linear polarizer parallel to the field isolates the two π components; a transverse polarizer selects the four σ components. Without polarization selection both sets are present. This count assumes a field small compared with fine structure coupling and neglects isotope-dependent hyperfine structure; the strong-field Paschen-Back effect or a resolved nonzero-spin isotope would require a different model. The spectral spacings are unnecessary for the count, but the distinct shifts verify that counting allowed transitions does not inadvertently count degenerate lines twice.

First make the units explicit. Use angular momentum operators in units of ℏ, so their squared eigenvalues are J(J+1) rather than ℏ2J(J+1); the coefficient A then has units of energy. The operator identity
J2=(J1​+J2​)2=J12​+J22​+2J1​⋅J2​
(7)
gives, at fixed J1​,J2​,
EJ​=2A​[J(J+1)−J1​(J1​+1)−J2​(J2​+1)].
(8)
Subtracting the expression for the adjacent allowed value J−1 leaves
EJ​−EJ−1​=2A​[J(J+1)−(J−1)J]=AJ.​
(9)
This is the Landé interval rule. Its content is that consecutive gaps grow in proportion to the higher total-angular-momentum quantum number, with the same coefficient within an isolated coupled multiplet. A negative A reverses the ordering of the levels, while positive observed transition frequencies alone measure gap magnitudes.
If the source symbols instead denote physical angular momenta with units of action, the Hamiltonian written literally as AJ1​⋅J2​ gives the gap Aℏ2J. Recovering the printed form then requires absorbing ℏ2 into the coefficient or writing H=(A/ℏ2)J1​⋅J2​. The convention is essential to dimensional consistency rather than an extra dynamical assumption.

b OurBigBook.com
For two valence electrons, the central-field shell structure should be the largest scale, residual electrostatic interactions should establish a reasonably definite configuration and atomic term, and spin-orbit coupling should be weaker than the separations between the relevant terms. Then L and S remain approximately good quantum numbers. Within a well-isolated term the leading fine structure interaction can be represented as AL⋅S. Its coefficient must be effectively constant for all J levels in that term. Mixing of neighboring terms, spin-spin interactions, higher-order corrections or strong applied fields can spoil the simple interval rule; hyperfine structure must be negligible or separately resolved.
A suitable approximate example is the magnesium 3s3p3PJ​ term, with L=1, S=1 and J=0,1,2. The interval-rule prediction is
E0​=−2A,E1​=−A,E2​=A,E1​−E0​=A,E2​−E1​=2A.​
(10)
An arbitrary common term energy may be added. For A>0 the levels ascend in the order 3P0​,3P1​,3P2​, with gap ratio one to two. Each level still contains 2J+1 degenerate magnetic sublevels in zero external field. The superscript three labels the spin multiplicity 2S+1, while the different subscripts identify fine structure components of that same term. This is an approximate example of LS coupling, not a claim that every real two-electron atom has exact one-to-two fine splitting.

Within a fixed isolated electronic J multiplet, the electronic field at the nucleus is a vector operator. Rotational symmetry and the Wigner-Eckart theorem imply that its projection into this multiplet is proportional to J:
PJ​Be​PJ​=bJ​J.
(11)
Its detailed orbital, spin-dipolar and contact contributions are included in the reduced coefficient bJ​. Consequently the projected magnetic-dipole hyperfine interaction becomes
Hhfs​=AI⋅J,A=−gI​μN​bJ​,​
(12)
in the same dimensionless angular-momentum convention as a. The proportionality is a statement about matrix elements within the chosen electronic manifold, not an identity for the electron field operator on the entire atomic Hilbert space.
Define F=I+J. In zero external field the quantum states obtained by addition of angular momentum are labelled ∣γJ,I;F,mF​⟩, where γ specifies the electron configuration and other fixed labels. The good quantum numbers include I,J,F,mF​, with F=∣I−J∣,…,I+J and mF​=−F,…,F. In a description using LS coupling the fixed electronic labels can also include L,S. The energies depend on F but not mF​:
EF​=2A​[F(F+1)−I(I+1)−J(J+1)].
(13)
This neglects coupling to other electronic manifolds, electric-quadrupole hyperfine interactions and the Zeeman effect from external fields. Those additions can shift the intervals or change which quantum numbers remain useful.

For a hyperfine multiplet governed by the magnetic-dipole hyperfine interaction, adjacent frequency gaps obey νF​=∣A∣F/h. The five measured gaps for the first isotope are approximately in the ratios 1:2:3:4:5. Therefore the six levels have F=0,1,2,3,4,5: the first measured gap corresponds to higher F=1. In particular Fmin​=∣I−J∣=0 and Fmax​=I+J=5, giving
I=J=5/2.​
(14)
This inference uses both the number of intervals and their successive ratios. Merely seeing six levels would specify only min(I,J)=5/2 and would not by itself establish equality.
Solving a linear least-squares problem for a fit through the origin, taking the rounded frequencies as equally weighted, gives
h∣A55​∣​=∑F=15​F2∑F=15​FνF​​=72.42 MHzapproximately.​
(15)
Thus the scale of the hyperfine structure is about 4.80×10−26 J, or 3.00×10−7 eV. The displayed frequencies have unequal rounding precision, so additional fit digits are not physically warranted. The gap pattern supports a leading dipole interval rule, with small residuals that do not establish higher-multipole constants without uncertainties.
The total width between F=0 and F=5 is 15∣A55​∣, about 1.086 GHz in frequency units. In the ideal three-level laser, pumping excites atoms out of the ground state to a higher auxiliary level, rapid relaxation fills a relatively long-lived upper laser level, and the laser transition returns to the ground state. A ruby laser is a standard example of this classification. Since the lower laser state initially holds most atoms, substantial pumping is needed to reverse the population per sublevel. With upper and lower degeneracies g2​,g1​ and N1​+N2​≃NT​, the positive-gain condition is
N2​/g2​>N1​/g1​,N2​>g1​+g2​g2​​NT​.​
(18)
For equal degeneracies more than half the active atoms must be excited before optical gain is even positive.
In a four-level laser the lower laser level is above the ground reservoir and empties rapidly after laser emission. Pumping again feeds a higher auxiliary level which relaxes into the upper laser state, but the lower laser-state population can remain small. The helium-neon laser provides a multilevel physical realization often represented by this scheme for a four-level laser. Positive population inversion then requires much less depletion of the ground reservoir, giving a much lower laser pump threshold for comparable lifetimes, emission cross sections and cavity losses.
The threshold of a four-level laser is not literally zero. Oscillation requires round-trip optical gain to overcome output coupling and other losses, and competing decay or incomplete lower-level removal raises the required pump. Real laser media contain more atomic states than ideal diagrams for a three-level laser or a four-level laser; the labels describe the relevant pumping and laser-transition cycle rather than the total number of states in the material.

Let A=A21​ and r=g2​/g1​. Before stimulated emission and stimulated absorption are added, the source and decay terms give
N˙2​=R2​−AN2​,N˙1​=AN2​−τ1​N1​​.
(19)
The upper level is fed at a specified rate of addition to the number density, its spontaneous decay feeds the lower level, and lower-level removal leads to states outside this two-level system. This is therefore an open pumped system, not a closed two-level system with conserved population.
At steady state,
N2(0)​=AR2​​,N1(0)​=R2​τ1​,N0∗​=R2​(A1​−rτ1​).​
(20)
The degeneracy-weighted population inversion compares population per magnetic sublevel. It is positive only if rAτ1​<1; a positive pump rate alone cannot guarantee optical gain if lower-level removal is too slow. A short lower-state lifetime is consequently helpful even when the upper-state pumping is unchanged.
The equations assume finite positive A and τ1​, approximately constant pumping and no other specified sources, sinks or branching channels. A finite steady state would not exist for continuous pumping if the lower level had no escape route. The fixed source rate also presumes access to an external reservoir; it does not imply that the two populations can grow beyond all available active atoms in a real finite medium.

Define σ(ω) as the emission cross section for an upper-level atom, averaged over the participating sublevels, and let W=σI/(ℏω). Detailed balance gives the upward stimulated rate per lower-level atom as rW, where r=g2​/g1​. With D=N2​−rN1​ the complete laser rate equations are
N˙2​=R2​−AN2​−WD,N˙1​=AN2​+WD−N1​/τ1​.
(21)
Adding them eliminates the internal transitions. Therefore steady state has N1​=R2​τ1​ even when the radiation is strong. Substitution into the upper equation gives
N2​=A+WR2​(1+rWτ1​)​,D=A+WR2​(1−rAτ1​)​=1+W/AD0​​.
(22)
One net stimulated transition changes the beam energy by ℏω, so the coefficient of optical gain for intensity is α(ω,I)=σ(ω)D. At line center, with σ0​=σ(ω21​),
α=1+I/Is​α0​​,α0​=σ0​R2​(A−1−rτ1​),Is​=σ0​ℏω21​A​.​
(23)
Here α0​ is the unsaturated intensity optical gain per length, which may be negative, and Is​ is the intensity at which its magnitude is halved in this model with homogeneous broadening. The cancellation of τ1​ from Is​ follows from exclusive upper-to-lower decay and constant upper-state pumping. Keeping N1​=Aτ1​N2​ in the stimulated problem would discard the stimulated feeding term and give an incorrect saturation scale. Other decay branches or different pump constraints lead to different expressions.
For a traveling beam with uniform pump, no additional loss and local steady populations,
dzdI​=1+I/Is​α0​I​,lnI(0)I(z)​+Is​I(z)−I(0)​=α0​z.​
(24)
If α0​>0 and I≫Is​, the increment becomes dI/dz≃α0​Is​=ℏω21​R2​(1−rAτ1​), approximately constant. Intensity then grows linearly, rather than exponentially, with distance while that strong-saturation regime persists. Negative α0​ gives saturable absorption and a decreasing beam.

Use an orthonormal basis of stationary states and project the Time-dependent Schrödinger equation onto state 2 after removing its free-evolution phase. The stationary Hamiltonian terms cancel the derivatives of those phases, leaving
iℏc˙2​=V21​(t)ei(E2​−E1​)t/ℏc1​+V22​(t)c2​.
(29)
Define d21​=⟨ψ2​∣ex∣ψ1​⟩ and d22​=⟨ψ2​∣ex∣ψ2​⟩. With the supplied time-dependent quantum perturbation,
ω0​=ℏE2​−E1​​,Ω=ℏEd21​​,V22​(t)=Ed22​cosωt,​
(30)
and therefore c˙2​=−iΩcos(ωt)eiω0​tc1​−iV22​c2​/ℏ. The state phases may be chosen so d21​ and Ω are real for this single driven pair; otherwise probabilities depend on ∣Ω∣2 and the conjugate matrix element appears in the equation for c1​. Our Ω is the resonant Rabi angular frequency in the usual convention whose rotating-wave off-diagonal Hamiltonian is ℏΩ/2.
For a field-free atom with symmetry under spatial reflection, each selected nondegenerate stationary orbital can be chosen with definite parity. Since x is odd, ⟨2∣x∣2⟩=−⟨2∣x∣2⟩, so V22​=0; the same reasoning gives V11​=0. Being an energy eigenstate alone is not sufficient. As an explicit mathematical counterexample, a displaced quantum harmonic oscillator with potential proportional to (x−xc​)2 has energy eigenstates with ⟨x⟩=xc​, giving V22​=eExc​cosωt when xc​=0. The vanishing used in the atomic model rests on its parity assumption, not merely on stationarity.

Resolve the weak broadband radiation into narrow frequency intervals whose relative phases are uncorrelated. The transition probabilities, rather than the coherent amplitudes, can then be added after ensemble or time averaging. For the x-polarized field, the energy density in one interval is ρ(ω)dω=ϵ0​Eω2​/2, so its squared coupling is ∣d21​∣2Eω2​/ℏ2. Inserting this into the weak rotating-wave result gives
P2​(t)=2ϵ0​ℏ2∣d21​∣2​∫0∞​ρ(ω)[(ω−ω0​)/2sin[(ω−ω0​)t/2]​]2dω.
(36)
For times long compared with the optical coherence time of the field, the narrow resonant kernel samples a nearly constant ρ(ω0​). Provided the resonance is well away from the zero-frequency boundary, extend the Rabi detuning integral to the full real line. Its value is 2πt, yielding
P2​(t)≃W1→2​t,W1→2​=ϵ0​ℏ2π∣⟨2∣ex∣1⟩∣2​ρ(ω0​).​
(37)
This is the stimulated absorption rate for the specified polarization and spectral radiation energy density per angular-frequency interval. The averaging window should also satisfy Wt≪1 while the lower state is treated as undepleted. A deterministic phase-locked broadband pulse would not in general permit adding these probabilities without its interference terms.
For isotropic unpolarized radiation with total spectral radiation energy density ρtot​, orientational averaging instead gives W=π∣d21​∣2ρtot​(ω0​)/(3ϵ0​ℏ2). The factor one third is not inserted into the original x-polarized formula. If ordinary frequency is used, ρν​=2πρω​ and the isotropic coefficient becomes ∣d21​∣2/(6ϵ0​ℏ2) multiplying ρν​(ν0​). The spectral convention and polarization must be stated together to avoid spurious factors of three or 2π.
