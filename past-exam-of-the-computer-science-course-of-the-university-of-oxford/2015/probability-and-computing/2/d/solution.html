If the bound in part (c) were available, the intended answer would be
jintended​(n)=2+⌈log2​(max{1,log2​n})⌉,n≥1.
(22)
Indeed 2jintended​(n)−2≥log2​n, so that bound would give xj(n)​≤n2−log2​n=1. Its growth is O(loglogn) in Big O notation. Because (c) is false, this substitution alone does not prove the result for the actual repeated singleton-removal allocation.
The requested order can nevertheless be established for the actual process. Here is a concrete, deliberately generous replacement, with j(0)=0 for the empty system:
j(n)={⌈log3/2​(2n)⌉,11+⌈log2​log2​n⌉,​1≤n<2120,n≥2120.​​
(23)
The large cutoff makes the numerical estimates below simple. A finite initial range does not affect the O(loglogn) asymptotic analysis.
First, whenever 2≤b≤n, a ball is served with probability at least
(1−n1​)n−1≥e−1>31​.
(24)
Thus part (a) gives E[Bt+1​∣Bt​]≤2Bt​/3, also for Bt​≤1. The law of total expectation yields xt​≤n(2/3)t. This proves xj(n)​≤1/2 for the small-n branch, including n=1, whose single ball is served immediately.
For the large-n branch, we need control of the random count, rather than an unjustified recurrence for its expectation. If b balls are allocated in a round, changing one chosen bin changes the number of singleton bins by at most two. The surviving count Fb​ therefore has the bounded differences property with constants 2. McDiarmid's inequality gives
P(Fb​>EFb​+u)≤exp(−2bu2​),u>0.
(25)
This can be applied conditionally after any history, since the next allocations are fresh independent random variables.
Use this concentration inequality in three stages. Write L=⌈log2​log2​n⌉.
  • For the first eight rounds, whenever b>n3/4, the conditional expectation is at most 2b/3. Taking u=b/12 shows that the next count is at most 3b/4, except with probability at most e−b/288. If the count has already fallen below n3/4, it stays there. Since (3/4)8<1/8 and n3/4≤n/8, this gives B8​≤n/8 outside the stated exceptional events.
  • Starting with a0​=n/8, set ar+1​=2ar2​/n. If B≤ar​ and ar​≥n3/4, part (a) and the union bound give EFB​≤ar2​/n. Taking u=ar2​/n gives a next count at most ar+1​, except with probability at most e−ar3​/(2n2)≤e−n1/4/2. The deterministic solution is ar​=(n/2)4−2r. It falls below n3/4 within L steps. After reaching that threshold the count cannot increase, so B8+L​≤n3/4 outside the exceptional events.
  • From b≤n3/4, the next conditional expectation is at most n1/2. A deviation u=n1/2 leaves at most 2n1/2 balls except with probability e−n1/4/2. From at most 2n1/2 balls, the next conditional expectation is at most 4. Taking u=n1/3−4≥n1/3/2 leaves at most n1/3 balls except with probability e−n1/6/16. Conditional on this final count bound, one more round has expectation at most n−1/3.
Every exceptional probability above is at most e−n1/6/300, and there are at most L+10 opportunities for an exception. For n≥2120, the elementary bounds n1/6/300≥4lnn and L+10≤n give, by the union bound, total exceptional probability at most n−3. The first numerical inequality holds at the cutoff and remains true because n1/6/lnn is increasing there. On an exceptional history there are at most n balls. Taking the last round's conditional expectation and then averaging gives
x11+L​≤n−1/3+nn−3=n−1/3+n−2<1.​
(26)
This proves the displayed replacement j(n) for the literal process without relying on either false assertion.
