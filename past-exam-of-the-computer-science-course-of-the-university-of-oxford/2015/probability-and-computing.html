The expectation of a real random variable X on a probability space is its integral against the probability measure:
EX=∫Ω​X(ω)dP(ω).​
(1)
For a discrete random variable, this becomes EX=∑x​xP(X=x); for a random variable with probability density function f, it becomes EX=∫−∞∞​xf(x)dx. A finite expectation requires E∣X∣<∞, making X an integrable random variable. An extended expectation can also be defined when just one of the positive and negative parts has finite expectation; the indeterminate difference ∞−∞ is not allowed.

For each of the b balls, let Ii​ be the indicator random variable that ball i survives the round of uniform balls-in-bins allocation. Condition on its chosen bin. Each of the other b−1 balls avoids that bin with probability 1−1/n, independently, so the probability that ball i is served is (1−1/n)b−1. Hence, for b≥2,
E[balls next round∣b]=b[1−(1−n1​)b−1].​
(15)
We used linearity of expectation to sum the indicator random variables; their mutual independence is unnecessary. For b=0 or b=1, the next count is zero. This states the boundary cases without an ambiguous 00 when n=1.

There is no proof of the printed assertion: it is false for the stated expectations. The original explicitly defines xj​ as the expected remaining count, so replacing it by a deterministic upper envelope changes the problem.[ref]
Let Bj​ be the actual count after j rounds and xj​=EBj​. The union bound gives the valid conditional estimate
E[Bj+1​∣Bj​=b]≤nb(b−1)​.
(16)
By the law of total expectation, the resulting unconditional bound is
xj+1​≤nE[Bj​(Bj​−1)]​=nxj2​−xj​+Var(Bj​)​.​
(17)
Discarding the variance term is an expectation closure error for a nonlinear recurrence. In particular, Jensen's inequality says EBj2​≥xj2​, which cannot justify the requested upper bound.
For an exact counterexample, start with two balls and two bins. A round serves both balls if they land separately and serves neither if they collide. Each outcome has probability 1/2, independently in successive rounds while both balls remain. Consequently
P(Bj​=2)=2−j,xj​=21−j.​
(18)
At j=2, the requested inequality would say x3​≤x22​/2, but
x3​=41​>2(1/2)2​=81​.​
(19)

If the bound in part (c) were available, the intended answer would be
jintended​(n)=2+⌈log2​(max{1,log2​n})⌉,n≥1.
(22)
Indeed 2jintended​(n)−2≥log2​n, so that bound would give xj(n)​≤n2−log2​n=1. Its growth is O(loglogn) in Big O notation. Because (c) is false, this substitution alone does not prove the result for the actual repeated singleton-removal allocation.
The requested order can nevertheless be established for the actual process. Here is a concrete, deliberately generous replacement, with j(0)=0 for the empty system:
j(n)={⌈log3/2​(2n)⌉,11+⌈log2​log2​n⌉,​1≤n<2120,n≥2120.​​
(23)
The large cutoff makes the numerical estimates below simple. A finite initial range does not affect the O(loglogn) asymptotic analysis.
First, whenever 2≤b≤n, a ball is served with probability at least
(1−n1​)n−1≥e−1>31​.
(24)
Thus part (a) gives E[Bt+1​∣Bt​]≤2Bt​/3, also for Bt​≤1. The law of total expectation yields xt​≤n(2/3)t. This proves xj(n)​≤1/2 for the small-n branch, including n=1, whose single ball is served immediately.
For the large-n branch, we need control of the random count, rather than an unjustified recurrence for its expectation. If b balls are allocated in a round, changing one chosen bin changes the number of singleton bins by at most two. The surviving count Fb​ therefore has the bounded differences property with constants 2. McDiarmid's inequality gives
P(Fb​>EFb​+u)≤exp(−2bu2​),u>0.
(25)
This can be applied conditionally after any history, since the next allocations are fresh independent random variables.
Use this concentration inequality in three stages. Write L=⌈log2​log2​n⌉.
  • For the first eight rounds, whenever b>n3/4, the conditional expectation is at most 2b/3. Taking u=b/12 shows that the next count is at most 3b/4, except with probability at most e−b/288. If the count has already fallen below n3/4, it stays there. Since (3/4)8<1/8 and n3/4≤n/8, this gives B8​≤n/8 outside the stated exceptional events.
  • Starting with a0​=n/8, set ar+1​=2ar2​/n. If B≤ar​ and ar​≥n3/4, part (a) and the union bound give EFB​≤ar2​/n. Taking u=ar2​/n gives a next count at most ar+1​, except with probability at most e−ar3​/(2n2)≤e−n1/4/2. The deterministic solution is ar​=(n/2)4−2r. It falls below n3/4 within L steps. After reaching that threshold the count cannot increase, so B8+L​≤n3/4 outside the exceptional events.
  • From b≤n3/4, the next conditional expectation is at most n1/2. A deviation u=n1/2 leaves at most 2n1/2 balls except with probability e−n1/4/2. From at most 2n1/2 balls, the next conditional expectation is at most 4. Taking u=n1/3−4≥n1/3/2 leaves at most n1/3 balls except with probability e−n1/6/16. Conditional on this final count bound, one more round has expectation at most n−1/3.
Every exceptional probability above is at most e−n1/6/300, and there are at most L+10 opportunities for an exception. For n≥2120, the elementary bounds n1/6/300≥4lnn and L+10≤n give, by the union bound, total exceptional probability at most n−3. The first numerical inequality holds at the cutoff and remains true because n1/6/lnn is increasing there. On an exceptional history there are at most n balls. Taking the last round's conditional expectation and then averaging gives
x11+L​≤n−1/3+nn−3=n−1/3+n−2<1.​
(26)
This proves the displayed replacement j(n) for the literal process without relying on either false assertion.

Consider any edge {u,v} of the simple graph. One endpoint comes first in the permutation σ; suppose it is u. Then v has a graph neighbour preceding it, namely u, so v∈/S(σ). Therefore no edge has both endpoints in S(σ):
S(σ) is an independent set for every σ.​

Choose a uniform random permutation of the n vertices, giving every one of the n! orders the same probability. For a fixed vertex i, consider i together with its di​ graph neighbours. The relative order of these di​+1 vertices is uniform, so each is equally likely to be first. The membership rule for S(σ) therefore gives
P(i∈S(σ))=di​+11​.​
(28)
Let Ii​ be the indicator random variable of this event. The cardinality is ∣S(σ)∣=∑i=1n​Ii​, and linearity of expectation gives
E∣S(σ)∣=i=1∑n​EIi​=i=1∑n​di​+11​.​
(29)
The indicator random variables are generally dependent; only the uniform random permutation and linearity of expectation were needed. An isolated vertex has di​=0 and is always included, as the formula correctly predicts.

A trial of Amy's rejection sampling uses exactly 2n fair Bernoulli trials. Exactly (n2n​) of the 22n equally likely binary sequences have n heads, so the acceptance probability is
pn​=22n(n2n​)​.
(31)
Independent retries make the number T of trials a geometric random variable on {1,2,…} with expectation 1/pn​. The total number C of coin tosses is 2nT, so
EC=(n2n​)2n22n​.​
(32)
Using Stirling's approximation, the central binomial coefficient satisfies
(n2n​)∼2πn(n/e)2n4πn​(2n/e)2n​=πn​4n​.
(33)
Consequently
EC∼2π​n3/2.​
(34)
The rejection sampling output is uniform among the balanced binary sequences: they have equal probability on every trial, and conditioning on acceptance preserves that equality. For n=0, the empty output needs zero tosses; the formula above concerns n≥1.

Betty's scheme is biased for every n≥2. The output HnTn occurs precisely when the first n tosses are heads: the algorithm stops there and appends n tails. Thus
P(HnTn)=2−n.
(35)
For comparison, take the output (HT)n. Its first 2n−2 symbols contain n−1 heads and n−1 tails, so neither stopping threshold has been reached. The next symbol is the nth head, at which point the last tail is appended. Its unique generating prefix has length 2n−1, giving
P((HT)n)=2−(2n−1).
(36)
Therefore
P((HT)n)P(HnTn)​=2n−1>1(n≥2).​
(37)
The faster stopping rule does not produce a uniform distribution on a finite set of balanced binary sequences. For example, at n=2 the six outputs have the following exact probabilities:
OutputBettyUniform target
HHTT1/41/6
HTHT1/81/6
HTTH1/81/6
THHT1/81/6
THTH1/81/6
TTHH1/41/6
For n=1, the two possible outputs each have probability 1/2, so that special case is uniform. For n=0, simply return the empty output.

c OurBigBook.com
Use uniform balanced binary sequence sampling by choosing the positions of the heads. Number the 2n=2m positions from zero to 2n−1. Start with no selected positions. Repeatedly toss the fair coin m times, interpret the resulting bits as a position, and select that position if it has not already been selected; otherwise discard this block of tosses and retry. Stop when n distinct positions have been selected. Put heads at those positions and tails everywhere else.
After s positions have been selected, every one of the remaining positions has the same probability of being the next new position. This follows either by summing a geometric series over rejected blocks or by conditioning a uniform distribution on a finite set on avoiding the selected positions. Every ordered list of n distinct selected positions consequently has probability
(2n)(2n−1)⋯(n+1)1​.
(38)
Each fixed n-element subset has n! such orders, so its probability is
(2n)(2n−1)⋯(n+1)n!​=(n2n​)1​.​
(39)
Thus every balanced binary sequence is equally likely.
When s positions are selected, a block succeeds with probability (2n−s)/(2n), so the geometric distribution gives expectation 2n/(2n−s) blocks until the next new position. By linearity of expectation, the total expected toss count is
ms=0∑n−1​2n−s2n​<2nm=O(nlogn),n≥1.​
(40)
The strict inequality holds since s<n, making each term less than two. A Boolean array records membership of each position; initializing it and constructing the output require O(n) additional work. The scheme terminates with probability one, since every stage has positive success probability.

Colour each edge of the complete graph Kn​ independently red or blue, with probability 1/2 for each colour. Every four-element subset A of vertices determines exactly one copy of K4​, containing six edges. These six edges are all red with probability 2−6 and all blue with probability 2−6. The events are disjoint events, so
P(A is monochromatic)=2⋅2−6=2−5.
(41)
Let IA​ be the indicator random variable for this event and let Z=∑A​IA​ count the monochromatic copies. Linearity of expectation gives
EZ=∑A​EIA​=(4n​)2−5.
(42)
The indicator random variables for overlapping copies need not be independent. Averaging over all edge colorings ensures that at least one has Z≤EZ. Thus the probabilistic method proves
Some two-colour edge coloring has Z≤321​(4n​).​
(43)
For 0≤n<4, both sides are zero and any edge coloring works. Copies are counted by their four-vertex subsets, rather than by their 4! possible labellings.

Set N=(4n​). Independently sample an edge coloring exactly as in part (d), count its monochromatic copies of K4​ by examining all four-vertex subsets, and accept precisely when 32Z≤N. Otherwise start over with a fresh edge coloring.
To prove an expected polynomial time bound, the existence argument alone is insufficient: we need a lower bound on the acceptance probability at this exact threshold. Since 32Z is a nonnegative integer with expectation N, rejection implies 32Z≥N+1. Markov's inequality therefore gives the integer-valued expectation threshold restart bound
P(reject)≤N+1N​,P(accept)≥N+11​.​
(44)
The number of attempts has a geometric distribution, with expectation at most N+1. Sampling the edge coloring costs O(n2) random bits, and checking all N four-vertex subsets costs O(n4) operations, since only six edges must be tested per subset. Hence
E[running time]=O((N+1)n4)=O(n8).​
(45)
This is a randomized algorithm that always returns an edge coloring meeting the original threshold and terminates with probability one. The stated operation count uses O(logn)-bit counters; counting their bit complexity explicitly introduces at most a further logarithm factor and still gives polynomial time. For n<4, return any edge coloring immediately.

Order the edges arbitrarily and fix their colours one at a time. After a partial assignment P, regard all unassigned edges as independent fair red/blue choices, and compute
Q(P)=E[Z∣P].
(46)
The conditional expectation of a monochromatic clique count is easy to compute. For each four-vertex subset A, let r be the number of its six edges still unassigned. Its contribution qA​(P) to Q(P) is
qA​(P)=⎩⎨⎧​0,2−r,21−r=1/32,​both colours already occur among its fixed edges,at least one edge is fixed, all in the same colour,none of its six edges is fixed.​
(47)
In the second case, every remaining edge must match the already fixed colour; this includes a completely fixed monochromatic copy, for which r=0 and the contribution is one. In the last case, the six unassigned edges may become either all red or all blue. By linearity of expectation,
Q(P)=∑∣A∣=4​qA​(P).
(48)
For the next unassigned edge e, compute Q(P,e=red) and Q(P,e=blue). The law of total expectation gives
Q(P)=21​Q(P,e=red)+21​Q(P,e=blue).
(49)
Choose the colour with the smaller value, resolving a tie by a fixed rule. At least one is at most their average, so the loop invariant is that Q never increases. Initially Q=(4n​)/32; once every edge is fixed, there is no randomness left and Q=Z. Therefore the method of conditional expectations guarantees
Z≤321​(4n​).​
(50)
A direct implementation recomputes both sums over all O(n4) four-vertex subsets for each of the O(n2) edges, requiring O(n6) arithmetic operations. The comparisons can be exact: every contribution is a multiple of 1/32, so store 32Q as an integer with O(logn) bits. This gives O(n6logn) bit complexity with elementary arithmetic, and hence a deterministic polynomial time construction. No random sampling is performed by this derandomization.
