Set Δx=xB​−xA​ and Δy=yB​−yA​. A displacement from A lies along the straight line precisely when its two-dimensional determinant with (Δx,Δy) vanishes. Thus an implicit equation is
Δy(x−xA​)−Δx(y−yA​)=0.​
(1)
Equivalently, write ax+by+c=0, with a=Δy, b=−Δx and c=ΔxyA​−ΔyxA​. The vector (a,b) is a normal vector. The formula handles vertical and horizontal straight lines without division. It presupposes A=B: coincident points do not determine a unique straight line.

The tight axis-aligned bounding box is Q=[0,2]×[0,2]. Bisect vertically at x=1, giving a right child R=[1,2]×[0,2]. For a scalar implicit function representing the same constructive solid geometry solid, use
Φ=max{f5​,f6​,f4​,f1​,min(f2​,f3​)},P={Φ≤0}.
(6)
With the convention fi​≤0 inside, set intersection corresponds to the maximum, and set union to the minimum. These operations encode membership; Φ is not a signed distance function.
Since every fi​ is an affine function, its extreme values on R occur at corners. Exact real-arithmetic interval arithmetic gives:
FunctionRange on Rf1​[−1−3​,3​]f2​[−1,1]f3​[0,1]f4​[−2,0]f5​[−2,−1]f6​[−2,0]min(f2​,f3​), propagated[−1,1]Φ, propagated[−1,3​]
Each leaf range is exact, but combining independent intervals forgets that the expressions share x,y. The final enclosure need not be the exact range of Φ. For example, equality Φ=−1 would require both f6​≤−1, hence y≥1, and min(f2​,f3​)≤−1, hence y≤0 on R, which is impossible. The enclosure is still sufficient for a reliable three-way decision:
supΦ(R)<0: inside;infΦ(R)>0: outside;0∈Φ(R): unresolved, so refine.​
(7)
Here the result is unresolved. This is a real mixed box, rather than only a pessimistic test: (5/4,3/4) is strictly inside, whereas (3/2,3/2) is outside. A zero endpoint alone can also mean contact with the boundary, so equality must not be treated as proof of strict interior or strict exterior. In floating-point arithmetic, certified bounds require outward rounding.
Continue splitting unresolved boxes, normally alternating axes or splitting the longest side. Propagate the interval arithmetic results through the constructive solid geometry tree, discard exterior boxes and stop refining strict interior boxes. In sufficiently small boundary boxes, locate crossings of the active implicit equations with box edges and connect them into boundary line segments. Retain corners and resolve ambiguous multiple crossings by further refinement; here every genuine boundary piece is straight, so its supporting implicit equation allows exact intersection rather than a curved approximation. A maximum boundary-box diagonal ε gives spatial localization at that scale, provided no boundary components are discarded; corner-sign sampling alone provides no such guarantee.
The tight box saves empty-space work, but it is awkward for extracting facets. Entire boundary line segments, notably EO, OA and DE, lie on the initial box boundary, and CD lies on the first splitting straight line. A sign-change-only method may have no exterior sample on the other side and lose such pieces; exact zeros also persist through refinement. A slightly padded initial axis-aligned bounding box, together with an explicit rule for boundary-coincident pieces, makes the faceting more robust. The tight box is therefore economical for enclosure but not the best unqualified choice for a simple faceting implementation.

Three useful geometric properties, with their concrete consequences here, are:
  • Endpoint interpolation. F(0)=A=(−2,0) and F(1)=C=(0,0). The middle control point B is a handle, not an interpolated point: Fy​(t)≤1/2, so the curve never reaches B=(0,1).
  • Endpoint tangents. F′(0)=2(B−A)=(4,2) and F′(1)=2(C−B)=(0,−2). Thus the tangent lines are parallel to the first and last sides of the control polygon, respectively; both endpoint derivatives are nonzero.
  • Convex-hull containment. The three Bernstein basis weights are nonnegative and sum to one on [0,1]. Every F(t) is therefore a convex combination of A,B,C, so the entire Bézier curve lies in their filled triangle.
These give the compact answer
endpoint interpolation; control-edge endpoint tangents; convex-hull containment.​
(20)
The same sum-to-one calculation also explains affine equivariance of a geometric basis: applying an affine map to the handles applies that map to the curve.

For solid modelling, a polyhedron is a solid whose boundary consists of finitely many planar polygons, called faces, meeting along straight edges and at vertices of a polyhedron. A face is not merely a list of arbitrary spatial points: its boundary must lie in one plane and bound a valid planar region. The boundary is closed and consistently oriented so that inside and outside are defined.
For example, a tetrahedron has four triangular faces, six edges and four vertices of a polyhedron. A more general polyhedron need not be convex and can have handles or cavities. The concise definition is
a solid enclosed by finitely many planar polygonal faces.​
(39)

For an ordinary closed manifold boundary representation, three requirements are:
  • Valid embedded faces. Each face is planar and bounded by simple closed edge loops with nonzero area; distinct face interiors do not cross, and faces meet only in their declared common edges or vertices of a polyhedron. A quadrilateral with four noncoplanar vertices of a polyhedron is not one planar face; a bow-tie loop does not bound a simple face. Holes, when allowed, need separate correctly oriented loops.
  • Closed manifold incidence. Exactly two faces meet at each edge, and the incident faces around each vertex of a polyhedron form one cyclic fan. A single missing face leaves edges incident with only one face. Three faces sharing an edge, or two closed shells touching only at one vertex of a polyhedron, violate the local surface condition. The second example shows why the edge count alone is insufficient.
  • Consistent orientation. Adjacent oriented faces traverse their common edge in opposite directions, giving compatible outward normal vectors. Reversing just one face of a tetrahedron breaks this condition. For a cavity, the material's outward normal vector points into the cavity, so shell orientation must also respect which region is solid.
Together these support the answer
embedded planar faces; closed manifold incidence; consistent outward orientation.​
(40)
For a connected orientable closed boundary of genus g, the Euler characteristic provides an additional check, V−E+F=2−2g, when faces are disk-like. The familiar value 2 applies to a sphere-like shell; it must not be imposed blindly on every polyhedron. Correct counts alone do not exclude intersections or invalid local incidence.

A boundary representation separates geometry, which supplies locations and supporting planes, from topology, which specifies incidence and orientation. A practical winged-edge data structure uses three related record types:
  • A vertex of a polyhedron record holds its three coordinates and a reference to one incident edge, sufficient to begin traversing the local fan.
  • A face record holds its supporting plane, an outward normal vector or equivalent orientation, and a reference to a boundary edge. Faces with holes need one entry per boundary loop.
  • An edge record holds its two endpoint vertices of a polyhedron, its left and right incident faces, and predecessor and successor edges around each of those faces: four “wing” references. “Left” and “right” refer to a chosen orientation of the edge and compatible face traversal conventions.
This explicitly stores adjacency rather than recovering it by searching all faces. A half-edge data structure expresses much the same information with two oppositely directed records per undirected edge: each has an origin vertex of a polyhedron, an incident face, its next boundary record and its opposite record; a previous record is often stored as well. The destination can be obtained from the opposite record's origin. This mesh record is distinct from a dangling half-edge of a graph.
The compact implementation answer is
vertex geometry + oriented face loops + explicit edge/face/vertex incidence.​
(41)
There is deliberate redundancy: the same adjacency can be reached from both an edge and a face, a stored previous edge is recoverable by walking the loop, and a face normal vector can be computed from its ordered noncollinear vertices of a polyhedron. These copies make neighbourhood queries and local edits cheap, and cached plane equations speed geometric tests. They impose consistency obligations: an edit must update reciprocal references, loops, orientations and affected geometric caches together. A half-edge data structure should share the undirected edge's geometry or keep its paired records synchronized.

Use the straightforward incremental three-dimensional convex hull algorithm. Assume distinct input points, at least four noncoplanar points, and general position with no four coplanar. Use exact orientation decisions, or robust predicates that certify the same signs. These assumptions avoid the separate bookkeeping for flat faces, points on the boundary and lower-dimensional initial data.
  1. Select four points forming a nondegenerate tetrahedron. Store its four triangular faces with outward normal vectors and consistent adjacency.
  2. For the next point p, evaluate every current oriented face f=(a,b,c) using
    σf​(p)=((b−a)×(c−a))⋅(p−a).
    (43)
    The current hull lies in σf​≤0. Thus a face is visible from p exactly when σf​(p)>0.
  3. If no face is visible, discard p: it lies in the current convex hull. Otherwise identify the horizon of a convex polyhedron seen from p, namely the edges incident with exactly one visible and one retained face.
  4. Delete the visible faces and cone the ordered horizon of a convex polyhedron to p, adding one triangle per horizon edge. Orient each new face consistently outward and continue with the next point.
The determinant σf​ is six times the oriented volume of the tetrahedron abcp; only its sign matters. A zero value needs a declared degeneracy policy if the general-position assumption is relaxed.
The data structure update is a separate local operation. Mark visible faces and walk their adjacency to collect the horizon cycle before deleting anything. Remove edges with two visible incident faces; retain horizon edges and their retained-face links. Insert a vertex of a polyhedron record for p and a new edge from p to each horizon vertex of a polyhedron. Add one face per horizon edge, connect its other two edges to its neighbours in the cone, and replace the deleted face reference on each horizon edge. Restore the cyclic boundary references and each retained vertex of a polyhedron's incident-edge reference; remove any old vertices of a polyhedron left without incident faces.
For an outward visible face with boundary edge a→b on the horizon, the new outward face can be ordered (a,b,p). Its retained neighbour already traverses that edge as b→a. As an independent orientation check, an interior point of the old hull must evaluate negatively against the new face.
The correctness step is geometric: the nonvisible part of a convex polyhedron remains supporting, while sight lines from p to the horizon create precisely the new supporting faces. Replacing the visible cap by its cone therefore gives
Hi+1​=conv(Hi​∪{p}).​
(44)
Induction from the initial tetrahedron yields the convex hull of all input points.

With i processed points, a triangulated three-dimensional convex hull has O(i) faces and edges. For a sphere-like triangular boundary, the Euler characteristic and 3F=2E give F=2V−4 and E=3V−6, with V≤i. The complete face scan, horizon construction and adjacency update in the implementation of part (e) therefore cost O(i) per insertion. Consequently,
T(n)=O(i=4∑n−1​i)=O(n2),working storage O(n).​
(45)
The quadratic worst case really occurs for this implementation. Choose all input points in strictly convex position, for example distinct points (t,t2,t3) on the three-dimensional moment curve. Each processed point remains a hull vertex of a polyhedron, so the hull has 2i−4 triangular faces after i insertions. Scanning every face at every next insertion takes Θ(n2) sign tests. This argument does not require every insertion to delete most faces: the repeated global scans already force the bound. More elaborate convex hull algorithms with different search machinery are not the implementation being analysed here.

Insert the points in their given order, starting from ABCD. Use outward face orientation and regard f≤0 as the interior side. The initial tetrahedron is nondegenerate because
((B−A)×(C−A))⋅(D−A)=(−2,0,2)⋅(0,1,1)=2=0.
(46)
Thus its volume is ∣2∣/6=1/3. The following table gives consistently oriented faces, conveniently scaled plane equations, and the omitted old vertex of a polyhedron test:

Every opposite vertex of a polyhedron is strictly on the interior side, so these orientations are correct. The intersection of these four half-spaces is exactly the initial tetrahedron.
Now test E=(1,0,0). In the same row order the face expressions give
(fACB​(E),fABD​(E),fADC​(E),fBCD​(E))=(1,1,−3,−3).​
(47)
Therefore ACB and ABD are visible and must be removed; ADC and BCD are retained. The common edge AB lies inside the deleted patch and is removed. The directed horizon is
A⟶C⟶B⟶D⟶A.
(48)

−2x−y+z−1≤0,−2x+y+z−1≤0,x−y−2z−1≤0,x+y−2z−1≤0,x+y+z−1≤0,x−y+z−1≤0.​​

Equivalently assemble the closed oriented boundary from ADC,BCD,ACE,CBE,BDE,DAE, with adjacent face loops traversing shared edges in opposite directions. It is a triangular bipyramid with base triangle CDE and apices A,B; CDE is internal, so it is not a boundary face. There are five vertices of a polyhedron, nine edges and six faces, giving the check 5−9+6=2. The initial tetrahedron similarly has 4−6+4=2.
