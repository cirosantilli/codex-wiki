a OurBigBook.com
For a finite normal-form game, a mixed strategy σi​ is a probability distribution over player i's pure strategies. Independent randomization by the players defines an expected payoff ui​(σ). A strategy profile σ is a Nash equilibrium if
ui​(σi​,σ−i​)≥ui​(τi​,σ−i​)
(1)
for every player i and every alternative mixed strategy τi​. By linearity in a player's own distribution, it is enough to check all pure deviations. Equivalently, every pure strategy in the support of a mixed strategy must be a best response to the other players' choices.

b OurBigBook.com
With rows and columns ordered Head, Tail, the payoff matrix of ordered pairs is
HT​H(−2,2)(Y,−Y)​T(X,−X)(−2,2)​​.
(2)
This is a zero-sum game. There is no pure Nash equilibrium: when the coins match, player 1 gains by switching to a mismatch; when they do not match, player 2 gains by making them match. Since X,Y>0, these improvements are strict.
Let p and q be the respective probabilities that players 1 and 2 choose Head. Player 1's payoffs from Head and Tail are X−(X+2)q and (Y+2)q−2. Player 2 is indifferent when the corresponding player-1 payoffs against its two pure actions, Y−(Y+2)p and (X+2)p−2, agree. The resulting unique fully mixed Nash equilibrium is
p=X+Y+4Y+2​,q=X+Y+4X+2​.​
(3)
Both numbers lie strictly between zero and one. If either player instead used a pure action, the other's unique best response would force the pure-action cycle above, so there are no additional partly mixed equilibria. Substitution gives player 1's expected payoff (XY−4)/(X+Y+4) and its negative for player 2.
For X=1,Y=3, the best response correspondences are
BR1​(q)=⎩⎨⎧​{1},[0,1],{0},​q<3/8,q=3/8,q>3/8,​BR2​(p)=⎩⎨⎧​{0},[0,1],{1},​p<5/8,p=5/8,p>5/8.​
(4)
Their intersection is (p,q)=(5/8,3/8), with expected payoffs (−1/8,1/8).

d OurBigBook.com
Under the intended positive rewards 0<h<d, a hunter at the all-hare strategy profile receives h. A unilateral switch to deer leaves only one participant. Since the integer threshold satisfies k>n/2 with n≥3, we have k≥2, so that deer hunt fails and pays zero. Thus no player can gain by switching, and the all-hare profile is a Nash equilibrium. At h=0 it remains an equilibrium through indifference; negative h would invalidate the claim.

e OurBigBook.com
Assume 0<h<d and the feasible threshold 2≤k≤n. An unsuccessful deer hunter would gain by switching to hare, so an equilibrium with deer participants must have s≥k. A deer hunter then earns d/s and must not gain by changing to hare; this requires d/s≥h. A hare hunter who joins would earn d/(s+1), so staying with hare requires h≥d/(s+1). Hence the necessary and sufficient conditions for the stated range 0<s<n are
k≤s<n,s+1d​≤h≤sd​.​
(5)
These conditions check every possible unilateral change in this two-action game, so they also prove sufficiency. Equality is allowed because a Nash equilibrium rules out strict improvements, not indifferent deviations. The endpoint s=n has no hare hunter to check; it is an equilibrium exactly when d/n≥h.

Continue with 0<h<d and set r=d/h. Part (e) requires an interior count to satisfy s≤r≤s+1 with s≥k; the all-deer endpoint s=n is allowed when r≥n. If r is nonintegral and k≤r<n, only s=⌊r⌋ works. If r=m is an integer with k+1≤m≤n, both s=m−1 and s=m work. At r=k only s=k remains, while r>n permits only s=n. When r<k, there is no positive equilibrium count.
Thus there are at most two positive counts, and exactly one precisely when
hd​≥kandhd​∈/{k+1,k+2,…,n}.​
(6)
This includes k=n, when the excluded set is empty. It counts numbers of deer hunters, not the many choices of which hunters participate. The argument is the threshold stag-hunt equilibrium count.
The original paper states only h<d explicitly. Its assertion needs the usual strict positivity of the hare reward. If zero is included, take n=6,k=4,d=1,h=0. Counts s=1 and s=2 both give unsuccessful hunters zero, and one additional deer hunter still cannot reach the threshold; all unilateral alternatives also pay zero. The count s=6 is an equilibrium as well. These are three positive equilibrium counts, contradicting an unrestricted reading. More generally, for h=0<d, the positive counts are 1,…,k−2 and n.

a OurBigBook.com
A game tree is a rooted tree representing successive histories. A nonterminal vertex specifies the player who moves, its outgoing edges are the available actions, and each leaf specifies a terminal payoff for every player. A pure strategy specifies an action at every decision point belonging to that player, including decision points not reached by the eventual play.
For a perfect-information game, each player observes the exact preceding history. An extensive-form game with imperfect information additionally groups indistinguishable decision points into information sets, with compatible available actions. Chance moves, if present, carry specified probabilities; the displayed game has none. A subgame perfect equilibrium must prescribe a Nash equilibrium in every proper subgame as well as in the whole game.

Solution

Words: 156
b OurBigBook.com
Apply backward induction in games, or Zermelo's algorithm, from the terminal decision points. At node 4, player 1 chooses R4​ because 6>3, giving continuation payoffs (6,9). At node 5, player 1 chooses L5​ because 4>2, giving (4,6). At node 3, player 2 compares its continuation payoffs nine and six, so chooses L3​. At node 2, player 2 chooses R2​, receiving four rather than two.
At the root, player 1 consequently receives six from either action: L1​ leads to (6,4) and R1​ to (6,9). Thus there are two pure subgame perfect equilibria, with strategies
(L1​,R4​,L5​; R2​,L3​),(R1​,R4​,L5​; R2​,L3​).​
(7)
The semicolon separates the players, and each list specifies the player's action at every one of its decision points. All four non-root choices are strict. If mixed strategies are included, every mixture between L1​ and R1​ at the root is also an equilibrium, with those other actions fixed. There are no further mixtures at the strict decision points.

c OurBigBook.com
For each history of the first two votes, voter 3 selects its favourite achievable winner. Writing rows for voter 1's vote and columns for voter 2's vote, the resulting continuation winners are
ABC​AABC​BBBB​CCBC​​.
(8)
For example, after votes B,C, voter 3 can make either B or C win, and prefers B; a vote for A would instead produce the tie-break winner A.
Voter 2 prefers C to B to A. Optimizing each row therefore gives continuation winners C,B,C after voter 1's respective choices A,B,C. Voter 1 prefers B to C and therefore votes B, giving winner B in every subgame perfect equilibrium.
Some histories admit several equally good votes, but they produce the same winning candidate. Since each voter strictly ranks the three possible winners, unique outcome under strict outcome preferences makes this winner independent of those action ties. This is backward induction over outcomes, not a claim that the entire equilibrium strategy profile is unique.

Yes: let the order be voter 2, then voter 3, then voter 1. When voter 1 moves last, it makes A win whenever that is possible; otherwise it prefers B to C. For the first two votes in this new order, its optimal continuation winners are
ABC​AAAA​BABA​CAAC​​.
(9)
Rows now record voter 2's initial choice and columns voter 3's choice. In a three-way split, the specified tie-break produces A, which explains, for example, the off-diagonal B,C entry.
Voter 3 optimizes its ranking B≻C≻A in each row, giving the continuation winners A,B,C. Voter 2 then chooses C, its favourite of the three. The winner is therefore C in every subgame perfect equilibrium, again by unique outcome under strict outcome preferences. In particular, if voter 2 starts with C, voter 3 must also vote C: either other vote lets the last voter force A.

e OurBigBook.com
In the final round, player 1 strictly accepts every q2​>0. In any subgame perfect equilibrium it must also accept q2​=0 with probability one. Otherwise, if that acceptance probability were a<1, offering zero would give player 2 only aα. A sufficiently small positive offer would be accepted and yield more. No positive offer maximizes player 2's payoff either, because a smaller positive offer improves it. The same supremum argument rules out a mixed offer distribution attaining the missing maximum. Thus the final-round equilibrium uniquely has q2​=0, accepted, with payoffs (0,α).
In round one, player 2 therefore rejects q1​<α and accepts q1​>α. At q1​=α it is indifferent, but equilibrium again forces acceptance with probability one: if acceptance were less likely, player 1 could improve on any proposed optimum with an offer just above α. Since α<1, player 1 strictly prefers the resulting positive payoff to rejection.
Consequently the unique equilibrium outcome is immediate agreement at q1​=α, giving payoffs (1−α,α). The full strategies are: player 1 offers α initially and accepts every nonnegative final offer; player 2 accepts initial offers at least α, rejects smaller ones, and offers zero in every reached final-round subgame. These prescriptions cover off-path histories too. Acceptance at indifference follows from the continuous-offer bargaining acceptance lemma, rather than an arbitrary tie-breaking convention.

Let Pk​ be the initial proposer's share, measured as a fraction of the pie currently available, with k rounds remaining. The last-round argument in part (e) gives P1​=1. Rejecting an offer leaves the responder as next proposer with a pie smaller by factor α, so its continuation value is αPk−1​. The current proposer offers exactly that amount, which is accepted by the continuous-offer bargaining acceptance lemma. Hence
Pk​=1−αPk−1​,Pk​=1−α+α2−⋯+(−α)k−1=1+α1−(−α)k​.​
(10)
The formula follows by summing a finite geometric series, or by induction in the recurrence. Agreement is immediate in every subgame, so this share is player 1's actual initial payoff.
For odd k, Pk​=(1+αk)/(1+α) lies above 1/(1+α) and decreases with k. For even k, Pk​=(1−αk)/(1+α) lies below that value and increases with k. Thus prefer odd to even, fewer rounds among odd choices, and more rounds among even choices. For the given k1​<k2​, this simplifies to choose k1​ if k1​ is odd, and choose k2​ if k1​ is even. All comparisons are strict because 0<α<1. This is the finite-horizon bargaining parity effect.

For an n-player transferable utility game with v(∅)=0, the Shapley value is
ϕi​(v)=S⊆N∖{i}∑​n!∣S∣!(n−∣S∣−1)!​(v(S∪{i})−v(S)).​
(11)
It averages player i's marginal contribution over uniformly random orders of all players. Exactly ∣S∣!(n−∣S∣−1)! of the n! permutations put precisely S before i. For a cooperative cost game, apply the same linear formula to the cost function; the result represents payments instead of distributed benefits.

b OurBigBook.com
Take three players and define the simple cooperative game
v(S)={1,0,​1∈S and S∩{2,3}=∅,otherwise.​
(12)
It is a superadditive coalitional game: two disjoint coalitions cannot both have value one because each would need player 1. If one has value one, their union does too; if neither does, the desired inequality is immediate.
The vector (1,0,0) belongs to the core of a cooperative game. More strongly, it is the unique core vector: singleton constraints give nonnegative coordinates, efficiency gives sum one, and the value-one coalitions {1,2} and {1,3} force x3​=x2​=0.
In a random player ordering, player 1 is pivotal unless it comes first, giving Shapley value 2/3. Player 2 is pivotal only in order 1,2,3, and player 3 only in order 1,3,2. Thus
ϕ=(2/3,1/6,1/6),Core(v)={(1,0,0)}.​
(13)
The coalition {1,2} receives only 5/6<1, so the Shapley value is outside the core. This proves that superadditivity and nonempty core do not imply Shapley stability.

Put c(∅)=0. For nonempty coalitions, the runway required by a union is the longer of the two required runways, so
c(S∪T)=max(c(S),c(T))≤c(S)+c(T).
(14)
The inequality uses nonnegative costs and also holds when either coalition is empty. Thus the cost function is subadditive, in particular for the stated disjoint coalitions. A subadditive cost game becomes a superadditive coalitional game after changing signs to v=−c.

d OurBigBook.com
Choose one plane requiring the longest runway and assign it the entire cost c(N); assign every other plane zero. These nonnegative payments sum to c(N). Any coalition containing that chosen plane also requires the whole longest runway, so its payment is exactly its standalone cost. Any coalition without it pays zero, which is at most its nonnegative standalone cost.
Hence this payment vector is in the cost core, proving the desired sharing always exists. This is an existence certificate, not a fairness assertion. If there are no planes, the empty allocation has total zero. The Shapley value of an airport game gives a more symmetric sharing rule and also lies in the core of this cooperative cost game.

Decompose the required runway into successive layers. The first layer has cost s and is needed by all four planes. The next costs 2s and is needed by the three medium-or-long planes. The final layer costs 4s and is needed only by the longest plane.
For each layer, the first requiring plane in a uniformly random ordering contributes its entire cost. Each of its requiring planes has equal probability of being first. By linearity of the Shapley value, divide each layer cost equally among those requiring it. The payments are therefore
ϕshort​=4s​,ϕmedium,1​=ϕmedium,2​=4s​+32s​=1211s​,ϕlong​=4s​+32s​+4s=1259s​.​
(15)
They sum to 7s=c(N). The equality of the two medium payments follows from their identical runway requirements; the random-order argument proves the amounts, not just their equality.

f OurBigBook.com
Remove any plane types with zero count, retaining the increasing order of the remaining lengths; if none remain, return the empty allocation. For the retained types put s0​=0 and Ni​=∑ℓ=ik​nℓ​. Then every plane of type j has Shapley value
ϕj​=λi=1∑j​Ni​si​−si−1​​.​
(16)
Compute suffix counts Ni​ in a backward pass. In a forward pass, accumulate A←A+λ(si​−si−1​)/Ni​ and output A as the common payment for type i.
For correctness, layer i costs λ(si​−si−1​) and is required by exactly Ni​ planes. A particular requiring plane is earliest among these in a random ordering with probability 1/Ni​. Its expected marginal contribution to the layer is therefore that layer's cost divided by Ni​. Summing the layers it requires proves the formula. This is the Shapley value of an airport game.
The two passes use O(k) arithmetic operations. If every individual payment must be explicitly listed, add the unavoidable O(∣N∣) output work. For counts given in binary, the compact per-type output is polynomial in the input size; expanding an exponentially large number of identical entries need not be. With integer lengths and rational λ, all fractions can be computed exactly with polynomial bit complexity: a common denominator divides a product of at most k suffix counts, whose bit length is O(klog∣N∣), in addition to the input denominator of λ. For arbitrary real λ, the same rational factors give symbolic multiples of it.
