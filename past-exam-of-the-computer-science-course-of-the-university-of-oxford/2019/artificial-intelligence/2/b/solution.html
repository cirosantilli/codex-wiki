Use the knight-variable representation and build a placement one knight at a time. The initial state is the empty placement. An action places the next knight on an empty square attacking no previously placed knight; the search transition model appends that square. The goal test is that k knights have been placed. Each action adds exactly one, so any solution has depth k.
Put N=n2. At depth j, there are at most N−j choices, hence branching factor at most N. A coarse search tree size bound is 1+N+⋯+Nk. Keeping the shrinking choice count gives the sharper bound
∑j=0k​(N)j​,(N)j​=(N−j)!N!​,
(8)
where (N)j​ is a falling factorial. This counts ordered placements before attack pruning.
For a full expansion down to depth k, the number of depth-k leaves is at most (N)k​, or approximately n2k when k is small relative to N. If Ik​ denotes the number of legal unlabelled k-knight placements, the exact number of legal depth-k leaves is k!Ik​. There can also be earlier dead-end leaves: a legal placement may leave no further nonattacking square. Writing Mj​ for the number of inclusion-maximal legal j-knight placements, the fully explored pruned tree has
k!Ik​+∑j=0k−1​j!Mj​
(9)
leaves. Its leaf count is still at most the unpruned (N)k​, since each early leaf replaces a disjoint nonempty subtree of the unpruned tree. Breadth-first search that stops at its first goal can construct less of the last layer; if no solution exists, it exhausts the reachable partial placements.
Identifying different insertion orders changes the representation. With a state that is an unlabelled set of occupied squares and repeated-state elimination, the corresponding depth-k state bound is (kN​), with Ik​ legal goal states. The factorial and binomial bounds refer to these different representations, not competing counts of the same tree.
