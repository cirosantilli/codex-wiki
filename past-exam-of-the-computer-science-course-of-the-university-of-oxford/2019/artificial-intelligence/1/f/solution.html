For the three-colourability problem, represent a candidate by a complete colour assignment c:V→{r,g,b}, initially chosen at random. Give it the conflict objective function
H(c)=∑{u,v}∈E​1c(u)=c(v)​.
(4)
A neighbor changes the colour of one vertex to either other colour. For hill climbing, maximize −H, or equivalently repeatedly choose a recolouring that reduces H. One can inspect all 2∣V∣ neighbors, or focus on conflicted vertices and evaluate their alternative colours using only incident edges. Stop successfully as soon as H=0, which is exactly a proper graph colouring with three colours.
If no improving move exists at positive H, the method is stuck in a local minimum. Random restarts, limited sideways moves or other exploratory local search choices can reduce this failure mode, but do not turn a finite unsuccessful run into a proof of impossibility. On a graph with no proper graph colouring using three colours, every complete colouring has H>0, so this procedure cannot return a valid colouring. Conversely, a positive-conflict stopping point also occurs on some graphs admitting a proper graph colouring with three colours. Failure of hill climbing alone does not certify non-colourability. For a concrete trap, use a bipartite graph with three vertices on each side and every cross-edge, and give each side one vertex of each colour. There are three conflicts. Recolouring any single vertex leaves it with exactly one same-colour neighbor, so no single change improves H, even though colouring each whole side differently solves the instance.
