For deterministic classical responses, denote the two possible local answers by x0​,x1​, y0​,y1​ and z0​,z1​. Certainty in the GHZ game would require
x0​⊕y0​⊕z0​x0​⊕y1​⊕z1​x1​⊕y0​⊕z1​x1​⊕y1​⊕z0​​=0,=1,=1,=1.​
(18)
Taking the exclusive or of all four equations gives zero on the left, since every answer occurs twice, but one on the right. This contradiction rules out deterministic certainty. Pre-shared randomness cannot cure it: conditioning on the random seed fixes a deterministic response table, and none succeeds on every allowed input. Thus noncommunicating classical players cannot win with certainty. No uniform distribution on the referee's questions is needed for this worst-case claim.
For the quantum strategy, share the GHZ state and measure the Pauli X gate observable when the local question is 0, or the Pauli Y gate observable when it is 1. Return bit 0 for measurement eigenvalue +1 and bit 1 for eigenvalue −1. The four relevant operator identities are
(X⊗X⊗X)∣GHZ⟩(X⊗Y⊗Y)∣GHZ⟩(Y⊗X⊗Y)∣GHZ⟩(Y⊗Y⊗X)∣GHZ⟩​=∣GHZ⟩,=−∣GHZ⟩,=−∣GHZ⟩,=−∣GHZ⟩.​
(19)
For the first identity, the three bit flips exchange ∣000⟩ and ∣111⟩. For each remaining identity, the two Y actions contribute i2=−1 on ∣000⟩ and (−i)2=−1 on ∣111⟩, so they exchange the terms with a minus sign. Local measurements on separate systems commute, and the product of their observed eigenvalues is therefore fixed by the displayed joint eigenvalue. That product equals (−1)x+y+z, giving exactly the required output parity for each promised input. Hence the quantum strategy wins with certainty without communication. This is the GHZ theorem in nonlocal-game form; it does not let a player control another player's individual outcome.
