Use a simultaneous two-player normal-form game, with X choosing the row and Y the column. Each player's pure strategies are b and nb; a payoff pair lists X first. Measure every dataset size in millions and use the stated normalization M=1. The source's reference to a shared half as “points” is interpreted in the same million-point units as the corpus, so the shared addition is n/2 to each initial dataset.
The bimatrix game has payoffs
X:bX:nb​Y:b(2(n+3)n+2​−2P​, 2(n+3)n+4​−2P​)(n+31​, n+3n+2​−P)​Y:nb(n+3n+1​−P, n+32​)(31​, 32​).​​
(1)
For example, joint purchase gives dataset sizes 1+n/2 and 2+n/2, total 3+n, and each buyer pays P/2. A sole buyer gets the full corpus and pays P. The total payoff is 1−P whenever a purchase occurs and 1 when neither buys: the game is not a zero-sum game, even though gross market shares always sum to one.
For the following calculations, set
t=n+3n​,0<t<1.
(2)
The payoff differences “buy minus do not buy,” with the other player's action held fixed, are
ΔX​ΔY​​opponent does not buy32t​−P3t​−P​opponent buys2t−P​2t−P​.​​
(3)
These four differences give all the best response comparisons without mixing up row and column payoffs.

b OurBigBook.com
A weakly dominant strategy must be a best response to either opponent action. For X this requires
32t​−P≥0,2t−P​≥0.
(4)
The first is stronger because t>0. Thus
X: b is weakly dominant iff 0<P≤3(n+3)2n​.​
(5)
For Y the comparisons are t/3−P≥0 and (t−P)/2≥0, giving
Y: b is weakly dominant iff 0<P≤3(n+3)n​.​
(6)
Below each upper endpoint the corresponding strategy is strictly better against both opponent actions; at the endpoint one comparison ties and the other is strict. Thus the answers also satisfy the convention that weak dominance requires a strict advantage somewhere. Since expected payoffs are linear in the opponent's mixing probability, checking both opponent pure strategies suffices for every opponent mixed strategy.

At joint purchase, each player's gain from continuing to buy rather than deviating to nb is (t−P)/2. Both actions are best responses exactly when this is nonnegative. Therefore
(b,b) is a Nash equilibrium iff 0<P≤n+3n​.​
(7)
At equality both players are indifferent to their individual unilateral deviations, which is permitted in a Nash equilibrium. The condition is less restrictive than requiring either player's action to be a weakly dominant strategy: only the opponent's actual action needs to be considered.

When neither buys, X's unilateral deviation gains 2t/3−P, while Y's gains t/3−P. For both to be nonpositive, P must exceed the larger threshold. Hence
(nb,nb) is a Nash equilibrium iff P≥3(n+3)2n​.​
(8)
Equality is included because a zero-gain deviation does not violate a Nash equilibrium. Consequently both joint-purchase and joint-nonpurchase Nash equilibria exist throughout 2t/3≤P≤t, a coordination region.

Solution

e OurBigBook.com
Neither asymmetric purchase outcome is a Nash equilibrium for any permitted P>0,n>0. At (b,nb), X's chosen purchase requires P≤2t/3, but Y's chosen nonpurchase, against X buying, requires P≥t. These requirements are incompatible because 2t/3<t.
At (nb,b), Y's chosen purchase requires P≤t/3, whereas X's nonpurchase against a buying Y requires P≥t. Again the inequalities are incompatible. Thus every Nash equilibrium in pure strategies has both players taking the same action; the conclusion is stronger than merely observing symmetry in their available actions, since their original market shares differ.

Write P=999/1000. If
t>P⟺n+3n​>1000999​⟺n>2997​,
(9)
then both players prefer buying against a buyer, since (t−P)/2>0. Both prefer not buying against a nonbuyer: 2t/3<2/3<P and t/3<1/3<P. These strict opposite best responses give the interior crossing of a coordination game.
An explicit fully mixed Nash equilibrium follows from indifference. Let p be X's probability of buying and q Y's. X is indifferent when
(1−q)(32t​−P)+q2t−P​=0,
(10)
and Y is indifferent when
(1−p)(3t​−P)+p2t−P​=0.
(11)
Solving gives
p=P+t/32(P−t/3)​,q=P−t/32(P−2t/3)​,t=n+3n​, n>2997.​
(12)

Thus p,q∈(0,1), and the equations make both actions best responses for each player. By linearity of expected payoff, neither player can profit by any other mixed strategy. At n=2997 these formulas reach p=q=1 and are not fully mixed; the strict threshold matters.

The assignment is Pareto optimal. Write ai​ for the room selected by the ith student. Suppose another assignment a′ makes every student at least as well off. The first student already selected their uniquely most valuable room, so a1′​=a1​. Once the rooms of students 1,…,i−1 are fixed, the rooms available to student i in the original serial dictatorship are exactly the rooms that remain after those fixed assignments. Student i chose the unique maximum among them, so weak improvement requires ai′​=ai​.
Mathematical induction fixes every student's room. Thus a′=a and nobody can be strictly better off, contradicting the definition of a Pareto improvement. Strict room rankings and the absence of allocation externalities are the essential assumptions: a student's utility is determined solely by their own room.[ref]

No: arbitrary ties can produce an assignment that is not Pareto optimal. A two-student counterexample suffices. Give the first student value 1 for both rooms; give the second student value 1 for r1​ and 0 for r2​. If the first student breaks the tie by taking r1​, the second must take r2​, and the utility vector is (1,0).
Swapping the rooms gives utility vector (1,1), so it is a Pareto improvement. Both choices in the original run maximized the corresponding student's available room value. The strict-ranking argument from (a) fails because the first student's utility need not decrease when that student's room changes.

Solution

Words: 445
e OurBigBook.com
Every subgame perfect equilibrium outcome is Pareto optimal under the stated consecutive-friend and bonus conditions. Partition the choosing order into blocks: a single student without a friend, or two friends who choose consecutively. Their slots in the choosing order are adjacent; the proof must separately establish when their allocated rooms will be adjacent.
Let R be the rooms remaining before a friend block, with leader i and follower j. Because 0≤vi,r​,vj,r​≤1 and fi​,fj​>1, each friend strictly prefers every adjacent-to-friend allocation to every nonadjacent one. After leader i takes room r with an available adjacent room, follower j therefore takes their uniquely highest-valued room among the available neighbors of r. The leader consequently knows they will receive the adjacency bonus.
If R contains at least one pair of adjacent rooms, let E(R) be its nonisolated rooms: the rooms having an available neighbor. Leader i must choose from E(R), because choosing there guarantees utility greater than 1, whereas an isolated choice gives at most 1. Since the bonus is then fixed and room values are distinct, the leader chooses their unique maximum-value room in E(R). The follower chooses their unique best neighbor. Later students cannot change either allocated room, so later play cannot alter these two students' utilities.
If R contains no adjacent pair, neither friend can obtain the bonus under any allocation of rooms from R. Both simply choose their uniquely most valuable remaining room in turn. Removing rooms cannot create a new physical adjacency, so no hidden later move can change that conclusion. A one-student block likewise chooses their unique best available room.
Now compare the equilibrium assignment a with any proposed Pareto improvement a′. Induct over these blocks, assuming earlier blocks' rooms are already fixed. Thus a and a′ have the same remaining set R at the present block. A lone student's room must stay fixed by strict preferences.
For a friend block obtaining adjacency in a, both students must retain adjacency in a′: losing a bonus larger than 1 cannot be compensated by any room-value increase. The leader's room in a′ must therefore belong to E(R). It cannot differ from the unique highest-valued room the leader selected there without reducing that leader's utility. Once the leader's room is fixed, the follower likewise must retain the uniquely best available neighbor. If R has no adjacent pair, both rooms stay fixed by the ordinary strict-ranking argument.
Thus every block's rooms are unchanged. The only assignment weakly improving all students is a itself, so no strict improvement is possible. This proves Pareto efficiency of serial dictatorship with consecutive friends. The proof applies to every subgame perfect equilibrium, since the required choices at each block are strict best responses determined by the available rooms.

A simple cooperative game is a transferable utility game (N,v) with
v:2N→{0,1},v(∅)=0,v(N)=1,S⊆T⟹v(S)≤v(T).​
(17)
A coalition of value 1 is a winning coalition; a value-0 coalition loses. The displayed definition uses the usual monotone, nontrivial convention: the empty coalition loses and the grand coalition wins.

An induced subgraph game is a transferable utility game represented by a weighted undirected graph with one vertex per player; a coalition's value is the sum of the edge weights in its induced subgraph. With no self-loops,
v(S)={i,j}⊆S, i=j∑​wij​,​
(18)
where each unordered pair is counted once and an absent edge has weight zero. Weights may have either sign. In an induced subgraph game with self-loops, write the loop weight at i as ℓi​ and add ∑i∈S​ℓi​, counting each loop once. In particular, v({i})=ℓi​ when loops are allowed and is zero otherwise.[ref]

Yes; without self-loops, such a nontrivial simple cooperative game has exactly one positive edge, of weight 1. For each pair,
wij​=v({i,j})∈{0,1},
(21)
because singleton and loop contributions are zero. Summing all edge weights gives v(N)=1, so precisely one unordered pair {a,b} has weight 1, and all other pairs have weight zero.
Thus the winning coalitions are exactly those containing both a and b. For any 0≤λ≤1, set
xa​=λ,xb​=1−λ,xi​=0(i∈/{a,b}).​
(22)
This payoff vector is efficient, since ∑i​xi​=v(N)=1. Every winning coalition receives 1 and every losing coalition receives a nonnegative total, so all inequalities x(S)≥v(S) hold. Hence it belongs to the core of a cooperative game.
In fact these are all core allocations. Singleton conditions require xi​≥0, and the winning pair requires xa​+xb​≥1; efficiency then forces equality and zero payoffs outside the pair. The endpoints are both veto players, consistent with the general characterization of the core of a simple cooperative game.

A loop-free induced subgraph game is a convex cooperative game if and only if every edge weight is nonnegative. Necessity follows by applying the supermodular inequality to S={i} and T={j}. Since both singleton values and the empty value are zero,
v({i,j})+v(∅)≥v({i})+v({j})⟹wij​≥0.
(24)
Conversely, direct cancellation of common edge contributions gives, for any coalitions S,T,
v(S∪T)+v(S∩T)−v(S)−v(T)=∑i∈S∖Tj∈T∖S​​wij​.
(25)
Every summand is nonnegative, proving that v is a supermodular set function and hence convexity. Equivalently, a player's marginal contribution to S is Δi​(S)=∑j∈S​wij​, which cannot decrease as S grows. This establishes the convexity criterion for an induced subgraph game directly.[ref]

There is exactly one such nontrivial game for every n≥1: the unanimity game requiring every player. Put
uN​(S)={1,0,​S=N,S=N.​Number of games=1.​
(26)
It is a simple cooperative game, and permuting players preserves every coalition value. Thus it has the required symmetry of players in a cooperative game. It is also a convex cooperative game: if both S,T are winning, all four terms in the supermodular inequality have value 1; if exactly one is winning, their union is N and their intersection loses, giving equality 1=1; if neither wins, the right-hand side is zero and the inequality follows from nonnegativity. For n=1 the same definition gives the sole player value 1.
To prove uniqueness, first establish that convex simple games are unanimity games. If S,T are winning coalitions, the supermodular inequality gives
2=v(S)+v(T)≤v(S∪T)+v(S∩T)≤2,
(27)
so their intersection is also winning. The family of winning coalitions is finite and nonempty. Its intersection K is therefore winning and nonempty, since the empty coalition loses. Every winning coalition contains K, and monotonicity makes every superset of K winning. Hence v=uK​.
If K were a proper nonempty subset of N, take i∈K and j∈/K, and let T=N∖{i,j}. Then T∪{i}=N∖{j} contains K and wins, whereas T∪{j}=N∖{i} loses. This violates symmetry of players in a cooperative game, which requires those two coalition values to agree. Thus K=N, proving the claimed count. The “nontrivial” convention excludes the identically zero game; if that degenerate game were also called simple, it would supply one additional symmetric convex example.
