There is exactly one such nontrivial game for every n≥1: the unanimity game requiring every player. Put
uN​(S)={1,0,​S=N,S=N.​Number of games=1.​
(26)
It is a simple cooperative game, and permuting players preserves every coalition value. Thus it has the required symmetry of players in a cooperative game. It is also a convex cooperative game: if both S,T are winning, all four terms in the supermodular inequality have value 1; if exactly one is winning, their union is N and their intersection loses, giving equality 1=1; if neither wins, the right-hand side is zero and the inequality follows from nonnegativity. For n=1 the same definition gives the sole player value 1.
To prove uniqueness, first establish that convex simple games are unanimity games. If S,T are winning coalitions, the supermodular inequality gives
2=v(S)+v(T)≤v(S∪T)+v(S∩T)≤2,
(27)
so their intersection is also winning. The family of winning coalitions is finite and nonempty. Its intersection K is therefore winning and nonempty, since the empty coalition loses. Every winning coalition contains K, and monotonicity makes every superset of K winning. Hence v=uK​.
If K were a proper nonempty subset of N, take i∈K and j∈/K, and let T=N∖{i,j}. Then T∪{i}=N∖{j} contains K and wins, whereas T∪{j}=N∖{i} loses. This violates symmetry of players in a cooperative game, which requires those two coalition values to agree. Thus K=N, proving the claimed count. The “nontrivial” convention excludes the identically zero game; if that degenerate game were also called simple, it would supply one additional symmetric convex example.
