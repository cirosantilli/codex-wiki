The difficulty is representing dictionary membership and the one-letter relationship in positive conjunctive STRIPS preconditions; a finite dictionary nevertheless admits an exact encoding. Pure STRIPS does not provide string indexing, a “differs in one position” operator, arbitrary disjunctive tests or a procedure that calls a dictionary lookup. Those relationships must be represented by ground facts or compiled into ground actions.
One exact whole-word encoding uses a planning fluent At(w) and a static predicate Next(w,w′) containing exactly the permitted dictionary mutations. For the action schema Change(w,w′), use
PreAddDel​={At(w),Next(w,w′)},={At(w′)},={At(w)}.​
(7)
Initialize At(s) and the static transition facts, and use goal At(t). Only one At fact is true initially. Each applicable ground action deletes it and adds exactly its permitted successor, so mathematical induction proves that exactly one current-word fact remains true and that every plan is a legal word ladder. Conversely, every legal word ladder directly supplies a plan. An isolated non-dictionary start is handled as in (c). A shortest plan has the fewest mutations.
This encoding moves the hard lexical relationship into preprocessing. For N dictionary words, checking all ordered pairs takes O(N2L) character comparisons, although indexing can do better; the number of stored transitions is at most NL(∣Σ∣−1) when all states are dictionary words. The STRIPS representation is finite, but its size and preprocessing cost matter.
A position-based representation instead uses planning fluents Letter(i,ℓ). Changing position i must delete its old letter and add its new one; all other positions persist through the STRIPS update rule. Merely requiring each position to contain a letter admits non-dictionary strings. A disjunction over all dictionary words is not a positive conjunctive STRIPS precondition. It can be compiled into separate ground actions, each requiring every letter of a valid source word and changing one position to reach a valid destination. This again enumerates lexical cases and can be large. Arbitrary letter changes followed by an unconstrained “validate” action are unsound: several illegal intermediate strings could otherwise lead to a legal goal. Legality must hold after every mutation, not only at the endpoint.
