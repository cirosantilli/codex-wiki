Two continuous maps f0​,f1​:X→Y are homotopic if there is a continuous map
H:X×I⟶Y,H(x,0)=f0​(x),H(x,1)=f1​(x).
(1)
Here the Cartesian product has the product topology. Such an H is a homotopy, written f0​≃f1​.
The topological spaces X,Y are homotopy equivalent if there are continuous maps f:X→Y and g:Y→X such that
gf≃idX​,fg≃idY​.​
(2)
Each is then a homotopy inverse of the other.
For A⊆X with its subspace topology with inclusion map i:A↪X, a homotopy retract, in the course's convention, is a continuous map r:X→A with
ri=idA​,ir≃idX​.​
(3)
Thus r is an actual retraction, and i,r give a homotopy equivalence. Requiring the latter homotopy to fix A pointwise at all times gives the stronger condition of a deformation retraction as used in this wiki. The course's definition of homotopy retract requires no such restriction on that homotopy.

Identify the bottom j(X)=q(X×{0}) with X; its natural map j is a topological embedding. To verify this directly, an open subset O⊆X gives the saturated open subset O×[0,1/2) of the space before taking the quotient topology; its image is open in M(f) and meets the bottom in j(O).
Suppose first that there is a retraction R:M(f)→j(X). Define the continuous map
g=j−1Ri:Y⟶X.
(16)
Then
L(x,t)=j−1R(q(x,t))
(17)
is a homotopy with L(x,0)=x and L(x,1)=g(f(x)). Hence gf≃idX​.
Conversely, suppose a continuous map g:Y→X has gf≃idX​. Reverse the parameter if necessary to choose a homotopy L with L(x,0)=x and L(x,1)=gf(x). Set
R(q(x,t))=j(L(x,t)),R(i(y))=j(g(y)).
(18)
At the glued end, j(L(x,1))=j(gf(x)), exactly the value prescribed at i(f(x)). The universal property of the quotient topology therefore gives a continuous map R:M(f)→j(X). At the bottom, R(j(x))=j(x), proving that R is a retraction. We have established the mapping-cylinder bottom retraction criterion:
j(X) is a retract of M(f)⟺∃g:Y→X continuous with gf≃idX​.​

A group presentation of G consists of a set S of symbols and a subset R⊆F(S) of relators, together with a group isomorphism
G≅⟨S∣R⟩:=F(S)/⟨⟨R⟩⟩.​
(20)
Here F(S) is the free group on S, and ⟨⟨R⟩⟩ is the normal closure of R. The images of S form a generating set of a group; the relators prescribe which words become the identity element.
For the canonical presentation of a group, use one symbol sg​ for every group element g∈G. The universal property of a free group gives a surjective group homomorphism
ε:F({sg​:g∈G})⟶G,sg​⟼g.
(21)
Let R(G)=kerε, namely all words whose evaluation in G is the identity element. Then
G≅⟨sg​ (g∈G)∣R(G)⟩.​
(22)
Indeed R(G) is already a normal subgroup, so its normal closure is itself, and the first isomorphism theorem identifies the resulting quotient group with G. This is the course's canonical presentation of a group.
An equivalent, smaller collection of relators is sg​sh​sgh−1​ for all g,h∈G. These encode the multiplication table. They imply s1​=1 and sg−1​=sg−1​, so every word reduces to the single symbol recording its product. This proves directly that they normally generate R(G), although the canonical collection above consists of every word in that group homomorphism kernel.

Take the free product F=G1​∗G2​, with its natural group homomorphisms kj​:Gj​→F, and form the normal closure
N=⟨⟨k1​(ϕ1​(h))k2​(ϕ2​(h))−1:h∈G0​⟩⟩.
(23)
The pushout of groups is the quotient group
P=(G1​∗G2​)/N,​
(24)
equipped with the group homomorphisms j1​,j2​ obtained by composing k1​,k2​ with the quotient map of groups. The defining relators ensure
j1​ϕ1​=j2​ϕ2​.
(25)
Thus the two prescribed images of every group element of G0​ agree in P. This construction works for arbitrary group homomorphisms ϕ1​,ϕ2​; no injectivity is assumed, and j1​,j2​ need not be injective. Its universal property is proved in the next part.

The universal property of a pushout of groups says: for every group H and group homomorphisms uj​:Gj​→H satisfying u1​ϕ1​=u2​ϕ2​, there is exactly one group homomorphism u:P→H such that
uj1​=u1​,uj2​=u2​.​
(26)
To prove existence, the universal property of a free product supplies a unique group homomorphism v:G1​∗G2​→H with vkj​=uj​. Every defining relator of N maps to
u1​(ϕ1​(h))u2​(ϕ2​(h))−1=1.
(27)
Since kerv is a normal subgroup, it contains the entire normal closure N. The universal property of a quotient group therefore gives a group homomorphism u:P→H through which v factors. Its restrictions are the required uj​.
For uniqueness, j1​(G1​) and j2​(G2​) together generate P: their preimages already generate the free product. Consequently a group homomorphism out of P is determined by its restrictions to these two subgroups. Any two candidates for u therefore coincide.
This also proves that the pushout of groups is unique up to a unique compatible group isomorphism. Given another such object P′, apply the universal property in both directions. Both composites restrict to the original maps from G1​,G2​, so uniqueness makes them the respective identity maps.

Put A=T2∖B2 and let E=∂A=S1 denote the attaching circle. The version of the Seifert-van Kampen theorem used here states that if X=U∪V is an open cover with U,V,U∩V path-connected and a common base point in U∩V, then
π1​(X)≅π1​(U)∗π1​(U∩V)​π1​(V).
(28)
The expression on the right means the pushout of groups of the two inclusion-induced group homomorphisms; they need not be injective.
Choose a thin collar neighbourhood CA​ of E inside A, and a thin equatorial band CS​ inside S2. For example, before the square's side identifications take CA​={v:1≤∣v∣<1+ϵ} with 0<ϵ<1/2, and take CS​={(x,y,z)∈S2:∣z∣<ϵ}. In the glued topological space set
U=A∪CS​,V=S2∪CA​.
(29)
These are open subsets: their preimages in the two pieces are open and respect the gluing. They cover X, and the three deformation retractions
U≃A,V≃S2,U∩V=CA​∪E​CS​≃E
(30)
fix E. Thus all three spaces are path-connected, and a base point b∈E is preserved.
For completeness, the fundamental group of a once-punctured torus is F(a,b), and its boundary loop of a once-punctured torus represents [a,b]=aba−1b−1, up to orientation and a change of base point. Here is the geometry behind both facts. In the square model, move each point of A radially outwards to the square boundary:
v⟼((1−t)+max(∣v1​∣,∣v2​∣)2t​)v,0≤t≤1.
(31)
The denominator never vanishes on A. The homotopy stays outside the missing open disk, stays inside the square, and fixes the square boundary, so it descends through the side identifications. The resulting retract is a wedge of two circles. Its fundamental group is the free group on the two oriented side based loops a,b. The inner boundary loop travels around the square once under this deformation retraction; its successive sides read a,b,a−1,b−1.
The sphere S2 is simply connected. Hence the group homomorphism from π1​(E)≅Z to π1​(V) is trivial, while its map to π1​(U) sends a group generator to [a,b]. Applying the Seifert-van Kampen theorem gives
π1​(X,b)≅F(a,b)/⟨⟨[a,b]⟩⟩=⟨a,b∣aba−1b−1=1⟩≅Z2.​
(32)
The final group isomorphism follows because the single relator makes a,b commute, leaving the free abelian group on two group generators. The two hemispheres kill the same boundary loop; they do not create another group generator.

Let n=(0,0,1) and use the open cover
U=Y∖{n},V={(x,y,z)∈S2:z>1/2}.
(33)
The cap V is open in Y: it equals Y∩{z>1/2} because the attached chord has z=0. The cap is homeomorphic to an open disk, and U∩V=V∖{n} is a punctured open disk, hence homotopy equivalent to a circle. Both sets and their intersection are path-connected. Choose the base point b in that intersection.
We first calculate π1​(U,b). A stereographic projection identifies S2∖{n} with R2 and sends the two chord endpoints to (−1,0),(1,0). Thus U becomes the Euclidean plane with an extra closed interval attached at those two points. This description is a homeomorphism: gluing the two closed pieces along their endpoints gives exactly the original subspace topology.
The Euclidean plane has a deformation retraction onto L=[−1,1]×{0} while fixing its endpoints. An explicit homotopy is
D((u,v),t)=((1−t)u+tc(u),(1−t)v),c(u)=max(−1,min(1,u)).
(34)
Extend it by the identity map on the extra chord. The pasting lemma for closed subspaces gives a deformation retraction of U onto two distinct closed intervals sharing just their endpoints, a circle. Consequently π1​(U,b)≅Z, with the routine base point transport if the retraction moves b.
The inclusion-induced group homomorphism π1​(U∩V,b)→π1​(U,b) is trivial: it factors through S2∖{n}≅R2, which is contractible. Also π1​(V,b)=1. All hypotheses of the Seifert-van Kampen theorem are now checked, and it gives the fundamental group of a sphere with an attached chord:
π1​(Y,b)≅Z∗Z​1≅Z,​
(35)
where both maps from the amalgamating infinite cyclic group are trivial. A group generator follows the chord from one endpoint to the other and returns along any path on the sphere, with a connecting path from the chosen base point.

A covering map is a continuous surjection p:X→X such that every x∈X has an open neighbourhood U for which
p−1(U)=α∈A⨆​Uα​,p∣Uα​​:Uα​⟶U a homeomorphism,​
(36)
where the Uα​ are pairwise disjoint open subsets of X. Such a U is an evenly covered neighbourhood, and the sets Uα​ are its covering sheets. The index set may be infinite. The requirement that every sheet map onto all of U is part of the definition.

ii OurBigBook.com
For a covering map over a nonempty connected space, the degree of a covering map is
degp=∣p−1(x)∣,​
(37)
the cardinality of any fibre of a function. To show independence of x, choose an evenly covered neighbourhood U. Each covering sheet contains exactly one point over every y∈U, so ∣p−1(y)∣ equals the number of covering sheets over U. The fibre of a function cardinality is therefore locally constant. For a fixed cardinal number κ, the points with that cardinality form an open subset, and their complement is also open. Connectedness forces the value to be constant everywhere.
For a path-connected base there is also an explicit fibre bijection by path lifting: lift a path from x to y starting at each point over x and take its endpoint. The path lifting theorem gives existence and uniqueness; path reversal gives the inverse bijection. The bijection may depend on the path, but the resulting equality of cardinalities does not.
A connected base is needed for one global degree. Without it, use a degree on each connected component on which the cardinality is constant. For example, a covering map of a discrete two-point topological space can have one point over the first point and two over the second. Thus the unqualified printed definition needs the usual connected-base convention, or the explicit assumption that every fibre of a function has the same cardinality.

With local path connectedness, the intended conclusion holds. Choose a base point x0​ and the standard covering map
p:R⟶S1,p(t)=e2πit.
(38)
The fundamental group of the circle is Z. Since the image of a finite group under a group homomorphism is finite and Z has no nontrivial finite subgroups,
f∗​:π1​(X,x0​)⟶π1​(S1,f(x0​))≅Z
(39)
is trivial. If X is also locally path-connected, the lifting criterion for a covering space supplies a continuous map f​:X→R with pf​=f, because p∗​π1​(R)=0. The homotopy
H(x,s)=p((1−s)f​(x)+sf​(x0​))​
(40)
runs from f to the constant function f(x0​). This proves the claimed null-homotopy under that additional hypothesis, in particular for CW complexes and the topological graphs considered later in the paper.
For arbitrary path-connected spaces, the printed assertion is false. Here is an explicit Warsaw circle counterexample, including the obstruction that the usual lifting argument misses. In the Euclidean plane take
G={(x,sin(π/x)):0<x≤1},L={0}×[−1,1].
(41)
Join P=(1,0) to Q=(0,−1) by the polygonal arc A passing successively through (2,0),(2,−2),(0,−2), and put W=G∪L∪A with its subspace topology. The arc meets G∪L only at P,Q. The topologist's sine curve G∪L has two path components, but the added arc joins them, making W path-connected.
Every loop in W is contained in
Tδ​=L∪A∪{(x,sin(π/x)):δ≤x≤1}
(42)
for some δ>0. To justify the assertion, a path cannot reach L along the oscillating tail: the intermediate value theorem for its x-coordinate would force its y-coordinate to attain both 1 and −1 arbitrarily close to the supposed arrival time, contradicting continuity. The only alternative route from the tail to L uses P and the joining arc. If a loop visited tail points with x→0, a convergent subsequence of their parameter values would approach a time at which the loop lies in L. On a sufficiently small parameter interval around that time its image avoids P. That would give a forbidden path from L into the tail without passing through P. Thus those tail coordinates have a positive lower bound. The space Tδ​ is a finite tree, in fact a single arc, so every such loop contracts. Consequently
π1​(W)=1.
(43)
Now define a continuous map h:W→S1 to be 1 on G∪L, and to make one full turn on A: for a parametrization a:[0,1]→A from P to Q, set h(a(t))=e2πit. The pieces G∪L and A are closed in W, and the formulas agree at P,Q, so the pasting lemma for closed subspaces proves continuity.
There is no continuous lift h:W→R. Any such lift is integer-valued on G∪L, so it is constant on each of the connected spaces G,L. Since G accumulates on L, continuity forces those two constants to be the same. But along A a lift of e2πit has the form t+k, giving values at Q and P differing by 1, a contradiction. Finally, if h had a null-homotopy, the homotopy lifting theorem for a covering map, starting with a lift of the final constant function and reversing time, would produce a continuous lift of h. This theorem needs no local path connectedness of the parameter space. Hence h is not null-homotopic, although W has finite, indeed trivial, fundamental group.
The native PDF explicitly assumes only path connectedness here. The missing local path connectedness cannot be inferred from the finiteness of the fundamental group.

A finite tree is a nonempty connected graph with no graph cycles. We show by mathematical induction on its number v of vertices that it has v−1 edges. For v=1 there are no edges, so the assertion holds.
If v>1, take a path in a graph of maximal length among those without repeated vertices. Each endpoint is a leaf of a tree: an additional incident edge would either extend the path in a graph or create a graph cycle. Delete one such leaf of a tree and its unique incident edge. The remaining graph stays connected, since a path in a graph between remaining vertices never needs that terminal edge, and it has no graph cycles. It is therefore a tree with v−1 vertices and, by mathematical induction, v−2 edges. Restoring the deleted edge gives e=v−1.
Thus its Euler characteristic is
χ(X)=v−e=v−(v−1)=1.​
(44)

Choose a spanning tree T of the finite connected graph X. It contains every vertex of X and has v(X)−1 edges by part (i). Collapsing T to a point is a homotopy equivalence: T is a contractible subcomplex, and collapsing a contractible cofibration in a CW complex preserves homotopy type.
Every edge outside T then becomes a circle attached to the common point. Hence the quotient space X/T is a bouquet of circles with
r=e(X)−e(T)=e(X)−v(X)+1
(45)
petals. The fundamental group of a bouquet of circles is the free group with one member of a free basis of a group for each petal. Therefore the rank of the fundamental group of a finite connected graph is
π1​(X)≅F1−χ(X)​,r=1−χ(X).​
(46)
More concretely, choose a vertex as base point; follow the unique path in a graph in T to one end of a non-tree edge, traverse it, and return through T. These based loops give the free basis of a group. Thus r is the number of free group generators, equivalently the minimum size of a generating set of a group, rather than the size of every possible redundant generating set of a group. Minimality also follows by abelianization, since Fr​ has abelianization Zr.

Assume p has a common finite degree of a covering map d. Declare the lifted vertices to be p−1(V(X)), so there are exactly dv(X) of them. Each open edge is homeomorphic to (0,1), a simply connected interval. The restriction of the covering map above it is a disjoint union of d copies of that open edge.
These copies acquire endpoints as follows. Lift the parametrizing path [0,1]→X of each oriented edge, starting at each of the d points over its initial vertex. The path lifting theorem gives a unique lift all the way to 1. Its endpoint lies over the terminal vertex, and the interiors of these lifted paths are exactly the d open edges already described. This also handles a graph loop, whose two endpoints may coincide downstairs or upstairs. Local covering sheets above a small star at a vertex show that the lifted incidence relations have precisely the original topology. Thus the graph structure lifted through a covering map is a finite topological graph with
v(X)=dv(X),e(X)=de(X).
(47)
Subtracting gives the Euler characteristic under a finite covering formula
χ(X)=dχ(X).​
(48)
The formula holds even when X is disconnected. If a disconnected base is allowed to have degrees dC​ that vary with its connected components C, the precise replacement is
χ(X)=∑C​dC​χ(C).
(49)
The printed formula with a single d uses the constant-degree convention explained in part (a)(ii).

Use a topological graph consisting of the ray [0,∞) with vertices vn​=n and one oriented graph loop ℓn​ attached at each vn​, for n=0,1,2,…. Set the base point to b=v0​. This is a connected, locally finite graph; it is deliberately infinite.
Define continuous maps f,g:X→X by
f(t)g(t)​=t+1=max(t−1,0)​​on the ray,on the ray,​f(ℓn​(s))g(ℓn​(s))​=ℓn+1​(s),={b,ℓn−1​(s),​n=0,n≥1.​​
(50)
Here each graph loop is parametrized by s∈[0,1] with its endpoints at the attaching vertex. The formulas agree at every vertex, and their restrictions to every closed edge are continuous. The weak topology of a CW complex therefore makes f,g continuous maps. Directly on the ray and on each graph loop,
gf=idX​.​
(51)
We check the two fundamental group maps at exactly the specified base points. The ray is a spanning tree. At b=v0​ a free basis of a group consists of an​, obtained by going along the ray to vn​, following ℓn​, and returning. At f(b)=v1​, use the corresponding free basis of a group cn​ with connecting paths starting at v1​; in particular c0​ travels backwards along the first edge before following ℓ0​. The standard spanning tree description applies to this infinite topological graph as well: every loop and every homotopy between based loops has compact image, so lies in a finite subcomplex.
The induced group homomorphisms are
f∗​(an​)=cn+1​,g∗​(c0​)=1,g∗​(cn​)=an−1​ (n≥1).​
(52)
Thus f∗​ misses c0​: words in c1​,c2​,… cannot equal c0​ by the normal form for free groups and free product of groups. Consequently f∗​ is not surjective. Meanwhile c0​ is a nontrivial free group element killed by g∗​, so g∗​ is not injective. Neither induced map is a group isomorphism, while functoriality of the fundamental group correctly gives g∗​f∗​=id.
