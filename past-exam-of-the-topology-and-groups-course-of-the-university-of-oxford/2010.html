A homotopy from f0​:Y→X to f1​:Y→X is a continuous map H:Y×[0,1]→X with H(y,0)=f0​(y) and H(y,1)=f1​(y) for every y. A covering map p:X→X is a surjective continuous map for which every x∈X has an open neighborhood U whose inverse image is a disjoint union of open sets, each mapped homeomorphically onto U. A lift of f:Y→X is a continuous map f​:Y→X satisfying p∘f​=f.
The path lifting theorem says that a path γ:[0,1]→X and a point x0​∈p−1(γ(0)) determine a unique path γ​:[0,1]→X with pγ​=γ and γ​(0)=x0​.
On S1∖{1} there is a continuous branch Arg(0,2π)​ of the complex argument, taking values in (0,2π). If f avoids 1, set θ(z)=Arg(0,2π)​f(z). Then the explicit homotopy
H(z,s)=exp(i((1−s)θ(z)+sπ))​
(1)
starts at f and ends at the constant map −1. It is continuous because the chosen argument branch is continuous on the whole image. More generally, a map omitting any w∈S1 is null-homotopic: apply the formula to wf and multiply the resulting homotopy by w.
For the based injective map, use the standard covering map E(t)=e2πit. Lift the path t↦g(E(t)) on [0,1] to a real-valued path G with G(0)=0. Since g(1)=1, its endpoint d=G(1) is an integer. On [0,1), G is injective: equality G(s)=G(t) implies g(E(s))=g(E(t)), then E(s)=E(t) by injectivity, and hence s=t in this half-open interval. A continuous injective real-valued function on an interval is strictly monotone.
If G is increasing, d>0. It cannot be at least 2, because the intermediate value theorem would produce t∈(0,1) with G(t)=1, giving g(E(t))=g(1) while E(t)=1. Thus d=1. If G is decreasing, the same reasoning gives d=−1. Now define
K(E(t),s)=exp(2πi((1−s)G(t)+sdt)).​
(2)
At t=0 and t=1 this has the same value 1 for every s, since d is an integer. Therefore the formula descends continuously from [0,1]×[0,1] to S1×[0,1]. At s=0 it is g; at s=1 it is z↦zd. Hence g is homotopic to the identity or to complex conjugation, with degree 1 or −1. This is the key property of an injective self-map of a circle.
Finally, let h be any continuous injective circle self-map. Normalize it to g(z)=h(1)​h(z), which is injective and fixes 1. If h were not surjective, it would omit some circle point, so the explicit missing-point construction would make h null-homotopic. Multiplying this homotopy by h(1)​ would make g null-homotopic too. But g is homotopic to the identity or conjugation, neither of which is null-homotopic by the permitted fact. This contradiction proves every continuous injective circle self-map is surjective. The lifting argument did not assume surjectivity, so the reasoning is not circular.

The normal closure ⟨⟨B⟩⟩G​ is the smallest normal subgroup of G containing B, equivalently the intersection of all normal subgroups containing B. Any such normal subgroup contains every gb±1g−1 and hence every finite product of these elements. Thus N⊆⟨⟨B⟩⟩G​.
Include the empty product so that 1∈N, in particular when B=∅. Products of elements of N are obtained by concatenating their factor lists. Inversion reverses the list and replaces ϵi​ by −ϵi​, so N is closed under inverses. For any k∈G,
k(∏i​gi​biϵi​​gi−1​)k−1=∏i​(kgi​)biϵi​​(kgi​)−1∈N.
(3)
Therefore N is a normal subgroup and it contains B. Minimality of the normal closure gives the reverse inclusion, proving
N=⟨⟨B⟩⟩G​.​
(4)
The notation n∈N in a finite-product description is understood to allow the empty product; otherwise the empty generating subset would require a separate identity convention.
In a group presentation, X is a set of formal generators and R is a subset of the free group F(X), consisting of relators which are required to become the identity. The presented group is
⟨X∣R⟩=F(X)/⟨⟨R⟩⟩F(X)​.​
(5)
The universal property of a group presentation says that a function X→H extends to a group homomorphism from the presented group precisely when every relator evaluates to 1 in H: first extend freely to F(X), then factor through the quotient because its kernel contains the normal closure of R.
A rotation α by 2π/n and a reflection β of a regular n-gon satisfy
αn=1,β2=1,βαβ=α−1.
(6)
The last identity holds because reflection reverses the sense of rotation, and it implies αβαβ=1. Hence all defining relators vanish, giving a homomorphism θ:D2n​→Mn​ with θ(σ)=α and θ(τ)=β.
To prove it is an isomorphism without presupposing the order of the presented group, use στ=τσ−1. Move every occurrence of τ to the left, cancel pairs by τ2=1, and reduce exponents modulo n. Every element has one of the forms
σjorτσj,0≤j<n.
(7)
Thus ∣D2n​∣≤2n. For the usual regular polygon with n≥3, the n rotations αj are distinct and the n reflections βαj are distinct; no rotation is a reflection, since their planar orientation behavior differs. The supplied description says these exhaust Mn​, so ∣Mn​∣=2n, and every one lies in the image of θ. Surjectivity and the order bound force ∣D2n​∣=2n and trivial kernel. Thus θ is an isomorphism.
For the requested Cayley graph, use right multiplication by the generators. The σ edges are the two directed 3-cycles
1→σ→σ2→1,τ→τσ→τσ2→τ.
(8)
The τ edges are the undirected pairs
1↔τ,σ↔τσ2,σ2↔τσ.
(9)
These pairs use σjτ=τσ−j. Thus the underlying graph is a triangular prism, with the two rotation cycles oriented oppositely when reflection pairs are drawn as radial edges.

A deck transformation of a covering p:X→X is a homeomorphism d:X→X with p∘d=p. A connected covering is a regular covering map if its deck transformation group acts transitively on each fiber; for a path-connected base, transitivity on the fiber over the chosen basepoint is equivalent.
Write H=p∗​π1​(X,b) and G=π1​(X,b). A based loop represents an element of H exactly when its lift starting at b is closed. Indeed a closed lift itself projects to a representative of an image element; conversely the image of a closed path lifts to that path, and the closed-endpoint property is unchanged by based homotopy.
Suppose the covering is regular, let h∈H, and let g∈G. Lift a representative of g from b to an endpoint v. By regularity some deck transformation sends b to v. Applying it to the closed lift of h gives a closed lift of h at v, by uniqueness in the path lifting theorem. The concatenation of the lift of g, that closed lift of h, and the reverse lift of g is therefore a closed lift of ghg−1. Thus gHg−1⊆H; applying the same argument to g−1 gives equality. Hence a regular covering has a normal image subgroup.

Choose the spanning tree consisting of the first five x edges from B to U. The tree paths to B,V,L,T,C,U have labels 1,x,x2,x3,x4,x5. For every directed non-tree edge vz​w, the path from B along the tree to v, then along the edge, then back along the tree from w, has label tv​ztw−1​. For a connected covering of a connected, locally path-connected, semilocally simply connected space, the classification of connected covering spaces says that the image subgroup is normal if and only if the covering is regular. The two-circle wedge and this connected finite graph satisfy those hypotheses. Since regularity fails, H is not normal in the base group π1​(X,b)≅F(x,y).
