The spin operators here are dimensionless: Sj=σj​/2, so Ω has units of inverse time and Φ is a dimensionless pulse area. Integrating the Schrödinger evolution across an infinitesimally short pulse gives UΦ​=e−iΦSx. The finite free Hamiltonian contributes a vanishing integrated phase within that pulse. Between pulses the free evolution is UΩ​=e−iΩTSz.
In the Sz eigenbasis, Sz=diag(1,−1)/2 and (σx​)2=I. Consequently
UΩ​=diag(e−iΩT/2,eiΩT/2),UΦ​=Icos(Φ/2)−iσx​sin(Φ/2).
(1)
Starting just before a pulse, the kick acts first and the free interval second. Operators act on states right to left, hence
U(NT−0+,−0+)=(UΩ​UΦ​)N.​
(2)
There are exactly N kicks, at times 0,T,…,(N−1)T; the pulse at NT is excluded. Sampling just after kicks would change the displayed product order. This endpoint convention specifies the delta-kicked spin Floquet operator.

Multiply each time-sliced path weight by exp[−∑j​V(xj​)Δt]. A nonnegative finite V is a local killing rate: the Feynman-Kac weight attenuates paths in proportion to the time spent in regions where V>0. Let that rate tend to infinity outside the allowed interval. Paths that exit are removed, leaving Brownian motion killed at its first boundary hit.
Consequently the required kernel is the Gaussian path measure restricted to surviving paths, equivalently
K∣x∣<L​=∫xi​xf​​Dxe−∫[x˙2/(4D)+V(x)]dt,​
(16)
with the infinite-barrier interpretation stated above. It solves ∂t​K=D∂x2​K in the interior with absorbing Dirichlet boundary conditions at ±L. A reflecting boundary condition for a diffusion would describe a different process.
Strictly, this is a transition probability density in the final position, jointly with survival; a probability for an interval of final positions includes an integral over xf​. Its integral over the whole allowed interval is at most one. It is not the normalized endpoint density conditional on survival, which would require division by the survival probability. In these diffusion units V and the eigenvalues of H have units of inverse time.

Outside a finite-range scattering potential, the stationary Schrödinger equation becomes the free Helmholtz equation. The specified incident state is a plane wave along +z. The scattered disturbance must be outgoing: the scattering experiment has an incoming incident beam but no independently imposed spherical wave arriving from infinity. At large radius its angular pattern is encoded by a scattering amplitude fk​(θ,ϕ):
ψk​(r)∼eikz+fk​(θ,ϕ)reikr​.​
(22)
An outgoing three-dimensional wave has amplitude proportional to 1/r because its probability current spreads over area proportional to r2. Indeed its radial current is (ℏk/m)∣fk​∣2/r2 to leading order, while the incident current density is ℏk/m. Therefore dσ/dΩ=∣fk​∣2. The sign e+ikr assumes the stationary time factor e−iEk​t/ℏ.

For example, a future timelike vector gives a positive-definite X, which remains positive definite under invertible congruence; its time direction is preserved. The matrices S and −S induce the same four-vector transformation, illustrating the Lorentz spinor double cover.
The source uses x+iy in the upper-right entry, corresponding to X=ctI+xσx​−yσy​+zσz​ for the standard Pauli matrices. This is a permissible basis convention, but its minus sign in the y basis must also be retained in the spinor identification.

For nonzero χ, the outer product X=χχ† is rank-one Hermitian positive semidefinite, so detX=0 and ct=χ†χ/2>0. It therefore represents a future-directed null vector. Conversely any nonzero future-null Hermitian X has eigenvalues 2ct and zero, so its positive eigenvector multiplied by 2ct​ supplies χ. The phase χ↦eiζχ leaves X unchanged. A past-directed null vector needs X=−χχ†, rather than the positive outer product alone.
For the printed normalized spinor, direct multiplication yields
X=21​(1+cosθsinθeiϕ​sinθe−iϕ1−cosθ​).
(61)
Comparing with the printed x+iy convention gives
(ct,x,y,z)=21​(1, sinθcosϕ, −sinθsinϕ, cosθ).​
(62)
Its usual spatial azimuth is −ϕ, not ϕ. The source's asserted usual-angle identification has a sign inconsistency. Either conjugating the two spinor phases or replacing the upper-right matrix entry by x−iy restores the usual azimuth ϕ. Neither alteration is made silently here. A further positive scale factor sets the magnitude of an arbitrary future-null vector; the displayed normalized spinor fixes ct=1/2 in the chosen coordinate units.

R=eκcos2(θ/2)+e−κsin2(θ/2)=coshκ+sinhκcosθ.
(63)
Dividing by R​ isolates the new direction without discarding the physical scale factor in X′. The lower-to-upper component ratio gives
tan2θ′​=e−κtan2θ​,ϕ′=ϕ(mod2π).​
(64)
Equivalently,
cosθ′=1+tanhκcosθcosθ+tanhκ​,sinθ′=Rsinθ​.​
(65)
This is relativistic aberration for the active positive-z boost specified in the question. At the poles, azimuth is undefined rather than a measurable transformed variable. Under the literal matrix convention from (c), the actual azimuth −ϕ is likewise unchanged. The time coordinate is multiplied by R; normalizing a spinor for its direction must not erase that boost-induced change of null-vector scale.
