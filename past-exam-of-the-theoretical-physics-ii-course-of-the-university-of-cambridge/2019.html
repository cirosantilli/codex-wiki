Use the ordered orthonormal basis (∣1s⟩,∣2px​⟩). The projected Hamiltonian between pulses is H0​=−Δσz​, so its free time-evolution operator through a period is
UΔ​=eiασz​=(eiα0​0e−iα​),α=ℏΔτ​.
(4)
Across one instantaneous pulse, integrating the Schrödinger equation gives
UE​=e−iβσx​=(cosβ−isinβ​−isinβcosβ​),β=ℏEx0​τ​,
(5)
using the exponential of a Pauli vector. The finite free Hamiltonian contributes vanishing evolution during the zero-duration kick. A period measured immediately before successive pulses therefore consists of the kick followed by free evolution. Composition gives
U(Nτ−ϵ,−ϵ)=(UΔ​UE​)N,ϵ→0+.​
(6)
This is the Floquet operator at the specified phase of the pulse train. Actual hydrogenic energy levels are not opposite numbers: the displayed convention subtracts their midpoint, with Δ=(E2p​−E1s​)/2. Restoring that midpoint multiplies the result by an unobservable global phase. Likewise E in the given Hamiltonian denotes the electric coupling per unit length; if it is instead a physical electric-field strength, its charge factor must be included in Ex0​.

UΔ​UE​=CI−iv⋅σ,C=cosαcosβ,v=(cosαsinβ,−sinαsinβ,−sinαcosβ).

Because C2+∣v∣2=1, a suitable angle and unit vector for the rotation are
θ=2arccos(cosαcosβ),n=1−cos2αcos2β​(cosαsinβ,−sinαsinβ,−sinαcosβ)​.​

In the two-orbital subspace the position operator is X=x0​σx​. The Heisenberg equation of motion between pulses gives P/m=(i/ℏ)[H0​,X]=(2Δx0​/ℏ)σy​, hence
⟨X⟩=x0​rx​,⟨P⟩=ℏ2mΔx0​​ry​.
(9)
Thus two components of the Bloch vector directly describe the mean position and momentum; its third gives the orbital population imbalance. The electric kick rotates about the position axis, leaving ⟨X⟩ unchanged at the kick, while free evolution mixes the position and momentum components. Starting in ∣1s⟩, one complete period gives
r=(−sin2αsin2β,−cos2αsin2β,cos2β),
(10)
which exhibits the induced motion explicitly. These are projected two-orbital observables.

Assume a real, central, short-range potential and elastic quantum scattering. At large radius, normalize the stationary wavefunction as
ψ(r)∼eikz+f(θ)reikr​,
(11)
where k=2mE​/ℏ and θ is the scattering angle. Equal incoming and outgoing speeds give the differential scattering cross-section dσ/dΩ=∣f(θ)∣2.
The partial-wave scattering expansion separates the spherical harmonics. The incident plane wave has expansion ∑ℓ≥0​iℓ(2ℓ+1)jℓ​(kr)Pℓ​(cosθ), with Spherical Bessel functions jℓ​ and Legendre polynomials Pℓ​. The regular radial solution outside the potential has asymptotic form uℓ​∝sin(kr−ℓπ/2+δℓ​). Its real scattering phase shift δℓ​ changes the ratio of outgoing to incoming waves to the Partial-wave S-matrix Sℓ​=e2iδℓ​. Matching outgoing coefficients yields
f(θ)=2ik1​ℓ=0∑∞​(2ℓ+1)(Sℓ​−1)Pℓ​(cosθ)=k1​ℓ=0∑∞​(2ℓ+1)eiδℓ​sinδℓ​Pℓ​(cosθ).​
(12)
Here ℓ labels the orbital angular momentum, with squared magnitude ℏ2ℓ(ℓ+1). The assumption of purely elastic scattering is what makes every Sℓ​ have unit modulus.

In the first Born approximation, let q=∣k′−k∣=2ksin(θ/2) be the transferred wave number. The angular integral in the three-dimensional Fourier transform is 4πsin(qr)/(qr), so
fB​(θ)=−2πℏ2m​∫e−iq⋅rV0​δ(r−R)d3r=−ℏ22mV0​R2​qRsin(qR)​.
(17)
Writing g=2mV0​R/ℏ2 and sincu=sinu/u, with value one at zero, the delta-shell Born amplitude gives
fB​=−gRsinc[2kRsin(θ/2)],dΩdσB​​=g2R2sinc2[2kRsin(θ/2)].​
(18)
For kR≪1 the scattering is approximately isotropic, with total cross section 4πg2R2. For kR≫1 it has a forward lobe of angular width of order (kR)−1 and oscillatory zeros where 2kRsin(θ/2)=nπ. At a fixed nonzero scattering angle, its envelope decreases as k−2. The right-hand panel of the figure shows this angular dependence.
For real V0​ the first-order scattering amplitude is real, so inserting it literally in the optical theorem would predict zero total cross section, contrary to its nonzero squared modulus. The first Born amplitude alone does not satisfy the optical theorem at order V02​. This is an order-of-approximation issue: the squared first-order amplitude is already second order, and the second Born term supplies the required second-order imaginary forward amplitude. At first order both sides vanish. A weak-shell regime is needed for the Born truncation itself to be accurate.

For bosonic creation operators and bosonic annihilation operators, the canonical commutation relations are
[ai​,aj†​]=δij​,[ai​,aj​]=[ai†​,aj†​]=0.
(19)
For fermionic annihilation operators and their adjoints, the canonical anticommutation relations are
{fi​,fj†​}=δij​,{fi​,fj​}={fi†​,fj†​}=0.
(20)
The respective number operators are ni​=ai†​ai​ and ni​=fi†​fi​. Their allowed eigenvalues are 0,1,2,… for bosons;0,1 for fermions​. In the fermionic case ni2​=ni​, so its eigenvalues must satisfy n2=n; this is the single-mode Pauli exclusion principle.

There is a normalization mismatch in the printed definitions: σx±iσy have nonzero entries of magnitude two, whereas a single fermionic creation or annihilation operator has magnitude one. To obtain the intended Jordan–Wigner transformation, use normalized spin ladders sj±​=(σjx​±iσjy​)/2. The given fermion expressions represent these sj±​. The two-site Jordan-Wigner normalization and signs then fix every factor unambiguously.
Put nj​=fj†​fj​ and P1​=eiπn1​=1−2n1​. The canonical anticommutation relations give P12​=I, P1​f1​=−f1​P1​, and [P1​,f2​]=[P1​,f2†​]=0. Hence
[s1−​,s1+​]=[f1​,f1†​]=1−2n1​=−σ1z​,[σ1z​,σ2z​]=4[n1​,n2​]=0,
(23)
[s1−​,s2−​]=f1​P1​f2​−P1​f2​f1​=−P1​{f1​,f2​}=0,
(24)
and
[s2−​,s2+​]=[P1​f2​,f2†​P1​]=f2​f2†​−f2†​f2​=1−2n2​=−σ2z​.
(25)
These are the spin commutation relations, with distinct sites commuting. If one retains the unscaled symbols σj±​=2sj±​, the first and last displayed commutators instead equal −4σjz​; the off-site commutators remain zero. Setting the unscaled σj−​ equal to fj​ would contradict the original Pauli matrices.

(26)
Therefore
H=2h−2h(n1​+n2​)−2J(f1†​f2​+f2†​f1​).​

The orthogonal transformation b=(f1​+f2​)/2​, c=(f1​−f2​)/2​ preserves the canonical anticommutation relations and diagonalizes this bilinear Hamiltonian:

A quantum state ensemble of normalized states ∣ψi​⟩, with probabilities pi​≥0 and ∑i​pi​=1, has density operator
ρ=i∑​pi​∣ψi​⟩⟨ψi​∣.​
(29)
It is a positive semidefinite Hermitian operator with trace one, and the Born rule assigns a measurement outcome with projector Π the probability Tr(ρΠ).
A pure state has ρ=∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣, hence ρ2=ρ and purity of a density operator Tr(ρ2)=1. Its only nonzero eigenvalue is one. A mixed state has at least two nonzero eigenvalues and Tr(ρ2)<1. The spectral theorem proves the converse criteria, since the nonnegative eigenvalues sum to one. Different quantum state ensembles may give the same density operator; the mere presence of several terms in a preparation list does not make the state mixed if all positive-weight terms represent the same quantum state ray.

Take the two magnetic moments along z to be ±μ, so H=−μBz​σz​ and the two energies are ∓μBz​. The quantum Gibbs state at inverse temperature β=1/(kB​T) is
ρE​=2cosh(βμBz​)eβμBz​σz​​=2cosh(βμBz​)1​(eβμBz​0​0e−βμBz​​)=2I+tanh(βμBz​)σz​​.​
