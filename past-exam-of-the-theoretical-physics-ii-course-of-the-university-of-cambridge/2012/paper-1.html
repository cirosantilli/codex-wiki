In a finite-dimensional basis, interchange the two finite sums:
Tr(AB)=∑j,k​Ajk​Bkj​=∑k,j​Bkj​Ajk​=Tr(BA)​.

For real ϵ, unitarity to first order gives
I=U†U=(I−iϵF†)(I+iϵF)=I+iϵ(F−F†)+O(ϵ2).
(5)
Hence the generator of an infinitesimal unitary transformation obeys F=F†: it is Hermitian. More precisely, U(ϵ)=I+iϵF+O(ϵ2) is a differentiable unitary family at the identity. The omitted second-order terms matter if ϵ is finite.

A normalized pure state has ρ=∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣, and therefore
ρ2=ρ,Trρ2=1.​
(10)
Conversely, diagonalize a permissible density operator. Its eigenvalues obey 0≤λj​≤1 and ∑j​λj​=1, so

The half-line square-root potential well is confining for a>0, which is assumed here. The hard wall imposes ψ(0)=0 and the smooth turning point is xt​=(E/a)2. With u=ax​/E, the WKB action becomes
∫0xt​​k(x)dx=ℏa222m​E5/2​∫01​u(1−u)1/2du=15ℏa282m​E5/2​.
(22)
The hard-wall and soft-turning-point WKB quantization condition therefore gives
EnWKB​=[82m​15πℏa2​(n+43​)]2/5,n=0,1,….​
(23)
The coefficient a here denotes the potential strength, not the integration limit in the generic quantization formula.
The WKB approximation is reliable in the interior where ∣k′∣/k2≪1, but fails near the soft turning point where k→0; a connection formula supplies its phase. The hard wall also requires its boundary condition, and V′=a/(2x​) makes the local slow-variation condition fail sufficiently close to zero. Higher-energy states have a larger action relative to ℏ, with smaller relative endpoint regions, so the leading WKB spectrum is generally more reliable at large n than for the ground state.

Split the time-evolution operator into N factors with ϵ=T/N, and insert N−1 position completeness relations between them. Each factor is evaluated by inserting momentum states normalized by ⟨x∣p⟩=(2πℏ)−1/2eipx/ℏ:
⟨xj+1​∣e−iϵp2/(2mℏ)∣xj​⟩​=∫R​2πℏdp​exp[ℏip(xj+1​−xj​)​−2mℏiϵp2​]=2πiℏϵm​​exp[2ℏϵim(xj+1​−xj​)2​].​
(28)
Multiplying the N kernels and integrating the intermediate positions produces the stated time-sliced path integral, including its N/2 normalization power. For a free particle this Gaussian short-time kernel is exact, so the convolution identity already holds at finite N. Oscillatory integrals are defined with a vanishing convergence regulator, consistently fixing the square-root phase.

For small fixed positive E, the stated approximate curve has maxima at
κa=(n+21​)π,σres,max​=mE2πℏ2​=k24π​,n=0,1,….​
(42)
Its formal minima are zero at κa=nπ. The peak height is the S-wave unitarity limit, and the peaks narrow as E/V0​ decreases.
To identify the bound-state threshold, let the bound-state energy be −ε with ε>0. The reduced radial wave is proportional to sin(Kr) inside and e−χr outside, with K=2m(V0​−ε)​/ℏ and χ=2mε​/ℏ. Matching gives Kcot(Ka)=−χ. As a new bound state emerges at ε↓0,
κcot(κa)=0 ⟹ κa=(n+21​)π.​
(43)
Thus the limiting peak locations coincide with the appearance of successive S-wave bound states. At the exact threshold the zero-energy radial solution is not square-integrable; a normalizable bound state appears just beyond it. Finite-energy maxima shift slightly from the zero-energy positions.
The formal minima require care because the assumption used in part d fails there. Retaining ka in the exact matching condition gives the spherical square-well scattering length
as​=a−κtan(κa)​,σ0​⟶4πas2​
(44)
away from a threshold. At κa=nπ, as​=a, so the true low-energy S-wave scattering contribution is 4πa2, not zero. Genuine leading-order zeros of that contribution instead satisfy tanq=q, for example q≃4.493409 and 7.725252. The original sketch compares the exact finite-energy S wave with the requested approximation, making this nonuniformity visible. Dominance by the S wave must be checked separately near a higher-partial-wave threshold. The scattering-length divergence and its bound-state interpretation are also derived in institutional lecture notes.[ref]
