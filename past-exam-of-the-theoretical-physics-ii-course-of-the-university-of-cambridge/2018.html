For independent bosonic modes, the canonical commutation relation is
[bi​,bj†​]=δij​,[bi​,bj​]=[bi†​,bj†​]=0.
(1)
For independent fermionic modes, the canonical anticommutation relations are
{ci​,cj†​}=δij​,{ci​,cj​}={ci†​,cj†​}=0,
(2)
where [A,B]=AB−BA and {A,B}=AB+BA. The creation operator is the adjoint of the annihilation operator. In either case the mode's number operator is
ni​=ai†​ai​​,
(3)
with ai​=bi​ or ci​ as appropriate. It measures the mode's occupation number. Its possible eigenvalues are
ni​=0,1,2,… for bosons;ni​=0,1 for fermions​.
(4)
For fermions, ci2​=0 implies ni2​=ci†​(1−ci†​ci​)ci​=ni​, so the number operator is a projection. This expresses the exclusion of double occupation of one fermionic mode. The total particle number is N=∑i​ni​.

Expand the anticommutators using the relations in part (a). The terms with two creation or two annihilation operators vanish, leaving
{γiA​,γjA​}=2δij​,{γiB​,γjB​}=2δij​,{γiA​,γjB​}=0​.
(5)
Each Majorana fermion operator is Hermitian and squares to the identity. In particular, (γiA​)†γiA​=1, so this expression cannot distinguish an empty mode from an occupied one. A single Majorana operator is its own adjoint and does not supply distinct creation and annihilation operators for such an occupation.
An occupation can instead be assigned after pairing Majorana operators into a fermionic mode. For two distinct Majorana operators γa​,γb​,
f=2γa​+iγb​​,nf​=f†f=21+iγa​γb​​​.
(6)
This is a nontrivial projection operator with eigenvalues zero and one. Thus occupation belongs to a chosen pair of Majorana operators, not to an individual Majorana operator.

a OurBigBook.com
For a short-range potential, an incident plane wave of unit amplitude and wave vector kez​ has the large-distance scattering wavefunction
ψ(r)=eikz+f(θ,ϕ)reikr​+O(r−2)(r→∞)​.
(17)
The coefficient f is the scattering amplitude. For a central potential it depends only on the scattering angle θ. The incident probability current is ℏk/m, while the scattered radial current is (ℏk/m)∣f∣2/r2 to leading order. Their ratio gives
dΩdσ​=∣f(θ,ϕ)∣2,σtot​=∫∣f(θ,ϕ)∣2dΩ​.
(18)
These are the differential scattering cross-section and total cross section for elastic scattering with the same incident and outgoing wave number.
The expression is asymptotic: it specifies the outgoing wave's leading 1/r term, not its finite-distance radial corrections or its form inside the interaction region. In particular, for angle-dependent f, the spherical term by itself has an angular-Laplacian contribution of order r−3 to the free wave equation, which is cancelled by subleading terms in the exact solution.

The scattering length is a0​=−limk→0​tanδ0​/k. Since sin(kR)∼kR and cos(kR)∼1, the exact phase shift gives, away from the pole,
a0​=R1+gg​,g=ℏ22mV0​R​​.
(27)
Thus plot a0​/R against g, so that both axes are dimensionless.
For repulsion, g>0, the curve rises from zero to the horizontal asymptote a0​/R=1, staying between zero and one. For weak attraction, −1<g<0, the scattering length is negative and tends to −∞ as g→−1+. For stronger attraction, g<−1, it is positive and greater than R, tending to +∞ as g→−1− and to R as g→−∞. Its only zero is g=0.
The pole at g=−1 is the zero-energy threshold of an S-wave bound state. To see this directly, use u∝sinh(κr) inside and u∝e−κr outside for energy −ℏ2κ2/(2m). The same derivative jump gives
g=−κR[1+coth(κR)]=−1−e−2κR2κR​.
(28)
This approaches −1 as κ↓0 and admits a positive κ for g<−1. At the threshold the scattering length diverges; this is a zero-energy scattering resonance, not a divergence of the finite-energy cross-section beyond its partial-wave unitarity bound. These are the main features of the scattering length of a delta-shell potential.

The delta-shell Born amplitude is first order in the potential strength:
fB​=−gRqRsin(qR)​.
(29)
It retains finite-energy angular dependence and includes the leading weak-coupling contributions of all partial waves. By contrast, the supplied −gR/(1+g)=−a0​ is the zero-energy limit of the exact S-wave scattering amplitude. It is isotropic, depends nonlinearly on g, and captures the scattering-length pole missed by first-order perturbation theory.
At finite energy the exact S-wave amplitude is instead
f0​(k)=2ike2iδ0​−1​=kcotδ0​−ik1​,
(30)
which is generally complex and obeys elastic unitarity. Its zero-energy limit is −a0​ for finite scattering length. Thus the two expressions printed in the question represent different approximations; the energy-independent S-wave expression is not the full finite-k result of part (c).
Their common regime requires both weak coupling and low energy:
∣g∣≪1,kR≪1​.
(31)
Then qR≤2kR≪1, so
fB​=−gR[1−6(qR)2​+O((qR)4)],−a0​=−1+ggR​=−gR+g2R+O(g3R).
(32)
Consequently
fB​∼f0​∼−gR=−ℏ22mV0​R2​​
(33)
in that joint regime. Low energy alone does not remove the nonperturbative denominator 1+g, while weak coupling alone does not remove higher-partial-wave angular dependence.

An ensemble with normalized quantum states ∣ψa​⟩ and probabilities pa​≥0, ∑a​pa​=1, has density matrix
ρ=a∑​pa​∣ψa​⟩⟨ψa​∣​.
(34)
Each rank-one projector is Hermitian, so ρ†=ρ. Taking the trace in any orthonormal basis gives
Trρ=∑a​pa​⟨ψa​∣ψa​⟩=1.
(35)
It is also positive: ⟨v∣ρ∣v⟩=∑a​pa​∣⟨ψa​∣v⟩∣2≥0. Its eigenvalues λa​ are therefore nonnegative and sum to one. Hence
Trρ2=∑a​λa2​≤(∑a​λa​)2=1,
(36)
with equality exactly when one eigenvalue is one and the others are zero. Thus the purity of a density operator distinguishes
pure state: Trρ2=1,mixed state: Trρ2<1​.
(37)
Equivalently, a pure state has ρ2=ρ and rank one. In a d-dimensional Hilbert space, purity is at least 1/d, attained by the maximally mixed state I/d.
