In second quantization, expand the annihilating quantized field in a complete orthonormal basis of one-particle states, ψ^​(x)=∑α​φα​(x)aα​. At equal times, the canonical commutation relation for bosons and the canonical anticommutation relations for fermions are, respectively,
[ψ^​(x),ψ^​†(x′)]=δ(x−x′),[ψ^​(x),ψ^​(x′)]=[ψ^​†(x),ψ^​†(x′)]=0,
(1)
and
{ψ^​(x),ψ^​†(x′)}=δ(x−x′),{ψ^​(x),ψ^​(x′)}={ψ^​†(x),ψ^​†(x′)}=0.
(2)
Here δ is a Dirac delta distribution. Multiplying by the basis wave functions and integrating gives [aα​,aβ†​]=δαβ​ for bosons, or {aα​,aβ†​}=δαβ​ for fermions; the two-annihilator and two-creator brackets vanish in the corresponding convention.
The mode number operator and total particle number operator are
nα​=aα†​aα​,N^=α∑​nα​.​
(3)
The bosonic occupation numbers are 0,1,2,…. For fermions, aα2​=0 and the canonical anticommutation relations imply nα2​=nα​, so the fermionic occupation numbers are only 0 and 1, as required by the Pauli exclusion principle.
Keeping only two modes later is a projection: the projected field bracket is ∑j=12​φj​(x)φj∗​(x′), rather than the full Dirac delta distribution.

Normalize the ring modes as φj​(x)=L−1/2eikj​x, with distinct kj​=2πℓj​/L and integers ℓj​. Inserting ψ^​(x)=φ1​(x)a1​+φ2​(x)a2​ into the contact interaction gives
Hint​=2g​∑i,j,k,l=12​Iijkl​ai†​aj†​ak​al​,Iijkl​=∫0L​φi∗​φj∗​φk​φl​dx.
(4)
Orthogonality makes Iijkl​=1/L when ki​+kj​=kk​+kl​, and zero otherwise: this is momentum conservation in the projected Hamiltonian operator. There is one all-mode-1 term, one all-mode-2 term, and four mixed terms with one particle in each mode. A pair conversion between distinct ring modes would require 2k1​=2k2​ and therefore vanishes.
Using aj†2​aj2​=nj​(nj​−1) and a1†​a2†​a1​a2​=n1​n2​ yields the contact interaction between two ring modes,
Hint​=2Lg​[n1​(n1​−1)+n2​(n2​−1)+4n1​n2​],α=β=2Lg​,γ=L2g​.​
(5)
The factor four comes from two independent choices of ordering for the creation operators and annihilation operators. This is the two-mode projected model; a full ring contact interaction can also scatter particles into other modes while conserving total momentum.

At fixed particle number N, write n1​=n and n2​=N−n. The contact interaction between two ring modes has energy eigenvalues
E(n)=2Lg​[N(N−1)+2n(N−n)],n=0,1,…,N.
(6)
For g>0, the nonnegative product n(N−n) is minimized at the endpoints. Consequently
Emin​=2LgN(N−1)​,∣ψmin​⟩=c1​∣N,0⟩+c2​∣0,N⟩,∣c1​∣2+∣c2​∣2=1.​
(7)
For N≥1 this is a two-dimensional degenerate ground state subspace. N=0 instead has the unique Fock vacuum. In this projected model, putting all particles into one traveling mode avoids the extra exchange contribution paid when both distinct modes are occupied.
For the alternative coefficients, put α=β=A<0 and γ=0. Then E(n)=A[N(N−1)−2n(N−n)] is again minimized at n=0,N, with Emin​=AN(N−1). Evenness of N is unnecessary for this result; the balanced Fock state maximizes this attractive-interaction energy instead of minimizing it.

Add the one-particle energy term H0​=ϵ1​n1​+ϵ2​n2​. It is diagonal in the same Fock state basis as the contact interaction between two ring modes. For N=2, the ordered basis (∣2,0⟩,∣1,1⟩,∣0,2⟩) gives
H=​2ϵ1​+g/L00​0ϵ1​+ϵ2​+2g/L0​002ϵ2​+g/L​​.​
(8)
For g>0, the middle eigenvalue exceeds the mean of the two endpoint eigenvalues by g/L, so it cannot be the smallest. The ground state is therefore ∣2,0⟩ if ϵ1​<ϵ2​, and ∣0,2⟩ if ϵ2​<ϵ1​, with
Emin​=2min(ϵ1​,ϵ2​)+g/L.​
(9)
If ϵ1​=ϵ2​, every normalized linear combination of the two endpoint Fock states is a ground state. The same endpoint argument works for general fixed N, since the interaction contribution is a concave quadratic function of the mode occupation number.

For distinct positive integers p,q, use normalized box modes φ1​=2/L​sin(pπx/L) and φ2​=2/L​sin(qπx/L). Their contact interaction overlaps obey
I1111​=I2222​=2L3​,I1122​=L1​.
(10)
Unlike traveling ring modes, these standing modes also allow pair conversion. For the first two box modes, or any distinct pair without a third-harmonic resonance, the contact interaction between two box modes is
Hint​=4L3g​[n1​(n1​−1)+n2​(n2​−1)]+L2g​n1​n2​+2Lg​(a1†2​a22​+a2†2​a12​).​
(11)
The last two terms change an individual mode occupation number by two while conserving total particle number. Thus the Hamiltonian operator is not diagonal in the Fock state basis, and minimizing diagonal entries alone does not find its ground state. For example, at N=2 with no dispersion, (∣2,0⟩−∣0,2⟩)/2​ has the lowest energy g/(2L), below either unmixed endpoint's 3g/(2L).
The wording permits modes beyond the first two. For completeness, the identity sin3u=(3sinu−sin3u)/4 gives
I1112​=−2Lδq,3p​​,I1222​=−2Lδp,3q​​.
(12)
When q=3p, add −g(a1†2​a1​a2​+a1†​a2†​a12​)/(2L); when p=3q, add −g(a1†​a2†​a22​+a2†2​a1​a2​)/(2L). These extra terms change an individual occupation number by one and require diagonalizing the corresponding fixed-N matrix as well.

A normalized oscillator coherent state is an eigenstate of the annihilation operator, a∣α⟩=α∣α⟩. Expanding it in Fock states gives
∣α⟩=e−∣α∣2/2∑n=0∞​n!​αn​∣n⟩.
(15)
Since x=ℏ/(2Mω)​(a+a†), ⟨a2⟩=α2, and ⟨aa†⟩=∣α∣2+1, the variance is ⟨x2⟩−⟨x⟩2=ℏ/(2Mω). Hence
Δx=2Mωℏ​​,​
(16)
independently of the complex amplitude α.
For A=αa† and B=−α∗a, the commutator is the scalar [A,B]=∣α∣2I. The Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula therefore terminates and gives the displacement operator
D(α)=eαa†−α∗a=e−∣α∣2/2eαa†e−α∗a.
(17)
Because a∣0⟩=0, applying it to the Fock vacuum yields exactly the Fock state expansion above:
∣α⟩=D(α)∣0⟩.​
(18)
For a general complex α, its complex conjugate is required in the annihilation term. The expression with −αa printed in the paper is valid when α is real.

The quantum state is continuous through a sudden finite-force quantum quench, so it is still ∣0⟩ at t=0+. Define
λ=2Mℏω3​F0​​,b=a−λ,θ=ωt.
(19)
The new Hamiltonian operator is H=ℏω(b†b+1/2)−ℏωλ2. The old Fock vacuum obeys b∣0⟩=−λ∣0⟩, so the coherent state after shifting an oscillator equilibrium rotates with amplitude −λe−iθ relative to the new equilibrium. In the original oscillator basis, the full state is
∣ψ(t)⟩=e−iθ/2+iλ2(θ−sinθ)​λ(1−e−iθ)⟩.​
(20)
One way to obtain the phase is to write
e−iHt/ℏ∣0⟩=e−iθ/2+iλ2θD(λ)e−iθa†aD(−λ)∣0⟩
(21)
and apply the displacement operator composition rule. The coherent-state oscillator expectation values give
⟨x⟩=Mω2F0​​(1−cosωt),⟨p⟩=ωF0​​sinωt.​
(22)
The position uncertainty stays ℏ/(2Mω)​. These mean values obey the forced classical equation, while the nonzero variance retains the quantum width.

Specify the magnetic coupling before fixing its sign: let the magnetic moment be μ=γS, where γ is a signed gyromagnetic ratio. The Zeeman effect gives HZ​=−γB⋅S. For spin one-half, S=ℏσ/2, so with B=∣B∣ and u=βγℏB/2,
ρ=Tre−βHZ​e−βHZ​​=21​[I+tanhuB^⋅σ],Z=2coshu.​
(23)
This is the quantum Gibbs state. Its eigenvalues are (1±tanhu)/2, so its purity is (1+tanh2u)/2. For nonzero level splitting it approaches a pure state as ∣u∣→∞: positive inverse temperature selects the lower energy, while negative inverse temperature selects the upper one. It is a maximally mixed state at u=0, including infinite temperature or zero magnetic splitting. At zero field the Gibbs state remains I/2; lowering temperature alone does not select a unique pure state from that degeneracy.
Negative temperature is meaningful for an internally equilibrated spin sector with a bounded energy spectrum and population inversion. It must be sufficiently isolated from unbounded motional degrees of freedom: the oscillator's unbounded spectrum, for instance, makes its partition sum diverge at negative inverse temperature. The two-level spin partition sum itself stays finite for either sign.

For B=Bez​, the quantum Gibbs state is diagonal in the Sz​ basis. Its two probabilities are proportional to e±βγℏB/2. Thus
⟨Sz​⟩=2ℏ​tanh(2kB​TγℏB​).​
(24)
At small inverse temperature, ⟨Sz​⟩≃βγℏ2B/4. As a function of u=βγℏB/2, the normalized spin component is the smooth odd curve tanhu: it crosses zero at u=0 and saturates at ±1. As a function of β its direction is reversed if γB<0. For γB>0, positive temperature favors Sz​=+ℏ/2, while negative temperature gives the inverted population.

For a time-independent Hamiltonian operator, the time-evolution operator is U(T)=e−iHT/ℏ, with T=t−t′. Insert its resolution of identity in normalized energy eigenstates:
⟨x∣U(T)∣x′⟩=∑α​⟨x∣α⟩e−iEα​T/ℏ⟨α∣x′⟩.
(25)
Since ⟨x∣α⟩=φα​(x), the retarded quantum propagator in the question is
K(x,t∣x′,t′)=Θ(T)α∑​φα​(x)φα∗​(x′)e−iEα​T/ℏ.​
(26)
The Heaviside step function imposes the chosen retarded convention. If the Hamiltonian operator has a continuous spectrum, the spectral sum includes its continuum integral; it must not be restricted to bound states. The spectral resolution of a quantum propagator also shows that K tends to δ(x−x′) as T↓0. With the step factor it obeys (iℏ∂t​−H)K=iℏδ(T)δ(x−x′); the corresponding Green function with a unit delta source is K/(iℏ).

φ′(0+)−φ′(0−)=−ℏ22mg​φ(0).

Choose the even-channel scattering phase shift convention φk,e​(x)=π−1/2cos(k∣x∣+δe​) for k>0. The delta potential derivative jump gives
−2ksinδe​=−2κcosδe​,

At the origin, the normalized bound state in a delta potential contributes ∣φ0​(0)∣2=κ, and the odd scattering eigenfunctions contribute zero. The even continuum functions from the preceding part have
∣φk,e​(0)∣2=π1​cos2δe​=π1​k2+κ2k2​.

For fixed g>0, write A=ℏT/(2m). The bound contribution to the quantum propagator of an attractive delta potential remains κeiℏκ2T/(2m). For its continuum, the stationary phase is at k=0, where k2/(k2+κ2)=k2/κ2+O(k4). The Gaussian oscillatory integral and its derivative give
∫−∞∞​e−iAk2dk=π​e−iπ/4A−1/2,∫−∞∞​k2e−iAk2dk=2π​​e−3iπ/4A−3/2.

a OurBigBook.com
Outside a short-range scattering region, the stationary Schrödinger equation reduces to the two-dimensional Helmholtz equation. Separation in polar coordinates gives outgoing radial Hankel functions, whose large-r dependence is r−1/2eikr. The same power follows from conserved probability current: a circle has length proportional to r, so the squared outgoing amplitude must decrease as 1/r.
For an incident plane wave in the x direction, adopt the dimensionless amplitude convention
ψ(r,θ)∼eikrcosθ+kr​eiπ/4​f(θ)eikr.​
(35)
The phase eiπ/4 and factor k−1/2 specify the definition of f; they can be absorbed into a differently normalized scattering amplitude. Here they are kept exactly so that the later two-dimensional scattering cross-section follows consistently.
