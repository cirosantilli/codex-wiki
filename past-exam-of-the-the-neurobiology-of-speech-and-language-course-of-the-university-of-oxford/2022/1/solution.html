Phonology and syntax usually show a left-hemisphere bias, whereas semantic processing depends on a more bilateral system with task-dependent asymmetries. None is confined absolutely to one cerebral hemisphere, and lateralisation of an experimental contrast is not equivalent to localisation of a representation. Phonology concerns structured sublexical units, semantics concerns meaning, and syntax concerns relations that organise words into sentences. These processes interact, so tasks must isolate the relevant contrast rather than merely require language.
Historical lesion observations motivated left language dominance, but their anatomical interpretation needs care. Modern MRI of Paul Broca's preserved cases showed damage extending beyond the conventional cortical region named after him. Severe impaired speech after a left lesion therefore supports lateralisation without proving that a small superficial patch alone constitutes the speech system. Large vascular lesions can also damage connections between surviving regions.[ref]
For phonological processing, Robert J. Zatorre and colleagues used PET while participants judged different attributes of the same speech material. Phonetic discrimination increased left inferior-frontal activity, whereas pitch discrimination recruited right prefrontal regions; acoustically matched speech engaged secondary auditory cortices bilaterally. Holding the signal relatively constant while changing the decision supports functional differentiation beyond a contrast between unrelated stimuli. It does not show that all phonological representations reside in inferior frontal cortex: articulatory recoding, attention and decision demands can contribute to the phonetic contrast.[ref]
The distinction matters because speech perception begins with bilateral auditory analysis, whereas selecting, sequencing or rehearsing phonological forms can have a stronger left bias. Impairment of repetition after damage to a dorsal language pathway and comprehension after other damage constrain different transformations. A deficit in producing a phoneme is not automatically a deficit in recognising it. Familiarity with a sound pattern and its linguistic role can also alter the task, so acoustic speed or frequency alone cannot define phonology.
Semantic processing illustrates why a leftward verbal-task result can conceal bilateral representational resources. In Gorana Pobric's rTMS experiment, perturbing either temporal pole slowed semantic associations for words and pictures while a perceptual comparison was spared; stimulation of a control site did not produce the same semantic effect. This supports a contribution from both anterior temporal lobes across input formats. It does not require identical response profiles throughout both lobes or imply that semantic retrieval and selection are entirely bilateral.[ref]
Verbal naming places additional demands on lexical retrieval and speech production. Consequently, stronger left activity in naming than in passive picture viewing can reflect access and output as well as conceptual knowledge. Conversely, right-sided involvement in nonverbal meaning does not establish a separate nonlinguistic semantic store. Comparisons should separate semantic association from perceptual similarity and control both response requirements and difficulty. Lesion and perturbation results help adjudicate whether an activated region contributes to the task.
For syntax, lesion studies and functional imaging converge on a predominantly left frontotemporal network, rather than a single grammar centre. Evelina Fedorenko's individual-localisation study showed language-selective and domain-general regions next to one another within the anatomical territory called Broca's area. Averaging across people can merge them. Thus, an inferior-frontal response to a hard sentence may reflect language processing, domain-general control or both; independent functional localizers and nonlinguistic controls improve interpretation.[ref]
Corianne Rogalsky's lesion study compared sentence-picture matching and plausibility judgments in 66 patients. The distribution of syntactic-comprehension impairment implicated a wider left-hemisphere system and challenged a simple equation between Broca's area damage and agrammatic comprehension. Convergence across tasks is useful because picture matching also demands visual analysis and response selection, while plausibility can recruit semantic expectations. Neither behavioural label makes the task syntactically pure.[ref]
Sentence comprehension in Japanese and Japanese Sign Language also showed left-dominant frontal and temporoparietal responses across sensory conditions. This directly addresses hemispheric comparisons, although comprehension versus nonword detection is not a pure assay of syntax.[ref]
A quantitative laterality index can summarise an explicitly defined response. For nonnegative left and right measurements L,R with L+R>0,
LI=L+RL−R​.
(1)
It ranges from −1 to 1, but depends on baseline, threshold and region selection. Responses (20,10) and (10,5) have the same index 1/3 despite different total responses. If left activity falls from 20 to 10 while right activity remains 10, the index becomes zero without any right recruitment. These are illustrative values, not imaging observations. Negative contrasts need a separately justified convention.
The most convincing evidence therefore combines within-person process contrasts, unilateral perturbations or lesions, and converging measurements of behaviour and neural responses. It also describes individual variation instead of assuming that every participant has the population-average pattern. The justified conclusion is graded functional asymmetry within interacting networks: strongest left biases for many phonological and syntactic operations, bilateral conceptual contributions, and substantial dependence on task and representation level.
