There is no secure, universal bilingual advantage in domain-general executive function. There are important benefits of knowing and using two languages, some positive task findings and suggestive associations with cognitive reserve, but these are different claims with different evidential requirements. Bilingualism varies with proficiency, acquisition age, frequency of use and the situations in which languages are switched. A binary label can obscure the experience most relevant to a proposed mechanism.
The executive-control hypothesis begins with competition: using one language while another is available may repeatedly exercise selection and interference control. Transfer to nonlinguistic tasks is plausible, but it is a far-transfer claim, not an automatic consequence of speaking two languages. The account should predict which control operation improves and under which switching experiences, rather than count every faster response as evidence of inhibition.
Ellen Bialystok's three studies of middle-aged and older adults reported smaller Simon interference costs in bilingual participants, with stronger effects in older groups and demanding conditions. In the Simon task, the required response depends on a relevant stimulus feature while an irrelevant spatial location can favour the competing response. Comparing congruent and incongruent trials operationalises conflict. These positive results support a conditional association; nonrandom language histories leave socioeconomic, educational and other selection explanations to be tested.[ref]
The relevant estimand must be stated. Let Cg​=RTg,incongruent​−RTg,congruent​ for group g. A conflict advantage requires Cbilingual​<Cmonolingual​, which is a group-by-congruency interaction. Illustratively, monolingual means of (500,550) ms and bilingual means of (450,500) ms for congruent/incongruent trials give equal costs of 50 ms. The bilingual group is faster overall, but this example supplies no selective conflict advantage. Accuracy and speed-accuracy trade-offs must also be examined. These numbers are a calculation, not observed study data.
Negative studies are therefore informative rather than disposable exceptions. Kenneth R. Paap and colleagues tested 15 executive-processing indicators across three studies, including Simon, flanker, antisaccade and switching tasks, and did not find a coherent bilingual benefit. Inconsistent cross-task correlations also challenged treating every task's interference score as a stable measure of one general control faculty. This does not prove an exactly zero effect for every bilingual population, but it contradicts a broad, easily expressed advantage.[ref]
Child data also constrain generality. Eneko Antón's study used the Attention Network Test in bilingual and monolingual children with careful attention to age and socioeconomic variables, and found no bilingual advantage in the principal attentional measures. A reliable conflict effect within participants verifies that a task manipulation worked; it does not guarantee reliable individual differences or a sensitive group contrast. Measurement reliability and adequate statistical power matter to both positive and negative interpretations.[ref]
Selective reporting can make the literature appear more consistent than the completed research. Angela de Bruin and colleagues followed conference abstracts into publication and found that results supporting an advantage were more likely to be published than null or opposing results. This is direct evidence about the research pipeline. It does not invalidate every positive experiment or by itself estimate the true average effect. Preregistration, shared outcomes and publication of null results address the identified bias.[ref]
A different question concerns later clinical symptoms. Suvarna Alladi's retrospective clinic series contained 648 people with dementia, including 391 bilingual participants, and reported symptom onset about 4.5 years later in the bilingual group after considering several covariates. The result is compatible with cognitive reserve. It is not evidence that bilingualism reduced dementia incidence: all participants already had dementia, and referral, survival, recalled onset and unmeasured life experiences can affect the comparison. Later symptoms also do not necessarily imply less underlying pathology.[ref]
A stronger test would measure language experience continuously, recruit groups from the same communities, prespecify a small set of well-measured outcomes and analyse multiple tasks with an explicit measurement model. Longitudinal studies with baseline cognitive performance reduce selection ambiguity; randomised language-learning interventions with active controls test narrower training effects, though short courses do not reproduce lifelong bilingual experience.
Finally, linguistic communication and cultural access are substantive benefits regardless of executive-task results. Metalinguistic awareness and language-specific proficiency are closer to the practised skill than general inhibition, and should be evaluated as separate outcomes. Bilingualism is valuable, but the evidence warrants specific, experience- and task-dependent claims rather than a universal cognitive enhancement or a promise of dementia prevention.
