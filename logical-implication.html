Logical implication (→)

 ... Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Mathematical logic Propositional logic Proposition Logical connective
OurBigBook.com Words: 404 Articles: 4
Logical implication forms the proposition A→B, read “if A, then B”. In classical propositional logic, it is false exactly when A is true and B is false. Equivalently, it has the same truth table as ¬A∨B, using logical negation and logical disjunction. A false hypothesis makes the logical implication true regardless of its conclusion; thus it asserts neither the hypothesis nor a causal connection.
For example, for every natural number n, divisibility by four implies that n is an even number: n=4k=2(2k). At n=8, both statements are true. At n=6, the hypothesis is false and the conclusion true; at n=3, both are false. The only forbidden case is a multiple of four that is not an even number.
In Intuitionistic propositional logic, a proof of A→B consists of a construction transforming any proof of A into a proof of B. The implication introduction rule proves B under a temporary assumption A; the modus ponens rule applies the result when A is available. The classical identification with ¬A∨B is not generally intuitionistically valid.
