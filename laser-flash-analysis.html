Laser flash analysis

 ... Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Heat Thermal conduction Thermal diffusivity
OurBigBook.com Words: 481
Laser flash analysis measures thermal diffusivity by heating one face of a specimen briefly and recording the opposite face's temperature rise. The original 1961 experiment used a flash tube rather than a laser: a blackened thin metal specimen sat in an opaque ceramic holder, with thermocouple wires pressed against its rear face. An amplifier, oscilloscope and camera recorded the signal. Uniform front illumination and the black coating promoted uniform absorption; the opaque low-thermal conductivity holder blocked direct rear illumination and reduced edge losses. The delayed rear response reveals internal thermal conduction rather than direct heating of the sensor. [ref]
A modern version uses a laser or flash lamp, a specimen of measured thickness L, controlled initial temperature, and a rear-facing infrared detector with a synchronized recorder. The readout is a temperature rise versus time, starting from a pre-pulse baseline. Thermal conductivity additionally requires mass density and specific heat capacity; it is not obtained from timing alone. [ref]

This coefficient comes from the stated ideal heat equation solution, not from an empirical sensor calibration. It has been independently checked by solving the series equation. Absolute absorbed pulse energy cancels from the normalized curve; determining heat capacity from its absolute height needs additional information. [ref]
Parker and colleagues identify finite flash duration, heat loss, nonuniform illumination, recording bandwidth and electrical interference as practical limits. A specimen too thin responds during the pulse; one too thick allows losses before the rear rise finishes. They shielded the electrical chain and recorded a baseline; their reported tests were preliminary metal measurements near ambient and moderately elevated temperatures. [ref]
Useful additional controls are to measure thickness at several points, verify the recorder's time scale and detector response independently, record a no-pulse baseline, and repeat at different pulse energies while avoiding phase transitions. Compare different thicknesses of the same homogeneous material: ideal half-rise times scale as L2. Failure of these checks can expose finite-pulse effects, sensor delay, optical transmission, coating storage or edge losses; a thickness error is especially consequential because the inferred thermal diffusivity depends on L2. These are proposed diagnostic controls, not invented measurements. When the pulse is not short or the sample exchanges appreciable heat with its surroundings, fit a model that includes those effects instead of applying the ideal half-rise coefficient unchanged.
