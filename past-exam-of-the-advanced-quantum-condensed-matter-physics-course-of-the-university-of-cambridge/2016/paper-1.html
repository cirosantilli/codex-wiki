The absorptive dielectric function counts optically accessible electron-hole pairs. In the electric dipole approximation the perturbation is proportional to a⋅p^​, with a the light polarization and p^​ the electronic momentum operator. The squared matrix element between occupied valence and empty conduction states is the transition strength; it includes polarization and band-symmetry selection rules. The sums enumerate initial and final subbands. photon momentum is negligible on the crystal-momentum scale, so the transition is approximately vertical in the in-plane wavevector k.
For each subband pair, C is the constant-transition-energy contour in the two-dimensional Brillouin zone. Its line element is dl, and the reciprocal gradient weights the states satisfying energy conservation. Specifically, the level-set representation of the joint density of states is
∫d2kδ(En′​(k)−En​(k)−ℏω)=∫C​∣∇k​(En′​−En​)∣dl​.
(1)
A small energy gradient means many nearly resonant states; a vanishing gradient can produce a Van Hove singularity or an edge. The displayed expression suppresses overall optical prefactors and assumes the momentum matrix element is approximately constant along each contour. More generally it stays inside that integral. Occupation factors fv​−fc​ are also implicit; at low temperature in an undoped well the relevant valence states are full and the conduction states empty. This is the independent-particle continuum contribution from Fermi's golden rule. Bound excitons add optical resonances below the corresponding free-pair thresholds.
The GaAs four-band model is rooted in bonding and spin-orbit coupling. Near the zone centre, the conduction edge is predominantly s-like and antibonding, while the upper valence manifold is predominantly p-like. Combining orbital angular momentum l=1 with spin s=1/2 splits the six p-derived states into a j=3/2 quartet and a lower j=1/2 doublet. The latter is the split-off valence band. Within the upper quartet, the mj​=±3/2 and mj​=±1/2 branches, defined relative to a propagation or quantization direction, have different curvatures and are called heavy holes and light holes. They coincide at the unstrained bulk zone centre but need not remain degenerate in a well. The conduction edge is a spin doublet. Thus “four bands” counts conduction, heavy-hole, light-hole and split-off branches, including their spin multiplicities; it does not count four individual states. Positive hole masses describe the downward valence curvature, whereas the conduction curvature is upward.

Eliminating nearly resonant real phonons requires more care than this second-order construction.
For ∣Δ∣<Ωq​ the denominator is negative: virtual phonon exchange is attractive in this low-energy channel. Physically, an electron changes the ionic density and a second electron can gain energy by responding to the delayed distortion. At large transfer the sign changes, and screened Coulomb repulsion must also be included when deciding the net low-energy pairing strength. The connection between lattice dynamics and superconductivity is supported by the isotope effect, but the mathematical attraction alone does not determine a measured transition temperature.[ref]

Cooper's problem asks whether two electrons added above an inert Fermi sea bind despite Pauli exclusion of lower states. An arbitrarily weak net attraction binds a zero-momentum singlet below the free-pair threshold, because the near-Fermi-surface integral diverges logarithmically as B↓0. Its wavefunction draws on many time-reversed momentum states and becomes dominated by a narrow region near the Fermi surface at weak coupling; the corresponding pair is spatially extended. This establishes a Cooper instability of the unpaired reference state.[ref] The full superconducting ground state needs a coherent many-electron treatment in which overlapping pairs and the reorganized sea are included. BCS theory supplies that step; the two-electron binding scale alone is neither a calculation of the many-body gap nor a derivation of zero resistance.[ref]

A Bravais lattice is the translation set Λ={n1​a1​+n2​a2​+n3​a3​:ni​∈Z} generated by primitive lattice translation vectors. Translating by any member of Λ leaves its surroundings equivalent. A crystal structure requires both this lattice and an atomic basis attached to every lattice point. The basis can lower the symmetry of the bare point lattice; a familiar-looking geometrical arrangement of atoms is not necessarily itself a Bravais lattice. For example, the diamond cubic structure is a face-centred cubic lattice with an identical-atom basis at fractional positions (0,0,0) and (1/4,1/4,1/4) in conventional cubic coordinates. The displacement between those two basis atoms is not itself a lattice translation.
The seven three-dimensional crystal systems are triclinic, monoclinic, orthorhombic, tetragonal, trigonal, hexagonal and cubic; the distinct translation types give 14 Bravais lattices. A conventional cell can contain several lattice points, while a primitive unit cell contains one. Simple cubic primitive lattice translation vectors can be chosen along the cube edges; for face-centred cubic they can be (a/2)(0,1,1), (a/2)(1,0,1) and (a/2)(1,1,0). Their primitive volume is a3/4, explaining the four lattice points per conventional face-centred cell.
A crystallographic point group comprises the orthogonal parts of the crystal's spatial symmetry operations. It constrains possible rotations, reflections, inversion and their combinations, while forgetting translations. Lattice preservation restricts proper rotational orders in three dimensions to 1,2,3,4,6; a fivefold axis cannot be a symmetry of a periodic three-dimensional lattice. One quick argument restricts a rotation's integral lattice representation through 1+2cosθ, an integer trace, producing these orders. There are 32 crystallographic point-group types. Examples are the cubic full octahedral group Oh​ with Hermann–Mauguin symbol m3ˉm, the tetrahedral class Td​ with symbol 4ˉ3m, and the polar tetragonal class C4v​ with symbol 4mm.
The space group retains the full isometry. In Seitz notation {R∣t} acts by x↦Rx+t, and multiplication is
{R∣t}{R′∣t′}={RR′∣Rt′+t}.
(20)
Its translation subgroup is normal and the quotient is the crystallographic point group. A screw axis combines rotation with translation along the axis; for example, applying a 21​ screw twice gives one whole lattice translation. A glide plane combines reflection with a translation parallel to the plane; applying the glide twice likewise gives a translation. These operations can preserve the complete crystal without their rotation or reflection alone preserving it.
A symmorphic space group admits an origin and representatives for which the point-group operations have no translation component. A nonsymmorphic space group has essential fractional translations that cannot all be removed by moving the origin. There are 230 three-dimensional space-group types, distinguished more finely than their point groups. These counts refer to ordinary, nonmagnetic periodic space groups; magnetic and aperiodic generalizations are different classifications.[ref]
For concrete comparisons, a monatomic simple-cubic crystal has space group Pm3ˉm and point group m3ˉm. Rock salt has a face-centred cubic lattice, a two-species basis and symmorphic space group Fm3ˉm. Diamond has the same cubic point group and translation-lattice type, but its basis leads to nonsymmorphic Fd3ˉm. Replacing the two diamond basis sites by distinct species gives the zinc blende structure, exemplified by GaAs, with F4ˉ3m and point group 4ˉ3m; inversion that would interchange the species is no longer a symmetry. The lattice specifies translations, the point group specifies the linear symmetry classes, and the space group specifies their complete spatial implementation. These examples show why none alone uniquely describes an atomic structure.

Connect the island weakly to source and drain reservoirs through tunnel barriers. sequential tunnelling is suppressed in a charge-stable region when neither adding nor removing an electron is energetically accessible. Sweeping the gate brings a charge electrochemical potential through the reservoir Fermi level, giving a Coulomb-blockade conductance peak. The gate lever arm is αg​=Cg​/CΣ​, so ΔVg​=Eadd​/(eαg​) locally if the capacitances and level spectrum do not vary appreciably. At finite source-drain bias, alignment with either reservoir defines the boundaries of a Coulomb diamond. A clear charging spectrum requires kB​T and tunnel broadening ℏΓ smaller than the relevant addition energy; resolving individual orbital levels additionally requires them smaller than the orbital spacing. An experimental check therefore combines gate sweeps with bias spectroscopy, temperature dependence and barrier tuning to separate level structure from broadening or uncontrolled charge traps.

Reproducing enhanced addition energies at electron numbers 2 and 6 in several devices strengthened the shell assignment. The electron temperature was independently estimated above the refrigerator base temperature, an essential distinction when judging spectral resolution. Field-dependent reordering also revealed exchange effects beyond a purely spin-degenerate orbital model.[ref]

Use a three-dimensional electron gas with a uniform positive background, the jellium model. The Hartree-Fock method minimizes the expectation of the interacting Hamiltonian over normalized Slater determinants. Antisymmetry is exact within that trial class, but correlations requiring a superposition of determinants are omitted. For orthonormal occupied spin orbitals ϕi​, the energy is
EHF​=∑i​⟨i∣h0​∣i⟩+21​∑ij​(Jij​−Kij​)+Ebackground​,
(25)
where Jij​ is the direct Coulomb integral and Kij​ the exchange integral; the spin overlap makes exchange vanish between opposite-spin orbitals. The self-interaction terms Jii​ and Kii​ cancel. Variation gives a self-consistent Fock operator f^​=h0​+∑j​(J^j​−K^j​); exchange is nonlocal. Its eigenfunctions define occupied orbitals of a stationary determinant, and the determinant must be compared with competing self-consistent solutions to select a variational ground state.
The eigenvalues are orbital Lagrange multipliers. Their sum counts each interaction twice:
EHF​=∑i∈occ​ϵi​−21​∑ij​(Jij​−Kij​)+Ebackground​.
(26)
Frozen-orbital removal energies may be estimated using Koopmans' theorem, but measured quasiparticle energies can contain relaxation and correlations absent from this approximation. Unoccupied orbital energies are not automatically an exact excitation spectrum. In particular, unscreened exchange in a uniform three-dimensional gas produces an unphysical logarithmic slope singularity at its Fermi surface; a one-determinant mean field does not reproduce the interacting Fermi liquid's screened response.
For a spatially uniform plane-wave determinant, electron-background and background-background terms cancel the uniform direct Coulomb contribution. Retain the background when making this cancellation: an isolated infinite electron charge distribution would have a divergent direct energy. The surviving contributions per particle are kinetic energy and negative same-spin exchange energy. Let (3/(4πn))1/3=rs​aB∗​ define the Dimensionless Wigner-Seitz radius, with aB∗​=4πεℏ2/(m∗e2), EH∗​=ℏ2/(m∗aB∗2​) and ζ=(n↑​−n↓​)/n. Then
NEH∗​EHF​(rs​,ζ)​=10rs2​3(9π/4)2/3​2(1+ζ)5/3+(1−ζ)5/3​−4πrs​3(9π/4)1/3​2(1+ζ)4/3+(1−ζ)4/3​.
(27)
For the unpolarized state this is approximately 1.105/rs2​−0.4582/rs​. The kinetic contribution penalizes spin polarization because it enlarges an occupied Fermi sphere; exchange favors polarization because equal-spin occupation gains more exchange energy. As density falls, the rs−1​ exchange scale overtakes the rs−2​ kinetic scale. The uniform fully polarized and unpolarized energies cross at
rs​=[3(9π/4)1/3/(4π)](21/3−1)[3(9π/4)2/3/10](22/3−1)​≃5.45.
(28)
This is a variational comparison within the homogeneous Hartree-Fock electron gas, not an exact physical ferromagnetic phase boundary. It demonstrates how Pauli kinetic energy and exchange compete and why the allowed trial states matter.
Allowing spatial symmetry breaking changes the result: the Overhauser instability lowers the paramagnetic determinant's Hartree-Fock energy through a spin density wave, even in the high-density limit. A calculation restricted to uniform Fermi spheres therefore does not give the unrestricted Hartree-Fock ground state.[ref] At still lower density, localization and a Wigner crystal compete with fluid states. electron-gas correlation energy beyond a single determinant is important in assessing their competition. Quantum Monte Carlo provides a concrete route to comparing correlated fluid and crystal energies; the original stochastic calculation demonstrates why exchange-only energy differences are insufficient.[ref]
Hartree-Fock is a useful variational baseline with exact exchange, not a controlled complete low-density phase diagram. At high density the kinetic scale dominates, but even a small symmetry-breaking energy gain can invalidate a restricted determinant as the exact Hartree-Fock minimizer. At low density neglected correlations, screening and localization can change both transition locations and the ordering of candidate phases. A numerical phase boundary should therefore state dimension, spin constraints, background model and whether correlation and spatially modulated states were included.
