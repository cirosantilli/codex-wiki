For a finite-range isotropic Heisenberg model, the Mermin-Wagner theorem excludes spontaneous magnetic order in one dimension at every nonzero temperature. Long-wavelength rotations cost very little energy: a smooth orientational fluctuation has δF=(ρs​/2)∫dx(∂x​θ)2, so its thermal variance contains (kB​T/ρs​)∫dq/q2, which diverges in the infrared. In a Heisenberg ferromagnet, the same obstruction appears as a divergent thermal occupation of quadratic long-wave spin waves. This explains why a finite magnetization cannot be supported, although correlations can be strong over a long finite distance. The rigorous statement requires short-range interactions and the stated continuous symmetry; interchain coupling and anisotropy change the problem. Symmetry forcing the mean to zero in a finite chain alone would not prove absence of thermodynamic order. [ref]
For the Ising model, use dimensionless si​=2Siz​=±1, h=gμB​B, K=βJ/2 and b=βh/2. Its pair Boltzmann factor is
Ps,s′​=exp[Kss′+2b​(s+s′)].
(1)
Multiplying N such factors around the periodic ring counts each bond once and each field term twice with weight one half. Summing the closed spin-index sequence therefore gives the transfer matrix for the one-dimensional Ising model
P=(eK+be−K​e−KeK−b​),Z=TrPN.​
(2)
The two eigenvalues are
λ±​=eK[coshb±sinh2b+e−4K​],Z=λ+N​+λ−N​.
(3)
At finite temperature every entry of P is positive, so the Perron–Frobenius theorem gives λ+​>∣λ−​∣. Consequently
F=−2NJ​−NkB​Tln[coshb+sinh2b+e−2βJ​]+o(N).​
(4)
The exact finite-N correction to the extensive expression is −kB​Tln[1+(λ−​/λ+​)N]. No assumption that λ−​ is positive is needed.
Differentiate at fixed temperature. For D=sinh2b+e−2βJ​, ∂b​ln(coshb+D)=sinhb/D. The Ising-chain magnetization with spin-half normalization gives total magnetic moment
M=−∂B∂F​=2NgμB​​sinh2(βgμB​B/2)+e−2βJ​sinh(βgμB​B/2)​.​
(5)
This is the thermodynamic-limit result; the moment per site is M/N. For every finite T>0, limB→0​M/N=0, even with the thermodynamic limit taken first. The zero-field magnetic susceptibility per site is (gμB​)2eβJ/(4kB​T), large but finite.
The Ising-chain domain-wall energy convention explains the result for J>0: changing a parallel bond into an antiparallel bond costs J in this spin-half normalization. Domain walls, occurring in pairs on a ring, acquire a nonzero density at any nonzero temperature. Indeed the zero-field correlation length is ξ=−a/ln[tanh(βJ/2)]∼(a/2)eβJ at low temperature. The Ising symmetry is discrete, so this is a separate one-dimensional domain-wall mechanism rather than an application of the continuous-symmetry theorem. At zero temperature a ferromagnetic chain can select a fully polarized state; the B→0 and T→0 limits do not commute.
