Thermal equilibrium

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 11
Two internally equilibrated systems are in thermal equilibrium if bringing them into contact that permits only heat exchange causes no net heat transfer. In ordinary nonrelativistic thermodynamics without appreciable gravitational temperature shifts, their thermodynamic temperatures are equal. Merely preventing heat transfer with insulation does not establish thermal equilibrium: two insulated bodies can have different temperatures. [ref]
A thermometer illustrates the definition. Its own temperature settles to the object's temperature after thermal contact, provided its effect on the object is negligible. The existing zeroth law of thermodynamics makes this comparison transitive: two objects separately in thermal equilibrium with the same thermometer are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This lets one calibrated scale compare different materials.
Equal temperatures do not imply equal internal energies or equal heat capacities. Nor does thermal equilibrium by itself imply mechanical equilibrium or chemical equilibrium; these require additional allowed exchanges. There is also a distinction between no net exchange in thermal equilibrium and a constant temperature maintained by equal incoming and outgoing heat. The latter can be a steady state with ongoing entropy production. Microscopic exchanges can continue at thermal equilibrium, but their opposing contributions balance on average.
Historically, OpenStax credits the name “zeroth law” to Ralph Fowler in the 1930s: the already numbered laws needed a logically earlier statement about thermal equilibrium. [ref]

Thermostat

Words: 43
Thermal equilibrium OurBigBook.com
A thermostat senses temperature and adjusts a heating or cooling actuator to approach a reference temperature. For precise regulation, sensor placement, thermal delay and feedback stability matter as well as the nominal reference. Thermal shielding can reduce rapid environmental disturbances while feedback corrects slower ones.

Thermal equilibration of two finite bodies (Tf​=(C1​T1,0​+C2​T2,0​)/(C1​+C2​))

Words: 267
Thermal equilibrium OurBigBook.com
Consider two rigid bodies forming an isolated system, with fixed composition and constant whole-body heat capacity at constant volume values C1​=1kJK−1 and C2​=3kJK−1. Their initial temperatures are T1,0​=300K and T2,0​=400K. Assume each body remains internally uniform, their contact stores negligible energy, no phase transition occurs and no work is done or extracted. These are stipulated model values, not material measurements.
Because dUi​=Ci​dTi​, write Ui​=ai​+Ci​Ti​ with arbitrary fixed reference constants ai​. The conserved relative internal energy is
E=U1​+U2​−a1​−a2​=C1​T1​+C2​T2​=1500kJ.
(1)
This is an energy reference convention, not a claim about actual heat capacities down to zero temperature. At thermal equilibrium the two temperatures equal Tf​, so the first law of thermodynamics gives
Tf​=C1​+C2​C1​T1,0​+C2​T2,0​​=375K,ΔU1​=75kJ,ΔU2​=−75kJ.
(2)
The larger heat capacity pulls the common temperature closer to its initial value: body 1 warms by 75K, while body 2 cools by 25K.
To predict a timescale, stipulate a constant contact conductance G=1/Rth​=0.1kJs−1K−1, the reciprocal of the contact's thermal resistance. The rate of heat transfer into body 1 is G(T2​−T1​), giving
C1​T˙1​=G(T2​−T1​),C2​T˙2​=−G(T2​−T1​).
(3)
Adding proves conservation of E. Subtracting gives exponential decay of the temperature difference, with time constant
τ=G(C1​+C2​)C1​C2​​=7.5s,T2​−T1​=100Ke−t/τ,
(4)
and therefore
T1​(t)=375K−75Ke−t/τ,T2​(t)=375K+25Ke−t/τ.
(5)
Exact equality is approached as t→∞ in this ideal model. Changing G changes the rate but not Tf​: equilibrium and the speed of reaching it are separate predictions. Real bodies can have internal temperature gradients, changing heat capacities and contact losses, so the plotted 7.5s is an assumed model timescale.

Tolman–Ehrenfest relation (T−k2​=constant)

Words: 47
Thermal equilibrium OurBigBook.com
In static thermal equilibrium, the local temperature times the stationary lapse is constant. Energy measured locally redshifts to conserved Killing energy by that same lapse; requiring a single equilibrium Boltzmann exponent gives the relation. The Killing vector field normalization fixes the constant but not the ratio of temperatures at different locations.

Boltzmann suppression (e−m/T)

Words: 103 Articles: 1
Thermal equilibrium OurBigBook.com
Boltzmann suppression is the exponential reduction of an equilibrium population when creating it costs energy much greater than the temperature. For a nonrelativistic species with negligible chemical potential, the leading factor is e−m/T, multiplied by its momentum phase-space volume. It concerns the equilibrium abundance; freeze-out can preserve a larger population than the instantaneous equilibrium value at later times.

Boltzmann suppression of a nonrelativistic relic (neq​≃g(mT/2π)3/2e−m/T)

Words: 47
Boltzmann suppression OurBigBook.com
A nonrelativistic species with g states and negligible chemical potential has equilibrium density g(mT/(2π))3/2e−m/T. If particle-antiparticle annihilation maintains equilibrium after the species becomes massive, its eventual freeze-out population can be much smaller than a relativistically decoupled population. The nonrelativistic annihilation rate and any asymmetry determine the final abundance.

Kinetic equilibrium

Words: 38
Thermal equilibrium OurBigBook.com
Momentum-exchange collisions maintain a common temperature and an equilibrium-shaped momentum probability distribution. Particle abundances and chemical potentials need not have their chemical equilibrium values: elastic scattering alone conserves particle number. Thus kinetic equilibrium does not by itself imply either zero chemical potential or conserved comoving entropy.

Heat bath

Words: 1k Articles: 3
Thermal equilibrium OurBigBook.com
A heat bath, or thermal reservoir, is an idealized system so large that exchanging a finite amount of heat does not appreciably change its temperature.
The ideal heat bath supplies a fixed absolute temperature Tb​ while its internal energy changes through heat transfer. A real body's suitability as a heat bath depends on its heat capacity relative to the exchange being studied, and on how quickly its internal temperature gradients disappear. Large physical size alone supplies neither condition. A finite heat bath makes the approximation quantitative; a regulated calibration bath instead uses feedback control to maintain its temperature by exchanging energy with external equipment.
For a weakly coupled system that exchanges energy but no matter with an ideal heat bath, the canonical ensemble assigns the Boltzmann distribution
pn​=Ze−En​/(kB​Tb​)​,Z=∑m​e−Em​/(kB​Tb​).
(6)
Here En​ is a microstate's energy, kB​ the Boltzmann constant and Z the partition function. The fixed temperature removes the need to track every exchange with the environment. A substantially changing bath temperature no longer gives this one fixed Boltzmann distribution.[ref]

Finite heat bath (ΔTb​=Qb​/Cb​)

Words: 777 Articles: 1
Heat bath OurBigBook.com
A finite heat bath has a finite whole-body heat capacity Cb​ and therefore changes temperature when it absorbs heat Qb​. For a rigid closed system with no work, fixed composition and no phase transition, the first law of thermodynamics gives
Qb​=ΔUb​=∫Tb,0​Tb,f​​Cb​(T)dT.
(7)
If the heat capacity at constant volume Cb​ is constant throughout this temperature interval, the integral becomes Qb​=Cb​(Tb,f​−Tb,0​). This statement concerns an internal energy difference; Cb​Tb​ need not be the body's absolute internal energy.
For an original example, put a rigid body 1 and a finite bath 2 into thermal contact inside an isolated system. Stipulate constant whole-body heat capacities C1​=100JK−1 and C2​=900JK−1, with initial temperatures T1,0​=350K and T2,0​=290K. Each body remains internally uniform; the connection stores negligible energy; no external work, matter exchange or phase transition occurs. These are constructed model values. The argument in thermal equilibration of two finite bodies applies directly: equal final temperatures and conserved total internal energy give
C1​(Tf​−T1,0​)+C2​(Tf​−T2,0​)=0,Tf​=C1​+C2​C1​T1,0​+C2​T2,0​​=296K.
(8)
Thus the heat Q transferred from body 1 to bath 2 is
Q=C1​(350−296)K=C2​(296−290)K=5400J.
(9)
Body 1 loses 5400J of internal energy and bath 2 gains exactly that amount. Body 1 cools by 54K, while bath 2 warms by 6K. Equal heat transfers produce unequal temperature changes because the heat capacities differ.
The heat bath approximation can now be assessed against a chosen allowed temperature change δT>0. Constant Cb​ requires ∣Qb​∣/Cb​≤δT, rather than merely calling the bath large. For this example, an allowed change of 0.1K would fail because the computed change is 6K. The exact bath shift and its ideal-reservoir limit are
Tf​−T2,0​=C1​+C2​C1​​(T1,0​−T2,0​),limC2​→∞​Tf​=T2,0​=290K.
(10)
Hold C1​ and both initial temperatures fixed in this limit. The transferred heat then approaches C1​(T1,0​−T2,0​)=6000J. The finite bath receives less heat because its warming reduces the available temperature difference. For heat capacities that vary, use the two integrals in the energy balance instead of this weighted average.
Conservation of energy also permits imagining the reverse transfer, so it cannot explain the direction of equilibration by itself. Entropy from heat capacity gives the additional test, using reversible comparison paths between equilibrium endpoints:
ΔS1​=C1​ln350296​≃−16.75737JK−1,ΔS2​=C2​ln290296​≃18.43068JK−1,
(11)
ΔStot​=C1​lnT1,0​Tf​​+C2​lnT2,0​Tf​​≃1.673308JK−1>0.
(12)
All ratios inside the natural logarithms use absolute temperatures. The actual exchange across unequal temperatures is irreversible, even though reversible paths calculate the endpoint entropy differences.
There is a short general proof of positivity. Put wi​=Ci​/(C1​+C2​) for positive constant heat capacities and positive initial temperatures. The strict concavity of the natural logarithm gives
C1​+C2​ΔStot​​=ln(w1​T1,0​+w2​T2,0​)−w1​lnT1,0​−w2​lnT2,0​≥0,
(13)
with equality only for equal initial temperatures. In this proof all temperatures inside the logarithms mean their numerical values in one common absolute unit. This is Jensen's inequality applied to the same weighted average that conservation of energy selected. The direction of heat transfer and the stability of its endpoint also follow from entropy maximization under thermal contact.

Heat bath relaxation with finite capacity (τ=C1​C2​/[G(C1​+C2​)])

Words: 301
Finite heat bath OurBigBook.com
The equilibrium calculation determines where the two temperatures end, without determining how long they take. Stipulate a constant thermal conductance G>0 and the linear heat transfer law underlying Newton's law of cooling. With Q positive from body 1 to bath 2, the ordinary differential equations are
Q˙​=G(T1​−T2​),C1​T˙1​=−G(T1​−T2​),C2​T˙2​=G(T1​−T2​).
(14)
The dot denotes a derivative with respect to time. This model assumes internal equilibration is fast compared with exchange between the bodies. Adding the two energy rates gives zero; subtracting the temperature rates gives
dtd​(T1​−T2​)=−G(C1​1​+C2​1​)(T1​−T2​),τ=G(C1​+C2​)C1​C2​​.
(15)
The temperature difference has exponential decay with time constant τ. For the finite heat bath example and the stipulated G=2WK−1=2Js−1K−1, τ=45s, and solving the ordinary differential equations gives
T1​(t)=296K+54Ke−t/(45s),T2​(t)=296K−6Ke−t/(45s),
(16)
Q(t)=5400J(1−e−t/(45s)).
(17)
At one time constant, T1​≃315.865K, T2​≃293.793K and Q≃3413.451J. Equal temperatures are approached as a limit at infinite time. The finite bath causes the temperature difference to shrink faster than the fixed-bath model, because both bodies change temperature.
For an ideal heat bath with the same G, set T2​=Tb​=290K rather than evolving its temperature. Newton's law of cooling then yields
T1​(t)=290K+60Ke−t/(50s),τ∞​=C1​/G=50s.
(18)
Changing G changes these time constants, while the final temperature is fixed by the capacities and initial conditions. The dynamical model independently checks the Second law of thermodynamics:
S˙tot​=T1​−Q˙​​+T2​Q˙​​=T1​T2​G(T1​−T2​)2​≥0.
(19)
Its integral over the relaxation reproduces the endpoint entropy change. The useful separation is between capacity, which governs how much the bath temperature shifts, and thermal conductance, which governs the exchange rate. Neither alone establishes a reliable heat bath.

Regulated calibration bath

Words: 229
Heat bath OurBigBook.com
A regulated bath holds its temperature near a chosen setting through feedback control, while stirring reduces internal temperature gradients. Its finite heat capacity buffers disturbances; external heating and cooling replace the ideal heat bath's unlimited capacity. This is useful for thermometer calibration and for maintaining a blackbody calibration source at a known temperature.
Joel B. Fowler's A Third Generation Water Bath Based Blackbody Source reports a NIST test of a stirred water bath containing a copper blackbody cavity. A bottom plate supplied heating and cooling; a double stirrer circulated water. Electronic feedback control regulated heating. An electronic thermometer with a thermistor probe provided the readout. The preparation was to approach a selected setting, wait for stabilization, adjust against the measured temperature and then log continuously.[ref]
To test spatial uniformity, one thermistor stayed near the cavity tip while another visited ten locations at three heights. The simultaneous reference distinguished spatial differences from temporal drift. An insulated dome reduced thermal conduction along probe casings. Table 1 reports nonzero departures at several settings: circulation did not make every location identical. The measurement uncertainty analysis separately included uniformity, stability, setting, immersion and readout contributions. These tests supported a bounded temperature assignment around the cavity, rather than infinite heat capacity or perfect thermal equilibrium throughout the apparatus.[ref]

Entropy maximization under thermal contact (∂Stot​/∂E1​=1/T1​−1/T2​)

Words: 340
Thermal equilibrium OurBigBook.com
At fixed volumes and material inventories, two internally equilibrated bodies that exchange only heat within an isolated system have fixed total internal energy E=E1​+E2​. Their total entropy is
Stot​(E1​)=S1​(E1​)+S2​(E−E1​).
(20)
Using 1/Ti​=(∂Si​/∂Ei​)Vi​,Ni​​, an interior stationary point obeys
dE1​dStot​​=T1​1​−T2​1​=0.
(21)
Thus entropy stationarity requires matching thermodynamic temperatures. Positive values of heat capacity at constant volume make this a strict local maximum because
dE12​d2Stot​​=−C1​T12​1​−C2​T22​1​<0.
(22)
For bodies with positive heat capacities throughout the accessible range, this concavity gives at most one interior maximum. This is the stability check behind the equal-temperature condition; see entropy concavity and positive heat capacity. [ref]
For thermal equilibration of two finite bodies, entropy from heat capacity gives
ΔStot​=C1​lnT1,0​Tf​​+C2​lnT2,0​Tf​​=(ln300375​+3ln400375​)kJK−1≃0.02952799kJK−1>0.
(23)
Body 1 gains about 0.22314355kJK−1 of entropy, while body 2 loses about 0.19361556kJK−1. Total energy is conserved even though total entropy increases. The entropy calculation uses reversible comparison paths between the endpoint states; actual exchange across a finite temperature difference is irreversible.
To construct the plot, vary T1​ while enforcing the energy constraint, rather than holding T2​ fixed. In numerical units of kelvin, T2​/K=(1500−T1​/K)/3, and the plotted function is
S(T1​)=C1​ln300KT1​​+C2​ln400KT2​(T1​)​,dT1​dT2​​=−C2​C1​​.
(24)
Here S means Stot​−Stot,initial​, so its zero is a reference choice. The chain rule yields
S′(T1​)=T1​C1​​−T2​C1​​,S′′(T1​)=−T12​C1​​−C2​T22​C12​​<0.
(25)
The unique maximum is at T1​=T2​=375K. The physical trajectory starts at T1​=300K and moves toward that maximum; the rest of the curve compares other possible energy partitions, not later times in the trajectory.
The contact model also proves the direction of change directly:
S˙tot​=G(T2​−T1​)(T1​1​−T2​1​)=T1​T2​G(T2​−T1​)2​≥0.
(26)
For G>0 and positive temperatures, equality holds exactly at thermal equilibrium. The appealing link is that a dynamical heat transfer law moves the system uphill on the constrained entropy curve, while conservation of energy keeps it on that curve. This predicts both why the endpoint is stable and why the reverse separation into hot and cold bodies cannot happen spontaneously in the isolated system.
