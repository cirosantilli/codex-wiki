Fisher-Neyman factorization theorem

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Sufficient statistic
OurBigBook.com Words: 228
Assume the full-data sample space and the range of the statistic T are standard Borel spaces, T is a measurable function, and the statistical model {Pθ​} is dominated by one common sigma-finite measure ν. Write fθ​=dPθ​/dν for its densities. Then T is a sufficient statistic if and only if there exist nonnegative measurable functions gθ​ and h, with h independent of θ, such that
fθ​(x)=gθ​(T(x))h(x)ν-almost everywhere for each θ.
(68)
The statistical parameter can enter gθ​ only through θ and the observed T(x). [ref]
For discrete data, take counting measure; for ordinary continuous data, take Lebesgue measure. Independence, identical sampling laws, smooth dependence on θ, and a fixed positive support of a probability distribution are not requirements of this theorem. Parameter-dependent support must nevertheless be included in fθ​, usually through an indicator function, and must fit the displayed factorization. The sufficient statistic for a symmetric uniform sample illustrates exactly this: its indicator function depends on the unknown scale through the observed absolute maximum. The null sets in the theorem concern versions of densities; the factors are not generally themselves normalized probability distributions.
For a discrete sample space, the cancellation behind the theorem is particularly transparent. On a group with positive probability, factorization gives
Pθ​(X=x∣T=t)=∑z:T(z)=t​h(z)h(x)1{T(x)=t}​​.
(69)
Conversely, multiply a common conditional probability mass function by Pθ​(T=T(x)) to obtain the required factorization. For continuous data, a sum over a group is not a density integral over the original Lebesgue measure: the group may have measure zero. Use the regular conditional distribution formulation and the Fisher-Neyman factorization theorem instead of that discrete calculation.
