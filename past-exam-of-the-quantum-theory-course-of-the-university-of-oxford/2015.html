The time-independent Schrödinger equation is the energy eigenvalue equation for the Hamiltonian operator:
−2mℏ2​dx2d2ψ​+V(x)ψ(x)=Eψ(x).​
(1)
Here ℏ is the reduced Planck constant. A corresponding stationary wavefunction evolves as Ψ(x,t)=ψ(x)e−iEt/ℏ, which follows by substitution in the Time-dependent Schrödinger equation. Its position probability density is time independent because the time factor has unit modulus. Physical energy eigenstates must also satisfy the system's boundary conditions; for a bound state, wavefunction normalization requires ∫−∞∞​∣ψ(x)∣2dx=1.

ii OurBigBook.com
Define the reflected function ϕ(x)=ψ(−x). The chain rule gives ϕ′(x)=−ψ′(−x) and ϕ′′(x)=ψ′′(−x). Evaluating the time-independent Schrödinger equation for ψ at −x and using the even function identity V(−x)=V(x) gives
−2mℏ2​ϕ′′(x)+V(x)ϕ(x)=Eϕ(x).
(2)
Thus reflection preserves the energy eigenvalue. The same substitution y=−x in the norm gives
∫−∞∞​∣ϕ(x)∣2dx=∫−∞∞​∣ψ(−x)∣2dx=∫−∞∞​∣ψ(y)∣2dy=1.​
(3)
This is the action of the parity operator Rψ(x)=ψ(−x), which is a unitary operator. For a reflection-symmetric physical problem it preserves the Hamiltonian operator's operator domain as well as its differential expression.

iii OurBigBook.com
By linearity of the time-independent Schrödinger equation, the even and odd parts
ψ+​(x)=2ψ(x)+ψ(−x)​,ψ−​(x)=2ψ(x)−ψ(−x)​
(4)
each satisfy the same energy eigenvalue equation whenever they are nonzero. They obey Rψ+​=ψ+​ and Rψ−​=−ψ−​, so they have definite parity. At least one is nonzero, and ψ=ψ+​+ψ−​. Each nonzero part can be normalized separately.
Therefore a basis of stationary solutions can be chosen to have definite parity, and solving the even and odd sectors loses no energy eigenvalues. This does not assert that every possible quantum superposition has definite parity; a superposition of the two sectors need not. For one-dimensional bound states in the usual regular setting, nondegeneracy of one-dimensional bound states further implies that each individual energy eigenstate has definite parity.

In either exterior region of the finite square well, the time-independent Schrödinger equation reduces to ψ′′=k2ψ, since E<0. Its general solution is a linear combination of ekx and e−kx. On the left, e−kx grows as x→−∞ and cannot be a square-integrable function, so its coefficient must be zero. On the right, the excluded growing term is ekx. Hence the surviving tails are Aekx for x≤−a and De−kx for x≥a.
Inside the well, E+V0​>0 and the equation becomes ψ′′=−κ2ψ, whose general solution is Bcosκx+Csinκx. Thus
ψ(x)=⎩⎨⎧​Aekx,Bcosκx+Csinκx,De−kx,​x≤−a,−a<x<a,x≥a.​​
(5)
The coefficients are initially independent; wavefunction matching at a finite potential step imposes the remaining relations. Here the paper's k is the exterior decay constant and κ is the interior oscillation constant.

At a finite jump in potential energy, the wavefunction and its first derivative must be continuous. A jump in the derivative would create a Dirac delta function in ψ′′, for which the finite potential has no balancing term. For the even solution, matching at x=a gives
Bcosβ=De−α,−Bκsinβ=−kDe−α,α=ka,β=κa.
(7)
A nonzero solution cannot have B=0. It also cannot have cosβ=0: the first condition would give D=0, while the second would force Bκsinβ=0, a contradiction. Division is therefore valid and yields
α=βtanβ.​
(8)
Parity makes the conditions at x=−a identical to those already imposed. Finally the definitions of the decay and oscillation constants give
α2+β2=a2(−ℏ22mE​+ℏ22m(E+V0​)​)=ℏ22mV0​a2​.​
(9)
The circle relation fixes the well strength; the tangent relation selects its even bound states.

Write ρ=a2mV0​​/ℏ>0. On the first tangent branch, 0<β<π/2, the matching relations are equivalent to

The position operator X and momentum operator P are self-adjoint operators, so the adjoint operator is
a+​=P+imωX.​
(15)
Use the canonical commutation relation with the order of factors preserved:
a+​a−​a−​a+​​=(P+imωX)(P−imωX)=P2+m2ω2X2+imω(XP−PX)=P2+m2ω2X2−mℏω=2m(H−21​ℏω)​,=P2+m2ω2X2−imω(XP−PX)=P2+m2ω2X2+mℏω=2m(H+21​ℏω)​.​
(16)

(17)
These creation and annihilation operators are unscaled and carry the paper's phase convention; their commutator is not the unit-normalized oscillator commutator.

The squared norm is obtained by moving the adjoint operator to the other side of the inner product:
∥a−​ψ∥2=⟨ψ,a+​a−​ψ⟩=2m⟨ψ,(H−21​ℏω)ψ⟩.
(20)
For an energy eigenstate, this is
∥a−​ψ∥2=2m(E−21​ℏω)∥ψ∥2.​
(21)
Since a squared norm is nonnegative, every nonzero energy eigenstate has E≥ℏω/2. Moreover a−​ψ=0 exactly when E=ℏω/2.

Write E0​=ℏω/2. Repeated application of the annihilation operator lowers the energy eigenvalue in steps of ℏω. The lower bound from part (ii) forces this process to stop. To see why it also quantizes the starting energy, write
ℏωE−E0​​=n+δ,n∈{0,1,2,…},0≤δ<1.
(22)
The first n lowering steps have positive squared norms. If δ>0, the next step is also nonzero by part (ii), and its energy would be E0​+(δ−1)ℏω<E0​, contradicting the bound. Thus δ=0, and
En​=(n+21​)ℏω,n=0,1,2,….​
(23)
Existence of the ground state can also be checked directly in the position representation. The equation a−​ψ0​=0 is
−iℏψ0′​−imωxψ0​=0,
(24)
whose solution is proportional to e−mωx2/(2ℏ). This Gaussian function is square-integrable, so it gives an actual ground state. Its normalization can be chosen using the Gaussian integral.
All the displayed levels occur: for a nonzero state at En​,
∥a+​ψn​∥2=⟨ψn​,a−​a+​ψn​⟩=2mℏω(n+1)∥ψn​∥2>0.
(25)
Thus repeated raising creates a nonzero energy eigenstate at every En​.

Let ψ have energy En​ and put γ=2mℏω. The lowering norm formula gives, inductively,
∥(a−​)jψ∥2=γj(n−j)!n!​∥ψ∥2,0≤j≤n.
(26)
In particular (a−​)nψ is a nonzero ground state. By the assumed nondegeneracy, it equals cψ0​ for some c=0.
It remains to show that raising this vector recovers ψ, rather than merely another state at the same energy. On an energy eigenstate at En​, factorization gives a+​a−​ψ=γnψ. Iterating the factorization down the ladder yields
(a+​)n(a−​)nψ=γnn!ψ.
(27)
Applying (a+​)n to (a−​)nψ=cψ0​ therefore proves
ψ=(2mℏω)nn!c​(a+​)nψ0​.​
(28)
For n=0 this is the original ground-state assumption. This proves nondegeneracy propagated by an oscillator ladder: every energy eigenspace is one-dimensional if the ground state is nondegenerate.

If a−​ψ=0, it has energy E−ℏω, distinct from the energy of ψ. Eigenstates of a self-adjoint operator with distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. Explicitly,
E⟨ψ,a−​ψ⟩=⟨Hψ,a−​ψ⟩=⟨ψ,H(a−​ψ)⟩=(E−ℏω)⟨ψ,a−​ψ⟩,
(29)
so ⟨ψ,a−​ψ⟩=0. If a−​ψ=0, the same conclusion is immediate.
Dividing by ∥ψ∥2 and using the definition of a−​ gives
Eψ​(P)−imωEψ​(X)=0.
(30)
Both expectation values are real because P and X are self-adjoint operators. Its real and imaginary parts therefore vanish separately:
Eψ​(X)=0,Eψ​(P)=0.​
(31)
An oscillator energy eigenstate is centered in both position and momentum, despite having nonzero quantum uncertainties.

Twice applying the creation operator or twice applying the annihilation operator changes the energy eigenvalue by 2ℏω, unless they give zero. The same orthogonality argument as in part (i) therefore gives
Eψ​(a+2​)=0=Eψ​(a−2​).
(32)
Also the factorization in part (a) gives
Eψ​(a+​a−​)=2m(E−ℏω/2),Eψ​(a−​a+​)=2m(E+ℏω/2).
(33)
Now P=(a+​+a−​)/2 and X=(a+​−a−​)/(2imω). Squaring in the correct operator order yields
Eψ​(P2)Eψ​(X2)​=41​Eψ​(a+2​+a+​a−​+a−​a+​+a−2​)=mE,=−4m2ω21​Eψ​(a+2​−a+​a−​−a−​a+​+a−2​)=mω2E​.​
(34)
By part (i), the means are zero, so these are the variances. Taking positive square roots gives the requested standard deviations:
Δψ​(X)=mω2E​​,Δψ​(P)=mE​.​
(35)
These harmonic-oscillator number-state second moments also show equal mean kinetic energy and potential energy, each E/2. Their uncertainty product is E/ω=(n+21​)ℏ, so the ground state saturates the Heisenberg uncertainty relation.

Introduce the reduced radial wavefunction u(r)=rψ(r). Multiplying the radial Schrödinger equation for a central potential by −2mr/ℏ2 gives
u′′−r2ℓ(ℓ+1)​u+ℏ2r2mKZ​u+ℏ22mE​u=0.
(36)
For the decaying bound state, take α>0 and put u=fe−αr. Differentiation yields
u′′=e−αr(f′′−2αf′+α2f).
(37)
Since 2mE/ℏ2=−α2, the two constant multiples of f cancel. Dividing by the nonzero exponential gives
f′′−2αf′−r2ℓ(ℓ+1)​f+ℏ2r2mKZ​f=0.​
(38)
This separates the large-radius exponential decay from the remaining Coulomb bound state equation.

ii OurBigBook.com
Apply the Frobenius method at the origin. The lowest power in f′′−ℓ(ℓ+1)f/r2 is rc−2, with coefficient
[c(c−1)−ℓ(ℓ+1)]a0​.
(39)
The terms −2αf′ and 2mKZf/(ℏ2r) start at power rc−1 and therefore cannot cancel a nonzero rc−2 coefficient. Because a0​=0, the indicial equation is
c(c−1)−ℓ(ℓ+1)=0,(c−ℓ−1)(c+ℓ)=0.​
(40)
Its roots are c=ℓ+1 and c=−ℓ. Since ψ=r−1fe−αr, their leading behaviors are respectively ψ∼rℓ and ψ∼r−ℓ−1. The physical choice is
c=ℓ+1.​
(41)
This is the Coulomb radial origin condition. For ℓ≥1, the other branch is not even locally normalizable: its radial probability integral behaves as ∫0ε​r2r−2ℓ−2dr=∫0ε​r−2ℓdr, which diverges.
For ℓ=0, the singular behavior 1/r is locally square-integrable with the measure r2dr, so local wavefunction normalization alone would not reject it. The ordinary three-dimensional Coulomb potential problem additionally requires the regular boundary condition u(0)=0. A 1/r branch violates that condition and has divergent kinetic energy integral ∫0ε​r2∣∂r​(1/r)∣2dr=∫0ε​r−2dr. Thus it is excluded from the physical energy domain as well. The regular choice gives u∼rℓ+1 in every case.

For brevity write γ=mKZ/ℏ2. With the physical indicial root c=ℓ+1, substitute f=∑n≥0​an​rn+ℓ+1 into the equation from part (i). The coefficient of rn+ℓ is
[(n+ℓ+2)(n+ℓ+1)−ℓ(ℓ+1)]an+1​+[2γ−2α(n+ℓ+1)]an​=0.
(42)
The indicial equation has already canceled the still lower power rℓ−1. Rearranging gives the recurrence relation
an+1​=2an​(n+ℓ+2)(n+ℓ+1)−ℓ(ℓ+1)α(n+ℓ+1)−mKZ/ℏ2​.​
(43)
Its denominator is (n+1)(n+2ℓ+2)>0 for every n≥0.
To impose wavefunction normalization at infinity, first suppose that the series never terminates. Then
an​an+1​​∼n2α​,n→∞.
(44)
This is the exponential-series growth associated with e2αr, which would overwhelm the factor e−αr in ψ. A direct lower-bound argument makes the conclusion precise. All sufficiently late recurrence ratios are real and positive, so after a common global phase choice the tail coefficients have one sign. For any 0<η<α, there is an index N such that
∣an​∣∣an+1​∣​≥n+12α−η​,n≥N.
(45)
Induction then bounds the tail below by a positive multiple of

Since the denominator never vanishes, termination requires the numerator to vanish at some nonnegative integer n:
α=(n+ℓ+1)ℏ2mKZ​.​
(47)
Then an+1​=0 and every later coefficient is zero. Consequently f is a polynomial of degree n+ℓ+1, with a factor rℓ+1; equivalently,
f(r)=rℓ+1Pn​(r),ψ(r)=rℓPn​(r)e−αr,

where Pn​ has degree n. This is series-termination quantization of the Coulomb radial equation. If Np​=n+ℓ+1 denotes the principal quantum number, the associated energy eigenvalues are
ENp​​=−2ℏ2Np2​mK2Z2​,Np​=1,2,….​
(49)
Here n counts the degree of the remaining radial polynomial; it is not the principal quantum number itself.

p(r)=4πr2∣ψ0​(r)∣2=4α03​r2e−2α0​r,r≥0.
