The Time-dependent Schrödinger equation, with the Hamiltonian for a particle of mass m in a real potential energy V, is
iℏ∂t∂Ψ​=HΨ,H=−2mℏ2​∂x2∂2​+V(x).
(1)
A stationary state is an energy eigenstate: its wavefunction evolves by a single global phase,
Ψ(x,t)=ψ(x)e−iEt/ℏ,−2mℏ2​ψ′′+Vψ=Eψ.​
(2)
Its probability density is independent of time. For a normalized wavefunction, the probability density and probability current are
ρ=Ψ∗Ψ,j=2miℏ​(Ψ∗∂x​Ψ−Ψ∂x​Ψ∗)=mℏ​Im(Ψ∗∂x​Ψ).​
(3)
Subtracting the complex conjugate of the Time-dependent Schrödinger equation, multiplied by Ψ, from the original equation multiplied by Ψ∗ cancels the real potential energy terms and gives the continuity equation ∂t​ρ+∂x​j=0. Thus j measures signed probability flow in the positive x direction.

Inside the infinite square well, the time-independent Schrödinger equation and Dirichlet boundary conditions are
−2mℏ2​ψ′′=Eψ(0<x<a),ψ(0)=ψ(a)=0,

E∫0a​∣ψ∣2dx=2mℏ2​∫0a​∣ψ′∣2dx>0.

The strict inequality follows because a function with zero derivative and zero endpoint values is zero. Put k=2mE​/ℏ. (6)
The normalization fixes the magnitude of the coefficient; its arbitrary global phase has been chosen real and positive.

The initial quantum state is the equal superposition of the first two box states, (ψ1​+ψ2​)/2​. The orthonormality of the two energy eigenstates makes its squared norm equal to one. Their separate stationary state phases give
Ψ(x,t)=a​1​[sinaπx​e−iE1​t/ℏ+sina2πx​e−iE2​t/ℏ].​
(7)
Write Ω=(E2​−E1​)/ℏ=3π2ℏ/(2ma2). The probability density is
ρ(x,t)=a1​[sin2aπx​+sin2a2πx​+2sinaπx​sina2πx​cosΩt].
(8)

∫0a/2​sinaπx​sina2πx​dx=21​∫0a/2​(cosaπx​−cosa3πx​)dx=3π2a​.

(10)
The quantum interference moves probability between the halves even though each component separately is a stationary state.

At the midpoint the second sine vanishes, whereas the derivative of the first sine vanishes. Thus the wavefunction and its spatial derivative simplify to
Ψ(a/2,t)=a​e−iE1​t/ℏ​,∂x​Ψ(a/2,t)=−a3/22π​e−iE2​t/ℏ.
(11)

j(a/2,t)=ma22πℏ​sinΩt.​
(12)

(13)

c OurBigBook.com
The issue is the square-integrable condition on the whole real line, not just decay at one end. Because the quantum harmonic oscillator potential is even, the even and odd parts of a square-integrable wavefunction are themselves square integrable and satisfy the same equation. It is therefore enough to examine each parity sequence separately.
For a nonterminating sequence, put bj​=a2j+p​, where p=0 or 1. Its recurrence relation gives
bj​bj+1​​=(2j+p+2)(2j+p+1)4j+2p+1−ϵ​∼j1​.
(19)
Since ϵ is real for an energy eigenvalue, the ratio is eventually positive. After multiplying by one constant phase, the tail coefficients are all positive. Choose 0<δ<1/2. For sufficiently large j, the ratio is at least (1−δ)/(j+1), so for some K>0 the tail coefficients obey bj​≥K(1−δ)j/j!. Consequently, for ξ>0, the magnitude of f eventually exceeds a positive multiple of ξpe(1−δ)ξ2 after subtraction of a fixed polynomial. Multiplication by e−ξ2/2 leaves exponential growth like e(1/2−δ)ξ2, incompatible with the square-integrable condition. Thus each nonzero parity series must terminate. The parity argument also excludes cancellation between the two nonterminating sequences at both infinities. This proves Normalization forces Hermite-series termination.
If the terminating polynomial has degree n, its top nonzero coefficient requires 2n+1−ϵ=0. Conversely, when ϵ=2n+1, choosing the seed of parity n nonzero makes the recurrence relation terminate exactly at degree n. The opposite seed must be zero, since that sequence does not terminate. Therefore
En​=(n+21​)ℏω,n=0,1,2,….​
(20)
The resulting polynomials are proportional to the physicists' Hermite polynomials, and ψn​∝Hn​(ξ)e−ξ2/2 is normalizable because any polynomial times this Gaussian function is square integrable. The two parity seeds describe the full solution space of the second-order differential equation; the preceding argument therefore exhausts the normalizable stationary states. Each eigenspace has dimension one. General time-dependent quantum states can of course be superpositions of these energy eigenstates.

The commutator of two linear operators is [A,B]=AB−BA​. Write T† for the adjoint operator, using the inner product convention linear in its second argument. Since A†=A and B†=B,
[A,B]†=B†A†−A†B†=BA−AB=−[A,B].
(23)
Thus the commutator is anti-self-adjoint. With C=−i[A,B], conjugation of the scalar gives
C†=i[A,B]†=−i[A,B]=C.​
(24)
All products are understood on the stated common operator domain; for physical unbounded linear operators, the state must lie in the relevant product domains and have the finite moments used below.

A normalized quantum state satisfies ⟨ψ,ψ⟩=1. Its expectation value is Eψ​(A)=⟨ψ,Aψ⟩​. The conjugate symmetry of the inner product and self-adjointness give
⟨ψ,Aψ⟩​=⟨Aψ,ψ⟩=⟨ψ,Aψ⟩,
(25)
so the expectation value is real. If Aϕ=λϕ with ϕ=0, normalize ϕ by dividing by its norm. Then
λ=⟨ϕ/∥ϕ∥,Aϕ/∥ϕ∥⟩∈R.
(26)
Every eigenvalue of a self-adjoint operator is real.

Let a=Eψ​(A), which is real. The dispersion, or quantum uncertainty, is the nonnegative standard deviation
Δψ​(A)=Eψ​(A2)−Eψ​(A)2​=∥(A−aI)ψ∥.​
(27)
Indeed, self-adjointness and normalization give
∥(A−aI)ψ∥2=⟨ψ,(A−aI)2ψ⟩=Eψ​(A2)−2aEψ​(A)+a2=Eψ​(A2)−a2.
(28)
A squared norm is nonnegative, so the square root is defined. It vanishes precisely when (A−aI)ψ=0, namely when ψ is an eigenvector of A. Conversely, an eigenvector with eigenvalue λ has a=λ and hence zero dispersion. Thus zero dispersion is equivalent to being an eigenstate.

Set u=Aψ, v=Bψ and z=⟨u,v⟩. The self-adjointness of A,B implies
z=⟨ψ,ABψ⟩,z=⟨ψ,BAψ⟩,Eψ​(C)=−i(z−z)=2Imz.
(29)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality followed by ∣z∣2≥(Imz)2 gives
Eψ​(A2)Eψ​(B2)=∥u∥2∥v∥2≥∣z∣2≥(Imz)2=41​(Eψ​(C))2​.
(30)
Both inequalities must be equalities. The exact conditions are Aψ and Bψ are linearly dependent, and Re⟨Aψ,Bψ⟩=0. Equivalently, equality holds if either vector is zero; when both are nonzero it holds precisely when
Bψ=iλAψfor some real λ=0.​
(31)
The zero-vector cases matter: they saturate the inequality but cannot all be expressed by solving for Bψ as a multiple of Aψ. The real-part condition can also be written Eψ​(AB+BA)=0, using the anticommutator. This is the uncentered second-moment commutator inequality.

Centre the position operator and momentum operator by their real expectation values:
A=X−x0​I,B=P−p0​I,x0​=Eψ​(X),p0​=Eψ​(P).
(32)
Scalar multiples of the identity operator commute with every linear operator, so the canonical commutation relation gives
[A,B]=[X,P]=iℏI,C=ℏI,Eψ​(C)=ℏ.
(33)
The second moments of the centred linear operators are the variances, Eψ​(A2)=Δψ​(X)2 and Eψ​(B2)=Δψ​(P)2. Applying the preceding inequality and taking nonnegative square roots proves the Heisenberg uncertainty relation:
Δψ​(X)2Δψ​(P)2≥4ℏ2​,Δψ​(X)Δψ​(P)≥2ℏ​.​
(34)
Subtracting the means is essential: the uncentred second moments alone do not equal the variances. The equality case of the Heisenberg uncertainty relation requires the centred vectors to be imaginary multiples, as in the preceding equality conditions.
