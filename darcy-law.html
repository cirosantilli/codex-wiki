Darcy law (q=−k(∇p−ρg)/μ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics Porous-media flow
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 43
Darcy law is a linear relation between the bulk Darcy flux and the net driving force per unit volume. For an isotropic porous medium containing one fully saturating Newtonian fluid,
q=−μk​(∇p−ρg).
(7)
Here q is the Darcy velocity relative to the solid framework, k is the intrinsic permeability of a porous medium, μ>0 is dynamic viscosity, p is averaged fluid pressure, ρ is mass density, and g is the downward gravitational acceleration vector. The pressure gradient exerts force density −∇p, while gravity contributes ρg; the minus sign makes the discharge follow their sum. The symbols u and q are both commonly used for Darcy velocity; neither denotes pore velocity here.
With upward coordinate z and positive gravitational acceleration magnitude g, g=−gez​. Constant ρ and g therefore give
∇p−ρg=∇p+ρgez​=ρg∇(z+ρgp​),q=−K∇h,K=μkρg​.
(8)
The hydraulic head h=z+p/(ρg) combines elevation and fluid pressure, and hydraulic conductivity K combines the medium and fluid properties. Hydrostatic pressure satisfies ∇p=ρg, so it produces zero Darcy flux even though fluid pressure increases downward. Conversely, equal pressures at the two ends of a vertical column do not imply zero flow: their elevations give different hydraulic heads.
The appeal of Darcy law is that a complicated connected pore geometry enters a macroscopic calculation through a measurable coefficient. It predicts filter discharge and aquifer flow; combined with mass conservation, it gives an equation for the evolving fluid pressure. Its simple linear form requires a small pore-scale Reynolds number, slow adjustment compared with pore-scale inertial relaxation, and a valid continuum approximation. For a characteristic pore size d and mean pore velocity v, one possible convention is Repore​=ρ∣v∣d/μ≪1; conventions using grain size or Darcy velocity have different numerical thresholds. Merely calling flow laminar does not guarantee negligible inertia. Unsaturated pores, several fluid phases, appreciable inertia, or a fluid whose stress is not Newtonian require further modeling. Woessner and Poeter discuss these limits in their authored hydrology text.[ref]
Henry Darcy's 1856 book Les fontaines publiques de la ville de Dijon was published by Victor Dalmont; the University Grenoble Alpes digital collection verifies this bibliographic record and supplies a digitized copy.[ref] Woessner and Poeter's account connects Darcy law to experiments on water flowing through sand filters, relating discharge to cross-sectional area, hydraulic head difference and sample length.[ref] The modern intrinsic permeability of a porous medium notation above should not be read as a quotation of Darcy's original notation.
