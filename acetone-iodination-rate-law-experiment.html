Acetone iodination rate-law experiment

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Reaction mechanism
OurBigBook.com Words: 426
Acetone reacts with iodine to form iodoacetone, iodide, and a hydrogen ion:
CH3​COCH3​+I2​⟶CH3​COCH2​I+H++I−.
(18)
In water, H+ denotes a solvated hydrogen ion; added hydrochloric acid provides acidic conditions.
Moorpark College's protocol mixes acetone, hydrochloric acid and water from burets in matched test tubes; pipet in iodine, swirl and time colour loss against white paper. Use water, then reacted solution, as references. Duplicate each mixture; keep total volume 5.00mL and run temperatures within 2∘C.[ref]
The stock dilutions give [acetone]0​=0.800, [H+]0​=0.200, and [I2​]0​=0.00100molL−1. B and C separately double acetone and acid; D halves iodine. The manual supplies no measured times; it states zero partial order of reaction in iodine and asks students to determine the other exponents.[ref]
The endpoint measurement is a time τ to an operational colour threshold, not direct observation of a reaction intermediate. If the iodine disappearance rate r is approximately constant until that endpoint, r≈[I2​]0​/τ. Halving [I2​]0​ at fixed other concentrations then approximately halves τ without changing r. A longer or shorter endpoint time alone is not a comparison of reaction rates unless the starting concentration is included. For an observed power-form rate law, doubling only acetone gives a rate ratio 2m and hence m=ln(rB​/rA​)/ln2; doubling only acid similarly estimates its own partial order of reaction. Those are inference formulas, not asserted experimental results.
A useful proposed reaction mechanism would explain how iodine is consumed after an earlier transformation sets the throughput, making the observed reaction rate insensitive to iodine over a tested range. The observed zero exponent would contradict treating this net chemical equation as a single elementary reaction with directional rate law k[acetone][I2​]. It would not uniquely prove which reaction intermediate or earlier step is involved. Independently detecting a candidate reaction intermediate would provide another kind of evidence.
The excess-reactant approximation has an explicit scale: the baseline iodine inventory is only 0.00100/0.800=0.125% of the initial acetone inventory, and its net hydrogen ion production is 0.00100/0.200=0.5% of the initial acid inventory, assuming only the stated one-to-one chemical reaction. These are calculated bounds on composition change, not measured reaction orders. Mixing delay, a subjective colour threshold, and temperature drift limit the inference. Repeats and a fixed viewing geometry help; a calibrated absorbance trace could test linearity during conversion instead of relying on an endpoint alone. That extension would require a reference blank and a justified Beer-Lambert law calibration, rather than treating visible colour as an exact concentration. A zero-order reaction approximation in iodine must fail at depletion; it cannot imply consumption after no iodine remains.
