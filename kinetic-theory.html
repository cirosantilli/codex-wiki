Kinetic theory (f(t,x,v))

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 30
Kinetic theory describes many-particle systems through a distribution on phase space, retaining both position and velocity. In the number-density convention, f(t,x,v)d3xd3v is the expected number of particles in a small position–velocity region. For identical particles of mass m, its integrals give the number density n and bulk velocity u:
n(t,x)=∫R3​fd3v,nu=∫R3​vfd3v.
(19)
This f is not normalized to one: its integral over the whole phase space gives the total expected particle number. Transport moves particles along trajectories, while collision or diffusion terms change the distribution. The Boltzmann equation, Hamiltonian Liouville equation and Fokker-Planck equation describe different physical assumptions, rather than interchangeable collision laws.
An ideal gas is a motivating example: the pressure on a wall comes from microscopic momentum transfers, even when the bulk velocity is zero. The Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution separates this random molecular motion from bulk motion; see ideal gas pressure from wall collisions. The attractive feature of kinetic theory is that a distribution can describe two opposing populations where one average velocity hides almost everything. It also tells us when averaging into a continuum approximation loses essential information: compare the intermolecular mean free path with the apparatus scale using the Knudsen number. [ref]
