Kinetic theory (f(t,x,v))

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 43
Kinetic theory describes many-particle systems through a distribution on phase space, retaining both position and velocity. In the number-density convention, f(t,x,v)d3xd3v is the expected number of particles in a small position–velocity region. For identical particles of mass m, its integrals give the number density n and bulk velocity u:
n(t,x)=∫R3​fd3v,nu=∫R3​vfd3v.
(1)
This f is not normalized to one: its integral over the whole phase space gives the total expected particle number. Transport moves particles along trajectories, while collision or diffusion terms change the distribution. The Boltzmann equation, Hamiltonian Liouville equation and Fokker-Planck equation describe different physical assumptions, rather than interchangeable collision laws.
An ideal gas is a motivating example: the pressure on a wall comes from microscopic momentum transfers, even when the bulk velocity is zero. The Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution separates this random molecular motion from bulk motion; see ideal gas pressure from wall collisions. The attractive feature of kinetic theory is that a distribution can describe two opposing populations where one average velocity hides almost everything. It also tells us when averaging into a continuum approximation loses essential information: compare the intermolecular mean free path with the apparatus scale using the Knudsen number. [ref]

Kinetic estimates of gas transport coefficients (D∼vλ/3,η∼nmvλ/3,κ∼ncv​vλ/3)

Words: 130
Kinetic theory OurBigBook.com
In a dilute gas whose mean free path λ is short compared with macroscopic gradient lengths, particles carry properties from their previous collisions over distances of order λ. A first-gradient isotropic estimate gives a transported flux −nvλ∇a/3 for a property a carried per particle. Applying this reasoning to species abundance, directed momentum mux​ and thermal energy with per-particle heat capacity cv​ gives diffusivity D∼vλ/3, dynamic viscosity η∼nmvλ/3 and thermal conductivity κ∼ncv​vλ/3. These numerical prefactors are estimates, not exact collision-integral results.
For a constant hard-sphere diameter d, λ∝1/(nd2) and v∝T/m​. Consequently η∝mT​/d2, κ∝cv​T/m​/d2 and D∝T/m​/(nd2). At fixed pressure the last scales as T3/2/p. The near pressure-independence of viscosity and thermal conductivity applies only while gas-gas collisions, rather than apparatus walls, determine the mean free path. Temperature-dependent cross-sections and internal molecular excitations change the simple power laws.

The Maxwell speed distribution describes an equilibrium ideal gas; the effusive beam speed distribution weights that population by its rate of arrival.

Boltzmann-Grad limit (Nad−1∼1,Nad→0)

Words: 62
Kinetic theory OurBigBook.com
For N hard particles in a fixed macroscopic volume, shrink their diameter a while keeping Nad−1 of order one. The occupied volume fraction tends to zero, but the mean free path stays macroscopic. Deriving the Boltzmann equation in this dilute limit requires control of recollisions and propagation of an incoming independence hypothesis related to molecular chaos for the Boltzmann equation. Its validity is not an automatic all-time consequence of small particle size.

Knudsen number (Kn=ℓmfp​/L)

Words: 689 Articles: 3
Kinetic theory OurBigBook.com
The Knudsen number is the dimensionless ratio of an intermolecular mean free path ℓmfp​ to a specified characteristic length L of the apparatus or flow. The mean free path measures the distance travelled between gas–gas collisions; L measures the distance over which boundaries or macroscopic conditions matter. Wall encounters must not be counted as gas–gas collisions in this comparison: doing so would hide the very wall-dominated behavior we want to detect. The same gas can therefore have a small Knudsen number in a large vessel and a large Knudsen number in a tiny opening.
For a dilute hard-sphere gas of identical molecular diameter d in thermal equilibrium, use the convention
ℓmfp​=2​nπd21​=2​πd2pkB​T​,Kn=2​πd2pLkB​T​.
(2)
Here n is the number density, p the pressure, T the absolute temperature, and kB​ the Boltzmann constant; the second equality uses the ideal gas law. The collision cross-section is πd2, and the 2​ accounts for both partners moving with independent equilibrium velocities. Mixtures and velocity-dependent collision cross-sections require a stated molecular model and mean free path convention; the displayed expression is not an exact formula for every real gas. [ref] [ref]
As a numerical toy example, choose T=300K and d=0.37nm, without claiming that this is a measured molecular diameter for a particular gas. The formula gives ℓmfp​≃68.1nm at p=105Pa. It yields Kn≃6.81×10−6 for a 1cm vessel but Kn≃0.681 for a 100nm gap. At p=1Pa the same toy gas has ℓmfp​≃6.81mm, comparable to the centimetre vessel. Reducing either pressure or apparatus size can thus expose molecular transport.
Choose L for the question being asked: a tube diameter compares the mean free path with wall separation, while tube length describes a different geometric scale. For a positive macroscopic field q, a local gradient scale can instead be Lq​=∣q∣/∣∇q∣, where its gradient is nonzero. A large vessel can contain a thin region with a large local Knudsen number. Small geometric Knudsen number alone does not guarantee a slowly varying near-equilibrium flow. The time, velocity and space scalings must also be stated when the Knudsen number appears as a coefficient in a Boltzmann equation. [ref]

Rarefied gas flow approximations

Words: 287 Articles: 2
Knudsen number OurBigBook.com
For Kn≪1, frequent gas–gas collisions can establish a local Maxwellian before macroscopic conditions change appreciably. Under this additional near-equilibrium assumption, the hydrodynamic limit of the Boltzmann equation motivates a continuum approximation with Navier-Stokes equations and heat conduction. A no-slip boundary condition is a leading wall approximation, not a statement about individual molecular velocities.
With a small but finite Knudsen number, a useful slip approximation retains continuum bulk equations and corrects wall velocity and temperature. A Navier slip boundary condition can represent the leading tangential velocity correction, but its slip length must come from the relevant gas–wall interaction model. A nearby Knudsen layer still requires kinetic treatment if its detailed structure is wanted. Slip corrections are perturbations; extending them into order-one Knudsen numbers has no general accuracy guarantee. At order-one Knudsen number, wall and intermolecular collisions both matter; this transitional situation calls for a kinetic theory model. At Kn≫1, free molecular flow becomes a useful limiting approximation. These are scale comparisons, with accuracy depending on geometry, flow conditions and the observable, rather than universal sharp numerical boundaries. [ref]

Continuum slip and jump conditions describe an effective boundary for the outer solution; they do not resolve this layer. [ref]

Free molecular flow

Words: 74
Rarefied gas flow approximations OurBigBook.com
In free molecular flow, gas–gas collisions are negligible over the trajectories relevant to the observable. Molecules still encounter walls, whose scattering of momentum and energy must be modeled. In a long narrow tube, ℓmfp​ exceeding the diameter does not by itself prove that every complete transmission path is collision-free: repeated wall reflections can create much longer paths. The approximation must be checked against those paths as well as the width. [ref]

Effusion

Words: 373 Articles: 5
Kinetic theory OurBigBook.com
Effusion is molecular passage through a small opening whose dimensions, including its thickness, are much smaller than the intermolecular mean free path. For an equilibrium ideal gas, the one-way particle rate through a thin hole of area A is N˙=Ap/2πmkB​T​. This weights molecules by their wall-normal velocity, rather than just counting all molecules in the reservoir. [ref]

Effusive molecular energy flux (FE​=2kB​Tnv/4)

Words: 61
Effusion OurBigBook.com
An equilibrium Maxwell-Boltzmann gas has number flux nv/4 onto a plane. Molecules crossing the plane are weighted by their positive normal velocity, so their mean translational kinetic energy is 2kB​T, rather than the bulk value 3kB​T/2. The translational energy flux is therefore FE​=nvkB​T/2. Internal degrees of freedom contribute their mean energy per crossing molecule when independent of translational velocity.

Collisionless effusion

Words: 40
Effusion OurBigBook.com
During collisionless effusion, selective loss changes the distribution of surviving particle velocities without collision-driven relaxation to a new Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. The loss law depends on flight paths and on the wall-reflection rule. Speed-dependent depletion and, for specular walls, directional structure can remain.

If a classical monatomic gas re-equilibrates by collisions while losing atoms by effusion, the escaping atoms carry mean kinetic energy 2kB​T. Two-vessel effusive mixing (τs​=4V/[Ah​(vs,L​+vs,R​)])

Words: 107
Effusion OurBigBook.com
For a species s in two well-mixed vessels of equal volume V joined by a thin opening of area Ah​, the effusion law gives n˙s,L​=−(Ah​/4V)(vs,L​ns,L​−vs,R​ns,R​). Each species' total inventory is conserved. With fixed temperatures and total density sum ns,L​+ns,R​=ns,Σ​, the left density relaxes exponentially with time constant τs​=4V/[Ah​(vs,L​+vs,R​)] to ns,L∞​=ns,Σ​TR​​/(TL​​+TR​​).
At equal temperatures, τs​=2V/(Ah​vs​)∝ms​​, and initially separated species mix at different rates. At unequal temperatures, the final density ratio is ns,L​/ns,R​=TR​/TL​​ for every species, while the partial-pressure ratio is ps,L​/ps,R​=TL​/TR​​. The mass-independent final ratios do not imply mass-independent transient rates. Maintaining different temperatures requires external heat exchange even after net particle transfer vanishes.

Thermal transpiration

Words: 821 Articles: 3
Kinetic theory OurBigBook.com
Thermal transpiration is gas transport driven by an imposed temperature difference in a rarefied connection. Gas can move from cold to hot even when the two end pressures start equal. If the reservoirs are closed, redistribution builds a counteracting pressure difference until the net transfer stops. This final stationary state is not global thermal equilibrium: the two temperatures remain different. It explains why a heated pressure sensor can read a different pressure from the vessel to which it is connected. The correction depends on gas, connection geometry and molecular interaction with its surfaces. [ref]
The simple molecular limit is beautiful: equal opposing particle rates need not mean equal pressures. Consider effusion through a thin hole connecting two locally equilibrated reservoirs of the same gas, at Th​>Tc​. Equating their one-way rates gives
Tc​​pc​​=Th​​ph​​,pc​ph​​=Tc​Th​​​.
(3)
At equal pressure, the hotter reservoir has lower number density; its faster molecules do not fully compensate for that reduction in outgoing particle rate. With Tc​=300K and Th​=351K, the ideal zero-transfer ratio is 351/300​≃1.0817. The hydrodynamic, zero-flow limit instead has equal pressures when other forces are absent. These are limiting models, not an interpolation formula for a glass capillary. [ref] In a real tube, incomplete exchange of molecular energy with the wall can alter the limiting ratio; a Knudsen number does not specify that exchange. [ref]

Closed-vessel thermal transpiration (Δp=2p0​q(q−1)/(q+1))

Words: 76
Thermal transpiration OurBigBook.com
Two equal sealed vessels of fixed volume, initially at T0​,p0​, exchange an ideal gas through a thin effusive opening. Hold one at T1​ and the other at T0​, with both interiors locally thermalized. Zero net particle transfer gives ph​/T1​​=pc​/T0​​. Writing q=T1​/T0​​, conservation of total particle number gives ph​=2p0​q2/(1+q) and pc​=2p0​q/(1+q). Thus Δp=2p0​q(q−1)/(1+q). The alternative pc​=p0​, Δp=p0​(q−1) requires a cold reservoir whose pressure is externally held fixed; it is not the closed equal-volume problem.

Thermal transpiration experiment with a glass microtube

Words: 510 Articles: 1
Thermal transpiration OurBigBook.com
Rojas Cardenas, Graur, Perrier and Méolans studied thermal transpiration in helium using a glass microtube of diameter 490±1μm and length 3.05±0.01cm in their 2012 experiment. They heated one reservoir, leaving the other near room temperature, and measured tube-end temperatures of 300 and 351K. Three contact thermometers and an infrared camera checked the thermal conditions; the hot reservoir was warmer than the adjacent tube end, so heater temperature was not an adequate substitute for tube-end temperature. Two capacitance diaphragm gauges recorded the reservoir pressures; a third gauge supported calibration. [ref]
Their earlier 2011 paper explains the measurement sequence. A wide bypass links the reservoirs in parallel with the microtube. After isolating the gas supply and pump, keep the bypass open to equalize pressures with the temperature difference already established. Closing the bypass defines t=0. The hot-side pressure rises and the cold-side pressure falls; ultimately their slopes approach zero at unequal pressures. Repeating at different initial pressures changes the degree of rarefaction without changing the microtube. The 2011 study also compared helium and nitrogen and imposed temperature differences of 50, 60 and 70K. [ref]
The quantities to record are both pc​(t) and ph​(t), the reservoir volumes, and the temperatures relevant to their particle inventories. In an idealized reservoir of fixed volume Vi​ and uniform fixed temperature Ti​, the ideal gas law gives N˙i​=[Vi​/(kB​Ti​)]p˙​i​, so opposite inventory changes test particle conservation. If temperature changes, differentiation instead gives N˙i​=(Vi​/kB​)[p˙​i​/Ti​−pi​T˙i​/Ti2​]: a thermal drift can imitate particle transfer. The authors' 2011 presentation explicitly checks this thermal correction when extracting an initial mass flow rate from the pressure slope. [ref]
The 2012 paper compares the final pressure ratio across near-free-molecular through slip conditions with a kinetic model and empirical corrections. It does not establish the thin-hole square-root law at every measured pressure. [ref] For a replication, useful additional controls follow from the interpretation: remove the temperature difference to test the null signal; reverse it to test the direction; check gauge zero offsets and leaks; and wait for stable temperatures before closing the bypass. These are suggested controls, not claims that the cited paper performed each one. A reproducible pressure split at zero net transfer makes the failure of a single continuum wall model observable, while the Knudsen number organizes which molecular and apparatus scales are competing.

Molecular chaos for the Boltzmann equation

Words: 63
Kinetic theory OurBigBook.com
The incoming two-particle distribution at a collision is approximated by the product of one-particle distributions. This closes the binary collision term into the quadratic nonlinear Boltzmann collision operator. It does not assert exact independence after collisions: outgoing pairs can be correlated, and time reversal generally converts those correlations into incoming correlations. A rigorous kinetic limit needs propagation and convergence estimates rather than a bare independence assertion.

Kac model

Words: 449 Articles: 8
Kinetic theory OurBigBook.com
This many-particle stochastic collision model rotates one uniformly selected velocity pair by a uniformly selected angle, conserving the sum of squared velocities. Its normalized angular averaging is the Kac collision operator, and its continuous-time evolution is the Kac master equation. In this version velocities range over RN and initial energy is not restricted to one sphere; the evolution preserves the initial energy distribution.

Kac chaos (Πk​(FN​)⇀f⊗k)

Words: 64
Kac model OurBigBook.com
A permutation-symmetric sequence of probability laws is chaotic with one-particle law f when, for every fixed k, its k-coordinate marginal distributions converge weakly to the product law f⊗k as N→∞. This is an asymptotic independence condition, not a consequence of symmetry alone. A rigorous kinetic limit also requires propagation of this property under the dynamics and sufficient convergence to pass collision integrals.

The jump process with total collision rate N and normalized pair-angle operator Q evolves by ∂t​FN​=N(Q−I)FN​. It preserves mass and the total squared velocity. Its one-coordinate distribution satisfies the Kac marginal evolution equation, and its Gaussian relative entropy in Kac's model decreases at the rate given by Kac entropy production.

For a permutation-symmetric density in the Kac master equation, integrate over all but the first velocity. Collisions among integrated coordinates cancel. Kac collision operator (Q=(2N​)−1∑i<j​(2π)−1∫Uij,θ​dθ)

Kac model OurBigBook.com
For N≥2, Uij,θ​F=F∘Rij,θ​ uses the genuine two-coordinate rotation (vi​,vj​)↦(vi​cosθ+vj​sinθ,−vi​sinθ+vj​cosθ). Average it uniformly over angles and pairs to obtain Q. Dirichlet form of the Kac collision operator OurBigBook.com
Zero Dirichlet form of the Kac collision operator forces invariance under every pair rotation; strong continuity upgrades almost every angle to every angle. Coordinate-plane rotations generate the special orthogonal group, whose action is transitive on each sphere for N≥2. The Vlasov equation describes collisionless evolution of a phase-space distribution function in a self-consistent force field: ∂t​f+v⋅∇x​f+F⋅∇p​f=0. The force can be electromagnetic or gravitational; the Collisionless dark-matter Vlasov equation is the cosmological gravitational specialization.
