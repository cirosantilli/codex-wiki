Sample space (Ω)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability space
OurBigBook.com Words: 828 Articles: 3
The sample space is the set Ω of outcomes represented by a probability model. One elementary outcome ω∈Ω specifies a complete result at the level of detail the model keeps. An event is a subset belonging to the model's sigma-algebra F; it occurs when the actual outcome lies in that subset. Together with a probability measure P, these form a probability space (Ω,F,P), with P(Ω)=1.[ref]
Choosing a sample space means deciding which distinctions matter. For two tosses, recording the ordered head-or-tail sequence distinguishes HT from TH; recording only the number of heads merges them. Neither representation is automatically wrong, but the second cannot answer a question about which toss was heads. Keeping a detailed sample space and defining a random variable for the desired observation lets several questions share one model.
A sample space alone assigns no probabilities. Even writing Ω={H,T}2 as a Cartesian product does not establish independence: that requires a condition on the probability measure. On a finite sample space with all subsets admitted as events,
P(A)=∑ω∈A​P({ω}).
(1)
Only when all elementary probabilities are equal does this become P(A)=∣A∣/∣Ω∣, where the bars denote cardinality. This is the uniform distribution on a finite set, an additional modelling choice.
Sample spaces can also be infinite. For example, take Ω=[0,1], its Borel sigma-algebra, and the continuous uniform distribution. Then P([a,b])=b−a for 0≤a≤b≤1, so each singleton has probability zero while P(Ω)=1. Thus an elementary outcome need not carry positive probability, and a continuous model cannot be understood by counting equally likely points. The distinction is developed in zero-probability event and countable additivity.
