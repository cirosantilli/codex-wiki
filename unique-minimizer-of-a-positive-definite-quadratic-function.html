Unique minimizer of a positive-definite quadratic function (f(x)=21​xTAx−bTx+c,x∗​=A−1b)

Let A be a real positive-definite matrix and minimize the displayed quadratic function over all of Rn. Its gradient is Ax−b, so its only stationary point solves the linear system Ax∗​=b. The matrix is an invertible matrix, making this solution exist and be unique. Completing the square around that solution proves the stronger global statement:
f(x)=c−21​bTA−1b+21​(x−x∗​)TA(x−x∗​),f(x)−f(x∗​)>0(x=x∗​).
(15)
The quadratic function is strictly convex, and its level sets above the minimum are translated ellipsoids. Positivity therefore turns solving a linear system into locating the bottom of a bowl.[ref]
For the opening matrix A=(31​13​), take b=(1,1)T and c=0. Then A−1=81​(3−1​−13​), x∗​=(1/4,1/4)T, and f(x∗​)=−1/4. Every displacement h raises the value by 21​hTAh; along the unit eigenvectors e+​ and e−​ the increases are respectively 2s2 and s2 for displacement se+​ or se−​.
For a singular positive semidefinite matrix, a solution of Ax=b is still a global minimizer, but adding any kernel vector gives another. If b is outside the image of a linear map defined by A, some kernel vector v has bTv=0, and f(sv)=c−sbTv is unbounded below. For an indefinite quadratic form, a negative eigenvalue gives a direction along which the quadratic term tends to −∞ regardless of b. Thus a stationary point alone does not establish a minimum. The same energy-difference identity extends to the quadratic variational principle for a symmetric positive operator, with additional existence hypotheses in infinite dimension.
