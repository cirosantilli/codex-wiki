James Prescott Joule (1818–1889)

Words: 259 Articles: 1
List of physicists OurBigBook.com
James Prescott Joule was an English physicist whose private experiments connected electricity, heat and mechanical work. His family ran a brewery in Salford; he developed an experimental laboratory while working in the family business.[ref] The Royal Society's archive identifies his occupation as physicist and records his life as 1818–1889.[ref]
His electrical heating experiments placed metal coils in water and measured the temperature change. Later, he used the brewery cellar to reduce ambient temperature fluctuations during friction experiments and took particular care over thermometer calibration.[ref] These practical choices address a central difficulty of calorimetry: a small temperature rise can reflect both the intended input of energy and unwanted heat transfer to the surroundings. A quiet baseline and a known heat capacity are part of the measurement, not decoration around the formula.
The contrast between Joule heating and Joule's paddle-wheel experiment makes the connection especially clear. One supplies electrical work; the other supplies mechanical work. In each case, calorimetry tracks the resulting change of internal energy. Comparing quantitatively controlled inputs with thermal changes gives an experimental test of conservation of energy, rather than merely showing that stirring or an electric current can warm water. The paddle-wheel account describes the descending weights, vanes, thermal controls and corrections needed for that comparison.
Joule's free-expansion experiment asks a different question: how does a gas's temperature change during expansion without external work? The Joule-Thomson effect instead concerns flow through a restriction and a balance involving enthalpy. Keeping the two experiments distinct prevents a shared historical name from hiding different system boundaries and different conserved quantities.

Albert Einstein (1879-1955)

Words: 195 Articles: 1
List of physicists OurBigBook.com
A physicist whose work connects special relativity, general relativity, Brownian motion and the photoelectric effect. He worked at the Institute for Advanced Study from 1933 until his death in 1955.[ref]
In 1905, Albert Einstein used the photoelectric effect to explore quantized light, related Brownian motion to molecular agitation, formulated special relativity, and connected changes in mass and energy. These problems link visible measurements to otherwise inaccessible structure: emitted electron energies, suspended-particle displacements and comparisons of clocks.[ref]
The Millikan photoelectric experiment follows one of those predictions into the laboratory. Its stopping-voltage measurements test a quantitative relation; the experiment's apparatus, surface preparation and electrical corrections matter to that inference. The Einstein field equations and general relativity lead to a different set of tests involving gravity and spacetime.
The Nobel Prize in Physics in 1921 recognized his theoretical work, particularly the law of the photoelectric effect; the award was presented in 1922.[ref]

John William Strutt, 3rd Baron Rayleigh (1842–1919)

Words: 139 Articles: 1
List of physicists OurBigBook.com
A physicist whose research joined waves, optics, acoustics and precise measurement. He maintained a laboratory at Terling and served as Cavendish professor of experimental physics from 1879 to 1884. At Cambridge he developed laboratory courses; later he donated his Nobel award proceeds toward a Cavendish laboratory extension.[ref]
The Rayleigh-Ramsay argon density experiment, pursued with William Ramsay, connects a small discrepancy in gas weighing to chemical separation. Its lesson is practical: agreement within one preparation method does not ensure that a second method produces the same chemical substance. The gas density model explains how composition and filling conditions both affect the readout.
