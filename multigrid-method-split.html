Multigrid method

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 6
A multigrid method solves a linear system by combining local relaxation with coarse-grid correction on a hierarchy of meshes. The key is to divide the work by scale: a suitable relaxation removes rapidly oscillating approximation error, while coarse-grid correction addresses slowly varying approximation error that relaxation barely changes. A broad error hump occupies many fine-grid points but fewer coarse-grid points; it becomes easier to resolve by relaxation after enough coarsening. This complementary action is why multigrid methods are useful for large finite difference method and finite element method systems.
For a concrete model, take the one-dimensional Poisson equation with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions:
−u′′(x)=f(x),0<x<1,u(0)=u(1)=0.
(1)
Use n interior points xj​=jh, h=1/(n+1), and the centered finite difference system
(Ah​uh​)j​=h22uh,j​−uh,j−1​−uh,j+1​​=bh,j​,bh,j​=f(xj​),uh,0​=uh,n+1​=0.
(2)
Thus Ah​=h−2tridiag(−1,2,−1) is a symmetric matrix and a positive-definite matrix. The minus sign in −u′′ fixes this positive convention; changing both Ah​ and bh​ to their negatives leaves the relaxation update unchanged. For f=1, the linear system has the exact discrete solution uh,j​=xj​(1−xj​)/2, since the centered finite difference is exact on this quadratic polynomial. In general, the exact discrete solution need not equal the sampled continuous solution: solving the linear system removes algebraic approximation error, not the global discretization error.
Let uh∗​ solve Ah​uh∗​=bh​, let vh​ be the current approximation, and define the signed approximation error eh​=uh∗​−vh​. The linear-system residual is rh​=bh​−Ah​vh​, so Ah​eh​=rh​. In particular, a linear-system residual is available without knowing uh∗​; it supplies the right-hand side for a correction equation. With D=2h−2I, a weighted Jacobi method sweep is
vh​←vh​+ωD−1rh​,eh​←Sω​eh​,Sω​=I−ωD−1Ah​.
(3)
The sine eigenvectors shared by Ah​ and the iteration matrix Sω​ are wj(k)​=sin(kπj/(n+1)), 1≤k≤n. Their eigenvalues are
λk​(Ah​)=4h−2sin22(n+1)kπ​,μk​(Sω​)=1−2ωsin22(n+1)kπ​.
(4)
For n=63, one weighted Jacobi method sweep with ω=2/3 multiplies the lowest Fourier frequency mode k=1 by about 0.999197, but the highest Fourier frequency mode k=63 by about −0.332530. Four sweeps retain about 99.68% of the first amplitude and 1.22% of the second. The sign reversal of the second amplitude is harmless when its magnitude shrinks. However, the plain Jacobi method, ω=1, has factor cos(kπ/(n+1)): at k=63 it is about −0.998795. A nearly alternating mode near the grid's Nyquist limit therefore persists almost as strongly as the smoothest mode. Plain Jacobi method convergence on a fixed Dirichlet boundary condition grid does not imply effective smoothing.[ref] The high-frequency smoothing factor of weighted Jacobi is uniformly at most 1/3 for ω=2/3 and k≥(n+1)/2; the exact finite-grid high-frequency Jacobi smoothing optimum distinguishes this convenient uniform choice from the finite-grid optimum.[ref]
One two-grid cycle applies a few weighted Jacobi method sweeps, computes the linear-system residual, performs coarse-grid correction, and applies a few more sweeps. The first relaxation makes the remaining approximation error easier to represent on a coarse mesh; the last relaxation damps oscillatory components left after the transferred correction. In a geometric multigrid V-cycle, replace the expensive exact coarse solve by one recursive cycle on the coarse correction equation, starting its correction from zero. Repeat until a tiny coarsest linear system can be solved directly, then return through the levels by adding each interpolated correction and post-smoothing. The descent and return give the cycle its V shape.[ref]
For this one-dimensional model, fixed sweep counts and bounded local finite difference and transfer work give a geometric multigrid V-cycle cost proportional to n+n/2+n/4+⋯<2n, hence O(n) in Big O notation. A fixed error reduction also needs a contraction factor bounded below one independently of h; per-cycle work alone does not establish that. Starting from arbitrary approximation error and demanding a relative reduction ε generally adds a factor proportional to log(1/ε) in the cycle count. Full multigrid uses coarse solutions as initial guesses on successively finer meshes to target the global discretization error efficiently. The two-grid Poisson factor with weighted Jacobi illustrates favorable convergence for this model. Other partial differential equations, strong anisotropy, or poorly chosen coarse spaces and relaxation can defeat these choices; a linear-work guarantee for arbitrary linear systems does not follow.
A historical example of the multilevel viewpoint is Achi Brandt's April 1977 paper, which analyzes adaptive meshes and reports numerical experiments on linear and nonlinear boundary value problems. This is a documented development of the approach, not a claim that the paper was its first invention.[ref] The figure above is generated from the displayed eigenvalues, with lines joining discrete nodes; it contains no experimental measurements.
