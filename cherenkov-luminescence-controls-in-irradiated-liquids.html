Cherenkov luminescence controls in irradiated liquids

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Electromagnetic wave Cherenkov radiation
OurBigBook.com Words: 238
In his 1958 lecture, Pavel Cherenkov recalls the 1934 reports by himself and Sergey Vavilov: radium gamma rays illuminated solutions and their solvents. Weak light was compared using dark-adapted human vision and threshold photometry. Heating and added fluorescence quenching agents left the solvent component and its polarization of an electromagnetic wave unchanged; parallel aqueous fluorescent controls visibly quenched. Its electric vibration direction was parallel to the exciting beam, unlike the compared polarized fluorescence. Further experiments attributed the emission to fast electrons produced by Compton scattering, rather than directly to gamma rays. These comparisons supported a mechanism distinct from ordinary luminescence.[ref]
The evidential step is the controlled comparison, rather than the mere presence of light. A weak glow could include fluorescence from impurities; suppressing a known fluorescent control tests whether the intervention actually works. Visual threshold comparisons also require a stable observer and viewing conditions. These early controls do not themselves calibrate absolute photon yield or supply a single-electron angular scan. Its polarization of an electromagnetic wave comparison also does not by itself measure the Cherenkov cone angle of an individual electron.
Ilya Frank and Igor Tamm supplied the theory in 1937; Ilya Frank also investigated nuclear physics experimentally and later directed neutron research.[ref] Sergey Vavilov, an optics and luminescence researcher and collaborator in the discovery, died in 1951. The 1958 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Pavel Cherenkov, Ilya Frank and Igor Tamm; Sergey Vavilov was not a recipient.[ref]
