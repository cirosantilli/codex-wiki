By the spectral theorem a symmetric positive-definite matrix C has C=Qdiag(ci​)QT, with Q orthogonal and ci​>0. Define S=Qdiag(ci​​)QT. It is symmetric positive-definite and S2=C. For uniqueness, any symmetric positive-definite square root T commutes with C=T2, hence preserves each eigenspace of C. On its c-eigenspace, T is symmetric positive-definite and all its eigenvalues have square c, so all equal c​. Therefore T=S, including repeated eigenspaces.
For detF>0, put
U=(FTF)1/2,R=FU−1,V=RURT=(FFT)1/2.​
(1)
Then RTR=I and detR=detF/detU>0, so R∈SO(3); also F=RU=VR. Any other right polar decomposition has FTF=U2, so uniqueness of the positive root fixes U and then R. The left factor is consequently fixed too.
The principal stretches vi​ are the positive eigenvalues of U, equivalently the singular values of F. For a unit vector N=∑i​ci​Ni​ in an orthonormal eigenbasis of U,
∣FN∣2=NTU2N=∑i​vi2​ci2​≤vmax2​∑i​ci2​=vmax2​.
(2)
Thus ∣N∣=1max​∣FN∣=vmax​​, attained precisely by the unit vectors in the eigenspace of U belonging to vmax​. With a simple largest eigenvalue these are its two opposite unit eigenvectors; degeneracy allows every unit combination within that eigenspace.
For the simple shear,
F=​100​γ10​001​​,C=FTF=​1γ0​γ1+γ20​001​​.
(3)
A line with nonzero direction N keeps its length exactly when
∣FN∣2−∣N∣2=γN2​(2N1​+γN2​)=0.
(4)
Hence N2​=0or2N1​+γN2​=0​, with N3​ arbitrary. Every segment in the cube parallel to one of these directions preserves length; there is no restriction on its starting point other than remaining in the cube.
The two in-plane stretches are v±​=(4+γ2​±γ)/2, and the third is one. A maximizing direction for γ>0 is N∥(1,v+​,0), since the C eigenvalue equation gives N2​/N1​=(v+2​−1)/γ=v+​. Therefore N→±(1,1,0)/2​​ as γ↓0. At exactly zero shear all directions tie; the limit refers to the selected maximizing direction for positive shear. These are the length-preserving directions and principal directions of simple shear.

Cauchy's stress hypothesis says that traction across a cut depends locally on its point, time and oriented normal, rather than on the global shape of the cut. Here all areas and normals are referential: E is a fixed material region in reference coordinates, ρR​ its mass density per reference volume, v the current particle velocity, b body force per unit mass, and sR​ force per unit reference area. The time derivative on the left is total material momentum rate; the two right terms are contact and body forces.
A shrinking pillbox gives sR​(−N)=−sR​(N). In a shrinking tetrahedron the volume contributions scale as its linear size cubed, whereas face forces scale as size squared. Their leading balance expresses traction on an arbitrary face as the normal-component-weighted sum of tractions on three coordinate faces. Defining those three tractions as tensor columns gives sR​(N)=TR​N​. Applying the divergence theorem to momentum balance and localizing over arbitrary E then yields
ρR​v˙=DivTR​+ρR​b.​
(5)
The referential tensor is the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress and need not be symmetric; it should not be silently identified with the spatial Cauchy stress.
Incompressibility is detF=1​. For a homogeneous hyperelastic material the referential constitutive relation becomes
TR​=DW(F)−pcofF=DW(F)−pF−T(detF=1),​
(6)
where the undetermined scalar pressure p enforces the constraint. Homogeneity removes explicit reference-position dependence from W, not the possible spatial variation of that pressure.
For the neo-Hookean energy, DW=2αF. For the positive diagonal ansatz the nominal principal stresses are ti​=2αvi​−p/vi​. Since F is constant, equilibrium requires cofF∇p=0, so p is constant. Stress-free endfaces give p=2αv32​>0​. The side normals include (0,−1,0) and ±(3​/2,1/2,0), so their prescribed tractions force t1​=t2​=T​.
For fixed positive p, the scalar function 2αv−p/v is strictly increasing on v>0. Thus equality of the two nominal stresses implies v1​=v2​=v. Incompressibility gives v3​=v−2, and substitution gives
T=2α(v−v−5),p=2αv−4.​
(7)
The load function has derivative 2α(1+5v−6)>0, tends to minus infinity as v↓0 and to infinity as v→∞. It therefore determines exactly one positive v for every specified load, with v≥1 for outward tensile T≥0. The resulting constant stress satisfies equilibrium and every prescribed face traction, proving existence and uniqueness within the requested positive diagonal class. It does not claim uniqueness among all possible nonhomogeneous deformations. This is the equibiaxial dead loading of a neo-Hookean prism.

Put n=x/R and q=r(R)/R. Differentiating y=qx uses ∇R=n and ∇q=(r′−q)n/R. Hence
F=qI+(r′−q)n⊗n.​
(8)
Its radial eigenvalue is r′ and its two tangential eigenvalues are q. They are positive by hypothesis, so they are also the principal stretches:
v1​=r′,v2​=v3​=q.​
(9)
For an isotropic stored energy the nominal stress has the same radial/tangential projectors,
P=DW(F)=Φ1​n⊗n+Φ2​(I−n⊗n),
(10)
where symmetry of Φ gives equal tangential derivatives. Since Div(n⊗n)=2n/R and the scalar coefficients depend only on R,
DivP=[dRdΦ1​​+R2​(Φ1​−Φ2​)]n.
(11)
Thus equilibrium is exactly dΦ1​/dR=2(Φ2​−Φ1​)/R​.
For the particular energy define δ=r′q2=detF>0. Its two derivatives are
Φ1​=μ+h′(δ)q2,Φ2​=μ+h′(δ)r′q.
(12)
Also q′=(r′−q)/R. Differentiating the first formula and substituting the equilibrium equation gives
h′′(δ)δ′q2+2h′(δ)qq′=2h′(δ)q(r′−q)/R=2h′(δ)qq′.
(13)
The last terms cancel. Strict positivity of h′′ and q yields δ′=0​, so r2r′=DR2 for one positive constant D. Integration gives r3=DR3+C.
The displacement data mean r(1)=λ1​ and r(2)=2λ2​, not r(2)=λ2​. Solving the two scalar equations gives
D=78λ23​−λ13​​,C=78(λ13​−λ23​)​,r=(DR3+C)1/3.​
(14)
The given inequality makes D>0. Since r3 increases from λ13​>0 to 8λ23​, it is positive on the whole shell, and r′=DR2/r2>0. Thus the formula is smooth and admissible and satisfies equilibrium. Conversely every admissible radial equilibrium had to have this constant determinant and these two integration constants, proving uniqueness. This is a constant-Jacobian radial equilibrium for a stretch-sum energy.

On each half-space the deformation is affine. Continuity on the separating plane makes the two affine constants equal and gives (A−B)t=0 for every tangent vector t⊥N. Decomposing any x into its tangential part and (x⋅N)N gives
(A−B)x=[(A−B)N](N⋅x),A−B=a⊗N,a=(A−B)N.​
(15)
This is the Hadamard jump condition. Since B=A−a⊗N=A(I−A−1a⊗N), the matrix determinant lemma gives
detB=detA(1−N⋅A−1a).
(16)
Both determinants are positive, hence A−1a⋅N<1​.
Write a1​=η1​, a2​=η2​, S=a12​+a22​ and Δ=a22​−a12​=0. The two proposed angles have c=2a1​a2​/S and s=κΔ/S, and c2+s2=1. Therefore the displayed rotations lie in SO(3). Direct multiplication gives
RU2​−U1​=SΔ​​−a2​κa1​0​−κa2​a1​0​000​​=SΔ​(−a2​,κa1​,0)⊗(1,κ,0).​
(17)
This is a rank-one connection for each sign. With a unit normal one may choose N=(1,κ,0)/2​ and jump vector a=2​Δ(−a2​,κa1​,0)/S. The two rotations are distinct because Δ=0; the permitted at-most-two result therefore proves these are all solutions. Flipping both a and N changes no connection.
It remains to test the material plane. Because y(0)=0, the affine constants vanish. Let t=(−p2​,p1​,0), so the plane is spanned by t and e3​. Both deformation gradients send e3​ to a1​e3​. If the plane is not the twin plane, it cuts the two half-spaces; its two image halves are coplanar exactly when the in-plane vectors Bt and At are parallel. Put d=(−a2​,κa1​) and n=(1,κ) in their two-dimensional sections. Then
2det​(Bt,At)​=SΔ​2det​(Bt,d)(n⋅t)=Sκa1​a2​Δ​(p1​−κp2​)2​.​
(18)
Its prefactor is nonzero, so coplanarity requires p1​=κp2​, precisely p∥(1,κ,0)∥N. Conversely, on that twin plane (A−B)x=0, so the two affine maps agree and its image is a plane. Thus Π remains plane⟺p∥N​. All other allowed planes acquire a kink along their intersection with the twin. This is the planarity test across a cubic-to-tetragonal twin.
