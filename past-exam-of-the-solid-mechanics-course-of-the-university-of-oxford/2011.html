a OurBigBook.com
A homogeneous hyperelastic material has a strain energy density per unit reference volume W(F) depending on the deformation gradient, with no explicit dependence on the reference configuration. For orientation-preserving gradients F∈GL+(3), its first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is the derivative:
T(F)=DF​W(F),TiA​=∂FiA​∂W​(F).​
(1)
Equivalently the infinitesimal stored-energy change is DW(F)[H]=T:H. Reference and spatial indices are distinguished here by upper- and lowercase index letters.
The material symmetry group may be written GW​={H∈GL+(3):W(FH)=W(F) for every F∈GL+(3)}. A volume-preserving relabelling convention restricts the candidate H to SL(3); the material isotropy criterion is the same. The material has material isotropy when SO(3)⊆GW​, meaning W(FQ)=W(F) for every proper rotation Q. If only rotational symmetries are included in the definition of the group, material isotropy means that group equals SO(3). This right-rotation invariance is distinct from material frame indifference, the left-rotation invariance W(QF)=W(F).
The polar decomposition in continuum mechanics gives the unique factorizations
F=RU=VR,R∈SO(3),U=(FTF)1/2,V=(FFT)1/2,​
(2)
where U,V are positive-definite symmetric matrices. Their common positive eigenvalues λ1​,λ2​,λ3​ are the principal stretches, equivalently the singular values of F. They describe the local stretch magnitudes after separating out a rigid rotation.

det(tI−B)=t3−IB​t2+IIB​t−IIIB​.

Take a singular value decomposition F=Q1​DQ2T​, with D=diag(λ1​,λ2​,λ3​) positive and Q1​,Q2​∈SO(3). Such a choice is possible because detF>0. Left material frame indifference removes Q1​, and right material isotropy removes Q2​, giving W(F)=W(D).
Moreover W(D) is unchanged when the positive diagonal entries are permuted. Every permutation can be implemented by a signed permutation matrix of determinant one; the signs disappear in conjugating a diagonal matrix. Thus the energy depends only on the unordered principal stretches.
The three invariants determine precisely this unordered triple: the roots of t3−IB​t2+IIB​t−IIIB​ are λ12​,λ22​,λ32​, and their positive square roots give the stretches. Consequently, on the set of admissible invariant triples, define h(IB​,IIB​,IIIB​) to be W(D). The preceding argument proves that this is well defined and that
W(F)=h(IB​,IIB​,IIIB​).​
(7)
Not every positive triple is realizable by three positive roots. Since the requested statement only asserts existence of a function on (0,∞)3, extend h arbitrarily on the nonrealizable triples; no choice there affects the formula for a deformation in continuum mechanics. Smooth invariant representations, where needed for differentiating the constitutive equation, are understood locally on the realizable set.

Retain the material frame indifference assumption used in part (c), in addition to material isotropy. Put p=u,1​, q=u,2​ and s=p2+q2. For this antiplane shear,
F=​10p​01q​001​​,B=​10p​01q​pq1+s​​,J=1.
(8)
The invariant identities give IB​=IIB​=3+s and IIIB​=1. Hence the restriction of the strain energy density is
W(Dy)=w(∣Du∣2),w(s)=h(3+s,3+s,1).​
(9)
Antiplane energy does not determine the full stress: the restriction gives one scalar energy along antiplane gradients. It does not determine the full stress derivative with respect to every entry of F.
Indeed, using the isotropic hyperelastic stress invariant representation and differentiating the energy, with hj​ denoting the corresponding partial derivatives, gives
DW=2h1​F+2h2​(IB​I−B)F+2IIIB​h3​F−T.
(10)
Using S=J−1TFT, the general objective isotropic Cauchy stress tensor is
S=J2​[h1​B+h2​(IB​B−B2)+IIIB​h3​I].​
(11)
All derivatives are evaluated at the current invariant triple. The extra terms generally remain nonzero on antiplane gradients, so the printed assertion that the full stress must be βB does not follow.
A stress-free, objective isotropic counterexample is the compressible neo-Hookean solid W(F)=2μ​(∣F∣2−3)−μlogJ, with μ>0. Its derivative is T=μF−μF−T, so on antiplane gradients
S=μ(B−I).
(12)
Take u(x1​,x2​)=x1​. Then B22​=1 but S22​=0, whereas B13​=1 and S13​=μ. The diagonal entry would force β=0, contradicting the shear entry. Thus this is not a matter of choosing the right scalar coefficient.
For the additional one-invariant constitutive assumption W(F)=w(IB​), the stronger formula does hold, with
β(IB​,IIIB​)=IIIB​​2w′(IB​)​.​
(13)
This is a valid special case, rather than a consequence of material isotropy alone. For the general restricted energy, the shear entries are still fixed: S31​=2w′(s)u,1​ and S32​=2w′(s)u,2​, since w′=h1​+h2​ along (3+s,3+s,1). The full normal stress needs the constitutive information that the printed claim omits. If material isotropy alone is interpreted without material frame indifference, even the first reduction to ∣Du∣2 needs that additional material frame indifference assumption.

In the classical smooth setting, a deformation in continuum mechanics is a sufficiently regular, injective map y:Ω→R3 with detDy>0. Injectivity excludes material interpenetration, and a positive determinant preserves orientation and local volume. We use C1 regularity for these kinematics and additional differentiability where stress-divergence identities are applied.
Cauchy's stress theorem states that the contact force on an oriented surface element at a current point depends on the point and its unit normal, rather than on the shape of the chosen subbody. Thus the contact force on the boundary of a current subbody v has the form ∫∂v​t(z,n)da. Static force balance, for a body force b per unit current volume, is
∫∂v​t(z,n)da+∫v​b(z)dv=0
(14)
for every subbody. A shrinking pillbox gives t(z,−n)=−t(z,n); a shrinking tetrahedron then gives linear dependence on the normal, t(z,n)=S(z)n. Applying the divergence theorem and using arbitrary subbodies gives
t(z,n)=S(z)n,divS+b=0.​
(15)
If body force is specified per unit mass instead, replace b by ρb. Symmetry of S follows additionally from conservation of angular momentum in the absence of body couples; it is not a consequence of force balance alone.

The surface element relation is Nanson's formula. The force on a corresponding pair of current and reference surface elements must agree:
tda=(S∘y)nda=(S∘y)cofFNdA=TNdA.
(16)
Therefore the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is
T=(S∘y)cofF=J(S∘y)F−T.​
(17)
It measures force per reference area, with a current force direction and a reference normal direction.
In components, differentiate TiA​=Sij​(y(x))(cofF)jA​. The Piola identity says ∂A​(cofDy)jA​=0. One proof writes the cofactor matrix as 21​εjkl​εABC​yk,B​yl,C​; differentiating in A produces symmetric second derivatives contracted against antisymmetric epsilon symbols, hence zero. The chain rule consequently gives
∂A​TiA​=∂yk​∂Sij​​(y)FkA​(cofF)jA​=J∂yj​∂Sij​​(y),
(18)
since FkA​(cofF)jA​=Jδkj​. Thus, with the coordinate composition made explicit,
Divx​T=J(divy​S)∘y.​
(19)
The distinction between reference divergence and current divergence is essential to this identity.

For R=∣x∣>0, DR=θ and Dθ=(I−θ⊗θ)/R. Differentiating the spherically symmetric deformation gives
F=r′(R)θ⊗θ+Rr(R)​(I−θ⊗θ).​
(20)
For an orientation-preserving spherically symmetric deformation with positive radius, r>0 and r′>0. The radial direction and its perpendicular plane are eigenspaces of this positive symmetric matrix, so
λ1​=r′(R),λ2​=λ3​=r(R)/R.​
(21)
At a positive diagonal matrix D, every diagonal rotation with two minus signs commutes with D. The supplied equivariance relation therefore forces T(D) to be a diagonal matrix: each off-diagonal entry changes sign under one such rotation while D stays fixed. Varying a positive diagonal entry shows that the diagonal entries of T(D) are Φ,j​. Permutation symmetry of Φ implies Φ,2​=Φ,3​ when λ2​=λ3​.
Choose a rotation whose first column is θ. Applying equivariance to F=QDQT now gives
T=Φ,1​θ⊗θ+Φ,2​(I−θ⊗θ),​
(22)
with the derivatives evaluated at (r′,r/R,r/R). Thus the stress has one radial and one repeated tangential principal value, independently of the choice of tangent basis.

The Green-Lagrange strain tensor has eigenvalues (λj2​−1)/2. Its Frobenius norm and trace are invariant under the orthogonal principal-axis change, so
W(F)=4μ​j=1∑3​(λj2​−1)2+8λ​(j=1∑3​λj2​−3)2.​
(23)
For the homogeneous dilation F=ρI, put K=μ+3λ/2. Then E=(ρ2−1)I/2. Differentiating the energy through E gives the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor 2μE+λtr(E)I=K(ρ2−1)I, and multiplication by F gives the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor. Thus
T=ρK(ρ2−1)I,S=J−1TFT=ρK(ρ2−1)​I.​
(24)
The normals of the reference and deformed spheres point in the same radial direction. As ρ↓0, the reference traction magnitude ∣TN∣ tends to zero, while the current traction magnitude ∣Sn∣ tends to infinity, provided K>0, as for physically stable Lamé parameters. The negative signs of the stress coefficients for 0<ρ<1 indicate compression.
There is no contradiction: the current area is ρ2 times the reference area, so the same force divided by the vanishing current area has the larger stress. Indeed TN=ρ2Sn. The limiting map at ρ=0 is not itself an admissible deformation in continuum mechanics; the statement concerns the approach through positive ρ. A degenerate choice K=0 would not have the asserted growth without bound.

a OurBigBook.com
The displacement field is u(x)=y(x)−x, and its gradient is Du=Dy−I. Linear elasticity expands about an undeformed stress-free reference state, taking y=x+ϵv with ∣Du∣≪1 and external loads of the same small order. A rigid translation need not itself be small; it is the deformation gradient that controls the infinitesimal strain tensor.
For W∈C2 with DW(I)=0, a Taylor series gives
W(I+H)=W(I)+21​AiAjB​HiA​HjB​+o(∣H∣2),AiAjB​=∂FiA​∂FjB​∂2W​(I).
(25)
The constant W(I) can be set to zero. The tensor A is the elasticity tensor. Commutation of continuous mixed partial derivatives proves its major symmetry:
AiAjB​=AjBiA​.​
(26)
Material frame indifference at a stress-free reference also makes the quadratic energy depend only on the infinitesimal strain tensor e=(H+HT)/2, leading to the usual minor symmetries after spatial and reference indices are identified.
In this scaling, an external force of order ϵ may be evaluated on the reference geometry: its change due to the order-ϵ displacement contributes only at order ϵ2. Thus the leading forces are fixed dead loads. Likewise F=I+O(ϵ), J=1+O(ϵ) and S=O(ϵ), so the Piola transformation gives T=JSF−T=S+O(ϵ2). Hence Cauchy and first Piola stresses agree to linear order. This reasoning uses the usual small-load, stress-free linearization; an order-one prestress or leading follower-load effect requires a different expansion.

Let the boundary split into a displacement part ΓD​ and a traction part ΓN​. The linearized stored elastic energy functional is
Ilin[u]=21​∫Ω​AiAjB​ui,A​uj,B​dx.​
(27)
Its corresponding total potential subtracts the work of fixed body and surface loads:
Π[u]=Ilin[u]−∫Ω​f⋅udx−∫ΓN​​g⋅udA.
(28)
The problem in the calculus of variations is to minimize Π among admissible displacements with u=uD​ on ΓD​. For a stable quadratic elastic energy this is the usual energy minimization formulation. Its stationarity condition suffices to derive equilibrium.
Vary by u+sv, with v=0 on ΓD​. Major symmetry combines the two differentiated quadratic terms, giving
∫Ω​Slin:Dvdx=∫Ω​f⋅vdx+∫ΓN​​g⋅vdA,SiAlin​=AiAjB​uj,B​.
(29)
Integration by parts, first with test functions compactly supported in the interior, gives −DivSlin=f. Then arbitrary traces on ΓN​ give the natural traction condition. The complete boundary value problem is
−DivSlin=f in Ω,SlinN=g on ΓN​,u=uD​ on ΓD​.​
(30)
For a homogeneous hyperelastic material the elasticity tensor is constant. In isotropic linear elasticity the constitutive equation is Slin=λdivuI+μ(Du+DuT), giving the familiar Navier-Cauchy equation −μΔu−(λ+μ)∇divu=f.

Taking traces of the constitutive equation gives
λtreλ​=31​(trSλ​−2μtreλ​).
(31)
The assumed finite limits of stress and strain make the right side bounded. Therefore treλ​=O(λ−1), and the limiting strain satisfies tre=0. The permitted interchange of derivatives and limits gives tre=divu. Define
q=limλ→∞​λtreλ​=31​trS,p=−q.
(32)
Then the limit stress is S=−pI+2μe. Passing to the limit in force balance and using 2Dive=Δu+∇divu gives
−μΔu+∇p=f,divu=0in Ω,u=0 on ∂Ω.​
(33)
The pressure acts as the Lagrange multiplier enforcing the volume constraint. The differential equations determine it up to an additive constant, while a specified absolute limit stress can fix that constant.
Nonlinear elastic incompressibility is detDy=1. Expanding the determinant for Dy=I+Du gives det(I+Du)=1+divu+O(∣Du∣2), so its first-order form is precisely divu=0. The volumetric energy term λ(divu)2/2 becomes increasingly expensive as λ grows, while the fixed positive μ leaves shear strain possible. Thus the infinite-bulk-stiffness limit is the incompressible limit of isotropic linear elasticity, with the same equilibrium operator as the steady Stokes equations.
