Solution

Words: 163
a OurBigBook.com
A homogeneous hyperelastic material has a stored energy per reference volume W(F) depending on the local deformation gradient but not explicitly on the material point. Its first Piola-Kirchhoff response is
T(F)=DW(F),Tiα​=∂W/∂Fiα​.​
(1)
A superposed rigid rotation changes the observer's description without changing the energy of the deformation. Material frame indifference is therefore
W(QF)=W(F)(F∈M+3×3​, Q∈SO(3)).​
(2)
This left-rotation invariance is distinct from a material isotropy assumption on right rotations. Differentiate it in an arbitrary matrix direction H:
DW(QF):(QH)=DW(F):H.
(3)
The Frobenius inner product then gives QTT(QF)=T(F), proving T(QF)=QT(F).
For the converse, that stress covariance makes the derivative in F of W(QF)−W(F) zero. The positive-determinant matrices form a connected set, as also follows from polar decomposition, so this difference is a constant c(Q) independent of F. Composition gives c(Q1​Q2​)=c(Q1​)+c(Q2​). For fixed F, continuity of W and compactness of SO(3) make c bounded. But c(Qn)=nc(Q) for every positive integer, forcing c(Q)=0. Thus stress covariance and frame indifference of the energy are equivalent. Compactness removes the possible additive energy constant in the converse.

For small displacement gradients write F=I+H. Taylor expansion around the stress-free reference state gives
W(I+H)=W(I)+21​Ciαjβ​Hiα​Hjβ​+o(∣H∣2),Ciαjβ​=D2W(I)iαjβ​.
(4)
The linear term vanishes because DW(I)=0. Drop the irrelevant constant and retain the quadratic term:
Wlin(H)=21​Ciαjβ​Hiα​Hjβ​.​
(5)
The major symmetry Ciαjβ​=Cjβiα​​ follows from equality of mixed second derivatives. For a skew matrix K, stress covariance along etK gives
T(etK)=etKT(I)=0.
(6)
Differentiation at zero yields DT(I)[K]=C[K]=0. Thus Ciαjβ​Kjβ​=0 for every skew matrix, which forces the input minor symmetry Ciαjβ​=Ciαβj​. Combining it with the major symmetry gives the output minor symmetry as well:
Ciαjβ​=Cαijβ​=Ciαβj​.​
(7)
These identify reference and spatial indices at F=I. Decompose H=e+K into symmetric and skew parts. Since C[K]=0 and the major symmetry also eliminates the other cross term,
Wlin(H)=21​C[e,e],e=(H+HT)/2.​
(8)
Thus infinitesimal rotations cost no energy in the quadratic approximation.

As a function of the full deformation gradient, use e=sym(F−I). Let Q=diag(1,−1,−1), a proper rotation. Assumed full frame indifference would give W(Q)=W(I)=0. For Fs​=I+s(Q−I), its infinitesimal strain is ssym(Q−I). Quadratic homogeneity therefore gives
W(Fs​)=s2W(Q)=0.
(9)
Choose s=1/4. Then
Fs​=diag(1,1/2,1/2),detFs​=1/4=1,W(Fs​)=0.​
(10)
The homogeneous deformation y(X)=Fs​X changes volume but has zero energy. This illustrates why a nondegenerate small-strain quadratic energy cannot also be imposed as a fully frame-indifferent finite-deformation energy.

a OurBigBook.com
A deformation maps a material point in the reference body to its current position, y:Ω→R3, with deformation gradient F=Dy. A homogeneous deformation has constant gradient and is affine, y(X)=FX+b.
If detF>0, the matrix FTF is symmetric positive definite. The matrix-square-root theorem says that any such matrix has a unique symmetric positive definite square root, obtained by taking positive square roots in its orthogonal spectral decomposition. Set
U=(FTF)1/2,R=FU−1.​
(11)
Then RTR=U−1FTFU−1=I and detR=detF/detU>0, so R∈SO(3) and F=RU. If also F=RU, then FTF=U2; uniqueness of the positive square root gives U=U and then R=R. This proves existence and uniqueness of polar decomposition.
The principal stretches are the positive eigenvalues of U, equivalently the singular values of F or square roots of the eigenvalues of FTF.

For R>0, differentiation gives ∂j​R=θj​ and ∂j​θi​=(δij​−θi​θj​)/R. Hence
Dy=Rr​(I−θ⊗θ)+r′θ⊗θ.​
(12)
The two matrices are the orthogonal projections onto the tangent plane of the reference sphere and its radial line. The gradient multiplies the radial direction by r′ and each tangent direction by r/R. With positive r,r′, it is itself symmetric positive definite, so its eigenvalues are its principal stretches:
λ1​=r′,λ2​=λ3​=r/R.​
(13)

c OurBigBook.com
The determinant condition is r′(r/R)2=1, or r2r′=R2. Integration gives r3=R3+C and the outer boundary value fixes C=t3−1. Thus
r(R)=(R3+t3−1)1/3.​
(14)
For t>1, r(R) tends to the positive cavity radius (t3−1)1/3 as R↓0. Its tangential stretches grow like that radius divided by R, so ∣F∣→∞. The deformation is singular at the origin and is interpreted as a finite-energy Sobolev deformation, with a point value irrelevant to its energy.
Since r′=R2/r2 and the determinant penalty is zero almost everywhere,
Ecav​(t)=4π∫01​(r(R)4R6​+2r(R)2)dR.​
(15)
For fixed t>1, the first integrand is O(R6) near zero and the second is bounded. Thus the energy is finite despite the unbounded gradient. In particular the gradient is square integrable, as required for this competitor.
For large t, r(R)/t→1 uniformly on [0,1]. The first integral is O(t−4), while ∫01​r(R)2dR∼t2. Consequently
Ecav​(t)∼8πt2.​
(16)
The spherical volume factor R2dR is what makes the R−1 tangential-gradient singularity integrable in three dimensions.

d OurBigBook.com
Choose the homogeneous dilation r(R)=tR​, which has the required endpoint values. For a radial field, put J=r′r2/R2. The radial energy density, after omitting its constant angular factor, is R2[r′2+2r2/R2+(J−1)2]. Its Euler equation simplifies to
R2r′′+2Rr′−2r+r2J′=0.
(17)
The chosen dilation has r′′=0, J=t3 constant, and satisfies it. More directly, the full first Piola-Kirchhoff response for the given energy is
T(F)=2F+2(detF−1)cofF.
(18)
At F=tI this is [2t+2(t3−1)t2]I, constant throughout the body. Its divergence vanishes, so the deformation satisfies the full force-free equilibrium equations as well.
Its energy is
Ehom​(t)=34π​[3t2+(t3−1)2].​
(19)
This grows like (4π/3)t6, whereas the same-boundary cavitating competitor has energy asymptotic to 8πt2. Therefore for sufficiently large t, the homogeneous equilibrium is not a global energy minimum in the finite-energy class admitting the cavitating deformation.
The admissible class matters: the competitor from the preceding part is not continuous at the center. This comparison is in that Sobolev class, not a claim about minimization restricted to smooth cavity-free deformations.

An antiplane displacement has the form v(x1​,x2​,x3​)=(0,0,u(x1​,x2​)), independent of the out-of-plane coordinate. Only e13​=e31​=ux1​​/2 and e23​=e32​=ux2​​/2 are nonzero, so its strain energy per unit out-of-plane thickness is μ∣∇u∣2/2.
The equilibrium minimizes total potential energy
Es​(u)=∫ω∖Ks​​(2μ​∣∇u∣2−fu)dx,u∈H1(ω∖Ks​),u=uD​ on ΓD​.​
(20)
Here f is the out-of-plane body-force component; the two crack faces have independent traces. For a variation η with zero outer-boundary trace, stationarity gives
μ∫∇u⋅∇η=∫fη.
(21)
Integrating by parts first for interior test functions and then for variations free on either crack face yields
−μΔu=f in ω∖Ks​,u=uD​ on ΓD​,μ∂n​u=0 on both crack faces.​
(22)
These are the weak equilibrium equation, prescribed displacement, and zero traction. The tip behavior is selected by finite energy; it is not an additional prescribed tip value.

Use −π<θs​<π, with the crack along the negative radial axis. In the coefficient convention used here, the leading crack-tip expansion is
u(s)=ureg​+ks​Rs1/2​sin(θs​/2),∇ureg​=o(Rs−1/2​).​
(23)
The square-root term is harmonic and has zero angular derivative on both crack faces. Regular contributions may include a constant and bounded-gradient terms under smooth loading.
If E(s) is the minimized elastic-plus-load potential, the energy release rate is G(s)=−dE(s)/ds​. With this particular square-root coefficient normalization,
G(s)=4πμ​ks2​.​
(24)
For example, the small-circle energy flux is ∫[Wn1​−μur​ux1​​]Rs​dθs​. The singular term gives W=μks2​/(8Rs​), ur​=ks​sin(θs​/2)/(2Rs​​) and ux1​​=−ks​sin(θs​/2)/(2Rs​​). Integration over (−π,π) produces the stated factor. Other stress-intensity normalizations change that factor.
For toughness Gc​>0, local stability against infinitesimal extension means G(s)≤Gc​​, since the derivative of E(s)+Gc​(s+1) is nonnegative. Global energetic stability against every extension is the stronger condition E(s)≤E(r)+Gc​(r−s) for all r≥s.
In full isotropic linearized elasticity,
Tlin=λtr(e)I+2μe.​
(25)
For the antiplane field this becomes
Tlin=μ​00ux1​​​00ux2​​​ux1​​ux2​​0​​,∣Tlin∣2=2μ2∣∇u∣2.
(26)
Ahead of the tip, θs​=0, the singular gradient is (0,ks​/(2Rs​​)). Thus
∣Tlin∣θs​=0​=2​μ∣ks​∣​Rs−1/2​+o(Rs−1/2​).​
(27)
This is stronger than the printed remainder o(Rs−1​). If ks​ denotes a nonnegative intensity, it has the printed leading coefficient; for a signed singular coefficient its absolute value is essential because the left side is a norm.

c OurBigBook.com
For a differentiable crack path, the local quasistatic Griffith laws with constant positive toughness are
ℓ˙(t)≥0,G(ℓ(t),t)≤Gc​,ℓ˙(t)[Gc​−G(ℓ(t),t)]=0.​
(28)
They express irreversibility, local stability, and energy balance during growth. A strictly subcritical release rate forces the crack to stop.
Fix a crack parameter s and let u(s,t) denote the equilibrium displacement with the prescribed time-dependent loads. The scalar equilibrium problem is linear and has a unique solution for its fixed Dirichlet data. Both body force and boundary displacement scale by t, so
u(s,t)=tu(s,1).​
(29)
The minimized potential energy is quadratic under this scaling: the strain term scales by t2 and so does the load term −f(t)u(s,t). Hence E(s,t)=t2E(s,1) and
G(s,t)=t2G(s,1).​
(30)
Equivalently the crack-tip coefficient scales by t and its squared energy-release formula gives the same result.
Put s0​=ℓ(0). Finiteness and continuity of G(s,1) make it bounded by some finite M≥0 on a neighborhood of s0​. Continuity of ℓ supplies a positive interval on which ℓ(t) stays in that neighborhood. Shorten it to [0,t0​] so that t02​M<Gc​. Then G(ℓ(t),t)≤t02​M<Gc​, and complementarity forces ℓ˙(t)=0 throughout. Therefore
ℓ(t)=ℓ(0)(0≤t≤t0​).​
(31)
No differentiating an unknown crack path or assuming its length already constant is needed; its continuity first gives the local release-rate bound.
