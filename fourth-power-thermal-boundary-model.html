Fourth-power thermal boundary model (q=Σ(Th4​−Tc4​))

 ... Statistical physics Thermodynamics Temperature Cryogenics Thermal boundary resistance Kapitza resistance
OurBigBook.com Words: 502
Choose a positive constant Σ and the illustrative interface law
q=Σ(Th4​−Tc4​),[Σ]=Wm−2K−4,Th​≥Tc​>0.
(37)
Here Th​ and Tc​ are the hot and cold interface-side temperatures, not distant thermometer readings. This is a low-temperature phonon model under restrictive assumptions, not a universal fit to Kapitza's measured exponent or a material-property table. Its coefficient Σ is not the Stefan-Boltzmann constant: the carriers in this model are phonons, rather than photons crossing a vacuum.
The fourth power has a simple counting explanation. In the three-dimensional low-temperature Debye model, a linear phonon dispersion relation gives a density of states proportional to ω2. A phonon has energy ℏω and Bose-Einstein distribution occupation [exp(ℏω/kB​T)−1]−1. If propagation speeds and the angle-averaged transmission introduce no additional frequency scale over the occupied modes, the outgoing energy flux has the form
J(T)∝∫0∞​eℏω/(kB​T)−1ω3dω​=(ℏkB​T​)4∫0∞​ex−1x3dx​∝T4.
(38)
Extending the upper integration limit assumes occupied modes lie well below the cutoff. Balancing the two outgoing fluxes, with coefficients satisfying zero net heat flux at equal temperatures, gives the chosen difference of fourth powers. Frequency-dependent transmission, different excitations or additional transport channels can change this conclusion.
The finite-drop area-specific thermal resistance follows by factoring a difference of powers:
rK,sec​=qTh​−Tc​​=Σ(Th​+Tc​)(Th2​+Tc2​)1​.
(39)
Near thermal equilibrium, write Th​=T+δT and Tc​=T. Expansion gives q=4ΣT3δT+O((δT)2), so the differential area-specific Kapitza resistance is
RK​(T)=limδT→0​q(T+δT,T)δT​=4ΣT31​[Km2W−1],RK​(T)=ARK​(T)​[KW−1].
(40)
At a finite bias, varying only the hot side gives (∂q/∂Th​)−1=1/(4ΣTh3​); this is generally different from rK,sec​. Stating what is held fixed makes a differential resistance reproducible.
For an original normalized example, choose reference scales T0​>0 and A0​>0, and set
θh​=T0​Th​​=1.2,θc​=T0​Tc​​=1,j=ΣT04​q​=1.24−1=1.0736.
(41)
For contact area A=2A0​, the total heat transfer rate is Q˙​=2.1472A0​ΣT04​. The finite-drop resistance satisfies ΣT03​rK,sec​=0.2/1.0736=125/671≈0.18629. Using the equilibrium tangent at T0​ instead would predict j=4(0.2)=0.8, illustrating why a substantial temperature rise requires the nonlinear law. Doubling the common equilibrium temperature reduces RK​ by eight in this chosen model.
A series check can be done without guessing any material properties. Stipulate a hot-side slab with dimensionless areal bulk resistance bs​=ΣT03​Ls​/ks​=0.10, and neglect the cold-side bulk drop. At the same j=1.0736, the hot reservoir must have TH​/T0​=1.2+bs​j=1.30736. The imposed reservoir drop splits into 0.10736 in the bulk and 0.20 at the interface. Applying the fourth-power law directly to TH​ would assign the bulk drop to the interface and overestimate the heat flux.
Finally, transfer heat at rate Aq between maintained interface-side temperatures Th​>Tc​>0. The hot side loses entropy at rate Aq/Th​, and the cold side gains it at rate Aq/Tc​. With no interface entropy accumulation, the entropy production per unit area is
AS˙prod​​=q(Tc​1​−Th​1​)=ΣTh​Tc​(Th​−Tc​)2(Th​+Tc​)(Th2​+Tc2​)​>0.
(42)
For the normalized example it is S˙prod​/(AΣT03​)=1.0736(1−1/1.2)=0.178933…. The same energy crosses both sides in a steady state, while total entropy increases, consistently with the Second law of thermodynamics. A finite temperature jump during heat transfer therefore expresses dissipation, not a failure of energy conservation.
