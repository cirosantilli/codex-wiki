Graph minor (H≼G)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Graph theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 7
A graph minor compresses connected regions of a graph into single vertices while retaining selected connections between them. For finite simple graphs, H≼G means that some sequence of deleting vertices, deleting edges, and performing edge contractions produces H up to graph isomorphism. Deleting a vertex also deletes all its incident edges; deleting an edge leaves its endpoints. The edge contraction convention here removes resulting self-loops and duplicate edges.[ref]
For an exact small example, let G be the cycle graph C4​ with vertices u,v,a,b and edges uv,va,ab,bu. Contract uv to a new vertex w. The surviving edges are wa,ab,bw, giving the complete graph K3​. Thus K3​≼C4​, although C4​ has no triangle in a graph as a subgraph. The branch sets of a graph minor are {u,v},{a},{b}, with the original edges va,ab,bu witnessing all three required adjacencies. The first panel of the edge contraction diagram shows this operation.
A subgraph uses existing vertices and edges; a topological minor permits a graph subdivision of the target as a subgraph, so target edges become graph paths with mutually disjoint interiors and no target vertex in an interior. An ordinary graph minor permits whole connected branch sets of a graph minor in place of target vertices. Every topological minor is a graph minor, by contracting the replacement graph paths.[ref]
The converse fails. Take the tree with edges uv,ua,ub,vc,vd. Contracting uv gives the star graph K1,4​. Yet the original tree has maximum degree three, whereas every graph subdivision of K1,4​ retains a centre of degree of a vertex four. Hence K1,4​ is a graph minor but not a topological minor of this tree. The difference is that a connected branch set of a graph minor may gather connections incident to several original vertices.
The counts of vertices and edges never increase under these operations. However, the C4​ example shows that being a bipartite graph is not preserved: its graph minor K3​ contains an odd cycle. Its chromatic number also increases from two to three. A connected graph stays connected under an edge contraction, because the image of each graph path still connects its endpoints, but deletion can disconnect it. Thus one must check the particular graph property before treating it as preserved by taking graph minors.
Being a planar graph is preserved: in a crossing-free drawing, shrink a contracted edge and bring its incident edges into the merged vertex; deletion merely removes parts of the drawing. This makes a nonplanar graph minor a certificate that the original graph is nonplanar. More strongly, for a finite simple graph, being a planar graph is equivalent to having neither the complete graph K5​ nor the complete bipartite graph K3,3​ as a graph minor. Finding the corresponding branch sets of a graph minor settles nonplanarity even when no literal K5​ or K3,3​ subgraph is present. The converse is a substantial theorem, rather than a consequence of preservation alone.[ref]

Topological minor (H≼top​G)

Words: 427
Graph minor OurBigBook.com
A topological minor records a network pattern whose vertices remain individual junctions but whose edges may become longer routes. For finite simple graphs, H≼top​G means that G contains a subgraph that is a graph subdivision of H up to graph isomorphism. Equivalently, choose distinct branch vertices of a graph subdivision bv​∈V(G) for v∈V(H) and a graph path Puv​ from bu​ to bv​ for every uv∈E(H). These are internally vertex-disjoint paths, and no internal vertex of any Puv​ is a branch vertex of a graph subdivision. Only endpoints corresponding to a shared target vertex may be shared.[ref]
The graph subdivision example below replaces two edges of a triangle in a graph by routes of two and three edges while retaining its attached leaf of a graph. If the host graph also contains an extra edge BD, the same routes still witness a topological minor: delete BD when selecting the subgraph. An induced subgraph is not required. However, routes that merely connect the right pairs are insufficient: using a branch vertex of a graph subdivision as an intermediate junction, or making two routes share an internal vertex, can change the local network structure.
Every topological minor is a graph minor. In each branch path of a graph subdivision, contract all but one edge; the disjoint interiors let this shorten each route without merging distinct branch vertices of a graph subdivision. This recovers H after deleting unused vertices and edges.[ref] The converse fails because an ordinary branch set of a graph minor can gather several junctions into one.
For the tree T with vertices u,v,a,b,c,d and edges uv,ua,ub,vc,vd, contracting uv produces a star graph K1,4​: the branch sets of a graph minor are {u,v},{a},{b},{c},{d}. Both u,v have degree of a vertex three; each of the other four vertices is a leaf of a graph with degree of a vertex one. The new centre w has four distinct graph neighbours a,b,c,d; its degree of a vertex is 3+3−2=4, in agreement with the edge contraction formula.
Yet K1,4​ is not a topological minor of T. In a graph subdivision of K1,4​, four branch paths of a graph subdivision must leave the centre through four different edges; sharing their first internal vertex would violate internal disjointness. Thus any host needs a vertex of degree of a vertex at least four, whereas Δ(T)=3. More generally, H≼top​G implies Δ(H)≤Δ(G), where Δ is the maximum degree of a graph. This bound concerns the host graph; extra unused edges may make its branch vertices of a graph subdivision have larger degrees of vertices than their target counterparts.
This distinction matters when a network's individual junctions have a limited number of connections: replacing a connection by a route preserves the number of routes meeting at each chosen junction, while contracting a connected region can combine connections from separate junctions. It also explains why a small nonplanar pattern can hide inside long routes. By Kuratowski's theorem, a finite simple graph is a planar graph exactly when it has neither the complete graph K5​ nor the complete bipartite graph K3,3​ as a topological minor. The obstruction is a graph subdivision inside the network, regardless of the number of intermediate vertices.

Rooted branch-set reduction (m=h−q/2)

Words: 892
Graph minor OurBigBook.com
Let q≥2 be even, let S={x1​,…,xq​}, and suppose a graph has h disjoint nonempty vertex sets C1​,…,Ch​, where h≥d+3q/2. Suppose each G[Ci​] is connected or every one of its graph components meets S, and each Ci​ is adjacent to all but at most d of the other Cj​ not meeting S. Assume also that no rooted separation of a graph of order below q avoids d+1 of these sets. Then there are m=h−q/2 disjoint connected vertex sets D1​,…,Dm​ such that xi​∈Di​ for 1≤i≤q and each of these first q sets is adjacent to all but at most d of Dq+1​,…,Dm​.
Here adjacency of sets means existence of an edge between them. A rooted separation of a graph is (A,B) with A∪B=V(G), S⊆A, no edges between A∖B and B∖A, and order ∣A∩B∣. It avoids Ci​ when A∩Ci​=∅. We prove the reduction by induction, first on the vertex count and then on the edge count; any counterexample chosen minimal in this ordering will be impossible.
We may delete edges joining two vertices of S. They cannot affect rooted separations of a graph, since S lies wholly on the first side. Within a branch set, their deletion can only split a graph component into graph components each containing an endpoint in S, so the branch-set hypothesis also persists. An isolated vertex outside S and all branch sets can be deleted. An isolated vertex v∈S is also impossible, since (S,V(G)∖{v}) has order q−1 and avoids at least h−q≥d+q/2≥d+1 sets. Thus in a minimal counterexample S is independent, and every isolated vertex outside S belongs to a singleton branch set.
Consider a rooted separation of a graph (A,B) of order exactly q avoiding at least d+1 branch sets, and put S′=A∩B. Restrict to G′=G[B]−E(G[S′]) and Ci′​=Ci​∩B. Each Ci′​ is nonempty: any Ci​ not itself avoided is adjacent to at least one of the d+1 avoided sets, and the endpoint of such an edge in Ci​ must lie in B. If a graph component of G′[Ci′​] misses S′, it lies in B∖A and is a whole graph component of G[Ci​], with no vertex of S. The original hypothesis then forces G[Ci​] to be connected and Ci′​=Ci​, entirely outside A. Thus each restricted set is connected or all its graph components meet S′. In particular every Cj′​ missing S′ equals an original set outside A, so all its required adjacencies from Ci′​ persist.
A rooted separation of a graph (A′,B′) of G′ avoiding d+1 restricted sets lifts to (A∪A′,B′) in G, of the same order: an avoided restricted set misses S′, hence is an original set outside A. The edges removed inside S′ do not obstruct lifting, since S′⊆A′. This proves the required separation hypothesis for G′. If G′ is smaller, induction gives the desired connected sets rooted at S′. Moreover G[A] has q disjoint graph paths from S to S′, by the Menger theorem. Indeed a separator of smaller order between these sets would give a rooted separation of a graph of G of smaller order still avoiding those d+1 original sets. Truncate the graph paths at their first visits to S′, label their endpoints accordingly, and adjoin each graph path to the corresponding rooted connected set. They meet B only in their distinct endpoints, so this gives the desired sets for G, a contradiction. Consequently in a minimal counterexample every such separation of order q has B=V(G) and A=S, an independent set.
Now contract any edge whose endpoints do not belong to two different branch sets. Its endpoints cannot both lie in S, so the image of S still has size q. The branch-set conditions persist. If the contraction created a forbidden rooted separation of a graph of order below q, its lift would have order below q, except possibly when the contracted vertex lies in its separator. In that case the lifted order increases by one and is at most q. Equality would put the contracted edge inside A, contradicting the just-proved independence of A for an order-q separation avoiding d+1 sets. Thus the separation hypothesis also persists. Applying induction in the contracted graph and expanding the contracted vertex would produce the required disjoint connected sets. Hence every edge in a minimal counterexample joins two different branch sets.
Every vertex outside the branch sets would be isolated, since every edge joins two branch sets; such an isolated vertex could be deleted. Thus the branch sets cover the graph. Each nonsingleton branch set is therefore an independent set, and all its vertices belong to S, because each of its singleton graph components must meet S. Let C be the union of the nonsingleton branch sets, and write c=∣C∣≤q. There is a matching in a graph from C into V(G)∖S. Otherwise the Hall marriage theorem gives X⊆C with neighbourhood Y⊆V(G)∖S satisfying ∣Y∣<∣X∣. Since S is independent, (S∪Y,V(G)∖X) is a rooted separation of a graph of order q−∣X∣+∣Y∣<q. It avoids every singleton branch set outside S∪Y, of which there are at least
∣V(G)∣−q−∣Y∣≥∣V(G)∣−q−c+1≥h−2c​−q+1≥h−23q​+1≥d+1.
(1)
The penultimate counting bound uses that the nonsingleton sets number at most c/2, so ∣V(G)∣−c≥h−c/2. This contradicts the separation hypothesis.
Use that matching in a graph to make a two-vertex connected set for each terminal in C; terminals outside C remain singleton sets. There are at least
∣V(G)∣−q−c≥h−23q​=m−q
(2)
unused vertices, all singleton original branch sets. Choose m−q of them for the remaining Di​. Every rooted Di​ contains a singleton original branch set: either its terminal, or its terminal's matched neighbour. That singleton misses at most d of the selected nonterminal singleton sets. This constructs the desired family and completes the proof.

Complete graph minor density threshold (c(t))

Words: 100 Articles: 1
Graph minor OurBigBook.com
The complete graph minor density threshold is
c(t)=inf{c:every nonempty finite graph G with e(G)≥c∣G∣ has a Kt​ minor}.
(3)
The density here is edge count divided by order, half the average degree of a vertex. For large t, this threshold has order tlogt​, with the logarithm taken to base e.

Dense minor with bounded order and high minimum degree

For each positive integer k, a nonempty graph satisfying e(G)≥11k∣G∣ has a graph minor H satisfying
∣H∣≤11k+2,2δ(H)≥∣H∣+4k−1.
(4)
The density hypothesis is a lower bound. Reversing its inequality would be false for an edgeless graph. This auxiliary lemma can be used to establish a 7tlogt​ upper bound for the complete graph minor density threshold.

Branch set of a graph minor

Words: 380 Articles: 1
Graph minor OurBigBook.com
A branch set of a graph minor is the connected region representing one target vertex. For finite simple graphs G and H, a witness to H≼G assigns a nonempty set Bx​⊆V(G) to each x∈V(H), such that the sets are pairwise disjoint, each induced subgraph G[Bx​] is a connected graph, and every xy∈E(H) has a witnessing edge of G between Bx​ and By​. Extra edges between branch sets of a graph minor are allowed: they can be deleted. Unused vertices are also allowed.[ref]
In the diagram, the host graph has vertex set {1,2,3,4,5,6} and edges
12, 34, 56, 23, 45, 61, 24.
(5)
Choose BA​={1,2}, BB​={3,4}, and BC​={5,6}. Their internal edges 12,34,56 establish connectedness. The edges 23,45,61 witness AB,BC,CA, respectively, so they form a model of the complete graph K3​. The additional edge 24 also joins BA​ to BB​; it supplies another witness for the same target edge, not a fourth target edge. Contracting 12,34,56 and merging duplicate edges gives exactly {AB,BC,CA}.
The same branch sets of a graph minor also witness the path graph A,B,C: retain the witnesses 23,45, and delete 61 to remove the unwanted AC adjacency. This demonstrates why the definition requires an edge for every target adjacency, without forbidding other adjacencies in the host graph. For a complete graph Kt​, there are no unwanted pairs: its model consists of t disjoint connected branch sets of a graph minor with an edge between every pair.
To turn a model into an operation sequence, keep a spanning tree inside each G[Bx​], keep one witnessing edge for each edge of H, and delete all other edges and unused vertices. Contract each chosen spanning tree to one vertex. Disjointness prevents two target vertices from merging; connectedness provides the edge contractions that merge each individual region. Conversely, track the original vertices merged into every surviving vertex during a deletion-and-edge contraction sequence. They form disjoint connected branch sets of a graph minor, and each surviving edge comes from an original edge between its two regions. A list of these regions, their connecting trees, and their witnessing edges therefore gives a directly checkable certificate.

Random branch-set construction of a complete graph minor

Words: 74
Branch set of a graph minor OurBigBook.com
In a dense graph with many common neighbours for every pair of vertices, choose disjoint random small sets. A set is good if few vertices have no neighbour in it. The Markov inequality controls the number of bad sets, and random sampling makes almost every pair of good sets adjacent. Add unused common neighbours to connect each set and to repair the remaining missing adjacencies. If the number of used vertices stays below every common-neighbour count, this constructs disjoint branch sets of a graph minor representing a large complete graph.

Edge contraction (G/e)

Words: 278
Graph minor OurBigBook.com
An edge contraction merges the two endpoints of an existing edge. For a finite simple graph G and e=uv, replace u,v by a new vertex w. Retain every edge with neither endpoint in {u,v}, and join w to every member of
NG/e​(w)=(NG​(u)∪NG​(v))∖{u,v}.
(6)
Here NG​(u) and NG​(v) denote the original open graph neighbourhoods, and NG/e​(w) is the new graph neighbourhood. Remove the self-loop arising from uv and merge duplicate edges, so the result G/e is again a simple graph. This is not deletion of uv, which keeps both original vertices.[ref]
The left example contracts uv in the cycle graph C4​ to give {wa,ab,bw}. Its endpoints have no common neighbours, so four edges become three. The right example contracts uv in the triangle in a graph with edges uv,ua,va. Its endpoints have the single common neighbour a, so the two edges ua,va become one edge wa: three edges become one.
For any such edge contraction, the exact counts are
∣V(G/e)∣=∣V(G)∣−1,∣E(G/e)∣=∣E(G)∣−1−∣NG​(u)∩NG​(v)∣.
(7)
The contracted edge accounts for one loss. Each common neighbour z contributes exactly one further loss, since uz,vz become a single edge wz. All other edges correspond one-to-one to surviving edges. In particular the new degree of a vertex is
degG/e​(w)=degG​(u)+degG​(v)−2−∣NG​(u)∩NG​(v)∣.
(8)
These formulas depend on simplifying to a simple graph; a multigraph convention that retains duplicate edges has different counts. In the six-vertex branch set of a graph minor diagram, contracting 12 loses one edge, contracting the resulting 34 loses two because of its common neighbour A, and contracting 56 loses one. Hence its seven edges become the three edges of K3​: 7−1−2−1=3.
An edge contraction can increase a degree of a vertex even while decreasing both total counts. For example, contracting uv in the tree with edges uv,ua,ub,vc,vd merges two vertices of degree of a vertex three into one of degree of a vertex four. This is the mechanism behind the distinction between a graph minor and a topological minor.
