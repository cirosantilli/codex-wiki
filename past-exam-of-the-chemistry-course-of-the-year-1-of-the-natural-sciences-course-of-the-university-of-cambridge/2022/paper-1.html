The configurations are C:1s22s22p2 and N:1s22s22p3. A simple complete-core-screening estimate subtracts the two 1s electrons, giving Zeff​≈4 for carbon and 5 for nitrogen. This neglects screening by the other valence electrons, and is an explicitly rough estimate.
A more systematic estimate using Slater's rules includes 0.85 for each 1s electron and 0.35 for each other electron in the 2s,2p group:
Zeff​(C)=6−[2(0.85)+3(0.35)]=3.25,Zeff​(N)=7−[2(0.85)+4(0.35)]=3.90.
(8)
Both models predict the important trend: nitrogen's valence shell experiences a greater effective nuclear charge. There is no unique numerical effective nuclear charge without specifying an orbital and a screening prescription; the one-electron formula in (a) does not by itself define neutral-atom orbital energies.[ref]

c OurBigBook.com
The 2s atomic orbital is spherically symmetric and has one radial node. A real 2p atomic orbital has two lobes of opposite quantum phase, one atomic-orbital nodal plane through the nucleus and no radial node. The three spatial orientations 2px​,2py​,2pz​ remain degenerate in a spherically symmetric atom when external fields and fine structure are neglected.
In a many-electron atom, 2s penetrates the screened core more effectively than 2p, experiences a stronger attraction and is lower in energy: ε2s​<ε2p​. The hydrogen-like ion degeneracy therefore does not survive screening. Both valence orbital types are generally more tightly bound and spatially contracted in nitrogen than in carbon, because of the larger effective nuclear charge. These are central-field orbital statements; the detailed atomic multiplet structure also involves electron repulsion and Hund's rules.

Take the bond axis as z. The heavy-atom 2s and 2pz​ atomic orbitals can mix with hydrogen 1s to make three sigma-symmetry molecular orbitals. In the qualitative valence ordering, these are a low predominantly heavy-atom 2s level, a bonding σ level and a higher σ∗ level. The perpendicular 2px​,2py​ orbitals form a degenerate approximately nonbonding π pair because hydrogen has no valence orbitals of the matching sideways symmetry. The filled heavy-atom 1s core is omitted from the diagram.
Counting valence electrons gives
CH:(σs​)2(σb​)2(πx​,πy​)1,NH:(σs​)2(σb​)2(πx​)1(πy​)1.
(9)
Thus both exhibit paramagnetism: CH has one unpaired electron and NH has two in its ground state. For NH, Hund's rules favour separate parallel-spin occupation of the two degenerate π orbitals. The corresponding observed ground terms are X2Π and X3Σ−; the total bond order is approximately one in this simple sigma-bond/nonbonding-pi description.[ref]
The nitrogen-derived orbital energies lie lower than the corresponding carbon-derived ones on an approximate common energy scale, while the isolated hydrogen 1s reference is unchanged. The bonding orbitals consequently have different polarizations and mixing coefficients; drawing identical energies for C and N would miss the screening trend. The figure specifies ordering and occupancy, not measured gaps or a numerical prediction from (a).

An internally consistent elementary orbital hybridisation model is sp2 at carbon and sp at both nitrogen atoms; the hydrogens use 1s orbitals. The three carbon hybrids form two C–H sigma bonds and one C–N sigma bond, leaving one perpendicular p orbital. The central nitrogen uses opposite sp hybrids for its two collinear sigma connections, leaving two perpendicular p orbitals for the two pi systems.
At terminal nitrogen, one sp hybrid points toward the central nitrogen and the other houses an axial lone pair. One perpendicular p orbital forms the second, N–N-only pi system; the other participates in the three-atom C–N–N pi system. This description remains the same across both resonance contributors; counting lone-pair domains in just one drawing and assigning a different terminal hybridisation to each contributor would obscure the delocalization. orbital hybridisation is a useful choice of localized basis, not a uniquely measurable label: advanced treatments of diazomethane require a superposition of bonding patterns.[ref]

g OurBigBook.com
The four sigma bonds are the two C–H connections, C–N and N–N, containing eight electrons. A terminal-nitrogen axial lone pair contains two more. Of the remaining six electrons, two occupy an N–N pi bond lying in the molecular plane, while four occupy a perpendicular three-centre four-electron pi bond extending over C–N–N. There are therefore 16 valence electrons in total.
For orientation, a three-site model with equal on-site energy α and adjacent interaction β<0 gives perpendicular pi levels at α+2​β, α, and α−2​β. The lower two are doubly occupied and the upper is empty. The middle level has a terminal-end combination with little central-atom amplitude; unequal C and N orbital energies change its coefficients and make it only approximately nonbonding.
The highest occupied molecular orbital is the upper occupied perpendicular pi orbital, with substantial carbon amplitude, broadly above the strongly bonding sigma framework and the lower occupied pi orbitals. It is not the terminal axial nitrogen lone pair. Real diazomethane has C–N bonding and N–N antibonding character in this frontier orbital, rather than being an exactly nonbonding equal-site model. Photoelectron and Rydberg-state experiments support this assignment.[ref]

a OurBigBook.com
For the ordinary ester, the carbon nucleophile of a grignard reagent attacks the carbonyl carbon and moves the C=O pi pair onto oxygen. Collapse of the tetrahedral intermediate expels ethoxide, transiently giving R1COR. This ketone is more electrophilic than the starting ester because it lacks an alkoxy group's stabilizing resonance donation. A second equivalent of the grignard reagent therefore adds to it before isolation:
R1CO2​Et ⟶ R1C(O−)(OEt)R ⟶ R1COR ⟶ R1C(O−)(R)2​ H+​ R1C(OH)(R)2​.
(13)
The negatively charged intermediates are associated with magnesium counterions. Merely reducing the amount of reagent to one equivalent does not generally trap the ketone selectively; it can leave unreacted ester together with the double-addition product.
In the first successful preparation, magnesium insertion into the primary C–Br bond gives n-butylmagnesium bromide. The carbon chain has four carbons, so its acetylation produces hexan-2-one, CH3​CO(CH2​)3​CH3​, not pentan-2-one. Attack on acetic anhydride forms CH3​C(O−)(nBu)OCOCH3​. Coordination of the anhydride-derived oxygens to magnesium can stabilize this tetrahedral addition complex. Under suitably cold, controlled-addition conditions it survives sufficiently to delay release of the free ketone until aqueous work-up has destroyed the remaining grignard reagent. Protonation followed by collapse releases acetate/acetic acid and the ketone.
This interpretation has direct experimental precedent: Melvin S. Newman and William T. Booth Jr. obtained methyl ketones by cold addition of grignard reagents to acetic anhydride, attributing selectivity to a persistent addition complex. The temperature and reagent-addition regime are genuine conditions on the explanation, rather than proof that an anhydride can never undergo overaddition; the exam scheme omits them.[ref]
For the benzonitrile route, n-butyl attacks the nitrile carbon and one C≡N pi pair moves to nitrogen, producing the magnesium ketimine salt PhC(nBu)=N−MgBr+. This anionic nitrogen-containing intermediate resists a second nucleophilic addition much more than a neutral carbonyl does. The ketone PhC(=O)nBu appears only during acidic aqueous work-up, after organomagnesium has been quenched. Protonation gives the iminium ion; water attacks its carbon, proton transfers give a carbinolamine, and C–N cleavage from its nitrogen-protonated form produces the protonated ketone and Ammonia, followed by deprotonation. In the acidic mixture Ammonia is present largely as ammonium. This sequence explains why nitrile addition stops at a ketone without requiring the ketone itself to be resistant to grignard reagents.
The atom-labelled hydrolysis sequence is
PhC(nBu)=N−​2H+​PhC(nBu)=NH2+​H2​O​PhC(nBu)(OH2+​)NH2​proton transfers​PhC(nBu)(OH)NH3+​⟶PhC(nBu)=OH++NH3​⟶PhC(nBu)=O.​
(14)
Water's lone pair attacks the iminium carbon while its C=N pi pair moves to nitrogen; the carbinolamine oxygen then reforms C=O while the C–N pair moves to the departing protonated amino group. Final deprotonation gives the neutral ketone. The arrows involving protonation and hydrolysis also exchange protons with the acidic aqueous reservoir; the condensed sequence tracks the carbon–nitrogen and carbon–oxygen transformations rather than displaying every solvent molecule.

The two starting ester groups are adjacent on the benzene ring. Select one ester carbon as the site receiving both incoming methyl groups. A first methyl addition gives a tetrahedral intermediate; its collapse expels methoxide to give the ortho-substituted ketone o−!MeO2​C−C6​H4​−C(=O)Me. A second methyl addition produces o−!MeO2​C−C6​H4​−C(O−)Me2​, with the alkoxide bound to magnesium.
The newly generated oxygen is now positioned to attack the remaining ester intramolecularly. Draw an arrow from its lone pair to that ester carbonyl and another from the C=O pi bond to its oxygen. Collapse of the resulting tetrahedral intermediate reforms the carbonyl and expels methoxide, closing a five-membered lactone. Protonation of magnesium-bound species in the work-up gives 3,3-Dimethylphthalide. Equivalently, protonation can first give the ortho hydroxy ester, followed by acid-assisted lactonization with loss of methanol; both descriptions show the same required bond formation.
The oxygen in the new ring is the oxygen of the first ester's original carbonyl; the surviving lactone carbonyl comes from the second ester. The product retains the six ring carbons and both original acyl carbons, loses the two ester methoxy carbons, and gains two carbons from methylmagnesium bromide. The favourable intramolecular five-membered closure accounts for the displayed product. It does not make the isolated lactone universally inert to a large excess of grignard reagent; stoichiometry and trapping/work-up conditions matter.

The oxygen substituent changes position through a halohydrin–epoxide sequence. First, hydroxide attacks the acetate carbonyl of BrCH2​C(Me)2​OAc, giving a tetrahedral intermediate. Collapse expels the substrate alkoxide, and proton transfer gives BrCH2​C(Me)2​OH plus acetate. This is saponification, breaking the acyl–oxygen bond rather than replacing bromide directly with hydroxide.
A small equilibrium population of BrCH2​C(Me)2​O− undergoes intramolecular SN2 reaction at the adjacent primary carbon: the O lone pair attacks CH2​Br and its C–Br pair leaves with bromide. The result is 2,2-Dimethyloxirane. Addition of HBr protonates the ring oxygen. bromide then attacks preferentially at the more substituted carbon, where the protonated ring has greater carbocation character, breaking that C–O bond and leaving oxygen attached to the former primary carbon:
BrCH2​C(Me)2​OH ⟶ (CH2​)O(CMe2​) HBr​ HOCH2​C(Br)(Me)2​.
(15)
The middle formula denotes a three-membered epoxide, not an open-chain ether. Acidic opening of this highly unsymmetrical epoxide has the opposite regioselectivity to a simple basic nucleophilic opening at its less substituted carbon.
Finally, the primary alcohol oxygen attacks acetyl chloride. The tetrahedral intermediate collapses with chloride departure, and triethylamine removes the O–H proton and captures HCl. The product is AcOCH2​C(Br)(Me)2​. oxygen is carried through the epoxide to the other carbon; it is not necessary to invent a methyl migration or a carbon-skeleton rearrangement.

The ideal gas model takes the particles' own volume to be negligible compared with the container volume and neglects intermolecular interactions except during idealized elastic collisions. These assumptions remove excluded-volume and attractive-force corrections to the ideal gas law. The standard enthalpy of formation is the enthalpy change on forming exactly one mole of the specified compound in its stated standard state from its constituent chemical elements in their reference states at the same specified temperature. The reference states are normally the stable elemental forms at the standard pressure. A standard state does not itself mean 298 K; temperature must be stated separately.

The Clausius statement of the second law forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is transfer of heat from a colder thermal reservoir to a hotter one. The Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is complete conversion of heat drawn from a single thermal reservoir into work. These are equivalent formulations of the Second law of thermodynamics; an ordinary refrigerator is allowed because supplied work is an additional effect.

In molecular boron trichloride, boron uses three approximately sp2 hybrids to form three equivalent B–Cl sigma bonds in a trigonal plane. Its perpendicular empty 2p orbital can accept electron density from the compatible combination of filled chlorine 3p lone pairs. The resulting pi donation is delocalized over the three B–Cl connections.
In the elementary model, one net pi bond is distributed over three equivalent sigma bonds, giving formal average B–Cl bond order 1+1/3=4/3. The corresponding resonance contributors each put a B=Cl connection in a different position, with formal negative boron and positive donating chlorine. They do not mean one experimentally distinct double bond and two single bonds. The 4/3 value is the idealized sigma-plus-delocalized-pi bookkeeping value; a computed bond index need not equal that exact rational number.
