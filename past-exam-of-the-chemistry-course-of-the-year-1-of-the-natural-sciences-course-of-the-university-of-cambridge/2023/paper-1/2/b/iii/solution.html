The third electron must enter the higher, degenerate and partly antibonding orbital pair rather than the already filled fully bonding orbital. At an equilateral geometry the configuration becomes (a1′​)2(e′)1. This weakens the collective bonding and creates orbital degeneracy, so a distortion can lower the singly occupied component and localize bonding. Along the ground-state reaction coordinate, formation of a normal two-electron H–H bond with a separated hydrogen atom becomes favourable.
The result is ground-state dissociation to H2​+H, not loss of a proton from the cation or formation of three isolated atoms. One should not claim that adding one electron exactly cancels two bonding electrons: a simple three-orbital count alone does not predict a zero bond order or prove dissociation. The important qualitative changes are occupancy of an antibonding/degenerate level and access to a lower-energy dimer-plus-atom arrangement. A complete dissociation statement requires the potential-energy surface, including nuclear repulsion; electronically excited neutral H3​ states are a separate issue.
