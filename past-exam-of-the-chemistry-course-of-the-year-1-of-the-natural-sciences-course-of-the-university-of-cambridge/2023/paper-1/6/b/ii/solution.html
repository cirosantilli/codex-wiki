carbon uses strong 2s/2p-derived covalent bonding, whereas retention or involvement of the low-energy Pb 6s pair distinguishes the two lead oxides. In CO, carbon has an approximately triple-bonded description with one carbon lone pair; the latter has substantial 2s character, while the sigma and two pi bonding combinations involve the valence shell, particularly carbon 2p orbitals for the pi bonds. In CO2​, carbon is formally in oxidation state +4 and its four valence electrons participate in two strongly covalent C=O double-bond frameworks. A localized account uses sp hybrids for sigma bonding and two p orbitals for the pi systems. oxidation states are bookkeeping devices here: neither molecule contains a bare C2+ or C4+ ion.
In PbO, the Pb(II) description removes or employs mainly the two 6p electrons, retaining a relatively low-energy 6s2 pair. In PbO2​, the Pb(IV) description additionally involves those two 6s electrons. The stabilization and poor availability of that pair underlie the inert-pair effect and the relative preference for Pb(II) chemistry. “Inert” does not mean that the 6s electrons have zero influence on bonding or shape: mixing with oxygen states and empty Pb states can make the Pb(II) lone pair stereochemically active. The more ionic formal electron counts in the lead oxides and the covalent molecular-orbital description of the carbon oxides are complementary models, not literal assignments of individual electrons to immutable atomic orbitals.[ref]
