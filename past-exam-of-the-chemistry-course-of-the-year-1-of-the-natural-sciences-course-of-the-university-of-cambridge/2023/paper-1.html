The reactive occupied orbital of ethoxide is an oxygen lone pair; the acyl electrophile accepts into its carbonyl π∗ orbital. Draw the ethoxide lone pair as a directional oxygen-centred lobe directed toward an electrophile. In a lithium-ion-pair picture the low unoccupied acceptor on lithium is an empty, diffuse Li-centred orbital, with 2s/2p character depending on coordination. It should not be confused with an occupied Li–O covalent bonding pair.
For methyl acetate, the occupied frontier donor is principally an oxygen nonbonding orbital, mixed with the ester orbital system. Its low-lying reactive acceptor is π∗(C=O), with opposite phases on carbon and oxygen and a relatively large accepting lobe on the carbonyl carbon. The corresponding sketch must show a node between the carbonyl atoms. These are chemically useful frontier-orbital descriptions; lithium alkoxide aggregation and ethanol solvation make a unique isolated-molecule orbital energy diagram an oversimplification.

The product-forming pairing is n(EtO−)→π∗(C=O). Orient an occupied ethoxide oxygen lobe toward the large carbon lobe of the carbonyl acceptor with matching phase. An oblique approach permits constructive overlap while the pi bond weakens; this is the usual Bürgi-Dunitz trajectory to acyl carbon. The high-energy occupied orbital on the anion makes ethoxide the stronger electron-pair donor than the neutral ester oxygen.
The reverse frontier pairing, n(ester O)→empty Li, gives oxygen-to-lithium coordination. It is a plausible stabilizing contact and can coexist with the first interaction, rather than constituting an alternative way to replace the ester alkoxy group. In the elementary frontier picture, the anionic donor-to-carbonyl interaction is the important pairing for the new covalent bond and is favoured by its donor strength and appropriate accepting coefficient at carbon. The Li contact can polarize the carbonyl and organize the reacting pair. Precise energy-gap rankings require a specified solvated lithium species; the sketches alone do not justify pretending that lithium coordination cannot occur.

MeC(=O)OMe+EtO−⇌MeC(O−)(OMe)(OEt).

Excess ethanol drives a reversible ester exchange mainly through the actual mixture composition, not through a large new bond-energy advantage. The net reaction exchanges one ester and one alcohol for a closely related ester and alcohol. It preserves the main bond types and has no obvious large exothermicity. Solvation and ion pairing can contribute to its enthalpy, but there is no basis here for asserting a large negative standard reaction enthalpy.
There are two molecules on each side, so an increase in particle count is not the entropy explanation. Instead use
Δr​G=Δr​G∘+RTlnQ,Q=amethyl acetate​aethanol​aethyl acetate​amethanol​​.

Both dimers have two electrons in a valence sigma-bonding orbital and a formal valence bond order of one. For hydrogen the minimal-basis configuration is (1σg​)2, with the 1s-derived 1σg​ as highest occupied molecular orbital and 1σu∗​ as LUMO. The bonding combination has no internuclear node; the antibonding combination does.
For lithium, include the filled core pair of levels as well as the valence levels:
Li2​: (1σg​)2(1σu​)2(2σg​)2.
(12)
The first two orbitals are predominantly core 1s combinations whose bonding and antibonding occupations cancel in a simple bond-order count. The highest occupied molecular orbital is predominantly σg​(2s), denoted 2σg​; the LUMO is 2σu​, predominantly the antibonding 2s combination but modified by mixing with compatible 2p contributions. The nearby empty 2p-derived sigma and pi levels must also be shown. Only orbitals of matching symmetry mix, so this does not mean that every 2s combination mixes with every 2p combination.
On a common qualitative energy reference, hydrogen 1s is much lower than lithium's valence 2s, while lithium's core 1s levels lie much deeper. The Li valence bonding–antibonding separation is smaller, consistent with its longer and weaker bond. The figure uses a compressed core region and schematic gaps, not measured orbital energies. The role of symmetry-compatible s–p orbital mixing in the first-row diatomics is illustrated in institutional molecular-orbital notes.[ref]

Two electrons can occupy a fully bonding orbital delocalized equally over all three hydrogen atoms. Three 1s atomic orbitals on an equilateral triangle form one totally symmetric a1′​ combination and a degenerate pair e′ of higher orbitals. In an orthonormal equal-interaction model with diagonal energy α and off-diagonal β<0,
E(a1′​)=α+2β,E(e′)=α−β.
(13)
The lower orbital is proportional to 1sA​+1sB​+1sC​, with constructive overlap along every edge. Its two electrons give a three-centre two-electron bond and equivalent interactions between every pair of nuclei. At the equilibrium distance, this bonding competes with nuclear Coulomb force to favour the symmetric triangular arrangement. The occupied shell is nondegenerate and closed, so there is no first-order Jahn-Teller incentive to distinguish the three edges. A straight-line or localized single-bond description would miss that equally shared bonding pattern.

The third electron must enter the higher, degenerate and partly antibonding orbital pair rather than the already filled fully bonding orbital. At an equilateral geometry the configuration becomes (a1′​)2(e′)1. This weakens the collective bonding and creates orbital degeneracy, so a distortion can lower the singly occupied component and localize bonding. Along the ground-state reaction coordinate, formation of a normal two-electron H–H bond with a separated hydrogen atom becomes favourable.
The result is ground-state dissociation to H2​+H, not loss of a proton from the cation or formation of three isolated atoms. One should not claim that adding one electron exactly cancels two bonding electrons: a simple three-orbital count alone does not predict a zero bond order or prove dissociation. The important qualitative changes are occupancy of an antibonding/degenerate level and access to a lower-energy dimer-plus-atom arrangement. A complete dissociation statement requires the potential-energy surface, including nuclear repulsion; electronically excited neutral H3​ states are a separate issue.

Li3​ can retain a bound triangular framework, but an equilateral triangle with three valence electrons is Jahn-Teller unstable. The six core electrons are effectively spectators. In the simplest triangular valence model, two electrons occupy the fully bonding level and one occupies the degenerate upper pair. Making one edge different splits that pair; the single electron occupies the lower component, giving an electronic stabilization that can outweigh the cost of a small distortion. An isosceles triangle is sufficient to lift the degeneracy, so three different edge lengths are not required.
The two 2.8-Å contacts are close to, but slightly longer than, the 2.7-Å dimer bond, while the 3.5-Å edge is much weaker. Delocalization and partial antibonding occupation explain why these cannot be treated as three independent full-strength Li–Li single bonds. lithium's nearby valence 2p functions provide additional polarization and mixing channels absent from a hydrogen 1s-only description, allowing a more flexible multicentre electronic structure. The existence of a bound minimum, unlike ground-state H3​, is an energetic fact rather than a consequence of three-electron counting alone. Calculated Li3​ surfaces show bound states, a conical intersection and Jahn-Teller coupling.[ref]

The ordering is allyl chloride > 1-chloropropane > 2-chloropropane. Iodide is a good nucleophile but a weak base, and polar aprotic solvent acetone favours its SN2 reaction. SN2 reaction and C–Cl cleavage occur in one step; precipitation of sodium chloride can also favour halide exchange without changing the basic steric comparison.
The primary saturated chloride supplies the reference rate. Allyl chloride is also primary, but its neighbouring pi system stabilizes the developing delocalized electronic structure in the substitution transition state. It can therefore react about 64 times faster without requiring a free allylic carbocation. The secondary chloride is sterically crowded at the attacked carbon, making backside approach and the crowded transition state less favourable; its rate is about 0.02 times the primary value. The leaving group and nucleophile are the same in the comparison, so assigning the ordering to different halide leaving groups would be incorrect. If prefactor differences are neglected, ΔΔG‡=−RTln(k/kref​) at 333 K gives roughly −11.5 and +10.8kJmol−1 for the allylic and secondary substrates respectively: modest barrier changes produce large rate ratios.

The first product is BrCH=C(CH3​)CH2​CH2​OCH3​, with the original alkene geometry retained. methoxide attacks the primary saturated carbon bearing bromine from the backside. Its oxygen lone pair forms the new C–O bond as the C–Br bonding pair leaves with bromide. The other bromine is vinylic, directly bonded to an sp2 carbon: ordinary SN2 reaction is unfavourable there, and ionization would require an unstable vinyl cation. Thus the primary alkyl bromide is replaced selectively, rather than replacing the vinylic halide or moving the double bond. There is no observable inversion label at the attacked carbon because it is a methylene.
The second product is sodium 4-propoxybutane-1-sulfonate, Na+−O3​S(CH2​)4​OCH2​CH2​CH3​. sodium hydride first removes the alcohol proton, giving propoxide and hydrogen. The propoxide oxygen then attacks the primary ring carbon bonded to the ring oxygen; simultaneous cleavage of that carbon–oxygen bond opens the sultone. The departing ring oxygen remains attached to sulfur as part of the resonance-stabilized sulfonate anion. The carbon attached directly to sulfur is not the substitution site, because cleavage there would not expel the same stable sulfonate leaving group. This is carbon-centred ring opening of a cyclic sulfonate ester, not addition across an S=O bond.

Aqueous base selectively converts the methyl ester to a carboxylate, and brief acidification gives the corresponding monoacid while retaining the tert-Butyl ester. At the methyl-ester end, a hydroxide lone pair attacks the acyl carbon and the carbonyl pi pair moves to oxygen. The tetrahedral intermediate collapses as the oxygen anion reforms C=O and the acyl C–OMe bond breaks to methoxide. proton transfer then gives the carboxylate and methanol. carboxylate formation makes the net saponification effectively irreversible under the basic conditions; protonation protonates that carboxylate.
The tert-Butyl ester reacts more slowly under these conditions because its bulky alkoxy group hinders acyl attack, and tert-butoxide is a stronger base and poorer leaving group than methoxide. The selectivity is kinetic, not an assertion that tert-butyl esters can never undergo base hydrolysis. The work-up should be sufficiently mild and brief to avoid the separate acid-labile pathway.
Anhydrous trifluoroacetic acid instead removes the tert-butyl group, leaving the methyl ester. protonation activates the ester; in a useful reactive protonation form the alkoxy oxygen bears the proton. The O–C(tert-butyl) bond then cleaves with its electron pair going to oxygen, producing the carboxylic acid and a tert-butyl-cation-like fragment. Loss of a methyl proton gives 2-methylpropene and regenerates the acid catalyst. Carbonyl-oxygen protonation and charge delocalization can precede cleavage; drawing alkoxy-oxygen protonation is a transparent way of tracking the bonds broken. The key distinction is alkyl–oxygen cleavage here versus acyl–oxygen cleavage in the base reaction. Methyl-cation formation is strongly unfavourable, and without water ordinary acid-catalysed methyl-ester hydrolysis is not available. No water is required to account for the tert-butyl deprotection or its alkene by-product.

a OurBigBook.com
The large drop from carbon to silicon is the main trend; the smaller reversals at Ge and Pb reflect changes in shielding and valence-electron binding. Allen electronegativity is based on the mean binding energy of valence electrons in free ground-state atoms, rather than an arbitrary charge-transfer count. Moving to larger principal shells generally makes the valence electrons more diffuse and more shielded, reducing their attraction to the nucleus. carbon's compact second-shell valence orbitals therefore give particularly high electronegativity.
The intervening filled 3d shell shields imperfectly, increasing effective nuclear attraction in germanium relative to a simple smooth extrapolation from silicon. This helps explain Ge being slightly above Si rather than below it. Increased size then lowers Sn relative to Ge. The filled inner d and f shells and relativistic stabilization of lead's 6s electrons modify the next step; contraction and stronger binding of the 6s contribution help Pb lie slightly above Sn. The small reversals should not obscure the much larger C-to-Si change. The spectroscopic definition includes both occupied s and p valence contributions, so the relativistic s effect can influence the Allen average even though not every electron participates equally in every bond.[ref]

carbon uses strong 2s/2p-derived covalent bonding, whereas retention or involvement of the low-energy Pb 6s pair distinguishes the two lead oxides. In CO, carbon has an approximately triple-bonded description with one carbon lone pair; the latter has substantial 2s character, while the sigma and two pi bonding combinations involve the valence shell, particularly carbon 2p orbitals for the pi bonds. In CO2​, carbon is formally in oxidation state +4 and its four valence electrons participate in two strongly covalent C=O double-bond frameworks. A localized account uses sp hybrids for sigma bonding and two p orbitals for the pi systems. oxidation states are bookkeeping devices here: neither molecule contains a bare C2+ or C4+ ion.
In PbO, the Pb(II) description removes or employs mainly the two 6p electrons, retaining a relatively low-energy 6s2 pair. c OurBigBook.com
CO and CO2​ are discrete molecules, whereas the other oxides gain stability from extended bonding or ionic lattices. carbon's compact 2p orbitals overlap effectively with oxygen 2p orbitals, allowing strong multiple bonds that satisfy the valence requirements within small molecules. Intermolecular forces between CO or CO2​ molecules are much weaker than those internal bonds, so low condensation temperatures and gaseous states at room conditions are unsurprising. CO2​ is nonpolar overall but still has London dispersion forces and interactions between electric quadrupoles; “nonpolar” does not mean no attraction.
For larger Group 14 atoms, overlap with oxygen 2p orbitals is less effective for strong localized multiple bonding. SiO2​ instead forms an extended network of SiO4​ tetrahedra with each oxygen bridging two silicon atoms. Breaking or melting the network involves much more than separating intact SiO2​ molecules. The Si–O electronegativity difference is 3.61−1.92=1.69, so these are strongly polar covalent bonds rather than evidence for a molecular solid.
For Pb–O the difference is 3.61−1.85=1.76, and ionic Pb2+/O2− or Pb4+/O2− descriptions provide useful starting points, with appreciable covalent contributions. PbO has layered structures with asymmetric oxygen coordination consistent with a stereochemically active 6s2 pair. A common PbO2​ structure is rutile-like, with PbO6​ octahedra linked through oxygen. Extended lattice interactions account for their solid states.[ref] The similar Si–O and Pb–O electronegativity differences show why a single difference threshold cannot determine the entire bonding type, coordination number or structure.

A suitable representative structure is a three-dimensional network of corner-sharing CO4​ tetrahedra, with C–O single bonds. Each carbon is four-coordinate; every bridging oxygen is bonded to two carbons. Sharing four oxygens between two tetrahedra gives 4/2=2 oxygens per carbon, so the network retains empirical formula CO2​ without containing separate O=C=O molecules.
High pressure favours a compact, highly coordinated network through its smaller molar volume: at fixed temperature, increasing pressure changes relative Gibbs energies according to d(ΔG)=ΔVdp. Heating helps overcome the large reconstructive barrier to breaking and redistributing molecular pi bonding and establishing an extended single-bond network. It should not be argued that high temperature by itself favours the lower-entropy network. Nor is merely freezing molecular CO2​ sufficient: dry ice remains a molecular solid.
The sketch shows connectivity and local tetrahedral coordination, not a unique predicted crystal structure. Real amorphous carbonia need not have perfect periodic order or exclusively one coordination environment. The original carbonia experiments combined high-pressure preparation with infrared, Raman and diffraction evidence for loss of molecular character and formation of an amorphous network.[ref]

e OurBigBook.com
Amine nitrogen binds the carbon end of CO2​; Mg2+ binds through oxygen. The polarized C=O bonds make carbon electrophilic and oxygen electron rich. An amine nitrogen lone pair therefore donates toward carbon, potentially giving a carbamate-like adduct after proton transfer. The hard Lewis acid Mg2+ accepts an oxygen lone pair, either from coordinated CO2​ or from an oxygen of a captured carbamate species. Simply reversing these assignments because nitrogen and oxygen both have lone pairs would put two electron-pair donors together without accounting for the electrophilic site.
The local binding pattern does not by itself determine the full proton-transfer state or whether cooperative chains form; those depend on the framework and amine arrangement. A documented diamine-appended framework shows cooperative carbon-dioxide insertion and ammonium carbamate chain formation, illustrating one possible implementation.[ref]
To regenerate the material, lower the CO2​ chemical potential using reduced pressure or a CO2​-free purge, and, where binding is exothermic, heat the framework to favour desorption. The temperature must remain compatible with framework stability. This reverses adsorption or reversible chemisorption; it does not require permanently destroying the metal–organic framework.
