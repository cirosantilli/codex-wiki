The assignments are A → spectrum 3, B → spectrum 4, C → spectrum 1, and D → spectrum 2. The useful infrared spectroscopy evidence is the combination of functional-group absorptions, rather than every feature of the fingerprint region.
A is a saturated diketone: spectrum 3 has a strong ketone C=O band near 1710cm−1 without the very broad acid O–H absorption. Its two symmetry-related carbonyls do not require two well-separated diagnostic bands.
B is a conjugated dienoic acid: spectrum 4 combines a broad carboxylic acid O–H envelope, approximately 2500–3300cm−1, with a conjugated C=O band near 1690–1700cm−1 and C=C absorptions around 1600–1650cm−1.
C is an alkynoic acid: spectrum 1 contains the broad acid O–H absorption, a carbonyl near 1700cm−1, and a conspicuous C≡C stretch near 2250cm−1. Its alkyne is internal, so no terminal ≡C–H stretch is expected.
D is a conjugated vinyl ester: spectrum 2 has a strong ester C=O absorption around 1730–1740cm−1, alkene features near 1640cm−1, and strong C–O fingerprint absorptions, but no broad acid O–H envelope. These are approximate readings of the original axes, not exact assignments of every vibrational mode.

The carbon spectra are A → 5, B → 8, C → 6, and D → 7. Number each acyclic acid or ester chain from its carbonyl carbon. The assignments below account for every carbon-13 NMR environment.
For A, both ketone carbonyl carbons have δ=208.3 ppm and all four methylene carbons have δ=36.5 ppm. Molecular symmetry and rapid conformational averaging explain why six carbons give only two resonances.

i OurBigBook.com
Carbon dioxide is linear, with two equivalent C–O bonds of formal bond order two; its Homo is predominantly oxygen nonbonding and its LUMO is C–O π∗ antibonding. The carbon uses two opposite sp hybrids for its sigma bonds and two perpendicular p atomic orbitals for pi bonding. A localized O=C=O picture assigns each terminal oxygen an sp2 sigma/lone-pair framework and one p orbital for its local pi bond.
In a symmetry-adapted description, each of the two perpendicular sets of oxygen and carbon p functions forms bonding, oxygen-centred nonbonding and antibonding combinations. The occupied nonbonding pair is labelled 1πg​ and the lowest empty pi pair 2πu∗​. The latter has appreciable amplitude at carbon, explaining its electrophilic reactivity. Hybridization of a terminal atom with only one bonded neighbour is a choice of localized basis, not an independently measurable bond angle; an alternative oxygen sp plus p basis can represent the same delocalized system.
There are 16 valence electrons: four in the two sigma bonds, four in the two net pi bonds, and eight in the remaining oxygen lone-pair combinations. These local electron counts and the delocalized orbital description are alternative bookkeeping schemes, not additional electrons.

Ozone is bent, with two equivalent O–O bonds of approximate bond order 1.5. Its useful localized orbital hybridization description is sp2 at all three oxygens. Two hybrids at the central oxygen form sigma bonds and a third contains a lone pair; the three perpendicular p orbitals form a three-centre four-electron pi bond.
The three pi orbitals are bonding, nonbonding and antibonding, filled as πb2​πnb2​π∗0​. The terminal-centred nonbonding orbital is the Homo, conventionally 1a2​; the delocalized antibonding orbital is the LUMO, 2b1​. The two principal resonance structures, −O−O+=O and O=O+−O−, distribute the extra pi bond across the two links.
The 18 valence electrons comprise four sigma-bond electrons, ten in five sigma-framework lone pairs, and four in the pi system. This gives a paired ground-state model. More accurate treatments include electron correlation; the simple bond-order picture is not an exact charge-density calculation.[ref]

The central carbon in carbon dioxide has two bonding directions and no lone pair, whereas the central oxygen in ozone has two bonding directions and one lone pair. In the VSEPR model, carbon dioxide therefore has a linear arrangement; ozone has an approximately trigonal-planar electron-domain arrangement but a bent atomic skeleton. Multiple bonding occupies one direction for this geometrical count. The extra central-oxygen lone pair, rather than a different number of atomic nuclei, creates the difference.

The product is carbonic acid, HO−C(=O)−OH. The oxygen lone pair Homo of water donates into the carbon-centred component of the carbon dioxide π∗ LUMO. Draw an electron-pair arrow from the water oxygen to carbon and a second from one C=O pi bond onto its oxygen:
CO2​+H2​O⟶ −O−C(=O)−OH2+​⟶HO−C(=O)−OH.

The elementary valence-MO model gives bond order(C2​)=2​. Each carbon supplies four n=2 valence electrons, so fill eight electrons in the ordering appropriate to the lighter second-row diatomics:
(σg​2s)2(σu∗​2s)2(πu​2p)4(σg​2p)0(πg∗​2p)0(σu∗​2p)0.
(4)
The two πu​ orbitals are degenerate, as are the two πg∗​ orbitals. The 2s bonding and antibonding occupations cancel; four net p-bonding electrons remain, giving (6−2)/2=2. Mixing between sigma orbitals of compatible symmetry places the occupied πu​ pair below the empty p-derived σg​ orbital in this diagram. All occupied orbitals are paired.
The omitted 1s orbitals form filled σg​1s and σu∗​1s levels. Their contributions to the formal bond order cancel, and their low energies and small spatial extent make them largely core-like. Omitting them simplifies this valence diagram without claiming they do not exist or have exactly no effect on molecular energies.

The ions are acetylide C22−​ and linear tricarbide C34−​. Charge balance gives Mg2+[C2​]2− and (Mg2+)2​[C3​]4−.
The acetylide has ten valence electrons, two more than neutral C2​. They fill the p-derived sigma bonding orbital, giving the formal configuration πu4​σg​(2p)2 above the cancelling 2s pair. Its bond order is three, with the Lewis structure −C≡C− and a lone pair at each end; each carbon is approximately sp hybridized.
The tricarbide has 3(4)+4=16 valence electrons and is isoelectronic with carbon dioxide. The cumulene contributor 2−C=C=C2− has a central sp carbon, two sigma bonds and two perpendicular pi bonds. The end carbons each have two lone pairs and can be described with an sp2 localized basis. Its two C–C bonds have formal order two in this model; the central C–C–C angle is 180∘. Other resonance contributors help describe its protonation chemistry.

The extra electron associated with trivalent lanthanum weakens the C–C bond by occupying states with C–C antibonding character. Calcium gives the ten-valence-electron acetylide description Ca2+[C2​]2−, with formal bond order three, consistent with the short 119.2 pm distance.

The intended ordinary-carbide comparison is energetic: the smaller, more strongly polarizing Mg2+ can stabilize a different balance of lattice energy and carbon-anion bonding from the larger Ca2+. Stabilization of the highly charged C34−​ group competes with acetylide formation; electron counting alone does not require the same carbide stoichiometries for both metals. Ionic size and charge density provide a qualitative explanation, not a complete phase-stability calculation.
The literal claim that calcium has only one carbide is too broad. Primary calculations and high-pressure synthesis have established additional calcium carbides, including Ca2​C3​ and Ca2​C; known calcium-carbide chemistry also includes CaC6​. Thus the conventional comparison must be limited to the familiar salt-like compounds and conditions intended here, rather than treated as a prohibition on other calcium–carbon compositions.[ref]

The two carbon skeletons are allene, CH2​=C=CH2​, and propyne, CH3​−C≡CH. “Linear” describes the three-carbon skeleton; it does not mean every hydrogen lies on the same line.
For allene, use the contributor 2−C=C=C2−. A terminal lone pair attacks a water hydrogen while the O–H pair returns to oxygen, giving HC−=C=C2− and hydroxide. Repeat at the still-dianionic end, then protonate each remaining terminal carbanion. The four successive proton transfers give
C34−​→HC33−​→H2​C32−​→H3​C3−​→H4​C3​,
(5)
with two hydrogens placed on each terminal carbon in this branch.
For propyne, the resonance contributor 3−C−C≡C− makes the alternative electron flow clear. Three successive protonations of the singly bonded terminal carbon convert its three lone pairs into C–H bonds; protonation of the acetylide end gives CH3​−C≡CH. These are formal protonation pathways for the multiply charged anion, not a claim that the highly charged resonance contributors are separately isolable species. Each transfer produces OH−; overall
Mg2​C3​+4H2​O⟶2Mg(OH)2​+C3​H4​.
(6)
Allene has one proton resonance integrating to 4H, ideally a singlet because its four hydrogens are chemically equivalent. Proton-decoupled carbon NMR has two resonances, in ratio two terminal carbons to one central carbon: approximately 75 and 210 ppm, respectively. The unusually downfield central cumulene carbon is not a carbonyl.
Propyne has two proton resonances, integrating 3H near 1.8–2.0 ppm and 1H near 2–3 ppm. If the small four-bond coupling is resolved, they are a methyl doublet and an acetylenic quartet; if unresolved, each can look like a singlet. Proton-decoupled carbon NMR has three resonances: one methyl carbon near 5 ppm and two distinct sp carbons around 65–85 ppm. These are approximate predictions, not supplied measured shifts.

The most acidic sites are: acetic-acid O–H, pKa​≈4.8; phenol O–H, ≈10; ethanal alpha-CH3​, ≈17–20; propyne terminal ≡C–H, ≈25. These are ordinary approximate organic-chemistry estimates; exact acidities depend on solvent and reference convention.

Its greater s character stabilizes negative charge relative to an ordinary sp2 or sp3 carbanion; the methyl hydrogens are much less acidic.

The first transformation is two successive dehydrohalogenations; the second opens the lactone by two methyl additions and then closes a six-membered cyclic ether. Both schemes must be accounted for within this part.
For the dichloride, write R=cyclohexyl. In the product-forming path, tert-butoxide removes a hydrogen from the methyl group of R−CCl2​−CH3​. Draw arrows from the base lone pair to H, from C–H into the C–C bond, and from C–Cl to Cl. This E2 elimination gives R−C(Cl)=CH2​. A second base-induced elimination removes a vinyl hydrogen and the remaining chloride, forming R−C≡CH. The second elimination is more demanding because a vinylic bond is involved, consistent with the strong-base, heated conditions. This route explains the displayed product without asserting that no other elimination regioisomer can ever form.
For the lactone, a methyl equivalent from methylmagnesium iodide attacks the ester carbonyl carbon, with its pi pair moving to oxygen. Collapse of the tetrahedral intermediate reforms C=O and cleaves the acyl–oxygen bond, opening the ring to the magnesium alkoxide corresponding to HO(CH2​)4​C(=O)CH3​. The newly formed ketone is more susceptible to a second methyl addition; its pi pair again moves to oxygen. After water workup, the product is the diol HO(CH2​)4​C(OH)(CH3​)2​.
Under acid and heat, protonate its tertiary OH, then move the C–O pair onto oxygen as water leaves. The resulting HO(CH2​)4​C+(CH3​)2​ is trapped intramolecularly by the remaining primary-alcohol oxygen. Its lone pair forms the closing O–C bond, producing a six-membered cyclic oxonium ion. Deprotonation yields 2,2-dimethyloxane, also called 2,2-dimethyltetrahydropyran. Ring closure retains four chain methylenes; it does not form a five-membered ring. The two methyl groups both derive from the two equivalents of Grignard reagent.

The alcohol is oxidized to cyclohexanone through an alkyl hypochlorite intermediate. Acid converts hypochlorite into electrophilic hypochlorous acid. The alcohol oxygen attacks chlorine, with acid-assisted proton transfers and loss of water giving the O–Cl derivative R2​CH−O−Cl.
A base such as acetate then removes the hydrogen attached to the carbinol carbon. The three coupled electron-pair motions are: base → H; C–H → C–O to form C=O; and O–Cl → Cl to release chloride. The resulting carbonyl is cyclohexanone. This is an elimination-like oxidation of the hypochlorite ester: the original alcohol oxygen becomes the ketone oxygen, while loss of its O-bound H and the carbinol C-bound H effects the net oxidation.

D is racemic trans-2-methylcyclohexan-1-ol. E is racemic 1-methyl-2-phenylcyclohexan-1-ol with OH and Ph trans, and Me and Ph cis. Number the starting alkene carbon carrying methyl as C1​ and its partner as C2​ in the reaction explanation below; the product name of D renumbers the OH-bearing carbon as 1.
For D, hydroboration proceeds through a concerted four-centre transition state: the alkene pi pair donates to boron while B–H supplies the new C–H bond. Boron attaches mainly to less substituted C2​ and hydrogen to C1​, on the same face. Under peroxide/base conditions, hydroperoxide attacks B; migration of the C–B bond to the adjacent oxygen, with O–O cleavage, replaces C–B by C–O with retention at carbon. Hydrolysis/proton transfer releases the alcohol. Thus the newly added H and OH are syn, making the pre-existing methyl and OH trans. Attack on either alkene face gives the enantiomeric pair.
For E, the peracid transfers its terminal oxygen across the alkene in concerted epoxidation, through cyclic electron flow that also breaks O–O and transfers the acidic proton to the peracid carbonyl oxygen. The resulting epoxide retains the relative configuration of the alkene substituents. The phenyl equivalent from phenylmagnesium bromide then attacks less substituted C2​ from the face opposite the epoxide oxygen. Its C–O bond breaks onto oxygen, an SN2-like ring opening; the oxygen stays attached to C1​. Water workup protonates that alkoxide. Consequently OH at C1​ and Ph at C2​ are anti, while the methyl at C1​ lies on the same relative face as Ph.

Three routes are donor doping, acceptor doping and above-gap illumination. All increase mobile carrier populations if the added states or excitations are effective rather than strongly trapped.
An appropriate donor impurity provides an occupied level just below the conduction band. Thermal ionization requires the smaller donor binding energy rather than the full host gap, supplying mobile electrons and n-type semiconductor behaviour. An appropriate acceptor impurity creates an available level just above the valence band; accepting a valence electron leaves a mobile hole, giving p-type semiconductor behaviour. Doping concentrations and compensation must permit useful carriers; an arbitrary impurity need not be an effective dopant.
For photoconductivity, absorbed photons with hν≳Eg​ can excite valence electrons into the conduction band, producing electron–hole pairs. For the stated 2.7eV gap, the nominal threshold is about 1240/2.7≃459 nm. Optical selection rules and absorption strength can shift the practical onset. The conductivity increase persists only while generation maintains carriers against recombination and trapping; it is not necessarily a permanent change to the material.
In each case, σ=e(nμn​+pμp​) connects increased carrier density to conductivity. Defect-induced reductions in mobility can offset some of the gain, so the diagrams specify mechanisms rather than guarantee a numerical improvement for an unspecified preparation.
