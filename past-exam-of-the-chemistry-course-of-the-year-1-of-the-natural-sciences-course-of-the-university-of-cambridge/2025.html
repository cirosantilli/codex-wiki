For a hydrogen-like ion, the Coulomb potential is stronger by the nuclear atomic number Z, and the energy levels scale as Z2:
En​=−n2Z2RH​​,n=1,2,….​
(4)
Here Z is the number of protons in the atomic nucleus; the ion has exactly one electron, so its net electric charge is (Z−1)e. End-on overlap produces σ and σ∗ molecular orbitals; side-on overlap produces π and π∗ molecular orbitals. Matching signs of the facing atomic orbital lobes give constructive interference and a bonding molecular orbital. Opposite signs give destructive interference and an additional nodal plane between the atomic nuclei in the antibonding molecular orbital.
Take the internuclear direction as x, and define both atomic px​ functions as positive on their own right-hand side. Up to wavefunction normalization, the combinations are
σσ∗ππ∗​px,A​−px,B​px,A​+px,B​py,A​+py,B​py,A​−py,B​​
(9)
The minus sign in the bonding px​ combination follows from this common-axis quantum phase convention: the facing lobes of the two unmodified px​ functions have opposite signs. It does not make that combination antibonding. Every π molecular orbital also has a nodal plane containing the internuclear axis; the additional midpoint node distinguishes π∗ from π.

One consistent localized orbital hybridization description uses the Lewis structures
H3​C−N+≡N,H2​C=N+=N− ↔ H2​C−−N+≡N.
(11)
For methyldiazonium, take C as sp3 and both N atoms as sp. For diazomethane, the double-bond resonance contributor suggests C as sp2, central N as sp, and terminal N as sp2. The central N uses two opposite sp hybrids for the linear C–N–N sigma bond framework. In the localized double-bond picture, terminal N uses one sp2 hybrid for its sigma bond, two for lone pairs, and its remaining p atomic orbital for a pi bond.
These labels specify an approximate bonding construction, not uniquely measurable atomic properties. An alternative representation takes terminal N as sp, with one sp lone pair and one p lone pair; the latter can participate in the delocalized C–N–N π system. It describes the same resonance more explicitly. Neither construction requires an unpaired electron or a bent central N.

The products are hexanenitrile, CH3​(CH2​)4​C≡N, and ethyl triphenylmethyl ether, Ph3​C−O−CH2​CH3​. The nitrile carbon comes from cyanide, so replacing chloride in the five-carbon chain gives a six-carbon product.
For the primary chloride, the carbon-end lone pair of CN− attacks the carbon carrying Cl from the back side while the C–Cl bond pair moves onto Cl. The single concerted nucleophilic substitution step forms the C–C bond and releases Cl−. Attack through the carbon end gives the nitrile, not an isocyanide.
For the triphenylmethyl chloride, the C–Cl bond first undergoes heterolysis to Ph3​C+ and Cl−. An ethanol oxygen lone pair then attacks the carbocation, giving Ph3​C−O+(H)Et. Finally pyridine accepts that O–H proton, with the O–H bond pair returning to oxygen, giving the neutral ether and pyridinium chloride.

The primary chloride undergoes an SN2 reaction; the triphenylmethyl chloride undergoes an SN1 reaction. The primary site is accessible to back-side attack, whereas three phenyl groups make back-side attack at the triphenylmethyl site extremely hindered and stabilize its carbocation through resonance.
For the SN2 reaction, a suitable transition state is
[N≡C⋯CH2​(CH2​)3​CH3​⋯Cl]‡−,v=k2​[CH3​(CH2​)4​Cl][CN−]​.
(13)
The entering C and leaving Cl lie on opposite sides of the reacting carbon, with partial C–C and C–Cl bonds. The total charge is −1. This transition state is not a discrete reaction intermediate.
For the SN1 reaction, the approximately planar Ph3​C+ carbocation is a genuine reaction intermediate. Assuming its formation by C–Cl heterolysis is rate determining and trapping is rapid,
v=k1​[Ph3​CCl]​.
(14)
Thus the limiting rate law is independent of ethanol concentration. The successive attack and proton transfer occur after the slow ionization; a cation-stabilizing environment and efficient trapping underpin this idealized SN1 description.

B is 4-hydroxy-1-methylpyrrolidin-2-one; C is 1-methylpyrrolidin-3-ol. Their ring connectivities are shown below. Each ring remains five membered; the attached N-methyl group is retained.
For B, a B–H bond of sodium borohydride supplies a hydride equivalent to the ketone carbonyl carbon, and the carbonyl π pair moves to oxygen. This gives an alkoxide, whose protonation during the reaction or workup gives the secondary alcohol. The lactam carbonyl remains. Converting one C=O to CH(OH) adds two H atoms and gives C5​H9​NO2​.
For C, lithium aluminium hydride reduces that ketone in the same net way and also reduces the lactam. Coordination of the amide oxygen to aluminium assists hydride attack at its carbonyl carbon, giving a tetrahedral intermediate. The N lone pair then forms a C=N bond while the C–O bond breaks to an aluminium-bound oxygen group. A resulting iminium ion accepts a second hydride at carbon, yielding the cyclic tertiary amine. Aqueous workup protonates the ketone-derived alkoxide.
The lactam conversion replaces N−C(=O) by N−CH2​; its oxygen is removed rather than retained as another alcohol. Together with ketone reduction, the net change is +4H−O, giving C5​H11​NO. No stereochemistry is specified: the new alcohol stereocentre is formed as a racemic mixture in an achiral environment.

Sodium borohydride is chemoselective for the ketone; lithium aluminium hydride reduces both the ketone and the lactam. D is (MeO)2​CHCH2​CH2​CO2​Me, and E is OHCCH2​CH2​CO2​Et. D results from transesterification; E results from acid-catalysed acetal hydrolysis.
For D, methoxide attacks the ester carbonyl carbon and moves the carbonyl π pair to oxygen. Collapse of the tetrahedral reaction intermediate reforms C=O and expels ethoxide. The spectroscopy is consistent with these products. ii OurBigBook.com
Basic methanol exchanges the ester alkoxy group while retaining the acetal; dilute aqueous acid removes the acetal while retaining the ethyl ester on the stated reaction timescale. An acetal lacks an electrophilic carbonyl and is stable to ordinary base: expelling methoxide directly would require a poor leaving group. The ester can instead undergo nucleophilic acyl substitution through a carbonyl-derived tetrahedral intermediate.
Under acid, protonating an acetal oxygen converts an alkoxy substituent into departing methanol, and the resulting oxocarbenium is stabilized by its remaining oxygen. Water then drives acetal hydrolysis toward the aldehyde. Ester hydrolysis is also possible under acid, but is slower under the conditions implied by the product data. The retained ethyl group and six distinct carbon-13 NMR environments support E rather than the carboxylic acid that prolonged hydrolysis could produce.

Photoelectron spectroscopy measures orbital-dependent binding energies from which an effective charge can be inferred using a stated model. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy accesses outer-shell ionizations; X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy accesses deeper core states. For an isolated gas-phase atom and a calibrated threshold, measure emitted-electron kinetic energy and use I=hν−Ekin​, with the appropriate energy reference. Optical atomic term spectra and series limits also constrain valence ionization energies.
A hydrogenic inference would set I≃R∞​Zeff2​/neff2​, hence Zeff​≃neff​I/R∞​​, with R∞​ here an energy of approximately 13.6eV and neff​ a chosen effective shell parameter. The measured binding energies are experimental; the extracted Zeff​ values remain model dependent. They need not equal Slater screening parameters fitted to radial functions.
Unlike the equal ns,np values in Slater's rules, spectroscopic values distinguish s from p because their penetration and shielding differ; the more tightly bound s states usually imply larger binding-energy-derived effective charges at a common shell parameter. Subshell, atomic-term and, especially for heavier atoms, spin-orbit coupling structure give further distinctions. Electron relaxation following ionization and electron correlation also matter. Thus real binding energies vary across the heavier group-15 atoms even where the simple Slater outer-shell estimates plateau; no universal constant correction to the Slater values follows.[ref]

Allen electronegativity is proportional to the average binding energy of the free ground-state atom's valence electrons. For an s,p main-group atom, use positive, spectroscopically inferred one-electron binding energies εs​,εp​ and their occupancies ns​,np​:
χAllen​=Cns​+np​ns​εs​+np​εp​​.
(40)
Here C>0 fixes the conventional scale; the one-electron energies are obtained from appropriate atomic ionization/term energies, not merely from the first ionization energy. For group 15 the weighted average is (2εs​+3εp​)/5.[ref]

At a fixed shell, stronger effective nuclear attraction generally raises the valence binding energies and therefore Allen electronegativity. Gallium phosphide forms a tetrahedral network of polar covalent bonds, with electron density displaced toward phosphorus. The alternating diamond-related structure is the zinc blende structure: each Ga has four P neighbours and each P has four Ga neighbours. A local orbital hybridization picture combines outward-pointing s,p-derived orbitals into four approximately sp3 bond directions; in the crystal these interactions form extended occupied bonding bands.
Since χAllen​(P)−χAllen​(Ga)=2.25−1.76=0.49>0, P attracts shared electron density more strongly. Write Gaδ+−Pδ− to indicate an ionic contribution superposed on covalent bonds. Formal oxidation states Ga(+3) and P(−3) are electron-assignment labels, not proof of completely transferred charges or a purely ionic crystal.
Neither atom requires 3d hybrids for these four bonds. The Ga 3d10 shell is filled and relatively core-like; the relevant Ga valence orbitals are 4s,4p. P uses principally 3s,3p; its empty 3d orbitals are too high in energy to be a necessary bonding set. Detailed band calculations may include d-state mixing or core–valence effects, but that does not turn the tetrahedral bonding into an expanded-valence d-hybrid construction.

Sulfur substituting for phosphorus acts as a donor impurity, giving n-type semiconductor behaviour and usually increasing electrical conductivity. A P site normally supplies five valence electrons; S supplies six. After forming the surrounding bonds, the extra electron can occupy a donor level below the conduction band and be thermally ionized into that band. The donor impurity then carries positive charge.
This assignment is supported experimentally: infrared transmission measurements on S-doped gallium phosphide resolved bound-donor transitions and determined a sulfur donor optical ionization energy of about 0.104eV, far smaller than the host band gap.[ref]
The effect depends on substitution on P sites, donor ionization, compensation by acceptors and charge carrier mobility. A dilute active donor population increases the electron term in σ=e(nμn​+pμp​); not every dopant must donate a mobile electron at room temperature. Sulfur's larger Allen electronegativity does not overturn this electron-counting argument or make a P-site sulfur atom an acceptor.
