A, spectrum 4: ethoxy CH3​ at about 1.3 is a NMR triplet, and OCH2​ at about 4.15 is a NMR quartet. The two alkene methyls at about 1.9 and 2.2 each integrate to three protons; they are apparent NMR singlets on the overview but have small allylic NMR doublet splitting. Both Be2​'s occupied bonding and antibonding 2s levels cancel in the simplest diagram. Beryllium's relatively accessible empty 2p orbitals allow mixing and correlated configurations to change that cancellation and produce a weak bond. An s-only single-configuration picture is therefore insufficient; spectroscopy and accurate correlated calculations establish its binding.[ref]
For He2​, (σ1s​)2(σ1s∗​)2 similarly gives zero elementary covalent bond order, but the empty-shell excitation gap is much larger and there is no comparable low-energy 2s/2p near-degeneracy. Thus it has no ordinary stable covalent bond.
The literal claim that He2​ does not exist needs qualification: an extraordinarily weak, cold van der Waals helium dimer has been observed. That does not contradict the zero covalent-bond-order diagram.[ref]
The beryllium dimer and helium dimer illustrate two physically different ways that the elementary bond-order picture can miss weak binding.

The alkene pi bond attacks Br2​ as the Br-Br bond breaks to bromide, forming a bridged, unsymmetrical bromonium ion. The carbon bearing the two methyl groups has greater carbocation-like character.
The tethered alcohol oxygen attacks that carbon from the side opposite the bromine bridge. Its lone pair forms the new O-C bond while the attacked C-Br bridge bond opens, giving a six-membered cyclic oxonium ion. Bromide or solvent removes the O-H proton to give the neutral bromoether.
This explains why O becomes attached at the more substituted alkene carbon while Br remains at the other carbon. Polar solvent assists charge separation, and intramolecular capture competes effectively with external bromide attack. Opening is anti; attack on the two faces of the achiral starting material gives a racemic product at the newly formed CHBr stereocentre.
The ring-forming step is bromoetherification.

First hydrolyze the benzylidene acetal. Protonate one acetal oxygen and break its bond to the Ph-CH centre, giving one free alcohol and a benzylic oxocarbenium ion. HO−CH(CH2​CH=CH2​)−C(CH2​OH)=CH−C(O)OMe.

A transition structure is a saddle point on the potential-energy surface: motion along the reaction coordinate lowers energy in either direction. It has one unstable vibrational direction and no intermediate residence basin.

Gold reverses that simple size trend: relativistic 6s stabilization/contraction, changes in the 5d orbitals and stronger s-d participation enhance bonding. A size-only argument cannot explain Au exceeding both Ag and Cu.
Group 15, As > Sb > Bi: bonding in these elemental solids involves directional p-orbital interactions. Increasing size and longer separations weaken overlap down the group, reducing enthalpy of atomization. Changes in detailed solid structure and relativistic effects refine the numbers, but the underlying weakening of p-derived bonding explains the trend.
Enthalpy of atomization measures removal from the entire standard-state solid to gaseous ground-state atoms; it is not simply the dissociation energy of one selected diatomic bond.

Graphite: each carbon forms three strong in-plane sigma bonds in an sp2 honeycomb layer. The remaining p orbitals form delocalized pi bands, allowing electrical conduction especially along the layers. Much weaker interlayer attraction permits sliding, explaining softness without implying weak in-plane C-C bonds.
Carborundum is silicon carbide, SiC. A suitable structure is a three-dimensional, tetrahedrally coordinated covalent network: each Si has four C neighbors and each C four Si neighbors. Strong directional bonding in all directions resists slip and gives hardness. Its filled bonding states are separated from empty states by a substantial band gap, giving low conductivity when pure and undoped.
“Non-conductor” is the intended contrast with graphite; SiC is more precisely a wide-bandgap semiconductor, whose conductivity can change with doping and temperature.

Potassium intercalates between graphite layers and donates electron density to the graphitic pi system. Occupation of formerly less populated electronic states raises the mobile-carrier density, increasing electrical conductivity under the stated comparison.
The gain is electronic doping of the carbon framework, rather than a claim that potassium strengthens every C-C bond or simply acts as a separate wire through the sample. Intercalation also changes layer separation and scattering, so conductivity depends on both carrier population and mobility.
The material is potassium graphite, a graphite intercalation compound.
