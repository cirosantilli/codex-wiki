Information entropy (H(X))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Information theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 5k Articles: 38
For a finite discrete random variable with probability mass function p, its information entropy is the expected value of its information content:
H(X)=−∑x:p(x)>0​p(x)log2​p(x)=E[−log2​p(X)].
(1)
Base-two logarithms give bits; choosing a different base rescales the units. The convention 0log2​0=0 is justified by a limit, so outcomes of zero probability contribute nothing. The definition also applies to countable distributions, but the sum may be infinite.
A fair bit has H=1. A certain outcome has H=0. A Bernoulli random variable that is one with probability 0.1 has H=h2​(0.1)≈0.469 bits, because most of its outcomes are predictable. A rare outcome still has large information content: the outcome of probability 0.1 contributes −log2​(0.1)≈3.322 bits of surprise when it occurs. Entropy is the average surprise, rather than the surprise of each outcome.
For m possible outcomes, 0≤H(X)≤log2​m, with the upper bound attained exactly by the uniform distribution on a finite set. The Gibbs inequality proves this by comparing p with the uniform distribution: D(p∥u)=log2​m−H(X)≥0. Thus increasing predictability reduces the required average description length. The four-symbol prefix code in information theory attains H=7/4 bits exactly; for general probabilities, integer codeword lengths prevent such a perfect match for every individual symbol. Shannon's source coding theorem explains how coding sufficiently long independent blocks approaches the entropy per symbol.
The logarithm is especially natural because independent outcomes have additive surprise: −log2​(pq)=−log2​p−log2​q. Taking expected values gives additive information entropy for independent random variables; for dependent ones, the chain rule for information entropy replaces the second term by conditional entropy. This is why the same quantity can govern both a single observation and a long message.

Joint entropy (H(X1​,…,Xn​))

Words: 229
Information entropy OurBigBook.com
Joint entropy is the information entropy of an entire random vector, treating its coordinates together as one outcome. For random variables with finite alphabets and joint probability mass function p,
H(X1​,…,Xn​)=−∑x1​,…,xn​:p(x1​,…,xn​)>0​p(x1​,…,xn​)log2​p(x1​,…,xn​).
(2)
It is measured in bits. The distinction from separate information entropies is whether shared uncertainty is counted once or repeatedly. Two copies of the same fair bit have joint entropy one bit, since only 00 and 11 can occur. Two independent fair bits have joint entropy two bits, since all four pairs are possible and equiprobable.
For a less symmetric example, assign probabilities 1/2,1/4,1/4 to (X,Y)=(0,0),(1,0),(1,1) respectively. Then
H(X,Y)=21​⋅1+41​⋅2+41​⋅2=23​ bits.​
(3)
Here X is fair, while P(Y=1)=1/4. Thus H(X)=1 and H(Y)=h2​(1/4)≈0.811278 bits, where h2​ is binary entropy. Adding the separate information entropies would give approximately 1.811278 bits, overcounting by the mutual information I(X;Y)≈0.311278 bits.
The chain rule for information entropy makes the shared information explicit:
H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y∣X).
(4)
In this example Y is certain given X=0 and fair given X=1, so its average conditional entropy is H(Y∣X)=21​⋅0+21​⋅1=1/2 bit. Sending the first coordinate changes how much uncertainty remains in the second. A prefix code with codewords 0, 10, 11 for the three possible pairs has average length 3/2 bits, exactly the joint entropy in this stipulated law.
For finite alphabets,
maxi​H(Xi​)≤H(X1​,…,Xn​)≤∑i​H(Xi​).
(5)
The lower bound follows because knowing the full random vector determines every coordinate; the upper bound follows from conditioning reduces entropy in the chain rule for information entropy. Equality in the upper bound means joint independence. The difference from that upper bound is total correlation, whose noisy parity total correlation example shows why separate pairwise comparisons need not find all the dependence.

Cross-entropy (H(P,Q)=−∑i​pi​logqi​)

Words: 917 Articles: 5
Information entropy OurBigBook.com
Cross-entropy measures the average logarithmic loss incurred when outcomes follow one probability distribution P, but a model assigns probabilities using another probability distribution Q. For a finite alphabet, write their probability mass functions as pi​ and qi​. With base-two logarithms,
H(P,Q)=−∑i:pi​>0​pi​log2​qi​=EP​[−log2​q(X)].
(6)
The expectation is under the true or stipulated source law P, rather than the model Q. Terms with pi​=0 contribute nothing. If pi​>0 but qi​=0 for any outcome, the cross-entropy is +∞: the model rules out an event that can occur. The result is measured in bits; natural logarithms give nats.
The useful identity is cross-entropy equals entropy plus relative entropy:
H(P,Q)=H(P)+DKL​(P∥Q).​
(7)
The source's information entropy H(P) is the baseline uncertainty. The Kullback-Leibler divergence is the extra average cost of a mismatched model, with the true law first and model second. Thus optimizing Q for a fixed P reduces the mismatch, rather than changing the source's own uncertainty. For finite alphabets, the minimum is H(P), attained exactly at Q=P.
Take a three-symbol source with P=(1/2,1/4,1/4). A matched binary prefix code assigns A↦0, B↦10, C↦11, with lengths (1,2,2). A mismatched prefix code instead assigns A↦10, B↦11, C↦0, with lengths (2,2,1), corresponding to Q=(1/4,1/4,1/2). Both prefix codes decode exactly, but the most common symbol is shorter in the matched code. Under the same source P,
H(P)H(P,Q)DKL​(P∥Q)​=21​⋅1+41​⋅2+41​⋅2=23​,=21​⋅2+41​⋅2+41​⋅1=47​,=21​log2​2+41​log2​1+41​log2​(1/2)=41​.​
(8)
The mismatched code costs an extra (1/4) bit per source symbol on average. No information is lost; the penalty is inefficient representation of this source. The mismatch penalty in lossless coding explains when this coding interpretation is exact.
Cross-entropy is also useful when evaluating a predictive distribution. A report that places tiny probability on the realized outcome receives a large logarithmic loss, even when it usually predicts the most common outcome correctly. The loss convention is the negative of the reward convention in the logarithmic scoring rule. Its expected loss is uniquely minimized by the correct probability distribution, making it a strictly proper scoring rule. Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation by Tilmann Gneiting and others treats this connection in §3.1, Example 3; its discussion uses rewards, so the signs are reversed for the losses here.[ref]
I. J. Good's Rational Decisions by I. J. Good appeared in 1952. Its published summary describes a method for encouraging accurate probability estimates and a connection with information theory. Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation by Tilmann Gneiting and others traces the logarithmic scoring rule at least as far back as this 1952 paper.[ref]

Cross-entropy equals entropy plus relative entropy (H(P,Q)−H(P)=DKL​(P∥Q))

For finite probability mass functions, with qi​>0 whenever pi​>0, subtract the two expectations of logarithmic loss:
H(P,Q)−H(P)​=i:pi​>0∑​pi​(log2​pi​−log2​qi​)=i:pi​>0∑​pi​log2​qi​pi​​=DKL​(P∥Q).​

The Gibbs inequality makes the difference nonnegative and zero exactly when P=Q. The finite information entropy still leaves the displayed identity valid in the extended sense. In more general settings, undefined differences such as ∞−∞ cannot be used as a derivation.

Mismatch penalty in lossless coding (EP​[ℓ(X)]=H(P,Q))

Words: 78
Cross-entropy OurBigBook.com
Suppose a binary prefix code has integer codeword lengths ℓi​=−log2​qi​. Under a source with probability mass function pi​, its average codeword length is exactly cross-entropy, ∑i​pi​ℓi​=H(P,Q). The Kraft inequality is satisfied because ∑i​2−ℓi​=∑i​qi​=1. This exact interpretation requires suitable dyadic rational probabilities; a general −log2​qi​ is not an integer codeword length. For positive qi​, a prefix code with lengths ⌈−log2​qi​⌉ exists by the Kraft inequality and has average length between H(P,Q) and H(P,Q)+1. The ceiling function causes the one-symbol rounding overhead. The cross-entropy term describes mismatch overhead; those are distinct costs.

Binary cross-entropy (H(r,q)=−rlog2​q−(1−r)log2​(1−q))

Words: 192
Cross-entropy OurBigBook.com
Let Y follow a Bernoulli distribution with true event probability r, and let the model report event probability q. The binary cross-entropy is its expected logarithmic loss:
H(r,q)=−rlog2​q−(1−r)log2​(1−q).
(10)
For 0<r<1, it is minimized at q=r, where the value is binary entropy h2​(r). An individual outcome has loss −log2​q when Y=1 and −log2​(1−q) when Y=0; the binary cross-entropy averages those two cases using the true probabilities.
For r=1/4, truthful reporting gives h2​(1/4)≈0.811278 bits. Reporting q=1/2 gives one bit. Reporting q=0.01 gives
H(1/4,0.01)=41​log2​100−43​log2​0.99≈1.671839 bits.
(11)
Both q=1/4 and q=0.01 select zero if one predicts only the more likely binary outcome. Their predicted probabilities are very different, however, and cross-entropy reveals the cost of underestimating the event that occurs a quarter of the time. Endpoint losses are computed using the realized outcome rather than multiplying zero by an undefined logarithm.

Empirical cross-entropy (Hn​(Q)=−n−1∑t=1n​log2​q(Xt​))

Words: 165 Articles: 1
Cross-entropy OurBigBook.com
For observations X1​,…,Xn​ of a categorical variable, empirical cross-entropy is the average logarithmic loss under the reported probability mass function q. Writing the empirical distribution as p^​i​=n−1#{t:Xt​=i} gives
Hn​(Q)=−n1​∑t=1n​log2​q(Xt​)=−∑i:p^​i​>0​p^​i​log2​qi​=H(Pn​,Q).
(12)
Under independent and identically distributed observations from P and a fixed model with positive probability for each possible outcome, the law of large numbers gives convergence to H(P,Q). A finite empirical distribution is an estimate of P, rather than the true source law; unobserved outcomes need not be impossible. If the same data are used to choose Q, evaluating the resulting empirical cross-entropy on that data need not estimate its prediction performance on fresh observations without bias.

Cross-entropy and maximum likelihood (argminθ​Hn​(Qθ​)=argmaxθ​∏t​qθ​(Xt​))

For a parametric statistical model with probability mass function qθ​ and an independent and identically distributed model, minimizing empirical cross-entropy is maximum likelihood estimation. The logarithm is strictly increasing, so maximizing ∏t​qθ​(Xt​) is equivalent to maximizing ∑t​log2​qθ​(Xt​). Negating and dividing by n gives the displayed minimization. Restricting the model family can prevent the optimum from matching the empirical distribution, and a finite optimizing parameter need not exist. This algebra does not by itself establish accuracy on new data.[ref]

Differential entropy (h(q)=−∫q(x)logq(x)dx)

Words: 1k Articles: 11
Information entropy OurBigBook.com
Differential entropy describes the spread of a continuous probability distribution relative to the volume scale of its coordinates. For a real random variable X with probability density function f, use natural logarithms and write
he​(X)=−∫R​f(x)lnf(x)dx=−E[lnf(X)].​
(13)
The convention is 0ln0=0. For a finite value, require ∫f∣lnf∣<∞; if both positive and negative contributions are infinite, the expression is undefined. Unlike a probability, a probability density can exceed one. Consequently differential entropy can be negative. Base-two logarithms give h2​=he​/ln2, in bits instead of nats.[ref]
This quantity does not give the exact number of bits needed to store a real number. A recording device supplies a resolution; equal-width bin entropy connects that resolution to discrete information entropy. The useful continuous quantity is what remains after removing the increasing cost of specifying finer bins. Its dependence on coordinates is visible already in differential entropy of a uniform law.

he​(X)=−∫aa+L​L1​lnL1​dx=lnL.
(14)

Relative entropy and coordinate dependence

Words: 146
Differential entropy OurBigBook.com
Apply the same invertible differentiable transformation to two probability distributions with densities f and r. Both transformed densities acquire the same Jacobian determinant factor. Write their transformed densities as fg​ and rg​. Their ratio is unchanged, and the integration measure transforms with the numerator density. Consequently their relative entropy is unchanged:
D(fg​∥rg​)=D(f∥r).
(23)
The densities describe two alternative laws in the same coordinate, with a nonzero Jacobian determinant on their common domain, and the expression is assumed well defined. This explains why comparing two laws can survive a change of measurement units even though either law's differential entropy changes. Mutual information, formulated as relative entropy from a product of marginals, has the same invariance under invertible changes to either observed coordinate.
Differential entropy is also the entropy term in the evidence lower bound for continuous variational inference. Its coordinate dependence must be combined consistently with that of the model density; changing units does not justify changing only one term of the objective.

Min-entropy (H∞​(X))

Words: 816 Articles: 1
Information entropy OurBigBook.com
For a discrete random variable X with a finite or countable alphabet and probability mass function px​=P(X=x), define pmax​=maxx​px​. Its min-entropy, using base-two logarithms, is
H∞​(X)=−log2​pmax​=minx:px​>0​(−log2​px​).
(26)
The unit is the bit. This is the smallest information content of any possible outcome: it records the easiest outcome to predict, whereas Shannon entropy averages information content over all outcomes. A normalized countable probability mass function has an attained positive maximum, so H∞​(X) is finite even when Shannon entropy is infinite. Outcomes of zero probability cannot minimize the surprise and are omitted from the minimum; including extra impossible symbols changes neither quantity.[ref]
Suppose someone knows the probability mass function but receives no observation correlated with X, and must name exactly one outcome. Naming x succeeds with probability px​, so naming a most likely outcome is optimal:
pguess​(X)=maxx​P(X=x)=2−H∞​(X).
(27)
Tied maxima give equally good guesses. Randomizing independently among guesses cannot improve success: if the guess has probability mass function qx​, its success probability is ∑x​qx​px​≤pmax​∑x​qx​=pmax​. This is the simplest instance of maximum a posteriori decoding. The identity gives min-entropy an operational meaning: H∞​(X)≥k bits means every single guess succeeds with probability at most 2−k, under these information assumptions.[ref]
For an exact analytical example on symbols A,B,C,D,E, compare the two probability mass functions
u=(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4,0),b=(1/2,1/8,1/8,1/8,1/8).
(28)
Under u, each possible outcome has information content 2 bits; under b, A has information content 1 bit and each other outcome has 3 bits. Consequently their Shannon entropies agree exactly,
H(u)=441​⋅2=2,H(b)=21​⋅1+481​⋅3=2,
(29)
but H∞​(u)=2 and H∞​(b)=1 bits. A best single guess therefore succeeds with probability 1/4 under u and 1/2 under b. The same average surprise can hide a factor of two in immediate predictability. The zero entry in u contributes zero to Shannon entropy, using 0log2​0=0.
For the discrete uniform distribution on m possible outcomes, H∞​=log2​m bits and the best single guess succeeds with probability 1/m. A certain outcome has H∞​=0 and success probability one. Thus min-entropy is useful when a common outcome could defeat an attempt to make outputs unpredictable, even if rare alternatives raise Shannon entropy.
For cryptography, this unconditional calculation alone does not certify security: an observer might know X already. If a report Y=X reveals a uniform four-outcome source, its unconditional min-entropy remains 2 bits but guessing after the report succeeds with probability one. For finite classical reports, the appropriate success calculation uses conditional probabilities and maximum a posteriori decoding:
pguess​(X∣Y)=∑y:pY​(y)>0​pY​(y)maxx​P(X=x∣Y=y).
(30)
The conditional entropy H(X∣Y) instead averages remaining surprise. A security argument must specify the observer's information and the protocol's required guarantee; neither unconditional min-entropy nor Shannon entropy supplies those assumptions.

Information entropy dominates min-entropy

Words: 389

For a discrete random variable X with probability mass function px​, using base-two logarithms for both Shannon entropy and min-entropy,
H(X)≥H∞​(X)≥0.
(31)
The first inequality says that average information content cannot be below the least information content of a possible outcome. For a finite alphabet, equality H(X)=H∞​(X) holds exactly when the probability mass function is uniform on its positive-probability outcomes. Impossible outcomes do not need to share that positive probability. In particular, (1/2,1/2,0) has both quantities equal to 1 bit, while (1,0,0) has both zero. The same equality characterization holds for a countable alphabet: equality forces only finitely many positive-probability outcomes, because all must have the same positive mass. The uniform maximum of Shannon entropy is discussed in Shannon's original treatment.[ref]
For the proof, 0<px​≤pmax​ gives −log2​px​≥−log2​pmax​; taking the expected value proves information entropy dominates min-entropy. More precisely, the nonnegative gap is
H(X)−H∞​(X)=∑x:px​>0​px​log2​px​pmax​​≥0.
(32)
It vanishes precisely when every positive term vanishes, namely px​=pmax​ for every possible outcome. With m such outcomes, normalization forces pmax​=1/m. If Shannon entropy is infinite, the inequality still holds and is strict, because min-entropy of a countable probability mass function is finite. No subtraction of two infinite quantities is needed.
An analytical family makes the gap visible. Give A probability q and each of B,C,D,E probability (1−q)/4, with 1/5≤q≤1. Then A is a most likely outcome, so its probability is also the best single-guess success probability, and
H∞​(q)=−log2​q,H(q)=h2​(q)+2(1−q),
(33)
where h2​ is binary entropy. The entropy bound with one prescribed probability explains the second expression: it separates whether A occurred from which of four equally likely alternatives occurred. At q=1/5, all five outcomes are uniform and both quantities are log2​5≈2.3219 bits. At q=1/2, Shannon entropy is 2 bits while min-entropy is 1 bit, reproducing the biased example in min-entropy. At q=1, both are zero; the four impossible alternatives contribute nothing. For every 1/5<q<1, the probability mass function is nonuniform on its possible outcomes and the gap is strictly positive.
For finite Shannon entropy, exponentiating the inequality gives the useful but potentially loose bound pguess​(X)≥2−H(X). Equality holds exactly for a discrete uniform distribution on the possible outcomes. Thus Shannon entropy alone supplies a lower bound on optimal success, not the upper bound needed to promise that every guess is unlikely. Shannon's source coding theorem concerns average compression under its hypotheses, while min-entropy answers the single-guess question directly. The two quantities serve different tasks even when both are expressed in bits.

Chain rule for information entropy (H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y∣X))

Words: 648 Articles: 1
Information entropy OurBigBook.com
For finite discrete random variables, the chain rule counts uncertainty in two stages:
H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y∣X).
(37)
The joint entropy equals the first variable's information entropy plus the average conditional entropy left in the second. All logarithms here have base two, so the unit is the bit; zero-probability terms contribute zero. The identity holds without assuming independence.[ref]
Iterating gives H(X1​,…,Xn​)=∑i​H(Xi​∣X1​,…,Xi−1​). The first summand means H(X1​), with no earlier observations. Each later summand counts only the new uncertainty after the preceding random variables are known. For independent random variables, every term is its ordinary information entropy; for dependent ones, adding those ordinary information entropies can count shared uncertainty repeatedly. This is the reason the chain rule for information entropy is useful in lossless source coding and in calculating an entropy rate.
A short proof starts from the factorization of the joint probability mass function into a marginal distribution and a conditional probability:
pX,Y​(x,y)=pX​(x)pY∣X​(y∣x),−log2​pX,Y​(x,y)=−log2​pX​(x)−log2​pY∣X​(y∣x).
(38)
Use this only where pX,Y​(x,y)>0, then average with those joint probabilities. Summing over y in the first term gives H(X); the second is precisely H(Y∣X). Repeating the same step with a longer random vector proves the iterated identity. Reversing the order also gives H(X,Y)=H(Y)+H(X∣Y): the two contributions can change with the order, but their total cannot.

Correlated binary variables (B=A⊕N,H(A,B)=1+h2​(p))

Words: 444
Chain rule for information entropy OurBigBook.com
Let A be a fair bit, let N be an independent Bernoulli random variable with P(N=1)=p, and set B=A⊕N, where ⊕ is exclusive or. Thus N=1 flips the first bit. This constructs a binary symmetric channel with a fair input, for 0≤p≤1. The joint probability mass function is
A=0A=1P(B=b)​B=0(1−p)/2p/21/2​B=1p/2(1−p)/21/2​P(A=a)1/21/21​​
(39)
The column sums show that B is also a fair bit, whatever p is. Both marginal distributions therefore have information entropy one bit. Given A, only the flip decision remains unknown, giving conditional entropy H(B∣A)=h2​(p), where
h2​(p)=−plog2​p−(1−p)log2​(1−p)
(40)
is the binary entropy, with h2​(0)=h2​(1)=0. The chain rule for information entropy and the definition of mutual information now give
H(A,B)=1+h2​(p),I(A;B)=1−h2​(p).​
(41)
Take p=1/10. The four joint probabilities, in row order, are (0.45,0.05,0.05,0.45). Directly from the definition of joint entropy,
H(A,B)=−2(0.45)log2​(0.45)−2(0.05)log2​(0.05)≈1.468995594 bits.
(42)
Conditioning on either value of A gives probabilities 0.9 and 0.1 for the two values of B, hence
H(B∣A)=h2​(0.1)≈0.468995594 bits,I(A;B)≈0.531004406 bits.
(43)
Adding H(A)+H(B)=2 would overcount by exactly I(A;B). These are average uncertainties under the stipulated probability distribution; the four possible pairs are still represented by four distinct outcomes.
There is another way to see the decomposition: (A,B)↦(A,A⊕B)=(A,N) is a bijection. It merely relabels the four outcomes, so it preserves joint entropy. The independence of A and N then gives H(A,B)=H(A,N)=H(A)+H(N)=1+h2​(p). Describing the initial bit and the flip decision separates shared uncertainty from new uncertainty.
At p=0, the bits are identical: B=A, so H(B∣A)=0 and I(A;B)=1. At p=1, they are complementary: B=1−A, with exactly the same conditional entropy and mutual information. In either case, observing one determines the other. At p=1/2, all four joint probabilities are 1/4; the bits are independent, H(A,B)=2, and I(A;B)=0. Perfect disagreement is therefore just as informative as perfect agreement.
For p>1/2, guessing A=1−B succeeds with probability p, better than guessing A=B. Changing B to 1−B replaces p by 1−p and preserves mutual information, since the relabeling is invertible. What matters is how well the observation distinguishes the input, rather than how often its symbol matches the input. The same symmetry makes the binary entropy curve peak at independence, while the mutual information curve reaches its minimum there.
