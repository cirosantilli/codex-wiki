Net radiative heat exchange (Q˙​rad​=ϵσA(T4−Ts4​))

 ... Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Heat Stefan–Boltzmann law
OurBigBook.com Words: 623 Articles: 1
For a small, uniform surface at temperature T, enclosed by much larger black walls all at temperature Ts​, the outward net heat transfer by thermal radiation is
Q˙​rad​=ϵσA(T4−Ts4​).
(31)
Assume an opaque grey body that is a diffuse thermal emitter, with the same emissivity ϵ and radiometric absorptance at the surface's current temperature. Its emitting area A sees only the enclosure: the view factor to the walls is one, with no self-viewing surface, opening or intervening obstruction. The intervening medium must neither absorb nor emit appreciable thermal radiation; a vacuum supplies a useful idealization. The enclosure is maintained at uniform Ts​. The small-body assumption makes that reservoir description physically reasonable; genuinely black isothermal walls supply the incoming irradiance even without an infinite area ratio. Positive Q˙​rad​ means net loss by the surface; negative means net gain. This expression counts thermal radiation alone, not all heat transfer.[ref]
The reasoning is a two-way energy balance. Black walls supply irradiance E=σTs4​, so the surface absorbs Pabs​=ϵAE and emits Pemit​=ϵAσT4. Subtract these to obtain Q˙​rad​. For an opaque surface the remaining fraction 1−ϵ of incoming radiant flux is reflected; counting emitted plus reflected outgoing radiant flux minus total incoming radiant flux gives the same result. Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation justifies equal spectral absorption and emission factors; the grey body assumption lets one factor be taken outside both spectral integrals.
Choose ϵ=0.8, A=0.01m2, T=500K and Ts​=300K. These are stipulated model inputs, not measured material properties. Using σ≈5.670374419×10−8Wm−2K−4 gives
Pemit​≈28.3519W,Pabs​≈3.6744W,Q˙​rad​≈24.6775W.
(32)
The incoming irradiance is 459.300Wm−2, corresponding to 4.5930W incident on the surface; only 80% is absorbed. At T=Ts​=300K, emitted and absorbed powers are both 3.6744W, so radiative equilibrium has zero net exchange despite continuing emission. Changing only emissivity to 0.2 reduces both one-way powers and the net loss by a factor of four, giving 6.1694W net. A low-emissivity coating therefore suppresses both radiative cooling to colder walls and radiative heating from hotter walls.
Near equal temperatures, write T=Ts​+ΔT. Taylor's theorem gives
Q˙​rad​=4ϵσATs3​ΔT+O((ΔT)2),hrad​=4ϵσTs3​.
(33)
For constant emissivity, the radiative heat transfer coefficient hrad​ is approximately 4.8992Wm−2K−1 at Ts​=300K and ϵ=0.8. This local linear approximation is useful for small temperature differences; the 200K difference in the worked example needs the full fourth-power expression.
A cooling experiment requires a wider first law of thermodynamics balance. In a uniform-temperature model without a heater,
C(T)dtdT​=−Q˙​rad​−Q˙​conv​−Q˙​cond​,
(34)
where C(T) is the sample's heat capacity and the last two terms are signed outward convection and thermal conduction losses. A vacuum suppresses convection but does not eliminate thermal conduction through supports or sensor leads. A cooling slope therefore cannot establish emissivity unless these paths and heat capacity are independently constrained. Multiple wall temperatures, incomplete view factors, wavelength-selective materials or appreciable intervening absorption require a more complete radiative transfer calculation.
