Pressure coefficient (Cp​=(p−p∞​)/q∞​)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics Lift (force)
OurBigBook.com Words: 798 Articles: 1
The pressure coefficient compares a local static pressure with a specified reference static pressure, using a fixed reference dynamic pressure:
Cp​(x)=q∞​p(x)−p∞​​,q∞​=21​ρ∞​U∞2​>0.
(10)
The reference mass density, speed, pressure and location must accompany a reported pressure coefficient. Keep the same q∞​ throughout a surface distribution; replacing it by local ρU2/2 gives a different quantity and fails at a stagnation point. The definition itself requires neither Bernoulli's principle nor inviscid flow, so measured pressure coefficients remain useful when those idealizations fail.
Positive Cp​ means p>p∞​; zero means equality; negative Cp​ means p<p∞​. This sign does not specify the sign of absolute pressure. For stipulated p∞​=101325Pa absolute and q∞​=240Pa, Cp​=−3 gives p=100605Pa absolute: a 720Pa deficit, not a negative absolute pressure. If p∞​ equals atmospheric pressure, the numerator is gauge pressure; a wind tunnel's reference static pressure need not equal room atmospheric pressure. These are reference choices, not different physical pressures.
Under the same-height, constant-mass density ρ=ρ∞​, steady flow and inviscid flow hypotheses of the Bernoulli equation, and with the same Bernoulli invariant as the reference flow,
p+21​ρU2=p∞​+21​ρU∞2​,Cp​=1−(U∞​U​)2.
(11)
The first equation immediately gives the second after subtracting p∞​ and dividing by q∞​. Hence a lossless stagnation point has Cp​=1, U=U∞​ gives Cp​=0, and U=2U∞​ gives Cp​=−3. For a real stationary wall satisfying the no-slip boundary condition, the velocity is zero right at the wall; inserting that wall velocity in an inviscid flow formula would wrongly predict Cp​=1 everywhere. A thin boundary layer approximation instead relates the wall pressure to the outer-flow pressure and uses the outer velocity where the Bernoulli equation applies.
For potential flow around a circular cylinder of radius a with zero circulation, the surface tangential velocity is uθ​=−2U∞​sinθ. Thus the ideal pressure coefficient is
Cp​(θ)=1−4sin2θ.
(12)
With θ measured from downstream, the front is at π and the rear at zero. Both ideal stagnation points have Cp​=1, while the sides at π/2 and 3π/2 have Cp​=−3. This exact model is a useful baseline for an experiment; its complete rearward pressure recovery is not a prediction for a separated real fluid wake.
To obtain a force, integrate the local normal contributions with their directions. Let n be the unit normal vector from the body into the fluid and Σ its closed wetted surface:
Fp​=−∫Σ​pndA=−q∞​∫Σ​Cp​ndA,∫Σ​ndA=0.
(13)
The second equality removes a spatially constant reference pressure over the closed surface; it does not remove a nonuniform hydrostatic pressure or justify dropping the reference term on an isolated open patch. A negative Cp​ contributes outwards in this reference-subtracted calculation, even though a positive absolute pressure still pushes inwards locally. Project Fp​ onto the chosen lift and drag directions. The full fluid force also includes ∫Σ​σvisc​ndA from the viscous stress tensor, including tangential shear stress; pressure tapping does not measure that contribution.
For the two-dimensional cylinder, force per unit span follows from dA=adθ per unit span and n=(cosθ,sinθ):
(Dp′​,Lp′​)=−q∞​a∫02π​Cp​(θ)(cosθ,sinθ)dθ.
(14)
Substituting Cp​=1−4sin2θ gives (0,0): opposite points have identical pressure coefficients and opposite normal vectors. Zero drag despite a strongly nonuniform pressure is the instructive limitation associated with D'Alembert's paradox. Real viscosity, boundary layers and flow separation can prevent rearward pressure recovery and produce pressure drag. Thus the measured curve can test the model and estimate a pressure contribution without proving that total drag vanishes.
A motivating measurement is Virginia Tech's Experiment 3 - Flow Past a Circular Cylinder. Its open-jet wind tunnel holds a rotatable 140mm cylinder with end plates and 36 one-millimetre pressure tappings at ten-degree intervals. A reference Pitot-static tube supplies p0∞​ and p∞​ to one digital manometer; a second connects a surface tap to its positive port and reference static pressure to its negative port. Record the simultaneous readings:
Δpref​=p0∞​−p∞​,Δpi​=pi​−p∞​,Cp,i​=Δpref​Δpi​​.
(15)
The last equality uses the low-speed stopping relation, not an additional speed measurement. The manual requires sealed tubing and zero checks with the tunnel off. It also provides a water-column manometer: read each column's initial level, and subtract the independently measured reference-to-atmosphere pressure from the tap-to-atmosphere readings. Record tap positions after rotation. Compare the two instruments and check spanwise uniformity; end plates do not guarantee two-dimensional flow. [ref]
The inferential distinction matters: the manometers directly measure pressure differences, the ratio estimates pressure coefficients, and a surface integral estimates the pressure part of the force. Measure room absolute pressure and temperature if converting the reference difference to speed through the ideal gas law ρ=pabs​/(RT); use thermodynamic temperature in that equation. For force reconstruction, cover the complete contour and use numerical integration with the tap geometry. Finite tap spacing, unknown end effects, probe disturbance and fluctuations limit that reconstruction; tubes and instruments also average or delay changing pressures. Retain the signed raw readings, repeated readings and reference readings so that these limits can be assessed. A small reference difference magnifies any zero error in the ratio, rather than producing a reliably large Cp​.
