Group velocity (vg​=∇k​ω)

 ... Area of mathematics Analysis Partial differential equation Wave equation Dispersion relation Phase velocity and group velocity
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 7
The group velocity of a differentiable, real dispersion relation branch is
cg​=∇k​ω(k0​),cg​=dkdω​(k0​) in one dimension.
(12)
It describes the leading translation of a narrow-band wave packet centred on wavevector k0​, assuming a linear model, a locally uniform medium and negligible loss. Narrow-band means that the appreciably excited wavevectors occupy a small neighbourhood on which one smooth branch can be linearized. Write its complex wavefield as
ψ(x,t)=∫a(q)ei[(k0​+q)⋅x−ω(k0​+q)t]dq.
(13)
The Taylor expansion ω(k0​+q)≃ω0​+cg​⋅q gives
ψ(x,t)≃ei(k0​⋅x−ω0​t)A(x−cg​t),A(y)=∫a(q)eiq⋅ydq.
(14)
Thus the rapid wave phase follows the phase velocity, while the slowly varying wave envelope follows the group velocity. This Fourier analysis argument is an outline of the approximation, rather than a statement that every wave packet translates rigidly forever. In one dimension a useful short-time condition is ∣ω′′(k0​)∣t(Δk)2≪1, where Δk is the characteristic spectral width; neglected higher derivatives must also remain small. Curvature of the dispersion relation eventually changes the wave envelope.[ref]
Two nearby plane waves make the derivative visible without an integral. For equal wave amplitudes a, define kˉ=(k1​+k2​)/2, ωˉ=(ω1​+ω2​)/2, Δk=k2​−k1​ and Δω=ω2​−ω1​. The superposition principle gives the exact identity
acos(k1​x−ω1​t)+acos(k2​x−ω2​t)=2acos(2Δkx−Δωt​)cos(kˉx−ωˉt).
(15)
The periodic wave envelope has speed Δω/Δk, approaching group velocity as the two wavenumbers coalesce, while the carrier wave phase has speed ωˉ/kˉ. Two tones alone form an infinite beat train, not a localized wave packet. The figure shows an exact two-tone surface gravity wave model with dimensionless ω=k​: k1​=0.9, k2​=1.1. Its carrier phase velocity is approximately 0.99875, its finite-difference envelope velocity approximately 0.50063, and ω′(1)=0.5 is the limiting group velocity. The marked carrier phase line need not coincide exactly with a maximum of the full modulated wave.
The deep-water surface gravity wave therefore has cg​=cp​/2, whereas the deep-water capillary wave limit has cg​=3cp​/2: crests can drift either forward or backward relative to a wave envelope. For finite-depth surface gravity waves, differentiating the dispersion relation gives
cg​=2cp​​(1+sinh(2kh)2kh​),k>0.
(16)
Here sinh is the hyperbolic sine. The shallow-water limit has cg​≃cp​≃gh​; the deep-water limit has cg​≃cp​/2.[ref]
In the wave tank, produce a finite train with a smooth turn-on and turn-off and time the same wave envelope feature at two probes separated by distance L; its transit estimate is L/Δt. Independently follow wave crests through that train. Techet's 2003 lab asks students to observe a deep-water train's foremost crest disappearing as the group moves forward, and to compare deep- and shallow-water cases. The interpretation is that a wave crest overtakes the slower wave envelope, dwindling at its front while other crests become visible at its rear. This is a qualitative expected observation in the teaching protocol, not a published set of measured curves.[ref]
A useful controlled version keeps the paddle wave amplitude small, records h and λ, and checks both ka≪1 and a/h≪1. Compare runs with kh≫1 and kh≪1, rather than assuming every ripple tank is shallow relative to every wavelength. Increase train duration at fixed central frequency to narrow its spectrum, and check that the envelope transit estimate stabilizes. Calibrate both probes, sample fast enough to resolve the carrier, and compare records before reflected waves return; an absorbing beach reduces this contamination. A current changes laboratory velocities, large wave amplitudes introduce nonlinear effects, and very short wavelengths require surface tension. Strong damping or noticeable reshaping makes the chosen threshold or peak an unreliable marker of simple group velocity. These are proposed controls and model limits, not claims that the cited lab measured every correction.
Three velocities should consequently be kept separate. The group velocity predicts narrow-band wave envelope motion. An energy-transport velocity is energy flux divided by energy density, using the appropriate time average; it agrees with group velocity in standard lossless linear wave models when all stored energy is included.[ref] A signal-front velocity concerns the earliest response to a new disturbance and requires a causal model over its full spectrum. A strictly band-limited wave packet has no sharply bounded onset, and a mathematical two-tone envelope cannot test front arrival. In strongly absorbing or broadband propagation, a single group velocity need not describe the observed peak, energy transport, or first detectable response. A complex ω or k also prevents interpreting its derivative as an ordinary real transport velocity without further analysis.
In particular, an unusually large or negative group velocity from pulse reshaping does not establish superluminal information transmission. For the causal dielectric model analysed by Fitzpatrick, the field ahead of the front vanishes for t<x/c, even when its phase velocity exceeds the vacuum speed of light c. That result concerns the electromagnetic model's causality; the ideal water-wave diagrams above contain no relativistic signal-front claim.[ref]
Finally, zero wavenumber requires care: the positive-frequency wave equation branch c∣k∣ is not differentiable at k=0, so it has no unique group velocity there, although its one-sided values are ±c. A smooth gapped branch such as the Klein-Gordon equation instead has ω′(0)=0 despite undefined phase velocity at k=0. The same signed branch must be used in both derivatives and ratios; a negative group velocity means an envelope moving in the negative coordinate direction, not a negative elapsed time. The practical value of group velocity is precisely this translation between a local slope on a dispersion diagram and a measurable packet transit, with its assumptions made explicit.
The Gaussian envelope in a Klein-Gordon medium supplies a quantitative next step: the first derivative translates the wave envelope, the second derivative spreads it, and higher derivatives limit that approximation. At finite spectral width, a peak's measured speed need not equal the group velocity at the central wavenumber. Neither a peak nor a fixed fraction of its height generally identifies front velocity.
