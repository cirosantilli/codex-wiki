Taylor series (∑n=0∞​f(n)(a)(x−a)n/n!)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Calculus Taylor's theorem
OurBigBook.com Words: 385 Articles: 2
For a function with derivatives of every order at a, its Taylor series is the power series
∑n=0∞​n!f(n)(a)​(x−a)n.
(64)
Its partial sums are Taylor polynomials. At a=0 it is called a Maclaurin series. Forming the coefficients, proving that its partial sums form a convergent sequence, and proving that their limit equals f(x) are separate tasks. A smooth function provides all the coefficients, but a real analytic function is additionally represented locally by the resulting power series.
For the exponential function, every derivative of ex at zero equals one, giving
ex=∑n=0∞​n!xn​.
(65)
Here equality holds for every real x: the Taylor remainder is bounded by e∣x∣∣x∣N+1/(N+1)!, which tends to zero for each fixed x. At x=1, the degree-five Taylor polynomial is 163/60≈2.71666667, and the absolute difference from e is less than e/720. The factorial denominator eventually overwhelms every fixed power, allowing local derivative information to reconstruct this function throughout the real line.
The geometric series shows why the region of validity matters. For f(x)=1/(1−x), the Taylor series at zero is ∑k≥0​xk, with radius of convergence one. For x=1, its finite Taylor polynomial satisfies the exact identity
1−x1​−∑k=0N​xk=1−xxN+1​.
(66)
At x=1/2 and N=5, the remainder is 1/32. At x=0.9, the same degree-five polynomial has value 4.68559 and remainder 5.31441, although the Taylor series still converges to 10 there. Convergence of the infinite expression does not guarantee accuracy from a particular small number of terms. For ∣x∣>1, even the terms fail to tend to zero, so this power series cannot represent the function. The standard expansions and the conditions for convergent series are discussed in OpenStax, working with Taylor series.
A stronger caution comes from the flat function
h(x)={e−1/x2,0,​x=0,x=0.​
(67)
It is a smooth function with h(n)(0)=0 for every n, so every Taylor polynomial at zero is zero and its Taylor series converges everywhere to zero. Nevertheless h(1/2)=e−4>0. Off zero, repeated differentiation produces a polynomial in 1/x times e−1/x2; the exponential decay makes each required limit at zero vanish. This explains how all the local coefficients can miss a positive function arbitrarily close to the centre. Real analyticity supplies the additional condition that rules out this behavior.
