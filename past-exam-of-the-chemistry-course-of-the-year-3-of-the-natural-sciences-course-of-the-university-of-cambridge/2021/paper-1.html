The isolobal analogy compares fragments through their frontier molecular orbitals: their number, approximate energies, shapes, symmetries and electron occupancies should correspond. It does not require the fragments to have the same total number of electrons or the same constituent elements. If the orbitals available for forming new bonds correspond, analogous ways of joining the fragments become plausible.
For example, CH3∙​ and Mn(CO)5∙​ are isolobal. The methyl radical has one singly occupied approximately outward-pointing orbital; removing one carbonyl ligand from an octahedral molecular geometry leaves an analogous frontier orbital on the 17-electron Mn(CO)5​ fragment. Joining two such fragments pairs the two electrons: the organic example is the C–C bond of ethane, and the organometallic example is the Mn–Mn bond of Mn2​(CO)10​. The analogy predicts a bonding pattern, rather than equality of bond energies or reaction rates.[ref]

Use Wade's rules with the metal included as a skeletal vertex. Each BH vertex supplies two skeletal electrons, each CH supplies three, and the two additional hydrogen atoms supply two more. The Ru(CO)3​ fragment supplies 8+3(2)−12=2 skeletal electrons after the metal's non-skeletal orbitals have been accounted for. Thus
Nskeletal​=3(2)+2(3)+2+2=16,SEP=8=6+2.
(1)
The six-vertex cluster is therefore nido, with a pentagonal-pyramidal skeleton: Ru is the apex, bonded to the five vertices of the planar B3​C2​ base, and the three carbonyl ligands project away from that base. This is the six-vertex fragment obtained by removing an equatorial vertex from a seven-vertex pentagonal bipyramid. The five ring atoms carry their terminal B–H or C–H bonds; the two additional hydrogens belong to the hydrogen-rich open-face bonding framework. The skeletal electron count does not, by itself, locate every bridging hydride, so a unique detailed hydrogen arrangement should not be inferred from it.

b OurBigBook.com
There are two carbon-placement isomers, represented by the cyclic base sequences CCBBB and CBCBB. The carbon vertices are adjacent in the first and separated by one boron vertex in the second. Rotation or reversal of a sequence does not produce another constitutional isomer.
Each arrangement has a reflection symmetry that exchanges a pair of boron sites while leaving the third distinct. Consequently each can give two 11B environments, with site populations 2:1, in a proton-decoupled NMR spectrum. This assumes that the hydrogen and carbonyl arrangements preserve, or rapidly average to, the corresponding skeletal symmetry. Two peaks alone neither choose between the two skeletons nor establish a unique hydrogen arrangement; accidental overlap or fluxionality can also reduce the number of observed signals.

The usual qualitative prediction is the carbon-separated CBCBB isomer. It has four B–C ring edges and one B–B edge, whereas the adjacent-carbon isomer has two B–C, two B–B and one C–C edge. Separating the more electron-rich carbon vertices distributes skeletal electron density more evenly and avoids concentrating both carbon vertices on one edge, a useful carborane stability heuristic.
This is a thermodynamic suggestion, not a consequence of the peak count. hydrogen placement, metal–ring interactions and entropy can affect the ordering. Establishing the preferred isomer experimentally would require reversible interconversion and an equilibrium composition, rather than simply identifying the product formed fastest.

A triangular prism has exactly the required nine metal–metal edges: six edges around two triangular faces and three edges joining corresponding vertices of those faces. It naturally offers two symmetry-distinct edge families, of sizes six and three.
With twelve Cl− ligands, neutral Re6​Cl12​ has total rhenium oxidation number +12, hence six formally Re(II), d5 centres. After treating the Re–Cl interactions as ligand bonds, the available metal d electrons are
6(7−2)=30 electrons=15 bonding pairs.
(2)
Two localized bond order assignments use those fifteen pairs:
III​six triangular edges12​three prism edges31​total bond order6+9=1512+3=15​
(3)
In scheme I the three inter-triangle edges should be shorter; in scheme II the six triangle edges should be shorter. Both give each Re five electrons' worth of metal–metal bond-order contributions: two singles plus a triple, or two doubles plus a single. The electron count permits both schemes; identifying which family is shorter distinguishes them at this level. Actual cluster molecular orbitals are delocalized, so these integer bond orders summarize bonding rather than asserting isolated textbook multiple bonds on every edge.

a OurBigBook.com
The key is coupling redox equilibrium to hydrolysis and precipitation. Ce(IV) has a much greater tendency than Ce(III) to form a hydrated cerium dioxide solid. Raising pH therefore preferentially stabilizes the oxidized form. In the presence of dissolved oxygen a representative net reaction is
4Ce3++O2​+6H2​O⇌4CeO2​(s)+12H+.
(4)
Removing H+ drives this reaction towards Ce(IV). Writing the solid as hydrated oxide changes its water content, not this redox argument.
The Nernst equation makes the pH effect explicit. At 25∘C, the reduction CeO2​+4H++e−→Ce3++2H2​O has a pH slope approximately −0.236 V per pH unit at fixed Ce(III) activity. The oxygen/water reduction has slope about −0.059 V per pH unit. Raising pH thus makes oxidation of Ce(III) by oxygen increasingly favourable within the relevant phase-stability region. Conversely strong acid favours soluble Ce(III) relative to the oxide and makes Ce(IV) a stronger oxidizing agent. pH changes the redox driving force; an electron acceptor or donor is still required, and slow oxygen evolution or oxidation kinetics can prevent immediate equilibration.

c OurBigBook.com
A natural feed can contain thorium and uranium, as well as other lanthanides. Th(IV) hydrolyses readily and can accompany the cerium precipitate; uranium speciation depends on its oxidation state and the ligands present. Raising pH too far also precipitates other rare-earth hydroxides. Even before their bulk precipitation thresholds, adsorption and coprecipitation on a high-area hydrated cerium oxide can contaminate the solid. Local high-pH zones during base addition and incomplete Ce(III) oxidation make the separation less reproducible.
Thus a cerium-rich precipitate need not be pure cerium. A sensible test measures the Ce oxidation state, analyses both filtrate and washed solid, and includes thorium-containing and thorium-free mixtures; otherwise low dissolved cerium could be mistaken for selective purification.

d OurBigBook.com
A better controlled route is selective solvent extraction in acid, followed by selective stripping, rather than precipitating everything near overlapping hydrolysis thresholds. Oxidize Ce(III) to Ce(IV) in a controlled acidic nitrate medium, extract its nitrate complexes into an organic donor phase, and separate the phases. Then reduce the extracted Ce(IV) to Ce(III) and back-extract it into an aqueous phase. Trivalent rare earths are less strongly extracted under suitable conditions.
This still needs a thorium separation: tributyl phosphate can coextract Th(IV). One demonstrated strategy deliberately coextracts Ce(IV) and Th(IV), then uses reducing peroxide solution to strip Ce as Ce(III), followed by a separate thorium strip. The appropriate controls are distribution measurements for each ion at the chosen acid concentration, a cerium oxidation-state check, and a complete mass balance over extraction and stripping. Selectivity comes from combining oxidation state and complexation, not from assuming that one extraction removes every contaminant.[ref]

Disconnect the tertiary alcohol at its bond to the alkenyl group. The required carbonyl partner is MeCOCH(Me)Et; the other partner is an E-configured PhCH2​CH=CH organometallic reagent. This disconnection fixes the alkene geometry before the alcohol is made.
First form the ethyl-side enolate of butan-2-one under equilibrating conditions, then trap it with ethyl iodide and isolate the required monoalkylated ketone:
MeCOCH2​Me⟶MeCOCH(Me)Et.
(5)
The incoming group is Et: methylation would give a carbon skeleton one carbon too short. Because an unsymmetrical ketone can form two enolates and undergo repeated alkylation, regioisomer separation and controlled conversion are part of this step.
Independently perform a halo-Wittig reaction on phenylacetaldehyde using Ph3​P=CHBr and isolate E-PhCH2​CH=CHBr from any geometrical mixture. magnesium insertion in dry ether gives the corresponding vinylmagnesium halide; add the isolated ketone at low temperature and protonate the resulting alkoxide. Addition to C=O preserves the pre-existing C=C geometry:
(E)-PhCH2​CH=CHMgBr+MeCOCH(Me)Et⟶(E)-PhCH2​CH=CHC(OH)(Me)CH(Me)Et.
(6)
The E alkene and the complete fifteen-carbon skeleton are obtained. No absolute configuration is specified at the newly formed alcohol centre, so an unselected stereoisomeric mixture there is acceptable. An alternative carbon–carbon disconnection is acetylide addition to the same ketone followed by a controlled trans-selective alkyne reduction; the vinyl-reagent route avoids relying on reduction of a tertiary propargylic alcohol.

Open the lactone formally at its acyl–oxygen bond, then replace the resulting secondary alcohol by its ketone precursor. With R=CH(Me)CH(Me)2​, the useful sequence of retrosynthetic intermediates is
lactone ⟸ HO2​CCH(Me)CH2​CH2​CH(OH)R ⟸ EtO2​CCH(Me)CH2​CH2​COR.
(7)
Disconnect the last compound as a Michael addition product of the methyl-side enolate of RCOCH3​ and ethyl methacrylate:
RCOCH3​+CH2​=C(Me)CO2​Et⟶RCOCH2​CH2​CH(Me)CO2​Et.
(8)
The substituted carbon of the methacrylate becomes the methyl-bearing carbon alpha to the lactone carbonyl. The ketone carbon becomes the oxygen-bearing ring carbon. This checks the six-membered ring connectivity.
The required branched methyl ketone itself comes from butan-2-one by ethyl-side aldol addition to acetone, followed by dehydration and selective hydrogenation:
MeCOCH2​Me⟶MeCOCH(Me)C(OH)Me2​⟶MeCOC(Me)=CMe2​⟶MeCOCH(Me)CHMe2​.
(9)
Choose and isolate the aldol regioisomer shown. For the subsequent Michael step, use a bulky base at low temperature to favour its less hindered methyl-side kinetic enolate. Reduce the resulting ketone selectively with NaBH4​ while retaining the ester, hydrolyse the ester, then acidify and promote intramolecular esterification with water removal. The hydroxy acid closes to the required six-membered lactone. There is no specified stereochemical relationship to enforce, but a stereochemically pure target would require an additional selectivity or resolution step.

7 OurBigBook.com
An acetal retrosynthesis separates the target into trans-cyclohexane-1,2-diol and cyclohexyl methyl ketone. Both fragments can be prepared using separate portions of cyclohexene.
For the diol, epoxidation with a peroxy acid such as mCPBA gives cyclohexene oxide. Acid-catalysed opening with water occurs by backside attack on the protonated epoxide, giving the anti, hence trans, diol. A syn dihydroxylation would give the wrong relative configuration.
For the ketone, add HBr to another portion of cyclohexene to obtain bromocyclohexane, form its grignard reagent with Mg in dry ether, and add acetonitrile. Hydrolyse the resulting magnesium imine salt to C6​H11​COMe. A nitrile is useful here because addition followed by hydrolysis stops at the ketone instead of giving the double addition associated with an ester.
Finally combine the isolated trans diol and ketone with catalytic acid and remove water to form the cyclic ketal. The ketone carbon becomes the acetal carbon bearing Me and cyclohexyl; the two original diol C–O bonds retain their relative configuration. Anti epoxide opening supplies the required trans relationship before ketal formation. ketal formation is an equilibrium and the trans-fused product may be less favourable than an unstrained acetal, so effective water removal and isolation are necessary; substituting a cis diol is not an acceptable shortcut.

The five-membered enone-like carbon framework can be obtained by an intramolecular aldol condensation of
MeCOCH2​CH(CN)CH(CN)CH2​COMe.
(10)
Enolization at an internal methylene next to one ketone followed by attack on the opposite ketone closes a five-membered ring. dehydration leaves one acetyl group attached to one alkene carbon and one methyl group on the other. The two nitrile-bearing carbons remain adjacent and their original cis relationship is retained if epimerization is avoided.
Now reverse a reductive ozonolysis: the diketone arises by opening a six-membered cyclohexene whose alkene carbons each carry Me. That cyclohexene is the Diels-Alder reaction product of 2,3-dimethylbuta-1,3-diene and maleonitrile. The latter is the supplied cis dinitrile. The stereospecificity of the cycloaddition puts the two CN groups on the same face.
Thus the forward plan is Diels–Alder addition, reductive ozonolysis, then five-membered aldol ring closure and dehydration. Use a reductive ozone work-up that stops at the diketone. During the final step, avoid prolonged strongly basic heating because the carbons bearing CN can be deprotonated and epimerized. Achiral reagents give the racemic pair with the required cis relationship; resolution would be necessary if only the depicted enantiomer were required. The diene supplies six carbons and maleonitrile supplies four, accounting for all ten carbons without an unexplained carbon deletion.

For the CO and NCO cases, the added fragment acts as a Lewis base at boron, rather than becoming a new skeletal vertex. Both have a triangular B3​ framework. A useful localized drawing has three BH2​ groups, one B–H–B bridge between two of them, and the donor attached to the third boron. The boron–boron framework includes three-centre two-electron bonds; its edges should not all be read as ordinary independent two-electron single bonds.
For the neutral carbonyl adduct, attach CO through carbon, giving B←C≡O at the donor-bearing BH2​ site. For the anionic isocyanate adduct, attach NCO− through nitrogen, giving a B–N=C=O connectivity and the overall −1 charge. In both drawings the hydrogen count is 3(2)+1=7. Weak or asymmetric additional B–H contacts can distort this idealized drawing; a hydrogen-rich borane is not fully characterized by a polygon alone. The carbonyl triborane structure was established in the original borane study.[ref]
The rhodium fragment is instead a skeletal building block. Its neutral electron count is 9+2(2)+1=14, so it contributes two skeletal electrons, like a BH vertex. Combining it with B3​H7​ gives 3(2)+4+2=12 skeletal electrons, or six pairs, for four vertices. A consistent low-vertex structure is a tetrahedral RhB3​ skeleton, with the RhH(PPh3​)2​ fragment capping the boron triangle. Keep the terminal Rh–H ligand distinct from the seven hydrogens on the borane portion. The latter can again be represented by three terminal BH2​ groups and a B–H–B bridge. The total formula then contains eight hydrogens outside the phenyl groups. Treating Rh as a pendant one-centre Lewis base would miss its skeletal role.

For a neutral borane Bn​Hm​ with one terminal B–H bond assigned to each vertex, the skeletal electron number is 3n+m−2n=n+m; an overall charge q− adds q electrons. Suitable twelve-electron correspondences are
C=B4​H8​,D=[B3​H8​]−.​
(12)
For C, four BH contributions plus four additional hydrogens give 8+4=12 electrons. For D, three BH contributions plus five additional hydrogens and the negative charge give 6+5+1=12. They map to the tetrahedral and triangular low-vertex skeletons respectively. The neutral formal alternative for D is B3​H9​, also with twelve skeletal electrons.
The electron counting correspondence alone must not be read as evidence that the neutral boranes are isolable stable bulk materials. Low-vertex hydrogen-rich boranes can be transient; the triangular octahydrotriborate anion is the established practical three-boron example, whereas neutral B3​H9​ is a formal analogy rather than an equally established preparative product. The requested skeletal correspondence should be distinguished from a claim about comparable stability or isolation conditions.[ref]

For E, each BH supplies two skeletal electrons and each phosphorus vertex supplies three after retaining a lone pair. Hence 10(2)+2(3)=26 electrons, or thirteen pairs, matching a twelve-vertex closo cluster. Place the two adjacent P vertices on a regular icosahedron. The remaining ten boron vertices fall into four symmetry classes: two sites adjacent to both P vertices, four adjacent to exactly one, two adjacent to neither but opposite the common-neighbour pair, and two opposite the P pair. The populations are 2:4:2:2, so four boron resonances are expected. Interchanging the identical phosphorus vertices is allowed when determining chemical equivalence.
For F, a ten-vertex bicapped square antiprism offers a consistent closo model. There are eight BH vertices, one B vertex bearing the NEt3​ donor instead of H, and one P vertex. Their skeletal contributions are respectively 16, 3 and 3, giving 22 electrons, eleven pairs. The donor-bearing B contribution follows from B plus the donated lone pair minus the exoskeletal B–N bond, 3+2−2=3.
One explicit arrangement consistent with six boron signals puts P at one axial cap and the NEt3​ substituent on one B vertex of the nearer square. The remaining mirror plane through that B, the opposite vertex of its square, and the cap axis leaves three B sites individually fixed: the donor-bearing B, its opposite square vertex and the other axial cap. The other six B sites form three exchanged pairs. Thus the nine borons give three singleton environments and three two-site environments, six signals in all. The original compound has a crystallographically characterized positional assignment; the spectrum alone asks for a compatible structure and does not establish a unique positional isomer. A single-crystal X-ray diffraction determination or assigned heteronuclear correlations would distinguish alternatives.[ref]

a OurBigBook.com
Strong ligand-to-metal pi donation is favoured by an occupied ligand p orbital and a suitable empty metal d orbital of matching symmetry, with reasonably similar energies and good spatial overlap. Small donor atoms such as O or N can supply short bonds and efficient overlap; oxo, imido and nitrido ligands are familiar examples. Early transition metals in high oxidation states, often d0, are particularly favourable because the metal acceptor orbitals are vacant and relatively low in energy. More spatially extended 4d and 5d orbitals can strengthen the overlap.
Metal-to-ligand pi backbonding is the complementary case: electron-rich, low-oxidation-state metals donate occupied d orbitals into low-lying ligand antibonding orbitals, as for CO. In both directions, filling a metal–ligand pi-antibonding orbital offsets the multiple-bond stabilization. The relevant prediction is orbital occupancy and overlap, rather than the presence of a formal double-bond line in a drawing.

Use the stated local axes: z is radial along O–Ru, x is tangential within the Ru–O plane, and y is perpendicular to that plane. The oxygen's three sp2 hybrids make the in-plane sigma framework. Its remaining py​ orbital is perpendicular to the plane and can overlap with one dyz​ orbital on each Ru.
Choose the local orbital phases so their O-facing lobes agree. Of the three metal orbitals, only
D=3​dyz(1)​+dyz(2)​+dyz(3)​​
(13)
has the appropriate combination to interact with oxygen py​. The other two independent combinations have coefficients summing to zero and are nonbonding with respect to the central oxygen. Mixing D with py​ gives one lower pi-bonding orbital and one upper pi-antibonding orbital. Each Ru also retains its dxy​ and dxz​ orbitals as nonbonding with respect to this central-oxo pi interaction. Thus the complete low-energy diagram contains one bonding level, eight metal-nonbonding levels, and one antibonding level. The eight consist of two dyz​ combinations, three dxy​ and three dxz​ orbitals. They need not be exactly degenerate once other ligands are considered.
For the neutral formula as printed, oxide contributes −2 and six acetates contribute −6. The Ru oxidation numbers therefore sum to +8, giving sixteen metal d electrons overall, formally two Ru(III) and one Ru(II), possibly delocalized. Including the oxygen pi pair gives eighteen electrons, filling the bonding orbital and all eight nonbonding orbitals, leaving pi* empty. If the often encountered singly charged cation were intended instead, there would be seventeen electrons and one singly occupied nonbonding level. This charge distinction changes occupancy, not the orbital connectivity.

The three dxz​ orbitals are in the Ru–O plane and can overlap directly along the Ru–Ru edges. Their locally rotated lobes have nonzero edge-directed overlap, producing metal–metal bonding and antibonding combinations; a simple triangle model resolves the three orbitals into a singlet and a doublet. The phases and ordering depend on the chosen local basis, so the important feature is the complete set of three combinations.
In the occupancy obtained in (i), this dxz​ set contains six electrons. All three combinations are filled: the stabilization of the bonding combinations is offset by occupation of their antibonding partners. Equivalently, the sum of the three eigenvalues of a pure off-diagonal interaction matrix is zero. Hence there is no net Ru–Ru bond order from this filled set in the stated model. Overlap alone is not evidence of a net metal–metal bond. This conclusion neglects higher-order mixing and the interactions explicitly excluded from the qualitative diagram.

An N-bound thiocyanate need not make an exactly linear Eu–N–C linkage. Eu(III) bonding is predominantly electrostatic, with weak directional covalent contributions from the shielded 4f orbitals. There is consequently less energetic reward for enforcing a precisely collinear metal–donor arrangement than in a strongly directional transition-metal pi bond. In the crowded eight-coordinate sphere, bending the external NCS groups can reduce ligand–ligand repulsion while retaining favourable Eu–N distances. Angles of 150∘–160∘ are compatible with bent N coordination; they do not require changing the linkage to sulfur. This concerns the Eu–N–C angle, not bending the internal N–C–S skeleton.

The bipyrimidine ligand has two distinct N,N-chelating pockets. It can use one pocket as a terminal bidentate ligand, or use both to bridge two Eu centres. Take all three nitrates per Eu to be bidentate O,O donors.
A consistent structure for B is Eu(NO3​)3​(bpm)2​, with two terminal, singly chelating bpm ligands. Six nitrate oxygens plus four bpm nitrogens give coordination number ten. The unused pocket of each terminal bpm points away from that Eu centre.
For C, use a dimer Eu2​(NO3​)6​(bpm)3​. Place one bpm between the metals, chelating each Eu through a different N,N pocket. Add one terminal bidentate bpm to each Eu and three bidentate nitrates to each Eu. Each metal again has 2+2+6=10 donor atoms, while the overall Eu:bpm ratio is 2:3. In this model all four nitrogens of the bridging bpm are used, but only two of each terminal bpm are used.
Both structures have their accessible coordination sites occupied by multidentate ligands, and the chelate effect favours retention of those donors over adding or exchanging a solvent molecule. This accounts for the absence of coordinated solvent while allowing a higher coordination number than the thiocyanate example. Stoichiometry alone does not exclude polymeric alternatives or different nitrate binding modes; the monomer/dimer pair is an explicit internally consistent prediction.

Disconnect the amide into 2-propylhexanoic acid and N-methyl-2-(4-hexylphenyl)ethylamine. This keeps the nitrogen substituents distinct: one is Me and the other is the two-carbon aryl side chain. The acid's alpha carbon must bear both n-butyl and n-propyl groups.
For the acid fragment, esterify the supplied hexanoic acid, form its ester enolate with LDA, and alkylate with 1-iodopropane:
EtO2​CCH2​(nBu)⟶EtO2​CCH(nPr)(nBu).
(15)
hydrolysis gives 2-propylhexanoic acid; conversion with SOCl2​ gives the acyl chloride for the final coupling. Limit the alkylating reagent and isolate the monoalkylated ester to avoid overalkylation.
For the aryl fragment, use another portion of hexanoic acid to make hexanoyl chloride. A Friedel-Crafts acylation of bromobenzene with this reagent and AlCl3​ gives ortho and para products; isolate the para bromoaryl ketone. A Wolff-Kishner reduction converts ArCO(CH2​)4​Me into ArCH2​(CH2​)4​Me without deliberately hydrogenolyzing the aryl bromide. Acylation followed by carbonyl reduction installs an unrearranged n-hexyl group; direct Friedel–Crafts alkylation is a less reliable way to do that.
Convert the retained aryl bromide into its grignard reagent, open ethylene oxide, and work up to obtain p−HexC6​H4​CH2​CH2​OH. Oxidize the primary alcohol to p−HexC6​H4​CH2​CHO under conditions that stop at the aldehyde, then perform reductive amination with methylamine and sodium triacetoxyborohydride. Excess methylamine helps favour the desired secondary amine over further alkylation. Finally acylate that secondary amine with the isolated acyl chloride, using a separate base to capture HCl.
The final amide has an N-methyl group, the para n-hexyl phenethyl group, and the required n-butyl/n-propyl acyl branching. The carbon check is 9 in the acyl fragment plus 1+2+6+6=15 in the amine fragment, giving 24 overall. No absolute stereochemistry is specified at the acid alpha carbon.

Disconnect the internal Z alkene by a non-stabilized Wittig reaction:
MeO2​C(CH2​)5​CH=PPh3​+OHCCH(nPr)CH2​CH=CH2​.
(16)
The aldehyde carbon becomes the right-hand internal alkene carbon; the ylide carbon becomes the left-hand one. Both the methyl ester and the remote terminal alkene survive the olefination.
Make the aldehyde from pentanal by Stork enamine reaction: form its pyrrolidine enamine, react at carbon with allyl bromide, then hydrolyse to OHCCH(nPr)CH2​CH=CH2​. The original alpha carbon of pentanal now bears allyl and n-propyl substituents. Control the alkylation to avoid multiple substitutions.
The other branch needs a three-carbon extension of the supplied four-carbon bromo alcohol. Protect the OH of HO(CH2​)4​Br as a tetrahydropyranyl ether. Convert the bromide into an organocuprate and add it conjugatively to methyl acrylate:
THPO(CH2​)4​−Cu+CH2​=CHCO2​Me⟶THPO(CH2​)6​CO2​Me.
(17)
Here the organocopper formula is shorthand for a suitable cuprate preparation, not a claim that an isolated monomer is necessary. The acrylate contributes two methylenes and the ester carbonyl. Remove the THP group, replace the resulting terminal OH by Br, and react with PPh3​ to form [Ph3​PCH2​(CH2​)5​CO2​Me]+Br−. Deprotonate adjacent to phosphorus to generate the non-stabilized ylide. This proton is much more acidic than the ester alpha protons, but controlled base stoichiometry and low temperature remain important.
Add the allylated aldehyde under conditions favouring the usual kinetic Z-selective Wittig olefination, and separate any E isomer. The result is
(Z)-MeO2​C(CH2​)5​CH=CHCH(nPr)CH2​CH=CH2​.​
(18)
The four-carbon starting alcohol plus the three-carbon acrylate backbone supplies the seven-carbon acid-derived chain; the ester Me gives an eighth carbon. Pentanal plus allyl gives the other eight. The total is sixteen carbons, including the terminal alkene. A two-carbon extension would produce the wrong chain length.

Disconnect the N–cyclohexyl bond as a late reductive amination of a primary amine with cyclohexanone. Disconnect the exocyclic methylene as a Wittig methylenation of a substituted cyclohexanone. The useful ring precursor is 3-methyl-5-substituted cyclohex-2-en-1-one, accessible by a Robinson annulation. The pendant substituent throughout ring construction should be (CH2​)4​NHBoc.
Start with nucleophilic substitution of the supplied bromo ester by cyanide:
Br(CH2​)3​CO2​Me⟶NC(CH2​)3​CO2​Me.
(19)
Reduce the nitrile selectively to the primary amine while retaining the ester, for example by controlled catalytic hydrogenation with Raney nickel in the presence of ammonia, and trap the amine promptly as its Boc carbamate. Avoid heating or concentrating the free amino ester, which can cyclize to a lactam. lithium aluminium hydride would be unsuitable at this stage because it also reduces the ester.
Partial reduction of the protected ester with DIBAL at low temperature gives BocNH(CH2​)4​CHO. The carbon of the original nitrile is now the methylene nearest nitrogen; the original ester carbonyl is the aldehyde carbon. Condense this aldehyde with acetone and dehydrate to BocNH(CH2​)4​CH=CHCOMe.
Now add the enolate of a second acetone molecule in a Michael addition. With R=(CH2​)4​NHBoc, the resulting chain is
MeCOCH2​CH(R)CH2​COMe.
(20)
Intramolecular aldol condensation gives 3-Me-5-R-cyclohex-2-en-1-one. The beta methyl derives from the carbonyl end attacked in the ring closure; the pendant aminoalkyl chain remains at C5. Hydrogenate the ring C=C selectively, retaining the ketone, then methylenate the ketone with preformed Ph3​P=CH2​. Do the hydrogenation before installing the exocyclic methylene so that it is not hydrogenated away.
Remove Boc under brief mild conditions, checking for acid-induced exocyclic alkene isomerization. reductive amination of the resulting primary amine with cyclohexanone, followed by selective imine reduction, installs the secondary N-cyclohexyl group. Use controlled stoichiometry to suppress tertiary amine formation. The methyl and four-carbon aminoalkyl substituents occupy the required 1,3-related ring positions, with the exocyclic methylene at the former carbonyl. No ring stereochemistry is specified. The target contains eighteen carbons: six ring carbons, one ring methyl, one methylene, four in the linker, and six in the N-cyclohexyl group.

The key stereochemical disconnection is the cis-1,3-disubstituted cyclopentane, not a direct functionalization of two arbitrary cyclopentane positions. A Diels-Alder reaction of cyclopentadiene with nitroethylene fixes both ends of the new two-carbon bridge on the same face of the original five-membered ring.
Isolate the useful nitronorbornene adduct, convert its secondary nitro group to a ketone by a Nef reaction, and then hydrogenate the remaining ring alkene to norcamphor. Performing the nitro-to-ketone conversion before catalytic hydrogenation avoids simply reducing the nitro group to an amine.
Use a Baeyer-Villiger oxidation to insert oxygen between the carbonyl carbon and its adjacent bridgehead carbon. Bridgehead migration is favoured over migration of the competing methylene; isolate the required lactone regioisomer if necessary. This opens the way to a hydroxy cyclopentyl side chain without breaking the original five-carbon ring. In the resulting bridged lactone, one original cyclopentadiene terminus is bonded to O and the other bears the CH2​CO segment.
Reduce the lactone selectively with DIBAL to its lactol. Ring opening of that lactol gives the aldehyde equivalent cis-3-hydroxycyclopentyl-CH2​CHO. The ring OH and the two-carbon aldehyde side chain are cis because their original attachments were the two ends of the Diels–Alder bridge; neither the migration nor lactol opening inverts those cyclopentane carbons.
Perform a Horner-Wadsworth-Emmons reaction on the lactol/aldehyde equilibrium using (EtO)2​P(O)CH2​CO2​R, where R=CH(Me)CH2​CH(Me)2​. Preform the phosphonate anion and allow for consumption of base by the lactol OH. Standard stabilized-phosphonate conditions favour the required E-CH=CHCO2​R unit. No further hydrogenation should follow it.
The required ester substituent can be prepared by reducing 4-methylpentan-2-one to 4-methylpentan-2-ol, esterifying with bromoacetic acid, and performing an Arbuzov reaction with triethyl phosphite to make the phosphonoacetate. No absolute configuration is indicated at that alcohol-derived centre.
Bridge-derived cis ring stereochemistry plus E-selective phosphonate olefination gives both specified relationships. Achiral cycloaddition and subsequent reagents give a racemic pair with this relative configuration; isolation of one absolute enantiomer would require resolution or an asymmetric variant. The acid fragment contains nine carbons: five from cyclopentadiene, two from nitroethylene and two from the phosphonoacetate. The ester alkyl group adds six, giving fifteen overall. The atom tracing in the original schematic in question 8 shows why oxygen insertion retains the five-membered ring rather than producing a six-membered carbocycle.
