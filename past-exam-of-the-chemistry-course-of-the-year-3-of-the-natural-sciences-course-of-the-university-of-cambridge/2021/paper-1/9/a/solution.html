For the CO and NCO cases, the added fragment acts as a Lewis base at boron, rather than becoming a new skeletal vertex. Both have a triangular B3​ framework. A useful localized drawing has three BH2​ groups, one B–H–B bridge between two of them, and the donor attached to the third boron. The boron–boron framework includes three-centre two-electron bonds; its edges should not all be read as ordinary independent two-electron single bonds.
For the neutral carbonyl adduct, attach CO through carbon, giving B←C≡O at the donor-bearing BH2​ site. For the anionic isocyanate adduct, attach NCO− through nitrogen, giving a B–N=C=O connectivity and the overall −1 charge. In both drawings the hydrogen count is 3(2)+1=7. Weak or asymmetric additional B–H contacts can distort this idealized drawing; a hydrogen-rich borane is not fully characterized by a polygon alone. The carbonyl triborane structure was established in the original borane study.[ref]
The rhodium fragment is instead a skeletal building block. Its neutral electron count is 9+2(2)+1=14, so it contributes two skeletal electrons, like a BH vertex. Combining it with B3​H7​ gives 3(2)+4+2=12 skeletal electrons, or six pairs, for four vertices. A consistent low-vertex structure is a tetrahedral RhB3​ skeleton, with the RhH(PPh3​)2​ fragment capping the boron triangle. Keep the terminal Rh–H ligand distinct from the seven hydrogens on the borane portion. The latter can again be represented by three terminal BH2​ groups and a B–H–B bridge. The total formula then contains eight hydrogens outside the phenyl groups. Treating Rh as a pendant one-centre Lewis base would miss its skeletal role.
