The terms are 3Σg−​, 1Δg​ and 1Σg+​. Closed molecular orbital shells contribute no net spin angular momentum or axial orbital projection, so only the two equivalent πg​ electrons need be coupled. Their individual projections are λ=±1.
Putting both electrons in the same projection gives ∣Λ∣=2 and forces opposite spins by the Pauli exclusion principle, yielding 1Δg​. With one electron in each projection, the spin-adapted symmetric and antisymmetric spatial combinations give a singlet Σ and a triplet Σ. Their reflection parities are + and −, respectively. By Hund's rules, the triplet is the lowest term, and 1Δg​ is the lower singlet.

The ionic and covalent diabatic curves cross; coupling replaces the crossing by an avoided crossing of the adiabatic curves. Take the separated neutral atoms as the zero of valence energy. A covalent diabatic state has one electron in a lithium-centred 2s orbital and one in a hydrogen-centred 1s orbital, coupled to an overall singlet. An ionic diabatic state has an empty lithium valence orbital and a doubly occupied hydrogen 1s orbital.
At large internuclear separation R, the ionic limit lies above the neutral one by I(Li)−A(H). At smaller R, its attractive Coulomb term −e2/(4πϵ0​R) lowers it; short-range repulsion eventually raises both curves. A two-state Hamiltonian gives
E±​(R)=2Ecov​+Eion​​±(2Ecov​−Eion​​)2+∣W(R)∣2​.
(13)
Where the diabatic energies would coincide, nonzero coupling W opens a gap 2∣W∣. The lower adiabatic state changes continuously from predominantly ionic character near the bond minimum to predominantly neutral covalent character at dissociation. The upper state exchanges character in the opposite direction. Near the crossing both are mixtures, so a sketch assigning a fixed charge pattern to an entire adiabatic curve would miss the important physics.

Vibrational and solvent relaxation remove energy before emission, producing a Stokes shift. Absorption initially excites a vertical transition from the populated ground-state vibrational levels to excited-state vibrational levels. Vibrational relaxation and solvent rearrangement then bring the molecule near the relaxed lowest excited singlet level before fluorescence. Restricted Hartree–Fock incorrectly retains ionic configurations when a homonuclear bond dissociates. As overlap vanishes, its symmetry-preserving occupied orbital approaches (ϕA​+ϕB​)/2​. The doubly occupied spatial product contains
ψ(1)ψ(2)=21​{ϕA​(1)ϕA​(2)+ϕA​(1)ϕB​(2)+ϕB​(1)ϕA​(2)+ϕB​(1)ϕB​(2)}.
(63)
The first and last terms place both electrons on one separated atom. Their combined probability remains one half, while the correct neutral singlet has one electron on each atom. The resulting spurious Coulomb repulsion raises the restricted dissociation limit. This is missing static electron correlation, not a failure of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation.
A two-configuration expansion using both σg2​ and σu2​ can cancel the ionic terms and recover the Heitler–London separated-atom singlet. Configuration interaction in the appropriate active space or a multiconfigurational self-consistent treatment supplies this remedy. The Unrestricted Hartree-Fock method can instead localize opposite spins on different atoms and give the correct separated-atom energy, but its single determinant breaks spin symmetry and is not the pure singlet at intermediate separation. The distinction between restricted and symmetry-broken descriptions of stretched hydrogen is treated in the original analysis of Coulson and Fischer.[ref]
