Wade's rules assign n+1, n+2 and n+3 pairs to closo, nido and arachno frameworks respectively. C, the neutral arachno B6​H12​, is the most plausible proton donor among A, B and C. Loss of an open-framework bridging proton produces the experimentally accessible, charge-delocalized conjugate base [B6​H11​]−. S42+​ is approximately square planar; Sn52−​ is a closo trigonal bipyramid. Both have 22 valence electrons, 4(6)−2=22 and 5(4)+2=22, but their different local orbital requirements prevent inferring identical shapes from this total alone.
For the sulfur ring, four S–S sigma bonds use eight electrons and four in-plane lone pairs use another eight, leaving six electrons in the perpendicular cyclic pi system. The resulting filled bonding and degenerate nonbonding pi levels give a six-pi-electron aromatic description of a four-membered ring, consistent with equivalent S–S links in the ideal square. The out-of-plane p orbitals are retained; this is not a tetrahedral four-vertex closo cage.
For tin, allocate one mainly s-rich lone pair to each atom. The remaining 22−5(2)=12 electrons form six skeletal pairs, exactly n+1 for n=5. Wade's rules therefore predict a trigonal bipyramid, with three equatorial vertices and two axial vertices. The axial pair is not joined by a polyhedral edge; its bonding is part of the delocalized three-dimensional framework. The contrast is a planar aromatic sigma/pi ring versus a three-dimensional multicentre-bonded Zintl cluster, rather than cationic charge alone imposing planarity or anionic charge alone imposing a polyhedron.

Solid MoCl2​ contains octahedral Mo6​ clusters, while α-MoCl3​ has paired molybdenum centres in a layered chloride lattice. The first material is better represented by Mo6​Cl12​ than by isolated bent or linear MoCl2​ molecules. Eight inner chlorides cap the triangular faces of an octahedral metal core, giving [Mo6​(μ3​−Cl)8​]4+. Outer chloride coordination completes the solid: one useful connectivity notation is Mo6​Cl8​Cl2​Cl4/2​, with two terminal outer chlorides and four intercluster-shared outer chlorides per octahedron. The empirical total is 8+2+4/2=12 chlorides for six metals.
Each Mo(II) is formally d4, giving 24 metal-based electrons. These support extensive Mo–Mo bonding along the 12 octahedral edges; they are not localized as four free spins on each molybdenum. In α-MoCl3​, Mo(III) is d3 in edge-sharing MoCl6​ octahedral layers. The metal honeycomb distorts into short Mo–Mo pairs. A localized dimer picture uses the six d electrons to fill one sigma and two pi bonding combinations, producing a formal triple bond and pairing the spins. The bulk crystal remains an extended chloride lattice, rather than a collection of unconnected Mo2​Cl6​ molecules.
By comparison, CrCl2​ has predominantly localized high-spin Cr(II), d4, in Jahn–Teller-distorted chloride octahedra; CrCl3​ has layered Cr(III), d3, with three locally unpaired electrons. Their magnetic exchange is not evidence for the same strong short multiple bonds as in the molybdenum solids. The greater radial extent of Mo 4d orbitals enables larger metal–metal overlap than the more contracted Cr 3d orbitals. Real structure and magnetism still depend on ligand geometry and phase, so the orbital comparison is a trend rather than a prohibition of chromium–chromium bonds in other compounds. Diffraction and magnetic/spectroscopic studies directly distinguish the dimerized α-MoCl3​ lattice from CrCl3​.[ref]

Radiation changes the solution's chemistry by radiolysis; it does not turn Am(VI) nuclei into Am(V) nuclei. An emitted alpha particle deposits energy into many water and solute molecules, generating ionized/excited species, hydrated electrons, hydrogen atoms and molecular products including hydrogen peroxide. Reduce the saturated ketone with sodium borohydride after installing SPh, then benzoylate the alcohol with benzoyl chloride and pyridine or a tertiary amine.
Disconnect the enone to RCOCH3​ and formaldehyde. Kinetic deprotonation on the methyl side of the hindered unsymmetrical ketone, trapping with formaldehyde, and dehydration gives RCOCH=CH2​. Finally, RCOCH3​=CH3​COCH(Me)CH2​CH2​CH3​ disconnects by alpha alkylation to butan-2-one and 1-iodopropane. Choosing ethyl iodide here would leave the target short by one carbon.
This is a constitutional synthesis: the target drawing specifies no configuration at either newly formed stereogenic site, so no unproved stereoselective reduction or alkylation is needed. Regioisomeric enolate alkylation and competing overalkylation are the practical selectivity issues to control, rather than something that follows from writing “base”.

The target is 5-Aminohexanoic acid, HO2​C(CH2​)3​CH(NH2​)Me. Disconnect its terminal amine/carboxylic-acid relationship through hydrolysis of 6-methylpiperidin-2-one. This six-membered lactam follows from a regioselected Beckmann ring expansion of 2-methylcyclopentanone oxime.
Start with cyclopentanone. Form its enolate with lithium diisopropylamide in dry THF at low temperature, add methyl iodide, then quench to obtain 2-methylcyclopentanone. Control the methyl iodide/base stoichiometry to suppress a second alkylation. React the ketone with hydroxylamine to form its oxime and select the geometrical oxime isomer in which the bond to the methyl-substituted adjacent ring carbon is anti to the departing oxime oxygen group.
Activate the oxime, for example under appropriate acid-promoted Beckmann conditions. The anti methyl-bearing C–C bond migrates onto nitrogen as the N–O-derived leaving group departs, inserting nitrogen between the original carbonyl carbon and the methyl-substituted ring carbon. Hydration/tautomerization gives 6-methylpiperidin-2-one. The opposite oxime geometry would insert nitrogen on the other side and ultimately place methyl at a different position in the amino acid; oxime geometry is therefore an essential part of this proposal.
Hydrolyse the lactam with aqueous acid and heat, then adjust the work-up to the desired acid/ammonium form. Cleavage of its acyl–nitrogen bond opens the ring to HO2​C(CH2​)3​CH(NH2​)Me after suitable neutralization. All five original cyclopentanone carbons are retained, and the one methyl carbon comes from methyl iodide. A racemic mixture product is appropriate to the stereochemically unspecified target. The reaction sequence is an organic ring-expansion synthesis and requires no biological transformation.

HO2​C−CH(nPr)−CH2​−CH(iPr)−CH(OH)Ph.
(6)

PhCOCH2​iPr+CH2​=C(nPr)CO2​Et⟶PhCOCH(iPr)CH2​CH(nPr)CO2​Et.
(7)
The nucleophile is the enolate or enamine of the phenyl ketone. The electrophile's propyl substituent must be alpha to its ester carbonyl; using a beta-propyl acrylate would move the substituent to the wrong ring position.
Disconnect PhCOCH2​iPr further to the indicated acetophenone. Crossed aldol addition to acetone followed by dehydration gives PhCOCH=CMe2​; selective conjugate reduction or controlled alkene hydrogenation then gives PhCOCH2​CHMe2​. This avoids requiring an efficient SN2 alkylation with a secondary isopropyl halide. The alpha-propyl acrylate can be obtained by alpha-hydroxymethylation/dehydration of ethyl pentanoate, EtO2​CCH2​nPr, using formaldehyde.
In the forward direction, perform the Michael coupling, reduce the phenyl ketone selectively with sodium borohydride while retaining the ordinary ester, hydrolyse the ester, and cyclize the delta-hydroxy acid by controlled acid-catalysed lactonization or an activated-acid coupling. The six-membered ring then has the order carbonyl–CH(nPr)–CH2​–CH(iPr)–CH(Ph)–O. This atom order checks all three substituent positions. No relative configuration is specified, so the scheme does not imply a single diastereomer from the Michael addition or reduction.

An isolobal analogy compares fragment frontier orbitals, not atomic sizes or total electron counts. The neutral BH fragment has four valence electrons; two make the external B–H bond, leaving two skeletal electrons in three frontier orbitals directed towards possible cluster neighbours. For the neutral CpCo fragment, Cp=C5​H5​ contributes five electrons and cobalt nine, giving 14. Removing the 12 electrons assigned to filled, predominantly non-skeletal fragment orbitals again leaves two electrons in three cluster-directed frontier orbitals.
The corresponding frontier sets have comparable numbers, spatial symmetries and occupations, allowing replacement of a BH vertex by CpCo in a suitable cluster-electron count. This is why the metal fragment can cap an open carborane face without adding an arbitrary new set of skeletal electrons. Exact orbital energies, ligand preferences and bond lengths need not be identical.[ref]

React pentaborane with an alkyne under relatively mild conditions to obtain a nido C2​B4​H8​ framework, conventionally nido-2,3-dicarbahexaborane(8) for acetylene. The two entering carbon atoms initially retain their adjacency. A specific mild example uses 2-butyne with triethylamine-assisted B5​H9​ at or below room temperature to give nido-Me2​C2​B4​H6​. This is a practical route to an explicitly nido carborane, rather than an assumption that an alkyne supplies a closo cage under every condition.[ref]
More energetic gas-phase reaction or pyrolysis promotes hydrogen loss, boron-fragment loss and cage rearrangement, giving closo carboranes, for example 1,5-C2​B3​H5​ and 1,6-C2​B4​H6​, with other cage sizes possible in the mixture. Thermolysis of a previously formed nido carborane is an alternative route to this stage. The point is not a clean same-composition nido-to-closo isomerization: the change of hydrogen content and sometimes cage size must be retained in the formulas.
Milder conditions favour kinetically trapped adjacent-carbon products; higher temperatures permit migration to carbon-separated arrangements, often increasing favourable B–C contacts relative to adjacent C–C/B–B contacts. Conditions and Lewis bases also influence which separated-carbon cage is isolated; temperature alone does not prescribe a single quantitative isomer distribution. Primary thermolysis studies and the small-carborane literature document both cage rearrangement and reagent-dependent product mixtures.[ref]

Write L=[C2​B9​H11​]2− for the open-face dicarbollide ligand. The structure consistent with the stated attachment is [LMo(CO)3​→Mo(CO)5​]2−, with a direct Mo–Mo connection, three carbonyls on the cage-capping Mo and five on the outside Mo. Ultrashort metal–metal contacts are favoured when the valence configuration fills strongly metal–metal-bonding orbitals while leaving their antibonding partners empty. Early or middle transition metals in low oxidation states can retain enough d electrons for multiple bonding without the unavoidable antibonding occupation of a more filled d shell. Good radial and angular overlap, a compatible metal–metal axis, and low competing ligand-field stabilization all matter; a short contact should be interpreted together with an orbital/electron-counting model, not automatically called a high-order bond.
Ligands should support a geometry in which the relevant d orbitals can overlap and protect the low-valent pair from oligomerization or oxidative attack. Bulky aryl-substituted bridging amidinates can provide this protection while maintaining an appropriate small coordination number. Strongly coordinating axial ligands or inappropriate pi interactions can compete with metal–metal bonding, especially its weaker delta components. For quadruple or quintuple bonds, retaining two appropriately oriented delta-capable orbitals is particularly important; a geometry optimized only for metal–ligand bonding can remove that advantage.
Ligand bridges need not literally squeeze two otherwise unbound metals together. Comparing bond lengths across oxidation states and across related ligands helps distinguish electronic multiple bonding from enforced steric compression. The lantern-type dichromium experiment in (ii) is informative because reduction changes the electron occupation within essentially the same three-bridge supporting framework.[ref]

ii OurBigBook.com
The large positive sum of ionization energies and large negative hydration enthalpy dominate, with a smaller but significant positive atomization term. Ionization depends on effective nuclear attraction, orbital energies and the stability of successive gas-ion electron configurations; removing the third electron may require disruption of a particularly stable divalent f configuration. Atomization depends on the metal's cohesive bonding and valence participation, and need not vary perfectly smoothly.
Hydration becomes more favourable for higher charge density. All the compared ions have charge +3, so smaller effective ionic radius generally gives more negative hydration enthalpy. Incomplete shielding by the 4f shell produces the lanthanide contraction and strengthens ion–water attraction along the series. A Born-type electrostatic estimate has a stabilization scale proportional to −z2/r, but hydration-shell structure, water polarization and temperature dependence prevent treating that crude scale as an exact enthalpy formula. The hydration trend is smoother than the sharp configuration-dependent ionization anomalies. The measured formation enthalpy reflects cancellation of large terms, so comparison of just one term can give a misleading prediction.

The unusually stable divalent configurations Eu(II) 4f7 and Yb(II) 4f14 make their third ionization particularly costly. Forming Eu(III), 4f6, or Yb(III), 4f13 removes an electron from a half-filled or filled f shell. The positive ionization-energy contribution therefore increases relative to the smoother background across the series.
Their trivalent ionic radii and hydration enthalpies do not supply a matching abrupt extra stabilization. Differences in atomization and the divalent character of elemental Eu and Yb partly compensate the ionization anomaly but do not erase the net less favourable trivalent aqueous formation enthalpy. This explanation concerns formation of the +3 aqueous ions, not a claim that Eu3+ or Yb3+ is intrinsically the most stable isolated f configuration.

There are three largely independent constructions: a substituted Robinson-annulation core, a protected vicinal diol, and the ester acyl fragment. Disconnect the ester first to an allylic alcohol on the bicyclic ring and the acid HO2​CCH(Et)CH(Me)2​. Disconnect the benzylidene acetal to a side chain −CH2​CH(OH)CH2​OH and benzaldehyde. Reversing 1,2-reduction of the allylic alcohol gives the corresponding bicyclic enone; its six-membered enone ring is a Robinson disconnection back to a suitably alpha-substituted cyclohexanone and methyl vinyl ketone.
An efficient way to protect the side chain before annulation is to prepare 4-(bromomethyl)-2-phenyl-1,3-dioxolane from BrCH2​CH(OH)CH2​OH and benzaldehyde with mild acid and water removal. The desired five-membered acetal protects the adjacent hydroxyl groups, while its external primary bromide remains an alkylating site. Alpha alkylation of the indicated cyclohexanone with this bromide gives a ring bearing −CH2​CH(O−)CH2​O− in the benzylidene-protected form. There is no need to expose the eventual ring enone to a nonselective late dihydroxylation.
Generate the enolate at the other, less substituted alpha position of the cyclohexanone and add methyl vinyl ketone. Hydrolysis of an enamine-mediated Michael variant or direct enolate trapping gives the appropriate 1,5-dicarbonyl. Intramolecular aldol condensation closes a six-membered ring onto the original cyclohexanone carbonyl. Dehydration gives the fused bicyclic enone, with the protected three-carbon side chain on the adjacent carbon of the original ring. Regioselecting the initial Michael addition is essential: reaction at the side-chain-bearing alpha site would place that substituent at the wrong fusion position.
Reduce the new enone carbonyl by 1,2-addition, for example sodium borohydride with cerium(III) chloride under Luche-type conditions, to retain the displayed ring C=C bond and produce the allylic alcohol. The benzylidene acetal should be maintained through these neutral/basic operations. The target has no wedge/dash assignments, so a constitutional solution does not require a claim of a unique fused-ring/alcohol diastereomer; isolate the required constitutional annulation product.
For the acyl branch, form the enolate of the indicated methyl butanoate, add acetone, then dehydrate the beta-hydroxy ester:
MeO2​CCH2​Et⟶MeO2​CCH(Et)C(OH)Me2​⟶MeO2​CC(Et)=CMe2​.
(14)
Hydrogenate this isolated alkene before coupling it to the bicyclic substrate, giving MeO2​CCH(Et)CHMe2​. saponification gives HO2​CCH(Et)CHMe2​; activate it as its acid chloride or use a mild ester-coupling reagent, then acylate the bicyclic allylic alcohol. Performing the hydrogenation on the separate acyl branch avoids reducing the target ring alkene. Strong acid should be avoided during the final esterification because the benzylidene group is acid-labile.
The carbon audit is particularly useful: the fused rings contain six carbons from cyclohexanone plus four from methyl vinyl ketone; the side chain has three carbons from the protected bromodiol; the benzylidene unit supplies its acetal carbon and phenyl group. In the acyl fragment, the four butanoate acyl carbons plus three acetone carbons give seven; the starting ester methoxy carbon is lost during hydrolysis.

Both side chains have the order ring–CH2​CH=CHMe, and both printed double bonds have the Z geometry. The two carbon side chains are cis to one another; equivalently the labelled ring H and ring Me are cis on the opposite face. A useful common late precursor therefore has two cis ring–CH2​CHO substituents, which can undergo two Z-selective ethylidene Wittig reactions.
Use the starting 2-methylcyclohex-2-en-1-one in a reductive alpha-alkylation. Conjugate reduction under dissolving-metal/enolate-trapping conditions, followed by a primary electrophile BrCH2​CH2​OTBS, installs a protected two-carbon alcohol side chain at the original methyl-bearing alpha carbon. This gives 2-methyl-2-(2-siloxyethyl)cyclohexanone. The carbon at that position is now quaternary, with its original methyl retained. Alternatively, reduce the enone to 2-methylcyclohexanone and deliberately form/select its more substituted enolate before alkylation; its opposite kinetic enolate would give the wrong regioisomer.
Convert the remaining ring ketone to an exocyclic enoate by Horner–Wadsworth–Emmons olefination with a phosphonoacetate:
ring C=O⟶ring C=CHCO2​Et.
(15)
Hydrogenate this sole alkene to give ring–CH2​CO2​Et at that carbon. Select the diastereomer with this substituent cis to the pre-existing protected hydroxyethyl side chain, so the newly installed ring H is cis to ring Me. Less hindered-face hydrogenation can assist this relationship, but the route should include separation/verification of the desired cis product rather than assert complete stereoselectivity without data.
Reduce the ester with lithium aluminium hydride to ring–CH2​CH2​OH and remove the TBS group from the other side chain. Both appendages are now two-carbon primary alcohols. Oxidize them to aldehydes under non-overoxidizing conditions, for example Dess–Martin oxidation, obtaining the cis dialdehyde with two ring–CH2​CHO groups. Treat with excess salt-controlled, nonstabilized ethylidenetriphenylphosphorane under conditions favouring kinetic Z olefination; isolate the Z,Z product. Each aldehyde carbon becomes the inner vinylic carbon of its crotyl group, while the ylide supplies CHMe.
This order postpones both stereodefined alkenes until after hydrogenation, so they are not accidentally saturated. It also avoids assuming that a crotyl grignard reagent adds without allylic transposition or that direct alcohol deoxygenation fixes the required ring stereochemistry. An achiral synthesis gives a racemic mixture pair with the specified relative configuration.

Use the nitro group as a masked carbonyl and a carbon nucleophile. The most informative disconnection reverses the six-membered aldol annulation. Before that closure, the cyclopentanone beta carbon bears
−CH(NO2​)CH2​CH2​COCH3​.
(16)
The carbon carrying NO2​ will become the lower carbonyl of the final six-membered ring by a Nef reaction. It comes from the specified nitromethane. The remote methyl ketone supplies the upper methyl-substituted alkene carbon after aldol condensation.
First, use a mild base to generate a nitromethane-derived nitronate and add it conjugatively to the indicated cyclopent-2-en-1-one. Protonation gives 3-(nitromethyl)cyclopentanone. Deprotonate its nitromethyl group and perform a second Michael addition to methyl vinyl ketone, giving cyclopentanone−!C3−CH(NO2​)CH2​CH2​COCH3​. The nitro-stabilized carbon is appreciably more acidic than a simple ketone alpha carbon, permitting selective nitronate formation; control the acceptor stoichiometry to avoid a further Michael alkylation of the remaining nitro-bearing H.
Form the enolate/enamine at the cyclopentanone carbon between its carbonyl and the side-chain-bearing carbon. Intramolecular attack on the remote methyl ketone closes a six-membered ring; the two shared atoms are the original enone alpha and beta carbons. Hydrolysis if required and dehydration create the alkene between the original alpha carbon and the newly formed tertiary carbinol carbon. That carbinol carbon retains its methyl substituent, placing the methyl exactly as required. The original cyclopentanone carbonyl remains the carbonyl of the five-membered ring.
Finally, form the secondary nitronate and carry out controlled acidic Nef hydrolysis to replace its CH(NO2​) unit by C=O. The resulting fused product contains the two required ketones. Carrying out the annulation before exposing this second ketone reduces competing enolate/aldol pathways that would be available in the corresponding triketone. The nitro intermediate's competing deprotonation means that the aldol step needs conditions designed to access the ring carbonyl enolate/enamine; simply treating every carbonyl position as equally reactive would not establish the route.
The carbon ledger is 5 from cyclopentenone, 1 from nitromethane and 4 from methyl vinyl ketone, giving the nine-carbon fused skeleton plus its methyl substituent. A primary-nitro Nef reaction performed before the second carbon alkylation would instead give an aldehyde and would lose this useful protected-carbonyl construction.

The left ring system is spiro, and the right-hand substituent is CH(OMe)Me, not OMe directly attached to the cyclohexene ring. Let Q denote the right cyclohexenyl group bearing that methoxyethyl substituent cis to its linker. The connection to the left alkene carbon is −(CH2​)3​−Q. These observations suggest two Diels–Alder constructions. A left-hand diene
(cyclopentane)C=C[(CH2​)3​Q]−CH=CH2​
(17)
has the original cyclopentane carbon as one terminal diene atom; cycloaddition with an ethylene equivalent gives the spiro six-membered ring. Its residual double bond is between the two internal diene atoms, putting the linker on the required alkene carbon.
Build Q and its four-carbon alcohol appendage first. Protect the OH group of the indicated 4-Bromobutan-1-ol, for example as a THP ether. Convert the primary bromide to its grignard reagent and formylate with DMF, giving THPOCH2​(CH2​)3​CHO. olefination with an allylidenetriphenylphosphorane, Ph3​P=CHCH=CH2​, supplies a conjugated diene THPOCH2​(CH2​)3​CH=CHCH=CH2​; select the required diene geometry rather than assume a single E/Z outcome.
Cycloaddition with methyl vinyl ketone produces a cyclohexene with a protected four-carbon alcohol chain at one saturated ring carbon and an acetyl group at its adjacent ring carbon. Choose and isolate the adjacent-substituent cis regioisomer, matching the two dashed substituent bonds in the target. Endo preference and diene geometry can aid the selection, but an unsymmetrical diene is not guaranteed to give a unique regioisomer. Reduce the acetyl ketone to CH(OH)Me, then methylate that alcohol with sodium hydride/methyl iodide while the chain-end OH is protected. Remove the THP group under mild acid conditions to reveal Q(CH2​)4​OH, preserving the secondary methyl ether and the ring alkene.
Oxidize this primary alcohol to Q(CH2​)3​CHO. Add vinylmagnesium bromide to give Q(CH2​)3​CH(OH)CH=CH2​, then oxidize the allylic alcohol to the enone Q(CH2​)3​C(=O)CH=CH2​. This carbonyl carbon is the original alcohol-end carbon of 4-bromobutanol; after olefination it will be the linker-bearing carbon of the left ring alkene.
In the other branch, reduce the specified cyclopentanone to cyclopentanol, convert to bromocyclopentane, and react with triphenylphosphine to obtain the cyclopentylphosphonium salt. Deprotonation gives the cyclopentylidene phosphorane, whose ylidic carbon is part of the five-membered ring. Wittig olefination of the just-prepared enone gives the substituted diene displayed above. This carbonyl–ylide pairing keeps the ring carbon terminal in the diene and avoids accidentally constructing a fused ring instead of a spiro ring.
Finally, cycloadd an ethylene equivalent. Ethylene itself requires suitably forcing/high-pressure conditions; a more reactive alternative is phenyl vinyl sulfone, followed by reductive removal of the sulfone substituent using a method compatible with the methyl ether and retained C=C bonds. The latter installs the same two otherwise unsubstituted saturated carbons. Control or separate any additional cycloaddition isomers, and retain the previously selected cis configuration on Q.
The carbon count provides a strong check: cyclopentanone supplies five, bromobutanol four, DMF formylation one, the first allylidene ylide three, methyl vinyl ketone four, O-methylation one, vinylmagnesium two, and the final ethylene equivalent two, totalling 22 target carbons. Three original bromobutanol carbons form the linker; its fourth becomes the substituted carbon of the spiro ring's alkene. The proposal is a multistep constitutional/relative-stereochemical route with explicit regioisomer and diastereomer selection, not an unsupported claim of a single quantitative cycloaddition product.
