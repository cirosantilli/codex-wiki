Tetrahedral molecular geometry

 Home Chemistry Molecule Molecular geometry
OurBigBook.com Words: 186
In tetrahedral molecular geometry, four neighbouring chemical atoms surround a central chemical atom in the directions of the vertices of a tetrahedron. In the regular idealization, the central atomic nucleus lies at the centre of a regular tetrahedron, and every pair of chemical bonds makes the same bond angle. Methane, CH4​, provides the standard example: its carbon has four bonding electron domains and no lone pair. See Chemistry 2e, §7.6: tetrahedral methane.
The exact ideal bond angle follows from four directions proportional to (1,1,1), (1,−1,−1), (−1,1,−1), and (−1,−1,1). Each has squared Euclidean norm 3, and each distinct pair has dot product −1. Hence
θ=arccos(−1/3)≈109.4712∘.
(38)
This is a statement about a regular tetrahedron, rather than a universal exact value for every molecule labelled tetrahedral. It also explains why a cross-shaped flat drawing with 90∘ gaps is misleading: four equal directions can spread out more evenly in three dimensions.
The same vectors sum to zero. Consequently, four equal bond-dipole contributions along those directions cancel. The symmetric methane molecule is nonpolar, but a tetrahedral shape alone does not imply a nonpolar molecule: replacing one neighbour can make the electronic distribution and bond contributions unequal. Chemical polarity depends on both molecular geometry and electron density. See McMurry, Organic Chemistry, §2.2: molecular dipoles and methane.
