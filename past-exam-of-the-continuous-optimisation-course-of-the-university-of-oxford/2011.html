i OurBigBook.com
Use F for the maximized objective function, and adopt optimization constraints gi​≥0 with
g1​=2−x1​,g2​=20−x12​−x22​,g3​=x2​−x12​.
(1)
The feasible set is nonempty, since it contains (0,0). It is closed as an intersection of inverse images of closed sets under continuous functions. It lies in the disk of radius 20​, so is bounded and therefore compact by the Heine-Borel theorem. The objective function is a polynomial, hence continuous. The extreme value theorem now guarantees a global maximizer exists.

All three active constraints force x1​=2 and x2​=4, which also satisfy x12​+x22​=20. For maximization use L=F+λTg, λ≥0. At x∗=(2,4),
∇F=(16,10),∇g1​=(−1,0),∇g2​=(−4,−8),∇g3​=(−4,1).
(2)
Stationarity gives the complete family
λ2​=t,λ3​=8t−10,λ1​=56−36t,45​≤t≤914​.​
(3)
In particular (λ1​,λ2​,λ3​)=(2,3/2,2) is strictly positive. Three gradients in two dimensions cannot be linearly independent, so the linear independence constraint qualification fails. But d=(−1,−1) gives ∇gi​⋅d=(1,12,3)>0, so the Mangasarian–Fromovitz constraint qualification holds.
For a nonzero linearized feasible direction d, each ∇gi​⋅d≥0, and
∇F⋅d=−2∇g1​⋅d−23​∇g2​⋅d−2∇g3​⋅d<0.
(4)
The inequality is strict because equality would require all three inner products to vanish, and the first and third gradients already span R2. This proves a strict local maximum: otherwise a sequence of nearby feasible points with no smaller objective would, after normalizing their differences from x∗ and taking a convergent subsequence, give a unit linearized feasible direction with nonnegative objective derivative, contradicting the displayed inequality. Thus
x∗=(2,4) is a strict local maximizer,F(x∗)=32.​
(5)

Write x1​=t, x2​=t2. The two inactive constraints are strict exactly for −2<t<2. Complementary slackness gives λ1​=λ2​=0. The KKT conditions reduce to
4t2−2tλ3​=0,t+2t2+λ3​=0,λ3​≥0.
(6)
If t=0 these give λ3​=0. Otherwise λ3​=2t and t=−3/2, which gives a negative multiplier and is inadmissible. Therefore
x=(0,0),λ=(0,0,0)​
(7)
is the only KKT point with this active set. Its active gradient (0,1) is nonzero, so the linear independence constraint qualification, and hence the Mangasarian–Fromovitz constraint qualification, holds.
It is not a local maximizer: (0,ε) is feasible for small ε>0 and has objective ε2>0=F(0,0). The feasible boundary path (t,t2) for small negative t has objective 2t3+t4<0, so it is not a local minimizer either.

Convert to minimization with f=−F and retain g≥0. A continuously differentiable inequality augmented Lagrangian method uses the merit function
Φ(x,λ,μ)=f(x)+2μ1​i=1∑3​(min{gi​(x)−μλi​,0}2−μ2λi2​),μ>0,λ≥0.​
(8)
For gi​≤μλi​ its contribution is −λi​gi​+gi2​/(2μ); otherwise it is the constant −μλi2​/2. This shifted truncation permits multipliers to decrease as well as increase while remaining nonnegative.
A useful initial estimate comes from the preceding pure quadratic penalty method at μ−1​:
λi[0]​=max{0,−gi​(x[0])/μ−1​}.
(9)
Here g(−1,−1)=(3,18,−2), so with μ−1​=2 choose
λ[0]=(0,0,1).​
(10)
Then use this estimate and μ0​=1 in the first augmented subproblem. Initial estimates are a choice, rather than uniquely specified data; zero multipliers would also define an initialization.

For the convention above, after approximately minimizing Φ(⋅,λ[k],μk​), update
λi[k+1]​=max{0,λi[k]​−μk​gi​(x[k+1])​}.​
(11)
The exact shifted-residual identity is
min{gi​(x[k+1]),μk​λi[k]​}=μk​(λi[k]​−λi[k+1]​).
(12)
If the constraint is violated, gi​(x[k+1])≤0, its left side is min{gi​,0}, and replacing the new multiplier by its limiting value gives the printed local estimate
min{gi​(x[k+1]),0}≈μk​(λi[k]​−λi∗​).​
(13)
For arbitrary iterates, the precise unshifted statement includes the negative part:
min{gi​(x[k+1]),0}=μk​min{λi[k]​−λi[k+1]​,0}.​
(14)
Thus the printed estimate describes approach from the violated side; it is not a universal signed equality on the feasible side. This qualification follows directly from the projection in the update.
Its numerical significance is that the constraint violation is controlled by μk​ times the multiplier error, rather than by μk​ alone. Once multipliers are accurate, high feasibility accuracy can be obtained with a moderate positive penalty parameter. This avoids forcing 1/μk​ to become enormous and reduces the ill-conditioning of the inner minimization. Inactive constraints eventually have zero multiplier and zero violation.

i OurBigBook.com
Assume the denominators are defined, so c,d=0. For a descent direction p, set φ(α)=f(x+αp). The Wolfe conditions are
φ(α)≤φ(0)+σαφ′(0),φ′(α)≥ηφ′(0),0<σ<η<1.
(15)
The first is sufficient decrease and the second is the curvature condition. Here
H=∇2f=(c−2+d−2c−2−d−2​c−2−d−2c−2+d−2​)
(16)
is positive definite, with eigenvalues 2/c2,2/d2. The exact step is
α∗​=−pTHp∇f(x)Tp​>0.
(17)
It gives φ′(α∗​)=0, so the curvature condition holds. Its decrease is φ(α∗​)−φ(0)=α∗​φ′(0)/2, so sufficient decrease holds for the usual choice 0<σ≤1/2, σ<η<1. Hence exact searches satisfy Wolfe with an Armijo constant at most one half. If one allowed σ>1/2, even an exact quadratic minimizer would fail that condition; the parameter range matters.

A standard form of the Zoutendijk theorem assumes that f is continuously differentiable and bounded below on the starting level set, that its gradient is Lipschitz on a neighborhood of that set, and that the steps satisfy fixed Wolfe conditions. For descent directions pk​, define
cosθk​=−∥∇f(xk​)∥∥pk​∥∇f(xk​)Tpk​​.
(18)
Then
k∑​cos2θk​∥∇f(xk​)∥2<∞.​
(19)
In particular, if cosθk​≥δ>0, then ∇f(xk​)→0. This last angle hypothesis is needed to turn the general descent theorem into gradient convergence.
For this quadratic, f≥0, the Hessian matrix is the constant positive-definite matrix H, and the gradient is globally Lipschitz with constant L=2max(c−2,d−2). Its level sets are compact. Thus all objective-function hypotheses hold; Wolfe holds for the exact steps with the parameters above. The direction hypothesis will be verified for alternating exact coordinate minimization in the next part.

Solution

iii OurBigBook.com
Use the exact line searches from part (i). After minimizing along one coordinate, the corresponding gradient component is zero. Unless the optimum has been reached, the whole nonzero gradient therefore lies along the other coordinate, which is selected next. The chosen signed coordinate direction then has cosθk​=1. A selected zero gradient component can be skipped; it already minimizes that coordinate.
The Zoutendijk theorem therefore gives ∇f(xk​)→0. The unique point with zero gradient is
x∗=(2a+b​,2a−b​),f(x∗)=0.​
(20)
Since ∇f(x)=H(x−x∗) and H is invertible, gradient convergence implies xk​→x∗. Positive definiteness makes this the unique global minimum.
Convergence also holds if “line search” is interpreted as any fixed-parameter Wolfe search. If coordinate i is selected, its signed step s satisfies s=−ωgi​/h, where h=c−2+d−2 and 1−η≤ω≤2(1−σ). Sufficient decrease gives f(xk​)−f(xk+1​)≥σ(1−η)gi​(xk​)2/h. Summation shows that selected gradient components tend to zero and step lengths tend to zero. The other component was selected in the preceding step; Lipschitz continuity then makes it tend to zero as well. Thus the full gradient converges to zero. The simple one-step angle argument and sharp Q-linear factor below use exact coordinate minimization; arbitrary Wolfe choices need not have that same Euclidean one-step factor.

A sequence is Q-linearly convergent to x∗ in a specified norm if ∥xk+1​−x∗∥≤q∥xk​−x∗∥ for all sufficiently large k, with some q<1. Its asymptotic factor is the limsup of consecutive error ratios.
Put h=c−2+d−2, b0​=c−2−d−2 and t=b0​/h. For an error e=x−x∗, an exact first-coordinate step sets e1​=−te2​, and an exact second-coordinate step sets e2​=−te1​. Once the first coordinate step has occurred, errors consequently alternate between the two lines
e=(−tz,z),e=(z,−tz).
(21)
The next error has its free coordinate multiplied in magnitude by ∣t∣, and both lines have the same Euclidean norm factor 1+t2​. Therefore, until finite termination,
∥ek​∥2​∥ek+1​∥2​​=∣t∣=c2+d2∣c2−d2∣​=κ2​(H)+1κ2​(H)−1​<1.​
(22)
This is a sharp bound, attained after the first step; the objective errors have factor t2 per step. If c2=d2, the problem separates and at most two exact steps suffice. When the condition number is large, the factor approaches one: the iterates zigzag slowly across a narrow tilted valley.

i OurBigBook.com
Take the usual symmetric meaning of positive definite Q. To include all data allowed by the source, first check feasibility. Put
B=AQ−1AT,x0​=Q−1ATB−1b,ρ2=bTB−1b.
(23)
Full row rank makes B positive definite. Every x satisfying Ax=b is uniquely x0​+z with Az=0, and zTQx0​=0. Thus
xTQx=ρ2+zTQz.
(24)
If ρ2>1, there is no feasible point. If ρ2=1, the only feasible point is x0​, and x0​ is optimal for every c. If ρ2<1, the Slater condition holds, and the following KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient:
Ax=b,xTQx≤1,γ≥0,c−ATy+2γQx=0,γ(xTQx−1)=0.​
(25)
Necessity follows from the constraint qualification proved in (ii). Sufficiency follows from convexity: the stationary optimization Lagrangian cTx+yT(b−Ax)+γ(xTQx−1) is minimized at x, while its feasible values never exceed the objective, and complementary slackness makes its value at x equal the objective.
The singleton case cannot be silently covered by ordinary KKT necessity. For example, let Q=I, A=(1 0), b=1, c=(0,1)T. The sole feasible point (1,0) is optimal, but stationarity would require (0,1) to lie in the span of (1,0), which is impossible. The feasibility split above supplies genuinely necessary and sufficient conditions without assuming an unprinted regularity hypothesis.

The equality gradients are independent everywhere. At an interior point of the ellipsoid, there is no active inequality, so the Mangasarian–Fromovitz constraint qualification holds. At a boundary point x=x0​+z, a qualifying direction must satisfy Av=0 and 2xTQv<0.
If ρ2<1, choose v=x0​−x=−z. At a boundary point zTQz=1−ρ2>0, and
2xTQv=−2zTQz<0.
(26)
Thus the qualification holds at every feasible point. If ρ2=1, the only feasible point is x0​; for every v∈kerA, x0T​Qv=0, so no strict direction exists. This proves
MFCQ at one feasible point⟺ρ2<1⟺MFCQ at every feasible point.​
(27)
The equivalence also identifies this case with strict feasibility of the inequality on the affine equality space.

For y∈Rm and γ≥0, define the optimization Lagrangian
L(x,y,γ)=cTx+yT(b−Ax)+γ(xTQx−1).
(28)
The Lagrangian primal is
x∈Rninf​ y∈Rm,γ≥0sup​L(x,y,γ).​
(29)
For feasible x, the supremum is cTx: equality terms vanish and the nonpositive inequality term has maximum zero at γ=0. If Ax=b, scale y in the direction b−Ax to make the supremum infinite. If the inequality fails, send γ→∞. Thus the inner supremum is exactly the objective extended to +∞ off the feasible set. This proves equivalence with the original problem, including its infeasible case.

Reverse the order of the two optimizations to obtain the Lagrangian dual
supy∈Rm,γ≥0​ infx​L(x,y,γ).
(30)
Put s=c−ATy and r=sTQ−1s​. For γ>0, completing the square gives
xmin​=−2γ1​Q−1s,q(y,γ)=bTy−γ−4γr2​.
(31)
At γ=0 the infimum is finite precisely if s=0, when it equals bTy. For r>0, the expression γ+r2/(4γ) is minimized at γ=r/2, with value r. The same optimized value holds when r=0, choosing γ=0. Eliminating γ therefore gives supy​{bTy−sTQ−1s​}. Introducing θ=r yields
max{−θ+bTy:ATy+s=c, sTQ−1s=θ2, θ≥0}.​
(32)
The nonnegative square root is enforced by θ≥0. Optimizers correspond by γ=θ/2, including the zero case.

v OurBigBook.com
For fixed y, s=c−ATy is fixed. The relaxed inequality and θ≥0 say precisely
θ≥sTQ−1s​.
(33)
The objective bTy−θ strictly decreases with θ, so its best choice is always equality. Replacing any feasible relaxed point by that choice preserves feasibility and improves or leaves unchanged the objective. Conversely every point satisfying equality is feasible for the relaxed problem. Thus the two dual formulations have the same optimal value and the same optimal y,s, with equality at any optimizer.
This algebraic equivalence in fact does not require Slater's condition. Under the stipulated condition, strong duality additionally identifies their attained optimum with the primal optimum. With Q−1/2s as a linear coordinate, the relaxed constraint is ∥Q−1/2s∥2​≤θ, so the relaxed formulation is a second-order cone program.
