Fix an iteration and abbreviate x=xk, s=sk, d=∇f(x)Ts. Since s is a descent direction, d<0. The directional derivative gives
f(x+αs)−f(x)−β1​αd=(1−β1​)αd+o(α).
(1)
Choose αˉ>0 so that ∣o(α)∣≤21​(1−β1​)(−d)α for 0<α≤αˉ. Then
f(x+αs)−f(x)−β1​αd≤21​(1−β1​)αd<0.
(2)
This is the Armijo condition for every such positive step size, and α=0 gives equality. In particular, a backtracking line search terminates after finitely many reductions.

Interpret boundedness above uniformly: there is an M<∞ such that Bk⪯MI for every k. Since Bk is a positive-definite symmetric matrix, the spectral theorem implies (Bk)−1⪰M−1I. Therefore
−∇f(xk)Tsk=∇f(xk)T(Bk)−1∇f(xk)≥M1​∥∇f(xk)∥2.
(8)
Combining the Armijo condition with αk≥C>0 yields
f(xk)−f(xk+1)≥Mβ1​C​∥∇f(xk)∥2.
(9)
If f≥L, a telescoping sum gives
Mβ1​C​∑k=0N​∥∇f(xk)∥2≤f(x0)−f(xN+1)≤f(x0)−L.
(10)
The nonnegative series converges, so its terms tend to zero:
k→∞lim​∥∇f(xk)∥=0​.
(11)
This proves first-order global convergence in the sense of stationarity from uniformly bounded quasi-Newton matrices. It does not imply that every nonconvex objective function reaches a global minimum, or that the iterates themselves converge. Every finite limit point is a stationary point, by continuity of the gradient.

At xk, choose a=f(xk), g=∇f(xk), H=∇2f(xk), and the current radius Δ=Δk​>0. A bounded symmetric matrix approximation to the Hessian matrix can also be used. Stop if the gradient is sufficiently small; otherwise approximately solve the trust-region subproblem to obtain sk​ with ∥sk​∥≤Δk​ and at least a fixed fraction of Cauchy decrease. Its predicted decrease is then positive, so calculate the trust-region acceptance ratio
ρk​=mk​(0)−mk​(sk​)f(xk)−f(xk+sk​)​.
(16)
Fix 0<η1​<η2​<1, 0<γd​<1<γu​, and a maximum radius Δmax​. One valid update is:
  • If ρk​<η1​, reject the trial: xk+1=xk and Δk+1​=γd​Δk​.
  • Otherwise accept xk+1=xk+sk​. If additionally ρk​>η2​ and the step reaches the boundary, enlarge the radius to min{γu​Δk​,Δmax​}; otherwise keep it unchanged.
The trust region controls the reliability of the model; the ratio decides whether that reliability was borne out by the actual objective function.

The Cauchy point provides both an inexpensive fallback step and a standard against which a better step can be judged. Requiring a fixed fraction of Cauchy decrease gives
mk​(0)−mk​(sk​)≥2κ​∥∇f(xk)∥min{Δk​,∥∇f(xk)∥/∥Hk​∥},κ>0.
(17)
Consequently an exact global minimum of each trust-region subproblem is unnecessary. A step from any inner solver is adequate if it meets this bound.
The convergence argument combines this decrease with model accuracy. For example, suppose the gradient is uniformly Lipschitz continuous on a neighborhood of the visited level set, the model Hessian matrices are uniformly bounded, and the usual acceptance and radius safeguards are used. If ∥∇f(xk)∥≥ϵ>0, the Taylor expansion gives a model error bounded by K∥sk​∥2. For sufficiently small Δk​, division by the Cauchy decrease bound makes ∣1−ρk​∣ small. Such trials are successful and cannot keep shrinking the radius. With the gradient bounded away from zero, the radius therefore has a positive lower bound, and each accepted step decreases f by a fixed positive amount. There must be infinitely many accepted steps, since repeated rejection would force the radius below the small-radius acceptance threshold. This contradicts boundedness below of f and proves liminfk​∥∇f(xk)∥=0.
Stronger standard conclusions can be obtained with further regularity and safeguards. Merely being twice continuously differentiable and bounded below does not supply uniform Hessian bounds on an unbounded level set; those uniform assumptions must be stated when using this argument.

Taking the natural logarithm of the positive product constraint gives h(x)=∑i​ai​logxi​=0. Its gradient has entries ai​/xi​>0, so the equality constraint qualification holds everywhere on the feasible set. Use the optimization Lagrangian
L(x,λ)=∑i​ai​xi​−λ∑i​ai​logxi​.
(18)
Stationarity requires ai​−λai​/xi​=0, so every xi​=λ. Substitution into the constraint, using ∑i​ai​=1, gives λ=1. Thus the only candidate for a local minimum is x=(1,…,1).
To prove global minimality without assuming the inequality to be established, set ui​=logxi​. The constraint becomes the affine hyperplane ∑i​ai​ui​=0, and the objective function becomes F(u)=∑i​ai​eui​. Its Hessian matrix diag(ai​eui​) is positive-definite, so F is strictly convex. The elementary tangent inequality et≥1+t, with equality only at zero, gives
F(u)≥∑i​ai​(1+ui​)=1.
(19)
Equality forces every ui​=0. Therefore
x∗​=(1,…,1),mini∑​ai​xi​=1​.
(20)
This point is the unique global minimizer and the only local minimizer. The logarithm change of variables also explains why a multiplicative constraint leads to a convex optimization argument.

For μ>0, the logarithmic barrier objective is
fμ​(x)=f(x)−μi=1∑p​logci​(x)​,D={x:ci​(x)>0 for all i}.
(26)
The domain of a function D is a nonempty convex set by the same concavity argument as in part (a), with strict inequalities. Differentiating twice on D gives
∇2fμ​(x)=∇2f(x)−μ∑i​ci​(x)∇2ci​(x)​+μ∑i​ci​(x)2∇ci​(x)∇ci​(x)T​.

Here ∇2f⪰0 by convexity, −∇2ci​⪰0 by concavity, and each outer product is a positive semidefinite matrix. Hence ∇2fμ​⪰0, proving convexity on D.
The domain qualification matters: the displayed real-valued logarithm expression is not defined on all of Rn. The precise whole-space version is the extended-real convex function equal to fμ​ on D and +∞ outside D.

Choose a strictly feasible point, an initial barrier parameter μ0​>0, and 0<θ<1. At outer iteration k, approximately minimize the logarithmic barrier objective fμk​​ over its strictly feasible domain of a function. For example, use damped Newton methods, choosing an inner step size by backtracking line search so that every ci​ stays positive and the barrier objective function decreases. Solve to a tolerance ∥∇fμk​​(xk)∥≤εk​, where εk​→0. Set μk+1​=θμk​ and use xk as the next starting point.
The associated Lagrange multiplier estimates are
λik​=ci​(xk)μk​​>0,λik​ci​(xk)=μk​.
(28)
Reducing μk​ makes these perturbed complementary slackness equations approach the KKT conditions. This describes the generic interior-point method; existence of a solution to every inner minimization needs appropriate additional attainment assumptions and does not follow from strict feasibility alone.

A precise conditional convergence statement is: with the smoothness and convexity assumptions of the question, a strict feasible point, μk​↓0, and barrier stationarity residuals
rk​=∇f(xk)−∑i​λik​∇ci​(xk)⟶0,
(29)
every convergent sequence of strictly feasible points xk→x∗​ has a global minimizer as its limit. The Lagrange multiplier estimates are bounded and have a subsequence converging to multipliers satisfying the KKT conditions. The theorem does not assert convergence of the primal sequence without further assumptions.
First, continuity gives ci​(x∗​)≥0. For an inactive optimization constraint, ci​(x∗​)>0, so
λik​=μk​/ci​(xk)⟶0​.
(30)
For the active constraints, the hint permits assuming λik​→λi∗​. Combining this with the inactive limits gives λ∗​≥0, and passage to the limit in the residual equation proves stationarity. Since λik​ci​(xk)=μk​→0, complementary slackness also holds. All KKT conditions follow, and part (b) proves global optimality.
The hint's multiplier assumption can in fact be weakened to selecting a subsequence. To see why the estimates are bounded, choose a strict feasible point z and put δ=mini​ci​(z)>0. Concavity gives
δ∥λk∥1​≤∑i​λik​ci​(z)≤pμk​+(∇f(xk)−rk​)T(z−xk).
(31)
The right side is bounded because xk→x∗​ and rk​→0. This is the bounded barrier multipliers under Slater's condition argument. A convergent multiplier subsequence then gives the same KKT point, which suffices to prove that the assumed primal limit is globally optimal.

iii OurBigBook.com
For an active constraint with λi∗​>0, the perturbed complementary slackness equation gives ci​(xk)∼μk​/λi∗​. The Hessian matrix from part (c) therefore includes
ci​(xk)2μk​​∇ci​(xk)∇ci​(xk)T∼μk​(λi∗​)2​∇ci​(x∗​)∇ci​(x∗​)T.
(32)
If ∇ci​(x∗​)=0, curvature normal to that boundary grows like 1/μk​. By contrast, a direction tangent to every active constraint annihilates these large normal outer products at the limiting point, and its curvature may remain of order one. A large spread of eigenvalues then makes the Newton linear system poorly conditioned. Under bounded remaining curvature and a positive bounded tangential curvature, the condition number typically grows like 1/μk​; this is the usual ill-conditioning of a logarithmic barrier.
It is not a universal rate: there may be no nonzero common tangent direction, some limiting Lagrange multipliers may vanish, or all curvatures may grow together. The Hessian formula identifies the source of the difficulty without assuming these extra nondegeneracy conditions.
