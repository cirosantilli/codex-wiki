Cayley's theorem

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Group theory Group embedding
OurBigBook.com Words: 947 Articles: 1
Every group G is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group on its underlying set. The construction uses the left regular action: each group element g becomes a permutation of all the group elements, given by
Lg​:G⟶G,Lg​(x)=gx,λ:G↪Sym(G),g⟼Lg​.
(37)
For a finite group of group order n, labeling these elements by 1,…,n gives a group embedding into Sn​. The same construction works for an infinite group, with the symmetric group of all bijections of its underlying set.[ref]
The beauty is that the multiplication rule itself supplies a concrete model. Whatever the group elements originally represent, each one rearranges the entire group by multiplication. A product becomes function composition, and no distinctions between group elements are lost. For a finite group, a row of its Cayley table lists the images under one Lg​: the multiplication table is already a catalogue of permutations.
Throughout this construction, products of permutations act rightmost first:
(στ)(x)=(σ∘τ)(x)=σ(τ(x)),Lg​Lh​=Lgh​.
(38)
Thus Lg​Lh​ first multiplies by h, then by g. This is a left group action, not a license to reverse the multiplication order in a nonabelian group. Ordinary right multiplication Rg​(x)=xg instead satisfies Rg​Rh​=Rhg​ with this convention; replacing it by x↦xg−1 restores the group homomorphism law.[ref]
For an explicit cyclic group example, take C4​=Z/4Z under addition, using modular arithmetic. Its left regular action consists of Lk​(x)=k+x(mod4). In permutation cycles on the labels 0,1,2,3,
L0​=id,L1​=(0 1 2 3),L2​=(0 2)(1 3),L3​=(0 3 2 1).
(39)
The function composition L1​L1​=L2​ sends 0 to 2, 1 to 3, 2 to 0, and 3 to 1: squaring the four-point permutation cycle produces two disjoint permutation cycles. Also L1​L3​=L0​. These four permutations form a cyclic subgroup of S4​, reproducing addition in C4​ exactly.
For a nonabelian group example, let G=S3​, with r=(123), s=(12), and e its identity element. The six group elements are e,r,r2,s,rs,r2s, where r3=s2=e and sr=r2s. Now the labels being permuted are themselves permutations of three points. Computing the left regular action gives permutations of these six labels:
Lr​=(e r r2)(s rs r2s),Ls​=(e s)(r r2s)(r2 rs).
(40)
For example, Ls​(r)=sr=r2s; putting rs here would accidentally switch to right multiplication. Noncommutativity survives in the model:
(Lr​Ls​)(e)=rs=(13),(Ls​Lr​)(e)=sr=(23).
(41)
These different outputs prove that Lr​Ls​=Ls​Lr​. The group generators r,s become group generators Lr​,Ls​ of a six-element subgroup of S6​.
Why does a group action here produce a group embedding? Any group action on a set X gives a group homomorphism ρ:G→Sym(X); it is a group embedding precisely when it is a faithful group action. The group kernel consists of the group elements fixing every point of X. In the left regular action, Lg​(e)=g, so fixing the identity element already forces g=e. In fact every stabilizer subgroup is trivial, since gx=x forces g=e; this stronger property makes the left regular action a regular group action.[ref]
Compare two group actions of the same cyclic group C4​. Its left regular action on four labels distinguishes all four group elements. On X={0,1}, the rule k⋅x=k+x(mod2) defines a transitive group action, but 0 and 2 both fix both points, while 1 and 3 both exchange them. Its group kernel is {0,2}, so it is not a faithful group action. Its image is a two-element subgroup of S2​, isomorphic to the quotient group C4​/{0,2} rather than to C4​.
The guaranteed number ∣G∣ of labels need not be the smallest possible for a faithful group action. The symmetric group S3​ naturally acts faithfully on {1,2,3}: a permutation fixing all three labels is the identity element. Its left regular action uses six labels instead. Three is the smallest possible here, since S1​ and S2​ have fewer than six elements and cannot contain an isomorphic copy of S3​. Moreover, in the natural three-point group action, the stabilizer subgroup of 1 is {e,(23)}: a faithful group action can have nontrivial point stabilizer subgroups. Cayley's construction supplies a universal model, while a smaller model can reveal particular symmetries.
The proof is short. Each Lg​ is a bijection with inverse Lg−1​. Associativity gives
Lg​(Lh​(x))=g(hx)=(gh)x=Lgh​(x),
(42)
so λ is a group homomorphism. If Lg​=Lh​, evaluating both at the identity element gives g=Lg​(e)=Lh​(e)=h. Hence λ is injective, and its image is the required isomorphic subgroup. No finiteness assumption entered the argument.
The historical connection is unusually close to the notation. The opening paragraph of Arthur Cayley's 1854 paper already treats permutations as one example of operations on a collection of quantities and explicitly performs the rightmost operation first in a product. It also allows noncommutativity while insisting on associativity. The modern left regular action shows how that viewpoint reaches every abstract group, by choosing the group itself as the set to be acted on.[ref]
