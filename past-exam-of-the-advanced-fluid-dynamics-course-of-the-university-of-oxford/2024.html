a OurBigBook.com
Start in an inertial frame with the inviscid magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation,
ρDt​v=−∇P+J×B−ρ∇Φ.
(1)
Here ρ is mass density, P is the isotropic fluid pressure, v is inertial-frame velocity, J is electric current density, B is the magnetic field, and Φ is gravitational potential per unit mass. In the low-frequency approximation to Ampère's law, J=∇×B/μ0​. The vector calculus identity
(∇×B)×B=(B⋅∇)B−∇(B2/2)
(2)
separates the Lorentz force density into magnetic tension and a magnetic pressure gradient. Transform the material acceleration to a rigid frame rotating with constant angular velocity Ω0​, using the stated frame transformation. The rotating-frame magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation is
ρDt​u=−∇(P+2μ0​B2​)+μ0​(B⋅∇)B​−ρ∇Φ−2ρΩ0​×u−ρΩ0​×(Ω0​×r).​
(3)
The final terms are the Coriolis force and centrifugal force densities; u is the velocity relative to the rotating frame, and Dt​=∂t​+u⋅∇ uses that frame.
This requires a smooth conducting-fluid continuum with nonrelativistic motion, a constant magnetic permeability μ0​, the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint, a negligible displacement current and negligible net electric-charge force. Viscous forces and anisotropic fluid stresses are omitted. A time-dependent frame would add an Euler force. Incompressible flow is not needed for this force equation. Finite electrical resistivity does not change its displayed instantaneous magnetic force; the later frozen-field calculation additionally uses ideal magnetohydrodynamics. A differentially rotating fluid does not define a single rigid frame with spatially varying Ω0​: choose a fixed local reference rate and retain the fluid's shear in u.

In an inertial frame, a circular orbit at radius R has radial acceleration −RΩ(R)2eR​. Neglecting fluid and magnetic forces gives
−ρRΩ(R)2=−ρdRdΦ​,dRdΦ​=RΩ(R)2.​
(4)
In a local rotating frame with Ω0​=Ω(R0​), the fluid is instantaneously at rest at the reference radius R0​. The radial centrifugal force +ρR0​Ω02​ then balances the inward gravitational force. Away from R0​, the fluid's nonzero rotating-frame velocity must also be retained; the entire differentially rotating disk is not at rest in that one frame.

Take outward radial force as positive and keep the displaced element's angular rate fixed at Ω0​=Ω(R0​). Its imbalance is
ΔfR​=ρ[(R0​+ξR​)Ω02​−Φ′(R0​+ξR​)].
(5)
Use the equilibrium relation from part b, Φ′(R)=RΩ(R)2, and a first-order Taylor expansion:
ΔfR​=−ρR0​dRdΩ2​​R0​​ξR​+O(ξR2​),∣ΔfR​∣≃ρ​R0​dRdΩ2​ξR​​.​
(6)
The absolute values are needed for a magnitude. As printed, the proposed magnitude has a signed right side; it would be negative for outward displacement in a Keplerian disk. For decreasing Ω2, the signed imbalance above points outward and therefore amplifies that fixed-angular-rate displacement. This calculation fixes the angular rate, rather than the specific angular momentum; the latter is the conserved quantity for an uncoupled inviscid radial displacement and leads to the Rayleigh centrifugal stability criterion.

d OurBigBook.com
Linearized magnetic flux freezing gives the Eulerian magnetic field perturbation
δB=∇×(ξ×B0​)=(B0​⋅∇)ξ−B0​∇⋅ξ.
(7)
The background is uniform, and incompressibility gives ∇⋅ξ=0. For a horizontal displacement proportional to eikz, δB=ikB0​ξ and δBz​=0. Thus the first-order magnetic pressure perturbation vanishes, and the magnetic-tension response to an incompressible displacement is
δf=μ0​B0​​∂z​δB=−μ0​k2B02​​ξ.​
(8)
Bending a field line produces a restoring force proportional to displacement, like a spring obeying Hooke's law. Per unit mass, its squared spring frequency is k2vA2​, where vA​=B0​/μ0​ρ​ is the Alfvén speed. The equilibrium can be independent of z while the perturbation varies with z; neglect of background vertical structure does not set this perturbation's wavenumber to zero.

Work locally at R0​ with Ω=Ω(R0​), and define
D=dlnRdΩ2​​R0​​,a=k2vA2​,vA​=μ0​ρ​B0​​,κ2=4Ω2+D.
(9)
Here κ is the radial epicyclic frequency. Combining the radial imbalance, the Coriolis force and the magnetic spring gives the spring model of magnetorotational instability:
ξ¨​R​−2Ωξ˙​ϕ​=−(D+a)ξR​,ξ¨​ϕ​+2Ωξ˙​R​=−aξϕ​.
(10)
For a normal mode proportional to e−iωt, the coefficient matrix is
(a+D−ω2−2iΩω​2iΩωa−ω2​).
(11)
Setting its determinant to zero yields the ideal magnetorotational dispersion relation,
ω4−ω2(2a+4Ω2+D)+a(a+D)=0.​
(12)
Its two values of ω2 are
ω±2​=a+2κ2​±21​(κ2)2+16aΩ2​,
(13)
so they are real. For a>0, their product is negative exactly when a+D<0, giving an exponentially growing normal mode. Conversely, a+D>0 also makes their sum positive and therefore gives two positive squared frequencies. A fixed nonzero magnetic mode is exponentially unstable for k2vA2​+D<0, oscillatory for k2vA2​+D>0, and marginal at equality. Stability against every allowed nonzero wavenumber in the unbounded ideal model requires D≥0; finite-domain restrictions can remove the unstable band.
For Φ=−GM/R, the equilibrium relation gives Ω2=GM/R3, so D=−3Ω2 and κ2=Ω2. Hence the Keplerian disk has the magnetorotational instability band
0<k2vA2​<3Ω2.​
(14)
Without the magnetic field, the polynomial becomes ω2(ω2−κ2)=0. The Rayleigh centrifugal stability criterion instead requires κ2>0, equivalently d(R4Ω2)/dR>0. A Keplerian disk satisfies that hydrodynamic criterion. Magnetic coupling therefore destabilizes a disk that has a restoring hydrodynamic epicyclic oscillation; the field couples angular-momentum exchange between elements, rather than acting only as an isolated restoring spring.
The plotted maximum is the maximum growth rate of ideal magnetorotational instability, 3Ω/4 at k2vA2​=15Ω2/16.

Using the given linear momentum balance, multiply by the velocity and integrate over the fixed volume:
dtd​∫V​21​ρui​ui​dV=∫V​ui​∂j​σij​dV=∫∂V​ui​σij​nj​dS−∫V​σij​∂j​ui​dV.
(15)
This is integration by parts component by component, or the divergence theorem. The first term is boundary stress power. With component-first velocity gradient Kij​=∂j​ui​, the second is the double contraction Φ=σ:K.
For a Newtonian fluid, σ=−pI+2μe, where e=(K+KT)/2 is the rate-of-strain tensor and μ≥0 is dynamic viscosity. Incompressibility removes the pressure contribution, and the symmetric tensor e is orthogonal to the antisymmetric part of K. Therefore
Φ=2μe:e=2μij∑​eij2​≥0.​
(16)
The displayed energy identity follows for the stated linear balance. A full advective momentum equation in a fixed control volume would also contribute an advective kinetic energy flux. The nonnegativity statement here is for a purely Newtonian viscous stress; a viscoelastic stress can exchange stored free energy with the flow.

Let Kij​=∂j​ui​, Cij​=⟨Ri​Rj​⟩, α=2H/ζ and DR​=2kB​T/ζ. The configuration equation of the Hookean dumbbell model becomes
∂t​ψ=−∇R​⋅[(KR−αR)ψ]+DR​∇R2​ψ.
(17)
Here the paper's k denotes the Boltzmann constant kB​. Integrating the second moment equation against Ri​Rj​ and discarding boundary terms at infinite separation gives
C˙ij​=Kiℓ​Cℓj​+Kjℓ​Ciℓ​−2αCij​+2DR​δij​.
(18)
For example the spring drift differentiates each factor Ri​,Rj​, while the Laplacian gives ∇R2​(Ri​Rj​)=2δij​. The Hookean dumbbell conformation equation is
C˙−KC−CKT=ζ4kB​T​I−ζ4H​C.​
(19)
In the row-vector convention of R⋅∇u, the displayed gradient tensor is KT, giving exactly the transposes in the requested equation. The dimensionless conformation tensor A=HC/(kB​T) consequently has upper-convected derivative −(A−I)/λ, with polymer relaxation time λ=ζ/(4H). This normalization matters: the unscaled C has equilibrium value (kB​T/H)I, rather than I.

r˙1,2​−u(r1,2​)=∓21​(R˙−KR).

The claimed equality does not hold for the printed joint distribution. The original PDF specifies a centered Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution of v=R˙ independent of R, with
⟨vi​⟩=0,⟨vi​vj​⟩=m2kB​T​δij​.
(22)
Since the double average includes number density n, evaluating the Gaussian integrals gives
⟨⟨2ζ​∣v−KR∣2⟩⟩=m3nζkB​T​+2nζ​tr(KCKT).​
(23)
The cross term vanishes because the conditional mean of v is zero. In particular, set K=0 and take the equilibrium configuration probability density ψeq​∝e−HR2/(2kB​T). The left side is 3nζkB​T/m>0, while nHC:K=0. This is a counterexample even at thermal equilibrium, so no rearrangement of the earlier conformation tensor equation can prove the printed statement.
The discrepancy is thermal bead drag and heat-bath power: gross viscous loss of microscopic thermal motion is replenished by the thermal bath. It is not identical to mechanical polymer stress power. Moreover, R˙ in the earlier configuration continuity equation is a probability current velocity, including a distribution-dependent osmotic drift; it cannot simultaneously be identified with an independently sampled centered Maxwellian velocity.
A consistent overdamped result is the free-energy balance of a Hookean dumbbell. Define q=HR+kB​T∇R​logψ and
F=nkB​T∫ψlogψeq​ψ​dR,V=KR−ζ2​q.
(24)
Differentiate the relative entropy and use the configuration Fokker-Planck probability current ψV. After integration by parts,
F˙=n∫ψq⋅VdR=nHC:K−ζ2n​∫ψ∣q∣2dR.
(25)
The affine osmotic term is nkB​T∫∇ψ⋅KRdR=−nkB​TtrK=0. Hence
nHC:K=F˙+D,D=ζ2n​∫ψ∣q∣2dR≥0.​
(26)
The polymer stress tensor σp​=n(HC−kB​TI) has σp​:K=nHC:K in an incompressible flow. It stores or releases free energy as well as dissipating it, so the total stress-work term in part a need not itself be a purely dissipative quantity for this viscoelastic material. Stress power equals D only for stationary free energy, and D uses the probability-current slip. Neither condition turns the printed independent-Maxwellian average into that expression.
