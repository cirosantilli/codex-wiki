a OurBigBook.com
Let u and p​ be the complex plane wave amplitudes and set kx​=k⊥​, kz​=k∥​. The linearized Euler equations in the rotating frame are
−iωux​−iωuy​−iωuz​k⊥​ux​+k∥​uz​​=−ik⊥​p​+2Ωuy​,=−2Ωux​,=−ik∥​p​,=0.​
(1)
Here p is pressure per unit mass density, including the centrifugal potential. The last line is incompressibility, not an additional equation of state.
Taking the curl eliminates pressure. For constant Ω and a divergence-free vector field, ∇×(2u×Ω)=2(Ω⋅∇)u. Thus the inertial-wave vorticity equation gives
−iω(ik×u)=2iΩk∥​u,ωk×u=2iΩk∥​u.
(2)
Apply k× once more and use k×(k×u)=−k2u. For a nonzero oscillatory amplitude,
−ωk2u=2iΩk∥​ω2iΩk∥​​u,
(3)
so the inertial-wave dispersion relation is
ω2=4Ω2k2k∥2​​,ωσ​=σ2Ωkk∥​​,σ=±1.​
(4)
The restoring influence is the Coriolis acceleration. In particular ∣ω∣≤2∣Ω∣. For k∥​=0, the projected linear dynamics have zero frequency; these are steady balanced disturbances rather than the nonzero-frequency branch used in the division above. The spatially uniform k=0 mode must be treated separately from this dispersion relation.

The group velocity, which gives wave-packet and energy propagation, is the gradient of the dispersion relation:
vg​=∇k​ωσ​=kσ2Ω​(z−k2k∥​​k).​
(5)
For k=(k⊥​,0,k∥​) this becomes
vg,x​=−σ2Ωk3k⊥​k∥​​,vg,z​=σ2Ωk3k⊥2​​,vg,z​vg,x​​=−k⊥​k∥​​.
(6)
Thus for ϵ=∣k∥​∣/k⊥​≪1, energy travels almost along the rotation axis, with a small horizontal component. By contrast, the normal-to-phase-front phase velocity vph​=ωk/k2 is almost horizontal. Indeed k⋅vg​=0, so these phase and group directions are perpendicular. Specifying which meaning of propagation is intended matters for an inertial wave.
For the wave polarization, incompressibility and the y equation in part a give
ux​=−k⊥​k∥​​uz​,uy​=k⊥​σik​uz​.​
(7)
The horizontal component parallel to k⊥​ is small, but ∣uy​∣≃∣uz​∣: the other horizontal and vertical components are in quadrature. More exactly, introduce the orthonormal transverse basis
e1​=y​,e2​=k×y​=k1​(−k∥​,0,k⊥​).
(8)
Then u=A(e1​−σie2​). The physical velocity at a fixed point traces a circle in the plane perpendicular to k; its handedness reverses between branches. In the anisotropic limit this plane is nearly vertical. The group velocity and polarization of an inertial wave therefore have quite different geometric roles.

Let L⊥​=k⊥−1​ and let U be the common size of horizontal and vertical velocity perturbations, as found in part b. The linear inertial wave time and nonlinear advection time are
τw−1​∼2Ωk⊥​∣k∥​∣​,τnl−1​∼k⊥​U.
(9)
The critical balance in a rapidly rotating fluid is therefore
ϵ=k⊥​∣k∥​∣​∼2Ωk⊥​U​≪1,ω∼k⊥​U∼2Ωϵ.​
(10)
The Rossby number is of order ϵ. Horizontal geostrophic flow balances the leading pressure gradient against Coriolis acceleration, so
u⊥​∼u∥​∼U,δp∼2ΩL⊥​U∼U2/ϵ,k∥​∼ϵk⊥​.​
(11)
These are dimensionally comparable statements, rather than comparisons of a dimensional frequency with a dimensional velocity. Equivalently, use L⊥​ and (2Ω)−1 as units. Then
k⊥​L⊥​=O(1),k∥​L⊥​=O(ϵ),2Ωω​=O(ϵ),2ΩL⊥​u⊥​​,2ΩL⊥​u∥​​=O(ϵ),(2ΩL⊥​)2δp​=O(ϵ).
(12)
Thus rotation stays O(1) in these units, while the motions evolve on the slower O(ϵ−1) time. Vertical advection u∥​∂z​ is smaller than horizontal advection by ϵ.
Write u⊥​=u⊥(0)​+u⊥(1)​+⋯, with u⊥(1)​=O(ϵU), and p=p0​+p1​+⋯. At leading horizontal order,
0=−∇⊥​p0​+2Ωu⊥(0)​×z.
(13)
Define the stream function Φ=p0​/(2Ω), including its horizontally uniform part in this convention. Then
u⊥(0)​=z×∇⊥​Φ=(−∂y​Φ,∂x​Φ),∇⊥​⋅u⊥(0)​=0.​
(14)
The next-order incompressibility condition is not zero horizontal divergence:
∇⊥​⋅u⊥(1)​=−∂z​u∥​.​
(15)
This term has size ϵk⊥​U and is essential: its product with the large rotation rate is a leading contribution to the vertical vorticity equation.
To see that contribution explicitly, write D⊥​=∂t​+u⊥(0)​⋅∇⊥​. At the first horizontal inertial order,
D⊥​u⊥(0)​=−∇⊥​p1​+2Ωu⊥(1)​×z.
(16)
Taking its vertical curl eliminates p1​. The two-dimensional advection identity and the two divergence orders give
D⊥​ζ=−2Ω∇⊥​⋅u⊥(1)​=2Ω∂z​u∥​,ζ=(∇⊥​×u⊥(0)​)z​=∇⊥2​Φ.
(17)
Equivalently the ageostrophic flow satisfies
u⊥(1)​=2Ωz×[D⊥​u⊥(0)​+∇⊥​p1​]​,
(18)
whose divergence is −D⊥​ζ/(2Ω). This explicitly verifies that the next-order divergence has the required sign.
The vertical momentum equation, at the same slow order, is
D⊥​u∥​=−∂z​p0​=−2Ω∂z​Φ.
(19)
Finally u⊥(0)​⋅∇⊥​f={Φ,f}, where the canonical two-dimensional Poisson bracket is {f,g}=fx​gy​−fy​gx​. The reduced equations for an anisotropic rotating fluid are
∂t​∇⊥2​Φ+{Φ,∇⊥2​Φ}∂t​u∥​+{Φ,u∥​}​=2Ω∂z​u∥​,=−2Ω∂z​Φ.​​
(20)
The omitted vertical-advection, ageostrophic-advection and relative-vorticity stretching terms are smaller by O(ϵ) in the equations retained here. Linearizing these equations recovers ω2=4Ω2k∥2​/k⊥2​, the anisotropic limit of part a.

Within the reduced equations for an anisotropic rotating fluid, a flow that remains purely horizontal has u∥​=0 and ∂t​u∥​=0. The second reduced equation therefore requires
0=−2Ω∂z​Φ,∂z​Φ=0,∂z​u⊥(0)​=0.​
(21)
Thus the retained horizontal motion is two-dimensional. This is a Taylor–Proudman theorem consequence in the slow balanced ordering; setting u∥​=0 at only one instant would not suffice, because vertical motion could subsequently develop.
The word “exactly” applies to the reduced model. It is not a theorem for every finite-Rossby number solution satisfying only Ω≫kU. For example, the full rotating Euler equation admits
u=Acos(kz)(cos2Ωt,−sin2Ωt,0),p=constant.
(22)
It is an incompressible flow, has zero nonlinear advection, and obeys ∂t​u=2u×Ω. It is purely horizontal but depends on z, and Ω≫kA can hold. This fast inertial oscillation lies outside the slow anisotropic critical balance used in part c; it shows why that ordering cannot be dropped from the conclusion.

For a uniform axial magnetic field, the anisotropic Shear Alfvén wave is predominantly horizontally polarized, whereas the pseudo-Alfvén wave is predominantly vertically polarized. Without rotation both have characteristic frequency ωA​∼∣k∥​∣vA​. Pressure projection makes the effective Coriolis acceleration couple these two transverse wave polarization states at the inertial-wave rate
ωI​∼2Ωk⊥​∣k∥​∣​.
(23)
Thus the useful comparison is not Ω alone, nor anisotropy alone, but
ωA​ωI​​∼k⊥​vA​2Ω​≪1.​
(24)
In this limit rotational coupling of shear and pseudo-Alfvén waves is negligible on an Alfvén propagation time, so the two familiar linear polarizations are decoupled to leading order. The factor k∥​ cancels: simply making both disturbances more anisotropic does not by itself remove their relative rotational coupling.
For a nonlinear reduction one must also make the rotational mixing slow compared with the nonlinear evolution being retained. Under Alfvénic critical balance, ∣k∥​∣vA​∼k⊥​U, the same inequality gives ωI​≪k⊥​U. Rapid rotation relative to the horizontal nonlinear rate can therefore coexist with leading Alfvénic decoupling in the window
k⊥​U≪2Ω≪k⊥​vA​.​
(25)
High electrical conductivity makes magnetic diffusion slow, and the corresponding inviscid approximation is also assumed. If 2Ω≳k⊥​vA​, rotational and magnetic restoring effects are comparable and the modes are mixed magneto-Coriolis disturbances; in the rotation-dominated limit they separate into fast inertial-like and slow magnetostrophic branches, rather than into independently evolving shear and pseudo-Alfvén polarizations.
At finite amplitude, independence is generally one-way in the usual anisotropic ideal magnetohydrodynamics reduction: the Alfvénic subsystem has no leading feedback from pseudo-Alfvénic perturbations, but it can still advect them. The small rotational-coupling condition removes an additional two-way coupling, rather than all nonlinear advection.
This is an asymptotic finite-timescale statement. The two nonrotating Alfvén polarizations are degenerate, so even a weak nonzero rotational coupling can rotate their superposition over the much longer time ωI−1​. Exact decoupling for arbitrarily long times does not follow from a small ratio alone. The strictly k∥​=0 nonpropagating limit is separate.

Use the same constant dynamic viscosity μ for both incompressible Stokes flows, and let f(a) be body force per unit volume. Their equations and Newtonian fluid stress tensor are
∂j​σij(a)​+fi(a)​=0,∂i​ui(a)​=0,σij(a)​=−p(a)δij​+2μeij(a)​,
(26)
where eij(a)​=(∂i​uj(a)​+∂j​ui(a)​)/2 is the strain-rate tensor. Consider the cross-work flux
Qj​=ui(2)​σij(1)​−ui(1)​σij(2)​.
(27)
By the product rule,
∂j​Qj​=(∂j​ui(2)​)σij(1)​−(∂j​ui(1)​)σij(2)​−ui(2)​fi(1)​+ui(1)​fi(2)​.
(28)
The pressure terms vanish by incompressibility. Symmetry of the stress tensor removes antisymmetric velocity gradient contributions, leaving 2μe(1):e(2) in both cross terms; they cancel. Apply the divergence theorem, with n outward from V, and rearrange:
∫V​f(1)⋅u(2)dV+∫∂V​u(2)⋅σ(1)ndS=​∫V​f(2)⋅u(1)dV+∫∂V​u(1)⋅σ(2)ndS.​​
(29)
This is the Lorentz reciprocal theorem with body forces. It depends on reciprocal linear viscous response in the same fluid; arbitrary unrelated constitutive laws would not supply the cross-term cancellation.

Write the supplied velocity in the radial form
u=f(r)U+g(r)(U⋅x)x,f=4r3a​+4r3a3​,g=4r33a​−4r53a3​.
(30)
At r=a, f=1 and g=0, giving the no-slip boundary condition u=U. At infinity u→0 and the supplied gauge-fixed pressure tends to zero. These are the laboratory-frame conditions for a sphere translating through otherwise quiescent fluid.
The exterior Stokes equations can also be checked directly:
∇⋅u=(U⋅x)(rf′​+rg′+4g)=0,
(31)
and
∇2u​=(f′′+r2f′​+2g)U+(g′′+r6g′​)(U⋅x)x=23a​[r3U​−r53(U⋅x)x​]=μ1​∇p.​
(32)
To compute surface traction in translating-sphere Stokes flow, let n=x/a point from the sphere into the fluid. At the surface, f′(a)=−3/(2a), g′(a)=3/(2a3) and g(a)=0. Hence
∂j​ui​∣a​=2a3​[−Ui​nj​+(U⋅n)ni​nj​],p(a)=2a3μ​(U⋅n).
(33)
Substitute into the Newtonian fluid stress tensor. Its viscous contribution to the surface traction is 3μ[−U+(U⋅n)n]/(2a), so the normal part cancels the pressure contribution:
σn=−2a3μ​U.​
(34)
The vector is constant over the entire sphere in the stated pressure gauge. Integrating it gives the fluid's drag force on the sphere,
FH​=∫r=a​σndS=−2a3μ​(4πa2)U=−6πμaU.​
(35)
This is Stokes's law. On an exterior fluid domain the outward domain normal at its inner boundary is −n; that distinction will matter in the reciprocal calculation.

Let Sa​ be the sphere surface, ns​ point from the sphere into the fluid, and write
w=uS​−u∞​,τ=σS​−σ∞​.
(36)
Because the prescribed exterior body forces are unchanged, they cancel on subtraction: the disturbance is a homogeneous Stokes flow in the exterior domain. On the sphere the no-slip boundary condition is
w=V+ωs​×x−u∞​,
(37)
allowing an arbitrary sphere rotation ωs​.
Use as auxiliary flow the translating-sphere solution of part b, with arbitrary test velocity U. Its surface velocity is U and its surface traction is −3μU/(2a). Applying the Lorentz reciprocal theorem on the exterior domain gives, after cancelling the common inner-normal minus sign,
∫Sa​​w⋅σ∗ns​dS=∫Sa​​U⋅τns​dS.
(38)
The outer surface terms vanish for decaying disturbances: velocities are at most O(r−1), stresses O(r−2), and the integrated cross-work is O(r−1).
There are no body forces inside the sphere in the original ambient problem. Thus the smooth ambient stress tensor has zero divergence there, and
∫Sa​​σ∞​ns​dS=∫r<a​∇⋅σ∞​dV=0.
(39)
Also ∫Sa​​xdS=0, so the rotation term drops out of the left side. Consequently the reciprocal identity reduces to
−2a3μ​U⋅[4πa2V−∫Sa​​u∞​dS]=U⋅FH​.
(40)
Since U is arbitrary, the result before imposing mechanical balance is
FH​=−6πμa[V−4πa21​∫Sa​​u∞​dS].​
(41)
For a freely suspended sphere with negligible inertia and no applied external force, FH​=0. Its sphere velocity as a surface average of ambient Stokes flow is therefore
V=4πa21​∫∣x∣=a​u∞​(x)dS.​
(42)
The implicit force-free condition is essential. More generally, an external force Fext​ gives V=⟨u∞​⟩Sa​​+Fext​/(6πμa). For example, an externally pulled sphere in the zero ambient flow has nonzero velocity, contrary to an unrestricted surface-average-only claim. No torque-free assumption is needed for this translational identity, because the auxiliary surface traction is constant.

The undisturbed ambient solution is smooth and free of body forces throughout the ball r≤a. Its Stokes equations imply
μ∇2u∞​=∇p∞​,∇⋅u∞​=0.
(43)
Taking the divergence gives ∇2p∞​=0, and a further Laplacian therefore gives
∇4u∞​=μ1​∇(∇2p∞​)=0.​
(44)
Each velocity component obeys the biharmonic equation in the whole ball.
Expand a component of u∞​ at the centre and average over x=an. The odd spherical moments vanish, while
⟨ni​nj​⟩=31​δij​,⟨ni​nj​nk​nl​⟩=151​(δij​δkl​+δik​δjl​+δil​δjk​).
(45)
Thus the spherical mean of a biharmonic function gives
⟨u∞​⟩Sa​​=[1+6a2​∇2+120a4​∇4+⋯]u∞​(0)=u∞​(0)+6a2​∇2u∞​(0).
(46)
The truncation is exact, not just a small-sphere approximation: all terms containing two or more Laplacians vanish. One can avoid any Taylor-convergence issue by letting M(r)=⟨u∞​(rn)⟩. Since ∇2u∞​ is harmonic, its spherical mean is ∇2u∞​(0), so
M′′+r2​M′=∇2u∞​(0),M(0)=u∞​(0),M′(0)=0.
(47)
Integrating gives M(r)=u∞​(0)+r2∇2u∞​(0)/6 for every admissible r.
Combining with the force-free result of part c proves Faxén's first law in the requested form:
V=(1+6a2​∇2)u∞​​x=0​.​
(48)
The force-free qualification carries over. Local absence of ambient body forces and regularity inside the ball are also needed; if forcing or a singularity lies inside it, the harmonic-pressure and biharmonic-mean argument does not apply unchanged.
