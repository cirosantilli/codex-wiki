a OurBigBook.com
Take the inviscid limit of the Navier-Stokes equation, neglect gravity, and use ideal magnetohydrodynamics. The governing equations are
∇⋅v=0,ρ(∂t​v+v⋅∇v)=−∇P+μ0​(∇×B)×B​,∂t​B=∇×(v×B),∇⋅B=0.
(1)
The continuity equation also gives Dt​ρ=0. Write the first-order magnetic perturbation as b=B′ and linearize about the uniform stationary background:
ρ0​∂t​v=−∇(P′+μ0​B0​bz​​)+μ0​B0​​∂z​b,∂t​b=B0​∂z​v,∇⋅v=∇⋅b=0.
(2)
For a nonzero-wavenumber plane wave proportional to ei(kz−ωt), the two divergence constraints imply vz​=bz​=0. The longitudinal momentum equation then gives P′=0 for that mode, and ∂t​ρ′=0 gives ρ′=0 for nonzero ω. Thus the propagating Alfvén wave is transverse. Its two independent transverse directions have the same dispersion relation.
The Linear Alfvén wave equations reduce to
−iωρ0​v=μ0​ikB0​​b,−iωb=ikB0​v.
(3)
Eliminating either amplitude gives
ω2=k2vA2​,vA​=μ0​ρ0​​B0​​,b=−ωkB0​​v.​
(4)
For B0​>0, the branch traveling along +z has ω=kvA​ and b=−μ0​ρ0​​v. The opposite branch reverses that amplitude sign. Finite viscosity would additionally damp the motion; it is neglected in obtaining the real ideal dispersion relation.

b OurBigBook.com
Subtract the constant equilibrium magnetic energy density. Since B0​⋅b=0, the perturbation densities are
EK​=21​ρ0​∣v∣2,EB​=2μ0​∣b∣2​.
(5)
The traveling-wave amplitude relation from part a gives
EB​=EK​,E=EK​+EB​=ρ0​∣v∣2=μ0​∣b∣2​.​
(6)
This is pointwise Alfvén wave energy transport for one traveling wave, not a claim that every superposition or standing wave has pointwise equipartition.
For the real fields written as v=Re(vei(kz−ωt)) and similarly for b, the period averages are
⟨EK​⟩=4ρ0​​∣v∣2,⟨EB​⟩=4μ0​1​∣b∣2,⟨E⟩=2ρ0​​∣v∣2.​
(7)
The factors one half distinguish a sinusoid's peak amplitude from its mean squared value; the same complex-vector convention also covers circular polarization of waves.

c OurBigBook.com
In ideal magnetohydrodynamics, the first-order electric field is E1​=−v×B0​ez​. Retaining its product with both the background and first-order magnetic field gives the Poynting vector
S=μ0​1​E1​×(B0​ez​+b)=μ0​B02​​v−μ0​B0​​(v⋅b)ez​.
(8)
The first term is transverse and oscillatory, with zero period average. The second is quadratic and transports wave energy. A potential quadratic ideal electric-field term −v×b vanishes here because the real traveling-wave fields are parallel or antiparallel.
For the +z branch, b=−μ0​ρ0​​v, so
⟨S⟩=ρ0​vA​⟨∣v∣2⟩ez​=vA​⟨E⟩ez​.​
(9)
For the opposite branch, its sign reverses. The averaged Poynting flux is the total wave energy density, including equal kinetic and magnetic shares, multiplied by the group velocity. This agrees with Alfvén wave energy transport. Although the flux in this expression is electromagnetic, it supplies the transport of both components of the coupled fluid-field wave.

d OurBigBook.com
In this question, the coefficient multiplying ∇2B is the magnetic diffusivity. Denote it ηm​; it is the paper's η, with dimensions of length squared per time. If ηOhm​ denotes electrical resistivity, then ηm​=ηOhm​/μ0​. The two conventions must not be interchanged.
For an imposed real positive ω, retain the momentum equation and use
−iωρ0​v=μ0​ikB0​​b,(−iω+ηm​k2)b=ikB0​v.
(10)
Elimination gives the exact Resistive damping of an Alfvén wave relation
ω2+iηm​ωk2−vA2​k2=0,k2=vA2​−iηm​ωω2​.​
(11)
Here k is complex because the driving frequency is real. Introduce the dimensionless diffusion parameter ϵ=ηm​ω/vA2​≪1. For propagation and attenuation along +z, choose the square-root branch with Rek,Imk>0. The spatial damping of a driven Alfvén wave is
k=vA​ω​(1−iϵ)−1/2=vA​ω​(1+2iϵ​+O(ϵ2)).​
(12)
With the convention ei(kz−ωt), the amplitude decays as e−Imkz. Define its e-folding length LA​=1/Imk and the wavelength λ=2π/Rek. Then
LA​∼ηm​ω22vA3​​,λLA​​∼πϵ1​.​
(13)
If damping length instead refers to wave energy, the squared amplitude decays twice as fast: LE​=LA​/2 and LE​/λ∼1/(2πϵ). Stating the convention avoids a factor-of-two ambiguity. The temporal damping rate ηm​k2/2 at fixed real k is a different specification from this imposed-frequency problem.

a OurBigBook.com
Expand the given translating-sphere Stokes flow into its Stokeslet and potential dipole parts:
u=43a​(rU​+r3(U⋅x)x​)+4a3​(r3U​−r53(U⋅x)x​).
(14)
The second parenthesis is ∇[(U⋅x)/r3] and has zero curl outside the sphere. For the first,
∇×rU​=r3U×x​,∇×r3(U⋅x)x​=r3U×x​.
(15)
Thus the vorticity of translating-sphere Stokes flow is
ω=∇×u=23a​r3U×x​.​
(16)
The result is independent of the irrotational finite-size dipole correction. It holds in the exterior r>a; the singular solid harmonics are used only outside their source.

b OurBigBook.com
Use the force flux at infinity in Stokes flow. With an outward normal from a large sphere, the force on the body is F=∫SR​​σndS. Let C=3a/4. The leading far-field velocity and pressure are
ui​=C(rUi​​+r3(U⋅x)xi​​)+O(r−3),p=2μCr3U⋅x​.
(17)
Their rate-of-strain tensor is
eij​=C(U⋅x)(r3δij​​−r53xi​xj​​)+O(r−4).
(18)
In the Newtonian fluid stress tensor σij​=−pδij​+2μeij​, the isotropic pieces cancel. Consequently,
σn=−R26μC​(U⋅n)n+O(R−4).
(19)
The second spherical moment integral ∫ni​nj​dΩ=(4π/3)δij​ follows from rotational symmetry and ∑i​ni2​=1. Since dS=R2dΩ, taking R→∞ gives the Stokes drag law:
F=−6μC34π​U=−6πμaU.​
(20)
The omitted dipole contributes a surface integral of order R−2 and vanishes in this limit. The sign refers to the fluid's drag on the moving sphere.

c OurBigBook.com
At the terminal velocity, buoyancy-corrected weight balances the Stokes drag law:
34πa3​(ρ​−ρ)g−6πμaU=0.
(21)
Hence
U=9μ2a2(ρ​−ρ)​g,∣U∣=9μ2a2∣ρ​−ρ∣​∣g∣.​
(22)
A denser sphere falls in the direction of g; a lighter one rises. Equal densities give no sedimentation. The calculation assumes negligible fluid Reynolds number, an unbounded fluid, and negligible transient body acceleration after terminal settling. The fluid mass density enters through buoyancy and through the small-Reynolds number condition.

d OurBigBook.com
Take z positive upward, let sphere A's centre be Z(t)ez​, and write its downward velocity as −Uez​, with Z˙=−U. At B's centre the displacement from A is r=ℓex​−Zez​. The vorticity of translating-sphere Stokes flow therefore gives
ω∞​=−2(ℓ2+Z2)3/23aUℓ​ey​.
(23)
B is held in translation but has zero applied torque. By Faxén's rotational law, it rotates with half the incident vorticity:
ΩB​=−4(ℓ2+Z2)3/23aUℓ​ey​.
(24)
Integrating the rotation induced by a passing Stokes sphere along the complete path cancels U:
Δθ=43aℓ​ey​∫+∞−∞​(ℓ2+Z2)3/2dZ​=−2ℓ3a​ey​.​
(25)
Its magnitude is 3a/(2ℓ) radians, with direction about the negative y axis by the right-hand rule. All instantaneous rotations have this same axis, so the integrated angular velocity gives the net angle without a noncommuting-rotation correction. The result is independent of B's radius in the prescribed incident-flow calculation.
The inequality ℓ>a+b ensures no overlap. It does not by itself make mutual hydrodynamic interactions negligible: the stated flow is the isolated-A solution, and a held finite B also disturbs A's flow. Thus the displayed angle is the result for that supplied incident flow, or the leading one-way approximation to separated bodies. A fully coupled two-sphere calculation uses the method of reflections for Stokes flow or an exact pair resistance solution.
