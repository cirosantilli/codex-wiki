Solution

a OurBigBook.com
Write the physical magnetic field as Bphys​ and use the constant mass density ρ0​ to introduce Alfvén velocity units, B=Bphys​/μ0​ρ0​​. If P is the fluid's mechanical pressure, the magnetohydrodynamic total pressure per unit mass density is p=P/ρ0​+B2/2. The ideal magnetohydrodynamic equations then reduce to
∂t​u+(u⋅∇)u∂t​B+(u⋅∇)B∇⋅u​=−∇p+(B⋅∇)B,=(B⋅∇)u,=0,∇⋅B=0.​​
(1)
The first equation is momentum conservation: the magnetic pressure has been included in p, leaving magnetic tension explicitly. The second is the ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation, expressing magnetic flux freezing. The last two equations impose incompressibility and the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint.
The model assumes a smooth conducting-fluid continuum with constant mass density and constant magnetic permeability, nonrelativistic velocity, and scales on which a single-fluid description is appropriate. Negligible electrical resistivity removes magnetic diffusion; negligible viscosity removes viscous momentum diffusion. Equivalently, the magnetic Reynolds number and the Reynolds number are large on the scales being retained. The low-frequency approximation neglects the displacement current; Hall and other kinetic corrections to Ohm's law are absent. Incompressibility is an imposed approximation, commonly justified when the Mach number is small. There is no independently evolved thermodynamic pressure: p enforces the incompressibility condition. We omit external body forces; a conservative one could instead be absorbed into p.

The magnetic second-moment tensor is Mij​=Bi​Bj​. This is the magnetic dyadic called the Maxwell tensor in the paper; the isotropic part of the usual Maxwell stress tensor is already contained in the magnetohydrodynamic total pressure. The solenoidal magnetic-field constraint gives
∂j​Mij​=Bj​∂j​Bi​+Bi​∂j​Bj​=Bj​∂j​Bi​.
(2)
Consequently the magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation in part a becomes Dt​ui​=−∂i​p+∂j​Mij​, where Dt​=∂t​+un​∂n​ is the material derivative. Applying its product rule and the ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation gives
Dt​Mij​​=(Dt​Bi​)Bj​+Bi​(Dt​Bj​)=Bn​Bj​∂n​ui​+Bi​Bn​∂n​uj​=Mnj​∂n​ui​+Min​∂n​uj​​.​
(3)
Thus the evolution uses only u,M,p, together with ∂i​ui​=0. To determine p, take the divergence of the magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation. Since ∂t​∂i​ui​=0 and uj​∂j​∂i​ui​=0,
∇2p=∂i​∂j​Mij​−(∂i​uj​)(∂j​ui​).​
(4)
This is the pressure Poisson equation for incompressible flow, with boundary conditions supplied by the imposed normal acceleration and normal stress, or by periodicity or decay at infinity as appropriate. For example, the normal component of the magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation requires n⋅∇p=n⋅(∇⋅M−Dt​u) at a prescribed boundary. The pressure is determined up to a spatially constant function of time.
For equivalence to one resolved magnetic field, the initial M must actually be BBT for a divergence-free vector field. In particular it is a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix of matrix rank at most one. The tensor equations also admit more general stresses; the averaged M in part d need not have matrix rank one.

With zero velocity and no time dependence, the magnetic second-moment tensor equation holds automatically, but the magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation still requires
∂j​Mij(0)​=∂i​p0​.​
(5)
The magnetic tension must therefore be a pressure gradient. Locally, its necessary integrability condition is
∇×(∇⋅M(0))=0;
(6)
on a simply connected region this is also sufficient for a scalar p0​, subject to the global boundary conditions. In physical units the same balance is ∇P0​=J0​×Bphys,0​: the Lorentz force density is balanced by mechanical pressure, rather than necessarily vanishing. A zero mean magnetic field does not make the local magnetic tension zero, so an arbitrary tangle is not automatically a static equilibrium.

Take the trace of the statistically isotropic magnetic second-moment tensor. There are three spatial directions, so
⟨B2⟩=⟨Bi​Bi​⟩=vA2​δii​=3vA2​.
(7)
Choosing the nonnegative speed,
vA​=3⟨B2⟩​​=3μ0​ρ0​⟨Bphys2​⟩​​.​
(8)
Thus vA​ is the root-mean-square value of any one Cartesian magnetic field component in Alfvén velocity units. It is not the magnitude of the mean Alfvén velocity, which is zero here. Statistical isotropy fixes a second moment even when the first moment vanishes.

Average the equations in part b and make exactly the prescribed scale-separation approximation: the retained perturbations vary on lengths much greater than ℓ, and perturbations at the tangle scale are omitted. Products of two retained perturbations can then be neglected. Writing v=δu, m=δM and π=δp, the linearized equations are
∂t​vi​=−∂i​π+∂j​mij​,∂t​mij​=vA2​(∂j​vi​+∂i​vj​),∂i​vi​=0.
(9)
No unmodelled small-scale response has been inserted into this closure.
Introduce the fluid displacement ξ by v=∂t​ξ and choose ∇⋅ξ=0. Integrating the magnetic second-moment tensor equation gives
mij​=vA2​(∂i​ξj​+∂j​ξi​)+sij​(r),​
(10)
where s is a time-independent integration tensor set by the initial stress. For a pure oscillatory normal mode, s=0. Its restoring force is therefore vA2​∇2ξ: the isotropic magnetic tangle has the same transverse response as an incompressible elastic medium with shear modulus ρ0​vA2​.
For a plane wave proportional to ei(k⋅r−ωt), with ω=0, incompressibility gives k⋅v=0. The tensor equation and magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation become
mij​=−ωvA2​​(ki​vj​+kj​vi​),−iωvi​=−iki​π+ikj​mij​.
(11)
Dotting the second equation with k yields π=0 for k=0, since ki​kj​mij​=0. Substitution then gives
ω2=vA2​k2,mij​=ivA2​(ki​ξ​j​+kj​ξ​i​),k⋅ξ​=0.​
(12)
There are two independent transverse polarizations. The phase speed is vA​ in every direction, and the two signs of ω give group velocities ±vA​k. These magnetoelastic waves in an isotropic magnetic tangle exist because a shear deformation distorts the second moment of the tangled magnetic field, producing a restoring magnetic tension even though its mean is zero.
For comparison, a uniform magnetic field with Alfvén velocity B0​ gives the incompressible Alfvén wave relation ω2=(k⋅B0​)2. Its frequency depends on the direction of k, and energy propagates along B0​. Here the averaged restoring response is isotropic: there is no preferred mean-field direction. Both systems resist transverse deformation through magnetic tension, rather than through compressive sound.
The propagating branch is not the entire perturbation space. For example, v=0, mij​=f(r)δij​ and π=f(r) solve the linearized equations without propagation, for any small, slowly varying f. Such a static stress perturbation in magnetoelastic wave equations balances a pressure gradient. Thus the requested wave conclusion applies to the oscillatory branch; it must not exclude stationary initial stress perturbations.

f OurBigBook.com
The common part of the magnetohydrodynamic-viscoelastic analogy is stretching and advection of a line element. With the component-first velocity gradient Lij​=∂j​ui​, the ideal tensor equation is
Dt​M−LM−MLT=0.
(13)
Its left side is an upper-convected derivative, the same deformation law that appears for a polymer conformation tensor when its relaxation is neglected.
There are two important physical differences. First, the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint prevents a magnetic field line from having an isolated source or sink inside the fluid. A polymer strand can have molecular endpoints, so there is no corresponding no-endpoint constraint on the strands. Second, a stretched polymer has a preferred relaxed distribution of configurations and a finite polymer relaxation time; it can recoil relative to the solvent through molecular drag and thermal motion. An ideal magnetic field has no analogous local tendency to return to a prescribed coil size. Real polymers also have a finite contour length, a limitation absent from the ideal field-stretching law.
For example, a normalized Hookean dumbbell conformation equation has the dissipative extension
Dt​C−LC−CLT=−τ1​(C−I).​
(14)
This is local relaxation toward an isotropic thermal configuration. It remains nonzero for a spatially uniform stretched C. By contrast, constant magnetic diffusivity ηm​ adds ηm​∇2B to the resistive induction equation. The product rule gives the resistive evolution of the magnetic second-moment tensor:
Dt​Mij​−Lin​Mnj​−Min​Ljn​=ηm​∇2Mij​−2ηm​(∂n​Bi​)(∂n​Bj​).​
(15)
The magnetic correction involves spatial gradients, vanishes for a uniform magnetic field, and does not create field-line endpoints: ∂t​(∇⋅B)=ηm​∇2(∇⋅B). It permits slippage and magnetic reconnection while preserving the no-monopole constraint. After averaging, its gradient-product term generally needs additional information beyond the averaged M. One cannot replace it indiscriminately by the local polymer relaxation term, or even by ηm​∇2M alone.

Work in the laboratory frame, with the fluid at rest at infinity. Set
f(r)=4r3a​+4r3a3​,g(r)=4r33a​−4r53a3​,u=fU+g(U⋅x)x.
(16)
At r=a, f=1 and g=0, so the no-slip boundary condition is u=U on the translating sphere. As r→∞, u=O(r−1)→0 and the gauge-fixed pressure tends to zero.
It is also possible to check the exterior Stokes equations directly. Differentiating the radial functions gives
∇⋅u=(U⋅x)(rf′​+rg′+4g)=0,
(17)
and
∇2u​=(f′′+r2f′​+2g)U+(g′′+r6g′​)(U⋅x)x=23a​(r3U​−r53(U⋅x)x​)=μ∇p​.​
(18)
Thus the Newtonian fluid stress tensor σ=−pI+μ(∇u+∇uT) satisfies ∇⋅σ=0 throughout r>a.
Let n point away from the common centre on each concentric sphere. Applying the divergence theorem to the intervening fluid shell, whose inner normal is −n, yields
Fdrag​=∫r=a​σndS=∫r=R​σndS,R>a.​
(19)
This is the force flux at infinity in Stokes flow. It is legitimate even though the stress tensor tends to zero pointwise, because the area of the large sphere grows as R2.
For an explicit evaluation, put A=3a/4 and n=x/R. The leading terms are u=A[U+(U⋅n)n]/R and p=2μA(U⋅n)/R2. Their velocity gradient is
∂j​ui​=R2A​[−Ui​nj​+Uj​ni​+(U⋅n)δij​−3(U⋅n)ni​nj​]+O(R4a3∣U∣​).
(20)
After symmetrizing and subtracting the pressure contribution,
σn=−2R29μa​(U⋅n)n+O(R4μa3∣U∣​).
(21)
By rotational symmetry, ∫ni​nj​dΩ=(4π/3)δij​. Hence
Fdrag​=−29μa​∫(U⋅n)ndΩ=−6πμaU​.
(22)
This is Stokes's law, with the force on the sphere opposite its velocity. For a component calculation with U=Uez​, the same integral is Fz​=−(9μaU/2)∫cos2θdΩ.

Neglect the inertia of the sphere and balance the applied force against Stokes drag. The sphere translates with U=F1​/ζ, where ζ=6πμa. The applied force is also the net force transmitted to the fluid; the fluid's drag on the sphere has the opposite sign.
Translate the origin to r1​. In the solution of part a, retain the terms of order a/r and discard those of order a3/r3. With n=(x−r1​)/r,
u(x)​∼4r3a​(I+nnT)U=8πμr1​(I+nnT)F1​​.​
(23)
This is the Stokeslet, or equivalently the Oseen tensor G(r)=(I+nnT)/(8πμr) acting on F1​. The omitted finite-size velocity is of order a2∣F1​∣/(μr3), so the displayed term is the leading far field for r≫a. The singular Stokeslet must not be evaluated at its own centre to obtain a particle's self mobility.

c OurBigBook.com
Use the deterministic Hookean dumbbell model, with a zero-rest-length spring and negligible connector drag. For R=r1​−r2​, its elastic forces on the two beads are
F1​=−HR,F2​=+HR.
(24)
Here H>0 is the spring constant. No independent thermal forcing from Brownian motion or bead inertia is included. The unperturbed linear velocity field is Lr, with trL=0 by incompressibility.
The fluid velocity used in each bead's drag law is the imposed flow plus the disturbance from the other bead, with its own singular field omitted. At leading order in a/R, the evenness of the Oseen tensor, G(−R)=G(R), gives
r˙1​r˙2​​=Lr1​+G(R)F2​+ζF1​​,=Lr2​+G(R)F1​+ζF2​​,ζ=6πμa​.​
(25)
These are the pair hydrodynamic mobility matrix relations. Their signs follow from balancing each spring force with −ζ(r˙i​−uext,i​), the fluid drag on that bead.
Adding the equations cancels both the direct spring slips and the cross-induced velocities, since F1​+F2​=0. For the midpoint rc​=(r1​+r2​)/2,
r˙c​=Lrc​.​
(26)
The midpoint therefore follows the undisturbed linear velocity field. Subtracting the equations instead gives the hydrodynamic interaction of a Hookean dumbbell:
R˙​=LR+(ζ−1I−G(R))(F1​−F2​)=LR−ζ2H​[I−8πμRζ​(I+RRT)]R​.​
(27)
The dimensionless interaction matrix is
ζG(R)=4R3a​(I+RRT).
(28)
Its transverse and longitudinal eigenvalues are 3a/(4R) and 3a/(2R), respectively. Since the spring force is longitudinal, the separation equation can also be written
R˙=LR−ζ2H​(1−2R3a​)R.​
(29)
The relative correction to the free-draining spring relaxation rate is therefore 3a/(2R), explicitly proportional to a/R. Entrainment by the other bead reduces their relative approach for given opposing spring forces.
This is a far-field hydrodynamic interaction, valid for R≫a; it is not a near-contact law and omits higher reflections and lubrication. The printed target also fixes the spring's rest length implicitly. For an ordinary spring obeying Hooke's law with nonzero rest length R0​, the elastic force would be −H(R−R0​)R, and every spring term above would acquire the factor 1−R0​/R. The displayed result uses the zero-rest-length spring convention appropriate to the ideal Hookean dumbbell model.
