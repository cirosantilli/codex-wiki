Start in an inertial frame with the inviscid magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation,
ρDt​v=−∇P+J×B−ρ∇Φ.
(1)
Here ρ is mass density, P is the isotropic fluid pressure, v is inertial-frame velocity, J is electric current density, B is the magnetic field, and Φ is gravitational potential per unit mass. In the low-frequency approximation to Ampère's law, J=∇×B/μ0​. The vector calculus identity
(∇×B)×B=(B⋅∇)B−∇(B2/2)
(2)
separates the Lorentz force density into magnetic tension and a magnetic pressure gradient. Transform the material acceleration to a rigid frame rotating with constant angular velocity Ω0​, using the stated frame transformation. The rotating-frame magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation is
ρDt​u=−∇(P+2μ0​B2​)+μ0​(B⋅∇)B​−ρ∇Φ−2ρΩ0​×u−ρΩ0​×(Ω0​×r).​
(3)
The final terms are the Coriolis force and centrifugal force densities; u is the velocity relative to the rotating frame, and Dt​=∂t​+u⋅∇ uses that frame.
This requires a smooth conducting-fluid continuum with nonrelativistic motion, a constant magnetic permeability μ0​, the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint, a negligible displacement current and negligible net electric-charge force. Viscous forces and anisotropic fluid stresses are omitted. A time-dependent frame would add an Euler force. Incompressible flow is not needed for this force equation. Finite electrical resistivity does not change its displayed instantaneous magnetic force; the later frozen-field calculation additionally uses ideal magnetohydrodynamics. A differentially rotating fluid does not define a single rigid frame with spatially varying Ω0​: choose a fixed local reference rate and retain the fluid's shear in u.
