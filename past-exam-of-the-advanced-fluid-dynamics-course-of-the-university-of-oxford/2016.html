a OurBigBook.com
Use the source's Gaussian units and neglect the displacement current, as in nonrelativistic magnetohydrodynamics. Write Δp=p⊥​−p∥​ for the pressure anisotropy. The pressure tensor is P=p⊥​I−Δpbb, so its force density is
−∇⋅P=−∇p⊥​+∇⋅(Δpbb).
(1)
Ampère's law and the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint give the Lorentz force decomposition
cj×B​=4π(∇×B)×B​=−∇8πB2​+4π(B⋅∇)B​=−∇8πB2​+∇⋅4πBB​.
(2)
Adding these two force densities and using BB=B2bb yields
ρDtDu​=−∇(p⊥​+8πB2​)+∇⋅[(Δp+4πB2​)bb].​
(3)
The first term is the gradient of the modified magnetohydrodynamic total pressure; the second is the anisotropic Maxwell stress tensor contribution together with the directional pressure force. Their effective coefficient is the pressure-anisotropy-modified magnetic tension, T=B2/(4π)+Δp. At fixed magnetic field, positive pressure anisotropy increases resistance to bending, while negative pressure anisotropy reduces it. When T becomes negative, bending experiences an amplifying force rather than a restoring force. Gradients of Δp can also drive motion, so the curvature interpretation refers specifically to the bending contribution.

Set Δp0​=p⊥0​−p∥0​ and T0​=Δp0​+B02​/(4π). Distinguish the scalar magnetic field strength perturbation δB from the vector perturbation δB. To first order,
δB=δBz+B0​δb,δbz​=0,k⊥​δbx​=−k∥​B0​δB​,
(4)
where the last identity is the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint. For a Fourier mode with the stated time convention, put
δq=δp⊥​+4πB0​δB​,δT=δp⊥​−δp∥​+2πB0​δB​.
(5)
The linear momentum conservation equation, after division by i, is
−ωρ0​u=−kδq+k∥​zδT+T0​[k∥​δb+z(k⋅δb)].
(6)
Use the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint in its z component. The coefficient of z reduces to −k∥​[δp∥​+Δp0​δB/B0​]. Thus
−ωρ0​u=−k⊥​x(δp⊥​+4πB0​δB​)−k∥​z(δp∥​+Δp0​B0​δB​)+k∥​T0​δb.​
(7)
Both scalar pressure perturbations act within the xz plane. The y component therefore decouples, independently of their closure. The ideal magnetohydrodynamic equations give
∂t​δB=B0​∂z​u−B0​z∇⋅u,−ωδby​=k∥​uy​,−ωρ0​uy​=k∥​T0​δby​.
(8)
Eliminating the amplitudes gives the Alfvén wave dispersion relation
ω2=ρ0​k∥2​T0​​=k∥2​vA2​(1+B02​4πΔp0​​),vA2​=4πρ0​B02​​.​
(9)
For this Shear Alfvén wave, the velocity and fluid displacement are along y, perpendicular to both k and the equilibrium magnetic field. Let ξ=ξy​y​; then
u=−iωξ,δB=ik∥​B0​ξ,δb=ik∥​ξ.
(10)
A real snapshot ξy​=ξ0​cos(k⋅r−ωt) bends the magnetic field lines sideways, with δBy​=B0​∂z​ξy​. There is no first-order compression or change of magnetic field strength: ∇⋅ξ=0, δρ=0 and δB=0. Scalar pressure perturbations are unnecessary in this transverse subsystem. For a traveling stable branch, δBy​/B0​=−k∥​uy​/ω, so the velocity and transverse magnetic field perturbations are in phase or opposite phase according to the branch. At k∥​=0 the bending force and this angular frequency vanish.

c OurBigBook.com
For k∥​=0 the Alfvén wave becomes a firehose instability when its squared angular frequency is negative:
p∥0​−p⊥0​>4πB02​​,p∥0​p⊥0​−p∥0​​<−β2​,β=B02​8πp∥0​​.​
(11)
Equality is marginal, rather than exponential growth. The pressure anisotropy must have negative sign; its absolute magnitude alone is insufficient.
For the intergalactic plasma, a negative relative pressure anisotropy of −10−2 reaches this fluid threshold only for β>200. In particular, the literal pair β=100 and relative pressure anisotropy −0.01 is stable because the threshold is −0.02. Since the quoted numbers are approximate and allow larger plasma beta, this weakly magnetized plasma is susceptible, rather than necessarily unstable at every quoted value. For example, β=300 and relative pressure anisotropy −0.01 are unstable. Positive pressure anisotropy does not excite this branch.
For the fusion-device plasma, β∼10−2 requires a negative relative pressure anisotropy below roughly −200, whereas its magnitude is only about 0.1. The stabilizing magnetic tension dominates, so it is not susceptible to this fluid firehose instability under the stated conditions. Indeed, nonnegative perpendicular pressure requires the relative pressure anisotropy to be no smaller than −1; at such a small plasma beta even this extreme cannot satisfy the threshold.

d OurBigBook.com
Magnetic flux freezing ties a transverse fluid displacement to a bend in the magnetic field lines. For the uniform background, the transverse displacement equation is
ρ0​∂t2​ξ⊥​=T0​∂z2​ξ⊥​.
(12)
For a sinusoidal bend, ∂z2​ξ⊥​=−k∥2​ξ⊥​. Positive pressure-anisotropy-modified magnetic tension therefore gives a restoring force, and inertia produces oscillation. In an isotropic plasma, this is precisely the positive magnetic tension B02​/(4π).
Particles moving predominantly along bent magnetic field lines contribute a transverse momentum flux. Their larger parallel pressure opposes the restoring curvature force supplied by the magnetic field and perpendicular pressure. Once p∥0​−p⊥0​>B02​/(4π), the net force points along the original displacement: a larger bend produces a larger outward force, giving positive feedback. Equivalently, the bending contribution to the quadratic potential energy is 21​T0​∣∂z​ξ⊥​∣2 and has become negative.
In this static, homogeneous fluid model the unstable roots are
ω=±iγ,γ=∣k∥​∣ρ0​p∥0​−p⊥0​−B02​/(4π)​​.​
(13)
With e−iωt the root +iγ grows. Its real angular frequency is zero, so the unstable pattern grows without propagation in the equilibrium rest frame; its partner decays. Advection by a background flow would change that frame-dependent statement. The growth increasing without bound as ∣k∥​∣ increases is a limitation of this long-wavelength model: finite gyroradius and other kinetic corrections cannot be neglected at sufficiently short scales.

a OurBigBook.com
Label a trajectory by a and write its flow map as X(a,t), with ∂t​X=u(X,t). An infinitesimal material line joining neighboring labels has tangent ℓi​=Fij​daj​, where Fij​=∂Xi​/∂aj​ is the deformation gradient. Differentiating at fixed labels gives
DtDℓi​​=∂j​ui​ℓj​,∂t​ℓ+u⋅∇ℓ=(ℓ⋅∇)u.​
(14)
This identity holds for an unnormalized material line tangent even in a compressible flow. Incompressibility also makes an advected magnetic field obey the same stretching law. A unit tangent would instead require a projection removing its change of length.
Use the source's velocity gradient convention Gij​=∂i​uj​. For a outer product Aij​=ℓi​ℓj​, the product rule yields
DtDAij​​=(∂k​ui​)Akj​+Aik​(∂k​uj​)=(GTA+AG)ij​.
(15)
The same law holds for a sum or locally transported average of such outer products. Thus the dyadic transport of material line elements motivates
A▽=∂t​A+u⋅∇A−AG−GTA.​
(16)
This upper-convected derivative vanishes for a symmetric tensor transported solely by stretching of material lines. It subtracts both advection and geometric deformation, allowing a constitutive equation to isolate intrinsic relaxation or production. More generally A=FAref​FT gives A▽=FA˙ref​FT. Under a time-dependent rigid rotation Q, its value transforms as QA▽QT, making it an objective time derivative. This is a suitable derivative for this transport type; symmetry alone does not make it the unique possible objective time derivative.

i OurBigBook.com
When deformation is stopped, the rate-of-strain tensor and velocity gradient vanish, leaving T+τ∂t​T=0. Hence
T(t)=T(0)e−t/τ.​
(17)
The positive constant τ is the viscoelastic relaxation time. For slow weak deformation, all the transport and quadratic terms are smaller than T, so T≃2μE. Thus μ>0 is the zero-rate dynamic viscosity, measured in stress times time. The corresponding short-time shear modulus of the Maxwell fluid response is Gel​=μ/τ. The source's α has units of time, since αTE must have units of stress.

Linearize about a relaxed state T=0 with constant mass density ρ0​. In the unforced momentum conservation equation ρ0​Dt​u=−∇p+∇⋅T, the x component of the pressure gradient is zero for a freely evolving unidirectional flow independent of x. A prescribed external pressure gradient would add a forcing term.
For u=u(y,t)x, incompressibility is automatic and the only nonzero entries of the rate-of-strain tensor are Exy​=Eyx​=21​∂y​u. Products of stress and velocity gradient are quadratic in the small amplitude. The constitutive equation consequently becomes T+τ∂t​T=2μE to first order. With T=Txy​=Tyx​, its shear component and the x component of momentum conservation are
ρ0​∂t​u=∂y​T,T+τ∂t​T=μ∂y​u.​
(18)
The diagonal stress components have only homogeneous relaxation at this order and vanish if initially relaxed. In particular, α does not enter this linear response.

Eliminating the shear stress from the two linear equations gives the Maxwell shear-wave dispersion equation in physical space,
τ∂t2​u+∂t​u=ν∂y2​u,ν=ρ0​μ​,
(19)
where ν is the kinematic viscosity. Substitution of a Fourier mode eiky+σt gives
τσ2+σ+νk2=0,σ±​=2τ−1±1−4τνk2​​.​
(20)
These are temporal exponents: the oscillation angular frequency is the imaginary part of σ, with the sign determined by the selected time convention. For 4τνk2<1 both exponents are real and nonpositive. At equality the repeated exponent is −1/(2τ); generic solutions include te−t/(2τ). Above the threshold the modes are damped propagating shear disturbances:
σ±​=−2τ1​±iτνk2​−4τ21​​.
(21)
At large ∣k∣ their phase velocity tends to ν/τ​=Gel​/ρ0​​.
For the limit τ→0 at fixed μ,ρ0​ and k, expansion of the square root gives
σslow​=−νk2+O(τ),σfast​=−τ1​+νk2+O(τ).​
(22)
After a short initial relaxation, the surviving branch obeys the diffusion equation ut​=νuyy​ of a Newtonian fluid. The fast branch disappears on the vanishing viscoelastic relaxation time; at k=0 the two exponents are exactly 0 and −1/τ. This limit is not uniform in k: modes with k of order (τν)−1/2 still sample the oscillatory transition. If instead Gel​=μ/τ is held fixed as τ→0, then μ→0 and the fixed-k slow exponent tends to zero. The ordinary Newtonian fluid limit therefore specifies which material parameter is held fixed.

Let g=γ˙​ and use Gyx​=g for the source's velocity gradient convention. Seek the homogeneous stationary stress reached under steady simple shear flow. The material derivative of that stress tensor vanishes. In the xy plane,
G=(0g​00​),E=21​(0g​g0​).
(23)
The xx, yy and zz components of the constitutive equation give, respectively,
Txx​−(2τ−α)gTxy​=0,Tyy​+αgTxy​=0,Tzz​=0.
(24)
Thus, putting D=1+α(2τ−α)g2 and using the shear stress calculated below,
Txx​=Dμ(2τ−α)g2​,Tyy​=−Dμαg2​,Tzz​=0.​
(25)
The out-of-plane shear components vanish on the homogeneous branch. The two normal-stress differences are
N1​=2τgTxy​=D2μτg2​,N2​=−αgTxy​=−Dμαg2​,N1​N2​​=−2τα​.​
(26)
The ratio is defined at nonzero shear when N1​=0; at g=0 both differences vanish, and the displayed constant is their limiting ratio. An arbitrary isotropic pressure in the total Cauchy stress tensor cancels from both differences; it is not an arbitrary addition to the constitutively defined extra stress T itself.

The xy component of the stationary constitutive equation is
Txy​−τgTyy​+2αg​(Txx​+Tyy​)=μg.
(27)
Insert Tyy​=−αgTxy​ and Txx​=(2τ−α)gTxy​. The coefficient of g2Txy​ becomes τα+α(τ−α)=α(2τ−α), giving
Txy​=1+α(2τ−α)γ˙​2μγ˙​​,ηsh​=γ˙​Txy​​=1+α(2τ−α)γ˙​2μ​.​
(28)
For C=α(2τ−α)>0, the small-rate shear stress is Txy​=μγ˙​−μCγ˙​3+O(γ˙​5). At large positive rate, Txy​∼μ/(Cγ˙​) and the shear viscosity decreases as μ/(Cγ˙​2). The shear stress is odd in rate, reaches a positive maximum at
γ˙​∗​=C−1/2,Txy,∗​=2C​μ​,dγ˙​dTxy​​=μ(1+Cγ˙​2)21−Cγ˙​2​,
(29)
and decreases thereafter. The original plot displays this homogeneous constitutive curve. Its decreasing branch can permit shear banding; the local model alone supplies neither an interface-selection rule nor a guaranteed stable homogeneous realization.
This is a Johnson-Segalman fluid without an added Newtonian fluid solvent contribution. To identify conventions explicitly, set a=1−α/τ and L=GT=E+W, with W the spin tensor. The derivative appearing in the constitutive equation becomes
Dt​T−WT+TW−a(ET+TE),
(30)
the Gordon--Schowalter derivative. Its usual slip interval ∣a∣≤1 corresponds to 0≤α≤2τ, while a nonsingular genuinely shear thinning curve requires
τ>0,μ>0,0<α<2τ.​
(31)
At α=0 the model is the Upper-convected Maxwell model; at α=2τ it is the lower-convected derivative counterpart, and both have constant shear viscosity in this test. At α=τ the derivative is the Jaumann derivative, giving the corotational Maxwell fluid. Merely requiring α>0 is insufficient: for α=3τ the denominator is 1−3τ2γ˙​2, which vanishes at ∣γ˙​∣=1/(3​τ) and then gives a negative steady shear viscosity. There is no solvent-induced large-rate plateau in the stated equation.
