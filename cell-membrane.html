Cell membrane

 Home Biology Biological cell
OurBigBook.com Words: 6k Articles: 113
The cell membrane, also called the plasma membrane, is the selective boundary separating a biological cell's cytoplasm from its surroundings. Both prokaryotes and eukaryotes have a cell membrane. Where a cell wall is present, it is a distinct structure outside the cell membrane; the two names are not synonyms. This separation lets a biological cell maintain internal conditions while exchanging material through membrane transport. See NHGRI: cell membrane.
The familiar architecture is a lipid bilayer containing membrane proteins. In a phospholipid-based lipid bilayer, two membrane leaflets face opposite ways: hydrophilic heads face the water on each side, while hydrophobic tails face one another in the interior. The heads are therefore exposed both outside and inside the biological cell. This arrangement combines a water-facing surface with a barrier to many water-soluble solutes; the schematic represents organization rather than an atomistic structure. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: the lipid bilayer.
Selective permeability means that different substances cross at different rates under comparable conditions. Small nonpolar molecules can pass through a lipid bilayer by diffusion, while ions encounter a strong barrier without a suitable route. An ion channel supplies a selective water-filled pathway when open; a carrier protein binds a solute and changes shape to expose it to the other side. These membrane proteins therefore add specific routes to the barrier. An open ion channel permits passive transport, whereas a carrier protein can mediate either passive transport or active transport. See Alberts et al.: principles of membrane transport.
The useful combination is retention plus controlled exchange: a completely impermeable boundary would prevent uptake, whereas unrestricted mixing would erase differences in concentration and membrane potential. The fluid mosaic model connects these functions to an organized but mobile lipid bilayer containing proteins.

Selective permeability

Words: 148 Articles: 2
Cell membrane OurBigBook.com
Selective permeability means that a cell membrane permits different substances to pass at different rates under comparable conditions. Both the lipid bilayer and the available membrane proteins contribute to this selection.
Artificial boundaries can also have selective permeability. For osmosis, specify which solutes cross and over what observation interval: a boundary that retains one solute need not retain another. A semipermeable membrane is the ideal solvent-only boundary used in the osmotic pressure calculation.

Membrane permeability

membrane permeability describes passage of a specified substance through a membrane under stated conditions. It is distinct from lateral diffusion within the membrane and from the mechanical force needed to rupture the membrane.

Semipermeable membrane

Words: 60
Selective permeability OurBigBook.com
A semipermeable membrane ideally passes the solvent while excluding the specified solutes. In the aqueous osmosis model below, water can cross but the counted dissolved particles cannot. Real selective permeability is often a matter of different rates, so this idealization needs checking over the measurement interval. Neither an impermeable sheet nor a freely mixing opening realizes this model.[ref]

Membrane protein

Words: 2k Articles: 25
Cell membrane OurBigBook.com
A membrane protein is a protein associated with a biological membrane. Its association, orientation and mobility are separate properties: being embedded does not imply spanning, and spanning does not imply moving freely. Membrane proteins supply routes for membrane transport and recognition for signal transduction.[ref]
An ion channel supplies a pathway for ions; a carrier protein changes which side can reach a bound solute; a cell-surface receptor connects an external ligand to intracellular signal transduction. These examples distinguish passage from communication: a signal can cross the boundary as a change of protein activity without the ligand itself crossing.[ref]
The same boundary can therefore retain substances and communicate selectively with its surroundings. Membrane topology tells us which parts can meet a given ligand; lateral mobility of membrane proteins tells us whether two parts can approach each other along the boundary. A diagram of a cell membrane becomes more useful when it answers both questions.

Membrane topology

Words: 302
Membrane protein OurBigBook.com
Membrane topology specifies the sidedness of a membrane-associated polypeptide chain: where its N-terminus, C-terminus and intervening protein domains lie relative to the lipid bilayer. Side-specific placement is essential to interpreting a cell-surface receptor.[ref]
For an original worked example, stipulate a continuous polypeptide chain with its N-terminus outside a biological cell, exactly three complete crossings of the cell membrane, and no additional excursions into the lipid bilayer. Its segments lie, in order, outside, inside, outside, inside. A protein domain between the first and second crossings faces the cytosol; one between the second and third faces the exterior; the C-terminus faces the cytosol. Counting crossings determines these placements without specifying an atomic protein structure.
The proof is a side-switching argument. Assign exterior the sign +1 and interior −1. Each complete crossing multiplies the sign by −1, so after m crossings,
sm​=(−1)ms0​.
(1)
An odd number puts the termini on opposite sides, an even number on the same side. This assumes complete crossings of one boundary; a chain that enters and returns to the same side without crossing must not be counted as a complete crossing. Nor does the rule determine which side the N-terminus starts on.
As a constructed accessibility test, put an epitope on the outside segment between crossings two and three, and another on the inside segment between crossings one and two. Stipulate an intact cell membrane that excludes the test antibody and accessible epitopes that bind it. Applying the antibody outside can label only the first epitope. If both become accessible after opening the boundary, the difference tests sidedness under those assumptions. Failure to label an epitope alone is insufficient: it could also be inaccessible because of protein folding or poor antibody affinity. This is a proposed reasoning exercise, separate from the historical mixing experiment below.

Lateral mobility of membrane proteins (⟨r2⟩=4Dt)

Words: 467 Articles: 1
Membrane protein OurBigBook.com
Lateral mobility of membrane proteins is movement along a membrane surface. It differs from membrane transport through the boundary and from reversing membrane topology. Attachment, crowding and barriers can restrict diffusion; the fluid mosaic model does not require every membrane protein to wander freely.[ref]
An original local model treats a small, approximately flat patch as an unbounded plane with uniform diffusion coefficient D, no drift and no trapping. Represent the two displacement coordinates by independent Brownian motions, each with zero expected value and variance 2Dt. Since r2=x2+y2, addition of the two coordinate expectations gives
⟨r2⟩=4Dt,rrms​=4Dt​.
(2)
This is a root-mean-square displacement, not distance traveled along a jagged trajectory or the displacement of every protein. For a stipulated D=0.08μm2s−1 and t=5s, ⟨r2⟩=1.6μm2 and rrms​≈1.265μm. Reaching a stipulated root-mean-square scale L=2μm gives t=L2/(4D)=12.5s. These model values are not measured diffusion coefficients or mixing times from the historical experiment.
Multiplying the target displacement scale by two multiplies the model time by four. This quadratic scaling is a useful way to see why local encounters and redistribution over an entire biological cell need not happen on the same timescale. A curved, bounded cell membrane, restricted paths and different mobile populations require more than this flat-patch model; disappearance of an initial pattern alone does not determine D.

Mouse-human heterokaryon membrane-mixing experiment

Words: 249
Lateral mobility of membrane proteins OurBigBook.com
In 1970 Larry Frye and Michael Edidin fused mouse C11D and human VA-2 cell lines with Sendai virus, forming heterokaryons. Suspensions were maintained in a shaking water bath at 37∘C. Removed aliquots were stained by indirect immunofluorescence: antibodies against parental surface antigens, then differently fluorescent secondary antibodies. A Leitz Ortholux fluorescence microscope used a mercury lamp and color-selecting filters. Green mouse and red human surface patterns became intermixed.[ref]
Same-species fusions tested antibody specificity; stable hybrid lines supplied a two-color control. Inhibitors of biological translation did not prevent mixing; lowering temperature inhibited it. These observations supported lateral redistribution over rapid synthesis. The authors could not conclusively exclude passage through cytoplasm and return. The readout compared populations, not trajectories of a continuously observed individual biological cell, and did not establish unrestricted motion of every membrane protein.[ref]
The decisive design idea is to begin with spatially distinguishable material. A uniformly labeled surface can look unchanged while its components move. Bringing together differently identifiable parental surfaces creates an initial pattern whose loss reveals redistribution; antibody specificity makes that loss interpretable. Membrane topology asks which side a part faces, whereas this experiment asks where it can move along that side.

Ion channel

Words: 603 Articles: 9
Cell membrane OurBigBook.com
An ion channel is a membrane protein that provides a selective pathway for ions across a cell membrane. An open pore permits passive transport down the relevant electrochemical gradient; it does not supply the free energy for uphill transport as a pump involved in active transport does. Two things are needed for a net electric current: an available pathway and an electrochemical potential difference. Opening a pore alone need not produce a net electric current. See Ion Channels and the Electrical Properties of Membranes.
The selective permeability of an ion channel depends on interactions between its pore and the passing ions, not simply on a hole's diameter. Some ion channels strongly favor potassium over sodium; others admit several kinds of positively charged ions. Thus selective does not necessarily mean exclusive to one ion species. See Purves et al., Neuroscience, 2nd edition (2001): molecular structure of ion channels and the mixed-channel example in Purves et al.: principles derived from studies of the neuromuscular junction.
Gating is the opening and closing of an ion channel's pathway. A voltage-gated ion channel responds to membrane potential; an ionotropic receptor can respond to a bound ligand. Gating changes the available electrical conductance, while the electrochemical gradient supplies the drive for passive transport. For a fixed reversal potential in the simple model below, opening more identical ion channels increases the magnitude of the electric current at a fixed nonzero drive, without choosing its sign independently of membrane potential. The two-state ion-channel gating model describes how the open fraction can change. See the Alberts reference above.
For a worked approximation, hold an open population's electrical conductance at g=10nS and its reversal potential at Erev​=−80mV. Use V=Vinside​−Voutside​ for membrane potential and inward-positive conventional current:
Iin​=g(Erev​−V),g≥0.
(3)
At V=−100,−80,−60mV, respectively, the electric currents are +200,0,−200pA, since 1nS×1mV=1pA. Positive electric current means net positive electric charge entering, or net negative electric charge leaving; it does not mean that every ion moves inward. For a pore passing only positive ions, the three results mean net inward passage, no net passage, and net outward passage. These stipulated values describe an illustrative Ohmic approximation, not measured properties of a particular ion channel; see reversal potential for the current plot and limitations.
The useful connection is between a molecular gate and a controllable electric current. A ligand can change electrical conductance through an ionotropic receptor, or membrane potential can change it through a voltage-gated ion channel, connecting recognition and electrical signaling. In a historical landmark, Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann reported currents from individual ion channels in frog muscle membranes in 1976. See Neher and Sakmann, Single-channel currents recorded from membrane of denervated frog muscle fibres, Nature 260, 799–802 (1976).

Membrane transport

Words: 3k Articles: 52
Cell membrane OurBigBook.com
Membrane transport is the passage of substances across a cell membrane. For the small solutes considered here, distinguish the available route from its driving force: a lipid bilayer, ion channel, or carrier protein determines how passage can occur, while differences in chemical potential and membrane potential determine which direction is energetically downhill. A large driving force cannot make an unavailable route conduct. See Alberts et al.: principles of membrane transport.
For an ideal dilute chemical solution at uniform temperature and pressure, with the same reference molar chemical potential on both sides, the molar electrochemical potential change for transferring a solute from compartment A to B is
Δμ​A→B​=RTln(cA​cB​​)+zF(VB​−VA​).
(4)
Here cA​,cB​ are concentrations, T is absolute temperature, R is the molar gas constant, z is signed ion charge number, F is the Faraday constant, and VB​−VA​ is the electric potential difference. The natural logarithm contains a dimensionless ratio. The electrical term vanishes for an uncharged solute. More generally, use thermodynamic activities in place of the ideal concentration ratio. This combines the ideal-solution chemical potential difference with electrical energy per mole; see IUPAC Gold Book: electrochemical potential. For a biological cell, taking B inside and A outside makes the voltage term use the existing membrane potential convention.

Membrane transport OurBigBook.com
Passive transport is net movement down the transported substance's electrochemical gradient without coupling that movement to another source of free energy. Facilitated diffusion is passive transport through an ion channel or a passive carrier protein. Providing such a route can speed passage, but does not turn an uphill transfer into a downhill one. For an uncharged solute under the ideal conditions above, net passive transport runs from higher to lower concentration; for an ion, the membrane potential also matters. See Cooper, The Cell: transport of small molecules.
In the figure, stipulate eight neutral solute tracers in A and two in B, with equal fixed volumes, identical surroundings, and no imposed electric potential difference. The concentration ratio is cA​/cB​=8/2=4, so A-to-B transfer has Δμ​=−RTln4≃−1.38629RT, or −3.458kJmol−1 at the assumed temperature T=300K. If this solute can cross, thermodynamic equilibrium has equal expected concentrations: five tracers per side on average. Individual crossings occur in both directions; an observed ten-particle snapshot need not contain exactly five on each side. These stipulated counts illustrate a driving force, not a measured flow rate or a prediction of the time to equilibrate.
For a separate illustrative ion calculation, keep cA​/cB​=4, but assign z=+1 and VA​−VB​=−60mV at 300K. Using R≃8.31446Jmol−1K−1 and F≃96485.33Cmol−1, B-to-A transfer gives RTln4−0.060F≃3.458−5.789=−2.331kJmol−1. Thus an appropriate open ion channel can permit passive transport toward the higher concentration: the electrical contribution outweighs the opposing chemical potential contribution. This stipulated membrane potential is not inferred from the tracer counts.

Facilitated diffusion

Words: 26
Passive transport OurBigBook.com
Facilitated diffusion is passive transport through a membrane transport protein, such as an ion channel or carrier protein, down the relevant electrochemical gradient. The protein supplies a route; it does not by itself provide free energy for uphill transfer.

Osmosis

Words: 2k Articles: 14
Passive transport OurBigBook.com
Osmosis is net movement of water across a boundary whose permeability differs for water and relevant solutes. The direction depends on the chemical potential of water, including both dissolved material and pressure. A cell wall can let water reach the cell membrane, yet oppose expansion through turgor pressure. Zero net movement does not mean individual water molecules have stopped crossing.
Imagine pure water on side A and a chemical solution on side B of a semipermeable membrane. At equal temperature and pressure, dissolved particles lower the chemical potential of water in B, and net water transfer initially favors A to B. Retaining the solutes lets water redistribute without simply mixing everything. Increasing B's pressure opposes entry; enough pressure gives thermodynamic equilibrium with unequal solute concentrations. This is why “water moves toward more solute” requires a pressure condition.[ref]
At thermodynamic equilibrium, the chemical potentials of exchangeable water are equal. Individual water molecules still move and cross in both directions, with equal mean opposing transfer rates and zero net flux. A stationary liquid level alone does not prove this: a blocked route can also give no visible transfer. Selective permeability provides a route; water potential supplies the direction, and neither alone determines the speed. The cell wall, turgor pressure and plasmolysis articles show why a living boundary's mechanical support and its selective permeability must be distinguished.
Water potential (Ψ)
Words: 266 Articles: 1
Osmosis OurBigBook.com
Water potential expresses the chemical potential of water relative to a stated pure-water reference in units of pressure. For a nearly incompressible liquid at fixed temperature, use a constant reference molar volume Vˉw​ and write
Ψ=Vˉw​μw​−μwref​​.
(5)
The reference is pure water at the same temperature and a chosen pressure P0​. Both sides must use the same reference. Neglecting height differences and interactions with solid surfaces, the approximation is
μw​≃μwref​+RTlnaw​+Vˉw​(P−P0​),Ψ≃(P−P0​)+Vˉw​RT​lnaw​=Ψp​+Ψs​.
(6)
Here aw​ is the dimensionless thermodynamic activity of water, R the molar gas constant, and T the absolute temperature. The natural logarithm is therefore applied to a dimensionless quantity. The pressure contribution Ψp​=P−P0​ increases with pressure; the solute potential Ψs​ is negative when aw​<1. Across an available passive water route, net water transfer favors higher to lower water potential. The familiar plant description adds effects of height and interactions with solid surfaces when they matter; they are omitted in the two liquid compartments here.[ref]
Adding solute and raising pressure can thus cancel in water potential while each contribution remains nonzero. Equality of water potential does not imply equality of pressure or of every solute concentration. In a supported plant cell, the cell wall permits a positive turgor pressure to balance a negative solute potential; plasmolysis illustrates the opposite loss of support after water leaves.
Solute potential (Ψs​)
Words: 64
Water potential OurBigBook.com
Solute potential is the contribution to water potential from dissolved material, with pure water assigned zero. In the nearly incompressible description, Ψs​=(RT/Vˉw​)lnaw​. In a dilute ideal chemical solution, Ψs​≃−RTcosm​, where cosm​ is the osmotic concentration of dissolved particles. Thus solute potential is negative while the corresponding osmotic pressure is positive: Ψs​≃−Π. This sign difference distinguishes a contribution to water potential from the compensating mechanical pressure.[ref]
Osmotic pressure (Π)
Words: 547 Articles: 3
Osmosis OurBigBook.com
Osmotic pressure is the excess pressure needed on a chemical solution to maintain osmotic thermodynamic equilibrium with pure solvent across a semipermeable membrane. For a nearly incompressible solvent, equality of chemical potential gives Π≃−(RT/Vˉw​)lnaw​. It is an equilibrium property; an observed initial swelling rate is a different measurement.[ref]
For a dilute ideal chemical solution of retained particles,
Π=VnRT​=RTcosm​.
(7)
Here n is the amount of substance of independently dissolved solute particles, measured in moles; V is the final volume of chemical solution, not the starting volume of pure solvent. Count the resulting ions separately if a solute dissociates; do not multiply twice if n already counts them. With cosm​ in molm−3 and R in Jmol−1K−1, RTcosm​ has units Jm−3=Pa. This resembles the ideal gas law, although the particles here are dissolved in a liquid.
The assumptions are uniform temperature, a dilute ideal chemical solution, a nearly incompressible solvent, and a semipermeable membrane that retains the counted solutes while passing solvent. Association, incomplete dissociation and interactions change the relation between added material and independent particles; nonideal thermodynamic activities invalidate simple particle counting. The formula does not describe a concentrated brine merely because the apparatus contains a membrane.
A short derivation explains why particles are counted. In an ideal mixture, aw​=xw​=nw​/(nw​+n), with nw​ the amount of substance of water. If n≪nw​, then −lnxw​=ln(1+n/nw​)≃n/nw​ and V≃nw​Vˉw​. Substitution gives Π≃RTn/V. This is a dilute-limit approximation, not a claim that water molecules stop moving or that dissolved particles are a gas.
Osmometry
Words: 77 Articles: 1
Osmotic pressure OurBigBook.com
Measure the pressure difference across a solvent-permeable barrier that retains the solute, at known temperature. For dilute polymers, the zero-concentration intercept of reduced pressure determines the number-average molar mass. Leakage, aggregation and ionic contributions can change the inferred molecular population.

Osmosis concentration-pressure example
Words: 232
Osmotic pressure OurBigBook.com
Take two reservoirs separated by a semipermeable membrane that passes water and retains one neutral solute. Stipulate T=300K, cA​=10molm−3 and cB​=30molm−3, equivalent to 10 and 30mmolL−1. Treat both chemical solutions as dilute and ideal. These are invented model conditions, not measurements or recommended biological conditions.
For pressures measured at the same height beside the membrane, define ΔP=PB​−PA​. The model predicts
ΨA​−ΨB​=RT(cB​−cA​)−ΔP,RT(cB​−cA​)≃49.89kPa.
(8)
At ΔP=0, net water transfer favors A to B. At ΔP≃49.89kPa, the opposing pressure balances the osmotic pressure difference, giving zero net transfer with unequal concentrations. At ΔP=75kPa, the sign reverses and net water transfer favors B to A, toward the more dilute reservoir. Raising pressure changes the total water potential, rather than changing the membrane into a one-way gate.
Fixed concentrations require large maintained reservoirs or an instantaneous comparison. In a closed membrane osmometer, entering water causes dilution of the receiving chemical solution and changes its volume; the final balance must use final concentrations. A supported cell wall provides another way to develop the opposing pressure, as explained under turgor pressure.
Membrane osmometer
Words: 356
Osmosis OurBigBook.com
A membrane osmometer connects osmosis to a visible liquid level or a measured balancing pressure. In a teaching version, a solute-containing pouch is joined to a narrow vertical tube and submerged in external water. Entry increases the enclosed volume and raises the meniscus. Both the column and any stretched pouch can contribute an opposing pressure.
The University of Toronto's Osmosis (Brine) by University of Toronto Department of Physics demonstration (activity Me-012b, Description, steps 1–14) specifies soaking a dialysis membrane tube, tying one end closed and fastening the other around a thistle tube's narrow end. A stand holds the tube. Colored brine is cooled to room temperature and injected using a syringe to reduce bubbles; the pouch is completely immersed in a 1 or 2L beaker of distilled water. A taped marker records the initial level; subsequent meniscus height is the readout. The protocol proposes another pouch containing distilled water as a comparison, and its older instructions explicitly check seals for leaks.[ref]
A rise is consistent with net water entry. To infer osmotic pressure quantitatively, use a retained solute and establish a stable equilibrium after accounting for dilution. If both free surfaces are at the same atmospheric pressure, the liquids have approximately equal mass densities ρ, and the pouch adds negligible mechanical load, the hydrostatic pressure difference at membrane height is approximately ΔP=ρgh, where h is the meniscus height above the external surface and g is gravitational acceleration. This is the liquid-column inference, not a pressure calibration supplied by the Toronto demonstration. For unequal mass densities, compare the two column pressures separately.
Useful additional controls are matched initial levels and geometry, constant temperature, replicate runs, and monitoring external solute to test retention. These are proposed checks, not reported Toronto results. Leaks, trapped bubbles, surface tension in the narrow tube, evaporation and membrane stretching can alter the level. In particular, a dialysis membrane may pass dissolved salt: the brine setup illustrates transient movement but cannot be assumed to retain every dissolved ion. Color improves visibility without proving salt retention. A stationary level can reflect weakening concentration differences or leakage rather than osmotic thermodynamic equilibrium; a rising level alone supplies neither n nor a verified value of nRT/V.
Osmotic concentration (cosm​)
Words: 134
Osmosis OurBigBook.com
In the dilute ideal particle-count description, osmolarity is the total amount of substance of independently dissolved solute particles per volume of chemical solution: cosm​=∑j​nj​/V. It includes both membrane-permeating and retained solutes; specifying a particular membrane is unnecessary to count particles. A neutral undissociated solute contributes one particle per molecule, whereas separated ions are counted individually. Common units are osmolL−1 or mOsmL−1, with one osmole denoting one mole of the counted particles. Osmolality uses mass of solvent instead of volume of chemical solution; the two numerical values should not be identified without the appropriate mass-to-volume conversion.[ref]
This ideal counting definition states its scope: interactions can require a thermodynamic activity description of osmotic pressure. Tonicity asks a different question, namely how that surrounding chemical solution changes a specified biological cell's volume through its particular cell membrane.
Tonicity
Words: 339 Articles: 4
Osmosis OurBigBook.com
Tonicity describes how a surrounding chemical solution changes cellular volume through osmosis. It depends on which solutes remain effectively excluded on the timescale of interest, not simply on the amount of dissolved material. The following comparisons take a functional cell membrane, an initially unpressurized biological cell, and effectively excluded solutes as the reference; rising turgor pressure can oppose entry in a walled biological cell.
Operationally, hypotonic solutions cause sustained swelling, hypertonic solutions sustained shrinking, and isotonic solutions no sustained volume change in the specified passive comparison. State the reference biological cell, membrane properties and observation interval. A permeating solute can transiently change water potential while crossing; after its chemical potential equilibrates, its initial unequal concentrations cannot be treated as a permanently retained osmotic load. Thus equal osmolarities need not imply isotonic solutions. Conversely, a stationary walled biological cell may have unequal osmolarities balanced by turgor pressure.[ref]
Permeating solute
Words: 147
Tonicity OurBigBook.com
A permeating solute can cross the specified membrane appreciably during the observation interval. This is a property of a solute-membrane-time comparison, not an immutable label attached to a molecule. Passage can reduce a concentration difference through passive transport while water is also moving; it does not require water to wait.
For an original dilute ideal example, stipulate a flexible model biological cell with 20mmolL−1 retained particles and an external reservoir with 10mmolL−1 retained particles plus 10mmolL−1 freely permeating neutral solute. Initially the total osmolarities are equal. Suppose the permeating solute eventually has equal internal and external concentrations, there is negligible pressure difference, no active transport, and the internal retained particle amount is conserved. The permeating contributions cancel from the final osmotic balance; the internal retained concentration must fall from 20 to 10mmolL−1. The required volume is twice its initial value. This illustrates an initially equal-osmolarity but hypotonic solution; it assumes the boundary can survive that expansion and predicts no universal time course.

Cytolysis
Words: 43
Osmosis OurBigBook.com
Osmotic lysis is rupture after excessive water entry and swelling. It is an outcome for susceptible biological cells, not an inevitable response of every biological cell to a hypotonic solution. Cell walls can bear the resulting load, and cellular mechanisms can regulate water balance. A protoplast challenge tests a particular preparation under particular conditions.

Active transport

Words: 226 Articles: 4
Membrane transport OurBigBook.com
Active transport moves a substance uphill in its electrochemical gradient by coupling the transfer to a downhill process. Some carrier proteins use ATP hydrolysis; others couple uphill transfer of one solute to downhill transfer of another solute, often an ion. The latter mechanism uses a stored electrochemical gradient and need not consume ATP directly at that carrier protein. Maintaining the driving electrochemical gradient still requires a source of energy. See Alberts et al.: carrier proteins and active membrane transport.
For the neutral 8:2 example, B-to-A transfer costs RTln4≃1.38629RT per mole. As an explicitly assumed bookkeeping example, suppose a tightly coupled cycle supplies a downhill free energy change of −2RT per mole of transferred solute. The total is then ΔGcycle​=RTln4−2RT≃−0.61371RT. This makes forward cycling energetically possible under those assumptions; it specifies neither a molecular mechanism nor a rate. Merely adding a carrier protein cannot supply the missing free energy. Conversely, as the transported concentration ratio rises, the required uphill cost rises, so a fixed driving process has a finite thermodynamic limit.

Exocytosis

Words: 37
Membrane transport OurBigBook.com
Exocytosis releases the contents of a membrane-bounded compartment to the extracellular space by fusion with the cell membrane. Synaptic vesicle fusion is an example. Its contents are released, while its membrane becomes connected to the cell membrane; see synaptic vesicle for recovery and reuse.

Endocytosis

Words: 126 Articles: 3
Membrane transport OurBigBook.com
Endocytosis takes material into a biological cell by inward budding and separation of part of its cell membrane. At a chemical synapse, it helps recover membrane and proteins after synaptic vesicle exocytosis. This recovery is distinct from uptake of individual neurotransmitter molecules through membrane transport proteins.

Synaptic vesicle recycling

Endocytosis OurBigBook.com
synaptic vesicle recycling retrieves and reuses membrane and components after synaptic release. Uptake, uncoating, sorting and preparation for subsequent release are distinct measurable steps.

clathrin-mediated endocytosis internalizes selected surface material through assembly of adaptors and a clathrin coat, membrane deformation, scission and subsequent coat release. Counts of coated puncta alone do not distinguish initiation, successful internalization and stalled intermediates.

Phagocytosis

Words: 39
Endocytosis OurBigBook.com
Phagocytosis encloses a large extracellular particle by extending and closing the cell membrane around it. It is one possible conceptual route for internalizing a symbiotic partner, but a modern phagocytic pathway should not be assumed to have existed before eukaryotic organization evolved.

Fluid mosaic model

Words: 86
Cell membrane OurBigBook.com
The fluid mosaic model describes a cell membrane as a fluid lipid bilayer with embedded membrane proteins. It connects a continuous barrier to a mosaic of functional components: diffusion within the membrane can coexist with selective passage across it. S. J. Singer and G. L. Nicolson presented the fluid mosaic model in Science in 1972; see Singer and Nicolson, The fluid mosaic model of the structure of cell membranes. The useful picture does not require every protein to move freely: attachments and interactions can restrict movement, as discussed in Cooper, The Cell: structure of the plasma membrane.
