Focal ratio (F=f/D)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Optical instrument
OurBigBook.com Words: 512
The focal ratio, or f-number, of a converging optical instrument focused at infinity is the dimensionless quantity
N=F=Df​,
(1)
where f>0 is effective focal length and D is the on-axis entrance pupil diameter. The notation f/4 means N=4, not f=4 in a particular unit. A 100mm focal length with D=25mm gives f/4. Closing the aperture stop until D=12.5mm gives f/8 while retaining the same focal length. The mechanical opening need not equal D. [ref].
For a thin lens in air with a circular aperture stop at the thin lens, the converging bundle from a distant on-axis point has half-angle α satisfying
tanα=2fD​=2N1​,α≃2N1​in radians.
(2)
The last step uses the small-angle approximation. For a compound optical lens, the cone is set by the image-side optical pupil and its distance from the receiving plane; the simple triangle is not an exact high-angle rule. At finite object distance a thin lens instead gives v=f(1+∣m∣), by its imaging equation and transverse magnification, so its working cone corresponds to v/D=N(1+∣m∣). Nominal focal ratio therefore does not alone specify close-range image irradiance. [ref].
Two useful scalings follow under stated conditions. For a fixed uniform incident beam and unchanged transmission, collected radiant flux scales as D2. For an extended source with fixed radiance, the central image irradiance scales approximately as N−2 in the paraxial approximation: the solid angle of the cone scales as (D/f)2. For an ideal uniformly illuminated unobscured circular entrance pupil, the first dark ring of the point spread function has angular radius and focal plane radius
θ1​≃1.22Dλ​,r1​≃fθ1​=1.22λN.
(3)
Here λ is wavelength. These are first-zero radii, not diameters or a universal rule for resolving arbitrary objects. A proof outline evaluates the circular aperture's Fraunhofer diffraction using its Fourier transform, then uses the first zero of the resulting Bessel function. [ref].
At λ=0.55μm, the example's f/4 and f/8 first-ring radii are 2.684 and 5.368μm. Closing the aperture stop halves D, quarters collected radiant flux and doubles this diffraction radius. It can also reduce optical aberrations by excluding outer light rays; actual sharpness reflects both effects. This explains why a smaller aperture need not produce a sharper image. [ref].
A proposed optical bench check puts a fixed collimator before a converging thin lens and a photodetector large enough to collect the entire image behind it. Compare two centred aperture stops of known diameters while fixing source output, detector gain and integration time. The integrated signal tests the D2 collection law; a spatially resolved optical camera at the focal plane can separately measure the point spread function. Repeat a dark reading and the first aperture stop, and check that neither the incoming beam nor detector clips either bundle. A resolved pinhole source, imperfect collimation, optical aberrations and detector pixel size can dominate the apparent spot width; a single peak reading does not measure total collected radiant flux.
