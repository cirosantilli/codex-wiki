Fano's inequality

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Information theory Conditional entropy
OurBigBook.com Words: 865 Articles: 2
Fano's inequality turns remaining uncertainty into a constraint on decision errors. If an observation leaves many plausible answers, a decoder cannot usually recover the answer reliably. Its elegant idea is to separate two uncertainties: whether the guess was wrong, and which alternative was correct when it was wrong.
Let X,Y be discrete random variables, with X taking values in a finite alphabet X of cardinality M≥2. Choose any deterministic decoder X=f(Y) whose output lies in X, and define its average error probability pe​=P{X=X}. With base-two logarithms, Fano's inequality states
H(X∣Y)≤h2​(pe​)+pe​log2​(M−1),0≤pe​≤1.
(13)
Here H(X∣Y) is conditional entropy and h2​ is binary entropy, with 0log2​0=0 at the endpoints. Both sides are measured in bits; using natural logarithms throughout gives the same inequality in nats. No uniform source, independence of X,Y, or optimality of the decoder is assumed. Requiring its output to belong to the same alphabet is what leaves at most M−1 alternatives after an error.[ref]
For an exact toy example, give X the discrete uniform distribution on {A,B,C,D}. A channel reports Y=X with probability 1−q and reports each of the other three symbols with probability q/3, where 0≤q≤1. Use the decoder f(y)=y. The output is uniform, and the conditional probabilities after any report y are
P(X=x∣Y=y)={1−q,q/3,​x=y,x=y.​
(14)
Thus pe​=q and direct calculation of the conditional entropy gives
H(X∣Y)=−(1−q)log2​(1−q)−33q​log2​3q​=h2​(q)+qlog2​3.
(15)
This is equality in Fano's inequality for every q∈[0,1]. At q=0.3, each report leaves probabilities (0.7,0.1,0.1,0.1), up to their order, and H(X∣Y)≈1.3568 bits. The two contributions are h2​(0.3)≈0.8813 bits for the error flag and 0.3log2​3≈0.4755 bits for identifying the correct alternative after an error. These are theoretical probabilities, not measured data.
The identity decoder in this example minimizes error only for 0≤q≤3/4: its guessed symbol has the largest posterior probability exactly when 1−q≥q/3. At q=3/4, X,Y are independent random variables and all four guesses tie. For q>3/4, guessing any one of the three other symbols has error probability 1−q/3<q. Equality in Fano's inequality therefore does not certify an optimal decoder.
For usefulness, combine h2​(pe​)≤1 with log2​(M−1)≤log2​M to obtain the simpler consequence
pe​≥max{0,log2​MH(X∣Y)−1​}.
(16)
For a uniform X, H(X∣Y)=log2​M−I(X;Y), so this becomes pe​≥max{0,1−(I(X;Y)+1)/log2​M}. This connects mutual information to unavoidable decision error in channel coding or classification in statistical learning; it can rule out reliable recovery regardless of the chosen decoder.[ref]
The sharper right-hand side FM​(p)=h2​(p)+plog2​(M−1) increases from zero to log2​M on 0≤p≤1−1/M, then decreases: in the interior, FM′​(p)=log2​((M−1)(1−p)/p). When interpreting the inequality by inverting a curve, use its increasing branch to obtain a lower error threshold. A decoder that chooses a largest posterior probability, as in maximum a posteriori decoding, always has pe​≤1−1/M, since the largest of M conditional probabilities is at least 1/M. Arbitrary decoders can have larger error. In particular, low conditional entropy does not force every decoder to work well: with a fair binary X and Y=X, the rule f(Y)=1−Y has pe​=1 even though H(X∣Y)=0. Fano's inequality supplies a constraint, not an exact error prediction for each rule.
