Let A,B,C represent a,b,c. Since c is central, C commutes with A and B. Over the complex number field C, C has an eigenvalue γ, and its corresponding eigenspace is invariant under all three operators. The irreducibility of V therefore makes this eigenspace all of V, so C=γI. Taking the trace of
C=[A,B]=AB−BA
(2)
gives (dimV)γ=0 by the cyclic property of the trace; hence C=0.
The remaining operators A and B commute. Two commuting operators on a nonzero finite-dimensional complex vector space have a common eigenvector, whose span is invariant. Irreducibility therefore forces dimV=1. Conversely, every pair (α,β)∈C2 defines a one-dimensional irreducible representation by
a⟼α,b⟼β,c⟼0.
(3)
These are all the finite-dimensional irreducible representations.

For a finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation ρ on V, the Trace form of a Lie algebra representation is
(x,y)V​=tr(ρ(x)ρ(y)).
(4)
Write again A=ρ(a), B=ρ(b), and C=ρ(c)=[A,B]. The operator C commutes with both A and B. Direct use of the cyclic property of the trace gives
tr(CA)tr(CB)tr(C2)​=tr(ABA−BA2)=0,=tr(AB2−BAB)=0,=tr(ABC−BAC)=0.​
(5)
In the last line, cyclicity and AC=CA turn tr(ABC) into tr(BAC). Thus the nonzero vector c∈g is orthogonal to the basis a,b,c, and hence to all of g. The bilinear form is therefore degenerate.

Use the Polynomial representation of the Heisenberg Lie algebra on the infinite-dimensional polynomial ring C[x]:
a⋅f=f′,b⋅f=xf,c⋅f=f.
(6)
The product rule gives [d/dx,x]=1, so this is a Lie algebra representation. It is a Faithful Lie algebra representation: if α(d/dx)+βx+γ is the zero operator, applying it first to 1 gives βx+γ=0, and then applying the remaining operator to x gives α=0.
To prove irreducibility, let W be a nonzero invariant subspace and choose a nonzero polynomial in W of least degree. If its degree were positive, repeated differentiation would produce a nonzero element of smaller degree, so W contains a nonzero constant. Invariance under multiplication by x then puts every monomial xn in W, and hence W=C[x].

The weights of Mλ​ lie below λ in the positive-root order, and every weight space is finite-dimensional. A nonzero submodule V is stable under the Cartan subalgebra, so it is a direct sum of its weight spaces. Choose a maximal weight μ occurring in V. Every positive-root operator would raise its weight; maximality therefore makes it kill any nonzero v∈Vμ​. Thus v is a singular vector.
The Casimir element is central and acts throughout Mλ​ by
∣λ+ρ∣2−∣ρ∣2.
(9)
The same element acts on the highest-weight vector v of weight μ by
∣μ+ρ∣2−∣ρ∣2.
(10)
Both are the action of one operator on the same module, so the scalars agree and
∣μ+ρ∣=∣λ+ρ∣.​
(11)

For the sl2 Lie algebra, let vλ​ be the highest-weight vector. The Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem gives the basis (frvλ​)r≥0​, and the defining Lie brackets imply
efrvλ​=r(λ−r+1)fr−1vλ​.
(12)
A positive-degree basis vector is singular exactly when r=λ+1 is a positive integer. Therefore Mλ​ is irreducible when λ∈/Z≥0​.
If λ=m∈Z≥0​, the vector fm+1vλ​ has weight −m−2 and generates a submodule isomorphic to M−m−2​. The latter is irreducible because −m−2∈/Z≥0​. Every nonzero submodule contains a singular vector by the preceding part, and the displayed coefficient shows that this is the only possible proper singular vector. Hence
U(sl2​)fm+1vλ​≅M−m−2​
(13)
is the unique proper nonzero submodule, as summarized by the Reducibility of an sl2 Verma module.
