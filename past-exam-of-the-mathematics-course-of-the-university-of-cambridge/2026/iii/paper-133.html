The based Schreier coset graph has vertices ⟨a⟩g and an s-edge from ⟨a⟩g to ⟨a⟩gs. At the base vertex ⟨a⟩ there is an a-loop. There are no other cycles: the fundamental group of the covering is the subgroup ⟨a⟩≅Z, and its displayed loop already generates it.
Equivalently, start with one a-circle at the base vertex and attach labelled trees so that every vertex has exactly one incoming and one outgoing a-edge and exactly one incoming and one outgoing b-edge. After deleting the base a-loop, the underlying graph is a tree. This describes the complete infinite covering and distinguishes it from its finite core, which is just the a-loop.

b OurBigBook.com
Whenever a combinatorial loop α traverses an oriented edge and immediately traverses the same edge backwards, delete that backtracking pair. Each deletion is a homotopy relative to endpoints inside Z and reduces the edge length by two, so the process terminates at a reduced, hence locally injective, combinatorial loop β.
The universal cover Y of a connected graph is a tree. The lift β​ is also locally injective because a covering map is a local graph isomorphism. A locally injective edge path in a tree cannot repeat a vertex: the segment between two successive visits would be a nonempty reduced closed path, whereas every closed path in a tree backtracks. Thus β​ is injective unless β is constant.
Now let a loop in Z become null-homotopic in Y. Its reduced representative β lifts to a closed path in Y. The preceding injectivity forces that lift, and hence β, to be constant. The original loop is null-homotopic in Z, proving that
π1​(Z,y0​)⟶π1​(Y,y0​)
(5)
is an injective group homomorphism.

Choose a finite generating set h1​,…,hr​ for H. Under the standard classification of connected covering spaces, each hi​ is represented by a based combinatorial loop γi​ in Y. Let Z be the union of the images of these finitely many finite edge paths. Then Z is a finite connected subgraph containing y0​.
Part (b) makes the inclusion-induced map
π1​(Z,y0​)⟶π1​(Y,y0​)=H
(6)
injective. Its image contains every hi​, because every γi​ lies in Z, and therefore contains the subgroup they generate, namely all of H. The map is consequently an isomorphism.

Let f:X→T be a (λ,ε)-quasi-isometry to a tree. The image under f of every geodesic segment in X is a (λ,ε)-quasigeodesic in T. By the Morse lemma for quasi-geodesics, it lies within a constant R=R(λ,ε) of the tree geodesic with the same endpoints.
Consider a geodesic triangle in X. A point p on one side maps within R of the corresponding side of the comparison triangle in T. Every geodesic triangle in a tree is 0-thin, so that comparison side is contained in the other two sides. Those two tree sides are in turn within R of the images of the other two sides of the original triangle. Hence some point q on one of those sides satisfies
dT​(f(p),f(q))≤2R.
(36)
The lower quasi-isometry inequality gives
dX​(p,q)≤λ(2R+ε).
(37)
Thus X is Gromov-hyperbolic metric space with δ=λ(2R+ε).

Retain the (λ,ε)-quasi-isometry f:X→T and the Morse lemma for quasi-geodesics constant R. Given x,y∈X, let m be the midpoint of a geodesic [x,y]. There is a point z on the tree geodesic [f(x),f(y)] with
dT​(f(m),z)≤R.
(38)
For any continuous path α from x to y, choose a partition fine enough that consecutive α(ti​) are at distance at most one. Consecutive images under f are then at distance at most
D=λ+ε.
(39)
Removing z separates f(x) from f(y) in the tree along their geodesic, so this finite D-chain must contain an element within D of z. For the corresponding ti​,
dT​(f(m),f(α(ti​)))≤R+D.
(40)
The lower quasi-isometry inequality yields
dX​(m,α(ti​))≤λ(R+D+ε).
(41)
This constant depends only on the chosen quasi-isometry, so every quasi-tree has the bottleneck property.

The hyperbolic plane H2 is a geodesic Gromov-hyperbolic metric space for some universal constant δ0​. It is not a quasi-tree. Indeed, for every C>0, choose two points on opposite sides of a large closed metric ball centred at the midpoint of their joining geodesic. The complement of that ball in H2 is path connected, so the endpoints can be joined by a continuous path that stays more than C from the midpoint. Thus H2 fails the bottleneck property, whereas part (b) shows that every quasi-tree satisfies it.
