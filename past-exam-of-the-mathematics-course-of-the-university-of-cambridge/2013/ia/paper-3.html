Lagrange's theorem states that for a subgroup L of a finite group G, ∣G∣=[G:L]∣L∣. In particular ∣L∣ divides ∣G∣; the equal-sized cosets partition G.
Apply the theorem to H∩K inside both subgroups. Its order divides both coprime orders, so H∩K={1}. For h∈H and k∈K, the group commutator hkh−1k−1 lies in K by K being a normal subgroup, and in H because kh−1k−1∈H. It is therefore the identity. Thus every element of H commutes with every element of K.
Define θ:H×K→G by θ(h,k)=hk. The cross-commutation just proved gives θ(h,k)θ(h′,k′)=hh′kk′=θ(hh′,kk′), so this is a group homomorphism from the direct product of groups. The product assumption makes it surjective. If hk=1, then h=k−1∈H∩K, so both entries are the identity and the kernel of a group homomorphism is trivial. Hence
G≅H×K,(h,k)⟼hk.​
(1)
This proves the internal direct product theorem in the present coprime-order setting, rather than merely matching the numbers of elements.

A cyclic group has one generator of a group: G=⟨a⟩={aj:j∈Z}. An abelian group has xy=yx for every pair of elements. Powers of one element commute because aras=ar+s=as+r=asar, so every cyclic group is Abelian. The Klein four-group C2​×C2​ is Abelian but is not a cyclic group: every nonidentity element has order two, whereas a generator of a group for a four-element cyclic group would have order four.
Fix a generator of a group x of Cn​. A group homomorphism is determined by g=ϕ(x) because ϕ(xj)=gj, and the relation xn=1 forces gn=1. Conversely, such a g defines ϕ(xj)=gj: exponents differing by a multiple of n give the same value, and addition of exponents verifies the group homomorphism law. Thus the homomorphism from a finite cyclic group correspondence is
Hom(Cn​,G) ⟷ {g∈G:gn=1}.​
(2)
For S4​, the order of a permutation is the least common multiple of its disjoint cycle lengths. The condition σ4=1 allows identity, transpositions, two disjoint transpositions, and four-cycles. The sixteen homomorphisms are ϕσ​(xj)=σj, with the full list of possible generator images
1;(12),(13),(14),(23),(24),(34);(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23);(1234),(1243),(1324),(1342),(1423),(1432).​​
(3)
The remaining eight elements of the symmetric group are three-cycles and do not qualify.

For a continuously differentiable vector field on all of R3, the necessary and sufficient condition for a conservative vector field is ∇×F=0. Necessity follows from equality of mixed partial derivatives of a potential; sufficiency uses the R3 being a simply connected space. On a general domain the topology cannot be omitted.
Here the relevant mixed partial derivatives are
∂y​Fz​=∂z​Fy​=2yez,∂z​Fx​=∂x​Fz​=−6z2,∂x​Fy​=∂y​Fx​=−2xsiny.
(7)
Thus the curl vanishes. Integrating the first component in x gives Φ=x2cosy−2xz3+A(y,z). Matching the second component gives Ay​=3+2yez, hence A=3y+y2ez+B(z). Matching the last component forces B′=0. A potential of a conservative vector field with the convention F=∇Φ is consequently
Φ=x2cosy−2xz3+3y+y2ez+C.​
(8)
If a physical potential is defined instead through F=−∇V, it is V=−Φ.

Place the cube's vertices at (±1,±1,±1). Its full symmetry group of a cube consists of signed permutation matrices. To see completeness, a cube symmetry fixes its center of a group and permutes the six face normals, so it sends coordinate axes to signed coordinate axes; conversely every such matrix preserves the cube. Coordinate permutations and sign changes take any edge to any other edge, so this is a transitive group action on edges.
Choose the edge e={(1,1,z):−1≤z≤1}. A symmetry stabilizing it must preserve its axis direction and its midpoint (1,1,0). It can swap the first two coordinates and independently reverse the third coordinate, but cannot change the signs of the two fixed coordinates. Hence
StabH​(e)=⟨(x,y,z)↦(y,x,z), (x,y,z)↦(x,y,−z)⟩≅C2​×C2​.​
(10)
There are twelve edges and four elements of the stabilizer subgroup. The orbit-stabiliser theorem therefore gives ∣H∣=48, including orientation-reversing symmetries, not just the twenty-four rotations.
The action defines a group homomorphism H→S12​. If an element fixes every edge as a set, it fixes every vertex, since each vertex is the unique common point of its three incident edges. A cube isometry fixing all vertices is the identity. Thus this is a faithful group action, and the cube symmetry action on edges embeds H as a subgroup with
[S12​:H]=4812!​=9979200.​
(11)
It is not a normal subgroup. Central inversion x↦−x belongs to H and swaps the twelve edges in six opposite pairs. In S12​, all permutations of cycle type 26 are conjugate, and their number is
266!12!​=10395>48.
(12)
If H were a normal subgroup, it would contain this entire conjugacy class, impossible for a group of order forty-eight. This avoids relying on a classification of normal subgroups of the symmetric group.

Write the matrix as M(a,b,x). Direct multiplication and inversion give
M(a,b,x)M(a′,b′,x′)=M(a+a′,b+b′,x+x′+ab′),M(a,b,x)−1=M(−a,−b,ab−x).
(16)
The identity is M(0,0,0) and all determinants are one, so these formulas verify the subgroup criterion inside GL3​(R). This is the real Heisenberg group.
The map M(a,b,x)↦a is a surjective group homomorphism to the additive group of real numbers; its kernel is exactly H. Hence H is normal and the first isomorphism theorem gives
G/H≅(R,+).​
(17)
Two matrices commute exactly when ab′=a′b. Requiring that relation for every a′,b′ forces a=b=0, while x is unrestricted. Therefore
Z(G)={M(0,0,x):x∈R}.​
(18)
Finally M(a,b,x)↦(a,b) is a surjective group homomorphism to the additive group R2 with precisely this kernel of a group homomorphism, giving G/Z(G)≅(R2,+). The central coordinate records noncommutativity: the group commutator is M(0,0,ab′−a′b).

Use the dihedral group presentation G=⟨r,s:r2n=s2=1, srs=r−1⟩. Every element is rj or srj. A nonidentity rotation has order two only when its exponent is n, while (srj)2=1 for every j. Thus the complete list is
rn,srj (0≤j<2n).​
(19)
The half-turn rn is central, so its conjugacy class is the singleton {rn} and its normal closure is {1,rn}.
For reflections, rk(srj)r−k=srj−2k and s(srj)s−1=sr−j. Hence the conjugacy class is exactly the n reflections whose exponents have the same parity as j:
{srj+2k:0≤k<n}.​
(20)
The subgroup generated by this class contains r2, as the product of two consecutive listed reflections, and contains srj. Conversely those two generators contain every listed reflection. Their subgroup is invariant under conjugation by both r and s, so the smallest normal subgroup containing srj is
⟨r2,srj⟩={r2k,srj+2k:0≤k<n},​
(21)
of order 2n and index two. This involutions in an even dihedral group classification distinguishes the two reflection classes from the central half-turn.

If one subgroup contains the other, their union is the larger subgroup. Conversely, if neither contains the other, choose h∈H∖K and k∈K∖H. If the union were a subgroup it would contain hk. If hk∈H, then k=h−1(hk)∈H, a contradiction; if hk∈K, then h=(hk)k−1∈K, again a contradiction. Thus
H∪K is a subgroup ⟺ H⊆K or K⊆H.​
(22)

For a nontrivial finite p-group G of order pm with m≥1, partition into conjugacy classes. A noncentral class has size [G:CG​(x)], a positive power of p larger than one by Lagrange's theorem. The class equation therefore says
∣G∣=∣Z(G)∣+∑noncentral classes​[G:CG​(x)],∣Z(G)∣≡0(modp).
(24)
The center of a group contains the identity, so ∣Z(G)∣≥p, proving its nontriviality. The positive-exponent qualification matters: the one-element group has no nonidentity central element.
If ∣G∣=p2, its center of a group has order p or p2. In the first case, the quotient group G/Z(G) has prime order and is cyclic. Whenever a central quotient group is cyclic, writing all elements as giz with z central shows that they commute: (giz)(gjw)=gi+jzw=(gjw)(giz). Thus that case would already make G Abelian and its center of a group all of G, a contradiction. Hence every group of order p2 is Abelian.
If there is an element of order p2, it is a generator of a group for G, giving Cp2​. Otherwise every nonidentity element has order p. Pick a=1 and b∈/⟨a⟩. Their cyclic subgroups have trivial intersection, and they commute; the p2 distinct products aibj exhaust G. Hence the classification of groups of order p squared is
G≅Cp2​orG≅Cp​×Cp​.​
(25)
Both groups exist and are nonisomorphic, since only the first has an element of order p2.

For a Cartesian change of basis represented by an orthogonal matrix Q, vectors transform as Ei′​=Qia​Ea​ and Bi′​=Qia​Ba​. Their squared norms are invariant and Qia​Qjb​δab​=δij​. Substitution into the given expression therefore gives

the transformation law of a Cartesian second-rank tensor. The magnetic field's extra axial sign under an improper physical reflection, if included, appears twice and cancels in its quadratic contribution.

Under the usual bounded-domain hypotheses, with piecewise smooth boundary and a sufficiently smooth vector field, the divergence theorem states
∫Ω​∇⋅FdV=∫∂Ω​F⋅ndS,​
(37)
where n is outward. Apply it to gF and use the product rule ∇⋅(gF)=F⋅∇g+g∇⋅F to obtain
∫Ω​(F⋅∇g+g∇⋅F)dV=∫∂Ω​gF⋅ndS.​
(38)
For uniqueness, let w=u1​−u2​ for two classical solutions with the same Dirichlet boundary data. Then Δw=0 and w=0 on the boundary. Taking F=∇w, g=w gives Green's first identity in the form
∫Ω​∣∇w∣2dV=∫∂Ω​w∂n​wdS−∫Ω​wΔwdV=0.
(39)
The continuous nonnegative integrand must vanish, so w is constant on each connected component. The zero boundary data make every such constant zero. Thus the solution of the Dirichlet problem for the Laplace equation is unique, if it exists. This establishes Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem; existence is assumed. Boundedness, or suitable conditions at infinity, is needed: on an unbounded half-space the harmonic function w=z has zero boundary values but is not identically zero.
