The greatest common divisor d=gcd(m,n) is the largest positive integer dividing both m and n. To prove Bézout's identity without assuming a factorization theorem, consider the nonempty set of positive integer linear combinations
L={am+bn>0:a,b∈Z}.
(1)
It contains m, so the well-ordering principle gives a least element d0​=am+bn. By Euclidean division, write m=qd0​+r with 0≤r<d0​. Then
r=(1−qa)m−qbn
(2)
is another integer linear combination. If r>0, it contradicts minimality, so r=0 and d0​∣m. Applying the same argument to n gives d0​∣n. Conversely, every common divisor divides every integer linear combination and therefore divides d0​. In particular every positive common divisor is at most d0​. Thus d0​=d and
gcd(m,n)=am+bnfor some a,b∈Z.​
(3)
This also proves the requested divisibility implication for any integer k: if k∣m and k∣n, then k∣(am+bn)=d.
For the scaling identity for greatest common divisors, put D=gcd(km,kn) with k>0. Since kd divides km and kn, D≥kd. On the other hand, multiplying Bézout's identity by k expresses kd as a(km)+b(kn), so D∣kd. Positivity gives D≤kd. Therefore without using prime factorization,
gcd(km,kn)=kgcd(m,n).​

A real sequence converges to a finite limit L if
∀ε>0 ∃N ∀n≥N:∣xn​−L∣<ε.
(5)
A series converges when its partial sums sN​=∑n=1N​xn​ form a convergent sequence with a finite limit. These are different conditions: a series concerns accumulated terms, whereas sequence convergence concerns the terms themselves.
If sN​→S, then sN−1​→S as well and xN​=sN​−sN−1​→0. Explicitly, choose N large enough that both partial sums lie within ε/2 of S; the triangle inequality gives ∣xN​∣<ε. Hence convergence of a series forces its terms to tend to zero.

A function is injective when f(x)=f(x′) implies x=x′. It is surjective when every element of its codomain equals f(x) for at least one element of its domain.
If g∘f is injective, then f(x)=f(x′) implies
(g∘f)(x)=(g∘f)(x′),
(17)
hence x=x′. Thus injectivity of the composition implies injectivity of the first map.
If g∘f is surjective, then for every z∈Z there is an x∈X with z=g(f(x)). Taking y=f(x) proves that z lies in the image of g. Thus surjectivity of the composition implies surjectivity of the second map. The arguments include empty domains and codomains whenever the specified functions exist.

For the cardinality of integer-periodic function spaces, fix a positive period n first. A periodic function Z→Z with that period is completely and uniquely determined by its n values at 0,…,n−1, so this class is in bijection with Zn. The integers are a countable set, and repeated pairing shows that a finite Cartesian product of countable sets is countable. Taking the union over positive n and using the countable union of countable sets proves that the integer-domain periodic functions form a countable set.
For the rational domain, even period one leaves infinitely many independent choices. Let qk​=1/(k+2) for k∈N0​; these are distinct members of Q∩[0,1). For every subset A⊆N0​, define
fA​(q)={1,0,​q−⌊q⌋=qk​ for some k∈A,otherwise.​
(33)
The fractional part is unchanged by adding one, so fA​ is an integer-valued periodic function on Q. Distinct subsets give distinct functions because fA​(qk​) records whether k∈A. Thus the uncountable power set of N0​ injects into these functions. The rational-domain periodic functions form an uncountable set. The difference is that Z has only finitely many residue classes modulo a fixed integer period, whereas Q has infinitely many.

Use the parabola obstruction to countable line coverings. The curve
P={(t,t2):t∈R}
(34)
is in bijection with the real line and hence is an uncountable set. A vertical line meets P in one point. A nonvertical line y=ax+b meets it only where t2−at−b=0, so it meets P in at most two points.
If countably many lines covered the plane, their intersections with P would cover P. That would express P as a countable union of countable sets, indeed of sets with at most two elements, contradicting its uncountability. Therefore no countable collection of lines covers the plane.

For Fermat's little theorem, first suppose p∤x. Multiplication by x permutes the nonzero residue classes modulo p: if xa≡xb(modp), the prime cannot divide x, so cancellation gives a≡b(modp). Multiplying the representatives in this permutation,
xp−1(p−1)!≡(p−1)!(modp).
(35)
Every factor of the factorial is nonzero modulo the prime, so the factorial has a modular inverse and can be cancelled. Thus xp−1≡1(modp) and xp≡x(modp). If p∣x, both sides are zero modulo p. Hence for every integer x,
xp≡x(modp).​
(36)
For completeness, the cancellation used above follows from Bézout's identity: a number relatively prime to p has an inverse modulo p.

P′=chν′​(1,cosθ′,sinθ′).​

ν=γν′(1+βcosθ′).​
(75)

cosθ=1+βcosθ′cosθ′+β​,sinθ=γ(1+βcosθ′)sinθ′​.​
