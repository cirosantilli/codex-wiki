For the normalized second-order equation, an ordinary point is one where both coefficient functions p,q are analytic. A singular point of a second-order linear ODE is a point that is not ordinary. It is a regular singular point if (x−x0​)p(x) and (x−x0​)2q(x) extend analytically to x0​; a singular point failing that condition is irregular.
Here p=0, q=1/x. Thus zero is singular but regular, because xp=0 and x2q=x are analytic. Write the analytic solution as y=∑n≥0​an​xn. Its constant equation gives a0​=0, and for n≥1,
n(n+1)an+1​+an​=0.

The factorial denominators show convergence for every finite x.
For the other branch, integrating the first correction equation twice and imposing its two conditions gives y1′​=−logx and
y1​=x−xlogx​.
(14)
Then y2′′​=logx−1, so y2′​=xlogx−2x+2 and

Expand the function into separated terms: f=g(x)+h(y), where g=x2−x4 and h=y8−y4. Then
fx​=2x(1−2x2),fy​=4y3(2y4−1).
(17)
Put a=2−1/2 and b=2−1/4. The nine critical points are all pairs from x∈{0,±a} and y∈{0,±b}. The diagonal Hessian matrix has entries 2−12x2 and 56y6−12y2.
At (0,±b) both entries are positive, giving strict local minima of value −1/4. At (±a,±b) they have opposite signs, giving four saddle points of value zero. At the three points on y=0 the Hessian is degenerate, so its sign alone does not classify them. Use the higher-order test for separated extrema: h(y)=−y4+O(y8) is negative for small nonzero y. At (0,0) the positive x2 term and negative y4 term give a saddle. At (±a,0), g−1/4=−(x2−1/2)2, so every sufficiently small nonzero displacement decreases f and these are strict local maxima.
There are two maxima at (±a,0), two minima at (0,±b), and five saddle points: (0,0) and (±a,±b).
For the level curves, the exact zero set consists of the two parabolas x=±y2 and the oval x2+y4=1. They cross at the four nondegenerate saddle points. Small positive levels enclose each maximum; small negative levels enclose each minimum. The degenerate central saddle has quartic narrowing, locally x2−y4=c. Far along the x axis the function is negative, while far along the y axis it is positive. These signs and zero curves determine the connectivity shown in the sketch.

G′(1)=E[X]=μ,G′′(1)=E[X(X−1)]=σ2+μ2−μ​.

In the Galton-Watson process, condition on Xn​=m. The next generation is a sum of m independent offspring counts, and so its conditional generating function is G(t)m. Averaging over Xn​ gives
Gn+1​(t)=E[G(t)Xn​]=Gn​(G(t))​.

The conditional mean is mμ and the conditional variance is mσ2. Thus iterated expectation gives E[Xn​]=μn from one ancestor, and the law of total variance gives vn+1​=μ2vn​+σ2μn, v0​=0. Iterating yields the branching-process generation moments

Let B mean that the selected coin is biased and F that it is fair. Both have prior probability 1/2. Conditional on either coin, tosses are independent. For the observed head count, the two binomial distribution likelihoods are (kn​)pk(1−p)n−k and (kn​)2−n. Bayes' theorem cancels the common coefficient and equal prior weights, giving
P(B∣k heads in n tosses)=pk(1−p)n−k+2−npk(1−p)n−k​​.
(37)
This applies for 0≤k≤n, including endpoint coin biases with zero-power factors interpreted by the corresponding likelihood. The fair-coin likelihood is positive, so the denominator cannot vanish.

For a finite stopping time n with target k≥1, necessarily n≥k. The last toss must be a head and the preceding n−1 tosses must contain k−1 heads. The negative binomial stopping argument therefore gives likelihood (k−1n−1​)pk(1−p)n−k for the biased coin, and (k−1n−1​)2−n for the fair coin. Applying Bayes' theorem again yields
P(B∣the kth head occurs at toss n)=pk(1−p)n−k+2−npk(1−p)n−k​​.
(38)
The two experiments have different combinatorial coefficients, but each coefficient is independent of the coin parameter. Thus posterior equality for proportional likelihoods explains their identical posterior formula. If p=0, a finite positive-head stopping observation has zero likelihood under the biased coin, consistently giving posterior zero. If the target is k=0, the experiment stops at n=0 without a toss and the posterior remains 1/2; a positive stopping time would then be impossible.
