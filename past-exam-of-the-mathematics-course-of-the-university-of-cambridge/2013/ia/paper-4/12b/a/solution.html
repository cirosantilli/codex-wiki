OurBigBook.com Words: 177
Use four-momentum with components (E/c,px​,py​), suppressing the identically zero third spatial component. The photon in the primed frame has
P′=chν′​(1,cosθ′,sinθ′).​
(73)
Its energy is hν′ and its spatial momentum has magnitude hν′/c. Apply the same Lorentz transformation as for the spacetime coordinates. With β=v/c,
P=chν′​(γ(1+βcosθ′), γ(cosθ′+β), sinθ′).
(74)
The time component gives the relativistic Doppler effect
ν=γν′(1+βcosθ′).​
(75)
The aberration of light follows by dividing spatial components by E/c:
cosθ=1+βcosθ′cosθ′+β​,sinθ=γ(1+βcosθ′)sinθ′​.​
(76)
In particular
tanθ=γ(cosθ′+β)sinθ′​=γ(1+βsecθ′)tanθ′​,
(77)
where the tangent expression is understood only when its denominators exist. The sine and cosine equations determine the correct quadrant and also cover directions where the tangent/secant expression is undefined.
For the photon-number half-cone under relativistic aberration, the forward half of the primed emission angles is −π/2≤θ′≤π/2. The aberration of light is monotone on this interval, and its endpoints have cosθ=β. Thus exactly half of a uniform angular photon distribution lies within the lab-frame cone
∣θ∣≤α,cosα=cv​.​
(78)
As v→c−, α=arcsin(1/γ)∼1/γ, so the cone becomes narrow. Here “half” counts emitted photons or ray directions; it is not a claim that half of the observed energy or power lies there, since the relativistic Doppler effect changes the photon energies with angle.
