For a finite nonempty set A, its additive energy is
E(A,A)=#{(a1​,a2​,a3​,a4​)∈A4:a1​+a2​=a3​+a4​}=∑s​rA+A​(s)2,
(1)
where rA+A​(s) counts ordered representations. Since ∑s​rA+A​(s)=∣A∣2, Cauchy-Schwarz gives
E(A,A)≥∣A+A∣∣A∣4​.
(2)
Thus ∣A+A∣≤K∣A∣ implies E(A,A)≥∣A∣3/K: a small doubling constant forces many additive quadruples.
A polynomial form of the Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem gives the converse after passing to a subset. There are absolute constants c,C>0 such that if E(A,A)≥η∣A∣3, 0<η≤1, then some A′⊆A satisfies
∣A′∣≥cηC∣A∣,∣A′+A′∣≤Cη−C∣A′∣.​
(3)
The exponent and constants here need not be optimal.
Precisely, a graph form of the Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem states the following. If A,B are finite sets of size n in an abelian group, Γ⊆A×B has at least δn2 edges, and its restricted sumset
A+Γ​B={a+b:(a,b)∈Γ}
(4)
has size at most Kn, then there are A′⊆A, B′⊆B with ∣A′∣,∣B′∣≥cδCn and
∣A′+B′∣≤Cδ−CKCn.
(5)
These are uniform absolute constants, valid for 0<δ≤1 and K≥1.
To apply this theorem to additive energy, let n=∣A∣ and retain the sums with rA+A​(s)≥ηn/2. The discarded sums contribute at most (ηn/2)∑s​r(s)=ηn3/2 to the additive energy. Since r(s)≤n, the retained pairs number at least ηn2/2. There are at most 2n/η retained sums. They therefore define a bipartite graph of density at least η/2 with restricted sumset size at most 2n/η. The graph theorem supplies large subsets A′,B′ with polynomially bounded ∣A′+B′∣.
For completeness, turn this cross-sumset bound into a doubling constant bound. The Ruzsa triangle inequality gives ∣A′−A′∣≤∣A′+B′∣2/∣B′∣. If D=∣A′−A′∣/∣A′∣, the Plünnecke inequality, applied to the pair A′,−A′, gives ∣A′+A′∣≤D2∣A′∣. The minimal-growth proof below in Question 5 applies to arbitrary finite pairs and proves this particular expansion estimate as well. All losses are fixed powers of η−1, proving the displayed converse after increasing C.
The requested dense bipartite common-neighbour lemma is a dependent random choice assertion. Call an ordered pair in V2 bad when it has fewer than ϵn common neighbours. Choose w∈W uniformly, and put S=N(w)⊆V. Then
E∣S∣=δm,E∣S∣2≥δ2m2.
(6)
For each bad pair, the probability that both endpoints lie in S is its number of common neighbours divided by n, which is less than ϵ. Thus the expected number b(S) of bad ordered pairs in S2 is at most ϵm2. Consequently
E[∣S∣2−2ϵδ2​b(S)]≥2δ2m2​.
(7)
Choose a neighbourhood V′=S attaining at least this expectation. It has ∣V′∣≥δm/2​≥δm/2, and
b(V′)≤δ22ϵ​∣V′∣2.
(8)
Hence at least the proportion 1−2ϵ/δ2 of its ordered pairs have at least ϵn two-edge connections. As usual these connections are counted by common neighbours, including a return walk for a diagonal ordered pair; assume δ>0, as the formula requires.
The relevance to the graph theorem is that many two-edge connections turn a sparse collection of permitted sums into additive control on a large vertex subset. On a connection a→b→a′, a−a′=(a+b)−(a′+b) expresses a difference using two elements of the restricted sumset. The abundance of connections, followed by standard cleaning and further path counting, bounds the number of differences without throwing away most vertices. This is the role of dependent random choice in obtaining polynomial losses in the graph form of the Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem.

Write e(t)=exp(2πit). A sequence in the circle group is an equidistributed sequence if, for every interval I⊆R/Z,
N1​#{1≤n≤N:ϕ(n)∈I}⟶∣I∣,

where ∣I∣ is its normalized length. Equivalently, averages of every continuous function along the sequence tend to its circle integral. Trigonometric polynomials approximate continuous functions, and interval indicators can be squeezed between continuous functions with arbitrarily close integrals. The nonconstant additive characters have integral zero. These facts give the Weyl criterion:
ϕ is equidistributed⟺En≤N​e(mϕ(n))→0for every m∈Z∖{0}.​
(10)
This is the link between equidistribution and cancellation in exponential sums.
Suppose every positive-shift difference sequence were equidistributed. Fix m=0 and set zn​=e(mϕ(n)). For every fixed h≥1, the Weyl criterion would give
N1​∑n=1N−h​zn​zn+h​​→0.

Some conventions omit the denominator; their interval norm differs by a fixed bounded factor, and the quadratic-phase norm is then ∥I∥U3(G)​.

A large Gowers U3 norm detects structure capable of changing four-term progression counts; quadratic phases are the basic example.
For the remaining proof use the ambient G just specified, define ∂h​F(x)=F(x+h)F(x)​, and take averages uniformly on G. For the quadratic phase, the multiplicative derivative on the overlap is ∂h​f0​(x)=e(2αhx+αh2). Hence an explicit choice is

A genuinely nonlinear example is the bracket-linear frequency function
θ(h)=3​{2​h}(mod1),H=[−N,N]∩Z.​
(26)
It has ≫N3 exact additive quadruples, yet agrees with any affine function ah+b(mod1) at only o(N) shifts, uniformly in the affine function. The question permits giving this example without proof, but the mechanism is useful. For a pair with sum s, the value ⌊2​h1​⌋+⌊2​h2​⌋ has only two possibilities, ⌊2​s⌋ or ⌊2​s⌋−1. There are O(N) pair classes, so Cauchy-Schwarz produces ≫N3 collisions with both additive relations.
To see why no long affine agreement occurs, three agreement points force the corresponding lattice points (h,⌊2​h⌋) to be collinear: eliminating the affine slope gives 3​ times an integer determinant equal to an integer, and irrationality makes that determinant zero. A rational line of slope p/q contains agreement shifts in one residue class modulo q, and closeness of ⌊2​h⌋ to 2​h bounds their span by 1/∣2​−p/q∣. Lines with q large have arbitrarily small possible density; for bounded q, irrationality of 2​ bounds the number uniformly. Thus the maximum agreement is o(N).

Use the phase-distance convention for a Bohr set:
B(R,ϵ)={x∈Z/NZ:∥rx/N∥R/Z​≤ϵ for every r∈R},
(27)
where the norm is distance to the nearest integer. This is the Bohr set in phase-distance convention; the alternative condition ∣e(rx/N)−1∣≤ρ has a different radius and corresponding constants. Assume d≥1, as 1/d requires.
For each residue x, form tx​=(rx/N)r∈R​ in the d-dimensional torus. Around these N points place translates of a box of side length ϵ in each coordinate. For 0<ϵ≤1, their volumes sum to Nϵd. If ϵ>N−1/d, two boxes overlap, and their distinct indices differ by a nonzero q with every phase distance less than ϵ. Thus the Bohr set contains a nonzero element at the stated threshold. For larger radii the entire group is already present.
Letting the box width decrease to N−1/d and using the finite number of possible nonzero q, obtain a nonzero q with ∥rq/N∥≤N−1/d for every r∈R. By the triangle inequality, jq lies in B(R,ϵ) whenever 0≤j<ϵN1/d. Since N is prime, these multiples are distinct up to length N. Therefore, in the usual radius range 0<ϵ≤1/2, the arithmetic progression
0,q,2q,…,(L−1)q,L=⌈ϵN1/d⌉​
(28)
has the requested length. For literally unrestricted ϵ>0, the correct lower bound is min(N,⌈ϵN1/d⌉): a progression of distinct residues cannot exceed N. For example, d=1, ϵ=2 makes the uncapped printed bound impossible. If R is empty, the Bohr set is the whole group and has a progression of length N; the displayed exponent is simply inapplicable.
Now transfer the dense integer set to a larger cyclic group. Choose a prime 8N<p<16N, using Bertrand's postulate, and regard A as a subset of G=Z/pZ. Its density α=∣A∣/p is at least 1/1600. Normalize Fourier coefficients on a finite abelian group and convolution by
f​(r)=Ex∈G​f(x)e(−rx/p),(f∗g)(x)=Ey​f(y)g(x−y).
(29)
The Fourier inversion and Parseval identity formulas are f(x)=∑r​f​(r)e(rx/p) and ∑r​∣f​(r)∣2=E∣f∣2; convolution turns into multiplication of Fourier coefficients on a finite abelian group.
For f=1A​, set R={r:∣f​(r)∣≥α3/2/2}. Then Parseval identity bounds
∣R∣≤4α−2≤D:=4⋅16002.
(30)
Also, with f​(x)=f(−x), the nonnegative convolution Q=f∗f∗f​∗f​ is supported exactly on the modular set 2A−2A, and
Q(x)=∑r​∣f​(r)∣4e(rx/p).
(31)
The frequencies outside R contribute in absolute value at most (α3/4)∑r​∣f​(r)∣2=α4/4. For x∈B(R,1/6), the real part of each phase from R is at least 1/2; moreover 0∈R and ∣f​(0)∣4=α4. Hence Q(x)≥α4/4>0. We have proved the cyclic Bogolyubov lemma with explicit phase radius:
B(R,1/6)⊆2A−2A,∣R∣≤D.
(32)
The previous Bohr set argument gives a modular arithmetic progression of length at least p1/D/6. The remaining step is lifting a short modular progression to an integer progression. Every one of its elements has a unique integer lift belonging to 2A−2A⊆[−2N,2N]. Successive lifted differences lie in [−4N,4N] and are congruent to the same residue. Since p>8N, at most one integer in this difference interval represents that residue, so every successive difference is the same. The lifts form a genuine integer arithmetic progression, not merely a modular one.
For sufficiently large N, the constants can be absorbed into half the exponent. Thus
2A−2A contains an arithmetic progression of length at least Nc,c=2D1​>0.​
(33)
No attempt to optimize this absolute constant is needed.
A counterexample comes from dense integer sets with only logarithmic-length progressions. To show that A itself need not have such a progression, choose a random subset B⊆[1,N] by independent inclusion with probability p0​=0.02. Its size has mean 0.02N and variance at most 0.02N, so Chebyshev's inequality shows P(∣B∣<0.01N)≤200/N. There are at most N2 increasing arithmetic progressions of any fixed length L≥2, and each belongs to B with probability p0L​. Taking L=⌈4logN/log(1/p0​)⌉ makes the union bound at most N−2. For large N, some realization has at least 0.01N elements and no length-L arithmetic progression. Take a subset of exactly 0.01N elements. It retains that avoidance. Such a set has no progression longer than O(logN), and therefore no progression of length Nc for any fixed c>0, eventually. Interpret the prescribed exact cardinality on values of N where it is integral, or use its integer part.

We first prove the needed Petridis minimal-growth lemma, including the expansion estimate rather than assuming it. For finite nonempty sets A,B, choose a nonempty X⊆A minimizing r=∣X+B∣/∣X∣. For any finite set C={c1​,…,cs​}, define
Xi​={x∈X:x+ci​∈/⋃j<i​(X+cj​)}.
(34)
Then ∣X+C∣=∑i​∣Xi​∣. An element from (X∖Xi​)+B+ci​ has already appeared in an earlier X+B+cj​, so the new contribution of X+B+ci​ has size at most
∣X+B∣−∣(X∖Xi​)+B∣≤r∣X∣−r∣X∖Xi​∣=r∣Xi​∣.
(35)
The inequality follows from minimality of r on subsets of A, with an empty subset contributing zero. Summing proves ∣X+B+C∣≤r∣X+C∣. Iteration gives ∣X+jB∣≤rj∣X∣, for every j≥0; in particular, ∣jB∣≤rj∣X∣ by translating jB into X+jB.
Apply this with B=A, so r≤K. It follows that
∣ℓA∣≤Kℓ∣A∣=KℓN(ℓ≥2).​
(36)
This is the required Plünnecke inequality.
We will also use the mixed-sumset bound ∣mA−nA∣≤Km+nN. To justify it, the Ruzsa triangle inequality says
∣P−Q∣∣T∣≤∣P−T∣∣T−Q∣.

Choose one representation z=pz​−qz​ for each z∈P−Q. The map (z,t)↦(pz​−t,t−qz​) is injective: adding the two coordinates recovers z, then the chosen representation recovers t. This proves the inequality. Apply it with P=mA, Q=nA, T=−X and the minimal-growth set already chosen. The bounds on X+mA and X+nA give the mixed-sumset estimate, including 0A={0}.
The Ruzsa covering lemma states: if S,T are finite, T=∅, and ∣S+T∣≤L∣T∣, then there is D⊆S, ∣D∣≤L, such that S⊆D+T−T. Choose D maximal with the translates d+T, d∈D, pairwise disjoint. They lie in S+T, so ∣D∣∣T∣≤∣S+T∣. Maximality says each s+T meets some d+T, which writes s=d+t1​−t2​. This proves both assertions.
Let T=A−A. The mixed-sumset bounds give ∣T∣≤K2N and ∣2T+A∣=∣3A−2A∣≤K5N. Apply the Ruzsa covering lemma to S=2T and the set A. There is D⊆2T of size q≤K5 with
2T⊆D+T.

∣ℓA∣≤K2(q−1ℓ+q−2​)N,1≤q≤K5.​
