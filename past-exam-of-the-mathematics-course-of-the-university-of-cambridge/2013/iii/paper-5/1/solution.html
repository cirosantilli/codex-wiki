We take algebras to be unital and modules to be unital. A finite-dimensional semisimple algebra over the complex numbers is one whose regular left module is a semisimple module, that is, a direct sum of simple modules. We use the basic finite-dimensional equivalences: this is equivalent to a zero Jacobson radical, and the Jacobson radical of any finite-dimensional algebra is a nilpotent ideal.
The Artin–Wedderburn theorem here says that there are positive integers d1​,…,dr​ with
A≅i=1∏r​Mdi​​(C).​
(1)
The nonisomorphic simple modules are the standard column modules of the factors, of dimensions di​, and the factor sizes are unique up to reordering.
Here is a proof. Decompose the regular left module as A​A=⨁i=1r​Si⊕ni​​ with pairwise nonisomorphic simple modules Si​. These are all the simple modules: any simple module is generated by a nonzero vector and is therefore a quotient of the regular module. The Schur lemma gives HomA​(Si​,Sj​)=0 for i=j and EndA​(Si​)=C. For the latter assertion, an endomorphism has an eigenvalue over C, and the kernel of its difference from that scalar is a nonzero submodule, hence the whole simple module. Consequently
EndA​(A​A)≅∏i​Mni​​(C).
(2)
Every regular-module endomorphism is right multiplication by its value at 1, so EndA​(A​A)≅Aop. Taking opposite algebras and using matrix transposition gives the asserted decomposition with di​=ni​.
For a matrix algebra Md​(C), the matrix units show that its only simple module is Cd: the spaces Ejj​S are isomorphic through Eij​, and a nonzero vector in one of them generates one copy of the column module. Simplicity makes that copy all of S. In a product algebra, the mutually orthogonal central idempotents decompose every module into its factor modules; a simple module uses exactly one factor. This proves the module assertion and also uniqueness, since the primitive central idempotents and the dimensions of their simple modules determine the factors.
