For an incompressible flow, write the Newtonian fluid stress tensor as σ=−pI+2μe, where e=(∇u+∇uT)/2 is the rate-of-strain tensor. The Stokes equation gives ∂j​σij​=0. Symmetry of the Cauchy stress tensor therefore gives
∂j​(ui​σij​)=σij​∂j​ui​=σ:e=2μe:e.
(1)
Integrating and applying the divergence theorem expresses the viscous dissipation as boundary power:
D=∫∂V​u⋅σndS.​
(2)
At a moving rigid body, the surface velocity is U+Ω×(x−xc​). Its boundary-power contribution is U⋅F+Ω⋅G, with the force and torque evaluated using the fluid's outward normal vector. Both resultants vanish for a force-free, torque-free inclusion. Thus the new viscous dissipation comes entirely from the unchanged outer boundary velocity. Subtracting the particle-free boundary power gives
D′=∫∂V0​​U0​⋅σ′ndS.
(3)
Apply the Lorentz reciprocal theorem to (u0​,σ0​) and (u′,σ′) in the fluid outside the inclusion. On the outer boundary u′=0, so
0=D′+∫A​(u0​⋅σ′−u′⋅σ0​)ndS.
(4)
Writing np​=−n for the particle's outward normal vector gives the required extra dissipation due to a rigid inclusion:
D′=∫A​(u0​⋅σ′−u′⋅σ0​)np​dS.​
(5)
This subtraction already accounts for the fluid volume displaced by the inclusion; it is not just the integral of the disturbance's local viscous dissipation.
For the sphere, the ambient rate-of-strain tensor E is symmetric and trace free. The sphere in a uniform straining Stokes flow has zero translational and rotational velocity: inversion symmetry eliminates its force, and symmetry of E eliminates its torque. To derive the disturbance, put r=∣x∣, Q=x⋅Ex and seek
u′=f(r)Ex+g(r)Qx,p′=μj(r)Q.
(6)
The incompressible flow condition and Stokes equation reduce to
rf′​+rg′+5g=0,f′′+r4​f′+4g=2j,g′′+r8​g′=rj′​.
(7)
A decaying family satisfying these equations is f=Ar−5, g=Br−5−5Ar−7/2, j=2Br−5. The no-slip boundary condition requires f(a)=−1 and g(a)=0, so A=−a5 and B=−5a3/2. Consequently
u′=−r5a5​Ex−2r55a3​(1−r2a2​)(x⋅Ex)x,p′=−r55μa3​(x⋅Ex).​
(8)
The total velocity is zero at r=a, and the disturbance decays as r−2. The pressure constant has been set to zero. These boundary and far-field conditions, together with Uniqueness of Stokes flow, establish the solution. There is no rotational background in a pure strain flow.
On the sphere u′=−u0​=−aEnp​. Contracting the supplied Newtonian fluid stress tensor with np​ makes its two terms proportional to (np​⋅Enp​)np​ cancel, leaving σnp​=5μEnp​. Hence the extra dissipation due to a rigid inclusion is
D′=5μa∫A​∣Enp​∣2dS=5μa3Eij​Eik​34π​δjk​=320π​μa3E:E​.
(9)
Here ∫S2​nj​nk​dΩ=4πδjk​/3. Taking the large outer boundary limit only after using the fixed-boundary power identity avoids replacing that prescribed boundary by an uncontrolled boundary at infinity.
If Nv​ denotes the number of spheres per unit volume, Nv​(4πa3/3)=ϕ. Thus
Nv​=4πa33ϕ​,Nv​D′=5μϕE:E.​
(10)
Add this to the particle-free viscous dissipation density 2μE:E. Comparing with 2μeff​E:E gives the Einstein viscosity formula for a dilute suspension:
μeff​=μ(1+25​ϕ)​
(11)
to first order in ϕ. The hydrodynamic interactions neglected here contribute beyond that dilute order.

For two Stokes flows (u(1),σ(1)) and (u(2),σ(2)) of the same dynamic viscosity in the same region, with no volume force, the Lorentz reciprocal theorem states
∫∂V​u(1)⋅σ(2)ndS=∫∂V​u(2)⋅σ(1)ndS.​
(12)
Indeed, the divergence of their cross-work difference is
∂j​(ui(1)​σij(2)​−ui(2)​σij(1)​)=2μ(e(1):e(2)−e(2):e(1))=0.
(13)
The pressure terms vanish by incompressible flow, and the derivatives of the Cauchy stress tensors vanish by the Stokes equation. The divergence theorem proves the result.
Use the convention that F,G are the force and torque exerted by the body on the fluid. By Linearity of Stokes flow, (F,G)=R(U,Ω). Applying the Lorentz reciprocal theorem to two independent rigid motions gives q1T​Rq2​=q2T​Rq1​, so the hydrodynamic resistance matrix is a symmetric matrix. The boundary-power identity gives
qTRq=F⋅U+G⋅Ω=2μ∫e:edV.
(14)
This is strictly positive for nonzero rigid motion: equality would imply e=0, hence a rigid motion throughout the connected fluid, which must vanish since the fluid is at rest at infinity. The no-slip boundary condition would then force q=0. Therefore R is a positive-definite matrix. Reversing to the fluid-on-body force changes the sign of the force law, not the positive resistance coefficients.
For the two rods, take the torque about O and use body axes. On the x-rod, X=(s,0,0), 0≤s≤2L, and its slender-body force density is
fx​=C(2Ux​​, Uy​+sΩz​, Uz​−sΩy​).
(15)
On the y-rod it is
fy​=C(Ux​−sΩz​, 2Uy​​, Uz​+sΩx​).
(16)
Integrate f and X×f along both rods, using ∫ds=2L, ∫sds=2L2 and ∫s2ds=8L3/3. A convenient dimensionally uniform statement of the complete right-angle two-rod resistance matrix is
​Fx​Fy​Fz​Gx​/LGy​/LGz​/L​​=CL​30000−2​030002​0042−20​0028/300​00−208/30​−2200016/3​​​Ux​Uy​Uz​LΩx​LΩy​LΩz​​​.​
(17)
In physical coordinates this means translation–translation entries scale as CL, the two translation–rotation blocks as CL2, and rotation–rotation entries as CL3. The planar block has inverse
​30−2​032​−2216/3​​−1=​1/2−1/61/4​−1/61/2−1/4​1/4−1/43/8​​.
(18)
This verifies the printed hydrodynamic mobility matrix with its third velocity component LΩz​; the TeX aid's Ωx​ is an OCR error.
Take laboratory vertical velocity positive upwards. The body axes are e1​=(cosθ,sinθ) and e2​=(−sinθ,cosθ). In quasistatic sedimentation the force and torque exerted on the fluid equal the gravitational resultants on the body:
Fx​=−(1+2λ)mgsinθ,Fy​=−(1+2λ)mgcosθ,Gz​=2λmgL(sinθ−cosθ).
(19)
The out-of-plane block is unforced, so its positive hydrodynamic resistance matrix gives Uz​=Ωx​=Ωy​=0. Substitution in the planar hydrodynamic mobility matrix gives
mgCL​Ux​=61−λ​cosθ−21+λ​sinθ,mgCL​Uy​=61−λ​sinθ−21+λ​cosθ,
(20)
and
CL2θ˙=4(1−λ)mg​(cosθ−sinθ).​
(21)
For 0≤λ<1, this ordinary differential equation has positive right side on 0≤θ<π/4, with a stable zero at π/4. Uniqueness prevents crossing that equilibrium in finite time. For λ>1 the initial angular velocity is negative, and the stable equilibrium reached from zero is −3π/4. The heavier-end body turns clockwise through 3π/4; it does not settle at π/4. More explicitly, with K=(1−λ)mg/(4CL2),
tan2θ−π/4​=−tan(π/8)e−2​Kt,
(22)
where the continuous branch has θ−π/4∈(−π,0). At λ=1, θ remains zero.
Transforming the translational velocity back to laboratory axes gives
Uh​=6CL(1−λ)mg​cos2θ​,Uv​=CLmg​[−21+λ​+61−λ​sin2θ]<0.
(23)
For λ=1, eliminate time between Uh​ and the angular velocity:
dθdxO​​=32L​(cosθ+sinθ),xO​(θ)−xO​(0)=32L​(1+sinθ−cosθ).
(24)
At either limiting orientation sinθ=cosθ. Thus both cases have the same net horizontal displacement,
xO​(∞)−xO​(0)=2L/3.​
(25)
For λ<1 the drift is monotonically rightwards. For λ>1 it first moves left, reaching xO​−xO​(0)=(2L/3)(1−2​) at θ=−π/4, then reverses. The total horizontal path length in this second case is (2L/3)(22​−1), whereas its net displacement is 2L/3 rightwards. At λ=1 it falls vertically without rotation or drift; taking the infinite-time limit before λ→1 is consequently singular.
For the requested sedimentation drift of a weighted two-rod body sketch, a full parametric trajectory follows by also integrating Uv​/θ˙. Put ψ=θ−π/4 and set the initial height to zero:
zO​(θ)=3(1−λ)22​L(1+2λ)​lntan(π/8)∣tan(ψ/2)∣​−322​L​(cosψ−2​1​).
(26)
It tends to −∞ in either case while xO​ tends to 2L/3. At λ<1 both rods end pointing upwards from O symmetrically; at λ>1 they end pointing downwards symmetrically. The asymptotic downward speed is (1+2λ)mg/(3CL).

For the motionless uniform layer, the heat equation reduces to Tzz​=0. The lower temperature and upper heat-transfer condition determine the quasistatic temperature of a conductively cooled film:
T(z)=T0​−κ+αhαΔT​z,Ts​=T(h)=T0​−ΔTκ+αhαh​.​
(27)
When αh/κ≪1, Ts​=T0​−ΔTαh/κ+O(ΔT(αh/κ)2). For a varying film let its vertical velocity scale as Uh/L, as required by incompressible flow. Relative to vertical thermal diffusion, horizontal thermal diffusion is smaller by (h/L)2, and either horizontal or vertical thermal advection is smaller by Uh2/(κL). Time variation on the film's transport timescale L/U has the same small factor. Thus the leading heat equation is again Tzz​=0 at each x,t when both specified dimensionless parameters are small. This argument retains the small αh/κ assumption. If thickness were externally varied on a faster timescale th​, one would additionally need h2/(κth​)≪1; the spatial conditions alone do not control arbitrary rapid time dependence.
Define b=γ′ΔTα/κ>0. The surface tension at the surface is γs​=γ0​+bh, so the Marangoni stress is γs,x​=bhx​. With capillary pressure p=−γ0​hxx​ to leading order, horizontal Stokes flow in the film solves μuzz​=px​, u(0)=0, μuz​(h)=γs,x​. Hence
u(z)=2μpx​​(z2−2hz)+μγs,x​​z,q=∫0h​udz=−3μh3​px​+2μh2​γs,x​.
(28)
The continuity equation gives the thermocapillary thin-film equation
ht​+∂x​(3μγ0​​h3hxxx​+2μb​h2hx​)=0.​
(29)
To justify using constant surface tension in the capillary pressure, compare the two volume fluxes on horizontal scale L: their magnitudes are γ0​h4/(3μL3) and bh3/(2μL). When both mechanisms are retained at leading order, their balance gives bh/γ0​=O((h/L)2)≪1. Thus replacing γs​ by γ0​ in the curvature term incurs only a relative small correction. Equivalently, small αh/κ ensures this if γ′ΔT/γ0​ is bounded; if it were parametrically large, the extra small surface-tension-variation condition would have to be imposed separately.
The capillary pressure is higher beneath a crest than beneath a trough, so pressure-driven volume flux drains the crest and smooths thickness. The Marangoni effect acts oppositely: a thicker region has a colder surface and therefore larger surface tension. Surface velocity is pulled towards that region, supplying more liquid and amplifying the thickness variation. The arrows below represent these two mechanisms separately.
Put ac​=γ0​/(3μ) and am​=b/(2μ). Substitution of h=h^H, x=h^X/ϵ, t=t^τ gives coefficients am​ϵ2t^ and ac​ϵ4t^/h^. Making both equal to one yields
ϵ2=2κγ0​3γ′ΔTαh^​,t^=3(γ′ΔTα)2h^4μκ2γ0​​.​
(30)
In these scales the fractional surface tension change is bh^/γ0​=2ϵ2/3, making the previous approximation explicit.
The linear stability analysis about H=1 gives ητ​+ηXX​+ηXXXX​=0. For a normal mode esτ+ikX,
s(k)=k2−k4,∣k∣max​=1/2​,smax​=1/4.​
(31)
Long waves with 0<∣k∣<1 grow, ∣k∣>1 decay, and k=0,±1 are neutral. The neutral k=0 mode changes the mean film thickness. The dispersion relation is even in k, with its two maxima at ±1/2​.
For a steady positive film with zero volume flux, divide H2HX​+H3HXXX​=0 by H3 and integrate to obtain HXX​+lnH=C. Choosing h^ as specified fixes C=0. Multiplication by HX​ and another integration then give the zero-flux thermocapillary film profiles first integral
21​HX2​+V(H)=E,V(H)=H(lnH−1).​
(32)
For H>0, V′=lnH and V′′=1/H>0: the unique minimum is V(1)=−1, while V(0+)=0 and V(e)=0. This potential energy interpretation classifies the profiles without assuming a sinusoidal shape at finite amplitude.
For E=−1, the only profile is the uniform film H=1. For −1<E<0, there are two positive turning heights H−​<1<H+​<e and a smooth periodic film oscillates between them. Its period is
P(E)=2∫H−​H+​​2[E−V(H)]​dH​.
(33)
For E=0, the maximum is e and the lower endpoint is zero. A drop reaches zero thickness in finite distance, with zero limiting slope but unbounded curvature since HXX​=−lnH. Its half-width is
ℓ0​=∫0e​2H(1−lnH)​dH​=2e​​∫0∞​u−1/2e−u/2du=πe​​.
(34)
Thus the formal limiting profile consists of drops of peak height e and footprint width 2πe​, with zero limiting contact angle. Identical drops can touch, or a zero-thickness region can separate them in the degenerate zero-flux model. These are limiting wet-region solutions, not everywhere positive twice differentiable films; the singular contact region is not resolved by lubrication theory. A useful parametric drop profile is
H=ee−w2,∣X−Xc​∣=πe​erf(w/2​),w≥0.
(35)
Finally let E=−1+δE with small positive δE. Writing H=1+η gives V=−1+η2/2+O(η3) and HXX​=−η+O(η2). The small-amplitude steady profile is H=1+2δE​cos(X−Xc​)+O(δE), with period tending to 2π. Its wavenumber tends to the neutral boundary ∣k∣=1 of the unstable band, not to the fastest-growing wavenumber.

Take Δρ>0 and τ>0, with x pointing along the imposed shear stress, and put G=Δρg. Relative to the upper fluid's hydrostatic reference, the hydrostatic pressure in the current is p=G(h−z). Thus the horizontal pressure gradient is G∇h. The lubrication theory momentum balance, lower no-slip boundary condition, and imposed upper shear stress give
u∥​(z)=−2μG​z(2h−z)∇h+μτ​zex​.
(36)
Its depth-integrated volume flux is
q=−3μG​h3∇h+2μτ​h2ex​.
(37)
Define D=G/(3μ) and A=τ/(2μ). The continuity equation gives the shear-driven viscous gravity current equation
ht​+A∂x​(h2)=D∇⋅(h3∇h).​
(38)
The two terms have opposite roles: imposed shear stress carries fluid downstream, whereas the hydrostatic pressure gradient spreads it from thick regions towards thin ones.
For the approximation, a representative horizontal velocity is U∼τH/μ+GH3/(μL), with vertical velocity UH/L. Small slopes require H/L≪1. Horizontal fluid inertia relative to vertical viscous resistance is ρc​UH2/(μL), where ρc​=ρ+Δρ. Hence sufficient small parameters, expressed without an unknown velocity, are
LH​≪1,μ2Lρc​τH3​≪1,μ2L2ρc​GH5​≪1.​
(39)
For the hydrostatic pressure and normal-stress approximation also require μU/(GHL)≪1. Its gravity-driven part is already (H/L)2; its shear-driven part adds
GLτ​≪1.​
(40)
This condition controls viscous normal stress, vertical viscous corrections and the normal projection of shear at a slightly tilted interface relative to GH. With it and the previous inertia bounds, vertical inertia is small as well. Time variations here are on the transport timescale L/U; separately imposed rapid forcing would need its own unsteady inertia bound. A small ordinary Reynolds number is a stronger sufficient restriction, but the reduced inertia ratios above are what the thin-layer momentum balance directly requires.
In the steady far-downstream regime, cross-stream derivatives dominate gravity-driven spreading. Dropping the smaller downstream gravity flux leaves
A(h2)x​=D(h3hy​)y​.
(41)
Write Y=yN​(x) and let hc​ be a typical central thickness. Balance gives Y2∼(D/A)hc2​x, while conservation of the source volume flux gives Q∼Ahc2​Y. Therefore the downstream similarity of a shear-driven gravity current has
Y∼(A2DQx​)1/3,hc​∝x−1/6.​
(42)
The omitted downstream gravity flux relative to the imposed shear flux is Dhc2​/(Ax)∼(Y/x)2≪1, so this approximation is self-consistent in the stated regime.
For the full profile put w=h2. Then h3hy​=(w2)y​/4 and
wx​=4AD​(w2)yy​.
(43)
This is a porous medium equation with downstream distance as its evolution coordinate. Conservation of ∫wdy=Q/A and a similarity solution w=x−1/3f(η), η=y/x1/3, give
−3A​(f+ηf′)=4D​(f2)′′,−3A​ηf=2D​ff′,
(44)
where symmetry gives zero integration constant at η=0. Inside the positive region, f′=−2Aη/(3D), so f=(A/(3D))(ηN2​−η2). Requiring the total downstream volume flux to be Q fixes the coefficient:
Q=A∫−YY​h2dy=9Dx4A2Y3​.
(45)
Consequently
yN​(x)=(τ23μΔρgQ​x)1/3,h(x,y)=[2Δρgxτ​(yN​(x)2−y2)]1/2​
(46)
for ∣y∣<yN​, with h=0 outside. The squared height is a parabolic Barenblatt solution; the height cross-section is a semicircular profile after rescaling its axes. The cross-stream volume flux vanishes at the edges even though the height slope becomes singular there. The profile describes the outer lubrication theory region, not a resolved microscopic front.
Near the point source the two horizontal dimensions are comparable, say ℓ. Upstream spreading arrests where outward gravity-driven volume flux balances downstream shear-driven volume flux:
ℓDh∗4​​∼Ah∗2​,Q∼Ah∗2​ℓ.
(47)
Eliminating h∗​ gives
xN​∼ADQ​​∼τμΔρgQ​​.​
(48)
This is a scaling estimate, not a determination of a numerical prefactor. The associated depth is h∗​∼(μQ/(Δρg))1/4. The same horizontal scale is obtained by setting yN​∼x in the downstream similarity solution, confirming where that solution fails.
For the line source the steady volume flux per unit width is q=Ah2−Dh3hx​. On the downstream constant-height branch, q=Q2d​ gives hd2​=Q2d​/A. Upstream there is no net flux through the finite nose, so q=0. Thus hhx​=A/D, and continuity of height at the source gives
h2(x)=hd2​+D2A​x=τ2μQ2d​​+Δρg3τ​x.
(49)
The line-source upstream reach under imposed shear is therefore
xN​=2A2DQ2d​​=3τ22μΔρgQ2d​​,h(x)=⎩⎨⎧​0,Δρg3τ​(x+xN​)​,τ2μQ2d​​​,​x≤−xN​,−xN​<x<0,x>0.​​
(50)
In the upstream part, shear-driven and gravity-driven volume fluxes cancel. The volume flux per unit width jumps by exactly Q2d​ at the source. As in the point-source profile, the square-root nose is a formal outer solution with an unresolved steep front; its finite upstream reach follows from flux balance, not from assuming a small slope all the way to the nose.
