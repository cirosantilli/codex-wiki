Write u=∂t​ξ. In the uniform equilibrium, the linearized ideal magnetohydrodynamic equations reduce to
∂t​δρ=−ρ∇⋅∂t​ξ,∂t​δp=−γp∇⋅∂t​ξ,∂t​δB=∇×(∂t​ξ×B).
(1)
Integrating from the undisplaced reference state, and using the uniform background magnetic field, gives
δρ=−ρ∇⋅ξ,δp=−γp∇⋅ξ,δB=(B⋅∇)ξ−B∇⋅ξ.​
(2)
These are the perturbations induced by the fluid displacement; independent time-independent changes to the reference state are excluded. The linear Lorentz force density is (∇×δB)×B/μ0​. Its magnetic pressure and magnetic tension parts give
ρ∂t2​ξ=−∇(δp+μ0​B⋅δB​)+μ0​(B⋅∇)δB​.​
(3)
For a Fourier mode, put s=k⋅ξ, q=B⋅ξ and K=k⋅B. Then δp=−iγps and δB=i(Kξ−Bs). Substitution gives
ρω2ξ=k[(γp+μ0​B2​)s−μ0​Kq​]+μ0​K​(Kξ−Bs).​
(4)
If s=q=0, the fluid displacement is perpendicular to both the wave vector and the magnetic field. Only magnetic tension restores it, and
ω2=(k⋅va​)2,va​=μ0​ρ​B​.​
(5)
This is the Alfvén wave. The Alfvén velocity is the field-directed propagation vector: ω=±k⋅va​. The signed phase velocity normal to the wavefront is ±va​cosθk; the group velocity is ±va​. The distinction matters for oblique propagation. For generic directions the Alfvén wave has one transverse polarization, with δρ=δp=0.
To obtain the other magnetohydrodynamic waves, take the scalar products with k and B:
ρω2s=k2[(γp+μ0​B2​)s−μ0​Kq​],ρω2q=γpKs.
(6)
The determinant condition for a nonzero pair (s,q) is
ω4−k2(cs2​+va2​)ω2+k4cs2​va2​cos2θ=0,cs2​=ργp​.
(7)
Thus the fast magnetosonic wave and slow magnetosonic wave have
vf,sl2​=2cs2​+va2​​±4(cs2​+va2​)2​−cs2​va2​cos2θ​.​
(8)
Their fluid displacements lie in the plane of k and B and are generally compressive. In the fast magnetosonic wave, gas and magnetic pressure provide the stronger restoring combination; in the slow magnetosonic wave, their perturbations oppose one another. The phase speeds depend on direction. They satisfy vsl​≤min(cs​,va​) and vf​≥max(cs​,va​). For parallel propagation the two speeds are cs​ and va​, with a degeneracy between a transverse branch and the Alfvén wave. For perpendicular propagation vf2​=cs2​+va2​ and vsl​=0; the latter is a nonpropagating limiting disturbance. These special directions require interpreting the polarizations by continuity rather than assuming three distinct nonzero frequencies.

For this geometry ∇⋅u=0, ∇⋅B=0 and u⋅∇=0. Consequently constant mass density and pressure satisfy the full nonlinear continuity and adiabatic pressure equations. Write B⊥​=(Bx​,By​) and u⊥​=(ux​,uy​). The ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation and the transverse ideal magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation become
∂t​B⊥​=Bz​∂z​u⊥​,ρ∂t​u⊥​=μ0​Bz​​∂z​B⊥​.
(9)
The longitudinal momentum equation is
0=−2μ0​1​∂z​∣B⊥​∣2.
(10)
Thus constant transverse magnetic magnitude eliminates the otherwise unavoidable magnetic pressure acceleration. Differentiating the ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation in time gives, for either transverse component,
∂t2​W=μ0​ρBz2​​∂z2​W.​
(11)
A constant-magnitude nonlinear Alfvén wave is obtained by taking Bz​=0 and
Bx​=AcosF(z−ct),By​=AsinF(z−ct),c=±μ0​ρ​Bz​​,u⊥​=U0​−Bz​c​B⊥​,
(12)
where A and U0​ are constants and F is any sufficiently differentiable real function. Both first-order equations hold, and Bx2​+By2​=A2. Taking F(ζ)=kζ gives an exact finite-amplitude circularly polarized wave. More general traveling rotations also work; an arbitrary superposition of oppositely traveling solutions of the wave equation need not preserve transverse magnetic magnitude and hence need not solve the full nonlinear system. If Bz​=0, the coupled first-order equations instead require time-independent transverse fields and velocities, with spatially constant transverse magnetic magnitude; there is no propagating Alfvén wave in the z direction.

Let e=ρu2/2+p/(γ−1) be the total gas energy density, with γ>1. The one-dimensional continuity and momentum equations are
∂t​ρ+∂z​(ρu)=0,ρ(∂t​u+u∂z​u)=−∂z​p.
(13)
Using continuity to put the momentum equation into conservative form gives
∂t​(ρu)+∂z​(ρu2+p)=0.
(14)
The internal energy density eint​=p/(γ−1) satisfies
∂t​eint​+∂z​(ueint​)=−p∂z​u,
(15)
by the adiabatic pressure equation. Multiplying the momentum equation by u and using continuity gives
∂t​(2ρu2​)+∂z​(2ρu3​)=−u∂z​p.
(16)
Adding these equations combines the pressure work into −∂z​(pu). The conservation law variables and conservation law fluxes are therefore
U=​ρρuρu2/2+p/(γ−1)​​,F=​ρuρu2+pu(ρu2/2+γp/(γ−1))​​.​
(17)
The three rows express conservation of mass, momentum and total energy. Their integral conservation laws remain meaningful across a shock wave, where the differential pressure and velocity equations cannot be applied pointwise.

Integrating each conservation law across a vanishingly thin interval around the stationary shock wave leaves equal conservation law fluxes on its two sides. Hence
ρ2​u2​=ρ1​u1​,p2​+ρ2​u22​=p1​+ρ1​u12​,u2​(2ρ2​u22​​+γ−1γp2​​)=u1​(2ρ1​u12​​+γ−1γp1​​).​
(18)
These are the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas. Signed velocities may both be negative when the material travels from positive to negative z; no sign change is needed in the conservation law fluxes.
Put r=ρ2​/ρ1​=u1​/u2​, P=p2​/p1​ and m=M12​=ρ1​u12​/(γp1​). Momentum conservation gives
P=1+γm(1−r1​).
(19)
Divide the energy condition by the nonzero mass flux. Equality of kinetic energy plus specific enthalpy gives
2γm​+γ−1γ​=2r2γm​+(γ−1)rγP​.
(20)
Substituting P and multiplying by 2(γ−1)r2/γ yields
(r−1)[((γ−1)m+2)r−(γ+1)m]=0.
(21)
The factor r−1 is the continuous, no-shock solution. On the nontrivial normal shock wave branch,
ρ1​ρ2​​=u2​u1​​=(γ−1)M12​+2(γ+1)M12​​,p1​p2​​=γ+12γM12​−(γ−1)​.​
(22)
An admissible compressive gas shock wave has M1​>1, so r>1 and P>1. The algebraic jump equations alone also allow a reversed expansive discontinuity; the entropy production in a perfect-gas shock excludes that branch. At M1​=1 the nontrivial branch joins the continuous solution.

Let D=u1​>0 denote the front speed in the stationary upstream frame. In the shock frame, the upstream and downstream velocities are −D and −D/r. Taking M1​→∞ in the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas gives
ρ2​=γ−1γ+1​ρ1​,p2​=γ+12​ρ1​D2.​
(23)
The downstream laboratory velocity is D−D/r=2D/(γ+1), which distinguishes the gas speed from the front speed.
For the planar blast-wave energy scaling of a self-similar blast wave, integration of the total energy density over the shocked interval 0<z<Z gives the energy per unit area on this side:
E+​=∫0Z​(2ρu2​+γ−1p​)dz=ρ1​ZZ˙2I,I=∫01​(21​fh2+γ−1g​)dη.
(24)
The similarity solution makes I time-independent; a finite positive explosion energy requires 0<I<∞. Conservation of energy therefore gives Z˙=(E+​/(ρ1​I))1/2Z−1/2 for the expanding front. Integrating from Z(0)=0,
32​Z3/2=(ρ1​IE+​​)1/2t,Z(t)=(4I9​)1/3(ρ1​E+​​)1/3t2/3.​
(25)
If E denotes the one-sided energy, C=(9/(4I))1/3. If the released energy E feeds two symmetric fronts, E+​=E/2 and C=(9/(8I))1/3. In either convention the requested scaling is Z=C(E/ρ1​)1/3t2/3. The constant depends on the similarity profiles and the energy convention; energy conservation determines the exponent without solving those profiles. The Strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions additionally fix f(1)=(γ+1)/(γ−1) and g(1)=h(1)=2/(γ+1).

In a steady flow the ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation gives ∇×(u×B)=0. Since both vectors have only poloidal magnetic field components, write u×B=Fϕ​eϕ​. Axisymmetry implies
∂z​Fϕ​=0,∂R​(RFϕ​)=0,Fϕ​=RC​.
(26)
For a field regular on the axis, C=0; equivalently one may impose zero toroidal electromotive force. Under this physical condition, u is parallel to B. Write ρu=kB. Continuity and ∇⋅B=0 then give B⋅∇k=0. The poloidal magnetic flux function has B⋅∇ψ=0, so locally on each connected magnetic surface,
u=ρk(ψ)​B.​
(27)
This regular-axis alignment of steady poloidal ideal flow needs its zero-circulation condition: axisymmetry alone does not imply this alignment on a domain excluding the axis. An explicit counterexample is available in a cylindrical annulus. Take nonzero constants U,b, positive ρc​,cs​, and
u=Uez​,B=Rb​eR​,ρ=(1+R2/a2)2ρc​​,p=cs2​ρ,Φ=2cs2​ln(1+R2/a2),a2=πGρc​2cs2​​.
(28)
Here ψ=−bz, ∇⋅B=∇×B=0 and u×B=Ubeϕ​/R has zero curl. The flow satisfies continuity, p′=−ρΦ′, and ∇2Φ=4πGρ. Its acceleration and Lorentz force density vanish, so it solves the steady equations while u and B are perpendicular. The nonzero toroidal circulation and the axial singularity explain why this counterexample is excluded from a regular polar accretion column. The remaining derivation uses the regular, aligned branch.
For a narrow stream tube, integrating continuity gives constant Aρ∣u∣. On a nonzero-flow tube the magnetohydrodynamic mass loading k is constant, so Aρ∣u∣=A∣k∣∣B∣. Therefore A∣B∣ is constant and A∝∣B∣−1. This also follows directly from conserved magnetic flux through a flux tube.
For constant isothermal sound speed, ∇p/ρ=cs2​∇ln(ρ/ρ0​), with arbitrary reference density ρ0​. Project momentum along B. The Lorentz force density has no component in that direction, and alignment implies B⋅(u⋅∇)u=B⋅∇(∣u∣2/2). Thus the isothermal magnetic Bernoulli integral is
21​∣u∣2+Φ+cs2​ln(ρ/ρ0​)=ϵ(ψ).​
(29)
Changing ρ0​ only shifts the Bernoulli function by a constant. Since k is constant along the magnetic field,
B⋅∇(2∣u∣2​)=∣u∣2[B⋅∇ln∣B∣−ρB⋅∇ρ​].
(30)
Differentiating the isothermal magnetic Bernoulli integral therefore gives
(cs2​−∣u∣2)B⋅∇ρ=−ρ[B⋅∇Φ+∣u∣2B⋅∇ln∣B∣].​
(31)
In the slender polar accretion column, the dipolar flux-tube area is A(z)=A∗​(z/R∗​)3. The decreasing axial field is a leading approximation, not an exactly solenoidal field (0,0,Bz​(z)) throughout a cylinder. Indeed ∇⋅B=∂z​Bz​=0 for that literal field. Near the axis a small radial component BR​≃−(R/2)∂z​Bz​=3RBz​/(2z) supplies the required radial divergence. Its magnitude is smaller by R/z, even though its divergence is leading order. Keeping this expanding flux tube while neglecting transverse forces yields the intended one-dimensional model.
Use the positive inward speed v=−uz​; the signed sonic velocity is uz​=−cs​. Conservation of mass and the Bernoulli equation give
Aρv=M˙,ρρ′​=−vv′​−z3​,vv′+z2GM∗​​+cs2​ρρ′​=0.
(32)
Eliminating ρ′ yields the isothermal dipolar accretion equation,
(v−vcs2​​)v′=z3cs2​​−z2GM∗​​.
(33)
A smooth transonic branch requires both sides to vanish at its sonic point, so
vs​=cs​,zs​=3cs2​GM∗​​.​
(34)
This is the critical point of the flow equation; a generic subsonic solution need not cross it. A crossing in the exterior column requires zs​≥R∗​, and a sonic point strictly outside the star requires zs​>R∗​. Differentiating the equation at the crossing gives 2(vs′​)2=3cs2​/zs2​. For inward accretion that accelerates toward the star, vs′​=−3/2​cs​/zs​.
The reservoir boundary condition fixes ϵ=cs2​ln(ρ∞​/ρ0​). At the sonic point, Φs​=−3cs2​ and vs2​/2=cs2​/2, giving
lnρ∞​ρs​​=3−21​=25​,ρs​=e5/2ρ∞​.​
(35)
Finally use the sonic mass flux through the dipolar flux-tube area:
M˙=ρ∞​cs​A∗​e5/2(3cs2​R∗​GM∗​​)3.​
(36)
This rate belongs to the smooth transonic branch of the idealized column, with A∗​ the supplied total loaded area. The reservoir condition alone does not force every steady solution onto this branch. A real narrow dipolar column must match an outer flow where the slender approximation ceases to apply.

Let primes denote radial derivatives of the equilibrium and put Δu=u′+2u/r. The linearized continuity, adiabatic pressure and Poisson equation are
∂t​δρ=−r21​∂r​(r2ρu)=−ρΔu−ρ′u,∂t​δp=−up′−γpΔu,∂r​(r2∂r​δΦ)=4πGr2δρ.
(37)
Take the time derivative of the Poisson equation and substitute continuity. Integration in radius gives
r2∂r​∂t​δΦ=−4πGr2ρu+C(t).
(38)
Centre regularity excludes a changing point mass at the origin, so C(t)=0 and
∂r​∂t​δΦ=−4πGρu.​
(39)
The perturbed self-gravity must be retained; this calculation does not make the Cowling approximation.
The radial linear momentum equation is ρ∂t​u=−∂r​δp−δρΦ′−ρ∂r​δΦ. Differentiate it in time and use the preceding equations:
ρ∂t2​u=∂r​(γpΔu)+∂r​(up′)+(ρΔu+ρ′u)Φ′+4πGρ2u.
(40)
The equilibrium has p′=−ρΦ′ and Φ′′+2Φ′/r=4πGρ. The remaining terms simplify as
∂r​(up′)+(ρΔu+ρ′u)Φ′+4πGρ2u=−r2up′​+ρu(4πGρ−Φ′′)=−r4up′​.
(41)
Consequently the radial stellar oscillation equation is
ρ∂t2​u=∂r​(γpΔu)−r4u​p′.​
(42)
The derivative acts on the full variable coefficient γ(r)p(r); discarding γ′ would change the result.
For u=rξ(r)e−iωt, Δu=(3ξ+rξ′)e−iωt. Substitute and multiply by −r3. Combining the two terms proportional to ξ′ into a total derivative gives the radial stellar pulsation equation,
ω2ρr4ξ=−drd​(γpr4ξ′)+r3drd​((4−3γ)p)ξ.​
(43)
Set P=γpr4, W=ρr4 and Q=r3[(4−3γ)p]′. The Sturm-Liouville operator is
Lξ=W1​[−(Pξ′)′+Qξ],⟨η,ξ⟩W​=∫0Rs​​Wη∗ξdr.
(44)
For regular physical perturbations, ξ is finite at the centre and Pξ′→0 at the free surface. For a nonzero-frequency mode the fluid displacement is u/(−iω), so vanishing Lagrangian pressure perturbation is equivalent to γp(3ξ+rξ′)→0. With p(Rs​)=0 and finite ξ, this gives Pξ′→0. Zero-frequency modes use the same displacement boundary condition directly. Assume bounded γ, positive ρ,p,γ in the interior and the usual finite-energy endpoint domain. Integration by parts gives
⟨η,Lξ⟩W​−⟨Lη,ξ⟩W​=[P(η′∗ξ−η∗ξ′)]0Rs​​=0.
(45)
Thus this physical self-adjoint differential operator has real eigenvalues ω2. The endpoint domain is essential: the fact that p vanishes does not by itself allow arbitrary singular trial functions. The regular free-surface realization of the Sturm-Liouville problem is the one used here.
The Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle gives the fundamental squared frequency as the infimum of the weighted stellar pulsation Rayleigh quotient,
ω02​=ξ=0inf​∫0Rs​​ρr4∣ξ∣2dr∫0Rs​​[γpr4∣ξ′∣2+r3((4−3γ)p)′∣ξ∣2]dr​.​
(46)
The infimum is over admissible finite-energy functions satisfying the physical endpoint conditions. If this quotient is nonnegative for every such function, all radial frequencies are real and there is no exponentially growing radial mode. A negative value for even one trial function proves a negative eigenvalue and an exponentially growing solution, because ω2<0. A zero lowest value is marginal and needs separate treatment of neutral motion.
Choose the homologous trial function ξ=1, which corresponds to radial velocity proportional to r. Its gradient contribution is zero, and
∫0Rs​​r3[(4−3γ)p]′dr=[r3(4−3γ)p]0Rs​​−3∫0Rs​​r2p(4−3γ)dr=−3∫0Rs​​r2p(4−3γ)dr.
(47)
The denominator ∫ρr4dr is positive. Hence
∫0Rs​​r2p(4−3γ)dr>0 ⟹ ω02​<0 ⟹ radial instability.​
(48)
This pressure-weighted radial instability criterion is sufficient, not necessary: another trial function can detect instability even if this one does not. For a constant stellar adiabatic exponent it recovers instability below 4/3. At constant γ=4/3, the homologous mode is neutral. For constant γ>4/3, Q=(4−3γ)r3p′>0 in a normally stratified hydrostatic equilibrium, so the quotient is positive and the star is radially stable.
