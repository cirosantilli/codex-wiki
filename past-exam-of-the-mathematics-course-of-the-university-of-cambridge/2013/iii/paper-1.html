The endomorphisms of the finite-dimensional vector space V over the complex numbers form the general linear Lie algebra L=EndC​(V) with the usual addition and scalar multiplication and Lie bracket [x,y]=xy−yx. This commutator is bilinear and antisymmetric, and expanding the six products verifies the Jacobi identity.
For a Lie subalgebra g=L1​, being an abelian Lie algebra means [g,g]=0. A nilpotent Lie algebra has γ1​=g, γj+1​=[g,γj​] eventually zero; a solvable Lie algebra has g(0)=g, g(j+1)=[g(j),g(j)] eventually zero. These are the lower central series of a Lie algebra and derived series of a Lie algebra, respectively. Nilpotence is a condition on the Lie bracket, and does not require every member to be a nilpotent endomorphism: a nonzero scalar multiple of the identity spans an abelian Lie algebra.
A flag of a vector space is an increasing chain of vector subspaces. The flag we construct is a complete flag, 0=V0​⊂V1​⊂⋯⊂Vd​=V, where dimVi​=i, and each Vi​ is an invariant subspace for g. We first prove the common-eigenvector assertion in the Lie theorem, by induction on dimg; the zero algebra is immediate. For nonzero solvable g, its derived algebra is proper, so there is a codimension-one ideal of a Lie algebra k containing [g,g]. Write g=k⊕Cx. By induction there are v=0 and a linear functional λ on k such that kv=λ(k)v for every k∈k.
Let W be the cyclic subspace spanned by v,xv,x2v,…. The commutator derivation identity and [k,x]⊆k show inductively that
kxjv≡λ(k)xjv(modspan{v,xv,…,xj−1v}).
(1)
Thus W and all its initial cyclic spans are k-invariant. If m=dimW, the first m cyclic vectors form a basis, W is also x-invariant, and trW​k=mλ(k). For k∈k, the trace of a matrix commutator gives
0=trW​[k,x]=mλ([k,x]).
(2)
Hence λ([k,x])=0, since the field has characteristic zero. The nonzero common weight space
Vλ​={w∈V:kw=λ(k)w for all k∈k}
(3)
is x-invariant: k(xw)=x(kw)+[k,x]w=λ(k)xw. The restriction of x to Vλ​ has an eigenvector, because C is an algebraically closed field. This is a common eigenvector for g. Its line is invariant, and repeating the argument on the quotient vector space gives the complete invariant flag. Equivalently, this proves simultaneous triangularization of a Lie algebra representation.
In a basis adapted to this complete flag, every member of g is upper triangular, so every member of its derived algebra is strictly upper triangular. Products of d strictly upper triangular matrices vanish, and each iterated Lie bracket of d such matrices is a sum of these products. Consequently the derived algebra is a nilpotent Lie algebra. We may therefore take L2​=[L1​,L1​]: it is an ideal, and the quotient Lie algebra L1​/L2​ is abelian. This also covers dimV=0.

A linear map α is a nilpotent endomorphism if αq=0 for some positive integer q, and a semisimple endomorphism if it is diagonalizable over C. Decompose V into its generalized eigenspaces Vλ​=ker(α−λI)d, where d=dimV. Define αs​ on Vλ​ to be λI, and set αn​=α−αs​. Then αs​ is diagonalizable, αn​ is nilpotent, and both preserve these vector subspaces and commute. Thus
α=αs​+αn​,[αs​,αn​]=0.​
(4)
For uniqueness, suppose α=S+N with S semisimple, N nilpotent and SN=NS. Both commute with α, so preserve each Vλ​. On an eigenspace of S of eigenvalue μ inside Vλ​, the map α is μI+N and has only the eigenvalue μ. Since the same subspace lies in Vλ​, μ=λ. Hence S=λI on Vλ​, proving S=αs​ and N=αn​. This is the additive Jordan–Chevalley decomposition.
We need a polynomial consequence of this decomposition. Hermite interpolation supplies a polynomial p with p(α)=αs​, by prescribing p(t)≡λ(mod(t−λ)d) for each eigenvalue. For any endomorphism T, its semisimple part can likewise be expressed as a polynomial in T with zero constant term: if zero is an eigenvalue, its interpolation condition already forces this; if not, add the independent condition p(0)=0.
On End(V), the maps adαs​ and adαn​ commute. The first is diagonalizable, with eigenvalues λ−μ on Hom(Vμ​,Vλ​). The second is nilpotent, since
(adN)k(T)=∑j=0k​(−1)j(jk​)Nk−jTNj,
(5)
which vanishes for k≥2q−1 when Nq=0. Uniqueness therefore proves adjoint compatibility of additive Jordan decomposition: adαs​=(adα)s​.
Now assume α∈M. The condition [α,W]⊆U⊆W implies that both W and U are invariant under adα, and every polynomial in adα with zero constant term maps W into U. Define β to be multiplication by λ on Vλ​. It commutes with α. On Hom(Vμ​,Vλ​), adβ acts by λ−μ​. Polynomial interpolation on the finite set of differences gives adβ=q(adαs​) with q(0)=0. The preceding paragraph then expresses adβ as a polynomial in adα with zero constant term. Consequently [β,W]⊆U, so β∈M.
The assumed trace orthogonality nilpotence lemma now follows directly. On Vλ​, α=λI+αn​ and β=λI, while the matrix trace of the nilpotent restriction of αn​ is zero. Hence
0=tr(αβ)=∑λ​dim(Vλ​)∣λ∣2.
(6)
Every summand is nonnegative, so every eigenvalue of α is zero. Its Jordan–Chevalley decomposition therefore has αs​=0, and α is nilpotent. Notice that neither U nor W was required to be a Lie subalgebra.

A finite-dimensional Lie algebra over the complex numbers L is a semisimple Lie algebra when its solvable radical is zero, equivalently when it has no nonzero solvable ideals. Its Killing form is
BL​(x,y)=trL​(adxady).
(7)
The cyclic property of the trace makes this bilinear form symmetric and gives its invariance of a bilinear form on a Lie algebra:
BL​([x,y],z)=BL​(x,[y,z]).
(8)
It follows that R={x:BL​(x,L)=0} is an ideal of a Lie algebra. For x∈R, adx induces the zero map on L/R. Therefore, for x,y∈R, the matrix trace splits over the invariant subspace R and the quotient to give BR​(x,y)=BL​(x,y)=0. In particular BR​([R,R],R)=0. The Cartan solvability criterion implies that R is solvable. Since L is semisimple, R=0: the Killing form is nondegenerate.
For completeness, the trace step in the Cartan solvability criterion is precisely the mechanism of the previous solution. For a complex matrix Lie algebra a with tr(xy)=0 for x∈[a,a], y∈a, set U=[a,a] and W=a. If β∈M and x=[a,b], then tr(xβ)=tr(a[b,β])=0, since [b,β]∈U. Linearity and the trace orthogonality nilpotence lemma show that every member of U is nilpotent. The Engel theorem makes U nilpotent and hence a solvable. Apply this to a=adR; the kernel of this Adjoint representation of a Lie algebra is the abelian center of R, so R is solvable as claimed.
For an arbitrary complex Lie algebra, a Cartan subalgebra H means a nilpotent Lie algebra that is self-normalizing: NL​(H)={x:[x,H]⊆H}=H. This definition does not assume that H is abelian. We prove that it is abelian when L is semisimple.
Use the generalized-weight decomposition for a nilpotent Lie algebra for the action of H on L. Its zero generalized weight space is
L0={x:(adh)dimLx=0 for every h∈H}.
(9)
We have H⊆L0, since H is nilpotent. If L0/H=0, the Engel theorem gives a nonzero coset annihilated by every h∈H. Its representative x satisfies [H,x]⊆H, contradicting NL​(H)=H. Thus L0=H.
For a nonzero generalized weight λ, choose h0​∈H with λ(h0​)=0. The operator adh0​ is invertible on Lλ and nilpotent on H. For h∈H, z∈Lλ, write z=(adh0​)mw with m large enough that (adh0​)mh=0. Invariance of the Killing form gives
BL​(h,z)=(−1)mBL​((adh0​)mh,w)=0.
(10)
On the other hand, H is solvable, so the Lie theorem triangularizes its action on L. For a,b,h∈H, the matrix ad[a,b] is strictly upper triangular, while adh is upper triangular. Thus BL​([H,H],H)=0. Together with L=H⊕⨁λ=0​Lλ, this yields BL​([H,H],L)=0. Nondegeneracy gives [H,H]=0.
Finally, if x commutes with H, it normalizes H, hence lies in H. Any abelian subalgebra containing H consists of such elements. Thus H is a maximal abelian subalgebra, indeed CL​(H)=H.

Here a reduced root system is crystallographic, as appropriate to a semisimple Lie algebra. It is a finite spanning set Φ⊂E∖{0} in a real inner product space of dimension r, invariant under each Weyl reflection
sα​(x)=x−(α,α)2(x,α)​α,
(11)
with 2(β,α)/(α,α)∈Z for every pair of roots and Φ∩Rα={α,−α}. A base of a root system Δ=(α1​,…,αr​) is a basis such that the coefficients of every root are integers that are either all nonnegative or all nonpositive. These define the positive system of a root system. We use the existing Cartan matrix convention
Aij​=⟨αi​,αj∨​⟩=(αj​,αj​)2(αi​,αj​)​.
(12)
Using the alternative denominator (αi​,αi​) transposes all the matrices below.
The classification of rank-two root systems gives A1​×A1​, A2​, B2​=C2​, and G2​. In orthonormal coordinates we may choose the following simple roots and Cartan matrices:
  • For A1​×A1​, take α1​=(1,0), α2​=(0,1) and A=(20​02​). The positive roots are α1​,α2​.
  • For A2​, take α1​=(1,0), α2​=(−1/2,3​/2) and A=(2−1​−12​). The positive roots are α1​,α2​,α1​+α2​.
  • For B2​, take the long root α1​=(1,−1) and short root α2​=(0,1), giving A=(2−1​−22​). The positive roots are α1​,α2​,α1​+α2​,α1​+2α2​. Interchanging the long and short convention gives type C2​, which is the same rank-two classification up to the usual identification.
  • For G2​, take the short root α1​=(1,0) and long root α2​=(−3/2,3​/2), giving A=(2−3​−12​). The positive roots are α1​,α2​,α1​+α2​,2α1​+α2​,3α1​+α2​,3α1​+2α2​.
Each complete root system consists of these positive roots and their negatives. To see why the list is exhaustive, the off-diagonal Cartan matrix entries of two distinct simple roots are nonpositive integers, and their product is 4cos2θ<4. Thus the product is 0,1,2 or 3, corresponding to simple-root angles π/2,2π/3,3π/4 or 5π/6. These determine the four systems and their length ratios.
The Weyl group of a rank-two root system is generated by the two simple Weyl reflections. Their product is a rotation of order m=2,3,4,6, respectively, so the groups are the dihedral groups of order 2m=4,6,8,12. The first is also the Klein four-group and the second the symmetric group S3​. The root-orthogonal lines cut the plane into 2m Weyl chambers, each of angle π/m. A chosen fundamental chamber of a root system is (x,α1​)>0, (x,α2​)>0; its walls are the two root-orthogonal lines. The Weyl group acts simply transitively on these open chambers.
The crystallographic hypothesis matters: if the integer-pairing condition is dropped, reduced noncrystallographic systems of type I2​(m) also occur. They are not additional Lie-algebra root systems.

Choose a positive system of a root system and the corresponding triangular decomposition of a Lie algebra L=n−⊕H⊕n+. A primitive element of a Lie algebra representation of weight ω∈H∗ is a nonzero vector v with hv=ω(h)v for every h∈H and n+v=0. Thus it is a highest-weight vector; the choice of positive roots is part of this definition. A highest-weight representation is generated by such a vector.
Let b=H⊕n+ be the corresponding Borel subalgebra. Define its one-dimensional Lie algebra representation Cω​ by the scalar ω(h) on H and zero on n+. This respects the Lie bracket, since [b,b]⊆n+. The induced Verma module
M(ω)=U(L)⊗U(b)​Cω​
(13)
is nonzero: the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem identifies its underlying vector space with U(n−), and v=1⊗1 is a primitive element of weight ω. Its weight spaces are finite dimensional, its top weight space is the line Cv, and all other weights are ω−∑ni​αi​ with ni​≥0 and at least one positive coefficient.
A proper submodule cannot contain v, because v generates M(ω). More strongly it has no component of weight ω: any finite sum of distinct weight vectors can be projected onto its individual components by a polynomial in a generic element of H. Therefore the sum N of all proper submodules still misses the top weight line and is proper. It contains every proper submodule, so the irreducible quotient of a Verma module
L(ω)=M(ω)/N​
(14)
is irreducible and retains the nonzero primitive element v+N. This constructs the requested representation for every ω∈H∗. It is not asserted to be finite dimensional for arbitrary ω.
For the sl2 Lie algebra, use h=(10​0−1​), e=(00​10​) and f=(01​00​), with [h,e]=2e, [h,f]=−2f, [e,f]=h. The classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations gives one irreducible Vn​ for every integer n≥0. In a basis v0​,…,vn​ its action is
hvj​=(n−2j)vj​,fvj​=vj+1​,evj​=j(n−j+1)vj−1​,
(15)
where v−1​=vn+1​=0. These formulas satisfy the three Lie brackets. Any nonzero invariant subspace contains a weight vector by polynomial projection using h; repeated application of e gives v0​, and applications of f then give the entire basis. Thus Vn​ is irreducible.
Conversely, in any finite-dimensional irreducible module, start with an eigenvector of h and apply e until reaching a nonzero vector v with ev=0. This process terminates because e increases the eigenvalue by two and only finitely many eigenvalues occur. Write hv=av. The sl2 highest-weight lowering formula gives
hfjv=(a−2j)fjv,efjv=j(a−j+1)fj−1v.
(16)
Let fnv=0 and fn+1v=0, which again follows from the finite set of weights. Applying e to the latter identity yields (n+1)(a−n)fnv=0, so a=n. The resulting n+1 distinct weight vectors span an invariant subspace, hence the entire irreducible module. Therefore dimVn​=n+1 and its primitive weight is ωn​(h)=n, with n=0,1,2,…; its weights are n,n−2,…,−n.

The complexification of a Lie algebra L0​ is L=L0​⊗R​C, with the Lie bracket extended complex-bilinearly. Equivalently write L=L0​⊕iL0​, where
[x+iy,z+iw]=[x,z]−[y,w]+i([x,w]+[y,z]).
(17)
Complex conjugation is an antilinear map and a Lie algebra automorphism whose fixed subalgebra is L0​.
If the solvable radical of L0​ is nonzero, its complexification is a nonzero solvable ideal of L. Conversely the solvable radical R of L is preserved by complex conjugation, because it is the unique largest solvable ideal. Consequently
R=R0​⊕iR0​,R0​=R∩L0​:
(18)
for z∈R, both (z+z)/2 and (z−z)/(2i) belong to R0​. If R=0, R0​=0 is a solvable ideal of L0​. This proves L0​ is semisimple if and only if L0​⊗R​C is semisimple. Consistently, the complex Killing form is just the complex-bilinear extension of the real one; its determinant in a real basis is unchanged by extending scalars.
Take the sl2R Lie algebra sl2​(R) and the special unitary Lie algebra su(2). Both complexify to sl2​(C). This is immediate for the former; for the latter, the real basis ih,e−f,i(e+f) consists of traceless matrices that are skew-Hermitian matrices and is also a complex basis of sl2​(C).
They are not isomorphic as real Lie algebras. Their Killing forms are B(X,Y)=4tr(XY), but on su(2) this is negative definite. On sl2​(R) the basis h,e+f,e−f has a diagonal Gram matrix with entries 8,8,−8, so the signature of a quadratic form is (2,1). A Lie algebra isomorphism preserves the Killing form and therefore its signature.
A real split semisimple Lie algebra has a Cartan subalgebra whose Adjoint representation of a Lie algebra is simultaneously diagonalizable over R, so its root-space decomposition is defined over R. The example sl2​(R) is split: the diagonal Cartan subalgebra Rh has eigenvalues 0,2,−2 and real root spaces Re,Rf. The example su(2) is not split. Invariance makes every adx skew-adjoint for the positive definite inner product −B, so its eigenvalues are purely imaginary. If it were diagonalizable over R, all these eigenvalues would be zero and adx=0. The center is zero, so only x=0 has this property; no nonzero split Cartan subalgebra exists. Thus sl2​(R) is split and su(2) is not.
