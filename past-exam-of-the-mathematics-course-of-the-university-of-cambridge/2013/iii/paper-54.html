Assume an axisymmetric thin disc rotating in the fixed potential of a dominant central mass, with Ω=(GM∗​)1/2r−3/2 independent of time and height. Neglect vertical mass loss and vertical angular-momentum flux at the two faces, as well as self-gravity and radial pressure corrections to the rotation law. Define the surface density and density-weighted kinematic viscosity by
Σ=∫ρdz,νˉ=Σ1​∫ρνdz,
(1)
and let vr​=Σ−1∫ρur​dz. These assumptions give the vertically averaged viscous disk equations
∂t​Σ+r1​∂r​(rΣvr​)=0,∂t​(Σl)+r1​∂r​(rΣvr​l−r3νˉΣΩ′)=0,
(2)
where l=r2Ω is specific angular momentum. Subtract l times conservation of mass from conservation of angular momentum. Since l is fixed in time,
rΣvr​l′=∂r​(r3νˉΣΩ′),vr​=−Σr1/23​∂r​(r1/2νˉΣ).
(3)
Substitution into conservation of mass proves the Keplerian viscous diffusion equation
∂t​Σ=r3​∂r​[r1/2∂r​(r1/2νˉΣ)].​
(4)
No assumption of height-independent kinematic viscosity is needed; its density-weighted average is the one appearing in the integrated stress. A wind or surface magnetic stress would add terms and must not be silently discarded.

The instantaneous kinematic viscosity law is νˉ(r,Σ), with no explicit constitutive memory. Expand its transported quantity at the possibly evolving background:
νˉ(r,Σ0​+Σ1​)(Σ0​+Σ1​)=νˉ0​Σ0​+∂Σ​(νˉΣ)∣0​Σ1​+O(Σ12​).
(9)

∂t​Σ1​=r3​∂r​[r1/2∂r​(r1/2qνˉ0​Σ1​)].​

For a razor-thin disk, integrate the delta function in height to get
Φϵ​(R)=−G∫∣R−R′∣2+ϵ2​Σ(R′)d2R′​.
(15)
The corresponding horizontal force kernel is proportional to (R−R′)/(∣R−R′∣2+ϵ2)3/2. It removes the short-distance point-force singularity and smooths forces on separations of order ϵ. This is height-evaluation gravitational softening.
To represent finite vertical thickness, choose ϵ of order the disc disk scale height H. There is no universal exact numerical choice: a true vertical mass density profile produces the Fourier reduction factor Σ−1∫ρ(z)e−k∣z∣dz, which is not generally e−kϵ. Matching the long-wave term gives ϵ=⟨∣z∣⟩ρ​, the vertical-profile softening match. For an exponential vertical profile this mean is H, while its full reduction factor is (1+kH)−1.

Let k=∣kx​∣, cs2​=(dP/dΣ)0​ and κ2=2Ω(2Ω−S). Linearize the barotropic closure of a razor-thin disk and shearing sheet about the given state. Write the radial and azimuthal velocity perturbations as u and v. Axisymmetry removes advection by the background shear, giving
−iωΣ1​+ikx​Σ0​u=0,−iωu−2Ωv=−ikx​(Φ1​+Σ0​cs2​Σ1​​),−iωv+(2Ω−S)u=0.
(18)
The softened potential is Φ1​=−2πGΣ1​e−kϵ/k. Eliminating the velocity components for the compressive branch gives the softened Toomre dispersion relation
ω2=κ2−2πGΣ0​ke−kϵ+cs2​k2.​
(19)
The Coriolis/shear combination supplies the radial epicyclic restoring term, pressure supplies the short-wave term, and self-gravity lowers the squared frequency. The full three-variable determinant also has a stationary axisymmetric geostrophic mode, with u=0 and azimuthal flow balancing the pressure-plus-gravity gradient. The displayed relation describes the density-wave pair; eliminating by division by ω must not silently deny that stationary mode.

Assume κ,cs​,Σ0​>0, and use the positive Toomre parameter. The dimensionless dispersion relation is
κ2ω2​=1+Q2s2−2se−δs​.
(20)
At fixed dimensionless gravitational softening δ, maximum growth corresponds to maximizing F(s)=2se−δs−s2. Differentiating gives the most unstable softened disk wavenumber
s∗​=e−δs∗​(1−δs∗​).​
(21)
For δ>0, the right side is strictly decreasing on 0<s<1/δ, while the left side increases; there is exactly one positive solution. The equation implies δs∗​<1 and s∗​<1−δs∗​, so
s∗​<1+δ1​.​

Qc2​=2s∗​e−δs∗​−s∗2​=s∗2​1−δs∗​1+δs∗​​,Q<Qc​ is unstable.​
(23)
The printed description of a minimum Q for instability reverses the threshold: Qc​ is the upper boundary of unstable values and the lower boundary of stable ones. Because s∗​<1/(1+δ)≤1 and 2s−s2 increases on [0,1],
Qc2​<2s∗​−s∗2​<1+δ2​−(1+δ)21​=(1+δ)21+2δ​≤1.

The plot holds Q fixed and changes gravitational softening. If Q is varied physically at fixed κϵ/cs​, remember δ=(κϵ/cs​)/Q: the marginal curve must be evaluated at its corresponding δ, rather than treating these two dimensionless parameters as independently fixed along that physical variation.

The conventional complete-flyby impulse approximation uses a straight incoming path x=x0​, y=−Sx0​t, with S>0. Integrate the satellite's transverse acceleration over the whole encounter. For x0​>0,
Δvx​=−x02​GMs​​∫−∞∞​(1+S2t2)−3/2dt=−Sx02​2GMs​​.
(25)
The hint's integral is one over a half encounter, and two over the full encounter. The leading longitudinal impulse vanishes by oddness. In weak elastic scattering, conservation of the relative speed V=S∣x0​∣ gives the next-order change along the original velocity as −Δvx2​/(2V). With the original outer flow along negative y, this means
Δvy​≃S3x05​2(GMs​)2​.​
(26)
The same signed expression holds for inner particles and has the opposite sign there. This is the complete-flyby gravitational impulse. Its weak-deflection requirement is GMs​/(S2∣x0​∣3)≪1. Rotation and tidal dynamics retained throughout the encounter give a more detailed response; this impulse model does not claim to solve the full Hill scattering problem exactly.
The printed coefficient 1/2 is four times smaller than this complete-flyby value. It is obtained if the single-sided transverse impulse GMs​/(Sx02​) is inserted into the same quadratic longitudinal estimate, leaving out the other half. Thus the scaling and sign agree, but that numerical coefficient is not derived by the usual full-encounter prescription. To keep the subsequent requested formulas unambiguous, write the satellite impulse normalization
Δvy​=χS3x05​(GMs​)2​:χ=21​ for the supplied model,χ=2 for the complete-flyby model.
(27)
The following parts are derived for general χ and specialize to the supplied value. The complete-flyby one-sided torque coefficient 8/27 is also the normalization used in the primary coplanar impulse calculation summarized by Chametla and collaborators.

A strip dx at x>0 passes the satellite at relative speed Sx, so its encounter mass flux is SxΣ(x)dx. Each unit mass gains specific angular momentum r0​Δvy​. Therefore the one-sided impulse torque on a disk is
Γ>0​=χS2r0​(GMs​)2​∫H∞​x4Σ(x)​dx.
(28)
Putting χ=1/2 gives exactly the requested expression. Inner strips have the opposite signed impulse but the same positive encounter flux. The net disc torque is consequently
Γ=χS2r0​(GMs​)2​∫H∞​x4Σ(x)−Σ(−x)​dx.​
(29)
An even surface density makes the integrand vanish pointwise, so the total torque is zero although each one-sided torque can be nonzero. The cutoff must keep the encounters in the weak-deflection regime; an appropriate physical value is at least of order the thickness or the relevant strong-scattering radius. The infinite-sheet integral also assumes convergence or a specified outer truncation.

The linear mass density law is a local Taylor approximation, not a nonnegative mass density on the entire infinite real line. With an outer local cutoff L≫H, the same calculation replaces H−2 by H−2−L−2; the extension to infinity is the leading local result. Small ∣β∣H/r0​ keeps the mass density perturbation small in the dominant encounter region.
Outer particles gain angular momentum and inner particles lose it. The satellite transmits angular momentum from inner to outer material. If β>0, the denser outer side receives the larger torque: the disc gains net angular momentum and the satellite loses it. If β<0, the net transfer reverses. At zero slope these exchanges cancel in the satellite's total torque.
