Write the cubic in the Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve as f(x)=x3+a2​x2+a4​x+a6​. A suitable invariant differential on an elliptic curve is
ω=2ydx​.​
(1)
It is regular and nonzero wherever y=0. At a point with y=0, nonsingularity gives f′(x)=0, and differentiating the equation shows ω=dy/f′(x), again regular and nonzero. At the identity O, use the parameter t=−x/y: the expansions start x=t−2+⋯, y=−t−3+⋯, so ω=(1+⋯)dt. Thus it is a nowhere-vanishing regular differential on the whole smooth projective curve.
Here is a direct proof of translation invariance of a Weierstrass differential. Fix Q=(u,v), let P=(x,y) vary, and write P+Q=(X,Y). On the open set where the chord-and-tangent group law uses an ordinary chord, put h=(y−v)/(x−u). The addition formulas are X=h2−a2​−x−u and Y=h(x−X)−y. The line-intersection identity is
f(T)−(v+h(T−u))2=(T−u)(T−x)(T−X).
(2)
Differentiate this polynomial in T at T=x to obtain f′(x)−2yh=(x−u)(x−X). Differentiating h along the curve now gives dh/dx=(x−X)/(2y), and hence
dxdX​=2hdxdh​−1=yY​,2YdX​=2ydx​.
(3)
This proves translation invariance on a dense open set. Since the translation on an elliptic curve is an automorphism and both sides are regular differentials, it proves invariance everywhere, including the exceptional addition cases. Translation by O is the identity.
Translation invariance also gives μ∗ω=pr1∗​ω+pr2∗​ω for the addition map: its differential on a tangent pair is the sum of the two translated tangent vectors. Therefore [j]∗ω=jω for every integer j.
Let dj​=deg[j], with the zero endomorphism assigned degree zero. The assumed degree identity, applied to [j] and [1], gives
dj+1​+dj−1​=2dj​+2,d0​=0,d1​=1.
(4)
Induction gives deg[j]=j2 for positive j; negation is an automorphism, so the result holds for negative j as well. This is quadratic degree recursion for elliptic multiplication.
For a prime ℓ=p, the invariant differential on an elliptic curve has nonzero pullback under [ℓ], so that map is a separable isogeny. Translation identifies all its fibres and their local multiplicities, so its geometric kernel has exactly its degree ℓ2 distinct points. It is killed by ℓ, and therefore
E(Fp​)[ℓ]≅(Z/ℓZ)2.​
(5)
The algebraic closure is essential here; the assertion is not generally true for the rational-point subgroup over Fp​ itself. This is prime-to-characteristic geometric torsion.
For [p], the pullback differential vanishes. Its total degree is p2, and its inseparable degree is at least p, so its kernel has at most p geometric points. Thus E(Fp​)[p] has dimension at most one over Fp​, while every other prime-torsion subgroup has dimension at most two. The rational-point group is finite and abelian. The structure theorem for finite abelian groups says that the number of cyclic factors of an ℓ-primary component equals the dimension of its subgroup killed by ℓ. Each component therefore has at most two factors. Combining the smaller primary factors into one cyclic group and the larger ones into another yields
E(Fp​)≅Z/mZ×Z/nZ,m∣n.​
(6)
One may also choose p∤m, since the p-primary component is cyclic. This explains two generators for elliptic curves over finite fields.

Put N(x)=x3+x+2, H(x)=x3+2x+1 and R(x)=N(x)/(x−1)2, all in characteristic three. Since N(1)=1, the numerator and denominator have no common factor, and R has degree three. The map x:E→P1 has degree two. Comparing the degrees in x∘ϕ=R∘x gives 2degϕ=3⋅2, so degϕ=3. This is the degree of an isogeny from its x-coordinate map.
In characteristic three, direct differentiation gives
R′(x)=(x−1)3H(x)​.
(7)
The supplied y-coordinate is H(x)y/(x−1)3, so the invariant differential on an elliptic curve satisfies
ϕ∗ω=2H(x)y/(x−1)3R′(x)dx​=ω.
(8)
In particular ϕ is separable. Its dual isogeny satisfies ϕ​∘ϕ=[3]. Since [3]∗ω=0 and ϕ∗ω=ω, the scalar by which ϕ​ pulls back the differential is zero.
Pullback on the elliptic invariant differential is additive for sums of homomorphisms, by the addition identity proved in (a). Consequently
(mϕ+nϕ​)∗ω=mω.
(9)
The differential criterion for separability of an isogeny therefore gives separability exactly when 3∤m, with n arbitrary. Such a map is automatically nonzero. When 3∣m, every nonzero resulting map is inseparable; the zero map is not a separable isogeny. In fact the zero map occurs only at (m,n)=(0,0): equality of the degrees of [m]ϕ and [n]ϕ​ in a nontrivial vanishing relation would force m=±n, and then cancellation would force ϕ=±ϕ​, contradicting their different differential scalars.

In this setting a formal group law means a one-dimensional commutative series F(X,Y)∈R[[X,Y]] with identity zero and
F(X,0)=X,F(0,Y)=Y,F(X,Y)=F(Y,X),F(F(X,Y),Z)=F(X,F(Y,Z)).
(10)
It starts with X+Y; there is a unique inverse series ι(T)=−T+⋯ satisfying F(T,ι(T))=0. For coefficients in Z, the series and its inverse converge on pZp​, making this ideal a topological abelian group under F.
Set a(T)=∂Y​F(T,0)∈1+TZ[[T]], and write a(T)−1=∑j≥0​bj​Tj. The formal logarithm is
L(T)=∫0T​a(U)dU​=T+∑j≥1​j+1bj​​Tj+1∈Q[[T]].
(11)
Differentiate associativity in the third variable at zero. It gives a(F(X,Y))=∂Y​F(X,Y)a(Y), so ∂Y​L(F(X,Y))=L′(Y). Subtracting L(Y) leaves a series independent of Y, whose value at zero is L(X). Thus L(F(X,Y))=L(X)+L(Y).
For odd p, the logarithm converges on I=pZp​: the valuation of its degree-n term is at least n−vp​(n), which tends to infinity. Put u(T)=L(T)−T. For x,y∈I, factor xn−yn to obtain
vp​(nbn−1​​(xn−yn))≥vp​(x−y)+n−1−vp​(n)≥vp​(x−y)+1
(12)
for every n≥2 and odd p. Hence u is a contraction with constant at most 1/p and maps I into pI. The equation L(x)=z is equivalent to x=z−u(x); the contraction mapping theorem gives a unique solution in I for every z∈I. The same estimate shows ∣L(x)−L(y)∣p​=∣x−y∣p​, so the logarithm is a topological group isomorphism. Dividing its values by p gives
F(pZp​)≅(pZp​,+)≅(Zp​,+)(p odd).​
(13)
This is the deep logarithm subgroup of a formal group argument, here with depth one.
For p=2, the degree-two estimate at depth one need not be strict. At depth two, however,
2(n−1)−v2​(n)≥1(n≥2),
(14)
so the same proof gives F(4Z2​)≅(4Z2​,+)≅Z2​. This subgroup has index two in F(2Z2​), because F(x,y)≡x+y(mod4) when x,y∈2Z2​.
Indeed the topological structure of a formal group on twice the 2-adic integers has just two possibilities. Let G=F(2Z2​), let its index-two subgroup H have topological generator h, and choose v∈/H. Write 2v=ch, with c∈Z2​, using group notation. If c is odd, 2v generates H and v generates G, giving G≅Z2​. If c is even, v−(c/2)h has order two and lies outside H, giving G≅H×Z/2Z. The Z2​ multiples are defined by continuity in this compact pro-two group.
For explicit contrasting examples, the formal additive group Fa​(X,Y)=X+Y gives Fa​(2Z2​)≅Z2​, which is a torsion-free group. The formal multiplicative group Fm​(X,Y)=X+Y+XY is identified by x↦1+x with 1+2Z2​. Its element x=−2 corresponds to −1 and has order two. Moreover
1+2Z2​={±1}×(1+4Z2​),
(15)
and the logarithm identifies the second factor with 4Z2​. The two resulting groups are Z2​ and Z/2Z×Z2​, so they are not isomorphic.

An elliptic curve has good reduction of an elliptic curve at p if it admits a Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve over Zp​ whose reduced projective cubic is nonsingular. Equivalently its minimal discriminant is a p-adic unit.
For the given integral short equation,
Δ=−16⋅27⋅2252=−243754.
(16)
It therefore directly supplies good reduction outside 2,3,5. These three primes cannot be rescued by changing the model. An admissible change of Weierstrass model changes the discriminant by a twelfth power, so its valuation changes by a multiple of twelve. The displayed valuations 4,7,4 are not congruent to zero modulo twelve. No integral model can have unit discriminant at any of those primes. The good primes are exactly p∈/{2,3,5}.

The chord from P1​ to P2​ has slope (−3−15)/(−6)=3, so its sum has x-coordinate 32−0−(−6)=15 and y-coordinate 3(0−15)−15=−60. Thus P1​+P2​=(15,−60).
Reduction at the good prime seven gives E:y2=x3+1. For x=0,1,2,3,4,5,6, the right sides are respectively 1,2,2,0,2,0,0. There are respectively 2,2,2,1,2,1,1 choices of y. Including the point at infinity gives #E(F7​)=12. Its nonzero torsion points of an elliptic curve of order two are (3,0),(5,0),(6,0), so it cannot be cyclic. Its two-primary component is (Z/2Z)2 and its three-primary component is cyclic of order three; hence
E(F7​)≅Z/2Z×Z/6Z.​
(18)
In particular its exponent is six.
The reduction of an elliptic curve is a group homomorphism defined on every Q7​-point by projectivity. Thus 6P reduces to the identity for every rational P. A finite point with integral coordinates reduces to an affine point, whose projective last coordinate is one, so it cannot reduce to the identity at infinity. Therefore every nonzero finite point 6P has nonintegral coordinates. If 6P=O, it has no affine coordinates at all. This is the reduction exponent obstruction to integral multiples.

The Lutz–Nagell theorem says that for a nonsingular short equation y2=x3+ax+b with a,b∈Z, every nonidentity rational torsion point of an elliptic curve (x,y) has x,y∈Z, and either y=0 or
y2∣4a3+27b2.​
(19)
This is a necessary condition for torsion, not a converse.
To prove integrality, fix a prime q. If vq​(x)<0, the x3 term dominates the right side of the equation. Hence 2vq​(y)=3vq​(x), so vq​(x)=−2s, vq​(y)=−3s for some s≥1. The parameter t=−x/y has valuation s and identifies the point with the formal neighbourhood of the identity, namely the formal group of an elliptic curve evaluated on qZq​.
For odd q, this group is a torsion-free group by the logarithm argument of Question 2. For q=2, use the special short-model structure. Negation sends t exactly to −t, so the formal multiplication series [2](t) is odd and has integral coefficients:
[2](t)=2t+t3g(t),g(t)∈Z2​[[t]].
(20)
If s=v2​(t)≥1, the first term has valuation s+1 and all the others have valuation at least 3s>s+1. Thus v2​([2](t))=s+1, and no iteration of doubling kills a nonzero point. Multiplication by an odd integer has unit linear coefficient and also cannot kill it. This proves the torsion-free formal subgroup for a short Weierstrass equation, including at two. Consequently a rational torsion point of an elliptic curve cannot have vq​(x)<0 at any prime. Its x is integral, and the equation then makes its rational y integral too.
For the divisibility conclusion suppose y=0. Then 2P=O and is also a rational torsion point of an elliptic curve, so x(2P) is integral. Its tangent slope λ=f′(x)/(2y) satisfies x(2P)=λ2−2x. Thus λ2∈Z; a rational number with integral square is itself integral. In particular y2∣f′(x)2. Reduce the supplied polynomial identity modulo y2=f(x): both terms on the left are divisible by y2, so the right side 4a3+27b2 is too. This is the divisibility proof in the Nagell–Lutz theorem.
For the specific curve, 4a3+27b2=3754. The computed equality 2P1​=−P1​ makes P1​ a point of exact order three. The coordinate y(P3​)=17 has square not dividing 3754, so P3​ has infinite order. To decide P2​, use the already computed point P1​+P2​=(15,−60). Its y-coordinate has even square, which cannot divide the odd number 3754, so that sum has infinite order. Since P1​ is torsion, P2​ must have infinite order as well. P1​ has order three; P2​ and P3​ both have infinite order.

Kummer theory studies extracting roots through Galois actions, and on an elliptic curve the corresponding operation is division of points. The bridge is a cocycle: different Galois conjugates of a chosen division point differ by torsion. Controlling the ramification of these cocycles is what proves the Weak Mordell-Weil theorem.
Classically, for a characteristic-zero field K, the sequence
1⟶μm​⟶K× z↦zm ​K×⟶1
(21)
is exact. Hilbert's theorem 90 gives H1(K,K×)=0, so Galois cohomology identifies
H1(K,μm​)≅K×/(K×)m.
(22)
The class of a is represented by σ↦σ(ma​)/ma​. If μm​⊂K, the module is constant and these are continuous characters into μm​; the associated fields are cyclic Kummer extensions of degree dividing m. Several such classes give abelian extensions of exponent dividing m. Without the roots-of-unity hypothesis the cohomological description still holds, but the action on μm​ must not be discarded.
For an elliptic curve over a number field K, multiplication by m≥2 gives the exact sequence of Galois modules
0⟶E[m]⟶E(K)[m]​E(K)⟶0.
(23)
The map is onto because it is a nonconstant isogeny over an algebraically closed field. For P∈E(K) choose Q∈E(K) with mQ=P, and set
δm​(P)σ​=σQ−Q∈E[m].
(24)
It satisfies δ(P)στ​=δ(P)σ​+σδ(P)τ​, the Galois 1-cocycle identity. Changing Q by a torsion point of an elliptic curve changes the cocycle by a coboundary. If the class is zero, a corresponding change of Q makes it Galois-fixed, so P∈mE(K). Conversely a rational division point gives the zero class. Thus the Kummer map of an elliptic curve is injective on the quotient, and the cohomology sequence gives
0⟶E(K)/mE(K)δm​​H1(K,E[m])⟶H1(K,E)[m]⟶0.​
(25)
This is the Kummer exact sequence of an elliptic curve. The last group parametrizes the appropriate torsor classes. It is important that the entire middle group need not be finite; an unrestricted Kummer class can ramify at arbitrarily many primes.
The Weak Mordell-Weil theorem states E(K)/mE(K) is finite for every number field K and every m≥1. The case m=1 is trivial. To prove the remaining cases, choose a finite set S containing the primes of bad reduction and the primes dividing m. At a prime outside S, the elliptic curve extends to a smooth proper group scheme over the valuation ring, and [m] is finite étale. Properness extends a local rational point to a section; its division fibre is a finite étale torsor over that ring. Therefore its Kummer class is unramified. This is unramified division torsors at good primes, and shows that δm​(E(K)) lies in first Galois cohomology unramified outside a finite set.
Let L=K(E[m]), enlarged if necessary to contain μm​, and include in S the finitely many primes ramified in L/K. This is a finite Galois extension. Over L the torsion module is constant, isomorphic to (Z/mZ)2, and so to two copies of μm​. Restricting a cocycle to GL​ therefore reduces it to two classical Kummer classes unramified outside the primes above S. Such a class is represented by a∈L× satisfying
vp​(a)≡0(modm)(p∈/SL​).
(26)
Write this power-class group as L(SL​,m). The implication follows locally because, away from residue characteristic dividing m, the valuation of an unramified mth-root extension must be divisible by m.
Its finiteness comes from two standard arithmetic finiteness results, rather than from an assertion that all of L×/(L×)m is finite. The valuations outside SL​ associate to a an ideal whose mth power is principal. There is an exact sequence
0⟶OL,SL​×​/(OL,SL​×​)m⟶L(SL​,m)⟶Cl(OL,SL​​)[m]⟶0.
(27)
The left group is finite by the finite generation of the S-unit group, and the right group is finite by finiteness of the ideal class group. This proves finiteness of S-unramified Kummer classes. Hence there are only finitely many restricted cocycles over L. The kernel of restriction from K is contained in H1(Gal(L/K),E[m]), which is finite because both group and module are finite. The restricted cohomology group over K is therefore finite, and its injected subgroup E(K)/mE(K) is finite too. This completes a Kummer-theoretic proof of the weak Mordell-Weil theorem.
For arithmetic computation one adds local conditions. The Selmer group of an elliptic curve consists of classes in H1(K,E[m]) whose restriction at every completion Kv​ lies in the image of the corresponding local Kummer map. They are unramified outside the same finite set, so this group is finite. The failure of a everywhere locally soluble torsor to have a global point is measured by the Tate–Shafarevich group, defined as the kernel of the local restriction map on H1(K,E). The resulting exact sequence is
0⟶E(K)/mE(K)⟶Sel(m)(E/K)⟶Sha(E/K)[m]⟶0.
(28)
Thus the Selmer group of an elliptic curve gives a computable upper bound for the quotient; actual rational points supply lower bounds. Equality need not follow just from local solubility. Finiteness of this fixed torsion subgroup does not prove finiteness of the whole Tate–Shafarevich group.
Finally, weak finiteness alone is not finite generation: the additive group Q has trivial quotients by multiplication and is not finitely generated. The additional ingredient for the Mordell-Weil theorem is height descent. Choose representatives Pi​ of E(K)/mE(K) and write P=Pi​+mQ. The quadratic growth of the canonical height of an elliptic curve, together with bounds for translation by the finitely many Pi​, makes Q have smaller height whenever P is sufficiently large. Northcott theorem gives only finitely many points of bounded height, and repeated descent yields a finite generating set. This explains the roles of Kummer theory, local information and heights without conflating the weak and full theorems.

A candidate d occurs precisely when the quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent
N2=dU4+aU2V2+(b/d)V4

Test these finitely many coverings over R and over relevant Qq​ to eliminate impossible classes; surviving locally soluble classes give an upper bound. Finding rational points on them proves membership and often makes the bound exact. Local solubility alone need not prove global solubility, so one must retain the possible Selmer obstruction.
For the illustration take
Ep​:y2=x3−p2x,Ep′​:Y2=X3+4p2X,
(36)
with p≡3(mod8). On Ep​ all possible square classes are 1,−1,p,−p. They all occur: O supplies 1 and the three points with y=0, at x=0,p,−p, supply −1,p,−p. Hence ∣α(Ep​)∣=4.
On Ep′​, a nonzero real point has X>0, because X(X2+4p2)=Y2. Thus only d=1,2,p,2p need be considered, and d=1 occurs already at O. The other three classes are excluded as follows.
For d=p, the covering is N2=p(U4+4V4). Reducing modulo p forces p∣N and U4+4V4≡0. If V were divisible by p, then U would be too, contradicting primitivity. Otherwise (U2/(2V2))2≡−1(modp), impossible since p≡3(mod4). For d=2p, the equation is N2=2p(U4+V4), which similarly forces (U2/V2)2≡−1(modp). Thus neither class occurs. These are odd-prime obstructions for the congruent-number isogeny coverings.
For d=2, the equation is N2=2(U4+p2V4). If exactly one of U,V is odd, its right side is 2(mod8), not a square. If both are odd, their fourth powers are 1(mod16) and p2≡9(mod16); hence the right side is 20(mod32), also not a square. Both even is forbidden by primitivity. This is the modulo-thirty-two obstruction for a congruent-number descent class. Consequently ∣α′(Ep′​)∣=1, and the rank formula gives
rankEp​(Q)=0.​
(37)
To complete the congruent-number conclusion, also determine torsion. At a good prime q≡3(mod4), the character sum for x3−p2x cancels in pairs x,−x, so #Ep​(Fq​)=q+1. Rational torsion injects into good reduction at every odd prime, by the formal kernel, which is a torsion-free group. If p=3, use q=3,7 to bound its order by gcd(4,8)=4. If p=3, use q=7,11, giving gcd(8,12)=4. The three rational points of order two already give four points, so Ep​(Q)≅(Z/2Z)2. This is rational torsion of a congruent number curve in the present cases.
A congruent number is the positive area of a right triangle with rational side lengths. A point (x,y) on Ep​ with y=0 would give such a triangle with sides
A=​yx2−p2​​,B=​y2px​​,C=​yx2+p2​​,
(38)
for which A2+B2=C2 and AB/2=p. Conversely a rational right triangle of area p gives a point with nonzero y, for example x=p(C+A)/B, y=2p2(C+A)/B2. But every rational point on the curve just determined is O or has y=0. Every prime p≡3(mod8) is therefore not a congruent number.
