Differentiating the velocity potential gives u=aωekzcos(kx−ωt) and w=aωekzsin(kx−ωt). Let A=aekz and ϑ=kx−ωt. To first order in the wave steepness ka, evaluate these velocities at the initial parcel position and integrate from time zero:
X−x=A[sin(kx)−sinϑ]+O(a2k),Z−z=A[cosϑ−cos(kx)]+O(a2k).​
(1)
These expressions satisfy both initial conditions and distinguish initial and mean parcel labels in a surface wave. Their actual short-time limits are
X−x=aωekzcos(kx)t+O(t2),Z−z=aωekzsin(kx)t+O(t2).​
(2)
The printed expressions omit the integration constants and therefore cannot be the small-time displacement from the prescribed initial point. For example, at x=0, they give Z(0)−z=aekz=0. This is a genuine inconsistency, rather than a missing step in the calculation.
The intended oscillatory orbit is recovered by using mean parcel coordinates instead. Set xc​=x+Asin(kx) and zc​=z−Acos(kx) to this order. Then
X−xc​≃−aekzc​sin(kxc​−ωt),Z−zc​≃aekzc​cos(kxc​−ωt).
(3)
Thus a deep-water gravity wave produces approximately circular parcel orbits with radius aekzc​, decaying exponentially with depth. The approximation is one of small amplitude over a wave cycle, not an assertion that these oscillatory coordinates vanish at time zero.

The leading Stokes drift comes from evaluating the oscillatory velocity at the displaced particle position. Taylor expansion gives
u(X,Z,t)−u(x,z,t)=(X−x)ux​+(Z−z)uz​+O(a3k2ω).
(4)
Here ux​=−akωekzsinϑ and uz​=akωekzcosϑ. Inserting the initial-position displacements from the preceding solution yields
u(X,Z,t)−u(x,z,t)=a2kωe2kz[1−cos(ωt)]+O(a3k2ω).​
(5)
In particular the instantaneous difference is zero at t=0, as it must be. The unaveraged constant equality in the PDF is incompatible with its initial labels. Averaging over a period T=2π/ω removes the oscillatory term:
us​≡⟨u(X,Z,t)−u(x,z,t)⟩=a2kωe2kz.​
(6)
Equivalently, using mean parcel labels and the purely oscillatory displacements gives a2kωe2kz(sin2ϑ+cos2ϑ) at this order. This recovers the intended constant result. The period-mean drift is in the wave-propagation direction and decreases as e2kz. The corresponding second-order vertical difference is a2kωe2kzsin(ωt) for initial labels and has zero mean. The resulting horizontal displacement per cycle is us​T; closed first-order circles do not imply zero second-order transport.

For the positive-frequency internal gravity wave branch, ω=NKh​/K. Differentiating with respect to the components of the wave vector gives the group velocity,
cg​=(Kh​K3Nkm2​,Kh​K3Nlm2​,−K3NKh​m​).​
(10)
The wavefront-normal phase velocity is cp​=ωk/K2. Directly,
k⋅cg​=Kh​K3Nm2(k2+l2)​−K3NKh​m2​=0,cp​⋅cg​=0.​
(11)
The negative-frequency branch changes both propagation signs but not their orthogonality. Another proof is that ω is homogeneous of degree zero in the wave vector, so Euler's homogeneous-function identity gives k⋅∇k​ω=0. Energy propagates along constant-phase surfaces, rather than normal to them. When m=0, ω=N and cg​=0; the scalar-product result remains true, but there is no nonzero group-velocity direction to describe geometrically.

Take k>0 without loss of generality, so α=kh/2>0, and require decay as Z→±∞. The base velocity is U~=Z everywhere; there is no jump in its derivative. Let C=c~ and E=e−2α. A decaying representation that already solves the layer equations is
ψ~​=Ae−α∣Z+1∣+Be−α∣Z−1∣.
(19)
It is continuous at both interfaces. At Z=−1 the value is A+BE and the derivative jump is −2αA; at Z=1 they are B+AE and −2αB. The density anomaly drops by one across each interface. The jump conditions for stratified inviscid shear flow therefore reduce to
(2α(1+C)2−J−JE​−JE2α(1−C)2−J​)(AB​)=0.​
(20)
For nonreal C the displacement denominators do not vanish. Neutral limiting values are obtained by continuation, rather than by dividing by zero at an interface.
Set q=J/(2α). A nonzero mode requires the determinant to vanish:
[(1+C)2−q][(1−C)2−q]−q2E2=0.
(21)
Expansion gives the dispersion relation for two density interfaces in uniform shear,
C4−(2+αJ​)C2+4α2(2α−J)2−J2e−4α​=0.​
(22)
Uniform shear throughout the exterior is important: replacing it with constant exterior velocities would introduce vorticity jumps and give a different dispersion relation.

For stable density jumps, J>0 and q=J/(2α)>0. Let y=C2. The two roots of the dispersion relation for two density interfaces in uniform shear are
y±​=1+q±4q+q2E2​,E=e−2α.
(23)
They are real, and y+​>0. An exponentially growing normal mode exists precisely when y−​<0, giving a pair of imaginary phase speeds; the member with positive imaginary part grows in eik(x−ct) for k>0.
Since y+​y−​=(1−q)2−q2E2, this condition is ∣1−q∣<qE. Solving both inequalities yields
1+E1​<q<1−E1​,1+e−2α2α​<J<1−e−2α2α​.​
(24)
At either endpoint y−​=0, so the exponential growth rate vanishes. Outside the open band both squared phase speeds are nonnegative and the interfacial modes have real frequencies. This is a modal instability test for the stably stratified configuration; unstable density inversions would require a separate analysis. Within the band the dimensional growth rate is kΔU−y−​​/2.

For large α, the evanescent interaction between the two interfaces is proportional to E=e−2α. Ignoring it first gives isolated interfacial gravity waves with phase speeds
C=−1±q​at the lower interface,C=1±q​at the upper interface.
(25)
The lower wave propagating positively relative to its local current and the upper wave propagating negatively have equal laboratory speed when
−1+q​=1−q​,q=1 or J=2α.​
(26)
Their common speed is zero. This is counterpropagating wave instability: the interfaces communicate through their decaying velocity fields, and near resonance their wave phases can lock while extracting energy from the mean shear.
The instability interval has asymptotic endpoints J±​=2α[1±E+O(E2)] and width
J+​−J−​∼4αe−2α.​
(27)
Near exact resonance the interaction is weak but destabilizing: at q=1, C−2​=2−4+E2​=−E2/4+O(E4), so ImC∼E/2. Thus the resonant band narrows and growth weakens exponentially with separation measured in decay lengths. The resonance here is between two density interfaces in globally uniform shear, not a resonance modified by additional vorticity jumps.

Let h be the water-layer thickness and z=0 the horizontal bedrock. In the hydrostatic approximation, with constant ice-interface pressure,
p(x,z,t)=p0​+ρw​g[h(x,t)−z],px​=ρw​ghx​.
(28)
The prescribed two-wall stress balance gives
δv​2μu​=−ρw​ghhx​,u=−Dhhx​,q=hu=−Dh2hx​,D=2μρw​gδv​​>0.​
(29)
In this subglacial current with constant viscous wall layers, the uniform-pressure ice roof is treated as a movable confining boundary; the closure neglects inertia in comparison with pressure and wall stress. The bulk may be turbulent, while the specified wall viscous boundary layer supplies the linear stress relation. A different turbulent drag law would give a different transport coefficient and similarity exponent.
Without melting, the depth-integrated continuity equation gives ht​+qx​=0, hence
ht​=D∂x​(h2hx​).​
(30)
To write this in the PDF's displayed plus-sign form, its constant must be
γ=−D=−2μρw​gδv​​.​
(31)
The signed value is essential. If the printed γ were interpreted as a positive diffusivity, its equation would drive water uphill and be backward parabolic. For example, linearizing around a uniform depth H would give a Fourier perturbation growth rate +γH2k2, whereas the derived physical equation damps it at −DH2k2. The plus-sign form is consistent only with the negative γ above.

Use the positive transport coefficient D from the preceding solution. The porous medium equation here is ht​=D(h2hx​)x​=(D/3)(h3)xx​. A planar pulse conserves the water cross-sectional area V=∫hdx. If the supplied lake volume is a three-dimensional volume V, introduce the constant out-of-plane width By​ and take V=V/By​; alternatively V may be understood as volume per unit span. A two-dimensional model cannot determine an absolute extent from an unspecified three-dimensional volume alone.
For a symmetric localized release, write h=t−af(x/tb). Area conservation requires a=b, and balancing the PDE powers gives a+1=3a+2b, hence a=b=1/4. With η=x/t1/4, the profile equation is
−41​(f+ηf′)=D(f2f′)′.
(32)
Integrate once, using symmetry and zero central flux: Df2f′=−ηf/4. Inside the wet region this gives f2=C−η2/(4D). The dry continuation is zero. Write the result as
h(x,t)=H(t)[1−L(t)2x2​]+1/2​,L(t)=2(πV​)1/2(Dt)1/4,H(t)=(πV​)1/2(Dt)−1/4.​
(33)
The normalization follows from ∫−LL​hdx=πHL/2=V. This cubic diffusion pulse has finite support −L<x<L, total extent 2L, and spreading speed L˙=L/(4t). Although hx​ is singular at the ideal nose, the flux vanishes there and u=−Dhhx​=x/(4t) tends to the finite front speed.
For a one-sided pulse on 0<x<L with a reflecting boundary at zero and the same area V, replace V in the displayed full-line formulas by 2V. The power laws are unchanged. Let Kw​ be the water thermal conductivity, Lm​ the latent heat of fusion per unit ice mass, and ρi​ the ice density. Define vm​>0 as the normal ice-retreat speed that enlarges the cavity. With the water thermal boundary layer at Ts​ on its warm side and Tm​ at the melting interface, the heat supply per unit interface area is approximately
qT​≃Kw​δT​Ts​−Tm​​.
(34)
The ice is specified to be uniformly at Tm​, so no leading sensible-heat flux into colder ice must be subtracted. The Stefan condition is therefore ρi​Lm​vm​=qT​, giving
vm​≃ρi​Lm​δT​Kw​(Ts​−Tm​)​,δT​∼δv​.​
(35)
Equivalently Kw​=ρw​cp​κT​ expresses the heat flux in terms of water thermal diffusivity. The imposed temperatures and constant boundary-layer thickness make this melt rate uniform and constant over the wetted interface. Melting adds latent energy demand to the flow; for an isolated finite pulse, maintaining Ts​ indefinitely would require heat replenishment. The constant-temperature model is consequently an imposed closure, not a prediction of a permanently hot finite water volume.

In the shallow, equal-density approximation, melting supplies water at rate vm​ per unit wetted area. Combining this source with the pressure-driven volume flux gives the melting-source gravity-current equation,
ht​+qx​=vm​,q=−Dh2hx​,ht​=D(h2hx​)x​+vm​​
(36)
inside the wet region, with no melting source ahead of the front. In the PDF's signed convention this is ht​+γ(h2hx​)x​=vm​. If ice and water densities are distinguished, the meltwater-volume source is sm​=(ρi​/ρw​)vm​; the same equations then use sm​. Geometrical excavation of ice has rate vm​, so a density difference requires the confining ice/water-volume mechanics to be treated consistently. The equal-density shallow model identifies these two volumes.
Let x−​(t)<x+​(t) bound the current, and A(t)=∫x−​x+​​hdx be its volume per span. The moving-boundary integral of continuity is
A˙=sm​(x+​−x−​)−q(x+​)+q(x−​)+h(x+​)x˙+​−h(x−​)x˙−​.​
(37)
This exhibits both the melting contribution and any volume swept out by an advancing boundary. For a dry zero-height nose at each end, with no flux through those ends, the boundary terms vanish and
A˙=sm​L(t),A¨=sm​L˙(t),L=x+​−x−​,​
(38)
where the second relation uses constant sm​. For a symmetric pulse L=2L, these become A˙=2sm​L and A¨=2sm​L˙; on a reflecting half-line they are sm​L and sm​L˙. Multiplication by By​ gives the three-dimensional water volume.
The meltwater production over an advancing footprint can equivalently be expressed as Am​(t)=sm​∫0t​L(t′)dt′. At an individual position melting begins only at its wetting time, so new area contributes no finite melt thickness at the instant of arrival. Advancement enlarges the area subsequently melting and hence increases the volume-production rate. The fixed-volume similarity from part (b) cannot simply be reused after adding this source.

Use the planar geometry indicated by the supplied x,z equation and streamfunction; the source heat input is then measured per unit out-of-plane span. Let Θ=T−T0​ and define
β=μρ0​gαΠ​>0,w=βΘ.
(39)
This is the buoyancy part of Darcy law. Let Ch​ denote the constant heat capacity per volume consistent with the given transport equation; for the fluid heat-flux convention Ch​=ρ0​cp​. Porosity or matrix heat-storage factors, if retained, must be used consistently in this coefficient and the effective transport parameters.
For plume width b≪z, continuity gives u∼wb/z. Both uΘx​ and wΘz​ are consequently of order wΘ/z: lateral advection must not be discarded. Lateral thermal diffusion is of order κΘ/b2, while vertical diffusion is smaller by (b/z)2. The slender steady porous thermal plume model is therefore
uΘx​+wΘz​=κΘxx​,ux​+wz​=0,w=βΘ.​
(40)
Writing it in conservative form,
∂z​(wΘ)+∂x​(uΘ−κΘx​)=0,
(41)
