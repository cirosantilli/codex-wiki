Actions and equations. Use a mostly-plus Minkowski metric and define the induced worldsheet metric hab​=∂a​X⋅∂b​X. For a nondegenerate timelike worldsheet, the Nambu–Goto action is
SNG​=−T∫d2σ−deth​.
(1)
Varying the independent metric in the Polyakov action sets its worldsheet stress tensor to zero:
hab​−21​γab​γcdhcd​=0.
(2)
In two dimensions this says hab​=Ω2γab​ for a positive local factor. The factor drops out of −γ​γab, and substitution gives the Nambu–Goto action. Conversely, any nondegenerate induced metric solves the auxiliary-metric equation up to a Weyl transformation. This classical equivalence of Polyakov and Nambu–Goto actions is a statement about classical embeddings; quantum equivalence additionally requires treatment of the metric measure and anomaly.
Variation of Xm in the metric action, followed by elimination of γ, gives the Nambu–Goto equations of motion
∂a​(−h​hab∂b​Xm)=0.​
(3)
A closed string has periodic X and no spatial endpoint variation.
For a background metric G, Kalb–Ramond field b, and dilaton Φ, one consistent Lorentzian convention is
SL​=−4πα′1​∫d2σ−γ​γabGmn​(X)∂a​Xm∂b​Xn+4πα′1​∫d2σϵabbmn​(X)∂a​Xm∂b​Xn−4π1​∫d2σ−γ​Φ(X)R(2),T=2πα′1​.
(4)
The orientation fixes the two-form sign; here the Lorentzian curvature convention is chosen so Wick rotation gives the positive Euclidean dilaton term SE,Φ​=∫γ​ΦR(2)/(4π). This avoids hiding the convention in the topology argument. For constant vacuum value Φ0​, the Gauss-Bonnet theorem gives SE,Φ​=Φ0​χ. A connected closed oriented surface of genus g has Euler characteristic χ=2−2g, so
e−SE,Φ​=e−Φ0​(2−2g)=gs2g−2​,gs​=eΦ0​.​
(5)
The connected vacuum amplitude has a string genus expansion ∑g≥0​gs2g−2​Ag​; disconnected vacuum diagrams exponentiate the connected sum. Each additional handle supplies a factor gs2​. This is dilaton Euler-characteristic weighting.
The rotating circle. For the specified embedding, the squared spatial speed and tangent length are both 1/2, and their spatial inner product is zero. Including X0=t therefore gives
hab​=(−1/20​01/2​),−h​=1/2.​
(6)
The metric is constant. Its equation reduces to X¨−X′′=0, satisfied by the left- and right-moving trigonometric components and the linear time component. On a constant-time slice with 0≤σ<2π, the proper length of a string is
L=∫02π​hσσ​​dσ=2​π.​
(7)
It is time-independent.
Let R(θ) be an ordinary two-dimensional rotation matrix. Rotate the first coordinate pair by R(t) and the second by R(−t). In these time-dependent Cartesian coordinates both pairs become (cosσ,sinσ)/2. A further fixed orthogonal change of basis gives
Y1​=2​Xrot1​+Xrot3​​=2​cosσ​,Y2​=2​Xrot2​+Xrot4​​=2​sinσ​,Y3​=Y4​=0.
(8)
Thus every spatial slice is a planar circle of radius 1/2​; its plane rotates in the ambient four-space. This is a rigid circular string in four spatial dimensions, not a pulsating circle. The rotating axes establish its spatial shape, not a transformation to an inertial spacetime frame.
The momentum density obtained from the Nambu–Goto action is Pm​=−T−h​hta∂a​Xm​. Here P0​=−T, hence the conserved target-space energy is
E=−∫02π​P0​dσ=2πT=2​TL.​
(9)
The material velocity is transverse to the tangent and has magnitude 1/2​. Its Lorentz factor is 2​, so the excess over TL is kinetic energy. The spatial circle being stationary in rotating axes does not eliminate this energy.
Constraints and endpoints. Varying the multipliers in the Nambu-Goto phase-space action imposes
C=21​(P2+T2X′2)=0,D=P⋅X′=0.
(10)
These are first-class constraints generating normal and tangential worldsheet diffeomorphisms. They remove the two longitudinal embedding degrees of freedom; they are not extra physical force laws. Hamilton's equations are
X˙m=ePm+uX′m,P˙m​=(eT2Xm′​+uPm​)′.​
(11)
For an open string, integration by parts leaves the spatial boundary variation
δSend​=−∫dt[(eT2Xm′​+uPm​)δXm]σ=0σ=π​.
(12)
Allowed variational open-string boundary conditions must make this vanish for every permitted endpoint displacement, and be preserved by the evolution. The bracketed expression is the open-string endpoint momentum flux. For free displacements, set that flux to zero. In a boundary-preserving gauge with u=0 and finite nonzero e, this is the Neumann boundary condition X′m=0 at each end. This shows that free-end string boundary conditions are consistent.
At such an end, C=0 gives P2=0, and X˙=eP gives X˙2=0. For a nontrivial endpoint trajectory with nonzero time velocity, its spatial speed is therefore one. This null motion of a free string endpoint follows from the boundary condition together with the constraints, not merely from the bulk wave equation. The induced metric may degenerate right at a free end; the result is understood as the endpoint limit or in the Polyakov description.
For a fixed spatial p-plane, split coordinates into XA along its (p+1)-dimensional worldvolume, including time, and Xi transverse to it. Set X′A=0 and Xi=yi at the ends. Tangential variations are free and their momentum flux vanishes; normal variations vanish, so their flux need not vanish. Evolution preserves the fixed normal values with Pi=0 at the boundary in the same gauge. These mixed Neumann boundary conditions and Dirichlet boundary conditions consistently restrict the endpoint worldlines to the plane.
The massless open-string excitations then separate into a gauge vector along the worldvolume and transverse scalar fields. A scalar displacement changes the corresponding yi; its interpretation is a fluctuation of the plane's embedding. These worldvolume fields from open-string massless states support the interpretation as a dynamical planar D-brane. In the bosonic theory the still lower ground state is a tachyon, indicating an unstable brane; the interpretation does not require pretending that this tachyon is a stable massless field. Appropriate superstring sectors can remove that instability.

First-class reduction. A regular set of independent constraints is first class when their Poisson brackets vanish on the constraint surface, equivalently locally {φi​,φj​}=Cij​kφk​. The coefficients can be functions on phase space. With the generator G=∑i​ξiφi​, an infinitesimal canonical gauge transformation is
δF={F,G}.​
(13)
Each independent first-class condition removes one phase-space dimension, and quotienting its independent gauge orbit removes another. Thus the regular physical phase space has
dimΓphys​=2N−2n.​
(14)
This regular first-class phase-space reduction requires independent constraints and gauge directions. The count can fail at singular strata or for reducible constraints; first-class closure alone does not guarantee independence. For a concrete counterexample to counting redundant equations, take two canonical pairs and φ1​=p1​, φ2​=2p1​. Both equations are first class, but there is only one independent condition and one gauge direction. The surviving pair (q2,p2​) has dimension two, whereas blindly substituting N=n=2 gives zero.
Oscillator symplectic form. The center-of-mass pair (xm,pm​) describes translations and total momentum. The nonzero Fourier coefficients αkm​ describe the standing-wave modes compatible with free-end Neumann boundary conditions; their reality condition is α−k​=αk∗​, and α0m​=2α′​pm in the standard normalization. For k>0, regarding αkm​ as a complex coordinate makes its conjugate momentum iα−k,m​/k. Equivalently write αk​=(qk​+isk​)/2​. Up to a total derivative, its kinetic term is −qk​⋅s˙k​/k, a real canonical form. Inverting this oscillator symplectic form of an open string gives
{xm,pn​}=δmn​,{αjm​,αkn​}=−ijηmnδj+k,0​.​
(15)
The zero oscillator commutes with nonzero oscillators but is not independent of p: {xm,α0n​}=2α′​ηmn. Brackets between the center pair and independent nonzero oscillators vanish.
The quadratic constraints obey
{αkm​,Ln​}=−ikαk+nm​.
(16)
For example, the two terms in the bracket with Ln​=21​∑j​αj​⋅αn−j​ give equal contributions after relabelling j. Applying this identity to both factors of Lm​ gives
{Lm​,Ln​}=−i(m−n)Lm+n​.​
(17)
This classical Virasoro constraint algebra has no central term and closes on the constraints, hence is first class. For G=∑n​ξ−n​Ln​, the oscillator gauge transformation is
δαkm​=−ikn∑​ξ−n​αk+nm​.​
(18)
Reality is respected when ξ−n​=ξn∗​.
Light-cone reduction and mass. Choose light-cone coordinates X±=(X0±XD−1)/2​; a vector square is −2α+α−+α2, with D−2 transverse components. On the proposed gauge slice αk=0+​=0, the variation becomes
δαk+​=−ikξk​α0+​.
(19)
For α0+​=0, every nonzero-mode gauge parameter has an invertible coefficient, so the conditions locally fix the corresponding gauge freedom. Globally this is the usual patch in which X+ is an admissible worldsheet clock; it is not a claim about strings for which that coordinate has turning points. The zero-mode reparameterization is left over.
On that slice the nonzero Virasoro constraints are linear in the longitudinal oscillators:
αk−​=2α0+​1​j∑​αj​⋅αk−j​,k=0.​
(20)
No nonzero longitudinal oscillator remains independent. The residual action is
Sred​=∫dt[x˙mpm​+∑k>0​ki​α−k​⋅α˙k​−λ0​(α′p2+∑k>0​α−k​⋅αk​)].
(21)
This residual mass-shell action in light-cone string gauge displays the remaining zero-mode constraint. At the quantum level normal ordering replaces its oscillator term by N−a. One can further set x+ equal to time and solve for the light-cone Hamiltonian p−=[p2+(N−a)/α′]/(2p+).
Canonical quantization gives the transverse relations
[αjI​,αkJ​]=jδIJδj+k,0​.​
(22)
Here ℏ=1, αk>0​ annihilate the oscillator vacuum, and α−k​ create excitations. Define akI​=αkI​/k​ for k>0. The string level operator is
N=∑k>0,I​α−kI​αkI​=∑k>0,I​kakI†​akI​.
(23)
Thus it counts oscillator number weighted by mode number, and has nonnegative integer eigenvalues. The residual mass-shell condition gives
M2=−p2=α′N−a​,α′=2πT1​.​
(24)
The constant a is the normal-ordering constant of a string, or intercept, not an extra classical tension. In the usual Lorentz-invariant critical bosonic string, level one carries the D−2 transverse polarizations of a massless vector. A massive vector would need D−1 polarizations; the longitudinal one is not present. Lorentz consistency therefore requires that this vector level be massless, giving a=1. Equivalently the regularized transverse zero-point value a=(D−2)/24 gives D=26. This critical-vector assumption in the string intercept argument concerns the usual critical quantum theory. In D=3 the lone transverse level-one polarization transforms trivially under the transverse rotation group; a massive scalar interpretation is not excluded by counting. Thus the stated masslessness conclusion is not a consequence of polarization counting for arbitrary D. An arbitrary intercept in a transverse oscillator model also need not define the usual covariant critical theory.
The full self-dual massless spectrum. In the closed-string sector, N and N are the independent nonnegative integer oscillator levels of the two chiral sectors. The integer n quantizes center momentum around the circle, pcircle​=n/R, and w counts how many times the string winds it. At the self-dual circle R=α′​, zero mass requires
2(N+N−2)+n2+w2=0,N−N=nw.
(25)
Both levels are nonnegative, so their sum is at most two. Exhausting these possibilities gives the massless spectrum at the bosonic self-dual circle:
  • N=N=1, (n,w)=(0,0): all states α−1I​α−1J​∣0;n=0,w=0⟩, with arbitrary transverse polarizations.
  • N=1,N=0, (n,w)=(1,1) or (−1,−1): one left-sector level-one oscillator with any transverse polarization.
  • N=0,N=1, (n,w)=(1,−1) or (−1,1): one right-sector level-one oscillator with any transverse polarization.
  • N=N=0, (n,w)=(2,0),(−2,0),(0,2),(0,−2): four oscillator ground states made massless by their momentum or winding energy.
The last family is easy to miss because the uncompactified ground state is a tachyon; its positive compact energy cancels that negative contribution at these charges. There are no other possibilities: at level sum one, n2+w2=2 forces both charges to be ±1; at sum zero, n2+w2=4 gives exactly the four listed pairs. With d=D−2 transverse oscillators the number of independent massless polarizations is d2+4d+4=D2, hence 676 in the critical D=26 bosonic theory. The circle oscillator is included among the d components; it should not be discarded when interpreting the lower-dimensional scalar states.

In the usual limiting a=1/2 theory it is the unprojected NS tachyon, removed by the standard GSO projection. Calling it tachyonic before specifying the sign of a would be too strong.
The half-level vector and its norm. Let ∣A;p⟩=Am​(p)b−1/2m​∣0;p⟩. The level is NNS​=1/2. Its L0​ condition and the only potentially nonzero positive supercurrent condition give

Its matter norm is proportional to A∗⋅A. For a real, nonzero on-shell momentum in ordinary D≥2 Minkowski space, three cases exhaust the possibilities:
  • If a<1/2, then p is timelike and its orthogonal complement is Euclidean. In its rest frame p⋅A=0 sets A0=0, so every nonzero physical polarization has positive norm.
  • If a=1/2, then p is null. Choose p=E(1,0,…,0,1), E=0. Transversality sets AD−1=A0, leaving A∗⋅A=∑I=1D−2​∣AI∣2≥0. Polarizations proportional to p are null.
  • If a>1/2, then p is spacelike. A frame with p=(0,k,0,…) allows A=(1,0,…), which satisfies p⋅A=0 but has norm −1. It is an explicit negative-norm physical polarization at this level.
Thus, with the usual nonzero particle-momentum hypothesis,
all physical half-level vector polarizations have nonnegative norm⟺a≤21​.​
(57)
This is a level-specific result, not a proof of the full NS no-ghost theorem in arbitrary dimension.
At the limiting intercept the vector becomes massless, and the longitudinal polarization is a null state. It is generated by G−1/2​∣0;p⟩=2α′​p⋅b−1/2​∣0;p⟩. Factoring out that null direction identifies A∼A+κp and leaves D−2 positive physical polarizations of a gauge vector. In the consistent critical NS string one additionally has D=10; the half-level positivity argument alone does not derive that dimension.
There is a genuine zero-momentum exception if the printed word “all” is read literally. At a=1/2 and p=0, the polarization A=(1,0,…) obeys every displayed positive-mode constraint and the mass shell, but has norm −1. Transversality is vacuous, and A∼A+κp removes nothing. This nonzero-momentum condition in the massless vector norm test is therefore necessary for the intended endpoint assertion. Including zero momentum changes the unrestricted elementary test to strict a<1/2; ordinary propagating massless particle states use nonzero null momentum.
Finally, an oscillator vacuum used to build the excited state is labelled by that state's momentum. It need not separately satisfy the scalar-ground-state mass shell: imposing both α′p2=a and α′p2=a−1/2 at the same momentum would incorrectly exclude all such excitations.
