Let H be the family of closed half-spaces containing C. Certainly C⊆⋂H∈H​H. To prove the reverse inclusion, exclude an arbitrary z∈/C.
Suppose first that C is nonempty. Its Euclidean projection onto a convex set p exists: minimizing the continuous squared distance may be restricted to a sufficiently large closed ball, whose intersection with the closed set is compact. Put d=z−p=0. For every x∈C, convexity puts p+t(x−p) in C for 0≤t≤1. Minimality of p gives
0≤dtd​∥z−p−t(x−p)∥2​t=0+​=−2⟨d,x−p⟩.
(1)
Thus the closed half-space
Hz​={x:⟨d,x⟩≤⟨d,p⟩}
(2)
contains C, while ⟨d,z⟩=⟨d,p⟩+∥d∥2 excludes z. Since every point outside C is excluded by some member of H,
C=H a closed half-spaceC⊆H​⋂​H.​

This is the half-space representation of a closed convex set. If C=∅, every point can again be excluded by a containing half-space, so the intersection is empty. If C=Rn, no proper half-space contains it and the empty intersection is, by convention, Rn.

The Legendre-Fenchel transform and biconjugate are
f∗(p)=supx​{⟨p,x⟩−f(x)},f∗∗(x)=supp​{⟨p,x⟩−f∗(p)}.
(4)
The Fenchel-Moreau theorem says that every proper convex function which is lower semicontinuous equals its biconjugate. More generally, if a proper extended-real f has an affine minorant, then
f∗∗=clconvf,​
(5)
where the right side is the greatest lower semicontinuous convex minorant. This is biconjugation as closed convexification. The affine-minorant hypothesis ensures that the closed convexification is proper; an unqualified statement including arbitrary improper functions would need separate conventions.
Here are the essential proof steps. The Fenchel–Young inequality gives f∗∗≤f. Every term in the supremum defining f∗∗ is an affine minorant of f, and conversely any affine minorant ⟨p,x⟩+a≤f(x) has a≤−f∗(p). Thus f∗∗ is exactly the supremum of all affine minorants, hence is convex and lower semicontinuous.
Put g=clconvf. Its epigraph is the closed convex hull of the epigraph of f. Applying the half-space representation of a closed convex set in Rn+1 recovers that epigraph from its containing half-spaces. A containing half-space written
⟨v,x⟩+qt≤a
(6)
has q≤0, since epigraphs extend upwards. If q<0, it is precisely the epigraph inequality of an affine minorant, t≥(⟨v,x⟩−a)/(−q).
Vertical half-spaces with q=0 must also be accounted for. Choose one affine minorant ℓ0​ of g, which exists because g is proper and closed: strictly separate (x0​,g(x0​)−1) from its epigraph for a finite domain point x0​; the separating coefficient of t cannot be zero, since that would not distinguish points with the same x0​. If a vertical containing inequality is ⟨v,x⟩≤a, then
ℓ0​(x)+L(⟨v,x⟩−a),L≥0,
(7)
is still an affine minorant on the domain of g. At a point violating the vertical inequality, these minorants tend to infinity as L→∞. Thus vertical domain restrictions are also recovered by the supremum of affine minorants.
Consequently g equals that supremum. Every affine minorant of g is below f, while the epigraph of any affine minorant of f contains its closed convex epigraph hull. Therefore f and g have the same affine minorants, completing g=f∗∗. The theorem in the previous solution supplies the geometric separation step behind biconjugation.

Use the maximizing-dual convention. For the perturbation function f(x,u), define
φ(x)=f(x,0),ψ(y)=−f∗(0,y),p(u)=xinf​f(x,u),q(v)=ysup​[−f∗(v,y)].​
(11)
The primal problem is infx​φ(x)=p(0); the Lagrangian dual problem is supy​ψ(y)=q(0). The sign convention makes the dual marginal q concave for jointly convex data; some formulations negate q to express a minimization problem.
The central convex perturbation duality calculation is
p∗(y)=supu,x​{⟨y,u⟩−f(x,u)}=f∗(0,y),q(0)=p∗∗(0)≤p(0).
(12)
The last inequality is weak duality. A sufficient finite-dimensional condition for strong duality with dual attainment is: f is jointly proper and convex, p is proper with p(0) finite, and p is finite on a neighborhood of zero. More generally 0∈ri(domp) suffices.
Indeed continuity of a convex function on the interior of its domain gives a supporting subgradient y∗​∈∂p(0), so
p(u)≥p(0)+⟨y∗​,u⟩⟹p∗(y∗​)=−p(0).
(13)
Therefore ψ(y∗​)=p(0) and the dual maximum is attained. This qualification establishes equality and dual attainment; it does not by itself establish a primal minimizer.

For each fixed z, choose the convex perturbation function
fz​(x,u)=k(x)+h(z+u−x).​
(14)
It is jointly convex and has marginal
pz​(u)=infx​[k(x)+h(z+u−x)]=F(z+u).
(15)
Because h and k are nonnegative and finite everywhere,
0≤F(w)≤k(0)+h(w)<∞
(16)
for every w. The inf-convolution is convex: for approximate decompositions of w1​,w2​, take their convex combination and use convexity of both costs; then let their approximation errors tend to zero. Thus F is a finite convex function on all of Rn, hence continuous everywhere. In particular pz​ is proper and finite near zero, so the qualification in the previous solution applies at every z:
xinf​[k(x)+h(z−x)]=ymax​[−fz∗​(0,y)].​
(17)
This is strong duality for a finite-valued infimal convolution.
No primal attainment was used. For example k(x)=ex and h(x)=0 meet all the stated assumptions, but F(z)=0 is approached only as x→−∞. This illustrates why a proof based on an assumed minimizing decomposition would be incomplete. The printed functions are real-valued everywhere, not merely extended-real; that distinction supplies continuity and the strong-duality qualification.

For practical subgradient computation, minimize the known convex dual objective
k∗(y)+h∗(y)−⟨z,y⟩.
(21)

∂F(z)=argymax​ψz​(y)={y:z∈∂(k∗+h∗)(y)}.​
(22)

Replacing it by ∂k∗(y)+∂h∗(y) requires the usual subdifferential sum rule qualification; it is not automatically justified solely by knowing the two conjugates.

One form of Farkas' lemma states that exactly one of the following holds:
∃z≥0: Az=b,∃y: ATy≥0, bTy<0.​
(23)
Their mutual exclusion is immediate: if both held, bTy=zTATy≥0.
For existence of the alternative certificate, let C={Az:z≥0}, the finitely generated cone of the columns of A. It is convex. It is also closed, a fact that must be justified rather than assumed for arbitrary linear images of closed cones. In a representation with dependent active generators, choose a nonzero dependence ∑j​dj​aj​=0 with at least one dj​>0. Subtract
td,t=mindj​>0​dj​λj​​
(24)
from the nonnegative coefficient vector. The represented point is unchanged, all coefficients remain nonnegative, and at least one active coefficient disappears. Iteration produces a representation with independent active columns. For a convergent sequence in C, pass to a subsequence using the same independent set, possible because there are finitely many sets. Its coefficients converge through a fixed left inverse, and their limits remain nonnegative. This proves closedness of finitely generated cones.
The indicator functional δC​ is therefore proper, lower semicontinuous and convex. Its Legendre-Fenchel transform is
δC∗​(w)={0,+∞,​ATw≤0,otherwise.​
(25)
Indeed a positive pairing with a cone generator can be scaled arbitrarily, while all nonpositive pairings give supremum zero. The Fenchel-Moreau theorem now gives
δC​(b)=δC∗∗​(b)=supATw≤0​⟨w,b⟩.
(26)
If b∈/C, the left side is infinite, so some feasible w has ⟨w,b⟩>0. Taking y=−w gives ATy≥0 and bTy<0. If b∈C, the first alternative holds. The biconjugation theorem applied to a closed finitely generated cone proves the alternative.

The forward subgradient step and backward subgradient step are the set-valued maps
Fτf​(x)=x−τ∂f(x)={x−τp:p∈∂f(x)},Bτf​=(I+τ∂f)−1.​
(32)
Thus u∈Bτf​(z) means z−u∈τ∂f(u). They are respectively the explicit and implicit time discretizations of subgradient flow x˙∈−∂f(x). At a differentiable point the forward update is x−τ∇f(x), while the backward update evaluates the gradient at the new point. For proper closed convex data the latter is the proximal operator
Bτf​(z)=argminu​{f(u)+2τ1​∥u−z∥2}.
(33)
To prove at most one output, suppose u,v∈Bτf​(z). Then (z−u)/τ∈∂f(u) and (z−v)/τ∈∂f(v). The two subgradient inequalities imply monotonicity of a convex subdifferential, giving
0≤⟨τz−u​−τz−v​,u−v⟩=−τ∥u−v∥2​.
(34)
Since τ>0, u=v. Convexity supplies this monotonicity; lower semicontinuity is not needed for this at-most-one argument.
Under the full printed assumptions the output actually exists at every z. Properness ensures a finite point and excludes −∞. Proper lower-semicontinuous convex f has an affine minorant, so the quadratic proximal objective is coercive. Lower semicontinuity makes its minimum attained on a compact sublevel set. Convexity and the positive quadratic curvature make it strongly convex, hence its minimizer is unique. The subgradient optimality condition, with the differentiable quadratic term, is exactly z−u∈τ∂f(u). Thus
Bτf​ is everywhere defined and single-valued.​
(35)
This also identifies precisely which assumptions provide existence, uniqueness and the update's optimality interpretation.

Use the nonnegative-pairing dual cone K∗={y:⟨y,s⟩≥0 for all s∈K}. The Lagrangian is L(x,y)=cTx−yT(Ax−b) for y∈K∗. Its infimum over unrestricted x is finite exactly when ATy=c. Therefore the conic dual problem is
ysup​bTysubject to ATy=c, y∈K∗=K.​

The final equality uses the self-dual cone assumption.
For the canonical self-concordant barrier, use its logarithmically homogeneous barrier normalization
F(ts)=F(s)−νlogt,⟨∇F(s),s⟩=−ν.
(41)
At parameter μ>0, the primal barrier problem is
minAx−b∈intK​{cTx+μF(Ax−b)}.
(42)
Write F†​(y)=F∗(−y), the Legendre dual cone barrier. The matching dual barrier problem is
minATy=cy∈intK​​{−bTy+μF†​(y)}.
(43)
Defining the dual barrier this way is valid generally; self-duality of the cone alone does not assert that an arbitrarily selected barrier equals its Legendre dual.
The joint central path characterization is
Ax−b=s∈intK,ATy=c,y∈intK,y=−μ∇F(s).​
(44)
The primal stationarity equation is c+μAT∇F(s)=0, exactly the dual feasibility equation after defining y. For the dual relation, logarithmic homogeneity gives ∇F(s/μ)=−y, so ∇F†​(y)=−s/μ. Thus dual stationarity is −b+μ∇F†​(y)+Ax=0, giving the same primal equation. At these points,
cTx−bTy=sTy=νμ.​

Linearizing the three equality conditions gives the central-path Newton system

The barrier Hessian is positive definite. Full column rank of A therefore makes ATHA positive definite and the reduced solve unique. Use a damped step with s+αΔs and y+αΔy remaining interior; a full Newton step need not do so.
At an exact point with parameter μ, choosing μ​<μ gives rp​=rd​=0 and rc​=(μ​−μ)∇F(s), the usual predictor towards the next path point. Infinitesimally,
dμdx​=−μ1​(ATHA)−1AT∇F(s),dμds​=Adμdx​,dμdy​=−∇F(s)−μHdμds​.
(49)

The printed full-rank condition by itself is insufficient. For example K=R+2​, A=(1,−1)T, b=0 and c=0 give the sole primal feasible point x=0, with no interior slack at all, despite full column rank. Rank guarantees the Newton solve at an interior point, not existence of the central path.
