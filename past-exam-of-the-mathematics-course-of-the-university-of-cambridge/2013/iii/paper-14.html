Let q:CP8→Q collapse the indicated Complex projective space. Write x∈H2(CP8;Z) for the positive generator, so the cohomology ring of complex projective space is Z[x]/(x9). The inclusion of CP3 sends x to its corresponding generator and is an isomorphism in degrees 0,2,4,6.
The CW complex structure makes the inclusion a cofibration, so the positive-degree cohomology of Q identifies with the relative cohomology of the pair. Its long exact sequence shows that q∗ is injective in positive degrees, with image the additive subgroup generated by x4,x5,x6,x7,x8. Equivalently, the surviving cellular cochain complex has one copy of Z in each of degrees 8,10,12,14,16 and zero differential. Let ui​ denote the class with q∗ui​=xi, for 4≤i≤8.
Naturality of the cup product, together with the injectivity of q∗, now determines all multiplication:
H∗(Q;Z)=Z{1,u4​,u5​,u6​,u7​,u8​},∣ui​∣=2i,u42​=u8​.​
(1)
Every other product of positive-degree basis elements is zero. Indeed, their degrees exceed 16. In particular, the surviving classes do not form a polynomial algebra on a degree-eight generator: the independent classes in degrees 10,12,14 must also be retained. This is an instance of the cohomology ring of a collapsed projective subspace.

Write A=S2×S2 and let i:A→X be its inclusion after the attachment. The cohomology ring of a product of two spheres is
H∗(A;Z)=Z[a,b]/(a2,b2),∣a∣=∣b∣=2,
(2)
where a,b come from the first and second factors and ab is the chosen orientation class. The diagonal pulls both a and b back to the same generator of H2(S2;Z).
There is one new three-cell. In the cellular chain complex, its boundary has coordinates (1,1) in the two-dimensional cells. Thus the relevant differential is
Z(11​)​Z2,
(3)
and the original four-cell still has zero boundary. This gives H2​(X;Z)=Z, H3​(X;Z)=0, H4​(X;Z)=Z, and no other positive homology groups. Equivalently, the relative cohomology sequence of (X,A) gives an injective restriction in degree two with image Z(a−b), and an isomorphism in degree four.
Choose u∈H2(X;Z) and v∈H4(X;Z) by i∗u=a−b, i∗v=ab. Naturality of the cup product gives
i∗(u2)=(a−b)2=−2ab.
(4)

H∗(X;Z)≅Z[u,v]/(u2+2v,uv,v2),∣u∣=2,∣v∣=4.​
(5)
Changing the sign of v would instead give u2=2v; the displayed sign uses the product orientation ab. The factor 2 is the characteristic feature of a cup square after a diagonal sphere attachment.

Call this simultaneous antipodal quotient of two spheres M. The simultaneous antipodal map acts freely on S2×S2, so M is a connected closed manifold of dimension four. Each antipodal factor has mapping degree −1; their product preserves orientation. Hence M is orientable.
The product S2×S2 is simply connected, so this double covering space is the universal cover. Consequently
π1​(M)=Z/2,H1​(M;Z)=Z/2,
(6)
using the abelianization of the fundamental group. The Euler characteristic under a finite covering gives χ(M)=4/2=2. Its rational first Betti number is zero, and Poincare duality gives b3​=b1​=0 and b0​=b4​=1. Thus b2​=0 as well.
The universal coefficient theorem for cohomology now gives H1(M;Z)=0 and
H2(M;Z)≅ExtZ1​(H1​(M;Z),Z)≅Z/2.
(7)
Here Hom(H2​(M;Z),Z)=0 because b2​=0. Integral Poincare duality further gives H3(M;Z)≅H1​(M;Z)=Z/2, and H4(M;Z)=Z.
Let a,b,c generate these groups in degrees 2,3,4 respectively. The only potentially nonzero positive-degree cup product is a2. But 2a2=(2a)a=0, while H4(M;Z) has no nonzero torsion subgroup, so a2=0. Every other positive product vanishes by dimension. Thus
H∗(M;Z)=Z{1,c}⊕(Z/2){a,b},∣a∣=2, ∣b∣=3, ∣c∣=4,​
(8)
with every product of positive-degree elements equal to zero. The degree-three torsion is essential: it would be lost by computing only rational cohomology.

If every f∗​ is an isomorphism, exactness makes Hi​(M)=0. Conversely, if every Hi​(M) is zero, the adjacent exact portions make every f∗​ both injective and surjective. Therefore
H∗​(M)=0⟺f is a quasi-isomorphism.​
(12)
This is the acyclicity criterion for a mapping cone, with the degree-dependent signs adjusted to the given convention.
For the assertion about spaces, use the cellular approximation theorem to replace f by a cellular map, and take the induced map of the finite free cellular chain complexes. Tensoring these complexes and their cone with Fp​ gives the corresponding mod-p complexes and cone. The same exact-sequence argument works over Fp​, so the assumed homology isomorphisms imply
Hi​(M⊗Fp​)=0for every i and every prime p.
(13)
The universal coefficient theorem for homology gives
0⟶Hi​(M)⊗Fp​⟶Hi​(M⊗Fp​)⟶Tor1Z​(Hi−1​(M),Fp​)⟶0.
(14)
In particular Hi​(M)⊗Fp​=0 for every prime. Each Hi​(M) is a finitely generated abelian group. A nonzero free summand would survive tensoring with every Fp​, while a nonzero finite cyclic summand would survive for a prime dividing its order. Thus Hi​(M)=0 in every degree, by detection of integral acyclicity modulo primes. Applying the cone criterion once more proves the integral homology map is an isomorphism in every degree. Finite generation is what makes detection by all prime fields sufficient.

Use the standard complex orientations and the product orientation. Let a,b∈H2(S2×S2;Z) satisfy a2=b2=0 and ⟨ab,[S2×S2]⟩=1, and let x∈H2(CP2;Z) satisfy ⟨x2,[CP2]⟩=1. These are the cohomology rings of the product of two spheres and the Complex projective plane.
For a map from the Complex projective plane, write f∗a=rx and f∗b=sx. Naturality of the cup product gives r2x2=f∗(a2)=0 and s2x2=f∗(b2)=0. Since x2 has infinite order, r=s=0. It follows that f∗(ab)=0, so every such map has degree 0.
In the reverse direction, write g∗x=ra+sb. Then
g∗(x2)=(ra+sb)2=2rsab,degg=2rs∈2Z.​
(15)
Every even mapping degree really occurs. To construct it, identify S2 with CP1 and use the Segre embedding
σ:CP1×CP1⟶CP3,([z0​:z1​],[w0​:w1​])⟼[z0​w0​:z0​w1​:z1​w0​:z1​w1​].
(16)
The generator x3​∈H2(CP3;Z) pulls back to a+b: restricting to either factor gives a projective line and hence coefficient 1. By the cellular approximation theorem, σ is homotopic to a cellular map. Its four-dimensional domain then maps into the four-skeleton CP2 of CP3. Denote that map by g0​; restriction of x3​ to CP2 is x, so g0∗​x=a+b and degg0​=2.
For any integer m, choose a map dm​:S2→S2 of mapping degree m. For m>0 one may use z↦zm on the Riemann sphere, for m<0 use z↦z∣m∣, and for m=0 use a constant map. Then gm​=g0​∘(dm​×id) satisfies gm∗​x=ma+b, so the possible degrees are exactly all even integers, with gm​ realizing 2m. The cellular approximation here deforms this particular map into the skeleton; it does not require a retraction of CP3 onto CP2.
Finally, take the connected sum of oriented manifolds with both summands carrying their standard complex orientations. Its degree-two cohomology has generators u,v with
u2=v2=w,uv=0,⟨w,[CP2#CP2]⟩=1.
(17)
This follows by choosing the generators supported away from the two balls used to form the connected sum, so mixed cup products vanish while each square gives the common orientation class. Write
h∗u=ra+sb,h∗v=ta+zb,P=(rs​tz​).
(18)
If degh=n, the three cup product relations say
PT(01​10​)P=nI2​.
(19)
Taking determinants gives −(detP)2=n2, which forces n=0. Therefore the only possible degree to the connected sum is 0, realized by a constant map. This is a degree constraint from intersection forms: the indefinite intersection form of the sphere product cannot pull back a definite form with a nonzero degree multiplier.

Work first with coefficients F2​. For a rank-r real vector bundle E→B over a CW complex, choose a fibre metric and its disk bundle D(E) and sphere bundle S(E). The mod-two Thom class is the unique class U∈Hr(D(E),S(E);F2​) restricting to the nonzero generator on every fibre pair. The Thom isomorphism theorem asserts that
T:Hj−r(B;F2​) ≅ ​Hj(D(E),S(E);F2​),a⟼π∗a∪U.
(20)
No orientation of the vector bundle is needed with these coefficients.
Let j:H∗(D(E),S(E))→H∗(D(E)) forget the relative condition, and let s:B→D(E) be the zero section. Set e2​(E)=s∗j(U), the top Stiefel–Whitney class wr​(E). Since D(E) retracts onto the zero section, j(U)=π∗e2​(E). Thus the relative-to-absolute map corresponds under the Thom isomorphism theorem to multiplication by e2​(E). Substituting these identifications into the long exact sequence in relative cohomology of (D(E),S(E)) gives the unoriented Gysin sequence
⋯⟶Hj−r(B;F2​)∪e2​(E)​Hj(B;F2​)p∗​Hj(S(E);F2​)⟶Hj−r+1(B;F2​)∪e2​(E)​Hj+1(B;F2​)⟶⋯,
(21)
where p is the sphere bundle projection.
Apply this to the real tautological line bundle γn​→RPn. Its sphere bundle is Sn, with projection the antipodal double cover, and put t=e2​(γn​)∈H1(RPn;F2​). Suppose n≥1. The map on H0 from the connected base to the connected sphere is an isomorphism, so exactness shows that multiplication by t is injective from H0 to H1. Since both groups are one-dimensional, t is a generator.
For 1<j<n, the adjacent sphere cohomology groups in the Gysin sequence vanish, so multiplication by t is an isomorphism from degree j−1 to degree j. If n>1, the top portion is
0⟶Hn−1(RPn;F2​)∪t​Hn(RPn;F2​)⟶Hn(Sn;F2​)⟶Hn(RPn;F2​)⟶0.
(22)
The last nonzero arrow is surjective between one-dimensional groups, hence an isomorphism; the preceding arrow is zero, so multiplication by t is again an isomorphism. For n=1 the earlier H0 argument already gives the top multiplication. Thus 1,t,…,tn are the nonzero generators in their respective degrees, while tn+1=0 by dimension. We obtain
H∗(RPn;F2​)≅F2​[t]/(tn+1),∣t∣=1.​
(23)
For n=0 this says simply H∗(RP0;F2​)=F2​, with t=0. This computes the mod-two cohomology ring of real projective space, including its multiplication rather than only its additive groups.
Now use integral coefficients and an oriented rank-n vector bundle. Its orientation selects an integral Thom class U. Define its Euler class by
e(E)=s∗j(U)∈Hn(B;Z).​
(24)
Again j(U)=π∗e(E). The cup product of two relative classes in H∗(D(E),S(E)) agrees with the mixed relative/absolute product after forgetting the relative condition on either factor. Consequently
U∪U=U∪j(U)=U∪π∗e(E).​
(25)
This is the cup square of a Thom class. If n is odd, graded commutativity of the cup product gives U∪U=−U∪U, hence 2U2=0. Since U∪π∗(2e(E)) is, up to the graded sign, the Thom isomorphism image of 2e(E), its vanishing implies
2e(E)=0(n odd).​
(26)
Thus the Euler class of an oriented odd-rank vector bundle is two-torsion; the conclusion is integral and does not require the base cohomology to be torsion-free.

For n≥1, let # denote concatenation in the first cube coordinate and let ⋅ denote pointwise multiplication of maps into the topological group. Both operations descend to the homotopy group, and the constant map is their common identity. They satisfy the interchange rule
(α#β)⋅(γ#δ)=(α⋅γ)#(β⋅δ),
(27)
which follows by considering the two halves of the first coordinate. Using the identity class 0 gives
[α]⋅[β]=([α]#0)⋅(0#[β])=([α]⋅0)#(0⋅[β])=[α]#[β].
(28)
Therefore [αβ]=[α]+[β]. The equality is on based homotopy classes; the usual reparametrization homotopies justify the unit identities for concatenation. This is the Eckmann-Hilton argument, and proves homotopy-group addition in a topological group even for n=1.
For the unit quaternions, the explicit inverse is
Φk,l−1​(r,s)=(s,s−krs−l).​
(29)
Both compositions cancel in the displayed order, without commuting the quaternions. Multiplication, inversion and all integer powers are continuous, so this proves that Φk,l​ is a homeomorphism for all integers k,l.
Put m=k+l. Let a,b be the standard generators of H3​(S3×S3;Z), represented by the first and second factors. The previous pointwise-multiplication result and the Hurewicz theorem imply that the mapping degree of q↦qm on S3 is m, including negative integers. Restricting Φ to the two factors therefore gives
Φ∗​a=ma+b,Φ∗​b=a,[Φ∗​]H3​​=(m1​10​).​
(30)
The map on H0​ is the identity. For the top homology group, take the dual degree-three cohomology classes x,y. We have Φ∗x=mx+y, Φ∗y=x. Since these classes have odd degree, graded commutativity of the cup product gives
Φ∗(xy)=(mx+y)x=yx=−xy.
(31)
Thus Φ∗​ is multiplication by −1 on H6​, and all other homology groups of the product are zero.
For the gluing, regard Φ as the attaching identification from ∂(D4×S3) to ∂(S3×D4). In these coordinates its first input is the boundary coordinate of D4, its second input the fibre coordinate; its second output is the boundary coordinate of the other D4. This makes the two pieces the usual two trivializations of a three-sphere bundle over the four-sphere.
Let A=S3×D4 and B=D4×S3, and parametrize their common boundary T=S3×S3 using the B coordinates. Collar neighbourhoods give an open cover with the same homotopy types, so the Mayer–Vietoris sequence applies. Both pieces retract onto S3. In degree three, the map into the homology of the pieces is
L=(iA∗​Φ∗​,−iB∗​):Z2⟶Z2,L=(m0​1−1​).
(32)
Indeed, inclusion into A retains the first output coordinate, while inclusion into B retains the second input coordinate. Exactness now gives
0⟶H4​(Xk,l​)⟶Z2L​Z2⟶H3​(Xk,l​)⟶0.
(33)
The second relation eliminates the second generator of the cokernel, leaving a single generator with relation m times that generator equal to zero. Thus cokerL=Z/mZ. The kernel is zero if m=0 and is generated by (1,0) if m=0.
The degree-six boundary class gives H7​(Xk,l​)≅H6​(T)=Z. All remaining positive-degree groups outside degrees 3,4,7 vanish by the same Mayer–Vietoris sequence; connectedness gives H0​=Z. Consequently the complete integral answer is
Hi​(Xk,l​;Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z/mZ,Z,0,​i=0,7,i=3,i=4 and m=0,otherwise,​m=k+l.​
(34)
Here Z/0Z means Z, and a negative m gives the same cyclic group as ∣m∣. In particular, m=±1 gives the integral homology of a seven-sphere, while m=0 gives the integral homology of S3×S4.
