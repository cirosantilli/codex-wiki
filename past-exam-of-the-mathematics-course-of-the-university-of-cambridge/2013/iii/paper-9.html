For a unit direction e, let Tδ​(a,e) be a length-one Kakeya tube with transverse radius δ, centered at a, and define the Kakeya maximal function by
Kδ​f(e)=supa​∣Tδ​(a,e)∣1​∫Tδ​(a,e)​∣f(x)∣dx.
(1)
Using normalized surface measure on the direction sphere, the Kakeya maximal conjecture is the following family of estimates, in the formulation relevant to this paper:
∥Kδ​f∥Ln(Sn−1)​≤Cn,ε​δ−ε∥f∥Ln(Rn)​(0<δ<1, ε>0).​
(2)
The constant is independent of δ and f. Replacing round Kakeya tubes by comparable rectangular tubes changes only dimensional constants.
A bounded Kakeya set contains a unit line segment in every direction. The Kakeya Minkowski dimension conjecture says that every such set has full Minkowski dimension n. The maximal estimate in fact gives full lower as well as upper Minkowski dimension.
To prove that implication, let Eδ​={x:dist(x,E)<δ}. Each unit segment in E has a thinner tube contained in Eδ​, so Kcδ​1Eδ​​(e)≥1 for every e, with a fixed dimensional c>0. Apply the maximal estimate to this indicator function:
1≲Cn,ε​δ−ε∣Eδ​∣1/n,∣Eδ​∣≳n,ε​δnε.
(3)
If Nδ​(E) is the smallest number of radius-δ balls covering E, that cover, enlarged by a fixed factor, covers Eδ​. Thus ∣Eδ​∣≲n​δnNδ​(E) and
Nδ​(E)≳n,ε​δ−n+nε.
(4)
Taking the lower limit of logNδ​(E)/log(1/δ) and then letting ε↓0 gives lower Minkowski dimension at least n. Bounded subsets of Rn have upper Minkowski dimension at most n. Consequently both dimensions equal n, which proves the requested implication.

It is enough to prove the stronger planar Kakeya maximal function bound
∥Kδ​f∥L2(S1)​≲log(2/δ)​∥f∥L2(R2)​.​
(5)
We first work with a δ-separated net of unoriented directions e1​,…,eM​, with M≍δ−1. Write αij​ for angular distance modulo π. Choose arbitrary length-one, width-δ rectangles Tj​ in these directions. The permitted rectangle intersection fact is
∣Ti​∩Tj​∣≲min(δ,αij​δ2​)≲δ+αij​δ2​.
(6)
The first alternative includes parallel rectangles. Their locations are arbitrary; only separation of their directions matters.
Define Af(j)=δ−1∫Tj​​f, and put ∥a∥ℓδ2​2​=δ∑j​∣aj​∣2. With these weights the adjoint operator is A∗a=∑j​aj​1Tj​​. Since a separated angular net has only a bounded number of directions at each distance scale kδ from ei​, its overlap matrix satisfies
∑j​∣Ti​∩Tj​∣≲δ+∑k=1O(δ−1)​kδ​≲δlog(2/δ).
(7)
The diagonal term is of size δ. Using ∣ai​aj​∣≤(∣ai​∣2+∣aj​∣2)/2 and symmetry gives the Schur test estimate
∥A∗a∥22​​≤i,j∑​∣ai​aj​∣∣Ti​∩Tj​∣≲δlog(2/δ)i∑​∣ai​∣2=log(2/δ)∥a∥ℓδ2​2​.​
(8)
By duality of Lp spaces, ∥Af∥ℓδ2​​≲log(2/δ)​∥f∥2​. This is uniform over every choice of the translated rectangles. For each direction choose a rectangle approaching the supremum for ∣f∣, then take the limit. That gives the same estimate for the discretized Kakeya maximal function.
To recover all directions, partition the direction circle into arcs of length comparable to δ, each with a net direction. A tube in an arc is contained in a rectangle in its net direction with width Cδ and length at most two. A bounded subdivision in the length direction reduces this to the same averaging operators; the wider tubes obey the same overlap estimate with fixed-factor changes. Therefore
∫S1​∣Kδ​f(e)∣2de≲δ∑j​∣KCδnet​f(ej​)∣2≲log(2/δ)∥f∥22​.
(9)
Finally log(2/δ)​≤Cε​δ−ε for every ε>0. The planar Kakeya maximal estimate therefore has the required arbitrary small power loss.

Use the Fourier transform convention ν(ξ)=∫e−ix⋅ξdν(x) throughout this question. The decay assumption implies square integrability, since polar coordinates give
∫R2​∣μ​(ξ)∣2dξ≲∫0∞​(1+r1+ϵ)2r​dr<∞.
(10)
Near zero the integrand is bounded by r, and at infinity it is bounded by r−1−2ϵ. The positive ϵ is what makes the latter integrable.
By the Plancherel theorem, there is a function g∈L2(R2) whose Fourier transform is μ​. It is the density of μ with respect to Lebesgue measure. To justify this step rather than assume a density, for every Schwartz function φ, Fourier inversion gives
∫φdμ=(2π)−2∫φ​(−ξ)μ​(ξ)dξ=∫φ(x)g(x)dx.
(11)
The finite measure and the locally integrable function thus define the same tempered distribution, so they agree as measures: dμ=gdx. In particular g≥0 almost everywhere and ∫g=μ(R2)<∞. This is the L2 density from a square-integrable Fourier transform principle.
For f∈L∞(μ), the density of fdμ is fg. The inequality ∣f∣≤∥f∥L∞(μ)​ holds wherever g>0, apart from a Lebesgue measure zero set, so
∥fg∥2​≤∥f∥L∞(μ)​∥g∥2​.
(12)
A second application of the Plancherel theorem yields the explicit bound
∥fdμ​∥2​=(2π)∥fg∥2​≤∥μ​∥2​∥f∥L∞(μ)​.​
(13)
The implicit constant in the requested estimate may depend on the measure and its Fourier-decay bound, but is independent of f.

Parametrize the unit circle by ω(θ)=(cosθ,sinθ), with −π≤θ<π and dσ=dθ/(2π). Extract the constant phase at ω(0):
eiξ1​ψdσ​(ξ)=2π1​∫ψ(ω(θ))e−i[ξ1​(cosθ−1)+ξ2​sinθ]dθ.
(14)
On the support, ∣θ∣≤2δ/C. The frequency rectangle and the elementary bounds ∣1−cosθ∣≤θ2/2, ∣sinθ∣≤∣θ∣ imply
∣ξ1​(cosθ−1)+ξ2​sinθ∣≤C22​+C2​.
(15)
Choose the fixed constant C large enough that this is less than π/3. Every phase then has real part at least 1/2. Nonnegativity of ψ prevents cancellation after this phase rotation, while its central plateau gives ∫ψdσ≥δ/(πC). Hence the Fourier transform satisfies
∣ψdσ​(ξ)∣≥Re(eiξ1​ψdσ​(ξ))≥21​∫ψdσ≥2πCδ​.​
(16)
This is the circle cap Fourier lower bound. No upper bound on the values of ψ is needed here; the support, plateau and nonnegativity suffice. The long radial scale δ−2 comes from the quadratic term cosθ−1, whereas the transverse scale δ−1 comes from the linear term sinθ.

Write Rj​ for the rectangles, xj​ for their centers and ej​ for their long-axis directions. For the finite exponent p in the displayed estimate, take smooth rotated cap functions ψj​, with 0≤ψj​≤1, equal to one on angular distance at most δ/C from ej​ and supported within 2δ/C. Choose C sufficiently large once and for all. The direction separation makes these cap supports disjoint, and ∫∣ψj​∣pdσ≲δ.

Set m=⌈p/100⌉ and d=m−1, so that d<p/100<p. The dimension of a bounded-total-degree polynomial space in n variables over Fp​ is (nn+d​). If #N were smaller than this dimension, evaluation at the points of N would impose fewer homogeneous linear conditions than unknown coefficients. The rank-nullity theorem would give a nonzero multivariate polynomial P of total degree at most d, vanishing on N.
For every x, select one of the promised rich affine lines in a vector space through x, and write it as ℓ={a+tv:t∈Fp​} with v=0. The polynomial restriction to a line Q(t)=P(a+tv) has degree at most d, and at least m=d+1 distinct roots of a polynomial from N∩ℓ. By the root bound for a polynomial, Q is identically zero, so P(x)=0. Since this works for every x, P vanishes at all pn points of Fpn​.
The Schwartz-Zippel lemma says that a nonzero multivariate polynomial of total degree d has at most dpn−1 zeros on this grid. Here d<p, so that count is strictly less than pn, a contradiction. The distinction between a formal polynomial and its function on a finite field is crucial: our degree bound is what rules out a nonzero polynomial vanishing everywhere.
We have proved the stronger quantitative rich line covering bound over a finite field
#N≥(nn+⌈p/100⌉−1​)=n!(d+1)⋯(d+n)​≥100nn!pn​.​
(22)
Thus #N≳n​pn, as required. The implied constant may depend on the fixed dimension n. The very large numerical lower bound on p is more than this proof needs.

For a finite collection L of distinct affine lines in a vector space in Rn, n≥2, a joint is a point incident to n lines whose direction vectors are linearly independent. The joints theorem asserts
#J(L)≲n​(#L)n/(n−1).​
(23)
In the customary three-dimensional formulation this is #J≲L3/2, with three noncoplanar incident lines at every joint. We prove the general form, which includes that formulation.
Let L=#L and M=#J. The conclusion is immediate when M=0. Otherwise set D=⌈nM1/n⌉. Suppose for contradiction that M>LD. Repeatedly delete any line incident to at most D of the currently retained joints, deleting those joints at the same time. Each deleted line loses at most D current joints, so even deleting all L lines could lose at most LD<M joints. Therefore the process must stop with a nonempty set J′ and a line collection L′ such that each retained line contains more than D retained joints. Every retained joint still has its original n independent incident lines: if any line through it had been deleted, the joint would have been deleted too.
There is a nonzero multivariate polynomial of total degree at most D vanishing on J′, because
(nD+n​)≥n!Dn​≥n!nn​M>M≥#J′.
(24)
Choose such a polynomial P of smallest possible total degree d≤D. This is an application of the polynomial method in combinatorics. Every line of L′ contains more than D≥d roots of a polynomial of its polynomial restriction to a line, so P vanishes identically on every such line.
At a retained joint x, differentiating along each of its independent line directions v1​,…,vn​ gives vi​⋅∇P(x)=0. Their linear independence therefore forces ∇P(x)=0. Each partial derivative of P vanishes on all of J′ and has smaller total degree. Minimality of d forces every partial derivative to be the zero polynomial. Over the real numbers, a polynomial with all partial derivatives zero is constant; a nonzero constant cannot vanish on the nonempty J′. This is the required contradiction.
It follows that M≤LD. Since D≤(n+1)M1/n for M≥1,
M(n−1)/n≤(n+1)L,M≤((n+1)L)n/(n−1).​
(25)
This proves the joints theorem by the pruning and minimal-degree polynomial argument.
The exponent is sharp. Take all axis-parallel lines passing through the grid {1,…,m}n. There are nmn−1 distinct lines and mn joints; the coordinate directions span Rn at every grid point. Thus no smaller power of the number of lines can bound all joint configurations.
