We take algebras to be unital and modules to be unital. A finite-dimensional semisimple algebra over the complex numbers is one whose regular left module is a semisimple module, that is, a direct sum of simple modules. We use the basic finite-dimensional equivalences: this is equivalent to a zero Jacobson radical, and the Jacobson radical of any finite-dimensional algebra is a nilpotent ideal.
The Artin–Wedderburn theorem here says that there are positive integers d1​,…,dr​ with
A≅i=1∏r​Mdi​​(C).​
(1)
The nonisomorphic simple modules are the standard column modules of the factors, of dimensions di​, and the factor sizes are unique up to reordering.
Here is a proof. Decompose the regular left module as A​A=⨁i=1r​Si⊕ni​​ with pairwise nonisomorphic simple modules Si​. These are all the simple modules: any simple module is generated by a nonzero vector and is therefore a quotient of the regular module. The Schur lemma gives HomA​(Si​,Sj​)=0 for i=j and EndA​(Si​)=C. For the latter assertion, an endomorphism has an eigenvalue over C, and the kernel of its difference from that scalar is a nonzero submodule, hence the whole simple module. Consequently
EndA​(A​A)≅∏i​Mni​​(C).
(2)
Every regular-module endomorphism is right multiplication by its value at 1, so EndA​(A​A)≅Aop. Taking opposite algebras and using matrix transposition gives the asserted decomposition with di​=ni​.
For a matrix algebra Md​(C), the matrix units show that its only simple module is Cd: the spaces Ejj​S are isomorphic through Eij​, and a nonzero vector in one of them generates one copy of the column module. Simplicity makes that copy all of S. In a product algebra, the mutually orthogonal central idempotents decompose every module into its factor modules; a simple module uses exactly one factor. This proves the module assertion and also uniqueness, since the primitive central idempotents and the dimensions of their simple modules determine the factors.

Assume first that S=0, as required for the irreducible-module conclusion. If B→EndC​(S) is surjective, any nonzero invariant subspace contains every image of one of its nonzero vectors under all endomorphisms, hence is all of S. Thus S is irreducible.
Conversely an irreducible B-module is finite dimensional: for s=0, S=Bs is a quotient of the finite-dimensional vector space B. Let D be the image of B in EndC​(S). It acts faithfully and irreducibly. The subspace J(D)S is a submodule, so is zero or all of S. The latter alternative would imply J(D)kS=S for every k, contradicting nilpotence of the Jacobson radical. Therefore J(D)S=0, and faithfulness gives J(D)=0.
The Artin–Wedderburn theorem makes D a product of matrix algebras. A faithful simple module forces there to be just one factor, because all other factors would annihilate that module. Thus D≅Md​(C) and S≅Cd, so the action is the full endomorphism algebra. This is the Burnside matrix-algebra theorem.
For nonzero S, surjectivity is equivalent to irreducibility. The zero module is a literal exception if it is admitted: its endomorphism algebra is zero, so the action map is surjective, whereas the zero module is not irreducible.

Maschke's theorem makes the group algebra CG semisimple, since G is a finite group and the ground field has characteristic zero. By the Artin–Wedderburn theorem, write CG≅∏i=1r​Mdi​​(C). Its simple modules give exactly the r nonisomorphic irreducible complex group representations.
The center of each matrix algebra consists of scalar matrices, so dimC​Z(CG)=r. On the other hand, an element ∑g​ag​g is central precisely when its coefficients are constant on conjugacy classes. The sums of the elements in the separate conjugacy classes are therefore a basis of the center. Hence the number of irreducible complex representations equals the number of conjugacy classes.

For an integer tuple ℓ=(ℓ1​,…,ℓm​), the monomial alternant is
Aℓ​(x)=∣xℓ1​,…,xℓm​∣=det(xiℓj​​)1≤i,j≤m​.
(3)
Negative exponents require nonzero coordinates; all exponents in the character expansion below are nonnegative. The power-sum symmetric polynomial is sk​(x)=∑i=1m​xik​. Put δ=(m−1,m−2,…,0), so Aδ​(x)=∏i<j​(xi​−xj​) is the Vandermonde determinant.
Let a conjugacy class of Sn​ have αk​ cycles of length k, with ∑kαk​=n, and put pα​(x)=∏k​sk​(x)αk​. The product pα​Aδ​ is an alternating polynomial homogeneous of degree n+(2m​). In an alternating polynomial, a monomial with two equal exponents has zero coefficient, since interchanging those variables fixes the monomial and reverses its sign. Grouping the remaining monomials by their permutation orbits gives a unique expansion in alternants Aℓ​ with ℓ1​>⋯>ℓm​≥0.
Such tuples of the indicated total degree are exactly ℓi​=λi​+m−i for partitions of an integer λ of n with at most m parts. Define the class function
ωλ​(α)=[x1λ1​+m−1​⋯xmλm​​]pα​(x)Aδ​(x).​
(4)
The displayed monomial occurs with coefficient 1 in Aλ+δ​ and in no other ordered alternant, so
pα​(x)Aδ​(x)=∑λ⊢n, ℓ(λ)≤m​ωλ​(α)Aλ+δ​(x).
(5)
This proves the expansion and explicitly defines its coefficients. They depend only on the cycle counts and hence are class functions. Identifying these coefficients with Specht module characters is the Frobenius alternant character formula, which the remaining parts allow us to assume.

Take m=2 and λ=(2,1), so λ+δ=(3,1). A transposition in S3​ has one singleton cycle and one two-cycle. The Frobenius alternant character formula therefore gives
χ(2,1)((12))=[x13​x2​](x1​+x2​)(x12​+x22​)(x1​−x2​)=[x13​x2​](x14​−x24​)=0​.
(6)
In the coordinate permutation representation on C3, a transposition fixes one basis vector, so its character is 1. This representation is the direct sum of the invariant line of constant vectors and the standard representation of the symmetric group, whose vectors have coordinate sum zero. Subtracting the trivial character gives χV​((12))=1−1=0, as required.

The Young permutation module Mλ has a basis of tabloids, equivalently ordered row sets of sizes λ1​,…,λr​. A tabloid is fixed by σ precisely when every cycle of σ lies entirely in one row. Assigning a length-q cycle to row j contributes xjq​. Distinct cycles can be assigned independently, so the character is
χMλ​(σ)=[x1λ1​​⋯xrλr​​]q=1∏n​(x1q​+⋯+xrq​)mq​.​
(7)
Padding to n variables with zero row sizes gives exactly the same coefficient. Explicitly, the character of a Young permutation module is
∑aqj​≥0: ∑j​aqj​=mq​∑q​qaqj​=λj​​​∏q​∏j​aqj​!mq​!​.
(8)
Here aqj​ counts the length-q cycles assigned to row j. Rows remain distinguished even when their sizes are equal, so there is no further division by permutations of equal rows.

For a Young tableau t, let Rt​ permute the entries within its rows and Ct​ within its columns. We use the Young symmetrizer convention
rt​=∑σ∈Rt​​σ,ct​=∑τ∈Ct​​sgn(τ)τ,ht​=ct​rt​.
(9)
Reversing the order gives another usual realization of the same irreducible polynomial module. On the tensor power T=V⊗n, the actions are
σ(v1​⊗⋯⊗vn​)=vσ−1(1)​⊗⋯⊗vσ−1(n)​,g(v1​⊗⋯⊗vn​)=gv1​⊗⋯⊗gvn​.
(10)
They commute because applying g to every factor commutes with permuting the factors.
We state the permitted combinatorial input explicitly. A Young symmetrizer satisfies ht2​=Hλ​ht​, where Hλ​ is the nonzero hook product of a partition. Thus et​=Hλ−1​ht​ is a primitive idempotent. The standard-tableau decomposition of the right regular module is CSn​=⨁λ,t standard​et​CSn​. Tensor this right-module direct sum with the left module T. The map et​CSn​⊗CSn​​T→et​T sending et​a⊗v to et​av is an isomorphism, with inverse w↦et​⊗w. Therefore the Young-symmetrizer tensor decomposition is
T=⨁λ,t standard​et​T=⨁λ,t standard​ht​T.
(11)
This is a direct sum of GL(V)-modules; individual summands need not be Sn​-invariant.
We also state the allowed Schur algebra result, namely Schur–Weyl duality: the two actions are mutual commutants, and
T≅⨁λ⊢n, ℓ(λ)≤m​Sλ⊗Dλ​(V),
(12)
where the Dλ​(V) are pairwise nonisomorphic irreducible homogeneous polynomial representations of degree n. We also use the standard Schur algebra equivalence between its modules and homogeneous degree-n polynomial representations, so these exhaust the irreducibles in that category. A primitive idempotent et​ has one-dimensional image on Sλ and zero image on the other simple factors, so ht​T≅Dλ​(V). This identifies the requested irreducibles. They classify the polynomial degree-n representations in this tensor power, not all rational representations of every degree.
The length bound for a Schur module is Dλ​(V)=0 if and only if ℓ(λ)≤m. A column of length greater than m antisymmetrizes more than m vectors and gives zero. Conversely, for ℓ(λ)≤m, fill every tensor position in row i with the ith basis vector of V. Row symmetrization multiplies it by ∏i​λi​!, and column antisymmetrization is nonzero because the vectors within each column are distinct basis vectors.
A rational representation of an algebraic group is a regular morphism into the general linear group of its representation space. For GLm​, its matrix entries belong to C[gij​,det(g)−1]; rational here permits determinant denominators but not arbitrary poles on GLm​. A one-dimensional rational character of C× is a Laurent polynomial p(z) with p(zw)=p(z)p(w) and p(1)=1. Comparing Laurent coefficients shows that just one monomial occurs and its coefficient is 1, hence p(z)=zr for r∈Z.
Restrict a one-dimensional rational character of GLm​ to its diagonal torus. The same argument in several variables gives ∏i​xiri​​. Conjugation by permutation matrices makes all ri​ equal. On every diagonalizable invertible matrix it consequently agrees with (detg)r. The allowed Zariski-density statement, and equality of regular functions on a dense subset, give
χ(g)=(detg)r,r∈Z.​
(13)
These are the one-dimensional rational characters of the general linear group.
For complete reducibility of rational GL and SL representations, use the compact-group averaging argument. Average any positive definite Hermitian inner product over SU(m) using normalized Haar measure. The orthogonal complement of an invariant subspace is then SU(m)-invariant. Differentiating makes it invariant under su(m) and therefore under its complex span slm​(C). The elementary unipotent matrices exp(tEij​) generate SLm​(C), so the complement is SLm​-invariant. This proves complete reducibility. Averaging over U(m) similarly gives complete reducibility for rational GLm​ representations.
Here is an explicit rational extension from SL to GL. Decompose the representation space by the finite scalar center of SLm​ into subspaces Wk​ on which ζI acts as ζk, with 0≤k<m and ζm=1. These subspaces are invariant. For g∈GLm​, choose a with am=detg, set s=a−1g∈SLm​, and define
ρ′(g)∣Wk​​=akρ(s)∣Wk​​.
(14)
Changing a to ζa changes s to ζ−1s, so the two factors cancel. It is a homomorphism, since scalar roots multiply up to the same harmless factor, and it restricts to ρ on SLm​.
It is rational as well. Every matrix coefficient of ρ∣Wk​​ has a polynomial representative on SLm​. Averaging that representative over the finite scalar center selects its homogeneous parts Pd​ with d≡k(modm). Substitution in the extension gives ∑d​(detg)(k−d)/mPd​(g), a regular function on GLm​. Each SLm​-invariant subspace decomposes into its Wk​ parts, and the extension acts on each part by a scalar times an SLm​ action. Thus these subspaces are also invariant under the chosen extension. Consequently ρ is irreducible if and only if this ρ′ is irreducible. For m=1, SL1​ is trivial and the trivial extension supplies the same conclusion.

For a decreasing integer tuple λ=(λ1​,…,λm​), let μi​=λi​−λm​. Then μ is a partition and the highest-weight classification of rational GL representations defines
Dλ​(V)=(detV)λm​⊗Dμ​(V).
(15)
This is a determinant twist of a Schur module. Every irreducible rational GLm​ representation becomes polynomial after multiplication by a sufficiently large positive determinant power, which clears all matrix-entry denominators. The polynomial degree decomposition and Schur–Weyl duality then identify it with a Schur module. Undoing the twist gives exactly one decreasing integer tuple λ. Distinct tuples have distinct highest torus weights, so these are the complete pairwise nonisomorphic irreducible rational representations.
The Weyl character formula specializes to
φλ​(diag(x1​,…,xm​))=det(xjm−i​)det(xjλi​+m−i​)​=(x1​⋯xm​)λm​sμ​(x).
(16)
It is a symmetric Laurent polynomial in the eigenvalues. Equality extends from the dense set of diagonalizable matrices to all invertible matrices: both the character and the expression in the characteristic-polynomial coefficients are regular functions on GLm​. For a polynomial representation this also extends to every endomorphism of V. For a general rational representation, the printed claim at singular endomorphisms needs this qualification: for example det−1 is undefined at a singular matrix. The displayed formula is valid on GLm​, and on all of End(V) when λm​≥0.
To compute the degree, set xj​=etcj​ with distinct cj​ and take t→0. For ℓi​=λi​+m−i and d=(2m​), the leading coefficient of an exponential alternant is
det(etℓi​cj​)=∏k=0m−1​k!td​∏i<j​(ℓi​−ℓj​)∏i<j​(ci​−cj​)+O(td+1).
(17)
Indeed, expand every exponential in powers of t. The first nonzero determinant uses the distinct powers 0,1,…,m−1; its coefficient is the product of the two Vandermonde determinants divided by ∏k!. Taking the same expansion in the denominator cancels the powers and the c factors, giving the Weyl dimension formula
degφλ​=dimDλ​(V)=i<j∏​j−iλi​−λj​+j−i​.​
(18)
This proof works for negative λm​ as well, since determinant twists have dimension one.
Every finite-dimensional rational module is completely reducible. The characters of its irreducible constituents are linearly independent: each Schur Laurent character has its highest dominant monomial xλ with coefficient 1, and only lower weights besides it. In a finite relation, choose a lexicographically highest remaining weight; its coefficient must vanish, and iterate. Therefore equal characters give equal multiplicities of every irreducible constituent, proving rational modules with the same character are isomorphic.
Finally the symmetric algebra of V⊕Λ2V has the formal torus character
ch(⨁j≥0​Sj(V⊕Λ2V))=∏i​(1−xi​)−1∏i<j​(1−xi​xj​)−1.
(19)
Each factor sums the symmetric powers of a one-dimensional weight space; the exterior square has weights xi​xj​ for i<j. The permitted Schur identity makes this ∑λ​sλ​(x). In each fixed scalar degree there are only finitely many terms, so complete reducibility and character independence apply degree by degree without a convergence assumption. Thus the multiplicity-free symmetric-algebra model for polynomial GL representations contains each irreducible polynomial representation exactly once. The word irreducible is necessary: arbitrary reducible polynomial modules, such as two copies of the trivial module, do not each occur once in a multiplicity-free sum.

For a box (i,j) of a Young diagram, its hook of a Young diagram contains that box, all boxes to its right in its row, and all boxes below it in its column. Its hook length is hij​=λi​−j+λj′​−i+1. The hook graph of a partition is the diagram with each box labeled by its hook length. Write Hλ​=∏(i,j)∈λ​hij​ for the hook product of a partition. The hook-length formula is
fλ=dimSλ=Hλ​n!​,n=∣λ∣.​
(20)
Pad λ to m rows and use the beta set of a partition ℓi​=λi​+m−i, so ℓ1​>⋯>ℓm​≥0 and ∑i​ℓi​=n+d, where d=(2m​). We prove the beta-set hook-product identity row by row. For 1≤j≤λi​, put qj​=m+j−λj′​−1; then hij​=ℓi​−qj​. The qj​ are distinct integers in [0,ℓi​−1], and none equals a beta number. Indeed, if k≤λj′​ then ℓk​≥qj​+1, whereas if k>λj′​ then ℓk​≤qj​−1. Exactly m−i beta numbers lie below ℓi​, so the remaining λi​ integers in that interval are precisely these qj​. Therefore
∏j=1λi​​hij​=∏k>i​(ℓi​−ℓk​)ℓi​!​,Hλ​=Δ(ℓ)∏i​ℓi​!​,Δ(x)=∏i<j​(xi​−xj​).
(21)
This gives the equivalent Specht module dimension expression fλ=n!Δ(ℓ)/∏i​ℓi​!.
The standard Young tableaux of shape λ form the dimension count for the Specht module. The largest entry n must occupy a removable corner. Deleting it bijects tableaux with the disjoint union of standard tableaux of the shapes obtained by deleting a corner, proving
fλ=∑i=1m​fλ−ei​.
(22)
Set a term to zero whenever λ−ei​ is not a partition: this includes equal adjacent rows and an attempted deletion from a zero row. The empty diagram has dimension 1.
For an algebraic proof that the proposed formula has the same recurrence, establish the Vandermonde shift identity
∑i=1m​xi​Δ(x1​,…,xi​+t,…,xm​)=(∑i​xi​+dt)Δ(x).
(23)
Its left side is an alternating polynomial in the xi​, because permuting the variables permutes the summands and changes the sign of every Vandermonde factor. It is therefore divisible by Δ(x). The quotient is symmetric in x and homogeneous of total degree one in (x,t), so has the form a∑i​xi​+bt. At t=0, a=1. Differentiating in t at zero and using the Euler theorem for homogeneous functions gives ∑i​xi​∂i​Δ=dΔ, so b=d. This proves the identity as a polynomial identity, including repeated coordinates.
Take x=ℓ and t=−1. Since ∑i​ℓi​=n+d, it gives ∑i​ℓi​Δ(ℓ−ei​)=nΔ(ℓ). For n≥1, this is exactly
∑i​∏j​ℓj​!(n−1)!ℓi​Δ(ℓ−ei​)​=∏j​ℓj​!n!Δ(ℓ)​.
(24)
The summand is the proposed dimension for λ−ei​; if two beta numbers collide its Vandermonde is zero, and if ℓi​=0 its coefficient is zero, so no negative factorial is needed. For the empty partition, ℓ=(m−1,…,0) and Δ(ℓ)=∏k=0m−1​k!, giving initial value 1. Induction now proves the hook-length formula from the tableau recurrence.
For the final sum, tuples with repeated coordinates contribute zero. Sorting each distinct nonnegative tuple with sum (2m+1​) gives one beta set of a partition of size m, since subtracting the staircase removes (2m​) from the sum. Conversely every partition of m, padded to m rows, supplies exactly m! ordered tuples, with the same squared summand. Hence the square-sum identity for shifted partition coordinates is
∑ℓi​≥0∑i​ℓi​=m(m+1)/2​​∏i​(ℓi​!)2Δ(ℓ)2​=(m!)2m!​∑λ⊢m​(fλ)2=(m!)2m!​dimCSm​=1​.
(25)
The middle equality uses the Artin–Wedderburn theorem for the group algebra and the complete classification of its simple modules by Specht modules. It explains why the last identity is a representation-dimension count rather than an accidental cancellation.
