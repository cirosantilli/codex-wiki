Wealth and admissibility. The dollar holding θ earns the risky return, while w−θ earns the continuous-time bank account return. Removing consumption therefore gives
dwt​=σθt​dWt​+[rwt​+(μ−r)θt​−ct​]dt.​
(1)
Here θ is a dollar amount, not a number of shares; the number of shares is θt​/St​. Both controls must use available information: take θ predictable and c≥0 progressively measurable, with
∫0t​(σθs​)2ds<∞,∫0t​(∣(μ−r)θs​∣+cs​)ds<∞
(2)
almost surely on every finite interval. Require a well-defined objective and an admissible trading strategy satisfying wt​≥0. At zero portfolio wealth this excludes continued risky gambling or positive consumption. The state-price budget constraint rules out doubling strategies. Throughout the diffusion calculations take σ=0, positive discount ρ, positive decay λ, and finite value; degeneracies are discussed where they affect the conclusions.
Satisfaction and dynamic programming. Write the consumption satisfaction stock as
ξt​=e−λt(ξ0​+∫0t​eλscs​ds).
(3)
The product rule gives, almost everywhere in time,
dξt​=(ct​−λξt​)dt.​
(4)
This state is a finite-variation process, so it has no quadratic covariation with portfolio wealth. Applying the Itô formula to the discounted value function over a short interval, then using dynamic programming, gives the interior HJB equation
0=U(ξ)−ρV+rwVw​−λξVξ​+supθ∈R​{(μ−r)θVw​+2σ2θ2​Vww​}+supc≥0​c(Vξ​−Vw​).
(5)
Assuming Vww​<0, completing the square gives
θ∗=−σ2μ−r​Vww​Vw​​,supθ​{⋯}=−2κ2​Vww​Vw2​​,κ=σμ−r​.
(6)
The last supremum is zero if Vξ​−Vw​≤0 and infinite otherwise. Thus the gradient constraint for unbounded consumption is Vξ​≤Vw​, and c=0 wherever the inequality is strict. Since there is no direct penalty for a very large consumption rate, an active boundary can involve singular consumption control. In that relaxed interpretation the HJB equation is
max{U(ξ)−ρV+rwVw​−λξVξ​−2κ2​Vww​Vw2​​, Vξ​−Vw​}=0.

With ordinary rate controls, this describes the supremum and its limiting transfer policy; it does not promise that an instantaneous transfer is attained by a finite rate.
Power reduction. An additive constant in the utility function only adds a control-independent constant divided by ρ to the value, so normalize U(ξ)=ξ1−R/(1−R). Put p=1−R and x=w/ξ for ξ>0. Scaling portfolio wealth, consumption satisfaction and the controls by the same positive number gives the wealth-to-satisfaction reduction
V(ξ,w)=ξpv(x),Vw​=ξ−Rv′,Vww​=ξ−R−1v′′,Vξ​=ξ−R(pv−xv′).
(8)
Consequently the reduced HJB equation is
max{p1​−[ρ+λp]v+(r+λ)xv′−2κ2​v′′(v′)2​, pv−(x+1)v′}=0.​
(9)
In the strict waiting region the first expression vanishes and consumption is zero. On a transfer region the second vanishes; integrating it gives v(x)=K(1+x)p. This reflects preservation of w+ξ during an instantaneous wealth-to-satisfaction transfer.
Why a waiting threshold is expected, and its qualification. The gradient constraint for unbounded consumption compares the benefit of increasing consumption satisfaction with the opportunity cost of spending financial portfolio wealth. When consumption satisfaction is already large relative to cash, waiting lets consumption satisfaction decay while financial portfolio wealth earns returns; consuming immediately can be wasteful. Homogeneity makes the comparison depend only on x. For fixed s=w+ξ, joint concavity of the value function makes qs​(a)=V(s−a,a) concave in financial portfolio wealth a. It is nondecreasing because an immediate transfer can reproduce any smaller financial allocation. Consequently its derivative Vw​−Vξ​ is nonnegative and nonincreasing in a: a strict waiting region, if present, starts at zero financial portfolio wealth and ends at a single transfer boundary. Homogeneity makes the corresponding boundary a ratio x∗​. In the usual finite-boundary regime this gives c=0 for x<x∗​, and transfers push a larger ratio down towards x∗​. The condition is a comparison of marginal values, not the ordinary formula c=(Vw​)−1/R: current utility function here depends on consumption satisfaction, not on current consumption.
A positive threshold is not guaranteed by the printed hypotheses alone. A useful sufficient local test illustrates the intended argument. Let a=ρ+λp>0. At zero portfolio wealth,
V(ξ,0)=paξp​,Vξ​(ξ,0)=aξ−R​.
(10)
Starting with a small extra portfolio wealth ε, holding it in the continuous-time bank account until a fixed time t, then transferring it into consumption satisfaction, has right derivative in ε at zero equal to
aξ−R​e(r−ρ+λR)t.
(11)
Therefore, if ρ<r+λR, the portfolio wealth marginal value is strictly larger than the consumption satisfaction marginal value at zero. With the usual continuity of marginal values, there is a positive interval on which the gradient constraint for unbounded consumption is strict. The fixed-total-resource concavity argument then gives the threshold structure.
For a concrete counterexample to an unconditional positive threshold, take R=1/2, λ=1, r=μ=1/10, σ=1 and ρ=2. There is zero market price of risk. The relaxed value is
F(ξ,w)=pa(ξ+w)p​,p=21​,a=25​.
(12)
Indeed Fξ​=Fw​, Fww​<0, and the optimized waiting residual, after division by ξp, is
−∫0x​(1+s)−Rds+ar+λ​x(1+x)−R≤0,
(13)
because (r+λ)/a<1 and the integrand is decreasing. The Itô formula gives an upper bound by F, while transferring all portfolio wealth into consumption satisfaction over intervals tending to zero attains that bound in the limit. Hence the transfer boundary is x∗​=0 in this example. The positive-threshold explanation needs a parameter regime supporting a genuine waiting region. For R>1, even the zero-wealth value is finite only if ρ>λ(R−1); otherwise decaying consumption satisfaction gives value −∞.

The joint generator. With θ again measured in dollars, portfolio wealth obeys
dw=σ(x)θdW+[rw+(μ(x)−r)θ−c]dt.
(14)
Its noise and the factor noise are driven by the same Brownian motion. Their quadratic covariation is σ(x)θα(x)dt, so the diffusion generator has a cross derivative. Dynamic programming and the Itô formula give
0=​21​α2Vxx​+βVx​−ρV+rwVw​+θ∈Rsup​{(μ−r)θVw​+σαθVwx​+21​σ2θ2Vww​}+c≥0sup​{U(c)−cVw​}.​​
(15)
All coefficient functions in this formula are evaluated at x. The cross derivative is essential: it gives intertemporal hedging demand. Normalizing CRRA utility as U(c)=cp/p, where p=1−R, the two optimizations give, at nonzero volatility,
c∗=(Vw​)−1/R,θ∗=−σ2Vww​(μ−r)Vw​+σαVwx​​,
(16)
and hence
0=21​α2Vxx​+βVx​−ρV+rwVw​−2σ2Vww​[(μ−r)Vw​+σαVwx​]2​+pR​(Vw​)p(−1/R).
(17)
Here p(−1/R)=1−1/R.
Homogeneity and the reduced equation. Scaling initial portfolio wealth and both controls preserves the portfolio wealth constraint and multiplies the objective by the positive number bp. Thus
V(w,x)=pwp​f(x),f(x)>0.
(18)
This expression is valid for both signs of p: V is negative when R>1, but its portfolio wealth derivative is positive. Its derivatives are
Vw​=w−Rf,Vww​=−Rw−R−1f,Vwx​=w−Rf′,Vx​=pwp​f′,Vxx​=pwp​f′′.
(19)
Substitution yields
21​α2f′′+βf′+(pr−ρ)f+2Rσ2fp[(μ−r)f+σαf′]2​+Rf(R−1)/R=0.​
(20)
The resulting feedback is
wc∗​=f−1/R,wθ∗​=Rσ2μ−r​+Rσα​ff′​.​
(21)
The first portfolio term is myopic, and the second is intertemporal hedging demand.
Constant market price of risk. If μ−r=σκ and volatility is nonzero, the portfolio term becomes p(κf+αf′)2/(2Rf), so the magnitude of stock volatility disappears. Applying the power transformation of a complete-market investment equation f=gR cancels the squared-gradient terms and gives the further reduction
21​α2g′′+(β+Rpκα​)g′−γM​g+1=0,γM​=Rρ−p(r+κ2/(2R))​.​
(22)
For γM​>0 its economic solution is g=1/γM​. Thus
V(w,x)=pγM−R​wp​,c∗=γM​w,θ∗=Rσ(x)κw​.​
(23)
To see why this solves the investment-consumption problem, optimize directly over Brownian portfolio exposures, writing y=σ(x)θ. The portfolio wealth equation becomes dw=ydW+(rw+κy−c)dt, which no longer contains X. With nonzero volatility the same admissible exposure processes are available for every factor state, so the attainable wealth-consumption pairs, and therefore the value, are exactly those of the Merton consumption-investment problem. This also excludes extraneous solutions of the linear equation without imposing artificial factor boundary data.
The printed boundedness assumptions do not ensure nonzero volatility or a finite value. The unsimplified HJB equation remains the correct control equation at a zero of σ. There the hedge term vanishes; if μ=r, the riskless excess return gives arbitrage with unrestricted holdings. Under μ−r=σκ, a zero-volatility state offers only the bank exposure at that instant. For example σ≡0, μ≡r satisfies this relation for any chosen κ, but its value uses γ0​=[ρ−pr]/R, not a fictitious nonzero risk premium. The constant-value formula using κ presupposes access to the Brownian exposure, with sufficient integrability for the corresponding holdings. Additive utility constants again only shift V by a constant divided by ρ.

Available portfolio wealth and the ruin boundary. Put h=rˉD, the constant interest payment on the fixed loan. The loan principal is already included in available portfolio wealth; it is not a growing portfolio holding. Therefore
dw=σθdW+[rw+(μ−r)θ−h−c]dt,w0​=x0​+D.​
(24)
In particular the interest outflow is rˉD, not (rˉ−r)D. Writing w=x+D would instead give net portfolio wealth drift rx+(μ−r)θ−(rˉ−r)D−c, which explains the distinction.
Let τ0​ denote the ruin time, to avoid confusing it with a fixed terminal horizon. The objective stops at τ0​; consequently the absorbing boundary is V(0)=0, without an obligation to keep financing the loan after ruin. Dynamic programming gives, for w>0,
ρV=(rw−h)V′+supθ​{(μ−r)θV′+21​σ2θ2V′′}+supc≥0​{U(c)−cV′}.
(25)
For increasing strictly concave value, put z=V′(w)>0 and use inverse marginal utility I. The optimal controls and the optimized HJB equation are
c∗=I(z),θ∗=−σκ​V′′V′​,0=(rw−h)V′−ρV−2V′′κ2(V′)2​+U(V′),
(26)
where U(z)=supc≥0​[U(c)−zc]. For CRRA utility with 0<R<1, write p=1−R and q=1−1/R<0. Then
I(z)=z−1/R,U(z)=pR​zq.
(27)
Dualization and the printed constant. Use the convex wealth-variable Legendre dual
J(z)=supw≥0​[V(w)−zw].
(28)
At an interior maximizing portfolio wealth, J′=−w, J′′=−1/V′′>0 and V=J−zJ′. The dual HJB equation is the linear Euler differential equation
21​κ2z2J′′+(ρ−r)zJ′−ρJ−hz+pR​zq=0.​
(29)
A trial power zt gives
Q(t)=21​κ2t(t−1)+(ρ−r)t−ρ.
(30)
Direct substitution gives
Q(q)=−γM​,γM​=Rρ+(R−1)(r+κ2/(2R))​.​
(31)
The PDF prints an additional factor 1/2 before κ2/(2R) in its definition of γM​. That printed definition is inconsistent with its own identity for Q(q). The expression above is the one used here; assume this corrected γM​>0.
Solution when κ=0. Assume positive discount ρ, nonzero σ, and h>0. Let m>0 and n<0 be the two roots of Q:
m,n=κ2−(ρ−r−κ2/2) ± (ρ−r−κ2/2)2+2κ2ρ​​.
(32)
Since Q(q)<0, n<q<0<m. Put C=R/(pγM​). For r=0, the general interior solution is
J(z)=Azm+Bzn−rh​z+Czq.
(33)
The appropriate large-wealth condition is the Merton consumption-investment problem bound
0≤V(w)≤pγM−R​​wp.
(34)
Indeed any original control consumes c+h in the debt-free comparison model until ruin, and U(c+h)≥U(c). Dualizing this bound gives 0≤J(z)≤Czq. Because n<q, convexity and this upper bound force B=0 as z↓0.
At the other endpoint portfolio wealth reaches zero. If z∗​=V′(0+), the dual ruin boundary with debt service requires
J(z∗​)=0,J′(z∗​)=0,J(z)=0(z≥z∗​).​
(35)
Solving these two equations gives

For r>0, m>1, and for r<0, 0<m<1, so the quantity defining z∗​ is positive in either case. These formulas determine the entire value. For each w>0, choose the unique z∈(0,z∗​) satisfying
w=rh​−Amzm−1−Cqzq−1.​

Then

(38)
Both terms in the bracket are positive, even when r<0 and A<0. Thus J′′>0, −J′ decreases from infinity to zero as z increases, and the portfolio wealth inversion really is unique. The extended dual is continuously differentiable and convex.
There is no additional condition J′′(z∗−​)=0. In fact
J′′(z∗−​)=C(1−q)(m−q)z∗q−2​>0.
(39)
Available portfolio wealth is killed at zero; the portfolio can have a nonzero limiting volatility immediately before ruin. Imposing a reflecting-boundary or zero-curvature condition would solve a different problem.
For r=0, the linear forcing resonates with the root m=1. Put b=ρ+κ2/2=Q′(1). The dual and boundary constants instead are
J(z)z∗​​=Az+bh​zlogz+Czq=[hCb(1−q)​]R,A=−bh​logz∗​−Cz∗q−1​.​(0<z<z∗​),​
(40)
Extend by zero for z≥z∗​. Here

and the controls remain c=z−1/R and θ=κzJ′′/σ, with J′′=h/(bz)+Cq(q−1)zq−2>0.
Verification and transversality. The candidate is nonnegative, increasing, strictly concave and zero at ruin. Its HJB equation makes the discounted value plus accrued utility a local supermartingale for every admissible control, and a local martingale for the stated feedback. Localization at positive lower and finite upper portfolio wealth levels gives the finite-horizon comparison. The investment value transversality condition follows from the same debt-free bound: applying the Itô formula to wp and maximizing its risky term gives
E[e−ρtwtp​1{t<τ0​}​]≤w0p​e−[ρ−p(r+κ2/(2R))]t=w0p​e−RγM​t.
(42)
The nonnegative consumption and debt-service drifts only decrease this bound. Thus the expected terminal candidate tends to zero. The feedback has at most linear growth, including a finite limit as portfolio wealth decreases to zero; stopping it at ruin gives an admissible policy. Letting localization levels and then the horizon tend to their limits proves that the candidate is the value, rather than just a formal dual solution. If D=0, the absorbing-debt boundary disappears and the ordinary Merton consumption-investment problem formula is recovered.
Zero market price of risk. With σ=0 and κ=0, the dual equation is first order. If ρ>r, the preceding formulas remain valid with m=ρ/(ρ−r) for r=0, and the logarithmic formula with b=ρ for r=0; there is no B term and θ∗=0. The same boundary and transversality argument verifies this deterministic consumption policy.
The remaining finite-value case has r>0 and pr<ρ≤r. Put γ0​=(ρ−pr)/R, wc​=h/(rR) and
zc​=(pγ0​hRr​)R.
(43)
The correct convex dual and its corresponding value are
J(z)=max{pγ0​R​zq−rh​z, 0},V(w)={zc​w,γ0−R​(w−h/r)p/p,​0≤w≤wc​,w≥wc​.​​
(44)
The two value branches have the same value and derivative at wc​. Above wc​, hold no stock and consume γ0​(w−h/r); the surplus over h/r grows at rate r−γ0​≥0. If ρ=r, the lower branch is attained by zero stock holding and constant consumption ph/R: portfolio wealth solves w˙=r(w−wc​) until ruin, and direct integration gives zc​w.
If ρ<r and 0<w<wc​, the lower branch is a supremum attained in a limit of increasingly rapid fair stock lotteries between zero and wc​, followed by the upper-branch policy on success. The success probability tends to w/wc​ and the fixed service cost during the lottery tends to zero. This is possible because unrestricted dollar holdings in the nonzero-volatility stock produce a fair Brownian motion exposure even when its excess drift is zero. The supporting linear branch has optimized waiting residual (r−ρ)zc​(w−wc​)<0; the fast lotteries, rather than a finite feedback optimizer, supply the missing control limit. The piecewise candidate is concave, has nonpositive waiting residual everywhere, and the preceding moment bound still supplies an upper-bound verification. This degenerate case can have a supremum without an ordinary maximizing strategy.

Pricing kernel and replication. In the nondegenerate Black-Scholes model, put κ=(μ−r)/σ and normalize the state-price density by ζ0​=1. The process is
ζt​=exp{−rt−κWt​−21​κ2t},dζt​=−ζt​(rdt+κdWt​).​
(45)
The density ertζt​ changes probability to the risk-neutral measure. Under that measure Wt​+κt is a Brownian motion and the stock drift is r. Thus an integrable contingent claim H has time-t price
Pt​=ζt​E[ζT​H∣Ft​]​=e−r(T−t)EQ[H∣Ft​].​
(46)
In the usual augmented natural Brownian filtration, the Brownian martingale representation theorem supplies a replicating strategy; this is the complete market assumption. For a nonnegative admissible trading strategy without intermediate consumption, the state-price budget constraint is E[ζT​wT​]≤w0​, with equality for a fully invested replicated claim. In particular
EζT​=e−rT,E[ζT​ST​]=S0​.
(47)
Feasibility and the largest slope. First take the intended regime r>0, T>0, w0​,S0​>0, and the usual nonnegative portfolio wealth constraint. The terminal wealth floor is
ξ=w0​+α(ST​−S0​).
(48)
For any feasible claim the state-price budget constraint implies
w0​≥E[ζT​wT​]≥E[ζT​ξ]=w0​e−rT+αS0​(1−e−rT).
(49)
Therefore α≤w0​/S0​. Conversely, when 0≤α≤w0​/S0​, hold α shares and put the remaining w0​−αS0​ in the continuous-time bank account. Its terminal portfolio wealth is
αST​+(w0​−αS0​)erT≥αST​+(w0​−αS0​)=ξ.
(50)
Hence
αˉ=S0​w0​​.​
(51)
At equality, ξ=αˉST​ has cost exactly w0​. Positivity of the state-price density forces wT​=αˉST​ almost surely: any strict improvement would cost more. Invest all initial portfolio wealth in w0​/S0​ shares and hold them until T.
Optimal payoff below the feasibility limit. For α<αˉ the floor is strictly positive, and its price is strictly less than w0​. Assume the utility function is increasing, differentiable and strictly concave, satisfies the Inada conditions, and has the integrability needed for the finite-budget optimization. These are the usual hypotheses implicit in using inverse marginal utility. For each positive multiplier λ, maximize
U(y)−λζT​yover y≥ξ
(52)
separately in every state. Its derivative decreases through zero at I(λζT​), so the floored marginal utility optimizer is
wT∗​=max{w0​+α(ST​−S0​),I(λζT​)}.​
(53)
The multiplier is characterized by
E[ζT​max{ξ,I(λζT​)}]=w0​,λ>0.​
(54)
Under the stated integrability hypotheses the left side is continuous and decreasing, tends to the floor cost as λ↑∞, and tends to infinity as λ↓0. It is strictly decreasing wherever it exceeds the floor cost: on the event where the inverse-marginal-utility payoff exceeds the floor, a larger multiplier strictly reduces that payoff. Therefore the budget determines a unique finite multiplier. For CRRA utility the inverse marginal utility is I(y)=y−1/R; lognormal moments provide the needed integrability.
For completeness, pointwise maximality gives, for any feasible competing terminal portfolio wealth Y,
U(Y)−λζT​Y≤U(wT∗​)−λζT​wT∗​.
(55)
Taking expectations and using E[ζT​Y]≤w0​=E[ζT​wT∗​] proves optimality. Strict concavity gives uniqueness of the terminal claim. Its price process
wt∗​=ζt​E[ζT​wT∗​∣Ft​]​
(56)
is nonnegative and starts from w0​; claim replication therefore turns the payoff optimizer into an admissible portfolio.
What the missing interest-rate hypothesis changes. The PDF does not explicitly assume r>0 or give the utility and admissibility hypotheses above. These omissions matter. At r=0, under nonnegative admissibility, the largest feasible slope remains w0​/S0​: for larger slopes the positive-part floor costs strictly more than w0​, since ST​ has support (0,∞). But every α≤w0​/S0​ already gives floor cost exactly w0​, so the only feasible terminal claim is ξ. There is no spare budget for an inverse-marginal-utility improvement. For example α=0, κ=0 and CRRA utility make E[ζT​max{w0​,I(λζT​)}]>w0​ for every finite λ. The prescribed positive finite multiplier then does not exist.
For negative r, nonnegative admissibility requires the effective floor ξ+​=max{ξ,0}. Define its cost
G(α)=E[ζT​(w0​+α(ST​−S0​))+​].
(57)
This is continuous and convex. For α≤w0​/S0​ it equals w0​e−rT+αS0​(1−e−rT), exceeding w0​ below the endpoint. At α0​=w0​/S0​, G(α0​)=w0​ and G′(α0​)=S0​(1−e−rT)<0. Above that endpoint the lognormal stock gives strict convexity, and G(α)→∞. Thus there is a unique second root α1​>α0​ of G(α1​)=w0​, the feasible slopes are [α0​,α1​], and the largest is α1​. Equivalently, for α>α0​ the floor price is α times the European call option price with strike S0​−w0​/α. At the upper endpoint replicate ξ+​; in the interval with strict budget slack the same floored marginal utility optimizer applies with ξ+​.
If instead portfolio wealth may be negative and utility is defined on all real portfolio wealth, the floor itself has its affine replication cost: at r=0 every slope is feasible, and at negative r every α≥w0​/S0​ is feasible. There is then no largest finite slope. The intended stock-only endpoint and strict-slack optimizer use positive interest and standard nonnegative admissibility.
