A separated dual pair consists of real vector spaces E,F and a bilinear form ⟨x,y⟩ that separates both variables: each nonzero x∈E pairs nontrivially with some y∈F, and conversely. Thus F embeds in the algebraic dual of E, and E embeds in the algebraic dual of F.
The weak topology σ(E,F) is the coarsest topology making all maps x↦⟨x,y⟩, y∈F, continuous. It makes E a Hausdorff locally convex space, with topology generated by the seminorms py​(x)=∣⟨x,y⟩∣. A neighbourhood base at zero consists of finite intersections
{x:∣⟨x,yj​⟩∣<εj​, 1≤j≤m},εj​>0.
(1)
Separation ensures that the common zero set of all these seminorms is just zero, giving Hausdorffness.
We determine the continuous dual of a weak topology. Suppose a linear functional ℓ is continuous. A basic neighbourhood as above is contained in {x:∣ℓ(x)∣<1}. If x is in the common kernel of the finitely many evaluations, every scalar multiple of x lies in that neighbourhood, so ℓ(x)=0. Hence ℓ factors through the image of
T:E⟶Rm,Tx=(⟨x,y1​⟩,…,⟨x,ym​⟩).
(2)
Extend the resulting linear functional on T(E) to Rm. It has the form z↦∑j​aj​zj​, giving ℓ(x)=⟨x,∑j​aj​yj​⟩. Conversely every such evaluation is continuous by the definition of the weak topology. Since the pairing separates F, this identification is injective, and
(E,σ(E,F))′=F.​
(3)
For the remaining parts every topological assertion about E′ or its subsets uses the stipulated weak-star topology σ(E′,E).

True. We prove weak-star topology on an entire infinite-dimensional Banach dual is not metrizable. Suppose instead that E′ has a countable local base (Wn​) at zero. Choose a basic weak-star topology neighbourhood Un​⊆Wn​, with its conditions involving a finite set Sn​⊆E. The Un​ still form a local base.
For any x∈E, the set {ϕ:∣ϕ(x)∣<1} is a neighbourhood of zero, so some Un​ is contained in it. Every functional annihilating Sn​, and every scalar multiple of that functional, belongs to Un​. Consequently every such functional also annihilates x. This forces
x∈spanSn​.
(10)
Indeed a finite-dimensional span is norm closed, and the Hahn-Banach theorem supplies a bounded linear functional vanishing on it and nonzero at any point outside it.
It follows that E=⋃n​spanSn​. Each span is a proper finite-dimensional closed vector subspace and has empty interior in the infinite-dimensional Banach space E. This contradicts the Baire category theorem. Hence
E′ with its weak-star topology is not metrizable.​
(11)
The uniform norm bound that made the metric work on B′ is absent on the whole dual. The answers to (i), (ii), (iii) and (iv) are therefore all true.

First we prove the Krein-Milman theorem in the required setting. If K is empty the conclusion is immediate, so assume it is nonempty. A face of a convex set is a convex subset F with the property that an interior point of a segment in K belongs to F only if both endpoints do. Consider all nonempty weakly compact faces of K, ordered by reverse inclusion. A chain has a nonempty intersection by compactness and the finite intersection property; that intersection is again a compact face. The Zorn lemma gives a minimal compact face F.
If F contained distinct points, a bounded linear functional separating them would be nonconstant on F. Its maximizer set is a nonempty proper compact face of F, hence a face of K, contradicting minimality. Therefore F is a singleton and its member is an extreme point of K. The same reasoning applies to each nonempty compact face of K, so each such face contains an extreme point of K.
Let H be the norm-closed convex hull of the extreme points of K. A weakly compact subset of a Banach space is weakly closed, hence norm closed, so H⊆K. If x∈K∖H, the Hahn-Banach separation theorem gives ℓ∈E′ with ℓ(x)>supH​ℓ. The maximizer face of ℓ on K contains an extreme point e of K. Then ℓ(e)≥ℓ(x)>supH​ℓ, contradicting e∈H. We conclude
K=convEx(K).​
(12)
Norm and weak closed convex hulls coincide by the same separation theorem. The printed phrase “closed convex cover” is understood in this standard closed-convex-hull sense.
Now work in the real space of continuous functions on a compact space C(C) on the Cantor set. The extreme points of a real continuous-function unit ball are exactly the continuous sign functions:
Ex(B)={f∈C(C):f(x)∈{−1,1} for every x∈C}.​
(13)
If ∣f(x0​)∣<1, continuity supplies a neighbourhood where ∣f∣≤1−ε. The Cantor cylinder sets form a clopen base, so choose a nonempty cylinder D inside that neighbourhood. Its indicator function is continuous, and f±ε1D​ are distinct members of B with midpoint f. Hence f is not extreme. Conversely, if f is pointwise sign-valued and f=(g+h)/2 with g,h∈B, equality at the endpoint of the scalar interval [−1,1] forces g(x)=h(x)=f(x) at every point.
To prove the closed-hull assertion without assuming weak compactness, take f∈B and ε>0. A sufficiently fine finite partition of C into Cantor cylinders has oscillation of f less than ε on each cell, by uniform continuity. Choose a value aj​∈[−1,1] on each cell and let h be the corresponding continuous step function. Then ∥f−h∥∞​<ε. For each sign vector s∈{−1,1}m, let es​ take value sj​ on cell j, and assign the weight
ws​=∏j=1m​21+sj​aj​​.
(14)
These weights are nonnegative, sum to one, and satisfy ∑s​ws​sj​=aj​. Thus h=∑s​ws​es​ is a convex combination of extreme points. This is clopen sign approximation in the real Cantor unit ball, proving
B=convEx(B).​
(15)
Nevertheless B is not weakly compact. The suggested functions fn​(x)=min(3nx,1) lie in B and converge pointwise to the function equal to zero at 0 and one at every other point of C. It is discontinuous at zero, since 2⋅3−j∈C tends to zero. If B were weakly compact, the sequence, viewed as a net, would have a weakly convergent subnet with limit in C(C). Point evaluations are bounded linear functionals, so that subnet would have the same pointwise limit; its indices are cofinal in the original sequence. The limit would therefore be the discontinuous function just described, a contradiction. This discontinuous pointwise limit obstruction to weak compactness gives the required failure of weak compactness, despite the closed-hull equality.

The relevant version of Wiener covering lemma is the finite ball selection lemma: from a finite collection of open Euclidean balls one can select pairwise disjoint balls Bj​ such that the original union is contained in ⋃j​3Bj​, where 3Bj​ has the same centre and three times the radius. To prove it, repeatedly retain a largest-radius remaining ball and discard every ball meeting it. If a discarded ball has radius r≤R and meets a retained ball of radius R, every point of the former is at distance less than r+(r+R)≤3R from the latter's centre. This proves the containment and, by scaling Lebesgue measure,
λd​(⋃ original balls)≤3d∑j​λd​(Bj​).
(16)
This is a covering result; no Fourier-algebra version of Wiener's lemma is involved.
We use it for a disjoint ball decomposition modulo null sets of V. The empty set needs only the empty family. Otherwise put V0​=V. For an open residual set Vk​ of positive finite measure, inner regularity of Lebesgue measure supplies a compact Kk​⊆Vk​ with λd​(Kk​)≥λd​(Vk​)/2. Cover Kk​ by finitely many open balls whose closures lie in Vk​, and use the Wiener covering lemma to select disjoint balls. Their total measure is at least λd​(Vk​)/(2⋅3d). Remove their closures to obtain the open residual set Vk+1​. Ball boundaries are null sets, so
λd​(Vk+1​)≤(1−2⋅3d1​)λd​(Vk​).
(17)
All retained balls, including those from different stages, are pairwise disjoint and contained in V. They form a countable family. The uncovered set is contained in ⋂k​Vk​ together with their countably many boundaries, and both have measure zero. Enumerating the retained balls gives
λd​(V∖n⋃​Un​)=0.​
(18)
Next consider the uncentered maximal function of a finite measure. For every real α, its strict superlevel set is
{x:mu​(x)>α}=⋃μ(Ur​(y))>αλd​(Ur​(y))​Ur​(y).
(19)
Indeed, membership in the ball is exactly the strict condition ∥x−y∥<r in the supremum. This union is open, proving that mu​ is lower semicontinuous as an extended nonnegative function; it may take the value infinity.
For α>0, take a compact subset K of this superlevel set and choose a finite cover by balls satisfying the displayed density inequality. The Wiener covering lemma selects disjoint balls Bj​ whose triples cover K. Since μ is a probability measure,
λd​(K)≤3d∑j​λd​(Bj​)<α3d​∑j​μ(Bj​)≤α3d​.
(20)
Take the supremum over compact K using inner regularity of Lebesgue measure. This works even if the open superlevel set was initially unbounded, and proves the uncentered maximal weak-type inequality
λd​{mu​>α}≤α3d​.​
(21)
For a finite positive measure of mass M, scaling gives 3dM/α instead.
One important use is the Lebesgue differentiation theorem. For f∈L1(Rd), approximate it in L1 by a compactly supported continuous g, and set h=∣f−g∣. The limiting mean oscillation of f at x is at most mhλd​​(x)+h(x), since that of g vanishes by continuity. The preceding weak-type estimate and the elementary integral bound on {h>t} give
λd​{limiting mean oscillation>2t}≤t3d+1​∥f−g∥1​.
(22)
Let the approximation error tend to zero and then take countably many t↓0. The local averages of f converge to f(x) almost everywhere. Localizing extends this to locally integrable functions. Thus the maximal estimate turns norm approximation by continuous functions into almost-everywhere information about local averages.

We prove Kantorovich duality theorem here by a positive-functional extension argument; it produces the minimizing transport plan at the same time as equality of the values. Let Y=X×X and c(x,y)=d(x,y). This is a bounded continuous function because X is a compact metric space. Write C(Y) for the real space of continuous functions on a compact space, and let
S={s∈C(Y):s(x,y)=f(x)+g(y), f,g∈C(X)}.
(23)
On this vector subspace define ℓ(s)=∫fdP+∫gdQ. It is well-defined: two representations differ by a constant in the first variable and the opposite constant in the second, and both measures have mass one. It is positive, since s≥0 implies minf+ming≥0 and hence ℓ(s)≥0. Also ℓ(1)=1.
Define the majorant envelope, which is a sublinear functional:
p(h)=inf{ℓ(s):s∈S, s≥h}(h∈C(Y)).
(24)
Constants majorize every h, and positivity gives minh≤p(h)≤maxh, so this quantity is finite. Taking approximate minimizing majorants proves subadditivity; rescaling majorants proves positive homogeneity. Moreover p(s)=ℓ(s) for s∈S, and subtracting s from a majorant proves
p(h+s)=p(h)+ℓ(s).
(25)
The dual value in the question is exactly
md​=sup{ℓ(s):s∈S, s≤c}=−p(−c).
(26)
Subadditivity at c+(−c)=0 gives md​≤p(c).
On S+Rc, assign the value ℓ(s)+tmd​ to s+tc. If c∈/S the representation is unique. If c∈S, then md​=ℓ(c), so the assignment is still well-defined. It is dominated by p: for t≥0, use tmd​≤tp(c)=p(tc); for t<0, positive homogeneity gives p(tc)=(−t)p(−c)=tmd​. Together with the translation identity, these verify domination in both cases.
The real Hahn-Banach theorem extends this functional to T:C(Y)→R with T≤p. If h≥0, then p(−h)≤ℓ(0)=0, so T(h)≥0. Thus T is a positive linear functional, with T(1)=1 and T(c)=md​. Positivity also gives ∣T(h)∣≤∥h∥∞​, so T is continuous. The Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem supplies a Borel probability measure π0​ on the compact space Y, satisfying T(h)=∫Y​hdπ0​.
For every f∈C(X), its pullback f(x) belongs to S, so ∫f(x)dπ0​=∫fdP. Likewise ∫g(y)dπ0​=∫gdQ. Uniqueness in the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem shows that these are exactly the two marginal distributions. Finally, every feasible pair f+g≤c gives a lower bound for the cost of every transport plan, by integration. Our constructed plan achieves that bound:
∫X×X​d(x,y)dπ0​(x,y)=md​=π with marginals P,Qmin​∫ddπ.​
(27)
This Kantorovich duality by positive extension establishes the requested existence and equality without assuming an optimal plan in advance.
The metric structure additionally gives the Kantorovich–Rubinstein theorem formulation. For a feasible pair define h(x)=infy​{d(x,y)−g(y)}. The triangle inequality for d makes h one-Lipschitz continuous, h≥f, and h(y)≤−g(y). Hence the dual objective is at most ∫hdP−∫hdQ. Conversely (h,−h) is feasible for every one-Lipschitz h. Therefore
md​=supLip(h)≤1​(∫hdP−∫hdQ).
(28)
Normalizing h(x0​)=0 for a fixed x0​ gives a uniformly bounded equicontinuous family; it is closed and compact in the uniform topology by the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem. The objective is uniformly continuous on this family, so the supremum is attained as well. In particular, md​ is the first Wasserstein distance between the two measures.
