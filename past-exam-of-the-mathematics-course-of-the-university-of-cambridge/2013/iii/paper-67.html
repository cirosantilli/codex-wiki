(1)
Seek the asymptotic expansion δ=A/an​+B/an3​+⋯. The coefficients of an​ and an−1​ give
−A1​=μ,A2B​+3A​=μA.
(2)
Thus the large roots near tangent poles satisfy
xn​=an​−μan​1​+an3​1/(3μ3)−1/μ2​+O(an−5​)​.
(3)
The first correction already supplies the requested dependence on μ. For μ>0 the root approaches the pole from below; for μ<0 it approaches from above. This labels roots by their nearby poles, avoiding an irrelevant finite shift in the enumeration of positive roots.
No positivity restriction on μ is required. The expansion requires μ=0 fixed and ∣μ∣an​≫1, so the displacement is small. It is not uniform as μ→0. At μ=0 the roots are exactly nπ, a different leading sequence; these cannot be recovered by setting μ=0 in the pole expansion. If μ varies with n, its size must be checked against the small-displacement and successive-term conditions rather than using the fixed-parameter remainder blindly.

The method of multiple scales introduces independent variables for the fast oscillation and its slow modulation, for example t0​=t, T=ϵt, and, when needed, T2​=ϵ2t. The derivative becomes d/dt=∂t0​​+ϵ∂T​+ϵ2∂T2​​+⋯. One expands the solution in ϵ while allowing its leading amplitudes and phases to depend on the slow times.
A solvability condition in the method of multiple scales removes resonant forcing of each fast homogeneous mode. Otherwise a correction contains a secular term, such as ϵtsint, and an ordinary asymptotic expansion fails on long times. The resulting slow amplitude equations incorporate that accumulated effect in the leading solution. One must still check that the amplitudes remain within the assumed weak-coupling regime and that omitted slower effects have not accumulated.

Thus exponential growth occurs when ∣Y∣>2∣α∣ and X=0, on the scale t=O(ϵ−1). Equality is a marginal case that can produce linear slow growth: for b=α=0 the displayed initial phase excites it, whereas for b=−α it does not. If X=0, x stays identically zero and the other oscillator is exactly uncoupled. The leading y amplitude is constant; its backreaction is a higher-order effect on this time scale.
These are leading approximations for bounded slow time T, hence t=O(ϵ−1), while the amplitudes stay O(1). First-order corrections can enforce the initial velocities exactly; the leading formula alone has an O(ϵ) initial-velocity mismatch. In the unstable case the expansion must not be extrapolated through arbitrarily large amplification, where weak coupling and omitted terms cease to be uniform.
For n=1, both leading oscillations have frequencies ±1, so their quadratic product contains only frequencies 0,±2. There is no first-order resonant coupling: the solvability condition in the method of multiple scales gives AT​=iαA/2 and BT​=0, a frequency shift without first-order amplitude growth. The constant and second-harmonic corrections can feed back into the fundamental at second order. Consequently the first possible growth scale from this second-order resonance from quadratic oscillator coupling is
T2​=ϵ2t=O(1),t=O(ϵ−2)​.
(15)
This identifies the possible slow scale, not a guaranteed instability for every parameter or initial state. In particular, the first-order α frequency shift may detune the second-order resonance unless appropriately tuned; a second-order amplitude analysis would decide actual growth.

Substitution of a normal mode gives the dispersion relation
D(k,ω)=−iω−αk2+βk4+γ=0,ω=iσ(k),σ(k)=αk2−βk4−γ.
(32)
For real k, σ is the temporal growth rate. Since β=0, its supremum is finite exactly when
β>0​.
(33)
For β<0, arbitrarily short wavelengths grow arbitrarily fast. For β>0, the finite maximum temporal growth rate is
σmax​={α2/(4β)−γ,−γ,​α>0,α<0.​
(34)
The Briggs-Bers criterion starts the inverse temporal Laplace transform above all temporal singularities and then deforms its contour downward while following the spatial roots. A finite growth bound supplies such an initial contour and a causal, high-frequency-controlled Green function. Unbounded temporal growth prevents that standard construction.
For absolute wave-packet instability, a candidate spatial pinch point must satisfy D=0, Dk​=0, and Imω>0. Here
Dk​=−2αk+4βk3=0
(35)
gives
k0​=0,ω0​=−iγ;k±​=±2βα​​,ω±​=i(4βα2​−γ).
(36)
Thus candidate growing saddles require γ<0 at k0​, or γ<α2/(4β) at k±​. Together with β>0, existence of at least one such candidate requires γ<α2/(4β).
A growing double root is not sufficient: the roots must pinch the spatial inversion contour from opposite sides. Collisions of branches originating in the same spatial half-plane do not obstruct the relevant deformation. This distinction is part of the Briggs-Bers criterion; it is stated, for example, in the primary study doi.org/10.1017/jfm.2016.195.
An explicit false spatial saddle in quartic dispersion is α=−2, β=1, γ=1/2. Its candidates k=±i have ω=i/2, but all real modes have σ=−k4−2k2−1/2<0. Each imaginary collision joins two branches in the same half-plane; neither is a relevant pinch. For ω=iΩ, the spatial roots obey k2=−1±1/2−Ω​, making those same-half-plane collisions transparent as Ω↓1/2.
For this particular real, even dispersion relation one can also establish the actual threshold directly. At the origin its impulse Green function is
G(0,t)=2π1​∫−∞∞​etσ(k)dk.
(37)
Laplace method selects the real maximum, and gives a positive prefactor times t−1/2eσmax​t because α=0. Therefore the actual absolute wave-packet instability condition is
β>0,{γ<α2/(4β),γ<0,​α>0,α<0.​​
(38)
These are sufficient for this model as well as necessary. They follow after identifying relevant real saddles; the earlier algebraic double-root test alone lacks the pinch information. Equality is marginal, not exponential absolute growth.

The ordinary Fourier transform does not exist. There is a real simple pole at x=−1, with
1+x31​=3(x+1)1​+O(1)(x→−1).
(42)
The two separate improper integrals diverge logarithmically. Thus no ordinary large-k expansion is defined under the stated integral convention. A Cauchy principal value or a contour prescription would be additional data, and must be stated rather than silently introduced.
For completeness, the symmetric Cauchy principal value has a precise answer. The real pole contributes the principal-value Fourier transform of a real pole
F(PV3(x+1)1​)=−3iπ​sgn(k)eik.
(43)
The other poles are z±​=(1±i3​)/2 with residues r±​=1/(3z±2​)=(−1∓i3​)/6. For k>0, close the contour integration in the lower half-plane, with clockwise orientation. The nonreal pole contributes −2πir−​e−ikz−​, while the real principal-value pole supplies the half-residue above. For k<0, the upper-half-plane contour gives the conjugate result. With s=sgn(k), the exact principal-value formula for k=0 is
f​PV​(k)=−3iπ​seik+3π​(3​+is)exp(−23​​∣k∣−2i​k)​.
(44)
These are its two nonzero asymptotic contributions: a nondecaying oscillatory real-pole term and an exponentially small complex-pole term. There is no second nonzero inverse-power term. This formula is qualified by the principal-value choice; it is not the ordinary transform requested in the statement.
Other prescriptions change the leading term. For example, replacing the real pole by its upper or lower boundary value changes the distribution by ∓iπδ(x+1)/3, and hence changes the transform by ∓iπeik/3. This demonstrates why a pole prescription is essential. The smooth Taylor series at the origin alone would miss the decisive real-pole contribution.
