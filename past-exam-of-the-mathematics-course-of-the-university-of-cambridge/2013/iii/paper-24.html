The martingale convergence theorem says that a discrete-time martingale (Mn​,Fn​) satisfying
supn​E∣Mn​∣<∞
(1)
has an almost sure convergence limit M∞​, finite almost surely and in L1. The theorem asserts that the limit is integrable; it does not assert convergence in L1. More generally, the almost sure submartingale convergence theorem applies to a submartingale with supn​EMn+​<∞. Uniform integrability is the additional condition that upgrades a martingale's convergence to convergence in L1.
For the requested distinction, let (ξk​) be independent fair Bernoulli variables and use their natural filtration. The coin-doubling martingale
M0​=1,Mn​=2n1{ξ1​=⋯=ξn​=1}​
(2)
is a nonnegative martingale: conditionally on Fn​, the next factor is 2ξn+1​ with mean one, so E(Mn+1​∣Fn​)=Mn​. Also E∣Mn​∣=EMn​=1 for every n, giving the required uniform L1 bound. The probability that all the Bernoulli variables equal one is limn​2−n=0. Therefore a zero is eventually encountered almost surely, after which Mn​ stays zero. Thus
Mn​⟶0 almost surely,E∣Mn​−0∣=1 for every n.​
(3)
There can be no other L1 limit, since convergence in L1 implies convergence in probability, whose limit must agree with the almost sure limit. This martingale satisfies the almost sure theorem but does not converge in L1.

A real Lévy process is a real-valued stochastic process (Xt​)t≥0​ with the following properties:
  • X0​=0 almost surely.
  • It has independent increments: increments over disjoint ordered time intervals are independent.
  • It has stationary increments: Xs+t​−Xs​ has the same law as Xt​ for s,t≥0.
  • It has stochastic continuity: Xs​→Xt​ in probability as s→t.
One convention also includes càdlàg paths in the definition. Equivalently, under the intrinsic definition above one chooses the càdlàg modification, which exists for such a stochastic process. Thus the usual working version of a Lévy process has right-continuous paths with left limits. There is no assumption of finite moments or continuous paths.

For fixed u, write h(t)=φXt​​(u). Independent increments and stationary increments give
h(s+t)=E[eiuXs​eiu(Xs+t​−Xs​)]=h(s)h(t),h(0)=1.
(10)
The preceding part gives continuity. Also h never vanishes: if h(t0​)=0 with t0​>0, then h(t0​/m)m=0 for every positive integer m, contradicting h(t0​/m)→1.
Here is a direct proof of the exponential form of Lévy characteristic functions. Choose a>0 small enough that ∣h(t)−1∣<1/2 on [0,a]. The principal complex logarithm gives a continuous L(t)=logh(t) there, with L(0)=0. For s,t≥0 with s+t≤a, the multiplicative identity implies
L(s+t)−L(s)−L(t)∈2πiZ.
(11)
This difference is continuous on the connected triangle of allowed (s,t) and equals zero at (0,0), so it is identically zero. Thus L satisfies the additive Cauchy functional equation locally. Subdivision gives L(a/m)=L(a)/m and L(ka/m)=kL(a)/m; continuity then gives L(t)=tL(a)/a for every t∈[0,a].
Define η(u)=L(a)/a. For any t≥0, choose an integer m with t/m≤a; then
φXt​​(u)=h(t)=h(t/m)m=etη(u).​
(12)
The coefficient is unique: if two coefficients give the same exponential for every t≥0, their derivatives at zero agree. In particular η(0)=0, and Reη(u)≤0 follows from ∣h(t)∣≤1. The characteristic exponent of a Lévy process has therefore been obtained from first principles, without invoking the Lévy–Khintchine formula.

Take two independent rate-one Poisson processes N+ and N−, and let
Xt​=Nt+​−Nt−​.
(13)
The difference of independent Poisson processes starts at zero and has stationary increments and independent increments. Its paths are càdlàg. For an interval of length h, the probability of any jump is 1−e−2h→0, proving stochastic continuity. Thus X is a Lévy process. The characteristic function of a Poisson distribution with mean t is exp(t(eiu−1)), so independence gives
EeiuXt​=exp(t(eiu−1)+t(e−iu−1))=e2t(cosu−1).​
(14)
The sample paths are integer-valued step functions with jumps +1 or −1. On every bounded interval there are only finitely many jumps, and independent Poisson arrival times coincide with probability zero. The combined arrival rate is two: holding times are independent exponentials of rate two, and each jump direction has probability 1/2, independently of the holding times. This is equivalently a Compound Poisson process of rate two with Rademacher distribution jump sizes. Its paths have finite variation on compact time intervals, although there are infinitely many jumps over the whole half-line almost surely.

A version of a stochastic process means a stochastic process on the same probability space such that, for every fixed t,
P(Xt​=Yt​)=1.
(25)
The exceptional null set may depend on t. Indistinguishability of stochastic processes means that there is one null set outside which Xt​=Yt​ for all t≥0 simultaneously.
For an example separating the definitions, let U have uniform distribution on (0,1), and set
Xt​=0,Yt​=1{t=U}​,t≥0.
(26)
For every fixed t, P(U=t)=0, so Y is a version of a stochastic process with original stochastic process X. But for every sample outcome the stochastic processes differ at its time t=U. Hence
P(Xt​=Yt​ for every t≥0)=0.​
(27)
The spike path of Y is not right-continuous at U, which explains why the next part's regularity assumption rules out this example.

Relative to a filtration (Ft​), progressive measurability means that, for every T<∞, the map
[0,T]×Ω⟶R,(t,ω)⟼Xt​(ω)
(29)
is measurable for the product sigma-algebra B([0,T])⊗FT​ and the Borel sigma-algebra on R.
Fix T>0 and divide [0,T] into 2m equal subintervals with mesh hm​=T/2m. Define
Xt(m)​=∑k=12m​Xkhm​​1[(k−1)hm​,khm​)​(t)+XT​1{T}​(t).
(30)
Since X is adapted, every random variable Xkhm​​ is Fkhm​​-measurable, hence FT​-measurable. Each approximation is consequently B([0,T])⊗FT​-measurable. For t<T, its sampling time lies strictly to the right of t, tends to t, and never exceeds T. Right continuity implies Xt(m)​→Xt​; at T equality is exact. Thus X is the pointwise limit of measurable functions on this product space. As T was arbitrary, X is progressively measurable. This is the theorem that right-continuous adapted processes are progressively measurable.
The right-endpoint approximations need not themselves be adapted at their intermediate times. What the proof requires is their joint measurability with respect to the single terminal sigma-algebra FT​. The proof uses the pathwise càdlàg convention. If path regularity is assumed only almost surely, under a completed filtration setting the stochastic process to zero on its common exceptional null event gives an indistinguishable progressively measurable version. Arbitrary values on that null event need not make the original stochastic process progressively measurable: even with a complete filtration, a null sample point may be assigned a non-Borel time function. This is why almost sure path regularity does not ensure progressive measurability.

Fix t and put σ=t∧τ. The stopping time property implies that σ is an Ft​-measurable random variable with values in [0,t]: for s<t,
{σ≤s}={τ≤s}∈Fs​⊆Ft​,

The evaluation map ω↦(σ(ω),ω) is measurable from (Ω,Ft​) to this product space: the inverse image of a measurable rectangle A×C is {σ∈A}∩C. Composing it with the jointly measurable stochastic process gives an Ft​-measurable random variable

(32)

The Skorokhod embedding of a centered random walk states that a random walk with independent identically distributed centered steps of finite variance σ2 can be realized on an appropriate probability space as
Sn​=BTn​​,0=T0​≤T1​≤T2​≤⋯,
(33)
where B is a standard Brownian motion and the Tn​ are finite stopping times. More precisely, the stopped positions have the same joint law as the given random walk, and the pairs
(Tn​−Tn−1​,BTn​​−BTn−1​​),n≥1,
(34)
may be chosen independent and identically distributed. Their spatial component has the step law, and E(Tn​−Tn−1​)=σ2. Repeating the one-step Skorokhod embedding theorem with the Strong Markov property gives this formulation. In the present normalization, the mean time increment is one, and the strong law of large numbers gives Tn​/n→1 almost surely.
The Donsker invariance principle states that the linearly interpolated diffusively rescaled random walk
Wn​(t)=n​S⌊nt⌋​+(nt−⌊nt⌋)X⌊nt⌋+1​​,0≤t≤1,S0​=0,
(35)
converges weakly as a random element of C[0,1], equipped with the uniform norm, to standard Brownian motion restricted to [0,1]. At t=1 the fractional term is zero. The only step assumptions needed here are zero mean, unit variance, and independent identical distributions; a higher moment or bounded support is not required. This is a functional central limit theorem, concerning the entire interpolated path rather than only its endpoint.
