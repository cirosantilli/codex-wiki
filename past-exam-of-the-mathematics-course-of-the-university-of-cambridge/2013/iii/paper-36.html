Let S=suppx be the support of a vector x, with ∣S∣≤s. Suppose the null space property holds. Every other feasible vector is z=x+v, where 0=v∈kerA. Splitting its L1 norm over S and Sc and using the triangle inequality gives
∥x+v∥1​=∥xS​+vS​∥1​+∥vSc​∥1​≥∥x∥1​−∥vS​∥1​+∥vSc​∥1​>∥x∥1​.
(1)
Thus the sparse vector x is the unique minimizer in basis pursuit. Notice that the argument works for complex coordinates: it uses the absolute value inequality, rather than a real sign function.
Conversely, suppose basis pursuit uniquely recovers every sparse vector of order s. Fix 0=v∈kerA and any S with ∣S∣≤s. Take x=−vS​ and z=vSc​. These vectors have the same measurements, because AvS​+AvSc​=0, and they are distinct since z−x=v=0. The vector x has at most s nonzero coordinates, so uniqueness gives
∥vS​∥1​=∥x∥1​<∥z∥1​=∥vSc​∥1​.
(2)
This is the null space property for every such S. Uniform unique recovery by basis pursuit is equivalent to the order-s null space property.

First the null space property implies sparse injectivity. If a nonzero v∈kerA had at most 2s nonzero coordinates, partition its support of a vector into disjoint S,T with ∣S∣,∣T∣≤s. Applying the null space property to each of these sets yields
∥vS​∥1​<∥vT​∥1​and∥vT​∥1​<∥vS​∥1​,
(3)
which is impossible. Hence the null space contains no nonzero sparse vector of order 2s.
The feasible vector x has ∥x∥0​≤s, where the L0 sparsity count counts its nonzero coordinates. Any different feasible z with ∥z∥0​≤∥x∥0​ would give a nonzero null space vector z−x with at most 2s nonzero coordinates, contrary to sparse injectivity. Consequently every different feasible z has strictly larger L0 sparsity count. The unique sparsest feasible vector is x:
Az=Axargmin​∥z∥0​={x}.​
(4)
This proof does not treat the L0 sparsity count as a genuine norm; no triangle inequality for it is needed.

Use the stated characterization by the Lq null space property. Raising an Lq quasi-norm comparison to its positive exponent preserves its order, so the hypothesis at q says that, for every nonzero v∈kerA and every ∣S∣≤s,
∑j∈S​∣vj​∣q<∑j∈/S​∣vj​∣q.
(5)
We prove the corresponding p inequality; this is monotonicity of uniform sparse recovery in the exponent.
Fix a nonzero null space vector and arrange its coordinate magnitudes as a1​≥⋯≥aN​≥0. Assume s≥1. The Lq null space property rules out a nonzero null space vector supported on at most s coordinates, so s<N and t=as​>0. Since p−q<0, the factors ajp−q​ are at most tp−q for j≤s and at least tp−q for j>s with aj​>0. Terms with aj​=0 contribute zero and require no negative power of zero. Thus
∑j=1s​ajp​≤tp−q∑j=1s​ajq​<tp−q∑j=s+1N​ajq​≤∑j=s+1N​ajp​.
(6)
The largest s coordinates maximize the p-power sum on any set of size at most s. Its complement therefore has the smallest complementary p-power sum. The displayed strict inequality proves the Lq null space property at exponent p for every allowed support of a vector. The stated recovery characterization now applies to the Lq quasi-norm at p.
If kerA={0}, the measurement constraint already singles out x, for every objective; if s=0, only the zero sparse vector needs recovery. These cases do not need a positive threshold. Uniform recovery at q implies uniform recovery at every 0<p<q:
q-recovery of order s ⟹ p-recovery of order s.​
(7)

Work in CN, the domain of the matrix A; the printed Cn in the introduction is a dimension typo. For a fixed nonempty support of a vector S, write u for the coordinates of x in S. Then
∥Ax∥22​−∥x∥22​=u∗(AS∗​AS​−I∣S∣​)u.
(8)
The Gram matrix AS∗​AS​ is a Hermitian matrix, so its difference from the identity matrix is also a Hermitian matrix. By the finite-dimensional spectral theorem, its matrix 2-norm is the largest absolute eigenvalue, equivalently
supu=0​∥u∥22​∣u∗(AS∗​AS​−I∣S∣​)u∣​=∥AS∗​AS​−I∣S∣​∥2→2​.
(9)
Indeed, an expansion in an orthonormal eigenbasis bounds every Rayleigh quotient by the largest absolute eigenvalue, and an appropriate eigenvector attains that bound. The restricted isometry constant must bound this quantity for every S of size at most s, and the maximum of these quantities suffices for all sparse vectors of order s. There are finitely many sets, so the maximum exists. The empty set contributes zero. Therefore
δs​(A)=∣S∣≤smax​∥AS∗​AS​−I∣S∣​∥2→2​.​
(10)

Use the natural empty-sum convention μ1​(0)=0 for cumulative coherence. This is needed for the printed case s=1. For x=0 the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise let S be its support of a vector, with k=∣S∣≤s. The Gram matrix G=AS∗​AS​ has diagonal entries one because the columns have unit Euclidean norm. Each off-diagonal row sum is bounded by
∑j∈S∖{i}​∣⟨ai​,aj​⟩∣≤μ1​(k−1)≤μ1​(s−1).
(11)
The last inequality uses monotonicity of cumulative coherence: enlarging an index set only adds nonnegative summands. There are enough indices to enlarge it because s≤N.
The Gershgorin circle theorem places every eigenvalue of G within distance μ1​(s−1) of one. Since G is a Hermitian matrix, its eigenvalues are real, and the finite-dimensional spectral theorem gives
(1−μ1​(s−1))∥x∥22​≤xS∗​GxS​=∥Ax∥22​≤(1+μ1​(s−1))∥x∥22​.
(12)
This is the cumulative coherence bound for restricted isometry. The lower bound remains valid when μ1​(s−1)>1, although it is then nonpositive. No assumption that the matrix is already a near isometry is required. The distortion is bounded by μ1​(s−1) on every order-s sparse vector.

For one-column Gram matrices, unit Euclidean norm of the columns gives AS∗​AS​=1. The restricted isometry constant formula therefore gives δ1​=0. For a two-column Gram matrix, put c=⟨ai​,aj​⟩. The difference from the identity matrix has the form
(0c​c0​),
(13)
up to the convention for the complex inner product. Its characteristic polynomial is λ2−∣c∣2, so the eigenvalues are ±∣c∣ and its matrix 2-norm is ∣c∣. Maximizing over pairs gives δ2​=μ, where μ is the mutual coherence.
Part (b), together with the minimality defining the restricted isometry constant, gives δs​≤μ1​(s−1). Every summand defining cumulative coherence is at most the mutual coherence, so μ1​(s−1)≤(s−1)μ. Thus, for normalized columns and N≥2,
δ1​=0,δ2​=μ,δs​≤μ1​(s−1)≤(s−1)μ(2≤s≤N).​
(14)
The upper range s≤N matters because the printed definition of cumulative coherence stops at N−1. For N=1, only δ1​=0 is needed; the maximum over pairs defining mutual coherence is otherwise empty.

Let σ=sgn(xS​), using the real sign function on the support of a vector x. The fixed-sign null space condition in the PDF uses Sc on its right-hand side. This complement is essential. For any real coordinate xj​=0, the supporting-line inequality for the absolute value is
∣xj​+vj​∣≥∣xj​∣+sgn(xj​)vj​.
(15)
Every distinct feasible vector is x+v with 0=v∈kerA. Summing the coordinate inequalities on S and adding the L1 norm on Sc gives
∥x+v∥1​−∥x∥1​≥⟨σ,vS​⟩+∥vSc​∥1​≥∥vSc​∥1​−∣⟨σ,vS​⟩∣>0.
(16)
The strict final inequality is precisely the fixed-sign null space condition. Hence x is the unique basis pursuit minimizer. Unlike the null space property of Question 1, this condition concerns the particular sign function values of x, rather than every vector supported in S.

Assume x is the unique basis pursuit minimizer. Fix 0=v∈kerA and set
α=⟨sgn(xS​),vS​⟩,β=∥vSc​∥1​.
(17)
There is ε>0 such that the sign function of xj​+tvj​ equals that of xj​ at every j∈S whenever ∣t∣<ε. To choose it, take less than the minimum of ∣xj​∣/∣vj​∣ over the nonzero vj​ in S; if that set is empty, any positive ε works. On this interval the L1 norm has the exact expression
∥x+tv∥1​−∥x∥1​=tα+∣t∣β.
(18)
For 0<t<ε, both x+tv and x−tv are distinct feasible vectors. Uniqueness forces their objective differences to be strictly positive, so α+β>0 and −α+β>0. Therefore
∣⟨sgn(xS​),vS​⟩∣<∥vSc​∥1​(0=v∈kerA).​
(19)
The fixed-sign null space condition is necessary as well as sufficient. This argument also covers an empty support of a vector: then α=0 and the nonzero null space vector has β>0. Strictness is indispensable: equality would make a sufficiently short feasible segment have the same objective as x.

Let u=A∗h be the strict dual certificate for basis pursuit supplied by condition (ii). For 0=v∈kerA, we have
0=⟨h,Av⟩=⟨A∗h,v⟩=⟨sgn(xS​),vS​⟩+∑l∈Sc​ul​vl​.
(20)
Here the adjoint operator is the real transpose. The injectivity of AS​ ensures vSc​=0: otherwise AS​vS​=0 would force v=0. Thus at least one nonzero term lies outside the support of a vector x. Since ∣ul​∣<1 at every such index,
∣⟨sgn(xS​),vS​⟩∣=​∑l∈Sc​ul​vl​​≤∑l∈Sc​∣ul​∣∣vl​∣<∑l∈Sc​∣vl​∣.

This proves the fixed-sign null space condition, so part (a) gives uniqueness in basis pursuit. If Sc is empty, the injectivity of AS​=A instead means there is no nonzero null space vector, and the feasible set is a singleton. Injective active columns and a strict dual certificate for basis pursuit ensure unique recovery. Both ingredients matter: strictness outside S cannot detect a nonzero null space direction supported entirely inside S.

Write σ=sgn(xS​) and G=AS∗​AS​. The injectivity of AS​ makes this Gram matrix a positive-definite matrix, since u∗Gu=∥AS​u∥22​>0 for u=0. Thus its matrix inverse exists. Construct the least-norm dual certificate
h=AS​(AS∗​AS​)−1σ.​
(22)
On the active coordinates, AS∗​h=GG−1σ=σ. For l∈/S, symmetry of the real Gram matrix and its matrix inverse gives
(A∗h)l​=al∗​AS​G−1σ=⟨G−1AS∗​al​,σ⟩.
(23)
Condition (iii) makes the absolute value of this coordinate strictly less than one. Consequently h is a strict dual certificate for basis pursuit, and part (c) applies. If S is empty, take h=0; the zero vector uniquely minimizes the L1 norm on its feasible set. Condition (iii) supplies an explicit certificate and therefore unique recovery. There is no claim that this particular least-norm dual certificate is necessary: other valid certificates may exist when this one fails.

Interpret the angle as the directed subspace angle defined by the infimum of projected unit vectors; it is different from the smallest angle between two subspaces. Write
a=cosθW,V⊥​>0,b=cosθV⊥,W​>0.
(24)
Consider the bounded linear operator T:W⟶V⊥ given by Tw=PV⊥​w. Its adjoint operator, between these two Hilbert spaces, is T∗u=PW​u: for w∈W and u∈V⊥, the orthogonal projections give ⟨Tw,u⟩=⟨w,u⟩=⟨w,PW​u⟩. The two positive directed subspace angle cosines yield
∥Tw∥≥a∥w∥,∥T∗u∥≥b∥u∥.
(25)
The first bound makes T injective and gives a closed range. Explicitly, if Twj​ converges, then ∥wj​−wk​∥≤a−1∥Twj​−Twk​∥, so (wj​) is a Cauchy sequence. The closed subspace of a Hilbert space W is complete, and its limit maps to the proposed range limit. The second bound gives kerT∗={0}. A vector u∈V⊥ orthogonal to the range has T∗u=0, so it must be zero. The range is therefore dense as well as closed in V⊥, and T is onto. This is the mechanism of invertibility from lower bounds on an operator and its adjoint.
For any f∈H, choose the unique w∈W with Tw=PV⊥​f. Then PV⊥​(f−w)=0, so f−w∈V. Moreover, if w∈W∩V, then Tw=0 and hence w=0. Every vector has a unique decomposition, and
H=W⊕V.​
(26)
The direct sum is a topological one as well: the component w=T−1PV⊥​f depends boundedly on f, with operator norm at most a−1.
For precision, the quoted equality of the norms of complementary oblique projections needs both summands nonzero. For example, with H=R, V=H and W={0}, the oblique projection is I, so ∥I∥=1 but ∥I−I∥=0. The secant function has value one here, so the second equality in the quoted formula fails. With nonzero complementary summands its intended version is valid. The proof above does not use that formula. The angle itself is undefined on a zero source space because it has no unit vectors; expressing the hypotheses as the two lower bounds handles zero spaces without ambiguity.

Use T=PV⊥​∣U​:U⟶V⊥. Positivity of the directed subspace angle cosine gives
∥Tu∥≥cosθU,V⊥​∥u∥,
(27)
so T is injective. The two vector spaces have the same finite dimension, n. By the rank-nullity theorem, T is also surjective. There is no need to assume a second positive directed subspace angle cosine in this finite-dimensional case.
For any f∈H, find u∈U with Tu=PV⊥​f. Then f−u∈V. If u∈U∩V, then Tu=0, and injectivity gives u=0. Hence
H=U⊕V.​
(28)
The direct sum is again bounded: u=T−1PV⊥​f. If n=0, then U={0} and V⊥={0}, so closedness of V gives V=H and the conclusion directly; no angle of an empty unit sphere is needed. Equal finite dimensions are essential to the surjectivity argument, whereas mere injectivity between infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces is insufficient.

The linear independence of the first n elements of each orthonormal system shows that Tn​ and Sn​ have the same finite dimension n and are closed subspaces of a Hilbert space. Apply part (b) with U=Tn​ and V=Sn⊥​. Since (Sn⊥​)⊥=Sn​, the positive directed subspace angle cosine gives
H=Tn​⊕Sn⊥​.
(29)
Let Q be the oblique projection onto Tn​ along Sn⊥​. Define f​n​=Qf. Its residual f−Qf is orthogonal to every ψj​ with 1≤j≤n, so the required measurements agree. Conversely, if g∈Tn​ has the same measurements, then f−g∈Sn⊥​; uniqueness of the direct sum decomposition gives g=Qf. Thus this is finite-dimensional Hilbert sampling reconstruction.
There is also an explicit coefficient description. Use the inner product convention linear in its first entry and put
Cjk​=⟨ϕk​,ψj​⟩,bj​=⟨f,ψj​⟩,f​n​=∑k=1n​ck​ϕk​.
(30)
Then Cc=b. The orthonormal systems show ∥PSn​​∑k​ck​ϕk​∥=∥Cc∥2​ and ∥∑k​ck​ϕk​∥=∥c∥2​, so the smallest singular value of C is cosθTn​,Sn​​>0. Hence c=C−1b, another direct proof of existence and uniqueness.
For n≥1, both summands of this direct sum are nonzero: the infinite orthonormal system (ψj​) contains ψn+1​∈Sn⊥​. The permitted oblique projection norm formula therefore applies without its degenerate exception, giving
∥Q∥=∥I−Q∥=secθTn​,Sn​​.
(31)
The operator norm immediately yields the stability estimate ∥f​n​∥≤secθTn​,Sn​​∥f∥. For the approximation bounds, put e=f−PTn​​f. Because Q fixes Tn​, we have
f−Qf=(I−Q)e,
(32)
so the operator norm estimate gives ∥f−Qf∥≤secθTn​,Sn​​∥e∥. For the lower bound, e⊥Tn​ while PTn​​f−Qf∈Tn​. The Pythagorean identity gives
∥f−Qf∥2=∥e∥2+∥PTn​​f−Qf∥2≥∥e∥2.
(33)
The unique measurement-matching reconstruction is stable and within the secant function factor of the best orthogonal projection approximation:
f​n​=Qf,∥f​n​∥≤secθTn​,Sn​​∥f∥,∥f−PTn​​f∥≤∥f−f​n​∥≤secθTn​,Sn​​∥f−PTn​​f∥.​
(34)
If a zero-dimensional reconstruction is admitted, it is simply f​0​=0 and its error equals the norm of f; that case is best stated directly instead of using the angle of a zero space.
