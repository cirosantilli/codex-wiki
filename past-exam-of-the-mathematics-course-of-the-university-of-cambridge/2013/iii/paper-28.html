For a Poisson distribution of parameter λ, its probability generating function is exp{λ(z−1)}. Applying the law of total expectation to the Poisson mixture gives
GN​(z)=E{E(zN∣λ)}=Ee(z−1)λ.
(3)
Thus
GN​(z)=Mλ​(z−1).​
(4)
For ∣z∣≤1 the absolute value of the integrand is bounded by one, so this identity always exists as a Laplace transform of the positive mixing variable. An extension to z>1 requires the corresponding moment-generating function to be finite; the conditional computation itself does not guarantee positive exponential moments.

The exponential distribution with rate β>0 has moment-generating function β/(β−t) for t<β. Shifting it by α multiplies the moment-generating function by eαt. Therefore the shifted-exponential Poisson mixture has probability generating function
GN​(z)=eα(z−1)β+1−zβ​.​
(5)
The probability generating function converges and is analytic for ∣z∣<β+1, and in particular is finite for real 0≤z<β+1.
Write p=β/(β+1) and q=1/(β+1), so p+q=1. The factor eα(z−1) is the probability generating function of V∼Poisson(α), while p/(1−qz) is that of a geometric distribution on {0,1,…}, with P(W=j)=pqj. Choose V and W independently. The product rule for the probability generating function of a sum of independent counts then proves N=dV+W. The support-zero convention for W is essential here.
The convolution of independent random variables gives the finite sum
pn​=e−αβ+1β​k=0∑n​k!αk​(β+1)−(n−k),n≥0.​
(6)
Taking the logarithmic derivative of the probability generating function gives
GN​(z)GN′​(z)​=α+β+1−z1​,(β+1−z)GN′​(z)={1+α(β+1)−αz}GN​(z).
(7)
Compare coefficients of zn−1, for n≥2. The left side is (β+1)npn​−(n−1)pn−1​, and the right side is {1+α(β+1)}pn−1​−αpn−2​. Hence the shifted-exponential Poisson count recursion is
pn​=n(β+1)n+α(β+1)​pn−1​−n(β+1)α​pn−2​,n≥2.​
(8)
The constant and linear coefficients supply the starting values
p0​=e−αβ+1β​,p1​=(α+β+11​)p0​.
(9)
These two values determine every subsequent probability by the recursion. The negative second term does not mean that the distribution has negative probabilities: the convolution formula exhibits every pn​ as a sum of nonnegative quantities.
At α=0 the independent Poisson distribution component is identically zero and the count has a geometric distribution. Both the finite sum and the recursion reduce to pn​=qpn−1​. Consequently the Panjer claim-count class parameters are
a=β+11​,b=0.​
(10)
Although the shifted model initially has positive α, this zero-shift boundary case is well defined.

For an aggregate claims model, let X denote one claim size, with expected value μ and variance σ2. The total S is zero when the claim count is zero. Given N=n, independence gives
E(S∣N)=Nμ,Var(S∣N)=Nσ2.
(11)
The law of total expectation and law of total variance, together with EN=Var(N)=λ for a Poisson distribution, imply
ES=λμ,Var(S)=λ(σ2+μ2)=λEX2.​
(12)
The raw second moment appears because the random count itself contributes λμ2 to the variance.
Conditional on N, the moment-generating function of the sum is MX​(t)N. Averaging with the Poisson distribution therefore gives the compound Poisson distribution transform
MS​(t)=exp{λ(MX​(t)−1)}.​
(13)
This is valid wherever MX​(t) is finite. Finite first and second moments alone do not ensure positive exponential moments; for positive claims the corresponding Laplace transform always exists.
For the independent portfolios put Λ=λ1​+λ2​>0 and wi​=λi​/Λ. Their Poisson distribution counts add to a Poisson distribution with parameter Λ. By Poisson-multinomial conditioning, conditional on the total count n the first risk count has a binomial distribution with parameters n,w1​, and the second is the remaining count. Thus one can generate the same total loss by drawing n independent risk labels with these weights, then drawing each claim from its label's law. The merged severity has mixture distribution
f(x)=w1​f1​(x)+w2​f2​(x).
(14)
It follows that T has a compound Poisson distribution with count parameter Λ and this severity law. This is the fixed-year version of Poisson superposition of insurance portfolios. Its expected value and variance are
ET=λ1​μ1​+λ2​μ2​,Var(T)=i=1∑2​λi​(σi2​+μi2​).​
(15)
Alternatively, multiplying the two independent aggregate Laplace transforms yields exp{Λ(w1​MX1​​(t)+w2​MX2​​(t)−1)} wherever finite, confirming the same compound Poisson distribution.
For the retained compound Poisson aggregate under per-claim reinsurance, replace each claim X by its retained payment g(X). Assume the retention is measurable, with 0≤g(x)≤x as usual. The count parameter remains Λ, and the severity law is the pushforward measure of the mixture severity under g. Thus the retained compound Poisson aggregate TI​ has a compound Poisson distribution with that transformed severity and
ETI​=i=1∑2​λi​Eg(Xi​),Var(TI​)=i=1∑2​λi​E[g(Xi​)2].​
(16)
Its moment-generating function is exp{∑i​λi​(Mg(Xi​)​(t)−1)} on its finite domain. For a general retention g, zero retained payments can occur and are allowed as compound-Poisson marks; they may equivalently be removed by Poisson thinning. The two specified contracts retain strictly positive payments for strictly positive claims.

For excess of loss reinsurance the insurer pays each claim up to its retention level:
g(x)=min(x,M),x−g(x)=(x−M)+​.​
(19)
The cap applies separately to every claim. In particular the retained annual loss is ∑j​min(Xj​,M), rather than a single cap on the annual total.
Let Fi​ be the cumulative distribution function for the claim size on risk i, and put Fi​=1−Fi​. The retained severity on that risk has the original probability density function on 0<y<M and an atom of a measure at M of mass Fi​(M). In the classical risk model write U(t)=u+ct−∑j=1N(t)​Xj​, where the Poisson process N(t) has rate λ and is independent of the claim sizes. Define the ruin time and ultimate ultimate ruin probability by
τu​=inf{t≥0:U(t)<0},ψ(u)=P(τu​<∞),u≥0.
(22)
The relative safety loading is ρ=c/(λμ)−1>0, so c=(1+ρ)λμ. The adjustment coefficient is the nonzero positive solution
λ{M(R)−1}=cR.​
(23)
Existence and uniqueness follow from the secant-slope existence criterion for an adjustment coefficient. Explicitly, λ(M(r)−1)−cr has derivative λμ−c<0 at zero and is strictly convex for positive claims. The assumed divergence of M makes it cross zero once at a positive R<r∞​. When r∞​=∞, an exponential lower bound from any positive tail event shows that M(r)/r→∞. In particular R is inside the finite-transform domain, not at its endpoint.
Put F=1−F. Replacing φ=1−ψ in the survival renewal equation for a classical risk model yields
ψ(u)=cλ​(μ−∫0u​F(x)dx)+cλ​∫0u​ψ(u−x)F(x)dx.
(24)
By the tail integral formula for moments, μ=∫0∞​F(x)dx. Therefore this is the defective renewal equation
ψ(u)=cλ​∫u∞​F(x)dx+cλ​∫0u​ψ(u−x)F(x)dx.
(25)
The original kernel has mass λμ/c=1/(1+ρ)<1. For the exponential tilt of the ruin renewal kernel, set
kR​(x)=cλ​eRxF(x),hR​(u)=cλ​eRu∫u∞​F(x)dx.
(26)
Multiplying by eRu gives the required proper renewal equation
Z(u)=hR​(u)+∫0u​Z(u−x)kR​(x)dx,Z(u)=eRuψ(u).​
(27)
To verify that this is a probability renewal kernel, Tonelli theorem gives, for r>0 in the finite-transform domain,
∫0∞​erxF(x)dx=E∫0X​erxdx=rM(r)−1​.
(28)
The adjustment coefficient equation consequently implies ∫0∞​kR​(x)dx=1. Its expected value is
mR​=∫0∞​xkR​(x)dx=cλ​R2RM′(R)−(M(R)−1)​=cRλM′(R)−c​.
(29)
It is finite because R is interior to the finite-transform domain and is positive because the strict convex crossing has derivative λM′(R)−c>0.
We quote the key renewal theorem in the following form: for a nonarithmetic distribution K of positive increments with finite positive mean m, and a directly Riemann integrable nonnegative function h, the locally bounded solution of z=h+z∗K satisfies z(u)→m−1∫0∞​h. It has renewal representation z=h∗∑j≥0​K∗j; this follows by iterating the equation, since the probability that arbitrarily many positive increments have sum at most a fixed u tends to zero.
Here KR​(dx)=kR​(x)dx is absolutely continuous, and hence nonarithmetic distribution, even if the original claim law has atoms. The function hR​ is continuous. Choose δ>0 with M(R+δ)<∞. The Markov inequality gives F(x)≤M(R+δ)e−(R+δ)x, and hence hR​(u)≤Cδ​e−δu. Continuity on compact intervals and this exponential bound make the upper Riemann sums finite with uniformly vanishing tails, proving direct Riemann integrability.
A further application of Tonelli theorem evaluates the forcing integral:
∫0∞​hR​(u)du​=cλ​∫0∞​F(x)∫0x​eRududx=cRλ​(RM(R)−1​−μ)=cRc−λμ​.​
(30)
All hypotheses of the key renewal theorem are now checked, so the interior adjustment coefficient ruin prefactor is
u→∞lim​eRuψ(u)=λM′(R)−cc−λμ​=M′(R)−(1+ρ)μρμ​.​
(31)
This proves the requested Cramér–Lundberg ruin asymptotic, including its constant.
For the two-component hyperexponential distribution, conditioning on the chosen exponential component gives
F(x)=21​e−x+21​e−x/2,x≥0,μ=23​.​
(32)
Its moment-generating function and derivative are
M(r)=2(1−r)1​+2(1−2r)1​,M′(r)=2(1−r)21​+(1−2r)21​,r<21​.
(33)
The moment-generating function diverges at 1/2, while the derivative of the adjustment equation at zero is negative. Strict convexity therefore places its unique positive adjustment coefficient in
0<R<21​.​
(34)
The adjustment equation, divided by R, becomes
2(1−R)1​+1−2R1​=23​(1+ρ).
(35)
Using this identity to subtract (1+ρ)μ from M′(R) gives
M′(R)−23​(1+ρ)=2(1−R)2R​+(1−2R)22R​.

Thus the asymptotic constant in terms of ρ and R is

Write m(λ)=E(Xi​∣λ) and v(λ)=Var(Xi​∣λ). The Bühlmann model uses the finite structural parameters
m0​=Em(λ),v=Ev(λ),a=Var(m(λ)).
(38)
Here v is the expected process variance and a is the variance of hypothetical means. The target is the latent conditional mean m(λ), rather than the realized next count. The Bühlmann credibility estimate is the best affine predictor of that target under mean squared error.
The law of total expectation and law of total variance give EXi​=m0​ and Var(Xi​)=v+a. Conditional independence gives Cov(Xi​,Xj​∣λ)=0 for i=j, and the law of total covariance therefore yields
Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=a(i=j),Cov(m(λ),Xi​)=a.
(39)
To derive the optimal predictor, consider d=A+∑i=1n​bi​Xi​. Minimizing with respect to the intercept gives A=m0​(1−∑i​bi​). Thus d=m0​+∑i​bi​(Xi​−m0​). The linear least-squares projection normal equations are
(vI+a11T)b=a1.
(40)
For v>0, subtraction of any two equations forces all bi​ equal. Substituting a common coefficient then gives bi​=a/(v+na). Equivalently, the mean squared error of the centered predictor is a−2a∑i​bi​+v∑i​bi2​+a(∑i​bi​)2, a convex quadratic with precisely these normal equations. Hence
mn​=Zn​Xn​+(1−Zn​)m0​,Zn​=na+vna​=n+Kn​,K=av​.​
(41)
The ratio notation assumes a>0; the credibility factor formula na/(na+v) also handles a=0,v>0. If a=0, the target is the constant m0​ almost surely. If v=0,a>0, one observation already equals m(λ) almost surely, and the average gives it exactly. If both vanish, the target and observations are constant.
The result optimizes over affine functions of the observations. It need not equal the unrestricted posterior mean; exact Bayesian inference generally depends on the whole prior and likelihood, whereas the Bühlmann credibility estimate uses these second-moment structural parameters.

Under quadratic loss, the Bayes estimator under squared error loss of the latent mean λ is its posterior mean. For the Poisson-uniform posterior mean, the prior distribution has density 1/2 on (1,3) and the one-count Poisson distribution likelihood is λe−λ. Consequently the Bayesian posterior density is proportional to λe−λ on that interval, with normalizing integral ∫13​λe−λdλ.
By conditional independence, E(X2​∣λ,X1​)=λ, so the law of total expectation makes the posterior predictive expected value equal to this same posterior mean. It is
E(λ∣X1​=1)=∫13​λe−λdλ∫13​λ2e−λdλ​.
(47)
The required antiderivatives are −(λ+1)e−λ and −(λ2+2λ+2)e−λ. Evaluating at the two endpoints gives
mBayes​=2e−1−4e−35e−1−17e−3​=2e2−45e2−17​≈1.85054.​
(48)
This estimate differs from 13/7: the Bühlmann credibility estimate is an optimal affine rule, while the Bayes estimator under squared error loss optimizes over all rules and uses the truncated uniform prior through its exact Bayesian posterior.
