An additive valuation on a field is a map v:K×→Γ, where Γ is a totally ordered abelian group, with v(xy)=v(x)+v(y) and v(x+y)≥min(v(x),v(y)) whenever x+y=0. Set v(0)=∞. We consider nontrivial valuations; if the trivial valuation is allowed, it must be listed separately from the asserted classification. Two valuations are equivalent when their valuation rings agree, or equivalently their ordered value-group images identify in a way compatible with the maps. Real-valued equivalent nontrivial valuations differ by positive scaling.
For a valuation on Q, every integer has nonnegative value, because it is a sum of copies of 1 or its negative. If all prime numbers had value zero, unique factorization would make the valuation trivial. Thus some prime p has v(p)>0. There cannot be two such primes: for distinct p,q, Bézout's identity gives ap+bq=1 with integers a,b, and the valuation inequality would give v(1)>0. All other prime values therefore vanish. Factoring the numerator and denominator of a rational number gives
v(x)=v(p)vp​(x)(x∈Q×).​
(1)
The image is the cyclic ordered group generated by v(p)>0, proving equivalence to the P-adic valuation. This is the classification of nontrivial valuations on the rational numbers; an Archimedean absolute value is not an additive valuation satisfying this ultrametric inequality.
The simple-root form of Hensel's lemma says that for a complete discrete valuation ring O with maximal ideal p, a polynomial f∈O[X] and a∈O with f(a)≡0(modp) and f′(a)≡0(modp) have a unique root in a+p.
For X3+1 over Q7​, all roots are integral: a negative valuation would make the leading term the unique term of lowest valuation. Modulo 7, the roots are 3,5,6, and 3X2 is nonzero at each. Hensel's lemma gives three distinct lifts, and a cubic has no further roots. The number is 3.
For X2+2X+4 over Q2​, a root is again integral and reduces to zero modulo 2. Write it as 2y with y∈Z2​. The equation becomes y2+y+1=0, impossible modulo 2. The number is 0.
For 3X3+X+3 over Q3​, a putative root of valuation k<0 gives term valuations 1+3k,k,1. The first is uniquely minimal, a contradiction. Thus all roots are integral. Reduction modulo 3 is X, whose root zero is simple since the derivative 9X2+1 is a unit everywhere. Hensel's lemma gives exactly one lift. The number is 1.

Normalize the discrete valuations so a uniformiser has value one, and write kK​,kL​ for the residue fields. The ramification index e is specified by vL​∣K​=evK​, and the residue degree is f=[kL​:kK​]. For finite extensions of complete discretely valued fields, [L:K]=ef. One way to see the degree equality is that OL​ is a finite free OK​-module of rank [L:K]; reduction modulo a uniformiser of K has e successive quotients isomorphic to kL​, hence dimension ef over kK​.
An unramified extension has e=1 and separable residue extension, equivalently [L:K]=f with separable residue extension. A totally ramified extension has f=1, equivalently [L:K]=e. The separability condition in the unramified extension definition matters if the residue field is imperfect; it is automatic for finite residue fields.
Suppose L/K is unramified. Choose a primitive element of a field extension xˉ for the finite separable extension kL​/kK​ and lift it to x∈OL​. Since the residue degree of K(x)/K is at least [kK​(xˉ):kK​]=[L:K], necessarily K(x)=L. Its monic minimal polynomial of an algebraic element fx​ has coefficients in OK​. Its reduction has degree [L:K] and annihilates xˉ, whose minimal polynomial over kK​ has that same degree. The two coincide, so fˉ​x​ is separable.
Conversely, suppose L=K(x), x∈OL​, and fˉ​x​ is separable. It must be irreducible: otherwise its coprime factors lift by the factorization form of Hensel's lemma, contradicting irreducibility of fx​. Hence xˉ has degree [L:K] over kK​. The degree equality forces f=[L:K] and e=1, and the residue extension is separable. We have proved the unramified generator criterion with separable reduction:
L/K unramified⟺L=K(x), x∈OL​, fˉ​x​ separable.​
(2)
Now let kL​=Fq​, q=pf. Every element of kL×​ is a simple root of Xq−1−1, whose derivative is a unit at each root. Hensel's lemma lifts each of these q−1 elements uniquely to a root in OL×​. Thus L contains all q−1 roots of Xq−1−1; these are the nonzero Teichmuller lifts.
More generally, a root of unity of order prime to p reduces injectively into kL×​. Indeed, if such a root reduces to 1, uniqueness in Hensel's lemma for Xm−1 makes it equal to 1. Consequently every prime-to-p order divides q−1.
A root whose order has a nontrivial p-part produces a primitive pth root of unity ζ. It reduces to 1 in characteristic p. Since
p=∏j=1p−1​(1−ζj),1−ζ1−ζj​=1+ζ+⋯+ζj−1≡j(modpL​),
(3)
all the factors have the same positive integral valuation. Hence
e(L/Qp​)=vL​(p)=(p−1)vL​(1−ζ)≥p−1.
(4)
This is the ramification bound for a primitive pth root of unity. Therefore
e<p−1⟹μ(L)=μpf−1​.​
(5)
For p=2 the hypothesis e<1 cannot occur, so that instance is vacuous rather than a claim excluding the ever-present root −1.

Put ζ=ζpn​, with n≥1. The shifted cyclotomic polynomial Φpn​(1+X) is Eisenstein at p: its constant term is p, and modulo p it is Xpn−1(p−1). Therefore L/Qp​ is totally ramified of degree pn−1(p−1) and π=ζ−1 is a uniformiser.
The Galois group is (Z/pnZ)×, with σa​(ζ)=ζa. For a=1, let r=vp​(a−1), 0≤r<n. Then ζa−1 is a primitive pn−rth root. Its difference from one is a uniformiser in the corresponding smaller cyclotomic field, and the relative ramification index is pr. Hence
vL​(σa​(π)−π)=vL​(ζa−1−1)=pr.

G−1​=G0​Gi​Gi​​=(Z/pnZ)×,=Uj​={1}​​if 1≤j≤n−1,pj−1≤i≤pj−1,if i≥pn−1.​​

Write α=33​ and ζ=ζ3​. The cubic is Eisenstein and adjoining ζ adds at most a quadratic extension, so [L:Q3​]≤6. Set
π=αζ−1​.
(11)
Using ζ2+ζ+1=0 and α3=3, compute π3=1+2ζ and π6=−3. Thus π satisfies the Eisenstein polynomial X6+3. This proves L=Q3​(π), degree six, with π a uniformiser and the extension totally ramified. It is the splitting field of X3−3, so its Galois group is S3​. This is the Eisenstein sextic presentation of the splitting field of X3 minus 3 over Q3.
The six automorphisms have σ(α)=ζaα, a∈{0,1,2}, and σ(ζ)=ζb, b∈{1,2}. Since vL​(3)=6, we have vL​(α)=2 and vL​(ζ−1)=3.
If b=1 and a=0, then σ(π)=ζ−aπ, whence vL​(σ(π)−π)=1+3=4. These are the two nonidentity elements of the cyclic subgroup C3​.
If b=2, use ζ2−1=(ζ−1)(ζ+1)=−(ζ−1)ζ2. Then σ(π)=−ζ2−aπ. The coefficient −ζ2−a−1 reduces to −2=0 in the residue field F3​, so the difference has valuation one. These three automorphisms are the transpositions.
The uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups consequently gives
G−1​=G0​=S3​,G1​=G2​=G3​=C3​,Gi​={1} (i≥4).​

As a consistency check, the different exponent is (6−1)+3(3−1)=11. The derivative of the monogenic Eisenstein polynomial gives the same value, vL​(6π5)=6+5=11. In particular, stopping the wild filtration at G1​ would give the wrong different.

A place of a number field is an equivalence class of nontrivial absolute values on a field. Its finite places correspond to nonzero prime ideals of the ring of integers of a number field; its infinite places come from real embeddings and conjugate pairs of complex embeddings.
For a embedding of a number field into a p-adic algebraic closure ι:K↪Q​p​, pull back the p-adic absolute value. This gives a place above p. Every element of Gal(Q​p​/Qp​) preserves that absolute value, by uniqueness of its extension to each finite local extension, so equivalent embeddings give the same place.
Conversely, a place v above p gives a completion of a number field at a prime ideal Kv​, a finite extension of Qp​. Embed it into Q​p​ and restrict to K. If two embeddings give the same place, they extend to two Qp​-embeddings of this same completion. They are conjugate under the absolute Galois group: their finite separable images lie in a finite normal closure, and an isomorphism of such images extends to an automorphism of the algebraic closure. The completion really is the closure of the embedded K, since rational coefficients are dense in the Qp​-span of a primitive element. Thus we obtain the embedding orbit description of finite places:
{v∣p} ⟷ Gal(Q​p​/Qp​)\Emb(K,Q​p​).​
(13)
For the product formula, use normalized local factors
∣x∣p​=(Np)−ordp​(x),∣x∣v​=∣σ(x)∣ at a real place,∣x∣v​=∣σ(x)∣2 at a complex place.
(14)
The squared modulus at a complex place counts its two embeddings. Equivalently one can use ordinary complex modulus and put exponent two in the product. These normalized local factors for the product formula must be specified; arbitrary representatives of place classes would not satisfy the unweighted printed formula.
The fractional principal ideal (x) has only finitely many nonzero prime exponents. Its ideal norm is ∣NK/Q​(x)∣: for integral x, multiplication by x on an integral basis has this determinant in absolute value, equal to the index of (x) in OK​; a quotient gives the fractional case. Hence the finite-place product is ∣NK/Q​(x)∣−1. The infinite-place product is ∣NK/Q​(x)∣, by the embedding expression for the field norm. Therefore all but finitely many local factors are one, and
v∏​∣x∣v​=1.​
(15)
Finally, let ΔK​=0 be the integer field discriminant, defined by the determinant of the trace pairing on an integral basis. If a rational prime p ramifies, the algebra OK​/pOK​ has a nonzero nilpotent element: use prime ideal factorization and the Chinese remainder theorem to see a nonzero nilpotent in a factor OK​/pe with e>1. If y is nilpotent, multiplication by yz is nilpotent for every z in this commutative algebra, and therefore has trace zero. Thus y is in the radical of its trace pairing, making the reduced discriminant zero. We have proved the discriminant obstruction to ramification:
p ramifies⟹p∣ΔK​.
(16)
Only finitely many rational primes divide this nonzero integer, so only finitely many primes ramify.

The idele group is the multiplicative restricted product
JK​=∏v′​Kv×​={(xv​):xv​∈Kv×​, xv​∈Ov×​ for all but finitely many finite v}.
(17)
Its restricted product topology has basic open sets ∏v∈S​Uv​×∏v∈/S​Ov×​, where S is finite and contains every infinite place, and Uv​ is open in Kv×​. In particular, the restriction to almost all local unit groups is part of the topology, not merely the unrestricted product topology.
The diagonal map K×→JK​ is injective and well-defined by finite support of the principal ideal's valuations; its image consists of principal ideles. To prove discreteness of principal ideles, take the neighbourhood of 1 which requires xv​∈Ov×​ at every finite place and ∣xv​−1∣<1/2 in the ordinary real or complex modulus at every infinite place. A diagonal element in it is a global unit, so x−1∈OK​. If x=1, the nonzero integer NK/Q​(x−1) has absolute value at least one, but the archimedean bounds make its absolute value less than 2−[K:Q]. This contradiction proves K× is a discrete subgroup of JK​.
For a modulus of a number field, write m for its finite ideal part together with any selected real places. Let Im​ be the group of fractional ideals coprime to the finite part. Let Pm​ consist of principal ideals (a) with a≡1(modpvmv​​) locally at each finite place in the modulus, and a>0 at each selected real place. The ray class group is
Clm​(K)=Im​/Pm​.​
(18)
Equivalently it is the quotient JK​/(K×Um​), with the corresponding principal-unit factors at finite places and positive multiplicative factors at selected real places. The question now assumes that no infinite places occur.
Put Rm​=∏v∈S​Ov×​/(1+πvmv​​Ov​). Global units map to Rm​ by reduction. To map Rm​ to the ray class group, choose, by the Chinese remainder theorem, an integral a having the prescribed unit residues at all v∈S, and send the tuple to the ray class of (a). A different choice changes the ratio by an element congruent to one at every such place, so the map is well-defined and multiplicative.
The forgetful map to the ordinary ideal class group is surjective: weak approximation for number fields multiplies an ideal by a principal ideal so that all its valuations in S vanish. Its kernel consists exactly of classes represented by principal ideals coprime to S, and their generators supply the local unit residues. Finally, a residue tuple maps to the identity ray class precisely when its chosen a differs from a congruence-one generator by a global unit. Thus the ray class exact sequence is
OK×​⟶Rm​⟶Clm​(K)⟶Cl(K)⟶0.​
(19)
The leftmost arrow need not be injective, explaining the absence of an initial zero.
For K=Q(2​), the ring of integers of Q of square root two is Z[2​]. Its absolute field norm is a Euclidean function. Indeed, for a+b2​∈K, choose nearest integers m,n; writing u=a−m, w=b−n, both bounded by 1/2, gives ∣u2−2w2∣≤1/2<1. Applying this to a quotient proves Euclidean division, hence the ring is a principal ideal domain and Cl(K)=0. This is norm-Euclideanity of the integers adjoined square root two.
Write p=(2​) and q=(1+22​). Their ideal norms are 2 and 7, respectively; p2=(2) and the residue field at q is F7​, with 2​≡3 because 1+22​≡0. The finite local quotient product for the requested modulus is
Rm​≅(F2​[t]/(t2))××F7×​≅C2​×C6​.
(20)
The first factor consists of 1 and 1+t.
The given fundamental unit ϵ=1+2​ and −1 generate the global units. Their images are
ϵ⟼(1+t,4),−1⟼(1,6).
(21)
The first image has order six; its cube is (1+t,1) and its square is (1,2), generating the first factor and the order-three subgroup of the second. The second image supplies the missing order-two element of F7×​. Thus the global unit map is surjective onto all 12 local unit residues. By the exact sequence, or the unit-surjectivity criterion for a trivial finite ray class group,
Cl2(v)+(v′)​(Q(2​))=0.​
(22)
There is no positivity condition here: the modulus has no infinite part, so using the unit −1 is legitimate and essential in the surjectivity calculation.
