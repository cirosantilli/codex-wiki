A quadratic form on a finite-dimensional real vector space is a function q(v)=B(v,v) for a symmetric bilinear form B. Conversely B(u,v)=21​(q(u+v)−q(u)−q(v)) recovers that bilinear form. Two quadratic forms are congruent if a linear isomorphism T satisfies q2​(v)=q1​(Tv); in coordinates this is matrix congruence, A2​=PTA1​P, with P invertible.
Sylvester's law of inertia says that a real quadratic form admits coordinates in which
q=∑j=1p​uj2​−∑j=p+1p+q​uj2​,
(1)
and the numbers of positive, negative and zero squares, (p,q,r), are independent of the coordinates. Here p+q+r=dimV; p and q are the maximal dimensions of subspaces on which the quadratic form is positive definite and negative definite, while r is the dimension of its radical of a bilinear form. These numbers are the inertia of a bilinear form. The rank of a quadratic form is p+q, and the signature of a quadratic form, in the signed-difference convention, is p−q.
Completing squares gives
q(x,y,z)=−2(x−2y​−3z)2−21​y2+19z2.
(2)
The change of coordinates is invertible, so the inertia is (1,2,0) and the signature is −1. If signature is recorded as an ordered pair instead, that pair is (1,2).

The conjugacy class of x is its orbit under the conjugation action, and its stabilizer is the centralizer CG​(x). The orbit-stabilizer theorem gives
∣ClG​(x)∣=[G:CG​(x)].
(3)

Rouché's theorem states that if f,g are holomorphic functions on a neighbourhood of a simple closed contour and its interior, and ∣g∣<∣f∣ on the contour, then f and f+g have the same number of zeros inside, counted with multiplicity.
On ∣z∣=1, use f=6z2: the other terms have absolute value at most 1+3+1=5<6. Hence there are two zeros inside the unit circle. On ∣z∣=2, use f=3z7: its modulus is 384, whereas the other terms have total modulus at most 256+24+1=281. Hence there are seven zeros inside the larger circle. The strict inequalities also exclude zeros on either boundary. There are 7−2=5 roots in the annulus, counted with multiplicity.

ϕ(z)=ϵ0​σz​,V=ϵ0​σd​.​

A matrix is in Jordan normal form if it is block diagonal with Jordan blocks Jr​(λ)=λI+N, where N has ones immediately above the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. For a Jordan normal form matrix J, number its diagonal positions 1,…,n and put
Jm​=J+εm​diag(1,…,n).
(22)
Choose positive εm​→0 avoiding the finitely many values solving λi​+iε=λj​+jε for i=j. The matrix remains upper triangular and now has distinct eigenvalues, so it is diagonalizable. Its entries converge to those of J. For arbitrary A=SJS−1, use Am​=SJm​S−1. This proves density of diagonalizable complex matrices.
If A is diagonalizable with eigenvalues λ1​,λ2​, conjugating B by the same basis change conjugates TA​ to Tdiag(λ1​,λ2​)​. On the four matrix units Eij​, this acts by (λi​+λj​)Eij​. Thus, writing s=trA and d=detA, its characteristic polynomial is
χTA​​(t)​=(t−2λ1​)(t−2λ2​)(t−λ1​−λ2​)2=(t−s)2(t2−2st+4d)​.​

The entries of TA​ depend linearly on those of A, and the coefficients of its characteristic polynomial depend polynomially, hence continuously, on those entries. This is the two-by-two anticommutator characteristic polynomial.

The contraction mapping theorem states that a map f of a nonempty complete metric space into itself, satisfying d(fx,fy)≤qd(x,y) for some 0≤q<1, has a unique fixed point. Iteration from any point converges to it; for example d(fnx,τ(f))≤qnd(x,fx)/(1−q).
Assume X is nonempty. Its boundedness makes δ finite. Symmetry, positivity and separation follow pointwise from d, and taking the supremum of d(fx,hx)≤d(fx,gx)+d(gx,hx) proves the triangle inequality. Hence δ is a uniform metric.
If (fn​) is a Cauchy sequence in this uniform metric, each (fn​(x)) converges by completeness of X; denote its limit by f(x). Given ϵ>0, choose N so that δ(fn​,fm​)<ϵ for n,m≥N. Letting m→∞ gives d(fn​(x),f(x))≤ϵ for all x, so fn​→f uniformly. To prove continuity, fix x and use
d(f(y),f(x))≤d(f(y),fN​(y))+d(fN​(y),fN​(x))+d(fN​(x),f(x)).
(25)
Choose N so the outside terms are small, then use continuity of fN​ for the middle term. Thus f∈Maps(X,X), proving completeness of the continuous-map space in the uniform metric.
The contraction subspace is not necessarily complete. On X=[0,1], fn​(x)=(1−1/n)x, n≥2, are contraction mappings and converge to the identity by uniform convergence. Their only possible uniform limit is not a contraction, so this Cauchy sequence has no limit in C.
Nevertheless the fixed point assignment is continuous. Fix f∈C with contraction constant qf​<1, and put u=τ(f), v=τ(g). Then
d(u,v)=d(fu,gv)≤d(fu,fv)+d(fv,gv)≤qf​d(u,v)+δ(f,g).
(26)
Consequently d(τ(f),τ(g))≤δ(f,g)/(1−qf​). This proves continuity of the fixed-point assignment at f without requiring one contraction constant valid for every g.

Fix x, and let Cx​ be the union of all connected subsets containing x. Their union is connected: a separation of the union would place x on one side, and every connected member meeting that side would have to lie entirely there. The other side would be empty. By the defining equivalence relation this union is exactly the connected component of x.
The closure of a connected set is connected as well. Indeed, if its closure had a separation into relatively open nonempty pieces, density would make both pieces meet the original set and give a separation of that connected set. Therefore Cx​​ is a connected subset containing x, so maximality gives Cx​​⊆Cx​. The reverse inclusion is automatic. Every connected component is connected and closed.

Let C and D be the connected components of x and y. First, C×D is connected in the product topology. For each c∈C, the connected slice {c}×D meets the connected slice C×{y}. Their union over c is connected by the common-intersection argument, and is exactly C×D.

A sufficient statistic is one for which the conditional distribution of the full sample, given its value, is independent of the parameter. For independent observations the likelihood is ∏i=16​pini​​, depending on the data only through the counts. The factorization criterion for sufficiency therefore makes (n1​,…,n6​) sufficient. Directly, all ordered samples with given counts have the same probability, so their conditional distribution is uniform over the n!/∏ni​! possible arrangements, independently of the pi​.
