A group action of G on X is a map (g,x)↦gx such that ex=x and (gh)x=g(hx). The orbit and stabilizer of x are
Gx={gx:g∈G},Gx​={g∈G:gx=x}.
(23)
For a finite group, the map
gGx​⟼gx
(24)
is a well-defined bijection from the left cosets of Gx​ to Gx. Each coset has ∣Gx​∣ elements, so the orbit-stabilizer theorem is
∣G∣=∣Gx∣∣Gx​∣​.
(25)
The Cauchy theorem for groups says that if a prime number p divides ∣G∣, then G has an element of order p. To prove it, let
X={(g1​,…,gp​)∈Gp:g1​⋯gp​=e}.
(26)
The first p−1 entries determine the last, so ∣X∣=∣G∣p−1, which is divisible by p. The cyclic group Cp​ acts on X by cyclically rotating the entries; rotation preserves the product condition because
g2​⋯gp​g1​=g1−1​(g1​⋯gp​)g1​=e.
(27)
Every orbit has size one or p. The fixed points are exactly the tuples (g,…,g) with gp=e. Their number is therefore divisible by p. Since the identity gives one fixed point, there is another, and its entry has order p.
Now let ∣G∣=33. Cauchy's theorem gives a subgroup H of order 11. Let H act by the conjugation action on the set X of subgroups of order 11. It fixes H. If it also fixed K=H, then H would normalize K; since H∩K={e}, the product HK would be a subgroup of order 121, which is impossible. Every other H-orbit in X therefore has size 11, so
∣X∣≡1(mod11).
(28)
Distinct members of X share only the identity and each contributes ten nonidentity elements. Hence 1+10∣X∣≤33, forcing ∣X∣=1. Thus H is a normal subgroup.
Conjugation now defines a group homomorphism
G⟶Aut(H).
(29)
Because H≅C11​, its automorphism group has order 10. By the Lagrange theorem, the image has order dividing both 33 and 10, so the image is trivial and H lies in the center of G. Cauchy's theorem also supplies x of order 3. If h generates H, then h and x commute and hx has order lcm(11,3)=33. Therefore
G=⟨hx⟩≅C33​​.
(30)
