Let g have finite order n. A group homomorphism preserves the group operation and the identity, so
ϕ(g)n=ϕ(gn)=ϕ(e)=e.

A group G is abelian when xy=yx for every x,y∈G. It is cyclic when some generator of a group g satisfies
G=⟨g⟩={gn:n∈Z}.
(6)
If x=gr and y=gs belong to a cyclic group, then
xy=gr+s=gs+r=yx,
(7)

The Klein four-group C2​×C2​ is abelian, but every nonidentity element has order two, so no element generates all four elements. Thus an abelian group need not be cyclic.
The condition on proper subgroups does not force G to be abelian. The quaternion group
Q8​={±1,±i,±j,±k}
(8)

⟨i⟩,⟨j⟩,⟨k⟩,
(9)
each of order four. They are all cyclic, so
the answer is no​.

A group action of G on X is a map (g,x)↦gx such that ex=x and (gh)x=g(hx). The orbit and stabilizer of x are
Gx={gx:g∈G},Gx​={g∈G:gx=x}.
(23)
For a finite group, the map
gGx​⟼gx
(24)
is a well-defined bijection from the left cosets of Gx​ to Gx. Each coset has ∣Gx​∣ elements, so the orbit-stabilizer theorem is
∣G∣=∣Gx∣∣Gx​∣​.
(25)
The Cauchy theorem for groups says that if a prime number p divides ∣G∣, then G has an element of order p. To prove it, let
X={(g1​,…,gp​)∈Gp:g1​⋯gp​=e}.
(26)
The first p−1 entries determine the last, so ∣X∣=∣G∣p−1, which is divisible by p. The cyclic group Cp​ acts on X by cyclically rotating the entries; rotation preserves the product condition because
g2​⋯gp​g1​=g1−1​(g1​⋯gp​)g1​=e.
(27)
Every orbit has size one or p. The fixed points are exactly the tuples (g,…,g) with gp=e. Their number is therefore divisible by p. Since the identity gives one fixed point, there is another, and its entry has order p.
Now let ∣G∣=33. Cauchy's theorem gives a subgroup H of order 11. Let H act by the conjugation action on the set X of subgroups of order 11. It fixes H. If it also fixed K=H, then H would normalize K; since H∩K={e}, the product HK would be a subgroup of order 121, which is impossible. Every other H-orbit in X therefore has size 11, so
∣X∣≡1(mod11).
(28)
Distinct members of X share only the identity and each contributes ten nonidentity elements. Hence 1+10∣X∣≤33, forcing ∣X∣=1. Thus H is a normal subgroup.
Conjugation now defines a group homomorphism
G⟶Aut(H).
(29)
Because H≅C11​, its automorphism group has order 10. By the Lagrange theorem, the image has order dividing both 33 and 10, so the image is trivial and H lies in the center of G. Cauchy's theorem also supplies x of order 3. If h generates H, then h and x commute and hx has order lcm(11,3)=33. Therefore
G=⟨hx⟩≅C33​​.
(30)

A Möbius transformation of the Riemann sphere is a map
z⟼cz+daz+b​,ad−bc=0,
(31)
with its natural values at the pole and at infinity.
For three distinct points z1​,z2​,z3​, define
Tz​(ζ)=(ζ−z3​)(z1​−z2​)(ζ−z2​)(z1​−z3​)​.
(32)
The usual limiting conventions cover an infinite zi​. This Möbius transformation sends (z1​,z2​,z3​) to (1,0,∞). Defining Tw​ similarly, the map
f=Tw−1​∘Tz​​
(33)
sends zi​ to wi​. If two Möbius transformations do so, their quotient fixes 0,1,∞. A Möbius transformation fixing infinity is affine, and fixing zero and one then makes it the identity. This proves uniqueness.
With this convention, the cross-ratio is
[z1​,z2​,z3​,z4​]=Tz​(z4​)=(z4​−z3​)(z1​−z2​)(z4​−z2​)(z1​−z3​)​.
(34)
Substitution shows that translations, nonzero scalings, and inversion preserve it; since these generate the Möbius group, every Möbius transformation preserves cross-ratios.
Conversely, suppose a bijection f of the Riemann sphere preserves every cross-ratio. Let m be the unique Möbius transformation agreeing with f at three chosen points z1​,z2​,z3​. For any other z, preservation by f and m gives
[z1​,z2​,z3​,z]=[f(z1​),f(z2​),f(z3​),f(z)]=[f(z1​),f(z2​),f(z3​),m(z)].
(35)
The last coordinate in a cross-ratio with three fixed distinct entries is injective, so f(z)=m(z). The equality already holds at the three base points, hence f=m everywhere and f is Möbius.
Finally, the map z↦az+b is constant when a=0 and therefore is not Möbius. If a=0, it is bijective and fixes infinity. Were it Möbius, it would have the affine form αz+β. Equality on real z forces α=a and β=b, whereas equality at z=i would require ai=−ai, contradicting a=0. Thus
there are no such a,b​.
(36)

A subgroup N≤G is normal when
gNg−1=N(g∈G).
(37)
Its left and right cosets then agree, and multiplication
(gN)(hN)=ghN
(38)
is well defined on the cosets. The identity is N, the inverse of gN is g−1N, and associativity descends from G, so these cosets form the quotient group G/N.
For a group homomorphism θ:G→H, its kernel and image are
kerθ={g:θ(g)=eH​},imθ={θ(g):g∈G}.
(39)
The kernel is normal because
θ(gkg−1)=θ(g)θ(k)θ(g)−1=eH​
(40)
whenever k∈kerθ. Conversely, if K⊴G, the quotient map
q:G⟶G/K,q(g)=gK,
(41)
is a homomorphism with kernel K. The image of any homomorphism is closed under products and inverses, so it is a subgroup of H. Finally, the map
G/kerθ⟶imθ,gkerθ⟼θ(g)
(42)
is well defined and bijective and preserves multiplication. This is the first isomorphism theorem.
Define

Euler's formula shows that Φ is a homomorphism, its image is the complex unit circle, and its kernel is Z. The first isomorphism theorem therefore gives

(44)
The image of Q/Z consists exactly of the roots of unity: if t=p/q, then Φ(t)q=1, while every element of finite order on the unit circle has an argument that is a rational multiple of 2π.

The set S(N) consists of all bijections N→N. The composition of two bijections is a bijection, composition is associative, the identity map is an identity, and every bijection has an inverse, so S(N) is a group.
An element of Sfin​(N) has finite support. The support of a composition is contained in the union of the two supports, and a permutation and its inverse have the same support. The identity has empty support. Hence Sfin​(N) is a subgroup.
Let σ be a cycle and choose n that it moves. Because σ has finite support, the sequence
n,σ(n),σ2(n),…
(45)
must repeat. Since σ is invertible, its first repetition returns to n; let the least positive return time be l. The cycle condition says that every moved point occurs in this orbit, so σl fixes every point. No smaller positive power fixes n. Therefore
ord(σ)=l​,
(46)
which is finite.
For τ∈Sfin​(N), partition its finite support into the orbits of the cyclic group ⟨τ⟩. On each orbit, let σi​ agree with τ and fix every point outside that orbit. Then each σi​ is a permutation cycle, their supports are pairwise disjoint, and
τ=σ1​⋯σk​.
(47)
Disjoint cycles commute because at every point at most one of them acts nontrivially.
Writing li​=ord(σi​), a power τm is the identity exactly when every σim​ is the identity, equivalently when every li​ divides m. Thus
ord(τ)=lcm(l1​,…,lk​)​.
(48)
