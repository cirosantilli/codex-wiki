The infinitesimal generator of a semigroup is the linear operator
Au=limh↓0​hU(h)u−u​
(1)
with generator domain
D(A)={u∈H:limh↓0​hU(h)u−u​ exists in H}.
(2)
For example, the Bochner integral
ut​=∫0t​U(s)uds
(3)
belongs to D(A) for every u∈H and t>0, since Aut​=U(t)u−u.
To prove that A is a closed linear operator, suppose un​∈D(A), un​→u, and Aun​→v. For vectors in the generator domain,
U(t)un​−un​=∫0t​U(s)Aun​ds.
(4)
Passing to the limit in the Banach space gives
U(t)u−u=∫0t​U(s)vds.
(5)
After division by t, strong continuity makes the right side converge to v as t↓0. Hence u∈D(A) and Au=v, so A is closed.
For Rez>ω, define the Bochner integral
R(z)u=∫0∞​e−ztU(t)udt.
(6)
It converges absolutely because
∥e−ztU(t)u∥≤Me−(Rez−ω)t∥u∥.
(7)
Integrating the semigroup difference quotient shows that R(z)u∈D(A) and (zI−A)R(z)u=u. The same computation for u∈D(A) gives R(z)(zI−A)u=u. Thus the Laplace-transform formula for a semigroup resolvent proves
{z∈C:Rez>ω}⊆ρ(A)​,∥R(z)∥≤Rez−ωM​.
(8)

The Laplace-transform formula for a semigroup resolvent is locally uniformly convergent in the half-plane Rez>ω, so it may be differentiated under the Bochner integral:
dzndn​R(z)u=(−1)n∫0∞​tne−tzU(t)udt.
(9)
The resolvent identity gives R′(z)=−R(z)2 and, by mathematical induction,
dzndn​R(z)=(−1)nn!R(z)n+1.
(10)
Therefore
n!(zI−A)−(n+1)u=∫0∞​tne−tzU(t)udt​.
(11)
For real λ>ω, the integral triangle inequality and the Gamma integral give
n!∥(λI−A)−(n+1)u∥​≤M∥u∥∫0∞​tne−(λ−ω)tdt=(λ−ω)n+1Mn!​∥u∥.​
(12)
Consequently
∥(λI−A)−(n+1)u∥≤(λ−ω)n+1M​∥u∥​.
(13)
The exponent −n+1 printed in the question is a typographical error: already at n=0 it contradicts the first-resolvent bound.

For y∈D(A), the semigroup property gives
hU(h)U(t)y−U(t)y​=U(t)hU(h)y−y​⟶U(t)Ay.
(18)
Hence U(t)y∈D(A) and
AU(t)y=U(t)Ay.
(19)
The generator domain is therefore an invariant subspace, and the operator norm bound gives
∥U(t)y∥Y​​=∥U(t)y∥+∥AU(t)y∥=∥U(t)y∥+∥U(t)Ay∥≤Meωt(∥y∥+∥Ay∥).​
(20)
Thus
∥U(t)y∥Y​≤Meωt∥y∥Y​​.
(21)
Moreover, strong continuity applied separately to y and Ay gives
∥U(t)y−y∥Y​=∥U(t)y−y∥+∥U(t)Ay−Ay∥⟶0.
(22)
Therefore the restrictions form the semigroup restricted to its generator domain. Its derivative at zero exists in the graph norm exactly when y∈D(A) and Ay∈D(A), namely when y∈D(A2), and then the derivative is Ay. Hence its generator is
A∣D(A2)​,D(A2)={y∈D(A):Ay∈D(A)}​.
(23)

The family
U(t)=e−ωtU(t)
(24)
inherits the identity, semigroup property, and strong continuity from U, while
∥U(t)u∥≤M∥u∥.
(25)
Its difference quotient satisfies
hU(h)u−u​=e−ωhhU(h)u−u​+he−ωh−1​u⟶Au−ωu
(26)
for u∈D(A). Conversely, existence of this limit implies existence of the generator limit for U, so D(A)=D(A) and
A=A−ωI​.
(27)
This is the exponentially shifted semigroup construction.
The Hille-Yosida theorem in the uniformly bounded case says that a linear operator B on a Banach space generates a C0-semigroup with ∥etB∥≤M if and only if:
  • B is a closed linear operator whose domain is a dense subset of the Banach space;
  • (0,∞)⊆ρ(B);
  • for every λ>0 and integer n≥1,
∥λn(λI−B)−n∥≤M​.
(28)

f OurBigBook.com
A solution operator for a nonautonomous evolution equation is an evolution family U(t,s) satisfying
U(s,s)=I,U(t,r)U(r,s)=U(t,s),
(29)
and, on a suitable common domain D,
∂t​U(t,s)u=A(t)U(t,s)u,∂s​U(t,s)u=−U(t,s)A(s)u.
(30)
One applicable nonautonomous generation theorem is the following. Suppose D is a dense linear subspace of H, each A(t) has domain D, the family is a stable family of semigroup generators with constants M,0, and t↦A(t) is continuously differentiable as a map from [0,T] to B(D,H), where D carries one of the uniformly equivalent graph norms. Then there is a unique evolution family such that:
  • (t,s)↦U(t,s)u is continuous for every u∈H and ∥U(t,s)∥≤M;
  • U(t,s)D⊆D, with a uniform bound on U(t,s) as an operator on D;
  • for u∈D, both displayed differential equations hold in H.
For the uniform partition tj​=s+j(t−s)/N, the frozen-generator product approximation is
UN​(t,s)=e(tN​−tN−1​)A(tN−1​)⋯e(t1​−t0​)A(t0​).
(31)
As N→∞, UN​(t,s)u→U(t,s)u in the norm of H for every u∈H, uniformly for (t,s) in the compact time triangle 0≤s≤t≤T. This is convergence in the strong operator topology, rather than convergence in the operator norm.
It remains to verify the second differential equation. The evolution family law gives, for h>0,
U(t,s+h)u−U(t,s)u=U(t,s+h)[u−U(s+h,s)u].
(32)
Divide by h. Since u∈D,
hU(s+h,s)u−u​⟶A(s)u,
(33)
while strong continuity gives U(t,s+h)A(s)u→U(t,s)A(s)u. Therefore
∂s​U(t,s)u=−U(t,s)A(s)u​.
(34)
The left derivative follows in the same way, so s↦U(t,s)u is differentiable.

Under the Fourier transform, the operator A(t)=∂x3​+ϕ(t)∂x​ is the Fourier multiplier operator
A(t)u​(ξ)=i(ϕ(t)ξ−ξ3)u(ξ).
(35)
Its symbol is purely imaginary because ϕ is real. Consequently A(t) is skew-adjoint on L2(R) with common domain H3(R) and generates the strongly continuous unitary group
erA(t)u(ξ)=eir(ϕ(t)ξ−ξ3)u(ξ).
(36)
The Plancherel theorem gives ∥erA(t)u∥2​=∥u∥2​, so every A(t)∈G(1,0). Products of the frozen groups are also unitary; hence this is a stable family of semigroup generators with constants 1,0.
For u∈H3(R),
∥(A(t)−A(s))u∥2​=∣ϕ(t)−ϕ(s)∣∥∂x​u∥2​≤∣ϕ(t)−ϕ(s)∣∥u∥H3​.
(37)
Since ϕ∈C1(R), the map t↦A(t) is continuously differentiable from H3 to L2. All hypotheses from part f are satisfied, so an evolution family exists on every finite interval [0,T].
In this commuting Fourier multiplier operator example the solution operator can also be written explicitly:
U(t,s)u​(ξ)=exp(i[−(t−s)ξ3+ξ∫st​ϕ(r)dr])u(ξ)​.
(38)
Its multiplier has absolute value one, directly confirming strong continuity, the evolution family law, preservation of H3, and the required derivatives. The equation combines the dispersive Airy equation with a time-dependent linear transport equation.

Let X=C([0,T];L2(R)) and define the map suggested by the variation-of-constants formula:
(Φu)(t)=U(t,0)u0​+∫0t​U(t,s)f(s,u(s))ds.
(39)
The strong continuity of the evolution family and the continuity of f imply that Φ maps X into itself. Because U(t,s) is unitary and ∥f(t,u)∥2​≤C,
∥Φu(t)∥2​≤∥u0​∥2​+Ct.
(40)
Equip X with the exponentially weighted supremum norm
∥u∥α​=sup0≤t≤T​e−αt∥u(t)∥2​.
(41)
This equivalent norm makes X a Banach space. Using that f is a globally Lipschitz function and that the unitary operator U(t,s) preserves the norm,
e−αt∥Φu(t)−Φv(t)∥2​​≤L∫0t​e−α(t−s)e−αs∥u(s)−v(s)∥2​ds≤αL​∥u−v∥α​.​
(42)
Choose α>L. Then Φ is a contraction mapping, so the Banach fixed-point theorem gives a unique fixed point u∈X. This fixed point is exactly the required mild solution of an abstract Cauchy problem:
u(t)=U(t,0)u0​+∫0t​U(t,s)f(s,u(s))ds​.
(43)
The weighted-norm argument works on the whole prescribed finite interval, so no subdivision of [0,T] is needed.
