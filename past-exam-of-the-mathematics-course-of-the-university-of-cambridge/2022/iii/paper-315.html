For the plane-parallel radiative transfer equation
μdτν​dIν​​=Iν​−Sν​,
(1)
angular integration gives the zeroth radiation-field moment equation
dτν​dHν​​=Jν​−Sν​.
(2)
The net radiative heating per unit volume is therefore
4π∫0∞​αν​(Jν​−Sν​)dν.
(3)
Radiative equilibrium requires it to vanish, equivalently that the frequency-integrated radiative flux be independent of depth:
∫0∞​αν​(Jν​−Sν​)dν=0​.
(4)
In local thermodynamic equilibrium with coherent isotropic scattering,
Sν​=(1−ων​)Bν​(T)+ων​Jν​,
(5)
where ων​ is the single-scattering albedo. Since αν​(1−ων​)=αν,abs​, the condition becomes
∫0∞​αν,abs​[Jν​−Bν​(T)]dν=0​.
(6)
Conservative scattering redistributes directions but contributes no net material heating.

Deep in an optically thick grey atmosphere, write Iν​=Bν​+δIν​ and retain the first spatial-gradient correction in the transfer equation:
δIν​≃−κρμ​dzdBν​​.
(10)
Angular and frequency integration then gives the radiative diffusion flux
F=−3κρ16σT3​dzdT​.
(11)
For a thin plane-parallel atmosphere, constant Rosseland mean opacity κ, negligible external irradiation, and radius nearly equal to R, radiative equilibrium gives F=L/(4πR2). Therefore
dzdT​=−64πσR2T33κρL​​.
(12)
With hydrostatic balance dP/dz=−ρg, the equivalent pressure form is
dPdT​=64πσGMT33κL​​,
(13)
where g=GM/R2.

Let brackets denote number densities and impose a local photochemical steady state. The atomic-oxygen and ozone balances are
2J1​[O2​]+J3​[O3​]=K2​[O][O2​][M]+K4​[O][O3​],
(14)
K2​[O][O2​][M]=J3​[O3​]+K4​[O][O3​].
(15)
Subtracting gives J1​[O2​]=K4​[O][O3​]. If photodissociation is the dominant direct ozone loss, J3​[O3​]≫K4​[O][O3​], the second balance becomes K2​[O][O2​][M]≃J3​[O3​]. Eliminating atomic oxygen yields the Chapman ozone equilibrium
[O3​]≃[O2​](K4​J3​K2​J1​[M]​)1/2​.
(16)
High in the atmosphere ultraviolet photons make J1​ large but the third-body density is small; low down, [M] is large but O2-dissociating ultraviolet radiation has been absorbed. Their product peaks at intermediate altitude, producing an ozone layer.

Comparable hydrostatic thermal escape requires comparable Jeans escape parameter λ=GMp​m/(kB​TRexo​). For the same escaping species and Rexo​≃Rp​,
T⊕​TJ​​≳M⊕​/R⊕​MJ​/RJ​​≃11.2318​≃28.
(17)
Thus Jupiter at 5 au needs an exobase temperature at least about thirty times Earth's at the same irradiation to have comparable Jeans escape flux.
For the inner planet, the usable EUV power is ηπRexo2​FEUV​. If the binding energy per unit escaping mass is GMp​/Rp​, energy-limited atmospheric escape gives
M˙=GMp​ηπRexo2​FEUV​Rp​​.
(18)
The time to lose a fraction x of the planetary mass is therefore
tx​=ηπRexo2​Rp​FEUV​xGMp2​​​.
(19)
This neglects Roche-lobe reduction, radiative cooling, changes in radius and flux, and the planet's orbital evolution. If the gas is lifted only from Rexo​, replace Rp​ in the denominator by Rexo​.

At exoplanet secondary eclipse, the full-phase planet-star flux ratio is the sum of reflected and thermal light. Approximating both bodies as unresolved blackbodies and taking wavelength-independent geometric albedo,
F∗,λ​Fp,λ​​=Ag​(aRp​​)2+(R∗​Rp​​)2Bλ​(T∗​)Bλ​(Tp​)​​.
(20)
The first term is a flat reflected-light level under the stated constant-albedo assumption. At short wavelength the cool planet lies in the Wien limit, so thermal emission is exponentially suppressed and reflection dominates. At long wavelength both spectra enter the Rayleigh-Jeans law, giving
F∗,λ​Fp,λ​​⟶Ag​(aRp​​)2+(R∗​Rp​​)2T∗​Tp​​.
(21)
The sketch therefore starts on the reflected plateau, rises where planetary blackbody radiation becomes important, and asymptotically approaches the long-wavelength plateau. This neglects spectral albedo features, phase dependence, stellar lines, and a nonisothermal planetary photosphere.

For a spherical stellar polytrope with P=Kρ1+1/n, the Lane-Emden equation gives
R∝K1/2ρc(1−n)/(2n)​,M∝K3/2ρc(3−n)/(2n)​.
(24)
Eliminating the central density at fixed composition and entropy gives the polytropic mass-radius relation
R∝Mβ,β=3−n1−n​​.
(25)
An incompressible rocky body has n=0 and β=1/3. A moderately massive gas giant is approximately an n=1 polytrope and has β=0, explaining its weak radius dependence on mass. In a more strongly degenerate nonrelativistic regime, n=3/2 gives β=−1/3.

For uniform density, Kelvin-Helmholtz contraction releases binding energy ∣U∣=3GM2/(5R). If mass and luminosity are constant and stellar heating is negligible at 90 au, the contraction age is

In an infrared-opaque single-layer greenhouse model, the atmospheric layer obeys 2σTa4​=σTs4​. The outgoing planetary flux is σTa4​, while the globally averaged absorbed stellar flux is (1−AB​)L/(16πa2). Radiative equilibrium therefore gives
2σTs4​​=16πa2(1−AB​)At−β​.
(31)
Hence the orbit at which the prescribed surface temperature can be maintained is
a(t)=[8πσTs4​(1−AB​)A​]1/2t−β/2​.
(32)
This assumes uniform redistribution, constant Bond albedo, unit longwave emissivity, a transparent atmosphere to starlight, and no internal heat. Without the greenhouse layer, replace 8π by 16π.

In an isothermal hydrostatic atmosphere, pressure falls as P(z)=P0​e−z/H. If a water-band line core becomes optically thick at pressure Pline​ and an opaque cloud fixes the nearby continuum at Pcl​, the exoplanet transmission spectrum feature spans
HΔz​=logPline​Pcl​​.
(33)
A two-scale-height feature therefore requires
Pcl​=e2Pline​​.
(34)
Taking a representative near-infrared water-band pressure Pline​∼1mbar gives Pcl​∼7mbar, so the appropriate estimate is an exoplanet cloud deck top near 10mbar. The numerical value scales directly with the assumed line-core pressure.
Other explanations include a high mean molecular weight, subsolar water abundance, a colder terminator, atmospheric haze, patchy two-limb clouds, stellar contamination, or instrumental systematics. Optical scattering slopes, broader James Webb Space Telescope molecular coverage, repeated transits, secondary-eclipse spectra, phase curves, and precise mass and radius measurements can distinguish these possibilities.

Approximate the atmosphere above Rp​ as isothermal with constant gravity and atmospheric scale height H, so
P(z)=P0​e−z/H.
(38)
Vertical transport over one scale height has eddy mixing time
τmix​≃Kzz​H2​.
(39)
The chemical quench level satisfies τchem​(zq​)=τmix​. Since τchem​=ηz,
zq​=ηKzz​H2​,Pq​=P0​exp(−ηKzz​H​)​.
(40)
Above this level, mixing is faster than reaction and freezes the deeper abundance of A. Larger Kzz​ moves the quench level deeper and raises Pq​. Important examples are carbon monoxide–methane quenching and nitrogen–ammonia quenching; phosphine destruction is another tracer of vertical quenching.

Exoplanet clouds and atmospheric haze add scattering and absorption opacity to an exoplanet transmission spectrum. A high opaque deck truncates the slant path and mutes molecular bands, while small aerosol particles can produce a blue scattering slope; patchiness creates mixtures of clear and cloudy limbs. In an exoplanet emission spectrum, aerosols move the photosphere to lower pressure, weaken or reshape molecular features, alter the geometric albedo, and can heat or cool layers depending on their shortwave and longwave absorption.
Observed aspects of exoplanet atmospheric dynamics include eastward equatorial atmospheric superrotation inferred from shifted thermal hot spots, day-night heat transport measured by phase-curve amplitude, and high-altitude winds measured from Doppler shifts of resolved spectral lines. Time-variable phase curves and eclipse maps also reveal changing cloud patterns and storms.
