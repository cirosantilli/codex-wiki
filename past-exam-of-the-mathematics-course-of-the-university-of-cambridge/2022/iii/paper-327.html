Using multi-index notation, the Schwartz space is
S(Rn)={φ∈C∞(Rn):pα,β​(φ)=supx∈Rn​∣xα∂βφ(x)∣<∞ for all α,β}.
(1)
A sequence φm​ converges to φ in this Fréchet space when pα,β​(φm​−φ)→0 for every α,β. The space S′(Rn) of tempered distributions is the continuous dual of S(Rn), and um​→u there means weak convergence of distributions, namely ⟨um​,φ⟩→⟨u,φ⟩ for every φ∈S.
Continuity of a linear functional immediately implies that φm​→0 entails ⟨u,φm​⟩→0. Conversely, enumerate the Schwartz seminorms as p1​,p2​,…. If u were not continuous, then for each m one could choose φm​ such that
pj​(φm​)≤m1​(1≤j≤m),∣⟨u,φm​⟩∣≥1.
(2)
Every fixed seminorm tends to zero along this sequence, so φm​→0 in S, contradicting the assumed sequential property. This is the sequential continuity criterion for a linear map on a metrizable topological vector space.

Because 0<α<n, ∣x∣−α is locally integrable at the origin and has only polynomial growth at infinity, so it defines a tempered distribution. It is a homogeneous distribution of degree −α and is radial. Its Fourier transform is therefore radial and homogeneous of degree α−n, so it must have the form cα​∣λ∣α−n. In particular, β=n−α​.
To determine the constant, use the stated Gamma integral representation, Fubini's theorem, and the Fourier transform of a Gaussian:
uα​(λ)​=Γ(α/2)1​∫0∞​τα/2−1(∫Rn​e−τ∣x∣2−iλ⋅xdx)dτ=Γ(α/2)πn/2​∫0∞​τ(α−n)/2−1e−∣λ∣2/(4τ)dτ.​
(9)
The change of variables formula s=∣λ∣2/(4τ) then gives
uα​(λ)=2n−απn/2Γ(α/2)Γ((n−α)/2)​∣λ∣α−n.
(10)
This is precisely the Fourier transform of the Riesz kernel.

The space of smooth functions E(X)=C∞(X) has the topology of uniform convergence of every derivative on every compact set K⋐X. Thus fj​→f exactly when
supx∈K​∣∂α(fj​−f)(x)∣⟶0
(11)
for every K and every multi-index α. Its continuous dual E′(X) is the compactly supported distribution space; convergence in the weak dual topology means pointwise convergence on every f∈E(X).

Let K contain the support of u∈E′(Rn). Choose a cutoff function that equals one near K. It makes
u(λ)=⟨u(x),e−iλ⋅x⟩
(12)
well-defined, and differentiating the parameter under the pairing gives
∂λγ​u(λ)=⟨u(x),(−ix)γe−iλ⋅x⟩.
(13)
Thus the Fourier transform of a compactly supported distribution is a smooth function. Since a compactly supported distribution has finite order, some N and C satisfy
∣⟨u,ψ⟩∣≤C∑∣α∣≤N​supK​∣∂αψ∣.
(14)
Applying this estimate to the exponential yields ∣u(λ)∣≤C′⟨λ⟩N.
Now take v∈E′(X), multiply by a cutoff function supported in X and equal to one near suppv, and regard the result as an element of E′(Rn). Choose m so large that
g(λ)=⟨λ⟩−2mv(λ)
(15)
is Lebesgue integrable. The inverse Fourier transform f=F−1g is a bounded continuous function, and the Fourier transform of a derivative gives
v=(1−Δ)mf
(16)
as a distributional identity. This is the Bessel potential proof of the structure theorem for compactly supported distributions.
The function f itself need not have compact support. Choose another cutoff ρ∈D(X) equal to one near suppv. Then v=ρ(1−Δ)mf. Repeatedly using
ρ∂αf=∑β≤α​(−1)∣α−β∣(βα​)∂β(f∂α−βρ)
(17)
expresses v as a finite sum ∑β​∂βfβ​, where every coefficient fβ​ is continuous and compactly supported in X.

The Malgrange–Ehrenpreis theorem states that every nonzero constant-coefficient linear partial differential operator P(D) on Rn has a fundamental solution of a linear differential operator: there is an E∈D′(Rn) such that P(D)E=δ0​.
Write D=−i∂. After an orthogonal change of coordinates and multiplication by a nonzero constant, its polynomial symbol may be written as a monic polynomial in the last frequency,
P(ξ′,z)=zM+∑m=0M−1​am​(ξ′)zm.
(24)
For each real μ′, this polynomial has M complex roots counted with multiplicity. Among a fixed finite collection of horizontal lines at bounded heights, one can choose a line that stays a positive distance from all those roots. Continuity of the roots preserves the choice on a neighborhood N(μ′). Take a countable locally finite cover by such neighborhoods, refine it to a measurable disjoint partition Rn−1=⨆j​Δj​, and let cj​ be the chosen height on Δj​. The resulting Hörmander staircase
Σ=⋃j​{(ξ′,s+icj​):ξ′∈Δj​, s∈R}
(25)
has bounded heights and may be chosen so that ∣P(ξ′,s+icj​)∣≥1 on each step.
For a test function φ, define
⟨E,φ⟩=(2π)n1​∑j​∫Δj​​∫R+icj​​P(ξ′,z)φ​(−ξ′,−z)​dzdξ′.
(26)
The Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem gives rapid decay in the real frequency directions and at most a fixed exponential factor in the bounded imaginary direction. Together with ∣P∣≥1, this proves that the integral defines a continuous distribution. Applying P(D) cancels the denominator. The remaining integrand is entire in z, so the Cauchy integral theorem shifts every horizontal contour to the real axis; the partition then recombines into Rn−1. The Fourier inversion theorem gives
⟨P(D)E,φ⟩=φ(0)=⟨δ0​,φ⟩,
(27)
which proves the theorem.
