Let J0​ be the standard complex structure on R2n, let ω0​ be the standard symplectic form, and let g0​ be the Euclidean inner product. They satisfy
g0​(u,v)=ω0​(u,J0​v),ω0​(u,v)=g0​(J0​u,v).
(1)
The two-out-of-three property for unitary structures says that a real linear map preserving any two of these structures preserves the third. In terms of the corresponding matrix groups,
GL(n,C)∩Sp(2n,R)=Sp(2n,R)∩O(2n)=O(2n)∩GL(n,C)=U(n).
(2)
For example, if A preserves J0​ and ω0​, then
g0​(Au,Av)=ω0​(Au,J0​Av)=ω0​(Au,AJ0​v)=g0​(u,v),
(3)
so A preserves g0​. If it preserves J0​ and g0​, the second displayed identity shows that it preserves ω0​. Finally, J0​ is uniquely determined by g0​(J0​u,v)=ω0​(u,v), so preservation of g0​ and ω0​ implies AJ0​=J0​A. The common intersection is therefore the unitary group.

The unitary group acts on the Lagrangian Grassmannian by A⋅L=A(L). Every Lagrangian subspace has an orthonormal basis, and adjoining its J0​-image gives a unitary basis, so this action is transitive. The stabilizer of the standard real subspace Rn⊂Cn consists exactly of real unitary matrices, namely O(n). Hence
U(n)/O(n)⟶LGr(R2n),A,O(n)⟼ARn
(4)
is a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space and is therefore a homeomorphism.
For n=1, every line in R2 is Lagrangian. Thus
LGr(R2)=RP1≅U(1)/O(1)=S1/{±1}≅S1.
(5)
Concretely, the line making angle θ with the real axis corresponds to e2iθ.

On the quotient from part (b), the Maslov map
μ:U(n)/O(n)⟶S1,[A]⟼det(A)2

ℓ(t)=eπitR⊕Rn−1,0≤t≤1.
(7)

⟨μ∗η,[ℓ]⟩=1.
(8)

d OurBigBook.com
Any symplectic form on S2 orients its tangent bundle. Choose a compatible complex structure and inner product; this reduces the structure group to SO(2). Since every line in an oriented symplectic plane is Lagrangian,
LGr(TS2)=P(TS2)
(10)
is an oriented circle bundle.
If a transition function of TS2 rotates vectors through an angle α, its action on unoriented lines rotates the coordinate e2iθ through 2α. The Euler class of the Lagrangian-line bundle of an oriented plane bundle therefore gives
e(LGr(TS2))=2e(TS2).
(11)
By the Poincaré-Hopf theorem,
⟨e(TS2),[S2]⟩=χ(S2)=2
(12)
for the chosen orientation, up to changing both signs. Hence the Euler number of LGr(TS2) is ±4, which is nonzero. A smoothly trivial oriented circle bundle has zero Euler class, so LGr(TS2)→S2 cannot be smoothly trivial for any choice of symplectic form.

M=T2n=R2n/Z2n

The Weinstein neighborhood theorem states that if L is a compact Lagrangian submanifold of (M,ω), then neighborhoods of L in M and of the zero section in its cotangent bundle T∗L are symplectomorphic. The symplectomorphism restricts to the identity on L, and the canonical form on T∗L is taken with the sign matching the chosen convention.

Take L=S2. For any Lagrangian embedding of S2 into a symplectic four-manifold, the symplectic form identifies the normal bundle with T∗S2. The self-intersection formula and the Euler characteristic give
[L]⋅[L]=⟨e(NL),[L]⟩=⟨e(T∗S2),[S2]⟩=2
(16)
up to the harmless orientation sign. If a smooth isotopy displaced L, its final image would represent the same homology class but have intersection number zero with L, a contradiction. This is the smooth non-displaceability from self-intersection.
For a compact ambient example, equip S2×S2 with ω⊕(−ω). Its diagonal
Δ={(x,x):x∈S2}
(17)
is Lagrangian because the two summands cancel on TΔ, and the preceding argument shows that it is not smoothly displaceable.

ii OurBigBook.com
Let M=S2 with an area form and let L be an equator dividing the sphere into two open hemispheres of equal area. Every curve in a symplectic surface is Lagrangian. A small normal push moves L to a nearby latitude, so it is displaceable by a smooth isotopy.
Suppose a symplectic isotopy had final image L′ disjoint from L. The curve L′ must lie in one hemisphere. Of the two discs bounded by L′, the one contained in that hemisphere has area strictly below half the total area, and the other has area strictly above half. On the other hand, a symplectomorphism maps the original two hemispheres to the two discs bounded by L′ and preserves their areas, so both would have half the total area. This contradiction is the symplectic non-displaceability of an area bisector.

Let
(M,ω)=(T2,dx∧dy),L={y=0}.
(18)
For any sufficiently small nonzero δ, translation
ϕt​(x,y)=(x,y+tδ)
(19)
is a symplectic isotopy with ϕ1​(L)∩L=∅. Choosing ∣δ∣ arbitrarily small makes the isotopy arbitrarily small in every Ck norm.
This isotopy has nonzero flux homomorphism, represented by δdx. In contrast, every Hamiltonian isotopy has zero flux. If a Hamiltonian image L′ were disjoint from L, the two homologous essential circles would bound an annulus A, and evaluation of the flux on [L] would equal the signed symplectic area
∫A​ω,
(20)
which is nonzero. This contradicts vanishing Hamiltonian flux, so L is not Hamiltonian displaceable.

Again take a symplectic two-sphere, but let L be a small latitude bounding a cap of area strictly below half the total area. A rotation carrying that cap to a disjoint cap carries L to a disjoint Lagrangian circle. Rotations of the symplectic sphere are flows of Hamiltonian vector fields; for rotation about an axis, a height function is a Hamiltonian function. Thus this rotation is a Hamiltonian isotopy, and L is Hamiltonian displaceable.

Moser's trick states that if M is compact and ωt​, 0≤t≤1, is a smooth family of symplectic forms whose de Rham cohomology class is independent of t, then there is an isotopy ϕt​ with
ϕt∗​ωt​=ω0​.
(21)
Since [ω˙t​]=0, choose a smooth family of one-forms σt​ with ω˙t​=dσt​. Nondegeneracy of ωt​ uniquely determines a vector field Xt​ by
ιXt​​ωt​=−σt​.
(22)
Compactness makes its flow ϕt​ exist for the whole interval. Cartan's magic formula and dωt​=0 give
dtd​ϕt∗​ωt​=ϕt∗​(ω˙t​+LXt​​ωt​)=ϕt∗​(dσt​+dιXt​​ωt​)=0,
(23)
which proves the theorem.
Smooth degree-d hypersurfaces form the complement of the discriminant in the projective space of degree-d homogeneous polynomials. This complement is path connected, so X and X′ lie in a smooth one-parameter family. The Ehresmann fibration theorem identifies the fibers smoothly. Under such an identification, the restrictions of the Fubini-Study form form a family ωt​ whose cohomology class is the fixed restricted hyperplane class. Moser's trick therefore gives the symplectic equivalence of smooth projective hypersurfaces.
It remains to construct the finite subgroup for one convenient hypersurface. On the Fermat hypersurface
XF​={z0d​+⋯+znd​=0}⊂CPn,
(24)
the group (μd​)n+1 acts by diagonal coordinate multiplication. It preserves both XF​ and the Fubini-Study form. The kernel of its projective action is the diagonal subgroup μd​, so the effective Fermat hypersurface diagonal symmetry group is
(Z/dZ)n+1/⟨(1,…,1)⟩≅(Z/dZ)n.
(25)
Conjugating this action by a symplectomorphism XF​→X gives the required subgroup of Symp(X,ωFS​∣X​).

The symplectic neighborhood theorem says that a neighborhood of a compact symplectic submanifold is determined, up to symplectomorphism, by the restricted symplectic form and its symplectic normal bundle. More precisely, if f:S0​→S1​ is a symplectomorphism and an isomorphism of symplectic normal bundles covers f, then that bundle isomorphism extends to a symplectomorphism between neighborhoods of S0​ and S1​.

Let Q⊂CP2 be a smooth complex conic. Its homology class is 2H, so its self-intersection number is
Q2=(2H)2=4.
(26)
Rescale the Fubini-Study form so that Q and C have the same symplectic area. Their normal bundles have opposite Euler numbers, +4 and −4, so the symplectic sum can be formed along Q and C.
Concretely, remove tubular neighborhoods ν(C) and ν(Q) and glue the boundaries by a fiber-reversing bundle map. The symplectic neighborhood theorem supplies the standard models needed for the gluing, and the symplectic-sum construction supplies a symplectic form on
(X∖ν(C))∪L(4,1)​(CP2∖ν(Q)).
(27)
The second piece is a rational homology ball. Thus this operation replaces the neighborhood of the −4 sphere by that rational ball and is the symplectic rational blowdown of a minus-four sphere.

Let F0​ and F1​ be general homogeneous cubic forms with base locus B of nine points. The incidence variety
E(1)={([z],[s:t])∈CP2×CP1:sF0​(z)+tF1​(z)=0}
(28)
is the blowup of CP2 at B. Projection onto the second factor is the elliptic fibration of the rational elliptic surface
π:E(1)⟶CP1,([z],[s:t])⟼[s:t],
(29)
and its fibers are connected plane cubics. Over any base point p∈B, the exceptional curve Ep​={p}×CP1 maps isomorphically to the base, so it is a holomorphic section.
Write H for the pullback of a line and E1​,…,E9​ for the exceptional classes. The fiber class and the canonical class of the rational elliptic surface are
F=3H−∑i=19​Ei​,KE(1)​=−3H+∑i=19​Ei​=−F.
(30)
The degree of π∣C​ is C⋅F=d. The Adjunction formula now yields
2g(C)−2=C2+KE(1)​⋅C=k−d.
(31)
Therefore the genus formula for a multisection of the rational elliptic surface is
g(C)=1+2k−d​.​
(32)
