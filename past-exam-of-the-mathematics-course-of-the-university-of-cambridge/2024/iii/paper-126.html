For a ring homomorphism A→B, the Module of Kähler differentials ΩB/A​ is the B-module generated by symbols db, subject to
d(b+b′)=db+db′,d(bb′)=bdb′+b′db,da=0(a∈A).
(1)
Equivalently, it represents A-linear derivations:
HomB​(ΩB/A​,M)≃DerA​(B,M).
(2)
For A→B→C, the Transitivity exact sequence for Kähler differentials is
ΩB/A​⊗B​C⟶ΩC/A​⟶ΩC/B​⟶0.
(3)
If B→C=B/I is surjective, the Conormal exact sequence for Kähler differentials is
I/I2⟶ΩB/A​⊗B​C⟶ΩC/A​⟶0,[f]⟼df⊗1.
(4)
Let L/K be a finite field extension. By the primitive element theorem, its maximal separable field extension Ls​/K is simple, and transitivity reduces the calculation to a simple algebraic extension. If L=K(α) with minimal polynomial mα​, then
ΩL/K​≃Ldα/(mα′​(α)dα).
(5)
Thus a separable simple extension has zero differentials. Conversely, if L/K is not separable, the purely inseparable part has a generator whose minimal polynomial has zero formal derivative in positive characteristic, producing a nonzero differential. Hence
ΩL/K​=0⟺L/K is separable.​
(6)
Now let charK=p>0. If L=K(a), ap∈/K, and ap2∈K, then the minimal polynomial is Tp2−ap2 and has zero derivative, so
ΩL/K​=Lda.​
(7)
If L=K(a,b), where a∈/K, b∈/K(a), and ap,bp∈K, then [K(a):K]=[L:K(a)]=p. Both defining equations have zero derivative, and
ΩL/K​=Lda⊕Ldb.​
(8)
For the three morphisms, compute the Sheaf of relative Kähler differentials on coordinate rings.
  • In (i), x=y2 and dx=0 relative to k[t], so
ΩX/Y​≃k[y],dy/(2ydy).
(9)
If chark=2, this is k[y]/(y)dy and its support is the origin V(y)=V(x,y). If chark=2, it is free of rank one and its support is all of X.
  • In (ii), d(x2)=2xdx and d(x3)=3x2dx vanish relatively, whence
ΩX/Y​≃k[x],dx/(2x,3x2)dx.
(10)
Its support is the origin V(x) in every characteristic; the module is k[x]/(x) unless chark=2, when it is k[x]/(x2).
  • In (iii), dx=0 and d(xy)=ydx+xdy give
ΩX/Y​≃k[x,y]/(xy,x)dy≃k[y],dy.
(11)
Its support is the entire component V(x)⊂X.

A morphism of schemes f:X→Y is a flat morphism when every local-ring map OY,f(x)​→OX,x​ makes OX,x​ a flat module.
In (i), the coordinate map is k[t]→k[y], t↦y2, and
k[y]=k[t]⊕yk[t].
(12)
It is therefore a free module of rank two and the morphism is flat, including in characteristic two.
In (ii), k[x] is finite over the cusp ring R=k[x2,x3] and has generic rank one. Were it flat, finite flatness over the local ring at the cusp would make it free of rank one. Its fiber there is instead
k[x]⊗R​R/(x2,x3)≃k[x]/(x2),
(13)
which has dimension two, so this morphism is not flat.
In (iii), the base coordinate t acts as x, and the nonzero element y satisfies ty=xy=0. Thus the coordinate ring has torsion as a k[t]-module. Since k[t] is a principal ideal domain and a module over it is flat exactly when it is torsion-free, this morphism is not flat. Consequently
only (i) is flat.​
(14)

For an affine morphism f:X→Y and a quasi-coherent sheaf F, every inverse image of an affine open V⊆Y is affine. Higher cohomology of a quasi-coherent sheaf on an affine scheme vanishes, so
(Rqf∗​F)∣V​=Hq(f−1V,F)=0(q>0).
(15)
The Leray spectral sequence therefore has only its zeroth row, and its edge maps give
Hp(X,F)≃Hp(Y,f∗​F)(p≥0).​
(16)

For X=U∪V, the Mayer-Vietoris sequence for sheaf cohomology is the long exact sequence
0→H0(X,F)→H0(U,F)⊕H0(V,F)→H0(U∩V,F)→H1(X,F)→⋯.
(17)
We prove the required vanishing by induction on the number m of open sets. The case m=1 is an assumption. Put U=U1​∪⋯∪Um−1​ and V=Um​. The induction hypothesis gives Hp(U,F)=0 for every p. The intersections Ui​∩Um​ cover U∩V, and every nonempty finite intersection among them is one of the intersections in the hypothesis, so the same induction gives Hp(U∩V,F)=0. We also have Hp(V,F)=0. Exactness of the Mayer-Vietoris sequence now yields
Hp(X,F)=0for every p≥0.​

The finite complex computing cohomology in a proper flat family gives a bounded complex K∙ of finite locally free A-modules such that, for every A-module M,
Hp(X,F⊗A​M)≃Hp(K∙⊗A​M).
(19)
In particular, M=A computes Hp(X,F) and M=κ(s) computes Hp(Xs​,F(s)).
Since Hp(K∙)=0 for p>n, the finite exact tail above degree n can be split successively: its last differential is surjective onto a projective module, hence splits, and induction moves left. Removing the resulting contractible summands leaves a finite locally free complex ending in degree n. Therefore
Hn(K∙)=coker(Kn−1→Kn),
(20)
and after tensoring with κ(s) the same formula computes the fiber cohomology.
If Hn(X,F)=0, the last differential is surjective, and remains so after every base change; hence every Hn(Xs​,F(s)) vanishes. Conversely, if all fiber groups vanish, the finitely generated cokernel C satisfies C⊗A​κ(s)=0 for every s∈SpecA. Localizing and applying Nakayama lemma gives Cs​=0 for every s, so C=0. Thus
Hn(X,F)=0⟺Hn(Xs​,F(s))=0 for every s∈SpecA.​
(21)

Take an affine open subscheme U=SpecA⊆Y. Properness and flatness survive base change, and A is reduced because Y is reduced. A bounded complex of finite locally free modules computes the cohomology of F on XU​.
If some Hp(XU​,F) were nonzero, choose the largest such p=n. All groups above degree n would vanish, while every fiber group in degree n vanishes by hypothesis. Part (iii) would force Hn(XU​,F)=0, a contradiction. Hence
Hp(XU​,F)=0
(22)
for every affine U⊆Y and every p≥0. These groups compute the sections of the higher direct images over affine opens, so Rpf∗​F=0 for all p, including f∗​F=0 when p=0. The Leray spectral sequence now gives
Hp(X,F)=0(p≥0).​

A group scheme over k is a k-scheme G with multiplication mG​:G×k​G→G, identity eG​:Speck→G, and inversion iG​:G→G satisfying the group axioms as identities of morphisms. A homomorphism of group schemes f:H→G is a k-morphism satisfying
f∘mH​=mG​∘(f×f),
(24)
and it then preserves the identity and inversion.
Assume G and H are commutative. The group G(H)=Mork​(H,G) has pointwise addition
f+g=mG​∘(f,g).
(25)
For every k-algebra R and x,y∈H(R),
(f+g)R​(x+y)=fR​(x)+fR​(y)+gR​(x)+gR​(y)=(f+g)R​(x)+(f+g)R​(y),
(26)
where commutativity permits the middle terms to be reordered. Hence f+g is a homomorphism. The zero morphism and pointwise inverse are also homomorphisms, so Homk​(H,G) is a subgroup of G(H). The definition immediately gives
(f+g)R​(x)=fR​(x)+gR​(x).​
(27)
Repeated pointwise addition gives (nf)R​(x)=nfR​(x). Since fR​ is a group homomorphism,
nfR​(x)=fR​(nx)=([n]G​)R​(fR​(x)).
(28)
The Yoneda lemma turns equality on all R-valued points into equality of morphisms, proving
nf=f∘[n]H​=[n]G​∘f.​
(29)

One form of the Mumford rigidity lemma says that if X is a complete variety, S is connected, and a morphism F:X×S→Z maps X×{s0​} to one point, then F factors through the projection to S. In particular, if F also maps {x0​}×S to that point, then F is constant.
Choose s0​∈S(k) and put g=f∣X×{s0​}​. To see that the pointed morphism g:X→Y is a homomorphism, apply rigidity to
D(x1​,x2​)=g(x1​+x2​)−g(x1​)−g(x2​).
(30)
It vanishes on X×{e}, so it factors through the second projection; it also vanishes on {e}×X, so it is identically zero. Now define
F(x,s)=f(x,s)−g(x).
(31)
Then F(x,s0​)=e for every x and F(e,s)=e for every s. Rigidity forces F to be identically e, so
f∣X×{s}​=g∈Homk​(X,Y)for every s∈S(k).​
(32)

The Theorem of the square says that for an abelian variety X, a line bundle L, and x,y∈X(k),
Tx+y∗​L⊗L≃Tx∗​L⊗Ty∗​L,​
(33)
where Tx​ denotes translation by x.

ii OurBigBook.com
For a line bundle L on X, define the homomorphism associated to a line bundle on an abelian variety
ϕL​:X(k)⟶Pic(X),ϕL​(x)=Tx∗​L⊗L∨.
(34)
The Theorem of the square gives
ϕL​(x+y)=Tx+y∗​L⊗L∨≃ϕL​(x)⊗ϕL​(y),
(35)
so ϕL​ is a homomorphism. Pullback distributes over the tensor product of sheaves, and therefore
ϕL⊗M​(x)=ϕL​(x)⊗ϕM​(x).​
(36)
Iterating the homomorphism law in x gives
ϕL⊗n​(x)=ϕL​(x)⊗n=ϕL​(nx).​
(37)
Suppose L⊗n∈Pic0(X). Then ϕL​(nx) is trivial for every x. The multiplication-by-n morphism on an abelian variety is surjective, so ϕL​ is trivial and L∈Pic0(X). Thus the Néron-Severi group
Pic(X)/Pic0(X)
(38)
is torsion-free.
Finally, put M=ϕL​(x). For every y,
ϕM​(y)=Ty∗​(Tx∗​L⊗L∨)⊗(Tx∗​L⊗L∨)∨≃Tx+y∗​L⊗Ty∗​L∨⊗Tx∗​L∨⊗L,
(39)
which is trivial by the Theorem of the square. Hence
imϕL​⊆Pic0(X).​
(40)
