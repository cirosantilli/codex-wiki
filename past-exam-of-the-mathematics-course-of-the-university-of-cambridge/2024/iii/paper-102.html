Write e,h,f for the standard generators of the sl2 Lie algebra. For the representation ϕ, use the normalization
Ω=ϕ(e)ϕ(f)+ϕ(f)ϕ(e)+21​ϕ(h)2.
(1)
This is the quadratic Casimir element, and by assumption it commutes with every ϕ(x).
Schur lemma says that an endomorphism of a finite-dimensional irreducible complex representation which commutes with the representation is a scalar. Hence Ω=cIV​ when V is irreducible.
Let v be a highest-weight vector of highest weight m, so ev=0 and hv=mv. Since [e,f]=h,
efv=(fe+h)v=mv,fev=0.
(2)
Therefore
Ωv=(m+21​m2)v=21​m(m+2)v.
(3)
It follows from scalarity that
Ω=21​m(m+2)IV​​.
(4)
This is the Casimir eigenvalue for sl2 in the chosen normalization.

The one-dimensional quotient V/W is trivial because every one-dimensional representation vanishes on the derived algebra [sl2​,sl2​]=sl2​. Choose v∈V mapping to 1∈V/W. Then
c(x)=xv∈W
(5)
is a 1-cocycle:
c([x,y])=x,c(y)−y,c(x).
(6)
We show that it is a coboundary.
Decompose W into generalized eigenspaces of its Casimir element Ω. These are subrepresentations because Ω is central. On a generalized eigenspace with nonzero eigenvalue, Ω is invertible. If (xi​) and (xi) are dual bases of sl2​ for the Killing form, put
u=∑i​xi​c(xi).
(7)
Invariance of the Killing form and the cocycle identity give the standard Casimir calculation
xu=Ωc(x).
(8)
Thus on every nonzero generalized eigenspace, c(x)=x(Ω−1u).
On the zero generalized eigenspace, every irreducible composition factor has zero Casimir eigenvalue. By part i and the Classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations, each such factor is trivial. In a basis adapted to a composition series, the image of sl2​ is therefore strictly upper triangular and hence solvable. Since sl2​ is simple and non-solvable, that image is zero. The cocycle then vanishes because it kills [sl2​,sl2​].
Combining the generalized eigenspaces gives w∈W such that c(x)=xw for every x. Hence v−w is invariant, and
V=W⊕C(v−w)
(9)
is a decomposition into subrepresentations. This proves the codimension-one case of the Weyl complete reducibility theorem.

For a finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation ϕ:g→gl(V), the Trace form of a Lie algebra representation is

(17)

B([z,x],y)+B(x,[z,y])=0,
(19)

Use the nondegenerate restriction of the Killing form to t to define tα​∈t by
κ(tα​,t)=α(t)(t∈t).
(21)
Killing-form invariance and the root-space decomposition show that gα​ pairs nondegenerately with g−α​ and orthogonally with every other root space. Choose nonzero e∈gα​ and f∈g−α​ with κ(e,f)=0. For t∈t,
κ([e,f],t)=κ(e,[f,t])=α(t)κ(e,f),
(22)

[(x,v),(y,w)]=([x,y],xw−yv)

Let g=sl2​(C) and let V=C2 be its defining irreducible representation. Set
h=g⋉V.
(28)
Since [g,g]=g and gV=V,
[h,h]=g⊕V=h.
(29)
If (x,v) is central, commuting with every (0,w) gives xw=0 for all w, so faithfulness of the defining representation gives x=0. Commuting with every (y,0) then gives yv=0 for all y; irreducibility and nontriviality give v=0. Thus Z(h)=0.
The nonzero abelian subspace V is a proper Ideal of a Lie algebra, so h is not simple. It is not a direct product of simple Lie algebras either, because such a product is semisimple and has no nonzero solvable ideal, whereas V is one.

Relative to g⊕V, the adjoint action has block form
ad(x,v)​=(adx​(y↦−yv)​0ϕ(x)​).

A Weyl chamber is a connected component of

A root basis Δ⊆Φ is a vector-space basis of E such that every root is an integer combination of elements of Δ with all nonzero coefficients of one sign.

Choose simple roots α,β. Their inner product is nonpositive, so their angle lies in [π/2,π). Part i leaves four possibilities for mn:
  • mn=0: angle π/2, giving A1​×A1​.
  • mn=1: angle 2π/3 and equal root lengths, giving A2​.

An irreducible root system is simply laced when every root has the same length, equivalently when its Dynkin diagram has no multiple edge.
If all roots have the same length, the two Cartan integers for α and β are equal. The root-system finiteness lemma then makes their product either zero or one, so
⟨α,β∨⟩∈{0,±1}
(49)
for α=±β.
Conversely, when all such Cartan integers lie in {0,±1}, any two nonorthogonal roots have Cartan integers of absolute value one in both directions. Their squared lengths are therefore equal. Irreducibility makes the graph joining nonorthogonal roots connected, so all roots have the same length. Thus the system is simply laced.

Choose a Borel subalgebra b=t⊕n+. For λ∈t∗, let Cλ​ be the one-dimensional b-module on which n+ acts by zero and h∈t acts by λ(h). The Verma module is
M(λ)=U(g)⊗U(b)​Cλ​.
(53)
Its universal property of a Verma module says that any vector of weight λ annihilated by n+ receives the canonical highest-weight vector under one unique module homomorphism from M(λ).
The sum of the proper submodules of M(λ) is its unique maximal proper submodule, because no proper submodule contains the highest-weight vector. Its quotient V(λ) is therefore the unique irreducible quotient of a Verma module, and hence the unique irreducible highest-weight module of weight λ.
The module V(λ) is finite-dimensional exactly when λ is a dominant integral weight:
⟨λ,αi∨​⟩∈Z≥0​
(54)
for every simple root αi​.
