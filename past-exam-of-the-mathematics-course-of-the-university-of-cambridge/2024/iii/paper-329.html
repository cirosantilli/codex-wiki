A rotation by π about the vertical symmetry axis maps a horizontal angular velocity Ω′ to −Ω′ but leaves a possible vertical force unchanged. Linearity and uniqueness of Stokes flow therefore require that vertical force to equal its own negative, so it vanishes. A horizontal force and a horizontal couple are allowed by the same symmetry.
Write λ=1−ϵ and examine the lower pole, where θ≪1. Since cosθ=1−θ2/2+O(θ4),
h=Δ[ϵ+2θ2​+O(ϵθ2+θ4)].
(14)
Thus h=O(ϵΔ) for θ=O(ϵ1/2), a circular patch of radius
ℓ=O(aϵ1/2)​.
(15)
In the broad region, the surface speed is O(Ω′a), the shear is O(μΩ′a/Δ), the area is O(a2), and the moment arm is O(a). Hence
Gbroad′​=O(ΔμΩ′a4​).
(16)
In the narrow patch, the shear rises to O(μΩ′a/(ϵΔ)), while its area falls to O(ϵa2); the moment arm remains O(a). Therefore
Gpatch′​=Gbroad′​=O(ΔμΩ′a4​)​.

For two body-force-free Stokes flows (u(1),σ(1)) and (u(2),σ(2)) in the same domain, the Lorentz reciprocal theorem for Stokes flow is
∫∂D​u(1)⋅σ(2)ndS=∫∂D​u(2)⋅σ(1)ndS.
(19)
Indeed, the difference of the two volume integrands is
∇u(1):σ(2)−∇u(2):σ(1)=0
(20)
because both flows are incompressible and the Newtonian fluid stress tensor is symmetric. The divergence theorem proves the boundary identity.
On a rigid body, u(r)=U(r)+Ω(r)×x. The reciprocal theorem becomes
U(1)⋅F(2)+Ω(1)⋅G(2)=U(2)⋅F(1)+Ω(2)⋅G(1).
(21)
Writing (F,G)T=R(U,Ω)T and choosing arbitrary pairs of rigid velocities shows that
R=RT​.
(22)
Thus the hydrodynamic resistance matrix is symmetric.

The expression in braces in the first equation is the net axial force. The term −πa2γ/a is the compressive force from the capillary pressure γ/a, 3πμa2wz​ is the Newtonian extensional tension with Trouton ratio three, and 2πaγ is the axial pull of surface tension around the circumference. Its z-derivative vanishes because axial force is conserved.
The final equation is conservation of an insoluble surfactant. The surface divergence wz​+u/a is the sum of axial and circumferential extension rates. Positive surface divergence increases interfacial area and dilutes C; negative divergence concentrates it. The absence of a diffusion term expresses the assumption of negligible surface diffusion.
Linearizing the area and surfactant equations gives
2ηt​=−a0​wz​,Ct′​=−C0​(wz​+a0​ηt​​)=a0​C0​​ηt​.
(45)
Thus
∂t∂​(a0​C′−C0​η)=0,
(46)
and hence
a0​C′(z,t)=C0​η(z,t)+ψ(z)​,
(47)
where ψ is fixed by the initial data.
Linearizing the displayed net axial force and using γ′=−AC′ gives
3πμa02​wz​+πγ0​η−πAa0​C′=0.
(48)
Eliminating wz​ and C′ therefore gives directly

The target formula printed later in the paper contains an additional factor of π in both denominators. That factor does not follow from the displayed equations because every term in the axial-force balance contains the same factor π. If the target formula is adopted as the intended normalization, its corresponding value is s=(γ0​−AC0​)/(6πμa0​).
Initially η=0 and C′>0, so ψ>0 and ηt​<0: a surfactant-rich, low-tension region begins to neck as neighboring higher tension pulls fluid away. The accompanying axial extension dilutes the surfactant.
If A<γ0​/C0​, then s>0. The Rayleigh–Plateau instability overwhelms the weak surface-elastic response: the necking perturbation grows in the linear model while the original concentration excess is diluted and eventually changes sign.
