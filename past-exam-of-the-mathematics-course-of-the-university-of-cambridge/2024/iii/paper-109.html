Proceed by mathematical induction on n. The cases n=0,1 are immediate. Given a symmetric chain decomposition of a Boolean lattice P([n−1]), consider one of its chains
Ar​⊂Ar+1​⊂⋯⊂An−1−r​,∣Aj​∣=j.
(1)
It produces the two chains
Ar​⊂⋯⊂An−1−r​⊂An−1−r​∪{n}
(2)
and, when nonempty,
Ar​∪{n}⊂Ar+1​∪{n}⊂⋯⊂An−2−r​∪{n}.
(3)
The first runs from rank r to rank n−r, and the second from rank r+1 to rank n−1−r; both endpoint ranks sum to n. They are disjoint and together contain the old chain both without and with n. Doing this for every old chain partitions P([n]) into symmetric chains.

Choose symmetric chain decompositions of P(X1​) and P(X2​). Their Cartesian products partition P(X) into grids C1​×C2​. Within one grid, two members in the same row differ only in X1​, and two in the same column differ only in X2​. The hypothesis on F therefore permits at most one member in each row and each column. Hence
∣F∩(C1​×C2​)∣≤min{∣C1​∣,∣C2​∣}.
(4)
Because n1​ and n2​ are even, both symmetric chains have odd length and are centred at ranks n1​/2 and n2​/2. The grid contains exactly min{∣C1​∣,∣C2​∣} points whose two ranks sum to n/2: they lie on its central antidiagonal. Thus the bound for F in each grid is the number of rank-n/2 subsets in that grid. Summing over all grids gives
∣F∣≤(n/2n​)​.
(5)

For a sequence J in Fp2​, write (r∣J) for the number of r-term subsequences whose sum is zero. We use the following consequence of the Chevalley-Warning theorem: if ∣J∣=3p−1, then
1−(p∣J)+(2p∣J)≡0(modp).​
(6)
Indeed, with one variable xi​ for each term (ai​,bi​), apply Chevalley--Warning to
∑i​xip−1​,∑i​ai​xip−1​,∑i​bi​xip−1​.
(7)
Their degree sum is 3(p−1)<3p−1. A common zero has support of size 0,p, or 2p modulo p, and each fixed support contributes (p−1)∣S∣ assignments. Reducing modulo p gives the displayed congruence.
Now let the given 3p terms have total sum zero. If no p of them summed to zero, delete any one term and apply the congruence to the remaining 3p−1 terms. It gives
(2p∣J)≡−1(modp),
(8)
so those remaining terms contain a zero-sum 2p-subsequence. Its complement in the original 3p terms has size p and sum zero, contradicting the assumption. Therefore the required p terms exist.

We use the coefficient form of the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz: if a polynomial of degree ∑i​ti​ has nonzero coefficient at ∏i​xiti​​, then it cannot vanish on every product set ∏i​Si​ with ∣Si​∣>ti​.
Write the terms as (ai​,bi​) and, over Fp​, put
A=∑i​ai​xi​,B=∑i​bi​xi​,S=∑i​xi​,
(9)
and
Ep​(x)=∑∣J∣=p​∏j∈J​xj​.
(10)
Consider
F(x)=(1−Ap−1)(1−Bp−1)[(1−Sp−1)(2−Ep​(x))−2∏i​(1−xi​)].
(11)
A direct multinomial-coefficient calculation, using Wilson's theorem, shows that the coefficient of ∏i=14p−2​xi​ in F is nonzero. The Combinatorial Nullstellensatz with every Si​={0,1} therefore gives an indicator vector x for which F(x)=0.
The zero indicator does not work. The first two factors force the selected vectors to have both coordinate sums zero. If the support size is not divisible by p, Fermat's little theorem makes 1−Sp−1=0, while the final product also vanishes; hence the support has size p, 2p, or 3p. Size p proves the claim, and size 3p reduces to part (a). For size 2p,
Ep​(x)=(p2p​)≡2(modp),
(12)
so the bracket vanishes, again contradicting F(x)=0. Only the size-p and reducible size-3p cases remain, and either yields the required zero-sum p-subsequence.

Let D(a) denote the stated constant term and suppose every ai​>0. The rational identity
∑k=1n​∏j=k​(1−Xj​Xk​​)−1=1
(13)
can be verified after clearing denominators, or by Lagrange interpolation. Multiplying it by the Dyson product and taking constant terms gives the recursion
D(a1​,…,an​)=∑k=1n​D(a1​,…,ak​−1,…,an​).
(14)
The multinomial coefficient
M(a)=a1​!⋯an​!(a1​+⋯+an​)!​
(15)
obeys the same recursion by the multinomial form of Pascal's identity.
If ak​=0, taking the constant term in Xk​ forces the zero term from every factor involving Xk​ and reduces the expression to the (n−1)-variable Dyson product with ak​ omitted. The same boundary reduction holds for M(a). Finally D(0,…,0)=M(0,…,0)=1. Induction on n and on a1​+⋯+an​ therefore proves the Dyson constant-term identity
D(a)=M(a)​.
(16)

Identify the additive group G with the finite field Fq​, where q=pγ. The sets A and B each have size k<p, and the field has odd characteristic. The Snevily matching theorem for an elementary abelian group states precisely that for two k-element subsets of the additive group of such a field, there is a bijection π:A→B for which the sums a+π(a) are pairwise distinct.
For context, its polynomial proof antisymmetrizes the Vandermonde polynomial
∏i<j​((ai​+xi​)−(aj​+xj​))
(17)
over all orderings of B. The coefficient furnished by the Dyson constant-term identity is a nonzero multiple of k!; it cannot vanish because k<p. Therefore at least one ordering (b1​,…,bk​) makes the Vandermonde product nonzero, which says exactly that
a1​+b1​,…,ak​+bk​ are pairwise distinct.​
(18)

Assume (i). An antipodal map g:Sn→Sn−1, followed by the inclusion Sn−1⊂Rn, would be an antipodal map with no zero. Hence (i) implies (ii). Conversely, if an antipodal f:Sn→Rn had no zero, then
x⟼∣f(x)∣f(x)​
(19)
would be an antipodal map to Sn−1. Thus (ii) implies (i).
If F:Bn→Sn−1 is antipodal on the boundary, regard Sn as two copies of Bn glued along their boundary. Use F(x) on the upper copy and −F(−x) on the lower copy. The boundary condition makes these definitions agree on the seam, and the resulting map Sn→Sn−1 is antipodal. Thus (ii) implies (iii).
Conversely, an antipodal map g:Sn→Sn−1 restricted to a closed hemisphere, identified with Bn, is antipodal on its equatorial boundary. Hence (iii) implies (ii). The three assertions are equivalent; they are standard forms of the Borsuk-Ulam theorem.

For v∈Sn−1, orthogonally project every member of Ci​ onto the oriented line ℓv​. These projections are compact intervals. They are pairwise intersecting because the original sets are, and pairwise intersecting intervals have a common intersection. Let mi​(v) be the midpoint of that common interval and let fi​(v) be its signed coordinate on ℓv​. Compactness makes fi​ continuous, and reversing the orientation gives
fi​(−v)=−fi​(v).
(20)
Define the continuous antipodal map
F:Sn−1⟶Rn−1,F(v)=(f1​(v)−fn​(v),…,fn−1​(v)−fn​(v)).
(21)
The Borsuk-Ulam theorem gives v with F(v)=0. Write the common value as t. The hyperplane
H={x∈Rn:x⋅v=t}
(22)
meets every set in every Ci​, because t=fi​(v) lies in the projection interval of each such set. Thus H is the required common hyperplane transversal.
