The Schwartz space on the real line is
S(R)={φ∈C∞(R):pm,n​(φ)<∞ for every m,n∈N0​},pm,n​(φ)=supx∈R​∣xmφ(n)(x)∣.
(1)
These seminorms define its Fréchet space topology. The space of tempered distributions is its continuous dual space,
S′(R)=S(R)∗.
(2)
With the angular-frequency convention, the Fourier transform of a Schwartz function is
φ​(λ)=∫R​e−iλxφ(x)dx.
(3)
It maps S continuously to itself. The transform of u∈S′ is defined through the dual pairing:
⟨u,φ⟩=⟨u,φ​⟩​,
(4)
up to the fixed reflection and 2π factor if the inverse-transform convention is used for the test function.

The space of test functions is
D(Rn)=Cc∞​(Rn).
(25)
A sequence φj​ converges to φ in D when all supports lie eventually in one compact set K and
supx∈K​∣Dα(φj​−φ)(x)∣⟶0
(26)
for every multi-index α. A distribution is a linear functional u:D→C such that, for every compact K, there are CK​>0 and mK​∈N0​ with
∣⟨u,φ⟩∣≤CK​max∣α∣≤mK​​supx∈K​∣Dαφ(x)∣
(27)
whenever suppφ⊆K. Convergence in D′ is pointwise convergence on test functions.
Continuity plainly implies sequential continuity. Conversely, suppose the linear form is sequentially continuous but the displayed estimate fails for some compact K. For every j, choose φj​ supported in K such that
max∣α∣≤j​supK​∣Dαφj​∣≤j1​,∣⟨u,φj​⟩∣≥1.
(28)
Then φj​→0 in D, while u(φj​)→0, a contradiction. Hence the seminorm estimate holds on every compact set and u∈D′.
For a translation vector h and a multi-index α, define
⟨τh​u,φ⟩=⟨u,φ(⋅+h)⟩,⟨Dαu,φ⟩=(−1)∣α∣⟨u,Dαφ⟩.
(29)
These definitions extend ordinary translation and differentiation to distributions.
If τtei​​u=u for every t, differentiating its pairing at t=0 gives ∂i​u=0. Conversely, if ∂i​u=0, then for every test function
dtd​⟨τtei​​u,φ⟩=⟨u,∂i​φ(⋅+tei​)⟩=−⟨∂i​u,φ(⋅+tei​)⟩=0.
(30)
The pairing is constant in t, hence τtei​​u=u. This proves both directions of translation invariance and vanishing distributional derivative.
Finally, the distributional differentiation obeys the linear chain rule under the linear coordinates s=x−y and t=x+y. Thus
∂x2​f(x−y)=f′′(x−y)=∂y2​f(x−y)
(31)
and likewise
∂x2​g(x+y)=g′′(x+y)=∂y2​g(x+y)
(32)
as distributions. Adding the two identities gives
uxx​−uyy​=0​,
(33)
the one-dimensional wave equation; this is the low-regularity form of the travelling waves in the D'Alembert formula.

A phase function is a real smooth function Φ on X×(Rk∖{0}) that is positively homogeneous of degree one in θ and has nonzero total differential dx,θ​Φ. The symbol class
Sym(X,Rk;N)
(34)
consists of smooth amplitudes for which, for every compact K⊂X and all multi-indices α,β,
∣Dxα​Dθβ​a(x,θ)∣≤CK,α,β​⟨θ⟩N−∣β∣.
(35)
Choose a smooth cutoff function χ equal to one near zero. The associated oscillatory integral is defined on a test function f by
⟨IΦ​(a),f⟩=limε↓0​∫X​∫Rk​eiΦ(x,θ)a(x,θ)f(x)χ(εθ)dθdx.
(36)
On the compact x-support of f, use an integration-by-parts operator L satisfying LeiΦ=eiΦ. Repeated application of its formal adjoint lowers the effective symbol order until the integral is absolutely convergent. The resulting bounds involve only finitely many derivatives of f, prove that the limit is independent of χ, and give the seminorm estimate required for
IΦ​(a)∈D′(X)​.
(37)
