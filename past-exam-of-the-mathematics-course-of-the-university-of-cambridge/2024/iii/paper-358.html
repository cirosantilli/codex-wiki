A computational problem in the SCI hierarchy is a quadruple
(Ξ,Ω,M,Λ).
(1)
Here Ω is the primary set of inputs, Λ is the set of permitted evaluation functions, (M,d) is the output metric space, and Ξ:Ω→M is the problem function. The Solvability complexity index is defined from the minimum height of a tower of algorithms that computes Ξ from finite subsets of Λ.
For the classical computational spectral problem, take
Ω=B(ℓ2(N)),λij​(A)=⟨Aej​,ei​⟩,Λ={λij​:i,j∈N},
(2)
and
Ξ(A)=Sp(A).
(3)
Since the spectrum of a bounded operator is a nonempty compact subset of the complex numbers, one may take M to be the nonempty compact subsets of C with the Hausdorff distance. The Attouch--Wets topology gives an equivalent convenient formulation on bounded spectral sets and also extends naturally to unbounded closed sets. This choice of M makes convergence mean convergence of the whole spectrum as a set, including both the absence of persistent spectral pollution and the approximation of every genuine spectral point.

A general algorithm in the SCI hierarchy Γ:Ω→M reads a finite set ΛΓ​(A)⊂Λ on each input A. Its output depends only on those values, and whenever another input B has the same values on ΛΓ​(A), the algorithm requests the same finite set and gives the same output. A tower of algorithms of height k satisfies
Ξ(A)=limnk​→∞​⋯limn1​→∞​Γnk​,…,n1​​(A)
(4)
for every A∈Ω. The Solvability complexity index is the least such k, with value zero when one finite algorithm computes Ξ exactly.
We reduce a known height-three finite-column decision problem to spectral computation. Let (aij​)i,j∈Z​ be a bi-infinite zero-one matrix and let Ξcol​ ask whether there is a number D such that every column either contains fewer than D ones or has infinitely many ones in both directions. The lecture lower-bound theorem states
SCI(Ξcol​)G​=3,
(5)
even for unrestricted general algorithms.
For a zero-one sequence a=(ai​)i∈Z​, define Ba​ on ℓ2(Z) to be the identity on coordinates where ai​=0 and the shift from each coordinate with ai​=1 to the next coordinate carrying a one. It is a direct sum of identity pieces and one shift chain. Consequently:
  • finitely many ones give Sp(Ba​)⊂{0,1};
  • infinitely many ones in both directions give the unit circle T;
  • a one-sided infinite sequence gives the closed unit disk D.
For the columns a(j)=(aij​)i​, form the bounded direct-sum operator
C(a)=⨁j∈Z​Ba(j)​.
(6)
Every finite set of matrix entries of C(a) is determined by finitely many entries of a, so this construction respects the finite-information condition for a general algorithm in the SCI hierarchy. The preceding trichotomy implies
Ξcol​(a)=No⟹Sp(C(a))=D,
(7)
whereas
Ξcol​(a)=Yes⟹Sp(C(a))⊂{0}∪T.
(8)
The two cases are separated by the point 1/2: its distance from the spectrum is respectively zero and 1/2.
If a height-two tower Γn2​,n1​​ computed the spectrum of every bounded operator, apply it to C(a) and inspect
αn2​,n1​​=dist(21​,Γn2​,n1​​(C(a))).
(9)
Use the disjoint intervals [0,1/8] and [3/8,∞) to turn each finite output into Yes or No, retaining the latest inner-stage value that lies in either interval. Convergence in the Hausdorff distance ensures that the inner limit stabilizes; the outer limit answers Ξcol​ correctly. This would give a height-two tower for a problem whose Solvability complexity index is three, a contradiction. Therefore
SCI(Sp,B(ℓ2(N)))≥3.​
(10)

Let Ln​=span{e1​,…,en​} and let Pn​ be its orthogonal projection. For z∈C, define
γn​(z)2=λmin​((1+∣z∣2)In​−zˉPn​APn​−zPn​A∗Pn​).
(11)
Because A is unitary,
γn​(z)=infx∈Ln​∥x∥=1​​∥(A−zI)x∥.
(12)
The displayed finite Hermitian matrix uses only finitely many matrix entries of A, and its least eigenvalue can be approximated by an arithmetic algorithm in the SCI hierarchy.
As Ln​ increases densely,
γn​(z)↓inf∥x∥=1​∥(A−zI)x∥.
(13)
A unitary operator is normal, so the spectral theorem for normal operators on a separable Hilbert space identifies the limit as
dist(z,Sp(A)).
(14)
The functions γn​ are continuous and decrease to a continuous function on the compact unit circle. The Dini theorem therefore gives uniform convergence there.
Take successively finer rational meshes Gn​ around T. From the finitely computed values of γn​, retain the mesh minima in the comparison neighborhoods whose radii are γn​(z); equivalently, use the standard local-minimum construction for a decreasing approximation to a distance function. Call the resulting finite set Γn​(A). Uniform convergence and the shrinking mesh imply
Γn​(A)⟶Sp(A)​
(15)
in Hausdorff distance. Every operation at stage n is finite and arithmetic, so (Γn​) is the required one-limit sequence.
There is no finite-stage certificate that the whole output has the correct Hausdorff error: the convergence of γn​ has no uniform computable rate over all unitary operators, and unseen matrix entries can still reveal a missing spectral component. A small computed residual can certify that an individual output point lies near the spectrum, but it cannot verify that Γn​(A) covers all of Sp(A). Thus the full finite-stage output is not verifiable without additional information.

A projection-valued measure on the Borel sets of C is a map E into the orthogonal projections on a separable Hilbert space H such that
E(∅)=0,E(C)=I,E(B∩C)=E(B)E(C),
(16)
and for pairwise disjoint Bj​,
E(⋃j​Bj​)v=∑j​E(Bj​)v
(17)
for every v∈H, with convergence in norm.
The spectral theorem for normal operators on a separable Hilbert space states that a bounded normal operator A has a unique projection-valued measure supported on Sp(A) for which
A=∫Sp(A)​zdE(z).​
(18)
More generally, the Borel functional calculus for a normal operator is
f(A)=∫f(z)dE(z).
(19)
For v,w∈H, the scalar spectral measures are
μv,w​(B)=⟨E(B)v,w⟩,μv​=μv,v​.
(20)
If A is self-adjoint, its spectrum and hence the support of E lie in R. Moreover,
μv​(B)=⟨E(B)v,v⟩=∥E(B)v∥2≥0,
(21)
so μv​ is a positive measure, and
μv​(R)=⟨Iv,v⟩=∥v∥2.​
(22)
The paper prints total mass ∥v∥; with the standard definition it is ∥v∥2, so the unsquared norm is a typographical error.

μv,w(n)​(B)=⟨En​(B)Pn​v,Pn​w⟩.

The scalar spectral measures converge weakly when
∫R​fdμv,w(n)​⟶∫R​fdμv,w​​
(24)
for every bounded continuous function f and every v,w∈H. By the spectral theorem for normal operators on a separable Hilbert space, this is equivalent to
⟨f(An​)Pn​v,Pn​w⟩⟶⟨f(A)v,w⟩.
(25)
The assumed moment identities say precisely that this convergence holds for every monomial f(x)=xm. It follows by linearity for every polynomial. For v=w, the m=2 identity gives
∫x2dμv(n)​(x)⟶∫x2dμv​(x),
(26)
so Markov inequality makes the positive measures (μv(n)​) tight. Higher even moments similarly control the tails of any fixed polynomial.
Given a bounded continuous f and ϵ>0, choose R so that the measure tails are uniformly small. The Weierstrass approximation theorem supplies a polynomial p with
sup∣x∣≤R​∣f(x)−p(x)∣<ϵ.

Moment convergence handles p; tightness and a sufficiently high even moment handle the two tails. Hence ∫fdμv(n)​→∫fdμv​. The polarization identity then gives the same conclusion for μv,w(n)​. This proves weak convergence of scalar spectral measures.
The assertion fails if only m=1 is assumed. Let H=ℓ2(N), let Hn​=span{e1​,…,en​}, take A=0, and let

ω(N)=0⟹ω(F−1(N))=0.
(41)
Equivalently, the pushforward measure F∗​ω(B)=ω(F−1(B)) satisfies F∗​ω≪ω.
For an essentially bounded observable g, define the Koopman operator
KF​g=g∘F.​
(42)
Nonsingularity makes this well defined on almost-everywhere equivalence classes. By the Radon-Nikodym theorem,
∥KF​g∥22​=∫∣g∣2d(F∗​ω)=∫∣g∣2dωd(F∗​ω)​dω.

Suppose first that the system is ergodic and KF​g=g. For each real t, the level set
Et​={x:Reg(x)>t}
(47)
is invariant modulo a null set, so ω(Et​)∈{0,1}. The distribution function of Reg can therefore jump only once, which makes Reg constant almost everywhere. The same argument applies to Img.
Conversely, if E is invariant, then KF​1E​=1E​. If every invariant L2 function is constant, the indicator function 1E​ is almost everywhere zero or one, and hence ω(E)=0 or 1. This proves the invariant-function characterization of ergodicity.

For inexact information in the SCI hierarchy, replace every exact evaluation λ∈Λ by a family of admissible approximations λm​ satisfying
d(λm​(A),λ(A))≤2−m.
(52)
An algorithm must converge for every admissible choice of approximations, not merely for one favored encoding.
For continuous nonsingular maps F:X→X, take the evaluations to be arbitrary point queries. At precision m, a query at x∈X returns any y satisfying
dX​(y,F(x))≤2−m.​
(53)
Thus the information set contains all triples (x,m,y) satisfying this inequality. This is a perfect measurement device for a dynamical system: it can sample any state, at any requested accuracy, with no fixed noise floor. The finite-information rule still requires each terminating computation to make only finitely many such measurements.

Assume for contradiction that a sequence of general algorithms Γn​ decides ergodicity from the perfect measurement data, so that Γn​(F) eventually equals Ξerg​(F) for every F∈Ω.
Restrict the input class to the circle rotations
Fa​(x)=x+a(mod2π).
(54)
From inexact information in the SCI hierarchy for the real number a, one can answer every requested measurement of Fa​(x) to the same precision. The supposed algorithms would therefore give a one-limit decision procedure for
a⟼1R∖Q​(a/π),
(55)
because part (b)(ii) identifies ergodicity with irrationality.
Every finite-information general algorithm is locally constant on a sufficiently small cylinder of the inexact data. A pointwise limit of a sequence of such functions is a Baire class one function. But the rationality indicator is discontinuous at every real number: every interval contains both rational and irrational numbers. The theorem that the discontinuity set of a Baire class one function is meagre, or directly rationality indicator is not Baire class one, gives a contradiction.
Hence no one-limit tower of general algorithms can decide ergodicity, even with the perfect measurement device:
{Ξerg​,Ω,{0,1},Λ}Δ1​∈/Δ2G​.​
(56)
