Assume the null distribution is a centrally symmetric probability distribution, so Xi​ and −Xi​ have the same law. A sign-flip randomization test draws signs si​∈{−1,1} independently and recomputes, for example,
T(s)=n​n1​∑i=1n​si​Xi​​2.
(16)
The exact p-value averages over all 2n sign vectors:
p=2−n#{s:T(s)≥T(1,…,1)}.
(17)
With B random sign vectors, including the observed configuration, the standard Monte Carlo version is (1+#{b:Tb​≥T0​})/(B+1), where T0​ is the observed statistic. Joint sign invariance under the null makes this finite-sample valid.

The squared-norm test is omnibus: every fixed nonzero mean eventually changes ∥Xn​∥. Its null law, however, is an infinite weighted chi-squared distribution and requires accurate estimation of enough covariance eigenvalues; noisy low-variance directions can also make calibration inefficient.
The FPCA mean test has the simple χK2​ limit and standardizes retained directions by their variances. It is effective when the signal lies in the leading principal component subspace, but choosing K introduces a tuning decision and truncation makes the test blind to alternatives orthogonal to that subspace. Close or repeated eigenvalues also make individual empirical eigenfunctions unstable.
The sign-flip randomization test can provide finite-sample calibration and avoids estimating a limiting covariance spectrum. Its exactness requires central symmetry, which is stronger than merely having zero mean, and exhaustive enumeration costs 2n evaluations; Monte Carlo sign flips introduce simulation error. Its power still depends on the statistic used inside the randomization scheme.
