Let P∙​→Z be a projective resolution of the trivial ZG-module. The projective-resolution definition of group cohomology is
Hn(G,M)=Hn(HomZG​(P∙​,M)).
(1)
This is independent, up to a natural isomorphism, of the chosen projective resolution.
The degreewise natural isomorphisms
HomZG​(Pj​,M1​⊕M2​)≅HomZG​(Pj​,M1​)⊕HomZG​(Pj​,M2​)
(2)
commute with the coboundary maps. Taking cohomology proves that group cohomology commutes with finite direct sums:
Hn(G,M1​⊕M2​)≅Hn(G,M1​)⊕Hn(G,M2​).
(3)
Now restrict P∙​ from G to a subgroup K. The group ring ZG is free as a ZK-module, so restriction carries free modules to free modules and projective modules to projective modules. Thus the restricted complex is a projective resolution of the trivial ZK-module. For the coinduced module Y=HomZK​(ZG,X), the Hom functor adjunction for a coinduced module gives an isomorphism of cochain complexes
HomZG​(P∙​,Y)≅HomZK​(P∙​,X).
(4)
Explicitly, a map F is sent to p↦F(p)(1); the inverse sends a ZK-linear map a to p↦(r↦a(rp)). Taking cohomology proves Shapiro's lemma:
Hn(G,Y)≅Hn(K,X).
(5)
For the conjugation module of a group ring N=ZGconj​, the basis G is the disjoint union of its conjugacy classes. Hence N is the direct sum of the integral permutation modules on those classes. The class of a representative gi​ is the transitive G-set G/CG​(gi​), where CG​(gi​) is its centralizer. Since G is finite, this permutation module is both induced and coinduced from the trivial CG​(gi​)-module Z. Applying group cohomology commutes with finite direct sums and Shapiro's lemma yields the group cohomology of a conjugation module:
Hn(G,N)≅⨁i​Hn(CG​(gi​),Z).
(6)
The symmetric group S3​ has three conjugacy classes, represented by the identity, a transposition, and a three-cycle. Their centralizers are respectively
S3​,C2​,C3​.
(7)
For any finite group L acting trivially on Z,
H1(L,Z)=Hom(L,Z)=0,
(8)
because a group homomorphism sends an element of finite order to an element of finite order, while the additive group of the integers contains no nonzero torsion elements. Therefore
H1(S3​,N)=0.
(9)
The periodic resolution of a finite cyclic group alternates the maps t−1 and 1+t+⋯+tm−1. After applying HomZCm​​(−,Z) with the trivial action, these become alternately zero and multiplication by m, proving
H2(Cm​,Z)≅Z/mZ.
(10)
Combining this calculation with the supplied H2(S3​,Z)≅Z/2Z gives
H2(S3​,N)≅Z/2Z⊕Z/2Z⊕Z/3Z.​
(11)

The Schur multiplier of a group G is
M(G)=H2​(G,Z),
(12)
the second group homology group with trivial integral coefficients. If G≅F/R is a free presentation, Hopf's formula states that
M(G)≅[F,R]R∩[F,F]​.
(13)
Write IF​=ker(ZF→Z) for the augmentation ideal. The presentation relation sequence is
0⟶R/[R,R]⟶ZG⊗ZF​IF​⟶ZG⟶Z⟶0,
(14)
where r[R,R]↦1⊗(r−1). If F is free on a set S, then IF​ is free as a left ZF-module on the elements s−1, so the two modules immediately preceding Z are free ZG-modules. Resolving the relation module R/[R,R] by free modules and splicing produces a free resolution of Z.
Apply the right-exact functor Z⊗ZG​− to this partial resolution. Its degree-two homology is the kernel of
(R/[R,R])G​⟶(ZG⊗ZF​IF​)G​.
(15)
The coinvariant module on the left is R/[F,R]. On the right, the map f−1↦f[F,F] identifies the coinvariants with the abelianization F/[F,F]. The displayed map is induced by the inclusion R↪F, so its kernel is
ker(R/[F,R]⟶F/[F,F])=[F,R]R∩[F,F]​.
(16)
This proves Hopf's formula.
For an abelian group A, the Schur multiplier of an abelian group is M(A)≅⋀2A. One way to see the direct-sum rule is the degree-two Künneth theorem:
H2​(A×B,Z)≅H2​(A,Z)⊕H2​(B,Z)⊕(H1​(A,Z)⊗H1​(B,Z)).
(17)
A cyclic group has zero second integral group homology, while
Cm​⊗Z​Cn​≅Cgcd(m,n)​.
(18)
Consequently
M(C2​×C4​×C6​)≅Cgcd(2,4)​⊕Cgcd(2,6)​⊕Cgcd(4,6)​≅C2​⊕C2​⊕C2​.​
(19)

For a,b∈I, the square-zero ideal condition gives
(1+a)(1+b)=1+a+b+ab=1+a+b,(1+a)−1=1−a.
(20)
Thus the square-zero unit subgroup 1+I is abelian, and
Iadd​⟶1+I,a⟼1+a
(21)
is a group isomorphism from the additive group of I.
Use the specified ring isomorphism R/I≅ZG. For x∈ZG, choose a lift r∈R and define x⋅a=ra for a∈I. Two lifts differ by an element of I, whose product with a vanishes, so this is well defined. Right multiplication is handled identically. The two actions commute by associativity, making I a bimodule. If u∈R lifts g∈G, then u is a unit: a lift v of g−1 makes both uv and vu elements of 1+I, hence units, and a ring element with both a left and a right inverse is invertible. Conjugation therefore defines
g⋅a=uau−1.
(22)
Changing u by an element of I does not change this expression because I2=0. Moreover,
u(1+a)u−1=1+uau−1,
(23)
so a↦1+a is an isomorphism of ZG-modules for these conjugation actions.
Let R×→(R/I)× be reduction on unit groups, and define U as the inverse image of the distinguished subgroup G⊆(ZG)×. Every g∈G has a unit lift by the preceding argument, and the kernel consists exactly of the units congruent to 1, namely 1+I. Multiplication in R therefore gives the group extension
1⟶1+I⟶U⟶G⟶1.
(24)
Choose a set-theoretic section s:G→U with s(1)=1. Its extension cocycle
c(g,h)=s(g)s(h)s(gh)−1∈1+I
(25)
satisfies the two-cocycle identity by associativity. A different section changes c by a group coboundary, so second group cohomology classifies group extensions gives a well-defined class
x=[c]∈H2(G,1+I).
(26)
The same construction for (R1​,I1​) gives U1​, an extension cocycle c1​, and x1​=[c1​]∈H2(G,1+I1​).
The answer to the final question is no. An abstract ring isomorphism R1​≅R need not carry the distinguished ideal I1​ to I, need not induce the identity under the two chosen identifications of the quotient rings with ZG, and need not induce the prescribed ZG-module isomorphism θ. Hence it need not give an isomorphism of the two displayed group extensions, so it imposes no equality ϕ(x1​)=x. That equality does hold if the ring isomorphism has all three compatibility properties, because it then carries one extension cocycle to the other up to a group coboundary.

Put Q=G/H and write MH for the invariant submodule. The five-term exact sequence in group cohomology associated with the Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence is
0⟶H1(Q,MH)inf​H1(G,M)res​H1(H,M)Qd2​​H2(Q,MH)inf​H2(G,M).
(27)
The inflation map in group cohomology composes a cocycle on Q with the quotient homomorphism G→Q. The restriction map in group cohomology restricts a cocycle from G to H. The quotient action on the middle term is, for q=gH and a one-cocycle f,
q⋅[f]=[h⟼g⋅f(g−1hg)].
(28)
This is independent of the lift and of the representative at the level of cohomology. Finally, the transgression in group cohomology extends a Q-invariant class on H to a one-cochain on G; its coboundary is H-basic and descends to the two-cocycle on Q representing d2​[f]. Changing the extension changes that cocycle by a group coboundary.
For the application, choose free generators x1​,…,xn​ of F and normal generators r1​,…,rn​ of R. Since a finite nonabelian simple group K is a perfect group, its abelianization is zero. The five-term sequence for 1→R→F→K→1 with trivial coefficients contains
0⟶Hom(K,Z)⟶Hom(F,Z)res​Hom(R,Z)K⟶H2(K,Z)⟶H2(F,Z).
(29)
The first term is zero because K is finite, and the last term is zero because a free group has cohomological dimension one. It remains to prove that restriction is surjective.
The invariant submodule of homomorphisms R→Z is exactly
Hom(R/[F,R],Z).
(30)
The images of the relators r1​,…,rn​ generate R/[F,R], so such a homomorphism λ is determined by the integer vector b=(λ(r1​),…,λ(rn​))T. Let A be the relator exponent-sum matrix. This square integer matrix presents Kab, which is zero, so A is a unimodular matrix. There is therefore an integer vector v satisfying Av=b. Define ψ:F→Z by assigning to xi​ the ith entry of v. The definition of A gives ψ(rj​)=λ(rj​) for every j. Since the relator images generate R/[F,R], the restriction of ψ to R equals λ. Restriction is surjective, exactness now gives
H2(K,Z)=0.​
(31)
