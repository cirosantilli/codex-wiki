This is Ladner's theorem. Assume P=NP and let S be the Boolean satisfiability problem. Enumerate all clocked polynomial-time deterministic machines as D0​,D1​,… and all clocked polynomial-time candidate reductions as R0​,R1​,…. A standard delayed-diagonalization schedule gives a nondecreasing, unbounded, polynomial-time computable function s:N→N that increases only by one. Define
L={x∈S:s(∣x∣) is even}.
(1)
The schedule alternates two requirements. At even stage 2j, it holds s fixed while searching successively larger finite sets of strings for an x on which Dj​(x) disagrees with membership in L; after finding one it increments s. At odd stage 2j+1, it searches for an x such that
x∈S⟺Rj​(x)∈/L,
(2)
and then increments s. Length and simulation budgets are increased slowly enough that each finite search is eventually exhaustive but computing s(n) still takes polynomial time. This is achieved, for example, by permitting only n simulation steps and searches on strings of logarithmic length before deciding s(n+1).
Every stage must finish. If an even stage 2j remained forever, then s would eventually be a fixed even number, so L and S would differ on only finitely many strings. If Dj​ decided L, those finitely many exceptions could be hardwired to decide S in P, contradicting P=NP. If an odd stage 2j+1 remained forever, then s would eventually be odd and L would be finite. A correct polynomial-time many-one reduction Rj​ from S to L would again put S in P. Thus every Dj​ fails to decide L, and every Rj​ fails to reduce S to L.
Finally, L∈NP: compute s(∣x∣) and, when it is even, use the usual polynomial-time certificate for satisfiability. Hence
L∈NP∖PandL is not NP-complete.​
(3)
