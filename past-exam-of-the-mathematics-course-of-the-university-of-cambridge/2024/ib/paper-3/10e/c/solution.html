Let G=Fp×​, and let m be the least common multiple of the orders of its elements. For every prime power qa dividing m, some element of G has order divisible by qa, and a suitable power of it has order exactly qa. Multiplying these elements over the distinct primes produces, because their orders are coprime, an element of order m.
Every element of G is a root of Xm−1. A nonzero polynomial of degree m over a field has at most m roots, so p−1≤m. On the other hand, every element order divides p−1 by Lagrange's theorem, so m≤p−1. Hence m=p−1, and
Fp×​ is cyclic​.

The squaring homomorphism has kernel {1,−1} because p is odd. Its image H therefore has order (p−1)/2 and index two. If p=3, then 3=0 is already a square. Otherwise 2,3∈G. In the two-element quotient G/H, either 2H=H, or 3H=H, or both are the nontrivial coset, in which case 6H=H. Thus one of 2,3,6 is a square modulo p.
