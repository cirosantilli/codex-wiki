For a divisor D on a smooth projective curve, let L(D)={f∈k(X):div(f)+D≥0}∪{0}, and write ℓ(D)=dimk​L(D). The Riemann-Roch theorem states
ℓ(D)−ℓ(KX​−D)=degD+1−g,​
(66)
where g is the genus, KX​ is a canonical divisor given by a nonzero rational differential, and the degree of a divisor is the sum of its coefficients since k is algebraically closed.
For genus one, a nonzero regular differential has a nonnegative divisor of degree 2g−2=0, hence no zeros; therefore KX​∼0. Negative-degree divisors have no nonzero sections. Thus ℓ(nP∞​)=n for n>0, ℓ(0)=1, and L(nP∞​)=0 for n<0.
Choose x∈L(2P∞​)∖L(P∞​) and y∈L(3P∞​)∖L(2P∞​). Their unique poles have orders two and three respectively. Distinct pole orders prove linear independence in the following complete list:
L(0)L(2P∞​)L(3P∞​)L(4P∞​)L(5P∞​)L(6P∞​)​=L(P∞​)=⟨1⟩,=⟨1,x⟩,=⟨1,x,y⟩,=⟨1,x,y,x2⟩,=⟨1,x,y,x2,xy⟩,=⟨1,x,y,x2,xy,x3⟩.​​
(67)
Since y2 also lies in the last six-dimensional space, it satisfies
y2=ax3+bxy+cx2+dy+ex+f,a=0.
(68)
The coefficient a cannot vanish because only x3 among the displayed basis has a sixth-order pole available to cancel the leading pole of y2. Homogenizing gives a plane cubic relation, valid also in characteristics two and three without completing squares or dividing by three.
It remains to prove that the map actually embeds the curve. For D=3P∞​, Riemann-Roch gives ℓ(D)=3, ℓ(D−Q)=2 for every point Q, and ℓ(D−Q−R)=1, including R=Q. The first equality makes the linear system base-point-free; the second separates distinct points, and the double-point case separates tangent directions. Therefore D is very ample and ϕD​ is a closed immersion into P2. Its pullback of a line has degree three, so its image has degree three. The displayed cubic equation consequently defines the image, which is smooth because it is isomorphic to X. Thus ϕ3P∞​​:X≅a smooth plane cubic​. The affine expression (1:x:y) extends across P∞​ to (0:0:1) by the pole orders.
