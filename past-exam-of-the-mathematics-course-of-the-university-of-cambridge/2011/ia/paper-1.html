The intermediate value theorem states that if f:[a,b]→R is continuous, then every real value between f(a) and f(b) is attained at some c∈[a,b]. Here is a proof using completeness of the real numbers. Endpoint values are immediate, so suppose f(a)<y<f(b); reversing the sign of f handles the other ordering. Let
S={x∈[a,b]:f(x)<y},c=supS.
(43)
The set is nonempty and bounded above. Continuity at a puts points to the right of a in S, while continuity at b excludes an entire interval to the left of b, so a<c<b. If f(c)<y, continuity puts some point larger than c in S, contradicting its supremum. If f(c)>y, continuity excludes S in a neighborhood of c, contradicting the existence of points of S arbitrarily close to its supremum from below. Therefore f(c)=y, proving the theorem.
For the fixed-point assertion, put g(x)=f(x)−x. This is continuous, with g(0)=f(0)≥0 and g(1)=f(1)−1≤0. If either endpoint value is zero it already gives a fixed point; otherwise the intermediate value theorem gives an interior zero. Hence
Every continuous self-map of [0,1] has a fixed point.​
(44)
The proof needs both continuity and the closed-interval endpoint information; the following counterexamples separate those hypotheses.

For the second construction, use a summable absolute-value cusp series:
G(x)=n=2∑∞​2−n​x−n1​​.​
(47)
On [−R,R], each summand is bounded by 2−n(R+1/2). The Weierstrass M-test therefore gives uniform convergence on every bounded interval, and the uniform limit theorem makes G continuous everywhere.

The fundamental theorem of calculus connects the Riemann integral with differentiation. Its first part says that for continuous h:[a,b]→R, the accumulation function H(x)=∫ax​h(t)dt is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable inside it, with
H′(x)=h(x).​
(52)
To prove this, boundedness of h gives ∣H(y)−H(x)∣≤∥h∥∞​∣y−x∣, proving continuity. For an interior point and a sufficiently small nonzero increment u,
uH(x+u)−H(x)​−h(x)=u1​∫xx+u​(h(t)−h(x))dt.
(53)
Its absolute value is at most sup∣t−x∣≤∣u∣​∣h(t)−h(x)∣, which tends to zero by continuity of h at x. This proves the derivative formula. The same estimate gives the appropriate one-sided endpoint derivatives.
The second part says that if Q is continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), and Q′=h with h continuous, then
∫ab​h(t)dt=Q(b)−Q(a).​
(54)
Indeed (Q−H)′=0, so the mean value theorem makes Q−H constant, and evaluating at the endpoints proves the formula. A useful stronger version only requires Q′ to be Riemann integrable. For a partition a=x0​<⋯<xN​=b, the mean value theorem on each subinterval provides ξj​∈(xj−1​,xj​) such that
Q(b)−Q(a)=∑j=1N​Q′(ξj​)(xj​−xj−1​).
(55)
As the mesh tends to zero, these tagged Riemann sums converge to ∫ab​Q′, proving that version too. Its integrability hypothesis must not be omitted.
An integrable integrand need not give an everywhere differentiable accumulation function. For a counterexample, let f(t)=0 for t<1/2 and f(t)=1 for t≥1/2. This bounded step function is Riemann integrable, but
F(x)=∫0x​f(t)dt={0,x−1/2,​x≤1/2,x≥1/2.​
(56)
Its left and right derivatives at 1/2 are zero and one. Thus F need not be differentiable at every interior point. The first part of the fundamental theorem of calculus does apply at every point where the integrand is continuous.
A derivative need not have a Riemann integral. Define on all of R
f(x)={x2sin(1/x2),0,​x=0,x=0.​
(57)
At zero f(h)/h=hsin(1/h2)→0, so f′(0)=0; elsewhere the product rule and chain rule give
f′(x)=2xsin(1/x2)−x2​cos(1/x2).
(58)
At xn​=(2πn)−1/2, this equals −2/xn​→−∞. Hence f′ is unbounded on [0,1], whereas a properly Riemann integrable function on a compact interval must be bounded. The requested Riemann integral therefore need not exist, even though f is differentiable everywhere. This example is an unbounded derivative obstruction to Riemann integrability; it does not contradict the theorem's version that explicitly assumes integrability of the derivative.
