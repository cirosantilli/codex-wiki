Take the cube with vertices (±1,±1,±1), and choose the edge E={(1,1,z):−1≤z≤1}. Here the symmetry group of a cube consists of all rigid symmetries, including reflections. A symmetry fixes the centre and permutes the three pairs of opposite faces, so its matrix is a signed permutation matrix.
A symmetry in the stabilizer subgroup of E must preserve its midpoint (1,1,0) and send its direction (0,0,1) to itself or its negative. In this cube symmetry action on edges, its only choices are to interchange the first two coordinates and to reverse the third coordinate. Thus the stabilizer subgroup consists of
(x,y,z)↦(x,y,z),(y,x,z),(x,y,−z),(y,x,−z).
(1)
The two generating reflections commute and have order two, so this subgroup is a Klein four-group, isomorphic to C2​×C2​ and has order four. Permuting coordinates and changing signs carries E to any edge. Its orbit of a group action is therefore the set of all twelve edges. The orbit-stabilizer theorem gives
∣G∣=12⋅4=48.​
(2)
If one restricts to orientation-preserving symmetries, only two of the four stabilizer elements remain, giving the rotational symmetry group of a cube of order 24; the full symmetry group asked for here has order 48.

Lagrange's theorem says that for a finite group G and a subgroup H,
∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣.
(4)
In particular, ∣H∣ divides ∣G∣. To prove it, consider the left cosets gH. Every element lies in one, and if gH and g′H intersect, then gh=g′h′ for some h,h′∈H. Hence g=g′h′h−1 and gH=g′H. Distinct cosets are therefore disjoint and partition G. Multiplication by g is a bijection H→gH, so every coset has ∣H∣ elements. Counting this partition proves the formula.
Write the dihedral group as
D2n​=⟨r,s:rn=s2=e, srs=r−1⟩,
(5)
where r is a rotation and s a reflection. If k∣n, the cyclic subgroup ⟨rn/k⟩ has order k. If k∣2n but k∤n, then k is even: an odd divisor of 2n would divide n. Put m=k/2, so m∣n. The subgroup
⟨rn/m,s⟩​
(6)
contains exactly the m rotations rjn/m and the m reflections rjn/ms, for 0≤j<m. The inversion relation makes this set closed under multiplication and inverses. These 2m=k elements are distinct, so it has the required order. This proves the dihedral subgroups of every divisor order property. The construction works for every even divisor, including those already covered by the cyclic construction.

The order of a group element g is the least positive integer m for which gm=e, if one exists. In a finite group, two terms of e,g,g2,…,g∣G∣ must agree. If gi=gj with i<j, cancellation gives gj−i=e. Thus such positive integers exist and have a least member: every element of a finite group has finite order.
If h=aga−1, repeated cancellation shows hm=agma−1 for every positive integer m. Consequently hm=e exactly when gm=e. The least positive exponents are therefore equal:
ord(aga−1)=ord(g).​
(20)
This proof also shows that conjugation preserves infinite order when the ambient group is not finite.

For a permutation g, start with a letter i and follow i,g(i),g2(i),…. Finiteness gives a repetition; since g is invertible, the first return is to i. The visited letters form a permutation cycle on which g acts by moving to the next letter. If there are letters left, repeat the construction starting with one of them. Its trajectory cannot meet an earlier one, because applying a suitable inverse power of g would put its starting letter in that earlier trajectory. Continuing partitions the letters into disjoint cycles, including fixed points as cycles of length one.
The resulting disjoint permutation cycles have disjoint supports, and their product agrees with g on every letter. This proves the cycle decomposition of a permutation. A power gm fixes a cycle of length l exactly when l∣m, so if the cycle lengths are l1​,…,lt​,
ord(g)=lcm(l1​,…,lt​).​
(21)
The least common multiple is essential: multiplying the lengths would overcount whenever cycles share a factor.

For either coefficient system Z or Fp​, multiplying two matrices keeps all entries in that system, and multiplicativity of the determinant preserves determinant one. The identity matrix has determinant one, and for a determinant-one matrix the matrix inverse is
(ac​bd​)−1=(d−c​−ba​).
(29)
Its entries again belong to the same coefficient system and its determinant is one. Together with associativity of matrix multiplication, these facts establish all the group axioms. Thus both sets are special linear groups.
Reduce each entry modulo five to define
π:SL2​(Z)⟶SL2​(F5​),M⟼M.
(30)
Reduction respects sums and products, so MN=MN. It also respects the determinant, ensuring that the image has determinant one. Hence π is a group homomorphism. Its kernel of a group homomorphism consists exactly of determinant-one integer matrices congruent to the identity modulo five:
kerπ={(1+5a5c​5b1+5d​)∈SL2​(Z):a,b,c,d∈Z}.​
(31)
The determinant-one condition remains part of this set; the four integers cannot be chosen independently without it. A kernel of a group homomorphism is a normal subgroup, since π(gkg−1)=π(g)eπ(g)−1=e for k∈kerπ. This is the principal congruence subgroup of level five.

The general linear group GL2​(F5​) consists of invertible two-by-two matrices over the finite field F5​, under matrix multiplication. Such a matrix has two linearly independent columns. There are 52−1=24 choices for a nonzero first column, and 52−5=20 choices for a second column outside its one-dimensional span. Thus
∣GL2​(F5​)∣=24⋅20=480.​
(32)
The determinant is a group homomorphism
det:GL2​(F5​)⟶F5×​,
(33)
where F5×​={1,2,3,4} is the multiplicative group of nonzero field elements. It is surjective because diag(t,1) has determinant t. Its kernel of a group homomorphism is SL2​(F5​). The first isomorphism theorem gives a quotient of order four, whence
∣SL2​(F5​)∣=480/4=120.​
(34)

H={M∈GL2​(F5​):detM∈{1,4}}​

For a group action, the orbit-stabilizer theorem identifies the orbit of a group action of x with the left cosets of its stabilizer subgroup:
G/Gx​⟶Gx,gGx​⟼gx.
(36)
This is well defined and bijective because gx=hx exactly when h−1g∈Gx​. In particular, for finite G,
∣Gx∣=[G:Gx​],∣G∣=∣Gx∣∣Gx​∣.​
(37)
Under the conjugation action g⋅x=gxg−1, a singleton orbit of a group action means that gxg−1=x for every g. This is equivalent to gx=xg for every g, so precisely these elements constitute the centre of a group
Z(G)={x∈G:xg=gx for all g∈G}.
(38)
The identity is central, and if x,y commute with every element then so do xy and x−1. Thus Z(G) is a subgroup. Each central element is fixed under every conjugation, giving gZ(G)g−1=Z(G): the centre is a normal subgroup.

By Lagrange's theorem, a stabilizer subgroup of G has order pj for some 0≤j≤n. The orbit-stabilizer theorem therefore gives
∣Y∣=pn−j.
(39)
Thus an orbit of a group action either has one element or has size divisible by p, even if the acted-on set is infinite.
Apply this to the conjugation action. Its singleton orbits of a group action are the elements of the centre of a group, while every other orbit has size divisible by p. Partitioning the finite group into these orbits gives the class equation, and hence
∣Z(G)∣≡∣G∣≡0(modp).

∣Z(G)∣≥p>1.​

A simple group has no normal subgroup except the identity subgroup and the whole group. To prove the Simplicity of the alternating group A5, we compute its conjugacy classes. Since the sign homomorphism is onto, ∣A5​∣=5!/2=60. Its possible nonidentity cycle types are a three-cycle, a product of two disjoint transpositions, and a five-cycle.
There are (35​)⋅2=20 three-cycles. The centralizer in S5​ of (1 2 3) consists of its three powers and an independent interchange of the two unused letters. Only the three powers are even. Its centralizer in A5​ therefore has order three, giving one conjugacy class of size 60/3=20 by the orbit-stabilizer theorem.
There are 5⋅3=15 products of two disjoint transpositions: choose the fixed letter and then a pairing of the other four letters. For (1 2)(3 4), a commuting permutation fixes 5 and may interchange the two pairs and swap within them, giving a centralizer of order eight in S5​. It contains an odd element, (1 2), so exactly half its elements are even. The centralizer in A5​ has order four, and these fifteen elements form one conjugacy class of size 60/4=15.
Finally, there are 4!=24 five-cycles. A permutation commuting with a five-cycle is determined by the image of one letter and must be a power of that cycle. All five powers are even, so the centralizer in A5​ has order five. Each such conjugacy class has size 60/5=12, giving two classes of size twelve.
A normal subgroup must be a union of conjugacy classes and must include the identity. Its order therefore has the form
1+20a+15b+12c,a,b∈{0,1},c∈{0,1,2}.
(46)
The resulting distinct possibilities are
1,13,16,21,25,28,33,36,40,45,48,60.
(47)
Of these, only 1 and 60 divide 60. Lagrange's theorem rules out every other possible order. Hence
A5​ is simple.​
(48)

(69)

(70)
The inward normal on the inner boundary is essential to the positive sign in this result.
