The intermediate value theorem states that if f:[a,b]→R is continuous, then every real value between f(a) and f(b) is attained at some c∈[a,b]. Here is a proof using completeness of the real numbers. Endpoint values are immediate, so suppose f(a)<y<f(b); reversing the sign of f handles the other ordering. Let
S={x∈[a,b]:f(x)<y},c=supS.
(43)
The set is nonempty and bounded above. Continuity at a puts points to the right of a in S, while continuity at b excludes an entire interval to the left of b, so a<c<b. If f(c)<y, continuity puts some point larger than c in S, contradicting its supremum. If f(c)>y, continuity excludes S in a neighborhood of c, contradicting the existence of points of S arbitrarily close to its supremum from below. Therefore f(c)=y, proving the theorem.
For the fixed-point assertion, put g(x)=f(x)−x. This is continuous, with g(0)=f(0)≥0 and g(1)=f(1)−1≤0. If either endpoint value is zero it already gives a fixed point; otherwise the intermediate value theorem gives an interior zero. Hence
Every continuous self-map of [0,1] has a fixed point.​
(44)
