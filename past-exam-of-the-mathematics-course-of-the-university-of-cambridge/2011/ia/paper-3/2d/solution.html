Lagrange's theorem says that for a finite group G and a subgroup H,
∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣.
(4)
In particular, ∣H∣ divides ∣G∣. To prove it, consider the left cosets gH. Every element lies in one, and if gH and g′H intersect, then gh=g′h′ for some h,h′∈H. Hence g=g′h′h−1 and gH=g′H. Distinct cosets are therefore disjoint and partition G. Multiplication by g is a bijection H→gH, so every coset has ∣H∣ elements. Counting this partition proves the formula.
Write the dihedral group as
D2n​=⟨r,s:rn=s2=e, srs=r−1⟩,
(5)
where r is a rotation and s a reflection. If k∣n, the cyclic subgroup ⟨rn/k⟩ has order k. If k∣2n but k∤n, then k is even: an odd divisor of 2n would divide n. Put m=k/2, so m∣n. The subgroup
⟨rn/m,s⟩​
(6)
contains exactly the m rotations rjn/m and the m reflections rjn/ms, for 0≤j<m. The inversion relation makes this set closed under multiplication and inverses. These 2m=k elements are distinct, so it has the required order. This proves the dihedral subgroups of every divisor order property. The construction works for every even divisor, including those already covered by the cyclic construction.
