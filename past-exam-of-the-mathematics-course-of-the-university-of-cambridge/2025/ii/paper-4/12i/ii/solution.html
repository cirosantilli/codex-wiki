For n≥0, put
Sn​={x∈R:F(n)(x)=0},En​=intSn​.
(120)
Each Sn​ is closed, the hypothesis gives R=⋃n​Sn​, and En​⊂En+1​ because a function that vanishes on an open set has derivative zero there.
The open set
Ω=⋃n≥0​En​
(121)
is dense. Indeed, inside any nonempty open interval choose a nondegenerate closed subinterval J. The complete metric space J is covered by the closed sets J∩Sn​, so the Baire's theorem makes one of them contain a relative open interval, which lies in some En​.
On each connected component I of Ω, the function F is one polynomial. To see this, any compact subinterval K⊂I is covered by the increasing family (En​). A finite subcover therefore gives K⊂EN​ for some N, and F(N)=0 on K. Hence F is polynomial on K; overlapping compact intervals force these polynomials to agree throughout I.
Let D=R∖Ω. This closed set has no isolated points. Otherwise, on the two sides of an isolated point x, F would equal polynomials. Smoothness makes all their one-sided derivatives agree at x, so the two polynomials are identical and extend across x. Some derivative would then vanish on a neighborhood of x, contrary to x∈D.
Suppose that D is nonempty. It is complete and
D=⋃n≥0​(D∩Sn​)
(122)
is a countable closed cover. A second application of Baire's theorem gives an index k and an open interval U0​ such that
∅=D∩U0​⊂Sk​.
(123)
Choose x0​∈D∩U0​ and a bounded open interval U centred at x0​ whose closure lies in U0​.
Because D has no isolated points, every x∈D∩U is approached by distinct points of D∩U. Difference quotients first give F(k+1)(x)=0, and induction gives
F(m)(x)=0(m≥k,x∈D∩U).
(124)
No component of Ω meeting U can cross x0​, so it has an endpoint in D∩U. If its polynomial had degree d≥k, then its dth derivative would approach a nonzero constant at that endpoint, contradicting the preceding display. Its degree is therefore less than k, so F(k) also vanishes on the part of that component in U. We conclude that F(k)=0 throughout U, which says U⊂Ek​⊂Ω and contradicts D∩U=∅.
Thus D is empty. The whole line is the single component of Ω, and the argument above shows that F is one polynomial on R. This is the smooth function with a pointwise vanishing derivative theorem.
