Let 0≤λ≤1 and put P=λP1​+(1−λ)P2​ and Q=λQ1​+(1−λ)Q2​. For each alphabet symbol x, apply the log-sum inequality to a1​=λP1​(x), a2​=(1−λ)P2​(x) and the corresponding b values. Summing over x gives

The preceding inequality supplies the upper bound on the supremum. If P has full support relative to Q, choose g(x)=log(P(x)/Q(x)); then EQ​eg=1 and the objective equals De​(P∥Q). If P(x)=0 at some symbols, use this choice on the support of P and put g(x)=−M elsewhere. Letting M→∞ gives the same value. Finiteness of D(P∥Q) guarantees that Q is positive on the support of P. This proves the Gibbs variational principle for relative entropy.

Let X1​,X2​,… be i.i.d. with mass function Q on a finite alphabet, and let Pn​ be their empirical distribution. Sanov theorem states that for every set Γ of probability mass functions,
−infR∈Γ∘​De​(R∥Q)≤liminfn→∞​n1​logP(Pn​∈Γ)
(5)
and
limsupn→∞​n1​logP(Pn​∈Γ)≤−infR∈Γ​De​(R∥Q),
(6)
where interior and closure use the probability-simplex topology. Thus the empirical distributions satisfy a large deviation principle with rate function De​(⋅∥Q).

The likelihood-ratio event depends only on the type R=Pn​ and is
∑x​R(x)logQ(x)P(x)​≥0,
(7)
equivalently D(R∥Q)≥D(R∥P). This constraint defines a closed subset of the finite probability simplex, so Sanov's upper bound gives the exponent
C(P,Q)=infR:D(R∥Q)≥D(R∥P)​De​(R∥Q).
(8)
It is strictly positive: the only distribution with zero divergence from Q is R=Q, but Q violates the constraint because 0=D(Q∥Q)<D(Q∥P). Compactness and continuity under full support keep the infimum away from zero. This exponent is the Chernoff information between P and Q.

For feasible mass functions Q1​,Q2​ at distortion levels d1​,d2​, the mixture Q=λQ1​+(1−λ)Q2​ has nonzero-symbol mass at most λd1​+(1−λ)d2​. Concavity of information entropy gives
H(Q)≥λH(Q1​)+(1−λ)H(Q2​).
(11)
Taking maximizing sequences proves
ϕ(λd1​+(1−λ)d2​)≥λϕ(d1​)+(1−λ)ϕ(d2​).
(12)

Regard the alphabet as the cyclic group Z/mZ and put E=X−Y. For each y, subtraction by y is a bijection, so
H(X∣Y=y)=H(E∣Y=y)≤ϕ(dy​),dy​=P(X=Y∣Y=y).

The previous part gives R(d)≥logm−ϕ(d) for uniform X. To attain the bound, take Y uniform on Z/mZ, choose an independent error E whose mass function attains ϕ(d), and set X=Y+E. Then X is uniform, P(X=Y)=P(E=0)≤d, and

The direct codes-distributions correspondence says that every mass function Q on a finite alphabet admits a binary prefix code with lengths L(x)=⌈−log2​Q(x)⌉. Conversely, every binary prefix code has lengths satisfying the Kraft inequality K=∑x​2−L(x)≤1, and hence defines the mass function QC​(x)=2−L(x)/K.

For any fixed threshold R, minimizing the excess-length probability means assigning the available strings shorter than R to the most probable symbols. Repeating this exchange argument simultaneously for every threshold orders symbols by decreasing probability and assigns binary strings from shortest to longest. There are 2ℓ words of length ℓ, so the kth word in shortlex order has length
L∗(xk​)=⌊log2​k⌋.
(19)
This is the optimal one-to-one binary code.

Relabeling an arbitrary alphabet in decreasing probability order makes part (ii) pointwise:
L∗(x)≤−log2​P(x).
(21)
It immediately implies
P(L∗(X)≥R)≤P(−log2​P(X)≥R)
(22)
and, after taking expectations,
EL∗(X)≤H(X).
(23)
This reverses the prefix-code lower bound from part (b). A general one-to-one code need not decode concatenated codewords instantaneously or uniquely, so it is not constrained by Kraft's inequality.
