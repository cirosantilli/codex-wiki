The phase screen adds the phase accumulated across its thickness, so the reduced field just after the screen is
E(0,z)=eikξw(z).
(1)
For a zero-mean random variable w with a normal distribution and variance σ2, its characteristic function gives
⟨E(0,z)⟩=exp(−21​k2ξ2σ2).​
(2)
The same expression is approximately valid for a non-Gaussian weak fluctuation: the cumulant expansion begins with −k2ξ2σ2/2, while higher cumulants give higher-order corrections.
For x>0, every realization obeys the parabolic wave equation
2ikEx​+Ezz​=0.
(3)
Linearity permits ensemble averaging, so
2ik∂x​⟨E⟩+∂z2​⟨E⟩=0.​
(4)
The initial mean is independent of z, hence diffraction does not change it and ⟨E(x,z)⟩=exp(−k2ξ2σ2/2) for every x≥0.

The inverse problem is ill posed for several related reasons. At one frequency and one incident direction, the data sample V only on the two-dimensional Ewald surface q=k0​(r−x0​), so three-dimensional reconstruction is nonunique without more illuminations or prior information. Finite aperture and a bounded frequency band omit additional Fourier components and limit resolution. The forward map is compact, so its singular values tend to zero and inversion strongly amplifies measurement noise. Finally, the Born approximation neglects multiple scattering; model error becomes significant when the contrast or support is too large, while uncertainty in the incident field, measurement geometry, and domain D creates further instability.

A regularization of an inverse problem consists of bounded operators Rα​:Y→X and a parameter rule α=α(δ,yδ) such that, whenever ∥yδ−y∥≤δ and y lies in the domain of A†,
α(δ,yδ)→0,Rα(δ,yδ)​yδ→A†y
(23)
as δ→0. It is needed because a compact operator on an infinite-dimensional space has singular values tending to zero, so direct inversion divides noisy data by arbitrarily small numbers and is generally discontinuous.
Tikhonov regularization defines
xαδ​=x∈Xargmin​(∥Ax−yδ∥2+α∥x∥2),
(24)
and its normal equation gives
xαδ​=(A∗A+αI)−1A∗yδ.​
(25)
For every fixed α>0, A∗A+αI is bounded below by αI, and the data-to-solution operator is bounded. Small changes in yδ therefore produce small changes in xαδ​.
