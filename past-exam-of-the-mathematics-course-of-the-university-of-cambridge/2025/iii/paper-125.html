The Hasse theorem for elliptic curves states that
∣#E(Fp​)−(p+1)∣≤2p​.
(1)
Let π be the Frobenius isogeny of an elliptic curve and put a=p+1−#E(Fp​). The degree on End(E) is a positive-definite quadratic form, its associated bilinear form gives tr(π)=a, and degπ=p. Consequently
deg([m]+[n]π)=m2+amn+pn2
(2)
for all integers m,n. If a2−4p>0, this real quadratic form is indefinite, so by density of rational slopes it is negative at some nonzero integer pair (m,n), contradicting nonnegativity of the degree. Hence a2≤4p, which is the claimed bound.

Equality of the two point groups implies equality of their orders. Since α2+β2=a2−2p,
p+1−a=p2+1−a2+2p,
(6)
so
a2−a=p(p+1).
(7)
Its two integral solutions are a=p+1 and a=−p. The Hasse theorem for elliptic curves excludes the first for every prime and permits the second only when p≤4. Thus p=2 or p=3.
Both occur. Over F2​, the smooth curve y2+y=x3+x has five rational points and trace −2. Over F3​, the smooth curve y2=x3−x+1 has seven rational points and trace −3. In either case the point-count formula gives #E(Fp2​)=#E(Fp​). Since E(Fp​)⊆E(Fp2​), equal orders give equality of groups.

A one-dimensional commutative formal group law over a ring R is a series F(X,Y)∈R[[X,Y]] satisfying
F(X,0)=X,F(X,Y)=F(Y,X),F(F(X,Y),Z)=F(X,F(Y,Z)).
(8)
An isomorphism from F to G is a series h(T)=uT+O(T2) with u∈R∗ and
h(F(X,Y))=G(h(X),h(Y)).
(9)
Over a characteristic-zero field K, every such formal group is isomorphic to the additive formal group. Differentiate the associativity identity and define the invariant differential
ωF​(T)=(∂Y∂F​(T,0))−1dT.
(10)
Termwise integration is possible in characteristic zero; the formal logarithm
logF​(T)=∫0T​ωF​
(11)
has leading term T. Invariance of ωF​ gives
dlogF​(F(X,Y))=dlogF​(X)+dlogF​(Y),
(12)
and evaluation at (0,0) removes the integration constant. Hence logF​(F(X,Y))=logF​(X)+logF​(Y). Its unit linear coefficient gives a compositional inverse, so it is an isomorphism to X+Y. Therefore any two one-dimensional commutative formal groups over K are isomorphic.

Apply the Riemann-Roch theorem to the divisors nO. Since the genus is one and the canonical divisor is trivial, ℓ(nO)=n for n≥1. Choose
x∈L(2O)∖L(O),y∈L(3O)∖L(2O).
(18)
Then x and y have exact pole orders two and three at O. The seven functions
1,x,y,x2,xy,x3,y2
(19)
lie in the six-dimensional space L(6O), so they satisfy one relation. Comparing pole orders and completing squares and cubes gives a nonsingular Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve
y2+a1​xy+a3​y=x3+a2​x2+a4​x+a6​.
(20)
The functions 1,x,y define the morphism P↦(x(P):y(P):1) away from O. Their pole orders show that it extends with O↦(0:1:0). It has degree one and is therefore an isomorphism of smooth projective curves.

The Weil pairing e3​:C[3]×C[3]→μ3​ is nondegenerate and Galois equivariant. If all of C[3] is rational over a field, pairing a basis produces a primitive cube root of unity in that field. Thus a quadratic field with full 3-torsion must contain Q(ζ3​) and must equal it.
More generally, if E(Fp​)[ℓ]≅(Z/ℓZ)2, Frobenius acts as the identity on E[ℓ]. Its characteristic polynomial X2−aX+p is therefore congruent to (X−1)2 modulo ℓ. Comparing coefficients gives
a≡2(modℓ),p≡1(modℓ).
(23)
In particular #E(Fp​)=1+p−a with a≡2(modℓ).

Writing X=u, Y=v, and Z=−w turns the cubic into X3+Y3=Z3. The birational coordinates
x=X+Y12Z​,y=X+Y36(X−Y)​
(24)
give the Weierstrass equation
y2=x3−432.
(25)
The original projective cubic has no common zero of its three partial derivatives modulo any p=3, so it is already a smooth proper model and C has good reduction at every such prime.
If p≡1(mod3), then μ3​⊂Fp​, all nine inflection points are rational, and C(Fp​) contains (Z/3Z)2, so it is not cyclic. If p≡2(mod3), cubing is a bijection on Fp​. Counting on the Fermat model gives #C(Fp​)=p+1. A finite elliptic-curve group has the form Z/dZ×Z/eZ with d∣e and d∣p−1, so d∣gcd(p−1,p+1)=2. For odd p, the equation x3=432 has exactly one root because cubing is bijective, so there is only one nonzero rational 2-torsion point and d=2. Hence the group is cyclic. For p=2 it has order three and is cyclic as well.

For [x]∈Δ, choose αx​ with αxn​=x and define the Kummer pairing
⟨σ,[x]⟩=αx​σ(αx​)​∈μn​.
(26)
Changing x by an nth power or changing its root leaves the quotient unchanged because μn​⊂K. Multiplication of radicals proves bilinearity. If an automorphism pairs trivially with every class, it fixes all generating radicals and hence all of L. If a class pairs trivially with every automorphism, its radical lies in K, so the class is trivial. Thus the pairing is well-defined, bilinear, and nondegenerate on both sides.

For [P]∈Γ, choose Q with nQ=P and define the elliptic Kummer pairing
⟨σ,[P]⟩=σ(Q)−Q∈E[n].
(27)
Replacing Q by Q+T with T∈E[n] changes nothing because all n-torsion is K-rational; replacing P by P+nR permits replacing Q by Q+R, again without changing the value. The group laws prove bilinearity. An automorphism pairing trivially with every class fixes every division point and hence L. Conversely, if [P] pairs trivially with every automorphism, a chosen Q is Galois fixed, so Q∈E(K) and P=nQ. The pairing is therefore nondegenerate.

K(S,n)={[x]∈K∗/(K∗)n:vp​(x)≡0(modn) for p∈/S}.

Take S to contain every finite prime dividing n and every prime of bad reduction of E. The local theory of reduction of an elliptic curve shows that Kummer classes of rational points are unramified outside S. Choose a basis of the constant group E[n]≅(Z/nZ)2. Kummer theory and the Weil pairing identify the resulting two scalar coordinates of
E(K)/nE(K)↪H1(K,E[n])

∣Γ∣≤∣K(S,n)∣2.​

For E:y2=x3+ax2+bx with rational 2-torsion (0,0), the quotient by that point is the two-isogenous curve
E′:y2=x3−2ax2+(a2−4b)x.
(33)
The two-isogeny descent maps a nonexceptional point to the square class of its x-coordinate, with (0,0) mapping to [b]. The images are finite collections of squarefree divisors of b and a2−4b, determined by testing the associated homogeneous quartics for rational points. If their orders are 2s and 2s′, then
2r+2=2s2s′,
(34)
which determines the Mordell-Weil rank r.
The method requires a rational 2-isogeny, and deciding whether every locally soluble quartic is globally soluble can be difficult. Computing only local conditions gives a 2-isogeny Selmer group and hence an upper bound; a nontrivial Tate-Shafarevich group can make that bound strict. Even after finding the rank, a separate saturation and point search may be needed to find generators.

For this curve, the square-class image in the first two-isogeny descent is contained in {1,−1,11,−11}. All four classes occur: Q=(−1,1) gives −1, T=(0,0) gives 11, and Q+T gives −11. The isogenous curve is
E′:y2=x3−26x2+125x.
(39)
Its image is contained in {1,−1,5,−5}. Negative x cannot occur because x(x2−26x+125)<0 for x<0, while 1 and 5 are represented by the identity and (0,0). Thus the two image orders are four and two, and
2r+2=4⋅2
(40)
gives r=1.
The point P has order three because 2P=−P, and T has order two, so the rational torsion contains a cyclic subgroup of order six. At the good primes 7 and 13, direct point counting gives
#E(F7​)=12,#E(F13​)=18.
(41)
Reduction bounds the rational torsion order by their greatest common divisor, namely six, so this is all the torsion. The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain now gives
E(Q)≅Z/6Z×Z.
(42)
Thus one may take d1​=1, d2​=6, and r=1.
