Every positive integer m has a unique binomial representation
m=(rar​​)+(r−1ar−1​​)+⋯+(sas​​),ar​>ar−1​>⋯>as​≥s≥1.
(1)
Define
fr​(m)=(r−1ar​​)+(r−2ar−1​​)+⋯+(s−1as​​).
(2)
The Kruskal-Katona theorem says that every r-uniform family A of size m has
∣∂A∣≥fr​(m),
(3)
and the initial segment of length m in colexicographic order attains equality.
We prove the inequality by simultaneous mathematical induction on the ground-set size and on r. We use the elementary binomial-shadow arithmetic lemma
fr​(a+b)≤max{fr​(a),b}+fr−1​(b).
(4)
To verify the lemma, greedily remove the largest term (rq​) from the binomial representation of a+b. If a reaches that term first, apply the induction hypothesis to the remainders; otherwise transfer the excess to b. In the crossing case Pascal's identity
(rq​)=(rq−1​)+(r−1q−1​)
(5)
gives exactly the two terms on the right. This induction also proves uniqueness of the greedy representation.
Choose the largest ground-set element x and split
A0​={A∈A:x∈/A},A1​={A∖{x}:x∈A}.
(6)
The members of the lower shadow that avoid x form ∂A0​∪A1​, while those containing x are the sets B∪{x} with B∈∂A1​. Hence, writing a=∣A0​∣ and b=∣A1​∣,
∣∂A∣=∣∂A0​∪A1​∣+∣∂A1​∣≥max{fr​(a),b}+fr−1​(b)≥fr​(a+b).
(7)
This completes the induction.
Finally, the initial colex segment first contains all r-subsets of [ar​], followed recursively by sets containing ar​+1 whose remaining elements form the appropriate initial segment at level r−1. Its shadow has the same recursive decomposition, and therefore has exactly fr​(m) members. This proves sharpness.

Let c(A)=[n]∖A and reverse the order of the ground set by j↦n+1−j. The resulting bijection sends lexicographic order on [n](r) to colexicographic order on [n](n−r). It also sends the iterated upper shadow of an r-uniform family at level r+1 to the lower shadow of the corresponding (n−r)-uniform family, up to the same harmless reversal of the ground set.
The Kruskal-Katona theorem says that this lower shadow is smallest for an initial colex segment. Undoing the complement and reversal therefore says that the upper shadow of a family of fixed size in [n](r) is smallest for the initial lex segment.

The Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem says that if n≥2r and A⊆[n](r) is an intersecting family, then
∣A∣≤(r−1n−1​).
(9)
The star of all r-sets containing one fixed point attains equality.
For the shadow proof, let ∇n−r​A be the iterated upper shadow at level n−r, and let
Ac={[n]∖A:A∈A}.
(10)
These two families are disjoint: if A⊆[n]∖B, then A and B are disjoint. The upper-shadow form of the Kruskal-Katona theorem says that if
∣A∣>(r−1n−1​),
(11)
then
∣∇n−r​A∣>(rn−1​).
(12)
But ∣Ac∣=∣A∣, so disjointness and Pascal's identity would give more than
(rn−1​)+(r−1n−1​)=(rn​)
(13)
members at level n−r, a contradiction.
For the Katona circle method, place [n] in a cyclic order. At most r of its cyclic intervals of length r can belong to an intersecting family. Indeed, after fixing one selected interval, every selected interval starts at one of the 2r−1 positions at cyclic distance below r from its start; apart from the fixed interval, these positions form r−1 pairs whose corresponding intervals are disjoint. Double-count pairs consisting of A∈A and a cyclic order in which A is consecutive. There are (n−1)! cyclic orders, at most r selected intervals in each, and each A is consecutive in r!(n−r)! cyclic orders. Therefore
∣A∣r!(n−r)!≤r(n−1)!,
(14)
which rearranges to the required bound.

For A in the n-dimensional Boolean hypercube, the edge-isoperimetric inequality in the discrete cube is
e(A)≤2∣A∣log2​∣A∣​,
(24)
where e(A) is the number of edges spanned by A. Since the cube is n-regular, the equivalent boundary form is
∣∂e​A∣=n∣A∣−2e(A)≥∣A∣log2​∣A∣2n​.
(25)
We prove the induced-edge form by induction on n. Split the cube according to its last coordinate, and let the two sections have sizes a≥b. At most b edges of A cross between the sections. The induction hypothesis gives
e(A)≤21​alog2​a+21​blog2​b+b.
(26)
Put x=b/(a+b)≤1/2. The required comparison with 21​(a+b)log2​(a+b) is equivalent to
H2​(x)≥2x,
(27)
where H2​ is the binary entropy function. This follows from concavity because the graph of H2​ lies above the chord joining (0,0) to (1/2,1). The induction is complete. Subcubes attain equality when ∣A∣ is a power of two.

Apply coordinate compression in a product of paths in both coordinates. Replacing every fiber by an initial interval preserves the number of edges inside that fiber; between two neighboring fibers, the compressed sets span the minimum of their sizes, at least their original intersection size. Repeating the operation therefore never decreases the number of edges spanned and eventually produces a down-set.
Write the nonzero row lengths of this down-set as
a1​≥a2​≥⋯≥aq​>0,∑i=1q​ai​=t2.
(29)
The horizontal edges number t2−q, and the vertical edges number
∑i=2q​ai​=t2−a1​.
(30)
Consequently
e(A)≤2t2−(q+a1​).
(31)
Since t2≤qa1​, the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality gives q+a1​≥2t. Hence
e(A)≤2t2−2t=2t(t−1)=e([t]2),
(32)
as required.

The Borsuk conjecture asserted that every bounded subset of Rd of positive diameter can be partitioned into d+1 subsets of strictly smaller diameter. We construct a Kahn-Kalai counterexample to the Borsuk conjecture.
For every 2p-subset A of [4p], let vA​∈{−1,1}4p be 1 on A and −1 outside it, and define
xA​=((vA​)i​(vA​)j​)1≤i<j≤4p​∈Rd,d=(24p​).
(38)
Because xA​=xAc​, retain one representative of each complementary pair. The resulting set X has 21​(2p4p​) points. For s=∣A∩B∣,
vA​⋅vB​=4(s−p),
(39)
and
xA​⋅xB​=2(vA​⋅vB​)2−4p​.
(40)
All xA​ have the same norm, so their distance is largest exactly when this inner product is smallest, namely when s=p.
Every smaller-diameter part of X therefore corresponds to a family with no pair having intersection p. Part ii bounds such a part by 2(p−14p​). Any smaller-diameter partition consequently needs at least
4(p−14p​)(2p4p​)​
(41)
parts. By Stirling formula, this ratio grows like (27/16)p up to a polynomial factor, whereas d+1=O(p2). For every sufficiently large prime p, the required number of parts exceeds d+1, disproving the conjecture.

Let D>0 be the diameter of the bounded set S, and choose x0​∈S. Then S⊆B(x0​,D). It is enough to prove a volumetric covering bound for the unit ball.
Choose a maximal 1/4-separated set N in B(0,1). The balls of radius 1/8 centred at points of N are disjoint and lie in B(0,9/8). Comparing volumes gives
∣N∣(81​)n≤(89​)n,∣N∣≤9n.
(42)
Maximality means that the balls of radius 1/4 centred at N cover the unit ball.
After translating and scaling, at most 9n balls of radius D/4 cover S. Assign each point of S to one covering ball containing it. This gives at most 9n disjoint pieces, each of diameter at most D/2<D. Thus the claim holds with the absolute constant C=9.
