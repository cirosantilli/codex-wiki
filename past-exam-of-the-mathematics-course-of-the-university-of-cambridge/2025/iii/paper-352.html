In small-amplitude oscillatory shear, expand the measured shear stress as
τ=ϵasin(ωt+δ)=ϵ(acosδsinωt+asinδcosωt).
(1)
Comparison with the definition
τ=ϵ(G′sinωt+G′′cosωt)
(2)
of the storage modulus and loss modulus gives
G′=acosδ,G′′=asinδ​,
(3)
and hence
a=(G′)2+(G′′)2​,tanδ=G′G′′​​.
(4)
Thus the measured phase directly gives the loss tangent.
At low angular frequency, a standard viscoelastic fluid has time to relax and responds mainly as a viscous liquid: stress is approximately in phase with strain rate, so δ→π/2 as ω→0. At high frequency it cannot relax during one cycle and responds mainly as an elastic solid, so stress is approximately in phase with strain and δ→0 as ω→∞.
For a material dominated by one viscoelastic relaxation time λ, the crossover between these regimes occurs when ωλ is of order one. Measure the frequency ωc​ at which G′=G′′, equivalently δ=π/4, and estimate
λ≃ωc−1​​.
(5)
A broad or multi-peaked crossover instead indicates a spectrum of relaxation times.

In a parallel-plate rheometer, a point at radius r on the upper plate moves at speed Ωr. The thin-gap approximation therefore gives the local shear rate
γ˙​(r)=hΩr​.
(6)
Define the rim value
γ˙​R​=hΩR​,γ˙​(r)=γ˙​R​Rr​.
(7)
An annulus of radius r and width dr has area 2πrdr; its tangential force is τ(r)2πrdr, and its moment arm is r. The measured torque is consequently
T=2π∫0R​τ(γ˙​(r))r2dr.
(8)
For a generalized Newtonian fluid, τ=η(γ˙​)γ˙​. Changing the integration variable from r to γ˙​ gives
T=γ˙​R3​2πR3​∫0γ˙​R​​η(γ˙​)γ˙​3dγ˙​​.

dγ˙​R​d​(Tγ˙​R3​)=2πR3η(γ˙​R​)γ˙​R3​.

(13)

η(γ˙​)=2πR3γ˙​3Tc​​+παR32​​.

τ=ηγ˙​=2πR33Tc​​+παR32​γ˙​,
(15)
so the inferred material is a Bingham plastic with yield stress 3Tc​/(2πR3).

At the final state of the slump test for yield stress, an axisymmetric deposit of height h(r) is just able to support itself. Hydrostatic pressure gives radial pressure gradient ρgh′(r), while lubrication theory makes the magnitude of the basal shear stress
∣τb​∣=ρgh∣h′∣.
(39)
The marginally yielded final profile therefore satisfies
ρgh(−h′)=τy​,h(R)=0.
(40)
Integration gives
h(r)=[ρg2τy​​(R−r)]1/2​.
(41)
Using mass conservation, the known volume is
V=2π∫0R​rh(r)dr=158π​(ρg2τy​​)1/2R5/2.
(42)
Solving for the yield stress produces the estimate
τy​=128π2R5225ρgV2​​.
(43)
Thus one measures the final radius R and inserts it with V and ρ. The estimate assumes a thin deposit, negligible surface tension, complete initial yielding, and a spatially uniform yield stress.
Dry sand is a granular material governed primarily by frictional stability. Its final free surface reaches the angle of repose θr​, so the deposit is approximately a cone,
h(r)=(R−r)tanθr​​.
(44)
This constant-slope profile differs from the square-root edge of the yield-stress-fluid model.

Let w(x) be the downward velocity between the vertical plates. A steady vertical momentum balance gives
dxdτxz​​=−ρg.
(45)
Symmetry requires τxz​(0)=0, so the stress magnitude is
∣τxz​(x)∣=ρg∣x∣.
(46)
The wall stress first reaches the yield stress when ρglc​=τy​. The onset measurement therefore gives
τy​=ρglc​​.
(47)
For l>lc​, define Δ=l−lc​. The central region ∣x∣≤lc​ is a plug flow of a yield-stress fluid, while the layers lc​<∣x∣≤l are yielded. The Herschel–Bulkley fluid law gives, for x≥lc​,
∣w′(x)∣=[Kρg(x−lc​)​]1/n.
(48)
Integrating from the no-slip wall w(l)=0 gives
w(x)=(Kρg​)1/nn+1n​[Δ1+1/n−(x−lc​)1+1/n]
(49)
in the yielded layer, and the plug moves at
wp​=(Kρg​)1/nn+1n​Δ1+1/n.
(50)
The volumetric flow rate per unit span is consequently
Q=2(Kρg​)1/n[2n+1n​Δ2+1/n+n+1nlc​​Δ1+1/n]​.
(51)
Near onset, the plug term dominates and
Q∼(l−lc​)1+1/n.
(52)
The observed exponent two therefore corresponds to n=1, a linear post-yield constitutive law at small shear rate. Far above onset, the fully yielded contribution scales as
Q∼l2+1/n.
(53)
The observed exponent four corresponds to n=1/2, a shear-thinning square-root law at high shear rate.
A plausible stress curve is therefore odd in γ˙​, starts at the two yield-stress values τ=±τy​, and has finite linear slope just after yield:
τ−τy​∼K1​γ˙​(γ˙​>0 small).
(54)
It then crosses smoothly to the concave high-rate behavior
τ−τy​∼K2​γ˙​1/2(γ˙​>0 large),
(55)
with the negative-rate branch fixed by odd symmetry. This combines the two power laws inferred independently from the measured flux limits.
