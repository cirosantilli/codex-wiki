Let u=(u,v) and let the layer thickness be H=H0​+η. The inviscid shallow water equations are
DtDu​+fz×u=−g∇(η−hb​),Ht​+∇⋅(Hu)=0.
(1)
They follow from a homogeneous incompressible fluid with small aspect ratio, hydrostatic pressure, negligible vertical acceleration, horizontal velocity nearly uniform through the depth, a material free surface, and a rigid stationary bottom. Rotation may be represented by an f-plane or beta plane. The model is useful because many atmospheric and oceanic motions are horizontally much broader than their depth, while the free surface or an internal density interface still supports waves and potential vorticity dynamics.
Let ζ=vx​−uy​. Taking the vertical curl of momentum gives
DtD(ζ+f)​=−(ζ+f)∇⋅u,
(2)
where Df/Dt=βv supplies the planetary-vorticity term when f varies. Continuity gives DH/Dt=−H∇⋅u. Combining the two equations yields material conservation of shallow-water potential vorticity:
q=Hζ+f​,DtDq​=0.​
(3)

Sverdrup balance applies to a steady, large-scale, small-Rossby number, hydrostatic and nearly geostrophic ocean interior on a beta plane. The flow is depth-integrated, relative-vorticity advection and interior friction are negligible, density is treated as constant for the barotropic balance, and the principal vorticity source is the curl of wind stress. The balance
βv=W​
(11)
says that wind input of vertical vorticity is balanced by meridional advection of planetary vorticity, or equivalently by the stretching needed to conserve potential vorticity as parcels move across latitude circles.

Use u=−ψy​, v=ψx​. Since v=W(y)/β and the eastern boundary may be chosen as the zero streamline,
ψ(x,y)=βx−LX​​W(y)=−βx−LX​​sinLY​2πy​.​
(12)
The lower half-basin has southward interior flow and a northward western return current, giving a clockwise ocean gyre. The upper half has northward interior flow and a southward western return current, giving a counterclockwise gyre. The interior solution cannot satisfy no slip at every wall, so viscous layers complete the circulation.
With constant lateral viscosity ν, the vorticity equation contains ν∇4ψ. At the western and eastern walls, balancing βψx​ against four x derivatives gives the Munk boundary layer scale
δW​∼δE​∼(ν/β)1/3.​
(13)
At the southern and northern walls, x variation remains on the basin scale LX​ while four y derivatives occur across the layer, so
δS​∼δN​∼(νLX​/β)1/4.​
(14)

For both y>0 and y<0, the velocity v=−W0​y/(2f0​) carries parcels toward y=0, where H=y2 decreases. Their leading potential vorticity f0​/H therefore increases along the path, exactly as required by the positive source W0​/H. The explicit interior velocity has zero relative vorticity, so the leading change comes from vortex-column stretching rather than from ζ/H. Relative vorticity and friction become important only where boundary layers close the circulation or where the singular depth profile near y=0 invalidates the approximation.

The quasi-geostrophic approximation requires small Rossby number, nearly horizontal geostrophic balance, hydrostatic vertical balance, small interface or density displacements, stable background stratification, and horizontal scales much larger than the vertical scale. The Boussinesq approximation and a beta plane are used, ageostrophic motion enters only at the order needed to evolve potential vorticity, and here the buoyancy frequency N is constant. Under these assumptions the materially conserved three-dimensional quasi-geostrophic potential vorticity is
q=∇h2​ψ+N2f02​​ψzz​+βy.​
(19)

For an equatorial Kelvin wave, set v=0. The zonal and meridional momentum equations give
u=ωk​ϕ​,dydϕ​​=−ωβk​yϕ​,
(32)
so
ϕ​=ϕ0​exp(−2ωβk​y2).
(33)
Hydrostatic balance and buoyancy evolution give σ=imϕ​ and w=−ωmϕ​/N2. Continuity then gives
ω2=m2N2k2​.
(34)
Because the mode must decay as ∣y∣→∞, one needs k/ω>0. With the stipulated ω>0, the acceptable branch is therefore
ω=∣m∣Nk​,k>0.​
(35)
The branch ω=−Nk/∣m∣ would require k<0 when ω>0, making the Gaussian exponent positive and the mode unbounded; it is not an equatorially trapped solution.
For real k,m,ω,
u′w′=21​Re(uw∗)=−21​N2km​∣ϕ​∣2.​
(36)
Upward group propagation has m<0, while a Kelvin wave has k>0, so u′w′>0. Dissipation makes this eastward momentum flux decrease with height; therefore −∂z​u′w′>0 and the wave exerts an eastward force on the mean flow.

Choose the phase so that the trapped meridional structure v(y) is real. The linear wave equations then make ϕ​ and u purely imaginary relative to v, so
u′v′=21​Re(uv∗)=0,F(y)=0.​
(37)
For every equatorial mode, zonal momentum, hydrostatic balance, and buoyancy evolution give
u=ωk​ϕ​+iωβy​v,σ=imϕ​,w=−N2ωm​ϕ​.
(38)
Substitution into the transformed flux shows that the terms involving vϕ​∗ cancel between momentum and buoyancy transport. Hence
F(z)=−u′w′+N2βy​v′σ′=21​N2km​∣ϕ​∣2.​
(39)
For upward-propagating waves m<0, so F(z) has the opposite sign to k. Where a wave dissipates, its flux tends toward zero with height: eastward modes with k>0 have F(z)<0 and exert an eastward force, while westward modes with k<0 have F(z)>0 and exert a westward force. Thus dissipation deposits each wave's signed zonal pseudomomentum in the stratospheric mean flow. The formula also shows why F(z) is a wave-activity or pseudomomentum flux rather than merely −u′w′: the eddy buoyancy transport supplies the correction required by the transformed circulation.
