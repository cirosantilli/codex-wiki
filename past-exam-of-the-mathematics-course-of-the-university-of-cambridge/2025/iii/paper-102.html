A Solvable Lie algebra is one whose derived series
g(0)=g,g(r+1)=[g(r),g(r)]
(5)
eventually vanishes. By the Lie theorem, the adjoint operators of a solvable complex Lie algebra are simultaneously upper triangular. If x∈[g,g], then adx​ is a sum of commutators of upper triangular matrices and is therefore strictly upper triangular. For every y∈g, the product adx​ady​ is strictly upper triangular, so
κ(x,y)=0.
(6)
Thus
[g,g]⊆g⊥​.
(7)
For a nonzero example, let g have basis h,e with [h,e]=e. It is solvable because [g,g]=Ce is abelian, but in the ordered basis (h,e),
adh​=(00​01​),κ(h,h)=1.
(8)

Invariance of the Killing form gives
κ([a,x],y)=κ(x,[y,a]).
(9)
If x∈g⊥, the right-hand side vanishes for every y, so [a,x]∈g⊥. Thus g⊥ is an ideal.
Let h=ad(g⊥)⊆gl(g). For x∈[g⊥,g⊥] and y∈g⊥,
tr(adx​ady​)=κ(x,y)=0.
(10)
The Cartan solvability criterion makes h solvable. The kernel of the adjoint map on g⊥ lies in its center and is abelian, so g⊥ is itself solvable. This proves the Solvability of the radical of the Killing form.

The Killing form of a complex Simple Lie algebra is nondegenerate. Since B is also nondegenerate, there is a unique endomorphism T of g satisfying
κ(x,y)=B(Tx,y).
(11)
Invariance of both forms gives
T([z,x])=[z,Tx],
(12)
so T intertwines the adjoint representation. That representation is irreducible because its invariant subspaces are ideals. The Schur lemma therefore gives T=cI. Since κ is nondegenerate, c=0, and
κ=cB​.
(13)
This is the uniqueness of an invariant bilinear form on a simple Lie algebra.

A Weyl chamber is a connected component of

Root bases correspond bijectively to Weyl chambers: the walls of a chamber determine its inward simple roots. The Weyl group acts transitively on the chambers. One proof joins interior points of two chambers by a generic line segment. Each time the segment crosses one reflecting hyperplane, reflect the remaining segment across that wall; the resulting product of root reflections sends the first chamber to the second. It consequently sends the first root basis to the second. Thus W acts transitively on root bases.

We induct on the Coxeter length ℓ(w). There is nothing to prove when ℓ(w)=0. Otherwise choose a simple root α such that
ℓ(wsα​)=ℓ(w)−1;
(20)
equivalently, wα is negative. Since λ and μ=wλ lie in the Closed dominant Weyl chamber,
0≤⟨λ,α∨⟩=⟨μ,(wα)∨⟩≤0.
(21)
Therefore ⟨λ,α∨⟩=0, and the simple reflection sα​ fixes λ. Moreover,
(wsα​)λ=wλ=μ.
(22)
The induction hypothesis writes wsα​ as a product of simple reflections that fix λ. Multiplying on the right by sα​ gives the required expression for w. This is the Weyl stabilizer of a dominant point lemma.

For existence, choose w∈W maximizing (wλ,ρ), where ρ lies in the interior of the dominant chamber. If ⟨wλ,α∨⟩<0 for a simple root α, then
(sα​wλ,ρ)−(wλ,ρ)=−⟨wλ,α∨⟩(α,ρ)>0,

Write the nonzero highest weight of V as
λ=∑j=1ℓ​mj​ωj​,mj​∈Z≥0​.
(34)
Choose i with mi​>0. Then λ−ωi​ is dominant. Every positive coroot is a nonnegative combination of simple coroots, so every numerator in the Weyl dimension formula for λ is at least the corresponding numerator for ωi​. Thus
dimV(λ)≥dimV(ωi​).

V≅V(ωi​)​,

The dominance order is
μ⪯λ⟺λ−μ=∑i​ni​αi​(ni​∈Z≥0​).
(51)
Suppose λ=μ. Put δ=λ−μ. Since μ is dominant,
(λ,δ)=(μ,δ)+(δ,δ)>0.
