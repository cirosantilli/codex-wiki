Suppose first that L/K is a totally ramified extension of degree n, and let α be a uniformizer of L. If the valuation on L is normalized by vL​(α)=1, then vL​(K×)=nZ. The value group of K(α) already contains both nZ and 1, so its ramification index over K is at least n. Hence [K(α):K]≥n, and therefore L=K(α).
Let
f(X)=Xn+an−1​Xn−1+⋯+a0​
(1)
be the minimal polynomial of α. Every conjugate of α has positive valuation, so each ai​ lies in the maximal ideal of OK​. Moreover
vL​(a0​)=vL​(NL/K​(α))=n,
(2)
which means vK​(a0​)=1. Thus f is an Eisenstein polynomial.
Conversely, if α is a root of an Eisenstein polynomial of degree n, the Eisenstein criterion makes that polynomial irreducible and its Newton polygon gives vL​(α)=1/n when vK​ is normalized. Consequently e(K(α)/K)≥n; equality with the field degree forces e=n and residue-field degree one. Thus K(α)/K is totally ramified.

The valuation ring and its maximal ideal are
OK​={x:v(x)≥0},m={x:v(x)>0}.
(15)
If OK​ is Noetherian, its maximal ideal is finitely generated. Ideals in a valuation ring are totally ordered, so every finitely generated ideal is generated by one of its generators; write m=(π). Then v(π) is the smallest positive element of the value group. Subtracting integral multiples of this value shows that every value is an integral multiple of v(π), so v is discrete.

One useful form of Hensel lemma is this: if R is a complete discrete valuation ring, f∈R[X], and
f(a1​)≡0(modπ),f′(a1​)≡0(modπ),
(19)
then there is a unique a∈R such that f(a)=0 and a≡a1​(modπ). Indeed, after constructing ar​ with f(ar​)≡0(modπr), choose the unique t modulo π for which
f(ar​)+πrtf′(ar​)≡0(modπr+1)
(20)
and put ar+1​=ar​+πrt. The resulting Cauchy sequence converges by completeness, and the same first-order congruence proves uniqueness.
Apply this to Xp−1−1. Every nonzero class in Fp​ is a simple root, so it has a unique Teichmuller representative in Zp​. These give all roots of unity of order prime to p. For odd p, the group 1+pZp​ has no nontrivial torsion: if u=1, then the binomial theorem gives vp​(up−1)=vp​(u−1)+1, which is incompatible with finite p-power order. Hence
μ(Qp​)≅Cp−1​qquad(p odd).
(21)
For p=2, the subgroup 1+4Z2​ is torsion-free by the same argument, while −1 supplies the extra torsion element. Thus μ(Q2​)={±1}≅C2​. This describes the roots of unity in a p-adic field for K=Qp​.

Choose a K-basis of L. Each extended absolute value is a norm on the finite-dimensional K-vector space L, and equivalence of norms in finite dimensions shows that every such norm induces the same topology when K is complete. Consequently any two extended absolute values induce the same topology on L. By the result of Question 2a, one is a positive real power of the other. Their restrictions to the nontrivially valued field K are both ∣⋅∣, so that power is one. In particular every extension is equal, and therefore equivalent, to ∣⋅∣L​.
