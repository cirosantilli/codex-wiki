For a harmonic function u, let
M(r)=∣∂Br​∣1​∫∂Br​(x)​u.
(1)
The divergence theorem gives
M′(r)=∣∂Br​∣1​∫∂Br​(x)​∂ν​u=∣∂Br​∣1​∫Br​(x)​Δu=0.
(2)
Since M(r)→u(x) as r↓0, M(r)=u(x). Integrating the spherical averages in the radial variable gives the corresponding ball average, proving the mean value property for harmonic functions. If u attains its maximum at an interior point, the average of the nonnegative function maxu−u on every sufficiently small centred sphere is zero. Continuity makes u constant on those spheres, and connectedness propagates that value through the domain. Thus the weak maximum principle for elliptic operators gives
min∂Ω​u≤u≤max∂Ω​u.
(3)

Suppose u1​ and u2​ are weak solutions with the same trace, and put w=u1​−u2​∈H01​(Ω). The weak formulation permits w itself as a test function, giving
0=∫Ω​Dw⋅Dw=∫Ω​∣Dw∣2.
(6)
Thus w is almost everywhere constant, and its zero trace makes that constant zero. This proves uniqueness.
The weak identity also says that Δu=0 in the sense of distributions. The Weyl lemma therefore gives u∈C∞(Ω) and Δu=0 pointwise. The assumed continuity on Ω retains the prescribed boundary values, so the weak solution is the unique classical solution in C∞(Ω)∩C0(Ω).

Fix a closed ball BR​(a)​⊂Ω, and let v solve the Dirichlet problem for the Laplace equation in this ball with boundary data u. Put w=u−v. The function w is continuous, vanishes on the boundary, and inherits the restricted spherical mean identity because v has the full mean value property for harmonic functions.
Suppose M=maxBR​(a)​​w>0. Its maximum set E is a nonempty compact subset of the open ball. Choose x∈E maximizing ∣x−a∣. For every sufficiently small radius in the sequence attached to x,
M=w(x)=∣∂Br​∣1​∫∂Br​(x)​w≤M.
(7)
Equality of the average with the maximum and continuity imply that the whole sphere belongs to E. Its point in the direction from a through x lies farther from a than x does; if x=a, any point on the sphere does. Both cases contradict the choice of x. Hence w≤0, and applying the same argument to −w gives w=0.
Thus u=v on every relatively compact ball. It is consequently harmonic and smooth locally, proving the local converse to the mean value property.

Write the flux as
Ai(x,z,η)=aij(x,z,η)ηj​.
(8)
After expanding the divergence, the principal symbol of a partial differential equation along a candidate solution u is determined by
Aik(x,u,Du)=∂ηk​∂Ai​=aik+∂ηk​∂aij​Dj​u.
(9)
Only the symmetric part As​=(A+AT)/2 contributes to AikDik​u. The problem is elliptic along u when ξTAs​ξ≥0 for every ξ, and it is strictly elliptic where this quantity is positive for every nonzero ξ. It is uniformly elliptic on a set when constants 0<λ≤Λ<∞, independent of the point, satisfy
λ∣ξ∣2≤ξTAs​(x,u,Du)ξ≤Λ∣ξ∣2.
(10)
These definitions separate pointwise positive definiteness from a quantitative lower and upper bound; a degenerate elliptic operator may lose strict ellipticity at some jets.

For the p-energy
F[u]=p1​∫Ω​∣Du∣pdx,
(11)
the first variation in the direction ψ∈Cc∞​(Ω) is
dtd​F[u+tψ]​t=0​=∫Ω​∣Du∣p−2Du⋅Dψ.
(12)
An integration by parts therefore gives the Euler-Lagrange equation
div(∣Du∣p−2Du)=0,
(13)
which is the p-Laplacian equation. In the notation of the question one takes aij(x,z,η)=∣η∣p−2δij.
For η=0, the principal coefficient matrix is
Aik(η)=∣η∣p−2δik+(p−2)∣η∣p−4ηi​ηk​.
(14)
Its eigenvalue in directions orthogonal to η is ∣η∣p−2, while its eigenvalue parallel to η is (p−1)∣η∣p−2. The coefficients are C1,α away from η=0, and the condition number there is at most p−1. On every region where 0<m≤∣Du∣≤M, this gives uniform ellipticity with constants depending on m, M, and p. At Du=0 all principal eigenvalues vanish, so the operator is degenerate there and is not strictly elliptic on a domain containing a critical point.

For
Lu=aijDij​u+biDi​u+cu,c≤0,
(19)
the weak maximum principle for elliptic operators states that Lu≥0 implies
maxΩ​u≤max{0,max∂Ω​u}.
(20)
In particular, a solution of Lu=0 cannot have a positive interior maximum exceeding its boundary maximum.
Because the coefficient matrix is positive definite and the closure of the smooth bounded domain is compact, strict ellipticity supplies a uniform lower bound after restricting to Ω. Rotate and translate coordinates so that Ω is bounded in the x1​ direction, and set h=eγx1​. For sufficiently large γ,
Lh=eγx1​(γ2a11+γb1+c)>0.
(21)
If u+εh had a positive interior maximum, its gradient would vanish and its Hessian matrix would be negative semidefinite there, giving L(u+εh)≤0. This contradicts L(u+εh)=εLh>0. Comparing on the boundary and sending ε↓0 proves the assertion.

Suppose vk​→v in C1,β and write uk​=T(vk​). The preceding uniform Schauder estimate bounds (uk​) in C2,αβ. Every subsequence therefore has a further subsequence converging in C1,β by the compact embedding of Hölder spaces. The composed coefficients converge uniformly, and lower-exponent Hölder compactness provides enough convergence of the second derivatives to pass to the linear equation. Every subsequential limit solves the problem defining T(v).
Uniqueness of that linear Dirichlet problem forces every such limit to equal T(v). Since every subsequence has a further subsequence with this same limit, the whole sequence converges to T(v) in C1,β. Thus T is continuous.

The Leray-Schauder fixed point theorem says that a continuous compact map T on a Banach space has a fixed point if the homotopy set
{u:u=tT(u) for some 0≤t≤1}
(26)
is bounded. If u=tT(u) with t>0, multiplying the equation for T(u) by t shows that u solves
aˉij(x,u,Du)Dij​u+tbˉ(x,u,Du)=0in Ω,u=tφon ∂Ω.
(27)
The case t=0 gives u=0. Therefore it is enough to prove one uniform C1,β estimate for all solutions of this family and all t∈[0,1]. The theorem then yields a fixed point u=T(u), which solves the original quasilinear problem.
Initially the construction gives u∈C2,αβ. This makes u and Du Lipschitz, so composing the original C0,α coefficients with the jet of u produces C0,α coefficients. A second application of the Global Schauder estimate gives u∈C2,α(Ω). The fixed-point theorem is an existence result and supplies no uniqueness.

For y in the closed upper half-space, write Br+​(y)=Br​(y)∩R+n​​. The boundary Simon absorption lemma has the following scaled form. Let S be a nonnegative set functional that is monotone and subadditive on finite covers by such half-balls. Given λ≥0 and 0<θ<1, there is δ0​=δ0​(n,λ,θ)>0 such that, if 0<δ≤δ0​ and every admissible nested pair satisfies
(θr)λS(Bθr+​(y))≤δrλS(Br+​(y))+γ,
(30)
then
RλS(BR+​(x))≤C(n,λ,θ)γ.
(31)
The same conclusion holds for half-balls centred on the flat boundary and truncated balls meeting it. Iteration over a finite covering absorbs the small first term into the left-hand side.

The Boundary Schauder estimate is
∥u∥C2,α(B1/2+​​)​≤C(∥u∥C0(B1+​​)​+∥f∥C0,α(B1+​​)​+∥φ∥C2,α(B1+​​)​),
(32)
where C depends only on the dimension, α, the ellipticity constants, and the coefficient Hölder norms.
Subtract a C2,α extension of φ to reduce to a function v with zero data on the flat boundary; this changes the forcing by a controlled C0,α term. The key local estimate is that for every δ>0,
[D2v]α;B1/2+​​≤δ[D2v]α;B1+​​+Cδ​(∥v∥C2(B1+​)​+∥Lv∥C0,α(B1+​)​).
(33)
To prove it, argue by contradiction. A failing normalized sequence has points xk​,yk​ at which the second-derivative Hölder quotient stays nonzero. Set rk​=∣xk​−yk​∣, subtract the appropriate second-order Taylor polynomial, and rescale space by rk​ and the functions by rk2+α​[D2vk​]α​. Necessarily rk​→0.
If the rescaled distance to the flat boundary tends to infinity, the domains converge to all of Rn; otherwise they converge to a half-space. Coefficient compactness freezes the principal matrix, while the normalized right-hand sides converge locally uniformly to zero. A linear change of variables turns every limiting equation into the Laplace equation. In the whole-space case the Polynomial-growth Liouville theorem for harmonic functions makes the limit a polynomial of degree at most two. In the half-space case the zero boundary data permit odd reflection across the flat boundary, after which the same theorem applies. The subtracted Taylor normalization forces that polynomial to vanish to second order, contradicting the nonzero limiting Hölder oscillation.
Apply the local estimate on all interior balls and boundary half-balls. The interior and boundary forms of the Simon absorption lemma absorb the term multiplied by δ, while the Hölder interpolation inequality absorbs the remaining lower C2 norm into the top seminorm and the C0 norm. Restoring φ yields the displayed estimate.
