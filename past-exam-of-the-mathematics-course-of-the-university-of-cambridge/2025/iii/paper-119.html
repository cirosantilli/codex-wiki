The Yoneda lemma states that for A∈C and P:C→Set there is a natural bijection
Nat(C(A,−),P)≅P(A),α⟼αA​(1A​).
(1)
If P↠Q is an epimorphism in the functor category, it is pointwise surjective, so P(A)→Q(A) is surjective. Yoneda identifies this map with
[C,Set](C(A,−),P)⟶[C,Set](C(A,−),Q).
(2)
Thus every representable functor is a projective object in a category.
The colimit form of the Special adjoint functor theorem says that a colimit-preserving functor from a locally small, cocomplete, well-copowered category with a small generating family into a locally small category has a right adjoint functor. For small C, the category [C,Set] is locally small and has colimits pointwise. Quotients of P are represented by compatible equivalence relations on the sets P(C), so they form a set; hence the category is well-copowered. The set of representables {C(C,−):C∈C} generates it by the Yoneda lemma. The theorem therefore gives a right adjoint to every small-colimit-preserving functor
[C,Set]⟶[D,Set].
(3)
In particular, product with a fixed functor P is computed pointwise, and −×P(C) preserves colimits in the Category of sets. Hence −×P preserves all small colimits and has a right adjoint (−)P. Thus [C,Set] is a Cartesian closed category.
Now work in [Cop,Set] and write yA=C(−,A). If C has binary products, then
(PyA)(C)≅Nat(yC×yA,P)≅Nat(y(C×A),P)≅P(C×A).
(4)
Thus exponentiation by yA is precomposition with −×A. Precomposition between functor categories has a right adjoint given by Right Kan extension, so yA is a tiny object.
Conversely, suppose C has a terminal object 1 and F is tiny. The representable y1 is the terminal presheaf, and the exponential adjunction plus Yoneda gives
[Cop,Set](F,P)≅(PF)(1).
(5)
Since F is tiny, (−)F is a left adjoint and preserves all colimits; evaluation at 1 also preserves pointwise colimits. Therefore the hom functor [Cop,Set](F,−) preserves coproducts and epimorphisms. Preservation of epimorphisms makes F projective, while preservation of coproducts makes it indecomposable.

For F:C⇄D:G with F⊣G, the unit and counit of an adjunction are
η:1C​→GF,ε:FG→1D​,
(6)
obtained by transposing identity morphisms. They satisfy the triangular identities εF​Fη=1F​ and GεηG​=1G​.
If G is full and faithful, fullness gives a map u:B→FGB with G(u)=ηGB​; the second triangular identity and faithfulness show that u is inverse to εB​. Conversely, if ε is an isomorphism, then for h:GB→GB′ the unique arrow whose transpose is h is
BεB−1​​FGBFh​FGB′εB′​​B′,
(7)
which proves that G is full, and the triangular identities prove faithfulness. Hence (i) and (ii) are equivalent. Condition (ii) immediately gives (iii). Conversely, a natural isomorphism FG≅1D​ combines with the adjunction bijection to show naturally that D(B,B′)→C(GB,GB′) is bijective; by naturality and the triangular identities this is the map induced by G up to invertible natural conjugation, so G is full and faithful. Thus all three conditions are equivalent.
Suppose L⊣F⊣R. If L is full and faithful, the unit 1→FL is an isomorphism. For A,B in the codomain of F, the two adjunctions then give natural bijections
D(A,FRB)≅C(LA,RB)≅D(FLA,B)≅D(A,B).
(8)
By the Yoneda lemma, the counit FRB→B is an isomorphism, so the fully faithful adjoint criterion makes R full and faithful. The converse is dual.
Now assume F is full and faithful. For L⊣F, its counit ϵ:LF→1 is invertible; for F⊣R, its unit α:1→RF is invertible. Define
θ=(αL​)−1Rη:R⟶L.
(9)
Applying F, then using naturality and the triangular identities, reduces this composite to the same map as
R(ϵR​)−1​LFRLβ​L.
(10)
Since F is faithful, the two displayed composites are equal.
For a morphism u:FB→A, let u=RuαB​:B→RA be its transpose under F⊣R. The identities just proved give
θA​u=LuϵB−1​.
(11)
If every θA​ is monic and Lu=Lv, this equation gives u=v, hence u=v; thus L is faithful on arrows whose domains are in the image of F. Conversely, if θA​f=θA​g for f,g:B→RA, naturality of ϵ and the second formula for θ give
L(βA​Ff)=L(βA​Fg).
(12)
Both maps have domain FB, so the assumed faithfulness gives βA​Ff=βA​Fg. These are the transposes of f and g, hence f=g. Therefore θ is pointwise monic exactly under the stated faithfulness condition.

A categorical limit of D:J→C is a terminal cone: it consists of an object L and compatible maps L→D(j) through which every other cone factors uniquely. For a finite diagram, take the product P=∏j​D(j) and the product Q=∏u:i→j​D(j). There are two maps P⇉Q: on the u:i→j coordinate one uses respectively D(u) after projection to D(i) and direct projection to D(j). Their equalizer is exactly the compatible-cone object. Hence finite products and equalizers construct every finite limit.
In the category of metric spaces and non-expansive maps, give X×Y the maximum metric
d((x,y),(x′,y′))=max{dX​(x,x′),dY​(y,y′)}.
(13)
The projections are non-expansive, and a pair of non-expansive maps into X and Y induces a non-expansive map into this product, proving the universal property.
Let two maps 1⇉X select a,b∈X. On the set quotient identifying a and b, define the quotient metric by shortest paths that may jump from a to b at zero cost. Explicitly,
dQ​([x],[y])=min{d(x,y),d(x,a)+d(b,y),d(x,b)+d(a,y)}.
(14)
This is the largest metric making the quotient map non-expansive. A map X→Z equalizing a and b factors through the set quotient and remains non-expansive by the path formula, so this is the coequalizer. Its underlying set is the set-theoretic coequalizer.
For X={0,1,5,6} with 0∼6, write ∗={0,6}. The quotient has
dQ​(∗,1)=dQ​(∗,5)=1,dQ​(1,5)=2.
(15)
Thus in Q×Y, with Y={0,3},
dQ×Y​((1,0),(5,3))=max{2,3}=3.
(16)
After first taking X×Y, the product of the parallel pair identifies (0,y) with (6,y) separately for y=0,3. In its quotient metric, every path from (1,0) to (5,3) has length at least 4, and length 4 is attained either directly or via one of those identifications. The canonical bijection from this coequalizer to Q×Y is therefore not an isometry. Hence −×Y does not preserve this coequalizer. In a Cartesian closed category, −×Y is a left adjoint and preserves all colimits, so Met is not cartesian closed.

For F:C⇄D:G with F⊣G, the monad induced by an adjunction is T=GF, with unit the adjunction unit and multiplication μ=GεF. An algebra for a monad is a map a:TA→A satisfying aηA​=1A​ and aT(a)=aμA​. The Eilenberg-Moore comparison functor is
K:D→CT,K(D)=(GD,GεD​),
(17)
and sends a morphism h to Gh.
Given a T-algebra (A,a), form in D the coequalizer
FTAFa​εFA​​​FA⟶J(A,a).
(18)
The pair is reflexive, with common section FηA​, so it exists by hypothesis. A map J(A,a)→D is equivalently a map FA→D equalizing the pair. Under adjunction this is exactly a map A→GD satisfying the T-algebra homomorphism equation. Hence
D(J(A,a),D)≅CT((A,a),K(D)),
(19)
naturally, and J⊣K. Iterating these comparison adjunctions gives the monadic tower; the monadic length is the least number of steps required to reach an equivalence.
For a pair of sets (X0​,X1​), define
F(X0​,X1​)=(X0​↪X0​⊔X1​).
(20)
A pair of maps X0​→A0​, X1​→A1​ extends uniquely to a commutative square from this inclusion to any injection A0​↪A1​, proving that F is left adjoint to the forgetful functor U:Mono(Set)→Set×Set.
The induced monad sends (X0​,X1​) to (X0​,X0​⊔X1​). Its algebra unit laws show that an algebra is precisely an arbitrary function X0​→X1​, with no injectivity requirement. Thus its Eilenberg-Moore category is the arrow category [2,Set], and the comparison functor is the full inclusion of injections into all functions. This inclusion is not an equivalence, so the original adjunction is not monadic. It is reflective: a function f:A0​→A1​ maps to its image inclusion imf↪A1​. The supplied result that reflections are monadic says that the next comparison is an equivalence. Therefore the original adjunction has monadic length 2.

The statement that limits of shape I commute with colimits of shape J means that for every D:I×J→C, the canonical commutation of limits and colimits map
colimj∈J​limi∈I​D(i,j)⟶limi∈I​colimj∈J​D(i,j)
(21)
is an isomorphism whenever the iterated limits and colimits exist.
A filtered category is a nonempty category in which every finite diagram has a cocone. Equivalently, any two objects map to a common object and any parallel pair becomes equal after postcomposition. A weakly filtered category requires cocones only for finite connected diagrams; equivalently, each connected component is filtered.
Write a weakly filtered category as the disjoint union ∐s​Js​ of its filtered connected components. Its colimit is the coproduct of the filtered colimits over the Js​. In sets, a connected finite limit commutes with coproducts: connectedness forces all coordinates of a compatible tuple to lie in the same coproduct summand. By the assumed theorem, the filtered colimit over each Js​ commutes with every finite limit. Applying these two facts successively proves that weakly filtered colimits commute with connected finite limits in Set.
The forgetful functor from abelian group to sets creates finite limits and filtered colimits and reflects isomorphisms. The comparison map for a filtered colimit and a finite limit therefore becomes the corresponding isomorphism of sets, so filtered colimits commute with finite limits in AbGp.
The dual claim fails because inverse limit need not preserve epimorphisms. Take the inverse systems
An​=Z,Bn​=Z/2nZ,
(22)
with identity bonding maps on An​, reduction maps on Bn​, and levelwise epimorphisms qn​:An​→Bn​. Then
lim​An​=Z,lim​Bn​=Z2​,
(23)
and the induced map Z→Z2​ is not surjective. Since an epimorphism in abelian groups is a finite-colimit cokernel, cofiltered limits do not commute with finite colimits in AbGp.

A regular category has finite limits, regular-epimorphism--monomorphism image factorizations, and regular epimorphisms stable under pullback. A relation A⇝B in Rel(C) is a subobject of A×B; composition forms the pullback over the middle object and then takes its image.
Suppose R:A⇝B has a right adjoint relation S, so 1A​≤SR and RS≤1B​. In the internal regular logic, the first inequality says that for every a there is a b with R(a,b) and S(b,a). If also R(a,b′), the second inequality forces b=b′. Thus R is total and single-valued. Categorically, if R↪A×B has projections p:R→A and q:R→B, totality makes p a regular epimorphism and single-valuedness makes it a monomorphism. Hence p is an isomorphism and R is the graph of a morphism as a relation qp−1:A→B. Conversely, the graph of any morphism is left adjoint to its converse relation, as the two required inequalities follow directly from equality. This proves the characterization.
Let L be a frame. Composition in the category of matrices valued in a frame is
(gf)(a,c)=⋁b∈B​f(a,b)∧g(b,c).
(24)
If f⊣g, the diagonal part of 1A​≤gf implies
1=⋁b​f(a,b)∧g(b,a)≤⋁b​f(a,b),
(25)
so every row of f joins to 1. For b=b′, distribute f(a,b)∧f(a,b′) over the displayed join. Every term vanishes by fg≤1B​, first using the factor with column b and then the one with column b′. Hence
f(a,b)∧f(a,b′)=0.
(26)
Conversely, if the rows of f join to 1 and have pairwise disjoint entries, define g(b,a)=f(a,b). Then
(gf)(a,a)=⋁b​f(a,b)=1,
(27)
while (fg)(b,b′)=0 for b=b′. Thus 1A​≤gf and fg≤1B​, proving the stated criterion.
Finally take L=Ω(X) for a connected topological space X. For fixed a, the opens f(a,b) are pairwise disjoint and cover X. Connectedness forces exactly one of them to be X and all the others to be empty. Hence a left adjoint matrix determines a unique function φ:A→B by f(a,φ(a))=X. Conversely every function gives this matrix, and matrix composition agrees with function composition. The left adjoints in Mat(Ω(X)) therefore form a category isomorphic to Set.
