An affine algebraic group over k is an affine variety G equipped with multiplication G×G→G, inversion G→G, and an identity element satisfying the group axioms, with multiplication and inversion both regular maps. Dually, the coordinate ring k[G] is a commutative Hopf algebra: multiplication on G induces the comultiplication Δ:k[G]→k[G]⊗k[G], inversion induces the antipode, and evaluation at the identity is the counit.

Choose algebra generators f1​,…,fr​ of the finitely generated coordinate ring k[G]. The right regular action is locally finite: writing
Δ(fi​)=∑j​fij​⊗hij​
(1)
shows that every right translate of fi​ lies in the finite span of the fij​. Hence all the fi​ lie in some finite-dimensional translation-stable subspace V⊆k[G].
This gives a rational representation ρ:G→GL(V). If g∈kerρ, then fi​(xg)=fi​(x) for every i and x. Setting x=e gives fi​(g)=fi​(e) for every algebra generator, and therefore for every regular function on G. Regular functions separate closed points of an affine variety, so g=e. Thus ρ is a faithful representation of an affine algebraic group.

For G=Ga​, write k[G]=k[t]. Under the left-right regular representation of an affine algebraic group,
((a,b)f)(t)=f(t−a+b),
(2)
so the action factors through the difference map Ga​×Ga​→Ga​. The degree filtration
k⊂k[t]≤1​⊂k[t]≤2​⊂⋯
(3)
is stable and has trivial one-dimensional successive quotients. The module is indecomposable; every nonzero submodule contains a nonzero translation difference of lower degree and, on iteration using suitable translations, meets the unique invariant line k⋅1.
For every finite-dimensional rational G-module V, the matrix-coefficient construction gives
HomG​(V,k[G])≅V∗,λ⟼(v↦[g↦λ(gv)]).
(4)
On the other hand,
HomG​(k[G],V)=0.
(5)
Indeed, a nonzero finite-dimensional quotient of k[G] would have a simple quotient. Every simple rational representation of the unipotent algebraic group Ga​ is trivial, so this would give a nonzero translation-invariant functional on k[t]. No such functional exists: if cj​ is its first nonzero value on a monomial, translating a sufficiently high monomial produces a nonzero coefficient times cj​, contradicting invariance.

An element g∈G(k) is semisimple when its image in a faithful finite-dimensional representation is diagonalizable, and unipotent when that image has every eigenvalue equal to one. These conditions are independent of the faithful representation. The Jordan decomposition in an affine algebraic group is the unique factorization
g=gs​gu​=gu​gs​
(9)
with gs​ semisimple and gu​ unipotent.
In G=GL2​, matrices with two distinct eigenvalues form a dense open subset and are semisimple, so semisimple elements are dense. Their full locus is not open: a scalar matrix is semisimple, but every neighbourhood of it contains a nontrivial Jordan block. The group also has nonidentity unipotent matrices.
For a contrasting example take G=Ga​×Gm​. Its semisimple locus is {0}×Gm​, which is closed and not dense, while (a,1) for a=0 is unipotent and (0,t) for t=1 is semisimple.

b OurBigBook.com
The derived subgroup of an affine algebraic group [G,G] is the closed subgroup generated by the commutators xyx−1y−1. If G is connected, the image of every finite product of commutator maps is connected and contains the identity. The closures of these images form an increasing sequence; once their dimensions stabilize, the stable member is closed under products and inverses and equals [G,G]. Hence [G,G] is connected.
Now suppose the connected group G is solvable. The Lie-Kolchin theorem conjugates a faithful representation of G into the upper triangular matrices. Every commutator then has all diagonal entries equal to one, so every element of [G,G] is unipotent. Moreover [G,G] lies in the upper unitriangular group, whose superdiagonal filtration is a central series. It is therefore a nilpotent group.
A diagonalizable algebraic group is a closed subgroup of a product of copies of Gm​. A unipotent algebraic group has only unipotent elements, while a semisimple algebraic group here means one all of whose elements are semisimple. A reductive algebraic group is smooth, connected, affine, and has trivial connected normal unipotent radical.

The Kolchin theorem conjugates a faithful representation of a unipotent algebraic group U into the upper unitriangular group Un​. Let Un(r)​ consist of matrices whose first r−1 superdiagonals vanish. Matrix multiplication gives
[Un(r)​,Un(s)​]⊆Un(r+s)​.
(10)
Since Un(n)​=1, this filtration is a finite central series, so Un​ and every subgroup of it are nilpotent groups. Hence U is nilpotent.
The converse fails: Gm​ is abelian, hence nilpotent, but its nonidentity points are semisimple rather than unipotent.

Let I=I(H)⊆k[G]. Choose finitely many generators of I and a finite-dimensional G-submodule U⊆k[G] containing them, using local finiteness of the right regular action. Set W=U∩I. Right translation by H preserves I, so H stabilizes W. Conversely, if gW=W, every chosen generator f has Rg​f∈I, and evaluation at the identity gives f(g)=0. Thus g∈H, and StabG​(W)=H.
Put d=dimW and take V=⋀dU. The line
ℓ=⋀dW∈P(V)
(11)
determines W uniquely, so its stabilizer is the stabilizer of W, namely H.

A flat algebraic-group torsor X→Y is a faithfully flat morphism with a right H-action for which
X×H⟶X×Y​X,(x,h)⟼(x,xh),
(12)
is an isomorphism.
The orbit Gℓ is a locally closed subvariety of P(V) by the orbit theorem for algebraic-group actions. The fibers of the orbit map G→Gℓ are precisely the right cosets of H=StabG​(ℓ), and the displayed action map is therefore an isomorphism. The theorem on quotients of affine algebraic groups by closed subgroups says that G/H exists and G→G/H is faithfully flat; the induced map G/H→Gℓ is an isomorphism. Hence the orbit map is a flat H-torsor.

i OurBigBook.com
Embed the space B of complete isotropic flags into
∏i=1n​Gr(i,V).
(16)
The incidence conditions Fi​⊂Fi+1​ and isotropy equations ⟨Fi​,Fi​⟩=0 are closed polynomial conditions. Since the Grassmannian is projective, B is a projective algebraic variety.
Every complete isotropic flag extends to a symplectic basis. A symplectic change of basis carries any such flag to any other, so G=Sp2n​ acts transitively. The stabilizer B of the standard flag consists of the upper triangular symplectic matrices. It is closed, connected, and solvable. The Lie-Kolchin theorem shows that every connected solvable subgroup fixes a complete flag in V; preservation of the symplectic form makes the resulting flag isotropic after taking its first half. Such a subgroup is conjugate into B, so B is maximal and hence a Borel subgroup.

For each signed permutation w∈W, choose its symplectic signed-permutation matrix w˙∈NG​(T). The Bruhat decomposition of a reductive algebraic group is the explicit disjoint union
Sp2n​=⨆w∈(Z/2)n⋊Sn​​Bw˙B.
(22)
To prove existence, compare the standard isotropic flag F∙​ with gF∙​. The ranks
rij​=dim(Fi​∩gFj​)
(23)
together with the symplectic orthogonality relations determine a unique signed permutation w. Symplectic row and column operations from B then reduce g to w˙, so g∈Bw˙B. Conversely, the same intersection dimensions are constant on a double coset and recover w, proving disjointness. This is symplectic Gaussian elimination and establishes the claimed decomposition.
