Let M=maxS​u and suppose u(z0​)=M. The sub-mean inequality on every closed disk contained in S gives
M=u(z0​)≤2π1​∫02π​u(z0​+reiθ)dθ≤M.
(1)
Equality holds throughout. If u were strictly below M at one point of the circle, upper semicontinuity would make it uniformly below M on a small arc, contradicting equality of the average. Thus u=M on every sufficiently small circle centered at z0​, and hence throughout a neighborhood of z0​.
The set {u=M} is therefore open. It is also closed because upper semicontinuity makes {u<M} open. Since the domain is connected and {u=M} is nonempty, it is all of S. This proves the maximum principle for subharmonic functions.
Let
R(z)=(A−zI)−1.
(2)
The resolvent set is open, and the resolvent of an element is operator-valued holomorphic on each of its components. Fix z0​ in the resolvent set. For any δ>0, choose unit vectors x,y such that
∣⟨R(z0​)x,y⟩∣>∥R(z0​)∥−δ.
(3)
The scalar function f(z)=⟨R(z)x,y⟩ is holomorphic. Its modulus is subharmonic, so
∥R(z0​)∥−δ<∣f(z0​)∣≤2π1​∫02π​∣f(z0​+reiθ)∣dθ≤2π1​∫02π​∥R(z0​+reiθ)∥dθ.
(4)
Letting δ↓0 proves that the resolvent norm is subharmonic on the resolvent component containing z0​.
Use the convention that the reciprocal resolvent norm is zero on the spectrum. Suppose a bounded component U of
{z:∥R(z)∥−1<ϵ}
(5)
contained no spectral point. A spectral point in its boundary would belong to the same pseudospectral component, so U is contained in the resolvent set. On ∂U one has ∥R(z)∥≤ϵ−1, whereas inside U one has ∥R(z)∥>ϵ−1. Continuity on the compact set U makes the resolvent norm attain a maximum at an interior point. The subharmonic maximum principle would make it constant, contradicting its boundary values. Hence every bounded component of the pseudospectrum contains spectrum:
U∩Sp(A)=∅​.
(6)

If A has finite rank, the image of its unit ball is bounded in the finite-dimensional space ranA. The closure of a bounded set in a finite-dimensional normed space is compact. Hence every bounded finite-rank operator is a compact operator.
Let Qn​=Pn∗​Pn​ be the orthogonal projection onto
Hn​=span{e1​,…,en​}.
(15)
Then Qn​→I strongly. Strong convergence is uniform on every compact subset: if K is compact, cover it by finitely many small balls and use ∥I−Qn​∥≤1 at their centers. Since the closure of A applied to the unit ball is compact,
∥(I−Qn​)A∥⟶0.
(16)
The adjoint of a compact operator is compact, so the same argument for A∗ gives
∥A(I−Qn​)∥=∥(I−Qn​)A∗∥⟶0.
(17)
Since An​=Qn​AQn​,
A−An​=(I−Qn​)A+Qn​A(I−Qn​).
(18)
Therefore
∥A−An​∥≤∥(I−Qn​)A∥+∥A(I−Qn​)∥⟶0​.
(19)
This is the finite-section approximation of a compact operator.

Write Qn​=Pn​=Pn∗​ for the orthogonal projections and regard the compression An​=Qn​AQn​ as acting on ranQn​. The compactness argument from part iii applies to any strongly convergent sequence of orthogonal projections, so
∥A−An​∥→0​.
(20)
We first rule out spectral pollution. Suppose λn​∈Sp(An​) and, after taking a subsequence, λn​→λ=0. Choose unit eigenvectors xn​∈ranQn​:
An​xn​=λn​xn​.
(21)
Compactness gives a convergent subsequence of Axn​. Because
∥(A−An​)xn​∥→0,
(22)
the relation xn​=λn−1​An​xn​ then makes xn​ converge to a nonzero vector x, and passage to the limit gives Ax=λx. Thus every nonzero limit of finite-section spectral points belongs to Sp(A). The only remaining possible limit is zero, which belongs to the spectrum of a compact operator on an infinite-dimensional space.
Conversely, the Riesz–Schauder theorem says that every nonzero λ∈Sp(A) is an isolated eigenvalue of finite algebraic multiplicity. Put a small contour around λ containing no other point of Sp(A). Norm convergence of An​ gives uniform resolvent convergence on the contour, so the associated Riesz projections converge in norm and eventually have the same positive rank. Hence Sp(An​) meets every neighborhood of λ.
Finally, zero is also approximated. Otherwise some subsequence would have all its eigenvalues bounded away from zero. Outside any small disk, Sp(A) has only finitely many eigenvalues, and the preceding Riesz-projection argument fixes the total algebraic multiplicity of nearby finite-section eigenvalues. This cannot account for
dimranQn​⟶∞.
(23)
Thus finite-section eigenvalues also approach zero. Both directed spectral distances vanish, proving
dH​(Sp(Pn​APn∗​),Sp(A))⟶0​.
(24)

Put T=A−zI. We use the following closed-range lemma:
0∈/Spess​(T∗T)⟺T is upper semi-Fredholm.
(25)
Indeed,
ker(T∗T)=kerT.
(26)
Away from this kernel, zero is separated from the spectrum of the positive self-adjoint operator T∗T exactly when
∥Tx∥≥c∥x∥,x⊥kerT,
(27)
for some c>0. This is equivalent to closed range. Zero is then absent from the essential spectrum exactly when dimkerT<∞. Similarly,
0∈/Spess​(TT∗)⟺T is lower semi-Fredholm,
(28)
because kerT∗=(ranT)⊥ measures the cokernel.
Applying the lecture definitions of the three essential spectra of a closed operator now gives
Spess,1​(A)={z:0∈Spess​(T∗T)∩Spess​(TT∗)}​,
(29)
Spess,2​(A)={z:0∈Spess​(T∗T)}​,
(30)
and
Spess,3​(A)={z:0∈Spess​(T∗T)∪Spess​(TT∗)}​.
(31)
If A is normal, so is T. The spectral theorem gives
T∗T=∣T∣2,
(32)
and maps the spectral mass of T at 0 exactly to the spectral mass of T∗T at 0. Thus zero is isolated with finite multiplicity for T∗T exactly when z is an isolated eigenvalue of finite multiplicity for A. Consequently
Spd​(A)={z:0∈Spd​(T∗T)}​.
(33)
Normality is essential. Let Uej​=ej+1​ be the unilateral shift and take A=U∗. Its spectrum is the closed unit disk, so 0 is not in the discrete spectrum. But
A∗A=UU∗=I−P{e1​}​,
(34)
whose zero eigenvalue is isolated and simple. Hence 0∈Spd​(A∗A) while 0∈/Spd​(A).

Set T=A−zI. The squares of the singular values of TPn∗​ are the eigenvalues of the finite-dimensional positive operator
Pn​T∗TPn∗​.
(35)
The Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle and its min-max characterization show that, as the trial space grows, its (j+1)-st eigenvalue counted upward cannot increase. Therefore, for each fixed j≥0,
σn−j(n)​(z,A) decreases with n​
(36)
once n>j. Being nonnegative, it has a limit
σinf+j​(A−zI)=n→∞lim​σn−j(n)​(z,A)​.
(37)
The core assumption ensures that these Ritz limits are the min-max values of T∗T, rather than values for a smaller closed restriction.
For a normal operator, the spectral theorem gives three possibilities.
  • If z∈/Sp(A), then ∥Tx∥≥c∥x∥ for some c>0, so every σinf+j​≥c.
  • If z is a discrete eigenvalue of multiplicity m, exactly
σinf​(T),…,σinf+m−1​(T)
(38)
vanish, while σinf+m​(T)>0.
  • If z lies in the essential spectrum, the min-max principle or an orthonormal Weyl sequence gives σinf+j​(T)=0 for every fixed j.
For k∈N, define
Hk​(z,A)=∑j=0k​max{0,1−kσinf+j​(A−zI)}.
(39)
In the first case Hk​=0 for all sufficiently large k. In the second case its first m summands tend to one and all remaining summands eventually vanish, so Hk​→m. In the third case Hk​=k+1→+∞. These are exactly the three values in the definition of h, and hence
h(z,A)=k→∞lim​j=0∑k​max{0,1−kσinf+j​(A−zI)}​.

For a densely defined closed operator S, define its lower norm
ν(S)=infx∈D(S)∥x∥=1​​∥Sx∥,μ(S)=min{ν(S),ν(S∗)}.
(50)
The closed-range theorem gives
μ(S)={∥S−1∥−1,0,​S is bijective,S is not bijective.​​
(51)
Therefore
Sp(T)={z:μ(T(z))=0},
(52)
and
Spϵ​(T)=Cl{z:μ(T(z))<ϵ}.
(53)
We next show that μ(T(z)) is accessible through the matrix-entry evaluations. Put Ln​=span{e1​,…,en​} and define
νn​(T(z))=infx∈Ln​∥x∥=1​​∥T(z)x∥,νn​(T(z)∗)=infx∈Ln​∥x∥=1​​∥T(z)∗x∥.
(54)
Because the canonical span is a core for both operators,
νn​(T(z))↓ν(T(z)),νn​(T(z)∗)↓ν(T(z)∗).
(55)
For fixed m,n and grid point z, form
Mm,n​(z)=(⟨T(z)ej​,ei​⟩)1≤i≤m1≤j≤n​​.
(56)
Its entries belong to Λ. The smallest singular value of Mm,n​(z) increases as m→∞ to νn​(T(z)). Applying the same construction to the conjugate-transposed entries gives νn​(T(z)∗). Thus two nested finite-matrix limits determine μ(T(z)).
The assumed gap continuity of T and the identity
δ(T(z)∗,T(w)∗)=δ(T(z),T(w))
(57)
make the corresponding lower-norm tests stable under movement of z. The rational grids Gn​ become dense on every bounded disk. Hence an arithmetic algorithm can, on Gn​∩Bn​(0), use the two finite-section levels above and a vanishing rational tolerance to output all grid cells certified by
μ(T(z))<ϵ.
(58)
Taking their closures and letting the mesh and tolerance vanish converges in the Attouch--Wets topology to
Cl{z:μ(T(z))<ϵ}=Spϵ​(T).
(59)
The strict existential inequality requires two nested limits, with approximants entering from the prescribed side. In the notation of the arithmetic hierarchy this proves
{Spϵ​,ΩNL​,MAW,Λ}∈Σ2A​​.
(60)
For the spectrum, equality to zero is the countable intersection
{z:μ(T(z))=0}=⋂q=1∞​{z:μ(T(z))<1/q}.
(61)
Use the preceding two-level pseudospectral procedure with threshold 1/q, and add an outer limit q→∞. Outer approximants remove every point with positive lower norm, while gap continuity and density of the grids retain every zero. Truncating to expanding disks and using vanishing mesh again gives Attouch–Wets convergence. The universal outer intersection reverses the one-sided classification and adds one level, proving
{Sp,ΩNL​,MAW,Λ}∈Π3A​​.
(62)
