A smooth map i:N→M is a smooth embedding when it is an injective immersion and a homeomorphism onto its image with the subspace topology. The unit sphere is the inverse image of the regular value 1 under x↦∣x∣2, so the preimage theorem makes it a smooth submanifold of Rn+1 and its inclusion an immersion. It is injective, and a continuous injection from the compact sphere into the Hausdorff Euclidean space is a homeomorphism onto its image. Thus the inclusion is an embedding.
Products of spheres can be embedded by iterated spinning. Start with a round Sn1​⊂Rn1​+1 translated into the half-space whose last coordinate r is positive. If a compact m-manifold N is embedded by
x⟼(y(x),r(x))∈Rm×(0,∞),
(1)
then
N×Sq⟶Rm×Rq+1,(x,u)⟼(y(x),r(x)u)
(2)
is an injective immersion; compactness again makes it an embedding. Iterating with q=n2​,…,nk​ embeds Sn1​×⋯×Snk​ in Rn+1, where n=∑i​ni​.

The de Rham cohomology is
HdRp​(M)=ker(d:Ωp→Ωp+1)/operatornameim(d:Ωp−1→Ωp).
(3)
The Poincare lemma says that every closed positive-degree differential form is locally exact, and is exact on every star-shaped open subset of Euclidean space.
Let n>1 and let α be a closed one-form on M. The hypothesis gives α=dg on M∖B. Choose a slightly larger coordinate ball B′ around B. The Poincare lemma gives α=dh on B′. The annulus B′∖B is connected when n>1, so d(g−h)=0 there and g−h is constant. Adjusting h by this constant makes g and h agree on the overlap, and they glue to a global primitive of α. Hence H1(M)=0. For n=1 the claim fails: remove a closed proper interval from S1. Its complement is an interval and has vanishing first de Rham cohomology, whereas H1(S1)≅R.
Finally choose a nowhere-vanishing n-form ω on Sn and a coordinate t on the finite interval I. Every (n+1)-form on Sn×I is a(x,t)ω∧dt. Fix t0​∈I and put
A(x,t)=∫t0​t​a(x,s)ds.
(4)
Since dSn​A∧ω=0 for dimensional reasons,
d((−1)nAω)=aω∧dt.
(5)
Every top-degree form is exact, so Hn+1(Sn×I)=0 without using the de Rham theorem.

If E has local frame transition matrices gab​ and the cotangent bundle has transitions Jab−T​, then E⊗ΛrT∗B has local trivializations with transitions
gab​⊗Λr(Jab−T​),
(15)
which satisfy the cocycle condition. Thus it is a well-defined tensor product of vector bundles.
A connection on a vector bundle is a linear map
∇:Γ(E)→Ω1(B;E)
(16)
satisfying ∇(fs)=df⊗s+f∇s. Contracting with a vector field gives the covariant derivative ∇X​s. In a local frame, ∇=d+A for a matrix-valued one-form A; under a frame change g the matrix transforms as
A′=g−1Ag+g−1dg.
(17)
Its covariant exterior derivative is defined by
dA​(s⊗α)=∇s∧α+s⊗dα,
(18)

dA​η=dη+A∧η.
(19)

Choose a normal ball on which expp​ is a diffeomorphism, and take ε smaller than half its radius. For ∣X∣<ε, t↦expp​(tX) is a geodesic of length ∣X∣. If a competing curve remains in the normal ball, write it in polar form. The Gauss lemma makes radial and angular velocities orthogonal, so its speed is at least the absolute radial speed and its length is at least ∣X∣. A curve leaving the larger normal ball already accumulates more than ∣X∣ in radial variation. Thus the radial geodesic minimizes length.
At q, both X and the geodesic sphere Σ are hypersurfaces. If their tangent hyperplanes were distinct, they would be transverse. The transverse intersection theorem would then make X∩Σ a submanifold of dimension
(m−1)+(m−1)−m=m−2.
(31)
For m≥3 this has positive dimension near q, contradicting that the intersection is the singleton {q}. Therefore Tq​X=Tq​Σ.
The conclusion fails in dimension two because a transverse intersection is zero-dimensional and may be isolated. In the Euclidean plane, let Σ be the unit circle, q=(1,0), and let X={(x,0):1/2<x<3/2}. Then X∩Σ={q}, but Tq​X is horizontal whereas Tq​Σ is vertical.
