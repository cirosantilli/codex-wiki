Choose the square root with −π/2<argz<3π/2, so the branch cut is the negative imaginary axis. The change of variable u=e−iπ/4z​ maps this sheet onto Reu>0 and gives z=iu2. Writing the exponential function as ekϕ(z), its phase becomes exactly quadratic:
ϕ(z)=−i(z−2eiπ/4z​)=(u−1)2−1.
(1)
The saddle point is therefore u=1, or zs​=i. On the descending line u=1+iv, v∈R, the phase is −1−v2. Its image is the parabola
z=−2v+i(1−v2),Imz=1−4(Rez)2​.​
(2)
The contour runs from left to right, corresponding to v decreasing from +∞ to −∞. A contour deformation in the right half of the u-plane moves the original indented contour to this line. The connecting tails vanish in the descending sectors, and no branch point or pole is crossed. Since dz=−2(1+iv)dv, the Gaussian integral and the odd function ve−kv2 give
I=2e−k∫−∞∞​(1+iv)e−kv2dv=2kπ​​e−k​.
(3)
Here the method of steepest descent actually gives an exact answer for k>0, because the quadratic phase and linear transformed amplitude have no further even correction.

For a fixed pole away from the saddle point, the smooth amplitude there is (i−z0​)−1. The simple-saddle contribution in steepest descent is
Jsd​(z0​)=i−z0​2π/k​e−k​[1+O(k−1)].
(4)
The residue theorem supplies an additional contribution precisely when the contour deformation crosses z0​. The region swept above the original contour has positive orientation: the original contour from left to right followed by the reversed saddle contour encloses it counterclockwise. Consequently the original integral equals the saddle integral plus the pole contribution:
J(z0​)=Jsd​(z0​)+2πiχekϕ(z0​).​
(5)
For ∣z0​∣<1, an upper-half-plane pole outside the indentation has χ=1, while a lower-half-plane pole has χ=0. The entire upper unit half-disk lies below the saddle parabola, so there is no further case inside that half-disk. With a fixed indentation radius δ, a pole inside the upper semicircle, ∣z0​∣<δ, is also below the original contour and has χ=0; real points in the indentation gap are excluded as well. Taking δ<∣z0​∣ for a fixed nonzero pole gives the usual upper/lower classification. The pole contribution need not always dominate exponentially; its size depends on Reϕ(z0​), so keeping both terms makes that dependence explicit.
A pole on the original contour requires a stated Cauchy principal value or an indentation prescription. A pole parameter on the negative imaginary branch cut still defines the integral: the contour stays on its fixed sheet, and only the denominator uses z0​. Such a pole is not crossed, so only Jsd​ is needed and no value of z0​​ must be chosen. The residue exponential above is evaluated only when χ=1. At z0​=0 the original indentation excludes the singular point, so no additional residue is crossed. These conventions matter before taking any limiting pole position.

The ordinary saddle-point approximation with a nearby pole loses uniformity when the pole lies within the width of the saddle's Gaussian function profile. The distinguished limit is
zd​=zs​=i,z0​−i=O(k−1/2).​
(6)
Use the exact quadratic phase coordinate s=2ik​(u−1) and define
sd​=2ik​(e−iπ/4z0​​−1)=k​(z0​−i)+O(k​(z0​−i)2).​
(7)
On the descending contour, s runs along the real axis from −∞ to +∞. The transformed differential has the useful partial fraction decomposition
z−z0​dz​=(u−u0​1​+u+u0​1​)du,u0​=e−iπ/4z0​​.
(8)
The first term contains the nearby pole; the second is regular at the saddle and contributes O(e−kk−1/2). Approaching from below the descending contour means Imsd​<0. The residue theorem then gives the Gaussian saddle-pole transition
J(z0​)=e−k[∫−∞∞​s−sd​e−s2/4​ds+2πie−sd2​/4]+O(e−kk−1/2).
(9)
The exponent is −s2/4, as in the original PDF; the converted TeX's −s2/2 is a transcription error. The exact identity kϕ(z0​)=−k−sd2​/4 recovers the pole exponential in the requested expression.
The Gaussian pole integral expresses the bracket as iπw(sd​/2), where w is the Faddeeva function. Thus a convenient uniform leading answer is
J(z0​)=iπe−kw(sd​/2)+O(e−kk−1/2).​
(10)
The error estimate applies for bounded scaled pole position on the indicated side. In the overlap 1≪∣sd​∣≪k​ below the real axis, the integral contributes −2π​/sd​ to leading order while the residue remains 2πie−sd2​/4. Since sd​∼k​(z0​−i), this reproduces the near-saddle limit of the separate saddle and residue terms. Boundary values on the descending contour are limits of this combined expression; the isolated real-axis pole integral itself needs a Cauchy principal value prescription.

The solution and its first derivative are continuous at x=1: a jump would create a Dirac delta distribution or its derivative, absent from the forcing. Put r=x−1, A=a+1 and B=b. The outer expansions, fixed by the respective endpoint boundary conditions, are
yL​=A+r+ε[r+1+Alog∣r∣]+⋯(r<0),yR​=B+εBlogr+⋯(r>0).
(11)
Balancing diffusion and advection near r=0 gives the interior layer at a simple zero of advection with inner variable z=r/ε. The leading inner expansion solves Y0′′​+2zY0′​=0, so matching to A and B gives
Y0​=c+derfz,c=2A+B​,d=2B−A​.
(12)
For the next term define the Gaussian drift primitives
E0​(z)=∫0z​e−t2∫0t​eu2dudt,E1​(z)=∫0z​e−t2∫0t​eu2erfududt.
(13)
Writing H for the Heaviside step function, let F(z)=zH(−z)+(π​/4)erf∣z∣. Its value and first derivative match at zero and (D2+2zD)F=2zH(−z). Thus
Y1​=F+2cE0​+2dE1​+α+βerfz,
(14)
where the logarithmic overlaps fix
α=21​−4π​​+clogε−2cC1​,β=−21​+dlogε−2dC2​.
(15)
Here C1​,C2​ are the constants in the supplied large-positive-z limits of E0​,E1​. The terms εlogε are essential switchback terms; discarding them would not give accuracy through O(ε).
Subtracting the common overlap from the inner expansion and outer expansions gives the additive composite expansion
ycomp​(x)=Y0​(z)+εY1​(z)+2εr​(1−erfz),z=εx−1​.​
(16)
The standard overlap subtraction leaves εrH(−r). Replacing H(−r) by (1−erfz)/2 changes the value only by O(ε2) uniformly and gives a composite with continuous first derivative. The last term restores the endpoint values through the retained order. When b=a+1, d=0 and the order-one error-function jump disappears. An O(ε) interior adjustment remains to accommodate the leading outer derivative mismatch, together with logarithmic matching when A=0.
Reversing the diffusion sign changes the leading inner equation to −Y0′′​+2zY0′​=0. Its nonconstant solution grows like the integral of ez2, so bounded matching forces the same leading value C on both sides. The bulk is C+r on the left and C on the right; both endpoint conditions are instead supplied by decaying endpoint layers for reversed diffusion, of width ε2. A weaker width-ε interior adjustment matches derivatives and selects C. Indeed, its first-order equation has a non-growing solution only if
∫−∞∞​e−z2(2C+2zH(−z))dz=0,C=2π​1​.
(17)
The leading structure is consequently
y∼C+rH(−r)+(a−C+1)e−2x/ε2+(b−C)e−2(2−x)/ε2,
(18)
with the smaller interior correction understood. Unlike the positive-diffusion case, the endpoint values are not transported into two distinct order-one inner limits.

The first of the Gaussian drift primitives, E0​, is an even function; the second, E1​, is an odd function. Their derivatives follow by the fundamental theorem of calculus. In particular E0′​ is the Dawson function, which behaves as 1/(2z)+O(z−3). Since the error function tends to ±1, the derivative of E1​ behaves as 1/(2∣z∣)+O(∣z∣−3) at either end. Integration therefore gives
E0​(z)=21​log∣z∣+C1​+o(1),E1​(z)=sgnz[21​log∣z∣+C2​+o(1)].
(21)
Also F∼z+π​/4 on the left and F∼π​/4 on the right. The two inner overlaps are consequently
Y0​+εY1​∼{A+ε[z+Alog∣z∣+π​/4+2cC1​−2dC2​+α−β],B+ε[Blogz+π​/4+2cC1​+2dC2​+α+β],​z→−∞,z→+∞.​
(22)
Expressing the outer expansions in r=εz requires the left constant 1+Alogε and the right constant Blogε. Equating those constants gives the stated α,β. This is exactly how a logarithmic overlap creates a switchback term: log∣r∣=logε+log∣z∣. Adding the outer solutions and removing these overlaps leaves the displayed additive composite expansion.

First remove the small damping term by writing
y=e−(1−e−2εt)/4v.
(23)
The transformed linear ordinary differential equation is
vtt​+[e−2εt+ε2e−2εt−4ε2​e−4εt]v=0.
(24)
The leading frequency is ω=e−εt. Its WKB approximation has amplitude ω−1/2 and phase ∫0t​ωds=(1−e−εt)/ε. The initial conditions select
yWKB​=eεt/2−(1−e−2εt)/4sin(ε1−e−εt​).​
(25)
This leading expression has y(0)=0 and yt​(0)=1. Its WKB approximation for a slowly varying oscillator requires ∣ωt​∣/ω2=εeεt≪1, so it fails around t∗​=ε−1log(ε−1).
To resolve the Bessel transition for an exponentially decaying oscillator, shift to T=εt−log(ε−1), put Z=e−T and rescale y=ε−1/2e−1/4Y(T). The exact transformed equation is
YTT​+e−2TY+ε2e−2TYT​=0.
(26)
For fixed T its leading form is YTT​+e−2TY=0. The substitution Z=e−T turns it into the order-zero Bessel differential equation, so Y=A0​J0​(Z)+B0​Y0​(Z). In the overlap 1≪Z≪ε−1, matching the large-argument Bessel functions to Z−1/2sin(ε−1−Z) gives, with θ=ε−1−π/4,
y∼ε−1/2e−1/42π​​[sinθJ0​(Z)−cosθY0​(Z)].​
(27)
Here J0​ and Y0​ are the Bessel function of the first kind and Bessel function of the second kind; the symbol Y0​(Z) in this question is unrelated to the leading inner function in the preceding question.
At late times T≫1, the small-argument expansions give
y∼ε−1/2e−1/42π​​[sinθ+π2​cosθ(T+log2−γ)],​
(28)
where γ is the Euler--Mascheroni constant. The solution becomes asymptotically linear rather than maintaining the exponentially growing WKB envelope. Its leading late-time slope is ε1/2e−1/42/π​cosθ. These are leading asymptotic coefficients as ε→0: near a zero of cosθ, higher-order phase corrections determine the small actual slope. The formula is not an absolute-error estimate uniform to arbitrarily late times. The exact late slope of an exponentially damped oscillator, obtained from a Kummer function and a Wronskian, provides a separate check even near those exceptional phases.

The transition equation reduces to the Bessel differential equation because d/dT=−Zd/dZ and d2/dT2=Z2d2/dZ2+Zd/dZ. Thus YTT​+e−2TY=0 becomes Z2YZZ​+ZYZ​+Z2Y=0.
The Bessel function of the first kind has J0​(Z)=1+O(Z2) at zero, while the Bessel function of the second kind has Y0​(Z)=(2/π)(log(Z/2)+γ)+O(Z2∣logZ∣). Since logZ=−T, these yield a constant and a linear function of T, explaining the late-time behavior. At large positive Z,
J0​(Z)∼πZ2​​cos(Z−π/4),Y0​(Z)∼πZ2​​sin(Z−π/4).
(29)
Matching A0​cos(Z−π/4)+B0​sin(Z−π/4) to π/2​sin(ε−1−Z) fixes A0​=π/2​sinθ and B0​=−π/2​cosθ. The large- and small-argument asymptotic expansions thereby connect the oscillatory WKB approximation to the nonoscillatory late-time solution through one Bessel transition for an exponentially decaying oscillator.
