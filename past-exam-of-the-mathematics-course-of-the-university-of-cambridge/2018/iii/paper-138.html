Put q=pn. A finitely generated module over A=k[X]/(Xq) is finite-dimensional over k, and multiplication by X is a nilpotent linear map T with Tq=0. Its Jordan normal form exists over k because its minimal polynomial is a power of X. Each Jordan block has size r≤q and is the cyclic module Mr​=k[X]/(Xr). Hence
M≅⨁r=1q​Mr⊕mr​​.
(2)
A submodule of Mr​ is an ideal of k[X]/(Xr), and its inverse image is an ideal of the principal ideal domain k[X] containing (Xr). The only possibilities are (Xj) with 0≤j≤r. Thus all submodules form the chain
Mr​⊃XMr​⊃⋯⊃Xr−1Mr​⊃0.
(3)
Every successive quotient is the one-dimensional simple module on which X acts as zero. This is its unique composition series, so Mr​ is a uniserial module and is an indecomposable representation: two nonzero direct summands would be incomparable submodules. Only M1​ is simple.
For G=⟨g⟩ of order q, the group algebra satisfies
kG≅k[T]/(Tq−1)≅k[X]/(Xq),X=g−1,
(4)
since (T−1)q=Tq−1 in characteristic p. Consequently, up to isomorphism, M1​,…,Mpn​​ are precisely the finite-dimensional indecomposable modules, and M1​ is the unique simple module​. The finite-dimensional convention follows from the finite-generation hypothesis; “exactly” counts isomorphism classes.

The roots-of-unity criterion for a splitting field for finite group representations says that the assumed mth roots of unity make k a splitting field for G. Fix a multiplicative identification of the group μm​(k) with the complex mth roots of unity. For a p-regular element g, its order divides m; the operator ρ(g) is diagonalizable because X∣g∣−1 has distinct roots in k. If its eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity, are λ1​,…,λd​, define the Brauer character by
χV​(g)=∑i=1d​λi​,
(5)
where hats denote the chosen complex lifts. This defines a class function on the p-regular elements, not on arbitrary elements of G.
A short exact sequence can be represented by block triangular matrices, so the eigenvalue multiset is the union of those on its submodule and quotient. Thus Brauer characters are additive on short exact sequences. If S1​,…,St​ are the simple kG-modules, the Jordan–Hölder theorem gives
χV​=∑j=1t​[V:Sj​]χSj​​.
(6)
We use the standard Brauer–Nesbitt theorem in its character form: over a splitting field the Brauer characters of the nonisomorphic simple modules are linearly independent over C. Therefore
χV​=χV′​⟺[V:Sj​]=[V′:Sj​] for every j​.
(7)
Both the modular splitting-field criterion and this independence theorem are the representation-theoretic results used here.

c OurBigBook.com
A nonabelian simple group is a perfect group, so every one-dimensional group representation, a homomorphism to the abelian group k×, is trivial. Thus a nontrivial irreducible Brauer character cannot have degree 1.
Suppose instead that its representation has dimension 2, working over a splitting extension if necessary. Its determinant is a one-dimensional representation and is therefore trivial. Its kernel is a normal subgroup, and the representation is nontrivial, so simplicity makes it faithful. Consequently G embeds in SL2​(k).
We use the Feit–Thompson theorem: every finite group of odd order is solvable. Therefore this nonabelian simple group has even order, and Cauchy's theorem for finite groups supplies an involution t. In odd characteristic, its representing matrix A satisfies A2=I and is diagonalizable with eigenvalues in {1,−1}. Since detA=1, it is I or −I. Faithfulness excludes I, so t acts as the scalar matrix −I. It commutes with the whole image; faithfulness then makes t central in G, contradicting nonabelian simplicity. Hence χ(1)>2​.
The odd order theorem is the deep standard group-theoretic input in this proof; its use is explicit rather than hidden in an unsupported assertion that G has an involution.

The modular representation ring Rk​(G) is the abelian group generated by isomorphism classes [V] of finite-dimensional kG-modules, with relations
[V]=[U]+[W]whenever 0→U→V→W→0 is exact.
(11)
The Jordan–Hölder theorem says that it is a free abelian group with basis the classes of the simple modules, and [V]=∑S​[V:S][S]. To see independence, composition multiplicity for each fixed simple S is an additive map to Z and reads off its basis coefficient.
Define [V][W]=[V⊗k​W], using the diagonal action in the tensor product of group representations. Tensoring over a field is exact in either argument, so multiplication respects the defining relations. Associativity and the symmetry v⊗w↦w⊗v give a commutative ring with identity [k], the trivial representation. Thus Rk​(G) is a commutative ring, freely based on simple modules​.

An integral form of a group representation V is a G-stable finite free O-submodule W⊂V with K⊗O​W≅V. Such a form exists: take a basis lattice L and replace it by ∑g∈G​gL, which is finite and torsion-free and therefore free over the discrete valuation ring O.
Choose ordinary simple modules Vi​ with forms Wi​, and modular simple modules Sj​ with projective covers Pj​. With π a uniformizer, put Wi​=Wi​/πWi​. The decomposition matrix and Cartan matrix of a group algebra have entries
dij​=[Wi​:Sj​],cℓj​=[Pj​:Sℓ​].
(17)
The first numbers do not depend on the integral form: the Brauer character of its reduction is the ordinary character restricted to p-regular elements, and part 2(a) determines all composition multiplicities from this restriction.
For the assertion in (i), set I=HomOG​(W,W′). It is a submodule of the finite free module HomO​(W,W′), hence is finite free over O. There is a natural injective map

For the preliminary definitions, a block is Re for a primitive central idempotent e, and a module belongs to it when eM=M. For a semisimple algebra, the Artin–Wedderburn theorem identifies its blocks with the factors Mni​​(Di​) in its product decomposition. Each factor is a block of a finite-dimensional algebra.
We prove Ext separation of finite-length modules. The extension hypothesis is Ext1(S,T)=Ext1(T,S)=0 for simples in opposite sets. First, for a simple S∈C1​ and a module N of finite composition length with factors in C2​, we have Ext1(S,N)=0. Induct on the length of N: for 0→N′→N→T→0 with T simple, the long exact sequence of the Ext functor contains
Ext1(S,N′)⟶Ext1(S,N)⟶Ext1(S,T),
(26)
whose outer groups are zero. The same argument works with the two sets interchanged.
Now induct on the length of M, with M=0 immediate. Take a simple quotient in 0→N→M→S→0. By induction, N=N1​⊕N2​ with factors in the respective sets. Suppose S∈C1​; the other case is symmetric. Quotienting by N1​ gives
0⟶N2​⟶M/N1​⟶S⟶0.
(27)
This splits by the preceding Ext vanishing. Let U1​ be the inverse image in M of the chosen complementary copy of S. Then U1​∩N2​=0, U1​+N2​=M, and 0→N1​→U1​→S→0. Thus U1​ has only factors in C1​, while U2​=N2​ has only factors in C2​.
If two finite-length modules have factors in disjoint sets, any homomorphism between them is zero: a nonzero image would, by the Jordan–Hölder theorem, have a simple factor belonging to both sets. For any submodule V of M with factors in C1​, projection onto U2​ is consequently zero, so V⊂U1​. The analogous argument applies to U2​. Hence
M=U1​⊕U2​,Ui​ is the unique largest submodule with factors in Ci​​.
(28)
In particular both summands are preserved by every endomorphism of M.

We prove (i)⇒(iii), the main step in Ext-connected components determine blocks. Suppose a block of a finite-dimensional algebra B=Re had more than one component of the graph of nonsplit extensions between its simple modules. Partition its simple types into one component and the union of the others. There are no cross-extensions, so part (a) decomposes the left regular B-module as B=U1​⊕U2​. Write A=kG, N=NG​(D) and C=CG​(D). The Brauer morphism is the coefficient projection
BrDG​:AD⟶kC,∑g∈G​ag​g⟼∑g∈C​ag​g,
(32)
where the superscript D denotes invariance under conjugation. This is an algebra homomorphism: for c∈C, the pairs (x,y) with xy=c are permuted by D; their coefficient products are constant on each orbit. Every nonfixed orbit has size divisible by p and contributes zero. The fixed pairs are exactly x,y∈C, giving the coefficient of c in the product of the projections. It is surjective since kC⊂AD.
We need two trace facts. The kernel is
kerBrDG​=∑Q<D​TrQD​(AQ).
(33)
Indeed, a basis of AD consists of conjugation-orbit sums. Nonfixed orbit sums are traces from their proper stabilizers, while fixed basis elements are exactly the elements of C. Traces from proper subgroups have zero coefficient at every fixed element, proving the reverse inclusion. Second, Brauer morphism and relative trace gives
BrDG​(TrDG​(a))=TrDN​(BrDG​(a)).
(34)
One can see this by letting D act on the cosets G/D: fixed cosets are exactly N/D, and all other orbit contributions vanish after projection. More generally, BrEG​(TrDG​(AD))=0 unless E is conjugate into D.
Set ID​=TrDG​(AD) and JD​=TrDN​(kC)=(kC)DN​. The first is an ideal of the center of an associative algebra Z(A); the second is an ideal of (kC)N, which is commutative because C⊂N. The displayed trace identity and surjectivity of the Brauer projection give a surjective algebra homomorphism
BrDG​:ID​↠JD​.
(35)
The ideals can lack identities, so we justify the required idempotent argument. A finite-dimensional commutative algebra is a product of Artinian local rings. Its ideal intersects each local factor either in the whole factor, containing its identity, or in a proper nilpotent ideal. Under a surjection of such ideals, the proper parts have nilpotent image. Each nonzero image of a local-factor identity has a local corner algebra, a quotient of that factor, and is therefore primitive. If u is the sum of these nonzero images, then j−uj lies in the nilpotent image of the proper-factor ideals for every j in the target; an idempotent has j−uj=0. Its components in the local corners are consequently zero or the corner identities. These orthogonal images account for every primitive idempotent in the target, because what remains is nilpotent. Thus primitive idempotents under a surjection of commutative Artinian ideals apply: the primitive idempotents of JD​ correspond exactly to primitive central idempotents b∈ID​ with BrDG​(b)=0.
To identify these with the required blocks, we prove the trace criterion for defect groups. Choose a subgroup D0​ of smallest order with b∈ID0​​. Such a subgroup exists: for a Sylow p-subgroup P, b=TrPG​(b/[G:P]). If BrD0​G​(b)=0, write b=TrD0​G​(a) and replace a by ba. The kernel formula and transitivity of trace would give
b∈∑Q<D0​​IQ​.
(36)
Multiplying by b gives the identity of the local algebra bZ(A) in a sum of the ideals bIQ​. If all were proper, they would lie in its unique maximal ideal; hence some bIQ​ contains b, contradicting minimality. Therefore BrD0​G​(b)=0.
The general trace-vanishing assertion shows that any E with nonzero Brauer image is conjugate into D0​. Conversely, every conjugate of D0​ has nonzero image. Hence the maximal such subgroups, each called a defect group of a block, are precisely the conjugates of D0​. It also follows, using trace transitivity, that b∈ID​ if and only if a defect group is conjugate into D. Combining this with BrDG​(b)=0 forces equality of the subgroup orders, so the idempotents singled out above are exactly those with defect group D.
We have proved the desired bijection
b⟼BrDG​(b):{blocks of kG with defect D}⟶∼​Prim(JD​)​.
(37)
Finally, Brauer first main theorem states that these blocks correspond bijectively to the blocks of kNG​(D) with defect group D. Apply the same argument to N: its normalizer of D is itself and its centralizer of D is still C. The target ideal is therefore the same JD​. Matching the two bijections proves the theorem and gives the Brauer correspondence, characterized by equal nonzero Brauer images. Notice that we never assume the entire kernel on ID​ is nilpotent; it may kill whole blocks of smaller defect.

(39)
Thus b has the full Sylow defect P, contradicting maximality of D. These are the 2-modular defect groups of A5, and C2​ cannot be a defect group​.
