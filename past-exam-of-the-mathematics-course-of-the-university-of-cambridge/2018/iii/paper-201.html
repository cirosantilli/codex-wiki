For a discrete-time martingale (Mn​) and real a<b, let UN​[a,b] be the upcrossing count: buy on the first observation at or below a, sell on the next observation at or above b, and repeat up to time N, counting only completed pairs. Writing z−=max(−z,0), the Doob upcrossing inequality is
(b−a)EUN​[a,b]≤E(MN​−a)−.​
(1)
This convention permits the first purchase at time zero. Equivalently, the right side is E(MN​−a)+−E(M0​−a), because the martingale has constant expected value.
For completeness, let Hk​∈{0,1} indicate whether the trading rule holds one unit during (k−1,k]. This is a predictable process, so the martingale transform GN​=∑k=1N​Hk​(Mk​−Mk−1​) has zero expected value. Every completed trade gains at least b−a, and an unfinished trade loses at most (MN​−a)−. Thus GN​≥(b−a)UN​[a,b]−(MN​−a)−, giving the inequality.

The martingale convergence theorem says that if a discrete-time martingale (Mn​) satisfies C=supn​E∣Mn​∣<∞, then there is an integrable random variable M∞​ such that
Mn​⟶M∞​almost surely,E∣M∞​∣≤C.​
(2)
The hypothesis does not by itself ensure convergence in L1.
For each pair of rational numbers a<b, the Doob upcrossing inequality gives EUN​[a,b]≤(C+∣a∣)/(b−a). The monotone convergence theorem therefore gives a finite expected value for U∞​[a,b]=limN​UN​[a,b], so this upcrossing count is finite almost surely. There are only countably many such pairs, hence all their upcrossing counts are simultaneously finite outside one event of probability zero.
If liminfn​Mn​<limsupn​Mn​, the density of the rational numbers supplies a<b strictly between them, forcing infinitely many upcrossings. Consequently Mn​ has a limit in the extended real numbers almost surely. By the Fatou lemma,
E[liminfn​∣Mn​∣]≤liminfn​E∣Mn​∣≤C.
(3)
A limit of either +∞ or −∞ would make this lower limit infinite, so the limit is finite almost surely, and the same inequality proves its integrability.

Let ξ1​,ξ2​,… be independent and identically distributed random variables with the fair Bernoulli distribution, and put
M0​=1,Mn​=2n1{ξ1​=⋯=ξn​=1}​.
(6)
Relative to Fn​=σ(ξ1​,…,ξn​), this fair-coin doubling martingale satisfies E[Mn+1​∣Fn​]=Mn​: while alive it doubles with probability 1/2 and becomes zero otherwise. Its expected value is EMn​=2n2−n=1.
The probability of an infinite run of ones is limn​2−n=0, so Mn​ is eventually zero almost surely. However,
Mn​→0 almost surely,E∣Mn​−0∣=1 for every n.​
(7)
Thus the martingale lacks convergence in L1 to its limit from almost sure convergence. Nor can it have convergence in L1 to any other limit: convergence in L1 implies convergence in probability, whose limit is unique up to almost sure equality.

A sequence of integrable random variables (Xn​) has uniform integrability exactly when
K→∞lim​n≥0sup​E[∣Xn​∣1{∣Xn​∣>K}​]=0.​
(8)
The uniformly integrable martingale convergence theorem states that a uniformly integrable discrete-time martingale (Mn​) has an integrable random variable M∞​ with
Mn​→M∞​ almost surely and in L1,Mn​=E[M∞​∣Fn​].​
(9)
Indeed, uniform integrability implies supn​E∣Mn​∣<∞, so the martingale convergence theorem gives almost sure convergence. The combination of uniform integrability and almost sure convergence gives convergence in L1. For m≥n, the martingale identity E[Mm​∣Fn​]=Mn​ passes to the limit by the L1 contraction of conditional expectation.
Conversely, uniform integrability of conditional expectations shows that a martingale of the form Mn​=E[Y∣Fn​] for one integrable random variable Y is uniformly integrable.

The original PDF has E∣XT​∣<∞ and E[∣Xn​∣1{T>n}​]→0. The TeX transcription drops the expected value and absolute value in the first condition, and the absolute value in the second. The proof uses the PDF's conditions.
Because the stopping time T is finite almost surely, Xn∧T​→XT​ almost surely. More strongly,
E∣Xn∧T​−XT​∣=E[∣Xn​−XT​∣1{T>n}​]≤E[∣Xn​∣1{T>n}​]+E[∣XT​∣1{T>n}​]⟶0.
(10)
The first term tends to zero by hypothesis; the second does so by the dominated convergence theorem. Thus there is convergence in L1.
Here is the needed L1 convergence implies uniform integrability argument. For any integrable random variables Z,Y and K>0, splitting according to ∣Y∣>K/2 gives
E[∣Z∣1{∣Z∣>K}​]≤2E∣Z−Y∣+E[∣Y∣1{∣Y∣>K/2}​].
(11)
Take Z=Xn∧T​ and Y=XT​. For large n, the first term is uniformly small by convergence in L1, and the second is small for large K by integrability. The finitely many remaining n are handled individually by integrability. Hence the stopped process is uniformly integrable. This proves the stopped-martingale uniform integrability criterion; the martingale assumption is not needed for this particular implication.

Let Gn​=σ(Sn​,Sn+1​,…). Since Xn+j​=Sn+j​−Sn+j−1​,
Gn​=σ(Sn​,Xn+1​,Xn+2​,…).
(18)
Permuting X1​,…,Xn​ preserves their joint probability distribution and leaves every generator of Gn​ unchanged. Therefore, for each bounded Gn​-measurable H,
E[X1​H]=E[Xj​H](1≤j≤n).
(19)
This is the symmetry of exchangeable random variables. By the defining identity of conditional expectation, all E[Xj​∣Gn​] are equal almost surely. Summing these conditional expectations and using that Sn​ is Gn​-measurable gives
nE[X1​∣Gn​]=E[Sn​∣Gn​]=Sn​.
(20)
Hence the conditional expectation of a summand given future partial sums is
E[X1​∣Sn​,Sn+1​,…]=nSn​​almost surely.​
(21)
The integrability of each Xj​ justifies every conditional expectation above.

The sigma-algebras Gn​=σ(Sn​,Sn+1​,…) decrease with n, and (b) gives Sn​/n=E[X1​∣Gn​]. The permitted reverse martingale convergence theorem therefore gives an integrable random variable L such that Sn​/n→L almost surely and with convergence in L1. In particular,
EL=limn​E[Sn​/n]=m.
(22)
It remains to prove that L is constant. We must not assume that ⋂n​Gn​ is the tail sigma-algebra of the Xj​; instead use tail measurability of limits of sample averages directly. Define the random variable valued in the extended real numbers L∗=limsupn​Sn​/n. For every fixed N,
L∗=limsupn→∞​nXN+1​+⋯+Xn​​,
(23)
because SN​/n→0 pointwise. Thus L∗ is measurable with respect to σ(XN+1​,XN+2​,…) for every N, and hence to the tail sigma-algebra. Also L∗=L almost surely.
By the Kolmogorov zero-one law, each tail event {L∗≤q}, for a rational number q, has probability zero or one. Since L∗ is finite almost surely, its distribution function can only be that of a constant c: taking c=inf{q∈Q:P(L∗≤q)=1} and using the density of the rational numbers gives L∗=c almost surely. Its expected value identifies c=m. This proves the strong law of large numbers:
nSn​​⟶malmost surely.​
(24)

A standard one-dimensional Brownian motion is a real-valued stochastic process (Bt​)t≥0​ satisfying B0​=0 almost surely, having continuous paths almost surely, and having independent increments with
Bt​−Bs​∼N(0,t−s)(0≤s<t).​
(29)
Here N(0,t−s) is the normal distribution with zero expected value and variance t−s. Thus the increments are also stationary increments.
If Brownian motion is defined relative to a specified filtration (Ft​), additionally require that it is an adapted process and that Bt​−Bs​ is independent of Fs​. For its natural filtration, this follows from independent increments. These are the defining properties of standard Brownian motion.

For any finite list t1​,…,tk​∈[0,1], the random vector (Ut1​​,…,Utk​​,B1​) has a multivariate normal distribution, since it is a linear image of a vector of Brownian motion coordinates. The Brownian covariance kernel and linearity of covariance give
Cov(Ut​,B1​)=Cov(Bt​,B1​)−tVar(B1​)=t−t=0.
(30)

σ(Ut​:0≤t≤1) is independent of σ(B1​).​

Put Dt​=Bt​−Bt′​. By independence of the two Brownian motions, Dt​/2​ is a standard Brownian motion: its independent increments have the normal distribution with variance equal to elapsed time. The stopping time τ is the first time D hits −1 or 3.
To justify finiteness without presupposing the desired hitting probability, each unit-time increment of D has the normal distribution N(0,2). P(Bτ​=Bτ′​−1)=43​.​

Fix an integer k≥1 and consider the independent increments
Δj,k​=B(j+1)/k​−Bj/k​,j=0,1,2,….
(33)
For fixed k these are independent and identically distributed random variables with the normal distribution N(0,1/k), so pk​=P(∣Δj,k​∣>1)>0. The second Borel-Cantelli lemma gives ∣Δj,k​∣>1 for infinitely many j, almost surely. Intersecting these probability-one events over the countably many k preserves probability one.
On any path in this intersection, uniform continuity on [0,∞) would give some δ>0 such that ∣Bt​−Bs​∣<1 whenever ∣t−s∣<δ. Choosing k with 1/k<δ contradicts the existence of an increment ∣Δj,k​∣>1. Therefore Brownian paths are not uniformly continuous on the half-line:
P(B is uniformly continuous on [0,∞))=0.​
(34)
The event is measurable: for continuous paths, uniform continuity can be tested by countably many pairs of nonnegative rational numbers and tolerances 1/m. Brownian motion has uniform continuity on every compact time interval, but almost surely fails it on the whole half-line.

For a stopping time T of a Brownian filtration, define the reflected stochastic process
Bt​={Bt​,2BT​−Bt​,​t≤T,t>T.​​
(35)
The Brownian reflection at a stopping time form of the Brownian reflection principle states that B is again standard Brownian motion. If T=∞, leave the path unchanged; the second branch is used only when T<t.
For an almost surely finite T, the Strong Markov property says that (BT+s​−BT​)s≥0​ is a fresh Brownian motion independent of FT​. Its negative has the same probability distribution, so reflecting the future preserves the full path probability distribution. Allowing T=∞ follows by applying this argument to T∧n and restricting to each fixed finite time interval.

Let Ty​=inf{s≥0:Bs​=y}. Reflect at this stopping time, using the Brownian reflection principle from (a). Path continuity gives {St​≥y}={Ty​≤t}, and on this event the reflected endpoint is Bt​=2y−Bt​. Consequently

The Lévy continuity theorem states that if characteristic functions φn​ of probability distributions μn​ converge pointwise on R to φ, and φ is continuous at zero, then φ is the characteristic function of a unique probability distribution μ and
μn​⇒μ.​
(44)
Here the arrow denotes weak convergence of probability measures, equivalently convergence in distribution of associated random variables. Conversely, weak convergence of probability measures implies pointwise convergence of their characteristic functions to the characteristic function of the limit. The continuity-at-zero condition is essential to ensure that the pointwise limit is a characteristic function.

Suppose Xn​→X with convergence in probability. For real t and δ>0, the elementary inequality ∣eiu−eiv∣≤min(2,∣u−v∣) gives

Let Fn​ be the distribution function of Xn​. The limiting constant zero has distribution function F(x)=0 for x<0 and F(x)=1 for x≥0. For every ε>0, both −ε and ε are continuity points of F. Thus convergence in distribution implies Fn​(−ε)→0 and Fn​(ε)→1. Therefore
0≤P(∣Xn​∣>ε)≤Fn​(−ε)+1−Fn​(ε)⟶0.
