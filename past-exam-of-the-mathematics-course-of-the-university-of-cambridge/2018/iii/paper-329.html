Write r=∣x∣ and take μ to be the dynamic viscosity. The Papkovich–Neuber representation of a homogeneous Stokes flow uses a harmonic vector potential Φ and harmonic scalar potential χ:
2μu=∇(x⋅Φ+χ)−2Φ,p=∇⋅Φ,∇2Φ=0,∇2χ=0.​
(1)
These formulas give incompressibility because ∇2(x⋅Φ)=2∇⋅Φ, and give μ∇2u=∇p, the Stokes equation away from forcing.
For a point force represented by a Dirac delta distribution, rotational covariance and the Linearity of Stokes flow suggest a vector monopole proportional to F/r: 1/r is a harmonic function outside the origin, and its slow decay gives the required nonzero resultant force. Take
Φ=−4πrF​,χ=0.
(2)
Substitution gives the Stokeslet velocity and pressure:
u=8πμ1​[rF​+r3(F⋅x)x​],p=4πr3F⋅x​.​
(3)
The normalization follows from the Newtonian fluid stress tensor. Differentiating the velocity gives the rate of strain and vorticity of a Stokeslet:
eij​=8πμr3F⋅x​(δij​−3r2xi​xj​​),ω=∇×u=4πμr3F×x​.​
(4)
Here eij​=(∂i​uj​+∂j​ui​)/2. Thus σij​=−pδij​+2μeij​=−3(F⋅x)xi​xj​/(4πr5). On a sphere enclosing the origin, its outward traction integrates to −F, so ∇⋅σ+Fδ(x)=0 in the distributional sense. All displayed fields away from the point force have r>0.
For a sphere in a uniform straining Stokes flow, centre the sphere at the origin and write the ambient velocity as Ex, with ET=E and trE=0. Faxén's first law and Faxén's rotational law give zero translation and rotation. To impose the no-slip boundary condition, use the decaying harmonic dipole and quadrupole potentials
Φ=Ar3Ex​,χ=Br5Q​,Q=E:xx.
(5)
Trace freedom makes Q/r5 harmonic, and each component of Ex/r3 is harmonic. The Papkovich–Neuber representation gives
2μu′=−3Ar5Qx​+B[r52Ex​−r75Qx​].
(6)
At r=a, the coefficient of Ex must give u′=−Ex, while the extra radial term must vanish. Hence B=−μa5 and A=5μa3/3, giving the complete disturbance
u′=−r5a5​Ex−2r55a3​(1−r2a2​)Qx,p′=−r55μa3​Q.​
(7)
The total velocity vanishes on the sphere and tends to the imposed strain at infinity. This field has no Stokeslet or rotlet contribution, consistent with a force-free, torque-free sphere. Its leading far field is the radial stresslet −5a3Qx/(2r5).
Now put the first sphere at 0 and the second at R, where R≫a, and let U0​=F/(6πμa). The first sphere's leading disturbance is its Stokeslet. The rate-of-strain tensor and vorticity incident on the second sphere are
E2​=4R33a​(U0​⋅R)(I−3RR),ω2​=2R33a​U0​×R.
(8)
The second sphere translates with the local flow and rotates with half its local vorticity. Its force-free and torque-free conditions therefore remove any reflected force or torque monopole. The incident strain produces the stresslet just obtained. Since
E2​:RR=−2R3a​(U0​⋅R),
(9)
its velocity at the first centre, using the relative vector −R, is
u2′​(−R)=2R55a3​(E2​:RR)R=−4R615a4​(U0​⋅R)R.
(10)
Faxén's first law then gives the self-mobility correction from a distant force-free sphere:
U=U0​−4R615a4​(U0​⋅R)R+O(U0​a5/R5).​
(11)
Finite-size terms and further reflections are beyond the leading order retained in this method of reflections for Stokes flow.
Only the imposed force does work, so the viscous dissipation is D=F⋅U. With ζ=6πμa, define εR​=a/R and γ=15a4/(4R6). At fixed F,
D=ζ[U02​−γ(U0​⋅R)2]+O(ζU02​εR5​),
(12)
so the leading change is negative when U0​⋅R=0. The direction is held fixed in taking εR​→0.
The fixed-force comparison of minimum viscous dissipation distinguishes the prescribed force from prescribed boundary velocity. Indeed,
U2=U02​−2γ(U0​⋅R)2+O(U02​εR5​),
(13)
and hence
D−ζU2=ζγ(U0​⋅R)2+O(ζU02​εR5​)>0to leading order.​
(14)
At the actual reduced velocity, adding the second sphere increases the dissipation relative to an isolated translating sphere at that same velocity, exactly as required by the Minimum-dissipation theorem for Stokes flow. One can extend the two-sphere velocity rigidly into the second sphere, adding zero strain and producing an admissible trial field in the domain without it. The reflected angular velocity of the first sphere contributes only a higher-order squared rotational power to this comparison.
Finally hold the second sphere's angular velocity at zero. Faxén's rotational law requires the applied couple
G2​=−4πμa3ω2​=−R36πμa4​U0​×R.
(15)
The resulting rotlet adds at the first centre
δUrot​=8πμR3G2​×(−R)​=−4R43a4​[U0​−(U0​⋅R)R].
(16)
This is the additional rotational constraint correction to sphere mobility. Including the strain reflection, the total leading change relative to the isolated velocity is
Ulocked​−U0​=−4R43a4​[U0​+4(U0​⋅R)R]+O(U0​a5/R5).​
(17)
For motion parallel to the line of centres the constraint adds nothing at this order; for transverse motion it supplies an extra resisting correction.

Take F,G to be the force and couple exerted by the body on the fluid, equivalently the external force and couple needed to maintain its motion. This fixes the sign for a positive hydrodynamic resistance matrix. If one uses the fluid's force on the body, both resultants have the opposite sign.
For two Stokes flows (u,σ) and (u,σ) in the same fluid domain D, with zero body force, the Lorentz reciprocal theorem for Stokes flow is
∫∂D​u⋅σndS=∫∂D​u⋅σndS.​
(18)
Here n points out of the fluid. To see the identity, take the divergence of the difference of the two cross-work fluxes. The stress divergences vanish, while incompressibility and symmetry of the Newtonian fluid stress tensor reduce the remaining terms to 2μ(e:e−e:e)=0. The divergence theorem proves the result.
On the body, the no-slip boundary condition is u=U+Ω×r, and the far-field contribution vanishes for decaying exterior flows. Reciprocity becomes
U⋅F+Ω⋅G=U⋅F+Ω⋅G.
(19)
With q=(U,Ω)T and (F,G)T=Rq, this is qTRq​=q​TRq for all pairs, so RT=R. The power identity gives
qTRq=F⋅U+G⋅Ω=2μ∫D​e:edV≥0.
(20)
Equality would force zero strain throughout the connected exterior domain, hence a rigid fluid motion. Decay at infinity makes that motion zero, and the no-slip boundary condition then gives q=0. This proves the symmetry and positivity of a rigid-body resistance matrix:
RT=R,qTRq>0 for q=0.​
(21)
For the helix, assume 0<ϕ<π/2 and use cylindrical unit vectors. Its arclength element and tangent are
dθds​=​dθdX​​=bsecϕ,t=dsdX​=cosϕeθ​+sinϕez​.
(22)
Integrating to θ=Lcosϕ/b gives total arc length=L​. In a combined axial translation and rotation, V=Uez​+bΩeθ​. The slender-body force density, or local resistive-force theory, gives
f=C[V−21​t(t⋅V)],C=∣logϵ∣4πμ​.
(23)
Writing sϕ​=sinϕ, cϕ​=cosϕ, its axial and azimuthal components are
fz​=C[(1−sϕ2​/2)U−bsϕ​cϕ​Ω/2],fθ​=C[b(1−cϕ2​/2)Ω−sϕ​cϕ​U/2].
(24)
The axial couple density is bfθ​. Integration over arclength gives the axial resistance matrix of a slender helix:
(Fz​Gz​​)=(AB​BD​)(UΩ​),A=2CL​(1+cϕ2​),B=−2CLb​sϕ​cϕ​,D=2CLb2​(1+sϕ2​).​
(25)
Thus pure translation gives (Fz​,Gz​)=(AU,BU), and pure rotation gives (Fz​,Gz​)=(BΩ,DΩ). The mixed coefficients agree, as reciprocity requires, and AD−B2=C2L2b2/2>0. The sign of B follows the handedness in the parametrization; reversing handedness reverses propulsion.
For the helical microswimmer with a spherical head, the diagram's rotation relation is Ω0​=Ω−ω. Neutral buoyancy and the absence of external forcing make the whole swimmer force-free and torque-free. Neglecting interactions between its parts gives
(A0​+A)U+BΩ=0,BU+DΩ+D0​(Ω−ω)=0.
(26)
With Δ=(A0​+A)(D0​+D)−B2>0, solving this pair gives
U=−ΔBD0​ω​,Ω=Δ(A0​+A)D0​ω​,Ω0​=−Δ(A0​+A)D−B2​ω.​
(27)
The head counterrotates, providing the reaction to the flagellum's rotation. The figure fixes this relative rotation convention; it is duplicated and corrupted in the local TeX.
For a very large head, the coefficient regime is A0​≫A and D0​≫D. Consequently
U≃−A0​Bω​,Ω≃ω(a≫L).​
(28)
The large translational resistance A0​ makes the microswimmer slow even though the flagellum rotates almost at the motor rate.
For a very small head, A0​≪A and D0​≪D. The appropriate denominator retains the translation-rotation coupling:
U≃−AD−B2BD0​ω​,Ω≃AD−B2AD0​ω​(a≪(Lb2)1/3).​
(29)
Both tend to zero with D0​∝a3, while Ω0​≃−ω. The motor mainly rotates the low-resistance head, and produces little flagellar motion relative to the fluid.
In the intermediate coefficient regime A0​≪A and D0​≫D, the head supplies a strong rotational reaction with little translational penalty. Then
U≃−ABω​=ωb1+cos2ϕsinϕcosϕ​,Ω≃ω.​
(30)
The head radius has dropped out. To find the optimal pitch of a helical microswimmer, put z=tanϕ. The dimensionless speed becomes z/(z2+2) and has derivative (2−z2)/(z2+2)2. Hence, for ω>0 with the chosen handedness,
tanϕopt​=2​,ϕopt​≃54.74∘,Umax​=22​ωb​.​
(31)
The angle is measured from the horizontal plane. An intermediate pitch combines axial and transverse tangent directions, allowing anisotropic drag to convert rotation into translation; either extreme removes that coupling.
The geometric head-size regimes quoted in the paper hold with the logarithmic slenderness factor treated as fixed. More precisely, A0​≪A requires a≪L(1+cos2ϕ)/(3∣logϵ∣), while D0​≫D requires a3≫Lb2(1+sin2ϕ)/(4∣logϵ∣). These coefficient inequalities state the validity of the intermediate approximation when the logarithm is quantitatively important. The many-turn and local-drag assumptions of slender-body theory also remain in force.

Let z measure distance normal to the plane, with the wall at z=0 and the free surface at z=h. Take density ρ, dynamic viscosity μ, and gravitational acceleration g. In lubrication theory, normal momentum balance gives hydrostatic pressure while inertia and streamwise viscous derivatives are smaller. With negligible surface tension and negligible air shear,
p=patm​+ρgcosα(h−z),μuzz​=ρg(cosαhx​−sinα),μvzz​=ρgcosαhy​.
(32)
Apply the no-slip boundary condition u=v=0 at z=0, and zero leading tangential stress uz​=vz​=0 at z=h. The velocity profiles are
u=μρg​(sinα−cosαhx​)(hz−z2/2),v=−μρg​cosαhy​(hz−z2/2).
(33)
Depth integration gives the volume flux, with K=ρg/(3μ):
qx​=Kh3(sinα−cosαhx​),qy​=−Kh3cosαhy​.
(34)
Combining incompressibility with the wall and surface kinematic conditions gives conservation of mass, ht​+∂x​qx​+∂y​qy​=0. Therefore the two-dimensional version of the gravity-driven thin film on an incline satisfies
ht​+Ksinα∂x​(h3)=Kcosα[∂x​(h3hx​)+∂y​(h3hy​)].​
(35)
Direct downslope gravity supplies the advective term, while gradients of the hydrostatic pressure spread the film along the surface. At α=0 this reduces to nonlinear gravitational spreading in a horizontal film.
