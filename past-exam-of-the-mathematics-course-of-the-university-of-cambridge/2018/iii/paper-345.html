Let p denote the fluid pressure perturbation divided by the reference mass density, and let b=−gρ′/ρ0​ be the buoyancy perturbation. The buoyancy frequency satisfies N2=−gρ^​z​/ρ0​. Linearization requires small boundary slope k∣η0​∣≪1, small displacements compared with the vertical scales of the disturbance and background, and advection small compared with the oscillatory acceleration. For a propagating internal gravity wave this includes ∣mη0​∣≪1; boundary slope alone is insufficient when ∣m∣≫k. The Boussinesq approximation also requires small relative mass density differences over the region of interest. These conditions must hold for the resulting disturbance, including any amplification by resonance.
The inviscid Linearized Boussinesq equations and linear kinematic boundary condition are
ut​=−px​,wt​=−pz​+b,bt​+N2w=0,ux​+wz​=0,W(0)=−iωη0​.
(1)
Write the velocity field as (u,w)=Re{(U(z),W(z))ei(kx−ωt)}. Incompressibility and horizontal momentum balance give
U=ki​W′,P=k2iω​W′,B=−ωiN2​W.
(2)
The vertical momentum balance therefore yields
W′′+k2(ω2N2​−1)W=0.
(3)
For a real vertical wavenumber m, this gives the dispersion relation
ω2=k2+m2N2k2​​.
(4)
When 0<ω<N, define ℓ=kN2/ω2−1​. The radiation condition selects m=−ℓ, because energy must leave the boundary upward. The boundary-forced internal gravity wave is
W=−iωη0​e−iℓz,U=kℓ​W​.
(5)
Its wavevector is K=(k,m), its phase velocity is cp​=ωK/(k2+m2), and its group velocity is
cg​=((k2+m2)3/2Nm2​,−(k2+m2)3/2Nkm​).
(6)
Thus the phase velocity points down and right, while the group velocity points up and right. They are perpendicular. Internal-wave polarization makes the particle motion an oscillation along the group velocity direction, with zero first-order mean transport. The constant-phase lines of an internal gravity wave are also parallel to the group velocity. Their inclination ϑ above the horizontal satisfies sinϑ=ω/N.
When ω>N, set κ=k1−N2/ω2​. Boundedness at infinity selects the evanescent wave
W=−iωη0​e−κz,U=−kiκ​W​.
(7)
The horizontal and vertical velocity components are in quadrature: fluid particles describe small ellipses, and the response decays over κ−1. There is no upward time-averaged energy flux, because P and W are in quadrature. A real vertical group velocity is not defined for this evanescent wave. The pattern travels horizontally with phase velocity ω/k.
For N=0 the response is the evanescent wave with κ=k; the ratios ω/N should not be used. At the cutoff ω=N>0, the bounded harmonic solution has W=−iωη0​, U=0: it neither decays nor has nonzero upward group velocity. It is the limiting cutoff response, rather than a localized radiating disturbance. A uniform nonzero buoyancy frequency in an infinitely deep Boussinesq approximation is itself a local idealization of the background mass density.

The central stratified layer supports upward and downward internal gravity waves, whose counterpropagating components form a standing wave. The unstratified regions support evanescent waves, and the upper disturbance decays as e−k(z−2H). Consequently the central layer is a stratified internal-wave guide, rather than a source of propagating energy at infinity.
The largest response occurs at its trapped normal mode frequencies. They can be specified without solving the forced problem. Set the boundary forcing to zero to find a free normal mode. The lower solution is proportional to sinh(kz), so continuity of fluid pressure and vertical velocity gives
W′(H)=aW(H),a=kcoth(kH),W′(2H)=−kW(2H).
(13)
In the central layer write
W∝sin(ℓ(z−H)+δ),δ=arctan(ℓ/a),ℓ=kN22​/ω2−1​.
(14)
The upper Robin boundary condition then gives the exact trapped-mode condition
ℓn​H+arctan(ℓn​/a)+arctan(ℓn​/k)=nπ,ωn​=k2+ℓn2​​N2​k​,n=1,2,…​.
(15)
The left side increases strictly from zero to infinity, so there is one positive root for each n; the frequencies accumulate at zero. This is constructive phase matching after reflection at both ends. The phase shifts from the evanescent waves matter: simply imposing integer half-wavelengths across the stratified layer is generally incorrect.
In the ideal inviscid model, exact resonant forcing has no bounded steady harmonic solution. The undamped normal mode grows secularly under sustained forcing. Weak viscosity or other losses would produce large finite peaks near the displayed frequencies; the linear approximation eventually fails if the disturbance becomes too large.
The illustrated free normal mode has an oscillatory central region and evanescent wave tails. Its lower tail reaches the fixed zero-displacement boundary; its upper tail decays to infinity.

Q=ρ0​πb2V,M=ρ0​πb2V2(cosθ,sinθ),F=g(ρ0​−ρ)πb2V​.
(33)

q=Q/ρ0​,m=M/ρ0​,f=F/ρ0​,m=∣m∣=πb2V2.
(34)
If the symbols Q,M,F instead denote the customary kinematic fluxes, simply omit the factors ρ0​ in all conversions below. Keeping the two conventions consistent is essential for the jet length and dimensional prefactors.
The Batchelor entrainment hypothesis prescribes inward edge velocity
ue​=αV​.
(35)
The constant entrainment coefficient relates ambient inflow to the local axial velocity. A centreline segment of arc length ds entrains volume 2πbue​ds. Hence, setting E=2απ​,
dsdq​=2παbV=Em​,dsdf​=0​,dsdQ​=2απρ0​∣M∣​,dsdF​=0.

The buoyancy flux is conserved because the homogeneous entrained ambient has zero density deficit, and mixing has no buoyancy source or sink. The Exner equation is solid-volume mass conservation: a downstream increase in sediment transport flux removes material and lowers the bed. The factor ϕb​ converts bed-height change into solid-volume change. The saturation length equation models downstream adjustment of actual sediment transport to the equilibrium sediment flux. Grain acceleration and entrainment/deposition need a finite distance, so q lags qsat​. The last relation is an empirical transport law above the sediment entrainment threshold, with χ>0 and normally γ>0; below threshold it must be interpreted with a positive part rather than raising negative excess stress to an arbitrary power. It is a constitutive closure, not a consequence of mass conservation.
Take the physical parameters Lsat​>0, ϕb​>0, χ>0 and γ>0. Linearize about a horizontal, uniformly transporting bed with Δ=τ0​−τth,0​>0 and q0​=ϕb​χΔγ. Set λ=σ−ikc and use the real parts of
η=η^​eλt+ikx,q=q0​+q^​eλt+ikx,qsat​=q0​+q^​sat​eλt+ikx,τ=τ0​+τ^eλt+ikx.
(57)
The locally planar approximation uses the small instantaneous bed slope to compute the inclined-bed sediment threshold. Since α=ηx​+O(ηx3​),
τ^th​=μs​ikτth,0​​η^​​.
(58)
A sinusoidal disturbance has both uphill and downhill slopes: the printed 0<α≪1 describes the uphill derivation, and its first-order continuation applies to both signs. The zero-slope reference is the one implicit in the printed decomposition with constant term τth,0​; a finite mean inclined bed would require a shifted base threshold.
Define C=χγΔγ−1. The linearized transport equations are
ϕb​λη^​=−ikq^​,(1+ikLsat​)q^​=q^​sat​,q^​sat​=ϕb​C(τ^−μs​ikτth,0​​η^​)​.
(59)
The linearization requires ∣ηx​∣≪1 and ∣τ^−τ^th​∣≪Δ.
A fluid-dynamical shear-response closure is missing from the printed question. The three sediment equations and local slope correction do not determine τ^ from η^​. Write the general bed shear response as τ^=T(k)η^​. A frequently used scale-invariant closure for k>0 is

Introducing them explicitly makes the linear stability analysis complete conditional on a specified flow response; it does not turn them into data supplied by the question. An example of this hydrodynamic closure is given in Fourrière, Claudin and Andreotti's bedform-instability analysis.

(63)
The packed-bed volume fraction cancels. For a real physical closure the negative wavenumber response is the complex conjugate; the displayed k>0 formulas cover the independent normal modes.
If A>0, B>0 are treated as constants, the upstream shear stress phase lead promotes growth, while the inclined-bed sediment threshold reduces it and the finite saturation length delays sediment transport. There is an unstable band precisely when b>0: 0<k<b/(aLsat​). Within this band c>0, so the growing bedforms migrate downstream. The fastest normal mode satisfies
(kmax​Lsat​)3+3kmax​Lsat​=2b/a​.
(64)
This last maximization assumes constant A,B; if the hydrodynamic coefficients vary with wavenumber, their derivatives also enter the selection condition. When b≤0 and a>0, all nonzero normal modes decay.
Just above the sediment entrainment threshold,
b≃τth,0​(B−1/μs​).
(65)
Thus the common regime B<1/μs​ is stable near onset: the slope penalty overwhelms the hydrodynamic phase lead. This conclusion is conditional on that inequality, not universal. For constant B>0, instability begins only when τ0​>τth,0​/(μs​B) as well as τ0​>τth,0​. The transport sensitivity C=χγΔγ−1 vanishes at onset for γ>1, is finite for γ=1, and is singular for 0<γ<1. Exactly at threshold, the positive-part transport law needs separate treatment; the above linearization with perturbations small compared with Δ is unavailable.
Far above the sediment entrainment threshold, b≃τ0​B. If A,B>0, the unstable cutoff tends to B/(ALsat​), and the dominant wavelength is set by the saturation length. Both growth rate and migration velocity scale with Cτ0​∼χγτ0γ​, apart from their length factors. Their numerical values and any detailed dependence on wavenumber still require a flow closure.
For example, prescribing uniform shear stress independently of the bed gives T=0 and

(66)
This equally admissible closure illustrates why instability cannot be asserted from the printed sediment equations alone.
