The tangent bundle is TM=⨆p∈M​Tp​M, with its smooth vector-bundle structure from coordinate charts. A vector field is a smooth section of this bundle. The Lie bracket of vector fields is their commutator as derivations:
[X,Y](h)=X(Yh)−Y(Xh).
(1)
In coordinates, [X,Y]k=Xj∂j​Yk−Yj∂j​Xk.
The four numbered conditions here are hypotheses of one argument, so the following sections assemble its proof. Let j:∂M↪M. Because all Xi​ are tangent to the boundary, evaluating η=ιX1​​⋯ιXn​​ω on a tangent vector Y there evaluates the volume form on n+1 vectors in the n-dimensional boundary tangent space. Alternation gives j∗η=0​. This uses the interior product of a differential form and will make the boundary restriction of the de Rham cohomology class vanish.

Let Φt​,Ψs​ be the local flows of X,Y. The derivative of the pullback of Y by Φt​ is Φt∗​[X,Y]. Therefore [X,Y]=0 makes Y invariant under the X flow. For fixed t, the two curves Φt​(Ψs​(p)) and Ψs​(Φt​(p)) then solve the same initial-value problem for Y. Uniqueness gives commuting flows wherever both compositions are defined. Conversely, commutation implies invariance of Y under Φt​, and differentiation at t=0 gives [X,Y]=0​. This proves vanishing Lie bracket is equivalent to commuting local flows.
For the contraction η, commute each Lie derivative past the other interior products using [LXi​​,ιXj​​]=ι[Xi​,Xj​]​=0. Each LXi​​ω is zero by volume preservation. Cartan's magic formula dιX​=LX​−ιX​d, applied successively, now gives
dη=∑i=1n​(−1)i−1ιX1​​⋯ιXi​​​⋯ιXn​​LXi​​ω+(−1)nιX1​​⋯ιXn​​dω=0.
(3)
The last term vanishes since ω has top degree. Hence η is closed​.

By the preceding sections, η is a nowhere-zero closed differential form and j∗η=0. Injectivity of j∗:HdR1​(M)→HdR1​(∂M) gives [η]=0. The definition of de Rham cohomology then gives
η=df​
(4)
for a smooth f, with no critical points. If the boundary were connected, j∗df=0 would make f constant there. A nonconstant function on a compact manifold attains both extrema; a value differing from the common boundary value gives an interior extremum, contradicting df=0. If f were constant everywhere there would be the same contradiction. Empty boundary is also impossible, because an extremum would necessarily be interior. Thus ∂M has at least two connected components​, the boundary obstruction for commuting volume-preserving vector fields.
The solid torus S1×D2 retracts onto its first circle. Its degree-one de Rham cohomology is generated by that circle's angular one-form, which restricts to a nonzero class on S1×S1; hence the restriction map is injective. Its boundary is connected. Extensions of the two specified coordinate vector fields would be tangent there and would satisfy all the forbidden conditions. Therefore no such pair of extensions exists, for any volume form.

A smooth rank-r smooth distribution D⊂TM is involutive distribution if the Lie bracket of vector fields of two local sections remains a section. It is an integrable distribution if every point lies on an immersed r-dimensional integral manifold with tangent spaces equal to D. The Frobenius theorem says these conditions are equivalent, and gives local coordinates with D=span(∂1​,…,∂r​).
For necessity, fields tangent to an integral manifold have brackets tangent to it: they annihilate functions vanishing on the manifold, and so does their commutator. For sufficiency, induct on r. The rank-zero case is immediate. Straighten a nonvanishing local section using the flow-box theorem to obtain ∂1​∈D. Choose a frame ∂1​,Y2​,…,Yr​ with the Yi​ having no ∂1​ component. Involutivity gives ∂1​Y=AY for the column of these fields and a smooth matrix A. Solve the matrix ordinary differential equation ∂1​B=−BA, with B=I on x1​=0. It stays invertible, and Z=BY satisfies ∂1​Z=0. On the transverse slice, the Zi​ span an involutive rank-(r−1) distribution. The induction hypothesis supplies adapted slice coordinates; extend them independently of x1​. These give the required rank-r coordinate distribution and its integral manifolds.
For the real Heisenberg group, multiplication is
(x,y,z)(a,b,c)=(x+a,y+b,z+c+xb).
(5)
Differentiating left translation at the identity gives its left-invariant frame of the real Heisenberg group:
E1​=∂x​,E2​=∂y​+x∂z​,E3​=∂z​.​
(6)
Then [E1​,E2​]=E3​ and the other basis brackets vanish. Since E3​∈/span(E1​,E2​), the Heisenberg horizontal distribution is not involutive, hence not integrable by the Frobenius theorem.

In coordinates, expanding dα and the Lie bracket of vector fields shows
dα(X,Y)=X(α(Y))−Y(α(X))−α([X,Y]).
(12)
The terms differentiating components of X,Y cancel against the bracket, leaving (∂i​αj​−∂j​αi​)XiYj. This is the exterior derivative of a one-form evaluated on vector fields.
A connection on a vector bundle is a linear map ∇:Γ(E)→Ω1(E) satisfying ∇(fs)=df⊗s+f∇s; evaluation on X defines ∇X​s. Its curvature form of a connection is dA2​, locally FA​=dA+A∧A. Direct expansion, using the displayed exterior-derivative identity, gives
FA​(X,Y)s=∇X​∇Y​s−∇Y​∇X​s−∇[X,Y]​s.​
(13)
The derivative terms on a scalar multiplying s cancel, and the expression is also linear over smooth functions in X,Y. Thus it is an alternating tensor with values in End(E), namely an element of Ω2(End(E)).
The dual connection and tensor product connection induce the endomorphism bundle connection on E∗⊗E:
(∇X​ϕ)(s)=∇X​(ϕs)−ϕ(∇X​s).
(14)
Expanding twice cancels the cross terms and gives
([∇X​,∇Y​]ϕ)(s)=[∇X​,∇Y​](ϕs)−ϕ([∇X​,∇Y​]s).
(15)
Here ϕ,s are local smooth sections; derivatives are not defined for isolated fiber elements without extensions. Subtract the corresponding ∇[X,Y]​ identity to get the curvature of an endomorphism bundle connection:
FEnd(A)​(X,Y)ϕ=[FA​(X,Y),ϕ].​
(16)
This vanishes for all ϕ exactly when each FA​(X,Y) is central in the full matrix algebra, hence scalar. For rank r>0, ω=r−1trFA​ is a smooth two-form and
FEnd(A)​=0⟺FA​=ωidE​.​
(17)
Conversely scalar curvature commutes with every endomorphism. This is the scalar-curvature criterion for a flat endomorphism connection; it permits a nonflat connection on E itself.

Use the energy of a curve normalization
E(γ)=21​∫ab​∣γ˙​∣g2​dt.
(18)
For a variation F(s,t) put T=∂t​F and V=∂s​F. The Levi-Civita connection is torsion free, so ∇s​T=∇t​V. Differentiating energy once gives E′=[⟨V,T⟩]ab​−∫⟨V,∇t​T⟩. Along a geodesic, ∇t​T=0. Differentiating again, commuting covariant derivatives, and integrating by parts gives the second variation of geodesic energy:
E′′(0)=[⟨∇s​V,T⟩]ab​+∫ab​(∣∇t​V∣2−⟨R(V,T)T,V⟩)dt.​
(19)
The curvature convention is R(X,Y)Z=∇X​∇Y​Z−∇Y​∇X​Z−∇[X,Y]​Z, consistent with the specified positive sectional curvature. Indeed ∇s​∇t​V=∇t​∇s​V+R(V,T)V, and ⟨R(V,T)V,T⟩=−⟨R(V,T)T,V⟩. The endpoint term vanishes for fixed endpoints or for periodic variations of a closed geodesic. The integral is the Riemannian index form I(V,V).
Let dimM=2m. Parallel transport around the closed geodesic preserves the metric and orientation, so lies in the special orthogonal group. It fixes the nonzero tangent T, and its restriction to T⊥ is an orientation-preserving orthogonal map of odd dimension 2m−1. An odd-dimensional special orthogonal transformation has a fixed vector: nonreal eigenvalues occur in conjugate pairs, while an odd-dimensional real orthogonal map with determinant one must have an eigenvalue +1. Choose a nonzero fixed vector v normal to T and parallel-transport it along the curve. It gives a nonzero smooth periodic normal field V with ∇t​V=0.
For the exponential variation γs​(t)=expγ(t)​(sV(t)), the endpoints match periodically. Strictly positive sectional curvature gives
E′′(0)=−∫⟨R(V,T)T,V⟩dt<0,E′(0)=0.
(20)
Thus E(γs​)<E(γ) for small nonzero s. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
L(γs​)2≤2(b−a)E(γs​)<2(b−a)E(γ)=L(γ)2,
(21)
where the final equality uses the geodesic's constant speed. This proves the instability of a closed geodesic in positive even-dimensional curvature.
For an embedded closed geodesic, sufficiently small variations remain embeddings, hence give a smooth isotopy with strictly shorter curves. For a nonembedded closed geodesic the construction gives a smooth deformation through immersions; an isotopy class of embeddings is not literally defined for such a curve. The stated isotopy conclusion therefore uses the usual embedded-curve interpretation, while the shorter-loop variation holds without it.
