The Lax-Milgram theorem assumes that H is a real Hilbert space and B:H×H→R is a bounded bilinear form and a coercive bilinear form: there are M,α>0 such that
∣B[u,v]∣≤M∥u∥H​∥v∥H​,B[u,u]≥α∥u∥H2​.
(14)
Then for every continuous linear functional ℓ∈H′ there is a unique u∈H satisfying
B[u,v]=ℓ(v)(v∈H),∥u∥H​≤α−1∥ℓ∥H′​.​
(15)
Symmetry of B is not required.
For the proof, the Riesz representation theorem provides a bounded linear operator T:H→H and g∈H with B[u,v]=(Tu,v)H​ and ℓ(v)=(g,v)H​. Coercivity and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality imply
α∥u∥H2​≤(Tu,u)H​≤∥Tu∥H​∥u∥H​,∥Tu∥H​≥α∥u∥H​.
(16)
Thus T is injective and its range is closed: if Tuj​ converges, the displayed inequality makes uj​ a Cauchy sequence; completeness gives uj​→u and Tuj​→Tu. If w lies in the orthogonal complement of the range, then B[v,w]=0 for all v. Taking v=w gives α∥w∥2≤B[w,w]=0, so w=0. A closed subspace with zero orthogonal complement is all of H, hence T is surjective. The unique solution is T−1g, and the same inequality gives the asserted norm bound.

The all-time conclusion in the original PDF is false. The expanding-flow transformation of a wave equation makes both the correct forward statement and a counterexample transparent. Put
X=ρe−t,S=e−t>0,u(ρ,t)=w(X,S).
(58)
For D=∂t​+ρ∂ρ​, the expanded operator from part (i) is D2+D−∂ρ2​. In the new variables D=−S∂S​ and ∂ρ​=S∂X​, hence
(D2+D−∂ρ2​)u=S2(wSS​−wXX​).
(59)
Thus it is the ordinary wave equation on the half-plane S>0. By the D'Alembert formula, any C2 solution has the form
u(ρ,t)=F(e−t(ρ+1))+G(e−t(ρ−1)).
(60)
At t=0, differentiating the zero displacement with respect to ρ and using the zero velocity gives, for −1<ρ<1,
F′(ρ+1)+G′(ρ−1)=0,(ρ+1)F′(ρ+1)+(ρ−1)G′(ρ−1)=0.
(61)
Subtracting (ρ−1) times the first equation from the second gives 2F′(ρ+1)=0, and then G′(ρ−1)=0. Thus F=c on (0,2) and G=−c on (−2,0). If t≥0 and −1<ρ<1, both arguments remain in those intervals. Consequently the valid forward result is
u(ρ,t)=0(−1<ρ<1, t≥0).​
(62)
For negative time the arguments need not stay in the initial intervals. Take F(s)=(s−2)+3​ and G=0, where q+​=max{q,0}. Then
u(ρ,t)=(e−t(ρ+1)−2)+3​​
(63)
is a global C2 solution, has the prescribed zero initial displacement and velocity on (−1,1), but u(0,−log3)=1. This directly disproves the assertion for t∈R.
The connection with part (i) is directional finite propagation speed. At a future point in the strip, the two characteristics traced back to t=0 meet it at ρ0​=e−t(ρ+1)−1 and ρ0​=e−t(ρ−1)+1, both in (−1,1). For negative time these intersections can lie outside that interval. The lines ρ=±1 are characteristic barriers for this forward domain of dependence, not barriers in both time directions.
