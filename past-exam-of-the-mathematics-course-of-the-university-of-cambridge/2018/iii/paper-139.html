For a projective scheme X of dimension of a scheme n and a Cartier divisor D, asymptotic Riemann–Roch gives
χ(X,OX​(mD))=n!Dn​mn+O(mn−1).​
(1)
Here Euler characteristic of a coherent sheaf means χ=∑i=0n​(−1)ihi, and Dn is the degree of the top intersection product with the fundamental cycle of X, including its component multiplicities. No ampleness assumption on D is needed.

ii OurBigBook.com
The Nakai–Moishezon criterion says that a Cartier divisor D on a projective scheme is ample exactly when
DdimY⋅Y>0
(2)
for every positive-dimensional integral closed subvariety Y. Kleiman's criterion says that the ample cone is the interior of the nef cone; equivalently, the numerical class of D is ample exactly when it is strictly positive on every nonzero element of the closed cone of curves NE(X). Positivity merely on individual curves is insufficient: the closure of the cone is essential. A nef divisor has nonnegative intersection number with every integral curve, and its restriction to every closed subscheme is nef.

Choose very ample divisors H2​ and H1​∼D+H2​, with defining sections avoiding the associated points of X. Such a choice is possible after taking a sufficiently high ample twist. Put Rm​=mD−H1​=(m−1)D−H2​. The two indicated short exact sequences of sheaves and their long exact sequences in sheaf cohomology imply
hj(X,mD)≤hj(X,(m−1)D)+hj(H1​,mD)+hj−1(H2​,(m−1)D).
(3)
We abbreviate hi(Z,OZ​(L)) by hi(Z,L). Each Ha​ has dimension n−1 and the restricted divisor is nef. For positive degrees below n−1, the assumed induction estimate bounds the error terms by O(mn−2). The displayed hypothesis omits top-degree cohomology; use top cohomology boundedness for nef twists for that degree, and Grothendieck vanishing above it. These give the same error bound. The auxiliary top-degree result follows from Fujita vanishing by cutting a coherent sheaf with an ample divisor, as proved in cohomology growth for nef twists.
For n≥2, summing the inequality over successive m gives hj(X,mD)=O(mn−1) for every j>1. For n≤1, all such groups vanish by Grothendieck vanishing.

By asymptotic Riemann–Roch and the preceding bounds for higher sheaf cohomology,
h0(X,mD)−h1(X,mD)=n!Dn​mn+O(mn−1).​
(4)
A nef divisor satisfies Dn≥0: for a fixed ample divisor A, the divisors D+εA are ample for positive rational ε, and continuity of the intersection product gives the inequality as ε→0. If h0(X,mD)=0 eventually, the left side is nonpositive. Dividing by mn forces Dn=0, since h1≥0 and Dn≥0. The same identity then gives h1(X,mD)=O(mn−1).

First suppose X is integral. Induct on its dimension of a scheme. The previous two parts handle all j>1 and also handle j=1 when h0(X,mD) eventually vanishes. Otherwise choose r>0 with a nonzero global section of rD. On an integral variety it defines an effective Cartier divisor E, possibly empty, and multiplication by the section gives
0→OX​((m−r)D)→OX​(mD)→OE​(mD)→0.
(5)
Thus h1(X,mD)≤h1(X,(m−r)D)+h1(E,mD). For n≥2, the induction hypothesis bounds the last term by O(mn−2); summing on each residue class modulo r gives O(mn−1). For n=1, E is zero-dimensional and its positive-degree sheaf cohomology vanishes, so the same recurrence is bounded.
For a general projective scheme, a nonzero section can be a zero divisor, so that argument requires an additional step. The general cohomology growth for nef twists supplies it: for every coherent sheaf F with support dimension d,
hi(X,F⊗OX​(mD))=O(md−i)(1≤i≤d).
(6)
Its proof uses Fujita vanishing, an ample section avoiding the associated points of F, and induction on support dimension. Taking F=OX​ gives the required O(mn−1) estimate for every j≥1, including nonreduced and reducible schemes; degrees above n vanish.

Write F=∑C​minG∈∣H∣​multC​(G)C. This is the fixed part of a linear system. The movable part of a linear system is ∣M∣, with M=H−F; multiplication by the defining section of F identifies its sections with those of H. In particular ∣H∣=F+∣M∣, and no curve occurs in every member of ∣M∣. The occurrence of ∣F∣ in the definition request is understood as ∣H∣.
For any integral curve C, choose a member G∈∣M∣ not containing C. Their intersection number is a sum of nonnegative local intersection multiplicities, so M⋅C=G⋅C≥0. Therefore the movable divisor M is nef. If M=0, the same assertion is immediate.

The relevant invariant of a divisor is its Iitaka dimension, also called its Kodaira dimension. If two linearly independent global sections s,t occur in H0(X,nH), their ratio is a nonconstant rational function. The sections sq,sq−1t,…,tq are linearly independent, because a nonconstant rational function over an algebraically closed field is transcendental over that field. Thus some complete linear system of a divisor has positive-dimensional image, contradicting κ(H)=0.
Consequently h0(X,nH)=1 for every n>0; the nonemptiness of ∣H∣ supplies a nonzero section in every multiple. Its unique divisor is entirely fixed. Hence every movable part Mn​ is zero.

Because ∣D∣ has no fixed components, choose two members G1​,G2​ with no common component. This uses the infinitude of the algebraically closed field and avoidance of finitely many proper linear subspaces of the section space. On a smooth algebraic surface, their intersection number is the sum of local intersection multiplicities. Since G1​⋅G2​=D2=0, they are disjoint. A basepoint of ∣D∣ would belong to both, so ∣D∣ is basepoint-free.
The resulting morphism has nonconstant image since dim∣D∣≥1. Its image cannot be a surface: a generically finite morphism defined by D would make D2 the positive product of its degree and the degree of its image. Thus the image is a curve. Apply Stein factorization to obtain g:X→C with connected fibers and g∗​OX​=OC​, where C is a smooth projective curve. There is a positive-degree line bundle L on C with OX​(D)=g∗L. The projection formula for sheaves and Riemann-Roch theorem give h0(X,mD)=h0(C,Lm)=mdegL+O(1). Therefore
κ(D)=1.​
(8)

Choose an ample divisor A. The Hodge index theorem for algebraic surfaces implies D⋅A>0: if it were zero, D2=0 would force the numerical class of D to be zero. Consequently −D cannot be effective, because every nonzero effective divisor has positive intersection with A, while −D⋅A<0.
The Riemann–Roch theorem for algebraic surfaces and the preceding computation give χ(X,OX​(D))=2+D2/2=2. By Serre duality, h2(X,D)=h0(X,−D)=0. Thus
h0(X,D)=2+h1(X,D)≥2.​
(11)

First derive the intersection constraints. Since D is nef and F,M are effective, D⋅F,D⋅M≥0; their sum is D2=0, so both vanish. Since the movable divisor M is nef, M2,M⋅F≥0, and M⋅D=0 forces both to vanish. In particular
D⋅F=D⋅M=M2=M⋅F=F2=0.
(12)
For every component E of F, nefness and D⋅F=0 imply D⋅E=0. The isotropic orthogonality consequence of the Hodge index theorem gives E2≤0. If E2=0, Riemann–Roch theorem for algebraic surfaces and Serre duality give h0(X,E)≥2, since −E cannot be effective. Choose E′∈∣E∣ different from E. It cannot contain E: otherwise E′−E would be a nonzero effective numerically trivial divisor, contradicting its positive intersection with an ample divisor. Choose a member of ∣M∣ avoiding E. Replacing one copy of E in F by E′ now produces a member of ∣D∣ with smaller multiplicity along E, contradicting the definition of the fixed part. Therefore
E2<0for every component of F.​

The strict statement as printed needs the qualification if F=0. Part (a) already shows F2=0 in the present situation, and the next part proves F=0; an unconditional F2<0 would contradict that conclusion.
To prove the needed conditional statement, suppose F=0. A fixed part has h0(X,F)=1. F=0 ⟹ F2<0.​

Choose a very ample divisor A such that A−D has a nonzero global section; this is possible by taking a sufficiently high ample twist. Since X is integral, multiplication by its mth power injects H0(X,mD) into H0(X,mA). By Serre vanishing and the Hilbert polynomial, h0(X,mA)=O(mn). Consequently
h0(X,mD)≤Cmn(m≫0).​
(16)
This is the polynomial bound for sections of a fixed divisor. The same proof works for a coherent sheaf of support dimension d, by choosing the multiplying section to avoid its associated points; the bound is then O(md).

For an effective Cartier divisor F, the divisor restriction exact sequence gives
0→OX​(kD−F)→OX​(kD)→OF​(kD)→0.
(17)
The scheme F has dimension at most n−1, so the permitted scheme version of part (i) bounds h0(F,OF​(kD)) by Ckn−1. For the infinitely many k in the hypothesis,
h0(X,kD−F)≥C′kn−Ckn−1>0
(18)
once k is sufficiently large. Thus infinitely many such k have a nonzero section of kD−F. This dimension-drop argument is the section subtraction lemma for big divisors.
If “effective divisor” is interpreted as an effective Weil divisor on a normal variety, use its coherent divisor ideal instead. The quotient by that ideal is supported in dimension at most n−1, so the polynomial bound for sections of a fixed divisor gives the same conclusion. For F=0 the assertion is immediate.

Fix any ample divisor A. Choose q≫0 so that both qA and (q−1)A have nonzero global sections, and choose F∈∣qA∣. Condition (1) and the section subtraction lemma for big divisors yield an integer j>0 with jD−qA∼E0​≥0. Adding an effective member of ∣(q−1)A∣ gives jD∼A+E. Thus (1) implies the stronger condition (2').
If jD∼A+E with A ample and E effective, multiplication by the section of qE injects H0(X,qA) into H0(X,qjD). The positive leading coefficient of the ample Hilbert polynomial supplies condition (1) along the infinite sequence m=qj. Condition (2) implies (3). Conversely, if jD≡A+E, put P=jD−E. The Cartier divisor P is numerically equivalent to A, hence ample by Kleiman's criterion; the actual equality jD=P+E gives (2).
We have proved (1)⇒(2')⇒(2)⇒(1), and (3)⇒(2)⇒(3). Also (2') implies (3'), while (3') implies (3). Hence all five conditions are equivalent. They characterize a big divisor; the ample-plus-effective formulation is Kodaira's lemma. The inconsistent use of n and j in the printed multiplier is resolved by using j throughout.

Choose a very ample divisor A. By the stronger form of Kodaira's lemma, jD∼A+E for some effective divisor E. The subsystem sE​H0(X,A)⊆H0(X,jD) defines the embedding given by A on X∖SuppE. Condition (a) is the definition of a big real divisor. Fix an ample Cartier divisor A. For each big Cartier divisor Di​, part (iii) gives ji​Di​∼A+Ei​ with Ei​ effective. Consequently
D∼R​(∑i​ji​di​​)A+∑i​ji​di​​Ei​.
(20)
The first coefficient is positive, proving (b).
For the converse, suppose D∼R​tA+E, with t>0 and E effective. Apply rational approximation of an ample-plus-effective real divisor: in a finite-dimensional space generated by Cartier divisors and the finitely many principal divisors occurring in this relation, express D as a positive convex combination of rational divisors Bν​, each satisfying Bν​∼Q​tν​A+Eν​ with tν​>0 and Eν​ effective. After clearing denominators, part (iii) shows that each Bν​ is a positive rational multiple of a big Cartier divisor. This gives the actual equality required by (a), rather than merely a numerical or linear equivalence.
The approximation lemma keeps the finitely many effectivity inequalities and linear-equivalence equations simultaneously; see its proof for the rational-face argument and the reduction of nonnormal varieties by finite normalization. Therefore (a) and (b) are equivalent.

Condition (b) immediately implies (c): a positive real multiple tA of an ample divisor is an ample real divisor, and real linear equivalence of divisors implies numerical equivalence of divisors.
More explicitly, the real numerical class of D is the sum of a class in the ample cone and an effective real divisor class. This separates strict positivity from the possibly degenerate effective part; it does not claim that the effective part itself is ample.

Suppose D≡A′+E′ as in (c), and set P=D−E′. Its numerical class is ample, so P is an ample real divisor. Fix an ample Cartier divisor A. The ample cone is open by Kleiman's criterion, so choose ε>0 with P−εA still ample. Express this ample real divisor as a positive real combination ∑bi​Bi​ of ample Cartier divisors. Some positive multiple of each Bi​ has an effective representative; dividing by that multiple gives Bi​∼Q​Gi​ with Gi​ effective. Hence
D∼R​εA+(E′+∑i​bi​Gi​),
(21)
which is (b). The equivalence of numerical ampleness with a positive combination of ample Cartier representatives follows by rational approximation inside the open ample cone; the rational approximation of an ample-plus-effective real divisor proof also accounts for principal-divisor directions.
Finally, if D′≡D, the same expression D′≡A′+E′ satisfies (c). By the equivalences just proved, D′ satisfies all three conditions exactly when D does. Thus bigness is invariant under numerical equivalence, for real as well as Cartier divisors.

The space of real numerical divisor classes is N1(X)R​. Write any big class as [D]=[A]+[E], with A an ample real divisor and E effective. For a sufficiently small perturbation δ∈N1(X)R​, the class [A]+δ remains in the open ample cone, so [D]+δ remains big by part (v). Thus Big(X) is open. Positive scaling and addition preserve the ample-plus-effective expression, so it is a convex cone.
For n≥1, fix a very ample divisor H. The linear functional ℓ([D])=D⋅Hn−1 is strictly positive on every big class: the ample part contributes positively and the effective part nonnegatively. No nonzero linear subspace can be contained in this cone, since it would contain both v and −v. In particular the zero class is not big in positive dimension. When n=0, N1(X)R​=0, so there is no positive-dimensional subspace to consider. Therefore
Big(X) is an open convex cone and contains no positive-dimensional vector subspace.​
(22)
