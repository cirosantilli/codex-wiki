The starting point of a conforming finite element method is a weak formulation in a Hilbert space X. For example, a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on a bounded Lipschitz domain gives X=H01​(Ω). One seeks
a(u,v)=ℓ(v)(v∈X),
(38)
where a is a bounded bilinear form, ∣a(w,v)∣≤L∥w∥X​∥v∥X​, and ℓ∈X∗ is a bounded linear functional. A conforming finite element space Xh​⊂X is finite dimensional and is typically built from functions that are polynomial on each element of a finite element mesh. With a basis φj​, the Galerkin method imposes the same identity only for vh​∈Xh​, giving
Kc=F,Kij​=a(φj​,φi​),Fi​=ℓ(φi​).​
(39)
The stiffness matrix is sparse when basis supports overlap only locally. Local element integrals assemble its entries; the mass matrix represents an L2 term. This separates the choice of trial functions from the variational principle determining their coefficients.
The fundamental well-posedness theorem is the Lax-Milgram theorem: if a(v,v)≥γ∥v∥X2​ for some γ>0, there is a unique solution for each ℓ∈X∗, with ∥u∥X​≤∥ℓ∥X∗​/γ. The same theorem on Xh​ gives a unique discrete solution. The constants must be independent of the mesh for uniform stability of a numerical method. Boundary conditions are built into the trial space when they are essential constraints; flux conditions arise through boundary terms in integration by parts and are natural constraints in the weak formulation.
When a is symmetric and a coercive bilinear form, the Ritz method minimizes
E(v)=21​a(v,v)−ℓ(v)
(40)
over Xh​. Its first variation is exactly the Galerkin method, so Ritz minimization and Galerkin testing coincide for symmetric coercive problems. The energy norm ∥v∥a​=a(v,v)​ turns the approximation into an orthogonal projection. Indeed, subtracting the exact and discrete weak equations gives Galerkin orthogonality, a(u−uh​,vh​)=0. For every vh​∈Xh​ the Pythagorean identity becomes
∥u−vh​∥a2​=∥u−uh​∥a2​+∥uh​−vh​∥a2​,
(41)
so
∥u−uh​∥a​=vh​∈Xh​inf​∥u−vh​∥a​.​
(42)
This exact best-approximation property is the central advantage of the Ritz method.
Symmetry is unnecessary for the Galerkin method. For any bounded, coercive form, the Céa lemma gives
∥u−uh​∥X​≤γL​vh​∈Xh​inf​∥u−vh​∥X​.​
(43)
To prove it, put e=u−uh​. Galerkin orthogonality yields a(e,e)=a(e,u−vh​), so coercivity and boundedness give γ∥e∥X2​≤L∥e∥X​∥u−vh​∥X​. Divide when e=0 and take the infimum. Thus approximation capability plus uniform coercivity gives convergence. In contrast, minimizing 21​a(v,v)−ℓ(v) for a nonsymmetric form differentiates its symmetric part and generally does not solve the original weak equation.
A concrete symmetric example is the one-dimensional reaction-diffusion problem from Question 5. Continuous piecewise-linear hat functions give the diffusion stiffness matrix S with diagonal 2/h and neighbouring entries −1/h, and the mass matrix M with diagonal 2h/3 and neighbouring entries h/6. Therefore K=S+M and Fi​=ih2. Its positive-definite matrix is the coordinate form of the continuous energy. The exact smooth solution u(x)=x−sinh(x)/sinh1 makes this an explicit test of the method, not just an abstract existence result.
For a nonsymmetric example, take positive ε, constant real β, and
−εu′′+βu′+u=f,u(0)=u(1)=0.
(44)
Its form is a(u,v)=ε∫u′v′+β∫u′v+∫uv. Since ∫v′v=0, it has coercivity constant min(ε,1) in the full H1 norm, although it is not symmetric when β=0. The Galerkin method remains well posed. On the uniform hat basis, K=εS+βC+M, where Ci,i+1​=1/2, Ci,i−1​=−1/2 and Cii​=0. The skew-symmetric matrix C cancels from cTKc, proving nonsingularity. A Ritz energy would omit precisely this advection contribution. Small ε may nevertheless make the approximation constant large and produce poorly resolved layers; algebraic solvability is not an accuracy guarantee.
Quantitative convergence uses a finite element interpolation estimate. On a shape-regular mesh, for piecewise-linear conforming elements and u∈H2(Ω) in the usual low-dimensional setting,
∥u−Ih​u∥H1​≤Ch∥u∥H2​,∥u−Ih​u∥L2​≤Ch2∥u∥H2​.
(45)
The Céa lemma therefore gives an O(h) energy error. The Aubin–Nitsche duality argument improves the L2 norm error when the dual elliptic problem has H2 regularity: solve a(v,z)=(e,v)L2​ and use
∥e∥L22​=a(e,z−Ih​z)≤Ch∥e∥H1​∥z∥H2​≤Ch∥e∥H1​∥e∥L2​.
(46)
Consequently
∥u−uh​∥H1​=O(h),∥u−uh​∥L2​=O(h2),​
(47)
with constants depending on the solution and regularity bounds. The second estimate is conditional on dual regularity; corners or insufficient data regularity can reduce the rate.
The same Galerkin method handles evolution equations. For the homogeneous heat equation, the semidiscrete finite element heat equation is Mc˙+Sc=0. Because M is a positive-definite matrix and S is a positive semidefinite matrix,
dtd​(cTMc)=−2cTSc≤0.
(48)
The decreasing quadratic form is exactly the L2 norm squared of the finite-element function. This separates spatial stability of a numerical method from the subsequent time integrator, just as in the Schrödinger equation example. Conformity, coercivity and approximation estimates together explain convergence.
