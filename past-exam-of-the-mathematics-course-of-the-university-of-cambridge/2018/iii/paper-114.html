For chain maps f,g:C∗​→D∗​, a chain homotopy from g to f is a family of group homomorphisms sq​:Cq​→Dq+1​ satisfying
fq​−gq​=dq+1D​sq​+sq−1​dqC​for every q.​
(1)
If z is a chain cycle, then (f−g)(z)=dDs(z) is a chain boundary. Consequently [f(z)]=[g(z)], so f∗​=g∗​​ on every homology group.
Order the vertices of the simplex as v0​,…,vn​. Its simplicial chain complex has
Sq​(Δn)=⨁0≤i0​<⋯<iq​≤n​Z[vi0​​,…,viq​​](0≤q≤n),
(2)
and zero groups otherwise. An arbitrary ordering represents the sign of the permutation times the increasing ordering. Its boundary operator is
dq​[vi0​​,…,viq​​]=∑r=0q​(−1)r[vi0​​,…,vir​​,…,viq​​](q≥1),d0​=0.
(3)
In d2 each face obtained by deleting the vertices in positions r<t occurs twice. Deleting r first gives sign (−1)r(−1)t−1, whereas deleting t first gives (−1)t(−1)r. These signs are opposite, so all terms cancel. The case d0​d1​=0 follows directly from d0​=0. Thus d2=0​.
For the homology calculation, add the augmentation ε:S0​→Z sending every vertex to 1. Regard Z as the group in degree −1 of an augmented chain complex. Define the simplicial cone chain contraction by
s−1​(1)=[v0​],sq​[vi0​​,…,viq​​]={[v0​,vi0​​,…,viq​​],0,​i0​>0,i0​=0.​
(4)
If the simplex does not contain v0​, expansion of its coned boundary gives ds(σ)=σ−sd(σ). If it contains v0​, the only face on which s is nonzero is the face omitting v0​, and sd(σ)=σ. In degree zero this uses the augmentation, and in degree −1 it says εs−1​=1. Hence
ds+sd=1on the augmented complex.​
(5)
Every positive-degree chain cycle is therefore a chain boundary. In degree zero, the same identity says z−ε(z)[v0​]=ds0​(z), so the augmentation induces an isomorphism H0​→Z, with inverse 1↦[v0​]. Thus, including n=0,
Hq​(S∗​(Δn))≅{Z,0,​q=0,q>0.​​
(6)
This is a direct chain homotopy calculation and uses no result about cellular homology.

Write v for the cone vertex. The quotient is the topological mapping cone Cf​=Y∪f​CX. First assume X=∅, as is implicit in having this vertex; a map then also forces Y=∅.
Take the open cover consisting of the image of X×[0,2/3) and the image of Y⊔(X×(1/3,1]). Denote these sets by U,V. The first contracts to v, the second has a deformation retraction onto Y, and U∩V≅X×(1/3,2/3) retracts onto X. Under these identifications the two inclusion maps induce the zero map into positive-degree homology of U and f∗​ into the homology of V. The Mayer–Vietoris theorem consequently gives, in positive degrees,
⋯⟶Hq+1​(Z;G)⟶Hq​(X;G)f∗​​Hq​(Y;G)⟶Hq​(Z;G)⟶⋯.​
(7)
The same assertion holds in degree zero, but needs care: the ordinary Mayer-Vietoris map is
H0​(X;G)⟶H0​(Y;G)⊕G,a⟼(f∗​a,−εX​a).
(8)
Since εY​f∗​a=εX​a, its kernel equals kerf∗​. On quotienting H0​(Z;G) by the direct summand G[v], its cokernel becomes cokerf∗​. That quotient is canonically identified with reduced homology by subtracting the augmentation times [v]. Thus the last nonzero part is
H1​(Z;G)⟶H0​(X;G)f∗​​H0​(Y;G)⟶H0​(Z;G)⟶0.
(9)
In particular the map from H0​(Y;G) sends a component class [y] to [y]−[v]. This proves the mapping cone exact sequence; finite generation of G is not needed for this construction.
There is a literal empty-space exception in the printed hypotheses. If X=∅ and Y is a point, the given quotient is just Y, and the asserted degree-zero exact sequence would be 0→G→0. Thus the displayed reduced sequence presupposes nonempty X. It holds also for empty X if one defines its cone to include a new isolated vertex. The later homology-isomorphism claim itself remains true for empty finite complexes, with the cases involving one empty complex dealt with directly in degree zero.
Now take nonempty finite CW complexes X,Y. Put Aq​=Hq​(Z;Z). The exact sequence gives
0⟶coker(f∗​:Hq​(X)→Hq​(Y))⟶Aq​⟶ker(f∗​:Hq−1​(X)→Hq−1​(Y))⟶0,
(10)
where H−1​=0. All Aq​ are finitely generated abelian groups, since the groups for X,Y are finitely generated. Moreover, by exactness,
f∗​ is an integral homology isomorphism⟺Aq​=0 for every q.
(11)
The reduced universal coefficient theorem for homology gives
0⟶Aq​⊗Fp​⟶Hq​(Z;Fp​)⟶Tor1Z​(Aq−1​,Fp​)⟶0.
(12)
If all Aq​ vanish, so does the middle term for every prime, and the exact sequence with coefficients proves that f∗​ is an isomorphism modulo every prime. Conversely, those isomorphisms imply that the middle terms vanish, hence Aq​⊗Fp​=0 for every prime p. By the Fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups, a nonzero free summand would survive modulo every prime, and a cyclic torsion summand would survive modulo a prime dividing its order. Thus Aq​=0 for every q, proving
f∗​:H∗​(X;Z)∼​H∗​(Y;Z)⟺f∗​:H∗​(X;Fp​)∼​H∗​(Y;Fp​) for every prime p.​
(13)
This argument uses the exact sequence to obtain finite generation; it does not assume in advance that the cone of an arbitrary continuous map is a finite CW complex.
Finally suppose f is a cellular map. Keep the cells of Y, add the vertex v, and add a (q+1)-cell for the cone on every q-cell of X. Its top face attaches to Y through f, and its other faces attach to cones on lower-dimensional cells. The cellular condition puts these attachments in the appropriate skeleton. This constructs a finite CW complex structure on Z.
Let A∗​=C∗cell​(X) and B∗​=C∗cell​(Y) be the ordinary cellular chain complexes, with A−1​=0. Orient each coned cell so that its boundary has the form f#​(e)−c(dA​e). Using [y]−[v] as the reduced generator for each vertex of Y gives
Cqcell​(Z)=Bq​⊕Aq−1​,Dq​(y,x)=(dB​y+f#​x,−dA​x)(q≥0).​
(14)
In particular C0cell​(Z)=B0​, and the boundary of a cone edge is its image vertex minus v. The chain map identity dB​f#​=f#​dA​ gives
D2(y,x)=(dB2​y+dB​f#​x−f#​dA​x,dA2​x)=0.
(15)
This is the cellular chain complex of a mapping cone, equivalently the algebraic mapping cone with the displayed summand and sign conventions.

All groups in the first calculation have integral coefficients. Let U=Sn∖j(W) and V=Sn∖intj(W). A two-sided tubular neighborhood of j(∂W) identifies V as a compact manifold with boundary, with interior U. Pushing its boundary inward in a collar neighborhood gives a homotopy equivalence U≃V. After thickening across the same collar, excision identifies the relative groups
Hr​(Sn,V)≅Hr​(W,∂W).
(16)
The orientation of Sn gives W an orientation. The Poincare-Lefschetz duality isomorphism therefore gives
Hr​(Sn,V)≅Hn−r(W).
(17)
Each component of W has boundary: a closed component would have open image by the local embedding condition and closed image by compactness, hence would occupy the whole connected sphere, leaving no room for the components with boundary. In particular U is nonempty, and Hn(W)=0.
Apply the long exact sequence in relative homology of (Sn,V), using reduced homology for the absolute terms. Away from the top-dimensional homology of the sphere, it identifies
Hi​(U)≅Hn−i−1(W).
(18)
The exceptional map is
Hn​(Sn)=Z⟶Hn​(W,∂W)=H0(W)≅Zr,
(19)
where r is the number of components of W. Local compatibility of the orientations sends 1 to (1,…,1), the relative fundamental classes of all the components. Its kernel is zero and its cokernel is H0(W). Thus the exceptional term has exactly the reduced form required, and all higher groups vanish. We obtain the Alexander duality formula
Hi​(Sn∖j(W);Z)≅Hn−i−1(W;Z)(i≥0),​
(20)
with negative-index cohomology zero. Ordinary degree zero is recovered by
H0​(Sn∖j(W);Z)≅Z⊕Hn−1(W;Z).​
(21)
Both formulas depend on the abstract manifold W, so they establish the requested independence up to group isomorphism. They make no claim that the complements themselves are homeomorphic or have isomorphic fundamental groups. The argument also covers n=1, when the diagonal map handles the degree-zero exception. This is the complement homology of a compact codimension-zero submanifold.
For the second calculation write F=F2​, C=Sn∖i(M) and c=n−k. First suppose c>0, with n≥1. The rank-c normal bundle has a mod-two Thom class, regardless of orientability. A tubular neighborhood, excision and the homological Thom isomorphism theorem identify
Hj​(Sn,C;F)≅Hj−c​(M;F).
(22)
The long exact sequence in relative homology becomes the Gysin sequence of an embedding:
⋯⟶Hj​(C;F)⟶Hj​(Sn;F)γ​Hj−c​(M;F)⟶Hj−1​(C;F)⟶⋯.
(23)
The map γ takes the mod-two fundamental class of Sn to that of M: restricting to each normal fiber evaluates the Thom class as 1. Since M is closed and connected, the degree-n map
F=Hn​(Sn;F)⟶Hk​(M;F)=F
(24)
is an isomorphism. This cancels the exceptional top term and gives Hi​(C;F)=0 for i≥n−1 when i>0. In all the intervening degrees the sphere groups vanish. In degree zero, the remaining F is removed by the augmentation. Consequently the mod-two homology of a submanifold complement is
Hi​(C;F)≅{Hi+k+1−n​(M;F),0,​0≤i≤n−2,i≥n−1,​​
(25)
where negative-index homology of M is zero. To recover ordinary homology, add one copy of F in degree zero and change nothing in positive degrees. For n=1 the first range is empty, so the complement of a connected zero-manifold has the homology of a point. For example, codimension at least two gives H0​(C;F)=F, while codimension one gives H0​(C;F)=F2 when n≥2.
If k=n≥1, an embedding of the closed connected manifold has image both open and closed in Sn, hence is onto; its complement is empty and all its ordinary homology groups vanish. The positive-codimension formula is not asserted in that case. The standard sphere calculations here assume n≥1; in ambient dimension zero the complement of the embedded connected point in S0 is the other point.

For connected oriented closed manifolds of dimension n, the degree of a map between oriented manifolds is the integer determined by
f∗​[M]=(degf)[N]in Hn​(N;Z),​
(26)
where the brackets are the chosen fundamental classes. Functoriality gives deg(g∘f)=deggdegf.
Give Sn its boundary orientation as the boundary of the unit ball in Rn+1. The antipodal map is the restriction of −I. Its ambient determinant is (−1)n+1, and it takes the outward normal at x to the outward normal at −x. Thus its effect on the boundary orientation has this same sign, giving
degA=(−1)n+1.​
(27)
The original PDF has A:Sn→Sn, correcting the extra prime on the target in the TeX transcription.
For even dimension n=2k≥2, cellular homology of real projective space gives Hn​(RPn;Z)=0. Thus the composite map on top homology factors through zero, and
degh=0if h:S2k→S2k factors through RP2k, k≥1.​
(28)
Zero is attained by constant maps.
For odd dimension n=2k+1≥3, Real projective space is orientable: the antipodal deck transformation has degree +1. Orient it so that the double covering map q:Sn→RPn has degree 2. Since Sn is simply connected, the lifting criterion for a covering space gives f=q∘f​ for a map f​:Sn→Sn. Hence
deg(g∘f)=2deggdegf​,
(29)
which is even. Every even integer occurs: collapse the complement of an oriented embedded disk in RPn to obtain a degree-one map g:RPn→Sn, and choose f​ of any prescribed integer degree m. Maps of all integer degrees on spheres are obtained, for example, by suspending the circle maps z↦zm. Setting f=q∘f​ gives degree 2m. Thus
{degh}=2Zin odd dimensions 2k+1≥3.​
(30)
These are the degrees of maps factoring through real projective space. If k=0 is permitted in the odd-dimensional clause, there is an exception: RP1≅S1, so every integer degree occurs. The lifting argument requires dimension at least two. The initial connected-manifold degree definition excludes S0; with the usual reduced-homology definition for self-maps of S0, a factorization through the one-point RP0 has degree zero.
Finally suppose degf=p is prime. Fix any prime q=p and work over F=Fq​. We claim that f∗:Hr(N;F)→Hr(Sn;F) is injective. If a=0, Poincare duality supplies b∈Hn−r(N;F) with ⟨a⌣b,[N]F​⟩=0. Naturality of the cup product and evaluation gives
⟨f∗a⌣f∗b,[Sn]F​⟩=p⟨a⌣b,[N]F​⟩=0,
(31)
because p is invertible in F. Hence f∗a=0, proving cohomological injectivity of a map of invertible degree.
The intermediate cohomology of the sphere is zero, so Hr(N;F)=0 for 0<r<n. Over a field, the universal coefficient theorem for cohomology identifies this with the dual of Hr​(N;F), so these homology groups vanish as well. The universal coefficient theorem for homology then gives
Hr​(N;Z)⊗Fq​=0(0<r<n, q=p).
(32)
Each integral group is a finitely generated abelian group, by the permitted finite CW complex model. Its decomposition can have no free summand, since that would survive modulo q, and no torsion summand divisible by any prime q=p. Thus it is a finite p-primary group. There are only finitely many intermediate degrees, so the exponents of their finite cyclic summands have a common bound pa. Taking a≥1 also covers the case in which all the groups vanish. Therefore
∃a>0:pax=0for every x∈Hr​(N;Z), 0<r<n.​
(33)
This proves that a prime-degree sphere map forces primary torsion, including one uniform exponent for all the degrees.

Put F=F2​. Under the usual bundle convention of a paracompact base, choose a fiber metric and let D(E),S(E) be the disk bundle and sphere bundle of the rank-n real vector bundle. A mod-two Thom class is a relative cohomology class
u∈Hn(D(E),S(E);F)​
(34)
whose restriction to every fiber pair (Dn,Sn−1) is the nonzero generator. Equivalently it is a class in Hn(E,E∖B;F), where B is identified with the zero section. No orientation choices are needed over F.
The Thom isomorphism theorem asserts that this class exists and that, for every r, the map
Φ:Hr−n(B;F)∼​Hr(D(E),S(E);F),Φ(a)=π∗a⌣u​
(35)
is an isomorphism. The disk bundle retracts to the zero section s:B→D(E), so Hr(D(E);F)≅Hr(B;F). Define the mod-two Euler class e2​(E)=s∗u∈Hn(B;F), where the pullback includes the passage from relative to absolute cohomology. Under the Thom identification, the map from relative to absolute cohomology sends a to a⌣e2​(E). Substituting into the pair's long exact sequence in cohomology gives the Gysin sequence of a sphere bundle:
⋯⟶Hr−n(B;F)⌣e2​(E)​Hr(B;F)π∗​Hr(S(E);F)π!​​Hr−n+1(B;F)⌣e2​(E)​Hr+1(B;F)⟶⋯.​
(36)
Here π!​ is the connecting homomorphism followed by Φ−1. Thus the sequence and the cup-product map have been derived from the Thom theorem, not just stated. The Thom/Gysin constructions are also discussed in Hatcher's Vector Bundles and K-Theory, §3.2.
The original PDF specifies the tautological bundle on RPn; the occurrences of Rn in this part of the TeX transcription have lost the projective-space symbol. Let L→RPn be this real line bundle. Its sphere bundle consists of pairs (ℓ,v) with v a unit vector in the line ℓ, so S(L)≅Sn, with projection the antipodal double covering. Write t=e2​(L).
The additive mod-two groups, obtainable from the one-cell-in-each-dimension cellular chain complex, are
Hr(RPn;F)≅{F,0,​0≤r≤n,otherwise.​
(37)
For n≥1, both the base and Sn are connected, so π∗:H0(RPn;F)→H0(Sn;F) is an isomorphism. Exactness makes the following π!​ zero and multiplication by t injective from H0 to H1. For 1≤r<n, the term Hr(Sn;F) vanishes, so the same Gysin sequence makes
⌣t:Hr(RPn;F)⟶Hr+1(RPn;F)
(38)
injective. Since these groups are one-dimensional, the maps are isomorphisms. Hence 1,t,…,tn are the respective nonzero generators, while tn+1=0 by dimension. The mod-two cohomology ring of real projective space is therefore
H∗(RPn;F)≅F[t]/(tn+1),∣t∣=1.​
(39)
For n=0 this is simply F, with t=0.
For integral coefficients, the additive groups are
Hr(RPn;Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z/2,Z,0,​r=0,r even and 0<r≤n,r=n and n odd,otherwise.​
(40)
Indeed, the cellular homology of real projective space has boundary multiplication by 2 in even positive chain degrees and zero in odd degrees, and the integral cochain differential is its transpose. It remains to determine the products; the additive groups alone do not do so.
For n≥2, the coefficient sequence 0→Z2​Z→F→0 has a Bockstein homomorphism β. Because H1(RPn;Z)=0 and H2(RPn;Z)=Z/2, exactness makes a=β(t) the nonzero integral degree-two class. Reduction ρ:H2(RPn;Z)→H2(RPn;F) is injective: its kernel is the image of multiplication by 2, which is zero. Thus
ρ(a)=t2.
(41)
More generally, reduction is injective in each positive even degree, and its compatibility with the cup product gives ρ(aj)=t2j. Consequently aj is the nonzero generator whenever 2j≤n. Also 2a=0, and powers beyond the dimension vanish.
If n=2m is even, these powers and the unit account for all groups, so the integral cohomology ring of real projective space is
H∗(RP2m;Z)≅Z[a]/(2a,am+1),∣a∣=2.​
(42)
If n=2m+1 is odd, add an integral top-dimensional orientation class b of degree 2m+1. Its reduction is the nonzero class t2m+1. Every product ab and b2 is zero by dimension, and b has infinite additive order. Hence
H∗(RP2m+1;Z)≅Z[a,b]/(2a,am+1,ab,b2),∣a∣=2,∣b∣=2m+1.​
(43)
These formulas include m=0: the even space is a point with ring Z, and the odd space is a circle with ring Z[b]/(b2), ∣b∣=1. The polynomial presentations are interpreted with the displayed grading and products; no extra positive-degree products are left unspecified.
