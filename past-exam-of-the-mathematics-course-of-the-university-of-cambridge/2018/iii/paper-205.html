Estimate the signal by one-dimensional total variation denoising, which penalizes adjacent differences rather than the constant level:
μ​λ​=argμ∈Rnmin​{2n1​∥Y−μ∥22​+λi=1∑n−1​∣μi+1​−μi​∣},λ>0.​
(1)
This is a fused Lasso objective. Its L1 norm penalty makes many adjacent differences exactly zero, producing a piecewise-constant fit. For change-point detection, estimate the locations by S={i:μ​i​=μ​i+1​}. The tuning parameter controls the strength of the penalty; it can be chosen, for example, using a validation criterion appropriate to the noise model.
For a general family of elementary nulls H1​,…,Hm​, the closed testing procedure forms every nonempty intersection HB​=⋂j∈B​Hj​ and gives it a local test of level α. Reject Hj​ precisely when all intersections with j∈B are locally rejected. This gives strong control of the familywise error rate: let T0​ be the indices of the true elementary nulls. If T0​ is empty there is no false rejection. Otherwise, rejecting any Hj​ with j∈T0​ requires rejection of the true intersection HT0​​. Its local test has rejection probability at most α, hence
P(at least one false rejection)≤α.​
(2)
This proof of closed-testing control of the familywise error rate needs no independence of the tests.
For the interval procedure, a true tested interval is contained in one maximal constant block B∈T. If I⊆B, the intervals over which pB​ takes its maximum are a subset of those used for pI​, so pI​≥pB​. Therefore rejection of a true I forces rejection of its containing block. Conversely, a rejected block is itself a rejected true null.
There is a small indexing issue in the PDF: its testing family excludes singleton intervals, while T may contain singleton blocks. Put T∗​={B∈T:∣B∣≥2}. Every true tested interval lies in one of these blocks, so the exact statement, using only hypotheses defined in the paper, is
{some false rejection}=B∈T∗​⋃​{pB​≤α}.​
(3)
Singleton blocks contain no eligible tested interval and can be ignored. Equivalently, one may define their nulls as never rejected.
For each B∈T∗​, the term J=B occurs in the maximum defining pB​, giving pB​≥nqB​/∣B∣. A valid p-value is a super-uniform random variable under its null, so for 0≤α≤1,
P(pB​≤α)≤P(qB​≤nα∣B∣​)≤nα∣B∣​.
(4)
The union bound and disjointness of the constant blocks now give
P(some false rejection)≤nα​∑B∈T∗​​∣B∣≤α,P(no false rejections)≥1−α.​
(5)
This establishes weighted interval testing for a piecewise-constant mean even when the interval p-values are dependent. Values pI​>1 do not affect rejection at levels at most one.

Use the convention that ridge regression minimizes ∥Y−Xb∥22​/(2n)+(γ/2)∥b∥22​. Its closed-form ridge regression estimator is
bγ​=(XTX+nγIp​)−1XTY.​
(6)
The factors n and 1/2 here specify the tuning convention. No intercept is needed for the centred responses and predictors.
For the two-component procedure, fix δ and put θ=Y−Xδ. Differentiating the objective with respect to its dense coefficient β gives
−n1​XT(θ−Xβ)+2λ2​β=0.
(7)
Thus, with Q=(XTX+2nλ2​Ip​)−1,
β​λ​=QXT(Y−Xδλ​).​
(8)
The inverse exists because λ2​>0, even for a rank-deficient design. The sum of the fitted sparse and dense components is the Lava estimator.
Multiplying by 2n and completing the square gives the exact profile calculation
∥θ−Xβ∥22​+2nλ2​∥β∥22​​=θTθ−2βTXTθ+βTQ−1β=(β−QXTθ)TQ−1(β−QXTθ)+θT(In​−XQXT)θ.​
(9)
Let B=In​−XQXT. A singular value decomposition of X shows that its eigenvalues are 2nλ2​/(ri2​+2nλ2​) in nonzero singular directions and one in the orthogonal complement. Hence B is a positive-definite matrix. Take its symmetric principal square root of a positive semidefinite matrix, A=B1/2. Profiling out β therefore yields the Lasso reduction for the Lava estimator:
δλ​∈argδmin​{2n1​∥AY−AXδ∥22​+λ1​∥δ∥1​},A=(In​−XQXT)1/2.​
(10)
No restandardization of AX is needed: this is the exact transformed objective with its original penalty.
Write X=AX, h=δλ​−δ0, and η=Xβ0+Aε. Then AY=Xδ0+η. Comparing the transformed Lasso objective at δλ​ and δ0 gives the Basic inequality for the Lasso
2n1​∥Xh∥22​≤n1​ηTXh+λ1​(∥δ0∥1​−∥δλ​∥1​).
(11)
On Ω, Holder inequality bounds the score by λ1​∥h∥1​. Using ∥h∥1​≤∥δλ​∥1​+∥δ0∥1​ cancels the fitted penalty and yields the slow-rate prediction bound for the Lasso:
n1​∥Xh∥22​≤4λ1​∥δ0∥1​.​
(12)
Finally, if κ=λmax​(XQXT)<1, then B⪰(1−κ)In​, so
∥Xh∥22​=(Xh)TB(Xh)≥(1−κ)∥Xh∥22​.
(13)
Consequently
n1​∥X(δλ​−δ0)∥22​≤1−κ4λ1​∥δ0∥1​​.​
(14)
Positive λ2​ actually guarantees κ<1 for every finite design; the factor measures how much prediction norm the profiling transformation can remove.

For a diagonal entry, put Zj​=∑i=1d​(Aij2​−1). Independence and the given moment-generating function inequality for the chi-squared distribution give
EeaZj​≤e2da2(∣a∣<1/4).
(15)
The Chernoff bound with a=t/4 yields
P(Zj​≥dt)≤e−adt+2da2=e−dt2/8.
(16)
Apply the same argument to −Zj​ and sum the two tails:
P(​d(ATA)jj​​−1​≥t)≤2e−dt2/8(0<t<1).​
(17)
To obtain the off-diagonal bound, set U=(V+W)/2​ and D=(V−W)/2​. They are jointly normal with unit variances and covariance zero, so independence of uncorrelated jointly normal variables makes them independent standard normals. Since VW=(U2−D2)/2, the moment-generating function of the product of two independent standard normal random variables is
EeaVW=(1−a)−1/2(1+a)−1/2=(1−a2)−1/2(∣a∣<1).​
(18)
For j=k, (ATA)jk​ is a sum of d independent such products. The given inequality implies Eea(ATA)jk​≤eda2 for ∣a∣≤1/2. Taking a=t/2, and repeating for the negative tail, gives
P(d∣(ATA)jk​∣​≥t)≤2e−dt2/4(0<t<1).​
(19)
Now take t=clogp/d​∈(0,1) and E=ATA/d−Ip​. There are p diagonal entries but only p(p−1)/2 distinct off-diagonal entries, since E is symmetric. By the union bound, the event ∥E∥max​≤t has probability at least
1−2pe−dt2/8−p(p−1)e−dt2/4​≥1−2p1−c2/8−p2−c2/4.​
(20)
This is the required entrywise concentration of a Gaussian Gram matrix.
On that single event, any vector z supported on at most s coordinates satisfies
∣zTEz∣≤t∑j,k​∣zj​zk​∣=t∥z∥12​≤st∥z∥22​,
(21)
where the last step is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on the support. If u,v each have at most s/2 nonzero coordinates, z=u−v has at most s. The same entrywise event applies simultaneously to all of them, so no union over vectors is needed. The sparse norm preservation from entrywise Gram control gives
​d∥u−v∥22​∥A(u−v)∥22​​−1​≤scdlogp​​(u=v).​
(22)
The printed ratio is undefined at u=v. Its equivalent quadratic statement, ∣∥A(u−v)∥22​/d−∥u−v∥22​∣≤st∥u−v∥22​, includes that case trivially. The nontrivial concentration argument assumes p≥2,c>0; at p=1 the stated probability lower bound is nonpositive and hence vacuous.

For a convex function f, its subdifferential at x is the set of supporting slopes
∂f(x)={g:f(y)≥f(x)+gT(y−x) for every y in its domain}.
(23)
The subgradient optimality condition is
x∈argminf⟺0∈∂f(x).​
(24)
Indeed, the defining inequality with g=0 is exactly the global minimum inequality. For the subdifferential of the L1 norm, the coordinates vary independently:
∂∥b∥1​={z:zj​=sgn(bj​) (bj​=0),zj​∈[−1,1] (bj​=0)}.​
(25)
Let r=Y−Xβ​λ​. The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions for Lasso give a vector z∈∂∥β​λ​∥1​ such that XTr/n=λz. If σλ​=∥r∥2​/n​>0, the unsquared residual norm is differentiable at this fit, with gradient
−n​∥r∥2​XTr​=−nσλ​XTr​.
(26)
Using the subdifferential sum rule, choose γ(λ)=λ/σλ​ to obtain
−nσλ​XTr​+γ(λ)z=0.
(27)
Convexity now proves the tuning relation between Lasso and square-root Lasso:
β​λ​∈argbmin​Qλ/σλ​​(b;Y).​
(28)
The positive residual assumption is needed both for this gradient and for the quotient defining the tuning parameter.
For the final score calculation, use the usual fixed-design interpretation of the normal linear model: regard X,Z and the tuning parameter γ in the regression of Z as fixed, and choose that regression's minimizer using only those inputs. Its nonzero residual R=Rγ​ then has the Square-root Lasso optimality condition
n​∥R∥2​XTR​=γz,z∈∂∥β​γ​∥1​,∥XTR∥∞​≤n​γ∥R∥2​.
(29)
Expanding the response residual gives
T=∥R∥2​RTε​+∥R∥2​RTX(β0−β​λ​)​=W+Δ.
(30)
Since R/∥R∥2​ is a fixed unit vector and ε∼Nn​(0,σ2In​), a linear image of a multivariate normal vector gives W∼N(0,σ2). Finally, Holder inequality and the residual score bound yield
∣Δ∣≤∥R∥2​∥XTR∥∞​​∥β0−β​λ​∥1​≤n​γ∥β0−β​λ​∥1​.​
(31)
This is the residual-score decomposition from square-root Lasso. The normality statement also holds conditionally on a design independent of the noise. If γ were chosen from the response noise, for example through the preceding γ(λ) formula, the direction R would require a separate independence argument; the final calculation treats γ as fixed.

The moral graph of a Directed acyclic graph is formed by joining any two parents of a common child and then removing the directions from all edges. For D-separation, a path in the underlying undirected graph is active given S when every noncollider on it is outside S, and every collider has itself or a descendant in S. Disjoint vertex sets A and B are D-separated by S if no such active path connects them.
The adjacency characterization is
u,v are adjacent in the DAG⟺no S⊆V∖{u,v} d-separates them.​
(32)
A one-edge path cannot be blocked by conditioning on other vertices. For a nonadjacent pair, choose the later vertex v in a topological ordering. The earlier vertex u is a nondescendant and is not a parent of v, so the parents of v separate the pair by the local Markov property of a directed acyclic graph.
Use faithfulness of a directed acyclic graph to mean that graphical and probabilistic independences agree, for all disjoint sets:
ZA​⊥ZB​∣ZS​⟺A and B are d-separated by S.
(33)
This includes the global Markov property of a directed acyclic graph as well as the absence of extra independences. The conditional independence graph of P is the undirected graph in which u−v is absent exactly when Zu​⊥Zv​∣ZV∖{u,v}​.
To identify this graph, condition on every vertex other than u,v. Any noncollider in the interior of a path is then conditioned on, so an active path must have only colliders internally. Two adjacent interior vertices cannot both be colliders: the arrow joining them has an arrowhead at only one of its ends. Thus an active path can have no interior vertex, giving a direct edge, or just one, giving u→w←v. In the latter case the conditioned common child w activates the path. These possibilities are exactly the edges added or retained by moralization. Faithfulness therefore proves
Moral graph of G=conditional independence graph of P.​
(34)
This is why the moral graph equals the conditional independence graph under faithfulness.
For Gaussian graph estimation, collect the data into an n×p matrix D and let Sn​=n−1∑i​xi​xiT​. The precision matrix Θ=Σ−1 has Θjk​=0 precisely for absent graph edges, by Gaussian conditional independence. In neighbourhood selection, fit a Lasso regression of each column on the others:
b(j)∈argminb​{2n1​∥Dj​−D−j​b∥22​+ρj​∥b∥1​}.
(35)
Select the nonzero coefficients as neighbours of j, then make the graph undirected using either the OR rule, retaining an edge selected in at least one regression, or the AND rule, requiring both. These are instances of Nodewise Lasso.
The Graphical Lasso estimates the whole precision matrix jointly, for example by
Θ∈argΘ≻0min​⎩⎨⎧​tr(Sn​Θ)−logdetΘ+ρj=k∑​∣Θjk​∣⎭⎬⎫​.​
(36)
Read off its nonzero off-diagonal entries as graph edges. This expression uses an off-diagonal penalty; an all-entry penalty is another convention.
To reduce computation in the PC algorithm, start its skeleton search from an estimate of the conditional independence graph rather than the complete graph. The exact graph contains the true DAG skeleton, so oracle tests can remove its extra edges while retaining the true adjacencies. Fewer initial edges and smaller neighbourhoods reduce both candidate pairs and candidate conditioning sets.
For correctness of the orientation stage, preserve valid separating-set records. With an exact conditional independence graph, an initially absent pair u,v has the full complement V∖{u,v} as a separating set. Record that set, and record the usual discovered sets for edges removed later. Leaving an initially absent pair with an empty default could falsely mark an unshielded noncollider as a collider. This describes PC initialization from a conditional independence graph; with an estimated initial graph, screening errors can propagate into the output.

Use the unscaled penalty convention consistent with the displayed bound:
f​λ​=argf∈Hmin​{i=1∑n​(Yi​−f(xi​))2+λ∥f∥H2​}.​
(37)
For an objective with averaged squared loss, the corresponding penalty parameter would be λ/n. We retain the unscaled λ throughout.
By the representer theorem, the kernel ridge regression minimizer lies in the span of k(xi​,⋅). A valid coefficient vector is a=(K+λIn​)−1Y, giving
f​λ​(⋅)=∑i​ai​k(xi​,⋅),m=HY,H=K(K+λIn​)−1.
(38)
The kernel matrix is a positive semidefinite matrix, so the inverse exists even if K is singular. In the coefficient objective ∥Y−Ka∥22​+λaTKa, this choice satisfies K{(K+λIn​)a−Y}=0; there is no need to cancel K. The function itself is unique because of the strictly convex squared Hilbert norm. The matrix H is the kernel-ridge hat matrix in the present normalization.
Let m0=(f0(xi​))i=1n​. Since Y=m0+ε, the zero mean and covariance assumption give the exact bias-variance decomposition for linear prediction
n1​E∥m−m0∥22​=n1​∥(In​−H)m0∥22​+nσ2​tr(H2).
(39)
No normality of the noise is required for this identity.
Choose an orthonormal eigenbasis vi​ of K, with eigenvalues di​≥0. Its shrinkage factors are di​/(di​+λ), so
tr(H2)=∑i​(di​+λ)2di2​​≤∑i​min{4λdi​​,1}=λ1​∑i​min(di​/4,λ).
(40)
The first bound follows from (di​+λ)2≥4di​λ and from each shrinkage factor being at most one.
To control the bias by the Reproducing kernel Hilbert space norm, for di​>0 define
gi​=di​​1​∑j​(vi​)j​k(xj​,⋅)∈H.
(41)
The reproducing property gives ⟨gi​,gℓ​⟩H​=δiℓ​ and
viT​m0=di​​⟨gi​,f0⟩H​.
(42)
If di​=0, the corresponding unscaled kernel combination has Hilbert norm squared viT​Kvi​=0, so viT​m0=0. There is therefore no bias component in a zero-eigenvalue direction. For the remaining directions,
∥(In​−H)m0∥22​​=di​>0∑​(di​+λ)2λ2di​​∣⟨gi​,f0⟩H​∣2≤4λ​di​>0∑​∣⟨gi​,f0⟩H​∣2≤4λ​∥f0∥H2​.​
(43)
The scalar bound is again (di​+λ)2≥4di​λ, and the final step is Bessel inequality. Combining the variance and bias bounds proves the prediction-risk bound for kernel ridge regression:
n1​Ei∑​{f0(xi​)−f​λ​(xi​)}2≤nλσ2​i∑​min(di​/4,λ)+4nλ​∥f0∥H2​.​
