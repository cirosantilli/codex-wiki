For a finite-dimensional complex Lie algebra g, the following three conditions are equivalent descriptions of a semisimple Lie algebra.
First, its solvable radical is zero. An ideal of a Lie algebra is a linear subspace I satisfying [g,I]⊆I. A solvable Lie algebra is one whose derived series, defined by I(0)=I and I(r+1)=[I(r),I(r)], eventually becomes zero. The solvable radical is the largest solvable ideal of a Lie algebra; its vanishing is equivalent to the absence of nonzero solvable ideals.
Second, the Killing form
κ(u,v)=tr(aduadv)
(1)
is a nondegenerate bilinear form: if κ(u,v)=0 for every v, then u=0. Here adu(v)=[u,v] is the Adjoint representation, and tr is the trace. This equivalence is the Cartan criterion for semisimplicity.
Third, g is a direct sum of Lie algebras that are nonabelian simple Lie algebras. A simple Lie algebra is nonabelian and has no ideals of a Lie algebra other than zero and itself. Thus the three equivalent criteria are
radg=0⟺κ nondegenerate⟺g=j⨁​gj​ with each gj​ simple.​
(2)

The derived series gives a direct test, with no structural theorem needed. By bilinearity and antisymmetry of the Lie bracket, every bracket in h is a multiple of z, and z=[x,y] actually occurs. Therefore
h(1)=[h,h]=Cz,h(2)=[Cz,Cz]=0.
(3)

A two-dimensional Lie algebra representation already provides a counterexample. In the basis e1​,e2​ of V=C2, define
ρ(x)=(00​10​),ρ(y)=ρ(z)=0.
(5)
The commutator [ρ(x),ρ(y)] is zero, agreeing with ρ(z); the other defining Lie brackets also map to zero. Thus ρ is a Lie algebra homomorphism, although it is not a Faithful Lie algebra representation.
The line L=Ce1​ is an invariant subspace. Any complementary line has the form C(e2​+ae1​) for some a∈C. But
ρ(x)(e2​+ae1​)=e1​∈/C(e2​+ae1​),
(6)
so no complementary line is an invariant subspace. Equivalently, every invariant line is killed by ρ(x): its restriction to a line is a scalar, and its square is zero, forcing that scalar to be zero. A decomposition into two invariant lines would then force ρ(x)=0, a contradiction. This representation is therefore not a semisimple representation. Thus
Not every finite-dimensional representation of h is completely reducible.​

Use the coroot normalization α∨=2α/(α,α), with the pairing understood via the root system and its invariant inner product. A dominant integral weight, called a dominant weight here, is a functional λ∈t∗ such that
⟨λ,αi∨​⟩∈Z≥0​for every simple root αi​.​
(8)
Equivalently, λ=∑i​ni​ωi​ with ni​∈Z≥0​, where the fundamental weights satisfy ⟨ωi​,αj∨​⟩=δij​. If dominance and integrality are defined separately, dominance means the inequalities and membership in the weight lattice means integrality.
A highest-weight vector is a nonzero weight vector v∈Vλ​, so h⋅v=λ(h)v for all h∈t, annihilated by every positive root space:
v=0,h⋅v=λ(h)v,gβ​⋅v=0(β∈Φ+).​
(9)
The last condition is equivalently annihilation by the raising operators for the simple roots.

Fix a simple root αi​ and choose generators ei​,fi​,hi​ of the sl2 subalgebra associated with a root, with hi​=αi∨​ and
[hi​,ei​]=2ei​,[hi​,fi​]=−2fi​,[ei​,fi​]=hi​.
(10)
The highest-weight vector satisfies ei​v=0 and hi​v=mi​v, where mi​=⟨λ,αi∨​⟩. The classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations already implies that mi​ is a nonnegative integer. One can also see the necessary integrality directly from the lowering operator.
Indeed, fik​v, if nonzero, has hi​ eigenvalue mi​−2k. These distinct eigenvalues cannot occur indefinitely in a finite-dimensional space, so there is a largest r≥0 with fir​v=0. The sl2 Lie algebra relations imply, by induction on k,
ei​fik​v=k(mi​−k+1)fik−1​v.
(11)
For k=r+1 the left side is zero, hence (r+1)(mi​−r)fir​v=0. Thus mi​=r∈Z≥0​. Since this holds for every simple root,
λ is a dominant integral weight.​

Choose the given short root α as a simple root, and let β be the long simple root. This entails no loss of generality: the Weyl group is transitive on the short roots of the G2 root system. The relevant Cartan integers are ⟨β,α∨⟩=−3 and ⟨α,β∨⟩=−1. The positive roots are
α, β, α+β, 2α+β, 3α+β, 3α+2β.
(13)
Under mα​≅sl2​, the root space for mα+nβ has hα​ eigenvalue 2m−3n. The subalgebra mα​ itself is the three-dimensional irreducible V(2) in the Adjoint representation.
The four root spaces along the root string β,α+β,2α+β,3α+β have eigenvalues −3,−1,1,3. Consecutive spaces are connected by nonzero raising operators and lowering operators, so their sum is V(3). The negative root string supplies another V(3). The root spaces for ±(3α+2β) have eigenvalue zero, and neither adding nor subtracting α gives a root; they are two copies of V(0). Finally, the one-dimensional space kerα⊂t commutes with mα​, giving one more V(0). We have accounted for all 14 dimensions, and therefore the G2 adjoint branching to a short-root sl2 subalgebra is
g≅V(3)⊕2⊕V(2)⊕V(0)⊕3.​
(14)
For a nonzero root vector x∈gα​, its Adjoint representation action is a nonzero scalar multiple of the raising operator on each summand. On each irreducible V(n) this operator has a one-dimensional kernel, including V(0). Since the Lie algebra centralizer is that kernel,
dimZg​(x)=2+1+3=6.​
(15)

Suppose first that x=0, and let E be the real span of the root system, of dimension equal to the rank of a semisimple Lie algebra ℓ. The images of the roots span E/Rα, so select roots β1​,…,βℓ−1​ whose images form a basis of this quotient.
For each i, take the highest endpoint γi+​ of the α root string through βi​, and the highest endpoint γi−​ of the root string through −βi​. By construction, γi±​+α is not a root. Moreover γi±​=−α, since their images in E/Rα are nonzero. The root-space decomposition and its bracket rule therefore give
[gγi±​​,x]=0.
(16)
These 2(ℓ−1) roots are distinct: their quotient images are the two signs of a basis, which are distinct. Their one-dimensional root spaces consequently contribute 2(ℓ−1) independent vectors to the Lie algebra centralizer.
In addition, the ℓ−1 dimensional subspace kerα⊂t commutes with x, because [h,x]=α(h)x. The line Cx⊂gα​ also commutes with x. These contributions are independent by the root-space decomposition; in particular none of the selected endpoint roots is α. Thus the centralizer lower bound for a root vector is
dimZg​(x)≥2(ℓ−1)+(ℓ−1)+1=3ℓ−2.​
(17)
If x=0, then Zg​(x)=g. The root system contains the 2ℓ distinct roots ±αi​, so dimg=ℓ+∣Φ∣≥3ℓ, which also proves the desired inequality. The argument includes rank one, where the list of βi​ is empty.

Write an element of the diagonal Cartan subalgebra as
H(t)=diag(t1​,t2​,t3​,−t1​,−t2​,−t3​),εi​(H(t))=ti​.
(18)
The root system of this Special orthogonal Lie algebra is
Φ={±εi​±εj​:1≤i<j≤3}.​
(19)
Thus the maps requested are H(t)↦±ti​±tj​, with the two signs independent. This is the D3 root system, with twelve roots.
For a direct check, the defining matrix identity gives the block form X=(AC​B−AT​) with BT=−B and CT=−C. If Eij​ denotes a matrix unit, the root space for εi​−εj​ is spanned by Eij​−Ej+3,i+3​; those for εi​+εj​ and −εi​−εj​ are spanned respectively by Ei,j+3​−Ej,i+3​ and Ei+3,j​−Ej+3,i​. Commuting these with H(t) gives exactly the displayed maps.

An automorphism of a root system outside the Weyl group is the sign change
τ(ε1​)=ε1​,τ(ε2​)=ε2​,τ(ε3​)=−ε3​.​
(23)
It preserves the set {±εi​±εj​}, fixes α1​, and interchanges α2​ and α3​. It therefore realizes the nontrivial symmetry of the Dynkin diagram while preserving the chosen root basis.
To verify that τ∈/W, note that s1​ swaps ε1​,ε2​, s2​ swaps ε2​,ε3​, and s3​ swaps ε2​,ε3​ while changing both their signs. Every product of these generators is a signed permutation with an even number of sign changes, whereas τ has one sign change. Thus it cannot be a Weyl group element. The parity here concerns sign changes, rather than the determinant of the permutation.

The Dynkin diagram in part (b) is a three-node chain, hence D3​=A3​. By the classification of complex semisimple Lie algebras by their root systems, this gives the Isomorphism between so6 and sl4:
so6​≅sl4​.​
(24)
There is also a concrete realization. On U=C4, choose a nonzero alternating four-form vol. The six-dimensional exterior square Λ2U carries the nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form B specified by
B(u∧v,w∧z)=vol(u,v,w,z).
(25)
It is symmetric because exchanging two pairs entails four transpositions, and it is nondegenerate because each basis bivector pairs nontrivially with its complementary pair. The special linear Lie algebra acts by A(u∧v)=Au∧v+u∧Av. Its action on Λ4U is multiplication by trA=0, so it preserves B. We obtain a nonzero Lie algebra homomorphism sl4​→so(Λ2U,B). Its kernel is an ideal of a Lie algebra, hence zero because sl4​ is a simple Lie algebra. Both sides have dimension 15, so this homomorphism is a Lie algebra isomorphism.

For a dominant integral weight λ, let V(λ) be the finite-dimensional irreducible highest-weight representation with highest weight λ. Set ρ=21​∑α∈Φ+​α, the half-sum of positive roots. With α∨ the coroot of α, the Weyl dimension formula is
dimV(λ)=α∈Φ+∏​⟨ρ,α∨⟩⟨λ+ρ,α∨⟩​.​
(26)
Here Φ+ is the positive system of a root system specified by the chosen root basis. The fundamental weights are the basis dual to the simple coroots: ⟨ωi​,αj∨​⟩=δij​. Thus λ=∑i​ai​ωi​ with nonnegative integers ai​. The denominators are positive; equivalently one can use (λ+ρ,α)/(ρ,α) in each factor, since the coroot normalization cancels.

In the defining representation of the symplectic Lie algebra sp4​, the weights are ε1​,ε2​,−ε1​,−ε2​. Their highest weight, for the root basis of part (b), is therefore
highest weight(V)=ε1​=ω1​.​

If v1​ is a highest-weight vector of weight ε1​, then v1​⊗v1​ is a highest-weight vector of weight 2ω1​ in the symmetric square. By Weyl complete reducibility theorem, this ensures an irreducible summand V(2ω1​) occurs. Its dimension is 10 by part (b), exhausting the symmetric square.
Let ω denote the preserved symplectic form. The symplectic contraction of an exterior square is the nonzero equivariant map
c:Λ2V⟶C,c(v∧w)=ω(v,w),
(31)
where the target is a trivial Lie algebra representation. Thus its kernel has dimension 5. Choose a weight vector v2​ of weight ε2​ in a symplectic basis, with ω(v1​,v2​)=0. The vector v1​∧v2​ belongs to this kernel and has weight ε1​+ε2​=ω2​. It is a highest-weight vector: none of ω2​+α, for α∈Φ+, is a weight of Λ2V, whose weights are ±ε1​±ε2​ and zero. The formal character of a weight module is chV=∑μ​(dimVμ​)eμ, where eμ are formal basis symbols in the group algebra of the weight lattice, satisfying eμeν=eμ+ν. For a finite-dimensional irreducible highest-weight representation, the highest weight has weight multiplicity one. Another standard result is that weight multiplicities are invariant under the Weyl group: dimVwμ​=dimVμ​.
A minuscule representation has all its weights in a single Weyl group orbit. Since the highest weight λ is present, that orbit is Wλ, and the invariance just stated shows every point of it is present with multiplicity one. Consequently the formal character of a minuscule representation is
chV(λ)=μ∈Wλ∑​eμ,dimV(λ)=∣Wλ∣.​
(33)
The sum is over distinct weights, rather than over all elements of W: summing ewλ over w∈W would count each weight ∣StabW​(λ)∣ times.

Use the coroot α∨ in the pairing. The original PDF retains this mark, which is lost in the local TeX transcription. Fix any positive root α, and put n=⟨λ,α∨⟩. For a dominant integral weight, n is a nonnegative integer; positive coroots are nonnegative integral combinations of simple coroots.
Let vλ​ be a highest-weight vector, and restrict to the sl2 subalgebra associated with a root α. It is killed by its raising operator eα​ and has hα​ eigenvalue n. If n≥1, its lowering operator produces a nonzero vector: otherwise [eα​,fα​]vλ​=nvλ​ would vanish. Therefore λ−α is a weight.
Choose a Weyl group invariant positive-definite inner product on the real weight space. All weights of a minuscule representation have the same norm, since they belong to Wλ. But
∥λ−α∥2−∥λ∥2=−2(λ,α)+(α,α)=(1−n)(α,α).
(34)
If n≥2, this is negative, a contradiction. Thus for every positive root,
⟨λ,α∨⟩∈{0,1},in particular ⟨λ,α∨⟩≤1.​

We first prove the useful lemma that dominant root-lattice highest weights have zero weight. Write Q=ZΦ for the root lattice, and let a dominant integral weight λ∈Q be the highest weight of a finite-dimensional irreducible representation.
First, λ is a nonnegative integral combination of simple roots. Indeed, write λ=λ+​−λ−​, separating its positive and negative coefficients in the root basis. The two parts have disjoint supports, and distinct simple roots have nonpositive inner product, so (λ+​,λ−​)≤0. If λ−​=0, then
(λ,λ−​)=(λ+​,λ−​)−(λ−​,λ−​)<0.
(36)
On the other hand, dominance gives (λ,αi​)≥0 for each simple root, hence (λ,λ−​)≥0. This contradiction proves λ−​=0.
Now suppose a nonzero weight μ=∑i​mi​αi​ has all mi​∈Z≥0​. The identity
0<(μ,μ)=∑i​mi​(μ,αi​)
(37)
shows that some i satisfies mi​>0 and ⟨μ,αi∨​⟩>0. We use the standard sl2 Lie algebra fact that its lowering operator is injective on any positive h eigenspace in a finite-dimensional representation. This follows from the classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations: in each irreducible V(n), the only weight killed by the lowering operator is the lowest weight −n≤0.
Consequently a nonzero vector of weight μ lowers to a nonzero vector of weight μ−αi​. Its simple-root coefficients remain nonnegative and their sum decreases by one. Starting at λ, repeated lowering must therefore reach the zero weight. Notice that intermediate weights need not remain dominant; positivity of the chosen coroot pairing is enough at each step.
For a minuscule representation, every weight belongs to Wλ, so the zero weight just obtained lies in this orbit. Every Weyl group element is invertible, and wλ=0 forces λ=0. This proves that minuscule weights in the root lattice are zero. Under the assumption X=Q, every possible highest weight lies in Q, hence
X=ZΦ ⟹ the only minuscule irreducible representation is V(0), the trivial one.​
(38)
Here V(0) is the one-dimensional trivial Lie algebra representation, by the classification of finite-dimensional irreducible highest-weight representations.
