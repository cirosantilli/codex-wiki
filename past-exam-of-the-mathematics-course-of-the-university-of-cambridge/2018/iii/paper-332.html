Take x in the injection direction and y along the flat interface. The specified U must be a Darcy velocity, so the pore-fluid velocity and unperturbed interface speed are U/ϕ. Write the perturbed interface as x=X(t)+η(y,t), with X˙=U/ϕ, and choose the normal from fluid 1 into fluid 2. The background pressure gradients from Darcy law are ∂x​pi(0)​=−μi​U/k.

p1′​=A1​(t)eα(x−X)eiαy,p2′​=A2​(t)e−α(x−X)eiαy,η=a(t)eiαy.

If x is measured from the unperturbed front and γ denotes its local apparent tension, set X=0 to obtain the usual fixed-coefficient expression. For a physically fixed spatial gradient, X=Ut/ϕ and the displayed rate is an instantaneous, generally time-dependent growth rate; the amplitude solves a˙=σ(α;X(t))a, rather than one constant exponential law for the entire displacement. The parameter β has dimensions of inverse length; the absolute tension gradient is γβ.
Assume 1+βX>0, as required for positive apparent surface tension, and define A=(μ2​−μ1​)U−kγβκ0​. If A>0, unstable modes satisfy 0<α2<A/[kγ(1+βX)]. Differentiating the cubic dispersion relation gives the fastest-growing mode

0<U≤Uc​=μ2​−μ1​kγβκ0​​,Ca≤2Mk(1+M)βκ0​​​.
(9)
If βκ0​≤0, no positive injection speed eliminates every long-wave unstable mode in an unbounded interface. The prescribed wetting variation is treated as an effective normal pressure law for porous-media flow.

Let w be downward Darcy flux, measured per unit total horizontal area. Neglect the resistance of the displaced phase, capillary pressure, and horizontal subsurface flow. Taking atmospheric pressure as zero, the upper end of the saturated column has pressure ρgh and its advancing lower end has pressure zero. Across a column of depth l, Darcy law therefore gives
w=μk​(lρgh​+ρg)=νgk​(1+lh​).
(10)
The pressure head from the overlying layer supplies h/l, and gravity supplies one. Because the new liquid fills a pore volume ϕdl per unit area, the vertical imbibition under a draining liquid layer obeys ϕl˙=w. Volume conservation gives h+ϕl=h0​, so, until the surface layer is exhausted,
l˙=ϕνgk​(lh0​​+1−ϕ),h=h0​−ϕl,0<l<h0​/ϕ​.
(11)
The initial condition is understood as a limiting solution of this singular equation. For 0<ϕ<1, separation of variables gives
t=gkϕν​[1−ϕl​−(1−ϕ)2h0​​log(1+h0​(1−ϕ)l​)].
(12)
At small penetration, expansion of the logarithm yields t∼ϕνl2/(2gkh0​), hence
l(t)∼(ϕν2gkh0​​t)1/2​.
(13)
A sufficient dimensional early-time condition is t≪ϕνh0​/(gk), for which l≪h0​. The printed t≪1 needs a chosen time unit or nondimensionalization. In the limiting case ϕ=1, l2=2gkh0​t/ν holds exactly until h=0. The divergent initial Darcy flux is an idealization of sudden contact with a dry continuum; it cannot describe penetration below the pore scale.
For the spreading surface layer, measure z upwards from the substrate. A thin, slow gravity current has hydrostatic pressure p=ρg(h−z) and a leading horizontal viscous balance νuzz​=ghx​. A no-slip boundary condition at z=0 and a stress-free boundary condition at z=h give
u=νg​hx​(2z2​−hz),q=∫0h​udz=−3νg​h3hx​.
(14)
This lubrication gravity-current flux drives horizontal spreading down the surface-pressure gradient. Surface volume conservation is ht​+qx​=−w, while conservation in the pores is ϕlt​=w. Combining these balances gives the deep-substrate drainage of a gravity current model
ht​−3νg​(h3hx​)x​=−νgk​(1+lh​)=−ϕlt​​.
(15)
Drainage transfers liquid from the surface layer into the substrate. Indeed (h+ϕl)t​+qx​=0.
For definiteness take a one-sided current on 0<x<xN​(t), supplied at x=0. The imposed volume is per unit span, so V0​ has dimensions of area. The boundary conditions and volume conservation constraint are
h,l≥0,h=l=0 outside the wetted region,h(xN​,t)=l(xN​,t)=0,q(xN​,t)=0,q(0,t)=τ33V0​t2​,∫0xN​(t)​[h(x,t)+ϕl(x,t)]dx=V0​(t/τ)3.​
(16)
The source starts from zero injected volume. Initially dry points acquire l>0 only when the advancing current reaches them; the formula h/l is not applied outside that region. At the advancing nose these are limiting conditions for a singular drainage equation. For a source feeding two identical sides, each side has half the volume and inlet flux; only the corresponding numerical prefactors change. The integral and inlet-flux conditions are equivalent once the local balance and nose conditions hold.
The physical approximations are lubrication theory, negligible inertia, slowly varying surface height, small pore-scale Reynolds number, and negligible capillary entry pressure. Treating the substrate as vertically draining columns also requires its lateral flow to be negligible and its depth to exceed the penetration depth. The model is valid while surface liquid remains available above each draining column.
For a similarity solution, let h and l scale as ta, and let the current length scale as tb. Since h/l then has no time dependence, drainage requires a−1=0. Balancing ht​ with the horizontal spreading term gives a−1=4a−2b, so a=1 and b=2. These exponents also give injected volume ta+b=t3, explaining the particular supply law.
Define scales
C=(gτ6νV02​​)1/5,D=(ντ9gV03​​)1/5,
(17)
which satisfy CD=V0​/τ3 and D2=(g/ν)C3. The cubic-input similarity for a draining gravity current is
ξ=Dt2x​,h(x,t)=CtH(ξ),l(x,t)=CtL(ξ),xN​(t)=ξN​(ντ9gV03​​)1/5t2​.

H−2ξH′−31​(H3H′)′=−K(1+LH​),ϕ(L−2ξL′)=K(1+LH​)​,
(19)
where
K=Cgk/ν​=k(ν3V0​g3τ3​)2/5.
(20)
Thus only K and ϕ remain in the dimensionless problem. Its constraints are
H(ξN​)=L(ξN​)=0,H3H′→0 as ξ→ξN−​,−H(0)3H′(0)=9,∫0ξN​​[H+ϕL]dξ=1,H,L>0 for 0<ξ<ξN​.​
(21)

For an axisymmetric thin elastic plate, define ∇r2​=r−1∂r​(r∂r​). Uniform excess pressure and bending stiffness B give B∇r4​h=p. The roof is attached to undeformed rock at the front, giving clamped boundary conditions h(R)=hr​(R)=0, and regularity excludes singular displacement or curvature at r=0. A regular particular solution is pr4/(64B); adding a constant and a multiple of r2 and enforcing the two edge conditions gives the axisymmetric clamped-plate deflection
h=64Bp​(R2−r2)2.
(22)
Integrating the magma intrusion volume,
V=2π∫0R​rhdr=192BπpR6​,
(23)
therefore yields
h(r,t)=πR23V​(1−R2r2​)2,p=πR6192BV​,κi​=hrr​(R)=πR424V​​.
(24)
Here the axisymmetric phrase requires the radial plate biharmonic operator; a Cartesian one-dimensional beam equation would not give the same pressure coefficient. Hydrostatic contributions are excluded as instructed.
For the early front, let Up​=R˙ be the leading propagation speed. The fluid and fracture radii differ only within a local region of length lp​≪R. In lubrication theory, the elastic pressure gradient near the front is of order Bhf​/lp5​, and the volume flux through the gap is of order Bhf4​/(μlp5​). In a frame following the fluid front that flux is of order Up​hf​. The viscous peeling of an elastic plate law is therefore
Up​∼μlp5​Bhf3​​.
(25)
Match the peeling region to the interior curvature: hf​/lp2​∼κi​. The stated vapour-gap relation hf​≃pT​lp4​/(24B), with pT​>0 interpreted as the relevant pressure magnitude, then gives
lp​∼(pT​Bκi​​)1/2,hf​∼pT​Bκi2​​.
(26)
Substitution gives the vapour-tip peeling of a magma intrusion speed
R˙∼μpT1/2​B3/2​κi7/2​∼μpT1/2​B3/2​R14V7/2​​.
(27)
With constant supplied volume flux Q and negligible initial intrusion volume, V=Qt. Integrating R14R˙∼B3/2Q7/2t7/2/(μpT1/2​) gives R15∼B3/2Q7/2t9/2/(μpT1/2​), or
R(t)∼(pT​μ2B3Q7t9​)1/30​.
(28)
Order-one coefficients require the full local peeling profile. This early regime is a front-controlled asymptotic regime, not an assertion that a continuum peeling zone remains thin at arbitrarily small t.
When fracture toughness instead imposes fixed κf​>0, equating it to the outer edge curvature gives the toughness-controlled magma intrusion law
R(V)=(πκf​24V​)1/4​.
(29)
Its pressure can be written in either useful form
p=R28Bκf​​=AV−1/2,A=38π​​Bκf3/2​​.
(30)
The pressure decreases as the intrusion grows on this advancing branch.
If Vr0​ is the reservoir volume before any magma was transferred to the initially negligible intrusion, volume conservation and negligible conduit storage give Vr​=Vr0​−V. With the supplied pressure-volume law and conduit conductance, the reservoir-fed magma intrusion obeys
dtdV​=β[E(Vr0​−V)−38π​​V​Bκf3/2​​]​.
(31)
In this law E is an effective reservoir pressure-volume stiffness, with dimensions of pressure per volume. If the stated initial reservoir volume refers instead to the start of the late regime, when the intrusion already has volume Vi​, replace Vr0​ by the total VT​=Vr0​+Vi​ throughout. An independent value of Vi​ would then be needed.
For an advancing solution, inflow ceases when the reservoir and intrusion pressures equalize. Its final volume is the larger positive root in 0<V<Vr0​ of
V∞​​(Vr0​−V∞​)=EA​,R∞​=(πκf​24V∞​​)1/4​.
(32)
Equivalently, y=V∞​​ solves y3−Vr0​y+A/E=0. The larger root is essential: V​(Vr0​−V) rises from zero to a maximum 2Vr03/2​/(33​) at V=Vr0​/3 and then decreases. A nonempty interval of positive inflow exists only if
EA​<33​2​Vr03/2​​.
(33)
There are then two positive equilibria V−​<Vr0​/3<V+​. At equilibrium the derivative of the volume ordinary differential equation is βE(Vr0​−3V)/(2V), so V−​ is unstable and V+​ has asymptotic stability. An initial advancing intrusion with V−​<Vi​<V+​ approaches V∞​=V+​. The equality case has a double equilibrium and no positive-inflow interval; above the threshold there is no advancing equilibrium branch of this model.
The late formula p∝V−1/2 is singular at V=0 and cannot nucleate the intrusion: the earlier propagation regime must supply its finite seed. Nor does this fixed-edge-curvature propagation law prescribe how an irreversible fracture closes under negative inflow. The final-size answer is conditional on reaching the advancing late branch. In particular the reservoir is generally not emptied, and β controls the approach rate rather than the equilibrium size.

Use a quasistatic surface energy balance with negligible heat storage in the surface or ice, a linear ice temperature profile, and a fixed freezing temperature at the ice-ocean interface. These are the assumptions behind the single-category sea-ice thermodynamic model used below. Write θ=t/τ, FO​=λO​(TO​−Tm​)>0 and
r=λA​ΔTFO​​,a=kλA​​,H=(Sκτ​)1/2,S=cp​ΔTL​.
(34)
Here λA​ and λO​ are heat transfer coefficients, k is thermal conductivity, κ=k/(ρcp​) is thermal diffusivity, and S is the inverse Stefan number. Here L is latent heat per unit mass. The thermal k denotes conductivity; the previous questions use k for permeability.
While there is open water, the upward ocean heat supply balances the loss to air: λO​(TO​−Ts​)=λA​(Ts​−TA​). Thus
Tswater​=λA​+λO​λA​TA​+λO​TO​​​.
(35)
Freezing begins when this surface reaches Tm​ on the cooling part of the annual cycle, which requires r<1 and gives
t1​=τarccos(−r),2π​<τt1​​<π​.
(36)
For r≥1 the surface never cools strictly below freezing and no positive ice thickness develops in this model. The limit r→0 gives t1​→πτ/2.
With ice present and a cold surface, thermal conduction and Newton's law of cooling give k(Tm​−Ts​)/h=λA​(Ts​−TA​). The surface temperature is
Tsice​=k+λA​hkTm​+λA​hTA​​(TA​<Tm​)​.
(37)
During surface melting the temperature is capped at Ts​=Tm​; the ice is not permitted to follow the above formula to a temperature higher than its melting point. The upward conductive heat flux during the cold phase is
FC​=1+ahλA​ΔT(−cosθ)​.
(38)
The Stefan condition at the ocean interface gives ρLh˙=FC​−FO​. Under the permitted approximation of negligible ocean heat during growth,
h˙=Sκ​h+1/a−cos(t/τ)​.
(39)
Starting from h(t1​)=0, integration yields the implicit growth law
2h(t)2​+λA​k​h(t)=Sκτ​[sin(t1​/τ)−sin(t/τ)]​.
(40)
The leading maximum occurs when the air returns to freezing from below:
t2​≃23π​τ,hm​=[(λA​k​)2+S2κτ​(1+sin(t1​/τ))]1/2−λA​k​​.
(41)
The equality for hm​ is within the ocean-heat-neglected growth model, with the freezing time set by the open-water balance. If aH≫1, conductive resistance through the ice dominates the atmospheric boundary resistance at maximum thickness. Consequently
hm​∼[1+sin(t1​/τ)]1/2(S2κτ​)1/2​.
(42)
This reproduces the displayed large-conductance limit. A sufficient condition for the quasistatic temperature assumption is hm2​/κ≪τ; large S ensures this in the thick-ice scaling.
The approximation concerning ocean heat needs care. Its ratio to atmospheric heat removal during the cold phase is
FC​FO​​=−cosθr(1+ah)​.
(43)
It is uniformly small away from the beginnings and ends of winter provided
r(1+ahm​)≪1,that is,λO​(TO​−Tm​)≪1+λA​hm​/kλA​ΔT​​.
(44)
For the thick-ice limit this requires both r≪1 and raH≪1. This also makes the integrated ocean heat small relative to the latent heat of the maximum ice column. It is not possible to neglect FO​ pointwise throughout the closed growth interval: at the actual freezing onset it balances the atmospheric removal, and at the true maximum the growth rate is zero because the two fluxes again balance. The ocean-heat correction to seasonal ice growth therefore gives narrow endpoint corrections. In the full model the maximum is slightly earlier than 3πτ/2, with
−cos(t2​/τ)=r[1+ah(t2​)].
(45)
Thus t2​=3πτ/2−O(τr[1+ahm​]) in the stated approximation, rather than an exact maximum time for finite ocean heat.
For 3πτ/2≤t≤2πτ, while ice survives, both the warm air and ocean melt it. The surface is at Tm​, and the total Stefan condition is
ρLh˙=−λA​ΔTcos(t/τ)−FO​.
(46)
Integrating from the leading maximum gives
h(t)=hm​−ρLλA​ΔTτ​[1+sin(t/τ)+r(τt​−23π​)]
(47)
until its first zero. The physical thickness is then zero for the rest of this warm interval and the open-water temperature applies. Therefore at the end of the first year,
h(2πτ)≃max{0,hm​−kSλA​κτ​[1+2π​λA​λO​​ΔTTO​−Tm​​]}​.
(48)
Positive ice at that time is equivalent, in the specified approximation, to
kSλA​κτ​[1+2π​λA​λO​​ΔTTO​−Tm​​]<hm​​.
(49)
This establishes the algebraic criterion printed in the paper.
There is, however, an actual qualification to the paper's word “perennial.” This inequality tests peak-temperature sea-ice survival, since t=2πτ is the next atmospheric temperature maximum. Surface melting continues until at least t=5πτ/2, so positive thickness at the maximum does not guarantee survival throughout the summer. In the same approximation, survival through that full warm half-cycle would require the stronger inequality
hm​>ρLλA​ΔTτ​(2+πr).
(50)
In fact, starting ice-free under the specified zero-mean sinusoidal forcing, even the zero-ocean-heat upper bound on winter growth cannot meet it. In that limit t1​=πτ/2 and
2hm2​​+ahm​​=2H2,hm​<2aH2=ρL2λA​ΔTτ​,
(51)
since hm2​/2>0. The expression on the right is already the atmospheric melt over the entire subsequent warm half-cycle. Positive ocean heat can only reduce the winter ice and increase summer melt. Thus the printed condition is a first-year peak-temperature survival test, not a sufficient criterion for literal year-round ice in this model. For example, with aH=1/2 and very small positive r, it gives hm​/H≃2(2​−1)≃0.828, while first-year melt is approximately 0.5H but full warm-season melt is approximately H. Ice is present at the year's endpoint and disappears later that same summer. No reinterpretation of the printed inequality as guaranteed perennial cover is justified without an additional seasonal assumption.
