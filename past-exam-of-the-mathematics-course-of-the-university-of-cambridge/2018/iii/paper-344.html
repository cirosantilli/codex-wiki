Write E(t)=δA/δx(t) for the functional derivative of the action. Under time reversal in classical mechanics, x˙ changes sign whereas E is even by assumption. Thus the forward and reversed Gaussian white noise histories associated with the same geometric path are
fF​=ζx˙−E,fB​=−ζx˙−E,fF2​−fB2​=−4ζx˙E.
(1)
The Onsager--Machlup path probability therefore gives
logPB​PF​​=−2σ21​∫t1​t2​​(fF2​−fB2​)dt,PB​PF​​=exp[σ22ζ​∫t1​t2​​x˙δx(t)δA​dt]​.
(2)
Here the path probabilities are densities conditional on their respective initial states; they are not separately normalized bridges conditioned on both endpoints. The noise-to-path Jacobian determinant must be treated with a consistent discretization: it cancels when it is invariant under time reversal in classical mechanics. This is the usual additive-noise Langevin dynamics convention, including a constant-mass Underdamped Langevin dynamics or the time-reversal invariance of a path Jacobian in midpoint overdamped Langevin dynamics. A completely arbitrary velocity-dependent Lagrangian would require specifying that measure rather than deducing its cancellation solely from the parity of E.

For an autonomous Lagrangian, the Euler-Lagrange equation uses
δxδA​=Lx​−dtd​Lx˙​,H=x˙Lx˙​−L.
(3)
Apply the chain rule to the Hamiltonian along an arbitrary smooth path, without assuming the unforced Euler-Lagrange equation:
dtdH​=x¨Lx˙​+x˙dtd​Lx˙​−Lx​x˙−Lx˙​x¨=−x˙δxδA​.
(4)
Consequently the energy balance for an autonomous Lagrangian is
−∫t1​t2​​x˙δx(t)δA​dt=H2​−H1​​.
(5)
With explicit time dependence, an additional −∂t​L enters dH/dt. For ideal Gaussian white noise, the identity is understood through smooth-noise regularization or the Stratonovich chain rule. The original PDF correctly differentiates L with respect to x˙ in H; the supplied TeX's derivative with respect to x, its endpoint t2, and its ordinary derivative of A are transcription errors.

At thermal equilibrium, microscopic reversibility equates the probabilities of a path and its reversed path when both include their Boltzmann distribution initial weights. Denote their endpoint states by z1​,z2​, including velocity if needed. Since the Hamiltonian is even under time reversal in classical mechanics,
ρeq​(z1​)PF​=ρeq​(z2​)PB​,ρeq​(z)∝e−βH(z),β=kB​T1​.
(6)
This detailed balance condition and the energy balance for an autonomous Lagrangian yield
PB​PF​​=e−β(H2​−H1​),σ22ζ​=β,σ2=2ζkB​T​.
(7)
This is the fluctuation-dissipation relation for a Langevin particle: the strength of Gaussian white noise is fixed by the damping and temperature, with kB​ the Boltzmann constant.
For an equilibrium coarse-grained variable, the unresolved microscopic states contribute entropy; their statistical weight is encoded in the Helmholtz free energy, rather than in a single microscopic energy. Relative to the same reference measure, ρeq​(x)∝e−βF(x), so microscopic reversibility becomes
PB​PF​​=e−β(F2​−F1​)​.
(8)
This extension assumes an equilibrium coarse-grained description with reversible path statistics; externally driven dynamics need not obey this relation.

The Neumann boundary conditions give Fourier modes q=nπ/L, including the spatially uniform mode q=0. In the frame translating with the mean deposition height, that mode has no restoring force and obeys
dh0​=σdWt​,h0​(t)=h0​(0)+σWt​,Varh0​(t)=Varh0​(0)+σ2t​.
(9)
Here Wt​ is Brownian motion, independent of the initial height. More generally ⟨h0​(t)2⟩=⟨h0​(0)2⟩+σ2t when these second moments exist. Removing the mean deposition drift does not remove the Gaussian white noise.
The Brownian zero mode of a fluctuating interface has no stationary distribution on the real height axis for σ2>0. This conclusion does not depend on assuming finite variance: a stationary characteristic function would satisfy μ​(k)=μ​(k)e−σ2k2t/2, hence vanish for every k=0, contradicting its continuity at zero and μ​(0)=1. Any stationary joint distribution of the whole height would have a stationary zero-mode marginal, which is impossible. Thus the full unpinned height has no Boltzmann equilibrium. Pinning the mean height, or retaining only the nonzero Fourier modes, removes this obstruction. The PDF has h˙q​; the TeX's ℏq​ is a transcription error.

For q=0 and α>0, each Fourier mode is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with decay rate rq​=αq2. Its explicit Ornstein-Uhlenbeck solution is
hq​(t)=e−rq​thq​(0)+σ∫0t​e−rq​(t−s)dWq​(s),
(10)
so the Itô isometry gives
⟨hq​(t)2⟩=e−2rq​t⟨hq​(0)2⟩+2rq​σ2​(1−e−2rq​t).
(11)
To identify the overdamped Langevin dynamics, choose a friction ζq​>0, a harmonic oscillator potential Vq​=ζq​αq2hq2​/2, and force noise fq​=ζq​ηq​. The fluctuation-dissipation relation for a Langevin particle then defines kB​Teff​=ζq​σ2/2. Its Boltzmann distribution is
Pq​(h)=πσ2αq2​​exp(−σ2αq2h2​),⟨hq2​⟩st​=2αq2σ2​∝q−2​.
(12)
This is the stationary spectrum of a linear fluctuating interface, also following from the equipartition theorem. The numerical prefactor uses exactly the mode-noise normalization given in the paper. The nonzero modes can therefore have a stationary Gaussian distribution even though the unpinned zero mode cannot.

Use the paper's velocity gradient convention Aij​=∂i​vj​, so Ω=(A−AT)/2 and D=(A+AT)/2. In a rigid motion there is rotation but no rate-of-strain tensor: D=0. A polar order parameter attached to the material must rotate exactly with it. In this convention its material derivative is −Ωp, and its corotational derivative of a polar vector must vanish.
Thus the compensating term is +Ωp with coefficient exactly one. This follows from an objective time derivative and is independent of material properties. By contrast, the response to Dp describes deformation and flow alignment of a polar order parameter; its coefficient ξ can depend on the material. The transposed velocity gradient convention is essential to the sign of Ω.

Let hi​=δF/δpi​ be the polar molecular field, with the positive functional derivative convention used in the paper. During a pure advective displacement uj​, the polar order parameter changes by
δpi​=−uj​∂j​pi​−Ωiju​pj​+ξDiju​pj​,Ωiju​=2∂i​uj​−∂j​ui​​,Diju​=2∂i​uj​+∂j​ui​​.
(13)
The free energy change is δF=∫hi​δpi​ddr. Assume periodic boundaries, or boundary conditions that eliminate the surface work, and incompressibility ∂j​uj​=0. Integration by parts in the advective term gives
−∫hi​uj​∂j​pi​ddr=∫uj​pi​∂j​hi​ddr=∫Σij(1)​∂i​uj​ddr,∂i​Σij(1)​=−pk​∂j​hk​.
(14)
Relabelling the dummy indices in the rotational and flow alignment of a polar order parameter terms gives
−hi​Ωiju​pj​=2pi​hj​−pj​hi​​∂i​uj​,ξhi​Diju​pj​=2ξ(pi​hj​+pj​hi​)​∂i​uj​.
(15)
Therefore δF=∫Σijp​∂i​uj​ddr, with the reversible stress of a polar liquid crystal
Σijp​=Σij(1)​+2pi​hj​−pj​hi​​+2ξ(pi​hj​+pj​hi​)​,∂i​Σij(1)​=−pk​∂j​hk​​.
(16)
For a local free-energy density f(p,∇p), an explicit choice is
Σij(1)​=(f−pk​hk​)δij​−∂(∂i​pk​)∂f​∂j​pk​.
(17)
Its divergence is exactly −pk​∂j​hk​, by the Euler-Lagrange equation for hk​. A pressure term can be reassigned in an incompressible flow; the chosen representative realizes the printed divergence without that ambiguity. The force density ∂i​Σijp​ has mechanical power −∫Σijp​∂i​vj​, the negative of the advective free energy rate, which checks the stress sign.

The polar molecular field is
hi​=(a+b∣p∣2)pi​−κ∇2pi​.
(18)
For a spatially uniform polar order parameter, h=(a+bp2)p, so it is colinear with p and its magnitude is ∣h∣=∣a+bp2∣p. The scalar factor can be negative; colinearity does not necessarily mean parallel orientation.
For the simple shear flow, the paper's velocity gradient convention gives
Ω=2g​(01​−10​),D=2g​(01​10​).
(19)
Substitute these matrices into p˙​=−Ωp+ξDp−Γh. Projection along and perpendicular to the polar order parameter yields
pp˙​​=2ξg​sin2θ−Γ(a+bp2),θ˙=2g​(ξcos2θ−1).
(20)
Assume g=0, Γ>0, b>0 and p>0. The flow-alignment angle in planar shear must satisfy
cos2θ=ξ1​,∣ξ∣≥1,tan2θ=ξ+1ξ−1​​.
(21)
For ξ=−1, use the cosine equation: θ=π/2 modulo π, and the displayed tangent is infinite. The radial equation gives
p2=b−a+ξgsin2θ/(2Γ)​=b−a ± gξ2−1​/(2Γ)​​,
(22)
where the sign distinguishes the angular branches, and only positive right-hand sides are ordered solutions. Linearizing the angular equation gives δθ˙=−gξsin2θδθ. For ∣ξ∣>1, the stable angular branch therefore has
pstable2​=b−a+∣g∣ξ2−1​/(2Γ)​>0​.
(23)
Its radial relaxation eigenvalue is −2Γbp2<0. At ∣ξ∣=1 the angular linearization is marginal and p2=−a/b requires a<0. If g=0, the shear restriction disappears and any orientation is allowed for p2=−a/b>0. The printed request for dependence only on a,b,Γ,g omits ξ: in general the magnitude necessarily depends on it.

At ξ=0, the polar order parameter obeys
θ˙=−2g​,p˙​=−Γ(a+bp2)p.
(24)
For a<0, b>0, the magnitude relaxes to −a/b​ while the direction rotates continuously. Thus the likely ordered behaviour is tumbling rather than a fixed alignment.
More generally, for ∣ξ∣<1 and g=0, ξcos2θ−1 never vanishes, so the tumbling of a polar order parameter persists. The polar order parameter completes a full 2π rotation in
Tpolar​=∣g∣1−ξ2​4π​​,
(25)
obtained by integrating dt=2∣dθ∣/(∣g∣(1−ξcos2θ)) over a full rotation. An unoriented nematic director repeats after half this period, whereas a polar order parameter distinguishes opposite directions. For ξ=0 its magnitude can also oscillate. If the polar order parameter decays to zero, its orientation is no longer a physical observable; the tumbling conclusion concerns a persistent ordered state.

Take b>0 for a stable quartic Landau free energy. The cubic-term removal in a conserved quartic Landau free energy uses ϕ=ψ−c/(3b) and gives, apart from a constant,
f=2a′​ψ2+4b​ψ4+d′ψ,a′=a−3bc2​,d′=d−3bac​+27b22c3​.
(26)
For a closed binary fluid mixture, the compositional order parameter has fixed integral. Adding d∫ϕ therefore adds the same constant to every allowed state. Equivalently, in the common-tangent construction for phase coexistence, a linear term changes the tangent slope, hence the chemical potential, by d, but leaves the coexistence compositions unchanged. This is why linear composition bias leaves coexistence compositions unchanged; it would not mean an unchanged composition at a fixed externally imposed chemical potential.
In the shifted variable, the binodal compositions are ψ±​=±−a′/b​ for a′<0. They merge at
ac​=3bc2​,ϕc​=−3bc​​.
(27)
The continuous phase transition therefore has the same scalar quartic Landau theory as the symmetric binary fluid mixture, with a shifted critical composition and control parameter. In the mean-field approximation, the composition difference vanishes with order-parameter critical exponent 1/2. This argument presumes the critical composition lies in the physically accessible composition range; conservation affects the dynamics, not this static phase coexistence construction.

The nematic order parameter is a symmetric second-rank tensor and a traceless second-rank tensor describing orientational anisotropy. For molecular unit axes s, Qij​ is proportional to ⟨si​sj​−δij​/3⟩. It vanishes in an isotropic phase and is unchanged by head-tail reversal s↦−s, unlike a polar order parameter. For uniaxial nematic order, the nematic director is thus an unoriented axis.
One convention for the Landau-de Gennes free energy through fourth order, respecting rotational symmetry, is
f(Q)=2a​TrQ2+3c​TrQ3+4b​(TrQ2)2​,b>0.
(28)
For a traceless second-rank tensor in three dimensions, the Cayley-Hamilton theorem gives TrQ4=(TrQ2)2/2, so there is only one independent quartic rotational invariant of a symmetric traceless tensor. The only possible linear scalar is TrQ=0; without an external anisotropy no linear term survives.
The cubic rotational invariant of a symmetric traceless tensor distinguishes prolate and oblate forms of uniaxial nematic order. Head-tail reversal leaves Q unchanged and does not impose Q↦−Q. Thus a cubic term is allowed in three dimensions and generically produces a first-order phase transition, rather than a symmetry-enforced continuous onset. In two dimensions a symmetric traceless second-rank tensor has eigenvalues s,−s, hence TrQ3=0: the cubic invariant vanishes identically in two dimensions. This is a statement about the Landau free energy; fluctuations in two dimensions require a separate treatment.

For the stated uniaxial nematic order normalization, the nematic director is an eigenvector of Q with eigenvalue λ, and the two perpendicular eigenvalues are −λ/2. Therefore
TrQ2=23​λ2,TrQ3=43​λ3,(TrQ2)2=49​λ4.
(29)
Using precisely the Landau-de Gennes free energy convention in the preceding solution gives
aˉ=43a​,bˉ=169b​,cˉ=4c​​.
(30)
If the quartic invariant were instead normalized as bTrQ4/4, its coefficient would be bˉ=9b/32; the physical predictions are unchanged after redefining b.
For a nonzero global minimizer of the free energy, compare opposite values of the scalar nematic order parameter:
f(λ)−f(−λ)=2cˉλ3.
(31)
The lower one has cˉλ<0, so
sgnλeq​=−sgncˉ(cˉ=0)​.
(32)
The claim concerns the stable ordered phase, not every metastable stationary point. For cˉ=0 the two signs are degenerate. For cˉ=0, equality of the ordered and isotropic free energies, together with stationarity, gives λt​=−cˉ/(2bˉ) and aˉt​=cˉ2/(4bˉ)>0. The finite jump λt​ exhibits the first-order phase transition driven by the cubic invariant.

A scalar coupling linear in the nematic order parameter requires a second-rank anisotropy. A single electric field supplies no such tensor, while Ei​Ej​ does. Its isotropic part gives E2TrQ=0. Hence the lowest joint order in E and Q of the electric-field coupling to nematic order is
fE​=−χEi​Qij​Ej​​.
(33)
Terms depending only on E do not affect minimization over Q. With ϑ the angle between the nematic director and the electric field,
fE​=−23​χλE2(cos2ϑ−31​).
(34)
For χλ>0, the lowest free energy occurs at cos2ϑ=1, so the nematic director aligns along either +E or −E. Then Ei​Qij​Ej​=λE2, giving
fE​=dˉλ,dˉ=−χE2<0(E=0, χ>0)​.
(35)
Unlike the linear composition term in a conserved binary fluid mixture, this term changes the equilibrium nematic order parameter: its spatial integral is not fixed.

Within this analytic quartic Landau theory, equilibrium of a nonconserved scalar order parameter requires f′=0. At a continuous phase transition, its restoring curvature vanishes, so f′′=0. A nonzero f′′′ would leave a cubic leading term in the Taylor expansion about that state and prevent it from being a local minimum. Thus a stable critical endpoint of a quartic Landau free energy requires all three derivatives to vanish, with f′′′′>0. The condition f′′=0 alone can instead identify a spinodal point.
For f=bˉλ4+cˉλ3+aˉλ2+dˉλ, bˉ>0, the successive equations are
f′′′=24bˉλ+6cˉ=0,f′′=12bˉλ2+6cˉλ+2aˉ=0,f′=4bˉλ3+3cˉλ2+2aˉλ+dˉ=0.
(36)
They give
λc​=−4bˉcˉ​,aˉc​=8bˉ3cˉ2​,dˉc​=16bˉ2cˉ3​​.
(37)
At these values, the free energy is exactly f(λc​)+bˉ(λ−λc​)4, proving stability. With no electric field, dˉ=0, so necessarily cˉ=0, followed by λc​=aˉc​=0.
For cˉ<0 and χ>0, the electric-field coupling to nematic order supplies the required negative dˉc​ at
Ec2​=−16χbˉ2cˉ3​,Ec​=4χ​bˉ(−cˉ)3/2​​>0.
(38)
Tuning the quadratic coefficient to aˉc​ simultaneously reaches the field-induced critical endpoint of the isotropic-nematic transition. The PDF's c~ in the last clause is undefined; it is interpreted here as the previously defined cˉ. The endpoint is a prediction of the stable quartic, small-field mean-field approximation; higher-order terms or a field beyond the expansion's validity can shift it.
