Use the usual homogeneous, local, analytic Landau-Ginzburg theory; rotational symmetry and Z2 symmetry alone do not specify locality, analyticity, or the signs of the coefficients. The local derivative expansion contains every rotational scalar with an even total number of fields:
F[ϕ]V(ϕ)​=∫ddx[V(ϕ)+21​K(ϕ)(∇ϕ)2+c1​(∇2ϕ)2+c2​((∇ϕ)2)2+⋯],=f0​+2r​ϕ2+4u​ϕ4+6v​ϕ6+⋯,K(ϕ)=γ+γ2​ϕ2+⋯.​
(1)
The omitted terms include all allowed higher field powers and contracted spatial derivatives. Terms differing by a total derivative are equivalent for bulk behavior with suitable boundary conditions. The displayed gradient energy assumes spatial homogeneity. If only rotations about an origin are imposed, rotationally invariant position-dependent coefficients are also possible.
In the mean-field approximation, take a spatially uniform order parameter and minimize its Landau free energy. For γ>0, u>0 and r=a(T−Tc​) with a>0, keeping the quadratic and quartic terms gives
ϕ0​=0(r≥0),ϕ0​=±−r/u​(r<0).
(2)
The single minimum splits into two minima as the temperature passes below the critical temperature. Choosing one minimum gives spontaneous symmetry breaking of the Z2 symmetry and a continuous phase transition. The order parameter is the expectation in a selected ordered state; averaging equally over both states gives zero. A Z2 symmetry does not guarantee a continuous phase transition: a negative quartic coefficient stabilized by a positive sextic coefficient can instead give a first-order phase transition.
Relaxing the Z2 symmetry permits odd terms. A nonzero linear source generally rounds this ordinary continuous phase transition. A cubic term can instead give a first-order phase transition: for V(ϕ)=rϕ2/2+wϕ3/3+uϕ4/4, u>0 and w=0, equal minima occur at
ϕ∗​=−3u2w​,r∗​=9u2w2​>0.
(3)
The order parameter jumps from zero to ϕ∗​. A continuous phase transition without an exact Z2 symmetry is still possible with additional tuning; odd terms do not logically exclude it.
For the multicritical even Landau potential, set r=μ2=a(T−Tc​) and assume an integer n≥2, λn​>0 and γ>0. For n>2, all lower even interactions must be absent or tuned to zero to obtain these mean-field critical exponents. The stationary equation is
0=rϕ0​+2nλn​ϕ02n−1​,∣ϕ0​∣2n−2=2nλn​−r​(r<0).
(4)
Its nonzero solutions are minima because V′′(ϕ0​)=−(2n−2)r>0. Thus the order-parameter critical exponent is
βMF​=2(n−1)1​​.
(5)
Substituting into the minimum free energy density gives
fmin​=−2nn−1​(2nλn​)−1/(n−1)(−r)n/(n−1).
(6)
The singular heat capacity per unit volume is cs​=−T∂T2​fmin​. Smooth variation of the other coefficients does not change the leading power. Consequently
cs​∝(Tc​−T)n/(n−1)−2,αMF​=n−1n−2​​.
(7)
For n=2, this means a finite heat capacity jump, with αMF​=0; for n=3, the tricritical point has βMF​=1/4 and αMF​=1/2. The case n=1 would contain only a quadratic potential and would not stabilize an ordered phase.
The Gaussian fluctuation correction near a critical point is more singular than the mean-field approximation contribution when
2−2d​>n−1n−2​⟺d<dc​=n−12n​​.
(8)
This is the upper critical dimension of an even scalar interaction. Equivalently, its coupling has engineering dimension 2n−(n−1)d, positive below dc​. Dominant fluctuations signal breakdown of the mean-field approximation; the Gaussian expression is not then a calculation of the exact interacting heat-capacity critical exponent. At d=dc​, equality of the powers allows logarithmic corrections.

Absorb kB​T into the free energy, so the statistical weight is e−F. Under the Gaussian fixed point rescaling, invariance of the gradient energy gives the engineering dimension
[ϕ]=2d−2​=21​(d=3)​.
(9)
The engineering dimensions of ϕ2,ϕ4,ϕ6 are respectively 1,2,3. Their coupling eigenvalues are d−[ϕ2m], hence 2,1,0. The quadratic and quartic interactions are relevant operators; the sextic interaction is a marginal operator at this order.
The three steps of the momentum-shell renormalization group are:
  • Split ϕ=φ+η into slow modes ∣k∣<Λ/b and fast modes Λ/b<∣k∣<Λ, and integrate over η.
  • Rescale x′=x/b to restore the ultraviolet cutoff to Λ.
  • Rescale the field as ϕ′(x′)=b(d−2)/2φ(bx′) to normalize the gradient energy.
Here b=es>1, so increasing s means flowing toward longer distances. At leading order, the fast field has a Gaussian distribution with covariance
Cb​(x−y)=∫Λ/b<∣k∣<Λ​(2π)3d3k​k2+μ02​eik⋅(x−y)​,Ib​=Cb​(0).
(10)
This perturbative integration requires k2+μ02​>0 on the shell. By the Wick theorem, odd fast-field moments vanish and ⟨η4⟩=3Ib2​, ⟨η6⟩=15Ib3​. Therefore the first term of the cumulant expansion of a coarse-grained free energy is
λ0​⟨(φ+η)6⟩=λ0​(φ6+15Ib​φ4+45Ib2​φ2+15Ib3​).
(11)
This is the quartic interaction generated by a sextic interaction. Including the rescaling, the requested flow is
g(b)=15bλ0​∫Λ/b<∣k∣<Λ​(2π)3d3k​k2+μ02​1​+O(λ02​)​.
(12)
In spherical coordinates, Ib​=(2π2)−1∫Λ/bΛ​k2/(k2+μ02​)dk. In particular, for μ02​=0,
g(b)=2π215λ0​Λ​(b−1)+O(λ02​),dsdg​=g+2π215Λ​λ+⋯.
(13)
The same integration shifts the quadratic coefficient by 90λ0​Ib2​ before rescaling and adds an irrelevant constant to the free energy. Thus setting the bare quartic coupling to zero does not place the theory on the tricritical critical surface.
To calculate the sextic renormalization-group beta function to second order, let V=λ0​∫d3x(φ+η)6. The required cumulant expansion is
Feff​=F0​+⟨V⟩−21​(⟨V2⟩−⟨V⟩2)+O(λ03​).
(14)
The subtraction removes disconnected contributions. The term 20λ0​φ3η3 at each vertex supplies the primitive sextic correction. The Wick contractions give
⟨η(x)3η(y)3⟩=6Cb​(x−y)3+9Ib2​Cb​(x−y).
(15)
The first term contains three propagators connecting the two vertices; its factor 6 is the number of bijections between the three fields at each vertex. The second contains a local tadpole diagram at each vertex and one connecting propagator. The primitive contribution to the free energy is therefore
ΔF6​=−1200λ02​∫d3xd3yφ(x)3φ(y)3Cb​(x−y)3.
(16)
Expanding the slow fields about a common point gives a negative local sextic correction proportional to
Jb​=∫d3zCb​(z)3=∫(2π)6d3pd3q​(p2+μ02​)(q2+μ02​)((p+q)2+μ02​)χb​(p)χb​(q)χb​(p+q)​≥0,
(17)
where χb​ restricts a momentum to the shell. This connected Feynman diagram has two independent loop momenta. Other Wick contractions also generate lower interactions and tadpole diagrams; they must be included or subtracted consistently when fixing the tricritical critical surface.
To extract the logarithmic tricritical sextic beta function, use normal ordering to remove local tadpoles and tune the quadratic and quartic relevant operators. The massless three-dimensional propagator is C(z)=1/(4π∣z∣). The same three-line contraction over short separations a<∣z∣<ba gives
∫a<∣z∣<ba​d3zC(z)3=16π21​logb.
(18)
Its positive coefficient and the negative sign of the second cumulant expansion term establish the sign of the flow. Matching this logarithm to a renormalized coupling, or performing consistent iterated shell integration, gives
dsdλ​=−Cλ2+O(λ3),C>0​,λ(s)=1+Cλ(0)sλ(0)​+⋯.
(19)
The precise normalization of C is not needed. In a sharp momentum cutoff scheme, simply forcing all three internal momenta into one infinitesimal shell does not extract this two-loop logarithm: the shell restrictions remove its linear-in-ds phase space. The finite-shell cumulant and the logarithmic renormalized calculation above are distinct stages of the computation. Field normalization corrections at order λ2 enter this renormalization-group beta function only at order λ3.
A positive, stabilizing sextic coupling is marginally irrelevant in three dimensions on the tricritical critical surface. Reversing the convention for s reverses the sign of the renormalization-group beta function. A negative sextic coupling without higher stabilizing powers makes the potential unbounded; moreover, the untuned bare choice g=0 generally flows away through its generated relevant operator.

Write ri​=μi2​ and assume g>0, as required for quartic stability. In the mean-field approximation, the two-component radial quartic Landau potential is
V=21​(r1​ϕ12​+r2​ϕ22​)+g(ϕ12​+ϕ22​)2.
(20)
Set x=ϕ12​≥0, y=ϕ22​≥0. At fixed ρ=x+y, the quartic term is fixed and the quadratic term is minimized by assigning all of ρ to the component with smaller ri​. Minimizing over ρ then gives
fmin​=−16gmin(0,r1​,r2​)2​​.
(21)
The resulting phase diagram has these regions:
  • If r1​>0 and r2​>0, ϕ1​=ϕ2​=0: the disordered phase preserves both Z2 symmetries.
  • If r1​<min(0,r2​), ϕ1​=±−r1​/(4g)​ and ϕ2​=0: the first Z2 symmetry undergoes spontaneous symmetry breaking.
  • If r2​<min(0,r1​), ϕ2​=±−r2​/(4g)​ and ϕ1​=0: the second Z2 symmetry undergoes spontaneous symmetry breaking.
On r1​=r2​=r<0, the symmetry is enhanced to the orthogonal group O(2), and the minima form the circle
ϕ12​+ϕ22​=−4gr​​.
(22)
Mixed-component minima occur on this line, rather than in an open coexistence region. Crossing the negative diagonal swaps the selected component discontinuously: this is a first-order phase transition, also called a spin-flop transition. For example, holding r2​=r<0 fixed and moving r1​ across r, the derivative ∂fmin​/∂r1​ jumps from −r/(8g) to zero. The angular degeneracy exactly on the line does not remove this discontinuity.
The positive half-axis r1​=0, r2​>0 is a continuous phase transition, as is r2​=0, r1​>0. Their endpoint at (0,0) is a bicritical point, where the two continuous boundaries meet the first-order negative diagonal. Approaching the origin radially makes the ordered amplitude vanish continuously. The negative half-axes lie inside ordered phases and are not additional transition lines. There are two continuous half-axis boundaries and one first-order negative diagonal, meeting at the origin.

At the Gaussian fixed point, [ϕi​]=(d−2)/2, so all three quartic couplings have engineering dimension d−4[ϕi​]=4−d. Thus
α1​=α2​=α3​=ϵ​.
(23)
Take A>0 and ϵ>0, as in the perturbative epsilon expansion. Define x=Ag1​/ϵ, y=Ag2​/ϵ, z=Aλ/ϵ. The renormalization-group fixed point equations become
x−36x2−z2=0,y−36y2−z2=0,z[1−8z−12(x+y)]=0.
(24)
If z=0, each of x,y is independently 0 or 1/36, giving four renormalization-group fixed points. If z=0, subtracting the first two equations gives
(x−y)[1−36(x+y)]=0.
(25)
The branch x+y=1/36 forces z=1/12; inserting this in the first equation yields a repeated root x=y=1/72. Hence every nonzero-z solution has x=y. Then z=1/8−3x, and
45x2−47​x+641​=0
(26)
has roots x=1/40 and x=1/72. The complete list of six coupled Ising fixed points near four dimensions, in coordinates (g1​,g2​,λ), is
Aϵ​⎩⎨⎧​(0,0,0),(361​,0,0),(0,361​,0),(361​,361​,0),(401​,401​,201​),(721​,721​,121​)​⎭⎬⎫​​.
(27)
The internal symmetries of these renormalization-group fixed points are:
  • The Gaussian fixed point has an O(2) orthogonal group acting on the two identical free massless fields.
  • (ϵ/(36A),0,0) is one Ising model Wilson-Fisher fixed point and one free field. Its linear internal symmetry is Z2​×Z2​; the two sectors cannot be exchanged. The point (0,ϵ/(36A),0) has the same symmetry with the roles reversed.
  • (ϵ/(36A),ϵ/(36A),0) describes two identical decoupled Ising models. It additionally permits field exchange, giving (Z2​×Z2​)⋊S2​, the eight-element dihedral group D4​.
  • (ϵ/(40A),ϵ/(40A),ϵ/(20A)) has λ=2g1​=2g2​. Its interaction is g(ϕ12​+ϕ22​)2, so it is the O(N) model Wilson-Fisher fixed point with N=2 and O(2) symmetry.
  • (ϵ/(72A),ϵ/(72A),ϵ/(12A)) has λ=6g1​=6g2​. Its symmetry is again the eight-element dihedral group D4​, not O(2).
The last point displays the field-rotation equivalence of decoupled Ising theories. With the orthogonal transformation ψ±​=(ϕ1​±ϕ2​)/2​,
g(ϕ14​+ϕ24​+6ϕ12​ϕ22​)=2g(ψ+4​+ψ−4​),2g=36Aϵ​.
(28)
It is the same pair of decoupled Ising models written in fields rotated by 45∘, but remains a distinct coordinate solution of the stated renormalization-group beta functions. Here D4​ denotes the dihedral group of order eight; an alternative convention calls it D8​. We have described linear internal transformations preserving the gradient energy; free massless sectors also have constant scalar-field shift symmetries. At ϵ=0, the six coordinates coalesce at the Gaussian fixed point.

Assume g>0 for stability. With equal quadratic coefficients and λ=2g, the internal symmetry is the full orthogonal group O(2). In two dimensions at positive temperature, with short-range interactions, the Mermin-Wagner theorem forbids spontaneous symmetry breaking of this continuous symmetry. Thus ⟨ϕ1​⟩=⟨ϕ2​⟩=0 in both phases in the infinite system; a nonzero average order parameter does not distinguish them.
Instead measure the correlation function
G(r)=⟨ϕ(x)⋅ϕ(0)⟩,r=∣x∣.
(29)
The low-temperature phase has quasi-long-range order and algebraic correlations; the high-temperature phase has a finite correlation length and exponential correlations. More precisely,
G(r)∼r−η(T) below TBKT​,G(r) decays exponentially for T>TBKT​​.
(30)
A finite order parameter amplitude allows ϕ≃v(cosθ,sinθ). Its long-distance Goldstone-mode effective free energy is Fθ​=(ρs​/2)∫d2x(∇θ)2. Restoring the Boltzmann constant and using the renormalized phase stiffness, the thermal phase fluctuations give
⟨[θ(x)−θ(0)]2⟩≃πρs​kB​T​log(r/a),η(T)=2πρs​kB​T​.
(31)
The Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless transition separates vortex-antivortex binding from unbound vortex defects. At the boundary, the universal stiffness jump gives ρs​(TBKT−​)=2kB​TBKT​/π and η(TBKT​)=1/4. The low-temperature phase stiffness is nonzero; it vanishes at long distances in the disordered phase. The exact transition location is fluctuation dependent and cannot be obtained just by setting the bare μ2 to zero.
