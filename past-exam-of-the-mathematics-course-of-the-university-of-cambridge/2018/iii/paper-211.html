For holdings h∈Rn, let V0​=h⋅P0​ and V1​=h⋅P1​. Under the investment-consumption arbitrage convention used here, an arbitrage satisfies
V0​≤0,V1​≥0 a.s.,V0​<0 or P(V1​>0)>0.​
(1)
The initial consumption is −V0​, and the terminal consumption is V1​: there is a possible gain with no external funding and no negative consumption. A pure-investment arbitrage additionally requires V0​=0. This convention is important because the last part distinguishes initial consumption from pure investment.

A pricing kernel is a strictly positive random variable R such that RP1i​ is integrable and
P0i​=E[RP1i​](i=1,…,n).​
(2)
It is a one-period state-price density, converting a terminal payoff into its initial price. Strict positivity means R>0 almost surely, not merely R≥0. When cash has price 1 at both dates, this identity forces ER=1, so dQ=RdP defines an equivalent martingale measure.

For any holdings h, the pricing kernel identity implies V0​=E[RV1​]. If V1​≥0 almost surely, then V0​≥0, so an arbitrage cannot have V0​<0. If also V0​=0, then the nonnegative integrable random variable RV1​ has zero expectation and hence is zero almost surely. Since R>0 almost surely, V1​=0 almost surely, ruling out a positive terminal gain. Therefore a pricing kernel rules out arbitrage.

Write (a,b,c) for holdings of cash, the stock and the European put option, and let γ=−V0​ be initial consumption. At the given price, a=−10b−c−γ, and the three terminal payoffs, in descending order of the stock price, are
5b−c−γ,2b−c−γ,c−b−γ.
(6)
Nonnegative terminal payoffs require c+γ≤2b and c−γ≥b. Together with γ≥0, these imply b≥2γ≥0. If b=0 they force γ=c=a=0, which is not an arbitrage. If b>0, the highest-state payoff is at least 3b>0, so every such choice is an arbitrage. Consequently the complete set is
(a,b,c)=(−10b−c−γ,b,c),b>0,0≤γ≤b/2,b+γ≤c≤2b−γ.​
(7)
The pure-investment arbitrages are exactly the choices γ=0:
(a,b,c)=(−10b−c,b,c),b>0,b≤c≤2b.​
(8)
For example, (a,b,c)=(−11,1,1) costs zero and pays (4,1,0).

Let τn​↑∞ be a localizing sequence for the discrete-time local martingale X. For t≥1, the increment of the stopped martingale is
Xt∧τn​​−X(t−1)∧τn​​=1{τn​≥t}​(Xt​−Xt−1​),
(9)
and {τn​≥t}∈Ft−1​. Thus, for any A∈Ft−1​,
E[1A​1{τn​≥t}​(Xt​−Xt−1​)]=0.
(10)
The assumed integrability gives the dominating random variable ∣Xt​∣+∣Xt−1​∣. The dominated convergence theorem now yields E[1A​(Xt​−Xt−1​)]=0, or E[Xt​∣Ft−1​]=Xt−1​. Iterating the tower property of conditional expectation proves an integrable discrete-time local martingale is a true martingale. The proof uses the discrete-time increment indicator; the conclusion does not extend to continuous time.

Assume the innovations are independent of the previous history and have finite exponential moments at every real argument, as required by the finite-valued affine definition. Conditioning on Xt−1​=x gives
logE[eθXt​∣Xt−1​=x]=aθx+bθ+ψ(θ).
(16)

Here and in the remaining parts, an affine process uses a time-homogeneous Markov process: the one-step transform is the same at every time. The displayed definition at time 1 alone would not determine later transitions of an arbitrary time-inhomogeneous Markov process.

A unit-face-value zero-coupon bond pays 1 at maturity. The martingale deflator pricing identity is
Pt,T​=Yt​E[YT​∣Ft​]​=E[exp(∑j=t+1T​Xj​)​Ft​].
(23)
Use the affine process Markov property with respect to the market filtration; if that filtration contains extra predictive information, the natural Markov property alone would not suffice. Part (b), with every future coefficient equal to 1, gives the exponential-affine form. More explicitly, the exponential-affine bond pricing recursion is
α(0)=β(0)=0,α(n+1)=A(1+α(n)),β(n+1)=β(n)+B(1+α(n)).​
(24)
Indeed, conditioning the first future step in an (n+1)-step horizon transforms e(1+α(n))Xt+1​+β(n) into eA(1+α(n))Xt​+B(1+α(n))+β(n). Therefore

(25)
This uses the true martingale deflator pricing identity; a merely local martingale deflator would not by itself justify replacing prices by conditional terminal expectations.

Let pK​=C(T,K+1)−2C(T,K)+C(T,K−1)>0 and DK​=C(T+1,K)−C(T,K). Given ST​=K, the next value is one of K−1,K,K+1. The martingale property makes its upward and downward conditional probabilities equal; their sum is the conditional variance of the increment, σ2(T,K)=2DK​/pK​. Thus
P(ST+1​=H∣ST​=K)=⎩⎨⎧​DK​/pK​,1−2DK​/pK​,0,​H=K−1 or K+1,H=K,otherwise.​​

Use positive initial asset prices and the usual augmentation of the natural Brownian filtration. Define the market price of risk
λt​=σt​μt​−rt​​.​
(40)
Continuity and strict positivity of σ imply ∫0T​λt2​dt<∞ on every finite horizon almost surely, because each path has a positive minimum of σ there. Hence the strictly positive stochastic exponential
Yt​=exp(−∫0t​rs​ds−∫0t​λs​dWs​−21​∫0t​λs2​ds)​
(41)
is defined and has Y0​=1 and dYt​=−Yt​(rt​dt+λt​dWt​). The Itô product rule gives
d(Yt​Bt​)=−Yt​Bt​λt​dWt​,d(Yt​St​)=Yt​St​(σt​−λt​)dWt​,
(42)
so it is a local martingale deflator.
For uniqueness, let Y be any normalized strictly positive local martingale deflator. The Brownian martingale representation theorem makes BY a continuous local martingale with a Brownian motion integral representation. Dividing by B therefore gives dY=−rYdt+ηdW. The vanishing drift of SY requires Y(μ−r)+ησ=0, so η=−Yλ. This scalar linear stochastic differential equation has exactly the exponential solution above, proving the normalized local martingale deflator is unique.
The assumptions do not make λ deterministically bounded: σ need not be bounded away from zero uniformly over outcomes. Thus a true martingale density or Novikov condition is not inferred here; the required conclusion is local.
