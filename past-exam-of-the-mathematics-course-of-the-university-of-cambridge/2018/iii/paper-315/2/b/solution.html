Take z to increase inward, so the positive coefficient A describes an inward-increasing temperature. Constant gravity and hydrostatic equilibrium give dP/dz=ρg, hence
dPdT​=gT3A​,∇rad​=gT4AP​.
(13)
Matching the radiative temperature gradient to the adiabatic temperature gradient gives the formal local boundary relation
Pb​=Ag​∇ad​Tb4​​.
(14)
The same result follows from radiative diffusion: for constant upward thermal flux F and Rosseland mean opacity κ, A=3κF/(16σSB​).
There is an important limitation to treating A as constant over the entire radiative layer. Integration from an irradiated outer boundary gives
T4=T04​+g4A​P,∇rad​=41​(1−T4T04​​)<41​.
(15)
For a diatomic ideal gas, ∇ad​=2/7>1/4, so this profile cannot actually reach a radiative-convective boundary. Formally, imposing a constant ∇ad​ would give
Pb​=AgT04​​1−4∇ad​∇ad​​,
(16)
which is positive only for 0<∇ad​<1/4. A finite boundary for a normal molecular atmosphere requires additional opacity, flux, or thermodynamic variation. The local matching formula is usable near a real boundary, but constant A is not a complete global model of it. This is the convective stability of a constant-opacity irradiated atmosphere.
For the intended order-of-magnitude scaling, suppose the local values of g/A and ∇ad​ are comparable for Jupiter and a hot Jupiter, and assume Tb​ scales with planetary equilibrium temperature. Equal absorbed-flux factors around the same stellar luminosity give Tb​∝a−1/2. Taking aJ​=5.2AU and a representative close-in orbit ah​=0.05AU yields
Pb,J​Pb,h​​∼(Tb,J​Tb,h​​)4∼(0.055.2​)2,Pb,h​∼103bar​.
(17)
This illustrates how irradiation can push a boundary much deeper. It is a conditional estimate calibrated from the supplied 0.1bar reference, not a self-consistent prediction of the globally constant-A model. Different intrinsic cooling flux, opacity, gravity, or atmospheric metallicity of a giant planet can substantially alter it.
