Write hA​=C(A,−). The covariant Yoneda lemma gives the natural bijection
Nat(hA​,F)≅F(A),α⟼αA​(1A​),
(1)
whose inverse sends x∈F(A) to the natural transformation with component u:A→B↦F(u)(x). This also identifies the two variables' naturality.
To prove that hA​ is a projective object in a category, let p:G↠F be an epimorphism in the functor category and α:hA​→F. By pointwise epimorphism in a functor category, lift αA​(1A​) through the surjective function pA​. The Yoneda lemma turns this lift into β:hA​→G with pβ=α.
For a small category, the set of pairs (A,x) with x∈F(A) indexes the canonical map
p:∐(A,x)​hA​⟶F,pB​(A,x,u)=F(u)(x).
(2)
It is natural, and every y∈F(B) is the image of (B,y,1B​). Thus it is an epimorphism, establishing the projective cover of a set-valued functor by representables construction. Here cover means a projective presentation; no minimality is asserted.
For the equivalence's first step, if f:B→D is a monomorphism, then hA​(f), the map u↦fu, is injective for every A. Hence every representable functor is a monofunctor. Conversely, injectivity of every hA​(f) is exactly left cancellation against every u,v:A→B, so it implies that f is a monomorphism. Therefore
(a)⟺(b),(A,x)∐​hA​↠F.​

Assume (b). A coproduct in a category of monofunctors in [C,Set] is again a monofunctor: at an object it is the disjoint union of the values, and every arrow acts within the same summand by an injective function. Distinct summands cannot become identified, and injectivity within a summand follows from its monofunctor property.
Apply this to the coproduct of representable functors constructed in part (a). Its canonical map to F is a pointwise epimorphism in a functor category. Thus every F is an epimorphic image of a monofunctor, proving
(b)⟹(c).​

Assume (c), and take an epimorphism p:M↠hA​ with M a monofunctor. Since hA​ is a projective object in a category, there is a natural transformation s:hA​→M with ps=1hA​​. A retract in a category of a monofunctor is a monofunctor: if hA​(f)(u)=hA​(f)(v), then naturality of s gives M(f)s(u)=M(f)s(v), injectivity of M(f) gives s(u)=s(v), and applying p gives u=v. Thus (c) implies (b), completing
(a)⟺(b)⟺(c).​
(5)
The stronger assertion that every set-valued functor is a monofunctor holds exactly when C is a groupoid. Sufficiency follows because a functor preserves inverses, so sends every arrow to a bijection.
For necessity, fix f:A→B, and form the pointwise pushout in a category
P=hA​⨿hB​​hA​,
(6)
using twice the natural transformation hB​→hA​ given by precomposition with f. At an object X, P(X) consists of two copies of C(A,X), with the two copies of kf identified for every k:B→X. No different underlying elements are identified: each generating relation simply joins the two copies of one element. In P(B), the two copies of f coincide, since f=1B​f. Hence P(f) sends the two copies of 1A​ in P(A) to the same element. If P is a monofunctor, those copies of 1A​ coincide. The description of the pushout implies rf=1A​ for some r:B→A. Thus every morphism is a split monomorphism.
Apply the same conclusion to r: there is t:A→B with tr=1B​. Then t=trf=f, and so fr=1B​ as well. Every arrow is invertible, proving
Every F:C→Set is a monofunctor ⟺ C is a groupoid.​

λA′​G(u′)=λA′​GF(a)G(u)=λA​G(u).

A reflexive pair f,g:A⇉B has a common section r:B→A with fr=gr=1B​. Form the pushout in a category of f and g, and write its two maps from B as j1​,j2​. Its relation j1​f=j2​g, composed with r, gives j1​=j2​=q. Thus qf=qg. Any h:B→C with hf=hg gives the compatible pair (h,h) for this pushout and therefore factors uniquely through q. This proves the coequalizer of a reflexive pair from a pushout construction.
There is a genuine transcription difference: the original PDF says finite products in the finite-colimit assertion, while the TeX says finite coproducts. The PDF assertion is false with products. Even retaining a pushout-existence assumption, the full subcategory of the Category of sets on nonempty sets has finite products, pushouts, and all coequalizers, but no initial object, hence no empty colimit. Regard the poset with elements 0,a,b,u0​,u1​,…, order
0<a,b<un+1​<un​(n≥0),
(13)
and incomparable a,b as a category. It has finite meets and top u0​, hence finite products in a category. Every reflexive pair in a poset is an equal pair and has its identity as a coequalizer. But a,b have no least upper bound, so they have no coproduct in a category.
For the corrected construction using finite coproducts, let D:J→C be a finite diagram in a category. Put
X=∐j∈J​D(j),Y=∐a:i→j​D(i).
(14)
Define s,t:Y⇉X on the summand indexed by a:i→j as νi​ and νj​D(a). Maps h:X→Z with hs=ht are exactly cocone under a diagram data on D. Hence their universal coequalizer is its colimit. Replace s,t by the reflexive pair
[s,1X​],[t,1X​]:Y⨿X⇉X,
(15)
whose common section is the second injection and whose coequalizer is unchanged. This proves construction of finite colimits from coproducts and reflexive coequalizers, including the empty diagram via the empty coproduct:
Finite coproducts and reflexive coequalizers suffice for all finite colimits.​

A monad is an endofunctor T:C→C with natural transformations η:1C​→T and μ:T2→T satisfying
μTη=μηT=1T​,μTμ=μμT.
(22)
Its Kleisli category has the same objects as C, with CT​(A,B)=C(A,TB), identity ηA​, and composite
g⋆f=μC​T(g)f(f:A→TB, g:B→TC).
(23)
The unit laws follow from naturality of η and the two monad unit laws. For h:C→TD, the two triple composites reduce, using naturality of μ, to
μD​μTD​T2(h)T(g)fandμD​T(μD​)T2(h)T(g)f;
(24)
they agree by the monad associativity law. Thus this is a category.
Define the free functor into a Kleisli category J and the right-hand functor U by
JA=A,J(f)=ηB​f,UA=TA,U(f)=μB​T(f).
(25)
The identity and composition formulas for J are
J(1A​)=ηA​,J(g)⋆J(f)=μC​T(ηC​)T(g)ηB​f=ηC​gf=J(gf).
(26)
For U, U(ηA​)=μA​TηA​=1TA​, and
U(g⋆f)=μC​TμC​T2(g)T(f)=μC​μTC​T2(g)T(f)=μC​T(g)μB​T(f)=U(g)U(f),
(27)
where the last step is naturality of μ. Hence both mappings are functors. The hom-set identity CT​(JA,B)=C(A,UB) gives the adjunction J⊣U. Its unit is η, and its counit at B is the Kleisli arrow represented by 1TB​:TB→TB. Applying U to that counit gives μB​, so the induced monad is the specified one.
For any L⊣R:D→C inducing this same monad, with counit ε, define the Kleisli comparison functor
KA=LA,K(f:A→TB)=εLB​L(f).
(28)
The triangular identities give K(ηA​)=1LA​, and naturality of the counit together with μ=RεL gives K(g⋆f)=K(g)K(f). Explicitly, naturality moves εLB​ past L(g), then moves the resulting counit past εLC​, reducing the composite to εLC​L(μC​T(g)f). Thus K is a functor, with KJ=L and RK=U, and it sends the Kleisli counit to εL​.
An adjunction morphism here is required to commute with the left and right adjoints and preserve their adjunction structure. Such a morphism must have the above object map; every Kleisli arrow f factors as its counit after J(f), so its arrow map is forced as well. This proves initiality of the Kleisli adjunction:
J⊣U is initial among adjunctions inducing (T,η,μ).​
(29)

A Cartesian closed category has finite products in a category and, for every F, an exponential object GF characterized naturally by
E(K×F,G)≅E(K,GF).
(38)
In [C,Set], finite products and the terminal functor are computed pointwise. With hB​=C(B,−), define
(GF)(B)=Nat(hB​×F,G).
(39)
This is a set because C is a small category. For v:B→B′, precomposition hB′​→hB​ induces (GF)(v) by precomposition of natural transformations, making GF a covariant functor.
Given α:K×F→G, define its curry α:K→GF by
(αB​(y))C​(u,x)=αC​(K(u)y,x)(u:B→C, x∈F(C)).
(40)

αB​(y,x)=(βB​(y))B​(1B​,x).

[C,Set] is cartesian closed,(GF)(B)=Nat(hB​×F,G).​

Let P be a tiny object of [C,Set], and let 0 be the initial object of C. The representable functor h0​=C(0,−) is the terminal functor, since each of its values is a singleton. The exponential adjunction and the Yoneda lemma give
Nat(P,F)≅Nat(h0​,FP)≅(FP)(0).
(48)
Since P is tiny, EP​:F↦FP is itself a left adjoint, so preserves all colimits. Evaluation at 0 also preserves colimits, because they are pointwise in a set-valued functor category. Therefore Nat(P,−) preserves the coproduct construction.
It also preserves epimorphisms. Indeed, every such epimorphism is a pointwise epimorphism in a functor category, hence the coequalizer of its kernel pair, as can be checked pointwise in sets. A left adjoint preserves that coequalizer and therefore sends it to a regular epimorphism, which is an epimorphism; evaluation at zero preserves surjectivity. Thus P is an irreducible projective in a set-valued functor category.
A Cauchy-complete category is one in which every idempotent morphism splits. By the characterization allowed in the question, irreducible projectives are representable functors for such an indexing category. Applying it to P proves the tiny covariant functor on a Cauchy-complete category with an initial object is representable conclusion:
P tiny ⟹ P≅C(A,−) for some A.​

In the category of complete join-semilattices, define addition of arrows pointwise by
(f+g)(a)=f(a)∨g(a),
(56)
and let the zero arrow be the constant-bottom map. These form commutative monoids. Pointwise join of join-preserving maps again preserves all joins, including the empty join, and composition is additive in each variable. Thus the category has the required semi-additive enrichment.
For an arbitrary set-indexed family (Ai​), the product poset P=∏i​Ai​, with coordinatewise joins, is the product in a category. Define νi​:Ai​→P by putting an element in coordinate i and bottom in all others. Given join-preserving fi​:Ai​→B, define
h((ai​))=⋁i​fi​(ai​).
(57)
This preserves arbitrary joins by interchanging the two joins, and hνi​=fi​. It is unique because every tuple is ⋁i​νi​(ai​). Therefore P is also the coproduct in a category with these injections, and its canonical coproduct-to-product map is the identity. This proves the biproducts of complete join-semilattices property, even for arbitrary set-indexed families, and in particular makes this a semi-additive category with the hypotheses of part (ii).
The two-element chain has distinct identity and constant-bottom endomorphisms, so the category is not equivalent to 1. Its addition is idempotent rather than cancellative; for example 1A​+1A​=1A​ itself supplies an absorbing endomorphism. Hence
CSLat is a nontrivial semi-additive counterexample.​
(58)
