For the first norm-one assertion, use the usual unital Banach algebra convention ∥1∥=1. A character of an algebra is a nonzero complex linear multiplicative functional, initially without any continuity assumption. It satisfies φ(1)=1, since otherwise φ(a)=φ(a1)=0 for every a. Moreover, a−φ(a)1 cannot be invertible: applying φ to an inverse identity would give 0=1. Thus
φ(a)∈σA​(a),∣φ(a)∣≤r(a)≤∥a∥.
(44)
This proves automatic continuity of characters and ∥φ∥≤1. Evaluation at the normalized identity gives the reverse inequality, so
φ is continuous and ∥φ∥=1.​
(45)
If a convention permits ∥1∥>1, only ∥φ∥≤1 follows in general: on C with norm 2∣z∣, the identity character has norm 1/2. The later completion argument needs only the upper bound.
If a is invertible, φ(a)φ(a−1)=1, so every character is nonzero on a. Conversely, if a is not invertible, its principal ideal is proper and lies in a maximal ideal M by Zorn lemma. The ideal M is closed: if its closure were the whole algebra, it would contain m with ∥1−m∥<1, making m invertible by the Neumann series, which is impossible for a proper ideal. Maximality therefore gives M=M. The quotient A/M is a complex Banach division algebra, hence is C by the Gelfand-Mazur theorem. The quotient map yields a character vanishing at a. Consequently
a is invertible⟺φ(a)=0 for every φ∈ΦA​.​
(46)
Now let A=C(K). Its evaluation characters give the map x↦δx​∈ΦA​. Every character has this form. Indeed, its kernel is a proper ideal. If the functions in that kernel had no common zero, compactness would give finitely many f1​,…,fm​ in the kernel with no common zero. Then h=∑j​fj​​fj​ belongs to the kernel and is everywhere positive, hence is invertible in C(K), a contradiction. At a common zero x, the fact that f−φ(f)1 belongs to the kernel gives f(x)=φ(f) for every f. Continuous functions distinguish points of the compact Hausdorff space K, so the map is bijective. It is continuous for the Gelfand topology, and the compact-to-Hausdorff continuous bijection theorem gives
ΦC(K)​≅K.​
(47)
For K=∅ the norm comparison is trivial; assume henceforth that K=∅.
Let B be the completion for the other algebra norm ∥⋅∥1​. It remains commutative and unital, with A embedded densely. The restriction of any ψ∈ΦB​ is a nonzero character of A, since it takes 1 to 1, and is automatically continuous for the original supremum norm. Thus R(ψ)=ψ∣A​ maps into ΦA​. It is injective because two continuous characters agreeing on the dense subalgebra A agree on B, and it is continuous because each coordinate R(ψ)(f)=ψ(f) is weak-star continuous.
The character space ΦB​ is weak-star compact even if its identity was not normalized: the spectral bound puts all its characters in BB∗​, and the conditions ψ(1)=1, ψ(bc)=ψ(b)ψ(c) define a weak-star closed set. Apply Banach-Alaoglu theorem. Consequently R is a homeomorphism onto a closed subset L⊆K.
Suppose x∈U=K∖L. The compact Hausdorff space K is normal, so choose an open V with x∈V⊆V⊆U. By the Urysohn lemma, choose g∈C(K) with g(x)=1 and g=0 on K∖V, and choose f∈C(K) with f=0 on V and f=1 on L. Then fg=0. Every character ψ of B restricts to evaluation at a point of L, so ψ(f)=1. The invertibility criterion proved above makes f invertible in B. Multiplying fg=0 by f−1 in B gives g=0, contradicting g(x)=1. Therefore U=∅ and R is onto K.
For each x∈K, choose a character ψx​∈ΦB​ extending δx​. Its spectral bound gives
∣f(x)∣=∣ψx​(f)∣≤∥f∥1​.
