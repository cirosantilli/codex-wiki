Use the Hilbert space interpretation of a linear inverse problem, with a bounded linear operator K:U→V. A least-squares solution minimizes ∥Ku−f∥V​ over u∈U. A minimum-norm least-squares solution additionally has the smallest U-norm among all least-squares solutions. When the equation is consistent this is the smallest-norm exact solution.
The least-squares solutions form a nonempty closed affine subspace precisely when
f∈R(K)⊕R(K)⊥.​
(1)
The closest point theorem in a Hilbert space then gives a unique nearest point to zero, hence a unique minimum-norm least-squares solution. Equivalently, it is the unique least-squares solution in N(K)⊥. If R(K) is closed, this exists for every datum; a nonclosed operator range can leave some data without any least-squares solution.
For example, on the l2 sequence space, take (Ku)n​=un​/n and fn​=1/n. The equation would require un​=1 for every n, which is not square summable. However, setting the first N coordinates of u to one and the rest to zero gives
∥Ku−f∥2=∑n>N​n21​⟶0.
(2)
Thus the least-squares infimum is zero but is not attained, and no minimum-norm least-squares solution exists for these data.

The map K∗K is a bounded linear operator, so the inverse image of the closed singleton {K∗f} is closed. That inverse image is also a convex set: if K∗Ku=K∗Kv=K∗f, every convex combination has the same image.
The identity N(K∗)=R(K)⊥ gives
K∗Ku=K∗f⟺f−Ku∈R(K)⊥.
(3)
Consequently such a u exists exactly when f is the sum of an element of R(K) and one of its orthogonal complement. Thus
L=∅ ⟺ f∈R(K)⊕R(K)⊥.​
(4)
These are precisely the least-squares solutions. Indeed, if r=Ku−f and K∗r=0, then for every h the Pythagorean identity gives
∥K(u+h)−f∥2=∥r∥2+∥Kh∥2≥∥r∥2.
(5)
Conversely, differentiating the squared residual along any real direction h forces Re⟨K∗r,h⟩=0, hence the normal equation for a linear inverse problem. For any u∗​∈L, the difference of two solutions of the normal equation for a linear inverse problem lies in N(K), since ⟨K∗Kh,h⟩=∥Kh∥2. Therefore
L=u∗​+N(K),​
(6)
a closed affine subspace; the empty case is closed and convex as well.

The Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator is the linear map
K†:D(K†)=R(K)⊕R(K)⊥⟶N(K)⊥
(7)
that assigns each admissible datum its minimum-norm least-squares solution. If f=y+z with y∈R(K) and z∈R(K)⊥, then K†f is the unique u∈N(K)⊥ satisfying Ku=y. In particular it vanishes on R(K)⊥, and
KK†f=PR(K)​​f,K†Ku=PN(K)⊥​u.
(8)
The continuity criterion, with the inherited data norm on its domain, is
K† is continuous ⟺ R(K) is closed.​
(9)
For a closed operator range, the restriction of K from N(K)⊥ to R(K) is a bounded bijection between Banach spaces, so its inverse is bounded by the bounded inverse theorem. Conversely, if K† is bounded and Kun​→y, replace each un​ by PN(K)⊥​un​=K†Kun​. This is a Cauchy sequence, hence converges to u, and Ku=y. Thus every limit of range elements remains in the operator range.

E(uˉ)=u∈Uinf​E(u)<+∞,domE={u:E(u)<+∞}.​

On U=R with its usual topology, the following examples isolate the three failures.
A nonproper functional is E(x)=+∞ for every x. Its effective domain is empty, so it has no finite-valued global minimizer. It nevertheless has coercivity and is sequentially lower semicontinuous. A noncoercive functional is E(x)=ex. It is a proper extended-real function and is continuous, but its infimum zero is approached as x→−∞ and is never attained. Hence it has no global minimizer.

The direct method in the calculus of variations gives the following existence theorem. Suppose bounded sequences in the Banach space U have τ-convergent subsequences, and E is a proper extended-real function with coercivity and is τ-sequentially lower semicontinuous. Then E has a finite-valued global minimizer.
To prove this, properness makes m=infE<+∞. First rule out m=−∞: a sequence with E(un​)→−∞ eventually lies in a fixed sublevel set, which is bounded by coercivity. A τ-convergent subsequence would then have a limit u with E(u)≤−∞, contradicting the codomain. Hence m is finite.
Choose a minimizing sequence with E(un​)→m. It eventually belongs to the bounded sublevel set {E≤m+1}. Extract unj​​τ​uˉ. By sequential lower semicontinuity,
m≤E(uˉ)≤liminfj​E(unj​​)=m.
(18)
Thus E(uˉ)=m. In particular, a reflexive Banach space with the weak topology supplies the required subsequence property by weak sequential compactness of bounded sequences in a reflexive Banach space. A strictly convex function has at most one minimizer; this is an additional property, not part of the existence theorem.

Take the weak topology on the reflexive Banach space U. A bounded linear operator is weak-to-weak continuous: un​⇀u implies ⟨Kun​,v⟩→⟨Ku,v⟩ for every v∈V, by its adjoint operator. The weak lower semicontinuity of the Hilbert norm therefore makes D(u)=∥Ku−f∥ weakly sequentially lower semicontinuous.
This functional is finite everywhere and nonnegative. If it has coercivity, the direct method in the calculus of variations supplies a global minimizer uˉ. Squaring the residual does not change its global minimizers. Here the adjoint operator has codomain U∗, since the source is a Banach space. Differentiating the squared residual along every real direction h∈U gives Re⟨K∗(Kuˉ−f),h⟩=0 in the dual pairing, hence the normal equation for a linear inverse problem K∗(Kuˉ−f)=0. Hence
f=Kuˉ+(f−Kuˉ)∈R(K)⊕R(K)⊥.​
(19)
The converse fails. For K:R2→R, K(x,y)=x, and f=1, the datum is in the operator range, but D(1,n)=0 while ∥(1,n)∥→∞. Thus admissible data do not imply coercivity of the residual; an unpenalized null space already prevents it.

First interpret the stated well-definedness as including uniqueness of the global minimizer for each positive parameter. Under that assumption, the parameter continuity of variational regularization follows from the direct method in the calculus of variations argument below.
Let M=D(0)=∥f∥2/2. For large n, αn​≥β:=α/2. Comparison with zero, using J(0)=0, gives
Φβ,f​(uαn​​)≤Φαn​,f​(uαn​​)≤M,J(uαn​​)≤M/β.
(47)
Coercivity of Φβ,f​ therefore bounds the sequence. Take a τ-convergent subsequence with limit uˉ. For any v∈domJ, optimality gives
Φα,f​(uαn​​)≤Φα,f​(v)+(αn​−α)J(v)+(α−αn​)J(uαn​​).
(48)
The last two terms vanish. Sequential lower semicontinuity at the fixed parameter α implies Φα,f​(uˉ)≤Φα,f​(v). Thus every cluster point is a global minimizer at α. Uniqueness makes it uα​, and the subsequence property proves
uαn​​τ​uα​.​
(49)
Indeed, a subsequence staying outside a neighborhood of uα​ would have a further convergent subsequence, whose limit must be uα​, a contradiction.
The listed existence hypotheses alone do not imply uniqueness or convergence of an arbitrary selection. For example, on R2, set K(x,y)=x, f=1, and
J(x,y)=x2+(max{∣y∣−1,0})2.

This is nonnegative, convex, continuous and has J(0)=0; every Φα,f​ has coercivity. Its minimizers are (1/(1+2α),y) for all ∣y∣≤1. Along αn​=α+1/n, selecting yn​=(−1)n prevents convergence. Without uniqueness, the valid conclusion is that every cluster point minimizes the limiting objective.

For a concrete variational regularization example, take a Hilbert space and J(u)=∥u∥2. The bound forces uα​→0 in norm. Since K is a bounded linear operator, Kuα​→0, and continuity of the squared norm gives
D(uα​)⟶21​∥f∥2.​
(58)
The same conclusion is not forced by J(uα​)→0 for a regulariser with a nontrivial zero set.

The small-parameter limit depends on data compatibility with the regularizer domain. Set
m=infv∈domJ​D(v),0≤m≤D(0).
(59)
For every such v, optimality gives
m≤D(uα​)≤Φα,f​(uα​)≤D(v)+αJ(v).
(60)
Taking the upper limit as α↓0 and then the infimum over v proves Φα,f​(uα​)→m. Its two nonnegative contributions above m must vanish, yielding the general result
D(uα​)⟶m,αJ(uα​)⟶0.​
(61)
In particular, the requested zero-misfit limit holds when f∈K(domJ)​. A sufficient condition is an exact solution v with Kv=f and J(v)<∞; then
0≤D(uα​)≤αJ(v),0≤αJ(uα​)≤αJ(v)⟶0.

Membership of f in R(K) alone is insufficient. Take U=V=R, K=I, f=1, and J the indicator functional of a constraint set {0}. All positive-parameter objectives are proper extended-real functions with coercivity and sequential lower semicontinuity, with the unique minimizer uα​=0, yet D(uα​)=1/2 for every α. Thus the first printed limit needs compatibility with the regulariser's effective domain; the second limit remains true under the given assumptions.

For a functional E on a real Banach space, its convex conjugate is defined on the dual space by
E∗(p)=u∈Usup​{⟨p,u⟩−E(u)},p∈U∗.​
(63)
Here ⟨p,u⟩ is the dual pairing. For complex spaces, use its real part, equivalently regard the space as real for convex analysis. The convex conjugate is a convex function, being the supremum of affine functions of p, even when E itself is not convex.

Let G=E□F. To prove convexity, take u1​,u2​ with finite G values, 0<t<1, and ε>0. By the definition of infimum, choose vi​ with
E(vi​)+F(ui​−vi​)≤G(ui​)+ε.
(64)
Put ut​=tu1​+(1−t)u2​ and vt​=tv1​+(1−t)v2​. Convexity of E and F gives
G(ut​)​≤E(vt​)+F(ut​−vt​)≤t[E(v1​)+F(u1​−v1​)]+(1−t)[E(v2​)+F(u2​−v2​)]≤tG(u1​)+(1−t)G(u2​)+ε.​

Let ε↓0. If either endpoint value is infinite, the convexity inequality is automatic, and t=0,1 gives equality. Thus the infimal convolution of two convex functions is convex under the stated properness assumption. The proof uses approximate minimizing splits, so no attainment of the inner infimum is assumed.

Write ιC​ for the indicator functional of a constraint set C and q(u)=∥u∥2/2. Then
dC​=ιC​□q.
(68)
Both terms are convex, hence their infimal convolution is the convex squared distance to a convex set. The closest point theorem in a Hilbert space supplies the unique metric projection onto a closed convex set PC​u and gives dC​(u)=∥u−PC​u∥2/2.
Identify the Hilbert space with its dual space using the Riesz representation theorem. Completing the square yields q∗(p)=∥p∥2/2, while ιC∗​(p)=supv∈C​⟨p,v⟩=σC​(p) is the support function. The conjugate of the squared distance to a convex set is therefore
dC∗​(p)=21​∥p∥2+σC​(p).​
(69)
For the closed unit ball, the metric projection onto a closed convex set is PC​u=u if ∥u∥≤1, and PC​u=u/∥u∥ otherwise. Therefore
dC​(u)=21​(max{∥u∥−1,0})2,dC∗​(p)=21​∥p∥2+∥p∥.​
(70)
