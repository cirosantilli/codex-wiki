The spectrum of a commutative ring is the set
SpecR={p⊊R:p is a prime ideal}.
(1)
For an ideal I⊆R, set V(I)={p:I⊆p}. The Zariski topology declares precisely these subsets to be closed sets. Using arbitrary subsets instead of ideals gives the same definition, because a prime ideal contains a subset exactly when it contains the ideal generated by that subset.
We verify the closed set axioms directly. First, V(0)=SpecR and V(R)=∅. For any family of ideals,
⋂λ​V(Iλ​)=V(∑λ​Iλ​),
(2)
since containing the sum is equivalent to containing every summand. For two ideals,
V(I)∪V(J)=V(IJ).
(3)
Indeed, containing either I or J implies containing their product of ideals. Conversely, if a prime ideal p contains IJ but does not contain I, choose a∈I∖p. For every b∈J, ab∈p, so the prime ideal property forces b∈p. Thus J⊆p.
The displayed identities establish arbitrary intersections and finite unions of closed sets, including their empty cases. Therefore these sets define a topology on SpecR.

The induced map on the spectrum of a commutative ring is contraction of an ideal:
f(q)=φ−1(q).​
(4)

For any ideal I⊆R, its extension of an ideal to S is IS=φ(I)S. We have
f−1(VR​(I))={q:I⊆φ−1(q)}=VS​(IS).
(5)
Thus inverse images of Zariski-closed sets are Zariski-closed sets, which proves that f is a continuous map.

Nil(A)=⋂p∈SpecA​p.
(6)
Every nilpotent element belongs to every prime ideal. In the other direction, if a is not nilpotent, the multiplicative subset T={1,a,a2,…} avoids zero. A polynomial ring in one variable over a field is a Euclidean domain, hence a principal ideal domain; its nonzero prime ideals are generated by irreducible polynomials. The fundamental theorem of algebra says that all irreducible polynomials over C are linear. Combining it with complex conjugation shows that the monic irreducible polynomials over R are exactly the linear polynomials and quadratics with no real root. Thus
SpecR[x]={(0)}∪{(x−a):a∈R}∪{((x−a)2+b2):a∈R, b>0}.​
(10)

SpecC[x]={(0)}∪{(x−z):z∈C}.

Let T=R∖P. Since P is a prime ideal, T is a multiplicative subset containing 1 and excluding 0. The localization at a prime ideal is
RP​=T−1R.​
(13)
Concretely, its elements are equivalence classes of pairs (r,s)∈R×T, written r/s, with
sr​=s′r′​⟺u(s′r−sr′)=0 for some u∈T.
(14)
This extra multiplier is essential when R has zero divisors. Addition and multiplication are
sr​+s′r′​=ss′rs′+r′s​,sr​s′r′​=ss′rr′​.
(15)
The equivalence criterion is compatible with these operations: after multiplying by the multipliers witnessing changes of representatives, the corresponding numerators agree. The commutative ring axioms follow from those of R; the identity is 1/1.
The canonical ring homomorphism ι:R→RP​, r↦r/1, supplies the algebra over a commutative ring structure, with scalar action a(r/s)=ar/s. It also has the universal property of localization: whenever a ring homomorphism ψ:R→A sends T to units, the unique extension is r/s↦ψ(r)ψ(s)−1.
For later use, RP​ is a local ring with maximal ideal PRP​. Reduction of numerators and denominators gives a surjective ring homomorphism RP​→Frac(R/P) with kernel PRP​, so this ideal is proper and maximal. Every fraction outside it has numerator outside P and is a unit, with inverse s/r. Thus it is the unique maximal ideal.

The equivalence relation defining the localization at a prime ideal gives
ker(R→RP​)={r∈R:sr=0 for some s∈/P}.
(16)
Therefore
R⟶RP​ is injective ⟺ every s∈R∖P annihilates no nonzero element of R.​
(17)
Equivalently, all zero divisors of R must lie in P. To prove necessity, if s∈/P and sr=0 with r=0, then r/1=0 in RP​, contradicting the hypothesis that the map is injective. Conversely, if every denominator annihilates no nonzero element, the displayed kernel of a ring homomorphism is zero, so the map is injective.
An integral domain automatically satisfies this criterion at every prime ideal, but the criterion itself does not require R to be an integral domain.

P1​=0×k,P2​=k×0.
(19)

For the localization at a prime ideal P1​, every denominator (u,v)∈/P1​ has u=0, and
RP1​​⟶k,(u,v)(a,b)​⟼ua​
(20)
is an isomorphism. It is surjective using constant first-coordinate fractions. It is injective because a numerator with first coordinate zero is annihilated by e1​∈/P1​, so its fraction is zero. Interchanging the coordinates proves RP2​​≅k.
Thus the localization at a prime ideal is a field in every case, although R has zero divisors. The obstruction to using the previous part's argument is that the two nonzero factors of a zero product can survive at different prime ideals.

Let ej​ be the sequence with 1 in coordinate j and zero elsewhere. In the direct product of rings R=kN, the ideals
(e1​)⊊(e1​,e2​)⊊(e1​,e2​,e3​)⊊⋯

Nevertheless, each a=(aj​)∈R has a coordinatewise generalized inverse b=(bj​), defined by bj​=aj−1​ when aj​=0 and bj​=0 otherwise. It satisfies
a=a2b.
(22)
This is the defining property of a Von Neumann regular ring. Fix any prime ideal P, and take a∈P. Since ab∈P, 1−ab∈/P; but
(1−ab)a=0.
(23)
Consequently a/1=0 in the localization at a prime ideal RP​. All elements of PRP​ therefore vanish. By the local ring description in part (a), PRP​ is the unique maximal ideal, so RP​ is a nonzero field. A field has only the ideals zero and itself, and thus is Noetherian.
We have proved the stronger conclusion
RP​ is a field for every prime ideal P⊂R.​
(24)
The argument applies to every prime ideal, without assuming that it comes from a coordinate projection.

A P-primary ideal is a proper ideal Q with radical of an ideal Q​=P, such that ab∈Q and a∈/Q imply bn∈Q for some n≥1. Equivalently, when Q​=P, the implication is ab∈Q, b∈/P⇒a∈Q.
True for a nonempty finite family. Let Q=Q1​∩⋯∩Qm​, with each Qj​ a P-primary ideal. This intersection of ideals is proper. Its radical of an ideal is P: if r∈P, choose nj​ with rnj​∈Qj​ and put N=maxj​nj​. Then rN∈Q. Conversely, rN∈Q implies r∈Qj​​=P.
If ab∈Q and b∈/P, the primary ideal property gives a∈Qj​ for every j, so a∈Q. Therefore
Q1​∩⋯∩Qm​ is P-primary(m≥1).​
(25)
As usual, the statement about finite intersections excludes the empty intersection, which is the whole ring and is not a primary ideal.

False: existence holds, but uniqueness fails. First, the Lasker–Noether theorem supplies existence; here is an argument from the preceding part. If some proper ideal in a Noetherian ring were not a finite intersection of irreducible ideals, the ascending chain condition would let us choose such an ideal I maximal under inclusion. It is not irreducible, so I=J∩K with both J and K strictly larger than I. By maximality, both are finite intersections of irreducible ideals, and therefore so is I, a contradiction. Part (d) makes each factor a primary ideal. The whole ring is represented by the empty intersection if that convention is needed.
For a counterexample to uniqueness, work in R=k[x,y] over a field k and put I=(x2,xy). The Hilbert basis theorem makes R a Noetherian ring. There are two distinct minimal primary decompositions:
(x2,xy)=(x)∩(x2,y)=(x)∩(x2,y−x).​
(34)
The ideal (x) is a prime ideal, hence a primary ideal. Each of the other factors has quotient ring isomorphic to k[x]/(x2), by substituting respectively y=0 and y=x. As in part (b), each is (x,y)-primary.
To check both intersections, let Qc​=(x2,y−cx), for c=0,1. Substitution identifies R/Qc​ with k[x]/(x2). The class of xg(x,y) is zero exactly when g(0,0)=0, equivalently when g∈(x,y). Therefore (Qc​:x)=(x,y) and
(x)∩Qc​=x(Qc​:x)=x(x,y)=I.
(35)
The two Qc​ are different, since y belongs to Q0​ but has nonzero class x modulo Q1​. Both decompositions are irredundant: x∈/Qc​, while y−cx∈/(x). Applying the radical of an ideal operation to the two components in either decomposition gives (x) and (x,y), respectively.

A ring is a Noetherian ring if it satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals: every chain
I1​⊆I2​⊆I3​⊆⋯
(36)
eventually becomes constant. Equivalently, every ideal has a finite generating set of an ideal. To see this equivalence, if an ideal were not finitely generated, one could repeatedly choose an element outside the ideal generated by the preceding choices, producing a strictly ascending chain. Conversely, the union of an ascending chain is an ideal; if it is generated by finitely many elements, all of them lie in one term of the chain, and every later term equals that term.
A ring is an Artinian ring if it satisfies the descending chain condition on ideals: every chain
I1​⊇I2​⊇I3​⊇⋯
(37)
eventually becomes constant. These are stabilization conditions on all ideals, not merely on principal ideals.

A Dedekind domain is a Noetherian ring D which is an integral domain, is an integrally closed domain, and has Krull dimension one. Explicitly, every ideal is finitely generated; every element of the fraction field that is an integral element over D already belongs to D; and every nonzero prime ideal is a maximal ideal, with D itself not a field.
With this convention, an equivalent local characterization is that D is a Noetherian integral domain that is not a field and whose localization at a prime ideal DP​ is a discrete valuation ring for every nonzero prime ideal P. The integrally closed domain condition rules out singularities such as those represented by the subring k[t2,t3]⊂k[t], where t is integral but missing.
Some conventions also include fields by allowing Krull dimension at most one; the conclusions about ideals below remain valid under that convention.

where the Pj​ are distinct nonzero prime ideals and the nj​ are positive integers. The prime ideals and their exponents are unique up to reordering. The unit ideal D is the empty product; the zero ideal is excluded.
Write vP​(I) for the prime-ideal valuation in a Dedekind domain, namely the exponent of P in this factorization, with zero assigned when P is absent. The associated containment rule is
I⊆J⟺vP​(I)≥vP​(J) for every nonzero prime ideal P.
(51)
Larger exponents give smaller ideals, just as higher divisibility gives smaller principal ideals in Z. The theorem concerns unique factorization of ideals; it does not assert unique factorization of elements or that these ideals are principal.

Let D be a Dedekind domain. The zero ideal is generated by 0, and D is generated by 1, so consider a nonzero proper ideal I. Choose 0=a∈I. We use these consequences of the unique factorization of ideals in a Dedekind domain: every nonzero ideal has finitely many nonzero prime-ideal valuations in a Dedekind domain; containment reverses the valuations as in part (b); and
vP​(J+K)=min{vP​(J),vP​(K)}.
(52)
The last formula follows from the containment rule: J+K is the smallest ideal containing both J and K, and the product with the indicated minimum exponents is exactly that ideal.
Let T be the finite set of prime ideals dividing (a). For each P∈T, set rP​=vP​(I). Since (a)⊆I, we have rP​≤vP​((a)). The containment rule also gives I⊆PrP​+1, so choose
bP​∈I∖PrP​+1.
(53)
Distinct nonzero prime ideals of D are distinct maximal ideals and hence comaximal ideals; their positive powers are also comaximal. Indeed, if u+v=1 with u∈P, v∈Q, for positive integers m,n expanding (u+v)m+n−1 shows 1∈Pm+Qn, since each term contains either at least m factors of u or at least n factors of v. We use the Chinese remainder theorem in its ideal form: for pairwise comaximal ideals J1​,…,Js​, the map D→∏j​D/Jj​ is surjective. Apply it to the powers PrP​+1 to choose eP​∈D with
eP​≡1(modPrP​+1),eP​≡0(modQrQ​+1)(Q∈T, Q=P).
(54)
Then b=∑P∈T​eP​bP​ belongs to I and satisfies b≡bP​(modPrP​+1) for every P∈T. Thus b∈/PrP​+1, whereas b∈I⊆PrP​, proving vP​((b))=rP​.

vP​((a,b))=min{vP​((a)),vP​((b))}=rP​.

Take
D=Z[−5​].​
(57)
We justify that this is a Dedekind domain. It is an integral domain inside the number field K=Q(−5​). As an additive group it is free of rank two over Z. The subgroup theorem for finitely generated abelian groups says that every subgroup of such a group is finitely generated. Thus every ideal has finitely many additive generators, and those same elements generate it as a D-ideal. This proves that D is Noetherian.
We use the standard formula for the ring of integers of a quadratic field: for squarefree d, it is Z[(1+d​)/2] if d≡1(mod4), and Z[d​] otherwise. Since −5≡3(mod4), D is the ring of integers of a number field K, meaning all elements of K integral over Z. We also use transitivity of integral extensions. If an element of K is integral over D, it is integral over Z because D is integral over Z, and therefore belongs to D. Thus D is an integrally closed domain.
Every nonzero prime ideal P is maximal. Indeed, choose 0=α=a+b−5​∈P. Multiplying by its algebraic conjugate puts the nonzero integer N(α)=a2+5b2 in P. Hence the integral domain D/P is a quotient of the finite ring D/N(α)D, and is finite. Every finite integral domain is a field, since multiplication by a nonzero element is injective and therefore surjective. The element 2 has no inverse in D, since 1/2∈/Z[−5​], so D is not a field. Consequently (0) is not maximal, and a maximal ideal above it is nonzero. Together with the preceding result, this gives Krull dimension exactly one.
Finally, consider the ideal
I=(2,1+−5​).
(58)
Reduction modulo 2 with −5​↦1 identifies D/I with F2​, so the additive index [D:I] is 2. If I were the principal ideal (a+b−5​), multiplication by its nonzero generator would have integer matrix
(ab​−5ba​)

The Krull dimension of a commutative ring R is
dimR=sup{n:p0​⊊p1​⊊⋯⊊pn​ is a chain of prime ideals of R}.​
(61)
The length counts strict inclusions, so a chain with n+1 prime ideals has length n. The supremum may be infinite. A field, for example, has just the prime ideal (0) and therefore has Krull dimension zero. For the zero ring, the spectrum of a commutative ring is empty; under the convention sup∅=−∞, its dimension is −∞.

dimA=1.​

Every nonzero proper ideal is therefore (πn) for some n≥1. Such an ideal is a prime ideal only when n=1: for n≥2, ππn−1∈(πn) while neither factor belongs to (πn). The ideal (π) is prime because it is maximal. Also (0) is prime because A is an integral domain. Thus

For a subring S⊆R with the same identity, R is an integral extension of S if every r∈R is an integral element over S: there exist n≥1 and s0​,…,sn−1​∈S such that
rn+sn−1​rn−1+⋯+s1​r+s0​=0.​
(64)
The requirement that the polynomial be monic is essential. Merely satisfying some polynomial equation over S is insufficient: for instance 1/2∈Q satisfies 2T−1=0 over Z, but is not integral over Z. To see this directly, a hypothetical monic equation for 1/2, multiplied by 2n, would say that an odd integer 1 plus a sum of even integers is zero.
The degree and coefficients may depend on r. Integrality does not by itself assert that the entire ring R is a finite S-module; it does imply that the subalgebra S[r] is generated as an S-module by 1,r,…,rn−1.

We use three theorems for an integral extension S⊆R, none requiring Noetherian hypotheses. The Lying-over theorem says that each prime ideal p of S is q∩S for some prime ideal q of R. The going-up theorem says that if p⊆p′ in S and q∩S=p, there is q′⊇q with q′∩S=p′. The incomparability theorem for integral extensions says that comparable prime ideals of R with the same contraction to S are equal.
Take any strict chain
q0​⊊⋯⊊qn​
(65)
in R. The contraction of an ideal operation gives pj​=qj​∩S, which are prime ideals of S, by the proof in Question 1(b), and remain strictly increasing by the incomparability theorem for integral extensions. Thus every chain length in R occurs in S, giving dimR≤dimS.
Conversely, take any strict chain p0​⊊⋯⊊pn​ in S. Use the Lying-over theorem to choose q0​ over p0​, then repeatedly use the going-up theorem to obtain q0​⊆⋯⊆qn​ with the prescribed contractions. Each inclusion must be strict, since its contractions are distinct. Hence every chain length in S occurs in R, giving dimS≤dimR.
Taking suprema proves integral extensions preserve Krull dimension:
dimR=dimS.​
(66)
The argument works equally well when the dimensions are infinite, since it compares all finite chain lengths.
