Use the Euler equations for an inviscid fluid with no magnetic or gravitational force. Let w denote specific enthalpy. For a homentropic flow, dw=dp/ρ, so the pressure acceleration is −∇w. The velocity identity
(u⋅∇)u=∇2u2​−u×ω,ω=∇×u,
(1)
therefore gives
∂t​u=u×ω−∇B,B=w+2u2​.
(2)
Taking the curl, using that the curl of a gradient is zero and that differentiation commutes for smooth fields, proves barotropic vorticity transport:
∂t​ω=∇×(u×ω).​
(3)
More generally the same proof works for any barotropic fluid, replacing w by a pressure potential ∫ρp′(s)ds/s. Here “isentropic” must supply this barotropic closure, for example through one common specific entropy throughout the fluid. Merely imposing entropy advection equation Ds/Dt=0 allows spatial specific entropy gradients; in that case the vorticity equation contains the additional baroclinic vorticity generation term ρ−2∇ρ×∇p.

Write q=u⋅ω for the kinetic helicity density, and retain B=w+u2/2. Since ω is a curl, ∇⋅ω=0. Differentiating the density and using the equations from the preceding part gives
∂t​q=−ω⋅∇B+u⋅∇×(u×ω).
(4)
The divergence of a cross product gives
∇⋅{u×(u×ω)}=(u×ω)⋅ω−u⋅∇×(u×ω).
(5)
The first term on the right is zero, while ω⋅∇B=∇⋅(Bω). Thus the kinetic helicity conservation law has flux
FHk​​=Bω+u×(u×ω)=qu+(w−2u2​)ω,∂t​q+∇⋅FHk​​=0.​
(6)
The last equality for the flux uses the vector triple product identity. Integrating and applying the divergence theorem gives d∫V​qdV/dt=−∫∂V​FHk​​⋅ndS for a fixed volume. Hence its total kinetic helicity is conserved when the boundary flux vanishes; for example, tangency of both velocity field and vorticity to the boundary is sufficient. The local conservation law does not by itself imply that its density follows each fluid particle unchanged.

Expanding the kinetic helicity conservation law separates advection from the other flux:
DtDq​=ω⋅∇(2u2​−w)−q∇⋅u.​
(7)
Thus material conservation of kinetic helicity density holds precisely when
ω⋅∇(2u2​−w)=q∇⋅u.​
(8)
An especially useful sufficient pair of conditions is incompressible flow, ∇⋅u=0, and constancy of w−u2/2 along integral curves of the vorticity, ω⋅∇(w−u2/2)=0. Together these conditions make both terms vanish without cancellation. They are not necessary individually, because the two terms in the displayed balance can cancel. An irrotational flow is a trivial conserved case with q=0.
For contrast, combining the balance with mass conservation gives
DtD​(ρq​)=−ρω​⋅∇(w−2u2​).
(9)
Consequently constancy of w−u2/2 along integral curves of the vorticity makes q/ρ an advected scalar even in compressible flow; it does not generally make q itself constant.

Work in the shock frame, assume a calorically perfect gas with fixed specific-heat ratio γ>1, and neglect magnetic stresses and body forces across the thin shock. The internal energy per unit mass is e=p/[(γ−1)ρ], and the specific enthalpy is w=γp/[(γ−1)ρ]. Integrating the local conservation laws across a thin stationary control volume makes the mass flux ρu, momentum flux p+ρu2, and energy flux u(ρu2/2+γp/(γ−1)) continuous. Hence the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas are
ρ1​u1​=ρ2​u2​=m,p1​+ρ1​u12​=p2​+ρ2​u22​,2u12​​+(γ−1)ρ1​γp1​​=2u22​​+(γ−1)ρ2​γp2​​.
(10)
Multiplication of the last equality by m gives continuity of the stated energy flux. These express that mass, momentum and energy cannot accumulate in an infinitesimally thin steady shock, although entropy production occurs on the physical compression branch. The same constant in the polytropic equation of state p=Kργ must therefore not be imposed on both sides. For flow from z>0 to z<0, both signed velocities are negative; the flux equations and ratios remain valid.
In the Strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions, p1​/(ρ1​u12​)=1/(γM12​) tends to zero. Put r=ρ2​/ρ1​. The first two flux balances give u2​=u1​/r and p2​=ρ1​u12​(1−1/r) to leading order. The energy balance becomes
21​=2r21​+γ−1γ​r1−1/r​,(r−1){(γ−1)r−(γ+1)}=0.
(11)
Discarding the unchanged-state root r=1 gives the compressive shock compression ratio and the required limits:
ρ1​ρ2​​⟶γ−1γ+1​,u1​u2​​⟶γ+1γ−1​,ρ1​u12​p2​​⟶γ+12​.​
(12)

The conservation of energy to use here is the total kinetic energy plus internal energy inside the shock, not just the internal energy of a uniform-pressure core. With no radiative loss and negligible initial ambient energy,
E˙t=4π∫0R(t)​(21​ρu2+γ−1p​)r2dr.
(19)
Substitute the adiabatic superbubble similarity solution and define the finite positive dimensionless integral
I=∫01​(21​f(η)h(η)2+γ−1g(η)​)η2dη.
(20)
Then E˙t=4πρ1​R˙2R3I. Since R˙=3R/(5t) and R5=α5(E˙/ρ1​)t3,
E˙t=2536π​α5E˙tI,α=(36πI25​)1/5.​
(21)
This is the energy normalization of a continuously driven spherical shock. It fixes α once the similarity profiles are determined. If the profiles describe only the shocked ambient layer, the energy of the interior hot bubble must also be included before using this normalization. A radiatively cooled shell has a different energy budget; its familiar numerical coefficient should not be substituted into this loss-free model.

Set A=Φ0​R0β​<0, q=λ2 and s=R2+qz2. The flattened power-law gravitational potential is Φ=As−β/2. In axisymmetric cylindrical coordinates, the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity gives
4πGρ=R1​∂R​(R∂R​Φ)+∂z2​Φ.
(22)
The first derivatives are ∂R​Φ=−βARs−(β+2)/2 and ∂z​Φ=−βAqzs−(β+2)/2. Differentiating again and collecting powers gives
∇2Φ=−βAs−(β+4)/2[(2+q)s−(β+2)(R2+q2z2)].
(23)
Therefore the required mass density is
ρ(R,z)=−4πGβΦ0​R0β​​(R2+λ2z2)(β+4)/2(λ2−β)R2+λ2[2−(β+1)λ2]z2​.​
(24)
This is a formal density for arbitrary λ, but a physical dark matter distribution must be nonnegative. The density positivity for a flattened power-law potential condition is
β≤λ2≤β+12​.​
(25)
Necessity follows by evaluating on the midplane and symmetry axis; sufficiency follows because both numerator coefficients are then nonnegative. In this range the origin is a locally integrable density cusp: the mass enclosed near radius r scales as r1−β and tends to zero, so no point mass needs adding. The scale-free distribution has infinite total mass at large radius and represents an idealized background, not a finite isolated halo.
For the cold, non-self-gravitating disk, radial balance of a circular orbit is RΩ2=∂R​Φ(R,0). Thus
Ω2(R)=−βΦ0​R0β​R−β−2,Ω(R)=R0​−βΦ0​​​(R0​R​)−(β+2)/2.​
(26)
The displayed positive root chooses the rotation orientation; the opposite orientation has the negative of this angular frequency.

BR​=−R1​∂z∂Ψ​,Bz​=R1​∂R∂Ψ​.​

Bz​(R,0)=R0δ​δΨ0​​Rδ−2,BR​(R,0)=Bz​(R,0)tanα.

For local cold magnetocentrifugal acceleration, the field must guide the gas and enforce approximate corotation with its disk footpoint in the sub-Alfvénic launching region. Let that footpoint be R=Rf​, and keep its field-line angular velocity Ωf​=Ω(Rf​) constant along the field. In this rotating frame the relevant effective potential is
V(R,z)=Φ(R,z)−21​Ωf2​R2.
(30)
The footpoint is a critical point because ∂R​Φ(Rf​,0)=Rf​Ωf2​ and ∂z​Φ(Rf​,0)=0. The meridional Hessian matrix there is
VRR​=−(β+2)Ωf2​,Vzz​=λ2Ωf2​,VRz​=0.
(31)
Take distance ℓ along a smooth outward magnetic field line whose initial tangent is (sinα,cosα) in the (R,z) plane. Since the gradient of V vanishes at the footpoint, field-line curvature does not contribute to its second derivative. Consequently
V(ℓ)−V(0)=21​Ωf2​[λ2cos2α−(β+2)sin2α]ℓ2+O(ℓ3).
(32)
A negative quadratic coefficient gives a downhill displacement and acceleration without thermal assistance. The magnetocentrifugal launching criterion in a flattened power-law potential is therefore
tan2α>β+2λ2​,α>αcrit​=arctan(β+2​∣λ∣​).​
(33)
Angles are measured from the vertical, with 0≤α<π/2. Below the critical angle the footpoint is a local minimum along the field, so a cold particle cannot cross the initial potential barrier. At equality the quadratic test is marginal; higher derivatives and the unspecified field curvature can decide the outcome. The threshold is a local condition for launch, not proof that the wind escapes along an arbitrary global field geometry. The spherical point-mass limit λ=1, β→1 recovers αcrit​=30∘, consistent with Ogilvie's discussion of cold disk winds, section 9.9.

For an axisymmetric vector field, write B=BR​eR​+Bϕ​eϕ​+Bz​ez​. Pure differential rotation gives
u×B=RΩBz​eR​−RΩBR​ez​.
(34)
This vector has no azimuthal component and is independent of ϕ, so its curl has zero radial and vertical components. Its azimuthal component is
{∇×(u×B)}ϕ​=∂z​(RΩBz​)+∂R​(RΩBR​)=R(Bz​∂z​Ω+BR​∂R​Ω)+Ω{R∂z​Bz​+∂R​(RBR​)}.
