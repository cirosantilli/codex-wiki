For a fixed-step linear multistep method with a nonzero leading coefficient, applied to an ordinary differential equation with a locally Lipschitz continuous vector field, the Dahlquist equivalence theorem states that
convergence ⟺ consistency and zero-stability.​
(1)
Here convergence is uniform on each fixed finite time interval as the step size tends to zero, for every set of starting values tending to the corresponding exact values. Consistency of a numerical method means that the normalized local defect tends to zero; for the characteristic polynomials of a linear multistep method it gives ρ(1)=0 and ρ′(1)=σ(1). Zero-stability is equivalent to the root condition for a multistep method: every root of a polynomial ξ of ρ satisfies ∣ξ∣≤1, and roots with ∣ξ∣=1 are simple. Order p≥1, together with zero-stability and starting errors O(hp), gives global error O(hp) under the usual smoothness assumptions.
To prove the necessity of the root condition for multistep convergence, use the test problem y′=0, y(0)=0. Its discrete error satisfies ρ(E)en​=0, where Een​=en+1​. Set h=T/N. If ∣ξ∣>1, the exact recurrence solution
en​=∣ξ∣−Nξn
(2)
has starting errors tending to zero at every fixed starting index, but ∣eN​∣=1. Thus convergence fails.
If a unit-modulus root has multiplicity m≥2, the recurrence instead admits
en​=N−(m−1)nm−1ξn.
(3)
Again every fixed starting error tends to zero while ∣eN​∣=1. These polynomial-times-exponential solutions follow from the repeated factor (E−ξ)m. Complex modes can be interpreted as a real two-component system, or their real and imaginary parts can be used. Therefore convergence requires precisely the root condition for a multistep method. Repeated roots strictly inside the unit disk are allowed, since their polynomial factors are dominated by exponential decay.

Factor the first characteristic polynomial as

dtd​∥u(t)∥L22​=2Re∫Ω​uut​=2Re[−i(∫Ω​uΔu−∫Ω​V∣u∣2)].

The starting point of a conforming finite element method is a weak formulation in a Hilbert space X. For example, a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on a bounded Lipschitz domain gives X=H01​(Ω). One seeks
a(u,v)=ℓ(v)(v∈X),
(38)
where a is a bounded bilinear form, ∣a(w,v)∣≤L∥w∥X​∥v∥X​, and ℓ∈X∗ is a bounded linear functional. A conforming finite element space Xh​⊂X is finite dimensional and is typically built from functions that are polynomial on each element of a finite element mesh. With a basis φj​, the Galerkin method imposes the same identity only for vh​∈Xh​, giving
Kc=F,Kij​=a(φj​,φi​),Fi​=ℓ(φi​).​
(39)
The stiffness matrix is sparse when basis supports overlap only locally. Local element integrals assemble its entries; the mass matrix represents an L2 term. This separates the choice of trial functions from the variational principle determining their coefficients.
The fundamental well-posedness theorem is the Lax-Milgram theorem: if a(v,v)≥γ∥v∥X2​ for some γ>0, there is a unique solution for each ℓ∈X∗, with ∥u∥X​≤∥ℓ∥X∗​/γ. The same theorem on Xh​ gives a unique discrete solution. The constants must be independent of the mesh for uniform stability of a numerical method. Boundary conditions are built into the trial space when they are essential constraints; flux conditions arise through boundary terms in integration by parts and are natural constraints in the weak formulation.
When a is symmetric and a coercive bilinear form, the Ritz method minimizes
E(v)=21​a(v,v)−ℓ(v)
(40)
over Xh​. Its first variation is exactly the Galerkin method, so Ritz minimization and Galerkin testing coincide for symmetric coercive problems. The energy norm ∥v∥a​=a(v,v)​ turns the approximation into an orthogonal projection. Indeed, subtracting the exact and discrete weak equations gives Galerkin orthogonality, a(u−uh​,vh​)=0. For every vh​∈Xh​ the Pythagorean identity becomes
∥u−vh​∥a2​=∥u−uh​∥a2​+∥uh​−vh​∥a2​,
(41)
so
∥u−uh​∥a​=vh​∈Xh​inf​∥u−vh​∥a​.​
(42)
This exact best-approximation property is the central advantage of the Ritz method.
Symmetry is unnecessary for the Galerkin method. For any bounded, coercive form, the Céa lemma gives
∥u−uh​∥X​≤γL​vh​∈Xh​inf​∥u−vh​∥X​.​
(43)
To prove it, put e=u−uh​. Galerkin orthogonality yields a(e,e)=a(e,u−vh​), so coercivity and boundedness give γ∥e∥X2​≤L∥e∥X​∥u−vh​∥X​. Divide when e=0 and take the infimum. Thus approximation capability plus uniform coercivity gives convergence. In contrast, minimizing 21​a(v,v)−ℓ(v) for a nonsymmetric form differentiates its symmetric part and generally does not solve the original weak equation.
A concrete symmetric example is the one-dimensional reaction-diffusion problem from Question 5. Continuous piecewise-linear hat functions give the diffusion stiffness matrix S with diagonal 2/h and neighbouring entries −1/h, and the mass matrix M with diagonal 2h/3 and neighbouring entries h/6. Therefore K=S+M and Fi​=ih2. Its positive-definite matrix is the coordinate form of the continuous energy. The exact smooth solution u(x)=x−sinh(x)/sinh1 makes this an explicit test of the method, not just an abstract existence result.
For a nonsymmetric example, take positive ε, constant real β, and
−εu′′+βu′+u=f,u(0)=u(1)=0.
(44)
Its form is a(u,v)=ε∫u′v′+β∫u′v+∫uv. Since ∫v′v=0, it has coercivity constant min(ε,1) in the full H1 norm, although it is not symmetric when β=0. The Galerkin method remains well posed. On the uniform hat basis, K=εS+βC+M, where Ci,i+1​=1/2, Ci,i−1​=−1/2 and Cii​=0. The skew-symmetric matrix C cancels from cTKc, proving nonsingularity. A Ritz energy would omit precisely this advection contribution. Small ε may nevertheless make the approximation constant large and produce poorly resolved layers; algebraic solvability is not an accuracy guarantee.
Quantitative convergence uses a finite element interpolation estimate. On a shape-regular mesh, for piecewise-linear conforming elements and u∈H2(Ω) in the usual low-dimensional setting,
∥u−Ih​u∥H1​≤Ch∥u∥H2​,∥u−Ih​u∥L2​≤Ch2∥u∥H2​.
(45)
The Céa lemma therefore gives an O(h) energy error. The Aubin–Nitsche duality argument improves the L2 norm error when the dual elliptic problem has H2 regularity: solve a(v,z)=(e,v)L2​ and use
∥e∥L22​=a(e,z−Ih​z)≤Ch∥e∥H1​∥z∥H2​≤Ch∥e∥H1​∥e∥L2​.
(46)
Consequently
∥u−uh​∥H1​=O(h),∥u−uh​∥L2​=O(h2),​
(47)
with constants depending on the solution and regularity bounds. The second estimate is conditional on dual regularity; corners or insufficient data regularity can reduce the rate.
The same Galerkin method handles evolution equations. For the homogeneous heat equation, the semidiscrete finite element heat equation is Mc˙+Sc=0. Because M is a positive-definite matrix and S is a positive semidefinite matrix,
dtd​(cTMc)=−2cTSc≤0.
(48)
The decreasing quadratic form is exactly the L2 norm squared of the finite-element function. This separates spatial stability of a numerical method from the subsequent time integrator, just as in the Schrödinger equation example. Conformity, coercivity and approximation estimates together explain convergence.

An s-stage Runge-Kutta method for y′=f(t,y) is defined by
Yi​=yn​+h∑j=1s​aij​f(tn​+cj​h,Yj​),yn+1​=yn​+h∑i=1s​bi​f(tn​+ci​h,Yi​).
(49)
The Butcher tableau records A=(aij​), b and c; internal consistency usually sets c=Ae, and first-order consistency requires bTe=1. A strictly lower triangular A gives an explicit method, while other stage dependencies generally require an implicit Runge-Kutta method. The stage solvability of an implicit Runge-Kutta method must be checked separately: if f is globally Lipschitz continuous in y with constant L, the stage fixed-point map is a contraction in the maximum stage norm whenever hLmaxi​∑j​∣aij​∣<1. This is a sufficient small-step condition, not a restriction intrinsic to the definitions of A-stability or B-stability.
Linear stability begins with the Dahlquist test equation y′=λy. Solving the stages gives
yn+1​=R(z)yn​,R(z)=1+zbT(I−zA)−1e,z=hλ.​
(50)
The linear stability domain consists of test values for which the stage system is well defined and ∣R(z)∣≤1. A-stability means this includes the closed left half-plane. For Reλ<0, this gives stability with no scalar decay-mode step restriction. For a normal matrix L in y′=Ly, unitary diagonalization of a normal matrix reduces the norm estimate to these scalar factors. For a non-normal matrix, eigenvalues alone do not establish a uniform norm bound: eigenvector conditioning and transient amplification matter. Mesh-uniform estimates for discretized PDEs must control operator powers, not only their spectra.
Every consistent explicit Runge-Kutta method has a nonconstant polynomial stability function and therefore cannot be A-stable, since that polynomial is unbounded on the negative real axis. For example, Forward Euler method has R(z)=1+z, stability disk ∣1+z∣≤1, and restriction 0≤hq≤2 for λ=−q<0. For a pure imaginary mode λ=iω=0, ∣1+ihω∣>1, so it is unstable for every positive step. The classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta method instead has
R(z)=1+z+21​z2+61​z3+241​z4,∣R(iq)∣2=1−72q6​+576q8​.
(51)
It is stable on the imaginary axis exactly for ∣q∣≤22​, an illustrative conditional stability range for oscillatory evolution.
Backward Euler method has R(z)=(1−z)−1 and is A-stable. L-stability adds R(z)→0 as ∣z∣→∞ in the left half-plane; backward Euler satisfies this and strongly damps unresolved rapidly decaying modes in a stiff differential equation. The implicit midpoint rule and the trapezoidal rule both have R(z)=(1+z/2)/(1−z/2), so they are A-stable but not L-stable. They preserve the modulus of a pure imaginary test mode, but their stiff-decay limit is −1, leaving oscillatory numerical remnants. The three-stage Lobatto IIIA method has the fourth-order rational function found in Question 2 and likewise lacks L-stability. Thus high order and A-stability do not themselves imply efficient stiff damping.
Nonlinear stability measures differences between solutions of the same equation. A one-sided Lipschitz condition is
Re⟨f(t,y)−f(t,y​),y−y​⟩≤ν∥y−y​∥2.
(52)
Differentiating the squared difference and applying the Gronwall inequality gives ∥y(t)−y​(t)∥≤eν(t−t0​)∥y(t0​)−y​(t0​)∥. A dissipative vector field has ν≤0 and is contractive. A B-stable method preserves this property for every positive step size for which the stages are well defined:
∥yn+1​−y​n+1​∥≤∥yn​−y​n​∥.​
(53)
This includes time-dependent vector fields when dissipativity holds at each common time argument. Applying it to scalar linear dissipative fields shows that B-stability implies A-stability when the linear stages are well defined; the converse fails.
The standard sufficient theorem is algebraic stability implies B-stability, subject to stage solvability. Define B=diag(b) and M=BA+ATB−bbT. Algebraic stability of a Runge-Kutta method means bi​≥0 and M is a positive semidefinite matrix. To prove the theorem, set d=yn​−y​n​, Di​=Yi​−Yi​ and Fi​=f(tn​+ci​h,Yi​)−f(tn​+ci​h,Yi​). Expanding the output difference and using Di​=d+h∑j​aij​Fj​ yields the Runge-Kutta contractivity identity
∥dn+1​∥2=∥d∥2+2h∑i​bi​Re⟨Di​,Fi​⟩−h2∑i,j​mij​Re⟨Fi​,Fj​⟩.
(54)
Dissipativity makes the first sum nonpositive, while positive semidefiniteness makes the last quadratic form nonnegative. The latter follows by factoring M=QTQ and writing it as a sum of squared norms of linear combinations of the Fi​. This proves the claimed contraction. It is a sufficient theorem; absence of algebraic stability is not, by itself, a proof of failure of B-stability for every representation.
Several examples make the distinctions concrete. For backward Euler, b=1, A=(1), so M=(1): it is both L-stable and B-stable. For implicit midpoint, b=1, A=(1/2), so M=(0): it is B-stable and preserves squared norms for a skew-Hermitian linear equation, but lacks stiff damping. For the two-stage Gauss--Legendre Runge-Kutta method,
A=(1/41/4+3​/6​1/4−3​/61/4​),b=(1/2,1/2)T,
(55)
the matrix M vanishes as well. The collocation theorem gives order four, and its stability function is exactly the same as the three-stage Lobatto IIIA function. Yet the Gauss method is algebraically stable, whereas that Lobatto method is not. Identical scalar linear stability functions need not imply identical nonlinear stability properties.
An example combining stiff damping and nonlinear contraction is the two-stage Radau IIA method, with
A=(5/123/4​−1/121/4​),b=(3/4,1/4)T,M=161​(1−1​−11​),R(z)=1−2z/3+z2/61+z/3​.
(56)
Its positive weights and positive semidefinite M prove B-stability; its rational function has denominator roots 2±i2​ and, for z=x+iy, satisfies
∣1−2z/3+z2/6∣2−∣1+z/3∣2=36∣z∣4−8x∣z∣2+24x2−72x​≥0(x≤0).
(57)
Thus it is A-stable; the limit R(z)→0 then proves L-stability. The standard Radau IIA collocation theorem gives order 2s−1, here three. These properties explain its usefulness for stiff nonlinear equations.
Finally, A-stability alone does not imply B-stability. The trapezoidal rule fails B-stability even for the scalar dissipative equation y′=−y3. With h=1, its step map T is uniquely defined by
T(y)+21​T(y)3=y−21​y3.
(58)
At y=2​, T(y)=0. Implicit differentiation gives
T′(y)=1+23​T(y)21−23​y2​,T′(2​)=−2.
(59)
Thus nearby starting values are separated by approximately twice their original distance after one step, although the exact flow is contractive. This example and the contractivity theorem show why scalar linear analysis, stiff damping and genuinely nonlinear norm estimates answer different stability questions.
