The Mellin transform is
M(f,s)=∫0∞​f(y)ys−1dy,​
(1)
where this integral converges. Rapid decay at infinity makes the integral over [1,∞) an entire function of s, but alone gives no control near zero. With the additional expansion, the integral initially converges absolutely for Res>−σ1​.
For Res>1, expand (eπy−1)−1=∑r≥1​e−πry. Absolute convergence justifies termwise integration, using the Gamma integral, and gives the scaled Bose integral
M(eπy−11​,s)=r≥1∑​(πr)−sΓ(s)=π−sΓ(s)ζ(s).​
(2)
There is a missing hypothesis in the general continuation claim: one needs σj​→+∞. The original PDF, like the TeX, only says that the sequence increases. Under the intended additional hypothesis, split the integral at one and subtract r terms of the asymptotic expansion:
M(f,s)=∫1∞​f(y)ys−1dy+∑j=1r​s+σj​cj​​+∫01​ys+σr+1​−1gr​(y)dy.
(3)
Continuity of gr​ at zero makes it bounded on [0,1]. The last integral is holomorphic on Res>−σr+1​: on every compact subset, its integrand and all its s derivatives are dominated by an integrable power of y times a power of ∣logy∣. The expressions for successive r agree on their common initial domain and hence everywhere they overlap by the identity theorem. As σr+1​→∞, these half-planes cover C. Taking r≥j with σr+1​>σj​ isolates the term cj​/(s+σj​), while all other terms are holomorphic near −σj​. Thus the corrected claim is
Ress=−σj​​M(f,s)=cj​,with no other poles, provided σj​→∞.​
(4)
This is meromorphic continuation of a Mellin transform from an asymptotic expansion.
To show why the correction matters, set σj​=1−1/j, cj​=2−j, and
f(y)=η(y)∑j≥1​2−jy1−1/j,
(5)
where η is continuous, equals one for 0<y≤1 and vanishes for y≥2. On [0,1], the remainder after division by yσr+1​ is a uniformly convergent series of nonnegative powers of y, so it extends continuously to zero with value cr+1​. Away from zero, define gr​ by the required remainder quotient; it is continuous on the rest of [0,∞) as well. Thus all the printed hypotheses hold. Its Mellin transform equals
∑j≥1​s+1−1/j2−j​+∫12​f(y)ys−1dy.
(6)
The series continues meromorphically on Res>−1 and has genuine poles at −1+1/j, accumulating at −1. A meromorphic function on C cannot have such an accumulation of poles. This is the accumulating asymptotic exponents obstruct Mellin continuation counterexample.
For the Gamma function, the Gamma function recurrence gives
Γ(s)=s(s+1)⋯(s+j)Γ(s+j+1)​.
(7)
At s=−j the numerator is Γ(1)=1 and the product of the nonzero denominator factors is (−1)jj!. Therefore the residues of the Gamma function are
Ress=−j​Γ(s)=j!(−1)j​(j≥0).​
(8)
The generating function of the Bernoulli numbers gives the convergent expansion near zero
eπy−11​=∑n≥0​n!Bn​πn−1​yn−1.
(9)
The subtraction proof above applies to this expansion, including its zero coefficients, which give no pole. At s=1−n, for n≥1, the residue of its transform is Bn​πn−1/n!, while the residue of π−sΓ(s) is πn−1(−1)n−1/(n−1)!. Their quotient defines a holomorphic continuation of the Riemann zeta function at this point, including when the first residue is zero. Dividing gives the Bernoulli formula for zeta values at nonpositive integers:
ζ(1−n)=n(−1)n−1Bn​​(n≥1).​
(10)
In particular the generating-series convention is B1​=−1/2, so the formula includes ζ(0)=−1/2.

Write x=g−1h and S(x)=∑χ∈G​χ(x), where G is the character group of a finite abelian group. The Fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups writes the finite group G as a product of cyclic groups. A character of a cyclic group factor of order r is determined by an arbitrary rth root of unity, so ∣G∣=∣G∣. This description also shows that the characters separate points: if x=1, a nonzero coordinate of x is detected by a character ψ with ψ(x)=1.
Multiplication by ψ permutes G, so
S(x)=∑χ​(χψ)(x)=ψ(x)S(x).
(11)
For x=1 this forces S(x)=0. For x=1 every summand is one. Since χ(g)−1χ(h)=χ(g−1h), the character-sum cancellation lemma gives
χ∈G∑​χ(g)−1χ(h)=∣G∣1g=h​.​
(12)
All character values are roots of unity, so inversion here is also complex conjugation. This is the finite-abelian version of character orthogonality.

Extend each Dirichlet character periodically to all integers by setting χ(n)=0 when gcd(n,N)>1. Its Dirichlet L-function is initially the absolutely convergent Dirichlet series
L(χ,s)=n≥1∑​nsχ(n)​(Res>1).​
(13)
The group (Z/NZ)× has φ(N) elements. Part (i) gives, for every integer n, including nonunits,
∑χ∈G​χ(a)−1χ(n)=φ(N)1n≡amodN​.
(14)
For a nonunit both sides are zero, because the fixed class a is a unit. Interchanging the finite character sum and the absolutely convergent Dirichlet series therefore proves
χ∈G∑​χ(a)−1L(χ,s)=φ(N)n≡amodNn≥1​∑​n−s(Res>1).​
(15)
This is Orthogonality of Dirichlet characters applied to a residue-class indicator. Any meromorphic continuations of the two sides agree by uniqueness of analytic continuation.

The original PDF has the summation condition gcd(n,N)=1; the TeX's gcd(n,n)=1 is a transcription error. There is also an actual missing hypothesis in the PDF's coefficient formula: that simplified formula requires a primitive Dirichlet character. We first derive a formula valid for every character, and then show both the primitive specialization and a counterexample to the unrestricted version.
For k>2, the character-twisted Eisenstein series converges absolutely and locally uniformly on the complex upper half-plane. On a compact subset, ∣mz+n∣ is bounded below by a positive constant times (m2+n2)1/2, and the corresponding two-dimensional lattice sum converges. Changing n to n+mN proves
Gk​(χ,z+N)=Gk​(χ,z).​
(16)
The condition χ(−1)=(−1)k makes the terms for (m,n) and (−m,−n) equal. The terms with m=0 contribute 2L(χ,k), and all other terms are twice the sum over m≥1.
For Imw>0, the cotangent identity and
πcotπw=−πi−2πi∑r≥1​e2πirw
(17)
give, after k−1 differentiations,
∑t∈Z​(w+t)−k=Ak​∑r≥1​rk−1e2πirw,Ak​=(k−1)!(−2πi)k​.
(18)
Differentiation is justified by locally uniform convergence. This is the cotangent partial-fraction Fourier kernel.
Write n=a+Nt, with a running through the unit classes modulo N, and define the finite Fourier transform
Sχ​(r)=∑(a,N)=1amodN​​χ(a)e2πira/N,g(χ)=Sχ​(1).
(19)
Here g(χ) is the Gauss sum of a Dirichlet character.
Applying the kernel with w=(mz+a)/N yields
Gk​(χ,z)=2L(χ,k)+Nk2Ak​​∑m,r≥1​rk−1Sχ​(r)e2πimrz/N.
(20)
The double series converges absolutely: ∣Sχ​(r)∣≤φ(N) and the exponential decay controls ∑m,r​rk−1e−2πmrImz/N. Grouping the terms with mr=n proves the general Fourier expansion of a character-twisted Eisenstein series:
c0​=2L(χ,k),cn​=Nk2Ak​​r∣n∑​rk−1Sχ​(r)(n≥1).​
(21)
If r is a unit modulo N, substitution a↦r−1a gives Sχ​(r)=χ(r)−1g(χ). Suppose now that χ is primitive and r is a nonunit. Choose a prime p∣gcd(r,N). Reduction of units modulo N onto units modulo N/p is surjective. Primitivity means that χ is nontrivial on its kernel, so there is a unit u≡1(modN/p) with χ(u)=1. Since ru≡r(modN), substitution by u forces Sχ​(r)=χ(u)−1Sχ​(r) and thus Sχ​(r)=0. This proves the finite Fourier transform of a primitive Dirichlet character identity, and consequently
cn​=(k−1)!Nk2(−2πi)k​g(χ)r∣n∑​χ​(r)rk−1when χ is primitive.​
(22)
The inverse-character notation in the question is understood to mean χ​(r), extended by zero on nonunits; literal inversion of χ(r)=0 would be undefined.
For a counterexample without primitivity, take N=2, the principal character, and k=4. Then Sχ​(r)=(−1)r, so g(χ)=−1 but Sχ​(2)=1. At n=2, the general formula gives c2​=7A4​/8, whereas the printed simplified formula, interpreted as zero on nonunits, gives −A4​/8. Hence the printed coefficient formula is false for arbitrary imprimitive characters; the general boxed formula above supplies the correction.

Let S=(01​−10​) and T=(10​11​). The modular group acts on the complex upper half-plane by fractional linear transformations, and
Im(γz)=∣cz+d∣2Imz​(γ=(ac​bd​)).
(23)
For fixed z=x+iy, the values ∣cz+d∣ with primitive integer pairs (c,d) have a positive minimum: the conditions ∣cz+d∣≤R bound both ∣c∣≤R/y and ∣d∣≤R+∣cx∣, leaving finitely many pairs. By Bezout identity, a minimizing pair is the bottom row of a matrix in SL2​(Z). Applying that matrix gives a point of maximal imaginary part in its orbit.
Translate it by a power of T into −1/2<x≤1/2. It must have modulus at least one, since otherwise S would increase its imaginary part. If it is on the unit circle with negative real part, apply S, which there changes x to −x. This gives an element of the specified half-open standard fundamental domain of the modular group. It proves existence by reduction to the standard modular region.
For uniqueness, if z lies in the closed standard region then y≥3​/2 and
∣cz+d∣≥1
(24)
for every primitive pair. If c=0, this follows from d=±1; if ∣c∣≥2, it follows from ∣c∣y≥3​. If ∣c∣=1, then d=0 uses ∣z∣≥1, while ∣d∣≥1 gives
∣cz+d∣2=∣z∣2+d2+2cdx≥1+d2−∣d∣≥1.
(25)
If both z and z′=γz lie in the region, applying this inequality to γ and γ−1 forces their imaginary parts to agree, hence ∣cz+d∣=1.
The equality cases describe every possible boundary identification. For c=0 the map is an integer translation; the half-open vertical strip retains one representative. For ∣c∣=1,d=0, equality requires ∣z∣=1, and the map is an inversion followed by an integer translation. Inversion exchanges the two halves of the circle, so retaining only the right half removes the duplication; the only translation that returns the exchanged endpoint is the one identifying the two corners. The remaining equality cases have ∣c∣=∣d∣=1, ∣z∣=1 and x=±1/2, and give only those same corner identifications. Our region retains the right corner alone. Therefore z′=z, proving
every orbit has exactly one representative in D.​
(26)
The equality analysis also gives the elliptic stabilizers of the modular group. The central matrices ±I fix every point. The point i is fixed by S, with S2=−I, and the retained corner ρ+​=eiπ/3 is fixed by R=TS, with R3=−I. There are no other noncentral stabilizers, and the possible bottom rows in the equality cases give exactly
StabΓ​(z)=⎩⎨⎧​⟨S⟩≅C4​,⟨TS⟩≅C6​,{I,−I},​z=i,z=ρ+​,otherwise.​​
(27)
The corresponding stabilizer orders in PSL2​(Z) are two, three and one; the requested group is SL2​(Z), so the center must be included.

Use the convention that a weak modular form is a holomorphic function f on the complex upper half-plane satisfying
f(γz)=(cz+d)kf(z)(γ∈SL2​(Z)),
(28)
with no condition at infinity. In particular f(z+1)=f(z), so it has a Laurent series f(z)=∑n∈Z​an​qn on 0<∣q∣<1, where q=e2πiz. It is a modular form when it is holomorphic at a cusp, here the unique level-one cusp at infinity, equivalently an​=0 for n<0. It is a cusp form when additionally a0​=0. If one uses the narrower weakly holomorphic convention, one also requires finitely many negative terms; the derivative argument below holds in that subspace as well. The matrix −I forces odd-weight forms to be zero.
Put (a)r​=a(a+1)⋯(a+r−1), with (a)0​=1, and
Cℓ,j​=(jℓ​)(k+j)ℓ−j​(0≤j≤ℓ).
(29)
We prove the derivative transformation of a weak modular form in the uniform form
f(ℓ)(−1/z)=∑j=0ℓ​Cℓ,j​zk+ℓ+jf(j)(z).
(30)
For ℓ=0 this is the weight-k transformation under S. Differentiating the formula and multiplying by z2, since d(−1/z)/dz=z−2, gives the recurrence
Cℓ+1,j​=(k+ℓ+j)Cℓ,j​+Cℓ,j−1​,
(31)
where out-of-range coefficients are zero. The displayed explicit coefficients satisfy it. Indeed, for interior j, extract the common polynomial (k+j)ℓ−j​; the remaining identity is
(jℓ​)(k+ℓ+j)+(j−1ℓ​)(k+j−1)=(jℓ+1​)(k+ℓ).
(32)
This follows from Pascal's identity and j(jℓ​)=(ℓ−j+1)(j−1ℓ​). The endpoint coefficients satisfy the recurrence directly. This polynomial proof also applies when a factor is zero, avoiding division by that factor. As Cℓ,ℓ​=1, the result is
f(ℓ)(−1/z)=zk+2ℓf(ℓ)(z)+j=0∑ℓ−1​(jℓ​)(k+ℓ−1)⋯(k+j)zk+ℓ+jf(j)(z).​
(33)
The letter l in the printed exponent is the same derivative order ℓ.
For integer k<0, take ℓ=1−k. If j<ℓ, the product runs from k+j≤0 through k+ℓ−1=0, and therefore vanishes. Consequently f(1−k) has weight k+2(1−k)=2−k under S. It is also invariant under T, by differentiating f(z+1)=f(z), and S,T generate the modular group. Its holomorphy is preserved by differentiation. With D=(2πi)−1d/dz=qd/dq, termwise differentiation of the locally convergent Laurent series gives the Bol identity for modular forms:
D1−kf=n∈Z∑​n1−kan​qn is a weak modular form of weight 2−k.​
(34)
For negative indices the exponent 1−k is a positive integer, and the constant term is killed, so every term is unambiguous.

We use left multiplication by Γ=SL2​(Z). For an integral matrix A of determinant n>0, the Bezout identity gives a determinant-one row operation sending its first column to (a,0)t, where a is the positive gcd of that column. The resulting matrix is (a0​bd​) with d=n/a>0. Adding a multiple of the second row to the first makes 0≤b<d.
These representatives are unique. Left multiplication by a unimodular matrix preserves the gcd of the first column, so two representatives in one orbit have the same a and hence d. A matrix taking (a,0)t to itself has the form (10​t1​), so it changes b by td; the prescribed range makes b unique. Thus the determinant-n matrix representatives for Hecke operators are exactly Πn​. In the printed set, 0≤b<d already forces d>0, and ad=n>0 forces a>0.
For positive determinant extend the slash operator for modular forms by
(f∣k​A)(z)=det(A)k/2(cz+d)−kf(Az).
(35)
The determinant factor makes this a right action: (f∣k​A)∣k​B=f∣k​(AB). Right multiplication by δ∈Γ permutes the left cosets represented by Πn​, since it preserves the set of determinant-n integral matrices. Hence Tn​f satisfies the modular transformation law. Its summands are holomorphic on the upper half-plane. For an upper-triangular representative,
Tn​f=nk−1∑a,d>0ad=n​​d−k∑b=0d−1​f(daz+b​).
(36)
Substitute the Fourier expansion of a modular form. The sum over b is zero unless the original index is divisible by d, in which case it equals d. Writing that index as dr gives
Tn​f=∑a,d>0ad=n​​ak−1∑r≥0​adr​(f)qar.
(37)
Only nonnegative powers occur, so Tn​f is holomorphic at infinity, and all level-one cusps are equivalent to infinity. We have proved Tn​f∈Mk​(Γ) and the Fourier coefficients of a composite-index Hecke operator formula
ar​(Tn​f)=a∣gcd(n,r)∑​ak−1anr/a2​(f)(r≥0),a1​(Tn​f)=an​(f).​
(38)
Here gcd(n,0)=n, giving a0​(Tn​f)=∑a∣n​ak−1a0​(f). In particular cusp forms stay cusp forms. For nonzero level-one cusp forms the weight is an even integer at least twelve, so the powers ak−1 are integers; the zero cusp space causes no exception. Thus every Tn​ preserves integral Fourier coefficients, and closure under addition, multiplication and integer scalars proves
TSk​(Γ,Z)⊆Sk​(Γ,Z).​
(39)
This is the integral Hecke algebra of level-one cusp forms.
We will also need commutativity. The coefficient formula directly gives
Tu​Tv​=Tuv​(gcd(u,v)=1),Tp​Tpr​=Tpr+1​+pk−1Tpr−1​(r≥1).
(40)
For the first identity, the divisors of u and v in the iterated coefficient sum combine uniquely into a divisor of uv. For the second, write a coefficient index as ptr0​ with p∤r0​; the formula is
aptr0​​(Tpr​f)=∑j=0min(r,t)​pj(k−1)apr+t−2jr0​​(f).
(41)
Applying Tp​ splits this into the two sums with shifts t+1 and t−1; their overlapping terms give exactly the stated recurrence. Since T1​ is the identity, induction expresses each Tpr​ as a polynomial in Tp​, and coprime multiplicativity expresses every Tn​ as a product of these polynomials. Distinct prime operators commute by the coprime identity. Hence all Hecke operators commute, and so do all elements of T.
Let Λ=Sk​(Γ,Z) and m=dimSk​(Γ). In the permitted basis Fj​=ΔjE4aj​​E6bj​​, the modular discriminant begins q+O(q2) and the normalized Eisenstein series begin 1+O(q), so
Fj​=qj+O(qj+1)(1≤j≤m).
(42)
The matrix of a1​,…,am​ on this integral basis is unitriangular and has determinant one. Integer elimination therefore gives an integral basis f1​,…,fm​ with ai​(fj​)=δij​ for 1≤i,j≤m. Thus a1​,…,am​ are a Z-basis of the dual module HomZ​(Λ,Z).
The map α is well-defined by lattice preservation and is Z-linear. Because α(Tj​)=aj​, it is surjective. For injectivity suppose α(T)=0. For every f∈Λ and every n≥1, commutativity gives
an​(Tf)=a1​(Tn​Tf)=a1​(TTn​f)=α(T)(Tn​f)=0.
(43)
The constant term is also zero because Tf is a cusp form. The identity theorem applied to its Fourier expansion of a modular form gives Tf=0. The integral basis spans the complex cusp space, so T=0 as an endomorphism. This proves the perfect integral Hecke pairing and
T≅HomZ​(Λ,Z),T=j=1⨁m​ZTj​.​
(44)
If m=0, both modules are zero and the asserted basis is empty.

A modular form of integer weight k for a finite-index subgroup Γ≤SL2​(Z) is a holomorphic function on the complex upper half-plane satisfying f∣k​γ=f for γ∈Γ and holomorphic at every cusp of a modular group. Explicitly, for each σ∈SL2​(Z) choose a positive integer hσ​ with σThσ​σ−1∈Γ. Then f∣k​σ is periodic with period hσ​, and its expansion in qhσ​​=e2πiz/hσ​ must have no negative powers. Equivalently it must be bounded as Imz→∞, uniformly in one period. This definition applies to noncongruence subgroups too. If −I∈Γ, nonzero forms require even weight.
Put d=[SL2​(Z):Γ] and let f=0. Form the product over left cosets
F(z)=∏γ∈Γ\SL2​(Z)​(f∣k​γ)(z).
(45)
Changing a representative does not affect a factor. Right multiplication permutes the cosets, so the product is a nonzero level-one modular form of weight kd. It is holomorphic on the upper half-plane and at infinity, since every factor is bounded there by cusp holomorphy.
Let h be the least positive integer with Th∈Γ, and write f=∑n≥0​bn​qhn​. If r is its first nonzero index, the h distinct cosets ΓTj, 0≤j<h, give the factors f(z+j). Their product has leading term a nonzero constant times e2πirz=qr. All remaining factors are bounded at infinity. Hence F(z)=O(e−2πrImz), so its level-one order satisfies v∞​(F)≥r. Notice that using just the single factor f would only give r/h; the entire translation orbit is needed.
The permitted valence formula for the modular group bounds v∞​(F)≤kd/12, because all its other weighted zero orders are nonnegative. Thus r≤kd/12. With R=⌊kd/12⌋, the map f↦(b0​,…,bR​) is injective: a nonzero form in its kernel would have r≥R+1. This proves the dimension bound for modular forms on a finite-index subgroup:
dimMk​(Γ)≤⌊kd/12⌋+1≤1+kd/12(k≥0).​
(46)
For the dilation assertion, the original PDF states N1​D∣N, a product-divisibility condition, rather than the TeX's chain N1​∣D∣N. Use the PDF condition. Put A=diag(D,1). If γ=(ac​bd​)∈Γ0​(N), then
AγA−1=(ac/D​Dbd​)∈Γ0​(N1​),
(47)
since N1​D∣c. Therefore, for g(z)=f(Dz), either direct substitution or the slash operator for modular forms gives g∣k​γ=g. It is holomorphic on the upper half-plane.
To verify every cusp, take σ∈SL2​(Z) and choose τ∈SL2​(Z) taking infinity to the rational cusp Aσ∞. Then B=τ−1Aσ is rational upper triangular, with positive ratio of its two diagonal entries. Consequently
(f∣k​A)∣k​σ=(f∣k​τ)∣k​B
(48)
is a nonzero constant factor times f∣k​τ evaluated at an affine map z↦uz+v with u>0. The imaginary part tends to infinity, where f∣k​τ is bounded. Thus g∣k​σ is bounded too. Together with its periodicity this proves cusp holomorphy by the Riemann removable singularity theorem. Hence the oldform by argument dilation satisfies
f∈Mk​(Γ0​(N1​)), N1​D∣N ⟹ f(Dz)∈Mk​(Γ0​(N)).​
(49)
Finally Γ0​(3) has index four: reduction modulo three gives a transitive SL2​(F3​) action on the four points of P1(F3​), and Γ0​(3) is the inverse image of the stabilizer of infinity. Reduction is surjective, since elementary determinant-one matrices generate SL2​(F3​) and lift integrally. The dimension bound gives dimM4​(Γ0​(3))≤2.
The normalized Eisenstein series E4​(z) and the oldform E4​(3z) both belong to this space. Their Fourier expansion of a normalized Eisenstein series starts as
E4​(z)=1+240q+O(q2),E4​(3z)=1+240q3+O(q6).
(50)
A linear relation between them has zero constant term only if its two scalars sum to zero; its q coefficient then forces both scalars to vanish. They are independent, proving the weight-four modular forms on Gamma 0 3 description
dimM4​(Γ0​(3))=2,{E4​(z),E4​(3z)} is a basis.​
(51)
