For 1≤k≤n, the nearest neighbour distance ρ(k)​(x) is the kth order statistic of Di​=∣Xi​−x∣. Each Di​ has continuous distribution function px​(r) on [0,∞). The probability integral transform makes Ui​=px​(Di​) independent and identically distributed random variables with the uniform distribution on (0,1). Monotonicity of px​ gives P=U(k)​, a uniform order statistic. Strict monotonicity is not needed at this stage.

P(P≤s)=∑j=kn​(jn​)sj(1−s)n−j.
(1)

fP​(s)=(k−1)!(n−k)!n!​sk−1(1−s)n−k,0<s<1.​

EP=n+1k​,EP2=(n+1)(n+2)k(k+1)​.​

This is the probability content of a nearest neighbour ball identity, specialized to intervals on the real line.

Since the nearest neighbour density estimator obeys f(x)/f​(k)​(x)=2(n+1)f0​px−1​(P)/k, its conditional bound follows from the Beta distribution moments:

The conditional inverse function bound also bounds px−1​(P), so the expected value in this calculation is finite under those formal hypotheses.
There is, however, a genuine flaw in the original PDF: the printed condition f(x)≥L​ has no admissible points when f is strictly positive on all of R. A probability density function on all of R cannot have Lipschitz constant zero, so L>0. Its Lipschitz bound forces
f(x+u)≥max{f0​−L∣u∣,0}.
(10)
Integrating this triangular lower envelope gives the Lipschitz density height bound
1=∫R​f(y)dy>∫−f0​/Lf0​/L​(f0​−L∣u∣)du=Lf02​​.
(11)
The inequality is strict because the probability density function has positive mass outside the finite interval. Thus f(x)<L​ everywhere. The last two conditional conclusions above are valid implications but vacuous as printed. Dropping strict positivity gives only f(x)≤L​, with equality forcing the entire probability density function to be the normalized triangular envelope. The local inverse bound does not justify replacing the printed condition by its reverse in the final expected value bound, since P ranges over all of (0,1).

Put vp​(u)=(1,u,…,up)T and wi​(x)=K((xi​−x)/h), where p is a nonnegative integer and h is the smoothing bandwidth. The local polynomial estimator is the intercept of the weighted least squares fit

mh​(x)=∑i​K((xi​−x)/h)∑i​K((xi​−x)/h)Yi​​.​
(14)
Multiplying every weight by 1/h changes neither fit.
For the uniform kernel, let Ix​={i:∣i/n−x∣≤h} and Nx​=∣Ix​∣. Then mh​(x)=Nx−1​∑i∈Ix​​Yi​. For 0<h<1/2 and x∈[h,1−h], the interval [x−h,x+h] is inside [0,1], and the regular design has

(15)
The same lower bound holds when the interval starts at zero, despite the absence of the design point zero. In particular the Nadaraya–Watson estimator is defined throughout the integration interval.
For every Lipschitz continuous mean function in ΘL​, the bias of an estimator and variance satisfy
∣Em​mh​(x)−m(x)∣≤Nx​L​∑i∈Ix​​∣i/n−x∣≤Lh,Varm​mh​(x)=Nx​σ2​≤nhσ2​.

m∈ΘL​sup​Em​∫h1−h​{mh​(x)−m(x)}2dx​≤(1−2h)(L2h2+nhσ2​)≤L2h2+2nh3σ2​.​

Thus
m∈ΘL​sup​Em​∫h1−h​{mh​(x)−m(x)}2dx≤L2h2+2nh3σ2​.​

The printed integration interval represents a nonnegative risk function for h≤1/2. For 1/2<h≤1, the written integral has reversed limits and is nonpositive, making the displayed upper bound trivial; it should not be interpreted as an integrated mean squared error in that range. For h>1, evaluation outside [0,1] would additionally require a specified extension of the mean function. The subsequent optimization restricts to h<1/3.

The PDF's suggested smooth bump function is exp(−1/(1−t2))1{∣t∣≤1}​, with value zero at the endpoints, rather than the corrupted TeX formula. A triangular alternative suffices because only Lipschitz continuity is needed.
The Le Cam two-point lemma says that for two data laws P0​,P1​ with parameter separation Δ=∣θ1​−θ0​∣, every estimator T satisfies
j=0,1max​Ej​(T−θj​)2≥8Δ2​{1−∥P0​−P1​∥TV​}.​

Indeed, classify according to the nearer parameter value. Misclassification incurs squared-error loss at least Δ2/4, and the sum of the two error probabilities is at least one minus the total variation distance. Averaging the two risk functions proves the stated form.
Let d=min(x0​,1−x0​)>0, m0​=0, and use the two-point lower bound with a triangular bump

minf​m∈ΘL​sup​Em​[(m(x0​)−m(x0​))2]≥641​(nLσ2​)2/3.​

This universal constant is valid for n≥N:=⌈max{1,σ2/(8L2d3),22​L/σ}⌉.

For n<N, use the constant mean functions m0​=0 and m1​=σ/n​. They have Kullback-Leibler divergence 1/2, so Pinsker's inequality and the Le Cam two-point lemma give minimax risk at least σ2/(16n). Combining the two ranges gives the explicit all-n choice

(25)

For independent and identically distributed random variables with distribution function F, let Mn​=max1≤i≤n​Xi​ be the sample maximum. In extreme value theory, F belongs to the maximum domain of attraction D(G) of a nondegenerate distribution function G when there exist an​>0 and bn​∈R such that
P(an​Mn​−bn​​≤x)=F(an​x+bn​)n⟶G(x)​
(26)
at every continuity point of G. Thus the normalized sample maxima have convergence in distribution to G.
A positive measurable function q has regular variation at infinity with index ρ, written q∈RVρ​, if
q(t)q(tu)​⟶uρ(t→∞),u>0.​
(27)
The case ρ=0 defines a slowly varying function; equivalently a regularly varying function is q(t)=tρℓ(t) with ℓ a slowly varying function.
Write F=1−F for the survival function and xF​=sup{x:F(x)<1} for the right endpoint of a distribution. For α>0, use the following standard extreme value theory normalization conventions:
Φα​(x)={exp(−x−α),0,​x>0,x≤0,​Ψα​(x)={exp(−(−x)α),1,​x<0,x≥0,​Λ(x)=exp(−e−x).
(28)
They are respectively the Fréchet distribution, the negative Weibull distribution, and the Gumbel distribution for maxima. The negative Weibull distribution is supported to the left of its finite endpoint; it is not the usual positive Weibull distribution.
The necessary and sufficient maximum domain of attraction criteria are:
  • For the Fréchet distribution,
    F∈D(Φα​)⟺xF​=∞ and F∈RV−α​.​
    (29)
    Equivalently F(tu)/F(t)→u−α for every u>0.
  • For the negative Weibull distribution,
    F∈D(Ψα​)⟺xF​<∞ and t↦F(xF​−1/t)∈RV−α​.​
    (30)
    Equivalently F(xF​−su)/F(xF​−s)→uα as s↓0, for every u>0.
  • For the Gumbel distribution,
    F∈D(Λ)⟺∃a(t)>0:F(t)F(t+a(t)x)​⟶e−x(t↑xF​),x∈R.​
    (31)
    Here a is a Gumbel auxiliary function, and xF​ can be finite or infinite. For each fixed x, the shifted argument lies below xF​ eventually. This exponential tail-ratio condition, rather than regular variation with a fixed finite index, characterizes this case.
For the corresponding sufficient hazard function conditions, assume F is absolutely continuous near xF​, its survival function tends to zero there, and its probability density function f is positive there. Write η(t)=f(t)/F(t). The Von Mises conditions for extreme values give
xF​=∞,tη(t)→αxF​<∞,(xF​−t)η(t)→αdtd​(η(t)1​)→0​⟹F∈D(Φα​),⟹F∈D(Ψα​),⟹F∈D(Λ).​​
(32)
The last condition additionally assumes that the reciprocal hazard function is continuously differentiable near the endpoint. It concerns the derivative of the reciprocal hazard function, not the derivative of the hazard function itself. Equivalently, when f is differentiable, it is F(t)f′(t)/f(t)2→−1.
To see the connection, taking an integral of the hazard function gives
logF(t)F(v)​=−∫tv​η(u)du.
(33)
The first two limits make the logarithmic tail ratios over multiplicative or endpoint-distance intervals tend to −αlogu or αlogu, giving the required regular variation. For the Gumbel distribution, set a(t)=1/η(t). The derivative condition makes a(t+a(t)v)/a(t)→1 uniformly on bounded v-intervals, so
logF(t)F(t+a(t)x)​=−∫0x​a(t+a(t)v)a(t)​dv⟶−x.
(34)
The endpoint assumptions ensure these shifts are admissible: at an infinite endpoint a(t)/t→0, and at a finite endpoint a(t)/(xF​−t)→0. These are sufficient smooth-tail conditions; the tail-ratio characterizations above are necessary and sufficient without differentiability assumptions. For a source using the same reciprocal-hazard convention, see Smith and Weissman's university lecture notes, equations (2.18)–(2.24).
For the Gamma distribution with integer shape m and rate one, let Fm​(t)=∫t∞​um−1e−udu/(m−1)!. The base case is F1​(t)=e−t, and integration by parts gives
Fm​(t)=(m−1)!tm−1e−t​+Fm−1​(t).
(35)
Induction proves
1−Fm​(t)=e−tj=0∑m−1​j!tj​,t≥0.​
(36)
This survival function has the asymptotic form Fm​(t)∼e−ttm−1/(m−1)!; for m=1 the relation is exact. Put tn​=βn​+x. For every fixed real x, tn​→∞ and tn​/logn→1. The proposed centering satisfies
ne−βn​=(logn)m−1(m−1)!​,
(37)
so
nFm​(βn​+x)=e−x(logn)m−1(m−1)!​∑j=0m−1​j!(βn​+x)j​⟶e−x.
(38)
Only the highest-degree term contributes to the limit, with the same reasoning covering m=1. The Taylor expansion log(1−u)=−u+O(u2) now gives
nlogFm​(βn​+x)⟶−e−x,
(39)
because nFm​(βn​+x)2→0. Therefore the Gumbel limit for gamma maxima is
Fm​(βn​+x)n⟶exp(−e−x),x∈R.​
(40)
Equivalently, Mn​−βn​ has convergence in distribution to the standard Gumbel distribution, with scale an​=1.

Let S=∑i​Yi​ and VH​=∑i​(bi​−ai​)2. To prove the Hoeffding lemma, take a centered random variable Y∈[a,b] and its cumulant-generating function ψ(t)=logEetY. Under exponential tilting, ψ′′(t) is the variance of Y. The Popoviciu inequality on variances gives
ψ′′(t)≤4(b−a)2​,
(41)
since under any such tilt the support remains [a,b], and Var(Y)≤E(Y−(a+b)/2)2≤(b−a)2/4. Because ψ(0)=ψ′(0)=0, integrating twice gives ψ(t)≤t2(b−a)2/8 for t≥0; applying the same argument to −Y handles the opposite tail.
The independence of the Yi​ gives EetS≤exp(t2VH​/8). The Markov inequality, in the Chernoff bound argument, yields
P(S>ϵ)≤inft>0​exp(−tϵ+8t2VH​​)=exp(−VH​2ϵ2​),
(42)
where the minimum is at t=4ϵ/VH​ when VH​>0. Apply the same argument to −S and add the bounds to obtain the Hoeffding inequality:
P(∣S∣>ϵ)≤2exp(−∑i​(bi​−ai​)22ϵ2​).​
(43)
If VH​=0, every centered Yi​ is identically zero and the tail probability is zero; the displayed exponential is interpreted by its zero-variance limit.
For the Bennett inequality, write v=nσ2, and first assume M>0 and v>0. For j≥2, boundedness and centering give
EYij​≤E∣Yi​∣j≤Mj−2EYi2​≤σ2Mj−2.
(44)
For t≥0, expanding the moment-generating function and using the permitted interchange of sum and expected value,
EetYi​​=1+j=2∑∞​j!tjEYij​​≤1+M2σ2​(etM−1−tM)≤exp(M2σ2​(etM−1−tM)).​
(45)
Negative odd moments cause no difficulty because the estimate is an upper bound term by term. The independence of the Yi​ and the Chernoff bound then give
P(S>ϵ)≤inft>0​exp[−tϵ+M2v​(etM−1−tM)].
(46)
The derivative vanishes at t∗​=M−1log(1+Mϵ/v), and substitution gives −(v/M2)ϕ(Mϵ/v) with the Bennett rate function ϕ(u)=(1+u)log(1+u)−u. The same absolute bounds apply to −Yi​, so
P(∣S∣>ϵ)≤2exp[−M2nσ2​ϕ(nσ2Mϵ​)].​
(47)
If σ2=0, all centered Yi​ are zero almost surely; if M=0, the same is immediate. Handle those cases directly rather than divide by zero. Both derived concentration inequalities also hold with ≥ϵ, by the same Markov inequality argument or by letting a smaller positive threshold increase to ϵ.
For the binomial distribution, write X=∑i=1n​Zi​ with independent and identically distributed random variables Zi​ having the Bernoulli distribution with parameter p=pn​. Then Yi​=Zi​−p has range [−p,1−p], width one, and variance p(1−p). For large n, p<1/2, so M=1−p and v=np(1−p). Set ϵ=Cv​.
The Hoeffding inequality gives
Hn​=2exp[−2C2p(1−p)]⟶2.
(48)
After truncation at one, this is asymptotically the trivial probability bound. The Bennett inequality instead gives
Bn​=2exp[−(1−p)2v​ϕ(v​C(1−p)​)].
(49)
Since v→∞ and ϕ(u)=u2/2+O(u3) by a Taylor expansion,
(1−p)2v​ϕ(v​C(1−p)​)=2C2​+O(v−1/2)⟶2C2​.
(50)
Thus the Bennett bounds for sparse binomial fluctuations yield
Bn​⟶2e−C2/2,Hn​⟶2.​
(51)
The Bennett inequality supplies the smaller raw exponential bound for every fixed C>0 and all sufficiently large n. If C≤2log2​, clipping probability upper bounds at one makes both eventually trivial; for C>2log2​, the Bennett inequality retains a nontrivial limiting bound. This distinction avoids claiming a probability improvement after clipping when both raw bounds exceed one.
