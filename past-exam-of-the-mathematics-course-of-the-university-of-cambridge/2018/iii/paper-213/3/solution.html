Use the slotted ALOHA model with one outstanding packet per active station and a collision channel: a slot with exactly one attempt succeeds, whereas an idle slot or a slot with two or more attempts delivers nothing. Let Yt​ be independent Poisson random variables of mean ν, independent also of the transmission choices. Arrivals join after the current slot's attempts. Given (St​,Nt​)=(S,N), the attempt count has a binomial distribution with parameters N and 1/S. Consequently, for N≥1,
p0​(N,S)=(1−1/S)N,p1​(N,S)=SN​(1−1/S)N−1,p∗​=1−p0​−p1​.
(24)
For N=0, set p0​=1 and p1​=0. The equation Nt+1​=Nt​+Yt​−1{Zt​=1}​ adds arrivals and removes precisely one packet following a successful slot. The estimate St​≥1 controls the common transmission probability and updates using the idle, success, or collision feedback. The conditional distribution of the next state depends only on (St​,Nt​), so this pair is a Markov chain.
Away from the reflecting floor in S, its one-step conditional expectations are
E[ΔN∣S,N]=ν−p1​,E[ΔS∣S,N]=ap0​+bp1​+cp∗​.
(25)
When N and S are large with N/S≈κ, the Poisson limit theorem gives p0​≈e−κ and p1​≈κe−κ. Treating these approximate drifts as derivatives motivates the fluid approximation of slotted ALOHA:
s˙=(a−c)e−κ+(b−c)κe−κ+c,n˙=ν−κe−κ,κ=n/s.​
(26)
The floor at S=1 is negligible in this interior scaling. This calculation motivates the ordinary differential equations; it is not a proof of a stochastic fluid limit.
For (a,b,c)=(2−e,0,1), put
g(κ)=1−(e−1+κ)e−κ,h(κ)=ν−κe−κ.
(27)
Then g′(κ)=(e−2+κ)e−κ>0 and g(1)=0. On the domain s>0, introduce time τ by dτ/dt=1/s. Differentiating κ=n/s gives
dτdκ​=ν−r(κ),dτds​=sg(κ),dτdt​=s,
(28)
where r(κ)=κ[1−(e−2+κ)e−κ].
The function r is strictly increasing from zero to infinity, and r(1)=e−1. To check the monotonicity explicitly,
r′(κ)=1−e−κ[e−2+(4−e)κ−κ2]>0.
(29)
Indeed, with d=3−e∈(0,1),
1+κ−[e−2+(4−e)κ−κ2]=κ2−dκ+d>0,
(30)
because its discriminant is d2−4d<0; and eκ≥1+κ. Thus the scalar ordinary differential equation for κ points toward the unique value κ∗​ satisfying r(κ∗​)=ν. Every trajectory with s(0)>0, n(0)≥0 has bounded κ and κ(τ)→κ∗​. Positivity of s follows from s(τ)=s(0)exp(∫0τ​g(κ(u))du).
If 0≤ν<e−1, then κ∗​<1 and g(κ∗​)<0. Thus s(τ) decays exponentially for large τ, and
T=∫0∞​s(τ)dτ<∞,(s(t),n(t))⟶(0,0)as t↑T.​
(31)
This is finite-time draining of a fluid model. The unmodified equations have n/s undefined at the origin, so the statement that trajectories converge to the origin needs this endpoint interpretation; continuing with an absorbing zero trajectory is an additional fluid-model convention, not a solution of the displayed equations at the origin.
If ν>e−1, then κ∗​>1 and g(κ∗​)>0. Now s(τ) grows exponentially, physical time tends to infinity, and
ts(t)​→g(κ∗​),tn(t)​→κ∗​g(κ∗​)>0.​
(32)
In particular the trajectory escapes to infinity. Even without the ratio analysis, n˙≥ν−e−1>0, since maxκ≥0​κe−κ=e−1.
For the actual Markov chain, it is essential not to replace the finite-N success probability by e−1: a single backlogged packet can succeed with probability one. Instead, for N≥2, maximizing over S≥1 gives the ALOHA throughput bound
p1​(N,S)≤(1−N1​)N−1⟶e−1.
(33)
If ν>e−1, choose K0​ and r0​<ν so that p1​(N,S)≤r0​ for all N>K0​, uniformly in S. For sufficiently small θ>0,
qθ​:=exp(ν(e−θ−1))[1+r0​(eθ−1)]<1,
(34)
because its derivative at zero is r0​−ν<0. Independence of arrivals and attempts gives
E[e−θΔN∣S,N]=exp(ν(e−θ−1))[1+p1​(N,S)(eθ−1)]≤qθ​
(35)
whenever N>K0​. For any integer K≥K0​, stop at the stopping time σK​=inf{t:Nt​≤K}. The stopped process e−θNt∧σK​​ is a nonnegative supermartingale. The optional stopping theorem, first at bounded times and then by a limit, yields the exponential-supermartingale escape bound
PS,N​(σK​<∞)≤e−θ(N−K)(N>K).​
(36)
From every state with N≤K, a Poisson distribution arrival burst has a uniformly positive probability of sending the next backlog to at least K+m, for any fixed m>0. From there the probability of never revisiting N≤K is at least 1−e−θm. By the Markov property at successive visits, the probability of infinitely many visits to this strip is zero: each visit has a uniform positive chance of being the last. This holds for every integer K≥K0​, so
Nt​→∞almost surely when ν>e−1.​
(37)
Therefore the Markov chain is transient for every finite choice of a,b,c. The proof is uniform in S and does not infer stochastic transience merely from the approximate ordinary differential equations.
