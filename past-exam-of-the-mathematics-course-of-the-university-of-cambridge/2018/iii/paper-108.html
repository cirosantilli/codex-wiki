Use the probability-system convention μ(X)=1. The Birkhoff ergodic theorem, also called the pointwise ergodic theorem, states that for a measure-preserving system and f∈L1(μ),
AN​f(x)=N1​∑n=0N−1​f(Tnx)⟶Eμ​[f∣I](x)almost everywhere,
(1)
where I is the invariant sigma-algebra. The limit is integrable and has the same integral as f. On a probability space the convergence also holds in L1, as in the allowed mean ergodic theorem. If T is an ergodic transformation, I is trivial modulo null sets, giving
AN​f(x)⟶∫X​fdμalmost everywhere.​
(2)
The integer multiplication map on the circle preserves Lebesgue measure: for any integrable g on [0,1),
∫01​g(Kxmod1)dx=∑j=0K−1​∫j/K(j+1)/K​g(Kx−j)dx=∫01​g(y)dy.
(3)
To prove the ergodicity of integer multiplication on the circle, suppose u∈L2(m) satisfies u∘TK​=u. Let cj​ be its Fourier coefficients in the Fourier basis ej​(x)=e2πijx. Since ej​∘TK​=eKj​, the Fourier coefficients of u∘TK​ at index j are zero if K does not divide j, and are cj/K​ otherwise. This identity holds for all L2 functions by approximation with trigonometric polynomials and the isometry u↦u∘TK​. Invariance gives
cj​={cj/K​,0,​K∣j,K∤j.​
(4)
Every nonzero integer j can be divided by K only finitely often. Thus cj​=0 for every j=0, and completeness of the Fourier basis makes u constant almost everywhere. Applying this to the indicator function of an invariant set gives measure zero or one, so
TK​ is ergodic for m(K≥2).​
(5)
A normal number in base K has every word w=(w1​,…,wr​) of r base-K digits occurring with limiting overlapping frequency K−r. Use the expansion that is not eventually equal to K−1 when there are two expansions. The word w corresponds to the half-open interval
Iw​=[Krj(w)​,Krj(w)+1​),j(w)=∑i=1r​wi​Kr−i.
(6)
A word starting at position n+1 occurs exactly when TKn​x∈Iw​. The Birkhoff ergodic theorem, applied to 1Iw​​, gives frequency m(Iw​)=K−r almost everywhere. There are countably many pairs (K,w), so their full-measure sets have a full-measure intersection. In particular,
m{x∈[0,1):x is normal in every integer base K≥2}=1.​
(7)
These are absolutely normal numbers, so existence follows as well. This interval description also proves normality and equidistribution under integer multiplication: the base-K intervals form arbitrarily fine grids, so their frequencies imply the correct frequency for every interval by approximation from inside and outside.
For the growth assertion, put g=∣f∣. For every ε>0, the Tonelli theorem gives the useful summability bound
∑n=1∞​μ(g>εn)=∫X​∑n=1∞​1{g(x)>εn}​dμ(x)≤ε∥f∥1​​.
(8)
Since T is a measure-preserving transformation, μ(g∘Tn>εn)=μ(g>εn). The first Borel-Cantelli lemma shows that g(Tnx)>εn occurs only finitely often almost everywhere. Intersecting the resulting full-measure sets for ε=1,1/2,1/3,… proves the linear growth bound for integrable observables, f(Tnx)/n→0. Multiplication by n1−a≤1 then gives
naf(Tnx)​⟶0almost everywhere, for every a≥1.​
(9)
The threshold is sharp. For 0<a<1, choose p with 1<p<1/a, and take the Bernoulli shift on X=(0,1)Z with the product measure of independent uniform coordinates. The left shift preserves that measure because it preserves the probability of every finite-coordinate event. Define f(x)=x0−1/p​; it is integrable because
∫X​fdμ=∫01​u−1/pdu=p−1p​<∞.
(10)
The variables f(Tnx)=xn−1/p​ are independent. For any fixed M≥1,
μ{f(Tnx)>Mna}=M−pn−ap,ap<1.
(11)
The probability sum diverges, so the second Borel-Cantelli lemma makes these events occur infinitely often almost surely. Intersecting over positive integer M even yields limsupn​f(Tnx)/na=∞. For a≤0, the constant observable f=1 already fails to give limit zero. Thus the sharpness of the linear growth bound for integrable observables gives
There is no a<1 for which the asserted limit holds in every system for every f∈L1.​
(12)

For an increasing sequence (nk​) of nonnegative integers, full-density sequence means that its range S has natural density one:
N∣S∩{0,…,N−1}∣​⟶1.
(13)
For a sequence (zn​) in a metric space, convergence in density of a sequence to z means that, for every ε>0,
N1​​{0≤n<N:d(zn​,z)≥ε}​⟶0.
(14)
In particular, a bounded nonnegative scalar sequence converging to zero in density has averages tending to zero: the average is at most ε plus its bound times the proportion of indices above ε.
Here is a Hilbert space form of the Van der Corput lemma, including the density version. Suppose ∥un​∥≤M, and put
ch​=limsupN→∞​​N1​∑n=0N−h−1​⟨un+h​,un​⟩​(h≥1).
(15)
Then
H1​h=1∑H​ch​⟶0⟹​N1​n=0∑N−1​un​​⟶0.​
(16)
Since 0≤ch​≤M2, it suffices for (ch​) to converge to zero in density. A frequently used special case is that the correlation average tends to zero for every fixed h≥1.
To prove the lemma, extend the finite list u0​,…,uN−1​ by zero outside this range, and write SN​=∑n=0N−1​un​. For 1≤H≤N,
HSN​=∑n=−H+1N−1​∑r=0H−1​un+r​.
(17)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for this sum, followed by expansion of the squared Hilbert space norm, gives
H2∥SN​∥2≤(N+H−1)[H∑n=0N−1​∥un​∥2+2∑h=1H−1​(H−h)Re∑n=0N−h−1​⟨un+h​,un​⟩].
(18)
The coefficient H−h counts the pairs of shifts separated by h. Dividing by H2N2, bounding real parts by absolute values, and first taking the limit superior in N for fixed H yields
limsupN→∞​​NSN​​​2≤HM2​+H2​∑h=1H−1​(1−Hh​)ch​.
(19)
The right side tends to zero as H→∞ under the stated hypothesis. This proves both the lemma and its density consequence.
For the polynomial application, fix a nonzero integer m and let un​=e2πimP(n)∈C. For each fixed h≥1, the correlation phase is linear:
P(n+h)−P(n)=2ahn+ah2+bh.
(20)
Thus, with qh​=e2πi(2mah),
N1​∑n=0N−h−1​un+h​un​​=e2πim(ah2+bh)N(1−qh​)1−qhN−h​​.
(21)
The coefficient 2mah is irrational, so qh​=1. This geometric series is bounded in modulus by 2/(N∣1−qh​∣) and tends to zero. All ch​ vanish, so the Van der Corput lemma gives
N1​n=0∑N−1​e2πim(an2+bn+c)⟶0(m∈Z∖{0}).​
(22)
The Weyl criterion now shows that the fractional parts form an equidistributed sequence. For completeness, its sufficiency here follows directly: the displayed limits give the correct average for every trigonometric polynomial, including the constant term. The Stone-Weierstrass theorem extends this to every continuous function by uniform approximation. Approximating an interval's indicator function above and below by continuous functions gives its length as the limiting frequency. Hence
(an2+bn+c)mod1 is equidistributed in R/Z.​
(23)
This proves the equidistribution of a quadratic polynomial with irrational leading coefficient using the required correlation argument.

Let ξ0n−1​=⋁j=0n−1​T−jξ denote the join of measurable partitions recording the first n observations, and set Hn​=Hμ​(ξ0n−1​), with H0​=0. The entropy of a countable measurable partition is Hμ​(ξ)=−∑A∈ξ​μ(A)logμ(A), using natural logarithms and 0log0=0. Null atoms can be discarded. Throughout, μ is a probability measure.
The stronger property needed for monotonicity of normalized block entropy is that its increments decrease. Define
ck​=Hμ​(ξ​⋁j=1k​T−jξ),c0​=Hμ​(ξ).
(24)
Since conditioning reduces entropy, ck+1​≤ck​. The conditional entropy chain rule and measure preservation give
Hk+1​=Hμ​(⋁j=1k​T−jξ)+ck​=Hk​+ck​,Hn​=∑k=0n−1​ck​.
(25)
It follows that cn​≤Hn​/n, and consequently
n+1Hn+1​​=n+1Hn​+cn​​≤nHn​​.​
(26)
This uses stationarity as well as the entropy chain rule; subadditivity alone would not establish monotonicity of every successive ratio.
The entropy rate of a measurable partition and the Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy are, respectively,
hμ​(T,ξ)=n→∞lim​n1​Hμ​(ξ0n−1​)=n≥1inf​nHn​​,hμ​(T)=η finite measurable partitionsup​hμ​(T,η).​
(27)
One may equivalently take the supremum over countable finite-entropy measurable partitions.
The Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem states that for a countable measurable partition with Hμ​(ξ)<∞, the normalized information
n1​Iμ​(ξ0n−1​)(x)=−n1​logμ(ξ0n−1​(x))
(28)
converges almost everywhere and in L1 to a T invariant function hξ​(x) with integral hμ​(T,ξ). Here ξ0n−1​(x) is the atom containing x. If T is an ergodic transformation, then hξ​(x)=hμ​(T,ξ) almost everywhere. We prove the general form, including an explicit formula for its limit.
Let Fk​ be the sigma-algebra generated by ⋁j=1k​T−jξ, let F0​ be trivial, and put F∞​=⋁j=1∞​T−jσ(ξ). For every atom A, the probabilities
pkA​=Eμ​[1A​∣Fk​]
(29)
form a bounded conditional-expectation martingale. The martingale convergence theorem gives pkA​→p∞A​=Eμ​[1A​∣F∞​] almost everywhere and in L1. Each of these probabilities is positive almost everywhere on A: for example, integrating 1A​ over the F∞​ measurable set {p∞A​=0} gives zero. Countability of ξ lets us choose a common full-measure set for every atom.
The conditional information functions
fk​=Iμ​(ξ∣Fk​)=−∑A∈ξ​1A​logpkA​
(30)
therefore converge almost everywhere to f∞​=Iμ​(ξ∣F∞​). We need an integrable bound on this sequence; convergence of the probabilities alone would not supply one after taking logarithms. The allowed maximal inequality for conditional information functions gives, for F=supk≥0​fk​,
μ(A∩{F>t})≤e−t(A∈ξ, t≥0).
(31)
Combining this with the bound by μ(A) and the tail integral formula for moments yields
∫Fdμ≤∑A∈ξ​∫0∞​min{μ(A),e−t}dt=∑A∈ξ​μ(A)(1−logμ(A))=1+Hμ​(ξ)<∞.
(32)
Thus 0≤f∞​≤F, and the dominated convergence theorem gives fk​→f∞​ in L1. In particular,
∫f∞​dμ=limk​ck​=hμ​(T,ξ),
(33)
because Hn​/n is the average of the decreasing sequence (ck​).
The information chain rule, applied from the final observation backwards, gives the exact identity
Iμ​(ξ0n−1​)(x)=∑j=0n−1​fn−1−j​(Tjx).
(34)
Indeed, the symbol at time j is conditioned on times j+1,…,n−1; pulling that conditional probability back by Tj gives fn−1−j​∘Tj. Measure preservation is sufficient for this pullback identity.
We now prove the required triangular ergodic averaging lemma in this instance. Put gM​=supk≥M​∣fk​−f∞​∣. Then gM​↓0 almost everywhere, gM​≤2F, and ∥gM​∥1​→0. Split the difference between the displayed triangular sum and ∑j=0n−1​f∞​(Tjx). Terms whose index is at least M are bounded by ∑j=0n−1​gM​(Tjx). The remaining terms are at most
∑k=0M−1​∣fk​−f∞​∣(Tn−1−kx).
(35)
After division by n, each of these finitely many end terms tends to zero almost everywhere by the linear growth bound for integrable observables proved in Question 1. Applying the Birkhoff ergodic theorem to gM​, for every fixed M, therefore gives
limsupn→∞​​n1​∑j=0n−1​(fn−1−j​−f∞​)(Tjx)​≤Eμ​[gM​∣I](x).
(36)
These conditional expectations decrease to zero almost everywhere: they decrease, and their integrals ∫gM​dμ tend to zero. Letting M→∞, then applying the Birkhoff ergodic theorem to f∞​, proves
−n1​logμ(ξ0n−1​(x))⟶hξ​(x)=Eμ​[f∞​∣I](x)almost everywhere.​
(37)
For L1 convergence, measure preservation gives the direct estimate
​n1​∑j=0n−1​(fn−1−j​−f∞​)∘Tj​1​≤n1​∑k=0n−1​∥fk​−f∞​∥1​⟶0.
(38)
Combine this with L1 convergence of the ergodic averages of f∞​. Finally, ∫hξ​dμ=∫f∞​dμ=hμ​(T,ξ). In the ergodic case the invariant sigma-algebra is trivial, completing the familiar form of the theorem:
−n1​logμ(ξ0n−1​(x))⟶hμ​(T,ξ)almost everywhere and in L1.​
(39)

The Rudolph measure rigidity theorem has an essential ergodicity hypothesis. If a Borel probability measure μ on the circle group is invariant under both T2​ and T3​, is ergodic for the semigroup generated jointly by these maps, and either map has positive Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy, then
μ=m.​
(40)
Equivalently, a jointly ergodic common invariant measure other than Lebesgue measure has zero entropy for both maps. Joint ergodicity means that every set invariant modulo μ under both maps has measure zero or one. Positive entropy without this hypothesis is insufficient: 21​m+21​δ0​, with δ0​ a Dirac measure, is a common invariant measure of positive entropy and is not m.
The Host equidistribution theorem states that if p,q≥2 are relatively prime integers and ν is invariant and ergodic under Tp​, with hν​(Tp​)>0, then for ν-almost every x the sequence (Tqn​x) is an equidistributed sequence for Lebesgue measure. Explicitly, for every continuous g on the circle,
N1​n=0∑N−1​g(Tqn​x)⟶∫gdmfor ν-almost every x.​
(41)
The non-ergodic form assumes Tp​ invariance and positive entropy for almost every component in the ergodic decomposition ν=∫νω​dλ(ω). Applying the ergodic theorem of Host on each such component gives the same almost-everywhere conclusion for ν. More generally, its conclusion holds on the part supported on positive-entropy components. A positive value of hν​(Tp​) alone does not eliminate zero-entropy components.
To deduce the joint version of the Rudolph measure rigidity theorem, suppose hμ​(T2​)>0; if only T3​ has positive entropy, interchange the roles. Write the T2​ ergodic decomposition as μ=∫νdτ(ν). Since T3​ commutes with T2​, its pushforward measure sends a T2​ ergodic component ν to a T2​ ergodic component (T3​)∗​ν. On each component, T3​ is a factor of a measure-preserving system with fibres of size at most three. We use the standard entropy preservation under a finite-to-one factor:
h(T3​)∗​ν​(T2​)=hν​(T2​).
(42)
The reason for this standard entropy fact is that, conditional on a complete factor point, every finite orbit name has at most three possibilities; its conditional entropy is bounded by log3, and division by the orbit length gives zero relative entropy.
The component at T3​x is (T3​)∗​νx​ almost everywhere; this follows from commutation and the componentwise ergodic averages. The component entropy function r(x)=hνx​​(T2​) is therefore invariant under both T2​ and T3​. Joint ergodicity makes it constant almost everywhere, and affinity of entropy under ergodic decomposition identifies the constant as hμ​(T2​)>0. Thus almost every T2​ component has positive entropy, exactly the condition required in the non-ergodic Host equidistribution theorem. It follows that μ-almost every point equidistributes for m under T3​.
For any continuous g, invariance under T3​ and the dominated convergence theorem now give
∫gdμ=∫N1​∑n=0N−1​g(T3n​x)dμ(x)⟶∫gdm.
(43)
Continuous functions determine Borel probability measures on the circle, so μ=m, proving the deduction.
For the normal-number example, let ω1​,ω2​,… be independent fair binary digits and define
π(ω)=∑j=1∞​3j2ωj​​,ν=π∗​((21​,21​)N).
(44)
This Cantor Bernoulli measure is supported on the middle-third Cantor set C. If S is the Bernoulli shift, then T3​∘π=π∘S on the circle. Consequently ν is T3​ invariant and ergodic: a T3​ invariant event pulls back to an S invariant event, which has probability zero or one.
Take the ternary digit measurable partition η={[0,1/3),[1/3,2/3),[2/3,1)}. Its block partition of length n has, up to null endpoints, 2n positive-measure atoms under ν, each of measure 2−n, and all other atoms have measure zero. Hence
Hν​(η0n−1​)=nlog2,hν​(T3​)≥hν​(T3​,η)=log2>0.
(45)
Apply the Host equidistribution theorem with p=3, q=2. For ν-almost every x∈C, the sequence (T2n​x) equidistributes for Lebesgue measure, so x is a normal number in base 2 by normality and equidistribution under integer multiplication.
The ternary expansion of ν-almost every such x contains only 0 and 2, so the frequency of digit 1 is zero rather than 1/3. The ambiguous ternary endpoints form a countable ν null set and can be removed. Therefore x is not a normal number in base 3. We have proved the stronger almost-everywhere existence statement
ν{x∈C∩[0,1):x is normal in base 2 and not normal in base 3}=1.​
(46)
