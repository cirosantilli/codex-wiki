Write α=1−1/r. The Erdős-Stone theorem states that, for a fixed graph F with chromatic number χ(F)=r+1≥2,
ex(n,F)=(1−r1​+o(1))(2n​).​
(1)
Here ex is the extremal number, and copies are subgraphs, not necessarily induced subgraphs. We write Ka​(b) for the balanced complete multipartite blow-up with a classes of b vertices each. All asymptotic errors below concern n→∞ with the forbidden graphs fixed.
The stability subgraph. We prove the high-minimum-degree multipartite stability subgraph assertion directly from the Erdős-Stone theorem. For r=1, the edgeless spanning subgraph suffices. Suppose r≥2.
First repeatedly remove a vertex whose degree of a vertex is less than (α−εn​) times the current order, where εn​→0 sufficiently slowly. The Erdős-Stone theorem gives, uniformly for every Kr+1​(t)-free subgraph J of order at most n,
e(J)≤2α​∣J∣2+ηn​n2,ηn​→0.
(2)
Indeed, apply the asymptotic bound at large orders; the contribution from bounded or sufficiently small orders is negligible relative to n2. If m vertices survive, the removed edges number at most
(α−εn​)∑j=m+1n​j=2α−εn​​(n2−m2)+O(n).
(3)
Comparing with e(Gn​)=αn2/2+o(n2) shows that εn​(n2−m2)=o(εn​n2), provided εn​ dominates ηn​, the initial relative error, and 1/n. Consequently m=n−o(n). The surviving induced subgraph G′ satisfies δ(G′)≥(α−o(1))m and e(G′)=αm2/2+o(m2).
By the Erdős-Stone theorem, G′ contains Kr​(L) for some L=Ln​→∞ as slowly as necessary. This follows because α exceeds the extremal number density for each fixed Kr​(L) by the positive gap 1/(r(r−1)). Choose L so slowly that rL=o(m) and (tL​)r=o(m), and denote its root classes by A1​,…,Ar​.
An outside vertex having at least t neighbours of a vertex in every Ai​ determines one choice of a t-subset in each Ai​. At most t−1 outside vertices can determine any fixed choice, since otherwise these vertices and the chosen root sets form Kr+1​(t). Thus at most (t−1)(tL​)r=o(m) outside vertices have this property. Exclude those vertices and the root classes; put each remaining vertex in a class Ci​ for which it has fewer than t neighbours of a vertex in Ai​.
Write δ(G′)≥(α−γn​)m, with γn​→0. Counting missing incidences with Ai​ gives
(L−t+1)∣Ci​∣≤∑u∈Ai​​(m−d(u))≤L(1/r+γn​)m.
(4)
Since ∑i​∣Ci​∣=m−o(m), it follows that ∣Ci​∣=m/r+o(m) for every i. The total missing incidences from all root vertices to assigned vertices are at most (1+rγn​)Lm. At least (L−t+1)(m−o(m)) of them are incidences with a vertex's own assigned root class. Hence only o(Lm) missing incidences go to other root classes.
Choose ρn​→0 slowly and discard the o(m) assigned vertices with more than ρn​L missing incidences to other root classes. Each remaining vertex of Ci​ is adjacent to all but at most ρn​L vertices of every Aj​, j=i. If its class contained a complete bipartite graph Kt,t​, the 2t vertices of this copy would have at least L−2tρn​L≥t common neighbours of a vertex in each other root class. These sets extend the copy to Kr+1​(t), a contradiction. By the bipartite case of the Erdős-Stone theorem, the number of internal edges in each surviving class is o(m2).
The surviving graph still has αm2/2+o(m2) edges, whereas the total possible cross-class edges, with these balanced classes, is αm2/2+o(m2). Thus only o(m2) cross-class edges are missing. Discard the o(m) vertices missing more than σn​m cross-class edges, with σn​→0 slowly, and delete all internal edges. The resulting r-partite subgraph H has classes Hi​ of size n/r+o(n) and
∣H∣=n−o(n),δ(H)=(1−1/r+o(1))n.​
(5)
For the lower bound, every surviving vertex misses only o(n) vertices outside its class; the upper bound follows from the class sizes. This also records the uniform cross-class error that we need next.
Minimum degree in an extremal graph. Fix F with χ(F)=r+1. Choose t large enough that F embeds in Kr+1​(t). An extremal F-free graph G is therefore Kr+1​(t)-free, and its edge count has the required asymptotics by the Erdős-Stone theorem. For r≥2 take H as above.
For any vertex v, remove v and introduce a new vertex v∗ whose vertex neighbourhood consists precisely of the vertices in H2​∪⋯∪Hr​ other than v. The new degree of a vertex is αn−o(n). This new graph remains F-free. Indeed, a new copy of F must use v∗, and all its neighbours of a vertex in that copy lie in the other classes of H. There are only boundedly many of them. Each misses only o(n) vertices in H1​, so an unused vertex w∈H1​ is adjacent to all of them. Replacing v∗ by w would give F in G−v, which is impossible.
Extremality now forces dG​(v)≥αn−o(n), uniformly in v. The upper bound for the minimum degree of a graph follows from δ(G)≤2e(G)/n. For r=1, e(G)=o(n2) already gives 0≤δ(G)=o(n). Thus
δ(G)=(1−1/r+o(1))n.​
(6)
This is the minimum degree of an extremal forbidden-subgraph graph principle.
The exact extremal graph for disjoint cliques. Define
Js​(n)=Ks−1​+Tr​(n−s+1),bs​(n)=(2s−1​)+(s−1)(n−s+1)+e(Tr​(n−s+1)).
(7)
The plus sign denotes the join of graphs. The Turán graph contains no Kr+1​, so every such clique in Js​(n) uses a vertex of Ks−1​. Therefore Js​(n) contains no s vertex-disjoint such cliques, and the extremal number is at least bs​(n).
Induct on s, with s=1 supplied by the Turan theorem, including its equality characterization. Let s≥2 and let G be extremal for sKr+1​. The preceding results give δ(G)≥αn−o(n) and the r-partite subgraph H. Keep its classes Hi​ fixed and assign each of the remaining o(n) vertices to a class in which it has fewest neighbours of a vertex in H. Write the resulting classes as Ci​; all have size n/r+o(n). Choose a positive τn​→0 so slowly that τn​n dominates the outside count, every cross-class error in H, and every error in the minimum degree of a graph bound.
Suppose some vertex x∈Ci​ has at least τn​n internal neighbours of a vertex. If x∈/H, the assignment rule shows that x has at least τn​n−o(n) neighbours of a vertex in every Hj​. If x∈Hi​, it has this many in Hi​ and n/r−o(n) in the other Hj​. If G−x contained s−1 disjoint Kr+1​, we could choose one vertex in each N(x)∩Hj​, successively avoiding those copies and the o(n) missing cross-neighbours of the already chosen vertices. This produces a further Kr+1​ through x. For r=1, this just chooses an unused neighbour of x. Hence G−x is (s−1)Kr+1​-free, and induction gives
e(G)≤bs−1​(n−1)+(n−1)=bs​(n).
(8)
Since the candidate attains bs​(n), equality holds throughout: x is a universal vertex and G−x is the inductively unique extremal graph. Thus G is isomorphic to Js​(n).
It remains to show that this case must occur. Otherwise all internal vertex degrees are less than τn​n=o(n). Together with the lower bound on δ(G) and ∣Ci​∣=n/r+o(n), this implies that every vertex misses only o(n) vertices outside its own class. If some Ci​ contains a matching in a graph of s edges, extend its edges one by one to Kr+1​, choosing one vertex from each other class. At every step only o(n) cross-neighbours and boundedly many used vertices are excluded. This gives s disjoint forbidden cliques, a contradiction.
A maximal matching in each class therefore has at most s−1 edges, and its at most 2(s−1) endpoints form a vertex cover of the internal edges. Their internal vertex degrees are all o(n), so the total internal edge count is o(n). The cross-class edge count is at most e(Tr​(n)), whence
e(G)≤e(Tr​(n))+o(n).
(9)
But the balanced part sizes of the Turán graph give
bs​(n)−e(Tr​(n))=rs−1​n+O(1),
(10)
contradicting extremality for s≥2. This argument also works for r=1. Consequently, for all sufficiently large n,
ex(n,sKr+1​)=bs​(n),G≅Ks−1​+Tr​(n−s+1).​
(11)
The uniqueness is up to isomorphic graphs, as usual for an extremal graph for disjoint cliques.

Symmetrization for an arbitrary real coefficient. Among graphs maximizing f(G)=e(G)−ck3​(G), choose one maximizing
Q(G)=∑x∈V(G)​d(x)2.
(12)
For a vertex x, put L(x)=d(x)−ce(G[N(x)]). This is the contribution of edges and triangles in a graph containing x. If u,v are nonadjacent, Zykov symmetrization replacing u by a clone of v changes f by L(v)−L(u); the opposite replacement changes it by L(u)−L(v). Maximality implies L(u)=L(v), so both changes preserve f.
Let S=N(v)∖N(u) and T=N(u)∖N(v). In the first replacement the vertex degrees in S increase by one and those in T decrease by one; in the opposite replacement these changes reverse. The changes to d(u)2+d(v)2 cancel when the two replacements are added. Since (d+1)2+(d−1)2−2d2=2,
Δu←v​Q+Δv←u​Q=2(∣S∣+∣T∣).
(13)
If the two vertex neighbourhoods differ, one replacement increases Q, a contradiction. Thus every pair of nonadjacent vertices has the same vertex neighbourhood. Nonadjacency, with equality allowed, is an equivalence relation: if u and v are nonadjacent and v and w are nonadjacent, their identical vertex neighbourhoods forbid uw as well. Its classes are independent sets and every cross-class edge is present. Therefore
some maximizer of e(G)−ck3​(G) is complete multipartite.​
(14)
This edge-triangle symmetrization works for either sign of c.
The exact supporting line. For n≥3, write
e2​=e(T2​(n)),e3​=e(T3​(n)),t3​=k3​(T3​(n)),c∗​=(e3​−e2​)/t3​.
(15)
We prove the triangle support line between bipartite and tripartite Turan graphs by maximizing e−c∗​k3​. First c∗​≥2/n. For n=3,4,5, its values are respectively 1,1/2,1/2. For n≥6, the exact balancing of the Turán graph gives e3​≥n2/3−1/3, while e2​≤n2/4 and the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality gives t3​≤n3/27. Hence
n(e3​−e2​)−2t3​≥108n3​−3n​≥0.
(16)
By edge-triangle symmetrization a maximizing graph can be taken complete multipartite graph; among such maximizers use one with the fewest nonempty parts. If there are at least four parts, let a,b be the two smallest sizes. Then a+b≤n/2. Merging them loses ab edges and ab(n−a−b) triangles in a graph, so the objective changes by
ab{c∗​(n−a−b)−1}≥0.
(17)
This contradicts the choice of the number of parts. Thus at most three parts remain.
For three part sizes a≥b≥d>0, the objective is
ad(1−c∗​b)+b(a+d).
(18)
If 1−c∗​b≤0, merging the parts of sizes a,d does not decrease it, again contradicting minimality. If 1−c∗​b>0 and a−d≥2, transferring one vertex from the largest to the smallest part raises ad by a−d−1>0 and strictly increases the objective. Thus a three-part maximizer has all sizes differing by at most one, and is T3​(n). A maximizer with at most two parts has objective at most e2​, since it has no triangles in a graph and the Mantel theorem bounds its edges. Finally,
e(T3​(n))−c∗​k3​(T3​(n))=e2​.
(19)
Therefore every graph satisfies e(G)−c∗​k3​(G)≤e2​. At the prescribed edge count this gives
k3​(G)≥θk3​(T3​(n)).​
(20)
For n≤2, the conclusion is simply the nonnegative lower bound zero. All rounding in the Turán graphs has been retained.
One edge above the bipartite threshold. Write n=2m. We prove the Triangle lower bound one edge above the Mantel threshold by induction on m≥2, allowing at least m2+1 edges. For m=2, five edges on four vertices give two triangles in a graph, and adding edges preserves this bound.
We will also use the following consequence of an inductive bound: on 2a vertices, at least a2+q edges, for an integer q≥1, force at least a+q−1 triangles in a graph. Delete surplus edges first to leave exactly a2+q. Repeatedly delete an edge in a triangle in a graph until a2+1 edges remain. The Mantel theorem guarantees such a triangle in a graph at every step; each deletion destroys at least one distinct triangle in a graph. Apply the inductive bound to the remaining graph.
Now take exactly m2+1 edges. If every edge lies in a triangle in a graph, the edge-triangle incidence bound gives 3k3​(G)≥m2+1. This is at least 3m for m≥3; the base case was handled separately.
Otherwise choose an edge uv in no triangle in a graph. Its endpoints have disjoint vertex neighbourhoods, so d(u)+d(v)≤2m. Deleting u,v removes exactly d(u)+d(v)−1 edges and leaves at least (m−1)2+1 edges. If d(u)+d(v)≤2m−1, it leaves at least (m−1)2+2 edges, and the strengthened inductive bound gives at least m triangles in a graph already.
If d(u)+d(v)=2m, induction gives at least m−1 triangles in a graph after deletion. There must be an additional triangle in a graph containing u or v. Otherwise both N(u) and N(v) are independent sets; since they are disjoint and cover all vertices, G would be bipartite graph, contradicting e(G)>m2 by the Mantel theorem. Hence
k3​(G)≥m=n/2.​
(21)
The bound is sharp: add one internal edge to one class of the complete bipartite graph Km,m​. Its triangles in a graph are precisely that edge together with each of the m vertices in the other class.

Statement and definitions. For disjoint nonempty sets A,B of vertices, their edge density of a bipartite graph is d(A,B)=e(A,B)/(∣A∣∣B∣). They form a regular pair of vertex sets, or an ε-uniform pair, if
∣d(X,Y)−d(A,B)∣≤ε
(22)
whenever X⊆A, Y⊆B, ∣X∣≥ε∣A∣ and ∣Y∣≥ε∣B∣.
The Szemerédi regularity lemma states that for every ε>0 and positive integer m0​, there are integers M,n0​ such that every graph of order n≥n0​ has a set partition
V(G)=V0​⊔V1​⊔⋯⊔Vk​
(23)
with m0​≤k≤M, ∣V0​∣≤εn, equal nonzero sizes ∣V1​∣=⋯=∣Vk​∣, and at most εk2 unordered pairs (Vi​,Vj​), 1≤i<j≤k, that fail to be ε-uniform. It is enough to prove this for 0<ε≤1/2, since decreasing the parameter strengthens the conclusion.
Energy increment. For the proof, regard each exceptional vertex as a singleton part and use the equitable regularity energy
q(P)=n21​∑A,B∈P​∣A∣∣B∣d(A,B)2.
(24)
The sum is over ordered pairs, including diagonal pairs; for those pairs define d as the mean adjacency indicator on A×B. Thus 0≤q≤1. Under a refinement, the subrectangle densities have the original density as their weighted mean. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, equivalently the nonnegativity of their weighted variance, gives q(P′)≥q(P).
Suppose there are more than εk2 irregular pairs, with main cell size m. For each such pair choose witnesses X⊆Vi​, Y⊆Vj​ of sizes at least εm and with ∣d(X,Y)−d(Vi​,Vj​)∣>ε. Refine every main cell by all its witness subsets. Each splits into at most 2k−1 atoms, and we may use the larger bound 2k.
For an ordered irregular rectangle, write its refined densities as dab​ and relative areas as wab​. The energy gain on that rectangle is
n2m2​∑a,b​wab​(dab​−d(Vi​,Vj​))2.
(25)
The witness rectangle has relative area at least ε2. Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the subrectangles it contains shows that this expression is greater than ε4m2/n2. There are two orientations of each irregular pair. Since km=n−∣V0​∣≥n/2, the total gain is greater than
2ε5n2k2m2​≥ε5/2.
(26)
Restoring equal sizes without losing energy. Put ℓ=⌊m/4k⌋, assuming m≥2⋅4k. Split every refined atom into sets of size ℓ, putting its leftover fewer than ℓ vertices into the exceptional set as singleton parts. This is a further refinement, so the energy cannot decrease. The number of new exceptional vertices is at most
k2kℓ≤km/2k≤n/2k.
(27)

k≤k′≤ℓkm​≤2k4k.

Three linearly large uniform sets. Apply the Szemerédi regularity lemma with
ε=10−5,m0​=1000,d0​=10−3.

For n≥n0​, delete all edges touching V0​, all internal main-cell edges, all edges of irregular pairs, and all edges of regular pairs whose edge density of a bipartite graph is less than d0​. The respective losses are bounded by

Use natural logarithms. A Kt​ graph minor is represented by t disjoint nonempty branch sets of a graph minor, each inducing a connected graph, with an edge between every pair. The complete graph minor density threshold is defined over nonempty finite graphs by
c(t)=inf{c:for every nonempty finite G, e(G)≥c∣G∣⟹G≻Kt​}.
(35)
It uses e(G)/∣G∣, half the average degree of a vertex; the nonempty convention excludes the vacuous density inequality for the empty graph. The PDF has the intended definition with a universal implication over G; the TeX transcription corrupts that definition.
Probabilistic lower bound. Put N=⌊tlogt​/4⌋ and take the binomial random graph G(N,1/2). Its edge count has mean N(N−1)/4. The Chernoff bound implies
P(e(G)≥N2/8)⟶1.
(36)
We show that the probability of a Kt​ graph minor tends to zero. Ignore connectedness, which only enlarges the set of possible models, and assign each of the N vertices one of t branch labels or an unused label. There are at most (t+1)N assignments.
For any assignment with t nonempty branch sets, at least t/2 of them have size at most 2N/t≤logt​/2. Between any two such small sets A,B, the probability of no edge is
2−∣A∣∣B∣≥exp(−4log2​logt)≥t−1/4.
(37)
The absence or presence of edges between different pairs of branch sets depends on disjoint sets of random edges, so these events are independent. For this assignment the probability that all small pairs are adjacent is at most
(1−t−1/4)(2⌊t/2⌋​)≤exp(−(2⌊t/2⌋​)t−1/4).
(38)
The union bound over assignments is therefore at most
exp(Nlog(t+1)−(2⌊t/2⌋​)t−1/4)⟶0,
(39)
since the positive term is O(t(logt)3/2) and the negative term has order t7/4. There is consequently a graph with no Kt​ graph minor and with e(G)/N≥N/8. Any constant having the universal forcing property must exceed this ratio. Since N/8≥tlogt​/64 for large t,
c(t)≥βtlogt​with β=1/64.​

Probabilistic upper bound and the constant seven. Suppose G is nonempty and e(G)≥7tlogt​∣G∣, and set
k=⌊117​tlogt​⌋,ℓ=⌈logt​⌉.

The corrected lemma gives a graph minor H of order N≤11k+2 with 2δ(H)≥N+4k−1. Since δ(H)≤N−1, we also have N≥4k+1. For large k, every vertex has at most N/3 nonneighbours, counting itself, because
N−δ(H)≤N/2−2k+1/2≤N/3;
(42)
the last inequality uses N≤11k+2≤12k−3. Moreover any two distinct vertices have at least
2δ(H)−N≥4k−1

Choose 2t disjoint random ℓ-sets A1​,…,A2t​ from V(H), uniformly, which is possible since 2tℓ<4k−1≤N for sufficiently large t. For a set A, let U(A) be the set of vertices having no neighbour in A. For each vertex, the probability that all members of a uniformly sampled ℓ-set are its nonneighbours is at most 3−ℓ, even when sampling without replacement. By linearity of expected value,
E∣U(A)∣≤N3−ℓ.
(44)
Call A good if ∣U(A)∣≤N3−19ℓ/20. The Markov inequality shows that a random set is bad with probability at most 3−ℓ/20=o(1). Thus the expected number of bad sets among our 2t samples is o(t).
Conditional on a fixed good Ai​, the marginal distribution of Aj​ is uniform among ℓ-sets outside Ai​. For there to be no edge between them, all its members must lie in U(Ai​). Hence
P(Ai​ good and e(Ai​,Aj​)=0)≤(N−ℓN3−19ℓ/20​)ℓ.
(45)
Here (N/(N−ℓ))ℓ=exp(O(ℓ2/N))=1+o(1). Consequently the expected number of nonadjacent pairs of good sets is at most
(22t​)(1+o(1))3−19ℓ2/20=O(t2−(19/20)log3)=o(t),
(46)
since (19/20)log3>1. Applying the Markov inequality to both counts shows that there exists a choice with fewer than t bad sets and at most t nonadjacent pairs of good sets. Select t of the good sets; they still have at most t missing adjacencies.
Make each selected set connected by fixing one of its vertices as a root and, for each of its other members, adding one unused common neighbour of that member and the root. This uses at most t(ℓ−1) additional vertices. Then for each pair with no original edge, choose one vertex in each set and add an unused common neighbour to one of the sets. The added vertex is joined to that set and to the other set, so it preserves connectedness and repairs their adjacency. At most t such repairs are required.
Throughout this process the total number of occupied vertices is at most
tℓ+t(ℓ−1)+t=2tℓ<4k−1.
(47)
Since every pair has at least 4k−1 common neighbours, an unused choice always exists. The final sets are disjoint connected branch sets of a graph minor with every pair adjacent. They represent Kt​ in H, and hence in G, by transitivity of graph minors. This random branch-set construction of a complete graph minor proves
c(t)≤7tlogt​for sufficiently large t.​

The sharp linear threshold for a four-vertex complete minor. We prove by induction that a graph on n≥4 vertices with at least 2n−2 edges has a K4​ graph minor. The case n=4 is K4​ itself. If some edge uv has at most one common neighbour, an edge contraction reduces the order by one and removes exactly 1+∣N(u)∩N(v)∣≤2 edges. The contracted graph has at least 2(n−1)−2 edges, so induction applies.
Otherwise every edge is in at least two triangles in a graph. Choose a vertex v incident with an edge. Each vertex u∈N(v) has at least two neighbours of a vertex in N(v), since these are the common neighbours of u,v. Thus G[N(v)] has minimum degree of a graph at least two. A longest path in this finite graph has an endpoint adjacent to an earlier nonconsecutive path vertex, and hence contains a cycle in a graph. Together with v, this cycle in a graph gives a wheel graph as a subgraph. Divide its rim into three consecutive nonempty connected arcs; these arcs and {v} form four pairwise adjacent branch sets of a graph minor. Therefore
e(G)≥2n−2 ⟹ G≻K4​.​
(49)
With 2n−3 edges the answer is no. Take the join of graphs of K2​ and an independent set of n−2 vertices. It has 1+2(n−2)=2n−3 edges. Among four disjoint putative branch sets of a graph minor, at most two can contain the two vertices of K2​. Any connected branch set avoiding them is a singleton in the independent set. At least two such singleton sets would therefore have no edge between them. This excludes a K4​ graph minor for every n≥4 and proves sharpness.
