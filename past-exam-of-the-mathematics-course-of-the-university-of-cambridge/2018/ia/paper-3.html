(13)(2457)(815)=(1724583),

Inspecting the partitions of 9 shows that the largest cycle-length least common multiple available in S9​ is 20, from the partition 5+4, but such a permutation is odd. Among integers from 16 through 20, orders 17 and 19 would require cycles of those prime lengths, order 16 requires a cycle length divisible by 16, and order 18 requires disjoint cycles using at least 9+2>9 letters. The disjoint product of a five-cycle and a three-cycle is even and has order 15. Hence the greatest order in A9​ is 15.

2D OurBigBook.com
Every Q∈O(3) with detQ=1 is a rotation in three dimensions, hence is the product of two reflections in planes through its axis whose angle is half the rotation angle.
If detQ=−1, its real eigenvalue is −1: the other two eigenvalues are either a complex-conjugate pair or two real signs, and their product is positive. Let v be a corresponding unit eigenvector and let H be reflection in v⊥. Then HQ fixes v and has determinant 1, so it is a rotation and hence a product of two reflections. Since Q=H(HQ), every member of O(3) is a product of at most three reflections.
Two reflections have determinant 1, whereas one reflection has eigenvalues (−1,1,1). The map −I∈O(3) has determinant −1 but is not a reflection, so it cannot be a product of at most two reflections. Thus three are sometimes necessary.

For σ∈Sn​, let Pσ​ be its permutation matrix and define
sgn(σ)=detPσ​∈{±1}.
(10)
Since a transposition exchanges two columns, it has determinant −1. Thus any expression of σ as r transpositions has sign (−1)r, proving that the parity is independent of the expression. Moreover, Pστ​=Pσ​Pτ​, so the determinant proves
sgn(στ)=sgn(σ)sgn(τ).
(11)
Hence the sign homomorphism is a surjective homomorphism for n≥2.
For any homomorphism φ:Sn​→{±1}, all transpositions have the same image because they are conjugate. Since transpositions generate Sn​, image +1 would make φ trivial. Surjectivity therefore forces every transposition to map to −1, and hence φ=sgn.

For a normal subgroup H⊴G, the quotient group G/H is the set of cosets gH with multiplication (gH)(kH)=gkH. If gH=g′H and kH=k′H, normality moves the intervening elements of H past k, proving that the product is independent of representatives.
If g has finite order m, then (gH)m=H, so ord(gH) divides m. For finite G, the greatest element order in G/H is therefore no greater than that in G.
Every kernel is normal because φ(ghg−1)=φ(g)φ(h)φ(g)−1=1 whenever h∈kerφ. Conversely, if H is normal, the quotient map G→G/H has kernel H. Thus H⊴G exactly when it is the kernel of a group homomorphism.
A group is metacyclic when it has a cyclic normal subgroup with cyclic quotient. A dihedral group has its cyclic rotation subgroup normal and quotient C2​, so every dihedral group is metacyclic.
Up to isomorphism the groups of order eight are
C8​,C4​×C2​,C23​,D8​,Q8​.
(17)
All except C23​ are metacyclic: use a cyclic subgroup of order four in the three noncyclic examples that have one. In C23​, every cyclic subgroup has order at most two and its quotient is not cyclic. The normal subgroups of A4​ are 1,V4​,A4​; none gives the required cyclic subgroup and quotient, so A4​ is not metacyclic. Finally, S1​,S2​,S3​ are metacyclic, while S4​ and S5​ are not, by their normal-subgroup structures. Hence
Sn​ is metacyclic for n≤5 exactly when n=1,2,3.​
(18)

The internal direct product theorem states that if H,K⊴G, H∩K={1}, and HK=G, then G≅H×K. Indeed, for h∈H,k∈K, the commutator [h,k] lies in both H and K, so it is trivial. The map (h,k)↦hk is consequently a homomorphism; the intersection condition makes it injective and HK=G makes it surjective. Conversely, the two factors in a direct product satisfy these conditions.
In odd dimension, −I is central and has determinant −1. Every Q∈O(3) has a unique expression Q=(−I)εR with R∈SO(3), so
O(3)≅SO(3)×C2​.​
(19)
By contrast, SO(2)×C2​ is abelian, while reflections in O(2) conjugate a rotation to its inverse. Thus O(2)≅SO(2)×C2​.
The center of the unitary group consists exactly of scalar unitary matrices:
Z(U(2))={zI:∣z∣=1}.​
(20)
The determinant gives a surjective homomorphism
det:U(2)⟶T
(21)
whose kernel is the special unitary group SU(2).
Nevertheless U(2) is not isomorphic to SU(2)×T. The center of U(2) has exactly one nonidentity element of order two, namely −I. The center of the proposed product is {±I}×T, which has three nonidentity elements of order two. Since an isomorphism preserves the center and element orders,
U(2)≅SU(2)×T.​
(22)
