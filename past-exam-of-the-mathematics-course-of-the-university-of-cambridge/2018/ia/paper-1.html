Let (un​) be increasing and bounded above, and let L=sup{un​:n∈N}. For every ε>0, the definition of the supremum supplies N with L−ε<uN​≤L. Monotonicity gives L−ε<un​≤L for every n≥N, proving un​→L. This is the monotone bounded sequence theorem.

The function is continuous at x when, for every ε>0, there is δ>0 such that ∣y−x∣<δ implies ∣f(y)−f(x)∣<ε.
If this holds and xn​→x, eventually ∣xn​−x∣<δ, so f(xn​)→f(x). Conversely, if continuity fails, some ε0​>0 allows, for every n, a point xn​ with ∣xn​−x∣<1/n but ∣f(xn​)−f(x)∣≥ε0​. Then xn​→x while f(xn​)→f(x), a contradiction. This is the sequential characterization of continuity in metric spaces.

Let the nonconstant polynomial have degree d and leading coefficient c. If d is odd, its limits at +∞ and −∞ have opposite signs, so the intermediate value theorem shows that its image is R. If d is even and c>0, it tends to +∞ at both ends. Outside a sufficiently large compact interval it exceeds f(0), while on that interval the extreme value theorem gives a global minimum a. Continuity and the intermediate value theorem then give image [a,∞). For c<0, applying this argument to −f gives (−∞,a]. These are exactly the three possible images.

The comparison test for series states that if 0≤an​≤bn​ eventually and ∑bn​ converges, then ∑an​ converges. The partial sums of ∑an​ are increasing and bounded above by a constant plus the bounded partial sums of ∑bn​, so the monotone bounded sequence theorem applies.
If ∑xn​ converges, then xn2​≤xn​ proves convergence of ∑xn2​. Also xn​→0, so eventually xn​≤1/2 and xn​/(1−xn​)≤2xn​, proving convergence of ∑xn​/(1−xn​). The converse for the square series is false: xn​=1/(n+1) has convergent square series but divergent original series. The converse for the fraction series is true because xn​≤xn​/(1−xn​).
If (xn​) is positive and decreasing and ∑xn​ converges, put m=⌊n/2⌋. Then
(n−m)xn​≤∑k=m+1n​xk​⟶0,
(35)
so nxn​→0. The converse fails for xn​=1/(nlogn) for n≥2: it is decreasing and nxn​→0, but the integral test for convergence shows that its series diverges.
Even convergence of ∑xn​ need not imply (nlogn)xn​→0. Choose integers Nj​=⌈e2j⌉ and set xn​=(Nj​logNj​)−1 for Nj−1​<n≤Nj​. This is decreasing, and the jth block contributes at most 1/logNj​≤2−j, so the series converges. At n=Nj​, however, (nlogn)xn​=1.

a OurBigBook.com
Assume an​→0. The function is bounded, and every interval has lower Darboux sum zero because it contains an irrational. Given ε>0, choose N so that an​<ε/2 for n>N. Put small intervals around the finitely many points q1​,…,qN​ whose total length makes their contribution to the upper sum less than ε/2, and refine their endpoints to a partition. On every remaining subinterval the function is at most ε/2, so the upper sum is less than ε. The Riemann integrability criterion proves that f is Riemann integrable with integral zero.
The condition an​→0 is sufficient but not necessary. Assign an​=1 when qn​=1/k for some positive integer k, and an​=1/n otherwise. Then (an​) does not tend to zero. Outside a short interval near zero only finitely many unit spikes occur and can be enclosed in intervals of arbitrarily small total length; all remaining values can be made uniformly small except for finitely many further points. The same Darboux-sum argument proves that this f is Riemann integrable with integral zero.
