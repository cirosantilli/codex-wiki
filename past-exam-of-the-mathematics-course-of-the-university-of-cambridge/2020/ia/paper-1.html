A matrix is Hermitian when A†=A, and unitary when U†U=UU†=I.
If Au=λu with u=0, then
λu†u=u†Au=(u†Au)∗=λ∗u†u,
(28)
so every eigenvalue is real. If Au=λu and Av=μv, then
μu†v=u†Av=(Au)†v=λu†v.
(29)
Distinct eigenvalues therefore have orthogonal eigenvectors.
The normalized eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis, so the matrix U=(u1​ ⋯ un​) satisfies U†U=I and is unitary. With
D=diag(λ1​,…,λn​),
(30)
the equations Auj​=λj​uj​ say AU=UD, hence A=UDU†. This is the finite-dimensional unitary diagonalization of a normal matrix.
A unitary conjugate of an arbitrary diagonal matrix need not be Hermitian: U=(1) and D=(i) give UDU†=(i). It is Hermitian exactly when all diagonal entries of D are real.
For the displayed matrix, one valid decomposition is
U=​i/2​01/2​​010​−i/2​01/2​​​,D=diag(5,2,−1)​.

Rolle theorem states that if h is continuous on the closed interval between two distinct points, differentiable between them, and has equal endpoint values, then h′ vanishes at an interior point.
Let PN​(t)=∑j=0N​f(j)(0)tj/j! and, for x=0, put
M=xN+1f(x)−PN​(x)​,h(t)=f(t)−PN​(t)−MtN+1.
(43)
Then h(x)=0 and h(j)(0)=0 for 0≤j≤N. Applying Rolle's theorem successively N+1 times gives a point ξ=θx, 0<θ<1, where h(N+1)(ξ)=0. Hence
M=(N+1)!f(N+1)(θx)​,
(44)
which is the stated Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder.
If f′=0, the N=0 case gives f(x)=f(0)+xf′(θx)=f(0). Thus f is constant; equivalently this is the immediate corollary of the mean value theorem.

Choose a closed interval containing the bounded sequence. Bisect it and retain a half containing infinitely many terms; repeat. The resulting intervals are nested and their lengths tend to zero. By the nested interval theorem they have one common point x, and choosing successively indexed terms from these intervals produces a subsequence converging to x. This proves the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem.
