The rank of a matrix is the dimension of its column space, equivalently the dimension of the image of a linear map represented by the matrix. For A∈Mn​, the rank-nullity theorem gives
rankA=n⟺kerA={0}⟺A is injective.
(28)
An injective endomorphism of a finite-dimensional vector space is surjective, hence invertible. By the adjugate matrix identity
AadjA=(detA)I,
(29)
A is invertible when detA=0; conversely, multiplicativity of the determinant shows that an invertible A has nonzero determinant. Therefore
rankA=n⟺A is nonsingular⟺detA=0​.
(30)
Let Eij​ be the matrix unit with its only nonzero entry at (i,j). The following n2 matrices are all nonsingular:
B={I}∪{I+Eij​:(i,j)=(n,n)}.
(31)
Indeed, I+Eij​ is an elementary shear when i=j, while I+Eii​ is diagonal with one diagonal entry equal to two. Their linear span contains every Eij​ except initially Enn​, because Eij​=(I+Eij​)−I, and it then contains
Enn​=I−∑i=1n−1​Eii​.
(32)
Thus B spans the n2-dimensional space Mn​ and, having n2 members, is a basis. This also covers n=1, when B={I}.
Now let A be a nonsingular zero-one matrix. If it had fewer than n−1 zero entries, at least two rows would contain no zero at all. Those two rows would both be the all-one row, contradicting linear independence. Hence every such matrix has at most
cn​≤n2−n+1​
(33)
ones. The bound is attained. Let J be the all-one matrix and set
A=J−diag(1,…,1,0),
(34)
where there are n−1 initial diagonal ones. If Ax=0 and S=∑j​xj​, the first n−1 row equations give xi​=S, while the last gives S=0. Hence every xi​=0, so A is nonsingular and has exactly n2−n+1 ones. Therefore
cn​=n2−n+1​.
(35)
