Use the Leibniz definition of the determinant:
detA=∑π∈Sn​​sgn(π)∏i=1n​ai,π(i)​.
(65)
For the permuted-column matrix, aijσ​=ai,σ(j)​. Reindex the sum by τ=σ∘π. The sign of a permutation is multiplicative, so sgn(π)=sgn(σ)sgn(τ). It follows directly that
det(Aσ)=sgn(σ)detA.
(66)
For the matrix transpose, the products in its determinant are ∏i​aπ(i),i​. Set j=π(i) to obtain ∏j​aj,π−1(j)​, and use sgn(π−1)=sgn(π). This proves det(At)=detA. These facts also show that equal columns, or equal rows by transposition, force the determinant to be zero: exchanging the equal pair leaves the matrix unchanged but changes the determinant's sign.
Let Mij​ be the matrix obtained by deleting row i and column j, with the empty determinant taken as one for n=1. Define the cofactor Cij​=(−1)i+jdetMij​ and the adjugate matrix by adj(A)ij​=Cji​. Grouping the Leibniz sum according to the column chosen in row i gives the cofactor expansion detA=∑k​aik​Cik​: deleting that row and its chosen column leaves a permutation of n−1 entries, with sign factor (−1)i+k. Therefore
(AadjA)ij​=∑k​aik​Cjk​.
(67)
When i=j this is detA. When i=j it is the cofactor expansion along row j of the matrix formed by replacing that row with row i; its remaining minors are unchanged, and its two equal rows give determinant zero. The column version proves the other product in the same way. Thus the adjugate identity is
Aadj(A)=adj(A)A=(detA)I,A−1=detAadj(A)​(detA=0).​
(68)
For the real matrices C,D, let p(t)=det(C+tD). The determinant definition makes this a real polynomial of degree of a polynomial at most n. Since C+iD is invertible, the allowed converse implies p(i)=0, so p is not the zero polynomial. It has at most n real roots of a polynomial. Choose a real λ outside those roots; the adjugate argument proves that C+λD is invertible.
Finally write the given complex similarity transformation as P=C+iD. The relation AP=PB, with A,B real, yields AC=CB and AD=DB by comparing real and imaginary parts. Choose the preceding λ and put Q=C+λD. Then AQ=QB, so
Q−1AQ=Bwith Q real and invertible.​
(69)
This proves real similarity from complex similarity by constructing a real intertwiner, rather than requiring C or D separately to be invertible.
