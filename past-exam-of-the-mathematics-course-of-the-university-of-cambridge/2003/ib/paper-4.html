Uniform convergence on an interval I means that for every ϵ>0 there is N such that ∣fn​(x)−f(x)∣<ϵ for every x∈I and every n≥N. Equivalently, the supremum of this difference tends to zero. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous: at a point x0​, choose one fN​ uniformly within ϵ/3 of f, then use continuity of fN​ to make ∣fN​(x)−fN​(x0​)∣<ϵ/3. The triangle inequality gives ∣f(x)−f(x0​)∣<ϵ. Thus f is integrable on the compact interval, and
​∫ab​fn​−∫ab​f​≤(b−a)sup[a,b]​∣fn​−f∣⟶0.
(1)
For the half-line example, differentiating gives fn′​(x)=n−2e−x/n(1−x/n). The maximum is attained at x=n, where fn​(n)=1/(en). Therefore fn​→0 uniformly on [0,∞). Nevertheless, the substitution x=ny gives
∫0∞​fn​(x)dx=∫0∞​ye−ydy=1​.
(2)
The integral does not tend to zero. This is the phenomenon that uniformly vanishing functions can retain a nonzero integral: the finite-length estimate above has no finite analogue for the entire half-line.

For the single-frequency response, use separation of variables with u=R(r)sinωt. Set k=ω/c and V=rR; then V′′+k2V=0, so V=Csinkr+Dcoskr. A finite origin value forces D=0, since coskr/r is singular. u(r,t)=Aωr/csin(ωr/c)​sinωt​,
(4)
with the quotient defined by its limit at zero.
This answers the literal finite-origin condition in the class of regular single-frequency responses. It is a standing combination of inward and outward waves: the numerator is proportional to cos(kr−ωt)−cos(kr+ωt). The source's wording supplies neither an initial field nor an outgoing singular-source normalization; without restriction to the stated harmonic response, a general initial-value problem is not uniquely determined by the one origin condition.

Morera's theorem states that a continuous complex-valued function on an open set is holomorphic if its integral around every triangle whose closed interior lies in the set is zero. It is enough to impose this condition locally on disks.
Fix a disk B contained in the open set and a base point z0​∈B. Define F(z) as the integral of f along the straight segment from z0​ to z. The zero triangle integral gives, for sufficiently small h,
F(z+h)−F(z)=∫[z,z+h]​f(w)dw=h∫01​f(z+th)dt.
(6)
Continuity makes the quotient by h tend to f(z), so F is holomorphic and F′=f. To justify that its derivative is holomorphic, apply the Cauchy integral formula for F on a smaller circle:
F′(z)=2πi1​∫Γ​(w−z)2F(w)​dw.
(7)
The right side may be differentiated again inside the circle because its denominator stays uniformly away from zero on the contour. Thus F′=f is holomorphic on B. Such disks cover the open set, proving the theorem.

For constrained optimization in minimization form, let x∈X, gi​(x)≤0 and hj​(x)=0, and define the Lagrangian
L(x,λ,ν)=f(x)+∑i​λi​gi​(x)+∑j​νj​hj​(x).
(9)
The Lagrangian sufficiency theorem says: if x∗​ is feasible, λi​≥0, λi​gi​(x∗​)=0 for every i, and x∗​ minimizes L(⋅,λ,ν) globally over X, then x∗​ minimizes f over the feasible set. Equality multipliers νj​ may have either sign.
Indeed, for every feasible x,
f(x)≥L(x,λ,ν)≥L(x∗​,λ,ν)=f(x∗​).
(10)
The first inequality uses the inequality-multiplier signs and the equality constraints; the last equality uses complementary slackness. This proves global optimality without assuming convexity or differentiability. The maximization version reverses the relevant signs, or follows by replacing f with −f.
When X is convex and the Lagrangian is differentiable and convex, the first-order condition ∇x​L(x∗​)=0 implies its global minimum, because L(x)≥L(x∗​)+∇L(x∗​)⋅(x−x∗​). Thus feasible stationarity plus complementary slackness is sufficient in that convex setting. Mere stationarity in a general nonconvex problem is not the theorem's global-minimum hypothesis.

For a standard collinear Lorentz boost, put β=v/c and Γ=(1−β2)−1/2. Its equations are x′=Γ(x−βct) and ct′=Γ(ct−βx). Define the rapidity ϕ=arctanhβ. Since cosh2ϕ−sinh2ϕ=1 and coshϕ>0, coshϕ=Γ and sinhϕ=Γβ. Therefore
x′=xcoshϕ−ctsinhϕ,ct′=−xsinhϕ+ctcoshϕ​.
(22)

The zero-composition subspaces of a linear map are subspaces because the defining equations are homogeneous linear equations in β: they hold for the zero map and are preserved by addition and scalar multiplication. A map with βα=0 vanishes on imα, so it factors uniquely through the quotient vector space V/imα. Conversely every map from that quotient to U gives such a β. Therefore
Ml(α)≅L(V/imα,U),dimMl(α)=(dimV−rankα)dimU​.
(50)
The condition αβ=0 says exactly that the image of β lies in kerα. Consequently
Mr(α)≅L(V,kerα),dimMr(α)=dimVdimkerα​.
(51)
Both dimension formulas use the fact that the space of maps between spaces of dimensions p,q has dimension pq: matrix units in any chosen bases supply a basis.

Irrotational flow means ∇×u=0; incompressible flow means ∇⋅u=0. The given streamfunction automatically satisfies the second condition, since ∂x​ψy​−∂y​ψx​=0. For an oriented path with unit tangent (dx/ds,dy/ds), choose its right-hand unit normal n=(dy/ds,−dx/ds). Then
u⋅nds=ψy​dy+ψx​dx=dψ,∫x1​x2​​u⋅nds=ψ(x2​)−ψ(x1​)​.
(56)
The opposite normal reverses the sign; the source's unspecified normal orientation must be chosen consistently. The volume flux between two streamlines is thus their streamfunction difference. A closed contour has zero net flux, expressing mass conservation for constant-density flow without a source inside.
For the channel, a rigid boundary has zero normal velocity and hence constant streamfunction. Choose zero on the lower wall. Uniform inflow at infinity must have horizontal velocity −m, so there ψ=−my. The upper wall then has constant −m. The unbroken left wall connects to it and also has constant −m. The jump by m between the walls at the corner represents the withdrawn flux. Finally the vorticity is vx​−uy​=−Δψ, so irrotationality gives Δψ=0.
Write ψ=−my+w. Then w vanishes at y=0,1 and at infinity, while w(0,y)=−m(1−y) for 0<y<1. Separation of variables gives the harmonic sine mode in a half-strip e−nπxsinnπy. The Fourier sine series coefficients of the left boundary are
bn​=2∫01​[−m(1−y)]sin(nπy)dy=−nπ2m​.
(57)
Therefore the corner sink in a semi-infinite channel is
ψ(x,y)=−my−π2m​n=1∑∞​ne−nπxsin(nπy)​​.
(58)
For x>0 the series and its differentiated series converge on compact subsets, making the sum harmonic. At x=0, ∑n≥1​sin(nπy)/n=π(1−y)/2 for 0<y<1, so the left-wall value is exactly −m. The other wall values and far-field limit follow directly.
