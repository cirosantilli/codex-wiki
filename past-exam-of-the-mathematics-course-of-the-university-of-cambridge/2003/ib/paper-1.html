We prove the equivalence by a cycle of implications. First assume closedness and boundedness. Given a sequence (xj​) in E, boundedness puts each coordinate in a fixed closed bounded real interval. Apply the one-dimensional Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem to its first coordinate to extract a convergent subsequence, then apply it to the second coordinate of that subsequence, and continue through the finitely many coordinates. The final subsequence converges coordinatewise to some x∈Rn. It also converges in the Euclidean norm, since the sum of the squares of its finitely many coordinate differences tends to zero. Since E is a closed set, this limit belongs to E. Thus closedness and boundedness imply the required subsequence property. No higher-dimensional compactness theorem has been assumed.

Assume the subsequence property, and let f:E→R be a continuous function. If f were unbounded, for each positive integer j we could choose xj​∈E with ∣f(xj​)∣>j. There is a subsequence xjk​​→x with x∈E. By continuity, f(xjk​​)→f(x), so that real sequence is bounded. But ∣f(xjk​​)∣>jk​→∞, a contradiction. Hence every continuous real-valued function on E is bounded. This is the bounded-function consequence of sequential compactness.

Assume every continuous function E→R is bounded. The Euclidean norm function x↦∥x∥ is continuous, so its boundedness proves that E is a bounded set.
If E were not a closed set, there would be a∈E∖E. Choose xj​∈E with ∥xj​−a∥<1/j. The function f(x)=1/∥x−a∥ is continuous everywhere on E, because its denominator is nonzero there. Yet f(xj​)>j, contradicting the hypothesis. Thus E is closed. Together with the two previous implications this proves all three conditions are equivalent. The empty set satisfies all three conditions trivially. This is the Euclidean case of the bounded-continuous-function characterization of compact metric spaces.

4F OurBigBook.com
Use the Poincaré half-plane model, H={x+iy:y>0} with metric ds2=(dx2+dy2)/y2. Its hyperbolic lines are vertical lines and semicircles centred on the real boundary, hence orthogonal to that boundary. Each has two ideal endpoints in R∪{∞}. Distinct parallel hyperbolic lines are disjoint inside H and share one ideal endpoint; distinct ultraparallel hyperbolic lines are disjoint and share none. Intersecting lines are neither. Because the metric is conformal to the Euclidean metric, the two notions of angle agree.
Apply a hyperbolic isometry taking one line l to the imaginary axis, whose ideal endpoints are 0,∞. If the other line l′ is ultraparallel, its endpoints are two finite nonzero real numbers of the same sign. Reflect if necessary to write them as 0<a<b. Then l′ is the semicircle with centre c=(a+b)/2 and radius r=(b−a)/2.
Every hyperbolic line perpendicular to the imaginary axis must be a semicircle centred at zero: at the intersection its tangent has to be horizontal, forcing its real centre to coincide with zero. Let its radius be R. Orthogonality to the circle representing l′ requires c2=R2+r2, since the radii to the intersection form a right triangle. Consequently
R2=c2−r2=ab.​
(8)
This has exactly one positive solution. The circles intersect above the real axis: c−r=a<ab​<b=c+r. Hence the required common perpendicular of ultraparallel hyperbolic lines exists and is unique.
Conversely, if l′ intersects the imaginary axis, its endpoints straddle zero and the same equation would require R2=ab<0, which is impossible. If l′ has endpoint zero, it gives R2=0, not a line. If its shared endpoint is infinity, l′ is another vertical line; no circle centred at zero meets a distinct vertical line with horizontal tangent. Thus parallel lines have no common perpendicular either. These cases exhaust distinct lines, proving a unique common perpendicular exists exactly for ultraparallel lines.

Nondegeneracy of b means both radicals are zero: if b(x,y)=0 for every y, then x=0, and the analogous implication holds in the other variable. Define linear maps BU​:U→V∗ and BV​:V→U∗ by BU​(x)(y)=b(x,y) and BV​(y)(x)=b(x,y). Both are injective. Finite dimensionality gives dimU≤dimV and dimV≤dimU, so they are isomorphisms.

A metric space is a set X with a nonnegative symmetric distance d, zero exactly for equal points, satisfying d(x,z)≤d(x,y)+d(y,z). A Cauchy sequence is a sequence for which, given any ϵ>0, there is N such that d(xn​,xm​)<ϵ whenever n,m≥N.
For a pseudometric, zero distance defines an equivalence relation. Reflexivity and symmetry are immediate; transitivity follows from d(x,z)≤d(x,y)+d(y,z)=0 when both terms vanish. Define dQ​([x],[y])=d(x,y). If x∼x′ and y∼y′, the triangle inequality gives d(x,y)≤d(x′,y′) and its reversed version gives equality. Thus the definition is independent of representatives. The nonnegative, symmetric and triangle properties descend, while zero distance means precisely that the two classes are equal. It is therefore a metric quotient of a pseudometric.
Now take two Cauchy sequences in the original metric space. Two applications of the triangle inequality give
∣d(xn​,yn​)−d(xm​,ym​)∣≤d(xn​,xm​)+d(yn​,ym​).
(16)
The right-hand side is arbitrarily small when both indices are large, so (d(xn​,yn​)) is a real Cauchy sequence and has a finite real limit. Consequently
d((xn​),(yn​))=limn​d(xn​,yn​)
(17)
is well defined. Nonnegativity, symmetry and zero self-distance follow by passing to the limit in the corresponding properties of d. Passing to the limit in d(xn​,zn​)≤d(xn​,yn​)+d(yn​,zn​) gives its triangle inequality. Thus d is a pseudometric on C, and the preceding quotient construction makes C/R a metric space.
Map x∈X to the class of its constant sequence. The distance between two such classes is d(x,y), so this is an injective isometric embedding. It is surjective exactly when every Cauchy sequence (xn​) is equivalent to a constant sequence (x), that is, when d(xn​,x)→0 for some x∈X. This is precisely completeness. Hence
X⟶C/R is bijective if and only if X is complete.​
(18)
The quotient is the Cauchy-sequence construction of a metric completion; the conclusion requires no assumption that X is compact.

Away from the source point, the Green function obeys the homogeneous equation and must satisfy the appropriate endpoint condition. Thus it has the form Asin(kx) to the left of ξ and Bsin(k(π−x)) to the right. It must be continuous at ξ: otherwise its second distributional derivative would contain an unwanted derivative of a Dirac delta. Integrating across ξ gives the jump condition Gx​(ξ+,ξ)−Gx​(ξ−,ξ)=1.
The left and right homogeneous solutions u=sin(kx), v=sin(k(π−x)) have constant Wronskian
uv′−u′v=−ksin(kπ).
(19)
This is nonzero for real noninteger k. Dividing the product of the left and right solutions by that Wronskian therefore gives the Dirichlet Helmholtz Green function on an interval
G(x,ξ)=−ksin(kπ)sin(kmin(x,ξ))sin(k(π−max(x,ξ)))​.​
(20)
The formula vanishes at both endpoints, is continuous at the source and has derivative jump one, verifying all distributional and boundary conditions. A difference of two such Green functions would be a homogeneous Dirichlet solution; it vanishes because sin(kπ)=0, proving uniqueness. Although zero is excluded by the stated noninteger condition, the removable k→0 limit is −min(x,ξ)(π−max(x,ξ))/π.

Assume the two normal samples are independent. Write nX​=6, nY​=21, and let SX​,SY​ denote their residual sums of squares about the respective sample means. The joint likelihood function, apart from its constant factor, is
L=(vX​)−nX​/2(vY​)−nY​/2exp[−2vX​∑(Xi​−μX​)2​−2vY​∑(Yj​−μY​)2​],
(23)
where vX​=σX2​, vY​=σY2​. For every fixed variance pair the means maximize this likelihood at Xˉ,Yˉ, because ∑(Xi​−μX​)2=SX​+nX​(Xˉ−μX​)2, and likewise for Y.
Under equal variances, differentiate the profiled log-likelihood to obtain v0​=(SX​+SY​)/(nX​+nY​). Without the order constraint, the separate variance maxima are vX​=SX​/nX​, vY​=SY​/nY​. If vX​>vY​ they lie in the alternative. If their order is reversed, maximizing over vX​≥vY​ puts the optimum on the equal-variance boundary. These assertions also follow by strict concavity in the precisions 1/vX​,1/vY​.
Thus the one-sided likelihood-ratio test for two normal variances uses the quotient of the null maximum to the maximum over null and ordered alternative:
Λ=⎩⎨⎧​[(SX​+SY​)/(nX​+nY​)](nX​+nY​)/2(SX​/nX​)nX​/2(SY​/nY​)nY​/2​,1,​R>1,R≤1,​R=SY​/nY​SX​/nX​​.
(24)
To identify the rejection direction, put q=SX​/(SX​+SY​). Up to constants, logΛ=(nX​/2)logq+(nY​/2)log(1−q). Its derivative is [nX​−(nX​+nY​)q]/[2q(1−q)], negative on the ordered branch q>nX​/(nX​+nY​). Hence small likelihood ratios mean a large sample variance ratio.
The null law must account for estimating both means. By Cochran's theorem, SX​/σ2 and SY​/σ2 are independent random variables with chi-squared distributions of 5 and 20 degrees of freedom. Consequently the exact null statistic is
F=SY​/20SX​/5​∼F5,20​.
(25)
It is an increasing multiple of R, so an upper-tail critical region is the likelihood-ratio rejection region. The observed statistic is F=(30/5)/(40/20)=3. The supplied 95th percentile is 2.71, so
3>2.71:reject equal variances at the 5 percent level in favor of σX2​>σY2​.​
(26)
The F-distribution degrees of freedom are 5,20, not 6,21, although the variance maximum-likelihood estimates themselves have denominators 6,21.

(36)
This is the vorticity equation, including vortex stretching. Without constant density there would in general be an additional baroclinic term; the displayed source equations use the constant-density interpretation.
For the specified velocity, direct differentiation gives ω=2Ω(t)k. It is spatially uniform, while (ω⋅∇)u=2βω. Thus Ω′=2βΩ and
ω=2Ω(0)e2βtk.​
(37)
If the positive z direction agrees with the initial vorticity, 2Ω(0)=ω0​ as written in the question. For opposite rotation the initial component is signed and the vector has the corresponding minus sign.
For a material particle, d(x2+y2)/dt=2x(−βx−Ωy)+2y(−βy+Ωx)=−2β(x2+y2), while dz/dt=2βz. The initial material circle therefore evolves into
a(t)=e−βt,x2+y2=a(t)2,z=e2βt=a(t)−2.​
(38)
The azimuthal rotation changes the labels of points on the circle but not the circle as a set. Its counterclockwise circulation, viewed from positive z, is
Γ=∮u⋅dr=2πΩ(t)a(t)2=2πΩ(0)=πω0​
(39)
for the positive-orientation convention. The strain contributes no tangential velocity. Thus circulation stays constant although vorticity grows and the enclosed material area shrinks, illustrating Kelvin's circulation theorem for a material loop in constant-density inviscid flow with no nonconservative body force.

The boundary circles intersect at z=1 and z=i: subtracting their equations gives x+y=1, and x2+y2=1 then gives these two points. Their intersection is the open lens in the first quadrant, bounded by the unit-circle arc from 1 to i and the corresponding lower-left arc of the circle centred at 1+i.
To construct a conformal map of a circular lens, apply the Möbius transformation
ζ(z)=z−i1−z​.
(40)
It sends the intersection points to zero and infinity, so both boundary circles become straight lines through zero. The boundary arc of the circle centred at zero maps to the ray of argument −π/4; the other arc maps to the ray of argument +π/4. For example, their arc midpoints eiπ/4 and (1+i)−eiπ/4 give these signs. The interior point (1+i)/2 maps to one, selecting the sector −π/4<argζ<π/4, not the complementary sector. As a Möbius bijection, ζ maps the whole lens onto that sector.
Squaring maps this sector bijectively onto the right half-plane: arguments double into (−π/2,π/2), and the inverse is the unique square root with argument in (−π/4,π/4). Thus
F(z)=(z−i1−z​)2maps A conformally onto {Rew>0}.​
(41)
The derivative never vanishes inside the lens: ζ and its derivative are nonzero there. Finally use the Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk w↦(w−1)/(w+1). Since ∣w−1∣<∣w+1∣ precisely when Rew>0, it maps the right half-plane bijectively to the unit disc. The desired second map is
G(z)=(1−z)2+(z−i)2(1−z)2−(z−i)2​.​
(42)
The denominator cannot vanish in the lens because F(z) has positive real part. The maps send the centre-line test point (1+i)/2 to one and zero respectively.

The expectation value of an observable is the mean outcome of repeated measurements on identically prepared systems in the normalized state. For a self-adjoint Q it is real and equals ⟨ψ,Qψ⟩. Assume the state belongs to the domains needed for the second moments and the commutator below, so all these quantities exist.
For real λ, positivity of the squared norm gives
0≤∥(Q+iλP)ψ∥2=⟨Q2⟩+λ2⟨P2⟩+iλ⟨[Q,P]⟩.
(50)
The commutator expectation is purely imaginary, so this is a real quadratic in λ. If ⟨P2⟩>0, its minimum is nonnegative exactly when its discriminant is nonpositive. If ⟨P2⟩=0, nonnegativity for all real λ forces the linear coefficient to vanish, yielding the same conclusion. Thus
⟨Q2⟩⟨P2⟩≥41​∣⟨[Q,P]⟩∣2.​
(51)
Replace the observables by their centred versions Q−⟨Q⟩I, P−⟨P⟩I. Their commutator is unchanged, and their second moments are the variances (ΔQ)2,(ΔP)2. Taking square roots gives the Robertson uncertainty principle
ΔQΔP≥21​∣⟨[Q,P]⟩∣.​
(52)
For the quantum harmonic oscillator, p=−iℏ∂x​ gives [x,p]ψ=iℏψ. The uncentred inequality above consequently gives ⟨x2⟩⟨p2⟩≥ℏ2/4. The arithmetic-geometric mean inequality now yields
⟨H⟩=2m⟨p2⟩​+2mω2⟨x2⟩​≥ω⟨x2⟩⟨p2⟩​≥21​ℏω​.
(53)
This is a bound for every normalized state of finite oscillator energy, not just an eigenstate. It is attained by the centred Gaussian ground state, for which ⟨p2⟩=m2ω2⟨x2⟩ and the uncertainty product equals ℏ/2.
