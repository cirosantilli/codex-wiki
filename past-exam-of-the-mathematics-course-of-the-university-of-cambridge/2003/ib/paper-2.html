Differentiability at (a,b) means that there is a linear map L:R2→R such that
f(a+h,b+k)−f(a,b)=L(h,k)+o(h2+k2​).
(1)
Necessarily L(h,k)=fx​(a,b)h+fy​(a,b)k when those partial derivatives exist. Under the stated assumptions, apply the one-variable mean value theorem separately to the two coordinate increments:
f(a+h,b+k)−f(a,b)=hfx​(a+θh,b+k)+kfy​(a,b+ηk),
(2)
for some intermediate θ,η∈(0,1), omitting a term if its increment is zero. Existence of the partial derivatives in a neighborhood supplies the differentiability along each segment required for that theorem. Their continuity at (a,b) makes the difference from fx​(a,b)h+fy​(a,b)k bounded by (∣h∣+∣k∣)ε(h,k), where ε(h,k)→0. Since ∣h∣+∣k∣≤2​h2+k2​, this is the required little-o remainder and proves Fréchet differentiability.
For the particular function, f(h,0)=f(0,k)=0, so the defining difference quotients give fx​(0,0)=fy​(0,0)=0. However f(t,t)=1/2 for every t=0. It is not continuous at the origin, and therefore not differentiable there. This illustrates why existence of partial derivatives alone is insufficient.

For the cofinite topology τ1​, the empty set and N are included. A nonempty union of cofinite sets is cofinite because its complement is contained in the finite complement of any one member. A finite intersection is cofinite because its complement is a finite union of finite sets; intersections involving the empty set are empty. This proves all topology axioms.
For the tail topology on the natural numbers τ2​, N=I1​. Any nonempty collection of tails has a smallest starting index, so its union is that tail; a finite intersection of tails is the tail with largest starting index. Empty sets and empty-family conventions supply the remaining cases. Thus τ2​ is also a topology.
We classify continuous maps between cofinite and tail topologies. Continuity is equivalent to
Ek​={n:f(n)≥k} being empty or cofinite for every k.
(8)
If f has unbounded image, each Ek​ is nonempty, hence cofinite. For every threshold k, eventually f(n)≥k, which is exactly f(n)→∞. If the image is bounded, it is a nonempty finite subset of N and has a maximum N. Then EN​ is nonempty and cofinite, but f(n)≤N always, so f(n)=N except at finitely many indices. These exhaust the two possibilities and prove necessity. Their sufficiency is checked separately below.

The set Y is path-connected as the continuous image of [1,∞), hence connected. Its closure is Y together with the unit circle. Indeed g(k+θ/(2π))→eiθ as k→∞, and any convergent sequence of spiral points either has bounded parameters, giving a point of Y, or has parameters tending to infinity along a subsequence, giving radius one. The closure of a connected set is connected: a separation of its closure would induce a separation of the dense original set, with neither open part missing that set. The closed disk is connected and meets this closure in the unit circle; their union Y∪D is therefore connected.
To show it is not path-connected, suppose a path α:[0,1]→Y∪D starts in Y and ends in the disk. Let u∗​ be its first contact with the disk. The disk is closed, so this first contact exists, and u∗​>0. Before it, the path belongs to Y and has a continuous uniquely determined parameter
t(u)=∣α(u)∣−11​,α(u)=g(t(u)).
(36)

This contradicts continuity at u∗​. Thus no path joins the spiral to the disk, proving failure of path-connectedness for the spiral accumulating on a circle.
