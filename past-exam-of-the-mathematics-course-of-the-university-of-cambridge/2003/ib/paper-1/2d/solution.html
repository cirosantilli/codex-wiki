For positive differentiable speed c(y) and a smooth ray written as a graph y(x), the Fermat principle minimizes travel time
T[y]=∫x0​x1​​c(y)1+y′2​​dx.
(1)
A variation vanishing at the endpoints gives the Euler-Lagrange equation for F=1+y′2​/c. Its derivatives are Fy​=−c′1+y′2​/c2 and Fy′​=y′/(c1+y′2​). Thus
dxd​Fy′​−Fy​=c(1+y′2)3/2y′′​+c21+y′2​c′​=0,

and multiplying by c2(1+y′2)3/2 gives
c(y)y′′+c′(y)(1+y′2)=0.​
(3)
The displayed differential equation requires differentiability, beyond mere continuity of c. For constant positive speed it reduces to y′′=0, so rays are straight lines; a straight segment minimizes travel time because it has the shortest Euclidean length.
For c(y)=y in the positive-height region, the Beltrami identity gives F−y′Fy′​=1/[y1+y′2​]=1/R. Hence y2(1+y′2)=R2, whose nonvertical solutions are (x−h)2+y2=R2. Formally imposing the given boundary points fixes h=1/2, R=1/2, and gives the ray in a linear-speed medium
y(x)=x(1−x)​,0<x<1.​
(4)
It is the upper semicircle of diameter one, with vertical limiting tangents at its endpoints.
There is a necessary endpoint qualification. The speed is zero at the two stated endpoints. For any rectifiable path entering positive height, ds/y≥∣dy∣/y, and its travel time diverges logarithmically on leaving or approaching height zero. Thus the semicircle is the formal interior ray, but there is no finite-time minimizer between the zero-speed endpoints. For endpoints at height ϵ>0 the circular ray has radius 1/4+ϵ2​ and finite travel time 2arsinh(1/(2ϵ)), which tends to infinity as ϵ↓0. This explains the intended semicircle as a limiting hyperbolic geodesic, without asserting finite travel time where c=0.
