Use the Half-range Fourier transform and the finite-time spectral boundary transforms
q​(k,t)=∫0∞​e−ikxq(x,t)dx,Q0​(k)=q​(k,0),Gj​(k,t)=∫0t​ew(k)sgj​(s)ds,
(3)
where gj​=∂xj​q(0,⋅) for j=0,1,2. The spatial transforms are holomorphic in Imk<0, while these finite-time transforms are entire functions. For now g2​ is an unknown boundary trace. Integrating the local relation over the half-strip, using spatial decay at infinity, gives the global relation
ew(k)tq​(k,t)=Q0​(k)+F(k,t),F=(1+k2)G0​−ikG1​−G2​.
(4)
The boundary sign follows from integrating the flux derivative: its contribution is minus the flux at zero.
Fourier inversion first gives the initial real-line integral plus a real-line integral of F. To put the boundary contribution on its useful complex contour, define
D+​={k=u+iv:v>0, Rew(k)<0}={u+iv:0<v<1+3u2​}.
(5)
Orient its full boundary with D+​ on the left. It consists of the real axis from left to right and the upper curve C:k=u+i1+3u2​ from right to left. The real-axis piece must not be discarded for this dispersion. The contour integration representation is
q(x,t)=2π1​∫R​eikx−w(k)tQ0​(k)dk+2π1​∫∂D+​​eikx−w(k)tF(k,t)dk.​
(6)
To justify the replacement, the integral of eikx−wtF along C is zero by closing above C. In that region Rew≥0, and each boundary term is an integral of e−w(t−s) over 0≤s≤t times a polynomial in k. This has no growing temporal exponential there; eikx suppresses the upper closing arc for x>0. The Cauchy integral theorem then applies. The figure shows the full contour and, separately, the sectors used in the nonlinear problem.
One may replace F(k,t) by F(k,T) for any finite T≥t. Their difference is a future-time integral over t<s<T; its factor ew(s−t) decays inside D+​, so its integral around ∂D+​ vanishes. All contour formulas are understood as limits of truncated contours, with standard small displacements if a boundary transform needs an Abel limit. Compatibility of the initial and boundary data controls the corner behavior. This is the two-trace half-line cubic dispersion representation.

For asymptotic analysis it is useful to separate the spectral density from the observation time. For a fixed horizon T≥t, H(k,T) already has this property. If the boundary traces extend to all positive times with suitable growth, their one-sided Laplace transforms give the infinite-horizon version
Gj∞​(k)=∫0∞​ew(k)sgj​(s)ds=Lgj​(−w(k)),H∞​=−Q0​(ν)+(k2−ν2)Lg0​(−w)−i(k−ν)Lg1​(−w).
(12)
For bounded traces these integrals converge in D+​; boundary values are taken by an Abel limit where needed. The finite-horizon formula permits this replacement: the tail from t to infinity contributes an analytic, decaying integrand with zero integral around ∂D+​. The resulting spectral functions are independent of t.
They can be made particularly explicit on the two contour pieces. On the real axis and upper curve, respectively,
νR​(k)=−2k​−2i​3k2+4​,νC​(k)=k.
(13)
The second identity follows because k3+k is real on C: the remaining roots are k and −2Rek. Therefore rewrite the solution as
q=2π1​∫R​eikx−i(k+k3)t[Q0​(k)+H∞,R​(k)]dk+2π1​∫C​eikx−i(k+k3)tH∞,C​(k)dk.
(14)
This isolates all large-time dependence in the exponential, allowing the stationary phase method or the method of steepest descent. Along x/t=ξ, the phase is i[kξ−k−k3], and its stationary points satisfy 3k2=ξ−1. Thus the real saddles coalesce at ξ=1; any poles arising from the boundary Laplace transforms also have to be retained during contour deformation.
The source specifies smooth boundary traces, not their large-time decay or growth. Accordingly a universal numerical long-time profile cannot be inferred. The infinite-horizon formula requires the stated additional transform hypothesis; for more general data the finite-horizon density H(k,T) remains valid, or an appropriately shifted Laplace contour must be used. This qualification does not leave an unknown boundary trace in the representation.

The two differentiated linear integral equations have now supplied both equations of the Lax pair. To verify the nonlinear consequence explicitly, put D=∂x​ and take the operator commutator in the order [N,M]=NM−MN. The product rule gives
[N,M]=−3qx​D2+3ikqx​D+(qt​+qxxx​+3qqx​)=−3qx​M+(qt​+qxxx​+6qqx​).
(22)
For example, [D3,q]=3qx​D2+3qxx​D+qxxx​ and [3qD,D2]=−6qx​D2−3qxx​D, which explain the cancellations. Acting on φ yields
(qt​+qxxx​+6qqx​)φ(k,x,t)=0.
(23)
The integral equation has the large-real-k normalization φ/Ek​=1+O(k−1) under the stated contour regularity, so its eigenfunction is not zero for every k at a fixed (x,t). Consequently qt​+qxxx​+6qqx​=0.
Thus a time-independent contour and measure determine φ through a linear spectral integral equation; q=−∂x​∫L​φdλ then reconstructs the nonlinear solution. This is the asserted linearisation. It does not assert that sums of reconstructed nonlinear solutions are again solutions, and it depends on the given uniqueness and differentiability hypotheses.

Take the spectral measure to have n point masses at kj​=iκj​, with distinct κj​>0 and positive weights cj​. Set
Ej​=e−κj​x+κj3​t,Φj​=φ(iκj​,x,t).
(24)
Evaluating the linear integral equation at these points gives the explicit algebraic system and reconstruction
Φi​+j=1∑n​κi​+κj​cj​Ei​​Φj​=Ei​,q=−∂x​j=1∑n​cj​Φj​.​
(25)
The sign is fixed by i/(iκi​+iκj​)=1/(κi​+κj​). No continuum integration remains.
There is also a useful determinant form. Define Aij​=cj​Ei​/(κi​+κj​), B=I+A, τ=detB and Λ=diag(κ1​,…,κn​). Then Ax​=−ΛA and ΛA+AΛ=EcT. Using the derivative of a determinant and cyclicity of the trace,
∂x​logτ=−tr(B−1ΛA)=−21​tr[B−1(ΛA+AΛ)]=−21​cTB−1E.
(26)
Hence the finite-rank KdV dressing determinant gives
q=2∂x2​logτ.​
(27)
Positivity ensures a regular real solution: the Cauchy matrix Cij​=1/(κi​+κj​) is the Gram matrix of the independent functions e−κi​s on s>0, so it is positive definite. The matrix A=diag(E)Cdiag(c) is diagonally similar to diag(cE​)Cdiag(cE​). Its eigenvalues are positive, and τ>0. The resulting reflectionless multisoliton solution has n distinct scales and velocities; the weights fix their positions. Arbitrary signed or complex weights need not give a nonsingular real solution.

Put θ=kx−4k3t. Conjugation by eiθσ3​ is the meaning of eiθσ3​. The normalized eigenfunctions have the Volterra integral equation representation
μj​=I+∫γj​​ei[k(x−x′)−4k3(t−t′)]σ3​[Qμj​dx′+Q​μj​dt′].
(31)
Choose γ1​ from (0,T) down the time boundary and then right, γ2​ from (0,0) up the time boundary and then right, and γ3​ horizontally from infinity. Compatibility of the Lax pair makes the resulting construction path-independent.
Let a=Imk and b=Imk3. The nontrivial exponential in column one has modulus e2a(x−x′)−8b(t−t′); column two has its reciprocal. For γ1​, x−x′≥0 and t−t′≤0, giving signs a,b≤0 for column one and a,b≥0 for column two. For γ2​, both differences are nonnegative, giving (a≤0,b≥0) and (a≥0,b≤0). For γ3​, x−x′≤0 and the time difference is zero, giving upper and lower half-planes, respectively.
Define D1​:(a>0,b>0), D2​:(a>0,b<0), D3​:(a<0,b>0) and D4​:(a<0,b<0). The bounded analytic column domains are therefore
μ1​:(D4​,D1​),μ2​:(D3​,D2​),μ3​:(C+​,C−​).​
(32)
These are the Volterra analyticity sectors for reverse-dispersion mKdV, shown in the right panel of the figure. D1​ is the pair of upper outer sectors, D2​ the upper middle sector, D3​ the lower middle sector and D4​ the lower outer pair. Their boundaries are the six rays argk=mπ/3. The Volterra integral equation converges and differentiates analytically where these kernels are bounded, assuming the usual sufficiently integrable spatial decay and smoothness needed by these normalizations. If decay is interpreted as merely pointwise convergence to zero without an integrability condition, existence and the stated large-k normalization need additional hypotheses. A finite-path eigenfunction may be entire for fixed coordinates, but boundedness for large k holds in the displayed domains; entire continuation does not enlarge those bounded sectors.

Ψx​=(ikσ3​+Q)Ψ,Ψt​=(−4ik3σ3​+Q​)Ψ.

Take the linear limit and write gj​(t)=∂xj​q(0,t). The spatial and temporal Volterra integral equations give, to first order,
b(k)=−Q0​(2k),B(k)=−∫0T​e8ik3s[g2​(s)+2ikg1​(s)−4k2g0​(s)]ds,
(48)
while a=A=1 to first order and c(k,T)=−q​(2k,T). The minus sign in b comes from the spatial path running from infinity to zero. For B, the inverse in S=e4ik3Tσ3​μ2​(0,T)−1 supplies the minus sign; its conjugation cancels the final-time exponential in the temporal kernel. Substitution in the global relation therefore yields
e8ik3Tq​(2k,T)=Q0​(2k)+4k2G0​−2ikG1​−G2​,Gj​=∫0T​e8ik3sgj​(s)ds.
(49)
This is the backward-sign Airy half-line global relation, because the linear limit is qt​−qxxx​=0, independently of λ.
Its constraint is especially transparent after a time Laplace transform. Let Rep>0, choose the principal root r=p1/3 with positive real part, and set k=−ir/2 in the lower transform half-plane. Then 8ik3=−p. Taking the infinite-horizon Laplace limit for a solution of sufficient exponential order gives
g​2​(p)=Q0​(−ir)−rg​1​(p)−r2g​0​(p).​
(50)
This proves that the second-derivative trace is fixed by the initial data and two boundary traces; it is not a third freely prescribable datum. Finite-horizon versions retain the terminal transform and express the same relation on 0<t<T.
To see that two are necessary, rather than merely sufficient to eliminate a transform, the homogeneous transformed spatial equation is ∂x3​u−pu=0. Its roots are r, e2πi/3r, and e4πi/3r. Since −π/6<argr<π/6, exactly two roots have negative real part. Decay at infinity retains two independent spatial modes. Their value/first-derivative boundary matrix has determinant equal to the difference of those two roots, which is nonzero. Thus prescribed g0​,g1​ fix both modes; one condition leaves a free mode, while three arbitrary traces overdetermine them.
Two independent admissible boundary conditions are needed at x=0, for example q(0,t) and qx​(0,t). This is the two boundary traces for reverse-dispersion Airy flow count and hence the perturbative count for the nonlinear modified Korteweg-De Vries equation. The argument determines the small-norm boundary count; it does not claim a global nonlinear existence theorem for arbitrary large boundary data or arbitrary incompatible choices of traces.
