A finite-field Kakeya set, also called a finite-field Besicovitch set, is a subset containing a complete affine line in a vector space a+Fp​v in every nonzero direction v, with scalar multiples counted as the same direction. The line's translate may depend on the direction.
For odd p, take the union of the lines y=mx−m2/4, m∈Fp​. Completing the square gives x2−y=(m/2−x)2. Thus that union is exactly K0​={(x,y):x2−y is a square, including zero}. For each fixed x there are (p+1)/2 such y, so ∣K0​∣=p(p+1)/2. It contains every nonvertical slope. Add the vertical line x=0, whose overlap with K0​ has (p+1)/2 points. The tangent-line finite-field Kakeya construction consequently has
∣K∣=2p2+2p−1​=21​p2(1+o(1)).​
(6)
The prime two is irrelevant to this asymptotic statement and can be handled by taking its whole plane.
For the high-dimensional lower bound, we give a direct polynomial method in combinatorics proof, which yields a stronger estimate. Suppose a finite-field Kakeya set K⊆Fpn​ has fewer than (np+n−1​) points. That binomial coefficient is the dimension of a bounded-total-degree polynomial space of polynomials of total degree at most p−1. Evaluation at the points of K imposes fewer linear conditions than this dimension. By the rank-nullity theorem, there is a nonzero polynomial P of total degree d≤p−1 vanishing on K.
For every nonzero v choose the line av​+tv⊆K. The polynomial P(av​+tv) in t has degree at most d<p and vanishes at all p field elements, so it vanishes identically. Its coefficient of td is the leading homogeneous polynomial part Pd​(v). Thus Pd​(v)=0 for every nonzero v. The case d=0 is already impossible for a nonzero constant vanishing on a nonempty set; for d>0, the absence of a constant term in this homogeneous polynomial also gives Pd​(0)=0.
A polynomial of degree less than p in each variable cannot vanish at every point of Fpn​ unless it is zero. To see this, induct on n: fix the first n−1 variables, apply the one-variable root bound in the last variable, then apply the induction hypothesis to each resulting coefficient polynomial. This forces Pd​=0, contradicting the choice of the leading part. We have proved the finite-field Kakeya polynomial bound
∣K∣≥(np+n−1​)≥n!pn​.
(7)
At n=15 this implies the required bound with an explicit absolute constant:
∣K∣≥15!p15​≥15!p9​,c=1/15!.​
(8)
The argument is valid in every prime characteristic and does not invoke the lower bound as an unproved theorem.
