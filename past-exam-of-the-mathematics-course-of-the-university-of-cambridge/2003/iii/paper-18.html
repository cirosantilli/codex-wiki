If f:A→B has a section g:B→A, functoriality gives Hf​Hg​=1HB​​. Thus Hf​ is a split epimorphism and in particular an epimorphism.

A terminal object and pullbacks in a category give a binary product X×Y by pulling back X→1←Y. Repeating this gives every finite product, with the empty product supplied by the terminal object. The preceding parts then construct equalizers of arbitrary parallel arrows.
For a diagram D:J→C with finitely many objects and arrows, form P=∏j∈ObJ​D(j) and Q=∏a:j→k​D(k). Define r,s:P→Q by ra​=D(a)πj​ and sa​=πk​. Their equalizer e:L→P has projections πj​e satisfying every cone equation. Conversely a categorical cone supplies a unique map to P, and its cone equations say that this map equalizes r,s, hence factors uniquely through L. Thus L is the categorical limit. This proves a terminal object and all pullbacks give all finite limits, including the empty diagram.

The categorical commutation of iterated categorical limits theorem is the following. For small categories I,J and a two-variable functor F:I×J→D, if the two collections of inner limits and their outer limits exist, then there are canonical isomorphisms
jlim​ilim​F(i,j)≅I×Jlim​F≅ilim​jlim​F(i,j).​
(7)
In particular all these limits exist if D has all I-shaped and all J-shaped limits. The isomorphisms are natural in F and uniquely characterized by preserving every projection to F(i,j).
Part (iii) proves that each iterated limit represents the functor of cones on the product diagram, with the same flattened projections. Two such representations have unique inverse cone-compatible arrows, proving the isomorphisms. For a natural transformation F→F′, the two composites in any naturality square have the same product-diagram projections, hence agree by limit uniqueness. This proves naturality as well. Completeness of D is a convenient sufficient hypothesis, rather than a necessary one for this individual diagram.

Let T:Cop×C→D. An end of a functor is an object E with maps eC​:E→T(C,C) satisfying T(1C​,f)eC​=T(f,1D​)eD​ for every f:C→D, universal among all such families. Thus every compatible family with vertex A factors uniquely through E. Write it as ∫C​T(C,C). These are the wedge equations for a dinatural transformation from a constant functor to T.
Dually, a coend of a functor is an object Q with maps iC​:T(C,C)→Q satisfying iC​T(f,1C​)=iD​T(1D​,f) as maps from T(D,C), universal among compatible families with a common target. Write it as ∫CT(C,C). These are cowedge equations, and every cowedge to A factors uniquely through Q.
For small C, if the needed products and equalizers exist, the end is the equalizer of ∏C​T(C,C)⇉∏f:C→D​T(C,D). If the needed coproducts and coequalizers exist, the coend is the coequalizer of ∐f:C→D​T(D,C)⇉∐C​T(C,C). The two arrows in each construction are precisely the two sides of the compatibility equation. This explains both the mixed variance and the universal property, rather than treating the integral notation as an ordinary numerical integral.

A presheaf on 0→1 is an arrow r:B=X(1)→A=X(0) in sets. A morphism of presheaves is a commuting square. Define
Π(r)=A,Γ(r)=B,Δ(S)=(S1​S),∇(S)=(S→1).
(16)
A commuting square from r to ΔS is determined by its map A→S, while a square from ΔS to r is determined by its map S→B. A square from r to ∇S is also determined by its map B→S, because the other component is the unique map A→1. Therefore these Hom-set bijections establish the adjoint chain for evaluation in an arrow category:
Π⊣Δ⊣Γ⊣∇.​
(17)
There is a further left adjoint to Π: Λ(S)=(∅→S). A square ΛS→r is exactly a map S→A, proving Λ⊣Π.
There is no right adjoint to ∇. If there were, ∇ would be a left adjoint and preserve the initial object. But it sends the empty set to (∅→1), whereas the initial object of this arrow category is (∅→∅). They are not isomorphic. Hence Π does have a left adjoint, while ∇ has no right adjoint.

Let D(S) be the discrete category on S, and I(S) the indiscrete category, with exactly one morphism between each ordered pair of objects. A functor D(S)→A is precisely a map S→O(A); a functor A→I(S) is precisely a map O(A)→S. Thus D⊣O⊣I.
Let C(A)=π0​A be the set of connected components, where objects are related by finite zigzags of morphisms in either direction. A functor A→D(S) must take the endpoints of every morphism to the same point, so it is exactly a map π0​A→S. This gives the adjoint chain for the objects of a category
C⊣D⊣O⊣I.​
(18)
It cannot extend to the left. The two functors from the terminal category to I({0,1}) selecting distinct objects have empty equalizer in Cat. Applying C gives two identical maps between singleton sets, whose equalizer is a singleton. Thus C does not preserve equalizers and cannot be a right adjoint.
It cannot extend to the right either. The coproduct of two copies of I(1) in Cat is the discrete two-object category. But I(1⨿1) is the indiscrete two-object category, with morphisms between its distinct objects. They are not isomorphic, or even equivalent. Hence I does not preserve binary coproducts and cannot be a left adjoint. Neither end of this chain has a further adjoint.

We prove both versions. In the general adjoint functor theorem, let G:D→C be a functor between locally small categories, with D complete. Then G has a left adjoint if and only if it preserves small limits and satisfies the solution-set condition: for each C∈C there is a set of arrows ui​:C→GDi​ such that every arrow u:C→GD factors as G(h)ui​ for some i and h:Di​→D.
First prove the initial-object lemma for complete categories with a weakly initial set. Let A be locally small and complete, and have a small weakly initial family (Wi​). Its product W is weakly initial: a map Wi​→A can be composed with the product projection. Take e:E→W to be the simultaneous equalizer of every endomorphism of W and its identity. This is a small limit since A(W,W) is a set. Maps E→A exist by composing e with a weakly initial map W→A.
For uniqueness, take any a,b:E→A and their equalizer j:Z→E. Weak initiality of W gives t:W→Z. The endomorphism ejt of W fixes e by its defining equalizer property, so ejte=e. Since e is monic, jte=1E​. Thus j is both monic and split epic, hence invertible, forcing a=b. Therefore E is initial. This proves the lemma without an unproved initial-object assertion.
Now fix C∈C. The comma category (C↓G) is locally small. It is complete: take the limit of the underlying diagram in D, use preservation by G to factor the compatible arrows from C, and obtain the universal comma cone. The solution set is exactly a small weakly initial family in this comma category. The lemma provides its initial object (FC,ηC​). Its universal property is the natural bijection D(FC,D)≅C(C,GD). For v:C→C′, initiality gives the unique Fv satisfying G(Fv)ηC​=ηC′​v; uniqueness proves identities and composition. Thus these objects form a left adjoint. Conversely a left adjoint provides the singleton solution set consisting of its unit arrow, and a right adjoint preserves limits: the adjunction bijections identify cones into the image diagram with cones from the corresponding left-adjoint object into the original diagram. This proves both directions of the general theorem.
The limit form of the special adjoint functor theorem assumes instead that D is complete, locally small and well-powered, and has a small cogenerating family (Qi​). For any locally small target, a functor G:D→C has a left adjoint exactly when it preserves small limits. Necessity is already proved. For sufficiency we construct a solution set, so that the general theorem applies.
Fix C. Given u:C→GD, consider all pairs h,k:D→Qi​ with G(h)u=G(k)u. There is only a set of these pairs. Intersect their equalizers to obtain a monomorphism m:D′→D. Since G preserves this limit and u equalizes the images of all pairs, it factors uniquely as u=G(m)u′ for u′:C→GD′.
For each realized pair (i,v) with v=G(h)u:C→GQi​, choose one such h. Any other k with the same pair restricts to the same map on D′. The restricted chosen maps jointly separate morphisms into D′: if two such morphisms become equal after all chosen maps, they become equal after every hm, and the cogenerating family then makes their composites with m equal; monicity of m cancels it. Hence the resulting map
D′⟶∏(i,v)∈J​Qi​
(19)
is monic, where J is a subset of the fixed set ∐i​C(C,GQi​).
There is only a set of possible J, a set of subobjects of each corresponding product by well-poweredness, and a set of arrows from C into G of each chosen subobject representative by local smallness of C. Collect all these representative comma objects. The factorization just constructed shows that they form a weakly initial set in (C↓G). This is the cogenerator bound for comma-category solution sets, with realized subsets avoiding any assumption that all proposed maps to cogenerators have lifts. The general theorem now gives the left adjoint and proves the special theorem.

μTη=1T​=μηT​,μTμ=μμT​.

Thus a monadic functor creates limits in the equivalence-invariant sense. If monadicity uses a comparison isomorphism over the base, the lift is literally on the prescribed underlying cone, proving strict creation as well.
A qualification is necessary if equivalence is used to define monadicity but literal unique lifting is demanded. Let C be the indiscrete category on two objects 0,1, let D be the terminal category, and let G select 0. The unique F:C→D is left adjoint to G, and the comparison to the induced algebra category is an equivalence: the two algebra objects have exactly one map between each pair. Yet the chosen terminal cone with vertex 1 in C has no literal lift, since the only object in the image of G is 0. The creation of limits up to isomorphism conclusion holds, because 0 and 1 are uniquely isomorphic. This distinguishes the two conventions rather than claiming an invalid strict conclusion from equivalence alone.

A monoidal category consists of a category C, a bifunctor ⊗:C×C→C, a unit object I, and natural isomorphisms aX,Y,Z​:(X⊗Y)⊗Z→X⊗(Y⊗Z), lX​:I⊗X→X and rX​:X⊗I→X. They satisfy the pentagon identity for a monoidal category
aW,X,Y⊗Z​aW⊗X,Y,Z​=(1W​⊗aX,Y,Z​)aW,X⊗Y,Z​(aW,X,Y​⊗1Z​)
(28)
and the triangle identity for a monoidal category (1X​⊗lY​)aX,I,Y​=rX​⊗1Y​. These axioms make different coherent rebracketings and unit removals agree.
It defines a bicategory with one object ∗. The hom-category B(∗,∗) is C; objects of C are its 1-cells and morphisms of C its 2-cells. Horizontal composition is the tensor bifunctor, the identity 1-cell is I, and the associator and unitors are the bicategorical coherence 2-cells. Vertical composition is composition of morphisms in C, and the interchange law follows from bifunctoriality of ⊗. Conversely the hom-category of any one-object bicategory has exactly this monoidal structure. Thus a monoidal category is precisely a one-object bicategory, not necessarily a strict one-object 2-category.

The Category of sets is monoidal under Cartesian product, with any singleton as unit. The tensor of maps is (f×g)(x,y)=(f(x),g(y)), the associator sends ((x,y),z) to (x,(y,z)), and the left and right unitors discard the singleton coordinate. These are natural bijections. Both pentagon paths send a nested quadruple to the same rebracketed quadruple, and both triangle paths discard the same singleton coordinate, verifying the axioms directly.
This is not the only structure. Disjoint union X⨿Y is another tensor bifunctor, with empty-set unit. Its associator changes parentheses while retaining the tag indicating the original summand, and its unitors remove an empty summand. Every coherence path preserves both the original tag and the element, so again the pentagon and triangle hold.
These product and coproduct monoidal structures on sets are distinct even up to monoidal equivalence. Any equivalence of the underlying category preserves initial and terminal objects up to isomorphism. It therefore cannot carry the empty-set unit of the coproduct structure to the singleton unit of the product structure. Hence Set has at least these two inequivalent monoidal structures.
