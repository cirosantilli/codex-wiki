Use the adiabatic form of ideal magnetohydrodynamics: heat conduction and external heating are absent, so ρDe/Dt=−p∇⋅u. Infinite electrical conductivity and zero viscosity alone would not remove the thermal source terms on the cover page. If those terms are retained, the energy equation below must acquire ∇⋅(λ∇T)+ϵ on its right-hand side.
Expanding the continuity equation gives its conservative form ∂t​ρ+∇⋅(ρu)=0. Using it to expand the conservative momentum derivative gives
∂t​(ρu)+∇⋅(ρuu)=ρDtDu​.
(17)
For ∇⋅B=0, the Lorentz force density is the divergence of the magnetic Maxwell stress tensor:
j×B=μ0​1​[(B⋅∇)B−∇(B2/2)]=∇⋅T,T=μ0​1​(BB−21​B2I).
(18)
Combining these identities with momentum balance proves
∂t​(ρu)+∇⋅(ρuu+pI−T)=−ρ∇Φ.​
(19)
The dyadic stress contains both magnetic tension and magnetic pressure; the sign in the flux is fixed by moving the magnetic force to the left.
For the energy equation, first dot momentum balance with u and add the adiabatic internal-energy equation. The continuity equation converts their material derivatives into conservative derivatives, while u⋅∇p+p∇⋅u=∇⋅(pu). The result is
∂t​(21​ρu2+ρe)+∇⋅[u(21​ρu2+ρe+p)]=−ρu⋅∇Φ+u⋅(j×B).
(20)
The ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation gives the magnetic counterpart. In fact,
∂t​2μ0​B2​​=μ0​1​B⋅∇×(u×B)=μ0​1​∇⋅[(u×B)×B]−u⋅(j×B).​
(21)
Thus the outward Poynting vector is (B×u)×B/μ0​, and magnetic work cancels the fluid work. Finally, the continuity equation implies
∂t​(ρΦ)+∇⋅(ρΦu)=ρ∂t​Φ+ρu⋅∇Φ.
(22)
Adding all three identities gives ideal magnetohydrodynamic energy conservation in the required local form:
∂t​E+∇⋅F=ρ∂t​Φ,​
(23)
where
EF​=21​ρu2+ρe+ρΦ+2μ0​B2​,=ρu(e+ρp​+21​u2+Φ)+μ0​1​(B×u)×B.​
(24)
The magnetic energy coefficient is 1/(2μ0​), as in the original PDF. For a self-consistent, time-dependent gravitational field this local balance should not be confused with a global self-gravitating energy integral: the latter uses gravitational field energy, or the familiar one-half factor in ∫ρΦ after the field is eliminated.
Work in the shock frame of a thin planar discontinuity, with normal speed u>0. A smooth gravitational potential has no finite jump through a vanishingly thin front; its advected contribution cancels using the common mass flux. Integrating the conservation laws gives constant normal mass, momentum and energy fluxes, together with the normal-field and tangential-electric-field conditions of the ideal magnetohydrodynamic shock conditions.
For a parallel magnetohydrodynamic shock, B=Bn​n and u=un. The magnetic solenoidal constraint makes Bn​ the same on both sides. The normal momentum flux is ρu2+p−Bn2​/(2μ0​); its magnetic term is therefore identical upstream and downstream. The Poynting vector is zero because u×B=0. Hence the nontrivial jumps are exactly the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas:
ρ1​u1​=ρ2​u2​,ρ1​u12​+p1​=ρ2​u22​+p2​,21​u12​+h1​=21​u22​+h2​,​
(25)
with specific enthalpy h=γp/[(γ−1)ρ]. Physically, motion along the straight field neither bends it nor compresses its transverse flux. The field consequently provides no changed stress or energy flux in this purely parallel geometry. This conclusion does not apply to a shock that develops a transverse magnetic field.
For a perpendicular magnetohydrodynamic shock, the magnetic field is tangential and the velocity normal. Steady induction makes uB continuous, while the continuity equation makes ρu continuous. Dividing gives the magnetic flux freezing relation
ρ1​B1​​=ρ2​B2​​.​
(26)
Put x=ρ2​/ρ1​, so u2​=u1​/x and B2​=xB1​. Constant normal momentum flux now reads ρu2+p+B2/(2μ0​). Substitution gives
p2​=p1​+ρ1​u12​(1−x1​)+2μ0​B12​​(1−x2).​
(27)
The magnetic correction is negative for compression: part of the incoming ram pressure builds magnetic pressure instead of thermal pressure.
Here the Poynting vector has normal component uB2/μ0​. Divide the constant energy flux by the nonzero mass flux to obtain
21​u12​+(γ−1)ρ1​γp1​​+μ0​ρ1​B12​​=21​u22​+(γ−1)ρ2​γp2​​+μ0​ρ2​B22​​.
(28)
With vA12​=B12​/(μ0​ρ1​), this gives the second expression for downstream pressure:
p2​=xp1​+γγ−1​ρ1​x[21​u12​(1−x21​)+vA12​(1−x)].​
(29)
In this expression x is the only downstream unknown. Equating the two pressure expressions and multiplying by 2γx/ρ1​ yields
(x−1)F(x)=0,
(30)
up to an overall minus sign, where c12​=γp1​/ρ1​ and
F(x)=(2−γ)vA12​x2+[(γ−1)u12​+2c12​+γvA12​]x−(γ+1)u12​.​
(31)
The discarded factor x=1 is the unchanged state, not a compressive shock. This derives the polynomial for the compression ratio of a perpendicular magnetohydrodynamic shock without omitting its trivial branch.
For the usual physical adiabatic index 1<γ≤2, F is strictly increasing for positive x, has F(0)<0, and has one positive root. Since
F(1)=2(c12​+vA12​−u12​),
(32)
that root is greater than one precisely when u12​>c12​+vA12​. The necessity is not limited to γ≤2: for any γ>1, eliminating u12​ using F(x)=0 gives
ρ1​p2​​=Dc12​[(γ+1)x−(γ−1)]/γ+(γ−1)vA12​(x−1)3/2​,D=(γ+1)−(γ−1)x.
(33)
For x>1, positive downstream pressure requires D>0 when c12​+vA12​>0. Moreover,
u12​−c12​−vA12​=D(x−1){(γ+1)c12​+vA12​[(2−γ)x+γ+1]}​>0.
(34)
The bracket is positive throughout 1<x<(γ+1)/(γ−1), even when γ>2. The exactly cold unmagnetized limit gives the familiar strong-shock upper compression ratio directly. Thus the physical compressive branch requires
u12​>c12​+vA12​.​
(35)
The PDF's final v1​ denotes this same incoming speed u1​. The right side is the squared speed of a perpendicular fast magnetosonic wave: the upstream flow must be super-fast-magnetosonic so that this compressive signal cannot run upstream against the incoming flow. At equality the shock amplitude vanishes into a characteristic disturbance.
