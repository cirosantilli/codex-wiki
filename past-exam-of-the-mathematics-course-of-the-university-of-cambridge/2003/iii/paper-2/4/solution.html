A precise version of the Hilbert-Serre theorem is as follows. Let R=⨁j≥0​Rj​ be a commutative graded ring, generated over an Artinian degree-zero ring R0​ by homogeneous elements y1​,…,ys​ of positive degrees d1​,…,ds​. Let M be a finitely generated graded module, bounded below. Each component has finite module length over R0​. Its Poincare series of a graded module, or Hilbert series, is
PM​(t)=∑j​ℓR0​​(Mj​)tj.
(22)
When R0​ is a field, these lengths are dimensions. The theorem asserts
PM​(t)=∏i=1s​(1−tdi​)q(t)​,q(t)∈Z[t,t−1].​
(23)
For a nonnegatively graded module the numerator can be taken in Z[t]. The finite-generation and finite-length hypotheses specify the meaning of the series; they are part of the theorem's statement.
We prove it by induction on s. Regard M as a finite graded module over S=R0​[Y1​,…,Ys​], with degYi​=di​, acting through the specified generators of R. This polynomial ring is Noetherian by the Hilbert basis theorem and Question 1. When s=0, finitely many homogeneous generators over R0​ mean that only finitely many components are nonzero, so the series is a Laurent polynomial. For the induction step, put y=Ys​, d=ds​, K=(0:M​y) and C=M/yM. Both K and C are finite graded modules; y annihilates them, so they are modules over R0​[Y1​,…,Ys−1​]. The finite generation of K uses Noetherianity of S.
The exact sequence of graded modules
0⟶K(−d)⟶M(−d) y ​M⟶C⟶0
(24)
gives, by additivity of component lengths,
(1−td)PM​(t)=PC​(t)−tdPK​(t).
(25)
Here the shift is defined so that PM(−d)​=tdPM​. By induction both series on the right have denominator ∏i<s​(1−tdi​). Dividing by 1−td proves the result. The annihilator term cannot be omitted unless multiplication by y is injective.
In the standard grading di​=1, the coefficients of (1−t)−s are (s−1j+s−1​). Multiplying by the finite numerator shows that the component dimensions agree with a polynomial for sufficiently large j. Cumulative dimensions have generating function PM​(t)/(1−t) and are also eventually polynomial. This consequence supplies the dimension used for Weyl algebra modules.
Use the Bernstein filtration Bm​An​, in which both coordinate generators and differentiation generators have degree one. The ordered monomial basis of a Weyl algebra gives
grB​An​≅C[x1​,…,xn​,ξ1​,…,ξn​],dimC​Bm​An​=(2nm+2n​).
(26)
Choose a finite-dimensional generating subspace M0​ of a nonzero finitely generated left module M and set Mm​=Bm​An​M0​. This is a good filtration of a module: the symbols of the generators generate its associated graded module over the displayed polynomial ring. The Hilbert-Serre theorem makes dimC​Mm​ an eventual polynomial. Define the Bernstein growth dimension of a Weyl algebra module by
d(M)=deg(dimC​Mm​ for m≫0).​
(27)
The dimension is independent of the chosen finite generating subspace. Each of two finite generating sets is contained in a fixed filtration step for the other, giving Mm​⊆Mm+c′​ and Mm′​⊆Mm+c′​. Such fixed shifts preserve the degree of growth. The same argument applies to any good Bernstein filtration. This dimension agrees with the Gelfand–Kirillov dimension of a module; one may set d(0)=−∞.
Let the nonconstant element D have Bernstein degree d≥1. Since grB​An​ is a domain, the product of two nonzero symbols cannot vanish. Hence
degB​(aD)=degB​a+d(a=0),(An​D)∩Bm​An​=(Bm−d​An​)D.
(28)
Right multiplication by D is injective, using the domain property of An​. Thus the quotient filtration on M=An​/An​D has cumulative dimensions
dimC​Mm​=(2nm+2n​)−(2nm−d+2n​)(m≥d).
(29)
The leading terms of degree 2n cancel, and the leading surviving term is
(2n−1)!d​m2n−1.
(30)
It is nonzero, because d≥1. Therefore
d(An​/An​D)=2n−1.​
(31)
Equivalently the Hilbert series of the associated graded quotient is (1−td)/(1−t)2n. Here d is Bernstein degree, not differential order: multiplication by x1​ has differential order zero but Bernstein degree one. Using the order filtration in this dimension argument would lose the finite-dimensional filtration pieces.
