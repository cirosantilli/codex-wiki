A finite field is a field with finitely many elements. Its characteristic is the least positive integer p for which p⋅1=0; such an integer exists because the additive group is finite. If p=ab with 1<a,b<p, then (a⋅1)(b⋅1)=0 although neither factor is zero, impossible in a field. Hence p is prime, and the multiples of 1 form its prime field Fp​. The whole field is a finite-dimensional vector space over this subfield, say of dimension s≥1, so its cardinality is ps. Thus q=ps and its characteristic is p.
For existence, let K be a splitting field over Fp​ of P(T)=Tps−T. Since P′(T)=−1, it has exactly ps distinct roots. Its root set F contains 0,1 and is closed under addition and multiplication, because iterated Frobenius endomorphism gives

For uniqueness, let L be any field with ps elements. Its nonzero multiplicative group has order ps−1, so Lagrange's theorem gives aps=a for every a∈L, including zero. Hence L is a splitting field of the same polynomial. Here is the embedding argument giving uniqueness, rather than merely invoking it by name. Extend the identity on Fp​ from one root at a time. If an embedding is already defined on E and a new root α has minimal polynomial of an algebraic element h over E, the transported polynomial divides P and therefore has a root b in L. Evaluation at b induces an embedding E[T]/(h)→L, extending the existing embedding to E(α). Repeating for the finitely many roots embeds the constructed field F in L. They have the same finite cardinality, so this embedding is an isomorphism. Thus the field of order ps is unique up to isomorphism.
We prove cyclicity of the multiplicative group, also establishing the more general finite multiplicative subgroup of a field is cyclic result. Let H be a finite subgroup of a field's multiplicative group, and let e be the least common multiple of all element orders. For every prime ℓ dividing e, choose an element whose order has the maximal ℓ-power ℓaℓ​; raising it to the factor of its order coprime to ℓ gives an element of order ℓaℓ​. Since the group is abelian, the product of these elements has order ∏ℓ​ℓaℓ​=e: for commuting elements of coprime orders, an equation (ab)t=1 forces at=b−t into the intersection of their cyclic subgroups, which is trivial, so both orders divide t. Every member of H is a root of Te−1. The Lagrange root bound over a field gives ∣H∣≤e, while the element just constructed has order e and gives e≤∣H∣. Thus equality holds and that element generates H. In particular
