The independence analysis is not justified without further assumptions. Three binary responses from the same subject generally remain correlated after conditioning on baseline covariates and treatment. The fitted independent-Bernoulli likelihood discards that correlation and gives incorrect model-based standard errors and tests if it is present. Positive within-subject correlation commonly makes those errors too small.
There is an important distinction between estimating the mean and estimating uncertainty. If the stated marginal logistic regression is correct and responses are fully observed, the independence score ∑i,j​wij​(Yij​−mij​) still has zero expectation. With independent subjects and regularity, its root can consistently estimate marginal coefficients despite within-subject dependence; a subject-level sandwich covariance matrix is then needed. True independence is a special case in which the original standard errors would be appropriate.
Dropout creates a separate issue. Restricting that score to observed responses need not preserve its zero expectation if observation depends on the response history or unseen depression outcomes. Therefore both point estimates and uncertainty may be wrong. Neither random treatment assignment nor baseline adjustment alone establishes an ignorable missingness mechanism. We need a model matched to the scientific estimand and explicit assumptions about missing data.

Introduce a subject effect Bi​∼N(0,τ2) independently across subjects and independently of treatment and baseline covariates, under the specified model. The population-averaged logistic regression describes P(Yij​=1∣zi​,xi​) after averaging over unmeasured individual heterogeneity. Its intercept is a marginal log odds at reference baseline values and time zero, its treatment coefficient is a population conditional-on-baseline log odds ratio, and its time coefficient is the change in that marginal log odds per month. Time zero is an extrapolation here because the recorded visits begin later; centring time at the first visit would give a more interpretable intercept.
In the random-intercept logistic model, the intercept is instead the conditional log odds for a subject with Bi​=0. The treatment and time coefficients apply at fixed Bi​; the subject's actual intercept is αC​+Bi​. Even with a treatment-independent random intercept, the conditional and marginal coefficients generally differ through noncollapsibility of the odds ratio. Indeed, if η=αC​+ϕC​z+βCT​x+δC​t, marginalization gives
M(η)=EB​[logit−1(η+B)],
(36)
which is not generally a logistic curve with the same linear coefficients. To quantify random-intercept attenuation of marginal logistic slopes, let pB​=logit−1(η+B). Then
M′=E[pB​(1−pB​)]=M(1−M)−Var(pB​),dηdlogitM​=1−M(1−M)Var(pB​)​.
(37)
For a nondegenerate finite random intercept this derivative lies strictly between zero and one and usually varies with η. Thus the marginal treatment contrast is an integrated, attenuated version of the conditional contrast, and the marginal time slope need not be constant. One should not equate the two sets of coefficients, even in a randomized controlled trial without treatment confounding.
For missing completely at random, an unweighted observed-response GEE with cluster-robust standard errors can consistently fit the marginal mean, and a correctly specified integrated GLMM likelihood is valid as well. Under missing at random depending on previous observed outcomes, ordinary unweighted GEE is generally biased because the remaining responses are selected by informative observed history. Correct inverse-observation-weighted estimating equations for longitudinal dropout recover the marginal target under the sequential MAR and positivity assumptions already stated. Covariate-only observation mechanisms are a simpler special case in which correctly conditioned unweighted mean equations can remain valid.
A correctly specified joint response GLMM, fitted by observed-data likelihood, is valid under ignorable missing at random with distinct parameters, including dependence of missingness on observed response history. This is a likelihood property, not a claim that every mixed model automatically fixes missingness. Under missing not at random, neither ordinary GEE, MAR-based weights nor a response-only GLMM is generally valid; the unseen-outcome dependence requires further modelling and sensitivity assumptions.

Another option is a shared-parameter model for informative dropout: retain the conditional Bernoulli response model, but let the dropout logit contain the same latent effect, for example aj​+cTxi​+dzi​+λYi,j−1​+κB​Bi​. Conditional on the shared effect and observed history the processes can be independent, while marginally dropout remains associated with unseen responses. The joint likelihood must integrate the response factors and dropout factors together over Bi​. A response-only mixed-model fit does not generally account for this dependence.
Because κ or κB​ can be weakly identified from the observed outcomes, report sensitivity of the treatment conclusion to plausible informative-dropout assumptions. One can fix a range of these dependence parameters and refit, or use pattern-mixture sensitivity analysis that shifts the imputed post-dropout log odds by a specified amount from the MAR prediction. Present population risks and treatment contrasts across these scenarios with their uncertainty. These are explicit identifying assumptions, not an empirical test proving which missingness mechanism is true. Neither a complete-case analysis nor carrying forward the last response supplies such a justification.
