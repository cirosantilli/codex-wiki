The baryon asymmetry is an excess of net baryon number over zero, conveniently measured by ηB​=(nB​−nBˉ​)/nγ​. Ordinary matter contains abundant baryons, whereas no comparable primordial antibaryon population is observed. Large nearby domains of matter and antimatter would produce radiation through particle-antiparticle annihilation at their boundaries. Antiparticles produced in energetic collisions are not evidence for a cosmological antimatter domain. The small nonzero baryon-to-photon ratio inferred from Big Bang nucleosynthesis is independently tested by baryon-density consistency between nucleosynthesis and the microwave background.
Starting with zero net baryon number, the Sakharov conditions require baryon-number violation, C and CP violation, and departure from thermal equilibrium. Conservation of baryon number would keep the initial value zero. C symmetry or CP symmetry would pair baryon production with equal antibaryon production; CP rate cancellation in baryogenesis explains why C violation alone does not suffice. In thermal equilibrium with zero imposed charge chemical potentials, CPT symmetry forbids the required net baryon number. An asymmetric rate in one reaction must not be mistaken for net production after all reverse reactions are included.
A simple example is heavy-boson decay baryogenesis. Take equally many heavy X bosons and their antiparticles, with decay channels
X⟶uu(r),X⟶dˉe+(1−r),Xˉ⟶uˉuˉ(rˉ),Xˉ⟶de−(1−rˉ).
(9)
The symbols in parentheses are branching fractions. The two X channels carry baryon numbers 2/3 and −1/3, so both cannot conserve one assigned baryon number for X. Their mean baryon number is r−1/3, while the mean for Xˉ is −rˉ+1/3. Consequently,
ΔBXXˉ​=r−rˉ,ϵ=2r−rˉ​,​
(10)
where ϵ is the asymmetry per decaying particle. CP violation permits r=rˉ without violating the equality of total decay widths required by CPT symmetry. Microscopically a direct CP asymmetry requires interference between decay amplitudes with both relative weak phases and absorptive phases; specifying complex couplings alone would not establish unequal partial decay widths.
For radiation domination, a sufficient decay condition is ΓX​≲H(T=MX​)=1.66g∗​​MX2​/mP​. The heavy population can then survive as the equilibrium abundance drops, before decaying while inverse production is suppressed. Its actual abundance differs from its thermal equilibrium value. If subsequent baryon-number washout is negligible and cosmological entropy conservation holds, an initial total abundance YX​=(nX​+nXˉ​)/s produces
YB​≡snB​−nBˉ​​=ϵYX​.​
(11)
Here s is the entropy density. Residual baryon-number washout multiplies this expression by an efficiency factor below one. The example realizes all three Sakharov conditions; it illustrates a mechanism, rather than identifying the actual cosmological source of the baryon asymmetry.

At neutrino decoupling, the interaction rate is comparable with the Hubble parameter. Equating the supplied rates gives
GF2​TD5​=mP​1.66g∗​​TD2​​,TD​=(GF2​mP​1.66g∗​​​)1/3≃1.6MeV.​

The supplied rate gives an order-of-magnitude neutrino decoupling estimate; a precise neutron-proton conversion temperature uses its own weak-rate coefficients.

Because η≪1, stable deuterium becomes abundant only far below the positive nuclear binding energy, not simply at T=ΔD​.

A longer neutron half-life leaves more neutrons available, increasing the primordial helium mass fraction. If the longer lifetime reflects weaker weak interactions, the accompanying earlier cosmological weak freeze-out can reinforce that effect.
During radiation domination, H∝Gg∗​​T2 whereas the weak conversion rate is proportional to T5. Hence Tf​∝(Gg∗​)1/6. Increasing the effective number of relativistic energy degrees of freedom raises Tf​, increasing rf​, and shortens the interval for free-neutron decay after freeze-out. Both increase the primordial helium mass fraction while nuclear burning remains efficient. The same reasoning gives the primordial helium response to a varying gravitational constant: larger G over this interval produces more helium, with fixed microscopic rates and the approximate Friedmann equation unchanged. Smaller G has the opposite effect. A particular varying-G model must specify its background expansion and time history; the simple trend must not be extrapolated to changes so large that nuclear burning itself fails.

Cosmic inflation addresses the horizon problem by extending the causal past of the observed region, the flatness problem by damping the curvature contribution, and the cosmological monopole problem by diluting unwanted relics. A canonical inflaton can drive this expansion when its potential energy density exceeds twice its kinetic energy density.
Use the unreduced Planck mass mP​=G−1/2, consistent with the coefficient in the Friedmann equation, and take V0​>0, c=0. Seek the exponential-potential power-law inflation solution ϕ=Alog(βt) and a=a∗​(t/t∗​)p. The kinetic energy density is proportional to t−2. The inflaton potential must have the same scaling, so
mP​cA​=2,A=c2mP​​,V(ϕ(t))=β2t2V0​​.
(18)
Substitution into the inflaton equation of motion gives
−A+3pA−mP​β2cV0​​=0,β2V0​​=c22mP2​​(3p−1).
(19)
The Friedmann equation now reduces to
p2=3mP2​8π​(c22mP2​​+c22mP2​​(3p−1))=c216πp​.
(20)
Consequently the nontrivial positive-potential solution is
p=c216π​,ϕ(t)=c2mP​​log(βt),β2=2mP2​(3p−1)V0​c2​,a(t)=a∗​(t∗​t​)p.​
(21)
A positive V0​ requires p>1/3. Accelerating expansion occurs exactly for p>1, giving
c2<16π.​
(22)
This is an exact solution, not a use of the slow-roll approximation. The first Hubble slow-roll parameter and equation-of-state parameter are constant:
ϵH​=−H2H˙​=p1​=16πc2​,w=−1+3p2​=−1+24πc2​.
(23)
If the reduced Planck mass M=mP​/8π​ is used instead, the dimensionless exponential slope is λ=c/8π​ and the exponent becomes p=2/λ2; these are the same solution.
For p>1, the comoving Hubble radius decreases as (aH)−1∝t1−p. A region initially in causal contact can grow beyond that radius before the hot radiation-dominated universe begins. In the ideal power law extrapolated all the way to t=0, the particle horizon integral ∫0t​dt′/a(t′) diverges, so the solution has no finite past particle horizon. A finite, physically trustworthy inflationary interval instead requires a sufficiently large number of e-folds to enlarge an initially causal patch.
For a small curvature contribution, the Friedmann equation gives ∣Ω−1∣=∣k∣/(a2H2)∝t2−2p→0, solving the flatness problem. Conserved nonrelativistic magnetic monopoles have number density proportional to a−3, while the inflaton energy density is proportional to t−2; their energy-density ratio therefore falls as t2−3p. This gives the required dilution, provided later reheating does not recreate them. Inflationary shear damping similarly suppresses anisotropic expansion.
The main failure is no automatic graceful exit or reheating. The first Hubble slow-roll parameter never reaches one, and the exponential inflaton potential has no finite minimum about which the field can oscillate. Additional dynamics are needed for a graceful exit from inflation and transfer to a hot radiation-dominated universe. The ideal solution also retains a curvature singularity at t=0; solving the horizon problem does not itself explain the beginning of the universe. For c=0, the constant inflaton potential instead permits constant-field de Sitter spacetime expansion; the logarithmic field solution is inapplicable.
