Let ζn​ be a primitive nth root of unity. The cyclotomic polynomial is
Φn​(X)=∏1≤a≤n(a,n)=1​​(X−ζna​),Xn−1=∏d∣n​Φd​(X).
(1)
Induction on n shows that Φn​ is monic with integer coefficients. Here is the essential irreducibility of cyclotomic polynomials argument. Let f be the minimal polynomial of ζn​ and let ℓ∤n be a prime. If ζnℓ​ had a different monic irreducible polynomial g, then f∣g(Xℓ). Reducing modulo ℓ gives fˉ​∣gˉ​ℓ, so fˉ​ and gˉ​ have a common factor. As fg∣Φn​, this would give a repeated factor of Xn−1 modulo ℓ, impossible because its derivative is nXn−1. Thus ζnℓ​ is conjugate to ζn​. Every positive integer prime to n is a product of such primes, so all primitive nth roots of unity are conjugate. Consequently
[Q(ζn​):Q]=φ(n),Gal(Q(ζn​)/Q)≅(Z/nZ)×,σa​(ζn​)=ζna​.​
(2)
In particular the cyclotomic field is an abelian extension. Complex conjugation corresponds to −1; for n>2 its fixed field is Q(ζn​+ζn−1​), the maximal real subfield, of degree φ(n)/2.
The ring of integers is Z[ζn​]. One way to see that there is no missing index is to work at each prime p. Write n=pam with p∤m. The prime-to-p roots of unity have separable reduction; their local factors generate unramified extensions with full integer rings. Over each such factor, adjoining ζpa​ is generated by the Eisenstein polynomial Φpa​(1+T), with uniformizer ζpa​−1, and hence gives the full integer ring of that totally ramified factor. The coprimality of pa and m means that ζn​ generates both sets of roots of unity. Thus Zp​[ζn​] is maximal at every p, proving the integral assertion.
The same local description computes ramification and splitting. For p∤n, the ramification index is one, the residue degree is the multiplicative order of p modulo n, and there are φ(n)/f primes above p. If p∣n, put m=n/pa; then
ep​=φ(pa),fp​=ordm​(p),gp​=fp​φ(m)​,
(3)
where ord1​(p)=1. Indeed, the prime-to-p part is unramified, and the p-power part is totally ramified by the preceding Eisenstein polynomial. Notice the exception p=2,a=1: its ramification index is one, consistently with Q(ζ2m​)=Q(ζm​) for odd m. Thus finite ramification occurs only at the indicated conductor primes, with that exception.
We now prove cyclotomic Artin reciprocity over the rational numbers, using arithmetic Frobenius automorphisms. If ℓ∤n and L lies over ℓ, then Frobenius sends the reduction of ζn​ to its ℓth power. Distinct nth roots of unity remain distinct modulo L, since Xn−1 has separable reduction. Hence the Artin symbol is exactly
Art((ℓ))=σℓ​.
(4)
Extend multiplicatively to the group I(n) of rational fractional ideals prime to n. Such an ideal has a unique positive rational generator q whose numerator and denominator are prime to n. The formula is
Art((q))=σqmodn​.
(5)
Every unit residue modulo n has a positive integer representative prime to n, so this Artin map is surjective. Its kernel is precisely the subgroup Pn∞​ generated by positive q≡1(modn). Therefore
I(n)/Pn∞​  ∼ ​Gal(Q(ζn​)/Q).​
(6)
This proves the Artin reciprocity law for this extension, including its principal-ray kernel. The infinite component of the modulus records positivity and therefore the possible action of complex conjugation.

The Kronecker–Weber theorem says that every finite abelian extension of Q is contained in a cyclotomic field. We prove its quadratic case by identifying the ramification that a quadratic number field must have.
For an odd prime ℓ, the cyclic Galois group of Q(ζℓ​)/Q has exactly one subgroup of index two. Its fixed field Fℓ​ is quadratic and is unramified at every finite prime other than ℓ. For a squarefree integer d, the discriminant of Q(d​) is d if d≡1(mod4), and 4d otherwise. A nontrivial quadratic discriminant supported on the odd prime ℓ must therefore be
ℓ∗=(−1)(ℓ−1)/2ℓ.
(7)
Indeed d=±1 cannot give a nontrivial extension unramified at two, whereas exactly one of ℓ,−ℓ is congruent to one modulo four. Thus
Fℓ​=Q(ℓ∗​)⊆Q(ζℓ​).
(8)
This identifies the quadratic subfield of a prime cyclotomic field using ramification, without a Gaussian-sum computation.
Now let F=Q(d​) with d squarefree. Multiply d by ∏ℓ∣d, ℓ odd​ℓ∗. Every odd prime then occurs with even exponent, so its square class is represented by one of 1,−1,2,−2. Thus adjoining the square roots of the ℓ∗ removes all possible odd-prime ramification; the remaining quadratic factor is contained in
Q(ζ8​)=Q(i,2​),2​=ζ8​+ζ8−1​.
(9)
The original d​ is a rational multiple of a product of these square roots. It belongs to the compositum of Q(ζ8​) and the prime cyclotomic fields just used. This compositum is itself contained in a cyclotomic field, proving the theorem for F.
The sharper conductor statement follows from the same ramification calculation. For the fundamental discriminant D of F, put
D=D2​∏ℓ∣Dℓ odd​​ℓ∗,D2​∈{1,−4,8,−8}.
(10)
The possibilities for D2​ follow immediately from the quadratic discriminant formula. The corresponding fields are trivial, Q(i), Q(2​) and Q(−2​), lying in the cyclotomic fields of conductors 1,4,8,8. Their conductors are coprime to the odd primes in the product. Hence quadratic Kronecker–Weber theorem by ramification gives the explicit conclusion
Q(D​)⊆Q(ζ∣D∣​).​

aj+1​=aj​−f′(aj​)f(aj​)​.

(14)
Thus dj​−2s grows at least geometrically, the corrections tend to zero, and completeness gives a limit a with f(a)=0. The first correction has strictly smaller valuation than every later nonzero correction, proving the asserted distance. If x,y are in the ball v(x−a0​),v(y−a0​)>s, then

We use this to establish existence and uniqueness of unramified local extensions. For each integer d≥1, choose a monic irreducible polynomial fˉ​∈k[X] of degree d and lift it to a monic f∈O[X]. Reduction and Gauss lemma show that f remains irreducible over K. Let

[L:K]=d=[kL​:k],e(L/K)=1.

This constructs an unramified extension of each degree.
Conversely, let M/K be an unramified extension of degree d. Its residue field is the unique degree-d finite field extension Fqd​/Fq​. Choose a residue root of fˉ​ in it. The root is simple, so Hensel lemma lifts it to a root of f in M. This embeds L into M, and equality of degrees makes it an isomorphism. All d residue conjugates lift in L itself; hence L/K is Galois, and reduction identifies its Galois group with the cyclic Galois group of the residue field extension. Its distinguished Frobenius automorphism reduces to x↦xq.

Gal(Kd​/K)≅Cd​,Kd​=K(μqd−1​),Ka​⊆Kb​⟺a∣b.​

A primitive such residue generates the finite field extension, so its lift generates Kd​. Taking the union gives the maximal unramified extension, with Galois group Z.

We give the full local-field norm-index argument. Let G=⟨σ⟩ be cyclic of order n. On an additive G-module set D=σ−1 and N=1+σ+⋯+σn−1. The two cyclic Tate cohomology groups are
H0(G,M)=MG/NM,H−1(G,M)=kerN/DM.
(27)
When these are finite, the Herbrand quotient is hG​(M)=∣H0(G,M)∣/∣H−1(G,M)∣. The periodic complex alternating D and N yields a six-term periodic exact sequence from a short exact sequence of modules. Taking alternating products of orders in that exact sequence proves
hG​(M)=hG​(M′)hG​(M′′)(0→M′→M→M′′→0).
(28)
For a finite module, ∣NM∣=∣M∣/∣kerN∣ and ∣DM∣=∣M∣/∣kerD∣ show that the two Tate cohomology groups have equal order. Thus the Herbrand quotient of a finite module is one. For the trivial integral module Z, the two groups are Z/nZ and zero, giving hG​(Z)=n.
We also need cyclic cohomology of a regular lattice. In R[G], fixed coefficient vectors are constant, and every such vector is a norm of a basis vector times its coefficient. The kernel of N consists of vectors whose coefficient sum is zero. For such a vector (ai​), solve bi−1​−bi​=ai​ successively around the cycle; the final equation holds precisely because ∑i​ai​=0. Thus kerN=DR[G]. Both Tate cohomology groups vanish, so hG​(R[G])=1, for R=Z or Zp​, and likewise for finite direct sums.
Now take a cyclic extension L/K of p-adic fields of degree n. Write ULr​=1+pLr​. For r>vL​(p)/(p−1), the convergent p-adic logarithm and p-adic exponential give mutually inverse G-equivariant group isomorphisms

In particular every unit is a norm in an unramified extension. These results give the norm assertions used in the quadratic Hilbert symbol computation.
We finish with the Hasse norm theorem: for a cyclic extension of number fields, an element is a global norm if and only if it is a norm at every place. More precisely the local norm is the norm from the product L⊗K​Kv​; in a Galois extension its factors have the same norm subgroup. The forward implication is immediate. Thus the local norm assumption makes its Brauer class zero at every place. Injectivity in the Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorem makes the global Brauer class zero, giving the desired global norm. This is the required proof outline; cyclicity is essential to this reduction.

A central simple algebra over K is a finite-dimensional associative K-algebra with no nonzero proper two-sided ideals and with center K. The Artin–Wedderburn theorem writes it uniquely as Mr​(D) with D a central division algebra. After a finite separable splitting field extension it becomes Md​, so its dimension is d2, and d is its degree of a central simple algebra. Passing from D to a matrix algebra over D does not change its Brauer class.
The Brauer group consists of these classes, with product induced by tensor product. Extending to a common splitting field shows that a tensor product of central simple algebras is again central simple; its two-sided ideals and center descend from the corresponding full matrix algebra. The neutral class is [K], and the inverse of [A] is [Aop], because
A⊗K​Aop≅EndK​(A),a⊗b↦(x↦axb).
(34)
The displayed map becomes the standard isomorphism for matrices over a splitting field, so it is already an isomorphism over K. Thus Brauer classes measure precisely the obstruction to being a split matrix algebra.
An explicit family is supplied by cyclic algebras. Let L/K be cyclic of degree n, with generator σ, and let a∈K×. Define
A=(L/K,σ,a)=⨁i=0n−1​Lui,ux=σ(x)u,un=a.
(35)
This is an associative algebra: form the skew polynomial ring with relation ux=σ(x)u and quotient by the central relation un−a. Reduction of powers gives the displayed n2-dimensional K-basis. An element commuting with every x∈L has no ui coefficient for i=0, since σi is not the identity. Commuting also with u forces the remaining coefficient into K. Hence the center is K.
Here is an explicit splitting construction. On Ln, with basis e0​,…,en−1​, put
D(x)ej​=σj(x)ej​,Uej​=ej−1​ (j≥1),Ue0​=aen−1​.
(36)
Then UD(x)=D(σx)U and Un=aI. After extending scalars to L, the separable decomposition L⊗K​L≅∏j=0n−1​L supplies all diagonal matrices, and their products with powers of U supply all matrix units. Therefore
A⊗K​L≅Mn​(L).
(37)
Any nonzero proper two-sided ideal of A would extend to one of Mn​(L), which is impossible. Thus this construction really gives a central simple algebra, with splitting field L.
Its central arithmetic feature is the splitting criterion for a cyclic algebra:
(L/K,σ,a) splits⟺a∈NL/K​L×.​
(38)
If a=Nc, let L act by multiplication on the K-vector space L, and let u act as cσ. Its nth power is multiplication by Nc=a, so this gives a representation A→EndK​(L). It is injective by simplicity and an isomorphism by equal dimension. Conversely, if A≅EndK​(V) with dimK​V=n, its embedded field L makes V one-dimensional over L. The relation for u says that u is σ-semilinear. In an L-basis it must have the form cσ, whose nth power is Nc. The relation un=a forces a=Nc.
More generally the crossed-product algebra of a Galois extension attaches an algebra to a multiplicative two-cocycle. Changing the cocycle by a coboundary rescales its basis and preserves the Brauer class. Descent of a split matrix algebra identifies
ker(Br(K)→Br(L))≅H2(Gal(L/K),L×).
(39)
For cyclic L/K, cyclic Tate cohomology identifies this second cohomology group with K×/NL×. In a crossed product, rescale successive cyclic basis elements to powers of a single u; the remaining datum is un=a, changed by a field norm when u is rescaled. This explains why cyclic algebras give every class split by a cyclic extension, and why multiplication of their parameters is addition of their Brauer classes.
For a finite field K=Fq​, every finite splitting field L=Fqn​ is cyclic over K. The field norm is the power map x↦x(qn−1)/(q−1) on its cyclic multiplicative group, and is onto Fq×​. Thus every relative Brauer class is zero, and every central simple algebra has zero class:
Br(Fq​)=0.​
(40)
For a non-Archimedean local field K, the valuation classification of central division algebras shows that every Brauer class splits over a finite unramified extension. For the degree-n unramified extension L/K with arithmetic Frobenius σ, units are norms and norm valuations are multiples of n. Hence the cyclic relative Brauer group is Z/nZ, represented by a=πr. The compatible local Brauer invariant is
invK​[(L/K,σ,a)]=nvK​(a)​(modZ).
(41)
The union over all n gives
Br(K)≅Q/Z.​
(42)
A class with invariant r/n in lowest terms has a central division algebra representative of degree of a central simple algebra n. This is the local valuation classification used above; the explicit cyclic algebras exhibit all of its invariants. At the Archimedean places, Br(C)=0, while Br(R)=Z/2Z: its nontrivial class is the quaternion algebra (C/R,conjugation,−1), since complex norms are positive.
For a number field, the Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorem describes the Brauer group by the exact sequence
0⟶Br(K)⟶⨁v​Br(Kv​) ∑v​invv​ ​Q/Z⟶0.
(43)
Thus its classes are exactly finite-support families of local invariants with sum zero; real invariants are 0 or 1/2 and complex invariants are zero. Every global Brauer class has a cyclic splitting extension, so cyclic algebras also realize all global classes. If χ(σ)=1/n, their local invariants are χv​(recKv​​(a)), with χv​ restricted to the local decomposition subgroup. The sum-zero relation for a principal parameter is the invariant form of Artin reciprocity. Injectivity supplies the local-to-global splitting test, which yields the Hasse norm theorem for cyclic extensions as explained in the preceding solution. Here the local valuation classification and the global invariant theorem are the structural theorems used to describe the Brauer groups; the construction and norm-splitting calculation have been proved explicitly.

(47)
The middle map lifts a residue and sign tuple to an element of K by weak approximation theorem, then takes its principal ideal as a ray class. Different lifts differ by a principal ray factor. Its kernel consists exactly of tuples represented by global units. Every ordinary ideal class has a representative prime to the finite modulus, again by weak approximation theorem; this gives surjectivity of the last map. If Um​ denotes the ray units, the kernel of the unit map is Um​. The Chinese remainder theorem and counting units in each prime-power quotient give the ray class number formula
∣Clm​(K)∣=[OK×​:Um​]hK​2∣m∞​∣ΦK​(m0​)​,ΦK​(m0​)=Nm0​p∣m0​∏​(1−Np1​).​
(48)
First take K=Q(−3​). Its ring of integers is Z[ω], where ω=(−1+−3​)/2 and ω2+ω+1=0. The Minkowski bound for ideal classes is (2/π)3​<2, so every ideal class has a representative of norm one and hK​=1. Its units are the six roots of unity. Modulo seven, the polynomial X2+X+1 has distinct roots 2,4, and therefore
OK​/(7)≅F7​×F7​,ω↦(2,4).
(49)
The unit group of this quotient has order 36. The unit 1+ω maps to (3,5), which has order six; consequently unit reduction is injective and U(7)​={1}. The ray class number formula gives 36/6=6.
We also identify the quotient explicitly. Conjugation interchanges the two factors, so the residue norm is (x,y)↦xy. Its kernel has order six and equals the image of the six units, since 3⋅5=1(mod7). Hence
Cl(7)​(K)≅F7×​≅C6​.
(50)
The field M=K(ζ7​)=Q(ζ21​) has degree six over K, since the degrees over Q are twelve and two. It is unramified outside seven by the cyclotomic computation and the base-change argument above. At primes over seven, K has completion Q7​, and M/K is the tame degree-six cyclotomic extension; its conductor exponent is one. Its relative arithmetic Artin symbol at an ideal a prime to seven acts by
ζ7​⟼ζ7Namod7​.
(51)
If a=(a) with a≡1(mod7OK​), then NK/Q​(a)≡1(mod7). Thus the Artin map kills the principal ray subgroup. It factors through the order-six ray class group; the residue norm quotient computed above shows that it is onto the degree-six Galois group, so the fields coincide. This proves the explicit ray class field of Q of square root minus three modulo seven:
K(7)​=K(ζ7​)=Q(ζ21​),[K(7)​:K]=6.​
(52)
Now take K=Q(−6​), and write s=−6​. Its ring of integers is Z[s], its discriminant is −24, and its units are ±1. The quotient by (s) is
Z[s]/(s)≅Z/6Z,
(53)
whose two units are both images of ±1. The ray class exact sequence therefore gives Cl(s)​(K)≅Cl(K).
For its class number, the Minkowski bound for ideal classes is (2/π)24​<4, so each ideal class contains an integral ideal of norm at most three. The primes of norms two and three are
p2​=(2,s),p3​=(3,s),p22​=(2),p32​=(3),p2​p3​=(s).
(54)
These are the only nontrivial possibilities within the bound, and their ideal classes coincide and have order at most two. The ideal p2​ is not principal: its being principal would give an integer solution to x2+6y2=2, which is impossible. Thus hK​=2, the ideal class group is C2​, and the ray field is the Hilbert class field.
We verify its actual identity rather than stopping at the degree. Put
H=K(2​)=Q(2​,−3​),b=21+−3​​.
(55)
The two quadratic fields Q(2​) and Q(−3​) are distinct, so [H:Q]=4 and [H:K]=2. The integral elements 1,2​,b,2​b are a rational basis. Their trace matrix is the Kronecker product of the two quadratic trace matrices, with discriminants 8 and −3. Its determinant is therefore 82(−3)2=576. The discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice shows that the field discriminant divides 576. On the other hand the relative discriminant ideal formula gives
∣DH​∣=∣DK​∣[H:K]NK/Q​(dH/K​)=576NK/Q​(dH/K​).
(56)
The norm on the right is a positive integer, so equality is forced. Thus the relative discriminant ideal is the unit ideal, and H/K is unramified at every finite prime. There are no real places of K. The extension is quadratic and therefore Abelian; its degree equals hK​, identifying it with the Hilbert class field of Q of square root minus six. Hence the ray class field of Q of square root minus six modulo square root minus six is
K(−6​)​=K(2​)=Q(2​,−3​),[K(−6​)​:K]=2.​
(57)
