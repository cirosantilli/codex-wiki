Use p for disturbance pressure divided by the constant inertial density. The ideal, nonrotating Linearized Boussinesq equations are
ut​=−∇p+σz,σt​+N2(z)w=0,∇⋅u=0.
(1)
Here N2>0 is a prescribed time-independent buoyancy frequency squared. Taking the scalar product of momentum with velocity gives ∂t​(∣u∣2/2)=−∇⋅(pu)+σw, using incompressibility. Multiplying the buoyancy equation by σ/N2(z) gives ∂t​[σ2/(2N2)]=−σw. No spatial derivative of N is needed in this cancellation. Thus
Et​+∇⋅(pu)=0,E=21​∣u∣2+2N2σ2​.​
(2)
This is positive disturbance energy per unit inertial density; its flux is pressure work.
For constant N, write a plane internal gravity wave with wave vector K=(kh​,m) and phase θ=kh​⋅xh​+mz−ωt. Let kh​=∣kh​∣>0 and K2=kh2​+m2. In complex amplitudes, incompressibility and horizontal momentum give
kh​⋅uh​+mw=0,uh​=ωkh​​p​,σ=−ωiN2​w.
(3)
Consequently p​=−ωmw/kh2​. Substitution into vertical momentum, −iωw=−imp​+σ, yields
ω2=K2N2kh2​​.​
(4)
The same dispersion relation follows directly from the displacement equation: its two terms become K2ω2ζ​ and −N2kh2​ζ​. The positive-frequency branch is ω=Nkh​/K; the other branch is its sign reversal.
Choose real vertical-displacement amplitude A. The internal-wave polarization can be written
ξ=Aecosθ,u=ωAesinθ,σ=−N2Acosθ,p=−kh2​ω2m​Asinθ,e=(−kh2​mkh​​,1).
(5)
Velocity and displacement lie along the phase planes, perpendicular to K, and are a quarter cycle out of phase. Buoyancy is opposite in sign to vertical displacement. In the requested two-dimensional sketch take k<0, m<0 and ω>0: the phase velocity ωK/K2 points down and left. Then e=(−m/k,1) points up and left, and positive p accompanies positive w and negative u. Negative-pressure bands have the opposite velocity. Multiplication by pressure therefore transports energy up and left in both signs of band.
Differentiating the positive-frequency dispersion relation gives the group velocity
cgh​=kh​K3Nm2kh​​,cgz​=−K3Nkh​m​=−K2ωm​.​
(6)
In particular K⋅cg​=0. Since ∣e∣2=K2/kh2​, the wavelength averages are
E=2N2A2​,pu​=−2kh2​ω3mA2​e.
(7)
Using N2=ω2K2/kh2​ in the last expression proves
pu​=cg​E.​
(8)
For k,m<0 the group velocity points up and left, perpendicular to the down-left phase velocity. The pressure sign pattern, rather than the direction of moving crests, determines the energy-flux direction shown below.
For the moving-boundary problem, first take the fluid to occupy z>0 with the boundary near z=0. An outgoing boundary-forced internal gravity wave must have cgz​>0. With the specified k>0 and ω>0, the radiation condition therefore selects
m=−kN2/ω2−1​.​
(9)
If instead the fluid occupies z<0, radiation requires cgz​<0 and the positive root for m. The fluid half-space is needed to select a unique sign; the following formulas use the upper half-space.
For slowly modulated internal-wave boundary forcing, put ζ=A(T,Z)eiθ, T=μt, Z=μz. Acting on A by ∂t​↦−iω+μ∂T​ and ∂z​↦im+μ∂Z​, the displacement equation has leading term (K2ω2−N2k2)A. Its order-μ term is
2iμωK2(AT​−K2mω​AZ​)=2iμωK2(AT​+cgz​AZ​).
(10)
Thus characteristics carry the boundary amplitude into the fluid:
A(T,Z)=a(T−Z/cgz​),ζ=A(T,Z)ei(kx+mz−ωt)+O(μ).​
(11)
Real parts are understood. To the same leading order,
ξ​=A(−m/k,0,1),u=−iωA(−m/k,0,1),σ=−N2A,p​=ik2ω2m​A.
(12)
Slow-envelope corrections to these polarization formulas are order μ. At T=2, the amplitude is ϵ for 0≤Z≤cgz​, falls smoothly across cgz​<Z<2cgz​, and is zero beyond 2cgz​. This translating ramp is also shown in the figure.
The mean boundary work per unit horizontal area is W=ρ00​p(0)w(0)​ at quadratic disturbance order. The small boundary displacement permits evaluation at the unperturbed plane. From the flux calculation,
W=ρ00​cgz​2N2a(T)2​.
(13)
The finite causal wave train has energy integral
IE​(t)=∫0∞​Edz=2μN2cgz​​∫0T​a(s)2ds.
(14)
Differentiation, or integration of the local energy law with zero flux beyond the front, proves
dtdIE​​=ρ00​W​.​
(15)
Finally, distinguish the oscillatory velocity from the second-order Eulerian mean u. The displacement polarizations give
ξx​u+ζx​w​=−2ωA2​(km2​+k)=−ωk​E.
(16)
The supplied mean-flow relation therefore yields ut​=(k/ω)Et​+O(μ2) at quadratic wave order. Equivalently, the horizontal momentum flux is uw=(k/ω)cgz​E, and envelope-energy transport gives the same acceleration through ut​=−∂z​uw.
In the frame moving horizontally at c=ω/k, the boundary pattern is stationary and the initial mean current is −c. Subtract that background's kinetic energy before integrating over the infinite half-space. The internal-wave work in a phase-speed frame is then
ΔKmean(c)​=2ρ00​​∫0∞​[(u−c)2−c2]dz=−ρ00​c∫0∞​udz+O(ϵ4).
(17)
Its rate of change is
dtdΔKmean(c)​​=−ρ00​cωk​dtdIE​​=−W.​
(18)
The mean-current cross term supplies the quadratic energy loss in this moving frame; the lab-frame mean kinetic energy alone is fourth order in wave amplitude. The equality is at the leading slow and quadratic orders used throughout the forcing calculation.

After removing the hydrostatic background pressure, the two-dimensional ideal Boussinesq equations are
ut​+uux​+wuz​=−px​,wt​+uwx​+wwz​=−pz​+σ,ux​+wz​=0,
(19)
with the buoyancy equation σt​+uσx​+wσz​+N2(z)w=0. The last equation follows by materially advecting total buoyancy B0​(z)+σ with B0′​(z)=N2(z). Take the streamfunction convention
u=ψz​,w=−ψx​,
(20)
so the y-component of vorticity is q=uz​−wx​=∇2ψ. Taking ∂z​ of horizontal momentum minus ∂x​ of vertical momentum cancels pressure. Incompressibility combines the remaining nonlinear terms into advection of q, giving qt​+uqx​+wqz​=−σx​. Hence the full equations are
∇2ψt​+σx​=ψx​(∇2ψ)z​−ψz​(∇2ψ)x​,​
(21)
σt​+ψz​σx​−ψx​σz​−N2(z)ψx​=0.​
(22)
There is no vortex stretching in this genuinely two-dimensional velocity field.
Write ψ=ψ0​(z)+ϕ, with ψ0′​=u=U(z) and background buoyancy anomaly zero. The Linearized Boussinesq equations become
(∂t​+U∂x​)∇2ϕ−U′′ϕx​+σx​=0,(∂t​+U∂x​)σ−N2ϕx​=0.
(23)
For a stationary disturbance ϕ=ψ​(z)eikx with real k=0 and U>0, the second equation gives σ=(N2/U)ψ​. The first therefore reduces to the stationary Taylor–Goldstein equation
ψ​′′+m2(z)ψ​=0,m2(z)=U2N2​−UU′′​−k2.​
(24)
The restriction k=0 is used when cancelling ik; a purely horizontally uniform alteration of the background is not this wave problem.
For the exponential-profile stationary stratified waves, define
C=U02​N02​​−M2,s=C−k2.
(25)
Since U′′/U=M2 and N/U=N0​/U0​, all coefficients are constant. The complete vertical solution set is
ψ​(z)=⎩⎨⎧​Acos(s​z)+Bsin(s​z),A+Bz,Ae−s​z+Be−−s​z,​s>0,s=0,s<0.​​
(26)
Shifting the origin of z simply changes the two constants. Vertical oscillation requires N0​/U0​>M2+k2​; equality gives an affine structure; below that value the structure is evanescent or growing, with admissibility determined by the physical boundaries.
The Rayleigh quotient with one Neumann endpoint must retain its upper boundary term. On a finite interval with D=z2​−z1​>0, choose a nonzero real vertical structure, so the denominator is strictly positive. Multiplication of the differential equation by ψ​ and integration by parts gives
R=∫z1​z2​​ψ​2dz∫z1​z2​​(Cψ​2−ψ​′2)dz​=k2−∫z1​z2​​ψ​2dz[ψ​ψ​′]z1​z2​​​.
(27)
With only ψ​′(z1​)=0, it equals k2 precisely when ψ​(z2​)ψ​′(z2​)=0. A lower Neumann boundary condition alone does not ensure this.
For s>0, the lower condition selects ψ​=Acos[s​(z−z1​)]. Equality of the quotient requires s​D=nπ/2 for a positive integer n: even n gives an upper Neumann condition, odd n an upper Dirichlet boundary condition. Thus
k2=C−4D2n2π2​>0,
(28)
which additionally restricts N02​/U02​>M2+n2π2/(4D2). If s=0, the lower condition leaves a constant structure; it has quotient C=k2, possible for a genuine wave only when C>0. If s<0, the lower condition leaves Acosh[−s​(z−z1​)], whose upper product is positive and whose quotient is strictly less than k2. On an infinite interval, none of these nonzero lower-Neumann structures is square-integrable, so the displayed ratio of integrals is not a defined finite-integral quotient there. Ratios of truncated integrals would be a different limiting prescription.
For every mode above, including the affine and exponential cases, the disturbance satisfies
∇2ϕ=−Cϕ=(M2−U02​N02​​)ϕ.​
(29)
This statement concerns the disturbance, not the sum with the background streamfunction; the latter generally has a different Laplacian eigenvalue. Its buoyancy polarization is
σ=α(z)ϕ,α(z)=U0​N02​​eMz.​
(30)
Thus no nonzero mode has a constant buoyancy-to-streamfunction ratio when M=0.
The nonlinear exactness test for a stratified streamfunction mode now distinguishes the two equations. The disturbance's vorticity self-advection vanishes because its Laplacian is proportional to itself. But its buoyancy self-advection is
ϕz​σx​−ϕx​σz​=−α′(z)ϕϕx​=−Mα(z)ϕϕx​.
(31)
For a nonzero real sinusoidal disturbance and M>0, this is a nonzero second harmonic somewhere. Consequently none of the nontrivial modes with the stipulated positive M is by itself an exact finite-amplitude solution of both nonlinear equations. Additional harmonics or mean corrections would be needed. In the limiting case M=0, U,N,α are constant; both self-interactions vanish, and these same single-wavenumber structures, or their superpositions with the same C, do solve the full ideal equations wherever their boundary conditions are admissible.

With the free-surface elevation denoted by ζ, the rotating shallow water equations over a fixed bottom are
DtDH​u​−fv=−gζx​,DtDH​v​+fu=−gζy​,ht​+∂x​(hu)+∂y​(hv)=0,h=h00​+ζ−b.
(32)
The momentum gradient is that of the surface elevation, not depth alone; bottom slope enters through the depth and continuity equation. Taking ∂x​ of meridional momentum minus ∂y​ of zonal momentum gives the vorticity equation
DtDH​ωr​​+(f+ωr​)∇H​⋅uH​=0,ωr​=vx​−uy​.
(33)
The terms quadratic in velocity gradients combine into ωr​∇H​⋅uH​; the constant Coriolis parameter contributes f∇H​⋅uH​. Continuity gives DH​h/Dt=−h∇H​⋅uH​. Dividing the two equations proves the exact shallow-water potential vorticity invariant
DtDH​​(hf+ωr​​)=0.​
(34)
For the quasi-geostrophic approximation, let Us​ and ℓ be representative velocity and horizontal length scales. Require the Rossby number Ro=Us​/(∣f∣ℓ)≪1, a shallow layer, and slow evolution on time ℓ/Us​. Put LR​=gh00​​/∣f∣ and assume Bu=(LR​/ℓ)2 is order one. Leading momentum then gives geostrophic balance, and a quasi-geostrophic streamfunction can be chosen as
ψ=fg​ζ,ug​=−ψy​,vg​=ψx​.
(35)
Its surface-height scale satisfies ∣ζ∣/h00​=O(Ro/Bu)≪1. Take the bottom-height ratio of the same small order, with gentle slopes, so η=ζ−b obeys ∣η∣/h00​≪1. Relative vorticity is order Rof, and expanding the exact potential vorticity gives
hf+ωr​​=h00​f​+h00​1​(∇H2​ψ−LR2​ψ​+h00​f​b)+higher orders.
(36)
Because the leading geostrophic velocity is horizontally nondivergent, ageostrophic advection of the anomaly is one order smaller. At the first evolving order the shallow-water quasi-geostrophic potential vorticity equation is
Qt​+J(ψ,Q)=0,Q=∇H2​ψ−LR−2​ψ+h00​f​b,J(ψ,Q)=ψx​Qy​−ψy​Qx​.​
(37)
The constant reference f/h00​ has zero gradient and drops out. These assumptions also require all topographically induced velocities to retain a small Rossby number; the small bottom-height condition alone is not a full justification of quasi-geostrophic balance.
The uniform eastward current has ψ0​=−Uy up to an additive constant, so ζ0​=−(fU/g)y. With b0​=−(fU/g)y, surface and bottom slopes are equal and h=h00​. Its relative vorticity vanishes and Q0​=0. This sloping background is used locally where its surface and bottom departures remain within the small-height regime.
Add the compact ridge and set ψ=−Uy+ϕ(x). Then u=U, v=ϕ′(x), and Q depends only on x. The steady equation becomes UQx​=0. Uniform incoming potential vorticity fixes that constant to zero, giving the cosine-ridge shallow-water geostrophic response
ϕ′′−LR−2​ϕ=−h00​f​b(x),ϕ→0 as ∣x∣→∞.​
(38)
Let a=πL/2, λ=LR−1​ and
A=L−2+LR−2​fϵ/h00​​.
(39)
The forcing is even, so the decaying response is even. A cosine particular solution inside the ridge leaves an even homogeneous term; outside, retain only the decaying exponential. Since the forcing is bounded and contains no delta function, both ϕ and ϕ′ are continuous at the edges. Write ϕ=Acos(x/L)+Dcosh(x/LR​) inside and ϕ=Be−(∣x∣−a)/LR​ outside. Matching at x=a gives
B=Dcosh(a/LR​),−A/L+(D/LR​)sinh(a/LR​)=−B/LR​.
(40)
Solving yields D=A(LR​/L)e−a/LR​, hence
ϕ(x)=⎩⎨⎧​A[cos(x/L)+LLR​​e−a/LR​cosh(x/LR​)],ALLR​​e−a/LR​cosh(a/LR​)e−(∣x∣−a)/LR​,​∣x∣≤a,∣x∣≥a.​​
(41)
A globally decaying homogeneous solution that is continuously differentiable is zero, so this matched solution is unique.
A steady streamline is a constant-ψ curve. If its upstream ordinate is y∞​, it therefore has
y(x)=y∞​+Uϕ(x)​.​
(42)
For f>0, ϕ is positive: the flow first turns toward increasing y, passes the crest at its largest deflection, then returns to its upstream ordinate. Reversing the sign of f reverses the deflection. For L=3LR​, the center deflection is A[1+e−3π/2/3]/U and each tail decays on scale LR​. The figure shows several streamlines and the ridge support; the deflection extends beyond the ridge.
One compatible realization of the initial condition has h(x)=h00​−b(x) and ζ=b0​(y). Its stated zonal velocity then satisfies u(x)h(x)=Uh00​, so the initial mass flux is constant and v=0. The data given only far upstream do not uniquely determine the initial height everywhere; this is the natural mass-flux-compatible choice. It is not fully balanced over the ridge: fu(x) differs from −gζy​=fU. The resulting transverse acceleration initiates geostrophic adjustment and launches fast inertia-gravity waves.
Initially the relative vorticity is zero, but exact shallow-water potential vorticity is f/[h00​−b(x)], not the uniform upstream value. That localized anomaly is materially transported downstream, approximately at U in the leading quasi-geostrophic approximation; it is not annihilated by adjustment. Fresh incoming parcels of uniform PV subsequently supply the ridge region, whose balanced potential-vorticity inversion approaches the steady solution above. The fast waves can carry unbalanced energy away on the inertia-gravity time scale, short compared with the advective time at small Rossby number. In an open domain this can leave the local balanced response; an ideal closed or reflecting domain need not settle monotonically, because undamped waves can return. Radiation, advection of the initial PV anomaly, and any weak damping must be distinguished from irreversible loss of PV.

A Rossby wave is a displacement of a materially conserved potential vorticity distribution whose induced balanced velocity propagates that displacement. In a quasi-geostrophic approximation interior, take a locally uniform zonal current U and meridional background PV gradient B=Q​y​. A small meridional parcel displacement η produces q′=−Bη. The linear potential-vorticity equation is
(∂t​+U∂x​)q′+Bv′=0.
(43)
For a shallow-water vertical mode, potential-vorticity inversion is q′=(∇H2​−LD−2​)ψ′ and v′=ψx′​. Substitution of ei(kx+ly−ωt) gives
ω=kU−k2+l2+LD−2​Bk​.​
(44)
The same structure holds for other vertical modes, with their own deformation lengths, and for barotropic waves with LD−1​=0. A topographic or shear-induced PV gradient can replace the planetary one: locally B=β−U′′ in a barotropic zonal current, with the corresponding bottom contribution when present.
The restoring process can be seen directly by choosing η=η0​cos(kx−ωt) in one horizontal dimension with no mean flow. Then q′=−Bη and ψ′=Bη/(k2+LD−2​). The resulting v′=ψx′​ is a quarter wavelength out of phase with the displacement and requires ω=−Bk/(k2+LD−2​). A PV anomaly is therefore not merely carried passively: its nonlocal balanced velocity moves the displaced contours.
For B>0, the intrinsic zonal phase velocity is
cx​−U=−k2+l2+LD−2​B​<0.​
(45)
Changing the sign of k gives the conjugate representation of the same real pattern, not an eastward intrinsic phase branch. More generally the intrinsic phase-velocity vector has nonpositive zonal component −Bk2/[∣k∣2(k2+l2+LD−2​)]. This one-way phase property contrasts with acoustic and internal gravity waves, whose frequency equations have two independent propagation branches. The direction reverses when the relevant PV gradient reverses; a sufficiently strong current can make the laboratory phase move eastward. Thus one-way refers to propagation relative to the mean flow and a specified gradient, not an absolute geographic direction in every problem.
Scale dependence is supplied by potential-vorticity inversion. At short horizontal scales, a fixed PV anomaly creates ψ′∼q′/∣k∣2, so the intrinsic phase speed is small, of order B/∣k∣2. At long scales with finite LD​, it approaches −BLD2​. The group velocity need not have the phase direction:
cgx​=U+B(k2+l2+LD−2​)2k2−l2−LD−2​​,cgy​=(k2+l2+LD−2​)22Bkl​.​
(46)
In particular, short nearly zonal waves can carry a packet eastward relative to the current while their crests move westward. A group-velocity statement requires a slowly varying, propagating packet and a smooth dispersion branch; turning regions and critical levels require further analysis.
Interior PV gradients are not the only support. In a stratified quasi-geostrophic approximation fluid the inversion operator includes ∂z​[(f2/N2)ψz′​]. The boundary buoyancy b′=fψz′​ also supplies inversion data. A meridional boundary-buoyancy gradient is equivalent, in the inversion problem, to a boundary PV sheet. Consequently Boundary Rossby waves can exist even if the interior PV gradient vanishes. Their perturbations decay away from the boundary over a horizontal-wavenumber-dependent Rossby height; opposite boundaries can support oppositely directed intrinsic waves because their effective sheet gradients have opposite signs.
The Eady model makes this concrete. For U=Λz, constant N and horizontal wave-vector magnitude kh​, put K=Nkh​/∣f∣. Zero interior PV gives ψzz′​−K2ψ′=0, and each rigid boundary has the buoyancy condition
(Ub​−c)ψz′​−Λψ′=0.
(47)
A lower isolated boundary has ψ′∝e−Kz, so clower​=U(0)+Λ/K. An upper isolated boundary has ψ′∝eK(z−H), so cupper​=U(H)−Λ/K. For positive shear, the lower wave is intrinsically eastward and the upper wave intrinsically westward, entirely consistently with their opposite effective PV gradients.
In the finite-depth Eady dispersion relation, γ=KH/2. For γ≫1, tanhγ and cothγ both tend to one. The square root therefore tends to γ−1, giving
c⟶KΛ​orΛH−KΛ​.​
(48)
These are the two uncoupled Eady edge waves, not a pair of opposite branches on a single boundary. Their overlap is exponentially small in KH. For smaller separation, the interaction can phase-lock the counterpropagating boundary waves; the square root becomes imaginary for 0<KH<2.399…, yielding Eady instability. The short-wave limit is stable and illustrates why interior-PV-only reasoning misses boundary wave propagation and baroclinic instability.
The governing balance requires a small Rossby number, weak fractional height or density perturbations, stable stratification where relevant, and frequencies slow relative to ∣f∣. The local gradient must change little across a wavelength; on a planetary beta-plane βℓ/∣f∣≪1 is the associated small parameter. A hydrostatic stratified example also requires a small vertical-to-horizontal aspect ratio and N large compared with its slow frequency. For reference, midlatitude ∣f∣∼10−4s−1 and β∼2×10−11m−1s−1. Atmospheric deformation lengths of order 106m give long-wave speeds of order βLD2​∼20ms−1 and synoptic-to-planetary times of days to weeks. An oceanic baroclinic deformation length of order 3×104m instead gives about 0.02ms−1 and much longer basin-crossing times. Structural constants and background advection matter; these are scale estimates, not universal measured speeds.
Finite-amplitude Rossby waves can overturn or strongly filament PV contours, especially near a critical layer where their phase speed approaches the local current. Smooth inviscid motion still conserves each parcel's PV. Apparent mixing comes from the resulting fine structure under coarse-graining or from weak diffusion; it can flatten a coarse-grained background PV gradient and sharpen neighboring jet-edge gradients. A simplest description of Rossby-wave PV mixing and zonal momentum is a downgradient flux
v′q′​=−κQ​y​,κ≥0.
(49)
For barotropic zonal averages with zero mean meridional transport, u′=−ψy′​, v′=ψx′​ and q′=∇H2​ψ′. Zonal integration by parts gives the Taylor identity for quasi-geostrophic flux
v′q′​=−∂y​u′v′,∂t​U=−∂y​u′v′=v′q′​≃−κQ​y​.​
(50)
Thus mixing down a positive PV gradient produces westward acceleration in the mixing region: wave-generated Reynolds stress redistributes zonal momentum. The inversion relation Q​y​=β−U′′ then relates PV homogenization to a reshaped current. Total zonal momentum cannot simply disappear in an isolated domain; integrated stress convergence is a boundary flux, so compensating momentum changes or boundary forcing must accompany localized drag.
In small conservative waves with B>0, the quadratic wave activity is A=q′2​/(2B). Multiplying the linear PV equation by q′/B gives At​=−v′q′​ for a zonal average on a locally uniform current. Together with the momentum equation this yields ∂t​(U+A)=0 during conservative growth or decay. In a commonly used momentum convention the Rossby-wave pseudomomentum is −A; signs must be stated rather than inferred from the word activity. Breaking and absorption invalidate the reversible small-wave description and deposit the wave's momentum through its flux convergence. A steady undamped conservative wave cannot provide sustained local drag without a flux divergence or boundary exchange, as expressed by the non-acceleration theorem for quasi-geostrophic waves.
Rossby propagation is the slow, direction-selective motion produced by PV advection coupled to balanced inversion. Interior gradients and boundary sheets share that mechanism; inversion determines the scale dependence, while wave packets, boundary-wave interaction and breaking govern how energy and momentum reach other regions.
