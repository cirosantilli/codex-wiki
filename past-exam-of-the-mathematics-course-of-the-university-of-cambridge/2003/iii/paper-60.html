Choose amplitude coordinates in which a quarter-turn and a diagonal reflection act as r(A,B)=(B,−A) and s(A,B)=(−A,B). These generate the natural two-dimensional group representation of the dihedral group D4​. An equivariant dynamical system satisfies F(gv)=gF(v). The two independent sign changes make FA​ odd in A and even in B, and FB​ odd in B and even in A. The quarter-turn equates the two linear coefficients and the two sets of cubic coefficients. No quadratic monomial is allowed. Thus the square-symmetric cubic steady-state normal form is
AT​=μA−aA3−bAB2+O(∣(A,B)∣5),BT​=μB−aB3−bA2B+O(∣(A,B)∣5).​
(1)
The bifurcation parameter has been scaled so that the critical linear eigenvalue is μ. Nondegeneracy at cubic order means a=0, a+b=0 and a−b=0. The equilibrium equations factor as A(μ−aA2−bB2)=B(μ−aB2−bA2)=0. When both components are nonzero, subtracting the two bracketed expressions gives (a−b)(A2−B2)=0. Therefore the complete leading list of small steady branches is:
  • The zero state, with two linear eigenvalues μ.
  • Four axial states (±μ/a​,0) and (0,±μ/a​), when μ/a>0. Their stability matrix has eigenvalues −2μ and μ(1−b/a).
  • Four diagonal states (±s,±s), with independent signs and s2=μ/(a+b)>0. Their eigenvalues are −2μ and 2μ(b−a)/(a+b).
Each family is one orbit of the dihedral group. Each nonzero state retains one reflection fixing its amplitude line; its full spatial symmetry depends also on the chosen planforms. Higher-order terms perturb the branch amplitudes but preserve the nondegenerate local classification. For μ>0, an axial branch is asymptotically stable precisely when a>0 and b>a, while a diagonal branch is asymptotically stable precisely when a+b>0 and b<a. Every cubic branch lying on μ<0 has a positive radial eigenvalue −2μ, so it is unstable. In the exceptional isotropic cubic case a=b, the cubic truncation instead has a circle of equilibria; higher-order anisotropy is then needed, which is why that case was excluded.

The third planform is fixed by a diagonal reflection and changes sign under a quarter-turn. Extend the preceding group representation by
r(A,B,C)=(B,−A,−C),s(A,B,C)=(−A,B,C).
(2)
The other diagonal reflection changes only the sign of B. Consequently the first two components of an equivariant dynamical system have factors A and B, whereas its third component is even in each of A,B. The quarter-turn exchanges A2 and B2 while negating C. Its admissible quadratic coupling is therefore A2−B2 in the third equation and opposite AC,BC couplings in the first two. At cubic order the only new terms are AC2,BC2,C3,C(A2+B2). The linear action on the two-dimensional irreducible mode has one coefficient μ1​, while the distinct scalar representation has its own coefficient μ2​. A small splitting of the two marginal linear modes supplies these two unfolding parameters. With real coefficients the resulting amplitude equations are
AT​BT​CT​​=μ1​A−α0​A3−λAB2−α1​AC−α2​AC2,=μ1​B−α0​B3−λA2B+α1​BC−α2​BC2,=μ2​C−γ0​C3+γ1​(A2−B2)−γ2​C(A2+B2).​
(3)
These equations follow by listing all equivariant monomials through degree three; the displayed minus signs are coefficient conventions, not consequences of symmetry.
In the specified reduced system put A=B=s, C=0. A nonzero branch exists for μ1​>0, with q=s2=μ1​/(1+λ). Write perturbations as u=(δA+δB)/2, v=(δA−δB)/2, and c=δC. Linearization gives
uT​=−2μ1​u,(vc​)T​=M(p4γ1​s​−α1​sμ2​​)​​(vc​),p=2(λ−1)q>0.
(4)
For the square-pattern interaction with a sign-changing scalar mode, the remaining eigenvalues have sum τ=p+μ2​ and product D=pμ2​+4α1​γ1​q=p(μ2​+K), where K=2α1​γ1​/(λ−1)>0. A real two-dimensional stability matrix has both eigenvalues in the left half-plane exactly when its trace is negative and its determinant is positive. Therefore
−K<μ2​<−p,0<μ1​<(λ−1)2(1+λ)α1​γ1​​.​
(5)
If μ2​<−K, the block has negative determinant and one growing direction, explaining the instability at sufficiently small μ2​. At the lower endpoint μ2​=−K, one eigenvalue is zero and the other is p−K<0. The symmetry (A,B,C)↦(B,A,−C) fixes the diagonal state and negates its critical (v,c) direction. Thus a generic nonlinear unfolding has a pitchfork bifurcation to unequal-amplitude states with nonzero C.
At the upper endpoint μ2​=−p, the trace vanishes and
ωH2​=detM=p(K−p)>0.​
(6)
The symmetric eigenvalue remains negative. The critical conjugate pair crosses the imaginary axis with derivative dReσ/dμ2​=1/2. If its cubic Hopf normal form coefficient is nonzero, this is a Hopf bifurcation and nearby periodic solutions exist on one side. Their stability and the side of existence depend on that coefficient; the linear calculation does not determine them. It does establish the generic possibility of oscillating square patterns. The equality p=K is a double-zero degeneracy, outside this nondegenerate Hopf bifurcation argument.

Represent the leading real pattern by A(T)eikc​x+A(T)e−ikc​x. Before forcing, continuous translation symmetry acts as A↦eiθA, and spatial reflection acts as A↦A. Equivariance excludes quadratic terms and permits a linear term and ∣A∣2A at cubic order. Symmetry under reflection makes their coefficients real. A supercritical bifurcation has a saturating cubic coefficient; rescaling amplitude and time makes it −1. The imperfection has a Fourier component at the same wavenumber, so its resonant projection contributes an additive real coefficient. On the spatially uniform wave envelope subspace the amplitude equation is therefore AT​=μA−∣A∣2A+α. Allowing modulations would additionally produce the spatial terms of the real Ginzburg–Landau equation.
For a forcing pattern moving in the positive x direction, its laboratory-frame coefficient is αe−iΩT, where Ω is proportional to kc​c after converting to slow time. The comoving amplitude is B=eiΩTA, since the fast carrier becomes eikc​(x−ct). Differentiation gives
BT​=(μ+iΩ)B−∣B∣2B+α.​
(7)
A constant phase choice makes α>0, and complex conjugation reverses the sign of Ω, so take Λ=Ω>0. Put B=α1/3a and τ=α2/3T. The rescaled linear coefficients are μ/α2/3 and Λ/α2/3, and the forcing becomes one. Below the rescaled variables are renamed A,T,μ,Λ, giving the directly forced cubic amplitude equation.

Let the equilibrium be A∗​=Reiθ and write δA=eiθ(u+iv). The linearization of the cubic term gives δ(∣A∣2A)=2sδA+A∗2​δA. Therefore the real stability matrix is
J=(μ−3sΛ​−Λμ−s​).
(11)
Its eigenvalues obey
σ2+2(2s−μ)σ+(3s−μ)(s−μ)+Λ2=0,σ±​=μ−2s±s2−Λ2​.​
(12)
The square root may be imaginary; stability uses real parts. The determinant D is exactly Fs​, so a zero eigenvalue occurs only at the turning points derived above. Away from the cusp those are ordinary saddle-node bifurcations; the parameterization joining point has D>0.
At a candidate Hopf bifurcation, the trace vanishes: μH​=2sH​. Substitution into the steady cubic gives sH​(sH2​+Λ2)=1, which has one positive root. The pair is genuinely imaginary only if
ωH2​=DH​=Λ2−sH2​=sH​1​−2sH2​>0.
(13)
Thus sH​<2−1/3 and, equivalently,
Λ>2−1/3,μH​=2sH​,ωH​=Λ2−sH2​​.​
(14)
This direct calculation proves the threshold without needing the additional monotonicity assumption. The equality gives a double-zero eigenvalue, not an ordinary Hopf bifurcation. Along a fixed-Λ branch, implicit differentiation gives ds/dμ=−2s(μ−s)/D; at this crossing dReσ/dμ=1+4sH2​/DH​>0. A nonzero nonlinear Hopf coefficient then gives the usual Hopf bifurcation.
The fold and Hopf thresholds of a directly forced cubic amplitude divide the diagrams into three regimes. At noncritical points the two eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity by the signs of their real parts, are classified as follows:
  • For Λ>3​/2, there is one equilibrium for every μ. It has two stable eigenvalues for μ<μH​ and two unstable eigenvalues for μ>μH​.
  • For 3​/2>Λ>2−1/3, the largest-amplitude branch has two stable eigenvalues; the intermediate-amplitude branch has one stable and one unstable eigenvalue. The smallest-amplitude branch emerging from the lower fold has two stable eigenvalues up to its Hopf crossing, and two unstable eigenvalues beyond it. The relative location of the Hopf point and the other fold in μ is not fixed throughout this interval.
  • For 0<Λ<2−1/3, the largest-amplitude branch again has two stable eigenvalues, the intermediate branch has one of each, and the smallest-amplitude branch has two unstable eigenvalues. The trace-zero point lies on the saddle segment and is not a Hopf bifurcation because its determinant is negative.
At a fold one eigenvalue is zero; at a Hopf point the pair has zero real part. These nonhyperbolic points are marked separately in the sketches.

Because ⟨B⟩=0, a periodic primitive H exists. Fix its additive constant by ⟨H⟩=0. Integrating the conserved-field equation once in space gives HT​=σ(HX​+δA2)X​: the possible integration constant vanishes on taking the spatial mean. Treat the spatial average as the inner product defining functional derivatives. Integration by parts, with no boundary terms under periodic boundary conditions, gives
δAδV​=μA+αA2−A3+AXX​−AHX​=AT​,δHδV​=21​(A2)X​+2δHXX​​=2δσHT​​.
(15)
Thus the conserved-field real amplitude equation is a gradient flow ascending V, or descending −V, with positive diagonal mobilities. Along a classical solution,
dTdV​=⟨AT2​+2δσHT2​​⟩≥0.​
(16)
For the mixed term put P=⟨A4⟩1/2 and Q=⟨HX2​⟩1/2. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and a completed square give
⟨−21​A2HX​−4δHX2​​⟩≤2PQ​−4δQ2​=4δP2​−4δ(Q−δP)2​≤4δ​⟨A4⟩.
(17)
Consequently, for β=1−δ>0,
V≤⟨2μ​A2+3α​A3−4β​A4−21​AX2​⟩≤maxa∈R​(2μ​a2+3α​a3−4β​a4)<∞.
(18)
The negative leading quartic coefficient proves the upper bound. The stronger pointwise square completion
V=⟨2μ​A2+3α​A3−4β​A4−21​AX2​−4δ(HX​+δA2)2​⟩
(19)
is also useful: a superlevel set V≥V(0) bounds the amplitude in L4, its spatial derivative in L2, and B=HX​ in L2, because a quartic polynomial with negative leading coefficient dominates its lower-order terms.
The dynamics dissipates toward the steady-state set rather than sustaining nonconstant periodic motion. Indeed V increases to a finite limit, and the time integral of ⟨AT2​+HT2​/(2δσ)⟩ is finite. For global regular trajectories with the usual parabolic precompactness, every limit point is stationary, by the LaSalle invariance principle. A nonconstant periodic or recurrent orbit would force a strictly increasing V to return to its original value and is impossible. Boundedness of a monotone functional alone does not prove convergence to one particular equilibrium; isolation of the limiting equilibrium or an additional convergence theorem supplies that stronger claim.

Let the uniform nonzero amplitude be R, so μ=R2−αR and B=0. Write r=R(2R−α). A perturbation with Fourier wavenumber k=nπ/L has stability matrix
Mk​=(−r−k2−2σδRk2​−R−σk2​),τk​=−r−(1+σ)k2,Dk​=σk2[k2+r−2δR2].
(20)
The constant B mode is disallowed because the conserved mean is prescribed. At k=0 the only allowed mode has eigenvalue −r, so homogeneous stability requires r>0. Then every nonzero-mode trace is negative, and its determinant is smallest in sign at the smallest allowed k2. The exact periodic stability criterion for a conserved-field uniform amplitude is
r>0,(Lπ​)2+2(1−δ)R2−αR>0.​
(21)
A zero in either inequality is a marginal case requiring nonlinear analysis. The zero-amplitude branch, separately, has amplitude growth rates μ−k2 and nonzero conserved-field growth rates −σk2, and is strictly stable when μ<0.
For the long-domain interpretation one may take α>0 by reversing the sign of A, and first consider the positive upper branch R>α/2. It is homogeneously stable even though sufficiently long spatial modulations grow whenever
2(1−δ)R2−αR<0.
(22)
For 0<δ<1, this gives
2α​<R<2(1−δ)α​,−4α2​<μ<4(1−δ)2α2(2δ−1)​.​
(23)
At a fixed finite L the actual unstable part is smaller: it must also satisfy (π/L)2<αR−2(1−δ)R2. For δ≥1, the right side is positive for every positive upper-branch R, so every such state is unstable to sufficiently long waves. The physical mechanism is feedback from the conserved field: an increase in A2 reduces local B, and the term −AB reinforces that increase.
There are two important limits on the wording of the requested stability conclusion. First,
dμdR​=2R−α1​
(24)
never vanishes at a finite regular point of this branch. At its fold the derivative is infinite. Thus a condition of zero dR/dμ cannot literally occur here. The meaningful contrast is that a positive-slope, homogeneously stable branch can still have a growing spatial mode.
Second, a long-wave conclusion cannot be asserted for every finite periodic domain. For example, α=1, δ=2, σ=1, R=1, μ=0, L=1 give r=1 and k2+r−2δR2=n2π2−3>0 for every nonzero integer n. This state is strictly stable even though δ>1. Nor does the unrestricted statement include every sign of R: α=1, δ=2, R=−1/10, μ=11/100 give r=3/25 and r−2δR2=2/25>0, hence stability for every period and every σ>0. These examples distinguish the exact stated real-variable periodic problem from its intended positive-amplitude, long-layer interpretation.

For a stationary finite-wavenumber instability, the relevant Landau–Ginzburg model is the real Ginzburg–Landau equation: its coefficients are real although its pattern amplitude is complex. The complex amplitude records both the strength and the position of the underlying pattern. Assume a simple critical pair of Fourier modes ±kc​, a quadratic nondegenerate maximum of the linear growth rate, and no additional conserved or marginal mode. The last assumption is essential: the conserved field in the previous problem requires an additional evolution equation.
To derive the model, write a real evolution equation schematically as ut​=L(r)u+N2​(u,u)+N3​(u,u,u)+⋯, with symmetric multilinear nonlinearities. Put r=ε2r2​, X=εx, T=ε2t and use the method of multiple scales expansion
u=ε[A(X,T)veikc​x+A(X,T)ve−ikc​x]+ε2u2​+ε3u3​+⋯.
(31)
Here L(kc​;0)v=0. At the next order the noncritical mean and second harmonic are slaved to the critical mode:
u2​=∣A∣2w0​+A2w2​e2ikc​x+A2w2​e−2ikc​x+gradient corrections,
(32)
where L(0;0)w0​=−2N2​(v,v) and L(2kc​;0)w2​=−N2​(v,v). These inverses exist under the no-other-critical-mode hypothesis. Let v∗ be an adjoint null vector normalized by ⟨v∗,v⟩=1. At order ε3, the Fredholm solvability condition requires the resonant forcing to be orthogonal to v∗. The cubic resonant contribution is
κ=⟨v∗,3N3​(v,v,v)+2N2​(v,w0​)+2N2​(v,w2​)⟩.
(33)
This shows why the cubic coefficient includes feedback from slaved harmonics as well as any original cubic nonlinearity. The resulting amplitude equation, after naming its linear control coefficient μ, is
AT​=μA+ξAXX​−g∣A∣2A,ξ=−21​λ′′(kc​)>0,g=−κ.​
(34)
Here λ(k) is the critical linear dispersion relation for growth. The condition g>0 gives a supercritical bifurcation; g<0 calls for higher-order saturation, for example the quintic real Ginzburg-Landau equation.
A concrete check is the cubic Swift–Hohenberg equation ut​=ε2μu−(1+∂x2​)2u−g0​u3. With u=ε(Aeix+Ae−ix)+⋯, its linear operator contributes 4AXX​ at resonant order, while the cubic resonance is 3∣A∣2A. Hence AT​=μA+4AXX​−3g0​∣A∣2A. This directly realizes the perturbation-theory coefficients above.
Symmetry independently constrains the form. A fast spatial translation rotates A by a constant phase, so every nondifferential monomial must transform like A. The leading allowed nonlinear term is ∣A∣2A, and a quadratic term is forbidden. A spatial reflection maps A(X) to A(−X); it makes the leading coefficients real and rules out a drift term in a reflection-symmetric rest frame. The vanishing first derivative of the linear growth rate at kc​ is consistent with the absence of a leading first spatial derivative. These arguments constrain terms but do not calculate ξ,g or replace the solvability calculation. Breaking reflection or studying an oscillatory instability generally leads to the complex Ginzburg–Landau equation with genuinely complex coefficients.
The principal steady solutions are the zero state and the Ginzburg-Landau plane waves
A=Rei(qX+θ0​),R2=gμ−ξq2​>0.​
(35)
Their physical carrier wavenumber is kc​+εq. For q=0 the constant phase is arbitrary, giving a circle of uniform-amplitude states. The zero state has Fourier growth rate μ−ξp2 and is stable for μ<0. For g>0, each existing plane wave is stable to spatially homogeneous amplitude perturbations, with radial eigenvalue −2gR2, and neutral to a constant phase shift.
Steady modulated states are also possible. Writing A=a(X)eiϕ(X) gives the conserved spatial phase current j=a2ϕX​ and
ξaXX​+μa−ga3−a3ξj2​=0.
(36)
Its first integral is ξaX2​/2+μa2/2−ga4/4+ξj2/(2a2)=E. Boundary conditions select nonuniform periodic amplitudes, fronts or defects from this spatial dynamics. In the invariant real subspace, for example,
A=μ/g​tanh(μ/(2ξ)​X)
(37)
is a steady kink joining opposite real states. It is not stable in the unrestricted complex equation: the imaginary perturbation v=sech(μ/(2ξ)​X) has eigenvalue μ/2>0 under vT​=ξvXX​+(μ−gA2)v. This illustrates why a real-amplitude calculation can miss a phase instability. With real coefficients the Lyapunov functional
F[A]=∫(ξ∣AX​∣2−μ∣A∣2+2g​∣A∣4)dX
(38)
satisfies AT​=−δF/δA and dF/dT=−2∫∣AT​∣2dX under compatible boundary conditions. It supplies a variational structure absent from a generic complex-coefficient model.
To test full linear stability of a plane wave, set A=eiqX(R+u+iv) with real perturbations u,v. Let b=gR2=μ−ξq2>0. The linear equations are
uT​=ξuXX​−2ξqvX​−2bu,vT​=ξvXX​+2ξquX​.
(39)
For perturbation wavenumber p, the sideband spectrum of a real Ginzburg-Landau plane wave is
σ±​=−ξp2−b±b2+4ξ2q2p2​.​
(40)
The phase branch at small p is
σ+​=−ξμ−ξq2μ−3ξq2​p2+O(p4).
(41)
Therefore the robust stable band on the infinite line is q2<μ/(3ξ), narrower than the existence band q2<μ/ξ. Crossing its boundary gives the longitudinal Eckhaus instability. At the boundary the quadratic phase-diffusion coefficient vanishes and the leading nonzero term is negative of order p4. More exactly, a nonzero sideband grows only when p2<6q2−2μ/ξ; a finite periodic domain for the wave envelope may exclude that interval. Constant-amplitude stability is understood modulo its neutral global phase, and the allowed perturbation wavenumbers must always be specified.
In a second spatial dimension, an anisotropic medium that selects one roll orientation permits an equation with ξx​AXX​+ξy​AYY​. For positive coefficients its plane-wave analysis is similar, with the second-dimensional contribution adding damping. But simply replacing AXX​ by an isotropic Laplacian is not a faithful extension for rotationally invariant stationary rolls. There the critical wavevectors form a circle. Near (kc​,0),
​(kc​+εq,ε1/2p)​=kc​+ε(q+2kc​p2​)+O(ε2).
(42)
This forces the anisotropic transverse scaling of an isotropic roll envelope: X=εx, Y=ε1/2y, T=ε2t. For the positive carrier convention used here the Newell–Whitehead–Segel equation is
AT​=μA+ξ(∂X​−2kc​i​∂Y2​)2A−g∣A∣2A.​
(43)
The opposite fast-carrier convention reverses the accompanying imaginary sign and detuning convention. For a transverse phase perturbation of A=ReiqX our convention gives σ=−ξqp2/kc​−ξp4/(4kc2​). Thus q<0 permits the transverse zigzag instability, in addition to the longitudinal Eckhaus instability. The fourth transverse derivative and mixed derivative in the square encode the critical-circle geometry.
One complex amplitude still describes only a narrow cone of nearby roll orientations. Competing well-separated wavevectors require coupled amplitude equations; resonant triples can admit quadratic interactions and produce the hexagonal convection amplitude equations when the underlying sign symmetry allows them. Strong disorder, defect cores with large gradients, far-from-onset patterns, boundaries, mean flows and conserved fields may all lie outside the single-amplitude approximation. The method of multiple scales gives controlled leading behavior for small amplitudes and slow modulations, rather than a universal model of all two-dimensional pattern selection.
