On the line c>1, the Dirichlet series and vertical integral have absolute convergence, justifying their interchange; at x=n the kernel is zero on either side.
Here is the smoothing step that establishes the requested asymptotic. Shift the line of this contour integral to the left. The pole of −ζ′/ζ at one has residue +1; at each Nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function ρ of zero multiplicity mρ​ its residue is −mρ​. The denominators also contribute at zero and minus one, and the trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function occur at −2,−4,…. For the limits of the contour integral, choose horizontal heights avoiding zero ordinates, where the logarithmic derivative on each fixed strip is O((logT)2). The factor s(s+1) makes their integrals tend to zero. On left edges chosen a fixed distance from the trivial zeros, the functional equation of the Riemann zeta function controls the logarithmic derivative by O(log(∣s∣+2)); for fixed x>1 the remaining vertical integral vanishes as that edge moves left. This is why the extra integration of ψ is useful.

∑ρ​∣ρ(ρ+1)∣1​<∞.
(15)
Indeed each dyadic height block contributes O((logT)/T). The zero-free line just proved, the nonvanishing Euler product on its right, and the functional equation of the Riemann zeta function put every Nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function in 0<ℜρ<1. Use the Jacobi theta function Θ(t)=∑n∈Z​e−πn2t, t>0. Its transformation law follows directly from the Gaussian Fourier transform. With the Fourier transform convention f​(ξ)=∫R​f(u)e−2πiξudu,

(20)
For example, differentiating the transform and using integration by parts gives f​′(ξ)=−(2πξ/t)f​(ξ); its value at zero is the Gaussian integral t−1/2. Periodize the Gaussian. Its Fourier coefficients are these transforms at integer frequencies, and both series converge absolutely, so evaluating its Fourier series at zero proves the Poisson summation formula here:
Θ(t)=t−1/2Θ(1/t).

Λ(s):=π−s/2Γ(s/2)ζ(s)=21​∫0∞​(Θ(t)−1)ts/2−1dt.
(22)
Split at one and substitute u=1/t in the lower part, using the transformation just proved. The contribution of t−1/2−1 is 1/(s−1)−1/s. We obtain the pole-subtracted theta integral for the completed zeta function
Λ(s)=s−11​−s1​+21​∫1∞​(Θ(t)−1)(ts/2−1+t(1−s)/2−1)dt.

The integral is entire: Θ(t)−1 decays exponentially, uniformly dominating the integrand and all its s derivatives on compact sets. Both the rational term and the integral are invariant under s↦1−s. This proves the meromorphic identity
π−s/2Γ(s/2)ζ(s)=π−(1−s)/2Γ((1−s)/2)ζ(1−s).​
(24)
Equivalently the Riemann xi function
ξ(s)=21​s(s−1)Λ(s)

is entire and satisfies ξ(s)=ξ(1−s). The reflection and duplication formulas for the Gamma function turn the same identity into
ζ(s)=2sπs−1sin(πs/2)Γ(1−s)ζ(1−s),​
(26)
understood by meromorphic continuation at removable exceptional points. This establishes the functional equation of the Riemann zeta function, rather than assuming it.
If N(T) counts zeros ρ=β+iγ in the critical strip with 0<γ≤T, including zero multiplicity, the Riemann–von Mangoldt formula is

(29)
As in the zero-counting formula, the sequence lists ordinates with zero multiplicity; “increasing” means nondecreasing here. The argument does not require the Riemann hypothesis or simplicity of zeros.

Fix a modulus q and a residue class a with (a,q)=1. The Dirichlet characters modulo q are the one-dimensional characters of a representation of (Z/qZ)×, extended by zero on nonunits. Let χ0​ be the principal Dirichlet character.
For every nonprincipal Dirichlet character the partial sums ∑n≤u​χ(n) are bounded: the sum over a full period is zero, and a remaining incomplete period has bounded length. Partial summation therefore continues its Dirichlet L-function holomorphically to ℜs>0 by
L(s,χ)=s∫1∞​(∑n≤u​χ(n))u−s−1du.
(42)
This shows in particular that it has no pole at one. By contrast
L(s,χ0​)=ζ(s)∏p∣q​(1−p−s)
(43)
has a simple pole there. We use the stipulated nonvanishing L(1,χ)=0 for every real nonprincipal Dirichlet character, and establish the remaining nonvanishing as follows.
For real σ>1, take the product over all Dirichlet characters. The Euler product logarithms and Orthogonality of Dirichlet characters give
log∏χmodq​L(σ,χ)=∑p∤q​∑j≥1​jpjσ∑χ​χ(p)j​=φ(q)∑p∤q​∑j≥1pj≡1(modq)​​jpjσ1​≥0.
(44)
Thus this product is real and at least one. If a nonreal Dirichlet character had L(1,χ)=0, complex conjugation would also give L(1,χ​)=0. These are distinct factors. Two zeros and only one possible simple pole force the whole product to tend to zero as σ↓1, contradicting the lower bound. Hence all nonprincipal Dirichlet characters have L(1,χ)=0, under the given real-character assumption.
For σ>1, absolute convergence of each Euler product gives
logL(σ,χ)=∑p∤q​pσχ(p)​+O(1),
(45)
with bounded remainder as σ↓1: the terms of prime-power exponent at least two have total modulus at most ∑n≥2​1/(n(n−1)). Since a nonprincipal L defines a holomorphic function that is nonzero near one, its Euler-product logarithm differs from a local holomorphic logarithm by a fixed integer multiple of 2πi on a short real interval. It is therefore bounded there. For the principal Dirichlet character the pole instead gives
logL(σ,χ0​)=logσ−11​+Oq​(1).
(46)
Apply the Dirichlet character orthogonality relation at each prime. The prime-character sum near one yields
∑p≡a(modq)​p−σ=φ(q)1​∑χmodq​χ(a)​logL(σ,χ)+Oq​(1)=φ(q)1​logσ−11​+Oq​(1).
(47)
This tends to infinity. A finite set of primes would have a bounded sum as σ↓1, so every coprime residue class contains infinitely many primes. This proves the Dirichlet theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions from the specified assumption. In fact it proves divergence of the corresponding reciprocal-prime series and gives Dirichlet density 1/φ(q).
Finally, for real s>1 each prime satisfies
p−s=s∫p∞​t−s−1dt.
(48)
The integrands are nonnegative, so Tonelli theorem permits summation inside the integral. At a fixed t, exactly π(t,q,a) primes from the residue class have entered. Therefore
p≡a(modq)∑​ps1​=s∫1∞​ts+1π(t,q,a)​dt.​
