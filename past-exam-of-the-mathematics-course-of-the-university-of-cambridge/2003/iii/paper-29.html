(2)
A nonnegative random variable of zero expected value vanishes almost surely. Therefore X=Y almost surely.​

Again put Y=E[X∣G]. For any fixed real c, the random variable
Dc​=∣X−c∣−sign(Y−c)(X−c)
(3)
is nonnegative and integrable. The sign function of Y−c is bounded and G-measurable, so conditioning and equality of the probability distributions give
EDc​=E∣X−c∣−E[sign(Y−c)(Y−c)]=E∣X−c∣−E∣Y−c∣=0.
(4)
Thus Dc​=0 almost surely. On {Y>c} this forces X≥c; on {Y<c} it forces X≤c; on {Y=c} it forces X=c. In particular {Y=c}⊆{X=c} up to a null set. These two events have equal probability, again by equality of the probability distributions, so they agree up to a null set. Consequently
sign(X−c)=sign(Y−c)almost surely.
(5)
Taking c=0 establishes the suggested sign assertion, but this alone would not exclude unequal positive values. Apply the same argument simultaneously to the countable set of rational c. Outside the union of their null sets, unequal X and Y would have a rational strictly between them, giving opposite signs. This proves the conditional expectation preserving a distribution result:
X=E[X∣G]almost surely.​
(6)
Only integrability was used; no finite second moment or hidden square-integrability assumption is needed.

A convenient precise version is the almost sure submartingale convergence theorem: if (Sn​) is a real submartingale and supn​ESn+​<∞, then Sn​ converges almost surely to a finite integrable random variable. In particular the martingale convergence theorem applies to any martingale with supn​E∣Sn​∣<∞. It asserts neither convergence in L1 nor preservation of the initial expected value without additional uniform integrability.
Here is a proof including the crossing estimate. Fix real a<b, and let Un​[a,b] count completed upcrossings by time n. Use a predictable indicator Hk​ which holds one unit during each crossing attempt: enter when Sk​≤a, exit when Sk​≥b. With
Gn​=∑k=0n−1​Hk​(Sk+1​−Sk​),
(7)
each completed trade gains at least b−a, while the possible unfinished trade loses no more than (Sn​−a)−. Hence Gn​≥(b−a)Un​[a,b]−(Sn​−a)−. The submartingale property and 0≤Hk​≤1 give EGn​≤E(Sn​−S0​): the complementary predictable gains have nonnegative expected value. Therefore

The Brownian first-passage time Ta​ is finite almost surely. Indeed the Brownian reflection principle gives P(Ta​≤t)=2P(Bt​≥a)→1 as t→∞. Continuity of the paths means the first passage to x+y occurs after the first passage to x. By the Strong Markov property, the process BTx​+s​−x is an independent fresh Brownian motion. Thus Tx+y​−Tx​ has the same probability distribution as Ty​ and is independent of Tx​. Taking the Laplace transforms of nonnegative random variables gives
φx+y​(λ)=φx​(λ)φy​(λ).​
(14)
For fixed λ≥0, these transforms lie in (0,1]. Put g(a)=−logφa​(λ). It is additive on positive arguments and nondecreasing, because the hitting times increase with the level. Writing c(λ)=g(1), additivity gives g(r)=rc(λ) for positive rational r, and rational upper and lower approximations then give g(a)=ac(λ) for every a>0. Hence φa​(λ)=e−ac(λ), with c(λ)≥0.
The Brownian scaling identity Ta​=da2T1​ gives
e−ac(λ)=φ1​(a2λ)=e−c(a2λ).
(15)
For λ>0, take a=λ​ in this relation with the transform parameter one. It follows that c(λ)=Cλ​, where C=c(1)≥0. At zero, finiteness of the hitting time gives c(0)=0. Thus φa​(λ)=e−aCλ​​, with the constant determined in part (b).

For probability measures on a metric space, weak convergence of probability measures μn​⇒μ means ∫fdμn​→∫fdμ for every bounded continuous real function f. On the real line it is equivalently convergence of the cumulative distribution functions at every continuity point of the limiting cumulative distribution function. Bounded continuous upper and lower approximations to interval indicators give one direction; a partition at continuity points, uniform approximation on a compact interval, and control of the tails give the reverse direction.
For a probability measure μ on R, its characteristic function is φμ​(t)=∫eitxμ(dx). The Lévy continuity theorem states: μn​⇒μ implies φμn​​(t)→φμ​(t) for every real t; conversely, if these characteristic functions have a pointwise limit φ which is continuous at zero, there is a unique probability measure μ with characteristic function φ, and μn​⇒μ. In particular, for a specified limiting probability measure, weak convergence is equivalent to pointwise convergence of its characteristic functions.
The forward implication follows by testing cosine and sine. The dominated convergence theorem shows that every characteristic function is continuous at zero and has value one there. For the converse, first establish tightness of probability measures. By Fubini's theorem, for h>0,
2h1​∫−hh​(1−Reφμn​​(t))dt=∫(1−hxsin(hx)​)μn​(dx).
(21)
The integrand on the right is at least 1/2 when ∣x∣≥2/h. This proves the characteristic-function tightness bound
μn​(∣x∣≥2/h)≤h1​∫−hh​(1−Reφμn​​(t))dt.
(22)
The integrals converge for each h by dominated convergence, and continuity of φ at zero makes the limit as small as desired by choosing h small. Thus all sufficiently late measures have a common tail bound; the finitely many earlier measures also have small tails once the radius is increased. This is uniform tightness of probability measures.
We give the required compactness argument explicitly. For any subsequence, choose a further diagonal subsequence whose cumulative distribution functions Fn​ converge at every rational q, to f(q). Define
F(x)=infq>xq∈Q​​f(q).
(23)
This function is nondecreasing and right-continuous: given a rational q>x with f(q) close to F(x), the same bound holds for F(y) when x<y<q. Tightness gives F(−∞)=0 and F(+∞)=1, so F is a cumulative distribution function of a probability measure ν. For rationals r<x<s, Fn​(r)≤Fn​(x)≤Fn​(s). The limits bracket F(x−) and F(x); at a continuity point of F they coincide. Thus Fn​(x)→F(x) at all such points. To obtain weak convergence, choose a compact interval with small tails and partition it using continuity points of F. A bounded continuous function is uniformly continuous there, so step-function approximations and convergence of interval probabilities prove convergence of its integral. This proves Helly selection for distribution functions and supplies a weak subsequential limit ν.
Its characteristic function is φ by the already proved forward implication. We also prove uniqueness rather than assume it. If two probability measures ν,ρ have the same characteristic function, convolve each with a centered normal distribution of variance ε2>0. Their densities are, by the Gaussian integral and Fubini's theorem,
pν,ε​(x)=2π1​∫R​e−itxφν​(t)e−ε2t2/2dt,
(24)
and likewise for ρ. The integrable Gaussian factor justifies exchanging the integrals, so these densities agree. For every bounded continuous f, letting ε↓0 in ∫E[f(x+εZ)]ν(dx), with Z a standard normal random variable, gives ∫fdν by bounded convergence theorem. Hence the two measures have equal integrals against all these test functions, and approximating interval indicators shows ν=ρ. This proves the uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions.
Every subsequence therefore has a further subsequence weakly converging to the same unique μ. If the original sequence failed weak convergence, some bounded continuous test function would have a subsequence of integrals staying a fixed distance from its integral under μ, contradicting that further convergence. This completes both directions of the theorem. Continuity at zero is essential: the laws N(0,n) have pointwise limiting characteristic function zero away from zero and one at zero, and are not tight.

Let B be a standard planar Brownian motion started at z. Neighbourhood recurrence means that, with probability one, every nonempty open subset of R2 is visited at arbitrarily large times. Not hitting points means that for each fixed w=z, Pz​(∃t≥0:Bt​=w)=0. If w=z, time zero must be excluded, and there is still no return at any positive time. This assertion concerns each prescribed point; it does not say that the path contains no points.
We prove both claims from the planar Brownian annulus hitting probability. Fix a center w and radii 0<r<ρ=∣z−w∣<R, and define the stopping times τr​=inf{t:∣Bt​−w∣=r} and τR​=inf{t:∣Bt​−w∣=R}. Their minimum σ is finite almost surely: a coordinate of Brownian motion cannot stay forever in a bounded interval, by the Brownian reflection principle, so exit from the outer disk is finite. On the annulus,
Δlog∣x−w∣=0.
(31)
The Itô formula therefore makes log∣Bt∧σ​−w∣ a local martingale. It is bounded between logr and logR, hence is a genuine martingale. The optional stopping theorem at t∧σ, followed by the bounded convergence theorem, gives
logρ=Pz​(τr​<τR​)logr+Pz​(τR​<τr​)logR.
(32)
Thus
Pz​(τr​<τR​)=log(R/r)log(R/ρ)​.​
(33)
The exit can only be at one of the two distinct boundaries by path continuity.
For a fixed r, let R→∞. These events increase to {τr​<∞}: any path up to a finite hitting time is bounded and so avoids some sufficiently large outer circle. The ratio tends to one, proving that any closed disk of positive radius is hit almost surely from any starting point outside it. An initial point inside it already counts as a hit.
To prove the stronger recurrence statement, take a closed disk contained in any prescribed nonempty open set. At each integer time n, the Markov property and the preceding disk-hitting result imply that some time t≥n visits this disk with probability one. Intersecting these events over all n proves visits at arbitrarily large times. A countable basis of disks with rational centers and rational radii makes this simultaneous for all nonempty open sets. This is recurrence of planar Brownian motion, or equivalently neighbourhood recurrence.
For point avoidance, keep R>ρ fixed and let r↓0. Hitting w before τR​ requires hitting every inner circle first. The displayed ratio tends to zero, so this point-hitting event has probability zero. Any finite hit of w would occur before exit from some outer circle with integer radius, again because the preceding path is bounded. A countable union now gives Pz​(Tw​<∞)=0 (z=w)​, the polar point for planar Brownian motion assertion.
Finally, even if the process starts at w, for each deterministic s>0 its normal distribution has a density, so P(Bs​=w)=0. The Markov property at s and the result from other starting points exclude any visit to w at or after s. Taking s=1/n excludes all positive-time returns. Thus planar Brownian motion repeatedly approaches every point, while almost surely never hitting any prescribed point at positive time.

Each successive crossing time Nj​, j≥1, is a stopping time, since in discrete time the event that a threshold is first met after the previous stopping time can be written as a finite union of events known at the candidate time. Use inf∅=∞, with all later crossing times then infinite as well.
For the supermartingale assertion, build a process by finitely many switches. Begin with the constant supermartingale one. At N1​ switch to Xn​/a; the new value at that time is at most one. At N2​ switch to the constant q; the old value there is XN2​​/a≥q. At N3​ switch to qXn​/a, whose value is at most q, and at N4​ switch to the constant q2, whose value is at most the previous process. More generally, the switches at N2ℓ−1​ and N2ℓ​ are respectively from cash qℓ−1 to qℓ−1Xn​/a and from that stock value to cash qℓ. Every switch is downward, including possible overshoots of either threshold.
