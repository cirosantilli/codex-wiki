Away from the line source, insert a leading wavefront form u∼a(x)W(t−T(x)) into the shear-horizontal wave equation and match the two most singular orders. The first gives the eikonal equation ∣∇T∣=1/β. The next gives
2∇T⋅∇a+aΔT=0,∇⋅(a2∇T)=0.
(9)
Equivalently, SH ray-tube amplitude transport conserves ρβa2J, where J is the transverse width of a two-dimensional ray tube. A line source has straight radial elastic rays and J=rdθ for fixed angular width, so
a(r)=Cr−1/2​.
(10)
The same geometrical spreading applies to the first nonzero derivative discontinuity or leading wavefront singularity; the source history determines which waveform W and normalization C occur. In three dimensions a point source instead has J∝r2 and amplitude proportional to r−1.
This does not contradict the SH line-source near-field singularity. Ray transport describes an arrival-front limit with t−r/β small, or the high-frequency field outside the immediate source neighborhood. The logarithm describes r↓0 at fixed elapsed time and includes the tail behind the two-dimensional wavefront. For example, a causal step force F0​H(t) has the exact radial displacement
u(r,t)=2πμF0​​arcoshrβt​ H(t−r/β).
(11)
For r≪βt, this is F0​log(2βt/r)/(2πμ), the near-source logarithm. For t=r/β+τ with 0<τ≪r/β, it is
u∼2πμF0​2β​​r−1/2τ​​.
(12)
The coefficient of the leading arrival singularity therefore has exactly the ray-theory dependence. A force impulse gives the corresponding r−1/2H(τ)/τ​ leading field. The limits describe different regions of one causal solution; neither is a uniform approximation to the other.
