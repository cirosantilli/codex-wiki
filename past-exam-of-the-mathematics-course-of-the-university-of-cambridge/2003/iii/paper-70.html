a OurBigBook.com
For a real matrix A with positive determinant, the polar decomposition of an invertible real matrix gives unique factorizations
A=RU=VR,R∈SO(3),U=UT>0,V=VT>0,​
(1)
where U and V are the right stretch tensor and left stretch tensor, respectively. Positivity here means positive definiteness, not merely nonnegative entries.
To prove existence, C=ATA is symmetric and vTCv=∣Av∣2>0 for every nonzero v, because A is invertible. The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices supplies an orthogonal matrix Q and positive eigenvalues cj​ such that C=Qdiag(c1​,c2​,c3​)QT. Define U using the principal square root of a positive semidefinite matrix construction:
U=Qdiag(c1​​,c2​​,c3​​)QT,R=AU−1.
(2)
Then RTR=U−1ATAU−1=I, so R is orthogonal. Also detR=detA/detU>0; an orthogonal matrix has determinant plus or minus one, hence detR=1. Set V=RURT. It is symmetric positive definite, V2=AAT, and VR=RU=A.
For uniqueness, any second factorization A=R1​U1​ with the same properties gives U12​=ATA=C. Since U1​ commutes with its square, it preserves every eigenspace of C. On a c-eigenspace its own positive eigenvalues must square to c, so it equals c​ times the identity there. Thus U1​=U, then R1​=AU−1=R, and finally V is fixed too. This proves both uniqueness and the polar decomposition in continuum mechanics, with the same proper rotation in its left and right forms.

A material fibre segment of reference length L and unit direction l has reference vector Ll. The uniform deformation gradient maps it to LAl, whose current length is
L∣Al∣=LlTATAl​.
(3)
Therefore its length is preserved if and only if
lTATAl=1.​
(4)
This is the inextensible fibre constraint. The quadratic form uses the right Cauchy-Green deformation tensor; a subsequent rigid rotation does not change it, consistently with the polar factorization A=RU.

For two distinct symmetric fibre families use reference directions l±​=(cosθ,±sinθ,0)T, with 0<θ<π/2. The second sign is needed for the orthogonality conclusion: the PDF prints only one direction. If both families literally had that same direction, their images under an invertible deformation would remain parallel, so part (ii) would be false. The following derivation states the intended two-family geometry explicitly.
Write c=cosθ, s=sinθ and take positive λ,α, as required for a positive stretch tensor. Applying the inextensible fibre constraint to either family gives
λα2c2+α−2s2​=1.
(5)
With b=α2, multiplication by b yields c2b2−λb+s2=0. Its discriminant is λ2−4c2s2=λ2−sin22θ, so the two-family inextensible-fibre pure stretch has
α2=2cos2θλ±λ2−sin22θ​​.​
(6)
This also shows why the angle endpoints need separate treatment: at θ=0 the admissible root is α2=λ, and at θ=π/2 it is α2=1/λ. The nominal two families then represent the same unoriented fibre direction, and the two-branch/orthogonality assertions do not apply.

For 0<θ<π/2 and λ>sin2θ, the discriminant is strictly positive. The two roots have positive sum λ/c2 and positive product s2/c2, so both roots are positive and distinct. Each gives exactly one positive α and hence a different right stretch tensor. If λ=1, neither tensor is the undeformed identity because its third diagonal entry is λ. Thus two distinct deformed configurations are possible. Excluding λ=1 removes the case in which one of the two configurations is the undeformed body; it is not needed just for positivity of the roots.

The smallest admissible axial stretch is λmin​=sin2θ, where the two branches merge and α2=tanθ=s/c. The current unit fibre vectors are m±​=Ul±​. Their dot product is
m+​⋅m−​=λ1​(α2c2−α−2s2).
(7)
At the minimum, both terms in parentheses equal cs, so
m+​⋅m−​=0.​
(8)
Hence the two families are orthogonal at the maximum possible contraction. There is also a geometric proof of the bound: their reference parallelogram area is sin2θ, and its current area is sin2θ/λ, because the in-plane area stretch is 1/λ. Two unit vectors span area at most one, with equality precisely when perpendicular. This gives λ≥sin2θ and the same equality condition without solving the quadratic. At θ=π/4 the minimum is one, so no axial contraction is possible and the fibres are already orthogonal.

Use the material-first nominal stress tensor convention Nαi​, whose reference-volume power is Nαi​A˙iα​. At a regular constraint point, F,A​=0, all admissible deformation rates satisfy F,Aiα​​A˙iα​=0. Equality of mechanical power and the derivative of the strain energy density gives
(Nαi​−W,Aiα​​)A˙iα​=0
(9)
for every vector in this eight-dimensional tangent hyperplane. Its orthogonal complement is the span of F,A​. Consequently the constraint reaction in hyperelastic stress is
Nαi​=W,Aiα​​+qF,Aiα​​.​
(10)
The scalar Lagrange multiplier q is not determined by the work identity: its contribution vanishes on every allowed rate. Regularity matters. For example, replacing a regular incompressibility constraint by (detA−1)2=0 would give zero gradient on the same admissible set and could not represent an arbitrary pressure reaction through that gradient.
For incompressibility use the regular function F=J−1, with J=detA. Differentiating the alternating-tensor expression for J gives
∂Aiα​∂J​=21​ϵijk​ϵαβγ​Ajβ​Akγ​=J(A−1)αi​.
(11)
The first expression is the cofactor entry; contracting it with Ajα​ gives Jδij​, proving the inverse formula. On J=1 this yields
Nαi​=W,Aiα​​+q(A−1)αi​.​
(12)
With this plus-sign convention q is the negative of the usual pressure multiplier.
For an equibiaxially stretched sheet, incompressibility requires the principal stretches (λ,λ,λ−2). The printed sheet expression's λ−1/2 is inconsistent with this constraint and must be λ−2; the balloon expression later on the same PDF page has the correct exponent. Let Wj​ denote the derivative with respect to principal stretch λj​, evaluated on this constrained path. With no applied thickness-direction traction,
0=N33​=W3​+qλ2,q=−λ−2W3​.
(13)
Isotropy gives W1​=W2​ at equal in-plane stretches. Hence
N11​=N22​=W1​−λ−3W3​,dλd​W(λ,λ,λ−2)=W1​+W2​−2λ−3W3​.
(14)
The equibiaxial nominal tension of an incompressible sheet is therefore
N11​=N22​=21​dλd​W(λ,λ,λ−2).​
(15)
The two equal in-plane nominal tensions do work 2N11​dλ per reference volume; the thickness-direction nominal stress does no work because it is zero. That is exactly the constrained energy differential, explaining energy conservation. As a check that the printed exponent is a genuine error, a neo-Hookean solid gives N=μ(λ−λ−5), zero at the undeformed state. The derivative with the erroneous exponent would instead give 3μ/4 at that state.
For the thin spherical balloon, use the leading reference shell volume 4πa02​h0​. Its two tangential stretches are λ=a/a0​, while its thickness stretch is λ−2 and its current thickness is h=h0​λ−2. Define W(λ)=W(λ,λ,λ−2). Its total elastic energy at leading thin-shell order is
E=4πa02​h0​W(λ).
(16)
During a quasistatic increment da, the enclosed volume changes by dV=4πa2da, and dλ=da/a0​. Equating energy change to work of the internal excess pressure gives
p4πa2da=4πa0​h0​W′(λ)da,p(a)=a0​λ2h0​​W′(λ).​
(17)
This is the inflation pressure of a thin incompressible elastic balloon. The approximation neglects through-thickness variation and curvature corrections beyond leading order, and requires h/a≪1. It also agrees with membrane force balance: p=2hσt​/a, σt​=λN, and N=W′/2. Here pressure is force per current area, while N is force per reference area; confusing them would lose a factor of λ.

For antiplane shear, the only nonzero infinitesimal strains are e13​=e31​=w,1​/2 and e23​=e32​=w,2​/2. Their trace is zero. The isotropic constitutive law σij​=λL​ekk​δij​+2μeij​ consequently gives
σ13​=σ31​=μw,1​,σ23​=σ32​=μw,2​,​
(24)
with every other component zero. The first two equations of motion vanish because the fields are independent of x3​. The third is ρw,tt​=σ31,1​+σ32,2​, hence
∇2w−c21​w,tt​=0,c2=μ/ρ.​
(25)
The PDF omits the final equals-zero; this is the equation supplied by momentum balance.
For a steadily moving field put x=x1​−Vt and β=1−V2/c2​. In the subsonic case ∣V∣<c, the equation becomes β2wxx​+wx2​x2​​=0. With y=βx2​ it reduces to wxx​+wyy​=0. Thus w is a harmonic function and, on a simply connected region, has a harmonic conjugate; there is a holomorphic function H(z) such that
w=ReH(z),z=x+iy.​
(26)
This is steadily moving antiplane shear. The representation is local on more general domains unless the conjugate's periods vanish. The subsonic condition is essential: at or above the shear-wave speed the coordinate rescaling is degenerate or the equation is hyperbolic rather than this elliptic harmonic problem.

Retain β=1−V2/c2​>0, but normalize the complex potential as F=βH, so that w=ReF/β. The Cauchy-Riemann equations give wx​=ReF′/β and wy​=−ImF′/β. Since ∂x2​​=β∂y​, the two stresses satisfy
βσ13​−iσ23​=μF′(z).​
(27)
Using the same unscaled H from part (a) would instead put an additional β on its derivative; the potential normalization matters.
Take the principal branch of z​, with its branch cut along the negative real axis and positive x​ for x>0. Write Q=F′. For the antisymmetric crack-opening response, reflection gives Q−(s)=−Q+(s)​ on the faces. The prescribed equal face stress gives ImQ±(s)=f(s)/μ. Multiplication by the square-root factor converts this face condition to an additive jump. In fact, with G=z​Q and r=−s​,
G+=irQ+,G−=−irQ−=irQ+​,G+−G−=−μ2​−s​f(s).
(28)
The Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem applied to this jump gives
G(z)=−πiμ1​∫−∞0​s−z−s​f(s)​ds+P(z),
(29)
where P is entire after imposing the usual finite-energy crack-tip regularity. This is the Hilbert problem for an antiplane crack, with the stress-potential phase chosen explicitly above.
For localized face loading with no additional remote loading, the far displacement is bounded and the weighted Cauchy transform decays. In particular, for compactly supported loading, G→0 at infinity and Liouville theorem sets P=0. The resulting Cauchy solution for a steadily moving semi-infinite antiplane crack is
F′(z)=−πμz​i​∫−∞0​z−s−s​f(s)​ds.​
(30)
Integrals and boundary values are understood for loadings with the corresponding convergence and regularity, or as limits of localized loading. The reflection used above is the pure antiplane opening response: reflection with a sign change of displacement leaves both prescribed face stresses unchanged, and the usual no-extra-load uniqueness condition eliminates an unforced symmetric addition.
One can check the face stresses directly. Put Ipv​(s)=PV∫−∞0​−t​f(t)/(s−t)dt. The Cauchy boundary limits give
Q+(s)=−πμ−s​Ipv​(s)​+μi​f(s),Q−(s)=πμ−s​Ipv​(s)​+μi​f(s).
(31)
Both have −μImQ±=−f(s), verifying the prescribed traction and its sign on both faces. The opposite real parts also give the required reflection. Ahead of the tip, the integral is real and Q is purely imaginary, so the bonded line has zero tangential displacement gradient and the two halves match.
For x=x1​−Vt>0, take z=x in the boxed analytic solution. The ahead-of-tip stress is
σ23​(x1​,0,t)=πx​1​∫−∞0​x−s−s​f(s)​ds.​
(32)
No β appears: at fixed co-moving position and the specified co-moving face loading, this stress is independent of the speed. The displacement, however, still scales with 1/β, so speed independence does not apply to the whole elastic field. If ∫−∞0​∣f(s)∣/−s​ds is finite, the near-tip stress is inverse-square-root with coefficient π−1∫f(s)/−s​ds.
The far-field condition is a genuine uniqueness requirement. A homogeneous stress-intensity field of a semi-infinite antiplane crack has Qh​=iC/z​, for real C. It adds no face traction but changes the stress ahead by −μC/x​. Although its stress tends to zero at infinity, its primitive is 2iCz​, which gives unbounded remote displacement and infinite total elastic energy. Thus only asking for stress to vanish at infinity would leave the homogeneous constant undetermined; excluding that added remote stress-intensity loading selects the displayed face-loaded solution. The ordinary inverse-square-root tip singularity itself has finite energy in a bounded neighbourhood and is not being excluded.
