For a nonzero complex unital Banach algebra, define
σA​(x)={λ∈C:λ1−x is not invertible in A}.
(13)
This is the spectrum of an element; its complement carries the resolvent of an element Rx​(λ)=(λ1−x)−1.
If ∣λ∣>∥x∥, the Neumann series
Rx​(λ)=λ−1∑j=0∞​(x/λ)j
(14)
converges in A. Multiplication of finite partial sums followed by a norm limit gives both inverse identities. Thus the spectrum is bounded by ∥x∥. The invertible set is open by the allowed assumption, and λ↦λ1−x is continuous, so the spectrum is closed. It is therefore compact if nonempty.
To prove nonemptiness, suppose the resolvent exists for every λ. At a fixed λ0​, the same series applied locally gives
Rx​(λ0​+h)=∑j=0∞​(−h)jRx​(λ0​)j+1
(15)
for ∣h∣∥Rx​(λ0​)∥<1, proving norm analyticity. Every bounded complex linear functional ψ∈A∗ therefore gives an entire scalar function ψ(Rx​(λ)). On compact disks it is bounded by continuity of inversion, and the large-λ series gives
∥Rx​(λ)∥≤∣λ∣−∥x∥∥1∥​⟶0.
(16)
Here the factor ∥1∥≥1 handles an algebra norm whose identity was not normalized to one. The Liouville theorem forces each scalar function to be identically zero. The Hahn-Banach theorem separates points of A, so Rx​(λ)=0, contradicting (λ1−x)Rx​(λ)=1. Hence
σA​(x) is nonempty and compact,σA​(x)⊆{∣λ∣≤∥x∥}​.
(17)
This includes an explicit proof of the nonemptiness of the Banach-algebra spectrum rather than using it as a named result.
For the algebra of entire functions, choose the compact-disk algebra norm on entire functions
∥f∥=sup∣z∣≤1​∣f(z)∣.
(18)
It is finite by continuity on a compact disk, satisfies the norm axioms, and is submultiplicative. If the norm is zero, f vanishes on the open disk, so the identity theorem gives f=0 on the plane. Thus it is an actual algebra norm, not merely a seminorm.
No complete algebra norm is possible. For the coordinate function Z(z)=z, the function Z−λ1 has a zero at λ for every complex λ and hence cannot have a multiplicative inverse among entire functions. Thus its algebraic spectrum is all of C, independently of any chosen norm. Were an algebra norm complete, the spectral theorem just proved would make that spectrum bounded, a contradiction. Therefore entire functions admit an algebra norm but no complete algebra norm. This distinguishes failure of every complete algebra norm from failure of just the displayed disk norm.
For continuous functions on the plane, construct disjoint plateaux explicitly. Let
χn​(z)=min{1,max{0,2−8∣z−n∣}},gn​(z)=max{0,1−8∣z−n∣},f(z)=∑n≥1​nχn​(z).
(19)
The supports of χn​ lie in the disks ∣z−n∣≤1/4, so they are pairwise disjoint and form a locally finite family. Thus f is continuous. On the support where gn​ is nonzero, χn​=1 and all other bumps vanish, giving fgn​=ngn​; also gn​(n)=1, so no gn​ is zero. If an algebra norm existed, submultiplicativity would imply
n∥gn​∥=∥fgn​∥≤∥f∥∥gn​∥,
(20)
so n≤∥f∥ for every positive integer, impossible. Consequently the algebra of all continuous functions on the plane admits no algebra norm, with no completeness hypothesis needed.
