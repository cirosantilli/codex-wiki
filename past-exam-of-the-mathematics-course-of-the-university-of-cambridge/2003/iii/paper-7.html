For a nonempty bounded set, let Nδ​(E) be the least number of radius-δ balls covering E. The lower and upper Minkowski dimensions are respectively
dim​M​E=liminfδ↓0​log(1/δ)logNδ​(E)​,dimM​E=limsupδ↓0​log(1/δ)logNδ​(E)​.
(1)
Using comparable cubes or ball diameters gives the same limits. For an unbounded set one must specify a local or bounded-piece convention; these are the usual bounded-set definitions used in the Kakeya set problem.
For the digit set, fixing the first k digits gives exactly 3k cylinders in intervals of length 9−k. They cover the set with at most 3k balls of comparable radius. Their base-nine prefix integers are separated by at least two, so these containing intervals have gaps at least 9−k. Choosing one point in each cylinder shows that a radius-9−k ball meets at most a bounded number of chosen points. Hence N9−k​(Q)≍3k. For 9−(k+1)<δ≤9−k, monotonicity sandwiches the covering number between constant multiples of 3k and 3k+1. This proves the box-counting dimension of separated digit sets formula here:
dim​M​Q=dimM​Q=log9log3​=21​.​
(2)
A planar Besicovitch set is a bounded set of zero Lebesgue measure containing a unit line segment in every unoriented direction. The following proof in fact applies to every bounded planar Kakeya set, whether its area is zero or positive.
Choose M≍δ−1 directions equally spaced in an angular interval of length, say, π/2, and one segment of the set in each direction. Let Tj​ be its rectangle of length one and width comparable to δ, lying in the Cδ neighborhood ECδ​. Put F=∑j​1Tj​​. Then ∫F≍Mδ≍1. Two such Kakeya tubes at angle α have overlap at most Cδ2/α, since the intersection of their supporting strips is a parallelogram of that area. The trivial bound Cδ applies for nearly parallel tubes. Thus, uniformly over their translations,
∣Tj​∩Tk​∣≤δ+αjk​Cδ2​≲1+∣j−k∣δ​.
(3)
Sum these intersections. Each row contributes at most Cδlog(2/δ), so ∫F2≲log(2/δ). The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on the union now gives the planar Kakeya neighborhood lower bound
∣ECδ​∣≥∣⋃Tj​∣≥∫F2(∫F)2​≳log(2/δ)1​.
(4)
A cover of E by Nδ​(E) radius-δ balls covers ECδ​ by their fixed-factor enlargements. Hence Nδ​(E)≳δ−2/log(2/δ). Boundedness gives the matching upper exponent Nδ​(E)≲E​δ−2. Taking the lower and upper limits proves
dim​M​E=dimM​E=2.​
(5)
The logarithmic neighborhood loss is compatible with zero area; it still forces full dimension.

A finite-field Kakeya set, also called a finite-field Besicovitch set, is a subset containing a complete affine line in a vector space a+Fp​v in every nonzero direction v, with scalar multiples counted as the same direction. The line's translate may depend on the direction.
For odd p, take the union of the lines y=mx−m2/4, m∈Fp​. Completing the square gives x2−y=(m/2−x)2. Thus that union is exactly K0​={(x,y):x2−y is a square, including zero}. For each fixed x there are (p+1)/2 such y, so ∣K0​∣=p(p+1)/2. It contains every nonvertical slope. Add the vertical line x=0, whose overlap with K0​ has (p+1)/2 points. The tangent-line finite-field Kakeya construction consequently has
∣K∣=2p2+2p−1​=21​p2(1+o(1)).​
(6)
The prime two is irrelevant to this asymptotic statement and can be handled by taking its whole plane.
For the high-dimensional lower bound, we give a direct polynomial method in combinatorics proof, which yields a stronger estimate. Suppose a finite-field Kakeya set K⊆Fpn​ has fewer than (np+n−1​) points. That binomial coefficient is the dimension of a bounded-total-degree polynomial space of polynomials of total degree at most p−1. Evaluation at the points of K imposes fewer linear conditions than this dimension. By the rank-nullity theorem, there is a nonzero polynomial P of total degree d≤p−1 vanishing on K.
For every nonzero v choose the line av​+tv⊆K. The polynomial P(av​+tv) in t has degree at most d<p and vanishes at all p field elements, so it vanishes identically. Its coefficient of td is the leading homogeneous polynomial part Pd​(v). Thus Pd​(v)=0 for every nonzero v. The case d=0 is already impossible for a nonzero constant vanishing on a nonempty set; for d>0, the absence of a constant term in this homogeneous polynomial also gives Pd​(0)=0.
A polynomial of degree less than p in each variable cannot vanish at every point of Fpn​ unless it is zero. To see this, induct on n: fix the first n−1 variables, apply the one-variable root bound in the last variable, then apply the induction hypothesis to each resulting coefficient polynomial. This forces Pd​=0, contradicting the choice of the leading part. We have proved the finite-field Kakeya polynomial bound
∣K∣≥(np+n−1​)≥n!pn​.
(7)
At n=15 this implies the required bound with an explicit absolute constant:
∣K∣≥15!p15​≥15!p9​,c=1/15!.​
(8)
The argument is valid in every prime characteristic and does not invoke the lower bound as an unproved theorem.

For the requested connection to Besicovitch sets, let δ be small and dilate the unit segments of a bounded planar Kakeya set by δ−2. Their δ-neighborhoods become direction-separated rectangles of length δ−2 and width δ−1. A smooth circle cap of angular width cδ has a Fourier extension operator of magnitude at least c′δ on such a rectangle: after removing the constant phase, the tangential phase variation is O(c) and the normal variation is O(c2). A frequency modulation translates the rectangle to any desired location. Choose separated caps for the rectangle directions, and multiply their modulated densities by independent Rademacher random variables. Because the caps are disjoint, the input remains bounded by one.
Apply the local fourth-moment estimate in a ball of radius CE​δ−2 containing the rectangles, then take expectation over the Rademacher random variables. The fourth moment dominates the square of the sum of squared packet magnitudes, giving

Scaling back gives ∣ECδ​∣≳E​1/log(1/δ), the planar Kakeya neighborhood lower bound. The covering-number argument in Question 1 then gives both Minkowski dimensions equal to two.

A Boolean function takes each vector of bits to either zero or one. Use independent uniform input bits; the influence of a variable is the probability that flipping that bit changes the value. A quite fair Boolean function has 1/4≤Ef≤3/4. All logarithms in the estimates below are natural, except where a base is explicitly written.
Here is a balanced tribes construction with unused coordinates valid for every n≥2. For each w≥1 put mw​=⌊(log2)2w⌋, and choose the largest w with wmw​≤n. Partition wmw​ of the bits into m=mw​ blocks of size w. Let f be one when at least one block is all ones, and ignore the other bits. This is a tribes function. With s=2−w, its zero probability is (1−s)m.
For w=1 the function is a single bit and has mean 1/2. For w≥2, ms≤log2 and ms≥log2−s. The elementary inequalities −s/(1−s)≤log(1−s)≤−s give
(1−s)m≥exp(−1−slog2​)≥41​,(1−s)m≤e−log2+s≤2e1/4​<43​.
(22)
Thus both output probabilities are between one quarter and three quarters.
A used bit is pivotal exactly when the other w−1 bits of its block are one and every other block fails. Hence its influence is 21−w(1−2−w)m−1≤21−w; unused bits have zero influence. Maximality of w gives n<(w+1)mw+1​≤(w+1)(log2)2w+1. Also w≤log2​n for n≥2: this is immediate for w=1, while for w≥2 one has wmw​≥2w. Therefore
Infj​(f)≤21−w<n4(log2)(w+1)​≤n4(logn+log2)​≤n8logn​<n10logn​​.
(23)
This proves the requested existence with room in the constant. The dimension-one boundary case cannot satisfy the printed logarithmic bound: a quite fair function of one bit is nonconstant and has influence one, whereas 10log1=0. Thus the statement requires the usual nontrivial range n≥2 (or its intended large-n interpretation).

We use the Bose-Chowla Sidon construction, deriving its difference property explicitly. Let p be an odd prime and choose a generator θ of the multiplicative group of a finite field of the quadratic finite field extension Fp2​. Such a generator cannot belong to Fp​, so 1,θ are linearly independent over the prime field. For each t∈Fp​, the nonzero field element θ+t has a unique exponent at​ modulo p2−1, with θat​=θ+t. The p exponents are distinct.
Suppose as​−at​=au​−av​ in the cyclic group. Multiplying the corresponding field elements gives
(θ+s)(θ+v)=(θ+u)(θ+t).
(26)
Subtract the common θ2 term. Linear independence of 1,θ gives s+v=u+t and sv=ut. Thus the two unordered pairs are the roots of the same monic quadratic polynomial, so {s,v}={u,t}. Either s=u,v=t, giving the same ordered difference, or s=t,v=u, giving two zero differences. This is exactly the Sidon set property.
Choose the integer representatives in {1,…,p2−1}. Equality of integer differences implies equality modulo p2−1, so their Sidon property persists in the integers. Now apply the given prime-gap hypothesis at x=N​ to get N​−N3/8≤p≤N​ for large N. The representatives fit inside {1,…,N}, because p2−1≤N, and their cardinality is
∣A∣=p≥N​−N3/8=N​−o(N​).​
(27)
Only the supplied short-interval prime existence and the elementary structure of a finite field are used.
