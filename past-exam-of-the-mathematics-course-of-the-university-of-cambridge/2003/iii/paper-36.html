For a linear code X≤Fn, its dual code is
X⊥={y∈Fn:∑i=1n​xi​yi​=0 for every x∈X}.
(1)
The standard bilinear form is nondegenerate. The map Fn→X∗ obtained by restricting the dot-product functional to X is surjective: extend a functional from a basis of X to a basis of Fn, then use the nondegenerate dot product to represent it. Its kernel is X⊥. The rank-nullity theorem therefore gives
dimX⊥=n−dimX.​
(2)
The first proposed description of the dual code is false without self-orthogonality. At length three take X=⟨(1,0,0)⟩. It has the specified dimension, but
X⊥={(0,a,b):a,b∈F2​},
(3)
whereas ⟨X,(1,1,1)⟩ contains (1,0,0) and (1,1,1), neither of which is in X⊥. The natural corrected statement is the odd-length binary self-orthogonal dual extension: if also X⊆X⊥, every word of X has even Hamming weight, the all-ones word lies in X⊥ but not in X, and dimensions then give X⊥=X⊕⟨1⟩.
For a binary self-dual code, x⋅x=0 for every x∈X. In F2​, this equals ∑i​xi​, so every codeword has even Hamming weight. Thus 1⋅x=0 for all x, giving 1∈X⊥=X. The dimension identity yields 2dimX=n, so n is even. Alternatively 1∈X and 1⋅1=0 itself gives evenness. Hence the second assertion is true.
For every n=2m, use
X={(u,u):u∈F2m​},G=(Im​∣Im​).
(4)
This linear code has dimension m, and (u,u)⋅(v,v)=2(u⋅v)=0. Therefore X⊆X⊥; both have dimension m, so equality holds. This constructs a binary self-dual code at every even length. Conversely the dimension identity already proves every self-dual length-n linear code has dimension n/2.
For a nonbinary example, over F5​ take X=⟨(1,2)⟩. Its generator has self-product 1+22=0 in F5​, so X⊆X⊥. Both are one-dimensional, proving
⟨(1,2)⟩≤F52​ is self-dual.​
(5)

A finite field is a field with finitely many elements. Its characteristic is the least positive integer p for which p⋅1=0; such an integer exists because the additive group is finite. If p=ab with 1<a,b<p, then (a⋅1)(b⋅1)=0 although neither factor is zero, impossible in a field. Hence p is prime, and the multiples of 1 form its prime field Fp​. The whole field is a finite-dimensional vector space over this subfield, say of dimension s≥1, so its cardinality is ps. Thus q=ps and its characteristic is p.
For existence, let K be a splitting field over Fp​ of P(T)=Tps−T. Since P′(T)=−1, it has exactly ps distinct roots. Its root set F contains 0,1 and is closed under addition and multiplication, because iterated Frobenius endomorphism gives
(a+b)ps=aps+bps,(ab)ps=apsbps.
(6)
It is also closed under additive inverses, and for a=0, (a−1)ps=(aps)−1=a−1. Thus F is a field with exactly ps elements. Since it contains every root, it is the entire splitting field. This proves existence.
For uniqueness, let L be any field with ps elements. Its nonzero multiplicative group has order ps−1, so Lagrange's theorem gives aps=a for every a∈L, including zero. Hence L is a splitting field of the same polynomial. Here is the embedding argument giving uniqueness, rather than merely invoking it by name. Extend the identity on Fp​ from one root at a time. If an embedding is already defined on E and a new root α has minimal polynomial of an algebraic element h over E, the transported polynomial divides P and therefore has a root b in L. Evaluation at b induces an embedding E[T]/(h)→L, extending the existing embedding to E(α). Repeating for the finitely many roots embeds the constructed field F in L. They have the same finite cardinality, so this embedding is an isomorphism. Thus the field of order ps is unique up to isomorphism.
We prove cyclicity of the multiplicative group, also establishing the more general finite multiplicative subgroup of a field is cyclic result. Let H be a finite subgroup of a field's multiplicative group, and let e be the least common multiple of all element orders. For every prime ℓ dividing e, choose an element whose order has the maximal ℓ-power ℓaℓ​; raising it to the factor of its order coprime to ℓ gives an element of order ℓaℓ​. Since the group is abelian, the product of these elements has order ∏ℓ​ℓaℓ​=e: for commuting elements of coprime orders, an equation (ab)t=1 forces at=b−t into the intersection of their cyclic subgroups, which is trivial, so both orders divide t. Every member of H is a root of Te−1. The Lagrange root bound over a field gives ∣H∣≤e, while the element just constructed has order e and gives e≤∣H∣. Thus equality holds and that element generates H. In particular
Fps×​≅Z/(ps−1)Z.​

A root of unity over a finite field is an element a∈Fq​ satisfying an=1, with n≥1; the algebraic closure is essential because all roots need not lie in the base field. Write p=charFq​ and n=pam, where p∤m. In characteristic p,
Tn−1=(Tm−1)pa.
(13)
The latter polynomial has m distinct roots, since the derivative mTm−1 is nonzero at every root. Those roots are closed under multiplication and inversion and contain 1, so E(n,q) is a finite multiplicative subgroup of a field. The proof in the preceding solution makes it a cyclic group. In particular
∣E(n,q)∣=m,∣E(n,q)∣=n if gcd(n,q)=1.​
(14)
This also explains why the coprimality qualification is needed.
The multiplicative group of a finite field Fqs×​ is cyclic of order qs−1. It contains the entire group of mth roots precisely when m∣qs−1. For necessity, a generator of E(n,q) has order m and Lagrange's theorem applies. For sufficiency, a cyclic group whose order is divisible by m contains m roots of Tm−1; since there are exactly m roots in the algebraic closure, these are all of them. Hence the least positive extension degree is
s=ordm​(q)(m>1),s=1(m=1).​
(15)
Here ordm​(q) is the multiplicative order of the residue class of q modulo m. If n is coprime to q, replace m by n. Existence of this order follows because q is a unit modulo m.
A primitive nth root of unity over a finite field has multiplicative order exactly n. If p∣n, no such element exists, since all roots have order dividing m<n. If gcd(n,q)=1, choose a generator ζ of E(n,q). The element ζj has order n/gcd(n,j), so it is primitive precisely when gcd(n,j)=1. Therefore there are φ(n) primitive roots, with φ the Euler totient function.
If ω is primitive of order n, then ω∈Fqℓ​ exactly when ωqℓ=ω, equivalently n∣qℓ−1. The implication from that equality to membership follows because the roots of Tqℓ−T form Fqℓ​, as proved in the preceding solution. Consequently
min{ℓ≥1:ω∈Fqℓ​}=ordn​(q)​
(16)
for n>1, and the degree is one for n=1. This is also the length of the finite-field Frobenius automorphism orbit of ω.
Using the same (1,u) basis of F9​ as before, the fourth roots of unity are
E(4,9)={1,−1,u,−u}={(1,0),(2,0),(0,1),(0,2)}.​
(17)
There are four, they are already in F9​, and the primitive fourth roots are the two vectors (0,1) and (0,2).

A cyclic code of length n over Fq​ is a linear code invariant under (c0​,…,cn−1​)↦(cn−1​,c0​,…,cn−2​). Associate the word with c(T)=∑i=0n−1​ci​Ti. The shift is multiplication by T modulo Tn−1, so shift-invariant linear subspaces are exactly the ideals of Fq​[T]/(Tn−1). The inverse image of such an ideal in Fq​[T] is principal and contains Tn−1; hence it is generated by a unique monic divisor g of Tn−1. The code has basis g,Tg,…,Tn−degg−1g, and dimension n−degg.
The defining zeros of a cyclic code are the roots of its generator g in a splitting field. Since g∣Tn−1, each is a root of unity over a finite field. Every codeword vanishes at each such root. If the characteristic divides n, root multiplicities must additionally be retained; in the Hamming construction below the modulus has no repeated roots.
The unrestricted ternary cyclic-equivalence assertion is false. At s=2 it would give a cyclic [4,2,3]3​ code. But
T4−1=(T−1)(T+1)(T2+1)
(18)
over F3​, with the quadratic irreducible. A dimension-two cyclic code needs a degree-two generator, so its only possibilities are T2−1 and T2+1. Each is a weight-two codeword. Therefore neither has minimum distance three, and no code equivalent to the ternary [4,2,3] Hamming code can be cyclic. A valid general version of the asserted construction assumes odd s. The usual distance-three Hamming parameters also require s≥2; when s=1 the code is the length-one zero code.
Here is the construction with its precise qualification. A ternary Hamming code has a parity-check matrix whose columns represent each one-dimensional subspace of F3s​ once. There are n=(3s−1)/2 such subspaces. Its rank is s, because the coordinate lines are included. Its minimum distance is at least three, because no column is zero and no two are proportional. For s≥2, two independent vectors and their sum determine three distinct columns, and, after adjusting their nonzero representative scalars, give a weight-three linear relation. Thus its parameters are [n,n−s,3]3​. Permuting columns and changing their nonzero representatives produces a monomial equivalence of linear codes.
Take odd s≥3 and choose α∈F3s×​ of order n. Since n is odd, −1 is not in the cyclic subgroup generated by α. Hence 1,α,…,αn−1 lie in distinct one-dimensional F3​-subspaces: a proportional pair would have ratio 1 or −1, neither possible for two distinct powers in this list. By their number they represent all projective points. Consequently
C={c∈F3n​:∑i=0n−1​ci​αi=0}
(19)
is monomially equivalent to the ternary Hamming code. Since αn=1, evaluation at α descends to F3​[T]/(Tn−1) and its kernel is an ideal, so C is cyclic.
The degree of α over F3​ is s. If it were d<s, its generated field would have 3d elements and its element order would give
n≤3d−1≤3s−1−1<23s−1​=n,
(20)
a contradiction. Its minimal polynomial of an algebraic element therefore has degree s. Applying Frobenius to a polynomial relation shows that α,α3,…,α3s−1 are its roots; they are distinct by the same field-degree argument. Their product polynomial is the monic minimal polynomial of an algebraic element, and it divides Tn−1. Thus
g(T)=j=0∏s−1​(T−α3j),Z(C)={α3j:0≤j<s}.​
(21)
This identifies all the defining zeros. For s=3, n=13, the defining exponents are 1,3,9 modulo 13.
There is also a uniform construction for all s≥2, explaining the missing qualification. Let β generate F3s×​, which has order 2n. The powers 1,β,…,βn−1 represent all projective points, since proportional powers differ by exponent 0 or n modulo 2n. But βn=−1, so the kernel of c↦c(β) is an ideal modulo Tn+1, a negacyclic code. Multiplication by T gives (−cn−1​,c0​,…,cn−2​), preserving its kernel. This code is always equivalent to the ternary Hamming code. If s is odd, n is odd too, and the substitution T↦−T, or coordinate scaling ci​↦(−1)ici​, changes this negacyclic construction into the cyclic one. In fact α=−β then has order n. These explicit constructions prove the qualified assertion rather than relying on a theorem name.
For decoding, use the printed 13×3 matrix H with received words as row vectors; the conventional 3×13 parity-check matrix is HT. The received word's syndrome is
s=rH=(2,0,2)in F33​.
(22)
A single-symbol error of value a at position i has syndrome ahi​, where hi​ is row i of the printed matrix. Here
(2,0,2)=2(1,0,1)=2h6​.
(23)
Therefore subtract 2 at the sixth coordinate, changing its received value 1 to 2 modulo three. The decoded codeword is
(2,1,2,0,1,2,0,0,2,1,1,2,0).​
(24)
Its syndrome is zero. The thirteen projective rows and the two nonzero scalars give all 26 nonzero syndromes exactly once, so this is the unique codeword at distance one. It is the transmitted word under the usual assumption of at most one symbol error. If positions 1,2,5 are designated check positions, their independent unit rows make the other ten symbols systematic data, namely (2,0,2,0,0,2,1,1,2,0).
