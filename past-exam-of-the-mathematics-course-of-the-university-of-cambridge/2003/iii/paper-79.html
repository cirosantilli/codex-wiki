Write SL​=∑i=1L​Ai​ and ZL​=SL​/L. The cumulant-generating function of one normal random variable is
κ(θ)=logEeθA1​=μθ+2σ2θ2​,θ∈R.
(2)
Cramér's theorem states that empirical means of independent and identically distributed random variables whose moment-generating function is finite on a neighborhood of zero satisfy a large deviation principle at the sample-size large-deviation speed, with good rate function equal to the Legendre-Fenchel transform of their cumulant-generating function. Here that gives
I(x)=supθ∈R​{θ(x−μ)−2σ2θ2​}.
(3)
For σ2>0, differentiation gives the maximizing parameter θ=(x−μ)/σ2, and hence
I(x)=2σ2(x−μ)2​.​
(4)
This rate function tends to infinity as ∣x∣→∞, so its finite sublevel sets are compact. Equivalently, the Gärtner–Ellis theorem applies because the limiting scaled cumulant-generating function is finite and differentiable everywhere; it is lower semicontinuous and essentially smooth, with no finite domain boundary at which to check steepness. If σ2=0 is permitted, ZL​=μ deterministically and the rate function is zero at μ and infinity elsewhere.

The product large-deviation principle states that independent families satisfying large deviation principles at the same speed with good rate functions J and I have joint good rate function J(b)+I(z). The contraction principle for large deviations states that a continuous map f sends such a principle to one with good rate function inff(u)=x​I(u). Apply these results to (B/L,SL​/L) and addition. The resulting infimal convolution is
K(x)=infb≥0​{λb+2σ2(x−b−μ)2​}.
(5)
For σ2>0, the unconstrained stationary point is b=x−μ−λσ2. Convexity gives the constrained minimum at b∗​=(x−μ−λσ2)+, so
K(x)=⎩⎨⎧​2σ2(x−μ)2​,λ(x−μ)−2λ2σ2​,​x≤μ+λσ2,x≥μ+λσ2.​​
(6)
The two branches agree in value and first derivative at their junction. Both tails tend to infinity, giving a good rate function. This is the fixed exponential perturbation of a Gaussian empirical mean.
There is a reason to use the product large-deviation principle rather than invoke the supplied Gärtner–Ellis theorem without checking its hypotheses. The limiting scaled cumulant-generating function here is
Λ(θ)={μθ+σ2θ2/2,+∞,​θ<λ,θ≥λ.​
(7)
At λ it is neither lower semicontinuous nor steep from the left: its left derivative tends to the finite value μ+λσ2. Its Legendre-Fenchel transform still equals K, but the stated Gärtner–Ellis theorem does not establish the full lower bound on the linear branch. The product large-deviation principle and contraction principle for large deviations do establish it. In the degenerate case σ2=0, the answer is K(x)=λ(x−μ) for x≥μ, and infinity otherwise.

All three normalized sums converge in probability to μ: the weak law of large numbers controls SL​/L, and the two fixed random variables vanish after division by L. Their rare fluctuations nevertheless differ. A fixed normal random variable would have to fluctuate by order L to alter the mean by a fixed amount, and that costs exp(−constantL2). A fixed exponential random variable can do the same for a cost of order exp(−constantL), exactly the large-deviation speed of the empirical mean.
Below μ+λσ2, the optimal exponential contribution is zero, and the Gaussian empirical mean supplies the fluctuation. Above it, the optimal empirical mean remains at μ+λσ2, while B/L supplies x−μ−λσ2. This explains the quadratic-to-linear change in the rate function and why only the upper tail is altered. Convergence to the same typical value does not imply the same large-deviation behavior.

Work in a Hausdorff space E, for example a metric space. A rate function is a lower semicontinuous map I:E→[0,∞]. A good rate function additionally has compact finite sublevel sets {x:I(x)≤r}.
A large deviation principle at large-deviation speed L requires, for every open set G and closed set F,
−infx∈G​I(x)≤liminfL→∞​L1​logP(XL​∈G),limsupL→∞​L1​logP(XL​∈F)≤−infx∈F​I(x).
(12)
The conventions are log0=−∞ and inf∅=+∞. A weak large deviation principle imposes the same lower bound but only the upper bound for compact sets. Exponential tightness supplies compact sets KM​ with limsupL−1logP(XL​∈/KM​)<−M for every M≥0. The strict inequality can equivalently be obtained from the common non-strict definition by choosing a larger containment level.

Fix a finite r≥0 and choose M>r. By exponential tightness, choose a compact set KM​ with outside probability having upper exponential rate strictly below −M. A compact set in a Hausdorff space is closed, so its complement is an open set. The lower bound of the weak large deviation principle gives
−infx∈/KM​​I(x)≤liminfL​L1​logP(XL​∈/KM​)≤limsupL​L1​logP(XL​∈/KM​)<−M.
(13)
Consequently infKMc​​I>M, and {I≤r}⊂KM​. Lower semicontinuity makes this sublevel set closed in E, hence a closed subset of a compact set. Thus it is compact. Every finite sublevel set is compact, so I is a good rate function.

Let F be any closed set. For each M>0, choose KM​ as above. The probability decomposition
P(XL​∈F)≤P(XL​∈F∩KM​)+P(XL​∈/KM​)
(14)
reduces the first term to a compact set, where the weak upper bound applies. For nonnegative numbers uL​,vL​, the inequality uL​+vL​≤2max(uL​,vL​) shows that the upper exponential rate of their sum is no larger than the maximum of their upper exponential rates. Hence
limsupL​L1​logP(XL​∈F)≤max{−infF∩KM​​I,−M}≤max{−infF​I,−M}.
(15)
Let M→∞. This proves the full closed-set upper bound, including when infF​I=+∞. The open-set lower bound was already assumed. Combining with part (b), the weak principle upgrades to a large deviation principle with good rate function I. This is exponential tightness upgrades a weak large deviation principle.

Qt​=(Qt−1​+at​−c)+.​
(16)

Q0(N)​(a,c)=max0≤n≤N​{∑j=0n−1​a−j​−cn},
(17)

Q0​(a,c)=n≥0sup​{An​(a)−cn},An​(a)=j=0∑n−1​a−j​,A0​=0.​
(18)
If the mean of the remote past is λ<c, then An​(a)/n→λ, so An​(a)−cn→−∞. The supremum is then finite and is attained at a finite window. There is an indexing issue in the printed topology. Its cumulative sums omit a0​, whereas the post-slot workload of a queue in part (a) includes a0​. To see the problem, take all at​=λ for t≤−1, with λ<c, and compare two inputs with a0​=λ and a0​=c+1. Their distance according to the printed cumulative-sum expression is zero, yet
Q0​(a,c)=0,Q0​(b,c)=1.
(19)
Thus the post-slot workload is not continuous in the literal printed topology. On two-sided sequences that expression is a seminorm, since it also ignores future coordinates; the fixed-mean input class is not a vector space (if signed inputs are allowed, it is an affine set).
The appropriate repair for the post-slot convention is to use one-sided past inputs including time zero and the weighted cumulative-input topology for a slotted queue:
d#​(a,b)=supn≥1​n+1∣An​(a)−An​(b)∣​.
(20)
This is a finite metric on the fixed-mean class, since cumulative differences are o(n); equality of all cumulative sums implies equality of all past coordinates. It is equivalent to augmenting the printed cumulative-difference distance by ∣a0​−b0​∣: their cumulative sums differ only by this extra coordinate and a one-step shift.
Here is the full corrected continuity proof. Fix a with An​(a)/n→λ<c, and let ε=(c−λ)/4. Choose N≥1 such that An​(a)/n≤λ+ε whenever n≥N. If d#​(a,b)<ε/2, then, for all such n,
An​(b)−cn≤n(λ+ε−c)+(n+1)d#​(a,b)≤n(λ+2ε−c)<0.
(21)
Both queue workloads therefore maximize over the same finite collection 0≤n<N, containing the value zero. For any two finite lists, the difference of their maxima is at most the maximum absolute difference of corresponding entries. Thus
∣Q0​(a,c)−Q0​(b,c)∣≤(N+1)d#​(a,b)​
(22)
in this neighborhood. In particular the queue workload is locally Lipschitz continuous and hence continuous.
An alternative indexing repair retains the printed topology but interprets the time-zero function as the workload before slot (−1,0), namely supn≥0​{∑j=1n​a−j​−cn}. The same finite-window argument proves its continuity. One must then shift the time labels consistently in the later recursions. The remaining answers use the post-slot convention and d#​.

Define the downstream queue workload by starting both queues empty at time −N and taking the increasing remote-past limit. For each finite N, part (c) makes their total queue workload exactly the single-server workload with service d, while the upstream queue workload is the single-server workload with service c. Therefore
R0(N)​(a,c,d)=Q0(N)​(a,d)−Q0(N)​(a,c).
(27)
The downstream limit is increasing: the coupled upstream and downstream recursions are increasing in their initial state, because Dt​=min(c,Qt−1​+at​) is increasing in Qt−1​. With finite upstream queue workload, taking the limit gives
R0​(a,c,d)=Q0​(a,d)−Q0​(a,c).​
(28)
The result is nonnegative because reducing service cannot reduce queue workload. It can be +∞ when the slower server is unstable; under λ<d<c, both quantities are finite. The finite-upstream condition matters: subtracting two infinite queue workloads would not define a downstream queue.

The indexing defect also affects the downstream claim. For the two inputs in part (b), now choose λ<d<c. The constant input has zero downstream queue workload, while the input with a0​=c+1 has
Q0​(b,d)=c+1−d,Q0​(b,c)=1,R0​(b,c,d)=c−d>0.
(29)
Their printed distance is zero, so the literal post-slot downstream continuity assertion is false as well.
In the corrected weighted cumulative-input topology for a slotted queue, apply the finite-window proof of part (b) separately with services d and c. Both have strictly negative long-window drift because λ<d<c. There are a neighborhood of a and finite constants Cd​,Cc​ such that
∣R0​(a,c,d)−R0​(b,c,d)∣≤∣Q0​(a,d)−Q0​(b,d)∣+∣Q0​(a,c)−Q0​(b,c)∣≤(Cd​+Cc​)d#​(a,b).
(30)
This proves continuity, and indeed local Lipschitz continuity, for the intended downstream function.
Suppose a family of random arrival paths a(L) has a large deviation principle in this corrected input space, at speed L with good rate function I. The contraction principle for large deviations then gives the downstream good rate function
JR​(r)=inf{I(a):Q0​(a,d)−Q0​(a,c)=r}.​
(31)
It is infinity for r<0. The path large deviation principle, its topology, and its subcritical domain must actually be established for the chosen stochastic model: a collection of finite-dimensional large deviation principles alone does not control infinitely long workload windows. Nor should upstream departures be assumed independent; the path contraction avoids that unjustified assumption.

Consider a stationary nonnegative work-input process in slotted time, with cumulative work An​ over the last n slots and a work-conserving server of capacity c per slot. Assume its past average tends almost surely to m<c. The Lindley recursion then defines the finite stationary queue workload
Q=supn≥0​(An​−cn).
(32)
This is work remaining, rather than the number of customers when customer service requirements vary. With unit work per customer the two notions coincide. Stationarity makes the distribution of a past cumulative window equal to that of a forward window of the same length.
For a parameter θ>0 and a positive window length n, the effective bandwidth is
α(θ,n)=θn1​logEeθAn​.​
(33)
It measures a traffic rate through exponential moments, so it retains information about rare bursts that the mean alone discards. If the limiting scaled cumulant-generating function exists, put
κ(θ)=limn→∞​n1​logEeθAn​,α(θ)=θκ(θ)​.
(34)
The finite-window effective bandwidth matters when the relevant overflow window is short or when arrivals are correlated; one should not replace it by its long-window limit without justification.
The following proof gives an actual upper bound for the infinite-horizon queue workload. For B>0, if Q>B, at least one positive integer n has An​−cn>B. The union bound followed by the Chernoff bound gives
P(Q>B)≤∑n≥1​P(An​>B+cn)≤e−θB∑n≥1​exp{θn[α(θ,n)−c]}.
(35)
Assume each finite-window exponential moment is finite and κ(θ)<cθ. Choose η>0 such that κ(θ)+η<cθ. For all sufficiently large n, the summand is at most e−n(cθ−κ(θ)−η). Its tail is a convergent geometric series, and its finite prefix is finite. Denote the resulting sum by Kθ​. The exponential workload bound from cumulative arrival moments therefore proves, for b>0,
limsupL→∞​L1​logP(Q>Lb)≤−θb.
(36)
Optimizing over admissible parameters gives
L→∞limsup​L1​logP(Q>Lb)≤−bθ∗​,θ∗​=sup{θ>0:κ(θ)<cθ}.​
(37)
Only parameters with the stated finite-window moments are included. If there are no such positive parameters, the argument yields only the trivial exponent zero. Infinite θ∗​ means that the upper rate is −∞. For a non-strict threshold Q≥Lb, first bound it by Q>L(b−ε) and then let ε↓0. This proof permits dependence between slots. It proves the required upper bound; it does not assert a matching lower bound for every arrival process.
This also gives a closed-set large-deviation upper bound for Q/L: set I+​(q)=θ∗​q for q>0, I+​(0)=0, and I+​(q)=+∞ for q<0. If a closed set F contains zero, its desired upper bound is the trivial bound zero. If F contains positive points but not zero, its positive part has a smallest point b>0, and P(Q/L∈F)≤P(Q≥Lb) gives the bound −infF​I+​. If it contains no nonnegative points, its probability is zero. This verifies the upper half of a large deviation principle; the actual rate function can be larger when a matching lower bound is unavailable.
For independent and identically distributed slot arrivals at​, let κ(θ)=logEeθa0​. Now logEeθAn​=nκ(θ), so effective bandwidth is independent of window length. For κ(θ)<cθ, the geometric-series prefactor is explicitly
Kθ​=1−eκ(θ)−cθeκ(θ)−cθ​.
(38)
Thus the familiar workload Chernoff bound for independent increments is a special case, and the largest admissible parameter is often the positive root of κ(θ)=cθ.
For these independent increments there is a sharper argument that works at the root itself when its exponential moment is finite. Along the past read in reverse order, put Sn​=An​−cn and Mn​=eθSn​. If Eeθ(a0​−c)≤1, then Mn​ is a nonnegative supermartingale starting at one. For the first crossing time τB​=inf{n≥0:Sn​≥B}, bounded optional stopping gives
1≥EMτB​∧N​≥eθBP(τB​≤N).
(39)
Let N→∞. Strict negative drift makes Sn​→−∞ almost surely, so its supremum is attained and the crossing event agrees with {Q≥B}. Consequently
P(Q≥B)≤e−θB.​
(40)
This strengthens the prefactor but uses temporal independence, which was unnecessary for the previous proof.
Several examples show what the effective bandwidth constraint means. Deterministic traffic of r work units per slot has κ(θ)=θr and effective bandwidth r at every parameter; if c≥r there is no stationary backlog. For independent on-off slots carrying r units with probability p and zero otherwise, the Bernoulli effective bandwidth is
α(θ)=θlog(1−p+peθr)​.
(41)
For 0<p<1, it rises from the mean pr to the peak r. When pr<c<r, strict convexity of κ(θ)−cθ, its negative derivative at zero, and its divergence to infinity give a unique positive root. When c≥r, no slot can increase the queue workload. In the specific case r=2,c=1,p<1/2, the workload increases by one with probability p and decreases by one, reflected at zero, with probability 1−p. Its stationary probabilities satisfy
πk+1​=1−pp​πk​,πk​=(1−q)qk,q=1−pp​.
(42)
The service-root equation has eθ∗​=(1−p)/p, and thus P(Q≥k)=qk=e−θ∗​k. This example realizes the upper exponent exactly.
For independent Poisson slot counts of mean ν, with unit work per arrival, the Poisson effective bandwidth is
α(θ)=θν(eθ−1)​.
(43)
Stability requires c>ν. There is a unique positive root of ν(eθ−1)=cθ, again by strict convexity, negative initial derivative after subtracting cθ, and growth to infinity. For independent exponentially distributed work with rate β, the corresponding expressions are
κ(θ)=−log(1−θ/β),α(θ)=θ−log(1−θ/β)​,0<θ<β.
(44)
If c>1/β, the positive service root lies strictly below β. The finite moment-generating function domain cannot be ignored.
To see statistical aggregation explicitly, let the entire arrival processes of k sources be independent, though each source may itself have dependence over time. Their cumulative totals satisfy
Eeθ∑j=1k​An(j)​=∏j=1k​EeθAn(j)​,αtotal​(θ,n)=j=1∑k​αj​(θ,n).​
(45)
Thus the shared queue has an exponential overflow bound when ∑j​αj​(θ)<c, with the finite-window conditions proved above. One must use a common parameter θ; adding individual service-root exponents is not the rule. For example, an independent Poisson source of mean ν and an independent on-off source of size r and probability p have aggregate cumulant-generating function
κtotal​(θ)=ν(eθ−1)+log(1−p+peθr).
(46)
For ν>0 and c>ν+pr, their positive service root exists and determines the optimized upper exponent. Several independent Poisson sources similarly have the same formula as one source with intensity ∑j​νj​.
The connection with large deviation principles also appears in a many-source scaling. For L independent copies of a source, aggregate service Lc and buffer Lb, fix a window n. Let Xi​(n) be each copy's cumulative input in that window. Their total exceeds L(b+cn) only if the empirical mean exceeds b+cn. Its Chernoff bound has exponent
Vn​(b)=supθ≥0​{θ(b+cn)−logEeθX1​(n)}=supθ≥0​θ{b+n[c−α(θ,n)]}.
(47)
If exponential moments exist near zero, Cramér's theorem gives the corresponding finite-window large deviation principle. The threshold exceeds the mean because m<c and b>0. A finite union of windows has upper exponential rate at most −minn​Vn​(b) over those windows. Passing to infinitely many windows requires uniform control of long-window probabilities, not simply an interchange of a limit with an infinite union. A good sample-path large deviation principle in a topology where the queue workload is continuous, as in Question 3, instead permits the contraction principle for large deviations to derive a workload rate function and its closed-set overflow upper bound.
Finally, the mean and peak limits of effective bandwidth quantify the safety margin. With exponential moments near zero, independent slot work of mean m and variance v has
α(θ)=m+2vθ​+O(θ2).
(48)
For bounded work, its large-parameter limit is the essential peak. Stronger tail requirements select larger θ and hence more capacity; finite-window effective bandwidths also reflect temporal burst correlations. A stable heavy-tailed input can lack every positive exponential moment, in which case this exponential-bound method supplies no positive decay rate. Stability and exponentially small overflow are distinct requirements.
