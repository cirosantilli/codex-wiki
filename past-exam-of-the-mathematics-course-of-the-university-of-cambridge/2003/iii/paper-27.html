The cellular approximation theorem says that a continuous map between CW complexes is homotopic to a cellular map, meaning that it sends each q-skeleton into the target's q-skeleton. Give Sn, for n>0, its CW complex structure with one zero-cell and one n-cell. Its n-skeleton is the whole sphere, so the theorem applies and gives the requested result. For n=0 use the two zero-cells of S0. In particular the conclusion is
f≃g,g(Sn)⊆Xn​.​
(1)
There is no local-finiteness assumption on X.
For completeness, here is a direct proof of the needed case of cellular approximation, including the point-avoidance step. We use three standard facts, stated explicitly. First, a compact subset of a CW complex lies in a finite subcomplex. Second, a continuous map from a smooth manifold into Euclidean space has arbitrarily close smooth approximations, and smooth cutoffs exist around compact subsets of open sets. Third, the Sard theorem says the critical values of a smooth map have measure zero; when the domain dimension is smaller than the target dimension, its entire image has measure zero.
The first fact has a useful short proof. If a compact set met infinitely many open cells, choose one point in each of countably many distinct cells. Closure-finiteness makes every closed cell meet the chosen set, and every subset of it, in finitely many points. The weak topology of a CW complex then makes all those subsets closed. The chosen set is an infinite closed discrete subset of a compact space, a contradiction. Taking the closures of the finitely many cells met proves the claim.
Now f(Sn) lies in a finite subcomplex Y. Take a top-dimensional open cell ed of Y with d>n, and identify its interior with the open unit ball in Rd using its characteristic map. On the open set U=f−1(ed), write F:U→Bd for the coordinate expression. Choose 0<r′<r′′<1 and a smooth cutoff χ supported in F−1(Br′′​) and equal to one near the compact set F−1(Br′​). By smooth approximation of maps into Euclidean space, choose a smooth h:U→Rd with ∣h−F∣<δ, where δ<min(r′/2,1−r′′). Replace F by
G=F+χ(h−F).
(2)
The straight-line interpolation is a homotopy supported away from the boundary of the cell and stays inside that cell. Where χ=1, we have ∣F∣>r′ and hence ∣G∣>r′/2. By the Sard theorem, choose z∈Br′/4​ outside h(U). Where χ=1 we have G=h, and elsewhere ∣G∣>r′/2, so the modified map avoids z everywhere.
Radial retraction of Dd∖{z} onto its boundary fixes that boundary. It therefore descends through the characteristic map, including its possibly noninjective boundary identifications, and fixes all other cells of Y. Composing gives a homotopy whose endpoint misses all of ed. Remove every cell of dimension greater than n in descending order. There are finitely many such cells, so their homotopies concatenate to yield g:Sn→Yn⊆Xn​. This proves dimension reduction of sphere maps into CW skeleta. The approximation step is essential: an arbitrary continuous image of Sn can fill a higher-dimensional region, so dimension alone would not supply an omitted point.

The Morse lemma states that if p is a nondegenerate critical point of a smooth real function f on an m-dimensional smooth manifold, there are smooth coordinates y1​,…,ym​, centered at p, such that
f(y)=f(p)−i=1∑λ​yi2​+i=λ+1∑m​yi2​.​
(3)
Here λ is the Morse index, the number of negative directions of the Hessian matrix at p. The equality is exact on a neighborhood, not merely a second-order expansion.
To prove it, start with any coordinates putting p=0. Since df(0)=0, integral Taylor theorem gives
f(x)−f(0)=∑i,j=1m​aij​(x)xi​xj​,aij​(x)=∫01​(1−t)∂i​∂j​f(tx)dt.
(4)
The matrix A(x)=(aij​(x)) is smooth and symmetric, with A(0)=21​Hessf(0). By Sylvester's law of inertia, a constant invertible linear coordinate change makes A(0)=diag(−Iλ​,Im−λ​).
We perform completion of squares with coefficients depending smoothly on the original x. The first pivot a11​(x) stays nonzero and keeps its sign on a sufficiently small neighborhood. Algebraically,
xTA(x)x=a11​(x)(x1​+∑j>1​a11​(x)a1j​(x)​xj​)2+∑i,j>1​(aij​(x)−a11​(x)ai1​(x)a1j​(x)​)xi​xj​.
(5)
The remaining coefficient matrix is the Schur complement. At zero it is the remaining signed diagonal matrix, so its first pivot is also nonzero nearby. Continue recursively, shrinking the neighborhood finitely many times. Every pivot di​(x) is smooth and nonzero, with sign ϵi​=−1 for i≤λ and +1 thereafter. Absorb ∣di​(x)∣ into the square by its smooth positive square root. This produces a smooth triangular matrix B(x) with nonzero diagonal such that
xTA(x)x=∑i​ϵi​(B(x)x)i2​.
(6)
All coefficients here are functions of the original x; no circular coordinate substitution is involved. Define Φ(x)=B(x)x. Its derivative at zero is DΦ(0)=B(0), which is invertible. The inverse function theorem makes y=Φ(x) a smooth coordinate system and gives the asserted exact quadratic form. The number of negative squares agrees with the Hessian matrix signature and hence with the Morse index. This is the smooth completing-square proof of the Morse lemma.

A Morse function is a smooth real-valued function all of whose critical points have nonsingular Hessian matrices. We use the descending convention for a gradient-like vector field V: away from critical points, df(V)<0, and near a critical point of Morse index λ, in Morse lemma coordinates f=f(p)−∣u∣2+∣v∣2, the field can be chosen as V=(2u,−2v).
For its flow ϕt​, define the unstable manifold Wu(p)={x:limt→−∞​ϕt​(x)=p} and the stable manifold Ws(q)={x:limt→+∞​ϕt​(x)=q}. Their dimensions are indp and m−indq. The Morse-Smale transversality condition is
Tx​Wu(p)+Tx​Ws(q)=Tx​Mfor every x∈Wu(p)∩Ws(q).
(7)
For a closed manifold, this lets us form the Morse-Smale complex over Z. Orient each unstable manifold and take
Cj​=⨁indp=j​Zp,∂p=∑indq=j−1​n(p,q)q.
(8)
The transverse intersection for an index difference of one is one-dimensional; quotienting by flow time gives a finite set of trajectories, and n(p,q) counts them with orientation signs. Compactifying the one-dimensional spaces of trajectories for an index difference of two adds broken trajectories. Their oriented boundary count is zero, giving ∂2=0. The descending cells are the unstable manifolds, and these counts are the incidence numbers of the corresponding cellular chain complex, so the homology of this complex is H∗​(M;Z). Ambient orientability is unnecessary: orientations of the unstable cells provide the requisite coorientations and trajectory signs.
For the Klein bottle, use the quotient of R2 by (s,t)∼(s+2π,−t) and (s,t)∼(s,t+2π). A model of the standard four-critical-point height function is
f(s,t)=coss+εcost,0<ε<1.
(9)
It is well defined under both identifications. Its critical points, their Morse indices, and critical values are
pointm=(π,π)b=(π,0)a=(0,π)q=(0,0)​index0112​f−1−ε−1+ε1−ε1+ε.​​
(10)
The Hessian matrix is diagonal with entries −coss and −εcost, so each point is nondegenerate. The flat quotient metric gives descending equations s˙=sins, t˙=εsint. Their saddle separatrices are the coordinate lines; the two saddle-to-saddle intersections are empty, and all remaining stable and unstable intersections are transverse. This is a Morse-Smale gradient flow. If one insists on the exact local-model version of a gradient-like vector field above, change the two coordinate speeds near the critical points to the standard quadratic-model speeds. This preserves the separatrices, signs and transversality.
Both branches of either saddle flow to the minimum with opposite endpoint signs, so ∂a=∂b=0. From the maximum to a, the two trajectories lie on s=0 and approach t=π from opposite directions. The identification in t is a translation, so their incidence signs are opposite and cancel. The two trajectories to b lie on t=0 and approach s=π from opposite directions. The identification in s reverses the transverse t-coordinate, reversing one additional orientation sign. These two contributions consequently have the same sign. Choosing the orientation of b gives ∂q=2b.
One can check the coefficient directly from the cell attachment of the top cell: its boundary word is aba−1b, with exponent sums zero on the base loop a and two on the fiber loop b. Thus the integral Morse complex of the Klein bottle is
0⟶Zq(20​)​Za⊕Zb0​Zm⟶0,
(11)
and
H0​(K;Z)=Z,H1​(K;Z)=Z⊕Z/2,H2​(K;Z)=0.​
(12)
Counting trajectories without signs, or working only modulo two, would miss the order-two class.

Work over Q. A closed connected orientable surface of genus g has Betti numbers (1,2g,1); the circle has Betti numbers (1,1). The Künneth theorem therefore gives
(b0​,b1​,b2​,b3​)(S1×Σg​)=(1,2g+1,2g+1,1).
(13)
We use the following precise consequence of the Morse handle-attachment theorem: a closed manifold with a Morse function has the homotopy type of a finite CW complex with one index-j cell for each index-j critical point. Its degree-j rational cellular chain group has dimension cj​, so its homology has dimension at most cj​. Consequently cj​≥bj​, the weak Morse inequalities. Summing yields
#Crit(f)≥1+(2g+1)+(2g+1)+1=4g+4.​
(14)
This counts every critical point, regardless of whether some critical values coincide.
To attain the bound, construct a surface Morse function h:Σg​→R from the standard orientable handle decomposition: start with a disk, attach 2g orientable bands in the usual paired pattern forming the genus-g surface with one boundary component, and cap that boundary with a disk. Give the first disk a minimum, each band one saddle with local form −x2+y2, and the final cap a maximum. Regular collar coordinates join successive levels without additional critical points. This constructs h with counts (1,2g,1).
On the product take
F(θ,x)=h(x)+δcosθ,δ>0.
(15)
Its critical points are exactly the pairs of a critical point of h with θ=0,π. The Hessian matrix is block diagonal, with surface block Hessh and circle entry −δcosθ. It is nonsingular and its Morse index is the sum of the two factor indices. Thus this product of Morse functions has counts
(c0​,c1​,c2​,c3​)=(1,2g+1,2g+1,1),
(16)
and exactly 4g+4 critical points. If distinct critical values are desired, choose δ outside the finite set producing collisions after first choosing distinct critical values for h. The minimal Morse critical count on a circle times an orientable surface is therefore attained for every g≥0.

An index-λ handle in dimension d is Dλ×Dd−λ. A handle attachment glues its attaching region Sλ−1×Dd−λ to a corresponding neighborhood in the boundary of the preceding manifold with boundary. Besides the embedding of the attaching sphere, one specifies a framing of an embedded sphere, namely the normal-disk directions used in this gluing. Corners are then smoothed.
In dimension four, a two-handle is D2×D2 and attaches along S1×D2. Its attaching circle is a knot in the boundary of the zero-handle D4. Identify that boundary with S3 and remove a point away from the circles to draw them in R3. Disjoint attaching circles give a framed link. Each integer records the twist of its normal framing relative to the zero Seifert framing; the integer is also the linking number of the circle and its framed push-off. The resulting boundary change is Dehn surgery on a framed link. This is the two-handle part of a Kirby diagram.
For S2×S2, use the following explicit handle decomposition. Write each factor as two disks, S2=D−2​∪D+2​, glued along their boundary circles. The four product pieces give
h0=D−2​×D−2​,h12​=D+2​×D−2​,h22​=D−2​×D+2​,h4=D+2​×D+2​.
(26)
The first is a four-dimensional zero-handle after rounding its corners. Its boundary is the union of the solid tori S1×D2 and D2×S1. The two middle product pieces attach along these tori. Their attaching circles S1×{0} and {0}×S1 form the standard Hopf link, with linking number one after choosing orientations.
Both framings are zero. For example, the normal disk of S1×{0} is the second disk factor. Its constant normal push-off has linking number zero with the original circle: the circle bounds D2×{0} in the four-ball, and a parallel push-off bounds a disjoint translated disk. Thus the framed self-intersection is zero; the same argument applies with the factors interchanged. These product framings are the zero Seifert framings.
After these two two-handles, the remaining product piece D+2​×D+2​ is a four-ball. Its boundary is exactly the boundary of the assembled first three pieces, so it attaches as a four-handle. Equivalently, zero surgery on the Hopf link gives S3, which this handle caps. We have therefore constructed the actual product, proving
S2×S2=one zero-handle+two zero-framed Hopf-linked two-handles+one four-handle.​
(27)
There are no one- or three-handles. As a check, the two factor spheres have self-intersection zero and meet once, giving the intersection form (01​10​). The explicit product construction, rather than the intersection form alone, identifies the smooth four-manifold. This is the zero-framed Hopf-link handle decomposition of the sphere product.
