In bond percolation on Zd, edges are independently open with probability p and closed otherwise. An open percolation cluster is a connected component using only open edges. In site percolation it is the graph vertices that are independently declared open. The percolation probability θ(p)=Pp​(0↔∞) measures the chance that the origin lies in an infinite percolation cluster, and the percolation critical probability is pc​=inf{p:θ(p)>0}. Monotone coupling of Bernoulli percolation by independent uniform distribution makes θ nondecreasing.
The sharpness of the percolation transition says that below pc​ connection probabilities on Zd decay exponentially with distance, while above pc​ the percolation probability is positive. There is almost surely a unique infinite percolation cluster in the supercritical regime. On the square lattice, the Harris-Kesten theorem gives bond threshold 1/2, and there is no infinite percolation cluster at percolation critical probability. The emergence of connections on arbitrarily large scales is the geometric meaning of the phase transition.
Several observables describe this singular behavior. The percolation susceptibility is χ(p)=Ep​∣C(0)∣ for p<pc​. A directional correlation length can be defined through exponential two-point decay, ξ(p)−1=−limn​n−1logPp​(0↔ne1​). Percolation critical exponents describe leading powers, conveniently defined logarithmically rather than requiring exact asymptotic amplitudes:
θ(p)=(p−pc​)β+o(1),χ(p)=(pc​−p)−γ+o(1),ξ(p)=(pc​−p)−ν+o(1).
(33)
The first limit of a sequence approaches from above, the other two from below. At percolation critical probability, percolation two-point connection probability is expected to have leading power ∣x∣−(d−2+η), and the number per site of percolation clusters of size s has leading power s−τ. This cluster-count probability distribution differs from the size-biased probability distribution seen by choosing a site. Scaling relations for critical exponents such as γ=(2−η)ν and 2β+γ=dν connect the exponents in their applicable scaling regime; hyperscaling relation is not valid above the upper critical dimension of percolation in ordinary mean-field regimes.
The percolation universality hypothesis predicts that dimension and large-scale symmetry, rather than microscopic graph details, determine these exponents and scaling functions. Thus square-lattice bond and triangular-lattice site models are expected to share the planar graph universality class despite different microscopic definitions. Universality does not mean identical critical probabilities, amplitudes or finite-lattice distributions. Exact values and existence of exponents require model-specific theorems, not merely this prediction.
Two dimensions have an additional structure: conformal maps preserve angles, and critical crossing probabilities are expected to be unchanged under conformal transport of the domain and its marked boundary arcs. A crossing probability then depends only on the conformal shape of that marked quadrilateral. The Cardy boundary crossing formula gives this dependence in terms of its boundary cross ratio. For critical triangular-lattice site percolation, conformal invariance and the resulting exponents are established; examples are β=5/36, γ=43/18, ν=4/3, with the rigorous statements in leading-power form. These values are summarized in Smirnov and Werner's original paper. Their universality extension to arbitrary planar lattices should not be presented as the same theorem.
An exploration path separating the two colors, with opposite boundary colors on two arcs, has a domain Markov property of a percolation exploration: conditional on the explored portion, the unexplored colors retain their independent laws with the newly exposed boundary colors. Conformal invariance, this domain Markov property and suitable regularity lead to Schramm–Loewner evolution. In the upper half-plane its chordal equation is
∂t​gt​(z)=gt​(z)−Ut​2​,Ut​=κ​Bt​,

where Bt​ is standard Brownian motion and time is normalized by half-plane capacity. Critical percolation corresponds to κ=6. Thus the complicated discrete interface becomes a conformally natural random curve driven by one real Brownian motion. Its crossing and arm probabilities supply critical exponents and, through scaling relations for critical exponents, near-critical singularities. This makes conformal methods predictive tools for the transition, rather than only descriptions of an interface's appearance.
