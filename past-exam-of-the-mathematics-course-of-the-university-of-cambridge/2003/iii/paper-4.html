Here is Witt's lemma with the hypotheses appropriate to the classical groups. Let V be finite-dimensional with a nonsingular alternating bilinear form, a nonsingular symmetric bilinear form in characteristic different from two, or a nonsingular Hermitian form with nontrivial involution. Every isometry of a space with a form ϕ:U→U′ between vector subspaces extends to a form-preserving automorphism of V. For quadratic forms, the same statement holds in every characteristic when the polar bilinear form of a quadratic form is nonsingular and the isometry of a space with a form preserves the quadratic form itself. The restricted forms on U,U′ may be degenerate. We prove these versions, including the characteristic-two quadratic case needed later.
For an alternating bilinear form B, split the restricted space as U=H⊕R, where R=U∩U⊥ and B∣H​ is nonsingular. Choose a symplectic basis for H and a basis r1​,…,rt​ of R. The nondegenerate form on V supplies vectors sj​ in H⊥ with B(ri​,sj​)=δij​. Adding linear combinations of the ri​ to the sj​ makes B(si​,sj​)=0: for each pair i<j one may cancel its pairing by adding an appropriate multiple of ri​ to sj​, without disturbing the dual pairings or previously treated pairs. This uses no division by two. Thus H together with the pairs (ri​,si​) is nonsingular and its orthogonal complement for a sesquilinear form has a symplectic basis.
Apply the same construction to ϕ(H),ϕ(ri​), choosing partners si′​ there. The two completed bases have identical alternating Gram matrices and equal remaining dimensions. The linear map matching these bases preserves B and agrees with ϕ on H⊕R=U. This proves the alternating version over an arbitrary field, including characteristic two.
For a quadratic form Q, define its polar bilinear form of a quadratic form by B(x,y)=Q(x+y)−Q(x)−Q(y). Its symmetry
sa​(x)=x−Q(a)B(x,a)​a(Q(a)=0)

preserves Q. The zero-dimensional case is immediate. Induct on dimU: choose Q(t)=0 and a hyperplane K⊆U∩t⊥. Extending ϕ∣K​ first reduces to ϕ∣K​=I. Put U=K⊕Fu, v=ϕ(u), d=u−v∈S=K⊥. If Q(d)=0, sd​ completes the extension. Otherwise B(u,d)=B(v,d)=0. The hyperplanes H=S∩u⊥, H′=S∩v⊥ are proper because S⊥=K.

Over F2​, choose a outside both hyperplanes. In the remaining case Q(a)=Q(d)=B(a,d)=0, and B(a,u)=B(a,v)=1. The explicit map

In odd characteristic a symmetric bilinear form is recovered by the polarization identity for Q(x)=B(x,x)/2, so the quadratic proof also proves its extension theorem. For completeness, the Hermitian form case has a similarly explicit induction. Take h linear in the first variable, with conjugation a↦aˉ. For anisotropic vectors for a form u,v with equal value A=h(u,u)=h(v,v)=0, let d=u−v and c=h(u,d). When c=0, the rank-one map
g(x)=x−ch(x,d)​d
(5)
sends u to v and preserves h, since h(d,d)=c+cˉ and the extra coefficient in h(gx,gy) is
−c1​−cˉ1​+ccˉc+cˉ​=0.
(6)
When c=0, insert λu with λλˉ=1, λ=1, and use two such maps. Such a scalar exists for a nontrivial involution: λ=a/aˉ with a=aˉ works. If the source vector subspace contains an anisotropic vector for a form, align it this way and induct in its nonsingular orthogonal complement for a sesquilinear form.
If instead every source vector is isotropic, the Hermitian polarization identity makes its whole restricted form zero. Choose e in its basis and a partner f pairing to one with e and to zero with the remaining source basis. Make h(f,f)=0 by replacing f by f−te, where t+tˉ=h(f,f). The trace map is onto the fixed field: in odd characteristic divide by two; in characteristic two scale a nonzero trace. Choose a+aˉ=0. The orthogonal vectors e+af and e−aˉf have nonzero values a+aˉ and its negative, so the preceding rank-one construction aligns this entire hyperbolic pair with its target pair. The remaining source basis lies in the pair's orthogonal complement for a sesquilinear form; induction finishes the Hermitian proof.
The hypotheses matter. For example, the identity-matrix symmetric bilinear form on F23​ has canonical vector c=(1,1,1) characterized by B(x,c)=B(x,x), so every isometry of a space with a form fixes c. The one-dimensional map e1​↦c preserves the restricted bilinear form but cannot extend. Thus “all symmetric forms in characteristic two” is not a correct replacement for the quadratic or alternating versions proved above.

The isomorphic pair is A8​≅PSL4​(2); PSL3​(4) is different. All three have order 20160, so order alone does not settle the question; explicitly ∣PSL3​(4)∣=(64−1)(64−4)(64−16)/(3⋅3)=20160. We give a concrete realization and an element-order distinction.
On W=⋀2F24​, the Pfaffian is
Q(x)=x12​x34​+x13​x24​+x14​x23​.
(7)
It is a nonsingular plus-type quadratic form: the three displayed pairs give a hyperbolic decomposition. Change of basis multiplies the Pfaffian by the determinant, which is always one in GL4​(2). Hence the exterior-square realization of PSL4 over F2 embeds GL4​(2) into O6+​(2). The kernel of a group homomorphism is trivial: an element acting trivially on the exterior square fixes every decomposable two-space, then every one-space as an intersection of two-spaces, and hence is a scalar; over F2​ this scalar is one.
For a second model let E be the even-weight vector subspace of F28​ and quotient by the all-one vector. The dot product descends to a nondegenerate alternating bilinear form on the six-dimensional quotient, and
q(xˉ)=wt(x)/2(mod2)
(8)
is well defined, since complementing an even subset changes half its weight from w/2 to 4−w/2. It has (1+70+1)/2=36 zero vectors, so is plus type. Coordinate permutations give a faithful S8​ action: fixing the quotient fixes each weight-two subset, because its complement has weight six, and fixing all two-subsets forces the identity permutation.
From the independently derived symplectic order and quadratic orbit count below,
∣O6+​(2)∣=36∣Sp6​(2)∣​=40320,∣GL4​(2)∣=(16−1)(16−2)(16−4)(16−8)=20160.
(9)
Thus O6+​(2)≅S8​ and the image of GL4​(2)=SL4​(2)=PSL4​(2) is its index-two subgroup A8​. The index-two subgroup is unique: a nontrivial group homomorphism S8​→C2​ sends every transposition to the same nonidentity element and is the sign map.
To separate PSL3​(4), observe that A8​ contains (12345)(678) of order 15. An odd-order projective element of PSL3​(4) lifts to a semisimple linear operator, since its order upstairs divides three times an odd order in characteristic two. The possible eigenspace degree patterns are 1+1+1, 2+1 and 3. In the split case all eigenvalues have order dividing three, so the projective element has no order 15. In the 2+1 case the eigenvalues are λ,λ4,λ−5 with λ∈F16×​; its fifth power is scalar because λ15=1, so its projective order divides five. In the irreducible case the determinant-one torus has order 42+4+1=21 and quotienting its scalar subgroup of order three gives projective order dividing seven. There is therefore no element of order 15 in PSL3​(4).
Several small-parameter coincidences connect the finite classical groups to permutation groups. The projective-line action immediately gives PSL2​(2)≅S3​, PSL2​(3)≅A4​, PGL2​(3)≅S4​ and PSL2​(4)≅A5​ by faithful actions of degrees three, four and five and their orders. Also PSL2​(5)≅A5​: its involutions have Klein-four centralizers, giving five Sylow subgroups; their conjugation action is faithful and embeds its order-60 simple group into S5​. Simplicity here can be checked from class sizes 1,15,20,12,12: no proper nontrivial normal class union has order dividing 60. The same five-subgroup action extends to PGL2​(5), with trivial kernel of a group homomorphism and order 120, giving S5​. Indeed its kernel intersects PSL2​(5) trivially, so centralizes that normal subgroup. Commuting projectively with both upper and lower elementary unipotents forces a representing matrix to be scalar, so this kernel is trivial.
The less immediate coincidence PSL2​(9)≅A6​ can be exhibited on the ten unordered triple partitions used in Question 4. Let a=(123), b=(456), w=(12)(36), assign ∞ to 123∣456 and 0 to 126∣345, and label the other partitions by applying axby to the zero partition, with t=x+iy∈F9​ and i2=−1. Inspection of these nine partitions gives
a:t↦t+1,b:t↦t+i,w:t↦−1/t.
(10)
Translations and inversion generate PSL2​(9): conjugating the upper unitriangular matrices by inversion gives the lower ones, and elementary elimination generates SL2​(9). Their projective action has order 9(92−1)/2=360. These partition permutations come from even permutations of six letters, whose partition action is faithful as proved below; hence they fill A6​.
Finally, on the even-weight module of F26​ modulo its all-one vector, coordinate permutations preserve a nondegenerate alternating bilinear form in dimension four and act faithfully, again by the weight-two-subset argument. Both S6​ and Sp4​(2) have order 720, proving Sp4​(2)≅S6​, with commutator subgroup A6​. These examples illustrate both the need to quotient group centers and the low-rank exceptions to general simplicity. They do not make equal orders sufficient for group isomorphism, as the two order-20160 examples already demonstrate.

Let B be a nondegenerate alternating bilinear form on V of dimension 2m over Fq​. A symplectic basis has B(ei​,fj​)=δij​ and all ei​,ej​ and fi​,fj​ pairings zero. Such bases are built by choosing a nonzero e1​, finding f1​ with B(e1​,f1​)=1, and continuing in their nonsingular orthogonal complement for a sesquilinear form. In these coordinates the group preserving B is
Sp2m​(q)={g:gTJg=J},J=(0−I​I0​).
(11)

∣Sp2m​(q)∣=(q2m−1)q2m−1∣Sp2m−2​(q)∣,∣Sp2m​(q)∣=qm2j=1∏m​(q2j−1).​
(12)
The center of a group consists of aI with a2=1, and has order gcd(2,q−1). To see there are no other central elements, a central element commutes with every symplectic transvection and hence fixes every symplectic transvection center line; a linear map fixing every line is scalar. Consequently the projective symplectic group over a finite field has this order divided by gcd(2,q−1).
Here is a proof of the main ingredients in simplicity. The symplectic transvection
Tv,c​(x)=x+cB(x,v)v
(13)
preserves B, has inverse Tv,−c​, and fixes v⊥ pointwise. These transformations generate Sp2m​(q). Indeed if B(u,w)=0, then Tw−u,1/B(u,w)​ sends u to w. If the pairing is zero, choose an intermediate vector having nonzero pairing with both, so two symplectic transvections suffice. This aligns the first vector of any symplectic basis. For partners f,f′ of the same e, their difference lies in e⊥. If B(f,f′)=0 a symplectic transvection centered at f′−f aligns them and fixes e. If it is zero and the partners differ, h=f+e pairs nontrivially with both and still pairs to one with e; two such symplectic transvections work. After fixing the pair, induction in its orthogonal complement for a sesquilinear form aligns the rest of the basis.
The projective action on one-spaces is faithful. For m≥2, a line stabilizer has three orbits: the line itself, the other lines perpendicular to it, and the lines not perpendicular to it. Witt's lemma proves transitivity within the latter two orbits by matching the two-dimensional restricted form. This action is primitive. A block containing distinct perpendicular lines L,M is invariant under both stabilizers and therefore contains a line perpendicular to M but not to L. A block containing distinct nonperpendicular lines likewise contains a line perpendicular to L but not to M, by using the M stabilizer. Such lines exist by nonsingularity in dimension at least four. Either possibility makes the block contain both nontrivial stabilizer orbits, hence every line. For m=1 the action on the projective line is two-transitive and thus primitive.
Symplectic transvections with one fixed center line form an Abelian normal subgroup of its stabilizer, and their conjugates generate the whole group. The Iwasawa simplicity lemma now reduces simplicity to being perfect. Its short proof is useful: any nontrivial normal subgroup of a faithful primitive group is transitive, since its orbits are blocks. Writing G=NH, where H is a point stabilizer, shows that all conjugates of the specified Abelian subgroup have the same image modulo N. Thus G/N is Abelian. If G is perfect, N=G.
The groups can also be shown to be perfect concretely. For q>3 choose a with a2=1 and let D scale e by a and f by a−1. Then
DTe,c​D−1Te,c−1​=Te,(a2−1)c​,
(14)
so every symplectic transvection is a group commutator. For q=3,m≥2, symplectic transvections of coefficient one are conjugate, have order three, and the four with centers e1​,e2​,e1​+e2​,e1​−e2​ in a two-dimensional totally isotropic subspace multiply to the identity. They commute, and the sum of their rank-one terms is zero modulo three. Their common class in the abelianization is therefore annihilated by both three and four, hence is zero. For q=2,m≥3, use the seven nonzero vectors of a three-dimensional totally isotropic subspace. The symplectic transvections commute and their product is the identity, because each diagonal coefficient occurs four times and each cross coefficient twice. Their common abelianized class is annihilated by two and seven, hence is zero. Generation now proves these groups are perfect.
It follows that
PSp2m​(q) is simple except for (m,q)=(1,2),(1,3),(2,2).​
(15)
The exceptions are S3​, A4​ and S6​ respectively. For odd q the full symplectic group over a finite field has nontrivial center of a group, so simplicity refers to its quotient group; for even q the center of a group is trivial.
A symplectic parabolic subgroup stabilizes a flag of totally isotropic subspaces. The maximal such parabolic subgroups Pr​ stabilize a single r-space, 1≤r≤m. In an adapted basis their Levi subgroup is GLr​(q)×Sp2m−2r​(q), with an arbitrary block of r(2m−2r) parameters and a remaining symmetric block of r(r+1)/2 parameters. Thus
Pr​=Ur​⋊(GLr​(q)×Sp2m−2r​(q)),∣Ur​∣=q2r(m−r)+r(r+1)/2.
(16)
These are the single-member flag stabilizers, equivalently the standard maximal parabolic subgroups obtained by omitting one node of the symplectic Dynkin diagram. Two particular cases have transparent descriptions.
For the symplectic point stabilizer, write V=⟨e,f⟩⊥W. Its unipotent radical consists of
u(b,t):e↦e,w↦w−B(w,b)e,f↦f+b+te,b∈W, t∈Fq​.

With group commutator convention uvu−1v−1, its commutator has central parameter 2B(b,b′): it is Heisenberg-type in odd characteristic, but Abelian in characteristic two. P1​=U1​⋊(Fq×​×Sp2m−2​(q)),[Sp2m​(q):P1​]=q−1q2m−1​.​

For the degree-ten partition action of S6, take unordered partitions of six letters into two triples. There are (36​)/2=10. Fix A∣Ac. Its stabilizer is (S3​×S3​)⋊S2​=S3​≀S2​, permuting each triple and interchanging them. The subgroup generated by a 3-cycle in each triple has order nine, so is a Sylow subgroup of S6​. Any normalizer preserves its two three-point orbits; conversely all the indicated within-triple permutations and the swap normalize it. This proves the asserted normalizer exactly.
For any different partition C∣Cc, ∣A∩C∣ is one or two after excluding the identical partition. Interchanging C and Cc identifies these two possibilities. The stabilizer of A∣Ac is transitive on each fixed intersection pattern, hence on all other nine partitions. The degree-ten action is two-transitive. It is also faithful: two distinct partitions have a common refinement with two singleton cells and two cells of size two. Every unordered pair of letters occurs as those singleton cells. A permutation fixing every partition therefore fixes every pair and consequently every letter.
For the subgroup factorization from coset transitivity, use cosets Hg (right cosets) and right multiplication by K. The orbit of H is precisely {Hk:k∈K}. This is every coset if and only if every g can be written hk, proving
G=HK⟺K is transitive on H\G.​
(21)
A natural S5​ fixing one letter is transitive on the ten partitions: the triple containing the fixed letter is determined by any two of the other five. Taking H=S3​≀S2​, K=S5​ gives S6​=HK; inversion gives the requested order as well,
S6​=S5​(S3​≀S2​).​

Now let B be the binary nondegenerate alternating bilinear form. Its binary quadratic refinements form an affine space: the difference of two such forms is linear, so for a fixed Q0​ every one is uniquely
Qv​(x)=Q0​(x)+B(v,x),v∈V.

In a symplectic basis each refinement is
Q(x,y)=∑i=1m​(xi​yi​+ai​xi​+bi​yi​),Arf(Q)=∑i=1m​ai​bi​∈F2​.

A two-dimensional summand with (ai​,bi​)=(1,1) admits a basis of singular vectors paired to one and is hyperbolic; the (1,1) summand is anisotropic. Two planes spanned by anisotropic vectors for a form together become two hyperbolic planes of a quadratic form: with anisotropic basis pairs (e1​,f1​),(e2​,f2​), the singular vectors e1​+e2​,f1​+e2​ pair to one; their orthogonal complement for a sesquilinear form has singular paired basis e1​+f1​+f2​,e1​+f1​+e2​+f2​. Consequently every refinement is equivalent to either m hyperbolic planes of a quadratic form or (m−1) hyperbolic planes of a quadratic form and one anisotropic plane (all three nonzero vectors are anisotropic vectors for a form).
These two types are different. Indeed the character sum of each plane is +2 or −2, so

The same character sum gives the corresponding numbers of singular vectors, including zero, in a fixed form of either type.
Fix a form Q of type ϵ. Completing the square by x↦x+v shows
∑x​(−1)Qv​(x)=(−1)Q(v)∑x​(−1)Q(x),ϵ(Qv​)=ϵ(Q)(−1)Q(v).

The stabilizer of Q is exactly O(V,Q). It sends the parameter v by its natural linear action or inverse action according to the chosen left/right convention. Other forms of the same type correspond to nonzero singular vectors v, and all forms of opposite type correspond to nonsingular vectors v. For two nonzero vectors of the same Q value, the map between their spans is an isometry of a space with a form. Witt's lemma extends it, so the orthogonal stabilizer is transitive on each of these two vector sets. For the Johnson graph, an edge exchanges one member of a k-set. Along one edge, intersection size with a fixed target can increase by at most one, so any path in a graph from A to B has length at least k−∣A∩B∣. Replacing the elements of A∖B successively by elements of B∖A gives a path in a graph attaining that bound. Hence the distance in a Johnson graph is
d(A,B)=k−∣A∩B∣.​
(29)
If two ordered pairs have the same distance r, their four regions (intersection, first-only, second-only and outside the union) have sizes k−r,r,r,n−k−r. Choose bijections between corresponding regions. Their union is a ground-set permutation sending the ordered pair to the other pair and preserving graph adjacency. The symmetric group is distance-transitive. For n≥2k the graph diameter is k.
For the Grassmann graph, a codimension-one exchange changes intersection dimension with a fixed target by at most one. More explicitly, if A,A′ are adjacent, their common (k−1)-space shows dim(A′∩B)≥dim(A∩B)−1, and exchanging the two gives the reverse bound. Thus distance is at least r=k−dim(A∩B).
Choose a basis c1​,…,ck−r​ of I=A∩B, complement it in A by a1​,…,ar​, and in B by b1​,…,br​. These combined vectors are independent because A∩B=I. The spaces
Aj​=I+⟨b1​,…,bj​,aj+1​,…,ar​⟩,0≤j≤r,
(30)
form a path in a graph of length r. Consequently
d(A,B)=k−dim(A∩B).​
(31)
Equal-distance ordered pairs have the same dimensions in this adapted basis construction. Extend both combined bases to bases of V, and match them by a invertible linear map. It maps the first ordered pair to the second and preserves dimensions of intersections, hence graph adjacency. GLn​(F) is distance-transitive, over an arbitrary field, with graph diameter k under n≥2k.
For the symplectic dual polar graph, vertices are Lagrangian m-spaces. Let I=A∩B have dimension m−r. The quotient I⊥/I is a nonsingular symplectic vector space of dimension 2r, and A/I,B/I are complementary Lagrangian subspaces. Their mutual pairing is nonsingular: a vector orthogonal to both lies in (A+B)⊥=I, so vanishes in the quotient. Choose dual bases e1​,…,er​ in A/I and f1​,…,fr​ in B/I, and lift them into A,B. Their span is a nonsingular symplectic 2r-space. Complete it by a basis er+1​,…,em​ of I and suitable dual partners. This gives the pair normal form
A=⟨e1​,…,em​⟩,B=⟨f1​,…,fr​,er+1​,…,em​⟩.
(32)
The spaces Aj​=⟨f1​,…,fj​,ej+1​,…,em​⟩ remain totally isotropic subspace: a present fi​ pairs only with the absent ei​. They give an adjacent-step path in a graph of length r. The previous intersection-dimension lower bound still applies, proving
d(A,B)=m−dim(A∩B).​
(33)
For two ordered pairs at the same distance, match their adapted symplectic bases. The resulting map preserves the alternating bilinear form and sends the pairs to each other, so lies in Sp2m​(F). Equivalently, construct the isometry of a space with a form on A+B from the displayed normal form and extend it by Witt's lemma. The action of the symplectic group over a field is distance-transitive, over every field, and its graph has graph diameter m. The zero-dimensional boundary cases give a single vertex and trivial transitivity.
