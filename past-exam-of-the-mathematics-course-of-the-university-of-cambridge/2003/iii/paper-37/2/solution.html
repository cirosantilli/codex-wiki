A linear polyhedron P⊆Rn is full-dimensional if its affine hull is Rn. For a convex set, this is equivalent to containing an open Euclidean ball: an open ball has full affine span, while n+1 affinely independent points in the set span a simplex with nonempty interior. In particular, a nonempty linear polyhedron need not be full-dimensional; a singleton or a hyperplane is an example.
Assume U≥1, increasing the coefficient bound to one if necessary. If x0​∈P and Ai​ denotes the ith row, then ∥Ai​∥2​≤n​U. For r=ε/(nU) and ∥h∥2​≤r, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
Ai​(x0​+h)≥bi​−∥Ai​∥2​∥h∥2​≥bi​−ε.
(2)
Thus B(x0​,r)⊆Pε​ with r>0, proving that Pε​ is a full-dimensional linear polyhedron. This part works for every positive ε; the particular small value also ensures that relaxation cannot create feasibility when P is empty.
Here is that exact-feasibility point. Put D=[(n+1)U]n+1. If P is empty, a Farkas certificate for linear inequalities gives λ≥0 with ATλ=0 and bTλ>0. Normalize 1Tλ=1, and maximize bTλ over the compact linear polyhedron
Q={λ≥0:ATλ=0, 1Tλ=1}.
(3)
Choose a maximizing extreme point. Its positive support has size k≤n+1: a dependence among its supported columns (AiT​,1) would permit small perturbations of both signs in Q, contradicting extremality. Select k independent equations for those k coordinates. Cramer's rule expresses them over a common nonzero integer determinant Δ, with ∣Δ∣≤k!Uk≤D. Since b is integer and bTλ>0, its numerator is a positive integer after choosing a positive denominator. Hence bTλ≥1/D. This is the normalized integer Farkas infeasibility gap. A point in Pε​ would imply
0=λTAx≥bTλ−ε≥D1​−2(n+1)D1​>0,
(4)
a contradiction. Consequently P=∅ if and only if Pε​=∅.
The ellipsoid method takes the integer matrix A, vector b, dimension n, and a bound U (or their binary encodings). It computes the prescribed ε, an inner radius r, and an outer radius R whose logarithms have polynomial size. For an explicit outer bound, let M=(nU)n. A nonempty P has a rational feasibility certificate for integer inequalities with ∣xj​∣≤M. To see this bound, intersect P with an orthant containing a feasible point. This intersection is a nonempty linear polyhedron containing no whole nontrivial affine line, and it has an extreme point. Indeed, if its active rows do not yet span the ambient space, move along a nonzero direction annihilating them. The orthant prevents motion in both directions from being feasible forever, so in at least one direction a new independent row becomes active. Repeating at most n times produces an extreme point. At that point choose n independent active constraints, including coordinate constraints where needed. Cramer's rule and the Hadamard determinant inequality bound the numerators by nn/2Un≤M, while a nonzero integer denominator has absolute value at least one. Thus the point has norm at most nM. Choosing R=2nM+2 ensures that, whenever P is nonempty, an entire radius-r Euclidean ball in Pε​ lies inside B(0,R).
Start with the ellipsoid E0​=B(0,R) and retain K=Pε​∩B(0,R). At each iteration test the centre c against every relaxed inequality. If it passes all tests, Pε​ is nonempty and hence so is P. Otherwise a violated row gives the separation oracle: the feasible set lies in {x:Ai​(x−c)≥0}. Cut the current ellipsoid by this half-space through its centre and replace it by the standard containing ellipsoid. Every step retains K and, for n≥2, reduces Lebesgue measure by a factor at most exp[−1/(2(n+1))]. In dimension one the corresponding step bisects an interval.
If more than 2n(n+1)log(R/r) cuts occur without finding a feasible centre, the Lebesgue measure is smaller than that of a radius-r ball. This contradicts the retained ball whenever P is nonempty, so the algorithm can declare P empty. Both logR and log(1/r) are polynomial in n and logU, and each inequality test uses O(mn) arithmetic operations for m rows. The bit implementation uses rational rounding with outward enlargement of the containing ellipsoids; the encoding bounds allow polynomial precision while keeping a constant fraction of the volume decrease. Detailed update and rounding formulae are not needed for this account. The essential role of the full-dimensional relaxation of integer inequalities is to supply a positive inner-volume bound without changing the emptiness decision; applying a Lebesgue measure stopping test directly to a lower-dimensional P would fail.
