A subdivision curve interrogation interface provides its control/topology data, a parameter interval or address, and a refinement operation returning restricted child pieces with their parameter correspondence. It also provides a certified enclosing bounding volume, endpoints or limit-point enclosures, and an error estimate for replacing a sufficiently small piece by its chord. Positional and derivative limit queries may be implemented by refinement or by conversion to the appropriate spline. Nonnegative masks often imply a control-point convex hull bound; a generic scheme with negative weights needs a separate bound.
These enquiries can be applied to a parametric curve by using interval restriction as the subdivision operation and constructing its enclosure from its representation. For example, a Bézier curve is split by De Casteljau's algorithm, and each child's control-point convex hull encloses exactly that child arc. For a convex hull bound, the extrema of this affine plane function are attained among its control points, so only dot products are required. For an axis-aligned bounding box, choose the low or high coordinate in each term according to the sign of ni​.

when the denominator is nonzero. It supplies an initial estimate, which is corrected on the actual curve. A chord of a piece with geometric error ε gives plane-function error at most ∥n∥ε; a transverse root additionally needs a positive derivative lower bound to convert that into a parameter error. A chord hit is not by itself a proof of the exact crossing count. Keep unresolved tangent contacts, merge shared-endpoint hits, and return any certified coplanar parameter intervals separately.
Good speed comes from a bounding volume hierarchy, conservative cheap rejection before accurate evaluation, caching shared subdivision work, local rather than uniform refinement, and fast root polishing only after isolation. Convex-hull or box extrema cost far less than solving every potential intersection. Tight bounds shrink rapidly with subdivision and reduce the active search to neighborhoods of actual crossings. Derivative information can prove monotonicity, accelerate with the Newton root-finding iteration, or tighten range bounds using interval arithmetic. There is no universal fixed speed guarantee when the curve is nearly coplanar or has arbitrarily many intersections: those are genuinely difficult inputs. The representation must provide meaningful bounds and restriction operations; point/derivative enquiries alone do not imply such capabilities.
