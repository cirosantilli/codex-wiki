Take a finite set of source-sink pairs S, with fixed demands bs​≥0, and finitely many permitted routes Rs​ for each pair. Every positive demand must have at least one available route. Let νr​ be route flow and ρj​ link throughput. The source-sink route incidence matrix and link-route incidence matrix are
Hsr​=1{r∈Rs​}​,Ajr​=1{j∈r}​,
(24)
for simple routes; if a route traverses a link repeatedly, its entry counts those traversals. Thus Hν=b conserves each demand and Aν=ρ aggregates link traffic.
At a Wardrop equilibrium, every route carrying positive flow has minimum delay among routes for the same source-sink pair. If
cr​(ρ)=∑j​Ajr​Dj​(ρj​),
(25)
there are numbers τs​ such that cr​(ρ)≥τs​ for r∈Rs​, with equality whenever νr​>0. This is a nonatomic routing game: one infinitesimal user's route change does not alter the link costs it sees.
For continuous nondecreasing link delays, define the Beckmann potential
V(ρ)=∑j​∫0ρj​​Dj​(z)dz.
(26)
Each term is convex because its derivative is nondecreasing. The feasible route-flow set F={ν≥0:Hν=b} is nonempty and compact: 0≤νr​≤bs​ on each pair's routes. Hence the continuous function V(Aν) attains a minimum. Its gradient with respect to route flow is
∂νr​∂V(Aν)​=∑j​Ajr​Dj​(ρj​)=cr​(ρ).
(27)
If r carries positive flow and r′ serves the same pair, moving a small amount of flow from r to r′ is feasible. At a minimizer its directional derivative cr′​−cr​ is nonnegative. Thus every used route minimizes delay, proving existence of a Wardrop equilibrium.
Conversely, at such an equilibrium, any feasible competitor ν satisfies
∇V(Aν)⋅A(ν−ν)=∑s​∑r∈Rs​​cr​(ρ)(νr​−νr​)≥0.
(28)
Indeed, the competitor's cost sum for pair s is at least τs​bs​, while the current flow's cost sum equals τs​bs​. This first-order variational inequality is sufficient for optimality of a differentiable convex function. Therefore
Wardrop equilibria are exactly the minimizers of V(ρ) with Hν=b, Aν=ρ, ν,ρ≥0.​
(29)
The argument proves both directions of the claimed optimization equivalence, including unused routes with costs tied at the minimum.
If each Dj​ is strictly increasing, each integral is strictly convex, hence so is V as a function of link throughputs. The feasible link-throughput set AF is convex. Two distinct minimizing vectors would have a feasible midpoint with strictly smaller potential, so the equilibrium link-throughput vector is unique. Strictly convex function in ρ need not give strictly convex function in ν, because A can have a nontrivial kernel.
For an explicit route-flow ambiguity, put two parallel links in each of two consecutive stages, with one unit of demand traversing both stages. Order the four routes by the first and second link choices: (1,1),(1,2),(2,1),(2,2). Set all four link delays to Dj​(z)=z. Then
ν=(t,1/2−t,1/2−t,t),0≤t≤1/2,
(30)
induces throughput 1/2 on each link, and every route has delay one. Every member of this family is an equilibrium, though its route-flow vector changes. This is route-flow nonuniqueness at a Wardrop equilibrium with strictly increasing delays.
For Braess paradox, use vertices s,u,v,t and one unit of s-to-t demand. The delays are Dsu​(z)=z, Dut​(z)=1, Dsv​(z)=1, and Dvt​(z)=z. Initially the only routes are sut and svt. If the upper route carries p, their costs are 1+p and 2−p, so equilibrium has p=1/2 and common delay 3/2.
Add the directed link u→v with delay zero. Let the upper, lower and new middle routes carry p,q,c, where p+q+c=1. Their costs become
csut​=2−q,csvt​=2−p,csuvt​=2−p−q.
(31)
If p>0, the middle route is strictly cheaper than the upper route, contradicting equilibrium; likewise q>0 is impossible. Thus all flow uses the middle route, with p=q=0,c=1, and all three route costs are two. Therefore
adding a zero-delay link increases the equilibrium delay from 3/2 to 2.​
(32)
The potential for the expanded network is
V=21​ρsu2​+ρut​+ρsv​+21​ρvt2​.
(33)
The old flow remains feasible and has potential 5/4; the new equilibrium has potential 1. The enlarged feasible set has indeed lowered the minimized potential. But total travel delay is ∑j​ρj​Dj​(ρj​), not this integral potential: it rises from 3/2 to 2. This explains the paradox within the optimization framework. The optimal total-delay value cannot worsen when the old flow is still allowed; selfish equilibrium minimizes a different function.
The phenomenon also survives strictly increasing delays on every link. Replace each constant-one delay by 1+εz and the new-link delay by εz. Symmetry gives upper and lower flows p=ε/(1+3ε) after expansion, with middle flow (1+ε)/(1+3ε). The common new delay is 2−ε(1−ε)/(1+3ε), while the old delay was 3/2+ε/2. For ε=1/10, these are 251/130>31/20. Thus the example does not depend on interpreting increasing delays as merely nondecreasing.
