Write the Hamiltonian operator as H=p​2/(2m)+V(q​), with [q​,p​]=i, and define the quantum-mechanical propagator by U(β,α;T)=⟨β∣e−iTH∣α⟩. Use N intervals of length ε=T/N, endpoint coordinates q0​=α,qN​=β, and the Trotter product formula. Inserting the completeness relations of the position eigenstates between the short-time factors gives
U=limN→∞​∫∏j=1N−1​dqj​∏j=1N​⟨qj​∣e−iεp​2/(2m)e−iεV(q​)∣qj−1​⟩.
(1)
Normalize momentum eigenstates by ⟨q∣p⟩=eipq/2π​. The Gaussian integral over the intermediate momentum is
∫2πdp​exp(ip(qj​−qj−1​)−2miεp2​)=(2πiεm​)1/2exp(2εim(qj​−qj−1​)2​).
(2)
The square-root branch is fixed by continuation from Imε<0. Consequently the precise time-sliced configuration-space path integral is
U=N→∞lim​(2πiεm​)N/2∫RN−1​j=1∏N−1​dqj​exp[ij=1∑N​{2εm(qj​−qj−1​)2​−εV(qj−1​)}].​
(3)
This formula defines the notation ∫DqeiS[q]; it is not an unnormalized infinite product of ordinary integrals. For real time take the boundary value from T−iδ, δ>0, or the corresponding oscillatory-distribution limit. For a self-adjoint, bounded-below Hamiltonian operator under the usual product-formula hypotheses, the operator limit supplies the definition even where the coordinate kernel is a distribution. The discrete exponent approaches the action with kinetic term mq˙​2/2, and the prefactor is part of the functional measure. A different consistent slicing gives the same kernel for this separated kinetic-plus-potential Hamiltonian.
For the quantum harmonic oscillator, expand the action about a classical path x, writing q=x+ξ with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on ξ. The only mixed term is
m∫0T​(x˙ξ˙​−ω2xξ)dt=m[x˙ξ]0T​−m∫0T​(x¨+ω2x)ξdt=0.
(4)
The boundary term vanishes and the Euler-Lagrange equation sets the remaining integrand to zero. Thus S[x+ξ]=S[x]+S[ξ] exactly, rather than only to quadratic approximation. Translation of each integrated coordinate has unit Jacobian determinant; in the continuum measure convention of the question this is Dq=Dξ. The classical-path factorization of a quadratic path integral follows:
U(β,α;T)=eiS[x]F(T),F(T)=∫ξ(0)=ξ(T)=0​DξeiS[ξ].​
(5)
The fluctuation action, measure and boundary values depend on T,m,ω but not on α,β. For real T with sinωT=0 the boundary-value classical path exists uniquely. At the conjugate times ωT=kπ, generic endpoints admit no such path and the kernel is a delta distribution; the formula is interpreted by the same regulated boundary limit, not as an ordinary finite prefactor times a nonexistent classical solution.

Use a real scalar field and Minkowski action S[ϕ], with the vacuum selected by a Feynman propagator pole prescription or adiabatic vacuum boundary conditions. The normalized correlation functions are
Gn​(x1​,…,xn​)=∫DϕeiS[ϕ]∫Dϕϕ(x1​)⋯ϕ(xn​)eiS[ϕ]​=⟨Ω∣T{ϕ​H​(x1​)⋯ϕ​H​(xn​)}∣Ω⟩.
(6)
Here ∣Ω⟩ is the interacting vacuum and T denotes time ordering. These are the canonical vacuum expectation values, rather than unordered products of noncommuting fields. The functional integral formula assumes a regulated measure and the same vacuum prescription on both sides.
Couple an external source J linearly to the scalar field and choose the normalized vacuum generating functional
Z[J]=∫DϕeiS[ϕ]∫Dϕexp(iS[ϕ]+i∫ddxJϕ)​,Z[0]=1.
(7)
Then functional derivatives insert the fields:
Gn​=i−nδJ(x1​)⋯δJ(xn​)δnZ​​J=0​,Z[J]=∑n=0∞​n!in​∫Gn​(x1​,…,xn​)∏j=1n​J(xj​)ddxj​.

In the convention used below, the connected generating functional is
Z[J]=eiW[J],W[J]=−ilogZ[J],Gnc​=i1−nδJ(x1​)⋯δJ(xn​)δnW​​J=0​.​
(9)
The logarithm removes products of disconnected components. For example the connected correlation function G2c​=G2​−G1​G1​; at three points one subtracts all three G2​G1​ products and adds 2G1​G1​G1​. Normalization has already removed vacuum bubbles.

Keep Z=eiW, define the mean scalar field φ(x)=δW/δJ(x), and locally invert the source-field relation. The quantum effective action is the Legendre transform
Γ[φ]=W[J]−∫ddxJ(x)φ(x),δφ(x)δΓ​=−J(x).​
(10)
Its value at zero source is expanded around φ0​=⟨ϕ⟩, which need not vanish. The kernels Γ(n)=δnΓ/δφn∣φ0​​ are the one-particle-irreducible vertices, with the Minkowski diagram vertex factor iΓ(n). A one-particle-irreducible Feynman diagram remains connected after any one internal line is cut. Amputation of external propagators removes the full two-point functions on its external legs. For higher-point connected graphs amputation alone need not remove internal one-particle-reducible Feynman diagrams; the derivatives of Γ select precisely the irreducible kernels.
To derive the three-point relation, use condensed indices that include spacetime integration. Put Dij​=W,ij​=iG2,ijc​ and K=Γ(2). Differentiating Γ,i​=−Ji​ gives Kia​Daj​=−δij​, so K=−D−1. A further derivative, using δDab​/δφk​=W,abℓ​(D−1)ℓk​ and the derivative of an inverse, gives
Γijk(3)​=(D−1)ia​(D−1)jb​(D−1)kc​W,abc​.
(11)
Since W(3)=−G3c​ and D−1=−i(G2c​)−1, the three-point amputation in the connected-action convention is
iΓijk(3)​=∫dadbdc(G2c​)ia−1​(G2c​)jb−1​(G2c​)kc−1​G3,abcc​.​
(12)
Equivalently, attach a full two-point correlation function to each leg of iΓ(3) to reconstruct G3c​. The connected functions are evaluated at the same zero-source background. The displayed factors of i follow from the declared Minkowski convention; Euclidean generating functionals redistribute these factors.

For a bosonic quadratic action S2​=21​∫ϕKϕ, completing the square in the generating functional gives Z0​[J]/Z0​[0]=exp(−iJK−1J/2). Two functional derivatives therefore give the Feynman propagator Δ=iK−1, with boundary conditions and a pole prescription included in the inverse. Zero modes must be removed or fixed before an inverse exists.
Take Minkowski metric η=diag(1,−1,−1,−1), Fourier convention e−ikx, and the Yang-Mills field strength Fμνa​=∂μ​Aνa​−∂ν​Aμa​+gfabcAμb​Aνc​. The interaction terms do not contribute to the quadratic action. Integration by parts in −Fμνa​Faμν/4−(∂⋅Aa)2/(2α) yields
S2​=21​∫ddxAμa​[ημν□−(1−α−1)∂μ∂ν]Aνa​.
(13)
The color kernel is diagonal. For k2=0, use the projectors PLμν​=kμkν/k2 and PTμν​=ημν−PLμν​. They obey PL2​=PL​, PT2​=PT​, PL​PT​=0, so
Kabμν(k)=−δabk2(PTμν​+α−1PLμν​),(K−1)μνab​=−k2δab​(PTμν​+αPLμν​).
(14)
Multiplication explicitly gives the identity on both transverse and longitudinal components. Continuing this inverse with the Feynman propagator prescription gives the gluon propagator
Δμνab​(x)=δab∫(2π)dddk​e−ikx[k2+i0−iημν​​+(k2+i0)2i(1−α)kμ​kν​​].​
(15)
The second term has the causal double-pole prescription. Away from the pole this is the usual −iδab[ημν​−(1−α)kμ​kν​/k2]/(k2+i0). For α=1 it becomes the Feynman-gauge adjoint propagator; α→0 gives a transverse covariant Landau gauge propagator.
The limit α→∞ removes gauge fixing and has no finite inverse on the full field space. The longitudinal eigenvalue −k2/α tends to zero and its inverse diverges. A contraction with conserved external currents kills the longitudinal term, but that does not define a gauge-unfixed Gaussian integral. This massless limit is not the massive-vector unitary-gauge limit: here it exposes the unfixed gauge orbit degeneracy.

Take M2>0 initially to avoid an infrared divergence. A Schwinger parameterization and the Euclidean Gaussian integral give, in the convergent range 0<Red<4,
(k2+M2)21​=∫0∞​dtte−t(k2+M2),∫(2π)dddk​e−tk2=(4πt)−d/2.
(16)
Integration over t supplies the Gamma function. Its analytic continuation defines the answer in dimensional regularization:
Id​(M2)=(4π)d/2Γ(2−d/2)​(M2)d/2−2.​
(17)
Use d=4−ϵ near four dimensions and d=6−ϵ near six dimensions. The Gamma function recurrence implies Γ(ϵ/2)=2/ϵ+O(1) and Γ(−1+ϵ/2)=−2/ϵ+O(1). Therefore
I4−ϵ​(M2)=(4π)2ϵ2​+O(1),I6−ϵ​(M2)=−(4π)3ϵ2M2​+O(1).​
(18)
Both are simple poles. Equivalently their residues as functions of d are −2/(4π)2 at d=4 and 2M2/(4π)3 at d=6. The negative pole near six dimensions is a feature of analytic continuation; it is not a negative value of the original convergent positive integral. A massless zero-momentum integral is scaleless and cannot be used indiscriminately to extract these ultraviolet poles, because dimensional regularization then combines ultraviolet and infrared contributions.
For the following Feynman diagrams, specify the Euclidean action and additive counterterms explicitly so their signs are unambiguous. The four-dimensional interaction is μϵλϕ4/4!, the six-dimensional interaction is μϵ/2gϕ3/3!, and the common free inverse scalar propagator is p2+m2. Here ϵ=4−d or 6−d, respectively. The factors of μ give dimensionless renormalized couplings and change no leading pole residues. The cubic Euclidean theory is treated as a formal perturbation expansion about its massive quadratic scalar field theory; its real cubic potential is not bounded below.

Let p be the total external Euclidean momentum crossing one four-point channel. The two internal scalar propagators produce the scalar bubble integral
Bd​(p)=∫(2π)dddk​(k2+m2)((k+p)2+m2)1​.
(19)
Using the Feynman parameter identity 1/(ab)=∫01​dx[xa+(1−x)b]−2 and shifting the loop momentum yields
Bd​(p)=∫01​dxId​(m2+x(1−x)p2).​
(20)
In phi-fourth theory the bubble diagram has two quartic vertices and a symmetry factor 1/2 from exchanging its two identical internal lines. There are three partitions of four labelled external legs into two pairs, the s,t,u channels. The connected amputated loop diagram has positive coefficient λ2B/2, whereas the Euclidean one-particle-irreducible vertex in the effective action has the opposite sign. Thus
ΓE(4)​(p1​,p2​,p3​,p4​)=μϵ(λ+δλ)−2μ2ϵλ2​[Bd​(p1​+p2​)+Bd​(p1​+p3​)+Bd​(p1​+p4​)]+O(λ3).
(21)
One can check this sign without external-line conventions by expanding the one-loop quantum effective action 21​Trlog(K+μϵλϕ2/2). Its second logarithmic term is −μ2ϵλ2Tr(K−1ϕ2K−1ϕ2)/16; four field derivatives give precisely the three negative bubble terms above.
The four-dimensional pole of Id​ is independent of its mass argument, so each channel contributes 2/[(4π)2ϵ]. The divergence of the proper four-point vertex is −3λ2/[(4π)2ϵ]. The minimal subtraction scheme therefore requires
δλ=(4π)2ϵ3λ2​,LE,ct(4)​=4!μϵδλ​ϕ4.​
(22)
For a Minkowski interaction −μϵλϕ4/4!, the corresponding local interaction counterterm is −μϵδλϕ4/4!. No momentum-dependent four-point counterterm is needed for these logarithmic poles. This calculation concerns the requested four-point bubble contributions; a massive two-point tadpole and vacuum diagrams require their own mass and vacuum-energy subtractions if those functions are also renormalized.

In phi cubed theory, a two-point bubble diagram has two cubic vertices, two internal scalar propagators and symmetry factor 1/2. Its connected amputated insertion is +μϵg2Bd​(p)/2. The inverse scalar propagator, or quadratic one-particle-irreducible vertex, therefore receives the negative insertion:
ΓE(2)​(p)=p2+m2+δZp2+δm2−2μϵg2​∫01​dxI6−ϵ​(m2+x(1−x)p2)+O(g4).
(23)
Equivalently, the connected two-point correction is G0​(p)2μϵg2Bd​(p)/2, and expansion of its inverse changes the sign. The same sign follows from the second term of 21​Trlog(K+μϵ/2gϕ).
Using the six-dimensional residue and ∫01​x(1−x)dx=1/6, the divergent inverse-propagator correction is
ΓE,div(2)​(p)=(4π)3ϵg2​(m2+6p2​).
(24)
Its momentum term requires wave-function renormalization, unlike the massive quartic tadpole. The minimal-subtraction two-point counterterms in cubic scalar theory are
δZ=−6(4π)3ϵg2​,δm2=−(4π)3ϵg2m2​,LE,ct(2)​=21​δZ(∂ϕ)2+21​δm2ϕ2.​
(25)
These are additive coefficients in a Lagrangian expressed in terms of the renormalized field. If ϕ0​=Z1/2ϕ, Z=1+δZ, and the bare mass is separately written m02​=m2+Δm2, then δm2=m2δZ+Δm2 at this order, so Δm2=−5g2m2/[6(4π)3ϵ]. Distinguishing these conventions prevents a spurious disagreement between mass counterterms. The local Minkowski counterterms are δZ(∂ϕ)2/2−δm2ϕ2/2. A complete cubic theory also has one-point and three-point subtractions; they are not the specified two-point bubble poles.

A Berezin integral over independent Grassmann variables selects the coefficient containing every variable. Fix the real orientation by ∫dω1​⋯dω2m​ω2m​⋯ω1​=1. For an antisymmetric matrix A, only the order-m term in the exponential survives:
2mm!(−1)m​(∑i,j​ωi​Aij​ωj​)m.
(26)
Anticommutation collects its top coefficient into the signed sum over pairings that defines the Pfaffian. Reversing the order of 2m variables gives (−1)m(2m−1)=(−1)m, cancelling the prefactor sign. The real Grassmann Gaussian integral is therefore
∫dω1​⋯dω2m​e−ωTAω/2=Pf(A)​
(27)
with this orientation, and a fixed sign times this answer with other orientations. In dimension two, write A12​=a; the exponential is 1−aω1​ω2​=1+aω2​ω1​, and integration gives a. Although Pf(A)2=detA, writing a positive square root detA​ would give ∣a∣ in this test and would lose the correct sign.
For the complex Grassmann Gaussian integral, treat θa​ and θˉa​ as independent integration variables. Every surviving top term contains each θ and each θˉ once. Expanding the exponential assigns one matrix entry from every row and column, and anticommutation supplies the permutation sign. Hence
∫a=1∏n​dθa​a=1∏n​dθˉa​e−θˉTMθ=Cn​detM,​
(28)
where Cn​ is a nonzero constant fixed by the order and normalization of the differentials, independent of M. This argument works for singular matrices as well and does not assume that M is Hermitian.
For the explicit n=2 check, put Q=θˉa​Mab​θb​. Moving all the θ variables before the barred ones gives
21​Q2=(−M11​M22​+M12​M21​)θ1​θ2​θˉ1​θˉ2​.
(29)
The linear and constant terms integrate to zero. Thus the integral is a fixed orientation sign times M11​M22​−M12​M21​, exactly the determinant. Choosing the top-monomial integral to be −1 gives C2​=1.
For the required real-complex compatibility of Grassmann Gaussian integrals, let ua​=ωa​ and va​=ωn+a​. Substitution gives
θˉTMθ=21​(uTMu+vTMv+iuTMv−ivTMu)=21​(uTMu+vTMv).
(30)
Indeed vTMu=−uTMTv=uTMv because the variables anticommute and MT=−M. The real quadratic matrix is consequently A=diag(M,M), of size 2n. The linear change of Grassmann variables has a constant nonzero Berezin integration Jacobian, so it changes only the allowed normalization factor. For even n, the two blocks give
Pf(A)=Pf(M)2=detM.​
(31)
For odd n, detM=det(−M)=(−1)ndetM implies detM=0. Each of the separate u and v quadratic exponentials has only even degree and cannot saturate an odd number of variables, so the real integral vanishes too. This covers the odd case without attempting to define a Pfaffian of an odd-dimensional matrix.

Let a gauge transformation act infinitesimally as δAμa​=(Dμ​ω)a, and define the Faddeev-Popov operator by variation of the chosen gauge functional:
Mab[A](x,y)=δωb(y)δFa[Aω](x)​​ω=0​.
(32)
Work in a perturbative regular gauge slice, with boundary conditions that remove residual zero modes and with one local representative of each gauge orbit. The Faddeev-Popov gauge-orbit identity is 1=ΔFP​[A]∫Dωδ[F[Aω]]. Inserting it and factoring out the formal gauge-group volume gives
Z=N∫DAδ[F[A]]detM[A]eiS[A].​
(33)
The functional delta imposes the gauge condition at every point and for every color. The determinant compensates the gauge-orbit Jacobian. For ordinary unoriented real gauge-orbit integration the local Jacobian is ∣detM∣; perturbatively its sign is fixed and absorbed into N, leaving the displayed determinant. Multiple global intersections, or a Gribov ambiguity, require more care; the local perturbative formula is not a global uniqueness assertion.
The complex Grassmann Gaussian integral now generalizes to spacetime and color indices. Introduce independent anticommuting Faddeev-Popov ghost fields ca(x),cˉa(x) and write
detM[A]=N′∫DcˉDcexp(i∫ddxddycˉa(x)Mab[A](x,y)cb(y)).
(34)
The factors i in the functional determinant differ from a real exponential only by a regulated, field-independent constant. These Grassmann fields are Lorentz scalars in the adjoint color representation; the antighost is independent of the ghost rather than an ordinary complex-conjugate commuting variable. They are unphysical fields, not external observable particles. Representing the functional determinant by a local ghost action when F is local makes ordinary Feynman rules available. Anticommutation gives the minus sign of a closed ghost loop, enabling cancellation of unphysical gauge contributions and maintaining the gauge identities in perturbation theory.
For example, the linear covariant choice Fa=∂μAμa​ gives Mab=∂μDμab​, so non-abelian ghost-gauge interactions remain. In an axial gauge Fa=nμAμa​ with constant n, however,
Mab=δabn⋅∂+gfacbn⋅Ac⟶δabn⋅∂
(35)
on the exact gauge slice. Its determinant is field-independent, so axial-gauge ghosts decouple and contribute only a normalization factor. This statement uses the strict delta-functional constraint; a finite-width Gaussian gauge weight is not the same exact slice.
The disadvantage is that choosing n obscures manifest Lorentz covariance and the axial-gauge propagator contains spurious 1/(n⋅k) poles. An axial-gauge pole prescription and control of residual transformations satisfying n⋅∂ω=0 are necessary; formal decoupling alone does not fix those problems.
Abelian ghosts do not always decouple. In a field-independent linear Lorenz gauge, δAμ​=∂μ​ω gives M=□ and they are free. But the quadratic Abelian gauge fixing F[A]=∂⋅A+κAμ​Aμ gives
δF=(□+2κAμ∂μ​)ω.
(36)
Its Faddeev-Popov operator depends on A, and the ghost action contains 2κcˉAμ∂μ​c. This is an explicit abelian ghost-gauge interaction. Abelianity removes the commutator term in the gauge transformation, not the field dependence of an arbitrary nonlinear gauge condition.
