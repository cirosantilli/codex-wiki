Use the physical dynamics of the Black-Scholes model, dSt​/St​=μdt+σdWt​, with bank rate ρ and σ>0. Write λ=(μ−ρ)/σ for the market price of risk. If wt​ is portfolio wealth, πt​ the dollar amount in stock, and ct​≥0 the consumption rate, the budget dynamics are
dwt​=[ρwt​+πt​(μ−ρ)−ct​]dt+σπt​dWt​.
(25)
Admissible controls are progressively measurable, locally integrable in the required drift and diffusion norms, and keep wt​≥0. Such a solvency restriction excludes doubling-type strategies. In a complete Brownian market, the investment-consumption problem is to maximize
E[∫0T​e−δtu(ct​)dt+e−δTU(wT​)],
(26)
where the utility functions are increasing and concave. A terminal-only problem omits the integral. Strict concavity gives uniqueness of optimal consumption and terminal wealth when an optimum exists.
The state-price density is
Ht​=e−ρtexp(−λWt​−λ2t/2).
(27)
The Itô formula gives
d(Ht​wt​)+Ht​ct​dt=Ht​(σπt​−λwt​)dWt​.
(28)
Thus Ht​wt​+∫0t​Hs​cs​ds is a nonnegative local martingale, hence a supermartingale, yielding the state-price budget constraint
E[HT​wT​+∫0T​Ht​ct​dt]≤w0​.
(29)
Conversely, a nonnegative consumption plan and terminal payoff ξ satisfying budget equality and the necessary integrability can be financed in the Brownian complete market. Define
wt​=Ht−1​E[HT​ξ+∫tT​Hs​cs​ds​Ft​].
(30)
The Brownian martingale representation theorem for the total future budget supplies a stochastic-integral coefficient; comparing it with Ht​(σπt​−λwt​) defines πt​. This reconstructs the wealth equation with wt​≥0. Localization gives the representation for integrable positive budgets when an L2 assumption is not available.
For smooth strictly concave utilities with the usual marginal-utility range, let Iu​=(u′)−1 and IU​=(U′)−1 be the inverse marginal utility functions. The Lagrange-multiplier solution is
ct∗​=Iu​(yeδtHt​),ξ∗=IU​(yeδTHT​),​
(31)
where y>0 makes the budget an equality. This is a global argument, not merely formal differentiation: the concave supporting-tangent inequality gives
e−δt[u(ct​)−u(ct∗​)]≤yHt​(ct​−ct∗​),
(32)
with the analogous terminal inequality. Taking expectations, integrating, and applying the budget bound makes the difference in objectives nonpositive. Replication of the candidate budget proves attainability and optimality whenever these expectations are well-defined and finite. If the marginal-utility ranges or integrability conditions fail, existence must be established separately rather than inferred from the first-order equations.
A detailed example is constant relative risk aversion utility u(c)=c1−γ/(1−γ) and U(w)=ηw1−γ/(1−γ), with γ>0, γ=1, η>0, and δ≥0. Put
k=γδ+(γ−1)(ρ+λ2/(2γ))​,b(τ)=∫0τ​e−ksds+η1/γe−kτ.
(33)
The optimal plans from the marginal-utility equations are ct∗​=(yeδtHt​)−1/γ and ξ∗=η1/γ(yeδTHT​)−1/γ. The normal distribution of Wt​ gives, for a=1−1/γ,
EHta​=exp[−aρt+21​a(a−1)λ2t].
(34)
The budget therefore becomes y−1/γb(T)=w0​, determining the multiplier explicitly. Independent Brownian increments in the conditional budget formula give wt∗​=ct∗​b(T−t). The diffusion coefficient of ct∗​ is (λ/γ)ct∗​, so matching the wealth diffusion determines the dollar stock holding. Consequently the finite-horizon power-utility investment and consumption optimum is
ct∗​=b(T−t)wt∗​​,πt∗​=γσ2μ−ρ​wt∗​,wT∗​=η1/γcT∗​.​
(35)
All candidate moments are finite on a finite horizon, since powers of a lognormal state-price density have finite moments. The budget and tangent argument above therefore verify optimality in this example, including admissibility and positive wealth.
For another verification of the formulas, the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation for a value function with discount measured from the current time is
0=Vt​−δV+supc≥0,π​{1−γc1−γ​+(ρw+π(μ−ρ)−c)Vw​+21​σ2π2Vww​},V(w,T)=1−γηw1−γ​.
(36)
Substitute V(w,t)=b(T−t)γw1−γ/(1−γ). The two concave maximizations give the displayed controls, and the remaining equation is b′(τ)=1−kb(τ) with b(0)=η1/γ, exactly solved by the formula above. Thus
V(w,t)=1−γb(T−t)γw1−γ​.​
(37)
For k=0, interpret the integral as τ, so b(τ)=τ+η1/γ. Unlike the infinite-horizon Merton consumption-investment problem, finite horizons do not require k>0. Consumption vanishes in the terminal-only formulation; then the integral term in b is absent and the optimal stock fraction remains (μ−ρ)/(γσ2). Logarithmic utility is handled directly with inverse marginal utility 1/z and the same wealth-to-consumption formula at γ=1, with k=δ.
