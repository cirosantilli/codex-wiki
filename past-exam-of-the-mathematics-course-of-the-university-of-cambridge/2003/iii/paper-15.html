We work over an algebraically closed field k, so the assertion concerns a geometric projective line. Let V=k4 and let W be the vector space of homogeneous cubic forms on V. It has dimension (36​)=20, so the parameter space of cubic equations up to nonzero scalar is P(W)=P19. The Grassmannian G=Gr(2,V) parametrizes lines in P3. Its standard graph charts are four-dimensional affine spaces, so its dimension is four. The Plücker embedding realizes it as a closed quadric in P5, proving projectivity. The general linear group GL4​ acts transitively on two-planes and is an irreducible open subset of the affine space of matrices; its orbit map proves that G is irreducible.
Consider the incidence variety
I={(ℓ,[F])∈G×P(W):F∣ℓ​=0}.
(1)
Restriction of a cubic to a fixed line is surjective onto the four-dimensional space of binary cubic forms: after choosing coordinates with that line given by x2​=x3​=0, extend any binary cubic using x0​,x1​ alone. Thus its kernel has dimension sixteen. On each Grassmannian chart these restriction maps depend algebraically on the line parameters and have constant rank four. Their kernels form a rank-sixteen vector bundle, and I is its projective bundle, with fibre P15. In particular, I is irreducible of dimension 4+15=19. It is closed in the product, since restriction vanishes precisely when four polynomial coefficients vanish.
The projection π:I→P(W) is a projective morphism, hence its image is closed. It remains to prove that this image has dimension nineteen. Equal dimensions alone would not suffice: we must control a fibre locally.
Take
F0​=x02​x2​+x12​x3​,ℓ0​={x2​=x3​=0}.
(2)
In the open Grassmannian chart around ℓ0​, every line is uniquely a graph
x2​=a0​x0​+a1​x1​,x3​=b0​x0​+b1​x1​.
(3)
Restriction of F0​ to that line is
a0​x03​+a1​x02​x1​+b0​x0​x12​+b1​x13​.
(4)
The four monomials are linearly independent over every field. Hence the only line in this chart lying on F0​ has a0​=a1​=b0​=b1​=0. The fibre π−1([F0​]) therefore has local dimension zero at (ℓ0​,[F0​]). We do not need F0​ to define a smooth surface, nor do we claim its entire fibre is finite.
The fiber dimension theorem, in its local dimension inequality, gives
0=dim(ℓ0​,[F0​])​π−1([F0​])≥dimI−dimπ(I).
(5)
Thus the closed image has dimension at least nineteen. A proper closed subset of the irreducible P19 has smaller dimension, so π(I)=P19. Every cubic equation consequently has a line in its zero set. In particular, every nonsingular cubic surface contains a line. The proof in fact establishes existence for arbitrary cubic surfaces over an algebraically closed field; it does not assert a rational line over an arbitrary nonclosed ground field.

Use the graded ring homomorphism
φ:k[x0​,x1​,x2​,x3​]⟶k[s,t],xi​⟼s3−iti,
(6)
where source degree m maps to target degree 3m. The homogeneous ideal of the twisted cubic is kerφ: a homogeneous polynomial vanishes on the parametrized curve precisely when its substitution vanishes identically. Here k is infinite, being an algebraically closed field, so vanishing at all parameter points implies the polynomial identity.
Let J be generated by
q1​=x0​x3​−x1​x2​,q2​=x0​x2​−x12​,q3​=x1​x3​−x22​.
(7)
Their substitutions vanish, hence J⊆kerφ. To prove the reverse inclusion as an equality of ideals, we need more than a set-theoretic description of the zero locus.
Modulo J, replace x12​ by x0​x2​, x22​ by x1​x3​, and x1​x2​ by x0​x3​. Each replacement strictly decreases the sum of the exponents of x1​ and x2​, so the reduction terminates. It shows that the degree-m quotient is spanned by the standard monomials of the twisted cubic
x0a​x3b​(a+b=m),x0a​x1​x3b​(a+b=m−1),x0a​x2​x3b​(a+b=m−1).
(8)
For m≥1 there are (m+1)+m+m=3m+1 of these. For m=0 the sole monomial is 1. Their substituted images have t-exponents respectively 3b, 3b+1 and 3b+2, so they are exactly the distinct monomials
s3m−jtj,0≤j≤3m.
(9)
These are linearly independent. Consequently any degree-m polynomial in kerφ, after reduction modulo J, has all its reduced coefficients zero. It belongs to J. Applying this to each homogeneous component gives
I(X)=kerφ=(x0​x3​−x1​x2​, x0​x2​−x12​, x1​x3​−x22​).​
(10)
This also proves that the quotient embeds in the domain k[s,t], so the displayed ideal is prime; no radicalization or saturation correction is concealed in the argument. Its image is the third Veronese subring ⨁m≥0​k[s,t]3m​, and the calculation gives the Hilbert polynomial 3m+1, consistent with degree three and genus zero.

The usual field norm NL/K​ is defined for a finite field extension. The printed hypothesis “algebraic” alone allows an infinite extension, for which this determinant norm is not defined. We therefore assume [L:K]<∞. For an arbitrary algebraic extension the meaningful elementwise statement is instead NK(b)/K​(b)∈A, and we prove this as well.
First a unique factorization domain is an integrally closed domain. Indeed, write an integral fraction as u/v with relatively prime numerator and denominator. A monic integral equation, after multiplication by vr, shows that v divides ur. Unique factorization forces v to be a unit.
Let mb​(T)=Tr+ar−1​Tr−1+⋯+a0​∈K[T] be the monic minimal polynomial of b. Because b is an integral element over A, it satisfies a monic polynomial f∈A[T], and mb​ divides f in K[T]. Every root of mb​ in an algebraic closure is therefore integral over A. This assertion includes repeated roots when the extension is inseparable. The coefficients of mb​ are elementary symmetric expressions in these roots. The integral elements form a ring: finitely many integral elements generate a finite A-module algebra, and the determinant trick shows every element of that algebra is integral. Thus all ai​ are integral over A. Since ai​∈K and A is integrally closed, ai​∈A.
Multiplication by b on K(b), in the basis 1,b,…,br−1, has the companion matrix of mb​, whose determinant is (−1)ra0​. If e=[L:K(b)], take a K(b)-basis of L. Multiplication by b preserves each summand in the resulting direct sum of e copies of K(b), so its determinant over K is
NL/K​(b)=((−1)ra0​)e∈A.​
(11)
In particular NK(b)/K​(b)=(−1)ra0​∈A. The argument requires no separability; in fact it holds for every integrally closed domain, not just a unique factorization domain.
For the connection with dimension, let B be the coordinate ring of an irreducible affine variety. By Noether normalization there is a finite integral inclusion A=k[t1​,…,td​]⊂B, with d=dimB. Their fraction fields form a finite extension. For any 0=b∈B, its minimal-polynomial constant term a0​ is nonzero and lies in A. Its equation gives
a0​=−b(br−1+ar−1​br−2+⋯+a1​)∈bB∩A.
(12)
Thus a nonzero function on the normalized variety produces a nonzero function on the base that vanishes wherever b vanishes. Up to sign, the field norm is a power of this a0​, so it too belongs to bB∩A.
Under the finite morphism to Ad, the image of VB​(b) is therefore contained in the proper Zariski-closed set VA​(a0​) of dimension at most d−1. Finite morphisms preserve the dimension of a closed subvariety and its image. Applying this to a nonzero element of the ideal of any proper closed subvariety proves proper closed subvarieties have strictly smaller dimension. This is the useful role of the norm argument: nonzero equations upstairs give nonzero equations downstairs under a finite normalization.

Let X be a separated Noetherian scheme over a field k and F a coherent sheaf. Coherent cohomology studies the groups Hq(X,F): global sections in degree zero and the obstructions to gluing local sections in higher degrees. Coherence means finite generation locally, and on a Noetherian scheme it is preserved by kernels, cokernels and extensions. This makes coherent sheaves suitable for exact-sequence calculations.
For an ordered open cover U=(Ui​), the Čech cochain groups are
Cˇq(U,F)=∏i0​<⋯<iq​​F(Ui0​​∩⋯∩Uiq​​),
(13)
with differential
(δc)i0​…iq+1​​=∑j=0q+1​(−1)jci0​…ij​​…iq+1​​​Ui0​​∩⋯∩Uiq+1​​​.
(14)
Each double omission occurs twice with opposite signs, so δ2=0. The Čech cohomology of the cover is Hˇq=kerδ/imδ, and taking the direct limit over refinements defines cover-independent Čech cohomology. In degree zero the sheaf gluing axiom identifies it with Γ(X,F).
The comparison with derived-functor sheaf cohomology is particularly effective algebraically. Positive-degree cohomology of a quasi-coherent sheaf on an affine scheme vanishes. A separated scheme has affine intersections of affine opens, so a finite affine cover is acyclic for F. Its Čech cochain complex consequently computes Hq(X,F) directly. For a short exact sequence of sheaves, sections over each affine intersection are exact on quasi-coherent sheaves. The resulting short exact sequence of Čech complexes gives the long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology. In particular, the connecting map measures the failure of a global section of the quotient to lift globally.
Several central projective results make the theory finite and computable. On a projective variety over k, every coherent cohomology group is finite-dimensional. The Grothendieck vanishing theorem gives Hq(X,F)=0 for q>dimX. If OX​(1) is ample, Serre vanishing gives Hq>0(X,F(m))=0 for sufficiently large m, and these large twists are globally generated sheaves. On projective space a coherent sheaf has a finite twisting resolution of a coherent sheaf on projective space: finite sums of O(a) resolve it. The explicit line-bundle cohomology below and the long exact sequences prove finiteness and positive-twist vanishing. For a closed embedding i:X↪Pn, i∗​F is coherent and Hq(X,F)=Hq(Pn,i∗​F), reducing these results to projective space. A sufficiently positive twist of a finite presentation by sums of O(−a) also proves global generation.
The Euler characteristic of a coherent sheaf
χ(X,F)=∑q​(−1)qdimk​Hq(X,F)
(15)
is therefore defined, and the long exact sequence makes it additive. With a fixed projective embedding, χ(X,F(m)) is its Hilbert polynomial, and for sufficiently large m it equals h0(X,F(m)). On a smooth projective variety of dimension r, Serre duality gives, for a vector bundle E,
Hq(X,E)∗≅Hr−q(X,E∗⊗ωX​).
(16)
For an arbitrary coherent sheaf the correct counterpart is Hq(X,F)∗≅ExtXr−q​(F,ωX​); replacing this Ext group indiscriminately by the cohomology of the ordinary sheaf dual would lose local extension information.
We now calculate the basic examples. For n≥1, use the standard cover Ui​=D+​(xi​) of Pn. Sections of O(m) on an intersection are degree-m Laurent monomials in which negative exponents are allowed only for coordinates inverted on that intersection. Decompose the Čech cochain complex by exponent vectors (a0​,…,an​) with sum m. If no exponent is negative, the corresponding simplex complex contributes only in degree zero. If all exponents are negative, the monomial occurs only on the full intersection and contributes only in degree n. When the negative-index set is nonempty and proper, inserting any index outside it gives a contracting homotopy, so the monomial complex is acyclic. Hence the cohomology of twisting sheaves on projective space is
Hq(Pn,O(m))≅⎩⎨⎧​k[x0​,…,xn​]m​,k(n−m−1​),0,​q=0, m≥0,q=n, m≤−n−1,otherwise.​
(17)
In the top group a basis consists of the Laurent monomials with every exponent at most −1. Writing ai​=−1−bi​ reduces the count to nonnegative solutions of ∑bi​=−m−n−1, giving the displayed binomial coefficient. In particular, all intermediate cohomology vanishes, and ωPn​=O(−n−1) agrees with Serre duality. For P0 separately, every twist is trivial and H0=k.
On the projective line this gives
h0(O(m))=max(m+1,0),h1(O(m))=max(−m−1,0),χ(O(m))=m+1.
(18)
Thus positive and negative degrees produce quite different groups, while their alternating difference is a single polynomial. The twisted cubic is isomorphic to P1 and its hyperplane bundle corresponds to OP1​(3). Consequently h0(X,OX​(m))=3m+1 for m≥0, agreeing with Q2, and h1(X,OX​(m))=max(−3m−1,0).
For a smooth plane curve C of degree d≥1, the ideal-sheaf sequence of a projective hypersurface is
0⟶OP2​(−d)⟶OP2​⟶i∗​OC​⟶0.
(19)
The long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology and the preceding formula give H0(C,OC​)=k and H1(C,OC​)≅H2(P2,O(−d)). Therefore
g(C)=2(d−1)(d−2)​.​
(20)
For d=1,2 this is zero; for a plane cubic it is one. Twisting the same exact sequence gives χ(C,OC​(m))=dm+1−g. These examples illustrate how Čech cohomology, vanishing, Serre duality and exact sequences convert local equations into global numerical invariants.

Use algebraic Chern classes in the Chow ring of a nonsingular algebraic variety Y. For a vector bundle E of rank r, the classes ci​(E)∈CHi(Y) are invariant under bundle isomorphism, have c0​(E)=1, vanish for i>r, and commute with pullback. Their Total Chern class is c(E)=∑i​ci​(E). The Whitney sum formula for Chern classes applies to every short exact sequence 0→E′→E→E′′→0:
c(E)=c(E′)c(E′′).
(21)
Normalization for a line bundle is c(OY​(D))=1+[D], where [D] is the divisor class. In particular, c1​(L⊗M)=c1​(L)+c1​(M), c1​(L−1)=−c1​(L), and the trivial bundle has total class one. These properties determine the classes: pull back to the iterated projective bundle where E has a filtration by line bundles. The projective bundle formula for Chow groups makes this pullback injective, and Whitney multiplication expresses ci​(E) as the elementary symmetric polynomial of the first classes of the line factors. This also gives ci​(E∗)=(−1)ici​(E).
Let X be the smooth degree-d projective hypersurface and set h=c1​(OX​(1)). The algebraic cotangent bundle has rank n−1. Restriction of the cotangent Euler sequence in homogeneous coordinates remains exact, because its terms and quotient are locally free. Thus
0⟶ΩPn1​∣X​⟶OX​(−1)⊕(n+1)⟶OX​⟶0
(22)
and Whitney multiplication gives
c(ΩPn1​∣X​)=(1−h)n+1.
(23)
The defining equation has degree d, so the conormal sheaf is OX​(−d). Smoothness makes its differential map injective with locally free quotient, giving the Conormal exact sequence for Kähler differentials
0⟶OX​(−d)⟶ΩPn1​∣X​⟶ΩX1​⟶0.
(24)
A second application of the Whitney sum formula for Chern classes yields
c(ΩX1​)=1−dh(1−h)n+1​.​
(25)
The inverse is the finite geometric series in the graded Chow ring: terms in codimension greater than n−1 vanish. Expanding gives every requested class,
ci​(ΩX1​)=(j=0∑i​(−1)j(jn+1​)di−j)hi(0≤i≤n−1),ci​=0 (i>n−1).​
(26)
For example,
c1​(ΩX1​)=(d−n−1)h,c2​(ΩX1​)=(d2−(n+1)d+(2n+1​))h2
(27)
when the corresponding ranks and codimensions exist. The first expression agrees with the canonical bundle of a smooth projective hypersurface detΩX1​=OX​(d−n−1). For a plane curve, degc1​=d(d−3)=2g−2, matching Q4. For a quartic surface in P3, c1​=0 and c2​=6h2, whose degree is 24. These checks also confirm the sign in the cotangent, rather than tangent, formula.
