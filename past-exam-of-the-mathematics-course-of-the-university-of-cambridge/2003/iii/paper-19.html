A well-quasi-ordering is a preorder (Q,≤) such that every infinite sequence (xn​) has indices i<j with xi​≤xj​. Reflexivity and transitivity are part of the definition; antisymmetry is unnecessary. An infinite sequence without such a pair is a bad sequence.
The perfect subsequence lemma strengthens this definition: every infinite sequence in a well-quasi-order has an infinite nondecreasing subsequence, meaning
i0​<i1​<⋯,xir​​≤xis​​(r<s).​
(1)
Here is a proof that does not assume the lemma implicitly. There must be an index i with infinitely many later indices j satisfying xi​≤xj​. Otherwise each index would have only finitely many such successors. Starting with any index, choose the next one beyond the union of those finite successor sets for the previously chosen indices. This constructs a bad sequence, contradicting the well-quasi-ordering property.
Choose i0​ with infinitely many successors above it, and restrict to those successors. Apply the same argument to that infinite subsequence, obtaining i1​>i0​ with infinitely many later successors above it within the restricted sequence. Continue. Every subsequent restriction lies inside all previous upper cones, so xir​​≤xis​​ for every r<s. This proves the lemma, including for a genuine quasi-order with distinct equivalent elements. Both alternatives below use it.

For finite rooted trees, use rooted-tree homeomorphic embedding: an injective vertex map preserves ancestors and lowest common ancestors, so edges may become paths and distinct branches remain distinct. The root can map to a vertex below the host root. More generally label vertices in a well-quasi-ordering Q and require each source label to precede its image label. We prove this stronger labelled version of Kruskal's tree theorem, from which the unlabelled result follows by taking one label.
We first need the finite-word closure, and prove it rather than merely invoking Higman lemma. If finite words over Q were not a well-quasi-ordering under order-preserving subsequence embedding with increased labels, choose a minimal bad sequence w0​,w1​,…, minimizing the length of wn​ among words admitting a bad continuation of the already chosen prefix. No wn​ is empty. Write wn​=vn​an​ by removing its last letter. The perfect subsequence lemma gives n0​<n1​<⋯ with anr​​≤ans​​ for r<s.
The sequence w0​,…,wn0​−1​,vn0​​,vn1​​,… is still bad. An earlier wi​ embedding into a later vnr​​ would embed into wnr​​, contradicting the original sequence. An embedding of vnr​​ into vns​​ extends by the last-letter comparison to an embedding of wnr​​ into wns​​. Both possibilities are excluded. This contradicts minimality at position n0​, since vn0​​ is shorter. Thus the finite-word closure holds. The perfect subsequence lemma also proves finite product closure: first pass to a nondecreasing subsequence in one coordinate, then in the next, preserving all previous comparisons.
Suppose now that the labelled trees admit a bad sequence T0​,T1​,…. Choose a minimal bad sequence by vertex number. Let U be all the proper descendant-rooted subtrees of these trees. We claim that U is a well-quasi-ordering. If it had a bad sequence of subtrees Ur​, its parent indices can be made strictly increasing, say Ur​ comes from Tnr​​. Indeed each finite initial collection of parent trees has only finitely many subtrees, and a bad sequence cannot contain infinitely many terms from a finite collection. Passing to a subsequence therefore makes n0​<n1​<⋯.
Then T0​,…,Tn0​−1​,U0​,U1​,… is bad. A prefix tree embedding into Ur​ would embed into its parent Tnr​​, contrary to the original badness, and the tail is bad by construction. But U0​ has fewer vertices than Tn0​​, contradicting minimality. This proves the claim.
Give the children of each root any fixed order. Its forest of child subtrees is a finite word in U. The word theorem and finite product closure show that the pairs consisting of the root label and the child-subtree word are a well-quasi-ordering. Thus some i<j have comparable root labels and an embedding of the child word of Ti​ into that of Tj​. Map the source root to the target root and use the obtained embeddings inside distinct target child subtrees. The paths from the target root into those distinct branches are disjoint except at the root, so this is a label-monotone tree embedding. It contradicts badness. Hence
finite rooted trees labelled in a WQO are a WQO under homeomorphic embedding.​
(2)
For Friedman's finite form of Kruskal's theorem, fix k∈N. There is N(k) such that any N(k) trees with ∣Ti​∣≤k+i contain i<j with Ti​⪯Tj​. Use indices starting at zero; shifting them only changes k. To prove this uniform finite bound, form the finite bad-sequence tree of finite bad prefixes obeying the size bounds. At position i there are only finitely many rooted-tree isomorphism types with at most k+i vertices, so every node has finitely many successors.
If no bound existed, this tree would have nodes of arbitrarily large depth. A node with arbitrarily long extensions has a child with arbitrarily long extensions, since it has only finitely many children. Choosing such a child repeatedly produces an infinite branch, as in the König infinity lemma. The branch is an infinite bad tree sequence, contradicting the theorem just proved. Therefore the required N(k) exists. The same proof works with any fixed finite label alphabet and any specified size bound finite at each position.

The PDF's displayed inequality uses the relation it purports to define and therefore is not itself a complete definition. The absorption order consistent with that inequality and with the requested result is
x⪯y⟺x+y=x.​
(3)
This is the reverse of the usual natural order of an incline. We specify it explicitly: using instead x+y=y would make the finite-generation conclusion false in general.
Idempotence gives x+x=x, proving reflexivity. If x+y=x and y+z=y, then associativity gives
x+z=(x+y)+z=x+(y+z)=x+y=x,
(4)
so the relation is transitive. If also y+x=y, commutativity forces x=y. Thus it is even a partial order. In this order x+y⪯x follows because (x+y)+x=x+y. Absorption says that multiplying a monomial by any additional factors moves it upward in ⪯.
Let g1​,…,gr​ generate the incline. Distributivity, commutativity and idempotence expand every element into a finite nonempty sum of monomials ga=g1a1​​⋯grar​​ with a∈Nr∖{0}. A zero exponent means that factor is omitted; no multiplicative identity is assumed. If a≤b componentwise, then ga+gb=ga by absorption, or by idempotence when a=b.
We prove the needed reverse-inclusion well-quasi-ordering of monomial ideals directly. Upward-closed subsets of Nr form a well-quasi-ordering under reverse inclusion. For r=1 these are the tails, plus the empty set, ordered like N∪{∞}. For the induction, write an upset U⊆Nr+1 as increasing slices U0​⊆U1​⊆⋯ in Nr. Their union is an upset with a finite minimal basis by Dickson lemma. To see that finite-basis fact here, Nr is a well-quasi-ordering by the finite product argument above, so its minimal elements cannot form an infinite antichain; every element lies above a minimal one because its lower coordinate box is finite. All basis elements of the union appear by some finite slice, so the slices eventually stabilize.
Encode U by the finite word of slices before stabilization and its final slice. By the finite-word theorem proved in the other alternative, and finite product closure, these encodings are a well-quasi-ordering with slice comparisons given by reverse inclusion. For two comparable encodings, let the earlier pre-stabilization slice Uk​ match the later slice Vf(k)​, where f(k)≥k. Then
Uk​⊇Vf(k)​⊇Vk​.
(5)
Beyond the earlier stabilization index, its final slice contains the later final slice and hence every later slice. Therefore U⊇V. This proves the induction, including empty slices and the empty upset.
For an incline element x, choose a finite exponent support Fx​ of a sum representing it and put ↑Fx​={b:∃a∈Fx​ (a≤b)}. Given an infinite sequence of elements, the just-proved result supplies i<j with ↑Fxi​​⊇↑Fxj​​. Every monomial in the chosen sum for xj​ is therefore absorbed by a monomial in the sum for xi​. Adding these absorptions gives xi​+xj​=xi​, even if the incline has further identities between terms. Hence
every finitely generated incline is well-quasi-ordered by ⪯.​
(6)
The order direction matters. On the positive integers take x+y=min(x,y) and multiplication to be ordinary addition of integers. This is an incline generated by 1. Its usual natural order x+y=y is the reverse of the ordinary numerical order, and the sequence 1,2,3,… is bad in that order. Its reverse absorption order is the ordinary numerical order, consistent with the theorem above.

A large cardinal axiom says that there is an uncountable cardinal with a strong closure, reflection, combinatorial or embedding property. Its interest is not merely the magnitude of the cardinal: it packages a universe-like portion of set theory, or a coherent way of comparing the universe with a proper inner model. Such axioms form a hierarchy extending ordinary ZFC, compared through relative consistency implications. The consistency qualifications are essential; an existence axiom is not a theorem of ZFC simply because its consequences are attractive.
The basic example is a strongly inaccessible cardinal: an uncountable regular cardinal κ which is a strong limit cardinal, so
cf(κ)=κ,2λ<κ(λ<κ).
(7)
These conditions imply ∣Vα​∣<κ for every α<κ. Prove this by induction along the cumulative hierarchy: the successor step uses the strong-limit property, and a limit step uses regularity to bound a union of fewer than κ smaller sets. Thus every member of Vκ​ has cardinality below κ.
Consequently Vκ​ is a transitive model of ZFC. Pairing, union, power set, separation, axiom of infinity and foundation stay within the hierarchy. For replacement, a definable function on x∈Vκ​ has fewer than κ values; regularity bounds their ranks below κ, putting the range in Vκ​. Ambient choice supplies a choice function for a family in Vκ​, and its graph also has bounded rank. This verifies the substantial closure axiom rather than only saying that the rank is large. It also shows why inaccessible existence cannot be proved in a consistent ZFC: it would yield a set first-order model of ZFC and hence its consistency, contrary to the Gödel second incompleteness theorem.
An uncountable regular limit cardinal is a weakly inaccessible cardinal; requiring the strong-limit inequality is stronger without additional hypotheses. Under GCH, every smaller infinite cardinal satisfies 2λ=λ+<κ, so the two notions coincide. This distinction separates cardinal-arithmetic assumptions from the regularity requirement.
A Mahlo cardinal is an inaccessible κ whose inaccessible cardinals below it form a stationary set. Thus every club set meets those inaccessibles, which strengthens mere unboundedness. There are inaccessible λ<δ<κ; Vδ​ is a first-order model of ZFC containing an inaccessible λ. Mahloness therefore supplies a consistency statement stronger than the bare existence of one inaccessible. Iterating the demand that smaller cardinals have the same reflection property leads to further levels of the hierarchy.
A weakly compact cardinal is a logical and combinatorial strengthening of inaccessibility. One standard characterization is an inaccessible κ with
κ⟶(κ)22​:
(8)
every two-colouring of pairs has a homogeneous subset of size κ. Equivalently one can use compactness for Lκ,κ​ theories of size at most κ, or the tree property at an inaccessible. These viewpoints explain the terminology: a local collection of compatible requirements has a global realization, and tall narrow trees cannot avoid cofinal branches. They are characterization statements; the elementary closure and measure arguments below are separate proofs of the consequences used here.
A measurable cardinal carries a nonprincipal kappa-complete ultrafilter U on κ. Every set of size less than κ is U-null, since it is a union of fewer than κ null singletons. Such a cardinal is regular: a cofinal sequence of length μ<κ would partition κ into μ bounded, hence null, pieces; completeness would make their union null, an impossibility.
It is also strong limit. If λ<κ and there were distinct sets Aα​⊆λ for α<κ, choose for each ξ<λ the U-large side of the partition according to whether ξ∈Aα​. The intersection of these fewer than κ large sides belongs to U, but all its indices designate the same subset of λ, so it has at most one member. This contradicts nonprincipality. Hence 2λ<κ, proving that every measurable cardinal is inaccessible.
The measure gives an ultrapower embedding. Form equivalence classes of functions f:κ→V under equality on a U-large set, with membership defined coordinatewise modulo U. Induction on first-order formulas proves elementarity: Boolean operations use the ultrafilter laws, and the existential step chooses coordinate witnesses on a large set. Countable completeness makes the ultrapower well-founded. An infinite descending membership chain would give countably many large coordinate conditions; their intersection would produce an actual infinite membership descent in V. Collapse the ultrapower to a transitive class M, obtaining j:V→M.
For α<κ, every function into α is constant on a large set by completeness; induction shows j(α)=α. The class of the identity function is larger than every such constant ordinal and lies below j(κ), so j(κ)>κ. Thus the critical point of an elementary embedding is κ. The measure viewpoint has become a precise self-similarity principle for the universe.
A supercompact cardinal imposes arbitrarily strong closure on these embeddings: for every λ≥κ there is j:V→M with critical point κ, j(κ)>λ, and Mλ⊆M. It implies measurability by the derived ultrafilter
U={X⊆κ:κ∈j(X)}.
(9)
Elementarity makes this an ultrafilter; fixed ordinals below κ show that singletons are null. For μ<κ, j fixes the indexing length, so it carries an intersection of μ sets to the intersection of their images. If all these images contain κ, so does their intersection, proving κ-completeness.
These examples illustrate three connected uses of large cardinals: obtaining set first-order models and stronger consistency statements, extending finite or countable compactness and partition phenomena to uncountable domains, and constructing elementary embeddings with controlled closure. They also interact with inner models: an inaccessible remains inaccessible in the constructible universe, since it cannot acquire a new short cofinal sequence there and there are fewer subsets of smaller ordinals. A measurable cardinal's measure need not belong to that inner model, so preservation of the stronger property is a different issue. Large cardinal axioms are thus structured additional hypotheses, with carefully distinguishable consequences and relative-consistency claims, rather than a single claim that sufficiently big cardinals exist.

All independence assertions here are relative consistency results. Begin with a first-order model of ZF; for the choice-preserving side use ZFC. The original first-order model already gives the side where foundation holds. We construct the opposite side while checking the other axioms, then add a symmetry restriction to make choice fail.
Let a=ω+1, and let π interchange the old objects a and {a}, fixing everything else. They are distinct. On the same domain define the Rieger-Bernays permutation model by
xEy⟺x∈π(y).​
(10)
Write S(b)=π−1(b); the E-members of S(b) are exactly the old members of b. In particular aEa, and a has no other E-member. Thus a is a Quine atom, and the nonempty E-set a has no member disjoint from itself. Foundation fails.
Here are the other axioms explicitly. Equal E-extensions imply π(y)=π(z) by old extensionality, hence y=z. An E-pair of x,z is S({x,z}). An E-union of y is
S(⋃{π(x):x∈π(y)}).
(11)
Its elements are precisely objects with an E-membership chain of length two into y. The E-power set of y is
S({S(b):b⊆π(y)}),
(12)
since x⊆E​y means π(x)⊆π(y). These are old sets by power set and replacement.
Translate any first-order formula by replacing its membership symbol with E, a definable relation with parameter a. Separation on y produces the representative of the old set {x∈π(y):φE(x)}. For replacement, functionality in the new structure is exactly functionality of the translated first-order formula on the old set π(y); old replacement gives its range, and S represents that range as an E-set. Thus the full schemas, not merely their quantifier-free instances, survive. The old finite ordinals and ω are fixed by π, so their empty-set and successor relations are unchanged; the old ω witnesses axiom of infinity.
If the original first-order model satisfies AC, the new one does too. For an E-family y of nonempty E-sets, old choice selects c(x)∈π(x) for each x∈π(y). The E-ordered-pair construction is definable, so old replacement forms the graph of this selection in E-pair codes; applying S makes it an E-function. It chooses an E-member from each member of the family. Therefore a full membership permutation alone does not refute choice. We have proved
Con(ZF)⇒Con(ZF−Foundation+¬Foundation),​
(13)
with choice preserved if it was initially present. Together with the unchanged well-founded first-order model, this proves foundation's independence from the remaining axioms.
For the choice-failing extension, work in an ambient first-order model of ZFC and build the extensional cumulative universe over Quine atoms. Let A be a countably infinite collection of distinct tagged objects. Give each q∈A the membership extension {q}. Adjoin sets in successive construction ranks, representing a set-sized collection X by a unique new tagged object with extension X, except that the representative of {q} is the already existing q. Denote this representative by S(X) again. Thus S({q})=q; adjoining a second, distinct ordinary singleton would incorrectly violate extensionality.
More formally take atom tags of kind zero, put D0​=A, and define
Dα+1​=Dα​∪{(1,X):X⊆Dα​, X={q} for every q∈A},Dλ​=⋃α<λ​Dα​.
(14)
The extension of (1,X) is X, and the extension of an atom q is {q}. Tags of the two kinds are disjoint. Any set-sized collection of objects has bounded construction ranks and hence a representative at a later stage. This proves pairing, union, power set, separation and replacement by representing their extensions, just as above. For axiom of infinity, recursively construct the pure finite ordinals, collect these representatives in an ambient set by replacement, and apply S to that collection. The resulting inductive set is fixed by every atom permutation. Every object has a unique extension, so full extensionality holds, although foundation fails at the atoms.
Let G be the full symmetric group of A. Extend g∈G by g(S(X))=S(g′′X), recursively on the non-atomic construction ranks. This preserves membership, including the atomic self-loops. An object is supported by a finite F⊆A if every permutation fixing F pointwise fixes it. Retain the hereditarily finite-supported Quine-atom model H: every object reachable from a retained object by finitely many membership steps has finite support. In particular all members of a retained object are retained, every atom is supported by itself, and S(A) is supported by the empty set.
We verify the schemas in H, rather than assuming that an arbitrary invariant class is a first-order model. For separation, a subset of a retained set x definable in H from parameters p1​,…,pm​ is fixed by permutations fixing the union of their finite supports. Its members are already in H, so its representative belongs to H. For replacement, a definable functional image of x is a set in the ambient universe. Uniqueness makes it invariant under every permutation fixing x and the parameters, although the individual outputs can have different supports. Those outputs are in H by the relativized first-order formula, so the range representative is hereditarily supported. This proves replacement without falsely requiring one finite support for every individual value.
The internal power set is the representative of
{S(Y):Y⊆ext(x), S(Y)∈H}.
(15)
It is an ambient set and is fixed by the support of x, because a permutation carries retained subsets of x to retained subsets of x. Every member is retained by its definition. This proves the internal power-set axiom. Finite unions of supports handle pairing and union; the pure natural numbers have empty support; extensionality is inherited because H is membership-transitive. Hence H satisfies all ZF axioms except foundation, which still fails.
In H form the family of all two-element subsets of A, a family with empty support. Suppose it had a choice function f∈H, with finite support F. Choose distinct q,r∈A∖F. Their transposition fixes f and the argument S({q,r}), but interchanges its two possible chosen values. Equivariance gives a contradiction. Thus
H⊨ZF−Foundation+¬Foundation+¬AC.​
(16)
The full construction over A, before restricting to H, has choice by ambient choice and the representability of choice graphs. Alternatively an ordinary ZFC first-order model gives the positive choice side. This proves the independence of AC from ZF minus foundation. The required metatheoretic consistency can be expressed using Con(ZF), since the constructible universe of a ZF first-order model supplies ZFC, as explained in the next solution. No assumption of a transitive model follows from mere consistency or is needed for these interpreted first-order model constructions.

An inner model of a universe of ZF is a transitive class M containing every ordinal and satisfying the required set-theoretic axioms with the inherited membership relation. Transitivity means that every member of a member of M belongs to M. Requiring all ordinals distinguishes an inner model from a transitive set first-order model such as Vκ​: a set first-order model has bounded ordinal height. One may specify an inner model of ZF, ZFC or a stronger theory; choice is not built into the term unless specified.
The fundamental example is the constructible universe. Form the constructible hierarchy
L0​=∅,Lα+1​=Def(Lα​),Lλ​=⋃α<λ​Lα​,L=⋃α∈Ord​Lα​.
(17)
Here definable power set means subsets definable over the set structure (Lα​,∈) by first-order formulas with finitely many parameters from that level. Each level is transitive, the levels increase, and Ord∩Lα​=α, by induction. Thus L is transitive and contains all ordinals.
The construction supplies a definable well-order: order objects first by their earliest construction stage, then by a least first-order formula code and a finite tuple of parameters in the already constructed level's well-order. Recursively extend the earlier well-order at each successor stage and unite compatible orders at limits. First-order formula codes and finite tuples of well-ordered parameters are well-orderable. This yields a canonical global ordering definable within L; its restrictions to sets will give choice once the ZF axioms are checked.
For that check, a useful reflection argument is explicit. Given finitely many first-order formulas and a starting level, pass to a higher level containing witnesses for their existential subformulas on all parameter tuples from the current level, choosing least witnesses in the canonical order. There are set-many tuples, so ambient replacement bounds their witness stages. Repeat for ω steps and take the union level Lθ​. Every relevant witness with parameters in that level appears at a later step. Induction on first-order formulas, with this witness property for the existential case, proves that Lθ​ reflects the chosen finite list of first-order formulas of L.
Separation on a constructible set is then definability over a sufficiently large reflecting level, so its subset appears at the next level. For replacement, reflect the first-order formula specifying a functional image and its required existence statements. All inputs belong to a level containing the domain as a set; all their unique outputs then lie in one reflecting level. The image is definable there and hence is constructible. For power set, the ambient set of constructible subsets of x has bounded construction stages by replacement in the ambient universe. At a level containing them all, this internal collection is definable by the bounded condition y⊆x, and therefore is itself in L. Pairing, union and axiom of infinity follow directly from definability over sufficiently large levels, while extensionality and foundation follow from transitivity. Thus L⊨ZF, and its canonical ordering gives L⊨AC.
This proves the important relative-consistency application
Con(ZF)⇒Con(ZFC).​
(18)
One does not conclude that choice is true in the original universe. Instead the inner model supplies a possibly smaller universe in which choice holds.
Constructibility also supplies GCH. Here is the cardinal-counting argument, making clear the role of the condensation lemma for the constructible universe. Work inside L. If x⊆κ is constructible, take a large limit Lθ​ containing x and an elementary hull of κ∪{κ,x} of size κ. Its transitive collapse is Lβ​ by condensation. The collapse fixes every ordinal below κ, and hence fixes x and κ. Since the hull has size κ, β<κ+. Therefore every constructible subset of κ appears below Lκ+​.
The condensation fact expresses the rigidity of this definability construction: first-order formula codes, finite parameter tuples and satisfaction in set-sized lower levels are preserved by the collapse. Inducting over the hull's ordinal indices identifies each collapsed lower level with the corresponding L-level; elementarity supplies every definable subset coded by parameters in that collapsed lower level. Taking unions identifies the entire collapsed hull with a level Lβ​.
Induction on construction stages gives ∣Lγ​∣≤∣γ∣+ℵ0​: each successor has only countably many first-order formula schemes with finite parameter tuples, and limits take unions. Hence ∣Lκ+​∣=κ+ for infinite κ. The preceding containment gives 2κ≤κ+ in L, and Cantor's theorem gives the reverse inequality. Consequently
L⊨ZFC+GCH,Con(ZF)⇒Con(ZFC+GCH).​
(19)
All these cardinal computations are internal to L; they need not equal the ambient cardinal computations.
Inner models are also useful for analyzing stronger axioms. If κ is inaccessible in V, it remains an uncountable regular cardinal in L, because a shorter cofinal sequence in L would already be one in V. For each ordinal λ<κ, its constructible subsets are fewer than κ even in the internal cardinal comparison: in V, P(λ) has size below κ, so its constructible part cannot have an L-well-order of length at least κ. Thus it remains strong limit. Accordingly L supplies relative-consistency information compatible with inaccessibles and GCH. Stronger embedding or measure properties require extra analysis, since the witnessing objects may disappear upon passing to an inner model.
There is a useful limitation: no uniform inner-model construction can refute choice from every starting ZF universe. Every inner model M of ZF contains the ambient L. Indeed, induction gives LαM​=Lα​: the same set-sized level has the same satisfaction relation for definitions, and limit stages take the same unions. If the starting universe already satisfies V=L, any inner model containing all ordinals must therefore be the whole universe, where choice holds. To obtain a choice-failing first-order model uniformly one needs a different method, such as the symmetry restriction in the preceding solution. Inner-model arguments establish consistency and preservation through carefully controlled smaller universes; they do not freely manufacture arbitrary desired axiom failures.

Here a monochromatic set must mean an infinite homogeneous set for a colouring: every singleton is a finite monochromatic set. The requested phenomenon holds for every finite n≥2. Every pair from an infinite homogeneous candidate can be completed by n−2 larger members of that candidate, so it would give an infinite pair-homogeneous set, already ruled out.
The uncountable positive theorem is the Erdős-Rado theorem for finite arities. For every infinite cardinal κ and finite r≥0, define ℶ0​(κ)=κ and ℶr+1​(κ)=2ℶr​(κ). Then
ℶr​(κ)+⟶(κ+)κr+1​.​
(22)
The partition relation means that every colouring of (r+1)-element subsets of the left-hand cardinal by at most κ colours has a homogeneous subset of size κ+. In particular, taking κ=ℵ0​ gives an uncountable homogeneous set for every finite arity, once the domain is sufficiently large.
We prove the end-homogeneity lemma needed for induction. Let μ be infinite, θ=(2μ)+, m≥1 finite, and c:[θ]m+1→κ with κ≤μ. In a sufficiently large Hχ​ choose an elementary substructure M of size 2μ containing c,θ and all ordinals below μ+, and closed under externally given sequences of length at most μ. Here Hχ​ consists of sets whose transitive closures have size below χ.
To justify that closure, begin with a hull containing the indicated parameters and ordinals. At each successor stage add every sequence of length at most μ from the preceding stage, then take a Skolem hull. There are at most (2μ)μ=2μ such sequences. Iterate for μ+ stages and take elementary unions at limits. The union still has size 2μ, and every sequence of at most μ of its elements is contained in one earlier stage by regularity of μ+, so appears at the next. This proves the claimed closed first-order model rather than assuming its existence.
Let β=sup(M∩θ)<θ. The inequality uses regularity of θ and ∣M∣<θ; M∩θ has no largest element, since it is closed under successor. Recursively choose increasing xα​∈M∩θ for α<μ+, imposing, for every m-tuple t of earlier points,
c(t∪{xα​})=c(t∪{β}).
(23)
At any stage there are at most μ earlier points and constraints. Each constraint consists of a tuple of elements of M and a colour below κ, also in M. The entire constraint code therefore belongs to M by closure. The bound above the previous points belongs to M as well. Although β itself need not belong to M, it witnesses in Hχ​ the existence of a point above that bound satisfying the coded constraints. Elementarity supplies such a point inside M. This proves the recursion.
On the resulting sequence X of length μ+, the colour of an (m+1)-tuple depends only on its first m entries: its last entry has the same colour contribution as β. This is the required closed elementary-submodel construction of an end-homogeneous sequence.
Now prove the theorem by induction on r. The case r=0 is the infinite pigeonhole principle: partitioning κ+ into at most κ classes of size at most κ cannot cover it. For r≥1, set μ=ℶr−1​(κ) and apply the lemma to the colouring on (2μ)+=ℶr​(κ)+. Define a colouring of r-tuples from its end-homogeneous sequence of length μ+ by their colour when completed with β. The induction hypothesis supplies a subset of size κ+ on which this derived colouring is constant. Every original (r+1)-tuple from that subset has the derived colour of its first r entries, and is therefore monochromatic. This completes the proof.
The unrestricted infinite-exponent situation is very different under AC. On [λ]ω, choose one representative R of each equivalence class modulo finite symmetric difference, and colour A by
c(A)=∣A△R∣(mod2).
(24)
If A is infinite and a∈A, deleting a remains in the same equivalence class and flips the colour. Every infinite candidate homogeneous set contains such an A and A∖{a} as subsets, so
λ↛(ω)2ω​for every infinite cardinal λ.​
(25)
More generally the same argument works for subsets of any fixed infinite cardinality: deleting one element preserves that cardinality, so there is no unrestricted analogue with a homogeneous set large enough to contain such subsets. For the order-type-ω convention, choose A in increasing order type ω and delete its first element; both sets still have that order type. Thus the distinction from the finite-arity theorem is genuine, not merely a notational ambiguity. This argument uses choice of representatives and is not a claim about every definability-restricted infinite-arity colouring.

A complete type over A⊆M is a maximal consistent set of first-order formulas in a fixed finite tuple of variables with parameters from A, consistent with the theory of that parameter expansion. A first-order model M is a saturated model of degree κ if every such type with ∣A∣<κ is realized in M. It suffices to require this for one-variable types and realize a finite tuple successively. Without a subscript, saturated commonly means ∣M∣-saturated; some contexts specify only finite-parameter saturation. We state the degree explicitly.
The saturated elementary extension theorem says that every first-order structure has a κ-saturated elementary extension for any specified infinite κ. It suffices to prove this for regular κ, since a larger regular degree implies the requested degree. At a single stage M, list all complete one-variable types over all subsets of M of size below κ. This collection is a set. Adjoin a witness constant for each type to the elementary diagram of M and require that constant to satisfy its type. Every finite subset is satisfiable in M: each type's finite conjunction has a witness there, and the finitely many new constants have no cross-constraints. The compactness theorem gives an elementary extension realizing all these types simultaneously.
Repeat this step along an elementary chain (Mα​)α<κ​, taking unions at limit stages, and let N be its union. The elementary chain theorem follows here by induction on first-order formulas: an existential statement true in the union has a witness in a later stage; elementarity brings an existential witness back to the earlier stage containing its parameters. Thus each stage is elementary in N.
If A⊆N has size below regular κ, all its elements lie in a single Mα​, by taking the supremum of their fewer-than-κ stage indices. Any complete type over A consistent with N is already consistent over Mα​, since these structures have the same parameter theory. It was therefore realized in Mα+1​. This proves κ-saturation of N.
A corresponding theorem about full saturation is available with a stated cardinal-size hypothesis: if λ is uncountable regular, ∣L∣<λ, and λ<λ=λ, then every infinite first-order model of size at most λ has an elementary extension of size λ which is λ-saturated. First adjoin λ pairwise distinct constants to the elementary diagram; every finite fragment is satisfiable because the original first-order model is infinite. A Skolem hull of their realizations and the original domain has size λ. There are at most λ small parameter sets and at most 2∣L∣+∣A∣+ℵ0​≤λ types over each of them. Compactness and the downward elementary-submodel construction keep each realization stage of size λ. Taking a Skolem hull of the preceding stage and the chosen witnesses closes under at most λ finite terms and proves this size bound and elementarity. The same length-λ chain is consequently a fully saturated model of size λ. The cardinal hypothesis is not silently asserted to hold for every cardinal.
We now prove consistency of NFU by the permitted alternative method of indiscernibles and a domain-shifting structure automorphism. This can start from an explicit set structure available in ZFC, so we need not assume that there is a first-order model of all ZFC. Let
H=Vω+ω​,F(n)=Vω+n​(n∈ω),
(26)
and give F the value ∅ off the natural numbers. Regard (H,∈,F,ω) as a first-order structure, with a named object ω. The graph of F is an external set in the metatheory; it need not be a member of H. This structure is extensional and satisfies every separation instance in the pure membership language: any subset of x∈H has rank below ω+ω and belongs to H. It also satisfies, for natural m<n,
∀X (X⊆F(m)⇒X∈F(n)),
(27)
since a subset of Vω+m​ has rank at most ω+m and hence belongs to Vω+n​.
Apply the indiscernible construction proved in question 7(i) to a Skolem expansion of this set structure, requiring (ci​)i∈Z​ to be increasing members of its named natural-number object. For every finite fragment use an increasing sequence of ordinary natural numbers in the initial structure in the Ramsey argument. The resulting generated elementary hull M has an external structure automorphism j with j(ci​)=ci+1​, preserving F and the named ω. Put Di​=FM(ci​) and D=D0​. Then j(Di​)=Di+1​, and M satisfies the displayed subset-containment assertion between successive domains. No claim that M satisfies replacement is required.
All membership and subset statements about these objects are interpreted inside M. The hull is necessarily externally nonstandard: the natural-number objects …,c−2​,c−1​,c0​ give an external descending chain. The external structure automorphism is not an object that must be definable within that first-order model.
On the external domain D define
S(y)⟺M⊨j(y)⊆D,xEy⟺S(y) and M⊨x∈j(y).​
(28)
This is the rank-shifting NFU model with explicit set predicate. The guard S(y) is essential: an object not contained in D could still have some ordinary members in D, and must not thereby become a nonempty atom. The objects failing S have no E-members. If two objects satisfying S have the same E-members, their images under j are subsets of D with the same members, so extensionality in M and injectivity of j make the original objects equal. Thus set-extensionality and the atom condition hold.
It remains to prove every comprehension instance. Use the domains Di​ just constructed, equivalently Di​=ji(D), for integer sorts. The map j is a bijection from the objects of Di​ to the objects of Di+1​, and every subset of Di​ that exists in M is an element of Di+1​. In sort i define the set predicate by y⊆Di−1​; from sort i to i+1 define membership by ordinary membership guarded by y⊆Di​.
For a stratified formula, assign integer types to its variables with equal types for equality and adjacent types for membership. Shift all types uniformly if necessary so the finitely many used types are nonnegative. Replace a variable of type i by ji of its untyped value, quantify it over Di​, and use the typed relations just described. Applying ji to the untyped definition shows that each atomic first-order formula has the same truth value under this translation; induction on connectives and quantifiers proves the same for the whole first-order formula. Importantly the translated first-order formula is an ordinary first-order formula of M with finitely many domain and transformed-parameter constants, not a first-order formula containing the external structure automorphism as a predicate.
If the comprehension variable has type i, separation in M produces its extension X⊆Di​. The subset-containment assertion gives X∈Di+1​. Put y=j−(i+1)(X)∈D. Then j(y)=j−i(X)⊆D, hence S(y), and for every x∈D,
xEy⟺ji(x)∈X⟺φ(x).
(29)
This verifies stratified comprehension with arbitrary parameters. The empty extension gives a distinguished empty set; the always-true first-order formula gives a universal set. The unstratified Russell predicate is not an allowed comprehension instance. Thus
ZFC⊢Con(NFU).​
(30)
Starting from the actual set structure H and applying compactness constructs a set first-order model, so this is a consistency proof in ZFC, not merely an assumption of Con(ZFC). It proves the requested consistency with an explicit interpretation; it does not depend on treating the much stronger full extensionality of NF as already consistent. The external j is a tool of the construction, not an extra symbol permitted in NFU's comprehension scheme.

One useful form of the Ehrenfeucht-Mostowski theorem is this: for an infinite first-order structure A and any total order I, there is a first-order model of Th(A) generated in a chosen Skolem expansion by an order-indiscernible sequence (ci​)i∈I​ of distinct elements. Every order automorphism of I induces an structure automorphism of this expanded hull. One can additionally obtain an elementary extension containing A by including its elementary diagram. Uniqueness of the induced structure automorphism is asserted in the Skolem expansion, not among arbitrary structure automorphisms of the reduct.
Choose a Skolem expansion A∗: for every existential first-order formula choose a witness function, satisfying ∃yφ(y,xˉ)⇒φ(fφ​(xˉ),xˉ). Iterate the expansion if necessary so witnesses are available for first-order formulas in the resulting language. Add constants ci​ and require ci​=cj​ for distinct indices. For every first-order formula φ(x1​,…,xm​) of the expanded language and every two increasing index tuples, add
φ(ci1​​,…,cim​​)⟷φ(cj1​​,…,cjm​​).
(31)
Together with Th(A∗), this is the indiscernibility theory.
Every finite fragment is satisfiable. It mentions only finitely many first-order formulas and index constants. Choose a countably infinite sequence of distinct elements of A∗. For each relevant arity, colour increasing tuples from that sequence by the finite truth vector of all first-order formulas of that arity in the fragment. Successive applications of the infinite Ramsey theorem for r-sets leave an infinite subset homogeneous for each of these finitely many finite colourings. Interpret the finitely many index constants by distinct elements of that subset in increasing enumeration order. All the required truth-value equivalences then hold, and the original structure satisfies the finite part of its own theory.
If an actual elementary extension of A is desired, include constants for its elements and the elementary diagram of A∗. A finite fragment still contains only finitely many parameter instances; include those truth vectors in the same colourings. Thus finite satisfiability is unchanged. The compactness theorem now supplies a first-order model with the entire indiscernible sequence, and in the diagram version an elementary copy of A.
Take the Skolem hull of the generators, including the diagram constants when present. The witness functions show directly that the hull is elementary: whenever an existential first-order formula with hull parameters is true in the ambient first-order model, its Skolem witness belongs to the hull. Induction on first-order formulas, or the Tarski-Vaught test, gives elementarity. Its reduct is therefore a first-order model of Th(A). In the version without diagram constants its cardinality is at most max(∣I∣,∣L∣,ℵ0​).
For an order automorphism ρ:I→I, define
ρ​(t(ci1​​,…,cim​​))=t(cρ(i1​)​,…,cρ(im​)​).
(32)
This is well defined. If two terms represent the same element, express their equality as a first-order formula on the increasing union of their generator indices. Indiscernibility and preservation of the index order carry that equality to the transported terms. The same argument for every relation shows preservation of the expanded structure; applying it to ρ−1 supplies the inverse. Every element is a term in the generators, so no other structure automorphism of the specified expansion can have the same action on them. Hence
order automorphisms of I extend uniquely to the generated Skolem expansion.​
(33)
For an application requiring the generators to lie in a definable infinite ordered class, impose that class predicate and its increasing-order first-order formulas too. The finite-fragment proof uses an increasing sequence in that class, so all these additional conditions are preserved. In particular the integer index order and its shift give the structure automorphism used in the NFU construction. The constants are order indiscernibles, not necessarily indiscernibles under arbitrary permutations of the index set.

In the game G(A), where A⊆ωω, the players alternately choose natural numbers and produce a sequence x. Player I wins exactly when x∈A. A strategy in an infinite game assigns a move to every finite position where its player is to move; it is winning if every play following it is won by that player. A game is determined if one player has a winning strategy. Both cannot have winning strategies, since their joint play would have to be won by both. The axiom of determinacy asserts
AD:every G(A), A⊆ωω, is determined.​
(34)
Without assuming AD, one can prove determinacy for finite games by backward induction, and for games with open or closed payoffs by the following argument. Give ωω its product topology of discrete move coordinates. If A is open, let W0​ be the finite positions whose entire cylinder of extensions lies in A. Form a winning-position attractor in an infinite game by transfinite recursion: add an I-position if some child has already been added, and add an II-position if all its children have already been added. Take unions at limits. The process stabilizes because there are only set-many positions and each genuine successor change adds a new one.
An added nonterminal position receives its least entry ordinal. At an I-position in the stable attractor, choose a child of smaller rank; at an II-position every child has smaller rank. Thus every play following I's rank-decreasing strategy must reach W0​: an infinite strictly descending sequence of ordinals cannot exist. The resulting play is in A, so I wins from any position in the attractor.
Outside the attractor, every child of an I-position remains outside, and an II-position has some child outside. Otherwise the ranks of all its children would have a set-sized supremum and the parent would have been added at a later stage. II chooses such a child and keeps the play outside W0​ forever. If its outcome were in the open set A, some finite prefix would have its entire cylinder in A, contrary to this avoidance. Therefore II wins from outside. With natural-number moves the least suitable child may be chosen at each position, so no choice axiom is needed for these strategies. This proves open determinacy. For a closed payoff, apply the same argument to its open complement with the players' winning roles interchanged. Clopen payoffs are included in both results.
Now assume AC and construct an undetermined game. The set of strategies for either player has cardinality c=2ℵ0​, since a strategy is a function on a countably infinite set of positions. Each fixed strategy is compatible with c plays: the other player's sequence of freely chosen moves determines distinct full plays. Use choice to enumerate I's strategies as (σα​)α<c​ and II's as (τα​)α<c​, and well-order the space of plays.
Recursively choose xα​ following σα​ and yα​ following τα​, with all selected plays distinct. At stage α fewer than c plays have been used, while each compatible-play set has size c, so fresh choices exist; take the least ones in the fixed well-order. This does not require regularity of c, since ∣α∣<c for every α below its initial ordinal.
Put A={yα​:α<c}. Every I-strategy σα​ has the losing compatible play xα​∈/A, and every II-strategy τα​ has the losing compatible play yα​∈A. Therefore neither player has a winning strategy. This choice diagonalization of an undetermined game proves
ZF⊢AC⇒¬AD.​
(35)
The elementary determinacy proofs above concern specified definable payoff classes; the diagonal construction explains why extending them to every arbitrary subset of the play space is incompatible with choice.

For Fodor's theorem, let κ be regular uncountable, S⊆κ stationary, and f:S→κ regressive: f(α)<α for every nonzero α∈S. A club set is unbounded in κ and closed under limits below κ; a stationary set meets every club. Removing 0 preserves stationarity. We prove that some fibre of f is stationary, not merely that one fibre is large.
We first check the club facts used in the argument. An intersection of fewer than κ club subsets of κ is club. Closure is immediate. For unboundedness, given γ and clubs (Ci​)i<μ​ with μ<κ, choose an increasing sequence γn​ starting above γ, such that γn+1​ is above a point of every Ci​ beyond γn​. Regularity bounds the supremum of the fewer-than-κ points at each step. Then δ=supn<ω​γn​<κ, and the chosen points in each Ci​ are cofinal in δ; closure puts δ in their intersection.
For a sequence (Cξ​)ξ<κ​ of clubs its diagonal intersection
Δξ<κ​Cξ​={δ<κ:∀ξ<δ (δ∈Cξ​)}
(36)
is also club. To obtain a point above γ, recursively choose γn+1​>γn​ in ⋂ξ≤γn​​Cξ​, possible by the preceding result, and take the supremum δ of the sequence. For each ξ<δ, every sufficiently late γn​ belongs to Cξ​, so δ∈Cξ​. For closure, if diagonal-intersection points are cofinal in δ<κ, then for each ξ<δ their sufficiently late points lie in Cξ​, and closure again puts δ there.
Suppose every fibre Sξ​={α∈S:f(α)=ξ} were nonstationary. Choose a club Cξ​ disjoint from each fibre. Stationarity supplies α∈S∩Δξ<κ​Cξ​, with α>0. Put ξ=f(α)<α. By diagonal membership α∈Cξ​, but by its definition α∈Sξ​, a contradiction. Thus
∃ξ<κ{α∈S:f(α)=ξ} is stationary.​
(37)
This is precisely the pressing-down assertion. Regularity and uncountability were used to keep the club-building suprema below κ.
For the Cantor normal form theorem, every nonzero ordinal has a unique expression
α=ωβ0​n0​+⋯+ωβm−1​nm−1​,β0​>⋯>βm−1​,0<ni​<ω.​
(38)
All operations are ordinal operations, so the order of the terms matters. The ordinal zero has the empty expression. We give the greedy existence proof and then prove uniqueness.
The powers ωβ are strictly increasing and continuous at limit exponents, and satisfy ωβ≥β. Hence the set of exponents with ωβ≤α is nonempty and bounded, and its supremum β0​ belongs to it by continuity (or is already its last successor member). Thus there is a largest such exponent and
ωβ0​≤α<ωβ0​+1=ωβ0​⋅ω.
(39)
Since the last product is the supremum of the finite multiples, there is a unique positive finite n0​ satisfying
ωβ0​n0​≤α<ωβ0​(n0​+1).
(40)
The initial segment of α of length ωβ0​n0​ leaves a tail of unique order type ρ, so
α=ωβ0​n0​+ρ,ρ<ωβ0​.
(41)
The bound follows from strict increase of ordinal addition in its right argument: a tail at least ωβ0​ would contradict the preceding interval. This is the relevant instance of the ordinal division algorithm.
If ρ>0, apply the same construction to it. Its leading exponent must be below β0​. Continue with the remainder. An infinite continuation would produce an infinite strictly descending sequence of ordinals, impossible by well-foundedness. Thus the process terminates after finitely many steps and gives the required form.
To prove uniqueness, first observe that a finite decreasing-exponent suffix with leading exponent γ is smaller than ωγ+1. This follows by induction on its number of terms: the later tail is below ωγ, so adding it to a finite multiple of ωγ stays below the next finite multiple and hence below ωγ⋅ω. Consequently the tail after a term with exponent β is below ωβ.
Any proposed normal form therefore places α in exactly the interval
ωβ0​n0​≤α<ωβ0​(n0​+1)<ωβ0​+1.
(42)
Its first exponent is recoverable as the largest exponent whose power does not exceed α, and its first coefficient is recoverable as the largest finite multiple not exceeding α. The right remainder is unique because ordinal addition is strictly increasing in its right argument. Apply the same reasoning to the remainder, successively fixing every exponent and coefficient. This proves uniqueness without treating ordinal addition as commutative or cancelling arbitrary left summands.
