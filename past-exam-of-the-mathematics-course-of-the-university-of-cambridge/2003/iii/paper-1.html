The Killing form is the bilinear form
B(X,Y)=tr(adXadY),adX(Z)=[X,Z].​
(17)
The Adjoint representation is linear, giving bilinearity, and the cyclic property of trace gives symmetry. To prove invariance, abbreviate the adjoint matrices of X,Y,Z by AX​,AY​,AZ​. The Jacobi identity gives A[X,Y]​=[AX​,AY​], and cyclic trace then gives
B([X,Y],Z)​=tr((AX​AY​−AY​AX​)AZ​)=tr(AX​(AY​AZ​−AZ​AY​))=B(X,[Y,Z]).​
(18)
Thus B([X,Y],Z)=B(X,[Y,Z])​, equivalently B([X,Y],Z)+B(Y,[X,Z])=0. This is infinitesimal invariance of the Killing form, with no choice of basis affecting the trace.

For the embedded Lie group, the tangent space Te​G consists of velocities γ′(0) of smooth curves in G satisfying γ(0)=e. The ambient coordinates identify these velocities with vectors in Rn, but the tangent space itself is intrinsic. Let cg​(a)=gag−1. Since this conjugation map fixes e, its derivative defines the Adjoint representation of a Lie group:
Adg​=(dcg​)e​:Te​G→Te​G.​
(22)
The inverse is Adg−1​, and cgh​=cg​∘ch​ gives Adgh​=Adg​Adh​. Thus this is a smooth group homomorphism into the linear automorphisms of Te​G.
Differentiate that representation at the identity. The tangent space of GL(Te​G) at its identity is End(Te​G), so ad=(dAd)e​ is a linear map from Te​G into those endomorphisms. The Lie bracket is
[X,Y]=ad(X)Y=dsd​​s=0​Adγ(s)​Y,γ(0)=e,γ′(0)=X.​

Equivalently, if η(0)=e and η′(0)=Y, this is the mixed derivative at (0,0) of γ(s)η(t)γ(s)−1 in ambient coordinates. For a matrix Lie group, conjugation is ordinary matrix conjugation and this formula reduces to [X,Y]=XY−YX.

Use the finite-dimensional complex-module setting of the course. We prove the Weyl complete reducibility theorem by Haar averaging produces invariant complements, defining its constructions rather than invoking complete reducibility itself.
Choose a compact real form k⊂g: it is a real Lie subalgebra with g=k⊕ik, and its simply connected Lie group K is compact. Existence of this form and compact group is a standard fact for complex semisimple Lie algebras. Restrict the representation to k; integration of a Lie-algebra representation gives a group representation R:K→GL(V). Since K is connected and generated by exponentials, the g-invariant subspace W is also K-invariant.
Choose any positive-definite Hermitian inner product on V. With the Haar measure of K normalized to mass one, define
⟨v,w⟩K​=∫K​⟨R(k)v,R(k)w⟩0​dk.
(29)
This is a Hermitian inner product, and it is positive-definite: for v=0 every R(k)v is nonzero, so the continuous positive integrand has positive integral. Right invariance of Haar measure gives ⟨R(a)v,R(a)w⟩K​=⟨v,w⟩K​ for a∈K.
Let W′=W⊥ for this averaged inner product. Finite-dimensional inner-product linear algebra gives V=W⊕W′. If v∈W′ and w∈W, then
⟨R(a)v,w⟩K​=⟨v,R(a−1)w⟩K​=0,
(30)
because R(a−1)w∈W. Thus W′ is K-invariant. Differentiating makes it k-invariant, and since it is a complex vector subspace, g=k+ik also preserves it. Therefore
W′=W⊥K​ is a g-submodule and V=W⊕W′.​
(31)
The zero or whole-space submodule cases are included. This proves the required complement theorem without circularly assuming it.
The finite-dimensional hypothesis is essential to the statement. The nonsplit zero-weight Verma extension for sl2 is a counterexample for unrestricted modules: M(0) has basis vn​ with hvn​=−2nvn​, fvn​=vn+1​ and evn​=n(1−n)vn−1​. Its submodule spanned by vn​ for n≥1 has trivial one-dimensional quotient. A complement would be trivial and contained in the zero-weight line Cv0​, yet fv0​=v1​=0. Hence no such complement exists in that infinite-dimensional case. The paper's finite-dimensional representation setting supplies the necessary hypothesis.

For a finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation V, a weight of a representation relative to h is a linear functional μ∈h∗ for which the simultaneous eigenspace
Vμ​={v∈V:H⋅v=μ(H)v for every H∈h}

The set of weights and their multiplicities are distinct information: several basis vectors can occupy the same point of a weight diagram.

In the semisimple Lie algebra convention, an abstract root system is a finite subset Φ of a finite-dimensional real Euclidean space E with the following properties: it spans E, does not contain zero, is reduced, is invariant under each of its root reflections, and satisfies crystallographic integrality. More explicitly, for every α,β∈Φ,
Φ∩Rα={α,−α},sα​(β)=β−(α,α)2(β,α)​α∈Φ,(α,α)2(β,α)​∈Z.​
(39)
Here ( , ) is a positive-definite inner product. The first condition is reducedness, the second gives closure under root reflections, and the last number is a Cartan integer. The rank is dimE. A simple root basis is a basis of E consisting of roots such that each root has integral coordinates that are either all nonnegative or all nonpositive. The two signs distinguish positive and negative roots. The diagrams in this problem concern these reduced crystallographic root systems; broader definitions sometimes omit one of these conditions.

This standard simple-root descent may also be quoted here. The eight-vector set just listed is stable under both generating reflections, and the orbits of the two simple roots already fill it. Hence no simple-root orbit, and therefore no root, lies outside that set. This establishes both the necessity of every drawn root and exhaustion of the root system.
