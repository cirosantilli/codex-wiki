Let R be a commutative Artinian ring. We prove finite composition length first, without assuming the conclusion that R is Noetherian.
Every prime ideal is maximal. Indeed, an Artinian integral domain is a field: for a=0, stabilization of (am) gives am=am+1b, and cancellation gives ab=1. There are only finitely many maximal ideals. To see this, choose a minimal finite intersection L=m1​∩⋯∩ms​ using the descending chain condition. Minimality gives L⊆m for any other maximal ideal m. Since m1​⋯ms​⊆L, primeness forces one mi​⊆m, and maximality makes them equal. Thus the Jacobson radical J is this finite intersection, and the Chinese remainder theorem gives
R/J≅∏i=1s​R/mi​.
(1)
We also need nilpotence of J. Its powers stabilize; put I=Jm at a stabilization stage, so I2=I. If I=0, choose an ideal L minimal subject to IL=0. Since I(IL)=IL=0, minimality gives IL=L. Choose a∈L with Ia=0. Minimality applied to the subideal Ra gives L=Ra. The equality IL=L then yields a=xa for some x∈I⊆J. But 1−x is a unit by the unit criterion for the Jacobson radical; hence a=0, a contradiction. Therefore Jm=0.
Each layer Ji/Ji+1 is an Artinian module over the finite product of fields R/J. Its components over those fields are finite-dimensional: an infinite-dimensional vector space has a strictly descending chain of subspaces. Each layer consequently has finite composition length. The finite filtration
R⊃J⊃⋯⊃Jm=0
(2)
proves that R itself has finite composition length, and therefore satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals. Every commutative Artinian ring is Noetherian. The zero ring satisfies the conclusion trivially.
The relevant Artin–Wedderburn theorem says that a semisimple ring which is a right Artinian ring has the form
R≅i=1∏s​Mni​​(Di​),​
(3)
where the Di​ are division rings and the positive integers ni​ and division rings are unique up to permutation and isomorphism. The division rings need not be commutative or finite-dimensional over a field. Conversely every such finite product is semisimple and a right Artinian ring.
Here is a proof which also works if semisimplicity is initially expressed as J(R)=0. A nonzero right ideal contains a minimal nonzero right ideal L by the descending chain condition; L is a simple module. If L2=0, then for every l∈L and r∈R, (lr)2=0, so 1−lr has inverse 1+lr. The unit criterion for the Jacobson radical would give L⊆J(R)=0, a contradiction. Thus some a∈L induces a nonzero map L→L by left multiplication. This map is an isomorphism, since L is simple. Choose e∈L with ae=a. Then a(e2−e)=0 and injectivity on L gives e2=e. We obtain L=eR and the splitting
RR​=eR⊕(1−e)R.
(4)
For a right ideal K containing e, the same argument splits K=eR⊕(K∩(1−e)R). Repeatedly split a minimal right ideal out of the remaining summand. The remaining summands form a strictly descending chain of right ideals, so the process terminates. Thus
RR​≅⨁i=1s​Si⊕ni​​
(5)
for pairwise nonisomorphic simple modules Si​.
A nonzero map between simple modules is an isomorphism: its kernel and image leave no alternatives. Thus Di​=EndR​(Si​) is a division ring, and HomR​(Si​,Sj​)=0 for i=j. This is the needed Schur lemma. Taking endomorphisms of the direct sum gives ∏i​Mni​​(Di​). Finally, left multiplication identifies R with EndR​(RR​): a right-module endomorphism sends r to f(1)r, and composition corresponds to multiplication in R, not its opposite. This proves the displayed ring isomorphism.
For the converse, the right regular module of Mn​(D) is the direct sum of its n row modules. Each row module is simple, since elementary matrices and nonzero scalar inverses send any nonzero row to any prescribed row. A finite product therefore has a finite semisimple regular module and is a right Artinian ring. The simple-module multiplicities and their endomorphism division rings recover the factors, establishing uniqueness.

Put A=R[[X]], and let J be any ideal of this formal power series ring. For d≥0 define an ideal of R by
Ld​={a∈R:some f∈J∩XdA has coefficient a at Xd}.
(6)
Multiplication by X shows Ld​⊆Ld+1​. Since R is Noetherian, this chain stabilizes at some d=N, and each Ld​ is finitely generated. For every 0≤d≤N, choose finitely many series fd,j​∈J∩XdA whose degree-d coefficients generate Ld​.
Given f∈J, cancel its degree-zero coefficient using the f0,j​, then its degree-one coefficient using the f1,j​, and so on. At a degree d≥N, use Xd−NfN,j​. Each residual remains in J and has zero coefficients below the next cancellation degree. For each of the finitely many chosen fd,j​, accumulate its multiplier into a formal power series hd,j​. The higher-degree increments of these multipliers converge coefficientwise. At every degree the cancellation procedure gives the exact coefficient identity, so
f=∑d=0N​∑j​hd,j​fd,j​.
(7)
Thus the finitely many lifts generate J as an actual ideal. No assumption that J is closed has been used. This proves Noetherianity of formal power series rings:
R[[X]] is Noetherian.​
(8)
Iterating the argument proves the assertion for R[[X1​,…,Xs​]], which is naturally the iterated formal power series ring.
For a surjective module map f:M→N, define the adic completion of a module by
M=lim​r≥1​M/IrM,N=lim​r≥1​N/IrN.
(9)
Take n∈N and choose representatives nr​∈N for its components. Compatibility says nr+1​−nr​∈IrN. Choose m1​ with f(m1​)=n1​. If f(mr​)=nr​, surjectivity gives f(IrM)=IrN, so choose δr​∈IrM with f(δr​)=nr+1​−nr​ and put mr+1​=mr​+δr​. The classes of mr​ modulo IrM are compatible and map to the chosen components of n. Hence
M⟶N is surjective.​
(10)
This surjectivity on adic completions does not require finite generation of M or N. The compatibility of the lifts is the point; independent lifts of the finite-level classes would not suffice.
Since R is Noetherian, write I=(a1​,…,as​). Define a ring homomorphism
Φ:R[[X1​,…,Xs​]]⟶R,Xi​⟼ai​.
(11)
Modulo Ir, evaluating a series requires only its finitely many terms of total degree below r. These evaluations commute with the reduction maps, and define Φ without needing the original ring to be complete or separated.
To prove surjectivity, begin with b∈R. Choose b0​∈R matching its component modulo I. After terms of total degree below r have been chosen, their evaluation differs from b at level R/Ir+1 by a class in Ir/Ir+1. This class is an R-linear combination of the monomials aα with ∣α∣=r. Choose the corresponding homogeneous degree-r polynomial pr​(X) as the next term. Then the series b0​+∑r≥1​pr​(X) evaluates to b at every level. This proves the power-series presentation of an adic completion, and therefore
R≅R[[X1​,…,Xs​]]/kerΦ is Noetherian.​
(12)
A quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian, since its ideals correspond to ideals containing the kernel. The cases I=0 and I=R give R=R and R=0, respectively, and satisfy the conclusion as well.

A regular element of a ring is an element c for which both multiplication maps are injective:
cr=0⟹r=0,rc=0⟹r=0.
(13)
It is thus neither a left nor a right zero divisor; it need not be a unit. Here “ideals” means two-sided ideals. Put A=An​(C), and let T2​(A) denote the specified upper triangular ring.
We first establish the additional facts about the Weyl algebra needed below. Its generators satisfy
[xi​,xj​]=[∂i​,∂j​]=0,[∂i​,xj​]=δij​.
(14)
Reordering gives a spanning set xα∂β. These monomials are independent. Indeed, represent the generators by multiplication and differentiation on C[x1​,…,xn​]. In a putative zero operator P=∑β​fβ​(x)∂β, choose β of maximal total derivative degree. Iterated commutation with multiplication by the coordinates, βi​ times in coordinate i, gives multiplication by β!fβ​(x). All other derivative terms of that total degree disappear unless they have exactly the same multiindex, and lower-degree terms disappear. Thus fβ​=0, and descending induction eliminates all coefficients. This proves the ordered monomial basis of a Weyl algebra and faithfulness of its polynomial action.
The Weyl algebra is a simple ring. In a nonzero two-sided ideal choose a nonzero element of least Bernstein filtration degree, where both xi​ and ∂i​ have degree one. Commuting it with any generator stays in the ideal and lowers that degree. Minimality therefore makes all these commutators zero. Commutation with every xi​ forces all derivative coefficients with β=0 to vanish; commutation with every ∂i​ then forces all partial derivatives of the remaining polynomial to vanish. In characteristic zero the element is a nonzero scalar. The ideal consequently contains 1. This proves simplicity of a Weyl algebra in characteristic zero. Its Jacobson radical is zero, since the radical is a proper two-sided ideal of a nonzero unital simple ring.
Use the two diagonal idempotents to isolate the entries of any ideal of T2​(A). It must have the form
(I11​0​I12​I22​​),
(15)
where all three entries are two-sided ideals of A and I11​A+AI22​⊆I12​. Conversely this condition makes the indicated set an ideal. Simplicity says that each Iij​ is 0 or A, and either nonzero diagonal entry forces I12​=A. Hence the five ideals of R1​ are
0,N=(00​A0​),(A0​A0​),(00​AA​),R1​.​
(16)
For a two-sided ideal of M2​(A), multiplication on both sides by elementary matrices isolates any entry and moves it to any matrix position. The entries obtained form one two-sided ideal I of A, and the original ideal is M2​(I). Thus the only ideals of R2​ are 0 and R2​.
Since N2=0, N lies in the Jacobson radical of R1​: for z∈N and r∈R1​, zr∈N and 1−zr has inverse 1+zr. On the other hand, R1​/N≅A×A has zero radical, so the image of the radical in this quotient is zero. The elementary unit criterion for the Jacobson radical justifies both assertions. Therefore
J(R1​)=N,J(R2​)=0.​
(17)
The second equality follows from simplicity of R2​.
We now construct the denominators used to describe regular matrices. A left Noetherian noncommutative domain satisfies the left Ore condition. For if Aa∩Ab=0 for two nonzero elements, the left ideals Ab,Aba,Aba2,… have direct sum: in a relation the first summand lies in Ab, all the others in Aa, so it vanishes; cancellation of the final a repeats the argument. Their finite partial sums form a strictly ascending chain, contradicting left Noetherianity. Thus Aa∩Ab=0, giving common nonzero left multiples and the required Ore equations. The analogous argument with right ideals gives the right Ore condition under right Noetherianity. Both hypotheses for A are allowed in the question.
The Ore localization therefore embeds A in a division ring Q. Briefly, its elements are left fractions a−1b, with nonzero a, and two fractions are identified after passing to a common left denominator. Common multiples supply addition and multiplication, while cancellation in the domain makes the embedding injective. A nonzero fraction has an inverse, obtained by interchanging its numerator and denominator in the appropriate Ore fraction calculation. The right fraction construction gives the same division ring, so finite lists have common left and common right denominators.
A triangular matrix c=(r0​st​) with r,t=0 is invertible over T2​(Q), with inverse
(r−10​−r−1st−1t−1​).
(18)
It is consequently regular over T2​(A). If r=0, then cE11​=0; if t=0, then E22​c=0. Hence
Reg(R1​)={(r0​st​):r,t=0}.​
(19)
A full matrix is regular exactly when it is invertible over Q:
Reg(R2​)=M2​(A)∩GL2​(Q).​
(20)
One implication follows immediately from invertibility. Conversely, a singular matrix over a division ring has a nonzero right kernel column and a nonzero left kernel row, by row reduction. A common right denominator turns the column into a nonzero column over A, providing a right annihilator matrix over A. A common left denominator does the same for the row and gives a left annihilator. Thus a singular matrix is not regular. Explicitly, for c=(rt​su​) with r=0, row elimination says that regularity is equivalent to u−tr−1s=0 in Q. If r=0, it is equivalent to both s=0 and t=0. A commutative determinant formula must not be substituted here.
Finally both classical left rings of quotients exist, and
Qcll​(R1​)=T2​(Q),Qcll​(R2​)=M2​(Q).​
(21)
To prove this directly, clear a common left denominator a=0 from all the finitely many entries of a matrix over Q. It becomes (aI2​)−1b with b in the original triangular or full ring and aI2​ regular. Every original regular element becomes a unit in the corresponding ring over Q, by the descriptions above. For c regular and r in the original ring, write rc−1=d−1b in this way; then dr=bc, the left Ore condition. Since the denominators are regular, the additional annihilator condition for localization is automatic. This proves the matrix and triangular localization over an Ore domain assertions. In particular, the nonzero nilpotent radical of the triangular ring does not prevent its classical localization; that localization retains the triangular radical.

A precise version of the Hilbert-Serre theorem is as follows. Let R=⨁j≥0​Rj​ be a commutative graded ring, generated over an Artinian degree-zero ring R0​ by homogeneous elements y1​,…,ys​ of positive degrees d1​,…,ds​. Let M be a finitely generated graded module, bounded below. Each component has finite module length over R0​. Its Poincare series of a graded module, or Hilbert series, is
PM​(t)=∑j​ℓR0​​(Mj​)tj.
(22)
When R0​ is a field, these lengths are dimensions. The theorem asserts
PM​(t)=∏i=1s​(1−tdi​)q(t)​,q(t)∈Z[t,t−1].​
(23)
For a nonnegatively graded module the numerator can be taken in Z[t]. The finite-generation and finite-length hypotheses specify the meaning of the series; they are part of the theorem's statement.
We prove it by induction on s. Regard M as a finite graded module over S=R0​[Y1​,…,Ys​], with degYi​=di​, acting through the specified generators of R. This polynomial ring is Noetherian by the Hilbert basis theorem and Question 1. When s=0, finitely many homogeneous generators over R0​ mean that only finitely many components are nonzero, so the series is a Laurent polynomial. For the induction step, put y=Ys​, d=ds​, K=(0:M​y) and C=M/yM. Both K and C are finite graded modules; y annihilates them, so they are modules over R0​[Y1​,…,Ys−1​]. The finite generation of K uses Noetherianity of S.
The exact sequence of graded modules
0⟶K(−d)⟶M(−d) y ​M⟶C⟶0
(24)
gives, by additivity of component lengths,
(1−td)PM​(t)=PC​(t)−tdPK​(t).
(25)
Here the shift is defined so that PM(−d)​=tdPM​. By induction both series on the right have denominator ∏i<s​(1−tdi​). Dividing by 1−td proves the result. The annihilator term cannot be omitted unless multiplication by y is injective.
In the standard grading di​=1, the coefficients of (1−t)−s are (s−1j+s−1​). Multiplying by the finite numerator shows that the component dimensions agree with a polynomial for sufficiently large j. Cumulative dimensions have generating function PM​(t)/(1−t) and are also eventually polynomial. This consequence supplies the dimension used for Weyl algebra modules.
Use the Bernstein filtration Bm​An​, in which both coordinate generators and differentiation generators have degree one. The ordered monomial basis of a Weyl algebra gives
grB​An​≅C[x1​,…,xn​,ξ1​,…,ξn​],dimC​Bm​An​=(2nm+2n​).
(26)
Choose a finite-dimensional generating subspace M0​ of a nonzero finitely generated left module M and set Mm​=Bm​An​M0​. This is a good filtration of a module: the symbols of the generators generate its associated graded module over the displayed polynomial ring. The Hilbert-Serre theorem makes dimC​Mm​ an eventual polynomial. Define the Bernstein growth dimension of a Weyl algebra module by
d(M)=deg(dimC​Mm​ for m≫0).​
(27)
The dimension is independent of the chosen finite generating subspace. Each of two finite generating sets is contained in a fixed filtration step for the other, giving Mm​⊆Mm+c′​ and Mm′​⊆Mm+c′​. Such fixed shifts preserve the degree of growth. The same argument applies to any good Bernstein filtration. This dimension agrees with the Gelfand–Kirillov dimension of a module; one may set d(0)=−∞.
Let the nonconstant element D have Bernstein degree d≥1. Since grB​An​ is a domain, the product of two nonzero symbols cannot vanish. Hence
degB​(aD)=degB​a+d(a=0),(An​D)∩Bm​An​=(Bm−d​An​)D.
(28)
Right multiplication by D is injective, using the domain property of An​. Thus the quotient filtration on M=An​/An​D has cumulative dimensions
dimC​Mm​=(2nm+2n​)−(2nm−d+2n​)(m≥d).
(29)
The leading terms of degree 2n cancel, and the leading surviving term is
(2n−1)!d​m2n−1.
(30)
It is nonzero, because d≥1. Therefore
d(An​/An​D)=2n−1.​
(31)
Equivalently the Hilbert series of the associated graded quotient is (1−td)/(1−t)2n. Here d is Bernstein degree, not differential order: multiplication by x1​ has differential order zero but Bernstein degree one. Using the order filtration in this dimension argument would lose the finite-dimensional filtration pieces.

The base ring must be specified in the algebraic definition. For a commutative k-algebra R, work in Endk​(R) and let mf​ mean multiplication by f∈R. Set Dk−1​(R)=0 and define recursively
Dki​(R)={P∈Endk​(R):[P,mf​]∈Dki−1​(R) for every f∈R}.
(32)
These are the operators of order at most i. The order of a nonzero differential operator relative to a base ring is the least i for which it belongs to Dki​(R); the zero operator can be assigned order −∞. An operator of order zero commutes with all multiplications and is itself multiplication by P(1), so Dk0​(R)=R. The union Dk​(R)=⋃i≥0​Dki​(R) is the ring of finite-order operators, with composition as multiplication. The identity
[PQ,mf​]=P[Q,mf​]+[P,mf​]Q
(33)
and induction on the sum of orders give Dkp​Dkq​⊆Dkp+q​. Sums have order at most the maximum of their orders, proving that this union is a ring.
For an arbitrary commutative ring, the absolute version uses k=Z, so the ambient endomorphisms are additive. For the polynomial ring in this question, the intended Weyl-algebra identification is the relative one with k=C. Absolute operators would also include nonzero derivations of the coefficient field C: a derivation on a rational function field in a transcendence basis over Q extends to its algebraic closure in characteristic zero, and hence to C. Extending it coefficientwise to C[x] gives an order-one additive operator which is not complex-linear. Such an operator cannot belong to the complex Weyl algebra. This explains why the linearity convention matters.
Now take R=C[x1​,…,xn​]. Coordinate multiplication has order zero, and each partial derivative ∂i​ has order one because [∂i​,mf​]=m∂i​f​. They satisfy the defining Weyl algebra relations, giving an algebra homomorphism An​→DC​(R). Reordering spans An​ by xα∂β. If an ordered expression acted as zero, commutation with the coordinates according to a maximal derivative multiindex would give multiplication by β!fβ​(x)=0; removing the top coefficients successively proves that every coefficient is zero. Thus the homomorphism is injective, as in the ordered monomial basis of a Weyl algebra proof.
For surjectivity, let P have order at most m, and put δi​P=[P,mxi​​]. The operations δi​ commute, since the coordinate multiplications commute. A product of more than m of them kills P. Define polynomial coefficients
aβ​(x)=β!1​(δβP)(1),∣β∣≤m.
(34)
Commuting P through mxα​ repeatedly gives the finite identity
P(xα)=∑β≤α​(βα​)xα−β(δβP)(1).
(35)
It follows by induction on ∣α∣ from Pmxi​​=mxi​​P+δi​P and Pascal's identity. Terms with ∣β∣>m vanish. Since
∂βxα=β!(βα​)xα−β
(36)
for β≤α, we obtain P(xα)=∑∣β∣≤m​aβ​(x)∂β(xα). The monomials are a complex basis of R, so complex linearity yields equality of the operators on all of R. Hence
DC​(C[x1​,…,xn​])=An​(C).​
(37)
For a nonzero ordered expression ∑β​aβ​(x)∂β, its order is exactly maxaβ​=0​∣β∣. The upper bound follows from composition; the lower bound follows by taking that many coordinate commutators to extract a nonzero multiplication operator. This describes the order filtration of a Weyl algebra:
Dm(R)=⨁∣β∣≤m​R∂β.
(38)
The associated graded ring is therefore
grD​An​≅C[x1​,…,xn​,ξ1​,…,ξn​],degxi​=0,degξi​=1.​
(39)
The symbol of ∂i​ is ξi​. In a product of ordered operators, moving derivatives past coefficients introduces terms of lower differential order. Thus top-order symbols multiply as ordinary polynomials, and the ordered basis proves there are no further relations. In particular [Dp,Dq]⊆Dp+q−1, with D−1=0: the top-order symbols of the two products agree and cancel. Unlike the Bernstein filtration, the order filtration's degree-zero part is the entire polynomial ring and its pieces are infinite-dimensional over C.
Form the Rees ring of a filtered algebra
A=⨁m≥0​Dm(R)hm⊆An​[h].
(40)
The central element h is a non-zero-divisor. This ring is generated by h, the xi​, and yi​=h∂i​, with
[yi​,xj​]=δij​h,[xi​,xj​]=[yi​,yj​]=0.
(41)
Its quotient modulo h is the associated graded ring just computed. Explicitly the degree-m component of hA is Dm−1hm, so the degree-m quotient is Dm/Dm−1. The quotient modulo h−1 recovers An​.
Since A/hA is commutative, any two lifts F,G∈A of symbols have [F,G]∈hA. Define
{F,G}=h[F,G]​(modhA).
(42)
Division by h is unique because h is a non-zero-divisor. If F is replaced by F+hU, the quotient changes by [U,G], which is zero modulo h; changing G works the same way. Thus the bracket is well defined. Antisymmetry and bilinearity follow from the commutator. The identity [F,GH]=[F,G]H+G[F,H] proves the Leibniz rule. For the Jacobi identity, lift each inner bracket by [G,H]/h; the cyclic sum of the outer expressions is the commutator Jacobi identity divided by h2, hence zero. These verifications prove that the graded polynomial ring is a Poisson algebra. This is the Poisson bracket from a central deformation parameter construction.
The generators give {ξi​,xj​}=δij​ and all coordinate-coordinate and symbol-symbol brackets zero. The Leibniz rule therefore determines the answer on every polynomial:
{f,g}=i=1∑n​(∂ξi​∂f​∂xi​∂g​−∂xi​∂f​∂ξi​∂g​).​
(43)
The sign here follows the chosen convention [P,Q]=PQ−QP: in particular {ξi​,xj​}=δij​, rather than {xi​,ξj​}=δij​. For homogeneous symbols the bracket has order degree p+q−1, and can equivalently be written {σp​(P),σq​(Q)}=σp+q−1​([P,Q]).
