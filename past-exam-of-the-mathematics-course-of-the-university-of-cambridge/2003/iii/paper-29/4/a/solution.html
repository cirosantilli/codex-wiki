For probability measures on a metric space, weak convergence of probability measures μn​⇒μ means ∫fdμn​→∫fdμ for every bounded continuous real function f. On the real line it is equivalently convergence of the cumulative distribution functions at every continuity point of the limiting cumulative distribution function. Bounded continuous upper and lower approximations to interval indicators give one direction; a partition at continuity points, uniform approximation on a compact interval, and control of the tails give the reverse direction.
For a probability measure μ on R, its characteristic function is φμ​(t)=∫eitxμ(dx). The Lévy continuity theorem states: μn​⇒μ implies φμn​​(t)→φμ​(t) for every real t; conversely, if these characteristic functions have a pointwise limit φ which is continuous at zero, there is a unique probability measure μ with characteristic function φ, and μn​⇒μ. In particular, for a specified limiting probability measure, weak convergence is equivalent to pointwise convergence of its characteristic functions.
The forward implication follows by testing cosine and sine. The dominated convergence theorem shows that every characteristic function is continuous at zero and has value one there. For the converse, first establish tightness of probability measures. By Fubini's theorem, for h>0,
2h1​∫−hh​(1−Reφμn​​(t))dt=∫(1−hxsin(hx)​)μn​(dx).
(21)
The integrand on the right is at least 1/2 when ∣x∣≥2/h. This proves the characteristic-function tightness bound
μn​(∣x∣≥2/h)≤h1​∫−hh​(1−Reφμn​​(t))dt.

The integrals converge for each h by dominated convergence, and continuity of φ at zero makes the limit as small as desired by choosing h small. Thus all sufficiently late measures have a common tail bound; the finitely many earlier measures also have small tails once the radius is increased. This is uniform tightness of probability measures.
We give the required compactness argument explicitly. For any subsequence, choose a further diagonal subsequence whose cumulative distribution functions Fn​ converge at every rational q, to f(q). Define
F(x)=infq>xq∈Q​​f(q).
(23)
This function is nondecreasing and right-continuous: given a rational q>x with f(q) close to F(x), the same bound holds for F(y) when x<y<q. Tightness gives F(−∞)=0 and F(+∞)=1, so F is a cumulative distribution function of a probability measure ν. For rationals r<x<s, Fn​(r)≤Fn​(x)≤Fn​(s). The limits bracket F(x−) and F(x); at a continuity point of F they coincide. Thus Fn​(x)→F(x) at all such points. To obtain weak convergence, choose a compact interval with small tails and partition it using continuity points of F. A bounded continuous function is uniformly continuous there, so step-function approximations and convergence of interval probabilities prove convergence of its integral. This proves Helly selection for distribution functions and supplies a weak subsequential limit ν.
Its characteristic function is φ by the already proved forward implication. We also prove uniqueness rather than assume it. If two probability measures ν,ρ have the same characteristic function, convolve each with a centered normal distribution of variance ε2>0. Their densities are, by the Gaussian integral and Fubini's theorem,
pν,ε​(x)=2π1​∫R​e−itxφν​(t)e−ε2t2/2dt,
(24)
and likewise for ρ. The integrable Gaussian factor justifies exchanging the integrals, so these densities agree. For every bounded continuous f, letting ε↓0 in ∫E[f(x+εZ)]ν(dx), with Z a standard normal random variable, gives ∫fdν by bounded convergence theorem. Hence the two measures have equal integrals against all these test functions, and approximating interval indicators shows ν=ρ. This proves the uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions.
Every subsequence therefore has a further subsequence weakly converging to the same unique μ. If the original sequence failed weak convergence, some bounded continuous test function would have a subsequence of integrals staying a fixed distance from its integral under μ, contradicting that further convergence. This completes both directions of the theorem. Continuity at zero is essential: the laws N(0,n) have pointwise limiting characteristic function zero away from zero and one at zero, and are not tight.
