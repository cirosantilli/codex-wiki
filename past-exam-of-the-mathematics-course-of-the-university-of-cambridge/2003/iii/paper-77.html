Write q=bu, ρ=ρ∞​ and r=ρ−ρs​>0. The first balance is depth-integrated momentum conservation. Its left side is the downstream increase of horizontal momentum flux. The wind contributes the applied stress S; the remaining term is the resultant hydrostatic force associated with the sloping base of the light layer. Because ρ​−ρ<0, that term opposes the wind when b′>0.
For a strip of unit alongshore width and length dx, let E=q′ be the entrained ocean-volume flux per unit area. Ocean entering the layer carries temperature T∞​ and concentration C∞​ but no horizontal momentum in this model. Take the reference liquid enthalpy at T∞​ to be zero and set
e=cp​(T−T∞​)−Lϕ.
(1)
The latent term is negative because ice has lower enthalpy than liquid at the same temperature. The incoming entrained water has zero relative enthalpy, and the outgoing minus incoming horizontal enthalpy flux is [eq]′dx. Atmospheric heat loss therefore gives [eq]′=−f. Similarly, solute conservation gives
[Cq]′=C∞​E=C∞​q′,[(C−C∞​)q]′=0.​
(2)
These are the second and third balances. Their reference-flux form has already subtracted the heat and solute brought in by entrainment; those fluxes must not be subtracted again.
There is a dimensional normalization to make explicit. With the printed specific cp​,L, the physical heat-flux balance is ρ[eq]′=−Fphys​. Thus f=Fphys​/ρ. The displayed exam equation uses its symbol F for f, or implicitly works with density-normalized heat flux. The derivation below uses f to keep these two meanings distinct.
With no incoming coastal jet or ice source, the conditions at x=0 are
q⟶0,ρbu2⟶0,eq⟶0,(C−C∞​)q⟶0.
(3)
The regular emerging layer also has T→T∞​, ϕ→0, C→C∞​ and b→0. Prescribed nonzero inlet fluxes would replace these conditions and destroy the coast-origin power law. A zero-thickness coast does not justify independently prescribing an arbitrary nonzero velocity there.
Integration of the solute and enthalpy balances yields C=C∞​ wherever q>0, and eq=−fx. Local phase equilibrium now gives
C=1−ϕC∞​​,T=−1−ϕmC∞​​,[L+1−ϕcp​mC∞​​]ϕq=fx.
(4)
For ϕ≪1, put
H=L+cp​mC∞​,K=Hf​,J=4ρ3S​.
(5)
To leading order, ϕq=Kx. The potential-energy closure implies rgϕbb′=S/4, and substituting it in the momentum equation gives
ρ(bu2)′=S−rgϕbb′=43S​,bu2=Jx.
(6)
Dividing the ice flux by this last relation gives ϕ=(K/J)u. If b∝xp, u∝xq, momentum gives p+2q=1, while the potential-energy balance gives 2p+q=1. Thus p=q=1/3. Substitution fixes the coefficients:
u=(ρrgK​x)1/3,b=u2Jx​,ϕ=JK​u.​
(7)
This is the dilute frazil polynya similarity solution. In particular, all three variables grow as x1/3, q grows as x2/3, and the solution stops being dilute when ϕ is no longer small. Density differences have been retained in buoyancy, but not in inertia or the normalized heat capacity.
To account for upward settling, one must distinguish retained suspension from crystals that leave it. A consistent depth-averaged extension has an upward exported ice-volume flux R and an entrainment flux E. If all phases retain the same mean horizontal velocity within the layer, its balances become
q′[Cq]′[eq]′ρ(qu)′​=E−R,=C∞​E,=−f−Res​,es​=cp​(T−T∞​)−L,=S−rgϕbb′−ρRu.​
(8)
The last term removes the momentum carried out by the ice; its density may be restored to ρs​ outside the Boussinesq approximation. Consequently
[(C−C∞​)q]′=C∞​R,​
(9)
so one may no longer set C=C∞​. The local equilibrium relations are unchanged, but use the new bulk concentration. The frazil export enthalpy balance also shows why a simple replacement −f↦−f−LR has the wrong sign: exported ice carries negative relative enthalpy.
A slip speed w requires a vertical particle-flux closure, for example R=wϕ for a well-mixed top boundary. A nonuniform suspension instead requires its surface concentration and buoyancy moment. The potential-energy closure must then include the gravitational energy released by rising light crystals, with scale rgwϕb, and any energy and buoyancy moment carried through the boundaries. For example, a well-mixed closure that transfers a fraction η of this released power to entrainment and neglects additional boundary potential-energy transport would replace the original work balance by
41​Su+ηrgwϕb=rgϕbub′.
(10)
The allocation η and exported potential-energy flux are extra closure information, not supplied constants of the original model. The original work balance cannot be retained unchanged while treating settling as an additional loss of suspended ice. Phase-dependent horizontal speeds would also replace qϕ and q(1−ϕ) by their separate phase fluxes.
It is important to compare total production, not merely the retained ice flux. Let
I(x)=ϕq,R∗​(x)=∫0x​R(ξ)dξ,P(x)=I(x)+R∗​(x).
(11)
Integrated solute conservation gives C(q−I)=C∞​(q+R∗​), hence
C−C∞​=q−IC∞​P​=qC∞​P​+higher dilute-order terms.
(12)
Integrating the heat balance, and neglecting products of the small concentration departure with the exported flux, gives
HP=fx,P′=f/H​
(13)
to dilute order, provided the total processed fraction P/q, not only the retained fraction ϕ, remains small. Thus upward settling reduces retained suspension, but does not increase the leading-order total production at fixed atmospheric heat loss. Beyond that order, efficient removal can reduce the subsequent sensible cooling of crystals and enhance mixing of ocean water; the usual expectation is a larger latent-heat fraction and hence greater production. Its amount, or an unconditional larger/smaller claim, needs the settling, mixing and surface-export closures just identified. At fixed f, energy conservation bounds the production by the available heat loss; it must not be inferred simply by observing fewer suspended crystals.

Use ℓ=L/cp​, ϵ=Ds​/Dl​​ and δ=Dl​/κ​, with 0<kD​<1. Equal reference densities and constant material properties are implicit in these diffusion balances. Put a(t)=2λDl​t​ and Ta​=−mCa​. The four similarity fields are
Ts​(x,t)Tl​(x,t)Cs​(x,t)Cl​(x,t)​=Ts​+(Ta​−Ts​)erfc(−δλ)erfc[−x/(2κt​)]​,=Tl​+(Ta​−Tl​)erfc(δλ)erfc[x/(2κt​)]​,=Cs​+(kD​Ca​−Cs​)erfc(−λ/ϵ)erfc[−x/(2Ds​t​)]​,=Ca​erfc(λ)erfc[x/(2Dl​t​)]​,​​x<a,x>a,x<a,x>a.​
(14)
Here the same letters Ts​,Tl​,Cs​ on the right denote their far-field values. Substitution into the heat and solute diffusion equations verifies the fields; their interface values and far-field limits verify every boundary condition. The concentration has an equilibrium jump from kD​Ca​ to Ca​, whereas temperature is continuous.
The interfacial conservation laws are
κ(Ts,x​−Tl,x​)=ℓa˙,Ds​Cs,x​−Dl​Cl,x​=(1−kD​)Ca​a˙.
(15)
For example,
Ts,x​(a)=πκt​erfc(−δλ)(Ta​−Ts​)e−δ2λ2​,Tl,x​(a)=πκt​erfc(δλ)(Tl​−Ta​)e−δ2λ2​.
(16)
Since a˙=λDl​/t​, dividing the two jump conditions by a˙ gives, for nonzero λ,
ℓ=−F(−δλ)Ta​−Ts​​−F(δλ)Tl​−Ta​​,(1−kD​)Ca​=−F(−λ/ϵ)kD​Ca​−Cs​​+F(λ)Ca​​,​
(17)
where F(z)=π​zez2erfcz. At λ=0, use the undivided flux conditions or the continuous limit, rather than divide by zero. The liquidus relation and the segregation coefficient also give the solidus TS​(C)=−mC/kD​.
For the thermal expansion, write z=δλ and use
F(z)=π​z−2z2+O(z3),F(z)1​=π​z1​+π2​+O(z).
(18)
Keeping the constant terms in these reciprocals is essential for the full first-order answer. The result is
Ta​=2Tl​+Ts​​+2π​​δλ[ℓ+π2​(Tl​−Ts​)]+O(δ3(∣ℓ∣+∣Tl​−Ts​∣)).​
(19)
The remainder has this form for bounded λ; a looser O(δ2) statement is also valid in the distinguished scaling ℓ=O(δ−1). Dropping the temperature-difference term while claiming first-order accuracy for fixed ℓ would be incorrect.
Solving the solute balance algebraically gives
Ca​=(1−kD​)+kD​/F(−λ/ϵ)−1/F(λ)Cs​/F(−λ/ϵ)​.
(20)
For fixed positive λ, F(−λ/ϵ)→−∞, and Ca​→0, with a positive exponentially small correction. For fixed negative λ, F(−λ/ϵ)→1, and
Ca​=F(λ)−1Cs​F(λ)​,λ<0.​
(21)
It falls from Cs​ as λ→−∞ to zero as λ→0−. The outer positive branch is zero. For finite ϵ the transition is rounded over ∣λ∣=O(ϵ); the exact zero-speed limit is Ca​(0)=ϵCs​/(1+kD​ϵ), not an actual concentration discontinuity.
To count speeds, take the leading distinguished thermal balance, set Σ=Tl​+Ts​ and K=π​δℓ, and combine Ta​=−mCa​ with the thermal result:
Σ=−2mCa​(λ)−Kλ.​
(22)
This is the intended leading approximation when δℓ=O(mCs​) and Tl​−Ts​=O(1). The full first-order expression instead replaces K by π​δ[ℓ+2(Tl​−Ts​)/π]. Thus the printed threshold is asymptotic, not an exact criterion for finite δ,ϵ.
For r=−λ>0, put H(r)=π​rer2erfc(−r). The small-solid-diffusivity melting concentration is Cs​H/(1+H), so
Σ(r)=Kr−2mCs​1+H(r)H(r)​,Σ′(r)=K−2mCs​[1+H(r)]2H′(r)​.
(23)
The second term decreases strictly from 2mCs​π​ to zero. One can check the strict decrease without guessing its shape. Set A(r)=π​er2erfc(−r), so A′=2rA+2, A≥π​+2r, and H=rA. Direct differentiation gives
2H′2−(1+H)H′′=2{(1+r2+2r4)A2+r(4r2−1)A+2r2−2}>0.
(24)
The bracket increases with A on r≥0. Substituting its lower bound yields a polynomial with constant coefficient π−2>0 and all other coefficients positive. Hence [H′/(1+H)2]′<0, as claimed.
If δℓ>2mCs​, then K>2mCs​π​ and Σ(r) increases from zero to infinity. The freezing branch has Σ=−Kλ<0 and is also monotone. Together these give one speed for each imposed sum within the approximation. If 0<δℓ<2mCs​, the melting branch initially decreases, has exactly one minimum and then increases to infinity. For sums between that minimum and zero there are two melting speeds plus one freezing speed: three solutions. At the lower fold there are two distinct speeds, one being a double solution. At zero the limiting outer branches join; finite solid diffusivity rounds the join into a nearby fold. Away from the fold values the other sums have one solution. At the threshold itself the initial slope vanishes; it is the transition between these cases.
For Σ>0, the solution is on the melting branch λ<0. Its thermal layers have scale κt​=Dl​t​/δ, much larger than the liquid-solute scale Dl​t​. Because the front is retreating, the solid solute layer is thinner still:
solid solute thickness∼∣a˙∣Ds​​=∣λ∣ϵ2Dl​t​​.​
(25)
This follows by expanding the large-positive-argument complementary error function ratio on the solid side: near a, its decay length is Ds​/∣a˙∣. Using simply Ds​t​ would miss the retreating-front compression of that layer.
Across this narrow layer, solid concentration drops from almost Cs​ to kD​Ca​, while temperature stays almost Ta​. At the interface Ta​=TS​(kD​Ca​). Moving slightly into the solid increases its concentration toward Cs​, lowering its solidus to −mCs​/kD​, whereas the temperature has barely changed. Since Ca​<Cs​ and kD​<1, Ta​=−mCa​>−mCs​/kD​. Thus an adjacent region can satisfy T>TS​(C): constitutional superheating. Its existence is especially clear when far-field solid is initially stable but heat diffuses across the narrow composition layer.
The phase-diagram path goes from (Cs​,Ts​) through a broad thermal rise, then nearly horizontally toward (kD​Ca​,Ta​) across the thin solid diffusion layer. At the interface it traverses the isothermal equilibrium tie line to (Ca​,Ta​). On the liquid side concentration falls to zero over the liquid-solute layer, while the remaining thermal adjustment to Tl​ takes place over the much wider heat layer. The path of the depleted solid approaches the solidus from its superheated side. That region should melt at additional sites or develop a two-phase mushy layer; the original single-boundary similarity construction is then a mathematical branch rather than a physically stable description of the entire sample.

This derives the sphere-plane dispersion force from the pairwise Van der Waals force. Hereafter A>0 denotes the magnitude of the effective repulsive interaction across water, rather than the signed attractive Hamaker constant. Its planar pressure magnitude is Π(d)=A/(6πd3).
For a nearly planar freezing front, the spherical gap is d(r)=d0​+r2/(2a). In the particle frame the incoming normal motion has speed V. Axisymmetric mass conservation gives the outward gap flux per circumference qr​=Vr/2. The lubrication flux law therefore gives

For the deformed interface let c=cosθ0​ and measure θ from the sphere's downward pole, as in the original geometry. The height below the remote melting isotherm is a(cosθ−c). Under the imposed temperature gradient, the undercooling is Ga(cosθ−c). With capillary depression neglected, the local phase-equilibrium pressure is

(37)
The factorizations are (1−c)2(c+2) and (1−c)3(c+3). They confirm positivity on the physical cap range and provide a numerically better-conditioned shallow-cap expression. Since h=a(1−c), the shallow-cap limit within d≪h is V∼A/(12πμah); it is not a limit in which the film can be identified with the flat-front gap d0​.
The local film formula diverges right at the rim θ0​. Accordingly the thin-film calculation is an outer approximation away from a small matching fringe, not a claim of a uniformly thin film at its contact with bulk water. Both integrated stress densities vanish at that rim; in the thin-film limit the fringe contributes only a higher-order correction. Surface-tension curvature, altered thermal gradients or appreciable density-change fluxes would require a different balance.
