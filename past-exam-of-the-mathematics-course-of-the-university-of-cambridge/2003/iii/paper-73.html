For the given harmonic potentials, ∇2(Φ⋅x)=2∇⋅Φ. Taking the divergence of the velocity representation therefore gives ∇⋅u=0. Taking its Laplacian gives ∇2u=−2∇(∇⋅Φ), so the Stokes equations hold with
p=−2μ∇⋅Φ+p∞​.​
(1)
This is the Papkovich–Neuber representation in the sign and normalization of this problem. The potentials used in other conventions can differ by an overall factor and sign; the velocity and pressure must be scaled together.
(a) Point force. Put the singularity at the origin, r=∣x∣, and choose Φ=F/(8πμr), χ=0. The resulting Stokeslet is
u=8πμ1​(rF​+r3(F⋅x)x​),p−p∞​=4πr3F⋅x​.​
(2)
The harmonic calculation applies away from the force. Distributionally this is the Green function for −∇p+μ∇2u+Fδ(3)(x)=0, with zero divergence.
(b) Point source. Set Φ=0, χ=−Q/(4πr). The three-dimensional point source field is
u=4πr3Qx​,p=p∞​.​
(3)
Its outward volume flux across every enclosing sphere is Q. The divergence is zero away from the source and equals Qδ(3)(x) distributionally, so the singular point represents volume injection rather than an incompressible material point.
(c) Stresslet. Let Gij​(x)=(δij​/r+xi​xj​/r3)/(8πμ) and use a symmetric trace-free stresslet tensor S with the convention ui​=−Sjk​∂k​Gij​. Differentiating the Stokeslet and using symmetry and zero trace gives
ui​=8πμr53xi​(x⋅Sx)​,p−p∞​=4πr53x⋅Sx​.​
(4)
For an axial force-dipole flow with axis n, write S=s(nn−I/3). Then the radial factor is proportional to s[3(n⋅x)2−1]. Specifying the derivative convention is essential for interpreting the signs of image strengths.
(d) Source dipole. A positive source and negative source separated by a vector ℓ have moment M=Qℓ. Their dipole limit is minus the directional derivative of the unit source field. Hence the source dipole has
u=4π1​[r53(M⋅x)x​−r3M​],χ=−4πr3M⋅x​,p=p∞​.​
(5)
It carries no net volume flux, and unlike a stresslet it is an irrotational potential flow.
Now set r=x−dn and R=x+dn, with d>0. Define the unscaled kernels K(R)=n/R+(n⋅R)R/R3, H(R)=R/R3, and let ∂n​=n⋅∇R​. The no-slip image system of a normal Stokeslet is
u=8πμF​{K(r)−K(R)+2d∂n​K(R)−2d2∂n​H(R)}.​
(6)
The reflected Stokeslet has force −Fn. With the conventions above, the remaining image strengths are
S=−2Fd(nn−I/3),M=μFd2​n.​
(7)
The isotropic part of S produces zero velocity, because ∂j​Gij​=0. Its inclusion simply puts the tensor in standard trace-free form. The pressure follows by superposition:
p−p∞​=4πF​[r3n⋅r​−R3n⋅R​+2d(R31​−R53(n⋅R)2​)].
(8)
To verify the no-slip boundary condition explicitly, write x=ρ on the plane, with ρ⋅n=0, and let b2=ρ2+d2. Then
K(r)−K(R)∂n​K(R)∂n​H(R)​=−2dρ/b3,=R(1/b3−3d2/b5),=n/b3−3dR/b5.​
(9)
The b−5 terms cancel, while the remaining image contribution is 2d(R−dn)/b3=2dρ/b3. Thus both normal and tangential velocity vanish. All image singularities lie outside the fluid, so the physical half-space contains only the prescribed force.
For the far field, expand about the wall origin. The pair of opposite forces starts with −2d∂n​K; the image stresslet cancels this entire r−2 term. The first surviving field is
u∼4πμFd2​(∂n2​K−∂n​H).
(10)
Writing z=x⋅n and now r=∣x∣, this is
u∼4πμ3Fd2​[−r52zx​−r5z2n​+r75z3x​].
(11)
Therefore the generic fixed-angle far-field velocity decays as r−3, rather than the r−1 free-space Stokeslet or an uncancelled r−2 stresslet. Along the wall it is exactly zero; at fixed height and large horizontal distance the leading tangential field instead scales as ρ−4, consistent with the angular zeros of the displayed far field.

Let n point from fluid − to fluid +, Ps​=I−nn, ∇s​=Ps​∇, and K=∇s​⋅n. A clean material interface without phase change has continuous velocity, with normal interface speed Vn​=u±​⋅n. Its surface stress is γPs​. The surface divergence of that tensor is ∇s​γ−γKn. Balancing forces on a small interfacial patch therefore gives the interfacial stress balance with variable surface tension
(σ+​−σ−​)n=γKn−∇s​γ,σ±​=−p±​I+μ±​(∇u±​+∇u±T​).​
(12)
The normal condition is n⋅(σ+​−σ−​)n=γK; the tangential condition is Ps​(σ+​−σ−​)n=−∇s​γ. On a stationary sphere with outward normal these reduce to p−​−p+​=2γ/a when viscous normal stresses vanish. This fixes the sign convention for the Marangoni stress rather than leaving the direction ambiguous.
For the bubble take negligible internal dynamic viscosity, no contaminants, and initially the standard effectively insulating gas-bubble approximation. With heat advection neglected, the exterior temperature satisfies Laplace's equation, approaches T0​+H⋅r remotely, and has zero normal heat flux at r=a. The temperature dipole around an insulating sphere gives
T=T0​+(H⋅r)(1+2r3a3​),Ts​=T0​+23​aH⋅n.
(13)
The zero-flux condition follows by differentiating the radial factor. Let γT​=(dγ/dT)T0​​. To first order in the weak gradient,
γs​=γ0​+23​aγT​H⋅n,∇s​γs​=23​γT​Ps​H.
(14)
In the laboratory frame the force-free bubble can be described by the harmonic Papkovich–Neuber representation potentials
Φ=0,χ=−2r3a3U⋅r​.
(15)
They give constant exterior pressure and the decaying source dipole field
u=−2r3a3​[U−3(U⋅r)r].
(16)
At the surface u⋅n=U⋅n, so the normal kinematic boundary condition holds for translation. In coordinates aligned with U, ur​=a3Ucosθ/r3 and uθ​=a3Usinθ/(2r3). Direct differentiation gives
σrθ​=μ(∂r​uθ​−uθ​/r+r−1∂θ​ur​)=−3μa3Usinθ/r4.
(17)
Equivalently, the surface tangential traction is (3μ/a)Ps​U. The tangential stress condition therefore requires
a3μ​Ps​U=−23​γT​Ps​H,U=−2μaγT​​H.​
(18)
For the usual negative γT​, the bubble moves toward hotter liquid. The normal condition is consistent as well: its viscous dipole is −6μU⋅n/a=3γT​H⋅n, exactly the dipolar part of 2γs​/a. The constant part fixes pgas​−p∞​=2γ0​/a. There is no Stokeslet term, hence no net applied force or drag imbalance.
The thermal assumption matters. The problem gives the exterior thermal diffusivity but no gas thermal conductivity. For an inviscid bubble with conductivity ratio K=kgas​/kliquid​, temperature and normal heat-flux continuity instead give
Tout​=T0​+(H⋅r)[1+2+K1−K​r3a3​],Ts​=T0​+2+K3​aH⋅n,
(19)
so the same stress calculation gives
U=−μ(2+K)aγT​​H.​
(20)
The insulating-bubble result uses K≪1. If instead an undisturbed surface temperature gradient is imposed, corresponding to K=1, the coefficient is 1/3 rather than 1/2. Exterior thermal diffusivity alone does not select between those thermal boundary conditions. This dependence is explicit rather than silently treating a missing gas property as a prescribed value.
The small parameters can now be stated precisely. With U∗​=a∣γT​∣∣H∣/(2μ) and ν=μ/ρ, require
Re=U∗​a/ν≪1,Pe=U∗​a/κ≪1,Ca=μU∗​/γ0​≪1,γ0​a∣γT​∣∣H∣​≪1.​
(21)
Small Reynolds number justifies Stokes flow; small Péclet number allows quasi-static heat conduction without advective distortion; small capillary number and fractional surface-tension variation support a nearly spherical interface and a weak perturbation of its base surface tension. These last two conditions are proportional for this velocity scale but describe different approximations. Linearizing γ(T) also needs a∣H∣∣γTT​/γT​∣≪1 if its derivative varies appreciably. Negligible internal viscosity and the insulating specialization require the corresponding gas-to-liquid viscosity and conductivity ratios to be small. Gravity has been excluded; if restored, its buoyancy-induced migration must be small compared with U∗​, not merely small enough to avoid deformation.
Finally, surfactants change both the constitutive surface tension and the surface stress dynamics. The surface flow driven by the thermal Marangoni effect carries insoluble surfactant toward the high-tension side, lowering its tension and producing an opposing concentration-driven gradient. When surface diffusion or exchange is slow, this tends to immobilize the interface, suppressing the clean-bubble thermocapillary migration of an insulating bubble, sometimes nearly stopping it. If the surfactant stays uniformly equilibrated, it can instead change γT​ without a large opposing gradient. Its concentration, surface diffusivity and adsorption kinetics must be supplied to compute a replacement speed; no universal numerical reduction factor follows merely from its presence.

Let z measure height above the substrate, and write μ=ρν. In the lubrication approximation, the horizontal scale ℓ is much larger than the typical height H0​. Vertical momentum balance is hydrostatic to leading order, while the horizontal balance retains the dominant vertical shear:
p=patm​+ρg(h−z),μuzz​=px​=ρghx​.
(22)
The no-slip boundary condition gives u(0)=0; negligible air viscosity and constant surface tension give uz​(h)=0. Integrating twice and then through the depth yields
u(x,z,t)=νghx​​(2z2​−hz),q=∫0h​udz=−3νg​h3hx​.
(23)
The free-surface kinematic boundary condition and incompressibility imply ht​+qx​=0. Therefore
ht​=3νg​(h3hx​)x​.​
(24)
The cross-sectional area A=∫hdx is conserved when the endpoint volume flux vanishes.
The relevant small groups follow from the same balances. Put ϵh​=H0​/ℓ and U∗​=gH03​/(3νℓ). Small ϵh​ suppresses horizontal viscous derivatives and makes vertical viscous corrections to hydrostatic pressure smaller by O(ϵh2​). The ratio of horizontal inertia to vertical viscous stress is
νℓU∗​H02​​=ϵh​ReH0​​=ϵh2​Reℓ​≪1.​
(25)
Thus a small full-length Reynolds number is sufficient but not necessary: thin-layer geometry reduces the inertial ratio. The unsteady viscous ratio H02​/(νt∗​) must likewise be small; on the spreading timescale t∗​=ℓ/U∗​ it equals the displayed group. If surface tension is retained, its pressure-gradient correction relative to gravity is
ρgℓ2γ​=Boℓ−1​≪1.​
(26)
The Bond number here uses the horizontal scale, because the capillary pressure is O(γH0​/ℓ2) whereas the hydrostatic pressure is O(ρgH0​). We also neglect air shear and evaporation and assume a Newtonian fluid with constant kinematic viscosity.
To find the similarity solution, put K=g/(3ν). Conservation of A gives h∼A/ℓ, and balancing the time derivative against the nonlinear diffusion gives ℓ5∼KA3t. A normalized ansatz is
h=(KtA2​)1/5f(ξ),ξ=(KA3t)1/5x−xc​​,∫fdξ=1.
(27)
Substitution gives (f3f′)′=−(f+ξf′)/5. For the symmetric zero-flux profile, one integration gives f3f′=−ξf/5, and on its positive support a second integration gives
f3=C−3ξ2/10.
(28)
Thus the solution is a compactly supported Barenblatt solution of the porous medium equation. Let ℓ(t) denote its half-width. In dimensional variables,
h(x,t)=[10gt9ν​{ℓ(t)2−(x−xc​)2}]+1/3​.​
(29)
Here the positive-part notation sets the height to zero outside ∣x−xc​∣<ℓ. The area normalization fixes the coefficient, not merely the exponent. With x−xc​=ℓsinθ,
A=2(10gt9ν​)1/3ℓ5/3∫0π/2​cos5/3θdθ.
(30)
Writing the remaining integral as I, the final widths are
ℓ(t)=(36νI35gA3t​)1/5,full drop width=2(36νI35gA3t​)1/5.​
(31)
The height falls like t−1/5 and the width grows like t1/5. The volume flux tends to zero at the front, even though the profile slope diverges; this gives a valid compactly supported weak solution of the reduced equation. The diverging edge slope means the lubrication approximation is not uniform at a microscopic contact line. Surface-tension or contact-line physics supplies an inner regularization without changing the gravity-dominated bulk similarity law in its validity regime. This is an exact source-type solution for t>0; an arbitrary finite initial drop approaches an appropriate late-time profile rather than being exactly represented by this singular initial profile.

Put τ=m/ζ. The causal Green function of the displacement equation is G(t)=ζ−1(1−e−t/τ) for t>0 and zero for t<0. Its derivative has the jump needed to give mG′′+ζG′=δ(t). For the specified zero position and velocity,
x(t)=ζ1​∫0t​[1−e−(t−s)/τ]f(s)ds,x˙(t)=m1​∫0t​e−(t−s)/τf(s)ds.​
(32)
For Gaussian white noise these are stochastic convolutions; their first two moments follow from the stated covariance even without assuming Gaussian higher moments. In particular ⟨x⟩=⟨x˙⟩=0, and
⟨x˙(t)2⟩=m2F​∫0t​e−2(t−s)/τds=2mζF​(1−e−2t/τ).
(33)
The required equipartition theorem limit therefore fixes the fluctuation-dissipation relation for a Langevin particle:
F=2ζkB​T.​
(34)
Here kB​ denotes the printed Boltzmann constant k. Set D=kB​T/ζ, the Stokes–Einstein relation. Squaring the displacement kernel gives
⟨x(t)2⟩=2D[t−2τ(1−e−t/τ)+2τ​(1−e−2t/τ)].​
(35)
Consequently limt→∞​⟨x⟩=0 and limt→∞​⟨x2⟩=+∞, with ⟨x2⟩=2Dt−3Dτ+o(1) for positive temperature. There is no finite equilibrium variance of position in an unconfined fluid. The inertial Brownian displacement with zero initial velocity has short-time variance 2Dt3/(3τ2)+O(t4), rather than the ballistic law associated with an initially thermalized velocity.
For constant D, the diffusion equation has fundamental density P(x,t)=(4πDt)−1/2e−x2/(4Dt), whose mean is zero and variance is 2Dt. It describes the large-time, overdamped limit of the Langevin dynamics. Under the usual Gaussian-force assumption, the finite-time position density is Gaussian with the variance above and obeys a time-dependent diffusion equation with coefficient D(1−e−t/τ)2. It does not obey the constant-D equation through the initial inertial transient. The given covariance alone determines the moments, not a Gaussian law; the density statement uses the standard Gaussian thermal-noise model.
Now write ζ(x)=ζ0​+εζ0′​x and x=x0​+εx1​+O(ε2), with τ0​=m/ζ0​ and D0​=kB​T/ζ0​. For a fixed realization of the additive force, the first-order equation is
mx¨1​+ζ0​x˙1​=−ζ0′​x0​x˙0​.
(36)
If the force amplitude is instead adjusted to local thermal equilibrium, its first-order noise term has zero mean in the inertial Itô formulation and does not change the following first-order mean calculation. The mixed unperturbed moment is
⟨x0​x˙0​⟩=21​dtd​⟨x02​⟩=D0​(1−e−t/τ0​)2.
(37)
Let M(t)=⟨x1​(t)⟩. Averaging and solving the scalar forced relaxation equation, with M(0)=M′(0)=0, gives
M′(t)M(t)​=−ζ0​ζ0′​D0​​[1−e−2t/τ0​−τ0​2t​e−t/τ0​],=−ζ0​ζ0′​D0​​[t−2τ0​​(1−e−2t/τ0​)−2τ0​{1−(1+t/τ0​)e−t/τ0​}].​
(38)
Thus the long-time first-order coefficient and local mean drift are
M(t)∼−ζ02​kB​Tζ0′​​t,Vdrift​=−εζ02​kB​Tζ0′​​.​
(39)
Literally limM=−∞ for ζ0′​>0, +∞ for ζ0′​<0, and zero for ζ0′​=0; the useful finite quantity is limM′. Because the viscosity gradient is only a local expansion, the physical drift result applies in the intermediate window τ0​≪t≪Lζ2​/D0​, where Lζ​=ζ0​/(ε∣ζ0′​∣). Extrapolating the linear drag law to arbitrarily large displacements could make the drag negative and would invalidate the model.
The isothermal diffusion with position-dependent drag interpretation uses locally matched thermal noise and D(x)=kB​T/ζ(x). Integrating the printed Fickian diffusion equation against x, with vanishing boundary fluxes, gives
dtd​⟨x⟩=−∫DPx​dx=∫D′(x)Pdx.
(40)
Since D′(x)=−εkB​Tζ0′​/ζ02​+O(ε2), this reproduces the same drift after the inertial transient. Equivalently the overdamped Itô diffusion has drift D′ and noise amplitude 2D​. Omitting that drift would give Pt​=(DP)xx​, which is a different Fokker-Planck equation and not the stated Fickian equation.
Noise conventions cannot be mixed silently. If the force covariance is held at the spatially constant value F0​=2ζ0​kB​T while drag varies, the local overdamped diffusion coefficient is instead D=F0​/(2ζ2) and its Itô drift is D′/2. Its density equation is Pt​=∂x​[DPx​+(D′/2)P], not the isothermal Fickian equation. This still gives the same displayed first-order mean drift at x=0, but has different spatial diffusion and equilibrium properties. The stated Fickian interpretation therefore requires local thermal-noise matching.
Physically, thermally driven excursions travel farther on the lower-drag side. Their displacement is correlated with velocity, so averaging the position-dependent friction leaves a net drift toward greater diffusivity, without an applied force. This is not a violation of equilibrium: in the isothermal Fickian model the drift and spatially varying noise cancel in the probability flux for a uniform density. It is a local diffusion-induced shift of the centroid of an initially localized particle distribution.
