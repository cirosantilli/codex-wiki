Fluid-loaded plate. Use complex amplitudes with time dependence e−iωt and take the velocity potential in the fluid above the elastic plate to be Φ. Let ρ0​ and c0​ be the undisturbed mass density and speed of sound. The linearized Euler equations give p=iρ0​ωΦ; the kinematic boundary condition is Φy​(x,0)=−iωη(x). We model loading from one fluid half-space, with no fluid loading on the other face. Loading by identical fluids on both faces instead doubles the fluid term below.
Define the Fourier transform and its inverse by
f​(k)=∫R​f(x)e−ikxdx,f(x)=2π1​∫C​f​(k)eikxdk.
(1)
The Sommerfeld radiation condition selects Φ(k,y)=Φ(k,0)e−γy, with γ2=k2−k02​ and k0​=ω/c0​. Here and in the diffraction calculation below, the outgoing square root is specified by first taking Imω>0. Draw a vertical cut upwards from +k0​ and another downwards from −k0​, and put
γ=γu​γl​,γu​=k+k0​​,−2π​<arg(k+k0​)<23π​,γl​=k−k0​​,−23π​<arg(k−k0​)<2π​.
(2)
Thus γu​ is analytic in the upper half-plane and γl​ in the lower half-plane. In the real-frequency limit,
γ=k2−k02​​>0(∣k∣>k0​),γ=−ik02​−k2​(∣k∣<k0​).
(3)
The Fourier transform contour C follows the real axis with the limiting-absorption pole prescriptions. In particular, an outgoing propagating component has positive vertical wavenumber.
The kinematic boundary condition gives Φ(0)=iωη​/γ, so p​(0)=−ρ0​ω2η​/γ. Eliminating the fluid pressure from the bending balance gives the line-forced fluid-loaded bending plate kernel
K(k)=βk4−mω2−γρ0​ω2​,D(k)=γK(k).
(4)
For harmonic line force amplitude F0​ at the origin, the plate displacement and fluid velocity potential are therefore
η(x)=2πF0​​∫C​D(k)γ(k)eikx​dk​,Φ(x,y)=2πiωF0​​∫C​D(k)eikx−γy​dk​.
(5)
An arbitrary localized loading replaces F0​ by its Fourier transform. This representation both calculates the response and identifies its different wave components through poles and branch points.
For an observer x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ, with 0<θ<π, the method of steepest descent uses the saddle point ks​=k0​cosθ. At fixed angle away from grazing, the acoustic far field is
Φrad​∼D(ks​)iωF0​sinθ​2πrk0​​​eik0​r−iπ/4,prad​=iρ0​ωΦrad​.
(6)
The r−1/2 spreading is that of cylindrical acoustic waves. The phase velocity parallel to the plate is ω/∣k∣: components with ∣k∣<k0​ are supersonic in that sense and radiate, whereas those with ∣k∣>k0​ are subsonic and exponentially decay normally to the plate. The latter have reactive fluid loading and zero mean normal acoustic energy flux.
There is also a real guided root κ>k0​. Indeed, K(k0​+)=−∞, K(+∞)=+∞, and
K′(k)=4βk3+γ3ρ0​ω2k​>0(k>k0​).
(7)
The residue theorem then gives an outgoing displacement contribution iF0​eiκ∣x∣/K′(κ). It transports energy along the plate while its fluid field decays as e−γ(κ)y. Other zeros on continued sheets can describe leaky or resonant waves; branch point contributions describe near and grazing fields. A fixed-angle acoustic far field expansion is not uniform near the plate, where these components can matter.
For a propagating spectral component, write ℓ=(k02​−k2)1/2. Its upward normal velocity is iℓΦ and its pressure is iρ0​ωΦ. Applying Parseval's identity to the time-averaged acoustic energy flux gives the upward power per unit length of the forcing line:
Py​=4πρ0​ω3∣F0​∣2​∫−k0​k0​​∣D(k)∣2k02​−k2​​dk=4πρ0​ω3k02​∣F0​∣2​∫0π​∣D(k0​cosθ)∣2sin2θ​dθ​.
(8)
Equivalently, the integrand is ∣η​∣2/ℓ with prefactor ρ0​ω3/(4π). Integrating the radial acoustic intensity of the far field over a semicircle gives the same expression. The input power ωIm(F0∗​η(0))/2 also feeds guided waves along the plate, so it must not be identified with Py​ alone.
Rigid half-plane diffraction. Suppress the time factor and assume 0<θ0​<π. Write a=k0​cosθ0​, b=k0​sinθ0​, and denote the incident spatial field by I=e−iax−iby. The Helmholtz equation, Sommerfeld radiation condition, and square-root branches are as above. Across the open positive axis the scattered field and its normal derivative are continuous. Across the rigid negative axis its normal derivative is also continuous, while its potential can jump. Let J(x) be this jump, supported on x<0. The transformed scattered field is
ϕ​(k,y)=21​sgn(y)Ju​(k)e−γ∣y∣.
(9)
The jump transform is analytic in the upper half-plane. The absence of an even scattered component follows from derivative continuity and the outgoing condition. Its normal derivative on either face is −γJu​/2.
On the negative axis this derivative must cancel −ibe−iax. Since the Half-range Fourier transform of H(−x)e−iax is i/(k+a), the Wiener-Hopf equation has the form
γu​γl​Ju​=k+a2b​+Gl​(k),
(10)
where Gl​ is the transform of an unknown function supported on x>0 and analytic in the lower half-plane. The incident pole k=−a lies below the Fourier contour. Divide by γl​ and perform pole subtraction in a Wiener-Hopf equation:
(k+a)γl​(k)2b​=(k+a)γl​(−a)2b​+k+a2b​(γl​(k)1​−γl​(−a)1​).
(11)
The first term is upper analytic and the second lower analytic, since the latter's apparent pole cancels. Equality of the two continued sides gives an entire function. The finite-energy edge condition, with J(x)=O(−x​), and the decay of the transforms at infinity force that function to vanish by Liouville's theorem. Consequently
Ju​(k)=γl​(−a)(k+a)γu​(k)2b​,ϕ(x,y)=2πγl​(−a)sgn(y)b​∫C​(k+a)γu​(k)eikx−γ∣y∣​dk​.
(12)
This is the Wiener-Hopf solution of rigid half-plane diffraction with the present transform and time conventions.
Deforming to a steepest descent contour gives a residue from the incident pole precisely when x+∣y∣cotθ0​<0. At that pole γ(−a)=−ib; its residue produces
ϕGO​=sgn(y)e−iax+ib∣y∣H(−x−∣y∣cotθ0​).
(13)
Thus the total geometrical optics field is I plus a reflected wave in the upper reflection region; in the lower shadow region the scattered residue cancels I, and outside that shadow the direct incident wave remains. This gives both the amplitude and the spatial support of the geometrical optics fields rather than merely naming a reflected wave.
For x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ, −π<θ<π, the saddle contribution uses γl​(−a)=−i2k0​​cos(θ0​/2) and γu​(ks​)=2k0​​cos(θ/2). The diffracted wave is
ϕd​∼2πk0​r​eik0​r+iπ/4​cosθ+cosθ0​2sin(θ/2)sin(θ0​/2)​=22πk0​r​eik0​r+iπ/4​[sec2θ+θ0​​−sec2θ−θ0​​]​.
(14)
The total field is I+ϕGO​+ϕd​ to this order. This far-field expression applies away from the shadow/reflection boundaries ∣θ∣=π−θ0​. There the pole and saddle coalesce, and a uniform Fresnel integral approximation replaces the divergent separate terms by a smooth transition. The exact contour integral remains well defined.

Write the perturbation Stokes streamfunction as ψ=ϕ(r)eikz−iωt, with ω=kc and nonzero k. Its radial and axial velocity amplitudes are u=−ikϕ/r and w=ϕ′/r. These automatically obey incompressibility. Linearizing the radial and axial Euler equations, with pressure amplitude P, gives
ik(U−c)u=−P′/ρ0​,ik(U−c)w+U′u=−ikP/ρ0​.
(15)
Thus
P/ρ0​=−(U−c)ϕ′/r+U′ϕ/r,P′/ρ0​=−k2(U−c)ϕ/r.
(16)
Differentiating the first expression, the terms involving U′ϕ′ cancel. Equating the two expressions for P′ proves the axisymmetric inviscid pipe stability equation:
(U−c)[r(rϕ′​)′−k2ϕ]−r(rU′​)′ϕ=0​.
(17)
At the axis, finite smooth axisymmetric velocity requires ϕ=O(r2), after choosing the irrelevant constant in the streamfunction. At the wall, impermeability gives ϕ(a)=0. There is no tangential no-slip condition in this inviscid problem. A decoupled azimuthal disturbance satisfies ik(U−c)uθ​=0 and does not introduce a growing discrete mode when U=c.
For real k, suppose c=cr​+ici​ with ci​=0, so division by U−c is legitimate. Multiply the ordinary differential equation by ϕ∗/[r(U−c)], integrate, and apply integration by parts. The endpoint terms vanish by the preceding regularity and wall conditions, leaving
∫0a​r∣ϕ′∣2+k2∣ϕ∣2​dr+∫0a​U−c(U′/r)′∣ϕ∣2​dr=0.
(18)
Taking its imaginary part yields
ci​∫0a​∣U−c∣2(U′/r)′∣ϕ∣2​dr=0.
(19)
A growing temporal mode therefore requires a sign change of (U′/r)′, rather than a sign change of U′′ alone. This is the cylindrical counterpart of Rayleigh's inflection-point theorem. If this derivative vanishes identically, the positive first integral rules out a nontrivial growing real-wavenumber mode as well. Isolated zeros without a sign change do not make the second integral vanish for a nonzero regular mode.
For the parabolic Poiseuille flow, U′/r=−2U0​/a2 is constant. Under the specified exclusion of critical levels, division gives
ϕ′′−rϕ′​−k2ϕ=0.
(20)
Setting ϕ=rf, or equivalently choosing n=1 in r−nϕ, turns this into
r2f′′+rf′−(k2r2+1)f=0.
(21)
It is the Modified Bessel differential equation of order one. Hence ϕ=r[AI1​(kr)+BK1​(kr)]. Since rK1​(kr) tends to a nonzero constant at the axis, it gives a singular radial velocity and must be excluded. The admissible mode shape is therefore
ϕ=ArI1​(kr),I1​(ka)=0​.
(22)
For k=0, direct solution gives ϕ=Cr2 after axis regularity, and the wall condition again forces the trivial solution. For nonzero k, the nonzero roots of I1​ are imaginary. Writing j1,l​>0 for the zeros of the ordinary Bessel function J1​, the evanescent potential modes of inviscid Poiseuille flow are
k=±ij1,l​/a,ϕ∝rJ1​(j1,l​r/a)​.
(23)
Their perturbation vorticity vanishes: ∂z​u−∂r​w=k2ϕ/r−(ϕ′/r)′=0. These modes grow exponentially in one axial direction and decay in the other. They can describe end-forced or spatially localized potential fields, but neither sign gives a bounded normal mode on a whole infinite pipe. Moreover, the reduced radial equation contains no c: it supplies no temporal eigenvalue condition. An arbitrary imaginary part assigned to ω=kc here is consequently not evidence for a physical temporal instability.
The real-wavenumber calculation does exclude growing discrete axisymmetric modes of this inviscid profile. It does not establish every form of stability: singular neutral disturbances with c in the range of U, transient dynamics, non-axisymmetric disturbances, and finite-amplitude transition require separate treatment. The exclusion U=c specifically removes the inviscid continuous spectrum.
With viscosity, linearize the Navier-Stokes equations instead and eliminate pressure to obtain a fourth-order cylindrical counterpart of the Orr-Sommerfeld equation. Impose both ϕ(a)=0 and ϕ′(a)=0, with appropriate regularity at the axis. The resulting eigenvalue problem involves the Reynolds number and determines complex frequencies, rather than leaving c arbitrary. Viscosity supplies diffusion, no-slip boundary layers and critical-layer regularization, so the inviscid sign argument alone no longer decides its spectrum. That observation does not imply that viscous pipe flow must have a growing linear eigenmode; finite-amplitude pipe transition is a different question.

The real-wavenumber dispersion relation has the explicit branch
ω(k)=21​(1−e−2ak),ω′(k)=ae−2ak>0(k∈R).
(24)
Its frequencies are real. A real-axis Fourier transform propagator multiplies every component by e−iω(k)t, of modulus one, so a square-integrable localized disturbance has constant L2 norm. For sufficiently smooth localized data with an integrable Fourier transform, its pointwise amplitude also has a time-independent upper bound. In particular, there is no exponential growth along a fixed or moving ray.
The Briggs-Bers criterion makes the same conclusion while enforcing causality. Start the temporal inversion line at Imω=σ>0 and the spatial inversion contour on the real k axis. The spatial roots can be followed explicitly:
kj​(ω)=−2a1​Log(1−2ω)+aiπj​,j∈Z.
(25)
Choose the principal logarithm along this upper temporal half-plane. Since Im(1−2ω)<0, the j=0 root lies above the real k axis; the j=−1 root lies below it. Successive roots remain separated by iπ/a. As σ is lowered, k0​ approaches the real axis when ω<1/2 is real. The spatial contour is then indented beneath that root, as required by continuation from positive σ. No lower root meets it: their separation stays nonzero.
Algebraically,
Dk​=−2ae−2ak=0
(26)
for every finite complex k, so there is no finite spatial pinch point. The logarithmic singularity at ω=1/2 sends roots to Rek=+∞; it is on the real-frequency boundary, not a positive-growth pinch in the upper temporal half-plane. The continuum of neutral poles is the real curve ω<1/2 in the ω plane. It is reached as a causal boundary value, rather than producing an upper-half-plane singularity.
The figure follows neighboring spatial roots while lowering horizontal temporal contours. It shows why merely finding roots in both spatial half-planes does not establish an instability: those roots never collide across the inversion contour.
These modes have neither absolute wave-packet instability nor convective wave-packet instability; they are temporally neutral. Their positive real group velocity transports wave packets downstream. Nonreal spatial roots describe spatial continuation and evanescence, not temporal amplification. The exponential growth criteria concern the causal impulse response, not an unrestricted choice of a complex root.

Introduce the slow time T=ϵt and expand y=y0​(t,T)+ϵy1​(t,T)+⋯. At leading order write
y0​=R(T)cosψ,ψ=t+φ(T),R(0)=1,φ(0)=0.
(60)
The first-order equation is
(∂t2​+1)y1​=−2∂t​∂T​y0​−(∂t​y0​)n=2RT​sinψ+2RφT​cosψ−(−1)nRnsinnψ.
(61)
Its resonant sine and cosine components must vanish by the solvability condition in the method of multiple scales. Let angle brackets denote a full-period average. The cosine projection of the nonlinear term is zero, since ⟨sinnψcosψ⟩=0. The sine projection gives
φT​=0,RT​=(−1)nRn⟨sinn+1ψ⟩​.
(62)
This is the averaged oscillator damping by a power of velocity.
If n is odd, define
Cn​=⟨sinn+1ψ⟩=2n+11​((n+1)/2n+1​)>0.
(63)
Then RT​=−Cn​Rn, giving
y(t)=e−ϵt/2cost+O(ϵ)(n=1)​,
(64)
y(t)=[1+(n−1)Cn​ϵt]−1/(n−1)cost+O(ϵ)(n>1 odd)​.
(65)
For every fixed n, these leading multiple-scale expansions are uniform on bounded slow-time intervals 0≤ϵt≤T0​. The unforced phase remains t to this order. A bounded first-order correction supplies the small adjustment to the initial derivative and initial displacement; it does not change this leading result.
If n is even, the average of the odd power sinn+1ψ vanishes. Thus RT​=0 and
y(t)=cost+O(ϵ)(n even, t=O(ϵ−1))​.
(66)
The first-order forcing has only a constant and even harmonics, so it produces no fundamental resonance. Its bounded particular solution can be supplemented by homogeneous sine and cosine terms to satisfy the initial conditions. There can be an O(ϵ2) frequency correction, but its phase accumulation is only O(ϵ) on the present time scale. In contrast to the odd case, the even-power force is invariant under velocity reversal and the equation is reversible; calling it positive damping for both signs of velocity would be wrong. The mechanical energy identity makes the distinction clear:
dtd​2(y′)2+y2​=−ϵ(y′)n+1.
(67)
This is nonpositive for odd n and changes sign for even n; its cycle average reproduces the amplitude equation above.
For large odd n, the central binomial coefficient gives
Cn​∼πn2​​,Ln​=(n−1)Cn​∼π2n​​,R=exp[−n−1log(1+Ln​ϵt)​].
(68)
This form reveals several time regimes. For Ln​ϵt≪1, R=1−Cn​ϵt+⋯; at t=O(1/(ϵn​)), the amplitude change is only O(1/n). For Ln​ϵt≫1 but log(Ln​ϵt)≪n, the leading change is R≃1−log(Ln​ϵt)/n. In particular, at t=O(1/ϵ) it is only O(logn/n), despite the long elapsed time. High powers damp mainly near velocity maxima, and even a small amplitude reduction strongly suppresses subsequent damping.
An order-one amplitude reduction in the averaged law requires log(Ln​ϵt)=O(n). Formally, t=e(n−1)σ/(ϵLn​) gives R∼e−σ, and for fixed odd n>1 its late envelope is an algebraic power t−1/(n−1). These exponential-in-n time scales are predictions of extending the averaged law; the fixed-n, bounded-ϵt multiple-scale error estimate alone does not establish uniform accuracy that far. A joint large-n, small-ϵ approximation also needs control of the increasingly sharp nonlinear peaks, rather than assuming fixed-n error constants are uniform. Taking n fixed first and then examining the large-n envelope avoids that unsupported interchange of limits.
