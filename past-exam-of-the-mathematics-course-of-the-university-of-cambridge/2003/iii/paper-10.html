Here “subspace” is understood to mean a closed closed linear subspace. Closedness matters: the finitely supported sequences c00​ are an infinite-dimensional algebraic subspace of ℓp, but contain no infinite-dimensional complete subspace. Indeed, any such subspace would be the countable union of its intersections with the finite-dimensional coordinate spaces, contrary to the Baire category theorem. We prove the assertion with the intended closedness convention, for 1≤p<∞.
Write E=ℓp, the l-p sequence space. Choose positive numbers δn​ with ∑n​2δn​<1. Starting with m0​=0, choose xn​∈X with ∥xn​∥p​=1 and its first mn−1​ coordinates zero. This is possible because the kernel of finitely many coordinate linear functionals on an infinite-dimensional vector space is still infinite-dimensional. Choose mn​>mn−1​ so that the part of xn​ after coordinate mn​ has norm below δn​. Let vn​ be its restriction to In​=(mn−1​,mn​], and put un​=vn​/∥vn​∥p​. The triangle inequality gives
∥xn​−un​∥p​≤∥xn​−vn​∥p​+∣1−∥vn​∥p​∣<2δn​.
(1)
The vectors un​ form a normalized block basic sequence. Their supports are disjoint, so
∥∑n​an​un​∥pp​=∑n​∣an​∣p.
(2)
Consequently U=span​{un​} is isometric to the l-p sequence space.
For each block choose a norm-one linear functional ϕn​ supported on In​ with ϕn​(un​)=1. The Hahn-Banach theorem supplies such a functional, including when p=1. Define
Qz=∑n​ϕn​(z)un​.
(3)
The series converges in the l-p sequence space, since
∥Qz∥pp​=∑n​∣ϕn​(z)∣p≤∑n​∥z∣In​​∥pp​≤∥z∥pp​.
(4)
Also Qun​=un​, so Q is a bounded projection onto U. Now define the bounded linear operator
A=I+∑n​ϕn​(⋅)(xn​−un​).
(5)
This series converges in operator norm and ∥A−I∥≤∑n​2δn​<1. The Neumann series therefore makes A invertible. Disjoint support gives Aun​=xn​. Thus Y=AU=span​{xn​} lies in the closed subspace X, is isomorphic to the l-p sequence space, and is the range of the bounded projection AQA−1 on the whole space E. This proves the required copy of ℓp is complemented in the ambient ℓp, not just in X.
Now let Z be an infinite-dimensional complemented subspace of E. The construction supplies Y⊂Z with Y≅E and a bounded projection R:E→Y. Its restriction to Z gives the topological direct sum
Z=Y⊕W,W=Z∩kerR.
(6)
If P:E→Z is a bounded projection, then (I−R)P is a bounded projection onto W: its range lies in W, and it fixes every element of W. Hence W is a complemented subspace of E, so E≅W⊕V for some closed V.
We give the absorption argument in the Pełczyński decomposition method explicitly. Reindexing coordinates by a bijection between N2 and N yields E≅(⨁n≥1​E)p​. Applying the fixed Banach space isomorphism E≅W⊕V in every coordinate gives
E⊕W≅(⨁n≥1​(W⊕V))p​⊕W≅(⨁n≥1​W)p​⊕(⨁n≥1​V)p​⊕W≅E.
(7)
The extra W is absorbed by shifting the countably many W summands. The maps are bounded with bounded inverses because the same two-space decomposition is used uniformly in each summand; we may equip each finite direct sum with its equivalent p-sum norm. Since Z≅E⊕W, Z≅ℓp.

We use independent symmetric Rademacher random variables εj​∈{−1,1}, the intended meaning of the Bernoulli variables in this context. Without this convention the assertion need not hold: for one 0/1 Bernoulli variable of success probability t<1/2 and one nonzero vector, the proposed inequality would be t​≤2​t, which is false.

For Banach spaces E,F and 1≤p<∞, define the weak p-norm of a finite family by
wp​(x1​,…,xm​)=supϕ∈BE∗​​(∑i=1m​∣ϕ(xi​)∣p)1/p.
(15)
A bounded linear operator T:E→F is an absolutely p-summing operator if (∑i​∥Txi​∥p)1/p≤Cwp​(x1​,…,xm​) for every finite family. The least constant is πp​(T).
The Pietsch factorization theorem states that such an operator admits a probability measure μ on K=BE∗​ with its weak-star topology such that
∥Tx∥≤πp​(T)(∫K​∣ϕ(x)∣pdμ(ϕ))1/p(x∈E).
(16)
Equivalently, let i:E→C(K) be the evaluation embedding i(x)(ϕ)=ϕ(x) and jp​:C(K)→Lp(μ) the natural map. There is a bounded linear operator V:H→F, where H=jp​i(E)​⊂Lp(μ), such that T=Vjp​i and ∥V∥≤πp​(T). Conversely, any such factorization makes T an absolutely p-summing operator, with πp​(T)≤∥V∥. The final operator is required on this closed subspace H; no extension to all of Lp(μ) is assumed.
Here is the existence proof. If T=0 the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise write C=πp​(T)>0. The Banach-Alaoglu theorem makes K compact, and ϕ↦∣ϕ(x)∣p is continuous for each x∈E. For finitely many vectors x1​,…,xm​, put
v(ϕ)=(∣ϕ(xi​)∣p)i=1m​,a=(C−p∥Txi​∥p)i=1m​.
(17)
We claim the compact convex hull of v(K) meets a+[0,∞)m. If it did not, finite-dimensional Hahn-Banach separation theorem would give weights λi​≥0 with
supϕ∈K​∑i​λi​∣ϕ(xi​)∣p<C−p∑i​λi​∥Txi​∥p.
(18)
The weights are nonnegative because the second convex set is unbounded in every positive coordinate direction. This strict inequality contradicts the defining summing inequality applied to λi1/p​xi​. Thus a finite convex combination of the vectors v(ϕ) lies coordinatewise above a. Equivalently, a finitely supported probability measure satisfies all these finitely many domination constraints.
By the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem, probability measures on compact K are the positive norm-one functionals on C(K) taking the constant function to one. They form a compact set in the weak-star topology, by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem. Each domination constraint ∫∣ϕ(x)∣pdμ≥C−p∥Tx∥p is closed. The finite feasibility just proved gives the finite intersection property, hence one measure μ satisfies every constraint.
Define V(jp​i(x))=Tx. The domination inequality makes this well-defined and bounded by C, and completeness of F extends it to H. Conversely, any factorization satisfies
∑i​∥Txi​∥p≤∥V∥p∫K​∑i​∣ϕ(xi​)∣pdμ(ϕ)≤∥V∥pwp​(x1​,…,xm​)p.
(19)
This proves both directions of the Pietsch factorization theorem, including the optimal factorization norm.
For the multiplication operator, let f∈Lp(0,1) and take g1​,…,gm​∈C([0,1]). Point evaluations are norm-one linear functionals on C([0,1]), so
∑i​∥fgi​∥pp​=∫01​∣f(t)∣p∑i​∣gi​(t)∣pdt≤∥f∥pp​supt∈[0,1]​∑i​∣gi​(t)∣p≤∥f∥pp​wp​(g1​,…,gm​)p.
(20)
Thus Mf​ is an absolutely p-summing operator and πp​(Mf​)≤∥f∥p​. Taking the single function g=1 gives the reverse inequality, so
πp​(Mf​)=∥Mf​∥=∥f∥p​.​
(21)
For f=0, the explicit domination measure on [0,1] is ∣f(t)∣pdt/∥f∥pp​, pushed into K by t↦δt​. For f=0 everything is zero; no essential boundedness of f is needed.

The real Grothendieck inequality asserts that a universal constant KG​ satisfies
​∑i,j​aij​⟨ui​,vj​⟩​≤KG​maxεi​,δj​∈{−1,1}​​∑i,j​aij​εi​δj​​
(22)
for every finite real scalar matrix and vectors of norm at most one in a real Hilbert space. For complex scalars the corresponding scalar supremum is over phases of modulus at most one; a universal complex constant also exists. We prove both versions, without needing the optimal constant.
First, for unit vectors u,v and a standard Gaussian vector G on their finite-dimensional span,
E[sgn⟨G,u⟩sgn⟨G,v⟩]=π2​arcsin⟨u,v⟩.
(23)
To see this, rotational symmetry makes the angle of the Gaussian vector uniform in the plane spanned by u,v. The two signs differ with probability arccos⟨u,v⟩/π. Subtracting twice this probability from one gives the identity; collinear cases follow directly. Zero Gaussian coordinates have probability zero.
Put c=log(1+2​), so sinhc=1 and 0<c<π/2. In the Hilbert direct sum of odd tensor powers, formed with the Hilbert tensor product inner product, define
U(u)=⨁k≥0​(2k+1)!c2k+1​​u⊗(2k+1),V(v)=⨁k≥0​(−1)k(2k+1)!c2k+1​​v⊗(2k+1).
(24)
The Hilbert tensor product satisfies ⟨u⊗m,v⊗m⟩=⟨u,v⟩m. For unit u,v, both transformed vectors have squared norm ∑k≥0​c2k+1/(2k+1)!=sinhc=1, and the power series for sine gives
⟨U(u),V(v)⟩=sin(c⟨u,v⟩).
(25)
For any finite collection of these transformed vectors, use a standard Gaussian vector on their finite-dimensional span. The sign identity and ∣c⟨u,v⟩∣<π/2 give
E[sgn⟨G,U(u)⟩sgn⟨G,V(v)⟩]=π2c​⟨u,v⟩.
(26)
Consequently, writing M for the scalar sign supremum,
​∑i,j​aij​⟨ui​,vj​⟩​=2cπ​​E∑i,j​aij​sgn⟨G,U(ui​)⟩sgn⟨G,V(vj​)⟩​≤2cπ​M.
(27)
Vectors of norm below one are padded to unit vectors in additional orthogonal directions, using separate directions for the two families so their cross inner products remain unchanged. This proves the real Grothendieck inequality with KR​=π/(2log(1+2​)).
For the complex Grothendieck inequality, rotate the total scalar sum to have nonnegative real part. Its real part is the sum of the two real bilinear expressions involving Reaij​Re⟨ui​,vj​⟩ and −Imaij​Im⟨ui​,vj​⟩. The real inner products are realized in the underlying real Hilbert space, rotating one family by i for the imaginary part. Each real coefficient matrix has real sign supremum at most the phase supremum of the original complex matrix. Applying the real inequality twice gives the valid constant KC​=2KR​. In what follows let KG​ denote the appropriate real or complex constant.
Now let T:L1(0,1)→H be a bounded linear operator into a Hilbert space, and write M=∥T∥. If M=0 there is nothing to prove. Approximate a finite family of functions by simple functions on a common disjoint measurable partition. For such a family, write fj​=∑k​ajk​1Ak​​ and mk​=∣Ak​∣>0. The vectors
hk​=T(1Ak​​/mk​)
(28)
have norm at most M. Choose unit vectors uj​ with ⟨Tfj​,uj​⟩=∥Tfj​∥; zero images contribute zero and can be assigned any unit vector. With inner products linear in their first argument, the Grothendieck inequality gives
∑j​∥Tfj​∥=M∑j,k​mk​ajk​⟨hk​/M,uj​⟩≤KG​Msup∣αj​∣≤1,∣βk​∣≤1​​∑j,k​mk​ajk​αj​βk​​=KG​Msup∣αj​∣≤1​​∑j​αj​fj​​1​.
(29)
Over the real field, phases here are signs. For any Banach space E, operator norm duality gives
sup∣αj​∣≤1​​∑j​αj​fj​​E​=supϕ∈BE∗​​∑j​∣ϕ(fj​)∣=w1​(f1​,…,fm​).
(30)
Indeed, interchange the two suprema and choose each phase to align the scalar values of the functional. Thus the required summing inequality holds for simple functions. Approximation in the Lp space L1 passes it to arbitrary finite families, since both sides vary continuously under finite sums of L1 errors. We obtain
π1​(T)≤KG​∥T∥;T:L1(0,1)→H is absolutely summing.​

Finally let R be the closed span of the Rademacher functions in L1(0,1). Independence, the scalar second moment, and Q2 give, for each finite scalar family,
2​1​(∑j​∣aj​∣2)1/2≤​∑j​aj​rj​​1​≤(∑j​∣aj​∣2)1/2.

∑j=1n​∥ej​∥2​=n,w1​(e1​,…,en​)=sup∥h∥2​≤1​∑j=1n​∣hj​∣=n​.
(33)
