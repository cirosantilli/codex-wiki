Here a monochromatic set must mean an infinite homogeneous set for a colouring: every singleton is a finite monochromatic set. The requested phenomenon holds for every finite n≥2. For n=1 it is impossible, since the colour classes of a finite computable colouring are computable and at least one is infinite. We treat this necessary qualification explicitly.
We construct a finite-injury computable colouring of pairs with no infinite computably enumerable homogeneous set, which is stronger than the requested absence of a recursive one. Enumerate the computably enumerable sets as W0​,W1​,…, with computable finite approximations We,s​. Requirement e seeks distinct markers ae​,be​∈We​. At stage s, consider indices e≤s not currently assigned markers. Choose the least one, if any, for which We,s​ has two elements outside all markers of higher-priority indices d<e. Assign two such elements as its markers, using a fixed least-element rule, and cancel all markers at lower-priority indices. Do nothing if no such index exists. The active markers are therefore pairwise distinct at every stage.
For x<y, simulate this finite construction through stage y and define
c({x,y})={1,0,​x is an active second marker be​ at stage y,otherwise.​
(20)
In particular every active first marker has colour zero against larger points. This is a total computable function: no question about eventual enumeration or eventual stabilization is used in computing a given pair.
Induction on e proves finite injury. Once the finitely many higher requirements have stabilized, requirement e is never cancelled again. If it has markers they stay fixed. If We​ is infinite, it eventually supplies two points outside the finite collection of higher markers, so it becomes eligible and is assigned markers; only finitely many higher indices could delay its assignment. Thus an infinite We​ eventually has permanent distinct markers ae​,be​. Choose y∈We​ beyond both markers and beyond their stabilization stage. Then
c({ae​,y})=0,c({be​,y})=1.
(21)
So We​ is not homogeneous. Every infinite recursive set is computably enumerable, proving the desired obstruction. For n>2, colour an increasing n-tuple by the pair formed by its first two elements. Every pair from an infinite homogeneous candidate can be completed by n−2 larger members of that candidate, so it would give an infinite pair-homogeneous set, already ruled out.
The uncountable positive theorem is the Erdős-Rado theorem for finite arities. For every infinite cardinal κ and finite r≥0, define ℶ0​(κ)=κ and ℶr+1​(κ)=2ℶr​(κ). Then
ℶr​(κ)+⟶(κ+)κr+1​.​
(22)
The partition relation means that every colouring of (r+1)-element subsets of the left-hand cardinal by at most κ colours has a homogeneous subset of size κ+. In particular, taking κ=ℵ0​ gives an uncountable homogeneous set for every finite arity, once the domain is sufficiently large.
We prove the end-homogeneity lemma needed for induction. Let μ be infinite, θ=(2μ)+, m≥1 finite, and c:[θ]m+1→κ with κ≤μ. In a sufficiently large Hχ​ choose an elementary substructure M of size 2μ containing c,θ and all ordinals below μ+, and closed under externally given sequences of length at most μ. Here Hχ​ consists of sets whose transitive closures have size below χ.
To justify that closure, begin with a hull containing the indicated parameters and ordinals. At each successor stage add every sequence of length at most μ from the preceding stage, then take a Skolem hull. There are at most (2μ)μ=2μ such sequences. Iterate for μ+ stages and take elementary unions at limits. The union still has size 2μ, and every sequence of at most μ of its elements is contained in one earlier stage by regularity of μ+, so appears at the next. This proves the claimed closed first-order model rather than assuming its existence.
Let β=sup(M∩θ)<θ. The inequality uses regularity of θ and ∣M∣<θ; M∩θ has no largest element, since it is closed under successor. Recursively choose increasing xα​∈M∩θ for α<μ+, imposing, for every m-tuple t of earlier points,
c(t∪{xα​})=c(t∪{β}).
(23)
At any stage there are at most μ earlier points and constraints. Each constraint consists of a tuple of elements of M and a colour below κ, also in M. The entire constraint code therefore belongs to M by closure. The bound above the previous points belongs to M as well. Although β itself need not belong to M, it witnesses in Hχ​ the existence of a point above that bound satisfying the coded constraints. Elementarity supplies such a point inside M. This proves the recursion.
On the resulting sequence X of length μ+, the colour of an (m+1)-tuple depends only on its first m entries: its last entry has the same colour contribution as β. This is the required closed elementary-submodel construction of an end-homogeneous sequence.
Now prove the theorem by induction on r. The case r=0 is the infinite pigeonhole principle: partitioning κ+ into at most κ classes of size at most κ cannot cover it. For r≥1, set μ=ℶr−1​(κ) and apply the lemma to the colouring on (2μ)+=ℶr​(κ)+. Define a colouring of r-tuples from its end-homogeneous sequence of length μ+ by their colour when completed with β. The induction hypothesis supplies a subset of size κ+ on which this derived colouring is constant. Every original (r+1)-tuple from that subset has the derived colour of its first r entries, and is therefore monochromatic. This completes the proof.
The unrestricted infinite-exponent situation is very different under AC. On [λ]ω, choose one representative R of each equivalence class modulo finite symmetric difference, and colour A by
c(A)=∣A△R∣(mod2).
(24)
If A is infinite and a∈A, deleting a remains in the same equivalence class and flips the colour. Every infinite candidate homogeneous set contains such an A and A∖{a} as subsets, so
λ↛(ω)2ω​for every infinite cardinal λ.​
(25)
More generally the same argument works for subsets of any fixed infinite cardinality: deleting one element preserves that cardinality, so there is no unrestricted analogue with a homogeneous set large enough to contain such subsets. For the order-type-ω convention, choose A in increasing order type ω and delete its first element; both sets still have that order type. Thus the distinction from the finite-arity theorem is genuine, not merely a notational ambiguity. This argument uses choice of representatives and is not a claim about every definability-restricted infinite-arity colouring.
