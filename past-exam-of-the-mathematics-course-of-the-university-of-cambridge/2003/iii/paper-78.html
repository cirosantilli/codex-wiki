At normal incidence the P wave and the two S wave polarizations decouple. In each uniform layer the relevant displacement satisfies ρutt​=Cuzz​, with C=λ+2μ for a longitudinal wave and C=μ for a shear wave. Its speed is v=C/ρ​ and its seismic impedance is Z=ρv. A welded interface preserves particle velocity and traction. For displacement or velocity amplitudes, incidence from medium 0 onto medium 1 therefore gives
1+r=t,Z0​(1−r)=Z1​t,r=Z0​+Z1​Z0​−Z1​​,t=Z0​+Z1​2Z0​​​.
(1)
Reflection reverses the displacement sign when the receiving seismic impedance is larger. The energy-flux fractions are
R=∣r∣2,T=Z0​Z1​​∣t∣2,R+T=1​.
(2)
The impedance factor matters: a displacement transmission coefficient larger than one does not imply energy amplification. Equal impedance eliminates reflection even when the densities and speeds differ.
For a stack, every interface creates reflected waves which can return to all earlier interfaces. A seismic layer transfer matrix incorporates those multiple reflections exactly. Use harmonic time dependence e−iωt, particle velocity V and compressive traction P=−σzz​ for a P-wave, or the negative relevant shear traction for an S-wave. Through layer j of thickness hj​,
(PV​)j,bottom​=Mj​(PV​)j,top​,Mj​=(cosδj​iZj​sinδj​​isinδj​/Zj​cosδj​​),δj​=vj​ωhj​​.
(3)
This follows by solving Vz​=iωP/Cj​ and Pz​=iωρj​V. Continuity means that the state passes unchanged across each welded interface. Put M=MN​⋯M1​=(ac​bd​), with ad−bc=1, and let the exterior impedances be ZL​,ZR​. A unit incident velocity gives top state (1+r,ZL​(1−r)) and bottom state (t,ZR​t). Eliminating these states yields
D=ZR​a+ZL​d−c−ZL​ZR​b,r=Dc+ZL​d−ZR​a−ZL​ZR​b​,t=D2ZL​​​.
(4)
For real moduli and no attenuation, conservation of elastic-wave energy flux gives ∣r∣2+(ZR​/ZL​)∣t∣2=1. Reciprocal incidence gives equal energy transmission in the reverse direction; flux-normalized amplitudes express that reciprocity without unequal exterior impedances obscuring it.
The wave phase accumulated in each layer determines constructive or destructive interference. For one layer between identical exterior media, an integral number of half-wavelengths makes Mj​=±I and gives perfect transmission, regardless of the layer impedance. At quarter-wave phase thickness, a single matching layer with Zj​=ZL​ZR​​ also gives zero reflection: the layer transforms the terminal impedance to Zj2​/ZR​=ZL​. Away from these matching conditions there are transmission peaks, reflection maxima and rapid frequency-dependent phase changes. In the time domain these are a direct transmitted pulse followed by delayed internal reverberations and reflected echoes. Long residence in a resonant stack produces narrow peaks and long ringing, without creating energy.
For a periodic two-layer stack, the half-trace of one cell is
cos(Kd)=cosδ1​cosδ2​−21​(Z2​Z1​​+Z1​Z2​​)sinδ1​sinδ2​.
(5)
This is the periodic seismic multilayer stop band criterion. When the absolute right side exceeds one, the cell eigenvalues are a reciprocal growing/decaying pair and a long finite stack has exponentially small transmission. The missing transmitted energy is reflected, not absorbed. Large impedance contrast and repeated quarter-wave layers strengthen such stop bands. In pass bands the cell modes propagate, and a finite stack superposes transmission resonances on that propagation. An irregular stack need not have equally spaced peaks: the individual travel times and impedance contrasts set its interference pattern.
At sufficiently low frequency a finite stack has M→I, so its leading reflection is that of the two exterior media alone. For many thin repeated layers with wavelength much longer than a cell, their effective density is the volume-weighted density and their normal modulus is the harmonic mean, Ceff−1​=⟨C−1⟩. This follows by expanding each propagation matrix to first order and averaging its two coefficients. At high frequency, sharp interfaces still reflect: there is no general disappearance of reflection, only increasingly rapid interference fringes. Smooth impedance grading instead suppresses short-wavelength reflection when its variation scale is long compared with the wavelength.
These calculations provide a reproducible investigation: vary the layer travel times, impedances and number of cells; compute the matrix product; plot reflected/transmitted fluxes and check their sum. Adding attenuation makes the flux sum smaller than one and reduces reverberation peaks, while oblique incidence introduces Snell law for elastic waves and P-SV mode conversion absent from this one-dimensional setting. Impedance contrasts generate reflections, propagation phases organize their interference, and energy conservation separates reflection from genuine absorption.

Let u(x,z,t) be displacement along the source line and let F(t) be its force per unit length. The shear-horizontal wave equation is
ρutt​−μΔu=F(t)δ(2)(x,z),μ=ρβ2.
(6)
Rotational symmetry about the line makes u=u(r,t). Integrate over a disk of radius r and use the divergence theorem:
ρ∫Dr​​utt​dA−2πμrur​=F(t).
(7)
At a fixed regular time, a logarithmic singularity and its time derivatives are locally integrable; the inertial integral is O(r2∣logr∣) and vanishes as r↓0. The leading balance is consequently rur​=−F(t)/(2πμ), giving the SH line-source near-field singularity
u(r,t)=−2πμF(t)​logr∗​r​+O(1)(r↓0)​.
(8)
The reference length r∗​ only changes the bounded term; boundary conditions and source history determine that term. This is a local fixed-time statement, interpreted distributionally in time if the source force has jumps. It establishes logarithmic spatial dependence directly, without extrapolating far-field ray spreading into the source.

Away from the line source, insert a leading wavefront form u∼a(x)W(t−T(x)) into the shear-horizontal wave equation and match the two most singular orders. The first gives the eikonal equation ∣∇T∣=1/β. The next gives
2∇T⋅∇a+aΔT=0,∇⋅(a2∇T)=0.
(9)
Equivalently, SH ray-tube amplitude transport conserves ρβa2J, where J is the transverse width of a two-dimensional ray tube. A line source has straight radial elastic rays and J=rdθ for fixed angular width, so
a(r)=Cr−1/2​.
(10)
The same geometrical spreading applies to the first nonzero derivative discontinuity or leading wavefront singularity; the source history determines which waveform W and normalization C occur. In three dimensions a point source instead has J∝r2 and amplitude proportional to r−1.
This does not contradict the SH line-source near-field singularity. Ray transport describes an arrival-front limit with t−r/β small, or the high-frequency field outside the immediate source neighborhood. The logarithm describes r↓0 at fixed elapsed time and includes the tail behind the two-dimensional wavefront. For example, a causal step force F0​H(t) has the exact radial displacement
u(r,t)=2πμF0​​arcoshrβt​ H(t−r/β).
(11)
For r≪βt, this is F0​log(2βt/r)/(2πμ), the near-source logarithm. For t=r/β+τ with 0<τ≪r/β, it is
u∼2πμF0​2β​​r−1/2τ​​.
(12)
The coefficient of the leading arrival singularity therefore has exactly the ray-theory dependence. A force impulse gives the corresponding r−1/2H(τ)/τ​ leading field. The limits describe different regions of one causal solution; neither is a uniform approximation to the other.

Y′′+q2Y=0,q2=β′2ω2​−k2,Y′(0)=0,Y(h)=0.

qn​=hχn​​,χn​=(n+21​)π,n=0,1,2,…,ω2=β′2k2+ωn2​,ωn​=hβ′χn​​.
(22)

cn​(ω)=1−ωn2​/ω2​β′​,Un​(ω)=β′1−ωn2​/ω2​,cn​Un​=β′2​.

The full Love wave problem replaces the rigid base by a decaying substrate displacement. With Y=Acos(qz) in the layer and Y=Be−κ(z−h) below it,
q2=β′2ω2​−k2,κ2=k2−β2ω2​>0,tan(qh)=μ′qμκ​,
(24)
where μ′=ρ′β′2 and μ=ρβ2. Displacement continuity gives B=Acos(qh) and traction continuity gives μ′qsin(qh)=μκcos(qh). The rigid base is therefore a good approximation only when μκ≫μ′q: then the substrate displacement is small and qh lies close to χn​=(n+1/2)π.
Put ϵ=β′/β, η=μ′/μ, y=qh, W=ωh/β′ and K=kh. The complete dispersion branch can be parameterized without root finding:
W(y)=1−ϵ2​y1+η2tan2y​​,K(y)=1−ϵ2​yϵ2+η2tan2y​​,nπ<y<χn​​.
(25)
Then c=β′W/K and U=β′W′/K′. At the lower end, c→β and U→β. The Love-wave cutoff frequencies are ωc,n​=nπβ′/[h1−ϵ2​], with zero cutoff for the fundamental. Thus the rigid-layer formula cannot describe the fundamental's low-frequency limit or the low-frequency part of an overtone: it would either give no propagating mode or predict c>β, outside the trapping interval β′<c<β.
For comparable densities and a fixed mode index, η=(ρ′/ρ)ϵ2≪1. Write y=χn​−δ near the strong-dispersion transition. In the region controlling the group-velocity minimum of a high-contrast Love wave, δ≪1 and η/δ≫ϵ. The leading derivatives give
β′U​≃ηχn​δ2​+δη​.
(26)
Their minimum is at
δmin​∼(2η2χn​​)1/3,ωmin,n​∼hβ′χn​​=ωn​​,β′Umin,n​​∼22/33​(χn​η​)1/3.
(27)
The frequency estimate follows by inserting δmin​ into W≃(χn​−δ)[1+η2/(2δ2)+ϵ2/2]: the two leading terms of size δ cancel. The real group velocity has a finite minimum and turns upwards near the rigid-layer threshold, whereas the rigid formula has zero group speed at its artificial threshold.
For a useful estimate of the validity range, put Δ=W−χn​>0. The rigid prediction has KR2​=W2−χn2​≃2χn​Δ. The finite-load phase correction is δ≃ηχn​/(κh); its changes to both K and dK/dW are small if

(28)
The first condition keeps KR​ away from the substrate propagation boundary; the second controls the rapidly changing interface phase. Equivalently, the rigid formulas are reliable sufficiently above the actual group-velocity minimum, beyond its narrow transition interval. At fixed ω/ωn​>1 separated from one, they become asymptotically accurate as the contrast grows. They are particularly simple at ω≫ωn​, where both velocities approach β′. Within the transition and below it the full dispersion relation is required.
The small speed ratio alone is insufficient if the layer density grows so strongly that μ′/μ is not small. For such materials the exact load criterion μκ≫μ′q and its frequency derivatives should be used; the preceding contrast estimates assume a bounded order-one density ratio and fixed n.

Let d(y,t)=[u[n]] denote the signed opening, positive when the faces separate, and let S be the crack surface. Its displacement jump is b=dn. The singular strain associated with this jump has moment density cijkl​bk​nl​. For the isotropic elastic stiffness tensor, contraction gives

Complete the crack normal to an orthonormal basis (e1​,e2​,n) with the first two vectors in the crack plane. The seismic moment tensor is diagonal in this basis:
M=V[λe1​e1T​+λe2​e2T​+(λ+2μ)nnT]​.
(41)
Each term gives an elastic force dipole, fi​=−Dei​ej​∂j​δ: two equal and opposite forces in direction e, separated along e, with moment D. Hence there are two identical in-plane dipoles of strength λV and one normal dipole of strength (λ+2μ)V. For positive opening and ordinary positive Lamé parameters, all are extensional and the normal one is larger by 2μV.
More generally, stable isotropic linear elasticity requires μ>0 and 3λ+2μ>0. These imply λ+2μ>∣λ∣, so the normal dipole has greater magnitude even if an auxetic material has negative λ and compressive in-plane dipoles. The point source has zero net force, because it differentiates a delta function, and zero net torque, because its moment tensor is symmetric. Its normal moment distinguishes opening from a pure shear dislocation.

Its radial part is outward near the poles, changes sign at cos2θ=1/8, and is inward in the crack plane. The tangential part has the same signed angular factor as the S radiation, but is permanent and has a different distance dependence. Its radial sign changes are not nodes of the whole vector because the tangential component generally remains nonzero there. Rotating a meridional sketch around the crack normal gives the complete three-dimensional axisymmetric pattern.
