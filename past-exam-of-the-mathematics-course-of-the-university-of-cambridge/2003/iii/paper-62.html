For an ideal gas with constant adiabatic index γ>1, let cv​ and cp​ be specific heat capacities and R=cp​−cv​. The ideal gas law and the internal energy of an ideal gas give P=ρRT and specific internal energy e=cv​T. Since γ=cp​/cv​, the internal energy per unit volume is ρe=P/(γ−1). Consequently a star with spherical symmetry has
U=γ−11​∫PdV=γ−14π​∫0R​P(r)r2dr.​
(1)
The radial integral requires the volume measure dV=4πr2dr: the first printed expression omits this factor if its differential is read literally as dr. The mass-coordinate expression confirms the required volume interpretation.
Define the enclosed mass M(r), total mass Mtot​ and dimensionless shell label m=M(r)/Mtot​. Mass conservation gives Mtot​dm=dM=ρdV, so the change of variables gives
U=γ−1Mtot​​∫01​ρ(m)P(m)​dm.​
(2)
Here m labels a fixed material shell rather than its radial position. In a mass-preserving stellar homology, r(m)=xr0​(m) with x=R/R0​. Both a shell's radius and thickness gain a factor x, so its volume gains x3 and its mass density becomes ρ(m)=x−3ρ0​(m). An adiabatic process keeps P/ργ fixed separately in each shell, even if that constant differs between shells. Thus P=x−3γP0​ and P/ρ=x−3(γ−1)P0​/ρ0​. Integration at fixed mass labels proves
U(x)=U0​x−3(γ−1).​
(3)
The spherical shell theorem gives the Newtonian gravitational potential energy by assembling shells against the mass already inside them:
W=−∫0Mtot​​r(M)GM​dM.
(4)
Every radius gains the same factor x, while M and dM remain fixed. Hence
W(x)=W0​x−1,E(x)=U0​x−a+W0​x−1,a=3(γ−1).​
(5)
This is the homologous adiabatic stellar stability test. The derivative with respect to the physical stellar radius is ∂R​=R0−1​∂x​. Stationarity at x=1 therefore requires −aU0​−W0​=0, or
∣W0​∣=3(γ−1)U0​.​
(6)
After imposing this equilibrium relation, E/U0​=x−a−a/x and
dxdE​=aU0​(x−2−x−a−1),∂R2∂2E​​R0​​=R02​a(a−1)U0​​.
(7)
Since a>0, a strict energy minimum under this homologous displacement requires
γ>34​.​
(8)
For a>1, contraction sends E to +∞, expansion sends it to 0 from below, and x=1 is the unique minimum, with E(1)=(1−a)U0​<0. For 0<a<1, contraction sends E to −∞, expansion sends it to 0 from above, and x=1 is the unique maximum. When a=1, the equilibrium relation makes E(x)=0 for every x: the scale direction is marginal, not strictly stable. The plotted curves display these three possibilities. This checks homologous radial stability; stability against every stellar displacement needs a fuller perturbation analysis.

Write σ02​=⟨vz2​⟩=vrms2​/3 for the one-dimensional thermal velocity dispersion of an isotropic monatomic ideal gas. An isothermal process keeps σ0​ fixed, and the vertical pressure is P=ρσ02​. Hydrostatic equilibrium in the Newtonian gravitational potential Φ(z) gives
σ02​dzdlogρ​=−dzdΦ​.
(9)
Planar symmetry reduces the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity to Φ′′=4πGρ. Combining the two equations yields
σ02​dz2d2logρ​=−4πGρ.​
(10)
The velocity factor here is a velocity second moment, not the square of a particular particle's instantaneous vertical velocity.
Set H2=σ02​/(2πGρ0​) and try the symmetric self-gravitating isothermal slab profile ρ=ρ0​sech2(z/H). Its logarithmic derivatives are
dzdlogρ​=−H2​tanh(z/H),dz2d2logρ​=−H22​sech2(z/H).
(11)
The chosen H makes the second expression satisfy the required equation. The central conditions ρ(0)=ρ0​ and ρ′(0)=0 fix the smooth even solution uniquely. In the scale-height convention used here,
ρ(z)=ρ0​sech2(2z0​z​),z0​=8πGρ0​​σ0​​,H=2z0​.​
(12)
The undefined constant printed as a in the scale-height denominator must be G; this follows both from substitution and dimensions.
Integrating hydrostatic equilibrium, with the additive potential constant chosen so that Φ(0)=0, gives
Φ(z)=2σ02​logcosh(z/H),z¨=−H2σ02​​tanh(z/H).​
(13)
The orbits in a self-gravitating isothermal slab separate into horizontal and vertical motions. There is no horizontal force, so x=xi​+vx​t and y=yi​+vy​t, with constant horizontal velocities. For collisionless test particles, or between gas collisions, conservation of energy fixes the vertical energy
Ez​=21​z˙2+Φ(z).
(14)
Every finite Ez​>0 has two turning points ±A, where
A=Harcosh(eEz​/(2σ02​)),Tz​=4∫0A​2[Ez​−Φ(z)]​dz​.
(15)
Thus the general orbit drifts uniformly in the plane while oscillating periodically across it. A particle with Ez​=0 stays in the midplane; an orbit with nonzero horizontal velocity is generally not closed.
Near the plane, Φ(z)=σ02​z2/H2+O(z4), so the vertical motion is a harmonic oscillator with ν2=2σ02​/H2=4πGρ0​. Far from the plane, Φ∼2σ02​∣z∣/H and the force tends to a constant magnitude. Indeed the surface density is Σ=∫ρdz=2ρ0​H, and that magnitude is 2πGΣ. Large-amplitude vertical motion is therefore approximately uniformly accelerated on each side, rather than sinusoidal. Because this ideal slab extends infinitely in the horizontal directions and its potential grows without bound as ∣z∣→∞, no finite-energy particle escapes vertically.

For an isolated smooth mass distribution with spherical symmetry, assemble each shell dM at radius r against the Newtonian gravitational potential of its interior mass. The spherical shell theorem gives
W=−G∫0∞​rM(r)​dM(r)=−2G​∫0∞​r1​d[M(r)2].
(16)
Assume finite total mass, finite binding energy and no central point-mass self-energy, so M(r)2/r vanishes at both endpoints. Integration by parts then gives
W=−2G​∫0∞​r2M(r)2​dr.​
(17)
For example, M(r)∝r3 near a regular center guarantees the central boundary condition, while finite total mass guarantees the one at infinity. A divergent central binding energy would fall outside this formula's finite-energy hypotheses.
For the discrete system use the centre of mass frame, let N be the particle number, and write Mtot​=Nm. The printed M in the particle-count factor and the subsequent n must be understood consistently as N. Define
K=21​∑i​m∣r˙i​∣2,W=−Gm2∑i<j​rij​1​,I=∑i​m∣ri​∣2.
(18)
By Newton's second law, differentiation gives
21​I¨=2K+∑i​ri​⋅Fi​.
(19)
Each pair contributes
ri​⋅Fij​+rj​⋅Fji​=(ri​−rj​)⋅[−Gm2rij3​ri​−rj​​]=−rij​Gm2​.
(20)
Therefore the exact dynamical virial theorem is I¨/2=2K+W. In a stationary statistical state, or on a bounded-motion time average for which the mean I¨ vanishes, it becomes
2⟨K⟩+⟨W⟩=0.​
(21)
There is no external pressure, tidal field or boundary work in this isolated point-particle derivation. For a time-averaged system, the following W and gravitational radius refer to that same averaged binding energy.
Using W=−G(Nm)2/Rg​ and 2K=Nm⟨veq2​⟩, virial equilibrium gives
⟨veq2​⟩=Rg​GNm​,veq,rms​=Rg​GNm​​.​
(22)
This is the three-dimensional mean squared speed about the centre of mass. For an isotropic system each one-dimensional velocity dispersion squared is one third of it.
Set the Newtonian gravitational potential to zero at infinity and exclude each particle's own field. The local escape velocity in the instantaneous frozen potential obeys vesc,i2​=−2Φi​, where Φi​=−Gm∑j=i​rij−1​. Since every pair occurs twice, ∑i​mΦi​=2W. Averaging over the identical particles proves the gravitational rms escape and collapse speeds relation
⟨vesc2​⟩=−Nm4W​=Rg​4GNm​=4⟨veq2​⟩,vesc,rms​=2veq,rms​.​
(23)
An actual encounter in a changing potential can exchange energy, so this is the usual local escape-speed definition, not a claim about every many-body trajectory.
Finally, dissipation-free collapse from rest at infinite separation has total energy zero. Conservation of energy at the specified binding energy therefore gives Kcoll​=−W, twice the equilibrium kinetic energy at the same W. Hence
⟨vcoll2​⟩=Rg​2GNm​=2⟨veq2​⟩,vcoll,rms​=2​veq,rms​.​
(24)
This mean squared speed includes coherent infall; a local random velocity dispersion after subtracting the infall velocity need not obey that ratio.

There is a genuine inconsistency in this part. For any nonnegative metallicity distribution function, its maximum is at least its mean. A population of positive mean solar metallicity cannot have a maximum equal to half that mean. For example, a uniform abundance distribution on [0,2Z⊙​] has mean Z⊙​ and maximum 2Z⊙​. Thus the printed maximum-to-mean ratio cannot be proved. The following calculation explains the supplied function, gives the consistent distribution, and also treats the literal birth-metallicity interpretation separately.
Let f(μ) denote the supplied abundance function. Algebraically it is
f(μ)=(Y+1)(1−μ)Y−(Y+1)μ+μY+1​.
(25)
With δ=1−μ, a Taylor series of (1−δ)Y+1 gives
f(1−δ)=2Yδ​+6Y(1−Y)δ2​+O(δ3).
(26)
Consequently
f(1−δ)≃2Yδ​.​
(27)
This is an early-consumption expansion: its fractional correction is (1−Y)δ/3+O(δ2). Small yield alone does not make the expression linear throughout most of the gas-consumption history. At fixed 0<μ<1, the small-Y expansion instead is
f(μ)=Y[1+1−μμlogμ​]+O(Y2).
(28)
In a well-mixed closed-box model of galactic chemical evolution, the gas fraction of a galaxy also labels the permanently locked stellar mass: dMs​=−Mg,init​dμ. Newly formed stars inherit the current gas-phase metallicity Zg​(μ). Therefore the mass-weighted mean stellar metallicity satisfies the cumulative and birth stellar metallicities relation
Z∗​(μ)=1−μ1​∫μ1​Zg​(u)du,Zg​(μ)=−dμd​[(1−μ)Z∗​(μ)].
(29)
Applying the derivative to f gives
Z∗​(μ)=f(μ)⟺Zbirth​(μ)=Zg​(μ)=1−μY.​
(30)
Thus the supplied function has the natural interpretation of a cumulative mean, not an individual star's birth abundance. Direct integration of 1−uY verifies that it gives exactly f. This is the saturating closed-box enrichment law: it corresponds to effective stellar yield Y(1−Zg​), with Zg​ measured as a fraction. The usual constant-yield trace-metal model instead gives Zg​=−Ylogμ. Both agree to first order when Y∣logμ∣≪1; their exact formulae should not be identified outside that regime.
Let the final gas fraction be μf​>0 and δf​=1−μf​. In the consistent mean-metallicity interpretation, Z∗​(μf​)=Z⊙​ while the most recently formed stars have the maximum zf​=1−μfY​. At early consumption,
Z∗​≃2Yδf​​,zf​≃Yδf​,
(31)
so the corrected result is
Z∗,max​≃2Z⊙​.​
(32)
The assumption here includes δf​≪1, equivalently 2Z⊙​/Y≪1 in this approximation. Exactly, zf​=1−μfY​ and Z⊙​=f(μf​) determine the maximum implicitly; it remains larger than the mean, but its ratio to the mean need not equal two after substantial consumption.
For the mass-weighted metallicity distribution function, stars formed while the gas fraction falls by −dμ have probability dP=−dμ/δf​. Writing their individual abundance as z=1−μY gives μ=(1−z)1/Y and the change of variables
dP=F(z)dz,F(z)=Y(1−μf​)(1−z)1/Y−1​,0<z<1−μfY​.​
(33)
The density is zero outside this interval. Its cumulative probability is [1−(1−z)1/Y]/(1−μf​), so it integrates to one; integrating zF(z) reproduces f(μf​). A star-count distribution has the same form if the initial mass function and survival selection give a fixed number of observed stars per unit locked mass. Luminosity-weighted samples require different weights.
For trace abundances, (1−z)1/Y−1≃e−z/Y to leading order at fixed z/Y. The corresponding ordinary closed-box metallicity distribution is
Ftrace​(z)=Y(1−μf​)e−z/Y​,0<z<−Ylogμf​.
(34)
In the early-consumption regime zf​≪Y, this becomes approximately uniform, F≃1/(Yδf​) on [0,Yδf​]. Its mean is half its upper endpoint, again producing the corrected maximum 2Z⊙​.
If the supplied f(μ) is instead retained literally as the individual birth abundance, set q=f(μ) rather than z=1−μY. The observation-time population mean is then δf−1​∫μf​1​f(u)du, not f(μf​). Its exact distribution can be specified parametrically using
dδdf​=1−μ1−μY−f(μ)​>0,Fliteral​(f(μ))=(1−μf​)[1−μY−f(μ)]1−μ​,μf​<μ<1.​
(35)
Positivity follows because f is the average of the increasing function 1−(1−δ)Y. Its support is 0<q<f(μf​), and normalization follows directly from dP=dδ/δf​. At early consumption it is uniform with Fliteral​≃2/(Yδf​), maximum Yδf​/2 and mean Yδf​/4. Even this literal alternative gives maximum twice the population mean, never half. The distinction between birth abundance and cumulative mean resolves the calculation; the printed inverse maximum-to-mean ratio is false under either interpretation.
