The standard CW complex structure on Complex projective space CP3 has one cell in each dimension 0,2,4,6 and no other cells. Its cellular chain complex therefore has zero differentials: each boundary has an odd-dimensional target group, which is zero. The product of the usual two-cell structures on S2 and S4 likewise has exactly one cell in dimensions 0,2,4,6. Thus cellular homology gives, for both spaces,
Hi​(−;Z)≅{Z,0,​i=0,2,4,6,otherwise.​​
(1)
The cohomology rings detect more than these additive groups. Let c∈H2(CP3;Z) be the hyperplane class. The cohomology ring of complex projective space is
H∗(CP3;Z)=Z[c]/(c4),∣c∣=2.
(2)
For the normalization, a hyperplane is Poincare dual to c; intersecting three generic hyperplanes gives one point with positive complex orientation, so ⟨c3,[CP3]⟩=1. Together with the computed additive groups this makes c,c2,c3 generators in their respective degrees.
Let u and v be the pullbacks of the orientation classes of S2 and S4. The Künneth theorem and naturality of the cup product give
H∗(S2×S4;Z)=Z[u,v]/(u2,v2),∣u∣=2,∣v∣=4,
(3)
where uv generates degree six. In particular, every degree-two class on the product has square zero, whereas c2=0. Any graded ring isomorphism would send c to ±u, contradicting this product relation. A homotopy equivalence induces an isomorphism of cohomology rings, so the spaces have isomorphic integral homology but are not homotopy equivalent.

f∗(c3)=(f∗c)3=k3u3=0.
(4)
With the product orientation on the source and the complex orientation on the target, the mapping degree d is defined by f∗​[S2×S4]=d[CP3]. Evaluation on the fundamental class gives
d=⟨c3,f∗​[S2×S4]⟩=⟨f∗(c3),[S2×S4]⟩=0.
(5)
Thus the induced group homomorphism on H6​ is zero. This is a cup-power obstruction to nonzero degree, rather than a consequence of the additive homology groups alone.

A simply connected manifold is connected and orientable: its orientation character is a group homomorphism π1​(X)→{±1}, and this group is trivial. Choose an orientation. The Hurewicz theorem in degree one gives H1​(X;Z)=0. The universal coefficient theorem for cohomology then gives H1(X;Z)=0 and
H2(X;Z)≅Hom(H2​(X;Z),Z),
(6)
because the possible Ext(H1​(X),Z) term is zero.
The homology groups are finitely generated for a closed manifold. The displayed dual group is therefore free abelian. Poincare duality identifies H2​(X;Z) with H2(X;Z), so H2​ itself has no torsion. Since the second Betti number is one, both groups are isomorphic to Z. This torsion argument is essential: rank one by itself would not rule out finite summands.
Again by Poincare duality, H3(X;Z)≅H1​(X;Z)=0, and H4(X;Z)≅H0​(X;Z)=Z. Thus the only nonzero cohomology groups are Z in degrees 0,2,4.
Let u generate H2. The integral intersection form
Q(a,b)=⟨a⌣b,[X]⟩
(7)
is unimodular. Indeed, cap product with [X] is the Poincare duality isomorphism to H2​, and the preceding universal-coefficient identification makes evaluation the full integral dual. Composing these isomorphisms identifies the pairing with an isomorphism H2→Hom(H2,Z). In rank one its matrix must therefore be [1] or [−1]. Consequently ⟨u2,[X]⟩=±1, so u2 generates H4.
All products in degrees above four vanish. Hence the assignment of the degree-two generator gives
H∗(X;Z)≅Z[u]/(u3),∣u∣=2,​
(8)
which is the cohomology ring of complex projective space for CP2. If the intersection form is negative, this ring isomorphism reverses the chosen top orientation generator; preserving that additional orientation is not part of the requested ring isomorphism.

Use integral singular homology. The singular chain group Cn​(B) is the free abelian group on continuous maps Δn→B, and its boundary operator is
∂σ=∑j=0n​(−1)jσ∣[0,…,j​,…,n]​.
(9)
For i<j, deleting vertex j and then vertex i has sign (−1)i+j, while deleting i and then the shifted vertex j−1 has sign (−1)i+j−1. Both yield the same codimension-two face. Pairing these terms proves ∂2=0. The inclusion A⊂B identifies Cn​(A) with the subgroup spanned by those simplices having image in A, so it is injective and preserved by ∂.
The relative chain complex is Cn​(B,A)=Cn​(B)/Cn​(A) with induced boundary ∂bˉ=∂b. Thus there is a degreewise short exact sequence of chain complexes
0⟶C∗​(A)i​C∗​(B)q​C∗​(B,A)⟶0.
(10)
Inclusion and quotient take homological cycles to homological cycles and boundaries to boundaries, hence define the group homomorphisms i∗​ and q∗​ on homology. We construct and prove exactness of the resulting sequence directly, rather than invoke an unproved algebraic lemma:
⋯⟶Hn+1​(B,A)δ​Hn​(A)i∗​​Hn​(B)q∗​​Hn​(B,A)δ​Hn−1​(A)⟶⋯
(11)
ending in H0​(A)→H0​(B)→H0​(B,A)→0.
For the relative homology connecting homomorphism, lift a relative cycle bˉ∈Cn​(B,A) to b∈Cn​(B). Since ∂bˉ=0, the chain ∂b belongs to Cn−1​(A); it is a homological cycle because ∂2=0. Define
δ[bˉ]=[∂b]∈Hn−1​(A).
(12)
Changing the lift by a∈Cn​(A) changes the boundary by ∂a, hence does not change its homology class. More generally, if a different relative cycle represents the same relative homology class, lifts satisfy b′−b=∂c+a for some c∈Cn+1​(B) and a∈Cn​(A). Then ∂b′−∂b=∂a. This proves independence both of the lift and of the homology representative. Lifting a sum by the sum of lifts proves additivity, so δ is a well-defined group homomorphism.
At Hn​(A), an element in the image of δ is represented by ∂b for some b∈Cn+1​(B), so its image in Hn​(B) is zero. Conversely, if a homological cycle in A a has i∗​[a]=0, then a=∂b for a B-chain b∈Cn+1​(B). Its quotient bˉ is a relative cycle and δ[bˉ]=[a]. Thus
im(δ:Hn+1​(B,A)→Hn​(A))=keri∗​.​
(13)
For completeness, the other terms are exact by the same elementary chain argument. At Hn​(B), clearly q∗​i∗​=0. If a homological cycle in B b has zero relative class, then bˉ=∂cˉ for a quotient chain cˉ. Lifting c gives a=b−∂c∈Cn​(A), and ∂a=0. Hence [b]=i∗​[a], proving kerq∗​=imi∗​. At Hn​(B,A), absolute homological cycles have zero connecting image. Conversely, if δ[bˉ]=0, then ∂b=∂a for some a∈Cn​(A); the chain b−a is an absolute homological cycle and maps to [bˉ]. Therefore kerδ=imq∗​. In degree zero every quotient chain lifts to an absolute homological cycle, because the differential to degree −1 is zero, so q∗​:H0​(B)→H0​(B,A) is surjective. These proofs establish the whole long exact sequence in relative homology and all the algebraic facts used in its construction.

Here closed oriented surfaces are connected, as in the usual single-genus convention. Let f:X→Y have nonzero mapping degree d. Work over Q, so multiplication by d is invertible and cannot vanish.
The Poincare duality pairing on H1(Y;Q) is nondegenerate. Thus for every 0=a∈H1(Y;Q) there is b∈H1(Y;Q) with ⟨a⌣b,[Y]⟩=0. Naturality of the cup product and evaluation on the fundamental class give
⟨f∗a⌣f∗b,[X]⟩=⟨f∗(a⌣b),[X]⟩=⟨a⌣b,f∗​[X]⟩=d⟨a⌣b,[Y]⟩=0.
(14)
In particular f∗a=0, so f∗:H1(Y;Q)→H1(X;Q) is injective. The cohomology ring of a closed oriented surface has dimH1(Σg​;Q)=2g. Comparing dimensions now gives
2g(Y)≤2g(X),g(X)≥g(Y).​
(15)
This proves genus monotonicity under nonzero-degree surface maps by rational cohomology injectivity of a nonzero-degree map, without requiring f to be a covering map or a smooth map.

A vector field on the sphere means a tangent vector field. For S2m⊂R2m+1 with m≥1, suppose V(x) were continuous and nowhere zero. Normalize it to w(x)=V(x)/∥V(x)∥. Tangency means ⟨x,w(x)⟩=0, and both vectors have norm one. Therefore
H(x,t)=cos(πt)x+sin(πt)w(x),0≤t≤1,
(16)
has squared norm cos2(πt)+sin2(πt)=1. It defines a homotopy through self-maps of the sphere from the identity to the antipodal map.
The identity has mapping degree 1. The antipodal map on Sn has degree (−1)n+1: it is the boundary restriction of −I on the oriented (n+1)-ball, and this linear map changes orientation by sgndet(−I)=(−1)n+1. For n=2m its degree is −1. Homotopy invariance of mapping degree would force 1=−1, a contradiction. Thus every continuous tangent field on an even-dimensional sphere vanishes somewhere. For S0 the tangent spaces are zero, so the conclusion holds directly. This proves the Hairy ball theorem using degree, rather than assuming it.

J(x0​,x1​,…,x2m​,x2m+1​)=(−x1​,x0​,…,−x2m+1​,x2m​).

An ellipse here is a nondegenerate ellipse, hence a smooth embedded circle. Write the first ellipse as
γ(t)=c+A(costsint​),t∈R/(2πZ),
(19)
where A is invertible. Write the second as Q(x)=0, where
Q(x)=(x−d)TB(x−d)−1
(20)
for a positive-definite symmetric matrix B. Its gradient is nonzero on the ellipse. Let g(t)=Q(γ(t)). At an intersection, transversality says the tangent vector γ′(t) is not tangent to Q=0, so
g′(t)=∇Q(γ(t))⋅γ′(t)=0.
(21)
Every intersection is thus a simple zero of g, and g changes sign there. The intersection set is finite: it is closed and compact, and transversality makes each of its points isolated. Start at a parameter with g=0 and traverse one full period. Returning to the same sign requires an even number of sign changes. Therefore the number of intersection points is even, including zero when the ellipses are disjoint.
For the upper bound, substituting the parametrization into the quadratic gives the trigonometric polynomial
g(t)=a0​+a1​cost+b1​sint+a2​cos2t+b2​sin2t.
(22)
It is not identically zero: otherwise the first ellipse would lie on the second, and its tangent would be tangent to the second everywhere, violating transversality. Put z=eit. Multiplying by z2 turns this function into the polynomial
P(z)=2a2​−ib2​​z4+2a1​−ib1​​z3+a0​z2+2a1​+ib1​​z+2a2​+ib2​​.
(23)
It is nonzero and has degree at most four, so it has at most four distinct roots. Distinct points on the first ellipse correspond to distinct parameters modulo 2π and hence distinct points z on the unit circle. Thus
∣E1​∩E2​∣∈{0,2,4}.​
(24)
Both the parity and the upper bound follow without using an algebraic intersection theorem. The four-point bound is attained by a unit circle and a concentric ellipse whose semiaxes lie on opposite sides of one.
