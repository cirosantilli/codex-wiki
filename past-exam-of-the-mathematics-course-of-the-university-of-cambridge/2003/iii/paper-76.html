The Rayleigh number compares destabilizing thermal buoyancy with viscous and thermal diffusion:
Ra=νκgβΔTh3​.​
(11)
The conductive heat flux is kΔT/h, and the Nusselt number is the actual upward heat flux divided by this value. The four-thirds convective heat-transfer law is the empirical large-Rayleigh closure Nu=CRa1/3. It gives
q=Ck(νκgβ​)1/3(ΔT)4/3.​
(12)
The layer depth cancels: thin thermal boundary layers control the resistance while convection mixes the interior. The shallow-water approximation requires the depth to be small compared with the horizontal scale of variation, small vertical accelerations and nearly hydrostatic pressure. Take cross-sectionally uniform lower-layer velocity and a sufficiently deep, effectively stationary upper layer. Under the Boussinesq approximation, g′=g(ρ1​−ρ0​)/ρ0​ is the reduced gravity. Conservation of lower-layer volume gives constant discharge Q=bhu.
Dividing the Bernoulli equation by g′ gives the shallow-water specific energy
E=h+2g′b2h2Q2​,∂h∂E​​Q,b​=1−g′b2h3Q2​=1−F2,F=g′h​u​.​
(17)
At fixed Q,b, the energy curve has its minimum at the critical depth hc​=(Q2/(g′b2))1/3, where Ec​=3hc​/2. Above this minimum there are two depth branches: deeper subcritical flow has F<1, and shallower supercritical flow has F>1.
The two long-wave speeds relative to the bed are u±g′h​. In subcritical flow one family can propagate upstream and one downstream; in supercritical flow both travel downstream for u>0. At hydraulic control, u−g′h​=0, and a smooth transcritical solution must also satisfy the geometric regularity condition. The flow may remain entirely on either branch, cross smoothly through the control from subcritical to supercritical, or take the reverse smooth branch under appropriate boundary conditions. A downstream hydraulic jump can return supercritical flow to the subcritical branch while losing energy.
The sketch distinguishes smooth branch changes at a critical section from a jump: a jump conserves mass and momentum, not the original Bernoulli head.

A∗=[(Cb​Ab​)−2+(Ct​At​)−22​]1/2.​
(57)
Then the throughflow in this displacement ventilation model is
Qb​=Qt​=Q=A∗g1′​(H−h1​)​.​
(58)
Lower-layer conservation requires Q=Qp​(h1​)=CB11/3​h15/3​. Global buoyancy conservation gives g1′​Q=B1​. Eliminating Q,g1′​ yields
Q3=(A∗)2B1​(H−h1​),H2C3/2A∗​=[1−h1​/H(h1​/H)5​]1/2.​
(59)
This uniquely determines an interface height between zero and H for a given positive area parameter. Opening area controls the nondimensional height; source strength controls the density contrast and flow magnitude. Factors of two depend on the stated effective-area convention, which has been fixed explicitly here.

The square root requires a positive remaining buoyancy head.
As ψ→0, the weak source vanishes and g1′​/g2′​→0: the middle and lower layers have the same density, leaving a single physical interface at h2​. The old h1​ no longer marks an independent density interface. The single-plume two-layer model is the meaningful limit; the additional closure in part (e) also gives h2​/h1​→1.
As ψ→1, the density ratio tends to one, so the middle and upper layers merge. There is then a single physical lower interface h1​, fed by two equal, separate plumes. The area prefactor tends to two and the head reduces to g′(H−h1​). The mathematical label h2​ ceases to identify a distinct density interface, so a finite limiting value assigned to it by an extra closure is not evidence for three distinct layers. Two separate equal plumes entrain more than one coincident plume of combined source strength; the distinction survives after the layers merge.

d OurBigBook.com
Above h1​, plume 2 entrains middle-layer fluid rather than the original ambient. That fluid is already warm and light, so the plume's buoyancy relative to its surroundings is reduced. Its pre-interface density contrast and zero-source pure plume formula cannot simply be continued unchanged through the interface.
A consistent model treats the middle layer as a uniform new ambient, assumes a sharp interface and weak overall density differences, and carries the incident volume and momentum fluxes continuously across it. The relative buoyancy flux immediately above the interface is
B2+​=B2​−g1′​q21​=B2​(1−ψ2/3).
(66)
Continue an entraining plume model from these nonzero incident fluxes, using buoyancy measured relative to the middle layer. In that uniform layer the relative buoyancy flux is conserved, while entrainment raises the absolute buoyancy carried relative to the outside environment. This is generally a finite-flux or forced plume continuation, not a newly reset point-source similarity solution. A virtual-origin approximation can be used only after matching its fluxes appropriately.
At its matching level h2​, the plume density reaches the upper-layer density; it spreads and detrains, with possible finite-momentum overshoot. The model neglects mutual plume entrainment, wall heat transfer, interface thickness and detailed intrusion dynamics, and assumes each ambient layer is well mixed. In the equal-source limit B2+​=0, the continuation is a neutrally buoyant momentum-driven flow, emphasizing why the upper interface loses its independent density meaning.
