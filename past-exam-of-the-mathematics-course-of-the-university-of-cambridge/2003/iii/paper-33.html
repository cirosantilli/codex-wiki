A continuous adapted process M is a local martingale if there are stopping times τn​↑∞ almost surely such that each stopped process Mtτn​​=Mt∧τn​​ is a martingale. In this definition M0​ is integrable. A continuous adapted process A has finite variation if, almost surely, its paths have finite total variation of a function on each compact time interval:
Vt​(A)=sup0=t0​<⋯<tr​=t​∑j=1r​∣Atj​​−Atj−1​​∣<∞.
(1)
The permitted fact that this total-variation process is continuous and adapted makes its level-hitting times stopping times.
Suppose M=A, and put N=M−M0​. Define
σn​=τn​∧n∧inf{t:Vt​(N)≥n}.
(2)
These times increase to infinity almost surely. The optional stopping theorem applied to the localized martingale shows that Nσn​ is a martingale; it is also bounded in absolute value by n, with total variation at most n. Fix n,t and write L=Nσn​. For a deterministic partition of an interval 0=t0​<⋯<tr​=t, orthogonality of square-integrable martingale increments gives
ELt2​=∑j​E(Ltj​​−Ltj−1​​)2.
(3)
Pathwise the sum on the right is bounded by nmaxj​∣Ltj​​−Ltj−1​​∣. For partitions with mesh tending to zero, continuity makes this converge to zero. It is bounded by 2n2, so dominated convergence gives ELt2​=0. First use rational t, then continuity, to obtain L≡0 almost surely. Letting n→∞ proves
Mt​=M0​for all t≥0 simultaneously, almost surely.​
(4)
This proves continuous finite-variation local martingale is constant without assuming integrability of the unstopped total variation. Continuity matters: compensated jump martingales can have finite variation without being constant.

The multidimensional Itô formula for continuous semimartingales states that a C2 transformation has first-order stochastic and finite-variation integrals plus one-half its Hessian contracted with the quadratic covariations. A continuous finite-variation process has zero quadratic variation and zero quadratic covariation with a continuous local martingale. Thus in the present case
f(Mt​,At​)=f(M0​,A0​)+∫0t​fx​(Ms​,As​)dMs​+∫0t​fy​(Ms​,As​)dAs​+21​∫0t​fxx​(Ms​,As​)d[M]s​.
(5)
The first integrand is continuous adapted, hence predictable, and locally bounded by stopping the two coordinate paths on exiting compact sets. The standard construction of the Itô integral then makes this integral a zero-starting continuous local martingale. The remaining integrals are pathwise Lebesgue-Stieltjes integrals. On each compact time interval their integrands are bounded along the continuous paths, so their total variations are bounded by a constant times Vt​(A)+[M]t​, which is finite. Therefore the normalized terms are
Mtf​=f(M0​,A0​)+∫0t​fx​(Ms​,As​)dMs​,Atf​=∫0t​fy​(Ms​,As​)dAs​+21​∫0t​fxx​(Ms​,As​)d[M]s​.​
(6)
The process Af is continuous adapted of finite variation and A0f​=0. When f(M0​,A0​) is integrable, Mf is a local martingale in the definition of part (a). Uniqueness follows from part (a): the difference of two normalized decompositions is both a continuous local martingale and a continuous finite-variation process starting at zero, hence vanishes.
An initial-value qualification is necessary under that definition. The printed assumptions do not themselves guarantee E∣f(M0​,A0​)∣<∞. Take M=0, At​=G with G a standard normal random variable known at time zero, and f(x,a)=ea2. All path assumptions hold, but EeG2=∞. Any decomposition with A0f​=0 would force the nonintegrable value M0f​=eG2, contradicting the local-martingale definition. Without an integrability hypothesis, the always-valid continuous semimartingale decomposition is X=X0​+N+Af, with N the zero-starting integral above. Alternatively one may use the convention that a local martingale permits an arbitrary finite initial value and localizes its centered increments. With deterministic initial data, as in part (c), there is no issue. This is initial-value integrability in normalized semimartingale decompositions.

Atf​=∫0t​g(Bs​,s)ds,g(x,s)=fy​(x,s)+21​fxx​(x,s).

For any fixed t>0, the normal distribution of Bt​ has a strictly positive density at every real point. Since g(⋅,t) is continuous, a nonzero value at any spatial point would give a nonzero value on an open interval of positive Brownian probability, a contradiction. fy​(x,y)+21​fxx​(x,y)=0for every x∈R, y≥0.​

These are space-time harmonic functions along Brownian motion, satisfying the backward heat equation. The second coordinate never takes negative values, so no equation is imposed for y<0, beyond compatibility with the assumed global C2 regularity. Subtract M0​, which does not affect either the integral or its quadratic variation. Let N=M−M0​. Choose a localizing sequence τn​ for N. By local boundedness of the previsible process H, there are increasing stopping times ρn​↑∞ and finite deterministic constants Kn​ with ∣Hs​∣≤Kn​ on 0<s≤ρn​. Put
σn​=τn​∧ρn​∧n∧inf{t:∣Nt​∣≥n or [M]t​≥n}.
(9)
Continuity and finiteness on compact intervals imply σn​↑∞ almost surely. The stopped process Nσn​ is a bounded square-integrable martingale, its bracket is bounded by n, and its stopped integrand is bounded by Kn​. The indicator 1{s≤σn​}​ is predictable, so the stopped stochastic integral identity is valid.
Fix n and abbreviate
Jt​=∫0t∧σn​​Hs​dMs​,Qt​=∫0t∧σn​​Hs2​d[M]s​.
(10)
The Itô isometry gives EJt2​=EQt​≤Kn2​n, so J is a square-integrable martingale. For F∈Fs​, apply the same isometry to the predictable integrand 1F​1(s,t]​H, stopped at σn​. This yields
E[1F​(Jt​−Js​)2]=E[1F​(Qt​−Qs​)].
(11)
Also E[Jt​−Js​∣Fs​]=0. Expanding Jt2​−Js2​ therefore proves that J2−Q is a true martingale. The process Q is continuous adapted increasing and starts at zero. By the characterization of continuous martingale quadratic variation as its increasing square compensator, and uniqueness of an increasing square compensator, we have [J]=Q.
Stochastic integration and quadratic variation commute with stopping; for the latter this follows either from squared-increment sums or from the same square-compensator characterization. Hence the desired identity holds up to every σn​. Since these times increase to infinity, they exhaust every finite time interval almost surely, and the continuous identities patch to give
[H⋅M]t​=∫0t​Hs2​d[M]s​.​
(12)
This is the localized isometry proof of stochastic-integral quadratic variation. No unstopped second-moment or bracket-integrability assumption has been made.

The one-dimensional Lévy characterization of Brownian motion states: a continuous adapted process L, with L0​=0, is standard Brownian motion relative to its filtration if and only if it is a local martingale and [L]t​=t for all t almost surely.
For necessity, the centered independent Gaussian increments of Brownian motion give both the martingale property and the martingale Lt2​−t. The uniqueness characterization of quadratic variation then gives [L]t​=t.
For sufficiency fix θ∈R. The Itô formula gives
Et​=exp(iθLt​+21​θ2t),dEt​=iθEt​dLt​.
(13)
The second-order term cancels the time derivative because d[L]t​=dt. Thus E is a complex local martingale. Its modulus is the deterministic quantity eθ2t/2, bounded on every finite horizon, so its real and imaginary parts are true martingales by the bounded local martingale criterion. For s<t,
E[eiθ(Lt​−Ls​)∣Fs​]=e−θ2(t−s)/2.
(14)
Uniqueness of characteristic functions says that the increment has normal distribution N(0,t−s) independently of Fs​. Indeed multiplying the identity by any bounded Fs​-measurable variable gives the product characteristic-function identity expressing independence. Iterating over deterministic times gives independent increments. Together with continuity and L0​=0, these are precisely the defining properties of standard Brownian motion. If the starting value is a fixed x, the same argument identifies L−x as standard Brownian motion.

Put Cu​=∫0u​e2Ws​ds. Its paths are continuous and strictly increasing. We also need C∞​=∞ to ensure that the printed inverse is finite for every t.
Here is a duration argument for Divergence of a driftless Brownian exponential clock. Start at zero and choose successive return times Tj​ to zero, each at least one time unit after the preceding return. Recurrence of one-dimensional Brownian motion makes all these times finite. By the Strong Markov property, each unit interval after Tj​ has the same fixed positive probability that the Brownian path stays above −1. The intervals are disjoint, and repeated strong Markov conditioning makes these trials independent. Infinitely many succeed almost surely, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma. Each successful interval contributes at least e−2 to C∞​, proving divergence.
Therefore τt​ is the unique finite inverse of C, with Cτt​​=t. It is an increasing family of stopping times, since {τt​≤u}={Cu​≥t}. The continuous-local-martingale time-change theorem states that inverse-bracket time change preserves the local-martingale property in the filtration Gt​=Fτt​​ and transforms the bracket by composition. Applied to Y, this gives
[Z]t​=[Y]τt​​=Cτt​​=t,Z0​=1.
(17)
The Lévy characterization of Brownian motion now proves that Z is Brownian motion starting at one; Z−1 is standard Brownian motion, in the time-changed filtration. If the term Brownian motion is reserved for a process starting at zero, Z itself fails only that normalization. The substantive Brownian property, including independent Gaussian increments in the new time, holds.

The original PDF specifies a bounded solution class Cb2​, rather than the C02​ transcribed in the TeX. Write ϕ(x)=min(1,max(0,−x)) and fix a time t>0. For Brownian motion starting at x, consider
Fs​=exp(−λ∫0s​ϕ(Br​)dr)u(t−s,Bs​),0≤s≤t.
(23)
The Itô formula and ordinary product rule give a drift proportional to −ut​+21​uxx​−λϕu, which vanishes by the equation. Therefore F is a local martingale. It is bounded on this horizon because u is bounded and ϕ≥0, so the bounded local martingale criterion makes it a true martingale. Its endpoint values are F0​=u(t,x) and Ft​=e−λ∫0t​ϕ(Br​)dr, using u(0,⋅)=1. Consequently
uλ(t,x)=Ex​exp(−λ∫0t​ϕ(Bs​)ds).​
(24)
This proves the required Feynman-Kac formula with a bounded potential directly, and also gives uniqueness within the stated bounded solution class.
Let I=∫0t​ϕ(Bs​)ds. By dominated convergence, e−λI→1{I=0}​ in expectation. Continuity of B and ϕ shows that I=0 exactly when Bs​≥0 throughout [0,t]: a negative value at any time produces a nontrivial interval with positive integrand. For x<0 such an interval occurs immediately, so the limiting probability is zero. For x>0, the Brownian reflection principle and the atom-free distribution of the running minimum give
Px​(mins≤t​Bs​≥0)=1−2Px​(Bt​≤0).
(25)
For x=0 this probability is zero: the reflection formula for levels −ε, followed by ε↓0, shows that Brownian motion goes below zero before t almost surely. Thus
λ→∞lim​uλ(t,x)={1−2Px​(Bt​≤0),0,​x≥0,x<0.​​
(26)
This is the soft killing limit for Brownian nonnegative survival. The assertion is pointwise for positive time, as requested; no claim of uniform convergence across the initial-time boundary is needed.

At a positive state x, the future is constant. At zero, the residual holding time is independent rate-one exponential and the next state is an independent uniform mark. Thus the conditional future given the natural filtration depends only on the present state: X is a Markov jump process. For any bounded Borel g, its transition semigroup is
Ph​g(0)=e−hg(0)+(1−e−h)∫01​g(y)dy,Ph​g(x)=g(x)(x>0).
(31)
The Lévy kernel of a Markov jump process, written in destination-state convention, is
q(x,dy)=1{x=0}​1(0,1)​(y)dy.​
(32)
In displacement convention it has the same expression K(x,dz)=1{x=0}​1(0,1)​(z)dz, since its only nonzero jump rate is from zero. The Markov jump-process generator is Lg(x)=1{x=0}​(∫01​g(y)dy−g(0)).
We state the generator martingale theorem used here: for a nonexplosive Markov jump process and a bounded g with bounded Lg, the process g(Xt​)−g(X0​)−∫0t​Lg(Xs−​)ds is a true martingale. In this one-jump model it also follows directly from part (a), first for mark indicators and then by bounded-function approximation.
Take gθ​(x)=eθx, and put
rθ​=∫01​(eθy−1)dy={(eθ−1)/θ−1,0,​θ=0,θ=0.​
(33)
Here Lgθ​(x)/gθ​(x)=rθ​1{x=0}​. Therefore choose the previsible process
Yt​=∫0t​rθ​1{Xs−​=0}​ds=rθ​(t∧T).​

Let (At​,Bt​) be a Markov jump process on integer populations, with rates
(a,b)⟶(a−1,b) at rate κb,(a,b)⟶(a,b−1) at rate κa
(36)
when a,b>0, where κ>0. Make both axes absorbing, so the fight ends when one population reaches zero. Start at (N,bN​), with bN​=⌊αN⌋; when αN is integral this is the printed initial condition exactly. The harmless rounding convention makes the model defined for every integer N.
Use the embedded jump chain (Aj​,Bj​) rather than physical time. Its probabilities of the two losses are respectively b/(a+b) and a/(a+b), independently of κ. It reaches an axis after at most N+bN​≤2N steps; hold it constant thereafter. Put
Dj​=Aj2​−Bj2​.
(37)
Before absorption its two increments are −2a+1 and 2b−1, so
μj​=E[Dj​−Dj−1​∣Fj−1​]=a+bb−a​,∣μj​∣≤1.
(38)
After absorption set μj​=0. Consequently
Mj​=Dj​−D0​−∑r=1j​μr​
(39)
is a discrete-time martingale. All populations are at most N, so each martingale difference is bounded in absolute value by 2N+2≤4N. The total drift up to step 2N is at most 2N. This is the squared-population near-invariant for stochastic attrition.
The two-sided Azuma-Hoeffding inequality states that a martingale with m differences bounded by C satisfies P(∣Mm​∣≥z)≤2exp(−z2/(2mC2)). Its exponential estimate follows by applying the conditional Hoeffding lemma to each centered difference, iterating, and optimizing the exponential Markov bound. With m=2N and C=4N it gives
P(∣M2N​∣≥ηN2)≤2exp(−η2N/64).
(40)
Let c=1−α2​>0, and let XN=A2N​/N be the final proportion. If the first population survives, then the second is zero and D2N​/N2=(XN)2. Hence ∣XN−c∣>δ implies
∣D2N​−c2N2∣=N2∣XN−c∣(XN+c)>cδN2.
(41)
If the first population is extinguished, XN=0 and D2N​≤0. This can satisfy the indicated deviation event only when δ<c, and then ∣D2N​−c2N2∣≥c2N2>cδN2. Thus the same implication holds in both cases.
The initial rounding error obeys ∣D0​−c2N2∣=∣α2N2−bN2​∣≤2N. Combining it with the drift bound shows that the deviation event forces
∣M2N​∣>cδN2−4N≥21​cδN2
(42)
for all sufficiently large N. Set η=cδ/2 in the preceding concentration inequality. We conclude
P(∣XN−1−α2​∣>δ)≤2e−(1−α2)δ2N/256​
(43)
for sufficiently large N, and therefore
N→∞limsup​N1​logP(∣XN−1−α2​∣>δ)≤−256(1−α2)δ2​<0.​
(44)
The symmetric stochastic attrition process thus has exponential concentration of terminal stochastic attrition survivors. The deterministic equations conserve a2−b2 and predict the same surviving fraction, but the martingale argument supplies the stronger exponential probability estimate required here.
