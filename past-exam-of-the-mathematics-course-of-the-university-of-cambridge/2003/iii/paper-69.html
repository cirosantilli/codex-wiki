For a continuous function f on [0,1], its Bernstein polynomial is
Bn​(f,x)=∑j=0n​f(j/n)(jn​)xj(1−x)n−j.
(1)
The weights are nonnegative and, by the binomial theorem, sum to (x+1−x)n=1. Thus ∣Bn​(f,x)∣≤∑j​∣f(j/n)∣(jn​)xj(1−x)n−j≤∥f∥∞​ at every x∈[0,1], including the endpoints. Taking the supremum norm gives
∥Bn​f∥∞​≤∥f∥∞​.​
(2)
In particular, Bn​ is a linear operator of norm one, since it preserves the constant function one.

Fix m and take n≥m. Comparing the two products defining fnm​ and the monomial gm​(x)=xm, change one factor at a time. Every factor has absolute value at most one on [0,1], so the triangle inequality gives
∥fnm​−gm​∥∞​≤∑r=0m−1​nr​=2nm(m−1)​,∣fnm​(1)−1∣≤2nm(m−1)​.
(4)

Let p∈A, r=f−p, E=∥r∥∞​ and M={x∈[0,1]:∣r(x)∣=E}. The Kolmogorov criterion for uniform approximation says
p is best⟺∀h∈A, x∈Mmin​r(x)h(x)≤0.​
(13)
If E=0, p=f is already best and the criterion holds. Suppose E>0. If q=p+h were strictly better, then ∣r(x)−h(x)∣<∣r(x)∣=E on M, which forces r(x)h(x)>0 there. Conversely, if a direction h has rh>0 everywhere on M, compactness gives a positive lower bound on this product. On a sufficiently small neighborhood of M, r and h therefore have matching signs with ∣h∣ bounded away from zero and ∣r∣ bounded away from zero. On its compact complement, ∣r∣≤E−δ for some δ>0. Choose ε>0 so small that ε∥h∥∞​<δ and that subtracting εh does not cross zero on that neighborhood. Then ∥r−εh∥∞​<E, so p is not a best uniform approximation. This proves both directions.

The Chebyshev alternation theorem states that p∈Pn​ is a best uniform approximation to real f precisely when there are n+2 increasingly ordered points x0​<⋯<xn+1​ with
f(xi​)−p(xi​)=η(−1)iE,η∈{−1,1},E=∥f−p∥∞​.
(14)
For sufficiency, an improving polynomial direction h would have to share these alternating signs, by the Kolmogorov criterion for uniform approximation. The intermediate value theorem would give at least n+1 distinct roots of a polynomial between consecutive points. A nonzero polynomial of degree at most n cannot do that. If E=0, no improvement is possible anyway.
For necessity, suppose E>0 and the active set M has no alternating sequence of length n+2. Its positive-error and negative-error subsets are disjoint compact sets, hence separated by a positive distance. Reading them from left to right splits M into finitely many consecutive sign blocks. If there are L blocks, choosing one point per block gives an alternating sequence, so L≤n+1. Choose zj​ in the gap between each consecutive pair of blocks. The polynomial h(x)=C∏j=1L−1​(x−zj​) has degree at most n; choose the sign of C to match the error on the first block. Its sign then matches the error on every block, and it is nonzero throughout M. Thus rh>0 on M, contradicting the Kolmogorov criterion for uniform approximation. There must therefore be n+2 alternating extrema.

Choose a best uniform approximation pn​∈Pn​. It exists because this vector subspace is a finite-dimensional vector space and a minimizing sequence qj​ is bounded: ∥qj​∥∞​≤∥f∥∞​+∥f−qj​∥∞​. Finite-dimensional equivalence of norms then supplies a convergent subsequence of coefficients. If En​(f)>0, the Chebyshev alternation theorem gives n+2 alternating error extrema. The intermediate value theorem supplies a zero xn,k​ of f−pn​ in each of the n+1 intervening open intervals. These interpolation nodes are distinct, and uniqueness of polynomial interpolation gives
ℓn​(f)=pn​,∥ℓn​(f)−f∥∞​=En​(f)⟶0​.
(15)
The convergence follows from the Weierstrass approximation theorem. If En​(f)=0, choose any n+1 distinct nodes: the polynomial interpolant is still f=pn​. This constructs interpolation nodes from best uniform approximation; the nodes depend on f, and no universal node system for all continuous functions is asserted.

∥σn−1​f−f∥∞​≤π2M​∫0π​tαFn−1​(t)dt.

The Markov interlacing lemma says that differentiation preserves interlacing roots of polynomials. More explicitly, if two degree-d real polynomials have ordered real roots satisfying a1​≤b1​≤a2​≤⋯≤ad​≤bd​, their kth derivatives have ordered roots aj(k)​,bj(k)​ satisfying
a1(k)​≤b1(k)​≤a2(k)​≤⋯≤ad−k(k)​≤bd−k(k)​,0≤k<d.
(19)
The analogous alternating order is preserved for polynomials of consecutive degrees. Common roots are allowed through weak inequalities; strictly interlacing simple roots give strict inequalities. When a derivative is constant, its root list is empty.

Order the interpolation nodes as t0​<⋯<tn​, as required by their alternating nodal data. Write w(x)=∏j=0n​(x−tj​) and ℓi​(x)=w(x)/[(x−ti​)w′(ti​)] for the Lagrange cardinal polynomials. Differentiating Lagrange interpolation gives
p(k)(x)=∑i=0n​p(ti​)ℓi(k)​(x),Mk​(x)=∑i=0n​∣ℓi(k)​(x)∣.
(20)
The upper bound is the triangle inequality; equality follows by choosing each independent nodal value to be the sign of its weight. Thus this is the nodal derivative norm from Lagrange cardinal polynomials.
To identify the maximizing signs, put Pi​=w/(x−ti​), a monic polynomial of degree n. Since signw′(ti​)=(−1)n−i,
(−1)iℓi(k)​(x)=∣w′(ti​)∣(−1)nPi(k)​(x)​.
(21)
For 0≤k<n, let ξi,j​ be the ordered roots of Pi(k)​, 1≤j≤n−k. The roots of Pi​ decrease componentwise as i increases: deleting a later node replaces one retained node by an earlier one. The monotonicity of polynomial critical points in their roots, iterated k times, therefore gives ξn,j​≤⋯≤ξ0,j​. Moreover, Pn​ and P0​ weakly interlace, so the Markov interlacing lemma gives ξ0,j​≤ξn,j+1​. Together,
ξn,j​≤ξn−1,j​≤⋯≤ξ0,j​≤ξn,j+1​.
(22)
These root bands have disjoint interiors. At a fixed x, all but at most one band lie wholly on one side of x. In the possible remaining band, ξi,j​ moves monotonically as i increases. Since the leading coefficients of all Pi(k)​ are positive, the sequence Pi(k)​(x) has at most one sign change after zero entries are ignored. The same holds for (−1)iℓi(k)​(x). If k=n, every Pi(n)​=n!>0, so the conclusion also holds.
The maximizing nodal signs can consequently be chosen as ±(−1)i throughout, or as ±(−1)i before one index s and ∓(−1)i from that index onward. Zero weights can be assigned either sign to complete such a pattern. These are precisely the nodal values of ±p∗ or ±qs​, proving
Mk​(x)=∣p∗(k)(x)∣orMk​(x)=∣qs(k)​(x)∣ for some 1≤s≤n​.
(23)
The argument works for all real x, hence in particular for [−1,1]. For k>n every derivative vanishes and the result is immediate.

Assume the spline knot sequence is ordered and its relevant support spans satisfy ti+k​>ti​. For distinct knots, the divided difference is a finite linear combination of evaluations, so it commutes with integration. With u∈[a,b],
∫ab​(u−t)+k−1​dt=∫au​(u−t)k−1dt=k(u−a)k​.
(24)
Taking the order-k divided difference in u gives
∫ab​Mi​(t)dt=k[ti​,…,ti+k​]k(u−a)k​=1​.
(25)
Indeed, the order-k divided difference of a degree-k polynomial is its leading coefficient, here 1/k. This proves the unit-integral normalization of a B-spline. Admissible repeated knots follow by coalescing knots, with the usual one-sided endpoint conventions.

Write Ni,k​ for the order-k B-spline. At order one, Ni,1​=1[ti​,ti+1​)​, and the finite sum is one on its basic knot interval. For larger order, the Cox-de Boor recurrence is
Ni,k​(t)=ti+k−1​−ti​t−ti​​Ni,k−1​(t)+ti+k​−ti+1​ti+k​−t​Ni+1,k−1​(t),
(26)
with a zero-denominator term defined as zero. Sum over i=1,…,n. For each interior lower-order index j=2,…,n, the combined coefficient is
tj+k−1​−tj​t−tj​​+tj+k−1​−tj​tj+k−1​−t​=1.
(27)
The two unmatched lower-order terms have supports [t1​,tk​] and [tn+1​,tn+k​], respectively, and vanish on the open basic interval (tk​,tn+1​). Induction therefore gives
i=1∑n​Ni,k​(t)=1,tk​≤t≤tn+1​​.
(28)
For simple knots and k≥2, continuity extends the identity to both endpoints. In the order-one case and at admissible repeated endpoint knots, use the one-sided value from the basic interval. Extending the knot sequence in both directions also proves the subpartition of unity for B-splines, 0≤∑i​Ni​≤1 everywhere: the finite sum is a subset of a nonnegative full partition. That weaker global inequality will be needed in Question 7(a).

Nonnegativity and the unit-integral normalization of a B-spline give ∣bi​∣≤∥f∥∞​∫01​Mi​=∥f∥∞​. The subpartition of unity for B-splines gives, on all of [0,1],

For distinct ordered knots, put hi​=ti+1​−ti​>0. The linear B-spline Ni​ is the triangular hat supported on [ti​,ti+2​], equal to (t−ti​)/hi​ on its rising segment and (ti+2​−t)/hi+1​ on its falling segment. Its integral-normalized partner is Mi​=2Ni​/(hi​+hi+1​). Thus
∫Ni2​=3hi​+hi+1​​,∫Ni​Ni−1​=6hi​​,∫Ni​Ni+1​=6hi+1​​.
(33)
For example, the left overlap is ∫0hi​​(u/hi​)(1−u/hi​)du=hi​/6. Nonadjacent hats have disjoint interiors of their supports, so their inner product is zero. The linear-spline mixed Gram matrix therefore has row
gii​=32​,gi,i−1​=3(hi​+hi+1​)hi​​,gi,i+1​=3(hi​+hi+1​)hi+1​​,gij​=0 (∣i−j∣≥2)​.
(34)
Retain only column indices in 1,…,n: the first or last row has no neighbor outside this range. Admissible repeated endpoint knots are obtained by a limit with zero spacings, provided hi​+hi+1​>0; a degenerate hat with zero support width is not a basis element.
