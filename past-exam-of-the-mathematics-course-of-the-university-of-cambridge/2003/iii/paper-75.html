Use a linear, lossless arterial pressure wave model with an incompressible fluid of mass density ρ, small wall displacement and negligible mean-flow advection compared with the wave speed. Let the parent vessel have equilibrium area A1​ and speed c1​. Linearizing mass conservation and axial momentum gives at​+A1​ux​=0 and ρut​=−px​. With wall compliance p=(dp/dA)a, these imply c12​=(A1​/ρ)dp/dA. A stiffer wall therefore has larger wave speed. A downstream travelling pressure wave carries flux Q+​=Y1​p+​, where Y1​=A1​/(ρc1​) is the characteristic admittance of an arterial wave; a returning wave has Q−​=−Y1​p−​.
Place the reflecting junction at x=L. Treat the daughters as outgoing-wave loads, with combined admittance Yd​=∑j​Aj​/(ρcj​) and no further returning waves. At the junction, pressure continuity and flux conservation give pI​+pR​=pT​ and Y1​(pI​−pR​)=Yd​pT​. Thus the bifurcation reflection of an arterial pressure wave has
r=pI​pR​​=Y1​+Yd​Y1​−Yd​​​.
(1)
A decrease in total area gives 0<r<1 if the parent and daughter wave speeds are equal. More generally this requires Yd​<Y1​, an assumption about both area and stiffness. To isolate the effect of ageing, assume all relevant wave speeds increase by the same factor, so their ratios and hence r stay fixed. Unchanged geometry alone does not ensure unchanged r if the vessels stiffen by different factors.
At a station x<L, put ϕ=ω(t−x/c1​) and ϑ=2ω(L−x)/c1​, the round-trip phase to the reflector. The incident pressure harmonic and its reflection give
p=PI​[cosϕ+rcos(ϕ−ϑ)],Q=Y1​PI​[cosϕ−rcos(ϕ−ϑ)].
(2)
For time convention eiϕ, the two phasors are P=1+re−iϑ and V=1−re−iϑ. Their maximum times are shifted by −argP/ω and −argV/ω, respectively. Hence the pressure-flow phase lag from one arterial reflection is
Δt=ω1​atan2(2rsinϑ,1−r2)​.
(3)
Indeed V/P has the argument of 1−r2+2irsinϑ. On the branch 0<ϑ<π, this phase lag is positive: the pressure maximum follows the flow maximum. There is no cycle-wrap ambiguity on this branch because 1−r2>0.
The age trend need not have the same sign at every station. Differentiating the phase gives
dϑd(ωΔt)​=(1−r2)2+4r2sin2ϑ2r(1−r2)cosϑ​.
(4)
Increasing c1​ decreases ϑ. At a distal station with 0<ϑ<π/2, the lag therefore decreases. At a more proximal, ascending-aortic station with π/2<ϑ<π, it increases. Thus one sufficient set of assumptions reproducing both trends is
0<c1​2ω(L−xdistal​)​<2π​<c1​2ω(L−xproximal​)​<π​.
(5)
As long as ageing keeps the stations in these ranges, the distal lag falls while the proximal lag rises. The effect is interference between pressure waves that add and flow waves that subtract, not merely the shortening of one travel time. The lag is largest at a round-trip phase of π/2.
This is a conditional explanation, with fixed representative harmonic frequency, fixed reflector location, negligible attenuation, negligible additional reflections and common stiffness scaling. The given stiffness and geometry facts alone do not specify the phase ranges, so they do not force either age trend without these extra assumptions. Here the two stations lie on the incident-plus-reflected segment. A station beyond the sole junction in an ideal outgoing daughter has only a transmitted wave and zero pressure-flow lag in this model. Actual peripheral measurements beyond that junction require returning waves from further junctions or terminal loads; a single-reflector calculation cannot establish their lag. Non-sinusoidal waveforms, distributed viscosity, mean flow and differently evolving reflection coefficients can also shift peak times. These limitations matter when applying the harmonic explanation to a real arterial network.

Scale axial distance with λh^ and normal velocity with U^/λ, while the dimensionless axial velocity has scale U^. Let r=R+y; keep R>0 fixed on the asymptotic scales. In the core, the ratio of transverse viscous terms to axial convective terms is of order λ/Re. A small indentation and the associated displacement create tangential slip of order ϵ at the edges of the wall layers.
For a layer of relative thickness δ≪ϵ, that slip makes axial convection of order ϵ2/λ, while transverse viscous diffusion is of order ϵ/(Reδ2). Balancing them gives
δ∼(Reϵλ​)1/2,δ≪ϵ⟺Re≫λϵ−3.
(20)
Unsteady diffusion gives the equivalent estimate δ∼(ReSt)−1/2 when λSt=O(ϵ). Thus a consistent regime supporting thin viscous boundary layers and an inviscid core is
ϵ≪1,λ≫1,Re≫λϵ−3,λSt=O(ϵ)​.
(21)
At a location where both imposed unsteadiness and slip vanish, a shear-dominated entrance balance can instead have thickness (λ/Re)1/3; the same strict Reynolds-number condition keeps even that scale below ϵ. The reduction below assumes that no wall-layer thickening invalidates the stipulated small thickness. The normal flux displaced by a slip layer is of order ϵδ, smaller than the retained ϵ2 core flux. Tangential no slip is imposed within these layers, not directly on the leading inviscid core.

Work in the body frame and approximate the narrow annulus locally by a planar gap. Let y=0 be the cylindrical wall, moving at −U in this frame, and y=h(x) the stationary cell surface. If d≤0, the stated leading positive-gap branch does not exist. Without the absolute-pressure relation, the problem gives a family parameterized by Q/U, rather than a unique Δp(U). Errors from finite L, higher powers of λ and higher powers of C have been omitted consistently in this leading approximation. Resolving the displayed O(C) correction to b−2 requires the omitted finite-domain and higher-λ corrections to be smaller than C; otherwise only its leading value is justified.
