Use ℓ=L/cp​, ϵ=Ds​/Dl​​ and δ=Dl​/κ​, with 0<kD​<1. Equal reference densities and constant material properties are implicit in these diffusion balances. Put a(t)=2λDl​t​ and Ta​=−mCa​. The four similarity fields are
Ts​(x,t)Tl​(x,t)Cs​(x,t)Cl​(x,t)​=Ts​+(Ta​−Ts​)erfc(−δλ)erfc[−x/(2κt​)]​,=Tl​+(Ta​−Tl​)erfc(δλ)erfc[x/(2κt​)]​,=Cs​+(kD​Ca​−Cs​)erfc(−λ/ϵ)erfc[−x/(2Ds​t​)]​,=Ca​erfc(λ)erfc[x/(2Dl​t​)]​,​​x<a,x>a,x<a,x>a.​
(14)
Here the same letters Ts​,Tl​,Cs​ on the right denote their far-field values. Substitution into the heat and solute diffusion equations verifies the fields; their interface values and far-field limits verify every boundary condition. The concentration has an equilibrium jump from kD​Ca​ to Ca​, whereas temperature is continuous.
The interfacial conservation laws are
κ(Ts,x​−Tl,x​)=ℓa˙,Ds​Cs,x​−Dl​Cl,x​=(1−kD​)Ca​a˙.
(15)
For example,
Ts,x​(a)=πκt​erfc(−δλ)(Ta​−Ts​)e−δ2λ2​,Tl,x​(a)=πκt​erfc(δλ)(Tl​−Ta​)e−δ2λ2​.
(16)
Since a˙=λDl​/t​, dividing the two jump conditions by a˙ gives, for nonzero λ,
ℓ=−F(−δλ)Ta​−Ts​​−F(δλ)Tl​−Ta​​,(1−kD​)Ca​=−F(−λ/ϵ)kD​Ca​−Cs​​+F(λ)Ca​​,​
(17)
where F(z)=π​zez2erfcz. At λ=0, use the undivided flux conditions or the continuous limit, rather than divide by zero. The liquidus relation and the segregation coefficient also give the solidus TS​(C)=−mC/kD​.
For the thermal expansion, write z=δλ and use
F(z)=π​z−2z2+O(z3),F(z)1​=π​z1​+π2​+O(z).
(18)
Keeping the constant terms in these reciprocals is essential for the full first-order answer. The result is
Ta​=2Tl​+Ts​​+2π​​δλ[ℓ+π2​(Tl​−Ts​)]+O(δ3(∣ℓ∣+∣Tl​−Ts​∣)).​
(19)
The remainder has this form for bounded λ; a looser O(δ2) statement is also valid in the distinguished scaling ℓ=O(δ−1). Dropping the temperature-difference term while claiming first-order accuracy for fixed ℓ would be incorrect.
Solving the solute balance algebraically gives
Ca​=(1−kD​)+kD​/F(−λ/ϵ)−1/F(λ)Cs​/F(−λ/ϵ)​.
(20)
For fixed positive λ, F(−λ/ϵ)→−∞, and Ca​→0, with a positive exponentially small correction. For fixed negative λ, F(−λ/ϵ)→1, and
Ca​=F(λ)−1Cs​F(λ)​,λ<0.​
(21)
It falls from Cs​ as λ→−∞ to zero as λ→0−. The outer positive branch is zero. For finite ϵ the transition is rounded over ∣λ∣=O(ϵ); the exact zero-speed limit is Ca​(0)=ϵCs​/(1+kD​ϵ), not an actual concentration discontinuity.
To count speeds, take the leading distinguished thermal balance, set Σ=Tl​+Ts​ and K=π​δℓ, and combine Ta​=−mCa​ with the thermal result:
Σ=−2mCa​(λ)−Kλ.​
(22)
This is the intended leading approximation when δℓ=O(mCs​) and Tl​−Ts​=O(1). The full first-order expression instead replaces K by π​δ[ℓ+2(Tl​−Ts​)/π]. Thus the printed threshold is asymptotic, not an exact criterion for finite δ,ϵ.
For r=−λ>0, put H(r)=π​rer2erfc(−r). The small-solid-diffusivity melting concentration is Cs​H/(1+H), so
Σ(r)=Kr−2mCs​1+H(r)H(r)​,Σ′(r)=K−2mCs​[1+H(r)]2H′(r)​.
(23)
The second term decreases strictly from 2mCs​π​ to zero. One can check the strict decrease without guessing its shape. Set A(r)=π​er2erfc(−r), so A′=2rA+2, A≥π​+2r, and H=rA. Direct differentiation gives
2H′2−(1+H)H′′=2{(1+r2+2r4)A2+r(4r2−1)A+2r2−2}>0.
(24)
The bracket increases with A on r≥0. Substituting its lower bound yields a polynomial with constant coefficient π−2>0 and all other coefficients positive. Hence [H′/(1+H)2]′<0, as claimed.
If δℓ>2mCs​, then K>2mCs​π​ and Σ(r) increases from zero to infinity. The freezing branch has Σ=−Kλ<0 and is also monotone. Together these give one speed for each imposed sum within the approximation. If 0<δℓ<2mCs​, the melting branch initially decreases, has exactly one minimum and then increases to infinity. For sums between that minimum and zero there are two melting speeds plus one freezing speed: three solutions. At the lower fold there are two distinct speeds, one being a double solution. At zero the limiting outer branches join; finite solid diffusivity rounds the join into a nearby fold. Away from the fold values the other sums have one solution. At the threshold itself the initial slope vanishes; it is the transition between these cases.
For Σ>0, the solution is on the melting branch λ<0. Its thermal layers have scale κt​=Dl​t​/δ, much larger than the liquid-solute scale Dl​t​. Because the front is retreating, the solid solute layer is thinner still:
solid solute thickness∼∣a˙∣Ds​​=∣λ∣ϵ2Dl​t​​.​
(25)
This follows by expanding the large-positive-argument complementary error function ratio on the solid side: near a, its decay length is Ds​/∣a˙∣. Using simply Ds​t​ would miss the retreating-front compression of that layer.
Across this narrow layer, solid concentration drops from almost Cs​ to kD​Ca​, while temperature stays almost Ta​. At the interface Ta​=TS​(kD​Ca​). Moving slightly into the solid increases its concentration toward Cs​, lowering its solidus to −mCs​/kD​, whereas the temperature has barely changed. Since Ca​<Cs​ and kD​<1, Ta​=−mCa​>−mCs​/kD​. Thus an adjacent region can satisfy T>TS​(C): constitutional superheating. Its existence is especially clear when far-field solid is initially stable but heat diffuses across the narrow composition layer.
The phase-diagram path goes from (Cs​,Ts​) through a broad thermal rise, then nearly horizontally toward (kD​Ca​,Ta​) across the thin solid diffusion layer. At the interface it traverses the isothermal equilibrium tie line to (Ca​,Ta​). On the liquid side concentration falls to zero over the liquid-solute layer, while the remaining thermal adjustment to Tl​ takes place over the much wider heat layer. The path of the depleted solid approaches the solidus from its superheated side. That region should melt at additional sites or develop a two-phase mushy layer; the original single-boundary similarity construction is then a mathematical branch rather than a physically stable description of the entire sample.
