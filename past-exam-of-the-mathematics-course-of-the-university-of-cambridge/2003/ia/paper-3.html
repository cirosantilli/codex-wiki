Let H,K be the normal subgroups of orders 3,5. Their intersection is a subgroup of both, so Lagrange's theorem makes its order divide both 3 and 5; hence H∩K={1}. For h∈H and k∈K, normality shows that hkh−1k−1 belongs to both H and K. It must be the identity, so hk=kh. This proves the needed instance of normal subgroups of coprime order commute.
Choose nonidentity h∈H and k∈K. Their orders are 3 and 5, since these orders divide the respective prime subgroup orders. If (hk)m=1, commutativity gives hm=k−m∈H∩K, so 3∣m and 5∣m. Conversely (hk)15=1. Thus the product of commuting elements of coprime order gives an element hk of order 15.
For the counterexample, take the dihedral group of symmetries of a regular pentagon:
D5​=⟨r,s∣r5=s2=1, srs=r−1⟩​.
(4)
Its ten elements are rj and srj, 0≤j<5. Nonidentity rotations have order 5, and (srj)2=r−jrj=1, so all reflections have order 2. There is no element of order 10.

Suppose ϕ1​,ϕ2​ are two sufficiently regular solutions with the same Dirichlet boundary condition, and put w=ϕ1​−ϕ2​. Then Δw=0 in V and w=0 on its boundary. Apply the divergence theorem to w∇w:
∫V​(∣∇w∣2+wΔw)dV=∫S​w∂n​wdS=0.
(6)
Thus ∇w=0 throughout V, so w is constant on each connected component. Its zero boundary values force every such constant to be zero. This proves uniqueness of the Dirichlet solution, without assuming its existence.
For a prescribed Neumann boundary condition, the difference instead has ∂n​w=0. The same energy argument still gives ∇w=0, but does not fix the constant. Consequently the solution is unique up to an additive constant on each connected component. A value at one point, or the integral mean, fixes the constant on a connected region. There is also a necessary compatibility condition: if g=∂n​ϕ on S, the divergence theorem gives
∫S​gdS=∫V​ΔϕdV=0​.
(7)
It is required on each component if the region is disconnected. Data failing this condition admit no harmonic solution. This is the homogeneous-source instance of the Neumann Poisson problem.

Write the row components as Eai​=(ea​)i​. Expanding the cross product in components,
(e1​×e2​)⋅e3​=ϵijk​(e1​)i​(e2​)j​(e3​)k​

equals the alternating six-term expansion of the determinant of E. Therefore (e1​×e2​)⋅e3​=detE.
Geometrically, ∣e1​×e2​∣ is the area of the parallelogram spanned by e1​,e2​, and its direction is perpendicular to that plane. A nonzero scalar triple product means both that this area is nonzero and that e3​ has a nonzero component normal to the plane. Thus the vectors are linearly independent and form a basis of R3, without needing to be orthogonal. Equivalently, their parallelepiped has nonzero volume.
The entries of EE^T are ea​⋅e^b​, so the required duality is exactly EE^T=I. Since E is invertible, this has the unique solution
E^T=E−1,E^=E−T​.
(16)
Its determinant is 1/detE=0, so its rows are again a basis. Put Δ=(e1​×e2​)⋅e3​. The cross product e2​×e3​ is perpendicular to e2​,e3​, and its dot product with e1​ is Δ. Consequently the reciprocal basis is
e^1​=Δe2​×e3​​,e^2​=Δe3​×e1​​,e^3​=Δe1​×e2​​​.
(17)
These vectors have precisely the required dot products, so uniqueness identifies them with the rows of E−T.
For the final request, an orthonormal basis of rows gives EET=I. If instead the reciprocal rows are orthonormal, then E^E^T=I and substitution of E^=E−T gives E−TE−1=I, again implying EET=I. Thus in either case E is an orthogonal matrix and E^=E.
The further printed claim that E must be a rotation matrix is false without an orientation assumption. The matrix E=diag(1,1,−1) has orthonormal rows and is its own reciprocal basis matrix, but detE=−1: it is a reflection. By the orientation of an orthonormal reciprocal basis, the corrected conclusion is
E∈O(3),E is a rotation matrix if and only if detE=+1.​
(18)
A right-handed original ordered basis supplies exactly this missing condition.

If all three points are finite, define the Möbius transformation
g(z)=β−αβ−γ​z−γz−α​​.
(19)
Its numerator vanishes at α, its pole is at γ, and substitution of β gives 1. The three points are distinct, so the numerator and denominator are not proportional and the associated 2×2 matrix has nonzero determinant.
If one point is infinite, the limiting formulas are
infinite pointα=∞β=∞γ=∞​g(z)(β−γ)/(z−γ)(z−α)/(z−γ)(z−α)/(β−α)​​​.
(20)
In each case the finite values and the limit at infinity give g(α)=0, g(β)=1, g(γ)=∞, and the corresponding determinant is nonzero. Distinctness permits at most one of the points to be infinite. Hence every ordered triple of distinct points on the Riemann sphere can be normalized to (0,1,∞).

Each element of H permutes the three-point set, giving a group homomorphism ρ:H→S3​. To prove injectivity, let an element fix all three points. Conjugate it by the normalizing Möbius transformation from part (i). The conjugate fixes 0,1,∞. A Möbius transformation z↦(az+b)/(cz+d) fixing 0 has b=0, and fixing infinity has c=0. It is then z↦kz; fixing 1 forces k=1. Thus the conjugate, and hence the original transformation, is the identity. The kernel of a group homomorphism is trivial.
For surjectivity, let (x1​,x2​,x3​)=(α,β,γ) and choose any permutation σ. Let g normalize the ordered triple (x1​,x2​,x3​) and let gσ​ normalize (xσ(1)​,xσ(2)​,xσ(3)​). Then gσ−1​g sends xj​ to xσ(j)​, lies in H, and induces the chosen permutation. Thus ρ is onto and
H≅S3​​.
(21)
For the normalized set, the six Möbius transformations permuting three points are z, 1−z, 1/z, 1/(1−z), z/(z−1), and (z−1)/z. Conjugating by g gives the corresponding six maps for the original set.

The characteristic polynomial has only the eigenvalue μ. If A is diagonalizable, there is an invertible matrix P and a diagonal matrix D with A=PDP−1. Every diagonal entry of D is an eigenvalue, hence equals μ. Thus D=μI and A=P(μI)P−1=μI. Conversely, μI is already diagonal in every basis. Hence
A is diagonalizable⟺A=μI​.
(24)
This argument uses the existence of an eigenvector basis rather than merely the repeated root of the polynomial; repeated eigenvalues alone do not imply diagonalizability.

Orient the triangular boundary from P=(1,0,0) to Q=(0,1,0) to R=(0,0,1) and back to P. This orientation corresponds to the positive normal vector (Q−P)×(R−P)=(1,1,1). The cyclic coordinate rotation (x,y,z)↦(z,x,y) sends the three directed edges to one another and transforms the vector field by the same rotation. Since rotations preserve dot products, the three edge contributions to the line integral are equal.
On the first edge use x=1−t, y=t, z=0, 0≤t≤1. Then
A⋅dtdx​=(−t2,(1−t)2,t2−(1−t)2)⋅(−1,1,0)=t2+(1−t)2.
(25)
Its integral is 2/3, so the required circulation is
∮C​A⋅dx=3⋅32​=2​.
(26)
For a sufficiently smooth vector field on an oriented piecewise smooth surface T, with induced boundary orientation on C=∂T, the Stokes theorem states
∫T​(∇×A)⋅ndS=∮C​A⋅dx.
(27)
Therefore the requested surface integral, with all normal components positive, is also 2.
To verify it directly, parametrize the triangle by r(y,z)=(1−y−z,y,z) on y≥0, z≥0, y+z≤1. Its two tangent vectors are ry​=(−1,1,0) and rz​=(−1,0,1), giving the vector area element
dS=(ry​×rz​)dydz=(1,1,1)dydz​.

Differentiation of the vector field gives ∇×A=2(y+z,z+x,x+y). On the triangle x+y+z=1, so its dot product with (1,1,1) is 4. Consequently
∫T​(∇×A)⋅dS=∫01​∫01−y​4dzdy=2,
(29)
confirming both the magnitude and the orientation sign.

For a sufficiently regular vector field F on a bounded region V with piecewise smooth boundary S, the divergence theorem states
∫V​∇⋅FdV=∫S​F⋅ndS,
(42)
where n is the outward unit normal vector. Apply it to F=fk for an arbitrary constant vector k. Since ∇⋅(fk)=k⋅∇f, it gives
k⋅∫V​∇fdV=k⋅∫S​fndS.
(43)
Equality for every constant k proves the vector gradient form of the divergence theorem:
∫V​∇fdV=∫S​fdS​.
(44)
Applying the same divergence theorem directly to G, with ∇⋅G=ρ, proves the flux law
∫S​G⋅dS=∫V​ρdV​.
(45)
This is the Gauss flux law for the prescribed source density, obtained directly from the divergence theorem. For the piecewise source in this problem the divergence relation is understood away from the interface, or almost everywhere; applying the theorem separately to the two sides gives the same law because the normal field is continuous across the interface and the internal boundary fluxes cancel.
For the radial field, its normal component on a sphere of radius r is the constant G(r), so its total flux is 4πr2G(r). For 0<r≤a the enclosed source is 4πρ0​r3/3; for r>a it is 4πρ0​a3/3. Thus the origin-regular spherical Poisson flux law gives
G(x)=⎩⎨⎧​3ρ0​​x,3r3ρ0​a3​x,​r≤a,r>a.​​
(46)
The value at r=0 is the continuous value zero. A singular inverse-square radial addition is excluded by the source equation throughout space, including the origin: it would add a point source there.
Since G=∇f, radial integration gives fr​=ρ0​r/3 inside and fr​=ρ0​a3/(3r2) outside. The decay condition fixes the exterior constant, giving f=−ρ0​a3/(3r) for r≥a. Integrating inside gives f=ρ0​r2/6+C0​, and matching at r=a yields C0​=−ρ0​a2/2. Hence the potential of a uniform spherical source is
f(x)=⎩⎨⎧​6ρ0​​(r2−3a2),−3rρ0​a3​,​r≤a,r≥a.​​
(47)
Both f and its radial derivative are continuous at a; there is no surface source hidden in the matching.
For any ball centred at the origin, the gradient G(x) is odd under x↦−x, so its volume integral vanishes by symmetry. On the bounding sphere, f is constant and outward normals at antipodal points are opposite, so ∫S​fndS=0 as well. Both sides of the vector gradient identity are therefore zero, for radii below, above, or equal to a.
