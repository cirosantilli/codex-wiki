Between the two saddles, its left arc has x<0 and travels upward from S−​ to S+​; its right arc has x>0 and travels downward from S+​ to S−​. These two heteroclinic orbits bound the central eye. Trajectories inside the eye are closed periodic orbits and do not tend to the centre as t→∞.
More explicitly, a level K=C has
x2=y4−y2+C.
(46)
For 0<C<1/4, its bounded central component is periodic, while its exterior components turn at ∣y∣=(1+1−4C​)/2​ and then escape. For C>1/4, x never vanishes and the two branches run from one vertical infinity to the other. Levels C≤0 have only exterior nonconstant components, apart from the origin at C=0.
On the upper outer separatrix, the x>0 branch arrives from positive infinity and approaches S+​ in forward infinite time; the x<0 branch leaves S+​ towards positive infinity. On the lower outer separatrix, the x<0 branch arrives from negative infinity and approaches S−​, while the x>0 branch leaves towards negative infinity. Approaching a saddle along its stable manifold is exponential at rate 2​. A trajectory close to but off a separatrix can spend a long time near the saddle before completing a periodic orbit or escaping.
The escape is finite-time blowup, not an asymptote at t=∞. On any escaping level, ∣x∣=y4−y2+C​∼y2, so the remaining travel time is bounded by a convergent integral proportional to ∫∞dy/y2. Only equilibria, periodic trajectories and trajectories tending to saddles have a future defined for all time in this conservative portrait.
For small positive α, the equilibria remain in place, but the origin's eigenvalues become α/4±i1−α2/16​, so it becomes a repelling focus. The saddles retain one eigenvalue of each sign, α/4±2+α2/16​. Since K˙≥0, and its time integral is strictly positive on every nonconstant trajectory, trajectories cross K-contours towards larger values. Near the centre this means outward spiralling. No nonconstant periodic orbit can remain, and the former saddle-to-saddle connections at equal K=1/4 are broken. Stable saddle trajectories approach from K<1/4; unstable ones depart into K>1/4. Generic central trajectories eventually escape, while the exceptional stable saddle manifolds still approach their saddles.

Independence requires each joint probability to equal the product of the relevant marginals. In particular it implies d=(c+d)(b+d), equivalent to ad=bc.
Conversely let p=c+d and q=b+d, and assume ad=bc. The covariance calculation gives d=pq. Then
c=p−d=p(1−q),b=q−d=(1−p)q,a=1−b−c−d=(1−p)(1−q).

(55)

An uneventful day leaves one aphid, a one-offspring day leaves the parent plus its offspring, and a two-offspring day leaves three aphids. Death leaves none. Thus the daily descendant counts are 0,1,2,3 with probabilities (1−q)t, q, (1−q)r, (1−q)s, respectively. The mean and probability generating function are
EX1​=q+(1−q)(2r+3s),G(z)=(1−q)t+qz+(1−q)rz2+(1−q)sz3.​
(56)
Independence across individuals and days makes the daily population a Galton-Watson process, with each surviving parent counted as one of the next day's descendants.
The standard Galton-Watson extinction fixed point result states that the eventual extinction probability of a branching process e from one ancestor is the smallest root in [0,1] of G(e)=e. Moreover a nondegenerate offspring law of mean at most one has extinction probability of a branching process one. Here, since q<1, the fixed-point equation reduces to
e=t+re2+se3,​
(57)
which contains no q. This proves laziness invariance of branching extinction: uneventful days change the timescale, not eventual extinction. The excluded case q=1 would keep one aphid forever.
If 2r+3s≤1, the offspring mean is at most one. In this parameter range t>0: if t=0, then r+s=1 would imply 2r+3s≥2. Thus the offspring law is not deterministically one, and the extinction criterion applies. Extinction is certain.
