Compose permutations from right to left. Tracking the four symbols through the two permutation cycles gives 1↦2↦1 and 3↦4↦3. Thus the cycle decomposition of a permutation is
(123)(234)=(12)(34).​
(1)

If y=gxg−1, cancellation of successive g−1g factors gives yk=gxkg−1 for every positive integer k. Hence yk is the identity element exactly when xk is, proving that conjugate group elements have the same order of a group element. For permutations, the sign group homomorphism similarly gives
sgn(gxg−1)=sgn(g)sgn(x)sgn(g)−1=sgn(x).
(2)
Thus conjugate permutations have the same sign of a permutation.
The converse is false: permutation order and sign do not determine conjugacy. In the symmetric group S6​, take x=(123) and y=(123)(456). Both have order three and positive sign. However, x fixes three symbols whereas y fixes none. If x fixes a symbol j, then gxg−1 fixes g(j), and applying the inverse conjugation gives a bijection between the fixed-symbol sets. Their different fixed-point counts therefore prove that x,y are not conjugate. Equivalently their cycle types 313 and 32 differ. Equal order and sign are necessary, but not sufficient, for conjugacy.

A real function is differentiable at an interior point x when the finite limit
f′(x)=limh→0, h=0​hf(x+h)−f(x)​
(7)
exists, with h restricted so x+h lies in its domain. In quantified form, for every ε>0 there is δ>0 such that 0<∣h∣<δ implies that the difference quotient differs from f′(x) by less than ε.
To prove differentiability implies continuity, the convergent difference quotient is bounded by ∣f′(x)∣+1 for sufficiently small nonzero h. Thus ∣f(x+h)−f(x)∣≤(∣f′(x)∣+1)∣h∣→0. This is exactly continuity at x.
At zero, the particular function has difference quotient hsin(1/h). The bound ∣hsin(1/h)∣≤∣h∣ and the squeeze theorem give
f′(0)=0.​
(8)
For x=0, the product rule and chain rule give
f′(x)=2xsin(1/x)−cos(1/x).
(9)
At xk​=1/(2πk) this equals −1, although xk​→0 and f′(0)=0. At yk​=1/((2k+1)π) it equals +1. Hence the derivative has no limit at zero and is not continuous there. This demonstrates that differentiability does not imply continuity of the derivative, even though the function itself is continuous.

The radius of convergence of a power series ∑n≥0​an​zn is the unique R∈[0,∞] for which the series converges absolutely when ∣z∣<R and diverges when ∣z∣>R. Its kernel is the rotational symmetry group of a cube, a normal subgroup of order twenty-four. Also {I,−I} is a central subgroup of order two: central inversion preserves the cube and commutes with every matrix. Both provide affirmative examples.

Since s−1 is not an upper bound, some positive integer k satisfies k>s−1. Then the positive integer k+1>s, contradicting the definition of s. Thus the positive integers are unbounded. Rn​(t)=n!f(n)(0)tn​+n!1​∫0t​f(n+1)(x)(t−x)ndx=n!f(n)(0)tn​+Rn+1​(t).
(52)
Induction starting from n=1 therefore proves
f(t)=k=0∑n−1​k!f(k)(0)tk​+(n−1)!1​∫0t​f(n)(x)(t−x)n−1dx.​
(53)
The same calculation holds for negative t: all integrals are oriented, and the segment joining 0 and t lies in (−1,1). Thus the proof covers every stated t and every positive integer n.
Now take f(x)=log(1−x). For k≥1, differentiation gives f(k)(x)=−(k−1)!/(1−x)k, and f(0)=0. With n=N+1 and t=1/2, the Taylor formula with integral remainder becomes
log2=∑k=1N​k2k1​+JN​,JN​=∫01/2​(1−x)N+1(1/2−x)N​dx.
(54)
For 0≤x≤1/2, 0≤(1/2−x)/(1−x)≤1/2 and (1−x)−1≤2. Hence 0≤JN​≤2−N after integrating over an interval of length 1/2. Thus the logarithmic series from an integral Taylor remainder satisfies
k=1∑∞​k2k1​=log2,0≤log2−k=1∑N​k2k1​≤2−N.​
(55)
The explicit vanishing remainder establishes convergence and identifies the sum, without using a pre-existing logarithmic power series.
