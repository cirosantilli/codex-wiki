The Rao-Blackwell theorem states that if T is sufficient and U is an estimator with finite variance, then
U∗=E[U∣T]
(22)

Suppose first that every ideal of R is finitely generated. For an ascending chain
I1​⊆I2​⊆⋯,
(40)
the union I=⋃n​In​ is an ideal. Its finite generating set lies in one IN​, so In​=IN​ for all n≥N. Thus R satisfies the ascending chain condition and is a Noetherian ring.
Conversely, if an ideal I is not finitely generated, choose a1​∈I, and after choosing a1​,…,an​, choose
an+1​∈I∖(a1​,…,an​).
(41)
This creates a strictly ascending chain of ideals, contradicting Noetherianity. Hence every ideal is finitely generated.
If φ:R→S is surjective and J⊲S, then φ−1(J) is an ideal of R. If it is generated by a1​,…,am​, then J is generated by φ(a1​),…,φ(am​). Thus every ideal of S is finitely generated, so S is Noetherian.
The Hilbert basis theorem states that if R is a commutative Noetherian ring, then R[X] is Noetherian. To prove it, let I⊲R[X]. The leading coefficients of polynomials in I generate an ideal of R; choose generators that occur as leading coefficients of f1​,…,fs​∈I, and let N=maxi​degfi​. For each degree below N, the leading coefficients of members of I of at most that degree likewise form a finitely generated ideal; choose finitely many corresponding polynomials. Any f∈I of degree at least N can have its leading term cancelled by a linear combination of monomial multiples of the fi​. Repeating lowers its degree below N, where the second finite list completes the reduction. These finitely many selected polynomials generate I, proving the theorem.
For every α∈C, the ring Z[α] is Noetherian. If α is transcendental, evaluation identifies it with Z[X]. If α is algebraic, it is a quotient of Z[X]. The integers are Noetherian, the Hilbert basis theorem handles Z[X], and quotients preserve Noetherianity.
For a unique factorization domain that is not Noetherian, take
k[x1​,x2​,…].

Every polynomial and every factorization uses only finitely many variables, so existence and uniqueness of factorization reduce to a finite-variable polynomial ring, which is a UFD. But
(x1​)⊊(x1​,x2​)⊊(x1​,x2​,x3​)⊊⋯

These are ideals and In​⊊In+1​: the distance function to [−1/(n+1),1/(n+1)] belongs to In+1​ but not to In​. This strict ascending chain proves the claim.
