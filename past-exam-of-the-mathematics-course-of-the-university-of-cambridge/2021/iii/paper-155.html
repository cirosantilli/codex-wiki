A coarse embedding f:X→Y has nondecreasing control functions ρ−​,ρ+​ such that
ρ−​(dX​(x,y))≤dY​(f(x),f(y))≤ρ+​(dX​(x,y)),ρ−​(t)⟶∞.

A family is a uniform coarse embedding of a family of metric spaces when all its maps use the same controls.
For Hn​={0,1}n with the Hamming distance, the coordinate map into ℓ1n​ satisfies
∥x−y∥1​=dH​(x,y),

(3)

The relation that X is finitely representable in Y means that every finite-dimensional subspace E⊆X has, for every ε>0, a linear copy in Y of distortion below 1+ε. A superreflexive Banach space is one for which every Banach space finitely representable in it is a reflexive Banach space.
Suppose first that X is reflexive and (xi​)⊆BX​. By weak compactness, the sequence has a weak cluster point x∈BX​. The point x belongs to the weak closure of every tail convex hull, and the weak and norm closures of a convex set agree by the Hahn-Banach separation theorem. Choose a convex combination
y∈conv{xi​:1≤i≤n}
(18)
within θ/2 of x, enlarging n beyond all indices used. Then choose
z∈conv{xi​:i>n}
(19)
within θ/2 of x. It follows that ∥y−z∥<θ.
Conversely, the James reflexivity criterion in convex-block form says that a nonreflexive Banach space has a number θ>0 and a sequence (xi​)⊆BX​ such that
dist(conv{x1​,…,xn​},conv{xn+1​,xn+2​,…})≥θ
(20)
for every n. One obtains this form from the usual bidual separation proof by applying the principle of local reflexivity to each finite-dimensional stage. Such a sequence contradicts the asserted property. This proves the convex-block separation criterion for reflexivity.
Consider now the uniform finite version. If it fails for some θ>0, choose for every N a sequence (xi(N)​)i=1N​⊆BX​ for which every cut has convex-hull distance at least θ. In a free ultrapower XU​, let
ξi​=[(xi(N)​)N≥i​],
(21)
filling the finitely many missing coordinates arbitrarily. Every initial-tail pair of convex hulls of (ξi​) remains at distance at least θ, so the first criterion makes XU​ nonreflexive. Since an ultrapower is finitely representable in X, this contradicts superreflexivity.
Conversely, if X is not superreflexive, choose a nonreflexive space Y finitely representable in X. The first criterion supplies a sequence in BY​ whose convex blocks are separated by some θ>0. For each N, transfer the span of its first N vectors to X with distortion arbitrarily close to one and normalize. The resulting N-term sequence violates the uniform condition, with separation at least, say, θ/2. This proves the uniform finite convex-block criterion for superreflexivity.
A purely metric equivalent is the diamond-graph characterization of superreflexivity:
X is superreflexive⟺cX​(Dm​)⟶∞.
(22)
For sufficiency, argue contrapositively. If X is not superreflexive, the preceding uniform criterion supplies arbitrarily long unit-ball sequences whose convex hulls stay a fixed distance apart across every cut. At each replacement step in the diamond graph, map the two new branches to convex combinations on opposite sides of the corresponding cut. The upper bound follows from convexity and the lower bound from the fixed separation. The standard recursive diamond construction therefore embeds every Dm​ into X with one distortion constant independent of m. Thus divergence of the diamond distortions forces superreflexivity.

The Bourgain embedding theorem states that every n-point metric space embeds into a Hilbert space with distortion at most Clogn; the random-subset construction may be taken to have dimension O((logn)2).
We use the following finite Fréchet embedding lemma. For every integer q≥2, an n-point metric space X has subsets A1​,…,Ak​ such that
k≤Cqn1/qlogn
(23)
and, for every x,y∈X, some Ai​ satisfies
∣d(x,Ai​)−d(y,Ai​)∣≥2q−1d(x,y)​.
(24)
Here is the probabilistic proof. Put ϑ=n−1/q. For every level i=1,…,q, independently form
m=⌈24ϑ−1logn⌉
(25)
random subsets Ai,j​ by including each point with probability pi​=min{1/2,n−i/q}.
Fix x,y and put Δ=d(x,y)/(2q−1). Form q+1 open balls B0​,…,Bq​ by taking Bt​ to have radius tΔ and center x for even t and y for odd t. Consecutive balls are disjoint because their radii sum to at most (2q−1)Δ=d(x,y). Partition the cardinality interval [1,n] into
[n(i−1)/q,ni/q],i=1,…,q.
(26)
Among the q+1 cardinalities ∣Bt​∣, either two consecutive ones lie in one interval, or some consecutive pair decreases. In either case there are i,t such that, after orienting the pair appropriately,
∣Bt​∣≥n(i−1)/q,∣Bt+1​∣≤ni/q.
(27)
At level i, a random set hits Bt​ with probability at least ϑ/3 and misses the disjoint Bt+1​ with probability at least 1/4. These events are independent, and when both occur the two distance-to-set values differ by at least Δ. Thus one sample succeeds with probability at least ϑ/12. All m samples at that level fail with probability at most
(1−ϑ/12)m≤n−2.
(28)
A union bound over the fewer than n2/2 unordered pairs leaves a simultaneous successful choice. There are qm≤Cqn1/qlogn coordinates, proving the lemma.

(29)

2q−11​d(x,y)≤∥F(x)−F(y)∥∞​≤d(x,y),

Take q=⌈logn⌉. Then n1/q≤e, k=O((logn)2), and the distortion into ℓ∞k​ is O(logn). Since
