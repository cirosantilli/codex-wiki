Give each nonempty finite set Xj​ the discrete topology. The product space X=∏j​Xj​ is compact by the Tychonoff theorem. For each i≤j, the compatibility condition fij​(xj​)=xi​ defines a closed subset Cij​⊆X.
These sets have the finite intersection property. Indeed, for finitely many conditions choose an index k above every index occurring in them, choose any xk​∈Xk​, and use the transition maps from k to define all required coordinates; choose the remaining coordinates arbitrarily. Compactness therefore gives
lim​j​Xj​=⋂i≤j​Cij​=∅.
(2)
This is the nonemptiness theorem for inverse limits of finite sets.

The product ∏j​Gj​ of the finite groups with their discrete topology is a topological group under coordinatewise multiplication and inversion. The compatibility equations defining G=lim​j​Gj​ are preserved by both operations, so G is a subgroup. Their restrictions to the subspace topology on G are continuous. Hence G with its standard inverse-limit topology is a topological group.

The quotient maps define a continuous homomorphism
Φ:G⟶lim​U∈U​G/U.
(19)
Its kernel is ⋂U∈U​U={1}, because U is a neighborhood basis and G is Hausdorff. Thus Φ is injective. The standard compactness argument for inverse limits makes it surjective: a compatible family of cosets has the finite intersection property, and the corresponding closed cosets in compact G have nonempty intersection. Finally, a continuous bijection from compact G to the Hausdorff inverse limit is a homeomorphism. Hence
G≅lim​U∈U​G/U
(20)
as topological groups.
