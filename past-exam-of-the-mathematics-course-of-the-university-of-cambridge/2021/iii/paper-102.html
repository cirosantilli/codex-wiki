The space End(V) becomes a Lie algebra under the commutator
[X,Y]=XY−YX.
(1)
A Lie subalgebra L is abelian when [L,L]=0; nilpotent when its lower central series of a Lie algebra γr+1​=[L,γr​] reaches zero; and soluble when its derived series of a Lie algebra L(r+1)=[L(r),L(r)] reaches zero.
If H⊊L and L is nilpotent, choose the least r with γr​(L)⊆H. Then γr−1​(L)⊈H, while
[γr−1​(L),H]⊆γr​(L)⊆H.
(2)
Thus the normalizer of a Lie subalgebra strictly contains H. If H is maximal proper, its normalizer must be all of L, so H is an ideal. Solubility is insufficient: in the two-dimensional affine Lie algebra L=⟨x,y⟩ with [x,y]=y, the maximal subalgebra Cx is not an ideal.
A derivation of a Lie algebra is a linear map D satisfying
D[x,y]=[Dx,y]+[x,Dy].
(3)
It is inner when D=adz for some z∈L.
Every nonzero finite-dimensional nilpotent Lie algebra has an outer derivation of a nilpotent Lie algebra. Choose a codimension-one maximal subalgebra K; it is an ideal by the result above, and write L=K⊕Cz. The centralizer CL​(K) is nonzero because it contains Z(L). Let n be largest such that
CL​(K)⊆γn​(L),
(4)
and choose z0​∈CL​(K)∖γn+1​(L). Define
D(K)=0,D(z)=z0​.
(5)
Because K is an ideal and z0​ centralizes K, the derivation identity holds on K×K and on z×K, hence everywhere. If D=adx, then D(K)=0 would put x in CL​(K)⊆γn​(L), so
D(z)=[x,z]∈γn+1​(L),
(6)
contrary to the choice of z0​. Thus D is outer.
The analogous assertion fails for soluble algebras. In the affine example, a derivation has
D(x)=by,D(y)=dy,
(7)
and equals ad(dx−by). Thus every derivation is inner although L is nonzero and soluble.

A Lie algebra is semisimple when its soluble radical is zero. Its Killing form is
BL​(x,y)=tr(adxady).
(8)
The radical of this invariant symmetric form is an ideal. The solvability result behind the Cartan criterion for semisimplicity applied to that ideal shows that it is soluble; semisimplicity therefore makes it zero. Hence BL​ is nondegenerate.
An abelian subalgebra H is a Cartan subalgebra when its elements are semisimple and it is maximal toral, equivalently when CL​(H)=H. An arbitrary abelian subalgebra need not lie in one: in sl2​, the line spanned by the nilpotent matrix e is abelian, whereas every element of a Cartan subalgebra is semisimple.
Let H=CL​(h0​) for a regular h0​∈H, as allowed. Generalized eigenspaces of adh0​ give
L=H⊕[h0​,L].
(9)
If x∈H is orthogonal to H, invariance gives
BL​(x,[h0​,y])=BL​([x,h0​],y)=0.
(10)
Thus x is orthogonal to all of L, and nondegeneracy gives x=0. Therefore BL​∣H​ is nondegenerate.
The commuting semisimple maps {adh:h∈H} can be simultaneously diagonalized. Consequently
L=H⊕α∈Φ⨁​Lα​,Lα​={x:[h,x]=α(h)x ∀h∈H}.​
(11)
Here L0​=CL​(H)=H, the nonzero weights Φ⊆H∗ are the roots, and the Jacobi identity gives [Lα​,Lβ​]⊆Lα+β​.

A finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation is a homomorphism ρ:L→gl(V). It is irreducible when V has no invariant subspaces other than 0 and V.
The algebra sl2​ has basis e,f,h with
[h,e]=2e,[h,f]=−2f,[e,f]=h.
(12)
For every n≥0, its (n+1)-dimensional irreducible module V(n) has basis v0​,…,vn​ and action
hvi​=(n−2i)vi​,fvi​=vi+1​,evi​=i(n−i+1)vi−1​,
(13)
with out-of-range vectors zero. Any nonzero invariant subspace contains a weight vector; repeated application of e reaches v0​, and repeated application of f then generates the whole module, proving irreducibility. The adjoint module of sl2​ is V(2), so every ideal is an invariant subspace and sl2​ is simple.
For a root α, nondegeneracy of the Killing pairing between Lα​ and L−α​ allows choices eα​,fα​ with
hα​=[eα​,fα​],α(hα​)=2.
(14)
After rescaling, (eα​,fα​,hα​) obey the sl2​ relations, giving a copy of sl2​ in Uα​.
Restrict the adjoint representation of L to this copy. Finite-dimensional sl2​ theory shows that the α-string through zero has one nontrivial summand V(2), with weight spaces L−α​, Chα​, and Lα​. Any additional vector in Lα​ would generate another weight-two summand and another independent zero-weight coroot, contradicting nondegeneracy of the root--coroot pairing on H. Hence
dimLα​=1.​
(15)
For β∈/Zα, the space
W=⨁k=−qp​Lβ+kα​
(16)
is stable under eα​,fα​,hα​. Adjacent raising and lowering maps are nonzero until the endpoints, and every root space is one-dimensional, so W is a simple sl2​-module of highest weight p+q. Its lowest and highest hα​-weights give
β(hα​)−2q=−(p+q),β(hα​)+2p=p+q,
(17)
and therefore β(hα​)=q−p. Since [Lα​,L−α​]=Chα​, every x in this bracket line satisfies
β(x)=2q−p​α(x).​
(18)

The Jacobson radical J=J(R) is the intersection of all maximal right ideals, equivalently the largest ideal annihilating every simple right module. The Artin–Wedderburn theorem gives
R/J≃a=1∏t​Mna​​(k)​
(19)
because k is algebraically closed.
The descending chain J⊇J2⊇⋯ stabilizes since R is finite-dimensional. If Jm=Jm+1, Nakayama lemma applied to the finite right module Jm gives Jm=0. Thus J is nilpotent.
For f∈EndR​(Pi​), the Fitting lemma gives
Pi​=kerfn⊕imfn
(20)
for large n. Indecomposability makes one summand zero, so f is either invertible or nilpotent. In the latter case 1−f is invertible. This is the criterion that EndR​(Pi​) is a local ring.
Let M=⨁i​Pi​. Reduction modulo MJ defines
θ:EndR​(M)⟶EndR​(M/MJ).
(21)
Since M/MJ=⨁i​Si​ and the Si​ are pairwise nonisomorphic simples,
EndR​(M/MJ)≃∏i​EndR​(Si​)≃kn
(22)
by Schur lemma. Arbitrary scalars on the direct summands lift to scalar identity maps on the Pi​, so θ is surjective.
If f∈kerθ, then f(M)⊆MJ. A product of r such maps sends M into MJr, so kerθ is nilpotent. A nilpotent ideal lies in the Jacobson radical, while the semisimplicity of the quotient kn gives the reverse inclusion. Hence
J(EndR​(M))=kerθ,EndR​(M)/J≃kn.​
(23)
This is exactly the definition of a basic algebra.

A simply-laced positive-definite Coxeter graph has no multiple edges and has positive-definite symmetric Cartan matrix, with diagonal entries 2 and entry −1 precisely across an edge. Its connected components are exactly the ADE classification
An​ (n≥1),Dn​ (n≥4),E6​,E7​,E8​.​
(24)
The graph underlying 1→2 is A2​. For simple roots α1​,α2​ with (αi​,αi​)=2 and (α1​,α2​)=−1, its roots are
±α1​,±α2​,±(α1​+α2​).​
(25)
There is one base for each Weyl chamber, hence six bases. The Weyl group is generated by the two root reflections with
s12​=s22​=1,(s1​s2​)3=1,
(26)
so W≃S3​. Relative to a chosen base, the two Coxeter elements are s1​s2​ and s2​s1​; both have order three.
A representation of the quiver is one linear map f:V1​→V2​. Choosing bases that put f in rank normal form decomposes it into copies of
(k→0),(0→k),(k1​k).
(27)
These are indecomposable and have dimension vectors (1,0), (0,1), and (1,1), the three positive roots of A2​. More generally, Gabriel theorem says that for any orientation of a simply-laced positive-definite Dynkin graph, indecomposable quiver representations are in bijection with its positive roots. The finite ADE root system therefore gives finitely many indecomposables.
