Write n=[L:K]. The field trace gives a nondegenerate K-bilinear trace pairing
(x,y)⟼TrL/K​(xy).
(1)
The inverse different, or codifferent, is the trace-dual lattice
DS/R−1​={x∈L:TrL/K​(xS)⊆R}.
(2)
It contains S, because the trace of an element integral over R belongs to the integrally closed ring R. It is stable under multiplication by S: if s,t∈S and x∈DS/R−1​, then Tr(sxt)=Tr(x(st))∈R. Nondegeneracy of the trace pairing and finite generation of S show that this trace dual is a finitely generated R-module spanning L. Therefore it is a fractional ideal of S.
Its inverse
DS/R​=(DS/R−1​)−1={x∈L:xDS/R−1​⊆S}
(3)
is the different ideal. Since S⊆DS/R−1​, every such x lies in S; hence DS/R​ is an integral ideal of S.
The discriminant ideal disc(S/R) is locally generated by
det(TrL/K​(αi​αj​))1≤i,j≤n​,
(4)
where α1​,…,αn​ is a local R-basis of S. Equivalently, it is the image of the determinant of the trace pairing
(detR​S)⊗2⟶R.
(5)
This formulation makes the definition independent of a basis, since changing a basis multiplies its discriminant by the square of the determinant of the change-of-basis matrix.
The asserted identity of ideals can be checked after localization at every nonzero prime ideal of R. We may therefore assume that R is a discrete valuation ring and choose a basis α1​,…,αn​ of S. Let β1​,…,βn​ be its trace-dual basis, so Tr(αi​βj​)=δij​; this is a basis of DS/R−1​. If T=(Tr(αi​αj​)), then
αj​=∑i​Tij​βi​.
(6)
Thus the determinant measuring the inclusion S⊆DS/R−1​ is detT. The determinant description of the norm of a fractional ideal consequently gives
NL/K​(DS/R​)=(detT)=disc(S/R).
(7)
Localization then proves the equality over the original Dedekind domain.
Finally, the determinant-of-pairing map identifies the invertible R-module (detR​S)⊗2 with the discriminant ideal. Hence in the ideal class group
[disc(S/R)]=[detR​S]2,
(8)
up to the harmless inverse caused by the convention used to identify invertible modules with fractional ideals. In either convention the class is a square.

For each place of a number field v, let Kv​ be the corresponding completion, and for finite v let Ov​ be its valuation ring. The adele ring is the restricted product
AK​=∏v′​Kv​={(xv​)v​:xv​∈Ov​ for all but finitely many finite v}.
(9)
Its restricted product topology has basic open sets ∏v​Uv​, where every Uv​⊆Kv​ is open and Uv​=Ov​ at all but finitely many finite places.
First take K=Q. The neighborhood
(−1/2,1/2)×∏p​Zp​
(10)
of zero meets the diagonal copy of Q only in zero: a rational number lying in every Zp​ is an integer, and the only integer in the indicated real interval is zero. Thus Q is discrete in AQ​.
Every rational adele is congruent modulo Q to an element of
[0,1]×∏p​Zp​.
(11)
Indeed, the finitely many negative p-adic principal parts can be removed simultaneously by subtracting a rational number, using the Chinese remainder theorem; subtracting an integer then moves the real component into [0,1]. This set is compact by the compactness of [0,1], the compactness of every Zp​, and the Tychonoff theorem. Its image covers the quotient, so AQ​/Q is compact.
Now choose a Q-basis of the number field K. The given topological isomorphism
AQ​⊗Q​K≃AK​
(12)
identifies the additive pair (AK​,K) with (AQn​,Qn). A finite product of discrete subgroups is discrete, and
AK​/K≃(AQ​/Q)n
(13)
is compact.
The idele group is
JK​=∏v′​Kv×​,
(14)
where the distinguished subgroup at a finite place is Ov×​. It carries the corresponding restricted product topology on the idele group. The inclusion j:JK​→AK​ is continuous: the inverse image of a basic adelic open set is locally a product of open subsets of Kv×​, and outside finitely many places every idele component already belongs to Ov×​⊆Ov​.
It is not a homeomorphism onto its image. Let pi​ be the ith rational prime and define the idele x(i) to equal pi​ at the place over pi​ and 1 everywhere else. In the adele topology, x(i)→1: the difference is zero at every fixed place once i is large, while pi​−1∈Zpi​​ at the single moving place. In the idele topology the sequence does not converge to 1, because the open neighborhood
∏v∣∞​Kv×​×∏v∤∞​Ov×​
(15)
contains no x(i): its pi​-component has positive valuation and is not a unit. Hence the inverse of j on its image is not continuous.

The Schwartz-Bruhat space S(F) of a non-Archimedean local field F is the vector space of locally constant, compactly supported complex-valued functions on F. Fix a nontrivial continuous additive character ψ:F→C× and a Haar measure dx. With the sign convention required in the question, the Fourier transform over a local field is
f​(y)=∫F​f(x)ψ(xy)dx.
(16)
Let
OF⊥​={y∈F:ψ(xy)=1 for every x∈OF​}
(17)
be the annihilator of the valuation ring. Translation invariance gives
1OF​​​(y)=∫OF​​ψ(xy)dx=vol(OF​)1OF⊥​​(y).
(18)
Indeed, the integral is the volume when the character is trivial; otherwise translation by an element on which the character is nontrivial multiplies the integral by a scalar different from one, forcing it to vanish. With the usual character of conductor OF​ and the normalization vol(OF​)=1, this becomes
1OF​​​=1OF​​.
(19)
For the canonical character induced from Qp​, the annihilator is instead the inverse different and the displayed general formula applies.
If g(x)=f(ax+b) with a=0, the substitution u=ax+b and the scaling rule dx=∣a∣−1du give
g​(y)​=∫F​f(ax+b)ψ(xy)dx=∣a∣−1∫F​f(u)ψ((u−b)y/a)du=ψ(−by/a)∣a∣−1f​(y/a).​
(20)
Every locally constant compactly supported function is a finite linear combination of characteristic functions of cosets b+aOF​: compactness extracts finitely many cosets on which the function is constant. The formula just proved, together with the transform of 1OF​​, shows that the transform of each such characteristic function is again locally constant and compactly supported. Therefore the Fourier transform over a local field maps S(F) to itself.

Suppose that L/Q were a Finite Galois extension with Galois group S3​ and unramified away from 7. Its quadratic subfield is the fixed field of the alternating subgroup A3​. A quadratic field ramified only at 7 must be Q(−7​): the classification by fundamental discriminants shows that −7 is the only nontrivial quadratic discriminant supported at 7. In particular, 7 ramifies in this quadratic subfield.
Because 7 does not divide ∣S3​∣=6, all ramification at 7 is tame ramification. Its inertia group is therefore cyclic. Its image in S3​/A3​≃C2​ is nontrivial because the quadratic subfield is ramified, so the inertia group is generated by a transposition and has order two; it cannot have order six because it is cyclic.
The discriminant exponent of a tame Galois extension is therefore
v7​(dL​)=[L:Q](1−e1​)=6(1−21​)=3.
(21)
There is no other finite ramification, so ∣dL​∣=73=343.
On the other hand, the Minkowski bound for ideal classes implies the Minkowski lower bound for a number-field discriminant
∣dL​∣≥(4π​)2r2​(6!66​)2.
(22)
Since 0≤r2​≤3, the right side is smallest at r2​=3 and is greater than 980. This contradicts ∣dL​∣=343, so no such extension exists.

Put s=−6​, so OK​=Z[s] and OK×​={±1}. The primes 2 and 3 are ramified:
(2)=p22​,(3)=p32​.
(23)
Thus the finite modulus in the question is
m=p22​p32​=(6).
(24)
The ray class exact sequence, with no real component in the modulus, gives
OK×​⟶(OK​/(6))×⟶Clm​(K)⟶Cl(K)⟶1.
(25)
The Chinese remainder theorem for unit groups and the ramification relations yield
(OK​/(6))×​≃(OK​/(2))××(OK​/(3))×≃(F2​[ϵ]/(ϵ2))××(F3​[ϵ]/(ϵ2))×≃C2​×C6​.​
(26)
The image of −1 kills the C2​ coming from F3×​. Consequently the kernel of the map from the ray class group to the ordinary class group is
(OK​/(6))×/{±1}≃C2​×C3​≃C6​.
(27)
Since the given class number is two, ∣Clm​(K)∣=12.
It remains to distinguish C12​ from C6​×C2​. Let
q=(5,s−2).
(28)
The prime 5 splits in K, and q represents the nontrivial ordinary ideal class because no element of Z[s] has norm 5. Direct multiplication, or comparison of norms and valuations at the two primes over 5, gives
q2=(1+2s).

Modulo 2, the element 1+2s is 1. Modulo 3, its class 1+2ϵ is nontrivial and has order three because ϵ2=0. Hence the ray class of q has order six: its square is a nontrivial element of order three in the congruence kernel. The remaining order-two factor of that kernel, coming from (OK​/(2))×, is independent of ⟨[q]⟩. Therefore
Clm​(Q(−6​))≃C6​×C2​.​
