A cyclic group is a group generated by a single element: there is g∈G such that every element is gn for some integer n. For a finite group of prime order p, choose g=e. Its cyclic subgroup ⟨g⟩ has order dividing p by Lagrange's theorem, and its order is greater than one. Hence ∣⟨g⟩∣=p, and ⟨g⟩=G. Thus every group of prime order is cyclic.
A group homomorphism from C11​=⟨a⟩ is determined by h=φ(a), since φ(aj)=hj. Its image generator must satisfy h11=e, so its order of a group element divides 11. As an element of C14​ its order also divides 14. These integers are coprime, so the only possible order is one. Thus h=e, and
φ(aj)=efor every j.​
(4)
This map is indeed a group homomorphism, so there is exactly one, the trivial homomorphism.

A group action is a map (g,x)↦g⋅x from G×X to X satisfying e⋅x=x and (gh)⋅x=g⋅(h⋅x). Define the orbit of a group action Gx={g⋅x:g∈G} and the stabilizer subgroup Gx​={g:g⋅x=x}. The latter is a subgroup: the identity fixes x, products of elements fixing x fix x, and inverses do too.
For finite G, the orbit-stabilizer theorem states
∣Gx∣=∣Gx​∣∣G∣​.​
(12)
To prove it, send a left coset gGx​ to g⋅x. If gGx​=hGx​, then h−1g∈Gx​ and g⋅x=h⋅x. Conversely, equality of the two images implies h−1g∈Gx​, hence equality of the cosets. Every point of the orbit of a group action has such a preimage. This is therefore a bijection, and each coset has ∣Gx​∣ elements, proving the formula.
For the action on subgroups, conjugation maps a subgroup K to a subgroup gKg−1 because it preserves products and inverses. Furthermore eKe−1=K and
(gh)K(gh)−1=g(hKh−1)g−1.
(13)
Thus this is the conjugation action. The stabilizer subgroup of H is its normalizer NG​(H), and H⊆NG​(H) since each h∈H conjugates H onto itself. Hence the number k of distinct conjugate subgroups is
k=∣NG​(H)∣∣G∣​≤∣H∣∣G∣​.
(14)
Now suppose H is proper. Put m=∣G∣/∣H∣≥2 and list the k distinct conjugate subgroups as H1​,…,Hk​. Each has ∣H∣ elements and all contain the same identity. Counting the identity just once, even if other elements overlap, gives
​⋃i=1k​Hi​​≤1+k(∣H∣−1)≤1+m(∣H∣−1)=∣G∣−m+1<∣G∣.
(15)
Thus some element of G lies in no conjugate of H. This proves conjugates of a proper subgroup do not cover a finite group by an explicit count; it does not require the conjugates to intersect only at the identity.

Represent the Riemann sphere by the complex projective line, with finite z represented by [z:1] and infinity by [1:0]. An invertible matrix A=(ac​bd​) acts by [u:v]↦[au+bv:cu+dv], giving the stated fractional map on finite points. Applying B and then A is exactly matrix multiplication AB, including at poles and infinity. Therefore
θ(AB)=θ(A)∘θ(B),
(16)
so θ is a group homomorphism from the special linear group to the Möbius group.
Every Möbius transformation has an invertible complex matrix representative A. Choose ρ∈C with ρ2detA=1. Such a nonzero square root exists in C, and ρA has determinant one while representing the same projective map. This proves surjectivity. If the map is the identity, fixing zero and infinity forces b=c=0, and fixing one forces a=d. The determinant-one condition gives a2=1. Conversely both scalar matrices I and −I act trivially. Hence
kerθ={I,−I},M≅SL2​(C)/{I,−I}.​
(17)
For the normal forms, use the fact that a complex 2×2 matrix has a Jordan normal form: either it is diagonalizable or it has one nontrivial Jordan block. For a determinant-one representative, distinct eigenvalues are λ,λ−1. Diagonalizing gives diag(λ,λ−1), whose projective action is z↦λ2z. Distinctness means λ2=1, so μ=λ2 is neither zero nor one.
If the eigenvalue is repeated, the determinant condition gives λ=±1. A diagonalizable representative would be λI and would give the identity, excluded here. A nontrivial Jordan block is similar to (λ0​1λ​), giving z↦z+1/λ=z±1. Any invertible conjugating matrix gives a Möbius transformation; rescaling it to determinant one does not change that map. Thus these matrix similarities are genuine conjugacies in the Möbius group, proving the requested Möbius conjugacy normal forms.
Conjugacy preserves fixed points bijectively. A nonidentity scaling has exactly the two fixed points zero and infinity, while either translation has exactly one, infinity. The identity fixes every point. This proves all three possibilities and excludes any further finite number of fixed points.
Finally, suppose P∘T∘P−1=S, with S(z)=z+ε and ε=±1. Then Sn(z)=z+nε→∞ for every finite z in the topology of the Riemann sphere; infinity remains infinity. Since P−1 is continuous on that sphere,
Tn(z)=P−1(Sn(P(z)))⟶P−1(∞)=z0​for every z.​
(18)
This is iteration of a one-fixed-point Möbius transformation. The limiting statement includes the unique fixed point itself and uses spherical convergence, which also handles intermediate iterates equal to infinity.

The identity belongs to the center of a group. If a,b are central, then for every g∈G,
g(ab−1)=agb−1=ab−1g,
(19)
so the center is a subgroup. For a central a, gag−1=a for every g, giving gZ(G)g−1=Z(G). Thus the center is a normal subgroup.
Write a matrix of the Heisenberg group as h(x,y,z). Direct matrix multiplication and inversion give
h(x,y,z)h(a,b,c)=h(x+a,y+b+xc,z+c),h(x,y,z)−1=h(−x,xz−y,−z).
(20)
The identity is h(0,0,0) and each matrix has determinant one, so these formulas show closure under products and inverses inside GL3​(R). Hence H is a subgroup.
The two products h(x,y,z)h(a,b,c) and h(a,b,c)h(x,y,z) differ only in their upper-right entries, by xc−az. Commutation with every h(a,b,c) therefore requires xc=az for all real a,c. Setting a=0,c=1 forces x=0; setting a=1,c=0 forces z=0. These conditions are also sufficient. Thus
Z(H)={h(0,y,0):y∈R}.​
(21)
Define the surjective group homomorphism F:H→(R2,+) by F(h(x,y,z))=(x,z). The multiplication formula gives F(hh′)=F(h)+F(h′), and its kernel of a group homomorphism is precisely Z(H). The first isomorphism theorem yields
H/Z(H)≅(R2,+),h(x,y,z)Z(H)⟼(x,z).​
(22)
This center quotient of the real Heisenberg group can also be checked directly: two elements have the same (x,z) coordinates exactly when they differ by multiplication by a central element, and quotient multiplication adds these coordinates.

Equality means w−ϕ is constant on each connected component; its zero boundary value forces that constant to be zero.
Now let w solve the heat equation and set E(t)=∫V​∣∇w∣2dV. Differentiation followed by Green's first identity gives
E′(t)=2∫V​∇w⋅∇wt​=2∫S​wt​∂n​w−2∫V​wt​Δw.
(33)
Under time-independent Dirichlet boundary conditions, wt​=0 on S, while wt​=Δw in V. Therefore

(34)
If equality holds at a particular time, continuity makes Δw=0 throughout V at that time. Its boundary values are still f, so uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem implies w=ϕ. Conversely w=ϕ has Δw=0 and gives equality. This proves the full equality criterion for Dirichlet energy dissipation for the heat equation.
For the dynamic boundary condition for the heat equation, substitute wt​=−α∂n​w directly into the boundary term. The interior equation is unchanged, so
E′(t)=−2∫V​wt2​dV−2∫S​α(∂n​w)2dS≤0.​

At a critical point p of a twice continuously differentiable real function, ∇f(p)=0 and the Taylor expansion is
f(p+h)=f(p)+21​hTHh+o(∣h∣2),H=D2f(p).
(43)
The real symmetric Hessian matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis, so writing h=∑i​ai​ui​ gives hTHh=∑i​λi​ai2​. If every eigenvalue is positive, this quadratic form is at least λmin​∣h∣2, and the remainder cannot change its sign for sufficiently small nonzero h. Thus p is a strict local minimum. If every eigenvalue is negative, the analogous argument gives a strict local maximum. If positive and negative eigenvalues both occur, displacement along the corresponding eigenvectors gives values above and below f(p), so p is a saddle point of a scalar function.
The second-derivative test can be inconclusive when the Hessian matrix is singular and semidefinite: higher-order terms along directions in the kernel of a linear map matter. For example x2+y4 and x2−y4 have the same Hessian diag(2,0) at zero, but the first has a strict minimum and the second a saddle point of a scalar function. A zero eigenvalue does not prevent a saddle conclusion if other eigenvalues already have both signs.
For the given function write r2=x2+y2. Its first derivatives are
fx​=ye−αr2(1−2αx2),fy​=xe−αr2(1−2αy2).
(44)
The origin is a critical point for every real α, with
D2f(0,0)=(01​10​).
(45)
Its eigenvalues are 1,−1, so it is always a saddle point of a scalar function. If one coordinate at a critical point is zero, the other must also be zero. If both are nonzero, the derivative equations force x2=y2=1/(2α), possible only for α>0. Hence for α≤0 the origin is the only critical point.
For α>0 there are four additional critical points,
(x,y)=2α​1​(ε,η),ε,η∈{−1,1}.
(46)
At such a point,
f=2αeεη​,D2f=−4αxye−1I2​=−e2εη​I2​.
(47)
Thus the two equal-sign points are strict maxima, and the two opposite-sign points are strict minima. They are global: 2∣xy∣≤r2 implies
∣f(x,y)∣≤2r2​e−αr2≤2αe1​,
(48)
where the last maximum occurs at r2=1/α and equality also requires x2=y2.
As α↓0, the four nonzero critical points have distance α−1/2 from the origin and escape to infinity, rather than merging with the origin. Their values diverge as ±1/(2eα). As α↑0 from below there are no extra critical points, while the origin stays a saddle throughout. This is critical points escaping to infinity in a Gaussian-weighted bilinear function.
