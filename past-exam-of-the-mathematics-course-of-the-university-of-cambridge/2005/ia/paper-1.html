A real number s is the supremum of a nonempty set A⊂R if every a∈A satisfies a≤s and no smaller real number is an upper bound. Equivalently, for every η>0 there is a∈A with s−η<a≤s. A greatest element, or maximum, additionally belongs to A.
Let s=supA and suppose s∈/A. Fix ε>0. The approximation property gives at least one element of A in (s−ε,s). If there were only finitely many, they would have a largest value m<s. Every other element of A is at most s−ε, so max{m,s−ε}<s would be an upper bound for the whole set. This contradicts the definition of the supremum. Therefore there are infinitely many such elements, the accumulation below an unattained supremum.
For a counterexample when the supremum is attained, take A={0}. Its supremum and greatest element are both zero, while every interval (−ε,0) contains no elements of A.

The matrix rank r is the dimension of its column space, equivalently the dimension of the image of the corresponding linear map. The equation is solvable precisely when b∈colA, equivalently when the augmented matrix has the same rank as A. If x0​ is one solution, every solution is x0​+u with u∈kerA, since subtracting any two solutions gives a kernel element. The rank-nullity theorem gives dimkerA=3−r.
Consequently, for rank three there is one solution for every b, namely A−1b. For rank two there are no solutions outside the column plane, and an affine solution space of a linear equation of dimension one when consistent. For rank one, consistency requires b to lie on the column line and the solution set is an affine plane of dimension two. For rank zero, A=0: the solution set is all of R3 if b=0, and empty otherwise. These descriptions include homogeneous systems, whose solution spaces pass through the origin.

Rolle theorem states that if a<b, f is continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), and f(a)=f(b), then there is c∈(a,b) with f′(c)=0.
If f is constant, any interior point works. Otherwise the extreme value theorem gives an attained maximum and minimum on the interval, and at least one of them differs from the common endpoint value. That extremum must occur at an interior point c. At an interior maximum, for small positive h the difference quotient [f(c+h)−f(c)]/h is nonpositive, while for negative h it is nonnegative. Existence of the common derivative forces it to be both nonpositive and nonnegative, hence zero. The same argument with inequalities reversed applies to an interior minimum. This proves Rolle theorem, including the derivative-at-an-extremum step rather than assuming it.
Now let the nonconstant polynomial p have distinct real roots r1​<⋯<rk​, with multiplicities m1​,…,mk​ adding to its degree n. If p(x)=(x−rj​)mj​qj​(x) and qj​(rj​)=0, differentiation gives
p′(x)=(x−rj​)mj​−1[mj​qj​(x)+(x−rj​)qj′​(x)].
(44)
The bracket is nonzero at rj​, so its multiplicity as a root of p′ is exactly mj​−1. These roots already contribute ∑j​(mj​−1)=n−k real roots counted with multiplicity. Rolle theorem supplies an additional root of p′ in each of the k−1 disjoint intervals (rj​,rj+1​). These additional roots are distinct from each other and from all the rj​.
We have therefore exhibited at least (n−k)+(k−1)=n−1 real roots counted with multiplicity. The derivative has degree n−1, so there is no room for any additional nonreal roots. Every root of p′ is real. This is the Rolle root count with multiplicities; for n=1 the nonzero constant derivative has no roots, as expected.
For failure of the converse, take
p(x)=x3+1,p′(x)=3x2.​
(45)
The derivative has only the real root zero, of multiplicity two. But p(x)=(x+1)(x2−x+1) and the quadratic factor has roots (1±i3​)/2, which are nonreal. Thus real-rootedness of the derivative does not imply real-rootedness of the original polynomial.
