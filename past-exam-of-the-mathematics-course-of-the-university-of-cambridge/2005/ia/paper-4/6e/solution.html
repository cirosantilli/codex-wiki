An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive: for all a,b,c∈S, one has aRa; aRb implies bRa; and aRb, bRc imply aRc. Define the equivalence class [a]={b∈S:aRb}. Reflexivity puts a in its class, so these classes cover S and are nonempty. If [a] and [b] share c, symmetry and transitivity give aRb; the same properties show that every element of either class is in the other. Thus two classes are equal or disjoint. The distinct equivalence classes form a partition of the set.
For the relation defined by a subgroup H≤G, a−1a=1∈H proves reflexivity. If a−1b∈H, its inverse b−1a belongs to H, proving symmetry. If also b−1c∈H, their product is a−1c∈H, proving transitivity. Finally,
[a]={b:a−1b∈H}=aH.
(16)
These are exactly the left cosets. The map H→gH, h↦gh, is a bijection, with inverse x↦g−1x. If G is finite and has r such cosets, their disjoint union gives ∣G∣=r∣H∣. This proves the Lagrange theorem:
∣H∣∣∣G∣.​
(17)
For g∈G, finitely many possible powers imply ga=gb for some a<b, so a positive power is the identity element. If its least positive exponent is n, the powers 1,g,…,gn−1 are distinct: equality of two would produce a smaller positive exponent. They are closed under products and inverses by reducing exponents modulo n, hence form the cyclic subgroup ⟨g⟩ of cardinality n. The Lagrange theorem gives n∣∣G∣, and consequently
g∣G∣=1for every g∈G.​
(18)
Now take a positive modulus m. On residue classes modulo m, consider the classes with representatives coprime to m. The following integer facts are used: multiplication is associative; reducing representatives respects products; a prime dividing a product divides a factor; and the Bezout identity gives ra+sm=1 when gcd(a,m)=1. If a,b are coprime to m, no prime divisor of m divides ab, so their product class is again in the set. The class of 1 is its identity element. The Bézout equation gives ra≡1(modm), and r is also coprime to m because any common divisor of r,m would divide 1. Thus every class has an inverse, and these classes form the group of units modulo an integer.
Representing classes in {1,…,m} gives precisely the group in the question; for m=1 this is the one-element group. Its cardinality is the Euler totient function φ(m). Applying the finite-group exponent result yields the Fermat-Euler theorem:
aφ(m)≡1(modm)if gcd(a,m)=1.​
(19)
For m=1 the congruence is automatic.
