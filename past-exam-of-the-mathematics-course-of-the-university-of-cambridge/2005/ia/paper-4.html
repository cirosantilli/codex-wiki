The Wilson theorem says that (p−1)!≡−1(modp) for a prime number p. Thus 18⋅17!≡−1(mod19), and since 18≡−1, we get 17!≡1. The Fermat little theorem says bp−1≡1(modp) when p does not divide b. Applying it to 3 gives 318≡1, so 316 is the inverse of 9 modulo 19. Since 9⋅17=153≡1, the required residue is
a=17.​
(1)
This is the unique representative in {1,…,19}.
For the simultaneous integer congruences, the modulus-2 condition is already implied by x≡3(mod4). Write x=3+4k; the modulus-3 condition becomes k≡1(mod3), hence x=7+12t. The modulus-5 condition then gives 2+2t≡4, or t≡1(mod5). Therefore
x=19+60j,j∈Z.​
(2)
Every such integer satisfies all four integer congruences; reversing the substitutions shows that there are no others. The uniqueness modulo 60 also follows from the Chinese remainder theorem applied to the coprime moduli 3,4,5.

For nonnegative integers n,m, the binomial coefficient (mn​) counts the m-element subsets of a fixed n-element set. It is zero when m>n, and (0n​)=1, including n=0. For 0≤m≤n, taking the complement is a bijection from m-element subsets to (n−m)-element subsets; taking a complement twice gives the identity. Hence
(mn​)=(n−mn​).​
(3)
For 0≤l≤k≤n, count ordered pairs (L,K) of subsets of an n-element set with L⊆K, ∣L∣=l and ∣K∣=k. Choosing K first and then L gives (kn​)(lk​). Choosing L first and then the k−l additional elements of K from its complement gives (ln​)(k−ln−l​). These count the same pairs, proving the nested-subset binomial identity:
(kn​)(lk​)=(ln​)(k−ln−l​).​
(4)
For the Vandermonde identity, let disjoint sets A,B have cardinalities m,n. A k-element subset of A∪B contains some number i of elements of A. For a fixed i, there are (im​)(k−in​) choices. The classes for different i are disjoint and exhaust all k-element subsets, so double counting gives
i=0∑k​(im​)(k−in​)=(km+n​).​
(5)
The zero convention makes this valid for all nonnegative m,n,k, even if k>m+n. No factorial cancellation is needed for either counting proof.

A countable set is a set that is finite or can be put in bijection with the natural numbers; equivalently, it admits an injective function into the natural numbers. To enumerate the rational numbers, list pairs (p,q) with p∈Z, q≥1 in increasing order of ∣p∣+q, and then list p/q, discarding repetitions. Each level is finite and every rational eventually appears. An enumeration q1​,q2​,… of the rationals then enumerates Q×Q by listing (qi​,qj​) in diagonals of constant i+j. Thus
Q×Q is countable.​
(15)
To prove that the real numbers are uncountable, suppose (0,1) had an enumeration x1​,x2​,…. Write each xn​ in its decimal expansion that does not end in an infinite string of nines. Define a new decimal y=0.d1​d2​⋯ by taking dn​=2 if the nth digit of xn​ is 1, and dn​=1 otherwise. Then y∈(0,1), has no ambiguous terminating/nines representation, and differs from xn​ at digit n. The Cantor diagonal argument contradicts the supposed enumeration. Consequently the real numbers are uncountable.
For the discs, use the enumeration of Q2 just constructed. Every positive-radius disc contains a rational-coordinate point: its interior contains a small open rectangle, and each coordinate interval contains a rational number. For each disc D, let j(D) be the least index of an enumerated rational-coordinate point in its interior. This minimum exists and requires no arbitrary simultaneous choice. Disjoint discs cannot receive the same point, so D↦j(D) is an injective function into the natural numbers. This proves the countability of pairwise disjoint open disks and also the same conclusion for closed discs, by using their disjoint interiors.
For circles, take all concentric circles centred at the origin with radii r∈(1,2). Two different radii give disjoint circles, and every radius is positive. The radius map is a bijection from the uncountable interval (1,2) to this family. An uncountable disjoint family of nontrivial circles is therefore possible: the rational-point argument for discs relies on their nonempty interiors, which circumferences do not have.

An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive: for all a,b,c∈S, one has aRa; aRb implies bRa; and aRb, bRc imply aRc. Define the equivalence class [a]={b∈S:aRb}. Reflexivity puts a in its class, so these classes cover S and are nonempty. If [a] and [b] share c, symmetry and transitivity give aRb; the same properties show that every element of either class is in the other. Thus two classes are equal or disjoint. The distinct equivalence classes form a partition of the set.
For the relation defined by a subgroup H≤G, a−1a=1∈H proves reflexivity. If a−1b∈H, its inverse b−1a belongs to H, proving symmetry. If also b−1c∈H, their product is a−1c∈H, proving transitivity. Finally,
[a]={b:a−1b∈H}=aH.
(16)
These are exactly the left cosets. The map H→gH, h↦gh, is a bijection, with inverse x↦g−1x. If G is finite and has r such cosets, their disjoint union gives ∣G∣=r∣H∣. This proves the Lagrange theorem:
∣H∣∣∣G∣.​
(17)
For g∈G, finitely many possible powers imply ga=gb for some a<b, so a positive power is the identity element. If its least positive exponent is n, the powers 1,g,…,gn−1 are distinct: equality of two would produce a smaller positive exponent. They are closed under products and inverses by reducing exponents modulo n, hence form the cyclic subgroup ⟨g⟩ of cardinality n. The Lagrange theorem gives n∣∣G∣, and consequently
g∣G∣=1for every g∈G.​
(18)
Now take a positive modulus m. On residue classes modulo m, consider the classes with representatives coprime to m. The following integer facts are used: multiplication is associative; reducing representatives respects products; a prime dividing a product divides a factor; and the Bezout identity gives ra+sm=1 when gcd(a,m)=1. If a,b are coprime to m, no prime divisor of m divides ab, so their product class is again in the set. The class of 1 is its identity element. The Bézout equation gives ra≡1(modm), and r is also coprime to m because any common divisor of r,m would divide 1. Thus every class has an inverse, and these classes form the group of units modulo an integer.
Representing classes in {1,…,m} gives precisely the group in the question; for m=1 this is the one-element group. Its cardinality is the Euler totient function φ(m). Applying the finite-group exponent result yields the Fermat-Euler theorem:
aφ(m)≡1(modm)if gcd(a,m)=1.​
(19)
For m=1 the congruence is automatic.
