Work first on x>0, where the proposed logarithm is defined. Substitution of y=xm into the Cauchy-Euler differential equation gives the indicial equation
m(m−1)+m+1=m2+1=0.
(4)
Its distinct indicial roots are m=±i. Using x±i=e±ilogx and Euler's formula, a real basis of solutions is cos(logx),sin(logx). Their Wronskian is 1/x, so they are linearly independent and span the two-dimensional solution space of a homogeneous linear differential equation. Therefore
y(x)=Acos(logx)+Bsin(logx),x>0.​
(5)
For the requested change of variables, set Y(z)=y(ez). The chain rule gives
y′=xY′​,y′′=x2Y′′−Y′​.
(6)
The transformed second-order linear differential equation is Y′′+Y=0. Its characteristic roots are again ±i, giving Y(z)=Acosz+Bsinz. Substituting z=logx recovers precisely the previous general solution.
If solutions on x<0 are wanted, the same calculation uses z=log∣x∣ and gives independent constants on that interval. Zero is a singular point of a differential equation; the logarithmic oscillations do not define a nonzero continuous solution through it.

The characteristic equation of a linear recurrence is
r2−2cosθr+1=0,r=e±iθ.
(17)
For 0<θ<π, these characteristic roots are distinct. Two real solutions of the second-order difference equation are cos(nθ) and sin(nθ). Their values at n=0,1 form a matrix with determinant sinθ=0, proving linear independence. Consequently they span the two-dimensional vector space of recurrence solutions.
At θ=0 the two roots coalesce at one. The linear recurrence becomes Xn+2​−Xn+1​=Xn+1​−Xn​, so its successive differences are constant. A basis is therefore 1,n, and every solution is A+Bn.
For the specified initial conditions, the zero-angle solution is Xn​(0)=n. For nonzero angle, it is convenient to center the basis at n=1:
Xn​(θ)=cos((n−1)θ)+sinθ2−cosθ​sin((n−1)θ),0<θ<π.​
(18)
This gives X1​=1 and X2​=cosθ+2−cosθ=2, and each summand obeys the linear recurrence. For every fixed integer n≥1, the elementary limit sinz/z→1 gives
sinθsin((n−1)θ)​⟶n−1,Xn​(θ)⟶1+(n−1)=n=Xn​(0).​
(19)
The convergence here is for fixed n; it does not assert uniform convergence over all indices.

W(x)=a0​j=0∑∞​(2j)!∏r=0j−1​(4r−E)​x2j+a1​j=0∑∞​(2j+1)!∏r=0j−1​(4r+2−E)​x2j+1.​

An empty product is one. For a nonterminating branch the ratio of consecutive terms in its own parity subsequence is asymptotic to ∣x∣2/j, hence tends to zero for every fixed x. The boundary condition W(0)=0 forces a0​=0; the solution is odd. Apart from the zero solution, which works for every E, a polynomial occurs exactly when an odd index d satisfies 2d−E=0. Before that index the odd coefficients are nonzero, and after it they vanish. Thus the odd polynomial solutions of the Hermite differential equation have
E=4j+2,degW=2j+1,j=0,1,2,….​

For r>0, the polar coordinates satisfy
r˙=rxx˙+yy˙​​=r(1−r2),θ˙=r2xy˙​−yx˙​=1.

A positive constant radius must therefore be one. The nonconstant periodic solutions are

(26)
with least period 2π and counterclockwise orientation. The origin is also a stationary solution, hence periodic in the degenerate sense of a constant function; it is a point rather than a nontrivial closed orbit. There are no other constant-radius solutions.
For r0​>0, put v=r2. Then v˙=2v(1−v), a logistic differential equation. Solving this separable differential equation gives
r(t)=[1+(r0−2​−1)e−2t]−1/2,θ(t)=θ0​+t,t≥0.​
(27)
The denominator is positive for every forward time. When 0<r0​<1, the radius increases monotonically to one; when r0​>1, it decreases monotonically to one; and when r0​=1 it stays there. Thus every nonzero orbit approaches the unit circle while continuing to rotate. More precisely, its distance from the phase-matched circular solution tends to zero; its Cartesian coordinates do not converge to a single point. For r0​=0, the solution stays at the origin.
The origin is the only equilibrium point. Its Jacobian matrix is
J(0)=(11​−11​),
(28)
whose eigenvalues are 1±i. It is an unstable focus. The unit circle is an attracting limit cycle, rather than a curve of equilibria: on it the vector field remains nonzero. Linearizing the radial equation at r=1 gives δr˙=−2δr+O(δr2), confirming radial attraction. All other radii are strictly monotone, so there are no additional nontrivial periodic orbits.

On an interval where p,q are continuous, define the Wronskian
W[u1​,u2​]=det(u1​u1′​​u2​u2′​​)=u1​u2′​−u1′​u2​.
(29)
Using the two second-order linear differential equations to eliminate the second derivatives gives
W′=u1​u2′′​−u1′′​u2​=−pW.
(30)
The integrating factor yields the Abel identity
W(x)=W(x0​)exp(−∫x0​x​p(s)ds).​
(31)
Thus the Wronskian either vanishes identically or is nonzero everywhere on the interval. If u1​,u2​ are linearly dependent, it is identically zero. Conversely, if W(x0​)=0, the two initial-data vectors are dependent, so some nontrivial linear combination has both value and first derivative zero at x0​. The uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations makes that combination identically zero. Hence the solutions are linearly independent exactly when their Wronskian is nonzero at one, and therefore every, point. Continuity and the common differential equation are essential to this converse.
For the particular equation, divide by x2 on either interval x>0 or x<0. The coefficient of y′ is zero, so its Wronskian is constant there. The proposed y1​=x2 satisfies x2y1′′​−2y1​=2x2−2x2=0. To construct a second solution by reduction of order, impose
x2y2′​−2xy2​=C=0.
(32)
Dividing by x4 shows that (y2​/x2)′=C/x4, giving
y2​=Dx2−3xC​.
(33)
Taking C=−3,D=0 produces
y2​(x)=x−1,W[x2,x−1]=−3.​
(34)
The nonzero Wronskian proves linear independence; hence the general solution on either nonsingular interval is y=Ax2+B/x. Zero is a singular point of a differential equation and is excluded from this construction.

The expectation of a complex random quantity is defined through its real and imaginary parts. Both cosX and sinX are bounded, so all expectations below exist. Expand the addition formulae and apply the expectation of a product of independent random variables identity:
Ecos(X+Y)Esin(X+Y)​=EcosXEcosY−EsinXEsinY,=EsinXEcosY+EcosXEsinY.​

ϕ(u)=EeiuWn​=(cosu)n.​

This is real for real u. Equivalently, the distribution of Wn​ is symmetric, making Esin(uWn​)=0.

Use the intended model of independent random variables A,B with exponential distributions of rates α,β>0. For M=min(A,B), independence gives the survival function
P(M>t)=P(A>t)P(B>t)=e−(α+β)t,t≥0.
(41)
Thus
M∼Exp(α+β),fM​(t)=(α+β)e−(α+β)t (t>0),sd(M)=α+β1​.​
(42)
The variance is 1/(α+β)2, obtained from the standard first two exponential moments, EM=1/(α+β) and EM2=2/(α+β)2.
For these competing exponential clocks, ties have probability zero. Integrating over Alice's firing time gives
P(A<B)=∫0∞​αe−αte−βtdt=α+βα​.​
(43)
Let H denote the event that the next shot hits, and use the stated success probability as P(H∣A<B)=1/2. Bill's corresponding conditional success probability is one. The law of total probability gives
P(H)=21​α+βα​+α+ββ​.
(44)
The Bayes' theorem therefore yields
P(A<B∣H)=α+2βα​.​
(45)
The printed assumptions specify the two marginal exponential distributions but do not explicitly specify their independence. The answers above require that extra assumption; marginals alone do not determine the race. For a concrete counterexample, take one Z∼Exp(1) and set A=Z/α, B=Z/β. These have the required marginals, but M∼Exp(max(α,β)); if α>β, Alice always fires first and P(A<B∣H)=1. Thus the independence qualification changes actual answers, rather than merely their derivation.

All the following distributions are conditional on the point lying in the unit disk. Initially the joint probability density of (S,T) is 1/4 on the square. The disk has area π, so the conditioning event has probability π/4. Dividing by this probability gives the constant conditional density 1/π on the disk, proving its uniform distribution there.
The polar coordinates S=rcosθ,T=rsinθ have Jacobian determinant r. Consequently
fR,Θ​(r,θ)=πr​,0<r<1,0≤θ<2π.​
(46)
Integrating the joint probability density gives fR​(r)=2r and fΘ​(θ)=1/(2π). Since the joint density equals the product of these marginal densities on a product support, R and Θ are independent. In particular U=R2 has the uniform distribution on (0,1).
The second change of variables preserves the angle and changes the radius to
Q=−2log(R2)​=−2logU​,Ψ=Θ.​
(47)
The event R=0 has probability zero, so arbitrary definitions at that point have no effect on the distribution. For q≥0, monotonicity of the logarithm gives
P(Q≤q)=P(U≥e−q2/2)=1−e−q2/2,fQ​(q)=qe−q2/2(q>0).
(48)
Since Q is a function of R, it remains independent of the uniform angle. Hence the new joint probability density is
fQ,Ψ​(q,ψ)=2πq​e−q2/2,q>0,0≤ψ<2π.​
(49)
Finally, (X,Y)=(QcosΨ,QsinΨ) has polar Jacobian determinant q. Dividing the preceding density by q gives the Cartesian joint probability density
fX,Y​(x,y)=2π1​e−(x2+y2)/2=(2π​e−x2/2​)(2π​e−y2/2​).
(50)
This factors into two standard normal distribution densities on R2. Thus X and Y are independent, each with mean zero and variance one. This derivation is the radial form of the Box-Muller transform.

For each i, let Ri​ be the relative rank of ai​ among a1​,…,ai​, with the smallest value assigned rank one. Then Yi​=1{Ri​=1}​. The independent relative ranks of a uniform random permutation can be proved directly: for any choices ri​∈{1,…,i}, insert the index i at position ri​ in the list of previous indices ordered by their values. After all insertions, assign the values 1,…,n along that list. This is a unique permutation with those relative ranks, and conversely every permutation yields one such insertion sequence.
There are ∏i=1n​i=n! possible insertion sequences, all equally likely because the uniform random permutation is uniform. Thus
P(R1​=r1​,…,Rn​=rn​)=n!1​=∏i=1n​i1​.
(51)
The ranks are independent and each is uniform on its i possible values. It follows that the independent record indicators have
P(Yi​=1)=i1​,P(Yi​=0)=1−i1​,Yi​∼Bernoulli(1/i).​
(52)
The first indicator is the constant one, which is also independent of the others. For Kn​=∑i=1n​Yi​, additivity of expectation and variance additivity for independent random variables give
EKn​=Hn​=i=1∑n​i1​,VarKn​=Hn​−i=1∑n​i21​.​
(53)
Here Hn​ is the harmonic number.
Let T be the year index of the second record. For 2≤i≤n, independence gives
P(T=i)=[j=2∏i−1​(1−j1​)]i1​=i(i−1)1​.​
(54)
The empty product for i=2 is one. There is no second record in the first n years with probability

(55)
This exposes a finite-horizon qualification in the final request: a permutation of length n alone does not specify when a record outside those years will occur. Under the usual continuation by an infinite sequence of independent, identically distributed observations with a continuous distribution, the same relative-rank argument holds for every finite prefix. Then P(T=i)=1/[i(i−1)] for every i≥2, and P(T>m)=1/m. These probabilities sum to one, so the second record occurs almost surely, but
