The exterior derivative is an R-linear operator d:Ωr(M)→Ωr+1(M) which agrees with the differential of a smooth function on degree zero, satisfies d2=0, and obeys the graded Leibniz rule
d(α∧β)=dα∧β+(−1)rα∧dβ,α∈Ωr(M).
(1)
For the uniqueness of the exterior derivative from its axioms, first note that these axioms force locality. If α vanishes near p, choose a smooth bump function χ equal to one near p and supported where α=0. Then 0=d(χα)=dχ∧α+χdα gives dα(p)=0. Thus dα(p) depends only on the form near p.
In a coordinate chart write α=∑I​aI​dxi1​∧⋯∧dxir​. Since d(dxi)=d2xi=0, the axioms force
dα=∑I,j​∂j​aI​dxj∧dxi1​∧⋯∧dxir​.
(2)
This also proves existence. Define d by that formula in each chart. The product rule for partial derivatives gives the graded Leibniz rule, and symmetry of mixed partial derivatives against antisymmetry of the wedge product of differential forms gives d2=0. On overlaps it still agrees with the differential of every function, by the chain rule, so the preceding uniqueness argument in the second coordinate system makes the formulas agree. They therefore glue to a global exterior derivative.
For a differential one-form ω=∑i​ai​dxi, the exterior derivative of a one-form evaluated on vector fields follows directly:
dω(X,Y)=∑i​(X(ai​)Yi−Y(ai​)Xi).
(3)
In X(ω(Y))−Y(ω(X)), the additional terms are ∑i​ai​[X(Yi)−Y(Xi)]=ω([X,Y]), where the bracket is the Lie bracket of vector fields. Hence
dω(X,Y)=X(ω(Y))−Y(ω(X))−ω([X,Y])​.
(4)
The de Rham cohomology is
HdRr​(M)=im(d:Ωr−1(M)→Ωr(M))ker(d:Ωr(M)→Ωr+1(M))​,
(5)
with zero denominator in degree zero. The Poincaré lemma states that a closed differential form of positive degree on a star-shaped open subset of Euclidean space is an exact differential form; consequently every closed positive-degree form is locally exact on a smooth manifold.
For the suspension isomorphism for top de Rham cohomology of spheres, put U=Sn∖{south pole} and V=Sn∖{north pole}. Both are diffeomorphic to Rn, while W=U∩V deformation retracts onto the equatorial Sn−1. The Mayer--Vietoris sequence for de Rham cohomology comes from the short exact sequence of cochain complexes
0⟶Ω∗(Sn)⟶Ω∗(U)⊕Ω∗(V)(α,β)↦α∣W​−β∣W​​Ω∗(W)⟶0.
(6)
The final map is surjective: a partition of unity χU​+χV​=1 lets a form η on W be lifted by extending χV​η to U and −χU​η to V by zero. The Poincaré lemma makes Hn−1(U),Hn−1(V),Hn(U),Hn(V) vanish when n>1. Exactness gives
0⟶HdRn−1​(W)δ​HdRn​(Sn)⟶0.
(7)
If j:Sn−1↪W is the equatorial inclusion, homotopy invariance of de Rham cohomology makes j∗ an isomorphism. Therefore
j∗δ−1:HdRn​(Sn)⟶HdRn−1​(Sn−1)​
(8)
is injective, indeed an isomorphism. Concretely, choose local primitives dαU​=ω∣U​, dαV​=ω∣V​, and send [ω] to [j∗(αU​−αV​)]. Their difference is closed; changing either primitive changes it by an exact form, so this agrees with the cohomological construction.

A d-dimensional embedded submanifold F⊆Mm has the subspace topology and, near each of its points, a manifold chart identifying it with a coordinate slice Rd×{0}. These slice charts for an embedded submanifold give its smooth structure, and its inclusion is a smooth embedding.
A regular value q∈Nn of a smooth map f:Mm→Nn is one for which dfp​:Tp​M→Tq​N is surjective at every p∈f−1(q). If the preimage is nonempty this forces m≥n.
The inverse function theorem used here says that if G:U⊆Rm→Rm is smooth and DGp​ is an invertible matrix, then its restriction to a suitable neighbourhood of p is a diffeomorphism onto an open neighbourhood of G(p), with smooth inverse.
To prove the regular level set theorem, fix p∈f−1(q) and choose coordinates centred at p,q. The coordinate representative f​ has a rank-n Jacobian matrix. After permuting domain coordinates, its first n columns form an invertible matrix. Define
G(x)=(f​1(x),…,f​n(x),xn+1,…,xm).
(9)
Its Jacobian matrix is block triangular with invertible diagonal blocks, so the inverse function theorem makes G a local coordinate change. In these coordinates, f−1(q) is precisely the slice with first n coordinates zero. Thus it is an embedded submanifold of dimension m−n, with
Tp​f−1(q)=kerdfp​​.
(10)
An empty preimage is harmless; the local assertion is then vacuous.
Three equivalent formulations of orientability of a smooth manifold are: an oriented atlas whose transition Jacobian determinants are positive; a smoothly varying orientation of a vector space on each tangent space; and a nowhere-vanishing smooth top-degree differential form. An oriented atlas gives compatible oriented coordinate frames. A partition of unity glues their positive local top forms, and a nonzero top form declares a frame positive precisely when it evaluates positively on that frame. These constructions prove the equivalence.
For the orientability of a regular fibre, write F=f−1(q). The derivative gives a short exact sequence of vector bundles
0⟶TF⟶TM∣F​df​F×Tq​N⟶0.
(11)
This is the reason the normal bundle of a regular fibre is trivial. Choose any fixed positive basis b1​,…,bn​ of Tq​N and an ambient orientation on M. Declare a frame v1​,…,vm−n​ of Tp​F positive when
b1​,…,bn​,v1​,…,vm−n​
(12)
is positive in Tp​M, where dfp​(bi​)=bi​. Changing a lift adds a tangent vector and leaves the determinant sign unchanged. Local smooth lifts exist because df is surjective, so this rule varies smoothly and agrees on overlaps. It gives a global orientation on F. Only an orientation of the single vector space Tq​N was chosen; N itself need not be orientable.

An orthogonal structure on a real vector bundle E of rank r is a choice of local frames whose transition matrices lie in the orthogonal group O(r). In such frames declare
h(∑i​ai​ei​,∑i​bi​ei​)=∑i​ai​bi​.
(13)
Orthogonal transitions preserve this expression, so it defines a smooth positive-definite fiber metric. Conversely, a fiber metric turns any local frame into a smooth orthonormal frame by the Gram-Schmidt process; the resulting transition matrices are orthogonal. The two constructions are inverse, giving the required equivalence.
An orthogonal local trivialization E∣U​≅U×Rr identifies each fiber isometrically with standard Euclidean space. Equivalently, its coordinate frame satisfies h(ei​,ej​)=δij​. This is local and does not assert the existence of a global frame.
Every real vector bundle over a smooth manifold admits a fiber metric. Choose a trivializing open cover and local Euclidean metrics hi​. The partition of unity theorem for a Hausdorff second-countable smooth manifold supplies nonnegative smooth functions ρi​, with locally finite supports contained in the respective cover members, such that ∑i​ρi​=1. Set
h=∑i​ρi​hi​,
(14)
extending each weighted metric by zero off its chart. Local finiteness makes this smooth. For every nonzero fiber vector v, at least one positive weight gives h(v,v)=∑i​ρi​hi​(v,v)>0. Thus h is a fiber metric and provides an orthogonal structure.
One definition of an orthogonal connection is that its covariant derivative preserves the fiber metric:
X(h(s,t))=h(∇X​s,t)+h(s,∇X​t)
(15)
for all vector fields X and smooth sections of a vector bundle s,t. Another is that its parallel transport along every smooth curve is an isometry between the endpoint fibers.
For the first implication, take parallel sections s,t along a curve. Their covariant derivatives vanish, so the displayed identity makes h(s,t) constant: parallel transport preserves a fibre metric. Conversely, if parallel transport preserves the inner product, choose parallel extensions of arbitrary initial fiber vectors along a curve with prescribed initial tangent X. Differentiating their constant inner product gives (∇X​h)(s,t)=0 at the initial point. Since X,s,t were arbitrary, the connection is metric-compatible.
The equivalent local description is that the connection matrix in an orthonormal frame is skew-symmetric. Writing ∇ej​=∑i​Aij​ei​ in an orthogonal trivialization, differentiate h(ei​,ej​)=δij​ to obtain
Aij​+Aji​=0,AT=−A​.
(16)
Conversely this matrix identity gives the metric-preservation formula by expanding arbitrary sections. It is the orthogonal group connection description of the same condition.

Let D be the ambient Levi-Civita connection and ∇ the one for the induced metric on M. The Gauss formula is
DX​Y=∇X​Y+II(X,Y),
(17)
where the second fundamental form II(X,Y)=(DX​Y)⊥ is normal. The tangential part of D is metric-compatible and torsion-free, so the existence and uniqueness of the Levi-Civita connection identifies it with ∇.
For a normal field ξ, the Weingarten formula defines the shape operator in a normal direction Aξ​ and the normal connection by
DX​ξ=−Aξ​X+∇X⊥​ξ.
(18)
Differentiate ⟨ξ,Y⟩=0 using metric compatibility to get
⟨Aξ​X,Y⟩=⟨II(X,Y),ξ⟩.
(19)
The symmetry of the second fundamental form follows from the ambient torsion-free condition:
II(X,Y)−II(Y,X)=(DX​Y−DY​X−[X,Y])⊥=0,
(20)
since the Lie bracket of vector fields tangent to M is tangent. Therefore
⟨Aξ​X,Y⟩=⟨X,Aξ​Y⟩​,
(21)
so every normal-direction shape operator is self-adjoint.
The paper uses R(X,Y)=D[X,Y]​−[DX​,DY​], so use the Gauss equation with reversed curvature convention. Applying the Gauss formula and Weingarten formula twice gives
(DX​DY​Z)⊤=∇X​∇Y​Z−AII(Y,Z)​X.
(22)
Subtracting in the stated curvature order yields
(RV(X,Y)Z)⊤=RM(X,Y)Z+AII(Y,Z)​X−AII(X,Z)​Y.
(23)
Pair with a tangent field W and use the defining relation for the shape operator in a normal direction:
⟨RM(X,Y)Z,W⟩=⟨RV(X,Y)Z,W⟩−⟨II(X,W),II(Y,Z)⟩+⟨II(Y,W),II(X,Z)⟩​.
(24)
This is the Gauss equation in the supplied sign convention. If one defines curvature by [DX​,DY​]−D[X,Y]​ instead, the two quadratic terms have the opposite signs.
For an orthonormal frame X,Y on a surface, positive Gaussian curvature with the convention here is K=⟨RM(X,Y)X,Y⟩. If the ambient space is Euclidean and ξ is a unit normal, the equation becomes
K=⟨II(X,X),II(Y,Y)⟩−∥II(X,Y)∥2=detAξ​​.
(25)
The Koszul formula determines ∇ from the induced metric, and hence determines its Riemann curvature tensor and K. Therefore the Gaussian curvature depends only on that metric, even though its expression as a product of principal curvatures appears extrinsic. This is Theorema Egregium.

Use the standard boundaryless-manifold convention, so M is a closed manifold. Its orientation and Riemannian metric determine a unique positive Riemannian volume form μg​ taking value one on every positively oriented orthonormal frame. In an oriented atlas it is
μg​=det(gij​)​dx1∧⋯∧dxn.
(26)
For the coordinate invariance of the Riemannian volume form, on an overlap put J=∂y/∂x. Then gx​=JTgy​J and dy1∧⋯∧dyn=(detJ)dx1∧⋯∧dxn. The oriented transition has detJ>0, so the square root of the metric determinant and the coordinate volume transform together. The local forms therefore agree and define μg​ globally.
For real r-forms the Hodge star operator is defined by
α∧∗β=⟨α,β⟩g​μg​.
(27)
The pointwise wedge pairing is nondegenerate, so this uniquely defines a smooth map ∗:Ωr(M)→Ωn−r(M). On an oriented orthonormal coframe, it sends a basis wedge to the signed complementary wedge. Applying it twice gives
∗2α=(−1)r(n−r)α.
(28)
For Hodge star eigenvalues on graded differential forms, extend ∗ complex-linearly to the direct sum of all form degrees. This interpretation matters: on a fixed degree it is an endomorphism only in middle degree. In positive odd dimension, r(n−r) is even for every r, so ∗2=I and only ±1 can occur. Both occur, with eigenforms 1+μg​ and 1−μg​.
In positive even dimension, ∗2 is +I on even-degree forms and −I on odd-degree forms. Hence ∗4=I and the only possible eigenvalues are ±1,±i. The first two occur as above. For the latter, choose a smooth local orthonormal frame and multiply its first dual differential one-form by a nonzero smooth bump function supported in the chart. This gives a form η for which η and ∗η are independent. For λ=±i,
∗(η+λ−1∗η)=λ(η+λ−1∗η).
(29)
Thus all four occur. Consequently, for n≥1,
Spec(∗)={{1,−1},{1,−1,i,−i},​n odd,n even.​​
(30)
On degree m in dimension 2m, the Hodge star has eigenvalues ±1 for even m and ±i for odd m; signed complementary middle-degree wedges show both occur for m≥1. In zero dimensions it is multiplication by the signed unit volume at each point, so only the signs actually present occur.
For Hodge integration by parts in arbitrary degree, let α∈Ωr−1(M) and β∈Ωr(M). The graded Leibniz rule and Stokes theorem on the closed manifold give
0=∫M​d(α∧∗β)=∫M​dα∧∗β+(−1)r−1∫M​α∧d∗β.
(31)
Using the given codifferential and the square of the Hodge star,
∗δβ=(−1)n(r+1)+1+(n−r+1)(r−1)d∗β=(−1)rd∗β.
(32)
Therefore
⟨dα,β⟩L2​=∫M​dα∧∗β=∫M​α∧∗δβ=⟨α,δβ⟩L2​​,
(33)
which proves that δ is the formal adjoint of d. The complex version inserts conjugation in the usual Hermitian inner product.
The Hodge decomposition theorem gives an L2-orthogonal decomposition
Ωk(M)=Hk(M)⊕dΩk−1(M)⊕δΩk+1(M),
(34)
where Hk(M)=kerΔ is finite dimensional and consists of the harmonic differential forms. Each de Rham cohomology class has a unique harmonic representative. Equivalently, with harmonic projection PH​ there is a smooth Green operator of the Hodge Laplacian G satisfying ΔG=GΔ=I−PH​.
The solvability condition for the Hodge Poisson equation is
Δα=β is solvable⟺⟨β,h⟩L2​=0 for every h∈Hk(M)​.
(35)
Necessity follows from ⟨Δα,h⟩=⟨α,Δh⟩=0. Conversely the orthogonality condition gives PH​β=0, so α0​=Gβ solves the Poisson equation for differential forms.
The nonnegativity of the Hodge Laplacian identity
⟨Δη,η⟩L2​=∥dη∥L22​+∥δη∥L22​
(36)
identifies its kernel with closed and coclosed forms. Thus the affine space of solutions of the Hodge Poisson equation is
α0​+Hk(M)​.
(37)
For completeness, if a closed form is decomposed as ω=h+dξ+δζ, then dδζ=0, and ∥δζ∥2=⟨ζ,dδζ⟩=0. Hence its cohomology class is represented by h. An exact harmonic form h=dξ must vanish, since ∥h∥2=⟨δh,ξ⟩=0. This proves the canonical isomorphism Hk(M)≅HdRk​(M), so the solution set is an affine space whose translation vector space is isomorphic to the requested de Rham cohomology.
