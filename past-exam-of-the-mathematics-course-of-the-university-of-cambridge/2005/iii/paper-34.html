For a martingale and a stopping time T≤N, expand the stopped value using its martingale differences:
MT​−M0​=∑k=1N​1{T≥k}​(Mk​−Mk−1​).
(1)
The event {T≥k}={T>k−1} belongs to the filtration Fk−1​. Each summand is integrable and its conditional expectation given Fk−1​ is zero. Taking expectations of this finite sum therefore proves EMT​=EM0​ for every bounded stopping time. This proves the forward direction of the bounded-stopping-time characterization of a martingale without assuming an optional stopping theorem.

The almost sure submartingale convergence theorem states that a discrete-time submartingale Yn​ satisfying supn​EYn+​<∞ converges almost surely to a finite integrable limit. In particular, a martingale with supn​E∣Mn​∣<∞ converges almost surely to an integrable M∞​, and E∣M∞​∣≤supn​E∣Mn​∣ by Fatou's lemma. This is the martingale convergence theorem. First construct a centred Gaussian process on the countable dyadic set with covariance function E(Bs​Bt​)=min(s,t). These consistent finite-dimensional normal distributions exist because, for coefficients aj​,
∑i,j​ai​aj​min(ti​,tj​)=∫01​(∑j​aj​1{u≤tj​}​)2du≥0;
(14)
the Kolmogorov extension theorem realizes them on one probability space. Every increment has the normal distribution N(0,∣t−s∣), so for every finite p≥1,
∥Bt​−Bs​∥p​=(E∣Z∣p)1/p∣t−s∣1/2.
(15)
Use the preceding estimate with β=1/2, p>2 and 0<α<1/2−1/p. It yields an almost surely Hölder continuous extension from the dense dyadic set to [0,1]. Approximate arbitrary times by dyadic times; continuity of the paths and of the Gaussian covariance function preserves all finite-dimensional laws. Disjoint increments are jointly Gaussian with zero cross-covariances, hence independent, with the required variances. Together with B0​=0 and continuity, this constructs Brownian motion.
For any α<1/2, choose p large enough. Taking a countable increasing sequence of exponents tending to 1/2 gives simultaneous Hölder regularity of every order α<1/2 on a common probability-one set; the same argument on each compact time interval gives local Brownian Hölder regularity. This method does not give the endpoint exponent. In fact that endpoint fails: if a path had a finite 1/2-Hölder seminorm K, all its 2n adjacent normalized increments at level n would have magnitude at most K. These are independent standard normal random variables, so for a fixed integer m the probability that their maximum is at most m is P(∣Z∣≤m)2n→0. The event of a finite K is contained in the union over m of events with this bound at every level, each of probability zero. Thus Brownian paths are not 1/2-Hölder continuous on [0,1] almost surely.

For 0<δ<1, define the compensated small-jump Poisson integral
Ytδ​=∫(0,t]×{δ<∣y∣≤1}​y(μ−ν)(dy,ds).
(33)
This is an ordinary compensated finite-intensity sum. Independent Poisson random variables give mean zero and the L2 construction of compensated Poisson integrals gives, for 0<η<δ,
E∣Ytη​−Ytδ​∣2=t∫{η<∣y∣≤δ}​y2K(dy)=2ct(δ−η).
(34)
Thus Ytδ​ is Cauchy in L2 space as δ↓0, defining the first integral. It is not the difference of two separately convergent integrals. On a compact time interval [0,L], the Doob L2 maximal inequality bounds the squared supremum of the same difference by 8cL(δ−η). Hence the truncated martingales are Cauchy in the expected squared supremum norm. A sufficiently rapidly decreasing sequence of cutoffs converges uniformly almost surely; its limit has càdlàg paths, so the construction also supplies a consistent process.
For large jumps, K({∣y∣>1})=2c<∞. There are only finitely many such atoms before any fixed time, almost surely, and each atom has a finite mark. The uncompensated large-jump integral is therefore a finite random sum, even though its absolute first moment is infinite. The two terms are well-defined for these different reasons.
There is also a literal signed-integral defect in the PDF: ∫yK(dy) on either symmetric region is not +∞. Its positive and negative parts both have infinite mass, so the ordinary signed Lebesgue integral is undefined. The correct divergence statement is
∫0<∣y∣≤1​∣y∣K(dy)=∫∣y∣>1​∣y∣K(dy)=∞.
(35)
A symmetric principal value may be zero, but is not an ordinary signed integral. Square-integrable compensation defines the small jumps; finite activity defines the large jumps.
