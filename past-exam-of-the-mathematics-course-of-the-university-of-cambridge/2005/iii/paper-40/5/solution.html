Label the two intermediate vertices 1,2. The four outer edges have capacity M and the middle edge has capacity one. The cut separating s from the other vertices has capacity 2M, and the two paths s→1→t and s→2→t can each carry M. Thus the maximum flow is 2M.
A fast run of the Ford-Fulkerson algorithm chooses those two direct paths and augments by M on each: it terminates in two augmentations. One augmentation cannot suffice, because every initial path has bottleneck at most M.
For a run with M+1 augmentations, first choose s→1→2→t and augment by one. The middle edge then has net flow 1 from 1 to 2. Its reverse residual capacity is 1−(−1)=2, not one. Alternate crossing paths, next s→2→1→t, then s→1→2→t, and so on. Each of the next M−1 augmentations has bottleneck two. More explicitly, after k crossing augmentations, 1≤k≤M, the total flow is 2k−1, and the outer-edge flow totals on the pairs (s1,2t) and (s2,1t) are k and k−1 in one order or the other. The middle net flow is ±1. The next opposite crossing path uses the less-loaded outer pair and has reverse-middle capacity two. Until k=M, its outer residual capacities are at least two.
After these M augmentations, one outer pair is full and the other has one unit left. One final opposite crossing augmentation of one reaches 2M. The total amounts are 1, then M−1 copies of 2, then 1, giving exactly M+1 augmentations. For M=1 the middle block is empty and this gives two augmentations as well. These are explicit requested path-choice histories. In an undirected network, M+1 need not be a bound on every possible history: for M=2, choosing the crossing path, the two direct paths, and then the crossing path again gives four successive unit augmentations.
For a general undirected network, let yij​=xij​−xji​ be the antisymmetric net flow. Feasibility gives ∣yij​∣≤cij​, so the directed residual network has nonnegative capacities cij′​=cij​−yij​. For any cut S with s∈S, t∈/S, conservation at the internal vertices gives
∑i∈S,j∈/S​yij​=f.
(22)
Thus its outgoing residual cut capacity is
C′(S)=∑i∈S,j∈/S​(cij​−yij​)=C(S)−f.
(23)
The subtracted flow value is the same for every cut. Taking the minimum and applying the max-flow min-cut theorem to the original and residual networks proves the residual maximum flow is the remaining optimality gap identity
f′=f∗−f​.
(24)
Suppose now f′>0, and let U consist of vertices reachable from s through residual arcs of capacity at least f′/m. If t∈/U, every outgoing arc from U has capacity strictly below f′/m, since its head would otherwise be reachable. At most m original undirected edges cross this cut, and each supplies exactly one outgoing directed arc. Hence C′(U)<m(f′/m)=f′, contradicting the residual minimum-cut value f′. Therefore t∈U​, and a widest augmenting path has bottleneck at least f′/m. When f′=0, the current flow is already optimal and no such path is needed.
Let Fk​ be the remaining residual maximum flow after k augmentations of the modified rule, starting from zero flow. Its chosen widest path increases the flow by at least Fk​/m, so
Fk+1​≤(1−m1​)Fk​,Fk​≤(1−m1​)kf∗​.
(25)
This is the geometric convergence of widest-path augmentation. For m≥2, Fk​≤e−k/mf∗. Starting from zero with integer capacities keeps all flow values and residual optimality gaps integer. Thus k>mlnf∗ makes Fk​<1 and hence Fk​=0, giving termination in O(mlogf∗)​ augmentations for f∗≥2. If f∗=1, one positive integer augmentation suffices; if f∗=0, none is required. A uniform statement is O(mlog(1+f∗)). With m=1, a nonzero flow is also completed in one augmentation, consistent with the recurrence's zero contraction factor.
