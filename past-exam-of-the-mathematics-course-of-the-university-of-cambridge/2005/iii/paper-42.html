The profile likelihood for the parameter of interest replaces the nuisance parameter by its constrained best fit: Lp​(ψ)=supλ​L(ψ,λ), with ℓp​(ψ)=ℓ(ψ,λψ​). It is not in general a normalized sampling density. A conditional likelihood instead uses the sampling law conditional on a statistic carrying the nuisance dependence. In the canonical exponential family here, conditioning on S removes λ because the factor involving λ is constant over the conditioning fibre.
Let Tˉ1​=n−1∑i​τ1​(yi​) and let the observed S equal s. For the cumulant-generating function of τ2​(Y) at fixed ψ,
K(t)=d(ψ,λ+t)−d(ψ,λ).
(1)
The saddlepoint solves K′(t)=s, equivalently dλ​(ψ,λψ​)=s with λψ​=λ+t. This is also the constrained likelihood equation, since ℓλ​=n(s−dλ​). Assume an interior saddle, dλλ​>0, and the usual smooth nonlattice conditions under which S has a density. Exponential tilting to λψ​ makes s the tilted mean. A local normal approximation gives tilted density {n/(2πdλλ​(ψ,λψ​))}1/2 at that mean; undoing the tilt gives the saddlepoint density approximation
f​S​(s;ψ,λ)=[2πdλλ​(ψ,λψ​)n​]1/2exp{n[d(ψ,λψ​)−d(ψ,λ)−(λψ​−λ)s]}.​
(2)
The factor n1/2 is for the sample mean, rather than the sum. A transform-based derivation gives the same expression by expanding K to second order at its stationary point.
The joint log-likelihood is nψTˉ1​+nλs−nd(ψ,λ)−∑i​Q(yi​). Subtracting logf​S​(s;ψ,λ) cancels every appearance of the original λ, leaving
ℓc​(ψ)=nψTˉ1​+nλψ​s−nd(ψ,λψ​)−∑i​Q(yi​)+21​logdλλ​(ψ,λψ​)−21​log2πn​.
(3)
Any change from the ambient density to a conditional density on S=s contributes only a data-dependent Jacobian determinant. Thus, up to a constant independent of ψ, the saddlepoint conditional likelihood adjustment is
ℓc​(ψ)=ℓp​(ψ)+B(ψ),B(ψ)=21​logdλλ​(ψ,λψ​).​
(4)
Equivalently B=21​logjλλ​(ψ,λψ​) up to the constant −21​logn, where jλλ​=ndλλ​ is the nuisance observed information. The adjustment has a positive sign. The printed exponential-family form alone does not guarantee a density for S: for example a continuous Y can have τ2​(Y)=1{Y>0}​. In a lattice case one must approximate the probability mass of the sum instead; the displayed density calculation uses the regular continuous interpretation.

A transformation model consists of a group G acting measurably and invertibly on the sample space, an induced group action on the parameter space, and a family {Pθ​} satisfying Y∼Pθ​⇒gY∼Pgθ​. In a generated, transitive model, every Pθ​ is the image of a reference distribution under some transformation. In the density formulation, the transformed density includes the appropriate sample-space Jacobian determinant; the model is a statement about whole probability laws, not merely about changing parameter names.
A maximal invariant A has two properties: A(gy)=A(y) for every g, and A(y)=A(z) implies z=gy for some g. It is therefore a coordinate for the group orbits, retaining exactly the information unaffected by the transformations. An equivariant estimator obeys T(gy)=gT(y), where the right action is the induced parameter action.
For the normalization construction, suppose the estimator determines a group element t(y)∈G with t(gy)=gt(y). Define A(y)=t(y)−1y. Then
A(gy)=(gt(y))−1gy=t(y)−1y=A(y).
(5)
If A(y)=A(z), rearranging gives z=t(z)t(y)−1y. This is an actual transformation in G, so the normalized sample is a maximal invariant. This group-valued assumption matters: an estimator of a parameter with a nontrivial stabilizer does not necessarily determine a unique normalization. For a transitive parameter space G/H, choose a transformation carrying the estimated parameter to the reference value and take the resulting normalized sample modulo the remaining H-action. Different choices then have the same residual orbit. Under a free action H is trivial and the simpler formula applies.
For a location-scale model, ga,b​(y1​,…,yn​)=(a+by1​,…,a+byn​), b>0, with composition (a,b)(c,d)=(a+bc,bd). Its parameter action is (μ,σ)↦(a+bμ,bσ). Choose location and scale estimates satisfying
m(a+by)=a+bm(y),s(a+by)=bs(y)>0.
(6)
For instance, on nonconstant samples take the sample mean and the positive square root of n−1∑i​(yi​−yˉ​)2. The standardized vector
A(y)=(s(y)yi​−m(y)​)i=1n​​
(7)
is invariant. Conversely, equality of these vectors gives zi​=c+dyi​ for all i, with d=s(z)/s(y)>0 and c=m(z)−dm(y). Hence it is maximal, not just invariant. Degenerate constant samples have zero fitted scale and form a separate orbit; define a separate invariant value there. The construction is on labelled sample vectors: one must not discard their order unless permutations are also included in the transformation group.

Let Xi​=Yi​−θ0​. Under the symmetric continuous null hypothesis, positive and negative signs have probability 1/2 and zeros have probability zero. For the sign test, take N+​=∑i​1{Xi​>0}​ and reject for small values: N+​∼Bin(n,1/2) under the null. Choose k with P0​(N+​≤k)≤α; randomization at the next value attains exact size if desired. The lower tail is appropriate for θ<θ0​.
For the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, rank ∣Xi​∣ increasingly and put W+​=∑i​Ri​1{Xi​>0}​. Continuous observations have no absolute-value ties almost surely. Conditional on the absolute values, the signs are independent fair signs because symmetry makes the sign independent of magnitude. under sufficient moment and smoothness/nonlattice conditions. Here H3​=z3−3z, H4​=z4−6z2+3, H6​=z6−15z4+45z2−15 are probabilists' Hermite polynomials. Parameters ψ and λ are orthogonal parameters if the cross block of the expected Fisher information matrix is zero throughout the relevant parameter region:
Iψλ​=Eθ​[ℓψ​ℓλT​]=−Eθ​ℓψλ​=0.
(32)

For regular maximum-likelihood estimators, n​(θ−θ) has limiting covariance equal to the inverse per-observation Fisher information matrix. Block diagonality therefore gives asymptotically independent fitted interest and nuisance parameters. The efficient information Iψψ​−Iψλ​Iλλ−1​Iλψ​ reduces to Iψψ​ at an orthogonal parametrization, although nuisance estimation can still matter at higher orders.

Let W=2[ℓ(θ)−ℓ(θ)] be a regular likelihood-ratio test statistic for r smooth restrictions, so its leading null law is χr2​. Suppose a higher-order calculation gives E0​W=r+a/n+O(n−2). The Bartlett correction rescales the statistic:
WB​=1+a/(rn)W​.​
(36)
Its mean is r+O(n−2), eliminating the first-order mean error. In regular likelihood models satisfying the usual Bartlett expansion, the same scaling removes the leading n−1 distributional error against the chi-square reference law, leaving an O(n−2) error for fixed tail probabilities. Matching a mean alone would not establish this distributional conclusion for an arbitrary statistic.
The coefficient depends on the model, null restrictions and nuisance parameters; it can be obtained from higher derivatives and cumulants of the log-likelihood, or estimated by null simulation when an analytic expression is inconvenient. Nuisance parameters require their appropriate null values or estimates. A correction improves finite-sample calibration within regular asymptotic theory; it does not turn a boundary-parameter or unidentifiable problem into a regular chi-square problem.

In statistical decision theory, the statistical invariance principle says that a procedure should respect transformations that leave the inferential problem unchanged. If a group action preserves the model and carries each of the null and alternative parameter sets into itself, an invariant test has rejection probability function φ(gy)=φ(y). A maximal invariant identifies its orbits, so every invariant test factors through that statistic: define its value at an orbit using any representative; invariance makes the definition independent of the representative. If the group is transitive on the null parameter set, the null law of any invariant statistic is free of that nuisance parameter.
For example, testing a normal mean μ=μ0​ with unknown scale, positive transformations yi​−μ0​↦b(yi​−μ0​) preserve a one-sided mean alternative. The statistic n​(Yˉ−μ0​)/s, with the usual unbiased sample-variance scale s, is invariant and has the Student t-distribution with n−1 degrees of freedom under the null. For estimation the corresponding requirement is an equivariant estimator, together with a loss function invariant under simultaneous transformation of decision and parameter. Invariance alone does not prove unrestricted optimality or supply a unique best procedure.
There is a distinct probabilistic use of the term: the Donsker invariance principle states that centred iid partial sums, normalized by σn​, converge as processes on [0,1] to Brownian motion. Its empirical-process version gives a Brownian bridge, used by goodness-of-fit tests in the next note. These functional limit theorems should be distinguished from the group-based inferential principle.

Population influence functions describe infinitesimal contamination; finite-sample analogues examine actual changes to an empirical sample. For its empirical distribution Fn​, add an observation z and define the sensitivity curve
SCn​(z)=(n+1)[T(n+1n​Fn​+n+11​δz​)−T(Fn​)].

For a regular iid model with a fixed-dimensional interior parameter θ0​, identifiability, sufficient differentiability and moment domination, and nonsingular per-observation Fisher information I(θ0​), a consistent maximum-likelihood estimator solves the score equation. Expanding it gives
n​(θ−θ0​)=I(θ0​)−1n​1​∑i​sθ0​​(Yi​)+op​(1)⇒Np​(0,I(θ0​)−1),
(41)
where sθ​=∂θ​logfθ​. The score has mean zero and covariance I under the regularity assumptions. This proves the usual large-sample information bound and asymptotic efficiency within regular parametric inference. The observed curvature j(θ)/n and the empirical score covariance consistently estimate I when the model is correctly specified.

The likelihood-ratio test and score test, evaluated using the constrained fit and nuisance-adjusted efficient information, have the same first-order chi-square law. A quadratic local likelihood expansion explains their asymptotic equivalence. Profile likelihood supplies inference about a parameter of interest with fitted nuisance parameters, and the corresponding chi-square cutoffs give approximate confidence regions.
These conclusions depend on the stated regularity: parameters at a boundary, nonidentifiability, singular information, changing dimension, or parameter-dependent support can give different rates and limits. Under misspecification, the fitted parameter generally approaches a pseudo-true value and its variance is a sandwich covariance matrix, not simply inverse Fisher information.
