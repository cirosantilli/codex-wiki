The rotated global relations form a square system for the three missing boundary traces. Solve this system and substitute the resulting finite-time spectral boundary transforms into the integral representation. The pieces containing Q(kj​,T) have zero contour contribution by analyticity, rotation of the relevant sectors and the Cauchy integral theorem. The remaining expression involves only Q0​,F0​,G0​,G1​. The finite-interval Airy spectral determinant has no nonremovable zeros in the contour domains, as established below, so this elimination introduces no extra spectral data there. Differentiating the representation, and using temporal inversion and compatibility at the corners, verifies the partial differential equation, initial value and the three prescribed boundary values.
For a direct Hadamard well-posedness check, the homogeneous difference satisfies
dtd​21​∫0L​∣q∣2dx=−21​∣qx​(0,t)∣2≤0.
(11)
This proves uniqueness and continuous dependence for homogeneous boundary data. More generally subtract a smooth quadratic boundary lift b(x,t) satisfying the three prescribed endpoint conditions. The remaining problem has homogeneous boundary conditions and forcing −bt​−bxxx​. Its semigroup generator is A=−d3/dx3 on H3(0,L) with v(0)=v(L)=v′(L)=0. It is a dissipative operator; for every real ζ>0 the equation (ζ−A)v=h has a unique solution, since the homogeneous third-order boundary system is injective by the same energy identity and therefore invertible. Thus A is maximal dissipative and generates a contraction semigroup. This gives existence and continuous dependence on the lifted data and forcing. Smooth compatible data therefore define a well-posed problem. This operator argument and the spectral elimination describe the same evolution.

In ideal computed tomography, the unknown is an absorption coefficient a(x). Along a straight ray the transmitted intensity satisfies dI/dτ=−aI, so
logIout​Iin​​=∫R​a(τd+ρn)dτ=Ra(ρ,θ).
(33)
Thus calibrated transmission measurements supply the Radon transform of the slice. The inversion above reconstructs the coefficient by filtered backprojection: filter each projection in its transverse variable and then accumulate it over the rays through each image point. This turns a collection of line-integral measurements into a spatial map, which is the key usefulness of the result.
The ideal derivation assumes known ray geometry, sufficiently complete angular sampling and a straight-ray attenuation model. The ramp Fourier multiplier grows at high frequency, so measurement noise is amplified. Practical reconstruction therefore uses discretization and regularization of an inverse problem, for example a window on the ramp filter. Limited angles or missing detector positions cannot be treated as if they were complete noiseless Radon transform data.

In single-photon emission computed tomography, the unknown is an emitting source, rather than the absorption coefficient reconstructed by transmission computed tomography. Photons emitted at different points on the same ray are weighted by their probability of surviving the path to the detector. If the known attenuation is a and the detector is at the positive end of the ray, the data are an attenuated Radon transform
Ra​f(ρ,θ)=∫R​f(τd+ρn)exp[−∫τ∞​a(sd+ρn)ds]dτ.
(34)
The directional transport equation is d⋅∇u+au=f with zero incoming intensity. An integrating factor derives this attenuation weight. The same complexification, boundary-value and jump strategy gives spectral reconstruction of an attenuated Radon transform when attenuation is known. The unattenuated inverse from part (d) applies directly only when a=0; for nonzero attenuation the weighted jump and inverse must be used. This distinction allows emission imaging of the source without confusing it with reconstruction of the absorbing medium.

Neither factor vanishes for λ>0. For sufficiently smooth compatible traces, the periodic extension of q is continuous and piecewise smooth; the Fourier series recovers q, with the usual endpoint convention. Reality implies Q−n​=Qn​​ even though the sampling formula uses complex arguments.
For λ=0, first impose the zero-flux compatibility from part (c). For n=0 take the nonzero root kn​=2πin/l and use the same coefficient formula with λ=0. The coefficient Q0​ is an arbitrary real constant times l, expressing the additive constant for this problem with a Neumann boundary condition. The zero sample cannot determine it. For example, f=0 admits every constant solution, so no uniquely specified q(s) is possible without a normalization.
For completeness, negative parameters can be handled without assuming away resonances. Let ϕj​ be a real orthonormal eigenbasis of the Neumann Laplacian, with −Δϕj​=μj​ϕj​, and define
Fj​=∫∂D​fϕj​ds,
(59)
using the corresponding oriented f on each side. The weak equation and Green's first identity give the explicit coefficient relation
(μj​+4λ)⟨q,ϕj​⟩=Fj​.
(60)
At a nonresonant parameter this determines the unique solution. Its common trace is the continuation of the displayed spectral Fourier coefficients from λ>0; choose either nonzero root of kn2​−(2πin/l)kn​+λ=0. If an individual quotient is 0/0 at a nonresonant parameter, use its removable limit in λ, rather than declare a new eigenvalue. For example, a double sampling root can make kn​−λ/kn​=0 without making the boundary problem singular. The weak coefficient relation proves that the solution is analytic in the parameter wherever no μj​+4λ vanishes, so these nonresonant limits exist.
At a genuine resonance λ=−μ∗​/4, the Fredholm solvability condition is precisely Fj​=0 for every μj​=μ∗​. Necessity follows directly from the coefficient relation. For compatible smooth data, sufficiency follows by dividing all nonresonant coefficients and leaving the resonant ones free. The boundary functional is continuous on H1(D), so ∑j​∣Fj​∣2/(1+μj​)<∞; away from the finite resonant eigenspace this implies ∑j​(1+μj​)∣Fj​/(μj​−μ∗​)∣2<∞. Thus the resulting series converges in H1(D) and satisfies the weak equation, not just its individual coefficient identities. The full solution and its boundary values are thus
q(x,y)=∑μj​=μ∗​​μj​−μ∗​Fj​​ϕj​(x,y)+∑μj​=μ∗​​bj​ϕj​(x,y),bj​∈R,
(61)
with convergence in the weak solution space and the associated trace sense. Rotation averaging selects the common-side component; a nonsymmetric homogeneous addition can have different traces on the three sides. Incompatible resonant data give no solution. For arbitrary real λ, compatibility and the free homogeneous components are essential; the source does not specify conditions that would select a unique trace at resonance.
