Let λ=(λ1​≥λ2​≥⋯) be a partition of an integer n. Its Young diagram consists of cells (i,j) with 1≤j≤λi​, and its conjugate partition has parts λj′​=#{i:λi​≥j}. A numbered Young tableau fills these cells bijectively with 1,…,n. Its row and column stabilizers Rt​,Ct​ permute entries within each row and each column respectively. A tabloid {t} remembers the sets in the ordered rows, forgetting their internal order. The Young subgroup Rt​≅∏i​Sλi​​ is its stabilizer, so the Young permutation module is
Mλ=F{tabloids of shape λ}≅IndRt​Sn​​F.
(1)
Here the group acts by permuting numbered entries and extending linearly. A submodule is an invariant linear subspace. Give the tabloid basis its orthonormal symmetric bilinear form, denoted ⟨ , ⟩. This form is nondegenerate and invariant under the group. Define the column antisymmetrizer and polytabloid by
bt​=∑g∈Ct​​sgn(g)g∈FSn​,et​=bt​{t},Sλ=spanF​{et​}.
(2)
Thus Sλ is the Specht module, generated by any one et​, because the group is transitive on numbered Young tableaux. The orthogonal complement (Sλ)⊥ consists of vectors pairing to zero with every polytabloid.
The key calculation is
bt​v=⟨v,et​⟩et​(v∈Mλ).​
(3)
To prove it, first take v={u}. If two entries of one column of t occur in one row of u, their transposition s fixes {u}; pairing the terms indexed by g and gs cancels bt​{u}. This also works in characteristic two, where the paired equal terms have sum zero. Otherwise each row of u contains at most one entry from each column of t. The first row has λ1​ entries and there are λ1​ columns, so it uses every column once. Removing those entries, the second row must use exactly the columns of remaining positive height, and induction does the same for all rows. Consequently there is a unique h∈Ct​ with {u}={ht}. Uniqueness follows from Rt​∩Ct​=1. In that case
bt​{u}=sgn(h)et​,⟨{u},et​⟩=sgn(h).
(4)
Linearity proves the identity.
Now let U≤Mλ be a submodule. If U is not contained in (Sλ)⊥, choose v∈U and t with ⟨v,et​⟩=0. The displayed identity puts et​ in U, so its group translates put all of Sλ in U. This proves the James submodule theorem without a semisimplicity assumption.
Put R=Sλ∩(Sλ)⊥. If R=Sλ, every proper submodule of Sλ lies in R, by the same theorem. Hence R is its unique maximal submodule, and Dλ=Sλ/R is irreducible. If R=Sλ, the quotient is zero. This conclusion remains true after every field extension E/F: the integral tabloid construction becomes MEλ​=E⊗F​Mλ, and the radical of the restricted form is the kernel of its Gram matrix, so it commutes with extension of scalars. A nonzero Gram entry stays nonzero in E. Applying the theorem over E makes E⊗F​Dλ irreducible. Thus a nonzero quotient is absolutely irreducible.
The precise nonvanishing criterion is
Dλ=0⟺p=0 or λ is a p-regular partition.​
(5)
A regular partition has fewer than p rows of each positive length. Here is the integral pairing argument behind this condition. Write mj​ for the number of rows of length j. Expanding two polytabloids into tabloids, simultaneous permutations of equal-length rows partition of an integer all contributions to their pairing into free orbits of size ∏j​mj​!; the signs agree within each orbit because the same row permutation is applied in every relevant column on both sides. Therefore every pairing is divisible by ∏j​mj​!. If some mj​≥p, the entire restricted form is zero modulo p. Conversely, reverse the order of the entries in each row of a tableau to obtain t∗. Counting the matching column permutations in the two expansions gives, up to the harmless overall sign,
⟨et​,et∗​⟩=±∏j​(mj​!)j.
(6)
Indeed the mj​ equal rows of length j can be matched independently in each of their j positions; after these choices the decreasing row lengths force every other matching. For a regular partition this integer is nonzero modulo p, so the form and its radical quotient are nonzero. In characteristic zero it is nonzero for every shape. This supplies the requested justification of the criterion.

The dominance order on partitions is defined by
λ⊵μ⟺∑i=1r​λi​≥∑i=1r​μi​for every r.
(7)
Write λ▹μ when the shapes are distinct as well. We first explain why the nonzero Dλ from regular partitions exhaust the simple modules. The argument also provides the required constraint on composition factors.
For a regular partition λ and a tableau t of that shape, the operator bt​ has rank one on Dλ: on Sλ its image lies in Fet​, and the nonzero pairing with the row-reversed polytabloid from Question 1 shows that this image survives in Dλ. On Mμ, a nonzero bt​{u} requires every row of u to meet each column of t at most once, by the same cancellation. Consequently the first r rows of u contain at most min(r,λj′​) entries from column j. Summing gives
∑i=1r​μi​≤∑j​min(r,λj′​)=∑i=1r​λi​.
(8)
Thus nonzero column action forces λ⊵μ. In particular, if Dλ≅Dμ, its nonzero column operators applied to the realization of either simple as a composition factor of the other's Young permutation module force both dominances; hence λ=μ.
Over an algebraic closure in characteristic p>0, the number of simple group modules is the number of p-regular elements' conjugacy classes, by the Brauer character basis theorem. Such classes in Sn​ have cycle lengths not divisible by p. The Glaisher partition bijection equates their number with the number of regular partitions: expand a part pad, with p∤d, into pa copies of d, and invert by the base-p expansion of each multiplicity. The distinct absolutely irreducible Dλ therefore form the complete list. Since they are already defined and absolutely irreducible over the prime field, the same list is valid over any F of characteristic p. In characteristic zero, the analogous count uses all partitions of an integer and all conjugacy classes.
If Dλ occurs in a composition series of Mμ, its nonzero bt​ action lifts through the subquotient, so bt​Mμ=0. The inequality above proves λ⊵μ. When λ=μ is regular, bt​ has rank one on Mμ and rank one on Dμ. An operator preserving a finite filtration has rank at least the sum of its ranks on the successive quotients: relative to an adapted basis its matrix is block triangular, and nonzero pivot minors of the diagonal blocks combine to a nonzero minor. Therefore Dμ can occur at most once. It occurs at least once as the quotient of Sμ⊂Mμ. We have proved
[Mμ:Dλ]=0⇒λ⊵μ,[Mμ:Dμ]=1 if μ is regular.​
(9)
For a nonregular μ, equality is impossible because Dμ=0.
Over Q, Young's rule gives the exact decomposition
Mμ≅⨁λ⊢n​(Sλ)⊕Kλμ​.
(10)
The Kostka number Kλμ​ counts Semistandard Young tableaux of shape λ and content μ: entry i occurs μi​ times, rows weakly increase, and columns strictly increase. In particular Kμμ​=1 and nonzero multiplicity requires dominance.
For the requested induction, products mean induction of the external tensor product of group representations from S5​×S2​ to S7​: on V⊠W, the pair (g,h) acts as v⊗w↦gv⊗hw. One can compute using Young's rule alone. The two-row permutation decompositions give
[S(3,2)]=[M(3,2)]−[M(4,1)],
(11)

ν=(5,2),(4,3),(4,2,1),(3,3,1),(3,2,2)

Since Sμ⊂Mμ, Question 2 shows that every composition factor of Sμ is Dλ for a regular partition λ⊵μ. If μ is regular, its radical quotient Dμ occurs once: it occurs as a quotient, and its multiplicity is bounded above by the multiplicity one in Mμ. All remaining factors have strictly dominating labels. A nonregular row has only strictly dominating regular labels.
Define dμλ​=[Sμ:Dλ]. Order the row labels, all partitions of an integer of n, and the column labels, the regular partitions of an integer, by the same total order refining decreasing dominance. Then
dμλ​=0 unless λ⊵μ,dλλ​=1.​
(14)
The matrix is generally rectangular. Its submatrix on regular rows is lower unitriangular in this decreasing convention; placing least dominant shapes first would transpose the visual triangular convention, not the indices.
This is the decomposition matrix, not just an abstract composition factor table. The standard polytabloid basis makes SZμ​ a free invariant integral lattice whose extension to characteristic zero is the ordinary irreducible Sμ. Reducing this same lattice modulo p gives exactly the modular Sμ. On p-regular elements, reduction of its ordinary character is the sum of the Brauer characters of its composition factors, with coefficients dμλ​. These are the defining decomposition numbers; the resulting class in the modular representation ring is independent of the chosen invariant lattice.
For S3​, take rows (3),(2,1),(1,1,1) in that order. In characteristic zero, or p>3, all three Specht modules are irreducible by Maschke's theorem, and
M=I3​.​
(15)
For p=2 the regular labels are (3),(2,1) and
M=​101​010​​.​
(16)
Indeed the sign representation equals the trivial representation. The two-dimensional S(2,1) is the sum-zero subspace of the three-point permutation module. Over an algebraic closure, a 3-cycle has two distinct nontrivial third-root eigenvalues there, and a transposition exchanges their lines. Neither line is invariant under S3​, proving absolute irreducibility.
For p=3 the regular labels are (3),(2,1) and
M=​110​011​​.​
(17)
In the same sum-zero space the vector (1,1,1) now spans an invariant trivial line. Its one-dimensional quotient is sign: the determinant of the sum-zero representation is sign, and the determinant of the invariant line is trivial. Therefore S(2,1) has one trivial and one sign factor, with sign its simple head D(2,1). The bottom Specht module is itself sign. This proves every entry of the three possible matrices.

Use [λ] for the ordinary irreducible character of S∣λ∣​. Multiplication of different degrees is the product in the induction ring of symmetric-group characters: it induces an external tensor product of group representations. For α⊆ν, the skew Young diagram ν/α is the set of cells of ν outside α. A filling of it is a semistandard Young tableau when entries weakly increase along rows and strictly increase down columns. Its content β means that i occurs βi​ times. Its reading word takes each row right to left and the rows top to bottom. A lattice word has, in every initial segment, at least as many i's as (i+1)'s for every i.
The Littlewood–Richardson rule says
[α][β]=∑ν⊢∣α∣+∣β∣​cαβν​[ν],
(18)
where the Littlewood–Richardson coefficient cαβν​ counts such skew semistandard fillings with content β and lattice reading word. If α is not contained in ν, the coefficient is zero. Over characteristic zero this is an actual direct-sum multiplicity statement; over other fields the analogous statement describes a Specht filtration, not necessarily a decomposition into simple modules.
For the required coefficient, the five skew cells are
(1,4),(1,5),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1).
(19)
Their columns are all distinct. Content (3,2) uses three 1's and two 2's. The first reading letter must be 1 by the lattice word condition, and row monotonicity forces both top cells to be 1. The remaining three cells, in reading order, can then be filled by 122, 212, or 221. The resulting words 11122, 11212, 11221 all satisfy the lattice inequalities, and exhaust the possibilities. Thus
c(3,2,1),(3,2)(5,3,2,1)​=3.​
(20)
For the determinantal form of a symmetric-group character, set [0]=1 in degree zero and [r]=0 for r<0. The one-row character [r] is trivial. The one-row specialization of the Littlewood–Richardson rule is the Pieri rule: multiplication by [r] adds a horizontal strip of r cells, since all entries are 1 and no column can contain two of them.
Here is an explicit determinant argument, so that the desired form is genuinely deduced. Under the Frobenius characteristic map, [r] corresponds to the complete homogeneous symmetric polynomial hr​, whose monomials enumerate weakly increasing sequences of r entries. Fix N variables and l rows. Let a directed lattice path run right or up from Ai​=(−i,1) to Bj​=(λj​−j,N), and assign weight xk​ to a horizontal step at height k and weight one to a vertical step. The sum of its weights is hλj​−j+i​. Expanding the determinant of this path matrix sums signed families connecting the sources to a permutation of the destinations.
Cancel intersecting families as follows. Choose the first intersection in a fixed order of vertices, and then the first pair of paths meeting there. Exchange their tails after that vertex. The total weight is unchanged, while the endpoint permutation changes by a transposition, reversing the sign. Choosing the intersection in this fixed order makes the operation an involution. The surviving families are nonintersecting; the ordered sources and ordered destinations force the identity endpoint pairing. Record the heights of the horizontal steps of path i as row i of a tableau. Rows weakly increase, and nonintersection of successive paths is precisely strict increase down the shared columns. Conversely every semistandard Young tableau gives one surviving path family. Thus the determinant has the semistandard-tableau generating function sλ​.
Young's rule identifies this generating function with the characteristic of [λ]: its coefficient at a monomial of weight μ is Kλμ​, the multiplicity obtained by pairing with the Young permutation module of type μ. The Frobenius characteristic map is injective and respects induction products. Transposing the path matrix does not change its determinant, so
[λ]=det([λi​+j−i])1≤i,j≤l​.​
(21)
This is the character version of the Jacobi–Trudi identity.
For the example the matrix is
[3,2,2]=det​[3][1][0]​[4][2][1]​[5][3][2]​​.
(22)
Expanding gives the concise answer
[3,2,2]=[3][2]2−[3]2[1]−[4][1][2]+[4][3]+[5][1]2−[5][2].​
(23)
At the identity the six induction-product dimensions are 210,140,105,35,42,21, respectively; their alternating sum is 21, agreeing with the hook-length formula for (3,2,2).

For a Young diagram λ, a Removable node of a Young diagram is a cell whose deletion leaves a partition of an integer diagram; equivalently it is the last cell of row i with λi​>λi+1​. Let λ− denote the distinct shapes obtained by deleting one such cell. The restriction branching rule for a symmetric group in characteristic zero is
Sλ↓Sn−1​​≅μ∈λ−⨁​Sμ.​
(24)
Its induction version adds one cell in each possible way. Over an arbitrary field, restriction instead has a Specht filtration with these successive quotients, each once; it need not split.
To prove restriction, list the removable nodes from top to bottom. By the standard polytabloid basis, a basis of Sλ consists of et​ with t standard. Its entry n is necessarily at a removable node. Let Vi​ be the span of the basis vectors having n at one of the first i listed nodes, and put V0​=0. These are Sn−1​-invariant. To verify this, apply a permutation of 1,…,n−1 and straighten the resulting polytabloid by column antisymmetry and Garnir relations. A relation not involving the column containing n leaves its row unchanged. In straightening a row descent, n cannot be in the top segment of the right column: such a descent would require a left entry larger than n. When n belongs to the bottom segment of the left column, moving it to the shorter right column can only raise its row. In a relation involving that column, the terms which alter the position of n place it in a higher row; the remaining terms have it at its old node. Thus straightening never moves it past the current top-to-bottom cutoff.
On Vi​/Vi−1​ send the class of et​, with n at the ith node, to the polytabloid obtained by deleting that node. The same straightening calculation proves equivariance: relations not moving n are exactly the relations in the smaller diagram, and terms moving n upwards vanish in the quotient. Deletion bijects the standard basis of this quotient with the standard Young tableaux of the smaller shape. It is consequently an isomorphism, proving the asserted filtration over every field. In characteristic zero, Maschke's theorem splits the filtration and gives the direct sum. Finally Frobenius reciprocity gives the induction rule, since a constituent Sν has multiplicity one exactly when λ∈ν−.
The hook-length formula states
fλ=dimSλ=∏(i,j)∈λ​hij​n!​,hij​=λi​−j+λj′​−i+1.​
(25)
The hook of a Young diagram consists of its cell, the cells to its right in the same row, and the cells below it in the same column. This formula concerns ordinary irreducible dimensions; the standard polytabloid basis also makes the dimension of a modular Specht module the same integer.
All transpositions are conjugate, have order two, and generate Sn​. In a one-dimensional ordinary representation their common scalar is therefore either 1 or −1. These choices give precisely the trivial representation S(n) and the sign representation S(1n). At n=1 they coincide. The standard shape (n−1,1) has dimension n−1: its standard Young tableau is specified by choosing the bottom entry from 2,…,n. Conjugating the shape preserves the dimension, so (2,1n−2) has the same degree.

If a non-linear, nonstandard shape has at least two removable nodes, none of its predecessors is a single row or single column: adding one cell to a row or column could produce only a linear or standard shape. The kernel intersection theorem for Specht modules uses maps that move entries between adjacent rows. For 1≤i<ℓ(μ) and 0≤v<μi+1​, let μ(i,v) replace the row lengths (μi​,μi+1​) by (μi​+μi+1​−v,v), keeping every other length unchanged. This is allowed to be a composition rather than a decreasing partition of an integer, and a zero row can be omitted. For a tabloid with row sets Rj​, define
ψi,v​:Mμ⟶Mμ(i,v)
(27)
by summing over all v-subsets B⊂Ri+1​: the new row i+1 is B, the new row i is Ri​∪(Ri+1​∖B), and all other rows are unchanged. These are module homomorphisms, since relabelling commutes with choosing subsets. The theorem is
Sμ=i=1⋂ℓ(μ)−1​v=0⋂μi+1​−1​kerψi,v​.​
(28)
It holds over every field. Column cancellation shows the inclusion of Sμ in each kernel; the full theorem identifies the common kernel with the Specht module.
For completeness, the semistandard homomorphism theorem concerns a partition of an integer λ, a composition α, and fillings T of shape λ with αj​ occurrences of j. Such a filling gives ΘT​:Mλ→Mα: replace its labels by the numbered entries of an input tableau, and sum the output tabloids over distinct permutations of the labels within each input row. Label j assigns an entry to output row j. The use of distinct assignments avoids dividing by any factorial. Let ΘT​ be its restriction to Sλ. The restrictions indexed by Semistandard Young tableaux are linearly independent. They form a basis of HomFSn​​(Sλ,Mα) when p=2, or when λ is two-regular. In characteristic two for a two-singular shape they need not span; omitting this exception would make the statement incorrect.
We now prove the invariant vector criterion for a Specht module directly from the first theorem. The group acts transitively on the tabloid basis of Mμ, so any invariant vector has all coefficients equal. Thus (Mμ)Sn​=Fzμ​, where zμ​ is the sum of all its tabloids and is nonzero over every field. Fix i,v, write a=μi​, b=μi+1​, and count preimages of one output tabloid. The v retained entries are prescribed; among the a+b−v entries of the enlarged row, exactly a must have come from its old row. Hence
ψi,v​(zμ​)=(aa+b−v​)zμ(i,v)​.
(29)
The common-kernel criterion becomes
zμ​∈Sμ⟺(ja+j​)≡0(modp)(1≤j≤b)
(30)
for every adjacent pair, where j=b−v.
Suppose p>0, and let t be least with b<pt. If a≡−1(modpt), its lowest t base-p digits are all p−1. For any 0<j<pt, adding j to a creates a carry at the lowest nonzero digit of j. At that position the digit of a+j is smaller than the corresponding digit of j. Lucas's theorem therefore gives (ja+j​)≡0. Conversely, if one of the first t digits ad​ is not p−1, take j=pd. Minimality of t gives pd≤b, while addition at that digit creates no carry. Lucas then yields
(pda+pd​)≡ad​+1≡0(modp).
(31)
This proves the claimed equivalence, and therefore
(Sμ)Sn​=0⟺μi​≡−1(modpti​) for every i,ti​=min{t≥0:μi+1​<pt}.​
(32)
The invariant space, when present, is one-dimensional. At the last row put μi+1​=0; then ti​=0 and the condition is vacuous. This congruence formulation is for positive characteristic. In characteristic zero the positive binomial coefficients cannot vanish, so an invariant vector exists precisely for the one-row shape (n).
Finally suppose μ is regular. It has a unique simple head Dμ, by Question 1. The given sign-twisted duality of Specht modules implies
HomSn​​(Sμ,sgn)≅HomSn​​((Sμ′)∗,F)≅HomSn​​(F,Sμ′).
(33)
Thus Dμ is sign exactly when the conjugate shape μ′ satisfies the invariant congruences.
Here is an explicit solution of those congruences under regularity. Write the positive column heights of μ as a1​≥⋯≥al​>0, so μ′=(a1​,…,al​). Regularity says ai​−ai+1​<p, including al​−al+1​=al​<p, because this difference counts rows of length i. Starting with 0<al​<p, the congruence for its preceding row forces al−1​≡−1(modp). Since al​≤al−1​<al​+p≤2p−1, the only possibility is al−1​=p−1. Repeating upwards forces every earlier height to be p−1. Consequently
μ′=((p−1)q,r),n=q(p−1)+r,0≤r<p−1,
(34)
where a zero final part is omitted. Conversely this shape satisfies every invariant congruence, so its conjugate is the desired regular label of the modular sign representation:
μ=((q+1)r,qp−1−r),n=q(p−1)+r,quad0≤r<p−1.​
(35)
Zero parts are omitted. For p=2 this is (n), as it should be because sign is trivial. In characteristic zero the answer is (1n).

A rim hook is a connected skew diagram containing no 2×2 square, whose removal leaves a partition of an integer diagram. Its leg length is its number of occupied rows minus one. The Murnaghan–Nakayama rule states that, if a permutation has cycle type (k,ρ), then
χλ(k,ρ)=η:λ/η a rim hook of size k∑​(−1)leg(λ/η)χη(ρ).​
(36)
Iteration removes one hook for each cycle length; the empty diagram has character one at the empty cycle type.
For shape (3,3,3) and cycle type (4,3,2), there are exactly two removable four-cell rim hooks. The remaining shapes are (3,2) and (2,2,1); the removed hooks have leg lengths one and two respectively. In shape (3,2) the only removable three-cell rim hook leaves (1,1) and has leg length one. In shape (2,2,1) the only such hook leaves (2) and again has leg length one. Since the characters of (1,1) and (2) at a transposition are −1 and 1, we obtain
χ(3,2)(3,2)=1,χ(2,2,1)(3,2)=−1,
(37)
and hence
χ(33)((1234)(56)(789))=−1−1=−2.​
(38)
The cycle lengths can be taken in any order; using 4,3,2 makes the two branches of the computation explicit.
Now consider the ordinary induction product [α][β]. A hook partition is (x,1y), equivalently a partition of an integer whose second row has length at most one. If a hook shape ν occurs in the Littlewood–Richardson rule, then α⊆ν, forcing α itself to be a hook. In the skew hook ν/α, the new cells consist of a segment in the first row and a segment down the first column, with no shared column between those two segments. Every first-row entry must be 1: its rightmost entry is the first letter of the lattice word, hence is 1, and weak row increase forces all entries to its left to be 1 too. The column segment has strictly increasing entries. Thus only 1 can be repeated, forcing the content β to be a hook as well. This also proves the assertion when the top segment is empty, since then every entry lies in a strictly increasing column.
Write the two nonempty hook factors as α=(a,1k) and β=(b,1l), with k=n−r−a and l=r−b. Let the prospective resulting hook have top-row length x and leg length y. Its new top segment has H=x−a cells and its new column segment has V=y−k cells. The content consists of b ones and the single letters 2,…,l+1. All those larger letters must be in the new column; its strict increase allows either no 1 or exactly one 1. There are therefore only two possibilities:
(H,V)=(b,l)or(b−1,l+1).
(39)
In the first case fill the top segment with b ones and the column with 2,3,…,l+1. In the second, use b−1 top ones and column 1,2,…,l+1. Each filling is unique and has a lattice word, including the case b=1 with an empty top segment in the second filling. This proves
[a,1n−r−a][b,1r−b]=[a+b,1n−a−b]+[a+b−1,1n−a−b+1]+non-hook constituents.​
(40)
Both displayed multiplicities are one.
To deduce the full-cycle value from this product result, rather than quote the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule again, form the virtual character
Fn​=∑j=0n−1​(−1)jχ(n−j,1j).
(41)
For every proper Young subgroup Sm​×Sn−m​ and every pair of its irreducible characters, the hook-product calculation gives
⟨Fn​,IndSm​×Sn−m​Sn​​(χα⊠χβ)⟩=0.
(42)
If a factor is not a hook there are no hook constituents; if both are hooks, their two consecutive leg lengths cancel in this alternating inner product. By Frobenius reciprocity and the completeness of the product irreducible characters, Fn​ restricts to zero on every such subgroup. Any permutation with more than one cycle preserves a nonempty proper subset and is conjugate into one of these subgroups. Thus Fn​ is supported only on the class of n-cycles.
Its inner product with the trivial character is one, since only the one-row hook contributes. That class has size (n−1)!, so Fn​(ρ)/n=1, giving Fn​(ρ)=n. For any irreducible character, the support calculation then yields
⟨Fn​,χν⟩=χν(ρ)​.
(43)
By character orthogonality, the left side is (−1)n−x for ν=(x,1n−x) and zero otherwise. These are real integers, proving
χν(ρ)={(−1)n−x,0,​ν=(x,1n−x),ν is not a hook.​​
(44)
For n=1 the same conclusion holds directly. This derives the requested special case from the Littlewood–Richardson rule and Frobenius reciprocity.

Choose m≥ℓ(λ), pad the partition of an integer with zeros, and define its beta numbers by
βi​=λi​+m−i(1≤i≤m).
(45)
They form a strictly decreasing set of nonnegative integers. Conversely, from its decreasing enumeration recover λi​=βi​−m+i. With m=ℓ(λ) these are exactly the first-column hook lengths, since hi1​=λi​+λ1′​−i=λi​+m−i. Padding changes the numbers: increasing m by one replaces B by {0}∪(B+1), and leaves the represented partition of an integer unchanged.
Arrange positions 0,1,2,… on p runners, putting position r+pk on runner r at height k. A bead occupies each position in the beta set. This is the abacus of a partition. The hook criterion in a beta set says that a bead b and a gap a<b correspond to a hook of length b−a; replacing b by a removes the corresponding rim hook. In particular removing a rim p-hook moves a bead from b to the vacant position b−p immediately above it on the same runner. Its leg length counts the beads in the intervening positions of the full linear numbering.
For an explicit example, take λ=(4,2,1), p=3, and m=3. Its beta set is {6,3,1}. Runner zero has beads at 3,6, runner one has a bead at 1, and runner two is empty. Packing upwards gives {3,1,0}, which represents (1); thus the three-core is (1) and the weight is two.
Every bead slide reduces the sum of occupied positions, so repeated slides terminate. The number nr​ of beads on each runner is unchanged. At termination they must occupy exactly
r,r+p,…,r+p(nr​−1)
(46)
on runner r, since any gap above a bead would allow a further slide. Hence the final beta set is uniquely determined by the initial runner counts, independently of the order of hook removals. Increasing the number of beads by p replaces B by {0,…,p−1}∪(B+p), adds one bead to each runner, and gives exactly the same represented core after packing. General changes in padding follow by applying B↦{0}∪(B+1); packing commutes with this operation up to the cyclic relabelling of runners. Therefore the result is independent of both removal order and padding:
the p-core Cp​(λ) is well-defined.​
(47)
The weight of a partition is wp​(λ)=(n−∣Cp​(λ)∣)/p.
A block of a group algebra is an indecomposable two-sided summand FSn​e, where e is a primitive central idempotent. The identity decomposes into pairwise orthogonal such idempotents; a module belongs to a block when its idempotent acts as identity and the other block idempotents act as zero. For ordinary characters, use a splitting p-modular system (K,O,F), or extend the residue field first: the block idempotents lift to OSn​, and determine which ordinary irreducible KSn​-modules lie in each p-block. The symmetric group is split over its prime field, so extending F introduces no change to the resulting partition of an integer labels.
The Nakayama block theorem, traditionally called the Nakayama Conjecture for symmetric groups, is
χλ and χμ belong to the same p-block⟺Cp​(λ)=Cp​(μ).​
(48)
The simple modules in that block are exactly the Dν with regular labels having that core. At fixed n, the common core also fixes the weight. Here is an essay proof route through central characters, specifying the main algebraic and combinatorial steps and why each is needed.
First introduce the commuting Young–Jucys–Murphy elements
J1​=0,Ji​=∑j<i​(j i)∈ZSn​.
(49)
Their commutators vanish because contributions supported on each triple of letters cancel. Symmetric polynomials in the Ji​ are central: for adjacent transpositions si​, the relations si​Ji​=Ji+1​si​−1, si​Ji+1​=Ji​si​+1 show that si​ commutes with symmetric expressions in Ji​,Ji+1​, and it commutes with the other Jj​. Since these transpositions generate Sn​, the symmetric expressions commute with the whole group algebra.
The substantial generation ingredient is the integral Jucys–Murphy description of the center of a symmetric-group algebra: every conjugacy-class sum is an integral symmetric polynomial in the Ji​. Its proof expands symmetric monomials in these sums of transpositions and organizes the resulting class sums by the number of moved letters and cycle filtration; triangular elimination supplies integral spanning, rather than merely rational spanning. A useful first identity in this construction is
∏i=1n​(t+Ji​)=∑g∈Sn​​t#cycles of gg.
(50)
Indeed, when adjoining the letter n, either choose t and leave it as a singleton cycle, or choose a transposition (j n) and insert it into the cycle containing j; this gives every permutation uniquely. The full generation step distinguishes the individual class sums which this first identity groups together. Its integrality is essential: a description only over Q could use denominators divisible by p and would not prove generation of the modular center. Since class sums form the integral basis of the center, this integral theorem reduces to the same generation statement over F.
Next determine their scalar action. In the characteristic-zero tableau basis of Sλ, Ji​ is diagonal with entry the Content of a Young-diagram cell occupied by i, namely column minus row. This follows recursively along the multiplicity-free branching chain: the commuting operators preserve each path line, and Ji+1​=si​Ji​si​+si​ determines the successive eigenvalues. Consequently a symmetric polynomial f(J1​,…,Jn​) acts on Sλ by
f({j−i:(i,j)∈λ}).
(51)
This is independent of the ordering of the cells. The scalar identity holds on the integral Specht lattice, so after modular reduction it depends only on the multiset of contents modulo p. Every composition factor has this same central character of a block.
Two simple modules are in the same block exactly when their central characters agree. The center of a block is a commutative local finite-dimensional algebra; its semisimple quotient gives one scalar character, while the orthogonal block idempotents separate different blocks. By the generation theorem, equality on all symmetric polynomials in the Ji​ is enough. Conversely, different residue multisets are detected by elementary symmetric polynomials: the monic polynomial ∏(i,j)∈λ​(z−(j−i)) over F recovers the multiplicity of each residue by unique factorization, including multiplicities larger than p. Thus block equivalence is exactly equality of the integers
cr​(λ)=#{(i,j)∈λ:j−i≡r(modp)},r∈Z/pZ.
(52)
This gives the residue-content criterion for symmetric-group blocks without assuming that the sum of contents alone distinguishes blocks.
Finally identify residue content with the core. A removable rim p-hook has consecutive contents modulo p, hence removes one cell of each residue. Therefore shapes with the same core and the same size have the same residue counts. For the converse choose m divisible by p and at least n, and write qr​=nr​−m/p for the runner charges. Starting from the empty partition of an integer gives qr​=0. Adding a cell of residue r moves a beta-number bead from residue r−1 to residue r, increasing qr​ and decreasing qr−1​. The same changes occur in cs​−cs+1​ when cr​ increases by one. Induction on the cells therefore gives
qr​=cr​−cr+1​(r interpreted modulo p).
(53)
Equal residue counts give equal charges and hence equal packed runner configurations, so they give the same core. This completes both directions of the block criterion. The argument exposes the three essential bridges: integral center generation, its scalar content evaluation, and the abacus identification of residue data with a unique core.
