The Baire category theorem states that in a complete metric space, a countable intersection of open dense sets is dense. Equivalently, a nonempty complete metric space cannot be a countable union of nowhere dense sets, where a set is nowhere dense if its closure has empty interior.
Here is the proof. Let G1​,G2​,… be open dense sets and let U be any nonempty open set. Choose a point a1​∈U∩G1​ and a radius 0<r1​<1/2 such that the closed ball B1​=B(a1​,r1​) lies inside U∩G1​. Inductively choose am+1​ in the nonempty open set B(am​,rm​)∩Gm+1​, and then a radius 0<rm+1​<2−m−1 such that
Bm+1​=B(am+1​,rm+1​)⊆B(am​,rm​)∩Gm+1​.
(1)
Thus the closed balls are nested and their radii tend to zero. For j,k≥m, both centers lie in Bm​, so d(aj​,ak​)≤2rm​. They form a Cauchy sequence. Completeness gives a limit a, and closedness puts it in every Bm​. Consequently a∈U∩⋂m​Gm​. As U was arbitrary, the intersection is dense.
To obtain the equivalent formulation, take complements of the closures of the nowhere dense sets; those complements are open dense. Conversely, complements of open dense sets are closed with empty interior. In particular, a countable closed cover of a nonempty complete metric space must contain a set with nonempty interior.

For each nonzero integer tuple k=(k1​,…,kn+1​), put
Hk​={x∈Rn:∑j=1n​kj​xj​=kn+1​}.
(2)
If its first n coordinates are all zero, then kn+1​=0 and Hk​ is empty. Otherwise it is a closed proper affine hyperplane. It has empty interior: from any of its points, an arbitrarily small displacement in the nonzero normal direction (k1​,…,kn​) leaves it. Thus every Hk​ is nowhere dense.
There are countably many integer tuples, and Rn is complete. The Baire category theorem therefore makes the complement of their union dense and nonempty. Choosing any point there gives
j=1∑n​kj​xj​=kn+1​for every nonzero integer tuple k.​
(3)
Equivalently, 1,x1​,…,xn​ are linearly independent over Q: multiplying a rational relation by a common denominator produces just such an integer relation. This is an instance of Baire avoidance of countably many affine hyperplanes.

The real space C(T) is a Banach space in the uniform norm: a uniformly Cauchy sequence has a uniform pointwise limit by completeness of R, and that uniform limit is continuous. Each functional LN​ from the preceding part is bounded, but their norms are unbounded.
For integers m≥1, define
Em​={f∈C(T):∣LN​f∣≤m for every N}.
(11)
Each Em​ is closed, being an intersection of inverse images of closed intervals under continuous functionals. It has empty interior. Otherwise some ball B(f0​,r) would be contained in it. For every h of norm at most one, both f0​ and f0​+(r/2)h would belong to that ball, so
2r​∣LN​h∣≤∣LN​(f0​+(r/2)h)∣+∣LN​f0​∣≤2m.
(12)
Taking the supremum over h would give ∥LN​∥≤4m/r for all N, contradicting the preceding part.
Thus the Em​ are nowhere dense. The Baire category theorem says their union cannot cover C(T). A function outside that union satisfies
Nsup​∣SN​(f,0)∣=∞,​
(13)
so its Fourier series diverges at zero. In fact these functions form a dense Gδ​ subset of C(T). This proves the relevant Uniform boundedness principle argument directly and establishes generic unbounded Fourier sums at a fixed point.

The hypotheses make p a sublinear functional and q a superlinear functional. In particular p(0)=q(0)=0. Apply the domination property to y+y′∈Y and the vector x+x′:
S(y)+S(y′)≤p(x+x′+y+y′)−q(x+x′).
(14)
Subadditivity of p, with the inserted vectors z and −z, gives
p(x+x′+y+y′)≤p(x+y+z)+p(x′+y′−z).
(15)
Superadditivity of q gives q(x+x′)≥q(x)+q(x′). Combining these inequalities and moving terms yields exactly
S(y′)−p(x′+y′−z)+q(x′)≤−S(y)+p(x+y+z)−q(x).​
(16)
Every vector appearing in the comparison is permitted by the assumed domination, so no membership of x,x′,z in Y is needed.

We prove the extension by Zorn's lemma, retaining the full domination condition. Consider all pairs (Y,S) extending (Y0​,T0​), with Y a subspace and S linear, satisfying S(y)≤p(x+y)−q(x) for every x∈X,y∈Y. Order these pairs by extension. A chain has an upper bound: take the union of its subspaces and maps. The union is a subspace, and its map is well defined and linear because any finitely many vectors lie together in one member of the chain. Its domination is inherited there. Thus a maximal extension exists.
Suppose its domain Y is proper, and choose z∈/Y. A linear extension to Y+Rz has the form Sa​(y+tz)=S(y)+ta. Define the admissible values for a sandwich extension by
A=supx′,y′​{S(y′)−p(x′+y′−z)+q(x′)},
(17)
B=infx,y​{−S(y)+p(x+y+z)−q(x)},
(18)
with x,x′∈X and y,y′∈Y. Part (i) shows every quantity in the supremum is at most every quantity in the infimum, hence A≤B. Both bounds are real and finite: choosing zero vectors provides A≥−p(−z) and B≤p(z), and the pairwise comparison then bounds them from the other sides. Choose a∈[A,B].
For t>0, positive homogeneity and the upper bound on a, used with x/t,y/t, give
S(y)+ta≤t[p(x/t+y/t+z)−q(x/t)]=p(x+y+tz)−q(x).
(19)
For t=−s<0, the lower bound on a applied to x/s,y/s gives
a≥S(y/s)−p(x/s+y/s−z)+q(x/s),
(20)
which rearranges to S(y)−sa≤p(x+y−sz)−q(x). For t=0 this is the existing domination. Thus Sa​ is a legitimate larger extension, contradicting maximality. The maximal domain is therefore all of X; call its map T.
Setting the first vector in the domination to zero yields T(v)≤p(v). Setting it to v and the domain vector to −v yields −T(v)≤−q(v). Consequently
T∣Y0​​=T0​,T(y)≤p(x+y)−q(x),q(v)≤T(v)≤p(v).​
(21)
The one-dimensional extension has checked both signs of its coefficient; positive homogeneity alone does not permit treating negative coefficients as positive ones.

Take Y0​={0} and T0​(0)=0. The hypothesis p(x)≥q(x) is exactly the required initial domination 0≤p(x+0)−q(x). Applying the extension proved in part (ii) gives
q(x)≤U(x)≤p(x)for every x∈X,​
(22)
with U linear. There is no reason that U must be nonzero; for instance p=q=0 forces U=0.
This is the sandwich form of the Hahn-Banach theorem. Conversely, any linear map between these two functionals satisfies the stronger mixed domination, since U(y)=U(x+y)−U(x)≤p(x+y)−q(x). Thus the mixed inequality used during extension is the appropriate invariant, not an extra final restriction.

Use a nonzero complex unital Banach algebra B with submultiplicative norm, and write its unit as e. The complex scalar field is the usual convention in this spectral statement; for a real algebra one uses its complexification. We derive the needed analytic facts from norm-convergent series and scalar complex analysis.
If ∥a∥<1, completeness makes the Neumann series ∑j=0∞​aj converge. Multiplying its finite partial sums by e−a gives e−am+1, which tends to e. Hence its sum is the inverse of e−a. If b is invertible, then b+h=b(e+b−1h) is invertible whenever ∥b−1h∥<1. This proves that the invertible elements form an open set and that inversion is locally norm-continuous.
Define the Banach algebra spectrum and the resolvent of an element by
σ(x)={λ∈C:λe−x is not invertible},R(λ)=(λe−x)−1(λ∈/σ(x)).
(23)
For ∣λ∣>∥x∥, the Neumann series gives
R(λ)=j=0∑∞​λj+1xj​,x0=e.​
(24)
Thus σ(x) is bounded by ∥x∥ and is closed, because invertibility is open. Also R(λ)→0 in norm at infinity; for example its norm is at most ∥e∥/(∣λ∣−∥x∥), since ∥e∥≥1 in a nonzero submultiplicative unital algebra. The standard normalization ∥e∥=1 is not needed.
For points where the Banach algebra resolvent exists λ,μ, multiply out the inverses to get the resolvent identity
R(λ)−R(μ)=(μ−λ)R(λ)R(μ).​
(25)
For λ0​ outside the Banach algebra spectrum, put R0​=R(λ0​). Locally,
R(λ0​+h)=R0​(e+hR0​)−1=∑j=0∞​(−h)jR0j+1​,∣h∣∥R0​∥<1.
(26)
This series and its differentiated series converge uniformly on every smaller disk. It therefore proves R is a Banach-space-valued holomorphic function, establishing complex differentiability in the Banach-space norm and gives R′=−R2, without assuming a Banach-algebra-valued analytic theorem. In particular every continuous complex linear functional ϕ makes ϕ(R(λ)) a scalar holomorphic function.
The Banach algebra spectrum is nonempty. Otherwise R would be defined and analytic everywhere. Its local norm continuity and decay at infinity make it globally bounded. For every continuous linear functional ϕ, the scalar entire function ϕ(R(λ)) is bounded and tends to zero at infinity, so it is identically zero: the scalar Cauchy estimate for its derivative on circles of radius r is M/r, which tends to zero, and the limiting value then fixes its constant as zero. Such functionals separate points by the Hahn-Banach theorem. Explicitly a bounded real functional can be extended from the real span of any nonzero element using its norm, and complexifying it by ϕ(v)=ℓ(v)−iℓ(iv) gives a bounded complex linear functional nonzero on that element. Therefore R itself would be zero, contradicting (λe−x)R=e. We have proved the Banach algebra spectrum is a nonempty compact subset of the complex plane.
We next establish the spectral radius formula. The power norms are submultiplicative. If some xm=0, their root norms are eventually zero. Otherwise, for any fixed m, write n=qm+r with 0≤r<m to obtain
∥xn∥≤∥xm∥qmax0≤r<m​∥xr∥.
(27)
Taking roots gives limsupn​∥xn∥1/n≤∥xm∥1/m. Taking the infimum in m, and using that every root norm is at least that infimum, proves existence of
ρ0​=limn→∞​∥xn∥1/n=infm≥1​∥xm∥1/m.
(28)
For ∣λ∣>ρ0​, the root test makes ∑xj/λj+1 converge, even when ∣λ∣≤∥x∥. The same telescoping multiplication proves it is a two-sided inverse. Hence
rσ​:=maxλ∈σ(x)​∣λ∣≤ρ0​.
(29)
For the reverse bound fix R>rσ​. If R1​>max(R,∥x∥), integrate the uniformly convergent exterior series term by term on ∣λ∣=R1​, obtaining
xn=2πi1​∫∣λ∣=R1​​λnR(λ)dλ.
(30)
The same integral equals the one on ∣λ∣=R: applying an arbitrary continuous linear functional reduces contour deformation through the Banach algebra spectrum-free annulus to the scalar Cauchy theorem, and separation of points restores equality in B. This proves the resolvent Cauchy coefficient formula rather than assuming vector-valued analytic calculus. Using the norm bound for the allowed vector integral gives
∥xn∥≤Rn+1max∣λ∣=R​∥R(λ)∥.
(31)
Taking nth roots yields ρ0​≤R, and letting R↓rσ​ finishes the proof:
ρ(x)=n→∞lim​∥xn∥1/n=λ∈σ(x)max​∣λ∣=sup{∣λ∣:λe−x is not invertible}.​
(32)
For the first example take the commutative algebra of dual numbers B=C[ε]/(ε2) with norm ∥a+bε∥=∣a∣+∣b∣. Completeness follows from finite dimension, and the product estimate makes the norm submultiplicative. For x=ε, x=0 but x2=0, so ρ(x)=0. Directly, λe−ε has inverse λ−1e+λ−2ε for λ=0, and its Banach algebra spectrum is {0}. For the second example take B=C with its absolute-value norm and x=1. Its Banach algebra spectrum is {1}, giving ρ(x)=∥x∥=1.

We construct the finite evaluation representation explicitly by Lagrange interpolation. This supplies all the polynomial facts needed for the bound.
Define the Chebyshev polynomial by T0​=1,T1​=x and
Tm+1​(x)=2xTm​(x)−Tm−1​(x).
(33)
Induction shows that Tm​ has degree m; the cosine addition identity also proves Tm​(cosθ)=cos(mθ). Therefore ∥Tn​∥[−1,1]​=1, and Tn​(−x)=(−1)nTn​(x) follows from the recurrence. The case n=0 is immediate, since both P and T0​ are constant.
For n≥1, choose the descending extremal nodes
xj​=cos(jπ/n),0≤j≤n,
(34)
so Tn​(xj​)=(−1)j. Define the fundamental interpolation polynomials
ℓj​(t)=∏k=j​xj​−xk​t−xk​​.
(35)
They satisfy ℓj​(xk​)=δjk​. Consequently
P(t)=j=0∑n​P(xj​)ℓj​(t)​
(36)
for every polynomial of degree at most n: the difference has degree at most n and vanishes at the n+1 distinct nodes, so it is zero. The root count used here follows by successively factoring (t−xj​) from a polynomial having such a root; a nonzero degree-n polynomial cannot have more than n distinct roots. Thus the interpolation identity has been proved.
If u>1, every numerator factor in ℓj​(u) is positive, while exactly j denominator factors are negative. Hence sgnℓj​(u)=(−1)j. Applying interpolation to Tn​ itself gives
∑j=0n​∣ℓj​(u)∣=∑j=0n​(−1)jℓj​(u)=Tn​(u)>0.
(37)
Putting M=sup[−1,1]​∣P∣ in the interpolation formula yields
∣P(u)∣≤∑j=0n​∣P(xj​)∣∣ℓj​(u)∣≤MTn​(u).
(38)
For u<−1, apply the proved positive-side inequality to Q(t)=P(−t) at −u>1, and use the parity identity. The result is
∣P(u)∣≤∥P∥[−1,1]​∣Tn​(u)∣,u∈/[−1,1].​
(39)
This is sharp, with equality for P=Tn​ or its scalar multiples.
In functional terms, the evaluation map Eu​:P↦P(u) on the normed polynomial space has ∥Eu​∥=∣Tn​(u)∣: the inequality gives the upper bound and Tn​ attains it. For u>1, its normalized version has the exact representation
Tn​(u)Eu​(P)​=∑j=0n​λj​P(xj​),λj​=Tn​(u)ℓj​(u)​,∑j=0n​∣λj​∣=1.
(40)
Thus Chebyshev interpolation represents external evaluation using n+1 nodes. The necessary finite representation and its norm are derived directly, so no convex-hull theorem is being invoked without proof.
