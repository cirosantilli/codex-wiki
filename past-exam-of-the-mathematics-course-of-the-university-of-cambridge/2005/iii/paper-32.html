For the Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve in this part, a1​=a2​=a6​=0, a3​=1, a4​=−1. Hence
b2​=0,b4​=−2,b6​=1,c4​=48,c6​=−216.
(1)
The elliptic-curve discriminant is therefore
Δ=1728483−2162​=37.​
(2)
Every prime other than 37 has unit discriminant, so this integral equation gives good reduction. At 37 the valuation is one. An Admissible change of Weierstrass coordinates changes discriminant valuations by a multiple of twelve; consequently no integral model can lower this value to zero. Thus the equation is minimal there and the reduction is genuinely bad, rather than an artifact of a nonminimal model. Explicitly (5,18) is singular on the reduced affine cubic: both partial derivatives vanish there. The set of bad reduction primes is
{37}.​
(3)

Over F2​, both x=0,1 give x3−x=0, and both y=0,1 give y2+y=0. There are four affine rational points, together with the point O at infinity. Over F3​, each x=0,1,2 gives x3−x=0, while precisely y=0,2 solve y2+y=0. Thus

Use reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve, which is injective on torsion of order prime to the residue characteristic. Any two-primary torsion point of an elliptic curve must inject into the group of order seven at three, so is zero. Any three-primary torsion point of an elliptic curve injects into the group of order five at two, so is zero. For a prime q=2,3, its primary torsion injects into both reduced groups and would have order dividing both five and seven, so is also zero. Decomposing the cyclic group generated by any torsion point of an elliptic curve into its primary parts shows that E(Q) has no nonidentity torsion at all. This is the two-prime bound on rational elliptic torsion, with the residue-characteristic exceptions handled explicitly. Since P=(0,0) is not O,
P has infinite order.​
(5)

The inverse on this Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve is (x,y)↦(x,−y−1). A line y=λx+ν through the points being added has third intersection R with x(R)=λ2−x1​−x2​, and the sum is −R.
At P=(0,0), implicit differentiation gives λ=(3x2−1)/(2y+1)=−1. The tangent is y=−x. Substitution into the equation gives x2=x3, so its third intersection is (1,−1), whose inverse is (1,0). Next the line through (0,0) and (1,0) is y=0; its remaining intersection is (−1,0), whose inverse is (−1,−1). Consequently
2P=(1,0),3P=(−1,−1).​
(6)

The good-reduction map respects elliptic addition. Since the finite groups at two and three have orders five and seven, respectively, 5P reduces to O at two and 7P reduces to O at three. These multiples are not O, since P has infinite order. The formal kernel of a minimal Weierstrass equation gives, for every nonidentity point in the reduction kernel at p,
vp​(x)=−2r,vp​(y)=−3r,r>0.
(7)
Thus both coordinates of 5P have negative two-adic valuation, and both coordinates of 7P have negative three-adic valuation. In particular
x(5P),y(5P),x(7P),y(7P)∈/Z.​
(8)
There is also an elementary way to see that both coordinates must fail integrality here. An integral affine pair cannot reduce to the point at infinity. If one coordinate had negative valuation and the other nonnegative valuation, the equation would give different valuations on its two sides. When both are negative the lowest-valuation terms give 2vp​(y)=3vp​(x), so both are indeed negative.
For a direct numerical confirmation, continuing the chord-and-tangent group law calculation gives
4P=(2,−3),5P=(1/4,−5/8),6P=(6,14),7P=(−5/9,8/27).
(9)
The original printed part repeats its request about 7P at the end; the two distinct requested multiples are 5P and 7P.

i OurBigBook.com
An isogeny of elliptic curves over k is a nonconstant k-morphism φ:E1​→E2​ which sends O1​ to O2​. A nonconstant morphism of smooth projective curves is finite and surjective. Its compatibility with the elliptic curve group law from Riemann-Roch can be seen without assuming it in the definition.
Identify a point P with the degree-zero divisor class [P−O1​]. A finite morphism pushes forward divisors, and pushes a principal divisor to the principal divisor of the norm of its rational function. It therefore induces a group homomorphism on degree-zero Picard groups of a curve. On geometric points, the pushforward of [P−O1​] is [φ(P)−O2​]. Since point addition is addition of these divisor classes, this proves
φ(P+Q)=φ(P)+φ(Q).​
(10)
The equality is defined over k, so in particular it holds on the groups of k-rational points and after every field extension. The argument also covers inseparable isogenies of elliptic curves; separability is not part of the definition.

The squares in F5​ are 0,1,4. The following table counts affine solutions at each x, with one point at infinity to be added:
xx3−x#{y:y2=x3−x}x3−x+1#{y:y2=x3−x+1}​00112​10112​21220​34201​40112​​
(11)
Hence #E1​(F5​)=#E2​(F5​)=8. In characteristic five, a finite point has order two precisely when y=0. The first cubic has three roots 0,1,4, giving four elements killed by two, including O. The second cubic has only the root 3, giving two elements killed by two. An isomorphism of groups would preserve these counts, so the groups are not isomorphic.
More precisely (2,1)∈E1​ doubles to (0,0) and has order four; the independent two-torsion point (1,0) gives C4​×C2​. On E2​, the point (0,1) doubles to (4,1), which doubles to (3,0); it therefore has order eight. Thus the eight-point isogenous elliptic curves over F5 have point groups
E1​(F5​)≅C4​×C2​,E2​(F5​)≅C8​.​
(12)

Put u=x1​−1, v=y1​. Then v2=u(u2+3u+2). After using the curve equation, the displayed rational functions become
X=x1​+x1​−12​=u+1+u2​,Y=v(1−u22​).
(13)
Set X0​=X+2=u+3+2/u. Direct substitution into v2=u(u2+3u+2) gives
Y2=X0​(X02​−6X0​+1)=X03​−X02​+X0​
(14)
over F5​. Replacing X0​ by X+2 makes this Y2=X3−X+1. This verifies the target equation explicitly, and identifies the functions with the two-isogeny formula after translating the source's two-torsion point to zero and then translating the target coordinate.
We must also check the omitted affine points. At T=(1,0), v is a local parameter, and u=v2/(x1​(x1​+1)) has order two, since x1​(x1​+1)=2 at T. Thus X has a pole of order two and Y one of order three. At O1​, the usual pole orders on a smooth cubic in Weierstrass form give poles two and three as well: the correction terms have higher order and do not cancel their leading terms. There are no other poles. A rational map from a smooth curve into a projective curve extends across these points; the indicated pole orders show that both T and O1​ map to O2​. Hence this is a nonconstant identity-preserving morphism, and part (i) makes it an isogeny of elliptic curves.
The pullback of the pole divisor 2O2​ of X is 2O1​+2T. Degrees of divisors multiply by the morphism degree, so 2degφ=4. We obtain
degφ=2,kerφ={O1​,(1,0)}.​
(15)
The degree is prime to five, so the elliptic isogeny is separable. Equivalently dX/Y=dx1​/y1​ is a nonzero pullback of an invariant differential.

For a Short Weierstrass form y2=x3+Ax+B, use c4​=−48A and Δ=−16(4A3+27B2). Both curves have A=−1, so c4​=48=3 in F5​. For E1​, B=0 gives Δ1​=64=4; for E2​, B=1 gives Δ2​=−368=2. Thus
j(E1​)=33/4=3,j(E2​)=33/2=1in F5​.​
(16)
The j-invariant of an elliptic curve is preserved by every isomorphism over the algebraic closure. Since these values differ, the two curves are not geometrically isomorphic, even though the preceding part gives a degree-two elliptic isogeny.

Choose an integral Minimal Weierstrass equation whose discriminant is a p-adic unit. Given P=[X:Y:Z]∈E(Qp​), scale the coordinates so that X,Y,Z∈Zp​ and at least one is a unit. Define
φ(P)=[Xˉ:Yˉ:Zˉ]∈E(Fp​).
(17)
Another primitive representative differs by a unit scalar, so the projective reduction is well defined. The reduced cubic is smooth by good reduction.
To prove the group homomorphism property, view this smooth projective cubic with its section O as the integral elliptic model E/Zp​. Its addition law is defined over Zp​, by adding degree-zero divisor classes, and thus is a regular morphism E×E→E. Properness identifies E(Qp​) with the integral sections just described. Reducing the addition morphism is the group operation of the smooth special fiber. Consequently
φ(P+Q)=φ(P)+φ(Q),φ(O)=O.​
(18)
This is the good-reduction map respects elliptic addition argument; it covers tangent, vertical and infinity cases where reducing a single affine slope formula would involve division by a nonunit. Smoothness also makes reduction surjective: at each reduced point a partial derivative is a unit, and the Hensel lemma lifts that point in a local affine chart.

Near O, put t=−x/y and w=−1/y, or projectively t=−X/Y, w=−Z/Y. The integral Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve becomes
w=t3+a1​tw+a2​t2w+a3​w2+a4​tw2+a6​w3.
(19)
The linear coefficient of w at the origin is a unit, so successive coefficient comparison gives a unique series w(t)∈Zp​[[t]] beginning t3. Expressing the addition morphism in the local parameters of its two inputs gives
F(t1​,t2​)=t1​+t2​+terms of total degree at least two∈Zp​[[t1​,t2​]].
(20)
Its identity, inverse, commutativity and associativity identities follow from the corresponding elliptic group identities. This one-dimensional commutative formal group law is the formal group of an elliptic curve E attached to the model.
A point reducing to O lies in the chart with Y a unit and has t,w∈pZp​. The implicit equation determines w uniquely from t. Conversely every t∈pZp​ gives the convergent solution w(t) and the point [−t:1:−w(t)]; t=0 gives O. Hence the parameter is a bijection from the reduction kernel to pZp​. The series F converges there and describes its addition. Therefore
kerφ=E1​(Qp​)≅E(pZp​).​
(21)
For nonzero t with vp​(t)=r>0, w(t)=t3 times a unit, so vp​(x)=−2r and vp​(y)=−3r. These are the formal kernel of a minimal Weierstrass equation valuations used earlier.

Write E[q∞] for the points killed by some power of q, rather than any notion of all points which are q-divisible. On the reduction kernel, formal multiplication is
[qm](t)=qmt+O(t2).
(22)
Since q=p, its linear coefficient is a unit. Recursive inversion gives an integral inverse series, convergent on pZp​; equivalently the Hensel lemma gives a unique solution to [qm](t)=s for every s∈pZp​. Thus multiplication by qm is bijective on the kernel, proving that its q-primary torsion is zero. The reduction group homomorphism is therefore injective on E(Qp​)[q∞].
For surjectivity on this torsion, take a reduced point P killed by qm. Lift it to P∈E(Qp​) using smooth reduction. Then qmP belongs to the kernel, so choose its unique kernel division point R with qmR=qmP. The point P−R is killed by qm and reduces to P. This proves the prime-to-p torsion lifts uniquely at good reduction statement:
E(Qp​)[q∞]∼​E(Fp​)[q∞].​
(23)
The right side is a subgroup of a finite group. Thus the left side is finite and has exactly the requested order. No claim that the whole formal kernel is torsion-free at p=2 is needed.

i OurBigBook.com
Translate the rational point of order two to (0,0) and complete the square. After clearing denominators by admissible scaling, write
E:y2=x(x2+ax+b),a,b∈Z,b(a2−4b)=0.
(24)
The companion curve is E′:Y2=X(X2−2aX+a2−4b). The two-isogeny formula gives maps ϕ:E→E′ and ψ:E′→E satisfying ψϕ=[2].
For the Weak Mordell-Weil theorem in this setting, use the two-torsion square-class homomorphism
α(O)=1,α((0,0))=[b],α((x,y))=[x]∈Q∗/Q∗2,
(25)
and its counterpart α′ on E′. These are homomorphisms: a nonvertical line intersecting the cubic in three points has the product of their x-coordinates a square, by comparison of the constant term of the intersection cubic. For a line through (0,0), the product of the other two coordinates is b, explaining the special value there. The kernels are ψE′(Q) and ϕE(Q), respectively, as follows by solving the elliptic isogeny preimage quadratic and testing its discriminant for a square.
The prime-support bound in two-isogeny descent makes both images finite. Indeed at a prime not dividing b, a negative valuation of x makes the leading cubic term dominate, giving 2v(y)=3v(x); a positive valuation makes x2+ax+b a unit, giving 2v(y)=v(x). In either case v(x) is even. Thus the square class of x is supported only on primes dividing b, together with a possible sign. The same argument uses b′=a2−4b on E′. Since 2E=ψϕE, the two finite elliptic isogeny quotients imply that E(Q)/2E(Q) is finite.
Finite generation still needs a descent on height. Let hx​ be the logarithmic height of the x-coordinate. The duplication formula has a degree-four rational x-coordinate map with coprime numerator and denominator. Polynomial height estimates, with the resultant supplying the lower bound, give the height growth under a morphism of the projective line relation hx​(2P)=4hx​(P)+O(1). Thus the convergent telescoping limit
h(P)=21​limn→∞​4−nhx​(2nP)
(26)
defines the canonical height of an elliptic curve, without assuming finite generation. It is nonnegative, satisfies h(2P)=4h(P) and the height parallelogram identity, and differs from hx​/2 by a bounded amount. Consequently its bounded subsets of rational points are finite by the Northcott theorem.
Choose the finite list Ri​ of representatives modulo 2E(Q) and let H=maxi​h(Ri​). For P=2Q+Ri​, the canonical height pairing inequality gives
h(Q)=41​h(P−Ri​)≤21​(h(P)+H).
(27)
Repeated halving brings the height below H+1. There are only finitely many such terminal points, and recovering P expresses it as an integer combination of those points and the Ri​. This proves the canonical-height proof of Mordell-Weil finite generation, so
E(Q)≅ZgE​⊕T,T finite.​
(28)

Retain E:y2=x(x2+ax+b) and put b′=a2−4b. The explicit two-isogeny formula is
ϕ(x,y)=(x+a+xb​, y(1−x2b​)),ψ(X,Y)=(4X−2a+b′/X​, 8Y(1−b′/X2)​),
(29)
with the maps extended at the kernel points. Direct substitution verifies the equations and ψϕ=[2].
Here is a concrete kernel test for the descent homomorphisms. A preimage of (X,Y)∈E′ has x satisfying x2+(a−X)x+b=0, whose discriminant is (X−a)2−4b. The target equation says Y2=X((X−a)2−4b), so for nonexceptional points this quadratic has a rational solution precisely when X is a square. The resulting y follows from the displayed elliptic isogeny formula. For a nonzero target two-torsion point, the preimage quadratic has a double root x=(X−a)/2, and the source equation gives y2=x2X, so the same square condition still applies. At (0,0)∈E′, a rational preimage exists precisely when b′ is a square, matching the special square-class value α′((0,0))=[b′]. The dual argument gives the other kernel. Thus
A:=α(E(Q))≅E(Q)/ψE′(Q),B:=α′(E′(Q))≅E′(Q)/ϕE(Q).
(30)
Let δ=[kerψ:kerψ∩ϕE(Q)]. The quotient map induced by ψ has this kernel, giving
[E(Q):2E(Q)]=δ#A#B​.
(31)
If b′ is a rational square, E has four rational two-torsion points and δ=1; otherwise it has two and δ=2. In either case δ#E(Q)[2]=4. Combining with finite generation therefore proves the two-isogeny rank formula
2gE​=4#A#B​.​
(32)
This derivation checks the factor four, including curves with full rational two-torsion.
To compute these images, enumerate the signed square-free divisors d of b and b′. Substituting x=d(U/V)2 yields the quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent
W2=dU4+aU2V2+(b/d)V4,(U,V)=(0,0).
(33)
The class d occurs precisely when this covering has a rational point, with the identity and two-torsion handled by the boundary cases. Test at the real place and at primes dividing 2bb′ to rule out candidates, and search for rational points to prove that surviving classes occur. The locally soluble classes form a finite elliptic isogeny Selmer group of an elliptic curve, giving an upper rank bound. rational points and independent height pairings give a lower bound. When the bounds agree the rank is proved. Local solubility alone does not prove a rational point exists: if a gap remains, a higher descent or further information about the covering obstructions is needed. This is why the procedure usually computes the rank but is not an unjustified universal termination claim.

For a rank-zero example, take E0​:y2=x3+x, with rational two-torsion (0,0). Its two-isogeny formula companion is E0′​:y2=x3−4x. The prime-support bound in two-isogeny descent allows only ±1 on E0​; all nonzero affine x are positive because x(x2+1) is a square. Hence A0​={1}. For E0′​ the possible classes are {1,−1,2,−2}, all realized: O gives 1, (0,0) gives [−4]=−1, and (2,0),(−2,0) give 2,−2. Therefore
2gE0​​=41⋅4​=1,gE0​​=0.​
(34)
This is the rank-zero elliptic curve with cubic x cubed plus x certificate; no unresolved locally soluble covering is being counted as soluble.
For a positive-rank example, take E1​:y2=x3−2x. Its companion is E1′​:y2=x3+8x. The first image is contained in {1,−1,2,−2}. The identity gives 1, (0,0) gives −2, and (2,2) gives 2; multiplicativity of the square-class group homomorphism also gives −1. Thus A1​ has all four classes. On E1′​, the same prime-support bound allows ±1,±2, but x(x2+8) is nonnegative only for x≥0, eliminating the negative classes. The identity gives 1 and (0,0) gives [8]=2, so B1​={1,2}. Consequently
2gE1​​=44⋅2​=2,gE1​​=1.​
(35)
The rank-one elliptic curve with cubic x cubed minus twice x therefore supplies the required positive-rank example. The exhibited point (2,2) is itself nontorsion: doubling gives (9/4,−21/8), whose nonintegral coordinates contradict the Nagell–Lutz theorem if it were torsion. No assertion that this point generates the full free part is needed for the rank calculation.
