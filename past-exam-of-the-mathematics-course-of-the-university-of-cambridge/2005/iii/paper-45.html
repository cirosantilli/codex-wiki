i OurBigBook.com
Parametric linkage analysis specifies a disease-transmission model: the mode of inheritance, disease-allele frequency and penetrance of each genotype. Combining these with Mendelian segregation and the recombination fraction gives a pedigree likelihood. One can estimate the recombination fraction or compare a linked model, θ<1/2, with the unlinked model, θ=1/2, using a likelihood-ratio test or LOD score. Incomplete penetrance and unobserved genotypes are accommodated by summing over the possible underlying inheritance states.
Nonparametric linkage analysis instead tests whether relatives with the same disease phenotype share marker alleles identical by descent more often than expected from their pedigree. For example, affected-sib-pair analysis compares sharing among affected full siblings with its Mendelian null distribution. It avoids specifying a complete disease-penetrance model and disease-allele frequency. “Model-free” still assumes an inheritance model for the marker, correct family relationships and a justified treatment of genetic-study ascertainment; it does not mean assumption-free.
Parametric analysis models the disease likelihood; nonparametric analysis tests departure from the marker's Mendelian sharing distribution.

Label the two paternal ancestral marker copies P1​,P2​ and the two maternal copies M1​,M2​. Treat these as four distinct ancestral copies in an outbred pedigree; equal observed alleles need not imply identity by descent. By Mendelian segregation, each child independently receives either paternal copy with probability 1/2. Thus the indicator IP​ that the two full siblings inherit the same paternal copy satisfies
P(IP​=1)=P(P1​,P1​)+P(P2​,P2​)=41​+41​=21​.
(1)
The same argument gives P(IM​=1)=1/2 for the maternal copy. Paternal and maternal meioses are independent, so IP​ and IM​ are independent. Consequently the number J=IP​+IM​ of copies shared identical by descent has a binomial distribution, giving
P(J=0)=41​,P(J=1)=21​,P(J=2)=41​.​
(2)
This is Mendelian IBD sharing of full siblings. It describes an ordinary, unselected sib pair: conditioning on a shared disease phenotype can change the distribution if there is genetic linkage to its causal locus.

For the Mendelian identity by descent count J, the expected shared proportion is
E[2J​]=21​(0⋅41​+1⋅21​+2⋅41​)=21​.​
(3)
The corresponding expected number of shared copies is one. These are different scales for the same random variable.
With genetic linkage to a disease locus, selecting two affected full siblings preferentially selects transmissions of disease-associated ancestral copies. A nearby genetic marker may then show excess identity by descent: the expected shared proportion can exceed 1/2, which is the basis of affected-sib-pair analysis. The precise distortion depends on penetrance, inheritance and the recombination fraction; disease status alone does not prescribe a universal alternative distribution. In the fully penetrant recessive model derived below, setting s=2θ(1−θ) gives
P(J=0,1,2∣both affected)=(s2,2s(1−s),(1−s)2),E[J/2∣both affected]=1−s.
(4)
Thus the expected shared proportion rises from 1/2 at θ=1/2 to one at complete genetic linkage, θ=0.

Let a denote the disease allele. Complete penetrance with recessive inheritance makes each affected child aa. The unaffected parents must therefore both be Aa genetic carriers. Condition on these parental genotypes and the fully informative marker; the distinct paternal and maternal marker copies identify which parent's copies are shared. Average over the two equally likely marker-disease haplotype phases in each parent. This is the phase-symmetric model implicit in the requested pedigree likelihood identities.
For one parent, suppose first that both children receive the same marker copy. If that marker copy is on the parental chromosome carrying a, both children receive a with probability (1−θ)2; in the opposite phase the probability is θ2. The phase-averaged probability is
v=2(1−θ)2+θ2​=21​−θ(1−θ).
(5)
If the children receive different marker copies, one disease-allele transmission is recombinant and the other is nonrecombinant in either phase. The probability that both receive a is therefore
u=θ(1−θ).
(6)
Paternal and maternal transmissions are independent. Sharing zero marker copies means the different-copy case for both parents; sharing two means the same-copy case for both; sharing one means one of each. Thus the recessive affected-sib-pair likelihood is
L0​L1​L2​​=θ2(1−θ)2=u2,=2θ(1−θ)​[(1−θ)2+θ2]=uv,=41​[(1−θ)2+θ2]2=v2.​​
(7)
Here Lj​ is the probability that both children are affected conditional on the marker sharing state J=j, not the probability of J=j conditional on their being affected. Since u,v≥0,
L0​L2​​=uv=L1​,L0​​+L2​​=u+v=21​.​
(8)
At θ=1/2, all three conditional disease probabilities equal 1/16, as expected from two independent children of an Aa×Aa mating. At θ=0, only the two-copy-sharing state can produce two affected children. Phase weights other than 1/2 would require a different likelihood function and need not obey these identities.

Then A+B=2N, and B is the total number of shared ancestral copies. At fixed N, the Fisher-Neyman factorization theorem makes B a sufficient statistic. The observed mean shared proportion is p​=B/(2N), and the binomial likelihood kernel gives
s=min(2NA​,21​),θ=21−1−2s​​.​
(14)

2N{p​log(2p​)+(1−p​)log(2(1−p​))},
(15)
whose derivative in p​ is 2Nlog[p​/(1−p​)]>0. Hence it orders samples exactly by excess IBD sharing. This is allele-sharing sufficiency for recessive linkage, not a general equivalence for all disease models. Use limiting values for terms such as 0log0, and assume N>0 for the estimators.

The kinship coefficient ϕXY​ is the probability that a randomly selected allele copy from individual X and an independently selected copy from individual Y at the same genetic locus are identical by descent. The sampling is of gene copies, not of whole genotypes. For a noninbred individual X, sampling its two copies with replacement gives ϕXX​=1/2; in general ϕXX​=(1+FX​)/2, where FX​ is its inbreeding coefficient.
For two full siblings in an outbred pedigree, the two sampled copies are both paternal with probability 1/4, both maternal with probability 1/4, and of different parental origin with probability 1/2. Given that both are paternal, their independent Mendelian segregation transmissions select the same paternal ancestral copy with probability 1/2; likewise for both maternal. The different-parent case cannot be identical by descent when the parents are unrelated. Therefore
ϕsib​=41​⋅21​+41​⋅21​+21​⋅0=41​.​
(16)
Equivalently, conditional on an IBD-sharing count J, the chance that the two random copies match is J/4, so ϕsib​=E[J]/4=1/4. The expected shared proportion E[J/2]=1/2 and the usual additive relationship coefficient 2ϕ=1/2 are twice this kinship coefficient; confusing these conventions would double the subsequent inbreeding coefficient.

Let the first cousins be X,Y, with parents (P,U) and (Q,V) respectively, where P,Q are full siblings and U,V have no ancestral relationship to one another or to that sibling family. To compute ϕXY​, choose one allele copy from each cousin. They originate from P and Q with probability 1/4. All other parental combinations have zero kinship coefficient under the otherwise outbred pedigree assumption. Hence
ϕXY​=41​ϕPQ​=41​⋅41​=161​.
(17)
The two gene copies within a child of X,Y are random transmissions from these parents. They are identical by descent precisely in the event defining parental kinship, so the child's inbreeding coefficient is
Fchild​=ϕXY​=161​.​
(18)
For a direct ancestral-path check, there are two shared noninbred grandparents. Through either grandparent, the two cousin-to-grandparent paths have two meioses each, and matching the ancestral grandparental copy supplies a further factor 1/2. Each contributes 2−2−2−1=1/32, giving the same first-cousin inbreeding coefficient. Additional ancestral relationships or inbred grandparents would change the result.

Assume a single disease allele a, complete penetrance of aa, no disease in Aa or AA, and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium in the outbred population. If its population frequency is q, the disease prevalence is q2=10−6, so q=10−3.
For a child with inbreeding coefficient F, the two inherited copies are identical by descent with probability F. In that component there is just one ancestral allele draw, and it is a with probability q. In the remaining component there are two independent ancestral draws, with probability q2 of aa. Thus the inbreeding genotype frequencies give
P(aa)=Fq+(1−F)q2=q2+Fq(1−q).
(19)
Using the first-cousin inbreeding coefficient F=1/16, the disease-risk ratio is
q2P(aa)​=1+qF(1−q)​=1+16999​=63.4375≃63.4.​
(20)
The absolute risk in this model is 6.34375×10−5. This calculation averages over the parental genotypes in first-cousin marriages before observing an affected child. It is not the risk conditional on both parents being known genetic carriers, and assumes the specified cousin relationship is independent of disease status rather than conditioning on a selected disease pedigree.

Assume the parents are unaffected, the disease has complete penetrance with a single recessive causal allele, and there are no phenocopies, new disease mutations or alternative causal loci. An affected first child has genotype aa, so each unaffected parent must be a heterozygote Aa. Conditional on these parental genotypes, Mendelian segregation in the second child's paternal and maternal meioses is independent of the transmissions to the first child. Therefore
P(second affected∣first affected, parents unaffected)=21​⋅21​=41​.​
(21)
This is the same sibling recurrence risk as for unrelated, unaffected parents under the same recessive model. Parental kinship increases the prior probability that both parents carry the same rare disease allele; after both are identified as carriers, it does not alter their Mendelian transmission probabilities. The factor 63.4 from the preceding part is therefore not an additional multiplier on the sibling recurrence risk.
If unaffected parents are not explicitly required, the rare-disease assumption is an approximation supporting the usual carrier-by-carrier calculation, rather than a logically exact consequence of an affected child alone. Under complete penetrance, the compatible parental matings are Aa×Aa, Aa×aa and aa×aa, with second-child risks 1/4,1/2,1 respectively. Let their prior probabilities be w11​,w12​,w22​, including both orders in w12​. Conditioning on the affected first child gives
P(second affected∣first affected)=w11​/4+w12​/2+w22​w11​/16+w12​/4+w22​​.
(22)
Without negligible or excluded affected-parent matings, this mixture need not equal 1/4 and may depend on consanguinity. Likewise incomplete penetrance or disease heterogeneity invalidates the assertion that an affected child of unaffected parents necessarily identifies an Aa×Aa mating. The stated 1/4 answer uses the unaffected-parent, fully penetrant single-locus assumptions.

Use standard diploid coalescent time units of 2Ne​ generations, where Ne​ is the fixed effective population size, and take n≥2. In the neutral Kingman's coalescent, each unordered pair of ancestral lineages merges at rate one. With j lineages, the next coalescence time therefore has an exponential distribution with rate
λj​=(2j​)=2j(j−1)​,E[Tj​]=j(j−1)2​.
(23)
The memoryless construction and the Markov property make these epoch durations independent. During epoch j there are j branches, each of duration Tj​, so the total branch length of a neutral coalescent is
L=∑j=2n​jTj​.
(24)
By linearity of expectation,
E[L]=j=2∑n​j−12​=2i=1∑n−1​i1​=2an​,an​=i=1∑n−1​i1​.​
(25)
The ancestral branch above the sample's most recent common ancestor is excluded: mutations there would be shared by all sampled chromosomes and would not create segregating sites.

Let μ be the mutation rate per chromosomal segment per generation, and use the population-scaled parameter θ=4Ne​μ. In 2Ne​-generation coalescent units, mutations occur on each ancestral lineage at rate θ/2. Independent mutations on all branches of a given phylogenetic tree superpose to give a Poisson distribution with mean θL/2.
Under the infinite sites mutation model, each mutation occurs at a distinct position, and every mutation below the sample's common ancestor separates a nonempty proper subset of sampled chromosomes from the remainder. Thus the mutation count is exactly the segregating site count, giving
S∣L,θ∼Poisson(2θL​).​
(26)
By the law of total expectation and the previous branch-length formula,
E[S∣θ]=E[2θL​]=θan​.​
(27)
This also gives the Watterson estimator S/an​ as an unbiased moment estimator of θ. Here all sample segregating sites are counted, including singletons; filtering sites by a minimum sample frequency or by external SNP discovery would introduce genetic-study ascertainment and change this calculation.

Use the joint prior density g(θ,t)=π(θ)q(t) as the proposal for rejection sampling. Since the Poisson probability ak​(θ,t) lies in [0,1], the following algorithm is valid:
  • Draw θ from the proper prior distribution π.
  • Independently draw Tj​ from the exponential distributions with rates (2j​), for j=2,…,n, and calculate L=∑jTj​.
  • Draw U uniformly on [0,1], independently of all previous draws. Retain (θ,T) if U≤e−θL/2(θL/2)k/k!; otherwise restart.
The joint density of a proposed pair together with its acceptance event is g(θ,t)ak​(θ,t). Its integral is mk​, so conditioning on acceptance gives precisely gak​/mk​, the desired posterior density. Repeating independent proposal trials until each acceptance produces independent posterior observations. This is Bayesian rejection sampling for a segregating-site count.
An equivalent implementation simulates S∗∼Poisson(θL/2) and accepts exactly when S∗=k. Its acceptance probability is the same Poisson mass. Propose from the joint prior and accept according to the exact observed-count likelihood. No approximation to the posterior distribution is involved in the accepted draws.

Let A∈{0,1} and B∈{0,1} encode the alleles at two loci on a randomly chosen population haplotype. Write pab​=P(A=a,B=b), pA​=p10​+p11​ and pB​=p01​+p11​. The linkage disequilibrium coefficient is
D=p11​−pA​pB​=p11​p00​−p10​p01​.​
(42)
The determinant identity follows by substituting the marginal frequencies and ∑pab​=1. All four haplotype frequencies can be recovered as
p11​p01​​=pA​pB​+D,=(1−pA​)pB​−D,​p10​p00​​=pA​(1−pB​)−D,=(1−pA​)(1−pB​)+D.​
(43)
Thus D=0 is precisely independence of the two allelic states. Its sign depends on which alleles are labeled one, and its possible magnitude depends on the marginal frequencies.
One other measure is squared allelic correlation. For segregating loci, 0<pA​,pB​<1, their indicator-variable covariance is D, and their variances are pA​(1−pA​) and pB​(1−pB​). Hence
r2=pA​(1−pA​)pB​(1−pB​)D2​,0≤r2≤1.​
(44)
This is the squared correlation, useful for measuring how well one marker predicts the other in association mapping. It is undefined if a locus is monomorphic. Linkage disequilibrium describes population haplotype frequencies; genetic linkage describes transmission within meioses. Physically linked loci can have D=0, and population mixing can produce LD even between unlinked loci.

Across a human chromosome, neighboring single-nucleotide polymorphisms often show substantial linkage disequilibrium, while average allelic correlation tends to decline with increasing genetic distance. A new mutation starts on one ancestral haplotype; repeated genetic recombination separates it from progressively more distant markers. In an ideal randomly mating two-locus population without further mutation, migration or selection, gamete formation gives p11′​=(1−c)p11​+cpA​pB​, where c is the recombination fraction. The marginal allele frequencies remain fixed, so
D′=(1−c)D,Dt​=(1−c)tD0​.
(45)
Here the prime means the next-generation value, not a separately standardized LD statistic. This simple decay explains the distance trend, rather than asserting an exact deterministic law for real finite populations.
The local pattern is heterogeneous. Many regions have haplotype blocks with strong LD and a small number of common haplotypes, separated by intervals showing more historical recombination, often recombination hotspots. A hotspot can therefore cause a rapid drop in LD between markers that are close in physical distance. Block lengths and boundaries are not uniform, perfectly sharp or independent of the population and the statistical block definition. These empirical features are documented in the HapMap data.
Population history also matters. Population bottlenecks can reduce the number of ancestral haplotypes and increase the extent of LD; population stratification or admixture can create longer-range associations, including between distant loci. Different populations can consequently have different haplotype frequencies and LD patterns at the same genomic interval. Raw D also varies with allele frequency, so comparing LD across markers requires attention to the chosen measure. The typical pattern is locally strong, block-like and highly variable LD, with a broad decline over genetic distance rather than a uniform chromosome-wide decay.

Suppose a disease-risk mutation first occurs on an ancestral haplotype. Descendants carrying the risk allele initially share that surrounding haplotype. Historical genetic recombination progressively replaces the flanks while retaining the disease allele, leaving linkage disequilibrium between the causal variant and some nearby genetic markers. A noncausal marker can therefore have different allele frequencies in affected individuals and suitably chosen controls. This is the basis of association mapping.
One first tests dense markers or multi-marker haplotypes for genetic association with the disease phenotype. Strongly correlated single-nucleotide polymorphisms can be represented by tag SNPs to locate an associated region efficiently. Within that region, denser genotyping and examination of disease-associated haplotypes locate historical recombination breakpoints: segments retained across independently recombined disease-bearing chromosomes are stronger candidates for containing the causal allele. Compare affected and unaffected carriers as well, since incomplete penetrance and other risk factors can prevent a perfect haplotype-disease correspondence.
Association mapping can achieve fine resolution because it uses recombination accumulated over many generations, rather than only the few observed meioses in a present-day linkage pedigree. Resolution depends on the local recombination history: a long region of strong LD may identify a disease-associated block while leaving many variants statistically indistinguishable. Additional informative haplotypes or populations with different LD can help separate them. Conversely, very weak LD requires closer marker spacing to avoid missing the causal region.
Population stratification must be controlled by ancestry-matched comparisons, statistical adjustment or an appropriate family-based association design, since ancestry can affect both disease frequency and marker frequency. A marker association alone establishes neither causality nor identity of the disease gene; nearby correlated variants must be distinguished by further genetic and functional evidence. Use LD to detect a disease-associated ancestral segment, then use historical recombinants to narrow the causal interval. The correlation between causal variants and their surrounding haplotypes, together with the value of representative markers, is illustrated in the HapMap study.

An ancestral recombination graph, or ARG, is the directed ancestry of a sample of chromosomal segments when ancestral lineages can both coalesce and undergo genetic recombination. It records which ancestral material each lineage carries, the times of events and the recombination breakpoints. At any fixed genomic position, following only the ancestral material at that position gives a marginal phylogenetic tree. Different positions can have different trees, while sharing substantial parts of their history. The graph is acyclic in time, although its underlying undirected graph can have loops because a lineage splits backwards and its ancestors can later rejoin.
For the standard neutral, constant-size, randomly mating diploid model, measure time in 2Ne​ generations and set ρ=4Ne​r, where r is the recombination rate across the whole segment. The backward process is a continuous-time Markov jump process on collections of lineages carrying ancestral material:
  • Each unordered pair of current lineages coalesces at rate one. The parent lineage carries the union of their ancestral material, with common ancestry recorded at overlapping positions. With k lineages the total coalescence rate is (2k​). The full ARG permits a pair to merge even when its carried material is disjoint; such an event creates no merger in a local tree at that instant.
  • A lineage splits into two parental lineages at a recombination breakpoint. With a uniform recombination map on a segment of normalized length one, the intensity of breakpoints along a fully ancestral lineage is ρ/2 per unit length. If a lineage carries only part of the segment, only cuts leaving ancestral material on both sides need be retained, giving rate (ρ/2)bi​, where bi​ is the span between its leftmost and rightmost ancestral positions. Material to the left goes to one parent and material to the right to the other. A gap within that span still permits a cut that separates two carried pieces.
For a state with k lineages, independent exponential event clocks give total rate
R=(2k​)+2ρ​∑i=1k​bi​.
(46)
The next waiting time has exponential distribution with rate R; choose its event type in proportion to the corresponding rate. A coalescing pair is uniform among unordered pairs; a recombining lineage is selected with weight bi​ and its breakpoint is uniform over its active span. With a nonuniform genetic map, use the corresponding integrated intensity and map-weighted breakpoint distribution. Initially all sample lineages span the whole segment, so the initial rate is (2n​)+nρ/2. Ancestral material no longer needed after its sample copies have found their local common ancestor can be pruned.
Mutations are placed independently on the resulting ancestral branches, with local rate θ/2 in coalescent units for a locus having scaled mutation parameter θ. Without recombination, the graph reduces to a single Kingman's coalescent tree. With recombination, each individual locus still has that marginal coalescent distribution, but trees at different loci are dependent. The ARG combines backward pairwise mergers with backward recombination splits; local trees are its position-specific projections. The neutral two-locus construction is developed in Hudson's two-locus model.

Five consequences of the ancestral recombination graph are:
  • Linkage disequilibrium and its distance dependence. Nearby sites often inherit the same local genealogy, so mutations on their shared ancestral branches produce correlated alleles. The chance of an ancestral breakpoint between sites increases with their genetic distance, reducing shared genealogy and, on average, linkage disequilibrium. This gives the genealogical explanation of the two-locus decay calculation.
  • Haplotype blocks and recombination hotspots. With a varying recombination map, the ARG has relatively few breakpoints within low-recombination regions and many near recombination hotspots. Long intervals can consequently retain a small number of common ancestral haplotypes, while genealogical relationships change more frequently across the intervening regions. This explains why LD blocks are heterogeneous rather than equally spaced units.
  • Mosaic inheritance and shared ancestral tracts. Backward splits assign different pieces of one contemporary chromosome to different ancestral chromosomes. Two sampled chromosomes can therefore share a recent ancestor over one interval but not its neighbors. For a specified total of m independent ancestral meioses, a tract of genetic length d has approximately md expected crossovers under a Poisson crossover model, so its probability of remaining intact is e−md. This relates long IBD segments to recent common ancestry, while distinguishing tract-boundary sampling from length-biased sampling at a random genomic point.
  • Incompatible site patterns and four observed gametes. Under an infinite sites mutation model on a single rooted tree, the descendant sets of two mutations are nested or disjoint. They cannot cross, so all four haplotypes 00,01,10,11 cannot occur. Recombination permits different local trees: one site's derived clade can be {10,11} and the other's {01,11}, producing all four without repeated mutation. The four-gamete test therefore detects a failure of the unrecombined, single-mutation-per-site model. Recurrent mutation would be an alternative explanation if the infinite-sites assumption were dropped.
  • Variation in local diversity and allele frequencies. Coalescent mergers describe genetic drift; their random times produce different branch lengths and descendant-group sizes. Recombination makes these genealogies vary along the chromosome. Conditional on a local genealogy, its segregating site count is Poisson with mean θL/2, so a longer tree tends to contain more variants. A mutation on a branch subtending j of the n samples appears at sample frequency j/n. Together, random branch lengths, descendant counts and mutations explain heterogeneous local diversity and the distribution of allele frequencies without requiring selection.
Shared ancestry explains correlation; recombination makes ancestry a mosaic; mutation on the resulting branches converts genealogy into observed variation. These explanations use the neutral ARG, with a variable recombination map where stated. Selection, population subdivision or changing population size can also be studied genealogically, but require extending the event model rather than being consequences of the constant-size panmictic neutral process alone.
