U=X1​−μ1​−V12​V22−1​(X2​−μ2​).
(1)
A linear transformation of a multivariate normal distribution is again normal. Direct calculation gives
Cov(U,X2​)=V12​−V12​V22−1​V22​=0,Cov(U)=V11​−V12​V22−1​V21​.

X1​∣X2​=x2​∼N(μ1​+V12​V22−1​(x2​−μ2​), V11​−V12​V22−1​V21​).​

This describes a regular conditional law; it does not require the equality event to have positive probability.

Applying the conditional multivariate normal distribution to the two differences gives, more generally,
D1​∣D2​=d∼N(a−cb−c​d, 2(a−c)−2(a−c)[2(b−c)]2​).
(8)
At d=0 the result is
X1​−Y1​∣X2​−Y2​=0∼N(0, a−c2(a−b)(a+b−2c)​).​
(9)
Both this law and the marginal law have mean zero, but the conditional variance is smaller. Specifically, with ρ=(b−c)/(a−c), the conditional variance is the marginal variance times 1−ρ2. The assumptions imply 0<ρ<1, so the decrease is strict. Matching the breadth differences to zero gives information about the correlated length differences; it does not force the length differences themselves to be zero. Let λ1​≥⋯≥λp​≥0 be the eigenvalues of the symmetric covariance matrix V, and choose an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors v1​,…,vp​. If ℓ=∑j​aj​vj​ has unit length, then ∑j​aj2​=1 and
Var(ℓTX)=ℓTVℓ=∑j​λj​aj2​≤λ1​.
(10)
Equality is attained by v1​, and precisely by unit vectors in the top eigenspace. Hence choose ℓ1​ to be any unit eigenvector for the largest eigenvalue; the maximum variance is λ1​. This proves the maximizing property of the Rayleigh quotient, rather than merely specifying a stationary point.

For the sample, put xˉ=n−1∑i​xi​ and use the sample covariance matrix
S=n−11​∑i=1n​(xi​−xˉ)(xi​−xˉ)T.
(12)
Order its eigenvalues λj​ decreasingly and choose unit orthogonal eigenvectors ℓj​. The sample principal components are the score columns
zij​=ℓjT​(xi​−xˉ).​
(13)
Their sample means are zero and their sample covariance is diagonal, since ℓjT​Sℓk​=λj​1{j=k}​. The jth sample variance is λj​, and its explained variance of a principal component is λj​/tr(S). Using divisor n instead changes the numerical variances by a common factor but leaves the component directions unchanged.
Standardizing means replacing a variable with positive sample variance by (xij​−xˉj​)/sj​. This gives principal component analysis on a correlation matrix, using C=D−1/2SD−1/2 with D=diag(S). It removes arbitrary differences of measurement units and prevents a large-variance coordinate from dominating solely through its scale. It also changes the question being optimized: small-variance and possibly noisy coordinates receive equal marginal weight. Principal component analysis based on the covariance matrix can be more appropriate when the variables have common meaningful units and absolute variability matters. Standardization is therefore a substantive choice, not an automatic improvement. A zero-variance variable must first be removed. In the word-rating example the variables share the same numerical range, but their across-word dispersions can differ; standardization gives each attribute equal initial variance.
For the standardized eight-variable analysis, total variance is tr(C)=8. The first three fractions of explained variance of a principal component are approximately 59.6%, 19.1% and 10.1%, respectively. The first two account for about 78.8%, and the first three for 88.9%. The first direction is dominant but one direction alone loses substantial variation. Retaining two principal components is a plausible descriptive summary: their eigenvalues exceed 1, whereas the third is below 1. That cutoff is a heuristic, and the third principal component may still have interpretable content.
The reported coefficient rows are not unit-length vectors. The printed rounded rows have squared lengths 4.7514, 1.5222 and 0.8063, consistent with their eigenvalues to the precision of the coefficient table. They are consistent with the scaled principal component loading convention rj​=λj​​ℓj​. Such rows remain eigenvectors, but the score direction with unit length is ℓj​=rj​/λj​​, or more accurately the row divided by its actual Euclidean norm when using rounded entries. Under this convention rij​ is the correlation of standardized attribute i with the variance-one score for component j:
Corr(Zi​,ℓjT​Z/λj​​)=λj​​ℓij​.
(14)
This explains both their sizes and why they must not be treated as the unit vectors from parts (i) and (ii).
The first principal component has positive coefficients on every attribute, strongest on friendliness, goodness, niceness, bravery and strength. With the favorable endpoints coded in the positive direction, it describes a broad favorable or impressive evaluation, with a potency contribution. The second contrasts movement and speed, with some size and strength, against the first three evaluative attributes; it is chiefly an activity dimension. The third is dominated by size, with smaller positive strength and negative movement/speed coefficients, distinguishing size or potency from activity. These are interpretations of directions of variation, not evidence of causal latent traits. Reversing a principal component's overall sign changes neither its meaning as an axis nor its explained variance; endpoint coding fixes only how its positive side is described.

The classification tree is grown by recursive binary partitioning, not by fitting a linear regression to numerical species codes. At a node t, count observations of each class, ntk​, and set p​tk​=ntk​/nt​. These are the multinomial maximum-likelihood probabilities. The classification-tree deviance is
D(t)=−2∑k​ntk​logp​tk​,0log0=0.
(15)
For each candidate predictor and threshold, calculate D(t)−D(tL​)−D(tR​) and choose the largest permitted decrease. Continue recursively until a node is pure or the stopping controls prevent another split, for example because it has too few observations or insufficient deviance to warrant further growth. Only petal length, petal width and sepal length appear in the selected splits; sepal width was available but did not win a split. This greedy construction need not find a globally optimal tree. The default deviance criterion and recursive partitioning are documented in the tree package manual.
Each node number encodes its position: node j has left and right children 2j and 2j+1. The reported sample size is the number reaching the node; its deviance is calculated from that node's class counts. The fitted class is the class with largest fitted probability, with a convention resolving ties. The root's three counts are equal, so its displayed class is only a tie choice. An asterisk marks a terminal node, where prediction stops.

Multivariate analysis of variance extends analysis of variance to vector responses. For example, a treatment may affect several correlated physiological measurements jointly. Separate univariate tests do not use the correlation geometry and require adjustment if they are interpreted as one multiple-testing procedure. A joint test asks whether group mean vectors differ in any direction.
In a one-way model, take independent Yri​∼Np​(μr​,Σ), r=1,…,g, i=1,…,nr​, with a common positive-definite covariance. Put N=∑r​nr​, and let Yˉr​ and Yˉ be group and overall sample means. The within-group and between-group scatter matrices are
E=∑r,i​(Yri​−Yˉr​)(Yri​−Yˉr​)T,H=∑r​nr​(Yˉr​−Yˉ)(Yˉr​−Yˉ)T.
(18)
Expanding around each group mean makes the cross terms vanish, giving total scatter E+H. To test H0​:μ1​=⋯=μg​, use the Wilks lambda statistic
Λ=∣E+H∣∣E∣​=j=1∏p​(1+θj​)−1,​
(19)
where θj​ solve the generalized eigenvalue problem Hv=θEv and E is nonsingular. Under the alternative, maximize the normal likelihood at the group means and covariance E/N; under the null, use the overall mean and covariance (E+H)/N. Their likelihood ratio is ΛN/2, so small Λ rejects equal means. Under the null, orthogonal projections of the Gaussian data give independent E∼Wp​(Σ,N−g) and H∼Wp​(Σ,g−1), supplying its null calibration. Here E is nonsingular almost surely when N−g≥p. Merely having more total observations than responses is not enough if group fitting consumes too many statistical degrees of freedom.
The test is invariant under a nonsingular linear change of response coordinates: both determinants acquire the same factor ∣A∣2. The directions associated with large θj​ identify mean separation relative to residual variability. With one response it becomes the ordinary ANOVA F-test. With two groups, writing Sp​=E/(N−2) and d=Yˉ1​−Yˉ2​, the version using Hotelling's T-squared statistic is
T2=Nn1​n2​​dTSp−1​d,p(N−2)N−p−1​T2∼Fp,N−p−1​(H0​),
(20)
with N>p+1. This formula demonstrates explicitly how the covariance weights a group difference.
The sketch shows hypothetical groups with strongly positively correlated responses and mean separation along their low-variance contrast. Marginal comparisons can look weak even when the joint comparison is informative. The ellipses represent individual-response covariance, not confidence regions for the means. Independence of observations, approximate multivariate normality and common covariance are substantive assumptions; outliers and unequal covariance can invalidate the usual calibration. Follow-up contrasts can identify the affected responses or groups, with appropriate multiplicity control. MANOVA uses shared covariance to test a multivariate mean difference, rather than testing unrelated coordinates in isolation.

Factor analysis models a large set of correlated measurements using a smaller collection of shared latent factors and variable-specific variation. A classical orthogonal factor model is
X=μ+LF+ϵ,EF=0,Cov(F)=Iq​,Cov(ϵ)=Ψ=diag(ψ1​,…,ψp​),Cov(F,ϵ)=0.​
(21)
Take q<p, centered errors, and positive specific variances for the inverse-covariance and likelihood formulas below. The factor loadings form the p×q matrix L, and the model covariance is
Σ=LLT+Ψ.
(22)
Thus for i=k, Cov(Xi​,Xk​)=∑j​Lij​Lkj​; the common latent factors explain off-diagonal correlation. The communality of variable i is hi2​=∑j​Lij2​, and Var(Xi​)=hi2​+ψi​. For standardized variables this is 1=hi2​+ψi​. The specific variance represents measurement noise or genuinely unshared variation; it is not another common latent factor.
This differs from principal component analysis. A principal component maximizes projected total variance and is a determined linear score after choosing its direction; a factor model attempts to explain shared covariance while reserving diagonal specific variance. Low-dimensional PCA truncation can help initialize a factor fit, but it does not justify setting all specific variances to zero. Latent factors are unobserved, not directly observed scores. When the latent factors and the errors are jointly Gaussian, their conditional mean is
E(F∣X=x)=LTΣ−1(x−μ),
(23)
which supplies one factor-score estimate, with remaining uncertainty. This follows from the block conditional normal formula in Question 1; it does not make the latent factor uniquely recoverable from one observation.
For the Gaussian likelihood, use the empirical covariance S0​=n−1∑i​(xi​−xˉ)(xi​−xˉ)T. Fit the factor model by minimizing log∣LLT+Ψ∣+tr(S0​(LLT+Ψ)−1) subject to admissible specific variances. Other approaches estimate communalities and extract latent factors from the reduced correlation matrix. Choose q using substantive plausibility, a scree plot, fitted residual correlations and model-fit assessment. Under local identifiability, a likelihood goodness-of-fit count for the covariance model is
2p(p+1)​−[pq+p−2q(q−1)​],
(24)
where the subtracted rotational dimension must be removed from the loading parameter count. This count alone does not establish identifiability, and boundary fits with zero specific variance need special care.
Factor rotation addresses interpretability and nonuniqueness. For every orthogonal matrix Q, replace (L,F) by (LQ,QTF); then (LQ)(LQ)T=LLT, so the fit is unchanged. The absolute orientation of unrestrained latent factors is therefore not identified by the covariance. Varimax rotation chooses orthogonal axes favoring a simple pattern of large and small squared loadings. Oblique rotation can permit correlated latent factors, provided their covariance is transformed consistently. Sign changes and permutations are further harmless relabelings; an interpretation must state its chosen convention.
The sketch uses four standardized hypothetical measurements sharing two orthogonal latent factors. Rotating the factor coordinates changes the loading descriptions while preserving every implied covariance and communality; the scree plot belongs to that same constructed covariance matrix. Factor analysis separates shared variation from specific variance, and its latent factors require an identifying or interpretive orientation.

Cluster analysis groups observations without given class labels. It starts by deciding what similarity means, usually through a dissimilarity matrix. Euclidean distance is natural for comparable continuous coordinates; a Mahalanobis distance can account for differing scales and correlations when a meaningful nonsingular covariance estimate is available. Scaling and variable selection can change the groups. Binary, ordinal or categorical data may need a different dissimilarity, rather than arbitrary numerical codes and Euclidean distance.
Agglomerative hierarchical clustering begins with singleton groups, repeatedly merges the pair with smallest intergroup dissimilarity, and records the merges in a dendrogram. The height represents the algorithm's merge criterion. Cutting the dendrogram gives partitions at different resolutions, and merges cannot subsequently be reversed. In single-linkage clustering, the criterion is the nearest cross-pair distance; this detects chains and nonconvex groups, but a thin bridge can connect otherwise separated clouds. Complete-linkage clustering uses the farthest cross-pair distance and tends to produce compact groups, at the cost of sensitivity to extreme points. Average-linkage clustering uses the mean cross-pair distance, lying between these two criteria for a given pair of clusters.
For squared Euclidean geometry, Ward clustering merges the pair that least increases the within-group sum of squares. Expanding deviations about the merged mean gives
Δ(A,B)=∣A∣+∣B∣∣A∣∣B∣​∥xˉA​−xˉB​∥2.
(25)
This favors low within-group dispersion, but interpreting it as a sum-of-squares increase requires Euclidean data. Different software may plot a rescaled height, so the vertical units of a dendrogram from Ward clustering must be stated.
A nonhierarchical alternative is k-means clustering. For a prescribed K, minimize
WK​=k=1∑K​i∈Ck​∑​∥xi​−mk​∥2.​
(26)
At fixed assignments, expanding squared distances or differentiating shows that the minimizing mk​ is the group mean. At fixed centroids, each point is assigned to a nearest centroid. Alternating these operations does not increase WK​; with a consistent tie rule and nonempty groups it reaches a locally stable partition. Initialization matters, so use multiple starts, and give an explicit rule for empty groups. It favors roughly spherical clusters and is sensitive to outliers and scaling. It cannot be expected to reproduce a nonconvex single-linkage partition.
The sketch displays a constructed point configuration and the complete-linkage merges computed from its actual Euclidean distances. A cut between the within-pair and between-pair merge heights yields three groups. Such visual separation is useful evidence, not proof of a uniquely correct number of populations. Select a resolution using subject knowledge, stability under resampling, separation diagnostics or the change of WK​ with K. Clustering always gives a partition even for unstructured data, and different distance or linkage choices can yield different sensible summaries. Clustering is an exploratory construction whose conclusions depend on the geometry and the chosen resolution.
