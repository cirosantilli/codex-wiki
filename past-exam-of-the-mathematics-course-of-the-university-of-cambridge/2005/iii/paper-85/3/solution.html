A right Ore localization at S is a unital homomorphism ι:R→Q such that every ι(s), s∈S, is a unit, every element of Q is ι(r)ι(s)−1, and
kerι={r∈R:rt=0 for some t∈S}.
(10)
Equivalently, the usual fraction construction has this zero criterion and is universal among homomorphisms that invert S. Injectivity is required only when the denominators are regular elements of a ring. If 0∈S, every homomorphism inverting S has zero target; this is the degenerate zero-ring localization. For a nonzero localization we require 0∈/S.
The necessary and sufficient conditions are that S be a right denominator set:
  • For r∈R and s∈S, there are t∈S and b∈R with rt=sb; this is the right Ore condition.
  • If sr=0 with s∈S, there is t∈S with rt=0; this is right reversibility.
For necessity, write the element ι(s)−1ι(r) as ι(b)ι(t)−1. Multiplying by ι(s) on the left and ι(t) on the right gives ι(rt−sb)=0. The zero criterion supplies u∈S with (rt−sb)u=0. Hence r(tu)=s(bu), the right Ore condition in R, not merely an equality of images in Q. Also sr=0 makes ι(r)=0 because ι(s) is invertible, and the zero criterion gives rt=0. This proves both necessities.
The sufficiency is the Ore theorem for denominator sets. Concretely, right fractions can be represented by (r,s); a common-denominator equality has su=tv∈S and ru=r′v for suitable u,v∈R. The two conditions make the induced fraction equivalence transitive and make addition and multiplication well defined; the zero fraction is exactly the kernel displayed above. The requested direction of the theorem was necessity, for which the complete argument has just been given.
For a right Noetherian domain, any two nonzero principal right ideals intersect. To see this without using the desired localization, suppose bR∩aR=0 with a,b=0. The sum
bR+abR+a2bR+⋯
(11)
is direct: reduce a finite relation modulo aR to get its first summand zero, then cancel a in the noncommutative domain and repeat. Every summand is nonzero, so the finite partial sums form a strictly ascending chain of right ideals, contradicting the ascending chain condition. A nonzero intersection rR∩sR thus provides rt=sb=0, with t=0. This is the right Ore condition for S=R∖{0}. Reversibility is automatic in a noncommutative domain, and the zero criterion makes the map injective. The localization is a division ring, since a nonzero rs−1 has inverse sr−1.
The Goldie theorem states that a semiprime ring has a semisimple Artinian classical right ring of quotients if and only if it has finite right uniform dimension and satisfies the ascending chain condition on right annihilators. A right Noetherian ring satisfies the annihilator condition because right annihilators are right ideals. Its right regular module has finite uniform dimension: an infinite direct sum of nonzero submodules would give a strictly ascending chain of finite partial sums. A prime ring is semiprime, so the theorem applies to a prime right Noetherian ring R.
To obtain a single matrix-ring factor, not just a finite product, prove that its quotient Q is prime. A nonzero two-sided ideal I⊆Q contains a nonzero fraction rs−1 and hence the nonzero element r=(rs−1)s∈I∩R. The same holds for any other nonzero ideal K. Since R is prime, (I∩R)(K∩R)=0, so IK=0. Thus Q is prime. The Artin–Wedderburn theorem gives a finite product of matrix rings over division rings; primeness excludes two different nonzero factors with zero product. This proves the prime classical quotient of a prime right Goldie ring:
Qclr​(R)≅Mn​(D)for a division ring D.​
(12)
For the requested counterexample to Noetherianity, take R=k[x1​,x2​,…]. It is a commutative integral domain and has its field of fractions as its classical right ring of quotients. But (x1​)⊊(x1​,x2​)⊊⋯ is a strictly ascending ideal chain: evaluation at x1​=⋯=xm​=0 shows xm+1​ is not in the preceding ideal. Therefore existence of a classical quotient does not imply right Noetherianity.
