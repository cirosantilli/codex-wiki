Let V⊆U be open subsets, and let s∈F3​(V). The assumed flasque-kernel section-lifting lemma lifts s to s∈F2​(V). Since F2​ is a flabby sheaf, extend s to a section on U, then project to F3​(U). Its restriction is s. Thus every restriction map of F3​ is surjective, proving that the quotient of two flabby sheaves in a short exact sequence is flabby.
For the construction of sheaf cohomology, embed a sheaf of abelian groups F into the sheaf
C(F)(U)=∏P∈U​FP​
(1)
by sending a section to all its germs. This is injective because a section with every germ zero is zero. Arbitrary families of stalk elements glue point by point, and restriction maps are projections; hence C(F) is a flabby sheaf. Set Q0=F, Ij=C(Qj) and Qj+1=Ij/Qj. Compose the quotient map with the next embedding to obtain the flasque resolution
0⟶F⟶I0⟶I1⟶⋯.
(2)
The sheaf cohomology groups are the cohomology groups of the resulting cochain complex of global sections:
Hj(X,F)=im(Γ(X,Ij−1)→Γ(X,Ij))ker(Γ(X,Ij)→Γ(X,Ij+1))​(j>0),H0(X,F)=Γ(X,F).
(3)
The usual comparison of resolutions identifies this construction with the right derived functors of global sections; the construction above is the Godement resolution.
If F is itself a flabby sheaf, the quotient argument shows inductively that every Qj is flabby. The assumed lifting result therefore makes
0→Γ(X,Qj)→Γ(X,Ij)→Γ(X,Qj+1)→0
(4)
an exact sequence for every j. The global-section complex is consequently exact in every positive degree. In particular,
Hj(X,F)=0(j>0)if F is flabby.​
(5)
This establishes the vanishing from the section-lifting property rather than assuming it in advance.
For the smooth algebraic curve X, a divisor on an algebraic curve is a finite formal sum D=∑P​nP​P of closed points with integer coefficients. At each closed point, the local ring of a smooth algebraic curve is a discrete valuation ring; write its normalized discrete valuation as vP​. For f∈k(X)∗ the principal divisor is div(f)=∑P​vP​(f)P. Only finitely many coefficients are nonzero: zeros and poles are proper closed subsets in the Noetherian Zariski topology of a curve. The divisor class group is the abelian group
Cl(X)=Div(X)/{div(f):f∈k(X)∗}.​
(6)
Equivalently, its elements are divisors on an algebraic curve modulo linear equivalence of divisors.

Put D=KX∗​/OX∗​, the quotient sheaf of abelian groups. At a closed point P, the discrete valuation gives an isomorphism
DP​=k(X)∗/OX,P∗​∼​Z,[f]⟼vP​(f).
(7)
Indeed, a local parameter on a smooth algebraic curve has valuation one, and the valuation kernel is precisely the unit group of the discrete valuation ring. Thus a local section represented by f determines its local principal divisor, and changing f by a regular function which is a unit does not change those coefficients.
These local valuations identify Γ(X,D) with Div(X). To see that they give finite support, represent a section locally by finitely many rational functions on an algebraic variety, using Noetherian Zariski topology to take a finite cover. Each representative has only finitely many zeros and poles, so their combined support is finite. Conversely, for D=∑nP​P, choose at each support point a local parameter tP​ and shrink its neighbourhood to exclude every other zero or pole of tP​ and every other support point. The local rational equation tPnP​​ represents the desired valuations there; on the complement of the support use 1. On overlaps these equations differ by units, so their classes glue in the quotient sheaf. Finally, a section with every valuation zero has zero germ everywhere and is the identity section. Hence the identification is both surjective and injective.
The map on global sections sends f∈k(X)∗ to div(f). Therefore its cokernel is
coker(H0(X,KX∗​)→H0(X,KX∗​/OX∗​))=Div(X)/Prin(X)≅Cl(X).​
(8)

For a divisor on an algebraic curve D=∑nP​P, define its line bundle associated to a divisor inside the sheaf of rational functions by
OX​(D)(U)={f∈k(X)∗:vP​(f)+nP​≥0 for every P∈U}∪{0}.
(9)
Locally choose a rational equation fi​ for D, as in the construction of the quotient sheaf above. Then
OX​(D)∣Ui​​=fi−1​OUi​​,OX​(D)P​=tP−nP​​OX,P​.
(10)
Consequently this is an invertible sheaf. Multiplication gives an isomorphism
OX​(D)⊗OX​​OX​(E)≅OX​(D+E),
(11)
as is checked on the displayed local generators. If D=div(f), multiplication by f identifies OX​(D) with OX​. Thus D↦[OX​(D)] induces a group homomorphism from the divisor class group to the Picard group.
For surjectivity, let L be an invertible sheaf. Trivialize its one-dimensional rational fibre over the function field k(X); equivalently, choose a nonzero rational section and identify L with a subsheaf of the rational-function sheaf. A finite trivializing cover gives
L∣Ui​​=ai​OUi​​,ai​∈k(X)∗.
(12)
On overlaps ai​/aj​ is a regular unit. The integers −vP​(ai​) therefore agree wherever two descriptions apply and define a finite divisor on an algebraic curve D. Its local description gives L=OX​(D). A different rational trivialization multiplies all ai​ by the same rational function, changing D by a principal divisor, so the resulting divisor class is intrinsic.
For injectivity, an isomorphism OX​(D)→OX​(E) becomes multiplication by some g∈k(X)∗ on the rational fibre. At each closed point its effect on the local free generators gives
gtP−nP​(D)​OX,P​=tP−nP​(E)​OX,P​,vP​(g)=nP​(D)−nP​(E).
(13)
Hence D−E=div(g). Conversely this equality gives the required isomorphism by multiplication by g. We have proved the group isomorphism
Cl(X)∼​Pic(X),[D]⟼[OX​(D)].​

Since X is an irreducible variety, every nonempty open subset is irreducible and the sheaf of nonzero rational functions on an irreducible variety has value k(X)∗ there. All its restriction maps between nonempty opens are identities, and restriction to the empty set is surjective. Thus KX∗​ is a flabby sheaf, and the introductory argument gives H1(X,KX∗​)=0.
Apply the long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology to the short exact sequence of sheaves
1⟶OX∗​⟶KX∗​⟶D⟶1.
(15)
Its relevant portion is
H0(X,KX∗​)⟶H0(X,D)∂​H1(X,OX∗​)⟶H1(X,KX∗​)=0.
(16)
The connecting homomorphism is therefore surjective, with kernel the image of k(X)∗. The computation in part (a) now gives
Cl(X)≅H1(X,OX∗​).​
(17)
Concretely, local rational equations fi​ of a divisor on an algebraic curve differ by regular units on overlaps. Their ratios give the gluing data of an invertible sheaf; changing the local equations by units changes those data by a coboundary. This is the transition-function interpretation of the identification with the Picard group. Reversing all transition ratios replaces a class by its inverse; either consistent convention gives the same group isomorphism.

Let X be an affine variety over an algebraically closed field k, with reduced coordinate ring A=k[X]. On an open subset U, define OX​(U) to be the functions h:U→k which, near every point, have an expression a/b with a,b∈A and b nowhere zero on that neighbourhood. Restriction is ordinary restriction of functions. Compatible local functions glue uniquely and remain locally quotients, so this defines the structure sheaf. On the principal open subset D(f) it gives Af​, and at P it gives the local ring AmP​​. The principal-open identification can also be seen by identifying D(f) with the affine algebraic set obtained by adjoining a coordinate t and the equation tf=1; its coordinate ring is A[t]/(tf−1)≅Af​. This construction permits several irreducible components; no division by a function vanishing identically on a component is assumed.
An abstract algebraic variety is a locally ringed space locally isomorphic to such affine models, with a finite affine cover; we use the usual separated convention. Reducible varieties are allowed here. The separation condition says that the diagonal morphism is a closed immersion. Two varieties are related by a birational map with an inverse when some dense open subset of one is isomorphic as a locally ringed space to a dense open subset of the other.
Define the ring of rational functions on a reduced variety by pairs (U,h), where U⊆X is dense open and h∈OX​(U), identifying two pairs when their restrictions agree on a common dense open subset. Finite intersections of dense open subsets are dense, so addition and multiplication on these intersections are well defined. For an irreducible variety this gives the usual function field; for a reducible variety it need not be a field. Restriction to any dense open subset V induces an isomorphism
Rat(X)≅Rat(V).
(18)
Indeed, intersecting domains with V defines the restriction, and a dense open subset of V is also dense open in X, which defines its inverse. An isomorphism between dense opens therefore induces an isomorphism of their rational rings. Thus birational varieties have isomorphic rings of rational functions, also in the reducible case.
For the finite-cover assertion, write the closed complement of U as V(I), where I=(f1​,…,fr​) is an ideal of the Noetherian ring A. Then
U=D(f1​)∪⋯∪D(fr​).
(19)
Let p1​,…,pt​ be the minimal prime ideals of A, corresponding to its irreducible components. Since U is dense, it meets every component, so I is contained in none of these primes. Prime avoidance supplies s∈I outside their union. Consequently D(s)⊆U meets a dense open subset of every component and is dense in X. Add D(s) to the finite cover if necessary. This proves that a finite principal-open cover can be chosen with a dense member. It does not say that every principal-open cover already has such a member: for X=V(xy), the dense open X∖{(0,0)}=D(x)∪D(y) has neither displayed member dense, although adding D(x+y) supplies a dense member.
For clarity, the zero divisors in this reduced Noetherian ring are exactly ⋃i​pi​. If ab=0 and a avoids every pi​, reduction in the domain A/pi​ forces b∈pi​ for all i, hence b=0. Conversely, if a∈pi​, choose for every j=i an element bj​∈pj​∖pi​, possible because distinct minimal primes are incomparable. Their product b is not in pi​ but belongs to all the other primes. Then b=0 and ab∈⋂j​pj​=0. For a single minimal prime, reducedness makes that prime zero and this argument uses b=1. It follows that
s is a non-zero-divisor⟺D(s) is dense.
(20)
The total quotient ring S−1A, where S consists of these non-zero-divisors, maps to Rat(X) by
a/s⟼the class of the regular function a/s on D(s).
(21)
The equivalence relation for localization makes this a well-defined k-algebra homomorphism. For surjectivity, a rational class represented on U restricts to the dense principal open D(s)⊆U constructed above. Its section there belongs to As​, so it is a/sr and lies in the image. For injectivity, if such a fraction is zero on a dense open subset, its numerator vanishes on that dense subset. The numerator's zero set is closed, so it vanishes everywhere on X and is zero in the reduced coordinate ring. Thus
Rat(X)≅S−1A.​
(22)
In particular A→S−1A is canonically injective: a/1=0 implies sa=0 for some non-zero-divisor s, hence a=0.
Finally let Y be the reduced closed union of exactly those irreducible components of X which contain P. Removing all the other components gives an open neighbourhood of P on which X and Y coincide, including their structure sheaves. Hence OX,P​≅OY,P​. Write B=k[Y] and let nP​ be its maximal ideal at P. Every minimal prime of B is contained in nP​, because every component of Y contains P. If b∈/nP​, it avoids each such prime and is a non-zero-divisor. Therefore the map of localizations
BnP​​⟶Q(B)=Rat(Y),a/b⟼a/b,
(23)
is defined and injective: if its value is zero, a non-zero-divisor annihilates a, so a=0. We obtain the desired local ring embeds in rational functions on incident components:
OX,P​↪Rat(Y),Y=P∈Xi​⋃​Xi​ with reduced structure.​
(24)
At an intersection point one must retain all incident components. For example, the local ring of V(xy) at the origin has two nonzero germs x,y with xy=0; it cannot embed into the function field of just one of its axes.

A quasi-coherent sheaf is a sheaf of modules which locally has a presentation by free module sheaves:
OU(J)​⟶OU(I)​⟶F∣U​⟶0,
(25)
where the index sets may be infinite. Equivalently, on affine neighbourhoods it is an affine module sheaf. A coherent sheaf is locally finitely generated, with the additional requirement that the kernel of every map from a finite free module sheaf is locally finitely generated. On a Noetherian scheme, these conditions are equivalent to being locally associated with a finitely generated module.
For an affine variety X with coordinate ring A and an A-module M, set
M(D(a))=Ma​,MP​=MmP​​.
(26)
Restriction on principal open subsets is the natural module localization map. On an arbitrary open subset, sections are families of these stalk values locally represented by a single fraction from a localization. This constructs the affine module sheaf; its global sections are M. Choose a free-module presentation A(J)→A(I)→M→0. Exactness of localization and its compatibility with direct sums yield
OX(J)​⟶OX(I)​⟶M⟶0.
(27)
Thus M is a quasi-coherent sheaf, without a finite-generation assumption.
We now prove coherence of an affine module sheaf. If M is finitely generated, take a surjection Ar→M. Since A is a Noetherian ring, its kernel is finitely generated, so M has a finite presentation of a module. More generally, on every principal affine open, the kernel of a map Aas​→Ma​ is a submodule of a finite free module over the Noetherian ring Aa​, and is finitely generated. On sheafifying, this verifies both parts of the definition of a coherent sheaf.
Conversely, if M is coherent, choose a finite principal-open cover X=⋃i​D(ai​) on which it has finitely many generators. Write those local generators as mij​/airij​​. Their numerators generate a finite submodule N=∑i,j​Amij​ of M, and Nai​​=Mai​​ for every i. To check that M/N=0, let q∈M/N. Each equality after localization gives an exponent ri​ such that airi​​q=0. The ideal generated by the airi​​ is the unit ideal: otherwise a maximal ideal containing all of them would give a point outside the principal-open cover. A linear combination of these powers equal to 1 consequently annihilates q. Every q is zero, proving
M is coherent⟺M is a finitely generated A-module.​
(28)
Next consider a morphism ϕ:G1​→G2​ between quasi-coherent sheaves. On an affine open V of the target variety, use the assumed affine module-sheaf equivalence to write Gj​∣V​=Mj​​, with Mj​=Γ(V,Gj​). The sheaf morphism is the sheafification of the module map ψ=Γ(V,ϕ). Indeed, its action on a section m/ar of a principal open is forced by module-linearity and restriction to be ψ(m)/ar. Therefore exactness of localization gives
(kerϕ)(D(a))=ker((M1​)a​→(M2​)a​)=(kerψ)a​.
(29)
These identities are compatible with restriction, so (kerϕ)∣V​=kerψ​. Since this holds on an affine cover, the kernel sheaf is quasi-coherent on every variety. This proof of kernels of quasi-coherent sheaf morphisms does not assert that taking global sections is right exact.
For direct image along a morphism of affine varieties, write Y with coordinate ring A, X with coordinate ring B, and let α:A→B be the induced ring homomorphism. If F=M on X, regard M as an A-module by restriction of scalars. For a∈A,
f−1(D(a))=D(α(a)),(f∗​F)(D(a))=Mα(a)​=Ma​.
(30)
The last equality identifies localization with the scalar action of a. It identifies the direct image sheaf with the affine module sheaf of the restricted module, so f∗​F is quasi-coherent.
Suppose Y is affine and X=⋃i=1r​Ui​ is a finite affine cover. Since a variety is separated, every Ui​∩Uj​ is affine: it is identified with the inverse image of the closed diagonal morphism inside the affine product Ui​×k​Uj​. Write gi​=f∣Ui​​ and gij​=f∣Ui​∩Uj​​. Form the morphism of sheaves of modules
d:⨁i​(gi​)∗​(F∣Ui​​)⟶⨁i,j​(gij​)∗​(F∣Ui​∩Uj​​),(si​)⟼(si​∣Ui​∩Uj​​−sj​∣Ui​∩Uj​​).
(31)
Both sums are finite, and every summand is quasi-coherent by the affine calculation. For every open V⊆Y, the sheaf gluing axiom on f−1(V) identifies the kernel of this map on sections with F(f−1(V)). Hence
f∗​F=kerd.
(32)
The kernel result proved above establishes quasi-coherence.
Finally, for arbitrary Y, take an affine neighbourhood V⊆Y and apply the preceding argument to f−1(V)→V. The open subset f−1(V) is again a variety and has a finite affine cover, because varieties have Noetherian Zariski topology. The restriction of f∗​F to V is exactly the direct image for this restricted morphism. Thus
f∗​F is quasi-coherent for every morphism of varieties.​
(33)
The finiteness and separation conditions are the relevant hypotheses, not affineness of the original source or target. More generally, quasi-coherence of direct image under a quasi-compact quasi-separated morphism is valid. If one permits nonseparated finite-type prevarieties, each overlap in the argument has a finite affine cover; replacing its summand by the sum of direct images from these charts gives the same kernel, since agreement can be tested on a cover. This also proves the assertion in that convention. It does not extend by this argument to arbitrary spaces with an unrestricted infinite cover.

Let S=k[X0​,…,Xn​] with its usual grading, and put S(m)d​=Sm+d​. Define the twisting sheaf on projective space by
OPn​(m)=S(m)​.
(34)
More explicitly, on Ui​=D+​(Xi​) its sections are the homogeneous degree-m part of SXi​​. They are a free rank-one module over (SXi​​)0​=k[Xj​/Xi​:j=i], with generator ei​=Xim​. These generators make sense for every integer m, since Xi​ is invertible on this chart. Their transition rule is
ei​=(Xi​/Xj​)mej​.
(35)
The ratios are regular units on overlaps and satisfy the cocycle identity, so they glue to an invertible sheaf. In particular O(m)⊗O(r)≅O(m+r).
For n≥1, a global section is a compatible homogeneous degree-m rational expression lying in every SXi​​. Inside the fraction field of S,
⋂i=0n​SXi​​=S.
(36)
To justify this, write a fraction in lowest terms in the unique factorization domain S. Membership in SXi​​ forces every irreducible divisor of its denominator to be associated with Xi​. Since there are at least two nonassociated variables, the denominator must be a unit. Therefore
H0(Pn,O(m))={Sm​,0,​m≥0,m<0,​(n≥1).
(37)
The degree-m monomials are indexed by nonnegative integers a0​,…,an​ with sum m. The stars and bars count gives
h0(OPn​(m))={(nm+n​),0,​m≥0,m<0,​(n≥1).​
(38)
Here and below, an all-integer binomial shorthand must use the counting convention (na​)=0 when a<n, including negative a. The polynomial extension of binomial coefficients to negative upper arguments does not give the stated dimensions. In dimension zero, P0 is one point and every OP0​(m) is trivial, so its space of global sections is k for every m; this case is treated separately.
For a closed inclusion i:H↪Pn, direct image sheaf formation is exact: its stalk is the original stalk at a point of H and zero outside H. It preserves flabby sheaves, since restrictions on the target are restrictions between the corresponding open subsets of H. Push forward a flasque resolution of F; exactness gives a flasque resolution of i∗​F, and its complex of global sections is the original one because
Γ(Pn,i∗​Ij)=Γ(H,Ij).
(39)
Taking cohomology of these identical complexes proves sheaf cohomology under a closed inclusion:
Hr(H,F)≅Hr(Pn,i∗​F)(r≥0).​
(40)
This argument applies even without quasi-coherence.
We derive the remaining sheaf cohomology dimensions using the given eventual vanishing, the long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology, and induction on dimension. Multiplication by the linear equation of a hyperplane H≅Pn−1 gives the hyperplane exact sequence for twisting sheaves
0⟶OPn​(m−1)⟶OPn​(m)⟶i∗​OH​(m)⟶0.
(41)
Exactness can be checked in each chart by quotienting its polynomial ring by the equation of H; multiplication is injective because that equation is a non-zero-divisor. We use the closed-inclusion cohomology identification to replace the cohomology of the final sheaf by that on H.
The dimension-zero base is a point, whose global-section functor is exact. Thus all its higher sheaf cohomology is zero. For n=1, the relevant long exact sequence is
0→H0(O(m−1))→H0(O(m))→k→H1(O(m−1))→H1(O(m))→0.
(42)
When m≥0, restriction of homogeneous polynomials to the hyperplane point is surjective: after choosing that point as [1:0], the section X0m​ restricts to a generator. Therefore the consecutive H1 groups are isomorphic for m≥0. The given vanishing for large m propagates down to m=−1. For m<0, both displayed H0 groups are zero, and the sequence becomes
0→k→H1(O(m−1))→H1(O(m))→0.
(43)
Starting with H1(O(−1))=0, induction downward gives h1(O(m))=−m−1 for m≤−2. For degrees i>1, the point contributes no cohomology to the exact sequence, so consecutive Hi groups are isomorphic for every m; the given eventual vanishing makes them all zero. Hence the complete positive-degree formula holds for P1.
Now let n≥2 and assume the formulas for H≅Pn−1. For every m, the restriction
H0(Pn,O(m))→H0(H,OH​(m))
(44)
is surjective: for m≥0 extend a polynomial in the hyperplane coordinates to the same polynomial on Pn, and for m<0 both groups vanish because H has positive dimension. Consequently the exact sequence gives an injection
H1(Pn,O(m−1))↪H1(Pn,O(m)).
(45)
For 2≤i≤n−1, the induction hypothesis says Hi−1(H,OH​(m))=0, and gives the same injection in degree i. For each fixed m, composing finitely many injections reaches a sufficiently large twist, whose higher cohomology is zero by the given hypothesis. Therefore
Hi(Pn,O(m))=0(0<i<n, every m).
(46)
For i>n, the two neighbouring hyperplane groups Hi−1(H,OH​(m)) and Hi(H,OH​(m)) are zero by induction. Consecutive Hi groups on Pn are then isomorphic, and eventual vanishing again makes them zero. This derives vanishing above the dimension too, without needing a special projective-space cohomology theorem.
For top cohomology the exact sequence now reduces to
0→Hn−1(H,OH​(m))→Hn(Pn,O(m−1))→Hn(Pn,O(m))→0.
(47)
Write bn​(m)=hn(OPn​(m)). The induction hypothesis and eventual vanishing give the recurrence
bn​(m−1)=bn​(m)+bn−1​(m),bn−1​(m)={(n−1−m−1​),0,​m≤−n,m≥−n+1.​
(48)
Consecutive top groups agree for m≥−n+1, so descending from a large twist gives bn​(m)=0 for m≥−n. Below this range, iterating the recurrence gives a finite sum:
bn​(m)=∑j=m+1−n​(n−1−j−1​)=∑ℓ=n−1−m−2​(n−1ℓ​)=(n−m−1​)(m≤−n−1).
(49)
The last equality follows by summing Pascal's identity. These short exact sequences also prove that every group whose dimension is being counted is finite-dimensional. Thus, for n≥1,
hi(OPn​(m))=0(0<i=n),hn(OPn​(m))={(n−m−1​),0,​m≤−n−1,m≥−n.​​
(50)
With the stated counting convention, the latter is the requested single binomial formula. The base P0 has h0=1 for all twists and no higher cohomology; it is not governed by the negative-twist H0 rule for positive-dimensional projective space.
Finally calculate the canonical line bundle of a smooth variety from its transition functions. On U0​ write tj​=Xj​/X0​ for 1≤j≤n. On Ui​, i>0, take the coordinates uj​=Xj​/Xi​ in increasing order of j=i. Thus
u0​=ti−1​,uj​=tj​/ti​(j=0,i).
(51)
In their wedge product, du0​=−ti−2​dti​ and
duj​=ti−1​dtj​−tj​ti−2​dti​. Every term involving a second dti​ vanishes, so reordering the remaining differentials gives
⋀j=i​duj​=(−1)iti−(n+1)​dt1​∧⋯∧dtn​.
(52)
This computation is valid in every characteristic. Multiply the ordered local generator on Ui​ by (−1)i. The resulting generators ηi​ satisfy ηi​=(Xi​/X0​)−n−1η0​ on Ui​∩U0​, and hence the corresponding transition rule on every overlap. They are exactly the transitions of O(−n−1). Therefore the canonical bundle of projective space is
ωPn​≅OPn​(−n−1).​
(53)
For a line bundle, Serre duality states
Hi(Pn,O(m))∗≅Hn−i(Pn,O(−m−n−1)).
(54)
The intermediate groups on both sides vanish. In the two outer degrees, the top group at twist m has the same dimension as the global-section group at twist −m−n−1:
hn(O(m))=h0(O(−m−n−1)).
(55)
The polynomial degree on the right is nonnegative precisely when m≤−n−1, and its dimension is (n−m−1​). Interchanging m and −m−n−1 checks the other outer degree. On P0 both sides are one-dimensional. Thus all the formulas are consistent with Serre duality, which was used only for this final consistency check, not to prove the cohomology formulas.
