For a Newtonian fluid, write σ=−pI+2μe, where e=(∇u+∇uT)/2 is the rate-of-strain tensor. The Stokes equation without body force gives ∂j​σij​=0. Since the stress tensor is symmetric, its contraction with the antisymmetric part of the velocity gradient is zero. Thus
∂j​(ui​σij​)=σij​∂j​ui​=σ:e.
(1)
The divergence theorem proves the boundary-work identity
D=∫V​σ:edV=∫∂V​u⋅σndS.​
(2)
For an incompressible flow, tre=0, so D=2μ∫V​e:edV≥0 is precisely the viscous dissipation.
Apply this identity to the particle-free volume and then to the fluid outside the added particle. On the rigid particle, the velocity has the form V+Ω×x. Its contribution to the boundary work is V⋅F+Ω⋅T, which vanishes because the particle is force-free and torque-free. The outer velocity is unchanged. Subtracting the two boundary-work identities therefore gives
D′=∫∂V0​​U0​⋅σ′ndS.​
(3)
To move this expression to the particle surface, apply the Lorentz reciprocal theorem to (u0​,σ0​) and (u′,σ′) in the punctured fluid domain. For completeness, its local proof is
∇⋅(u0​⋅σ′−u′⋅σ0​)=2μe0​:e′−2μe′:e0​=0.
(4)
On the outer boundary u′=0. Let N=−n be the particle-outward normal, opposite to the fluid-outward normal there. Integrating the preceding divergence identity gives
D′=∫A​(u0​⋅σ′−u′⋅σ0​)NdS.​
(5)
This expression includes the removal of the original fluid inside the particle; that removal must not be counted separately again.
For a sphere in a uniform straining Stokes flow, E is symmetric and trace free, and u0​=Ex. Symmetry gives zero rigid translation and rotation. Put H=x⋅Ex and r=∣x∣. Use the Unscaled Papkovich–Neuber representation printed on the paper, with the decaying harmonic potentials
Φ′=Cr3a3Ex​,χ′=Br5a5H​.
(6)
Their harmonicity follows from xi​/r3=−∂i​(1/r) and from H/r5 being a trace-free second derivative of 1/r. Direct differentiation gives
u′=−3Cr5a3Hx​+Ba5(r52Ex​−r75Hx​).
(7)
At r=a, the no-slip boundary condition requires u′=−Ex. Matching the independent Ex and Hx terms fixes B=−1/2 and C=5/6. The disturbance velocity and pressure are consequently
u′=−r5a5​Ex−2r55a3​(1−r2a2​)Hx,p′=−r55μa3H​.​
(8)
The pressure follows from p′=2μ∇⋅Φ′. The disturbance decays, the total velocity vanishes at the sphere, and the harmonic representation verifies the Stokes equation. The boundary traction has zero total force by odd symmetry, and zero total torque because E is symmetric, confirming the assumed force-free and torque-free motion.
At the sphere u′=−u0​, so the extra dissipation due to a rigid inclusion becomes ∫A​u0​⋅(σ0​+σ′)NdS. Contracting the supplied total stress tensor with N cancels its terms proportional to (N⋅EN)N, leaving 5μEN. Thus
D′=5μa∫A​∣EN∣2dS.
(9)
Using ∫A​Ni​Nj​dS=4πa2δij​/3 proves the single-sphere dissipation increase
D′=320π​μa3E:E.​
(10)
One may perform the comparison in a large bounded vessel with prescribed straining velocity and then take its size to infinity; the given near-sphere approximation makes this limit compatible with the fixed-outer-velocity definition of D′.
The sphere number density is ns​=ϕ/(4πa3/3)=3ϕ/(4πa3). Neglecting hydrodynamic interactions, the additional viscous dissipation per unit volume is ns​D′=5μϕE:E. Adding the particle-free value 2μE:E gives
2μeff​E:E=2μE:E+5μϕE:E,μeff​=μ(1+25​ϕ).​
(11)
This is the Einstein viscosity formula for a dilute suspension, to first order in the particle volume fraction.

Use the Unscaled Papkovich–Neuber representation with ek​=eikx−ky and harmonic potentials
Φ=(0,iUek​),χ=−kiU​ek​.​
(12)
To see how these coefficients arise, start with Φ=(0,Aek​) and χ=Bek​. The representation gives
u=ik(Ay+B)ek​,v=(−A−kB−kAy)ek​.
(13)
At y=0, the prescribed tangential velocity requires ikB=U, while zero normal velocity requires A=−kB=iU. Hence the decaying half-space flow is
u=U(1−ky)ek​,v=−ikUyek​,p=−2iμkUek​.​
(14)
The pressure follows from 2μ∂y​Φy​. These harmonic potentials verify the Stokes equation and incompressibility; the polynomial factors do not prevent decay as y→∞.
For the Newtonian fluid stress tensor, σxy​=μ(uy​+vx​) and σyy​=−p+2μvy​. On the boundary uy​=−2kUeikx, vx​=0 and vy​=−ikUeikx. Therefore
σxy​(x,0)=−2μkUeikx,σyy​(x,0)=0.​
(15)
Take real parts for a physical sinusoidal flow. Together with the normal-velocity result supplied in the question, these formulas give the Fourier traction map for a viscous half-space; for signed wave numbers its resistance coefficient is 2μ∣k∣.

Under the plug-like extensional viscosity approximation, incompressibility gives wz​=−ux​. Symmetry about z=0 fixes w=−zux​. Remove the reference hydrostatic stress of the middle fluid, and write S for its leading vertical normal stress. Vertical traction balance at the upper surface gives
S=−Δρgh+σzz+​.
(16)
The middle fluid's horizontal normal stress exceeds S by 4μux​: the horizontal strain is ux​ and the vertical strain is −ux​. Hence
σxxmiddle​=S+4μux​.
(17)
At a slightly inclined upper interface, tangential traction continuity gives, to the order retained in the small-outer-viscosity extensional approximation,
σxzmiddle​(h)=σxz+​+4μhx​ux​.
(18)
The slope term is needed even though the interface is nearly horizontal: dropping it would miss the derivative of h in the extensional resultant. Terms involving the outer deviatoric normal-stress difference times the slope are smaller in this approximation. Integrating horizontal force balance over the symmetric layer, or over its upper half, now gives
0=h∂x​(S+4μux​)+σxzmiddle​(h).
(19)
Substitution yields the extensional-flow equation
4μ∂x​(hux​)=h∂x​(Δρgh−σzz+​)−σxz+​.​
(20)
The full layer thickness is 2h, so its conservation of mass is (2h)t​+(2hu)x​=0, or
ht​+(hu)x​=0.​
(21)
This explains the factor 4μ in the extensional stress rather than the shear viscosity coefficient alone.
For a Fourier mode of wave number k>0, set h=h0​+ηeikx−st and u=ueikx−st. Neglect σzz+​ as stipulated. The Fourier traction map for a viscous half-space, with outer dynamic viscosity λμ, gives σxz+​=−2λμkueikx−st. The two linear equations are
−4μh0​k2u=ikΔρgh0​η+2λμku,−sη+ikh0​u=0.
(22)
Eliminating u proves the positive decay rate for relaxation of a symmetric viscous layer:
s=2μΔρgh0​​λ+2κκ​,κ=kh0​.​
(23)
When λ≪κ, inner longitudinal extension dominates the viscous dissipation, and s∼Δρgh0​/(4μ). When κ≪λ while the plug approximation remains valid, tangential motion of the semi-infinite outer fluids supplies the main resistance, and s∼Δρgh0​κ/(2λμ). The PDF's second inequality is κ≪λ; the TeX converts it incorrectly to κ≪1. At much larger outer viscosity the plug assumption fails, as the next calculation shows.

Applying the stated long-wave expression formally in the intended high-viscosity regime, the numerator is dominated by 2λκ2/3 and the displayed denominator by λ. It gives
s∼3μΔρgh0​​κ2.​
(24)
The mechanism is inner transverse shear: the outer fluids almost immobilize the interfaces tangentially, so the middle layer has a pressure-driven parabolic velocity instead of the plug-like extensional velocity of part (b). Indeed, with nearly stationary faces at z=±h, its integrated flux is −2h3px​/(3μ). With px​=Δρghx​, conservation of mass gives precisely the displayed decay rate. The leading viscous dissipation is then in the middle layer, even though its neighbours have much greater dynamic viscosity.
There is a nonuniformity in taking this conclusion literally for every λ≫κ−1. A long-wave remainder written for fixed λ cannot be assumed uniform as λ diverges. Here this matters physically because sufficiently viscous outer fluids also resist normal interface motion. The distinction can be resolved directly, without an unspecified remainder, from the Stokes equation. For a symmetric thickness mode, the middle vertical velocity has the form
w(z)=Asinh(kz)+B(kz)cosh(kz),u=ki​wz​.
(25)
It is odd in z, and the middle horizontal velocity is even. At z=h0​, the exact outer traction map is σxz+​=−2λμku and σzz+​=−2λμkw. Tangential continuity and the normal hydrostatic jump therefore give
wzz​+k2w=−2λkwz​,−k2μ​wzzz​+3μwz​+2λμkw=−Δρgη.
(26)
The latter uses p=μ(wzzz​−k2wz​)/k2, obtained from horizontal force balance. The kinematic condition is w(h0​)=−sη. Writing S=sinhκ and C=coshκ, tangential continuity fixes
A(S+λC)+B[S+κC+λ(C+κS)]=0.
(27)
Eliminating A,B in the remaining two equations gives the exact symmetric-layer rate
s=2μκΔρgh0​​λcosh(2κ)+(SC+κ)+λ2(SC−κ)S2+λ(SC−κ)​.
(28)
For κ≪1 and λκ≫1, this proves the uniform high-viscosity limit of viscous-layer relaxation
s∼2μΔρgh0​​1+(2/3)λκ3(2/3)κ2​.​
(29)
Thus the intended inner-shear answer is valid in the distinguished range κ−1≪λ≪κ−3. If λκ3≫1, the main resistance is instead outer normal motion and
s∼2λμkΔρg​.
(30)
Both ranges satisfy the printed λ≫κ−1≫1. For example λ=κ−4 lies in the latter range and has s∼[Δρgh0​/(2μ)]κ3, rather than a rate proportional to κ2. The exact denominator contains a λ2κ3 contribution hidden by a fixed-parameter remainder. The inner-shear limit is the intended result, but an additional upper restriction on λ is needed to make it a uniform conclusion.

Choose the axial origin at the sphere's centre. Reflection x↦−x leaves the straight tube, the spherical geometry and the transverse displacement of the centre unchanged. It reverses the prescribed axial Hagen-Poiseuille flow, while it leaves a hypothetical transverse particle velocity unchanged. On the other hand, kinematic reversibility of Stokes flow says that reversing the imposed flow reverses every particle velocity, including that transverse component. The force-free and torque-free constraints are preserved by both operations. Uniqueness of the coupled Stokes flow and rigid-body motion therefore makes the transverse velocity equal to its own negative. It must vanish.
Consequently c˙=0 as the particle is advected. This is no lateral migration of a single sphere in a uniform tube in Stokes flow. The argument uses the fore-aft symmetry of a rigid sphere and a uniform tube; it does not extend automatically to inertial migration or deformable particles.

Integrate axial force balance ∇⋅σ=0 over the fluid between the two remote tube sections, excluding the particle. The wall's axial traction contributes
a∫x1​x2​​∫02π​σrx​(a,θ,x)dθdx.
(31)
At each remote section the Hagen-Poiseuille flow has ux​ independent of x, so σxx​=−p. The downstream and upstream caps therefore contribute −πa2p(x2​)+πa2p(x1​). The particle contributes zero net axial force because it is force-free. Adding the three contributions proves the wall-shear and pressure-drop balance in a tube:
a∫x1​x2​​∫02π​σrx​(a,θ,x)dθdx=πa2[p(x2​)−p(x1​)].​
(32)
Here p is the physical pressure. The sign follows from the outward normals on the two caps; no convention for a positive pressure-drop magnitude has yet been introduced.

In this continuation λ measures eccentricity, not the viscosity ratio used in Question 2. Take ϵ→0 with 0≤λ<1 fixed. For the nearly occluding sphere in a cylindrical tube, the narrow gap has thickness H∼ϵa and axial length ℓ∼aϵ​, obtained by balancing h0​ and x2/(2a). In the sphere frame the tangential velocity scale is U. The lubrication approximation then gives the gap scales
∣px​∣∼a2ϵ2μU​,∣p−pfar​∣∼aϵ3/2μU​,∣σxy​∣∼aϵμU​.​
(33)
The large local pressure excursion is distinct from the eventual net pressure jump. Away from the sphere the velocity varies across a radius a, so the far Hagen-Poiseuille flow has
∣px​∣∼a2μU​,∣σrx​∣∼aμU​.​
(34)
These scales also show why the gap pressure can be much larger than its shear stress while the leading net jump can be smaller than that pressure.
Use y=a−r, so y=0 is the moving tube wall and y=h is the locally stationary sphere. The no-slip boundary conditions are u(0)=−U, u(h)=0. Integrating μuyy​=px​, with pressure independent of y to leading order, gives the Couette-Poiseuille flow in a thin gap
u(y)=2μpx​​y(y−h)+U(hy​−1),q=∫0h​udy=−12μh3​px​−2Uh​.
(35)
Differentiate at both surfaces and eliminate px​ using this moving-boundary lubrication flux:
μσxy​(0)​=h4U​+h26q​,μσxy​(h)​=−h2U​−h26q​.​
(36)
The coordinate reversal means σrx​=−σxy​ at the tube wall.
At leading order q is independent of x in each angular strip, though it may depend on θ. Both boundaries are stationary as shapes in the sphere frame and impermeable, so the integrated continuity equation is qx​+a−1(qθcirc​)θ​=0. The pressure changes along x over aϵ​ but along the circumference over a. Thus circumferential velocity is only O(Uϵ​), and its flux is O(Uaϵ3/2). Circumferential flux divergence is smaller than the possible leading axial divergence by a factor ϵ. Consequently qx​=0 to the order needed. This is not an assumption of equal flux at different angular positions.
For fixed θ define the lubrication resistance integrals for a nearly occluding sphere
Jn​(θ)=∫−∞∞​h(θ,x)ndx​=2a​h01/2−n​In​.
(37)
In particular, J1​=π2a/h0​​, J2​=J1​/(2h0​) and J3​=3J1​/(8h02​). The local pressure relation gives
∫px​dx=−12μqJ3​−6μUJ2​.
(38)
A putative generic value q=O(Uh0​) makes this jump O(μU/(aϵ3/2)). However, the integrated wall shear in part (ii) is only O(μUa/ϵ​), so the force-free global balance permits a common net pressure jump only of order μU/(aϵ​). The outer flow has circumferentially uniform pressures on both sides. Therefore the O(ϵ−3/2) pressure jump must cancel in every angular strip, not merely after angular averaging. The pressure recovery condition in lubrication flow determines
−12μqJ3​−6μUJ2​=0,q=−2U​J3​J2​​=−32​Uh0​(θ).​
(39)
This explains how the leading-order part of the global force balance selects the local flux.
The total sphere-frame volume flux is now
Qframe​=a∫02π​q(θ)dθ=−34​πϵa2U.
(40)
Transforming back to the laboratory adds πa2U, so Qlab​=πa2U(1−4ϵ/3) to this order. For physical pressure, Hagen-Poiseuille flow gives Qlab​=−πa4px​/(8μ). Thus
−px​=a28μU​(1−34​ϵ)+o(a2ϵμU​).​
(41)
The printed positive expression is the pressure-drop gradient in the direction of the laboratory flow; the Poiseuille hint omits the minus sign when px​ is read as the derivative of physical pressure. With the specified wall velocity −U, the laboratory flow is positive and its physical pressure gradient is negative.
Next insert the leading flux into the wall shear stress:
σxy​(0)=4μU(h1​−h2h0​​),∫σxy​(0)dx=4μU(J1​−h0​J2​)=2μUJ1​.
(42)
Let Δp>0 be the localized extra pressure drop, after subtracting the regular far Poiseuille gradient. Contributions outside the neck are only O(μU/a) and are smaller than the O(ϵ−1/2) jump. Since σrx​=−σxy​, part (ii) gives πa2Δp=a∫∫σxy​(0)dxdθ. Hence the leading pressure drop is
Δp∼ϵ2​​a2μU​∫02π​1+λcosθ​dθ​.​
(43)
For example, the concentric limit is Δp∼4πμU2/ϵ​/a. The integral grows as the minimum gap shrinks; the fixed-λ<1 qualification prevents treating a further near-contact limit as uniformly covered.
On the sphere the leading shear stress is σxy​(h)=−2μU/h+4μUh0​/h2, so
∫−∞∞​σxy​(h)dx=−2μUJ1​+4μUh0​J2​=0.​
(44)
Positive and negative tangential stresses cancel within each angular strip. Thus the leading tangential resultant, and its leading torque contribution, vanish: negligible rotation is consistent with the torque-free condition. The shear is not zero pointwise, and the pressure forces are not zero. Pressure on a sphere cannot produce a torque about its centre because its normal is radial; its force contributions and the matched far pressure jump remain essential to the force-free transport of a nearly occluding sphere.

Write G=Δρg>0 and take τ>0 in the positive x direction. Removing the ambient hydrostatic pressure leaves excess pressure G(h−z), so the horizontal pressure gradient in the dense gravity current is G∇h. The lubrication approximation gives
μuzz​=Ghx​,μvzz​=Ghy​.
(45)
The no-slip boundary condition at z=0 and the imposed upper shear stress require u(0)=v(0)=0, μuz​(h)=τ and μvz​(h)=0. Integration yields
u=μτ​z+μG​hx​(2z2​−hz),v=μG​hy​(2z2​−hz).
(46)
Consequently the depth-integrated volume flux is
q=2μτh2​ex​−3μGh3​∇h.
(47)
Using conservation of mass, ht​+∇⋅q=0, proves the evolution equation for a shear-driven viscous gravity current:
ht​+2μτ​(h2)x​=3μG​∇⋅(h3∇h).​
(48)
The gravity term smooths thickness gradients, while the imposed shear transports the current downstream.
The approximations need more than a small ordinary Reynolds number. With thickness H, horizontal scale L, dense-fluid density ρc​=ρ+Δρ and typical horizontal speed V, sufficient small parameters are
LH​≪1,μLρc​VH2​≪1,GHLμV​≪1.​
(49)
The first neglects horizontal viscous derivatives relative to vertical ones. The second compares convective inertia with vertical viscous stress: it is the reduced Reynolds number, ReH​(H/L), rather than a requirement that ReH​ itself be small. The third keeps viscous normal stress small relative to the excess hydrostatic pressure GH. Here V is of order τH/μ+GH3/(μL), so this last condition requires τ/(GL)≪1 together with (H/L)2≪1. Explicit sufficient inertia conditions are ρc​τH3/(μ2L)≪1 and ρc​GH5/(μ2L2)≪1. If an independently imposed unsteady time scale is T, also require ρc​H2/(μT)≪1; for T∼L/V this is the same reduced-inertia condition.
Set A=τ/(2μ) and D=G/(3μ). Far downstream, the transverse scale Y=yN​ is much smaller than x, so the downstream gravity-flux divergence is smaller than the transverse one by order (Y/x)2. For the steady flow, away from the source,
A(h2)x​=D(h3hy​)y​,Q=A∫−YY​h2dy.
(50)
The latter expresses conservation of the prescribed point-source volume flux. In scaling form, Q∼AH2Y and AH2/x∼DH4/Y2. Eliminating H gives Y3∼DQx/A2, proving yN​∝x1/3 and H∝x−1/6.
For the exact downstream similarity solution, put w=h2. Since h3hy​=(w2)y​/4, the reduced equation is the porous medium equation
wx​=4AD​(w2)yy​.
(51)
Seek w=x−1/3F(η) with η=y/x1/3. Substitution gives
−3A​(F+ηF′)=4D​(F2)′′.
(52)
Integrate once, using symmetry and zero transverse flux at η=0, to obtain −AηF/3=DFF′/2. Where F>0, this gives F′=−2Aη/(3D), hence F=A(ηN2​−η2)/(3D). Vanishing thickness fixes its support. The volume-flux condition then reads
Q=3DxA2​∫−YY​(Y2−y2)dy=9Dx4A2Y3​.
(53)
Thus the downstream profile and half-width are
yN​(x)h(x,y)​=(τ23μΔρgQ​x)1/3,=[2Δρgxτ​(yN​(x)2−y2)]1/2(∣y∣<yN​),​​
(54)
with h=0 outside that interval. This is the downstream similarity of a shear-driven gravity current. The square-root edge is the formal outer thin-layer solution; arbitrarily close to that edge its slope is not small, so the local front requires a separate description. This does not change the downstream scaling and flux calculation.
Near the source the two horizontal scales are comparable, say ℓ, so the far-downstream reduction cannot locate the upstream nose. In the upstream direction the downstream shear flux AH2 must balance the upstream gravity flux of order DH4/ℓ. Hence ℓ∼(D/A)H2. The total discharge through a region of width ℓ is Q∼AH2ℓ, giving H4∼Q/D. Combining these estimates gives the upstream reach scale
ℓup​ is of order τμΔρgQ​​.​
(55)
The shear advects liquid toward positive x, while the hydrostatic thickness gradient drives liquid back toward negative x; their balance stops further upstream spreading. A numerical prefactor requires the full two-dimensional near-source free-boundary solution and cannot be fixed by these scaling arguments alone.
