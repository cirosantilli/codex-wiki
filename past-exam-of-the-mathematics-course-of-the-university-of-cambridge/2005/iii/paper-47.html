A real weakly stationary process has finite second moments, a time-independent mean μ, and autocovariance function γk​=Cov(Xt​,Xt+k​) depending only on the lag. Use the angular-frequency convention
γk​=∫−ππ​eikλfX​(λ)dλ,fX​(λ)=2π1​k∈Z∑​γk​e−ikλ.​
(1)
The time-series spectral density is nonnegative and integrates to γ0​. The displayed Fourier series is ordinarily justified by ∑k​∣γk​∣<∞. More generally, weak stationarity gives a spectral measure of a stationary time series, not necessarily a spectral density. If a spectral density exists without this summability, its Fourier inversion can be understood using Fejér sums, which converge in L1 and at its Lebesgue points. Thus spectral density existence should not be inferred from weak stationarity alone.
A white noise process WN(0,σ2) has mean zero and Cov(εt​,εs​)=σ21{t=s}​. Neither independence nor a normal distribution is needed for this second-order definition. Its autocovariance function is zero at nonzero lags, so
fε​(λ)=2πσ2​.​
(2)
For a real absolutely summable filter (cr​), the series defining Yt​ has mean-square convergence: its tails have L2 norm at most ∥X0​∥2​∑tail​∣cr​∣. Its mean is μ∑r​cr​, and
γY​(k)=∑r,s∈Z​cr​cs​γX​(k+r−s).
(3)
The sum is absolutely convergent, since ∣γX​(j)∣≤γX​(0) by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. It depends only on k, proving that the output is a weakly stationary process. Substitute the spectral integral for γX​ and interchange the sums and integral, justified by fX​∈L1 and ∑r,s​∣cr​cs​∣<∞. This gives
γY​(k)=∫−ππ​eikλC(eiλ)C(e−iλ)fX​(λ)dλ.
(4)
Because the coefficients are real, the two factors are complex conjugates. Hence the spectral density transformation under a linear filter is
fY​(λ)=∣C(e−iλ)∣2fX​(λ).​
(5)
For complex coefficients the same formula holds with the conjugate-covariance convention.
Applying this result to the order-two moving-average model yields
fX​(λ)=2πσ2​∣1+θ1​e−iλ+θ2​e−2iλ∣2=2πσ2​[1+θ12​+θ22​+2θ1​(1+θ2​)cosλ+2θ2​cos2λ].​
(6)
Equivalently, its nonzero autocovariances are γ0​=σ2(1+θ12​+θ22​), γ1​=σ2θ1​(1+θ2​) and γ2​=σ2θ2​, together with γ−k​=γk​.
For the order-one moving-average process, invert its filter by a geometric series. The required coefficients are
cr​=(−θ)r,r=0,1,2,….​
(7)
They are absolutely summable for ∣θ∣<1. Direct cancellation gives
∑r=0n​(−θ)rXt−r​=εt​−(−θ)n+1εt−n−1​⟶εt​
(8)
with mean-square convergence. Thus the inverse filter recovers exactly the original white noise process, including its variance; it is an invertible time-series representation.

The series therefore has mean-square convergence and defines a linear filter of a stationary time series; multiplying by Φ verifies the equation.

Φ(z)=1−αz−α2z2=(1−αφz)(1+αz/φ).

A linear congruential generator starts with an integer seed S0​∈{0,…,M−1} and iterates
Si+1​=(aSi​+c)modM,Ui​=Si​/M.
(14)
The outputs lie in [0,1) and are used as pseudorandom numbers approximating a uniform distribution. They are deterministic once the seed is fixed. The largest possible period is M, since there are only M states; good period alone does not establish independence or good multivariate uniformity.
The full-period criterion for a mixed congruential generator says that period M for every seed occurs exactly when
gcd(c,M)=1,p∣(a−1) for every prime p∣M,4∣M ⟹ 4∣(a−1).​

The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm is useful when a target posterior can be evaluated up to a normalizing constant but direct independent sampling or simple conditional sampling is unavailable. It constructs a Markov chain with that posterior as invariant law. After adequate convergence, averages of functions of the chain estimate posterior expectations, and the retained draws approximate marginal distributions, quantiles and credible intervals.
For the Random-walk Metropolis algorithm, at current θ propose θ′=θ+Z, where Z has a symmetric distribution, often N(0,Σ). With a symmetric proposal the acceptance probability is
a(θ,θ′)=min{1,π(θ′∣X)/π(θ∣X)}.​
(38)
Accept the proposal when an independent uniform is below this probability; otherwise retain the current value. For a nonsymmetric proposal q(θ′∣θ), include q(θ∣θ′)/q(θ′∣θ) in the ratio. Detailed balance follows because the accepted flow is the minimum of the two proposed directional flows; rejection supplies the remaining self-transition probability. Irreducibility, appropriate recurrence and aperiodicity are also needed for convergence.
In the displayed trace plots, the first parameter moves in small increments with pronounced long-term wandering and a shift from its early region to a later region. This is consistent with an undersized proposal or a strongly correlated slow direction. Measure its acceptance rate: if it is very high, increase that coordinate's proposal variance. The second parameter moves rapidly within regions but changes its level around iteration 4500; that is evidence against treating the full run as a settled stationary sample, and it suggests investigating dependence with the first parameter or separated posterior regions. Its local scale may already be reasonable, so a blanket reduction of all proposal variances would be unwarranted. The third parameter has conspicuous long plateaus, indicating many repeated states: if the recorded acceptance is low, reduce its proposal variance; also check constraints and dependence before deciding that scale alone is the cause.
Rerun with coordinate-specific scale changes, inspect joint dependence, consider a covariance-based block proposal, and run longer chains from dispersed starting points. The MCMC trace diagnosis and proposal tuning should include actual acceptance rates and effective sample sizes; the three marginal trace plots do not by themselves identify correlations or prove convergence. Further practical details are in the three subparts.

Convergence means that the chain's distribution approaches the intended posterior, independently of its initial state, under the relevant ergodicity assumptions. It is separate from obtaining enough effectively independent draws for a precise answer. Start several chains at dispersed plausible points, examine trace plots and stability of posterior summaries, compare between-chain and within-chain variation, and inspect autocorrelation and effective sample size of a Markov chain. Discard an initial transient when appropriate and report Monte Carlo uncertainty.
No finite diagnostic proves convergence. Agreement among chains started in the same basin can miss another mode. If different starting points give incompatible summaries, extend runs or improve proposals rather than merely deleting more observations. Extra thinning does not repair poor exploration; retaining all post-transient draws usually preserves more information for estimating uncertainty.

Single-component updates propose one coordinate while holding the others fixed, with acceptance based on the resulting target ratio. They are cheap and permit individual scale tuning, but can crawl along a narrow correlated ridge. Block updates propose several coordinates jointly and can follow posterior covariance directions, potentially improving mixing substantially.
A practical compromise uses small correlated blocks, or a full Gaussian block with covariance estimated in a pilot run and rescaled for acceptance. Blocking independent coordinates need not help, and a badly scaled high-dimensional block may be rejected frequently. Compare computing cost per effective draw, rather than acceptance rate alone. A Metropolis-within-Gibbs sweep of coordinate updates and a properly accepted block update both preserve the target.

For minimizing an energy F(θ), simulated annealing at temperature T>0 uses the Boltzmann distribution πT​(θ)∝e−F(θ)/T. At each temperature run a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm or Gibbs transition preserving that law, then reduce T. With a symmetric proposal, accept θ′ with probability min{1,e−[F(θ′)−F(θ)]/T}: downhill moves are accepted and uphill moves remain possible. Keep the best state encountered. As temperature decreases, equilibrium mass favors global minima, provided the target is proper and the relevant concentration hypotheses hold. An arbitrary finite cooling run does not by itself prove global convergence.
Let s=∑i=1m​xi​, xˉ=s/m and S=∑i​(xi​−xˉ)2. For the Poisson distribution model the observations must be nonnegative integers. Its log-likelihood is

Also ET​(μ−xˉ)2=(T/m)ET​v→0.
There is a direct convergence proof for the actual changing-temperature Gibbs algorithm, beyond convergence of its equilibrium probability densities. Start at a finite deterministic v0​>0, choose deterministic 0<Tn​≤m/10 with Tn​→0, draw μn​ conditional on vn−1​, then draw vn​ conditional on μn​ using the two boxed distributions. Conditional normal moments give
E[(μn​−xˉ)2∣vn−1​]=Tn​vn−1​/m,E[(μn​−xˉ)4∣vn−1​]=3(Tn​vn−1​/m)2.
(53)
The inverse-gamma distribution first and second moments consequently yield
Evn​=m−4Tn​S+Tn​Evn−1​​,
(54)
and
Evn2​=(m−4Tn​)(m−6Tn​)S2+2STn​Evn−1​+3Tn2​Evn−12​​.
(55)
In the first recursion the coefficient of the preceding mean is at most 1/6; in the second the coefficient of the preceding second moment is at most 1/8. The means therefore stay bounded, and then so do the second moments. Letting Tn​→0 in these formulas gives Evn​→S/m and Evn2​→(S/m)2. Together with E(μn​−xˉ)2=Tn​Evn−1​/m→0, this proves
(μn​,vn​)⟶(xˉ,S/m)in mean square and hence in probability.​
(56)
This Gaussian likelihood Gibbs annealing convergence uses the explicit conditional structure and does not assume that one Gibbs sampling sweep is an exact equilibrium draw.
For the Poisson distribution model, the likelihood-power law with respect to dλ has the normalized probability density function
πT​(λ)=Γ(s/T+1)(m/T)s/T+1​λs/Te−mλ/T(λ>0),
(57)
so
λT​∼Gamma(s/T+1,m/T),EλT​=ms+T​,Var(λT​)=m2sT+T2​.​
(58)
The mean tends to xˉ and the variance to zero, proving convergence to a point mass at the Poisson maximum-likelihood estimate, including the zero-boundary case.
The Akaike information criterion is 2k−2ℓ(θ), counting one parameter for Poisson and two for normal. With 0log0=0 and S>0, the two forms are
AICP​=2+2s−2slog(s/m)+2i∑​log(xi​!),​
(59)
and
AICN​=4+m[log(2πS/m)+1].​
(60)
Choose the smaller criterion. Likelihood constants that differ between models, including the factorial and 2π terms, cannot be discarded.
For a fully specified trans-dimensional annealing for penalized likelihood, let M=P or N and define
EP​(λ)=2−2ℓP​(λ),EN​(μ,v)=4−2ℓN​(μ,v).
(61)
Use the combined target, relative to counting measure on models and Lebesgue measure on their parameters,
ΠT​(M,ϑ)∝wM​e−EM​(ϑ)/(2T)=wM​e−kM​/TLM​(ϑ)1/T,
(62)
where wP​,wN​>0 are fixed reference weights. For S>0 and 0<T<m/3 both model components are integrable. The factor 2T is a convenient temperature convention keeping the within-model laws exactly those already derived. The extra factor e−kM​/T makes the zero-temperature objective AIC rather than unpenalized likelihood.
Use, for example, probability 1/2 for a model-change attempt in either model and probability 1/2 for a within-model update. Within the Poisson distribution model draw a fresh gamma value from its boxed law; within the normal distribution model perform the boxed Gibbs sampling sweep. For a Poisson-to-normal proposal, draw u∼q(u)=N(0,τ2) with a fixed τ2>0, and set
(μ,v)=(λ+u,λ).
(63)
The inverse normal-to-Poisson proposal is λ=v with reverse auxiliary u=μ−v. This dimension matching uses one auxiliary variable to augment the one-dimensional Poisson parameter space. The absolute Jacobian determinant is
J=​det(11​10​)​=1.
(64)
If the forward model-jump selection probability is b and the reverse probability is d, the reversible-jump Markov chain Monte Carlo acceptance ratio is
RP→N​=wP​bq(u)wN​d​exp[−2TEN​(λ+u,λ)−EP​(λ)​]J.​
(65)
Accept with probability min(1,RP→N​). For the reverse move compute λ=v,u=μ−v and use
RN→P​=wN​dwP​bq(μ−v)​exp[−2TEP​(v)−EN​(μ,v)​]J−1.​
(66)
For the suggested equal selection probabilities, b=d=1/2 and those factors cancel. The probability density function q must still be included, despite symmetry of its normal distribution: it is an auxiliary draw in only the forward dimension-increasing proposal. Pairing a move with its inverse makes the accepted forward and reverse probability flows equal after the change of variables, proving detailed balance for each fixed-temperature model-switch kernel.
Cool through proper temperatures, allow enough model and parameter exploration at each stage, and retain the smallest observed EM​. The minima of the two energies are exactly the two AIC values above. Low-temperature equilibrium mass concentrates on the better criterion and its fitted parameters; if they tie, the minimizing set contains both models. Unlike the direct normal Gibbs argument, this multimodel exploration claim needs an appropriate cooling/mixing justification and is not guaranteed by an arbitrary short run.
Finally, an exact statistical comparison must put both likelihoods on the same observable and reference-measure convention. A Poisson distribution model is a probability mass function on counts, whereas the displayed normal distribution model is a continuous probability density function. The formulas above are the question's formal Gaussian working-likelihood comparison. A coherent exact normal approximation for count observations can instead use probabilities of rounding intervals; that changes its likelihood and generally loses the simple analytic normal estimates. This distinction matters when interpreting the numerical AIC difference, while leaving the dimension-matching and annealing construction applicable to properly specified likelihoods.
