For the amount of work Aj​(0,t] offered by one flow in a time interval of length t, its effective bandwidth at parameter θ>0 and timescale t>0 is
αj​(θ,t)=θt1​logEeθAj​(0,t].​
(29)
Its units are work per unit time. The parameter θ measures the price placed on bursts: a stringent rare-overflow target generally requires a larger value of θ than a loose target. The timescale t describes the duration of the congestion episode. When the limit exists, write
Λj​(θ)=limt→∞​t−1logEeθAj​(0,t],aj​(θ)=Λj​(θ)/θ.
(30)
This is the long-time effective bandwidth. It includes a burstiness cost, rather than just the mean offered rate. For a finite-time flow with the required moments, limθ↓0​αj​(θ,t)=EAj​(0,t]/t, and effective bandwidth is increasing in θ. If Aj​(0,t]/t is bounded, its limit as θ→∞ is the essential supremum of that interval's input rate, the peak-rate limit. The analogous long-time mean limit requires the usual interchange or regularity assumptions.
For independent flows their cumulant-generating functions add, so
αtotal​(θ,t)=∑j​αj​(θ,t),atotal​(θ)=∑j​aj​(θ).
(31)
The Chernoff bound therefore gives, for any θ>0 in the common domain,
P(Atotal​(0,t]>Ct+b)≤exp{−θ[b+Ct−tαtotal​(θ,t)]}.
(32)
Optimizing over θ produces the relevant Legendre-Fenchel transform. The Cramér theorem states that sample means of independent and identically distributed inputs whose moment-generating function is finite near zero have a large deviation principle, with rate function equal to the Legendre-Fenchel transform of their cumulant-generating function. More generally, the Gärtner–Ellis theorem applies to a convergent scaled log moment-generating function when zero lies inside its domain and the limit is lower semicontinuous and essentially smooth. It gives the same transform as a good rate function. A sample-path version, together with the contraction principle for large deviations for the queue workload, translates input deviations into overflow deviations. The compact-time and infinite-horizon continuity/tail hypotheses must be checked for the chosen traffic model.
A particularly useful rigorous queue bound holds for inputs with stationary increments and independent increments, such that EeθA(t)=etΛ(θ). If Λ(θ)≤Cθ, then
Mt​=eθ(A(t)−Ct)
(33)
is a nonnegative supermartingale, since its conditional increment factor is e(t−s)(Λ(θ)−Cθ)≤1. The maximal inequality for a nonnegative supermartingale on [0,T], followed by T→∞, gives the continuous-time exponential workload bound
P(W≥b)≤e−θb,W=dt≥0sup​(A(t)−Ct),j∑​aj​(θ)≤C.​
(34)
Here W is the stationary queue workload of the stable infinite-buffer queue, represented by input from the past. This directly bounds the supremum over time; applying a one-time Chernoff bound alone would not establish it.
For a nonzero Compound Poisson process with bounded positive packet sizes and mean input rate less than C, a positive root θ∗​ of Λ(θ)=Cθ exists and is unique. Here the root theorem can be made explicit:
e−θ∗​(b+smax​)≤P(W≥b)≤e−θ∗​b(b>0).
(35)
The upper bound was just proved. For the lower bound, use exponential tilting with density eθ∗​(A(t)−Ct) at time t. Under the tilted law the net input has positive mean drift Λ′(θ∗​)−C>0, by strict convexity and the positive-root equation. The strong law of large numbers then makes the first crossing time τb​ finite almost surely under that law. Its overshoot is at most smax​. Changing measure at τb​, first on {τb​≤T} and then letting T→∞, gives
P(τb​<∞)=E∗​[e−θ∗​(A(τb​)−Cτb​)]≥e−θ∗​(b+smax​).
(36)

Thus the familiar admission rule compares ∑j​aj​(θ) with C, choosing θ from the desired buffer-tail exponent. For more general inputs, a logarithmic root theorem requires its own light-tail and renewal or sample-path hypotheses; the displayed supermartingale upper bound needs only the moment and increment assumptions stated above.
