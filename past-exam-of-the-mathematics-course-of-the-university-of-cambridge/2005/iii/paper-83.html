The Hamiltonian functional is
H[ψ,ψ∗]=∫[2mℏ2​∣∇ψ∣2+Vext​∣ψ∣2+2U​∣ψ∣4]d3x.
(1)
Treating the field and its complex conjugate as independent variables, and integrating the gradient term by parts with periodic, decaying or fixed-field boundary conditions, gives
δψ∗δH​=−2mℏ2​∇2ψ+Vext​ψ+U∣ψ∣2ψ.
(2)
Thus iℏψt​=δH/δψ∗ is the Gross–Pitaevskii equation. The factor 1/2 in the interaction energy avoids counting each pair twice.
At prescribed particle number, minimize H subject to N=∫∣ψ∣2d3x. Introducing the chemical potential as a Lagrange multiplier, the variation of H−μN vanishes at a ground-state profile ψ0​, proving
[−2mℏ2​∇2+Vext​+U∣ψ0​∣2]ψ0​=μψ0​.​
(3)
The corresponding time-dependent field is ψ(x,t)=e−iμt/ℏψ0​(x). A solution of this stationary equation is only a candidate ground state; the ground state is the minimizer among all fields satisfying the normalization. For the uniform repulsive gas in a periodic volume, the gradient energy is nonnegative and ∫n2d3x≥N2/V, so the constant-density, constant-phase state is indeed the minimum. The uniform-background solutions below assume U>0 and a positive background density.

Let the uniform background number density be n∞​>0 and set μ=Un∞​. The subtraction of this chemical potential requires a phase rotation as well as a rescaling:
ψphys​(x,t)=n∞​​e−iμt/ℏΨ(x,t),x=ℓ0​x​,t=t0​t​.
(4)
Substitution into the physical Gross–Pitaevskii equation gives
iμt0​ℏ​Ψt​=−2mμℓ02​ℏ2​∇2Ψ+(∣Ψ∣2−1)Ψ.
(5)
Consequently the requested coefficients follow from
ℓ0​=2mμ​ℏ​,t0​=2μℏ​,ψ unit=μ/U​.​
(6)
The length unit is the specified healing length. Removing tildes produces the paper's dimensionless equation; in particular the coefficient of its time derivative fixes t0​, not its reciprocal. Without the phase rotation the linear +Ψ term would not appear.
The dimensionless ground state is Ψ=eiχ for any constant phase χ, conventionally Ψ=1. Its subtracted energy is proportional to ∫[∣∇Ψ∣2+(1−∣Ψ∣2)2/2]d3x, which is nonnegative and vanishes for this state. In physical variables it has density n∞​ and phase rotating at μ/ℏ.

Use translating coordinates Z=z−vt and a profile ψ(x,t)=Φ(x,y,Z); the background condensate remains at rest in the laboratory. Then ψt​=−vΦZ​, and the Gross–Pitaevskii solitary wave equation is
2ivΦZ​=∇2Φ+(1−∣Φ∣2)Φ,Φ→1 far from the wave.​
(7)
This is a coordinate translation of a traveling solution, rather than an additional phase boost to change the background flow.
Set Φ=1+f+ig with small real f,g. Since ∣Φ∣2=1+2f+O(f2+g2), the real and imaginary linearized equations are
∇2f−2f+2vgZ​=0,∇2g−2vfZ​=0.​
(8)
For a longitudinal sinusoid, choose f=Acos(kZ) and g=Bsin(kZ). Their coefficients obey
(k2+2)A=2vkB,k2B=2vkA.
(9)
A nonzero solution therefore requires 4v2=k2+2, giving the positive-direction phase velocity
v(k)=21​k2+2​.​
(10)
Equivalently the time-dependent linear Bogoliubov spectrum is ω2=k2(k2+2)/4 and v=ω/k. Its long-wavelength sound speed is 1/2​, which becomes μ/m​ in physical units using ℓ0​/t0​=2μ/m​. For a general wavevector k, the stationary resonance is 4v2kz2​=k2(k2+2); specifying only its magnitude does not determine the velocity unless its direction is also fixed. This sinusoidal dispersion describes linear radiation about the background, not an amplitude-independent dispersion law for every nonlinear solitary wave; localized subsonic waves do not require a real oscillatory far-field wavenumber.

For a straight unit-winding quantum vortex, the real radial amplitude satisfies
R′′+s1​R′−s2R​+(1−R2)R=0,R(0)=0,R(∞)=1.
(11)
In the frame translating with the ring, put Z=z−ut and Φ=eiθ(R+ϕ), where ϕ is independent of the angular coordinate. The cylindrical Laplacian has the exact identity
∇2[eiθ(R+ϕ)]=eiθ[s1​∂s​(s∂s​(R+ϕ))+∂Z2​ϕ−s2R+ϕ​].
(12)
After inserting this into the translating Gross–Pitaevskii equation, subtract the straight-vortex equation. Because R is real,
∣R+ϕ∣2=R2+R(ϕ+ϕ∗)+∣ϕ∣2,
(13)
and direct expansion of the nonlinear difference gives
(1−∣R+ϕ∣2)(R+ϕ)−(1−R2)R=(1−2R2)ϕ−R2ϕ∗−Rϕ2−2R∣ϕ∣2−∣ϕ∣2ϕ.
(14)
Thus the exact ring-on-line perturbation equation, including its quadratic and cubic terms, is
2iuϕZ​=s1​∂s​(sϕs​)+ϕZZ​−s2ϕ​+[1−2R2−R(ϕ+2ϕ∗)−∣ϕ∣2]ϕ−R2ϕ∗.​
(15)
The two appearances of R∣ϕ∣2 are responsible for the coefficient 2 multiplying ϕ∗ inside the bracket. No small-amplitude approximation is needed for this identity.

The single-particle Hamiltonian H0​=−ℏ2∂x2​/(2m)+V(x) has a periodic potential V(x+d)=V(x). Define the unitary spatial translation operator Td​f(x)=f(x+d). Its derivatives commute with translation, and the potential's periodicity gives H0​Td​=Td​H0​.
Each energy eigenspace is therefore preserved by Td​, so within it one may choose simultaneous energy and translation eigenstates. On a large periodic interval of M cells, TdM​=1 and its eigenvalues are phases λ=eikd, with k=2πj/(Md); the infinite-volume limit gives continuous quasimomentum modulo 2π/d. For a simultaneous state in band j, this proves
ψjk​(x+d)=eikdψjk​(x).​
(16)
Then ujk​(x)=e−ikxψjk​(x) obeys ujk​(x+d)=ujk​(x), which constructs the usual Bloch state rather than assuming its form. The label j distinguishes energy bands at the same crystal momentum ℏk. In a degenerate energy eigenspace an arbitrary superposition need not itself have one translation phase; the result refers to the simultaneous Bloch states.

Let ψ0​ be the normalized stationary profile. Multiplying its stationary Gross–Pitaevskii equation by ψ0∗​, integrating and integrating the kinetic term by parts yields
μ=N1​∫[2mℏ2​∣∇ψ0​∣2+Vext​∣ψ0​∣2+U∣ψ0​∣4]d3x.​
(17)
This is not simply H/N: the quartic contribution is twice its contribution to that energy per particle. For an infinite periodic system one uses one cell and the corresponding cell particle number, or equivalently average densities.
At fixed lattice spacing, mean density n, coupling and temperature, the commonly used condensate compressibility in an optical lattice is
κ−1=n(∂n∂μ​)s​.​
(18)
If compressibility means the thermodynamic volume response instead, the Gibbs-Duhem equation dp=ndμ at zero temperature gives κT​=[n2(∂μ/∂n)s​]−1=κ/n. Stating the convention removes this factor-of-density ambiguity; the lattice-depth derivative has the same sign at fixed n.
For the stable repulsive ground-band Bose-Einstein condensate, the intended trend is ∂κ/∂s<0 for increasing nonzero lattice depth, with mean density held fixed. Deeper wells localize the field, enhancing the repulsive interaction response. To quantify this, in the weak-interaction band approximation write ψ0​=n​fs​, with the cell average of ∣fs​∣2 equal to one. Perturbation in Un gives
μ=E0​(s)+UnI(s)+⋯,I(s)=d1​∫0d​∣fs​∣4dx,κ≃UnI(s)1​.
(19)
In deep wells the localized orbital is approximately a normalized Gaussian of width aho​=d/(πs1/4). Neglecting interwell overlap, I≃d/(2π​aho​)=π/2​s1/4, hence ∂κ/∂s≃−κ/(4s)<0. This explicitly establishes the sign in that controlled regime; the repulsive ground-band calculation describes the broader trend.
The sign statement needs the repulsive, stable ground-band interpretation, not merely the literal assumption U=0. It also need not be a strictly nonzero derivative at s=0: at weak interaction fs​=1+(s/8)cos(2πx/d)+O(s2), with normalization corrected at second order, gives I=1+s2/32+O(s3) and a zero initial slope. A leading density-dominated Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate with density positive everywhere gives μ=Un+sER​/2 and no depth dependence of this response at that approximation's order. These limits do not justify a strict universal sign for every unspecified interaction and parameter regime.

Use the stationary quantum vortex field
ψ(r,θ,t)=e−iμt/ℏR(r)eiNθ,
(20)
with no dependence on the coordinate along the vortex line. The polar Laplacian contributes R′′+R′/r−N2R/r2. Consequently
−2mℏ2​(R′′+rR′​−r2N2R​)+[sER​sin2(dπr​)+UR2]R=μR.​
(21)
A regular vortex with nonzero integer winding number has R∼Cr∣N∣ near the origin. The angular phase gives the superfluid velocity ℏN/(mr) outside the core. For s=0, the exterior amplitude responds to the periodically varying potential; it is not generally a constant bulk value. The radial potential here is not a Cartesian translation-invariant optical lattice, so the Cartesian Bloch theorem is not an exact theorem for its full radial equation.

Set r=ℓ0​r, R=μ/U​f(r), using the healing length ℓ0​=ℏ/2mμ​ and U,μ>0. For zero external potential, division by μR produces
f′′+r1​f′−r2N2​f+(1−f2)f=0,f(∞)=1.​
(22)
Primes now denote dimensionless-radius derivatives. Let f=1−a/r2+O(r−4). Then f′′+f′/r=O(r−4), while the centrifugal term is −N2/r2+O(r−4) and (1−f2)f=2a/r2+O(r−4). Their leading balance gives 2a=N2. Therefore the inverse-square tail of a Gross–Pitaevskii vortex yields
r→∞lim​r2[1−f(r)]=2N2​.​
(23)
For a singly quantized vortex the answer is 1/2. This is the amplitude deficit; the density deficit 1−f2 has limiting coefficient N2 instead.

A nonzero radial lattice prevents a unique universal curve without values of the depth, period, chemical potential, coupling and winding number. An explicit example of the requested profile is shown below for N=1, repulsive coupling, d=4ℓ0​ and sER​/μ=0.6; since ER​/μ=π2/16, this corresponds to s≃0.973.
The plotted dimensionless amplitude is f=R/μ/U​. The generator solves the nonlinear radial equation
f′′+rf′​−r2f​+[1−0.6sin2(4πr​)−f2]f=0
(24)
by finite differences and damped Newton iteration; r here is in healing length units. It imposes regularity f(0)=0 and a zero radial derivative at r=32, well outside the plotted interval 0≤r≤20. The exterior boundary is at a potential minimum, and its placement is checked by an extended-domain calculation.
The quantum vortex core still forces the amplitude to vanish at the origin. Farther out, amplitude minima occur near potential maxima, where repulsion and trapping reduce the local equilibrium density, and amplitude maxima occur near the low-potential annuli. Gradient energy smooths the modulation, so the profile does not exactly follow the local estimate fTF​=[1−V/μ]+​​. It approaches a periodically modulated exterior background rather than the homogeneous limit one; the dashed zero-lattice curve is included solely as a reference. At sufficiently deep lattice barriers the density troughs can become much more pronounced than in this example.

Take x′=x−ut, with the same time coordinate in both frames. The normal fluid transforms as vn′​(x′,t)=vn​(x,t)−u. The field transformation is the unit-modulus Schrödinger boost
ψ′(x′,t)=e−iu⋅x+i∣u∣2t/2ψ(x,t),A=−iu,B=−2i​∣u∣2.​
(25)
Writing the phase as F=−iu⋅x′−i∣u∣2t/2 makes differentiation at fixed primed position transparent:
∇′ψ′=eF(∇ψ−iuψ),∂t′​ψ′=eF(ψt​+u⋅∇ψ−2i​u2ψ).
(26)
It follows that both −i∂t′​ψ′ and ∇′2ψ′/2 gain the same terms eF[−iu⋅∇ψ−u2ψ/2]. The density is unchanged, ρ′=∣ψ′∣2=ρ, so the nonlinear local term has the same form.
The remaining material derivative transforms as
(∂t′​+vn′​⋅∇′)ρ′=[∂t​+u⋅∇+(vn​−u)⋅∇]ρ=(∂t​+vn​⋅∇)ρ.
(27)
Thus the density-damping term acquires only the common phase factor, proving invariance of the complete equation under a Galilean boost. Keeping the normal velocity unchanged in both frames would fail this check; the reference flow must transform along with the condensate.

Choose the amplitude U real and positive locally, absorbing its phase into Θ, and work away from vortex cores or other density zeros. Put ρ=U2 and θ=Θ/ϵ. Direct separation of the real and imaginary parts of the Gross–Pitaevskii equation gives the exact Madelung equations
ρt​+∇⋅(ρvs​)=0,vs​=∇θ,
(28)
θt​+21​∣vs​∣2=1−ρ+2U∇2U​−ζDn​ρ,Dn​=∂t​+vn​⋅∇.
(29)
For example, the imaginary part is −Ut​=∇U⋅∇θ+(U/2)∇2θ, which proves the density equation after multiplication by 2U. The real damping coefficient affects the phase balance rather than adding a particle source.
Using this continuity equation and ∇⋅vn​=0,
Dn​ρ=−∇⋅(ρvs​)+vn​⋅∇ρ=−∇⋅[ρ(vs​−vn​)].
(30)
Taking a gradient of the phase equation gives the exact velocity equation
∂t​vs​+21​∇∣vs​∣2=−∇(ρ−1)+21​∇(U∇2U​)+ζ∇∇⋅[ρ(vs​−vn​)].
(31)
Now ∇x​=ϵ∇X​, ∂t​=ϵ∂T​, and vs​=∇X​Θ is order one. The inertial and chemical-potential gradients are O(ϵ); the double-gradient bulk term is O(ϵ2); and the quantum potential force contains three slow derivatives and is O(ϵ3), provided U stays bounded away from zero. Through order ϵ2 this proves the requested bulk-viscous limit of a material-density-damped condensate with
ρs​=U2,vs​=∇X​Θ,μ=U2−1,ξ=ζ.​
(32)
Adding a spatially constant value to μ does not affect the equation, so μ=U2 is an equivalent convention. The density and viscosity coefficients are in the equation's dimensionless units. The bulk viscosity term is the gradient of the divergence of the relative mass flux, not a shear-viscosity term. At density zeros the slow-amplitude expansion and the division by U fail; there one must retain the original field equation.

A constant-amplitude plane wave has no density derivative and therefore no damping. The complete family is
ψ=Aexp[i(k⋅x−ωt+χ)],ω=2k2​+A2−1,A≥0,​
(33)
with arbitrary constant phase χ. The background in the question is the member A=1.
Use the exact amplitude–phase equations derived above with vn​=0. To first order in the perturbation, ρ=1+2ϵR and vs​=k+ϵ∇Θ, so
(∂t​+k⋅∇)R=−21​∇2Θ,
(34)
(∂t​+k⋅∇)Θ=21​∇2R−2R−2ζRt​.
(35)
These equations retain the quantum potential contribution because no long-wavelength truncation is assumed in this subpart. In particular damping contains Rt​, not the advective derivative along the moving condensate: its reference is the stationary normal fluid.
For the proposed periodic perturbation, write q=k⋅l and L=l2. The amplitude coefficients satisfy
(σ+iq2+L/2+2ζσ​−L/2σ+iq​)(R0​Θ0​​)=0.
(36)
A nonzero perturbation therefore requires the plane-wave dispersion of a material-density-damped condensate
(σ+iq)2+ζl2σ+l2+4l4​=0,​
(37)
or equivalently σ2+(2iq+ζl2)σ+l2+l4/4−q2=0. At zero background flow the roots are −ζl2/2±ζ2l4/4−l2−l4/4​, whose real parts are negative for l=0. As ζ→0, σ=−iq±il2+l4/4​ recovers the advected Bogoliubov spectrum with long-wavelength sound speed one in these units. This differs from question 1's time normalization, whose sound speed is 1/2​.

Since each species number Ni​=∫∣ψi​∣2d3x is separately conserved, minimize E−μ1​N1​−μ2​N2​ with two independent Lagrange multipliers. Variation with respect to each complex-conjugate field, with boundary terms removed, gives
[−2m1​ℏ2​∇2+V1​+U11​∣ψ1​∣2+U12​∣ψ2​∣2]ψ1​=μ1​ψ1​,​
(38)
[−2m2​ℏ2​∇2+V2​+U22​∣ψ2​∣2+U12​∣ψ1​∣2]ψ2​=μ2​ψ2​.​
(39)
These are the two stationary Gross–Pitaevskii equations; the time-dependent equilibrium fields are e−iμi​t/ℏψi​(x). The cross term contributes U12​∣ψj​∣2ψi​ to each equation, with no factor two, while differentiating the self-interaction term cancels its factor 1/2. Separate number conservation does not require the two chemical potentials to be equal.

For an occupied uniform two-component Bose-Einstein condensate, the gradient and trapping terms vanish. Dividing each stationary equation by its nonzero field gives
μ1​=U11​n1​+U12​n2​,μ2​=U12​n1​+U22​n2​.​
(40)
The same relations follow by differentiating the uniform interaction-energy density e=U11​n12​/2+U22​n22​/2+U12​n1​n2​ with respect to each number density. When D=U11​U22​−U122​=0, the inverse relations are
n1​=DU22​μ1​−U12​μ2​​,n2​=DU11​μ2​−U12​μ1​​.
(41)
These describe a genuinely overlapping mixture only when both densities are nonnegative. An unoccupied component cannot be divided out of its stationary field equation; it requires the appropriate constrained equilibrium condition instead.

At fixed particle numbers, the perturbations satisfy ∫δni​d3x=0. Expanding the uniform interaction energy, its linear term is ∫(μ1​δn1​+μ2​δn2​)d3x=0, and its exact quadratic change in this contact-interaction approximation is
δE=21​∫[U11​(δn1​)2+2U12​δn1​δn2​+U22​(δn2​)2]d3x.
(42)
Completing the square gives
δE=21​∫[U11​(δn1​+U11​U12​​δn2​)2+(U22​−U11​U122​​)(δn2​)2]d3x.
(43)
Hence the miscibility criterion for a two-component condensate is
U11​>0,U22​>0,U122​<U11​U22​.​
(44)
These are precisely strict positive definiteness of the interaction matrix, and make every nonzero admissible long-wavelength density perturbation cost energy. With both equilibrium densities positive, a negative eigenvalue would instead allow a small sinusoidal perturbation along its eigenvector, with zero mean and negative energy change. The omitted positive gradient term cannot cure arbitrarily long wavelengths in an unbounded system.
If cross-repulsion exceeds the geometric mean of the self-repulsions, the unstable direction favors separation of species; sufficiently strong cross-attraction favors a joint density increase and collapse. Equality gives a zero-cost direction at this order, so it is marginal rather than strictly stable. Constraints near an absent component or finite-system wavelength restrictions require a separate boundary analysis.

Set Ai​=mi​ωi2​/2, so Vi​=Ai​r2, and D=U11​U22​−U122​. In the overlap region, the Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate neglects the gradient terms and requires
(U11​U12​​U12​U22​​)(n1​n2​​)=(μ1​−A1​r2μ2​−A2​r2​).
(45)
Inverting this matrix proves the unambiguous profiles
n1​=DU22​μ1​−U12​μ2​−(U22​A1​−U12​A2​)r2​,​
(46)
n2​=DU11​μ2​−U12​μ1​−(U11​A2​−U12​A1​)r2​.​
(47)
Using Ri2​=μi​/Ai​ and introducing
λ=A1​A2​​=m1​ω12​m2​ω22​​,​
(48)
these Thomas–Fermi profiles for unequal condensate trap curvatures become
n1​=1−U122​/(U11​U22​)μ1​/U11​​[1−U22​μ1​U12​μ2​​−R12​r2​(1−λU22​U12​​)],
(49)
n2​=1−U122​/(U11​U22​)μ2​/U22​​[1−U11​μ2​U12​μ1​​−R22​r2​(1−λ1​U11​U12​​)].
(50)
There is a genuine printed error: the PDF puts λ rather than 1/λ in the second bracket as well. In the first bracket its coefficient requires λ=A2​/A1​, while in the second it requires λ=A1​/A2​; for nonzero cross-coupling these cannot both hold unless the curvatures are equal. The TeX additionally drops both occurrences of λ. The matrix-inverse formulas above give the corrected general answer directly.
For example, U11​=U22​=1, U12​=1/2, μ1​=μ2​=2, A1​=1, A2​=2 give the physical overlap profiles n1​=4/3, n2​=4/3−2r2. Using λ=2 in both printed brackets would instead give n2​=4/3, which fails the second equilibrium equation at every r=0 in the overlap region.
Only the region where both computed densities are positive belongs to this overlap solution. Beyond its edge, a remaining species has ni​=(μi​−Ai​r2)/Uii​ where positive, and both vanish outside the support; simply retaining negative values from the coupled inverse is unphysical. The actual chemical potentials are determined by the two normalization integrals. If the reference uncoupled radii are defined at the coupled solution's chemical potentials, the identities Ri2​=μi​/Ai​ are algebraic scales, not the actual interacting-cloud edges; holding particle numbers fixed and removing cross-coupling generally changes those chemical potentials. The approximation also fails in the healing regions at the edges.
