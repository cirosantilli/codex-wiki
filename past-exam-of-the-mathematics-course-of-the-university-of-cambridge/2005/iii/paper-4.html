Take B to be linear in its first argument and conjugate-linear in its second, with aˉ=aq. For a Hermitian form over a quadratic finite field, B(v,v)∈Fq​. We first establish the quadratic finite-field norm and trace surjectivity needed for orthonormalization of a finite-field Hermitian form. The field norm N(a)=aq+1 maps Fq2∗​ onto Fq∗​ because the multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic of order q2−1. The field trace Tr(a)=a+aq is an Fq​-linear map into Fq​. It is nonzero: a nonzero polynomial of degree q cannot vanish at all q2 elements. It is therefore onto, including in characteristic two.
There must be v with B(v,v)=0. Otherwise, for any v,w,a, expanding the diagonal value gives
0=B(v+aw,v+aw)=Tr(aˉB(v,w)).
(1)
If B(v,w)=0, varying a contradicts the surjectivity of the field trace. Thus all pairings would vanish, contrary to nondegeneracy. Choose a with N(a)=B(v,v)−1; then v1​=av has norm one. Every w decomposes uniquely as
w=B(w,v1​)v1​+(w−B(w,v1​)v1​),V=Fq2​v1​⊕v1⊥​.
(2)
The restricted Hermitian form over a quadratic finite field on the orthogonal complement for a sesquilinear form is nondegenerate: a vector there orthogonal to that complement is also orthogonal to v1​, hence to all of V, and is zero. Induction produces an orthonormal basis v1​,…,vn​.
The unitary group over a finite field is the group of invertible Fq2​-linear maps preserving B. In the orthonormal basis just constructed, its concise description is
Un​(q2)={g∈GLn​(Fq2​):gˉ​Tg=In​}.​
(3)
The notation here uses the size of the matrix field; it is also frequently written Un​(q). There is no determinant-one condition in this definition.

We construct a Hermitian hyperbolic plane over a finite field inside each successive two-dimensional piece. By the field norm surjectivity proved above, choose a∈Fq2​ with aaˉ=−1. From two vectors in an orthonormal basis, form e=v1​+av2​. Then B(e,e)=1+aaˉ=0, so e is a nonzero isotropic vector. Nondegeneracy supplies g with B(e,g)=1; rescaling the second argument achieves this normalization. In fact g=v1​ already works in this two-dimensional piece. By quadratic finite-field norm and trace surjectivity, choose c with c+cˉ=B(g,g) and put f=g−ce. Direct expansion gives
B(e,f)=1,B(f,f)=B(g,g)−c−cˉ=0.
(4)
Thus e,f are linearly independent, and their Gram matrix is (01​10​), which is invertible even in characteristic two. Their span is nondegenerate, so its orthogonal complement for a sesquilinear form is again nondegenerate. Repeat the construction there. In even dimension this exhausts V; in odd dimension the final one-dimensional complement has a nonzero diagonal value, which can be normalized to one using the field norm. Calling its vector d, we obtain
B(ei​,fj​)=δij​,B(ei​,ej​)=B(fi​,fj​)=0,B(d,d)=1,B(d,ei​)=B(d,fi​)=0,​
(5)
with the d terms present only in odd dimension. This proves all the requested pairings and the basis assertion.
The printed hint needs a qualification. If X2−X−1 is irreducible over Fq​, its two roots are conjugate, so ζ+ζˉ​=1 and ζζˉ​=−1; the hinted vectors then do work. They need not work when it splits. For example, over F11​ its roots are 4,8, fixed by conjugation in F121​. For either choice the proposed e has norm 1+ζ2, respectively 6 or 10, rather than zero. The norm-and-trace construction above proves the assertion without that restriction.

Use the course convention that a root system is finite and is a reduced root system satisfying the crystallographic root system condition. These hypotheses are essential for the angle restriction. For roots r,s, the two Cartan integers
a=(s,s)2(r,s)​,b=(r,r)2(r,s)​
(6)
satisfy ab=4cos2θ. If r,s are not parallel, the strict Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and integrality give ab∈{0,1,2,3}. These values give angles π/2, π/3 or 2π/3, π/4 or 3π/4, and π/6 or 5π/6, respectively. If the roots are parallel, reducedness implies s=±r, giving 0 or π. Therefore
θ∈{0,π/6,π/4,π/3,π/2,2π/3,3π/4,5π/6,π}.​
(7)
Here are examples accounting for every angle. In the A1 root system, the pairs (r,r) and (r,−r) give 0,π. Two roots from different summands of A1​⊕A1​ give π/2. The six unit vectors at angles kπ/3 form an A2 root system: taking one root at angle zero and another at π/3 or 2π/3 gives those two angles. In the B2 root system {±e1​,±e2​,±e1​±e2​}, pair e1​ with e1​+e2​ or −e1​+e2​ to obtain π/4,3π/4. In a G2 root system, take short unit roots at angles kπ/3 and long roots of length 3​ at angles π/6+kπ/3. A short root at zero paired with long roots at π/6,5π/6 gives the remaining angles.

Choose a fundamental system of a root system {α,β}. Distinct simple roots have nonpositive inner product. Their Cartan matrix has diagonal entries two and off-diagonal entries −p,−q, where either p=q=0, or p,q are positive integers. The root-system finiteness lemma gives pq<4. After exchanging the simple roots, the possibilities are
(20​02​),(2−1​−12​),(2−2​−12​),(2−3​−12​).
(10)
Their angles and relative lengths determine the two simple roots up to an orthogonal transformation and common scaling, except that the two orthogonal components can be scaled separately.
To see that no extra roots remain unspecified, write a positive root γ=mα+nβ with nonnegative integer coefficients. If it is not simple, some simple root δ has (γ,δ)>0: otherwise ∥γ∥2=m(γ,α)+n(γ,β)≤0. The root reflection in δ subtracts a positive integer multiple of δ. The other coordinate stays nonnegative; since γ is not proportional to δ, it is positive, so the reflected root must still be positive by the defining sign property of a fundamental system of a root system. Its height decreases. Repeating reduces γ to a simple root. Thus every root is in a Weyl group orbit of a simple root.
For the four matrices, applying their simple reflections gives, respectively, the following positive roots:
A1​⊕A1​A2​B2​G2​​α,βα,β,α+βα,β,α+β,α+2βα,β,α+β,α+2β,α+3β,2α+3β​
(11)
where in the last two rows α is long, β short. For example, sβ​(α)=α+qβ and sα​(β)=β+α for the displayed matrices when the convention is aij​=2(αi​,αj​)/(αi​,αi​). The sets comprising these positive roots and their negatives are invariant under both reflections, as direct substitution verifies. The height argument therefore shows they are the whole root system. This proves the classification of rank-two root systems:
A1​⊕A1​,A2​,B2​,G2​.​
(12)

A Dynkin diagram has one vertex for each simple root. Vertices i,j are joined by aij​aji​ bonds, where aij​ are the off-diagonal Cartan integers. No bond means orthogonality; one, two and three bonds mean angles 2π/3,3π/4,5π/6. A multiple-bond arrow points to the shorter root. Thus the Dynkin diagram determines the Cartan matrix, including the relative lengths inside each connected component.
An irreducible root system corresponds to a connected Dynkin diagram. The classification of finite crystallographic Dynkin diagrams gives
Al​ (l≥1), Bl​ (l≥2), Cl​ (l≥3), Dl​ (l≥4), E6​,E7​,E8​,F4​,G2​.​
(13)
Here C2​ is already represented by B2​, and B1​,C1​ by A1​. General systems are orthogonal direct sums of these, with separate component scales. The following diagrams specify all the types; dots denote a continued chain, not additional vertices.
For orientation, the An Dynkin diagram is a single chain, the Bn Dynkin diagram has a terminal double bond pointing to its terminal short root, and the Cn Dynkin diagram reverses that arrow. The Dn Dynkin diagram has arm lengths 1,1,l−3. The En Dynkin diagram has arm lengths 1,2,2; 1,2,3; or 1,2,4. The F4 Dynkin diagram has a central double bond and the G2 Dynkin diagram a triple bond.
The positivity behind this finite list is the positive-definite Gram matrix of the simple roots. In particular the underlying graph is a tree: if it contained a cycle, the sum of its normalized roots would have squared norm at most zero, since each bonded inner product is at most −1/2. This contradicts linear independence. Restricting the possible bonds and branches by positive definiteness gives precisely the listed chains and trees. Conversely their Cartan matrices are positive definite after symmetrization and their reflection-generated root sets give the indicated finite root systems.

Here A1​(K) denotes the adjoint Chevalley group of type A1; the isogeny form matters, since the simply connected form is SL2​(K) itself. Over C, take the sl2 Lie algebra with Chevalley basis
e=(00​10​),f=(01​00​),h=(10​0−1​).
(16)
Their Lie brackets satisfy [h,e]=2e, [h,f]=−2f, [e,f]=h. Conjugation defines a group homomorphism ρ:SL2​(C)→Aut(sl2​(C)). Since e2=f2=0, u(t)=exp(te) and l(s)=exp(sf). Differentiating conjugation, or expanding its finite polynomial on this basis, gives
ρ(u(t))=exp(tade),ρ(l(s))=exp(sadf).
(17)
The adjoint Chevalley group is generated by these two families. Part (i) therefore proves that it is exactly the image of ρ. A matrix commuting with e has the form (a0​ba​); also commuting with f forces b=0. Thus the kernel of a group homomorphism kerρ consists of scalar matrices of determinant one, namely {I,−I}. The first isomorphism theorem now gives A1​(C)≅SL2​(C)/{±I}=PSL2​(C).
For a general field, first compute the Chevalley basis root operators as integral polynomials, and only then reduce their coefficients to K. Explicitly,
x+​(t)ex−​(s)f​=e,=f,​x+​(t)hx−​(s)h​=h−2te,=h+2sf,​x+​(t)fx−​(s)e​=f+th−t2e,=e−sh−s2f.​
(18)
They are still precisely conjugation by u(t),l(s) on the three-dimensional trace-zero matrix space. These formulas remain valid in characteristic two, where h=I but e,h,f remain linearly independent. Part (i) still identifies the generated image with the conjugation image of SL2​(K). The same computation with e,f gives kernel of a group homomorphism {aI:a2=1}, exactly its scalar center. Consequently
A1​(K)≅SL2​(K)/Z(SL2​(K))=PSL2​(K).​
(19)
In characteristic two the scalar center is trivial. The integral formulas avoid the invalid operation of dividing by factorials inside a field of small positive characteristic.
