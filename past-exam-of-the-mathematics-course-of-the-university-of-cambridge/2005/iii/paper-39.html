Equip S with a sigma-algebra S. A Poisson random measure with intensity measure of a point process μ is a random counting measure Π such that, for every measurable A with μ(A)<∞,
P(Π(A)=k)=e−μ(A)k!μ(A)k​,k=0,1,…,
(1)
and the counts on every finite collection of pairwise disjoint measurable sets are independent. Thus EΠ(A)=μ(A). For a sigma-finite measure μ, sets of infinite intensity contain infinitely many points almost surely: exhaust such a set by increasing finite-intensity sets and use the Poisson count tails as their means tend to infinity. The count is a measurable random variable for every measurable A, which is part of saying that Π is a random measure.
Under the usual point representation write Π=∑j​δZj​​, counting multiplicities. If μ is a non-atomic measure, this is a simple Poisson point process; if μ has atoms, several point occurrences can have the same location, and each occurrence is counted separately. In a topological setting, local finiteness requires that the intensity be finite on compact sets.
Sufficient existence conditions are that S is a standard Borel space and μ is a sigma-finite measure. This gives existence of a Poisson random measure on a standard Borel space. If a simple locally finite configuration is desired, additionally take a non-atomic, locally finite intensity on a locally compact second-countable Hausdorff space. These conditions are sufficient, not claimed necessary. In particular sigma-finiteness suffices for a countable family of occurrences that can each be independently coloured.
Let R and G be the red and green counting measures. The marking assumption means that, conditional on Π, the colours of all point occurrences are independent, with the same red probability r. For a measurable set A of finite intensity m=μ(A), condition on its total count. To obtain a red and b green points the total must be a+b, so direct multiplication gives the joint red and green Poisson count formula
P(R(A)=a,G(A)=b)​=e−m(a+b)!ma+b​(aa+b​)ra(1−r)b=[e−rma!(rm)a​][e−(1−r)mb!((1−r)m)b​].​
(2)
Thus the two counts are independent Poisson variables with respective means rm and (1−r)m. This computation includes zero intensity with the usual convention for the probability of zero counts.
For disjoint finite-intensity sets A1​,…,Ak​, the original counts are independent. Conditional marking in different sets uses disjoint collections of independent colours. Repeating the preceding calculation therefore gives
P(R(Ai​)=ai​,G(Ai​)=bi​ for all i)=∏i=1k​[e−rμ(Ai​)ai​!(rμ(Ai​))ai​​]∏i=1k​[e−(1−r)μ(Ai​)bi​!((1−r)μ(Ai​))bi​​].
(3)
This proves the disjoint-count definition for each coloured Poisson point process and independence of their two count vectors.
To obtain independence of the whole processes, take any finite list of red test sets and any finite list of green test sets, initially all of finite intensity. Refine their union into the disjoint cells given by their membership patterns. Every test-set count is the sum of the counts of its cells. The entire red cell vector is independent of the entire green cell vector by the product formula, hence so are the corresponding test-set count vectors. For arbitrary measurable test sets, intersect first with a finite-intensity exhaustion of S and pass to the increasing count limits. The count cylinder events generate the two evaluation sigma-algebras, and independence extended from generating pi-systems gives independence of those sigma-algebras. Consequently
R and G are independent Poisson point processes, with intensities rμ and (1−r)μ.​
(4)
This is a direct count proof of the Poisson thinning theorem; no general marking or thinning result has been used to deduce it.

The first limit in the PDF has a reciprocal error when λ denotes point density. The correct strong law for Poisson arrival times is
Yn​/n⟶1/λalmost surely,λ>0.​
(5)
To prove it, put N(t)=Π((0,t]). The unit-interval increments N(j)−N(j−1) are independent and identically distributed random variables with the Poisson distribution of mean λ. The strong law of large numbers gives N(m)/m→λ along integers. For m≤t<m+1,
m+1N(m)​≤tN(t)​≤mN(m+1)​,
(6)
so N(t)/t→λ for all real t→∞. The continuous intensity gives distinct points almost surely, and local finiteness and infinitely many points give Yn​→∞. Since N(Yn​)=n, the same pathwise limit along Yn​ implies n/Yn​→λ, proving the reciprocal limit. For instance at density 2 the limit is 1/2, not 2. The printed limit would instead describe a parameter interpreted as mean interarrival time, but not the stated density.
For the Non-homogeneous Poisson point process on (0,1), every closed subinterval strictly inside (0,1) has finite intensity. The intensity is non-atomic, so the process is simple and has no point at 1/2 almost surely. Both half-intervals have infinite intensity: the density behaves as x−2 at zero and as (1−x)−1 at one. More explicitly, exhaust either half by finite-intensity intervals of means mj​→∞. For any fixed integer K,
P(Π(half-interval)≤K)≤e−mj​∑i=0K​i!mji​​⟶0.
(7)
Thus there are infinitely many points on each side.
Local finiteness around 1/2 gives a last point on its left and a first point on its right. To see the former directly, choose any left point; between it and 1/2 there are only finitely many points, so there is a largest one. Repeat below each chosen point to label the left sequence by X−1​,X−2​,…, and similarly label the right sequence by X0​,X1​,…. No infinite sequence of points can accumulate in the interior, because a compact neighbourhood there has finite count. Consequently
Xn​→0 as n→−∞,Xn​→1 as n→+∞.​
(8)
This constructs the required two-sided order statistics of a boundary-singular Poisson process.
Use its integrated intensity to find the rates. Partial fractions give
x2(1−x)1​=x21​+x1​+1−x1​.
(9)
The left cumulative-intensity coordinate is
L(x)=∫x1/2​u2(1−u)du​=x1​−2−log1−xx​,0<x<1/2.
(10)
It maps the left half bijectively onto (0,∞), reversing its order. The image points form a unit-rate Poisson process directly from the count definition: inverse images of disjoint parameter intervals are disjoint, and their intensity equals their parameter lengths. In particular L(X−k​) is its kth arrival, so the result just proved gives L(X−k​)/k→1. Also xL(x)→1 as x↓0, because xlogx→0. Hence
kX−k​=L(X−k​)k​X−k​L(X−k​)⟶1.​
(11)
This is exactly the requested negative-index limit.
For the right side, the increasing coordinate is
F(x)=∫1/2x​u2(1−u)du​=2−x1​+log1−xx​,1/2<x<1.
(12)
Its image is another unit-rate Poisson process; F(Xn​) is the (n+1)st arrival because the first right point has index zero. Thus F(Xn​)/(n+1)→1. As x↑1,
F(x)=1−log(1−x)+o(1).
(13)
Combining these facts gives the positive-index asymptotics
nlog(1−Xn​)​⟶−1,(1−Xn​)1/n⟶e−1almost surely.​
(14)
Equivalently 1−Xn​=exp(−n+o(n)) almost surely. In particular np(1−Xn​)→0 for every fixed p>0, unlike the inverse-linear scale at the left endpoint.
There is also an exact distributional description, if desired. For x∈(1/2,1), Xn​>x means that at most n right-hand points precede x, so
P(Xn​>x)=e−F(x)∑j=0n​j!F(x)j​.
(15)
Thus F(Xn​) has the gamma distribution with shape n+1 and rate one. This makes the indexing shift explicit and describes the fluctuations left unspecified by the logarithmic limit.

Represent the raindrops by a Poisson random measure on the space of location, impact time and volume. Physical volume is nonnegative, so take the intensity density to vanish for v<0, or equivalently work on R2×R×[0,∞) and extend it by zero to R4. Assume the radius function is measurable and nonnegative for elapsed times at least zero. Its stated monotonicity means patches grow rather than dry away.
A drop contributes to wetting ξ at time τ precisely if its impact time is no later than τ and its current patch contains ξ. Thus define the deterministic measurable set
Aξ,τ​={(x,t,v):t≤τ, v≥0, ∣x−ξ∣≤r(τ−t,v)}.
(16)
The number of covering drops is Π(Aξ,τ​). Its intensity measure of a point process is
mξ,τ​=∫−∞τ​∫0∞​∫∣x−ξ∣≤r(τ−t,v)​λ(x,t,v)dxdvdt.
(17)
Since the point is dry if and only if that count is zero, the Poisson count definition gives the void probability for growing Poisson rain patches
P(ξ is dry at time τ)=exp(−mξ,τ​).​
(18)
Here e−∞=0: when the intensity is infinite, exhaust the covering set by increasing finite-intensity sets; their decreasing zero-count events have probabilities tending to zero. This also covers storms with infinitely many drops that can reach the point. The formula is valid for spatially inhomogeneous intensity. Only if the intensity is spatially constant may the spatial integral be replaced by πr(τ−t,v)2 times that intensity. Whether the circular boundary is included makes no difference for a density with respect to Lebesgue measure, since each such boundary has zero spatial area. This construction is a time-dependent Poisson germ-grain model.
For total rainfall, the required general result is the Campbell first-moment formula: if Π is a Poisson random measure with intensity μ and h is any nonnegative measurable function, then
E[∫h(z)Π(dz)]=∫h(z)μ(dz),
(19)
with infinite values permitted. For a signed h, it holds with finite absolute expectation whenever ∫∣h∣dμ<∞. No square-integrability hypothesis is required for this first-moment identity. The result is stated rather than proved, as allowed here.
The total rainfall volume is Vtotal​=∑(X,T,V)∈Π​V=∫vΠ(dxdtdv), counting every drop once, independently of overlaps between its patch and other patches. Apply the stated formula with h(x,t,v)=v on the physical mark space. Extending by zero to negative volume gives
E[Vtotal​]=∫R4​vλ(x,t,v)dxdtdv.​
(20)
If the displayed nonnegative integral is finite, the total volume is integrable and finite almost surely. If it is infinite, its expectation is infinite; this alone does not imply that the random total is infinite almost surely. The radius affects wet-area coverage but does not change this total-volume expectation.
