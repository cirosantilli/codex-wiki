Use Gaussian units, uniform number density n, and ion mass density ρ=nmi​. Here e>0 is the magnitude of the elementary charge, as fixed by the printed current relation. Neglect Electron inertia and displacement current. Then
j=4πc​∇×B,ue​=u−4πenc​∇×B.
(1)
Freezing the magnetic flux into this Electron velocity, while retaining the ion momentum equation, gives the closed Hall magnetohydrodynamics system
∂t​B=∇×[(u−4πenc​∇×B)×B],​
(2)
∂t​u+(u⋅∇)u=−ρ∇p​+4πρ(∇×B)×B​,∇⋅u=∇⋅B=0.​
(3)
The pressure enforces the incompressible flow constraint; taking the divergence of momentum determines it up to the usual boundary-dependent constant. The induction equation preserves the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint. One can absorb B2/(8π) into magnetohydrodynamic total pressure, replacing the Lorentz term by magnetic tension. Keeping ion inertia here is important: electron magnetohydrodynamics with fixed ions will be a later limit, not the complete two-field system.

Linearize about zero bulk flow and the uniform guide field. Put b=δB/4πρ​, and take plane waves proportional to ei(k⋅x−ωt). Let k∥​=k⋅z^, k=∣k∣, q=k∥​vA​, and
di​=ωpi​c​,vA​di​=4πencB0​​.
(4)
Both u and b are perpendicular to k. Projecting momentum onto that plane eliminates total pressure. The remaining magnetic-tension term gives
−iωu=iqb,ωu=−qb.
(5)
In the induction equation, the Hall contribution is −c(B0​∂z​)(∇×δB)/(4πen); its two derivative factors of i in the Fourier mode produce a positive coefficient multiplying k×b. Thus
−iωb=iqu+qdi​k×b,ωb=−qu+iqdi​k×b.
(6)
Eliminating u gives
(ω2−q2)b=iωqdi​k×b.
(7)
On the transverse plane, (ik×)2=k2, so applying that operator again proves the incompressible Hall-MHD wave dispersion:
(ω2−k∥2​vA2​)2=ω2k∥2​vA2​k2ωpi2​c2​.​
(8)
Equivalently, in a helicity decomposition of a transverse Fourier mode with ik×bh​=hkbh​, h=±1, each polarization obeys ω2−hqkdi​ω−q2=0. For k∥​=0 the projected linear restoring terms vanish and these modes have zero frequency; the propagating calculation assumes k∥​=0.

Solving the polarization quadratic gives all four signed roots:
ωh,s​=2q​(hkdi​+s4+k2di2​​),h,s=±1.​
(9)
A sign-independent description is the pair of positive frequency magnitudes
Ωfast,slow​=∣k∥​∣vA​[1+4k2di2​​​ ± 2kdi​​].​
(10)
For wavelengths long compared with the ion skin depth, kdi​≪1, both magnitudes approach ∣k∥​∣vA​. The Hall correction is then small and the usual Alfvén wave dispersion ω2=k∥2​vA2​ is recovered. In an incompressible system the two transverse polarizations include the shear and pseudo-Alfvén wave sectors; away from this limit Hall coupling selects circular helicity combinations. A small parallel wavenumber alone does not remove the relative Hall splitting: the expansion parameter is the full kdi​.

When kdi​≫1, expand the square root in the exact frequencies. The fast branch has
Ωfast​=∣q∣(kdi​+kdi​1​+O((kdi​)−3)),
(11)
while the slow branch has Ωslow​=∣q∣[(kdi​)−1+O((kdi​)−3)]. Hence
ωfast2​≃k∥2​vA2​k2di2​,ωslow2​≃k2di2​k∥2​vA2​​.​
(12)
The fast dispersive wave is the mode sought in the question's subsequent fast-time limit. In a strongly oblique wave, k≃k⊥​, so its frequency scales as ∣ω∣≃∣k∥​∣vA​k⊥​di​. The slow branch instead tends to (∣k∥​∣/k)Ωi​, where the ion cyclotron frequency is Ωi​=vA​/di​=eB0​/(mi​c).
There is a terminology qualification: in the pressure-free incompressible Hall equations used here, the fast branch is conventionally a whistler wave. The question calls the requested small-scale waves kinetic Alfvén waves, but a general kinetic-Alfvén dispersion includes pressure/kinetic ordering absent from these equations. The boxed dispersion is the result for the printed model; no extra pressure response or kinetic term has been silently added.

Write ϵ=l/di​≪1 and δ=vA​τ/l≪1. For magnetic amplitudes of order B0​, ion acceleration is of order vA2​/l. Thus its change over the fast time is
Δu∼lvA2​τ​=δvA​≪vA​.
(13)
The ions are consequently fixed to leading order on this timescale. Their first nonzero response is driven by the momentum equation, with negligible self-advection for an initially resting ion flow; one need not assert that the dimensional Lorentz acceleration literally vanishes. In fast-time, Alfvén-velocity dimensionless variables, the ion equation reads ∂t^​u^=O(δ), giving ∂t^​u^=0 at leading order.
Meanwhile the electron-current velocity is j/(en)∼vA​di​/l=vA​/ϵ, larger than a bulk flow of order vA​. The ion-advection term in induction is smaller than Hall induction by O(ϵ) even if that slow flow is retained. The leading magnetic equation is therefore
∂t​B=−4πenc​∇×[(∇×B)×B],∇⋅B=0,​
(14)
with stationary ions at leading order. This is the fast-time electron-MHD limit of Hall magnetohydrodynamics and is precisely electron magnetohydrodynamics.
The already obtained fast-wave eigenvector has u/b=∣q/ω∣∼1/(kdi​)≪1, confirming that ion motion cannot affect its leading frequency. The closed magnetic equation therefore suffices to obtain that branch without using momentum to close it. Its timescale is τw​∼1/(vA​di​∣k∥​∣k); compatibility with τw​≪l/vA​ requires ∣k∥​∣di​≫1 when l∼k−1. Arbitrarily small k∥​ can violate this fast-time assumption even though kdi​≫1. The slow branch does not obey the stated ordering and is not retained by the fixed-ion reduction.

For the theta pinch, the electric current is azimuthal:
j0​=4πc​∇×(B0​z^)=−4πcB0′​​θ^.
(15)
Its Lorentz force is j0​×B0​/c=−B0​B0′​r^/(4π). Static radial balance with the pressure gradient therefore gives
p0′​+4πB0​B0′​​=0,p0​(r)+8πB0​(r)2​=constant.​
(16)
The constant is the equilibrium magnetohydrodynamic total pressure.

Suppress the common Fourier mode factor and define the transverse and full divergences of the fluid Lagrangian displacement by
D⊥​=r1​(rξr​)′+rim​ξθ​,D=D⊥​+ikξz​.
(17)
The perturbed magnetic field is Q=(B0​⋅∇)ξ−(ξ⋅∇)B0​−B0​D. Its components are
Qr​=ikB0​ξr​,Qθ​=ikB0​ξθ​,Qz​=−B0​D⊥​−B0′​ξr​.
(18)
Thus the magnetic part of the energy integrand is
4π∣Q∣2​=4πB02​​[k2(∣ξr​∣2+∣ξθ​∣2)+∣D⊥​∣2]+4π(B0′​)2​∣ξr​∣2+4πB0​B0′​​(D⊥∗​ξr​+D⊥​ξr∗​).
(19)
Use p0′​=−B0​B0′​/(4π) from force balance. The pressure-gradient cross term is
D∗ξr​p0′​=−4πB0​B0′​​D⊥∗​ξr​+4πikB0​B0′​​ξz∗​ξr​.
(20)
The current term has the azimuthal cross-product component ξz∗​Qr​−ξr∗​Qz​, giving
cj0​⋅(ξ∗×Q)​=−4πikB0​B0′​​ξz∗​ξr​−4πB0​B0′​​ξr∗​D⊥​−4π(B0′​)2​∣ξr​∣2.
(21)
Every term involving B0′​ cancels in the sum, including the possibly complex ξz∗​ξr​ terms. The magnetohydrodynamic energy principle therefore reduces exactly to
δW=2πLz​∫rdr[γp0​∣D∣2+4πB02​​(∣D⊥​∣2+k2(∣ξr​∣2+∣ξθ​∣2))]≥0.​
(22)
For physical p0​≥0 and positive adiabatic index γ, every surviving term is nonnegative. On the admissible displacement domain for the supplied functional, squared ideal-mode frequencies cannot be negative. This is the positive ideal energy of a theta pinch, proving absence of exponentially growing ideal modes. The qualification is stable or marginal, not strictly positive energy for every motion: at k=0, suitable incompressible displacements can have zero energy. As usual, the energy principle presumes regularity and boundary conditions eliminating unwanted surface terms; additional external systems are not part of the given functional.

Interpret the multiplicative Gaussian white noise as the limit of a smooth short-correlation-time velocity field, hence in the Stratonovich integral convention. Put ∂i​=∂/∂Bi​. For a realization, differentiating the field-space Dirac delta gives its field-space continuity equation
∂t​P=−∂i​(σim​Bm​P).
(23)
The velocity-gradient covariance has zero trace variance: contracting Tmnij​ with m=i,n=j gives 3−(9+3)/4=0. Thus the gradient is trace free, consistently with the incompressible ideal induction equation.
A noise impulse in component σjn​ changes Bj​ by Bn​ times that impulse. Its instantaneous retarded action on the Dirac delta is therefore
δσjn​δP​=−∂j​(Bn​P).
(24)
Only earlier times affect the present field. In the white-noise limit the integral over this causal interval has ∫0t​δ(t−t′)dt′=1/2, corresponding to the symmetric short-time regularization. Applying the Furutsu–Novikov formula and then averaging gives
⟨σim​P⟩=−2κ2​​Tmnij​∂j​(Bn​P).
(25)
Substitute into the averaged field-space continuity equation:
∂t​P=2κ2​​∂i​[Bm​Tmnij​∂j​(Bn​P)].
(26)
The differentiated Bn​ term vanishes because ∑j​Tmjij​=0, and the remaining contraction is
Tmnij​Bm​Bn​=B2δij​−21​Bi​Bj​.
(27)
Consequently the closed vector Fokker-Planck equation for Gaussian white-noise magnetic stretching is
∂t​P=2κ2​​∂i​[(B2δij​−21​Bi​Bj​)∂j​P].​
(28)
This is a positive diffusion operator: the displayed matrix has eigenvalues B2 on the two transverse directions and B2/2 along B. Replacing the endpoint half-weight by one would double the diffusion and fail to reproduce the later rate.

For isotropic initial statistics, isotropic noise preserves P=P(B,t). Isotropy of the noise alone does not turn an arbitrary anisotropic initial vector distribution into an isotropic one; alternatively take the angularly averaged magnitude distribution. The radial gradient satisfies ∂j​P=PB​Bj​/B, so the diffusion-vector contraction in the preceding equation is BBi​PB​/2. Its divergence gives
Pt​=4κ2​​(B2PBB​+4BPB​)=4B2κ2​​∂B​(B4PB​).
(29)
Now F=4πB2P is the magnitude probability density function, normalized by ∫0∞​FdB=1. Since
4πB4PB​=B2FB​−2BF,
(30)
we obtain the radial magnetic-field Fokker-Planck equation
Ft​=D∂B​(B2FB​−2BF),D=4κ2​​=5γ​.​
(31)
The outward Fokker-Planck probability current is J=−D(B2FB​−2BF). Zero probability flux at zero and infinity preserves normalization. For positive initial magnitude the solution below remains on B>0; an atom initially at B=0 instead remains an absorbing zero-field component.

In Gaussian units, the ensemble-averaged magnetic energy density is
EB​(t)=8π1​∫0∞​B2F(B,t)dB.​
(32)
Let Mq​=∫0∞​BqFdB, and assume the relevant moment exists and endpoint terms vanish. Twice applying integration by parts to the conservative magnitude equation gives
dtdMq​​=−Dq∫0∞​Bq−1(B2FB​−2BF)dB=Dq(q+3)Mq​.
(33)
In particular, the magnetic stretching moment growth for q=2 is
dtd⟨B2⟩​=10D⟨B2⟩=2γ⟨B2⟩,EB​(t)=EB​(0)e2γt.​
(34)
This is a kinematic magnetic dynamo result with prescribed velocity statistics and no Ohmic loss. Once the Lorentz force appreciably changes the flow, this indefinitely exponential kinematic approximation is no longer self-consistent.

For B0​>0, change to logarithmic magnitude and its correctly transformed probability density function:
x=log(B/B0​),f(x,t)=BF(B,t),fdx=FdB.
(35)
Using ∂B​=B−1∂x​, the radial equation becomes
ft​=Dfxx​−3Dfx​.
(36)
This is diffusion with constant drift. Set y=x−3Dt and g(y,t)=f(y+3Dt,t); then gt​=Dgyy​. The initial Dirac delta transforms to f(x,0)=δ(x), so the heat-kernel solution gives
f(x,t)=4πDt​1​exp[−4Dt(x−3Dt)2​].
(37)
Dividing by B yields the lognormal magnetic-field amplification law
F(B,t)=B4πDt​1​exp[−4Dt(log(B/B0​)−3Dt)2​],B>0.​
(38)
Expanding the square and using B=B0​ex gives the equivalent requested form
F(B,t)=B0​(4/5)πγt​e−(9/20)γt​(B0​B​)1/2exp[−(4/5)γt[log(B/B0​)]2​].​
(39)
The logarithm is a Gaussian random variable with mean 3Dt and variance 2Dt, which proves normalization and spreading directly. The exponential moments of that Gaussian distribution give ⟨Bq⟩=B0q​eDq(q+3)t, independently checking the energy growth. The limit at t↓0 is the initial Dirac delta distribution, not a pointwise finite function.

Use incompressible ideal MHD at scales above the ion skin depth, a strong uniform mean field, and a statistically steady local energy cascade with mean flux ϵ per unit mass. Assume balanced counterpropagating wave populations, comparable velocity and magnetic amplitudes in velocity units, and no additional scale-dependent alignment or intermittency correction. Write b=δB/4πρ​ and introduce the fluctuation Elsässer variables z±=u±b. Their equations are
∂t​z±∓vA​∂∥​z±+(z∓⋅∇)z±=−∇Π,∇⋅z±=0.
(40)
Only opposite populations distort one another. A single population has no leading nonlinear self-interaction, so the balance assumption is essential to a one-amplitude cascade estimate.
Let δzl​ denote the typical balanced amplitude and let l⊥​,l∥​ be coherence lengths relative to the local mean field. The Alfvén wave time and eddy turnover time are
τA​=vA​l∥​​,τnl​=δzl​l⊥​​,χ=τnl​τA​​.
(41)
For χ≪1, one counterpropagating encounter changes the packet fractionally by order χ. Assume phases decorrelate between encounters. After N encounters, random accumulation is of order N​χ; an order-one change requires N∼χ−2. The weak wave cascade time is therefore
τcas​∼NτA​=τA​τnl2​​.
(42)
Weakness by itself does not prove this random walk estimate; phase decorrelation and the interaction geometry are additional assumptions.
For the Iroshnikov-Kraichnan spectrum, impose isotropy, l∥​∼l⊥​∼l, in addition to δzl​≪vA​. Then τA​=l/vA​≪τnl​=l/δzl​ and
τcas​∼δzl2​vA​l​,ϵ∼τcas​δzl2​​∼vA​lδzl4​​.
(43)
Hence δzl​∼(ϵvA​l)1/4. A local shell-integrated turbulent energy spectrum has δzl2​∼kE(k), k∼l−1, so
EIK​(k)∼(ϵvA​)1/2k−3/2.​
(44)
The isotropy is imposed phenomenologically; a strong guide field actually distinguishes directions and does not force this isotropic picture. The spectrum is not an automatically valid asymptotic result for every strongly magnetized cascade.
For the anisotropic weak Alfvénic cascade, keep l∥​ approximately fixed while cascading to smaller l⊥​, and require χ≪1. This geometry follows from resonant three-wave Alfvén interactions: frequency conservation together with parallel-wavenumber conservation makes one member of an oppositely propagating resonant triad have zero parallel wavenumber, so the propagating modes retain their parallel wavenumber in the leading resonant transfer. Thus there is no leading parallel cascade in this ideal weak-wave ordering. Using the fixed parallel scale,
τcas​∼δzl2​l∥​vA​l⊥2​​,ϵ∼vA​l⊥2​δzl4​l∥​​.
(45)
Therefore δzl​∼(ϵvA​/l∥​)1/4l⊥1/2​. For the one-dimensional perpendicular spectrum, δzl2​∼k⊥​E⊥​(k⊥​) gives
E⊥,weak​(k⊥​)∼(l∥​ϵvA​​)1/2k⊥−2​.​
(46)
Unlike the isotropic estimate, the parallel length does not decrease with the perpendicular length. Since χ=δzl​l∥​/(vA​l⊥​)∝l⊥−1/2​, the interactions become stronger down the cascade. If χ0​≪1 at outer perpendicular scale L⊥​, the weak approximation breaks at l⊥,∗​∼L⊥​χ02​. This is the weak-to-strong transition of an Alfvénic cascade.
For Goldreich–Sridhar turbulence, abandon the weak-collision estimate and impose critical balance, τA​∼τnl​. The local cascade is strong, with transfer time of order one eddy turnover time. Then
ϵ∼l⊥​δzl3​​,δzl​∼(ϵl⊥​)1/3,E⊥,GS​(k⊥​)∼ϵ2/3k⊥−5/3​.​
(47)
The parallel scale is set dynamically, not held fixed:
l∥​∼vA​ϵ−1/3l⊥2/3​,k∥​∼vA​ϵ1/3​k⊥2/3​.​
(48)
At smaller scales l∥​/l⊥​ grows, so the eddies are increasingly elongated along the local magnetic field. Strong turbulence does not mean δzl​∼vA​: small transverse amplitudes can still be strong when the eddies are sufficiently anisotropic to satisfy critical balance. Imbalance, alignment or nonlocal interactions would require different cascade reasoning.
Finally, in the usual isotropic hydrodynamic Kolmogorov 1941 theory inertial-range argument, the only relevant dimensional quantities are ϵ and k. With [ϵ]=L2T−3 and [E]=L3T−2, writing E∝ϵakb forces a=2/3, b=−5/3. This uses the physical assumptions that outer-scale, viscous and intermittency effects do not supply another leading dependence; dimensions alone are not a proof of those assumptions.
A magnetic guide field supplies the independent speed vA​, and anisotropy adds independent parallel lengths. Even before adding anisotropy, dimensions permit
E(k)=ϵ2/3k−5/3Φ(vA​ϵ−1/3k1/3),​
(49)
with arbitrary dimensionless Φ. For example Φ(x)∝x1/2 reproduces the isotropic Iroshnikov-Kraichnan spectrum estimate. This dimensional freedom of an MHD spectrum explains why propagation, decorrelation, anisotropy and cascade-time assumptions must select among the spectra: purely dimensional considerations do not select a unique MHD exponent.
