Let L/K be a finite Galois extension of number fields, with Galois group G, and let p be a nonzero prime ideal of OK​. Write its prime ideal factorization as
pOL​=∏j=1g​Pjej​​,fj​=[OL​/Pj​:OK​/p].
(1)
The Galois group acts transitively on the primes above p. One proof uses the Chinese remainder theorem. If Q were outside the orbit of P, choose an integral element a which is zero modulo Q and one modulo every conjugate of P. Its field norm ∏σ∈G​σ(a) belongs to Q∩OK​=p, hence to P. But each factor is one modulo P, a contradiction. Transitivity also makes all ej​ equal to a common ramification index e and all fj​ equal to a common residue degree f.
Fix P above p. Its decomposition group is the stabilizer
DP​={σ∈G:σP=P}.
(2)
The orbit-stabilizer theorem gives ∣DP​∣=∣G∣/g. Elements of DP​ act on the residue field κ(P) over κ(p); the group kernel is the inertia group
IP​={σ∈DP​:σ(a)≡a(modP) for all a∈OL​}.
(3)
Thus inertia measures the automorphisms invisible after reduction, whereas decomposition measures all automorphisms preserving the chosen prime.
The completion LP​/Kp​ is a Galois extension with group DP​: preservation of P lets an automorphism extend continuously to the completion, and the local embeddings of this normal extension arise in this way. The degree of a field extension formula is [LP​:Kp​]=ef. Reduction gives the exact sequence
1⟶IP​⟶DP​⟶Gal(κ(P)/κ(p))⟶1.
(4)
For surjectivity, lift a primitive residue-field generator by the Hensel lemma to obtain the maximal unramified subextension of a local field extension of LP​/Kp​. Its automorphisms are the residue-field automorphisms. They extend to LP​ because the latter is normal. Since a finite residue field extension has a cyclic Galois group of order f, the exact sequence yields
∣DP​∣=ef,∣IP​∣=e,∣DP​/IP​∣=f,[L:K]=gef.​
(5)
The fixed field of local inertia is the maximal unramified subextension of a local field extension, and the extension above it is totally ramified. Primes in the same global orbit have conjugate decomposition groups and inertia groups, so the orders do not depend on the chosen prime.
If e=1, inertia is trivial and DP​ is generated by the unique Frobenius automorphism acting on residues by z↦zNp. Its order is f. In a ramified extension this residue action determines only a coset modulo inertia. A prime splits completely exactly when e=f=1, equivalently its decomposition group is trivial. A totally ramified prime has g=f=1 and IP​=DP​=G; an unramified prime has IP​=1. In an abelian extension, conjugation does nothing, so the associated groups and unramified Artin symbol are independent of the prime above p.

Use arithmetic Frobenius automorphisms: residues are raised to the size of the base residue field. Let S contain the finite primes ramified in the finite abelian extension L/K. The Artin map is the group homomorphism
ArtL/K​:IKS​⟶Gal(L/K),∏p∈/S​pnp​⟼∏p∈/S​Frobpnp​​,
(6)
where IKS​ is the group of fractional ideals supported outside S. The Artin symbol Frobp​ is independent of the prime above p, since the extension is abelian. Commutativity makes the displayed extension from prime ideals well defined. Defining this map does not yet assert the Artin reciprocity law, which describes its congruence group kernel.
For an intermediate field K⊆E⊆L, restriction gives
ArtL/K​(a)∣E​=ArtE/K​(a).​
(7)
To prove it, start with an unramified prime p and a prime of L above it. Its Frobenius automorphism acts on the residue of every integral element of E by raising it to Np. Its restriction is therefore the Frobenius automorphism for E/K at the restricted prime. The identity for all allowed fractional ideals follows by the group homomorphism property. On Galois groups, restriction is the quotient by Gal(L/E), so passing to a subextension quotients the Artin map accordingly.
Now let E/K be any finite extension and put M=LE. This is the translated extension: M/E is abelian, and restriction identifies Gal(M/E) with Gal(L/(L∩E)). A prime q of E above a prime p∈/S is unramified in M/E, because forming a compositum with E preserves an unramified extension. Put r=f(q/p). The residue-field sizes satisfy Nq=(Np)r. Consequently the restriction to L of Frobenius at q acts on its integral residues by raising them to (Np)r, giving
Frobq​(M/E)∣L​=Frobp​(L/K)r.
(8)
But the ideal norm is NE/K​q=pr. Multiplying this prime-by-prime identity proves
ArtM/E​(a)∣L​=ArtL/K​(NE/K​a).​
(9)
This is restriction and norm compatibility of the Artin map. The fractional ideal a must be supported over primes unramified in L/K. No assumption that E/K itself is unramified or Galois is needed. With geometric rather than arithmetic Frobenius, both maps are inverted and the compatibility identities remain unchanged.

A useful strong form of the Hensel lemma is the following. Let F be a complete discretely valued field, with valuation ring O, normalized valuation v, and P∈O[T]. If a0​∈O satisfies
v(P(a0​))>2v(P′(a0​)),
(10)
then there is a unique root a∈O in the ball v(a−a0​)>v(P′(a0​)). When P(a0​)=0 the assertion is immediate; otherwise the condition includes P′(a0​)=0.
Here is a proof by Newton iteration over a valued field. Write c=v(P′(a0​)) and t0​=v(P(a0​))>2c, and set
aj+1​=aj​−P′(aj​)P(aj​)​.
(11)
Suppose v(P′(aj​))=c and tj​=v(P(aj​))>2c. The correction has valuation tj​−c>c≥0, so aj+1​∈O. Taylor expansion over O cancels the linear term and gives
v(P(aj+1​))≥2(tj​−c),v(P′(aj+1​)−P′(aj​))≥tj​−c>c.
(12)
Thus the derivative still has valuation c, and tj+1​−2c≥2(tj​−2c). Unless an exact root is reached earlier, the corrections tend to zero with exponentially increasing valuations. Completeness gives aj​→a, and continuity gives P(a)=0 and v(a−a0​)=t0​−c>c. For uniqueness, if a,b are in this ball, polynomial expansion around a0​ gives
P(a)−P(b)=(a−b)(P′(a0​)+R),v(R)>c.
(13)
The second factor has valuation exactly c and is nonzero, so two roots in the ball must be equal. In particular, a simple residue root lifts uniquely: v(P(a0​))≥1 and v(P′(a0​))=0 satisfy the hypothesis.
For the square-class group of a p-adic field, write every element as pru with u∈Zp×​. Multiplication by a square changes r by an even integer, so valuation parity gives one independent factor C2​.

Qp×​/(Qp×​)2≅C22​,qquadrepresentatives 1,u0​,p,pu0​(p=2).​

For a,b∈Qp×​, the quadratic Hilbert symbol (a,b)p​ is one if b is a field norm from Qp​(a​), and minus one otherwise; a square a gives the trivial extension and symbol one. It depends only on the two square classes. Equivalently, the conic z2=ax2+by2 has a nonzero rational point precisely when the symbol is one. Interchanging a and b in the conic gives (a,b)p​=(b,a)p​. The Hilbert norm residue symbol is a group homomorphism in each argument; for nonsquare a, the cyclic local norm index makes its group kernel a subgroup of index two. (a,−a)p​=1,(a,a)p​=(a,−1)p​,

We give the detailed norm-index argument for a cyclic extension L/K of p-adic fields; thus K is a finite extension of Qp​. Write G=⟨σ⟩, ∣G∣=n. For an additive G-module M, put D=σ−1 and N=1+σ+⋯+σn−1. The two periodic Tate cohomology of a cyclic group groups and the Herbrand quotient are
H0(G,M)=kerD/NM,H−1(G,M)=kerN/DM,hG​(M)=∣H−1(G,M)∣∣H0(G,M)∣​,
(25)
when these groups are finite. For multiplicative modules, N is the product of conjugates and D(z)=σ(z)/z.
The periodic complex alternating D and N gives a six-term exact sequence for every short exact sequence of modules. Taking the alternating product of the finite group orders in that exact sequence proves
hG​(M)=hG​(M′)hG​(M′′)if 0→M′→M→M′′→0.
(26)
For a finite module, the counting fibers gives ∣NM∣=∣M∣/∣kerN∣ and ∣DM∣=∣M∣/∣kerD∣, so the two Tate groups have equal order. This proves the Herbrand quotient of a finite module is one. It follows that replacing a module by a finite-index submodule does not change its quotient, whenever the cohomology groups are finite.
Two basic calculations drive the norm computation. For the trivial module Z, D=0 and N is multiplication by n, so hG​(Z)=n. For a regular lattice R[G], with R=Zp​, invariants are constant coefficient vectors and every such vector is a norm: put its constant coefficient in just one coordinate before applying N. The group kernel of N consists of the coefficient vectors of sum zero. Successively taking partial sums solves the cyclic difference equation Dx=y for every such vector y. Thus both Tate groups vanish and hG​(R[G])=1. This is cyclic cohomology of a regular lattice.
Let UL​=OL×​ and ULr​=1+PLr​. For sufficiently large r, the p-adic logarithm and p-adic exponential are inverse G-equivariant isomorphisms
log:ULr​∼​PLr​.
(27)
To make the range explicit, r>vL​(p)/(p−1) suffices: the higher terms in the logarithm and exponential have greater valuation than their leading terms, their series converge there, and the identities log(expx)=x and exp(log(1+x))=1+x remain valid. Thus a deep multiplicative unit group becomes an additive Zp​-lattice in L.
By the normal basis theorem, L≅K[G] as a K[G]-module. As a Qp​[G]-module it is therefore Qp​[G][K:Qp​]. Transport the regular lattice M0​=Zp​[G][K:Qp​] into L. Both M0​ and PLr​ are full G-stable Zp​-lattices, so their intersection has finite index in each. The exact sequences with finite quotients show that the Tate groups of PLr​ are finite and that
hG​(ULr​)=hG​(PLr​)=hG​(M0​)=1.
(28)
Since UL​/ULr​ is finite, the exact-sequence formula for the Herbrand quotient gives hG​(UL​)=1. Finally, the valuation sequence
1⟶UL​⟶L×vL​​Z⟶0
(29)
has trivial G-action on Z. Hence the Herbrand quotient of the local multiplicative group is
hG​(L×)=hG​(UL​)hG​(Z)=n.
(30)
For completeness, the denominator vanishes by Hilbert theorem 90. If NL/K​a=1, put c0​=1 and cj​=aσ(a)⋯σj−1(a). Independence of distinct field automorphisms lets us choose t∈L with b=∑j=0n−1​cj​σj(t)=0. Since cn​=1, shifting the sum gives σ(b)=b/a. Thus a=b/σ(b), a multiplicative coboundary. Therefore H−1(G,L×)=1. The invariant subgroup of L× is K×, so
[K×:NL/K​L×]=[L:K]=n.​
(31)
This proves the cyclic local norm index without assuming local reciprocity. If the extension has ramification index e and residue degree f, the valuation formula vK​(Nz)=fvL​(z) shows that the valuation contribution to the index is f. A norm is a unit only when z is a unit, so the remaining contribution is
[UK​:NL/K​UL​]=e.
(32)
In particular, unit norms are surjective in an unramified cyclic extension. The norm subgroup is open: on deep units its logarithm is TrL/K​(PLr​), a nonzero full lattice in K, and therefore contains a sufficiently deep principal-unit group. This connects the index calculation to the open finite-index norm subgroups classified by local class field theory.
To finish, the Hasse norm theorem for a cyclic extension of number fields states that a nonzero element is a global norm if and only if it is a norm at every finite and infinite completion. Here is its Brauer-theoretic proof outline. Associate to a∈K× the cyclic algebra A=(L/K,σ,a), with relations zℓ=σ(ℓ)z and zn=a. The splitting criterion for a cyclic algebra says that it splits exactly when a=Nc: if a=Nc, multiplication by L and the operator cσ realize it as EndK​(L); conversely, in a split matrix representation, the underlying n-dimensional space is one-dimensional over L, so z must act as cσ and zn=Nc.
Apply this criterion at every completion, using the restricted cyclic character and any one completion of L above that place. If a is a local norm everywhere, every localized Brauer class is zero. Injectivity in the Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorem forces [A]=0 in Br(K); the global splitting criterion gives a=Nc. Conversely, a global norm is a local norm after completion, since the norm on L⊗K​Kv​ is the product of the component norms, whose images coincide in the Galois case. The cyclic hypothesis is essential; the stated local-global principle does not hold for arbitrary extensions.

The Brauer group of a field consists of equivalence classes of central simple algebras, with addition induced by tensor product, zero represented by matrix algebras, and inverse represented by the opposite algebra. Localization sends a global algebra to its scalar extensions at the completions. For a non-Archimedean completion of a number field, the local Brauer invariant is an isomorphism
invv​:Br(Kv​)∼​Q/Z.
(33)
Normalize it so that an unramified cyclic algebra of degree m, with arithmetic Frobenius generator and parameter a, has invariant v(a)/m. For a real completion, Br(R)=Z/2 with the Hamilton quaternion algebra mapped to 1/2; for a complex completion the Brauer group is zero.
The global computation is the exact sequence in the Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorem:
0⟶Br(K)⟶v⨁​Br(Kv​)∑v​invv​​Q/Z⟶0.​
(34)
Thus a global Brauer class is determined uniquely by a finite-support family of local invariants. Such a family occurs if and only if its sum is zero; at real places the only allowed entries are 0,1/2, and at complex places the entry is zero. Injectivity is a local-global splitting principle; the middle exactness is the reciprocity constraint; surjectivity of the sum follows already from any one finite local summand. These are separate assertions, not merely different descriptions of the sum formula.
For an explicit group description, choose one finite place v0​. Assign arbitrary finite-support invariants at all other finite places and arbitrary allowed invariants at the real places, then set the invariant at v0​ equal to minus their sum. This gives
Br(K)≅(⨁v mfinite, v=v0​​Q/Z)⊕(Z/2)r1​.
(35)
The description by the entire invariant family is canonical; this displayed elimination of one coordinate depends on the choice of v0​.
The connection with Artin reciprocity is especially transparent for cyclic algebras. Let χ:Gal(L/K)↪Q/Z describe a cyclic extension, with χ(σ)=1/n. Write (χ,a) for the class of (L/K,σ,a). The local reciprocity pairing is
invv​(χv​,av​)=χv​(ArtKv​​(av​)).
(36)
It follows from the local fundamental class construction of the Local Artin map; on an unramified character it is precisely the normalization v(av​)/n. The group kernel in the parameter is the local norm group, and restriction/corestriction give the norm compatibility of this pairing.
For a principal parameter a∈K×, the zero-sum identity for its global cyclic algebra therefore reads
∑v​χv​(ArtKv​​(a))=0.
(37)
On the other hand the idelic Artin reciprocity law is
∏v​ArtL/K,v​(a)=1(a∈K×),
(38)
and evaluating this product by χ gives exactly that zero-sum identity. Conversely, testing every cyclic character of a finite abelian Galois group separates its elements, so the zero-sum identities for cyclic algebras imply this principal-idele reciprocity law for every finite abelian extension. At almost all places a unit and an unramified character pair trivially, so the sums and products are finite.
The full idelic statement identifies the group kernel as K×NL/K​JL​ and induces JK​/(K×NL/K​JL​)≅Gal(L/K). It explains why the local norm-residue pairings must glue globally. The global Brauer theorem supplies, in addition, the injectivity and realization statements needed to compute all algebra classes; the principal-product identity alone would not prove those statements. For cyclic algebras its injectivity yields the Hasse norm theorem, as in Question 3. In degree two, an invariant is either zero or 1/2, so the zero-sum law becomes the Hilbert reciprocity law ∏v​(a,b)v​=1. For example, a quaternion algebra over Q can be ramified at precisely a prescribed finite set of places, including possibly the real place, if and only if that set has even cardinality; the invariants uniquely determine its Brauer class. This illustrates how arithmetic reciprocity becomes a concrete constraint on global algebra structure.

For a number field K and a modulus of a number field m, let IK​(m) be the group of fractional ideals prime to its finite part. Let PK​(m) consist of principal ideals generated by elements congruent to one modulo its finite part and positive at each real place in its infinite part. The ray class group is Clm​(K)=IK​(m)/PK​(m).
The classification theorem of class field theory asserts the existence of a ray class field Km​ and an Artin map isomorphism
Clm​(K)∼​Gal(Km​/K).
(39)
Its intermediate fields correspond contravariantly to subgroups of this ray class group. Equivalently, for each congruence subgroup of fractional ideals PK​(m)⊆H⊆IK​(m) there is a unique finite abelian extension with Artin group kernel H and group IK​(m)/H. Every finite abelian extension occurs for some modulus. For a fixed modulus, these are exactly the extensions whose conductor divides that modulus; the same field may be presented using larger moduli, with the appropriate pulled-back group kernel.
We quote the conductor facts needed to specialize this classification. The finite conductor exponent is zero exactly at the unramified primes. A real place occurs in the conductor exactly when it becomes complex. Thus conductor one means unramified at every finite prime and split at every real place. With m=1 the ray class group is the ordinary ideal class group, so classification gives an extension HK​/K with
Gal(HK​/K)≅Cl(K),[HK​:K]=hK​.​
(40)
It is unramified at all finite primes, and its real places split. Conversely, every finite abelian extension with those properties has conductor one and lies in K1​=HK​. This proves the existence and maximality of the ordinary Hilbert class field. Its degree is finite by finiteness of the ideal class group. The real-place convention matters: allowing real places to become complex gives the narrow class field instead; for the imaginary quadratic field in part (ii) this distinction disappears.

Identify the required class in Gal(H/K) through the Artin map. We can use the explicit representation 17=2+15: 2+t has norm 34, so the prime above 17 obtained from (2+t)p2−1​ has class [p2​]. Its Frobenius in the multiquadratic extension acts on the three radicals with signs
((172​),(17−3​),(175​))=(1,−1,−1).
(50)
Their product is one, so this automorphism fixes K. Because H/Q is abelian, for any unramified rational prime p that splits in K, the Frobenius at either prime of K of norm p is its rational Frobenius restricted to H/K. The Artin isomorphism therefore makes the required class condition equivalent to
(p2​)=1,(p−3​)=−1,(p5​)=−1.
(51)
Conversely, these signs already imply splitting in K, since their product is (p−30​)=1. No extra splitting condition is missing.
The supplementary law for 2, quadratic reciprocity for −3 and 5, and then the Chinese remainder theorem turn these sign conditions into
p≡1 or 7(mod8),p≡2(mod3),p≡2 or 3(mod5).
(52)
Their four simultaneous residue classes modulo 120 are 17,23,47,113. Finally, 2 is represented by (x,y)=(±1,0), whereas 3 and 5 cannot be represented: y=0 gives a value at least 15, and y=0 gives twice a square. We have proved the complete criterion
p=2x2+15y2 is soluble⟺p=2 or p≡17,23,47,113(mod120).​
