The accepted values θ(1),…,θ(R) have marginal posterior density

Estimate this one-dimensional density by a histogram or kernel density estimation from the simulated values. Where π(θ)>0, likelihood reconstruction from posterior simulation gives

Use bins or a sufficiently fine grid, and refine near the maximum. For a histogram with bins Ib​, its posterior mass divided by prior mass estimates a prior-weighted average of the likelihood in that bin:
∫Ib​​π(u)duR−1#{r:θ(r)∈Ib​}​ ⟶ mk​∫Ib​​π(u)du∫Ib​​π(u)ℓk​(u)du​.

Narrowing the bins gives pointwise likelihood reconstruction at continuous positive-prior points. A flat proper prior over the search interval makes the highest posterior-density bin approximately the highest-likelihood bin. With a nonconstant prior, maximizing the posterior alone gives a maximum a posteriori estimate, generally different from maximum likelihood estimation. Choose prior support covering the intended search region and include a possible boundary maximum; for k=0, ℓ0​(θ)=E[e−θL/2] decreases with θ, so the maximum-likelihood estimate is 0.
The sampled genealogies provide a second likelihood reconstruction when the one-dimensional prior integral is computable. Put
rk​(t)=∫0∞​π(u)ak​(u,t)du.
(40)
Their posterior density is q(t)rk​(t)/mk​. Therefore, for any trial value ϑ,
R1​∑r=1R​rk​(T(r))ak​(ϑ,T(r))​ ⟶ mk​1​∫q(t)ak​(ϑ,t)dt=mk​ℓk​(ϑ)​.

This is an importance sampling estimate of the likelihood curve using the posterior genealogies. It can be maximized over a grid without estimating a posterior density in θ, provided rk​(t)>0 wherever the target integrand is positive and the weights are numerically usable. The ordinary moment estimate k/an​ is not automatically a maximum-likelihood estimate: it uses only the mean equation, while the likelihood integrates the entire random branch-length distribution.
