Use signed velocities in the shock frame; the Mach number depends on their squares. For a genuine nonradiative perfect gas shock wave, the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas give a common mass flux j=ρL​uL​=ρR​uR​, a common momentum flux p+ρu2, and a common specific stagnation enthalpy γp/[(γ−1)ρ]+u2/2. Here e in the given equation of state is specific internal energy, despite the source's informal “energy density” wording.
Put r=ρR​/ρL​, P=pR​/pL​ and M2=ρL​uL2​/(γpL​). Mass and momentum give uR​=uL​/r and P=1+γM2(1−1/r). Substituting these into the energy relation and clearing denominators gives
(r−1)[((γ−1)M2+2)r−(γ+1)M2]=0.
(1)
The r=1 factor is the no-jump solution. On the nontrivial shock wave branch,
r=(γ−1)M2+2(γ+1)M2​,P=γ+12γM2−(γ−1)​.​
(2)
In particular r−1=2(M2−1)/[(γ−1)M2+2]. With γ>1, compression is therefore equivalent to ML2​>1. The other Mach number is
MR2​=rPML2​​=2γML2​−(γ−1)(γ−1)ML2​+2​.
(3)
Positive pressure makes its denominator positive, and MR2​−1 has the opposite sign to ML2​−1. Thus a nontrivial physical shock wave has a supersonic side and a subsonic side; the entropy-admissible flow goes from the former to the latter.
The shock compression ratio also yields uL​uR​=(γ−1)uL2​/(γ+1)+2γpL​/[(γ+1)ρL​]. Rearranging produces the first velocity identity in the question. The pressure relation gives the second identity in the equivalent form
pR​=γ+12ρL​uL2​−(γ−1)pL​​.
(4)
The numerator here contains mass density, as the original PDF shows; the converted TeX incorrectly replaces that mass density by pressure.
For reflection, the once-shocked gas is the common left state in both applications. Before rebound, its velocity in the shock frame is a=us​−U+​<0 and the gas ahead has velocity −U+​; after rebound, it is b=us​+U−​>0 and the gas next to the wall has velocity U−​. In both cases the right velocity minus the left velocity equals −us​. Applying the first identity therefore gives the same quadratic equation,
v2−2γ+1​us​v−ρs​γps​​=0,
(5)
with roots a and b. Their opposite signs distinguish the two roots, so (us​−U+​)(us​+U−​)=−γps​/ρs​​.
Write q0​=p0​/ps​, q1​=p1​/ps​. The pressure identity applied to each shock wave gives
a2=2ρs​ps​​[(γ+1)q0​+γ−1],b2=2ρs​ps​​[(γ+1)q1​+γ−1].
(6)
Squaring the root-product relation and eliminating the velocities yields
[(γ+1)q0​+γ−1][(γ+1)q1​+γ−1]=4γ2,
(7)
and hence the normal shock reflection at a rigid wall relation
ps​p1​​=γ−1+(γ+1)p0​/ps​3γ−1−(γ−1)p0​/ps​​.​
(8)
Taking p0​/ps​→0 gives p1​/ps​→(3γ−1)/(γ−1)​; for a monatomic gas this limiting amplification is six.

Linearizing the ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation about a static magnetic field and using u′=∂t​ξ gives the fluid Lagrangian displacement-generated magnetic field perturbation b=∇×(ξ×B). The two solenoidal vector field conditions reduce the curl identity to
bi​=Bj​∂j​ξi​−ξj​∂j​Bi​.​
(9)
Its divergence vanishes because it is a curl. Directly, the two products of first derivatives in ∂i​bi​ cancel after exchanging their indices, while the remaining terms differentiate ∇⋅ξ or ∇⋅B, both zero. Similarly the integrated linear continuity equation gives ρ′=−∇⋅(ρξ), so ρ′=−ξ⋅∇ρ​.
Let Π=p+B2/(2μ0​) be the magnetohydrodynamic total pressure. Magnetostatic equilibrium and the momentum equation in the linearized ideal magnetohydrodynamic equations are
∂i​Π=−ρ∂i​Φ+μ0−1​Bj​∂j​Bi​,
(10)
−σ2ρξi​=−∂i​π′−ρ′∂i​Φ+μ0−1​(Bj​∂j​bi​+bj​∂j​Bi​),π′=p′+B⋅b/μ0​.
(11)
There is no perturbed gravitational potential here because the field is fixed. Multiply by ξi∗​ and integrate. The pressure term vanishes by the assumed boundary cancellation and ∇⋅ξ∗=0. Thus
σ2I=−∫ξi∗​ξj​ρ,j​Φ,i​dV+μ0−1​M,I=∫ρ∣ξ∣2dV,
(12)
where M=−∫ξi∗​(Bj​∂j​bi​+bj​∂j​Bi​)dV.
Integrate the first magnetic term along B, using ∇⋅B=0, then insert the expression for b. This gives the positive term ∫∣(B⋅∇)ξ∣2dV and two mixed derivative terms. For clarity, the troublesome term transforms as
−∫(Bk​∂k​ξi∗​)ξj​∂j​Bi​=∫ξi∗​Bk​(∂k​ξj​)∂j​Bi​+∫ξi∗​ξj​Bk​∂k​∂j​Bi​.
(13)
The first term on the right cancels the other mixed term. Combining the remaining derivative with the term containing ∂j​Bk​∂k​Bi​ gives
M=∫∣(B⋅∇)ξ∣2dV+∫ξi∗​ξj​∂j​(Bk​∂k​Bi​)dV.
(14)
Differentiating the equilibrium relation now combines the gradient of mass density and magnetostatic equilibrium terms:
−ρ,j​Φ,i​+μ0−1​∂j​(Bk​∂k​Bi​)=Π,ij​+ρΦ,ij​.
(15)
We obtain the incompressible magnetic displacement energy identity
σ2I=∫ξi∗​ξj​Π,ij​dV+∫ρξi∗​ξj​Φ,ij​dV+μ0​1​∫∣(B⋅∇)ξ∣2dV.​
(16)
The two Hessian matrices are real symmetric, making their Hermitian form evaluations real. The last term is a squared norm, and I>0 for a nonzero displacement in fluid with positive mass density. Thus σ2 is real​. It may be negative, in which case one temporal branch has exponential growth; real squared frequency is not itself a proof of stability.
For the atmosphere with a horizontal magnetic field, Φ=gz has zero Hessian matrix and the equilibrium equation is
dzd​(p+2μ0​B2​)=−ρg.​
(17)
Consequently the interchange stability of an incompressible magnetized atmosphere is governed by the numerator
Q[ξ]=−g∫ρz​∣ξz​∣2dV+μ0​1​∫B2∣∂x​ξ∣2dV.
(18)
For ξz​=sinky, the magnetic tension term is zero: these fluid Lagrangian displacements interchange neighboring magnetic field lines without varying along them. A mass density increasing with height gives negative energy and instability; decreasing mass density gives positive energy for these vertical interchanges. If ρz​≤0 everywhere, the displayed form is nonnegative for every admitted incompressible displacement. If the gradient changes sign, suitably localized divergence-free interchange trials detect an unstable inverted layer; For example, take ξz​=f(z)eiky and ξy​=if′(z)eiky/k, with f supported in that layer. Their divergence is zero, and neither component varies along the magnetic field.
For ξz​=sinkx, magnetic field lines are bent. Averaging over a full horizontal wavelength gives the trial Rayleigh quotient
IQ​=∫ρdz−g∫ρz​dz+(k2/μ0​)∫B2dz​,
(19)
under the stipulated admissible boundary/integrability conditions. It is a trial quotient, not a claim that this displacement is an exact eigenfunction for every profile. mass density decreasing upward is stable here too. For inverted mass density, magnetic tension can stabilize sufficiently short waves along the magnetic field, but does not remove the transverse interchange instability. Locally the two contributions have the familiar scale −gρz​/ρ+k2vA2​, with the Alfvén speed vA​=B/μ0​ρ​.

A spatially uniform pressure is a useful leading approximation when the sound speed makes the acoustic crossing time much shorter than the radiative cooling and thermal conduction times. The motions must remain at low Mach number, and gravity and other stresses must be negligible. Sound then redistributes pressure before appreciable thermal evolution occurs. Small pressure corrections can still drive the slow motion; imposing exactly zero pressure gradient in the full momentum equation at every order would be a stronger assumption.
The equation of state gives the specific internal energy of an ideal gas as e=p/[(γ−1)ρ]. With D/Dt=∂t​+v∂x​, the continuity equation is Dρ/Dt=−ρvx​. Differentiating e therefore yields
ρDtDe​=γ−11​(DtDp​+pvx​).
(20)
Insert this into the internal energy equation, whose compressional term is −pvx​, and use spatially uniform pressure, so Dp/Dt=pt​. The result is
γ−1pt​​+γ−1γp​vx​+ρ2Λ(T)−∂x​(λTx​)=0.​
(21)
For temporally constant pressure put A=μp/R, so the mass density is ρ=A/T. The continuity equation now gives vx​=T−1DT/Dt. Multiplication of the last equation by (γ−1)T/(γp), with λ=λ0​Tα, gives
DtDT​+CTΛ(T)​−γγ−1​pλ0​T​∂x​(TαTx​)=0,C=γγ−1​(Rμ​)2p.​
(22)
This is the isobaric conductive cooling in mass coordinates transformation before changing coordinates.
There is a boundary assumption implicit in the proposed Lagrangian coordinate. For a mass integral from a fixed origin, integration of the continuity equation gives
mx​=ρ,mt​=−ρv+ρ(0,t)v(0,t),DtDm​=ρ(0,t)v(0,t).
(23)
Thus m is material when the base mass flux vanishes, as appropriate for the stationary cold reservoir below. If the origin permits mass flux, replace m by m−∫tρ(0,s)v(0,s)ds; this corrected label has zero material derivative. In a material mass coordinate, D/Dt=∂t​∣m​ and ∂x​=(A/T)∂m​. In particular
∂x​(TαTx​)=TA2​∂m​(Tα−1Tm​).
(24)
The conduction coefficient therefore becomes Cλ0​, with the same C as the cooling coefficient. With τ=Ct, the equation is
Tτ​+TΛ(T)​−λ0​∂m​(Tα−1Tm​)=0.​
(25)
In the hot gas there is no volumetric radiative cooling. The initial temperature is uniform and the cold boundary introduces no finite mass length. The remaining nonlinear diffusion equation is invariant under m↦bm, τ↦b2τ with the same temperature scale. Its natural similarity solution is therefore
T=T0​f(ξ),ξ=λ0​T0α−1​τ​m​,f(0)=0,f(∞)=1.​
(26)
The far-field condition also recovers the initial temperature at every fixed m>0 as τ↓0. Substitution gives the ordinary differential equation
(fα−1f′)′+2ξ​f′=0.
(27)
For α=1, this reduces to f′′+ξf′/2=0. Integrating first for f′ gives f′=Kexp(−ξ2/4). The two boundary conditions fix K=1/π​, hence the error-function isobaric cooling front is
f(ξ)=erf(ξ/2),T(m,τ)=T0​erf(2λ0​τ​m​).​
(28)
Here the error function has normalization 2/π​, as printed in the original PDF; the converted TeX's 2/π is incorrect. The following original sketch shows the temperature profiles. The cooling layer broadens as τ​, and at fixed positive mass label the temperature falls as time increases, while the remote hot gas remains at T0​.
For α=1, the magnitude of the heat flux into the cold reservoir is
L=λTx​∣0+​=λ0​ATm​(0,τ).
(29)
Although the thermal conductivity tends to zero there, the physical temperature gradient diverges: their product has this finite limit. The stationary reservoir has no incoming advective energy flux and radiates the conducted energy immediately. Since Tm​(0,τ)=T0​/πλ0​τ​, the radiated power per unit area is
L=πτ​AT0​λ0​​​=T0​(γ−1)πγpλ0​​​t−1/2.​
(30)
Thus the requested exponent is k=1/2​. The initial divergence is the usual idealization of an instantaneous temperature discontinuity in a diffusion equation.

Set a=2πGρ0​. The axisymmetric Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity integrates to (RΦR​)′=4πGρ0​R. Regularity on the axis fixes the integration constant, so ΦR​=aR. Radial force balance in the rotating fluid gives
pR​=ρ0​(RΩ2−ΦR​)=ρ0​(k2R3−aR),p=pc​+ρ0​(4k2R4​−2aR2​).
(31)
With the stated central pressure, pc​=ρ0​a2/(4k2), this is a perfect square:
p(R)=4ρ0​k2​(R2−R02​)2,R02​=k2a​=k22πGρ0​​.​
(32)
The free surface lies at the first zero of pressure. For the source's positive rotation parameter, R0​=2πGρ0​​/k; allowing either rotation direction requires ∣k∣ in the denominator. The inward effective gravity is ΦR​−RΩ2=R(a−k2R2), so geff​(R0​)=0​. In particular pR​(R0​)=0. This is the critically rotating self-gravitating cylinder.
For a normal mode proportional to eiωt+imϕ, the background material derivative becomes iσ, where σ=ω+mΩ(R). Linearizing the centrifugal term gives −2Ωuϕ​ in the radial equation. In the azimuthal equation the background velocity gradient and cylindrical geometric term together give (2Ω+RΩ′)uR​=3ΩuR​. Since the interior mass density is constant, write W=p′/ρ0​+Φ′. The two momentum equations and incompressible flow condition are consequently
iσuR​−2Ωuϕ​3ΩuR​+iσuϕ​uR′​+uR​/R+imuϕ​/R​=−W′,=−imW/R,=0.​​
(33)
For m=0, the last two equations express the other amplitudes in terms of u=uR​:
uϕ​=mi​(Ru′+u),W=m2iR​{3mΩu−σ(Ru′+u)}.
(34)
Differentiate the second expression using RΩ′=Ω and Rσ′=mΩ, then substitute into the radial momentum equation. Before any division by σ, the result is
−σR2u′′−3σRu′+[3mΩ+(m2−1)σ]u=0.
(35)
Away from corotation this gives the required ordinary differential equation
u′′+R3​u′+R2u​(1−m2−σ3mΩ​)=0.​
(36)
At corotation the original coupled equations, or the undivided equation, must be used. A regular axisymmetric radial velocity would vanish by the incompressible flow condition; the requested dipolar calculation has m=1.
For m=1, use the amplitude normalization u=A(ω+kR), which remains useful when ω tends to zero. Substitution into the undivided equation gives zero identically. For nonzero ω this is the source's 1+kR/ω after choosing A=1/ω. The other amplitudes are
uR​=A(ω+kR),uϕ​=iA(ω+2kR),W=iAR[(kR)2−ω2].​
(37)
These formulas also satisfy the three original equations directly. By the regularity assumption in the question, this is the interior velocity family to which the free surface conditions must be applied.
The gravitational potential of a displaced cylindrical interface supplies an essential part of those conditions. For surface fluid displacement ηeimϕ, the bulk mass density perturbation vanishes but the displaced mass density step contributes ρ0​ηδ(R−R0​). The interior and exterior gravitational potential amplitudes solve Laplace's equation, are proportional to Rm and R−m for m≥1, and are continuous at the surface. Integrating the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity across the mass density step gives
ΦR,out′​−ΦR,in′​=4πGρ0​η.
(38)
Since the two radial derivatives at the surface are −mΦs′​/R0​ and mΦs′​/R0​, respectively,
Φs′​=−maR0​​η.​
(39)
It would be incorrect to set the entire potential perturbation to zero merely because the bulk mass density is unchanged.
Let Ωs​=kR0​, so a=Ωs2​. Zero fluid Lagrangian perturbation of surface pressure gives ps′​+ηpR​(R0​)=0, hence ps′​=0. The surface momentum potential and kinematic condition for m=1 are therefore
Ws​=−Ωs2​R0​η,i(ω+Ωs​)η=A(ω+Ωs​).
(40)
If ω+Ωs​=0, the kinematic condition fixes η=−iA. The same value follows if the radial fluid Lagrangian displacement uR​/[i(ω+kR)]=−iA is required to extend continuously to the free surface, including a possible surface-corotation limit. Comparing the gravitational condition with the interior value of Ws​ then gives
iAR0​(Ωs2​−ω2)=iAR0​Ωs2​,ω2=0​
(41)
for a nontrivial perturbation. These dipolar perturbations of a critically rotating cylinder describe a displacement of the whole cylinder's axis. Translating an isolated self-gravitating system gives another equilibrium, so there is no external restoring force and the translation is neutral. A uniform drift of the centre also follows from invariance under a Galilean transformation, explaining the double zero of the frequency equation. The singular 1/ω in the originally chosen velocity normalization does not obstruct the finite-amplitude limiting solution.
There is an admissibility qualification to the frequency conclusion as printed. Regular interior fluid Eulerian perturbation of velocity alone does not justify cancelling the surface factor ω+Ωs​. Eliminating η without that cancellation instead gives
Aω2(ω+Ωs​)=0.
(42)
The additional surface-corotation case ω=−Ωs​ formally satisfies every displayed velocity equation and the usual linearized surface conditions with
η=0,Φ′=0,uR​=A(kR−Ωs​),uϕ​=iA(2kR−Ωs​),p′=ρ0​iAR[(kR)2−Ωs2​].
(43)
Here uR​(R0​)=p′(R0​)=0, and the velocity is finite on the axis. However, its interior radial fluid Lagrangian displacement tends to −iA, whereas the undisplaced surface has η=0. Thus a regular single-frequency fluid Lagrangian displacement continuous up to the surface excludes this degenerate branch. The intended ω2=0 conclusion holds with that regular-displacement condition, or with surface corotation excluded; the printed velocity regularity assumption by itself leaves this formal exception.
