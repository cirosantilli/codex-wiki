Let T(N) count pairs n,n+2 both prime with n+2≤N. The Selberg sieve replaces primality by the necessary condition that the polynomial F(n)=n(n+2) have no small prime divisor. Its strength is a nonnegative quadratic majorant with optimizable divisor weights; its limitation is that a sifted integer need not be prime.
For a prime p, let ν(p) count the forbidden residues of n modulo p. We have ν(2)=1, since the two roots coincide modulo two, and ν(p)=2 for every odd prime, where the roots are zero and −2. The Chinese remainder theorem makes ν(d) multiplicative on squarefree integers. With g(d)=ν(d)/d,
#{n≤N:d∣F(n)}=Ng(d)+O(ν(d))(d squarefree).
(63)
These local densities constitute the sieve distribution for the twin-prime problem. Since ∑p≤z​g(p)=2loglogz+O(1), it is a dimension-two sieve, which is why the expected upper bound has two logarithms in the denominator.
Let P(R)=∏p≤R​p and choose real Selberg sieve weights λd​ supported on squarefree d≤R, with λ1​=1. If gcd(F(n),P(R))=1, the only supported divisor of F(n) is one. Hence
1gcd(F(n),P(R))=1​≤(∑d∣F(n)d≤R​​λd​)2.
(64)
Expanding and using the distribution of the polynomial roots gives
S(N,R):=#{n≤N:gcd(F(n),P(R))=1}≤NQ(λ)+O(∑d,e≤R​∣λd​λe​∣ν([d,e])),
(65)
Q(λ)=∑d,e≤R​λd​λe​g([d,e]).
(66)
Here [d,e] denotes the least common multiple. The divisor-square support reaches R2, although each individual weight is supported only to R; keeping these two levels distinct is essential for estimating the remainder.
The Selberg sieve diagonalization solves the main-term minimization exactly. On squarefree integers put

These weights have ∣λd​∣≤1, which supplies a simple rigorous remainder estimate. Indeed, multiply the inner sum by ∏p∣d​(1−g(p))−1=∑e∣d​w(e). The terms are w(em) for distinct squarefree integers em≤R with e∣d and (m,d)=1, hence form a subset of the sum defining G(R). Since ν([d,e])≤ν(d)ν(e) and ν(d)≤τ(d),
∑d,e≤R​∣λd​λe​∣ν([d,e])≤(∑d≤R​ν(d))2≪R2log2(2R).
(71)
Thus
S(N,R)≤G(R)N​+O(R2log2(2R)).
(72)
For this sieve w(2)=1 and w(p)=2/(p−2) for odd primes. Here is a direct proof that G(R)≍log2R, sufficient for the upper bound. Put
W(z)=∏p≤z​(1+w(p))=∏p≤z​(1−g(p))−1.
(73)
The Mertens theorem for reciprocal primes implies W(z)≍log2z, by taking logarithms and using the convergence of ∑p​g(p)2. Give each divisor d∣P(z) probability w(d)/W(z). Its expected logarithm is
E(logd)=∑p≤z​1+w(p)w(p)​logp=∑p≤z​g(p)logp=2logz+O(1),
(74)
by the Mertens first theorem. With z=R1/8 this is at most 31​logR for large R. The Markov inequality shows that at least two thirds of the total product weight has d≤R. These terms occur in G(R), so G(R)≫W(R1/8)≫log2R. Conversely G(R)≤W(R)≪log2R, proving the claim without treating an untruncated Euler product as a truncated divisor sum.
Choose, for example, R=N1/4. Any twin-prime pair with smaller member greater than R survives this sieve; pairs with smaller member at most R contribute at most O(R). Consequently
T(N)≤G(N1/4)N​+O(N1/2log2N+N1/4)≪(logN)2N​.​
(75)
The chosen level is deliberately enough to prove the correct logarithmic order; sharper level and remainder analyses improve the numerical upper-bound constant.
The local correction factor also explains the conjectured main-term constant. Relative to two independent primality conditions, the singular series is
S=∏p​(1−1/p)21−ν(p)/p​=2∏p>2​(1−(p−1)21​)=2C2​.
(76)
The twin-prime constant C2​ is positive and convergent because its logarithm has an absolutely convergent tail. The factor two at p=2 records that only one parity is allowed, while each odd-prime factor records the two distinct forbidden residues. The Hardy–Littlewood twin-prime asymptotic conjecture predicts
T(N)∼2C2​∫2N​(logt)2dt​∼(logN)22C2​N​.
(77)
This is a conjecture, not a consequence of the upper-bound calculation.
The parity problem in sieve theory is the obstruction to reversing the argument. The nonnegative square majorizes all survivors, including composites with sufficiently large prime factors; small-divisor information does not isolate the desired prime-factor parity or force both factors to be prime. Therefore the Selberg sieve establishes the correct upper-bound scale and the arithmetic local factors, but by itself does not prove a positive lower bound for twin primes, their infinitude, or the conjectured asymptotic.
