Use the integer norm lower bound for rational approximation to a quadratic irrational. The integer 2q2−a2 is nonzero, since 2​ is irrational. If a/q<2, then
​2​−qa​​=q2(2​+a/q)∣2q2−a2∣​>4q21​,
(83)
because 2​+a/q<2​+2<4. If a/q≥2, the difference is at least 2−2​>1/4≥1/(4q2). Thus
​2​−qa​​>4q21​(a,q≥1).​
(84)
In particular, 2​ is a badly approximable number.
To estimate the prime-weighted exponential sum, let Q=⌊N1/2⌋. The Dirichlet approximation theorem gives a reduced fraction a/q with
1≤q≤Q,​2​−qa​​≤qQ1​≤q21​.
(85)
Combining this with the lower bound gives q>Q/4. Thus q≍N1/2; the approximation cannot have a small denominator.
The relevant analytic input is the prime exponential-sum estimate from Vaughan identity:
∑n≤N​Λ(n)e(αn)≪(q​N​+N4/5+Nq​)log4(2N)(​α−qa​​≤q−2, (a,q)=1).
(86)
Here is the structure behind that estimate. With Dirichlet convolution and cutoffs U,V, the Vaughan identity is
Λ=μ≤U​∗log−μ≤U​∗Λ≤V​∗1+Λ≤V​+μ>U​∗Λ>V​∗1.
(87)
It follows from Λ=μ∗log, log=Λ∗1, and μ∗1=δ1​. The first two Dirichlet convolution terms become type I sums with one short factor; the last becomes a bilinear type II sum with both factors bounded below. Split these sums into dyadic blocks and use U=V=N2/5. For the type I sums, the inner finite geometric series has bound min(L,(2∥αm∥)−1). For the type II sums, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality followed by expansion introduces analogous sums in differences of indices. Rational approximation separates the phases into blocks of length comparable to q, bounding these reciprocal-distance sums. The Dirichlet convolution coefficients are divisor-bounded, producing the logarithmic factors. These steps give the displayed estimate; no unproved cancellation of arbitrary weighted sums is being assumed.
With α=2​ and q≍N1/2, the two denominator-dependent terms are O(N3/4). Consequently
n≤N∑​Λ(n)e(n2​)=O(N4/5log4(2N))=O(N9/10).​
(88)
The final passage absorbs the logarithm using the positive exponent margin 9/10−4/5=1/10.
