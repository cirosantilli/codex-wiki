A fixed point of the left shift satisfies xk+1​=xk​ for every k∈Z, so its entire two-sided sequence is constant. The constant sequence with value i belongs to the subshift of finite type precisely when Aii​=1: this is exactly the requirement that its repeated transition be allowed by the symbolic transition matrix. There is one such fixed point for each diagonal entry equal to one. Thus
#Fix(σ)=i=1∑m​Aii​=trA.​
(1)
This also covers an empty subshift of finite type, for then no permitted constant sequence exists.

Write Wn​(i,j) for the number of locally admissible words (x0​,…,xn​) with x0​=i and xn​=j. Here local admissibility means Axr​xr+1​​=1 for each consecutive pair; length counts symbols, so there are n transitions. For n=0, the single-symbol word gives W0​(i,j)=δij​=(A0)ij​, and for n=1 we have W1​(i,j)=Aij​.
To append the last symbol j, choose the penultimate symbol k. Every choice gives a locally admissible word exactly when its length-n prefix is locally admissible and Akj​=1. Different prefixes or penultimate symbols give different words, and every admissible word is obtained once. Therefore
Wn+1​(i,j)=∑k=1m​Wn​(i,k)Akj​.
(2)
This is precisely the rule for matrix multiplication. Mathematical induction consequently gives
Wn​(i,j)=(An)ij​.​
(3)
Equivalently, expanding the matrix entry sums the products Aix1​​Ax1​x2​​⋯Axn−1​j​ over all intermediate symbols; each product is the indicator function of one allowed transition chain.
The intended meaning of “allowed” here is local admissibility. If instead it means a word that actually occurs in some two-sided sequence in ΣA​, the assertion needs an additional extension assumption. For example, A=(00​10​) permits the word (1,2) and has (A)12​=1, but ΣA​ is empty because no symbol can continue after 2. Having a nonzero entry in every row and every column is sufficient: one can successively choose a predecessor and a successor to extend every locally admissible word indefinitely in both directions. No such assumption is needed for the local counting formula.

The equation σnx=x says xr+n​=xr​ for every r∈Z. Thus a solution is determined uniquely by the block (x0​,…,xn−1​), repeated in both directions. It belongs to the subshift of finite type exactly when all transitions inside the block, and the closing transition from xn−1​ to x0​, are allowed. Hence these fixed points of the nth iteration of a map are in bijection with the closed transition chains of n steps with a specified symbol at time zero. By the counting argument in part (b), the number starting at i is (An)ii​. Summing over i proves the trace formula for periodic points of a subshift:
#Fix(σn)=tr(An).​
(4)
Every closed transition chain extends by periodic repetition, so the extension issue in part (b) does not affect this formula. We count points, not periodic orbits: a least-period-d periodic orbit contributes its d distinct points.
Here period n means fixed by σn, so the least period may divide n. If Pd​ denotes the number of points of least period exactly d, division with remainder shows that a point is fixed by σn exactly when its least period divides n. Consequently
tr(An)=∑d∣n​Pd​,qquadPn​=∑d∣n​μM​(n/d)tr(Ad),
(5)
where μM​ is the Möbius function and the second equality is Möbius inversion. For example, A=(1) has one point fixed by every power but no point of least period two; interpreting the printed formula as an exact-period count would therefore be false.

The convention intended by this question is existence of a dense forward orbit, also called point transitivity. On a nonempty compact metric space, this means that some x∈X satisfies
{fn(x):n≥0}​=X.​
(6)
Equivalently, that forward orbit visits every nonempty open set.
The other common definition of topological transitivity uses intersections of iterated open sets, as in part (b). The distinction matters on spaces with isolated points: parts (b) and (c) ask precisely how the two conventions are related. We use point transitivity for the dense-orbit convention throughout this solution. As usual for this definition, the state space is assumed nonempty; on an empty space the open-set condition is vacuous and there is no point with a forward orbit.

A compact metric space is second countable. To see this concretely, for each positive integer r choose a finite cover by balls of radius 1/r. Their centres, over all r, form a countable dense subset; balls centred there with positive rational radii form a countable basis of a topology. Enumerate its nonempty members as (Vj​)j≥1​, repeating members if the basis is finite.
For each j, put
Gj​=⋃n≥1​f−n(Vj​).
(7)
Each Gj​ is an open set, because all iterations of a map fn are continuous. It is dense: if U is any nonempty open set, the hypothesis supplies n≥1 and u∈U with fn(u)∈Vj​, so u∈U∩Gj​.
A compact metric space is a complete metric space, hence a Baire space. The Baire category theorem implies that G=⋂j≥1​Gj​ is dense, and in particular nonempty. For any x∈G, the forward orbit of x meets every Vj​, and therefore every nonempty open set. Its orbit closure is X. Thus the open-set intersection hypothesis implies point transitivity, and in fact the set of points with a dense forward orbit contains the dense intersection G.

Let x have a dense forward orbit O={fj(x):j≥0}, and suppose that f is surjective. Every tail Ok​={fj(x):j≥k} is then dense. Indeed, continuity gives
fk(O)⊆fk(O)​=Ok​​,
(8)
and the left side equals fk(X)=X by surjectivity.
Given nonempty open sets U,V, choose k≥0 with fk(x)∈U. The dense tail Ok+1​ supplies j≥k+1 with fj(x)∈V. Taking n=j−k≥1 gives fj(x)∈fn(U)∩V. This proves the required positive-time intersection property.
The result can fail without surjectivity. Take the compact metric space
X={0}∪{1/j:j≥1},qquadf(0)=0,qquadf(1/j)=1/(j+1),
(9)
with the metric induced from the real line. All nonzero points are isolated, so continuity there is automatic; at zero, f(1/j)→0=f(0) proves continuity. The forward orbit of 1 is {1,1/2,1/3,…}, whose closure is X, so this is point transitivity. But f(X)=X∖{1}. The singletons U={1/2} and V={1} are nonempty open sets, and for every n≥1,
fn(U)={1/(n+2)},qquadfn(U)∩V=∅.
(10)
The isolated initial point can be visited only at the start of the dense forward orbit; deleting that initial segment destroys density.

Use the separated-set definition of topological entropy. For a compatible metric d and n≥1, define the Bowen metric and separated-set count by
dn​(x,y)=max0≤k<n​d(fkx,fky),sn​(d,ϵ)=max{∣E∣:dn​(x,y)≥ϵ for distinct x,y∈E}.
(11)
For fixed n, the metric dn​ generates the original topology: it includes the k=0 term and all finitely many iterations of a map are continuous. Thus its space is compact and sn​(d,ϵ) is finite. With natural logarithms, set
hd​(f,ϵ)=limsupn→∞​nlogsn​(d,ϵ)​,qquadhd​(f)=limϵ↓0​hd​(f,ϵ).
(12)
The last limit exists, possibly with value infinity, because decreasing ϵ can only increase separated-set counts.
Let d′ be another compatible metric. The identity (X,d)→(X,d′) is a continuous function on a compact domain, hence uniformly continuous. For every ϵ>0, choose δ>0 such that d(x,y)<δ implies d′(x,y)<ϵ. The same implication at every iterate gives
dn​(x,y)<δ ⟹ dn′​(x,y)<ϵ.
(13)
By its contrapositive, every ϵ-separated set for dn′​ is δ-separated for dn​. Consequently sn​(d′,ϵ)≤sn​(d,δ) for every n, and
hd′​(f,ϵ)≤hd​(f,δ)≤hd​(f).
(14)
Taking ϵ↓0 gives hd′​(f)≤hd​(f). Interchanging the two compatible metrics gives the reverse inequality. Therefore
hd′​(f)=hd​(f)=htop​(f).​
(15)
Compactness is used for uniform continuity, not merely for pointwise equivalence of the two metrics.

Let H:X→Y be a homeomorphism with H∘f=g∘H. This is topological conjugacy. Choose a compatible metric e on Y and pull it back to X:
dH​(x,x′)=e(Hx,Hx′).
(16)
Because H is a homeomorphism, dH​ is a compatible metric. The conjugacy identity implies Hfk=gkH for every k≥0, so the corresponding Bowen metrics satisfy
(dH​)n​(x,x′)=max0≤k<n​e(gkHx,gkHx′)=en​(Hx,Hx′).
(17)
The bijection H therefore transports separated sets in either direction without changing their separation, and sn​(dH​,ϵ)=sn​(e,ϵ). Their exponential growth rates, and then their limits as ϵ↓0, agree. Part (a) lets us replace dH​ by any compatible metric on X. Hence
htop​(f)=htop​(g).​

Write the hyperbolic toral automorphism as TA​([v])=[Av] on T2=R2/Z2, where A∈GL(2,Z). Its determinant is ±1. The eigenvalues cannot be a nonreal conjugate pair: their product would have absolute value one, forcing each to have absolute value one, contrary to hyperbolicity. The same product condition and hyperbolicity imply two distinct real eigenvalues, labelled so that
0<∣λs​∣<1<∣λu​∣,qquad∣λs​λu​∣=1.
(19)
We will prove
htop​(TA​)=log∣λu​∣.​
(20)
This is the entropy of a hyperbolic toral automorphism; the absolute value is important when the expanding eigenvalue is negative.
Choose real eigenvectors vs​,vu​ and the adapted norm ∥avs​+bvu​∥∗​=max(∣a∣,∣b∣). Its quotient metric on the torus is
d([v],[w])=minℓ∈Z2​∥v−w−ℓ∥∗​.
(21)
It is a compatible metric, so part (a) allows its use. Let ρ=min0=ℓ∈Z2​∥ℓ∥∗​>0 and D=∥A∥∗​=∣λu​∣. Fix ϵ>0 small enough that 2ϵ<ρ and (D+1)ϵ<ρ.
If [v] lies in the Bowen ball Bn​(0,ϵ), each TAk​[v] has a unique lift zk​ with ∥zk​∥∗​<ϵ. Uniqueness follows because two such lifts differ by a lattice vector of norm less than 2ϵ<ρ. Moreover, zk+1​−Azk​ is a lattice vector, and
∥zk+1​−Azk​∥∗​<(D+1)ϵ<ρ.
(22)
It must be zero. Thus zk​=Akz0​: there is no hidden lattice jump between successive lifted iterates.
Write z0​=avs​+bvu​. The simultaneous bounds ∥Akz0​∥∗​<ϵ for 0≤k<n are exactly
∣a∣<ϵ,qquad∣b∣<ϵ∣λu​∣−(n−1).
(23)
The stable coordinate is largest at time zero and the unstable coordinate at time n−1. Conversely, every vector in this rectangle satisfies all the Bowen ball bounds. The quotient is injective on this rectangle by 2ϵ<ρ. If J=∣det(vs​,vu​)∣, its normalized Haar measure is therefore
ν(Bn​(0,ϵ))=4Jϵ2∣λu​∣−(n−1).
(24)
The quotient metric is translation invariant and TA​ is linear, so dn​(x,y)=dn​(0,y−x); every Bowen ball has this same measure, independently of its centre.
Take an ϵ-separated set of largest cardinality sn​(d,ϵ). It is maximal under inclusion, so its radius-ϵ Bowen balls cover the torus: a point outside all these balls could be added. The covering bound gives
sn​(d,ϵ)≥4Jϵ21​∣λu​∣n−1.
(25)
Its radius-ϵ/2 Bowen balls are disjoint, by the triangle inequality for dn​. Their measures sum to at most one, giving
sn​(d,ϵ)≤Jϵ21​∣λu​∣n−1.
(26)
Taking logarithms, dividing by n, and letting n→∞ yields hd​(TA​,ϵ)=log∣λu​∣ for every sufficiently small ϵ. The limit as ϵ↓0 proves the answer. Contraction in the stable direction contributes no exponential growth; only expansion in the unstable direction does.

Let (X,B,μ) be a probability space, let T be a measure-preserving transformation, and let g∈L1(μ). The Birkhoff ergodic theorem asserts that
An​g(x)=n1​∑k=0n−1​g(Tkx)⟶g∗(x)=Eμ​[g∣I](x)
(27)
for almost every x, where I={B∈B:μ(T−1B△B)=0} is the invariant sigma-algebra. The limit is integrable, satisfies g∗∘T=g∗ almost everywhere, and has ∫g∗dμ=∫gdμ. On a probability space the convergence also holds in L1. No invertibility of T is required.
If T is ergodic, its invariant sigma-algebra is trivial modulo null sets, so its conditional expectation is constant. In that case the concise form is
An​g(x)⟶∫X​gdμfor almost every x.​
(28)

Identify the circle group with [0,1) modulo its endpoints, and let λ be normalized Lebesgue measure. We first check invariance. On the rth inverse branch, x=(y+r)/m for 0≤r<m. For an integrable function g, substitution gives
∫01​g(Em​x)dx=∑r=0m−1​m1​∫01​g(y)dy=∫01​g(y)dy.
(29)
In particular, testing indicator functions shows that the integer multiplication map on the circle preserves λ.
To prove ergodicity, let B be a measurable set with λ(Em−1​B△B)=0 and put h=1B​. Then h(Em​x)=h(x) almost everywhere. Its Fourier coefficients are
ck​=∫01​h(x)e−2πikxdx(k∈Z).
(30)
Using invariance of h and substituting separately on the same m branches gives
ck​​=∫01​h(Em​x)e−2πikxdx=m1​r=0∑m−1​e−2πikr/m∫01​h(y)e−2πi(k/m)ydy={0,ck/m​,​m∤k,m∣k.​​
(31)
In the last line, the finite geometric series of mth roots of unity is zero unless m divides k, when it equals m. For any nonzero integer k, divide repeatedly by m until the resulting integer is no longer divisible by m. The recurrence then implies ck​=0.
Since the Fourier basis is a complete orthonormal basis of L2([0,1),λ), the absence of every nonconstant Fourier coefficient implies h=c0​=λ(B) almost everywhere. An indicator function takes only the values zero and one, so λ(B) must be zero or one. This proves
Em​ is ergodic for Lebesgue measure for every integer m≥2.​
(32)

We first prove invariance, before using any assertion that requires it. Write An​φ(x)=n−1∑k=0n−1​φ(fkx). Choose a point whose forward orbit has the prescribed convergence against every continuous function; the hypothesis gives a set of such points of full measure. For each continuous function φ, telescoping gives
An​(φ∘f)(x)−An​φ(x)=nφ(fnx)−φ(x)​⟶0,
(35)
because a continuous function on a compact metric space is bounded. Both test functions are continuous, so their averages converge to their prescribed integrals. Hence
∫X​φ∘fdμ=∫X​φdμ
(36)
for every continuous function φ. The continuous-test criterion for an invariant measure therefore gives f∗​μ=μ. The points in the hypothesis can now be called generic points for an invariant measure.
For ergodicity, let B be a Borel set invariant modulo null sets, and set h=1B​, p=μ(B). Then h∘f=h almost everywhere. Since invariance preserves null preimages, deleting the union of all iterated preimages of the exceptional null set gives h(fkx)=h(x) for every k≥0 on one full-measure set. Thus An​h=h almost everywhere for every n.
Given ϵ>0, the continuous approximation of Borel indicators on a compact metric space, proved in the supporting steps below, supplies φ∈C(X) with 0≤φ≤1 and ∥h−φ∥L1(μ)​<ϵ. By the hypothesis, An​φ→∫φdμ almost everywhere. Since 0≤An​φ≤1, the dominated convergence theorem also gives convergence in L1(μ). Invariance and the triangle inequality give the uniform estimate
∥An​(h−φ)∥L1(μ)​≤n1​∑k=0n−1​∫X​∣h−φ∣∘fkdμ=∥h−φ∥L1(μ)​<ϵ.
(37)
Consequently,
∥h−p∥L1(μ)​​≤∥An​h−An​φ∥L1(μ)​+​An​φ−∫φdμ​L1(μ)​+​∫φdμ−p​<2ϵ+​An​φ−∫φdμ​L1(μ)​.​
(38)
Letting n→∞ and then ϵ↓0 proves ∥h−p∥1​=0. Explicitly,
∥1B​−p∥1​=p(1−p)+(1−p)p=2p(1−p),
(39)
so p∈{0,1}. Every invariant Borel set has measure zero or one, which proves
μ is invariant and ergodic.​
(40)
This proof controls measurable-set averages through a uniform L1 estimate; convergence only against continuous test functions is not silently assumed to include indicator functions.

Here is an alternative route for the ergodicity step using the Birkhoff ergodic theorem. After invariance has been proved, the theorem identifies the almost-everywhere limit of An​φ with Eμ​[φ∣I]. The hypothesis therefore gives
Eμ​[φ∣I]=∫φdμ
(41)
for every continuous function φ. For an invariant Borel set B, approximate h=1B​ in L1 by such a φ. Since h is measurable with respect to the invariant sigma-algebra, its conditional expectation is h itself. The L1 contraction of conditional expectation gives
∥h−μ(B)∥1​≤∥Eμ​[h−φ∣I]∥1​+​∫(φ−h)dμ​≤2∥h−φ∥1​.
(42)
Arbitrarily accurate continuous approximation forces h=μ(B) almost everywhere. Thus this use of the theorem reaches the same zero-or-one criterion as the direct averaging proof above, without assuming in advance that μ is ergodic.

For a Borel set B and ϵ>0, inner regularity supplies a compact set K⊆B with μ(B)−μ(K)<ϵ/2. Independently, outer regularity supplies an open set U⊇B with μ(U)−μ(B)<ϵ/2. Because K⊆U and the measure is finite,
μ(U∖K)=μ(U)−μ(K)<ϵ.
(43)
This creates an arbitrarily small measurable transition region between a compact inner approximation and an open outer approximation. The cutoff in the next step then approximates 1B​, rather than merely approximating the number μ(B).

For the compact set K⊆B⊆U chosen in the preceding step, the Urysohn lemma gives a continuous function 0≤φ≤1 equal to one on K and zero on X∖U. In a metric space this cutoff can also be written explicitly, when K and X∖U are both nonempty:
φ(x)=d(x,K)+d(x,X∖U)d(x,X∖U)​.
(44)
The two sets are disjoint and closed, so the denominator never vanishes, and their distance functions are continuous. If K is empty, take φ=0; if U=X and K is nonempty, take φ=1. These choices satisfy the required boundary conditions in the exceptional cases.
The difference φ−1B​ vanishes on K and outside U, and its absolute value is at most one everywhere. Therefore
∥φ−1B​∥L1(μ)​≤μ(U∖K)<ϵ.​
(45)
This is the precise continuous approximation required by both proofs of ergodicity, and also gives ∣∫φdμ−μ(B)∣<ϵ.
