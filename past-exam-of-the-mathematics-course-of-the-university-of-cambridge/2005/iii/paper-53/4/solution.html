An antilinear involution need not preserve norms, so the required physical assumption is that momentum reversal maps a complete normalized basis to a normalized basis. For a spinless sector take T^∣p⟩=η(p)∣−p⟩ with ∣η∣=1 and relativistic normalization ⟨p∣q⟩=(2π)32Ep​δ3(p−q). Any extra degeneracy labels must likewise be mapped by a norm-preserving permutation or unitary matrix. The condition T^2=1 requires η(−p)η(p)∗=1 in the scalar basis.
For arbitrary normalizable wave packets ∣ϕ⟩=∫dΠp​f(p)∣p⟩ and ∣ψ⟩=∫dΠp​h(p)∣p⟩, where dΠp​=d3p/[(2π)32Ep​], antilinearity conjugates their coefficients. Using the normalized reversed basis gives
⟨T^ϕ∣T^ψ⟩=∫dΠp​f(p)h(p)∗∣η(p)∣2=[∫dΠp​f(p)∗h(p)]∗.
(23)
Completeness proves ⟨T^ϕ∣T^ψ⟩=⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∗ for all states, so this quantum time-reversal operator is antiunitary. The same proof with sums over extra labels uses the unitarity of their reversal matrix.
For a Hermitian interaction-picture Hamiltonian, the condition of time-reversal symmetry is
T^V(t)T^−1=V(−t),V(t)†=V(t).​
(24)
Expand the scattering matrix as a Dyson series, using ordered integration rather than commuting operators:
S=1+∑n≥1​(−i)n∫t1​>⋯>tn​​dt1​⋯dtn​V(t1​)⋯V(tn​).
(25)
Conjugation by T^ conjugates (−i)n to in but preserves the product order. Substitute sj​=−tj​; the integration domain becomes s1​<⋯<sn​ and
T^ST^−1=1+∑n≥1​in∫s1​<⋯<sn​​ds1​⋯dsn​V(s1​)⋯V(sn​).
(26)
On the other hand, taking the adjoint of the original series conjugates its coefficient and reverses the operator order. Relabel s1​=tn​,…,sn​=t1​ in each adjoint integral; it is exactly the same series. Thus
T^ST^−1=S†.​
(27)
Equivalently, time ordering turns into anti-time ordering after reversing the time arguments. One can first use a finite symmetric time interval and the same time-symmetric switching prescription before taking the scattering limit; the argument never assumes [V(t),V(s)]=0.
Antiunitarity relates amplitudes by
⟨T^f∣T^ST^−1∣T^i⟩=⟨f∣S∣i⟩∗.
(28)
Using T^ST^−1=S† and taking a complex conjugate gives the time-reversal reciprocity of the scattering matrix
⟨f∣S∣i⟩=⟨T^i∣S∣T^f⟩.​
(29)
The reverse process starts with the reversed final state and ends with the reversed initial state. Intrinsic state phases can appear when these reversed states are rewritten in a separately chosen basis, but the corresponding squared amplitudes agree. Equal amplitudes do not mean identical numerical cross sections when the reversed process has different flux or phase-space factors.
For a spin-zero complex scalar field, choose the transformation convention T^ϕ(t,x)T^−1=ηT​ϕ(−t,x). Its mode expansion of a free field is
ϕ(t,x)=∫dΠp​[a(p)e−iEp​t+ip⋅x+b†(p)eiEp​t−ip⋅x].
(30)
Apply T^ to the expansion. Its antiunitarity conjugates both numerical plane waves, giving coefficients T^aT^−1 multiplying eiEp​t−ip⋅x and T^b†T^−1 multiplying e−iEp​t+ip⋅x. In ηT​ϕ(−t,x), replace p by −p to obtain precisely these same plane waves. Comparing independent modes proves time reversal of a complex scalar field:
T^a(p)T^−1=ηT​a(pP​),T^b†(p)T^−1=ηT​b†(pP​),pP​=(Ep​,−p).​
(31)
Taking adjoints gives T^b(p)T^−1=ηT∗​b(pP​). Positive energy is preserved, and an annihilation operator remains an annihilation operator. Time reversal does not exchange particles with antiparticles.
The coefficient ηT​ is an intrinsic time-reversal phase. Applying T^ twice gives T^2aT^−2=ηT∗​ηT​a, so ∣ηT​∣=1; it need not be restricted to ±1. Write ηT​=eiϑ and redefine a′=eiϑ/2a. Because the phase is conjugated by T^,
T^a′T^−1=e−iϑ/2ηT​a(pP​)=a′(pP​).
(32)
Thus the scalar intrinsic phase can be absorbed into the annihilation-operator convention. Redefining b′=e−iϑ/2b simultaneously implements the corresponding whole-field rephasing ϕ′=eiϑ/2ϕ and removes the phase from both mode transformations.
