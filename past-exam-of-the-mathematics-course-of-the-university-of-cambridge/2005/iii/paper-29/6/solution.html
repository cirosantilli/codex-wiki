The abc conjecture asserts that for every ϵ>0 there is Kϵ​≥1 such that coprime positive integers A+B=C satisfy
C≤Kϵ​rad(ABC)1+ϵ,​
(26)
where the radical is the product of the distinct prime divisors.
For a primitive positive Fermat triple, the three integers are pairwise coprime. Apply the conjecture to A=xn,B=yn,C=zn. Since x,y<z, rad(ABC)=rad(xyz)≤xyz<z3. Taking ϵ=1/6 yields zn−7/2≤K1/6​. For n≥4 and z≥2 this simultaneously gives
z≤K1/62​,n≤27​+log2​K1/6​.​
(27)
Thus there are finitely many primitive triples and exponents. The primitivity restriction is essential to this abc argument: a nonprimitive triple is a common multiple of a primitive one, and the conjecture normalizes that factor away rather than bounding it. Consequently the intended finiteness conclusion counts primitive triples, or scaling classes. The literal unrestricted Fermat assertion is true for the independent reason that Fermat's last theorem gives no positive solutions for n>2; it does not follow from the displayed primitive height bound alone.
There is a genuine exception to the second printed assertion. If a−b=c, then x=y=1 solves the equation for every n>2; for example (a,b,c)=(2,1,1) gives an infinite family. The correct uniform finiteness statement excludes max(x,y)=1.
For that corrected statement put H=max(x,y)≥2 and d=gcd(axn,byn). Since their difference is c, d∣c. Apply abc to byn/d+c/d=axn/d. Its radical is at most rad(abc)xy, and its largest term is at least min(a,b)Hn/c. Therefore
Hn−2(1+ϵ)≤min(a,b)cKϵ​rad(abc)1+ϵ​.
(28)
With ϵ=1/4, enlarge the fixed right side to D≥1. For n≥3 the exponent is at least 1/2, so
H≤D2,n≤25​+log2​D.​
(29)
This proves finiteness of all nonconstant-power solutions, without requiring x,y coprime.
For an unconditional proof, the diagonal case x=y=H≥2 satisfies (a−b)Hn=c and is elementary. If a=b, the unequal-variable gap is at least 2n−1, also bounding n. In the remaining case interchange variables and coefficients, changing the sign of c if necessary, to have 1≤X<Y and
AXn−BYn=δ,∣δ∣=c.
(30)
The nonzero real logarithmic form Λ=log(A/B)+nlog(X/Y) satisfies eΛ−1=δ/(BYn). Once BYn>2c, ∣Λ∣≤2c/(BYn), so log∣Λ∣≤−nlogY+Oa,b,c​(1). The two-logarithm Baker lower bound for a homogeneous linear form in logarithms, with the fixed height of A/B and height of X/Y at most logY, gives
log∣Λ∣≥−C(a,b)logYlog(2n).
(31)
Hence n≤C(a,b)log(2n)+Oa,b,c​(1) because Y≥2, and n is bounded. The omitted case BYn≤2c has bounded exponent and variables directly. For each of the finitely many exponents, aXn−bYn has at least three distinct projective roots. The stronger nonzero-value Thue theorem argument from question 5 therefore gives finitely many pairs, whether or not that binomial form is irreducible. This establishes the exponent bound for a binomial power equation and the corrected finiteness statement with no unproved hypothesis. The constant-power family remains the unavoidable exception.
