Write f(z)=∑n≥0​zln. The authoritative PDF has exponent ln; the geometric-series exponent in the converted TeX is a transcription error. On the unit disk this Fredholm series satisfies
f(zl)=f(z)−z.​
(10)
First it is transcendental as a function. If ζls=1, the terms with n≥s in f(ρζ) are the positive terms ρln and their sum tends to infinity as ρ↑1; the finite preceding sum stays bounded. Thus every root of unity of l-power order is a singularity. These roots are dense on the unit circle. An algebraic function has only finitely many possible branch points or poles, as seen from its defining polynomial's leading coefficient and discriminant. Hence f cannot be algebraic over C(z).
Suppose now that α is algebraic, 0<∣α∣<1, and a=f(α) is algebraic. For a positive integer N, a polynomial P(X,Y)∈Z[X,Y], nonzero and of degree at most N in each variable, can be chosen so that
G(z)=P(z,f(z))=O(zT),T=(N+1)2−1.
(11)
Indeed the first T Taylor coefficients impose T homogeneous integer equations on (N+1)2 coefficients. There is a nonzero rational solution, which can be cleared to an integer one. Functional transcendence ensures G is not identically zero.
Iterating the functional equation gives
f(αlk)=a−∑j=0k−1​αlj.
(12)
Thus βk​=G(αlk) belongs to the fixed field K=Q(α,a) and is nonzero for all sufficiently large k, since a nonzero analytic G has no sufficiently small nonzero zeros. Its analytic upper bound is
∣βk​∣≤CP​∣α∣Tlk.
(13)
Choose an integer q≥1 making qα,qa algebraic integers, let d=[K:Q], and choose M≥2 bounding every conjugate of α and a. Then qlkαlk and qlk−1f(αlk) are algebraic integers. Therefore
Dk​βk​ is integral,Dk​=qN(lk+lk−1).
(14)
For every embedding σ of K, the coefficient sum L(P) bounds the polynomial and gives
∣σβk​∣≤L(P)(k+1)NMN(lk+lk−1).
(15)
Taking the norm of the nonzero integral Dk​βk​ yields a nonzero rational integer, of absolute value at least one. Consequently
log∣βk​∣≥−NC0​lk−ON​(log(k+1)),C0​=(1+1/l)[dlogq+(d−1)logM].
(16)
On the other hand the upper bound is log∣βk​∣≤−Tλlk+OP​(1), where λ=−log∣α∣>0. Choose N so large that Tλ>NC0​, possible because T=N2+2N. Letting k→∞ contradicts the two bounds. This proves, by the Mahler method, f(α) is transcendental for 0<∣α∣<1​ at every algebraic α.
For the shorter rational-approximation argument at 1/2, truncate after n=m and write sm​=pm​/qm​ with qm​=2lm. The numerator is odd because its last summand is one and all previous summands are even, so this fraction is reduced. The tail satisfies
0<f(1/2)−qm​pm​​<2qm−l​.
(17)
The value is irrational: if it were P/Q, the positive difference from a truncation would be at least 1/(Qqm​), contradicting the tail bound for large m. If it were algebraic irrational and l≥3, choose 0<ϵ<l−2. Eventually the displayed upper bound is below qm−2−ϵ​ for infinitely many reduced fractions. The Roth theorem says that an algebraic irrational admits only finitely many such approximations. This contradiction proves transcendence directly. The argument does not cover l=2, because its exponent is not strictly larger than two; the Mahler proof does cover that case.
