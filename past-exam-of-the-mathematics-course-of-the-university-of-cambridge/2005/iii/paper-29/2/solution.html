An integer version of Siegel lemma is as follows. For M<N homogeneous linear equations Az=0, with integer matrix entries bounded in absolute value by A0​≥1, there is a nonzero integer solution satisfying
∥z∥∞​≤⌊(2NA0​)M/(N−M)⌋.​
(8)
Put the displayed integer bound equal to Q and evaluate the equations at every vector in {0,…,Q}N. There are (Q+1)N inputs and at most (2NA0​Q+1)M outputs. But 2NA0​Q+1≤2NA0​(Q+1) and (Q+1)N−M>(2NA0​)M, so the number of inputs is larger. Two distinct inputs have identical outputs; their difference proves the claim. This is the pigeonhole proof of integer Siegel lemma. Rational coefficients are handled by clearing denominators. Over a number field of degree d, expanding in a rational basis gives at most dM rational equations, so the same construction applies when N>dM, with the height of the resulting integer matrix controlling the bound.
Siegel's original arithmetic definition starts with an entire series E(z)=∑n≥0​an​zn/n!, with all an​ in a fixed number field. For every ϵ>0 there must be positive integers dn​ such that dn​a0​,…,dn​an​ are algebraic integers, and both dn​ and the absolute values of every conjugate of an​ are Oϵ​(nϵn). The growth condition ensures convergence everywhere. The stronger, now customary strict E-function definition uses bounds Cn+1 for a fixed C and also includes the requirement of a nonzero linear differential equation over Q​(z). In the original formulation, that differential condition is explicitly part of the system hypothesis in the theorem below. These definitions should be distinguished; the exponentials used here satisfy even the stronger conditions.
The Siegel–Shidlovsky theorem states that if a vector of E-functions satisfies f′=A(z)f, with A(z) a matrix of rational functions over Q​, then for every nonzero algebraic ξ which is not a pole of A,
trdegQ​​Q​(f1​(ξ),…,fs​(ξ))=trdegQ​(z)​Q​(z)(f1​(z),…,fs​(z)).​
(9)
In particular, algebraic independence of the functions implies algebraic independence of the values. The exclusion of zero is necessary: their Taylor values there are algebraic.
Take rationally independent algebraic γ1​,…,γs​ and fj​(z)=eγj​z. Their coefficients are γjn​, their conjugates grow exponentially, and powers of a fixed common denominator clear all coefficients through degree n. They satisfy the nonsingular diagonal system fj′​=γj​fj​.
To check functional independence, a polynomial relation among these exponentials, after clearing rational-function denominators, would be ∑m​Pm​(z)eλm​z=0 with distinct λm​=∑j​mj​γj​. Such an exponential polynomial cannot vanish identically. To isolate a nonzero term with exponent λ0​, apply ∏λ=λ0​​(D−λ)degPλ​+1. All other terms vanish, while the remaining polynomial is acted on by operators D+λ0​−λ, each injective on polynomials because its nonzero constant preserves a nonzero leading coefficient. Thus the remaining term is nonzero, a contradiction.
The theorem at ξ=1 now makes eγj​ algebraically independent. For arbitrary distinct algebraic exponents αi​, choose a rational basis of their span and divide its elements by a common denominator so that every αi​ is an integer combination of the resulting γj​. Then eαi​ are distinct Laurent monomials in eγj​. A linear relation among them becomes a nonzero polynomial relation after multiplying by a common monomial, contradicting independence. These particular E-functions therefore yield the full Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem.
