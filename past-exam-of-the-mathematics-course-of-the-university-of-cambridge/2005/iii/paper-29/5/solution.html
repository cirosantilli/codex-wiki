Use the following fixed-number form of a logarithmic estimate: for chosen logarithms of fixed nonzero algebraic numbers a1​,…,as​, a nonzero form Λ=∑bj​logaj​ with integer coefficients ∣bj​∣≤B satisfies ∣Λ∣≥c(2B)−C, for fixed positive constants c,C. This follows from the Baker lower bound for a homogeneous linear form in logarithms.
By the Dirichlet unit theorem, write units as x=ζ∏ϵjmj​​ and y=ξ∏ϵjnj​​, with finitely many roots of unity and fixed fundamental units. If the unit rank is zero, there are already only finitely many choices. Otherwise put B=max(1,∣mj​∣,∣nj​∣). The real logarithmic embedding of the free unit group has full rank and lies in the product-formula hyperplane. Equivalence of norms in that finite-dimensional space shows that a unit with exponent size B has some archimedean conjugate of modulus at most e−c0​B: a large positive logarithmic coordinate forces a comparably large negative one, and a large negative coordinate itself suffices.
Interchanging the two terms if necessary, choose such a conjugate for y. At this embedding the equation makes σ(αx)=1−σ(βy) with ∣σ(βy)∣≤C0​e−c0​B. For large B define the logarithm near one. It is nonzero because βy=0, and
0<∣Λ∣=∣log(σ(αx))∣≤2C0​e−c0​B.
(23)
On fixed branches it is
Λ=logσ(α)+logσ(ζ)+∑j​mj​logσ(ϵj​)+2klog(−1)

for an integer k=O(B). The bound on k follows from summing the fixed imaginary parts and then reducing to the near-one branch. There are only finitely many possible embeddings and roots of unity, so the stated lower bound applies uniformly with coefficients O(B). It contradicts the exponential upper bound when B is large. Hence the exponents are bounded and αx+βy=1 has finitely many unit solutions​ in OK×​. This is the unit equation over a number field proof; “unit” here means an algebraic-integer unit, not an arbitrary nonzero field element.
For the Thue theorem, let F∈Z[X,Y] be irreducible homogeneous of degree d≥3 and let m=0 be fixed. In a splitting field L, write F(X,Y)=a∏i​(X−θi​Y) with distinct roots. Choose a fixed integer c so that each cθi​ is integral and cdm/a is a nonzero integer. For a solution (u,v) the nonzero algebraic integers ti​=c(u−θi​v) satisfy ∏i​ti​=cdm/a. Their principal ideals therefore divide a fixed ideal and have only finitely many possibilities. For each occurring principal ideal choose a generator γi​, so ti​=γi​εi​ with εi​ a unit.
Any three distinct roots give the identity
(θ2​−θ3​)t1​+(θ3​−θ1​)t2​+(θ1​−θ2​)t3​=0.
(25)
Divide by the third term and absorb the finitely many generator ratios into the coefficients. This is a fixed-coefficient unit equation in ε1​/ε3​ and ε2​/ε3​, so it has finitely many solutions for each generator choice. Thus ρ=t1​/t3​ has finitely many values. The relation (1−ρ)u=(θ1​−ρθ3​)v fixes a rational direction whenever an integer solution exists; ρ=1 simply gives v=0. On each such direction, (u,v)=k(u0​,v0​) for a primitive integer pair, and kdF(u0​,v0​)=m allows finitely many k. This proves the desired finiteness. When m=0, irreducibility excludes a nonzero rational root direction, leaving only (0,0).
The argument also proves finiteness for any binary form with at least three distinct projective roots: irreducibility was used only to guarantee distinct roots and to dispose of the zero-value case. A rational invertible change of variables can move every projective root away from infinity; after clearing its fixed denominators, the resulting pairs lie in an integer lattice and the same proof applies. This stronger nonzero-value version will apply to the binomial forms in question 6.
