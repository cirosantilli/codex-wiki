Let C∗​(X) be the integral singular chain complex. Augment it by the homomorphism C0​(X)→Z sending every point simplex to one. The homology of this augmented complex is reduced homology: for a nonempty space, it agrees with ordinary homology in positive degrees and H0​(X) is the kernel of H0​(X)→Z. The relative homology H∗​(X,A) is the homology of C∗​(X)/C∗​(A); a relative cycle has boundary in A, and chains differing by a boundary or a chain in A represent the same class.
Excision theorem: if U⊂A⊂X and U⊂intX​A, the inclusion (X∖U,A∖U)↪(X,A) induces isomorphisms on all relative homology groups. In particular, for a nonempty CW subcomplex A, choose a neighborhood N deforming onto A, with the deformation fixing A. Such neighborhoods exist for CW pairs. Then N/A is contractible. The long exact sequence of a pair gives H∗​(X,A)≅H∗​(X,N) and H∗​(X/A,{∗})≅H∗​(X/A,N/A). Excision removes A and the collapsed point respectively; the resulting pairs (X∖A,N∖A) and ((X/A)∖{∗},(N/A)∖{∗}) are homeomorphic. Consequently
H∗​(X,A;Z)≅H∗​(X/A;Z).​
(1)
Here the nonempty-subcomplex condition is the usual quotient convention in this statement. If A=∅, use the pointed cofiber X+​ with a disjoint basepoint; the literal unpointed quotient X/∅=X would not give the asserted degree-zero identity.
For one embedded circle, its long exact sequence of a pair reduces to
0⟶Z⟶H2​(Σg​,A)⟶Zi∗​​Z2g⟶H1​(Σg​,A)⟶0,H0​(Σg​,A)=0.
(2)
A separating circle bounds a subsurface, so its homology class is zero. A nonseparating circle has a transverse closed curve meeting it once, showing that its class is nonzero and primitive. Thus i∗​ is either zero or an injection with torsion-free cokernel. The resulting possibilities are
[A]=0[A]=0​H2​(Σg​,A)Z2Z​H1​(Σg​,A)Z2gZ2g−1​​​
(3)
with all other relative groups zero. These ranks determine whether the circle is null-homologous.
For g≥2, a disk boundary and a separating circle bounding a once-punctured torus have the same relative groups. The first is null-homotopic; the second is not. For a direct verification, use the surface-group presentation ⟨a1​,b1​,…,ag​,bg​∣∏i​[ai​,bi​]=1⟩. Send a1​,b1​ to free generators u,v, send a2​,b2​ to v,u, and all remaining generators to one. The relation maps to [u,v][v,u]=1, so this defines a homomorphism to the free group. The separating circle represents [a1​,b1​] and maps to the nonempty reduced word uvu−1v−1; it is therefore not null-homotopic. This proves the requested failure to detect nullhomotopy. There is a necessary low-genus qualification: on Σ0​ or Σ1​, an embedded circle is nullhomologous exactly when it is nullhomotopic. On the sphere every circle bounds a disk; on the torus every essential embedded circle is nonseparating. The printed nondetection assertion therefore needs g≥2.
For two disjoint embedded circles let r be the rank of their image in H1​(Σg​), and let c be the number of connected components of the cut surface. Removing open annular neighborhoods and applying excision gives the compact cut surface W relative to its boundary. Poincare-Lefschetz duality gives H2​(W,∂W)≅H0(W)=Zc and H1​(W,∂W)≅H1(W), which is free. Thus there is no hidden torsion. The long exact sequence gives c=3−r, and the Euler characteristic of the pair is 2−2g, giving
H2​(Σg​,A)=Z3−r,H1​(Σg​,A)=Z2g+1−r,Hj​=0 (j=1,2).​
(4)
The possibilities are r=0,1,2: two separating circles give zero; a nonseparating circle with a disk boundary, or two parallel essential circles, give one; circles around two different handles give two. For genus zero only r=0 occurs; for genus one r=0,1 occur; for every genus at least two all three occur. This is relative homology of a surface modulo disjoint circles.

For integral singular cochains a∈Cp(X) and b∈Cq(X), define the cup product on an ordered simplex by
(a⌣b)(σ)=a(σ[0,…,p])b(σ[p,…,p+q]).
(5)
The same construction works over any commutative coefficient ring. Expanding the coboundary as the alternating sum of faces gives the Leibniz identity
δ(a⌣b)=δa⌣b+(−1)pa⌣δb.
(6)
Indeed faces deleting vertices before the split occur in the first sum, faces after the split occur in the second, and the two terms at the common vertex cancel with opposite signs. Thus the product of cocycles is a cocycle. Replacing a by a+δc changes the product with a cocycle b by δ(c⌣b); replacing b by b+δd changes the product with a cocycle a by (−1)pδ(a⌣d). Hence the product is independent of representatives and defines a well-defined product on cohomology classes.
With F2​=Z2​ coefficients, the real-projective-space cohomology ring is
H∗(RPr;F2​)=F2​[t]/(tr+1),∣t∣=1.
(7)
For n>m≥1, the pullback of the degree-one generator is either zero or t. The second choice would send the zero class tm+1 to the nonzero class tm+1∈Hm+1(RPn;F2​), contradicting the naturality of the cup product. Thus the generator pulls back to zero, and so do all its positive powers. For m=0 the target's reduced cohomology is already zero. Therefore every such map induces zero on reduced mod-two cohomology.
Suppose a continuous f:Sn→Rn had f(x)=f(−x) everywhere. Then
h(x)=∣f(x)−f(−x)∣f(x)−f(−x)​
(8)
is a continuous odd map to Sn−1 and descends to hˉ:RPn→RPn−1. For n≥2, a path from x to −x projects to a loop detected by the degree-one generator of the double cover. Its image lifts to a path from h(x) to −h(x), so is detected by the target's double cover as well. Thus hˉ∗ is nonzero in degree one, contradicting the preceding calculation. For n=1, an odd continuous map from the connected circle to S0 is already impossible. The case n=0 is immediate. Hence
There exists x∈Sn with f(x)=f(−x).​
(9)
This proves the Borsuk-Ulam theorem rather than assuming it.
For a closed cover A1​,…,An+1​, assume no member contains an antipodal pair. Apply the just-proved theorem to F(x)=(d(x,A1​),…,d(x,An​)), using any metric on the sphere. Empty sets can be assigned a constant positive coordinate. It gives equal distances at x,−x. If either point belongs to an Ai​ with i≤n, zero distance and closedness place both points in it, a contradiction. Hence neither lies in any of the first n sets, and both must lie in An+1​, another contradiction. This proves the Lusternik-Schnirelmann-Borsuk theorem for closed sets.
Four closed sets on S2 need not contain an antipodal pair. Take the four unit vertices vi​ of a regular tetrahedron centered at zero and define Ci​={x:x⋅vi​≥x⋅vj​ for every j}. These closed sets cover the sphere by maximizing the four scalar products. If x and −x belonged to Ci​, both inequalities would force all four scalar products to be equal. Since ∑vi​=0, they would all be zero, and because the vertices span R3, x would be zero. This is impossible on the sphere. Thus these four sets give the antipodal-free closed cover from simplex Voronoi cells counterexample.

For a locally compact space, compactly supported cohomology is
Hcq​(X;Z)=lim​K⊂X compact​Hq(X,X∖K;Z),
(10)
with transition maps induced by enlarging supports. In Rn closed balls are cofinal; their complements retract onto Sn−1. The relative cohomology long exact sequence and contractibility of Rn give
Hcq​(Rn;Z)={Z,0,​q=n,q=n.​​
(11)
This is compactly supported cohomology of Euclidean space; for n=0 it is simply the cohomology of a point.
For an oriented n-manifold without boundary, Poincare duality in its compact-support form states that capping with the locally finite fundamental class gives Hcq​(M;Z)≅Hn−q​(M;Z). If M is closed, the ordinary fundamental class [M]∈Hn​(M;Z) gives Hq(M;Z)≅Hn−q​(M;Z) and a perfect complementary-degree pairing over Q.
Choose an oriented embedded closed ball B in a connected closed oriented M. Collapse M∖intB to a point, identifying B/∂B with Sn using its orientation. The quotient map is continuous. By excision, the fundamental class maps to the local oriented generator in Hn​(B,∂B), and then to the generator of Hn​(Sn). Hence the collapse map has degf=1​.
The reverse direction is not always possible. More generally a degree-one map f:N→M between closed oriented manifolds induces an injective map on rational cohomology: if α=0, duality supplies β with ⟨α⌣β,[M]⟩=0, and
⟨f∗α⌣f∗β,[N]⟩=deg(f)⟨α⌣β,[M]⟩=0.
(12)
In particular there is no degree-one map S2→T2, since H1(T2;Q)=Q2 but H1(S2;Q)=0. This is a counterexample to an unconditional reverse assertion.
Finally let p:CP3→S4 be the given bundle projection. The standard cohomology ring of complex projective space is H∗(CP3;Z)=Z[h]/(h4), ∣h∣=2; the integral generator u of H4(S4) has p∗u=mh2 for some integer m. If a section of a fiber bundle s existed, p∘s=1 would give s∗p∗=1 by functoriality. But H2(S4)=0, so s∗h=0 and s∗(mh2)=0, contradicting s∗p∗u=u=0. The facts used here are the sphere cohomology groups, the projective-space ring just stated, and multiplicative functoriality of cohomology. Thus the bundle has no section, the cohomological obstruction to a section of the twistor bundle.

Let π:E→B be an oriented real vector bundle of rank r over a paracompact base. A Thom class is a class U∈Hr(E,E∖B;Z) restricting on every oriented fiber pair (Rr,Rr∖0) to its positive generator. The Thom isomorphism theorem states that this class exists uniquely with those fiber restrictions and that
Hq(B;Z) ≅ ​Hq+r(E,E∖B;Z),a⟼π∗a⌣U.​
(13)
Here B is identified with the zero section. Equivalently use the disk/sphere bundle pair. The compact-support version is Hcq​(B;Z)≅Hcq+r​(E;Z), with fiberwise compact support in the Thom construction. If the bundle is not oriented, the integral assertion requires the corresponding orientation local system.
For a closed co-oriented embedded submanifold Y of codimension r, co-orientation means an orientation of its normal bundle. A tubular neighborhood identifies a neighborhood of Y with that bundle. Its Thom class, transferred by excision, is a class in Hr(M,M∖Y;Z). For compact Y this maps into the direct limit defining compactly supported cohomology, giving εY​∈Hcr​(M;Z)​. The construction is independent of the tubular neighborhood and uses the chosen co-orientation. In an oriented ambient manifold it is the Poincare dual of the oriented submanifold. Here “closed submanifold” has the usual compact-without-boundary meaning; if it meant merely a closed subset and Y were noncompact, the supported relative class would still exist but need not have compact support.
We next prove the fixed-point implication without imposing an unprinted orientability assumption on M. Set
L(f)=∑k​(−1)ktr(f∗:Hk(M;Q)→Hk(M;Q)).
(14)
Use a finite triangulation of the closed smooth manifold. The elementary Hopf trace identity says that the alternating trace of a chain endomorphism equals its alternating trace on homology. To verify it, split each finite-dimensional chain space as boundaries, a complement representing homology, and a complement mapping isomorphically onto the preceding boundaries. The traces on these two boundary summands cancel in adjacent degrees, leaving exactly the homology traces. The cohomology traces equal the homology traces because the induced maps are dual over Q.
If f has no fixed points, compactness gives δ=minx​d(x,f(x))>0. Choose a triangulation K of mesh less than δ/4 and a sufficiently fine subdivision K′ admitting a simplicial approximation F:K′→K uniformly within δ/4 of f. The subdivision chain map s:C∗​(K)→C∗​(K′) sends each simplex to simplices inside it. For a vertex v of such a small simplex, d(v,F(v))>3δ/4, so F(v) cannot lie in the original coarse simplex. Consequently F#​s has zero diagonal coefficient on every coarse simplex. Its trace vanishes in every degree, while it induces f∗​ on homology. The trace identity yields L(f)=0. Taking the contrapositive proves
L(f)=0 ⟹ f has a fixed point.​
(15)
This is the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem, established here rather than invoked. For oriented M the same identity is the graph-diagonal formula for the Lefschetz number: L(f)=⟨(1,f)∗εΔ​,[M]⟩, which vanishes when the graph misses the diagonal. Finally, homotopic maps induce the same cohomology maps, so their Lefschetz numbers agree.

Take the double cover of S2 branched at 2g+2 points, with deck involution ι. One explicit construction cuts the sphere along g+1 disjoint arcs pairing the branch points and cross-glues two copies along the cuts. Its Euler characteristic is 2χ(S2)−(2g+2)=2−2g, so it is an oriented closed surface of genus g. Locally at a branch point the projection is z↦z2 and the involution is z↦−z; thus the involution is nonidentity and orientation-preserving, also when g=0.
The invariant rational cohomology of a finite surface action identifies with the cohomology of its quotient. For this action, triangulate the quotient with the branch points as vertices and lift the triangulation. Quotient cochains identify with invariant cochains, and averaging over the two deck transformations makes taking invariants exact over Q. Hence the invariant part of H1(S;Q) is H1(S2;Q)=0. Since ι2=1, its eigenvalues are ±1 and it is therefore −I on the 2g-dimensional H1. Its actions on H0 and H2 are both the identity. Thus
L(ι)=1−(−2g)+1=2g+2=0.​
(16)
Homotopy invariance and the result just proved show that every map homotopic to this involution has fixed points, for every genus.

Let P be a sphere with g+1 disjoint open disks removed, and form its double S by gluing two copies along the boundary. Then χ(S)=2χ(P)=2(1−g), so S is an oriented closed surface of genus g. The involution ι swapping the two halves fixes precisely the g+1 seam circles.
Use disjoint collars with coordinates (s,t)∈R/Z×(−η,η), where ι(s,t)=(s,−t). Choose a smooth even bump function χ(t) supported inside the collar, with χ(0)=1, and choose 0<ϵ<1. Define an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism R on each collar by R(s,t)=(s+ϵχ(t),t) and as the identity outside the collars. Scaling ϵ from zero gives an isotopy from the identity to R, so F=R∘ι is homotopic to ι.
A fixed point of F in a collar would satisfy −t=t, hence t=0. Its other coordinate would then require s+ϵ=s modulo one, impossible for the chosen ϵ. Outside the collars, F=ι exchanges the interiors of the halves and has no fixed points. Therefore ι has fixed circles but is homotopic to the fixed-point-free map F. This is the fixed-point-free perturbation of a doubled-surface reflection; the construction includes genus zero, where the double is a sphere.

For a based space (X,x0​) and k≥1, the homotopy group πk​(X,x0​) is the set of based homotopy classes of maps (Ik,∂Ik)→(X,x0​), equivalently based maps Sk→X. Concatenating along one coordinate defines its group operation; reversing that coordinate gives the inverse. The group is abelian for k≥2. The symbol π0​(X) records path components and is generally only a pointed set. An Eilenberg–MacLane space K(G,n) is a connected CW space with πn​=G and all other positive homotopy groups zero; for n>1 the group G must be abelian.
Projection onto coordinates gives
πk​(X×Y)≅πk​(X)×πk​(Y):
(17)
a map to a product is exactly a pair of maps, and a based homotopy to the product is exactly a pair of based homotopies. These identifications respect concatenation. Thus K(G,n)×K(H,n) has exactly the homotopy groups required of K(G×H,n). Taking CW models and using the allowed uniqueness of Eilenberg–MacLane spaces gives
K(G,n)×K(H,n)≃K(G×H,n).​
(18)
Let p be prime. Take S∞ in complex coordinates, with Cp​ acting by scalar multiplication by a primitive pth root of unity. This is a free action. Every map from a finite-dimensional sphere lands in a finite stage and becomes null-homotopic in a sufficiently higher sphere, so S∞ is weakly contractible; the Whitehead theorem for CW complexes makes it contractible. Therefore the infinite lens space S∞/Cp​ has contractible universal cover, fundamental group Cp​, and no higher homotopy: it is a model of K(Cp​,1).
Here is the group cohomology computation, including the ring structure. For the cyclic group generated by g, the standard free resolution alternates the maps g−1 and N=1+g+⋯+gp−1. Applying HomZ[Cp​]​(−,Fp​) makes both maps zero, so each cohomology degree is one-dimensional. The period-two identity chain map represents a class b in degree two, and cup multiplication by this periodicity class is an isomorphism from each degree to the degree two higher. For odd p, a nonzero degree-one class a has a2=0 by graded commutativity, while bj and abj are nonzero by periodicity. For p=2 the model is homotopy equivalent to RP∞, whose ring is the infinite version of that computed in question 2. Consequently the mod-p cohomology ring of a cyclic group is
H∗(K(Cp​,1);Fp​)={F2​[t],Λ(a)⊗Fp​[b],​p=2,∣t∣=1,p odd,∣a∣=1, ∣b∣=2.​​
(19)
The standard results used are the contractible-cover description of a K(G,1), its identification of singular cohomology with group cohomology, and the cyclic periodic resolution with cup-periodicity just stated.
The product equivalence and the assumed Künneth theorem over the field give
H∗(K(Cp​×Cp​,1);Fp​)={F2​[t1​,t2​],Λ(a1​,a2​)⊗Fp​[b1​,b2​],​p=2,p odd.​​
(20)
The tensor product is graded: for odd p, a1​a2​=−a2​a1​ and each ai2​=0. The dimensions in degree d are d+1: for p=2 count t1i​t2d−i​; for odd p the generating series is (1+t)2/(1−t2)2=(1−t)−2.
If an n-dimensional CW complex has exactly one cell in dimension n+1, then Cn+1(X;Fp​)=Fp​. Cellular cohomology is the quotient of the kernel of its outgoing coboundary by the image of the incoming one, a quotient of a subspace of this one-dimensional vector space. Thus
Hn+1(X;Fp​) is either 0 or Fp​.​
(21)
This one-cell bound on next-degree cohomology is valid even with infinitely many higher cells; the printed finite-per-dimension hypothesis is more than it needs.
Now a free finite-group action makes Sn→M=Sn/G a covering. For n>1 the sphere is simply connected, so π1​(M)=G, πi​(M)=0 for 1<i<n, and πn​(M)=Z. The covering also identifies every higher homotopy group with that of Sn. Use the homotopy-group effect of attaching higher cells: attaching (i+1)-cells, i≥2, preserves homotopy groups below i and quotients πi​ by the subgroup generated by the attaching classes and their fundamental-group translates. Attach a single (n+1)-cell along a generator of πn​(M); its translates do not require additional cells since the original generator already generates the underlying group. This kills πn​ without changing π1​ or the lower groups.
Successively in each higher degree attach cells representing generators of the remaining homotopy group. In the union every higher homotopy group vanishes, since a map and its relevant homotopy have compact domain and are handled at a finite stage. The result is a K(G,1) with exactly one (n+1)-cell. No finiteness assumption on the number of later cells is needed for the preceding cochain bound. If desired, one can retain finitely many per degree using the Serre finiteness theorem for homotopy groups: a simply connected CW complex of finite type has finitely generated homotopy groups. At each finite stage the universal cover is simply connected and finite type, since G is finite; choosing finitely many generators then preserves finite type inductively. The first stage needs just the stated πn​(Sn)=Z.
If G=Cp​×Cp​, the resulting K(G,1) would have dimHn+1≤1 by its single cell, but its already computed cohomology has dimension n+2>1 in that degree. This contradiction proves
G≅Cp​×Cp​for every prime p.​
(22)
This is the free sphere actions exclude elementary abelian subgroups of rank two obstruction.
