For integral singular cochains a∈Cp(X) and b∈Cq(X), define the cup product on an ordered simplex by
(a⌣b)(σ)=a(σ[0,…,p])b(σ[p,…,p+q]).
(5)
The same construction works over any commutative coefficient ring. Expanding the coboundary as the alternating sum of faces gives the Leibniz identity
δ(a⌣b)=δa⌣b+(−1)pa⌣δb.
(6)
Indeed faces deleting vertices before the split occur in the first sum, faces after the split occur in the second, and the two terms at the common vertex cancel with opposite signs. Thus the product of cocycles is a cocycle. Replacing a by a+δc changes the product with a cocycle b by δ(c⌣b); replacing b by b+δd changes the product with a cocycle a by (−1)pδ(a⌣d). Hence the product is independent of representatives and defines a well-defined product on cohomology classes.
With F2​=Z2​ coefficients, the real-projective-space cohomology ring is
H∗(RPr;F2​)=F2​[t]/(tr+1),∣t∣=1.
(7)
For n>m≥1, the pullback of the degree-one generator is either zero or t. The second choice would send the zero class tm+1 to the nonzero class tm+1∈Hm+1(RPn;F2​), contradicting the naturality of the cup product. Thus the generator pulls back to zero, and so do all its positive powers. For m=0 the target's reduced cohomology is already zero. Therefore every such map induces zero on reduced mod-two cohomology.
Suppose a continuous f:Sn→Rn had f(x)=f(−x) everywhere. Then
h(x)=∣f(x)−f(−x)∣f(x)−f(−x)​
(8)
is a continuous odd map to Sn−1 and descends to hˉ:RPn→RPn−1. For n≥2, a path from x to −x projects to a loop detected by the degree-one generator of the double cover. Its image lifts to a path from h(x) to −h(x), so is detected by the target's double cover as well. Thus hˉ∗ is nonzero in degree one, contradicting the preceding calculation. For n=1, an odd continuous map from the connected circle to S0 is already impossible. The case n=0 is immediate. Hence
There exists x∈Sn with f(x)=f(−x).​
(9)
This proves the Borsuk-Ulam theorem rather than assuming it.
For a closed cover A1​,…,An+1​, assume no member contains an antipodal pair. Apply the just-proved theorem to F(x)=(d(x,A1​),…,d(x,An​)), using any metric on the sphere. Empty sets can be assigned a constant positive coordinate. It gives equal distances at x,−x. If either point belongs to an Ai​ with i≤n, zero distance and closedness place both points in it, a contradiction. Hence neither lies in any of the first n sets, and both must lie in An+1​, another contradiction. This proves the Lusternik-Schnirelmann-Borsuk theorem for closed sets.
Four closed sets on S2 need not contain an antipodal pair. Take the four unit vertices vi​ of a regular tetrahedron centered at zero and define Ci​={x:x⋅vi​≥x⋅vj​ for every j}. These closed sets cover the sphere by maximizing the four scalar products. If x and −x belonged to Ci​, both inequalities would force all four scalar products to be equal. Since ∑vi​=0, they would all be zero, and because the vertices span R3, x would be zero. This is impossible on the sphere. Thus these four sets give the antipodal-free closed cover from simplex Voronoi cells counterexample.
