Take positive volumetric flow rate in the direction of increasing x. For an arterial pressure wave, the characteristic admittance of an arterial wave gives
qB​=Y(PB​E−−PBR​E+).​
(1)
The backward wave contributes the opposite flow sign. Write pj​ for each junction's complex pressure amplitude, suppressing the common eiωt factor, and put r=RY, Cβ​=cosβ, Sβ​=sinβ. The topology is the two paths K→L→N and K→M→N; their intermediate and common distal junctions each have a hydraulic resistance. The coordinate convention in the source puts the upstream end at −ℓ and the downstream end at zero in each segment. Consequently the wave transfer matrix of an arterial segment is
(pu​qu​​)=(Cβ​iYSβ​​iSβ​/YCβ​​)(pd​qd​​).
(2)
Equivalently qu​=Y(Cβ​pu​−pd​)/(iSβ​) and qd​=Y(pu​−Cβ​pd​)/(iSβ​), with their continuous limits understood when Sβ​=0.
Symmetry gives pL​=pM​=ps​. Distal mass conservation and the resistive relation QN​=pN​/R give
2iSβ​Y​(ps​−Cβ​pN​)=RpN​​,ps​=(Cβ​+2riSβ​​)pN​.
(3)
At L, the incoming flow supplies both its bed and segment D, so
pK​=(2Cβ​+riSβ​​)ps​−pN​.
(4)
Choose the incident amplitude PA​ at K. If PAR​ is the reflected amplitude in vessel A, pressure continuity equation gives pK​=PA​+PAR​, while inlet flow is 2Y(PA​−PAR​)=2Y(2PA​−pK​). Equating this to the two outgoing segment flows yields
ps​=eiβpK​−2iSβ​PA​.

QN​=PA​RY2eiβ(2RY+1)2+e−iβ(2RY−1)8e−iβ​.​

QN​∼R2PA​​e−2iβ,QM​=QL​∼R2PA​​e−2iβcosβ.​
(7)
These leading terms must not be used as nonzero denominators near a phase at which the normal intermediate flow vanishes.

Once C is removed, the remaining connected segments form the chain K→L→N→M, of total length 3ℓ. For RY≫1, each hydraulic resistance draws a small flow, so to leading order the far end at M is reflecting, with zero segment flow there. Successive wave transfer matrices of an arterial segment give
pN​=cosβpM​,pL​=cos2βpM​,pK​=cos3βpM​,qK​=iYsin3βpM​.
(8)

QMblocked​∼RD8PA​​,QNblocked​∼RD8PA​cosβ​,QLblocked​∼RD8PA​cos2β​.​
(9)
The modulus of D equals γ=∣3e4iβ+e−2iβ∣, because the expressions differ by a unit-modulus factor. Comparison with the normal flows gives the amplitude ratios
∣QNnormal​∣∣QNblocked​∣​∼γ4∣cosβ∣​,∣QMnormal​∣∣QMblocked​∣​∼γ∣cosβ∣4​,∣QLnormal​∣∣QLblocked​∣​∼γ∣cosβ∣4∣cos2β∣​.​
(10)
For small phases the cosines are positive and the absolute values can be removed. The original PDF prints an additional cos(β/2) in the middle numerator. That factor does not follow from the displayed equal-length network: the transfer relation from M to K above fixes pM​=8PA​/D, with no such factor. The corrected middle ratio is shown here.
Since γ2=10+6cos6β, one has γ=4[1−27β2/8+O(β4)]. Thus the three corrected ratios, in the order N,M,L, are
1+823​β2+O(β4),1+831​β2+O(β4),1+815​β2+O(β4).
(11)
All three exceed one for sufficiently small nonzero β; they equal one at zero frequency. This is an oscillatory arterial wave reflection effect, not an increase in steady conductance. Removing a branch changes the standing wave pattern and the total inlet pressure associated with a fixed incident wave. Reflected waves can enhance junction pressures and hence peripheral flow amplitudes. Neither total inlet pressure nor total inlet flow was held fixed, and no extra energy source is implied.

The first equation is mass conservation for an incompressible flow whose area may change: local area storage balances the divergence of the volume flux αu. The two left-hand momentum terms are local acceleration and convective acceleration, while −px​ is the driving pressure gradient and −R(α)u models viscous drag per unit mass. The pressure-area law of a collapsible tube expresses transverse wall balance using internal minus prescribed external pressure.
For example, choose a reference area A∗​, length L and velocity U∗​. If dimensional variables are A,s,td​,v,P, take
α=A/A∗​,x=s/L,t=U∗​td​/L,u=v/U∗​,p=P/(ρU∗2​).
(12)
The dimensionless external pressure uses the same scale. A dimensional friction rate rf​(A) becomes R(α)=Lrf​(A∗​α)/U∗​, and the dimensional wall pressure law is divided by ρU∗2​. These choices make both inertia and the pressure-gradient coefficient unity; other equivalent reference scales are possible. On the mechanically stable wall branch, increasing area requires increased transmural pressure, whereas an enlarged tube reduces resistance. Thus P′(α)>0,R′(α)<0​.

Use the pressure scale ρU2, and denote the scaled transverse velocity by v. The steady incompressible flow equations under the prescribed coordinate scaling are
ux​+vy​uux​+vuy​λ−2(uvx​+vvy​)​=0,=−px​+Rλ​(uyy​+λ−2uxx​),=−py​+λR1​(vyy​+λ−2vxx​).​
(27)
The no-slip boundary condition is u=v=0 at y=1 and at the lower wall y=ϵF(x), taking F=0 upstream. Far upstream, u→U0​=6y(1−y), v→0, and px​→−12λ/R. These are the dimensional Navier-Stokes equations divided by the streamwise and transverse inertial scales, respectively; the transverse equation's λ−2 is essential.
A consistent nonlinear wall-layer regime can be chosen as
Rλ​=ϵ3,R1/7≪λ≪R,ϵ≪1.​
(28)
Near a wall the oncoming plane Poiseuille flow is linear in distance, so a layer of thickness ϵ has u=O(ϵ) and v=O(ϵ2). Its inertia is O(ϵ2); its transverse viscous term is (λ/R)O(ϵ−1)=O(ϵ2), explaining the distinguished balance. The resulting pressure perturbation is O(ϵ2). In the core the induced transverse pressure variation is O(ϵ/λ2), negligible compared with that pressure perturbation when λ2ϵ≫1, equivalently λ≫R1/7. Also λ≪R makes both the indentation and the viscous boundary layer thin. Streamwise viscous derivatives are smaller still. Thus the core perturbation is inviscid at first order and pressure is common to the two wall layers at their leading order.
Write u=U0​+ϵu1​+⋯ and v=ϵv1​+⋯. The core pressure gradient only enters at order ϵ2, so first-order continuity equation and streamwise momentum give
u1x​+v1y​=0,U0​u1x​+U0′​v1​=0.
(29)
Eliminating u1x​ shows (v1​/U0​)y​=0. Let that ratio be −A′(x); integration in x, with the unperturbed upstream condition, then gives
u=U0​(y)+ϵA(x)U0′​(y)+O(ϵ2),v=−ϵA′(x)U0​(y)+O(ϵ2).​
(30)
This displacement form automatically has zero first-order change in total core flux, because ∫01​U0′​dy=0.
For the lower boundary layer, use
y=ϵ[z+F(x)],u=ϵU(x,z),v=ϵ2[V(x,z)+F′(x)U(x,z)],p=p0​(x)+ϵ2P(x),
(31)
where p0′​=−12ϵ3 is the smaller upstream pressure gradient. The extra term in v removes the velocity induced by the moving coordinate surface. The chain rule gives ∂x​∣y​=∂x​∣z​−F′∂z​ and ∂y​=ϵ−1∂z​; the wall-slope terms cancel from both continuity equation and convection. At leading order the Prandtl boundary-layer equation is
Ux​+Vz​=0,UUx​+VUz​=−P′(x)+Uzz​,U=V=0 at z=0.​
(32)
Matching to the core near the lower wall gives U∼6[z+F+A], so Hlower​=F+A.
For the upper boundary layer, set y=1−ϵz, u=ϵU, v=−ϵ2V. The same equations, wall data and common P(x) result, but the core matching gives U∼6[z−A], so Hupper​=−A. The two pressure-driven wall-layer problems have the same upstream shear, wall conditions and pressure forcing. Under the question's uniqueness assumption their solutions coincide, and their displacement limits must coincide. Thus the symmetric viscous interaction in an indented Poiseuille channel gives
F+A=−A,A=−F/2,Hlower​=Hupper​=F/2.​

U=x1/3G′,V=−x−1/3(32​G−31​ηG′).

Direct differentiation gives UUx​+VUz​=x−1/3[(G′)2/3−2GG′′/3], whereas −P′+Uzz​=x−1/3[−2pˉ​/3+G′′′]. The one-third-power similarity of an indented channel boundary layer is therefore governed by

For positive λ,α and 0<s<1, the constant term of the quadratic equation is negative. Its roots are therefore real with opposite signs, so one has positive growth. At s>1, both the coefficient of β and the constant term are positive. If the roots are real they are both negative; if complex they have negative real parts. Thus the growing band remains 0<k2a2<1​. Without surfactant, capillary pressure drives a surface flow towards a growing bulge. Advection compresses the insoluble surfactant in converging parts of that motion, increasing concentration and lowering surface tension. The resulting gradient supplies a tangential Marangoni stress that opposes the surface motion. This additional resistance slows capillary-driven redistribution of liquid even after the base-tension decrease has already been accounted for.
In the strong-elasticity limit, concentration gradients suppress tangential interface motion. The surface behaves as effectively immobile at leading order. Setting us​≈0 in the velocity formula gives σx​≈hpx​/2, hence
q≈−12μh3​px​,
(47)
one-quarter of the clean shear-free mobility h3/(3μ). This mobility reduction explains the quarter-rate limit; it slows the unstable capillary mode without removing the long-wave unstable band.
