The central problem of spectral geometry is how much of a Riemannian metric is determined by the eigenvalues, with multiplicities, of its Laplace-Beltrami operator. For a membrane with fixed boundary, the squared vibration frequencies are proportional to the Dirichlet eigenvalues. Kac's 1966 paper brought this inverse problem into focus: the full sequence of frequencies might encode a domain's shape, but recovering a few geometric quantities is much weaker than recovering an isometry class.
The first major line of development extracts geometric information from spectral sums. On a smooth closed d-dimensional manifold, the heat trace has the short-time expansion
Z(t)=∑j​e−tλj​∼(4πt)−d/2(Vol(M)+6t​∫M​RdV+⋯),
(1)
where λj​ are the nonnegative eigenvalues of P=−Δ and R is scalar curvature. The coefficients are heat invariants, formed by integrating local curvature expressions. They recover dimension, Riemannian volume and total scalar curvature. For smooth planar domains with the Dirichlet boundary condition,
Z(t)=4πtArea(Ω)​−8πt​Length(∂Ω)​+O(1).
(2)
Thus area and boundary length are audible. The constant term also contains topological information through Gauss-Bonnet theorem, with corner corrections when the boundary is polygonal. The Weyl law is the corresponding leading eigenvalue-counting result. Neither the leading term nor the whole list of local heat coefficients automatically reconstructs global geometry.
In the 1970s, the wave trace introduced a complementary link with global closed geodesics. The distribution
W(t)=∑j​eitλj​​
(3)
is spectral, while its singularities are governed by periodic geodesic motion. The 1975 Duistermaat-Guillemin work made this relationship precise. Its trace formula places singularities at geodesic lengths and computes their coefficients under suitable clean or nondegenerate hypotheses. When a coefficient is nonzero, the corresponding length is audible. Possible cancellation must be addressed before identifying the entire length spectrum with the singular support. This approach led to spectral rigidity questions, rather than just the recovery of integral curvature quantities.
The second main line constructs counterexamples to metric determination. A precedent was the 1964 flat-torus example: different sixteen-dimensional Euclidean lattices could give the same Laplacian eigenvalues. Subsequent lattice constructions lowered the dimension; Conway and Sloane's 1992 work exhibited four-dimensional Euclidean lattices with matching lattice theta series. For a flat torus Rd/Λ, the spectrum is the multiset 4π2∣ξ∣2 for ξ∈Λ∗, so equality of lattice theta series of the dual lattices gives isospectrality even when the Euclidean lattices are not related by an orthogonal transformation.
A systematic geometric construction arrived with the Sunada theorem in 1985. For a common Riemannian cover with finite isometry group T, almost conjugate subgroups have the same number of invariant vectors in each Laplacian eigenspace. Their freely acting quotients are therefore isospectral manifolds. This reduces an analytic equality to a finite-group representation identity. It produces many examples on surfaces and in higher dimensions, but nonconjugacy of the subgroups must be supplemented by an argument excluding extra quotient isometries. Transplantation theorem provides an explicit version: piecewise eigenfunctions are recombined by a fixed invertible linear transformation that respects the gluing and boundary conditions.
The planar problem itself was answered negatively by Gordon, Webb and Wolpert in 1992: noncongruent simply connected polygonal plane domains have equal Dirichlet spectra. This is stronger than counterexamples among abstract manifolds or flat tori, since these are genuine two-dimensional planar membranes. It does not settle every restricted version, such as asking for two domains within a specified smooth or convex class.
By the 1990s, isospectrality was also known to coexist with continuous metric variation and changes in local geometry. For example, a 1997 construction gives continuous isospectral families on products of spheres and tori whose members need not be locally isometric; in some examples the maximum scalar curvature changes. Conversely, rigidity and compactness results show that an isospectral class is not unrestricted. The 1988 surface compactness theorem makes fixed-spectrum sets of closed orientable surface metrics compact modulo diffeomorphisms. Compactness does not mean there is only one metric, or even finitely many metrics. By 2005, the subject therefore combined local heat invariants, global geodesic information, group-theoretic constructions, explicit transplantation and geometric rigidity. The spectrum determines substantial geometry, while generally failing to determine the full metric.
