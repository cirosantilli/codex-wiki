Use natural units ℏ=c=kB​=1 and the unreduced Planck mass MP​=G−1/2. Neutrino decoupling occurs when the weak interaction rate falls below the Hubble parameter. The weak-decoupling temperature estimate equates the given rate with the radiation-era expansion rate:
GF2​Td5​≃1.66g∗​​MP​Td2​​,Td​≃(GF2​MP​1.66g∗​​​)1/3≃1.8 MeV.​
(7)
Here g∗​ counts the effective number of relativistic energy degrees of freedom, and the Fermi constant is GF​≃10−5 GeV−2. The omitted reaction-rate coefficients make this an order-of-magnitude estimate: decoupling occurs at a temperature of order one MeV.
The charge-conserving channel is n+νe​↔p+e−, together with p+νˉe​↔n+e+. The positron sign in the printed neutron-capture channel violates electric charge conservation. Chemical equilibrium requires μn​+μνe​​=μp​+μe​. Negligible lepton chemical potentials therefore give μn​≃μp​. Since neutrons and protons are nonrelativistic particles, their Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution gives
ni​=gi​(2πmi​T​)3/2e(μi​−mi​)/T,np​nn​​=gp​gn​​(mp​mn​​)3/2e−(mn​−mp​)/T.
(8)
Both nucleons have two spin states, and their masses agree closely in the prefactor. Thus neutron-proton chemical equilibrium implies
np​nn​​=Xp​Xn​​≃e−Q/Td​,Q=mn​−mp​>0.​
(9)
The heavier neutron is suppressed by a Boltzmann factor.
For the deuterium equilibrium abundance, retain the paper's convention B=mD​−mn​−mp​<0; the positive nuclear binding energy is εD​=−B≃2.22 MeV. Chemical equilibrium of n+p↔D+γ gives μD​=μn​+μp​ because the equilibrium photon chemical potential vanishes. Using the nonrelativistic Maxwell--Boltzmann number density for each species and dividing gives
nn​np​nD​​=gn​gp​gD​​(mn​mp​mD​​)3/2(T2π​)3/2e−B/T.
(10)
Here gD​=3 and gn​=gp​=2. With the baryon-to-photon ratio η=nB​/nγ​, the photon number density nγ​=2ζ(3)T3/π2, and mD​≃2mN​, mn​≃mp​≃mN​, this becomes
Xn​Xp​XD​​≃π​12ζ(3)​η(mN​T​)3/2e−B/T≃8.14η(mN​T​)3/2eεD​/T.​
(11)
In this convention XD​=nD​/nB​ counts nuclei, so its contribution to the fraction of baryons in deuterium is 2XD​.
The deuterium bottleneck explains why helium-4 formation waits until T is far below εD​. At higher temperature, there are vastly more photons than baryons; even the dilute energetic tail can destroy newly formed deuterium. A crude estimate balances η−1e−εD​/T against one, giving T∼εD​/log(η−1)∼0.1 MeV for η of order 10−9. The power-law factors in the deuterium equilibrium abundance and the nuclear reaction rates refine this estimate. Once stable deuterium accumulates, further reactions can efficiently place nearly all surviving neutrons into helium-4.
Write rN​=nn​/np​ at the onset of nuclear burning. Each helium-4 nucleus requires two neutrons and contains four baryons. Neutron-limited helium synthesis therefore gives the primordial helium mass fraction
Y4​≃nB​4nHe​​≃2Xn​(TN​)=1+rN​2rN​​.​
(12)
For a realistic rough cosmological weak freeze-out estimate, rf​ is about 1/6 and free-neutron decay after freeze-out reduces it to about 1/7, giving roughly one quarter of the baryonic mass in helium-4. The survival relation that conserves baryon number is Xn​(TN​)=rf​(1+rf​)−1e−Δt/τn​; the corresponding rN​ is Xn​/(1−Xn​).
The rough neutrino decoupling rate by itself does not determine that one-quarter value. Substituting Td​=1.8 MeV literally into e−Q/Td​ would give r≃0.49 and Y4​≃0.65 before neutron decay. Neutron-proton chemical equilibrium persists to a lower temperature, approximately 0.7--0.8 MeV once the actual conversion rates are included. The rate scaling fixes the MeV scale; accurate abundances need the conversion coefficients and the delay through the deuterium bottleneck. This sequence is described in David Tong's discussion of nucleosynthesis.
Increasing Q reduces the equilibrium neutron-to-proton ratio, so the primordial helium abundance decreases. Holding the freeze-out temperature Tf​ and the decay survival factor fixed, the primordial helium response to the neutron-proton mass difference is
Y4​(Q)Y4​(1.1Q)​≃1+e1.1Q/Tf​1+eQ/Tf​​.​
(13)
For Q≃1.29 MeV and Tf​=0.7--0.8 MeV this is about 0.85--0.87, a reduction of roughly fifteen percent. A larger Q also changes neutron decay and detailed weak rates; their quantitative effects require more microscopic information. The sign from the Boltzmann factor is already clear.
