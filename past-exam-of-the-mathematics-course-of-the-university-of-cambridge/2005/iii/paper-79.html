The Briggs-Bers technique starts with a temporal Laplace transform and spatial Fourier transform of a localized disturbance. The temporal inversion contour must initially lie above every temporal singularity. Lower it while deforming the spatial contour away from the roots of the dispersion relation. A collision of spatial branches originating on opposite sides of that contour can create a spatial pinch point. A contributing pinch with Imω∗​>0 gives absolute wave-packet instability: exponential growth at a fixed position. Growth that is transported away while a fixed position decays is convective wave-packet instability. Solving D=Dk​=0 finds candidate double roots, but does not by itself establish a pinch.
Substitution of the normal mode gives
D(k,ω)=−iω−αk2−iβk3+γ=0,ω(k)=iαk2−βk3−iγ.​
(7)
For real wavenumber k, the temporal growth rate is
Imω(k)=(Reα)k2−(Imβ)k3−Reγ.
(8)
A nonzero real cubic coefficient in this expression makes it unbounded above on one end of the real axis. Once that coefficient vanishes, a positive quadratic coefficient also makes it unbounded above. Hence the bounded temporal growth condition for cubic dispersion is
Imβ=0,Reα≤0.​
(9)
The maximum is then −Reγ, attained at k=0 and, when Reα=0, at all real k. A finite upper bound is necessary for choosing the initial temporal inversion contour; arbitrarily fast high-wavenumber growth prevents the usual Briggs-Bers criterion from defining that causal inversion for generic localized data.
For β=0, the stationary candidates satisfy
ω′(k)=k(2iα−3βk)=0,
(10)
so
k∗​=0,ω∗​=−iγ;k∗(1)​=3β2iα​,ω∗(1)​=−i(γ+27β24α3​).
(11)
Each candidate must pass the spatial-branch pinch test before its imaginary frequency is used. In particular, under the finite-growth conditions the real-wavenumber evolution has multiplier modulus at most e−tReγ. For integrable transformed initial data this bounds its fixed-position amplitude by a constant times that exponential. Thus a further necessary condition is
Reγ<0.​
(12)
The false complex saddle in dissipative cubic dispersion illustrates why the second formal candidate cannot override this bound. For Reα<0, the neighborhood k=0 gives a fixed-position impulse response proportional to e−γt/t​: the cubic term is smaller on its k=O(t−1/2) scale. It therefore supplies the usual growing pinch when Reγ<0. The purely dispersive cases have stationary-phase algebraic prefactors instead. If β=0, only the quadratic stationary point remains; if both derivative coefficients vanish, the equation is local multiplication and its growth is directly e−γt.

The two-dimensional Orr-Sommerfeld equation then has the same transverse operator because k′2=q2, and the same viscous coefficient because k′R′=kR. Its boundary conditions on v and v′ are unchanged. Thus it has the same eigenfunction and the same phase-speed eigenvalue.
If the original disturbance grows, Imc>0, so the transformed disturbance also grows. Since R′≤R, every unstable three-dimensional mode maps to an unstable two-dimensional mode at a no larger Reynolds number. It follows that the minimum critical Reynolds number is attained among two-dimensional disturbances. This is the Squire theorem for temporal normal-mode onset, not a statement that three-dimensional transient amplification is impossible. The case k=0 is treated separately without dividing by k; its streamwise-uniform viscous normal modes decay, although coupling can produce transient growth.
