For these linear systems of divisors, take a complex K3 surface X and a nef big divisor D, so D2>0. The exceptional terminology refers to the complete system ∣D∣, rather than to an arbitrary subsystem.
A monogonal linear system on a K3 surface has the form
D=mE+Γ,m≥2,E2=0,EΓ=1,Γ2=−2,∣D∣=Γ+∣mE∣,
(33)
where ∣E∣ is an elliptic pencil on a K3 surface and Γ is its section. Its intersection with an elliptic fibre is one, and its fixed part is Γ. For a concrete existence example, take the smooth Fermat quartic surface X⊆P3 and two skew lines on it. Such lines can be written as
Γ0​: x0​=ζx1​, x2​=ηx3​,Γ: x0​=ζ′x1​, x2​=η′x3​,
(34)
with ζ4=(ζ′)4=η4=(η′)4=−1, ζ=ζ′ and η=η′. The equations show that they are disjoint, and the four partial derivatives show that the quartic is smooth. The adjunction formula gives KX​=0, and the hypersurface exact sequence of sheaves 0→OP3​(−4)→OP3​→OX​→0, together with the intermediate cohomology vanishing of these twists, gives H1(X,OX​)=0. Thus it is a K3 surface, and each line has square −2. If H is the hyperplane class, the planes through Γ0​ cut out Γ0​ plus a residual cubic pencil ∣E∣, E=H−Γ0​. This pencil has no base point off Γ0​. On Γ0​, its two local residual equations are the two normal derivatives of the quartic equation; they cannot vanish simultaneously because X is smooth. Thus it is basepoint-free, with
E2=4−2−2=0,EΓ=1.
(35)
The degree-one map of Γ to the pencil base makes it a section and forces connected fibres. Bertini smoothness theorem and adjunction formula then make a general fibre a smooth genus-one curve.
Now choose D=mE+Γ. For every irreducible curve other than Γ, both E and the effective curve Γ have nonnegative intersection with it. Also DΓ=m−2≥0, so D is nef, and D2=2m−2>0. On a general elliptic fibre, the restriction of OX​(D) is the degree-one bundle of the section point. Its unique section vanishes at that point. Every global section of OX​(D) therefore vanishes along a dense subset of Γ, hence along Γ itself. Removing this fixed divisor gives exactly ∣mE∣. This verifies the monogonal example directly; its general divisor is a section plus m fibres.
A hyperelliptic linear system on a K3 surface is a basepoint-free nef big system whose morphism is generically two-to-one. Equivalently its general smooth curve is hyperelliptic. Let π:X→P2 be a double cover branched over a smooth sextic, and put D=π∗O(1). The double-cover construction gives
π∗​OX​=O⊕O(−3),KX​=π∗(KP2​+3H)=0,H1(X,OX​)=0.
(36)
Thus X is a K3 surface. Further,
D2=2,H0(X,D)=H0(P2,O(1)),
(37)
since the second summand contributes H0(O(−2))=0. The map given by ∣D∣ is precisely the double cover. A general member is the double cover of a line branched at six points, so Riemann-Hurwitz formula gives genus two. This is the hyperelliptic example.
A trigonal linear system on a K3 surface is a basepoint-free nonhyperelliptic nef big system whose general smooth curve has gonality three. For an example, take the same smooth quartic with a line Γ0​ and the hyperplane system ∣H∣. Its general member is a smooth plane quartic of genus three; adjunction formula identifies its plane embedding with its canonical map, so it is not hyperelliptic. The elliptic pencil E=H−Γ0​ has
H⋅E=4−1=3.
(38)
Its restriction to a general hyperplane curve is a basepoint-free degree-three pencil. The curve is neither rational nor hyperelliptic, so its gonality is exactly three. This verifies the trigonal example.
Thus the three examples exhibit respectively a fixed section with elliptic fibres, a double-plane map, and an embedded quartic with trigonal hyperplane sections. The last example also illustrates why low-genus trigonal sections should not be confused with a blanket numerical criterion for every higher-genus model.
