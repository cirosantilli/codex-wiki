Use natural units with c=1, write H=a˙/a, and measure cosmic time from the Big Bang. The cosmological continuity equation follows by differentiating the Friedmann equation and using the Friedmann acceleration equation; for pressureless matter it gives ρ˙​+3Hρ=0, hence ρ=ρ0​a−3 when the present scale factor is a0​=1. The present critical density and cosmological density parameter give
ρc0​=8πG3H02​​,ρ0​=Ω0​ρc0​,k=H02​(Ω0​−1)>0.
(1)
Consequently, with C=H02​Ω0​, the Friedmann equation becomes a˙2=C/a−k.
Introduce conformal time through dt=adτ. A prime denotes a derivative with respect to τ, so a′=aa˙ and
(a′)2=Ca−ka2=k[(2kC​)2−(a−2kC​)2].
(2)
Set u=k​τ, with u=0 at the Big Bang. On the expanding branch the substitution a=C(1−cosu)/(2k) satisfies this equation. Integrating dt=adu/k​, with t(0)=0, gives the closed matter-dominated Friedmann solution:
a(τ)=2(Ω0​−1)Ω0​​(1−cosu),t(τ)=2H0​(Ω0​−1)3/2Ω0​​(u−sinu),u=k​τ.​
(3)
The parametrization continues smoothly through the maximum scale factor, reached at u=π, onto the contracting branch. Its curve in the (t,a) plane is a rescaled cycloid.
For a presently expanding universe, H0​>0 selects 0<u0​<π. The normalization a(u0​)=1 gives
cosu0​=Ω0​2​−1,sinu0​=Ω0​2Ω0​−1​​,u0​=arccos(Ω0​2​−1).
(4)
Substitution yields the age of a closed dust universe:
t0​=2H0​(Ω0​−1)3/2Ω0​​[arccos(Ω0​2​−1)−Ω0​2Ω0​−1​​].​
(5)
The first later zero of the scale factor occurs at u=2π, so the lifetime of a closed dust universe gives the Big Crunch time
tBC​=H0​(Ω0​−1)3/2πΩ0​​.​
(6)
This is the total lifetime measured from the Big Bang; the remaining cosmic time is tBC​−t0​. As a check, the limit Ω0​↓1 gives H0​t0​→2/3, while the Big Crunch recedes to infinite time, as expected for a flat matter-dominated universe with zero cosmological constant.

Use natural units ℏ=c=kB​=1 and the unreduced Planck mass MP​=G−1/2. Neutrino decoupling occurs when the weak interaction rate falls below the Hubble parameter. The weak-decoupling temperature estimate equates the given rate with the radiation-era expansion rate:
GF2​Td5​≃1.66g∗​​MP​Td2​​,Td​≃(GF2​MP​1.66g∗​​​)1/3≃1.8 MeV.​
(7)
Here g∗​ counts the effective number of relativistic energy degrees of freedom, and the Fermi constant is GF​≃10−5 GeV−2. The omitted reaction-rate coefficients make this an order-of-magnitude estimate: decoupling occurs at a temperature of order one MeV.
The charge-conserving channel is n+νe​↔p+e−, together with p+νˉe​↔n+e+. The positron sign in the printed neutron-capture channel violates electric charge conservation. Chemical equilibrium requires μn​+μνe​​=μp​+μe​. Negligible lepton chemical potentials therefore give μn​≃μp​. Since neutrons and protons are nonrelativistic particles, their Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution gives
ni​=gi​(2πmi​T​)3/2e(μi​−mi​)/T,np​nn​​=gp​gn​​(mp​mn​​)3/2e−(mn​−mp​)/T.
(8)
Both nucleons have two spin states, and their masses agree closely in the prefactor. Thus neutron-proton chemical equilibrium implies
np​nn​​=Xp​Xn​​≃e−Q/Td​,Q=mn​−mp​>0.​
(9)
The heavier neutron is suppressed by a Boltzmann factor.
For the deuterium equilibrium abundance, retain the paper's convention B=mD​−mn​−mp​<0; the positive nuclear binding energy is εD​=−B≃2.22 MeV. Chemical equilibrium of n+p↔D+γ gives μD​=μn​+μp​ because the equilibrium photon chemical potential vanishes. Using the nonrelativistic Maxwell--Boltzmann number density for each species and dividing gives
nn​np​nD​​=gn​gp​gD​​(mn​mp​mD​​)3/2(T2π​)3/2e−B/T.
(10)
Here gD​=3 and gn​=gp​=2. With the baryon-to-photon ratio η=nB​/nγ​, the photon number density nγ​=2ζ(3)T3/π2, and mD​≃2mN​, mn​≃mp​≃mN​, this becomes
Xn​Xp​XD​​≃π​12ζ(3)​η(mN​T​)3/2e−B/T≃8.14η(mN​T​)3/2eεD​/T.​
(11)
In this convention XD​=nD​/nB​ counts nuclei, so its contribution to the fraction of baryons in deuterium is 2XD​.
The deuterium bottleneck explains why helium-4 formation waits until T is far below εD​. At higher temperature, there are vastly more photons than baryons; even the dilute energetic tail can destroy newly formed deuterium. A crude estimate balances η−1e−εD​/T against one, giving T∼εD​/log(η−1)∼0.1 MeV for η of order 10−9. The power-law factors in the deuterium equilibrium abundance and the nuclear reaction rates refine this estimate. Once stable deuterium accumulates, further reactions can efficiently place nearly all surviving neutrons into helium-4.
Write rN​=nn​/np​ at the onset of nuclear burning. Each helium-4 nucleus requires two neutrons and contains four baryons. Neutron-limited helium synthesis therefore gives the primordial helium mass fraction
Y4​≃nB​4nHe​​≃2Xn​(TN​)=1+rN​2rN​​.​
(12)
For a realistic rough cosmological weak freeze-out estimate, rf​ is about 1/6 and free-neutron decay after freeze-out reduces it to about 1/7, giving roughly one quarter of the baryonic mass in helium-4. The survival relation that conserves baryon number is Xn​(TN​)=rf​(1+rf​)−1e−Δt/τn​; the corresponding rN​ is Xn​/(1−Xn​).
The rough neutrino decoupling rate by itself does not determine that one-quarter value. Substituting Td​=1.8 MeV literally into e−Q/Td​ would give r≃0.49 and Y4​≃0.65 before neutron decay. Neutron-proton chemical equilibrium persists to a lower temperature, approximately 0.7--0.8 MeV once the actual conversion rates are included. The rate scaling fixes the MeV scale; accurate abundances need the conversion coefficients and the delay through the deuterium bottleneck. This sequence is described in David Tong's discussion of nucleosynthesis.
Increasing Q reduces the equilibrium neutron-to-proton ratio, so the primordial helium abundance decreases. Holding the freeze-out temperature Tf​ and the decay survival factor fixed, the primordial helium response to the neutron-proton mass difference is
Y4​(Q)Y4​(1.1Q)​≃1+e1.1Q/Tf​1+eQ/Tf​​.​
(13)
For Q≃1.29 MeV and Tf​=0.7--0.8 MeV this is about 0.85--0.87, a reduction of roughly fifteen percent. A larger Q also changes neutron decay and detailed weak rates; their quantitative effects require more microscopic information. The sign from the Boltzmann factor is already clear.

Use natural units and the unreduced Planck mass MP​=G−1/2; it is 8π​ times the reduced Planck mass. A homogeneous canonical inflaton has ρϕ​=ϕ˙​2/2+V and Pϕ​=ϕ˙​2/2−V. Vacuum domination by a scalar field requires ϕ˙​i2​/2≪V(ϕi​), with other matter and the curvature term negligible in the Friedmann equation. The equation-of-state parameter is then near −1, and the Friedmann acceleration equation gives accelerating expansion.
For sustained slow-roll inflation, Hubble friction must dominate the inflaton acceleration: ∣ϕ¨​∣≪3H∣ϕ˙​∣, with the scalar potential changing only slightly in one Hubble time. In terms of the potential slow-roll parameter and second potential slow-roll parameter, this requires
ϵV​=16πMP2​​(VV′​)2≪1,∣ηV​∣=​8πMP2​​VV′′​​≪1.
(14)
For the quartic scalar potential,
ϵV​=πϕ2MP2​​,ηV​=2πϕ23MP2​​.
(15)
Thus ∣ϕi​∣≫MP​ supplies a broad slow-roll range when λ≪1. At an initial density of order the Planck density, potential domination gives
ϕi2​∼λ​2MP2​​,Vi​∼MP4​,​
(16)
so both potential slow-roll parameters are small for small λ. Small initial kinetic energy is a separate condition; the height of the scalar potential alone does not enforce it. These classical equations describe the post-Planck evolution under the model assumptions, rather than resolving quantum gravity at the initial endpoint.
Take ϕi​>0; the negative branch follows by the symmetry ϕ↦−ϕ. Dropping kinetic energy from the Friedmann equation and acceleration from the inflaton equation of motion gives
H≃MP​1​32πλ​​ϕ2,3Hϕ˙​≃−λϕ3.
(17)
Writing b=MP​λ/(6π)​, the quartic-potential slow-roll solution is
ϕ(t)≃ϕi​e−b(t−ti​),a(t)≃ai​exp[MP2​πϕi2​​(1−e−2b(t−ti​))].​
(18)
Indeed, ϕ˙​≃−bϕ, and integrating H=a˙/a gives log(a/ai​)=π(ϕi2​−ϕ2)/MP2​. These expressions apply during slow-roll inflation; their formal late-time saturation must not be used beyond the end of that approximation.
The exact end of accelerating expansion is ϵH​=−H˙/H2=1, equivalently ϕ˙​2=V for the scalar-dominated flat model. The quartic-inflation endpoint estimate uses the leading potential condition ϵV​≃1, giving
∣ϕe​∣∼MP​,∣ϕe​∣≃π​MP​​ using ϵV​=1.​
(19)
The numerical coefficient is an endpoint estimate: the slow-roll approximation breaks down there. Imposing ∣ηV​∣=1 estimates when slow roll loses accuracy and gives a different order-one coefficient, not a different exact acceleration condition.
The slow-roll e-fold count is
N≃MP2​8π​∫ϕe​ϕi​​V′V​dϕ=MP2​π​(ϕi2​−ϕe2​).
(20)
The quartic-inflation expansion from Planck density combines this initial estimate with the conventional endpoint to give
N∼λ​2π​−1,ai​ae​​∼exp(λ​2π​−1).​
(21)
For small λ the dominant term is enormous; the order-one endpoint uncertainty does not affect that conclusion.
To estimate the reheating temperature, assume prompt, efficient reheating converts the end-of-inflation energy into a thermal radiation bath. With the same endpoint convention, Ve​≃λMP4​/(4π2), and equating this to the radiation energy density gives
30π2​g∗​TR4​∼4π2λMP4​​,TR​∼(2π4g∗​15λ​)1/4MP​∼λ1/4g∗−1/4​MP​.​
(22)
The quartic-inflation prompt-reheating estimate fixes the scaling; the endpoint kinetic energy changes the coefficient by order one. Without an inflaton coupling and its thermalization history, V alone cannot specify the actual reheating temperature.
Finally, during cosmic inflation the comoving Hubble radius decreases:
dtd​aH1​=−a1−ϵH​​<0.
(23)
An initially causally connected patch is enlarged enough that the present observable region can lie within it. Its portions may subsequently be far outside one another's Hubble radii while retaining their common earlier thermal history. This solves the horizon problem if the number of e-folds is sufficiently large, conventionally of order sixty for the relevant thermal history; N∼2π/λ​ can readily exceed that. The necessary count depends on reheating and later expansion. The causal mechanism and the conversion back to a hot universe are discussed in David Tong's account of inflation.

Use natural units, assume negligible neutrino chemical potentials, and approximate neutrino decoupling as occurring before electron-positron annihilation in cosmology. At high temperature, weak interactions maintain a common temperature for neutrinos, antineutrinos, Electrons, Positrons and photons. Since Γν​/H∝T3, the reaction rate eventually loses against expansion. Decoupled relativistic neutrinos then free stream; their momenta redshift as a−1, so their distribution retains a temperature parameter satisfying aTν​=constant.
The still-coupled electromagnetic bath receives the entropy of annihilating Electrons and Positrons. Its effective number of relativistic entropy degrees of freedom is initially
g∗sEM​=2+87​(2+2)=211​,
(24)
where the two photon polarizations count as bosonic states and the four electron-positron spin states have the fermion entropy weight 7/8. After the pairs disappear, g∗sEM​=2. Cosmological entropy conservation in this coupled bath gives g∗sEM​(aTγ​)3=constant. The three already decoupled neutrino species are excluded from this bath's entropy count. At decoupling Tν​=Tγ​, hence
Tγ​Tν​​=(11/22​)1/3=(114​)1/3≃0.714.​
(25)
Subsequent expansion preserves this ratio in the idealized history. Residual interactions during annihilation give small corrections to the instantaneous-decoupling approximation. If a relic species later becomes massive, Tν​ here remains the redshifting momentum-distribution parameter; it need not be its nonrelativistic kinetic temperature.
A single ordinary neutrino species includes one populated neutrino helicity and one antineutrino helicity. For negligible chemical potential, integration of the Fermi-Dirac distribution gives
nν​+nνˉ​=2π23ζ(3)​Tν3​,nγ​=π22ζ(3)​Tγ3​.
(26)
Therefore the number density of a relativistic neutrino relative to photons, with the antiparticle included, is
nγ​nν​+nνˉ​​=43​(Tγ​Tν​​)3=113​.​
(27)
The factor 3/4 is the fermionic number-density weight, distinct from the energy and entropy weight 7/8. This relation continues to hold after this species becomes nonrelativistic if its comoving particle number is conserved.
Under the proposed matter-dominated, spatially flat model, the Friedmann equation requires the present relic-neutrino energy density to be approximately the critical density. Using the unreduced Planck mass MP​=G−1/2 gives
ρc0​=8π3H02​MP2​​≃mν​(nν​+nνˉ​).
(28)
Thus the critical-density mass of a thermal relic neutrino is
mν​≃16ζ(3)11π​Tγ03​H02​MP2​​.​
(29)
Taking the paper's rounded values, Tγ0​=2×10−13 GeV, H0​=h/(4.5×1041 GeV−1) and MP​∼1019 GeV, gives
mν​∼1.1×10−7h2 GeV∼102h2 eV.​
(30)
For 0.5<h<1, this is of order 30--100 eV with these approximate constants. More precise constants change the coefficient, not the requested mass scale. This mass is much larger than the present momentum scale Tν0​≃1.4×10−4 eV, validating the nonrelativistic energy density approximation. The result assumes that one thermal relic species dominates the matter density; it is conditional on that model rather than a measurement of a neutrino mass.
