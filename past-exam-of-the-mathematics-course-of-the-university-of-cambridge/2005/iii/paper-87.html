Work over the finite field F2​, and write Ai​ for row i of the PDF's 12×12 matrix. The first row has Hamming weight eleven; all other rows have Hamming weight seven. To check their intersections economically, index the last eleven columns modulo eleven. The last eleven rows, after deleting the first column, are the successive cyclic shifts of the set D={0,1,3,4,5,9}. Direct calculation gives
tD∩(D+t)​1{1,4,5}​2{0,3,5}​3{1,3,4}​4{4,5,9}​5{3,5,9}​​.
(1)
The intersection sizes for −t equal those for t. Thus two different rows among A2​,…,A12​ meet in four positions, including their shared first coordinate, whereas A1​ meets each of these rows in six positions. All these intersection sizes are even numbers, while each row weight is an odd number. Consequently AAT=I. The first row and column agree, and the entry of the last eleven-by-eleven block depends only on the sum of its row and column indices modulo eleven, because successive rows are left shifts. Thus A is a symmetric matrix, so A2=I.
The generator matrix G=(I∣A) has rank twelve, and
GGT=I+AAT=0.
(2)
Hence its linear code C lies in its dual code C⊥. Both have dimension twelve, so C=C⊥: it is a self-dual code. Moreover AG=(A∣A2)=(A∣I), and A is an invertible matrix, so this is another generator matrix for the same code. In the column parity-check matrix convention of the question,
C=C⊥,G′=(A∣I),H=(IA​),GH=0.​
(3)
The matrix H has rank twelve, so its nullspace condition cH=0 characterizes C exactly.
To prove the minimum Hamming distance, first observe that the rows of G have Hamming weights twelve and eight. For binary vectors,
wt(v+w)=wt(v)+wt(w)−2∣supp(v)∩supp(w)∣.
(4)
Every partial sum of generator rows is orthogonal to every further generator row, by GGT=0. Its intersection with that row therefore contains an even number of positions. Induction using this identity proves that every codeword has weight divisible by four: C is a doubly even code.
The systematic-matrix proof of extended Golay distance now excludes weight four. Write a codeword as (u,uA) and put k=wt(u). If its total weight were four, 1≤k≤4. For k=1, the right half is a row of A and has weight seven or eleven. For k=2, the right half is the sum of two distinct rows; the intersection counts above give weight 11+7−2⋅6=6 or 7+7−2⋅4=6. For k=3, the right half would be a unit vector, but then u=(uA)A would be a row of A, of weight seven or eleven. For k=4, the right half would vanish, contradicting invertibility of A. Thus no nonzero word has weight four. Every nonzero weight is consequently at least eight, and any generator row other than the first attains eight. Since the minimum Hamming distance of a linear code is its least nonzero weight,
d(C)=8,C has parameters [24,12,8].​
(5)
Define the length-23 binary Golay code C23​ as the punctured code obtained by deleting the first coordinate of this extended binary Golay code. This puncturing is injective on C, because a nonzero word in its kernel would have weight one. Thus dimC23​=12, and deleting one coordinate gives d(C23​)≥7. The sum of the first two generator rows has weight eight and first coordinate one, so its puncture has weight seven. Therefore d(C23​)=7. In fact any coordinate can be punctured: every left coordinate except the first occurs in a weight-eight generator row, the first occurs in the sum just used, and every right coordinate occurs in at least one of the weight-eight generator rows.
The radius-three Hamming balls around the 212 words of C23​ are disjoint, since two centers in intersecting balls would have Hamming distance at most six. Each ball contains
∑j=03​(j23​)=1+23+253+1771=2048=211
(6)
words. Their combined size is 212211=223, the size of the entire binary Hamming space. Hence
C23​ is a perfect [23,12,7] code, correcting three errors.​
(7)
For decoding in C, write the received word as y=c+(p,q), where (p,q) is the unknown error in its two halves. Its syndrome is
s=yH=p+qA,t=sA=pA+q.
(8)
Let ei​ denote the length-twelve unit vector. For bounded-distance decoding, the three-error syndrome decoding of extended Golay code is the following finite search, returning the first successful candidate error:
  • If wt(s)≤3, return (s,0).
  • Otherwise, if some i satisfies wt(s+Ai​)≤2, return (s+Ai​,ei​).
  • Otherwise, compute t=sA; if wt(t)≤3, return (0,t).
  • Otherwise, if some i satisfies wt(t+Ai​)≤2, return (ei​,t+Ai​).
  • If no test succeeds, declare that no error of weight at most three has this syndrome.
Every returned candidate has total Hamming weight at most three and the required syndrome. Conversely, an error of total weight at most three has a half of weight zero or one. The tests exhaust respectively q=0, q=ei​, p=0, and p=ei​, so they find every such error. Two distinct successful candidates would differ by a nonzero codeword of weight at most six, contradicting d(C)=8. Thus the result is unique, and the decoded word is y+(p,q). The condition wt(s)≥3 still allows the first test when the weight is exactly three; for larger syndrome weight begin with the second test. A large syndrome weight alone does not imply more than three errors.
The decoder corrects every error of weight at most three; failure means that the received word is outside all radius-three decoding balls. Unlike its puncture, C is not a perfect code: its radius-three balls cover only 212(1+24+276+2024)=212⋅2325<224 words. Without the three-error assumption, a successful test identifies a nearby codeword but need not identify the transmitted word.
For an arbitrary received word, a complete minimum-distance decoding fallback is to enumerate q∈F212​ and minimize wt(s+qA)+wt(q). Every error with the observed syndrome has exactly the form (s+qA,q), so a minimizer gives a nearest codeword y+(s+qA,q). Report all minimizers if there is a tie. This also decodes outside the guaranteed radius in the nearest-word sense, while making no unsupported claim that the transmitted word is determined. Thus a syndrome of weight at least three alone is insufficient for guaranteed recovery; either the three-error hypothesis or a choice among nearest words is needed.

Represent a word (c0​,…,cn−1​) in the Hamming space Hn,q​ by the polynomial c(x)=∑j=0n−1​cj​xj. A cyclic code is a linear code invariant under the cyclic coordinate shift (c0​,…,cn−1​)↦(cn−1​,c0​,…,cn−2​). Under this identification the shift is multiplication by x modulo xn−1. A shift-invariant vector subspace is closed under multiplication by every polynomial, since such multiplication is a linear combination of shifts. It is therefore an ideal of
R=Fq​[x]/(xn−1).
(9)
Conversely every ideal of R is a vector subspace closed under multiplication by x, hence a cyclic code. Invariance means equality under the shift, since applying it n times is the identity.
Let J be the inverse image of the code ideal under the quotient map Fq​[x]→R. It contains (xn−1) and is nonzero. Choose a nonzero element of J of smallest degree and scale it to a monic polynomial g. For any f∈J, polynomial division gives f=ag+r with degr<degg. Since r=f−ag∈J, minimality forces r=0. Thus J=(g) and in particular
g∣xn−1,C=(g​)⊆R.​
(10)
This is the required generator polynomial of a cyclic code; the divisibility direction in the printed formula is reversed. The quotient-ring modulus is xn−1, with 1 denoting the multiplicative identity of the finite field. If r=degg, every codeword has a unique representative ag with dega<n−r. Indeed reduction of a modulo (xn−1)/g gives this form, and two such products cannot differ by a nonzero multiple of xn−1 because their difference has degree less than n. Therefore
dimC=n−r,g,xg,…,xn−r−1g form a basis.​
(11)
For the zero code the convention is g=xn−1 and this basis is empty; for the full word space g=1.
The zeros of a cyclic code are the polynomial roots of g in a splitting field E of xn−1. Suppose first that the characteristic p of Fq​ does not divide n. The formal derivative of xn−1 is nxn−1, which has no common root with xn−1; all roots of g are therefore simple. If they are α1​,…,αr​, then g∣c is equivalent to c(αi​)=0 for all i. A root-evaluation parity-check matrix of a cyclic code, written with checks as rows, is consequently
HE​=​1⋮1​α1​⋮αr​​⋯⋯​α1n−1​⋮αrn−1​​​,HE​cT=0.
(12)
Its entries may lie in E, rather than in the original finite field. To obtain a parity-check matrix over Fq​, choose a basis of E over Fq​ and replace each equation by the equations for all of its field coordinates. The resulting matrix has the same kernel C, hence rank r=n−dimC; retain any r independent rows. More economically, choose one root from each Frobenius conjugate orbit. For c∈Fq​[x], c(αq)=c(α)q, so checking one representative checks its whole orbit. Expanding that representative's equation in a basis of Fq​(α) gives precisely the number of checks equal to its orbit length.
No coprimality hypothesis is stated in the question, so the repeated-root cyclic code case must also be distinguished. Distinct roots alone cannot determine these codes: in F2​[x]/(x2−1), the generators x+1 and (x+1)2 have the same zero, but generate codes of dimensions one and zero. In general write g=∏i​(x−αi​)mi​ in E[x]. The correct checks are
c[ℓ](αi​)=0(0≤ℓ<mi​),c[ℓ](x)=∑j≥ℓ​(ℓj​)cj​xj−ℓ,
(13)
using Hasse derivatives. Indeed c(αi​+z)=∑ℓ​c[ℓ](αi​)zℓ, so vanishing of the first mi​ coefficients is equivalent to divisibility by (x−αi​)mi​. A repeated-root parity-check matrix over E thus has rows indexed by (i,ℓ) and entries
(HE​)(i,ℓ),j​={(ℓj​)αij−ℓ​,0,​j≥ℓ,j<ℓ.​​
(14)
As before, expand to equations over Fq​ and retain degg=∑i​mi​ independent rows. The ordinary root-evaluation matrix is exactly the special case mi​=1.
For the binary Hamming code, let s≥2, n=2s−1, and let ω generate the multiplicative group of a finite field F2s×​. This is possible because that group is a cyclic group. Choose a basis of F2s​ over F2​. The coordinate columns of 1,ω,…,ωn−1 then run through every nonzero vector of F2s​ exactly once. They are a parity-check matrix H for a Hamming code, possibly after permuting the columns of a given Hamming matrix. Thus
C={c∈F2n​:∑j=0n−1​cj​ωj=0}={c:c(ω)=0}
(15)
is equivalent to the Hamming code. Multiplication by x modulo xn−1 multiplies its evaluation at ω by ω, so C is a cyclic code.
The parity-check matrix has rank s because its columns contain a basis. It has no zero column or repeated column, so no nonzero word of weight one or two is in its kernel. Two distinct nonzero columns have a nonzero sum, which is a third distinct column; these three columns give a word of weight three. Hence C has parameters [2s−1,2s−s−1,3].
A polynomial over F2​ vanishes at ω exactly when it is divisible by its minimal polynomial of an algebraic element mω​. The finite-field Frobenius automorphism gives all conjugates of ω as ω2j. Their orbit length is s: if ω2d=ω with 0<d<s, then the order 2s−1 of ω would divide 2d−1, which is a smaller positive integer. Therefore the Hamming code as a cyclic code has
g(x)=mω​(x)=j=0∏s−1​(x−ω2j),zeros ω,ω2,ω22,…,ω2s−1.​
(16)
The last exponent here is 2s−1. The printed exponent 2s−1 would give ω2s−1=1, which is not a root of the degree-s irreducible minimal polynomial of an algebraic element for s≥2.

For distinct evaluation points a0​,…,an−1​∈Fq​ and 1≤k≤n, the Reed-Solomon code is
RSk​(a)={(f(a0​),…,f(an−1​)): f∈Fq​[x], degf<k}.
(17)
This is a linear code of dimension k: evaluation is linear, and a nonzero polynomial of degree less than k≤n cannot vanish at all n distinct points. The assertion of cyclicity concerns the primitive, unextended construction: set n=q−1 and aj​=ωj, where ω generates the multiplicative group of a finite field Fq×​. Arbitrary evaluation sets or arbitrary coordinate orders need not make a Reed-Solomon code a cyclic code.
For this ordered full-length construction, shifting the evaluation vector to the right gives (f(ωj−1))j​. This is the evaluation vector of f(ω−1x), whose degree is still less than k, so it is a cyclic code. Its generator polynomial follows from the finite geometric series
∑j=0n−1​ωjt={n,0,​n∣t,n∤t.​
(18)
Here the scalar n=q−1 is nonzero in Fq​. Write f(x)=∑a=0k−1​ma​xa and c(x)=∑j=0n−1​f(ωj)xj. For 1≤ℓ≤n−k,
c(ωℓ)=∑a=0k−1​ma​∑j=0n−1​ωj(a+ℓ)=0,
(19)
since 1≤a+ℓ≤n−1. Thus every word is divisible by g(x)=∏ℓ=1n−k​(x−ωℓ). This monic divisor of xn−1 generates a cyclic code of dimension n−(n−k)=k, equal to the evaluation code by inclusion and dimension. Hence
RSk​(1,ω,…,ωn−1)=⟨g⟩,g(x)=ℓ=1∏n−k​(x−ωℓ),n=q−1.​
(20)
It is useful to allow a starting exponent b for the consecutive zeros of a cyclic code. Define the primitive cyclic Reed-Solomon code
Ck,b​=⟨∏ℓ=bb+n−k−1​(x−ωℓ)⟩.
(21)
All exponents are understood modulo n. The same geometric-sum calculation shows that
Ck,b​={(aj1−b​f(aj​))j=0n−1​:degf<k}.
(22)
Indeed evaluating its word polynomial at ωb+ℓ, 0≤ℓ<n−k, gives sums with exponents a+1+ℓ between one and n−1. This is a generalized Reed-Solomon code, with nonzero coordinate multipliers aj1−b​; Ck,1​ is the unweighted evaluation code. Directly, a right shift corresponds to replacing f(x) by ωb−1f(ω−1x), so these offset codes are also cyclic codes.
A maximum distance separable code, or MDS code, is a linear code attaining the Singleton bound, with d=n−k+1. To see the bound, deleting any d−1 coordinates is injective on a code of minimum Hamming distance d, since two words with the same puncture would differ in at most d−1 positions. Therefore qk≤qn−d+1 and d≤n−k+1. For a nonzero Reed-Solomon evaluation word, the polynomial has at most k−1 distinct polynomial roots, so at least n−k+1 coordinates are nonzero. Nonzero coordinate multipliers do not change these zero positions. This proves d≥n−k+1 for both Reed-Solomon codes and generalized Reed-Solomon codes. Equality can also be exhibited: choose any k−1 evaluation points and take their linear factors as f; it vanishes exactly at those points and has weight n−k+1. Thus
RSk​ and Ck,b​ have parameters [n,k,n−k+1] and are MDS codes.​
(23)
The duality of primitive cyclic Reed-Solomon codes is especially transparent in the evaluation form. Assume first 1≤k<n. A vector of Ck,b​ has coordinates aj1−b​f(aj​), degf<k; a vector of Cn−k,1−b​ has coordinates ajb​h(aj​), degh<n−k. Their standard dot product is
∑j=0n−1​aj​f(aj​)h(aj​)=0.
(24)
Every monomial in xf(x)h(x) has exponent at least one and at most 1+(k−1)+(n−k−1)=n−1, and every such power sum vanishes. Thus Cn−k,1−b​⊆Ck,b⊥​. Both sides have dimension n−k, giving
Ck,b⊥​=Cn−k,1−b​,g⊥(x)=ℓ=1−b∏k−b​(x−ωℓ).​
(25)
In particular Ck,1⊥​=Cn−k,0​ has generator ∏ℓ=0k−1​(x−ωℓ). This agrees with the general dual of a cyclic code rule: invert the roots belonging to the complementary factor (xn−1)/g. The dual is a Reed-Solomon code in the consecutive-zero convention. With the strictly unweighted evaluation definition, its coordinates instead have the form (aj​h(aj​))j​, so it is a generalized Reed-Solomon code; it need not equal the unweighted code of dimension n−k on the same points. This convention distinction is essential. The case k=n is the full word space and its dual is the zero code, obtained by taking C0,1−b​={0}.
For code encoding, use the finite-field discrete Fourier transform and its inverse:
cℓ​=∑j=0n−1​cj​ωjℓ,cj​=n−1∑ℓ=0n−1​cℓ​ω−jℓ.
(26)
For a message (m0​,…,mk−1​), put cb−1−a​=nma​ for 0≤a<k, and set all other transform coordinates to zero. The inverse discrete Fourier transform returns
cj​=aj1−b​a=0∑k−1​ma​aja​,aj​=ωj.​
(27)
The n−k zero transform coordinates are precisely the root checks defining Ck,b​. This gives non-systematic encoding by evaluation. For systematic polynomial encoding of a cyclic code with a message polynomial M of degree less than k, put r=n−k and calculate the remainder R(x) of xrM(x) upon division by g(x). Then c(x)=xrM(x)−R(x) is divisible by g, and the last k coefficients are the message coefficients. Both methods use the same generator polynomial and the same ordered powers of ω.
For bounded-distance decoding, let y=c+e, put r=n−k, and assume at most T=⌊r/2⌋ symbol errors. The following description uses the Reed-Solomon key equation and fixes the transform and locator conventions explicitly.
  • Compute the syndromes Sℓ​=y(ωb+ℓ)=y​b+ℓ​ for 0≤ℓ<r. The corresponding codeword transform values vanish. An error of value Ei​ at coordinate ji​ contributes Ei​Xib+ℓ​, where Xi​=ωji​, so Sℓ​=∑i​Ei​Xib+ℓ​.
  • Form S(z)=∑ℓ=0r−1​Sℓ​zℓ. Use the Berlekamp-Massey algorithm, or the polynomial extended Euclidean algorithm applied to zr and S(z), to solve ΛS≡Ω(modzr) with Λ(0)=1, degΛ=ν≤T, and degΩ<ν. Choose the minimal-degree locator; zero syndrome gives Λ=1, Ω=0. The error locator polynomial is Λ(z)=∏i=1ν​(1−Xi​z), and Ω is the error evaluator polynomial.
  • Perform a Chien search: test Λ(ω−j) for 0≤j<n. Its ν distinct zeros identify the erroneous coordinates. A mismatch between the degree and the number of distinct permitted roots is a decoding failure.
  • Recover each error value by the Forney algorithm, with the present syndrome offset:
Ei​=−Xi1−b​Λ′(Xi−1​)Ω(Xi−1​)​.​
(28)
  • Set eji​​=Ei​, all other error coordinates to zero, and output c=y−e. Verify that all r root checks vanish and that the correction has weight at most T. Recover the message from the unconstrained transform coordinates by ma​=n−1cb−1−a​, or from the last k coefficients if systematic encoding was used.
The denominator in the Forney algorithm is nonzero for distinct locations. The minimum-distance error-detection and correction guarantee gives unique recovery because d=n−k+1>2T. If the required locator or a valid correction cannot be found, declare decoding failure. Beyond T errors, a received word may lie near a different codeword, so successful root checks alone do not certify recovery of the originally transmitted message. The guaranteed correction radius is ⌊(n−k)/2⌋ symbol errors.
