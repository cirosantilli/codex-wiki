Work with finite groups, as required for these Sylow subgroup counts. Let P act by conjugation on the set of Sylow p-subgroups. The stabilizer of Q is P∩NG​(Q). Since Q is the normal Sylow subgroup of its own normalizer, every p-subgroup of that normalizer lies in Q. Consequently the stabilizer is exactly P∩Q, and the orbit-stabilizer theorem gives orbit size [P:P∩Q]. The orbit of P has size one. Every other orbit size is a power of p at least pa, hence is divisible by pa. Adding orbit sizes proves the strengthened Sylow congruence from intersections:
np​≡1(modpa).​
(1)
Now suppose G is simple of order 2e⋅15. If e=0, its Sylow 5-subgroup is normal because its number divides three and is one modulo five. If e=1, an involution in the regular permutation action of G swaps fifteen pairs, so its sign is negative. The sign of a permutation would give a nontrivial group homomorphism G→C2​ with a proper nontrivial normal kernel. Both cases contradict simplicity. Thus e≥2.
The Sylow theorems make n2​ a divisor of fifteen. It is not one, since a nontrivial Sylow subgroup would then be normal. It is not three, since the conjugation action on three Sylow subgroups would give a faithful group homomorphism G↪S3​, impossible by order. If n2​=5, take a Sylow subgroup P itself: its normalizer has subgroup index five. If n2​=15, not all distinct Sylow subgroups can intersect P in subgroup index at least four, since that would give 15≡1(mod4). Choose Q=P with [P:P∩Q]=2. Put R=P∩Q, of order 2e−1. It has subgroup index two in both P,Q, so is normal in each. Hence NG​(R) contains both distinct Sylow subgroups and has order greater than 2e. It is proper: otherwise R would be a nontrivial proper normal subgroup of G. Its order is therefore 2e⋅3 or 2e⋅5. The latter would give an index-three subgroup and again an impossible faithful action on three cosets. Thus [G:NG​(R)]=5.
In either case we have the requested subgroup of order 2e or 2e−1 with index-five normalizer. The action on the five cosets of that normalizer is nontrivial and faithful by simplicity. Its image lies in A5​, since composing with sign cannot give a nontrivial map from this simple group of order greater than two to C2​. Thus 2e⋅15 divides 60, forcing e=2 and equality of orders. This proves the simple groups with order a power of two times fifteen conclusion:
G≅A5​.​
(2)
For SL2​(5), choose the first column: there are 52−1=24 nonzero choices. For each, determinant one is a nonzero linear equation in the second column and has five solutions. Therefore ∣SL2​(5)∣=120. A central matrix commuting with (10​11​) has zero lower-left entry and equal diagonal entries; commuting also with (11​01​) makes the upper-right entry zero. It is scalar. Determinant one then gives
∣SL2​(5)∣=120,Z={I,−I},∣Z∣=2.​
(3)
To establish the projective special linear group over the field with five elements isomorphism without assuming its simplicity, compute its classes. For determinant-one matrices, the characteristic polynomial is X2−tX+1 with trace t. The possibilities over F5​ exhaust the group:

(4)
For trace zero the eigenvalues are 2,3, so the determinant-one centralizer is the split diagonal torus of order four. For traces ±1, the discriminant is the nonsquare 2: the centralizer is the norm-one subgroup of F25×​, of order six. In both semisimple cases the determinant map on the full linear centralizer is surjective onto F5×​; consequently each trace type is a single special-linear class. For the noncentral traces ±2, a Jordan block has full linear centralizer {(a0​ba​):a=0} with determinant a2. The determinant image consists of the two squares, so each general-linear class splits into two special-linear classes; the determinant-one centralizer has order 2⋅5=10. These sizes sum to 120.
Quotienting by Z identifies a matrix with its negative. The trace-zero class gives one projective class of size fifteen; the two traces ±1 give one class of size twenty; the four noncentral unipotent classes give two classes of size twelve. Their respective projective orders are 2,3,5,5, by the characteristic polynomials. A normal subgroup is a union of classes containing the identity. The nontrivial proper possible union sizes are 13,16,21,25,28,33,36,40,45,48, none of which divides 60. Thus the quotient is simple. Applying the order-60 result above gives
PSL2​(5)≅A5​.​
(5)
Finally, if A2=I over an odd-characteristic field, its minimal polynomial divides the square-free polynomial (X−1)(X+1), so it is diagonalizable with eigenvalues ±1. In dimension two, determinant one requires both eigenvalues equal. Hence A=I or A=−I. This proves the unique involution in SL2 over an odd field assertion: the only element of order two in SL2​(5) is −I. If an index-two subgroup H existed, it would be normal and have order sixty. By Cauchy theorem it would contain an involution, hence contain Z. Then H/Z would be a normal subgroup of order thirty in the simple quotient PSL2​(5), a contradiction. Thus SL2​(5) has no index-two subgroup.
