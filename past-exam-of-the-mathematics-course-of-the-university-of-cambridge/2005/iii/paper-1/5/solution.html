A subnormal series of a finite group is a chain 1=G0​◃G1​◃⋯◃Gr​=G. A normal series of a group requires every Gi​ normal in the whole group. This distinction matters: a composition series is a subnormal series with nontrivial simple factors, whereas a chief series is a strict, unrefinable normal series of a group, whose factors are minimal normal subgroups of the appropriate ambient quotients. Both exist in a finite group, by successively choosing maximal proper normal subgroups or a maximal chain of ambient-normal subgroups. The direct-product result proved above shows that chief factors are powers of isomorphic simple groups, rather than necessarily simple themselves.
The Jordan–Hölder theorem says that any two composition series have the same multiset of simple factors, up to isomorphism and order. Here is an induction proof. The assertion is trivial for the identity group. Let A,B be the penultimate terms of two series for G; they are maximal proper normal subgroups. If A=B, apply induction there. Otherwise AB=G, since its image is a nontrivial normal subgroup in each simple quotient. Put C=A∩B. The maps to the quotients give
A/C≅G/B,B/C≅G/A.
(24)
For example, a↦aB has kernel C and is onto because AB=G. Take a composition series of C and append A, obtaining a composition series of A because A/C is simple; do the same with B. Induction inside these smaller groups shows that the factors in the two original series are, in both cases, the factors of C together with G/A and G/B. This proves the theorem and justifies the phrase Jordan–Hölder factors independently of a chosen series.
The derived series is G(0)=G, G(i+1)=[G(i),G(i)]. Its terms are characteristic subgroups, hence normal. A group is soluble if this series reaches the identity. Equivalently it admits a subnormal series with abelian factors. One direction uses the derived series itself. For the other, note the extension principle: if N◃G has derived length a and G/N has derived length b, then G(b)≤N, so G(a+b)=1. Applying this principle along an abelian-factor series proves solubility.
Subgroups and quotients of a soluble group are soluble: H(i)≤G(i) for a subgroup, and the commutator subgroup of a quotient is the image of the commutator subgroup. A finite soluble simple group is abelian, since its commutator subgroup is normal and cannot equal the whole group if the derived series terminates. An abelian simple finite group is cyclic of prime order. Therefore a finite group is soluble exactly when all its Jordan–Hölder factors are cyclic of prime order: necessity follows from the subgroup/quotient property, and sufficiency follows from the extension principle. Its chief factors are elementary abelian, by the minimal-normal-subgroup result.
For nilpotence, the lower central series is γ1​(G)=G, γi+1​(G)=[γi​(G),G]. A group is nilpotent if γc+1​=1 for some c. An ascending central series 1=N0​≤⋯≤Nc​=G satisfies [Ni+1​,G]≤Ni​. Reversing a terminating lower central series gives such a central series. Conversely, induction gives γi​(G)≤Nc−i+1​, so any such central series forces γc+1​=1. The upper central series Z0​=1, Zi+1​/Zi​=Z(G/Zi​) contains every ascending central series term by term, and thus reaches G exactly in the nilpotent case. Because [γi​,γi​]≤[γi​,G]=γi+1​, induction gives G(i)≤γi+1​: nilpotent groups are soluble.
For finite groups there is the stronger structural characterization: a finite group is nilpotent exactly when it is the direct product of groups formed from its Sylow subgroups. To prove the forward direction, the upper central series gives the normalizer condition. If H<G, choose its first term Zi​ not contained in H, with Zi−1​≤H. An element of Zi​∖H has all group commutators with H in Zi−1​, so normalizes H. Hence H<NG​(H).
For a Sylow subgroup P, its normalizer is self-normalizing. Indeed, if x normalizes NG​(P), the two Sylow subgroups Px,P of that normalizer are conjugate there; adjusting x by an element of NG​(P) puts it in NG​(P) already. Applying the strict normalizer condition to NG​(P) shows that it cannot be proper, so P is normal. Normal Sylow subgroups of distinct primes commute, since their group commutators lie in their trivial intersection. Their product has cardinality ∣G∣ and is direct.
Conversely, a nontrivial finite p-group has nontrivial centre: in the class equation every noncentral conjugacy class has size divisible by p, so p divides the centre's order. Induct on its order through the quotient by its centre to obtain an upper central series. Thus every finite p-group is nilpotent, and a finite direct product of groups of them is nilpotent because its upper central series is obtained componentwise.
A finite supersolvable group has a normal series of a group with cyclic prime-order factors. It is soluble, but the ambient-normal condition is stronger than the condition on a composition series. Finite nilpotent groups are supersolvable: a p-group has a central subgroup of order p, and induction in its quotient builds an ambient-normal prime-factor series; direct products of groups of the Sylow factors combine these series. The converses fail. The group S3​ is supersolvable via 1<C3​<S3​, but is not nilpotent because its Sylow 2-subgroups are not normal. The group A4​ is soluble via 1<V4​<A4​, but is not supersolvable: no subgroup of order two is normal, since the three involutions are conjugate, and no subgroup of order three is normal, since there are four such subgroups. In this example 1<C2​<V4​<A4​ is a composition series whose first C2​ is not normal in A4​, while 1<V4​<A4​ is a chief series with a nonsimple chief factor V4​.
Finally, a perfect group satisfies G′=G. In any finite group, the derived series eventually stabilizes at a perfect characteristic subgroup, because a descending sequence of finite subgroup orders eventually stops decreasing. It is trivial exactly when the group is soluble. A nonabelian simple group is perfect: its nontrivial commutator subgroup is normal and must be the whole group. Thus series distinguish successively stronger notions, summarize the building blocks through Jordan–Hölder factors, and separate soluble behaviour from a persistent perfect part.
