The endpoint value printed in this part is erroneous. The correct conclusion is
t↑1lim​Xt​=0almost surely​.
(8)
Already EXt2​=t(1−t)→0 gives convergence in probability to zero and rules out an almost sure limit of one.
To prove the stronger endpoint convergence, use time reversal of a Brownian bridge. Set Yt​=X1−t​, including Y0​=X1​=0. Both X and Y are centered Gaussian processes, and for s≤t,
E[Ys​Yt​]=(1−t)(1−(1−s))=s(1−t).
(9)
They therefore have the same finite-dimensional distributions. The path of X is continuous at zero, since its stochastic integral is continuous there. In particular its values at positive rational times tend to zero at zero with probability one. Equality of the laws on the countable rational coordinates transfers this event to Y. The path of Y is continuous at every time in (0,1], so its supremum on any interval bounded away from zero is determined by rational times. The rational endpoint condition consequently implies Yt​→0 as t↓0 along all real times. Hence Xt​→0 as t↑1, and setting X1​=0 gives a continuous version on the whole closed interval. This proof uses equality of coordinate laws, without assuming terminal continuity in advance.

Use the standard global convention that Mc2​ consists of L2-bounded continuous martingales, here starting at zero. This matters for arbitrary unbounded stopping times in part (iv); square-integrability at each separate deterministic time alone is weaker. Such martingales have terminal L2 limits and satisfy the optional sampling theorem at arbitrary finite stopping times. Infinite stopping times can also be included by evaluating at the terminal limit. The weakly orthogonal continuous martingales condition compares cross-time expectations. The following four parts prove its equivalence with the equal-time, bracket-mean and forward-stopping tests.

For a stopping time T≤s, optional sampling for N gives E[Ns​∣FT​]=NT​, hence
E[MT​Ns​]=E[MT​NT​].
(13)
This is justified by truncation and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, as in solution. If MN is a martingale, bounded optional sampling gives E[MT​NT​]=E[M0​N0​]=0, proving the required condition.
Conversely suppose the required condition holds. Every bounded stopping time T lies below some deterministic s, so the displayed identity gives E[(MN)T​]=0. The product is continuous and adapted, and its stopped values are integrable: the Doob L2 maximal inequality bounds the product of the two path suprema in L1 by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. The characterization of a martingale by bounded continuous-time stopped expectations therefore makes MN a martingale. Thus orthogonality is equivalent to the backward-stopping expectation test. In contrast to weakly orthogonal continuous martingales, this tests the product at random times, not only deterministic times.

a OurBigBook.com
The existence and pathwise uniqueness theorem for a stochastic differential equation states that locally Lipschitz drift b and diffusion σ on an open domain give a pathwise unique strong solution of a stochastic differential equation up to a maximal lifetime ξ. The initial value is measurable with respect to the initial sigma-algebra, and the filtration satisfies the usual conditions for a filtration. On Rd, if Tn​ are exits from increasing balls, then Tn​↑ξ; a finite lifetime entails escape from every compact subset. The coefficients need not satisfy a global growth bound for this local result.
One construction truncates the coefficients outside each compact region to globally Lipschitz coefficients, solves the resulting global equations, and patches their solutions until the exits. Pathwise uniqueness makes solutions from larger truncations agree on the smaller stopped interval. Their union is the maximal local solution of a stochastic differential equation, and any extension would contradict the exhaustion of the domain by the exit times.

The terminal bracket value printed as zero is incompatible with a strictly increasing bracket starting at zero. If the intended value is infinity, solution applies directly. In fact the substantive conclusion holds for every deterministic continuous bracket a(t)=[M]t​, whether its terminal value is finite or infinite, and without strict increase.
For u∈R, the Itô formula makes
Zt​=exp(iuMt​+21​u2a(t))
(26)
a complex local martingale. Its modulus is the deterministic value eu2a(t)/2, bounded on every finite horizon, so its real and imaginary parts are true martingales there. Conditioning between s and t gives
E[eiu(Mt​−Ms​)∣Fs​]=exp(−21​u2(a(t)−a(s)))​.

This deterministic conditional characteristic function shows that the increment is independent of Fs​ and is normal with mean zero and variance a(t)−a(s). Write the diffusion process as dXt​=b(Xt​)dt+σ(Xt​)dBt​, with a=σσT. Its diffusion generator is
Lφ=21​∑i,j​aij∂ij​φ+∑i​bi∂i​φ.
(34)
The Itô formula says that φ(Xt​)−φ(X0​)−∫0t​Lφ(Xs​)ds is a local martingale, and is a true martingale when the stopped terms are integrable. Taking expectations gives Dynkin formula for a diffusion. This is the bridge from infinitesimal stochastic dynamics to differential equations.
For the transition semigroup Pt​g(x)=Ex​g(Xt​), the Markov property gives Pt+s​=Pt​Ps​. On a smooth generator domain where differentiation is valid, differentiating the semigroup identity yields the Kolmogorov backward equation ∂t​Pt​g=L(Pt​g), with initial value g. If a transition density p(t,x,y) exists, integration against smooth compactly supported tests and integration by parts give the Fokker-Planck equation
∂t​p=Ly∗​p,L∗p=21​∑i,j​∂ij​(aijp)−∑i​∂i​(bip).
(35)
The backward equation acts on the starting point, while this forward equation describes the evolving probability density.
Here is a full representation proof with a source and potential. Suppose X is nonexplosive, V≥0 is bounded, f,g are bounded, and u is a bounded classical solution on a finite slab of
ut​+Lu−Vu=−f,u(T,x)=g(x).
(36)
For a process started from x at time t, put Ds​=exp(−∫ts​V(Xr​)dr). The Itô product rule gives
d(Ds​u(s,Xs​))=−Ds​f(s,Xs​)ds+Ds​∇u(s,Xs​)σ(Xs​)dBs​.
(37)
Therefore Ds​u(s,Xs​)+∫ts​Dr​f(r,Xr​)dr is a local martingale. It is bounded in absolute value by ∥u∥∞​+(T−t)∥f∥∞​, so it is a true martingale, without requiring a global derivative bound. Its endpoint expectations give the parabolic Feynman-Kac formula with a source
u(t,x)=Et,x​[e−∫tT​V(Xr​)drg(XT​)+∫tT​e−∫ts​V(Xr​)drf(s,Xs​)ds]​.
(38)
This proves uniqueness in the bounded classical class. Setting V=f=0 recovers the backward heat or diffusion representation; the minus sign in the killing term produces the negative exponential.
For an elliptic problem, stop at the first exit τ from a bounded regular domain D. Suppose Ex​τ<∞, u∈C2(D)∩C(Dˉ) is bounded and solves (L−V)u=−f with boundary data g. The same calculation, stopped at t∧τ, gives a martingale dominated by ∥u∥∞​+∥f∥∞​τ. Continuity puts Xτ​ on the boundary. Taking the limit and using dominated convergence gives the elliptic Feynman-Kac formula
u(x)=Ex​[e−∫0τ​V(Xr​)drg(Xτ​)+∫0τ​e−∫0s​V(Xr​)drf(Xs​)ds]​.

For V=f=0, harmonic functions for L give stopped martingales and are averages of their exit-boundary values. For Brownian motion L=21​Δ, this is the probabilistic solution of the Dirichlet problem. Suitable smooth coefficients and uniform ellipticity give familiar settings where the required diffusion, exit-time integrability and classical solutions exist. The representation itself follows from the stated regularity and integrability conditions, rather than claiming classical solvability for arbitrary coefficients.

At the unscaled state (x,y), write the Lévy kernel of a Markov jump process on jump displacements z as
K((x,y),dz)=λxδ(1,0)​(dz)+Nμxy​δ(−1,0)​(dz)+λyδ(0,1)​(dz)+Nμxy​δ(0,−1)​(dz)​.
(40)
The destination-kernel convention translates each Dirac mass by (x,y). The total holding rate is the mass of this kernel. At either zero population the corresponding death rates vanish, so no negative states are reached. Births in the total population are bounded by a Yule process with per-individual rate λ, while deaths only decrease it; this also gives nonexplosion.
Put ZN=(X/N,Y/N). This is a density-dependent Markov jump process, with jumps of size 1/N and rates of order N. Its drift gives the deterministic approximation
x˙=λx−μxy,y˙​=λy−μxy,(x(0),y(0))=(1,1)​.
(41)
Uniqueness preserves the diagonal x=y=z. For positive λ,μ, the resulting logistic differential equation has solution
z(t)=μ+(λ−μ)e−λtλ​​.
(42)
If λ<μ, it decreases from one to λ/μ; if λ>μ, it increases to that same equilibrium; equality gives the constant solution one. In particular positive rates do not give extinction on this exactly symmetric deterministic trajectory. Transverse perturbations behave differently: subtracting the equations gives (x−y)′=λ(x−y), so the positive symmetric equilibrium has an unstable difference direction. The symmetric competition model distinguishes diagonal logistic relaxation from this symmetry-breaking instability.
For an explicit error estimate, use the martingale decomposition of a density-dependent jump process:
ZtN​=(1,1)+∫0t​F(ZsN​)ds+MtN​,F(x,y)=(λx−μxy,λy−μxy).
(43)
Fix t0​,δ>0 and choose R>supt≤t0​​z(t)+δ. Stop at the first exit σR​ from the box [0,R]2. On that box, F has Lipschitz constant LR​=λ+2μR in the supremum norm. Each coordinate of MN has jumps bounded by 1/N and predictable quadratic variation at most
⟨(MN,i)σR​⟩t0​​≤NCR​t0​​,CR​=λR+μR2.
(44)
Indeed a coordinate's positive and negative jump rates are Nλx and Nμxy, and the squared jump size is 1/N2. Independent jump clocks give zero cross-bracket.
The Bernstein bound for bounded-jump martingales gives, for each coordinate,
P(supt≤t0​​∣Mt∧σR​N,i​∣≥η)≤2exp(−2(CR​t0​+η/3)Nη2​).
(45)
The Gronwall inequality applied up to σR​ gives
supt≤t0​∧σR​​∥ZtN​−(z(t),z(t))∥∞​≤eLR​t0​supt≤t0​​∥Mt∧σR​N​∥∞​.
(46)
An exit from the box already entails an error larger than δ, by the choice of R, so the stopped estimate controls the event of any error on the full interval. Take η=δe−LR​t0​ and use the union bound on the two coordinates:
P(t≤t0​sup​∥ZtN​−(z(t),z(t))∥∞​>δ)≤min{1,4exp(−2(CR​t0​+δe−LR​t0​/3)Nδ2e−2LR​t0​​)}​.
(47)
For fixed time horizon and tolerance this is exponentially small in N, proving a fluid limit. The transverse instability explains why this finite-horizon estimate should not be extrapolated to arbitrarily long times without changing its constants.
