Normalize by a riskless bank account with B0​=1, B1​=R>0. For d risky assets let Y=S1​/R−S0​ be the vector of discounted gains, and let H denote a discounted square-integrable contingent claim. An initial total capital x and stock holding ϑ have discounted terminal portfolio wealth x+ϑTY: the bank holding finances the initial purchase of the stocks. Thus the space of attainable claims is
A=span{1,Y1,…,Yd}⊂L2(P).
(1)
It is finite-dimensional and hence closed. The market is complete market when every square-integrable claim is in this span; with finitely many assets this is also equivalent to replication of every bounded claim.
A dominated martingale measure Q is a probability measure with Q≪P and EQ​Y=0, with the gains integrable under Q. Its Radon-Nikodym derivative satisfies Z≥0, EZ=1 and E[ZY]=0. An equivalent martingale measure has Z>0 almost surely. A signed martingale measure obeys the same normalization and pricing equations without requiring nonnegativity. The distinction matters for quadratic hedging. For the completeness assertion use the usual no-arbitrage hypothesis, expressed here by existence of an equivalent martingale measure Q0​.
The unrestricted one-period quadratic hedge minimizes
E(H−x−ϑTY)2.
(2)
Put m=EY, C=Cov(Y) and k=Cov(Y,H). Remove redundant gains so that C is invertible. Differentiating in x gives x=EH−ϑTm. The remaining squared error is
Var(H)−2ϑTk+ϑTCϑ=Var(H)−kTC−1k+(ϑ−C−1k)TC(ϑ−C−1k).
(3)
Therefore
ϑ∗​=C−1k,x∗​=EH−mTC−1k.​
(4)
The residual is orthogonal in L2(P) to 1 and every gain, making the fitted claim the orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace onto A. Its minimum mean-square error is Var(H)−kTC−1k, and it vanishes exactly when H is attainable. If initial capital x is fixed instead, the normal equation is
(C+mmT)ϑ=k+m(EH−x).
(5)
This separates hedging with prescribed capital from simultaneous optimization of capital and risky holdings.
The minimal martingale measure in a one-period market is characterized by leaving unchanged the expectation of every centered square-integrable L orthogonal to the martingale part Y−m. For square-integrable pricing densities this forces Z−1 into the span of Y−m: the orthogonal complement consists exactly of those L. The pricing equation then determines
Z∗​=1−mTC−1(Y−m).​
(6)
Indeed EZ∗​=1 and E[Z∗​Y]=m−CC−1m=0. For every such orthogonal L, E[Z∗​L]=0. Conversely a normalized density preserving all these L must have the displayed form, since its coefficients must satisfy Cβ=−m. The least-squares initial capital is consequently x∗​=E[Z∗​H].
For any other square-integrable signed pricing density Z, put L=Z−Z∗​. Both densities price 1 and all gains, so L is orthogonal to their span, including Z∗​. Hence
EZ2=EZ∗2​+E(Z−Z∗​)2≥EZ∗2​=1+mTC−1m.​
(7)
Equality holds only when Z=Z∗​. Densities with infinite second moment cannot improve this finite minimum. This proves the requested minimum over dominated martingale measures whenever Z∗​≥0, and over all square-integrable signed martingale measures without that extra condition. If Z∗​>0, it is also an equivalent martingale measure.
Absence of arbitrage alone does not guarantee that the minimal density is a probability density. For a concrete example, let a single discounted gain take values −1,1,10 with probabilities 1/10,4/5,1/10. Then m=17/10, C=801/100 and Z∗​(10)=−610/801. Nevertheless pricing probabilities (109/200,89/200,1/100) are strictly positive and give mean gain zero, so an equivalent martingale measure exists. This is negative minimal density in an arbitrage-free one-period market. If the term martingale measure is reserved for probabilities, the printed minimum assertion needs the extra nonnegativity hypothesis; otherwise it is the signed minimum-norm assertion just proved. Minimizing over the nonnegative pricing densities instead is a different constrained projection problem.
Finally, prove completeness and uniqueness of dominated martingale measures. If the market is complete market, every event indicator can be replicated. Under any dominated martingale measure, its expectation equals its initial discounted cost. Consequently any two such measures agree on every event and are equal; existence is supplied by Q0​.
Conversely suppose A has dimension k and is a proper subspace of the whole payoff space. Choose k+1 disjoint events Ai​ of positive probability. Such events exist whenever L2(P) has dimension greater than k: otherwise the space has at most k atoms and dimension at most k. Let f1​,…,fk​ be a basis of A. The k+1 vectors
(EQ0​​[fj​1Ai​​])j=1k​
(8)
are linearly dependent. Choose coefficients ai​, not all zero, whose linear combination is zero, and set h=∑i​ai​1Ai​​. It is bounded, not almost surely zero under Q0​, and satisfies EQ0​​[hf]=0 for every f∈A, in particular for 1,Y1,…,Yd. For 0<ε<∥h∥∞−1​ define
dPdQ±​​=dPdQ0​​(1±εh).
(9)
These densities are strictly positive, normalized, have zero gain means, and are different. Thus incompleteness contradicts uniqueness, proving
Under an equivalent pricing measure, completeness⟺a unique dominated martingale measure.​
(10)
The existence hypothesis cannot be discarded: with three positive-probability states and gain Y=0,1,2, the sole dominated martingale probability concentrates on Y=0, yet the two-dimensional attainable space does not span all three states. That market permits arbitrage.

Take a riskless gross return R>0 per period and a stock whose independent multiplicative moves are u and d, with physical up probability p∈(0,1). Assume 0<d<R<u. With Kk​ up moves by date k,
Bk​=Rk,Sk​=S0​uKk​dk−Kk​.
(11)
A self-financing portfolio chooses each stock holding from information available before the next move, and invests the remainder in the bank account. At any node the two possible next payoffs can be matched by two holdings because u=d. Backward induction therefore makes the binomial market complete.
The unique risk-neutral probability of an up move is determined by the discounted-stock martingale equation:
qu+(1−q)d=R,q=u−dR−d​∈(0,1).​
(12)
The risk-neutral measure assigns product probabilities to the successive moves. A path with K=Kn​ up moves has physical probability pK(1−p)n−K and pricing probability qK(1−q)n−K. Its Radon-Nikodym derivative is therefore
Z=dPdQ​=(pq​)K(1−p1−q​)n−K.
(13)
Since Sn​=S0​dn(u/d)K, eliminate K to obtain the requested terminal-price expression:
Z=(1−p1−q​)n(S0​dnSn​​)β,β=log(u/d)log(q(1−p)/(p(1−q)))​.​
(14)
The pricing density depends only on the terminal stock price even though different paths to that price have different intermediate histories.
For w0​>0, a nonnegative terminal wealth X with initial cost w0​ satisfies
EP​[ZX]=EQ​X=Rnw0​.
(15)
The utility function has derivative v′(x)=x−(γ−1)/γ. Put η=γ/(γ−1)>1. Its first-order condition makes v′(X) proportional to Z, so X=CZ−η. Choosing C to satisfy the pricing budget constraint gives the binomial power-utility terminal wealth
X∗​=EP​[Z1−η]Rnw0​​Z−η.​
(16)
All states have positive probability and the state space is finite, so this payoff is positive, integrable and attainable. The normalizing constant is explicit: independence gives
EP​[Z1−η]=[p(pq​)1−η+(1−p)(1−p1−q​)1−η]n.
(17)
Combining these two formulas with the terminal-price expression for Z gives X∗​ entirely as a function of Sn​.
To prove global optimality, strict concavity gives v(X)−v(X∗​)≤v′(X∗​)(X−X∗​). Because v′(X∗​)=C−1/ηZ, the expectation of the right side is zero for any other payoff satisfying the same pricing budget constraint. Equality requires X=X∗​ almost surely. Backward replication supplies its self-financing strategy, so this static optimum is attainable by trading. If w0​=0 and terminal wealth must be nonnegative, the only feasible terminal wealth is zero. For p=q, the density is one and the optimizer is the riskless wealth Rnw0​.

On a finite horizon [0,T], let W be a standard d-dimensional Brownian motion and let θ be a predictable process with ∫0T​∣θs​∣2ds<∞ almost surely. Suppose the stochastic exponential
Zt​=exp(−∫0t​θs​⋅dWs​−21​∫0t​∣θs​∣2ds)
(18)
is a true martingale with EZT​=1. The Novikov condition Eexp(21​∫0T​∣θs​∣2ds)<∞ is a sufficient condition. The Girsanov theorem says that under dQ=ZT​dP,
WtQ​=Wt​+∫0t​θs​ds is standard Brownian motion.​
(19)
Because ZT​>0, the two measures are equivalent on the terminal sigma-algebra.
For the proof sketch, Itô formula gives dZt​=−Zt​θt​⋅dWt​. Apply the product rule to Zt​WtQ,i​. Its finite-variation term Zt​θti​dt is cancelled by d[Z,Wi]t​=−Zt​θti​dt. After localization the product is a martingale under P, and the conditional change-of-measure formula makes WQ,i a local martingale under Q. Adding finite variation leaves its covariations unchanged: [WQ,i,WQ,j]t​=δij​t. The Lévy characterization of multidimensional Brownian motion completes the proof. Localization is essential if the integrands are not bounded; the true-martingale hypothesis on Z permits the final measure change.
For the deal, write T=t0​ and use constant interest rate ρ, volatility σ>0 and no stock dividends. Under the risk-neutral measure, the Black-Scholes model is
St​=S0​exp((ρ−21​σ2)t+σWtQ​).
(20)
For example this follows from the preceding theorem with θ=(μ−ρ)/σ when the physical stock drift is μ. The delivered shares have terminal cash value ST​max(r,c/A), so its initial value is
V0​=e−ρTEQ​[ST​max(r,c/A)].
(21)
The factor ST​ must be included: the deal is a stock delivery, not a cash payment of the random number of shares.
Use the stock-numeraire measure in the Black-Scholes model, whose density is
dQdQS​=S0​e−ρTST​​=exp(σWTQ​−21​σ2T).
(22)
The Girsanov theorem gives WtS​=WtQ​−σt as Brownian motion under QS. Therefore
logA=logS0​+(ρ+21​σ2)tˉ+nσ​∑i=1n​Wti​S​,tˉ=n1​∑i=1n​ti​.
(23)
The discrete geometric average under the stock-numeraire measure has normally distributed logarithm with
m=logS0​+(ρ+21​σ2)tˉ,v=n2σ2​∑i,j=1n​min(ti​,tj​)=n2σ2​∑i=1n​(2i−1)ti​.
(24)
The last identity uses the printed descending order of sampling times. Each ti​ is the minimum in exactly 2i−1 ordered pairs. In particular v>0.
Assume the ordinary positive deal parameters r,c>0 and put k=log(c/r), d=(m−k)/v​. The change of numeraire gives V0​=S0​EQS​max(r,ce−Y), where Y=logA. Split the integral at k. Completing the square in e−y times the normal probability density gives
P(Y≥k)=Φ(d),E[e−Y1Y<k​]=e−m+v/2Φ(v​k−m+v​).
(25)
Thus the stock-delivery option with a geometric average has value
V0​=S0​[rΦ(d)+ce−m+v/2Φ(−d+v​)].​
(26)
Here Φ is the standard normal distribution function. Delivery time T does not enter separately once the sampling dates are fixed: the stock numeraire cancels the final stock factor and discounting. As a check, for one sample at T, the payment is max(rST​,c) and the formula becomes the value of r shares plus a put on their terminal value. If c=0 the value is rS0​; if r=0 the value is cS0​e−m+v/2.

A self-financing portfolio changes its holdings using only its existing wealth. If it holds gt​ shares and ht​ units of a traded bond or bank account Bt​, its value Vt​=gt​St​+ht​Bt​ obeys the gain identity
dVt​=gt​dSt​+ht​dBt​.
(27)
The changes in holdings do not add or withdraw capital. Set T=t0​, Bt​=e−ρ(T−t), so dBt​=ρBt​dt, and let dSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​, with σ>0. Write Vt​=p(St​,t) and suppress the arguments of the smooth holdings g,h.
Differentiate p=xg+Bt​h:
px​=g+xgx​+Bt​hx​,pt​=xgt​+Bt​ht​+ρBt​h.
(28)
The Itô formula minus the proposed portfolio gains gives
dp−gdS−hdB=σSt​(px​−g)dWt​+[pt​+μSt​(px​−g)+21​σ2St2​pxx​−ρBt​h]dt.
(29)
Uniqueness of the local-martingale and finite-variation decomposition shows that self-financing requires px​=g. Since St​ has full support on (0,∞) for each t>0, smoothness gives the corresponding functional identity for every positive x, and continuity extends it to the interval endpoints. Thus
xgx​+Bt​hx​=0.
(30)
Differentiating this identity once more in x gives gx​+xgxx​+Bt​hxx​=0, so pxx​=gx​. Substitute both identities into the drift condition to obtain
xgx​+Bt​hx​=0,21​σ2x2gx​+xgt​+Bt​ht​=0.​
(31)
Conversely these equations give px​=g, pxx​=gx​ and zero drift and diffusion in the displayed gain difference. They therefore prove the claimed self-financing conditions for smooth stock and bond holdings in both directions.
Using Bt​h=p−xg in the gain identity and setting g=px​, self-financing implies
pt​+21​σ2x2pxx​+ρxpx​−ρp=0.​
(32)
This is the Black-Scholes equation, independent of the physical drift μ. Conversely, if a smooth p satisfies it, define its delta hedge by
g=px​,h=Bt​p−xpx​​.
(33)
The preceding Itô formula verifies that these holdings are self-financing.
For prescribed arbitrary holdings, the value equation alone is insufficient. The corrected equivalence is the Black-Scholes value equation needs delta-compatible holdings: a specified portfolio is self-financing if and only if its value solves the equation and g=px​. Indeed p=0, g=1, h=−x/Bt​ satisfies the value equation but its proposed gains are dSt​−ρSt​dt, which have nonzero Brownian part while dp=0. Thus the printed final equivalence is valid as an existence assertion for the delta hedge, not for every decomposition of the same value. No change to the valid two holdings equations is needed.
With continuous underlying stock dividends, the stock's gain is dSt​+θSt​dt, so self-financing becomes dVt​=gt​(dSt​+θSt​dt)+ht​dBt​. Under the risk-neutral measure the ex-dividend stock drift is (ρ−θ)St​. Matching its diffusion still gives g=px​, and matching its gain drift gives the Black-Scholes equation with continuous stock dividends
pt​+21​σ2x2pxx​+(ρ−θ)xpx​−ρp=0.​
(34)
Only the coefficient of xpx​ changes. The −ρp discount term remains, because the claim is financed at the bank rate; there is no separate source term for a dividend paid by the claim itself. The dividends here are paid by the underlying stock.

A one-factor short-rate model uses a single scalar state, normally the short rate itself. Under the physical probability measure write
drt​=μ(t,rt​)dt+η(t,rt​)dWt​,Bt​=exp(∫0t​rs​ds).
(48)
The second process is the continuous-time bank account. Specifying the physical diffusion alone does not determine bond prices: it must be accompanied by the market price of risk λ(t,r), or equivalently by pricing-measure dynamics. If the Girsanov theorem applies to the likelihood E(−∫λdW), then under the risk-neutral measure
drt​=μQ​(t,rt​)dt+η(t,rt​)dWtQ​,μQ​=μ−ηλ.
(49)
Changing measure alters drift, not quadratic variation. The coefficients need conditions for a nonexplosive solution and for the relevant discounted prices and measure-change density to be true martingales.
A unit zero-coupon bond maturing at T has price
P(t,r;T)=EQ[exp(−∫tT​rs​ds)​rt​=r].​
(50)
The Markov property makes current time and rate sufficient state variables. Apply Itô formula to P(t,rt​;T)/Bt​. Its drift must vanish, giving the short-rate bond pricing equation
Pt​+μQ​Pr​+21​η2Prr​−rP=0,P(T,r;T)=1.
(51)
Conversely, under the appropriate boundary and integrability conditions the Feynman-Kac formula identifies its solution with the displayed discounted expectation. For a terminal rate-dependent payoff H(rT​), the same equation has terminal condition H instead of one. A coupon bond is a sum of such unit-bond prices weighted by the coupon payments.
The common market price of risk can also be derived from trading, instead of being introduced arbitrarily for each maturity. Under the physical measure a bond's diffusion exposure is ηPr​ and its excess drift above bank financing is Pt​+μPr​+η2Prr​/2−rP. For two nondegenerate traded bonds, combine their holdings to cancel their Brownian exposure. Absence of arbitrage requires that combination to earn the bank rate; hence their excess-drift-to-exposure ratios must coincide. Calling that common ratio λ yields the pricing equation with μQ​=μ−ηλ for every maturity. A derivative V(t,r) is then hedged locally by holding Vr​/Pr​ units of a nondegenerate bond and financing the remaining value through the bank account. Their Brownian exposures agree, and substituting both pricing equations proves the drift gain identity. This is short-rate diffusion hedging; it requires ηPr​=0, rather than a bare assertion that one factor automatically gives completeness everywhere.
The entire yield curve is generated consistently from these bond prices. The continuously compounded yield and the instantaneous forward rate are
y(t,T)=−T−tlogP(t,rt​;T)​,f(t,T)=−∂T​logP(t,rt​;T),f(t,t)=rt​.
(52)
These encode different summaries: the yield averages the forward rate over the remaining lifetime. Bond prices must satisfy P(t,t)=1 and be positive; they need not be below one in models permitting negative rates.
The Vasicek model has constant pricing dynamics
drt​=a(b−rt​)dt+ηdWtQ​,a,η>0.
(53)
Solving the linear equation gives rt+u​=b+(rt​−b)e−au+η∫tt+u​e−a(t+u−v)dWvQ​. Consequently, with τ=T−t and B(τ)=(1−e−aτ)/a,
∫tT​rs​ds=bτ+(rt​−b)B(τ)+η∫tT​B(T−v)dWvQ​.
(54)
This conditional normal random variable has variance η2∫0τ​B(u)2du. Its normal exponential moment yields
P(t,T)=A(τ)e−B(τ)rt​,logA=−b(τ−B)+2η2​∫0τ​B(u)2du.
(55)
Evaluating the elementary integral gives
B=a1−e−aτ​,logA=(b−2a2η2​)(B−τ)−4aη2​B2.​
(56)
This explicit form makes estimation and valuation convenient. The rate is Gaussian, its conditional mean reverts to b, and its long-run variance is η2/(2a). Its disadvantage, when nonnegative rates are desired, is a positive probability of negative rates.
The CIR model replaces constant volatility by square-root volatility:
drt​=a(b−rt​)dt+ηrt​​dWtQ​,a,b,η>0.
(57)
The standard solution stays nonnegative. The Feller positivity condition for the CIR model is 2ab≥η2 in this parameterization; with a positive initial rate it makes zero inaccessible. Below this threshold zero can be reached, although the process does not cross into negative rates. For an affine diffusion bond pricing ansatz P=A(τ)e−B(τ)r, substitution into the bond equation and matching its constant and rate coefficients gives
B′=1−aB−21​η2B2,(logA)′=−abB,B(0)=0, A(0)=1.
(58)
Writing d=a2+2η2​, factor the quadratic in the first equation and integrate from B=0; integrating the second equation then gives the CIR bond pricing formulas
B(τ)=(d+a)(edτ−1)+2d2(edτ−1)​,A(τ)=[(d+a)(edτ−1)+2d2de(a+d)τ/2​]2ab/η2.​
(59)
The square-root form produces an affine term structure while respecting nonnegative rates. These parameters are pricing-measure parameters; historical mean-reversion estimates alone do not determine them without a risk-premium specification.
Both preceding constant-parameter models constrain the family of initial curves. The Hull-White model allows a deterministic time-dependent drift:
drt​=(β(t)−art​)dt+ηdWtQ​.
(60)
For a smooth desired initial forward curve f0​(t), its exact forward-curve fit in the Hull-White model is obtained by taking
r0​=f0​(0),β(t)=f0′​(t)+af0​(t)+2aη2​(1−e−2at).​
(61)
To derive this rather than merely assert calibration, write rt​=xt​+ϕ(t), where dxt​=−axt​dt+ηdWtQ​, x0​=0. The conditional Gaussian-integral calculation at time zero gives

Finally, one-factor models are economical and often give explicit bond prices, but their single Brownian driver is restrictive. Bond diffusion exposures vi​=ηPi,r​ give the instantaneous covariance matrix vvT, of rank at most one. Nondegenerate maturity changes are instantaneously perfectly correlated, with correlation +1 or −1, though their finite-horizon correlations need not equal one. A single factor therefore cannot independently describe level, slope and curvature shocks to the whole yield curve. Multiple factors or a full forward-rate model offer greater flexibility. A valid one-factor model must specify the pricing drift, fit the initial curve to the required accuracy, and handle boundary and martingale conditions; naming a rate diffusion alone is not a complete pricing model.
