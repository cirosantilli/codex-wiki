For the polar decomposition of an invertible real matrix, start with C=FTF. For nonzero y, yTCy=∣Fy∣2>0, since detF>0 makes F invertible. The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices gives an orthonormal basis na​ with Cna​=λa2​na​ and λa​>0. Define
U=∑a​λa​na​⊗na​,R=FU−1.
(1)
Then U is a positive-definite symmetric matrix, U2=C, and
RTR=U−1FTFU−1=I,detR=detUdetF​=1,
(2)
because detU=det(FTF)​=detF. Thus R is an orthogonal matrix with positive determinant. Set V=RURT; it too is a positive-definite symmetric matrix, and VR=RU=F. Moreover V2=FFT. This proves
F=RU=VR,U=(FTF)1/2,V=(FFT)1/2,RTR=I,detR=1.​
(3)
The positive square root is unique: any positive symmetric square root of C commutes with C, preserves its eigenspaces and has the positive scalar square root on each of them. Hence U, then R and V, are unique. These are the right stretch tensor, rotation and left stretch tensor of the polar decomposition in continuum mechanics.
A standard spectral strain measure is
Ef=f(U)=∑a​f(λa​)na​⊗na​.
(4)
Take f∈C1(0,∞), f(1)=0 and f′(1)=1, with f strictly increasing; the customary stronger condition f′(λ)>0 gives a regular one-to-one parameterization of all positive principal stretches. The zero condition makes a rigid rotation strain free, monotonicity distinguishes extension from contraction and prevents nonunit stretch from being strain free, and the derivative normalization gives the ordinary infinitesimal strain. Indeed, for F=I+H,
U=I+2H+HT​+O(∥H∥2),Ef=2H+HT​+o(∥H∥).
(5)
Under a superposed spatial rotation F↦QF, FTF and hence Ef remain unchanged, giving material frame indifference. Choosing f(λ)=λ−1 satisfies all these conditions and gives the Biot strain tensor
E(1)=U−I.​
(6)
The meaning of work-conjugate stress and strain here is that a symmetric material tensor Tf satisfies
Tf:E˙f=PIi​F˙iI​
(7)
for every admissible rate, with both powers measured per reference volume. To keep the nominal-stress index convention explicit, write MiI​=PIi​, so M=PT and the power is M:F˙. Differentiating F=RU gives F˙=R(ΩU+U˙), where Ω=RTR˙ is skew. Conservation of angular momentum gives symmetry of MFT, or equivalently of RTMU. Therefore
M:F˙​=tr((RTM)TΩU)+(RTM):U˙=tr((RTMU)TΩ)+sym(RTM):U˙=sym(RTM):U˙.​
(8)
The first term vanishes because a symmetric tensor has zero contraction with a skew tensor. Since E˙(1)=U˙, the symmetric Biot stress is
T(1)=sym(PR)=21​(PR+RTPT).​
(9)
If the nominal stress is instead stored spatial-index first as M, the same answer is sym(RTM). The symmetrization is essential: PR need not itself be symmetric.

Let w=v′−v and D(v)=sym∇v. The supporting-hyperplane inequality for the convex viscous dissipation potential gives
Ω(D′)−Ω(D)≥σij​(Dij′​−Dij​).
(44)
The stress is symmetric because its argument is a symmetric rate-of-strain tensor, so σij​(Dij′​−Dij​)=σij​wi,j​. Subtracting the two functional values yields
F(v′)−F(v)≥∫D​σij​wi,j​dx−∫D​ρgi​wi​dx−∫St​​ti0​wi​dS.
(45)
Integration by parts turns the first integral into
∫∂D​σij​nj​wi​dS−∫D​σij,j​wi​dx.
(46)
Inertialess force balance is σij,j​+ρgi​=0. The variation vanishes on Sv​, and the traction is ti0​ on St​. Thus both the volume and boundary contributions cancel exactly, proving the convex viscous potential minimum principle
F(v′)≥F(v)for every admissible v′.​
(47)
Only convexity, not strict convexity, is required, so no uniqueness follows just from this argument. For incompressible flow, admissible trial velocities must also be divergence free. The pressure term then does no work, because (−pI):(D′−D)=−pdivw=0; the same proof applies to the deviatoric constitutive law.

For γ˙​=(2D:D)1/2>0, differentiation in the symmetric tensor space gives ∂γ˙​/∂Dij​=2Dij​/γ˙​. Therefore the Bingham fluid viscous dissipation potential gives
σij′​=2(γ˙​τ0​​+μ0​)Dij​.​
(48)
The deviatoric stress is trace free since D is trace free; an arbitrary pressure reaction completes the Cauchy stress tensor.
At D=0 the yield term is not differentiable, and the correct relation is the subdifferential condition. A symmetric trace-free stress S is admissible there if
S:E≤τ0​(2E:E)1/2for every symmetric trace-free E.
(49)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality shows that this is equivalent to ∥S∥F​≤2​τ0​; necessity follows by choosing E proportional to S. Hence the no-strain-rate restriction is
(21​σij′​σij′​)1/2≤τ0​.​
(50)
The quadratic term has zero derivative at zero and does not change this restriction. This is the unyielded part of the Bingham plastic law.
For flow along the plates, write y=x2​, v=(u(y),0,0) and take G=−p,1​>0 as the pressure-drop magnitude. The only nonzero rate-of-strain tensor entries are D12​=D21​=u′/2, so γ˙​=∣u′∣ and the yielded shear stress law is
σ12​=τ0​signu′+μ0​u′.
(51)
Force balance gives G+σ12,y​=0. The prescribed odd, continuous shear stress fixes its integration constant to zero, proving
σ12​(y)=−Gy.​
(52)
For μ0​>0 the inverse shear law is
u′(y)={0,(−Gy+τ0​signy)/μ0​,​∣Gy∣≤τ0​,∣Gy∣>τ0​.​
(53)
The original PDF also calls u odd. That assumption is inconsistent with a nonzero flow between identical fixed plates: the inverse shear law makes u′ odd, whereas the derivative of an odd u is even. Both can hold only if u′=0, and the no-slip wall values then give u=0. Thus with the literal odd-velocity requirement, there is no admissible flowing solution when Gh>τ0​. The standard pressure-driven velocity is even; replacing that erroneous parity yields the intended profile below.
If Gh≤τ0​, the whole gap is unyielded and the fixed no-slip walls force u=0. Otherwise let y0​=τ0​/G<h. The plug flow of a yield-stress fluid occupies ∣y∣≤y0​. In the upper yielded layer u′=(−Gy+τ0​)/μ0​, and integration from u(h)=0 gives
u(y)=2μ0​G​[(h−y0​)2−(y−y0​)2](y0​≤y≤h).
(54)
In the plug the velocity is the constant G(h−y0​)2/(2μ0​), and reflection gives the lower layer. The complete plane Poiseuille flow of a Bingham fluid answer is
Nonzero flow requires G>τ0​/h,u(y)=2μ0​G​[(h−y0​)2−(∣y∣−y0​)+2​].​
(55)
The profile is continuous with continuous first derivative at the plug boundaries, vanishes at both walls and tends to the ordinary parabolic profile when τ0​=0. If signed pressure gradients of either direction are allowed, the threshold is ∣G∣h>τ0​, and reversing the gradient reverses the velocity.

a,1​+b,2​=0,f(a,b)=k.

dsda​=α(−fb​a,1​+fa​a,2​)=α(fb​b,2​+fa​a,2​)=0.
(57)
Similarly, differentiating the yield relation in x1​ gives
dsdb​=α(−fb​b,1​+fa​b,2​)=−α(fb​b,1​+fa​a,1​)=0.
(58)
The associated flow rule and ϵ˙i3​=w,i​/2 give w,1​=2λ˙fa​, w,2​=2λ˙fb​. Consequently
dsdw​=2αλ˙(−fb​fa​+fa​fb​)=0.
(59)
Thus both shear stresses and the antiplane velocity are constant on each characteristic. Assume a regular yield gradient (fa​,fb​)=0. Since the stress is constant on a characteristic, its tangent (−fb​,fa​) has fixed direction, so the curves are straight lines, apart from arbitrary reparameterization by α.
In a centred antiplane plastic fan those lines emanate from the crack tip. Hence
a=a(ϕ),b=b(ϕ),w=w(ϕ),
(60)
and a radial line at angle ϕ from the x1​ axis must have
(cosϕ,sinϕ)∥(−fb​,fa​),fa​cosϕ+fb​sinϕ=0.

tanϕb​=−fb​fa​​at b=0,​

with the direction interpreted directly when the denominator vanishes. The original PDF reverses this ratio. Its displayed ratio is dx1​/dx2​, whereas the tangent of an angle from the x1​ axis is dx2​/dx1​. For the elliptic yield surface, with A,B>0,
