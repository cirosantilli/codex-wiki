Start with the prime decomposition of a closed orientable three-manifold. Its fundamental group is a free product of the prime-summand groups. A nontrivial abelian group cannot be a free product of two nontrivial groups. The same is true of a nontrivial finitely generated nilpotent group: it has nontrivial center, whereas such a free product has trivial center. Thus all nontrivial fundamental group comes from a single prime summand; simply connected summands do not affect the argument.
A prime but nonirreducible orientable summand is S2×S1, giving Γ≅Z. Otherwise the infinite-group prime summand is an irreducible three-manifold. The sphere theorem for three-manifolds gives π2​=0. Its simply connected universal cover is noncompact, so its top ordinary homology vanishes; its first and second homology also vanish by simple connectivity and the Hurewicz theorem. It is therefore acyclic and contractible by the Whitehead theorem. The summand is aspherical, and its closed orientable three-dimensional classifying space gives integral cohomological dimension of a group equal to three: the cellular resolution gives the upper bound and top Poincare duality gives the lower bound.
If Γ is torsion-free abelian, finite generation makes it Zr. Its integral cohomological dimension of a group is r, as one sees from the standard r-torus. The aspherical case therefore has r=3. We obtain
Γ≅ZorZ3.​
(10)
Both occur, on S2×S1 and the three-torus.
For the infinite torsion-free nilpotent case, the same reduction gives either Z or an aspherical group of cohomological dimension three. By the Malcev completion of a torsion-free nilpotent group, the latter is a lattice in a simply connected three-dimensional nilpotent Lie group. A nonabelian three-dimensional nilpotent Lie algebra has abelianization of dimension at least two: a nilpotent algebra generated modulo its derived algebra by one element would be generated by that element and thus be abelian. Its derived algebra is therefore one-dimensional, and nilpotence makes that line central. Hence it has a basis with a single nonzero bracket [Y,T]=X; the other possibility is an abelian algebra. In the nonabelian case the lattice's center is an isolated infinite cyclic subgroup, generated by x, and the quotient by it is Z2. Choose lifts y,t of a basis of that quotient. Their commutator is xb for a nonzero integer b, and normal forms give
Γb​=⟨x,y,t∣[x,y]=[x,t]=1, tyt−1=xby⟩.
(11)
The subgroup A=⟨x,y⟩≅Z2 is normal and Γb​/A≅Z. In its ordered basis x,y, conjugation by t acts by
(10​b1​).​
(12)
This is the unipotent torus-bundle group. Indeed, its normal forms multiply according to
(xmyntk)(xm′yn′tk′)=xm+m′+bkn′yn+n′tk+k′.
(13)
Projection to the n,k coordinates, followed by inspection of the central m coordinate, proves torsion-freeness. All commutators lie in ⟨xb⟩, which is central, proving nilpotence. The mapping torus of the torus automorphism with this matrix realizes every integer b as a closed orientable three-manifold; b=0 gives Z3, while b=0 gives the Nil cases. Thus the complete list of groups under the nilpotent hypotheses is Z and the groups Γb​.
Here is the center argument, with its literal qualification. If a nilpotent group with abelian quotient Γ/A were nonabelian, the last nonzero term γc​Γ of its lower central series would satisfy
1=γc​Γ⊆[Γ,Γ]⊆A,γc​Γ⊆Z(Γ).
(14)
Consequently Z(Γ)∩A=1 forces the group to be abelian. For the specific nontrivial rank-two A in the printed family the hypothesis cannot actually occur: when b=0, x∈Z(Γb​)∩A; when b=0, the intersection is all of A. The implication is valid, but should not be used to invent an example satisfying this impossible extra condition.
A torsion-free finite extension of a lattice with quotient C4​ is realized by the quarter-turn flat three-manifold. On R3 let
a(u,v,w)=(u+1,v,w),b(u,v,w)=(u,v+1,w),s(u,v,w)=(−v,u,w+1/4).
(15)
Let c be unit translation in the third coordinate. Then sas−1=b, sbs−1=a−1, scs−1=c, and s4=c. The lattice Λ=⟨a,b,c⟩≅Z3 is normal and the quotient is C4​. A representative ambncksj, 0≤j<4, with j=0 has third-coordinate displacement k+j/4=0, so has no fixed point; for j=0 a nonidentity element is a nonzero translation. This proves torsion-freeness. The action is properly discontinuous, cocompact and orientation preserving, so it gives a closed orientable flat three-manifold with precisely the required extension.
For faithful linear holonomy on the maximal translation lattice, the holonomy groups of closed orientable flat three-manifolds are
1,C2​,C3​,C4​,C6​,C2​×C2​.​
(16)
The cyclic examples replace the quarter-turn by a planar lattice rotation of order m=2,3,4,6 and a translation of 1/m along its axis. The square and triangular plane lattices realize these orders; the trace condition 2cos(2π/m)∈Z explains the crystallographic restriction. The remaining case is obtained from
α(u,v,w)=(u+1/2,−v,−w),β(u,v,w)=(−u,v+1/2,−w+1/2).
(17)
Their squares translate by one in the first and second directions, and (αβ)2 translates by minus one in the third. Their linear parts generate C2​×C2​. The three nontranslation cosets have half-integral displacement along their respective fixed axes, so every element acts without a fixed point. These constructions realize every member of the orientable three-dimensional Euclidean holonomy classification.
The maximality/faithfulness restriction is essential. Taken literally as a question about arbitrary finite group quotients, the wording has more answers: Z has every finite cyclic quotient, and Z3 has, for example, quotient C5​. An exact criterion for the literal extension question is as follows. Choose an action Q→Aut(A) and an extension class e∈H2(Q,A), where A=Z or Z3. For every subgroup ⟨q⟩ of prime order p, require
res⟨q⟩​e=0  in  Aq/(1+q+⋯+qp−1)A.​
(18)
To prove this, lift q to g. Its pth power is in Aq, and replacing g by ag changes that power by the displayed norm of a. Thus a lift of order p exists exactly when this restriction is zero. Any torsion element has a prime-order power, so the condition is both necessary and sufficient. For an oriented flat realization in rank three the linear action must also preserve orientation. This distinguishes a complete extension criterion from the finite faithful-holonomy list.
