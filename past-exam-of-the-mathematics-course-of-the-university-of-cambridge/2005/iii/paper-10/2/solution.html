The standard simplicial decomposition of a sphere Fn is the boundary of the cross-polytope with vertices ±e1​,…,±en+1​, identified radially with Sn. A face chooses distinct coordinate indices and one sign for each; it never contains an antipodal pair.
A regular antipodal triangulation of a sphere is invariant under the antipodal map and has the nested coordinate equators S0⊂S1⊂⋯⊂Sk−1 as subcomplexes. In particular each equator separates its sphere into two triangulated hemispheres. These conditions are preserved by barycentric subdivision.
For a simplicial map f:F→Fn, a positive alternating simplex of dimension j has distinct image labels
+ei0​​,−ei1​​,…,(−1)jeij​​,i0​<⋯<ij​.
(7)
The opposite alternating pattern is negative; all other simplices, including collapsed images, are neutral.
To prove the antipodal alternating-simplex parity lemma, let pj​ count positive j-simplices in the equatorial Sj. There is exactly one positive vertex in S0, so p0​=1. A j-simplex has an odd number of positive alternating facets precisely when it is positive or negative alternating. Indeed an alternating sequence has exactly one positive alternating deletion; a nonalternating sequence has either zero or two. For a collapsed image, a positive facet can occur only when exactly one label is repeated; deleting either copy gives two such facets.
Count incidences of positive (j−1)-faces with j-simplices in one closed hemisphere. Interior faces contribute twice and equatorial faces once. The count modulo two is therefore pj−1​. It is also the number of positive or negative j-simplices in that hemisphere modulo two. Because f is antipodal, each alternating pair σ,−σ has one positive and one negative member, with one member in each hemisphere. Thus
pj​≡pj−1​(mod2).
(8)
Induction gives odd pk​, proving the existence of a positive k-simplex. In particular, an antipodal simplicial map F→Fk−1 is impossible: a positive k-simplex would require k+1 distinct coordinate labels but only k are available.
The Borsuk-Ulam theorem states that every continuous map g:Sk→Rk has some x with g(x)=g(−x). First rule out a continuous antipodal map h:Sk→Sk−1 for k≥1. By uniform continuity, take a sufficiently fine regular barycentric subdivision so that endpoints of each edge have images less than 2/k​ apart. Label a vertex by sgn(hi​)ei​, where i is the smallest index maximizing ∣hi​∣. Since h is unit length, this maximum is at least 1/k​; two adjacent vertices cannot have opposite labels. The labels therefore define an antipodal simplicial map to Fk−1, contradicting the preceding result.
If the stated Borsuk-Ulam theorem failed, the map
h(x)=∥g(x)−g(−x)∥g(x)−g(−x)​
(9)
would be just such a continuous antipodal map. This proves the required version; k=0 is immediate.
For the Kneser conjecture, equivalently Lovász theorem on Kneser graphs, the claim is
χ(KG(N,r))=N−2r+2(N≥2r≥2)​.
(10)
The Kneser graph joins disjoint r-subsets of [N]. Let d=N−2r+1. Colour each r-set by its least element in [d] when it has one, and otherwise by one final colour. A colour class indexed by i is an intersecting family because all its members contain i; the final class lies in a (2r−1)-set and is also intersecting. This proves the upper bound d+1.
For the lower bound, first deduce the open-cover Lusternik-Schnirelmann-Borsuk theorem. An open cover U0​,…,Ud​ of Sd admits a continuous partition of unity ϕ0​,…,ϕd​ subordinate to it. Apply the Borsuk-Ulam theorem to (ϕ1​,…,ϕd​). Equal values at x,−x and ∑i​ϕi​=1 make all values equal. At least one common value is positive, so some Ui​ contains an antipodal pair.
Now suppose the r-sets could be coloured with only d colours, each an intersecting family. Choose N points v1​,…,vN​∈Sd such that every d+1 of their vectors are linearly independent. For example, normalize (1,ti​,…,tid​) for distinct real ti​; the Vandermonde determinant proves this property. The general-position hemisphere count says that at most d points lie on any equator. Since N−d=2r−1, at least one of the opposite open hemispheres determined by x,−x contains an r-set of the labelled points.
Let Ui​ be the open set of directions whose positive open hemisphere contains an r-set of colour i. It contains no antipodal pair, as such a pair would give two disjoint sets of that colour. The compact set K=Sd∖⋃i=1d​Ui​ also contains no antipodal pair: otherwise each of the two opposite hemispheres would have at most r−1 points, contradicting the count 2r−1.
If K is nonempty, compactness gives a positive set distance between K and −K; an open neighbourhood U0​ of radius less than half that set distance still has no antipodal pair. If K is empty, take U0​=∅. In either case U0​,U1​,…,Ud​ is an open cover with no member containing an antipodal pair, contradicting the Lusternik-Schnirelmann-Borsuk theorem. Hence at least d+1 colours are required.
