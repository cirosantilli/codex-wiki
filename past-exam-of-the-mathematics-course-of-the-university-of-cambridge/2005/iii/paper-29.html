We first give an auxiliary-polynomial proof, rather than assuming a transcendence theorem for exponentials. Suppose iπ were algebraic, and let δ1​=iπ,δ2​,…,δt​ be its conjugates. Since the first factor vanishes,
∏ν=1t​(1+eδν​)=0.
(1)
Expand and collect equal subset sums of the δν​. This gives k+∑j=1s​bj​eβj​=0, where k≥1 counts the zero sums, the βj​ are distinct nonzero algebraic numbers, and each bj​ is a positive integer. The exponents and their weights are invariant under every Galois permutation.
Choose a positive integer c such that all cβj​ are algebraic integers. Their Galois-invariant monic polynomial R(u)=∏j​(u−cβj​) has rational algebraic-integer coefficients, hence lies in Z[u]. Its constant term A=R(0) is a nonzero integer. For a prime p define
fp​(z)=(p−1)!(cz)p−1R(cz)p​,Fp​(z)=∑h=0(s+1)p−1​fp(h)​(z).
(2)
At each βj​, derivatives below order p vanish. Every higher derivative is chh!/(p−1)! times an algebraic integer, so Fp​(βj​) is divisible by p in the ring of algebraic integers. At zero the only derivative not visibly divisible by p is the one of order p−1, and thus
Fp​(0)∈Z,Fp​(0)≡cp−1Ap(modp).
(3)
Galois invariance makes ∑j​bj​Fp​(βj​) rational; it is therefore an integer, divisible by p. Consequently
Sp​=kFp​(0)+∑j​bj​Fp​(βj​)∈Z,Sp​≡kcp−1Ap(modp).
(4)
For every prime not dividing kcA, this is a nonzero integer.
The finite derivative sum satisfies Fp′​−Fp​=−fp​, so integration along the straight segment to βj​ gives
eβj​Fp​(0)−Fp​(βj​)=eβj​∫0βj​​e−zfp​(z)dz.
(5)
Sum with weights bj​ and use ∑bj​eβj​=−k. The result is
Sp​=−∑j​bj​eβj​∫0βj​​e−zfp​(z)dz.
(6)
On these finitely many fixed compact segments, ∣cz∣, ∣R(cz)∣ and ∣e−z∣ are bounded independently of p. Hence ∣Sp​∣≤C1​C2p​/(p−1)!→0. There are arbitrarily large primes not dividing the fixed integer kcA, contradicting ∣Sp​∣≥1. Therefore π is transcendental​. This is the Hermite auxiliary-polynomial proof for pi.
The more general Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem states that exponentials of distinct algebraic numbers are linearly independent over Q​. Equivalently, exponentials of rationally independent algebraic numbers are algebraically independent over Q​. In particular, the exponential of a nonzero algebraic number is transcendental.
Suppose C=cosαcosβ were algebraic. The exponential formula for cosine gives
ei(α+β)+ei(α−β)+ei(−α+β)+e−i(α+β)−4Ce0=0.
(7)
After combining equal exponents this is an algebraic-coefficient linear relation among exponentials of distinct algebraic numbers. At least one exponent is nonzero, since α+β and α−β cannot both vanish when α,β=0. Its coefficient is a positive integer, even when two exponents coincide. The relation is therefore nontrivial and contradicts the theorem. Thus cosαcosβ is transcendental​, including the cases α=±β.

An integer version of Siegel lemma is as follows. For M<N homogeneous linear equations Az=0, with integer matrix entries bounded in absolute value by A0​≥1, there is a nonzero integer solution satisfying
∥z∥∞​≤⌊(2NA0​)M/(N−M)⌋.​
(8)
Put the displayed integer bound equal to Q and evaluate the equations at every vector in {0,…,Q}N. There are (Q+1)N inputs and at most (2NA0​Q+1)M outputs. But 2NA0​Q+1≤2NA0​(Q+1) and (Q+1)N−M>(2NA0​)M, so the number of inputs is larger. Two distinct inputs have identical outputs; their difference proves the claim. This is the pigeonhole proof of integer Siegel lemma. Rational coefficients are handled by clearing denominators. Over a number field of degree d, expanding in a rational basis gives at most dM rational equations, so the same construction applies when N>dM, with the height of the resulting integer matrix controlling the bound.
Siegel's original arithmetic definition starts with an entire series E(z)=∑n≥0​an​zn/n!, with all an​ in a fixed number field. For every ϵ>0 there must be positive integers dn​ such that dn​a0​,…,dn​an​ are algebraic integers, and both dn​ and the absolute values of every conjugate of an​ are Oϵ​(nϵn). The growth condition ensures convergence everywhere. The stronger, now customary strict E-function definition uses bounds Cn+1 for a fixed C and also includes the requirement of a nonzero linear differential equation over Q​(z). In the original formulation, that differential condition is explicitly part of the system hypothesis in the theorem below. These definitions should be distinguished; the exponentials used here satisfy even the stronger conditions.
The Siegel–Shidlovsky theorem states that if a vector of E-functions satisfies f′=A(z)f, with A(z) a matrix of rational functions over Q​, then for every nonzero algebraic ξ which is not a pole of A,
trdegQ​​Q​(f1​(ξ),…,fs​(ξ))=trdegQ​(z)​Q​(z)(f1​(z),…,fs​(z)).​
(9)
In particular, algebraic independence of the functions implies algebraic independence of the values. The exclusion of zero is necessary: their Taylor values there are algebraic.
Take rationally independent algebraic γ1​,…,γs​ and fj​(z)=eγj​z. Their coefficients are γjn​, their conjugates grow exponentially, and powers of a fixed common denominator clear all coefficients through degree n. They satisfy the nonsingular diagonal system fj′​=γj​fj​.
To check functional independence, a polynomial relation among these exponentials, after clearing rational-function denominators, would be ∑m​Pm​(z)eλm​z=0 with distinct λm​=∑j​mj​γj​. Such an exponential polynomial cannot vanish identically. To isolate a nonzero term with exponent λ0​, apply ∏λ=λ0​​(D−λ)degPλ​+1. All other terms vanish, while the remaining polynomial is acted on by operators D+λ0​−λ, each injective on polynomials because its nonzero constant preserves a nonzero leading coefficient. Thus the remaining term is nonzero, a contradiction.
The theorem at ξ=1 now makes eγj​ algebraically independent. For arbitrary distinct algebraic exponents αi​, choose a rational basis of their span and divide its elements by a common denominator so that every αi​ is an integer combination of the resulting γj​. Then eαi​ are distinct Laurent monomials in eγj​. A linear relation among them becomes a nonzero polynomial relation after multiplying by a common monomial, contradicting independence. These particular E-functions therefore yield the full Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem.

Write f(z)=∑n≥0​zln. The authoritative PDF has exponent ln; the geometric-series exponent in the converted TeX is a transcription error. On the unit disk this Fredholm series satisfies
f(zl)=f(z)−z.​
(10)
First it is transcendental as a function. If ζls=1, the terms with n≥s in f(ρζ) are the positive terms ρln and their sum tends to infinity as ρ↑1; the finite preceding sum stays bounded. Thus every root of unity of l-power order is a singularity. These roots are dense on the unit circle. An algebraic function has only finitely many possible branch points or poles, as seen from its defining polynomial's leading coefficient and discriminant. Hence f cannot be algebraic over C(z).
Suppose now that α is algebraic, 0<∣α∣<1, and a=f(α) is algebraic. For a positive integer N, a polynomial P(X,Y)∈Z[X,Y], nonzero and of degree at most N in each variable, can be chosen so that
G(z)=P(z,f(z))=O(zT),T=(N+1)2−1.
(11)
Indeed the first T Taylor coefficients impose T homogeneous integer equations on (N+1)2 coefficients. There is a nonzero rational solution, which can be cleared to an integer one. Functional transcendence ensures G is not identically zero.
Iterating the functional equation gives
f(αlk)=a−∑j=0k−1​αlj.
(12)
Thus βk​=G(αlk) belongs to the fixed field K=Q(α,a) and is nonzero for all sufficiently large k, since a nonzero analytic G has no sufficiently small nonzero zeros. Its analytic upper bound is
∣βk​∣≤CP​∣α∣Tlk.
(13)
Choose an integer q≥1 making qα,qa algebraic integers, let d=[K:Q], and choose M≥2 bounding every conjugate of α and a. Then qlkαlk and qlk−1f(αlk) are algebraic integers. Therefore
Dk​βk​ is integral,Dk​=qN(lk+lk−1).
(14)
For every embedding σ of K, the coefficient sum L(P) bounds the polynomial and gives
∣σβk​∣≤L(P)(k+1)NMN(lk+lk−1).
(15)
Taking the norm of the nonzero integral Dk​βk​ yields a nonzero rational integer, of absolute value at least one. Consequently
log∣βk​∣≥−NC0​lk−ON​(log(k+1)),C0​=(1+1/l)[dlogq+(d−1)logM].
(16)
On the other hand the upper bound is log∣βk​∣≤−Tλlk+OP​(1), where λ=−log∣α∣>0. Choose N so large that Tλ>NC0​, possible because T=N2+2N. Letting k→∞ contradicts the two bounds. This proves, by the Mahler method, f(α) is transcendental for 0<∣α∣<1​ at every algebraic α.
For the shorter rational-approximation argument at 1/2, truncate after n=m and write sm​=pm​/qm​ with qm​=2lm. The numerator is odd because its last summand is one and all previous summands are even, so this fraction is reduced. The tail satisfies
0<f(1/2)−qm​pm​​<2qm−l​.
(17)
The value is irrational: if it were P/Q, the positive difference from a truncation would be at least 1/(Qqm​), contradicting the tail bound for large m. If it were algebraic irrational and l≥3, choose 0<ϵ<l−2. Eventually the displayed upper bound is below qm−2−ϵ​ for infinitely many reduced fractions. The Roth theorem says that an algebraic irrational admits only finitely many such approximations. This contradiction proves transcendence directly. The argument does not cover l=2, because its exponent is not strictly larger than two; the Mahler proof does cover that case.

The Gelfond–Schneider theorem says that if a=0,1 is algebraic and b is algebraic irrational, every number ebλ with eλ=a is transcendental. Thus the statement includes every determination of the power, not merely a chosen principal branch.
Here is an auxiliary-function outline with the arithmetic and analytic steps specified. Assume v=ebλ were algebraic, and put K=Q(a,b,v), d=[K:Q]. For a large integer T, choose degrees L1​≍TlogT and L2​≍T/logT, with constants large enough that (L1​+1)(L2​+1)>2d(T+1)2. Apply Siegel lemma to impose
P(s+tb,asvt)=0(0≤s,t≤T)
(18)
on a nonzero polynomial P(X,Y)∈Z[X,Y] of these two degrees. Each field equation gives at most d rational equations. Clearing denominators and bounding conjugates gives equation-coefficient logarithmic size O(L1​logT+L2​T); the surplus of variables therefore permits coefficient height H with
logH=O(L1​logT+L2​T)=o(T2).
(19)
The entire function F(z)=P(z,eλz) is nonzero: its polynomial-exponential terms have distinct exponents jλ, and their independence follows by the differential-operator argument in question 2. Here λ=0. Its maximum modulus satisfies logMF​(R)=O(logH+L1​logR+L2​R). The grid points s+tb are distinct because b is irrational, and lie in a disk of radius (1+∣b∣)T.
Suppose the vanishing grid has been extended to 0≤s,t≤B, for some B≥T. Divide F by the product of its (B+1)2 known linear zero factors and use the maximum principle on a disk of radius CB, with C large and fixed. At a point of the doubled grid, the quotient estimate gives
log∣F(s+tb)∣≤−c1​B2+O(logH+L1​logB+L2​B),0≤s,t≤2B,
(20)
if the value is nonzero. But this value equals the algebraic number P(s+tb,asvt). Clearing its denominators and taking its norm gives the opposite bound
log∣F(s+tb)∣≥−C1​(logH+L1​logB+L2​B).
(21)
For sufficiently large initial T, the terms on the right are o(B2) uniformly for every B≥T: L2​/B=O(1/logT) and L1​logB/B2=O((logT)2/T). Thus every doubled-grid value must be zero. Iterating doubles the grid indefinitely. This supplies quadratically many distinct zeros in disks of linearly growing radius, whereas Jensen's formula and the exponential-polynomial growth bound allow only O(R) zeros for a nonzero F. The contradiction proves the theorem. This is the Schneider lattice proof of the Gelfond–Schneider theorem.
Now fix any logarithms of algebraic α,β=0,1. Their values are nonzero. If q=logα/logβ were algebraic irrational, then eqlogβ=α would be algebraic, contradicting the theorem with base β. Hence
logβlogα​ is either rational or transcendental.​
(22)
For arbitrarily many logarithms the qualitative Baker theorem on algebraic linear combinations of logarithms states that logarithms of algebraic numbers which are linearly independent over Q remain independent over Q​, even after adjoining one to the list. Equivalently a nonzero homogeneous algebraic-coefficient linear combination of such logarithms is transcendental. Rational relations are therefore the only source of homogeneous algebraic relations among the chosen logarithms.

Use the following fixed-number form of a logarithmic estimate: for chosen logarithms of fixed nonzero algebraic numbers a1​,…,as​, a nonzero form Λ=∑bj​logaj​ with integer coefficients ∣bj​∣≤B satisfies ∣Λ∣≥c(2B)−C, for fixed positive constants c,C. This follows from the Baker lower bound for a homogeneous linear form in logarithms.
By the Dirichlet unit theorem, write units as x=ζ∏ϵjmj​​ and y=ξ∏ϵjnj​​, with finitely many roots of unity and fixed fundamental units. If the unit rank is zero, there are already only finitely many choices. Otherwise put B=max(1,∣mj​∣,∣nj​∣). The real logarithmic embedding of the free unit group has full rank and lies in the product-formula hyperplane. Equivalence of norms in that finite-dimensional space shows that a unit with exponent size B has some archimedean conjugate of modulus at most e−c0​B: a large positive logarithmic coordinate forces a comparably large negative one, and a large negative coordinate itself suffices.
Interchanging the two terms if necessary, choose such a conjugate for y. At this embedding the equation makes σ(αx)=1−σ(βy) with ∣σ(βy)∣≤C0​e−c0​B. For large B define the logarithm near one. It is nonzero because βy=0, and
0<∣Λ∣=∣log(σ(αx))∣≤2C0​e−c0​B.
(23)
On fixed branches it is
Λ=logσ(α)+logσ(ζ)+∑j​mj​logσ(ϵj​)+2klog(−1)
(24)
for an integer k=O(B). The bound on k follows from summing the fixed imaginary parts and then reducing to the near-one branch. There are only finitely many possible embeddings and roots of unity, so the stated lower bound applies uniformly with coefficients O(B). It contradicts the exponential upper bound when B is large. Hence the exponents are bounded and αx+βy=1 has finitely many unit solutions​ in OK×​. This is the unit equation over a number field proof; “unit” here means an algebraic-integer unit, not an arbitrary nonzero field element.
For the Thue theorem, let F∈Z[X,Y] be irreducible homogeneous of degree d≥3 and let m=0 be fixed. In a splitting field L, write F(X,Y)=a∏i​(X−θi​Y) with distinct roots. Choose a fixed integer c so that each cθi​ is integral and cdm/a is a nonzero integer. For a solution (u,v) the nonzero algebraic integers ti​=c(u−θi​v) satisfy ∏i​ti​=cdm/a. Their principal ideals therefore divide a fixed ideal and have only finitely many possibilities. For each occurring principal ideal choose a generator γi​, so ti​=γi​εi​ with εi​ a unit.
Any three distinct roots give the identity
(θ2​−θ3​)t1​+(θ3​−θ1​)t2​+(θ1​−θ2​)t3​=0.
(25)
Divide by the third term and absorb the finitely many generator ratios into the coefficients. This is a fixed-coefficient unit equation in ε1​/ε3​ and ε2​/ε3​, so it has finitely many solutions for each generator choice. Thus ρ=t1​/t3​ has finitely many values. The relation (1−ρ)u=(θ1​−ρθ3​)v fixes a rational direction whenever an integer solution exists; ρ=1 simply gives v=0. On each such direction, (u,v)=k(u0​,v0​) for a primitive integer pair, and kdF(u0​,v0​)=m allows finitely many k. This proves the desired finiteness. When m=0, irreducibility excludes a nonzero rational root direction, leaving only (0,0).
The argument also proves finiteness for any binary form with at least three distinct projective roots: irreducibility was used only to guarantee distinct roots and to dispose of the zero-value case. A rational invertible change of variables can move every projective root away from infinity; after clearing its fixed denominators, the resulting pairs lie in an integer lattice and the same proof applies. This stronger nonzero-value version will apply to the binomial forms in question 6.

The abc conjecture asserts that for every ϵ>0 there is Kϵ​≥1 such that coprime positive integers A+B=C satisfy
C≤Kϵ​rad(ABC)1+ϵ,​
(26)
where the radical is the product of the distinct prime divisors.
For a primitive positive Fermat triple, the three integers are pairwise coprime. Apply the conjecture to A=xn,B=yn,C=zn. Since x,y<z, rad(ABC)=rad(xyz)≤xyz<z3. Taking ϵ=1/6 yields zn−7/2≤K1/6​. For n≥4 and z≥2 this simultaneously gives
z≤K1/62​,n≤27​+log2​K1/6​.​
(27)
Thus there are finitely many primitive triples and exponents. The primitivity restriction is essential to this abc argument: a nonprimitive triple is a common multiple of a primitive one, and the conjecture normalizes that factor away rather than bounding it. Consequently the intended finiteness conclusion counts primitive triples, or scaling classes. The literal unrestricted Fermat assertion is true for the independent reason that Fermat's last theorem gives no positive solutions for n>2; it does not follow from the displayed primitive height bound alone.
There is a genuine exception to the second printed assertion. If a−b=c, then x=y=1 solves the equation for every n>2; for example (a,b,c)=(2,1,1) gives an infinite family. The correct uniform finiteness statement excludes max(x,y)=1.
For that corrected statement put H=max(x,y)≥2 and d=gcd(axn,byn). Since their difference is c, d∣c. Apply abc to byn/d+c/d=axn/d. Its radical is at most rad(abc)xy, and its largest term is at least min(a,b)Hn/c. Therefore
Hn−2(1+ϵ)≤min(a,b)cKϵ​rad(abc)1+ϵ​.
(28)
With ϵ=1/4, enlarge the fixed right side to D≥1. For n≥3 the exponent is at least 1/2, so
H≤D2,n≤25​+log2​D.​
(29)
This proves finiteness of all nonconstant-power solutions, without requiring x,y coprime.
For an unconditional proof, the diagonal case x=y=H≥2 satisfies (a−b)Hn=c and is elementary. If a=b, the unequal-variable gap is at least 2n−1, also bounding n. In the remaining case interchange variables and coefficients, changing the sign of c if necessary, to have 1≤X<Y and
AXn−BYn=δ,∣δ∣=c.
(30)
The nonzero real logarithmic form Λ=log(A/B)+nlog(X/Y) satisfies eΛ−1=δ/(BYn). Once BYn>2c, ∣Λ∣≤2c/(BYn), so log∣Λ∣≤−nlogY+Oa,b,c​(1). The two-logarithm Baker lower bound for a homogeneous linear form in logarithms, with the fixed height of A/B and height of X/Y at most logY, gives
log∣Λ∣≥−C(a,b)logYlog(2n).
(31)
Hence n≤C(a,b)log(2n)+Oa,b,c​(1) because Y≥2, and n is bounded. The omitted case BYn≤2c has bounded exponent and variables directly. For each of the finitely many exponents, aXn−bYn has at least three distinct projective roots. The stronger nonzero-value Thue theorem argument from question 5 therefore gives finitely many pairs, whether or not that binomial form is irreducible. This establishes the exponent bound for a binomial power equation and the corrected finiteness statement with no unproved hypothesis. The constant-power family remains the unavoidable exception.
