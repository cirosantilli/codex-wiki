Take rings and modules to be unital. Let Jℓ​ denote the intersection of the maximal left ideals. For each nonzero vector v of a simple module M on the left, the map R→M, r↦rv, is onto and its kernel is a maximal left ideal. Consequently Jℓ​ annihilates every simple module on the left. Conversely, annihilating R/L forces an element to belong to the maximal left ideal L. Thus
Jℓ​=⋂M simple left​AnnR​(M),
(1)
which shows that Jℓ​ is a two-sided ideal.
For j∈Jℓ​ and r∈R, the left ideal R(1−rj) cannot be proper: a containing maximal left ideal would also contain rj and hence 1. Therefore u(1−rj)=1 for some u∈R. Here u=1+urj also differs from 1 by an element of Jℓ​, so it too has a left inverse v. Multiplying the first equality by v gives 1−rj=v; hence (1−rj)u=1. We have proved that 1−rj is a unit, not just left invertible.
The identity
(1−ba)−1=1+b(1−ab)−1a
(2)
is verified by multiplying on either side, using (1−ab)−1(1−ab)=(1−ab)(1−ab)−1=1. It implies that 1−jr is a unit as well. If j were outside a maximal right ideal I, then I+jR=R, so 1−jr∈I for some r. A proper right ideal cannot contain a unit, giving a contradiction. Thus Jℓ​ belongs to every maximal right ideal. Applying the same argument to the opposite ring proves the reverse inclusion. This proves the Jacobson radical is independent of handedness:
J(R)=L maximal left⋂​L=I maximal right⋂​I.​
(3)
For the zero ring the intersections are interpreted as the whole ring, so the equality remains valid.
If a two-sided ideal N satisfies Nm=0, then for x∈N and r∈R, (rx)m=0. The finite geometric series ∑i=0m−1​(rx)i inverts 1−rx. The unit criterion for the Jacobson radical, also proved above by the maximal-ideal argument, gives N⊆J(R). This assertion concerns a nilpotent ideal; arbitrary nilpotent elements of a noncommutative ring need not lie in its Jacobson radical.
For the formal power series ring over the p-adic integers, a series f=∑i≥0​ai​ti is a unit exactly when a0​ is a unit of Zp​. Necessity follows by comparing constant coefficients. If a0​ is invertible, construct the inverse recursively by b0​=a0−1​ and bm​=−a0−1​∑i=1m​ai​bm−i​. Therefore the nonunits are exactly the series whose constant coefficient lies in pZp​. They form the unique maximal ideal (p,t), and the quotient is Fp​. Hence
J(Zp​[[t]])=(p,t).​
(4)

The variable t is central. To prove the leading-coefficient proof for a central polynomial extension, let I be any right ideal of R[t] and set
Ld​={ad​:∑i=0d​ai​ti∈I}⊆R.
(5)
Zero leading coefficients are allowed. Addition and right multiplication by constants show that Ld​ is a right ideal. Multiplication by t gives Ld​⊆Ld+1​. Since R is a right Noetherian ring, this chain stabilizes, say at LN​, and each Ld​ for d≤N has finitely many generators adj​. Choose fdj​∈I of degree at most d lifting these coefficients.
These finitely many polynomial lifts generate I on the right. Indeed, for f∈I of degree m, let d=min(m,N). Write its leading coefficient as ∑j​adj​rj​. Then
f−∑j​fdj​rj​tm−d
(6)
belongs to I and has degree less than m, because Lm​=Ld​ when m>N. Induction on degree reduces f to a right linear combination of the chosen lifts. This proves finite generation of every right ideal, and hence
R right Noetherian⟹R[t] right Noetherian.​
(7)
Centrality matters: it is what permits the displayed leading-coefficient cancellation without twisting the coefficients.

Use the degree-one convention implicit in this enveloping-algebra assertion: a degree-one almost commutative algebra has F0​R=k, a finite-dimensional F1​R containing 1 which generates R, and commutative associated graded ring. Taking the leading symbol of a commutator gives
[Fi​R,Fj​R]⊆Fi+j−1​R,
(8)
in particular [F1​R,F1​R]⊆F1​R. Thus g=F1​R, equipped with [u,v]=uv−vu, is a finite-dimensional Lie algebra. Bilinearity and antisymmetry are immediate, and expanding the three nested commutators proves the Jacobi identity.
Its inclusion into the associative algebra R is a Lie algebra homomorphism. The universal enveloping algebra is T(g) modulo the relations u⊗v−v⊗u−[u,v]. Sending the tensor generators to their elements of R kills these relations and gives an algebra homomorphism U(g)→R. It is surjective because F1​R generates R. Therefore
R≅U(g)/ker(U(g)→R),dimk​g<∞.​
(9)
The vector 1∈F1​R is retained as a central Lie algebra generator and maps to the associative identity; its corresponding relation in the kernel is that generator minus 1U(g)​. One should not first replace F1​R by F1​R/k1, since commutators of generators can be nonzero scalars.
Terminology is important here. Merely requiring a commutative finitely generated associated graded ring for an arbitrary weighted filtration is a broader definition of almost commutative algebra; it does not supply the finite-dimensional bracket-closed first step used in this argument. The degree-one assumptions make the requested enveloping presentation precise. The Noetherianity in part (b) also follows directly under the broader finite-generation convention.

Give U(g) its degree filtration. The Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem identifies its associated graded ring with Symk​g, a polynomial ring in dimk​g variables. The Hilbert basis theorem makes this graded algebra Noetherian on both sides.
Here is the required transfer, including why a finite list of symbols gives actual generators. For a right ideal I of a nonnegatively filtered ring A, its induced graded ideal grI is a homogeneous right ideal of grA. Choose finite homogeneous generators and lift them to u1​,…,um​∈I. For u∈I of degree d, express its leading symbol as a graded right linear combination of these symbols, using homogeneous coefficients of complementary degrees. Lift the coefficients to A and subtract the corresponding ∑i​ui​ai​. The remainder has degree strictly below d. Induction, terminating because degrees are nonnegative, proves I=∑i​ui​A. The identical argument with coefficients on the left treats left ideals.
This ascending filtered-graded transfer of Noetherianity proves that U(g) is right and left Noetherian. A quotient ring inherits either property: pull an ascending ideal chain back to the original ring, or take images of ideal generators. Part (a) therefore gives R right and left Noetherian. Alternatively, under the broader almost commutative algebra convention, its commutative finitely generated associated graded ring is directly a quotient of a finite-variable polynomial ring, and the same leading-symbol argument proves the conclusion.

A right Ore localization at S is a unital homomorphism ι:R→Q such that every ι(s), s∈S, is a unit, every element of Q is ι(r)ι(s)−1, and
kerι={r∈R:rt=0 for some t∈S}.
(10)
Equivalently, the usual fraction construction has this zero criterion and is universal among homomorphisms that invert S. Injectivity is required only when the denominators are regular elements of a ring. If 0∈S, every homomorphism inverting S has zero target; this is the degenerate zero-ring localization. For a nonzero localization we require 0∈/S.
The necessary and sufficient conditions are that S be a right denominator set:
  • For r∈R and s∈S, there are t∈S and b∈R with rt=sb; this is the right Ore condition.
  • If sr=0 with s∈S, there is t∈S with rt=0; this is right reversibility.
For necessity, write the element ι(s)−1ι(r) as ι(b)ι(t)−1. Multiplying by ι(s) on the left and ι(t) on the right gives ι(rt−sb)=0. The zero criterion supplies u∈S with (rt−sb)u=0. Hence r(tu)=s(bu), the right Ore condition in R, not merely an equality of images in Q. Also sr=0 makes ι(r)=0 because ι(s) is invertible, and the zero criterion gives rt=0. This proves both necessities.
The sufficiency is the Ore theorem for denominator sets. Concretely, right fractions can be represented by (r,s); a common-denominator equality has su=tv∈S and ru=r′v for suitable u,v∈R. The two conditions make the induced fraction equivalence transitive and make addition and multiplication well defined; the zero fraction is exactly the kernel displayed above. The requested direction of the theorem was necessity, for which the complete argument has just been given.
For a right Noetherian domain, any two nonzero principal right ideals intersect. To see this without using the desired localization, suppose bR∩aR=0 with a,b=0. The sum
bR+abR+a2bR+⋯
(11)
is direct: reduce a finite relation modulo aR to get its first summand zero, then cancel a in the noncommutative domain and repeat. Every summand is nonzero, so the finite partial sums form a strictly ascending chain of right ideals, contradicting the ascending chain condition. A nonzero intersection rR∩sR thus provides rt=sb=0, with t=0. This is the right Ore condition for S=R∖{0}. Reversibility is automatic in a noncommutative domain, and the zero criterion makes the map injective. The localization is a division ring, since a nonzero rs−1 has inverse sr−1.
The Goldie theorem states that a semiprime ring has a semisimple Artinian classical right ring of quotients if and only if it has finite right uniform dimension and satisfies the ascending chain condition on right annihilators. A right Noetherian ring satisfies the annihilator condition because right annihilators are right ideals. Its right regular module has finite uniform dimension: an infinite direct sum of nonzero submodules would give a strictly ascending chain of finite partial sums. A prime ring is semiprime, so the theorem applies to a prime right Noetherian ring R.
To obtain a single matrix-ring factor, not just a finite product, prove that its quotient Q is prime. A nonzero two-sided ideal I⊆Q contains a nonzero fraction rs−1 and hence the nonzero element r=(rs−1)s∈I∩R. The same holds for any other nonzero ideal K. Since R is prime, (I∩R)(K∩R)=0, so IK=0. Thus Q is prime. The Artin–Wedderburn theorem gives a finite product of matrix rings over division rings; primeness excludes two different nonzero factors with zero product. This proves the prime classical quotient of a prime right Goldie ring:
Qclr​(R)≅Mn​(D)for a division ring D.​
(12)
For the requested counterexample to Noetherianity, take R=k[x1​,x2​,…]. It is a commutative integral domain and has its field of fractions as its classical right ring of quotients. But (x1​)⊊(x1​,x2​)⊊⋯ is a strictly ascending ideal chain: evaluation at x1​=⋯=xm​=0 shows xm+1​ is not in the preceding ideal. Therefore existence of a classical quotient does not imply right Noetherianity.

Use the descending chain condition on the finite intersections of maximal right ideals. It gives a minimal such intersection K=M1​∩⋯∩Mr​. Intersecting K with any other maximal right ideal cannot decrease it, by minimality. Thus K lies in every maximal right ideal, and K=J(R). When J(R)=0, the diagonal map
RR​⟶⨁i=1r​R/Mi​,a⟼(a+Mi​)i​
(13)
is injective, and each summand on the right is a simple module.
For completeness, a submodule N of a finite direct sum of simple modules is semisimple. Induct on the number of summands. For S⊕T with S simple, N∩S is either 0 or S. In the first case projection into T identifies N with a submodule of T, so induction applies. In the second case subtracting the S coordinate shows N=S⊕(N∩T), and induction again applies. This also shows finite module length. Apply it to the image of the diagonal map to obtain
J(R)=0 and R right Artinian⟹RR​ semisimple.​
(14)
This is the semisimplicity of a right Artinian ring with zero radical. It does not assume right Noetherianity in order to prove it.

Maximal right ideals of R/J correspond to maximal right ideals of R, all of which contain J. Their intersection in the quotient is zero. Hence R/J is a right Artinian ring with zero Jacobson radical, and part (a) makes it semisimple.
Part (b) gives the finite radical filtration
R⊇J⊇J2⊇⋯⊇Jm=0.
(16)
Each factor Ji/Ji+1 is annihilated by J, so it is a right module over R/J. Every module over a semisimple ring is semisimple: a free module is a direct sum of simple summands of copies of the regular module, and a quotient of a semisimple module is again semisimple, because the images of its simple summands are either zero or simple and span the quotient. A maximal independent family of these simple images spans: any simple image not contained in the sum intersects it trivially and could be added. Each factor is also Artinian, as a subquotient of RR​. A semisimple Artinian module has only finitely many simple summands, since an infinite direct sum would give a descending chain by successively deleting summands. Consequently every factor has finite module length.
Concatenating composition series of these finitely many factors makes RR​ a module of finite module length. Such a module satisfies both chain conditions, since every strict change consumes a positive amount of length. Therefore R is right Noetherian. This is the Hopkins-Levitzki theorem, obtained from the two preceding results rather than invoked in their proofs.

Let K=k(z), where z is an indeterminate, and form the right Artinian triangular ring that is not left Noetherian
T=(k0​KK​).​
(17)
Multiplication uses the natural k–K bimodule structure on the upper-right entry. With the two diagonal idempotents e1​,e2​, the right regular module is e1​T⊕e2​T. The off-diagonal submodule of e1​T is one-dimensional over the bottom-right field K, hence simple. Its quotient is one-dimensional over the top-left field k, hence simple. Also e2​T is a simple right module over K. Thus TT​ has a composition series of length three, and in particular T is right Artinian.
On the left, multiplication of an off-diagonal matrix gives
(a0​mb​)(00​u0​)=(00​au0​).
(18)
Every k-subspace U⊆K therefore yields a left ideal of off-diagonal matrices with entries in U. The subspaces Um​=spank​{1,z,…,zm} are strictly increasing because z is transcendental. Their associated left ideals violate the ascending chain condition. Hence T is not left Noetherian, even though it is right Artinian and right Noetherian.

In a commutative ring, if xm=0, then the principal ideal (x) satisfies (x)m=0. Thus a semiprime ring has no nonzero nilpotent elements. Conversely, if Im=0 for an ideal I, then every x∈I satisfies xm=0; absence of nonzero nilpotent elements forces I=0. Hence commutative semiprimeness is exactly reducedness.
For a prime ideal P, let S=R∖P. Every ideal I of RP​=S−1R is the extension of its contraction Ic: if r/s∈I, multiply by the unit s/1 to get r/1∈I, so r∈Ic. If R is Noetherian, write Ic=(r1​,…,rm​); their images generate I. This proves that RP​ is Noetherian.
The extended ideal PRP​ is proper. Indeed 1=p/s would imply u(s−p)=0 for some u∈/P, and then us=up∈P, contradicting primeness and u,s∈/P. A fraction with numerator outside P is invertible, with inverse s/r. A fraction with numerator in P lies in the proper ideal PRP​ and cannot be invertible. Thus the nonunits are precisely PRP​, making it the unique maximal ideal. Therefore
RP​ is a Noetherian local ring with maximal ideal PRP​.​
(19)
To prove reducedness is detected by prime localizations, let x be a nilpotent element of R. Its image in every RQ​ is zero because these localizations are semiprime, hence reduced. If x=0, its annihilator Ann(x) is a proper ideal; choose a maximal ideal Q containing it. The zero criterion for localization says x/1=0 in RQ​ only if sx=0 for some s∈/Q, which would put s in Ann(x)⊆Q, a contradiction. Hence x=0, and R is semiprime. This argument does not need Noetherianity.
Primeness is not detected in the same way. Take R=k×k. Its two prime ideals are P1​=0×k and P2​=k×0, and its corresponding localizations are both isomorphic to the field k. To see these are all the prime ideals, the orthogonal idempotents e1​,e2​ have product zero, so a prime ideal contains one of them and is then the kernel of the other projection. Both localizations are prime rings, but R is not prime: e1​,e2​=0 and e1​e2​=0.

This part proves (c)⇒(a). Write the Weyl algebra generators as x1​,…,xn​,D1​,…,Dn​, with [Di​,xj​]=δij​ and the other generator commutators zero. The ordered monomials xαDβ form a basis over any field: one may construct the algebra by successively adjoining the commuting derivations Di​ to k[x1​,…,xn​], with multiplication Di​f=fDi​+∂f/∂xi​, obtaining unique ordered forms. This also makes An​(k) infinite-dimensional for n≥1.
Assume chark=0 and let I be a nonzero two-sided ideal. Choose a nonzero element a=∑cαβ​xαDβ∈I, and a nonzero term of maximal total degree, say (α0​,β0​). The relations give
[Di​,xαDβ]=αi​xα−ei​Dβ,[xi​,xαDβ]=−βi​xαDβ−ei​.

Apply (adD)α0​(adx)β0​ to a. Every lower-degree term vanishes. A term of the same degree survives only if all its exponents dominate (α0​,β0​), which forces equality. Thus the resulting element of I is

This part proves (a)⇒(b). If M=0 were a finite-dimensional unital module, its action would give a homomorphism
An​(k)⟶Endk​(M).
(22)
The kernel is a two-sided ideal and is proper because the identity acts as the identity on M. A simple ring therefore makes the homomorphism injective. This is impossible: An​(k) has the infinitely many linearly independent ordered monomials established in part (a), whereas the endomorphism algebra has dimension (dimk​M)2. This agrees with the Weyl algebra has no nonzero finite-dimensional modules criterion, but the injectivity argument proves the requested implication without presupposing characteristic zero.

The Bernstein inequality for Weyl algebra modules requires characteristic zero: for a nonzero finitely generated left or right module M over An​(k),
GKdimAn​(k)​M≥n.​
(24)
Equivalently, its Bernstein growth dimension of a Weyl algebra module is at least n. Without the characteristic-zero hypothesis this is false, as the module in part (c) has dimension zero in the growth sense. The proof below works over any characteristic-zero field, not just an algebraically closed one.
For a left module, choose a nonzero finite-dimensional generating subspace M0​ and put Mj​=Fj​An​M0​, where Fj​ is the Bernstein filtration. Ordered monomials give
dimk​Fj​An​=(2nj+2n​).
(25)
Multiplication defines the linear action map
Fj​An​⟶Homk​(Mj​,M2j​),a⟼(m↦am).
(26)
We prove this map injective by induction on j, which is the essential faithful finite-step action of a Weyl algebra argument. For j=0, an element is a scalar, and a scalar killing M0​=0 is zero. Suppose a∈Fj​ kills Mj​. For any generator z and m∈Mj−1​, both azm and zam vanish because zm∈Mj​ and m∈Mj​. Thus [a,z]∈Fj−1​ kills Mj−1​, so induction gives [a,z]=0 for every generator. By the explicit commutator formulas in part (a), commuting with all Di​ forces all positive xi​ exponents of a to vanish, and commuting with all xi​ forces all positive Di​ exponents to vanish. Characteristic zero is used to cancel the nonzero integer exponents. Hence a is scalar and, because it kills M0​, is zero. This proves injectivity.
Taking dimensions yields
(2nj+2n​)≤(dimk​Mj​)(dimk​M2j​)≤(dimk​M2j​)2.
(27)
The left side is asymptotic to j2n/(2n)!. Consequently dimk​M2j​≥Cjn for all sufficiently large j and some C>0. Taking logarithms and the limsup in the definition of Gelfand–Kirillov dimension of a module proves the claimed lower bound. For a good Bernstein filtration, the associated graded module is finite over k[x1​,…,xn​,ξ1​,…,ξn​]; its cumulative Hilbert function is eventually polynomial, and the same comparison says its degree is at least n.
For right modules, use the opposite ring isomorphism An​(k)≅An​(k)op sending xi​ to xi​ and Di​ to −Di​. It respects the Bernstein filtration, so the identical estimate applies. The bound is sharp: the polynomial module k[x1​,…,xn​], generated by 1, has Mj​ equal to the polynomials of degree at most j, of dimension (nj+n​). Its Gelfand–Kirillov dimension of a module is exactly n.
