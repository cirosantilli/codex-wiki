Let f be holomorphic on a neighborhood of the closure of a bounded region with piecewise smooth positively oriented boundary, with no boundary zeros and with f not identically zero. The argument principle states
2πi1​∫∂G​f(z)f′(z)​dz=a∈G:f(a)=0∑​ma​,​
(1)
where ma​ is the zero multiplicity. More generally a closed contour avoiding the zeros gives ∑a​ma​Ind(Γ,a), and the integral is the winding number of f∘Γ about zero.
To prove it, factor near a zero a as f(z)=(z−a)ma​h(z) with h(a)=0. Then f′/f=ma​/(z−a)+h′/h. Remove small disjoint circles about the finitely many zeros in the region. On the remainder f′/f is holomorphic, so the Cauchy integral theorem reduces the boundary integral to the sum of the small-circle integrals. Each is 2πima​ because h′/h is holomorphic on its small disc. This proves the formula. Along a contour, the imaginary part of dlogf is the change in a continuous argument, giving the winding interpretation and the indexed version.
Now choose a closed disc D(z0​,r)​⊂Ω on which z0​ is the only zero of the nonconstant limit f. Its boundary has min∣f∣>0. Local uniform convergence gives max∣fn​−f∣<min∣f∣ there for all sufficiently large n. The contour functions f+t(fn​−f), 0≤t≤1, have no boundary zero. Their winding numbers are constant under this homotopy, so the argument principle shows that fn​ has exactly the same positive number m of zeros, counted with multiplicity, inside the disc. This is the Rouche theorem argument, here derived from the contour count.
Choose a zero zn​ in that disc. For every 0<ϵ<r, the compact annulus ϵ≤∣z−z0​∣≤r contains no zero of f, so uniform convergence makes fn​ nonzero on it eventually. Therefore all of the chosen zeros eventually lie within ϵ of z0​. Consequently
fn​(zn​)=0,zn​⟶z0​.​
(2)
The maps fn​ are eventually not identically zero, since they converge at any fixed point where f is nonzero; thus their zero counts above are legitimate.
A double zero need not persist as a double zero. On the unit disc take f(z)=z2, fn​(z)=z2+1/n. Its zeros ±i/n​ converge to zero but are simple, while fn′​ vanishes only at zero and fn​(0)=1/n=0. Hence the additional simultaneous derivative condition cannot always be imposed. Local uniform convergence preserves total nearby multiplicity, not its concentration at a single point.

Use the curvature-minus-one normalization of the hyperbolic metric:
dsD​=λ(z)∣dz∣,λ(z)=1−∣z∣22​.​
(3)
The associated distance is d(z,w)=2artanh∣(z−w)/(1−wz)∣. A connected Riemann surface has its complete canonical Poincare metric on a Riemann surface precisely when its universal cover is conformally the unit disc. By the uniformization theorem, the other simply connected covering types are the complex plane and Riemann sphere. Disc automorphisms preserve λ(z)∣dz∣, as direct differentiation of (z−a)/(1−az) shows; therefore the metric descends through the deck group. This is the uniformization notion of hyperbolicity, not the different potential-theoretic Green-function classification.
First prove the needed Schwarz lemma. If F:D→D is holomorphic and F(0)=0, then F(z)/z extends holomorphically at zero. On ∣z∣=r<1 its modulus is at most 1/r. The maximum modulus principle and r↑1 give ∣F(z)∣≤∣z∣ and ∣F′(0)∣≤1.
Write ψa​(z)=(z−a)/(1−az). Conjugating f by the source and target disc automorphisms, ψf(a)​∘f∘ψa−1​ fixes zero. Applying the derivative bound gives
1−∣f(a)∣2∣f′(a)∣​≤1−∣a∣21​,f∗dsD​≤dsD​.​
(4)
Integrate along any piecewise smooth path and then take the infimum of lengths: d(f(z),f(w))≤d(z,w). This proves the Schwarz-Pick theorem. Contraction means nonexpansion, not a uniform Lipschitz constant strictly smaller than one; automorphisms preserve all distances.
If both R and S have disc universal covers, lift g∘πR​ to g​:D→D through πS​. The disc is simply connected, so the lift exists, and it is holomorphic because the covering maps are local biholomorphisms. Both covering maps are local hyperbolic isometries. The disc contraction therefore descends to g∗dsS​≤dsR​ and, by the path-length argument, dS​(g(p),g(q))≤dR​(p,q). If only S is known to have a hyperbolic metric, a nonconstant g forces R to have one too: otherwise lift from the plane or sphere universal cover of R to the disc cover of S. A bounded entire function is constant by the Liouville theorem, and a holomorphic map from the compact sphere into the disc is constant by the maximum modulus principle. Thus a nonconstant map into a hyperbolic surface is distance-nonincreasing between the canonical hyperbolic metrics; a nonhyperbolic source admits only constant such maps.
For the prescribed point, Schwarz-Pick theorem gives ∣ψw0​​(f(z))∣≤∣ψz0​​(z)∣. Therefore
h(z)=ψz0​​(z)ψw0​​(f(z))​
(5)
is holomorphic after filling the removable singularity at z0​, and ∣h∣≤1. Inverting the target automorphism proves
f(z)=w0​(z−z0​)h(z)+(1−z0​z)(z−z0​)h(z)+w0​(1−z0​z)​,Dsup​∣h∣≤1.​
(6)
Conversely every holomorphic h with this bound gives an admissible disc map: ∣ψz0​​(z)h(z)∣<1, its inverse target automorphism remains in the disc, and the denominator cannot vanish.
Fix z and put r=∣ψz0​​(z)∣<1. The possible intermediate values fill ∣v∣≤r, since constant choices h≡c, ∣c∣≤1, attain them all. Thus the one-point value region of a holomorphic disc map is ∣ψw0​​(w)∣≤r. Completing the square in ∣w−w0​∣2≤r2∣1−w0​w∣2 gives its Euclidean centre and radius:
C=1−r2∣w0​∣2(1−r2)w0​​,s=1−r2∣w0​∣2r(1−∣w0​∣2)​.​
(7)
This closed disc lies strictly inside the unit disc because r<1. For z=z0​ it degenerates to the single point w0​. Its boundary is attainable by unimodular constant h; requiring f to map into the open disc does not remove that boundary.

For a nonconstant meromorphic map, write Tf​(R)=m(R,f)+N(R;∞) in the usual normalization, where m=(2π)−1∫log+∣f(Reiθ)∣dθ. Let N(R;a) count a-points with their local degrees, and let N(R;a) count each distinct point once, using the same integrated logarithmic weights. At infinity the local degree is the pole order. The Nevanlinna deficiency and Nevanlinna ramification index are
δf​(a)=1−R→∞limsup​Tf​(R)N(R;a)​,θf​(a)=R→∞liminf​Tf​(R)N(R;a)−N(R;a)​.​
(8)
The latter is an asymptotic excess-multiplicity index, not the integer local ramification degree. The Nevanlinna first main theorem gives N(R;a)≤Tf​(R)+O(1) and identifies deficiency with the lower limiting normalized proximity. The target a ranges over the Riemann sphere, not just the real line.
The exponential is entire and has no poles. Consequently
Te​(R)=2π1​∫02π​max(0,Rcosθ)dθ=πR​​.
(9)
Thus c=1/π in this normalization.
To obtain the rational-composition growth, represent the nonconstant degree-d sphere map by relatively prime homogeneous polynomials (P,Q) of degree d. Their joint norm on the unit sphere in C2 has a positive minimum and finite maximum: simultaneous vanishing would contradict relative primeness on the projective line. Homogeneity therefore gives constants 0<A≤B<∞ with
A∥(w,s)∥d≤∥(P(w,s),Q(w,s))∥≤B∥(w,s)∥d.
(10)
Apply this to the entire nonvanishing lift (ez,1). The logarithmic joint norm differs from dlog∥(ez,1)∥ by a bounded function. Its circular mean minus the value at zero is the spherical characteristic. It differs from the ordinary characteristic by O(1): Jensen's formula applied to the entire denominator counts its zeros, while
0≤21​log(1+x2)−log+x≤21​log2
(11)
controls the proximity replacement. If the denominator vanishes at zero, factor its zero and use its leading coefficient in Jensen's formula; the discrepancy is still a basepoint-dependent constant. This proves the rational composition law for the Nevanlinna characteristic here and gives
TU∘e​(R)=πd​R+O(1)∼πd​R.​
(12)
If U is constant, d=0 and its characteristic is bounded instead.
For a simultaneous nonzero deficiency and ramification example choose
f(z)=ez(ez−1)2,
(13)
a degree-three polynomial composed with the exponential. Its characteristic is 3R/π+O(1). Its only zeros are 2πin, n∈Z, all of multiplicity two. The number of distinct such points in a large disc is R/π+O(1), so integration of their count gives
N(R;0)=R/π+O(logR),N(R;0)=2R/π+O(logR).
(14)
The origin contribution is handled by its usual logarithmic counting term. Therefore
δf​(0)=31​,θf​(0)=31​.​
(15)
This is simultaneous deficiency and ramification for an exponential polynomial: the missing exponential value contributes a deficit, while the attained value is attained with double multiplicity.

The growth order of a meromorphic function is
ρ(f)=limsupR→∞​logRlog+Tf​(R)​.
(16)
The order of the integrated count is defined analogously using log+N(R;a), with an identically zero count assigned order zero. Constants have function order zero. These are growth orders, not local zero or pole orders. For nonconstant f, the first main theorem gives ρ(N(⋅;a))≤ρ(f) for every target.
Use the truncated Nevanlinna second main theorem in the following standard form: for distinct fixed targets a1​,…,aq​,
(q−2)Tf​(R)≤∑j=1q​N(R;aj​)+Sf​(R),Sf​=O(log+Tf​+logR),
(17)
outside an exceptional set of radii of finite linear measure. For finite order the error is O(logR); replacing each truncated count by its full count preserves the inequality.
Suppose 0<ρ(f)<∞ and three targets have counting orders below ρ(f). Choose μ strictly between the maximum of their three orders and ρ(f). Each full count is at most Rμ eventually. The theorem with q=3 then gives Tf​(R)≤CRμ for sufficiently large radii outside the exceptional set. Its tail measure is eventually less than one, so every interval [R,R+1] contains a nonexceptional radius R′. Monotonicity of the characteristic yields Tf​(R)≤Tf​(R′)≤C(R+1)μ for all large R. This contradicts its order. If ρ(f)=0, all the counting orders are already zero. Hence the counting-order exceptions for a finite-order meromorphic function satisfy
ρ(N(⋅;a))=ρ(f)apart from at most two targets.​
(18)
The exponential has order one but omits both zero and infinity, so those two counts are zero; this realizes two exceptions.
For a Möbius map U(w)=(aw+b)/(cw+d) with ad−bc=0, translation and multiplication by a nonzero constant change T by O(1), as follows directly from the proximity inequalities and unchanged pole multiplicities. The first main theorem also gives T(1/h)=T(h)+O(1). If c=0, U is affine. If c=0, write U(w)=a/c+K/(cw+d) with K=0 and use these three elementary operations. Thus TU∘f​=Tf​+O(1), proving
ρ(U∘f)=ρ(f).​
(19)
For the sum, a pole of f1​+f2​ has order no larger than the sum of the two pole orders, and
log+∣f1​+f2​∣≤log2+log+∣f1​∣+log+∣f2​∣.
(20)
Therefore Tf1​+f2​​≤Tf1​​+Tf2​​+O(1) and
ρ(f1​+f2​)≤max(ρ(f1​),ρ(f2​)).​
(21)
If ρ(f1​)>ρ(f2​), apply the same inequality to f1​=(f1​+f2​)−f2​. It forces ρ(f1​)≤max(ρ(f1​+f2​),ρ(f2​)), hence the sum's order equals ρ(f1​). The reversed case is identical. This reasoning also handles an infinite larger order. For equal orders cancellation can be strict: f1​=ez, f2​=−ez both have order one, but their sum is zero of order zero.

This derivative growth outside a finite-measure set controls exceptional radii in Nevanlinna estimates. For example let Ts​ be the smooth spherical characteristic and A(r)=rTs′​(r) its increasing area-counting function. Apply the lemma to Ts​+1 and A+1: outside the union of two finite-measure sets,

(24)
Their logarithms are consequently O(logr+log(Ts​+1)). Such derivative/area bounds in estimates from the Poisson-Jensen formula give the Nevanlinna logarithmic derivative lemma, m(r,f′/f)=O(log+Tf​+logr) outside a finite-length set. The second main theorem uses that estimate as its error term. The elementary growth lemma controls that error and its exceptional set; it is not itself a substitute for the analytic estimates.
We use the truncated second main theorem stated in the preceding solution, together with the fact that a transcendental meromorphic function has Tf​(r)/logr→∞. Here is a justification of the latter fact. If there are infinitely many poles, fixing any arbitrarily large finite pole count gives N(r;∞)≥Alogr−OA​(1). If there are only finitely many poles, multiply by a polynomial p removing them to obtain a transcendental entire function h=pf. Its Taylor series has nonzero coefficients of arbitrarily high degrees n. Cauchy's coefficient estimate gives logMh​(r/2)≥nlogr−On​(1); the subharmonic function Poisson estimate gives logMh​(r/2)≤3m(r,h). Since Th​≤Tf​+O(logr), letting n be arbitrarily large proves the claimed ratio limit. Thus the second-theorem error is o(Tf​) along large nonexceptional radii.
Let Nsimple​(r;a) count just the simple a-points. A multiple a-point of degree m≥2 contributes one to the truncated count and at least two to the full count; a simple point contributes one to each. Hence, with the same positive logarithmic weights for large radii,
N(r;a)≤21​N(r;a)+21​Nsimple​(r;a).
(25)
If each of the five values had only finitely many simple preimages, each simple counting function would be O(logr). Summing and using the first main theorem would give ∑j=15​N(r;aj​)≤(5/2)Tf​+O(logr). But the second theorem requires 3Tf​≤∑N+o(Tf​), a contradiction. This proves the stronger result five values force infinitely many simple preimages:
At least one of the five values has infinitely many simple preimages.​
(26)
In particular more than one simple zero is necessary, but more than one target with simple zeros is not necessary.
For the last distinction, take a nonsingular Weierstrass elliptic function scaled so that ℘′2=4℘(℘−1)(℘+1); it has branch values −1,0,1,∞. These are the four totally ramified Weierstrass values: finite branch preimages have local degree two, and all poles are double. Set
f(z)=℘(z)−21​,{a1​,…,a5​}={−31​,−21​,−1,0,1}.
(27)
The first four targets are the images of those branch values under the Möbius transformation. Their preimages remain double; at a lattice pole, for example, f(z)∼(z−z∗​)2. The last target corresponds to ℘=3, where ℘′2=96=0, so every such preimage is simple. The degree-two torus map attains that regular value, and periodicity produces infinitely many preimages in the plane. This nonconstant elliptic function is transcendental because it has infinitely many poles. Exactly one of the five selected finite targets therefore has simple preimages.

There are two necessary qualifications to the printed formula. Critical points satisfy g′=0, not g′=1. At a point with g′=1, the local degree is one and the printed summand is zero. In addition, log∥g′(0)∥ is finite only when g′(0)=0; constant maps and origin-critical maps need separate treatment.
These are genuine issues even for otherwise ordinary disc maps. The map g(z)=(z−1/2)2/4 maps the unit disc into itself and has g′(0)=0. Its derivative never equals one in the disc, so the printed count is zero, but it has a ramification point at 1/2 of multiplicity one. For R>1/2 the correct identity has the additional term log(2R), proving that the displayed identity with the literal printed count is false. Also g(z)=z2 shows the undefined basepoint logarithm in an origin-critical example.
