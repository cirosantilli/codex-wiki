A monad on a category C consists of an endofunctor T, a unit natural transformation η:1C​⇒T, and a multiplication μ:T2⇒T, satisfying
μTμ=μμT,μTη=1T​=μηT.
(1)
An algebra for a monad is (A,a:TA→A) with aηA​=1A​ and aTa=aμA​. A morphism of algebras for a monad h:(A,a)→(B,b) satisfies ha=bTh. These objects and arrows form the Eilenberg-Moore category CT.
For an adjunction F:C⇄D:U with F⊣U, the monad induced by an adjunction is T=UF, with μ=UεF. The Eilenberg-Moore comparison functor is K(D)=(UD,UεD​) and K(h)=Uh. The functor U is monadic when this comparison is an equivalence of categories; this is a monadic adjunction.
The precise monadicity theorem says that U:D→C is monadic exactly when it has a left adjoint, reflects isomorphisms, and satisfies the following condition: whenever the image of a parallel pair in D admits a split coequalizer in C, the pair has a coequalizer in D and U preserves that coequalizer. Equivalently, these coequalizers are created up to the canonical isomorphism identifying their underlying coequalizer objects. Here a split coequalizer of f,g:A⇉B consists of q:B→Q, s:Q→B, and t:B→A with
qf=qg,qs=1Q​,ft=1B​,gt=sq.
(2)
These equations prove its universal property: if hf=hg, then h=hft=hgt=hsq, and hs is the unique factor through q. Every functor preserves these equations, so every functor preserves split coequalizers.
First consider the forgetful functor UT:CT→C. It has the free algebra functor A↦(TA,μA​) as a left adjoint: the inverse transposition formulas are h↦hηA​ and v↦bTv. An monad algebra morphism whose underlying arrow is invertible has an algebra-morphism inverse, by rearranging its structure equation. Thus UT reflects isomorphisms.
Suppose f,g:(A,a)⇉(B,b) have an underlying split coequalizer q:B→Q. Applying T gives a coequalizer of Tf,Tg. The equation qbTf=qfa=qga=qbTg therefore gives a unique c:TQ→Q with cTq=qb. The monad algebra laws follow by cancellation: composing cηQ​=1Q​ with the epimorphism q reduces it to bηB​=1B​, and composing cTc=cμQ​ with T2q reduces it to bTb=bμB​. Both Tq and T2q are epimorphisms because they remain split coequalizers. Thus (Q,c) is an algebra for a monad and q is an monad algebra morphism. If an algebra map h:(B,b)→(R,r) equalizes f,g, its underlying factor v:Q→R obeys vc=rTv, since this equation becomes hb=rTh after composition with Tq. This proves that UT creates the required coequalizers. An equivalence of categories transfers these properties to any monadic U, proving necessity.
Conversely assume the three conditions and write T=UF. Given (A,a)∈CT, take the pair
FTAεFA​​Fa​​FA.
(3)
Its image is μA​,Ta:T2A⇉TA, with split coequalizer a:TA→A: the splittings are s=ηA​ and t=ηTA​, because
aηA​=1A​,μA​ηTA​=1TA​,TaηTA​=ηA​a.
(4)
Let q:FA→Da​ be its coequalizer in D. Preservation gives a canonical isomorphism UDa​≅A under which Uq=a. Transporting the algebra action of K(Da​) along this isomorphism gives α:TA→A. Naturality of the adjunction counit says that K(q) is an monad algebra morphism, hence αTa=aμA​. But aTa=aμA​ too, and Ta is a split epimorphism, with section TηA​. Therefore α=a. Every algebra is consequently isomorphic to some K(Da​).
It remains to establish full faithfulness, rather than merely construct objects. For B∈D, put b=UεB​. The adjunction counit εB​:FUB→B coequalizes εFUB​,Fb. The coequalizer supplied by the hypothesis has underlying coequalizer b:TUB→UB. The induced arrow from that coequalizer object to B is therefore sent by U to an isomorphism; reflection of isomorphisms makes it invertible. Thus εB​ itself is this coequalizer.
Now let h:K(B)→K(C) be an monad algebra morphism, and put c=UεC​. The arrow v=εC​Fh:FUB→C coequalizes the two arrows in the preceding presentation. To check this directly, use transposition along F⊣U: the transposes of vFb and vεFUB​ are respectively hb and cTh, which agree by the algebra-morphism equation. Hence a unique hˉ:B→C satisfies hˉεB​=v. Applying U gives (Uhˉ)b=cTh=hb; cancellation of the split epimorphism b yields Uhˉ=h. Finally, any arrow r:B→C satisfies rεB​=εC​FUr by naturality, so two arrows with equal image under U are equal by the coequalizer property. The comparison is full and faithful and essentially surjective, hence an equivalence of categories. This proves the theorem.
For the application, the free compact Hausdorff space on a set S is the Stone-Čech compactification β(Sdisc​) of the corresponding discrete space. Its standard extension property says that every set map S→UX, for compact Hausdorff X, extends uniquely to a continuous map βS→X. This constructs the required left adjoint. Also a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism, so U reflects isomorphisms.
Take continuous maps f,g:X⇉Y whose underlying functions have a split coequalizer q:Y→Q with splittings s,t. The splittings need not be continuous. Let
E={(f(x),g(x)):x∈X}⊆Y×Y,R={(y,z):q(y)=q(z)}.
(5)
The set E is compact and hence closed. Since qf=qg, it is contained in R. Conversely (y,sq(y))=(ft(y),gt(y))∈E. Thus
R={(y,z):∃w∈Y, (y,w)∈E, (z,w)∈E}.
(6)
The set of triples on the right is closed in the compact Hausdorff space Y3, so its projection onto the first two coordinates is compact and closed. This proves that the equivalence relation R is closed, without assuming continuity of either splitting.
Give Q≅Y/R the quotient topology. The allowed closed-relation criterion makes it a compact Hausdorff space. If a continuous map h:Y→Z equalizes f,g, the split-coequalizer property makes it constant on the fibres of q; its unique factor Q→Z is continuous by the quotient topology. Hence this is a coequalizer in compact Hausdorff spaces, preserved by U. Its topology is forced uniquely, since any continuous surjection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a quotient map. All three conditions hold, and therefore the forgetful functor from compact Hausdorff spaces to sets is monadic.

An essay arguing against the claim. A good definition can change which mathematical questions are visible. Nevertheless, I would argue that category theory derives its explanatory force from the interaction between definitions and theorems; there is no sound general ordering in which definitions contribute more than theorems.
The strongest case for the contrary view comes from universal properties. A product in a category is characterized by maps into it, rather than by a particular construction of ordered pairs. The same definition applies to sets, groups, and topological spaces. An adjunction goes further: its natural correspondence of arrows puts free constructions, quotient constructions, and several kinds of completion into a common language. Such definitions remove irrelevant choices and expose relationships that would be difficult to recognize from separate constructions.
But a universal-property definition does not supply an object. The definition of a product in a category says what a product would accomplish; an existence theorem says whether the category actually has one. Even uniqueness up to unique isomorphism is a short proof, not an extra clause to be silently supplied by the word “universal”. Given two products, their universal properties produce maps in both directions, and uniqueness forces the two composites to be identities. This elementary theorem makes the definition usable independently of a chosen construction.
The Yoneda lemma illustrates the distinction especially clearly. A representable functor is a definition, but the lemma proves that its natural transformations into any set-valued functor are determined by one element. Its proof evaluates at the identity and reconstructs the whole transformation by functoriality. Consequently the Yoneda embedding is full and faithful: an object's pattern of incoming arrows recovers its morphisms without loss. That is a substantive explanation of why the chosen definition is powerful. Merely calling a functor representable would not establish it.
Similarly, defining a monadic adjunction gives a precise meaning to recovering structured objects as algebras for a monad. It does not decide whether a given forgetful functor is monadic. The precise monadicity theorem translates that difficult classification into a usable test involving adjoint functors, reflected isomorphisms, and specific coequalizers. For compact Hausdorff spaces, the crucial step is proving that a set-split relation is closed. The definition of a monad alone does not contain this topological argument. The theorem connects the algebraic language to an independently meaningful class of spaces.
There is also a danger in praising definitions without hypotheses. A Cartesian closed category has exponential objects, but a category of structured objects need not be cartesian closed merely because underlying sets have function sets. The correct exponential in a functor category generally involves natural transformations out of representable functors, not pointwise function sets. Its existence and universal property require an argument. Theorems also reveal when tempting definitions cannot support the intended construction.
Definitions provide the vocabulary and select the structures worth studying; proofs establish existence, consequences, and limitations. Category theory makes this partnership unusually explicit because its definitions are often expressed through universal properties. Its distinctive achievement is to turn well-chosen definitions into reusable theorems, rather than to make theorems secondary.
An alternative essay arguing for the claim. In category theory, theorems are indispensable, but a carefully chosen definition often performs the greater act of organization. It identifies what many apparently unrelated constructions have in common and makes whole families of proofs possible. In that explanatory sense, definitions can be the subject's most influential contributions.
Consider a universal property. Describing a product in a category as an object representing pairs of incoming arrows replaces several different concrete constructions by one invariant pattern. The corresponding uniqueness proof is important but short; the lasting insight is that the same pattern is the right question in every category. Once the definition is available, it becomes meaningful to ask which functors preserve products, whether the products exist in a new category, and how they interact with other universal constructions.
An adjunction is an even more striking example. The existence of free groups, free modules, and a Stone-Čech compactification can be proved separately. The definition of an adjunction recognizes their common relation to forgetful functors and explains why arrows out of a free object are determined by arrows out of its generators. It also packages the relation as a monad. A large collection of isolated existence results becomes an instance of a shared structure, with reusable consequences about composition and categorical limits.
A valuable definition also has to distinguish structures rather than merely rename them. An abelian category is not just an additive category with categorical kernels and categorical cokernels: normality of monomorphisms and epimorphisms matters. The additive example with truncated primary torsion shows why those clauses belong in the definition. The definition therefore preserves exactly the structure on which theorems about images and exact sequences depend.
This view does not make proofs dispensable. The Yoneda lemma justifies the use of representable functors, and the precise monadicity theorem tests when a proposed algebraic description is valid. Rather, it argues that the best categorical definitions have unusually broad explanatory reach: they select the invariant structures to which those proofs apply. If “matter more” means providing the reusable framework in which many theorems become instances of one idea, category-theoretic definitions have a strong claim to that role.

Nat(C(A,−),F)≅F(A),α⟼αA​(1A​).
(7)

Conversely, naturality of α at f:A→B gives αB​(f)=F(f)αA​(1A​). The two constructions are inverse. They are natural in F, because a natural transformation F⇒G commutes with evaluation. They are also natural in A: an arrow v:A′→A induces C(A,−)→C(A′,−) by precomposition, and the resulting map Nat(C(A′,−),F)→Nat(C(A,−),F) corresponds to F(v):F(A′)→F(A). Applying the result to the opposite category gives Nat(C(−,A),H)≅H(A) for H:Cop→Set.

(GF)(C)=Nat(C(C,−)×F,G).

(12)
so it too is recovered. The formulas are natural in H and G, giving the required adjunction (−)×F⊣(−)F. Therefore the covariant functor category is cartesian closed. This proof supplies the actual exponential and does not need a colimit presentation of its arguments.
Finally suppose C itself is a small category which is a Cartesian closed category. In its presheaf category, the same construction, applied to Cop, gives
((YB)YA)(C)​=Nat(YC×YA,YB)≅Nat(Y(C×A),YB)≅C(C×A,B)≅C(C,BA)=Y(BA)(C).​

The first isomorphism uses the product's universal property: a map into C×A is precisely a pair of maps into C and A. The next is the Yoneda lemma, and the last is the exponential adjunction in C. Each step is natural in C, A, and B. Under these identifications, evaluation corresponds to Y of the evaluation BA×A→B. For an adjunction L:C⇄D:R, write its adjunction unit as η and its adjunction counit as ε. It is an idempotent adjunction when the induced monad has invertible multiplication μ=RεL:RLRL⇒RL. The dual condition is invertibility of the induced comonad comultiplication δ=LηR:LR⇒LRLR.
To prove equivalence of these conditions, suppose μ is invertible. The two unit identities imply Tη=ηT=μ−1, where T=RL. For any algebra for a monad a:TA→A, naturality gives
ηA​a=TaηTA​=TaTηA​=T(aηA​)=1TA​,

Apply this implication to the opposite adjunction, interchanging the roles of the monad and comonad, to obtain the converse. Therefore idempotence is self-dual.
The open-set frame O(X) is ordered by inclusion. Its arbitrary joins are unions; arbitrary meets are interiors of intersections. A filtered category is nonempty, any two objects have arrows to a common object, and any parallel pair u,v:A⇉B is equalized by some arrow B→C. Equivalently, every finite diagram has a cocone: first choose a common target for its finitely many objects, then successively equalize the finitely many discrepancies along its arrows. A weakly filtered category requires a cocone only for finite nonempty connected diagrams. Equivalently, each connected component of a category is filtered. To see this, two objects in one component are joined by a finite zigzag; a cocone on that zigzag gives a common target, and a parallel-pair diagram gives the equalization condition. Conversely a connected diagram lies in one filtered component. An empty category is weakly filtered, since it has no such diagrams.
The unique outgoing-arrow lifting property is precisely that of a discrete opfibration. Let F:C→D have this property, with D weakly filtered. Take a diagram H:J→C with J finite, nonempty and connected, and choose a cocone αj​:FH(j)→D in D. Lift each αj​ uniquely from H(j), obtaining αj​:H(j)→Cj​. For an arrow u:j→k, the arrow αk​H(u) and the lift αj​ have the same source and the same image αk​FH(u)=αj​. Unique lifting gives equality of the arrows and, importantly, Cj​=Ck​. Connectedness of J makes all targets a single object C0​. The lifts are a cocone in C, so the domain of a discrete opfibration over a weakly filtered category is weakly filtered.
Consider discrete opfibrations F:A→D and G:B→D and their pullback in a category E=A×D​B. The assumed creation of pullbacks describes its objects as pairs (a,b) with Fa=Gb, and its arrows as pairs (u,v) with Fu=Gv. There is a canonical map
Φ:π0​(E)→π0​(A)×π0​(D)​π0​(B),[(a,b)]↦([a],[b]),
(20)
where π0​ denotes connected components of a category. Assume the vertices are weakly filtered categories.
For surjectivity, choose components [a] and [b] whose images belong to the same component of D. That component is filtered, so there are arrows Fa→d and Gb→d. Lifting them from a and b gives a→a1​ and b→b1​ with Fa1​=Gb1​=d. Thus (a1​,b1​)∈E maps to the chosen component pair.
For injectivity, suppose (a,b) and (a′,b′) have the same images under Φ. Filteredness of the relevant components upstairs gives arrows
au​a0​u′​a′,bv​b0​v′​b′.
(21)
Write d=Fa=Gb and d′=Fa′=Gb′. In D the four arrows Fu,Gv,Fu′,Gv′ make a finite connected diagram with objects d,d′,Fa0​,Gb0​. It has a cocone, so there are r:Fa0​→z and s:Gb0​→z with
rFu=sGv,rFu′=sGv′.
(22)
Lift r from a0​ and s from b0​, obtaining arrows to a1​,b1​ with Fa1​=Gb1​=z. Their composites with (u,v) form an arrow (a,b)→(a1​,b1​) of E, and their composites with (u′,v′) form an arrow (a′,b′)→(a1​,b1​). Hence the two representatives lie in the same component. This proves the connected components of discrete-opfibration pullbacks formula
π0​(A×D​B)≅π0​(A)×π0​(D)​π0​(B)​.
(23)

Now fix a small filtered category C. Every object D→C of Disc/C has weakly filtered domain by the lifting argument, and every arrow of this slice is a discrete opfibration. Pullbacks in the slice are the created pullbacks above; their resulting domains are weakly filtered as well, since a pullback projection is a discrete opfibration onto a weakly filtered domain. The terminal slice object is 1C​:C→C. Because C is nonempty and filtered, it is connected, so π0​(C) is a singleton. Thus the component functor preserves the terminal object and pullbacks. Therefore the functor from this slice to sets preserves all finite limits.

A preadditive category has an abelian group of morphisms between each pair of objects, and composition is additive in both variables. An additive category is a preadditive category with a zero object and finite products in a category; equivalently one may require finite coproducts. The equivalence, and the stronger assertion that these constructions agree canonically, follow from the argument below. An abelian category is an additive category with kernels in a category and cokernels in a category, in which every monomorphism is a categorical kernel and every epimorphism is a categorical cokernel. A monomorphism with the first property is a normal monomorphism; an epimorphism with the second is a conormal epimorphism.
Let P=∏i=1n​Ai​, with projections pi​. There is a unique ij​:Aj​→P with pi​ij​=δij​1Aj​​, interpreting the other components as zero. Since every pj​ sends ∑i​ii​pi​ to pj​, the product's universal property gives
pi​ij​=δij​1Aj​​,∑i​ii​pi​=1P​.
(24)
Given arrows fi​:Ai​→B, put f=∑i​fi​pi​. Then fij​=fj​. Conversely any arrow with these restrictions equals f1P​=∑i​fii​pi​, so it is uniquely this sum. Thus (P,ii​) is a coproduct in a category. The empty product is the zero object and also the empty coproduct. The dual argument starts from coproducts and constructs projections; it proves the equivalent definition. Hence finite products and coproducts are the same biproducts, with the displayed identities giving the canonical identification.
For the image factorization in an abelian category, let h:A→B, let c:B→Q be its cokernel, and let i:I→B be the kernel of c. Since ch=0, write h=ie. We prove that e:A→I is epic. Suppose t:I→Z satisfies te=0 and let l:L→I be its categorical kernel. Then e=lr for some r:A→L. The composite il is monic and hence is a normal monomorphism, say il=kerz for z:B→W. Since zh=zilr=0, the categorical cokernel property makes z factor through c, and therefore zi=0. The categorical kernel property of il gives s:I→L with ils=i. Cancellation of i yields ls=1I​; since l is monic this also gives sl=1L​. Thus l is invertible and t=0. In a preadditive category, ue=ve now implies (u−v)e=0, hence u=v. This proves e is an epimorphism and supplies the required factorization.
For uniqueness, an epimorphism that is a categorical cokernel is the categorical cokernel of its own categorical kernel. Indeed, if e=cokerr and k=kere, then r factors through k; an arrow killing k therefore kills r and factors uniquely through e. Dually, a normal monomorphism is the categorical kernel of its own categorical cokernel. If h=mp is any epi–mono factorization, monicity of m gives kerp=kerh. Both p and e are consequently categorical cokernels of this same categorical kernel. Their universal properties give a unique isomorphism θ between the intermediate objects with θp=e, and cancellation of p gives iθ=m. Therefore the epi–mono factorization is unique up to the unique compatible isomorphism.
Here is an additive category with truncated primary torsion, with the prime fixed as 2. Its objects are finitely generated abelian groups having no element of order 4, and its morphisms are all group homomorphisms. Equivalently their 2-primary torsion subgroup has exponent at most 2. It is a full subcategory of abelian groups, closed under finite direct sums and subgroups. Its hom groups and bilinear composition are inherited, and the zero group and finite direct sums belong to it, so it is additive. Categorical kernels are ordinary subgroup categorical kernels, which are still finitely generated and have no element of order 4.
To construct categorical cokernels, for an arbitrary finitely generated abelian group G let t2​(G) be its 2-primary torsion subgroup, and define
R(G)=G/(2t2​(G)).
(25)
The Fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups shows that this quotient lies in the subcategory: it leaves the free and odd-primary parts unchanged and replaces each cyclic 2-primary summand by either 0 or Z/2Z. Every homomorphism G→H to an allowed group kills 2t2​(G), because it sends 2-primary torsion into a subgroup killed by 2. Thus G→R(G) has the universal property of a reflection. For f:A→B, its categorical cokernel in the subcategory is
B⟶R(B/f(A)).
(26)
This has exactly the categorical cokernel universal property by the preceding factorization. Finite categorical limits exist because equalizers are categorical kernels of differences and finite products exist. Finite colimits exist dually because coequalizers are categorical cokernels of differences and finite coproducts exist.
The epimorphisms in this example are precisely the surjective homomorphisms. A surjection is epic already in abelian groups. Conversely, if f is epic in the subcategory, its categorical cokernel is zero, so R(B/f(A))=0. A nonzero finitely generated abelian group has nonzero reflection: a nonzero free or odd-primary summand survives, and a nonzero finite 2-primary summand has a nonzero quotient modulo twice itself. Hence B/f(A)=0 and f is surjective. Its ordinary categorical kernel belongs to the subcategory, and its quotient by that categorical kernel is its codomain, which also belongs to the subcategory. It is therefore the categorical cokernel of its categorical kernel. This proves that every epimorphism is conormal.
The monomorphism
m:Z→Z,m(n)=4n
(27)
is not normal. Its categorical cokernel in this category is reduction modulo 2, since R(Z/4Z)=Z/2Z. The categorical kernel of that categorical cokernel is the inclusion 2Z↪Z, whereas the image of m is 4Z. A normal monomorphism is the categorical kernel of its own categorical cokernel, as proved above, so m cannot be normal. In fact multiplication by 2 is itself the categorical kernel of reduction modulo 2, up to the identification Z≅2Z. Its composite with itself is multiplication by 4. Thus this example already exhibits two normal monomorphisms whose composite is not normal.
For the general assertion, let m:A→B be a nonnormal monomorphism in any additive category of the specified kind. Put c=cokerm and k:K→B be its categorical kernel, and factor m=kg. Both m and k are monic, so g is monic. Set d=cokerg and l:L→K be its categorical kernel. Then k,l are normal, and g=lr for some r.
Suppose their composite were normal, say kl=kert. Since tm=tklr=0, t factors through c, whence tk=0. The categorical kernel property of kl supplies s:K→L with kls=k; cancellation shows ls=1K​, and monicity makes l an isomorphism. Because dl=0, this forces d=0. In a preadditive category, vanishing of the categorical cokernel of g implies that g is epic: any u,v with ug=vg have u−v factor through the zero categorical cokernel. By hypothesis g is therefore a categorical cokernel, and it is also monic. If g=cokera, monicity and ga=0 force a=0; a categorical cokernel of a zero arrow is an isomorphism. Thus g is invertible, making m=kg normal, a contradiction. Consequently normal monomorphisms cannot be closed under composition in any such category; the explicit pair k,l witnesses the failure.
