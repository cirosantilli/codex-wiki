In a unital Banach algebra, the Neumann series gives (1−u)−1=∑k=0∞​uk whenever ∥u∥<1: the series converges in norm by submultiplicativity and completeness, and multiplication of its partial sums on either side gives 1−uN+1→1. Consequently, if g is invertible and ∥g−1∥∥h−g∥<1, write
h=g(1+v),v=g−1(h−g).
(1)
The second factor has a Neumann series inverse, so h is invertible. This exhibits a ball around each g in the group of invertible elements of a Banach algebra, proving that group is an open set.
The same series proves continuity of inversion in a Banach algebra, with the useful quantitative estimate
∥h−1−g−1∥=∥[(1+v)−1−1]g−1∥≤1−∥g−1∥∥h−g∥∥g−1∥2∥h−g∥​.
(2)
The right side tends to zero as h→g. Since applying inversion twice gives the original element, it is a bijection whose inverse is the same continuous map. Thus inversion is a homeomorphism of the open group of invertibles onto itself.
For the final unheaded request, define
E={λ∈C:1−λa is invertible}.
(3)
It is open by the preceding argument and contains 0. If λ0​ were a boundary point, a sequence λn​∈E would converge to λ0​, while openness gives λ0​∈/E. Then 1−λn​a→1−λ0​a is a noninvertible limit of invertibles. The one-sided-inverse argument in part (ii) shows that this limit has neither a left inverse of an algebra element nor a right inverse of an algebra element, contradicting the hypothesis. Therefore E has empty boundary; it is both open and closed in the connected complex plane, and E=C.
For every nonzero μ, put λ=μ−1. The identity μ1−a=μ(1−λa) proves invertibility, so the spectrum of an element a is contained in {0}. It is nonempty: if the resolvent of an element existed for all μ∈C, it would be an entire algebra-valued function tending to zero at infinity by the Neumann series. Applying any bounded linear functional and then the Liouville theorem would make it zero; the Hahn-Banach theorem separates points and would force the resolvent itself to be zero, contradicting its inverse equation. Hence
σA​(a)={0}.​
(4)
