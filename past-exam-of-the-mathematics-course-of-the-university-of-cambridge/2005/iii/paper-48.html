Refining space alone at fixed positive time step does not generally converge. The diffusion Courant number is σ2k/h2, which grows by four at each halving of h. For positive volatility it eventually violates the explicit diffusion stability restriction. The explicit log-price scheme for local-volatility pricing then amplifies grid-scale errors, rather than approaching the pricing solution.
The strike kink makes the failure concrete. At the terminal node xi​=logK, uim​=0, while Dxx​uim​=K/h+O(1). Even a single step of fixed size k gives uim−1​=kaim​K/h+O(1), unbounded as h↓0 if aim​>0. Thus the problem is not cured by monitoring exactly at the money. A stable joint space-time refinement is required; the zero-diffusion case does not support the same diffusion argument and centered explicit advection has its own stability problem.

Refining time alone converges to the fixed-grid, spatially discretized solution, assuming the time-dependent coefficients and boundary forcing are suitably regular. In time-to-maturity τ=T−t, the grid values solve a finite system dU/dτ=Ah​(τ)U+Fh​(τ). For fixed h, the explicit Euler method converges to this system as k↓0; time-step error decreases to zero.
The spatial finite difference error remains. Thus the limit is not in general the exact continuous pricing solution: sufficiently small time steps reveal an error plateau of order h2 under the usual smooth-solution assumptions. A truncated-domain boundary error also remains fixed unless negligible as stipulated.

Suppose the starting steps satisfy the nonnegative-weight restrictions given below, the volatility is bounded away from zero, and the coefficients and pricing solution have the regularity needed for the usual error expansion at the monitored positive remaining maturity. The combined refinement h↦h/2, k↦k/4 preserves k/h2, improves the spatial drift restriction, and yields
uh,k​=u+Cx​h2+Ct​k+o(h2+k).
(19)
If k=ρh2, put C=Cx​+ρCt​. Then uh​=u+Ch2+o(h2), so the difference between successive refinement levels is 3Ch2/4+o(h2). Consequently successive differences shrink by a factor of approximately four, giving second-order convergence in space and first-order convergence in time. If the leading coefficient happens to vanish, a faster rate or an irregular ratio is possible.
The volatility bounds alone do not guarantee that expansion: coefficient smoothness, stability and treatment of the nonsmooth terminal payoff matter. The statement applies at fixed positive remaining maturity after appropriate smoothing, not to Greeks at the terminal strike. If the starting diffusion Courant number is already outside the stable range, the proposed coupled refinement preserves that defect and does not establish convergence.

A credit rating is an agency's ordered assessment of an issuer's or particular obligation's capacity and willingness to meet its promised payments. Corporate and sovereign issuers are classified on letter scales, with finer notches, broad investment-grade and speculative-grade groupings, and default categories. Agencies such as Standard & Poor's, Moody's and Fitch also revise ratings as information changes; sovereign assessments involve public finances and institutional or political factors as well as debt-servicing capacity.
A credit rating summarizes relative creditworthiness rather than specifying an exact, immutable credit default probability. Historical defaults and migrations within rating classes can inform a statistical model. Assuming a homogeneous Poisson process within one class is a simplifying choice: it posits a constant event intensity and independent increments, neither of which follows merely from a shared rating. The credit risk model must also distinguish default frequency from loss given default severity.

For n observed complete independent exponential gaps, let Tn​=∑i=1n​τi​. The likelihood function is L(λ)=λne−λTn​, and the log-likelihood has derivative n/λ−Tn​ and second derivative −n/λ2<0. Hence the unique maximum-likelihood estimator is

(33)
Alternatively, observe m unit-year increments Yj​=Nj​−Nj−1​. These are independent Poisson counts of mean λ. Their joint likelihood is proportional to λ∑Yj​e−mλ, giving
λ=m1​j=1∑m​Yj​=Nm​/m.​
(34)
It is unbiased, with variance λ/m. A zero observed count gives the boundary estimate zero, or the supremum as λ↓0 if the parameter space is restricted to positive rates.
The Poisson observation scheme and rate likelihood distinction matters when comparing the techniques. For a fixed observation horizon, conditional on the total number of events the event locations have a distribution independent of λ. Thus exact timestamps add no rate information to the total count under the homogeneous Poisson model; unit-interval counts already contain that total. The correct fixed-horizon likelihood includes the final unfinished waiting interval, and gives count divided by the full exposure, not by the sum of only completed gaps.
The first calculation instead stops after a fixed number n of events, so its exposure Tn​ is random. Its estimate has mean nλ/(n−1) for n>1 and an infinite mean for n=1, unlike the unbiased fixed-horizon count estimate. For n>2 its variance is n2λ2/[(n−1)2(n−2)], asymptotic to λ2/n. There is no universally more informative sampling technique without specifying the stopping rule and exposure. Counts are sufficient and convenient for estimating a truly constant rate; timestamps additionally help diagnose departures from constant intensity or independent gaps.

For nonnegative losses it has a zero atom at least e−λ, so if that mass exceeds p its exact value at risk is zero. With a small rate or a strongly skewed severity distribution, a normal approximation can therefore be poor. A loan book with dependent defaults, rating migration or varying exposures also needs more than the constant-intensity independent-severity model. These are model qualifications, not deductions from the credit rating alone.
