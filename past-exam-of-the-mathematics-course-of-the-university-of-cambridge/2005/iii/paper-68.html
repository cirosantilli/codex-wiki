Put x=cosθ. The trigonometric identity
cos((n+1)θ)+cos((n−1)θ)=2cosθcos(nθ)
(9)
gives
Tn+1​(x)=2xTn​(x)−Tn−1​(x).​
(10)
Starting with T0​=1 and T1​=x, this recurrence proves inductively that each Chebyshev polynomial is a polynomial. For n≥1, the highest-degree term in 2xTn​ cannot cancel against Tn−1​, so the degree increases by one and its leading coefficient doubles. Thus
degTn​=n,lc(Tn​)=2n−1(n≥1).​
(11)
The constant polynomial T0​ has leading coefficient one.
For n≥1, the points xj​=cos(jπ/n), j=0,…,n, give Tn​(xj​)=(−1)j. They are all the points where ∣Tn​∣=1, since ∣cos(nθ)∣=1 exactly when nθ is an integer multiple of π. Therefore there are n+1 equioscillation points, including the two endpoints. Reversing their order makes an increasing alternating sequence. For n=0 the polynomial is constant: every point has unit value, but an alternating sequence has at most one point.
Now take n≥1 and c=21−n. Since cTn​ is monic, p0​(x)=xn−cTn​(x) has degree at most n−1, and ∥xn−p0​∥∞​=c. This proves the upper bound for the best error.
For the lower bound, suppose a polynomial p of degree at most n−1 had ∥xn−p∥∞​<c. At each of the n+1 ordered extrema, h=p−p0​ must have the same strictly nonzero sign as Tn​: if cTn​=c, then ∣c−h∣<c gives h>0, and if cTn​=−c, then ∣−c−h∣<c gives h<0. Between every consecutive pair, the intermediate value theorem gives a zero of h. These are n distinct zeros, impossible for a nonzero polynomial of degree at most n−1; h=0 is incompatible with the strict error improvement. This first-principles sign argument proves the monic Chebyshev extremal polynomial bound without using the alternation theorem:
En−1​(xn)=21−n.​
(12)
The displayed p0​ supplies an actual best approximating polynomial.

As printed in the original PDF, this claim is false for n≥2. The displayed frequency is one, so the function already belongs to the space of trigonometric polynomials of degree at most n−1. Its best approximation is then the function itself, with zero error; for example, α=1,β=0 gives a zero-approximant error ∫02π​∣cosx∣dx=4 instead. For n=1, zero is indeed the best constant approximation. Thus the literal answer is
p∗​=f(n≥2),p∗​=0(n=1).​
(17)
The natural intended version has frequency n. Set g(y)=αcosy+βsiny and fn​(x)=g(nx). The sign function h(y)=signg(y) is integrable and satisfies h(y+π)=−h(y), so its mean is zero. Part (b) makes h(nx)=signfn​(x) orthogonal to every degree-at-most-n−1 trigonometric polynomial. Part (a), with p∗​=0, now proves
The best approximation to αcos(nx)+βsin(nx) from Tn−1​ in L1 is 0.​
(18)
The error is 4α2+β2​ for the unnormalized integral over a period.
The best approximant is unique. If the amplitude is nonzero and another continuous trigonometric polynomial p has the same optimal error, equality in part (a)'s pointwise inequality holds almost everywhere. Near each zero of fn​, the function changes sign; if p did not vanish at that zero, continuity would force an interval on one side where fn​−p has the wrong sign, making the integral inequality strict. Thus p vanishes at all 2n distinct zeros of fn​. A nonzero trigonometric polynomial of degree at most n−1 has at most 2n−2 zeros in one period, since multiplication by ei(n−1)x turns it into a polynomial in eix of degree at most 2n−2. Hence p=0. Zero amplitude is immediate. The same reasoning proves the literal frequency-one assertion when n=1, while retaining the counterexample for larger n.

There are n basis splines for the stated n+k knots; the upper limit k in the printed basis list is an indexing slip. Let
Aij​=Nj​(xi∗​),i,j=1,…,n.
(28)
The strictly increasing sites and ti​<xi∗​<ti+k​ give invertibility by the Schoenberg–Whitney theorem. The needed additional matrix property is total nonnegativity of B-spline collocation matrices: every ordered minor of A is nonnegative.
Here is a structural justification of that sign property. Knot insertion refines spline coefficients by rules of the form bj​=αj​aj​+(1−αj​)aj−1​, 0≤αj​≤1, with the usual unchanged coefficients before the insertion and shifted coefficients after it. Each coefficient map is a nonnegative rectangular bidiagonal matrix; all its ordered minors are nonnegative, as its nonzero terms preserve row/column order. Products preserve this property by the Cauchy–Binet formula. Insert each ordered sampling site until it has full knot multiplicity. Evaluation at such a break is one of the refined coefficients, with a consistent one-sided convention. Consequently the B-spline collocation matrix is an ordered row submatrix of the product of refinement matrices, proving its nonnegative minors. This also applies to repeated admissible original knots.
Since A is invertible and totally nonnegative, detA>0. The adjugate identity gives the checkerboard inverse of a totally nonnegative matrix:
(A−1)ij​=(−1)i+jdetAdetAj​,i​​,(−1)i+j(A−1)ij​≥0.
(29)
Now for any spline s=∑j​aj​Nj​, its sampled-value vector is y=Aa. Hence the coefficient linear functional in the dual basis is exactly
μi​(s)=ai​=j∑​(A−1)ij​s(xj∗​).​
(30)
In particular μi​(Nℓ​)=δiℓ​, verifying the hinted formula rather than assuming it. The operator norm of this linear functional is taken for the uniform norm on the spline's interval, containing all sampling sites. Evaluation at a sampling site has norm at most one, so
∣μi​(s)∣≤(∑j​∣(A−1)ij​∣)∥s∥∞​,∥μi​∥≤∑j​∣(A−1)ij​∣.
(31)
For the given unit-norm alternating spline, s∗​(xj∗​)=(−1)j, and the checkerboard signs make all terms in a fixed inverse row align:
ai∗​=μi​(s∗​)=∑j​(A−1)ij​(−1)j=(−1)i∑j​∣(A−1)ij​∣.
(32)
Since ∥s∗​∥∞​=1, this supplies the reverse operator norm inequality and proves the Chebyshev spline coefficient and dual norm equality
∣ai∗​∣=∥μi​∥=j∑​∣(A−1)ij​∣,i=1,…,n.​
(33)
This spline attains the operator norm of each coefficient linear functional.
