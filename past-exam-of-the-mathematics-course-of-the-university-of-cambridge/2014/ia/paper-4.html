In classical charged particle in a uniform magnetic field motion, the Lorentz force and Newton's second law give
mv˙=qv×B.
(8)
Assume B=0 and choose an orthonormal coordinate system with B=Bez​, B=∣B∣. Write Ω=qB/m, retaining the sign of the electric charge. The velocity equations are
v˙x​=Ωvy​,v˙y​=−Ωvx​,v˙z​=0.
(9)
Thus the parallel velocity is constant, and the perpendicular velocity rotates at constant angular frequency. Choosing the time origin and perpendicular axes conveniently gives
vx​=v⊥​cosΩt,vy​=−v⊥​sinΩt,vz​=v∥​.
(10)
Integrating the velocity, one obtains
x=xc​+Ωv⊥​​sinΩt,y=yc​+Ωv⊥​​cosΩt,z=z0​+v∥​t.
(11)
The first two coordinates describe a circle of radius mv⊥​/(∣q∣B), while the third advances uniformly. This is helical motion in a uniform magnetic field: the helix has axis parallel to B through the guiding centre. Its cyclotron frequency is
ωc​=m∣q∣B​​,
(12)
and its signed angular velocity vector about that axis is −qB/m. The minus sign follows from the ordering v×B in the Lorentz force. The helix degenerates to a circle when v∥​=0 and to a straight line when v⊥​=0. If q=0 or B=0, there is only straight-line motion. In a relativistic extension with fixed Lorentz factor γ, the frequency becomes ∣q∣B/(γm); the classical result above uses mv as momentum.

A four-vector is a collection of four components Aμ=(A0,A) that transforms by the same Lorentz transformation as (ct,x). Use the metric signature (−,+,+,+). The Lorentzian inner product is
A⋅B=−A0B0+A1B1+A2B2+A3B3.
(13)
A nonzero four-vector is a timelike vector, null vector or spacelike vector according as A⋅A is negative, zero or positive. Reversing the metric signature reverses the signs used to name these three classes, without changing their geometric meaning.
For a frame moving at speed v along the positive x axis, write β=v/c and use the Lorentz factor γ=(1−β2)−1/2. The component Lorentz transformation is
A′0=γ(A0−βA1),A′1=γ(A1−βA0),A′2=A2,A′3=A3.​
(14)
Expanding the first two squares gives
−(A′0)2+(A′1)2=γ2(1−β2)(−(A0)2+(A1)2)=−(A0)2+(A1)2.
(15)
The other two components are unchanged, so A′⋅A′=A⋅A. Consequently a timelike vector remains timelike under a Lorentz transformation.
For a nonzero null vector, ∣A∣=∣A0∣ and A0=0. Set a=A0 and n^=A/a. Then ∣n^∣=1 and A=a(1,n^). If the zero four-vector is included among null vectors, take a=0 and any unit vector.
For two future-pointing null vectors, write A=a(1,n^) and B=b(1,m^) with a,b>0. The sum of future-pointing null vectors satisfies
(A+B)⋅(A+B)=−2ab(1−n^⋅m^)≤0,
(16)
because the ordinary inner product of two unit vectors is at most one. The sum is null if their spatial directions coincide, and timelike otherwise. Its positive time component makes the sum nonzero and future-pointing.

To prove divergence of the harmonic series, group the terms with 2j<n≤2j+1: their sum is at least 2j/2j+1=1/2. Thus its partial sums are unbounded. The Fermat-Euler theorem states that, for an integer N≥2 and any integer a coprime to N,
aφ(N)≡1(modN),​
(21)
where the Euler totient function φ(N) counts the residue classes coprime to N.
Let r1​,…,rφ(N)​ be representatives of these residue classes. Multiplication by a gives a permutation of them: each ari​ remains coprime to N, and ari​≡arj​ implies ri​≡rj​ by cancellation using the modular inverse of a. Multiplying all the resulting modular congruences yields
aφ(N)r1​⋯rφ(N)​≡r1​⋯rφ(N)​(modN).
(22)
The product is itself coprime to N, so it has a modular inverse and can be canceled. This proves the Fermat-Euler theorem. For N=1, the congruence is trivial; no separate argument is needed.

Suppose x is a quadratic nonresidue. On the nonzero residue classes modulo p, define the involution
T(a)=xa−1.
(25)
It is an involution because T(T(a))=a. A fixed point would satisfy a2=x, which is excluded by the quadratic nonresidue hypothesis. Thus it partitions the p−1 classes into (p−1)/2 disjoint pairs {a,xa−1}, each with product x. Consequently
∏a=1p−1​a≡x(p−1)/2(modp).
(26)
Now evaluate the same product by pairing every class with its own modular inverse. The only classes equal to their modular inverses solve (a−1)(a+1)=0 and hence are 1 and −1. All other pairs have product one. The complete product is therefore −1, so
x(p−1)/2≡−1(modp).​
(27)
Together with the preceding part, this is Euler's criterion; the pairing argument establishes the converse without assuming it.

A set is a countable set if it admits an injective function into the natural numbers; this includes finite sets and countably infinite sets. A countably infinite set can be listed by a bijection from N.
Let Z be the set of infinite binary sequences. Suppose it were countable and list all its members as s(1),s(2),…, writing sn(j)​ for the nth entry of row j. A finite listing could be repeated to make such a list as well. Define the binary sequence y by yn​=1−sn(n)​. It differs from the nth row in coordinate n, for every n. Thus it belongs to Z but is missing from the list, a contradiction. The set Z is uncountable, by Cantor's diagonal argument.

Use N={1,2,…}. If a bijection f:N→N maps S onto the even natural numbers, its restriction is a bijection from S onto an infinite set. Moreover, its restriction to the complement of S maps onto the odd natural numbers. Both sets are therefore infinite.
Conversely, suppose that S and its complement are infinite. Applying the increasing enumeration of an infinite subset of natural numbers to each gives
S={s1​<s2​<⋯},N∖S={t1​<t2​<⋯}.
(29)
For completeness, successively choose the least unused member. The process never stops because the set is infinite, and it lists every member: an omitted member would have infinitely many selected natural numbers below it, which is impossible. Define
f(sj​)=2j,f(tj​)=2j−1.​
(30)
This is injective on each list, their images are disjoint, and together their images cover N. Thus it is a bijection and maps S onto 2N. Both infinitude conditions are necessary and sufficient. If a convention includes zero in N, index each list from zero and use 2j, 2j+1 instead.

Use uncountability by interleaving prescribed binary coordinates. For each arbitrary binary sequence b=(bk​)k≥1​, define another binary sequence by
x3k−2​=0,x3k−1​=a3k−1​,x3k​=bk​(k≥1).
(31)
There are infinitely many zero coordinates, namely 3k−2, so x∈X. There are also infinitely many coordinates agreeing with the prescribed binary expansion, namely 3k−1, so x∈Y. The coordinates 3k recover b, making this map an injection of the uncountable set of all binary sequences into Y. If Y were a countable set, composing with an injection into N would make all binary sequences countable, contrary to part (i). Therefore Y is uncountable.​ The construction works for every prescribed binary sequence (an​); no special distribution property of the digits of 2​ is needed.

First apply momentum conservation to the rocket and the infinitesimal amount of expelled gas. During dt, the exhaust mass is αdt and its inertial velocity, to first order, is v−u. With no dust, the momentum balance is
mv=(m−αdt)(v+dv)+αdt(v−u)+o(dt).
(38)
It gives the rocket equation mv˙=αu​, while m˙=−α.
For a dust-collecting rocket, the incoming dust mass is βvdt with zero initial momentum. The same balance has rocket mass m+(βv−α)dt after the interval, so
mv=(m+(βv−α)dt)(v+dv)+αdt(v−u)+o(dt).
(39)
Consequently
m˙=βv−α,mv˙=αu−βv2.​
(40)
The second term in the acceleration equation accounts for the momentum required to bring collected dust up to rocket speed.
Set λ=α/(βu)​ and V=λu, so αu=βV2. As long as the engines operate and 0≤v<V, the velocity increases and time can be eliminated:
dvdlogm​=V2−v2v−λ2u​=−2(V−v)λ−1​−2(V+v)λ+1​.
(41)
Integrating from v=0, m=m0​, gives
logm0​m​=2λ−1​logVV−v​−2λ+1​logVV+v​.
(42)
Thus the required mass–velocity relation is
m=λm0​u(λu+v)λ+1(λu−v)λ−1​​.​
(43)
If λ>1, this formula would give m→0 as v→V−. In this interval, m˙=β(v−λ2u)<0. A real rocket retains positive body mass even after its fuel is gone, and collected dust only increases that residual mass. It therefore cannot follow this branch all the way to zero mass: its fuel must run out while v<λu. Indeed the hypothetical time integral dt/dv=m/[β(V2−v2)] is integrable at V when λ>1, so even the formal zero-mass endpoint would occur at finite time.
If 0<λ<1, the same relation instead gives m→∞ as v→V−. The mass first decreases, reaches its minimum at v=λ2u, and then increases because dust arrives faster than fuel is expelled. The time integral now diverges at V: under indefinitely maintained thrust, v approaches λu asymptotically while stored dust grows without bound. At that limiting speed, thrust αu balances the dust-loading term βv2, and m˙→βuλ(1−λ)>0. This is the physical reason the total rocket mass no longer signals fuel exhaustion. A rocket with a finite initial fuel supply still runs out at finite time t=Mfuel​/α and before reaching V; increasing total mass does not create new fuel.

For a massive particle of rest mass m, its four-momentum is Pμ=mUμ=(E/c,p), where the four-velocity is Uμ=γ(c,v), E=γmc2 and p=γmv. With metric signature (−,+,+,+), P⋅P=−m2c2. A photon has four-momentum (E/c,(E/c)n^), a future-pointing null vector. Four-momentum conservation states that the total incoming and outgoing four-momenta agree for an isolated interaction; this includes both energy and momentum conservation in every inertial frame.
Take the incoming photon direction as x. Its energy is ℏω=μmc2, so the total initial four-momentum is
P=mc(1+μ,μ,0,0),−P⋅P=m2c2(1+2μ).
(60)
The invariant mass available after photon absorption is therefore M=m1+2μ​. In the centre-of-momentum frame, the two equal daughters have opposite momenta. Their total energy is at least their combined rest energy 2αmc2, with equality precisely when both daughters have zero momentum in that frame. Thus the mass threshold after photon absorption is
αmax​=21​1+2μ​.​
(61)
The bound is attainable kinematically by taking each daughter four-momentum equal to P/2; each then has the required rest mass. It immediately gives αmax​→1/2 as μ→0.
At this threshold both daughters move with the centre-of-momentum frame. The common laboratory velocity follows from v=cP/P0:
v=1+μcμ​=1+μ−1c​.​
(62)
For μ=0, its continuous limiting value is zero. At smaller allowed daughter rest masses, some of the centre-of-momentum energy appears as their relative kinetic energy, so their four-momenta need not be P/2.
