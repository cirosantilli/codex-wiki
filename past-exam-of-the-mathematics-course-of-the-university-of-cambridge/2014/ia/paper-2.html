For continuously differentiable coefficients on a simply connected domain, the exactness condition is Py​=Qx​. Locally the same condition suffices without a global topological assumption. An exact first-order ordinary differential equation has a potential F with Fx​=P and Fy​=Q, so dF/dx=P+Qy′=0 along a solution. Thus its implicit solution is F(x,y)=C. One local construction, on a rectangle about (x0​,y0​), is
F(x,y)=∫x0​x​P(s,y0​)ds+∫y0​y​Q(x,t)dt.
(3)
The equality of cross derivatives verifies both required derivatives; on a simply connected domain one may use the corresponding path-independent integral of the exact differential form.
Here P=4x+3y, Q=3x+3y2 and Py​=Qx​=3. Integrating P with respect to x gives F=2x2+3xy+K(y). Matching Fy​=Q gives K′=3y2, so take K=y3. The initial value sets C=F(1,2)=16. Therefore
2x2+3xy+y3=16.​
(4)
This is the requested explicit relation between the coordinates. Since Fy​(1,2)=15=0, the implicit function theorem gives a unique local graph through the initial point, with slope −10/15=−2/3.

Write the countable supports as Di​ and the marginal masses as pi​(xi​). Independence gives joint mass ∏i​pi​(xi​). Assuming finite expected values, absolute integrability is justified by Tonelli theorem:
E[∏i​∣Xi​∣]=∑x1​∈D1​,…,xn​∈Dn​​∏i​∣xi​∣pi​(xi​)=∏i​E∣Xi​∣<∞.

Consequently Fubini's theorem permits factorization of the signed sum, proving the expectation of a product of independent random variables:

The second claim holds as a general assertion with the additional hypothesis that the positive variables are integer-valued. For a nonnegative integer-valued Z, the pointwise identity Z=∑m≥0​1{Z>m}​ and Tonelli theorem give the tail-sum formula
E[Z]=∑m=0∞​P(Z>m).

If each Xi​ is integer-valued, their product is too. Apply this identity to each factor and to the product, then use independence:
i∏​m≥0∑​P(Xi​>m)=i∏​E[Xi​]=E[i∏​Xi​]=m≥0∑​P(i∏​Xi​>m).​
(10)
Under the literal broader meaning of a discrete random variable, the second claim is false. Two independent constants X1​=X2​=3/2 are positive and discrete, but the left side is 2⋅2=4 and the right side is 3. More generally the integer-indexed tail sum of a positive real-valued variable is E⌈Z⌉, not EZ. This is the integer-valued hypothesis in the tail-sum formula.

For c=0 and on an interval where x=0, the logarithmic derivative substitution gives
y′=cxx′′​−cx2x′2​,cy2=cx2x′2​.
(11)
The quadratic terms cancel. Multiplying by cx gives the linearization of a Riccati equation:
x′′+a(t)x′+cb(t)x=0.​
(12)
Multiplying x by a nonzero constant leaves y unchanged. If c=0, that substitution is undefined, but the original equation is already a first-order linear ordinary differential equation, solvable by an integrating factor.
For the particular equation, c=1 and the linear equation is x′′+x′/t−λ2x/t2=0. Use the PDF's logarithmic coordinate τ=logt and write X(τ)=x(eτ). The chain rule gives x′=X′/t and x′′=(X′′−X′)/t2, leaving X′′−λ2X=0. Thus x=Atλ+Bt−λ, as also follows from the Euler-Cauchy equation. Recovering the Riccati equation solution yields
y(t)=tλ​At2λ+BAt2λ−B​.
(13)
The initial value imposes A=−3B; choose A=3,B=−1. Therefore
y(t)=tλ​3t2λ−13t2λ+1​,t∗​=3−1/(2λ).​
(14)

Linearize about rest and a flat surface. An appropriate small-amplitude scaling has ∣ζ∣/h≪1 and ∣u∣/gh​≪1, with gradients on fixed common length/time scales. Products such as (ζu)x​ and uux​ are then higher order. The linearized shallow water equations become
ζt​+hux​=0,ut​+gζx​=0.
(15)
Differentiate the first with respect to time and substitute the second:
ζtt​=ghζxx​,c=gh​.​
(16)
The shallow-water dispersion relation therefore has a constant wave speed. In these equations g is gravitational acceleration, despite the source's label “gravitational constant”; gh has units of speed squared.

With zero initial time derivative, the integral term in the D'Alembert formula vanishes. The Heaviside step function representation gives
ζ(x,t)=21​[H(x+ct+1)−H(x+ct−1)+H(x−ct+1)−H(x−ct−1)].​
(20)
The rectangular pulse splitting under the wave equation consists of two width-two pulses of height 1/2, moving left and right at speed c. At t=0 they coincide and add to the initial height-one pulse. Before ct=1 the overlapping interval has height one; after ct=1 the two pulses are separated. Values exactly at the fronts depend on the chosen H(0) convention. Away from the fronts the equation holds classically, and across the fronts it holds as a weak solution of the wave equation.

Differentiate the mechanical energy and use the damped pendulum equation:
dtdE​=θ˙(θ¨+sinθ)=−cθ˙2≤0.​
(26)
The energy dissipation of a damped pendulum makes E nonincreasing, rather than strictly decreasing at every instant: its derivative vanishes at turning points. It is constant along an equilibrium trajectory. Along every nonstationary trajectory it strictly decreases over any nonzero time interval, because a vanishing integral of cθ˙2 over an interval would force an equilibrium there and hence everywhere by uniqueness.

The small-angle approximation replaces sinθ by θ, giving the damped harmonic oscillator equation θ¨+cθ˙+θ=0. Its characteristic roots satisfy r2+cr+1=0.
For 0<c<2, put ν=1−c2/4​; the motion is underdamped:
θ(t)≃e−ct/2[Acos(νt)+Bsin(νt)].​
(27)
It oscillates with decaying amplitude. For c=2, the repeated root gives critical damping:
θ(t)≃(A+Bt)e−t.​
(28)
For c>2, the motion is overdamped:
θ(t)≃Ae(−c+c2−4​)t/2+Be(−c−c2−4​)t/2.​
(29)
Both real exponents are negative, so there is no oscillatory factor. The two constants are arbitrary in each case; these approximations require the displacement to remain small.

The phase plane system is x˙=y, y˙​=−cy−sinx. Its equilibrium points are (kπ,0) for every integer k. At such a point the Jacobian matrix and characteristic equation are
J=(0−(−1)k​1−c​),r2+cr+(−1)k=0.
(30)
For even k, the equilibrium point is a stable focus for 0<c<2, with eigenvalues −c/2±i1−c2/4​; it is a stable node for c>2, with two distinct negative real eigenvalues. The excluded value c=2 gives the repeated critical case.
For odd k, the eigenvalues are (−c±c2+4​)/2. One is positive and one negative, so each is a saddle equilibrium for every c>0. These classifications follow from the linearization of a dynamical system, since all the relevant eigenvalues have nonzero real part for c>0. The even equilibria remain hyperbolic at c=2 too, although their linearization then has a repeated eigenvalue; that critical case is excluded from the requested classification.

For c=1, the even equilibria are stable foci with eigenvalues (−1±i3​)/2, and the odd equilibria are saddle equilibria with eigenvalues r±​=(−1±5​)/2. The local saddle directions are y=r±​[x−(2j+1)π].
The phase portrait shows clockwise spiraling into each even equilibrium: on its right-hand horizontal axis, the flow initially points down. The stable separatrices of the saddles divide attraction basins on the unwrapped angle axis, while the unstable branches flow into neighboring sinks. Initial conditions with enough mechanical energy can cross one or more potential crests before being captured. The curves repeat under x↦x+2π. They are trajectories, not conservative energy contours: energy dissipation of a damped pendulum rules out nonconstant periodic orbits.

A probability space consists of a sample space Ω, a sigma-algebra F of events, and a probability measure P satisfying:
  • P(A)≥0 for every A∈F.
  • P(Ω)=1.
  • For pairwise disjoint events, P(⋃j≥1​Aj​)=∑j≥1​P(Aj​).
For arbitrary events, Boole's inequality is P(⋃j​Aj​)≤∑j​P(Aj​). To prove it, replace each Aj​ by Dj​=Aj​∖⋃i<j​Ai​. The Dj​ are disjoint, have the same union, and satisfy Dj​⊆Aj​. Countable additivity and monotonicity give P(⋃j​Aj​)=∑j​P(Dj​)≤∑j​P(Aj​). Finite unions are included by taking subsequent events empty.
Let Hi​ be the ith heads event. For every m, the event of infinitely many heads lies inside ⋃i≥m​Hi​. By the union bound, its probability is at most ∑i≥m​pi​, which tends to zero. Thus the probability of infinitely many heads is 0. This is the First Borel-Cantelli lemma, and requires no independence of the coin tosses.
For the dice experiment there are 4 ordered outcomes giving a sum of 5 and 6 giving 7, out of 36. Each trial therefore has p5​=1/9, p7​=1/6 and irrelevant-outcome probability 13/18. By independence between trials, summing over the number of initial irrelevant outcomes gives
P(5 before 7)=k≥0∑​(1813​)k91​=p5​+p7​p5​​=52​.​
(31)
The geometric series also shows that one of the two relevant sums eventually appears almost surely. Equivalently, first-step analysis gives p=p5​+(13/18)p.

For λ≥0, a Poisson distribution has mass P(X=k)=e−λλk/k! for k=0,1,…; at λ=0 it is concentrated at zero. Its moment-generating function is
MX​(s)=k≥0∑​eske−λk!λk​=exp[λ(es−1)],s∈R.​
(32)
For independent X,Y, the moment-generating function of their sum is the product, exp[(λ+μ)(es−1)]. Uniqueness of the moment-generating function near zero therefore gives X+Y∼Poisson(λ+μ). Repeating the argument gives Sn​=∑i=1n​Xi​∼Poisson(n) when every parameter is one.
The displayed sequence is an​=P(Sn​≤n). The classical central limit theorem states that independent identically distributed variables with finite mean m and nonzero finite variance σ2 have standardized sum (Sn​−nm)/(σn​) converging in distribution to the standard normal distribution. Here m=σ2=1, so
an​=P(n​Sn​−n​≤0)⟶Φ(0)=21​.​
(33)
Convergence of the distribution functions at the continuity point zero justifies the last step. This is the Poisson cumulative probability at its growing mean.

Write M(x)=E[N(x)]. The waiting time for two increments larger than 1/2 dominates N(x) for 0≤x≤1 and has mean 4, so M is finite. P(X>s+t∣X>s)=e−λse−λ(s+t)​=e−λt=P(X>t).​

fZ​(z)=2λe−2λz(z≥0),P(X>Y)=21​.​
