The uniform error bound for nearby decaying exponentials therefore gives an O(ε2) contribution even for x=O(ε−1). The middle-root error is O(ε) with coefficient O(ε), again giving O(ε2). The fast-root error is O(1) with magnitude O(ε−1) and coefficient O(ε2), giving only O(ε3). Coefficient errors are O(ε2) or smaller. This estimate concerns y itself, rather than asserting the same uniform order for all derivatives or a relative error at its zero.
For the sketch, on x=εX one obtains
y(x)∼ε2(X−1+e−X).
(6)
Thus y∼x2/2 at the origin and rises smoothly after the fast layer. For x=O(1) it rises towards a plateau of height approximately ε/2, then decays on the much longer scale x=O(ε−1). The bulk maximum lies near x=log(4/ε)/(2−ε/2) and has height asymptotic to ε/2.
The sign of the parameter matters on an infinite interval. If ε→0− is allowed, the singular mode grows rather than decays. For each fixed x>0, its dominant contribution is y(x)∼ε2exp[(−1/ε+2)x]. The extra 2 in that exponent is needed for relative leading accuracy at fixed x. The positive-parameter uniform absolute bound and decaying sketch do not extend to that regime.

Apply the stationary phase method to the real part of the exponential integral for the Bessel function of the first kind. With n fixed, use oscillatory phase sinϑ and oscillatory integral amplitude e−inϑ. The unique stationary point is ϑ0​=π/2, with second derivative −1. Its contribution is
π1​ℜ[e−inπ/2eix−iπ/4x2π​​].
(7)
The leading endpoint terms of the exponential integral are imaginary and do not contribute to its real part. Hence the large-argument asymptotic expansion of the Bessel function of the first kind is
Jn​(x)=πx2​​cos(x−2nπ​−4π​)+O(x−3/2)​.
(8)
This is an additive asymptotic formula: a relative ratio is inappropriate at zeros of the leading cosine. The envelope decreases like x−1/2 while the oscillation frequency approaches one.

Initially suppose 0<α<π/2, the usual oscillatory range. The oscillatory phase is nΦ(ϑ) with Φ=secαsinϑ−ϑ. Its stationary point is ϑ=α, since secαcosϑ=1. At that point
Φ(α)=tanα−α,Φ′′(α)=−tanα.
(9)
The stationary phase method therefore gives the Debye Bessel asymptotic
Jn​(nsecα)=πntanα2​​cos[n(tanα−α)−4π​]+O(n−3/2)​.
(10)
The error is additive for fixed positive α bounded away from π/2; this formula is not uniform as α→0, when the stationary point joins the endpoint and its curvature vanishes.
The printed condition α>0 alone includes other trigonometric branches. All defined fixed real cases can be covered as follows. If 0<∣cosα∣<1, put β=arccos∣cosα∣∈(0,π/2). Use the displayed formula with β instead of α, and multiply it by 1 if secα>0, or by (−1)n if secα<0. This follows from the integer-order parity Jn​(−x)=(−1)nJn​(x), which is also obtained from the defining integral by ϑ↦π−ϑ. Introduce the slow time T=εt and treat t,T independently in the method of multiple scales. Write
y=y0​(t,T)+εy1​(t,T)+⋯,y0​=R(T)cosϕ,ϕ=t+θ(T).
(15)
At the next order,
(∂t2​+1)y1​=f(Rcosϕ,−Rsinϕ)+2RT​sinϕ+2RθT​cosϕ.
(16)
For fixed R define the period average by
⟨h⟩=2π1​∫02π​h(ϕ;R)dϕ.
(17)
Here f inside the averages is evaluated at the leading position and velocity Rcosϕ,−Rsinϕ. Orthogonality to both fundamental harmonics is the solvability condition in the method of multiple scales: it removes the resonant forcing that would otherwise generate a secular term. Since the squared sine and cosine averages are 1/2, the amplitude-phase equations are
RT​=−⟨fsinϕ⟩,θT​=−R1​⟨fcosϕ⟩​.

These equations describe a bounded, weakly perturbed oscillation over t=O(ε−1) while the amplitude remains in a range where the expansion is ordered. At zero amplitude the oscillation phase coordinate is singular; Cartesian harmonic coefficients or an equilibrium analysis should replace it.

Keep the prescribed outer slip velocity fixed when perturbing the unsteady Prandtl equation. Cancelling the base equation and retaining terms linear in the disturbance gives the linearized unsteady Prandtl equation
u~t​+Uu~x​+Vu~y​+Ux​u~+Uy​v~=u~yy​,u~x​+v~y​=0.
(41)
The no-slip boundary condition supplies u~=v~=0 at the wall; the fixed outer velocity supplies u~→0 at infinity. There is no prescribed zero normal-velocity disturbance at infinity: a finite displacement-related value is permitted.
With the frozen parallel profile U=U(y), V=0, the coefficients are invariant under translations in x and t. Normal modes therefore separate those variables. For the mode convention used here the equations are
iα(U−c)u+U′v=u′′,iαu+v′=0.
(42)
Eliminate u=−v′/(iα) to obtain the Prandtl normal-mode equation
v′′′=iα[(U−c)v′−U′v]​,v(0)=v′(0)=0,v′(∞)=0,
(43)
with a bounded far-field v. Requiring v(∞)=0 would incorrectly eliminate the proposed outer profile. For real U and real α, complex conjugation of the equation and its boundary conditions replaces (α,c,v) by (−α,c∗,v∗). This proves the stated eigenvalue symmetry. The paired modes have the same temporal growth rate αImc, so choosing positive wavenumber loses no real physical disturbance.
Here the prescribed parallel profile is a local frozen-coefficient model. A nontrivial arbitrary U(y) with V=0 and constant outer slip is not generally an exact steady solution of the unforced unsteady Prandtl equation; its base equation would require U′′=0. The following stability calculation uses the simplifying local model stipulated for the mode analysis.
Set c0​=Uc​=U(yc​). Away from the critical point the dominant equation is (U−c0​)v0′​−U′v0​=0, whose solutions are multiples of U−c0​. Choosing the coefficient to be zero below yc​ and one above it satisfies the wall and tangential far-field conditions. The choice c0​=Uc​ makes v0​ continuous across the joining point; U′(yc​)=0 also makes its first derivative continuous. Its second derivative jumps. Thus it is a valid leading outer solution away from yc​, but viscosity must smooth the join in a critical layer in a shear flow.
Near the join, U−c0​∼(y−yc​)2/2. If the layer width is ℓ, matching gives v=O(ℓ2). The advection side of the mode equation scales as αℓ3 and v′′′ scales as ℓ−1. Balance gives ℓ4∼α−1=ε/k. Equivalently, the eigenvalue correction ε1/2c1​ balances (y−yc​)2, so the quarter-power critical layer has
p=41​,q=21​​,y−yc​=ε1/4Y,v=ε1/2w(Y)+⋯.
(44)
Substitution, retaining the leading terms and cancelling their common factor, gives
kYw−k(2Y2​−c1​)wY​=iwYYY​​.
(45)
At order ε1/2 outside the layer,
(U−c0​)v1′​−U′v1​=c1​v0′​.
(46)
A solution consistent with the chosen lower branch and the wall conditions is v1​=0 below the join. Above it a particular solution is v1​=−c1​; a multiple of U−c0​ represents an arbitrary amplitude renormalization and may be set to zero. Matching consequently requires
w→0(Y→−∞),w−(2Y2​−c1​)→0(Y→+∞)​.
(47)
The corresponding derivatives match as wY​∼Y, wYY​→1 on the positive side and as zero on the negative side. The matching statements require that growing homogeneous corrections be absent.
To symmetrize these different end conditions and remove k and i, choose
a=(ik)1/4=k1/4eiπ/8,z=aY,C=a2c1​,W=2a2w−(2z2​−C).
(48)

The derivative is third order, as required by the original mode equation. Initially the two ends are along the rotated rays z=aY with real Y. Continuing them to the real z axis is compatible with the asymptotic end conditions: the decreasing homogeneous correction behaves exponentially as exp[−z2/(22​)] and still decreases throughout the rotation from angle π/8 to zero. Thus the supplied real-axis spectral normalization uses the same recessive conditions; a complex coordinate change should not be mistaken for a real stretching alone.
For each supplied real eigenvalue Cn​=(4n+7)/2​, the phase-speed correction is
c1​=k−1/2e−iπ/4Cn​,Imc=−2k​Cn​​ε1/2+o(ε1/2).

Hence the frozen non-monotone flow has high-wavenumber instability of a non-monotone Prandtl layer, with arbitrarily rapid linear growth as the positive wavenumber increases. The normal-mode calculation demonstrates linear instability; it is not by itself a claim about the final nonlinear state.
