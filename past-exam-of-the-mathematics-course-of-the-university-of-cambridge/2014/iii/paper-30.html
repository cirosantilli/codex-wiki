For independent identically distributed observations Y1​,…,Yn​, the likelihood function and log-likelihood are
Ln​(θ)=∏i=1n​f(Yi​,θ),ℓn​(θ)=∑i=1n​logf(Yi​,θ).
(1)
A maximum-likelihood estimator is a measurable choice θn​∈argmaxθ∈Θ​Ln​(θ), when a maximizer exists. It need not be unique. The one-observation score function is sθ​(y)=∇θ​logf(y,θ), and the Fisher information matrix is
I(θ)=Eθ​[sθ​(Y)sθ​(Y)T]=−Eθ​[∇θ2​logf(Y,θ)].
(2)
Under the regularity assumptions, differentiation beneath the integral gives Eθ​sθ​=∇θ​∫f=0. Differentiating again gives the second information identity. Independent observations have total Fisher information nI(θ).
Assume fixed parameter dimension, an interior true parameter θ0​, a positive-definite matrix I(θ0​), the standard differentiability and integrability conditions, and statistical consistency of θn​. Then the asymptotic normality of a maximum likelihood estimator is
n​(θn​−θ0​) d​ Np​(0,I(θ0​)−1).​
(3)
Here I is the information per observation. Thus the leading covariance matrix of the estimator itself is I(θ0​)−1/n.
To prove this, statistical consistency places the estimator in an interior ball about θ0​ with probability tending to one, where its score function vanishes. Write dn​=θn​−θ0​ and use the integral first-order Taylor formula for a vector map:
0=∇ℓn​(θ0​)+{∫01​∇2ℓn​(θ0​+tdn​)dt}dn​.
(4)
This integral matrix is needed in a vector problem; one does not have to assert a common scalar mean-value point for every score component. Put
Jn​=−n1​∫01​∇2ℓn​(θ0​+tdn​)dt.
(5)
The regular local uniform law of large numbers, continuity of the expected Hessian matrix, and statistical consistency give Jn​P​I(θ0​). The multivariate central limit theorem gives
n​1​∇ℓn​(θ0​)=n​1​∑i=1n​sθ0​​(Yi​) d​ Np​(0,I(θ0​)).
(6)
The limiting information matrix is nonsingular, so Jn−1​P​I(θ0​)−1. Solving the Taylor identity and applying the Slutsky theorem proves the displayed normal limit, since I−1II−1=I−1. Boundary parameters or singular information are outside this regular theorem.

Represent the subset model by a set M of k column indices and let XM​ contain those columns. Its ordinary least squares estimator sets the omitted coefficients to zero and has
θM​=(XMT​XM​)−1XMT​Y,XθM=PM​Y,PM​=XM​(XMT​XM​)−1XMT​.
(7)
For k=0, use PM​=0. Full column rank of X implies full column rank for each XM​. The matrix PM​ is an orthogonal projection matrix of rank k.
Put μ=Xθ and bM​=(I−PM​)μ. Projection orthogonality gives the bias-variance decomposition of mean squared error
R(M)=E∥PM​Y−μ∥2=∥bM​∥2+kσ2,
(8)
while the expected residual sum of squares is
E∥(I−PM​)Y∥2=∥bM​∥2+(n−k)σ2.
(9)
These equations distinguish the mean-vector prediction risk in this problem from the additional noise in predicting a new response vector.
First take p<n. Let PX​ project onto the full column space of X. Since the full model contains μ, the residual estimate of Gaussian noise variance
σ2=n−p∥(I−PX​)Y∥2​
(10)
is unbiased: its numerator has expectation (n−p)σ2. Consequently an unbiased Gaussian projection risk estimate is
R(M)=∥(I−PM​)Y∥2+(2k−n)σ2.​
(11)
Indeed its expectation is ∥bM​∥2+(n−k)σ2+(2k−n)σ2=R(M) for every θ, even when the subset model omits true effects. Independence of the two terms is not needed for this expectation calculation. The full-model variance estimate is important: the subset residual variance generally includes omitted-variable bias.
For a finite candidate collection, choose a model minimizing R(M), breaking ties in favour of a smaller model. The term −nσ2 is common to all models, so this amounts to minimizing
RSS(M)+2kσ2,
(12)
the Mallows Cp form. The fit improvement competes with an increasing dimension penalty. Unbiasedness holds for each fixed model; the estimate at the data-selected model need not remain unbiased, because selection favours downward fluctuations. The method is a heuristic model selection rule, rather than an assertion that it finds the true model with certainty.
The printed p≤n includes a genuine qualification. If p=n, there are no full-model residual degrees of freedom. Except in the balanced case 2k=n, no integrable data-only unbiased estimator can satisfy the requested identity for all means and unknown variances. Here is a proof of the unknown-variance risk estimation in a saturated Gaussian model obstruction. Since X is invertible, μ ranges over all Rn. Suppose T(Y) had the required expectation at every μ and variance s>0. For v>0, let U∼Nn​(μ,vI) and let Y∣U∼Nn​(U,sI). Marginally Y∼Nn​(μ,(s+v)I). Applying the assumed identity conditionally gives
ET(Y)=E∥(I−PM​)U∥2+ks=∥bM​∥2+(n−k)v+ks,
(13)
whereas applying it to the marginal distribution gives ∥bM​∥2+k(s+v). Their difference is (n−2k)v, which must vanish. Integrability under the marginal Gaussian justifies conditioning. If 2k=n, the obstruction disappears and the residual sum of squares alone has expectation ∥bM​∥2+kσ2. Thus
p=n:R(M)=RSS(M) is unbiased exactly in the case 2k=n.​
(14)
The general construction therefore requires p<n, or an independently available unbiased variance estimate.

The ordinary column Gram matrix is G=XTX. Use the normalized empirical Gram matrix
Σ=n1​XTX,
(15)
so that standard Gaussian entries give EΣ=Ip​. This is the normalization needed for concentration around ∥θ∥22​.
The restricted isometry property of order s with constant 0≤δ<1 means that the normalized map X/n​ approximately preserves the Euclidean norm of all vectors with at most s nonzero coordinates:
(1−δ)∥u∥22​≤n1​∥Xu∥22​≤(1+δ)∥u∥22​.
(16)
Equivalently, every principal block ΣMM​ with ∣M∣≤s satisfies ∥ΣMM​−I∥op​≤δ. The least such δ is its restricted isometry constant. In the unnormalized definition apply this property to X/n​ itself.
For a standard normal variable g, direct Gaussian integration gives Eeλg2=(1−2λ)−1/2 for λ<1/2. Independence therefore yields the moment-generating function of a chi-squared distribution, centred here at its mean:
logEeλZ=n(−λ−21​log(1−2λ))≤1−2λnλ2​,0≤λ<21​.
(17)
The inequality follows from −log(1−u)−u=∑j≥2​uj/j≤u2/(2(1−u)). For t>0, the Chernoff bound with λ=t/(2(n+t)) gives
Pr(Z≥t)≤exp(−λt+1−2λnλ2​)=exp(−4(n+t)t2​).
(18)
At t=0 the trivial probability bound suffices. This proves the requested bound, with a stronger prefactor one.
For the lower tail, log(1+u)≥u−u2/2 gives logEe−λZ≤nλ2 for λ≥0. Taking λ=t/(2n) yields Pr(Z≤−t)≤e−t2/(4n). Combining both tails gives the useful chi-squared concentration inequality
Pr(∣Z∣≥t)≤2exp(−4(n+t)t2​),t≥0.​
(19)
For z≥0 set t=4(nz​+z). A direct calculation shows
t2−4z(n+t)=12nz+16znz​≥0.
(20)
Thus the exponent is at least z, proving
Pr(Z≥4(nz​+z))≤2e−z.​
(21)
The same threshold bounds the two-sided tail.
Finally fix a deterministic θ and put r=∥θ∥2​. If r>0, independence of the Gaussian rows gives Xi​θ/r∼N(0,1) independently. Therefore
θTΣθ=r2(1+nZ​).
(22)
The two-sided bound just proved supplies
Pr(​θTΣθ−∥θ∥22​​>4∥θ∥22​(nz​​+nz​))≤2e−z.​
(23)
For r=0 the quadratic form is deterministically zero; the strict inequality makes the formula valid in that case too. When r≤1, replacing the threshold by 4(z/n​+z/n) gives an absolute bound independent of θ. In particular every fixed such direction concentrates at rate n−1/2 for a fixed confidence level. The restricted isometry property requires a simultaneous statement over sparse directions; this fixed-direction calculation alone is not that stronger assertion.

Let Δn​=supθ∈Θ​∣Qn​(θ)−Q(θ)∣. For any ε>0, the set
Fε​={θ∈Θ:∥θ−θ0​∥≥ε}
(24)
is compact. If it is empty there is nothing to prove. Otherwise continuity and the unique minimum give a strictly positive separation gap
dε​=minθ∈Fε​​{Q(θ)−Q(θ0​)}>0.
(25)
Take any measurable attained minimizer of Qn​. Its defining inequality gives
Q(θn​)≤Qn​(θn​)+Δn​≤Qn​(θ0​)+Δn​≤Q(θ0​)+2Δn​.
(26)
Consequently
Pr(∥θn​−θ0​∥≥ε)≤Pr(Δn​≥dε​/2)⟶0.​
(27)
This proves argmin consistency under uniform convergence in probability. No continuity of Qn​ is needed once the minimizer exists; compactness and continuity concern the deterministic separation gap. The uniform convergence in probability assumption controls all candidate parameters, including the random minimizer.
For the estimating equation, fix ε>0 and put a=θ0​−ε, b=θ0​+ε. The prescribed signs make η=21​min{−S(a),S(b)}>0. Pointwise convergence in probability at just these two points implies
Pr(Sn​(a)≥0)≤Pr(∣Sn​(a)−S(a)∣≥η)⟶0,
(28)
and similarly Pr(Sn​(b)≤0)→0. With probability tending to one, Sn​(a)<0<Sn​(b). The intermediate value theorem then gives a zero inside (a,b), and uniqueness identifies it with θn​. Hence
Pr(∣θn​−θ0​∣≥ε)≤Pr(Sn​(a)≥0)+Pr(Sn​(b)≤0)⟶0.​
(29)
This is consistency of a uniquely bracketed zero. It needs no monotonicity, no continuity of the limit S, and no uniform convergence of the Sn​. The bracket interval must lie in the domain of Sn​: read literally, the printed sign condition for every positive ε puts every real point into Θ, so this requirement is satisfied. More generally it is enough to have an interval about θ0​ and sign brackets arbitrarily close to it.
