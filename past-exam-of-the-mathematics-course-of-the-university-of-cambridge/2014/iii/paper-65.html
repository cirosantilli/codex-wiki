If C=Rn, the family of proper containing half-spaces is empty and its intersection is, by convention, Rn. If C=∅, every closed half-space contains it and their intersection is empty. Now suppose C is a nonempty proper closed convex set. Take x∈/C and let z be its Euclidean projection onto a convex set. This projection exists: a minimizing sequence can be restricted to a bounded ball, and closedness gives attainment. It is unique by convexity and strict convexity of squared distance.
For y∈C, the segment z+t(y−z) remains in C for 0≤t≤1. Minimality at t=0 implies
dtd​∥x−z−t(y−z)∥2​t=0+​≥0,⟨x−z,y−z⟩≤0.
(1)
Thus the closed half-space Hx​={y:⟨x−z,y−z⟩≤0} contains C but excludes x, since ⟨x−z,x−z⟩>0. Every point outside C is excluded by at least one containing half-space. The reverse inclusion is immediate, giving
C=⋂{H:H is a closed half-space and C⊆H}.​
(2)
This is the half-space representation of a closed convex set. The argument gives an explicit separating hyperplane rather than just citing a Hahn-Banach separation theorem.

For an extended-real function define its Fenchel conjugate by f∗(p)=supx​(⟨p,x⟩−f(x)) and its biconjugate by f∗∗(x)=supp​(⟨p,x⟩−f∗(p)). The Fenchel-Moreau theorem states, in the standard proper-envelope setting,
f∗∗=clconvf.​
(3)
Here the right side is the largest lower semicontinuous convex function below f, equivalently the function whose epigraph is the closed convex hull of epif. It is enough to assume f is proper and has an affine minorant, ensuring this envelope is proper. In particular, for a proper convex function that is lower semicontinuous, f∗∗=f​.
First, ⟨p,x⟩−f∗(p)≤f(x) by the definition of the convex conjugate. The biconjugate is a supremum of continuous affine functions, so it is convex, lower semicontinuous and no greater than f. Second, the best intercept for an affine minorant with slope p is −f∗(p): ⟨p,x⟩+a≤f(x) for all x precisely when a≤−f∗(p). Thus f∗∗ is the supremum of all affine minorants.
To prove that no part of the closed convex envelope is missed, set E=clconv(epif). The half-space representation of a closed convex set from part (a), applied in Rn+1, separates any (x0​,t0​)∈/E from E by an inequality a⋅x+bt≤d. Because E is upward closed, b≤0. If b<0, division by −b gives an affine minorant ℓ with ℓ(x0​)>t0​.
A vertical separator has b=0. Let ℓ0​ be an existing affine minorant. Combine a⋅x≤d with ℓ0​(x)≤t to obtain
ℓ0​(x)+εa⋅x−d​≤ton E.
(4)
Since a⋅x0​−d>0, a sufficiently small positive ε makes this affine function exceed t0​. Thus vertical half-spaces can be approximated by nonvertical epigraph supports. Every point below the envelope is excluded by an affine minorant, so the supremum of these minorants is exactly the envelope. This is the decisive use of part (a).
Properness and the minorant convention matter for unrestricted extended-real functions. For example, f(x)=−x2 on R has no affine minorant; f∗≡+∞ and f∗∗≡−∞. With the corresponding improper-envelope convention its closed convex envelope is also −∞. The theorem should not silently describe such an envelope as proper. The identically +∞ function is another degenerate case, handled separately by extended-real conventions.

Applied to a discrete gradient, the Huber gradient regularizer penalizes small slopes quadratically and large slopes linearly. Let r(u)=∥u−g∥22​ and fix the reference noise budget σ. Define the convex perturbation function
f(u,z)=TV(u)+δ(−∞,0]​(r(u)−σ−z).
(12)
Positive z increases the allowed squared noise level. The feasible epigraph set r(u)≤σ+z is convex and closed, so this is a proper lower semicontinuous jointly convex perturbation. Writing the inequality indicator as a supremum over its multiplier gives
uinf​λ≥0sup​L(u,λ),L(u,λ)=TV(u)+λ(r(u)−σ).​
(13)
The Lagrange dual function is d(λ)=infu​L(u,λ) for λ≥0. In the signed convex conjugate convention of part (a), y=−λ; positive y gives dual objective −∞ because the perturbation can be made arbitrarily large.
The feasible ball is nonempty and compact, so lower semicontinuity of total variation gives a primal minimizer. For σ>0, u=g satisfies r(g)=0<σ, providing the Slater condition. Total variation is finite everywhere in the given discretization and hence continuous. Strong duality and dual attainment give a finite optimal λ∗≥0. r(u∗)≤σ,λ∗≥0,λ∗(r(u∗)−σ)=0,0∈∂TV(u∗)+2λ∗(u∗−g).

For a step size τ>0, define the set-valued maps
Fτf​=I−τ∂f,Bτf​=(I+τ∂f)−1.​
(19)
The forward subgradient step maps x to the set {x−τp:p∈∂f(x)}. If f is differentiable this is the explicit gradient step x−τ∇f(x). The backward subgradient step consists of the y satisfying x−y∈τ∂f(y), an implicit step for the subgradient flow. It is the resolvent of a monotone operator associated with ∂f.
Suppose y1​,y2​∈Bτf​(x). Then pi​=(x−yi​)/τ∈∂f(yi​). The two defining subgradient inequalities are
f(y2​)≥f(y1​)+⟨p1​,y2​−y1​⟩,f(y1​)≥f(y2​)+⟨p2​,y1​−y2​⟩.
(20)
Their sum proves monotonicity of a convex subdifferential, ⟨p1​−p2​,y1​−y2​⟩≥0. But p1​−p2​=−(y1​−y2​)/τ, so
0≤−τ1​∥y1​−y2​∥2,y1​=y2​​.
(21)
Convexity supplies the subgradient inequalities and monotonicity; membership in the subdifferential ensures the two function values are finite, so subtraction is legitimate. Positivity of τ supplies the decisive sign. Properness rules out the identically infinite and negative-infinity pathologies in the overall setting, but lower semicontinuity is not needed for this at-most-one argument. Its role is in existence, proved next. The backward step cannot have two values, though uniqueness alone has not yet shown its domain is all of Rn.

Introduce the primal slack s=Ax−b∈K. The Lagrangian is L(x,y)=c⊤x−⟨y,Ax−b⟩, with y∈K∗=K. Minimizing over x is finite only when A⊤y=c. Thus the conic dual problem is
ysup​b⊤ysubject to A⊤y=c,y∈K.​
(30)
The primal-dual gap at feasible points is c⊤x−b⊤y=⟨s,y⟩≥0. Self-duality specifies the multiplier cone; it does not alone guarantee feasible or bounded problems. The central-path discussion assumes primal and dual strict feasibility and a finite optimum. These additional existence conditions are not implied by the given full column rank of A.
Let F be the canonical logarithmically homogeneous self-concordant barrier, with parameter ν and F(ts)=F(s)−νlogt. For each μ>0, minimizing c⊤x+μF(Ax−b) defines the primal central path. Its stationarity defines the dual path through y=−μ∇F(s)∈intK. The joint characterization is
s=Ax−b∈intK,A⊤y=c,y∈intK,y+μ∇F(s)=0.​
(31)
Euler's identity for logarithmic homogeneity gives ⟨s,∇F(s)⟩=−ν, so ⟨s,y⟩=νμ​. The gap tends to zero as μ↓0. If F∗​(y)=sups∈intK​[−⟨y,s⟩−F(s)] is the dual barrier, the equivalent dual relation is s=−μ∇F∗​(y). A self-dual cone does not justify identifying two arbitrary primal and dual barrier functions without this relation.
For a target parameter μˉ​>0, form residuals rp​=Ax−b−s, rd​=A⊤y−c and rc​=y+μˉ​∇F(s). Linearization gives the central-path Newton system
AΔx−Δs=−rp​,A⊤Δy=−rd​,Δy+μˉ​∇2F(s)Δs=−rc​.​
(32)
For a strictly feasible primal-dual iterate with rp​=rd​=0, elimination reduces it to
μˉ​A⊤∇2F(s)AΔx=−A⊤rc​,Δs=AΔx,Δy=−rc​−μˉ​∇2F(s)Δs.
(33)
The barrier Hessian is positive definite and A has full column rank, so the reduced matrix is positive definite. Alternatively a changing path parameter can be included as an additional linear term Δμ∇F(s); the displayed system instead fixes the new target parameter before solving.
This is an interior-point method because iterates stay in the cone interiors where the barrier and its gradient/Hessian are defined. A full Newton step need not do so. Use a fraction-to-boundary or backtracking step: decrease α>0 until s+αΔs and y+αΔy lie in intK, then enforce an appropriate barrier or residual decrease. Such a positive step exists because the current points are interior. Local barrier norms can also certify an interior step via the Dikin ellipsoid.
For a practical starting point, choose e∈intK and solve a conic phase-I problem, for example minimizing t subject to Ax−b+te∈K and t≥−1. A large positive t with an arbitrary x gives a strictly feasible start for this auxiliary problem. A feasible point with t<0 certifies Ax−b∈intK. If the original problem is strictly feasible, a small negative t is feasible, so phase I can find such a certificate. A similar feasibility procedure handles the dual equality and interior. Alternatively an infeasible-start primal-dual method or homogeneous self-dual embedding starts with interior cone variables while allowing nonzero linear residuals, and can report infeasibility rather than presume an interior solution exists.

A kernel support vector machine constructs a large-margin classifier in a feature Hilbert space. Given training points xi​ and labels yi​∈{−1,1}, choose a feature map Φ:X→H and an affine score f(x)=⟨w,Φ(x)⟩+b. Prediction is the sign of the score. Normalizing the functional margin to one makes the closest separating geometric margin 1/∥w∥, and the full margin between the two supporting hyperplanes is 2/∥w∥. Maximizing that margin therefore minimizes 21​∥w∥2.
For separable data, the hard-margin support vector machine primal is
w,bmin​21​∥w∥H2​subject to yi​(⟨w,Φ(xi​)⟩+b)≥1.​
(37)
Noisy or nonseparable data use slack variables and the soft-margin support vector machine:
w,b,ξmin​21​∥w∥H2​+Ci∑​ξi​,yi​(⟨w,Φ(xi​)⟩+b)≥1−ξi​,ξi​≥0,C>0.​
(38)
Eliminating ξ gives the equivalent hinge loss objective 21​∥w∥2+C∑i​(1−yi​f(xi​))+​. The parameter balances margin size against violations; it is not a hard bound on their number. The bias is unpenalized here, which determines the equality constraint in the dual.
Attach multipliers αi​≥0 to 1−ξi​−yi​f(xi​)≤0 and ηi​≥0 to −ξi​≤0. The Lagrangian is
L=21​∥w∥2+C∑i​ξi​+∑i​αi​[1−ξi​−yi​(⟨w,Φ(xi​)⟩+b)]−∑i​ηi​ξi​.
(39)
Stationarity gives w=∑i​αi​yi​Φ(xi​), ∑i​αi​yi​=0 and C−αi​−ηi​=0. Eliminating w,b,ξ yields
αmax​i∑​αi​−21​i,j∑​αi​αj​yi​yj​k(xi​,xj​),i∑​αi​yi​=0,0≤αi​≤C,​
(40)
where k(x,x′)=⟨Φ(x),Φ(x′)⟩ is a positive-definite kernel, meaning positive semidefinite Gram matrices. The hard-margin dual is the same objective with only αi​≥0, without the upper bound. Soft-margin strict feasibility follows by taking w=b=0 and all ξi​>1, so strong duality and the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions apply. For separable hard-margin data a separator can be rescaled to give strict margins, supplying the analogous qualification. Although the feature space may be infinite dimensional, projecting w onto the finite span of the training feature vectors preserves scores and cannot increase its norm, so this optimization reduces to a finite span.
The complementary slackness relations are
αi​[yi​f(xi​)−1+ξi​]=0,(C−αi​)ξi​=0.
(41)
Points with αi​>0 are support vectors. If 0<αi​<C, then ξi​=0 and yi​f(xi​)=1; such a point gives b=yi​−∑j​αj​yj​k(xj​,xi​). A point with αi​=C can lie inside the margin or be misclassified, while αi​=0 contributes no term to w. If no coefficient lies strictly between the bounds, an admissible bias must be obtained from the KKT inequalities, rather than dividing by a nonexistent margin vector. Bias and dual coefficients can be nonunique even when the optimal feature-space weight is unique.
The kernel trick operates both during training and prediction. The dual optimization uses only the Gram matrix Kij​=k(xi​,xj​), so the feature vectors need not be formed. Prediction likewise uses
f(x)=i:αi​>0∑​αi​yi​k(xi​,x)+b.​
(42)
A nonlinear kernel therefore makes a linear separator in feature space represent a nonlinear boundary in input space. For example, on R2 the degree-two polynomial kernel (x⋅x′)2 corresponds to Φ(x)=(x12​,2​x1​x2​,x22​). Gaussian kernels yield infinite-dimensional feature spaces. An arbitrary similarity is not automatically a valid kernel: for every finite collection and real coefficients ai​, one needs ∑i,j​ai​aj​k(xi​,xj​)≥0. This makes the dual quadratic form positive semidefinite and the maximization concave.
Mercer's theorem gives a spectral realization under its additional analytic hypotheses. For a continuous symmetric positive-semidefinite kernel on a compact domain with a finite full-support measure, the integral operator (Tk​f)(x)=∫k(x,x′)f(x′)dμ(x′) is compact, self-adjoint and positive. The Mercer expansion is
k(x,x′)=∑j​λj​ej​(x)ej​(x′),λj​≥0,
(43)
with the standard uniform convergence conclusions under these hypotheses. Since ∑j​λj​ej​(x)2=k(x,x)<∞, the nonlinear feature map
Φ(x)=(λj​​ej​(x))j​∈ℓ2,k(x,x′)=⟨Φ(x),Φ(x′)⟩ℓ2​​
(44)
is well defined. This explains the connection of Mercer kernels to Hilbert space features, rather than treating the kernel trick as a purely formal substitution.
The finite-Gram positivity condition is more general than this compact-domain spectral theorem. Every such kernel generates a Reproducing kernel Hilbert space: on finite sums of kernel sections define
⟨∑i​ai​k(xi​,⋅),∑j​bj​k(zj​,⋅)⟩=∑i,j​ai​bj​k(xi​,zj​),
(45)
quotient out zero-norm elements, and complete. The resulting space satisfies the reproducing property h(x)=⟨h,k(x,⋅)⟩. Its canonical feature map is Φ(x)=k(x,⋅), whose inner product is exactly k(x,x′). A feature map need not be injective; calling it an embedding does not by itself prove distinct inputs remain distinct. The kernel support vector machine uses this geometry together with convex duality to fit and evaluate a maximum-margin classifier while accessing the geometry only through kernel evaluations.
