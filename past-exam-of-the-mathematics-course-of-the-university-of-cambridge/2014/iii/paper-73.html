A convenient normalization of the Papkovich–Neuber representation uses a harmonic vector h and harmonic scalar ϕ:
u=h−21​∇(x⋅h+ϕ),p=−μ∇⋅h.​
(1)
Indeed, Δ(x⋅h)=2∇⋅h, so ∇⋅u=0 and μΔu=∇p. This is a rescaling of the usual harmonic-potential representation of Stokes flow.
Measure x from the sphere center, set r=∣x∣ and n=x/r. A translational vector harmonic U/r provides the decaying 1/r force field, while the scalar dipole (U⋅x)/r3 adjusts the surface velocity without changing that leading far field. For rotation, (Ω×x)/r3 is harmonic, divergence-free and perpendicular to x. Thus try
h=ArU​+Br3Ω×x​,ϕ=Cr3U⋅x​.
(2)
The translational velocity is 2rA​(I+nn)U−2r3C​(I−3nn)U. Matching its independent tangential and radial components at r=a gives A=3a/2, C=−a3/2; matching rotation gives B=a3. Consequently
u=[4r3a​(I+nn)+4r3a3​(I−3nn)]U+r3a3​Ω×x,p=2r33μa​U⋅x.​
(3)
This superposes the translating sphere in Stokes flow and the rotating sphere in Stokes flow. It is exactly U+Ω×x at the sphere and tends to zero at infinity; the additive ambient pressure is set to zero.

Write n=R/R. At fixed geometry, Linearity of Stokes flow makes the translational velocities linear in the couples. With zero forces and couples perpendicular to R, isotropy and reflection symmetry allow only terms proportional to Gj​×n in each Ui​. There is no longitudinal term: reflect in the plane containing R and the common torque axis, taking account of the axial-vector transformation of torque, and use linearity under torque reversal. Hence
R˙=n⋅(U2​−U1​)=0,R is constant.​
(4)
The direction of R can still change; constant separation does not mean stationary centers.
When the couples are equal, a rotation through π about their common axis exchanges the identical spheres and reverses the in-plane translational velocities while leaving the axial angular velocities unchanged. Uniqueness of Stokes flow then gives
U2​=−U1​,Ω2​=Ω1​.​
(5)
The midpoint is stationary and the pair can orbit it. The body spin need not equal the angular rate of that orbit. These symmetry statements hold at arbitrary noncontact separation, not just in a distant rotlet approximation.

The minimum dissipation theorem fixes the boundary velocities and the far-field velocity: among admissible incompressible velocity fields, the Stokes flow minimizes
D=2μ∫e:edV,e=21​(∇u+∇uT).
(6)
For a competitor u+v with zero boundary data for v, integration by parts eliminates the cross term and leaves D[u+v]−D[u]=2μ∫e(v):e(v)dV≥0.
Extend the actual two-sphere velocity rigidly through sphere 2. It is an admissible field in the one-sphere exterior, with the same translation and rotation of sphere 1. It is continuous across the filled boundary, incompressible, and adds zero strain dissipation inside. Applying the theorem to the exact isolated-sphere flow therefore gives
Dpair​≥6πμa∣U1​∣2+8πμa3∣Ω1​∣2.
(7)
With all applied couples and the second force zero, the boundary-work identity is Dpair​=F1​⋅U1​. Thus 6πμa∣U1​∣2≤F1​⋅U1​≤∣F1​∣∣U1​∣, which proves
U1​⋅F1​≤6πμa∣F1​∣2​.​
(8)
This fixed-force comparison of minimum viscous dissipation uses a comparison at fixed actual velocity first; directly comparing different-force or different-velocity solutions would not justify the result.
If G1​=0, the power is instead F1​⋅U1​+G1​⋅Ω1​, so the preceding estimate no longer bounds the force contribution alone. The two-sphere hydrodynamic mobility matrix generally has a nonzero self translation-rotation coupling: the freely moving second sphere reflects the first sphere's torque field. In the planar geometry this coupling produces a velocity perpendicular to R; by choosing the sign and magnitude of the couple when the force has a component in that direction, its contribution to F1​⋅U1​ can exceed the isolated force-only value. There is no universal force-only inequality with an additional applied couple. Special geometries can eliminate the coupling, but do not restore a general theorem.

Let ζ=6πμa, ϵ=a/R and define the dimensionless leading cross-mobility tensor
M=4R3a​(I+nn).
(9)
For a given applied force, the outgoing Stokeslet is fixed by that force, regardless of the sphere's entrainment velocity. At the other center the Faxén translation law gives its force-only velocity plus the incident Stokeslet velocity. The finite-size dipole and the Laplacian Faxén correction first contribute O(ϵ3∣V∣); an emitted stresslet reflection is still smaller at this order. Therefore
U1​=U2​=(I+M)V+O(ϵ3∣V∣).​
(10)
There is no second-order translational term for prescribed equal forces.
The vorticity of translating-sphere Stokes flow is ω=3aV×r/(2r3); its potential dipole has zero curl. Apply the torque-free Faxén rotation law to the other sphere's field, using r=−R at sphere 1 and r=R at sphere 2. This yields
Ω1​=4R23a​n×V,Ω2​=−4R23a​n×V,​
(11)
through second order in the dimensionless spin aΩ/∣V∣. Neglected spin terms are higher order. This is the method of reflections for Stokes flow, with the ambient field at each application excluding that sphere's own disturbance.

Sphere 2 is now force-free and torque-free. Its translation and spin follow the incident rigid-motion components, so it emits neither a Stokeslet nor a rotlet. Its first disturbance is a stresslet from the incident rate of strain, of magnitude μa3(a∣V∣/R2). Its return velocity at sphere 1 is consequently O(a4∣V∣/R4):
∣U1​−V∣=O(∣V∣ϵ4).​
(14)
More precisely, that leading correction is −(15/4)ϵ4(V⋅n)n. It vanishes for purely transverse forcing, for which the first translational correction is of order ϵ6.
The first-return spin requires an additional cancellation. The leading incident strain at sphere 2 is
E=4R23a​(V⋅n)(I−3nn).
(15)
It is axisymmetric about the line of centers, so the reflected stresslet has zero vorticity at sphere 1 on that axis. A naive vorticity estimate of order (∣V∣/a)ϵ5 therefore has zero coefficient. The finite-size correction to the incident strain and the next force-free scattering multipoles are two powers of ϵ smaller; these generically give
∣Ω1​∣=O(a∣V∣​ϵ7).​
(16)
This suppressed spin from a force-free distant sphere is also consistent with the reciprocal torque-to-self-translation coupling. Purely longitudinal forcing gives zero spin exactly by axial symmetry. Distinguishing the stresslet's on-axis curl from its generic off-axis scale is essential here.

Use y for height above the horizontal wall. In the lubrication approximation, vertical momentum is hydrostatic and the interfacial stress balance with variable surface tension gives
p=patm​+ρg(h−y)−γ(C)hxx​,B:=px​=[ρgh−γ(C)hxx​]x​.

The horizontal equation is μuyy​=B, with u(0)=0 and μuy​(h)=γx​=−ACx​. Thus

(19)
j=−(μAhC​+Ds​)Cx​−2μCh2​[ρgh−(γ0​−AC)hxx​]x​.​
(20)
These include Marangoni stress, hydrostatic leveling, capillarity and surface diffusion with their signs fixed by the surface traction. In particular the derivative acts on the product of surface tension and curvature, not just on curvature.

(22)

With capillarity and diffusion neglected, solve these two linear equations for the gradients, in the region H>0,Γ>0:
ΓX​=H26Q​−HΓ4J​,HHX​=HΓ6J​−H212Q​.​

For positive Q,J, the phase plane nullclines are H=3QΓ/(2J) for ΓX​=0 and H=2QΓ/J for HX​=0. Above both lines, trajectories go left and upward; between them they go left and downward; below both they go right and downward. There is no positive-quadrant equilibrium. The axes are singular boundaries of this positive-flux reduction, not regular equilibria.

Take z positive upward, and write b=ρ​g. For a straight steady viscous buoyant conduit, the inner and outer axial equations are
λμr1​(rwi′​)′=Pi′​−b,μr1​(rwo′​)′=Po′​.
(33)
Regularity gives wi′​(0)=0, the wall has wo​(R)=0, and velocity and tangential traction are continuous at r=a0​: wi​=wo​, λμwi′​=μwo′​. With no interfacial tension specified, normal traction is continuous; for this straight steady flow that sets Pi​=Po​ at the interface. Thus the modified gradients are common in this case.
When their value is zero, integration gives
wo​(r)=2μba02​​ln(R/r),wi​(r)=wo​(a0​)+4λμb​(a02​−r2).
(34)
Consequently
wi​(a0​)wi​(0)​=1+2λln(R/a0​)1​.​
(35)
The large center-to-interface speed ratio explains why the inner parabolic component dominates at small viscosity ratio.
For a slowly varying radius, set B=b−Pi,z​. The local inner solution has wi​=wa​+B(a2−r2)/(4λμ), so
q=πa2wa​+8λμπa4B​.
(36)
The shear-driven outer interface speed has scale a2Bln(R/a)/μ. The pressure-driven return flow needed for zero total flux adds a velocity of order q/R2 and a shear-driven contribution of order a2B/μ. Relative to the inner parabolic velocity these terms are O(λln(R/a)) and O((a/R)2), which are small under the stated assumptions. Axial viscous derivatives are also smaller because the plume is slender. Therefore
q=8λμπa4​(b−Pi,z​)to leading order.​
(37)
For clarity, the outer pressure-gradient estimate must include both return flux and transmitted interface shear. Their flux scales are R4Po,z​/μ, a2R2B/μ and q. Zero total flux hence implies
∣B∣∣Po,z​∣​=O((a/R)2+λ(a/R)4​)≪1.
(38)
This justifies the negligible outer pressure gradient. We subsequently choose its negligible pressure reference as zero, as in the reduced model.
Near the moving interface the outer radial source flow is ur​=aat​/r: the interface's axial advection is smaller because its axial velocity is small compared with the inner core speed. Its radial strain is ∂r​ur​=−aat​/r2. Continuity of normal traction at r=a, neglecting the much smaller inner normal viscous stress, gives
−Pi​=−Po​−2μat​/a,Pi​−Po​=2μat​/a.​
(39)
With A=a2/a02​, this is Pi​=μAt​/A after dropping Po​. Conservation of plume volume is π(a2)t​+qz​=0.
Let the axial and velocity scales be
ℓ=8λ​a0​​,U0​=8λμba02​​,Z=z/ℓ,T=tU0​/ℓ.​
(40)
Since μU0​/(bℓ2)=1, substitution gives the conduit equation
AT​+∂Z​{A2[1−∂Z​(AT​/A)]}=0.​
(41)
The buoyant flux and the viscous normal-stress pressure are both retained at this scaling.
Linearizing about unit area gives aT​+2aZ​−aTZZ​=0. Thus
ω=1+k22k​,ω/k=1+k22​>0.​
(42)
Wave crests propagate upward. The group velocity is 2(1−k2)/(1+k2)2: it is downward for ∣k∣>1, so the phase direction is not the direction of every wave packet.
For a travelling wave f(ζ), integrating once and using its uniform far field gives
−cf+f2+c(ff′′−f′2)=f02​−cf0​=:K.
(43)
Divide by f2, multiply by f′/f and integrate again. A convenient potential, defined up to an additive constant, is
2c​f2f′2​+V(f)=V(f0​),V(f)=lnf+fc​+2f2f02​−cf0​​.​
(44)

This is the solitary-wave amplitude-speed relation for the conduit equation. A nontrivial elevation wave has α>1 and c>2f0​; as α↓1, the speed tends to the long-wave speed 2f0​. Positive area is required throughout the derivation.

Let the actual dimensional thickness be d=ϵah, and let zˉ increase downward. The leading curvature of the outer free surface is
κs​=a1​−a2d​−dzˉzˉ​.
(46)
The Young–Laplace equation gives pzˉ​=−γ(dzˉ​/a2+dzˉzˉzˉ​). In the lubrication approximation, the downward velocity at distance y from the wall is (ρg−pzˉ​)(dy−y2/2)/μ, from no slip and zero free-surface shear. Integrating this profile gives
q=3μd3​[ρg+γ(dzˉ​/a2+dzˉzˉzˉ​)].
(47)
Circumferential corrections are higher order in d/a, so volume conservation is dtˉ​+qzˉ​=0 at leading order. Define
z=zˉ/a,t=μaγϵ3​tˉ,G=γϵρga2​,h=hˉ/a.​
(48)
Then the thin liquid film on a vertical cylinder satisfies
ht​+31​∂z​[h3(hzzz​+hz​+G)]=0.​
(49)
The circumferential-curvature term hz​ is retained along with axial curvature.
The perturbation calculation gives
s(k)=31​(k2−k4)−iGk.​
(50)
Long waves with 0<∣k∣<1 grow, while ∣k∣>1 decay; the fastest growth is 1/12 at ∣k∣=1/2​. Disturbances drift downward at speed G. The dimensional unstable wavelengths exceed 2πa, with most unstable wavelength 2π2​a. This is the thin-film version of the Rayleigh–Plateau instability driven by circumferential curvature and opposed by short-wave axial curvature.
For a travelling wave, one integration fixed by H→1 gives
H3(H′′′+H′+G)=3c(H−1)+G,​
(51)
or H′′′+H′=3c(H−1)/H3−G(1−H−3). In the large positive-speed core, substitute the expansion using the supplied assumptions. The left side at order c2/3 is H2′′′​+H2′​, while the gravity term is order one and 3c/H2 is only order c−1/3. Thus H2′′′​+H2′​=0. Its general solution is a constant plus a sine and cosine. The vanishing leading height and slope at both endpoints select
H2​(x)=K2​(1−cosx),K2​>0.​
(52)
The core length is 2π in the axial coordinate; its amplitude is fixed by transition matching.
At either edge set X=(3c)1/3(x−xe​), with xe​=0 at the trailing edge and xe​=2π at the leading edge. Keeping the height H of order one, the exact wave equation becomes
H3HXXX​=H−1−(3c)−2/3H3HX​−3cG​(H3−1).
(53)
Thus the transition equation is H3HXXX​=H−1. Its linearization about H=1 has exponents 1 and −1/2±i3​/2. Behind the pulse, approaching the uniform film as X→−∞ permits only the positive real exponent. The positive-growing, nontrivial branch has one amplitude that is removed by translation, giving the relevant unique H−​. In front, decay as X→+∞ permits the two real oscillatory amplitudes; translation removes one parameter and leaves a one-parameter family H+​. These are the unstable manifold and stable manifold dimensions for the transition system. The literal boundary condition also admits the constant solution and an opposite departing branch, so uniqueness here is specifically for the branch matching a raised positive pulse.
Match the supplied trailing quadratic asymptotic to the core near zero. It gives K2​=κ32/3, because 21​c2/3K2​x2=21​κ(3c)2/3x2. The leading maximum occurs at x=π, so
dmax​∼2κaϵ(3c)2/3.​

Λcap​=35/6c1/34πa​.​

H0​=A0​+B0​cosx+G(sinx−x).​
