An acoustic analogy is an exact rearrangement of the fluid equations into a chosen linear propagation operator acting on an acoustic variable, with everything left over placed on the right as effective forcing. The rearrangement becomes a sound-prediction method only after a reference medium, boundary conditions and approximations to the forcing are specified. In particular, a right-hand-side term need not represent independently generated sound.
Use Einstein summation convention and write ∂i​=∂/∂xi​. Differentiating the continuity equation in time and taking the divergence of the momentum equation eliminates ∂t​(ρui​):
ρtt​=∂i​∂j​(ρui​uj​−σij​)+∇2(p+χ).
(1)
Set q=p−p​0​ and Wij​=ρui​uj​−σij​. Since p+χ=q+P, the constant P has zero Laplacian. The prescribed reference mass density and reference speed of sound are independent of time, so
Qtt​=c02​qtt​​−ρtt​.
(2)
Combining the two identities gives
(c0−2​∂t2​−∇2)q=∂i​∂j​Wij​+∂t2​Q.​
(3)
Only conservation of mass and conservation of momentum have been used; no equation of state or energy equation was needed. Spatial variation of c0​ creates no omitted derivative in this identity, because it multiplies a time derivative. The double divergence of the momentum flux tensor has the structure of an acoustic quadrupole.
For a localized flow, choose the reference fields to match the stationary surrounding medium: ρ​0​=ρ0​ and c02​=c02​ outside the flow, with P chosen so p​0​=p0​ there. A uniform surrounding fluid permits ambient constant mass density and adiabatic sound speed, giving the familiar homogeneous wave equation. A nonuniform surrounding fluid calls for its actual stationary reference profiles, extended sensibly through the flow region. This makes the acoustic variable vanish in the unperturbed exterior and minimizes artificial contrast terms. In a uniform isentropic exterior the leading acoustic relation p′=c02​ρ′ also makes Q vanish to first order. In a stratified exterior, propagation and entropy-advection effects can remain in Q, as the next part demonstrates.

At rest, all time derivatives and advective terms vanish. The continuity equation and the stated energy equation are then identities, while the momentum equation reduces to ∇(p0​+χ)=0​. The reference state need not have uniform mass density or entropy. Assume smooth positive reference pressure and mass density, and retain only first-order perturbations.
The linearized continuity equation, momentum balance and adiabatic energy relation are
ρt′​+u′⋅∇ρ0​+ρ0​∇⋅u′=0,ρ0​ut′​=−∇p′,
(4)
pt′​+u′⋅∇p0​=c02​(ρt′​+u′⋅∇ρ0​)=−ρ0​c02​∇⋅u′.
(5)
The perturbation of c2 multiplies a vanishing reference material derivative, so it does not enter at first order. Differentiate the last equation in time, then substitute the linearized momentum equation:
ptt′​=ρ0​∇p0​​⋅∇p′+ρ0​c02​∇⋅(ρ0​∇p′​).
(6)
Expanding the divergence yields the stratified acoustic pressure equation
c02​ptt′​​−∇2p′=(c02​ρ0​∇p0​​−ρ0​∇ρ0​​)⋅∇p′.​
(7)
For a perfect gas, c02​ρ0​=γp0​. Put a(x)=p01/γ​/ρ0​. Then
∇loga=γp0​∇p0​​−ρ0​∇ρ0​​,a1​∇⋅(a∇p′)=∇2p′+∇loga⋅∇p′.
(8)
Thus the divergence-form stratified acoustic pressure equation is
c0−2​ptt′​−a−1∇⋅(a∇p′)=0.​
(9)
This is homogeneous linear propagation through the static medium, including its inhomogeneity. In the acoustic analogy of part (a), W has no first-order contribution when viscosity is neglected and the reference velocity is zero. Nevertheless the chosen left-hand operator lacks the gradient terms in the genuine propagation equation. Those effects must consequently appear in Qtt​. They describe propagation of an existing disturbance, rather than an independent source. Since reference-field choices also change the division between the operator and forcing, Qtt​ cannot be identified unambiguously with newly generated noise. Its presence in the right-hand side is a consequence of the chosen analogy, not a source-classification theorem.

Retain a=p01/γ​/ρ0​ from part (b), and use time translation invariance to write G(x,t;y,τ)=g(x,y;t−τ). Define the temporal Fourier transform by
G(x,y;ω)=∫R​g(x,y;s)e−iωsds.
(10)
Multiplying the transformed Green function equation by −a(x) gives
∇⋅(a∇G)+c02​aω2​G=−a(y)δ(x−y).
(11)
This is a symmetric divergence-form spatial operator, although the original unweighted operator need not be symmetric in the ordinary volume measure.
Let Gj​(x)=G(x,yj​;ω). The product rule gives the bilinear Green second identity
∇⋅{a(G1​∇G2​−G2​∇G1​)}=G1​∇⋅(a∇G2​)−G2​∇⋅(a∇G1​).
(12)
The frequency terms cancel. Integrating over the domain, the right side becomes
−a(y2​)G(y2​,y1​;ω)+a(y1​)G(y1​,y2​;ω).
(13)
The boundary integral is zero for common homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition, Neumann boundary condition or reciprocal Robin boundary condition conditions. In an unbounded domain, use the same outgoing limiting-absorption prescription for both Green functions; it gives the corresponding vanishing boundary pairing. This identity has no complex conjugation: it proves transpose wave reciprocity, not a Hermitian or time-reversal identity. We obtain the weighted acoustic Green-function reciprocity
G(y1​,y2​;ω)=a(y1​)a(y2​)​G(y2​,y1​;ω).
(14)
The factor is frequency independent, so inverse Fourier transform gives the same relation at equal time lag. Both time arguments below have lag t−τ2​, hence
G(y1​,t;y2​,τ2​)=G(y2​,t+τ1​−τ2​;y1​,τ1​)[p0​(y1​)]1/γρ0​(y2​)[p0​(y2​)]1/γρ0​(y1​)​.​
(15)
Reciprocity exchanges source and receiver while preserving elapsed time; it does not turn a causal response into an advanced one.

Suppress the common time factor eiωt, take ω>0, and fix the spatial Fourier transform convention
Φ(k,y)=∫−∞∞​ϕ(x,y)eikxdx,ϕ(x,y)=2π1​∫Γ​Φ(k,y)e−ikxdk.
(16)
A plus transform is supported on x>0 and analytic above Γ; a minus transform is supported on x<0 and analytic below it. For the outgoing radiation condition with this time convention, initially take k0​=ω/c0​−iε, ε>0, and pass to the limit at the end. Thus +k0​ lies below the contour and −k0​ above it.
Choose γ=(k2−k02​)1/2 to have positive real part on the real transform line. Its branch cuts run from +k0​ into the lower half-plane and from −k0​ into the upper half-plane, without crossing Γ; downward and upward vertical rays are suitable. In the zero-absorption limit, γ>0 for real ∣k∣>k0​ and γ=+ik02​−k2​ between the branch points. This ensures that e−γ∣y∣ represents decay or outgoing radiation, rather than an incoming exterior field.
Evenness in y and the Helmholtz equation give the transformed fields
Φ=A(k)cosh(γy)(∣y∣<b),Φ=B(k)e−γ(∣y∣−b)(∣y∣>b).
(17)
At y=b, the one-sided normal derivatives of the scattered field agree for x<0, because that part of the interface is open. For x>0 they both vanish by rigidity. Their common trace is therefore a minus function, denoted V−(k)=∂y​Φ−∣y=b​. Consequently
A=γsinh(γb)V−​,B=−γV−​.
(18)
The jump in the total scattered transform is
J=Φ(b+)−Φ(b−)=−γV−​{1+coth(γb)}=−LV−​,L=γsinh(γb)e−γb.
(19)
For x<0, continuity of the total mass density requires the scattered jump to cancel the incident jump, so ϕ(b+)−ϕ(b−)=eik0​x. Its minus transform is −i/(k+k0​). The unknown plate-side jump is the plus function J+=[Φ+]b−b+​. Thus J=J+−i/(k+k0​), and the Wiener-Hopf equation follows:
LV−​+J+=k+k0​i​.​
(20)
Use the Wiener-Hopf factorization L=L+L−, with factors analytic and nonzero in their designated half-planes. Their analytic continuations allocate outgoing modal zeros to L+ below the contour, and the opposite zeros to L− above it. Set C=L+(−k0​). Multiplication by L+ and pole subtraction give
L−V−​−k+k0​iC​=−L+J++k+k0​i(L+(k)−C)​=E(k).
(21)
The pole in the upper expression is removable by its numerator. The two expressions analytically continue to the common entire function, which is zero under the stipulated edge/growth assumption. Hence
V−=k+k0​iCL−​,J+=k+k0​i​(1−L+C​),
(22)
and the transformed fields are
Φ(k,y)=(k+k0​)γsinh(γb)iCL−(k)cosh(γy)​(∣y∣<b),​
(23)
Φ(k,y)=−(k+k0​)γiCL−(k)​e−γ(∣y∣−b)=−(k+k0​)L+(k)iCsinh(γb)​e−γ∣y∣(∣y∣>b).​
(24)
A constant reciprocal rescaling of the factors does not alter CL− or the physical field.
Inside the guide, cosh(γy) and γsinh(γb) are even entire functions of γ, while L− is analytic in the lower half-plane. Thus continuation across the lower branch cut changes none of the interior transform: that cut is removable. The exterior expression retains the cut, corresponding to radiation into the open exterior.
For x>0, the inverse-transform contour closes downwards, clockwise. Away from modal cutoffs, its enclosed singularities are the outgoing simple poles
kn​=k02​−(nπ/b)2​,n=0,1,…,
(25)
with positive real part for propagating modes and negative imaginary part for decaying modes; k0​ is the n=0 root. The opposite roots lie above the contour or cancel against zeros of L−. Symmetry excludes odd transverse modes. At n≥1, γ=inπ/b, and differentiation of D(k)=γsinh(γb) gives
D′(kn​)=bkn​(−1)n,D′(k0​)=2bk0​.
(26)
Each inverse-transform contribution is −i times its residue. Therefore the outgoing modes of an open rigid acoustic waveguide are
ϕ(x,y)=A0​e−ik0​x+n=1∑∞​An​cos(bnπy​)e−ikn​x,​
(27)
A0​=4bk02​L+(−k0​)L−(k0​)​,An​=bkn​(kn​+k0​)(−1)nL+(−k0​)L−(kn​)​(n≥1).​
(28)
The original time factor multiplies this expression. Thus every cut-on mode propagates in the positive x direction; cut-off modes are evanescent waves decaying into the guide, not additional backward waves. Strictly, a complete mode sum includes these evanescent modes as well as propagating ones. The displayed simple-pole amplitudes apply away from exact cutoffs; cutoff values use the outgoing limiting-absorption continuation before taking the limit. The reflected plane-wave amplitude relative to the unit incident wave is A0​ above. It has the expected negative sign in the long-wavelength leading kernel approximation L≃b(k2−k02​).

Write the negative-flux Inviscid Burgers equation as fZ​+∂θ​F(f)=0, with F(f)=−f2/2. Along a characteristic curve,
dZdθ​=−f,dZdf​=0,
(29)
so a characteristic starting at θ0​ has θ=θ0​−f0​(θ0​)Z and
f(Z,θ0​−f0​(θ0​)Z)=f0​(θ0​)​.
(30)
This formula is a classical solution only while the method of characteristics is one-to-one; after characteristic crossing one must select an entropy solution.
For a discontinuity with left and right values f−​ and f+​, the Rankine-Hugoniot condition follows by integrating conservation across a moving small interval:
θs′​(f+​−f−​)=F(f+​)−F(f−​).
(31)
For a nonzero jump, factor the difference of squares to obtain
θs′​=−21​(f+​+f−​).​
(32)
The negative flux makes increasing jumps compressive. Decreasing jumps spread into rarefaction waves; they cannot be retained as nonphysical expansion shock waves.
In the central ramp of each period, f0​(θ0​)=θ0​−2m, so its method of characteristics is θ−2m=(1−Z)(θ0​−2m). For 0<Z<1, the retained ramp therefore occupies ∣θ−2m∣<1−Z. At the odd boundary r=2m+1, the initial limiting values are +1 on the left and −1 on the right. Their characteristic speeds are −1 and +1, giving the fan f=−(θ−r)/Z on ∣θ−r∣<Z. Together these give the periodic backward-sawtooth Burgers solution
f(Z,θ)={(θ−2m)/(1−Z),−(θ−(2m+1))/Z,​∣θ−2m∣<1−Z,∣θ−(2m+1)∣<Z,​0<Z<1,m∈Z.​
(33)
These intervals tile the real line up to their matching endpoints, where both formulas agree at ±1. The increasing ramp steepens, but the wave's maximum magnitude remains one before breaking. A steep continuous regularization of the original downward jump produces exactly the limiting fan, as suggested by the characteristic construction.
At Z=1, each increasing ramp collapses at θ=2m. The fans on either side meet there with values −1 and +1, producing a compressive stationary shock wave. For all Z≥1 the fan between successive shock waves remains centered at the odd point, giving
f(Z,θ)=Z2m+1−θ​(2m<θ<2m+2),θs​=2m.​
(34)
At a shock wave the limiting values are f−​=−1/Z and f+​=1/Z. Their average is zero, so the Rankine-Hugoniot condition keeps the shock wave fixed. Their characteristic speeds satisfy 1/Z>0>−1/Z, confirming compression into the shock wave. The arbitrary pointwise value at a shock wave does not affect the weak solution. The post-breaking amplitude decays as 1/Z, despite the lack of explicit viscosity, because shock wave dissipate the wave.

Let h=logψ, with ψ>0, and set f=2αhθ​. Direct differentiation gives
fZ​−ffθ​−αfθθ​=2α∂θ​{hZ​−αhθθ​−αhθ2​}.
(35)
The heat equation ψZ​=αψθθ​ implies hZ​=α(hθθ​+hθ2​), so the bracket vanishes. Conversely its being independent of θ can be absorbed into a Z-dependent multiplicative normalization of ψ, leaving f unchanged. This proves the Cole-Hopf transformation with the positive sign appropriate to the negative-flux Burgers convention. Although the algebra works for nonzero α, the given forward Gaussian function diffusion kernel and a physical vanishing-viscosity limit require α>0.
Take U,L>0 for the stated Burgers N-wave. Integrating ∂θ​logψ(0,θ)=f0​(θ)/(2α) and normalizing the exterior value to one gives
ψ0​(θ)={exp{U(L2−θ2)/(4α)},1,​∣θ∣<L,∣θ∣≥L.​
(36)
This function is continuous at ±L; its logarithmic derivative has the specified jumps. Convolution with the heat kernel is positive and solves the heat equation for Z>0. Splitting the integral into the exterior baseline and the interior correction yields
ψ=1−Iα​(θ,L,Z)+4παZ​eUL2/(4α)​∫−LL​exp{−4αUφ2​−4αZ(φ−θ)2​}dφ.
(37)
Put a=1+UZ. Completing the square gives
Uφ2+Z(φ−θ)2​=Za​(φ−aθ​)2+aUθ2​.
(38)
Changing the interior integration variable to η=aφ changes its limits to ±La and the Gaussian function width to Za. The Jacobian and normalization leave the factor a−1/2. Thus the Cole-Hopf solution for a Burgers N-wave is
ψ=1−Iα​(θ,L,Z)+Iα​(θ,La,Za)ψ​,ψ​=a−1/2exp{4αU​(L2−aθ2​)},f=2α∂θ​logψ.​
(39)
Here Iα​ is the normalized Gaussian function mass of its indicated interval. Its explicit error function representation is
Iα​(θ,b,w)=21​{erf(4αw​b−θ​)+erf(4αw​b+θ​)}.
(40)
For fixed w>0, the Gaussian function concentrates at φ=θ as α↓0. Consequently
Iα​(θ,L,w)⟶H(L2−θ2)​
(41)
away from the endpoints; at θ=±L the limit is 1/2. The transition layer has width O(αw​). This is an approximate identity argument, not a uniform step approximation across the endpoints.
For fixed Z>0 and ∣θ∣≪L, both interval masses tend to one, and the exponentially large positive ψ​ dominates the exterior correction. Its logarithmic derivative therefore gives
f(Z,θ)≃−1+UZUθ​(∣θ∣≪L, α↓0).​
(42)
For ∣θ∣≫La, both interval masses are exponentially small and the weighted interior integral is also negligible, while the exterior contribution tends to one. Therefore f(Z,θ)≃0​ in the specified far exterior.
An exponentially weighted Gaussian function tail should not be discarded solely because its unweighted interval mass tends to zero. In fact, comparing the order-one exterior term with ψ​ gives the sharper inviscid Burgers N-wave fronts ∣θ∣=L1+UZ​, not La. Away from these fronts, the vanishing-viscosity limit is
f(Z,θ)⟶{−Uθ/(1+UZ),0,​∣θ∣<L1+UZ​,∣θ∣>L1+UZ​.​
(43)
Inside ∣θ∣<La, this follows from the sign of L2−θ2/a in the exponential. Outside La, the constrained Gaussian function maximum lies at an interval endpoint and has negative exponent. The right shock wave's speed is UL/(2a​), equal both to the derivative of La​ and to minus half the sum of its two limiting states; the left shock wave is its reflection. This checks consistency with the Rankine-Hugoniot condition and shows that the requested near-center and far-exterior approximations are compatible with the full entropy limit.
