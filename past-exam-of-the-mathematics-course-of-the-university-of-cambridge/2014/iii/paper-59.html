Synchronous radius and its limits. Kepler's third law for a circumplanetary orbit gives
as​=(4π2GMP2​)1/3.
(1)
Here G is the gravitational constant. A planet-synchronous orbit repeats after one spin period. A planet-stationary orbit additionally requires a circular orbit, zero orbital inclination to the equator and prograde motion. Then an antenna fixed on the ground points continuously at the same satellite; merely matching the orbital period does not give this property.
Write the planetary radius as Rp​=(3M/(4πρ))1/3. Requiring the semi-major axis to exceed Rp​ gives P2>3π/(Gρ). The stellar tidal force restricts the circumplanetary orbit to the Hill sphere, of radius rH​=a(M/(3M⋆​))1/3. Thus
Gρ3π​​<P<3GM⋆​4π2a3​​.​
(2)
These are the surface and Hill-radius constraints in the idealized spherical, small-M/M⋆​ model. Long-term prograde stability generally requires a radius appreciably inside the Hill sphere; for nonzero orbital eccentricity, the surface constraint applies to the periapsis, not just to the semi-major axis.
Collision time and the launch population. A phase-mixed isotropic swarm occupies a shell of radial scale eas​. Its number density scales as Ns​/(eas3​), its relative speed as as​/P, and its geometric collision cross-section as r2. Consequently the total collision rate scales as Ns2​r2/(Peas2​). To give the numerical normalization used here, adopt an effective shell volume V=4πeas3​. At a fixed position the velocity directions are uniformly distributed in the tangent plane, so their mean relative speed is 4vK​/π=8as​/P. For an unordered pair, the collision cross-section is 4πr2, giving
Kss​=4πeas3​4πr2(8as​/P)​=Pe8​(as​r​)2,Γss​=21​Kss​Ns​(Ns​−1)≃ANs2​​,A=4Pe​(ras​​)2.​
(3)
Thus the mean interval between collisions is A/Ns2​ in this large-population kinetic model. The effective shell width fixes an order-one coefficient: small orbital eccentricity and random planes alone do not specify a unique radial probability density. Phase mixing, negligible gravitational focusing, r≪eas​, and uncorrelated encounters are implicit in this estimate. Exactly identical orbital periods with perfectly fixed phases do not themselves produce a memoryless collision process.
For nearly planet-stationary orbits with e≪I≪1, the swarm volume is smaller by a factor of order I, while the relative speed is smaller by the same factor, since vertical motion of scale IvK​ dominates the eccentric motion. The two changes cancel in the rate nσv: there is no parametric factor I in the collision time within the same phase-mixed kinetic approximation. Numerical factors and phase correlations can differ. This is not an argument that bringing all satellites into one nearly circular plane makes their phases random.
With Ns​=Rt, an Inhomogeneous Poisson process has cumulative hazard function
H(t)=∫0t​AR2u2​du=3AR2t3​=3ARNs3​​.

This is a characteristic first-event population, with probability 1−e−1 of an earlier event. It is not a median: the median has an additional factor (log2)1/3.
Which population collides next? Immediately after the first disruption let S=Ns0​−2 and F=2x−3. The latter follows from mass conservation for equal-density spherical pieces. Using the same unordered-pair counting and geometric collision cross-sections as above, define b=(1+x)2/2; then
Γss​=AS2​,Γsf​=AbSF​,Γff​=Ax2F2​.

Here S2,F2 stand for the usual large-population approximations to S(S−1),F(F−1). The factor two distinguishing identical and different species is essential. The probability that the next collision is fragment–fragment is
pff​=4x−4+(1+x)2Sx−3+S24x−4​.​
(7)
It is the most likely type if S<4/[x(1+x)2] and S<2/x2. It has probability greater than one half if

(8)
For x≪1, a strongly fragment-dominated next event therefore requires Sx≪4; the fragment–satellite comparison is more restrictive than the satellite–satellite comparison. If S=0, the next event must be fragment–fragment, provided fragments remain.
Population equations and the normalization discrepancy. Every satellite–satellite event produces 2x−3 fragments and destroys two satellites. Every satellite–fragment event produces a net x−3−1 fragments and destroys one satellite; every fragment–fragment event destroys two fragments. Consistent collision counting therefore gives
AN˙f​AN˙s​​=2x−3Ns2​+b(x−3−1)Nf​Ns​−2x2Nf2​,=−2Ns2​−bNf​Ns​.​​
(9)
For x≪1, b≃1/2 and x−3−1≃x−3. The last two terms in AN˙f​ are then twice those in the printed equation. This is a genuine factor-of-two inconsistency: equal-size fragment pairs have a collision cross-section smaller by x2, so their event rate must be x2Nf2​/A if the satellite event rate is Ns2​/A; destroying both fragments necessarily gives the sink −2x2Nf2​/A.
If the printed equation is taken as a prescribed approximate rate model instead, its implicit event rates are Ns2​/A, Nf​Ns​/(4A) and x2Nf2​/(2A). Under precisely that mixed normalization, its corresponding satellite equation is
N˙s​=−A−1(2Ns2​+41​Nf​Ns​).​
(10)
The printed source term also neglects the consumed fragment in a satellite–fragment event, a legitimate relative O(x3) approximation. That approximation does not repair the pair-counting discrepancy.
The ensuing cascade. This is a two-size fragmentation cascade. First use the printed approximate model, dropping satellite–satellite events as requested. Put S=Ns​, F=Nf​, k=4x2 and take x<1/2. Then
S˙=−4AFS​,F˙=AF​(4x3S​−x2F),dSdF​−SkF​=−x−3.

(13)
Afterwards satellites are depleted and fragment–fragment losses dominate. For S0​>0 the continuum solution has S→0, F→0, with
F(t)∼x2tA​,S(t)∝t−1/(4x2).​
(14)
When no satellites remain initially, F(t)=F0​/[1+x2F0​t/A] directly. Small integer populations eventually invalidate these deterministic differential equations.
The consistently counted model has, to leading order in x, exactly the same curve F(S) and peak, but both retained time derivatives are twice as large. Its growth time is 2Ax3/S0​, and F(t)∼A/(2x2t). Keeping (1+x)2 and the consumed fragment replaces k by 4x2/(1+x)2 and x−3 in the curve by x−3−1. This explicitly separates the physical collision bookkeeping from the printed normalization while giving the evolution under both conventions.

Specific orbital energy, orbital eccentricity and the distribution. Let μ=GM⋆​, vK​=μ/a​ and c=cosθ. Choose positive sinθ to mean an outward radial kick. At release the tangential and radial velocities are vt​=vK​(1+γc) and vr​=γvK​sinθ. The specific orbital energy and specific angular momentum are
ε′=2aμ​(−1+2γc+γ2),h′=avK​(1+γc).

The denominator must be positive for an elliptic Kepler orbit. A vanishing denominator describes a parabolic Kepler orbit; negative a′ is the signed semi-major axis of a hyperbolic Kepler orbit.

For example γ=2, c=−1 reverses the circular velocity and gives a retrograde circular orbit with e′=0; the displayed stationary value is then not the global minimum.

The 1:1 encounter window. Exact equality of orbital periods requires a′=a, hence c0​=−γ/2. There are two kick directions with this cosine when 0<γ<2. To quantify a finite window one must specify a return time: near exact commensurability, consider the particle's first complete return to its release point. During that time the source advances through 2π(a′/a)3/2. Its longitudinal miss distance is, to first order,
d≃2πa​(aa′​)3/2−1​≃3πa​aa′​−1​.
(22)
Thus d<Δ gives the two-sided cosine window
∣c−c0​∣<6πaγΔ​.
(23)
Integrating fc​ over that window gives, to leading order for a narrow window away from γ=2,
freturn​≃3π2aγ1−γ2/4​Δ​.​
(24)
The printed fraction is half this value. It is obtained by retaining only one side of the semi-major axis window, or only one of the two kick-angle branches. Neither restriction is in the question. Thus the printed coefficient cannot be shown for the stated uniform 0≤θ<2π population and a symmetric first-return distance criterion. This already exhibits the discrepancy under the usual longitudinal approximation; minimizing the distance over the encounter interval introduces a further velocity-direction correction, not a missing branch. With arbitrarily long observation times, noncommensurate bound trajectories can also return arbitrarily close to the common release point, so an eventual-encounter fraction is a different, time-dependent question.
Rotating-frame sketches. In the rotating reference frame of the source put x radially outward, y along the source's motion and s=nt, where n=vK​/a. The linear Hill equations for a stellar Kepler orbit, with initial displacement zero, give the velocity-kick epicycle
γax​γay​​=2cosθ(1−coss)+sinθsins,=cosθ(4sins−3s)+2sinθ(coss−1).​​
(25)
These solve x¨−2ny˙​−3n2x=0, y¨​+2nx˙=0 and reproduce the initial kick. For θ=0, the particle starts forward, moves outward and drifts backward; the curve has a loop each orbital cycle, with net Δy=−6πγa per cycle. For θ=π, both coordinates reverse: it moves inward and drifts forward by 6πγa. For θ=π/2, the leading trajectory is a closed epicyclic ellipse,
(γax​)2+(2γay+2γa​)2=1.
(26)
It starts at the top of the ellipse moving radially outward and returns to the source after one period to this order. Its exact semi-major axis differs from a at order γ2, so exact closure is not implied. The local sketches require ∣x∣,∣y∣≪a, in particular γs≪1 for the drifting cases.

Validity and the complete angular equation. The Lagrange planetary equations here describe a planar, circular-planet, small-orbital eccentricity, weakly perturbed exterior mean-motion resonance. Require Mpl​/M⋆​≪1, e≪1, one isolated slowly varying resonant argument, perturbation times long compared with an orbital period, and no close planetary encounters. Other resonant harmonics and short-period terms must be negligible; coefficients may be frozen only across a narrow range of semi-major axis. Take p>0, q=1 or 2 in a reduced integer ratio. Exactly e=0 makes the longitude of periapsis undefined, so the displayed angular variables must then be replaced by nonsingular eccentricity vector components.
Neglecting the perturbation to the accumulated mean longitude gives λ˙≃n(a); this is an accumulated-phase approximation, not differentiation of a fictitious expression n(t)t while discarding tn˙. With n2a3=GM⋆​,
ϕ˙​=(p+q)n−pnpl​−2qCs​−q2Cr​eq−2cosϕ.
(27)
Differentiating, using n˙=−3na˙/(2a) and the supplied orbital element equations, gives the full result within this constant-coefficient model:
ϕ¨​=[3(p+q)2nCr​eq+q3(q−2)Cr2​e2q−4cosϕ+q2Cr​eq−2ϕ˙​]sinϕ.​

An equivalent formula replaces the last two terms in brackets by q2Cr​eq−2[(p+q)n−pnpl​−2qCs​]−2q3Cr2​e2q−4cosϕ. No derivatives of Cr​,Cs​ are included because they were specified as constants.
A full fixed point requires sinϕ=0 and ϕ˙​=0. Hence ϕ∗​=0 or π, with its corresponding detuned semi-major axis fixed by

At either point a˙=e˙=0. The longitude of periapsis still precesses, so the fixed point refers to the reduced resonant dynamics.
Linear stability analysis and encounter geometry. Linearizing the full equation about a fixed point gives

For q=2, π is a center equilibrium and zero is a saddle equilibrium. For q=1, π is always a center equilibrium, while zero is a saddle equilibrium if
3(p+1)2n∗​e∗3​>Cr​.
(31)
At sufficiently small orbital eccentricity the full truncated equations instead admit a center at zero as well; equality is a degenerate case requiring higher-order analysis. Thus an unconditional instability claim at zero does not follow from the full equation. In the usual fixed-eccentricity weak-resonance regime the inequality holds and the stable libration center is π for either order.
The physical explanation is resonance protection. At equal mean longitudes, the conjunction direction relative to periapsis obeys q(Λ−ϖ)=ϕ modulo 2π. For q=1, ϕ=0 places conjunction near periapsis, where the exterior particle comes closest to the planet; ϕ=π places it near apoapsis. For q=2, zero includes both apsidal conjunctions, one of them near periapsis, whereas π places the two conjunction branches near quadrature. The protected arrangements give restoring kicks in the positive-Cr​ leading-harmonic model. This geometric explanation is conditional on its non-crossing, small-orbital eccentricity approximation; it does not override the low-e term retained in the full stability calculation.
Small resonant-argument librations. Define the nominal resonant semi-major axis and the displaced center by

Eigenfrequencies and mode geometry. Write d=A11​−A22​ and D=d2+4A12​A21​​>0. The characteristic polynomial is λ2−(A11​+A22​)λ+A11​A22​−A12​A21​. Therefore the two distinct real eigenvalues are
λp​=2A11​+A22​+D​,λm​=2A11​+A22​−D​.​
(40)
The sign assumptions imply λp​>max(A11​,A22​) and λm​<min(A11​,A22​); they do not alone imply λm​>0. A positive lower frequency additionally requires detA>0, as in the usual nondegenerate physical Laplace-Lagrange secular matrix.
Distinct eigenvalues give an eigenbasis, and each secular eigenmode evolves by multiplication by eiλt. Hence
zj​(t)=ejp​ei(ϕjp​+λp​t)+ejm​ei(ϕjm​+λm​t),
(41)
with nonnegative amplitudes and initial phases. The second row of each eigenvalue equation gives z1​/z2​=(λ−A22​)/A21​. For the upper eigenvalue this ratio is negative, whereas for the lower one it is positive. Thus
rp​=e2p​e1p​​rm​=e2m​e1m​​​=A21​A22​−λp​​,=A21​λm​−A22​​,​ϕ1p​ϕ1m​​=ϕ2p​+π,=ϕ2m​.​​
(42)
The upper secular eigenmode has anti-aligned apsides, and the lower has aligned apsides. If one amplitude vanishes, its phase is immaterial.
The initial vector sums. The planet-1 modal vectors point at 30∘ and 120∘. Their resultant at 60∘ requires e1p​=3​e1m​. The planet-2 vectors point at 210∘ and 120∘; their resultant at 150∘ requires e2m​=3​e2p​. These follow by resolving components perpendicular to each resultant. In particular the specified phases constrain the matrix: rp​/rm​=3. They cannot be imposed on an arbitrary matrix satisfying only the sign assumptions.

Each modal vector rotates counterclockwise if its eigenvalue is positive; their sum executes a beat at frequency D=λp​−λm​. For these phases,

The orbital eccentricities exchange their maxima and minima, with respective ranges Bs​(3​−1) to Bs​(3​+1) and B(3​−1) to B(3​+1). The longitude of periapsis is the argument of each displayed vector sum; it does not in general advance uniformly at either individual eigenfrequency. The complex eccentricity trajectories are generally quasiperiodic; they close only for commensurate modal frequencies. This example has a dominating upper mode in planet 1 and a dominating lower mode in planet 2, so their apsidal phases have different winding rates.

Tidal dissipation and damping. Assume the tidal dissipation contributes only the specified linear eccentricity damping, without changing the conservative matrix or adding apsidal precession. Then

Both gp​,gm​ are positive because D>∣d∣, so both secular eigenmodes decay as e−gp,m​t, although only planet 1 feels direct eccentricity damping. The coupling transmits that damping to planet 2. Their precession frequencies change only at second order; their eigenvectors acquire small complex corrections, so exact alignment or anti-alignment becomes a small apsidal phase lag. The more slowly damped mode eventually dominates, unless its initial coefficient vanishes: it is the lower aligned mode for d>0 and the upper anti-aligned mode for d<0. At d=0 both leading damping rates equal 1/(2τ).
The stated slow-secular condition presumes a nonzero positive λm​. It need not imply Dτ≫1 near a nearly degenerate pair, so the exact square-root formula is the appropriate answer there.
