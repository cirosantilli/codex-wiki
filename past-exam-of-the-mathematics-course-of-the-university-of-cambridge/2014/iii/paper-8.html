A subspace is topologically complete if its subspace topology is induced by some complete metric. The compatible complete metric need not be the restriction of the displayed ambient metric. For example, an open interval is topologically complete although its usual metric is incomplete.
Suppose ρ is a compatible complete metric on Y. For each n≥1 and y∈Y, choose an ambient open set Vn,y​ containing y, contained in Bd​(y,1/n), and with ρ-diameter of Vn,y​∩Y at most 1/n. Such sets exist because the two topologies on Y agree. Put Un​=⋃y∈Y​Vn,y​. Clearly Y⊆⋂n​Un​.
If x∈⋂n​Un​, choose yn​∈Y with x∈Vn,yn​​. Then d(x,yn​)<1/n, so yn​→x in the ambient metric. For fixed m, the point x belongs to some open Vm,z​, and eventually all yn​ belong to this set. Their pairwise ρ-distances are then at most 1/m. Hence (yn​) is ρ-Cauchy, and completeness gives a limit y∈Y. Compatibility gives d(yn​,y)→0, forcing x=y. Thus
Y=n≥1⋂​Un​.​
(1)
This proves the G-delta criterion for topological completeness in the required direction.
The converse is true in a complete metric ambient space. If Y=⋂n​Un​ with each Un​ open in complete X, define an​(y)=1/d(y,X∖Un​), omitting any term whose complement is empty. A compatible complete metric on Y is
D(y,z)=d(y,z)+n≥1∑​2−nmin{1,∣an​(y)−an​(z)∣}.​
(2)
Continuity of each an​ and the uniformly small series tail show that D has the original topology. A D-Cauchy sequence is d-Cauchy, hence converges to some x∈X. For each n, its real coordinates an​ form a Cauchy sequence and remain bounded. Since distance to a closed set is continuous, d(x,X∖Un​)>0, so x∈Un​ for every n. The same coordinate convergence and series-tail argument give convergence in D. Thus D is complete.
For the normed-space claim, embed E densely in its norm completion E. Topological completeness and the preceding criterion make E a dense G-delta set in E, hence comeagre. Every translate x+E is also comeagre. The Baire category theorem in the complete space E implies that E∩(x+E) is nonempty. If z=x+e lies in that intersection, then x=z−e∈E. This holds for every x∈E, so
E=E and E is a Banach space.​
(3)
This is the comeagre subgroup completeness argument.
Finally let B={ℓ∈E∗:∥ℓ∥<1} with its weak-star topology. Put rn​=1−1/(n+1) and Fn​={ℓ∈B:∥ℓ∥≤rn​}. The dual norm is the supremum of the continuous evaluation moduli on the unit ball of E, so every Fn​ is relatively closed. Also B=⋃n​Fn​.
Each Fn​ has empty relative interior. Given ℓ∈Fn​ and a basic weak-star neighborhood restricting finitely many evaluations on x1​,…,xk​, the Hahn-Banach theorem gives a nonzero functional h annihilating their finite-dimensional span, because E is infinite-dimensional. All ℓ+th retain those evaluations. Their norm varies continuously with t and becomes unbounded; choose t so that rn​<∥ℓ+th∥<1. This point lies in the neighborhood inside B but outside Fn​. Thus B is a nonempty countable union of relatively closed nowhere dense sets and is not a Baire space. Consequently
B with its weak-star topology is not topologically complete.​
(4)
This weak-star open dual ball category obstruction works without assuming that E is separable or that the weak-star ball is metrizable.

The weak topology of probability measures is the smallest topology making every map μ↦∫hdμ continuous for bounded continuous real h on X. Define the bounded-Lipschitz metric by
β(μ,ν)=∥h∥∞​+Lipd​(h)≤1sup​​∫hdμ−∫hdν​.​
(5)
Using the maximum of the two bounds instead of their sum changes the metric by at most a factor of two and gives the same topology. Symmetry and the triangle inequality follow from the supremum formula. Bounded Lipschitz functions distinguish probability measures by the open-set approximations below, so β is a metric. On a separable metric space, β induces the weak topology.
Here are the essential details. If β(μn​,μ)→0, integrals of every bounded Lipschitz function converge after rescaling its Lipschitz bound. For an open set A=X, the functions
hm​(x)=min{1,md(x,X∖A)}
(6)
increase to 1A​; for A=X take hm​=1. Thus μ(A)=supm​∫hm​dμ. It follows both that μ↦μ(A) is lower semicontinuous for the weak topology, as a supremum of continuous maps, and that beta convergence implies
nliminf​μn​(A)≥μ(A)for every open A.​
(7)
This is the open-set Portmanteau criterion. Its closed-set equivalent, by complements, is
nlimsup​μn​(F)≤μ(F)for every closed F.​
(8)
Either family of inequalities is necessary and sufficient for weak convergence of probability measures. For sufficiency of the open inequalities, apply the layer-cake formula and Fatou's lemma to the open superlevel sets of a continuous function 0≤h≤1, obtaining liminf∫hdμn​≥∫hdμ. Applying the same argument to 1−h gives the reverse bound. Scaling handles every bounded continuous function.
Conversely, weak convergence implies beta convergence. Choose a compact set K of arbitrarily large μ-mass, using tightness of a probability measure on a Polish space. The unit bounded-Lipschitz class is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous on K, so finitely many of its members approximate all others uniformly there. On the open δ-neighborhood Kδ, approximation errors increase by at most 2δ. The open-set inequality makes μn​(Kδ) large for all sufficiently large n. Weak convergence for the finitely many selected functions, combined with these approximation errors and the small mass outside Kδ, bounds the supremum defining β by an arbitrarily small number. The same finite-test and open-mass bounds define weak neighborhoods, so the argument gives equality of the topologies, not only their convergent sequences. It does not first assume uniform tightness of the entire sequence.
The map j=i∗​ is injective and continuous: for h∈C(X), h∘i is bounded continuous on X, and ∫hdj(μ)=∫h∘idμ. To prove inverse continuity on its image, suppose j(μn​)→j(μ). For every open A⊂X, there is an open U⊂X with i(A)=U∩i(X). Hence μn​(A)=j(μn​)(U) and μ(A)=j(μ)(U). The open-set criterion on X now gives the criterion on X, so μn​→μ. Both spaces are metrizable, and this sequential argument proves
j:P(X)⟶j(P(X)) is a homeomorphism.​
(9)
This is the probability pushforward embedding theorem. The printed claim needs the words “onto its image”: it is generally not onto all P(X), since a Dirac mass at a point outside i(X) is not in its image. By Question 1, the completely metrizable subspace i(X) is a Borel G-delta set, and
j(P(X))={λ∈P(X):λ(i(X))=1}.
(10)
Now let S be weakly closed and uniformly tight. Take any sequence (μn​) in S. The space P(X) is compact for the weak topology. For completeness, this compactness follows by choosing a countable uniformly dense family in C(X), extracting a diagonal subsequence of its bounded integrals, and extending the limits to a positive normalized functional on C(X); the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem gives the limiting probability measure. Thus, along a subsequence, j(μn​)→λ.
For every m, choose compact Km​⊂X with μ(Km​)≥1−2−m for every μ∈S. The set i(Km​) is compact and closed in X, so the closed-set criterion gives
λ(i(Km​))≥limsupn​j(μn​)(i(Km​))≥1−2−m.
(11)
Therefore λ(i(X))=1, and λ=j(μ) for some μ∈P(X). Inverse continuity gives μn​→μ, and closedness puts μ∈S. Hence S is sequentially compact, and metrizability makes it compact. This proves the required closed uniformly tight compactness criterion, rather than assuming it from Prokhorov's theorem.
Such a compact metrizable extension is always available: for a countable dense set (xk​), the coordinates d(x,xk​)/(1+d(x,xk​)) embed X homeomorphically into [0,1]N. Injectivity and inverse continuity follow by choosing xk​ close to a specified point and using the triangle inequality. The closure of the image gives X.
To show separability of P(X), use finite sums of Dirac measures on a countable dense subset of X with nonnegative rational weights summing to one. This is a countable family. Given μ, cover a large-mass compact set by finitely many small balls centered in that subset, move the mass in each piece to its center, and move the remaining small mass to one fixed center. The bounded-Lipschitz error is at most the ball radius plus twice the exceptional mass. Approximate the resulting weights by rational weights. Thus these atomic measures are beta-dense.
Finally use a complete compatible metric d on the Polish space X, as required for the granted total-boundedness-to-tightness assertion. A beta-Cauchy sequence is beta-totally bounded, and so is its closure in P(X). By the allowed assertion this closure is uniformly tight; it is weakly closed because the weak and beta topologies agree. The compactness result just proved gives a convergent subsequence, and the Cauchy property forces the whole sequence to converge in beta. Consequently beta is complete for this choice of d, and
(P(X),w) is a Polish space.​
(12)
If the originally displayed compatible metric is incomplete, replace it by a complete compatible metric for this last argument; the weak topology itself is unchanged.

A transport plan is a Borel probability measure on X×Y with marginals μ,ν. It is c-cyclically monotone when it is concentrated on a set Γ such that every finite list (xi​,yi​)∈Γ satisfies
∑i=1m​c(xi​,yi​)≤∑i=1m​c(xi+1​,yi​),xm+1​=x1​.
(13)
Equivalently, one can allow every permutation of the destinations, since a permutation decomposes into cycles. The word c-monotone here means this cyclic condition, not merely a two-point test for an arbitrary cost.
The potential-certificate meaning of the printed “strictly c-monotone” is strong c-monotonicity: there are Borel functions u:X→R∪{−∞} and v:Y→R∪{−∞} such that
u(x)+v(y)≤c(x,y) everywhere,u(x)+v(y)=c(x,y)π-almost everywhere.​
(14)
The functions are finite on full marginal-measure sets. This is a certificate by Kantorovich potentials, not literal strict inequality in every nonidentity cycle. Such a literal interpretation could not satisfy the requested implication, already for c=0.
Here is a direct transport potential path construction. Since c is finite and continuous, the closure of a cyclically monotone set remains cyclically monotone. We may therefore use the closed support Γ of π and choose a countable dense subset Γ0​, containing an anchor (x0​,y0​). For a chain (xj​,yj​)∈Γ0​, j=0,…,m, starting at that anchor, define
Lm​(z)=∑j=0m−1​[c(xj+1​,yj​)−c(xj​,yj​)]+c(z,ym​)−c(xm​,ym​),u(z)=infm,chains​Lm​(z).
(15)
The infimum is over countably many continuous functions, so u is upper semicontinuous and Borel, and never +∞ because the zero-length chain is available. Cyclical monotonicity applied to a chain closing at the anchor gives u(x0​)=0. If (x,y)∈Γ, closing a chain through this extra pair gives
Lm​(x)≥c(x,y)−c(x0​,y),
(16)
so u(x) is finite on the first projection of Γ.
Append the pair (x,y) to a nearly minimizing chain ending at x. If the pair is outside Γ0​, approximate it by pairs in Γ0​ and use continuity of the finitely many costs. This gives, for every z,
u(z)≤u(x)+c(z,y)−c(x,y)((x,y)∈Γ).
(17)
Now put
v(y)=infz:u(z)>−∞​[c(z,y)−u(z)].
(18)
It is again an infimum of continuous functions of y, and therefore upper semicontinuous and Borel. The anchor bounds it above by c(x0​,y). Feasibility u(z)+v(y)≤c(z,y) is immediate. For (x,y)∈Γ, the preceding chain inequality gives v(y)≥c(x,y)−u(x), while testing z=x gives the opposite inequality. Hence equality holds on Γ, and v is finite on its second projection. This proves cyclical monotonicity implies the potential certificate.
To deduce optimality, we must not subtract possibly infinite marginal integrals. Use the symmetric clipping proof of transport optimality: let un​=max(−n,min(u,n)) and similarly vn​. Because c≥0, simultaneous clipping preserves the feasible inequality
un​(x)+vn​(y)≤max{u(x)+v(y),0}≤c(x,y).
(19)
For any competitor π′ with the same marginals, boundedness gives
∫(un​+vn​)dπ=∫un​dμ+∫vn​dν=∫(un​+vn​)dπ′≤∫cdπ′.
(20)
On the full-measure equality set for π, u+v=c≥0. There the clipped sums are nonnegative and increase to c: when the two signs differ, their large equal clipping levels initially cancel, then the sum increases to the nonnegative original sum. Thus the monotone convergence theorem gives
∫cdπ≤∫cdπ′for every competitor.​
(21)
This proves optimality even if the eventual integral is infinite. Conversely, a potential certificate implies the cyclic inequalities by summing and cancelling the potentials on its full-measure equality set.
The converse from optimality is true for finite-cost optimal plans. To see this, suppose points in the support violate a finite cyclic inequality by a positive amount. Continuity supplies product neighborhoods of those points on which every selected tuple still violates it, with all involved costs bounded. Normalize the restrictions of π to these neighborhoods to probability measures αi​, with marginals μi​,νi​. Subtract a sufficiently small common multiple of ∑i​αi​ and add the same multiple of ∑i​μi+1​⊗νi​. Positivity is ensured by choosing the multiple at most mini​π(Ui​)/m, even if neighborhoods overlap. Both marginals are unchanged, but integration of the strict cyclic improvement over the product of the αi​ decreases the finite total cost. This contradicts optimality, so the support is cyclically monotone.
Without a finite-value hypothesis, the unrestricted converse is false. On the discrete Polish spaces X=Y=N, take μ(n)=ν(n)=C/n2 and
c(m,n)=m+n+1{m=n}​.
(22)
This is finite, continuous and nonnegative, yet every coupling has infinite cost because its marginals have infinite first moments. The diagonal plan is therefore an extended-value minimizer. Its support is not cyclically monotone: two distinct diagonal pairs cost 2m+2n+2, while swapping their destinations costs 2m+2n. Thus under the literal printed hypotheses, optimality alone need not imply c-monotonicity; the usual finite-cost converse needs that qualification.

The transport cost separates into a function of y and a function of x, so for every transport plan
∫(y−x)dπ=∫12​ydν(y)−∫01​xdμ(x).
(23)
The value is fixed by the marginals. Hence every transport map from μ to ν is optimal, and indeed every coupling is optimal.
For an explicit description of the complete set, let F(x)=∫0x​f(s)ds and G(y)=∫1y​g(s)ds. Their strictly positive continuous densities make F:[0,1]→[0,1] and G:[1,2]→[0,1] increasing homeomorphisms. The full family of deterministic plans is
πT​=(Id,T)∗​μ,T=G−1∘S∘Fμ-almost everywhere,​
(24)
where S:[0,1]→[0,1] is any measurable Lebesgue-measure-preserving map. Indeed F∗​μ is uniform measure and G∗​ν is uniform measure, so any such S produces the required pushforward. Conversely, for any transport map T, the map S=G∘T∘F−1 preserves uniform measure. This is the measure-preserving parametrization of one-dimensional transport maps; no monotonicity is required for the linear cost.

The answer is the monotone rearrangement
T(x)=G−1(F(x)),π=(Id,T)∗​μ.​
(25)
It pushes μ to ν by the same cumulative-distribution argument as in part (a).
For the squared transport cost, the two-point swap difference is
(y1​−x1​)2+(y2​−x2​)2−(y2​−x1​)2−(y1​−x2​)2=−2(x2​−x1​)(y2​−y1​).
(26)
Thus any optimal support has no crossed pairs: x1​<x2​ forces y1​≤y2​. This follows from the finite-cost converse proved above; every cost here is bounded. Conversely, an increasing graph is c-cyclically monotone for this cost. After removing the marginal terms ∑xi2​+∑yi2​, the cycle inequalities say that pairing the sorted xi​ and yi​ maximizes ∑xi​yi​. Exchanging any inverted pairing increases that sum by the nonnegative product of the two differences, so repeated exchanges prove the inequality. Therefore the displayed increasing transport is optimal.
For uniqueness, let π be any noncrossing coupling. The sets A={x≤a} and B={y≤b} cannot both have positive mass in A∖B and B∖A: a point from each would give a strictly crossed pair. Consequently one of these differences has zero mass, and
π{x≤a,y≤b}=min{F(a),G(b)}.
(27)
This determines the joint distribution uniquely and is exactly the distribution of the common-quantile coupling (F−1(U),G−1(U)) for uniform U. Since F is continuous and strictly increasing, that coupling is induced by T=G−1F. Hence there is one optimal deterministic plan, with maps differing only on μ-null sets; in fact it is the unique optimal coupling. This proves the one-dimensional quadratic transport uniqueness criterion directly.

Use the standard setting of a compact metrizable convex set K in a Hausdorff locally convex real topological vector space, with its metrizable topology. Write A(K) for the continuous affine real functions on K. The affine upper envelope of a bounded real function is
f​(x)=inf{a(x):a∈A(K), a≥f on K}.​
(28)
Constants make this infimum finite, and f≤f​. An infimum of affine continuous majorants is concave and upper semicontinuous, hence Borel. For continuous f it is the upper concave envelope appropriate to barycentric measures; it is not the pointwise maximum of f and a selected affine function.
For a fixed probability μ, define p(g)=∫K​g​dμ on real C(K). Affine majorants show
g+h​≤g​+h,λg​=λg​ (λ≥0),g+a​=g​+a (a∈A(K)).
(29)
Thus p is sublinear and p(a)=∫adμ for affine a. On the span of f, the linear functional taking f to p(f) is dominated by p: the negative-scalar condition follows from −p(f)≤p(−f). For f=0 start from the zero subspace. The Hahn-Banach theorem extends it to a linear functional L on C(K) with L≤p and L(f)=p(f).
If g≥0, then p(−g)≤0, so L(g)≥0. Also p(1)=1 and p(−1)=−1, forcing L(1)=1. Positivity gives ∣L(g)∣≤∥g∥∞​, and the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem produces a Borel probability ν with L(g)=∫gdν. Therefore
∫fdν=∫f​dμ,∫gdν≤∫g​dμ(g∈C(K)).​
(30)
For affine a, testing both a and −a gives ∫adν=∫adμ: the two measures have the same barycenter. This proves the requested supporting measure lemma for affine upper envelopes.
Choquet's theorem: every x∈K has a Borel probability measure λ concentrated on the extreme points of K whose barycenter is x; equivalently,
λ(ExK)=1,a(x)=∫K​adλ(a∈A(K)).​
(31)
Concentration is a measure-one assertion, not a claim that the topological support must be a closed subset of ExK.
To prove it, let Mx​ be the measures satisfying all the displayed affine equalities. It is nonempty because it contains δx​, and it is weakly closed in the compact space P(K), hence compact. Let h∈C(K) be strictly convex, as permitted. Choose λ∈Mx​ maximizing ∫hdλ. Apply the supporting measure lemma with μ=λ and f=h. It gives ν with the same affine integrals, so ν∈Mx​, and
∫hdλ≥∫hdν=∫hdλ≥∫hdλ.
(32)
Thus h−h≥0 has integral zero. If z is not extreme, write z=ty+(1−t)w with 0<t<1 and distinct y,w∈K. Strict convexity and every affine majorant give
h(z)<th(y)+(1−t)h(w)≤h(z).
(33)
Therefore the nonnegative gap is strictly positive at every nonextreme point. It is Borel, and ExK is Borel by the allowed G-delta set assertion. Its zero integral forces λ(K∖ExK)=0, proving Choquet's theorem by strict convexity. No uniqueness is asserted for the representing measure.
For the real L∞ example, give its closed unit ball K the weak-star topology σ(L∞,L1), not the norm topology. The Banach-Alaoglu theorem makes it compact, and separability of L1[0,1] makes this ball metrizable. Its extreme points are exactly the classes h satisfying ∣h∣=1 almost everywhere. Indeed, if ∣h∣≤1−ε on a positive-measure set, adding and subtracting ε times its indicator decomposes h nontrivially inside the ball. Conversely, if ∣h∣=1 almost everywhere and h=(u+v)/2 with ∣u∣,∣v∣≤1, pointwise equality at the endpoints of [−1,1] forces u=v=h almost everywhere. This is the extreme-point criterion for the L-infinity unit ball.
For the hinted case f=1A​−1B​, put C=[0,1]∖(A∪B) and h±​=1A​−1B​±1C​. Then h±​ are extreme and
ν=21​δh+​​+21​δh−​​​
(34)
has barycenter f. If C is null, the two point masses coincide.
For a general real f, choose a measurable representative in [−1,1] and set, for 0≤t≤1,
Ht​(s)=21{t≤(1+f(s))/2}​−1,ν=(t↦Ht​)∗​Leb[0,1]​.​

Every Ht​ is extreme. The map into the weak-star compact ball is Borel: for each u∈L1, the function t↦∫u(s)Ht​(s)ds is measurable by joint measurability and integration; a countable dense family of such tests generates the ball's topology and Borel sigma-algebra. (36)

If L∞ is instead taken over complex scalars, its extreme points satisfy the same unit-modulus condition. Write f(s)=r(s)ζ(s), with r=∣f∣ and ∣ζ∣=1, choosing ζ=1 where f=0.
