For a Lie algebra g, write [A,B] for the linear span of brackets with one argument in each indicated subspace. The three definitions are
abelian:solvable:nilpotent:​[g,g]=0,g(r)=0 for some r,g(0)=g,g(j+1)=[g(j),g(j)],γs​(g)=0 for some s,γ1​=g,γj+1​=[g,γj​].​
(1)
These are respectively an Abelian Lie algebra, a solvable Lie algebra and a nilpotent Lie algebra. The second and third sequences are the derived series of a Lie algebra and the lower central series of a Lie algebra.
An Abelian Lie algebra has γ2​=0, and a nilpotent Lie algebra is a solvable Lie algebra: induction gives g(j)⊆γj+1​. Thus all the implications are generated by
abelian ⟹ nilpotent ⟹ solvable.​
(2)
None of the reverse implications holds. The Heisenberg Lie algebra with basis x,y,z and [x,y]=z, all other basic brackets zero, is nonabelian but has γ2​=Cz, γ3​=0. The two-dimensional affine Lie algebra of the line with [h,e]=e has g(1)=Ce and g(2)=0, but γj​=Ce for every j≥2. It is solvable and neither nilpotent nor abelian. These two examples answer all six ordered-pair comparisons.
The Lie theorem says that a finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation of a finite-dimensional solvable Lie algebra over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero has a common eigenvector whenever its representation space is nonzero. Equivalently it admits an invariant complete flag, or simultaneous upper triangularization. Here the field may be taken to be C; these hypotheses are essential.
To prove the common-eigenvector assertion, induct on dimg. The zero algebra is immediate. Since g is nonzero and solvable, its derived algebra is proper. Choose a codimension-one Lie algebra ideal h containing it, and write g=h⊕Cx. The Lie algebra h is solvable, so induction supplies v=0 and a linear functional λ:h→C with hv=λ(h)v.
Consider the finite-dimensional cyclic subspace W=span{v,xv,x2v,…}. Until the first linear dependence, these powers form a basis. The identity
hxjv=x(hxj−1v)+[h,x]xj−1v
(3)
and [h,x]∈h show by induction, simultaneously for all h∈h, that W is h-invariant and that h is upper triangular on it with every diagonal entry λ(h). It is also x-invariant by construction. Hence
0=trW​[x,h]=(dimW)λ([x,h]).
(4)
The trace of a commutator is zero, and characteristic zero gives λ([x,h])=0.
The simultaneous eigenspace Eλ​={w:hw=λ(h)w for every h∈h} is nonzero and x-invariant, since
h(xw)=x(hw)+[h,x]w=λ(h)xw.
(5)
Over an algebraically closed field, x∣Eλ​​ has an eigenvector, which is therefore a common eigenvector for all of g. This finishes induction. Apply the same assertion to the quotient representation by its invariant line, and then to successive quotients. A basis adapted to the resulting complete flag gives the stated simultaneous triangularization of a Lie algebra representation, completing the proof of the Lie theorem.
A nilpotent Lie algebra is a solvable Lie algebra, so the inclusion g⊆gl(V) satisfies the Lie theorem and is upper triangular in a suitable basis, for finite-dimensional complex V.
This is insufficient to prove the Engel theorem. Its matrix version starts with a Lie subalgebra of nilpotent endomorphisms and concludes that they are simultaneously strictly upper triangular; its abstract version concludes nilpotence from nilpotence of every Adjoint representation endomorphism. Merely upper triangular matrices can have nonzero diagonal entries: the one-dimensional algebra CIV​ is an Abelian Lie algebra and a nilpotent Lie algebra, but acts by a nonnilpotent identity matrix. This is nilpotent Lie algebras need not act nilpotently. Moreover, in the abstract Engel theorem nilpotence of the algebra is a conclusion, so assuming it first to invoke the Lie theorem would be circular. Abstract nilpotence and nilpotence of each representing matrix are different conditions.

The Weyl complete reducibility theorem states that every finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation of a finite-dimensional complex semisimple Lie algebra is a direct sum of Irreducible Lie algebra representations. Equivalently, every invariant vector subspace has an invariant complement.
We use the permitted Casimir operator properties in the following precise form. There is a central quadratic operator C commuting with the action on every module; it is zero on the trivial Lie algebra representation, and on every nontrivial finite-dimensional Irreducible Lie algebra representation it is a nonzero scalar c. This follows from the Schur lemma and the Casimir eigenvalue cλ​=(λ,λ+2ρ), using the Killing form normalization. We also use the permitted one-dimensional-representation fact: a complex semisimple Lie algebra has only trivial one-dimensional representations. Equivalently, it is a perfect Lie algebra, [g,g]=g, so a character g→C annihilating brackets must vanish. Neither fact assumes complete reducibility of the module being proved reducible.
First prove that every finite-dimensional short exact sequence
0⟶W⟶E⟶C⟶0
(6)
with trivial quotient splits. If W is nontrivial irreducible, the Casimir operator CE​ has image in W and restricts to cIW​ there. Consequently kerCE​ is a one-dimensional invariant complement to W. If W is trivial irreducible, E has a basis in which every action is (00​a(x)0​). The Lie algebra representation identity makes a([x,y])=0, so the one-dimensional-representation fact gives a=0 and again the sequence splits.
For general W, induct on dimW. Choose an irreducible submodule W1​⊆W. The induced sequence with kernel W/W1​ and middle term E/W1​ splits by induction. The inverse image E1​⊆E of its invariant complement is a submodule fitting into 0→W1​→E1​→C→0. The irreducible-kernel case gives an invariant line in E1​ mapping isomorphically to the quotient. It is also an invariant complement to W in E. The case W=0 starts this induction. This proves splitting of a trivial quotient for a semisimple Lie algebra, including kernels that are not assumed completely reducible.
Now let U⊆V be any invariant subspace. On the Hom representation Hom(V,U) the action is
(x⋅T)(v)=x⋅T(v)−T(x⋅v).
(7)
Let E consist of the maps whose restriction to U is a scalar multiple of IU​. This is a submodule, and restriction gives
0⟶Hom(V/U,U)⟶E⟶C⟶0.
(8)
The right-hand map is surjective because an ordinary linear projection V→U exists; its quotient action is trivial because a commutator with IU​ is zero. The splitting just proved supplies an invariant T∈E with T∣U​=IU​. Thus T intertwines the actions, T2=T, and
V=U⊕kerTas g-modules.​
(9)
The cases U=0 and U=V are immediate. Choosing an irreducible submodule and repeating this complement construction proves the Weyl complete reducibility theorem. This last step is invariant complement from an equivariant projection.
Dropping finite dimensionality gives an example with the complex simple Lie algebra sl2​. Its Verma module of highest weight zero has a basis v0​,v1​,… with
fvk​=vk+1​,hvk​=−2kvk​,evk​=k(1−k)vk−1​,
(10)
where ev0​=0. These actions obey [h,e]=2e, [h,f]=−2f, [e,f]=h. The span of v1​,v2​,… is a proper submodule and the quotient is trivial. It has no invariant complement: such a complement would be a trivial line, while f is injective on the entire module. Thus a simple Lie algebra can have an infinite-dimensional representation that is not completely reducible, even over C.

Use Dynkin labels (a,b) for the highest weight aω1​+bω2​ of the complex special linear Lie algebra sl3​. The A2 root system has α1​=(2,−1) and α2​=(−1,2) in these coordinates. In the drawings, ω1​ and ω2​ have equal lengths and angle 60∘; a label at a point records its weight multiplicity, not a further copy at a different position.

(r−(p+q−r), (p+q−r)−(4−p−q)).

The appropriate abstract object is a finite reduced crystallographic root system R in a real inner product space E. Its axioms are: R is finite, spans E, and does not contain zero; for α∈R, R∩Rα={α,−α}; each root reflection
sα​(v)=v−(α,α)2(v,α)​α
(19)
permutes R; and every Cartan integer nαβ​=2(α,β)/(β,β) is an integer. The restriction to a reduced root system and crystallographic integrality distinguishes roots of complex semisimple Lie algebras from more general reflection configurations.
For any two roots of a root system, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
0≤nαβ​nβα​=(α,α)(β,β)4(α,β)2​≤4.
(20)
If the inner product is zero, both Cartan integers vanish. Otherwise their signs agree, and the absolute value of each is a positive integer. Dividing their product by an integer of absolute value at least one proves
0≤∣nαβ​∣≤4.​
(21)
For nonproportional roots the product is strictly less than four. In a reduced root system, proportional roots are just ±α and have Cartan integers ±2, so the printed bound is intentionally looser than the resulting bound of three.
A fundamental system of a root system is a basis Δ of E made of roots, such that each root is an integer combination of Δ with either all coefficients nonnegative or all nonpositive. Its members are the simple roots. Suppose distinct α,β∈Δ had nαβ​>0. Then
sβ​(α)=α−nαβ​β∈R
(22)
has a positive coefficient of α and a negative coefficient of β, contradicting the defining sign condition. Thus nαβ​≤0. Distinct simple roots are linearly independent, so their Cartan-integer product is strictly less than four. Combining integrality and the sign condition gives
nαβ​∈{0,−1,−2,−3}(α=β simple).​
(23)
Here nonpositive is the intended sense of the printed convention that includes zero among “negative” numbers; orthogonal simple roots really do give zero.
To form a Dynkin diagram, place a vertex at each simple root. Join two vertices by nαβ​nβα​ bonds, hence zero, one, two or three. A multiple bond has an arrow toward the short root. Indeed nαβ​/nβα​=(α,α)/(β,β) determines the squared length ratio, and the diagram with the Cartan matrix reconstructs the angles and relative lengths. A single bond joins equal-length roots.
The connected finite Dynkin diagrams are the following. The descriptions include bond multiplicities and arrow directions, so distinguish dual diagrams:
  • An Dynkin diagram, An​ for n≥1: a chain of n vertices with only single bonds.
  • Bn Dynkin diagram and affine extension, Bn​ for n≥2: a chain whose last bond is double, with its arrow toward the terminal short root; all earlier bonds are single. Only its finite diagram is used here.
  • Cn Dynkin diagram, Cn​ for n≥3: the same chain with the double-bond arrow toward the penultimate short root and away from the terminal long root. C2​ and B2​ describe the same rank-two type after relabelling.
  • Dn Dynkin diagram, Dn​ for n≥4: a simply laced tree with one trivalent vertex and arms of lengths 1,1,n−3, counting edges.
  • En Dynkin diagram, E6​,E7​,E8​: simply laced trees with a trivalent vertex and arms of lengths respectively (1,2,2), (1,2,3), (1,2,4).
  • F4 Dynkin diagram, F4​: a chain of four vertices, with a double central bond and two single outer bonds. Two consecutive vertices are long and two are short; the arrow goes from the long pair toward the short pair.
  • G2 Dynkin diagram, G2​: two vertices joined by a triple bond, with arrow toward the short root.
There are no other connected finite Dynkin diagrams. Low-rank conventions also identify B1​=C1​=A1​ and D3​=A3​; D2​ is disconnected, so introduces no further connected type. Affine diagrams are outside this finite classification.
Finally suppose the underlying graph contained a cycle on m≥3 distinct simple roots α1​,…,αm​. Put ui​=αi​/∥αi​∥. Any bonded pair has
(ui​,uj​)=−21​nαi​αj​​nαj​αi​​​≤−21​,
(24)
and all other distinct pairs have nonpositive inner products. The cycle contributes at least m bonded pairs, so
∥∑i=1m​ui​∥2=m+2∑i<j​(ui​,uj​)≤m−m=0.
(25)
But the simple roots, and hence these normalized vectors, are linearly independent, making the displayed sum nonzero. Positive definiteness gives a contradiction. Thus the underlying graph of a finite Dynkin diagram has no cycle. This acyclicity of a finite Dynkin diagram argument also excludes cycles with extra chords or multiple bonds; multiple bonds themselves are not treated as two-edge cycles.

Let ρ:g→gl(V) be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation. The form denoted BV​ is
BV​(X,Y)=trV​(ρ(X)ρ(Y)).​
(26)
It is the Trace form of a Lie algebra representation; the Killing form B without a subscript is specifically the case V=g, ρ=ad. The distinction matters: the form of the trivial representation cannot detect whether the algebra is semisimple.
Linearity of ρ and trace proves bilinearity, and cyclicity of the trace gives symmetry. With A=ρ(X), D=ρ(Y) and C=ρ(Z), the representation identity gives
BV​([X,Y],Z)​=tr((AD−DA)C)=tr(ADC−ACD)=tr(A(DC−CD))=BV​(X,[Y,Z]).​
(27)
Thus it is an invariant bilinear form on a Lie algebra, and in particular the Adjoint representation preserves the Killing form.
The Cartan solvability criterion has two useful formulations. For a finite-dimensional complex Lie algebra g,
g is solvable⟺B(g,[g,g])=0.​
(28)
Its matrix version says that a Lie subalgebra L⊆gl(V) is solvable precisely when tr(xy)=0 for all x∈[L,L] and y∈L. We prove the matrix version first, with ordinary trace in V.
If L is solvable, the Lie theorem makes all its matrices upper triangular. Their commutators are strictly upper triangular, so multiplying such a matrix by an upper triangular one still has zero diagonal and hence zero trace. This proves the easy direction.
Conversely assume the trace-orthogonality condition and fix x∈[L,L]. We show that all eigenvalues of x vanish. Use its Additive Jordan decomposition x=xs​+xn​ with [xs​,xn​]=0. On the generalized eigenspace Vλ​ of x, define an auxiliary endomorphism y to be λI. This y need not be in L; we only need control of its commutator with L.
On Hom(Vμ​,Vλ​), adxs​ acts by λ−μ and ady by λ−μ​. Choose a polynomial q with q(0)=0 and q(λ−μ)=λ−μ​ at the finitely many distinct differences. Polynomial interpolation supplies it because equal differences have equal conjugates. Therefore ady=q(adxs​).
The adjoint compatibility of additive Jordan decomposition identifies adxs​ as the semisimple part of adx. Elementary Jordan–Chevalley decomposition gives adxs​=p(adx) for a polynomial p with p(0)=0. Hence ady is a polynomial in adx with zero constant term. Since adx maps L into [L,L] and preserves that Lie algebra ideal, we obtain
[y,L]⊆[L,L].
(29)
No assumption that xs​, xn​ or y belongs to L was made.
Write x=∑i​[ai​,bi​] with ai​,bi​∈L. Cyclicity and the assumed orthogonality now give
tr(xy)=∑i​tr([ai​,bi​]y)=∑i​tr(ai​[bi​,y])=0.
(30)
On the other hand the nilpotent parts have zero trace on each generalized eigenspace, so
tr(xy)=∑λ​(dimVλ​)λλ=∑λ​(dimVλ​)∣λ∣2.
(31)
Thus all λ are zero and x is a nilpotent endomorphism. This is the Conjugate-spectrum proof of Cartan solvability.
The permitted Engel theorem, in the form needed here, states: a finite-dimensional Lie subalgebra of endomorphisms in which every element is nilpotent has a nonzero vector annihilated by all its elements, and iteration on quotients makes every element simultaneously strictly upper triangular. Apply it to [L,L]. The strictly upper triangular algebra is nilpotent, so [L,L] is a nilpotent Lie algebra and therefore solvable. Since L/[L,L] is abelian, the derived series of a Lie algebra of L terminates too. This proves the matrix criterion.
Apply it to L=adg. The condition on B is exactly the matrix condition on L. Thus adg is solvable. The kernel of ad is the center of a Lie algebra, which is abelian, so g is solvable as well: once the derived series maps to zero it is central, and its next term vanishes. Conversely a solvable g has solvable adjoint image, proving the abstract Cartan solvability criterion in both directions.
A finite-dimensional Lie algebra is a semisimple Lie algebra when it has no nonzero solvable Lie algebra ideal, equivalently its solvable radical is zero. Let R=radB={x:B(x,g)=0}. Invariance makes R an ideal. For x,y∈R, adx induces zero on g/R, and the block trace gives BR​(x,y)=Bg​(x,y)=0, where BR​ is the adjoint Killing form of R itself. The Cartan solvability criterion therefore makes R solvable. If g is semisimple, R=0.
Conversely suppose B is a nondegenerate bilinear form. If a nonzero solvable ideal existed, its last nonzero derived term A would be an abelian ideal of g. For a∈A and x∈g, the operator adaadx has image in A and is zero on A, so its square and its trace are zero. Thus B(A,g)=0, contradicting nondegeneracy. This is Abelian ideals lie in the radical of the Killing form. We conclude the Cartan criterion for semisimplicity:
g is semisimple⟺B is nondegenerate.​
(32)

A real Lie group is a group G equipped with a finite-dimensional real smooth manifold structure, conventionally Hausdorff and second countable, for which multiplication G×G→G and inversion G→G are smooth.
Its tangent space at the identity e is defined by smooth curves through e: two curves represent the same tangent vector when their derivatives in a local chart agree at zero. For X∈Te​G, left translation Lg​(h)=gh determines the left-invariant vector field
XL(g)=(dLg​)e​X.
(33)
The commutator of these derivations on smooth functions is another left-invariant vector field, so define the Lie bracket
[X,Y]=[XL,YL](e),Lie(G)=Te​G.​
(34)
This construction supplies the Lie algebra structure. In a Matrix Lie group, XL(g)=gX and differentiating the two fields gives [X,Y]=XY−YX.
For the special linear group, differentiate the determinant along a curve g(t) through I. Expansion of the determinant, or its differential DdetA​(H)=det(A)tr(A−1H), gives
dtd​det(I+tX)​t=0​=trX.
(35)
The determinant differential is surjective at I, so the level set det=1 has tangent space equal to its differential's kernel. Equivalently, every tangent matrix has zero trace, and every zero-trace matrix supplies a curve etX of determinant ettrX=1. Hence
Lie(SLn​(R))=sln​(R)={X:trX=0},​
(36)
with the matrix commutator bracket. The same calculation over C gives the complex special linear Lie algebra sln​(C); as a real group, the complex group has that space viewed as a real Lie algebra.
The matrix exponential is the everywhere-convergent series
expX=k=0∑∞​k!Xk​,X∈gln​,​
(37)
whose inverse matrix is exp(−X). The matrix logarithm is locally defined near I by
log(I+A)=k=1∑∞​k(−1)k+1​Ak,∥A∥<1.​
(38)
These maps are inverse on suitable neighbourhoods of zero and I, giving a logarithmic chart of a matrix Lie group. A logarithm is not a globally single-valued inverse of the exponential.
Every invertible complex matrix nevertheless has at least one matrix logarithm. Put it in Jordan normal form. For a block J=λI+N, λ=0 and Ns=0, choose any complex scalar logarithm ℓ of λ and set
L=ℓI+∑k=1s−1​k(−1)k+1​(λN​)k.
(39)
The finite logarithm and exponential identities in the nilpotent variable give eL=λ(I+N/λ)=J. Combine the blocks and conjugate back. Thus the exponential map is surjective on GLn​(C), by existence of a logarithm for every invertible complex matrix.
A connected counterexample is SL2​(R). It is connected: polar decomposition of an invertible real matrix writes each element as KP, with K∈SO(2) and P positive definite symmetric of determinant one; SO(2) is connected and P is connected to I through Pt.
But g=diag(−2,−1/2) belongs to this group and is not eX for any real X. Such an X would commute with eX=g. Since g has two distinct real eigenvalues, direct commutation makes X a real diagonal matrix. Its exponential has positive diagonal entries, a contradiction. Therefore
exp:sl2​(R)⟶SL2​(R) is not surjective.​
(40)
This is an exponential-surjectivity obstruction from distinct negative eigenvalues; connectedness does not eliminate the obstruction.
