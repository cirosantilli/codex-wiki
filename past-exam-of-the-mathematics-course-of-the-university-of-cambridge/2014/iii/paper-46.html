Use the mostly-plus Minkowski metric, and write ≈ for equality on the constraint surface. Assume that the mechanical constraints are locally independent. They are first-class constraints when
{φi​,φj​}=Cij​k(q,p)φk​.
(1)
Thus their Poisson brackets vanish on the constraint surface, and their Hamiltonian flows preserve that surface. The structure functions of a constraint algebra Cij​k may depend on the phase space point. The finite real span of the constraints is a Lie algebra if it closes with constant structure coefficients, in a suitable choice of generators. The Jacobi identity then gives the usual conditions on the structure constants of a Lie algebra. With general structure functions the finite real span need not close, even though the Poisson bracket of all smooth functions is itself a Lie bracket.
To see the gauge invariance directly, let G=ϵi(t)φi​ generate a canonical gauge transformation:
δqI={qI,G},δpI​={pI​,G},δλk=ϵ˙k−λiϵjCij​k.​
(2)
The variation of the phase-space action integrand is
δ(pI​q˙​I−λiφi​)=dtd​(pI​δqI−G)+(ϵ˙k−δλk−λiϵjCij​k)φk​.
(3)
The second term cancels without using the equations of motion. Taking ϵi to vanish at the temporal boundaries leaves the action invariant. Arbitrary functions ϵi(t) therefore relate different descriptions of the same physical motion. This reasoning also works with structure functions; constant structure coefficients are only needed for the finite-dimensional Lie algebra claim.
For a closed string, choose 0≤σ<2π and periodic fields. A convenient Nambu-Goto phase-space action is
I=∫dtdσ[Pm​X˙m−2e​(P2+T2X′2)−vP⋅X′].
(4)
Here e and v are Lagrange multipliers. The Nambu–Goto phase-space constraints are H0​=(P2+T2X′2)/2≈0 and H1​=P⋅X′≈0, with canonical Poisson brackets
{Xm(σ),Pn​(σ′)}=δnm​δ2π​(σ−σ′).
(5)
Let J±m​=Pm±TX′m. Differentiating the periodic Dirac delta function gives
{Pm(σ),X′n(σ′)}=ηmn∂σ​δ2π​(σ−σ′),{X′m(σ),Pn(σ′)}=ηmn∂σ​δ2π​(σ−σ′).
(6)
The opposite signs in J+​ and J−​ cancel these terms, so {J+m​(σ),J−n​(σ′)}=0. Replace the original constraints by the equivalent chiral densities
H±​=4TJ±2​​,H0​=T(H+​+H−​),H1​=H+​−H−​.
(7)
Their mixed Poisson brackets vanish. Choose opposite Fourier orientations for the two sectors:
Ln​=∫02π​dσeinσH+​(σ),Ln​=∫02π​dσe−inσH−​(σ).
(8)
The chiral constraint algebra of a closed string is
{Lm​,Ln​}=−i(m−n)Lm+n​,{Lm​,Ln​}=−i(m−n)Lm+n​,{Lm​,Ln​}=0.​
(9)
Each is the Witt algebra: the vector fields ℓn​=ieinσ∂σ​ on a circle satisfy [ℓm​,ℓn​]=(m−n)ℓm+n​. Fourier expansion identifies each real algebra, with Ln∗​=L−n​, with the Lie algebra of vector fields on the circle. The two commuting copies give Diff1​⊕Diff1​, not a quantum central extension.
For an open string, allowed boundary conditions must remove the endpoint term in the variation of the action, consistently with the allowed endpoint variations. The spatial boundary term is
δI​∂σ​=−∫dt[(eT2Xm′​+vPm​)δXm]σ=aσ=b​.
(10)
It expresses the open-string endpoint momentum flux. In the temporal gauge for a string X0=t, take a boundary-adapted parametrization with v=0 at the ends. Fixing Xi(t,a)=0 gives δXi(t,a)=0, a Dirichlet boundary condition. At the other end allow arbitrary spatial variations; for nonzero e these require X′i(t,b)=0, a Neumann boundary condition. Also X′0=0 in this temporal gauge for a string, so X′m(t,b)=0. The constraint at this free-end string boundary condition reduces to P2=0. Hamilton's equation X˙=eP+vX′ consequently gives X˙2=0 there. Since X˙0=1, the free endpoint has spatial speed one. This is the null motion of a free string endpoint.
A straight rotating string with one fixed endpoint supplies the required solution in at least two spatial dimensions. Set e=1/T, v=0, and
X0=t,X(t,σ)=Lsin(σ/L)(cos(t/L),sin(t/L),0,…),0≤σ≤2πL​.​
(11)
Take Pm​=TX˙m​. The Hamilton's equations become X¨=X′′, which holds because both second derivatives give −X/L2. The Nambu–Goto phase-space constraints are satisfied by
X˙⋅X′=0,X˙2+X′2=−1+sin2(σ/L)+cos2(σ/L)=0.
(12)
The endpoint at σ=0 stays at the origin, while X′=0 at σ=πL/2 and the endpoint moves around a circle of radius L with angular speed 1/L. At each time the whole string lies on a straight radial segment. Its spatial proper length is
∫0πL/2​∣X′∣dσ=∫0πL/2​cos(σ/L)dσ=L.
(13)
The velocity is everywhere perpendicular to the segment, so this also equals the sum of local rest-frame lengths. The induced worldsheet metric becomes degenerate at the null free endpoint, as expected for the limiting free-end solution.

Use x±=(x0±xD−1)/2​, transverse coordinates xi, i=1,…,d=D−2, and the Minkowski metric
ds2=−2dx+dx−+dxidxi,p2=−2p+​p−​+pi​pi​.
(14)
The relativistic particle phase-space action becomes
I=∫dt[x˙+p+​+x˙−p−​+x˙ipi​−2e​(−2p+​p−​+pi2​+μ2)].
(15)
In the light cone gauge x+=t, solve the mass-shell condition for p+​, assuming p−​=0. The reduced phase-space action is ∫dt(x˙−p−​+x˙ipi​−H), with
H=−p+​=−2p−​pi2​+μ2​=2p+pi2​+μ2​,p+=−p−​>0.​
(16)
The last equality selects the future-directed momentum sector and makes positivity transparent. With ℏ=1 and pa​=−i∂a​, the Schrödinger equation is
i∂+​Ψ=−2(−i∂−​)−Δ⊥​+μ2​Ψ.
(17)
The inverse acts only on Fourier modes with nonzero p−​. Multiplication by 2p−​ gives 2∂−​∂+​Ψ=(Δ⊥​−μ2)Ψ. Therefore
(□D​−μ2)Ψ=0,□D​=−2∂+​∂−​+Δ⊥​.​
(18)
The light-cone Hamiltonian thus gives the same Klein-Gordon equation as covariant quantization.
For the massive two-form field, take μ=0. Apply ∂n to its field equation. Antisymmetry of Fmnp​ makes ∂n∂mFmnp​=0, so
∂nAnp​=0.
(19)
Expanding F=dA, the other divergence terms vanish by this condition, leaving (□D​−μ2)Anp​=0. The light-cone decomposition of a massive two-form makes its dependent components explicit. The divergence equation is
−∂−​A+n​−∂+​A−n​+∂i​Ain​=0.
(20)
Taking n=− and n=i, respectively, gives
A+−​=−∂−−1​∂i​A−i​,A+i​=−∂−−1​∂+​A−i​+∂−−1​∂j​Aji​.​
(21)
The n=+ equation follows from these expressions: the two terms containing ∂+​∂i​A−i​ cancel and ∂i​∂j​Aji​=0. Consequently A−i​ and Aij​ are independent, each satisfying the Klein-Gordon equation with mass μ. The number of independent particle polarizations is
d+(2d​)=(2D−1​).​
(22)
This is the exterior square of the vector representation of the massive little group SO(D−1). In the analogous Proca equation, ∂mAm​=0 determines A+​ from A−​ and Ai​, leaving D−1 components. A massive field has no gauge freedom that would justify setting these longitudinal components to zero. If μ=0, instead use the two-form gauge field symmetry A↦A+dΛ: the light-cone gauge for a two-form removes A−m​, leaving (2D−2​) transverse particle polarizations. The massive and massless counts are different.
In the closed-string mode expansion, xm,pm​ are center-of-mass canonical variables, while αki​,αki​ are independent left- and right-moving transverse string oscillators. Their complex conjugates are α−ki​,α−ki​. The two zero-mode Lagrange multipliers impose the remaining mass-shell condition and closed-string level matching. The string level operators are
N=∑k>0​α−k​⋅αk​,N=∑k>0​α−k​⋅αk​.
(23)
Their quantum definitions use normal ordering. The symplectic terms in the phase-space action give
[xm,pn​]=iδnm​,[αmi​,αnj​]=mδijδm+n,0​,[αmi​,αnj​]=mδijδm+n,0​,
(24)
with all brackets between distinct sectors zero. The nonzero-index string oscillators obey αni†​=α−ni​ and similarly for the right-moving sector. Define the momentum-labelled oscillator vacuum by
αki​∣0;p⟩=αki​∣0;p⟩=0(k>0),pm​∣0;p⟩=pmlabel​∣0;p⟩.
(25)
For k>0, aki​=αki​/k​ has [aki​,alj†​]=δkl​δij. Hence
N=∑k,i​kaki†​aki​,[N,α−ki​]=kα−ki​.
(26)
Starting with N∣0;p⟩=0, a finite product with rki​ creation operators of mode k has eigenvalue ∑k,i​krki​. The Fock space is generated by these products; both level operators have nonnegative integer eigenvalues. This establishes the integer string oscillator level property. Subtracting their physical zero-mode constraints enforces N=N.
There is a distinction between the displayed classical zero modes and their quantum constraints. With the normal-ordering constant of a string a, these are
8πTp2​+N−a=0,8πTp2​+N−a=0.
(27)
At the massless first closed-string level, the states are
α−1i​α−1j​∣0;p⟩.
(28)
Their transverse polarization tensor splits into a symmetric trace-free part, an antisymmetric part, and its trace. These are the graviton, Kalb–Ramond field, and dilaton, with respective particle polarization counts d(d+1)/2−1, d(d−1)/2, and one. They have the transverse little group representations of massless particles. In a Lorentz-consistent bosonic string theory, the first chiral level is a massless vector, not a massive vector with one missing physical polarization; the closed-string products are therefore massless. This fixes a=1. Equivalently, regularized transverse zero-point energy gives a=(D−2)/24, and Lorentz consistency fixes the critical dimension of the bosonic string D=26.
It follows that the bosonic string mass spectrum is
MN2​=−p2=8πT(N−1)=α′4​(N−1),α′=2πT1​,N=N.​
(29)
The ground state has M02​=−8πT and is a tachyon; level one is massless; for N≥2 the mass is MN​=8πT(N−1)​. The masslessness claim uses the consistent quantum theory, rather than an unshifted reading of the classical L0​.
A massive two-form at closed-string level two is present. To see it without confusing it with the level-one massless Kalb–Ramond field, the level-two states in one chiral sector are
α−2i​∣0⟩,α−1i​α−1j​∣0⟩.
(30)
They have d+d(d+1)/2=(D−1)D/2−1 components and assemble into the symmetric traceless square S02​V of the massive little group vector space V=RD−1. The full closed-string level is S02​V⊗S02​V. For two symmetric trace-free matrices S,S, the map
(S,S)⟼[S,S]ab​=Sac​Scb​−Sac​Scb​
(31)
is an equivariant map onto antisymmetric matrices. To verify surjectivity, take S diagonal with distinct entries in positions a,b and S with only its symmetric ab entry nonzero. Their commutator gives the ab antisymmetric basis element. Finite-dimensional representations of the compact little group are completely reducible, so this quotient representation is also a subrepresentation. It has exactly (2D−1​) particle polarizations and is described by the massive field equation with μ2=8πT. At D=26 this gives 300 particle polarizations, consisting in light-cone coordinates of 24 components A−i​ and 276 components Aij​.

For the relativistic particle phase-space action, the first-class constraint φ=(p2+m2)/2 generates
δxm=ϵ(t)pm,δpm​=0,δe=ϵ˙(t).​
(32)
Indeed the integrand varies by 21​d[ϵ(p2−m2)]/dt. The canonical gauge transformation is an invariance when the gauge parameter vanishes at fixed temporal endpoints, or when all fields and the parameter are periodic. The boundary restriction matters for the proper-time modulus.
Normalize the worldline interval to [0,1]. Then
s=∫01​e(t)dt
(33)
is invariant because δs=ϵ(1)−ϵ(0)=0. Every allowed e in its orbit can be written e(t)=s+ϵ˙(t): set ϵ(t)=∫0t​[e(u)−s]du. Thus s remains a gauge-invariant integration variable, not another removable nonconstant mode. For a worldline circle the constant gauge parameter is a residual zero mode. Without the endpoint restriction, the assertion that s is invariant would not hold.
The worldline gauge-orbit determinant is the Jacobian from gauge-orbit coordinates ϵ to the nonconstant part of e is the Faddeev-Popov determinant of ∂t​. Equivalently, the gauge-fixing identity has the form
1=ΔFP​[e]∫Dϵδ(eϵ−s),ΔFP​=det′∂t​.
(34)
The determinant is taken between the appropriate boundary-condition spaces, with the modulus removed; on a circle the prime also removes the constant parameter. Gauge fixing therefore leaves a factor det′∂t​ and a modulus measure, after division by any residual gauge volume. Even though this determinant is field independent in the present Abelian example, it is the required change-of-variables Jacobian. A Grassmann integral over the Faddeev-Popov ghosts exponentiates it:
det′∂t​ ∝ ∫DbDceiIgh​,Igh​=i∫01​dtbc˙.
(35)
The overall determinant phase depends on the integration convention and can be absorbed into normalization. Zero modes and the same endpoint restrictions must be treated separately rather than included in an invertible determinant.
For the free-ended open string, take 0≤σ≤π. A canonical cosine expansion at a fixed time is
Xm(σ)=xm+πT​i​∑n=0​nαnm​​cos(nσ),Pm​(σ)=πpm​​+πT​​∑n=0​αnm​cos(nσ).
(36)
It implements the Neumann boundary conditions and has αn†​=α−n​. With α0m​=pm/πT​, its Nambu-Goto phase-space action, up to a total time derivative, is
I=∫dt[pm​x˙m+∑n>0​ni​α˙n​⋅α−n​−∑n∈Z​λ−n​Lncl​],Lncl​=21​∑k∈Z​αn−k​⋅αk​.
(37)
Reality requires λ−n​=λn∗​. Numerical factors can be absorbed into these Lagrange multipliers. In this covariant quantization of the bosonic string the oscillators retain all D spacetime components, in contrast to the transverse oscillators in the preceding solution. The canonical commutation relations are
[xm,pn​]=iδnm​,[αmr​,αns​]=mηrsδm+n,0​.
(38)
The oscillator vacuum ∣0;p⟩ is annihilated by αnm​ for n>0. Its momentum label will sometimes be suppressed.
Define the matter Virasoro algebra generators using normal ordering:
Ln​=21​∑k​:αn−k​⋅αk​:,qquadL0​=α′p2+k>0∑​α−k​⋅αk​,quadα′=2πT1​.​
(39)
No additive intercept is included in this definition of L0​. For n>0 the indices of the two factors in each term add to n; they cannot both be negative. After normal ordering there is a positive-mode annihilation operator on the right, possibly accompanied by the zero mode. Hence Ln​∣0;p⟩=0 for every n>0. In L0​, commuting positive modes past negative modes formally adds 2D​∑k>0​k. This divergent constant needs a prescription, and a finite shift is an ordering ambiguity. Our convention instead puts the physical string intercept into the constraint L0​−a.
With this convention the matter Virasoro algebra is
[Lm​,Ln​]=(m−n)Lm+n​+12D​(m3−m)δm+n,0​.​
(40)
A different additive constant in L0​ would change the linear-in-m central term, so stating the convention is essential.
For the worldsheet ghost fields, use
{bm​,cn​}=δm+n,0​,{bm​,bn​}={cm​,cn​}=0,
(41)
and choose a ghost oscillator vacuum with
bn​∣0⟩gh​=cn​∣0⟩gh​=0(n>0),b0​∣0⟩gh​=0.
(42)
Then c0​∣0⟩gh​=0 since {b0​,c0​}=1. This choice specifies the ghost zero-mode doublet; it is not a claim that both zero modes annihilate one state. With the printed ghost Virasoro zero-mode convention, L0gh​∣0⟩gh​=0 and Lmgh​∣0⟩gh​=0 for m>0. The latter follows by putting positive ghost modes on the right; a possible contraction requires m=0 and is absent here.
Apply the supplied BRST charge to the matter state times this ghost oscillator vacuum. Terms with a rightmost cm​, m>0, vanish, as do the positive-mode ghost generators. Thus
QBRST​(∣Ψ⟩⊗∣0⟩gh​)=(L0​−1)∣Ψ⟩⊗c0​∣0⟩gh​+∑m>0​Lm​∣Ψ⟩⊗c−m​∣0⟩gh​.
(43)
These one-ghost states are independent, as can also be seen by applying b0​,bm​. Therefore the BRST physical-state constraints of an open string are
(L0​−1)∣Ψ⟩=0,Lm​∣Ψ⟩=0(m>0).​
(44)
For the matter oscillator vacuum, L0​=α′p2, so p2=1/α′ and M2=−1/α′=−2πT: the physical ground state is a tachyon. The momentum must satisfy this equation; the zero-momentum oscillator vacuum by itself would not be BRST-closed.
Matter and ghost generators commute with one another. Add their two algebras and write Lm​=Lm​+Lmgh​. The given ghost constant must be retained:
[Lm​,Ln​]=(m−n)(Lm+n​−δm+n,0​)+12D−26​(m3−m)δm+n,0​.​
(45)
Define the shifted generators Lm​=Lm​−δm,0​. Then
[Lm​,Ln​]=(m−n)Lm+n​+12D−26​(m3−m)δm+n,0​.
(46)
BRST nilpotence requires cancellation of the anomalous central term in this shifted constraint algebra, together with the intercept one already present in the charge. At m=2,n=−2 the remaining anomalous coefficient is (D−26)/2, so D=26. At this value the shifted total generators obey the Witt algebra. The unshifted Lm​ still have the displayed linear zero-mode shift; it must not be silently discarded.

Take an oriented closed string in flat, critical bosonic string theory, with α′=1/(2πT) and a mostly-plus target Minkowski metric. After continuation of the worldsheet to Euclidean signature, the Polyakov path integral sums over embeddings X and worldsheet metrics, divided by worldsheet diffeomorphisms and Weyl transformations. In a flat target its kinetic action is
SE​=4πα′1​∫Σ​d2zg​gab∂a​Xm∂b​Xm​.
(47)
Target-time continuation or analytic continuation of external momenta defines the Lorentzian scattering amplitude; a naive real Euclidean Gaussian for timelike X0 would not be convergent.
An external tachyon is represented by the tachyon vertex operator Vp​(z,zˉ)=:eip⋅X:, with conformal weights h=hˉ=α′p2/4. The physical integrated vertex has (h,hˉ)=(1,1), so p2=4/α′. Schematically its tree amplitude is
AN​ ∝ ∫Vol(Diff×Weyl)DXDg​e−SE​∏a=1N​∫Σ​d2za​g​Vpa​​(za​,zˉa​).
(48)
The Faddeev-Popov determinant from conformal gauge is represented by the worldsheet ghost fields. At tree level the worldsheet is a Riemann sphere, whose unpunctured complex structure has no moduli. Its residual conformal automorphisms are the Möbius transformations, PSL(2,C). Fix three insertion points, accompanying their unintegrated vertices by the required cc ghost factors. The remaining N−3 complex insertion positions are integrated over the sphere; equivalently one integrates all positions and divides by the residual conformal group.
The embedding fields are free, with
⟨Xm(z)Xn(w)⟩=−2α′​ηmnlog∣z−w∣2.
(49)
Their zero-mode integral gives momentum conservation, and their nonzero-mode Gaussian integral gives the Koba-Nielsen factor
(2π)DδD(∑a​pa​)∏a<b​∣za​−zb​∣α′pa​⋅pb​.
(50)
This explains the sphere tachyon position integral without needing its evaluation. For four tachyons the resulting Virasoro–Shapiro amplitude displays the exchanged string spectrum directly.
Use all-incoming external momenta and introduce u=−(p1​+p4​)2/(8πT) alongside the two printed dimensionless Mandelstam variables. Since pa2​=8πT and ∑a​pa​=0, one obtains s+t+u=−4. The physical center-of-mass energy squared in the s channel is sphys​=8πTs=4s/α′. The t channel measures the analogous crossed momentum transfer, with sign determined by the mostly-plus convention. Rewriting the Gamma factors in a symmetric form gives
A(s,t)=∏x=s,t,u​Γ(2+x)Γ(−1−x)​.
(51)
The Gamma function poles imply, at generic fixed values of the other invariant,
s=n−1ort=n−1,n=0,1,2,….​
(52)
The denominator Gamma factors can remove residues at special intersecting channel kinematics; the statement concerns a generic single-channel limit. An s-channel pole occurs when the intermediate momentum p1​+p2​ satisfies the mass-shell condition for a closed-string state:
Mn2​=8πT(n−1).
(53)
The pole at s=−1 exchanges the ground-state tachyon, the pole at s=0 exchanges massless states, and the positive integer poles exchange the infinite massive tower. The t-channel interpretation is the crossed version. Factorization means that each residue is a sum of products of couplings to intermediate physical states that couple to the chosen external particles. It need not expose every representation at that mass.
For example, the Gamma function recurrence and Gamma function residue at a nonpositive integer give the dimensionless Virasoro–Shapiro amplitude pole residue
Ress=n−1​A(s,t)=−(n!)2[(t+2)n​]2​,(t+2)n​=∏j=0n−1​(t+2+j).
(54)
The residue polynomial has degree 2n, consistent with maximum spin 2n in the exchanged level. The amplitude also has the corresponding u-channel poles by crossing symmetry.
The massless fields can be treated as target backgrounds rather than separate asymptotic insertions. Write the target metric as Gmn​=ηmn​+hmn​, introduce a Kalb–Ramond field Bmn​, and a dilaton Φ. In conventional Euclidean signs their string nonlinear sigma model action is
SE​[X;G,B,Φ]=4πα′1​∫d2z[g​gabGmn​(X)∂a​Xm∂b​Xn+iϵabBmn​(X)∂a​Xm∂b​Xn]+4π1​∫d2zg​Φ(X)R(2).
(55)
Here ϵab is the antisymmetric tensor density. The three backgrounds correspond to the graviton, antisymmetric tensor and scalar states at closed-string level one. Expanding the vacuum functional in h,B,Φ, then Fourier expanding the backgrounds, produces exactly their integrated string vertex operators. Its functional derivatives therefore generate the amplitudes with massless external strings. The connected vacuum functional organizes connected amplitudes; the spacetime effective action organizes the corresponding vertices after treating massless propagation consistently.
At momenta small compared with 1/α′​, massive string propagators can be expanded in powers of momenta over their masses. The analytic part of the amplitudes consequently determines local higher-derivative interactions, ordered by powers of α′. Massless exchange poles are retained through propagation of the massless fields, rather than expanded into local contact terms. Up to field redefinitions, the leading massless-sector action in the string-frame metric is
Sefftree​=2κ02​1​∫dDx−G​e−2Φ[R+4(∇Φ)2−121​Hmnp​Hmnp+O(α′)],H=dB.​
(56)
The terms denoted O(α′) contain additional derivatives, including curvature-squared terms in the bosonic theory. The expansion concerns the massless sector around the perturbative bosonic background; the tachyon instability remains and is not cured by omitting its field from this displayed action. Thus this is a formal perturbative effective description, not a claim of a stable bosonic vacuum.
Finally split the dilaton into a constant and its variation, Φ=Φ0​+ϕ. By the Gauss-Bonnet theorem, its dilaton Euler-characteristic weighting on a connected closed oriented surface follows from
SΦ0​​=4πΦ0​​∫Σ​g​R(2)=Φ0​χ(Σ)=Φ0​(2−2g).
(57)
Define the string coupling by gs​=eΦ0​. A genus-g path integral is weighted by
e−SΦ0​​=gs2g−2​.​
(58)
The sphere carries gs−2​, the torus carries gs0​, and each extra handle adds gs2​. At higher genus one integrates over complex-structure moduli as well as insertion points, with the associated antighost insertions supplying the correct moduli measure. With canonically normalized external vertices an N-point genus-g amplitude scales as gs2g−2+N​.
Summing connected worldsheets of every genus of a surface yields the string-loop effective action expansion
Seff​=g=0∑∞​gs2g−2​Sg​[G,B,ϕ;α′].​
(59)
Each coefficient has its own low-energy α′ expansion. The two parameters have different roles: α′ resolves finite string size through higher derivatives, while gs2​ counts additional string loops.
