For u∈E′, choose a cutoff function χ=1 on a neighborhood of its support. Its extension to smooth functions makes
F(z)=⟨ux​,χ(x)e−iz⋅x⟩
(12)
independent of the choice of cutoff. For real frequency, the definition of the distributional Fourier transform gives F(λ)=u(λ): interchange the pairing with the integral of a Schwartz function, using the finite-order estimate on the cutoff's compact support. On each compact set of complex frequencies the exponential and all its x derivatives have uniformly convergent power series. Continuity of the distribution allows termwise differentiation, with
∂zα​F(z)=⟨ux​,(−ix)αe−iz⋅x⟩.
(13)
Consequently F is entire on Cn.
For the precise exponential type, a fixed enlarged support would give an unnecessarily enlarged radius. Instead use the shrinking-cutoff exponential-type estimate. On one fixed compact neighborhood of the closed radius-δ ball, u has finite order of a distribution m. Choose χε​=1 near that ball, supported in the radius-(δ+ε) ball, with ∣∂αχε​∣≤Cα​ε−∣α∣ for 0<ε≤1/2. The finite-order estimate gives
∣F(z)∣≤Cε−m(1+∣z∣)me(δ+ε)∣Imz∣.
(14)
Take ε=[2(1+∣z∣)]−1. Its extra exponential is bounded by e1/2, while the derivative cost is polynomial. Thus
∣u(z)∣≤C′(1+∣z∣)2meδ∣Imz∣.​
(15)
The entire function was defined with a fixed cutoff; only its bound uses a frequency-dependent cutoff, so no holomorphic dependence is lost. This proves the required estimate with some N≥0 without enlarging δ.
For the converse, set V(z)=eiz⋅yU(z). Its restriction to real frequencies has polynomial growth. Define the inverse tempered distribution v by
⟨v,ϕ⟩=(2π)−n∫Rn​V(ξ)ϕ​(−ξ)dξ,ϕ∈S.
(16)
Rapid decay of the Schwartz function transform proves convergence and continuity, and Fourier inversion gives v=V on real frequencies.
We prove its support directly by contour shifting, rather than assuming the support conclusion of the Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem. Let ω be a real unit vector and take a test function ϕ supported in x⋅ω≥δ+ϵ for some ϵ>0. For every integer M≥0, integration by parts in the real-frequency Fourier integral gives
ϕ​(−ξ−itω)∣ϕ​(−ξ−itω)∣​=∫eiξ⋅xe−tω⋅xϕ(x)dx,≤CM​(1+t)2M(1+∣ξ∣2)−Me−t(δ+ϵ),t≥0.​
(17)
Indeed, move (1−Δx​)M from the oscillatory exponential to e−tω⋅xϕ; its derivatives supply at most 2M powers of t.
The product V(z)ϕ​(−z) is entire. Rotate coordinates so that ω is the first coordinate vector and apply Cauchy integral theorem to a rectangle in that one complex coordinate, integrating the remaining real coordinates afterwards. For fixed t, choose 2M>N+n+2; the displayed decay estimate, uniformly on the intervening imaginary segment, makes the vertical faces vanish as the real rectangle width tends to infinity. The horizontal integrals are absolutely convergent. Hence
⟨v,ϕ⟩=(2π)−n∫Rn​V(ξ+itω)ϕ​(−ξ−itω)dξ.
(18)
Using (1+∣ξ+itω∣)N≤C(1+t)N(1+∣ξ∣)N and the growth hypothesis bounds this pairing by
C′(1+t)N+2Me−ϵt∫Rn​(1+∣ξ∣)N(1+∣ξ∣2)−Mdξ.
(19)
It tends to zero as t→∞, so the pairing vanishes. Every point outside the closed radius-δ ball lies in such a separating half-space; a finite partition of unity for the support of a test function outside the ball proves suppv⊂B(0,δ).
Translate this compactly supported distribution by y: define ⟨u,ϕ⟩=⟨v,ϕ(⋅+y)⟩. The Translation property of the Fourier transform gives
u(ξ)=e−iξ⋅yV(ξ)=U(ξ),suppu⊂B(y,δ).​
(20)
The compact-support entire extension agrees with U everywhere by the identity theorem, applied successively in the complex coordinates. Fourier inversion also gives uniqueness. This completes both directions of the ball version of the Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem.
For the wave equation, take a real constant c=0. The Fourier transform method for the wave equation gives
∂t2​Et​(ξ)+c2∣ξ∣2Et​(ξ)=0,Et​(ξ)=cos(ct∣ξ∣)f​(ξ).​
(21)
This inverse tempered distribution is twice differentiable in t: time derivatives introduce polynomial frequency factors, still integrable against every Schwartz function. It has the specified initial displacement and zero initial velocity and satisfies the equation distributionally.
To apply the support theorem, replace the real norm by the entire wave cosine multiplier
Ct​(z)=∑j=0∞​(2j)!(−1)j(ct)2j(z⋅z)j​.
(22)
This series is entire and equals cos(ctz⋅z​) regardless of the square-root choice. Write z=a+ib and q=z⋅z​. Since ∣z⋅z∣≤∣a∣2+∣b∣2,
(Imq)2=2∣z⋅z∣−(∣a∣2−∣b∣2)​≤∣b∣2.
(23)
Together with ∣cosw∣≤e∣Imw∣, this yields ∣Ct​(z)∣≤e∣c∣t∣Imz∣. The forward estimate for the translated initial support now gives
∣eiz⋅yCt​(z)f​(z)∣≤C(1+∣z∣)Ne(δ+∣c∣t)∣Imz∣.
(24)
The converse therefore proves finite propagation with the stated speed:
suppEt​⊂B(y,δ+∣c∣t),t>0.​
(25)
In particular, z⋅z​ itself need not be entire; it is the even cosine series that provides the required entire extension.
For completeness this construction identifies the distributional Cauchy solution even without initially assuming spatial temperedness. The zero-displacement wave multiplier is Sτ​(z)=∫0τ​Cs​(z)ds, entire with bound ∣Sτ​(z)∣≤τe∣c∣τ∣Imz∣. Given a compactly supported smooth ϕ and final time T, the backward solution w(s)=F−1[ST−s​ϕ​] is smooth, has support in one compact ball for 0≤s≤T by the same support theorem, and satisfies w(T)=0, ws​(T)=−ϕ. For the difference h of two distributional solutions with zero initial data,
dsd​(⟨hs​,w⟩−⟨h,ws​⟩)=c2(⟨Δh,w⟩−⟨h,Δw⟩)=0.
(26)
All pairings use compactly supported test functions. The expression vanishes initially and equals ⟨h(T),ϕ⟩ finally. Hence h(T)=0, establishing uniqueness in the usual time-differentiable distributional solution class.
