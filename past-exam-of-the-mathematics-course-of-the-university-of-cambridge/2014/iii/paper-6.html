Use the Hausdorff convention for a locally convex space: a real vector space X is equipped with a separating family P of seminorms. Thus each p is nonnegative, subadditive and satisfies p(tx)=∣t∣p(x), and for every x=0 some p∈P has p(x)>0. The topology has a neighborhood basis at a∈X consisting of
a+{x:p1​(x)<ε1​,…,pm​(x)<εm​},pi​∈P,εi​>0.
(1)
Equivalently, it is the coarsest vector-space topology making all these seminorms continuous. The separating condition is precisely what makes this topology Hausdorff. If the Hausdorff requirement were omitted, continuous linear functionals could not distinguish points in the common kernel of the seminorms.
The continuous dual space X∗ consists of all continuous linear functionals X→R. We first prove the needed Hahn-Banach theorem. Let p be a real-valued sublinear function on X, meaning p(a+b)≤p(a)+p(b) and p(ta)=tp(a) for t≥0, and let g be linear on a vector subspace M, with g(m)≤p(m). To extend across v∈/M, write
g​(m+tv)=g(m)+tc.
(2)
The necessary bounds on c are
L=supm∈M​[g(m)−p(m−v)]≤c≤infn∈M​[p(n+v)−g(n)]=U.
(3)
They are compatible because
g(m)+g(n)=g(m+n)≤p(m+n)≤p(m−v)+p(n+v).
(4)
Taking one variable equal to zero also shows that L,U are finite. Choose c∈[L,U]. The upper bound proves domination when t>0, after dividing m+tv by t; the lower bound proves it when t<0, after dividing by −t. Thus g​≤p on M+Rv. This is the one-dimensional dominated extension of a real linear functional.
Order all dominated extensions of g by extension of their domains. A chain has an upper bound obtained by taking the union of the domains and linear functionals. Zorn's lemma gives a maximal extension, and the one-dimensional construction shows that its domain must be all of X. We have therefore proved the real dominated-extension theorem. In particular, when p is a seminorm, domination at both x and −x gives ∣f(x)∣≤p(x).
For x0​=0, choose a continuous seminorm p with p(x0​)>0, and define g(tx0​)=tp(x0​) on its one-dimensional span. Then ∣g(tx0​)∣≤p(tx0​). Extend by the theorem just proved to f with ∣f(x)∣≤p(x). This bound makes f continuous, and f(x0​)=p(x0​)>0. Applying this to the difference of two distinct points proves that X∗ separates the points of X, the continuous-dual separation theorem for Hausdorff locally convex spaces.
For separation from a closed linear subspace, choose a basic balanced neighborhood V of zero such that (x0​+V)∩Y=∅. Write
V={x:q(x)<1},q(x)=max1≤i≤m​εi​pi​(x)​.
(5)
Then q(x0​−y)≥1 for every y∈Y. On Y+Rx0​, define g(y+tx0​)=t, which is well-defined since x0​∈/Y. For t=0,
q(y+tx0​)=∣t∣q(x0​+y/t)≥∣t∣=∣g(y+tx0​)∣;
(6)
for t=0 the inequality is immediate. The proved extension theorem gives a continuous f dominated by q, with
f(x0​)=1,f∣Y​=0.​
(7)
This proves separation of a point from a closed linear subspace without using any unproved Hahn-Banach extension or separation result.

The weak-star topology σ(X∗,X) is the topology of pointwise convergence on X. A basic neighborhood of f0​ has the form
{f∈X∗:∣f(xj​)−f0​(xj​)∣<η, 1≤j≤m},xj​∈X,η>0.
(8)

J(BX​)​σ(X∗∗,X∗)=BX∗∗​.​
(9)
Here both balls are closed unit balls. Thus approximation is by finitely many dual evaluations at a time; the theorem does not assert norm density in the bidual space.

Consider the restriction operator
T:X⟶E∗,(Tx)(e)=e(x),a=x∗∗∣E​.
(10)
It is onto: otherwise its range, a vector subspace of the finite-dimensional vector space E∗, would have a nonzero annihilator in E∗∗=E. That annihilator would be an e∈E vanishing on all of X, so e=0, a contradiction. Moreover T is open. To see this directly, choose preimages of a basis of E∗; they define a linear right inverse S:E∗→X, continuous because its domain is finite-dimensional. Small changes of an image can then be lifted by small changes using S.
Put r=1+ε and C=T({x:∥x∥<r}). It is open and convex in E∗. If a∈/C, the Hahn-Banach separation theorem gives a nonzero real linear functional on E∗, represented by some e∈E, such that
a(e)≥supz∈C​z(e)=r∥e∥.
(11)
But a(e)=x∗∗(e)≤∥e∥, contradicting r>1. Therefore a∈C, and the finite-dimensional interpolation form of Goldstine's theorem yields
∥x∥<1+ε,e(x)=x∗∗(e) for every e∈E.​
(12)
If E={0} one simply takes x=0.
To recover the Goldstine theorem, start with x∗∗∈BX∗∗​ and finitely many tests e1​,…,em​∈X∗. Apply the result to their span with a small parameter δ>0. The resulting x need not lie in BX​, but v=x/(1+δ) does. For every test,
∣ej​(v)−x∗∗(ej​)∣=1+δδ​∣x∗∗(ej​)∣≤δ∥ej​∥.
(13)
Choosing δ sufficiently small puts Jv in any prescribed basic weak-star neighborhood. This proves the asserted weak-star density of the closed unit ball. The reverse inclusion follows because BX∗∗​ is weak-star closed, being the intersection of the conditions ∣x∗∗(e)∣≤∥e∥ for e∈X∗.

Let q:X∗→X∗/Y be the quotient map. Since Y is norm closed and has finite codimension, the quotient is a finite-dimensional normed space. Choose a basis of its continuous dual and compose its coordinate linear functionals with q. This gives ϕ1​,…,ϕn​∈X∗∗ such that
Y=⋂k=1n​kerϕk​,F=span{ϕ1​,…,ϕn​}.
(14)
If Jx∈F, it vanishes on Y. Evaluation at x is weak-star continuous, and Y is weak-star dense, so it vanishes on all of X∗. The dual norm formula gives x=0. Hence F∩JX={0}.
Assume X={0}; the zero space is trivially normed by any positive constant. We claim that
d=dist(SX​,F)>0.​
(15)
If not, there would be xj​∈SX​ and ψj​∈F with ∥Jxj​−ψj​∥→0. The sequence ψj​ is bounded. Finite-dimensionality of F gives a norm-convergent subsequence with limit ψ∈F. Then Jxj​→ψ, and JX is norm closed because X is Banach. Thus ψ∈F∩JX and ∥ψ∥=1, a contradiction. This is positive distance between a unit sphere and a disjoint finite-dimensional subspace.
Fix x∈SX​, identifying it with Jx, and put δ=dist(x,F)≥d. On F+Rx, define h(ϕ+tx)=tδ. The distance definition gives
∣h(ϕ+tx)∣=∣t∣δ≤∥ϕ+tx∥.
(16)
The Hahn-Banach theorem extends it to Ψ∈X∗∗∗ with ∥Ψ∥≤1, Ψ∣F​=0 and Ψ(x)=δ.
Apply part (ii) with the Banach space X∗, its finite-dimensional dual vector subspace E=F+Rx⊆X∗∗, and the bidual space element Ψ∈X∗∗∗. For every η>0 it supplies yη​∈X∗ satisfying
∥yη​∥<1+η,ϕ(yη​)=0 (ϕ∈F),yη​(x)=δ.
(17)
Thus yη​∈Y. Normalize by 1+η and let η↓0 to obtain
supy∈Y, ∥y∥≤1​y(x)≥δ≥d.
(18)
Homogeneity gives, for all x∈X,
d∥x∥≤y∈Y, ∥y∥≤1sup​y(x).​
(19)
Consequently a finite-codimensional weak-star dense dual subspace is norming, with c=d. Since Y is a real linear vector subspace and its ball is symmetric, the same supremum is obtained if an absolute value is inserted. The argument uses near-unit interpolation and a limiting supremum, not an assertion that the supremum is attained.

Suppose the weak-star closure of Z were a proper linear vector subspace of X∗. The Hahn-Banach separation theorem would give a nonzero weak-star continuous linear functional vanishing on it. A continuous dual of a weak-star topology consists precisely of evaluations at points of X: continuity bounds the linear functional by finitely many evaluations, so it factors through their finite-dimensional coordinate map and is a linear combination of them. Thus some 0=x∈X would satisfy z(x)=0 for all z∈Z. The norming inequality would imply c∥x∥≤0, a contradiction. Every norming subspace of X∗ is weak-star dense.
For the infinite-codimension example, take
X=ℓ1(N;R),X∗=ℓ∞(N;R),Z=c0​.​
(20)
Here X is the absolutely summable sequence space and c0​ is the space of sequences converging to zero, a norm-closed vector subspace of ℓ∞. The dual identification is y(x)=∑n​xn​yn​: a bounded sequence defines a linear functional of norm ∥y∥∞​, and every linear functional on ℓ1 has this form by evaluating on the coordinate vectors and using density of finitely supported sequences.
For x∈ℓ1, use the finitely supported sequence y(N) whose first N entries are sgn(xn​) and whose remaining entries vanish. It belongs to c0​, has norm at most one, and
y(N)(x)=∑n=1N​∣xn​∣⟶∥x∥1​.
(21)
The reverse inequality follows from ∣y(x)∣≤∥y∥∞​∥x∥1​. Hence c0​ is 1-norming for ℓ1, an instance of vanishing sequences norm the summable sequence space.
To prove infinite codimension, take disjoint infinite sets
Aj​={2j−1(2m−1):m≥1},j≥1.
(22)
Their indicator sequences have linearly independent classes in ℓ∞/c0​. Indeed, a finite combination has the constant value aj​ on Aj​; if it tends to zero, every aj​ must vanish. Thus the quotient is infinite-dimensional.

The Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem says that a positive linear functional on C(K) has the form f↦∫K​fdν for a unique finite positive regular Borel measure ν, with norm ν(K). Its complex form says that every bounded complex linear functional is represented by a unique finite regular complex measure μ, and
C(K)∗≅M(K),Lμ​(f)=∫K​fdμ,∥Lμ​∥=∣μ∣(K).​
(23)
The extension from positive to arbitrary linear functionals follows by positive/negative decomposition of real linear functionals and then real/imaginary decomposition. The last quantity is the total variation norm of a measure, so this identifies the dual isometrically.
An extreme point e of a convex set C cannot be written as e=ta+(1−t)b with 0<t<1 and distinct a,b∈C. The Krein-Milman theorem, applied with the underlying real locally convex weak-star topology, states
C=conv(extC)​w∗,extC=∅.​
(24)
We next prove Milman's converse to the Krein-Milman theorem. Suppose an extreme point e were outside the weak-star closure of S. A basic weak-star neighborhood of e disjoint from S uses finitely many real coordinates: real and imaginary parts of evaluations at elements of X. Its complement is the union of finitely many closed half-spaces
ℓj​(c)−ℓj​(e)≥εj​orℓj​(c)−ℓj​(e)≤−εj​.
(25)
Intersect these with C, discard empty intersections, and call the resulting compact convex sets C1​,…,Cm​. They cover S, and none contains e.
The convex hull of their union is compact. Every point in it can be written ∑i=1m​ti​ci​ with (ti​) in the finite simplex and ci​∈Ci​, by combining terms from the same convex set. The map from the simplex times ∏i​Ci​ to that sum is weak-star continuous, so its image is compact and closed. It therefore contains convSw∗=C, in particular e. But extremality forces every ci​ having a positive coefficient in a representation of e to equal e, contradicting e∈/Ci​. Hence
extC⊆Sw∗.​
(26)
Assume K=∅. We claim that the extreme points of the dual unit ball of C(K) are
extBC(K)∗​={αδt​:t∈K, ∣α∣=1}.​
(27)
A measure of norm less than one is not extreme, since it admits a small nonzero perturbation ±ηδt​ within the ball. For a measure μ of norm one, suppose ∣μ∣ is not a point mass. There is a Borel set E with 0<a=∣μ∣(E)<1: if the measure support has two points, choose disjoint neighborhoods of positive mass; a regular probability measure supported at just one point is the corresponding point mass. Then
μ=aaμ∣E​​+(1−a)1−aμ∣K∖E​​
(28)
is a convex combination of distinct norm-one measures. Thus an extreme measure must have variation concentrated at one point, and must be αδt​ with ∣α∣=1.
Conversely, if αδt​=(ν+η)/2 with ∥ν∥,∥η∥≤1, then
1=​2ν({t})+η({t})​​≤2∣ν({t})∣+∣η({t})∣​≤1.
(29)
Equality throughout forces both measures to have all their variation at t, and forces their phases to be α. Hence ν=η=αδt​, proving extremality. If K=∅, the dual ball is {0} and its sole extreme point is 0.
Every finite Borel measure on [0,1] is regular, so the given μ belongs to the dual unit ball of C[0,1]. Apply Banach-Alaoglu theorem and Krein-Milman theorem to that ball. It is the weak-star closed convex hull of these phased point masses. The unit ball of the finite-dimensional vector subspace F is norm compact. Choose a finite ε/4-net f1​,…,fm​ in it. There is a convex combination
ν=∑i=1N​ai​αi​δwi​​,ai​≥0,∑i​ai​=1,∣αi​∣=1,
(30)
whose integrals differ from those of μ by less than ε/2 on every fj​. Put ti​=ai​αi​. Then ∑i​∣ti​∣=1 and ∥ν∥≤1. For any f in the unit ball of F, choose fj​ with ∥f−fj​∥<ε/4. The linear functional Lμ​−Lν​ has norm at most two, so
​∫fdμ−∑i​ti​f(wi​)​<2ε​+24ε​=ε.
(31)
Scaling yields the requested estimate for every f∈F. This is atomic approximation on finite-dimensional spaces of continuous functions. Repeated nodes are allowed: keeping their individual terms preserves the exact sum of coefficient magnitudes even if their phases cancel. If F={0}, one point with coefficient one suffices.

Give O(U) the compact-open topology, that is, uniform convergence on compact subsets. Put R(z)=(z1−x)−1. Choose a finite oriented contour Γ in U∖σ(x) with winding number one on σ(x) and zero outside U; boundaries of a finite union of sufficiently small rectangles around the compact spectrum provide such a contour. Define the holomorphic functional calculus by
Θx​(f)=2πi1​∫Γ​f(z)R(z)dz.​
(32)
The integral is a Bochner integral along the piecewise smooth contour. It is independent of the admissible contour by the Cauchy integral theorem, since the resolvent is holomorphic off the spectrum. In particular, restricting f to a smaller open spectral neighborhood leaves its value unchanged.
Linearity is immediate, and
∥Θx​(f)∥≤2πlength(Γ)​maxz∈Γ​∥R(z)∥maxz∈Γ​∣f(z)∣
(33)
proves continuity for the compact-open topology. For the constant and coordinate functions, move the corresponding resolvent integrals to a large circle. The norm-convergent expansion R(z)=∑n≥0​xnz−n−1 there gives Θx​(1)=1 and Θx​(u)=x.
To check multiplication, take two nested admissible contours, with z on the outer one and w on the inner one. The resolvent identity gives
R(z)R(w)=z−wR(w)−R(z)​.
(34)
In the double integral for Θx​(f)Θx​(g), the term with R(w) integrates in z to f(w)R(w)g(w) by the Cauchy integral formula. The term with R(z) integrates in w to zero, because z lies outside the inner region. Thus Θx​(fg)=Θx​(f)Θx​(g). Nested regions can be chosen with closures inside U, so the argument also handles disconnected U and contours with several boundary components.
For uniqueness, use the following form of the Runge theorem: rational functions whose poles lie outside an open set U are dense in O(U) for uniform convergence on compact subsets; polynomials are included, with a pole at infinity allowed. This follows by a Runge exhaustion of U; no connectedness of U is required. Any unital algebra homomorphism sending u to x must send (u−a)−1 to (x−a1)−1 for a∈/U. Consequently it agrees with the constructed map on every such rational function. Continuity and density give uniqueness. This is continuity and uniqueness of holomorphic functional calculus.
For the holomorphic spectral mapping theorem, first suppose μ∈/f(σ(x)). The reciprocal g=1/(f−μ) is holomorphic on some smaller open neighborhood V of σ(x), even if it is not holomorphic on all of U. Restriction consistency and multiplicativity on V show that Θx​(g) is an inverse of Θx​(f)−μ1. Thus σ(Θx​(f))⊆f(σ(x)).
Conversely, if λ∈σ(x), then
h(z)=z−λf(z)−f(λ)​
(35)
is holomorphic on U, with the singularity at λ removed. Hence
Θx​(f)−f(λ)1=(x−λ1)Θx​(h).
(36)
In a commutative algebra, an invertible product has invertible factors. The displayed product cannot be invertible because x−λ1 is not. Therefore
σ(Θx​(f))=f(σ(x)).​
(37)
For the operator conclusion, work over complex scalars and let σ(T)=K1​∪K2​ be a partition into disjoint nonempty compact sets. To apply the preceding commutative-algebra result precisely, take the closed unital subalgebra of B(X) generated by T and all its resolvents (zI−T)−1, z∈/σB(X)​(T). The generators commute, so this is a resolvent-generated commutative algebra. Its spectrum of T is exactly the operator spectrum: every resolvent outside the latter was included, and an inverse inside the subalgebra would also be an inverse in B(X).
Choose disjoint open neighborhoods U1​,U2​ of K1​,K2​, and let f equal one on U1​ and zero on U2​. The corresponding Riesz projection P=f(T) satisfies
P2=P,PT=TP,σ(P)={0,1}.​
(38)
The last spectrum is computed in the chosen commutative algebra and already ensures P=0,I. Its range Y=PX=ker(I−P) is closed, nonzero and proper. The commutation identity makes it invariant under T. This proves that a disconnected spectrum yields a nontrivial invariant subspace. Using all resolvents avoids inadvertently replacing the operator spectrum by the possibly larger spectrum in a polynomial-generated subalgebra.

A character of an algebra is a nonzero complex linear multiplicative map ϕ:A→C. It necessarily satisfies ϕ(1)=1. If λ=ϕ(x), then x−λ1 cannot be invertible, since applying ϕ to an inverse identity would give 0=1. Therefore ϕ(x)∈σ(x). The Neumann series bound σ(x)⊆{z:∣z∣≤∥x∥} gives
∣ϕ(x)∣≤∥x∥,​
(39)
proving automatic continuity of characters. In particular every algebra character belongs to the closed unit ball of A∗; no prior continuity was needed.
The character space of an algebra is ΦA​, the set of all algebra characters. Its Gelfand topology is the weakest topology making every evaluation ϕ↦ϕ(x) continuous, equivalently the weak-star topology inherited from A∗. Within the weak-star compact ball BA∗​, it is the closed set specified by
ϕ(1)=1,ϕ(ab)=ϕ(a)ϕ(b)(a,b∈A).
(40)
These equations are closed conditions on finitely many evaluations at a time. By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, ΦA​ is compact, and it is Hausdorff because distinct characters differ on an evaluation.
To obtain every spectral value from a algebra character, take λ∈σ(x). Commutativity makes the ideal (x−λ1)A proper, so it is contained in an algebraic maximal ideal m. Every such maximal ideal is norm closed: its closure is an ideal, and cannot be all of A, because an element of m sufficiently close to 1 would be invertible by the Neumann series. Maximality then makes its closure equal to itself.
The quotient A/m is a complex Banach division algebra, hence is C by the Gelfand-Mazur theorem. Composing the quotient map with this scalar identification gives a algebra character with ϕ(x)=λ. Together with the first inclusion,
σ(x)={ϕ(x):ϕ∈ΦA​}.​
(41)
The same maximal-ideal argument shows that the algebra character space of a nonzero algebra is nonempty.
Now suppose A is a commutative C-star algebra. All its elements are normal. The C-star identity gives
∥x2∥2=∥(x∗)2x2∥=∥(x∗x)2∥=∥x∗x∥2=∥x∥4,
(42)
since the self-adjoint element h=x∗x satisfies ∥h2∥=∥h∥2. Iterating yields ∥x2n∥=∥x∥2n. The spectral radius formula along this subsequence proves
r(x)=∥x∥.​
(43)
This is spectral radius norm equality for normal elements, applied to the commutative case.
For a self-adjoint h, the element vt​=exp(ith) is unitary for every real t, so ∥vt​∥=1. Algebra character continuity and multiplicativity give
ϕ(vt​)=exp(itϕ(h)),∣exp(itϕ(h))∣≤1(t∈R).
(44)
Its modulus is exp(−tImϕ(h)), forcing ϕ(h)∈R. Write x=h+ik with h,k self-adjoint. Then
ϕ(x∗)=ϕ(h)−iϕ(k)=ϕ(x)​.​
(45)
This proves that characters of a C-star algebra respect the involution. The conjugation bar is present in the original PDF and must be retained; the TeX aid omits it.
The Commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem states that a complex commutative unital C-star algebra is isometrically star-isomorphic to C(K) for a compact Hausdorff space, canonically K=ΦA​. Its Gelfand transform is
G:A⟶C(ΦA​),x(ϕ)=ϕ(x).
(46)
It is a unital algebra homomorphism; the conjugation identity just proved makes it preserve the involution. The algebra character description of the spectrum and the spectral-radius equality give
∥x∥∞​=ϕ∈ΦA​sup​∣ϕ(x)∣=r(x)=∥x∥.​
(47)
Thus it is injective and isometric, and its range is closed because A is complete. The range contains constants, is closed under complex conjugation, and separates points of ΦA​: two distinct algebra characters differ on some element of A.
The complex Stone-Weierstrass theorem says that a self-adjoint unital subalgebra of C(K) separating points is uniformly dense. For completeness, its familiar lattice argument explains the last step. The real part of its uniform closure is closed under absolute value, by polynomial approximation of ∣t∣ on bounded real intervals, and hence under finite maxima and minima. Constants and point separation permit a real function matching any given real continuous g at any chosen pair of points. Fixing the first point, take a maximum of finitely many such functions to obtain a function above g−ε everywhere and equal to g at that first point. It is below g+ε in a neighborhood of that point. A finite cover by these neighborhoods and the minimum of their associated functions then lies between g−ε and g+ε everywhere. Real and imaginary parts give density for complex functions.
Apply this to G(A). Its range is both dense and closed, so it is all of C(ΦA​). Therefore
A≅C(ΦA​)as unital C-star algebras, isometrically.​
(48)
This proves the commutative theorem in the setting of the question. For the zero algebra the corresponding compact space is empty and the representation is C(∅)={0}.
