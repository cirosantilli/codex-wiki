The Roth theorem on three-term arithmetic progressions says that, for each fixed δ>0, a sufficiently long interval cannot have a subset of density of a finite subset at least δ with no nonconstant three-term arithmetic progression. Equivalently, the maximum size of a three-term-progression-free subset of [N]={1,…,N} is o(N). Here is a density increment proof, including its analytic ingredients.
Write e(t)=exp(2πit) and embed I=[N] into the cyclic group G=Z/(2N+1)Z of order M. For normalized Fourier analysis on a finite abelian group take h(r)=M−1∑x​h(x)e(−rx/M). The orthogonality of complex exponentials follows from a finite geometric series: its average is zero for a nonzero frequency and one for frequency zero. Expanding in these linear phases consequently proves both Fourier inversion on a finite group and the Parseval identity on a finite group. In particular,
Ex​∣h(x)∣2=∑r​∣h(r)∣2,Λ(h0​,h1​,h2​):=Ex,d​h0​(x)h1​(x+d)h2​(x+2d)=∑r​h0​(r)h1​(−2r)h2​(r).
(1)
The second identity follows by summing the frequency expansion over x,d: the two constraints leave precisely the frequencies (r,−2r,r). Since M is odd, multiplication by two permutes the frequencies. Also a congruence x+z=2y with x,y,z∈I is an equality in the integers, since ∣x+z−2y∣<M.
Let A⊆I have subset density α=∣A∣/N≥δ, and suppose it contains no nonconstant three-term arithmetic progression. Its balanced indicator function of a finite subset is f=1A​−α1I​, extended by zero outside I. Then f​(0)=0. Counting endpoints with the same parity gives
Λ(1A​,1A​,1A​)=M2αN​,Λ(α1I​,α1I​,α1I​)=M2α3⌈N2/2⌉​.
(2)
If N≥4α−2, their difference in absolute value is at least α3N2/(4M2). Telescope the difference of the products into three terms, each containing f and two factors chosen from 1A​,α1I​. Put s=maxr​∣f​(r)∣. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the Parseval identity on a finite group bound each term by sαN/M, since the squared normalized L2 norm of either other factor is at most αN/M. Therefore some nonzero frequency r satisfies
s≥12Mα2N​≥36α2​,​∑x∈I​f(x)e(−rx/M)​≥36α2N​.
(3)
This is the Fourier detection of a progression-free subset of an interval step; it has just been proved rather than assumed.
We now turn this Fourier coefficient on a finite abelian group into a density increment. Set Q=⌊N​⌋. Partition the unit interval into Q equal pieces and place the Q+1 fractional parts of 0,r/M,…,Qr/M in them. The pigeonhole principle gives 1≤q≤Q with ∥qr/M∥≤1/Q. This proves the particular Dirichlet approximation theorem needed here. Put κ=α2/(1000π) and L=⌊κQ⌋. For sufficiently large N depending only on δ, L≥κQ/2 and 2L≤Q. Each residue chain modulo q in I has at least Q elements. Split each such chain into arithmetic progressions with between L and 2L−1 elements, absorbing its final short remainder into the previous block.
The linear phase changes by at most 4πL/Q≤α2/250 within any block. Freezing the linear phase at the first element of each block therefore changes the preceding correlation by at most α2N/250, because ∣f∣≤1. The triangle inequality gives
∑P​​∑x∈P​f(x)​≥72α2N​.
(4)
The block sums of the balanced indicator function of a finite subset add to zero, so their positive total is half their absolute total. Some block consequently satisfies
∣P∣≥cα2N​,∣P∣∣A∩P∣​≥α+144α2​.​
(5)
Here c>0 is absolute, and shrinking it absorbs the floors. An affine parametrization of P identifies A∩P with a subset of [∣P∣] and preserves arithmetic progressions.
Repeat this Roth density-increment step. Every step raises the subset density by at least δ2/144, so fewer than T=⌈144/δ2⌉+1 steps are possible. All the lengths satisfy Nj+1​≥cδ2Nj​​ while above a threshold depending only on δ. Choose the initial N so large that this lower recursion stays above that threshold for T steps; this is possible by working backwards through finitely many squarings. We would then force a subset density greater than one. Thus every fixed positive density eventually forces a nonconstant three-term arithmetic progression. For an infinite subset of the positive integers with positive upper asymptotic density, apply the finite conclusion along initial intervals whose subset density is bounded away from zero; this gives the usual infinite formulation as well.

The high-energy Roth theorem concludes that, for fixed θ>0, a sufficiently large finite set of integers with additive energy at least θ∣A∣3 contains a nonconstant three-term arithmetic progression. The size threshold depends on θ, and there is no assumption about the diameter of A.
Here is why additive energy replaces interval subset density. The Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem in the small-difference set form proved in Question 3 supplies B⊆A with ∣B∣≥cθ​∣A∣ and ∣B−B∣≤Kθ​∣B∣. The Petridis minimal-growth lemma, the Ruzsa triangle inequality, and the Ruzsa modeling lemma, all proved there, then give a subset B′⊆B of size at least ∣B∣/16 and a Freiman s-isomorphism of order eight onto D⊆Z/qZ, where
∣B′∣≤q≤Cθ​∣B′∣,∣D∣/q≥Cθ−1​.
(6)
Represent D by residues in {0,…,q−1}. One of the two consecutive half-intervals has subset density bounded below by a positive constant depending only on θ; their lengths tend to infinity with ∣A∣. Apply the Roth theorem on three-term arithmetic progressions proved in part (i) to this interval. The resulting three distinct residues satisfy d0​+d2​=2d1​ modulo q. The inverse Freiman homomorphism preserves that equality and distinctness, so its preimages form a nonconstant three-term arithmetic progression in B′, hence in A.
Large additive energy therefore gives the same three-term progression conclusion after passing to a dense finite model. A forward reference to the detailed lemmas in Question 3 avoids reproving them here.

We will prove a polynomial progression in a high-energy fourfold difference set. The route is to extract a large subset with a small difference set, construct a dense Freiman s-isomorphism model, and use the Bogolyubov lemma and the pigeonhole principle. To respect the proof requirement, every ingredient beyond the elementary rules for Freiman homomorphisms is established below.
Assume n≥1. Since three entries of an additive quadruple determine the fourth, E(A)≤n3, so a nonvacuous hypothesis has 0<θ≤1. Let r(t)=∣{(a,b)∈A2:a+b=t}∣. Then E(A)=∑t​r(t)2 and ∑t​r(t)=n2. Define the popular sum set S={t:r(t)≥θn/2}. The contribution to additive energy from its complement is at most θn3/2. Since r(t)≤n, the bipartite graph on two copies of A whose edges have sums in S has at least θn2/2 edges. Moreover ∣S∣≤2n/θ.
Put δ=θ/2 and τ=δ2/16. Here is a dependent random choice argument producing the small difference set. Independently choose two right vertices, allowing repetition, and let X be their common left neighbourhood. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives E∣X∣≥δ2n. Call an ordered pair of left vertices bad if its common right neighbourhood has fewer than τn elements. The expected number b(X) of bad ordered pairs in X is at most τ2n2, because a fixed bad pair survives both random choices with probability at most τ2. Thus
E(∣X∣−δ2n64b(X)​)≥43​δ2n.

Choose a realization attaining at least this expectation. Then ∣X∣≥3δ2n/4, and b(X)≤δ2n∣X∣/64≤∣X∣2/48. Remove from X every vertex having more than ∣X∣/4 bad partners in X. At most ∣X∣/12 vertices are removed. The remaining set B has ∣B∣≥δ2n/2, and any a,b∈B have at least ∣X∣/2 vertices z∈X for which both (a,z) and (z,b) are good.
For such a z, there are at least τn right vertices y adjacent to a,z, and at least τn right vertices y′ adjacent to z,b. These paths give

a representation with four members of S. For fixed a,b, the tuple of four sums determines y,z,y′ uniquely, so there are at least ∣X∣τ2n2/2 distinct tuples. Select one ordered pair a,b for each member of B−B. Tuples belonging to different differences are disjoint. Counting all possible tuples in S4 proves
∣B−B∣≤∣X∣τ2n22∣S∣4​≤Dθ​n,Dθ​=222θ−10.

In particular, with m=∣B∣, we have m≥θ2n/8 and ∣B−B∣≤Km for K=225θ−12. This is the required small-difference set form of the Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem, proved by counting paths rather than cited.
We next control higher iterated sumsets. Choose a nonempty X⊆B minimizing ρ=∣X−B∣/∣X∣, so ρ≤K. We prove the Petridis minimal-growth lemma in the form ∣X−B+C∣≤ρ∣X+C∣ for finite C. Order C={c1​,…,ct​}, and let Xi​ consist of those x∈X for which x+ci​ is new in the union of the translates X+cj​. Then ∑i​∣Xi​∣=∣X+C∣. Every member of (X∖Xi​)−B+ci​ already occurs in an earlier translate of X−B. By minimality, ∣(X∖Xi​)−B∣≥ρ∣X∖Xi​∣, also when the complement is empty. Thus the new contribution from X−B+ci​ is at most ρ∣Xi​∣. Sum over i to prove the lemma. Iterating with C=−(j−1)B gives ∣X−jB∣≤Kj∣X∣.
For completeness, the Ruzsa triangle inequality in the needed form is
∣U−W∣∣V∣≤∣U−V∣∣V−W∣.
(17)
For each d∈U−W, fix d=ud​−wd​. The map (d,v)↦(ud​−v,v−wd​) is injective: adding the coordinates recovers d, and then v. Take U=kB, W=ℓB, V=X and apply the preceding bounds. We obtain the Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality
∣kB−ℓB∣≤Kk+ℓ∣X∣≤Kk+ℓm.

In particular T=8B−8B has at most K16m elements.
We now prove a slightly wasteful version of the Ruzsa modeling lemma, avoiding any assumption about the diameter of B. Set q=8∣T∣. Choose a prime p larger than all ∣t∣, t∈T, and much larger than q. Arbitrarily large primes exist by the elementary argument that a prime divisor of one plus the product of all primes up to a given bound exceeds that bound. For a uniformly chosen nonzero multiplier r modulo p, each nonzero t∈T has rt uniform among the nonzero residues. Call a multiplier forbidden if some such rt agrees modulo p with jq, where ∣jq∣<p. The Freiman s-isomorphism induces a bijection Φ:2B′−2B′→2D−2D: define it on a representation by taking the same two positive and two negative images. Equality of representations uses an order-four relation, and injectivity uses the converse relation. If three consecutive members yj−1​,yj​,yj+1​ of P satisfy yj−1​+yj+1​=2yj​, their preimages satisfy the same equality, since its expansion is an equality of eight-term sums in B′. A clean proof outline uses hypergraph removal rather than the quadratic density increment route. The structure is: establish removal for the three-uniform tetrahedron, encode four-term arithmetic progressions as its copies, and use the many edge-disjoint copies coming from constant progressions to contradict removal. We give the technical regularity stage in outline, as requested, and prove the counting inequality and the encoding that make the argument work.
The needed tetrahedron removal lemma says that for each η>0 there is ρ>0 such that a three-uniform hypergraph on v vertices with fewer than ρv4 copies of K43​ can be made K43​-free by removing fewer than ηv3 hyperedges. Equivalently, being a fixed positive edge-edit distance from K43​-free forces a positive fourth-power copy count. Constants and whether copies are ordered are immaterial after rescaling ρ.
Here is the global proof of this hypergraph removal input. Strong regularity for three-uniform hypergraphs partitions both vertices and the bipartite pair sets between vertex classes. The resulting three-class cells, called triads in a hypergraph regularity partition, are supported on three pair cells. On all but a prescribed small total weight, the triple-edge function has approximately constant relative density and a small relative three-dimensional box norm. This two-level refinement is essential: a vertex partition alone does not capture correlations carried by pairs. The regularity construction uses the squared L2 norms of conditional expectations of edge indicators as bounded energies. Refinement increases this energy by the squared L2 norm of the difference between the new and old conditional expectations, by orthogonality. Thus a nonuniformity witness causing a definite discrepancy causes a definite energy increment. A witness to nonuniformity refines the relevant pair or triple partition and raises the appropriate energy. A hierarchy of tolerances, with sufficiently strong refinement at the pair level, makes these increases terminate after a bounded number of stages. Bounds may depend very badly on the requested tolerance, which is harmless for a qualitative theorem.
Delete hyperedges with two vertices in the same vertex class, hyperedges meeting exceptional classes or irregular cells, hyperedges supported on pair cells of very small density, and hyperedges in triads of very small triple density. Choose the cutoffs and error hierarchy so that the total deleted is less than ηv3. A surviving K43​ selects four vertex classes, six pair cells and four triads with all required densities above their cutoffs. The relative tetrahedron counting lemma gives at least ρv4 copies in those cells. This contradicts the assumed sparse copy count, and proves the tetrahedron removal lemma. The strong regularity and relative counting estimates are the technical portion outlined here; the dependence of their constants is not needed.
To show the key analytic mechanism in that counting step, define the three-dimensional box norm of a complex function on X1​×X2​×X3​ by
∥g∥□38​=Exj0​,xj1​​∏ω∈{0,1}3​C∣ω∣g(x1ω1​​,x2ω2​​,x3ω3​​),
(25)
where C denotes complex conjugation. If the other three functions are bounded by one, then
∣Ex0​,x1​,x2​,x3​​g(x1​,x2​,x3​)g1​(x0​,x2​,x3​)g2​(x0​,x1​,x3​)g3​(x0​,x1​,x2​)∣≤∥g∥□3​.
(26)
For a proof, fix x0​ first. Apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality over (x2​,x3​) to remove g1​, duplicating x1​. Next apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality over the variables other than x2​ to remove the two factors from g2​, duplicating x2​. A third application removes the four factors from g3​ and duplicates x3​. The resulting eighth power is exactly the cube average displayed above. Averaging over x0​ proves the inequality. This also shows nonnegativity of the cube average by its successive squared-sum form.
For example, on complete pair supports, if four triple-edge functions fi​ have constant parts di​ and ∥fi​−di​∥□3​≤ξ, telescope the fourfold product and apply this inequality to each term. Their hypergraph clique density differs from d0​d1​d2​d3​ by at most 4ξ, so it is positive when 4ξ<∏i​di​. On general pair supports the same successive squaring argument is combined with sufficiently regular pair cells; choosing relative errors small compared with the pair-density product gives the relative tetrahedron counting lemma. This explains both the counting step and why its support must be regularized before the triple-edge densities. It supplies a proved important step without pretending that the strong regularity theorem is a one-line assertion.
We now prove the four-term progression hypergraph encoding completely. Let A⊆[N] have subset density at least δ>0, choose M=4N+1, and take four disjoint copies X0​,…,X3​ of the cyclic group G=Z/MZ. Put a hyperedge on the three vertices excluding part i precisely when
Li​((xj​)j=i​)=∑j=i​(j−i)xj​∈A.
(27)
For a transversal quadruple let S0​=∑j​xj​ and S1​=∑j​jxj​. Its four edge conditions are S1​−iS0​∈A, i=0,1,2,3. Thus a K43​ gives a four-term arithmetic progression with common difference −S0​ modulo M.
Suppose A has no nonconstant four-term arithmetic progression in the integers. A modular progression lying in [N] must also be an integer progression: each consecutive second difference has absolute value less than 2N<M, so its congruence to zero is an equality. Therefore every K43​ in this hypergraph has S0​=0, and all four values equal some a∈A. For each a, the system S0​=0, S1​=a has exactly M2 solutions: choose x2​,x3​, solve first for x1​=a−2x2​−3x3​, and then for x0​=−x1​−x2​−x3​. There are exactly ∣A∣M2=O(M3) copies of K43​.
These copies are edge-disjoint. Indeed, an edge missing part i has exactly one completion with S0​=0, obtained by setting xi​=−∑j=i​xj​. Its common value is the already prescribed Li​∈A. Hence destroying all copies requires at least ∣A∣M2 edge deletions. Since M≤5N, this is at least δM3/5. Apply the tetrahedron removal lemma to the v=4M vertices with, for instance, η=δ/640. It would permit fewer than δM3/10 deletions once M is large: the copy count O(M3) is eventually below its fixed threshold ρ(4M)4. This is impossible.
Every fixed positive density therefore forces a nonconstant four-term arithmetic progression in sufficiently long intervals. This proves the length-four case of the Szemerédi theorem. Applying it to sufficiently large initial intervals along a subsequence witnessing positive upper asymptotic density also gives the usual infinite-set formulation.
