Put Q(u)=inf{x∈R:F(x)≥u} for 0<u<1. This is the quantile function, rather than an ordinary inverse requiring strict increase. The limits of the distribution function at infinity make Q(u) finite, and the fact that F is a right-continuous function ensures F(Q(u))≥u. Consequently
Q(u)≤x⟺u≤F(x).
(1)
Increasing u shrinks the set in the infimum, so the quantile function is a monotone function. To prove the left continuity of the quantile function, fix u and let L=supv<u​Q(v)≤Q(u). If L<Q(u), choose L<y<Q(u). Then F(y)<u, so some v∈(F(y),u) satisfies Q(v)>y, a contradiction. Thus Q(v)↑Q(u) as v↑u. At a flat stretch of the distribution function, the quantile function may jump immediately to the right, consistent with this left-continuous convention.

P{U(j)​≤u}=∑r=jn​(rn​)ur(1−u)n−r,0<u<1.

Differentiating makes adjacent terms telescope: use r(rn​)=n(r−1n−1​) and (n−r)(rn​)=n(rn−1​). The probability density function is therefore
gj​(u)=(j−1)!(n−j)!n!​uj−1(1−u)n−j1{0<u<1}​.​
(3)
In particular, U(j)​ has the Beta distribution with parameters j,n−j+1.
Write m=Q(1/2) for the specified median. Continuity of the distribution function gives F(m)=1/2 and no mass at m, even if the distribution function has a flat stretch there. Thus B=∑i​1{Xi​<m}​ has the binomial distribution with parameters n,1/2. Except on a null event, the order-statistic confidence interval for a median covers m exactly when j≤B≤n−j. For the lower bound over the whole function class, the absence of a uniform bound on derivatives permits a particularly direct argument. It also avoids assuming a normal distribution for errors when the model only supplies their first two moments. Fix n and x0​. Choose a smooth bump function b on the interval, with b(x0​)=1, whose support contains no design point except possibly x0​. Such a bump exists because the design is finite; at an endpoint use the restriction of a smooth bump on the real line. Every ma​=ab, a∈R, belongs to the differentiability class.
If x0​ is not a design point, all observation means are zero for every a. The Le Cam two-point lemma applied to a=±A has total variation distance zero and gives a lower bound tending to infinity with A. In fact, the supremum of the mean squared error is infinite at every such x0​.
If x0​ is a design point, only its response depends on a. The resulting location family is the single observation Y=a+ϵ; all remaining observations are independent of a and give no extra information. The single-observation location minimax bound is
infT​supa∈R​E{T(a+ϵ)−a}2=Var(ϵ)=1.
(23)
Here is a proof covering discrete as well as continuous errors. Give a the prior distribution U[−A,A], independently of the noise. Put pM​=P(∣ϵ∣≤M) and vM​=Var(ϵ∣∣ϵ∣≤M). Consider an easier experiment in which an oracle also reveals whether ∣ϵ∣≤M, and reveals a itself on the complementary event. On the event ∣ϵ∣≤M and ∣Y∣≤A−M, every possible truncated noise value places a=Y−ϵ within [−A,A]. The flat prior distribution then makes the Bayesian posterior noise law exactly its truncated original law. The smallest conditional squared-error loss is its conditional variance vM​, attained by the posterior mean.
The probability of that event is at least pM​(1−2M/A) for A>2M, by restricting further to ∣a∣≤A−2M. Thus every rule's supremum risk function is at least the original Bayes risk, which is at least the easier experiment's Bayes risk, and therefore at least pM​(1−2M/A)vM​. Let A→∞ and then M→∞. Finite second moment and zero mean give pM​vM​→1. The rule T(Y)=Y has constant mean squared error one, proving the equality.
Consequently, at every x0​∈[0,1], the unrestricted differentiability class satisfies the stronger conclusion
m∈Fsup​E{m(x0​)−m(x0​)}2≥1≥n−2/3.​
(24)
Thus c=1 works for every n≥1. There is no conflict with the preceding fixed-function limit: the worst functions, including arbitrarily narrow bumps, can vary with n. This is the pointwise versus uniform risk distinction. A smoothness ball with a common derivative bound would pose a different minimax risk problem.

A non-degenerate probability distribution is not concentrated at a single point. In extreme value theory, a non-degenerate distribution function F is max-stable if, for each integer n≥1, constants an​>0,bn​∈R satisfy F(an​x+bn​)n=F(x) for every x. This says that a normalized sample maximum has the same law as one observation. A distribution function F belongs to the maximum domain of attraction of a non-degenerate G if there are an​>0,bn​ such that
F(an​x+bn​)n⟶G(x)
(25)
at every continuity point of G. Equivalently, (Mn​−bn​)/an​ has convergence in distribution to G.
The extremal types theorem says that any such non-degenerate limit is max-stable and, up to a positive affine change of variable, is exactly one of the following:
Λ(x)Φα​(x)Ψα​(x)​=e−e−x,=e−x−α,=e−(−x)α,​​x∈R,x>0,Φα​(x)=0 (x≤0),x<0,Ψα​(x)=1 (x≥0),​α>0.
(26)
These are respectively the Gumbel distribution, Fréchet distribution, and negative Weibull distribution. The theorem is also known as the Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem.
Useful sufficient conditions can be expressed using the survival function F=1−F and the right endpoint of a distribution xF​. An infinite endpoint with F(tx)/F(t)→x−α for every x>0 gives the Fréchet distribution domain. A finite endpoint with F(xF​−sx)/F(xF​−s)→xα as s↓0 gives the negative Weibull distribution domain. For the Gumbel distribution, it suffices that a positive Gumbel auxiliary function a(t) gives F(t+a(t)x)/F(t)→e−x for every real x as t↑xF​. These are regular variation and exponential tail-ratio conditions; they need no proof here.
In case (i), F1​(x)=(1−x)2 on 0<x<1, so the finite-endpoint ratio is exactly x2. Therefore the domain is negative Weibull with shape 2. With an​=n−1/2 and bn​=1,
P{n​(Mn​−1)≤x}=(1−nx2​)n⟶e−x2(x<0),
(27)
and the limit is one for x≥0.
In case (ii), F2​(t)=e−λt has exponential tail ratio with the constant Gumbel auxiliary function 1/λ. Therefore the domain is Gumbel. Choosing an​=1/λ and bn​=logn/λ gives
P{λMn​−logn≤x}=(1−ne−x​)n⟶e−e−x.
(28)
In case (iii), F3​(t)=(1+t)−λ has regular variation of index −λ, so the domain is Fréchet with shape λ. The convenient choices an​=n1/λ and bn​=−1 give
P{(Mn​+1)/n1/λ≤x}=(1−nx−λ​)n⟶e−x−λ(x>0),
(29)
with limit zero for x≤0.
Finally, define the empirical distribution function Fn​(x)=n−1∑i=1n​1{Xi​≤x}​. On a sample with positive threshold t=X(n−k)​ and exactly k observations strictly above t, its survival function has 1−Fn​(t)=k/n. Using the Tonelli theorem to integrate the finite nonnegative sum gives
∫t∞​1−Fn​(t)1−Fn​(x)​xdx​=k1​∑i:Xi​>t​∫tXi​​xdx​=k1​j=1∑k​logX(n−k)​X(n−j+1)​​=γ​H​.​
(30)
This is the Hill estimator as an empirical plug-in version of the tail-integral limit. It does not prove consistency for fixed k; that is a separate issue.
There is a finite-sample qualification because the question permits ties and does not assume positive observations. The formula requires 1≤k<n and t>0. If r=#{i:Xi​>t}<k because the threshold is tied, direct substitution gives
r1​∑j=1k​logtX(n−j+1)​​(r>0),
(31)
where terms equal to the threshold contribute zero. This is generally k/r times the displayed Hill estimator, rather than the same estimator. If r=0, the empirical plug-in denominator is zero. For a continuous probability distribution the no-tie condition holds almost surely; for a Fréchet distribution domain and fixed k, the threshold is positive with probability tending to one. Thus the stated plug-in identity holds at a positive untied threshold, and the literal claim for all samples from an arbitrary distribution function needs this qualification.
