Use the spectral parameter for a linear boundary value problem k∈C and define the dispersion relation ω(k)=k2−iαk. Direct differentiation gives the divergence form
∂t​(e−ikx+ωtu)−∂x​(e−ikx+ωt[ux​+(ik+α)u])=0.​
(1)
Indeed the coefficient of u left over after expansion is ω−k2+iαk=0, and the remaining factor is ut​−uxx​−αux​. Thus this is equivalent to the advection-diffusion equation for every k.
Introduce the Half-range Fourier transforms and finite-time spectral boundary transforms
u(k,t)=∫0∞​e−ikxu(x,t)dx,u0​(k)=∫0∞​e−ikxu0​(x)dx,Gj​(k,t)=∫0t​eω(k)sgj​(s)ds,
(2)
where g1​(t)=ux​(0,t) is the unknown normal derivative with the positive-x convention. The outward normal at zero instead gives −g1​. The spatial transforms are analytic for Imk<0 and continuous on the real axis under the stated decay assumptions. Integrating the divergence form on (0,∞)×(0,t) gives the global relation
u0​(k)−eω(k)tu(k,t)=G1​(k,t)+(ik+α)G0​(k,t),Imk≤0.​
(3)
The sign follows from the lower spatial endpoint: the integrated spatial derivative is minus its value at zero. This sign will determine the boundary-forcing term in the solution.

Apply Fourier inversion to the global relation on the real axis, extending u by zero to negative x. At an interior point x>0 this gives
u(x,t)=2π1​∫R​eikx−ωtu0​(k)dk−2π1​∫R​eikx−ωt[G1​+(ik+α)G0​](k,t)dk.
(4)
The finite-time spectral boundary transforms are entire functions of k. Define the upper decay domain
D+={k=a+ib:b>0, Reω(k)<0}={a+ib:b>α, a2<b(b−α)}.
(5)
Orient ∂D+ from its left infinite end through iα to its right infinite end, so that D+ lies to the left. Its finite point is iα, not zero. On the contour, Reω=0; the factor eikx decays because x>0.
For the boundary term, write e−ωtGj​=∫0t​e−ω(t−s)gj​(s)ds. Between the real axis and ∂D+, Reω≥0, so this factor has no exponential growth. Contour deformation and Jordan lemma, with the usual cutoffs for oscillatory integrals, therefore give
u(x,t)=2π1​∫R​eikx−ωtu0​(k)dk−2π1​∫∂D+​eikx−ωt[G1​+(ik+α)G0​](k,t)dk.​
(6)
This is the requested complex-plane representation with the unknown finite-time spectral boundary transform still present. The decay domain must agree with the sign of the drift in the dispersion relation.

The dispersion symmetry elimination of a boundary trace uses the symmetry of the dispersion relation
ν(k)=iα−k,ω(ν(k))=ω(k).
(7)
For k∈D+, Imν(k)≤0, so the global relation is valid at ν(k). Since iν+α=−ik, it gives
G1​(k,t)=u0​(ν)−eωtu(ν,t)+ikG0​(k,t).
(8)
Substitution into the contour integral representation produces an unwanted integral ∫∂D+​eikxu(ν(k),t)dk. Its integrand is analytic in D+ and decays on closing the contour upwards; Jordan lemma makes this integral zero for x>0. Hence the Fokas method eliminates the unknown normal derivative:
u(x,t)​=2π1​∫R​eikx−ω(k)tu0​(k)dk−2π1​∫∂D+​eikx−ω(k)t[u0​(iα−k)+(2ik+α)G0​(k,t)]dk.​​
(9)
All quantities here are determined by the prescribed initial and Dirichlet boundary data up to time t.
For verification and for numerical evaluation it is useful to evaluate the spectral contour integrals, giving a half-line drift reflection kernel. Put
H(r,t)=4πt​e−r2/(4t)​,Kα​(x,y,t)=H(x−y+αt,t)−eαyH(x+y+αt,t),
(10)
and define the half-line drift boundary kernel
Pα​(x,t)=2π​t3/2x​exp[−4t(x+αt)2​].
(11)
Fubini's theorem, the Gaussian Fourier transform and contour deformation give the equivalent causal formula
u(x,t)=∫0∞​Kα​(x,y,t)u0​(y)dy+∫0t​Pα​(x,t−s)g0​(s)ds.​
(12)
For the reflected initial term, u0​(iα−k)=∫0∞​eαy+ikyu0​(y)dy on the contour; the evaluated Gaussian supplies the necessary large-y decay. If eαyu0​(y) is not integrable, truncate the initial conditions first, evaluate, and pass to the limit using Gaussian bounds. No extra exponential-decay assumption on the original data is needed for this kernel formula.
The boundary kernel follows particularly simply from
−2π1​∫∂D+​(2ik+α)eikx−ω(k)tdk=−(2∂x​+α)H(x+αt,t)=tx​H(x+αt,t).
(13)
This calculation independently checks both the sign and the coefficient of the boundary forcing.

The partial differential equation. Each term in Kα​ is a translated heat kernel and satisfies (∂t​−∂x2​−α∂x​)Kα​=0 for t>0. The same holds for Pα​, either by direct differentiation or because it is −(2∂x​+α)H(x+αt,t). For x>0, Pα​(x,t) and its derivatives vanish faster than any power as t↓0. Therefore differentiating the boundary convolution creates no extra upper-endpoint term. Gaussian domination justifies differentiating both integrals on compact subsets of x,t>0, proving the advection-diffusion equation.
The initial condition. For fixed x>0, the first Gaussian in Kα​ is an approximate identity centered at y=x+αt→x. Its integral tends to u0​(x). The reflected Gaussian is exponentially small as t↓0, because its center lies outside the half-line. The boundary convolution also tends to zero for x>0. Thus u(x,t)→u0​(x).
The Dirichlet boundary condition. The identity
H(−y+αt,t)=eαyH(y+αt,t)
(14)
shows that Kα​(0,y,t)=0, so the initial-data contribution vanishes at zero. The boundary contribution must be evaluated as a limit, not by substituting x=0 inside its singular integral. The positive kernel satisfies
∫0∞​Pα​(x,s)ds=e−αx⟶1,∫ϵ∞​Pα​(x,s)ds⟶0(x↓0)
(15)
for every ϵ>0. Hence it is a one-sided approximate identity at zero time, and continuity of g0​ gives
x↓0lim​u(x,t)=g0​(t),0<t<T.​
(16)
The mass formula follows from the Gaussian Laplace integral, or from the decaying solution of the corresponding constant-coefficient ordinary differential equation. Compatibility u0​(0)=g0​(0) gives the continuous corner value. These arguments also verify the equivalent contour solution in (iii). In the decaying energy class the solution is unique: the difference v of two solutions has zero data and dtd​∫0∞​v2dx=−2∫0∞​vx2​dx for real solutions, with the analogous modulus identity for complex solutions.
The unheaded sine transform question. The direct classical Fourier sine transform does not close on u. If
S(k,t)=∫0∞​sin(kx)u(x,t)dx,C(k,t)=∫0∞​cos(kx)u(x,t)dx,
(17)
then integration by parts gives
St​=−k2S+kg0​−αkC.
(18)
The drift introduces an unknown cosine transform; the usual scalar sine-transform solution of the heat equation is therefore unavailable directly.
A Dirichlet gauge transform for constant drift does provide a qualified alternative. Set v=eαx/2+α2t/4u. Then vt​=vxx​, with v0​=eαx/2u0​ and v(0,t)=eα2t/4g0​(t). If these weighted data have the decay needed for an ordinary Fourier sine transform, it solves the transformed problem and produces exactly the kernel above. Mere decay of u0​ does not ensure this weighted integrability. Thus not directly by the classical sine transform of u; yes after a gauge transformation when the required weighted-transform hypotheses hold, or after a justified cutoff/limit argument.

Use the Wirtinger derivatives ∂z​=(∂x​−i∂y​)/2 and ∂zˉ​=(∂x​+i∂y​)/2, and area measure dA=dxdy=(i/2)dz∧dzˉ. The supplied boundary-integral identity is the planar Generalized Stokes theorem; the usual Poincaré lemma is a different local exactness result.
Let f∈C1(D) and z∈D. Remove a disk of radius ϵ around z, and apply Generalized Stokes theorem to the one-form f(ζ)dζ/(ζ−z) on the punctured domain. Away from the puncture,
d(ζ−zf(ζ)​dζ)=ζ−zfζˉ​​(ζ)​dζˉ​∧dζ=2iζ−zfζˉ​​(ζ)​dA(ζ).
(19)
The outer boundary is counterclockwise and the small inner circle clockwise. Its counterclockwise integral tends to 2πif(z). Passing to the limit gives the Cauchy-Pompeiu formula
f(z)=2πi1​∫∂D​ζ−zf(ζ)​dζ−π1​∫D​ζ−zfζˉ​​(ζ)​dA(ζ).​
(20)
The weak 1/∣ζ−z∣ singularity is locally integrable. If the boundary term vanishes on expanding D to the whole plane, the formula becomes f(z)=π−1∫C​fζˉ​​(ζ)/(z−ζ)dA(ζ). In particular it yields the distributional normalization ∂zˉ​[1/(πz)]=δ0​. For holomorphic functions the area term vanishes and one recovers the Cauchy integral formula.

Work first with q in the Schwartz space, so that all Fourier manipulations and spectral contour integrals are justified; weaker classes follow by the usual density or distribution arguments. Define
E(z,k)=ekzˉ−kˉz,Q(k)=∫C​ekˉζ−kζˉ​q(ζ)dA(ζ).
(21)
The phase is purely imaginary. Since ∂zˉ​E=kE, setting F=EΦ reduces the spectral equation to ∂zˉ​Φ=E−1q. The whole-plane Cauchy-Pompeiu formula therefore constructs the solution decaying spatially at infinity:
F(z,k)=πE(z,k)​∫C​z−ζekˉζ−kζˉ​q(ζ)​dA(ζ).​
(22)
The freedom to add E times an entire function is removed by this decay condition. For every fixed k, the integral is a spatial Cauchy-Green operator applied to a modulated source.
Now differentiate in the conjugate spectral parameter. The two exponential derivatives produce ζ−z, canceling the Cauchy denominator, so
∂kˉ​F(z,k)=−π1​E(z,k)Q(k).​
(23)
This is the spectral dbar equation: its right-hand side is the forward transform of q multiplied by a known plane wave. Apply the whole-plane Cauchy-Pompeiu formula again, now in k:
F(z,k)=−π21​∫C​k−ℓE(z,ℓ)Q(ℓ)​dA(ℓ).
(24)
The spatial spectral equation also gives F(z,k)=−q(z)/k+o(k−1) as ∣k∣→∞. One way to justify this is to integrate by parts in the first Cauchy integral: F=−q/k+Tk​(qzˉ​)/k, where the modulated Cauchy integral Tk​(qzˉ​) tends to zero by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma. Comparing the 1/k coefficient of the spectral contour integral therefore gives
Q(k)=∫C​ekˉz−kzˉq(z)dA(z),q(z)=π21​∫C​ekzˉ−kˉzQ(k)dA(k).​
(25)
This derives the transform pair from two uses of the Cauchy-Pompeiu formula, not from an assumed inversion formula.
To identify the usual normalization, write z=x+iy and k=k1​+ik2​. Then kˉz−kzˉ=2i(k1​y−k2​x). Set ξ1​=2k2​, ξ2​=−2k1​, so dξ1​dξ2​=4dA(k). The result is exactly the two-dimensional Fourier transform pair
q​(ξ)=∫R2​e−iξ⋅xq(x)dx,q(x)=(2π)21​∫R2​eiξ⋅xq​(ξ)dξ.​
(26)
The factor four in the real-frequency change of variables is essential.

Assume for this spectral construction that the source and known attenuation are sufficiently smooth and decaying, for instance compactly supported, and that μ≥0 for the physical interpretation. The following steps identify both the forward attenuated Radon transform and the route to its inversion.
First write the complex transport operator as
Lk​=k∂z​+k−1∂zˉ​.
(27)
For k=eiθ this is the real directional derivative dθ​⋅∇, with dθ​=(cosθ,sinθ). Put nθ​=(−sinθ,cosθ) and x=pnθ​+sdθ​. The spectral equation on the unit circle becomes
∂s​F−μ(p,s)F=f(p,s).
(28)
Its minus sign fixes the appropriate endpoint condition: use F(p,+∞)=0. The integrating factor gives
F(p,s)=−∫s∞​exp[−∫sr​μ(p,v)dv]f(p,r)dr.
(29)
Thus the measured quantity at the opposite end of the line is
Rμ−​f(p,θ)=−F(p,−∞)=∫−∞∞​f(pnθ​+sdθ​)exp[−∫−∞s​μ(pnθ​+rdθ​)dr]ds.​
(30)
The exponent is the attenuation accumulated between the source point and the detector at the negative end. The common convention with detector at the positive end is the same transform after reversing the direction, Rμ+​f(p,θ)=Rμ−​f(−p,θ+π). Using an incoming zero condition at the negative end while retaining the printed minus sign would instead give a growing integrating factor, not physical attenuation.
Next, for ∣k∣=1, the operator Lk​ is a complex elliptic first-order operator. Its decaying whole-plane Green function is
Gk​(z)=π(kzˉ−k−1z)sgn(∣k∣2−1)​,Lk​Gk​=δ0​.
(31)
This is obtained by a real-linear change of variables in the Cauchy-Green operator; its change of orientation explains the sign. Write Gk​a for convolution with this kernel. Solve Lk​h=μ, and set F=ehΨ. This removes the attenuation:
Lk​Ψ=e−hf,h=Gk​μ,Ψ=Gk​(e−hf).
(32)
The normalized spectral solution is analytic separately inside and outside the unit circle. As k approaches that circle, the Green function's characteristic singularity produces two limiting values. Their relation is computed from the weighted line integrals above together with transverse Hilbert transforms; the known attenuation determines the integrating factor weights. The two spectral limits are not individually just the incoming and outgoing real characteristic solutions: the singular-kernel prescription matters.
Finally formulate the resulting additive Riemann-Hilbert problem on the unit circle. Its jump is determined by the measured attenuated Radon transform and known μ. With the unit circle oriented counterclockwise and jump J=Finside​−Foutside​, a Cauchy integral formula reconstructs the normalized spectral solution:
F(z,k)=2πi1​∫∣ζ∣=1​ζ−kJ(z,ζ)​dζ.
(33)
The normalization at zero supplies the compatibility condition ∫J/ζdζ=0. Recover f from Lk​F−μF, or from its small-k coefficient: if F=kF1​+O(k2), then f=∂zˉ​F1​. Equivalently the large-k coefficient gives f=∂z​F−1​ when F=k−1F−1​+O(k−2). This spectral reconstruction of an attenuated Radon transform is the analogue of recovering q from a coefficient in (ii). Known attenuation and full directed line data are inputs; one does not determine an arbitrary unknown attenuation and source simultaneously from this argument.

Put Eλ​=e−iβ(λz−zˉ/λ). The Wirtinger derivatives give (Eλ​)z​=−iβλEλ​ and (Eλ​)zˉ​=iβEλ​/λ. Write W=Adz+Bdzˉ, where
A=Eλ​(uz​+iβλu),B=−Eλ​(uzˉ​−iβu/λ).

Using the exterior derivative, dW=(Bz​−Azˉ​)dz∧dzˉ. The terms involving uz​ and uzˉ​ cancel, leaving
dW=−2Eλ​(uzzˉ​−β2u)dz∧dzˉ=iEλ​(Δu−4β2u)dx∧dy.​

Because Eλ​ never vanishes for λ=0, dW=0 if and only if uzzˉ​−β2u=0. In real coordinates this is the modified Helmholtz equation with mass parameter 2β, not β. In fact any one nonzero spectral parameter suffices for the equivalence; the full family supplies many independent boundary tests.

Interpret the printed coordinate notation as the square corners 1+i, 1−i, −1−i, −1+i. This listed order is clockwise, contrary to the counterclockwise convention in (ii). Keep the printed first side directed from top to bottom. Then m1​=1, h1​=−i, and z(s)=1−is. Put g1​(s)=u(1,−s) and q1​(s)=ux​(1,−s), where q1​ is the outward normal derivative on the right side. The pullback formula, without any orientation shortcut, gives
W1​(s,λ)=e−iβ(λ−λ−1)−βs(λ+λ−1)[−iq1​(s)+β(λ−λ−1)g1​(s)].​
(39)
Indeed h1​uz​−hˉ1​uzˉ​=−i(uz​+uzˉ​)=−iux​, and iβ(λh1​+hˉ1​/λ)=β(λ−λ−1). If one reverses the side to match the counterclockwise convention, its parameter is 1+is and its integrand is −W1​(−s,λ). Reversing every side multiplies the global relation by minus one, leaving its zero value unchanged.
The unheaded numerical reconstruction request. The four unknown Neumann boundary conditions are four functions on the sides, not four scalar values. The polygonal global relation is linear in their outward normal derivatives, and its remaining terms depend only on the prescribed Dirichlet boundary data.
Choose a finite approximation on each side, for example an expansion of qj​(s) in Legendre polynomials or piecewise polynomials. Substitute those expansions into the consistently oriented global relation. At chosen nonzero spectral parameters for a linear boundary value problem, integrate the exponential kernels against each basis function to assemble a complex linear system; the known right-hand side is obtained by integrating the given Dirichlet boundary data. Use enough independent samples to resolve all side coefficients, and preferably oversample. The conjugate global relations for the modified Helmholtz equation provide useful companion tests; for complex data use the corresponding independent adjoint relation rather than assuming the traces real.
Solve the scaled system by least-squares solution using a stable factorization such as a singular value decomposition. Sampling directions should probe all sides, and exponential row scaling avoids overflow and poor conditioning. Refine the side approximation and spectral samples until the recovered traces and unused global relation residuals stabilize. Corner incompatibilities or limited corner regularity call for mesh refinement or enriched endpoint basis functions. This realizes a numerical Dirichlet-to-Neumann map without first discretizing the whole interior.
The underlying Dirichlet problem is uniquely solvable in the usual trace class for Δu−4β2u=0 with β>0: the homogeneous problem has ∫Ω​(∣∇u∣2+4β2∣u∣2)dA=0. This supports the boundary reconstruction, although uniqueness of the continuous problem alone does not guarantee that an arbitrary finite set of spectral samples is well conditioned.
