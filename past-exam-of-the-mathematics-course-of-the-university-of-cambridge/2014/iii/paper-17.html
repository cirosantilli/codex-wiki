Let Ap,q(X) be the space of smooth complex differential forms of type (p,q). The Dolbeault operator satisfies ∂ˉ2=0, and the Dolbeault cohomology is
H∂ˉp,q​(X)=im(∂ˉ:Ap,q−1→Ap,q)ker(∂ˉ:Ap,q→Ap,q+1)​.​
(1)
For q=0 the denominator is zero; forms outside the dimension range are zero.
A sequence of sheaves is an exact sequence of sheaves precisely when its sequence of stalks at every point is exact. Here this means the first map is injective on stalks, the last is surjective on stalks, and the image equals the kernel at the middle stalk. In particular, a section of the last sheaf need only have local lifts; surjectivity on all global sections is not required.
The associated long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology of Čech cohomology, understood in the direct limit over open covers, is
0​⟶Hˇ0(X,F)⟶Hˇ0(X,E)⟶Hˇ0(X,G)δ0​​Hˇ1(X,F)⟶⋯⟶Hˇq(X,F)⟶Hˇq(X,E)⟶Hˇq(X,G)δq​​Hˇq+1(X,F)⟶⋯.​
(2)
The degree-zero groups are global sections. The connecting map is obtained by locally lifting a Čech cocycle to the middle sheaf and taking its Čech coboundary, which takes values in the first sheaf. Different lifts change it by a Čech coboundary.

The Dolbeault theorem identifies
H∂ˉp,q​(X)≅Hˇq(X,ΩXp​),​
(3)
where ΩXp​ is the sheaf of holomorphic differential forms of degree p.
The local analytic ingredient is the Dolbeault-Poincaré lemma: a ∂ˉ-closed smooth (p,q)-form with q>0 is locally ∂ˉ-exact. Here is a local proof. On a polydisc, the one-variable Cauchy-Green operator in coordinate zj​ is
(Tj​g)(z)=π1​∫C​zj​−ζχ(ζ)g(z1​,…,ζ,…,zm​)​dA(ζ),
(4)
with a smooth cutoff supported in the coordinate disc and equal to one on a smaller disc. The fundamental-solution identity ∂zˉ​(1/(πz))=δ0​ gives ∂zˉj​​Tj​g=g on that smaller disc. The operator is smooth in the parameters and commutes with derivatives in the other coordinates.
For a (0,q)-form, write α=dzˉm​∧β+γ, with neither β nor γ containing dzˉm​. Subtract ∂ˉ(Tm​β). The remainder contains no dzˉm​, remains closed, and its coefficients are holomorphic in zm​. Repeat with zm−1​ and then the other coordinates, shrinking discs as needed. Each new integral preserves the holomorphic dependence already achieved. At the end the closed remainder has positive antiholomorphic degree but contains no antiholomorphic differential, so it is zero. Factoring out each holomorphic basis form dzI​ gives the same assertion for (p,q)-forms, with the fixed degree sign included in the primitive. At degree zero, the kernel of ∂ˉ consists precisely of holomorphic coefficients.
Thus the following is an exact Dolbeault resolution of holomorphic differential forms of sheaves:
0⟶ΩXp​⟶Ap,0∂ˉ​Ap,1∂ˉ​⋯∂ˉ​Ap,m⟶0.
(5)
Each sheaf of smooth forms is a fine sheaf: multiplication by a smooth partition of unity supplies endomorphisms supported in its open cover. Fine sheaves on a paracompact manifold have zero positive sheaf cohomology; the Čech contraction sums a cochain against the partition of unity, giving δh+hδ=I in positive degrees. The general acyclic resolution theorem therefore computes Hˇq(X,ΩXp​) as the cohomology of the global-section complex above. That complex is exactly the Dolbeault resolution of holomorphic differential forms, proving the claimed isomorphism. The sheaf-cohomology/Čech comparison and the acyclic-resolution principle are the stated general Čech properties used here.

Cover the product by U0​=Cz​×Cw​ and U∞​=Cζ​×Cw​, with ζ=1/z on their intersection C∗×C. The permitted vanishing and the Dolbeault theorem make this an acyclic cover for both O and Ω2. The acyclic cover theorem lets its two-term Čech complex compute the cohomology. In particular, all groups with q≥2 vanish.
For p=0, a global holomorphic function is constant on each compact complex projective line fibre, by the maximum modulus principle. Its remaining dependence on w is entire. Hence H∂ˉ0,0​=O(C). For the first Čech group, a function on the intersection has a Laurent series
F(z,w)=∑j∈Z​aj​(w)zj.
(6)
Each coefficient is entire in w by its Cauchy integral formula. The nonnegative powers extend to U0​, and the negative powers extend to U∞​ in coordinate ζ. These two series converge locally uniformly with the parameter w, by the usual Laurent estimates on compact parameter sets. Thus every intersection function is a Čech coboundary and H∂ˉ0,1​=0.
For p=2, write a two-form on the intersection as F(z,w)dz∧dw. The other chart has
dζ∧dw=−z−2dz∧dw.
(7)
Forms extending from U0​ have coefficient powers j≥0; those extending from U∞​ have powers j≤−2. A globally defined two-form would need to have both types of expansion, so it is zero. In the first Čech quotient, precisely the z−1 term remains, and its coefficient is an arbitrary entire function of w. Consequently
H∂ˉp,q​(P1×C)≅⎩⎨⎧​O(C),O(C),0,​(p,q)=(0,0),(p,q)=(2,1),p∈{0,2} and all other q.​​
(8)
The second nonzero group is represented in Čech cohomology by a(w)z−1dz∧dw. This explicit residue description is the Dolbeault cohomology of the projective line times the affine line; it also identifies that group naturally with the holomorphic one-forms on the affine factor.

A rank-r holomorphic vector bundle is a complex manifold E with a holomorphic projection to X and local holomorphic trivializations E∣Ui​​≅Ui​×Cr that are complex-linear on each fibre. Its transition maps have the form (x,v)↦(x,gij​(x)v) with holomorphic gij​:Ui​∩Uj​→GLr​(C) satisfying the cocycle identities.
The holomorphic Picard group consists of isomorphism classes of holomorphic line bundles, with tensor product as multiplication, the trivial line bundle as identity and the dual bundle as inverse. The classification on the complex projective line gives
Pic(P1)≅Z,n⟼[OP1​(n)].​
(9)
The inverse is degree; O(1) is the positive generator.

In a local trivialization, identify the lines in each fibre with Ui​×P1. On overlaps glue by
(x,[v])⟼(x,[gij​(x)v]).
(10)
This action is well defined because gij​(x) is invertible and scaling v does not change the resulting line. It is holomorphic: in affine projective coordinates it is a ratio of holomorphic functions on the open set where its denominator is nonzero. The transition maps satisfy the cocycle condition and have holomorphic inverses. They give the holomorphic projectivization by lines its complex-manifold atlas, of dimension dimC​X+1, on the usual projective-bundle topology. Points over different base points are separated by base neighborhoods; points over the same base point are separated inside a common local product chart. A countable trivializing cover and the standard charts of the complex projective line give second countability.
The local projections agree, defining a holomorphic map p:P(E)→X whose fibre is P(Ex​)≅P1.
Define the relative tautological line bundle S⊂p∗E by Sl​=l. Its local construction is holomorphic, and its restriction to a fibre is OP1​(−1). The required relative hyperplane line bundle is therefore
L=S∗,L∣p−1(x)​≅OP1​(1).​
(11)
This fixes the lines convention for projectivization and the sign of the fibre degree.

Pullback and tensor product preserve holomorphic line bundles and their isomorphisms. Interpret negative tensor powers of L as powers of L∗. Thus the formula defines a group homomorphism on isomorphism classes.
To prove injectivity, suppose p∗M⊗L⊗n is trivial. Restrict it to any fibre. A line pulled back from its base point is trivial there, so its fibre restriction is OP1​(n). By the Picard group classification, n=0. It remains to show that p∗M trivial implies M trivial.
Let σ be a nowhere-zero holomorphic section of p∗M. On a sufficiently small open set where both E and M are trivial, write σ=ai​(x,[v])p∗ei​ for a frame ei​ of M. For each fixed x, ai​(x,⋅) is a holomorphic function on the compact complex projective line, hence is constant. It is therefore bi​(x); evaluating at a constant projective point in the local trivialization shows bi​ is holomorphic in x. The section is nowhere zero, so each bi​ is nowhere zero. Its overlap laws are exactly those of a section of M, and bi​ei​ glue to a global holomorphic frame. Thus M is trivial.
The kernel consists only of (OX​,0), proving
Pichol​(X)×Z↪Pichol​(P(E)).​
(12)
This is the Picard injection for holomorphic projective bundles. The argument uses local fibrewise constancy, so no global section of the projective bundle is required.

Write Lα=ω∧α, the Lefschetz operator of a Kähler manifold. The metric and volume form define the L2 inner product on smooth complex forms, and Λ=L∗ is its formal adjoint. Similarly, ∂ˉ∗ is the formal adjoint of the Dolbeault operator, characterized by ⟨∂ˉβ,α⟩=⟨β,∂ˉ∗α⟩. The Dolbeault Laplacian is
Δ∂ˉ​=∂ˉ∂ˉ∗+∂ˉ∗∂ˉ.​
(13)
On the compact manifold without boundary, integration by parts gives
⟨Δ∂ˉ​α,α⟩=∥∂ˉα∥22​+∥∂ˉ∗α∥22​.
(14)
If the Laplacian vanishes, both terms are zero. Conversely, if both operators annihilate α, the defining formula annihilates it. Thus harmonicity is equivalent to being both ∂ˉ-closed and ∂ˉ∗-closed.
Because ω is closed and has type (1,1), ∂ˉω=0 and [L,∂ˉ]=0. The supplied identity from the Kähler identities gives [L,∂ˉ∗]=−i∂. With ordinary commutators for the even-degree operator L,
[L,Δ∂ˉ​]​=[L,∂ˉ]∂ˉ∗+∂ˉ[L,∂ˉ∗]+[L,∂ˉ∗]∂ˉ+∂ˉ∗[L,∂ˉ]=−i(∂ˉ∂+∂∂ˉ)=0.​
(15)
The last identity follows from d2=0. This also proves that the Lefschetz operator preserves harmonic forms.
For the cohomology map one can work directly with forms: ∂ˉ(ωk∧α)=ωk∧∂ˉα, since ω has even degree. It takes closed forms to closed forms and exact forms to exact forms. Therefore the kth power of L induces
ϕω,k​([α])=[ωk∧α].​
(16)
The bidegree is (p+k,q+k), including the zero groups outside the dimension range. No isomorphism claim is needed here.
Finally put θ=−i∂f, so ∂ˉθ=i∂∂ˉf=ω′−ω. Let
Tk​=∑j=0k−1​(ω′)j∧ωk−1−j.
(17)
Both Kähler forms are ∂ˉ-closed, so ∂ˉTk​=0. For a closed representative α,
(ω′)k∧α−ωk∧α=∂ˉ(θ∧Tk​∧α).
(18)
This primitive has type (p+k,q+k−1); the degree-one sign produces no additional term because Tk​∧α is closed. Hence
ϕω,k​=ϕω′,k​for every k≥1.​
(19)
This is the dependence of Lefschetz maps on the Dolbeault class.

The unheaded definitions use the complex structure J on the real tangent bundle. Compatibility means g(Ju,Jv)=g(u,v). Its fundamental Hermitian form is ω(u,v)=g(Ju,v); the compatibility identity makes this real, alternating and of type (1,1). The metric is a Kähler metric precisely when dω=0. In complex dimension one a real three-form is zero, so every compatible metric is a Kähler metric.
Now assume closedness and prove the Kähler normal holomorphic coordinates condition. Start with holomorphic coordinates w centered at the point and make a complex-linear change so that the positive Hermitian coefficient matrix satisfies h(0)=I. Closedness of the (1,1) form gives
∂wk​​hijˉ​​=∂wi​​hkjˉ​​.
(20)
Define aijˉ​,k​=∂wk​​hijˉ​​(0); it is symmetric in i,k. Choose new coordinates implicitly by
wj​=zj​−21​∑i,k​aijˉ​,k​zi​zk​.
(21)
The derivative of this holomorphic map at zero is the identity, so the holomorphic inverse function theorem makes z valid local coordinates. In the new coordinates,
hijˉ​′​(z)=∑a,b​habˉ​(w(z))∂zi​∂wa​​∂zj​∂wb​​​.
(22)
At zero, h′=I, and differentiating gives
∂zk​​hijˉ​′​(0)=aijˉ​,k​+∂zi​​∂zk​​wj​(0)=0.

Hermitian symmetry makes all antiholomorphic first derivatives zero as well. Taylor's theorem for a smooth function now yields

Thus condition (a) implies condition (b), with the exact factor i/2 in the fundamental-form convention retained.

To prove (a) implies (c), shrink to a contractible coordinate neighborhood. The real Poincaré lemma gives a real one-form η with ω=dη. Write η=η1,0+η0,1. Because ω has type (1,1), ∂ˉη0,1=0 and ∂η1,0=0. The Dolbeault-Poincaré lemma supplies a smooth function ψ with η0,1=∂ˉψ. Reality of η gives η1,0=∂ψ​. Thus
ω=∂∂ˉψ+∂ˉ∂ψ​=∂∂ˉ(ψ−ψ​)=i∂∂ˉ(2Imψ).
(26)
Taking the real function f=2Imψ gives the local real potential for a closed (1,1)-form. Conversely, d(i∂∂ˉf)=0, by ∂2=∂ˉ2=0 and anticommutation. Hence (a) and (c) are equivalent, completing all three conditions.
For the unheaded radial continuation, take the real potential f as in (c). Rotation invariance makes f constant on every circle of radius r>0, so
u(t)=f(et/2),t∈R,​
(27)
is well defined and smooth by composition. This avoids treating a smooth function as if it had a convergent Taylor series. At nonzero z, put t=log∣z∣2. The identities ∂z​t=1/z and ∂zˉ​t=1/zˉ give
fzzˉ​(z)=∣z∣2u′′(t)​=e−tu′′(t).
(28)
Let q(z)=fzzˉ​(z), which is smooth on the whole plane. Then
ω=iq(z)dz∧dzˉ,g=2q(z)(dx2+dy2).
(29)
At the origin, rotation invariance gives the finite Taylor expansion f(z)=f(0)+b∣z∣2+O(∣z∣4), with b=q(0). Alternatively continuity of q directly gives
limt→−∞​e−tu′′(t)=q(0).
(30)
The metric is positive away from the origin exactly when u′′(t)>0 for all finite t, and it is positive at the origin exactly when this limit is positive. Smoothness at the origin is already supplied by the original smooth potential. Consequently the positivity criterion for a radial Kähler potential is
u′′(t)>0 for every t∈R,t→−∞lim​e−tu′′(t)>0.​
(31)
Closedness is automatic from the potential, and positivity completes the Kähler condition. No completeness of this metric is asserted. The rotation-invariant Kähler potential on the complex plane is understood as real; if a complex radial potential is initially allowed with real ω, its smooth radial imaginary part is harmonic and hence constant, so that constant can be removed.

Use the convention that a Hermitian metric h is complex-linear in its first argument and conjugate-linear in its second. It is a smoothly varying positive-definite Hermitian form on each fibre. A connection on a vector bundle is a complex-linear operator D:Γ(E)→Ω1(X,E) satisfying D(as)=da⊗s+aDs for smooth complex functions a. Metric compatibility means, for every real vector field V,
Vh(s,t)=h(DV​s,t)+h(s,DV​t).​
(32)
This is the metric-compatible connection condition with the sesquilinear convention fixed.
Apply the smooth Gram-Schmidt process to a local frame to obtain an h-orthonormal smooth frame s1​,…,sr​. Positivity guarantees that all normalization denominators are nonzero and depend smoothly on the base point. Write Dsj​=∑i​Aij​si​. Differentiating h(si​,sj​)=δij​ and using compatibility gives
Aji​(V)+Aij​(V)​=0
(33)
for every real V. Thus A(V)†=−A(V)​: the connection matrix is skew-Hermitian. This smooth unitary frame for a Hermitian connection is generally not holomorphic; the requested local-frame assertion requires only a smooth frame.
The holomorphic dual vector bundle E∗ is obtained by dualizing fibres and using transition matrices gij−T​ when E has transitions gij​. Their entries are holomorphic because matrix inversion is holomorphic on GLr​(C). Their cocycle property follows from preservation of the fibrewise evaluation pairing, defining the natural holomorphic bundle with fibre (Ex​)∗.
The conjugate vector bundle E has the same underlying real fibres but opposite scalar action: λ⋅v=λv. Its smooth transition matrices are gij​​; they need not be holomorphic on X. The conjugate bundle is used here as a smooth complex bundle, whereas E∗ is holomorphic.
Define the smooth dual tensor H by
H(s⊗t)=h(s,t).
(34)
It is complex-linear in each tensor factor, because conjugating the second bundle converts the conjugate-linearity of h into linearity. Thus H∈Γ((E⊗E)∗).
The conjugate connection is defined on real vector fields by DV​(s)=DV​s​ and extended complex-linearly on the conjugate bundle. The tensor product connection is
D⊗​(s⊗t)=Ds⊗t+s⊗Dt.
(35)
Its dual connection D0​ is uniquely characterized by
(D0,V​H)(u)=V[H(u)]−H(D⊗,V​u).
(36)
The Leibniz rule makes this a genuine connection on (E⊗E)∗. Evaluate it on the decomposable tensor u=s⊗t:
(D0,V​H)(s⊗t)=Vh(s,t)−h(DV​s,t)−h(s,DV​t).​
(37)
Decomposable tensors span every fibre. Hence this tensor-valued one-form vanishes precisely when the compatibility identity holds for every V,s,t:
D is compatible with h ⟺ D0​H=0.​
(38)
This is metric compatibility as parallelism of a Hermitian tensor.
