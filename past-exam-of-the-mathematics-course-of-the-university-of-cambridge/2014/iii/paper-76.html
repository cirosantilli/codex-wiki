For the long-wave convection equation with broken Boussinesq symmetry, use a sufficiently smooth real temperature field. To make the spatial average and integrations meaningful, take a periodic pattern, or an existing long-interval average with bounded derivatives and vanishing averaged endpoint fluxes. Boundedness of the temperature alone does not guarantee all those averaging properties. Multiply the evolution equation by Θ and average. Integration by parts gives
⟨ΘΘxx​⟩=−⟨Θx2​⟩,⟨ΘΘxxxx​⟩=⟨Θxx2​⟩,
(1)
and ⟨Θ(Θxj​)x​⟩=−⟨Θxj+1​⟩. Thus the energy method yields
21​dtd​⟨Θ2⟩=−⟨Θ2⟩+μ⟨Θx2​⟩−⟨Θxx2​⟩+s⟨Θx3​⟩−⟨Θx4​⟩.​
(2)
The energy square completion for long-wave convection starts from
⟨(Θ+Θxx​)2⟩=⟨Θ2⟩−2⟨Θx2​⟩+⟨Θxx2​⟩≥0.
(3)
Put v=Θx​. The energy identity becomes
21​dtd​⟨Θ2⟩=−⟨(Θ+Θxx​)2⟩+⟨(μ−2)v2+sv3−v4⟩.
(4)
Since sv−v2=s2/4−(v−s/2)2≤s2/4 pointwise,
21​dtd​⟨Θ2⟩≤⟨(μ−2)Θx2​+sΘx3​−Θx4​⟩≤(μ−2+s2/4)⟨Θx2​⟩.​
(5)
Therefore μ<2−s2/4 excludes growth of the mean-square temperature, for arbitrary amplitude within this smooth averaging class. This is a nonlinear energy-stability criterion, not a proof of pointwise monotonicity at each position. A spatially constant component instead decays through the −Θ term. The criterion is sufficient; it need not coincide with the linear instability threshold.

For the second-harmonic feedback in a long-wave convection amplitude equation, at μ=2 the linear steady operator is L0​=−(1+∂x2​)2. Its eigenvalue on einx is −(1−n2)2, so the critical Fourier modes are n=±1. More generally the linear dispersion relation is λ(k)=−1+μk2−k4, giving first onset at μ=2,k=1.

Θ1x2​=−A2e2ix−A2e−2ix+2∣A∣2.

Θ2​=92is​A2e2ix+c.c.​

0=L0​Θ3​−μ2​Θ1xx​−2s(Θ1x​Θ2x​)x​+(Θ1x3​)x​.

μ2​A,98s2​∣A∣2A,−3∣A∣2A.

∣A∣2=p(s)μ2​,p(s)=27−8s29​.​

At s2=27/8, the cubic coefficient vanishes and the quoted relation is singular: higher-order nonlinear terms and a different detuning balance are needed. One must not assert a finite p there.

The quadratic nonlinearity produces only the zeroth and second harmonics, with the zeroth differentiated away, so it cannot balance a first-harmonic contribution at that order. Solvability would give μ1​A=0. Hence a nontrivial critical-mode expansion forces μ1​=0 and first balances detuning against cubic amplitude effects at order ϵ3.

The unforced onset is a stationary pattern-forming instability. Under a horizontal translation, the critical Fourier mode transforms as A↦eiϕA. A cubic amplitude equation without forcing must have the same phase weight: its leading terms are μ​A−c∣A∣2A. Reflection of the unforced spatial pattern conjugates A, permitting real coefficients in this stationary problem. They are determined by a weakly nonlinear expansion and projection onto the adjoint eigenfunction; symmetry alone does not calculate their values or guarantee a nonzero coupling.
Represent the third-harmonic forcing by a complex coefficient F multiplying e3ikc​x, whose phase weight is three. The product FA2 has weight 3−2=1 and therefore resonates with the critical positive harmonic. Neither a direct third-harmonic term nor FA has the required wavenumber balance. A travelling boundary pattern makes
F(T)=ϵe3i(ΩT+δ).
(12)
A response phase and the sign of its coefficient can be incorporated into δ. The resulting three-to-one spatially forced amplitude equation is
AT​=μ​A−c∣A∣2A+ϵA2e3i(ΩT+δ).​
(13)
This retains the leading resonant forcing term, linear detuning and cubic saturation, while dropping higher powers and nonresonant harmonics. The forcing is weak, the unforced critical eigenvalue is near zero, and the amplitude varies on a slow time; very high forcing frequency outside that slow scaling would require a different averaging argument. Reduction to the specific saturating canonical form in part (i) additionally assumes c>0 and nonzero forcing.

For the rotating-frame normalization of resonant amplitude forcing, use a rotating phase, A=Bexp[i(ΩT+δ)]. The real δ must occur inside the factor of i; the printed change of variables omits that i on the phase constant. The correctly phased substitution gives
BT​+iΩB=μ​B−c∣B∣2B+eB2,e=ϵ>0.
(14)
A negative real forcing coefficient can be made positive by changing the forcing phase. Assuming c>0, set
B=ce​C,T=ce2​T,μ=e2cμ​​,ω=e2cΩ​.
(15)
All three terms then have the same coefficient scale, giving
CT​+iωC=μC−∣C∣2C+C2.​
(16)
Renaming T as T gives the requested canonical equation. This rescaling preserves forward time. If c<0, a forward-time normalization instead leaves a positive cubic term, and if c=0 a cubic normalization is impossible. If e=0, the unforced Landau amplitude equation must be treated separately. Thus the printed canonical form implicitly concerns saturation in a supercritical bifurcation with nonzero forcing.

To find the threefold phase-locked equilibria, write C=Reiθ with R>0; separating real and imaginary parts gives
RT​=R(μ−R2)+R2cos3θ,θT​=−ω−Rsin3θ.​
(17)
A steady state satisfies
cos3θ0​=R0​R02​−μ​,sin3θ0​=−R0​ω​,
(18)
so
(μ−R02​)2+ω2=R02​.​
(19)
Writing y=R02​ and D=1+4μ−4ω2, the amplitude branches are
y±​=μ+21​±21​D​.​
(20)
There are two distinct positive roots for μ>ω2−1/4, except at (μ,ω)=(0,0), where y−​=0 and only the upper root is nonzero. Indeed their sum is positive in this range and their product is μ2+ω2. Below the fold condition there are none. At equality, there is one positive repeated root y=ω2+1/4, the saddle-node bifurcation limit.
For each positive amplitude, the sine and cosine determine 3θ0​ modulo 2π, giving three phases separated by 2π/3. Thus the source's “two states” means two amplitude branches modulo the threefold spatial symmetry. Generically there are six nonzero complex equilibria, three on each branch, not literally two.
The polar Jacobian matrix at an equilibrium is
J=(−μ−yω/R0​​3ωR0​3μ−3y​),trJ=2μ−4y,detJ=3y(2y−2μ−1).
(21)
On the lower branch, detJ=−3y−​D​<0, so it is a saddle equilibrium and unstable. On the upper branch, detJ=3y+​D​>0 and
trJ=−2(μ+1+D​)<0,
(22)
because existence implies μ>−1/4. Therefore every upper-branch equilibrium is asymptotically stable, and every lower-branch equilibrium is a saddle equilibrium, away from the degenerate endpoints. The eigenvalues are unchanged by the smooth polar coordinate transformation at R0​>0. The origin, not covered by those coordinates, has eigenvalues μ±iω and is stable for μ<0 and unstable for μ>0.

For ω=0, put μ=μ​ω2, C=ωz and τ=ωT, where z=C. Then CT​=ω2zτ​ and division by ω2 gives
zτ​+iz=z2+ω(μ​z−∣z∣2z).​
(23)
We may take ω>0 without loss of the dynamics by conjugating the original equation if necessary; otherwise τ reverses the time orientation. The scaling is singular at ω=0 and is not a transformation for that exactly zero-frequency case.
The Hamiltonian limit of three-to-one forcing drops the terms proportional to ω. For z=x+iy the remaining real system is
xτ​=y+x2−y2,yτ​=−x−2xy.
(24)
The proposed first integral is
H=x2+y2+2x2y−32​y3=∣z∣2+3iz3−z3​.​
(25)
Indeed xτ​=Hy​/2 and yτ​=−Hx​/2, hence Hτ​=0. The unperturbed equation is a planar Hamiltonian system, with a center equilibrium at the origin and three saddle equilibria at
(x,y)=(0,1),(3​/2,−1/2),(−3​/2,−1/2).
(26)
All saddle equilibria have H=1/3. The factorization
H−31​=(1+2y)[x2−3(y−1)2​]
(27)
shows that their central separatrix consists of the three sides of an equilateral triangle. Each level 0<H<1/3 inside this triangle is a closed periodic orbit. Indeed, inside the triangle 0<r<1 and ∂r​H=2r(1+rsin3θ)>0. Each ray from the origin therefore meets each such level once, giving a compact simple closed contour with no equilibrium point on it. The nonzero vector field traverses this contour periodically; the period grows without bound as the separatrix is approached. This supplies an infinite family, not a claim that every level outside the central region is closed.
Restore the small radial perturbation. Its exact effect on the first integral is
Hτ​=ω(μ​−r2)(xHx​+yHy​)=2ω(μ​−r2)r2(1+rsin3θ),r=∣z∣.
(28)
Consequently the continuum of Hamiltonian system orbits generally does not persist. The origin becomes a weak attracting focus for μ​<0 or a repelling focus for μ​>0, and the three hyperbolic saddle equilibria persist with perturbed stable manifold and unstable manifold. For a small positive μ​, outward drift on very small orbits balances cubic damping on somewhat larger ones, selecting a stable limit cycle rather than an arbitrary energy level. Near the center equilibrium H≃r2, so its leading radius is r≃μ​​ when μ​ is also small.
For a more general closed unperturbed orbit Γh​, the averaged area criterion for perturbed Hamiltonian cycles says that persistence requires the averaged energy drift ∮Γh​​Hτ​dτ to vanish. Since the unperturbed speed is ∣∇H∣/2, the planar divergence theorem converts this leading drift to
∮Γh​​Hτ​dτ=4ω[μ​∣Dh​∣−2∫Dh​​r2dA],
(29)
where Dh​ is the enclosed region. Isolated zeros select candidate periodic orbits; a drift changing from positive inside to negative outside gives an attracting limit cycle. The separatrix triangle has mean r2=1/4, so its leading flux changes sign at μ​=1/2. This marks the leading possible heteroclinic transition, with higher-order corrections needed to locate it precisely.
As a cycle approaches the saddle equilibria, long residence times and splitting of the heteroclinic cycle become important. Orbits can instead drift inward to the equilibrium point at the origin or leave the periodic island and approach one of the stable states with phase locking of the full canonical equation. Those upper-branch threefold phase-locked equilibria have C=O(1), so they lie outside the local C=O(ω) scaling. Thus the small perturbation gives energy selection, attracting or repelling oscillations, and possible switching/locking transitions; it does not preserve a conserved H or an infinite family of neutral periodic solutions. This qualitative picture does not assume all global parameter values have the same attractor.

Rotating Rayleigh-Bénard convection combines buoyancy-driven instability with the Coriolis force. Consider a plane layer of depth d rotating uniformly about the vertical axis, heated from below. Adopt the Boussinesq approximation, fixed boundary temperatures and, for explicit formulas, impermeable stress-free boundary conditions. The conductive state of Rayleigh-Bénard convection is motionless with a linear temperature profile. The dimensionless controls are the Rayleigh number, Prandtl number and Taylor number:
Ra=νκgαT​ΔTd3​,P=Pr=κν​,Ta=(ν2Ωd2​)2.
(30)
Here ν is kinematic viscosity and κ thermal diffusivity. In thermal-diffusion time units, linear perturbations satisfy
P−1ut​+Ta​ez​×u=−∇p+∇2u+Raθez​,θt​=w+∇2θ,∇⋅u=0.
(31)
Rotation does no direct mechanical work, since u⋅(ez​×u)=0, but couples vertical motion to vertical vorticity and changes the damping and oscillation balance.
For horizontal wavenumber k and vertical mode n, put m=n2π2 and a=k2+m. Use w=Wsin(nπz), θ=Θsin(nπz) and vertical vorticity ζ=Zcos(nπz) with growth rate σ. Curling the momentum equation and eliminating pressure gives
a(σ/P+a)W+Ta​nπZ=Rak2Θ,(σ/P+a)Z=Ta​nπW,(σ+a)Θ=W.
(32)
Their determinant, without dividing by a possibly zero factor, is the rotating-convection growth-rate polynomial
(σ+a)[a(σ/P+a)2+Tam]−Rak2(σ/P+a)=0.
(33)
This makes the linear stability analysis question precise: onset occurs when a root reaches zero real part and all other modes still decay.
A stationary neutral root has σ=0, giving
Rs​(k,n)=k2a3+Tam​.​
(34)
Rotation raises this stationary threshold. An oscillatory neutral root has σ=iϖ with ϖ=0. Real-imaginary separation gives the oscillatory neutral curve of rotating convection
ϖ2=P2[1+P1−P​aTam​−a2],Ro​(k,n)=k22(1+P)a3+2P2Tam/(1+P)​.​
(35)
This branch is admissible only when ϖ2>0, requiring P<1 and sufficiently strong rotation. The restoring Coriolis force coupling permits an inertial/thermal oscillation whose phase-lagged buoyancy can overcome dissipation. At large P, temperature and momentum diffusion do not permit that overstability mechanism at primary onset, so the exchange of stabilities is stationary. The actual threshold is the minimum of the stationary and admissible oscillatory curves over all allowed modes, not an arbitrary formal value of Ro​. Rigid plates require a different vertical eigenproblem and Ekman layers, so the explicit free-slip numbers are not universal.
At large Taylor number, the first vertical mode is selected in the ideal plane layer. Let t=k2,m=π2. Minimizing the stationary curve gives
(t+m)2(2t−m)=Tam.
(36)
Thus the stationary neutral curve of rotating convection has
ks,c​∼(Taπ2/2)1/6,Rs,c​∼3(Taπ2/2)2/3.​
(37)
The physical horizontal wavelength is 2πd/kc​, hence decreases as Ta−1/6; its prefactor depends on the boundary convention. Thin nearly vertical cells reconcile the strong Coriolis force constraint with viscous and thermal diffusion. The oscillatory minimization replaces the right side of the wavenumber equation by P2Tam/(1+P)2, giving the same Taylor number exponent at fixed positive P. Where its frequency remains admissible,
Rs,c​Ro,c​​∼(1+P)1/32P4/3​.
(38)
Equality is 8P4−P−1=0, whose positive root is approximately P∗​=0.6766. Accordingly, for sufficiently rapid rotation in this free-slip problem, P<P∗​ selects oscillatory onset and P>P∗​ stationary onset. The weaker condition P<1 is only necessary for an oscillatory neutral mode; it does not by itself identify the first instability. Finite Taylor number, finite lateral geometry, allowed discrete wave numbers and plate conditions change the selection.
For the counterpropagating Hopf amplitudes in rotating convection near a simple oscillatory onset, the Hopf bifurcation produces slow complex amplitudes for counterpropagating roll waves. After separating the fast carrier oscillation, symmetry permits the cubic equations
Z˙±​=rZ±​−(gs​∣Z±​∣2+gc​∣Z∓​∣2)Z±​,
(39)
with generally complex coefficients; an iω0​Z±​ term restores the fast frequency if desired. The real parts govern amplitude saturation and the imaginary parts give nonlinear frequency shifts. Write as​=Regs​, ac​=Regc​. For a travelling wave from a supercritical bifurcation with only one amplitude nonzero, ∣Z±​∣2=r/as​ with r,as​>0, and the competing wave's growth rate is r(1−ac​/as​). It is amplitude-stable against that competitor when ac​>as​. A standing wave has equal intensities r/(as​+ac​); provided this is positive, its intensity-difference mode is stable when as​>ac​. Temporal and spatial phase symmetries leave neutral phase directions, so these are orbital/amplitude stability statements, not decay of every phase displacement.
These coefficients follow from nonlinear interactions and the Fredholm solvability condition obtained by projection onto an adjoint eigenfunction; symmetry alone cannot decide their signs. A negative saturating coefficient gives subcritical bifurcation behavior requiring higher-order terms. Spatial modulation leads to coupled complex Ginzburg–Landau equations with group velocities and diffusion; phase instabilities, mean-flow coupling and differently oriented rolls can destabilize a wave stable in the restricted two-amplitude system. A weakly nonlinear expansion of oscillations therefore predicts travelling waves or standing waves, frequency shifts, modulation and possible secondary mode competition, not a unique universal periodic state.
The Küppers–Lortz instability is a different route to time dependence: it destabilizes steady saturated rolls against oblique roll perturbations. For stationary-roll amplitudes of orientations ϕj​, a leading competition system has
A˙i​=rAi​−g0​∣Ai​∣2Ai​−∑j=i​g(ϕj​−ϕi​)∣Aj​∣2Ai​.
(40)
A pure roll has ∣Ai​∣2=r/g0​ with g0​>0. An infinitesimal new roll at relative angle ϑ grows at
λinv​=r[1−g0​g(ϑ)​].​
(41)
For sufficiently strong rotation in appropriate boundary and Prandtl number regimes, some finite oblique angle has g(ϑ)<g0​, so a steady roll is unstable arbitrarily close above its stationary onset. Rotation is handed and allows g(ϑ)=g(−ϑ), so replacement of one roll by another can favor a definite cyclic sense. Three or more competing orientations can form a heteroclinic cycle; whether it attracts depends on contraction/expansion rates and other modes. Noise, spatially varying domains and modulation can turn this competition into repeated orientation switching and irregular patterns.
The invading rolls are three-dimensional disturbances even when the original straight roll is described by a two-dimensional section. The finite-angle Küppers–Lortz instability mechanism should also be distinguished from the small-angle instability of rotating convection rolls mediated by large-scale mean flow at finite Prandtl number. Numerical thresholds and favored angles depend on mechanical boundaries and material parameters; the essential criterion is the cross-coupling relative to self-saturation. Rotation both changes primary onset and wavelength, and can prevent the resulting steady roll pattern from remaining a stable nonlinear state.
