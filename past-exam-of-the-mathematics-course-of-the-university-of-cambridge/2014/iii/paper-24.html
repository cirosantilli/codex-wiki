An absolute value on a field is a map ∣⋅∣:K→R≥0​ satisfying
∣x∣=0⟺x=0,∣xy∣=∣x∣∣y∣,∣x+y∣≤∣x∣+∣y∣.
(1)
A Non-Archimedean absolute value satisfies the stronger ultrametric inequality ∣x+y∣≤max(∣x∣,∣y∣). Two equivalent absolute values induce the same topology, or equivalently differ by a positive real power. The trivial absolute value on a field takes value one on every nonzero element. The rational classification and the compactness criterion below concern nontrivial absolute values on a field; the trivial exceptions are given explicitly.
Here the additive valuation has real values. For any c>1, the mutually inverse constructions are
v(x)=−logc​∣x∣,v(0)=+∞,∣x∣=c−v(x).
(2)
Multiplicativity becomes v(xy)=v(x)+v(y), and the ultrametric inequality becomes v(x+y)≥min(v(x),v(y)). Equivalent real-valued valuations differ by positive scaling, so these constructions give the required bijection on equivalence classes. Changing c merely rescales the valuation.
To justify the topology formulation, ∣a∣<1 is equivalent to an→0. Thus two nontrivial absolute values on a field with the same topology give the same strict positivity relation on their additive valuations. Fix t with v1​(t)>0. Comparing the signs of mvj​(t)−nvj​(x)=vj​(tmx−n), for integers m and positive integers n, shows that v1​(x)/v1​(t) and v2​(x)/v2​(t) have identical rational cuts. They are equal, proving v2​=cv1​ for c>0. If one allows valuations in arbitrary ordered groups, the nontrivial classes arising this way are precisely rank-one valuations: higher-rank ordered value groups do not embed order-preservingly in R.
For a nontrivial Non-Archimedean absolute value on Q, ∣n∣≤1 for every integer n, by repeatedly applying the ultrametric inequality to sums of ones. Some prime p must have ∣p∣<1, otherwise prime factorization and multiplicativity would make every nonzero rational have value one. There is at most one such prime: if both ∣p∣,∣q∣<1, a Bezout identity ap+bq=1 contradicts the ultrametric inequality. If p∤m, another Bezout identity gives ∣m∣=1. Hence
∣x∣=∣p∣vp​(x)=∣x∣pα​,α=−logplog∣p∣​>0.​
(3)
This proves the non-Archimedean part of the Ostrowski theorem. If the trivial absolute value on a field is admitted, it supplies one additional class and is not equivalent to any p-adic absolute value.
The valuation ring, its maximal ideal, and its residue field are
R={x:∣x∣≤1},m={x:∣x∣<1},k=R/m.
(4)
Suppose the absolute value on a field is nontrivial and R is compact. The ideal m is an open additive subgroup of R, so k is discrete; as a continuous image of a compact space it is finite. The subgroup m is also closed, since all its cosets are open, and is therefore compact. The continuous function ∣⋅∣ attains a maximum ρ on m, with 0<ρ<1. Choose π with ∣π∣=ρ. Then the positive values of v=−log∣⋅∣ have least element −logρ. Division with remainder in this additive subgroup of R proves v(K×)=v(π)Z. Thus K is a discretely valued field and π is a uniformizer.
Conversely, normalize the discrete valuation by v(π)=1. If k has q elements, R/πNR has qN elements. Each quotient therefore supplies a finite cover by balls of radius tending to zero. The ring R is a closed subset of the complete metric field K, hence complete and totally bounded, so it is compact. Equivalently,
R≅Nlim​​R/πNR,R compact⟺v(K×) discrete and ∣k∣<∞.​
(5)
This is the local compactness criterion for a complete non-Archimedean field, with nontriviality understood. For the trivial absolute value on a field, R=K has the discrete topology and is compact exactly when K is a finite field; its value group is zero rather than a nonzero discrete cyclic group.

The inverse different is the trace-dual lattice

The integral closure OL​ is finite free over the complete discrete valuation ring OK​. Choose an integral basis and use the nondegenerate trace pairing to form its dual basis over K. This exhibits D−1 as a full, finite OK​-lattice. It is stable under multiplication by OL​, because bOL​⊆OL​ for b∈OL​, and bounded denominators make it a fractional ideal of OL​. Integral elements have integral field traces, so OL​⊆D−1. A nonzero fractional ideal of a discrete valuation ring is invertible; its inverse is consequently an integral ideal, the different ideal DL/K​.
Assume now A=OK​[x]=OL​, with monic separable minimal polynomial g of degree n. Lagrange interpolation at its distinct roots gives
TrL/K​(g′(x)xj​)={01​0≤j<n−1,j=n−1.​
(7)
Indeed, these are the leading coefficients in the interpolation formula for Xj. For any element of A, this trace is the coefficient of Xn−1 in its degree-less-than-n representative modulo g. The resulting pairing on the basis 1,x,…,xn−1 is integral and unimodular: reversing the order of one basis makes its matrix triangular with diagonal ones, since entries vanish when the exponent sum is less than n−1. Thus it identifies A with its full OK​-dual. Translating back to the trace pairing proves
DL/K−1​=g′(x)−1OL​,DL/K​=g′(x)OL​.​

For a totally ramified extension of degree n, any uniformizer πL​ is an Eisenstein generator of a totally ramified extension, and OL​=OK​[πL​]. Choose β with βp(p−1)=−p and put N=p(p−1). The Eisenstein polynomial TN+p shows that L=Qp​(β) has degree N, is totally ramified, and has uniformizer β. Its subfield Qp​(βp) is the field K from the previous part. Because p is odd,
a=−βp−1satisfiesap=p.
(18)
Thus L contains a and ζp​, and contains all roots aζpj​ of Tp−p. Conversely [Qp​(a):Qp​]=p, while [K:Qp​]=p−1. Their coprime degrees force their compositum to have degree N. It is contained in L and hence equals it. Therefore L is exactly the splitting field, not merely an extension containing it.
The Galois group has a normal subgroup H=Gal(L/K) of order p, acting by β↦ζpj​β. The (p−1)st roots of unity already lie in Qp​ by the Hensel lemma. The maps β↦ωβ, with ωp−1=1, supply a complement of order p−1. Its conjugation acts faithfully on H, so G≅Cp​⋊Fp×​.
Normalize vL​(β)=1. Total ramification and the uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups reduce the calculation to iG​(σ)=vL​(σβ−β). For nonidentity σ∈H,
iG​(σ)=1+vL​(ζpj​−1)=1+p,
(19)
since e(L/K)=p and vK​(ζpj​−1)=1. For σ∈/H, write σβ=ωζpj​β with ω=1. Its multiplier has residue ω=1, so iG​(σ)=1. The lower ramification numbering is therefore
G−1​=G0​=G,G1​=⋯=Gp​=H≅Cp​,Gp+1​=1.​
(20)
All later groups are trivial. The wild lower break is p, not one. In the upper ramification numbering, the Herbrand function sends this break to p/(p−1): G0=G, Gu=H for 0<u≤p/(p−1), and Gu=1 above it.
As an independent consistency check, the different exponent from ramification groups is (N−1)+p(p−1)=2N−1. The derivative of the Eisenstein polynomial gives the same answer, vL​(NβN−1)=N+(N−1), since vL​(p)=N.

The idele group is the multiplicative restricted product
JK​=∏v′​Kv×​
(21)
with respect to Ov×​ at the finite places of a number field; Kv​ is the completion of a valued field at the place v. Thus each tuple has nonzero components, and all but finitely many finite components are units. Its restricted product topology on the idele group has basic open sets ∏v∈S​Wv​×∏v∈/S​Ov×​, where S is finite and contains the infinite places, and each Wv​ is open in Kv×​. In particular UK​ is an open subgroup.
Embed K× diagonally. Take a neighbourhood of one whose finite components all lie in Ov×​ and whose infinite components satisfy ∣xv​−1∣<1/2 in the usual real or complex modulus. A diagonal element there is an algebraic unit a. If a=1, then a−1 is a nonzero algebraic integer, so its field norm is a nonzero integer. But
0<∣NK/Q​(a−1)∣=∏σ real​∣σ(a)−1∣∏σ complex​∣σ(a)−1∣2<1,
(22)
a contradiction. Thus K× is discrete. It is also closed: in a topological group, a subgroup with an isolated identity cannot have an external accumulation point, since quotients of two nearby subgroup elements would approach the identity.
Send an idele to its associated fractional ideal by
I(x)=∏v finite​pvordv​(xv​)​.
(23)
Only finitely many exponents are nonzero. This homomorphism is onto, by choosing powers of local uniformizers, and its kernel is UK​. Diagonal elements map to principal fractional ideals. The resulting quotient gives
JK​/(K×UK​)≅Cl(K).​
(24)
It is a topological isomorphism when the ideal class group is given the discrete topology, since UK​ is open.
Use normalized local moduli: real modulus, squared complex modulus, and ∣πv​∣v​=(Npv​)−1 at a finite place. The idelic modulus ∥x∥=∏v​∣xv​∣v​ defines JK1​=ker∥⋅∥, the norm-one idele group. The product formula puts K× inside this kernel. Every ideal class has a representative in JK1​, because an infinite component can be rescaled to correct the modulus without altering its fractional ideal. The compact space JK1​/K× therefore maps continuously onto the discrete ideal class group. Its image must be finite, proving Cl(K) is finite.
For the Dirichlet unit theorem, put U1=UK​∩JK1​ and E=K×∩U1=OK×​. Let r1​ count real embeddings and r2​ count conjugate complex pairs. Infinite logarithms define a continuous surjection
ℓ:U1⟶H={(t1​,…,tr1​+r2​​)∈Rr1​+r2​:∑i​ti​=0},
(25)
using log∣xv​∣ at real places and 2log∣xv​∣ at complex places. Its kernel is compact: it consists of real signs, complex unit circles, and the product of compact finite-place unit groups. More generally the inverse image of a bounded closed subset of H is compact. Since E is closed and discrete, its intersection with each such inverse image is finite. Hence Λ=ℓ(E) is discrete in H, and the kernel E∩kerℓ is a finite group. It is exactly the roots of unity μ(K), since every element of a finite multiplicative group has finite order and every root of unity has all local moduli one.
The image of U1 in JK1​/K× is an open subgroup, hence also closed, and is homeomorphic to U1/E. The assumed compactness therefore makes U1/E compact, and its continuous quotient H/Λ is compact. A discrete cocompact subgroup of a real vector space is a full Euclidean lattice, of rank dimH=r1​+r2​−1. Thus E/μ(K)≅Zr1​+r2​−1, and lifting a lattice basis splits off the free factor:
OK×​≅μ(K)×Zr1​+r2​−1.​
(26)
This derives both finiteness and the unit rank from the stated compactness assumption, rather than assuming either conclusion to prove compactness.

For this non-Archimedean local field, the Schwartz-Bruhat space S(F) consists of locally constant, compactly supported complex-valued functions. Choose a nontrivial continuous additive character ψ:F→C× of modulus one and an additive Haar measure dx. We use the plus-sign convention for the Fourier transform over a local field:
f​(y)=∫F​f(x)ψ(xy)dx.
(27)
Compact support makes the integral absolutely convergent. The additive character and the scale of the Haar measure are part of the definition; without them there is no canonical numerical transform.
Let q=∣OF​/πF​OF​∣, and write V=vol(OF​). Define the integer c by the annihilator of the valuation ring OF⊥​=πFc​OF​. Equivalently, ψ is trivial on πFc​OF​ and not on πFc−1​OF​. Such a conductor exists: continuity puts the image of some additive ball inside an arc containing no nontrivial circle subgroup, so the character is trivial on that ball; nontriviality bounds the possible ball exponents below. Then Hn​=πFn​OF​ has volume Vq−n and character annihilator Hn⊥​=πFc−n​OF​.
Translation gives
1a+Hn​​​(y)=ψ(ay)∫Hn​​ψ(ty)dt.

The integral is the volume if y∈Hn⊥​. Otherwise translate it by t0​∈Hn​ with ψ(t0​y)=1; Haar measure invariance multiplies the same integral by a nonidentity scalar, so it must vanish. Thus

The transform of each indicator has compact support and is locally constant, since ψ has open kernel. Consequently f​∈S(F) for every f∈S(F).
