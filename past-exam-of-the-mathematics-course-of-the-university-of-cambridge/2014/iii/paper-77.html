a OurBigBook.com
Choose positive tilt towards +x, so the rod tangent is t=(sinθ,cosθ) and its normal is n=(cosθ,−sinθ). The opposite angular convention reverses the signed phase optimum later. In resistive-force theory, a point with centred arclength s∈[−L/2,L/2] has velocity u=x˙ex​+sθ˙n, and its force on the fluid is Du, where D=c⊥​I+(c∥​−c⊥​)tt. The force on the rod has the opposite sign.
The pure centred rotational contribution is odd in s and therefore gives zero net force instantaneously in either direction. In the combined oscillating rigid rod in resistive-force theory, however, orientation modulates the translational force. After half a period, x˙↦−x˙ and θ↦−θ. Reflection in the y axis takes one configuration and its forcing into the other. A force component in x reverses under this reflection, whereas a component in y does not. Explicitly,
Fx​=L[c⊥​+(c∥​−c⊥​)sin2θ]x˙,Fy​=L(c∥​−c⊥​)sinθcosθx˙.
(1)
The first is half-period antisymmetric and the second is half-period symmetric. Hence ⟨Fx​⟩=0, while symmetry permits ⟨Fy​⟩=0. The possible mean y force comes from the coupled translation and rotation, not from pure centred rotation alone.

In the oscillating rigid rod in resistive-force theory, sinθcosθ=θ+O(ϵ3) and x˙=−ϵaωsinωt. Since
⟨θx˙⟩=−ϵ2aω⟨cos(ωt+ϕ)sinωt⟩=2ϵ2aω​sinϕ,
(2)
the mean transverse force from a rocking rod, on the fluid in the tilt convention of part (a), is
⟨Fy​⟩=−21​(c⊥​−c∥​)Lϵ2aωsinϕ+O(ϵ4).​
(3)
The parallel and perpendicular drag coefficients of a slender filament are per unit length here. Their difference is essential: isotropic local drag gives zero transverse force. The hydrodynamic force on the rod is the negative of the displayed answer.

c OurBigBook.com
For the usual anisotropy c⊥​>c∥​ and a>0, the maximum positive mean transverse force from a rocking rod occurs at
ϕ=−π/2(mod2π),⟨Fy​⟩max​=21​(c⊥​−c∥​)Lϵ2aω.​
(4)
The maximum magnitude has either quadrature phase ϕ=±π/2. If positive tilt is defined towards −x, the positive-force optimum is instead +π/2. At our optimum θ=ϵsinωt is opposite in sign to x˙: the rod tilts so that the weaker tangential drag and stronger normal drag combine into a positive y force on both strokes.
For ϕ=0 or π, orientation is a single-valued function of the centre displacement. The configuration retraces its path under time reversal, and kinematic reversibility of Stokes flow makes the force impulse cancel. Equivalently, ⟨θx˙⟩=0. The enclosed loop in the displacement-orientation plane is largest in quadrature and vanishes for a reciprocal stroke, illustrating the principle behind the scallop theorem.

The instantaneous work on the fluid equals the integral of u⋅Du. For the oscillating rigid rod in resistive-force theory, u⋅t=x˙sinθ and u⋅n=x˙cosθ+sθ˙. Odd terms in s integrate to zero, giving
P=L[c∥​sin2θ+c⊥​cos2θ]x˙2+12c⊥​L3​θ˙2.
(5)
Thus the leading power of a rocking rod is
⟨P⟩=21​c⊥​ϵ2ω2(La2+12L3​)+O(ϵ4).​
(6)
Both terms are quadratic in ϵ. Small angle does not make rotation a higher-order contribution: points away from the centre have rotational speed sθ˙=O(ϵωL), just as the translational speed is O(ϵωa). Their ratio is L2/(12a2), controlled by geometry rather than by ϵ.

e OurBigBook.com
Use the printed Maxwell-filtered local drag law at fixed material arclength, after transients have decayed to a periodic response. Averaging the force equation over a period makes the time-derivative term vanish. Therefore the mean force equals the mean instantaneous resistive-force theory driving force, even though its oscillating part is filtered. In particular,
⟨Fy​⟩=−21​(c⊥​−c∥​)Lϵ2aωsinϕ+O(ϵ4),​
(7)
with the same sign convention as before.
At first order the tangent is ey​ and both velocity components are single harmonics of frequency ω. The linear Maxwell fluid response multiplies their complex force amplitudes by (1+iωλ)−1. Only its in-phase real part contributes to the mean work. Consequently
⟨P⟩M​=2[1+(ωλ)2]c⊥​ϵ2ω2​(La2+12L3​)+O(ϵ4).​
(8)
The leading power is strictly smaller when λ>0 and ω>0, and equal in the Newtonian limit. The zero-frequency component generating the mean force is unattenuated, whereas elastic storage and phase lag reduce the dissipative harmonic response. This conclusion concerns the specified linear local force law and prescribed kinematics; it is not a claim for every nonlinear viscoelastic constitutive model.

b OurBigBook.com
Scale time by ω−1, length by k−1, velocity by ω/k, pressure by μω, and streamfunction by ω/k2. Write A=α/k, ξ=x−t, and drop stars from dimensionless coordinates. The surface is y=ϵsinξ and its material velocity in the sheet frame is (0,−ϵcosξ). The no-slip boundary condition is therefore
u(x,ϵsinξ,t)=(0,−ϵcosξ),u⟶Uex​(y→+∞),U=kU/ω.
(10)
The stationary matrix identifies a preferred frame. In the sheet frame it translates with Uex​, so matrix-relative Brinkman velocity gives the consistent equations
−∇p+∇2u=A2(u−Uex​),∇⋅u=0.​
(11)
Equivalently one may keep the printed right-hand side A2u after defining p​=p−A2Ux; then the far-field pressure has the gradient needed to balance that term. Using the printed equation with a uniform far-field flow and no such adjustment is inconsistent. The distinction first matters at second order, since the first-order swimming speed is zero. We solve the upper half-space; the lower half-space is its reflected counterpart.

Taking the curl of the Brinkman equation eliminates pressure and the constant matrix velocity. With u=(ψy​,−ψx​) and vorticity −∇2ψ, the Brinkman swimming sheet equation is
(∇2−A2)∇2ψ=0.​
(12)
The surface conditions are conditions on partial derivatives evaluated on the moving boundary:
ψy​(x,ϵsinξ,t)=0,ψx​(x,ϵsinξ,t)=ϵcosξ.​
(13)
At infinity, ψy​→U and ψx​→0. The derivative of ψ along the surface also equals ϵcosξ, because ψy​=0 there, so a convenient gauge has ψ(x,ϵsinξ,t)=ϵsinξ. Decaying perturbations and the force-free condition determine the swimming solution.

Expand ψ=ϵψ1​+ϵ2ψ2​+⋯ and U=ϵ2U2​+⋯. The screened first-order transverse sheet flow has ψ1​=f(y)sinξ. Set s=1+A2​. The two decaying roots of the Brinkman swimming sheet operator are 1 and s, and f(0)=1, f′(0)=0 select
f(y)=s−1se−y−e−sy​,u1​=(f′(y)sinξ,−f(y)cosξ).​
(14)
The physical first-order flow is ϵ(ω/k)u1​ in scaled coordinates. The dimensionless first-order pressure, determined from momentum balance, is
p1​=−s(s+1)e−ycosξ.​
(15)
Although the expression for f appears singular at A=0, its continuous limit is f=(1+y)e−y, the ordinary transverse mode of a Taylor swimming sheet.

A Taylor expansion of the tangential no-slip boundary condition gives u2x​(0)=−sinξ∂y​u1x​(0). Since f′′(0)=−s,
⟨u2x​(0)⟩=s⟨sin2ξ⟩=s/2.
(16)
For matrix-relative Brinkman velocity, the mean second-order tangential flow is
u2x​(y)=U2​+Ce−Ay.
(17)
The mean tangential traction has no first-order geometric remainder: at the surface ∂y​σxy,1​=s(s+1)sinξ and σxx,1​=s(s+1)cosξ, so the mean displaced-boundary and tilted-normal corrections cancel. Thus the force-free condition gives u2x′​(0)=0, hence C=0. In the A=0 limit boundedness likewise excludes mean shear. The swimming speed of a Brinkman sheet is therefore
U=2ϵ2​1+A2​+O(ϵ4),U=2b2kω​1+(α/k)2​+O(kω​ϵ4).​
(18)
It is larger than the simple-fluid speed by 1+(α/k)2​ for fixed waveform and frequency. The matrix offers a reaction structure for the transverse stroke and more strongly confines the induced motion; it acts as a footing against which the wave pushes. This is a prescribed-stroke comparison, not a guarantee at fixed motor power. The small-amplitude expansion is at fixed A; a large-A use also needs ϵs≪1 to control variation across the displaced boundary.

At the undeformed boundary, the screened first-order transverse sheet flow has v1​=−cosξ and ∂y​v1​=−f′(0)cosξ=0. Hence the normal stress is σyy,1​=−p1​=s(s+1)cosξ. The sheet does work on the upper fluid at rate −⟨uS​⋅σn⟩, where n points from the sheet into that fluid. To second order only the first-order normal velocity and stress are needed. The power of a Brinkman sheet per projected area on one side is
P+​=μb2kω22s(s+1)​+O(kμω2​ϵ4),s=1+(α/k)2​.​
(19)
For fluid on both sides, double this answer. At α=0 the one-sided power is μb2kω2, so the fixed-stroke cost increases by s(s+1)/2>1. The mechanical energy supplied to the fluid accounts for both viscous dissipation and work against the matrix drag; the stress still has the printed Newtonian form. Enhanced speed therefore comes with increased energetic cost.

A Stokeslet is the flow produced by a point force. With C=1/(8πμ), its tensor is Gij​=C(δij​/r+ri​rj​/r3). A separated pair of equal and opposite forces has no force monopole. Expanding their difference in the separation gives a directional derivative of this tensor. For an axial pair, choose the sign of the strength so that
ui​=−Sej​ek​∂k​Gij​=8πμS​[−r31​+3r5(e⋅r)2​]ri​.
(20)
The trace part of ee does not affect the flow because ∂j​Gij​=0. The remaining symmetric force-dipole contribution is the stresslet, decaying as r−2 instead of the Stokeslet's r−1. A force-free swimming cell has thrust balanced by drag, so this is typically its leading far field. With S>0 it is a pusher microswimmer: axial outflow and transverse inflow. It is distinct from the antisymmetric torque singularity, the rotlet.

On the axis of an identical stresslet, r=ℓe gives velocity Se/(4πμℓ2), while the point on the opposite axis has its negative. Their identical intrinsic swimming velocities cancel from the relative motion. The axial repulsion of pusher stresslets obeys
ℓ˙=2πμℓ2S​>0,ℓ(t)=(ℓ03​+2πμ3S​t)1/3.​
(21)
This point-singularity approximation assumes the bodies are separated by distances large compared with their own sizes.

c OurBigBook.com
For a flat impermeable free surface with zero tangential stress, the image of a parallel stresslet is a stresslet of the same strength and parallel orientation at the reflected point. Reflection makes the normal velocity odd and tangential velocity even across the plane, enforcing zero normal flow and zero tangential shear. This is the free-surface image of a force dipole; it is not the no-slip-wall image system.
The cell at z=−h lies a distance 2h vertically below its image. Since e⋅r=0 there, its induced vertical velocity is S/(32πμh2) towards the surface. There is no image-induced tangential velocity, vorticity or tangent-normal strain at this point, so a parallel orientation remains parallel in this idealization. Therefore the finite-time free-surface approach of a point stresslet is
h˙=−32πμh2S​,h(t)=(h03​−32πμ3S​t)1/3,t∗​=3S32πμh03​​.​
(22)
The trajectory combines its parallel self-propulsion with this normal drift. Finite-time contact is the point-model prediction for S>0, carried over from part (b). A puller with S<0 moves away instead. For a finite cell, the far-field approximation fails before h reaches zero; surface deformation and near-contact physics can change the final encounter.

Let C=S/(8πμ). The image of the other stresslet is at relative vector (ℓ,0,−2h), for which r2=ℓ2+4h2. Summing direct and image interactions gives the free-surface interaction of two parallel stresslets
ℓ˙=ℓ24C​+2Cℓ(ℓ2+4h2)5/22ℓ2−4h2​,h˙=−4h2C​+2Ch(ℓ2+4h2)5/22ℓ2−4h2​.
(23)
The direct neighbour gives no vertical velocity. Its image gives a vertical correction of order Ch/ℓ3, compared with the own-image attraction of order C/h2. Thus for h/ℓ≪1,
h˙=−32πμh2S​[1+O((h/ℓ)3)],ℓ˙=πμℓ2S​[1+O((h/ℓ)2)].​
(24)
The height evolution is unchanged to leading order, while the repulsion is twice the unbounded-fluid value: the neighbouring image lies almost at the same horizontal separation as the real neighbour. At this order ℓ3=ℓ03​+3St/(πμ) and the height follows part (c). The equality for height is asymptotic, not exact; weak neighbour-induced orientation changes are also neglected in the stated parallel configuration.

e OurBigBook.com
The rotlet is the point-torque fundamental solution of Stokes flow. Its torque strength R is an axial vector, and its circulating velocity decays as r−2. A freely swimming cell with no external torque is torque-free. The motor applies opposite internal torques to its body and flagellar apparatus, so the net torque monopole satisfies
Rtotal​=0.​
(25)
Individual body and flagellar torques need not vanish. A spatially separated pair can therefore leave a rotlet dipole even though there is no far-field rotlet monopole.

f OurBigBook.com
A flagellar bundle and the counterrotating body supply oppositely signed axial torques separated along the swimming direction. Their leading torque moments cancel, and the next term is a rotlet dipole, decaying as r−3. For the ideal axisymmetric bacterium the torque vector is parallel or antiparallel to e.
To fix the sign convention, define a signed dipole strength D with units torque times length and set
uRD​=8πμD​(e⋅∇)r3e×r​.
(26)
Differentiating gives
uRD​=−8πμ3D​r5(e⋅r)(e×r)​.​
(27)
More generally, differentiating R×r/r3 gives R×e/r3−3(e⋅r)(R×r)/r5; the first term vanishes when R∥e. A derivative with respect to source position instead of observation position changes the definition of the signed moment. The physical handedness depends on which torque is ahead, so it cannot be fixed without that convention.

Solution

g OurBigBook.com
Take e=ex​ and the surface normal ez​. Under reflection in the free surface, polar vectors transform by M=diag(1,1,−1) and an axial torque transforms by det(M)M. A parallel torque therefore reverses sign, while the separation direction ex​ does not. The free-surface image of a rotlet dipole is a dipole of strength −D at z=+h.
The observation point at the cell is directly below the image, so e⋅r=0 and the image velocity vanishes. A symmetry proof of the vanishing normal drift is also useful: reflection in the vertical plane containing e reverses the axial dipole moment, preserves the normal component of a polar velocity, and leaves the geometry unchanged. Linearity would make that same component change sign with D, forcing it to be zero. Thus this singularity alone produces no attraction or repulsion.
Write X,Y,Z relative to the image, so the cell is at (0,0,−2h). The image field is
uim​=8πμ3D​(X2+Y2+Z2)5/2X(0,−Z,Y)​.
(28)
Although its value at the cell is zero, its velocity gradient is not. In particular, the surface-induced yaw of a rotlet dipole has
ωz​=(∂X​uY​−∂Y​uX​)cell​=128πμh43D​=0(D=0).​
(29)
A spherical torque-free body rotates with the local fluid angular velocity Ωz​=ωz​/2. Its swimming direction therefore turns in the surface plane and, at fixed height and speed, it traces a circle with radius Vswim​/∣Ωz​∣. The sign of D sets the sense of turning in this convention. An elongated swimmer also responds to strain; its rotation coefficient depends on its shape and need not equal one half of the vorticity. When the stresslet is included, its changing height produces a changing turning rate rather than an exactly fixed-radius circle.

Let t=(xs​,ys​) be the unit tangent of the inextensible filament. Define
ρ=c⊥​c∥​​,β=Λ1​∫0Λ​xs2​ds,δ=Λ1​∫0Λ​xs​ys​ds.​
(30)
These quantities are invariant under a shift of the periodic arclength coordinate. Since xs2​+ys2​=1, the average tangent tensor is M=(βδ​δ1−β​). Also ∫tds=λex​.
For finite-amplitude propulsion of an inextensible periodic filament, distinguish axial phase speed from tangential material speed. The V in the displayed formula is the axial speed of the travelling pattern relative to the mean filament motion. In the wave frame an inextensible material line slides backwards tangentially at the constant speed Q=VΛ/λ: its mean axial sliding is Qλ/Λ=V. If the filament translates at −Uex​ in the laboratory, its local fluid-relative velocity is
u=(V−U)ex​−Qt.
(31)
This relation is the axial and arclength travelling-wave speeds conversion. It ensures the arclength-averaged material velocity is −Uex​, rather than confusing wave propagation with material transport.
Integrating the resistive-force theory hydrodynamic force over a period and imposing zero axial force gives
0=c⊥​Λ(V−U)[1+(ρ−1)β]−c∥​Qλ.
(32)
Substitution of Qλ=VΛ yields
VU​=1+β(ρ−1)(1−ρ)(1−β)​.​
(33)
For 0<ρ<1 and β<1 it is positive: anisotropic drag drives swimming opposite to the wave. A straight waveform has β=1 and gives no propulsion; isotropic drag ρ=1 also gives zero. The denominator (1−β)+ρβ is positive. If instead V denotes arclength propagation speed, the left-hand ratio acquires the extra factor λ/Λ; the two speeds are not interchangeable.
There is a geometric qualification to the asserted direction. For free swimming strictly along x, zero transverse force also requires δ=0, which holds for the usual reflection-symmetric waveforms. The periodicity assumptions alone do not imply that symmetry. A general waveform has cross-resistance of an asymmetric planar waveform and can require a transverse translation. If the mean laboratory velocity is (−Ux​,Uy​), the full force-free condition is
[I+(ρ−1)M](Uy​V−Ux​​)=ρV(01​).
(34)
Writing A0​=1+(ρ−1)β, B0​=1+(ρ−1)(1−β) and D=A0​B0​−(ρ−1)2δ2, one obtains
VUx​​=1−DρB0​​,VUy​​=−Dρ(ρ−1)δ​.​
(35)
The printed expression is recovered when δ=0, or as the axial force-balance result if transverse motion is externally constrained. For an explicit permitted asymmetric shape, concatenate tangents (3​/2,1/2) and (1/2,−3​/2) with arclength fractions p=3​/(1+3​) and 1−p. Their mean vertical tangent is zero, but δ=3​(2p−1)/4=0. Repeating the segments gives a periodic graph; the corners can be smoothed without removing the nonzero cross integral. Thus pure opposite-to-wave free swimming also needs a zero cross-resistance assumption.
