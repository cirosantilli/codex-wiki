A one-dimensional Sobolev representative is absolutely continuous. For x<y, the fundamental theorem of calculus and Holder inequality give
∣u(y)−u(x)∣≤∫xy​∣u′(s)∣ds≤∥u′∥Lq(0,1)​∣y−x∣1−1/q.
(1)
Thus u has a representative in C0,1−1/q([0,1]). The qualification about representatives matters because a Sobolev space element is an almost-everywhere equivalence class.
In two dimensions, the Sobolev fundamental theorem of calculus on lines and Fubini's theorem imply that almost every horizontal and vertical slice belongs to W1,q(0,1) and has this one-dimensional Hölder continuity. The slice seminorm depends on the slice; this does not give one uniform pointwise estimate on the square. For q>2, Morrey's inequality additionally gives a globally Hölder continuous representative of exponent 1−2/q. For 1<q≤2, global continuity need not hold. For example, with a smooth cutoff around an interior point, u(x)=∣x−x0​∣−a lies in W1,q when 0<a<2/q−1, but is unbounded. At q=2, the cutoff version of loglog(e/∣x−x0​∣) is unbounded while its gradient has finite squared integral, since
∫0ε​rlog2(e/r)dr​<∞.
(2)
These examples distinguish Sobolev slicing and planar continuity from a false two-dimensional application of the interval exponent.
Put Ω=(0,1)2. A BV space is a function u∈L1(Ω) whose distributional derivative Du is a finite vector-valued Radon measure. Equivalently its total variation seminorm is finite:
∣Du∣(Ω)=φ∈Cc1​(Ω;R2)∣φ(x)∣≤1​sup​∫Ω​udivφdx.​
(3)
The BV space has norm ∥u∥L1​+∣Du∣(Ω). For u∈W1,1(Ω), integration by parts against the compactly supported field gives ∫udivφ=−∫∇u⋅φ≤∫∣∇u∣. Conversely the measurable choice φ=−∇u/∣∇u∣ on nonzero gradients attains the pointwise bound. Approximating this bounded field by smooth fields, using interior cutoffs and the finite measure ∣∇u∣dx, justifies the supremum and gives
∣Du∣(Ω)=∫Ω​∣∇u∣dx.​
(4)
It is a norm of the derivative measure, rather than a pointwise derivative at jump discontinuities.
There is a genuine mismatch in the printed definition of the next functional. Its constraints on φ0​ and φ are independent. Hence its stated supremum, denoted Abox​, separates as
Abox​(u)=∣Ω∣+∣Du∣(Ω).​
(5)
The scalar supremum is ∣Ω∣, by cutoffs approaching one, and the vector supremum is the variation. For an affine image signal with ∣∇u∣=1, this gives 2∣Ω∣, whereas the displayed square-root area would give 2​∣Ω∣. The intended relaxed graph-area functional instead uses the coupled pointwise constraint φ02​+∣φ∣2≤1, giving
A(u)=∫Ω​1+∣∇u∣2​dx+∣Dsu∣(Ω),
(6)
where Dsu is the singular part of Du. Both readings have a minimizer, but their equations are different.
Here is the direct method in the calculus of variations for either reading. Let A∙​ be the literal Abox​ or the corrected A, and define the energy on BV(Ω)∩L2(Ω), assigning infinity elsewhere. A minimizing sequence has bounded energy by comparison with u=0. Both A∙​≥∣Du∣(Ω), so its variation is bounded, and the fidelity bounds ∥u−g∥2​, hence also ∥u∥2​ and ∥u∥1​. By bounded-variation compactness, a subsequence converges strongly in L1 to u∈BV, and after another subsequence almost everywhere. Fatou's lemma proves
∫Ω​(u−g)2≤liminfj​∫Ω​(uj​−g)2.
(7)
The regularizer is a supremum of affine functionals continuous in L1, since the test-field divergence is bounded. It is therefore lower semicontinuous. Combining the two lower bounds proves existence of a minimizer. In fact the convex regularizer and the strictly convex squared fidelity make the minimizer unique up to null sets. This does not assert that the minimizer must belong to W1,1.
For the intended graph area, conditionally assume that the minimizer is in W1,1. For η∈Cc∞​(Ω), differentiate at u+tη. The derivative of the integrand is bounded by ∣∇η∣, so dominated convergence applies. The weak equation is
α∫Ω​1+∣∇u∣2​∇u⋅∇η​dx+∫Ω​(u−g)ηdx=0,
(8)
that is,
u−g−αdiv1+∣∇u∣2​∇u​=0in D′(Ω).​
(9)
This is the graph-area Euler-Lagrange equation. Compactly supported variations impose no boundary condition in this statement.
For the literal printed supremum, the constant ∣Ω∣ drops out and one obtains total variation denoising. Its total variation calibration form is
u−g−αdivz=0,∣z∣≤1,z⋅∇u=∣∇u∣ a.e.​
(10)
The distributional equation means α∫z⋅∇η+∫(u−g)η=0. In particular z=∇u/∣∇u∣ wherever the gradient is nonzero; writing this quotient without handling zero gradients would be incomplete. Formally the one-sided derivative of ∫∣∇u∣ is
∫{∇u=0}​∣∇u∣∇u​⋅∇η+∫{∇u=0}​∣∇η∣.
(11)
Minimality in the directions η and −η bounds the remaining linear functional by the second integral. The Hahn-Banach theorem extends it on that zero-gradient set to a bounded vector field of magnitude at most one, furnishing z and the displayed weak equation. Thus the literal definition has a nonsmooth subgradient equation, not the square-root equation above.
