Use complex-linear distribution pairings, without conjugation. Write D=Cc∞​(Rn) for the space of test functions. The smoothing convolution with a test function is
(u∗φ)(x)=⟨uy​,φ(x−y)⟩.​
(1)
On a compact set of x values, all the translated test functions have support in one compact set. Continuity of the distribution therefore permits differentiation in x, giving ∂xα​(u∗φ)(x)=⟨u,∂αφ(x−⋅)⟩ for every multi-index. In particular this convolution is a smooth function, even if u is not tempered.
For the first associativity identity, integration against ψ and the distribution pairing can be interchanged: the integrand has a common compact support in y, depends smoothly on the integration variable, and satisfies the finite-order continuity estimate there. Consequently
((u∗φ)∗ψ)(x)​=∫ψ(z)⟨uy​,φ(x−z−y)⟩dz=⟨uy​,∫φ((x−y)−z)ψ(z)dz⟩=u∗(φ∗ψ)(x).​​
(2)
The common-support argument matters: a general distribution cannot be paired with arbitrary noncompact functions.
For convolution of distributions with a compactly supported factor, first take v with compact support K and define
⟨w,χ⟩=⟨ux​,⟨vy​,χ(x+y)⟩⟩,χ∈D.​
(3)
The inner pairing is interpreted using a cutoff function equal to one near K. It is smooth in x and has support in suppχ−K. To see continuity, restrict χ to a fixed compact support A. The order of a distribution estimate for v controls derivatives of the inner function by finitely many derivatives of χ, and its support lies in the fixed compact set A−K. Applying the corresponding estimate for u gives
∣⟨w,χ⟩∣≤CA​max∣α∣≤mA​​sup∣∂αχ∣.
(4)
Thus w is a distribution, not merely a formal iterated pairing.
Choose an additional cutoff function in x equal to one on a neighborhood of A−K. The resulting joint kernel is compactly supported in both variables, so the tensor product of distributions permits reversing the pairings. One justification is to approximate that smooth compact kernel, in all the required derivative seminorms, by finite sums of products of one-variable kernels; the two orders agree on such products and their continuity estimates pass to the limit. Reversing x,y consequently gives u∗v=v∗u. If u rather than v has compact support, use the same construction with the roles reversed; pairing u against a smooth function is then legitimate.
Evaluating the resulting smoothing convolution with a test function gives
(w∗φ)(x)=⟨uy​,⟨vz​,φ(x−y−z)⟩⟩=u∗(v∗φ)(x).​
(5)
If v has compact support, v∗φ is itself a test function; if u has compact support, its action on the smooth inner convolution uses a cutoff. This explains the meaning of the formula in either case. It also proves uniqueness: (w∗φ)(0)=⟨w,φ(−⋅)⟩, and reflection runs through all test functions. When both factors have compact support, the same definition gives supp(u∗v)⊂suppu+suppv, their Minkowski sum.
The Schwartz space consists of smooth functions for which every seminorm supx​∣xα∂βϕ(x)∣ is finite. A tempered distribution is a continuous linear functional on this space. Fix the angular-frequency Fourier transform convention
ϕ​(λ)=∫e−iλ⋅xϕ(x)dx,⟨u,ϕ⟩=⟨u,ϕ​⟩,F−1g(x)=(2π)−n∫eiλ⋅xg(λ)dλ.
(6)
The Fourier transform isomorphism of the Schwartz space makes the dual definition continuous. For a compactly supported distribution, a fixed cutoff function χ=1 near its support extends the action to smooth functions by ⟨u,g⟩=⟨u,χg⟩. A finite-order estimate controls this by finitely many Schwartz space seminorms, so both compactly supported factors are tempered.
Their Fourier transform of a compactly supported distribution is the smooth function u(λ)=⟨u,e−iλ⋅x⟩. Applying the compact-support convolution definition to the exponential gives
u∗v(λ)​=⟨ux​⊗vy​,e−iλ⋅(x+y)⟩=⟨ux​,e−iλ⋅x⟩⟨vy​,e−iλ⋅y⟩=u(λ)v(λ).​​
(7)
The convolution theorem has no extra factor with this normalization.
For the spherical surface measure convolution, put k=∣λ∣. Rotate the polar axis to the direction of λ; rotational invariance of surface area gives
ua​(λ)=2πa2∫−11​e−iaksds=k4πasin(ak)​.​
(8)
At k=0 the removable value is 4πa2, the total sphere area. Hence ua​∗ub​​(λ)=16π2absin(ak)sin(bk)/k2.
For r=∣x∣>0, angular integration in Fourier inversion now gives
(ua​∗ub​)(x)=r8ab​∫0∞​ksin(ak)sin(bk)sin(rk)​dk.
(9)
This conditional integral can be made rigorous by first inserting e−εk and then taking ε↓0 in tempered distributions. The supplied sine identity gives an integral of π/4 when ∣a−b∣<r<a+b, and zero off that interval. Thus
ua​∗ub​=∣x∣2πab​1{∣a−b∣<∣x∣<a+b}​​
(10)
as a regular distribution. To justify the limiting density as well as the signs, expand the product of sines into four sine terms and use ∫0∞​e−εksin(sk)dk/k=arctan(s/ε). The four arctangents are uniformly bounded; the regularized inverse is bounded by a constant times 1/r, which is a locally integrable function in three dimensions. Dominated convergence theorem therefore identifies the distributional limit with the displayed density.
Changing the two endpoint sphere values does not change the regular distribution; this includes the source's closed-interval representative. At a jump, symmetric Fourier inversion instead takes the half-value. If a=b, the 1/r singularity at the origin remains locally integrable and is not a point mass. As a normalization check,
∫R3​∣x∣2πab​1{∣a−b∣<∣x∣<a+b}​dx=4π2ab((a+b)2−(a−b)2)=16π2a2b2,
(11)
exactly the product of the original sphere areas.

For u∈E′, choose a cutoff function χ=1 on a neighborhood of its support. Its extension to smooth functions makes
F(z)=⟨ux​,χ(x)e−iz⋅x⟩
(12)
independent of the choice of cutoff. For real frequency, the definition of the distributional Fourier transform gives F(λ)=u(λ): interchange the pairing with the integral of a Schwartz function, using the finite-order estimate on the cutoff's compact support. On each compact set of complex frequencies the exponential and all its x derivatives have uniformly convergent power series. Continuity of the distribution allows termwise differentiation, with
∂zα​F(z)=⟨ux​,(−ix)αe−iz⋅x⟩.
(13)
Consequently F is entire on Cn.
For the precise exponential type, a fixed enlarged support would give an unnecessarily enlarged radius. Instead use the shrinking-cutoff exponential-type estimate. On one fixed compact neighborhood of the closed radius-δ ball, u has finite order of a distribution m. Choose χε​=1 near that ball, supported in the radius-(δ+ε) ball, with ∣∂αχε​∣≤Cα​ε−∣α∣ for 0<ε≤1/2. The finite-order estimate gives
∣F(z)∣≤Cε−m(1+∣z∣)me(δ+ε)∣Imz∣.
(14)
Take ε=[2(1+∣z∣)]−1. Its extra exponential is bounded by e1/2, while the derivative cost is polynomial. Thus
∣u(z)∣≤C′(1+∣z∣)2meδ∣Imz∣.​
(15)
The entire function was defined with a fixed cutoff; only its bound uses a frequency-dependent cutoff, so no holomorphic dependence is lost. This proves the required estimate with some N≥0 without enlarging δ.
For the converse, set V(z)=eiz⋅yU(z). Its restriction to real frequencies has polynomial growth. Define the inverse tempered distribution v by
⟨v,ϕ⟩=(2π)−n∫Rn​V(ξ)ϕ​(−ξ)dξ,ϕ∈S.
(16)
Rapid decay of the Schwartz function transform proves convergence and continuity, and Fourier inversion gives v=V on real frequencies.
We prove its support directly by contour shifting, rather than assuming the support conclusion of the Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem. Let ω be a real unit vector and take a test function ϕ supported in x⋅ω≥δ+ϵ for some ϵ>0. For every integer M≥0, integration by parts in the real-frequency Fourier integral gives
ϕ​(−ξ−itω)∣ϕ​(−ξ−itω)∣​=∫eiξ⋅xe−tω⋅xϕ(x)dx,≤CM​(1+t)2M(1+∣ξ∣2)−Me−t(δ+ϵ),t≥0.​
(17)
Indeed, move (1−Δx​)M from the oscillatory exponential to e−tω⋅xϕ; its derivatives supply at most 2M powers of t.
The product V(z)ϕ​(−z) is entire. Rotate coordinates so that ω is the first coordinate vector and apply Cauchy integral theorem to a rectangle in that one complex coordinate, integrating the remaining real coordinates afterwards. For fixed t, choose 2M>N+n+2; the displayed decay estimate, uniformly on the intervening imaginary segment, makes the vertical faces vanish as the real rectangle width tends to infinity. The horizontal integrals are absolutely convergent. Hence
⟨v,ϕ⟩=(2π)−n∫Rn​V(ξ+itω)ϕ​(−ξ−itω)dξ.
(18)
Using (1+∣ξ+itω∣)N≤C(1+t)N(1+∣ξ∣)N and the growth hypothesis bounds this pairing by
C′(1+t)N+2Me−ϵt∫Rn​(1+∣ξ∣)N(1+∣ξ∣2)−Mdξ.
(19)
It tends to zero as t→∞, so the pairing vanishes. Every point outside the closed radius-δ ball lies in such a separating half-space; a finite partition of unity for the support of a test function outside the ball proves suppv⊂B(0,δ).
Translate this compactly supported distribution by y: define ⟨u,ϕ⟩=⟨v,ϕ(⋅+y)⟩. The Translation property of the Fourier transform gives
u(ξ)=e−iξ⋅yV(ξ)=U(ξ),suppu⊂B(y,δ).​
(20)
The compact-support entire extension agrees with U everywhere by the identity theorem, applied successively in the complex coordinates. Fourier inversion also gives uniqueness. This completes both directions of the ball version of the Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem.
For the wave equation, take a real constant c=0. The Fourier transform method for the wave equation gives
∂t2​Et​(ξ)+c2∣ξ∣2Et​(ξ)=0,Et​(ξ)=cos(ct∣ξ∣)f​(ξ).​
(21)
This inverse tempered distribution is twice differentiable in t: time derivatives introduce polynomial frequency factors, still integrable against every Schwartz function. It has the specified initial displacement and zero initial velocity and satisfies the equation distributionally.
To apply the support theorem, replace the real norm by the entire wave cosine multiplier
Ct​(z)=∑j=0∞​(2j)!(−1)j(ct)2j(z⋅z)j​.
(22)
This series is entire and equals cos(ctz⋅z​) regardless of the square-root choice. Write z=a+ib and q=z⋅z​. Since ∣z⋅z∣≤∣a∣2+∣b∣2,
(Imq)2=2∣z⋅z∣−(∣a∣2−∣b∣2)​≤∣b∣2.
(23)
Together with ∣cosw∣≤e∣Imw∣, this yields ∣Ct​(z)∣≤e∣c∣t∣Imz∣. The forward estimate for the translated initial support now gives
∣eiz⋅yCt​(z)f​(z)∣≤C(1+∣z∣)Ne(δ+∣c∣t)∣Imz∣.
(24)
The converse therefore proves finite propagation with the stated speed:
suppEt​⊂B(y,δ+∣c∣t),t>0.​
(25)
In particular, z⋅z​ itself need not be entire; it is the even cosine series that provides the required entire extension.
For completeness this construction identifies the distributional Cauchy solution even without initially assuming spatial temperedness. The zero-displacement wave multiplier is Sτ​(z)=∫0τ​Cs​(z)ds, entire with bound ∣Sτ​(z)∣≤τe∣c∣τ∣Imz∣. Given a compactly supported smooth ϕ and final time T, the backward solution w(s)=F−1[ST−s​ϕ​] is smooth, has support in one compact ball for 0≤s≤T by the same support theorem, and satisfies w(T)=0, ws​(T)=−ϕ. For the difference h of two distributional solutions with zero initial data,
dsd​(⟨hs​,w⟩−⟨h,ws​⟩)=c2(⟨Δh,w⟩−⟨h,Δw⟩)=0.
(26)
All pairings use compactly supported test functions. The expression vanishes initially and equals ⟨h(T),ϕ⟩ finally. Hence h(T)=0, establishing uniqueness in the usual time-differentiable distributional solution class.

Use Dj​=−i∂xj​​, so the Fourier transform of Dαu is λαu. Distinguish the full degree-N polynomial P(λ) from its homogeneous top-degree part pN​(λ), the principal symbol. The elliptic differential operator condition is
pN​(λ)=0for every real λ=0.​
(27)
It does not require the full polynomial to be nonzero at small frequencies. Compactness of the unit sphere gives c0​=min∣θ∣=1​∣pN​(θ)∣>0. Homogeneity and the lower-degree remainder imply
∣P(λ)∣≥c0​∣λ∣N−C(1+∣λ∣)N−1.
(28)
For sufficiently large ∣λ∣ the second term is at most half the first, so the high-frequency lower bound for an elliptic polynomial is
∣P(λ)∣≥c⟨λ⟩N,​⟨λ⟩=(1+∣λ∣2)1/2.
(29)
Here ⟨λ⟩ is the Japanese bracket. The order-zero case just means a nonzero constant and is immediate.
For real s, the Sobolev space definition with the current Fourier normalization is
Hs(Rn)={u∈S′:⟨λ⟩su∈L2},∥u∥Hs2​=(2π)−n∫⟨λ⟩2s∣u(λ)∣2dλ.
(30)
Changing the harmless constant in the norm gives the same space. A distribution on X belongs to the Local Sobolev space Hlocs​(X) when χu, extended by zero outside X, belongs to Hs(Rn) for every test function χ∈Cc∞​(X).
A compactly supported distribution of finite order of a distribution m has a smooth Fourier transform satisfying ∣u(λ)∣≤C(1+∣λ∣)m, by applying the finite-order estimate to a fixed cutoff times the exponential. Thus its weighted squared transform is bounded by C⟨λ⟩2(s+m). This is integrable precisely in the sufficient range 2(s+m)<−n, and proves
u∈Hs(Rn)for every s<−m−n/2.​
(31)
This is the negative Sobolev regularity of a compactly supported distribution; the strict inequality is important, and is not a claim that this sufficient threshold is optimal for each distribution.
To establish elliptic regularity without assuming the answer as an a priori smoothness hypothesis, first obtain a global constant-coefficient gain. For a compactly supported distribution w with P(D)w∈Hq, the polynomial lower bound at high frequency gives
∫∣λ∣≥R​⟨λ⟩2(q+N)∣w∣2dλ≤C∫∣λ∣≥R​⟨λ⟩2q∣P(D)w​∣2dλ<∞.
(32)
On ∣λ∣≤R, the transform of w is smooth and bounded. Therefore P(D)w∈Hq implies w∈Hq+N. Possible low-frequency zeros of P are harmless; we never divide by them.
Two elementary mapping facts supply the variable-coefficient argument. Distributional derivatives of order j map Hr into Hr−j. Sobolev multiplication by a smooth cutoff is bounded on Hr for every real r, including negative ones. Indeed, for a compactly supported smooth a, the Peetre weight inequality
⟨ξ⟩r≤Cr​⟨η⟩r⟨ξ−η⟩∣r∣
(33)
and aw=(2π)−na∗w reduce the bound to Young's convolution inequality with the integrable kernel ⟨ζ⟩∣r∣∣a(ζ)∣. Smooth coefficients only need this property on compact subsets, where they can be multiplied by another cutoff.
Write the lower-order part as B=∑∣α∣<N​fα​Dα, with N≥1, and suppose provisionally that u∈Hlocr​ on a relatively compact neighborhood. For a cutoff function χ supported there,
P(D)(χu)=χLu−χBu+[P(D),χ]u.
(34)
The bracket is the operator commutator. Its order is at most N−1: in the product rule, every surviving term has at least one derivative falling on χ. Choose a second cutoff function equal to one near suppχ when estimating the products. The derivative and smooth-multiplication bounds then give
χBu, [P(D),χ]u∈Hr−N+1,χLu∈Hs.
(35)
The global gain just proved applies to the compactly supported distribution χu, and yields
u∈Hlocmin(s+N,r+1)​.​
(36)
This is the cutoff bootstrap for local elliptic regularity; it works for real indices, not just nonnegative integers.
There is always a legitimate starting index. Around any fixed point choose χ0​=1 on a smaller neighborhood. The compactly supported distribution χ0​u has the negative Sobolev regularity proved above, so u∈Hlocr0​​ on that neighborhood for some finite r0​. The index need not be uniform over all of X. Repeating the one-step gain finitely many times reaches s+N, or the target is already reached if r0​≥s+N. Since the point was arbitrary,
Lu∈Hlocs​(X) ⟹ u∈Hlocs+N​(X).​
(37)
For N=0 the result follows directly by dividing by the nonzero constant.
Finally, if Lu=0, its forcing belongs to every Local Sobolev space. The gain consequently gives every local Sobolev order for u. For each integer j≥0, choose an order larger than j+n/2 and apply the Sobolev embedding theorem to a localized solution. It has a Cj representative; the representatives agree because they represent the same distribution. Thus every distributional solution of Lu=0 is smooth on X.
