The Fréchet derivative at p=(x0​,y0​) is a linear map L:R2→R for which
f(p+h)=f(p)+Lh+o(∥h∥)(h→0).
(34)
This is differentiability; if it holds, L(h1​,h2​)=D1​f(p)h1​+D2​f(p)h2​.
To prove the sufficient condition, take a rectangle about p contained in the open domain. Split the increment along its coordinate directions:
f(x0​+h,y0​+k)−f(p)=[f(x0​+h,y0​+k)−f(x0​,y0​+k)]+[f(x0​,y0​+k)−f(p)].
(35)
The one-variable mean value theorem, applicable because the restrictions are differentiable, represents these two terms as
hD1​f(x0​+θh,y0​+k)+kD2​f(x0​,y0​+ϕk),0<θ,ϕ<1.
(36)
The zero-increment cases are interpreted directly. Subtract D1​f(p)h+D2​f(p)k. By continuity of both partial derivatives at p, the remainder is at most ε(∣h∣+∣k∣)≤2​εh2+k2​ once the increment is small. This is the required little-oh remainder and proves differentiability.
The converse is false. Set f(x,y)=x2sin(1/x) for x=0, and f(0,y)=0. At each point with x=0, its Fréchet derivative is zero, since the function increment has magnitude at most h2=o(h2+k2​). It is smooth away from this line. But
D1​f(x,y)=2xsin(1/x)−cos(1/x)(x=0),D1​f(0,y)=0,
(37)
is not continuous there. Thus a function can be differentiable everywhere while its partial derivatives are discontinuous.
For h, every point with x=0 is a smooth point. At the origin, ∣h(x,y)∣≤∣xy∣≤(x2+y2)/2, so h is differentiable with zero derivative. At (0,y0​) with y0​=0, the putative first partial derivative would require the limit y0​sin(1/x), which does not exist. Consequently the exact set of differentiability is
{(x,y):x=0} ∪ {(0,0)}.​
(38)
