With the usual commutative, unital conventions, the chain is
field ⟹ principal ideal domain ⟹ unique factorization domain ⟹ integral domain.​
(31)
A field is an integral domain, and every nonzero ideal contains an invertible element and is consequently the whole ring. Its only ideals are (0) and (1), so it is a principal ideal domain.
To prove the middle implication, first show that a principal ideal domain has the ascending chain condition on ideals. The union of an ascending chain is an ideal, hence is (a); its generator belongs to one member of the chain, and all later members equal (a). Now suppose a nonzero nonunit admitted no factorization into irreducible elements. It would factor into two nonunits, at least one of which again has no such factorization. Repeating would produce a strictly increasing chain of principal ideals: if a=bc with c a nonunit, (a)⊊(b), since equality and cancellation would make c invertible. This contradicts the ascending chain condition. Existence of factorization follows. By the permitted fact that every irreducible element is a prime element in a principal ideal domain, any irreducible factor in one factorization divides a factor in the other; cancellation then proves uniqueness up to units and order. This is a unique factorization domain. The final implication is part of the definition of a unique factorization domain.
None of the adjacent implications reverses. The principal ideal domain Z is not a field, since 2 is not invertible. The polynomial ring Q[x,y] is a unique factorization domain, by applying the polynomial extension theorem for unique factorization domains twice, but its ideal (x,y) is not principal: a generator would divide both x and y, so would be a unit, whereas every member of this ideal vanishes at the origin. Finally, Z[−5​] is an integral domain as a subring of C, but is not a unique factorization domain. Its multiplicative norm is N(a+b−5​)=a2+5b2. There is no element of norm 2 or 3. Hence 2,3,1+−5​,1−−5​, of norms 4,9,6,6, are all irreducible: a nontrivial factorization would require one of those missing norms. The only units have norm one and are ±1. The two factorizations
6=2⋅3=(1+−5​)(1−−5​)
(32)
are therefore not related by associates and reordering.

A metric space is complete if every Cauchy sequence in it converges to a point of that space. The stated shrinking-set characterization is the Cantor intersection theorem together with its converse.
First assume completeness, and choose xn​∈An​. If m,n≥N, then both points belong to AN​, so d(xm​,xn​)≤diam(AN​). The vanishing diameters imply that (xn​) is a Cauchy sequence, hence it converges to some x∈X. For every fixed N, its tail belongs to the closed set AN​, so x∈AN​. Thus x∈⋂N​AN​. In fact the intersection contains only one point, since any two of its points have distance at most every diameter.
Conversely assume the stated intersection property and let (xn​) be a Cauchy sequence. Put
AN​={xn​:n≥N}​X.
(39)
These closures are nonempty, closed and nested. Taking a closure does not increase the diameter: approximate two points of the closure by points of the set and use continuity of distance. The Cauchy sequence therefore makes diam(AN​)→0. Choose x in the intersection. For n≥N, both xn​ and x belong to AN​, and d(xn​,x)≤diam(AN​)→0. Every Cauchy sequence converges in X, proving completeness.

(48)
With ρ=2r and ϕ=θ/2, this becomes dρ2+ρ2dϕ2, the Euclidean metric in polar coordinates. The map (r,θ)↦(2rcos(θ/2),2rsin(θ/2)) is therefore a local isometry. It is local rather than global because increasing θ by 2π increases ϕ by π.
Given two points, choose their angle representatives so that ∣θ2​−θ1​∣≤π. Their developed plane angles then differ by at most π/2. The straight segment between their developed positions stays away from the origin: it lies in an angular sector of width at most π/2 containing both positive-radius endpoints. Develop that segment back onto the cone. Since local isometries preserve geodesics, it gives a geodesic joining the two points; it can be parametrized by arc length. This constructs geodesics on a punctured circular cone. For a repeated point the constant curve is the degenerate geodesic. In particular, this argument does not appeal to completeness of the punctured cone.
The joining geodesic need not be unique. For endpoints with equal positive radius and opposite azimuth, one may use the angular changes +π and −π. In the plane these give two straight segments from (ρ,0) to (0,ρ) and (0,−ρ), respectively. Both avoid the origin. Their lifts pass on opposite sides of the cone and have distinct images, so a reparametrization cannot identify them.

On a directed flow network with capacities ce​, choose flows 0≤fe​≤ce​ satisfying inflow equals outflow at every vertex except source s and sink t, and maximize the net source outflow ∣f∣. For a cut of a flow network S containing s but not t, its capacity is ∑u∈S,v∈/S​cuv​. The max-flow min-cut theorem states
fmax​∣f∣=s∈S, t∈/Smin​c(S,Sc).​
(78)
Every feasible flow is bounded above by every cut capacity; equality for a feasible flow and a cut proves optimality.
The Ford-Fulkerson algorithm starts with a feasible flow, for instance zero. A forward edge has residual capacity cuv​−fuv​ and its reverse edge has residual capacity fuv​. Find a source-to-sink augmenting path in this residual network, and increase the flow along it by the minimum residual capacity on the path, reducing original-edge flows when reverse edges are used. Repeat until no such path exists. The vertices reachable from the source then define a saturated minimum cut of a flow network. With integer capacities and zero initial flow, all residual capacities and augmentations are integers. Each augmentation increases ∣f∣ by at least one, while ∣f∣ is bounded by the finite sum of source capacities, so termination takes finitely many steps.
