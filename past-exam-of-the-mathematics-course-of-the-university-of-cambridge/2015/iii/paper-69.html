i OurBigBook.com
For positive linear operators on continuous functions, a general Korovkin theorem, in its compact Korovkin test space form, can be stated using a test space H⊂C(K,R), where K is a compact Hausdorff space. Assume H contains a strictly positive function h0​. Require that, for every x∈K, the only positive linear functional L on C(K) with L(h)=h(x) for all h∈H is evaluation at x. Then convergence on the test space implies convergence on every continuous function:
∥Un​h−h∥∞​⟶0 (h∈H)⟹∥Un​f−f∥∞​⟶0 (f∈C(K)).​
(1)
By the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem, the functional condition equivalently says that a positive measure with these test moments must be δx​. This permits arbitrary test families, not just the interval tests 1,x,x2.
For completeness, let c=minK​h0​>0. Positivity gives Un​1≤c−1Un​h0​, so the operator norms are uniformly bounded. If the conclusion failed for some f, choose xn​ where its error is bounded away from zero. The evaluation functionals Ln​(g)=Un​g(xn​) have uniformly bounded norm. Compactness of K and the Banach-Alaoglu theorem give a subnet on which xn​→x and Ln​ converges in the weak-star topology to a positive L. For every h∈H, uniform test convergence gives L(h)=h(x). The hypothesis forces L(f)=f(x), contradicting the chosen errors. Complex-valued functions follow by applying the real result to their real and imaginary parts.
A useful concrete sufficient condition is that 1∈H and for each x there is gx​∈H with gx​≥0 and zero set exactly {x}. Its zero moment forces the representing measure to be supported at x, while the constant moment fixes its mass.
For the circle, take H=span{1,sint,cost}. The nonnegative function gx​(t)=1−cos(t−x) belongs to H and vanishes only at x on the circle. Thus the periodic Korovkin test set is
1, sinx, cosx.​
(2)
Uniform convergence on these three functions is sufficient for uniform convergence on C(T).

Apply the periodic Korovkin theorem to the constant sequence Un​=U. Since it fixes the three test functions, the theorem gives Uf=f for every f.
One can also see the rigidity directly. For fixed x, the positive linear functional Lx​(f)=(Uf)(x) has Lx​(1)=1 and is represented by a probability measure. The fixed sine and cosine imply Lx​(1−cos(t−x))=0. The integrand is nonnegative with unique zero x on the circle, so the measure is δx​. Thus (Uf)(x)=f(x) at every point. The operator is uniquely the identity: U=I​.

An orthonormal multiresolution analysis consists of closed subspaces (Vj​)j∈Z​ of the L2 space L2(R) with Vj​⊂Vj+1​, dense union, intersection {0}, and the dilation rule f∈Vj​⟺f(2⋅)∈Vj+1​. A scaling function ϕ has integer translates forming an orthonormal basis of V0​. Equivalently,
ϕj,n​(x)=2j/2ϕ(2jx−n),n∈Z,
(7)
form an orthonormal basis of Vj​. Let Wj​=Vj+1​⊖Vj​. Density and the trivial intersection imply L2(R)=⨁j∈Z​Wj​.
Nesting gives the scaling refinement equation ϕ(x)=∑n​an​ϕ(2x−n), with hn​=an​/2​ the coefficients in the normalized finer-scale basis. Orthogonality of the translates gives ∑n​an​an−2r​​=2δr0​. Its Fourier form is the quadrature mirror filter identity ∣m(t)∣2+∣m(t+π)∣2=1, where m(t)=21​∑n​an​e−int.
Set bn​=(−1)na1−n​​ and define ψ(x)=∑n​bn​ϕ(2x−n) in L2. Its high-pass symbol is q(t)=−e−itm(t+π)​. The matrix
(m(t)q(t)​m(t+π)q(t+π)​)
(8)
is unitary almost everywhere. Under the identification of V1​ with its finer-scale coefficient space, this two-channel unitary transform splits that space into the translates of ϕ and the translates of ψ. The latter are therefore an orthonormal basis of W0​. Dilating gives an orthonormal wavelet basis
{2j/2ψ(2jx−n):j,n∈Z} of L2(R).​
(9)
This wavelet completion of a multiresolution filter explains how the multiresolution analysis supplies a single orthonormal wavelet. The argument uses coefficient-space completeness, not just pairwise orthogonality.

Use the unnormalized Fourier transform in the question and the Plancherel theorem, with inverse factor 1/(2π). For a general L2 scaling function, the transform is interpreted in the L2 sense; the printed integral need not be absolutely convergent. All frequency identities are almost-everywhere statements.
Changing variables in the Fourier transform gives
ϕ(2⋅−n)​(ξ)=21​e−inξ/2f(ξ/2).
(10)
Thus taking the transform of the scaling refinement equation gives f(ξ)=m(ξ/2)f(ξ/2), or
f(2t)=m(t)f(t),m(t)=21​n∑​an​e−int.​
(11)
Conversely the same calculation and injectivity of the Fourier transform recover refinement, with convergence understood in L2.
To justify both the orthogonality assertion and this convergence precisely, put P(t)=∑k∈Z​∣f(t+2πk)∣2. It belongs to L1[−π,π] by monotone integration. The Plancherel theorem gives
⟨ϕ,ϕ(⋅−j)⟩=2π1​∫−ππ​P(t)e−ijtdt.
(12)
Hence orthonormality of all integer translates is equivalent, by uniqueness of Fourier coefficients in L1, to the periodized-energy condition
P(t)=1almost everywhere.​
(13)
When this holds, the squared norm of ∑n​an​ϕ(2⋅−n) is 21​∑n​∣an​∣2, so square-summable coefficient series converge in L2. In the converse direction the refinement identity and P=1 give, by splitting the periodization of ∣f(2t)∣2 into even and odd translates,
1=∣m(t)∣2+∣m(t+π)∣2.
(14)
Thus m is bounded and its Fourier coefficients an​/2 are square summable. If mN​ are its Fourier partial sum, then
∫R​∣mN​(ξ/2)−m(ξ/2)∣2∣f(ξ/2)∣2dξ=2∫−ππ​∣mN​(t)−m(t)∣2P(t)dt⟶0.
(15)
This verifies the transformed refinement series converges to f, completing the equivalence of the two pairs of conditions without imposing an unnecessary L1 assumption on ϕ.

The half-open intervals [−π+2πk,π+2πk) tile the line, so exactly one term contributes to ∑k​∣f(t+2πk)∣2. It equals one almost everywhere.

ω2​(f,∣t∣)≤(1+∣t∣/h)2ω2​(f,h).

Let u∗∈U, e=f−u∗, d=∥e∥∞​, and E={x:∣e(x)∣=d}. The real Kolmogorov criterion for uniform approximation says u∗ is best if and only if
for every v∈U,x∈Emin​e(x)v(x)≤0.​
(25)
Equivalently, no direction v has the error's sign strictly at every extremal point. If d=0, the criterion is automatic.
For sufficiency, a point with e(x)v(x)≤0 gives ∣e(x)−v(x)∣≥d, so u∗+v cannot improve the norm. For necessity, suppose e(x)v(x)>0 on E. Compactness gives a positive lower bound there and on a neighbourhood of E. On that neighbourhood, (e−τv)2=e2−2τev+τ2v2<d2 for sufficiently small positive τ. Off the neighbourhood, ∣e∣ has a strict gap below d, and small τ preserves that gap. Thus u∗+τv has smaller error, a contradiction. This also explains why the criterion involves all active extrema.

Let p∈Pn​ and e=f−p, with d=∥e∥∞​>0. The Chebyshev alternation theorem is
p is best⟺∃x0​<⋯<xn+1​,e(xi​)=ε(−1)id,ε∈{1,−1}.​
(26)
If such points exist and a polynomial v∈Pn​ had e(x)v(x)>0 at every extremum, its signs at those n+2 points would alternate. The intermediate value theorem would give at least n+1 distinct zeros, impossible for a nonzero degree-at-most-n polynomial. The Kolmogorov criterion for uniform approximation therefore proves sufficiency.
For necessity, let E+​={e=d} and E−​={e=−d}. They are disjoint compact sets. If one set is empty there is just one sign block. Otherwise their positive separation implies that, reading the extremal set from left to right, its signs form finitely many alternating blocks. If there are fewer than n+2 alternating extrema, there are at most n+1 blocks. Place one point ξj​ in each extremum-free gap between consecutive blocks. A scalar multiple of v(x)=∏j​(x−ξj​) can be chosen to have exactly the sign of e on every block. Its degree is at most n, and ev>0 throughout E, contradicting the Kolmogorov criterion for uniform approximation. Thus there must be at least n+2 alternating extrema. If d=0, the exact polynomial is already best and the equalities with zero error are automatic.

Fix the degree bound n. Existence can be proved without a general uniqueness theorem: a minimizing sequence in Pn​ is uniformly bounded because its distances from f are bounded. Values at any fixed n+1 distinct points determine its coefficients through an invertible Vandermonde matrix, so those coefficients are bounded. A convergent subsequence supplies a polynomial attaining the minimum.
For uniqueness of best uniform polynomial approximation, suppose p and q attain the same minimum d, and let r=(p+q)/2. The triangle inequality makes r another minimizer. If d=0, both polynomials equal f, so assume d>0. Put e=f−r and E={∣e∣=d}. At each x∈E, the two real errors f−p and f−q lie in [−d,d] and their average equals an endpoint. Hence they are equal there, and p(x)=q(x).
The set E must contain at least n+2 points. Otherwise interpolate the values signe(x) on its at most n+1 points by a polynomial v∈Pn​. Then ev>0 on E. Continuity gives this positivity on a neighbourhood of E, while the error has a strict gap below d on the compact complement. A sufficiently small positive multiple of v decreases the maximum error, contradicting optimality of r. This is an elementary perturbation argument, not an appeal to Haar's theorem.
Thus p−q has at least n+2 distinct zeros. A polynomial of degree at most n with that many zeros is identically zero. The best uniform polynomial is unique for every fixed degree bound: p=q​.

Write gt​(y)=(y−t)+k−1​. Begin with distinct increasing knots so ordinary polynomial interpolation is unambiguous. The two interpolants agree at the k−1 common knots ti+1​,…,ti+k−1​. Their difference is consequently a scalar multiple of the monic polynomial ωi​ of degree k−1.
The leading coefficient of an interpolant is its highest divided difference. Thus this scalar is
[ti+1​,…,ti+k​]gt​−[ti​,…,ti+k−1​]gt​=(ti+k​−ti​)[ti​,…,ti+k​]gt​=Ni​(t).
(27)
The equality is precisely the divided-difference recurrence. This proves the Lee interpolation identity:
ℓi+1​(x,t)−ℓi​(x,t)=ωi​(x)Ni​(t).​
(28)
For k=1 the common-knot product is empty; the same argument is a difference of constants. Interpret (y−t)+0​ as 1y>t​ to obtain the usual half-open order-one B-splines. Repeated knots use the standard confluent or limiting interpretation, where the interpolation data are defined.
Sum over i=1,…,n. The right side telescopes to ℓn+1​(x,t)−ℓ1​(x,t). When tk​<t<tn+1​, the first interpolation nodes all lie below t, so ℓ1​=0. The last interpolation nodes all lie above t, so ℓn+1​ interpolates the polynomial (x−t)k−1 of degree k−1 and equals it identically. Hence the Marsden identity follows:
(x−t)k−1=i=1∑n​ωi​(x)Ni​(t),tk​<t<tn+1​.​

Use the standard partition normalization of B-splines, not rescaling each spline to have supremum exactly one. Let hi​=ti+k​−ti​>0. Then Mi​=kNi​/hi​. In the ordinary real inner product, the mixed-normalization spline Gram matrix is G=D−1H, where D=diag(hi​/k) and Hij​=(Ni​,Nj​). Since the Ni​ form a basis, H is positive definite; D is invertible, so G is invertible. The undeclared upper index h in the printed matrix should be n.
We need the standard positivity and normalization properties with their justification. The Cox-de Boor recurrence is
Ni,k​(t)=ti+k−1​−ti​t−ti​​Ni,k−1​(t)+ti+k​−ti+1​ti+k​−t​Ni+1,k−1​(t),
(37)
with zero-width terms interpreted as zero. It starts from nonnegative interval indicators. On each lower-order spline's support, its recurrence coefficients are nonnegative, so induction gives Ni​≥0. Extend the finite knots outside their endpoints to a full knot sequence. In the full sum, coefficients of each lower-order spline add to one, so the recurrence preserves partition of unity. Our finite collection is a subset of nonnegative B-splines; therefore ∑i=1n​Ni​(t)≤1 everywhere. The Marsden identity gives equality on the basic knot interval.
The divided-difference definition also gives support in [ti​,ti+k​]: below the left endpoint the knot-data function is a polynomial of degree k−1, whose order-k divided difference vanishes; above the right endpoint the knot data vanish. Choose a<ti​ and b>ti+k​. Integrating in t and then taking the divided difference in its knot variable gives
∫Mi​(t)dt=k[ti​,…,ti+k​](k(⋅−a)k​)=1,
(38)
because an order-k divided difference of a monic degree-k polynomial is one. The support lies inside [0,1], so ∫01​Mi​=1.
Orthogonality of f−s∗ to the spline space gives Ga=r, with ri​=(Mi​,f). Since Mi​≥0 and has unit integral, ∣ri​∣≤∥f∥∞​. The induced matrix maximum norm is the absolute row-sum norm, so
∥a∥ℓ∞​≤∥G−1∥ℓ∞​∥f∥∞​,∣s∗(t)∣≤∥a∥ℓ∞​∑i​Ni​(t)≤∥a∥ℓ∞​.
(39)
Taking the supremum over nonzero f proves the maximum-norm bound for spline projection:
∥PS​∥∞​≤∥G−1∥ℓ∞​.​
(40)
The range is S, so the printed phrase “onto C[0,1]” must mean into C[0,1], and onto S. Continuity of the range requires continuous B-splines, for example k≥2 and internal multiplicities at most k−1. For order-one or discontinuous B-splines, the same bound is valid with codomain L∞[0,1] rather than C[0,1].

Fix i. If t<ti​, all knot values of the function x↦(x−t)+k−1​ agree with a polynomial of degree k−1. Its order-k divided difference vanishes. If t>ti+k​, all the knot values are zero, so the same divided difference again vanishes. Thus the support is finite:
suppMi​⊆[ti​,ti+k​],suppNi​⊆[ti​,ti+k​].​
(41)
The support and Gram bandwidth of B-splines also holds at repeated knots: the same conclusion follows with the standard limiting convention. If j≥i+k, then tj​≥ti+k​, so the two supports have no positive-length overlap. An endpoint intersection contributes zero to the integral defining Gij​. The case i≥j+k is identical. Hence
Gij​=0when ∣i−j∣≥k,d=k.​
(42)
For strictly increasing knots, B-splines whose indices differ by k−1 overlap on a positive-length interval and their product is positive there, so this universal bound is sharp. In standard matrix terminology the half-bandwidth is k−1 and the full bandwidth is 2k−1; the paper's integer d is k.
