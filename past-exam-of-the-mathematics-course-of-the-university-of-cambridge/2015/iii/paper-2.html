A nilpotent endomorphism x satisfies xN=0 for some positive integer N. A nilpotent Lie algebra g has a terminating lower central series of a Lie algebra: with γ1​=g and γr+1​=[g,γr​], one has γr​=0 for some r. These are different conditions: the first concerns a particular linear map, whereas the second concerns repeated Lie brackets.
The representation form of Engel theorem says that if a finite-dimensional Lie subalgebra L⊆Endk​(V) consists entirely of nilpotent endomorphisms, with 0<dimV<∞, then V contains a nonzero vector annihilated by every element of L. Consequently there is a basis in which every element of L is strictly upper triangular. No assumption on the characteristic of a field or algebraic closure is required.
We prove the common-vector assertion by induction on dimL, simultaneously for every nonzero finite-dimensional representation space. The zero algebra is immediate. First, nilpotence of commutation by a nilpotent endomorphism follows from
(adx)r(T)=∑j=0r​(−1)j(jr​)xr−jTxj.
(1)
If xN=0, the right-hand side vanishes for r≥2N−1, in every characteristic of a field. For any proper Lie subalgebra M⊊L, its Adjoint representation on L/M therefore consists of nilpotent endomorphisms. Its image has dimension at most dimM<dimL, so the inductive assertion gives a nonzero class y+M annihilated by M. Equivalently y∈/M and [M,y]⊆M. Thus the Engel normalizer lemma gives M⊊NL​(M).
Choose a maximal proper Lie subalgebra M. Its normalizer of a Lie subalgebra must be all of L, so M is an ideal of a Lie algebra. Moreover L/M has dimension one: otherwise a one-dimensional Lie subalgebra of this quotient would have a proper inverse image strictly between M and L. By induction the space W={v∈V:Mv=0} is nonzero. It is invariant under L, since m(xw)=x(mw)+[m,x]w=0. Write L=M+kx. The restriction of the nilpotent endomorphism x to W has a nonzero kernel, and any vector in that kernel is annihilated by all of L. This completes the induction. Apply the assertion repeatedly to V/kv to obtain a complete invariant flag 0=V0​⊂V1​⊂⋯⊂Vd​=V with LVj​⊆Vj−1​. A basis adapted to that flag makes every matrix strictly upper triangular.
A nilpotent abstract Lie algebra need not act by nilpotent matrices. For V=0, the scalar algebra kI⊆gl(V) is an abelian Lie algebra, hence a nilpotent Lie algebra, but IN=I=0. No change of basis makes I strictly upper triangular. The failed implication is precisely the distinction described in nilpotent Lie algebras need not act nilpotently.
If g is a nilpotent Lie algebra, then (adx)rg⊆γr+1​, so every adx is a nilpotent endomorphism. Conversely, apply Engel theorem to adg acting on g. Its invariant flag is lowered by every Adjoint representation matrix, so every product of d=dimg such matrices vanishes. Since γd+1​ is spanned by expressions adx1​⋯adxd​(y), the lower central series of a Lie algebra terminates. Therefore
g is nilpotent⟺adx is nilpotent for every x∈g.​
(2)
Finally suppose k is an algebraically closed field. If some adx is not a nilpotent endomorphism, it has a nonzero eigenvalue λ and an eigenvector y, giving [x,y]=λy. The vectors x,y are linearly independent and span a nonabelian two-dimensional Lie subalgebra. Conversely, a Lie subalgebra of a nilpotent Lie algebra is nilpotent because its lower central series of a Lie algebra lies termwise in the ambient series. A nonabelian two-dimensional Lie algebra has a basis u,v with [u,v]=v: choose v spanning the nonzero derived algebra and rescale a complementary vector. Its Adjoint representation has the nonzero eigenvalue 1, so it is not nilpotent. This proves the two-dimensional subalgebra criterion for Lie algebra nilpotence:
g is nilpotent⟺every two-dimensional subalgebra is abelian.​
(3)

Use the sl2 Lie algebra relations [H,X]=2X, [H,Y]=−2Y and [X,Y]=H. A highest-weight vector satisfies Xv=0 and Hv=mv, and HYjv=(m−2j)Yjv. For k=0, XYv=YXv+Hv=mv. Assuming the formula at k−1, one gets
XYk+1v=(YX+H)Ykv=[k(m−k+1)+m−2k]Ykv=(k+1)(m−k)Ykv.
(4)
Thus the sl2 highest-weight lowering formula is
XYk+1v=(k+1)(m−k)Ykv(k≥0).​
(5)
In particular a finite-dimensional Irreducible Lie algebra representation has highest weight m∈Z≥0​ and weight vectors v,Yv,…,Ymv, as in the classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations.
Let Vλ​ be a finite-dimensional Irreducible Lie algebra representation of a complex semisimple Lie algebra, with highest weight λ. Write mλ​(μ)=dim(Vλ​)μ​, taking it to be zero when μ is not a weight, and let ρ be the half-sum of positive roots. The Killing form induces an inner product on the real span of weights. Freudenthal multiplicity formula states
((λ+ρ,λ+ρ)−(μ+ρ,μ+ρ))mλ​(μ)=2α>0∑​j≥1∑​(μ+jα,α)mλ​(μ+jα).​
(6)
The sums are finite. For a weight μ=λ, the coefficient on the left is positive: move μ into the dominant Weyl chamber, use that a weight is below λ in dominance order, and note that (μ,ρ) does not increase on moving back out of that chamber. The resulting recursion starts from mλ​(λ)=1.
Here is a proof using the allowed Casimir operator. Choose root vectors Eα​,Fα​ for positive and negative roots, normalized by B(Eα​,Fα​)=1, and let tα​∈h satisfy B(tα​,h)=α(h). Invariance of a bilinear form on a Lie algebra gives [Eα​,Fα​]=tα​. If hi​,hi are dual bases of the Cartan subalgebra under B, the Casimir operator is
C=∑i​hi​hi+∑α>0​(Eα​Fα​+Fα​Eα​).
(7)
On the weight space of μ, the first sum acts by (μ,μ). Replacing Eα​Fα​ with Fα​Eα​+tα​ gives
C​(Vλ​)μ​​=(μ,μ+2ρ)I+2∑α>0​Fα​Eα​​(Vλ​)μ​​.
(8)
Define Tα​(μ)=tr(Fα​Eα​​(Vλ​)μ​​). The cyclic trace identity between adjacent weight spaces and the commutator relation yield
Tα​(μ)=tr(Eα​Fα​​(Vλ​)μ+α​​)=Tα​(μ+α)+(μ+α,α)mλ​(μ+α).
(9)
Iterate upwards until the weight spaces vanish to obtain Tα​(μ)=∑j≥1​(μ+jα,α)mλ​(μ+jα). Finally take the trace of the displayed restriction of C. Its Casimir eigenvalue is (λ,λ+2ρ), so subtracting (μ,μ+2ρ)mλ​(μ) proves the formula. This trace argument handles weight multiplicities greater than one without choosing a separate sl2 Lie algebra string through each vector.

Work over C. Decompose V as the direct sum of the generalized eigenspaces Vλ​ of X. Define Xs​ to be λI on Vλ​ and set Xn​=X−Xs​. Then Xs​ is a diagonalisable endomorphism, Xn​ is a nilpotent endomorphism, and they commute. For uniqueness, any commuting decomposition X=S+N has S and N commuting with X, hence preserving each Vλ​. Decompose Vλ​ further into eigenspaces of S. On a nonzero such space with eigenvalue σ, the operator X=σI+N has only the eigenvalue σ, so σ=λ. Since S is diagonalizable, S=λI throughout Vλ​. Thus S=Xs​ and N=Xn​. This is the Additive Jordan decomposition.
The Chinese remainder theorem for the pairwise coprime polynomials (t−λ)rλ​ also gives a polynomial p with p(t)≡λ(mod(t−λ)rλ​), where rλ​ is the largest Jordan block size. Consequently Xs​=p(X) and Xn​=X−p(X). This polynomial description shows that both parts preserve every X-invariant subspace.
On Hom(Vλ​,Vμ​) the operator adXs​ is the scalar μ−λ, so it is diagonalizable. The nilpotence of commutation by a nilpotent endomorphism makes adXn​ nilpotent. They commute, since [adXs​,adXn​]=ad[Xs​,Xn​]=0. Uniqueness of the Additive Jordan decomposition therefore gives the adjoint compatibility of additive Jordan decomposition:
(adX)s​=adXs​,(adX)n​=adXn​.​
(10)
Now let g⊆gl(V) be a complex semisimple Lie algebra and X∈g. Since the semisimple part of adX is a polynomial in adX, it preserves g. Thus [Xs​,g]⊆g. By the Weyl complete reducibility theorem, the Adjoint representation of g on End(V) has a decomposition End(V)=g⊕M into invariant subspaces. Write Xs​=y+z with y∈g and z∈M. For a∈g, the vector [z,a]=[Xs​,a]−[y,a] lies in g, and invariance of M puts it in M as well. Hence [z,a]=0.
Decompose V=⨁j​Uj​ into Irreducible Lie algebra representations. Each Uj​ is preserved by Xs​=p(X) and by y, hence by z. The Schur lemma makes z∣Uj​​=cj​I. A semisimple Lie algebra is a perfect Lie algebra, so every representing element of g has zero trace on every Uj​. Also tr(Xs​∣Uj​​)=tr(X∣Uj​​), because Xn​ is nilpotent there. It follows that cj​dimUj​=tr(z∣Uj​​)=0. In characteristic zero, cj​=0, so z=0. Therefore Xs​∈g and Xn​=X−Xs​∈g. This proves that semisimple matrix Lie algebras are closed under additive Jordan decomposition, including representations with repeated isomorphic irreducible summands.

Take the Lie subalgebra b⊆gl2​(C) of all upper-triangular matrices. It is a solvable Lie algebra with a nonzero abelian ideal of a Lie algebra, hence is not a semisimple Lie algebra. For every X∈b, the polynomial description Xs​=p(X) in the Additive Jordan decomposition keeps Xs​ upper triangular; then Xn​=X−Xs​ is upper triangular too. Thus the upper-triangular matrix algebra is closed under Additive Jordan decomposition despite being nonsemisimple. A diagonal matrix algebra gives an even simpler abelian Lie algebra example.

Let N=(00​10​) and g=C(I+N)⊆gl2​(C). This one-dimensional abelian Lie algebra is not a semisimple Lie algebra. For X=I+N, its Additive Jordan decomposition is Xs​=I and Xn​=N. Neither belongs to g: comparing diagonal entries and then the upper-right entry rules out both scalar multiples. Therefore nonsemisimple matrix Lie subalgebras need not be closed under Additive Jordan decomposition.

Define the exterior square over the arbitrary field k by ⋀2V=(V⊗V)/span{v⊗v:v∈V}. Write the image of u⊗v as u∧v. Then v∧v=0 and u∧v=−v∧u; the basis is vi​∧vj​ with i<j, in characteristic two as well. The exterior-power Lie algebra representation is
x⋅(u∧v)=(xu)∧v+u∧(xv).​
(11)
The tensor product of Lie algebra representations descends to this quotient: x⋅(v⊗v)=xv⊗v+v⊗xv is a linear combination of square tensors, namely (v+xv)⊗(v+xv)−v⊗v−xv⊗xv. On the wedge basis its coefficients follow directly from the original representing matrices. Expanding both actions shows [ρ∧2​(x),ρ∧2​(y)]=ρ∧2​([x,y]).
The inclusions of V and W into their direct sum define the map
⋀2V⊕⋀2W⊕(V⊗W)⟶⋀2(V⊕W),(a,b,v⊗w)⟼a+b+(v,0)∧(0,w).
(12)
A combined basis shows that this sends a basis to the pure-V, pure-W and mixed wedge basis vectors, with no overlap and no omission. The defining action on each wedge proves equivariance. Hence the exterior square of a direct sum gives
⋀2​(V⊕W)≅⋀2​V⊕⋀2​W⊕(V⊗W).​
(13)
No division by 2 is involved, so the proof remains valid in characteristic two.
For the complex special linear Lie algebra sl3​, write Γa,b​ for the irreducible highest-weight representation with Dynkin labels (a,b), and V=Γ1,0​. First the sl3 decomposition of the symmetric-square dual tensor product is
A:=(S2V)⊗V∗≅Γ2,1​⊕Γ1,0​.
(14)
Indeed contraction c((uv)⊗f)=f(u)v+f(v)u is a surjective Lie algebra representation homomorphism onto V. Its 15-dimensional kernel contains the highest-weight vector e12​⊗e3∗​ of weight (2,1). The Weyl dimension formula gives dimΓ2,1​=15, and the Weyl complete reducibility theorem identifies the kernel and splits the map. With B=Γ2,1​, the direct-sum identity reduces the requested calculation to ⋀2B, B⊗V and ⋀2V.
For completeness, these decompositions can be checked entirely by formal characters. Let x1​x2​x3​=1, h1​=x1​+x2​+x3​, h1∗​=x1−1​+x2−1​+x3−1​ and h2​=∑i​xi2​+∑i<j​xi​xj​. Then χB​=h2​h1∗​−h1​. The Weyl character formula takes the determinant form
χa,b​(x)=det(x12​x22​x32​​x1​x2​x3​​111​)det​x1a+b+2​x2a+b+2​x3a+b+2​​x1b+1​x2b+1​x3b+1​​111​​​.
(15)
Substitute χB​ into χ∧2B​(x)=21​(χB​(x)2−χB​(x12​,x22​,x32​)) and collect the determinant characters. This gives the exterior square of the sl3 representation of highest weight (2,1) and the sl3 highest-weight tensor rule:
⋀2​BB⊗V​≅Γ5,0​⊕Γ2,3​⊕Γ3,1​⊕Γ1,2​⊕Γ0,1​,≅Γ3,1​⊕Γ1,2​⊕Γ2,0​,⋀2​V≅Γ0,1​.​
(16)
The first line has dimensions 21+42+24+15+3=105=(215​); the tensor product has dimension 24+15+6=45. Equivalently, enumerate weights with the sl3 interlacing character formula and subtract characters from the highest weight downwards. Finally
⋀2​((S2V)⊗V∗)≅Γ5,0​⊕Γ2,3​⊕2Γ3,1​⊕2Γ1,2​⊕Γ2,0​⊕2Γ0,1​.​
(17)
Its dimension is 21+42+48+30+6+6=153=(218​). The exterior square here is taken of the entire 18-dimensional tensor product; taking it only of S2V would be a different representation.

For a complex semisimple Lie algebra g, a Cartan subalgebra h is a maximal abelian subalgebra consisting of elements whose Adjoint representation matrices are diagonalizable. Equivalently it is a nilpotent self-normalizing Lie subalgebra. The root-space decomposition is
g=h⊕⨁α∈R​gα​,gα​={x:[h,x]=α(h)x for every h∈h},
(18)
where the roots are the nonzero weights of that Adjoint representation.
We use these properties of the Killing form B: it is nondegenerate on g and on h, it is invariant, distinct root spaces are orthogonal unless their roots sum to zero, and B pairs gα​ nondegenerately with g−α​. Define tα​ by B(tα​,h)=α(h), and choose e∈gα​, f∈g−α​ with B(e,f)=1. Since weights add, [e,f]∈h, and invariance gives
B([e,f],h)=B(e,[f,h])=α(h),[e,f]=tα​.
(19)
The nonisotropic root lemma shows α(tα​)=0. Here is its short proof: if this number were zero, the span of e,f,tα​ would be a solvable Lie algebra with tα​ central. Apply the Lie theorem to its action on g. The commutator adtα​=[ade,adf] would be strictly upper triangular and hence nilpotent. But tα​∈h makes it diagonalizable. It would therefore be zero, putting tα​ in the zero center of a Lie algebra of g, contrary to its definition. Set
Hα​=α(tα​)2tα​​,Xα​=e,Yα​=α(tα​)2f​.
(20)
Then
[Hα​,Xα​]=2Xα​,[Hα​,Yα​]=−2Yα​,[Xα​,Yα​]=Hα​,​
(21)
so their span is the sl2 subalgebra associated with a root.
The abstract reduced crystallographic root system axioms are as follows. In a finite-dimensional real inner product space E, the set R is finite, consists of nonzero vectors and spans E; for every α∈R, one has R∩Rα={α,−α}; the root reflection sα​(β)=β−2(β,α)α/(α,α) preserves R; and 2(β,α)/(α,α) is an integer for every pair of roots. We verify the positive-definite real form as well as these axioms, rather than assuming the complex Killing form is already positive.
First R is finite and has no zero element by its definition. The roots span h∗ over C: any h∈h annihilated by all roots commutes with the whole root-space decomposition and is central, hence zero. Therefore the tα​, and also the Hα​, span h over C. For every root β, the classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations applied to the Adjoint representation of the root subalgebra gives β(Hα​)∈Z. Put hR​=spanR​{Hα​:α∈R}. Every root takes real values on this space, and
B(h,h)=tr((adh)2)=∑β∈R​(dimgβ​)β(h)2>0(0=h∈hR​).
(22)
The strict inequality follows because the roots span h∗. This also shows that the complexification of hR​ injects into h: an equality h1​+ih2​=0 with real hj​ would contradict positivity of B(h1​,h1​) and B(h2​,h2​). Since its complex span is all of h, it is a real form. Moreover B(Hα​,Hα​)=4/α(tα​)>0, so tα​ is a real scalar multiple of Hα​. The dual inner product thus makes E=spanR​R a Euclidean space, as in the Euclidean subspace of a Cartan subalgebra.
To prove reducedness without assuming it, consider the root-subalgebra module
Mα​=h⊕⨁j∈Z∖{0}​gjα​,
(23)
with absent root spaces understood to be zero. Its Hα​ weights are even, so every nontrivial Irreducible Lie algebra representation in it has even positive highest weight and one-dimensional weight-zero space. The action of Xα​ on its weight-zero space h has image exactly CXα​, of dimension one. Therefore there is precisely one nontrivial irreducible summand, the already embedded adjoint sl2​ module of highest weight 2. Thus dimgα​=1 and 2α∈/R. If β=cα is any root on the same real line, the integral numbers β(Hα​)=2c and α(Hβ​)=2/c have product 4. Hence c is one of ±21​,±1,±2; the half and double cases are excluded by applying the preceding argument to the appropriate root. This proves the root-space reducedness lemma and R∩Rα={±α}.
For a root β not parallel to α, the sum of root spaces ⨁j∈Z​gβ+jα​ is stable under the root sl2​. Its integer Hα​ weights are symmetric under sign in each irreducible summand. Therefore the weight −β(Hα​) also occurs, at the root β−β(Hα​)α. The Killing form normalization gives
β(Hα​)=(α,α)2(β,α)​,
(24)
so this root is exactly sα​(β). For β=±α the reflection just swaps the two roots. This proves reflection invariance and the Cartan integer condition. All axioms of the reduced crystallographic root system have now been verified.

A real Lie group is a finite-dimensional real smooth manifold with a group structure whose multiplication and inversion are smooth. Its tangent space at the identity element e is the vector space g=Te​G of velocities of smooth curves through e. For the general linear group GLn​(R) it identifies with Mn​(R). The matrix exponential and local matrix logarithm are
expX=∑r=0∞​r!Xr​,log(I+A)=∑r=1∞​r(−1)r+1Ar​(∥A∥<1).
(25)
The exponential is globally defined, and each matrix expX is invertible with inverse exp(−X). The logarithm here is a local map near I; it is not a globally defined inverse on every real invertible matrix. The differential of exp at zero is the identity, and the two power series are inverse locally.
For a general G, the assumed Exponential map of a Lie group with dexp0​=I has a smooth local inverse by the inverse function theorem, giving a local exponential chart. Define the Lie bracket from local group commutators by
[X,Y]=∂s∂t∂2​​(0,0)​log(exp(sX)exp(tY)exp(−sX)exp(−tY)).​
(26)
For small s,t the group commutator lies in that chart. More explicitly, the map F(u,v)=log(expuexpvexp(−u)exp(−v)) is smooth and has F(u,0)=F(0,v)=0. Its mixed second differential at (0,0) is a bilinear map of X,Y, proving bilinearity. Swapping the two group elements inverts the commutator, and log(g−1)=−logg near e. Thus F(v,u)=−F(u,v) and [Y,X]=−[X,Y]. In the matrix group, expansion to the mixed term st gives the familiar commutator XY−YX.
For g∈G, let cg​(h)=ghg−1 and define the Adjoint representation of a Lie group by Adg​=d(cg​)e​∈GL(g). It is smooth and satisfies Adgh​=Adg​Adh​. Its derived representation is ad=d(Ad)e​:g→End(g). Naturality of the Exponential map of a Lie group under conjugation gives
exp(sX)exp(tY)exp(−sX)=exp(tAdexp(sX)​Y).
(27)
At t=0, the derivative of the logarithm of the commutator is Adexp(sX)​Y−Y: the derivative of multiplication at (e,e) adds tangent vectors, and dloge​=I. Differentiate in s to conclude
ad(X)Y=[X,Y].​
(28)
To prove the Jacobi identity without assuming it in the bracket construction, first establish that the differential of a Lie group homomorphism preserves Lie brackets. For a homomorphism f:G→H, the allowed exponential identity gives logH​(f(expG​u))=dfe​(u) locally. Apply this identity to the group commutator and take the mixed derivative to obtain dfe​([X,Y])=[dfe​X,dfe​Y]. In particular f=Ad:G→GL(g) gives
ad[X,Y]=[adX,adY].
(29)
Applying both sides to Z yields [[X,Y],Z]=[X,[Y,Z]]−[Y,[X,Z]]. Rearranging with antisymmetry proves
[X,[Y,Z]]+[Y,[Z,X]]+[Z,[X,Y]]=0.​
(30)
Neither injectivity of ad nor the existence of a global logarithm was used.
Finally let H be a normal Lie subgroup of G, with its corresponding Lie algebra h⊆g. For each g∈G, conjugation restricts to H, so its differential preserves h: Adg​h=h. Differentiate along g=exp(tX) to get [X,Y]=ad(X)Y∈h for every X∈g and Y∈h. Thus the Lie algebra of a normal Lie subgroup is an ideal of a Lie algebra. The assertion concerns a subgroup carrying the corresponding Lie-subgroup structure, for example any closed subgroup; no arbitrary abstract subgroup is being assigned a tangent space.
