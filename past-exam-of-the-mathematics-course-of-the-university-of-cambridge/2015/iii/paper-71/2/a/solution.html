We place the definitions and the unheaded preliminary requests here before addressing the first labelled property. The Schwartz space consists of smooth functions with finite seminorms
qα,β​(f)=supx∈Rn​∣xα∂βf(x)∣
(8)
for every pair of multi-indices. Convergence means convergence in each seminorm. The tempered distribution space S′(Rn) is its continuous dual space, with weak convergence tested against every Schwartz function. A continuous functional satisfies a bound by finitely many of these seminorms, equivalently by one sufficiently large weighted derivative seminorm.
Use the Fourier transform convention
f​(ξ)=∫e−ix⋅ξf(x)dx,f(x)=(2π)n1​∫eix⋅ξf​(ξ)dξ.
(9)
Differentiation under the integral and integration by parts express ξα∂ξβ​f​ as a constant of modulus one times the Fourier transform of ∂xα​(xβf). Its supremum is bounded by the L1 norm of that function. For s>n, this norm is at most a constant times finitely many Schwartz seminorms, using the integrable weight (1+∣x∣)−s. Thus F:S→S is continuous.
For completeness, Fourier inversion follows by inserting e−ε∣ξ∣2 in the inverse integral and using Fubini's theorem. The result is convolution with the Gaussian approximate identity
(4πε)−n/2e−∣x∣2/(4ε).
(10)
It tends to f, while integrability of f​ allows the damping factor to be removed by dominated convergence theorem. Consequently F2f=(2π)nf(−⋅). Reflection preserves every Schwartz seminorm, so the inverse transform is continuous as well. This proves the Fourier transform isomorphism of the Schwartz space.
Define the Fourier transform of a tempered distribution by transposition,
⟨u,ψ⟩=⟨u,ψ​⟩.
(11)
The Schwartz-space continuity just proved makes this a tempered distribution. Its inverse is (2π)−nRF, where ⟨Ru,ψ⟩=⟨u,ψ(−⋅)⟩. These maps are continuous for weak convergence, since each pairing is a pairing with a fixed transformed test. They are also continuous for the strong dual topology, because the Schwartz-space maps take bounded sets to bounded sets.
The convolution of a tempered distribution with a Schwartz function is
(u∗φ)(x)=⟨uy​,φ(x−y)⟩.
(12)
Smooth dependence of translated Schwartz functions gives ∂α(u∗φ)(x)=⟨u,∂αφ(x−⋅)⟩. The finite-seminorm estimate and 1+∣y∣≤(1+∣x∣)(1+∣x−y∣) show that each derivative has at most polynomial growth. In particular, u∗φ is a smooth function defining a tempered distribution. It need not itself be a Schwartz function; for example 1∗φ=∫φ.
Writing φˇ​(y)=φ(−y), its distributional pairing is ⟨u∗φ,ψ⟩=⟨u,φˇ​∗ψ⟩. The inner convolution is a Schwartz function, and this identity follows by integration in the Schwartz topology, justified by the weighted seminorm estimates. A direct Fubini's theorem calculation gives φˇ​∗ψ​=F(φ​ψ). Hence
⟨u∗φ​,ψ⟩=⟨u,F(φ​ψ)⟩=⟨u,φ​ψ⟩,u∗φ​=uφ​.​
(13)
Multiplication is well defined because multiplication by φ​ acts continuously on S.
Now write the Hilbert transform as convolution with K=π−1pv(1/x), the principal-value reciprocal distribution. The given Heaviside function transform, together with F2H=2πH(−⋅), yields
π−iF(pv(1/x))=2πH(−ξ),K(ξ)=−isgnξ.
(14)
The value of the sign function at zero is irrelevant to its regular distribution. Thus the Hilbert-transform Fourier multiplier is
Hφ​(ξ)=−isgn(ξ)φ​(ξ).
(15)
Applying Plancherel theorem, whose normalization here is ∥f∥22​=(2π)−1∥f​∥22​, proves the isometry:
∥Hφ∥L2​=∥φ∥L2​.​
(16)
The principal-value integral agrees with this convolution: near its singular point subtract φ(x), and use odd cancellation; at infinity the Schwartz decay gives convergence. The Hilbert transform has domain S, but generally does not take values in S, as the tail in part (c) demonstrates.
