A strong lifting lemma. Let R be a complete discrete valuation ring, with fraction field F and normalized discrete valuation v. A useful strong form of Hensel lemma says that for f∈R[T] and a∈R satisfying
v(f(a))>2v(f′(a)),
(1)
there is a root b∈R with
v(b−a)=v(f(a))−v(f′(a)).​
(2)
If f(a)=0, take b=a. Otherwise the hypothesis ensures that f′(a)=0. The root is unique among b satisfying v(b−a)>v(f′(a)). In particular, a simple root of the reduction of f lifts uniquely with its specified residue.
Here is a proof by Newton iteration over a valued field. Put a0​=a and
aj+1​=aj​−f′(aj​)f(aj​)​.
(3)
Write Aj​=v(f(aj​)) and B=v(f′(a0​)). Taylor expansion over R shows, inductively, that the increment has valuation Aj​−B>B, that v(f′(aj+1​))=B, and that
Aj+1​≥2Aj​−2B,Aj​−2B≥2j(A0​−2B).
(4)
Indeed, the constant and linear terms of f(aj+1​) cancel, leaving terms of valuation at least twice that of the increment. The change in f′ has valuation greater than B, so its valuation stays B. Thus all iterates lie in R and form a Cauchy sequence. Completeness and continuity give a root b. The later increments have strictly larger valuations than the first, proving the displayed distance formula. For uniqueness, if b,c are in the stated ball, Taylor expansion factors
f(b)−f(c)=(b−c)(f′(a)+η),v(η)>B.
(5)
The second factor is nonzero, so two roots must coincide. This proves Hensel's lemma in the form needed here.
Odd residue characteristic. For odd p, a p-adic unit u is a square exactly when its reduction is a square in Fp×​. Necessity follows by reduction. For sufficiency, a nonzero residue root of T2−u has derivative 2T invertible modulo p, so the Hensel lemma lifts it. The multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic, and its subgroup of squares has index two. Therefore
Zp×​/(Zp×​)2≅C2​(p odd).​
(6)
Representatives are 1 and any unit whose reduction is a quadratic nonresidue.
Residue characteristic two. An odd integer has square congruent to one modulo eight, so a square 2-adic unit must lie in 1+8Z2​. Conversely, if u≡1(mod8), take f(T)=T2−u and a=1. Then v2​(f(1))≥3>2v2​(f′(1))=2, and the strong form of Hensel lemma produces a square root. Hence
(Z2×​)2=1+8Z2​,Z2×​/(Z2×​)2≅(Z/8Z)×≅C2​×C2​.​
(7)
The four unit square classes of the p-adic integers have representatives 1,3,5,7.

The Hasse-Minkowski theorem says that a nondegenerate quadratic form over a number field has a nonzero isotropic vector over that field if and only if it has one over every completion. In particular, over Q one tests R and every Qℓ​. For the present integral form, a rational isotropic vector can be multiplied by a common denominator to give an integer one. This is the Hasse-Minkowski principle.
Write P=p, R=p+12 and S=p+24. All three primes are odd and exceed three: p=2 or 3 would make p+12 composite. The second supplementary law for quadratic reciprocity gives
(P2​)=(−1)(P2−1)/8.
(8)
Necessity at P. Scale any nonzero vector over QP​ so that its coordinates lie in ZP​ and at least one is a unit. Reduction of its equation modulo P gives y2=2z2, since twelve is invertible modulo P. If the Legendre symbol (2/P) is −1, this forces P∣y,z. The original equation then forces P∣x, because its other two terms are divisible by P2. This contradicts the normalization. Thus
a nonzero solution requires p≡1 or 7(mod8).​
(9)
Sufficiency at the coefficient primes. Suppose (2/P)=1. A nonzero solution modulo P is (x,y,z)=(0,2​,1). Its y-derivative is nonzero modulo P, so fixing the other two coordinates and applying the Hensel lemma lifts it to QP​.
Since S≡P(mod8), the same Legendre symbol criterion gives (2/S)=1. Modulo S, the equation reduces to y2=2x2, and the vector (1,2​,0) is a simple zero in the y coordinate. It lifts to QS​. Modulo R, the coefficients of x2 and z2 are respectively −12 and 12, so (1,0,1) is a zero with nonzero x-derivative. It lifts to QR​.
The remaining odd primes. If ℓ is odd and divides none of PRS, the reduced ternary quadratic form is nondegenerate. An elementary finite-field argument finds a zero with z=1: if its coefficients are A,B,C, the sets
{Ax2:x∈Fℓ​},{−C−By2:y∈Fℓ​}
(10)
both have (ℓ+1)/2 elements, so intersect. The resulting vector has nonzero z-derivative 2C, and the Hensel lemma lifts it. This isotropy of nondegenerate ternary quadratic forms over finite fields verifies every remaining odd completion without an extra congruence condition.
The dyadic and real places. Setting y=1,z=2 reduces the dyadic problem to
x2=−3−P84​.
(11)
The right side is a 2-adic unit congruent to −3−4=1 modulo eight. The unit square classes of the p-adic integers computed above show that it is a square in Q2​. This also explains the printed hint: its integer can be taken as m=−(p+21)/2. Over R, the positive and negative coefficients make the form indefinite; for instance x=0,z=1,y=S/R​ gives a nonzero zero.
All completions now have a nonzero isotropic vector. The Hasse-Minkowski theorem, followed by clearing denominators, proves
a nonzero integer solution exists ⟺p≡±1(mod8).​
(12)

Let e1​,…,ed​ be a Qp​-basis of K, and define the coordinate norm
​∑j​xj​ej​​0​=maxj​∣xj​∣p​.
(13)
First an extending absolute value on a field is automatically non-Archimedean. Its values on integers are at most one, and the binomial theorem gives
∣x+y∣m≤(m+1)max(∣x∣,∣y∣)m.
(14)
Taking mth roots and letting m→∞ proves the ultrametric inequality.
Consequently
∣x∣≤C∥x∥0​,C=maxj​∣ej​∣.
(15)
This bound makes x↦∣x∣ continuous for the coordinate topology. The coordinate sphere S={x:∥x∥0​=1} is a closed subset of Zpd​, hence compact. It contains no zero, so the continuous positive function ∣x∣ attains a positive minimum c there. Scaling any nonzero vector by a power of p to put it in S proves
c∥x∥0​≤∣x∣≤C∥x∥0​.​
(16)
This is the compact-sphere proof of finite-dimensional non-Archimedean norm equivalence.
A Cauchy sequence for ∣⋅∣ is therefore Cauchy in each coordinate. The completeness of Qp​ gives a coordinate limit, and the upper bound gives convergence to it for ∣⋅∣. Thus every extending absolute value makes K complete.
For uniqueness, if ∣⋅∣1​ and ∣⋅∣2​ are two extensions, their respective bounds give constants A,B>0 with
A∣x∣1​≤∣x∣2​≤B∣x∣1​.
(17)
Apply this to xm and use multiplicativity:
A1/m∣x∣1​≤∣x∣2​≤B1/m∣x∣1​.
(18)
Letting m→∞ proves ∣x∣1​=∣x∣2​​ for every x. This proves the unique extension of an absolute value to a finite extension without assuming that an arbitrary coordinate norm is multiplicative.

A number-field completion is finite. Let F be the given number field and L=Fp​ its completion. Normalize its absolute value on a field so that the restriction to Q is ∣⋅∣p​: if vp​(p)=e, use ∣x∣=p−vp​(x)/e. Changing the original absolute value by this positive power does not change its completion. The closure of Q in L is an isometric copy of Qp​.
Take a Q-basis of F and let V be its Qp​-span in L. Choose a maximal linearly independent subset of that finite spanning set. The compact-sphere proof of finite-dimensional non-Archimedean norm equivalence applies to this basis too, so V is complete and therefore closed in L. Since F⊂V is dense in L, it follows that V=L. Hence
[Fp​:Qp​]≤[F:Q]<∞.​
(19)
Every finite extension is obtained this way. Let E/Qp​ be finite. Use its extending Non-Archimedean absolute value, whose completeness and uniqueness were proved above. In characteristic zero the primitive element theorem gives E=Qp​(α); scaling α by a power of p, we may arrange that it is integral. Its monic minimal polynomial f belongs to Zp​[T]. If the degree is one, E=Qp​ is already the completion of Q.
Otherwise choose
0<δ<minα′=α​∣α−α′∣,
(20)
where α′ runs over the conjugates in an algebraic closure. Approximate the coefficients of f by integers, obtaining a monic g∈Z[T] of the same degree. Since f′(α)=0, sufficiently accurate approximation gives ∣g(α)∣<∣g′(α)∣2 and makes ∣g(α)/g′(α)∣<δ. The strong form of Hensel lemma in E produces a root β∈E of g with ∣β−α∣<δ.
For completeness, the required Krasner's lemma follows from the unique extension of an absolute value to a finite extension. In a finite Galois extension containing α,β, every automorphism σ fixing β is an isometry, and
∣σα−α∣≤max(∣σα−β∣,∣β−α∣)=∣α−β∣.
(21)
If σα=α, this contradicts the choice of δ. Thus every automorphism fixing β fixes α, and Galois correspondence gives Qp​(α)⊆Qp​(β). Since β∈E, equality follows. In particular g is irreducible over Qp​, and therefore over Q.
Put F=Q(β)⊂E. It is a number field, and the closure of F in E contains Qp​(β)=E, so F is dense. Let
p={b∈OF​:∣b∣<1}.
(22)
Every element of the ring of integers of a number field has absolute value at most one: otherwise its highest-degree term would strictly dominate all other terms of a monic integer equation. Thus reduction into the residue field of E makes p a prime ideal, and p∩Z=pZ. The restriction of the valuation to F is the valuation of this prime, up to normalization. Indeed, the localized discrete valuation ring (OF​)p​ maps into OE​, its units have absolute value one, and its uniformizer has absolute value less than one. Density and completeness give
Fp​≅E.​
(23)
This proves the local realization as a completion of a number field.

The valuation ring and local compactness. The opening, unlettered requests use
OK​={x:∣x∣≤1},m={x:∣x∣<1},k=OK​/m.​
(24)
The ultrametric inequality makes OK​ a valuation ring; its elements outside m are exactly the units, so m is its unique maximal ideal, and k is the residue field.
Here the customary nontrivial-valuation hypothesis for a local field is necessary. With the trivial absolute value, any infinite field is discrete and locally compact, but its residue field is infinite and there is no nonzero uniformizer. We use the nontrivial valuation in the conclusions below.
Local compactness first gives a compact neighborhood of zero. Choose a sufficiently small nonzero t so that the closed set tOK​ is contained in that compact neighborhood. Then tOK​, and hence OK​, is compact. Since m is open, its cosets give a discrete compact quotient k, which must be finite. Write ∣k∣=q. The subgroup m now has finite index and is closed in OK​, hence compact. The continuous function ∣⋅∣ attains on m a maximum c with 0<c<1. Choose π with ∣π∣=c. Every x∈m has ∣x/π∣≤1, so
m=πOK​,∣k∣=q<∞.​
(25)
Successive division by π shows that every nonzero element of OK​ is a unit times a power of π: division must terminate because ∣π∣j→0. Thus the value group is discrete. A Cauchy sequence eventually lies in a translate of the compact ring OK​, has a convergent subsequence, and consequently converges itself. These arguments prove the discrete valuation from nontrivial local compactness and the completeness of locally compact nontrivially valued fields.
The iterated-power limit. Normalize the discrete valuation by v(π)=1, and write q=ℓf, where ℓ is the residue characteristic. For u∈1+πsOK​ with s≥1, the binomial theorem gives
v(uq−1)≥s+1.
(26)
The linear term gains a factor q∈m (or is zero in positive characteristic), while all terms of degree at least two have valuation at least 2s≥s+1.
For a∈OK×​, reduction gives aq−1∈1+m, because k× has order q−1. Iterating the inequality yields
v(a(q−1)qj−1)≥j+1,v(aqj+1−aqj)≥j+1.
(27)
The sequence is Cauchy and converges to a unit ω(a). Since the reduction of aqj is always aˉ, its limit has the same residue. Taking limits after shifting the sequence gives ω(a)q=ω(a), whence
aqj⟶ω(a),ω(a)q−1=1,ω(a)≡a(modπ).​
(28)
This is the Teichmuller representative. The Hensel lemma applied to Tq−1−1, whose derivative is a unit at every nonzero residue, shows uniqueness for each residue. Consequently the prime-to-ℓ roots give a canonical splitting
OK×​=μq−1​×(1+m).
(29)

Logarithms on sufficiently deep units. Suppose charK=0, let ℓ be the residue characteristic, and put e=v(ℓ). For every integer r>e/(ℓ−1) the p-adic logarithm and p-adic exponential function, evaluated in K, converge on the required domains:
log(1+x)=∑m≥1​m(−1)m+1xm​,exp(y)=∑m≥0​m!ym​.
(30)
The bounds v(m!)=evℓ​(m!)≤e(m−1)/(ℓ−1) and v(m)≤e(m−1)/(ℓ−1) show that, for x,y∈πrOK​, every term beyond the linear one has valuation strictly greater than v(x) or v(y). The series therefore converge and preserve these lattices; in particular exp(y)∈1+πrOK​. Their formal composition and addition identities are valid by convergence, giving inverse continuous group homomorphisms
log:1+πrOK​≅​πrOK​,exp:πrOK​≅​1+πrOK​.
(31)
Thus the logarithm isomorphism on deep principal units is
1+πrOK​≅(OK​,+),u⟼π−rlogu,r>ℓ−1e​.​
(32)
The right-hand group is torsion-free.
Roots of unity in Qp​. For odd p, take ℓ=p,e=1,r=1. The principal unit group 1+pZp​ is torsion-free by the logarithm isomorphism on deep principal units. The Teichmuller representative splitting leaves precisely the p−1 roots in μp−1​.
For p=2, take r=2. Each odd unit is uniquely ±u with u∈1+4Z2​, since its residue modulo four is either one or minus one. This latter group is torsion-free. Consequently
∣μ(Qp​)∣={p−1,2,​p odd,p=2,​μ(Q2​)={1,−1}.​
(33)
This computes the roots of unity in the p-adic numbers.
The units of Q2​(i). Set π=1−i. Its polynomial T2−2T+2 is Eisenstein, so
OK​=Z2​[i],v(π)=1,v(2)=2,k=F2​.
(34)
The Eisenstein polynomial facts justifying this are stated in the next solution. Write Us​=1+πsOK​. Reduction gives OK×​=U1​, and the successive quotients of principal-unit groups
Us​/Us+1​≅(k,+),1+πsb⟼bˉ
(35)
have order two for s≥1. Hence [U1​:U3​]=4. The four roots in μ4​={1,i,−1,−i} have distinct images modulo U3​, since
v(i−1)=v(−i−1)=1,v(−1−1)=2.
(36)
They exhaust the quotient and intersect U3​ trivially. Since r=3>2/(2−1), the logarithm isomorphism on deep principal units gives U3​≅(OK​,+). Therefore the unit decomposition of the 2-adic Gaussian field is
OK×​=μ4​×U3​≅μ4​×(OK​,+).​
(37)
In particular these four elements are all its roots of unity.
Quadratic extensions. A nonzero element of K is uniquely a power of π times a unit, so K×≅πZ×OK×​. Squaring on the decomposition above acts as squaring on μ4​ and multiplication by two on the additive ring OK​=Z2​⊕Z2​i. Thus the square-class group of the 2-adic Gaussian field is
K×/(K×)2≅C2​×(μ4​/μ42​)×(OK​/2OK​)≅C24​.​
(38)
By quadratic extensions from square classes, in characteristic different from two the nontrivial square classes classify quadratic extensions K(a​): two such extensions are K-isomorphic exactly when a/b is a square. There are therefore 24−1=15​ quadratic extensions up to K-isomorphism.

The filtration and its meaning. Let L/K be a finite Galois extension of non-Archimedean local fields, with group G, normalized valuation vL​, and residue fields kL​/kK​ of characteristic ℓ. The lower ramification numbering is
G−1​=G,Gj​={σ∈G:vL​(σa−a)≥j+1 for every a∈OL​}(j≥0).
(39)
The higher ramification groups are normal, decrease with j, and eventually become trivial: for each nonidentity automorphism some integral element is moved by a nonzero amount. The first group G0​ is the inertia group, with
G/G0​≅Gal(kL​/kK​).
(40)
Its fixed field is the maximal unramified extension inside L. The wild inertia group is G1​. For a uniformizer πL​, there are injective homomorphisms
G0​/G1​↪kL×​,σ⟼σ(πL​)/πL​​,Gj​/Gj+1​↪(kL​,+),σ⟼πLj+1​σ(πL​)−πL​​​(j≥1).
(41)
The kernels are the indicated next groups; the additive homomorphism assertion follows by expanding a product of automorphisms modulo πLj+2​. These maps show that G0​/G1​ is cyclic of order prime to ℓ, while G1​ is an ℓ-group. Thus tame ramification is exactly the case G1​=1.
To calculate the groups, the uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups says
Gj​={σ∈G0​:vL​(σπL​−πL​)≥j+1}.​
(42)
Indeed, OL​ is generated by πL​ over the integers of the maximal unramified subextension, which G0​ fixes. Differences of powers of πL​ are divisible by σπL​−πL​, so the one test implies all the defining inequalities.
Eisenstein facts used in the calculations. For a complete discrete valuation ring A with uniformizer ϖ, a monic polynomial
f(T)=Td+ad−1​Td−1+⋯+a0​
(43)
is an Eisenstein polynomial if ϖ∣aj​ for every j<d and ϖ2∤a0​. The Eisenstein criterion makes it irreducible. If θ is a root, its valuation relative to the base normalization is 1/d, the extension has degree and ramification index d, its residue field is unchanged, and
OK(θ)​=A[θ],θ is a uniformizer.​
(44)
The root valuation follows by comparing the terms of its equation. The ramification index must then be at least d, hence exactly d. In the basis 1,θ,…,θd−1, the valuations of bj​θj are distinct modulo d, so an integral linear combination has every bj​∈A. This proves the integer-ring assertion. Conversely, a uniformizer in a totally ramified extension generates the field, and its minimal polynomial is Eisenstein; this follows from its value 1/d, equal valuations of its conjugates, and the valuation one of its norm.
For a monogenic separable integer ring A[θ], the different ideal is generated by f′(θ). For a Galois extension, the different exponent from ramification groups is
vL​(DL/K​)=j≥0∑​(∣Gj​∣−1).​
(45)
In the totally ramified case these agree directly: f′(πL​)=∏σ=1​(πL​−σπL​), and an automorphism with displacement valuation h contributes once to each of G0​,…,Gh−1​.
Upper numbering. Extend the lower indexing to real s≥0 by Gs​=G⌈s⌉​, and set
φL/K​(s)=∫0s​[G0​:Gt​]dt​,ψL/K​=φL/K−1​,Gu=GψL/K​(u)​.
(46)
On [−1,0] take φ(s)=s. This Herbrand function slows the indexing as the groups shrink. Lower numbering is compatible with subgroups, while upper ramification numbering is compatible with quotients: for normal H, (G/H)u=GuH/H. This is the fact that upper ramification groups commute with quotients. Thus upper breaks are particularly useful when comparing intermediate Galois extensions.
The eighth-root cyclotomic extension. Put ζ=ζ8​ and π=ζ−1. The shifted cyclotomic polynomial
(1+T)4+1=T4+4T3+6T2+4T+2
(47)
is Eisenstein at two. Hence L=Q2​(ζ) is totally ramified of degree four, vL​(2)=4, and π is a uniformizer. Its Galois group consists of σa​(ζ)=ζa for a=1,3,5,7, and is C2​×C2​.
Since ζ is a unit,
vL​(σa​π−π)=vL​(ζa−1−1).
(48)
For a=3,7, this is the valuation of i−1 or −i−1, namely two, because their squares are units times two. For a=5, it is vL​(−2)=4. The ramification groups of the eighth-root cyclotomic extension of the 2-adic field are therefore
G−1​=G0​=G1​=C2​×C2​,G2​=G3​={1,σ5​}≅C2​,Gj​=1 (j≥4).​

The lower breaks are one and three, and
φ(s)=⎩⎨⎧​s,1+(s−1)/2,2+(s−3)/4,​0≤s≤1,1≤s≤3,s≥3.​

Gu=⎩⎨⎧​C2​×C2​,{1,σ5​},1,​−1≤u≤1,1<u≤2,u>2.​​
(51)
The different exponent from ramification groups is 3+3+1+1=8, agreeing with vL​(4ζ3)=8 from the derivative of T4+1.
The cubic splitting field at three. Let α3=2, ζ=ζ3​, and put
β=α+1,λ=ζ−1.
(52)

β3−3β2+3β−3=0,λ2+3λ+3=0

Its ramification index is divisible by both three and two, hence equals six; therefore L/Q3​ is totally ramified. It is the splitting field of T3−2, with Galois group S3​.
