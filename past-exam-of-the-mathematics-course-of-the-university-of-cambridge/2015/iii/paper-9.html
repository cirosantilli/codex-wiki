The Weak Harnack inequality is an estimate for a nonnegative weak supersolution. Write F=(f1,…,fn), let B2R​(x0​)⊂⊂Ω, and assume u≥0 almost everywhere on this ball. There are p>0 and C<∞, depending only on the dimension, the uniform ellipticity bounds, q, and the scaled norms of the lower-order coefficients, such that
(∣BR​(x0​)∣1​∫BR​(x0​)​up)1/p≤C(BR​(x0​)essinf​u+R1−n/q∥F∥Lq(B2R​)​+R2−2n/q∥g∥Lq/2(B2R​)​).​
(1)
Here ⟨v⟩E​=∣E∣−1∫E​v is the integral average. The weak supersolution inequality uses the sign convention Lu≤divF+g. For instance, the coefficient dependence can be expressed using
R1−n/q(∥b∥Lq(B2R​)​+∥c∥Lq(B2R​)​),R2−2n/q∥d∥Lq/2(B2R​)​.
(2)
Thus one may use the same p,C on all sufficiently small balls when the global coefficient norms and uniform ellipticity bounds are fixed. The nonnegativity condition is essential to this formulation; a signed weak supersolution may first be shifted, with the resulting change in its forcing included. As usual, supremum and infimum statements for functions in a Sobolev space mean essential suprema and essential infima. The standard multidimensional statement uses n≥2, so q/2>1. In dimension one the analogous formulation requires q≥2; the printed restriction q>n alone would allow q/2<1, which does not suffice. To see the obstruction, smooth the nonnegative capped function min{1,∣x∣/ε} by a mollifier of width τ. Its positive second derivative is a bump of mass 2/ε and has Lq/2 size of order ε−1τ2/q−1. For fixed ε and 1<q<2, this size and the minimum both tend to zero as τ↓0, whereas the average on a fixed larger interval stays positive. Thus the Weak Harnack inequality cannot have the displayed uniform forcing bound in that range. Shrinking ε and choosing τ still smaller likewise defeats a uniform Hölder estimate based on that norm alone.

Let M=esssupΩ​u. The standard local boundedness of weak elliptic subsolutions gives a finite upper bound on every relatively compact ball. In particular, the assumed equality of essential suprema makes M finite. Equivalently, when using the maximum principle for an upper-bounded weak subsolution, this preliminary fact is already part of the hypothesis.
Set v=M−u. Because the divergence-form elliptic operator here annihilates constants, v≥0 and Lv=−Lu≤0. The hypothesis says essinfB​v=0. Cover the compact closure of B by finitely many balls Dj​ whose doubled balls lie in Ω. At least one Dj​ has essential infimum zero: otherwise the minimum of their finitely many positive lower bounds would be a positive lower bound on B. The Weak Harnack inequality with zero forcing now gives
(∣Dj​∣1​∫Dj​​vp)1/p≤CessinfDj​​v=0.
(3)
Consequently v=0 almost everywhere on Dj​. If another admissible ball overlaps Dj​ in an open set, its essential infimum of v is also zero, and the same argument makes v vanish there. Since a domain is connected, a chain of overlapping interior balls reaches every point of Ω. This propagation of zeros by the weak Harnack inequality proves u=M almost everywhere on Ω, which is the claimed strong maximum principle. Notice why the absence of the b,d terms matters: it is precisely what makes L(M−u)=−Lu.

For a bounded domain, the nondivergence-form elliptic operator obeys the weak maximum principle: Lu≥0 and c≤0 imply
Ωsup​u≤max{0,∂Ωsup​u}=∂Ωsup​u+.​

For the bounded-domain proof choose γ>0 so large that λγ2−∥b1∥∞​γ−∥c∥∞​>0. The positive exponential h(x)=eγx1​ then satisfies
Lh=eγx1​(γ2a11+γb1+c)>0.
(12)

In the bounded-domain setting of the weak maximum principle, let u1​,u2​ be two classical solutions with the same forcing and boundary values. Their difference z=u1​−u2​ satisfies Lz=0 and z=0 on the boundary. Apply the weak maximum principle first to z and then to −z:
supΩ​z≤0,supΩ​(−z)≤0.
(13)
Thus u1​=u2​, proving uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem. The same proof works on an unbounded domain if the difference and its negative satisfy the required condition at infinity. As literally printed without either restriction, uniqueness need not hold: on {xn​>0} the two harmonic functions 0 and xn​ have the same zero boundary values.

Use this intended boundary condition throughout. Let f=Δu. A maximum-principle barrier gives a uniform bound on u: with ψ(x)=(1−∣x∣2)/(2n), we have Δψ=−1 and ψ=0 on ∂B1​. Since ∣f∣≤1,

with analogous bounds for (cu)σ​ and fσ​. The coefficients need only be bounded on W. Therefore uσ​ is bounded in H1(U). It converges to u in L2(U), and weak sequential compactness in a Hilbert space in this Sobolev space gives u∈H1(U). Since U was arbitrary, u∈Hloc1​(Ω).
Now expand the distributional derivative:
Δu=b⋅∇u+(divb+c)u+f.
(46)
Its right side belongs to Lloc2​, so the interior H2 elliptic regularity estimate gives u∈Hloc2​. More generally, if u∈Hlocm​ for an integer m≥1, multiplication by the smooth coefficients puts the right side in Hlocm−1​. Interior elliptic regularity then gives u∈Hlocm+1​. This elliptic regularity bootstrap proves u∈Hlock​ for every integer k.
For any nonnegative integer ℓ, choose k>ℓ+n/2. The Sobolev embedding theorem on compact interior subsets gives u∈Clocℓ​. Taking all ℓ proves u∈C∞(Ω), for its smooth representative. The first gain from L2 to H1 is the step supplied by the mollified equation; assuming H1 in the initial definition would miss that step.
