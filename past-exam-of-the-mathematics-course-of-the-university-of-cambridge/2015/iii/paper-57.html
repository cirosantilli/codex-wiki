Write the radial velocity as u(r), allowing u<0 for accretion flow, and let c2=γp/ρ be the squared adiabatic sound speed. The steady continuity equation, radial Euler equations for an inviscid fluid and entropy advection equation give
drd​(r2ρu)=0,udrdu​=−ρ1​drdp​−drdΦ​,udrd​(ργp​)=0.
(1)
On a nonzero smooth flow branch, the polytropic equation of state is p=Kργ with constant K. Thus the two useful first integrals for spherically symmetric adiabatic flow are
4πr2ρu=J,2u2​+γ−1c2​+Φ=B.​
(2)
Here J is the signed mass flux and B is the Bernoulli function. The specific enthalpy is c2/(γ−1); the prescribed Newtonian gravitational potential has no contribution from the gas's own gravity. Eliminating the density derivative using mass conservation gives the differential form
(u−uc2​)u′=r2c2​−Φ′.​
(3)
This sonic point equation displays both the singular coefficient and the numerator that must vanish for a smooth transonic branch.

f(β)=4−β4+β​,1<γ<f(β).​

The strict inequality also follows from local sonic-point slope discriminant analysis, including the potentially cold zero-energy endpoint. Put X=(r∗​/u∗​)u∗′​. Since mass conservation implies (c2)′/c2=−δ(u′/u+2/r), differentiating the sonic point equation at r∗​ yields

dlnrdln(∣u∣/c)​​∗​=2δ+2​X+δ=±22β−(4−β)δ​​.

The solenoidal constraint on the magnetic field reduces to ∂x​Bx​+∂z​Bz​=0. Locally, or globally in a simply connected cross-section, it permits a Cartesian magnetic flux function satisfying
Bx​=−ψz​,Bz​=ψx​,B=∇×(ψey​)+By​ey​.​
(15)
The transverse magnetic field lines are contours of ψ, because B⋅∇ψ=0. The independent component By​ supplies the twist of the flux tube; it is not restricted by the solenoidal constraint.
For the Lorentz force, take the curl explicitly:
∇×B=(−By,z​,−∇2ψ,By,x​).
(16)
Its cross product with B has transverse components −(∇2ψ)∇ψ−By​∇By​ and y component ψx​By,z​−ψz​By,x​. Consequently
fL​=−μ0​1​[(∇2ψ)∇ψ+By​∇By​+∇ψ×∇By​].​
(17)
The term −By​∇By​/μ0​ is the transverse gradient of the magnetic pressure associated with the axial field, while the other terms include magnetic tension.

In magnetostatics, −∇p−ρ∇Φ+fL​=0. The plane-parallel Newtonian gravitational potential and all thermodynamic fields are independent of y, so the y component of the Lorentz force must vanish:
∇ψ×∇By​=0.
(18)
Away from a null of ∇ψ, this says that By​ is constant along each connected contour of the Cartesian magnetic flux function. Thus, on a regular flux region,
By​=b(ψ).​
(19)
This statement is local; disconnected contours with the same numerical flux label need not share one global function without the usual flux-tube connectivity assumption.
Since ∇By​=b′(ψ)∇ψ, the transverse magnetostatic equilibrium equation becomes
μ0​1​[∇2ψ+b(ψ)b′(ψ)]∇ψ+∇p+ρ∇Φ=0.​
(20)
This is a Cartesian magnetostatic flux-function equilibrium. Projecting it along a transverse magnetic field line gives hydrostatic balance along that line; projecting across the line balances the gas pressure and weight against magnetic pressure and magnetic tension.

The MHD induction equation can be written in material form as
DtDB​=(B⋅∇)u−B∇⋅u,DtD​=∂t​+ux​∂x​+uz​∂z​.
(21)
Its y component immediately gives
DtDBy​​=B⋅∇uy​−By​∇⋅u.​
(22)
For the Cartesian magnetic flux function, the x,z components of the MHD induction equation, or the y component of the magnetic vector potential equation, give
∂t​ψ+ux​∂x​ψ+uz​∂z​ψ=C(t).
(23)
The additive function of time in ψ has no effect on B. Choose this gauge so that C=0. Then the flux label is materially conserved:
DtDψ​=0.​
(24)
This is magnetic flux freezing in the two-dimensional geometry.
To prove the absence of axial motion, construct an invariant solution with uy​=0. Along each Lagrangian trajectory, ψ is fixed, and the assumed divergence gives
DtDBy​​=−g(ψ,t)By​,By​(x,t)=f(ψ(x,t))exp[−∫0t​g(ψ(x,t),s)ds].
(25)
Thus By​ remains a function of ψ and time alone. Its gradient stays parallel to ∇ψ, so the y component of the Lorentz force remains zero. The axial momentum equation is
ρDtDuy​​=μ0​1​(Bx​∂x​+Bz​∂z​)By​=0,
(26)
because (Bx​∂x​+Bz​∂z​)ψ=0. With uy​=0 initially, it remains zero. Hence neither axial force nor axial motion is generated. This flux-surface preservation during magnetic-tube expansion argument assumes the smooth ideal evolution for which the initial-value problem is unique; the particular in-plane rising motion need not be calculated.

The logarithmic density term is the barotropic fluid energy for p=cs2​ρ, rather than the thermodynamic heat content of an isolated gas. Define
e(ρ)=cs2​ln(ρ/ρ0​),Eh​=ρ(2v2​+Φ+e),EB​=2μ0​B2​.
(31)
For any scalar q, the continuity equation implies ∂t​(ρq)+∂z​(ρwq)=ρDq. Therefore De=−cs2​w′, DΦ=wg, and dotting the momentum equations with ρv gives
∂t​Eh​+∂z​(wEh​)=−∂z​(wp)+μ0​Bz​​(vx​Bx′​+vy​By′​)−wEB′​−aρvx​vy​.
(32)
The MHD induction equation gives the complementary magnetic energy balance
∂t​EB​+∂z​(wEB​)=μ0​Bz​​(Bx​vx′​+By​vy′​)−(EB​−μ0​Bz2​​)w′+μ0​aBx​By​​.
(33)
Adding these two identities, and collecting the product derivatives, yields the barotropic magnetic energy equation
∂t​E+∂z​F=S,F=w(E+p+2μ0​B2​)−μ0​Bz​​(v⋅B),S=a(μ0​Bx​By​​−ρvx​vy​).​
(34)
Here E=Eh​+EB​. The two stress terms in S describe magnetohydrodynamic shear work: the maintained background shear flow can supply energy to, or remove energy from, the perturbation flow and magnetic field. The sign depends on the off-diagonal total stress; this is why the energy excluding the background shear flow is not generally conserved. Changing ρ0​ merely adds a constant multiple of the conserved density to E and the corresponding mass flux to F, leaving S unchanged.

For a steady flow, mass conservation gives ρw=constant and ρ′/ρ=−w′/w. Define the Alfvén velocity va​=B/μ0​ρ​ and C=w2−vaz2​. Combining the horizontal momentum and MHD induction equations without dividing by C gives
CBx′​=−wBx​w′,CBy′​=−wBy​w′+a(wBx​−Bz​vx​).
(35)
Dot these identities with Bx​/(μ0​ρ) and By​/(μ0​ρ), respectively:
μ0​ρC​(Bx​Bx′​+By​By′​)=−w(vax2​+vay2​)w′+avay​(wvax​−vaz​vx​).
(36)
The vertical momentum equation and the isothermal equation of state give
(w−wcs2​​)w′=−g−μ0​ρBx​Bx′​+By​By′​​.
(37)
Multiply by C and eliminate the magnetic derivative to obtain
[w4−(cs2​+va2​)w2+cs2​vaz2​]ww′​=g(vaz2​−w2)+avay​(vx​vaz​−wvax​).​
(38)
No division by w2−vaz2​ was needed in deriving this necessary relation.
The magnetosonic critical speeds in the z direction are
cf,s2​=21​[cs2​+va2​ ± (cs2​+va2​)2−4cs2​vaz2​​].
(39)
The plus sign gives the fast magnetosonic wave speed and the minus sign the slow magnetosonic wave speed. Thus the differential coefficient is (w2−cf2​)(w2−cs2​). A smooth outflow proceeding from below both speeds to above both must normally pass through both magnetosonic critical speeds. At each crossing, the right-hand side must also vanish; this is the regularity at a magnetosonic point condition. The derivative coefficient changes sign at each nondegenerate crossing. For w>0 and w′>0, the driving term must be positive below the slow point, negative between the points and positive above the fast point.
The Alfvén speed component satisfies cs2​≤vaz2​≤cf2​. With downward gravity g>0, the gravitational term is consequently nonnegative at the slow point and nonpositive at the fast point. The magnetohydrodynamic shear work contribution must balance it at each point, and can provide the upward driving needed to pass the fast point. For a=0 and strictly positive g, generic separated slow and fast points cannot satisfy the required zero numerator; special vanishing-gravity or coincident-speed cases need separate treatment.
There is also Alfvén-point compatibility in a plane-parallel sheared flow. At w2=vaz2​, the original transverse equations require
wBx​w′=0,wBy​w′=a(wBx​−Bz​vx​).
(40)
These restrictions are not generally visible as a zero of the scalar differential coefficient, which there equals −vaz2​(vax2​+vay2​). In particular, a strictly accelerating regular solution must have Bx​=0 at that point. The scalar relation is therefore a necessary wind equation, not a substitute for regularity of all the original ideal magnetohydrodynamic equations. Degenerate cases such as a purely longitudinal magnetic field can merge characteristic speeds and reduce the number of distinct critical conditions.

Use the fluid displacement ξ, with velocity perturbation δu=∂t​ξ. Denote Eulerian perturbations by δ and the corresponding Lagrangian pressure perturbation by ΔL​p=δp+ξ⋅∇p. For adiabatic perturbations, the linearized mass conservation, momentum, adiabatic equation of state and Poisson equation are
δρρ∂t2​ξδp+ξ⋅∇p∇2δΦ​=−∇⋅(ρξ),=−∇δp−δρ∇Φ−ρ∇δΦ,=−γp∇⋅ξ,=4πGδρ.​​
(41)
These self-gravitating adiabatic displacement equations retain the perturbation of the star's own Newtonian gravitational potential. In the uniform-density interior, δρ=−ρ∇⋅ξ, ∇Φ=ωd2​r, and ∇p=−ρωd2​r. The dynamical frequency of a uniform-density star ωd​=(GM/R3)1/2 sets the natural timescale. For a normal mode with time factor e−iωt, replace ∂t2​ by −ω2. The requested interior analysis needs no surface or exterior matching conditions.

The spatial factor rlYlm​ is a regular solid harmonic. Besides the Laplace equation ∇2F=0, homogeneity gives r⋅∇F=lF. The divergence of the fluid displacement is
∇⋅(UFr)∇⋅(V∇F)​=[rU′+(l+3)U]F,=rl​V′F.​
(42)
Define the scalar dilation amplitude Dr​=rU′+(l+3)U+(l/r)V′; this is the quantity denoted Δ in the question, not the notation ΔL​ for a Lagrangian pressure perturbation. Then mass conservation gives ρ​=−ρDr​.
The equilibrium pressure gradient and homogeneity identity give
ξ⋅∇p=−ρωd2​(Ur2+lV)F.
(43)
Consequently the adiabatic equation of state yields
ρ​=−ρDr​,p​=ρωd2​(Ur2+lV)−γpDr​.​
(44)
For the force equations, the product rule gives
∇(p​F)=p​′Fer​+p​∇F,∇(ΦF)=Φ′Fer​+Φ∇F.
(45)
Equating the coefficients of Fer​ and ∇F in the self-gravitating adiabatic displacement equations gives
ρω2Ur=ρ​ωd2​r+ρΦ′+p​′,ρω2V=ρΦ+p​.​
(46)
Finally, applying the Laplacian to the gravitational perturbation gives

Φ′′+r2(l+1)​Φ′=4πGρ​,Dr​=rU′+(l+3)U+rl​V′.​

x±​=2H±H2+4l(l+1)​​.

For every l>0, their product is −l(l+1)<0. Therefore one branch oscillates and the other grows exponentially, for every positive γ. At large n or large γ, x+​∼γL/2−4 is a stiff compressive branch, while x−​∼−l(l+1)/(γL/2−4) is a slower unstable buoyancy branch.
The buoyancy frequency supplies the physical explanation. The background density has zero gradient, but pressure decreases outward:
N2=−γ(R2−r2)2ωd2​r2​<0(0<r<R).​

The specific entropy of the perfect gas decreases outward, because p/ργ does. An adiabatically displaced fluid parcel therefore experiences destabilizing buoyancy. The model has convective instability of a uniform-density star, even when its radial compression modes are stable. The divergence of this expression at the zero-pressure surface also reflects the artificial equilibrium; the interior negative sign is already decisive.
For l=0, the formal quadratic factors as

ωd2​ωradial2​​=2γ​n(n+1)−4.​

The lowest allowed degree, n=2, has ωradial2​=(3γ−4)ωd2​. Hence all these radial modes are stable for γ>4/3; the fundamental radial mode is marginal at γ=4/3 and unstable below it. Higher n have their individual thresholds γ=8/[n(n+1)]. These radial stability of a uniform-density star thresholds coexist with nonradial convective instability. They are conclusions within the polynomial interior family assumed in the question; no boundary-condition quantization is being asserted.
