OurBigBook.com Words: 510
A nilpotent endomorphism x satisfies xN=0 for some positive integer N. A nilpotent Lie algebra g has a terminating lower central series of a Lie algebra: with γ1​=g and γr+1​=[g,γr​], one has γr​=0 for some r. These are different conditions: the first concerns a particular linear map, whereas the second concerns repeated Lie brackets.
The representation form of Engel theorem says that if a finite-dimensional Lie subalgebra L⊆Endk​(V) consists entirely of nilpotent endomorphisms, with 0<dimV<∞, then V contains a nonzero vector annihilated by every element of L. Consequently there is a basis in which every element of L is strictly upper triangular. No assumption on the characteristic of a field or algebraic closure is required.
We prove the common-vector assertion by induction on dimL, simultaneously for every nonzero finite-dimensional representation space. The zero algebra is immediate. First, nilpotence of commutation by a nilpotent endomorphism follows from
(adx)r(T)=∑j=0r​(−1)j(jr​)xr−jTxj.
(1)
If xN=0, the right-hand side vanishes for r≥2N−1, in every characteristic of a field. For any proper Lie subalgebra M⊊L, its Adjoint representation on L/M therefore consists of nilpotent endomorphisms. Its image has dimension at most dimM<dimL, so the inductive assertion gives a nonzero class y+M annihilated by M. Equivalently y∈/M and [M,y]⊆M. Thus the Engel normalizer lemma gives M⊊NL​(M).
Choose a maximal proper Lie subalgebra M. Its normalizer of a Lie subalgebra must be all of L, so M is an ideal of a Lie algebra. Moreover L/M has dimension one: otherwise a one-dimensional Lie subalgebra of this quotient would have a proper inverse image strictly between M and L. By induction the space W={v∈V:Mv=0} is nonzero. It is invariant under L, since m(xw)=x(mw)+[m,x]w=0. Write L=M+kx. The restriction of the nilpotent endomorphism x to W has a nonzero kernel, and any vector in that kernel is annihilated by all of L. This completes the induction. Apply the assertion repeatedly to V/kv to obtain a complete invariant flag 0=V0​⊂V1​⊂⋯⊂Vd​=V with LVj​⊆Vj−1​. A basis adapted to that flag makes every matrix strictly upper triangular.
A nilpotent abstract Lie algebra need not act by nilpotent matrices. For V=0, the scalar algebra kI⊆gl(V) is an abelian Lie algebra, hence a nilpotent Lie algebra, but IN=I=0. No change of basis makes I strictly upper triangular. The failed implication is precisely the distinction described in nilpotent Lie algebras need not act nilpotently.
If g is a nilpotent Lie algebra, then (adx)rg⊆γr+1​, so every adx is a nilpotent endomorphism. Conversely, apply Engel theorem to adg acting on g. Its invariant flag is lowered by every Adjoint representation matrix, so every product of d=dimg such matrices vanishes. Since γd+1​ is spanned by expressions adx1​⋯adxd​(y), the lower central series of a Lie algebra terminates. Therefore
g is nilpotent⟺adx is nilpotent for every x∈g.​
(2)
Finally suppose k is an algebraically closed field. If some adx is not a nilpotent endomorphism, it has a nonzero eigenvalue λ and an eigenvector y, giving [x,y]=λy. The vectors x,y are linearly independent and span a nonabelian two-dimensional Lie subalgebra. Conversely, a Lie subalgebra of a nilpotent Lie algebra is nilpotent because its lower central series of a Lie algebra lies termwise in the ambient series. A nonabelian two-dimensional Lie algebra has a basis u,v with [u,v]=v: choose v spanning the nonzero derived algebra and rescale a complementary vector. Its Adjoint representation has the nonzero eigenvalue 1, so it is not nilpotent. This proves the two-dimensional subalgebra criterion for Lie algebra nilpotence:
g is nilpotent⟺every two-dimensional subalgebra is abelian.​
(3)
