The degree and its local signs. Orient Sn and let [Sn] generate its top reduced homology Hn​(Sn;Z)≅Z. The mapping degree is the integer characterized by
f∗​[Sn]=(degf)[Sn].​
(1)
For n≥1 this is the usual top homology definition using the fundamental class; using reduced homology also covers S0.
Suppose f is smooth and p is a regular value. Each x∈f−1(p) has invertible tangent map Dfx​, so the inverse function theorem makes f−1(p) discrete. It is also closed in the compact sphere, hence finite. Choose disjoint small neighborhoods of these points on which f is a local diffeomorphism. The Excision theorem identifies the source local homology with the direct sum of one copy of Z for each inverse image. The induced local map is multiplication by +1 or −1 according as Dfx​ preserves or reverses orientation. The map from the global fundamental class to these local orientation classes therefore proves the degree as a sum of local degrees formula:
degf=x∈f−1(p)∑​ϵx​,ϵx​=sgndetDfx​.​
(2)
Here the determinant is computed in positively oriented tangent bases. Thus the mapping degree counts inverse images with signs, rather than just their cardinality.
The quotient map. Put m=n+1 and write f=F∣Sm−1​. Give Dm its standard orientation and its boundary the induced orientation. The connecting homomorphism
∂:Hm​(Dm,Sm−1;Z)⟶Hm−1​(Sm−1;Z)
(3)
is an isomorphism: the disk has zero positive reduced homology. It sends the relative fundamental class [Dm,Sm−1] to [Sm−1]. If the map on relative homology induced by F multiplies this class by d, naturality gives
f∗​[Sm−1]=f∗​∂[Dm,Sm−1]=∂F∗​[Dm,Sm−1]=d[Sm−1].
(4)
Thus d=degf. Collapsing the boundary gives a sphere Dm/Sm−1≅Sm, and the quotient map identifies its top reduced homology with the top relative homology of the disk pair. Give this quotient sphere the orientation determined by that identification. The relation qF=Fq now shows
degF=deg(F∣Sn​).​
(5)
This is the quotient-sphere degree identity.
The graph intersection. Orient Dm×Dm by the product orientation, orient N=Dm×{0} by its first factor, and orient the graph of a function Γ by x↦(x,F(x)). An intersection is precisely a zero of F, and no such zero lies on the boundary because F(Sm−1)⊂Sm−1. At an intersection, transverse intersection means that DFx​ is surjective, hence invertible. The zeros are consequently isolated and finite.
For the smooth intersection number use the ordered tangent spaces Tx​N first and T(x,F(x))​Γ second. Relative to the product basis their concatenated basis has matrix
(I0​IDFx​​).
(6)
Its determinant is detDFx​, so the intersection sign is ϵx​=sgndetDFx​. By the same local Excision theorem argument, now in relative homology at the interior point 0, the sum of these signs equals the multiplier of F∗​ on Hm​(Dm,Sm−1;Z). Therefore the graph intersection formula for mapping degree is
deg(F∣Sn​)=N⋅Γ=x:F(x)=0∑​sgndetDFx​.​
(7)
If the tangent spaces are ordered Γ first and N second, every sign changes by (−1)m; the order above specifies the appropriate convention.
The one-dimensional model F(x)=2x3−x on [−1,1] illustrates the graph intersection formula for mapping degree: its three zeros have signs +1,−1,+1, and its endpoint map has mapping degree 1 on reduced homology.

The tensor differential. Use homological grading, so each differential lowers degree by one. The tensor product of chain complexes has
(C⊗R​C′)k​=⨁p+q=k​Cp​⊗R​Cq′​,D(x⊗y)=dC​x⊗y+(−1)px⊗dC′​y
(8)
for x∈Cp​. This Koszul sign rule gives
D2(x⊗y)=dC2​x⊗y+((−1)p−1+(−1)p)dC​x⊗dC′​y+x⊗dC′2​y=0.
(9)
Thus the graded tensor product is a chain complex.
The Hom differential. Write Mj​=∏p​HomR​(Cp​,Cp+j′​). For a degree-j element of this graded Hom complex of chain complexes, use the prescribed differential
dM​f=fdC​+(−1)j−1dC′​f.
(10)
The next application uses degree j−1, so
dM2​f=fdC2​+((−1)j−1+(−1)j−2)dC′​fdC​+(−1)2j−3dC′2​f=0.
(11)
In degree zero, dM​f=fdC​−dC′​f, so its kernel consists exactly of chain maps. A degree-one element h has dM​h=hdC​+dC′​h, which is exactly the change between two chain maps related by a chain homotopy. Consequently
H0​(M(C,C′))≅{chain maps C→C′}/chain homotopy.​
(12)
This is natural: precomposition and postcomposition by chain maps preserve both degree-zero cycles and degree-zero boundaries.
The dual complex and the sign adjustment. Define the reversed dual chain complex
Xi​=HomR​(C−i​,R),dX​ϕ=ϕdC​.
(13)
Here dX​ϕ is a functional on C1−i​, so it belongs to Xi−1​, and dX2​=0. The absence of an additional sign in dX​ is intentional.
Interpret finite generation of the free chain complexes as finiteness of their total graded free modules. Then only finitely many degrees occur, and the finite-free evaluation isomorphisms assemble into a graded isomorphism
ψ:X⊗R​C′⟶M(C,C′),ψ(ϕ⊗y)(c)=ϕ(c)y,
(14)
where ϕ∈Xi​, y∈Cj′​, and this component is zero outside C−i​. The dual module construction and evaluation make ψ natural. Finite rank is needed for evaluation to be an isomorphism; finite total support also makes the sums on the tensor side agree with the products on the Hom functor side.
Under ψ, the Hom functor differential has the form
ψ−1dM​ψ(ϕ⊗y)=dX​ϕ⊗y+(−1)i+j−1ϕ⊗dC′​y.
(15)
The usual tensor product of chain complexes instead has second coefficient (−1)i. Set
ρ(j)=2j(j−1)​,A∣Xi​⊗Cj′​​=(−1)ρ(j)id.​
(16)
This is integer-valued for every j∈Z, including negative j, and satisfies ρ(j)−ρ(j−1)=j−1. Conjugating the usual tensor differential by A leaves the first term unchanged and changes its second coefficient to (−1)i+j−1. Hence dM​=ψADA−1ψ−1, giving the sign conjugation for the tensor-Hom identification
Φ=ψA:X⊗R​C′≅​M(C,C′),Φ(ϕ⊗yj​)(c)=(−1)j(j−1)/2ϕ(c)yj​.​
(17)
This is an isomorphism of chain complexes, not merely of their homology. If one instead assumes only degreewise finite rank with unbounded grading, the ordinary tensor need not identify with the product defining M; the finite-total convention is essential to this last conclusion.

Integral groups for M4​. Put r=n−1. The space Mn​ is the inversion mapping torus of a torus, fibred over S1 with fiber Tr. The cohomology ring of a torus is the exterior algebra on r degree-one generators. Inversion acts as −1 on each such generator, hence as (−1)q on Hq(Tr;Z)≅Z(qr​).
The Wang sequence therefore gives
0⟶coker(F∗−1:Hq−1(Tr;Z)→Hq−1(Tr;Z))⟶Hq(Mn​;Z)⟶ker(F∗−1:Hq(Tr;Z)→Hq(Tr;Z))⟶0.
(18)
The right-hand term is free, so this short exact sequence splits as a sequence of groups. In even fiber degree, F∗−1=0; in odd fiber degree it is multiplication by −2. For r=3 this computes the integral cohomology of an inversion mapping torus:
Hq(M4​;Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z3⊕(Z/2)3,Z3,Z/2,0,​q=0,1,q=2,q=3,q=4,otherwise.​​
(19)
The top torsion group is consistent with M4​ being nonorientable: inversion of its three-dimensional fiber reverses orientation.
Mod-two groups. Over F2​, inversion acts as the identity on the fiber cohomology. The Wang sequence gives
0→Hq−1(Tr;F2​)→Hq(Mn​;F2​)→Hq(Tr;F2​)→0.
(20)
Thus
dimF2​​Hq(Mn​;F2​)=(qr​)+(q−1r​)=(qn​),​
(21)
with out-of-range binomial coefficients interpreted as zero. These are exactly the dimensions of the graded groups H∗(Tn;F2​).
The intersection pairing for M2​. The mapping torus of reflection of the circle is the Klein bottle. Let a be the section loop through a fixed point of the reflection and b a fiber circle. They form a basis of H1​(M2​;F2​). The section has a normal neighborhood homeomorphic to a Möbius band, so it is one-sided and a transverse displacement meets it once modulo two. The fiber is two-sided and can be displaced disjointly. The two loops meet once. The mod-two intersection pairing of the Klein bottle therefore has matrix
Q=(a⋅ab⋅a​a⋅bb⋅b​)=(11​10​)​.
(22)
Take the evaluation-dual basis t,x∈H1(M2​;F2​), with t(a)=1,t(b)=0 and x(a)=0,x(b)=1. By Poincare duality, the cup product matrix in this dual basis is Q−1, not Q:
Q−1=(01​11​).
(23)
Writing w for the nonzero top class gives t2=0, tx=w and x2=w. Hence the cohomology ring is
H∗(M2​;F2​)≅F2​[t,x]/(t2,x2+tx),∣t∣=∣x∣=1.​
(24)
These relations already force all degrees above two to vanish.
The ring for general n. For each of the r fiber coordinates, projection induces Mn​→M2​. Pull back the classes above, calling the common base class t and the fiber-coordinate classes x1​,…,xr​. Naturality of the cup product gives t2=0 and xi2​=txi​. The square-free products xI​=∏i∈I​xi​ restrict to the exterior algebra basis of the fiber cohomology. The Leray-Hirsch theorem then says that xI​ and txI​ form an additive basis. This proves the full mod-two cohomology ring of an inversion mapping torus:
H∗(Mn​;F2​)≅F2​[t,x1​,…,xn−1​]/(t2, xi2​+txi​ (1≤i≤n−1)).​
(25)
For n≥2, xi2​=txi​ is nonzero by this basis description. In the cohomology ring of a torus every degree-one class squares to zero: the generators square to zero and the cross terms occur twice in characteristic two. The degree-one cup-square obstruction to ring isomorphism therefore proves that the rings are not isomorphic for any n≥2, despite their isomorphic graded groups. For n=1, both spaces are circles and the rings are isomorphic. Even if grading is forgotten, the rings differ for n≥2: every element of the torus ring has square either zero or one, whereas xi2​=txi​ is nonzero and is not the unit.
Integral cup products in M4​. To specify H∗(M4​;Z) also as a ring, let a∈H1 be the pullback of the positive generator of H1(S1;Z). Let ρ denote reduction modulo two and set τi​=β​(xi​) using the integral Bockstein homomorphism. The degree-one Bockstein square identity gives ρτi​=xi2​=txi​, so the τi​ are the three independent order-two classes in degree two. Choose free classes b12​,b13​,b23​∈H2 which restrict to the corresponding two-fold fiber products and have reductions ρbij​=xi​xj​. Such choices exist: reduction in degree two is surjective because H3(M4​;Z) is free, and adding the τi​ removes any txi​ terms from an initial lift.
The Wang sequence identifies ab12​,ab13​,ab23​ as a free basis of H3. Let κ generate H4≅Z/2, with ρκ=tx1​x2​x3​. Reduction in degree four is an isomorphism. The integral cup products in the four-dimensional inversion mapping torus are consequently determined by
​a2=0,aτi​=0,τi​τj​=0,bij2​=0,b12​b13​=b12​b23​=b13​b23​=κ​,bij​τk​={κ,0,​{i,j,k}={1,2,3},otherwise,​​2τi​=2κ=0.​
(26)
Together with the unit, graded commutativity and vanishing above degree four, these give every product. For example, ρ(b12​b13​)=x12​x2​x3​=tx1​x2​x3​, whereas ρ(b122​)=t2x1​x2​=0. The products aτi​ vanish because they are torsion in the free group H3.

Cells and attaching maps. Regard RPk as the lines in Rk+1 and include RPk−1 as the lines whose last coordinate is zero. Its complement consists of lines with a unique representative (x1​,…,xk​,1), and is therefore an open k-cell. Inductively this makes Real projective space a CW complex with one cell in each dimension from zero to n.
A characteristic map is obtained from the northern closed hemisphere of Sk: send a unit vector to the line it spans. Its interior maps homeomorphically onto the open cell, while its equator has the antipodal identification. Thus the k-cell is attached by the quotient map
Sk−1⟶RPk−1,x⟼[x],
(27)
which is the antipodal two-sheeted covering. This includes the two endpoints of the one-cell attaching to the zero-cell.
The cellular chain complex has Ckcell​=Z for 0≤k≤n and zero otherwise. To compute its differential, follow the attaching map by collapse of the (k−2)-skeleton. The resulting map to RPk−1/RPk−2≅Sk−1 has two local contributions. They differ by the mapping degree (−1)k of the antipodal map on Sk−1. With compatible cell orientations,
dk​=1+(−1)k={2,0,​k even,k odd.​​
(28)
For k=1 the two oriented endpoints cancel, giving the same formula. Consecutive differentials compose to zero, as required.
The mod-two cup products. Modulo two every cellular differential vanishes, so cellular cohomology gives a one-dimensional group in each degree 0,…,n. Let a∈H1(RPn;F2​) be the Poincare dual of a projective hyperplane. This class is nonzero: a projective line transverse to that hyperplane meets it once. Intersecting j generic projective hyperplanes produces RPn−j, and the cup product of their Poincare duals is the Poincare dual of that intersection. In particular, an evaluates to one on the mod-two fundamental class. Therefore every aj, 0≤j≤n, is nonzero, since otherwise multiplying it by an−j would contradict an=0. Dimension makes an+1=0. The mod-two cohomology ring of real projective space is
H∗(RPn;F2​)≅F2​[a]/(an+1),∣a∣=1.​
(29)
For n=0 this simply means the cohomology of a point.
The product with integral coefficients. The final product's coefficients are unstated; take Z as the default. Dualizing the cellular chain complex above gives
​H∗(RP2;Z):H0=Z, H2=Z/2,H∗(RP3;Z):H0=Z, H2=Z/2, H3=Z,H∗(RP4;Z):H0=Z, H2=H4=Z/2,​
(30)
with all unlisted groups zero. The integral Künneth theorem has tensor terms with i+j=q and Tor functor terms with i+j=q+1:
0→⨁i+j=q​Hi(A;Z)⊗Hj(B;Z)→Hq(A×B;Z)→⨁i+j=q+1​Tor1Z​(Hi(A;Z),Hj(B;Z))→0.
(31)
For these finite free cellular complexes it splits additively, though not canonically. Both the tensor and Tor functor of two Z/2 summands give Z/2, so no order-four summands occur.
For an efficient count, write FA​(t) for the free-rank polynomial and TA​(t) for the number of Z/2 summands in each degree. The integral Künneth torsion polynomial rule is
FA×B​=FA​FB​,TA×B​=FA​TB​+TA​FB​+(1+t−1)TA​TB​.
(32)
The last two factors record respectively the tensor contribution in summed degree and the Tor functor contribution one degree lower. The three factors have
(F2​,T2​)=(1,t2),(F3​,T3​)=(1+t3,t2),(F4​,T4​)=(1,t2+t4).
(33)
The first two give F23​=1+t3 and T23​=2t2+t3+t4+t5. Multiplying by the third gives
F=1+t3,T=3t2+3t3+5t4+6t5+5t6+4t7+2t8+t9.
(34)
Consequently the integral cohomology of a product of finite real projective spaces in this case is
Hq(RP2×RP3×RP4;Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,0,(Z/2)3,Z⊕(Z/2)3,(Z/2)5,(Z/2)6,(Z/2)5,(Z/2)4,(Z/2)2,Z/2,0,​q=0,q=1,q=2,q=3,q=4,q=5,q=6,q=7,q=8,q=9,otherwise.​​
(35)
If the intended coefficients were instead F2​, the Künneth theorem over a field gives the dimension polynomial
(1+t+t2)(1+t+t2+t3)(1+t+t2+t3+t4).
(36)
Thus the mod-two groups in degrees zero through nine are respectively
(dimF2​​Hq)q=09​=(1,3,6,9,11,11,9,6,3,1),​
(37)
and all other degrees vanish.

Euler number and self-intersection. Orient the rank-two normal bundle νS​ by the orientations of S and M: the ordered tangent and normal spaces have the ambient orientation. Choose a smooth normal section s transverse to the zero section. Its zeros are isolated, and their signed number is the evaluation ⟨e(νS​),[S]⟩ of the Euler class on the fundamental class. Scale the section sufficiently small to lie in a tubular neighborhood. Its graph is a push-off S′ isotopic to S.
The intersections of S with S′ occur exactly at the zeros of s. In a positively oriented local splitting TS⊕νS​, the basis formed from the tangent spaces to S and to the graph has block matrix
(I0​IDs​).
(38)
Its determinant is detDs, so the local intersection sign is precisely the local zero index of the section. Summing proves the Euler number equals self-intersection identity
⟨e(νS​),[S]⟩=S⋅S′=[S]⋅[S].​
(39)
The conic. The given map is the degree-two Veronese map [u:v]↦[u2:uv:v2] followed by the projective linear transformation
A=​101​010​10−1​​,detA=−2=0.
(40)
The Veronese map is injective with nonvanishing differential: on the chart u=0 its coordinates are [1:t:t2], and on v=0 they are [s2:s:1]. It is therefore a smooth embedding, and S is a smooth plane conic diffeomorphic to CP1≅S2. Its equation in the given coordinates is z02​−z22​=4z12​.
Let L be a projective line, with its complex orientation. The cohomology ring of complex projective space gives [L]⋅[L]=1 in CP2. The hyperplane z1​=0 meets S at [u:v]=[1:0] and [0:1]. The zeros of uv are simple in the respective local coordinates, and complex intersections have positive signs. Hence [S]⋅[L]=2, so [S]=2[L]. The self-intersection number and the normal Euler class are
[S]⋅[S]=4,e(νS​)=4u,​
(41)
where u∈H2(S2;Z) has ⟨u,[S2]⟩=1.
The boundary of the tubular neighborhood. Write N=ν(S) for the closed disk tubular neighborhood and E=∂N for its boundary. This distinguishes the disk neighborhood N from the vector normal bundle νS​. The space E is the oriented circle bundle of νS​, with Euler class 4u. The Gysin sequence contains
0→H1(E;Z)→H0(S2;Z)⌣4u​H2(S2;Z)→H2(E;Z)→0.
(42)
The middle map is multiplication by four, yielding H1(E;Z)=0 and H2(E;Z)=Z/4. The same Gysin sequence gives H0(E;Z)=H3(E;Z)=Z. The total space is a closed connected oriented three-manifold, so Poincare duality gives
Hk​(∂ν(S);Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z/4,0,​k=0,3,k=1,otherwise.​​
(43)
The exterior. Work first with W=CP2∖intN. A collar neighborhood of ∂W shows that its interior, the requested CP2∖N, has the same homotopy equivalence type: push the boundary a small positive distance into the collar. The Excision theorem and Thom isomorphism theorem identify
Hk​(CP2,W;Z)≅Hk​(N,E;Z)≅Hk−2​(S2;Z).
(44)
Only degrees two and four are nonzero, each a copy of Z.
In degree four the map H4​(CP2;Z)→H4​(N,E;Z) sends the ambient fundamental class to the relative fundamental class of N. Under the Thom isomorphism theorem this becomes [S]. Thus this map is multiplication by one with compatible orientations. The long exact sequence in homology gives
0→H4​(W;Z)→Z1​Z→H3​(W;Z)→0,
(45)
so H4​(W)=H3​(W)=0.
In degree two, the Thom isomorphism theorem identifies the map H2​(CP2)→H0​(S2) with intersection against [S]. A projective line intersects the conic twice, so this map is multiplication by two. The long exact sequence in homology is
0→H2​(W;Z)→Z2​Z→H1​(W;Z)→0.
(46)
It yields H2​(W)=0 and H1​(W)=Z/2. In degree zero the relative groups vanish, so H0​(W)≅H0​(CP2)≅Z. This computes the homology of the complement of a smooth conic:
Hk​(CP2∖ν(S);Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z/2,0,​k=0,k=1,k≥2.​​
(47)
The normal Euler number 4, the degree-two intersection map and the degree-four restriction map 1 play different roles; distinguishing them explains why the boundary has first homology Z/4 while the exterior has first homology Z/2.
