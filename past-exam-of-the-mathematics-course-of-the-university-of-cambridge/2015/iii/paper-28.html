A set S of prime numbers has Dirichlet density δ if the following limit exists:
δ(S)=lims↓1​log(1/(s−1))∑p∈S​p−s​=δ.
(1)
Equivalently, the denominator can be ∑p​p−s, since that sum is log(1/(s−1))+O(1). Adding or removing finitely many prime numbers leaves the Dirichlet density unchanged.
Put α=42​>0 and L=Q(α,i). This is the splitting field of X4−2, whose four roots of a polynomial are α,iα,−α,−iα. The polynomial is an Eisenstein polynomial at 2, so [Q(α):Q]=4. Since Q(α) is contained in the real numbers, it does not contain i, giving [L:Q]=8. Thus L/Q is a Galois extension of degree 8. Its Galois group is the dihedral group of order 8: the field automorphisms r(α)=iα, r(i)=i and s(α)=α, s(i)=−i satisfy r4=s2=1 and srs=r−1.
For an odd prime number p, the condition p≡1(mod4) means that the finite field Fp​ contains all fourth roots of unity. Under this condition, 2 is a quartic residue exactly when X4−2 has a root in Fp​; multiplying that root by 1,i,−1,−i gives all four distinct roots. Conversely, complete splitting of X4−2 over Fp​ gives both a fourth root of 2 and a primitive fourth root of unity, so it also forces p≡1(mod4).
The polynomial discriminant of X4−2 is −211. Hence every odd prime number is unramified in L, and the root-splitting criterion is equivalent to its Frobenius automorphism acting trivially on all the roots. Since the roots generate L, this is equivalent to the Frobenius automorphism being the identity, or to p being a completely split prime of L/Q. The Chebotarev density theorem says that the unramified prime numbers with Frobenius conjugacy class C have Dirichlet density ∣C∣/∣Gal(L/Q)∣. Here C={1}, so
δ(S)=81​.​
(2)

A normalization is important here. I use the covolume-one theta function of a fractional ideal, for which the requested limit is 1, and also give the formula for the unnormalized Gaussian theta sum.
Let n=[K:Q], let dv​=[Kv​:R]∈{1,2}, and choose one field embedding σv​ for each Archimedean place. The Minkowski embedding of a number field identifies
K∞​=Rr1​×Cr2​,n=r1​+2r2​,
(3)
with a real inner product space having
⟨x,z⟩=∑v real​xv​zv​+2∑v complex​Re(xv​zv​​),∣x∣2=∑v​dv​∣xv​∣2.
(4)
Its Euclidean Lebesgue measure is dμ=∏v real​dxv​∏v complex​2dRexv​dImxv​. With this convention, the covolume of a fractional ideal lattice j(b) is
Cb​=∣DK​∣​N(b),
(5)
where DK​ is the field discriminant and N(b) is the positive absolute norm of a fractional ideal. Thus the Euclidean lattice Λb​=Cb−1/n​j(b) has covolume 1. Define
Θ(y,b)=a∈b∑​exp(−πCb−2/n​v∈S∞​∑​dv​yv​∣σv​(a)∣2).​
(6)
This Gaussian theta sum converges absolutely whenever every yv​>0.
The trace dual of a fractional ideal is
b∨=DK/Q−1​b−1={z∈K:TrK/Q​(zb)⊆Z}.
(7)
Here DK/Q−1​ is the inverse different. Since N(DK/Q​)=∣DK​∣, we have Cb∨​=Cb−1​. There is a subtle distinction between the trace pairing and the positive inner product: the dual lattice of j(b) is j(b∨)​, where the bar conjugates the complex coordinates and fixes the real ones. Indeed,
⟨j(a),j(z)​⟩=TrK/Q​(az).
(8)
Consequently Λb∗​=Λb∨​​. Coordinatewise complex conjugation preserves the weighted squared lengths in the Gaussian theta sum.
Here are the precise analytic formulas used in the proof. For a Schwartz function on K∞​, take the Fourier transform to be
f​(z)=∫K∞​​f(x)e−2πi⟨x,z⟩dμ(x).
(9)
For a full Euclidean lattice Λ of covolume C, the Poisson summation formula for a Euclidean lattice is
∑x∈Λ​f(x)=C−1∑z∈Λ∗​f​(z).
(10)
For fy​(x)=exp(−π∑v​dv​yv​∣xv​∣2), the Gaussian Fourier transform, applied in orthonormal real coordinates, gives
f​y​(z)=∥y∥−1/2exp(−π∑v​dv​yv−1​∣zv​∣2),∥y∥=∏v​yvdv​​.
(11)
More generally, exp(−πxTAx)​(z)=(detA)−1/2exp(−πzTA−1z) for a real positive-definite matrix A that is symmetric. Each complex coordinate contributes two real coordinates, which explains its exponent dv​=2.
Apply the Poisson summation formula for a Euclidean lattice to Λb​, whose covolume is 1, and use the preceding description of its dual lattice. The anisotropic theta functional equation is
Θ(y,b)=∥y∥−1/2Θ(y−1,b∨),(y−1)v​=yv−1​.​
(12)
For comparison, the unnormalized Gaussian theta sum
θ(y,b)=∑a∈b​exp(−π∑v​dv​yv​∣σv​(a)∣2)
(13)
has the functional equation
θ(y,b)=Cb−1​∥y∥−1/2θ(y−1,b∨),
(14)
and its corresponding limit is Cb−1​ rather than 1. Explicitly, our normalization is Θ(y,b)=θ(Cb−2/n​y,b).
For the small-parameter asymptotic of a lattice theta sum, y→0 means that every coordinate tends to zero. In Θ(y−1,b∨), the zero lattice vector contributes 1. Every nonzero vector contributes a term tending to zero. Once every yv​≤1, all these terms are bounded by the summable Gaussian theta sum ∑λ∈Λb∨​​e−π∣λ∣2. The dominated convergence theorem therefore gives Θ(y−1,b∨)→1. Using the anisotropic theta functional equation,
y→0lim​∥y∥1/2Θ(y,b)=1.​
(15)
The condition that every coordinate tends to zero matters; ∥y∥→0 alone would not justify this argument.

For a finite extension E/K of number fields, the inverse different is the fractional ideal
DE/K−1​={x∈E:TrE/K​(xOE​)⊆OK​}.
(16)
The different ideal is its inverse. The trace pairing is nondegenerate because extensions of number fields are separable, so this definition gives a full fractional ideal. The inclusion OE​⊆DE/K−1​ shows that DE/K​ is integral.
Write its prime ideal factorization as DE/K​=∏P​PdP​. For P above p, with ramification index eP​ and residue characteristic ℓ, the different exponent and tame ramification theorem says
dP​≥eP​−1,dP​=eP​−1 ⟺ ℓ∤eP​.​
(17)
The equivalence uses the separability of finite residue-field extensions. In particular, dP​=0 precisely at unramified prime ideals; in the wild case dP​≥eP​. This theorem does not require a Galois extension. The relative discriminant is the norm of the different ideal:
dE/K​=NE/K​(DE/K​).
(18)
For K=Q, this gives ∣DE​∣=N(DE/Q​).
Now take α3=m and K=Q(α). For any prime number q∣m, the square-free integer hypothesis makes X3−m an Eisenstein polynomial at q. It is therefore irreducible, and K is a pure cubic number field of degree 3. Since α is an algebraic integer, A=Z[α] is an order in a number field. The discriminant of elements of a number field for its basis 1,α,α2 is
disc(1,α,α2)=disc(X3−m)=−27m2.
(19)
For example, the resultant of X3−m and 3X2 is 27m2, and the degree-three sign in the polynomial discriminant is negative. If I=[OK​:A], the discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice gives
−27m2=I2DK​.
(20)
Only prime numbers dividing 3m can therefore divide I.
For q∣m, the Eisenstein polynomial gives a totally ramified extension of Qq​ of degree 3. Thus K has a unique prime ideal P above q, with ramification index 3 and residue-field degree 1. Since q=3, this is tame ramification, and the different exponent and tame ramification theorem gives dP​=2. Hence vq​(DK​)=2. Comparing with vq​(−27m2)=2 in the discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice yields vq​(I)=0.
At 3, use the shifted Eisenstein polynomial of β=α−m:
(T+m)3−m=T3+3mT2+3m2T+(m3−m).
(21)
Because 3∤m, the constant term is divisible by 3. Moreover,
9∣m3−m ⟺ m≡±1(mod9)
(22)
when 3∤m: the factors m−1 and m+1 cannot both be divisible by 3. The hypotheses therefore give v3​(m3−m)=1, so the translated polynomial is an Eisenstein polynomial at 3. The resulting completion is a totally ramified extension of degree 3, with residue-field degree 1, and its ramification index is divisible by the residue characteristic. Thus its different exponent is at least 3.
It follows that v3​(DK​)≥3. But v3​(−27m2)=3, so
3=2v3​(I)+v3​(DK​)
(23)
forces v3​(I)=0 and v3​(DK​)=3. No prime number divides I. We have proved the integral basis of a nonexceptional pure cubic field:
OK​=Z[α],{1,α,α2} is an integral basis,DK​=−27m2.​
(24)
Using local Eisenstein polynomials here only establishes the local ramification indices; it does not assume that Z[α] is already the full ring of integers of a number field.

Here a divisor is a modulus of a number field, rather than an arbitrary real-weighted divisor. Write
c=c0​c∞​,c0​=∏p​pmp​,
(25)
where c0​ is a nonzero integral ideal, the finite multiplicities mp​ are nonnegative integers, and c∞​ is a set of real Archimedean places. The multiplicity of a place in a modulus is mv​(c)=mp​ at the finite place corresponding to p, is 1 at a real place in c∞​, and is 0 at every other infinite place. In particular complex places have multiplicity 0.
Let Ic​ be the group of nonzero fractional ideals prime to c0​. Put
Kc,1×​={a∈K×:vp​(a−1)≥mp​ for p∣c0​,σv​(a)>0 for v∈c∞​}.
(26)
Let Pc​ consist of the principal ideals (a) with a∈Kc,1×​. The generalized ideal class group is the ray class group
Hc​=Ic​/Pc​.​
(27)
Let U=OK×​ be the unit group, let Uc​=U∩Kc,1×​ be the group of ray units, and define the residue and signature group of a modulus
Gc​=(OK​/c0​)××{±1}c∞​.
(28)
The first factor is omitted when c0​=OK​. There is a group homomorphism ρ:U→Gc​ recording the unit's finite residues and its signs. Its kernel is Uc​.
Let Pc consist of all principal fractional ideals prime to c0​, and put Rc​=Pc/Pc​. The two short exact sequences are
1⟶U/Uc​ρ​Gc​⟶Rc​⟶1,​
(29)
and
1⟶Rc​⟶Hc​⟶Cl(OK​)⟶1.​
(30)
For the first short exact sequence, weak approximation for number fields realizes every choice of finite unit residues and real signs by some a∈K× prime to c0​. Send that data to the class of (a) in Rc​. Changing a without changing its residues and signs multiplies it by an element of Kc,1×​, so the map is well-defined. Its kernel consists exactly of data arising from units: if (a)=(b) with b∈Kc,1×​, then a/b∈U. This gives Rc​≅Gc​/ρ(U). For the second short exact sequence, forget the ray conditions. Its kernel is Rc​, and weak approximation for number fields gives a representative prime to c0​ for every ideal class. These are the two parts of the ray class exact sequence.
For K=Q(15​), the ring of integers of a quadratic field is OK​=Z[15​]. The finite modulus is trivial and both real places occur, so Hc​ is the narrow ideal class group and Gc​={±1}2. The two field embeddings send 15​ to 15​ and −15​. The given unit ϵ=4+15​ is positive at both places, since 4−15​>0; the unit −1 is negative at both. Thus the unit signature map has image
ρ(U)={(+,+),(−,−)},∣Rc​∣=24​=2.
(31)
The given ideal class group has order 2. The second short exact sequence therefore gives
∣Hc​∣=2⋅2=4.​
(32)
To determine the group structure, retain the ideal p=(2,1+15​) that generates the ordinary ideal class group. We have p2=(2): all generators 4, 2(1+15​) and (1+15​)2 lie in (2), while
(1+15​)2−2(1+15​)−3⋅4=2
(33)
puts 2 in p2. Since 2 is totally positive, [p]2=1 in the narrow ideal class group. Its image in the ordinary ideal class group is nontrivial, so [p] has order exactly 2.
The class of the principal ideal (15​) is a nontrivial element of Rc​. Its two signs are (+,−), and multiplying by a unit can only reverse both signs or neither, so no generator of this ideal is totally positive. Its square is (15), which does have a totally positive generator. Thus [(15​)] is another element of order 2, distinct from [p] because its ordinary ideal class is trivial. These two elements are independent and generate all four classes. Consequently
Hc​≅(Z/2Z)2.​
(34)
The nontrivial ordinary ideal class already has a lift of order 2, so the extension in the second short exact sequence splits; it cannot be cyclic of order 4.
