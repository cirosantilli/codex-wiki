A Noetherian ring is a ring whose ideals satisfy the ascending chain condition; equivalently every ideal is finitely generated. For the surjective ring endomorphism, put Kj​=kerfj. The ascending chain of ideals K1​⊆K2​⊆⋯ stabilizes, say Km​=Km+1​. If a∈kerf, surjectivity of fm gives b with fm(b)=a. Then fm+1(b)=0, so b∈Km+1​=Km​ and a=0. The endomorphism is therefore injective, hence a ring automorphism.
The nilradical is the ideal
N(R)=(0)​={a∈R:aj=0 for some j≥1}.
(1)
For example, if au=bv=0, the binomial expansion gives (a+b)u+v−1=0; multiplication by any ring element also preserves nilpotence. Every prime ideal contains each nilpotent element, since aj∈P implies a∈P. Conversely, if a is not nilpotent, the multiplicative subset {1,a,a2,…} avoids (0). By Zorn's lemma, choose an ideal maximal among those disjoint from this subset. It is a prime ideal: if xy∈J but neither factor is in J, then J+(x) and J+(y) both meet the subset, and multiplying their two witnesses puts a power of a in J, a contradiction. This prime ideal excludes a. Hence
N(R)=P∈SpecR⋂​P.​
(2)
For the zero ring the empty intersection is the whole ring, as required.
Here is a finite prime-intersection representation of a radical argument that does not assume the result. In a Noetherian ring, suppose some ideal J has ideal radical not expressible as a finite intersection of prime ideals, and choose such a J maximal by the ascending chain condition. It is proper and cannot be prime. Choose a,b∈/J with ab∈J. The two larger ideals J+(a) and J+(b) have finite prime-intersection radicals, and
J​=J+(a)​∩J+(b)​.
(3)
Indeed, if xu∈J+(a) and xv∈J+(b), then xu+v∈J+(ab)=J. This contradicts the choice of J. Applying the result to (0) proves that N(R) is the intersection of finitely many prime ideals. Empty intersections handle the unit ideal.
For reducedness of a formal power series ring, the inclusion of constants gives one implication: a nonzero nilpotent element of R remains nonzero and nilpotent in R[[X]]. For the other, let R be a reduced ring and take a nonzero formal power series F=∑j≥r​aj​Xj with ar​=0. The coefficient of Xmr in Fm is arm​=0, because R is reduced. Thus F is not nilpotent. Therefore
N(R)=0⟺N(R[[X]])=0.​
(4)
No Noetherian ring hypothesis is needed for this argument.
For a nilradical that is not nilpotent, take
R=k[t1​,t2​,…]/(t12​,t22​,…),N(R)=(tˉ1​,tˉ2​,…).​
(5)
Every element of this ideal uses finitely many variables and is nilpotent: an element using s variables lies in an ideal whose (s+1)st power is zero. The quotient by this ideal is the field k, so it is exactly the nilradical. Nevertheless tˉ1​⋯tˉm​=0 for every m, because the square-free monomials form a vector space basis. Thus N(R)m=0 for all m.

A prime ideal P is a proper ideal such that ab∈P implies a∈P or b∈P. Equivalently R/P is an integral domain.
For each i, and each j=i, choose aji​∈Pj​∖Pi​, using incomparability. Put
bi​=∏j=i​aji​.
(6)
Then bi​ belongs to the other two prime ideals and does not belong to Pi​, by primality. In particular each bi​ lies in the union. But b1​+b2​+b3​∈/Pi​ for every i: modulo Pi​, the sum equals bi​=0. The union is not closed under addition and is not an ideal. This is a concrete instance of prime avoidance.
The union of three incomparable nonprime ideals example can retain pairwise incomparability. In the ring
R=F2​[u,v]/(u2,uv,v2),
(7)
take
I1​=(u),I2​=(v),I3​=(u+v).​
(8)
These distinct two-element ideals are pairwise incomparable, yet
I1​∪I2​∪I3​={0,u,v,u+v}=(u,v),
(9)
which is an ideal. None is prime: its quotient ring still contains a nonzero element of square zero. The three one-dimensional vector subspaces cover the two-dimensional vector space over F2​.

Use the convention that a multiplicative subset contains 1 and is closed under multiplication. Usually 0 is excluded; allowing it gives the harmless zero-ring localization. A prime ideal disjoint from S is mapped to
S−1P={a/s:a∈P, s∈S}
(10)
in the localization of a ring. This ideal is proper, and
(S−1R)/(S−1P)≅S−1(R/P)
(11)
is an integral domain, since the denominators are nonzero in the integral domain R/P. Thus S−1P is a prime ideal.
Conversely, for a prime ideal Q in S−1R, its contraction
P={a∈R:a/1∈Q}
(12)
is a prime ideal disjoint from S: each s/1 is a unit and cannot belong to a proper ideal. Membership in Q is equivalent to numerator membership in P, since a/s=(a/1)(s/1)−1. Hence Q=S−1P.
To check the other composite, a/1∈S−1P implies sa∈P for some s∈S, by the equality criterion for fractions in a localization of a ring. Since s∈/P, primality gives a∈P. Therefore extension and contraction are mutually inverse:
{P∈SpecR:P∩S=∅} ⟷ Spec(S−1R),P↦S−1P.​
(13)
If 0∈S, both sets are empty. This is the prime ideal correspondence for localization.

Suppose S is a saturated multiplicative subset, and take a∈/S. The principal ideal (a) is disjoint from S: otherwise ra∈S would force a∈S. The prime ideal avoiding a multiplicative subset argument from Question 1 extends (a) to a prime ideal Pa​ disjoint from S. Thus every point of the complement belongs to such a prime ideal, and
R∖S=P∩S=∅⋃​P.​
(14)
If S=R, this is the empty union.
Conversely suppose R∖S is a union of prime ideals. If xy∈S and x∈/S, some prime ideal in that union contains x, and hence xy, a contradiction. The same holds for y. Thus The saturated complement as a union of prime ideals characterization holds.
For units with no finite prime-union complement, take
R=Z,S=Z×={−1,1}.​
(15)
Products of units are units, and a product is a unit only if both factors are units, so S is a saturated multiplicative subset. Every nonunit integer lies in some (p) with p a positive prime, giving Z∖S=⋃p​(p). If finitely many prime ideals sufficed, discard any (0) and write the others as (p1​),…,(pr​). A prime ℓ outside this finite list belongs to none of them but is a nonunit, a contradiction. There is always such an ℓ: a prime divisor of p1​⋯pr​+1 is outside the list.

An integral extension R⊆T means every t∈T satisfies a monic polynomial
td+ad−1​td−1+⋯+a0​=0,aj​∈R.
(16)
To prove the Lying-over theorem, localize at S=R∖P. The injection RP​↪S−1T remains injective, and S−1T is integral over RP​: a monic relation for t divided by suitable powers of s gives one for t/s. This is a nonzero ring, so choose a maximal ideal M of it.
The contraction of M to RP​ is maximal. To see this directly, a field integral over a subring forces that subring to be a field. For nonzero a in the subring, a−1 is integral; multiply its monic equation of degree d by ad−1 to express a−1 as a polynomial in a with coefficients in the subring. Apply this to the integral extension
RP​/(M∩RP​) ⊆ (S−1T)/M.
(17)
Since RP​ is a local ring with unique maximal ideal PRP​, we obtain M∩RP​=PRP​.
Contract M to T, obtaining a prime ideal Q. Its contraction to R is the contraction of PRP​, namely P. Thus
there exists Q∈SpecT with Q∩R=P.​
(18)

The key is a Laurent normalization by a monomial change of variables. We prove the slightly stronger assertion that every nonzero quotient of a Laurent polynomial ring in r variables is finite as a module over an embedded Laurent polynomial ring in some number m≤r of variables.
Induct on r. If the defining ideal is zero, the ring itself works. Otherwise choose a nonzero Laurent polynomial
f=∑a∈F​ca​Xa
(19)
in that ideal, with nonzero coefficients. For a sufficiently large positive integer N, the weights
Na1​+N2a2​+⋯+Nr−1ar−1​+ar​
(20)
are distinct for all a∈F. Indeed, each difference gives a nonzero polynomial in N, which has only finitely many integer roots. Make the invertible monomial substitution
Xj​=Yj​YrNj​ (j<r),Xr​=Yr​.
(21)
Its inverse is Yj​=Xj​Xr−Nj​, so it is an automorphism of the Laurent polynomial ring. When r=1 there is no substitution and distinct exponents already give distinct weights.
As a Laurent polynomial in Yr​, the transformed relation has unique lowest and highest exponents. Their coefficients are nonzero scalar multiples of monomials in Y1​,…,Yr−1​, hence units in
B0​=k[Y1±1​,…,Yr−1±1​].
(22)
Multiplying by a power of Yr​ and the inverse leading coefficient gives
Yrd​+bd−1​Yrd−1​+⋯+b0​=0,bj​∈B0​,b0​∈B0×​.
(23)
Here d>0: a single-term Laurent polynomial would be a unit and the quotient would be zero. Reversing the equation and dividing by b0​Yrd​ gives a monic equation for Yr−1​. Thus both Yr​ and Yr−1​ are integral over the image B of B0​ in T. In fact b0​ being a unit also expresses Yr−1​ as a polynomial in Yr​, so T is generated as a B-module by 1,Yr​,…,Yrd−1​.
The ring B is itself a nonzero quotient of an (r−1)-variable Laurent polynomial ring. By induction, it is finite over an embedded Laurent polynomial ring in m≤r−1 variables. Finiteness of modules is transitive, so the same is true of T; a finite ring extension is an integral extension, by the determinant trick applied to multiplication by an element.
Applying this to r=3 yields an embedded subring
k[Y1±1​,…,Ym±1​]⊆T,T integral over it.​
(24)
The base case m=0 means the subring k; finiteness over it would make T a finite-dimensional vector space. The hypothesis excludes this, so 1≤m≤3, as required. No infinitude assumption on k is used.

The height of a prime ideal is the supremum of the lengths of strict chains of prime ideals ending at P:
htP=sup{d:P0​⊊P1​⊊⋯⊊Pd​=P}=dimRP​.
(25)
The last equality follows from the prime ideal correspondence for localization. Here is a length-growth proof of the Krull height theorem that makes the bound explicit.
We first establish a prime-chain lower bound for local length. For a Noetherian local ring (B,m) admitting a prime chain of length d, put HB​(t)=length(B/mt). Then
HB​(t)≥ctd(t≫0)
(26)
for some c>0. For d=0, use HB​(t)≥1. For d>0, quotient by the first prime ideal of the chain; this only decreases HB​ and reduces us to a local integral domain B with
0⊊P1​⊊⋯⊊Pd​=m.
(27)
Take 0=a∈P1​ and put C=B/(a). It has a prime chain of length d−1, so induction bounds HC​(t) below by a positive multiple of td−1.
The Artin-Rees lemma applied to (a)⊆B gives, for a fixed integer c0​≥1,
(a)∩mt⊆amt−c0​(t≥c0​).
(28)
Because a is a non-zero-divisor, the first term in the exact sequence
0⟶(a)/((a)∩mt)⟶B/mt⟶B/((a)+mt)⟶0
(29)
has length at least HB​(t−c0​). Hence
HB​(t)≥HB​(t−c0​)+HC​(t).
(30)
Iterate this inequality about t/(2c0​) times, while its arguments remain at least t/2. This gives the desired positive multiple of td. The particular Artin-Rees lemma inclusion used here follows directly from finite generation: the Rees algebra ⨁j≥0​mjtj is Noetherian, by the Hilbert basis theorem, and its graded submodule ⨁j​((a)∩mj)tj has finitely many homogeneous generators. A bound on their degrees gives c0​.
Now localize at the given minimal prime ideal P. Write B=RP​, m=PRP​, and J=IRP​. The only prime ideal of B containing J is m, so J​=m. Since m is finitely generated, some mq⊆J. Thus B/J has finite module length, say L. The ideal J is generated by at most n elements.
For n>0, products of these generators give a surjection from (n−1j+n−1​) copies of B/J onto Jj/Jj+1. Summing the lengths gives the generator bound for primary-ideal length
length(B/Jt)≤L∑j=0t−1​(n−1j+n−1​)=L(nt+n−1​)=O(tn).
(31)
Since J⊆m, we have Jt⊆mt, and therefore
HB​(t)≤length(B/Jt)=O(tn).
(32)
The prime-chain lower bound for local length now excludes any chain of length greater than n. Consequently
htP=dimRP​≤n.​
(33)
For n=0, minimality over the zero ideal says that P is a minimal prime ideal, so its height is zero. This also handles that boundary case.

A local ring is a nonzero ring with one maximal ideal m. Write κ=R/m for its residue field. Its Krull dimension dimR is the supremum of lengths of strict chains of prime ideals; in a local ring chains may be extended to end at m.
For the local Hilbert-function convention, define the Hilbert–Samuel growth dimension
d(R)=deg(the eventual polynomial HR​(t)=length(R/mt)).​
(34)
Equivalently it is the order of the pole at z=1 of the Hilbert series of the associated graded ring
grm​R=⨁j≥0​mj/mj+1.
(35)
This finite standard graded κ-algebra is generated by m/m2. The Hilbert-Serre theorem makes the cumulative Hilbert function eventually polynomial, which establishes the definition. For an Artinian local ring, the polynomial is constant and nonzero, so d(R)=0.
For every prime chain of length s, the prime-chain lower bound for local length proved in Question 4 gives HR​(t)≥cts. A polynomial of degree d(R) cannot satisfy this when s>d(R). Thus the requested inequality is
dimR≤d(R).​
(36)
For clarity, the embedding dimension is the different invariant
e(R)=dimκ​(m/m2)=the minimal number of generators of m.
(37)
The equality follows from the Nakayama lemma. Applying Question 4 to I=m gives dimR≤e(R), while the polynomial-algebra surjection below also gives d(R)≤e(R). In particular, growth dimension and embedding dimension should be kept distinct.
A regular local ring is a Noetherian local ring with e(R)=dimR. Let this common value be e and choose a minimal generating set x1​,…,xe​ of m. The initial forms give a surjective graded ring homomorphism
κ[T1​,…,Te​]↠grm​R.
(38)
For e=0, the Nakayama lemma gives m=0, so R is a field. Assume e>0. If the kernel contained a nonzero homogeneous polynomial F of degree q>0, the cumulative Hilbert function of the target would be bounded by that of κ[T1​,…,Te​]/(F). Multiplication by F is injective in the polynomial integral domain, so this bound is
(et+e−1​)−(et−q+e−1​)=O(te−1)(t>q).
(39)
But dimR=e provides a prime chain of length e, and Question 4 gives HR​(t)≥cte. This is a contradiction. Hence the associated graded ring of a regular local ring is
grm​R≅κ[T1​,…,Te​],​
(40)
an integral domain.
Finally the Krull intersection theorem gives ⋂j​mj=0. One can see the needed separatedness directly: for the finitely generated ideal K=⋂j​mj, the Artin-Rees lemma gives K=mK, and the Nakayama lemma gives K=0. Therefore each nonzero a∈R has finite m-adic order r, with nonzero initial form in mr/mr+1. For nonzero a,b, their initial forms have nonzero product in the graded integral domain. It follows that ab=0, and indeed its order is the sum of their orders. Thus R is an integral domain.

For a∈R, write ma​ for multiplication by a in Endk​(R). The algebraic differential operator ring is defined by the filtration
Dk−1​(R)=0,Dkj​(R)={θ∈Endk​(R):[θ,ma​]∈Dkj−1​(R) for every a∈R},Dk​(R)=⋃j≥0​Dkj​(R).
(41)
The order of an algebraic differential operator is the least j for which θ∈Dkj​(R); the zero operator may be assigned order −∞. In degree zero, commuting with all multiplication maps means θ(a)=aθ(1), so Dk0​(R)={ma​:a∈R}≅R. The commutator identity [UV,ma​]=U[V,ma​]+[U,ma​]V proves by induction that Dkr​Dks​⊆Dkr+s​, so the union is a ring under addition and composition.
The derivations of an algebra form
Derk​(R)={δ:R→R linear over k:δ(ab)=aδ(b)+bδ(a)}.
(42)
Every such derivation has δ(1)=0 and [δ,ma​]=mδ(a)​, so has differential order at most one. Conversely, if θ∈Dk1​(R), set c=θ(1) and δ=θ−mc​. Then δ(1)=0 and [δ,ma​] is a multiplication map. Evaluating at 1 shows it is mδ(a)​; evaluating at b gives the Leibniz rule. Hence the first-order algebraic differential operator decomposition is
Dk1​(R)=R+Derk​(R).​
(43)
It is a direct sum as k-vector spaces: a multiplication map that is a derivation vanishes at 1, and is zero.
The complex Weyl algebra has generators x1​,…,xn​,∂1​,…,∂n​ and relations
An​=C⟨xi​,∂i​⟩/([xi​,xj​],[∂i​,∂j​],[∂i​,xj​]−δij​).​
(44)
Take n≥1. If a nonzero unital module M had finite dimension of a vector space over C m, its representing matrices would satisfy [∂1​,x1​]=Im​. Taking the matrix trace gives 0=m, impossible in characteristic zero. The Weyl algebra has no nonzero finite-dimensional modules. For n=0 the algebra is C and this assertion has the obvious exception.
For a nonzero finitely generated Weyl algebra module, use the Bernstein filtration
Fj​An​=spanC​{xa∂b:∣a∣+∣b∣≤j}.
(45)
The ordered monomials form a vector space basis, so
dimC​Fj​An​=(2nj+2n​),grF​An​≅C[X1​,…,Xn​,Ξ1​,…,Ξn​].
(46)
To justify the ordered basis, the relations move all ∂i​ to the right and give spanning. Independence follows in the action on C[x1​,…,xn​]: apply a putative zero operator ∑b​pb​(x)∂b coefficientwise to the formal exponential et⋅x, obtaining ∑b​pb​(x)tb=0. Thus every coefficient vanishes.
Choose a finite-dimensional nonzero generating subspace M0​⊆M and set Mj​=Fj​An​M0​. The associated graded module is finitely generated over the polynomial associated graded ring, so the Hilbert-Serre theorem implies dimC​Mj​ is eventually a polynomial. Define
d(M)=deg(the eventual polynomial dimC​Mj​)=GKdimAn​​M.​
(47)
This Bernstein growth dimension of a Weyl algebra module equals the Gelfand–Kirillov dimension of a module, not its ordinary vector-space dimension. If a different finite generating subspace is used, each lies in some fixed filtration step of the other. The corresponding Mj​ are sandwiched between shifted filtrations, so the degree is unchanged.
We prove Bernstein inequality for Weyl algebra modules using an injectivity estimate. The faithful finite-step action of a Weyl algebra claim is that the map
Fj​An​⟶HomC​(Mj​,M2j​),a⟼(v↦av)
(48)
is injective for every j. Induct on j. For j=0, a scalar annihilating M0​=0 is zero. Suppose a∈Fj​An​ annihilates Mj​. For any generator z=xi​ or ∂i​, the commutator [a,z] belongs to Fj−1​An​ and annihilates Mj−1​: both a(zv) and z(av) vanish. The induction hypothesis gives [a,z]=0 for every generator. In the ordered basis,
[a,xi​]=∑a,b​bi​ca,b​xa∂b−ei​,[a,∂i​]=−∑a,b​ai​ca,b​xa−ei​∂b.
(49)
In characteristic zero these identities force a to be a scalar. Since it annihilates M0​, it is zero. This proves injectivity.
Consequently
(2nj+2n​)≤(dimC​Mj​)(dimC​M2j​).
(50)
The left side grows as a positive constant times j2n; the right side has growth degree 2d(M). Comparing powers proves
d(M)≥n.​
(51)
The polynomial module C[x1​,…,xn​], with the usual multiplication and differentiation actions, has d(M)=n, showing the bound is sharp.
