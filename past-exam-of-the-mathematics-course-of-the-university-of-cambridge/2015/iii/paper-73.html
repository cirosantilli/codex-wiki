One convention for the Papkovich–Neuber representation uses a harmonic vector potential Φ and a harmonic scalar potential χ:
2μu=∇(r⋅Φ+χ)−2Φ,p=∇⋅Φ,∇2Φ=0,∇2χ=0.​
(1)
These are harmonic functions away from any singular force point. Since ∇2(r⋅Φ)=2∇⋅Φ, the representation gives ∇⋅u=0 and μ∇2u=∇p, the equations of homogeneous Stokes flow.
Place the point force at the origin. A velocity linear in F, decaying as r−1 and having the rotational symmetry of a point force is obtained from Φ=cF/r, χ=0. The vector components are harmonic functions for r>0; scalar dipole potentials would instead generate higher-order decaying singularities. The force normalization fixes c=−1/(4π). Indeed, substitution gives the Stokeslet:
u(r)=8πμ1​(rF​+r3(F⋅r)r​),p(r)=4πr3F⋅r​.​
(2)
To verify its strength, the Newtonian fluid stress tensor is σij​=−3(F⋅r)ri​rj​/(4πr5). Its outward traction integrated over any sphere surrounding the origin is −F, because ∫nndΩ=(4π/3)I. Thus the localized force applied to the fluid is F, as required. Translation of the origin gives the same Stokeslet centered at any prescribed force point.

Thus the rotation is clockwise when viewed from positive z, with the magnitude of the displayed leading term.
The deflection tends back to zero downstream: Y(+∞)=Y∞​. More generally, kinematic reversibility of Stokes flow combined with reflection in the plane X=0 makes a passing trajectory fore-aft symmetric. One can see this without using the distant-sphere approximation: the relevant translational hydrodynamic mobility matrix has the form m⊥​(R)I+[m∥​(R)−m⊥​(R)]nn. Hence X˙ is even in X and Y˙ is odd in X. Uniqueness of the trajectory through X=0 then gives Y(X)=Y(−X).
For Y∞​=a/100, the numerical deflection and spin approximations above are invalid: the encounter enters a narrow gap and requires lubrication theory. However, the same return to the incoming offset holds for an ideal passing encounter of perfectly smooth spheres in Stokes flow. Lubrication resistance prevents finite-time contact under a bounded force, and does not itself destroy kinematic reversibility of Stokes flow. Contact, surface roughness or nonhydrodynamic forces could change that conclusion; they are additional physics, not part of the ideal model. This distinction is the fore-aft symmetry of a sedimenting-sphere encounter.

Take z positive upwards and let f denote axial body force per unit volume. With inertia and surface tension omitted, the extensional equations for a slender Newtonian column are
∂t​(a2)+∂z​(a2w)=0,a23μ​∂z​(a2∂z​w)=∂z​pext​−f.​

Mass conservation gives the first equation. The second uses the Trouton ratio three: the leading radial velocity is ur​=−rwz​/2, the radial normal traction gives p=pext​−μwz​, and the axial stress is −pext​+3μwz​. An axial control volume balance then gives the displayed equation. The approximation requires a slender column and slowly varying radius.
Let h=R−a. Since the container base is closed, the total volume flux at every section is zero. The column carries flux πa2w, so the annulus carries −πa2w and has mean speed of order a∣w∣/h. Its interfacial shear stress is therefore of order λμa∣w∣/h2. Transmitting this stress through the core produces an axial velocity variation of order λa2∣w∣/h2. The plug-flow criterion for a column in a low-viscosity annulus is consequently
∣w∣δw​=O(h2λa2​)≪1whenλ≪a2h2​.​

Use modified pressure P=pext​+ρgz, removing the annular fluid's hydrostatic pressure. In a local planar gap, with y=0 at the column and y=h at the container wall, lubrication theory gives
v(y)=w(1−hy​)+2λμPz​​y(y−h),qg​=2wh​−12λμh3Pz​​.

Pz​=h36λμa​w[1+O(h/a)].​

At leading order in the lubrication approximation, surface arclength is horizontal distance. An insoluble surfactant is transported by the surface velocity, without bulk exchange or diffusion. Conservation of insoluble surfactant on a moving interface therefore becomes
Ct​+∂x​(Cus​)=0,us​=u(x,h,t).​
(26)
The local hydrostatic pressure and capillary normal stress give
px​=ρghx​−∂x​(γhxx​).
(27)
Solve μuzz​=px​ with no-slip boundary condition at z=0 and Marangoni stress μuz​(h)=γx​=−ACx​. The velocity, surface velocity and volume flux per unit width are
u(z)us​q​=2μpx​​(z2−2hz)+μγx​​z,=−2μh2px​​−μAhCx​​,=−3μh3px​​−2μAh2Cx​​.​
(28)
Mass conservation, ht​+qx​=0, yields the thin-film equations with insoluble surfactant:
ht​=2μA​∂x​(h2Cx​)+3μρg​∂x​(h3hx​)−3μ1​∂x​[h3∂x​(γhxx​)],​
(29)
and the corresponding surfactant equation is
Ct​=μA​∂x​(hCCx​)+2μρg​∂x​(Ch2hx​)−2μ1​∂x​[Ch2∂x​(γhxx​)].​
(30)
The hydrostatic and capillary terms thus affect both the film flux and the surface transport, with different coefficients.
For finite-mass Marangoni spreading on a liquid film, first neglect both pressure-gradient terms. If the spread has size ℓ, then C∼M/ℓ and Cx​∼M/ℓ2, giving surface speed us​∼AMh0​/(μℓ2). Equating ℓ/t to this speed gives
ℓ(t)∼(μAMh0​t​)1/3.​
(31)
Set ℓ=(AMh0​t/μ)1/3 exactly as a similarity scale, and write
η=x/ℓ,h=h0​H(η),C=(M/ℓ)Γ(η).
(32)
On the positive half of the pool, substitution produces two ordinary differential equations:
−32​ηH′=(H2Γ′)′,−31​(ηΓ)′=(HΓΓ′)′.​
(33)
Symmetry fixes the flux integration constant in the second equation to zero, giving HΓ′=−η/3 wherever Γ>0. Substitution into the first equation gives ηH′=H. Thus the linear similarity profiles for surfactant spreading have the form
H=kη,Γ=3kηN​−η​,0≤η≤ηN​.
(34)
The concentration vanishes at the moving front. The two integral constraints are total surfactant mass and conservation of the fluid volume relative to the undisturbed layer:
2∫0ηN​​Γdη=1,∫0ηN​​(H−1)dη=0.​
(35)
The first gives k=ηN2​/3; the second gives kηN​=2. Therefore ηN3​=6. In physical variables, the solution is
xN​=(μ6AMh0​t​)1/3,h=xN​2h0​∣x∣​,C=xN​M​(1−xN​∣x∣​)(∣x∣<xN​).​
(36)
Outside the pool the leading outer solution has h=h0​, C=0. In particular, h(xN−​,t)=2h0​: the subscript means the one-sided limit just behind the front. As a consistency check, the surface speed there is xN​/(3t)=x˙N​ and the fluid jump satisfies the moving-front mass conservation condition. This ideal outer solution has a height jump from 2h0​ to h0​.
That jump cannot persist in a physical interface with nonzero gravity or surface tension. Across a smoothing region of width Δ, h changes by O(h0​). The Marangoni flux scales as AMh02​/(μxN2​). At g=0, its balance with the capillary flux γ0​h04​/(μΔ3) gives capillary smoothing of a Marangoni front:
Δ∼(AMγ0​h02​xN2​​)1/3∝t2/9.​
(37)
Here the surface tension approaches γ0​ at the surfactant-free edge. The assumed concentration-gradient scale is M/xN2​ even inside the smoothing region.
When gravity dominates capillarity, the hydrostatic flux is ρgh04​/(μΔ). The same balance gives gravity smoothing of a Marangoni front:
Δ∼AMρgh02​xN2​​∝t2/3.​
(38)
The gravity-dominated condition is Δ2≫γ0​/(ρg), the square of the capillary length. The printed condition omits ρ; the expression above restores dimensional consistency.
This width grows faster than xN​. It becomes comparable with the pool size when
xN∗​∼ρgh02​AM​,t∗∼(ρg)3h07​μ(AM)2​.​
(39)
The time is a scaling estimate, so numerical factors cannot be fixed by the width balance. For t≫t∗, the sharp-front similarity profile no longer describes the film: hydrostatic pressure levels the layer over the whole pool, and its thickness tends towards h0​ with an increasingly small depression beneath the surfactant. The surface can still spread by Marangoni stress while a hydrostatic-pressure-driven return flow nearly cancels the net film flux. Use pressure with the ambient hydrostatic pressure removed, and let ΔP be the pressure below the falling sphere minus that above it. Away from the sphere the tube flow is Hagen-Poiseuille flow. The upper and lower clear lengths add to L to leading order in a/L, so their pressure drops add:
Q=8μLπa4ΔP​.​
(40)
The large exterior reservoir returns this flux with negligible pressure loss compared with the long narrow tube. The sphere is assumed far enough from the ends that entrance effects and its occupied length give only lower-order corrections.
Now use a frame translating downwards with the sphere, and take z positive upwards from its center. The sphere is stationary and the tube wall moves upwards with speed U. The narrow gap is
h(z)=a−a2(1−ϵ/2)2−z2​≃2a​[ϵ+(z/a)2].
(41)
It has axial length scale aϵ​, making lubrication theory appropriate. The sphere-frame flux in a tube is constant: the upward relative flux through the whole section is πa2U−Q, so its flux per unit circumference is
q=2πaπa2U−Q​.​
(42)
With y=0 on the sphere and y=h on the wall, Couette-Poiseuille flow in a thin gap gives
u(y)=2μpz​​y(y−h)+hUy​,q=−12μh3pz​​+2Uh​,pz​=h26μU​−h312μq​.
(43)
Since ΔP=−∫pz​dz, the pressure-jump relation is
6μΔP​=2qI3​−UI2​,In​=∫−∞∞​h(z)ndz​.​
(44)
The parabolic inner gap can be extended to infinite z because these integrals are dominated by ∣z∣=O(aϵ​). The lubrication resistance integrals for a nearly occluding sphere are
In​=2na1−nϵ1/2−nJn​,Jn​=∫−∞∞​(1+ξ2)−ndξ.
(45)
Setting ξ=tanθ evaluates J1​=π, J2​=π/2, J3​=3π/8, hence
I1​=2πϵ−1/2,I2​=a2π​ϵ−3/2,I3​=a23π​ϵ−5/2.​
(46)
For the force, enclose the fluid between sections just above and below the sphere, including the annular layer. The net upward pressure force is πa2ΔP. The tube wall exerts upward shear traction μuy​(h) on this fluid; the sphere exerts a downward force equal to the upward hydrodynamic force F on itself. From the gap solution,
uy​(h)=2μpz​h​+hU​=h4U​−h26q​.
(47)
The axial control volume force balance therefore gives the control-volume drag formula for a sphere in a tube:
F=πa2ΔP+2πaμ(4UI1​−6qI2​).​
(48)
Using a control volume avoids having to integrate the curved-sphere normal pressure and tangential shear separately.
With Q∗=Q/(πa2U), ΔP∗=aΔP/(6μU), q∗=2q/(aU), F∗=F/(6πμaU), In∗​=an−1In​ and L∗=4L/(3a), the three flux/pressure equations become
Q∗=L∗ΔP∗​,ΔP∗=q∗I3∗​−I2∗​,q∗=1−Q∗.
(49)
Solving these linear relations gives
Q∗=L∗+I3∗​I3∗​−I2∗​​,q∗=L∗+I3∗​L∗+I2∗​​,ΔP∗=L∗+I3∗​L∗(I3∗​−I2∗​)​.​
(50)
The force relation is F∗=ΔP∗+34​I1∗​−q∗I2∗​. Substitution first yields
F∗=L∗+I3∗​L∗I3∗​−2L∗I2∗​+34​L∗I1∗​+34​I1∗​I3∗​−(I2∗​)2​.
(51)
Because I2∗​/I3∗​=2ϵ/3 and I1∗​/I3∗​=2ϵ2/3, the terms −2L∗I2∗​ and 34​L∗I1∗​ are lower order than L∗I3∗​. Keeping the uniformly relevant leading terms gives
F∗≃L∗+I3∗​L∗I3∗​+34​I1∗​I3∗​−(I2∗​)2​=L∗+3πϵ−5/23πL∗ϵ−5/2+4π2ϵ−3​.​
(52)
The squared I2∗​ is essential; replacing it by I2∗​I3∗​ would give the wrong sign and scaling. This establishes the three drag regimes for a nearly occluding sphere.
For L∗≪ϵ−1/2,
F∗∼34π​ϵ−1/2,Q∗∼1,q∗∼32​ϵ,ΔP∗∼L∗,ΔP∗F∗​≫1.​
(53)
The sphere nearly carries the tube's fluid down with it: the far-field flux is close to the piston displacement rate. The relative annular leakage is small in total volume, and the dominant force comes from the local annular shear term. The pressure force associated with the overall tube resistance is smaller.
For ϵ−1/2≪L∗≪ϵ−5/2,
F∗∼L∗,ΔP∗∼L∗,Q∗∼1,q∗≃32​ϵ+3πL∗ϵ5/2​≪1,ΔP∗F∗​∼1.​
(54)
The flow remains approximately piston-like, but the dominant force is now the pressure needed to drive Hagen-Poiseuille flow through the long clear tube. Both displayed terms in q∗ are retained because their relative size changes inside this regime without producing an additional leading drag regime.
For L∗≫ϵ−5/2,
F∗∼ΔP∗∼3πϵ−5/2,Q∗∼L∗3πϵ−5/2​≪1,q∗∼1,ΔP∗F∗​∼1.​
(55)
The large tube resistance makes its net through-flow negligible. The sphere's displaced fluid instead passes upwards relative to it through the thin annular gap, with gap velocity of order U/ϵ. Its pressure drop and resulting pressure force dominate.
Finally consider two identical co-moving spheres, with nonoverlapping lubrication regions and the same total clear-tube length to leading order. The common through-flux is not doubled. In the middle regime it is still approximately πa2U, so the same total tube pressure drop is shared equally between the two identical spheres. The load sharing between co-moving spheres in a tube gives half the single-sphere force on each sphere in regime (ii). In regime (i), drag is set locally by the nearly pressure-balanced annular shear and is unchanged on each sphere. In regime (iii), each sphere must pass essentially its own displacement flux through its annular gap, giving the same local pressure drag as before; adding another pressure jump changes the already small tube flux but not either leading drag. The force is unchanged in regimes (i) and (iii).
