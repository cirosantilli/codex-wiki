Use the time-harmonic wave convention Re(ψe−iωt), with a positive background wavenumber k. The scalar Helmholtz equation for a constant-density acoustic model is
ψxx​+ψzz​+k2n2ψ=0.
(1)
Writing ψ=eikxE gives the exact envelope equation
Exx​+2ikEx​+Ezz​+k2(n2−1)E=0.
(2)
The paraxial approximation neglects Exx​ relative to 2ikEx​, giving the parabolic wave equation
2ikEx​+Ezz​+k2(n2−1)E=0,Ex​=2ki​Ezz​+2ik​(n2−1)E.​
(3)
The choice of carrier and the direction of propagation matter: this is a forward, slowly varying envelope approximation. Sufficient scale conditions are ∣Exx​∣≪2k∣Ex​∣, transverse spectral components ∣p∣≪k, and medium/envelope variation on longitudinal scales large compared with 1/k. With the carrier fixed at the background k, small ∣n−1∣ makes the refractive phase vary slowly too. Large-angle propagation, appreciable backscattering, or rapid longitudinal variation violates the approximation. Acoustic models with variable mass density can have additional gradient terms, so the scalar Helmholtz equation itself is a model assumption. For the Gaussian beam in part (b), useful initial conditions are kD≫1 and D/∣F∣≪1, with the second condition absent for an initially uncurved beam. A narrow angular spectrum, rather than merely the label “Gaussian”, justifies the paraxial approximation.

No: the first moment generally depends on the transverse coordinate. At entry to the random medium, the initial data are deterministic, so
m1​(x0​,z)=Efree​(x0​,z),
(8)
which is a nonconstant Gaussian beam profile. Statistical homogeneity of the medium says that shifting both the medium and the incident data gives correspondingly shifted field statistics. It does not make the response to fixed, localized incident data translation invariant.
In particular, Gaussian coherent-field propagation in the Markov approximation, derived in part (d), gives
m1​(x,z)=e−γ(x−x0​)Efree​(x,z)
(9)
for the linear weak-index model. A homogeneous attenuation factor multiplies the varying beam profile. Thus spatially homogeneous medium statistics can coexist with a transversely inhomogeneous coherent field. The first moment is not the mean wave intensity, and disappearance of the coherent component is not itself disappearance of the total wave energy.

Write m=⟨E⟩ and L0​=i∂z2​/(2k). Substituting n=1+μW into part (a), without prematurely replacing n2 by its linear part, gives
Ex​=L0​E+ikμWE+2ikμ2​W2E.
(10)
Taking the expectation yields the exact mean equation within the paraxial approximation:
mx​=L0​m+ikμ⟨WE⟩+2ikμ2​⟨W2E⟩,m(x0​,z)=Efree​(x0​,z).​
(11)
The correlation term cannot be discarded just because ⟨W⟩=0: the field depends on the same random medium as W. Nor can ⟨W2E⟩ be factored exactly from the given variance alone. The printed statistics do not specify the autocorrelation function of a random field, so they do not determine a unique local closed equation for m.
A useful covariance-dependent equation follows to second order in μ. Define
C(s,η)=⟨W(x,z)W(x−s,z−η)⟩,C(0,0)=1,
(12)
and let
Gs​(η)=2πisk​​exp(2sikη2​),s>0,
(13)
be the Fresnel propagator kernel for one transverse coordinate. The Duhamel principle applied to the linear fluctuation term gives
E(x,z)=E(0)(x,z)+ikμ∫x0​x​∫R​Gx−s​(z−ζ)W(s,ζ)E(0)(s,ζ)dζds+O(μ2),
(14)
where E(0)=Efree​. Multiply by W(x,z) and average. Also ⟨W(x,z)2E(x,z)⟩=E(0)(x,z)+O(μ). Since m=E(0)+O(μ2), replacing E(0) by m in terms already multiplied by μ2 preserves second-order accuracy. We obtain the weak-fluctuation memory equation for the coherent field
mx​(x,z)​=L0​m(x,z)+2ikμ2​m(x,z)−k2μ2∫x0​x​∫R​C(x−s,z−ζ)Gx−s​(z−ζ)m(s,ζ)dζds+O(μ3).​​
(15)
This perturbation statement is for fixed propagation intervals with sufficient covariance regularity and moment bounds. The kernel is understood as an oscillatory integral. If the field is jointly Gaussian, odd perturbative contributions vanish and the remainder can be improved under the corresponding expansion hypotheses. Joint Gaussianity itself still leaves the covariance unspecified. The term ikμ2m/2 is the quadratic refractive-index shift in the coherent field; it comes from the mean of W2.
For the usual local answer, impose the additional Markov approximation for a random medium. Its longitudinal correlation length must be much shorter than the distance on which the envelope varies, and diffraction across that correlation distance must not resolve substantial transverse covariance variation. Away from the entry layer, replace the slowly varying factors in the memory integral by their local values and extend the longitudinal integral to infinity. Define the integrated covariance
B(η)=∫−∞∞​C(s,η)ds,γ=2k2μ2​B(0)=k2μ2∫0∞​C(s,0)ds.
(16)
For an integrable real stationary covariance, C(s,0) is even, and B(0)≥0 is its zero-longitudinal-frequency spectral density. The conventional leading weak-index model keeps only the linear random potential ikμW; in that model the local first-moment equation is
mx​=2ki​mzz​−γm.​
(17)
The factor 1/2 can be checked independently in the white-noise model. If Bx​(z) is a Brownian field with ⟨dBx​(z)dBx​(z′)⟩=B(z−z′)dx, write
dE=L0​Edx+ikμE∘dBx​(z).
(18)
Converting the Stratonovich integral to an Itô integral adds −k2μ2B(0)Edx/2. The remaining stochastic integral has zero mean, giving the boxed equation and
m(x,z)=e−γ(x−x0​)q(x)−1/2exp(−q(x)Az2​).​
(19)
If the finite-correlation model retains n2 consistently through order μ2, the local second-order expansion also has +ikμ2m/2, adding the phase factor eikμ2(x−x0​)/2. This phase is computed before the white-noise idealization: squaring ideal Gaussian white noise is not the finite-variance operation W2.
The need for a correlation assumption can be seen without any closure argument. The stationary Gaussian random field W(x,z)=Z, with a single standard normal variable Z, has all the printed one-point statistics. In the linear weak-index model it gives
E=eikμZ(x−x0​)Efree​,m=e−k2μ2(x−x0​)2/2Efree​.
(20)
Its attenuation is quadratic in propagation distance, whereas the Markov model gives linear-distance exponential attenuation. The local attenuation equation requires the additional correlation/Markov assumption; it does not follow from statistical homogeneity and unit variance alone.

Take the same e−iωt convention and an outgoing field. Define the scattering potential with the positive-contrast sign,
Q(r)=k2[n(r)2−1],(Δ+k2)ψ=−Qψ.
(21)
The outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation is
Gk​(r,r′)=4π∣r−r′∣eik∣r−r′∣​,(Δ+k2)Gk​=−δ.
(22)
Thus the Lippmann-Schwinger equation has a plus sign:
ψ=ψi​+Tψ,(Tϕ)(r)=∫V​Gk​(r,r′)Q(r′)ϕ(r′)d3r′.
(23)
Replacing the total field inside the integral by the incident field gives the Born approximation for scalar wave scattering
ψs(1)​(r)=4πk2​∫V​[n(r′)2−1]∣r−r′∣eik∣r−r′∣​eikr0​⋅r′d3r′.​
(24)
More generally, iterating the integral equation gives the Born series ψs​=∑j≥1​Tjψi​; its first term is the boxed expression. A sufficient convergence condition is ∥T∥<1 on the chosen space of fields in V, and accurate single-scattering truncation needs the neglected terms to be small. Weak refractive contrast is useful, but for a sufficiently large or resonant scatterer it is not by itself a guarantee of negligible multiple scattering.

For r=Rr with the observation distance much larger than the scatterer, expand ∣r−r′∣=R−r⋅r′+O(R−1). The far-field pattern in the Born approximation for scalar wave scattering is consequently
ψs(1)​(Rr)=ReikR​f∞(1)​(r,r0​)+O(R−2),
(25)
f∞(1)​=4πk2​∫V​[n(r′)2−1]e−iks​⋅r′d3r′,ks​=k(r−r0​).​
(26)
This convention includes the factor 1/(4π) in f∞​; moving that factor into the outgoing-wave definition would change the pattern's normalization.
The absolute energy flux depends on what physical amplitude ψ represents. For acoustic pressure in a homogeneous fluid of mass density ρ0​ and wave speed c0​, the momentum equation gives the particle-velocity amplitude v=∇ψ/(iρ0​ω). The time average of harmonic power and acoustic intensity then give
⟨I⟩=21​Re(ψv∗)=2ρ0​ω1​Im(ψ∗∇ψ).
(27)
The radial derivative of the outgoing factor is (ik−1/R)eikR/R. Its imaginary current is therefore k∣f∞​∣2/R2 to leading order. The incident unit-amplitude plane wave has imaginary current kr0​. Hence, with k=ω/c0​,
Es​(R)=2ρ0​ωR2k​∣f∞(1)​∣2+O(R−3),Ei​=2ρ0​ωk​=2ρ0​c0​1​.​
(28)
The scattered energy flux points along r at leading order, and the incident energy flux along r0​. If ψ is a velocity-potential amplitude instead, both leading expressions use the common prefactor ρ0​ωk/2 in place of k/(2ρ0​ω). A normalized scalar current uses k as the common prefactor. The paper does not specify this amplitude normalization; the ratio in part (c) is independent of that choice.

Let the medium's autocorrelation function of a random field and power spectrum use the explicit convention
C(s)=⟨W(r+s)W(r)⟩,SW​(q)=∫R3​C(s)e−iq⋅sd3s,
(30)
C(s)=(2π)31​∫R3​SW​(q)eiq⋅sd3q.
(31)
Since n2−1=2μW+μ2W2, only the linear term in μ is needed to obtain the cross-section through second order:
f∞(1)​=2πμk2​∫V​W(r)e−iks​⋅rd3r+O(μ2).
(32)
Squaring and taking the expectation gives
⟨σd​⟩=4π2μ2k4​∫V​∫V​C(r−r′)e−iks​⋅(r−r′)d3rd3r′+O(μ3).​
(33)
This is the finite-volume formula in the Born approximation for scalar wave scattering; the zero mean of W does not make its squared scattered amplitude vanish. For a jointly centered Gaussian random field, the third-order contribution is zero, so the next perturbative contribution is fourth order when that expansion is valid.
Introduce the overlap volume HV​(s)=∣V∩(V−s)∣. Changing variables rewrites the double integral as
∫R3​HV​(s)C(s)e−iks​⋅sd3s.
(34)
When the linear dimensions of a regular scattering volume are large compared with the correlation length, and C is integrable, HV​(s)≃∣V∣ over the significant covariance range. This gives the finite-volume random scattering spectrum result
⟨σd​(r,r0​)⟩≃4π2μ2k4∣V∣​SW​(ks​),ks​=k(r−r0​).​
(35)
There are two distinct approximations here: the weak-scattering expansion in μ and the negligible-boundary-overlap approximation in the volume-to-correlation-scale ratio. If S instead denotes the power spectrum of the index fluctuation n−1=μW, then S=μ2SW​ and the external factor μ2 is absorbed into S.
Statistical homogeneity alone allows an anisotropic spectrum, so its argument is generally the vector ks​. If statistical isotropy is additionally assumed, SW​ depends only on
ks​=∣ks​∣=2ksin(θ/2),cosθ=r⋅r0​.​
(36)
The scalar notation S(ks​) is valid in that isotropic case. For the alternative convention Snorm​=(2π)−3SW​, the bulk formula is 2πμ2k4∣V∣Snorm​(ks​). Changing the sign of the scattering vector does not change the spectrum of a real stationary scalar field; changing the Fourier transform normalization does change the displayed 2π factors. Both conventions have therefore been stated explicitly.

For noisy data, exact equality Ax=y may be impossible: the data can have a component outside the operator range. A least-squares solution of a linear inverse problem instead minimizes
J(x)=21​∥Ax−y∥Y2​.
(37)
For any variation h∈X, the adjoint operator gives
DJ(x)[h]=Re⟨Ax−y,Ah⟩Y​=Re⟨A∗(Ax−y),h⟩X​.
(38)
Thus statistical homogeneity in every direction is the normal equation for a linear inverse problem, A∗Ax=A∗y. It is also sufficient for a global minimum: when it holds,
J(x+h)−J(x)=21​∥Ah∥Y2​≥0.​
(39)
The residual is orthogonal to the closure of the range, since it lies in kerA∗. A normal-equation solution therefore fits the component of the data representable by the forward map, while leaving the perpendicular component unfitted. If there is more than one such solution, select the minimum-norm least-squares solution, which lies in (kerA)⊥.
These facts explain the formulation, but do not make the inverse problem well posed. A compact operator of infinite rank typically has nonclosed operator range, so even the least-squares infimum need not be attained. A least-squares solution exists exactly when the orthogonal projection of y onto RanA belongs to RanA. In a singular system of a compact operator, this becomes the Picard criterion. Also, the nonzero eigenvalues of A∗A are the squares of the singular values of A: solving the operator normal equation directly can worsen numerical conditioning. The operator normal equation expresses least squares; it does not itself regularize the inverse problem. The subsequent finite Landweber iteration and its stopping rule provide the inverse-problem regularization.

Choose a fixed positive Landweber relaxation parameter τ and rearrange the normal equation for a linear inverse problem as the fixed point equation
x=(I−τA∗A)x+τA∗y.
(40)
Successive substitution gives Landweber iteration
xn+1​=xn​+τA∗(y−Axn​),x0​=0.​
(41)
Set B=A∗A and P=I−τB. Induction yields the closed polynomial-operator expression
xn​=τj=0∑n−1​PjA∗y=hn​(B)A∗y,hn​(t)=⎩⎨⎧​t1−(1−τt)n​,nτ,​t=0,t=0.​​
(42)
For n=0 the sum is empty, giving zero. The expression is well defined without an inverse of A∗A, which can have a kernel of a linear map and an unbounded inverse on its range. Each iterate lies in the closure of RanA∗, so initialization at zero eliminates the arbitrary null-space component of a solution. The unrelaxed choice τ=1 gives xn+1​=(I−A∗A)xn​+A∗y and the corresponding sum with τ=1; it needs the convergence restriction in part (d).

The geometric series then gives the full inverse coefficient, or Landweber full inverse filter,
gα​(σ)=σ1−(1−τσ2)1/α​,α=1/n,n∈N.​
(45)
In particular, for the unrelaxed iteration τ=1, gα​(σ)=[1−(1−σ2)1/α]/σ. The dimensionless Landweber spectral filter is instead σgα​(σ); the printed expression uses the full coefficient and therefore needs the denominator σ.

For nonzero A, a sufficient step-size condition for strong convergence of relaxed Landweber iteration is
0<τ<∥A∥22​.​
(46)
For the unrelaxed iteration, this reads ∥A∥2<2. The data must also admit a minimum-norm least-squares solution x†; for the exact problem, y∈RanA is sufficient. More generally, the projected-data condition in part (a), or the Picard criterion ∑i​∣⟨y,ui​⟩∣2/σi2​<∞, ensures the required solution.
Every positive singular value satisfies ∣1−τσi2​∣<1, so for fixed σi​>0,
α→0lim​gα​(σi​)=σi​1​.​
(47)
This is the correct inverse coefficient. For exact data with a least-squares solution, the norm error is
∥xn​−x†∥2=∑i​∣1−τσi2​∣2nσi2​∣⟨y,ui​⟩∣2​⟶0.
(48)
The summable majorant is precisely the Picard criterion, so the dominated convergence theorem proves strong convergence, despite the absence of a uniform contraction constant when the singular values approach zero. Initialization at zero selects the solution orthogonal to kerA. At τ=2/∥A∥2, a top singular mode of a nonzero compact operator has multiplier −1 and can oscillate indefinitely; the strict step bound avoids this failure.
Pointwise convergence of filters alone is not a full noisy-data inverse-problem regularization proof. For each finite n, put t=τσ2; the step restriction gives ∣1−t∣≤1, and
∣1−(1−t)n∣≤min(nt,2),∣g1/n​(σ)∣≤min(nτσ,2/σ)≤2nτ​.
(49)
This proves the uniform noise bound for relaxed Landweber iteration
∥xn(δ)​−xn​∥≤δ2nτ​.
(50)
Together with the exact-data convergence,
∥xn(δ)​−x†∥≤δ2nτ​+∥xn​−x†∥⟶0​
(51)
whenever n=n(δ)→∞ and δn(δ)​→0. Equivalently, the a priori regularization parameter choice satisfies α(δ)→0 and δ/α(δ)​→0. For example, n(δ)=⌊δ−1⌋ works as δ→0. The sharper familiar bound δnτ​ is available when τ∥A∥2≤1.
Thus finite iteration followed by an appropriate stopping rule is a convergent regularization of an inverse problem. Taking n→∞ for fixed noisy data is not the same operation and can amplify the small-singular-value noise without bound. If A=0, the zero starting iterate stays zero and is the minimum-norm least-squares solution for every datum; the step restriction is needed only for nonzero A.
