For an explicit algorithm, keep an unnormalized residual R, initially A, whose row and column sums all equal a common mass τ, initially one. While τ>0, find a perfect matching in its positive support, form its permutation matrix P, and subtract αP, where α is the smallest selected entry. Record (α,P) and replace τ by τ−α.
If the residual mass is positive, the same proof of the Hall marriage theorem condition applies after division by τ. Every subtraction removes at least one positive entry and creates no new positive entries. Before the last subtraction, any positive-mass residual has at least n positive entries; the last step removes all its entries. If r is the initial number of positive entries, then
k≤r−n+1≤n2−n+1.​
(39)
Since the residual ultimately vanishes, summing the recorded subtractions gives A=∑αi​Pi​, and the row sums give ∑αi​=1.
A perfect matching can be found by at most n searches for an augmenting path in a matching, each costing O(n2) in a graph with at most n2 edges. Thus each iteration costs O(n3), including forming the support and updating the residual, and there are O(n2) iterations. Hence
running time O(n5) arithmetic/comparison operations.​
(40)
This bound uses exact real arithmetic. For rational input, a common denominator lets all residual subtractions be performed on integers of polynomial bit length, giving a polynomial bit-time implementation as well.
