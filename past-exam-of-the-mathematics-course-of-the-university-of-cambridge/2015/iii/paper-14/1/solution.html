Use probability measure-preserving systems: T:X→X is measurable and μ(T−1A)=μ(A). Its invariant sigma-algebra is
I={A∈B:μ(T−1A△A)=0}.
(1)
Ergodicity means that every invariant measurable set has measure zero or one:
A∈I ⟹ μ(A)∈{0,1}.​
(2)
Equivalently, every invariant measurable function is constant almost everywhere. Indeed all rational sublevel sets of a real invariant function belong to I, so the zero-or-one alternative forces its distribution to concentrate at one value. Apply this to real and imaginary parts for complex functions. Conversely, an invariant set has an invariant indicator, so the function criterion implies the set criterion. This is the invariant-function characterization of ergodicity.
For an irrational rotation of the circle, T(x)=x+α(mod1) with α∈/Q, an invariant g∈L2(m) has Fourier coefficients satisfying
(e2πikα−1)g​(k)=0.
(3)
For k=0 the multiplier is nonzero. Completeness of the Fourier series basis therefore makes g constant. Apply this to an invariant indicator to prove the irrational rotation is ergodic. A second example is the cyclic permutation of q points with equal masses: a nonempty invariant set contains the whole cycle and hence has measure one.
The identity on [0,1] with Lebesgue measure is not ergodic, since [0,1/2] is invariant and has measure 1/2. Nor is rotation by 1/5 on the circle: the set
A={x∈R/Z:5x(mod1)∈[0,1/2)}
(4)
is invariant and has measure 1/2. These examples also show why preservation of measure does not imply ergodicity.
Let U=UT​ be the Koopman operator, Uf=f∘T, and set
AN​f=N1​∑n=0N−1​Unf.
(5)
The Von Neumann mean ergodic theorem says that, for f∈L2(μ), these Cesaro averages converge in L2 to the orthogonal projection onto the invariant functions:
AN​f⟶PFixU​f=Eμ​[f∣I]in L2.​
(6)
For an invertible system, U is a unitary operator. The Hilbert-space theorem states more generally that the Cesaro averages of any unitary operator converge strongly to its fixed-space projection.
Here is the requested invertible-case proof. Since U∗=U−1,
(Ran(I−U))⊥=ker(I−U∗)=ker(I−U).
(7)
Thus the orthogonal decomposition for unitary ergodic averages is
L2(μ)=FixU⊕Ran(I−U)​.
(8)
On the first summand, AN​f=f. On a vector (I−U)g, telescoping gives
AN​(I−U)g=Ng−UNg​,∥AN​(I−U)g∥2​≤N2∥g∥2​​⟶0.
(9)
Because ∥AN​∥≤1, approximation extends this convergence to the closure of the range. The orthogonal decomposition now proves the theorem for every f. An invariant L2 function is I-measurable, and an I-measurable function is invariant by approximation with indicators; hence the projection is the stated conditional expectation.
In an ergodic system, invariant functions are constants by the criterion already proved. Their orthogonal projection is obtained by taking the inner product with 1, whose norm is one. Therefore the ergodic limit is the space average:
AN​f⟶(∫X​fdμ)1in L2.​
(10)
The Birkhoff ergodic theorem states that, for f∈L1(μ),
N1​n=0∑N−1​f(Tnx)⟶Eμ​[f∣I](x)for μ-almost every x.​
(11)
The limit is invariant, integrable, and has the same integral as f. On a probability space convergence also holds in L1. In an ergodic system the limit is ∫fdμ almost everywhere.
For the topological sharpening, take a continuous map T on a compact metric space. Unique ergodicity means there is exactly one invariant Borel probability measure μ. The uniform ergodic convergence for uniquely ergodic systems says that every continuous real or complex f satisfies
x∈Xsup​​N1​n=0∑N−1​f(Tnx)−∫X​fdμ​⟶0.​
(12)
Thus convergence holds at every point and uniformly in that point. The continuity, compactness and continuous-observable hypotheses belong to this theorem; it is not a claim of uniform convergence for all measurable L1 functions.
