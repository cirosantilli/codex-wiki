Use the Gaussian units convention implicit in the 4π coefficient. For a uniform dielectric and a counterion-only electrolyte, the Boltzmann distribution and Poisson-Boltzmann equation are
n(r)=n0​e−βqϕ(r),ρ(r)=qn(r),r1​drd​(rdrdϕ​)=−ϵ4πqn0​​e−βqϕ,β=(kB​T)−1.
(1)
The reference electric potential is ϕ(a)=0, so n0​=n(a); it is not a prescribed nonzero bulk concentration at infinity. A salt reservoir with additional ion species would require their charge densities as well. With u=log(r/a), the cylindrical Laplacian is r−2d2/du2. Since βqϕ=Φ+2u and r2=a2e2u, the factors of e2u cancel:
Φ′′=−Ce−Φ,C=ϵ4πβq2a2n0​​.​
(2)
Here primes mean differentiation with respect to u, and the surface condition is Φ(0)=0 in this coordinate.
Multiply by Φ′ to obtain a first integral:
21​Φ′2=Ce−Φ+K.
(3)
The two specified far-field limits set K=0. For a solution tending upwards to infinity, take the positive square root. Writing b=C/2​ gives (eΦ/2)′=b, hence the cylindrical counterion-only Poisson-Boltzmann profile
Φ(u)=2log(1+bu),βqϕ(r)=2logar​+2log(1+blogar​).​
(4)
For b>0, Φ→∞ and Φ′=2b/(1+bu)→0. The corresponding number density is
n(r)=[1+blog(r/a)]2n0​(a/r)2​.​
(5)
Apply Gauss's law to a coaxial surface per unit cylinder length. Define the positive accumulated cylindrical screening charge by
Q(r)=2π∫ar​sρ(s)ds.
(6)
The signed outward electric field is Er​=2[−λ+Q(r)]/(ϵr), so its magnitude is 2∣λ−Q(r)∣/(ϵr). In the branch here, Q(r)<λ, the field is inward and
E(r)=ϕ′(r)=ϵr2​[λ−2π∫ar​sρ(s)ds],Qscreening​=2π∫a∞​rρ(r)dr.​
(7)
In this display, ϕ′ is a radial derivative. The quantities Q are electric charges per unit axial length, not total charges of an infinite cylinder.
At the surface, aϕ′(a)=2λ/ϵ. Define the charge-q Bjerrum length ℓB​=βq2/ϵ and the Manning parameter η=λℓB​/q. Matching the derivative of the solution gives
Φ′(0)=βqaϕ′(a)−2=2(η−1)=2b,b=η−1.
(8)
Thus the condensed branch exists only for η>1, and its normalization is
λc​=ℓB​q​,n0​=2πℓB​a2(η−1)2​(λ>λc​).​
(9)
The sign condition b>0 is essential: simply squaring η−1 would produce a spurious subcritical density. Direct integration using rn(r)dr=n0​a2du/(1+bu)2 yields
Q(r)=λc​b[1−1+blog(r/a)1​],Qscreening​=λc​b=λ−λc​.​
(10)
Equivalently, E(r)=2λc​[1+b/(1+blog(r/a))]/(ϵr): the remaining far-field line-charge magnitude is λc​. The Manning condensed fraction is 1−1/η.
Below threshold, no positive-density profile can satisfy those same far-field conditions. Indeed Φ′′≤0, so a negative initial slope 2(η−1) cannot increase to zero. The physical interpretation is the infinite-dilution limit of cylindrical counterions: start with a neutral finite cylindrical cell and send its outer radius to infinity. For η≤1, counterions escape to arbitrarily large radii, leaving n0​→0 and no finite condensed charge. The bare-cylinder Boltzmann distribution n∝r−2η also shows why its radial normalization ∫r1−2ηdr diverges in this regime. In the local zero-density limit, Φ=2(η−1)u; this does not obey the condensed branch's imposed limits. At η=1 the branch has zero amplitude and Φ=0, rather than Φ→∞.
Consequently the intended counterion condensation result, interpreted as a local infinite-dilution density, is
n0​=2πℓB​a2[max(η−1,0)]2​,Qcondensed​=max(λ−λc​,0).​
(11)
Every finite neutral cell still contains enough counterions to neutralize the cylinder; the missing residual charge in the local limiting profile is carried to infinity. Taking the infinite-cell limit before the charge integral differs from integrating the entire finite cell first. In SI units, the same reference-charge Bjerrum length is q2/(4πϵabs​kB​T); the electrostatic prefactors must be changed consistently.

For one small transverse displacement, the elastic energy of the elastic filament is
U[h]=2A​∫0L​[h′′(x)]2dx,
(12)
where A is the filament bending modulus. Its first variation is
δU=A[h′′δh′−h′′′δh]0L​+A∫0L​h′′′′δhdx.
(13)
The clamped boundary conditions remove the left boundary terms. A force-free and torque-free tip permits independent δh(L) and δh′(L), so the natural boundary conditions for a free endpoint are h′′(L)=h′′′(L)=0: zero bending moment and transverse shear.
There is an important distinction between an energy minimum and a normal mode. The unloaded higher-order Euler-Lagrange equation is h′′′′=0, whose only solution with these four boundary conditions is h=0. A general fluctuating shape is a sum of modes, not one of the stated sinusoidal/hyperbolic functions. To obtain the clamped--free bending modes, extremize the bending Rayleigh quotient, or equivalently U[W]−21​Ak4∫W2dx with a fixed norm. Its Euler-Lagrange equation is
W′′′′=k4W.​
(14)
This is the constrained variational characterization of bending modes. Its four characteristic roots are ±k,±ik. Clamping gives F=−D and E=−B, hence
W=D(coskx−coshkx)+B(sinkx−sinhkx).
(15)
The free-tip conditions reduce to
(cosq+coshqsinq−sinhq​sinq+sinhq−(cosq+coshq)​)(BD​)=0,q=kL.
(16)
Its determinant is −2(1+cosqcoshq), so nontrivial modes require
cosqn​coshqn​=−1,kn​=qn​/L.​
(17)
No zero mode exists, since a cubic satisfying the homogeneous clamp/free conditions is zero. The clamped-free bending spectrum starts with q1​∈(π/2,2); bisection or Newton iteration gives q1​≃1.875104, so k1​≃1.875104/L. Choosing D=N(sinqn​+sinhqn​) and B=−N(cosqn​+coshqn​) gives
Wn​=N[(sinqn​+sinhqn​)(coskn​x−coshkn​x)−(cosqn​+coshqn​)(sinkn​x−sinhkn​x)].​
(18)
The second boundary condition follows from the root equation, and N is arbitrary until a normalization is chosen.

Write the deterministic drift of the Adler phase equation as f(θ)=ω−ϵsinθ. Its minimum is ω−ϵ and its maximum is ω+ϵ. For 0<ω<ϵ, the zero condition has exactly two solutions in the specified interval:
θs​=α=arcsin(ω/ϵ),θu​=π−α.​
(24)
The linearization of a dynamical system at an equilibrium point gives δθ˙=f′(θ∗​)δθ, with f′=−ϵcosθ. Defining κ=ϵ2−ω2​ gives f′(θs​)=−κ<0, so θs​ is stable; f′(θu​)=+κ>0, so θu​ is unstable. For ω>ϵ, f is positive everywhere and there are no equilibrium points: the phase runs continuously. This is the distinction between phase locking and running phase dynamics.
With mobility scaled to one, the effective force is −V′. Therefore the tilted washboard potential is
V(θ)=−ωθ−ϵcosθ+C.​
(25)
For ω<ϵ, its alternating local minima and maxima trap noise-free trajectories in wells. Minima coincide with θs​+2πj, and maxima with θu​+2πj. For ω>ϵ, V′=ϵsinθ−ω<0 everywhere: there are no wells and the particle slides down the tilt. The potential is defined on the unwrapped phase and obeys V(θ+2π)=V(θ)−2πω; it is not a single-valued periodic equilibrium potential on the circle.
At the transition ω=ϵ, the two equilibrium points merge at θ=π/2 in a saddle-node bifurcation. There f≃ϵ(θ−π/2)2/2, so the point is attracting from the left and repelling from the right; a zero linear derivative alone does not establish stable trapping.

For 0<ω<ϵ, let x=θ−θs​ and κ=ϵ2−ω2​. In a single well, sufficiently small fluctuations obey the linear overdamped Langevin dynamics
x˙=−κx+ξ(t),⟨ξ(t)ξ(t′)⟩=2Teff​δ(t−t′).
(26)
This is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. Its solution is
x(t)=e−κtx(0)+∫0t​e−κ(t−s)ξ(s)ds.
(27)
The mean decays, while the noise integral gives ⟨x(t)2⟩→Teff​/κ. For τ≥0, the future-noise contribution is independent of x(t), so ⟨x(t)x(t+τ)⟩=e−κτ⟨x(t)2⟩. The intrawell phase autocorrelation is therefore
⟨θ(t)θ(t+τ)⟩≃arcsin2(ω/ϵ)+ϵ2−ω2​Teff​​exp[−ϵ2−ω2​∣τ∣].​
(28)
The connected unnormalized time autocorrelation is just the exponentially decaying second term. The constant first term is necessary because the requested raw correlation is not centered at the equilibrium point. This is the harmonic-well approximation. If a full first-order expansion in Teff​ is wanted, the quadratic term in the drift is ωx2/2. Local stationarity gives ⟨x⟩=ωTeff​/(2κ2)+O(Teff2​), so the raw correlation gains a constant αωTeff​/κ2 at that order. The connected correlation is unchanged to first order; the boxed formula is the conventional linearized result rather than a complete nonlinear first-order raw-correlation expansion.
The required time window is long compared with κ−1 but short compared with the escape time, with the lag also short compared with escape. Fluctuations of size Teff​/κ​ must be small compared with the distance to a neighboring maximum, and the relevant barriers must greatly exceed Teff​. For a fixed nonzero noise strength, the unwrapped phase eventually makes phase slips; its raw correlation does not have this stationary infinite-time limit. Thus “large time” here refers to local relaxation within the occupied well, not to the limit after arbitrarily many barrier crossings.
For thermally activated phase slips, the forward saddle is at π−α and the backward saddle at −π−α. Their barriers above the same minimum are
ΔV+​=2κ−ω(π−2α),ΔV−​=2κ+ω(π+2α),ΔV−​−ΔV+​=2πω.​
(29)
In the Kramers escape rate approximation, both saddles have curvature −κ and the minimum has curvature +κ, so the prefactors agree:
p±​∝2πκ​e−ΔV±​/Teff​,p−​p+​​=exp(Teff​2πω​).​
(30)
This is the forward-backward bias of phase slips. It favors forward motion because each forward step lowers the tilted potential by 2πω. For ω≪ϵ, ΔV±​=2ϵ∓πω+O(ω2/ϵ), which gives the same ratio; the exact barrier difference is independent of ϵ. These probabilities may be read as rates in a common short observation interval, or as normalized competing exit probabilities. The Teff​ in the exponent is the noise scale as defined in the Langevin equation; no additional kB​ factor is inserted.
