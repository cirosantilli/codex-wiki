Integrating the standard thin-disk dissipation flux over annular area, with both faces already included in H, gives
LD​=23GM∗​M˙​∫r∗​rout​​(r−2−r∗1/2​r−5/2)dr=2r∗​GM∗​M˙​[1−3rout​r∗​​+2(rout​r∗​​)3/2].
(12)
Thus the disk luminosity for rout​≫r∗​ is
LD​≃2r∗​GM∗​M˙​.​
(13)
The Newtonian gravitational potential decreases by approximately GM∗​/r∗​ per unit mass from a distant outer edge to the surface, giving a potential-energy release rate GM∗​M˙/r∗​. The standard thin-disk luminosity is half of this.
The missing half remains as kinetic energy of nearly circular orbital motion: at the inner edge vK2​=GM∗​/r∗​, so the specific orbital kinetic energy is GM∗​/(2r∗​). Equivalently, circular motion has total specific mechanical energy −GM∗​/(2r∗​). Matter joining a slowly rotating star must shed this orbital motion in an accretion-disk boundary layer, producing approximately another GM∗​M˙/(2r∗​) of luminosity. A rotating star can retain some energy in spin, so equal disk and accretion-disk boundary layer luminosities assume slow stellar rotation. For a central black hole, there is no material surface, and energy can instead be carried inward.

For a slowly rotating central star, the accretion-disk boundary layer radiates a luminosity LBL​∼GM∗​M˙/(2r∗​). A layer of thickness comparable to the disk scale height has emitting area ABL​∼r∗​H times a geometric constant. Applying the Stefan–Boltzmann law to this blackbody area gives
σTBL4​∼r∗2​HGM∗​M˙​.
(14)
Comparing with σTin4​=3GM∗​M˙/(8πr∗3​) gives the boundary-layer temperature scaling
TBL​∼(Hr∗​​)1/4Tin​.​
(15)
Only the scaling is fixed: for a two-faced annulus of area 4πr∗​H, for example, TBL4​/Tin4​=r∗​/(3H). A surface belt gives a different order-one coefficient.
For a thin disk, H/r∗​≪1, so a comparable luminosity emerges from a smaller emitting area at a higher effective temperature. The boundary-layer emission is harder than the disk emission, with its Planck law peak shifted to higher frequency. It is also closer to a single-temperature component than the broad multitemperature blackbody disk spectrum, under the adopted uniform-temperature approximation. Rapid stellar rotation weakens the heating and this conclusion's temperature contrast.

Write Dt​=∂t​+u⋅∇ for the material derivative. For any specific quantity f, conservation of mass gives
∂t​(Σf)+∇⋅(Σfu)=ΣDt​f.
(16)

∂t​(21​Σu2)+∇⋅(21​Σu2u)=−u⋅∇P−Σu⋅∇Φt​.
(17)
The Coriolis acceleration does no work because u⋅(ez​×u)=0. Since the shearing-sheet tidal potential is time-independent, its advected potential-energy density obeys
∂t​(ΣΦt​)+∇⋅(ΣΦt​u)=Σu⋅∇Φt​.
(18)
For the isothermal equation of state, define the barotropic energy density U=cs2​Σln(Σ/Σref​), with fixed positive reference surface density Σref​. The continuity equation implies
∂t​U+∇⋅(Uu)=−cs2​Σ∇⋅u=−P∇⋅u.
(19)

∂t​E+∇⋅F=0,E=21​Σu2+cs2​ΣlnΣref​Σ​+ΣΦt​,F=(E+P)u.​
(20)
Choosing the density unit so that Σref​=1 reproduces the printed logarithm. Changing the reference adds a multiple of the conserved mass to E and its advective energy flux. The barotropic energy density is the mathematical energy of this fixed-isothermal sound speed closure; it is not the microscopic thermal energy of a thermally isolated gas. Maintaining an isothermal process can require heat exchange.

For a local axisymmetric Fourier mode, the Toomre stability criterion balances three contributions to the squared oscillation frequency:
ω2=κ2+cs2​k2−2πGΣ0​∣k∣.
(21)
The radial epicyclic frequency supplies rotational restoration at long wavelengths; isothermal sound speed and pressure stabilize short wavelengths; disk self-gravity destabilizes intermediate wavelengths. Minimizing over ∣k∣ gives ∣k∣=πGΣ0​/cs2​ and ωmin2​=κ2(1−Q−2). Axisymmetric gravitational instability occurs when Q<1.
In a centrally dominated Keplerian disk, κ=Ω and vertical hydrostatic equilibrium gives cs​≃HΩ. Using Ω2=GM∗​/r3 and a local disk-mass estimate MD​∼πr2Σ0​,
Q∼rH​MD​M∗​​,instability when M∗​MD​​≳rH​.​
(22)
This is the disk mass form of the Toomre criterion. An actual enclosed mass depends on the radial surface density profile and changes an order-one coefficient. In particular, a profile proportional to r−3/2 has MD​(<r)≃4πr2Σ0​ when its inner cutoff is negligible.
For the protosolar estimate, take M∗​≃M⊙​≃2×1033g. This solar mass is implicit in identifying the central star with the young Sun. The supplied constant disk aspect ratio gives
Q(r)=πΣ0​(r)r20.1M⊙​​≃π×103×10262×1032​(AUr​)−1/2≃6.4×102(AUr​)−1/2.
(23)
Using the printed approximate astronomical unit and gravitational constant, one can also obtain Ω(1AU)≃3.7×10−7s−1 and cs​≃3.7×105cms−1; they give the same Q. The gravitational constant cancels from the mass form.
At one astronomical unit the disk is very stable, with Q≃640. Formal extrapolation gives
rQ=1​≃(6.4×102)2AU≃4×105AU.​
(24)
This is far beyond the planetary region and any plausible extent of the minimum-mass solar nebula. Moreover, the extrapolated enclosed disk mass is already a substantial fraction of a solar mass, so the centrally dominated approximation becomes questionable. The formal radius is not a prediction of a real unstable outer nebula. Direct gas fragmentation by gravitational instability is unlikely to have formed Solar System planets in this model. Core accretion is the more natural route; an earlier substantially more massive or colder disk would be a different model. Even Q<1 alone does not guarantee fragmentation, because sufficiently rapid cooling is also needed.

For nonzero real wavenumber k, seek a Fourier mode of the Newtonian gravitational potential in the form Φ′=f(z)eikx. Away from the razor-thin sheet, the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity becomes f′′−k2f=0. Requiring the perturbation to decay on both sides and remain continuous gives f=Ae−∣k∣∣z∣.
Integrating the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity through z=0 fixes the derivative jump:
f′(0+)−f′(0−)=4πGΣ0​,−2∣k∣A=4πGΣ0​.
(25)
Thus the perturbing gravitational potential is
Φ′(x,z)=−∣k∣2πGΣ0​​eikx−∣k∣∣z∣.​
(26)
At the midplane this reduces to the razor-thin disk Poisson kernel. The observable real disturbance is the real part. Writing the perturbation amplitude as Σa​ instead of Σ0​ gives the same formula with Σa​; linearity makes it independent of the background density. The k=0 disturbance is a uniformly changed sheet and has potential proportional to ∣z∣, rather than a decaying nonzero-wavenumber solution.

First eliminate the density-dependent definition of the auxiliary g. The Newtonian gravitational stress tensor can be written without derivatives of its normalization:
Tij​=4πG1​(∂i​Φ∂j​Φ−21​δij​∂k​Φ∂k​Φ).
(27)
Here δij​ is the Kronecker delta, and repeated indices are summed. Differentiating gives
∂j​Tij​=4πG1​(∂j​∂i​Φ∂j​Φ+∂i​Φ∇2Φ−∂k​Φ∂i​∂k​Φ)=4πG∂i​Φ​∇2Φ=ρ∂i​Φ.
(28)
The first and third terms cancel because mixed partial derivatives commute; the final step uses the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity. Therefore the gravitational force density is a stress divergence:
−ρ∇Φ=−∇⋅T.​
(29)
The identity holds for spatially varying mass density: substituting the definition of g before differentiating prevents erroneous extra density-gradient terms.
For the gravitational stress contribution to angular-momentum transport, the anisotropic term ρgi​gj​ supplies angular momentum transport. In cylindrical components its radial–azimuthal entry is
Trϕ​=4πG∂r​Φ(r−1∂ϕ​Φ)​=ρgr​gϕ​.
(30)
In the momentum conservation equation, rTrϕ​ is the radial stress contribution to the angular-momentum flux, whose sign depends on the correlated radial and azimuthal field components. It vanishes for a perfectly axisymmetric potential but can be nonzero for spiral disturbances.
The other term is isotropic and acts as an effective negative pressure, Pg​=−ρg2/2=−∣∇Φ∣2/(8πG). Its force contribution is −∇Pg​=+∇(ρg2/2), modifying normal compression and force balance. It has no off-diagonal shear component and therefore no direct radial angular momentum transport. In an axisymmetric averaged disk it supplies no azimuthal torque. The complete symmetric Newtonian gravitational stress tensor also expresses conservation of angular momentum without an internal couple.

When the imposed magnetic field vanishes, the dynamical dispersion relation becomes
σ2[σ2+(N2+Ω2)]=0.
(38)
The nonstationary hydrodynamic branch therefore has σ2=−(N2+Ω2). The radial Solberg–Høiland instability criterion in this local model is
N2+Ω2<0.​
(39)
The radial buoyancy frequency supplies either restoration or a driving force, while the radial epicyclic frequency κ=Ω supplies rotational restoration. Zero sum is marginal, and positive sum gives stable oscillations. The neutral roots do not themselves signify exponential growth.
Because both equilibrium pressure and dimensionless specific entropy decrease outward, their radial gradients have the same sign. The leading minus sign in the expression for N2 therefore makes N2<0: the radial stratification is adverse. For smooth profiles varying over radius r, ∣∂r​P∣∼P/r and ∣∂r​S∣∼1/r, so
N2∼−ρr2P​∼−r2cs2​​∼−(rH​)2Ω2.
(40)
For a thin disk, the magnitude of this negative radial buoyancy frequency squared is much less than Ω2. Rotation stabilizes the hydrodynamic mode despite the adverse entropy gradient. This estimate assumes gradients on the global radial scale; a sharp thermal feature may be different. It addresses the ideal nondiffusive equations here. Processes such as convective overstability require additional thermal relaxation and are not ruled out by this particular criterion.

Let A=k2vA2​>0 and C=N2+Ω2. Solving the radially stratified magnetorotational dispersion relation as a quadratic in s=σ2 gives
s±​=−A−2C​±21​C2+16Ω2A​.
(41)
Both roots are real. If A+N2−3Ω2<0, their product is negative, so exactly one root is positive and gives exponential growth. Conversely, when A+N2−3Ω2≥0, the coefficient 2A+N2+Ω2≥A+4Ω2>0, so both roots are nonpositive. Thus the instability criterion for a specified nonzero wavenumber is
0<k2vA2​<3Ω2−N2.​
(42)
In a local continuum of allowed wavenumbers, some unstable mode exists exactly when N2<3Ω2. A finite disk only permits wavelengths fitting its vertical boundaries, so this existence statement also requires an allowed mode in the interval.
The growing root is s+​, whose derivative and curvature are
dAds+​​=−1+C2+16Ω2A​4Ω2​,dA2d2s+​​=−(C2+16Ω2A)3/232Ω4​<0.
(43)
Setting the derivative to zero gives the fastest-growing interior mode:
kmax2​vA2​=Ω2−16Ω2(N2+Ω2)2​.​
(44)
For a real, nonzero wavenumber, this expression requires ∣N2+Ω2∣<4Ω2, equivalently
−5Ω2<N2<3Ω2.​
(45)
Nonnegative right-hand side permits the endpoints, but there the stationary point is at k=0, outside the nonzero-wavenumber mode used above. Substituting the interior maximizing value gives
smax​=16Ω2(3Ω2−N2)2​,σmax​=4Ω3Ω2−N2​.​
(46)
This is the maximum growth rate of radially stratified magnetorotational instability, taking Ω>0. For N2=0 it reduces to the usual 3Ω/4, at k2vA2​=15Ω2/16. Negative N2 enhances growth and extends the unstable band; positive N2 suppresses growth and eventually eliminates it at N2≥3Ω2. Magnetic tension enables angular-momentum exchange between displaced parcels, weakening the rotational stabilization that protected the adverse hydrodynamic stratification.
If N2≤−5Ω2, the interior formula is no longer the physical maximum: s+​ decreases for A>0, and its supremum as k→0 is −N2−Ω2. The corresponding limiting growth rate is −N2−Ω2​, dominated by the already unstable hydrodynamic branch. At N2=−5Ω2 this joins continuously to 2Ω. For the smooth thin disk estimate N2∼−(H/r)2Ω2, the ordinary interior maximum applies and its enhancement over 3Ω/4 is only of fractional order (H/r)2.
