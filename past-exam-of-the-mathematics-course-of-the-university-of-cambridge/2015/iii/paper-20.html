A morphism of schemes is a morphism of locally ringed spaces. Thus it consists of a continuous map f:∣X∣→∣Y∣ and a homomorphism of sheaves of rings
f#:OY​⟶f∗​OX​
(1)
such that, for every x∈X, the induced map on stalks
fx#​:OY,f(x)​⟶OX,x​
(2)
is a local homomorphism: it carries the maximal ideal of the source into the maximal ideal of the target. Equivalently, the inverse image of the target maximal ideal is the source maximal ideal. The locality condition on every stalk distinguishes a morphism of schemes from a general morphism of ringed spaces.

b OurBigBook.com
The affine-target adjunction for schemes gives the natural bijection
HomSch​(X,SpecA)≃HomRing​(A,Γ(X,OX​)).​
(3)
A morphism of schemes f gives the homomorphism on global sections induced by f#, using Γ(SpecA,O)=A.
Conversely, let α:A→Γ(X,OX​) be a ring homomorphism. Choose an affine open subscheme cover Ui​=SpecBi​ of X. Restriction of global sections gives homomorphisms A→Bi​, hence morphisms of schemes fi​:Ui​→SpecA. On any affine open subscheme W⊆Ui​∩Uj​, both restrictions correspond to the same homomorphism A→Γ(W,OW​). They therefore agree on W. Such affine opens cover the overlap, so the fi​ glue uniquely to a morphism of schemes f:X→SpecA.
The two constructions are inverse: the first recovers α on each Ui​, hence on all of X, and the second recovers every restriction f∣Ui​​ of a given f. This proof needs neither affineness nor quasi-compactness of X.

Let m be the maximal ideal of the local ring A. Every open subset of SpecA containing the closed point m is the whole spectrum of a commutative ring: it contains a principal open subscheme D(a) with a∈/m, and that a is a unit, so D(a)=SpecA.
Given f:SpecA→PZn​, choose a standard affine open subscheme D+​(xi​) containing f(m). Its preimage is consequently all of SpecA. The affine-target adjunction for schemes expresses f in this chart by elements bj​∈A for j=i, the images of xj​/xi​. It is represented by homogeneous coordinates with ai​=1 and aj​=bj​.
Conversely, a tuple (a0​,…,an​) with some ai​∈A× defines a morphism of schemes into D+​(xi​) by xj​/xi​↦aj​/ai​. Choosing another unit entry gives the same morphism of schemes, since the usual projective space transition functions identify the ratios. Multiplying all entries by one unit does not change any ratio. If two such tuples define the same morphism of schemes, choose a unit entry ai​ in the first and a unit entry aj′​ in the second. In the second chart, the function xi​/xj​ pulls back to ai′​/aj′​. Because the whole map lies in D+​(xi​), this ratio is a unit, so ai′​ is a unit too. Equality in this chart gives aj​/ai​=aj′​/ai′​ for every j, hence aj′​=(ai′​/ai​)aj​.
Thus the correspondence is exactly
Hom(SpecA,PZn​)={(ai​):some ai​∈A×}/A×.​
(4)
This is the projective coordinates over a local ring description.
For a general ring, the key open-neighbourhood argument fails. Even a tuple generating the unit ideal need not have any unit entry. For example, over A=k×k the pair ((1,0),(0,1)) defines a map to P1 whose two points have images [1:0] and [0:1]. Neither coordinate is a unit, and no common unit multiple changes that fact. The map lies in no single standard chart. More generally, maps into projective space correspond to invertible sheaf quotients of An+1; the quotient need not be a free rank-one module outside the local ring case.

Compute each scheme-theoretic fibre by tensoring with the residue field of the chosen base point.
For the first map, put L=κ(p) for a point p of Speck[T] and let t be the image of T in L. Then
Xp​=SpecL[U]/(U2−t2).​
(7)
If the characteristic of a field is not two and t=0, the two factors U−t and U+t are coprime, so the Chinese remainder theorem gives L[U]/(U2−t2)≃L×L: the scheme-theoretic fibre is two distinct L-points. If t=0, its ring is L[U]/(U2), a dual number ring, so it is a nonreduced double point. In characteristic two, U2−t2=(U−t)2 at every point, giving a nonreduced double point in every scheme-theoretic fibre. This covers the generic point, where L=k(T), as well as closed points defined by irreducible polynomials.
For the arithmetic map, the generic scheme-theoretic fibre is
SpecQ[T]/(T2+1)=SpecQ(i).​
(8)
Over a closed point (p) it is SpecFp​[T]/(T2+1). At p=2 this is SpecF2​[ϵ]/(ϵ2), since T2+1=(T+1)2. For odd p, the finite field multiplicative group is cyclic, and −1 is a square exactly when p≡1(mod4). Thus
X(p)​≃⎩⎨⎧​Spec(Fp​×Fp​),SpecFp2​,SpecF2​[ϵ]/(ϵ2),​p≡1(mod4),p≡3(mod4),p=2.​​
(9)
In the second case there is one degree-two closed point over Fp​, which becomes two points after extending the residue field to an algebraic closure. The case p=2 remains nonreduced after such extension.
For SpecC→SpecZ, the unique source point maps to the generic point (0). Since all nonzero integers are invertible in C,
X(0)​=SpecC,X(p)​=∅ for every prime p.​
(10)
Indeed C⊗Z​Q=C, whereas C⊗Z​Fp​=C/pC=0. The distinction between a reduced split scheme-theoretic fibre and a nonreduced double point is essential in the first two examples.

Necessity follows from commutativity of the fibre product of schemes square: if p(z)=x and q(z)=y, then f(x)=g(y).
For sufficiency, put s=f(x)=g(y) and K=κ(s), L=κ(x), L′=κ(y). The maps on local rings induce field embeddings K↪L,L′. The canonical point maps into X and Y therefore give a morphism of schemes
Spec(L⊗K​L′)=SpecL×SpecK​SpecL′⟶X×S​Y.
(11)
The tensor product of commutative algebras L⊗K​L′ is nonzero. To see the hinted fact directly, choose a K-basis of L containing 1; tensoring that basis with L′ makes 1⊗1 nonzero. A nonzero unital commutative ring has a prime ideal, so its spectrum of a commutative ring has a point w. Its projections to the two field spectra are their unique points. The image z of w consequently projects to x and y.
The desired point exists precisely when the base images coincide. This is the point-lifting property of a scheme fibre product. It does not claim that such a point is unique: different prime ideals of the tensor product may give different points over the same pair.

d OurBigBook.com
Let f:X→Y be a surjective morphism of schemes and let Y′→Y be any morphism of schemes. Take any point y′∈Y′ and let y be its image in Y. Surjectivity supplies x∈X with f(x)=y. By the point-lifting property of a scheme fibre product proved in part (c), there is a point of X×Y​Y′ projecting to both x and y′. Thus every point of Y′ is hit by the projection.
This proves that surjectivity is preserved by base change. No restriction is imposed on the base change of a morphism of schemes. No flatness or finite-type assumption is required.

For a smooth projective curve C of geometric genus g and a divisor on an algebraic curve D, write ℓ(D)=h0(C,OC​(D)) and let KC​ be a canonical divisor. The curve form of the Riemann-Roch theorem is
ℓ(D)−ℓ(KC​−D)=degD+1−g.​
(12)
Here degKC​=2g−2 and ℓ(KC​)=g. In line bundle form,
h0(C,L)−h0(C,ωC​⊗L−1)=degL+1−g.
(13)
The usual curve convention is a smooth geometrically integral projective curve; geometric embedding tests may be checked after extending the ground field to an algebraic closure.

b OurBigBook.com
First use the canonical map to obtain the embedding. Its canonical divisor has degree six and h0(KC​)=4. Over an algebraically closed ground field, every effective degree-two divisor on an algebraic curve E satisfies h0(E)=1: a second section would give a nonconstant rational function with poles bounded by E, hence a map C→P1 of degree at most two. Degree one would make C rational, and degree two would make it hyperelliptic, both excluded here.
The Riemann-Roch theorem now gives
h0(KC​−E)=deg(KC​−E)+1−g+h0(E)=4+1−4+1=2.
(14)
Thus the complete linear system of divisors ∣KC​∣ separates every pair of points and every tangent direction, including the tests E=2p. By the length-two criterion for a very ample linear system, it is a very ample linear system. Consequently the canonical map embeds C in P3, with degree of a projective curve six. Its image is a nondegenerate projective variety, since the four canonical sections are linearly independent.
The space of homogeneous quadrics in four variables has dimension ten, while the Riemann-Roch theorem gives
h0(C,2KC​)=12+1−4=9.
(15)
Restriction therefore has a nonzero kernel, giving a quadric Q containing C. This quadric is irreducible: a reducible quadric is a union of two planes, or a double plane, and an integral curve lying in it would lie in a plane, contradicting nondegeneracy.
Similarly, homogeneous cubics form a twenty-dimensional space, whereas
h0(C,3KC​)=18+1−4=15.
(16)
There are at least five independent cubics vanishing on C. The multiples of Q by linear forms give only four dimensions, so choose a cubic F vanishing on C which is not divisible by Q.
The polynomials Q,F are a regular sequence, hence their intersection D is a projective complete intersection of pure dimension one and degree 2⋅3=6. It contains C, which already has degree six. The unmixedness of a complete intersection excludes embedded components. Equal degrees force C to be the only irreducible component and force multiplicity one at its generic point. By generic reducedness with no embedded components, this makes D reduced, so D=C as schemes. We have proved
C≃V(Q,F)⊆P3,degQ=2,degF=3.​
(17)
This is a canonical genus-four curve as a quadric-cubic intersection. The quadric may be smooth or a cone; the argument does not require it to be smooth. The same construction descends over the ground field when the curve is geometrically nonhyperelliptic, since the restriction maps and canonical embedding are defined there.

Let L=OC​(1). Its degree is five. Because the linear span of C has dimension at least three, restrictions of linear forms supply at least four independent global sections, so
h0(C,L)≥4.
(18)
If h1(C,L)=0, the Riemann-Roch theorem gives h0(L)=6−g, and therefore g≤2 immediately.
If instead L is special, the Clifford inequality for curves gives
h0(L)≤1+2degL​=27​,
(19)
so its integer dimension is at most three, a contradiction. Here is a short proof of the required Clifford inequality for curves, so the bound need not be assumed. Put r=h0(L)>0 and s=h0(ωC​⊗L−1)>0. At a smooth geometric point, choose bases of these two section spaces with strictly increasing orders of vanishing a1​<⋯<ar​ and b1​<⋯<bs​. Such bases exist because each successive order-of-vanishing quotient has dimension at most one. The products of the first basis with the first element of the second basis, followed by products of the last element of the first basis with the remaining elements of the second, have orders
a1​+b1​<⋯<ar​+b1​<ar​+b2​<⋯<ar​+bs​.
(20)
They are linearly independent global sections of ωC​, proving the product dimension bound for sections on a curve. Thus r+s−1≤h0(ωC​)=g. Combining this with Riemann-Roch theorem, r−s=degL+1−g, gives 2r≤degL+2, which is exactly the displayed Clifford inequality for curves.
The special case is impossible, and hence
g(C)≤2.​
(21)
This is the genus bound for a nonplanar degree-five curve. The geometric argument can be carried out after extension to an algebraic closure, which preserves these section dimensions.

A closed immersion i:X→Y is a morphism of schemes whose underlying map is a homeomorphism onto a closed subset and whose map of structure sheaves
OY​⟶i∗​OX​
(22)
is surjective. Equivalently, X is the closed subscheme defined by a quasi-coherent ideal sheaf of a closed subscheme on Y. The affine-local characterization is particularly useful: for every affine open subscheme V=SpecA⊆Y,
i−1(V)≃Spec(A/I)⟶SpecA​
(23)
for an ideal I⊆A, with the map induced by the quotient homomorphism.

Work over an affine open subscheme V=SpecA of Z. Since g is a closed immersion, its inverse image is Spec(A/I) for some ideal I. Since f is a closed immersion, the inverse image of this affine scheme in X is Spec((A/I)/J) for an ideal J⊆A/I. Let J be the inverse image of J in A. Over an affine open subscheme SpecA of the target, a closed immersion has the form Spec(A/I)→SpecA. Pulling it back along an affine scheme map SpecB→SpecA gives
Spec((A/I)⊗A​B)≃Spec(B/IB).
(25)
The identification follows directly by tensoring the quotient presentation; it does not require B to be flat over A. Since B→B/IB is surjective, this is again a closed immersion. These affine calculations cover every base change of a morphism of schemes.
Being a closed immersion is stable under arbitrary base change of a morphism of schemes.

A morphism of schemes f:X→Y is a separated morphism if its diagonal morphism
Δf​:X⟶X×Y​X​
(26)
is a closed immersion. The diagonal morphism is induced by the pair of identity maps on X. Thus separatedness is a condition relative to the given target Y, not a requirement that X be absolutely separated.

A line bundle L is a globally generated line bundle when the evaluation map
H0(X,L)⊗k​OX​⟶L
(29)
is surjective. Equivalently, its global sections span each fibre of the line bundle; locally at every point, some section is a generator.
Choose a finite generating family s0​,…,sn​. On the open set where si​ generates L, the ratios sj​/si​ are regular functions, giving a morphism of schemes into the standard chart D+​(xi​) of projective space. The ratios obey the usual transition rules on overlaps, so these chart maps glue to
φ:X⟶Pkn​,x⟼[s0​(x):⋯:sn​(x)].​
(30)
The tuple is computed using any local trivialization of L; changing that trivialization multiplies all entries by the same invertible function. The construction has φ∗O(1)≃L and pulls back the coordinate sections to the chosen si​.
There is a finiteness qualification for an arbitrary X: global generation alone need not provide such a finite family. It does if X is quasi-compact, since the open sets on which individual sections generate have a finite subcover. Without that hypothesis, take X=∐m≥1​Pkm​ and let L restrict to O(1) on each component. This line bundle is globally generated, but any N global sections have a common zero on a component Pm with m≥N. Thus this is a globally generated line bundle without finite generators. This is finite global generation on a quasi-compact scheme. The finite-family construction is automatic in the projective case asked next.
For projective nonsingular X, put V=⟨s0​,…,sn​⟩. The length-two criterion for a very ample linear system says that φ is a closed immersion precisely when, after extending to an algebraic closure, V separates distinct points and tangent directions. Equivalently, the evaluation
V⟶H0(E,L∣E​)
(31)
is surjective for every length-two geometric closed subscheme E⊆X. Two distinct points give point separation; a nonreduced length-two subscheme supported at one point gives separation of a direction in the Zariski tangent space. For the complete space V=H0(X,L), this says exactly that L is a very ample line bundle. For a chosen smaller V, it is the chosen linear system of divisors which must be a very ample linear system; mere global generation is insufficient.

Let U=P2∖C. The localization sequence for the divisor class group gives
Z[C]⟶Cl(P2)⟶Cl(U)⟶0.
(32)
Every prime Weil divisor of U extends by closure to one of P2, and the only removed prime Weil divisor is C. Rational functions have the same function field on the two spaces, so the kernel on divisor class groups consists exactly of multiples of [C].
The divisor class group of P2 is Z, generated by the class [H] of a line. To see the degree identification, if a plane curve D has degree e and homogeneous equation FD​, then FD​/ℓe, for a line equation ℓ, is a rational function with principal Weil divisor D−eH. Degrees of principal Weil divisors are zero, so [H] has infinite order. In particular, [C]=d[H]. The localization sequence therefore yields
Cl(P2∖C)≃Z/dZ.​
(33)
This is the divisor class group of a plane-curve complement. It includes d=1, when the group is zero, and does not require the removed curve to be nonsingular.

The hyperplane sections through P are spanned by the three linear forms x1​,x2​,x0​−x3​. Their common zero on X is precisely P, so away from P the linear system of divisors is base-point-free and defines the projection from a point on a smooth quadric
φ([x0​:x1​:x2​:x3​])=[x1​:x2​:x0​−x3​].​
(34)
In particular, the chart-ratio construction in part (a) makes this a morphism of schemes on X∖{P}, not merely a rational map there.
Write a target point as [u:v:w]. The corresponding line through P consists of points
[λ+μw:μu:μv:λ].
(35)
Substituting in the quadric equation gives
μ((u−v)λ+uwμ)=0.
(36)
Removing P means μ=0, so set μ=1. Over the residue field K of the target point, its scheme-theoretic fibre is consequently
SpecK[λ]/((u−v)λ+uw).​
(37)
If u=v, this is one reduced point, with λ=−uw/(u−v). If u=v and uw=0, it is empty: the corresponding line meets the quadric only at the removed point, with intersection multiplicity two. If u=v and uw=0, the line lies entirely on the quadric, and the scheme-theoretic fibre is AK1​, the line with P removed.
The last case occurs at exactly [0:0:1] and [1:1:0]. Their lines are respectively
L1​={x1​=x2​=0},L2​={x0​=x3​, x1​=x2​},
(38)
the two rulings of a smooth quadric surface through P. Thus the image consists of the complement of the line u=v, together with those two points. The line u=v parametrizes directions in the tangent plane x1​=x2​ at P. The calculation describes the scheme-theoretic fibres over arbitrary target points and works in every characteristic.

Two successive point blowups of a smooth algebraic surface suffice. Let X2​→X1​=A2 be the blowup of the affine plane at the origin. In its chart x=uy, with coordinates (y,u), the total-transform equation is
x2−y5=y2(u2−y3).
(39)
Removing the exceptional factor gives the strict transform
C2​: u2−y3=0.
(40)
Above the original origin it has just one point, (y,u)=(0,0), which is still singular. The other chart is y=vx, where the strict transform has equation 1−v5x3=0 and does not meet the exceptional divisor x=0. Thus there are no other points above the origin to resolve.
Blow up the remaining point to obtain X3​→X2​. In the chart u=vy, with coordinates (y,v), the total transform of C2​ is
u2−y3=y2(v2−y),
(41)
and hence its strict transform is
C3​: y=v2.​
(42)
The derivative of v2−y with respect to y is −1, so this is nonsingular, even in characteristics two or five. In the other chart y=wu, the strict transform has equation 1−w3u=0 and does not meet the exceptional divisor u=0. Therefore the only point of C3​ mapping to the original origin is the smooth point (y,v)=(0,0).
The required sequence is
X3​=Bl(0,0)​X2​⟶X2​=Bl0​A2⟶X1​=A2.​
(43)
The local parameter v there gives y=v2 and x=v5, also verifying the resolved branch directly. This is the resolution of the (2,5) cusp by two blowups. Its tangency to an exceptional divisor does not affect the requested nonsingularity of the strict transform; making the whole total transform have normal crossings is a stronger task.
