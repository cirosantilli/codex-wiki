Fix an integer radius m≥0 and put r=⌊m/2⌋. Starting with Ax​, scan the 2r layers of Kr from right to left. In each layer retain precisely those bond orthogonal projections whose supports meet the current support; multiply them onto the current operator and enlarge that support to include their bonds. Discard every other projection: it commutes through the current operator and acts as the identity on ∣ψ0​⟩, by frustration freeness.
If Lr​ denotes the ordered product of the retained projections, define
Ax(m)​=Lr​Ax​.​
(10)
Each layer expands the support by at most one lattice spacing. Therefore the local projector cone in a frustration-free chain lies within radius 2r≤m of x, and
KrAx​∣ψ0​⟩=Ax(m)​∣ψ0​⟩,∥Ax(m)​∥≤∥Ax​∥,⟨ψ0​∣Ax(m)​=⟨ψ0​∣Ax​.
(11)
The last equality holds because every retained projection also fixes the ground-state bra.
For r≥1, the stronger estimate from (i) gives
∥(P0​Ax​−Ax(m)​)∣ψ0​⟩∥≤∥Ax​∥q2r−1≤q−2∥Ax​∥qm.
(12)
For r=0, choose Ax(m)​=Ax​; the error is at most ∥Ax​∥, so the same bound holds. Hence
∥(P0​Ax​−Ax(m)​)∣ψ0​⟩∥≤q−2∥Ax​∥e−αm.​
(13)
Here α is exactly the value in (i), and the prefactor is independent of m and chain length. Counting Kr as r rather than 2r layers would give an incorrect support claim; the powers of a product of two orthogonal projections are what avoid a lost factor of two in the decay rate.

The history-subspace propagation Hamiltonian has diagonal entries 1/2 at the endpoints and 1 in the interior, and nearest-neighbour entries −1/2. The spectrum of a path propagation Hamiltonian is
λk​=1−cos(T+1πk​),k=0,…,T.​

In the history basis, M(s)=(1−s)D+sE is a Hermitian tridiagonal matrix. Its first diagonal entry is s/2, its interior entries are 1, its last entry is 1−s/2, and each off-diagonal entry is −s/2.
The Gershgorin disc theorem confines the first row's eigenvalue to [0,s], the interior rows to [1−s,1+s], and the last row to [1−s,1]. For 0≤s≤1/3, the first disc is disjoint from all the others and therefore contains exactly one eigenvalue. Since M(s) is positive semidefinite, that eigenvalue is the ground state energy. All other eigenvalues are at least 1−s, while the lowest is at most s. Hence the Gershgorin gap bound for an adiabatic history path gives
Δ(M(s))≥1−2s≥31​(0≤s≤1/3).​
(25)
This argument also covers s=0, where the first disc is the isolated point zero.

Part (a) shows that L is invariant throughout the interpolation. As the Hamiltonians are Hermitian, L⊥ is invariant as well: evolution starting in L has no leakage into other sectors. The quantum adiabatic theorem may therefore be applied using the restricted spectral gap, even if other sectors of the full Hamiltonian have different low-energy levels.
Combining (b)(iv) with the supplied bound for s>1/3 gives mins​Δ(M(s))≥c/(T+1)2 for a constant c>0. The matrix E−D has diagonal entries 1/2,0,…,0,−1/2 and off-diagonal entries −1/2. Its Gershgorin discs all lie in [−1,1], so ∥E−D∥≤1. Thus the runtime criterion for adiabatic preparation of a computational history state is satisfied by
τ=O(ϵ(T+1)6​)​
(26)
with a sufficiently large constant.
Starting from the supplied initial ground state ∣e0​⟩, the resulting state ρout​ obeys
∥ρout​−∣φ⟩⟨φ∣∥1​≤ϵ.​
(27)
Here the trace norm follows the question's convention; the conventional trace distance is half this norm. The Hamiltonian has O(T) explicitly specified terms, each acting on at most five qubits, so its description and the runtime are polynomial in circuit size. If ϵ−1 is polynomially bounded, the whole preparation has polynomial cost. The initial computational input is assumed to have been prepared, as in the premise of the quantum adiabatic theorem.

A promise problem (Lyes​,Lno​) belongs to QMA if there are polynomials p,q and a uniform family of quantum circuits Vx​ of size at most q(∣x∣), taking a p(∣x∣)-qubit witness and clean ancillas, with one output qubit interpreted as acceptance, such that
x∈Lyes​x∈Lno​​⟹∃ρ: Pr[Vx​ accepts ρ]≥32​,⟹∀ρ: Pr[Vx​ accepts ρ]≤31​.​​
(28)
No condition is imposed outside the promise. The witness may be an arbitrary density operator, and the verifier is efficient but need not know how to prepare an honest witness. Pure witnesses suffice for completeness because the acceptance probability is linear in the witness. QMA is quantum verification with polynomially many witness qubits and a constant completeness-soundness gap.

Interpret the high-success formulation as completeness at least 1−δ(n) and soundness at most δ(n), where δ(n)≤1/p(n) for a growing polynomial p. This is the standard error-reduction claim. Literally placing O around a success probability approaching one supplies no useful lower bound; it is the failure probability that must be inverse-polynomially small.
Let Mx​ be the quantum verifier acceptance operator acting on one witness register:
Mx​=(I⊗⟨0∣)Vx†​(I⊗∣1⟩⟨1∣out​)Vx​(I⊗∣0⟩),0≤Mx​≤I.
(29)
Acceptance on ρ is Tr(Mx​ρ). On a NO instance, ∥Mx​∥≤1/3; on a YES instance, an honest witness has acceptance at least 2/3.
Ask for r witness registers, with r odd, run Vx​ independently on each register with fresh ancillas, and accept a strict majority. The QMA parallel repetition with entangled witnesses acceptance operator is
Mx(r)​=∑z∈{0,1}r∣z∣>r/2​​⨂j=1r​Mxzj​​(I−Mx​)1−zj​.
(30)
Diagonalize Mx​. In the tensor-product eigenbasis, an eigenvalue of Mx(r)​ is the majority probability for independent Bernoulli random variables with parameters equal to the selected eigenvalues of Mx​. On a NO instance every parameter is at most 1/3. Coupling these variables using independent uniform random numbers shows that their upper majority tail is bounded by the tail with every parameter 1/3. This bounds the operator norm and therefore covers every supplied witness, including a witness entangled across the registers. No independence assumption about a dishonest witness is being made.
For a valid Chernoff bound, if S is a sum of r independent Bernoulli variables of parameter at most 1/3, Markov's inequality applied to 2S yields
Pr(S≥r/2)≤2−r/2(32​+31​2)r=(322​​)r=e−cr,c=21​ln89​>0.
(31)
An honest product witness on a YES instance gives independent trials with rejection probability at most 1/3, so its failure probability has the same bound. The printed concentration bound is not valid as stated: for five trials at parameter 1/3, the upper-majority probability is 17/81>e−5/3. The valid bound above suffices for a rigorous proof.
Choose the next odd integer above c−1lnp(n). Then
completeness≥1−p(n)1​,soundness≤p(n)1​.​
(32)
The witness length and circuit size increase by only O(logp(n)); the majority calculation has polynomial overhead. Conversely, inverse-polynomial failure probability is at most 1/3 for all sufficiently large input lengths. Finitely many smaller lengths can be handled by hard-wired verifiers, giving the usual constants. Hence the two definitions give the same class QMA. The same QMA error reduction argument also yields exponentially small failure with polynomially many copies.

The local Hamiltonian problem is a promise problem specified by a sum H=∑j=1M​hj​ on n qubits, where M is polynomial in n, each Hermitian term acts on at most a fixed number k of qubits, and the terms and two thresholds a<b are described with polynomially many bits. The promise gap satisfies b−a≥1/poly(n). In a convenient normalization, 0≤hj​≤I. The task is to distinguish
YES: λmin​(H)≤a,NO: λmin​(H)≥b.​
(33)
There is no required answer when the minimum energy lies between the thresholds. A formulation with Hermitian terms of polynomially bounded norm is equivalent: shift each term by a known scalar lower bound and rescale all terms by a polynomial bound to obtain positive semidefinite terms of norm at most one, while shifting and rescaling a,b in parallel. The promise gap remains inverse-polynomial.

Use an n-qubit state as the witness. Uniformly choose a term hj​ and perform the two-outcome positive operator-valued measure {I−hj​,hj​}. Regard the second outcome as an energy flag. For witness ρ, its probability is
Pr(energy flag)=M1​∑j​Tr(hj​ρ)=Tr(MH​ρ).
(34)
This is an efficient local-energy measurement verification: a unitary operator on the k relevant qubits and one ancilla qubit can implement the measurement using the isometry
∣ψ⟩∣0⟩⟼I−hj​​∣ψ⟩∣0⟩+hj​​∣ψ⟩∣1⟩.
(35)
The local dimension is constant; the term matrices and the measurement can be approximated to inverse-polynomial accuracy. Uniform choice of j and the classical threshold comparison also have polynomial overhead.
Set
py​=Ma​,pn​=Mb​,δ=pn​−py​,θ=2py​+pn​​.
(36)
In the nontrivial case 0≤a<b≤M, δ is inverse-polynomial. Cases outside this energy range are decided directly from 0≤H≤MI.
Ask for r witness registers, measure an independently sampled local term on each, and accept if the mean number of energy flags is at most θ. An honest product ground state on a YES instance has flag probability at most py​. The Hoeffding inequality gives failure at most e−rδ2/2. For completeness, the required Bernoulli concentration estimate follows by bounding the centered log moment-generating function f(λ)=lnEeλ(Y−p): f(0)=f′(0)=0 and f′′(λ) is a tilted Bernoulli variance, at most 1/4. Hence f(λ)≤λ2/8; exponential Markov's inequality optimized at λ=4u yields Pr(Y−p≥u)≤e−2ru2, and the lower-tail version follows in the same way.

In their product eigenbasis its eigenvalues are lower-tail probabilities of independent Bernoulli variables whose parameters are each at least pn​. Their lower tail is bounded by the identical-parameter tail at pn​, using the same uniform-variable coupling as in QMA parallel repetition with entangled witnesses. The total witness length rn and circuit size are polynomial. Thus

Here is a finite-dimensional form of the Kitaev geometrical lemma. Let A,B be positive operators, with nullspaces SA​,SB​ and all positive eigenvalues at least γ>0. First assume SA​∩SB​={0}. Let P,Q be the orthogonal projections onto those nullspaces, and define their smallest angle between two subspaces by
cosϑ=supu∈SA​, v∈SB​∥u∥=∥v∥=1​​∣⟨u,v⟩∣=∥PQ∥,0<ϑ≤π/2.
(39)
If either nullspace is zero, set cosϑ=0. Then
A+B≥γ(1−cosϑ)I=2γsin2(ϑ/2)I.​
(40)
To prove it, spectral decomposition gives A≥γ(I−P) and B≥γ(I−Q). Write C=P+Q and c=cosϑ. For every vector z,
⟨z,C2z⟩​=∥Pz∥2+∥Qz∥2+2Re⟨Pz,Qz⟩≤∥Pz∥2+∥Qz∥2+2c∥Pz∥∥Qz∥≤(1+c)(∥Pz∥2+∥Qz∥2)=(1+c)⟨z,Cz⟩.​
(41)
Thus C2≤(1+c)C. Since C is positive semidefinite, each of its eigenvalues lies in [0,1+c], so 2I−C≥(1−c)I. Consequently
A+B≥γ(2I−P−Q)≥γ(1−c)I,
(42)
as claimed.
If there is a common nullspace S=SA​∩SB​, restrict to S⊥ and define the angle between SA​∩S⊥ and SB​∩S⊥ there. The same proof bounds the smallest positive eigenvalue of A+B by 2γsin2(ϑ/2), while S is its zero-energy subspace. This version gives a spectral gap above a possibly degenerate ground state space.

But h12​∣000⟩=∣000⟩, whereas h12​∣010⟩=0. The global ground state therefore does not minimize g(12), proving that the resulting decomposition is not a frustration-free Hamiltonian.
There is a useful positive result if the original decomposition already is a frustration-free Hamiltonian. If aZ​=λmin​(hZ​) and hZ​∣ϕ0​⟩=aZ​∣ϕ0​⟩, then hZ​−aZ​I≥0. Averaging its unitary conjugates with nonnegative w gives g(Z)−aZ​I≥0, while g(Z)∣ϕ0​⟩=aZ​∣ϕ0​⟩. Thus filtering preserves existing frustration freeness; it does not create it for an arbitrary gapped Hamiltonian.
