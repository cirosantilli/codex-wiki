Let Z be a real random variable with finite expectation m and finite variance σ2. The Chebyshev inequality is
P(∣Z−m∣≥t)≤t2σ2​(t>0).​
(1)
The proof is the pointwise comparison
t21{∣Z−m∣≥t}​≤(Z−m)2.
(2)
Taking expectations and using the definition of variance gives
t2P(∣Z−m∣≥t)≤E(Z−m)2=σ2.
(3)
Division by the positive number t2 proves the Chebyshev inequality, including the case σ2=0. Equivalently, this is the Markov inequality applied to the nonnegative random variable (Z−m)2.

Write p=p(n) and let T be the triangle count in a binomial random graph. For every three-element set S of vertices, let IS​ be the indicator random variable that its three edges are present. Then
T=∑∣S∣=3​IS​,μ=ET=(3n​)p3.
(4)
For two distinct triples S,R, their indicator random variables are independent random variables unless they share an edge. Sharing just one vertex still leaves the sets of relevant edges disjoint. If they share an edge, their union has five edges, and therefore
Cov(IS​,IR​)=p5−p6.
(5)
There are (2n​)(2n−2​) unordered pairs of distinct triangles in a graph sharing an edge: choose that edge, and then choose their two additional vertices. The variance of a sum consequently gives the exact formula
VarT=(3n​)(p3−p6)+2(2n​)(2n−2​)(p5−p6).
(6)
For sufficiently large n, the hypothesis np→∞ ensures p>0, so μ>0. Dropping negative terms and simplifying the binomial coefficients,
μ2VarT​≤(3n​)p31​+n(n−1)(n−2)p18(n−3)​=O((np)31​+n2p1​)⟶0.
(7)
Here n2p=n(np)→∞ as well. The event T≥2μ implies ∣T−μ∣≥μ. Applying the Chebyshev inequality from part (i) gives the requested conclusion:
P(T≥2p3(3n​))≤μ2VarT​⟶0.​
(8)
Thus the triangle count is concentrated around its expectation throughout this range of the binomial random graph.

Use normalized Fourier analysis on a finite abelian group, identifying A with its indicator function a=1A​ on the cyclic group Zn​. The conventions are
Ex​h(x)=n1​∑x∈Zn​​h(x),h(r)=Ex​h(x)ω−rx,(h∗k)(x)=Ey​h(y)k(x−y).
(9)
The last operation is normalized convolution on a finite group. With these conventions the Fourier coefficients on a finite abelian group use a normalized average, while sums over frequencies use counting measure.
Expanding the normalized convolution on a finite group and putting z=x−y gives
f​(r)=Ex​Ey​a(y)a(x−y)ω−rx=(Ey​a(y)ω−ry)(Ez​a(z)ω−rz)=a(r)2.

∑r​∣a(r)∣2=Ex​∣a(x)∣2=α.

(14)

For the physical-space L2 norm use ∥h∥22​=Ex​∣h(x)∣2. By part (i) and the Parseval identity on a finite group,
∥f−g∥22​=∑r​∣1−ω−ru∣2∣a(r)∣4.
(16)
Split this sum into the large spectrum K and its complement. Because u belongs to the Bohr set in the question, for r∈K,
∣1−ω−ru∣=∣1−ωru∣≤ε.
(17)
Consequently the contribution from K is at most ε2∑r​∣a(r)∣4≤ε2α3 by part (ii). Outside K, ∣a(r)∣<θ, and ∣1−ω−ru∣≤2. Thus that contribution is at most
4θ2∑r∈/K​∣a(r)∣2≤4θ2α.
(18)
Adding the estimates proves
∥f−g∥22​≤ε2α3+4θ2α.​
(19)
The argument also covers empty A or empty K. As usual the radius and threshold are nonnegative; a negative radius makes the premise empty whenever K is nonempty. The estimate expresses Bohr-set almost periodicity of a convolution: a translation of a function by an element of the Bohr set barely changes the large Fourier coefficients on a finite abelian group, while the small ones have little total energy.

An additive form of the Plünnecke inequality is the following. If A,B are nonempty finite subsets of an abelian group and ∣A+B∣≤K∣A∣, then there is one nonempty X⊆A such that, simultaneously for every integer m≥0,
∣X+mB∣≤Km∣X∣.​
(20)
Here mB is an iterated sumset and 0B={0}. In particular, its Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality consequence is
∣kB−ℓB∣≤Kk+ℓ∣A∣(k,ℓ≥0).​
(21)
We prove both statements, so that the sumset and difference set formulations are covered.
Choose a nonempty X⊆A minimizing the ratio ∣X+B∣/∣X∣, and denote this minimum by κ. Such a minimizer exists because A is finite, and κ≤K. Every Z⊆X satisfies ∣Z+B∣≥κ∣Z∣, with the empty case also valid. We first prove the Petridis minimal-growth lemma
∣X+B+C∣≤κ∣X+C∣
(22)
for every finite nonempty C.
List C={c1​,…,ct​}. Let Ui​=X+{c1​,…,ci​} and U0​=∅. Define
Xi​={x∈X:x+ci​∈/Ui−1​},Zi​=X∖Xi​.
(23)
The new points of Ui​ are exactly Xi​+ci​, so ∣X+C∣=∑i​∣Xi​∣. Since Zi​+ci​⊆Ui−1​, the sumset Zi​+B+ci​ is already contained in Ui−1​+B. It follows that the new points introduced into Ui​+B are contained in
((X+B)∖(Zi​+B))+ci​.
(24)
As Zi​+B⊆X+B, their number is at most
∣X+B∣−∣Zi​+B∣≤κ∣X∣−κ∣Zi​∣=κ∣Xi​∣.
(25)
Summing these increments proves the Petridis minimal-growth lemma. Taking C=(m−1)B for m≥1 and iterating now gives
∣X+mB∣≤κ∣X+(m−1)B∣≤κm∣X∣≤Km∣X∣.
(26)
This proves the Plünnecke inequality with the same minimizing set X for every m.
To obtain the difference set bound, we also prove the required Ruzsa triangle inequality. For finite U,V,Z with Z=∅, choose one representation d=ud​−vd​ of each d∈U−V. The map
(U−V)×Z⟶(U−Z)×(Z−V),(d,z)⟼(ud​−z,z−vd​)
(27)
is an injection: the sum of the output coordinates recovers d, and the first coordinate then recovers z because ud​ was fixed. Thus
∣U−V∣∣Z∣≤∣U−Z∣∣Z−V∣.
(28)
Use U=kB, V=ℓB, and Z=−X. The already proved Plünnecke inequality yields
∣kB−ℓB∣≤∣X∣∣X+kB∣∣X+ℓB∣​≤κk+ℓ∣X∣≤Kk+ℓ∣A∣.
(29)
This finishes the proof of the stated Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality. The use of an abelian group is essential in commuting the translates and sumsets in the minimal-growth argument.

We derive the triangle removal lemma from the permitted Szemerédi regularity lemma, then turn a corner in an integer grid into a triangle in a graph.
The triangle removal lemma says that for every η>0 there are ρ>0 and N0​ such that a graph on N≥N0​ vertices with fewer than ρN3 triangles in a graph can be made triangle-free by deleting at most ηN2 edges. Here triangles in a graph are unordered triples of distinct vertices. We prove this consequence with the necessary quantitative dependence.
We may assume 0<η<1. Put d=η/4, choose an integer m0​≥4/η, and choose
0<ε≤min{η/8,d/4,1/4}.
(30)
Apply the Szemerédi regularity lemma to obtain an exceptional class V0​ of size at most εN and equal-sized classes V1​,…,Vk​ of size L, where m0​≤k≤M, and at most εk2 pairs are not regular pairs of vertex sets. The constant M depends only on these chosen parameters. Delete all edges incident with V0​, all edges within a class, all edges between irregular pairs, and all edges between pairs whose edge density of a bipartite graph is less than d. The respective costs are at most
εN2,2m0​N2​,εN2,2d​N2.
(31)
Their sum is at most ηN2/2, hence certainly at most ηN2.
If a triangle in a graph survives, it lies in three distinct classes whose three pairs are ε-regular pairs of vertex sets of edge density of a bipartite graph at least d in the original graph. We now check the needed regular triangle counting lemma. In the first class all but at most 2εL vertices have at least (d−ε)L neighbours in each of the other two classes; otherwise the definition of a regular pair of vertex sets would be violated. For each such vertex v, its two neighbour sets each have size at least εL, so their mutual edge density of a bipartite graph is at least d−ε. It follows that the original three classes contain at least
(1−2ε)(d−ε)3L3≥8d3​L3
(32)
triangles in a graph. The elementary lower bound holds because ε≤1/4 and ε≤d/4. Also L=(N−∣V0​∣)/k≥N/(2M). Thus a surviving triangle in a graph implies at least d3N3/(64M3) original triangles in a graph. Set ρ=d3/(128M3) and take N0​ large enough for the Szemerédi regularity lemma. Fewer than ρN3 original triangles in a graph force the cleaned graph to be triangle-free, proving the triangle removal lemma.
Now suppose 0<δ≤1 and A⊆[n]2. Use the tripartite graph encoding of a grid to construct a tripartite graph with disjoint labelled classes
X=[n],Y=[n],Z=[2n].
(33)
For each (a,b)∈A, put in the three edges joining a∈X, b∈Y, and a+b∈Z. These yield ∣A∣ canonical triangles in a graph. They are pairwise edge-disjoint triangles: an XY edge determines (a,b) directly, an XZ edge determines b=z−a, and a YZ edge determines a=z−b. Any deletion making the graph triangle-free must therefore remove at least ∣A∣ edges.
On the other hand, an arbitrary triangle in a graph with labels (x,y,z) gives three points of A:
(x,y),(x,z−x),(z−y,y).
(34)
Writing d=z−x−y, these are (x,y),(x,y+d),(x+d,y). If d=0, they form the required corner in an integer grid. If there is no such corner in an integer grid, every triangle in a graph is canonical, and the total number is precisely ∣A∣≤n2.
Apply the triangle removal lemma with η=δ/32. The constructed graph has N=4n vertices. For sufficiently large n, N≥N0​ and
n2<ρ(4n)3.
(35)
In the absence of a corner in an integer grid, the triangle removal lemma would destroy all triangles in a graph by deleting at most
η(4n)2=2δ​n2<δn2≤∣A∣
(36)
edges, contradicting the pairwise edge-disjoint triangles. Every sufficiently large grid therefore has the asserted corner in an integer grid in every subset of positive fixed density of a finite subset. For δ>1 there is no eligible subset, so the assertion is vacuous. This proves the corners theorem, including the stipulated nonzero displacement; no positivity of d was required.

Apply the polynomial method in combinatorics to the finite-field Kakeya set A. We will actually obtain the stronger estimate
∣A∣≥(np+n−1​)≥n!pn​.​
(37)
Let P be the vector space over the prime field Fp​ of multivariate polynomials in n variables of total degree of a polynomial at most p−1. By the dimension of a bounded-total-degree polynomial space, its monomial basis consists of x1a1​​⋯xnan​​ with ai​≥0 and ∑i​ai​≤p−1. Adding a slack exponent gives n+1 nonnegative exponents summing to p−1; the stars and bars count shows that
dimP=(np+n−1​).
(38)
If ∣A∣<dimP, the evaluation linear map P→FpA​ has a nonzero element in its kernel of a linear map. Thus there is a nonzero polynomial P of total degree of a polynomial d≤p−1 vanishing at every point of A. Its degree cannot be zero, since A contains a line and is nonempty. Write Pd​ for its nonzero top homogeneous polynomial part.
For every nonzero v∈Fpn​, the directional hypothesis supplies an affine line in a vector space
{av​+tv:t∈Fp​}⊆A.
(39)
Directions are one-dimensional vector subspaces; rescaling a representative does not change this line. The univariate polynomial P(av​+tv) has degree at most d<p and vanishes for all p values of t. The root bound for a polynomial makes it the zero polynomial. Its coefficient of td is exactly Pd​(v), so Pd​(v)=0 for every nonzero v. Because d>0, Pd​(0)=0 too.
To finish, we prove the relevant polynomial nonvanishing below the field size. A polynomial over Fp​ of degree at most p−1 in each variable cannot vanish on all of Fpn​ unless it is zero. Induct on n. The one-variable case is the root bound for a polynomial. For more variables, write
Q(x1​,…,xn​)=∑j=0p−1​Qj​(x1​,…,xn−1​)xnj​.
(40)
Fixing the first n−1 coordinates gives a univariate polynomial with p roots of a polynomial, so all its coefficients are zero. Hence every Qj​ vanishes on Fpn−1​, and the induction hypothesis makes every Qj​ zero. Applying this to Pd​, whose total degree of a polynomial is less than p, contradicts its choice as nonzero.
Therefore ∣A∣≥dimP. Finally,
(np+n−1​)=n!p(p+1)⋯(p+n−1)​≥n!pn​,
(41)
which proves the requested lower bound. The crucial observation in the finite-field Kakeya polynomial bound is that complete lines force the highest homogeneous polynomial part to vanish in every direction.

Use the part-preserving bipartite four-cycle density convention
t4​(G)=n41​∑x,x′∈X​∑y,y′∈Y​G(x,y)G(x,y′)G(x′,y)G(x′,y′).
(42)
Here G(x,y) is the indicator function of adjacency, and repeated labels are allowed. This is the homomorphism density convention appropriate to the four-cycle norm. We derive the needed counting and operator norm facts directly.
Let M=(G(x,y)) be the n-by-n biadjacency matrix, let 1 be the all-ones vector, let J=11T, and put H=M−δJ. The degree hypothesis says
M1=δn1,MT1=δn1,
(43)
so the centered matrix H annihilates 1 on both sides. In particular, HJ=JHT=0, and
MMT=δ2nJ+HHT.
(44)
The two terms have zero products in either order. Expanding the matrix trace directly counts four adjacency factors:
tr((MMT)2)=∑x,x′,y,y′​Mxy​Mx′y​Mx′y′​Mxy′​=n4t4​(G).
(45)
Since J2=nJ and trJ=n, we obtain
n4t4​(G)=δ4n4+tr((HHT)2).
(46)
The given upper bound therefore implies
tr((HHT)2)≤c4δ4n4.
(47)
This computation proves the centered four-cycle identity for a biregular graph, rather than assuming a four-cycle norm identity.
The matrix Q=HHT is a positive semidefinite matrix. By the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices it has nonnegative eigenvalues λj​, and
λmax2​≤∑j​λj2​=tr(Q2).
(48)
Moreover, for the Euclidean norm,
∥H∥op2​=λmax​(HHT).
(49)
Indeed ∥HTv∥22​=vTQv, whose maximum on unit vectors is λmax​; the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality characterization ∥H∥op​=sup∥u∥2​=∥v∥2​=1​∣uTHv∣ also shows that H and HT have the same operator norm. Taking fourth roots thus gives
∥H∥op​≤cδn,
(50)
where the error parameter c is nonnegative, as in the claimed bound.
Finally use the indicator vectors a=1A​, b=1B​. Their Euclidean norms are ∣A∣​ and ∣B∣​, so the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the operator norm estimate yield
∣e(A,B)−δ∣A∣∣B∣∣​=∣aTHb∣≤∥a∥2​∥H∥op​∥b∥2​≤cδn∣A∣∣B∣​=cδαβ​n2.​
(51)
This is the required discrepancy bound. It also holds when either subset is empty or δ=0. In fact, because H annihilates constants, replacing a,b by a−α1,b−β1 gives the stronger bound cδn2α(1−α)β(1−β)​. This is the spectral discrepancy bound for a biregular graph.
