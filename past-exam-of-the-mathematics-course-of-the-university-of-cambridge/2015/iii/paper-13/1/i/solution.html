The Lusternik-Schnirelmann-Borsuk theorem has the following two equivalent covering formulations. For every integer d≥0, a cover of the sphere Sd by d+1 closed sets has a member containing an antipodal pair. The same assertion holds with open sets in place of closed sets. Thus d+1 antipodal-pair-free open sets, or d+1 antipodal-pair-free closed sets, cannot cover Sd. The dimension-zero case simply says that one set covering the two-point sphere contains both points.
Here is why the two versions agree. A finite open cover of a compact metric space admits a closed shrinking that still covers: sufficiently small closed balls subordinate to the open cover can be grouped according to their containing open member. Applying the closed version to that shrinking proves the open version. Conversely, if a nonempty closed subset C of Sd avoids antipodal pairs, compactness gives positive distance between C and −C. A sufficiently small open neighbourhood of C still avoids antipodal pairs. Enlarge each member of a hypothetical closed counterexample in this way; the open version rules it out. Empty members cause no difficulty.
Another common equivalent formulation is the Borsuk-Ulam theorem: every continuous f:Sd→Rd has f(x)=f(−x) for some x, or, equivalently, every continuous odd function g:Sd→Rd has a zero. For example, if d+1 antipodal-pair-free open sets covered Sd, a subordinate partition of unity (ϕ1​,…,ϕd+1​) would give the odd function
g(x)=(ϕi​(x)−ϕi​(−x))i=1d+1​∈{y∈Rd+1:∑i​yi​=0}≅Rd.
(1)
It cannot vanish, since some ϕi​(x)>0, whereas antipodal-pair-freeness forces ϕi​(−x)=0. In the other direction, if an odd function g:Sd→Rd never vanishes, choose d+1 vectors ui​ forming a regular simplex centred at the origin in Rd. For d≥1, the open sets {x:ui​⋅g(x)>0} cover Sd and none contains an antipodal pair, contradicting the covering theorem.
