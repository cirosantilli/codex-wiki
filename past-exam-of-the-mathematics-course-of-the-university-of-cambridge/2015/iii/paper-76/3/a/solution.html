For noisy data, exact equality Ax=y may be impossible: the data can have a component outside the operator range. A least-squares solution of a linear inverse problem instead minimizes
J(x)=21​∥Ax−y∥Y2​.
(37)
For any variation h∈X, the adjoint operator gives
DJ(x)[h]=Re⟨Ax−y,Ah⟩Y​=Re⟨A∗(Ax−y),h⟩X​.
(38)
Thus statistical homogeneity in every direction is the normal equation for a linear inverse problem, A∗Ax=A∗y. It is also sufficient for a global minimum: when it holds,
J(x+h)−J(x)=21​∥Ah∥Y2​≥0.​
(39)
The residual is orthogonal to the closure of the range, since it lies in kerA∗. A normal-equation solution therefore fits the component of the data representable by the forward map, while leaving the perpendicular component unfitted. If there is more than one such solution, select the minimum-norm least-squares solution, which lies in (kerA)⊥.
These facts explain the formulation, but do not make the inverse problem well posed. A compact operator of infinite rank typically has nonclosed operator range, so even the least-squares infimum need not be attained. A least-squares solution exists exactly when the orthogonal projection of y onto RanA belongs to RanA. In a singular system of a compact operator, this becomes the Picard criterion. Also, the nonzero eigenvalues of A∗A are the squares of the singular values of A: solving the operator normal equation directly can worsen numerical conditioning. The operator normal equation expresses least squares; it does not itself regularize the inverse problem. The subsequent finite Landweber iteration and its stopping rule provide the inverse-problem regularization.
