Choose local coordinates y1,…,yD−1 on the hypersurface Σ, and let Φs​ be the local flow of the vector field X. The map F(s,y)=Φs​(p(y)) follows the integral curves of a vector field starting at p(y)∈Σ. At s=0, its differential maps the s direction to X and the remaining directions to a basis of Tp​Σ. Transversality makes these vectors linearly independent, so the inverse function theorem makes F a local manifold chart.
Set x0=s and transport the yi unchanged along each integral curve of a vector field. Then the adapted coordinates satisfy
X=∂x0∂​,X(xi)=0.​
(1)
The flow-box theorem gives this construction locally. The parameter is the flow parameter, and need not be arc length; a global manifold chart would require additional hypotheses.

In a stationary spacetime chart, take V=∂0​. Its squared length is g00​<0, so V is a timelike vector. Since its components are constant, the Lie derivative of a tensor field obeys (LV​g)μν​=∂0​gμν​=0. For the Levi-Civita connection,
(LV​g)μν​=∇μ​Vν​+∇ν​Vμ​,
(2)
so V satisfies the Killing equation.
Conversely, a timelike Killing vector field is nowhere zero. Apply the flow-box theorem to choose coordinates with V=∂0​. The Killing equation then gives ∂0​gμν​=0, and g00​=g(V,V)<0. Thus local stationarity is equivalent to the existence of a timelike Killing vector field.

Use the standard meaning of static spacetime: a stationary spacetime chart whose time-space components also vanish. The printed static definition needs to inherit the timelike requirement g00​<0; without it, the requested timelike conclusion does not follow.
The preceding argument shows that V=∂0​ is a timelike Killing vector field. Its metric-dual one-form is v=Vμ​dxμ=g00​dx0. Its exterior derivative satisfies
v∧dv=g00​dx0∧(dg00​∧dx0)=0.
(3)
For a torsion-free connection, antisymmetrizing the covariant derivative of a one-form gives its exterior derivative. Consequently the Killing vector has hypersurface orthogonality:
V[α​∇μ​Vν]​=0.​
(4)
Geometrically, the hypersurfaces x0=constant are orthogonal to V.

Write F=Vρ​Vρ<0 and Aμν​=∇μ​Vν​. The Killing equation makes Aμν​ antisymmetric. Expanding hypersurface orthogonality and contracting with Vα gives
Vα​Aμν​+Vμ​Aνα​+Vν​Aαμ​=0,FAμν​+21​Vμ​∂ν​F−21​Vν​∂μ​F=0.
(5)
Here metric compatibility gives ∂μ​F=2VρAμρ​. Therefore
2FAμν​=Vν​∂μ​F−Vμ​∂ν​F,
(6)
and the normalized Killing one-form satisfies
∇μ​(F−1Vν​)−∇ν​(F−1Vμ​)=F2Aμν​​−F2Vν​∂μ​F−Vμ​∂ν​F​=0.
(7)
Thus the required exponent is n=−2​. The notation ∣V∣2 in this paper denotes the negative scalar F, rather than a positive squared norm; the answer uses only its real reciprocal. More generally, for a multiplier h(F) the antisymmetric derivative is (h/F+h′)(Vν​∂μ​F−Vμ​∂ν​F), so h=C/F is the universally valid choice. If F is constant, other exponents can work in that particular metric.

The normalized Killing one-form η=F−1Vμ​dxμ is a closed differential form. The Poincaré lemma gives a local scalar potential ϕ with η=dϕ, hence Vμ​=F∂μ​ϕ. Choose the adapted chart of part (i), so V=∂0​. Then
∂0​ϕ=η(V)=1,ϕ=x0+f(x1,x2,x3),∂i​f=g00​g0i​​.
(8)
The closed differential form condition supplies the integrability of these spatial derivatives. For a fixed g and V, f is determined locally up to an additive constant; its derivatives cannot be chosen freely despite the wording in the paper.
Use xˉ0=ϕ, xˉi=xi. The Jacobian determinant is one, so this is a local coordinate change. Holding xˉi fixed gives V=∂0ˉ​, while its metric-dual one-form is v=Fdxˉ0. Thus gˉ​00​=F<0 and gˉ​0i​=0. The Killing equation still holds, so ∂0ˉ​gˉ​μν​=0. The metric is locally static:
gˉ​0i​=0,∂0ˉ​gˉ​μν​=0.​
(9)
A closed differential form need not be globally exact; neither a global potential nor a global static chart follows without additional topological assumptions.

The contracted Bianchi identity gives ∇aRab​=21​∇b​R. Combining the proposed equation with stress-energy conservation therefore imposes ∇b​R=0. Taking its trace gives R=κT, with T=gabTab​, so for nonzero κ the matter trace must also be constant. Ordinary matter need not satisfy this extra condition. For example, if asymptotic flatness forces the constant scalar curvature to vanish, the proposal would require T=0 everywhere.
The problem is an unjustified restriction on the matter trace, rather than the absence of every possible solution. The divergence-free Einstein tensor avoids it:
Gab​=Rab​−21​Rgab​,∇aGab​=0.​
(10)
The Einstein field equations Gab​+Λgab​=κTab​, with constant cosmological constant Λ, are consequently compatible with stress-energy conservation.

Contract the Christoffel symbol formula for the Levi-Civita connection. The first and third derivative terms cancel after relabelling their summed indices:
Γρρμ​=21​gρσ∂μ​gρσ​.
(11)
The Jacobi determinant derivative formula gives ∂μ​g=ggρσ∂μ​gρσ​. In a Lorentzian chart g<0, and hence ∂μ​−g​=21​−g​gρσ∂μ​gρσ​. Thus the trace is the logarithmic derivative of the volume density:
Γρρμ​=−g​1​∂μ​−g​.​
(12)
In particular, the covariant divergence formula for a vector field is ∇μ​Aμ=(−g)−1/2∂μ​(−g​Aμ).

Vary the inverse metric tensor while holding the matter fields fixed. Differentiating gaμ​gμb=δa​b gives δgab​=−gaμ​gbν​δgμν. The Jacobi determinant derivative formula consequently implies the inverse metric volume variation
δ−g​=−21​−g​gαβ​δgαβ.
(13)
Since the matter Lagrangian in this question contains no derivatives of the metric tensor, its metric variation is algebraic. The product rule gives
∂gαβ∂(−g​L)​=−g​(∂gαβ∂L​−21​gαβ​L).
(14)
Multiplying by −2/−g​ proves the equivalence of the stress-energy definitions:
Tαβ​=−2∂gαβ∂L​+Lgαβ​.​
(15)
The variations of the symmetric metric tensor are understood symmetrically. If the matter action contained metric derivatives, the definition would instead require a functional derivative and the corresponding integration by parts.

For the minimally coupled scalar field, keep the metric tensor fixed and vary ϕ. A scalar's first covariant derivative equals its ordinary derivative, so
δS=∫M​−g​[−∇μϕ∇μ​δϕ−V′(ϕ)δϕ]d4x.
(16)
Apply integration by parts for tensor fields. The boundary contribution is proportional to δϕ and vanishes under the given boundary condition, leaving
δS=∫M​−g​[□ϕ−V′(ϕ)]δϕd4x.
(17)
The interior variation is arbitrary. Thus the scalar equation of motion is
□ϕ−V′(ϕ)=0,□ϕ=−g​1​∂μ​(−g​gμν∂ν​ϕ).​
(18)
Here the d'Alembert operator contains the Levi-Civita connection in the second covariant derivative, even though the first derivative of the scalar field is ordinary.

For each fixed coordinate function xα, the expression □xα is a scalar: α labels four chosen functions and is not a free tensor index. The equation is therefore coordinate invariant when those functions are held fixed. However, the demand that the currently chosen coordinates themselves be harmonic coordinates is a chart condition, not a four-vector equation invariant under arbitrary changes of coordinate functions.
Indeed, for new functions xˉα=fα(x) the d'Alembert operator gives
□xˉα=fα,β​□xβ+gβγfα,βγ​.
(21)
The second term prevents a vector transformation law. An affine redefinition preserves harmonic coordinates; an arbitrary nonlinear one does not. For example, a Cartesian spatial coordinate x in Minkowski spacetime has □x=0, whereas □(x2)=2, and x2 is a valid new coordinate on x>0. The equations are scalar equations for specified functions, while harmonic gauge is chart dependent.

Contract the printed Riemann curvature tensor using Rab​=Rμaμb​. Only the derivative terms can contain second derivatives of the metric tensor:
Rab​=∂μ​Γμab​−∂b​Γμaμ​+Γτab​Γμτμ​−Γτaμ​Γμτb​.
(25)
Substituting the Christoffel symbol formula, its second derivative part is
21​gμν(∂μ​∂a​gνb​+∂μ​∂b​gνa​−∂μ​∂ν​gab​−∂a​∂b​gμν​).
(26)
Set Hb​=gbλ​Hλ=gμν∂μ​gνb​−21​gμν∂b​gμν​. The first, second and fourth terms are the second derivative terms of 21​(∂a​Hb​+∂b​Ha​). Derivatives of the inverse metric tensor produce only products of first derivatives. Thus
Rab​=−21​gμν∂μ​∂ν​gab​+21​(∂a​Hb​+∂b​Ha​)+Qab​(g,∂g),
(27)
where Q contains at most first metric derivatives. In harmonic coordinates, Hλ vanishes throughout the chart, so Hb​ and its derivatives vanish. The vacuum equations have the wave principal part
Rab​=−21​gμν∂μ​∂ν​gab​+Qab​(g,∂g)=0.​
(28)
These are quasilinear partial differential equations with the principal part of a wave equation, because gμν has Lorentzian signature. This is the harmonic reduction of the vacuum Einstein equations.

a OurBigBook.com
A spacetime is a smooth four-dimensional manifold equipped with a smooth Lorentzian metric, conventionally of metric signature (−,+,+,+), and a choice of time orientation. The usual manifold assumptions include the Hausdorff space and second-countable space conditions. A physical model also specifies matter fields and requires the Einstein field equations and matter equations.
Diffeomorphism invariance of general relativity means that relabelling events by a smooth invertible map, while transforming the metric tensor and every matter field together, preserves the form of the equations. Passively, a coordinate change gives new components for the same geometric fields. Actively, pulling all fields back by a diffeomorphism gives another representative of the same physical geometry, subject to any prescribed boundary conditions or boundary symmetries.
The geometry and matter together carry the physical content; coordinate labels do not. A diffeomorphism need not be an isometry of a fixed metric, and unrelated metrics are not automatically physically equivalent.

Use the connection convention ∇α​Yρ=∂α​Yρ+Γρμα​Yμ, with the derivative index last. This is consistent with the printed curvature formula and the final formula in part (iv). The paper's Tμν​ρ=Γρμν​−Γρνμ​ is then the negative of the geometric torsion tensor defined by T(X,Y)=∇X​Y−∇Y​X−[X,Y]. Keep the paper's component convention throughout this question.
Set Aμνρ​=gρλ​(Γλμν​−Sλμν​). The difference of affine connections is a tensor. Since the Levi-Civita connection S is symmetric and metric compatible, expanding the nonmetricity tensor gives
Tμνρ​=Aμνρ​−Aνμρ​,Nμνρ​=−Aμρν​−Aνρμ​.
(34)
Here Tμνρ​=gρλ​Tμν​λ is antisymmetric in its first two slots, while Nμνρ​ is symmetric in its first two slots. Cyclically permuting these identities and eliminating the other components gives
Aμνρ​=21​(Tμνρ​+Tνρμ​−Tρμν​+Nμνρ​−Nνρμ​−Nρμν​).
(35)
Raise the last slot to obtain the connection decomposition
Γρμν​=Sρμν​−Kμν​ρ+Wμν​ρ,​
(36)
where
Kμν​ρ=−21​(Tμν​ρ+Tν​ρμ​−Tρμν​),Wμν​ρ=21​(Nμν​ρ−Nν​ρμ​−Nρμν​).
(37)
The PDF is missing the factor 1/2 in its definition of K. With the printed K, the decomposition would instead contain −K/2. In these formulas Tρμν​ raises the first slot of the fully lowered tensor; it must not be confused with Tμν​ρ, which raises the last slot.

Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
The Levi-Civita connection is the unique affine connection that has both vanishing torsion tensor and metric compatibility. The affine connection decomposition shows exactly how an arbitrary affine connection departs from it: the contorsion tensor contributes the metric-compatible torsion correction, and the disformation tensor contributes the nonmetricity correction. The torsion and nonmetricity tensor, together with the metric tensor, determine the difference uniquely.
In the paper's convention the actual contorsion correction is −K, with the corrected half factor from part (i), and the disformation correction is W. Both corrections are tensors, although the separate connection coefficients Γ and S are not tensors. If both torsion and nonmetricity vanish, then Γ=S​. Vanishing nonmetricity alone permits torsion, and vanishing torsion alone permits nonmetricity.

Let γX​(s) be the affinely parametrized geodesic of the affine connection with γX​(0)=p and γ˙​X​(0)=X. For fixed t, the curve η(u)=γX​(tu) has initial velocity tX. The geodesic equation is homogeneous of degree two in the velocity, so its left-hand side for η is t2 times the left-hand side for γX​, and is zero. Uniqueness of the geodesic initial value problem gives η=γtX​.
By the definition of the affine exponential map, rescaling the initial velocity rescales affine parameter:
expp​(tX)=γtX​(1)=γX​(t).​
(38)
At t=0 both sides equal p. The statement is made where the geodesics exist; without geodesic completeness, the affine exponential map is defined only on a suitable neighbourhood of zero in Tp​M, not necessarily all of Tp​M. An affine parameter distance need not be a metric length.

Choose a basis of Tp​M and use the affine exponential map to define affine normal coordinates. Its differential at zero is the identity, so the inverse function theorem gives a chart near p. By part (iii), radial geodesics have coordinates xμ(s)=sXμ. Their geodesic equations at p imply Γλμν​(p)XμXν=0 for every X. The polarization identity therefore gives Γλ(μν)​(p)=0.
The disformation tensor Wμν​λ is symmetric in μ,ν, whereas Tμν​λ is antisymmetric. Also Tν​λμ​=−Tλνμ​. Taking the symmetric part of the corrected affine connection decomposition consequently gives the normal-coordinate identity
Sλμν​+Wμν​λ−21​Tλμν​−21​Tλνμ​=0at p.​
(39)
Thus the final identity in the PDF is correct after the half-factor repair in part (i). In general Γλμν​(p)=21​Tμν​λ(p) in the paper's convention, rather than zero. Neither S nor the first derivatives of the metric tensor need vanish in these affine normal coordinates. With the Levi-Civita connection, both torsion and nonmetricity vanish and the usual vanishing of the Christoffel symbols is recovered.
