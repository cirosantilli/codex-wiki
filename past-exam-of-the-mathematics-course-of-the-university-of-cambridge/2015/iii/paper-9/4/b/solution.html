We prove compactness of zero-boundary Poisson solutions. Here the printed ∂Ω must mean ∂B1​; otherwise the boundary condition refers to an unspecified set. Use this intended boundary condition throughout. Let f=Δu. A maximum-principle barrier gives a uniform bound on u: with ψ(x)=(1−∣x∣2)/(2n), we have Δψ=−1 and ψ=0 on ∂B1​. Since ∣f∣≤1,
Δ(u−ψ)=f+1≥0,Δ(−u−ψ)=−f+1≥0.
(31)
The weak maximum principle gives −ψ≤u≤ψ, so ∥u∥∞​≤1/(2n).
Standard elliptic regularity on the smooth ball first gives u∈C2,μ(B1​​). The global Schauder estimate then gives
∥u∥C2,μ(B1​​)​≤C(∥u∥C0(B1​​)​+∥f∥C0,μ(B1​​)​)≤C(1+2n1​).
(32)
Thus the functions and all their derivatives through order two are uniformly bounded and equicontinuous on the closed ball. The Arzelà-Ascoli theorem gives a subsequence converging uniformly along with all these derivatives. Integration along line segments identifies the limiting derivatives, so the convergence is in C2(B1​​).
It remains to keep the limit in the set, rather than merely proving relative compactness. If uj​→u in C2, then the zero boundary values pass to the limit and fj​=Δuj​→f=Δu uniformly. For each pair of distinct points,
∣x−y∣μ∣f(x)−f(y)∣​=limj​∣x−y∣μ∣fj​(x)−fj​(y)∣​.
(33)
Taking suprema gives [f]μ​≤liminfj​[fj​]μ​, while ∥fj​∥∞​→∥f∥∞​. Hence ∥f∥C0,μ​≤1. The limit belongs to the set, proving sequential compactness in C2(B1​​). The fixed positive Hölder exponent supplies the compactness; a bound on the supremum norm of Δu alone would not supply this argument.
