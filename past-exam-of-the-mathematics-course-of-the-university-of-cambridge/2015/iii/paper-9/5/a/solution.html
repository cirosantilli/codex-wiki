They are continuous for 0≤y≤1 and bounded by 2∥u∥∞​. On compact subintervals of 0<y<1, the equation is uniformly elliptic with smooth coefficients. Local elliptic regularity at the flat vertical sides with zero boundary values permits differentiation and integration by parts there. Thus
y2bn′′​(y)=−π2​∫0π​uxx​(x,y)sin(nx)dx=n2bn​(y).

For completeness, this identity also follows in the distributional sense without assuming boundary derivatives initially. Integrate on (ε,π−ε) against sin(nx) and a test function of y with support away from zero and one. The interior gradient estimate near a zero Dirichlet side gives ∣ux​∣≤Cε−1sup∣u∣ on a nearby ball of radius comparable to ε; continuity and the zero side value make that supremum o(1) uniformly over the support in y. Therefore the boundary products ux​sin(nx) and ucos(nx) tend to zero. Move the y derivatives to the test function before taking ε↓0. This proves the ordinary differential equation, which makes each bn​ smooth in 0<y<1 and yields the same classical identity.
The Cauchy-Euler differential equation has indicial roots
pn±​=21±1+4n2​​,bn​(y)=An​ypn+​+Bn​ypn−​.

For n≥1 the negative root is strictly negative. Boundedness as y↓0 forces Bn​=0. Continuity at y=1 identifies

Because pn+​>n, the series ∑n≥1​An​ypn+​sin(nx) converges absolutely and uniformly for 0≤y≤ρ<1. On compact subsets with y>0, differentiated series converge as well. At each fixed 0<y<1, its Fourier coefficients equal those of u(⋅,y); completeness of the Fourier sine basis in L2(0,π) and continuity in x make the two functions equal. Therefore
u(x,y)=n=1∑∞​An​y(1+1+4n2​)/2sin(nx),(x,y)∈R.​

This is also the requested sum over integers: take all coefficients with n≤0 to be zero. Negative indices give redundant Fourier modes, and the n=0 sine vanishes. No pointwise convergence of the ordinary Fourier series on the top boundary is needed.
An important consequence is a forced zero trace at a quadratically degenerate boundary. Indeed the absolute sum is bounded by 2∥u∥∞​∑n≥1​yn=2∥u∥∞​y/(1−y) for y<1, which tends to zero as y↓0, uniformly in x. Thus u(x,0)=0 on the entire lower side. It follows also from bn​(0)=0 for every sine coefficient and completeness.
