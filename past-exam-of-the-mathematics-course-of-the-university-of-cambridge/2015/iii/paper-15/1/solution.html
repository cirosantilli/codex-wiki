The degree and its local signs. Orient Sn and let [Sn] generate its top reduced homology Hn​(Sn;Z)≅Z. The mapping degree is the integer characterized by
f∗​[Sn]=(degf)[Sn].​
(1)
For n≥1 this is the usual top homology definition using the fundamental class; using reduced homology also covers S0.
Suppose f is smooth and p is a regular value. Each x∈f−1(p) has invertible tangent map Dfx​, so the inverse function theorem makes f−1(p) discrete. It is also closed in the compact sphere, hence finite. Choose disjoint small neighborhoods of these points on which f is a local diffeomorphism. The Excision theorem identifies the source local homology with the direct sum of one copy of Z for each inverse image. The induced local map is multiplication by +1 or −1 according as Dfx​ preserves or reverses orientation. The map from the global fundamental class to these local orientation classes therefore proves the degree as a sum of local degrees formula:
degf=x∈f−1(p)∑​ϵx​,ϵx​=sgndetDfx​.​
(2)
Here the determinant is computed in positively oriented tangent bases. Thus the mapping degree counts inverse images with signs, rather than just their cardinality.
The quotient map. Put m=n+1 and write f=F∣Sm−1​. Give Dm its standard orientation and its boundary the induced orientation. The connecting homomorphism
∂:Hm​(Dm,Sm−1;Z)⟶Hm−1​(Sm−1;Z)
(3)
is an isomorphism: the disk has zero positive reduced homology. It sends the relative fundamental class [Dm,Sm−1] to [Sm−1]. If the map on relative homology induced by F multiplies this class by d, naturality gives
f∗​[Sm−1]=f∗​∂[Dm,Sm−1]=∂F∗​[Dm,Sm−1]=d[Sm−1].
(4)
Thus d=degf. Collapsing the boundary gives a sphere Dm/Sm−1≅Sm, and the quotient map identifies its top reduced homology with the top relative homology of the disk pair. Give this quotient sphere the orientation determined by that identification. The relation qF=Fq now shows
degF=deg(F∣Sn​).​
(5)
This is the quotient-sphere degree identity.
The graph intersection. Orient Dm×Dm by the product orientation, orient N=Dm×{0} by its first factor, and orient the graph of a function Γ by x↦(x,F(x)). An intersection is precisely a zero of F, and no such zero lies on the boundary because F(Sm−1)⊂Sm−1. At an intersection, transverse intersection means that DFx​ is surjective, hence invertible. The zeros are consequently isolated and finite.
For the smooth intersection number use the ordered tangent spaces Tx​N first and T(x,F(x))​Γ second. Relative to the product basis their concatenated basis has matrix
(I0​IDFx​​).
(6)
Its determinant is detDFx​, so the intersection sign is ϵx​=sgndetDFx​. By the same local Excision theorem argument, now in relative homology at the interior point 0, the sum of these signs equals the multiplier of F∗​ on Hm​(Dm,Sm−1;Z). Therefore the graph intersection formula for mapping degree is
deg(F∣Sn​)=N⋅Γ=x:F(x)=0∑​sgndetDFx​.​
(7)
If the tangent spaces are ordered Γ first and N second, every sign changes by (−1)m; the order above specifies the appropriate convention.
The one-dimensional model F(x)=2x3−x on [−1,1] illustrates the graph intersection formula for mapping degree: its three zeros have signs +1,−1,+1, and its endpoint map has mapping degree 1 on reduced homology.
