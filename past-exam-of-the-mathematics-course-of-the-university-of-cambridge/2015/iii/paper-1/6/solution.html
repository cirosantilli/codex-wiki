For a∈R, write ma​ for multiplication by a in Endk​(R). The algebraic differential operator ring is defined by the filtration
Dk−1​(R)=0,Dkj​(R)={θ∈Endk​(R):[θ,ma​]∈Dkj−1​(R) for every a∈R},Dk​(R)=⋃j≥0​Dkj​(R).
(41)
The order of an algebraic differential operator is the least j for which θ∈Dkj​(R); the zero operator may be assigned order −∞. In degree zero, commuting with all multiplication maps means θ(a)=aθ(1), so Dk0​(R)={ma​:a∈R}≅R. The commutator identity [UV,ma​]=U[V,ma​]+[U,ma​]V proves by induction that Dkr​Dks​⊆Dkr+s​, so the union is a ring under addition and composition.
The derivations of an algebra form
Derk​(R)={δ:R→R linear over k:δ(ab)=aδ(b)+bδ(a)}.
(42)
Every such derivation has δ(1)=0 and [δ,ma​]=mδ(a)​, so has differential order at most one. Conversely, if θ∈Dk1​(R), set c=θ(1) and δ=θ−mc​. Then δ(1)=0 and [δ,ma​] is a multiplication map. Evaluating at 1 shows it is mδ(a)​; evaluating at b gives the Leibniz rule. Hence the first-order algebraic differential operator decomposition is
Dk1​(R)=R+Derk​(R).​
(43)
It is a direct sum as k-vector spaces: a multiplication map that is a derivation vanishes at 1, and is zero.
The complex Weyl algebra has generators x1​,…,xn​,∂1​,…,∂n​ and relations
An​=C⟨xi​,∂i​⟩/([xi​,xj​],[∂i​,∂j​],[∂i​,xj​]−δij​).​
(44)
Take n≥1. If a nonzero unital module M had finite dimension of a vector space over C m, its representing matrices would satisfy [∂1​,x1​]=Im​. Taking the matrix trace gives 0=m, impossible in characteristic zero. The Weyl algebra has no nonzero finite-dimensional modules. For n=0 the algebra is C and this assertion has the obvious exception.
For a nonzero finitely generated Weyl algebra module, use the Bernstein filtration
Fj​An​=spanC​{xa∂b:∣a∣+∣b∣≤j}.
(45)
The ordered monomials form a vector space basis, so
dimC​Fj​An​=(2nj+2n​),grF​An​≅C[X1​,…,Xn​,Ξ1​,…,Ξn​].
(46)
To justify the ordered basis, the relations move all ∂i​ to the right and give spanning. Independence follows in the action on C[x1​,…,xn​]: apply a putative zero operator ∑b​pb​(x)∂b coefficientwise to the formal exponential et⋅x, obtaining ∑b​pb​(x)tb=0. Thus every coefficient vanishes.
Choose a finite-dimensional nonzero generating subspace M0​⊆M and set Mj​=Fj​An​M0​. The associated graded module is finitely generated over the polynomial associated graded ring, so the Hilbert-Serre theorem implies dimC​Mj​ is eventually a polynomial. Define
d(M)=deg(the eventual polynomial dimC​Mj​)=GKdimAn​​M.​
(47)
This Bernstein growth dimension of a Weyl algebra module equals the Gelfand–Kirillov dimension of a module, not its ordinary vector-space dimension. If a different finite generating subspace is used, each lies in some fixed filtration step of the other. The corresponding Mj​ are sandwiched between shifted filtrations, so the degree is unchanged.
We prove Bernstein inequality for Weyl algebra modules using an injectivity estimate. The faithful finite-step action of a Weyl algebra claim is that the map
Fj​An​⟶HomC​(Mj​,M2j​),a⟼(v↦av)
(48)
is injective for every j. Induct on j. For j=0, a scalar annihilating M0​=0 is zero. Suppose a∈Fj​An​ annihilates Mj​. For any generator z=xi​ or ∂i​, the commutator [a,z] belongs to Fj−1​An​ and annihilates Mj−1​: both a(zv) and z(av) vanish. The induction hypothesis gives [a,z]=0 for every generator. In the ordered basis,
[a,xi​]=∑a,b​bi​ca,b​xa∂b−ei​,[a,∂i​]=−∑a,b​ai​ca,b​xa−ei​∂b.
(49)
In characteristic zero these identities force a to be a scalar. Since it annihilates M0​, it is zero. This proves injectivity.
Consequently
(2nj+2n​)≤(dimC​Mj​)(dimC​M2j​).
(50)
The left side grows as a positive constant times j2n; the right side has growth degree 2d(M). Comparing powers proves
d(M)≥n.​
(51)
The polynomial module C[x1​,…,xn​], with the usual multiplication and differentiation actions, has d(M)=n, showing the bound is sharp.
