The key is a Laurent normalization by a monomial change of variables. We prove the slightly stronger assertion that every nonzero quotient of a Laurent polynomial ring in r variables is finite as a module over an embedded Laurent polynomial ring in some number m≤r of variables.
Induct on r. If the defining ideal is zero, the ring itself works. Otherwise choose a nonzero Laurent polynomial
f=∑a∈F​ca​Xa
(19)
in that ideal, with nonzero coefficients. For a sufficiently large positive integer N, the weights
Na1​+N2a2​+⋯+Nr−1ar−1​+ar​
(20)
are distinct for all a∈F. Indeed, each difference gives a nonzero polynomial in N, which has only finitely many integer roots. Make the invertible monomial substitution
Xj​=Yj​YrNj​ (j<r),Xr​=Yr​.
(21)
Its inverse is Yj​=Xj​Xr−Nj​, so it is an automorphism of the Laurent polynomial ring. When r=1 there is no substitution and distinct exponents already give distinct weights.
As a Laurent polynomial in Yr​, the transformed relation has unique lowest and highest exponents. Their coefficients are nonzero scalar multiples of monomials in Y1​,…,Yr−1​, hence units in
B0​=k[Y1±1​,…,Yr−1±1​].
(22)
Multiplying by a power of Yr​ and the inverse leading coefficient gives
Yrd​+bd−1​Yrd−1​+⋯+b0​=0,bj​∈B0​,b0​∈B0×​.
(23)
Here d>0: a single-term Laurent polynomial would be a unit and the quotient would be zero. Reversing the equation and dividing by b0​Yrd​ gives a monic equation for Yr−1​. Thus both Yr​ and Yr−1​ are integral over the image B of B0​ in T. In fact b0​ being a unit also expresses Yr−1​ as a polynomial in Yr​, so T is generated as a B-module by 1,Yr​,…,Yrd−1​.
The ring B is itself a nonzero quotient of an (r−1)-variable Laurent polynomial ring. By induction, it is finite over an embedded Laurent polynomial ring in m≤r−1 variables. Finiteness of modules is transitive, so the same is true of T; a finite ring extension is an integral extension, by the determinant trick applied to multiplication by an element.
Applying this to r=3 yields an embedded subring
k[Y1±1​,…,Ym±1​]⊆T,T integral over it.​
(24)
The base case m=0 means the subring k; finiteness over it would make T a finite-dimensional vector space. The hypothesis excludes this, so 1≤m≤3, as required. No infinitude assumption on k is used.
