Write z−=max(−z,0). For a<b, let UN​[a,b] be the upcrossing count obtained by successively buying at an observation at or below a and selling at an observation at or above b, with the first purchase allowed at time zero. The Doob upcrossings lemma for an integrable discrete-time martingale is
(b−a)EUN​[a,b]≤E(XN​−a)−−E(X0​−a)−≤E(XN​−a)−.​
(1)
The weaker last bound is also the usual Doob upcrossing inequality.
To prove it, hold one unit between each purchase and sale, and zero units otherwise. If Hk​ records the holding over (k,k+1], the decision is Fk​-measurable, with Hk​∈{0,1}. The resulting martingale transform has gain
GN​=∑k=0N−1​Hk​(Xk+1​−Xk​),EGN​=0.
(2)
Each completed upcrossing earns at least b−a. An unfinished holding loses at most (XN​−a)−. If a purchase occurs at zero, its price is X0​≤a, giving the additional discount (X0​−a)−, whether that holding has been sold or remains open. If no purchase occurs at zero, this extra term is zero. Therefore, path by path,
GN​≥(b−a)UN​[a,b]−(XN​−a)−+(X0​−a)−.
(3)
Taking expectations proves the bound.
The martingale convergence theorem, stated without proof, says that a discrete-time martingale satisfying supn​E∣Xn​∣<∞ converges almost surely to a finite integrable random variable X∞​. In particular every nonnegative martingale converges almost surely to a finite limit. Under uniform integrability, the uniformly integrable martingale convergence theorem additionally gives convergence in L1 and Xn​=E[X∞​∣Fn​].

A martingale has uniform integrability when its entire family of time values has uniformly small integrable tails:
K→∞lim​n≥0sup​E[∣Xn​∣1{∣Xn​∣>K}​]=0.​

Put Zn​=Xn∧T​. The stopped martingale in discrete time identity
Zn+1​−Zn​=1{T>n}​(Xn+1​−Xn​)
(5)
shows that Z is a martingale: the indicator function is Fn​-measurable and the increment has zero conditional expectation. Integrability follows because Zn​ is selected from the finitely many integrable values X0​,…,Xn​.
For the bounded stopping time τ=n∧T, the optional stopping theorem in its conditional form gives
Xτ​=E[Xn​∣Fτ​].
(6)
Here Fτ​ is the stopping-time sigma-algebra. Indeed, for A∈Fτ​, partition A into A∩{τ=k}∈Fk​ and apply the martingale identity E[Xn​∣Fk​]=Xk​ on each piece. This proves the displayed conditional expectation identity directly.
Let C=supn​E∣Xn​∣<∞ and An​={∣Zn​∣>K}∈Fn∧T​. Conditional absolute-value domination and the Markov inequality give P(An​)≤C/K and, for any R>0,
E[∣Zn​∣1An​​]​≤E[∣Xn​∣1An​​]≤E[∣Xn​∣1{∣Xn​∣>R}​]+RP(An​)≤jsup​E[∣Xj​∣1{∣Xj​∣>R}​]+KRC​.​
(7)
First choose R using the uniform integrability of X, and then choose K. The estimate is uniform in n, proving uniform integrability of a stopped uniformly integrable martingale. No finiteness assumption on T is needed.

A càdlàg martingale is an adapted process X with càdlàg paths, integrable Xt​ at each finite time, and
E[Xt​∣Fs​]=Xs​(0≤s≤t).​
(8)
The càdlàg requirement means that the chosen path version is right-continuous and has a finite left limit at every positive time. With a complete filtration, it suffices that this holds on one common event of probability one, since the process can be redefined on the exceptional null set.
The usual hypotheses for a filtration are completeness and right continuity. Completeness means that F0​ contains every subset of every null event of the ambient probability space; right continuity means
Ft​=u>t⋂​Fu​.​

Fix t>0 and partition [0,t] into m equal intervals, with rk​=kt/m. Round t∧T upward within this interval. The corresponding sampled value is
Ym​=X0​1{T=0}​+∑k=1m−1​Xrk​​1{rk−1​<T≤rk​}​+Xt​1{T>rm−1​}​.
(10)
Every time in this sum is at most t. The stopping time property makes each indicator function Ft​-measurable, while the adapted process property makes each sampled value Ft​-measurable. Consequently Ym​ is Ft​-measurable.
The rounded times approach t∧T from the right, so right continuity gives Ym​→Xt∧T​ for the chosen pathwise càdlàg version. If path regularity is instead stated only almost surely, the usual complete filtration handles the exceptional null set; on an incomplete filtration one should formulate that case as existence of an adapted version. At t=0, the value is simply X0​. Thus XT is an adapted process, by adaptedness of a stopped right-continuous process. The argument actually needs neither the martingale property nor boundedness of T.

First prove nowhere monotonicity of Brownian motion. On a fixed interval [a,b] with rational endpoints and a<b, the 2m increments over its equal subdivision are independent centered normal random variables. If the Brownian motion path were nondecreasing, all these increments would be nonnegative, an event with probability 2−2m. Letting m→∞ gives probability zero. The same argument excludes nonincreasing paths. A countable union over rational intervals shows that, almost surely, no nontrivial interval supports a monotone function restriction of the path, since every such interval contains one with rational endpoints.
Work on this event and on the event of continuous paths. Inside any open interval I⊂(0,∞), choose two separated smaller intervals, the first to the left of the second. The first contains r<s with Br​<Bs​, because its restriction is not nonincreasing. The second contains u<v with Bu​>Bv​, because its restriction is not nondecreasing. Thus r<s<u<v, all in I.
By the extreme value theorem, the path attains its maximum on [r,v]. This value exceeds Br​, since it is at least Bs​, and exceeds Bv​, since it is at least Bu​. A maximizing time therefore lies in (r,v) and is a local maximum of Brownian motion. Every open interval in the half-line contains a positive-time interval of this kind. Consequently the set of local maxima of Brownian motion is dense in [0,∞) almost surely.

For fixed rational 0≤t1​<t2​<t3​<t4​, write
maxt3​≤t≤t4​​Bt​−maxt1​≤t≤t2​​Bt​=ZBt3​​−Bt2​​​​+Vt3​≤t≤t4​max​(Bt​−Bt3​​)​​+WBt2​​−t1​≤t≤t2​max​Bt​​​.
(16)
By independent increments, Z is independent of (V,W) and has normal distribution N(0,t3​−t2​). Conditional on (V,W), the displayed difference has a continuous probability density function; in particular it equals zero with probability zero. Taking the countable intersection over all such rational quadruples proves that, on one event of probability one, the maxima over any two separated rational closed intervals are distinct.
Suppose a local maximum at t were not a strict local maximum. There is a neighbourhood in which every value is at most Bt​, and, arbitrarily close to t, some other time u strictly inside that neighbourhood has Bu​=Bt​. Choose separated rational closed intervals containing t and u, both lying inside that neighbourhood. When one time is zero, use a first interval with left endpoint zero. Both interval maxima equal Bt​, contrary to the preceding event.
Therefore every local maximum of Brownian motion is a strict local maximum almost surely, simultaneously over all times. This countable-interval argument avoids an invalid intersection of probability-one events over uncountably many candidate times.

A standard form of the Kakutani solution of the Dirichlet problem uses a bounded domain D⊂Rd, continuous Dirichlet boundary data f∈C(∂D), and regularity of every boundary point for the Dirichlet problem. The boundedness of D makes ∂D a compact set, so f is a bounded function and a uniformly continuous function. A bounded domain with C2 boundary is a sufficient geometric case; one must not omit boundary regularity for an arbitrary bounded domain.
One standard analytic characterization of a regular boundary point on a bounded domain is the existence of a positive harmonic barrier: for each ξ∈∂D there is a harmonic function hξ​∈C(D) with hξ​(ξ)=0 and hξ​(x)>0 for x∈D∖{ξ}. This is the harmonic form of a barrier for the Dirichlet problem; the barrier characterization of regularity is a standard fact of potential theory.
Let B be d-dimensional Brownian motion started at x∈D and let τD​=inf{t≥0:Bt​∈/D} be its Brownian exit time. Then the unique solution in C(D)∩C2(D) is
u(x)=Ex​[f(BτD​​)](x∈D),u(ξ)=f(ξ)(ξ∈∂D).​
(17)
Equivalently, u(x)=∫∂D​f(ξ)ωD​(x,dξ), where ωD​ is harmonic measure, the exit probability distribution of Brownian motion. The harmonic function equation is Δu=0, with the Laplace operator convention Δ=∑j​∂j2​.

The construction is well defined. Since D is contained in a ball, its Brownian exit time is finite almost surely: at successive integer times there is a fixed positive probability that the next independent unit-time Brownian motion increment has length greater than the ball's diameter, forcing an exit. The survival probability is therefore bounded by a geometric sequence. Path continuity gives BτD​​∈∂D. Thus the bounded Dirichlet boundary data give a bounded Borel function u on D.
For interior harmonicity, fix a ball with closure in D, centred at x, and let σ be its Brownian exit time. The Strong Markov property, followed by the tower property of conditional expectation, gives
u(x)=Ex​[u(Bσ​)].
(18)
The orthogonal invariance of Brownian motion makes Bσ​ uniform on the boundary sphere. Hence u has the spherical mean value property for harmonic functions for every such ball. A locally bounded Borel function with this property is smooth and harmonic: integrating the spherical averages against any smooth radial mollifier gives u=u∗ρ locally, which first proves smoothness; the mean value property for harmonic functions then implies Δu=0.
For the boundary limit at ξ, choose the harmonic barrier for the Dirichlet problem hξ​ from (i). The Itô formula, localized inside D, makes hξ​(Bt∧τD​​) a bounded martingale. Compact localization and the bounded convergence theorem, first to the exit and then as t→∞, yield
Ex​hξ​(BτD​​)=hξ​(x).
(19)
For ε>0, if ∂D∖B(ξ,ε) is nonempty, compactness and barrier positivity give
cε​=minη∈∂D, ∣η−ξ∣≥ε​hξ​(η)>0,Px​(∣BτD​​−ξ∣≥ε)≤cε​hξ​(x)​⟶0(x→ξ).
(20)
If that boundary subset is empty, the probability is already zero. Therefore
∣u(x)−f(ξ)∣≤supη∈∂D, ∣η−ξ∣<ε​∣f(η)−f(ξ)∣+2∥f∥∞​Px​(∣BτD​​−ξ∣≥ε).
(21)
First let x→ξ and then ε↓0. The continuity of the Dirichlet boundary data proves u(x)→f(ξ).
Finally, the difference of two continuous solutions is a harmonic function vanishing on the boundary. The maximum principle for harmonic functions on the bounded domain gives that it is zero. Thus the Kakutani solution of the Dirichlet problem exists, attains every prescribed boundary value, and is unique.

For A>0, the first time TA​=inf{n≥0:Xn​<−A} is a stopping time. On {TA​≥1}, the bounded increments assumption controls the overshoot:
XTA​​≥−A−M.
(22)
Before that time, Xn​≥−A. Allowing the possibility TA​=0, the process
Yn​=Xn∧TA​​+A+M+X0−​
(23)
is a nonnegative martingale. Indeed, the stopped martingale in discrete time is a martingale, and the integrable F0​-measurable variable X0−​ is a constant-in-time martingale. If TA​=0, nonnegativity follows from X0​+X0−​=X0+​. Its expectations are constant and finite, so the martingale convergence theorem makes Yn​ converge to a finite value almost surely.
On {TA​=∞} this proves finite convergence of Xn​. Taking the countable union over positive integer A, we conclude that Xn​ converges finitely on the event {infn​Xn​>−∞}. Apply the same reasoning to the martingale −X to obtain finite convergence on {supn​Xn​<∞}.
Outside the event of finite convergence, both the infimum and the supremum of the sequence must therefore be infinite in the respective directions. Removing any finite initial segment cannot change this, because each of its values is finite. Hence
P({Xn​ converges finitely}∪{nliminf​Xn​=−∞, nlimsup​Xn​=+∞})=1.​
(24)
This is the bounded-increment martingale convergence-or-oscillation dichotomy. The X0−​ term ensures the proof also covers an unbounded integrable initial value.

Let independent Bernoulli random variables Yn​ have success probabilities 2−n, for n≥1, and use their natural filtration. Define
X0​=0,Xn​=k=1∑n​(1−2kYk​).​
(25)
Each increment is integrable, finite, and independent of the previous filtration, with expectation
E(1−2nYn​)=1−2n2−n=0.
(26)
Thus X is a martingale. The increments have no common finite bound, since a success produces a jump of size 2n−1.
Since ∑n​P(Yn​=1)<∞, the First Borel-Cantelli lemma says that only finitely many successes occur almost surely. Consequently
Xn​=n−∑k≥1​2kYk​
(27)
for all sufficiently large n, with the random sum finite almost surely. Therefore Xn​→+∞ almost surely, so neither finite convergence nor two-sided oscillation occurs. This rare-jump martingale divergence example gives probability zero, rather than one, for the union in (i).

Use the intrinsic definition of a Lévy process: X0​=0 almost surely, its increments over disjoint time intervals are independent, their probability distributions depend only on interval length, and X has stochastic continuity. In symbols,
Xs+t​−Xs​=dXt​,Xt+h​⟶Xt​ in probability as h→0
(28)
with times restricted to the half-line. Such a stochastic process has a càdlàg modification, and is usually represented by that version. Requiring càdlàg paths in the definition is a common equivalent convention at the level of modifications; it is important to distinguish this from a claim about the paths of an arbitrary supplied version.
For the characteristic function, write
EeiθXt​=etψ(θ)=e−tΨ(θ),Ψ=−ψ.​
(29)
Here ψ(0)=0 and ψ is the positive-time-sign characteristic exponent of a Lévy process. The independent increments and stationary increments give ϕs+t​(θ)=ϕs​(θ)ϕt​(θ); stochastic continuity gives continuity in time and ϕ0​=1. This continuous multiplicative semigroup has the stated exponential form. Its exponent has the Lévy–Khintchine formula
ψ(θ)=ibθ−2σ2θ2​+∫R∖{0}​(eiθy−1−iθy1{∣y∣≤1}​)ν(dy),
(30)
where b∈R, σ2≥0, and the Lévy measure ν satisfies ∫(1∧y2)ν(dy)<∞. The truncation convention fixes the drift coefficient b; the displayed sign convention agrees with Ψ=−ψ.

Construct a rate-λ Poisson process, for λ>0, from independent waiting times Ej​ with exponential distribution Exp(λ). Put Sk​=E1​+⋯+Ek​, S0​=0, and Nt​=max{k:Sk​≤t}. The strong law of large numbers gives Sk​/k→1/λ, so there are only finitely many arrivals on each finite interval. Consequently the counting paths are càdlàg, and N0​=0 almost surely.
At any deterministic time s, the memorylessness of the exponential distribution says that the residual waiting time has again exponential distribution Exp(λ) and is independent of the observed history. Subsequent waiting times are fresh independent copies. Thus the Poisson process restarts independently at s, proving independent increments and stationary increments. Integrating the joint waiting-time densities over 0<s1​<⋯<sk​≤t<sk+1​ gives
P(Nt​=k)=e−λtk!(λt)k​,k≥0,
(31)
so its time values have the expected Poisson distribution.
For h↓0,
P(Nh​=0)=1−e−λh⟶0.
(32)
Together with stationary increments, this gives stochastic continuity at every time, from either side where applicable. All the Lévy process requirements hold, and
EeiθNt​=exp{λt(eiθ−1)},ψ(θ)=λ(eiθ−1).​
(33)
For λ=0, the identically zero process gives the degenerate case.

First, convergence in probability at t=0 gives X0​=0 almost surely, since every X0n​=0. For any finite set of times, the corresponding vectors converge in probability: the union bound controls the probability that any coordinate differs by more than a fixed tolerance. The same holds for their increment vectors, hence also for their convergence in distribution.
For 0=t0​<t1​<⋯<tm​, set Djn​=Xtj​n​−Xtj−1​n​ and Dj​=Xtj​​−Xtj−1​​. The characteristic function of a random vector factors for the independent increments of each Xn:
Eexp(i∑j=1m​θj​Djn​)=∏j=1m​Eeiθj​Djn​.
(34)
Pass to the limit using convergence in distribution and bounded continuous functions. The resulting factorization of the characteristic function of a random vector, with the uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions, proves independence of the Dj​. Similarly Xs+tn​−Xsn​=dXtn​ passes to the limit, proving stationary increments for X.
It remains to prove stochastic continuity. For ε>0, the triangle inequality and the union bound imply, for every fixed n,
t↓0limsup​P(∣Xt​∣>ε)​≤t↓0limsup​P(∣Xt​−Xtn​∣>ε/2)+t↓0limsup​P(∣Xtn​∣>ε/2)=t↓0limsup​P(∣Xt​−Xtn​∣>ε/2).​
(35)
The second term vanishes by stochastic continuity of Xn. Now let n→∞ and use the additional near-zero approximation hypothesis. We obtain Xt​→0 in probability as t↓0. The stationary increments transfer this to every time: both Xs+h​−Xs​ and Xs​−Xs−h​ have the law of Xh​ for h>0 when defined. Thus X has all the intrinsic Lévy process properties, proving closure of Lévy processes under locally controlled convergence in probability.
If one requires the supplied process itself to be càdlàg, the hypotheses justify a càdlàg modification, rather than that stronger pathwise assertion. To see the distinction, take Xn≡0, let U have uniform distribution on (0,1), and put Xt​=1{t=U}​. At each fixed time t, Xt​=0 almost surely, so both approximation hypotheses hold with zero error probability. Nevertheless every path has an isolated spike at U and is not right-continuous there. Its identically zero modification of a stochastic process is a Lévy process with càdlàg paths. The conclusion is exact under the intrinsic definition, and exact up to modification under the convention requiring càdlàg paths.
