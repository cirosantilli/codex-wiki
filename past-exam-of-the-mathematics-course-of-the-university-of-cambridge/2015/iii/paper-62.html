Use a mass-normalized galactic distribution function F, so that ρ=∫Fd3v and ρ⟨vi​vj​⟩=∫vi​vj​Fd3v. Multiplying the stationary Collisionless Boltzmann equation by vj​ and integrating over velocity space gives
∂i​∫vi​vj​Fd3v−Φ,i​∫vj​∂vi​​Fd3v=0.
(1)
Assume the galactic distribution function decays sufficiently rapidly for the velocity boundary term to vanish. Integration by parts then gives ∫vj​∂vi​​Fd3v=−δij​ρ, and therefore the Jeans equations are
∂i​(ρ⟨vi​vj​⟩)=−ρΦ,j​.​
(2)
The second moment includes both ordered motion and velocity dispersion; no assumption of zero mean velocity was needed.
To obtain the tensor virial theorem, multiply the jth Jeans equation by xi​ and integrate over position. For an isolated, finite system with a vanishing spatial surface term,
−∫ρ⟨vi​vj​⟩d3x=−∫ρxi​Φ,j​d3x=Wij​.
(3)
Since the left side is −2Kij​, 2Kij​+Wij​=0. This convention puts all stellar second moments into Kij​; it does not separate ordered and random kinetic energies.
For a self-gravitating system, the trace W is the Newtonian gravitational potential energy. Indeed, symmetrizing the pair integral gives
W=−2G​∬∣x−x′∣ρ(x)ρ(x′)​d3xd3x′=21​∫ρΦd3x.
(4)
Thus the trace of the tensor virial theorem is 2K+W=0, and the total energy obeys
E=K+W=−K=W/2.​
(5)
These identities require self-gravity without an additional external potential or an omitted confining boundary pressure.
Let ⟨vI2​⟩ now denote a mass-weighted average over the entire initial system. Then KI​=MI​⟨vI2​⟩/2. The gravitational radius is defined by RI​=−GMI2​/WI​, so the virial theorem immediately yields
EI​=−21​MI​⟨vI2​⟩=−2RI​GMI2​​.​
(6)
The gravitational radius measures total binding energy, rather than a particular geometric edge or half-mass radius.
For the accreted systems define the mass-weighted internal mean-square speed by MA​⟨vA2​⟩=∑s​Ms​⟨vs2​⟩. If each satellite initially satisfies the virial theorem, its internal energy contributes to EA​=−MA​⟨vA2​⟩/2. In parabolic dry-merger energy accounting, the orbital energy at large separation is zero. Assume a dry galaxy merger, no loss of mass or energy through escaping stars, no external work, and a final relaxed system satisfying the virial theorem. Then conservation of energy gives EF​=EI​+EA​ and MF​=MI​+MA​. Energy transferred by Chandrasekhar dynamical friction remains part of the total energy under these assumptions. Consequently,
EF​=−21​MI​⟨vI2​⟩(1+ϵη),η=MI​MA​​,ϵ=⟨vI2​⟩⟨vA2​⟩​.
(7)
Applying the final virial theorem and dividing by the initial relation gives
⟨vI2​⟩⟨vF2​⟩​=1+η1+ϵη​,RI​RF​​=1+ϵη(1+η)2​.​
(8)
For a mass doubling, η=1. A merger of two identical equilibrated galaxies has ϵ=1, hence RF​/RI​=2. For accretion through many minor galaxy mergers whose satellites have much smaller internal mean-square speeds, ϵ≪1, hence RF​/RI​≃4, tending to 4 in the cold-satellite limit. Equivalently, nearly fixed total energy makes the gravitational radius scale as M2 during cold minor-merger size growth. Small satellite mass alone does not logically imply ϵ≪1: the factor four also requires this weak-binding assumption. Likewise, equal masses require comparable internal binding to give the factor two. These are the physical limits behind the two stated merger comparisons.

A phase-space distribution function describes the mass in a small cell of positions and velocities: F(x,v,t)d3xd3v is that mass. Its velocity integral is the spatial mass density, and F/ρ at a fixed position is a normalized velocity probability density. A number-normalized galactic distribution function differs by the stellar mass convention.
Take a>0 and v0​>0. For the cusped logarithmic spherical galaxy model, differentiate the gravitational potential and use the spherical Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity:
Φ′=a+rv02​​,4πGρ=r21​drd​(r2Φ′)=v02​r(a+r)22a+r​.
(9)
Thus
ρ(r)=4πGv02​​r(a+r)22a+r​,M(r)=G(a+r)v02​r2​.​
(10)
A circular orbit requires vc2​/r=Φ′. Its galaxy rotation curve is therefore
vc​(r)=v0​a+rr​​.​
(11)
It rises as v0​r/a​ at small radius and approaches v0​ from below at large radius.
The outer flat galaxy rotation curve is qualitatively useful for galaxies. However, the density has an inner 1/r cusp and an outer 1/r2 tail, while M(r)∼v02​r/G diverges. The model therefore requires an outer truncation for a finite galaxy, and does not describe a central constant-density core. Its gravitational potential cannot be normalized to zero at infinity.
For a star, the specific energy is E=v2/2+Φ and the specific angular momentum is L=x×v. Since the gravitational potential is stationary and spherical,
dtdE​=v⋅(v˙+∇Φ)=0,dtdL​=x×(−Φ′rx​)=0.
(12)
Thus both the specific energy and the entire angular-momentum vector are conserved.
For Jeans theorem, follow a stellar trajectory in phase space. The Collisionless Boltzmann equation is precisely
dtdF​=∂t​F+v⋅∇x​F−∇Φ⋅∇v​F=0.
(13)
In a steady system ∂t​F=0, so the value of the galactic distribution function is constant along every trajectory: it is itself an integral of motion. Conversely, if I1​,…,Im​ are time-independent integrals of motion, then F=h(I1​,…,Im​) satisfies dF/dt=∑h,a​I˙a​=0. Locally, a complete set of invariant orbit labels expresses any steady galactic distribution function this way. This is the weak form of Jeans theorem; a stronger assertion about a small set of isolating integrals needs additional orbital regularity or mixing assumptions. In particular, the theorem does not say that every steady galaxy must have a galactic distribution function depending on only E and L.
For the proposed constant-anisotropy distribution function, write vT​=(vθ2​+vϕ2​)1/2 and L=rvT​. Introduce a polar angle ψ in the tangential velocity plane. Then d3v=dvr​vT​dvT​dψ, so the apparent 1/L singularity cancels against the velocity Jacobian. This inverse-angular-momentum velocity integration makes the density finite for every r>0.
Let t=e−Φ/v02​=a/(a+r), c1​=1, c2​=2, and αk​=k/(2v02​). Each exponential component contributes
ρk​=8π3Garck​tk​∫−∞∞​e−αk​vr2​dvr​∫0∞​e−αk​vT2​dvT​∫02π​dψ=4πGarv02​​kck​​tk.
(14)
Here the two Gaussian integrals and the angular integral have product π2/αk​. Consequently,
ρ=4πGarv02​​(t+t2)=4πGv02​​r(a+r)22a+r​.
(15)
The galactic distribution function is nonnegative, gives the required density, and depends on conserved E,L; by Jeans theorem it is a self-consistent steady solution.
Differentiating the Gaussian integral with respect to αk​ shows that each component has ⟨vr2​⟩k​=1/(2αk​)=v02​/k, and the same value for ⟨vT2​⟩k​. Angular averaging gives ⟨vθ2​⟩k​=⟨vϕ2​⟩k​=v02​/(2k). Weighting these by ρ1​,ρ2​ yields the velocity dispersions
σr2​=v02​t+t2t+t2/2​=2v02​​2a+r3a+2r​,σθ2​=σϕ2​=4v02​​2a+r3a+2r​.​
(16)
The radial numerator includes 2r, as in the original PDF. All mean velocities vanish by parity. The same parity, or the zero angular average of sinψcosψ, gives ⟨vr​vθ​⟩=⟨vr​vϕ​⟩=⟨vθ​vϕ​⟩=0.
The velocity-anisotropy parameter is
β=1−2σr2​σθ2​+σϕ2​​=21​.​
(17)
The constant-anisotropy distribution function therefore favors radial motion at every radius: the radial variance is twice either individual tangential variance. It still has a substantial population with nonzero angular momentum, rather than exclusively radial orbits. The L−1 factor enhances low-angular-momentum stars. The radial velocity dispersion approaches 3v02​/4 at the centre and v02​ far out; each individual tangential variance is half as large. There is no mean rotation, despite the well-defined galaxy rotation curve for hypothetical circular orbits.

In a stationary axisymmetric gravitational potential, conservation of angular momentum gives Lz​=R2ϕ˙​. Eliminating ϕ˙​ introduces the effective potential
Φeff​(R,z)=Φ(R,z)+2R2Lz2​​,R¨=−∂R​Φeff​,z¨=−∂z​Φ.
(18)
For an equatorial circular orbit, radial balance is ∂R​Φeff​(Rc​,0)=0, giving
Φ,R​(Rc​,0)=Rc3​Lz2​​.​
(19)
Vertical balance also requires Φ,z​(Rc​,0)=0.
For epicyclic motion, assume a twice differentiable stationary gravitational potential symmetric under z↦−z, small displacements ∣x∣,∣z∣≪Rc​, and a stable circular orbit. Choose the epicyclic guiding center using the conserved Lz​. Reflection symmetry makes Φ,Rz​(Rc​,0)=0, eliminating radial-vertical coupling at first order. Expanding the equations about (Rc​,0) gives
x¨=−κ2x,z¨=−ν2z,κ2=Φ,RR​+Rc4​3Lz2​​,ν2=Φ,zz​.​
(20)
The derivatives are evaluated at the guiding centre, and stability requires κ2,ν2>0. Without midplane symmetry, a mixed Hessian term can couple the two oscillations. The radial epicyclic frequency and vertical epicyclic frequency are the frequencies of these independent linear oscillations.
Set Ω=Lz​/Rc2​. Along the family of equatorial circular orbits, Φ,R​=RΩ2. Differentiating this relation and adding the centrifugal contribution gives
κ2=RdRdΩ2​+4Ω2.​
(21)
To interpret the common frequency range, let q=dlogvc​/dlogR. Since vc​=RΩ, one has κ2=2(1+q)Ω2. A Keplerian disk has q=−1/2 and κ=Ω, a flat galaxy rotation curve has q=0 and κ=2​Ω, and solid-body rotation has q=1 and κ=2Ω. Typical galactic rotation curves lie between these slopes. Thus Ω≲κ≲2Ω is a useful galactic range, not a theorem for every possible axisymmetric potential. As a precise sufficient example, epicyclic frequency bounds for monotone spherical density follow from κ2=Ω2+4πGρ and 0≤ρ≤3M/(4πR3).
Use a Cartesian frame rotating with the epicyclic guiding center, with x pointing radially outwards and y=Rc​(ϕ−Ωt) in the direction of rotation. To first order, conservation of angular momentum gives
ϕ˙​=(Rc​+x)2Lz​​=Ω(1−Rc​2x​)+O(x2/Rc2​),y˙​=−2Ωx.
(22)
The radial harmonic oscillator solution and its azimuthal integral are
x=Xcos(κt+χ),y=−κ2ΩX​sin(κt+χ)=−Ysin(κt+χ),YX​=2Ωκ​.​
(23)
A constant in y merely changes the azimuthal origin of the guiding centre. The epicyclic ellipse obeys x2/X2+y2/Y2=1. In the usual frequency range it is elongated azimuthally, and the star travels clockwise when x points right and y up: its small motion relative to the prograde guiding centre is retrograde.
For the Oort constants, subtract the defining expressions to obtain Ω=A−B and insert RΩ′=−2A into the radial epicyclic frequency formula:
κ2=4Ω(Ω−A)=−4ΩB,YX​=A−B−B​​.
(24)
The solar-neighbourhood values give Ω=26.5kms−1kpc−1, κ=1272​kms−1kpc−1, and
X/Y=24/53​≃0.673.​
(25)
The solar epicyclic ellipse is therefore about 1.49 times longer azimuthally than radially.
A complete radial oscillation takes Tr​=2π/κ. The oscillatory part of ϕ˙​ has zero average over this interval, so the epicyclic azimuthal advance is
Δψ=ΩTr​=κ2πΩ​=2π(4+dlogRdlogΩ2​)−1/2.​
(26)
This describes the advance between consecutive radial turning points of the same type; it need not be a full 2π revolution. For the Sun,
Δψ=π53/24​≃4.669 rad≃267.5∘.​
(27)
The azimuthal advance estimate uses the same linear epicyclic motion approximation as the axis ratio.

For a spherical system, use the spherical shell theorem with shell mass dm=4πρ(s)s2ds. Inner shells contribute −Gdm/r to the gravitational potential, while an outer shell contributes its constant interior potential −Gdm/s. With Φ(∞)=0, assuming the required integrals converge,
Φ(r)=−4πG[r1​∫0r​ρ(s)s2ds+∫r∞​ρ(s)sds].​
(28)
On differentiation, the two terms containing ρ(r)r cancel. Hence Φ′=GM(r)/r2, where M(r)=4π∫0r​ρ(s)s2ds. Radial balance for a circular orbit gives vc2​=rΦ′=GM(r)/r.
For a razor-thin axisymmetric astrophysical disk, the element of mass is dm=Σ(s)sdsdϕ′, where Σ is the surface density. Superposing Newtonian gravitational potentials therefore gives
Φ(R)=−G∫0∞​Σ(s)sds∫02π​R2+s2−2Rscos(ϕ′−ϕ)​dϕ′​.​
(29)
Axisymmetry permits ϕ=0. Unlike the spherical case, an exterior annulus generally exerts a radial force: enclosed mass does not determine a disc rotation curve.
For the Legendre expansion of thin-disk gravity, split the radial integral at s=R. In the inner part the kernel expands in s/R, while in the outer part it expands in R/s. The angular integrals of odd Legendre polynomials vanish, and those of even degree n equal 2αn​, where
αn​=π[2n((n/2)!)2n!​]2(n even).
(30)
Introduce In​(R)=∫0R​Σ(s)sn+1ds and Jn​(R)=∫R∞​Σ(s)s−nds. The gravitational potential becomes
Φ(R)=−2G∑k=0∞​α2k​[R−(2k+1)I2k​(R)+R2kJ2k​(R)].
(31)
When differentiating each bracket, the moving-limit terms cancel: R−(n+1)In′​=Σ(R) and RnJn′​=−Σ(R). Since vc2​=RΦ′, this gives
vc2​=2G∑k=0∞​α2k​[(2k+1)R−(2k+1)I2k​−2kR2kJ2k​].
(32)
The zeroth term has α0​=π and M(R)=2πI0​. Separating it proves
vc2​=RGM(R)​+2Gk=1∑∞​α2k​[(2k+1)R−(2k+1)∫0R​Σ(s)s2k+1ds−2kR2k∫R∞​Σ(s)s−2kds].​
(33)
The inner correction is inward, while the exterior correction is outward. For a smooth surface density, the original potential singularity at coincident points is integrable. Its radial force is understood through a symmetric Cauchy principal value or a vanishing-thickness regularization; the paired interior and exterior terms above retain the cancellation at s=R. This avoids treating the two singular local force contributions separately.
For an exponential galactic disk, write Σ(s)=Σ0​e−s/Rd​, so M∞​=2πΣ0​Rd2​ and M(R)=M∞​[1−(1+R/Rd​)e−R/Rd​]. At large R, the missing mass and exterior-ring terms are exponentially small. The leading nonspherical interior term is k=1, with α2​=π/4 and I2​(∞)=6Σ0​Rd4​. Consequently the exponential-disk Keplerian asymptotic is
vc2​(R)=RGM∞​​+R39πGΣ0​Rd4​​+O(R−5)=RGM∞​​[1+29​(RRd​​)2+O((Rd​/R)4)].
(34)
The positive leading correction shows that the rotation curve approaches the Keplerian limit from above. The finite-order large-radius expansion is sufficient here; an infinite moment expansion need not converge for a disk extending to infinity.
A useful special example is the Mestel disk, with surface density Σ(s)=C/s for s>0, C>0. It has M(R)=2πCR. For every positive even n,
In​=n+1CRn+1​,Jn​=nCR−n​,(n+1)R−(n+1)In​−nRnJn​=C−C=0.
(35)
All the nonspherical corrections cancel, giving
vc2​=RGM(R)​=2πGC=vM2​.​
(36)
Thus the Mestel disk has a perfectly flat galaxy rotation curve.
This example has infinite total mass and a singular central surface density. The absolute gravitational potential cannot be set to zero at infinity, but the radial force exists as a limit of disks with increasing outer cutoff. Potential differences are Φ(R)−Φ(R0​)=vM2​log(R/R0​). The earlier potential integral is therefore interpreted up to a radius-independent divergent constant for this example; the force calculation remains valid. Truncating the Mestel disk gives a more physical finite system but changes the exact flat curve near its edges.
