A phase-space distribution function describes the mass in a small cell of positions and velocities: F(x,v,t)d3xd3v is that mass. Its velocity integral is the spatial mass density, and F/ρ at a fixed position is a normalized velocity probability density. A number-normalized galactic distribution function differs by the stellar mass convention.

The model therefore requires an outer truncation for a finite galaxy, and does not describe a central constant-density core. Its gravitational potential cannot be normalized to zero at infinity.
For a star, the specific energy is E=v2/2+Φ and the specific angular momentum is L=x×v. Since the gravitational potential is stationary and spherical,

For Jeans theorem, follow a stellar trajectory in phase space. The Collisionless Boltzmann equation is precisely
dtdF​=∂t​F+v⋅∇x​F−∇Φ⋅∇v​F=0.
(13)
In a steady system ∂t​F=0, so the value of the galactic distribution function is constant along every trajectory: it is itself an integral of motion. Conversely, if I1​,…,Im​ are time-independent integrals of motion, then F=h(I1​,…,Im​) satisfies dF/dt=∑h,a​I˙a​=0. Locally, a complete set of invariant orbit labels expresses any steady galactic distribution function this way. This is the weak form of Jeans theorem; a stronger assertion about a small set of isolating integrals needs additional orbital regularity or mixing assumptions. In particular, the theorem does not say that every steady galaxy must have a galactic distribution function depending on only E and L.

The constant-anisotropy distribution function therefore favors radial motion at every radius: the radial variance is twice either individual tangential variance. There is no mean rotation, despite the well-defined galaxy rotation curve for hypothetical circular orbits.
