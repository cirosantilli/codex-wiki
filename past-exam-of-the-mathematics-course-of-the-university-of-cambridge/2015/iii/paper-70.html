Use the half-line Fourier transform and finite-time boundary transforms
q​(k,t)=∫0∞​e−ikxq(x,t)dx,Gj​(k,t)=∫0t​eik2sgj​(s)ds,gj​(t)=∂xj​q(0,t),j=0,1.
(1)
The half-line Fourier transform is analytic for Imk<0 under spatial decay. All spectral integrals below have their usual oscillatory, or vanishing Gaussian damping, interpretation until absolute convergence is established.
The free Schrodinger equation has the local divergence identity
∂t​(e−ikx+ik2tq)=i∂x​[e−ikx+ik2t(qx​+ikq)].
(2)
Integrating in x,t gives the global relation for the half-line free Schrodinger equation
eik2tq​(k,t)=q​0​(k)+kG0​(k,t)−iG1​(k,t),Imk≤0.​
(3)
Let D+​={Rek>0,Imk>0}. Orient its boundary from i∞ down to 0, and then from 0 to +∞, so that D+​ lies on the left. Fourier inversion followed by contour integration in the second quadrant yields
q(x,t)=2π1​∫R​eikx−ik2tq​0​(k)dk+2π1​∫∂D+​​eikx−ik2t[kG0​(k,t)−iG1​(k,t)]dk.​
(4)
This is a complex spectral representation involving the initial trace and both boundary traces. The contour deformation works because each boundary-time integrand contains e−ik2(t−s) with s≤t, which decays in the second quadrant, as well as eikx for x>0. This fixes both the quadrant and the orientation; changing either without changing the signs would produce an incorrect representation.

The unknown Neumann boundary condition can be removed by the Fokas method. Evaluate the global relation for the half-line free Schrodinger equation at −k:
eik2tq​(−k,t)=q​0​(−k)−kG0​(k,t)−iG1​(k,t).
(5)
Consequently kG0​−iG1​=2kG0​−q​0​(−k)+eik2tq​(−k,t). In the representation from part (a), the last term has integral ∫∂D+​​eikxq​(−k,t)dk=0, by the Cauchy integral theorem and Jordan lemma. It is analytic in the upper half-plane, and the exponential decays on the closing first-quadrant arc. Hence an expression involving only the given data is
q(x,t)=2π1​∫R​eikx−ik2tq​0​(k)dk+2π1​∫∂D+​​eikx−ik2t[2kG0​(k,t)−q​0​(−k)]dk.​
(6)
One may replace t by T in the upper limit defining G0​: the contribution of boundary times s>t closes to zero in D+​, since e−ik2(t−s) then decays there. Using t makes causality transparent.
For an explicit proof of uniform convergence, it is useful to apply boundary lifting before inversion. Set h(x)=e−x, c=g0​(0), w0​(x)=q0​(x)−ch(x), and
S0​(k)=∫0∞​sin(kx)w0​(x)dx,H(k)=∫0∞​sin(kx)h(x)dx=1+k2k​,f(t)=ig0​(t)−g0′​(t).
(7)
The compatibility condition gives w0​(0)=0. The Fourier sine transform of w=q−g0​h satisfies St​+ik2S=Hf, with initial value S0​. The integrating factor therefore gives the equivalent representation
q(x,t)=g0​(t)e−x+π2​∫0∞​sin(kx)[e−ik2tS0​(k)+1+k2k​∫0t​e−ik2(t−s)f(s)ds]dk.​
(8)
This last integral is absolutely and uniformly convergent for x≥0, 0≤t≤T under concrete sufficient hypotheses w0​,w0′​,w0′′​∈L1, decay of the boundary terms, and g0​∈C2[0,T]. Indeed, two integrations by parts give ∣S0​(k)∣≤∥w0′′​∥1​/k2 for k≥1. Another integration by parts, this time in s, gives
​∫0t​e−ik2(t−s)f(s)ds​≤k22∥f∥∞​+T∥f′∥∞​​.
(9)
Thus the initial term is uniformly O(k−2) and the forcing term uniformly O(k−3). For 0<k<1, use ∣S0​(k)∣≤∥w0​∥1​ and the bounded time integral. An integrable majorant proves the claimed uniform convergence and permits evaluation at both boundaries.
At t=0, Fourier sine inversion gives q(x,0)=ce−x+w0​(x)=q0​(x). At x=0, the integral vanishes, giving q(0,t)=g0​(t), including the compatible corner. To verify the equation, note that h′′=h and the transformed equation implies
iwt​+wxx​=−(ig0′​+g0​)h.
(10)
Adding the lifted part gives iqt​+qxx​=0. Under the stated smoothness, this holds classically in the interior; differentiated spectral integrals can first be Gaussian-regularized, or read in the sine-transform L2 sense and then identified with the smooth solution. Uniform convergence of q itself does not require claiming uniform convergence of every differentiated integral at the corner.

Splitting the free Schrodinger equation into real and imaginary parts gives
ut​=−vxx​,vt​=uxx​.​
(11)
Differentiating the first relation in time and using the second yields the Euler-Bernoulli beam equation utt​+uxxxx​=0. This is the Schrodinger factorization of the elastic beam equation.
Assume the initial velocity has an integrable first spatial moment, as allowed by sufficient decay, and define
v0​(x)=−∫x∞​(s−x)u1​(s)ds,q0​(x)=u0​(x)+iv0​(x).
(12)
Then v0′′​=−u1​ and v0​ decays at infinity. To encode the second boundary datum, define
vb​(t)=v0​(0)+∫0t​u1​(s)ds,g0​(t)=u0​(t)+ivb​(t).
(13)
The Dirichlet boundary condition for the resulting free Schrodinger equation is compatible at the corner, since q0​(0)=g0​(0). Insert these explicit q0​,g0​ into the data-only complex integral in part (b), with q​0​ and G0​ defined as in part (a). The required displacement is the real part of that integral. Equivalently, the uniformly convergent lifted integral in part (b) may be used with the same complex data.
The Schrodinger factorization of the elastic beam equation verifies every condition: u(x,0)=u0​(x), ut​(x,0)=−v0′′​(x)=u1​(x), u(0,t)=u0​(t), and
uxx​(0,t)=vt​(0,t)=vb′​(t)=u1​(t).
(14)
The corner requirements on u0′′​ and u0′​ ensure consistency of these derivative traces; the natural interpretation of the last printed compatibility is u0′​(0)=u1​(0). If its prime were instead imposed for every t, that would simply be an extra restriction on the data, and the same construction would still solve them.

Use the Wirtinger derivative qz​=(qx​−iqy​)/2 and write z=x+iy. Since q solves the Laplace equation, qz​ is a holomorphic function. The transforms in the question select solutions with sufficient decay at infinity. In this class the solution, when it exists, is unique; hence reflection of the symmetric data about y=ℓ/2 gives
q(x,ℓ−y)=q(x,y),q(x,ℓ)=q(x,0).
(15)
For γ>0, this uniqueness follows directly from Green's first identity: the homogeneous problem has integral ∫∣∇q∣2+γ∫y=0,ℓ​q2=0. For arbitrary real γ, the decaying-class qualifications are explained in part (c). Without a condition at infinity, symmetry of the data alone would not force symmetry of every solution.
Put c=q(0,0), f(x)=q(x,0), and introduce a known transform
ψ(s)=21​∫0∞​esxf(x)dx,H(k)=21​∫0ℓ​ekyg(y)dy.
(16)
The bottom Robin boundary condition gives qy​=γf, while the top one gives qy​=−γf. The bottom side is traversed from infinity to zero. Integration by parts therefore gives
G1​=−21​∫0∞​e−ikx(f′−iγf)dx=2c​−i(k−γ)ψ(−ik)​.
(17)
The top side is traversed from iℓ towards infinity, and its exponential supplies ekℓ, giving
G3​=ekℓ[−2c​+i(k+γ)ψ(−ik)].​
(18)
On the vertical side, dz=idy and the prescribed Neumann boundary condition is qx​=g. Thus
G2​=21​∫0ℓ​eky(qy​+ig)dy=ϕ(k)+iH(k)​.
(19)
Both the side orientations and the factor 1/2 from the Wirtinger derivative are essential to these signs.

The Cauchy integral theorem applied to e−ikzqz​ yields the global relation
G1​(k)+G2​(k)+G3​(k)=0.
(20)
Initially this follows for Imk≤0, where the far vertical closing segment vanishes. The identities below can be compared on the real axis, and then continued wherever the respective transforms are analytic.
Define the characteristic factor of the symmetric Robin strip transform
D(k)=(k−γ)−(k+γ)ekℓ.
(21)
Substituting part (a) into the global relation gives
ϕ(k)=iD(k)ψ(−ik)−iH(k)−2c​(1−ekℓ).
(22)
Reflection symmetry gives H(−k)=e−kℓH(k) and ϕ(−k)=−e−kℓϕ(k), because qy​(0,ℓ−y)=−qy​(0,y). Also D(−k)=e−kℓD(k). Use the global relation at −k and multiply by −ekℓ to obtain
ϕ(k)=−iD(k)ψ(ik)+iH(k)−2c​(1−ekℓ).
(23)
Comparison therefore proves
ψ(−ik)=D(k)2H(k)​−ψ(ik),ϕ(k)=iH(k)−iD(k)ψ(ik)−2c​(1−ekℓ).​
(24)
At a zero of D, this identity is interpreted through the necessary solvability condition and a removable limit when a decaying solution exists. For γ>0, D has no real zeros, so no such qualification is needed on the two real integration rays.

Substitute part (b) into the spectral representation. The terms containing ψ(ik) are
2πi​[(∫0∞​−∫0i∞​)eikz(k−γ)ψ(ik)dk+(∫0i∞​−∫0−∞​)eik(z−iℓ)(k+γ)ψ(ik)dk].
(25)
The transform ψ(ik) is analytic for Imk>0. Write k=a+ib. In the first quadrant,
∣eikz∣=e−ay−bx,
(26)
which decays since x,y>0. The Cauchy integral theorem in that quadrant equates the two integrals in the first parentheses. In the second quadrant,
∣eik(z−iℓ)∣=e−a(y−ℓ)−bx,
(27)
which decays since a<0, b>0, and y<ℓ. The same contour integration equates the two integrals in the second parentheses. Therefore ψ(ik) makes no contribution. This is upper-quadrant cancellation of reflected boundary transforms, and it uses the whole paired expression rather than attempting to discard an individual integral.
The terms involving c cancel by the same two quadrant arguments with the analytic factors k±γ and ψ omitted. There is a sign defect in the printed reconstruction prefactor. The sides specified in part (a) form a clockwise boundary, so the Cauchy integral theorem gives a negative reconstruction sign. Indeed, integrating each spectral ray first yields i/(z−ζ), and hence the sum of the three ray integrals is i∮clockwise​qz​(ζ)/(z−ζ)dζ=−2πqz​(z). Thus the printed positive prefactor reconstructs −qz​. The requested cancellation above holds with either overall sign, but the actual data-dependent derivative is
qz​=−πi​[−∫0∞​eikz(k−γ)D(k)H(k)​dk+∫0i∞​eikzH(k)dk+∫0−∞​eikz+kℓ(k+γ)D(k)H(k)​dk].​
(28)
Decay at infinity fixes the integration constant when recovering q from its Wirtinger derivative.
For completeness, the missing condition at infinity and the unrestricted printed γ deserve an explicit solvability of a decaying Robin strip check. Let Yn​ be orthonormal eigenfunctions from a Sturm-Liouville problem of −d2/dy2 with Yn′​(0)=γYn​(0) and Yn′​(ℓ)=−γYn​(ℓ), and eigenvalues λn​. With gn​=∫0ℓ​gYn​dy, separation of variables gives
q(x,y)=−∑λn​>0​λn​​gn​​e−λn​​xYn​(y),gn​=0 required whenever λn​≤0.
(29)
Indeed, each coefficient obeys qn′′​−λn​qn​=0 and qn′​(0)=gn​. A positive eigenvalue has one decaying exponential; a zero eigenvalue has only affine solutions and a negative eigenvalue only oscillatory solutions, neither of which decays unless its coefficient vanishes. This also proves uniqueness in the decay class and justifies reflection symmetry there. For γ>0, all eigenvalues are positive. For γ=0, the constant eigenfunction requires ∫0ℓ​gdy=0; the apparent zero of D at the origin is then removable. For γ<0, compatibility with every nonpositive eigenmode is necessary. These restrictions cannot be inferred from smoothness and reflection symmetry alone. The printed boundary value problem without any far-field condition permits additional growing or nondecaying homogeneous solutions, whereas the spectral transforms select the decaying branch.

Fix the convention Δq−m2q=0, m>0, and let cm​=m2/4. With z=x+iy, two families of modified Helmholtz adjoint plane waves are
E+​(z,k)=e−ikz+icm​zˉ/k,E−​(z,k)=eikzˉ−icm​z/k,k=0.
(30)
Both satisfy ΔE±​=m2E±​, since the products of their z and zˉ exponents are m2/4. They obey E−​(z,k)=E+​(z,kˉ)​. Green second identity gives the two conjugate global relations for the modified Helmholtz equation
G±​(k)=∫∂Ω​(E±​∂n​q−q∂n​E±​)ds=0.​
(31)
For real boundary traces, G−​(k)=G+​(kˉ)​. Thus the second identity is the conjugate spectral companion, rather than an unrelated extra boundary condition. In complex differential form the first relation is equivalently
∫∂Ω​E+​[(qz​+ikq)dz−(qzˉ​−kicm​​q)dzˉ]=0.
(32)
Its integrand is a closed one-form: differentiating its coefficients gives 2E+​(qzzˉ​−cm​q)=0. This supplies a direct derivation of the global relation as well as its outward-normal sign convention.
Write the known Dirichlet boundary condition on side j as fj​(s) and the unknown outward normal derivative as hj​(s), with −1<s<1. For any linear combination v of the adjoint waves, the global relation is
∑j​∫−11​vj​(s)hj​(s)ds=∑j​∫−11​fj​(s)∂nj​​vj​(s)ds.
(33)
Expanding the hj​ in a finite basis and enforcing these identities at selected collocation points for a global relation gives a linear system with a known right side. The square permits especially simple paired tests.
Let pn​=nπ/2, ηn​=m2+pn2​​, and φn​(s)=sin[pn​(s+1)] for n≥1. Define four real adjoint solutions
vB,n​vL,n​​=e−ηn​(y+1)φn​(x),=e−ηn​(x+1)φn​(y),​vT,n​vR,n​​=eηn​(y−1)φn​(x),=eηn​(x−1)φn​(y).​
(34)
They satisfy the modified Helmholtz equation because ηn2​−pn2​=m2. Each is a linear combination of two adjoint exponentials, hence is obtained from the two spectral global relations. More explicitly, put ρn±​=(ηn​±pn​)/2, so ρn+​ρn−​=cm​. For the E+​ convention above, the required spectral pairs are
{ρn+​,ρn−​} (T),{−ρn+​,−ρn−​} (B),{iρn+​,iρn−​} (R),{−iρn+​,−iρn−​} (L).​
(35)
Appropriate phase-weighted differences produce sin[pn​(s+1)]. For example the top test is e−ηn​[eipn​E+​(ρn−​)−e−ipn​E+​(ρn+​)]/(2i). The conjugate relation ensures a real system for real data. This is sine collocation of square modified Helmholtz global relations.
The functions φn​ are an orthonormal Fourier sine basis on [−1,1]. Set hj,n​=∫−11​hj​φn​ds and compute the known quantity Rj,n​=∫∂Ω​f∂n​vj,n​ds by numerical integration. Since φn​(±1)=0, each adjoint test vanishes on both adjacent sides. On its own side it equals φn​, and on the opposite side it equals δn​φn​, where δn​=e−2ηn​. Thus the square modified Helmholtz Dirichlet-to-Neumann coefficients obey
(1δn​​δn​1​)(hB,n​hT,n​​)=(RB,n​RT,n​​),(1δn​​δn​1​)(hL,n​hR,n​​)=(RL,n​RR,n​​).
(36)
Each block is inverted explicitly: for opposite sides j,j′,
hj,n​=1−δn2​Rj,n​−δn​Rj′,n​​.​
(37)
Compute these coefficients for 1≤n≤N and reconstruct hj(N)​=∑n=1N​hj,n​φn​. This is a semi-analytical scheme: the spectral basis integrals and two-by-two inverses are explicit, while the known boundary integrals are evaluated numerically. Increase N and the quadrature resolution until the desired convergence is observed; compatible smooth side data have convergent normal-trace expansions, while corner singularities require the usual weaker trace interpretation and more careful quadrature.
The requested weak interaction between sides is particularly clear: the adjacent-side unknown traces contribute exactly zero, and the opposite-side coefficient is e−2m2+(nπ/2)2​→0. The own-side coefficient stays equal to one. Thus diagonal dominance of paired square global-relation collocation is genuine after pairing; individual uncombined exponential samples need not have the same conditioning. The eigenvalues of each block are 1±δn​, so its condition number is (1+δn​)/(1−δn​).
There is also a direct localization check for an individual adjoint wave. On side j with outward unit normal nj​ and tangent tj​, the normalized test eη(nj​⋅r−1)+iptj​⋅r has η=m2+p2​. Its opposite-side magnitude is e−2η, and its magnitude integrates to at most 1/η along either adjacent side. This side localization of modified Helmholtz plane waves explains why suitable large spectral parameters suppress remote-side effects even before the exact sine cancellation.
Finally the interior solution can be evaluated from the computed traces using the two-dimensional modified Helmholtz fundamental solution
Γm​(r,s)=2π1​K0​(m∣r−s∣),q(r)=∫∂Ω​[Γm​(r,s)h(s)−f(s)∂ns​​Γm​(r,s)]ds.
(38)
Here K0​ is the Modified Bessel function of the second kind, and (−Δ+m2)Γm​=δ. Replace h by its computed finite Fourier sine series and use numerical integration; at interior points the boundary kernels are smooth. The sign follows from Green second identity with this fundamental-solution convention. This completes the numerical integration of the boundary value problem with a Dirichlet boundary condition, rather than stopping at an equation for its unknown boundary derivatives.
