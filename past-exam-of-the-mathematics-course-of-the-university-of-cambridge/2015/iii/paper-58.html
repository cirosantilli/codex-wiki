Write R=k/(μH), where H is the atomic mass unit used in the mean molecular weight convention, and let κ be the Rosseland mean opacity per unit mass. For a static spherical star with negligible thermal storage, the stellar structure equations are
drdm​=4πr2ρ,drdP​=−r2Gmρ​,drdL​=4πr2ρϵ,drdT​=−16πacr2T33κρL​,P=RρT.​
(1)
Here ϵ is the net stellar energy-generation rate per unit mass; a is the radiation energy-density constant and c the speed of light. The last differential equation follows from radiative diffusion, F=−(c/3κρ)d(aT4)/dr, with L=4πr2F. There is no gravothermal storage term in the specified stellar energy balance equation. Neglecting radiation pressure in force balance does not remove radiation as an energy carrier. Regular central conditions are m(0)=L(0)=0; take negligible external pressure, P(R)=0, for the analytic hydrostatic equilibrium model below.
Set x=r/R. Integrating the enclosed mass directly gives
m(r)=4πρc​(3r3​−4Rr4​),M=3πρc​R3​.
(2)
Thus
ρc​=πR33M​,m(r)=M(4x3−3x4).​
(3)
The stellar hydrostatic equation then gives
P(r)=4πGρc2​R2∫x1​(3t​−4t2​)(1−t)dt=4πGρc2​R2(1445​−6x2​+367x3​−16x4​).
(4)
Factorizing makes the surface behaviour transparent:
P(r)=Pc​(1−x)2(1+2x−59​x2),Pc​=365πGρc2​R2​=4πR45GM2​.​
(5)
The ideal gas equation of state gives the temperature profile
T(r)=Tc​(1−x)(1+2x−59​x2),Tc​=Rρc​Pc​​=12RR5GM​=12kR5μHGM​.​
(6)
No small-x approximation was needed. In particular, close to the centre,
m=4Mx3+O(x4),Pc​P​=1−524​x2+O(x3),Tc​T​=1+x−519​x2+O(x3).
(7)
These exact hydrostatic formulas reveal a limitation of the prescribed linear-density stellar model. Its mass density has a central cusp, and T′(0+)=Tc​/R>0. A positive opacity with positive outward luminosity would instead require T′<0 in radiative diffusion. Moreover, regular positive nuclear heating gives L=O(r3) and therefore T′=O(r) at the centre. Thus the imposed profile is a hydrostatic toy model, not a complete positive-heating radiative-equilibrium solution of all the stellar structure equations. The requested algebraic profiles and nuclear-luminosity integral can still be computed consistently as properties of that toy model.

L(r)=4πϵ0​ρc2​R3(T0​Tc​​)ν∫0r/R​x2(1−x)2+ν(1+2x−59​x2)νdx.

For a spherical hydrostatic star with negligible surface pressure, the stellar virial theorem is
Ω+3∫PdV=0.
(16)
For a monatomic nonrelativistic perfect gas, U=23​∫PdV, so 2U+Ω=0 and the total energy is E=U+Ω=Ω/2. Half the released binding energy raises the internal energy; only the other half can be radiated. With fixed mass, no nuclear supply and no external work, conservation of energy gives
L=−dtdE​=−21​dtdΩ​.
(17)
For the uniform-density stellar model, this becomes the contraction-luminosity evolution equation
L(t)=−10R23GM2​dtdR​,dtd​(R1​)=3GM210L(t)​.​
(18)
It relates luminosity to the contraction rate; it does not by itself prescribe L(t). Given L(t),
R(t)1​=R0​1​+3GM210​∫0t​L(t′)dt′.
(19)
For constant luminosity, R(t)=R0​/[1+t/t0​] with t0​=3GM2/(10R0​L). This describes quasi-static Kelvin-Helmholtz contraction, not dynamical free fall. If the gas has a different constant specific-heat ratio γg​, the appropriate stellar virial theorem is 3(γg​−1)U+Ω=0, so the radiated fraction of binding release is (3γg​−4)/[3(γg​−1)] rather than universally one half.

The conventional Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time is of order GM2/(RL). For the monatomic uniform-density stellar model, its precise accessible total-energy reservoir is ∣E∣=3GM2/(10R), so
tmodel​=L∣E∣​=10R⊙​L⊙​3GM⊙2​​≃9.4×106yr.​
(20)
Using M⊙​≃1.9885×1030kg, R⊙​≃6.957×108m and L⊙​≃3.828×1026W, the usual order-of-magnitude normalization without the structural factor is tKH​≃3.1×107yr. The full gravitational binding reservoir ∣Ω∣/L is about 1.9×107yr, but that overcounts the radiatable energy in virial equilibrium.
The Sun and the Solar system are approximately 4.6×109yr old, much older than any of these contraction estimates. Gravitational contraction cannot sustain the Sun's long-lived present power. The dominant long-term source is stellar nuclear fusion. Contraction can still supply transient luminosity, especially in a pre-main-sequence star. The timescale comparison rules out a contraction-only explanation over the observed age; it does not assert that all gravitational energy release is absent.

For a stellar polytrope of index n>0, write P=Kρ1+1/n and combine hydrostatic equilibrium with mass conservation to obtain
r21​drd​(ρr2​drdP​)=−4πGρ.
(21)
Introduce Lane-Emden variables for a stellar polytrope, ρ=ρc​θn, P=Pc​θn+1 and r=αξ, where
α2=4πGρc2​(n+1)Pc​​.
(22)
Since ρ−1dP/dr=(n+1)Pc​θ′/ρc​α, the mechanical equation reduces to the Lane-Emden equation
ξ21​dξd​(ξ2dξdθ​)=−θn,θ(0)=1,θ′(0)=0.​
(23)
A constant-density interior corresponds to the formal polytrope of index zero, with ρ=ρc​θ0=ρc​ where θ>0. At n=0, regularity first gives ξ2θ′=−ξ3/3, and a second integration gives
θ(ξ)=1−6ξ2​,ξ1​=6​,R=α6​.​
(24)
Here P=Pc​θ=Pc​(1−r2/R2) and α2=Pc​/(4πGρc2​), reproducing Pc​=2πGρc2​R2/3. The mass density jumps from its constant interior value to zero at the surface, while pressure vanishes continuously. The relation P=Kρ1+1/n is singular at n=0; the regular dimensionless pressure/mass density formulation defines this incompressible structural limit. It does not mean that the gas's perturbative stellar adiabatic exponent is infinite: the hydrostatic ideal-gas toy model and its adiabatic response are distinct choices.

Use the printed stellar gas-pressure fraction, β=Pg​/P, with P=Pg​+Prad​, and keep composition fixed. For a monatomic perfect gas plus equilibrium blackbody radiation, the specific heats of a monatomic gas-radiation mixture follow from its specific internal energy and specific enthalpy:
u=23​RT+ρaT4​,h=u+ρP​=25​RT+3ρ4aT4​.
(25)
At fixed total pressure, differentiating the equation of state gives
0=βdlogρ+(4−3β)dlogT,(∂logT∂logρ​)P​=−β4−3β​.
(26)
In particular, β is not held constant during that derivative. The specific heat capacity at constant pressure is cP​=(∂h/∂T)P​, so
cP​=25​R+3ρ4aT3​(4+β4−3β​).
(27)
Since aT3/(3ρ)=R(1−β)/β, this simplifies to
cP​=2β2R​(32−24β−3β2)=2μHβ2k​(32−24β−3β2).​
(28)
The pure-gas limit is 5R/2. For a gas with unspecified molecular degrees of freedom, replace 3R/2 in u by its gas heat capacity cV,g​: then cP​=cV,g​+R+4R(1−β)(4+β)/β2. The boxed formula uses the conventional monatomic stellar-gas interpretation.

The stellar adiabatic exponents are fixed-composition, constant-specific entropy derivatives:
Γ1​=(∂logρ∂logP​)s​,Γ2​Γ2​−1​=(∂logP∂logT​)s​,Γ3​−1=(∂logρ∂logT​)s​.​
(29)
The chain rule gives Γ1​(Γ2​−1)/Γ2​=Γ3​−1. Define the pressure derivatives χρ​=(∂logP/∂logρ)T​ and χT​=(∂logP/∂logT)ρ​. The first law of thermodynamics, with du=Tds+Pdρ/ρ2, gives
Γ3​−1=ρTcV​PχT​​,cP​−cV​=ρTP​χρ​χT2​​.
(30)
Combining this with dlogP=χρ​dlogρ+χT​dlogT on an adiabat gives
Γ1​=χρ​+χT​(Γ3​−1)=χρ​cV​cP​​,γ≡cV​cP​​=χρ​Γ1​​.​
(31)
Thus the specific-heat ratio is not generally equal to the three stellar adiabatic exponents.
For the mixture, χρ​=β and χT​=4−3β. The specific heat capacity at constant volume, obtained by differentiating u at fixed mass density, is
cV​=R2β24−21β​.
(32)
Therefore the adiabatic exponents of a monatomic gas-radiation mixture and its specific-heat ratio are
Γ3​−1Γ1​Γ2​γ​=24−21β8−6β​,=24−21β32−24β−3β2​,=24−18β−3β232−24β−3β2​,=β(24−21β)32−24β−3β2​=βΓ1​​.​​
(33)
If a relation involving only the exponents is wanted, eliminate β from Γ1​=β+(4−3β)(Γ3​−1):
γ=Γ1​−4Γ3​+4Γ1​(4−3Γ3​)​(0<β≤1).
(34)
For a pure monatomic perfect gas, β=1 and γ=Γ1​=Γ2​=Γ3​=5/3. For any calorically perfect gas with constant heat capacities, the same equality holds with its own γg​. In a genuine gas-radiation mixture, 0<β<1, γ=Γ1​/β while the exponents are given separately above.
In the radiation limit, Γ1​=Γ2​=Γ3​=4/3 and the adiabatic temperature gradient is 1/4. This follows independently from photon entropy: a comoving volume V has S∝VT3, so an adiabat obeys T∝V−1/3 and P∝V−4/3. However, cP​/cV​ is singular in the pure-radiation limit, not 4/3. This is the pure-radiation constant-pressure heat-capacity singularity. The equation P=aT4/3 fixes temperature whenever pressure is fixed, so an ordinary constant-pressure temperature derivative is not available; along the mixture limit γ→∞ and βγ→4/3. For photons alone, mass-specific quantities additionally require a material mass label. The often quoted radiation index 4/3 is its pressure-density adiabatic exponent, not a finite constant-pressure/constant-volume heat-capacity ratio.

Let g=Gm/r2>0, HP​=−dr/dlogP>0, ∇=dlogT/dlogP, and ∇μ​=dlogμ/dlogP. Consider a small radial fluid displacement ξ whose sound-crossing time is short enough to keep its pressure equal to its surroundings. Its composition is frozen and its heat exchange negligible. This is the local stellar convective stability test.
For gas-pressure-dominated ideal gas matter, ρ∝μP/T. The ambient gradient is therefore
drdlogρ​=−HP​1−∇+∇μ​​.
(35)
The displaced element conserves specific entropy and mean molecular weight, giving (dlogρ/dr)parcel​=−(1−∇ad​)/HP​, where ∇ad​=(Γ2​−1)/Γ2​. The parcel-minus-environment mass density difference is
ρρparcel​−ρambient​​=HP​∇ad​−∇+∇μ​​ξ.
(36)
Its buoyancy acceleration is minus g times this difference. Thus
ξ¨​+N2ξ=0,N2=HP​g​(∇ad​−∇+∇μ​).​
(37)
Positive stellar buoyancy frequency squared gives a restoring force. The gas-pressure-dominated Ledoux criterion is therefore
stable: ∇<∇ad​+∇μ​,unstable: ∇>∇ad​+∇μ​.​
(38)
Equality is marginal in this ideal adiabatic test. For monatomic gas ∇ad​=2/5. An inward increase of mean molecular weight has ∇μ​>0 and stabilizes the layer; uniform composition recovers the Schwarzschild criterion. For a radiative layer one substitutes the stellar radiative temperature gradient, ∇rad​=3κLP/(16πacGmT4).
More generally define δ=−(∂logρ/∂logT)P,μ​ and φ=(∂logρ/∂logμ)P,T​. The same displacement argument gives N2=(g/HP​)[δ(∇ad​−∇)+φ∇μ​] and stability for ∇<∇ad​+(φ/δ)∇μ​. Both coefficients equal one in the gas-dominated ideal-gas limit used above. This is local dynamical stability against convection; it is distinct from global radial collapse and from instabilities requiring heat or composition diffusion.

Take a static, nonrotating, nonmagnetic spherical equilibrium with ∇P=−ρ∇Φ and ∇2Φ=4πGρ. Use a fluid displacement ξ(r)e−iωt. Write p1​,ρ1​,ϕ1​ for Eulerian fluid perturbations. The Lagrangian pressure perturbation and corresponding mass density change obey ΔP=p1​+ξ⋅∇P, Δρ=ρ1​+ξ⋅∇ρ. Linearizing mass conservation, the Euler equations for an inviscid fluid and Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity gives
ρ1​−ω2ρξ∇2ϕ1​​=−∇⋅(ρξ),=−∇p1​−ρ1​∇Φ−ρ∇ϕ1​,=4πGρ1​.​​
(39)
There is no equilibrium acceleration to multiply a perturbed mass density. The adiabatic process condition, with composition carried by the parcel, closes the system:
PΔP​=Γ1​ρΔρ​,Δρ=−ρ∇⋅ξ,p1​=−Γ1​P∇⋅ξ−ξ⋅∇P.​
(40)
These are the complete linear adiabatic stellar oscillation equations, including the perturbation of self-gravity. Neglect of heat exchange is appropriate when oscillation periods are short compared with relevant thermal relaxation times; it does not determine nonadiabatic excitation or damping.
For the pressure and buoyancy modes, separate angular dependence using spherical harmonics:
ξ=ξr​(r)Yℓm​r^+ξh​(r)∇Ω​Yℓm​,(p1​,ρ1​,ϕ1​)=(pℓ​,ρℓ​,ϕℓ​)Yℓm​.
(41)
The horizontal amplitude ξh​ has dimensions of length. Angular differentiation gives ∇⋅ξ=[r−2(r2ξr​)′−ℓ(ℓ+1)ξh​/r]Yℓm​, and tangential momentum gives ω2rξh​=pℓ​/ρ+ϕℓ​. Define the adiabatic sound speed, stellar buoyancy frequency and Lamb frequency by
cs2​=ρΓ1​P​,N2=g(Γ1​1​drdlogP​−drdlogρ​),Sℓ2​=r2ℓ(ℓ+1)cs2​​.
(42)
The adiabatic mass density relation becomes ρℓ​/ρ=pℓ​/(ρcs2​)+(N2/g)ξr​. Substitution produces a radial form of the full oscillation equations for nonzero ω:
drdξr​​drdpℓ​​r21​drd​(r2drdϕℓ​​)−r2ℓ(ℓ+1)​ϕℓ​​=(cs2​g​−r2​)ξr​+ρcs2​1​(ω2Sℓ2​​−1)pℓ​+r2ω2ℓ(ℓ+1)​ϕℓ​,=ρ(ω2−N2)ξr​−cs2​g​pℓ​−ρdrdϕℓ​​,=4πG(cs2​pℓ​​+ρgN2​ξr​).​
(43)
The apparent N2/g factor is evaluated through its defining gradient at the centre rather than by dividing two zeros.
Regularity at the centre excludes singular solutions. At a free surface the Lagrangian pressure perturbation vanishes, ΔP=0; outside the star the gravitational perturbation decays as r−ℓ−1. For a model with mass density tending to zero at its surface, continuity of ϕℓ​ and its radial derivative gives ϕℓ′​(R)=−(ℓ+1)ϕℓ​(R)/R. If the equilibrium mass density jumps to vacuum, include the displaced-surface mass sheet: the outward-minus-inward derivative jump is 4πGρ(R)ξr​(R), so the interior condition is ϕℓ′​(R)=−(ℓ+1)ϕℓ​(R)/R−4πGρ(R)ξr​(R). An atmospheric boundary condition can replace the ideal free surface.
These conditions make ω2 an eigenvalue, not an arbitrary local sound frequency. With conservative boundary conditions the adiabatic operator is self-adjoint, giving real ω2; negative values describe instability. The frequencies depend on P(r),ρ(r),Γ1​(r), self-gravity, stratification and boundaries, as well as angular degree ℓ and radial order. In a spherical nonrotating star they are degenerate in m. For homologous equilibrium structures, their scale is
ω∼(R3GM​)1/2;​
(44)
thus the typical oscillation time measures inverse square root of mean mass density, while individual frequencies probe the interior adiabatic sound speed and stellar buoyancy frequency profiles.
For radial modes, write ξr​=rη. Eliminating the pressure and gravitational perturbations gives the radial stellar pulsation equation
drd​(Γ1​Pr4drdη​)+r3drd​[(3Γ1​−4)P]η+ρr4ω2η=0.​
(45)
This is a Sturm-Liouville problem. Multiplication by η and integration, with vanishing boundary terms, gives its Rayleigh quotient
ω2=∫0R​ρr4η2dr∫0R​Γ1​Pr4(η′)2dr−∫0R​r3[(3Γ1​−4)P]′η2dr​.
(46)
For constant Γ1​>4/3, P′<0 makes both numerator contributions nonnegative. At Γ1​=4/3, a homologous displacement is neutral; for constant Γ1​<4/3 the same trial displacement makes the quotient negative. For the uniform-density stellar model, η= constant is an exact mode and ω2=(3Γ1​−4)GM/R3. With varying Γ1​, the integral criterion, rather than a universal pointwise threshold, controls radial stability.
The Cowling approximation neglects ϕℓ​ while retaining the equilibrium gravitational field. It is useful for short-wavelength modes, but is not needed for the full derivation above. In a locally slowly varying region, take both remaining amplitudes proportional to ei∫kr​dr and retain the leading derivative terms. Then
ikr​ξr​≃ρcs2​Sℓ2​/ω2−1​pℓ​,ikr​pℓ​≃ρ(ω2−N2)ξr​.
(47)
Eliminating either amplitude gives the acoustic-gravity propagation relation
kr2​≃cs2​ω2(ω2−Sℓ2​)(ω2−N2)​.​
(48)
Positive kr2​ is oscillatory propagation; negative kr2​ means an evanescent wave. The high-frequency branch, ω2>Sℓ2​,N2, describes stellar acoustic modes, restored chiefly by compressibility and pressure. At frequencies well above N, this gives ω2≃cs2​[kr2​+ℓ(ℓ+1)/r2]. The low-frequency propagating branch in stable stratification, ω2<N2,Sℓ2​, instead describes stellar gravity modes, restored by buoyancy. At ℓ=0 there is no such nonradial gravity-wave cavity.
A stellar acoustic mode is trapped between an inner turning point near ω=Sℓ​ and an outer reflecting region. Low-ℓ modes penetrate deeply; radial modes reach the centre. Higher-degree modes turn farther out. Standing waves require the WKB quantization condition
∫r1​r2​​kr​dr≃π(n+α),
(49)
where the phase α depends on the central or turning-point behaviour and surface reflection. For high radial order and small degree, the leading acoustic travel-time result is the large frequency separation
Δν≃(2∫0R​cs​dr​)−1,νnℓ​≃Δν(n+2ℓ​+ε),ν=2πω​.​
(50)
The ℓ/2 term is the leading central angular phase shift; smaller frequency separations depend on detailed interior gradients. The near-surface mass density stratification sets the acoustic cutoff frequency. In a plane-parallel isothermal atmosphere with mass density scale height Hρ​, ωac​≃cs​/(2Hρ​). Modes below this cutoff can reflect and form a cavity; waves sufficiently above it escape and need an outgoing-wave boundary condition. Nonadiabatic damping, driving and rotation alter real-star mode properties, but the adiabatic frequency problem isolates their dependence on the equilibrium structure.
