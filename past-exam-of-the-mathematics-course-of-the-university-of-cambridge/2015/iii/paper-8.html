Write z=(x,v) and let Φ(t,s,z) denote the Hamiltonian flow from time s to time t. The characteristic curves satisfy Hamilton's equations:
X˙i​(t)=∂vi​​H(t,X(t),V(t)),V˙i​(t)=−∂xi​​H(t,X(t),V(t)),(X(s),V(s))=(x,v).​
(1)
The Hamiltonian Liouville equation then reduces along each curve to
dtd​f(t,X(t),V(t))=h(t,X(t),V(t)).
(2)
The signs and derivative variables here are those in the PDF.
The global characteristic flow for a Hamiltonian with bounded Hessian follows, for example, from H∈C2(R×R2d) and, for every finite T,
sup∣t∣≤T, z∈R2d​∥Dz2​H(t,z)∥≤LT​<∞,sup∣t∣≤T​∣∇z​H(t,0)∣≤AT​<∞.
(3)
Thus the Hamiltonian vector field b=(∇v​H,−∇x​H) is globally Lipschitz continuous in z on each finite time interval and satisfies ∣b(t,z)∣≤AT​+LT​∣z∣. The Picard-Lindelöf theorem gives local existence and uniqueness, while the Gronwall inequality gives, for example,
∣Φ(t,s,z)∣≤(∣z∣+AT​∣t−s∣)eLT​∣t−s∣(∣s∣,∣t∣≤T).
(4)
This excludes finite-time escape. There is a unique Hamiltonian flow for all finite forward and backward times, and Φ(s,t) is the inverse of Φ(t,s). These sufficient conditions are deliberately stronger than necessary.

For St​(z)=Φ(t,0,z), differentiability with respect to initial data gives the variational equation
Y′(t)=Dz​b(t,St​(z))Y(t),Y(0)=I2d​,Y(t)=Dz​St​(z).

The Jacobi determinant derivative formula gives

(6)
The mixed derivatives in the Hamiltonian vector field cancel:
divz​b=∑i=1d​(∂xi​​∂vi​​H−∂vi​​∂xi​​H)=0.

The same argument applies to Φ(t,s) for every starting time s. This is the Liouville theorem in Hamiltonian mechanics. Explicit time dependence of H does not affect the cancellation.

For an autonomous Hamiltonian function, the chain rule and Hamilton's equations give
dtd​H(X(t),V(t))=∇x​H⋅∇v​H−∇v​H⋅∇x​H=0.
(9)
Thus the energy is constant along each characteristic:
H(X(t),V(t))=H(x,v).​
(10)
The time-independent hypothesis is implicit in the displayed expression in this subpart. For the time-dependent Hamiltonian function allowed earlier, the correct Hamiltonian energy balance is instead
dtd​H(t,X(t),V(t))=∂t​H(t,X(t),V(t)).​
(11)
For example H(t,x,v)=t+∣v∣2/2 has a unique global Hamiltonian flow, but its value along a curve increases at unit rate. Thus conservation cannot be claimed for arbitrary time-dependent H.

Assume ω=0. With h=0, the method of characteristics gives f(t,Φ(t,0,z))=f0​(z). Hence
suppft​=Φ(t,0,suppf0​).

Let K=suppf0​ and E∗​=maxK​Hω​; if f0​=0, the conclusion is immediate. By the Hamiltonian energy balance, every image point of K remains in the energy sublevel

For the globally invertible Hamiltonian flow from (a), the general characteristic formula is
f(t,z)=f0​(Φ(0,t,z))+∫0t​h(Φ(s,t,z))ds.
(19)
Every Φ(s,t) preserves Lebesgue measure, by (b). Thus composition with it is an isometry of each Lp space. The Minkowski integral inequality gives
∥f(t)∥p​≤∥f0​∥p​+t∥h∥p​.​
(20)
Applying the Minkowski inequality also in time yields the finite-time Lp bound for Hamiltonian transport:
∥f∥Lp([0,T]×R2d)​≤T1/p∥f0​∥p​+(p+1Tp+1​)1/p∥h∥p​<∞.​
(21)
The smoothness assumptions allow the characteristic construction; the norm estimate itself only uses the Lp space data and volume preservation.
For a concrete failure on infinite time, choose ω=1, f0​=0, and
h(x,v)=e−(∣x∣2+∣v∣2)/2=e−H1​(x,v).
(22)
This is smooth, time independent and in every finite Lp space; it is invariant under the isotropic harmonic oscillator flow. Therefore the solution grows linearly:

(23)
This invariant-source secular growth in Hamiltonian transport supplies the counterexample even with zero initial data.

Work in the Banach space BT​ of bounded strongly measurable maps [0,T]→Lx,v2​, with norm ∥g∥BT​​=sup0≤t≤T​∥g(t)∥2​. Keeping actual representatives at every time matches the pointwise-in-time mild formulation. The Boltzmann Volterra operator is bounded on this space, and the factorial bound for a Volterra iterate gives
∥τn∥BT​→BT​​≤n!(CT)n​.
(36)
Thus the Volterra series for the linear Boltzmann equation converges in operator norm for every finite T, even when CT≥1. Set
f=n=0∑∞​τnF(f0​,a).​
(37)
For its partial sums, (I−τ)∑n=0N​τnF=F−τN+1F. The remainder tends to zero by the factorial estimate. Hence (I−τ)f=F, precisely the required characteristic integral equation.
The norm bound in (b) gives an explicit choice of the existence constant:

Let f1​,f2​ have the same initial data and satisfy the characteristic integral equation. Their difference g=f1​−f2​ satisfies g=τg, hence g=τng for every n.
