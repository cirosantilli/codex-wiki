Write mX​=EX1​ and vX​=Var(X1​). For the random sum of independent claims, conditioning on N gives
E[S∣N]=NmX​,Var(S∣N)=NvX​.
(1)
The law of total expectation and the law of total variance therefore give the aggregate moments
ES=(EN)mX​,Var(S)=(EN)vX​+Var(N)mX2​.​
(2)
The first term in the variance measures variation of the individual claims at a fixed count; the second measures variation of the count itself. These formulas require the indicated moments to be finite.
For the moment-generating function, independent random variables give
E[etS∣N]=MX​(t)N,MS​(t)=GN​(MX​(t)),​
(3)
where GN​(z)=E[zN] is the probability generating function. This identity holds wherever the expectations are finite; in particular a moment-generating function need not exist for positive t for an arbitrary positive claim distribution. The empty sum for N=0 is zero.

To distinguish the random intensity from its possible values, write it as Λ. Its law is a gamma distribution with shape 2 and rate p/q. Hence
λ0​=EΛ=p2q​​,Var(Λ)=p22q2​.
(6)
For the Poisson mixture, conditional expectation and conditional variance both equal Λ. The law of total expectation and the law of total variance yield
EN=p2q​,Var(N)=EΛ+Var(Λ)=p22q​,
(7)
where p+q=1. Applying the random sum of independent claims formulas with the exponential distribution of the claim sizes gives the portfolio B moments
ESB​=p2qμ​,Var(SB​)=p22q(1+p)μ2​.​
(8)
At the matched intensity λ0​, the expected value for portfolio A is also 2qμ/p, whereas its variance is 4qμ2/p. Thus the expected totals agree, but mixing increases the variance:
Var(SB​)−Var(SA​)=p22q2μ2​>0.​
(9)
The extra term is precisely μ2Var(Λ).

Conditioning on the intensity in the Poisson mixture gives
GN​(z)=E[eΛ(z−1)]=(1−qzp​)2.
(10)
Thus N has the negative binomial distribution with two successes and success probability p, counting failures; explicitly P(N=j)=(j+1)p2qj for j≥0. The probability generating function is finite for real z<1/q.
The claim-size moment-generating function is MX​(t)=(1−μt)−1. Substituting into the aggregate moment-generating function and using p+q=1 gives
MS​(t)=(p−μtp(1−μt)​)2​,t<μp​.
(11)
Put L(t)=p/(p−μt), the moment-generating function of an exponential distribution with rate p/μ. The identity
p−μtp(1−μt)​=p+qL(t)
(12)
then yields
MS​(t)=p2+2pqL(t)+q2L(t)2.
(13)
The gamma-mixed Poisson aggregate with exponential claims has three nonnegative mixture weights summing to one. By uniqueness of the moment-generating function near zero, the aggregate distribution is
L(S)=p2δ0​+2pqExp(p/μ)+q2Gamma(2,rate p/μ).​
(14)
Here δ0​ is the Dirac measure at zero. In particular P(S=0)=p2, consistently with P(N=0). The positive components have respective expected values μ/p and 2μ/p; their mixture distribution accounts for the possibility of no aggregate payout.

For quota share reinsurance, each claim and therefore its aggregate are retained in the same proportion. For aggregate stop loss reinsurance, the insurer pays the aggregate up to the retention, and the reinsurer pays the excess. Thus the insurer's payouts are
SI∗​=αS,SI​=min(S,M)=S−(S−M)+​.​
(15)
The subscript + denotes the positive part. The stop loss contract here applies to the annual aggregate, rather than separately to each claim.

The expected value and variance under quota share reinsurance follow by scaling the exponential distribution:
ESI∗​=αμS​,Var(SI∗​)=α2μS2​.​
(16)
The retained stop loss moments for an exponential aggregate follow from the payout Y=min(S,M), its survival function equals e−x/μS​ for 0≤x<M and zero for x≥M. The tail integral formula for moments gives, with m=M/μS​ and r=e−m,
EY=∫0M​e−x/μS​dx=μS​(1−r),EY2=2∫0M​xe−x/μS​dx=2μS2​[1−(1+m)r].
(17)
Consequently the retained moments are
ESI​=μS​(1−e−M/μS​),Var(SI​)=μS2​[1−2(M/μS​)e−M/μS​−e−2M/μS​].​
(18)
Matching the two expected values forces α=1−r, which lies strictly between zero and one. The difference of the variances simplifies to
Var(SI∗​)−Var(SI​)=2μS2​e−M/μS​(μS​M​−1+e−M/μS​)≥0.​
(19)
Indeed h(m)=m−1+e−m has h(0)=0 and h′(m)=1−e−m≥0 for m≥0. Because M>0, the difference is actually positive. At equal retained expected value, aggregate stop loss reinsurance reduces the variance more than quota share reinsurance.

Write Y=g(S) and Z=min(S,M). Their common expected value is c. Expanding about the retention gives the requested variance identity
E[(Y−M)2]−(M−c)2=E[Y2]−2Mc+M2−(M2−2Mc+c2)=Var(Y)​.
(20)
The stop loss variance minimization principle follows from a pointwise comparison. For 0≤x≤M, the constraint 0≤g(x)≤x implies
∣g(x)−M∣=M−g(x)≥M−x=∣min(x,M)−M∣.
(21)
For x>M, the squared distance of min(x,M)=M from M is zero, so the same squared-distance comparison is immediate. Therefore
E[(Y−M)2]≥E[(Z−M)2].
(22)
Subtracting the same (M−c)2 proves optimality of the retained stop loss payout:
Var(g(S))≥Var(min(S,M)).​
(23)
Since Z is bounded, its variance is finite; if E[Y2]=∞, the inequality remains valid with infinite variance on the left. When it is finite, equality requires g(S)=min(S,M) almost surely, because the pointwise squared-distance inequality is strict whenever the two payouts differ.

Use cprem​ for the premium income rate, reserving c later for the smaller exponential decay rate. In the classical risk model, the surplus is
Ut​=u+cprem​t−∑j=1Nt​​Xj​,cprem​=(1+ρ)λμ.
(24)
Here ρ is the relative safety loading. The aggregate claims form a Compound Poisson process. Define Lt​=∑j=1Nt​​Xj​−cprem​t, so ruin occurs when Lt​>u. By independent increments and the exponential formula for a marked Poisson sum,
E[eR(Lt​−Ls​)]=exp{(t−s)[λ(M(R)−1)−cprem​R]}=1.
(25)
Thus Zt​=eRLt​ is a nonnegative continuous-time martingale with Z0​=1, because the adjustment coefficient makes the exponent vanish.
Let τ=inf{t:Ut​<0}. Apply the optional stopping theorem at the bounded stopping time τ∧T. On {τ≤T}, Lτ​>u, so
1=E[Zτ∧T​]≥eRuP(τ≤T).
(26)
Letting T increase proves the Lundberg inequality and the unscaled limit:
ψ(u)≤e−Ru,u→∞lim​ψ(u)=0.​
(27)
For the precise asymptotic, put
h=cprem​λμ​=1+ρ1​,z(u)=eRuψ(u),k(x)=heRxfI​(x),g(u)=heRu∫u∞​fI​(x)dx.
(28)
The given exponential integral identity makes k a probability density. Multiplying the given defective renewal equation by eRu turns it into the ordinary renewal equation
z(u)=g(u)+∫0u​z(u−x)k(x)dx.
(29)
For clarity, the version of the key renewal theorem used here is: if the interarrival law is nonarithmetic, has mean mk​∈(0,∞), and g is directly Riemann integrable, the locally bounded solution of this renewal equation satisfies z(u)→mk−1​∫0∞​g(v)dv. The infinite-mean version gives zero for nonnegative directly Riemann integrable g.
All the hypotheses can be checked here. The density k gives a nonarithmetic distribution. The Tonelli theorem gives
∫0∞​g(u)du=h∫0∞​fI​(x)ReRx−1​dx=R1−h​.
(30)
Furthermore
g(u)=∫u∞​e−R(x−u)k(x)dx,g′(u)=Rg(u)−k(u)almost everywhere.
(31)
Thus g is continuous and integrable, and ∫0∞​∣g′(u)∣du≤R∫g+1<∞. On a mesh of width δ, the difference between its upper and lower sums is at most δ∫∣g′∣; its upper sum is at most ∫g+δ∫∣g′∣. This proves direct Riemann integrability rather than assuming it. Also 0≤z(u)≤1 by the Lundberg inequality, so the solution is locally bounded. Its renewal representation is z=g∗Uk​, where Uk​=∑j≥0​K∗j and K(dx)=k(x)dx; the residual after iteration tends to zero on compact intervals because sums of positive interarrivals tend to infinity.
Writing J=∫0∞​xeRxfI​(x)dx, the tilted interarrival expected value is mk​=hJ. The key renewal theorem gives the Cramér–Lundberg ruin asymptotic
u→∞lim​eRuψ(u)=RhJ1−h​=R∫0∞​xeRxfI​(x)dxρ​=A.​
(32)
If J=∞, the same formula is interpreted as A=0. A positive finite asymptotic constant requires J<∞; this extra integrability is not explicitly stated in the paper.
For the final two-exponential case, evaluate the defective renewal equation at zero:
h=ψ(0)=a+b,ρ=a+b1−a−b​​.
(33)
One can identify the adjustment coefficient without silently assuming A>0. For 0<r<c, set
P(r)=∫0∞​eruψ(u)du=c−ra​+d−rb​.
(34)
It is finite and positive. Integrating the nonnegative terms of the defective renewal equation, using the Tonelli theorem, first shows that FI​(r)=∫0∞​erxfI​(x)dx is finite and then gives
P(r)=rh​[FI​(r)−1]+hP(r)FI​(r),hFI​(r)=1+rP(r)h+rP(r)​.
(35)
As r↑c, P(r)→∞ because a>0. By monotone convergence theorem, hFI​(c)=1. The integrated tail distribution in the classical risk model has density fI​(x)=P(X1​>x)/μ, so FI​(r)=[M(r)−1]/(μr) by the tail integral formula for moments. Hence c solves the adjustment equation, and its stipulated uniqueness implies R=c. Finally the displayed form of ψ gives the remaining constants
R=c,A=a,ρ=a+b1−a−b​.​
(36)
In particular the decay exponent d and the coefficient b do not affect R or A. The c in these final answers is the printed decay rate, not cprem​.

In the Bühlmann model, a latent risk parameter Θ is drawn from a population distribution. Conditional on Θ, the yearly observations are independent and identically distributed random variables, with conditional expectation m(Θ) and conditional variance v(Θ). Define the structural parameters
m=E[m(Θ)],v=E[v(Θ)],a=Var(m(Θ)).
(37)
Here v is the expected process variance, while a is the variance of hypothetical means. The Bühlmann credibility premium is the best affine estimate of m(Θ) from the observed claims, under mean squared error. Predicting the next claim gives the same affine estimate: the extra conditional observation noise contributes the constant v to the prediction error.
The law of total variance and conditional independence give
Var(Xj​)=a+v,Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=a(i=j),Cov(m(Θ),Xj​)=a.
(38)
An affine estimate can be written as m=m+∑j=1n​bj​(Xj​−m): for any chosen bj​, optimizing the constant makes its expected value equal to m. The normal equations for the linear least-squares projection are
vbi​+a∑j=1n​bj​=a,i=1,…,n.
(39)
For v>0 they force all bi​ to agree, with bi​=a/(v+na). Thus the credibility factor and premium are
Z=v+nana​=n+v/an​,m=ZX+(1−Z)m,​X=n1​∑j=1n​Xj​.
(40)
The credibility factor increases with the observation count and between-risk variance, and decreases with within-risk variance. If a=0, the risk mean is known and Z=0; if v=0 and a>0, one observation reveals it and Z=1. If both vanish, the premium is the fixed value m and the factor is immaterial.
In the specified model, the conditional law is a gamma distribution with shape α and scale θ. Therefore
m(θ)=αθ,v(θ)=αθ2.
(41)
The prior is an inverse-gamma distribution with shape k and scale λ. To obtain its moments directly, substitute y=λ/θ in the defining integral, obtaining
E[Θr]=λrΓ(k)Γ(k−r)​,r<k.
(42)
The Gamma function recurrence yields
EΘ=k−1λ​,EΘ2=(k−1)(k−2)λ2​,Var(Θ)=(k−1)2(k−2)λ2​.
(43)
The assumption k>2 makes both structural variances finite. Hence
m=k−1αλ​,v=(k−1)(k−2)αλ2​,a=(k−1)2(k−2)α2λ2​,av​=αk−1​.
(44)
It follows that the model-specific credibility estimate is
Z=nα+k−1nα​,mB​=ZX+(1−Z)k−1αλ​=k+nα−1α(λ+∑j=1n​Xj​)​.​
(45)
For the Bayes estimator under squared error loss, the quantity to estimate is αΘ, so the optimum is its posterior mean. The likelihood function, viewed as a function of θ, is proportional to
θ−nαexp(−θ∑j​xj​​).
(46)
Multiplication by the prior shows gamma scale inverse-gamma conjugacy:
Θ∣x1​,…,xn​∼InvGamma(k+nα,λ+∑j​xj​).
(47)
Although the printed hint only mentions integer shapes, the same substitution and Gamma integral normalize this posterior for every positive real shape, so no integrality of α is needed. Its posterior mean gives the Bayesian estimate and comparison
mBayes​=αE[Θ∣X1​,…,Xn​]=k+nα−1α(λ+∑j=1n​Xj​)​=mB​.​
(48)
This exact Bühlmann credibility for gamma claims holds for every observed sample, not merely on average. Here the posterior mean is affine in the sample mean, so the best affine Bühlmann credibility premium is also the unrestricted Bayes estimator under squared error loss.
