The integers are countable: j(z)=2z+1 for z≥0 and j(z)=−2z for z<0 is an injection Z→N. Hence Z×N is a countable set. The map (a,b)↦a/b is a surjection onto the rational numbers, so the image result already proved gives Q is countable. Unique reduced fractions are not required for this surjection argument.
For the final unheaded geometry request, suppose for contradiction that no two distinct circles in the collection intersect. Restrict to the subcollection C0​ of circles tangent to the x-axis. Each such nondegenerate circle has a unique tangency point, and the tangency-point map C0​→R is a surjection by the hypothesis.
The closed disk of a circle tangent at (a,0) meets the x-axis only at (a,0). Two distinct members of C0​ cannot have nested disks: if one disk were inside the other, its tangency point would belong to the larger disk, so both tangency points would be equal and both circle boundaries would pass through it. That already contradicts nonintersection. Circles with disjoint boundaries and no nesting have disjoint disks. Thus their open disks are pairwise disjoint.
By the density of the rational numbers, every nonempty open disk contains a point of Q2, which is a countable set. Fix an enumeration of Q2 and assign to each disk the first enumerated point inside it. Disjointness makes this assignment an injection. This is the countability of pairwise disjoint open disks, and proves that C0​ is a countable set. Its surjection onto R would make the real numbers countable, contrary to the Cantor diagonal argument. Therefore
The collection contains two distinct intersecting circles.​
(22)
The proof allows circles on either side of the axis and counts tangential contact as intersection; it does not assume that all circles in the original collection are tangent to the axis.
