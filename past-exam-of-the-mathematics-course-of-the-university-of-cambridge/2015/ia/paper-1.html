Denote the four displayed matrices, from left to right, by T1​,T2​,T3​,T4​. An orthogonal matrix has an orthonormal set of columns, equivalently TTT=I. Direct column dot products give TjT​Tj​=I for j=1,2,4. The first column of T3​ has squared Euclidean norm (1+6+1)/6=4/3, so the third matrix is the unique nonorthogonal matrix.
For T1​, the determinant is 1. A nonidentity orthogonal transformation of R3 with determinant 1 is a rotation: it has a fixed axis and restricts to a plane rotation on its orthogonal complement. Consequently T1​ is the rotation.
For T2​, calculation gives
detT2​=−1,det(T2​−I)=−34​.
(7)
A plane reflection fixes its entire plane, and therefore has eigenvalue 1. Since T2​−I is invertible, T2​ is not a plane reflection. Thus T2​ is the combination of a rotation and a reflection. More explicitly, (1,−2,0)T is an eigenvector with eigenvalue −1. On the perpendicular plane, T2​ is a rotation with cosϕ=2/3, because trT2​=−1+2cosϕ=1/3. This gives a three-dimensional improper orthogonal transformation.
For T4​, set n=(1,2,2)T/3. Then
T4​=I−2nnT.
(8)
This reflection matrix reverses the normal n and fixes every vector perpendicular to n. Therefore T4​ is reflection in the plane x+2y+2z=0. In particular, detT4​=−1 and det(T4​−I)=0. Finally T3​ represents none of the three listed orthogonal transformations, since each preserves the Euclidean norm.

A complex power series with radius of convergence R converges absolutely at every z with ∣z∣<R and diverges at every z with ∣z∣>R. This makes no general assertion at ∣z∣=R: boundary points must be examined separately. The series always converges at z=0. If R=0, no nonzero point lies in the convergence disk; if R=∞, the series converges absolutely throughout the complex plane.
If p is the zero polynomial, every coefficient vanishes, so R=∞. Otherwise let d be its degree and c=0 its leading coefficient. Since p(n)=cnd(1+o(1)),
​p(n)p(n+1)​​⟶1.
(13)
The ratio test therefore proves absolute convergence when ∣z∣<1. For ∣z∣>1, the terms p(n)zn do not tend to zero, so the series diverges. 2(α−1)(t−(α+1)u)=0.
(21)
For α=1, this forces t=(α+1)u, v=−2u and w=3u. Hence

(22)

For α=1, the parameters u,t are free and v=2u−2t, w=−3u+3t. Thus

The intermediate value theorem says that if f:[a,b]→R is a continuous function, then every value between f(a) and f(b) is attained at some point of [a,b].
Endpoint values are already attained. Suppose f(a)<y<f(b) and define E={x∈[a,b]:f(x)<y}. It is nonempty and bounded above, so c=supE exists. Continuity at a ensures that E contains points to the right of a, and continuity at b ensures that no point sufficiently close to b belongs to E. Hence a<c<b.
By the definition of the supremum, there are points of E approaching c. If f(c)>y, continuity would exclude all such nearby points from E, a contradiction. If f(c)<y, continuity would put some point to the right of c in E, again a contradiction. Therefore f(c)=y. When f(a)>f(b), apply this argument to −f. This proves the intermediate value theorem in all cases.

For a chord of length 1/2, consider the continuous function h(x)=f(x+1/2)−f(x) on [0,1/2]. Since f(0)=f(1),
h(1/2)=f(1)−f(1/2)=−h(0).
(49)
An endpoint zero already supplies the chord. Otherwise the endpoint values have opposite signs, so the intermediate value theorem supplies h(α)=0 at some α∈(0,1/2). Thus a horizontal chord of length 1/2 exists.
For each integer n>1, define hn​(x)=f(x+1/n)−f(x) on [0,1−1/n]. The sampled differences telescope:

If any sampled value is zero, it gives the required chord. Otherwise there must be both a positive and a negative sampled value. The intermediate value theorem, applied between their sample points, then gives hn​(α)=0. For a partition P, write U(f,P) and L(f,P) for the upper Darboux sum and lower Darboux sum. The Riemann integrability criterion for bounded f is that for every ε>0 some partition satisfies U(f,P)−L(f,P)<ε. Thus if the gaps for Dn​ tend to zero, the Riemann integrability criterion immediately proves that f is Riemann integrable.
Conversely, suppose f is Riemann integrable, and choose a fixed partition P with U(f,P)−L(f,P)<ε/2. Let r be the number of its interior division points and choose M≥1 with ∣f∣≤M. Call a cell of Dn​ bad when its interior contains a division point of P. There are at most r bad cells, and their total length is at most r/n.
Every other cell lies in a single cell of P, so its oscillation is no larger than that of the containing cell. Summing the contributions of these good cells gives at most U(f,P)−L(f,P), while each bad cell has oscillation at most 2M. Therefore the finite bad-cell estimate for Darboux sums gives
0≤U(f,Dn​)−L(f,Dn​)≤U(f,P)−L(f,P)+n2Mr​.
(51)
For large enough n, the right-hand side is less than ε. This proves the uniform-mesh Darboux criterion
f is Riemann integrable⟺U(f,Dn​)−L(f,Dn​)⟶0.​
(52)
This argument does not assume that the partitions Dn​ are nested; in general they are not.
For the composition, f takes values in [−M,M]. Since g is continuously differentiable, the extreme value theorem bounds both ∣g∣ and ∣g′∣ on that interval. In particular, let K=sup[−M,M]​∣g′∣<∞. The mean value theorem states that a function continuous on the interval between s,t and differentiable in its interior satisfies g(s)−g(t)=g′(ξ)(s−t) for some intermediate ξ. Hence
∣g(s)−g(t)∣≤K∣s−t∣(s,t∈[−M,M]).
(53)
On each partition cell, this bounds the oscillation of g∘f by K times the oscillation of f, whether or not the local extrema are attained. It follows that
U(g∘f,Dn​)−L(g∘f,Dn​)≤K(U(f,Dn​)−L(f,Dn​))⟶0.
(54)
The composition is bounded, so the proved uniform-mesh Darboux criterion applies. Thus g∘f is Riemann integrable. The reusable fact is Lipschitz composition preserves Riemann integrability; continuous differentiability supplies the required bound on the range of f.
