Let Dx=λx with x=0. Commutation gives
D(Cx)=C(Dx)=λCx.
(32)
Thus Cx belongs to the eigenspace Eλ​(D), with the zero vector allowed. Since D has n distinct eigenvalues, each eigenspace is one-dimensional. Hence Cx=μx for some scalar μ. This is the fact that commuting maps preserve eigenspaces.
The standard linear independence theorem for eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues supplies a basis x1​,…,xn​ of eigenvectors of D. The preceding argument makes every one an eigenvector of C as well. Their column matrix therefore simultaneously diagonalizes C and D. This is simple spectrum gives simultaneous diagonalization.
For real matrices, this conclusion always holds over C; a real common basis follows when the distinct eigenvalues of D are real. Real matrix entries alone do not ensure a real eigenbasis: a quarter-turn of R2, with C=D, has eigenvalues ±i. The matrices in this question have real spectra, so their common basis is real.
