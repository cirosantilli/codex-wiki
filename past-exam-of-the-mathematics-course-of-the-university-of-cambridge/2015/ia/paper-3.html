The two-involution characterization of a dihedral group uses the extended convention that includes C2​ and the infinite dihedral group; the two generators need not be distinct. Put a=xy and b=y. The inversion relation gives
a2=xyxy=x(yxy−1)y2=xx−1=e,b2=e,x=ab.
(1)
If a=e, both a,b are involutions and generate the original group. If a=e, then x=y and the group is C2​; choose the same nonidentity element twice. Thus two elements of order two generate the group, including the degenerate case allowed here. The distinction between square equal to the identity and order of a group element exactly two is essential.

Let N be a normal subgroup and let π:G→G/N be the quotient group homomorphism. By the first part, G=⟨a,b⟩ for involutions a,b, so the quotient group is generated by π(a),π(b), whose squares are the identity. If both images are nonidentity, they are involutions and the second part applies. If only one image is nonidentity, it generates C2​; choose it twice. Both images cannot be trivial because the quotient group is nontrivial. Hence every nontrivial quotient is dihedral in the stated extended convention. This is the nontrivial-quotient closure of two-involution groups.

Every finite cyclic subgroup of order m has exactly φ(m) generators, where φ is the Euler totient function: if g has order m, then gk generates precisely when gcd(k,m)=1. Each element generates exactly one cyclic subgroup. Thus counting cyclic subgroups by their generators amounts to counting elements by order of a group element and dividing by φ(m).
Use the cycle decomposition of a permutation in S5​. A permutation with aj​ cycles of length j occurs 5!/∏j​(jaj​aj​!) times: order the five letters, then divide by the cyclic choices of starting point and the orderings of equal-length cycles. Its order is the least common multiple of the cycle lengths. The resulting count is
cycle type1521322131232415​elements1101520203024​order1223645​cyclic subgroups110151010156​​1+10+15+10+10+15+6=67.​

Lagrange's theorem says that, for a finite group G and subgroup H,
∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣,∣H∣∣∣G∣.​
(29)
Here [G:H] is the number of left cosets. Every g∈G lies in gH. If gH and g′H meet, write gh=g′h′; then g′=gh(h′)−1, so g′H=gH. Hence the left cosets partition G. The bijection h↦gh makes each coset have ∣H∣ elements. Adding their cardinalities proves Lagrange's theorem.

The identity belongs to the center of a group. If z,w commute with every g, then zw does too; rearranging gz=zg also shows that z−1 commutes with g. Hence Z(G) is a subgroup. Moreover gzg−1=z for z∈Z(G), so it is a normal subgroup.
Suppose ∣G∣=pn>1. The conjugation action partitions G into conjugacy classes. The orbit-stabilizer theorem gives class size [G:CG​(x)], where CG​(x) is the centralizer of x. By Lagrange's theorem, this size is a power of p; it is one exactly when x is central. Thus the class equation has the form
∣G∣=∣Z(G)∣+∑noncentral classes​pmi​,mi​≥1.
(34)
It follows that p∣∣Z(G)∣. Since the identity is central, ∣Z(G)∣≥1, and therefore
∣Z(G)∣≥p,Z(G)={e}.​
(35)
This is the nontrivial-centre property of a finite p-group.

Write the Heisenberg group element as M(x,y,z). Matrix multiplication and inversion give
M(x,y,z)M(x′,y′,z′)=M(x+x′, y+y′+xz′, z+z′),M(x,y,z)−1=M(−x,−y+xz,−z).

Z(H)={M(0,y,0):y∈R}.​

Stokes theorem equates the circulation around the induced boundary orientation of an oriented surface to the normal flux of curl:
∮∂S​F⋅dx=∫S​(∇×F)⋅dS.​
(50)
The usual version requires a piecewise smooth oriented surface and a vector field continuously differentiable on a neighbourhood of it. We use the upward orientation wherever the surface is a graph; each outer circle is counterclockwise viewed from above, and an inner circle is clockwise.
Write ρ=(x2+y2)1/2. The surface of revolution has z=ρ2+1−λ​ with 0≤z≤1. For 0<λ<1, it is a disk-shaped cap of a two-sheeted hyperboloid, running from ρ=0,z=1−λ​ to ρ=λ​,z=1. For λ=1, it is a right circular cone, z=ρ, including its apex. For λ>1, it is an annular strip of a one-sheet hyperboloid, between the circles ρ=λ−1​,z=0 and ρ=λ​,z=1.
For z>0, differentiation of the graph gives zx​=x/z and zy​=y/z, so the upward vector surface element of a graph and its scalar area are
dS=(−zx​,−zy​,1)dxdy,dS=1+z2x2+y2​​dxdy.​
(51)
For λ>1 this graph expression is singular at the lower circle because the surface has a vertical tangent there, rather than a singular geometric surface. A regular parametrization is
X(z,ϕ)=(z2+λ−1​cosϕ,z2+λ−1​sinϕ,z),Xz​×Xϕ​=−ρeρ​+zez​.
(52)
It proves that the graph surface integral is interpreted by its integrable endpoint limit. The same parametrization works away from the pole or apex in the other regimes.
Direct differentiation of the first vector field gives
∇×F=(0,0,2).​
(53)
Its surface integral is twice the projected area, namely 2πλ for 0<λ≤1 and 2π[λ−(λ−1)]=2π for λ>1. On an oriented circle of radius R, F⋅dx=R2dϕ. The outer circulation is 2πλ; an inner circle, when present, contributes −2π(λ−1). Thus
∮∂S​F⋅dx=∫S​(∇×F)⋅dS=2πmin(λ,1).​
(54)
At λ=1, excise a small apex circle of radius ε, apply Stokes theorem to the smooth truncated cone, and let ε→0. The removed flux and its inner circulation are both 2πε2, so the limiting equality holds. This avoids silently applying a smooth-surface theorem at the cone's nonsmooth apex.
The second vector field is the azimuthal inverse-radius vector field, G=eϕ​/ρ. Away from the axis,
∂x​(x2+y2x​)=(x2+y2)2y2−x2​=∂y​(x2+y2−y​),∇×G=0(ρ>0).​
(55)
The other two curl components vanish because the field is independent of z and has zero z component. On any positively oriented circle around the axis, G⋅dx=dϕ, so its circulation is 2π, independent of radius. Therefore
∮∂S​G⋅dx={2π,0,​0<λ≤1,λ>1.​​
(56)
For λ>1 the surface avoids the axis, so Stokes theorem applies directly and returns zero curl flux. For 0<λ<1 the disk cap meets the axis, and for λ=1 the apex lies on it: G is undefined there, so the neighbourhood hypothesis of Stokes theorem fails. Removing a small circle gives cancelling inner and outer circulations; the inner circulation remains −2π as its radius tends to zero. Hence it cannot be discarded as it could for F. A nonzero circulation here is compatible with zero curl away from the excluded axis.

∇⋅(ψ∇ϕ)=∇ψ⋅∇ϕ+ψ∇2ϕ,(ψ∇ϕ)⋅n=ψ∂n∂ϕ​.
(57)

∫V​(ψ∇2ϕ+∇ψ⋅∇ϕ)dV=∫∂V​ψ∂n∂ϕ​dS.​
(58)
The normal is outward. This identifies the boundary term and its sign directly from the divergence theorem.

For the usual Dirichlet problem on a bounded regular volume, suppose ϕ1​,ϕ2​ both satisfy the stated data. Their difference u=ϕ1​−ϕ2​ is a harmonic function, with ∇2u=0 in V and u=0 on ∂V. In Green's first identity, choose both functions equal to u:
∫V​∣∇u∣2dV=∫∂V​u∂n∂u​dS=0.
(59)
The nonnegative continuous integrand forces ∇u=0. Thus u is constant on each connected component, and its zero boundary values force each constant to vanish. This proves uniqueness for the Dirichlet problem.
If the boundary value is a constant c, the constant function c is itself a harmonic function with those data. Uniqueness therefore yields
ϕ≡c throughout V.​
(60)
Boundedness, or an appropriate condition at infinity replacing it, is essential. On the upper half-space both u=0 and u=z are harmonic functions with zero data on its boundary plane. They show why unrestricted uniqueness must not be asserted for an unbounded volume.

Use the Cartesian second-rank tensor transformation convention T′=RTRT, where R is the orthogonal matrix converting components to the new orthonormal basis. The coordinate half-turns are
Rx​=diag(1,−1,−1),Ry​=diag(−1,1,−1),Rz​=diag(−1,−1,1).
(65)
For a diagonal rotation R=diag(s1​,s2​,s3​), invariance gives tij​=si​sj​tij​, with no summation in this equation. For each i=j, one of the listed half-turns makes si​sj​=−1, so tij​=−tij​=0. Thus
T=diag(t11​,t22​,t33​).​
(66)
This is coordinate half-turn invariance of a second-rank tensor. It does not force equal diagonal entries; invariance under all rotations would be the stronger isotropic second-rank tensor condition.
