Write the composite number as n=ab, with 2≤a≤b<n. If a<b, both a and b occur as distinct factors of the factorial (n−1)!, so their product n divides that factorial.
If a=b, then n=a2 and n>4 implies a≥3. Both a and 2a are distinct factors in (n−1)!, because 2a≤a2−1. Their product is 2n, so again n divides the factorial. Hence
(n−1)!≡0(modn)for every composite n>4.​
(2)
The exception n=4 matters: its factorial 3!=6 is not divisible by 4. This argument handles perfect squares without incorrectly counting the same factor twice.

The Chinese remainder theorem says that for pairwise coprime integers m1​,…,mr​>0, any prescribed residue modulo each mi​ is realized by exactly one residue class modulo ∏i​mi​. The Fermat little theorem says that for a prime number q and an integer a not divisible by q, aq−1≡1(modq); equivalently, aq≡a(modq) for every integer a.
For the given prime number, write p=2k+1. Its square is p2=1+4k(k+1)=1+8t for some integer t, since one of two consecutive integers is even. Therefore p4=(1+8t)2≡1(mod16). The Fermat little theorem applied modulo 3 gives p2≡1(mod3), hence p4≡1(mod3); applied modulo 5 it gives p4≡1(mod5). These applications are valid because p>5.
The three moduli 16,3,5 are pairwise coprime integers and have product 240. By the Chinese remainder theorem,
p4≡1(mod240).​

Use the Minkowski metric convention +−−−. A particle's four-momentum is P=(E/c,p), with P⋅P=m2c2 for rest mass m. For a photon, its energy and wavelength obey E=hc/λ, and its four-momentum is null. Here h is the Planck constant.
The initially stationary electron has P=(mc,0,0,0). Choose the scattering plane as the xy plane. The incoming and outgoing photon four-momenta are
K1​=λ1​h​(1,1,0,0),K2​=λ2​h​(1,cosθ,sinθ,0).
(6)
By four-momentum conservation, the final electron has P′=P+K1​−K2​. Its rest mass is unchanged, so the relativistic energy-momentum relation gives P′2=P2=m2c2. Since K12​=K22​=0, expansion yields
P⋅(K1​−K2​)=K1​⋅K2​,
(7)
or
mch(λ1​1​−λ2​1​)=λ1​λ2​h2​(1−cosθ).
(8)

λ2​−λ1​=mch​(1−cosθ)=mc2h​sin2(θ/2).​
(9)
It is nonnegative and vanishes for forward scattering, consistent with the recoil energy being supplied by the photon.

For an equivalence relation ∼ on X, the equivalence class of x∈X is [x]={y∈X:y∼x}. A set partition is a family of nonempty, pairwise disjoint subsets whose union is X.
Reflexivity ensures x∈[x], so every equivalence class is nonempty and every element belongs to one. If [x]∩[y] contains z, then z∼x and z∼y; symmetry and transitivity give x∼y. If w∈[x], transitivity gives w∼y, so w∈[y]; interchanging x,y gives the reverse inclusion. Thus intersecting equivalence classes are equal.
Taking the collection of distinct equivalence classes therefore produces a set partition of X. Every element lies in exactly one class. If X is empty, the empty family is its set partition.

Reflexivity follows by taking the two exponents equal to 1, and symmetry is built into the two divisibility conditions. For transitivity, suppose m∣na, n∣mb, n∣ℓc and ℓ∣nd. Then m∣ℓac and ℓ∣mbd, with positive exponents. Thus the relation is an equivalence relation.
Its equivalence classes have a useful description by prime factorization. Let S(n) be the finite set of prime factors of n, with S(1)=∅. If m∣na, every prime factor of m divides n; the reverse divisibility gives S(n)⊆S(m). Conversely, if S(m)=S(n), write m=∏p∈S​pαp​ and n=∏p∈S​pβp​, with all exponents positive. Choosing a≥maxp​⌈αp​/βp​⌉ and b≥maxp​⌈βp​/αp​⌉ gives the required divisibilities. The empty case is exactly m=n=1.
Therefore the prime-support equivalence relation is
m∼n⟺S(m)=S(n)⟺rad(m)=rad(n),​
(10)
where the radical of an integer is the product of its distinct prime factors. The class with empty support is {1}. For every nonempty finite support S, all positive exponent choices give one class, and varying just one exponent produces infinitely many different integers in it.
There are infinitely many classes because each prime number gives a different singleton support. For completeness, if there were only finitely many prime numbers p1​,…,pr​, a prime factor of p1​⋯pr​+1 would differ from them all. There are infinitely many classes; the unique finite class is {1}.

Suppose two base-p expansions represent the same nonnegative integer. Pad the shorter one by trailing zero digits so that both have the same length. Reducing modulo p shows that their first digits satisfy k0​≡k0′​(modp). Since both digits lie between 0 and p−1, they are equal as integers.
Subtract that common digit and divide by p. The remaining equality is an equality of two base-p expansions with one fewer digit. Repeating this argument shows that every pair of corresponding digits agrees. Equivalently, repeated Euclidean division recovers the digits as remainders. The digits are unique up to padding by zeroes. The finite nonnegative-digit representation presupposes k≥0; a negative integer has no such expansion.

For a finite family of subsets A1​,…,AN​ of a finite set Ω, the inclusion-exclusion principle states
​⋃i=1N​Ai​​=∑∅=J⊆{1,…,N}​(−1)∣J∣+1​⋂j∈J​Aj​​.
(17)
Let Aij​ be the set of permutations with the two-cycle (ij) in their cycle decomposition of a permutation. Two distinct specified pairs sharing a label cannot both occur, so their intersection is empty. An intersection containing r pairwise disjoint specified pairs fixes those 2r labels and leaves (n−2r)! choices for the other labels.
The number of ways to choose r disjoint unordered pairs is n!/[2rr!(n−2r)!]: first order the selected labels, then divide by the two orders within each pair, by the r! orders of the pairs, and by the orders of the unused labels. Applying inclusion-exclusion to the complement of the union yields the count of permutations without two-cycles:
f(n)=r=0∑⌊n/2⌋​(−1)r2rr!n!​.​
(18)
The term r=0 is n!. Fixed points and cycles of length at least three are allowed; the restriction concerns two-cycles, not whether a permutation can be expressed as a product of transpositions.
Dividing by n! gives partial sums of the absolutely convergent exponential series, so
n→∞lim​n!f(n)​=r=0∑∞​r!(−1/2)r​=e−1/2.​
(19)

A countable set means here a finite set or a countably infinite set, equivalently a set admitting an injection into N. This convention includes the empty set. An infinite set is countably infinite precisely when it can be put in bijection with N.

Choose an injection j:A→N. For each b∈B, its fiber under the surjection f is nonempty, so define
h(b)=min{j(a):f(a)=b}.
(20)
Two distinct fibers cannot contain the same label j(a), because j is an injection and f(a) has a single value. Thus h:B→N is an injection, proving that B is a countable set. Taking the least label avoids any need for a separate choice of one element from each fiber. If A is empty, surjectivity makes B empty as well.
For the unheaded enumeration request, list pairs of positive natural numbers by their sum. An explicit Cantor pairing function is
π(m,n)=2(m+n−2)(m+n−1)​+n.​
(21)
For d=m+n−2≥0, the positive integers n=1,…,d+1 give the consecutive labels d(d+1)/2+1,…,(d+1)(d+2)/2. These blocks are disjoint, consecutive and cover N. Consequently π is a bijection from N×N to N, so N×N is countable.

ii OurBigBook.com
The integers are countable: j(z)=2z+1 for z≥0 and j(z)=−2z for z<0 is an injection Z→N. Hence Z×N is a countable set. The map (a,b)↦a/b is a surjection onto the rational numbers, so the image result already proved gives Q is countable. Unique reduced fractions are not required for this surjection argument.
For the final unheaded geometry request, suppose for contradiction that no two distinct circles in the collection intersect. Restrict to the subcollection C0​ of circles tangent to the x-axis. Each such nondegenerate circle has a unique tangency point, and the tangency-point map C0​→R is a surjection by the hypothesis.
The closed disk of a circle tangent at (a,0) meets the x-axis only at (a,0). Two distinct members of C0​ cannot have nested disks: if one disk were inside the other, its tangency point would belong to the larger disk, so both tangency points would be equal and both circle boundaries would pass through it. That already contradicts nonintersection. Circles with disjoint boundaries and no nesting have disjoint disks. Thus their open disks are pairwise disjoint.
By the density of the rational numbers, every nonempty open disk contains a point of Q2, which is a countable set. Fix an enumeration of Q2 and assign to each disk the first enumerated point inside it. Disjointness makes this assignment an injection. This is the countability of pairwise disjoint open disks, and proves that C0​ is a countable set. Its surjection onto R would make the real numbers countable, contrary to the Cantor diagonal argument. Therefore
The collection contains two distinct intersecting circles.​
(22)
The proof allows circles on either side of the axis and counts tangential contact as intersection; it does not assume that all circles in the original collection are tangent to the axis.
