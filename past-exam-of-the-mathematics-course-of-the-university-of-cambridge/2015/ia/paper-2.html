Introduce ω=θ˙. The pendulum with quadratic damping is the autonomous planar system
θ˙=ω,ω˙=−sinθ−cω∣ω∣.​
(9)
Its equilibrium points are (kπ,0). Since the derivative of ω∣ω∣ is zero at zero, the linearization of a dynamical system at each equilibrium point is
Jk​=(0−cos(kπ)​10​).
(10)
At an odd multiple of π its eigenvalues are ±1, so it is a saddle equilibrium; the stable and unstable tangents are ω=−(θ−kπ) and ω=θ−kπ. At an even multiple the eigenvalues are ±i. This linearization is a centre and does not decide the nonlinear stability. In particular it would be incorrect to infer a linear damped focus from this Jacobian.
For the full system the mechanical energy is a Lyapunov function near an even equilibrium point:
H(θ,ω)=21​ω2+1−cosθ,H˙=−c∣ω∣3≤0.​
(11)
A sufficiently small sublevel set is compact and contains only that equilibrium point. The only invariant motion with H˙=0 has ω=0 and sinθ=0, hence is the equilibrium point itself. The LaSalle invariance principle proves asymptotic stability. Near it the trajectories turn clockwise and spiral inward, as the near-linear angular motion persists while energy decreases. Thus even equilibria are nonhyperbolic asymptotically stable spiral points; odd equilibria remain saddles.
For explicit trajectories put W=ω2 on a branch where ω=0. The chain rule gives dW/dθ=2ω˙, so the two signs yield the first-order linear differential equations W′+2cW=−2sinθ for ω>0, and W′−2cW=−2sinθ for ω<0. Their integrating factors give
ω2ω2​=K+​e−2cθ+1+4c22(cosθ−2csinθ)​,=K−​e2cθ+1+4c22(cosθ+2csinθ)​,​​ω>0,ω<0.​​
(12)
Only portions with a nonnegative right-hand side are physical. At a turning point the constants of the two branches are matched using ω=0; neither expression is one global trajectory through both signs. When c=0 they reduce to energy contours, with closed orbits around the even equilibrium points. For every c>0, the quadratic damping destroys those closed orbits and makes the centres attracting spirals. Its effect is higher order in amplitude, so decay is weaker near rest than with linear damping. The saddle equilibria remain unstable, though their separatrices change shape.

Let N=a+b and measure Lionel's fortune in unit stakes. This is gambler's ruin: the fortune moves by +1 with probability p and −1 with probability q, stopping at 0 or N. Let mi​ be the expected value of the absorption time starting at i. For a nonnegative random variable Y with finite expected value, and a>0, Markov's inequality states P(Y≥a)≤E[Y]/a. Indeed Y≥a1{Y≥a}​, and taking expected values proves the bound. For a random variable X with mean μ and finite variance σ2, apply Markov's inequality to (X−μ)2 at threshold ε2. This proves Chebyshev's inequality:
P(∣X−μ∣≥ε)≤ε2σ2​.​
(32)
For independent and identically distributed random variables X1​,X2​,… with finite mean μ and variance σ2, the sample mean has expected value μ and variance σ2/n. Thus
P(​n1​∑i=1n​Xi​−μ​≥ε)≤nε2σ2​⟶0.
(33)
This is the weak law: n−1i=1∑n​Xi​→μ in probability​, with the finite-variance hypotheses used in this direct deduction.
The more general weak law of large numbers needs only E∣X1​∣<∞. To obtain that version, truncate Yi(K)​=Xi​1{∣Xi​∣≤K}​. For fixed K the bounded variables obey the result just proved. Their means tend to μ, while Markov's inequality bounds the probability that the two sample means differ by more than ε/3 by 3E[∣X1​∣1{∣X1​∣>K}​]/ε. First take n→∞ and then K→∞; the integrable tail vanishes, proving the full integrable i.i.d. version as well.
Finally assume E[X]=0 and Var(X)=σ2. For every b>0, X≥a implies (X+b)2≥(a+b)2, so Markov's inequality gives
P(X≥a)≤(a+b)2E[(X+b)2]​=(a+b)2σ2+b2​.
(34)
If σ2>0, the derivative of the right-hand side is 2(ab−σ2)/(a+b)3, so its minimum is at b=σ2/a. The Cantelli inequality follows:
P(X≥a)≤σ2+a2σ2​.​
(35)
If σ2=0, X=0 almost surely and the same bound is immediate. The bound is sharp: put mass σ2/(σ2+a2) at a and the remaining mass at −σ2/a; this has exactly the prescribed mean and variance.

Interpret random choice of coin as equal prior probabilities, and the tosses as independent conditional on the chosen coin. Let H2​ denote two heads. The conditional probabilities are P(H2​∣A)=1/16 and P(H2​∣B)=9/16. For posterior coin selection from repeated tosses, Bayes' theorem gives
P(B∣H2​)=(1/2)(1/16)+(1/2)(9/16)(1/2)(9/16)​=109​.​
(36)
The tosses need not be unconditionally independent, because the same hidden coin is used twice. If the coin-choice prior were instead P(B)=π, the answer would be 9π/(1+8π); equal choice gives the stated 9/10.
For nondecreasing uniform samples in the lottery, all nr ordered samples have equal probability by the discrete uniform distribution and independence. A nondecreasing sample is uniquely specified by its multiplicities k1​,…,kn​≥0, with k1​+⋯+kn​=r. The stars and bars argument places n−1 separators among r identical markers, giving (n−1r+n−1​) multiplicity vectors. There is exactly one nondecreasing ordered sample for each such vector, so the probability is
nr(n−1r+n−1​)​=nr(rr+n−1​)​.​
(37)
This counts samples with ties correctly; multiplying a single strictly increasing count by r! would not handle the repeated values.
