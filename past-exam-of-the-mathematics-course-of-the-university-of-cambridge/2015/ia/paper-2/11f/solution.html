For a nonnegative random variable Y with finite expected value, and a>0, Markov's inequality states P(Y≥a)≤E[Y]/a. Indeed Y≥a1{Y≥a}​, and taking expected values proves the bound. For a random variable X with mean μ and finite variance σ2, apply Markov's inequality to (X−μ)2 at threshold ε2. This proves Chebyshev's inequality:
P(∣X−μ∣≥ε)≤ε2σ2​.​
(32)
For independent and identically distributed random variables X1​,X2​,… with finite mean μ and variance σ2, the sample mean has expected value μ and variance σ2/n. Thus
P(​n1​∑i=1n​Xi​−μ​≥ε)≤nε2σ2​⟶0.
(33)
This is the weak law: n−1i=1∑n​Xi​→μ in probability​, with the finite-variance hypotheses used in this direct deduction.
The more general weak law of large numbers needs only E∣X1​∣<∞. To obtain that version, truncate Yi(K)​=Xi​1{∣Xi​∣≤K}​. For fixed K the bounded variables obey the result just proved. Their means tend to μ, while Markov's inequality bounds the probability that the two sample means differ by more than ε/3 by 3E[∣X1​∣1{∣X1​∣>K}​]/ε. First take n→∞ and then K→∞; the integrable tail vanishes, proving the full integrable i.i.d. version as well.
Finally assume E[X]=0 and Var(X)=σ2. For every b>0, X≥a implies (X+b)2≥(a+b)2, so Markov's inequality gives
P(X≥a)≤(a+b)2E[(X+b)2]​=(a+b)2σ2+b2​.
(34)
If σ2>0, the derivative of the right-hand side is 2(ab−σ2)/(a+b)3, so its minimum is at b=σ2/a. The Cantelli inequality follows:
P(X≥a)≤σ2+a2σ2​.​
(35)
If σ2=0, X=0 almost surely and the same bound is immediate. The bound is sharp: put mass σ2/(σ2+a2) at a and the remaining mass at −σ2/a; this has exactly the prescribed mean and variance.
