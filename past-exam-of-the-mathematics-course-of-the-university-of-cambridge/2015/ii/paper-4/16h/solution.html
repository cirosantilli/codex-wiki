For a number field K, let OK​ be its ring of integers of a number field, r1​ the number of real embeddings and r2​ the number of conjugate pairs of nonreal complex embeddings, so [K:Q]=r1​+2r2​. The Dirichlet unit theorem says
OK×​≅μ(K)×Zr1​+r2​−1,
(35)
where μ(K) is the finite group of roots of unity in K. Thus an imaginary quadratic number field has only roots of unity as units: usually {±1}, with four for d=−1 and six for d=−3. A real quadratic number field has units ±ϵn for a fundamental unit ϵ>1 and integers n.
For K=Q(26​), OK​=Z[26​] and the field norm is x2−26y2. The element ϵ=5+26​ has norm −1 and is a unit. It is the smallest positive unit greater than one. Indeed for 1<u<ϵ, its conjugate is ±1/u, so the coefficient y=(u∓1/u)/(226​) is a positive integer less than two. Therefore y=1, and x2=26±1 forces x=5, giving u=ϵ, a contradiction. The Dirichlet unit theorem now gives OK×​={±(5+26​)n:n∈Z}​. Its least positive norm-one generator is ϵ2=51+1026​.
For the generalized Pell equation, write α=x+y26​ with norm N=±10. Change the sign if necessary and multiply by a power of ϵ to place it in 10​≤α<ϵ10​. These operations preserve integrality and the absolute norm, though the latter operation can change the norm's sign. Since α′=N/α, we have x=(α+N/α)/2≥0 and y=(α−N/α)/(226​)≥0. The upper bound gives y<4. Checking y=0,1,2,3 in x2=26y2±10 leaves only (x,y)=(4,1),(6,1). Both reduced elements lie in the chosen interval. Therefore all integral solutions of either sign are
x+y26​=±(4+26​)(5+26​)nor±(6+26​)(5+26​)n,n∈Z.​
(36)
Negative powers are integral because ϵ−1=26​−5. In the first family the norm is −10(−1)n and in the second it is 10(−1)n, so this also separates the two equations. The reduction proves exhaustion rather than merely generating examples.
