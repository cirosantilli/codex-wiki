A Hamiltonian evolution equation has the form ut​=JδH/δu, where H is a functional and the Hamiltonian operator J induces a bilinear antisymmetric bracket obeying the Jacobi identity. For a local functional H=∫h(u,ux​,…)dx, its variational derivative is the coefficient of a test perturbation after all integrations by parts.
Linearizing about zero drops the cubic term and gives ut​+uxxx​=0. A plane wave ei(kx−ωt) has ω=−k3, so phase velocity −k2 and group velocity −3k2 depend on wavenumber. This proves small-amplitude wave dispersion.
Take
H[u]=21​∫(ux2​+u4)dx,J=∂x​​.
(69)
Then δH/δu=−uxx​+2u3 and JδH/δu=−uxxx​+6u2ux​, the required flow. On sufficiently smooth functionals define the Poisson bracket
{F,G}=∫δuδF​∂x​δuδG​dx​.
(70)
Variational differentiation and integration are linear, proving linearity in both arguments. Integration by parts and the rapid decay give {F,G}=−{G,F}. The constant skew differential operator ∂x​ also satisfies the Hamiltonian Jacobi condition, so this is indeed a Poisson bracket.
Along the flow, the functional chain rule gives dI/dt=∫(δI/δu)ut​dx={I,H}. Thus an autonomous functional is a first integral exactly when its bracket with H vanishes identically on phase space. Direct multiplication of the equation by 2u gives
∂t​(u2)=−2uuxxx​+12u3ux​=−∂x​(2uuxx​−ux2​−3u4).
(71)
Integrating the resulting local conservation law over the line makes the boundary flux vanish. Hence I=∫u2dx is conserved and {I,H}=0. As a direct check, its variational derivative is 2u, and integration by parts makes ∫2u(−uxxx​+6u2ux​)dx=0.
