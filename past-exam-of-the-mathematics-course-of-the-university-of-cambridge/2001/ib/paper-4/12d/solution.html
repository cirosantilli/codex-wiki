Assume n≥2 and σ2>0. Up to a constant, the log-likelihood of the normal sample is
ℓ(μ,s)=−2n​logs−2s1​∑i​(Xi​−μ)2,s=σ2.
(36)
For each s, the square is minimized at μ=Xˉ; optimizing the remaining one-variable likelihood gives the maximum-likelihood estimators
μ​=Xˉ,σ2=n1​i∑​(Xi​−Xˉ)2.​
(37)
They are the global maximum when the residual sum is positive, which occurs with probability one. On the exceptional all-equal sample the likelihood increases without bound as s↓0.
To derive the distributions and independence, write Zi​=(Xi​−μ)/σ and perform an orthogonal change of coordinates whose first row is n−1/2(1,…,1). The transformed components Z1′​,…,Zn′​ remain independent standard normal random variables, since their joint density depends only on the squared Euclidean norm. Then
μ​=μ+n​σ​Z1′​,σ2nσ2​=∑j=2n​(Zj′​)2.
(38)
The first expression uses only Z1′​, the second only the other components. Consequently
μ​∼N(μ,σ2/n),nσ2/σ2∼χn−12​,μ​ and σ2 are independent.​
(39)
This also exhibits why the maximum-likelihood variance uses divisor n, even though its chi-squared degrees of freedom are n−1.
The independent future observation satisfies X0​−Xˉ∼N(0,σ2(1+1/n)). It is independent of the residual sum of squares, because both X0​ and Xˉ are independent of the residual vector. Combining this normal variable with the independent chi-squared variable gives
T=σ(n+1)/(n−1)​X0​−μ​​∼tn−1​.
(40)
The event in the proposed prediction interval is equivalent to ∣T∣<γ(n−1)/n​. By symmetry and continuity of Student's t-distribution, exact joint coverage 1−α therefore requires
γ=n−1n​​tn−1,1−α/2​.​
(41)
This is the normal prediction interval with maximum-likelihood variance. The factor n/(n−1)​ cannot be dropped: the interval uses σ, not the unbiased residual standard deviation. Coverage here is over repeated joint sampling of the old and new observations, not a claim of parameter-free conditional coverage for every realized sample.
