A function f:I→R is uniformly continuous if
∀ε>0 ∃δ>0 ∀x,y∈I:∣x−y∣<δ⟹∣f(x)−f(y)∣<ε.
(1)
The crucial point is that δ works throughout the interval, not just near one chosen point.
Yes, it is bounded on the closed unit interval. Choose δ for ε=1, and choose finitely many grid points tj​ such that every x∈[0,1] is within δ of some tj​. Then ∣f(x)∣≤1+maxj​∣f(tj​)∣, a finite bound. Equivalently, uniform continuity implies continuity, and a continuous real function on a compact set is bounded.
Yes, a bounded derivative gives uniform continuity on the half-line. If ∣f′∣≤K, the mean value theorem gives ∣f(x)−f(y)∣≤K∣x−y∣. This is a Lipschitz condition, so for K>0 use δ=ε/K. If K=0, the function is constant. As usual for a derivative on this closed half-line, continuity at its endpoint is included.

Assume a<b. On continuous functions on this compact interval, both proposed distances are finite and symmetric, and vanish on identical functions. For the supremum norm, zero distance plainly implies pointwise equality. For the L2 norm, if a continuous difference is nonzero at any point, it stays bounded away from zero on a subinterval of positive length. Its squared integral is then positive, so zero distance also implies equality.
For the first metric, the pointwise triangle inequality followed by the supremum gives d1​(x,z)≤d1​(x,y)+d1​(y,z). For the second, put u=x−y, v=y−z. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

Taking square roots proves the required triangle inequality. Thus both are metrics.
The continuous-function space is complete in the uniform metric. If (xn​) is a Cauchy sequence in d1​, at each t its scalar values have a limit x(t), by completeness of the scalar field. Reindexing a convergent periodization gives periodicity; the integral argument always gives its periodic representative almost everywhere. This proves the Fourier-coefficient content of the Poisson summation formula.
There is a genuine qualification to the printed pointwise claim: the periodization of an integrable function need not agree pointwise with its Fourier series under the stated hypotheses alone. Set F(0)=1 and F(x)=0 for x=0. This is absolutely integrable, and on one closed period the periodization has only two possibly nonzero summands, so the series is uniformly convergent. Yet f(0)=1, while F(m)=0 for every m. The claimed pointwise equality at zero is therefore false. This counterexample also works for Riemann integrability, not only for an almost-everywhere convention.

For convergence in the L1 norm, split the convolution error into small translations, controlled by continuity of translation in the integral norm, and the remaining tail. The same split gives pointwise convergence at a continuity point. If the periodization is piecewise continuously differentiable, the ordinary Fourier series has the usual midpoint limit; if it is continuous and its coefficients are absolutely summable, the displayed unweighted formula holds pointwise. First work over a field of characteristic different from two. If a nonzero symmetric bilinear form had ϕ(v,v)=0 for every vector, polarization would give
2ϕ(u,v)=ϕ(u+v,u+v)−ϕ(u,u)−ϕ(v,v)=0,
(72)
a contradiction. Hence choose e with ϕ(e,e)=0. Every v decomposes uniquely as
v=ϕ(e,e)ϕ(v,e)​e+(v−ϕ(e,e)ϕ(v,e)​e),
(73)
with the second summand orthogonal to e. Induct on the orthogonal complement to obtain a diagonal basis. If the restricted form becomes zero, any basis of that remaining space completes the diagonalization. In this basis the rank of a quadratic form is exactly the number of nonzero diagonal coefficients; the remaining basis vectors span its radical of a bilinear form.
Over the reals, rescale the nonzero basis vectors so the diagonal entries are p copies of +1, q copies of −1 and z=n−p−q zeros. Define the signature of a quadratic form by σ=p−q, so r=p+q. To prove it is basis independent, note that p is the largest dimension of a linear subspace on which the form is positive-definite. The positive coordinate linear subspace attains dimension p; on any other positive linear subspace, projection to those positive coordinates is injective, since its kernel has nonpositive quadratic value. Thus its dimension is at most p. The same argument for the negative form characterizes q. This proves Sylvester's law of inertia and that the signature is well-defined.
Let ei​ and fi​ be positive and negative normalized basis vectors, and let Z be the radical of a bilinear form. The span of Z and ei​+fi​ for 1≤i≤min(p,q) is a totally isotropic subspace: all mutual bilinear pairings vanish. Its dimension is z+min(p,q). Conversely, project any totally isotropic subspace U to the nondegenerate positive-plus-negative quotient. The projected linear subspace is still null, and its projections to both the positive and negative coordinate spaces are injective: a vector with one component zero would have strictly signed quadratic value unless it vanished. Its dimension is therefore at most min(p,q), while the kernel of the quotient map on U has dimension at most z. Hence
maxdimU=z+min(p,q)=n−2r+∣σ∣​​.
(74)
This proves both construction and sharpness of the maximum dimension of a totally isotropic subspace.
For the five-variable example, the symmetric coefficient matrix is

(76)
Their product is 2, and three are positive and two negative. Therefore
detA=2,r=5,σ=1​.
(77)
