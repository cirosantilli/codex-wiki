An eigenvalue of C is a scalar λ for which a nonzero vector v satisfies Cv=λv. Equivalently, det(λI−C)=0, because the homogeneous linear system has a nontrivial solution exactly when its coefficient matrix is singular. This characteristic polynomial is the monic quadratic
λ2−(trC)λ+detC.
(11)
A nonzero quadratic has at most two distinct roots, proving the eigenvalue bound. Eigenvalues and diagonalization are taken over C, also for a matrix with real entries.
For the matrix commutator, the cyclic trace identity follows directly from its entries:
tr(AB)=∑i,j​Aij​Bji​=∑i,j​Bij​Aji​=tr(BA).
(12)
Hence tr[A,B]=0. Factoring the characteristic polynomial as (λ−λ1​)(λ−λ2​) and comparing the coefficient of λ gives trC=λ1​+λ2​, with the eigenvalues counted with algebraic multiplicity. If both eigenvalues are zero, trace and determinant are zero. The Cayley-Hamilton theorem then gives C2=0; no diagonalizability assumption is needed.
Now put M=[A,B]. Since its trace is zero, its characteristic polynomial is λ2+detM and the scalar square of a traceless two-by-two matrix identity gives
[A,B]2=αI,α=−det[A,B]=21​tr([A,B]2).​
(13)
This proves the scalar-square assertion for both real and complex entries. If detM=0, it gives M2=0. If detM=0, the two eigenvalues are the distinct nonzero numbers ±−detM​. Their eigenvectors are linearly independent: a nonzero vector cannot satisfy two different eigenvalue equations. They form a basis, making M a diagonalizable matrix. Thus either the commutator is diagonalizable over C or its square is zero. These alternatives need not be disjoint, since the zero matrix has both properties. Over R the diagonalization assertion would require real eigenvalues; complex diagonalization is essential to the unrestricted statement.
