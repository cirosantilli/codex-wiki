At a critical point x0​ of a twice continuously differentiable real function, the Taylor expansion is
f(x0​+h)=f(x0​)+21​hTHh+o(∣h∣2),H=∇2f(x0​).
(24)
The Hessian matrix is real symmetric, so an orthogonal eigenbasis diagonalizes its quadratic form. If every eigenvalue is positive, that form is bounded below by a positive multiple of ∣h∣2, which dominates the remainder and gives a strict local minimum. If every eigenvalue is negative, the same argument gives a strict local maximum. If both signs occur, displacement along the corresponding eigenvectors produces values above and below f(x0​), so the point is a saddle point of a scalar function. These exhaust a nonsingular Hessian matrix.
A singular Hessian matrix does not always make the classification impossible: nonzero eigenvalues of both signs still give a saddle. If the Hessian matrix is positive or negative semidefinite, higher-order terms in its null directions must be examined. For example, x2+y4 and x2−y4 have the same Hessian matrix diag(2,0) at zero, but the first has a strict minimum and the second a saddle. The function x2 has a non-strict minimum along a whole line.
For the given polynomial,
fx​=2x(α−3y+4x2),fy​=2y−3x2.
(25)
A stationary point has y=3x2/2 and then x(2α−x2)=0. Hence the origin is always critical, and two additional points exist exactly for α>0:
(x,y)=(±2α​,3α).​
(26)
The Hessian matrix is
H=(2α−6y+24x2−6x​−6x2​).
(27)
At the origin it is diag(2α,2), giving a strict local minimum for α>0 and a saddle for α<0. At either extra critical point it has determinant 64α−36(2α)=−8α<0, so both extra points are saddles.
At α=0, complete the square:
f(x,y)=(y−23​x2)2−41​x4.
(28)
Along x=0 it is positive away from zero, whereas along y=3x2/2 it is negative away from zero. Thus the origin remains a degenerate saddle despite its nonnegative Hessian matrix. This is the parabolic quartic critical-point classification. The complete result is
