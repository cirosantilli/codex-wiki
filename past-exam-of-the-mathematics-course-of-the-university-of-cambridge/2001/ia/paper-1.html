Suppose f is continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b) and satisfies f(a)=f(b), with a<b. Apply the extreme value theorem to both f and −f to obtain a maximum and a minimum. If f is constant, every interior derivative is zero. Otherwise some value differs from the common endpoint value, so either a maximum or a minimum occurs at an interior point c. At an interior maximum, difference quotients are nonpositive from the right and nonnegative from the left; since the derivative exists, it must be zero. The minimum case is identical with the inequalities reversed. This proves Rolle's theorem: f′(c)=0​ for some c∈(a,b).
For the mean value theorem, subtract the endpoint chord:
g(t)=f(t)−f(a)−b−af(b)−f(a)​(t−a).
(8)
Then g(a)=g(b)=0, so Rolle's theorem supplies c∈(a,b) with g′(c)=0. Hence
f′(c)=b−af(b)−f(a)​.​
(9)
If f′(t)>0 everywhere, applying the mean value theorem to any x<y gives f(y)−f(x)=f′(c)(y−x)>0. Thus f is strictly increasing. The converse is false: f(t)=t3​ is strictly increasing but f′(0)=0.

To prove the intermediate value theorem in this setting, let A={t∈[a,b]:f(t)<v} and c=supA. The endpoint inequalities and continuity give an interval immediately to the right of a contained in A and an interval immediately to the left of b disjoint from A. Thus a<c<b. Choose points of A tending to c; continuity gives f(c)≤v. If f(c)<v, continuity would place some point larger than c in A, contradicting the supremum. Hence f(c)=v​ at an interior point.
For a continuous self-map of [a,b], define g(t)=f(t)−t. The range condition implies g(a)≥0 and g(b)≤0. If either is zero, its endpoint is a fixed point. Otherwise apply the just-proved intermediate value theorem to −g with target zero to obtain d∈(a,b) and g(d)=0. Therefore
Every continuous self-map of [a,b] has a fixed point.​
(31)
This proves the fixed-point property of a closed interval without requiring f itself to be monotone.
