Put t=trA=a+d and δ=detA=ad−bc. Direct matrix multiplication gives the two-dimensional Cayley-Hamilton theorem identity A2−tA+δI=0. If A2=0, determinant multiplicativity gives δ2=0, hence δ=0. The identity then says tA=0. If A=0, its trace is zero; otherwise a nonzero entry forces t=0. Thus d=−a and bc=ad=−a2.
Conversely, those two scalar relations give t=δ=0, so the same Cayley-Hamilton theorem identity implies A2=0. Therefore
A2=0⟺d=−a and bc=−a2.​
(1)
This is the square-zero criterion for a two-by-two matrix. If A3=0, determinant multiplicativity first gives δ3=0, hence δ=0. Now A2=tA and A3=t2A. Either A=0, or a nonzero entry gives t2=0 and hence t=0; in both cases A2=0. The converse follows by multiplying A2=0 by A. Consequently A3=0⟺A2=0. A nonzero nilpotent matrix of this size therefore has nilpotency index two.

A Möbius transformation is MA​(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d) with Δ=ad−bc=0. On the Riemann sphere, a zero denominator represents infinity, and MA​(∞)=a/c when c=0; when c=0, infinity is fixed. The numerator and denominator cannot vanish together because Δ=0.
The matrix A=(ac​bd​) acts on homogeneous coordinates [z:1], so composition satisfies MA​∘MB​=MAB​. The product is invertible since det(AB)=detAdetB=0, proving closure. Composition is associative because these are maps of the sphere. The identity is z↦z, and
MA−1​(z)=−cz+adz−b​
(2)
is again a Möbius transformation. Thus the transformations form a group. Nonzero scalar multiples of a matrix give the same transformation, but the identity and inverse assertions concern the transformations themselves.
Write Tq​(z)=z+q, Sk​(z)=kz with k=0, and H(z)=1/z. If c=0, then a,d=0 and MA​=Tb/d​∘Sa/d​. If c=0, division gives
cz+daz+b​=ca​−z+d/cΔ/c2​.
(3)
Hence the Möbius translation-scaling-inversion factorization is
MA​=Ta/c​∘S−Δ/c2​∘H∘Td/c​(c=0).​
(4)
The scaling coefficient is nonzero. At z=−d/c the reciprocal sends the translated zero to infinity; at infinity it sends infinity to zero. Thus the factorization holds on the entire Riemann sphere, including both exceptional points, and proves the stated generating property.

For a differentiable scalar function and a differentiable curve, the multivariable chain rule is
dtd​f(x(t),y(t))=fx​dtdx​+fy​dtdy​.​

Lf=(Lu)fu​+(Lv)fv​=−3vfv​=6f.

f=v−2F(u)=y2x2​F(x2y),xy=0,​

The axes need care because v is undefined at x=0 and the Jacobian vanishes at y=0. Reparameterizing F(u)=u2G(u) gives the simpler chart expression
f(x,y)=x6G(x2y)(x=0).​

An eigenvalue of C is a scalar λ for which a nonzero vector v satisfies Cv=λv. Equivalently, det(λI−C)=0, because the homogeneous linear system has a nontrivial solution exactly when its coefficient matrix is singular. This characteristic polynomial is the monic quadratic
λ2−(trC)λ+detC.
(11)
A nonzero quadratic has at most two distinct roots, proving the eigenvalue bound. Eigenvalues and diagonalization are taken over C, also for a matrix with real entries.
For the matrix commutator, the cyclic trace identity follows directly from its entries:
tr(AB)=∑i,j​Aij​Bji​=∑i,j​Bij​Aji​=tr(BA).
(12)
Hence tr[A,B]=0. Factoring the characteristic polynomial as (λ−λ1​)(λ−λ2​) and comparing the coefficient of λ gives trC=λ1​+λ2​, with the eigenvalues counted with algebraic multiplicity. If both eigenvalues are zero, trace and determinant are zero. The Cayley-Hamilton theorem then gives C2=0; no diagonalizability assumption is needed.
Now put M=[A,B]. Since its trace is zero, its characteristic polynomial is λ2+detM and the scalar square of a traceless two-by-two matrix identity gives
[A,B]2=αI,α=−det[A,B]=21​tr([A,B]2).​
(13)
This proves the scalar-square assertion for both real and complex entries. If detM=0, it gives M2=0. If detM=0, the two eigenvalues are the distinct nonzero numbers ±−detM​. Their eigenvectors are linearly independent: a nonzero vector cannot satisfy two different eigenvalue equations. They form a basis, making M a diagonalizable matrix. Thus either the commutator is diagonalizable over C or its square is zero. These alternatives need not be disjoint, since the zero matrix has both properties. Over R the diagonalization assertion would require real eigenvalues; complex diagonalization is essential to the unrestricted statement.

A left group action of G on a set X is a map (g,x)↦g⋅x satisfying e⋅x=x and (gh)⋅x=g⋅(h⋅x). The group orbit of x is G⋅x={g⋅x:g∈G}, and its stabilizer subgroup is Gx​={g∈G:g⋅x=x}. The stabilizer subgroup contains the identity, is closed under products, and contains g−1 whenever it contains g, so it is indeed a subgroup.
The orbit-stabilizer theorem for a finite group says
∣G∣=∣G⋅x∣∣Gx​∣.​
(14)
To prove it, map a left coset gGx​ to g⋅x. This is well-defined because every element of Gx​ fixes x. Conversely, g⋅x=h⋅x precisely when h−1g∈Gx​, which is precisely equality of the two cosets. Thus this map is a bijection between the cosets and the group orbit. Each coset has ∣Gx​∣ elements, by the bijection k↦gk, and the cosets partition G, giving the formula.
Apply this to the rotational symmetry group of a cube acting on its six faces. The action is transitive: rotations through right angles about coordinate axes can send the top face to each of the other faces. A rotation fixing a face must fix its outward normal, so it rotates about the axis through that face's center and the opposite face's center. Preservation of its square boundary permits exactly four angles modulo 2π: 0,π/2,π,3π/2. All four preserve the cube, so this face stabilizer subgroup has order four. The group is finite because a rotation permutes the six face normals and is determined by their images. The orbit-stabilizer theorem gives
∣Rot(cube)∣=6×4=24.​
(15)
These are orientation-preserving rotations; reflections are not being counted.

Lagrange's theorem states that for a subgroup H of a finite group G, ∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣; in particular ∣H∣ divides ∣G∣. This follows by partitioning G into cosets, each in bijection with H.
If ∣G∣=p is prime, take any g=e. Its cyclic subgroup has order dividing p, but its order is not one. Thus it has order p and equals G. Hence
Every group of order p is cyclic and isomorphic to Cp​.​
(16)
This describes a single group isomorphism type for each prime.
Now let G=⟨x⟩ have order n, and let H be any subgroup. Choose the least positive integer d with xd∈H; this exists because xn=e∈H, even when H is trivial. Divide n=qd+r with 0≤r<d. Since xr=xn(xd)−q∈H, minimality forces r=0, so d divides n. Likewise, dividing an exponent k=qd+r for any xk∈H shows r=0. Therefore H=⟨xd⟩.
Conversely, for each divisor d of n, ⟨xd⟩ is a subgroup of order n/d. The least positive exponent property makes d unique. This proves the complete subgroups and quotients of a cyclic group classification:
H=⟨xd⟩ (d∣n),∣H∣=n/d;Hm​=⟨xn/m⟩ is the unique subgroup of order m (m∣n).​
(17)
The choices d=n and d=1 include the trivial subgroup and the whole group.

X−1PX=(31)(24)=Q,X−1QX=(32)(14)=(14)(23)=PQ.​
(18)
Direct composition also gives PQ=QP=(14)(23). Thus V={e,P,Q,PQ} is a Klein four-group. Conjugation by X cycles its three nonidentity elements, so X normalizes V.
The cosets V,VX,VX2 have four elements each and are distinct. If two coincided, a nontrivial power of X would belong to V, impossible because it has order three whereas every nonidentity element of V has order two. Hence the subgroup generated by X,P,Q contains at least twelve elements. These generators are even permutations, so the subgroup lies in the alternating group A4​, whose order is 4!/2=12; multiplying by one fixed transposition pairs even and odd permutations to justify this count. Therefore
A4​=⟨X,P,Q⟩.​
(19)
The calculation also describes A4​ as the semidirect product V⋊⟨X⟩, with the order-three factor permuting the three involutions.

The direct product of groups uses componentwise multiplication:
(g,h)(g′,h′)=(gg′,hh′),e=(eG​,eH​),(g,h)−1=(g−1,h−1).
(20)
Associativity follows in each factor. The maps g↦(g,eH​) and h↦(eG​,h) are injective group homomorphisms onto subgroups, giving the required copies of G and H.
Use the PDF's convention that the subscript in Dn​ is the group order. Write the hexagon group as D12​=⟨r,s∣r6=s2=e, srs=r−1⟩ and the triangle group as D6​=⟨a,b∣a3=b2=e, bab=a−1⟩. Let z generate C2​. In D12​, r2 has order three and s conjugates it to its inverse, so ⟨r2,s⟩ is a copy of D6​. The element r3 has order two and is central, since sr3s=r−3=r3.
Define
Φ(aibj,zk)=r2i+3ksj,0≤i<3,0≤j,k<2.
(21)
The generator relations are preserved and r3 commutes with the other images, so Φ is a group homomorphism from the direct product. The six residues 2i+3k are distinct modulo six: reduction modulo two first determines k, then reduction modulo three determines i. Together with j, they give all twelve distinct normal forms rmsj in D12​. Thus Φ is bijective and
D12​≅D6​×C2​.​
(22)
This is the dihedral splitting when the half-rotation order is odd with half-rotation order three.

The even cycle types on four symbols are the identity, a 3-cycle, and two disjoint transpositions; their orders are respectively one, three and two. Therefore A4​ has no element of order six. At a critical point x0​ of a twice continuously differentiable real function, the Taylor expansion is
f(x0​+h)=f(x0​)+21​hTHh+o(∣h∣2),H=∇2f(x0​).
(24)
The Hessian matrix is real symmetric, so an orthogonal eigenbasis diagonalizes its quadratic form. If every eigenvalue is positive, that form is bounded below by a positive multiple of ∣h∣2, which dominates the remainder and gives a strict local minimum. If every eigenvalue is negative, the same argument gives a strict local maximum. If both signs occur, displacement along the corresponding eigenvectors produces values above and below f(x0​), so the point is a saddle point of a scalar function. These exhaust a nonsingular Hessian matrix.
A singular Hessian matrix does not always make the classification impossible: nonzero eigenvalues of both signs still give a saddle. If the Hessian matrix is positive or negative semidefinite, higher-order terms in its null directions must be examined. For example, x2+y4 and x2−y4 have the same Hessian matrix diag(2,0) at zero, but the first has a strict minimum and the second a saddle. The function x2 has a non-strict minimum along a whole line.
For the given polynomial,
fx​=2x(α−3y+4x2),fy​=2y−3x2.
(25)
A stationary point has y=3x2/2 and then x(2α−x2)=0. Hence the origin is always critical, and two additional points exist exactly for α>0:
(x,y)=(±2α​,3α).​
(26)
The Hessian matrix is
H=(2α−6y+24x2−6x​−6x2​).
(27)
At the origin it is diag(2α,2), giving a strict local minimum for α>0 and a saddle for α<0. At either extra critical point it has determinant 64α−36(2α)=−8α<0, so both extra points are saddles.
At α=0, complete the square:
f(x,y)=(y−23​x2)2−41​x4.
(28)
Along x=0 it is positive away from zero, whereas along y=3x2/2 it is negative away from zero. Thus the origin remains a degenerate saddle despite its nonnegative Hessian matrix. This is the parabolic quartic critical-point classification. The complete result is
α<0α=0α>0​(0,0) is the only critical point, a saddle(0,0) is the only critical point, a degenerate saddle(0,0) is a strict local minimum; (±2α​,3α) are saddles.​​
(29)
For α>0 the local minimum is not global, since along the same parabola f=αx2−x4/4→−∞.

∫V​∇⋅udV=∫∂V​u⋅ndS,

∫V​∇ΩdV=∫∂V​ΩdS.​

For the cone and its cap, the volume has 0≤z≤1 and 0≤r≤3​z in cylindrical coordinates. Its volume is ∫01​π(3​z)2dz=π. Since ∇Ω=−ez​,
∫V​∇ΩdV=−πez​.

The cap at z=1 has area 3π, outward normal ez​ and Ω=a−1, so its contribution to the surface integral is 3π(a−1)ez​.

Let ϕ1​,ϕ2​ be two sufficiently regular solutions with the same source and boundary data, and set w=ϕ1​−ϕ2​. Then ∇2w=0 and α∂n​w+w=0. Green's first identity gives
∫V​∣∇w∣2dV=∫∂V​w∂n​wdS=−∫∂V​α(∂n​w)2dS≤0.
(40)
The left side is nonnegative, so it vanishes. Thus w is constant on each connected component. Its normal derivative is zero, and the boundary condition then forces that constant to be zero. This proves at most one solution. The Poisson uniqueness with a nonnegative Robin normal coefficient proof never divides by α, so it includes the points or boundary portions where α=0.
For the exponential-sine modes, direct differentiation gives
∂x2​(e±ℓxsinℓy)=ℓ2e±ℓxsinℓy,∂y2​(e±ℓxsinℓy)=−ℓ2e±ℓxsinℓy.
(41)
Their sum is zero, so both modes are harmonic functions for every real ℓ.
For the square, the horizontal Dirichlet data suggest separation of variables in the form ϕ(x,y)=F(x)sin(ky), where the positive integer k makes the sine vanish at both horizontal edges. The Laplace equation gives F′′−k2F=0, hence F=Acosh(kx)+Bsinh(kx). At x=0, the outward normal points in the negative x direction. The Robin boundary condition is therefore F(0)−F′(0)=0, or A=kB. The right edge requires F(π)=1, fixing the remaining coefficient. The resulting separated Laplace mode with a Robin edge is
ϕ(x,y)=kcosh(kπ)+sinh(kπ)kcosh(kx)+sinh(kx)​sin(ky).​
(42)
The denominator is positive for k>0. The function satisfies the Laplace equation and all four boundary conditions directly; for example its left-edge value and x derivative agree, giving the correct outward-normal Robin sign. It extends regularly to the corners.
On the left edge the uniqueness coefficient is α=1; on the other three edges it is α=0 with the prescribed Dirichlet value. The same Green's first identity argument applies to the square's piecewise smooth boundary, whose corners have zero boundary measure. Thus this is the unique regular solution, or equivalently the unique finite-energy solution with these boundary traces. Unbounded corner singularities are outside that boundary-value class.
