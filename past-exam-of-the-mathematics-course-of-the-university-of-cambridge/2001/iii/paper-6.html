Start with the approximate surjectivity with geometric correction argument. For a fixed y, put r0​=y. Choose xj​ successively so that
∥xj​∥≤R∥rj−1​∥,rj​=rj−1​−Txj​,∥rj​∥≤k∥rj−1​∥.
(1)
Induction gives ∥rj​∥≤kj∥y∥ and ∥xj​∥≤Rkj−1∥y∥. The series ∑j​xj​ is absolutely convergent in the Banach space V, because the sum of its norms is bounded by the convergent geometric series R∥y∥/(1−k). Write its sum as x. The bounded linear operator T commutes with this limit, while T∑j=1m​xj​=y−rm​→y. Consequently
Tx=y,∥x∥≤1−kR​∥y∥.​
(2)
The choices need not depend linearly on y; no bounded linear right inverse is being asserted.
For the bounded extension, use the restriction bounded linear operator S:Cb​(X;R)→Cb​(Y;R) on the Banach spaces of bounded continuous functions, each with its supremum norm. We will construct an approximation to any g with ∥g∥∞​=M>0. The two sets
A={y∈Y:g(y)≤−M/3},B={y∈Y:g(y)≥M/3}
(3)
are disjoint closed sets in X, since Y is closed. If both are nonempty, the distance to a set construction defines a continuous function
u(x)=3M​d(x,A)+d(x,B)d(x,A)−d(x,B)​.
(4)
The denominator is positive at every point: zero distance to a closed set means membership in that set. Thus ∥u∥∞​≤M/3, with u=−M/3 on A and u=M/3 on B. On A and B, and also on the intervening range ∣g∣<M/3, this implies
∥Su−g∥∞​≤2M/3.
(5)
If only A is empty take u=M/3, if only B is empty take u=−M/3, and if both are empty take u=0; the same bounds hold. For M=0 take u=0. This proves the approximation hypothesis with R=1/3 and k=2/3. Applying the argument above to S yields an element h of the bounded continuous functions with h∣Y​=g and ∥h∥∞​≤∥g∥∞​. Restriction gives the reverse inequality, so the extension preserves the exact supremum norm:
∥h∥∞​=∥g∥∞​.​
(6)
This proves the bounded metric-space case of the Tietze extension theorem rather than presupposing it.
For an arbitrary real continuous function f on Y, let g=tanhf. Its values lie strictly between −1 and 1, although its supremum norm can be 1. The bounded result supplies an extension h with ∣h∣≤1. Let D={x:∣h(x)∣=1}, a closed set disjoint from Y. If D is nonempty and Y is nonempty, put
η(x)=d(x,D)+d(x,Y)d(x,D)​,q(x)=η(x)h(x).
(7)
Both factors are continuous functions; η=1 on Y and η=0 on D. Off D we have ∣h∣<1, and on D we have q=0, so ∣q(x)∣<1 everywhere. Therefore a real continuous extension is
F(x)=arctanhq(x).​
(8)
On Y it equals f. If D is empty use q=h; if Y is empty simply take F=0. This handles unbounded f without requiring a uniform margin between ∣g∣ and 1.

Here ϕ is a finite real convex function on the whole real vector space; no topology or continuity is required. We prove the convex domination form of the Hahn-Banach theorem by extending across one vector. Suppose a linear functional f is already dominated on a vector subspace W and choose v∈/W. For w∈W and positive t,s, the proposed extension g(w+τv)=f(w)+τc must satisfy the bounds
L=supw∈W, t>0​tf(w)−ϕ(w−tv)​≤c≤U=infw∈W, s>0​sϕ(w+sv)−f(w)​.
(9)
To prove compatibility, take w1​,w2​∈W and t,s>0. The convex combination of w1​−tv and w2​+sv with weights s/(s+t) and t/(s+t) lies in W. Domination there and convexity give
sf(w1​)+tf(w2​)≤sϕ(w1​−tv)+tϕ(w2​+sv).
(10)
Rearranging proves every lower candidate is at most every upper candidate. Also the candidates w=0,t=s=1 show
−ϕ(−v)≤L≤U≤ϕ(v).
(11)
Thus both endpoints are finite, and some c∈[L,U] exists. For τ>0 the upper bound gives domination of f(w)+τc; for τ<0 the lower bound gives it; and for τ=0 domination was assumed. This defines a dominated linear functional on W+Rv extending f.
Order all dominated extensions by inclusion of their domains and agreement of values. A chain has the union extension, still linear and dominated. By Zorn's lemma there is a maximal extension. The one-vector construction would enlarge any proper domain, so its domain is V. Hence there is a full linear extension with g∣W​=f,g≤ϕ​. A convex function here need not be positively homogeneous; this is why the bounds included all positive s,t.
Now fix y∈V. The translated convex function φy​(z)=ϕ(y+z)−ϕ(y) vanishes at zero. Apply the just-proved extension theorem to the zero linear functional on {0} to obtain a linear functional ℓy​ with ℓy​(z)≤φy​(z) for every z. Set
ay​(x)=ϕ(y)+ℓy​(x−y).
(12)
This is an affine function, satisfies ay​(x)≤ϕ(x) for every x, and has ay​(y)=ϕ(y). Let A={ay​:y∈V}. Every member is an affine minorant, while the member indexed by x attains ϕ(x) at x. Thus the exact representation is
ϕ(x)=a∈Asup​a(x)(x∈V).​
(13)
This is the finite convex function as supremum of affine minorants property. The linear functionals are algebraic; without a topology no assertion of continuous affine minorants is intended.

Let F=C(X,[0,1]). Form the evaluation map into a product space
e:X⟶[0,1]F,e(x)=(f(x))f∈F​,βX=e(X)​.
(14)
The cube is compact by Tychonoff's theorem and Hausdorff, so its closed subspace βX is a compact Hausdorff space. A completely regular Hausdorff space has enough continuous functions to separate points and closed sets. Consequently e is injective and continuous, and is a topological embedding: given x∈O open, choose f with f(x)=0 and f=1 off O; the coordinate neighborhood f<1/2 pulls back to a neighborhood of x inside O. Identify X with this dense embedded subspace. This constructs the Stone-Čech compactification.
Each f∈F extends as its coordinate function. Rescaling therefore extends every bounded real continuous function; real and imaginary parts extend bounded complex continuous functions. The extension is unique because X is dense and the target is Hausdorff. Its supremum norm is unchanged by density, so restriction is an isometric isomorphism of normed spaces of Banach spaces
C(βX)≅Cb​(X).​
(15)
These are the space of continuous functions on a compact space and the space of bounded continuous functions, respectively.
More generally, embed a compact Hausdorff space K in its own evaluation cube [0,1]C(K,[0,1]). This is an embedding by complete regularity, and its image is closed by compactness. For a continuous map u:X→K, extend all the bounded coordinates f∘u to βX. The resulting continuous map into the cube takes its values in the closed copy of K: it does so on the dense subset X, and the inverse image of that closed copy is closed. Thus
u:X→K extends uniquely to u:βX→K.​
(16)
Uniqueness again follows from density. This is the universal property of the Stone-Čech compactification. It implies uniqueness of the compactification up to a homeomorphism fixing X, by extending the identity in both directions. Every Hausdorff compactification of X is the image of βX under a continuous surjection fixing X, since that image is compact and contains the dense copy of X. A continuous map X→Y between completely regular Hausdorff spaces likewise extends uniquely to βX→βY after composing with the embedding of Y; these extensions preserve identities and composition. If X is already compact, its dense copy is closed, so βX=X.
For discrete X, every indicator 1D​, D⊆X, is continuous. Its extension to βX takes only values 0,1, since this closed condition holds on the dense subset X. Its one-set is precisely D and is clopen: it is closed, and every neighborhood of one of its points meets D by density and continuity. This also gives the discrete Stone-Čech compactifications are extremally disconnected property: for open O, density implies O=O∩X, which is clopen.
For the requested separation, a compact Hausdorff space is normal, so Urysohn's lemma supplies u:βX→[0,1] with u=0 on A and u=1 on B. Put D={x∈X:u(x)<1/2} and
U=D,V=βX∖U.
(17)
The preceding indicator argument makes both sets clopen. At a point of A, the neighborhood u<1/2 has its dense X-part inside D, so the point lies in U. At a point of B, the neighborhood u>1/2 misses D, so the point lies outside U. We have proved the stronger clopen separation in discrete Stone-Čech compactifications conclusion
βX=U∪˙V,A⊆U,B⊆V,U,V clopen.​
(18)
Finally take K=βN​, the Stone-Čech compactification of the natural numbers. It is a compact Hausdorff space and is separable, because its embedded copy of N is countable and dense. For every D⊆N, the indicator extends to an element hD​∈C(K). For distinct D,D′, evaluating at an integer in their symmetric difference gives ∥hD​−hD′​∥∞​=1. There are uncountably many such indicators. A separable metric space cannot contain an uncountable family at pairwise distance one: disjoint balls of radius 1/3 would require distinct members of a countable dense set. Hence C(K) is not separable, despite separability of K.

The weak topology σ(E,E∗) is the coarsest topology making every continuous linear functional in E∗ continuous. A basic neighborhood of x imposes finitely many inequalities ∣fj​(z−x)∣<ε, and a sequence converges weakly precisely when each f(xn​) converges to f(x). The Hahn-Banach theorem makes the dual separate points, so the weak topology is Hausdorff.
Here is a proof of the requested weak subsequence property, the compact-to-sequential direction of the Eberlein-Šmulian theorem. Let Y be the closed linear span of the countable set {xn​:n≥1}. It is a separable Banach space. The Hahn-Banach theorem separates points outside a closed vector subspace from that subspace, so Y is weakly closed in E. Therefore C=K∩Y is weakly compact.
Choose a countable dense set (uj​) in the unit sphere of Y, and choose norming functionals fj​∈Y∗ with ∥fj​∥=1 and fj​(uj​)=1. They separate points: for y=0, a sufficiently close uj​ to y/∥y∥ gives ∣fj​(y)∣>0. This is a countable norming family. Extend the functionals to E by the Hahn-Banach theorem. The evaluation map
C⟶FN,y⟼(fj​(y))j≥1​
(19)
is weakly continuous and injective. Its domain is compact and its target Hausdorff, so it is a homeomorphism onto its image: images of closed subsets are compact, hence closed. A countable product of real or complex lines is metrizable, for example by ∑j​2−jmin(1,∣aj​−bj​∣). Thus C has a compact metrizable weak topology. Compact metric spaces are sequentially compact, giving a subsequence with xnj​​⇀x∈C⊆K​. If Y={0} the result is immediate. We did not assert that a separable Banach space has a separable dual or that its weak topology is generally metrizable.

Assume weakly null sequences have norm-null images. Let (yn​) be any sequence in T(K), where K is weakly compact, and choose xn​∈K with yn​=Txn​. By the weak subsequence result, some xnj​​⇀x∈K. Then xnj​​−x⇀0, and the assumed property gives
∥ynj​​−Tx∥=∥T(xnj​​−x)∥⟶0.
(20)
Thus every sequence in T(K) has a norm-convergent subsequence with limit in T(K). In a metric space, sequential compactness is equivalent to compactness. Therefore T(K) is norm compact​. This is one characterization of a completely continuous operator.

Assume instead that T sends weakly compact sets to norm-compact sets. If xn​⇀0, then K={0,x1​,x2​,…} is weakly compact: an open cover has a member containing zero, which contains all but finitely many terms of the convergent sequence. Hence T(K) is norm compact. A bounded linear operator is weak-to-weak continuous, because each g∈F∗ pulls back to g∘T∈E∗. Thus Txn​⇀0. If ∥Txn​∥ did not tend to zero, a subsequence with norms bounded below by some ε>0 would have a norm-convergent further subsequence with limit y. That further subsequence also converges weakly to both y and zero; uniqueness of limits in the Hausdorff weak topology forces y=0, contradicting the lower bound. Therefore ∥Txn​∥→0​, proving the equivalence and the characterization of completely continuous operators.
Now consider the inclusion of the space of continuous functions on a compact space C(T) into L2 space for normalized Lebesgue measure. It is a bounded linear operator, since ∥f∥2​≤∥f∥∞​. For a weakly null sequence (fn​) in C(T), point evaluations are continuous linear functionals, so fn​(t)→0 for every t. The Uniform boundedness principle, applied to the canonical embeddings fn​∈C(T)∗∗, gives a uniform bound ∥fn​∥∞​≤M: each dual functional is bounded on the weakly convergent sequence. The dominated convergence theorem now yields
∥j2​fn​∥22​=∫T​∣fn​∣2dμ⟶0.
(21)
Thus j2​ satisfies both equivalent conditions.
It is nevertheless not a compact operator. Over complex scalars the functions fn​(t)=eint lie in the unit ball of C(T) and form an orthonormal sequence in L2(μ), so
∥j2​fn​−j2​fm​∥2​=2​(n=m).​
(22)
There is no norm-convergent subsequence. Over real scalars use cos(nt), whose distinct images have distance 1. Complete continuity controls weakly compact sets, rather than all bounded sets.

For a bounded linear operator T:E→F, write the weak square norm of a finite family as
w2​(x1​,…,xm​)=supϕ∈E∗, ∥ϕ∥≤1​(∑j=1m​∣ϕ(xj​)∣2)1/2.
(23)
It is a 2-summing operator if (∑j​∥Txj​∥2)1/2≤Cw2​(x1​,…,xm​) for all finite families, and π2​(T) is the least such C. This specializes the definition of an absolutely p-summing operator.
First let E=H1​, F=H2​ be Hilbert spaces. For the column operator A:ℓ2m​→H1​, Aej​=xj​, the Riesz representation theorem gives w2​(x1​,…,xm​)=∥A∗∥=∥A∥. If T is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator, the Parseval identity applied to orthonormal bases gives
∑j=1m​∥Txj​∥2=∥TA∥HS2​=∑α​∥A∗T∗fα​∥2≤∥A∥2∑α​∥T∗fα​∥2=∥A∥2∥T∥HS2​.
(24)
Thus π2​(T)≤∥T∥HS​. Conversely test the summing inequality on any finite orthonormal family in H1​. Its weak square norm is 1, by the Bessel inequality, with equality by choosing one of its vectors. Hence ∑j​∥Tej​∥2≤π2​(T)2. Taking the supremum over finite subsets of an orthonormal basis proves the Hilbert-Schmidt operator condition and the reverse norm inequality. These basis sums work even for nonseparable Hilbert spaces, being understood as suprema of finite subsums. Therefore
T is Hilbert-Schmidt⟺T is 2-summing,π2​(T)=∥T∥HS​.​
(25)
For an n-dimensional normed vector space E, again associate a family (xj​) with A:ℓ2m​→E. By the Hahn-Banach theorem and scalar Euclidean duality,
∥A∥=sup∥ϕ∥≤1​(∑j​∣ϕ(xj​)∣2)1/2=w2​(x1​,…,xm​).
(26)
Let Q be the orthogonal projection onto (kerA)⊥, whose dimension is at most n. Since A=AQ, the trace of Q gives
∑j​∥xj​∥E2​≤∥A∥2∑j​∥Qej​∥22​=∥A∥2rankA≤nw2​(x1​,…,xm​)2.
(27)
This proves π2​(IE​)≤n​ for every norm, without comparing that norm to an arbitrary Euclidean norm.
To obtain equality and the projection bound, choose a maximum-volume centered ellipsoid inside the unit ball BE​ and use coordinates in which it is the Euclidean ball B2​. In the real case this is the John ellipsoid; in the complex case maximize among Hermitian ellipsoids. There are unit contact vectors ur​∈∂BE​∩∂B2​ and positive weights cr​ with the John contact decomposition
∑r​cr​ur​ur∗​=IE​,∑r​cr​=n.
(28)
For completeness, the maximum-volume argument gives this identity. If IE​ were outside the closed convex cone of the contact matrices, finite-dimensional separation would supply a symmetric, or Hermitian, matrix D with trD>0 and u∗Du≤0 at every contact. The cone is closed because its compact generators have trace one. Subtract a sufficiently small multiple of the identity to make all contact inequalities strict while retaining positive trace. The support function of (I+tD)B2​ decreases near the contact directions for small t>0; away from them the strict gap between the support functions of BE​ and B2​ permits the same small deformation. Thus this deformed ball still lies in BE​. Its volume increases, since the real determinant is 1+ttrD+O(t2) in the real case and ∣detC​(I+tD)∣2=1+2ttrD+O(t2) in the complex case. This contradicts maximality. The finite-dimensional convex-cone representation gives finitely many contacts, and taking traces gives the sum of the weights.
At a contact ur​, a supporting hyperplane to BE​ also supports B2​. The Euclidean ball has a unique supporting direction there, so the corresponding functional is ϕr​(x)=ur∗​x and has dual norm one on E. In the complex case balancedness of BE​ turns the real support bound into ∣ϕr​(x)∣≤∥x∥E​. Also B2​⊆BE​ gives ∥x∥E​≤∣x∣2​. For any dual-unit functional ϕ, this containment implies that its Euclidean norm is at most one. Apply the summing definition to xr​=cr​​ur​:
∑r​∥xr​∥E2​=∑r​cr​=n,∑r​∣ϕ(xr​)∣2=∣ϕ∣22​≤1.
(29)
The weak square norm is exactly one, since any contact functional attains that Euclidean norm. Hence π2​(IE​)≥n​. Together with the upper estimate, the norm is
π2​(IE​)=n​.​
(30)
This proves the 2-summing norm of a finite-dimensional identity formula.
Finally retain the preceding finite-dimensional hypothesis when E⊆X. Extend each ϕr​ to a dual-unit functional ϕ​r​ on X using the real or complex Hahn-Banach theorem. Define
P:X→E,Px=∑r​cr​ϕ​r​(x)ur​.
(31)
The contact identity gives P∣E​=IE​, hence P2=P and its range is E. The synthesis operator (ar​)↦∑r​cr​​ar​ur​ has Euclidean operator norm one, because its product with its adjoint is the identity. Therefore
∥Px∥E​≤∣Px∣2​≤(∑r​cr​∣ϕ​r​(x)∣2)1/2≤∑r​cr​​∥x∥X​=n​∥x∥X​.
(32)
Thus the linear projection satisfies ∥P∥≤n​​, the Kadec-Snobar projection bound. If n=0, the zero projection gives the same conclusion. No assertion about arbitrary infinite-dimensional subspaces is required.
