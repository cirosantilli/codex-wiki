For the example, take a once-punctured torus P, an essential nonseparating simple closed curve c⊂P, and
M=P×I,A=c×I.
(14)
The inclusion of the annulus A induces the injection ⟨c⟩↪π1​(P), so A is an incompressible surface. Cutting gives (P cut along c)×I, a pair of pants times an interval. A compact connected surface with boundary times an interval is a handlebody of genus 1−χ(P): cut the surface to a disk by proper arcs and reverse those cuts as one-handle attachments. Both the punctured torus and the pair of pants have Euler characteristic −1. Therefore
M and M cut along A are both genus-two handlebodies.​
(15)
This self-reproducing incompressible annulus cut also shows why arbitrary incompressible cutting choices need not terminate.
A three-manifold hierarchy is a finite sequence of cuts along properly embedded two-sided incompressible surfaces, with no spherical components, whose final manifold is a disjoint union of three-balls. Disks are permitted. In an orientable ambient manifold two-sided surfaces are orientable; for an ambient manifold which is not orientable we permit two-sided surfaces without assuming their orientability as well.
For S1×F, first cut along {p}×F, obtaining I×F. This surface is two-sided and its fundamental group injects as a direct factor. Next choose essential two-sided curves in F until the cut surface has boundary, and cut along their products with I. For orientable F one nonseparating curve suffices. For F which is not orientable and has genus at least two, the boundary of an embedded Möbius band is a two-sided essential curve; neither side is a disk, so its fundamental group injects. Cutting this curve leaves pieces with boundary, including the Möbius band. For each resulting compact surface choose a finite system of proper arcs cutting it into disks. The corresponding vertical disks give the remaining three-manifold hierarchy cuts. Essential annuli have injective fundamental group, and disks have trivial fundamental group, so all cuts are incompressible. The terminal products are D2×I, hence three-balls. This construction covers the possibilities without orientability in the stated range as well as the orientable ones.
Here is a terminating construction for a compact connected orientable irreducible three-manifold M with nonempty boundary. We use the permitted general results in the following precise forms: a boundary-incompressible non-ball component has a connected two-sided nonseparating incompressible surface with nonempty boundary; and a sufficiently large collection of disjoint closed incompressible surfaces has two parallel components with no intervening member. These are respectively the existence and finiteness results for incompressible surfaces.
First perform essential boundary disk compressions as long as possible. Their number is finite: each nonseparating compression decreases boundary genus, while a separating compression divides a boundary component into two of smaller genus. More explicitly, the sum of max(0,2g−1) over the closed boundary components decreases at every essential compression. Each cut preserves three-manifold irreducibility. To see this for any incompressible cutter, a sphere in the cut manifold bounds a three-ball in the uncut manifold. A closed nonspherical incompressible component of the cutter cannot lie in this three-ball, since its fundamental group could not inject into the trivial group. A component with boundary cannot lie in an interior three-ball either. Thus the three-ball misses the cutter and survives in the cut manifold. Fundamental-group injectivity of two-sided incompressible surfaces follows from the Loop theorem, applied to their two boundary copies after cutting.
Push the boundary of each non-ball residual component slightly into the original M to make a closed frontier F1​, omitting spherical components. This frontier is incompressible on its residual side, since all boundary compressions there have been exhausted. It is also incompressible on the side already cut off. Indeed, a hypothetical compression disk on that side can be made transverse to the earlier cutting surfaces. An innermost intersection circle bounds a disk on its cutting surface by incompressibility; replacing the corresponding subdisk removes the circle, without introducing intersections with earlier cutters. Repeat in the order of the cuts. The disk would finally lie in a product collar of the frontier, where its boundary is fundamental-group-injective, a contradiction. Thus F1​ is incompressible in M.
If a residual component is not a three-ball, choose the permitted nonseparating incompressible surface with boundary, cut it, and compress the new boundary fully again. Push that boundary in to obtain a new closed frontier F2​ disjoint from F1​. Continue. Three-manifold irreducibility persists, and the same innermost-circle proof makes every nonspherical frontier incompressible in the original M.
We explain why the parallel frontiers forced by the finiteness theorem cannot occur in this construction. The required product fact is: a connected nonseparating incompressible surface in G×I cannot be proper and disjoint from G×{1}. To prove it, intersect the surface with vertical annuli c×I for essential curves c in a closed G. Inessential intersection circles can be removed using incompressibility and the three-balls supplied by three-manifold irreducibility. An outermost intersection arc has both endpoints on the lower boundary; boundary compression along its annular bigon reduces the intersections. At least one resulting component stays nonseparating, since the mod-two relative class of the original surface is the sum of the resulting classes. Essential intersection circles can likewise be removed by surgery along the annulus running to its lower boundary. Retain a nonseparating incompressible component after each surgery. These operations reduce the intersection number and cut the base surface to one with boundary. Now intersect with the vertical disks of a proper arc system cutting that base to a disk. The same innermost-circle and outermost-arc operations make the surface disjoint from these disks. It then lies in a three-ball D2×I, where any proper two-sided surface separates. This is the contradiction proving the product fact.
If two frontiers in the construction were parallel with no other frontier between, their product region would contain one of the intervening nonseparating cutters, disjoint from the later frontier. The product fact forbids this. Consequently the closed-frontier construction has only finitely many stages. Once a residual irreducible component has only spherical boundary, push one boundary sphere inward. The three-ball it bounds cannot be on the collar side, which contains a boundary component of the manifold. The three-ball and the collar therefore exhaust that component, making it a three-ball. We have constructed a finite three-manifold hierarchy, and can choose all its cutting surfaces to have nonempty boundary.
We will use one further consequence in the map argument: these manifolds are aspherical. Work backwards through the three-manifold hierarchy. A three-ball is contractible. At one gluing stage, the van Kampen theorem normal form identifies the lifts of the cut pieces and cutting surface in the universal cover with a tree of spaces. The lifted pieces are contractible by induction, and the lifted cutting surfaces are contractible because the surfaces have boundary and are fundamental-group-injective. A finite subtree is contractible by removing terminal vertices; every sphere map meets only finitely many pieces. All higher homotopy groups therefore vanish at this stage, and induction proves asphericity from a three-manifold hierarchy. Apply this tree argument separately at each gluing stage.
