The specific intensity of a blackbody is direction-independent over its outward hemisphere. Its outward flux per unit frequency is therefore
Fν​=∫outward​Bν​cosθdΩ=2πBν​∫0π/2​cosθsinθdθ=πBν​.
(1)
Multiplying by the stellar surface area gives the spectral luminosity
Lν​=4π2Rs2​Bν​.​
(2)
Using the Planck law, the hydrogen-ionizing photon production rate follows by dividing each spectral contribution by its photon energy and integrating above the threshold:
QH​=∫IH​/h∞​hνLν​​dν=c28π2Rs2​​∫IH​/h∞​ehν/kTs​−1ν2​dν.
(3)
With x=hν/(kTs​) and y=IH​/(kTs​), this becomes
QH​=h3c28π2IH3​​y3Rs2​​∫y∞​ex−1x2​dx.
(4)
For the initial hydrogen-only photon balance, assume an ionization-bounded nebula at steady photoionization equilibrium, negligible dust absorption and ionizing-photon leakage, negligible collisional ionization, and local reabsorption of photons from direct ground-state recombinations. A ground-state recombination then causes another photoionization and produces no net removal of a proton. Net removal is counted by Case B recombination, whose coefficient is β=∑n≥2​αn​. Integrating the local balance gives
∫Ne​N+​βdV=QH​=h3c28π2IH3​​y3Rs2​​∫y∞​ex−1x2​dx.​
(5)
The stellar temperature Ts​ sets the photon supply; the gas temperature sets the recombination coefficients. They are not generally equal. For uniform hydrogen and constant gas temperature, the Strömgren radius is
RH​=(4πβH​Ne​NH​3QH​​)1/3,
(6)
with Ne​=NH​ in fully ionized pure hydrogen.
For the hydrogen-helium mixture, define the blackbody ionizing photon function
F(y)=∫y∞​ex−1x2​dx,Q(E0​)=h3c28π2Rs2​(kTs​)3​F(E0​/kTs​).
(7)
The three relevant thresholds are approximately 13.6, 24.6 and 54.4eV, for H0, He0 and He+ respectively. Higher stellar temperature increases the relative supply of the harder photons. These cumulative photon rates cannot be assigned independently to the three species: a photon above a helium threshold can instead ionize hydrogen, and helium recombination radiation can also ionize hydrogen. The absorption cross-sections and the diffuse radiation determine the effective allocation.
A useful sharp-front model of nested hydrogen-helium ionization zones has an inner He++ sphere of radius R2​, a He+ shell ending at R1​, and an outer hydrogen-ionized region ending at RH​, with R2​≤R1​≤RH​. Let nH​,nHe​ be uniform nuclear number densities. The electron number densities in these three regions are nH​+2nHe​, nH​+nHe​ and nH​. For constant gas temperature, integrating the recombination rates gives
QHeff​QHeIeff​QHeIIeff​​=34π​βH​nH​{nH​RH3​+nHe​(R13​+R23​)},=34π​βHeI​nHe​(nH​+nHe​)(R13​−R23​),=34π​βHeII​nHe​(nH​+2nHe​)R23​.​
(8)
Here the effective budgets mean net species-specific ionizations after eliminating local ground-state recycling. Their calculation must include photon competition and any interspecies diffuse contribution; the helium coefficients label the recombining ion's emitted spectrum. Once these budgets are specified, solve the third equation for R23​, the second for R13​−R23​, and the first for RH3​.
For an isolated helium front with approximately common electron number density, the useful scaling is
(RH​RHe​​)3≃QHeff​QHeeff​​nHe​βHe​nH​βH​​.
(9)
Thus the small helium abundance partly compensates for the smaller hard-photon supply. A cooler central star produces little He++ and can have a substantially smaller helium-ionized zone. A sufficiently hot star can ionize helium throughout most of the hydrogen region. If independent radius estimates place a helium front beyond the hydrogen front, the independent photon allocations are inconsistent: the fronts and transfer must be treated together. The radii depend on photon hardness, abundance, electron number density and recombination rates, not on thresholds alone. The sharp-front equations are an approximation; they do not assert three exact independently conserved photon budgets for a real mixed nebula.
For a nonuniform nebula, an observed recombination line with effective recombination coefficient αℓeff​ has luminosity
Lℓ​=hνℓ​∫Ne​Nparent​αℓeff​dV.
(10)
If gas temperature and density permit a known approximately constant ratio β/αℓeff​, the integrated line gives
Qeff=αℓeff​β​hνℓ​Lℓ​​.
(11)
The unknown density distribution cancels because the same recombination integral appears in both quantities. For a stellar continuum measurement at a nonionizing frequency νo​,
Fνo​,∗​=πBνo​​(Ts​)d2Rs2​​.
(12)
When the inferred recombination budget traces the full appropriate ionizing-photon supply, define ΦE0​​(T)=∫E0​/h∞​Bν​(T)/(hν)dν and obtain the Zanstra method equation
hνℓ​Fνo​,∗​Fℓ​​αℓeff​β​=Bνo​​(Ts​)ΦE0​​(Ts​)​.​
(13)
Both Rs​ and the distance d cancel. Hydrogen and helium recombination lines probe different hardness thresholds, so their ratios and the stellar continuum constrain Ts​ and test the assumed spectrum and trapping. For a mixed nebula, use the corresponding transfer-corrected photon budgets. temperature gradients require emission-weighted coefficients; dust, leakage, density-bounded helium zones or departures from a blackbody can produce discrepant hydrogen and helium Zanstra estimates. Line fluxes alone without an appropriate budget or continuum normalization need not uniquely determine Ts​.
Finally, locate the derived Ts​ and luminosity on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram. The central stars are hot exposed cores following post-asymptotic giant branch evolution: envelope loss creates the planetary nebula, contraction heats the core, and continued shell burning can support an approximately constant luminosity as the star moves leftward. Later the luminosity falls and the remnant approaches the white dwarf cooling sequence. Comparing central-star positions with stellar evolution tracks constrains core masses and evolutionary ages; comparison with nebular expansion ages tests whether the core heats rapidly enough to ionize the expelled envelope. The central-star locus traces the transition from an envelope-losing giant to a compact stellar remnant.

The Boltzmann distribution for two atomic levels at thermodynamic equilibrium is
Nb​Na​​=ωb​ωa​​exp[−kTEa​−Eb​​],​
(14)
where ω is the statistical weight of an atomic level. For a resolved level of total angular momentum J, ω=2J+1 when magnetic substates are unresolved.
To extend this counting to the continuum, assume an ideal, nondegenerate plasma with classical free Electrons and neglect the small difference between the translational masses of the two heavy ionic species. Let ze​=eμe​/kT be the electron fugacity in the Saha equation, with the Electron chemical potential measured relative to its rest energy. The two Electron spin states and the phase-space cell volume h3 give
Ne​=h32ze​​∫R3​e−p2/(2mkT)d3p=2ze​(h22πmkT​)3/2.
(15)
Chemical equilibrium for capture equates the bound species' chemical potential to the sum for its parent ion and free Electron. Thus applying Boltzmann factors to a bound level at energy −In​ gives Nn​/N+​=(ωn​/ω+​)ze​eIn​/kT. Eliminating the Electron fugacity proves the level-specific Saha ionization equation:
Ne​N+​Nn​​=2ω+​ωn​​(2πmkTh2​)3/2eIn​/kT.​
(16)
The factor two is the Electron spin multiplicity; the factor with power 3/2 is the inverse free-electron translational state density. A total-ionization Saha equation sums the bound states into an internal partition function, whereas this expression refers to one specified level.
For dielectronic recombination, write the entrance ion as X(z+1)+ in ground state i, and the intermediate state of Xz+ as d=(j,nℓ). Capture excites the core from i to j while binding the incident Electron. The resonance energy relative to the entrance continuum is Eˉ=Eij​−Inℓ​>0. The doubly excited state can eject the Electron by autoionization, or undergo radiative stabilization to a bound state.
Let Adia​ be the partial autoionization rate back to the entrance ion, Aa​ the total autoionization rate, Ar​ the total radiative decay rate, and As​ the rate of radiative decays that stabilize into bound levels. For an isolated narrow resonance, the Saha-Boltzmann equilibrium ratio has the bound-state energy replaced by the positive resonance energy:
Ne​Ni​Ndeq​​=2gi​gd​​(2πmkTh2​)3/2e−Eˉ/kT.
(17)
At equilibrium, inverse dielectronic capture and autoionization fluxes balance: Ne​Ni​Cd​=Ndeq​Adia​. This establishes the capture coefficient even when the actual plasma is not in local thermodynamic equilibrium. In the low-density, weak-radiation recombining plasma, the intermediate level instead has the stationary balance
Ne​Ni​Cd​=Nd​(Aa​+Ar​).
(18)
Multiplying its population by the successful stabilization rate gives the actual recombination coefficient
αd​(d)=2gi​gd​​(2πmkTh2​)3/2e−Eˉ/kTAa​+Ar​Adia​As​​.​
(19)
Here the equilibrium population was used only to determine the capture rate by detailed balance; it was not assumed to equal the intermediate population when photons escape. This is precisely why the competing decay probabilities appear.
Use the supplied core-transition relation between the Einstein coefficients and atomic oscillator strength,
Aji​=2a0​α4c​(IH​Eij​​)2gj​gi​​fij​,2πmh2​=4πa02​IH​.
(20)
Here α is the fine-structure constant and a0​ the Bohr radius, not a recombination coefficient. Factor out Aji​ and define
βj,nℓ​=2gj​gd​​Aji​(Aa​+Ar​)Adia​As​​.​
(21)
Substitution yields
αd​(j,nℓ)​=4π3/2α4a02​cIH1/2​(kT)3/2Eij2​​e−Eˉ/kTfij​βj,nℓ​=4π3/2α4a02​c(IH​Eij​​)1/2(kTEij​​)3/2e−Eˉ/kTfij​βj,nℓ​.​​
(22)
This definition accommodates several entrance and decay channels. In the common spectator-electron approximation with one autoionization channel and stabilization by the core transition, As​=Ar​=Aji​ and Adia​=Aa​. If all coupled substates of the captured nℓ Electron are summed, gd​=2(2ℓ+1)gj​, giving
βj,nℓ​=(2ℓ+1)Aa​+Aji​Aa​​.​
(23)
For an intermediate state resolved by fine structure, retain its actual gd​ instead of that summed weight. The stabilization probability itself is As​/(Aa​+Ar​); the requested βj,nℓ​ is not just this branching probability, because the prefactor has already factored out the core radiative rate and statistical weights of atomic levels. Radiative transitions to another still-autoionizing state require that state's eventual survival probability, rather than automatically counting every emitted photon as stabilization.
The total dielectronic recombination coefficient sums all accessible positive-energy resonances. In this isolated-resonance approximation it has the form
αd​(T)=T−3/2∑d​cd​e−Eˉd​/kT,cd​≥0,
(24)
with atomic factors included in cd​. The resonance-temperature dependence of dielectronic recombination follows by differentiating a single term's logarithm:
dTd​logαd​(d)=−2T3​+kT2Eˉd​​,kTpeak​=32​Eˉd​.​
(25)
Thus low temperatures suppress a positive-energy resonance, although resonances very near threshold can still matter; at sufficiently high temperature a fixed set of resonances falls as T−3/2. Several core-excitation series can produce several peaks. When autoionization is much faster than radiation, the capture-and-survival product is limited by the stabilizing radiative rate; when capture is weak, autoionization is the limiting inverse-capture rate. For a fixed Rydberg angular channel, autoionization typically decreases at high principal quantum number while the core radiative rate changes little. Capture into very high levels is vulnerable to subsequent collisional or field ionization, so finite-density survival can suppress the total rate. dielectronic recombination is resonant and strongly temperature-selective; its magnitude can be important in coronal ionization balance.

The solar corona is a hot, dilute plasma in which collisional ionization mainly proceeds by direct Electron impact and by excitation-autoionization. The principal inverse ion-changing processes are radiative recombination and dielectronic recombination. Three-body recombination is negligible at ordinary coronal densities. Photoionization, stimulated recombination and charge exchange are normally secondary in this fully ionized, collision-dominated regime, although they should be reconsidered near irradiated or neutral interfaces.
For collisional ionization equilibrium, the adjacent ion stages satisfy
Ne​Nq​Cq​(Te​)=Ne​Nq+1​αq+1​(Te​),
(26)
together with conservation of the element's total abundance. Thus the low-density ion fraction depends primarily on Te​. This fixes the amount of Si X available for line emission, but not the populations of its excited levels: a coronal approximation does not impose a Boltzmann level distribution.
Within an ion, Electrons collisionally excite, de-excite and redistribute levels. Their collision rate coefficient for an atomic transition is
qij​(Te​)=∫0∞​σij​(v)vfTe​​(v)dv.
(27)
The rate per ion is Ne​qij​. Maxwellian detailed balance gives, for Ej​>Ei​,
qji​=gj​gi​​e(Ej​−Ei​)/kTe​qij​.
(28)
Strong allowed transitions undergo rapid spontaneous emission; forbidden magnetic-dipole and electric-quadrupole transitions give much smaller decay rates and can leave a metastable atomic level appreciably populated. Radiative cascades feed levels from higher states. Absorption and stimulated emission add rates proportional to the local radiation field through the Einstein coefficients when significant. Proton collisions can also mix closely separated levels separated by fine structure and should be included when their rates matter.
Write fi​=Ni​/N(Si X). A stationary collisional-radiative model solves
j=i∑​fj​Pji​−fi​j=i∑​Pij​=0,i∑​fi​=1,​
(29)
where, in the simplest electron-plus-radiative model, Pij​=Ne​qij​+Aij​ with Aij​=0 for an upward transition. Add proton and radiation rates when appropriate. Include enough levels and radiative branches to capture population feeding. The thermal collision data, radiative probabilities and wavelengths then determine each line's optically thin atomic line intensity:
Iul​=4πhνul​​∫N(Si X)fu​(Ne​,Te​)Aul​ds.​
(30)
The factor 4π distributes isotropic emission over solid angle. photon intensities omit hν. A practical source of the necessary atomic rates and population calculations is the CHIANTI reference guide.
Label the two ground-configuration levels 1,2 and the two indicated excited levels 3,4:
13​:2s22p2P1/2​,:2s2p22D3/2​,​24​:2s22p2P3/2​,:2s2p22D5/2​.​
(31)
The common 1s2 core is omitted. Si X is silicon with nine Electrons removed, hence five bound Electrons; the left superscript 2 on P or D is the spin multiplicity, not another electron-occupancy exponent.
The observed lines correspond to decays 3→1 and 4→2. For approximately uniform plasma at the indicated Te​≃1.3×106K, form the theoretical energy-intensity ratio
R(Ne​)=I347​I356​​=ν31​A31​f3​(Ne​,Te​)ν42​A42​f4​(Ne​,Te​)​.​
(32)
The common abundance, Si X fraction and path length cancel, leaving a ratio governed by level populations. The frequency factor is ν42​/ν31​=347/356 for the rounded wavelengths. With observed photon counts, first apply the detector calibration and use the corresponding photon-ratio convention.
The electron-density diagnostic from metastable populations can be seen in a reduced rate model. The upper ground level 2 has only a weak radiative decay to 1. Ignoring higher-level feeding for this explanation, their balance gives
r:=N1​N2​​=A21​+Ne​q21​Ne​q12​​.
(33)
At low density r≃Ne​q12​/A21​; above the critical density of an atomic transition A21​/q21​ it approaches q12​/q21​=(g2​/g1​)e−ΔE21​/kTe​, approximately two when the gap due to fine structure is small compared with kTe​. This saturation can occur while the strong upper-level transitions still remain radiatively dominated.
Let A3​,A4​ be the total radiative loss rates of levels 3,4, and b31​=A31​/A3​, b42​=A42​/A4​ their line branching fractions. In that upper-level low-density regime,
N3​≃A3​Ne​(N1​q13​+N2​q23​)​,N4​≃A4​Ne​(N1​q14​+N2​q24​)​.
(34)
Thus
R≃Kq13​+rq23​q14​+rq24​​,K=ν31​b31​ν42​b42​​.
(35)
The two lines receive different relative feeding from the two ground levels, so their ratio changes as collisions populate the metastable level. More explicitly,
drdR​=K(q13​+rq23​)2q24​q13​−q14​q23​​.
(36)
Different, nonproportional feeding coefficients give a usable density-sensitive interval. With s=R/K, this reduced model even permits explicit inversion:
r=q24​−sq23​sq13​−q14​​,Ne​=q12​−rq21​A21​r​.​
(37)
Physical solutions require positive density and a ratio within the allowed calibration range. The actual Si X calibration should use the full multilevel population system, not discard cross-excitation, cascades or all competing branches simply to use this illustrative formula.
Operationally, evaluate the model ratio at the known temperature over a density grid and match the calibrated observed ratio to it, propagating line-flux and atomic-data uncertainties. No observed ratio or numerical atomic rate set is supplied here, so the result is this inference procedure rather than a numerical density. At low- or high-density saturation, the ratio constrains density poorly. Deconvolve blends or sum every unresolved transition in the theoretical feature; for example, the neighboring 2D3/2​→2P3/2​ branch can contribute near the 356-Angstrom feature when unresolved. Atomic line identifications and that nearby branch are tabulated in Del Zanna's coronal-spectroscopy thesis.
For a nonuniform sightline, the ratio is instead
Robs​=∫j347​(s)ds∫j347​(s)R(Ne​(s),Te​(s))ds​.
(38)
A single-density inversion gives an emission-weighted effective density from a line ratio, generally not the volume-average electron number density. The density estimate is meaningful within its temperature, optical-depth, line-blending and spatial-uniformity assumptions. This is the precise interpretation of an average density obtained from the observed pair.
