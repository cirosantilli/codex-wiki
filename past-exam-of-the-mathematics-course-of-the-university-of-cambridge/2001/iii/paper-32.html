Work in a regular statistical model, with fixed support, differentiability under the integral, and nonsingular Fisher information. Let U=(Uψ​,Uλ​) be the score function. The blocks are orthogonal statistical parameters at θ when
Iψλ​(θ)=Eθ​[Uψ​UλT​]=−Eθ​[ℓψλ​]=0.​
(1)
This is global parameter orthogonality if it holds throughout the parameter domain; otherwise it is an assertion at a specified point.
For an independent size-n sample, the regular maximum-likelihood estimator satisfies
n​(ψ​−ψλ−λ​) d​ N(0,(Iψψ−1​0​0Iλλ−1​​)),
(2)
where I is the information in one observation. Thus the fitted blocks are independent to first order in their limiting normal distribution. The efficient information for ψ is generally the Schur complement Iψψ​−Iψλ​Iλλ−1​Iλψ​; under orthogonality it is just Iψψ​. Estimating the nuisance block therefore has no first-order variance cost for the interest block, compared with knowing the nuisance parameter.
This also reduces local movement of the constrained nuisance fit. If j=−ℓ′′ is the observed information, differentiating its nuisance score equation gives
∂ψT∂λψ​​=−jλλ−1​jλψ​.
(3)

Partition the statistical parameter as θ=(ψ,λ), with ψ of interest. For each fixed ψ, maximize over the nuisance parameter to obtain λψ​. The profile likelihood and profile log-likelihood are
Lp​(ψ)=L(ψ,λψ​),ℓp​(ψ)=ℓ(ψ,λψ​).
(7)

jp​=jψψ​−jψλ​jλλ−1​jλψ​.
(8)

Under the regular conditions of Wilks theorem, 2{ℓp​(ψ​)−ℓp​(ψ0​)} has a limiting chi-squared distribution with dimension of ψ degrees of freedom. Profiling is maximization, rather than integration or conditioning; Lp​ need not be a normalized data density, and nuisance fitting can cause appreciable higher-order distortion.
The modified profile likelihood corrects for nuisance curvature and sample-coordinate distortion. In suitable coordinates (ψ​,λ,a), with a an ancillary statistic, set
Jψ​=​det∂λ∂λψ​​​,

(14)

In particular, the modified coefficient accounts for the fitted mean nuisance parameter.

Let a group G act on the sample space. A statistic T is a maximal invariant when it is invariant and separates group orbits:
T(gx)=T(x),T(x)=T(y) ⟹ y=gx for some g∈G.
(42)
Together these conditions say that T(x)=T(y) exactly when x and y belong to the same orbit.
The basic result is that invariant tests factor through a maximal invariant. Specifically, a test function φ(x)∈[0,1] is invariant exactly when it can be written b(T(x)). To prove the nontrivial direction, define b(t)=φ(x) for any representative with T(x)=t. Two representatives are in the same orbit by maximality, and invariance gives them the same test value, so b is well-defined. Conversely b(T(gx))=b(T(x)) proves invariance. Measurability is taken on the quotient statistic space; for the finite rank spaces used below it is automatic.
If a null family is transitive under the group action, the null law of T is parameter-free. Indeed, if Pθ′​ is the law of gX when X∼Pθ​, then for every measurable set B,
Pθ′​{T∈B}=Pθ​{T(gX)∈B}=Pθ​{T(X)∈B}.
(43)
Thus invariant procedures use exactly the information in T, and its common null law calibrates tests without estimating the transformed nuisance distribution. This explains the role of maximal invariants in nonparametric statistics.
For simultaneous strictly increasing bijections of the real line, the labeled ranks of observations are a maximal invariant on samples without ties. Increasing maps preserve every comparison. Conversely, two such samples with the same ranks can be matched by a piecewise linear increasing bijection through their corresponding ordered observations, extended with positive-slope tails. This proves ranks as a maximal invariant under increasing transformations. Under independent sampling from any common continuous distribution, every labeled ordering is equally likely by exchangeability. Hence rank tests have distribution-free null calibration even without a global transitivity assertion for the entire class of continuous distributions.

The density version needs the corresponding local smoothness. A symmetric distribution has γ3​=0, removing the first correction. Truncation can produce negative densities or nonmonotone distribution functions far from the center; it is not automatically a uniformly reliable relative tail approximation. A lattice distribution needs its lattice correction.
A Laplace approximation instead approximates an integral dominated by a sharp maximum. Suppose h has a unique interior maximizer θ∈Rd, with A=−h′′(θ) positive definite, and the integral is localized near that point. For smooth amplitude g,
In​=∫g(θ)enh(θ)dθ.

The Edgeworth series corrects a sampling distribution through its cumulants and Fourier inversion; Laplace's method corrects an integral through local derivatives at its dominant point. Neither should be confused with simply replacing a likelihood by a normalized posterior density.

Consider a regular d-parameter statistical model for which the data can locally be expressed as (v,a), where v=θ is the maximum-likelihood estimator and a is a suitable exact or higher-order ancillary statistic. Write the log-likelihood as ℓ(θ;v,a), and let
j(v;v,a)=−∂θ​∂θT​ℓ(θ;v,a)​θ=v​
(58)
be its fitted observed information. The P-star approximation to the conditional sampling density of v, given a, is
p∗(v∣a;θ)=c(θ,a)∣j(v;v,a)∣1/2exp{ℓ(θ;v,a)−ℓ(v;v,a)}.​
(59)
The normalizing factor is independent of v and is chosen so the density integrates to one over the feasible estimator coordinates. Its leading Gaussian value is (2π)−d/2, but exact normalization can change it. Both the fitted likelihood and the fitted information vary with v; the observed dataset is not held fixed while integrating over this sampling coordinate.
To see the structure, expand the likelihood difference for v close to θ. Its leading term is minus one half of the information-weighted quadratic displacement, while ∣j(v)∣1/2 supplies the local density scale. This recovers the first-order multivariate normal distribution of an efficient maximum-likelihood estimator. The formula is invariant under smooth one-to-one parameter changes: at a likelihood maximum the score vanishes, so the Hessian transforms as a quadratic form. Its square-root determinant supplies exactly the Jacobian determinant required for the estimator density to transform.
There is a direct derivation in a full regular exponential family. Let S be the sufficient statistic for an independent sample, with likelihood
ℓ(θ;s)=θTs−nκ(θ)+data term.
(60)
The maximum-likelihood equation is s=n∇κ(v), and its derivative is j(v)=nκ′′(v). Applying a multivariate saddlepoint density approximation to S gives
fS​(s;θ)≃(2π)−d/2∣j(v)∣−1/2exp{ℓ(θ;s)−ℓ(v;s)}.
(61)
Changing variables from s to v multiplies by ∣ds/dv∣=∣j(v)∣, producing the displayed P-star form. In more general models, ancillary conditioning supplies the relevant local sample coordinates; an ancillary is not an arbitrary extra statistic.
For an illustrative exact case, take an independent sample from an exponential distribution with mean θ, so v=Yˉ. The fitted observed information is j(v)=n/v2, and
ℓ(θ;v)−ℓ(v;v)=nlog(v/θ)−nv/θ+n.
(62)
Thus P-star has density shape vn−1θ−ne−nv/θ. Normalizing gives
p∗(v;θ)=Γ(n)θnnn​vn−1e−nv/θ,v>0,
(63)
which is exactly the gamma distribution sampling density of the mean. Here sample ratios provide ancillary coordinates and are independent of the mean.
In suitable regular models the unnormalized leading construction has relative error of order n−1, and higher-order ancillary constructions with normalization can attain relative order n−3/2. Such accuracy needs the stated smoothness and ancillary hypotheses; it is not a universal consequence of asymptotic normality. The exact scale-model example is likewise a special property, not a general identity. The formula is useful for refined conditional inference, score distributions, and nuisance-likelihood adjustments, including modified profile likelihood. It describes a sampling density of the estimator, rather than a posterior density of the parameter.

A finite-dimensional exponential family has common dominating measure and density
f(y;θ)=h(y)exp{η(θ)TT(y)−κ(η(θ))}.
(64)
Here η is the natural parameter of an exponential family, and normalization determines the cumulant function of an exponential family κ. A full regular family lets η vary over an open natural parameter domain. A curved exponential family restricts η to a lower-dimensional submanifold, while a minimal representation removes redundant affine combinations of the statistic.
Differentiating the normalization under regularity gives
∇κ(η)=Eη​T(Y),κ′′(η)=covη​(T(Y)).
(65)
Consequently κ is convex and its Hessian is the Fisher information matrix in natural coordinates. For an independent size-n sample, S=∑i​T(Yi​) is a sufficient statistic: the joint density factors into ∏i​h(yi​) times exp{ηTS−nκ(η)}, so the conditional law given S loses its parameter dependence. An interior maximum-likelihood estimator solves
S/n=∇κ(η​),
(66)
matching empirical and theoretical sufficient-statistic means. Under minimality the Hessian is positive definite, so the log-likelihood is strictly concave; existence of an interior maximizer still needs the observed statistic to be in the appropriate interior convex support.
For a full open natural family, the canonical sufficient statistic is a complete statistic: if Eη​g(S)=0 throughout the open domain, exponential tilting about an interior point makes the Laplace transform of the integrable signed measure g(s)Pη0​​(ds) zero on a neighborhood. Transform uniqueness makes that measure zero, so g(S)=0 almost surely. This requires integrability under the family. It is not an automatic property of curved restrictions. The sufficient-statistic reduction, explicit information and cumulant identities make these families especially useful for exact and higher-order inference. Normal distributions, Poisson distributions and gamma distributions supply familiar examples.
A transformation model is built differently: a group acts on the sample and parameter spaces, and its probability laws satisfy
Y∼Pθ​⟹gY∼Pgθ​.
(67)
In a transitive model all laws are transforms of a fixed reference law. The location family is generated by translations, with density fμ​(y)=f0​(y−μ). The positive-scale family has density fσ​(y)=σ−1f0​(y/σ), and the location-scale family combines them as
fμ,σ​(y)=σ−1f0​((y−μ)/σ),σ>0.
(68)
The Jacobian factor is essential. For independent observations its power is σ−n. The base density need not have moments, and a transformation model need not have fixed support.
An equivariant estimator respects the parameter action. With a well-defined equivariant fit T(y) valued in the transformation group, the normalized sample
a(y)=T(y)−1y
(69)
is a maximal invariant. Equivariance gives a(gy)=a(y); if normalized samples agree, y′ is obtained from y by T(y′)T(y)−1. In a transitive transformation model its law is parameter-free, so it is an ancillary statistic. For location and positive scale, centered standardized residuals provide this construction on nondegenerate samples. If the parameter action has a nontrivial stabilizer, normalization must retain its quotient action rather than pretending that a unique group element is determined.
This geometry motivates conditioning on an ancillary and using the P-star approximation for the fitted parameter. Ancillarity means parameter-free distribution; it does not by itself assert independence from the estimator. In a normal location model with known scale, the sample mean and centered residuals are independent by their joint Gaussian law and zero covariance. In an exponential scale model, the sample sum and its normalized proportions are independent, again an additional model property.
The two classes overlap but are distinct. A normal location-scale family is both an exponential family and a transformation model; an exponential mean family is both a one-dimensional natural exponential family and a scale model. A general Cauchy distribution location family is a transformation model without a fixed finite-dimensional regular exponential-family sufficient-statistic reduction. Exponential-family structure emphasizes sufficient statistics and natural-parameter convexity; transformation structure emphasizes equivariance, group orbits and ancillary reduction.
