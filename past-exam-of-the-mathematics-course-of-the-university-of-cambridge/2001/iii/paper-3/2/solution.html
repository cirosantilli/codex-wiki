A soluble group is one whose derived series terminates: G(0)=G, G(i+1)=[G(i),G(i)], and G(r)=1 for some finite r. Each factor of this series is abelian. Conversely, if a subnormal series has abelian factors, repeated commutation moves down the series, so the derived series terminates. This explains the usual alternative definition in terms of abelian factors. The least r is the derived length.
Subgroups are soluble because H(i)≤G(i); quotients are soluble because a group homomorphism maps a derived subgroup onto the derived subgroup of its image. Solubility is also closed under extensions: if N◃G has derived length at most a and G/N has length at most b, then G(b)≤N and G(a+b)=1. Abelian and nilpotent groups are soluble; S3​ has derived subgroup A3​ and derived length two. In a finite group, solubility is equivalent to every composition factor being cyclic of prime order: simple soluble groups are abelian and therefore cyclic of prime order, while a composition series with these factors builds a soluble group by extensions. This need not give an ambient-normal series with cyclic prime-order factors, which is the stronger property of supersolubility.
A Hall subgroup for a prime set π is a subgroup H whose order uses only primes in π and whose index uses only primes outside π. Its order is the full π-part of ∣G∣. Sylow's theorem is the singleton-prime case; solubility allows all prime sets, although Hall subgroups need not be normal, as the order-two subgroups of S3​ show.
Prove Hall subgroup existence in soluble groups by induction on ∣G∣. The trivial group is immediate. Choose a nontrivial minimal normal subgroup N. It is elementary abelian of some prime characteristic p: N′ is characteristic in N and normal in G, so minimality and solubility force N′=1. A nontrivial Sylow subgroup of this finite abelian group is characteristic, making N a p-group. The subgroup of pth powers is characteristic and proper, so minimality makes it trivial. This proves minimal normal subgroups of finite solvable groups are elementary abelian.
By induction G/N has a Hall π-subgroup H. Let K be its inverse image. Then [G:K] is a π′-number. If p∈π, the whole K is a π-group and is the desired Hall subgroup. If p∈/π, K/N=Q=H has order s prime to p. We now prove the required complement existence explicitly, rather than invoking a splitting theorem as a substitute.
Write N additively as an Fp​-vector space. Choose representatives u(x) for x∈Q with u(1)=1. Conjugation gives a well-defined action of Q on N, because changing a representative by an element of the abelian group N does not change that action. Define f(x,y)∈N by u(x)u(y)=f(x,y)u(xy). Associativity yields
f(x,y)+f(xy,z)=x⋅f(y,z)+f(x,yz).
(2)
Since s is invertible in Fp​, set c(x)=s−1∑z∈Q​f(x,z). Sum the identity over z and use the bijection z↦yz to obtain
f(x,y)=c(x)+x⋅c(y)−c(xy).
(3)
Changing representatives to u′(x)=(−c(x))u(x) removes the multiplication error:
u′(x)u′(y)=u′(xy).
(4)
Thus x↦u′(x) is a homomorphic section. Its image H has order ∣Q∣, intersects N trivially, and complements N in K. This is coprime splitting over an elementary abelian normal subgroup. Finally ∣H∣ is a π-number and [G:H]=[G:K]∣N∣ is a π′-number, so
every finite soluble G has a Hall π-subgroup for every prime set π.​
(5)
Only existence was needed here; the fuller Hall theorem also gives conjugacy and containment of arbitrary π-subgroups. Solubility makes the elementary-abelian minimal-normal induction possible and cannot simply be omitted.
