For the exit law, the two identified processes have the same argument. Starting at zero, neither can skip −a or b: Brownian motion is continuous, and the symmetric Poisson difference process has unit jumps with integer boundary levels. Therefore ∣Xt∧T​∣≤K=max(a,b), and if T is finite, XT​∈{−a,b}.
To justify finiteness rather than presume it, apply the optional stopping theorem to the martingale Xt2​−t at the bounded stopping time t∧T:
E(t∧T)=EXt∧T2​≤K2.
(48)
The first boundary hitting time is a stopping time in both cases: in the continuous case use the continuous-path hitting criterion, and in the jump case inspect the successive jump times. Monotone convergence now gives ET≤K2, so T<∞ almost surely. The stopped position tends to XT​ as t→∞ and is bounded, making dominated convergence applicable. Stopping X at t∧T and taking this limit yields EXT​=0.
If π=P(XT​=−a), then 0=−aπ+b(1−π). Thus in both cases the entire distribution is
P(XT​=−a)=a+bb​,P(XT​=b)=a+ba​.​
(49)
This is the same balanced gambler's ruin law despite the different one-time distributions. As a further consistency check, passing to the limit in the stopped square-martingale identity gives
ET=EXT2​=a2a+bb​+b2a+ba​=ab.
(50)
All uses of an unbounded exit time were obtained by limits of bounded-time sampling with an explicit bound on the stopped position.
