Let M be the given closed three-dimensional manifold. It is connected by the meaning of simply connected. An orientation chosen at one point can be transported along paths, and its sign change around loops defines a homomorphism π1​(M)→{±1}. Since the fundamental group is trivial, this monodromy vanishes; thus M is orientable.
The first homology group is the abelianization of the fundamental group, giving H1​(M;Z)=0. Also H0​(M;Z)=Z. The universal coefficient theorem for cohomology in degree one gives
0⟶ExtZ1​(H0​(M),Z)⟶H1(M;Z)⟶Hom(H1​(M),Z)⟶0.
(1)
Both outer groups vanish: the first because Z is free and the second because H1​=0. Hence H1=0. Integral Poincare duality now gives H2​(M;Z)≅H1(M;Z)=0 and H3​(M;Z)≅H0(M;Z)=Z. Homology in degrees greater than three vanishes by the same duality with negative-degree cohomology. Consequently
Hi​(M;Z)={Z,0,​i=0,3,otherwise.​​
(2)
These are precisely the homology of a sphere for S3, proving that simply connected closed three-manifolds are homology spheres. No homeomorphism classification of three-manifolds is needed.

The universal cover of S1×S2 is
p:R×S2⟶S1×S2,p(t,z)=(e2πit,z).
(3)
Because S3 is simply connected, any continuous map f:S3→S1×S2 lifts to f~​:S3→R×S2 after choosing a lift of one base point. The lifting criterion for a covering space applies because f∗​π1​(S3)=0. Equivalently, path lifting defines the lift and simple connectedness makes its value independent of the chosen path.
The cover deformation retracts onto {0}×S2, so H3​(R×S2;Z)=0. Since f=p∘f~​, functoriality of homology gives
f∗​:H3​(S3;Z)f~​∗​​0p∗​​H3​(S1×S2;Z).
(4)
Therefore f∗​=0 and degf=0​, with the last equality using the oriented three-dimensional fundamental classes.

Put Γ=π1​(X,x) and R=Z[Γ]. Give Z its trivial left R-module structure, through the augmentation ε:R→Z, ∑ng​g↦∑ng​. The universal cover X has the lifted cell structure, with Γ acting by deck transformations.
For each cell of X, choose one lifted cell and orient every translate compatibly with its projection. The translates form one copy of the regular R-module. Thus
Ck​(X)≅⨁k-cells of X​R
(5)
is a free left R-module, and its boundary is R-linear. The augmentation C0​(X)→Z sends every vertex to one. The contractibility of X says that its reduced homology vanishes, so the augmented complex
⋯⟶C2​(X)⟶C1​(X)⟶C0​(X)⟶Z⟶0
(6)
is exact. It is therefore a free resolution of the trivial module. By the definition of the Ext functor,
ExtRi​(Z,Z)=Hi(HomR​(C∗​(X),Z)).
(7)
An R-linear cochain with values in the trivial module has the same value on every translate of a lifted cell. It is therefore exactly an ordinary integral cellular cochain on X. The correspondence does not depend on which lift was initially chosen. It also respects the coboundary: applying a constant-on-orbits cochain to the lifted cellular boundary sums precisely the incidence coefficients of the boundary in the base. Hence
HomR​(C∗​(X),Z)≅Ccell∗​(X;Z)
(8)
as cochain complexes, not just as graded groups. Cellular cohomology computes the cohomology of a cell complex, giving
Hi(X;Z)≅ExtRi​(Z,Z)for all i≥0.​
(9)
At i=0 both sides are Z, by connectedness and HomR​(Z,Z)=Z. This is the cellular free resolution from a contractible universal cover description of group cohomology.

Work over Q and put bi​=dimHi(M;Q). For a closed orientable six-dimensional manifold, Poincare duality gives bi​=b6−i​, so
χ(M)=2(b0​−b1​+b2​)−b3​.
(10)
The remaining Betti number is even. Indeed, the cup product pairing
H3(M;Q)×H3(M;Q)⟶Q,(u,v)⟼⟨u⌣v,[M]⟩
(11)
is nondegenerate by duality and skew-symmetric by graded commutativity, since (−1)3⋅3=−1. If its dimension were odd, a matrix A for it would satisfy detA=det(−AT)=−detA, forcing detA=0 in characteristic zero, a contradiction. Thus b3​ is even and
χ(M)≡0(mod2).​
(12)
This is the Euler characteristic parity in dimensions congruent to two modulo four. If the manifold has several components, apply the argument to each component and add.
The orientability assumption is essential. The Real projective space RP6 has one cell in each dimension from zero to six, giving
χ(RP6)=1−1+1−1+1−1+1=1​.
(13)
It is nonorientable: its double cover S6 has the orientation-reversing antipodal deck transformation, of degree (−1)7=−1.
For the construction, CP3 is a closed orientable real six-dimensional manifold with cells in dimensions 0,2,4,6, hence Euler characteristic four. The product S3×S3 is also closed and orientable, with Euler characteristic χ(S3)2=0. The six-sphere has Euler characteristic two.
In dimension six, removing an open ball subtracts one from the Euler characteristic. To see this, write M as the punctured manifold joined to a closed ball along S5, whose Euler characteristic is zero, and apply the cellular inclusion-exclusion formula. Gluing two punctured manifolds along S5 therefore gives
χ(M#N)=χ(M)+χ(N)−2.
(14)
A connected sum of r copies of CP3 and s copies of S3×S3 has
χ=2+2r−2s,
(15)
with the empty sum interpreted as S6. Given any even integer n, take
r=max{n/2−1,0},s=max{1−n/2,0}.
(16)
Both are nonnegative integers and the displayed Euler characteristic equals n. Hence every even integer is the Euler characteristic of a closed oriented six-manifold, including zero and negative integers; the construction is connected.

Use integral singular chains. The inclusion Y⊆X identifies Ck​(Y) with the subgroup of Ck​(X) generated by the singular simplices whose images lie in Y. It is a chain subcomplex. Define
Ck​(X,Y)=Ck​(X)/Ck​(Y),∂ˉ(c+Ck​(Y))=∂c+Ck−1​(Y).
(17)
This boundary is well defined because ∂Ck​(Y)⊆Ck−1​(Y), and its square is zero because ∂2=0. Its homology is, by definition, Hk​(X,Y). A relative cycle has a representative c with ∂c∈Ck−1​(Y); representatives of the same relative homology class differ by ∂b+y, where b∈Ck+1​(X) and y∈Ck​(Y).
Inclusion and quotient induce homomorphisms i∗​:Hk​(Y)→Hk​(X) and j∗​:Hk​(X)→Hk​(X,Y). Define the relative homology connecting homomorphism by
δ:Hk​(X,Y)⟶Hk−1​(Y),δ[c]=[∂c].
(18)
Here ∂c is a cycle in Y since ∂2c=0. If c is changed to c+∂b+y, its boundary changes by ∂y, a boundary in Y, proving independence of representatives. The formula is additive, so δ is a homomorphism.
The long exact sequence in relative homology is
⋯⟶Hk​(Y)i∗​​Hk​(X)j∗​​Hk​(X,Y)δ​Hk−1​(Y)i∗​​Hk−1​(X)⟶⋯​
(19)
and ends in
H0​(Y)i∗​​H0​(X)j∗​​H0​(X,Y)⟶0.
(20)
We prove all three types of exactness directly, so no unproved abstract lemma about chain complexes is required.
At Hk​(X), j∗​i∗​=0 because a chain in Y is zero in the relative chain group. Conversely, let an absolute cycle c have j∗​[c]=0. The definition of a relative boundary gives c=∂b+y with y∈Ck​(Y). Since c is a cycle, ∂y=0. Thus [c]=i∗​[y], proving kerj∗​=imi∗​.
At Hk​(X,Y), δj∗​=0 because an absolute cycle has zero boundary. Conversely, suppose δ[c]=0. Then ∂c=∂y for some y∈Ck​(Y). Therefore c−y is an absolute cycle and j∗​[c−y]=[c]. Hence kerδ=imj∗​.
At Hk−1​(Y), i∗​δ=0 because ∂c is a boundary in X. Conversely, if a cycle y∈Ck−1​(Y) becomes zero in Hk−1​(X), then y=∂c for some c∈Ck​(X). That c is a relative cycle, and δ[c]=[y]. Thus keri∗​=imδ.
These arguments also prove the assertions at shifted indices. Finally, every zero-dimensional relative cycle lifts to a chain in C0​(X), and every zero-chain is an absolute cycle; this proves surjectivity onto H0​(X,Y). All maps, representative changes, compositions and exactness assertions have consequently been justified from the chain definitions.

We first prove that fixed-point-free sphere maps are homotopic to the antipodal map. If f:Sd→Sd has no fixed point, define
Ht​(x)=∥(1−t)f(x)−tx∥(1−t)f(x)−tx​,0≤t≤1.
(21)
The denominator could vanish only if f(x) is a positive multiple of x. Both are unit vectors, so this would require f(x)=x and t=1/2, excluded by the hypothesis. Thus H is a continuous homotopy from f to x↦−x.
On S2r, with r≥1, the antipodal map has degree (−1)2r+1=−1, since it is the boundary map of the ambient linear transformation −I on R2r+1. Homotopy invariance therefore gives degree minus one for every fixed-point-free action map. The degree of each homeomorphism is ±1, and functoriality makes degree a group homomorphism
G⟶{1,−1}.
(22)
Every nonidentity element in a free action has degree minus one, so this homomorphism has trivial kernel. Hence
∣G∣≤2.​
(23)
For S0 the same bound follows immediately from its two points. This proves that free actions on even-dimensional spheres have order at most two, without an Euler-characteristic argument requiring a cell structure on the quotient.

Identify S2b−1 with the unit sphere in Cb, and put ζ=e2πi/a. The generator of the cyclic group Z/a acts by
(z1​,…,zb​)⟼(ζz1​,…,ζzb​).
(24)
It preserves the sphere because ∣ζ∣=1. If its kth power fixes a point, then (ζk−1)zj​=0 for every j. Some coordinate is nonzero, so ζk=1, meaning k≡0(moda). Thus the scalar root-of-unity action is free. The case a=1 is the trivial group and also satisfies freeness. The quotient is a standard lens space.

Suppose a nontrivial finite group acts freely on Rn by homeomorphisms. Choose a nonidentity element of finite order m, a prime p dividing m, and the power of that element with exponent m/p. It has order p, so a subgroup Cp​ also acts freely.
Let M=Rn/Cp​. A finite free action is properly discontinuous: around any point choose mutually disjoint neighborhoods of the finitely many orbit points, then intersect their inverse translates to obtain an evenly covered neighborhood. Thus M is an n-manifold with universal cover Rn, which is contractible. The group-cohomology argument of Question 2 applies even if no cell structure has been chosen: singular simplices in M lift to the cover, their lifts form free deck-group orbits, and the augmented singular chains of the contractible cover form an exact free resolution. Therefore
Hi(M;Z)≅ExtZ[Cp​]i​(Z,Z).
(25)
We calculate the right-hand side explicitly. Write R=Z[Cp​], let t generate Cp​, and set N=1+t+⋯+tp−1. The periodic resolution of a finite cyclic group is
⋯t−1​RN​Rt−1​Rε​Z⟶0.
(26)
Its exactness can be seen in coefficients. For r=∑j=0p−1​aj​tj, (t−1)r=0 exactly when all aj​ are equal, giving ker(t−1)=ZN=imN. Also Nr=ε(r)N, so kerN=kerε. If ε(r)=0, then
r=∑j=1p−1​aj​(tj−1)=(t−1)∑j=1p−1​aj​(1+t+⋯+tj−1).
(27)
Hence kerN=kerε=(t−1)R, proving exactness everywhere.
Apply HomR​(−,Z) with the trivial action. Multiplication by t−1 induces zero, and multiplication by N induces multiplication by p. The cochain complex is therefore
Z0​Zp​Z0​Zp​⋯.
(28)
Its positive even-degree cohomology is
H2j(M;Z)≅Z/pZ(j≥1),
(29)
while its positive odd-degree cohomology is zero.
This contradicts the dimension of the quotient manifold. The noncompact form of Poincaré duality for noncompact manifolds, with the orientation local system when needed, is
Hi(M;Z)≅Hn−iBM​(M;OM​).
(30)
Here Borel-Moore homology is the homology of locally finite chains. Its negative-degree chain groups are zero, so Hi(M;Z)=0 for i>n, whether or not M is orientable. Taking any 2j>n contradicts the displayed cyclic group calculation. Thus
a finite group acting freely on Rn must be trivial.​
(31)
This proves finite groups cannot act freely on Euclidean space for general homeomorphisms; it does not assume that the action consists of affine maps or isometries.

Let γ be the real tautological line bundle over RP2. A homogeneous polynomial of degree a defines a section of the real line bundle
La​=(γ∗)⊗a.
(32)
Indeed, on a projective line ℓ⊂R3 its restriction satisfies F(rv)=raF(v), exactly the transformation law for a linear functional on ℓ⊗a. Its zero set is well defined without treating an odd-degree polynomial as a global real-valued function on projective space.
Put u=w1​(γ)∈H1(RP2;F2​). This is the nonzero generator: the tautological line changes sign along the generating projective loop. Dualization preserves this sign, while tensoring multiplies transition signs, so
w1​(La​)=au,w1​(Lb​)=bu.
(33)
For generic coefficients both polynomial sections are transverse to zero and their zero curves meet transversely. This genericity follows from parametric transversality: at each projective point some coordinate is nonzero, and varying the coefficient of its ath power varies the section's value freely; the two polynomial coefficient sets vary the two values independently. The common zero set is consequently a zero-dimensional submanifold of a compact space, and is finite.
We justify the needed parity of zeros of a real line-bundle section. Along a closed loop transverse to the zero curve, trivialize the pulled-back line bundle on the cut interval. At a simple zero the real component function changes sign. The product of these sign changes equals the sign of the bundle's transition around the loop, which is the evaluation of its first Stiefel–Whitney class. Therefore the intersection parity of the zero curve with every loop equals the evaluation of w1​ on that loop. This says that its mod-two Poincare dual is w1​, so
PD2​[A]=au,PD2​[B]=bu.
(34)
The mod-two Poincare duality intersection rule gives
∣A∩B∣(mod2)=⟨PD2​[A]⌣PD2​[B],[RP2]2​⟩.
(35)
There are no orientation signs in these coefficients, so every transverse intersection contributes one. Two distinct projective lines meet in exactly one point; both have dual class u by the preceding degree-one calculation. Hence ⟨u2,[RP2]2​⟩=1, and
∣A∩B∣≡ab(mod2).​
(36)
This proves the parity of real projective plane-curve intersections.
Equality with ab need not hold. Choose a=1, b=2, with the line and conic
A={z=0},B={x2+y2−z2=0}.
(37)
On the line, the conic equation becomes x2+y2=0, which has no nonzero real solution. Thus ∣A∩B∣=0=2=ab​. Both real curves are smooth and nonempty, and they are disjoint, so their real intersection is transverse vacuously. Even over the complex numbers they meet in two distinct transverse points [1,i,0] and [1,−i,0]; the example is not a tangency or shared-component exception. On the unit sphere F2+g2 has a positive minimum, so disjointness also persists under sufficiently small real coefficient perturbations, so it is compatible with the generic real situation.
