A periodic pattern breaks continuous translation symmetry: translating it changes its phase without changing its shape or its energy. Consequently a uniform phase change is neutral. A slowly varying phase change alters the local spacing or orientation and can grow even while ordinary uniform amplitude perturbations decay. These are long-wavelength sideband instabilities. The relevant wavelengths are long compared with the carrier pattern, so their leading dynamics is often a phase modulation equation. Positive phase diffusion smooths spacing errors; negative phase diffusion amplifies them. Near onset, an amplitude equation computes this diffusion explicitly. Away from onset, one must calculate it from the full nonlinear periodic pattern.
For a supercritical stationary pattern in one dimension, rescale the real Ginzburg–Landau equation to
At​=μA+Axx​−∣A∣2A.
(40)
A detuned periodic pattern is represented by A=Reiqx, with R2=μ−q2>0. Perturb its magnitude and phase by A=(R+u)ei(qx+ϕ). Linearization gives
ut​=−2R2u+uxx​−2qRϕx​,ϕt​=ϕxx​+R2q​ux​.
(41)
For a Fourier mode proportional to eλt+ikx, the two eigenvalues are the sideband spectrum of a real Ginzburg-Landau plane wave:
λ±​=−R2−k2±R4+4q2k2​.
(42)
The magnitude branch remains damped at small k. The phase branch expands as
λ+​=−D∥​k2+O(k4),D∥​=μ−q2μ−3q2​.
(43)
Hence the Eckhaus-stable band is q2<μ/3​, narrower than the existence band q2<μ. This Eckhaus instability is longitudinal: it compresses and dilates the pattern along its wavevector. The coupling to the relaxing magnitude is crucial; examining phase diffusion while holding the magnitude fixed would miss the destabilizing 2q2/R2 correction. At the stability boundary the k2 coefficient vanishes, and the next term is negative, −2q4k4/R6. Beyond the boundary, a phase distortion can create amplitude zeros and phase slips, changing the number of periods. These nonlinear events are possible outcomes, not conclusions of the linear calculation.
A fixed periodic domain allows only discrete sideband wavenumbers. Indeed, the displayed spectrum is unstable only if 0<k2<6q2−2μ. A domain too short to support such a Fourier mode can suppress the nominal long-wave instability. Thus an infinite-domain stability band and a finite-domain threshold must be distinguished.
The zigzag instability concerns transverse bending of rolls in an isotropic two-dimensional system. Let kc​ be the preferred carrier wavenumber, use an x-directed carrier, and denote its slow longitudinal detuning by q. Rotational covariance near onset leads to the anisotropic envelope equation
At​=μA+ξ(∂X​−2kc​i​∂Y2​)2A−g∣A∣2A,ξ,g>0.
(44)
The derivative combination comes from expanding ∣k∣−kc​: a small transverse carrier component changes the length of the wavevector only at second order. A straight-roll envelope A=ReiqX has gR2=μ−ξq2. For a purely transverse phase perturbation A≃ReiqX(1+iϕ(Y,t)), magnitude and phase decouple at linear order, and
ϕt​=kc​ξq​ϕYY​−4kc2​ξ​ϕYYYY​.
(45)
The transverse growth rate is therefore
λ⊥​=−kc​ξq​kY2​−4kc2​ξ​kY4​.​
(46)
Rolls with q<0, whose total wavenumber is below the preferred value, are zigzag-unstable to sufficiently long transverse modulations. Bending increases their local wavevector length and can relieve this mismatch. Rolls with q>0 are transversely stable in this near-onset model; at q=0 the restoring force first appears at fourth spatial order. Combining this with the longitudinal Eckhaus instability gives the robust interior stable band 0<q<μ/(3ξ)​ for this simplest isotropic roll model. Anisotropy, a pinned orientation, additional mean fields, or other pattern symmetries can modify this conclusion.
For oscillatory patterns the coefficients of the amplitude equation are generally complex. Consider the scaled complex Ginzburg–Landau equation
At​=A+(1+ic1​)Axx​−(1+ic3​)∣A∣2A,
(47)
where c1​ measures linear dispersion and c3​ the amplitude-dependent frequency shift. The uniform oscillation is A=e−ic3​t. Put A=e−ic3​t(1+u+iv), with real infinitesimal u,v. The linearized magnitude and phase equations are
ut​=−2u+uxx​−c1​vxx​,vt​=−2c3​u+c1​uxx​+vxx​.
(48)
Their characteristic polynomial for a sideband of wavenumber k is
λ2+(2+2k2)λ+2(1+c1​c3​)k2+(1+c12​)k4=0.
(49)
The magnitude mode has leading eigenvalue −2, while the neutral phase mode has
λ=−(1+c1​c3​)k2+O(k4).
(50)
Thus the Benjamin-Feir instability occurs for 1+c1​c3​<0​; the complementary Benjamin-Feir stability condition is 1+c1​c3​>0. Both dispersive couplings matter: the phase perturbs the magnitude through c1​, and the magnitude feeds back into phase through c3​. In the unstable regime, higher spatial derivatives regularize sufficiently short disturbances. On a finite domain, an unstable mode must satisfy 0<k2<−2(1+c1​c3​)/(1+c12​). At equality of the phase-diffusion coefficient to zero, the leading phase damping is fourth order, not exponential growth at order k2.
Oscillatory plane waves with a nonzero carrier detuning also have a generalized Eckhaus boundary. For the Ginzburg-Landau plane wave A=Rei(qx−ωt), one has R2=1−q2 and ω=c1​q2+c3​R2. Eliminating the damped magnitude mode to second order in a sideband wavenumber gives
λ=−2i(c1​−c3​)qk−D(q)k2+O(k3),D(q)=1+c1​c3​−1−q22(1+c32​)q2​.
(51)
The imaginary linear term transports the modulation; the sign of D(q) determines its leading growth. If 1+c1​c3​>0, the long-wave stable carrier band is
q2<3+c1​c3​+2c32​1+c1​c3​​.
(52)
For c1​=c3​=0 this reduces to the real-equation Eckhaus band. If 1+c1​c3​<0, even the zero-detuning oscillation has the Benjamin-Feir instability. Nonlinear outcomes can include smooth phase modulation, defect production or phase turbulence, depending on the equation and parameters.
The general nonlinear-pattern problem starts from a periodic solution u0​(x) of the full field equations, not from a small-amplitude envelope. Linearize about it to obtain an operator L with periodic coefficients. Write a disturbance as eλt+iq⋅xv(x) with v periodic on the pattern cell. The Bloch stability operator
L(q)=e−iq⋅xLeiq⋅x
(53)
acts on this fixed cell and determines the entire sideband spectrum. Translation covariance gives L(0)∂x​u0​=0 in one dimension, and corresponding neutral directions for each independent broken translation in several dimensions. Oscillatory base patterns require the analogous Floquet theory calculation over their temporal period, or a rotating frame where applicable.
For a simple translational zero eigenvalue, the phase-diffusion calculation can be made explicit. Expand
L(q)=L0​+iqL1​−q2L2​+⋯,v(q)=v0​+iqv1​+O(q2),λ(q)=icq−Dq2+O(q3),
(54)
where v0​=u0′​ and L0​v0​=0. Choose an adjoint neutral function w with L0∗​w=0, ⟨w,v0​⟩=1, and impose ⟨w,v1​⟩=0. First-order solvability, using the Fredholm alternative, gives
c=⟨w,L1​v0​⟩,L0​v1​=cv0​−L1​v0​.
(55)
At second order, applying the same adjoint solvability condition gives the phase-diffusion coefficient from a Bloch cell problem:
D=⟨w,L2​v0​+L1​v1​⟩.​
(56)
This calculation exhibits why the nonlinear pattern profile matters: both v0​ and the cell correction v1​ depend on it. A negative D signals a long-wave instability provided the rest of the spectrum is stable. With several phase directions, or an extra neutral conserved mean, one obtains a coupled modulation system and diffusion matrices rather than this scalar formula. A two-dimensional phase equation can schematically have ϕt​=D∥​ϕxx​+D⊥​ϕyy​+⋯. Changing the sign of D∥​ gives the longitudinal Eckhaus instability; changing the sign of D⊥​ gives the transverse zigzag instability. For oscillatory waves, nonvariational magnitude-phase coupling gives the Benjamin-Feir instability.
These mechanisms extend beyond onset, but the near-onset numerical boundaries do not: their coefficients must be replaced by those obtained from the actual nonlinear pattern and its cell problems. Long-wave linear stability is also only one part of stability; finite-wavenumber eigenvalues, additional modes, defects, and boundary constraints can intervene. The unifying calculation is the splitting of translation-induced neutral modes at small modulation wavenumber, together with the coupling to the relaxing magnitude and any other slow fields.
