The constitutive convolution is the response of a linear viscoelastic fluid close to its relaxed state. Deformations and rotations during the material's memory time must be small, so the stress is linear in the deformation history and evaluating that history at a fixed position is consistent with linearization about rest. For an isotropic incompressible flow, the deviatoric stress is proportional to the history of the rate-of-strain tensor; an arbitrary isotropic pressure is separate. Small amplitude permits arbitrary frequency: the approximation does not require ωτ≪1 in an oscillatory experiment. Large accumulated deformation, substantial advection of memory, and nonlinear normal-stress differences are outside this formula's scope.
If a small step of strain ε0​ is imposed at time zero and then held, E(t)=ε0​δ(t) to linear order. The convolution consequently gives σ′(t)=2G(t)ε0​ for t>0. In simple shear flow, this reads σxy​(t)=G(t)γ0​ for a shear-strain step γ0​. Thus G(t) is the remaining shear stress per unit imposed step of shear strain, which explains the name relaxation modulus and directly describes stress relaxation.
Use the convention E(t)=Re[Eeiωt]. Substitution into the convolution defines the complex viscosity:
σ′=2μ​(ω)E,μ​(ω)=∫0∞​G(s)e−iωsds.​
(1)
Write μ​=μ′−iμ′′. Its real part μ′ multiplies the component in phase with the rate-of-strain tensor, causing dissipation; its imaginary part describes the out-of-phase, elastic response. More precisely the complex shear modulus is G∗=iωμ​, so the storage modulus and loss modulus are
G′=ωμ′′=−ωImμ​,G′′=ωμ′=ωReμ​.
(2)
For shear-rate amplitude γ˙​​ the mean dissipated power per unit volume is 21​μ′∣γ˙​​∣2. For the exponential Maxwell fluid memory kernel, direct integration gives
μ​=1+iωτG0​τ​,μ′=1+ω2τ2G0​τ​,μ′′=1+ω2τ2G0​ωτ2​.​
(3)
The zero-shear viscosity is μ0​=G0​τ, and the high-frequency storage modulus tends to G0​.
For the channel, let vx​(y,t)=Re[U(y)eiωt] and interpret Δp as the signed amplitude of ∂x​p, not the positive pressure drop. The Cauchy momentum equation and no-slip boundary conditions give
iωρU=−Δp+μ​U′′,U(±h)=0.
(4)
Define k2=iωρ/μ​ and z=kh, choosing either square-root branch consistently; the flux expression is even in z. The symmetric solution is
U(y)=−iωρΔp​[1−cosh(kh)cosh(ky)​].
(5)
Its integral across the channel gives the oscillatory channel flux of a linear Maxwell fluid:
Q=∫−hh​U(y)dy=−iωρ2Δph​(1−ztanhz​),z2=G0​τiωρh2(1+iωτ)​.​
(6)
This includes inertia while using the linear constitutive response. The pressure-gradient amplitude must be small enough that the resulting deformation stays in that response regime.
As ω→0, tanhz/z=1−z2/3+2z4/15+⋯. Hence
Q=−3μ​2Δph3​[1−52​z2+O(z4)]=−3μ0​2Δph3​[1+iω(τ−5μ0​2ρh2​)+O(ω2)].
(7)
In particular Q→−2Δph3/(3G0​τ), the usual plane Poiseuille flow flux with viscosity μ0​. The fluid has enough time to relax and behaves as a Newtonian fluid at leading order.
For fixed positive h,τ,ρ,G0​, let c=G0​/ρ​. At large frequency the square root with positive real part satisfies
z=2cτh​+icωh​+O(ω−1).
(8)
Its real part tends to a positive constant, so tanhz remains bounded although it oscillates. Thus tanhz/z=O(ω−1) and
Q=−iωρ2Δph​[1+O(ω−1)](ω→∞).​
(9)
The leading volume-integrated response is an inertial balance and is in quadrature with the driving force −Δp. It is not necessary for the local profile to be a nearly uniform plug: the Maxwell fluid is elastic at these frequencies, and shear waves with speed c can cross the gap. The oscillatory part of U largely cancels on integration. Small attenuation allows resonance-like enhancements at finite frequencies; the leading flux limit still holds for fixed nonzero relaxation and gap parameters. A Newtonian thin viscous-layer argument would not justify the Maxwell profile here.
Finally take the different ordering ωτ≫1 but ρω2h2/G0​≪1. Now z2=−ρω2h2/G0​+iρωh2/(G0​τ) is small, so the small-z expansion remains appropriate even at a large Deborah number. The result is
Q≃−3G0​τ2Δph3(1+iωτ)​,Q∼−3G0​2iωΔph3​.​
(10)
Here inertia is negligible throughout the narrow gap. Dividing the velocity by iω gives the displacement amplitude, whose parabolic profile is determined by an elastic shear balance with modulus G0​. The velocity therefore grows like frequency for a fixed displacement response. This is not the fixed-gap high-frequency limit just obtained: with h fixed, the condition ρω2h2/G0​≪1 eventually fails.

The usual elastic secondary flow around a rotating sphere is inward towards the equator, then poleward beside the sphere and outward near both poles. It is superposed on the primary azimuthal rotating sphere in Stokes flow. Tensile normal stresses along curved azimuthal streamlines act as hoop stresses, pulling liquid towards the rotation axis. Incompressibility supplies the axial return flow. Unlike the primary rotation, this meridional circulation does not reverse when the rotation is reversed.
The direction assumes the usual polymeric sign c=ψ1​+2ψ2​>0 and negligible inertia. A general second-order fluid has circulation proportional to c: a negative value reverses the arrows and c=0 eliminates the leading elastic circulation. Inertial secondary circulation is a different contribution, so slow rotation alone does not determine which contribution dominates. The sketch shows the elastic contribution for c>0:
For a quantitative check of the sketch, take sphere radius a, angular velocity Ω and K=Ωa3. The primary azimuthal velocity is vϕ​=Ksinθ/r2, with θ measured from the rotation axis. Its only nonzero strain components in the spherical orthonormal basis are Erϕ​=Eϕr​=−3Ksinθ/(2r3). The leading elastic secondary circulation around a rotating sphere has the stream function
Ψ=H(r)sin2θcosθ,H(r)=4μa3cK2​[1−3(a/r)2+2(a/r)3],
(11)
with vr​=H(3cos2θ−1)/r2 and vθ​=−H′sinθcosθ/r. The bracket is (1−a/r)2(1+2a/r)>0 outside the sphere. Thus vr​<0 at the equator and vr​>0 near the poles when c>0, and both components vanish on the sphere. The diagram uses these velocities, not independently chosen arrows.
Two other manifestations of the same elastic normal-stress differences are rod climbing, in which inward hoop stresses raise a free surface around a rotating rod, and die swell, in which relaxation of the axial stress and stored polymer deformation allows an extrudate to expand sideways after leaving the die. These are not explained by a scalar shear viscosity alone. A Newtonian extrudate can also swell through velocity-profile rearrangement, so the elastic contribution should not be identified with every instance of swelling.
The second-order fluid is a slow, weak-flow expansion of the objective constitutive history of an isotropic simple fluid. Material frame indifference requires objective tensor rates. For an incompressible flow, the leading isotropic stress is arbitrary pressure, the linear deviatoric term is 2μE, and at the next order the independent contributions can be represented by E2 and an objective rate of E. Isotropic scalar contributions are absorbed into the pressure. Their coefficients can be expressed through the two low-rate normal-stress differences, giving
σ=−pI+2μE+4(ψ1​+ψ2​)E2−ψ1​E△.
(12)
Here E△ is the lower-convected derivative, not a division by E. This local expansion is appropriate when deformation and temporal change over the relevant viscoelastic relaxation time are small: both the Weissenberg number and the appropriate Deborah number must be small. It captures leading normal stresses in slow flows, but not arbitrary finite-strain history, rapid transients or strongly nonlinear shear thinning. In particular its restrictions are stronger than small-amplitude linear oscillatory response at arbitrary frequency.
For steady simple shear flow v=(γ˙​y,0,0), use component-first Lij​=∂j​vi​, so the paper's ∇v is LT. The relevant tensors are
E=2γ˙​​​010​100​000​​,E2=4γ˙​2​diag(1,1,0),E△=LTE+EL=γ˙​2diag(0,1,0).
(13)
Substitution gives
σ=​−p+(ψ1​+ψ2​)γ˙​2μγ˙​0​μγ˙​−p+ψ2​γ˙​20​00−p​​.​
(14)
Thus the first and second normal-stress differences are N1​=ψ1​γ˙​2 and N2​=ψ2​γ˙​2, and the shear stress is μγ˙​.
For the velocity-preservation results, the definitions imply E△=E∘+2E2. Consequently the non-Newtonian stress can be written
S=2(ψ1​+2ψ2​)E2−ψ1​E∘,
(15)
where E∘ is the Jaumann derivative. Let vN​ solve the steady incompressible Stokes flow problem with the prescribed boundary velocities. The supplied identity says that ∇⋅E∘N​ is curl-free. If ψ1​+2ψ2​=0, then ∇⋅SN​=∇Φ for a scalar Φ on the ordinary simply connected flow domain. The momentum equation at vN​ is
−∇(pN​+Φ)+μ∇2vN​+∇Φ=0.
(16)
Hence the Newtonian velocity itself is a solution of the second-order constitutive model, with altered pressure p=pN​+Φ. For nontrivial topology one also needs the curl-free force to admit a single-valued pressure; this is implicit in the usual boundary-value problem.
On the regular weakly non-Newtonian branch, Uniqueness of Stokes flow fixes the velocity correction. At first order it solves homogeneous Stokes flow, with zero boundary velocity, after the gradient force has been absorbed into pressure. Multiplying its equation by the correction and integrating gives 2μ∫E1​:E1​dV=0, hence the correction vanishes. Higher perturbative corrections vanish in turn because the previous velocities remain vN​. The velocity is v=vN​ in this second-order-fluid solution; only the pressure and stresses change. This is Newtonian velocity preservation in a second-order fluid. It does not assert global uniqueness of distant nonlinear solution branches or exact equality for a real fluid whose higher-order constitutive terms have been omitted.
For a planar incompressible velocity (u(x,y),v(x,y),0), the rate-of-strain tensor has the form
EN​=​ab0​b−a0​000​​,EN2​=(a2+b2)diag(1,1,0).
(17)
All quantities are independent of z, so ∇⋅EN2​=∇(a2+b2). The extra quadratic term is therefore also a pressure gradient. Together with the supplied identity for the Jaumann derivative, the entire non-Newtonian force is a gradient for arbitrary ψ1​,ψ2​. The same boundary-value and weak-branch argument proves v=vN​ for every such planar second-order-fluid flow with prescribed boundary velocities, without requiring ψ1​+2ψ2​=0. Specifying stresses or a free surface instead of velocities would not give the same conclusion automatically, because the extra stresses affect those boundary conditions.

For the specified affine linear PTT fluid, perturb about the relaxed state A=0, v=0. Products of A with the velocity gradient and the term α(trA)A are second order in the perturbation. The material-advection term is also second order. The linearized equation is therefore
∂t​A+τA​=2E.
(18)
With vanishing stress in the remote past, an integrating factor gives
A(t)=2∫0∞​e−s/τE(t−s)ds.
(19)
Since σ′=G0​A, comparison with the linear viscoelastic fluid convolution yields G(s)=G0​e−s/τ and zero-shear viscosity G0​τ. The nonlinear relaxation parameter does not appear at linear order; the response is that of a Maxwell fluid.

(f−2q)a11​=2q,(f+q)a22​=(f+q)a33​=−q.
(25)
Set T=f−2q. Then
a11​=T2q​,a22​=a33​=−T+3qq​,trA=T(T+3q)6q2​.
(26)
Since f−1=αtrA and f=T+2q, multiplication gives
T(T+2q−1)(T+3q)=6αq2.​
(27)
This is the cubic for uniaxial extension of an affine linear PTT fluid. For α>0, choose its physical root T>max(0,1−2q), continuous from T=1 at rest. The product on the left grows strictly from zero to infinity in that interval, so this root is unique. The other algebraic roots need not describe an admissible steady material state.
The extensional viscosity is the axial-minus-transverse normal stress divided by the principal extension rate. Isotropic pressure cancels, leaving
ηE​=γ˙​G0​(a11​−a22​)​=G0​τ(T2​+T+3q1​).

ηE​⟶3G0​τ(q→0),​
(29)
the Trouton ratio of three relative to the zero-shear viscosity. At large q with fixed α>0, the positive cubic root remains bounded and tends to α: dividing the cubic by 6q2 gives T[1+O(q−1)]=α. Therefore
ηE​⟶α2G0​τ​(q→∞, α>0).​

The nonlinear relaxation prevents the divergent high-rate response of the affine Maxwell model. If α=0, the physically stable steady branch instead has f=1 and ends at q=1/2; no finite positive high-rate limit follows. The finite plateau consequently presumes the usual positive PTT relaxation parameter, as well as G0​,τ>0.
