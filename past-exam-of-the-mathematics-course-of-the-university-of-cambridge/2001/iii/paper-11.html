Use the random alteration method. Take arbitrarily large n divisible by 2k, and sample a binomial random graph with edge probability p=n−1+1/(2g). Let Z count cycles of lengths 3,…,g−1. For each length ℓ, its expected count is at most (np)ℓ/(2ℓ), so
EZ≤∑ℓ=3g−1​2ℓnℓ/(2g)​=o(n).
(1)
The sum is empty if g=3. By the Markov inequality, Z≤n/2 with probability tending to one.
Set s=n/(2k). The expected number of independent s-vertex sets is
(sn​)(1−p)(2s​)≤2nexp(−2ps(s−1)​)⟶0,
(2)
because the negative exponent has order n1+1/(2g), dominating the term of order n. Thus with probability tending to one there is no independent set of size s.
Choose a realization having both properties. Delete one vertex from each of its short cycles, using at most Z deletions. The induced remaining graph H has at least n/2 vertices and girth at least g. Deletion cannot create a new independent set, so α(H)≤s−1. Every colour class is independent, giving
χ(H)≥α(H)∣V(H)∣​≥s−1n/2​>k.
(3)
In particular a graph of the required girth and chromatic number exists. This proves graphs of arbitrarily high girth and chromatic number rather than invoking that existence theorem.

On an independent Bernoulli product measure, the Harris-Kleitman inequality states that increasing events E,F obey
Pr(E∩F)≥Pr(E)Pr(F).​
(12)
The same holds for two decreasing events; an increasing and a decreasing event have the reversed inequality.
Prove the stronger function version by induction on the number of coordinates. For increasing bounded functions f,g, condition on the last coordinate Z, with Pr(Z=1)=p. The law of total covariance gives
Cov(f,g)=E[Cov(f,g∣Z)]+p(1−p)(E[f∣1]−E[f∣0])(E[g∣1]−E[g∣0]).
(13)
The first term is nonnegative by induction, and the other factors are nonnegative by monotonicity. The one-coordinate case is the same formula without the first term. Event indicators prove the assertion. Two decreasing functions follow by replacing both by their negatives; replacing only one gives negative correlation of increasing and decreasing events.
Here is a precise Janson inequality and its lower-tail concentration form. Let Ii​ indicate containment of a specified set of independent Bernoulli coordinates, set X=∑i​Ii​, μ=EX, and let
Δ=∑i=j:Si​∩Sj​=∅​E(Ii​Ij​).
(14)
This Janson dependency sum is ordered and excludes the diagonal. Then
Pr(X=0)≤e−μ+Δ/2,Pr(X≤μ−a)≤exp[−2(μ+Δ)a2​](0≤a≤μ).​
(15)
A zero denominator means the trivial case X=0 almost surely.
To prove the avoidance bound, expose the avoidance events in order. For a fixed i, separate earlier indices into those whose supports are disjoint from Si​ and those overlapping it. Write C for avoidance of the first group and D for avoidance of the second. The event C is independent of Ii​, while C is decreasing and {Ii​=Ij​=1} is increasing. The correlation inequality therefore gives
Pr(Ii​=1∣C∩D)≥Pr(Ii​=1,D∣C)≥EIi​−∑j<i:j∼i​E(Ii​Ij​).
(16)
The first inequality divides a nonnegative numerator by Pr(D∣C)≤1; the second uses a union bound and negative correlation with C. Conditioning events of zero probability already make full avoidance impossible. Multiply the resulting conditional avoidance bounds to obtain the Janson sequential product lemma. Taking logarithms and using log(1−u)≤−u gives −μ+Δ/2, since each unordered dependency pair is counted once in the sequential sum.
For the Janson lower-tail bound by independent thinning, attach independent selectors of probability t to the events. Their total expectation and dependency sum are tμ,t2Δ, so avoidance of the selected events gives
E(1−t)X≤exp(−tμ+t2Δ/2).
(17)
Set t=1−e−s and apply the exponential Markov bound to e−sX. Using s−s2/2≤1−e−s≤s yields
Pr(X≤μ−a)≤exp[−sa+s2(μ+Δ)/2].
(18)
Choosing s=a/(μ+Δ) proves the concentration assertion. For avoidance alone, optimizing −tμ+t2Δ/2 also gives e−μ2/(2Δ) when Δ≥μ, and the original bound gives e−μ/2 when Δ≤μ.
To outline the sharp chromatic application, view an independent set of G(n,p) as a clique of its complement, whose edge probability is q=1−p. Fix 0<ε<1, let m=⌈n/(logn)2⌉ and r=⌊(2−ε)logb​n⌋. For a particular m-vertex set, let X count independent r-sets. Then μ=(rm​)q(2r​), and the overlap calculation gives
μ2Δ​≤∑ℓ=2r−1​(rm​)(ℓr​)(r−ℓm−r​)​b(2ℓ​)=Op,ε​(m2r4​).
(19)
Overlap of only one vertex shares no edge, hence is independent. For completeness, the summand is at most (r2/(m−r))ℓb(2ℓ​)/ℓ!. Since r≤(2−ε/2)logb​m eventually, large overlaps are bounded by m−cε​ℓ; finitely many smaller overlaps are dominated by ℓ=2. Also logμ=Ωp,ε​((logm)2), so the diagonal contribution 1/μ is negligible. The concentration bound consequently gives
Pr(X=0)≤exp[−cp,ε​m2/(logn)4].
(20)
A union bound over at most 2n vertex sets still tends to zero. Thus independent sets in every large subset of a dense random graph are available uniformly, including adaptively obtained remainders. Repeatedly remove an independent r-set and give it a new colour, then colour the fewer than m leftover vertices individually. This uses at most n/r+m colours. Combining with the first-moment lower bound and letting fixed ε be arbitrarily small gives the accurate leading asymptotic
χ(G(n,p))=2logb​nn​(1+o(1))with probability tending to one.​
(21)
The uniform subset estimate, rather than merely the existence of one large independent set, is what makes the sharp upper bound possible.

If v∗​=0, nonnegative weights give supremum zero. Linear functionals have the same minimum on the hull as on its original vectors, so this is exactly the quantified definition of the Talagrand convex distance.

Replace δ by min(δ,1) if necessary; this only weakens the hypothesis. We prove the result for finite graphs using a random proper partial graph coloring, then a deterministic completion. All constants below may depend on this fixed positive δ.
First record the extension of a partial colouring by neighbour-colour savings. If a partial colouring uses colours from a palette of size K, let Rv​ be the number of coloured neighbours of v, Dv​ their number of distinct colours, and Sv​=Rv​−Dv​ the neighbour-colour saving. There are K−Dv​ available colours and d(v)−Rv​ uncoloured neighbours. Thus
K−d(v)+Sv​≥1
(38)
at every vertex suffices for greedy completion: each subsequently coloured neighbour removes at most one available colour while reducing the uncoloured-neighbour count by one.
Call v high-degree if d(v)≥(1−δ/8)Δ. Its graph neighbourhood has at least
(2d(v)​)−(1−δ)(2Δ​)≥4δ​Δ2
(39)
nonadjacent pairs for all sufficiently large Δ. Assign independently to every vertex one of C=⌈Δ/2⌉ tentative colours. Erase the colour of every vertex having a neighbour with the same tentative colour. The remaining colouring is proper; erasure is based on the original assignments and is not an iterative cascade.
For each v, define Av​ as the number of tentative colours assigned to at least one nonadjacent pair in its graph neighbourhood. Let Bv​ count those same colours for which at least one neighbour of v loses its colour. Then Xv​=Av​−Bv​≥0 counts repeated colours with no erased occurrence among the neighbours, so Sv​≥Xv​.
For a particular nonadjacent pair u,w∈Γ(v), consider the event that both receive one colour and no vertex of (Γ(v)∪Γ(u)∪Γ(w))∖{u,w} receives that colour. This guarantees that its exactly two occurrences in Γ(v) both survive. Its probability is at least
C1​(1−1/C)3Δ≥Δe−12​.
(40)
Here C≥Δ/2, C≤Δ, and log(1−1/C)≥−2/C for C≥2. Different pairs cannot be counted by the same colour in this event, since each event specifies exactly two occurrences in the graph neighbourhood. Therefore
EXv​≥βΔ,β=4δe−12​,
(41)
for every high-degree vertex. This is the expected colour savings from sparse neighbourhoods.
We need simultaneous concentration, not just a positive expectation. A single tentative-colour change can affect Av​ or Bv​ only in the old and new colour categories, so each function changes by at most two. A witness for Av​≥s fixes two vertices for each of s colours. A witness for Bv​≥s fixes such a nonadjacent pair and an erased neighbour together with a same-coloured adjacent vertex, at most four vertices per colour. Thus both are certifiable functions with Lipschitz constant 2 and certificate parameter at most 4. Both are bounded by C≤Δ.
To see exactly what concentration follows, let an integer-valued Z change by at most L per coordinate and have certificates of size at most rb. For an input with Z≥b, fix such a certificate J. If y has Z(y)≤a<b, form a hybrid agreeing with that input on J and with y outside J. The hybrid has value at least b, so at least (b−a)/L certificate positions differ from y. Equal weights on J show that its Talagrand convex distance from {Z≤a} is at least (b−a)/(Lrb​). Talagrand's convex distance inequality therefore gives the two-threshold concentration for certifiable functions,
Pr(Z≤a)Pr(Z≥b)≤exp[−4L2rb(b−a)2​].
(42)
At an integer median m, each median-side probability is at least 1/2. Taking thresholds m−t,m or m,m+t and using 0≤Z≤Δ gives
Pr(∣Z−m∣≥t)≤4e−t2/(64Δ)
(43)
for L=2,r=4. Summing these tails gives ∣EZ−m∣=O(Δ​). For sufficiently large Δ, depending on β, it follows that
Pr(∣Z−EZ∣≥βΔ/4)≤4e−β2Δ/4096.
(44)
Apply this to Av​ and Bv​. Except with probability at most 8e−β2Δ/4096, Xv​≥EXv​−βΔ/2≥βΔ/2.
A bad event at v depends only on tentative colours at distance at most two. It is independent of the entire collection of bad events at distance greater than four. Its dependency graph of events consequently has maximum degree at most 2Δ4 for large Δ. The Lovász local lemma now makes all high-degree vertices good simultaneously: exponential decay beats this polynomial dependency bound.
The needed local-lemma step can be checked directly. If every bad-event probability is at most x(1−x)D, induction on conditioning sets gives Pr(E∣avoiding specified other events)≤x: split those events into non-neighbours of E, independent of it, and at most D neighbours, whose avoidance probability is at least (1−x)D by the induction. Multiplying the resulting conditional avoidance probabilities makes total avoidance positive. Taking x=1/(D+1) works whenever ep(D+1)≤1, since (1−1/(D+1))D≥e−1. Our bound satisfies this criterion once Δ≥Δ0​(δ).
Choose a successful partial colouring and set K=⌈(1−β/4)Δ⌉. This contains the original palette. High-degree vertices satisfy
K−d(v)+Sv​≥βΔ/4≥1.
(45)
Low-degree vertices have K−d(v)≥(δ/8−β/4)Δ≥1 after increasing Δ0​ if necessary. Greedy completion therefore gives χ(H)≤K. Finally, for Δ≥8/β,
χ(H)≤(1−γ)Δ,γ=8β​=32δe−12​>0.​
(46)
The constants are deliberately conservative; existence of a fixed positive improvement is the point. The same proof uses an external maximum degree bound, so if infinite locally finite graphs are allowed, every finite subgraph has this uniform colouring bound and compactness extends the colouring to the whole graph.
For the stronger triangle-free result, the appropriate brief route is semi-random palette refinement for triangle-free colouring. Start with K=C0​Δ/logΔ colours and repeatedly make small tentative random assignments, retain conflict-free choices, and update the uncoloured vertices' available lists. Triangle-freeness makes each graph neighbourhood independent. Track list size and average residual neighbour congestion per colour, prune unusually congested colours, and use concentration inequalities and the Lovász local lemma to preserve these invariants through the rounds. The ratio of residual congestion to list size decreases until a final completion using the Lovász local lemma or greedy coloring succeeds. Average control and pruning are important: four-cycles are allowed, so assuming tight uniform concentration for every individual colour load would be unjustified. With a sufficiently large absolute C0​, this programme gives χ(H)=O(Δ/logΔ). The one-round fixed-fraction saving proved above alone does not establish that logarithmic improvement.
To show the order is best possible, let d→∞ through integers and sample G(n,d/n) with n=d7. A Chernoff bound and union bound give maximum degree at most 2d with probability tending to one. The expected triangle count is at most d3/6=o(n), so fewer than n/2 vertices need be deleted to remove all triangles with high probability. For s=⌈4nlogd/d⌉, the expected number of independent s-sets is at most
(sen​)sexp[−nd​(2s​)]⟶0:
(47)
its logarithm is at most s[log(ed/(4logd))−2logd+o(1)], which tends to minus infinity. The altered triangle-free graph thus has at least n/2 vertices and independence number below s, giving χ≥(c+o(1))d/logd for an absolute positive c. Append a disjoint star graph whose centre has vertex degree 2d if needed to make its maximum degree exactly Δ=2d. This proves the triangle-free chromatic lower bound at bounded maximum degree, χ=Ω(Δ/logΔ), so the preceding order cannot be improved in general.
