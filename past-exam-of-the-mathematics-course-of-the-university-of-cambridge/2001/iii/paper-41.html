For a radial null geodesic in the FRW metric, define χ(r)=∫0r​(1−kr′2)−1/2dr′. Two successive wave crests emitted and received at the same comoving positions obey
χe​=c∫te​t0​​a(t)dt​=c∫te​+Δte​t0​+Δt0​​a(t)dt​.
(1)
Subtracting and keeping the first order in the wave periods gives Δt0​/a(t0​)=Δte​/a(te​). Thus the cosmological time dilation and the cosmological redshift have the same factor:
1+z=λe​λ0​​=ν0​νe​​=Δte​Δt0​​=a(te​)a(t0​)​=a(te​)1​.​

Here the emitter and observer are comoving; an additional peculiar velocity would supply a separate kinematic redshift. The derivation uses the null geodesic propagation of the crests rather than interpreting a finite cosmological distance as an ordinary Doppler effect.

The luminosity distance is defined by F=L/(4πdL2​), where L is the bolometric luminosity and F the received radiative flux. In the FRW metric a sphere through the observer around a comoving source has area 4πa02​re2​. The cosmological redshift reduces each photon's energy by (1+z)−1, and cosmological time dilation reduces their arrival rate by another such factor. Hence
F=4πa02​re2​(1+z)2L​,dL​=(1+z)a0​re​=(1+z)re​.​
(3)
The positive radial lookback integral is χe​=c∫te​t0​​dt/a(t)=∫0re​​dr/1−kr2​. The printed time limits are reversed; also the factor c is required unless units c=1 are adopted. The areal coordinate re​ and the radial comoving distance χe​ agree only when k=0.
For an Einstein-de Sitter universe, the Friedmann equation gives H(z)=H0​(1+z)3/2. Using the redshift-time relation in the radial null geodesic integral yields
re​=c∫0z​H(z′)dz′​=H0​2c​[1−(1+z)−1/2].
(4)
Thus the Einstein-de Sitter luminosity-distance relation is
dL​(z)=H0​2c​[(1+z)−1+z​].​
(5)
Its small-redshift expansion dL​=(c/H0​)[z+z2/4+O(z3)] recovers Hubble's law.

A Type Ia supernova can be standardized as a standard candle by correcting its peak brightness using the light curve shape and colour index. Its host spectrum gives the redshift; the corrected apparent magnitude and calibrated absolute magnitude give the distance modulus
μ=m−M=5log10​(10pcdL​​).
(6)
The resulting Hubble diagram measures the shape of the luminosity distance as a function of redshift. A spatially flat matter-and-vacuum model, for example, predicts
dL​(z)=(1+z)H0​c​∫0z​Ωm​(1+z′)3+ΩΛ​​dz′​.
(7)
Nonzero cosmological spatial curvature changes both the expansion rate and the conversion from radial to transverse comoving distance. Comparing the observed Hubble diagram with these predictions constrains the matter density parameter, cosmological constant and cosmological spatial curvature; a single low-redshift observation cannot separate them. The nearby sample calibrates the common intercept, while distant Type Ia supernovae supply the redshift-dependent shape. Relative faintness at a specified redshift means a greater inferred luminosity distance.
Type Ia supernova cosmology requires correction for interstellar extinction, possible evolution of the standardized luminosity, and selection bias. The absolute magnitude–Hubble constant degeneracy means that an uncalibrated sample constrains the shape rather than independently determining H0​.

Use the luminosity convention in the supplied flux relation: Pν​=Lν​/(4π), where Lν​ is the total spectral luminosity. For a general spectrum Pν​∝να, the cosmological spectral flux-density relation gives
Sν0​​=dL2​(1+z)P(1+z)ν0​​​=re2​Pν0​​(1+z)α−1​.
(8)
In a spatially flat universe, a shell subtending the solid angle dΩ contains dN=n0​re2​dre​dΩ sources, because n0​ is their comoving number density. The cosmological spectral background intensity per unit solid angle is consequently
Iν0​​(zmax​)=n0​Pν0​​∫0re​(zmax​)​(1+z)α−1dre​=H0​cn0​Pν0​​​∫0zmax​​(1+z)α−5/2dz.
(9)
This is the cosmological background intensity from comoving emissivity specialized to identical, nonevolving sources in an Einstein-de Sitter universe. For α=1 the integrand reduces to (1+z)−3/2, or directly dIν0​​=n0​Pν0​​dre​. The finite comoving particle horizon therefore gives
Iν0​​(zmax​)=H0​2cn0​Pν0​​​[1−(1+zmax​)−1/2],Iν0​​(∞)=H0​2cn0​Pν0​​​.​
(10)
For α=2, instead,
Iν0​​(zmax​)=H0​2cn0​Pν0​​​[1+zmax​​−1]⟶∞.​
(11)
The emitted frequency grows with redshift, and the rising spectral luminosity now defeats the redshift dimming. The Einstein-de Sitter spectral-background convergence criterion is α<3/2; equality produces a logarithmic divergence. This divergence describes the unbounded power-law, eternal-population model. A finite formation epoch, a high-frequency spectral break or absorption changes that model and can make its background finite.

The term 2Hδ˙ is Hubble friction in the equation for the density contrast. During expansion, H>0, it damps the peculiar motion producing density growth. It therefore slows gravitational instability relative to the otherwise identical static model; it does not necessarily stop growth.
Multiplying by a2 shows the mechanism without confusing it with microscopic dissipative friction:
dtd​(a2δ˙)=4πGρm​a2δ.
(12)
When the self-gravity term is negligible, the expansion-weighted derivative of a passive density perturbation is conserved, so δ˙∝a−2. Expansion also reduces ρm​∝a−3 and hence weakens the source on the right. A contracting background has H<0 and reverses the sign of this effect.

For constant a and constant ρm​>0, Hubble friction vanishes. Put ωJ​=4πGρm​​. The constant-coefficient ordinary differential equation is δ¨−ωJ2​δ=0, so its characteristic polynomial has the real roots of a polynomial ±ωJ​:
δ(t)=C+​eωJ​t+C−​e−ωJ​t.​
(13)
These are the pressureless special case of the Static-universe Jeans modes. A positive growing-mode coefficient gives exponential gravitational instability, with time scale (4πGρm​)−1/2. For initial data δ(t∗​)=δ∗​ and δ˙(t∗​)=v∗​, the equivalent answer is δ∗​cosh[ωJ​(t−t∗​)]+(v∗​/ωJ​)sinh[ωJ​(t−t∗​)]. This solves the stated perturbation model; a globally static dust-only FRW metric would require additional background support.

After the decay products dominate the background, radiation domination gives ρr​∝a−4. Neglecting curvature and vacuum terms, the Friedmann equation implies a˙/a=C/a2, so a∝(t−tB​)1/2 and
H=2(t−tB​)1​.​
(16)
The displayed 1/(2t) form uses a cosmic-time origin with tB​=0. After a late transition the extrapolated radiation-era origin need not be the original Big Bang; this time shift does not change the growth law.
For the remaining pressureless matter, neglect its self-gravity to leading order in ρm​/ρr​. Its density contrast obeys δ¨+(t−tB​)−1δ˙=0. Using the conserved expansion-weighted derivative of a passive density perturbation gives
δ(t)=C1​+C2​log(t∗​−tB​t−tB​​)=A+Bloga(t).​
(17)
Thus logarithmic growth of matter perturbations during radiation domination replaces the static exponential growth and the matter-era t2/3 growth. The expansion persists while its dominant radiation component supplies little sustained clustering in this pressureless test-component approximation. The residual matter can eventually become important again because ρm​/ρr​∝a; the approximation is for the radiation-dominated interval. The equation is not a pressureless growth equation for the relativistic decay products themselves, whose pressure and, when a fluid description applies, radiation acoustic oscillations must be retained.

For a statistically homogeneous matter distribution, the cosmological two-point correlation function of the mean-zero density contrast δ=(ρ−ρˉ​)/ρˉ​ is
ξ(r)=⟨δ(x)δ(x+r)⟩,r=∣r∣.​
(18)
Spatial homogeneity removes dependence on x, and isotropy reduces the separation argument to its magnitude. For discrete galaxies, the galaxy two-point correlation function equivalently specifies the excess pair probability at distinct positions:
dP12​=nˉ2[1+ξ(r)]dV1​dV2​.
(19)
Consequently the mean density of other galaxies, conditioned on a galaxy at the origin, is nˉ[1+ξ(r)]. The self-pair at zero separation is excluded from this definition; its Dirac delta contribution is the galaxy power spectrum shot noise of the unsmoothed number-density field.

Apply the isotropic cosmological correlation-power-spectrum relation and put K=kmax​, q=Kr. The band-limited linear density correlation is
ξ(r)=2π2rA​∫0K​k2sin(kr)dk.

Consider a logarithmic band of wavenumbers around k∼R−1, where R is a comoving scale. The dimensionless cosmological power spectrum of the density contrast is Δδ2​(k)=k3Pδ​(k)/(2π2). For Pδ​=Ak this scales as k4, so the rms density amplitude in such a band scales as R−2.
The cosmological Poisson equation gives −k2Φk​=4πGa2ρˉ​m​δk​, with Φ a dimensionful peculiar gravitational potential. Its spectrum and its potential fluctuations per logarithmic wavenumber are therefore
PΦ​(k)=k3(4πGa2ρˉ​m​)2A​,ΔΦ2​(k)=2π2(4πGa2ρˉ​m​)2A​,​
(28)
independent of k. Equivalently, ΦR​∼Gρˉ​m​(aR)2δR​ is independent of R because δR​∝R−2. This is the scale-local meaning of the Harrison-Zeldovich spectrum. An uncut spectrum has a divergent total potential variance; equal amplitude per logarithmic band does not imply a finite unsmoothed variance over all scales. For wavelengths beyond the Hubble radius, the statement is formulated using the corresponding conserved primordial curvature mode and its adiabatic matter-era potential, rather than treating the subhorizon Poisson approximation as universally valid.
On angular scales larger than roughly a degree, the Sachs-Wolfe effect relates the last-scattering potential to the observed Cosmic microwave background anisotropy: ΔT/T≃Φ/(3c2) for adiabatic modes in matter domination. Thus the observed large-angle amplitude constrains A. Scale-invariant potential bands produce a Sachs-Wolfe plateau, ℓ(ℓ+1)Cℓ​ approximately constant, rather than constant Cℓ​. Its amplitude and any tilt test the normalization and spectral shape. Cosmic variance, Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect and assumptions about adiabatic initial conditions limit this inference; the density spectrum's late-time transfer must also be included when comparing it with galaxy clustering.

For approximately circular motion in a spherically dominated Newtonian gravitational field, vc2​(r)=GM(<r)/r. If most gravitating mass followed the concentrated luminous galaxies, then beyond their main light distribution the enclosed mass would nearly saturate, giving a declining Keplerian galaxy rotation curve, vc​∝r−1/2.
Instead, extended galaxy rotation curves commonly remain roughly flat. The spherical mass profile for a flat rotation curve then requires
M(<r)≃Gv02​r​,ρ(r)≃4πGr2v02​​.​
(29)
The inferred mass continues growing where the luminous contribution is small, so the mass-to-light ratio increases outward. Under the assumed gravitational dynamics this is evidence for an extended dark matter halo. Measurements of neutral-gas motion can probe radii beyond the bright stellar disk. A realistic disk contribution must be computed using its flattened geometry; the spherical formula describes a halo-dominated region and does not turn every disk rotation curve into a spherical mass profile.

The three probes constrain the same deep gravitational potential by different observations. A large member-galaxy velocity dispersion requires a large binding mass. Applying the virial theorem, with a gravitational scale radius R and approximately isotropic orbits, gives a cluster velocity-dispersion mass estimate of order M∼Rσv2​/G, with a profile-dependent numerical coefficient. The inferred mass-to-light ratio is much larger than the stellar value. Membership, orbital anisotropy and equilibrium assumptions affect the precision.
The hot intracluster medium radiates through thermal bremsstrahlung and line emission. Its X-ray spectrum measures temperature, while its surface brightness constrains gas density. Confining this gas requires a potential with characteristic GM/R∼kB​T/(μmp​). Combining resolved profiles through the cluster hydrostatic mass estimator gives the total mass, not just the gas mass. The detected gas contributes substantial baryonic mass but does not normally account for the full gravitating mass. Nonthermal pressure or a merging cluster can invalidate a purely thermal hydrostatic equilibrium estimate.
Finally, gravitational lensing measures mass through light deflection. Strong gravitational lensing supplies multiple images and arcs; weak gravitational lensing measures coherent background-image distortion over larger radii. The lensing convergence is projected surface mass density divided by the critical surface density, irrespective of whether the material emits light. Cluster gravitational-lensing mass estimates agree broadly with the high masses implied by galaxy motions and hot gas, while having different equilibrium assumptions. Together these observations support a dominant nonluminous component in galaxy clusters, after accounting for their stars and observed gas.

Use conservation of energy for an isolated spherical perturbation of fixed mass M, with negligible initial random motion and no significant surface-pressure term. For a homologous density profile its Newtonian gravitational potential energy is U=−αGM2/R, with the same positive structure coefficient α at both stages; a uniform sphere has α=3/5.
At spherical-collapse turnaround, coherent radial motion momentarily vanishes, so E=Umax​=−αGM2/Rmax​. In the final virialized state, the virial theorem gives 2Tvir​+Uvir​=0, hence E=Tvir​+Uvir​=Uvir​/2. Therefore
−Rmax​αGM2​=−2Rvir​αGM2​,Rvir​=21​Rmax​.​
(30)
This is the virial radius from turnaround energy in the spherical-collapse model. It is an idealized virial-radius estimate rather than the radius of the formal pressureless point collapse. If the profile coefficient changes, the same argument instead gives Rvir​/Rmax​=αvir​/(2αmax​). Significant energy loss, mass loss or boundary work also changes the result.

Let ρg​(r) be the gas density and M(<r) the total gravitating mass. For a spherically symmetric gas in hydrostatic equilibrium, radial force balance is
drdP​=−ρg​r2GM(<r)​.
(31)
Using the ideal gas law P=ρg​kB​T/(μmp​), with constant mean molecular weight μ, differentiate the product rather than setting T constant:
drdP​=μmp​rkB​Tρg​​(dlogrdlogρg​​+dlogrdlogT​).
(32)
Substitution and cancellation of ρg​ give the cluster hydrostatic mass estimator
M(<r)=−μmp​GkB​T(r)r​[dlogrdlogρg​​+dlogrdlogT​].​
(33)
Here kB​ is the Boltzmann constant, denoted K in the question. The prefactor has units of mass; a pressure decreasing outward makes the bracket negative and the inferred mass positive. For an isothermal gas with ρg​∝r−β, this reduces to M(<r)=βkB​Tr/(μmp​G). The density in the logarithmic derivative is the gas density, not the total density. A varying μ or nonthermal pressure would require the corresponding extra terms.

Measure the cluster's baryonic mass, dominated by its intracluster medium with a smaller stellar contribution, and divide it by an independently inferred total mass. If this cluster baryon fraction fb​ is representative of the cosmic baryon fraction, then fb​=Ωb​/Ωm​. Thus the cluster baryon-fraction estimate of matter density is
Ωm​=fb​Ωb​​.​
(34)
Big Bang nucleosynthesis constrains the baryon-to-photon ratio and hence Ωb​h2 through primordial light-element abundances. A specified h converts this to Ωb​; the cluster mass and gas-mass estimates also have distance-dependent h factors, which must be used consistently. This method estimates the total nonrelativistic matter density, including baryonic and dark matter. It does not add radiation or vacuum energy to that inferred Ωm​; in a matter-dominated interpretation the question's Ω is this matter parameter.
Primordial deuterium as a baryon-density indicator works because increasing baryon density makes the destruction of deuterium into heavier nuclei more efficient during Big Bang nucleosynthesis. Smaller inferred primordial D/H therefore implies larger Ωb​, with the other nucleosynthesis assumptions fixed. For a fixed measured cluster baryon fraction, a smaller primordial deuterium abundance raises the inferred matter density. A low abundance caused instead by stellar processing is not a lower primordial abundance; depletion, gas ejection and the representativeness of clusters must also be assessed before identifying the cluster fraction with the cosmic one.
