Write a=ΛT. The deformation gradient of one interval of shear flow is S(a)=(10​a1​). An initial unit material line at angle θ therefore becomes (cosθ+asinθ,sinθ). Its orientation, including the correct quadrant, is
θT​=atan2(sinθ,cosθ+asinθ),ℓ(θ)=1+asin2θ+a2sin2θ​.​
(11)
For an unoriented line the angle is taken modulo π. If Λ=0, there is no shear flow: every λN​=1 and μ=0. The fluctuation and maximizing calculations below concern nonzero shear.
To quantify the renovating shear flow, use the intended isotropic model: each shear orientation is independent and uniform modulo π. Independence by itself does not specify a probability law and cannot fix the answer. Under the uniform law, conditional on every earlier deformation, the relative angle to the next shear is again uniform. Thus the logarithmic increments ξn​=logℓ(θn​) are independent and identically distributed, even though the absolute orientation of the stretched material line need not be uniform. Since logλN​=∑n=1N​ξn​, only the one-step angular mean is needed.
Write ℓ2=A+Bcos(2θ−δ), where A=1+a2/2, B=∣a∣1+a2/4​ and A2−B2=1. The supplied angular logarithmic integral, with parameter B/A, gives
r(a):=⟨ξ⟩=21​log2A+A2−B2​​=21​log(1+a2/4).
(12)
Therefore the mean logarithmic stretching rate is
μ=2Tlog(1+Λ2T2/4)​=∣Λ∣2∣a∣log(1+a2/4)​.​
(13)
This is the mean logarithmic stretching in an isotropic renovating shear, and the strong law of large numbers gives the same almost-sure Lyapunov exponent. Taking Λ>0 without loss for this statistic puts it in the requested form ΛF(ΛT).
For b=∣Λ∣T, F(b)=log(1+b2/4)/(2b). At small b, μ=Λ2T/8+O(Λ4T3); at large b, μ∼log(b/2)/T, tending to zero when T increases at fixed shear rate. The maximizing nonzero b solves
logz=2(1−z−1),z=1+b2/4.
(14)
The difference 2(1−z−1)−logz rises from zero up to z=2 and then strictly decreases to −∞, so there is exactly one root beyond 2. Numerically,
∣Λ∣T≃3.9605826,μmax​≃0.2012∣Λ∣.​
(15)
Very rapid renovation gives little deformation per interval and cancels the first-order mean stretch. Long intervals provide strong deformation but progressively align a material line with the shear direction, so one interval stretches it only algebraically in T. Intermediate persistence combines substantial deformation with frequent reorientation, producing the finite maximum.
For the distribution, let γ=arsinh(∣a∣/2), so the singular stretches of S(a) are e±γ. In the right-singular-vector basis, ℓ2=e2γcos2φ+e−2γsin2φ. Put q=tanhγ=∣a∣/a2+4​. Factoring this expression and expanding its logarithm gives
ξ=r(a)+∑j=1∞​j(−1)j+1qj​cos(2jφ),v(a):=Varξ=21​∑j=1∞​j2q2j​.
(16)
The variance follows from angular orthogonality. These bounded independent increments imply
Nv(a)​logλN​−Nr(a)​ ⟹ N(0,1).​
(17)
Accordingly the central part of the large-N stretch distribution is approximately described by a log-normal distribution, with density [λ2πNv​]−1exp[−(logλ−Nr)2/(2Nv)]. This central limit theorem is not an assertion about arbitrarily extreme tails: the exact stretch has support inside e−Nγ≤λN​≤eNγ.
For ∣a∣≪1, the log-stretch fluctuations in an isotropic renovating shear have
r(a)=8a2​−64a4​+O(a6),v(a)=8a2​−1283a4​+O(a6).
(18)
To leading order the mean and variance of logλN​ both equal Na2/8. Typical stretch is exp(Na2/8), while its mean grows as exp(3Na2/16). These moment statements can be justified directly rather than extrapolating the central Gaussian approximation: expansion of ℓp gives
⟨ℓp⟩=1+16p(p+2)​a2+O(a4),⟨λNp​⟩=exp[16Np(p+2)a2​+O(Na4)]​
(19)
for fixed p. In particular ⟨λN2​⟩=(1+a2/2)N exactly. The leading small-a log-normal approximation is especially precise in the many-small-renovations scaling with Na2 fixed and Na4→0.
If the orientation law is not isotropic these formulas need not hold. For example, repeating a fixed shear direction gives S(a)N=S(Na), only algebraic stretch, and zero asymptotic logarithmic rate. The associated degenerate direction variables are independent, illustrating why the stated independence condition alone is insufficient to select the isotropic result.
