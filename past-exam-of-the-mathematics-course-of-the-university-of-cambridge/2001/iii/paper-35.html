Write τ=t−t0​, let R be the scale factor, H=R˙/R the Hubble parameter, and μ=Λ/3​ the asymptotic expansion rate for the positive cosmological constant. The continuity equation for a homogeneous Hubble flow gives ρR3=ρ0​. Isotropy and the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity give a relative acceleration proportional to displacement from any chosen origin:
g=−34πGρ−Λ​r.
(1)
This uses the local relative gravitational acceleration, rather than trying to sum an absolutely convergent potential over an infinite universe. A fluid element with constant comoving coordinate x has r=Rx. The spatially uniform pressure has no gradient, so the Euler equations for an inviscid fluid give
R¨=−3R24πGρ0​​+3Λ​R.​
(2)
Multiply by 2R˙ and integrate. With A=8πGρ0​/3, the resulting first integral is
R˙2=A/R+μ2R2−κ.​
(3)
For an expanding branch that reaches arbitrarily large R, R˙/R→μ; more precisely, H=μ+O(R−2) and the correction to lnR−μt is integrable. Therefore R∼Ceμt. Positive Λ alone does not make every initially expanding branch escape: sufficiently large positive κ can give a turning point or a limiting static radius. Under the stated vacuum-dominance inequalities at R=1, however, A/R and ∣κ∣ remain small compared with μ2R2 for the future expanding branch, and R≃eμτ is the required approximation.
To derive the linear cosmological density perturbation equation, write δ=ρ′/ρ and v=Hr+u, where u is the peculiar velocity. At fixed x=r/R, the linearized continuity equation, Euler equations for an inviscid fluid and perturbation Poisson equation are
δ˙+R−1∇x​⋅u=0,u˙+Hu=−R−1∇x​(cs2​δ+ϕ),∇x2​ϕ=4πGR2ρδ.
(4)

ε¨+2Hε˙+(R2cs2​k2​−4πGρ)ε=0.
(5)
The adiabatic sound speed for the four-thirds polytropic equation of state is cs2​=dp/dρ∝ρ1/3=ρ01/3​/R, hence cs2​=c02​/R. Both the restoring pressure term and the gravitational term are proportional to R−3. Thus, in the exponential approximation,
ε¨+2με˙+η2e−3μτε=0,η2=c02​k2−4πGρ0​.​
(6)
The sign of η2 is the Jeans instability distinction, but exponential expansion makes the total subsequent amplification finite.
Remove Hubble friction by setting ε=e−μτy. The standard-form equation is
y′′+Q(τ)y=0,Q=η2e−3μτ−μ2.

Where Q>0 and ∣Q′∣≪Q3/2, the WKB approximation gives
ε≃e−μτQ−1/4[Acos(∫τQ​ds)+Bsin(∫τQ​ds)].

ε≃e−μτq−1/2[Ae∫τqds+Be−∫τqds],∣q′∣≪q2.

For η2=−s2<0, q=(μ2+s2e−3μτ)1/2 never vanishes. The exponential integral can be evaluated explicitly:
∫0τ​q(t)dt=3μ2​[Ψ(q(0))−Ψ(q(τ))],Ψ(q)=q+2μ​lnq+μq−μ​.
(10)
Differentiate this expression, using q′=−3μ(q2−μ2)/(2q), to verify it. Early on, if coefficients change little over the interval considered, the two local exponents for ε are −μ±μ2+s2​: one increases and one decreases. At late times q→μ and the displayed integral is μτ+O(1). The nominally growing WKB approximation branch of y therefore becomes a constant branch of ε, while the other branch decays like e−2μτ. The WKB approximation is not automatically accurate at all intermediate times; its stated derivative criterion must be checked.
For η2>0, the early frozen-coefficient exponents are −μ±μ2−η2​. There are damped oscillations if η2>μ2, a repeated-root solution (A+Bτ)e−μτ at equality, and two real decaying local branches if 0<η2<μ2. In the oscillatory case Q vanishes at τt​=ln(η2/μ2)/(3μ): the ordinary WKB approximation breaks down there, and a turning-point connection is needed. After that transition the solutions again approach a constant or decay. A positive pressure-minus-gravity restoring term does not imply indefinitely continuing oscillation; its physical frequency is redshifted away.
The limiting conclusion does not depend on the accuracy of WKB approximation. With x=2∣η∣e−3μτ/2/(3μ), the equation for y is
x2yxx​+xyx​+(sgn(η2)x2−94​)y=0.
(11)
For η2>0 its basis is J2/3​(x),Y2/3​(x), the Bessel functions of the first kind and Bessel functions of the second kind. For η2<0 use I2/3​(x),K2/3​(x), the Modified Bessel functions of the first kind and modified Bessel functions of the second kind. Their small-x branches are proportional to x2/3 and x−2/3. Multiplication by e−μτ gives the decaying and constant branches respectively. At η2=0 the exact solution is A+Be−2μτ. Thus Bessel density modes during exponential expansion prove
ε(τ)⟶ε∞​for every finite initial pair and every real η2, Λ>0.​
(12)
The same conclusion holds in the full four-thirds perturbation equation on an exponentially escaping background: (R2ε˙)′=−η2R−1ε. Its integrated equation has an absolutely integrable Volterra kernel, bounded by a constant times e−3μτ. The Gronwall inequality first bounds ε; then R−1ε is integrable and ε˙=O(R−2) is integrable. A finite limiting amplitude follows without setting H exactly constant. This argument requires positive Λ and the escaping branch; it is not a claim about Λ=0 or recollapse.
The requested strict increase of that limit needs an initial-velocity qualification. For η2=−s2<0,
(e2μτε˙)′=s2e−μτε.
(13)
If ε(0)>0 and ε˙(0)≥0, this identity prevents a first loss of positivity and makes the amplitude strictly increase for τ>0. Integrating it twice gives
ε∞​=ε(0)+2με˙(0)​+2μs2​∫0∞​e−3μτε(τ)dτ>ε(0).
(14)
This is monotone Jeans growth with positive cosmological constant. Without the extra initial condition the printed assertion is false: ε=e−μτI2/3​(2se−3μτ/2/(3μ)) is positive at τ=0 and tends to zero. An arbitrarily small multiple remains an infinitesimal linear cosmological density perturbation, so linearity does not remove the counterexample.

The energy-conserving self-similar blast wave requires a stage in which the original stellar radius, ejecta mass, ambient pressure and cooling have ceased to supply important scales. In particular, the swept-up mass must greatly exceed the ejecta mass; R≫R∗​ alone need not ensure this in a very dilute interstellar medium. For fixed explosion energy E, uniform ambient mass density ρ0​ and elapsed time t, dimensional analysis gives R=CEaρ0b​td. Matching mass, length and time gives a=1/5, b=−1/5, d=2/5. Equivalently, a self-similar strong shock wave has E∼ρ0​R3R˙2, whose constant-energy solution has the same exponents:
R=ξ0​(E/ρ0​)1/5t2/5.​
(15)
The dimensionless coefficient depends on γ and the post-shock profiles; dimensional reasoning cannot fix it.
For completeness, obtain the Strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions in the shock frame. Let v1​=V−u denote downstream speed relative to the outward-moving front. With negligible upstream pressure and enthalpy, conservation of mass, momentum and energy gives
ρ0​V=ρ1​v1​,ρ0​V2=p1​+ρ1​v12​,2V2​=2v12​​+(γ−1)ρ1​γp1​​.
(16)
Put q=ρ1​/ρ0​. Substituting v1​=V/q into the last two equations and selecting the compressive, nontrivial branch yields q=(γ+1)/(γ−1). Hence
ρ1​=γ−1γ+1​ρ0​,p1​=γ+12​ρ0​V2,u=γ+12​V.​
(17)
Here the symbol u, written as u=2V/(γ+1) in what follows, is the laboratory-frame radial velocity; it is not the relative speed v1​.
Use the thin-shell approximation for a spherical blast wave. Set W=4πR3/3 and Ms​=ρ0​W. Equating the swept-up mass to 4πR2dρ1​ gives d/R=(γ−1)/[3(γ+1)]. The growing shell's momentum is Ms​u. Since newly swept gas has zero laboratory momentum, the net force is the cavity pressure acting on the inner shell, to thin-shell accuracy:
dtd​(Ms​u)=4πR2pc​,pc​=αp1​.
(18)
The pressure at the shock is already accounted for by the jump conditions and mass loading; subtracting p1​ as an additional external pressure would count the shock reaction twice. With u=2V/(γ+1), the last equation becomes
RV˙+3V2=3αV2,dlnRdlnV​=3(α−1).
(19)
Therefore, for constant α,
R˙=KR3(α−1).​
(20)
The shell volume is Ms​/ρ1​=W/q. Its internal energy and kinetic energy are
Es,int​=q(γ−1)p1​W​=(γ+1)22ρ0​WV2​,Es,kin​=21​Ms​u2=(γ+1)22ρ0​WV2​.
(21)
Treat the cavity volume as W at the same thin-shell level, so its internal energy is Ec,int​=αp1​W/(γ−1). Their sum is proportional to R3V2 for constant α and γ. Constant total energy thus requires 3+6(α−1)=0, giving α=1/2. Consequently
E=ρ0​WV2(γ−1)(γ+1)25γ−3​.
(22)
Using V=2R/(5t), this becomes
ξ05​=16π75​5γ−3(γ−1)(γ+1)2​.​
(23)
For γ=5/3, d/R=1/12 and the approximate energy fractions are Es,kin​/E=1/4, Es,int​/E=1/4, Ec,int​/E=1/2.
This is an approximate normalization, not an exact treatment of finite shell thickness. If instead one subtracts the shell volume and uses Wc​=W(1−1/q) for the cavity, while retaining the other shell assumptions, the energy coefficient becomes 2(2γ−1)/[(γ−1)(γ+1)2] and ξ05​=75(γ−1)(γ+1)2/[32π(2γ−1)]. The difference is an order-d/R geometric correction of this crude shell model; a consistent exact coefficient requires the full self-similar profiles.
In the idealized mass budget all swept gas is assigned to the shell, so the cavity inertia is neglected. The hot cavity's residual mass density is not specified, so its kinetic energy cannot be assigned a unique numerical fraction from the stated data. If its low mass is Mc​ and its velocity is of order u, then Ec,kin​∼Mc​u2/2 and
EEc,kin​​∼Ms​Mc​​5γ−32(γ−1)​≪1when Mc​≪Ms​.
(24)
For an illustrative uniform-density cavity with homologous velocity v(r)=ur/R, direct radial integration gives Ec,kin​=3Mc​u2/10, so the preceding fraction has an extra factor 3/5. The cavity can carry substantial internal energy despite negligible kinetic energy because it is hot. Neglect of its inertia is justified in the swept-mass-dominated stage, not merely by its small initial radius.
The calculation describes the adiabatic expansion stage of a supernova remnant, giving an estimate of its age or explosion energy from its radius, expansion speed and ambient mass density. For example t≃2R/(5V), and E∝ρ0​R3V2. Such blasts inject energy and momentum into the interstellar medium and redistribute stellar ejecta. The early ejecta-dominated stage, radiatively cooled late shell, nonuniform ambient medium, finite ambient sound speed, magnetic fields, nonspherical geometry and energy transfer to cosmic rays violate different assumptions. Even in the adiabatic spherical stage, the real profiles are not a uniform thin shell and a uniform-pressure cavity, so ξ0​ here is an estimate.

A spherical harmonic of angular degree ℓ has horizontal wavenumber kh​=L/r, with L=ℓ(ℓ+1)​. The local acoustic dispersion relation therefore gives the radial wavenumber
kr2​=ω2/c2−L2/r2.
(25)
In the high-order regime a travelling stellar acoustic mode accumulates phase ∫kr​dr across its radial cavity. Reflection at the inner turning point and at the near-surface boundary fixes additional phases. A round trip must return the same complex amplitude, so WKB quantization gives
∫rt​ro​​ω2/c2−L2/r2​dr=(n+α)π.​
(26)
Here n counts radial oscillations, up to the choice of origin for radial order, and α incorporates both reflection phases. The outer acoustic reflection need not coincide exactly with the geometric stellar radius; replacing it by R and absorbing its correction in α is the leading asymptotic approximation. The model neglects buoyancy, perturbations of self-gravity and rapid background variations on a wavelength.
Resolved observations of the Sun measure line-of-sight surface velocity through the Doppler effect, or brightness fluctuations. Projection on spherical harmonics identifies ℓ and azimuthal order m; a temporal Fourier transform identifies the mode angular frequencies ω. The sequences of peaks of successive radial order form ridges in the frequency-degree diagram. Their ordering and comparison with a reference stellar oscillation model identify n; the surface image alone does not count inaccessible radial nodes. Fit the residual phase of the radial quantization relation across the observed mode set to estimate α. A simultaneous fit with the sound-speed profile, or a calibrated reference model, is needed: α is not determined independently by one mode, and an integer shift of the numbering of n shifts α oppositely. In a more accurate fit the surface phase depends on frequency. These steps constitute helioseismology.
Set a(r)=c(r)/r and w=ω/L. Assume a decreases outwards in the region to be inverted. With the surface approximation above, the Duvall law is
F(w)=ω(n+α)π​=∫rt​(w)R​1−a(r)2/w2​c(r)dr​.
(27)
Thus a suitably scaled mode set samples one function of w. Smooth the measured values before differentiating; inversion amplifies observational noise. At a finite outer reference point rs​, the already known contribution exterior to rs​ must be subtracted. For the remaining cavity, let ws​=a(rs​) and h(b)=−dlnr/db. Changing integration variable from radius to b=a(r) gives
F(w)=∫ws​w​bh(b)​1−b2/w2​db,F′(w)=∫ws​w​w2w2−b2​h(b)b​db.
(28)
The boundary term vanishes because the square root vanishes at b=w. Now multiply the derivative by w/a2−w2​ and interchange the integrals. The kernel identity
∫ba​ww2−b2​a2−w2​dw​=2abπ​
(29)
follows by setting w2=b2+(a2−b2)sin2θ and then integrating 1/[b2cos2θ+a2sin2θ]. It yields the Abel inversion of stellar acoustic travel times:
lnr(a)rs​​=π2a​∫ws​a​a2−w2​wF′(w)​dw=π2a2​∫ws​/a1​1−x2​xF′(ax)​dx.​
(30)
Knowing the monotone relation r(a) then gives c=ar. Observed turning points delimit the region recoverable without extrapolation; an unknown surface contribution or nonmonotone a prevents this simple unique inversion.
There are genuine inconsistencies in the printed numerical example. Since w and w0​ have dimensions of angular frequency, F must have dimensions of time, but the displayed empirical right-hand side is dimensionless. Moreover its derivative supplies a factor 1/w0​, whereas the requested inversion supplies 1/w02​. The dimensionally repaired relation that produces the requested inversion integral is
Fc​(w)=w02​w​(1−2πw02​3w2​),Fc′​(w)=w02​1​(1−2πw02​9w2​).
(31)
Substitution in the boxed Abel transform inversion gives precisely the exponent coefficient 2a2/(πw02​), with the square-root denominator inside the integral. This repairs the first normalization but does not repair the later algebra.
Indeed, put xs​=ws​/a and b=1−xs2​​. The required elementary integrals evaluate to
∫xs​1​1−x2​xdx​=b,∫xs​1​1−x2​x3dx​=b−3b3​.
(32)
The finite-reference-point answer following from Fc​ is therefore
lnrrs​​=πw02​2a2​[b−2πw02​9a2​(b−3b3​)].​
(33)
It still depends on ws​. The later printed profile suppresses that dependence, which requires the additional surface limit ws​→0, rs​→R. Even in that limit it is not the result of this integral. To see this exactly, write L=ln(R/r) and y=a2/(πw02​). The integral gives
L=2y−6y2,c(r)=rw0​6π​(1−1−6ln(R/r)​)​.​
(34)
The minus branch is selected by a→0 at the surface. Its decreasing-radius branch extends only to ln(R/r)=1/6, where a2=πw02​/6; it cannot describe r=R/e. The stated lower bound on the largest sampled w does not fix this algebraic obstruction. In contrast, squaring and rearranging the printed closed profile would require L=18y−81y2. This differs identically from 2y−6y2, so the two printed steps cannot both follow from the same data.
The surface polytrope test can nevertheless be performed exactly, and clarifies the final discrepancy. For a plane-parallel polytropic atmosphere of index m=3, take g=GM/R2 constant and z=R−r. The stellar hydrostatic equation and p=Kρ4/3 give 4p/ρ=gz, hence the surface sound speed of a polytropic star is
c2≃4R2γGM​z,c0​=4R2γGM​​.​
(35)
The exponent in the equilibrium polytropic equation of state need not equal the perturbation adiabatic exponent γ. For the corrected inversion above, expansion at z/R≪1 gives c2≃πw02​Rz/2, so matching the surface polytrope fixes w02​=γGM/(2πR3).
Alternatively, if the later printed closed profile is adopted as a separate imposed model, its surface expansion gives c2≃πw02​Rz/18. It also has the correct square-root depth dependence, but requires
w02​=2πR39γGM​for the printed closed profile.​
(36)
At r=R/e that imposed profile then gives
c(R/e)=e1​2RγGM​​.​
(37)
The claimed coefficient π/3 is inconsistent with this substitution; 1/e=π/3. Thus the surface scaling is demonstrable, but the empirical normalization, the requested closed inversion and the final numerical coefficient cannot all be asserted as printed. Stellar rotation is a problem of coupled force balance, heat transport and angular momentum transport. A star need not rotate as a rigid body: in the Sun, the convective envelope rotates faster near the equator than near the poles, while much of the radiative interior rotates more nearly uniformly. The tachocline is the thin transition near the base of the convection zone. Surface tracking and Doppler effect measurements constrain the exterior flow; solar rotational mode splitting in helioseismology constrains interior stellar rotation through integrals of the form δωnℓm​≃m∫Knℓm​ΩdV. Multiple kernels are needed, so surface rotation is not a measurement of every depth.
A useful mean velocity decomposition is U=ϖΩeϕ​+vm​, where ϖ=rsinθ is distance from the axis and vm​ is stellar meridional circulation. In a frame of constant angular velocity Ω0​, use the relative velocity V=U−Ω0​×r. Its momentum equation includes
DtDV​+2Ω0​×V=−ρ∇p​−∇(Φ−21​Ω02​ϖ2)+μ0​ρ(∇×B)×B​+ρ1​∇⋅τ.
(38)
The Coriolis force changes the direction of a parcel's motion but does no work. Thermal driving, release of differential rotational kinetic energy, contraction, or external torques supply the energy of the flows. Solid-body stellar rotation in a suitable hydrostatic equilibrium does not by itself require a meridional flow.
For slowly circulating, approximately axisymmetric material, the leading mechanical balance in an inertial frame is ∇p/ρ=−∇Φ+ϖΩ2eϖ​. Taking its curl gives the stellar thermal-wind balance
ϖ∂z​Ω2=ρ2(∇p×∇ρ)ϕ​​≃rcp​g​∂θ​S.
(39)
Here z is the axial coordinate, S is specific entropy and cp​ is the specific heat at fixed pressure. The last form uses approximately radial hydrostatic equilibrium and uniform composition. If the material is barotropic, the right-hand side vanishes and angular velocity is constant on cylinders. Small latitudinal entropy differences can instead support the observed noncylindrical differential rotation. Stresses and inertial terms modify this leading balance. The equation relates shear to baroclinicity; it does not give the circulation speed or explain how the shear is maintained.
That second question requires an angular-momentum budget. With j=ϖ2Ω and density-weighted mean quantities, conservation of angular momentum can be written schematically as
∂t​(ρj)+∇⋅(ρvm​j+FJ​)=0,
(40)
FJ​=ρϖ⟨uϕ′​um′​⟩−μ0​ϖ​⟨Bϕ​Bm​⟩−ρνϖ2∇Ω.
(41)
The three contributions are Reynolds stress, magnetic Maxwell stress tensor and viscosity transport. Appropriate density weighting replaces the simple correlations in a strongly compressible average. Since ∇⋅(ρvm​)=0 in a steady star, the stationary equation gives gyroscopic pumping in a star:
ρvm​⋅∇j=−∇⋅FJ​.​
(42)
A torque that maintains differential rotation therefore drives a meridional response unless another stress cancels it. Conversely the circulation transports angular momentum and feeds back on the rotation. An assumed meridional pattern without its driving torque is not a closed dynamical model.
In the convection zone, outward heat transport drives convection. Rotation deflects the rising and falling parcels and makes their correlations anisotropic. Nondiffusive convective angular momentum transport can drive shear even from an initially uniform rotation rate; treating every Reynolds stress as a positive eddy viscosity would only smooth shear and miss its source. A limiting parcel that conserves j when it moves outwards slows relative to uniform rotation, whereas rapid rotational constraint and correlated horizontal motions change the sign and direction of the net transport. Thus the solar equator-fast pattern requires the actual convective correlations, not a universal parcel argument. The Rossby number uc​/(2Ωℓc​) compares convective inertia with rotational deflection: deep, slower, larger-scale convection is more rotationally constrained than the fastest photospheric motions. Turnover times ℓc​/uc​ range from minutes near the visible surface to weeks or longer at greater depth. On these times the Reynolds stresses can redistribute angular momentum and drive circulation, while the star's total spin changes much more slowly.
In a radiative region, rotation distorts equipotential surfaces. Under uniform rotation and uniform composition, leading hydrostatic equilibrium makes pressure, density and temperature constant on each such surface, but effective gravity varies along it. Radiative diffusion then produces a flux F=f(Φeff​)∇Φeff​. Its divergence contains f′∣∇Φeff​∣2, which varies over a surface and generally cannot match a nuclear source constant there. This is the radiative-equilibrium obstruction in a rotating barotropic star. Thermal imbalance drives Eddington-Sweet circulation, with heat advection entering
ρT(∂t​S+vm​⋅∇S)=ρϵnuc​−∇⋅F+dissipative heating.
(43)
Its global thermal estimate is
ϵΩ​∼GMΩ2R3​,tES​∼ϵΩ​tKH​​,tKH​∼RLGM2​.​
(44)
A rotational thermal imbalance of order ϵΩ​L processes the thermal reservoir on this longer time. For the slowly rotating Sun, tKH​ is of order 3×107 years and ϵΩ​ of order 2×10−5, giving tES​∼1012 years, much longer than the solar age. Stable composition gradients impede the flow further. Thus classical radiative Eddington-Sweet circulation cannot by itself establish the Sun's interior rotation rapidly. In a rapidly rotating star the same estimate is shorter and rotational mixing can compete with its evolutionary lifetime. Torque-driven convection-zone circulation and radiative thermal circulation have different controlling balances.
Magnetic fields introduce a further coupling. A poloidal magnetic field linking different layers is wound by differential rotation into a toroidal magnetic field, roughly ∂t​Bϕ​≃ϖBp​⋅∇Ω. The resulting Maxwell stress tensor transport angular momentum. Communication along a field occurs on an Alfvén speed crossing time tA​∼ℓμ0​ρ​/B, which can be far shorter than molecular diffusion if a coherent connecting field exists. In steady ideal axisymmetric induction, absence of continuing winding requires Bp​⋅∇Ω=0; this is constancy along the field, not automatically solid-body rotation throughout the star. The topology and strength of an interior field matter. Solar dynamo action, especially the interaction of shear with poloidal-field regeneration, couples stellar rotation to the magnetic cycle. Magnetic feedback can change shear and circulation on dynamical or cycle times; an approximately eleven-year activity cycle and twenty-two-year magnetic polarity cycle are distinct from secular spin-down.
A magnetized stellar wind also supplies an external torque. It enforces approximate corotation out to an effective Alfvén radius RA​, giving wind-driven magnetic braking of a solar-type star J˙∼−M˙w​ΩRA2​. Its spin-down time is tJ​∼I/(M˙w​RA2​), much longer than a convective turnover time. For slowly changing moment of inertia and an unsaturated field scaling that gives J˙=−KΩ3, integration gives Ω−2=Ω0−2​+2Kt/I, the Skumanich rotation law. A growing or contracting star also changes I and can spin up or down even without torque. Differential spin-down can create shear between layers unless internal transport couples them.
Finally, shear can excite Kelvin-Helmholtz instability, and convection can launch internal gravity waves into the stable radiative interior. Wave damping deposits angular momentum; the net transport depends on excitation and selective absorption and is not fixed by a wave-crossing time alone. Such stresses, magnetic coupling and circulation are candidate agents for the nearly uniform radiative rotation and the narrow tachocline. Molecular kinematic viscosity has a diffusion time ℓ2/ν generally too long to couple the entire solar interior over its age, whereas a turbulent effective kinematic viscosity can act much faster but is not appropriate to every stable layer. A circulation has an advection time ℓ/Um​, thermal diffusion has time ℓ2/χ, and magnetic diffusion has time ℓ2/ηm​; these must be compared with the local stellar rotation and evolutionary times rather than conflated with them. The solar dynamical time R3/(GM)​ is about half an hour, its rotation period is of order a month, and secular magnetic braking takes an evolutionary time. Rapid force adjustment, convective maintenance of shear, cyclic magnetic feedback and slow global spin evolution are separate processes. Their coupled conservation equations and timescale hierarchy are the basis of a consistent account of solar rotation.
