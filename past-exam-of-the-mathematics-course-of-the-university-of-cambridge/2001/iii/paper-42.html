Assume axisymmetry in a thin disk orbiting a fixed central mass M. To leading order in thickness, the Keplerian rotation is independent of height, Ω=(GM/r3)1/2, with slow radial drift compared with rΩ. Neglect radial pressure support in the orbital balance, changes in the central potential, mass loss through the disk surfaces and applied surface torques. The Cauchy stress tensor can represent viscous or effective turbulent/magnetic transport; only its vertically integrated azimuthal component enters the conservation of angular momentum equation.
Define the surface density and its mass-weighted radial velocity by
Σ=∫ρdz,Σuˉr​=∫ρur​dz,
(1)
and set Wrϕ​=∫Trϕ​dz. Integrating mass conservation vertically gives
∂t​Σ+r1​∂r​(rΣuˉr​)=0.
(2)
The azimuthal equation in cylindrical coordinates, multiplied by r and combined with mass conservation, is the conservative equation for specific angular momentum l=r2Ω. The cylindrical stress tensor divergence contributes r−1∂r​(r2Trϕ​)+∂z​(rTϕz​). With vanishing surface torque, vertical integration therefore gives
∂t​(Σl)+r1​∂r​(rΣuˉr​l)=r1​∂r​(r2Wrϕ​).
(3)
Because l(r) is fixed in time, subtracting l times the mass equation yields
Σuˉr​drdl​=r1​∂r​(r2Wrϕ​).
(4)
Define the effective viscosity defined by disk stress by
νˉΣrdrdΩ​=Wrϕ​,νˉ=−3ΣΩ2​∫Trϕ​dzfor Keplerian rotation.​
(5)
This sign convention treats Trϕ​ as the shear stress appearing with +∇⋅T. For the Newtonian fluid stress tensor it reduces to the density-weighted viscosity of a disk, νˉ=Σ−1∫ρνdz, rather than an unweighted height average.
Using l=GMr​ and Wrϕ​=−(3/2)νˉΣΩ in the conservation of angular momentum equation gives
uˉr​=−Σr​3​∂r​(r​νˉΣ).
(6)
Substitution into mass conservation proves the Keplerian viscous diffusion equation:
∂t​Σ=r3​∂r​[r​∂r​(r​νˉΣ)].​
(7)
The outward viscous torque in an accretion disk is G=−2πr2Wrϕ​=3πGM​r​νˉΣ.
For νˉ=Ar with A>0, make the heat-equation transform for linear-radius disk viscosity
x=23Ar​​,g=r​νˉΣ=Ar3/2Σ.​
(8)
Writing y=r​, the diffusion equation becomes gt​=(3A/4)gyy​; the displayed rescaling of y gives exactly gt​=gxx​​, with the original time unchanged. The torque is 3πGM​g, so the zero-torque inner boundary condition at the origin becomes g(0,t)=0 on the half-line x>0.
A positive similarity solution obeying this boundary condition is
g=Cxt−3/2e−x2/(4t),Σ=A3A​2C​rt−3/2​e−r/(3At),C>0.​
(9)
For example, substitution of g=t−1F(x/t​) reduces the heat equation to F′′+(η/2)F′+F=0, and F=Cηe−η2/4 satisfies it and vanishes at zero. A replacement of t by t+t0​ gives a finite-mass initial profile at time zero if desired.
To determine the global integrals, transform the mass and angular momentum:
Md​=2π∫0∞​Σrdr=2πA3​​∫0∞​gdx,Jd​=2πGM​∫0∞​Σr3/2dr=3πGM​∫0∞​xgdx.
(10)
The Gaussian integrals give
Md​=4πC3/A​t−1/2,Jd​=6π3/2CGM​=constant.​
(11)
Thus the accreting similarity disk with viscosity proportional to radius loses mass as t−1/2 while conserving angular momentum; its characteristic radius grows proportional to At. The accretion rate at the origin is −M˙d​=2πC3/A​t−3/2. Its radial velocity uˉr​=r/t−3A/2 shows inward flow at small radius and outward spreading at large radius. The integrable inner density singularity is part of the idealized extension to r=0.
For the other profile, direct differentiation gives
ggt​​=−2t1​+4t2x2​=ggxx​​,g=t−1/2e−x2/(4t).
(12)
It also satisfies the heat equation, but has g(0,t)=t−1/2=0 and gx​(0,t)=0. It therefore has a nonzero inner torque and zero mass flow at the origin, not the previous zero-torque inner boundary condition. Here
Σ=Ar3/2t−1/2​e−r/(3At),uˉr​=tr​,
(13)
Md​=2π3π/A​=constant,Jd​=6πGM​t1/2,G(0,t)=J˙d​=3πGM​t−1/2.​
(14)
An arbitrary positive amplitude multiplies these quantities. This constant-mass disk with viscosity proportional to radius spreads outward under a central torque that supplies angular momentum, with no ongoing central mass supply. It has the reflecting Neumann boundary condition for the heat equation and can be regarded as the spreading of an initially central mass distribution driven by an applied torque. It is distinct from the accreting zero-torque solution; the ideal origin does not model a finite stellar surface or a relativistic inner disk.

The magnetohydrodynamic total pressure is
Π=p+2μ0​B2​.​
(15)
The Lorentz force has been split into the gradient of magnetic pressure and the remaining magnetic tension term. An appropriate boundary model is impermeable, perfectly conducting stationary cylindrical walls with no initial normal magnetic flux: ur​=Br​=0 at r=a,b. In an inviscid fluid this is a slip condition, not a no-slip condition on uϕ​ or uz​. It also makes the tangential ideal electric field vanish at the walls. Take perturbations periodic in ϕ and periodic or Fourier-resolved in z; the wall reaction supplies the normal pressure force without an extra prescribed pressure value.
Write the steady velocity and magnetic field as U(r)eϕ​ and B(r)eϕ​. They satisfy both divergence constraints. Their material accelerations have only radial curvature terms, and the steady ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation is identically satisfied because the parallel azimuthal flow and field have cancelling curvature terms. The radial momentum equation is
−ρrU2​=−drdΠ​−μ0​rB2​.
(16)
Hence the most general such equilibrium is
U(r),B(r) arbitrary smooth functions,Π(r)=Π0​+∫r0​r​(sρU(s)2​−μ0​sB(s)2​)ds.​
(17)
Its gas pressure is p=Π−B2/(2μ0​); on a bounded annulus the additive constant can be chosen to keep it positive. Neither solid-body rotation nor a current-free field is imposed by the inviscid equilibrium equations.
For the linear stability calculation, put Ω=U/r, V=B/μ0​ρ​, and define h=δΠ/ρ. Use axisymmetric normal modes proportional to eikz−iωt. Denote the velocity amplitudes by (u,v,w) and the magnetic amplitudes in velocity units by (br​,bϕ​,bz​)=δB/μ0​ρ​. Linearization of the cylindrical momentum equations gives
−iωu−2Ωv−iωv+(2Ω+rΩ′)u−iωw​=−h′−r2V​bϕ​,=(V′+V/r)br​,=−ikh.​
(18)
The linearized ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation and divergence equations are
−iωbr​−iωbϕ​−iωbz​r(ru)′​+ikw​=0,=rΩ′br​+(V/r−V′)u,=0,=0,r(rbr​)′​+ikbz​=0.​
(19)
The curvature terms are essential: although the perturbations have no azimuthal dependence, the cylindrical unit vectors do.
For ω=0, br​=bz​=0. Introduce the radial Lagrangian fluid displacement by u=−iωξ. The azimuthal amplitudes reduce to
v=−(2Ω+rΩ′)ξ,bϕ​=(V/r−V′)ξ.
(20)
Consequently the radial and vertical equations, combined with the incompressible flow constraint, give
h′=(ω2−D)ξ,h=k2ω2​r(rξ)′​,
(21)
where the Michael criterion for axisymmetric toroidal-field interchange coefficient is
D(r)=κ2−rdrd​(r2V2​),κ2=4Ω2+2rΩΩ′.​
(22)
For k=0, the required single displacement equation is therefore
ω2[−drd​(r(rξ)′​)+k2ξ]=k2Dξ,ξ(a)=ξ(b)=0.​
(23)
The same differential equation holds for u, which is a constant multiple of ξ.
Set y=rξ. Multiplication by y∗ and integration by parts with the wall conditions gives the global variational form of the Michael criterion:
ω2=k2∫ab​(∣y′∣2+k2∣y∣2)/rdr∫ab​D∣y∣2/rdr​.​
(24)
The denominator is strictly positive for a nonzero displacement, so every mode's ω2 is real. If D≥0 everywhere, no exponentially growing mode exists. Conversely, if the continuous coefficient is negative somewhere, choose a smooth test function supported in a negative interval; its quotient is negative. For sufficiency, let A=−d(r−1d/dr)/dr+k2/r be the positive Sturm-Liouville operator with Dirichlet boundary conditions and let C multiply by k2D/r. The generalized mode equation ω2Ay=Cy is equivalent to the compact operator and self-adjoint operator problem A−1/2CA−1/2f=ω2f. The negative test quotient guarantees a negative eigenvalue and therefore a growing solution ω=iγ. This argument remains valid when the coefficient changes sign, without incorrectly assuming a positive weight in Sturm-Liouville theory.
Thus, for any fixed nonzero vertical wavenumber,
axisymmetric exponential instability⟺κ2−rdrd​(μ0​ρr2Bϕ2​​)<0 somewhere.​
(25)
Equality is marginal. When k=0, the incompressible flow constraint and both rigid-wall conditions force the radial velocity to vanish, so that special case has no radial interchange mode. When the field vanishes, the coefficient reduces to κ2=r−3(r4Ω2)′, recovering the Rayleigh centrifugal stability criterion.
For a Keplerian disk, κ2=Ω2. If Bϕ​∝rp, the toroidal interchange field threshold in a thin Keplerian disk is
D=Ω2−2(p−1)V2/r2<0,p>1,V2>2(p−1)r2Ω2​.​
(26)
For ordinary radial gradients on the scale r, the Alfvén speed must therefore be comparable to the orbital speed. A field on the gas pressure scale has V≲cs​∼HΩ≪rΩ in a thin disk, and cannot normally meet this condition. Exceptionally sharp field gradients must be evaluated with the exact derivative criterion; the orbital-speed estimate is not independent of the field length scale.
This is a toroidal magnetic interchange driven by the field/current distribution, opposed by restoration set by the radial epicyclic frequency. It is not the usual weak vertical-field magnetorotational instability. In the vertical-field problem, a nonzero kBz​ bends magnetic field lines and couples displaced fluid elements, allowing decreasing Ω to supply rotational kinetic energy even when κ2>0. Here the axisymmetric perturbation has no scalar variation along the purely toroidal background field, and no such weak-field coupling: a sufficiently adverse toroidal-field gradient, rather than merely dΩ/dr<0, is required. Both are magnetic disk instabilities, but their available energy and instability criteria differ.

Take z=0 to be the vertical equilibrium plane and expand the vertical gravitational force to leading order in H/r≪1: ∂z​Φ≃Ωz2​z. The vertical hydrostatic equilibrium equation for uniform density is ∂z​p0​=−ρΩz2​z. With vacuum pressure at both free surfaces, it gives
p0​(r,z)=21​ρΩz​(r)2[H(r)2−z2],∣z∣≤H.​
(27)
The associated radial pressure correction is small at leading thin-disk order; use the prescribed orbital and radial epicyclic frequency at the local radius. Here Ωz​ is the vertical epicyclic frequency.
For the axisymmetric waves of a homogeneous incompressible disk, use a local WKB approximation proportional to exp[i∫k(r)dr−iωt], with ∣kr∣≫1. Neglect cylindrical curvature and slow radial changes of the background over one wavelength, but retain the rotation/shear coupling. Let (u,v,w) be the velocity perturbations and h=p′/ρ. The leading equations are
−iωu−2Ωv−iωv+2Ωκ2​u−iωwiku+wz​​=−ikh,=0,=−hz​,=0.​
(28)
For ω=0 and ω2=κ2, these give u=ωkh/(ω2−κ2) and w=−ihz​/ω. The incompressible flow constraint then yields
pzz′​=ω2−κ2ω2​k2p′.​
(29)
The vertical Lagrangian fluid displacement satisfies w=−iωξz​, so ξz​=pz′​/(ρω2). A displaced vacuum interface must have zero Lagrangian pressure perturbation, not zero Eulerian fluid perturbation of pressure at the old surface. Neglecting the higher-order radial surface-slope contribution,
Δp=p′+ξz​∂z​p0​=p′−ρΩz2​zξz​=0.
(30)
Thus the free-surface pressure condition of an incompressible disk is
p′−ω2Ωz2​​zpz′​=0at z=±H.​
(31)
Because the background and boundary conditions are symmetric about the midplane, choose pressure modes of even function or odd function symmetry in z. For ω2>κ2, put q=kω/ω2−κ2​ and Q=qH. The solutions are p′∝cosh(qz) or sinh(qz); substituting the surface condition gives the two surface modes of an incompressible disk:
ω2=Ωz2​QtanhQ,ω2=Ωz2​QcothQ,​even pressure,odd pressure,​(kH)2=Q2(1−ω2κ2​).​
(32)
These are implicit dispersion relations, or complete parametric equations with parameter Q and the indicated condition ω2>κ2.
For 0<ω2<κ2, put s=kω/κ2−ω2​ and S=sH. The solutions are p′∝cos(sz) or sin(sz), giving the inertial modes of an incompressible disk:
ω2=−Ωz2​StanS,ω2=Ωz2​ScotS,​even pressure,odd pressure,​(kH)2=S2(ω2κ2​−1).​
(33)
The restrictions 0<ω2<κ2 select the admissible segments of the trigonometric functions, producing infinitely many vertical orders. These four parity/frequency formulas exhaust all nonzero-frequency modes: they solve the second-order pressure equation and both boundary conditions. For finite k=0, an exactly epicyclic frequency ω2=κ2 makes the horizontal equations require p′=0, then the vertical equation and the incompressible flow constraint force all velocity components to vanish, so it is not a missing finite-wavenumber branch.
For completeness, the energy identity for free-surface disk waves shows why these modes have real stable frequencies when κ2≥0 and Ωz2​>0. After elimination of the azimuthal velocity, the radial and vertical displacement equations are (ω2−κ2)ξr​=ikh and ω2ξz​=hz​, with ikξr​+ξz,z​=0. Multiply by conjugate displacements, integrate in z and use the boundary condition to obtain
ω2∫−HH​(∣ξr​∣2+∣ξz​∣2)dz=κ2∫−HH​∣ξr​∣2dz+Ωz2​H{∣ξz​(H)∣2+∣ξz​(−H)∣2}.
(34)
The right side is real and nonnegative, excluding additional growing wave branches in this stable case. At zero frequency there can separately be a stationary geostrophic balance: vertically constant h, u=w=0, v=ikh/(2Ω), and static interface displacements satisfying h=Ωz2​zξz​ at the surfaces. This neighbouring-equilibrium perturbation is not a propagating branch omitted by dividing by ω.
For the large-kH limit, the two surface branches have Q≫1, so both tanhQ and cothQ tend to one. Eliminating Q gives their common leading surface dispersion,
ω2∼21​[κ2+κ4+4Ωz4​(kH)2​]=Ωz2​kH+21​κ2+O((kH)−1).​
(35)
In particular ω∼Ωz​kH​. The effective surface gravity is gs​=Ωz2​H, and the pressure decays into the interior on a scale k−1. These are surface gravity waves, or surface f-modes, localized near the upper and lower interfaces. Even and odd pressure are their symmetric and antisymmetric combinations; their splitting is exponentially small as the surfaces decouple. Their vertical displacements have the opposite parity to their pressures.
For each fixed inertial vertical order, large kH means ω≪κ. The boundary condition then requires pz′​≃0 at the surfaces, so the limiting vertical wavenumbers are S=nπ/2, n=1,2,…. Thus
ωn​∼2kHκnπ​(kH→∞ at fixed n).​
(36)
Even n have even pressure, odd n odd pressure. These vertically oscillatory, rotation-restored inertial waves occupy 0<ω<κ and approach zero at fixed order as radial wavenumber increases. The limit is not uniform in order: arbitrarily high vertical orders remain near κ at any fixed kH. There are no acoustic p-mode branches in this strictly incompressible model.
For Ωz​>κ>0, the two surface curves are unique. The even branch approaches κ from above as kH→0, with Q tending to the positive solution of QtanhQ=κ2/Ωz2​. The odd branch approaches Ωz​ because QcothQ→1 as Q→0. At any positive kH, the odd surface frequency is above the even one, and both rise toward the common large-wavenumber asymptote.
The inertial branches all approach κ from below as kH→0. For even pressure there is one admissible branch in each interval ((j+1/2)π,(j+1)π), j≥0, terminating at S=(j+1)π as kH→∞. For odd pressure there is one in each interval (jπ,(j+1/2)π), again terminating at its upper endpoint; the first interval is admissible because κ2/Ωz2​<1. Their curves decrease toward zero as shown in the sketch. The small-kH endpoints are formal local-dispersion limits; the WKB approximation still requires kH≫H/r for a finite-thickness disk.
The plot shows both surface branches and the lowest six inertial orders; higher orders accumulate below κ. If κ=0, the inertial frequencies collapse to zero, while the surface formulas reduce to ω2=Ωz2​(kH)tanh(kH) and Ωz2​(kH)coth(kH). A negative κ2 instead introduces instability described by the Rayleigh centrifugal stability criterion and is outside the real-frequency stable sketch.
