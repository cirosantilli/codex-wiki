For a submartingale (Zn​) and a<b, let UN​(a,b) count the completed upcrossings of [a,b] by time N. The Doob upcrossing inequality gives
(b−a)EUN​(a,b)≤E(ZN​−a)+.
(1)
For a martingale (Xn​), a useful sharper form is
(b−a)EUN​(a,b)≤E(XN​−a)−.​
(2)
Here x+=max(x,0) and x−=max(−x,0). To see the martingale form, use a bounded predictable process Hk​∈{0,1} that holds one unit after an observation at or below a and releases it on the next observation at or above b. Its gain GN​=∑k=1N​Hk​(Xk​−Xk−1​) is at least (b−a)UN​−(XN​−a)−: completed trades earn at least b−a, and any unfinished trade loses at most (XN​−a)−. Since EGN​=0, the bound follows. For the submartingale version, apply the same strategy to (Zn​−a)+, itself a submartingale. Each purchase occurs at value zero, so its gain is at least (b−a)UN​. The complementary predictable strategy has nonnegative expected gain, bounding this strategy's expected gain by E(ZN​−a)+−E(Z0​−a)+, and in particular by the stated right side.
The almost-sure martingale convergence theorem says that if C=supn​E∣Xn​∣<∞, then Xn​ converges almost surely to a finite, integrable random variable Y, with E∣Y∣≤C. Indeed, the Doob upcrossing inequality bounds the expected total number of upcrossings of every rational interval by (C+∣a∣)/(b−a). By monotone convergence, each such total count is finite almost surely; intersect these probability-one events over all rational a<b. If the lower and upper limits of a sample path differed, some rational interval strictly between them would be crossed infinitely often. Thus each remaining path has a limit in the extended real line. The Fatou lemma gives
E[liminfn​∣Xn​∣]≤C,
(3)
so the limit is finite almost surely and integrable. Almost-sure convergence by itself neither preserves expectations nor supplies this uniform first-moment bound.
For the loss of expectation, let (ηk​) be independent variables with the fair Bernoulli distribution and set
X0​=1,Xn​=2n1{η1​=⋯=ηn​=1}​.
(4)
Conditional on the past, survival doubles the value with probability 1/2 and otherwise sets it to zero, so this is a nonnegative martingale. An infinite string of successes has probability zero. The process therefore eventually vanishes almost surely, giving yes: X0​=1 and Y=0 almost surely are possible. Its expectations remain one at every finite time; the process is not uniformly integrable.
A nonintegrable limit is also possible for a signed martingale. Take independent ξk​ with
P(ξk​=4k)=P(ξk​=−4k)=2−k−1,P(ξk​=0)=1−2−k,k≥1,
(5)
and let M0​=1, Mn​=1+∑k=1n​ξk​. Every increment has mean zero and a finite absolute expectation, so each Mn​ is integrable and (Mn​) is a martingale. Since ∑k​P(ξk​=0)<∞, the Borel-Cantelli lemma makes only finitely many increments nonzero almost surely; hence the finite limit Y=1+∑k​ξk​ exists almost surely. To compute its absolute expectation, let Ak​ be the event that only the kth increment is nonzero. Independence gives
P(Ak​)=2−k∏j=k​(1−2−j)≥c2−k,c=∏j≥1​(1−2−j)>0.
(6)
The infinite product is positive because its tail logarithms are bounded in absolute value by a constant times the summable series ∑j​2−j. The events Ak​ are disjoint, and on Ak​, ∣Y∣≥4k−1. Therefore
E∣Y∣≥c∑k≥1​2−k(4k−1)=∞.
(7)
Thus yes: a finite almost-sure martingale limit can have infinite absolute expectation, even with deterministic M0​=1. This is almost-sure martingale convergence to a nonintegrable limit; the missing hypothesis is the uniform first-moment bound. For a nonnegative martingale, in contrast, the Fatou lemma would force the limit to be integrable.

Write si​=pi​+qi​>0 and di​=pi​−qi​ for i=0,1. For the parity correction hθ​(k)=k−θ1{k odd}​, the conditional expectations of the increments of hθ​(Xn​) are
{p0​(1−θ)+q0​(−1−θ)=d0​−θs0​,p1​(1+θ)+q1​(−1+θ)=d1​+θs1​,​Xn​ even,Xn​ odd.​
(8)
The holding move contributes zero. Thus the period-two martingale corrector for a birth-death chain exists precisely when
θ=s0​d0​​=−s1​d1​​,s0​d0​​+s1​d1​​=0.​
(9)
This is the condition that hθ​ be a harmonic function for a Markov chain. The process has integrable values from a fixed initial state because ∣Xn​∣≤∣X0​∣+n; the Markov property therefore turns the zero conditional increments into the required martingale property. Conversely, both parities are reached with positive probability because all right and left probabilities are positive, so the two conditional equations are necessary. Expanding their numerator also gives the equivalent balance condition p0​p1​=q0​q1​. Positivity makes ∣θ∣<1.
Put L=−aN, U=bN. The exit time TN​ is almost surely finite. Indeed, set m=U−L and p∗​=min(p0​,p1​)>0. From any interior point, consecutive right steps reach U in at most m moves, with probability at least p∗m​. The Markov property gives P(TN​>km)≤(1−p∗m​)k. This is the finite-interval exit times have geometric tails argument. The walk cannot skip a boundary because its jumps have size at most one.
Under the balance condition, the stopped martingale hθ​(Xn∧TN​​) is bounded, because the stopped position stays in [L,U]. Apply the optional stopping theorem at n∧TN​, then dominated convergence as n→∞. Starting from zero gives
0=πN​hθ​(L)+(1−πN​)hθ​(U).
(10)
With εa​=1{aN odd}​ and εb​=1{bN odd}​,
πN​=(a+b)N+θεa​−θεb​bN−θεb​​,N→∞lim​πN​=a+bb​.​
(11)
The bounded parity corrections disappear after division by N; the holding probability of a Markov chain may be positive or zero and may differ between parities.
For failure of balance, use scale increments for a birth-death chain rather than the nonexistent bounded correction. Let u(k) be the upper exit probability from k for boundaries L,U. The Markov property yields
pk​(u(k+1)−u(k))=qk​(u(k)−u(k−1)),u(L)=0,u(U)=1,
(12)
where pk​,qk​ depend only on parity. Choose positive numbers wj​ with wj+1​=(qj​/pj​)wj​. Then u(k)=∑j=L+1k​wj​/∑j=L+1U​wj​ satisfies these equations and boundary values. Stopping this bounded harmonic function proves that it is the exit probability. In particular,
πN​=∑j=−aN+1bN​wj​∑j=1bN​wj​​,wj+2​=ρwj​,ρ=p0​p1​q0​q1​​.
(13)
If ρ<1, the positive-index sum stays bounded as N→∞, while the negative-index contribution diverges geometrically, so πN​→0. If ρ>1, the negative-index sum stays bounded while the positive-index sum diverges, so πN​→1. Therefore the complete classification is
N→∞lim​πN​=⎩⎨⎧​0,b/(a+b),1,​p0​p1​>q0​q1​,p0​p1​=q0​q1​,p0​p1​<q0​q1​.​​
(14)
Thus a fixed nonzero imbalance drives the macroscopic exit to the corresponding side, for every positive a,b.

True. The preceding distributional argument alone shows that almost-sure convergence to zero cannot hold with probability one. To establish almost-sure nonconvergence, rather than merely failure of a probability-one convergence claim, use the Kolmogorov zero-one law.
Let A={Bn​/n​→0}. For every fixed m, subtracting Bm​/n​→0 shows that
A={n​∑k=m+1n​(Bk​−Bk−1​)​⟶0}.
(28)
Thus A belongs to the tail sigma-algebra of the independent unit-time increments; it is a tail event. The Kolmogorov zero-one law gives P(A)∈{0,1}. If its probability were one, then Bn​/n​→0 in probability, contrary to its standard normal distribution at every n. Therefore P(A)=0. Continuous-time convergence would imply integer-time convergence, so
P(Bt​/t​ does not converge to 0)=1.​
(29)
The more general Brownian fluctuations exceed the square-root scale result is consistent with this direct tail-event proof. No independence of the normalized values themselves has been assumed.

For the continuous adapted process X, the supremum on each compact time interval is attained. Let Dt​=(Q∩[0,t])∪{t}. Continuity gives sups≤t​Xs​=supr∈Dt​​Xr​, and hence

Each event inside the union is in Fr​⊆Ft​, so this proves carefully that T is a stopping time. Merely checking rational times for Xr​≥λ would miss a path that touches the level at an isolated irrational time; the approximation from below avoids this issue. This is the closed-level hitting times of continuous adapted processes criterion. For λ=0, nonnegativity makes T=0 and the later inequality is immediate.
For λ>0, put A={T≤t} and τ=T∧t. On A, continuity implies Xτ​≥λ. To justify the optional sampling step without extra filtration assumptions, round τ upwards to a finite dyadic grid in [0,t], obtaining stopping times τn​↓τ. The event A belongs to Fτ​, and hence to Fτn​​: directly, A∩{τ≤u}={T≤u} for u<t, while for u≥t it is A. Discrete optional sampling for the sampled submartingale gives
E(Xt​1A​)≥E(Xτn​​1A​).

E(Xt​1A​)≥E(Xτ​1A​)≥λP(A).

Consequently the Doob maximal inequality for a nonnegative submartingale takes the precise form
λP(Xt∗​≥λ)≤E(Xt​1{Xt∗​≥λ}​).​

For t>0, put Mt​=sup0≤s≤t​Bs​. For λ>0, reflect the Brownian path after its first hit of λ. The Strong Markov property and symmetry of subsequent Brownian increments make this reflection preserve the path distribution. It exchanges the events {Mt​≥λ,Bt​<λ} and {Bt​>λ}. P(Mt​>λ)=2P(Bt​>λ)=2(1−Φ(λ/t​)),t>0, λ≥0.​

When paths move only by jumps of size ±1, there is neither a Gaussian component nor a continuous drift. The Lévy measure is supported on {−1,1} and has finite mass because these points are bounded away from zero. The process is therefore a Compound Poisson process, expressible as a difference of independent Poisson processes
Xt​=Nt+​−Nt−​,
(44)
where the rates are λ+​,λ−​ respectively. The martingale assumptions imply
0=EXt​=(λ+​−λ−​)t,Var(Xt​)=(λ+​+λ−​)t=t.
(45)
Hence λ+​=λ−​=1/2​. The embedded jump chain is a simple symmetric random walk, and the total jump rate is one. In particular,
X1​=dN+​−N−​,N+​,N−​ independent Poisson(1/2).​
(46)
This is the symmetric Skellam distribution, with the explicit mass function
P(X1​=k)=e−1j=0∑∞​j!(j+∣k∣)!2−(2j+∣k∣)​,k∈Z.​
(47)
For k≥0, sum the joint probabilities of N+​=j+k,N−​=j; for k<0, interchange the two variables. The characteristic function is EeiuX1​=exp(cosu−1), consistent with the rate-one symmetric Poisson difference process. The condition that paths move only by jumps is essential to exclude a separate continuous component.

For the exit law, the two identified processes have the same argument. Starting at zero, neither can skip −a or b: Brownian motion is continuous, and the symmetric Poisson difference process has unit jumps with integer boundary levels. Therefore ∣Xt∧T​∣≤K=max(a,b), and if T is finite, XT​∈{−a,b}.

E(t∧T)=EXt∧T2​≤K2.

Monotone convergence now gives ET≤K2, so T<∞ almost surely. The stopped position tends to XT​ as t→∞ and is bounded, making dominated convergence applicable.
