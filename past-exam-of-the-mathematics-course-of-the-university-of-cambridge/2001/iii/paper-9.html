Let f be a nonzero meromorphic function in a neighborhood of the closed disk ∣z∣≤R, with no boundary zero or pole. List its interior zeros aj​ with multiplicities mj​ and its interior poles bk​ with orders nk​. Define the disk Green function and Poisson kernel by
GR​(z,a)=log​R(z−a)R2−az​​,PR​(z,θ)=∣Reiθ−z∣2R2−∣z∣2​.
(1)
At every interior point that is neither a zero nor a pole, the Poisson-Jensen formula is
log∣f(z)∣=2π1​∫02π​PR​(z,θ)log∣f(Reiθ)∣dθ−j∑​mj​GR​(z,aj​)+k∑​nk​GR​(z,bk​).​
(2)
In particular, at z=0 when f(0) is finite and nonzero, this becomes Jensen's formula, with each zero contributing −mj​log(R/∣aj​∣) and each pole contributing nk​log(R/∣bk​∣).
Here is a proof that also explains the signs. The disk Blaschke factors
Ba​(z)=R2−azR(z−a)​
(3)
have a simple zero at a, no poles in the disk and boundary modulus one. Therefore
F(z)=f(z)∏j​Baj​​(z)mj​∏k​Bbk​​(z)nk​​
(4)
extends to a zero-free holomorphic function throughout the disk. Its logarithmic modulus is a harmonic function and agrees with log∣f∣ on the boundary. The Poisson integral represents log∣F(z)∣ by that boundary data. Since log∣Ba​∣=−GR​(⋅,a), substitution gives the displayed Poisson-Jensen formula. Boundary zeros or poles can be handled by taking limiting radii: their logarithmic singularities are integrable. When zero is itself a zero or pole, factor out its signed power of z before evaluating Jensen's formula there.

We use the complete hyperbolic metric of curvature −1; on the unit disc its length element is 2∣dz∣/(1−∣z∣2). This normalization matters in the exponential summability criterion. A nonzero bounded holomorphic function on the entire plane is constant by Liouville's theorem, so the prescribed infinite zero set forces Ω=C. The Riemann mapping theorem supplies a biholomorphism ϕ:Ω→D, and transports the disk hyperbolic metric to Ω.
Fix w∈Ω and choose ϕ(w)=0. Put an​=ϕ(zn​) and F=f∘ϕ−1. First suppose F(0)=0. For radii avoiding its zeros, Jensen's formula gives
∑∣an​∣<r​log∣an​∣r​≤log∥F∥∞​−log∣F(0)∣.
(5)
The monotone convergence theorem as r↑1 gives ∑n​−log∣an​∣<∞, hence ∑n​(1−∣an​∣)<∞. If F has a zero at zero, factor off its finite order first; the resulting holomorphic function is still bounded, since division by that power is bounded away from zero and extends near zero. Thus the Blaschke condition holds in either case, with the possible origin zero treated as one additional finite term.
The Hyperbolic distance in the Poincare disc satisfies
ρ(w,zn​)=log1−∣an​∣1+∣an​∣​,e−ρ(w,zn​)=1+∣an​∣1−∣an​∣​≤1−∣an​∣.
(6)
Consequently the exponential distances are summable. This holds for every basepoint: the triangle inequality bounds the ratio of e−ρ(w,zn​) and e−ρ(w0​,zn​) between e−ρ(w,w0​) and eρ(w,w0​).

A simply connected plane domain carrying a hyperbolic metric is proper. By the Riemann mapping theorem, choose a biholomorphism ϕ:Ω→D sending a basepoint to zero. The same distance formula now gives
1−∣an​∣=(1+∣an​∣)e−ρ(w,zn​)≤2e−ρ(w,zn​),an​=ϕ(zn​).
(7)
Thus the Blaschke condition holds. In particular the an​ have no interior accumulation point: near any such point the quantities 1−∣an​∣ would stay bounded away from zero.
Construct the Blaschke product
B(ζ)=ζm∏an​=0​an​∣an​∣​1−an​​ζan​−ζ​,m∈{0,1}.
(8)
Here m=1 precisely when the sequence contains zero. For ∣ζ∣≤r<1, its normalized Blaschke factor ba​ satisfies
1−ba​(ζ)=1−aζ(1−∣a∣)(1+(∣a∣/a)ζ)​,∣1−ba​(ζ)∣≤1−r1+r​(1−∣a∣).
(9)
The Blaschke condition therefore makes the infinite product converge uniformly on every compact subdisk. Away from the prescribed zeros its tail factors tend uniformly to one with summable errors, so their logarithms converge and the product is nonzero. It has exactly the prescribed zeros, and the maximum modulus principle bounds every finite partial product by one, hence ∣B∣≤1. Thus f=B∘ϕ is the required bounded analytic function, with no extra zeros.
Simple connectivity cannot be dropped. On the punctured disk choose
w=e−1,zn​=e−n2,n≥2.
(10)
The hyperbolic metric on the punctured disk is ∣dz∣/(∣z∣log(1/∣z∣)). Its universal covering map τ↦eiτ pulls it back to the curvature-minus-one upper-half-plane metric. The imaginary axis is a geodesic; the lift from i to in2 has length logn2, and any other lift differs by a real deck translation and has at least that distance. Therefore
ρ(w,zn​)=2logn,∑n≥2​e−ρ(w,zn​)=∑n≥2​n21​<∞.
(11)
The triangle inequality again makes the sum finite for every basepoint. However, any bounded holomorphic function on the punctured disk extends over zero by the Riemann removable singularity theorem. These zeros accumulate at zero, so the identity theorem makes the extension identically zero. It cannot have exactly the prescribed zero set. This example of hyperbolic zero sequences on a punctured disk satisfies the distance condition but not the analytic zero-set condition.

Use the Nevanlinna proximity function m(r,f)=(2π)−1∫log+∣f(reiθ)∣dθ and the Nevanlinna integrated counting function N(r,f) for poles, counting multiplicity. If the pole order at zero is ν0​, then
N(r,f)=ν0​logr+∑0<∣b∣<r​νb​log∣b∣r​,T(r,f)=m(r,f)+N(r,f).
(12)
Boundedness of the Nevanlinna characteristic refers to r↑1, equivalently to a uniform bound on r0​≤r<1 for any fixed r0​>0. This avoids the harmless origin normalization when ν0​>0.
The Nevanlinna first main theorem states, for every fixed finite value a and every nonconstant meromorphic function on the disk,
N(r,1/(f−a))+m(r,1/(f−a))=T(r,f)+O(1).​
(13)
For a=∞, this is the definition of T. The error is bounded independently of r. Translation changes T by O(1), while Jensen's formula gives T(r,h)−T(r,1/h)=log∣c∣ when h(z)=czν(1+O(z)) near zero; these observations explain the first theorem.
The Nevanlinna second main theorem in the unit disc states that for q≥3 distinct values aj​ on the Riemann sphere,
(q−2)T(r,f)≤j=1∑q​N(r;aj​)+O(log+T(r,f)+log1−r1​)​
(14)
as r↑1 outside an exceptional set E satisfying ∫E​dr/(1−r)<∞. The truncated Nevanlinna counting function N counts each distinct preimage once. The boundary-distance error cannot simply be suppressed: on the disk it need not be o(T). The disk error and exceptional-set estimates follow from the logarithmic-derivative estimates in mathweb.tifr.res.in/Documents/Publications/Lectures/tifr17.pdf .
Suppose first that T(r,f) is bounded. Since m(r,f)≥0, its poles satisfy the Blaschke condition, counting their orders: let r↑1 in their integrated count and use 1−∣b∣≤−log∣b∣. Let B be their Blaschke product, including the origin factor if necessary. Then g=Bf extends to a holomorphic function on the whole disk, and ∣B∣≤1 gives
2π1​∫02π​log+∣g(reiθ)∣dθ≤m(r,f)≤C.
(15)
Here the last bound follows from bounded T and the uniform lower bound N(r,f)≥ν0​logr0​ on r≥r0​.
The nonnegative subharmonic function log+∣g∣ has a finite harmonic majorant. Indeed its Poisson integrals HR​ on expanding disks dominate it by the maximum principle for subharmonic functions, increase with R, and have HR​(0)≤C. The Harnack inequality for harmonic functions bounds them on every compact subdisk, so their increasing limit is a finite harmonic function H≥log+∣g∣. Since the disk is simply connected, H has a harmonic conjugate, giving a holomorphic function A with ReA=H. Consequently
u=ge−A,v=Be−A,∣u∣≤1,∣v∣≤1,v≡0,f=u/v.
(16)
This proves the constructive half of the quotient characterization of bounded characteristic.
Conversely, if f=u/v with u,v bounded holomorphic functions and v≡0, each pole of f is a zero of v of at least the same order. The elementary inequality log+∣u/v∣≤log+∣u∣+log+∣1/v∣ and the Nevanlinna first main theorem give
T(r,f)≤m(r,u)+T(r,1/v)+O(1)≤log+∥u∥∞​+T(r,v)+O(1)=O(1).
(17)
Any cancellation at an origin zero contributes only a bounded multiple of logr on r≥r0​, which is included in this O(1). The zero function is immediate; constant nonzero v is handled directly. Thus bounded characteristic is exactly a quotient of two bounded analytic functions.
An unbounded example with bounded characteristic is
g(z)=1−z1​.​
(18)
It is a ratio of the bounded holomorphic functions 1 and 1−z. More explicitly, Jensen's formula gives the mean of log∣1−reiθ∣ as zero, so m(r,1/(1−z))=m(r,1−z)≤log2, although g(r)→∞.
Finally, set Y=(1+∣g∣2)p/2≥1. Pointwise log+∣g∣≤p−1logY. Jensen's inequality for the concave logarithm gives
T(r,g)=m(r,g)≤p1​∫logY2πdθ​≤p1​log∫Y2πdθ​<plogC​.
(19)
There is no pole term because g is holomorphic. Hence the stated integral bound implies bounded characteristic. This argument in fact works for every p>0.

The printed proximity integral is missing a factor 1/(2π). Denote that literal integral by mprint​ and write m=mprint​/(2π). The correct Annular spherical Jensen formula, with the printed definitions of N and T, is
T(r)=I(a)log(1/r)+N(r;a)+m(r;a).​
(20)
The following derivation proves this identity and also isolates the normalization issue.
For the chordal metric put ua​(z)=−logk(f(z),a) and let Ma​(s)=(2π)−1∫02π​ua​(seiθ)dθ. In a finite target coordinate,
ua​=21​log(1+∣f∣2)−log∣f−a∣+21​log(1+∣a∣2).
(21)
For a=∞ use u∞​=21​log(1+∣f∣2). Differentiating away from the preimages of a gives half the squared spherical derivative of a meromorphic function. At an a-point of local degree d, ua​=−dlog∣z−z0​∣+O(1); the second distributional derivatives give the Laplacian of log∣z−z0​∣ is 2πδz0​​. Thus, including poles by changing spherical coordinates,
Δua​=21​(1+∣f∣22∣f′∣​)2−2π∑f(z)=a​degz​(f)δz​.
(22)
Because a is off the image of ∣z∣=1, ua​ is smooth near that circle and I(a)=Ma′​(1) is finite and independent of r.
Apply Green's second identity to the harmonic weight w(z)=log(∣z∣/r) on r<∣z∣<1. On the outer boundary w=log(1/r) and ∂n​w=1; on the inner boundary w=0 and ∂n​w=−1/r. Hence
∫r<∣z∣<1​wΔua​dA=2π[Ma′​(1)log(1/r)+Ma​(r)−Ma​(1)].
(23)
The distributional formula makes the left side 2πT(r)−2πN(r;a), and Ma​(r)−Ma​(1)=m(r;a), proving the corrected Annular spherical Jensen formula. Initially take an inner circle avoiding a-points. Logarithmic singularities have continuous angular means, and a point on the inner circle has zero logarithmic counting weight, so continuity extends the identity to every 0<r≤1.
There is no way to absorb the missing factor into a different constant I(a). For the holomorphic function f(z)=z and a=∞, direct integration gives
N(r;∞)=0,mprint​(r;∞)=πlog21+r2​,T(r)=21​logr1​+21​log21+r2​.
(24)
The value of (T−mprint​)/log(1/r) tends to 1/2 as r↓0 and to π as r↑1. Therefore the literal printed identity has no constant I(∞), whereas the corrected identity has I(∞)=1/2.
For the singularity criterion, define the nondecreasing normalized spherical area
s(t)=4π1​∫e−t<∣z∣<1​(1+∣f∣22∣f′∣​)2dA,t≥0.
(25)
Tonelli's theorem applied to the logarithmic weight gives T(e−l)=∫0l​s(t)dt. Averages of a nondecreasing function converge to its supremum: for fixed t0​<l, (1−t0​/l)s(t0​)≤T(e−l)/l≤s(l). Thus the limit, not just the lower limit, exists in [0,∞] and equals
r↓0lim​log(1/r)T(r)​=4π1​∫0<∣z∣<1​(1+∣f∣22∣f′∣​)2dA.​
(26)
If f has a removable singularity or pole at zero, it extends as a holomorphic map to the Riemann sphere; the pulled-back spherical density is smooth and bounded on the closed disk, hence its area is finite. Conversely an essential singularity gives, by the meromorphic version of the Great Picard theorem, infinitely many preimages of every spherical target with at most two exceptions in each punctured neighborhood. The area formula gives
∫0<∣z∣<1​(fround#​)2dA=∫S2​nf​(a)dAS2​(a)=∞.
(27)
The formula follows by applying change of variables on inverse-function neighborhoods away from the discrete critical points, then summing with multiplicity; the countable critical values have area zero. This proves the finite spherical area criterion for an isolated singularity and the requested equivalence between a finite lower limit and a removable singularity or pole.

A covering-surface formulation of the Ahlfors second fundamental theorem is as follows. For a regular finite bordered Riemann surface X mapped holomorphically to a fixed metric bordered Riemann surface Y, let S=Af​(X)/A(Y) be the average sheet number and L the pulled-back length of the relative boundary, namely the boundary mapped into the interior of Y. With the modern sign convention for the Euler characteristic,
max{−χ(X),0}≥−Sχ(Y)−hL,
(28)
where h depends on Y and its metric, not on X or the map. The associated area comparison is ∣SD​−S∣≤hD​L for each fixed regular target region D. These formulations and the sign convention are explained in www.math.purdue.edu/~eremenko/dvi/ahl.pdf .
The island version of the Ahlfors second fundamental theorem for a disk source, which is the useful form here, says that for q≥3 fixed Jordan domains Dj​ with disjoint closures on the Riemann sphere,
j=1∑q​nj​(R)≥(q−2)S(R)−hL(R).​
(29)
Here nj​ counts simply connected islands of a meromorphic function over Dj​, once each without weighting by mapping degree, and
S(R)=4π1​∫∣z∣<R​(fround#​)2dA,L(R)=∫∣z∣=R​fround#​∣dz∣.
(30)
An island of a meromorphic function is a relatively compact inverse-image component mapped properly onto its target domain; components reaching the source boundary are not counted. The simply connected island estimate is recorded, for spherical disks, in Lemma 2 of pdfs.semanticscholar.org/d247/562607e237af7c8fc6e683b77be6b94829fa.pdf . Regular target regions give the same estimate with a region-dependent constant. General Jordan domains can be enclosed in slightly larger regular Jordan domains still having disjoint closures. A simple island over an enlarged domain restricts to one over the original domain, so it suffices to prove the conclusion for regular domains.
Suppose a nonconstant meromorphic function on the plane has no simple islands over five such domains. On every counted simply connected island its proper map has integer degree at least two. The area formula therefore gives
2nj​(R)≤SDj​​(R),SDj​​(R)=A(Dj​)1​∫{∣z∣<R}∩f−1Dj​​(fround#​)2dA.
(31)
Together with area comparison and the Ahlfors second fundamental theorem, this yields
3S(R)≤∑j​nj​(R)+hL(R)≤25​S(R)+CL(R),21​S(R)≤CL(R).
(32)
The area comparison can also be seen directly. The two-form [1D​/A(D)−1/(4π)]dA on the target sphere has zero integral and a bounded one-form primitive. One construction solves its Poisson equation: the gradient of the spherical Green function has an integrable 1/distance singularity, so convolution against the bounded density gives a bounded primitive. Pulling back and applying Stokes theorem, or smooth approximation at the domain boundary, bounds the integral by a constant times L(R).
We now derive the needed length-area exhaustion of the complex plane. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on a circle gives
L(R)2≤2πR∫∣z∣=R​(fround#​)2∣dz∣=8π2RS′(R).
(33)
If L(R)/S(R)≥ε>0 for all sufficiently large R, then S(R)>0 and
(S(R)1​)′=−S(R)2S′(R)​≤−8π2Rε2​.
(34)
Integration makes 1/S(R) negative, which is impossible. Hence there are radii Rk​→∞ with L(Rk​)/S(Rk​)→0. This contradicts S/2≤CL and proves the Ahlfors five islands theorem: one of the five target domains has a bounded simply connected inverse-image component mapped biholomorphically onto it.
Four domains do not suffice. Take the Weierstrass elliptic function ℘ for the square lattice Z+iZ. The equation
(℘′)2=4(℘−e1​)(℘−e2​)(℘−e3​)
(35)
shows the three distinct finite branch values e1​,e2​,e3​; the fourth is infinity, because all poles are double. Equivalently, the degree-two map from the elliptic torus to the Riemann sphere has these four branch values, with all their preimages of local degree two. Choose four small disjoint Jordan domains, each containing one of these four totally ramified Weierstrass values. A degree-one island of a meromorphic function over any one would contain a preimage of its central value of local degree one, contradicting total ramification. Thus ℘ provides four domains with no simple island.

Write s=A(S)=4π, L=L(∂U), and a=min{A(U),A(S∖U)}. We first prove a constant-version spherical isoperimetric inequality directly, including regions with several boundary components.
If L≥1, the arithmetic bound A(U)A(S∖U)≤s2/4=4π2 already gives A(U)A(S∖U)≤4π2L2. If L=0, a region with smooth boundary is empty or the whole connected sphere up to boundary-null changes, and its area product is zero. For 0<L<1, write the smooth boundary as finitely many disjoint simple closed curves γj​ of lengths lj​. Fix a point pj​ on each curve. The shorter boundary arc from pj​ to any other point has length at most lj​/2, so γj​ is contained in the closed geodesic ball of radius lj​/2 about pj​.
The complement of that small ball is connected and lies entirely on one side of γj​. The Jordan curve theorem therefore gives a disk Ej​ bounded by γj​ contained in the ball. Its area satisfies
A(Ej​)≤2π(1−cos(lj​/2))≤4π​lj2​.
(36)
The indicator function of U and the sum ∑j​1Ej​​ have the same jumps modulo two across every boundary curve. Their difference modulo two is consequently constant throughout the sphere off the boundary. Thus either U or its complement lies in ⋃j​Ej​, up to its area-zero boundary. a≤∑j​A(Ej​)≤4π​∑j​lj2​≤4π​L2,A(U)A(S∖U)≤sa≤π2L2.
