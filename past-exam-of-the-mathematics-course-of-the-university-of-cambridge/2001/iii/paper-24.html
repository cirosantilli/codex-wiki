Write the predictable sigma-algebra on positive times as
P=σ{B×(s,t]:0≤s<t<∞, B∈Fs​}.
(1)
It is equivalently the smallest sigma-algebra making all left-continuous adapted processes measurable. Indeed, the indicator of each generating rectangle is left-continuous and adapted, and approximating any left-continuous adapted process by its values at the preceding points of deterministic partitions gives the reverse inclusion. Previsibility records information available immediately before observation. If time zero is included, add B×{0} with B∈F0​. The displayed description in part (b) uses positive times, as indicated by its product space.

Observing Xs​ reveals exactly whether ω<s. Thus the natural filtration is
Ft​=σ{(0,s):0≤s≤t}.
(2)
Every generating set either contains the entire tail [t,∞) or avoids it, so this tail remains one indistinguishable atom. Conversely, these initial intervals generate the full relative Borel sigma-algebra on (0,t): use rational endpoints below t, together with (0,t) itself. Therefore every Borel set contained in (0,t), and its complement, belongs to Ft​. We obtain
Ft​={B∈F:B⊆(0,t) or Bc⊆(0,t)}.​
(3)
The strict endpoint is essential: the value at ω=t is still Xt​=1, so it cannot be distinguished from a later lifetime.

Put D={(ω,t):ω≥t} and let C denote the proposed family of sets. This is a sigma-algebra: complements replace T and A by their complements, and countable unions replace them by their unions. For a generating predictable rectangle B×(s,u], part (a) says either B⊆(0,s) or Bc⊆(0,s). In the first case its trace on D is empty; in the second it is exactly D∩(Ω×(s,u]). Its trace on Dc is always product-Borel. Hence P⊆C.
For the reverse inclusion, X is left-continuous and adapted, so D={X=1} belongs to the predictable sigma-algebra. Every deterministic Borel set in time also belongs to that sigma-algebra, hence D∩(Ω×T) is predictable. For a product rectangle E×T,
(E×T)∩Dc=⋃s∈Q>0​​((E∩(0,s))×(s,∞))∩(Ω×T).
(4)
Each term is predictable because E∩(0,s)∈Fs​. Every pair with ω<t lies in such a term by choosing ω<s<t. The class of product-Borel sets A for which A∩Dc is predictable is a sigma-algebra containing the product rectangles, so it contains F⊗F. Thus
P={(D∩(Ω×T))∪(Dc∩A):T∈F, A∈F⊗F}.​
(5)
This survival-observation predictable sigma-algebra expresses a sharp distinction: before and at the lifetime the only observable coordinate is time, whereas strictly after it the lifetime is known.

For a simple predictable process, define its stochastic integral by
(H⋅M)t​=∑k=0n−1​Ztk​​(Mt∧tk+1​​−Mt∧tk​​).
(6)
Common refinement of the deterministic partitions makes this definition independent of the representation. Each summand is a martingale increment multiplied by information already available at its left endpoint, so the resulting process is a continuous square-integrable martingale starting at zero. Its terminal value is the same sum with t replaced by tn​.
Write Δk​M=Mtk+1​​−Mtk​​. If j<k, then Ztj​​Δj​MZtk​​ is Ftk​​-measurable. By the conditional expectation identity for increments of a martingale,
E[Ztj​​Δj​MZtk​​Δk​M]=0.
(7)
The conditional bracket isometry for stopped martingale increments gives
E[(Δk​M)2∣Ftk​​]=E([M]tk+1​​−[M]tk​​∣Ftk​​).
(8)
For completeness, this identity follows by applying the optional stopping theorem to M and M2−[M] and expanding the square. The latter process is a true uniformly integrable martingale here: the Doob L2 maximal inequality bounds Esupt​∣Mt​∣2, stopped square identities and Fatou's lemma give E[M]∞​<∞, and its absolute supremum is bounded by supt​∣Mt​∣2+[M]∞​. Consequently all the stopped identities pass to the limit.
Multiplying the conditional identity by Ztk​2​, taking expectations, and using the vanished cross terms proves the Itô isometry:
E[(H⋅M)∞2​]=k∑​E[Ztk​2​([M]tk+1​​−[M]tk​​)]=E∫0∞​Hs2​d[M]s​.​
(9)
To extend the stochastic integral, use the finite quadratic-variation measure νM​(C)=E∫1C​d[M] on the predictable sigma-algebra. The density of simple predictable processes for finite measures gives approximations to every H∈L2(νM​). The Itô isometry makes the corresponding terminal integrals Cauchy in L2, and the Doob L2 maximal inequality makes the whole processes Cauchy in expected squared supremum. Their limit defines a continuous martingale, and the isometry persists. For a locally bounded predictable process, choose increasing stopping times tending to infinity on which it is bounded, also restrict the time horizon, and perform this construction after stopping. The isometry shows that the stopped definitions agree on overlaps, so they patch into a uniquely defined continuous local martingale H⋅M.

The Itô formula decomposes the smooth transform into a continuous local martingale and a finite-variation process:
f(Mt​)−f(0)=Nt​+21​∫0t​f′′(Ms​)d[M]s​,Nt​=∫0t​f′(Ms​)dMs​.
(10)
If f(M) has finite variation, then N also has finite variation. A continuous finite-variation local martingale is constant, so N=0 and
0=[N]t​=∫0t​f′(Ms​)2d[M]s​.
(11)
It remains to show that the second-order term vanishes; the preceding observation alone does not establish this.
Let Z={a:f′(a)=0}. By the occupation-times formula for the local time of a semimartingale,
0=∫R​f′(a)2Lta​(M)da.
(12)
Therefore Lta​(M)=0 for almost every a∈/Z. Also f′′(a)=0 for almost every a∈Z: if f′(a)=0 and f′′(a)=0, continuity of f′′ makes f′ strictly monotone in a neighborhood of a, so that zero is isolated. Such isolated zeros form a countable set. Localizing to bounded paths and finite quadratic variation, then using the occupation-times formula once more, gives
∫0t​∣f′′(Ms​)∣d[M]s​=∫R​∣f′′(a)∣Lta​(M)da=0.
(13)
Both terms in the Itô formula now vanish. Apply this on all rational times and use continuity to obtain the simultaneous conclusion
f(Mt​)=f(0)for every t≥0 almost surely.​
(14)
This is the finite-variation smooth transform of a continuous local martingale property.

There is an initial-value qualification in the printed conclusion. As written, nothing requires A0​=0. For example, M≡0 and A≡1 satisfy the hypotheses, because M2−A≡−1 is a local martingale, but Mt​ is not N(0,1). The correct variance is At​−A0​; the printed formula holds under the usual normalization A0​=0.
Indeed, M2−[M] is a continuous local martingale. Subtracting it from M2−A shows that [M]−A is a continuous local martingale with finite variation, hence is its initial constant −A0​. Thus
Ct​:=At​−A0​=[M]t​.
(15)
In particular, C is deterministic, continuous, nonnegative and increasing, regardless of the apparent allowance of a general finite-variation process in the premise.
For a real parameter θ, the Itô formula gives the complex local martingale
Zt​=exp(iθMt​+2θ2​Ct​),dZt​=iθZt​dMt​.
(16)
On every deterministic interval [0,T], its modulus is at most eθ2CT​/2, so its real and imaginary parts are bounded local martingales, hence true martingales. Taking expectations and using Z0​=1 yields
EeiθMt​=e−θ2Ct​/2.
(17)
By the uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions,
Mt​∼N(0,At​−A0​).​
(18)
A zero variance means the point mass at zero. This proves the requested normalized version by a direct characteristic function calculation.

First stop the solution on leaving [−n,n], at τn​, and set Fn​(t)=Esupu≤t​∣Xu∧τn​​∣2. The stochastic integral representation, the Doob L2 maximal inequality and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality give, for 0≤t≤1,
Fn​(t)​≤2Eu≤tsup​​∫0u∧τn​​σ(Xs​)dBs​​2+2Eu≤tsup​​∫0u∧τn​​b(Xs​)ds​2≤8E∫0t∧τn​​σ(Xs​)2ds+2tE∫0t∧τn​​b(Xs​)2ds≤8A∫0t​(1+Fn​(s))ds.​
(19)
Here stopping ensures square integrability before the calculation, and 8σ2+2tb2≤8(σ2+b2) explains the constant. The Gronwall inequality gives Fn​(t)≤e8At−1. For globally Lipschitz functions as coefficients, the global existence theorem for stochastic differential equations with Lipschitz coefficients supplies a nonexplosive solution. Letting n increase and using Fatou's lemma proves the slightly stronger maximal second-moment bound under linear growth:
Et≤1sup​∣Xt​∣2≤e8A−1≤e8A.​
(20)
For coefficients that are only locally Lipschitz functions, let πn​(x)=max(−n,min(x,n)) and replace the coefficients by σ(πn​(x)) and b(πn​(x)). These are globally Lipschitz functions, agree with the originals on [−n,n], and obey the same linear growth condition for an SDE, since ∣πn​(x)∣≤∣x∣. The globally defined solutions agree until their common exits from [−n,n] by pathwise uniqueness. The uniform preceding estimate and Markov's inequality imply
P(τn​≤1)≤n2e8A−1​⟶0.
(21)
Patch the solutions before their increasing exit times. Their limiting lifetime exceeds 1 almost surely by this bound. Thus a pathwise unique strong solution exists throughout [0,1] even for locally Lipschitz coefficients.

Separate L into a diffusion generator and the constant killing term −1/2. The appropriate stochastic differential equation and its explicit geometric Brownian motion solution are
dXs​=Xs​dBs​+21​Xs​ds,X0​=x,Xs​=xeBs​.​
(22)
The second-order term in the Itô formula cancels the drift of log∣X∣ when x=0; if x=0, the solution stays zero.
Fix a terminal time t. Apply the Itô formula to e−s/2u(t−s,Xs​) for 0≤s≤t. Its drift is
e−s/2(−ut​+2Xs2​​uxx​+2Xs​​ux​−21​u)(t−s,Xs​)ds=0.
(23)
After localization this is a local martingale, and it is bounded because u is bounded. It is therefore a true martingale. Taking expectations at its endpoints gives the Feynman-Kac formula
u(t,x)=e−t/2E[g(xeBt​)].​
(24)
For t>0 and x=0, the change of variable y=xez in the normal density produces the killed geometric Brownian heat kernel with respect to Lebesgue measure:
p(t,x,y)=⎩⎨⎧​∣y∣2πt​e−t/2​exp[−2tlog2(∣y/x∣)​],0,​xy>0,xy≤0.​​
(25)
For xy>0, an equivalent form is
p(t,x,y)=∣x∣2πt​1​exp[−2t(log∣y/x∣+t)2​].
(26)
In particular,
p(t,1,y)=2πt​1​exp[−2t(logy+t)2​](y>0).​
(27)
The apparent missing 1/y in this last expression is absorbed into the completed square, together with the killing factor; it is not a transcription error. The total mass is e−t/2, as expected for killing at rate 1/2. At x=0 the fundamental kernel is instead the measure e−t/2δ0​(dy); it has no Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to Lebesgue measure. Thus u(t,0)=e−t/2g(0). At t=0 the kernel is δx​, and continuity of bounded g gives the initial condition. This also specifies the fundamental solution at the degenerate point omitted by a density-only formula.

Use coordinates 1,2,3 for paper, scissors and stone. Let XN take values in the lattice simplex SN​={(n1​,n2​,n3​)/N:ni​≥0, ∑i​ni​=N}. A uniformly selected unordered pair consists of types i and j, i=j, with probability 2ni​nj​/(N(N−1)). Losing copies the winner, so the possible jumps of the continuous-time Markov chain are
Ne1​−e3​​,Ne2​−e1​​,Ne3​−e2​​.
(28)
Ties make no change. For arbitrary event rate λ, the respective transition rates are 2λNx1​x3​/(N−1), 2λNx2​x1​/(N−1) and 2λNx3​x2​/(N−1). Choose
λN​=2N−1​.​
(29)
Then the rates are exactly Nx1​x3​, Nx2​x1​, Nx3​x2​. The infinitesimal generator of this cyclic imitation chain is
LN​f(x)=N∑ℓ∈J​βℓ​(x)(f(x+ℓ/N)−f(x)),
(30)
where J={e1​−e3​,e2​−e1​,e3​−e2​} and the three β functions are, in that order, x1​x3​,x2​x1​,x3​x2​. Its drift is precisely
F(x)=∑ℓ​ℓβℓ​(x)=(x1​(x3​−x2​), x2​(x1​−x3​), x3​(x2​−x1​)).
(31)
Here is a precise fluid limit with its error control. Suppose X0N​→x0​ in probability, and let x solve x˙=F(x), x(0)=x0​. For every finite T,
0≤t≤Tsup​∥XtN​−xt​∥⟶0in probability.​
(32)
Indeed, the martingale decomposition of a density-dependent jump process gives
XtN​=X0N​+∫0t​F(XsN​)ds+MtN​,⟨MN⟩t​=N1​∫0t​∑ℓ​ℓℓTβℓ​(XsN​)ds.
(33)
This follows by compensating each transition count by its integrated rate; the compensated counts are square-integrable martingales, with variance equal to the expected compensator. Since ∥ℓ∥2=2 and x1​x2​+x2​x3​+x3​x1​≤1/3 on the simplex, the Doob L2 maximal inequality, applied coordinatewise, gives
Esupt≤T​∥MtN​∥2≤4E∥MTN​∥2≤3N8T​.
(34)
The polynomial drift is Lipschitz on the compact simplex, with some finite constant L. It preserves the sum of the coordinates, and each coordinate solves an equation of the form x˙i​=xi​hi​(x), so nonnegativity is preserved. Thus the ordinary differential equation stays in the simplex and exists for all time. The Gronwall inequality gives
supt≤T​∥XtN​−xt​∥≤eLT(∥X0N​−x0​∥+supt≤T​∥MtN​∥),
(35)
which proves the claim by Markov's inequality. This proves the required version directly, rather than invoking an unspecified approximation theorem.
For an interior trajectory, differentiating the logarithm of the product of numbers gives
dtd​log(x1​x2​x3​)=(x3​−x2​)+(x1​−x3​)+(x2​−x1​)=0.
(36)
The same identity for the product itself holds on the boundary, so x1​(t)x2​(t)x3​(t) is conserved. A strictly positive initial product keeps the deterministic trajectory away from the boundary. Except at (1/3,1/3,1/3) these interior trajectories are periodic: the strictly concave function ∑i​logxi​ has smooth compact level curves surrounding its unique maximum, and the vector field is nonzero and tangent to each such curve.
The finite Markov chain behaves differently at long times. For P(x)=x1​x2​x3​, the change in P during the first jump is
P(x+Ne1​−e3​​)−P(x)=x2​(Nx3​−x1​​−N21​).
(37)
The other two terms are cyclic permutations. Their first-order contributions cancel, leaving the exact product decay in a cyclic imitation chain identity
LN​P=−N3​P,EP(XtN​)=e−3t/NEP(X0N​).​
(38)
For N≥3, while all three counts are positive, their product is at least N−2, so P(XtN​)≥(N−2)/N3. A missing type cannot reappear. If τ is the first loss of a type, then
P(τ>t)≤N−2N3​e−3t/NEP(X0N​)⟶0.
(39)
Once only two types remain, one always wins, so its count increases until an absorbing monochromatic vertex is reached, almost surely in finite time. The cases of one or two initially present types follow the same argument without the first stage. Every finite chain eventually becomes monochromatic, whereas an interior deterministic solution retains its positive product. The fluid limit holds on each fixed finite interval; it does not justify exchanging the limits N→∞ and t→∞.
