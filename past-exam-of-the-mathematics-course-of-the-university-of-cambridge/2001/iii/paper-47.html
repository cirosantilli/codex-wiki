Let B=ATA. Since detA>0, B is a positive-definite matrix. The spectral theorem gives a unique principal square root of a positive semidefinite matrix
U=B1/2,R=AU−1.
(1)
Then RTR=U−1BU−1=I and detR=detA/detU=1, so R is a rotation matrix. Define V=RURT. It is a symmetric matrix, is positive definite, and satisfies V2=AAT. Consequently the polar decomposition in continuum mechanics is
A=RU=VR,U=(ATA)1/2,V=(AAT)1/2.​
(2)
Here U and V are the right stretch tensor and left stretch tensor.
For the Cauchy stress tensor, use both material isotropy and material frame indifference. Their respective transformation laws are
σ(AQ)=σ(A),σ(QA)=Qσ(A)QT,Q∈SO(3).
(3)
The first law and A=VR give σ(A)=σ(V). Combining the two laws gives σ(QVQT)=Qσ(V)QT. In a principal stretch basis, V=diag(λ1​,λ2​,λ3​). Each half-turn about a coordinate axis leaves V unchanged; its conjugation changes the signs of the corresponding off-diagonal stress tensor entries. Those entries must therefore vanish. If two principal stretches coincide, rotations within their eigenspace additionally force the stress tensor to be scalar on that eigenspace. Thus the Cauchy stress tensor and left stretch tensor have common principal axes, including at repeated principal stretches. This proves coaxiality of isotropic elastic stress.
The precise symmetry conclusion is equivariance of isotropic principal stresses. Every permutation of the principal stretches can be realized by a signed permutation rotation matrix, so
σi​(λπ(1)​,λπ(2)​,λπ(3)​)=σπ(i)​(λ1​,λ2​,λ3​)
(4)
when the indexing is permuted consistently. Equivalently, permuting the arguments permutes the labelled principal stresses in the same way. In particular, σ1​ is symmetric in λ2​,λ3​, and analogous statements hold for σ2​,σ3​.
For distinct principal stretches, interpolate the three values σi​ at the three points λi​:
σ=β0​I+β1​V+β2​V2,σi​=β0​+β1​λi​+β2​λi2​.
(5)
Uniqueness of this interpolation implies that the scalar coefficients βj​ are symmetric functions of the unordered principal stretches; they may be expressed through the elementary symmetric invariants. Smooth isotropic constitutive laws have the corresponding invariant representation across repeated stretches as well. The wording about symmetric functions must be understood in this collective sense: each labelled principal stress need not be symmetric in all three arguments. For example, the objective isotropic law σ=V2 has σi​=λi2​, which disproves that stronger interpretation.

Generic finite-amplitude quasi-transverse waves may contain longitudinal displacement; arbitrary vector boundary forcing need not generate a single simple wave.

Take the difference w of two solutions with the same body force, initial displacement and initial velocity. It satisfies the homogeneous linear elasticity equations. With e=e(w) the infinitesimal strain tensor, define the bulk energy
Ebulk​(t)=21​∫D​{ρ∣wt​∣2+Cijkl​eij​ekl​}dV.
(26)
The usual stability assumptions make the elastic quadratic form nonnegative and ρ>0. Multiplying momentum balance by wt​, using integration by parts and using symmetry of the stress tensor gives
dtdEbulk​​=∫S​wt​⋅tw​dS,tw​=σ(w)n.
(27)
All interior terms cancel: the strain term is exactly the time derivative of elastic energy.
For the restoring boundary law, tw​=kw. The prescribed support displacement cancels in the difference. Since k is a time-independent symmetric matrix,
∫S​wt​⋅kwdS=21​dtd​∫S​w⋅kwdS.
(28)
Hence the elastic energy uniqueness with restoring boundary springs identity is
dtd​(Ebulk​−21​∫S​w⋅kwdS)=0.​
(29)
The boundary contribution is nonnegative because k is negative semidefinite. The initial difference and its velocity vanish, so the conserved energy is zero. Its kinetic term implies wt​=0 everywhere; the zero initial displacement then gives w=0. This proves uniqueness without needing to exclude rigid displacements separately.
Physically, −k is a nonnegative boundary spring-stiffness matrix, and the prescribed vector U is the moving support position. The traction restores the displacement towards that support; null directions of k have no spring force. The interpretation and conserved-energy proof use a fixed spring matrix, as indicated by the given boundary law.
On the mixed part S1​, write t=σn. The condition is
(I−nnT)t=n×F,u⋅n=N.
(30)
Thus the tangential traction and normal displacement are prescribed. The normal traction is a constraint reaction; tangential displacement is free. Only the tangential component of F matters. On S2​, the entire displacement is prescribed.
For the difference of two such solutions, w⋅n=0 and (I−nnT)tw​=0 on S1​. Consequently wt​ is tangential and tw​ normal, so wt​⋅tw​=0. On S2​, wt​=0. The boundary power vanishes everywhere, giving dEbulk​/dt=0. The same zero-initial-energy argument gives w=0. Therefore the mixed boundary problem also has at most one solution.

Write n=(x−ξ)/R for the receiver direction. With the printed time convention eiωt, an outgoing spherical wave has phase e−iωR/v. The leading terms of the elastodynamic Green tensor are of order 1/(α2R) and 1/(β2R), while the remaining terms have orders 1/(ωvR2) and 1/(ω2R3). Thus the far-field approximation requires
ωR/α≫1,ωR/β≫1.​
(41)
This is an absolute asymptotic comparison of the tensor terms. At an angular node of a leading radiation component, the smaller near field can still dominate that component. The PDF prints an opposite phase sign in the near-field shear exponential; that would be incoming with this time convention. The outgoing leading terms, which determine all the requested far-field patterns, are unambiguous.
For a unit time-harmonic point force in direction d, the far-field P and S radiation from a point force is
uP​=4πα2Re−iωR/α​n(n⋅d),uS​=4πβ2Re−iωR/β​{d−n(n⋅d)}.
(42)
The normalization here follows the given elastodynamic Green tensor; a separate force-density convention would include its corresponding density factor. If θ is the angle between n and d, the signed radial P wave amplitude is cosθ, and the magnitude of the S wave amplitude is sinθ. The P wave has two oppositely signed lobes and a nodal plane perpendicular to d. The S wave is polarized in the projection of d onto the receiver's tangent plane, has nodes along ±d, and has its maximum on the perpendicular great circle. Rotating its planar section about d gives the full toroidal S wave magnitude pattern.
For a point source with seismic moment tensor M, convolution with the derivatives of the Dirac delta function gives ui​=Mjk​∂ξk​​Gij​. In the far field, differentiating the outgoing phase contributes iωnk​/v; differentiating R−1 or n is a lower-order effect. Thus the point-moment elastodynamic displacement is
uP​=4πα3Riωe−iωR/α​n(nTMn),uS​=4πβ3Riωe−iωR/β​(I−nnT)Mn.​
(43)
The double couple has M=e1​e2T​+e2​e1T​. Its double-couple radiation pattern therefore has signed radial P wave factor and tangential S wave vector
AP​=2n1​n2​,AS​=(n2​,n1​,0)−2n1​n2​n,∣AS​∣2=n12​+n22​−4n12​n22​.

In the x1​x2​ plane, n=(cosϕ,sinϕ,0), giving AP​=sin2ϕ and AS​=cos2ϕ, where the signed S wave component is along (−sinϕ,cosϕ,0). The P wave nodal planes are n1​=0 and n2​=0, and the alternating lobes peak on their bisectors. The planar S wave lobes peak along the coordinate axes. For the complete three-dimensional pattern, write n=(sinθcosϕ,sinθsinϕ,cosθ):
AP​=sin2θsin2ϕ,ASV​=sinθcosθsin2ϕ,ASH​=sinθcos2ϕ.
(45)
The full S wave has additional zeros at ±e3​ as well as at the planar bisectors.
For the explosion source, M=I. Then nTMn=1 and (I−nnT)Mn=0. The explosion radiation in an isotropic elastic solid is therefore an isotropic radial P wave, with no S wave radiation. A dilatational point source is a gradient source; its far-field force is parallel to the propagation direction, so the transverse projection vanishes.

The surface radius and surface slowness fix the normalization. Varying μ reconstructs radius and speed parametrically; the centre is recovered by a limit when the data extend that far. The calculation assumes smooth regular turning rays and complete branch data, not merely the first arrival at each epicentral angle.
For a check, a constant speed v0​ gives ηa​=a/v0​, Δ(p)=2arccos(p/ηa​) and T(p)=2a2−p2v02​​/v0​. These satisfy dT/dΔ=p, and the inversion returns r(μ)=v0​μ.
If η decreases outwards on an interval, then it increases as a surface ray travels inward through that interval. Such a ray cannot turn there, since turning requires the decreasing inward value of η to reach p. Rays that penetrate this interval can traverse it and turn deeper, but the map η↦r now has several branches. The angular data constrain combinations of those branch contributions, rather than the single function h(η) used above. This is a hidden turning-ray zone from nonmonotone spherical slowness. The stated single-valued inversion fails and does not uniquely locate the speed profile in that interval without additional information. Missing turning rays and possible multiple travel-time branches are the obstruction, not a failure of the algebraic Abel-kernel identity.
