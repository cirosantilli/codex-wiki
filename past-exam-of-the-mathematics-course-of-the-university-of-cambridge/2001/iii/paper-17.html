Let O=[O(S)op,Set], with categorical presheaf restrictions X(U)→X(V) when V⊆U. The five functors are
ΠX=X(∅),ΓX=X(S),(ΔA)(U)=A,
(12)
(ΛA)(U)={A∅​U=∅,U=∅,​(∇A)(U)={A{∗}​U=S,U=S.​
(13)
In ΔA all restriction maps are identities. In ΛA and ∇A, all nonidentity restrictions are the unique maps permitted by the displayed sets. These definitions are valid also when A is empty: a restriction out of (∇A)(S) still has singleton target. Maps of sets act at the displayed A-components, and natural transformations act on Π and Γ by evaluation.
A natural transformation ΛA→X is determined by an arbitrary map A→X(∅), since all other source components are empty. A natural transformation X→ΔA is determined by its empty-open component X(∅)→A: the component at U must first restrict X(U)→X(∅). Similarly, a natural transformation ΔA→X is determined by A→X(S), followed by the restriction X(S)→X(U). O(ΛA,X)≅Set(A,ΠX),Set(ΠX,A)≅O(X,ΔA),

O(ΔA,X)≅Set(A,ΓX),Set(ΓX,A)≅O(X,∇A).
(15)
Thus the five adjoints for constant presheaves on open sets are
Λ⊣Π⊣Δ⊣Γ⊣∇.​
(16)
The values at the empty open set are genuine presheaf values here; no sheaf condition or sheafification is being imposed.

Let CA=π0​A be the set of connected components of a category: two objects of a category are identified when joined by a finite zigzag of morphisms. Let DA be the discrete category on A, and let IA be the indiscrete category on A, with exactly one morphism for every ordered pair of objects.
A functor A→DA must give the same object value along every arrow, hence along every zigzag. Conversely a function π0​A→A uniquely defines such a functor. A functor DA→A is just a choice of an object of a category for each element of A. Use the preservation obstructions to extending an adjoint chain: a functor having a further left adjoint must preserve categorical limits, while one having a further right adjoint must preserve colimits.
At the left end of the presheaf category chain, Λ does not preserve the terminal object. The terminal categorical presheaf has singleton value everywhere, whereas (Λ1)(S)=∅ because S=∅. Thus Λ cannot itself be a right adjoint. At the right end, ∇ does not preserve the initial object: (∇∅)(∅)={∗} since the empty open set is proper. Thus ∇ cannot itself be a left adjoint.
For the category of small categories chain, take two functors from the terminal category 1 to the two-object indiscrete category I{0,1}, selecting its distinct objects. Their equalizer is the empty category. But their images under C=π0​ are the same function between singleton sets, whose equalizer is a singleton. Hence C does not preserve equalizers and has no further left adjoint. Finally, I1⨿I1 is the two-object discrete category, while I(1⨿1) has arrows between its distinct objects. The canonical comparison is not an isomorphism of categories, so I fails to preserve this coproduct and has no further right adjoint.
Neither chain extends in either direction.

The general adjoint functor theorem says that, for a functor U:C→D with C a complete category and both categories locally small, U has a left adjoint exactly when it preserves small categorical limits and satisfies the solution-set condition. The latter means that for each D∈D there is a set of arrows fi​:D→U(Ci​) such that every f:D→U(C) factors as
f=U(h)fi​for some i and h:Ci​→C.
(58)
Equivalently, (D↓U) has a weakly initial set.
The limit form of the special adjoint functor theorem says that, if in addition C is well-powered and has a small cogenerating family, then U has a left adjoint exactly when it preserves small limits. These are the limit/left-adjoint forms; their duals interchange limits with colimits, left with right adjoints, and cogenerators with generators.
For the first route, let E be a complete category that is locally small and has a weakly initial set (Wi​). Put P=∏i​Wi​. It is weakly initial: for any X, choose an arrow Wi​→X and precompose with the corresponding product projection. The set E(P,P) indexes a simultaneous equalizer
e:E⟶P,ue=efor every u:P→P.
(59)
It exists by completeness, for example as the equalizer of the two maps P⇉∏u:P→P​P with components u and 1P​. Since E→P→X exists for each X, E is also weakly initial.
Choose r:P→E. Applying the defining equation to er:P→P gives ere=e, whence re=1E​ because e is a monomorphism. If v:E→E is any endomorphism, apply it to evr:P→P to obtain evre=e, hence ev=e and v=1E​. Thus E has only its identity endomorphism.
For two arrows a,b:E⇉X, take their equalizer j:Y→E. Weak initiality of E gives t:E→Y. Since jt is an endomorphism of E, jt=1E​. The monic arrow j with this right inverse is invertible; therefore a=b. Existence of an arrow E→X and this uniqueness show that E is initial.
For the general adjoint functor theorem, limit preservation makes each comma category (D↓U) complete, and the solution-set condition makes it weakly initially generated by a set. The lemma provides an initial object (FD,ηD​). For v:D→D′, the universal property uniquely defines Fv:FD→FD′ by
U(Fv)ηD​=ηD′​v.
(60)
Uniqueness gives the functor laws. The same property gives natural bijections
C(FD,C)≅D(D,UC),
(61)
so F⊣U. Conversely a right adjoint preserves limits, by applying its hom-set bijections to cones, and its unit (FD,ηD​) is already an initial object of each comma category, hence a singleton solution set. This proves necessity as well as sufficiency.
For the second route, assume the general adjoint functor theorem and the additional hypotheses of the Special adjoint functor theorem. Let (Qi​)i∈I​ be a small cogenerating family. Fix D∈D and an arrow f:D→UC. A limit-preserving functor preserves monomorphisms, by the self-pullback criterion. Intersect the set of all subobjects B↪C supporting f. Preservation of this categorical limit gives a minimal supporting pair (C0​,f0​:D→UC0​) and a monomorphism m0​:C0​→C with U(m0​)f0​=f.
If g,h:C0​⇉Qi​ have Ugf0​=Uhf0​, their equalizer supports f0​ and must be invertible by minimality. Thus
C(C0​,Qi​)↪D(D,UQi​),h⟼Uhf0​.
(62)
The evaluation map into the product of all these cogenerator targets is monic, because the family coseparates arrows. Its coordinates inject into the fixed set
BD​=∐i∈I​D(D,UQi​).
(63)
For every subset J⊆BD​, form the product PJ​ whose coordinate (i,z) has target Qi​. Each minimal C0​ is isomorphic to a subobject of one such product, using precisely its realized coordinates. There is a set of subsets J; the well-powered category condition gives a set of representative subobjects of each product. For each representative B, include every arrow D→UB, a set by local smallness of D.
This is a solution-set condition: the minimal pair is isomorphic to one of these representatives, and its inclusion into C supplies the required factorization of f. The general adjoint functor theorem now gives the left adjoint. This is the cogenerator bound for comma-category solution sets proof of the special theorem. Necessity follows again because a right adjoint preserves limits. Both alternative proof routes are therefore supplied.

A monad on C consists of an endofunctor T and natural transformations η:1→T, μ:T2→T satisfying the unit and multiplication of a monad laws
μ∘Tη=1T​=μ∘ηT,μ∘Tμ=μ∘μT.​
(64)
An algebra for a monad is an object A equipped with a:TA→A such that
aηA​=1A​,aT(a)=aμA​.
(65)
A morphism of algebras for a monad f:(A,a)→(B,b) is a morphism f:A→B satisfying fa=bT(f). Identities and composition obey this equation, giving the Eilenberg-Moore category CT and its forgetful functor UT(A,a)=A.
For an adjunction F⊣U:D→C with induced monad T=UF, the Eilenberg-Moore comparison functor is
K:D→CT,K(D)=(UD,UεD​),K(h)=Uh.
(66)
The functor U is monadic when it has such a left adjoint and this comparison is an equivalence of categories; a stricter convention requires an isomorphism of categories over C. We will identify which limit-lifting conclusion each convention supports in part (iii).

For a monadic adjunction, the comparison satisfies U=UTK. If K is an isomorphism of categories over the base, the preceding unique lift transports back literally, so U is a limit-creating functor. If monadic means that K is only an equivalence of categories, the precise conclusion is creation of limits up to isomorphism: the lifted algebra is isomorphic to an object K(D), and its limiting cone transports along that isomorphism. Full faithfulness supplies the arrows and their uniqueness.
This distinction concerns prescribed underlying objects, not existence of limits. Literal creation does not follow from an arbitrary equivalence alone. For example, the inclusion of the one-object category into the two-object indiscrete category, choosing one of its objects, is an equivalence of categories and is monadic under the equivalence convention. Either object of the target is terminal, but the other chosen terminal object has no literal object preimage. Thus the strict reading of the requested assertion needs the strict comparison convention; the equivalence-invariant statement is proved above.

A form of Beck's monadicity theorem compatible with equivalence-based monadic adjunctions is this. A functor U:D→C is monadic exactly when it has a left adjoint, is a conservative functor, and D has coequalizers of all pairs whose images under U admit a split coequalizer, with U preserving these coequalizers. In the strict lifting formulation, one states creation of these coequalizers. Such a pair is a functor-split coequalizer pair; the splitting is required in the base category, not necessarily upstairs.
A split coequalizer fork Ar​s​​Bq​Q has maps u:B→A and t:Q→B such that
qr=qs,qt=1Q​,ru=1B​,su=tq.
(77)
Every functor preserves this coequalizer: if hr=hs, then h=hru=hsu=htq, and q is split epic, so the factor through q is unique. The same equations remain true after applying any functor.
For necessity, let UT:CT→C be the monad algebra forgetful functor. It has the free-algebra left adjoint. It reflects isomorphisms, because if an algebra map f is invertible in C, the equation fa=bTf rearranges to f−1b=aT(f−1).
If two algebra maps r,s:(A,a)⇉(B,b) have an underlying split coequalizer q:B→Q, then Tq and T2q are also coequalizers. Since qb equalizes Tr,Ts, there is a unique c:TQ→Q satisfying
cTq=qb.
(78)
After composing with the epimorphism q, the equation cηQ​=1Q​ reduces to the unit law for b. After composing with the epimorphism T2q, the equation cT(c)=cμQ​ reduces to bT(b)=bμB​. Thus (Q,c) is an algebra and q an algebra map. If an algebra map h:(B,b)→(Z,z) equalizes r,s, its unique underlying factor k:Q→Z satisfies kc=zTk: this follows by precomposing with the epimorphism Tq. Therefore this is a coequalizer upstairs as well. This proves that the monad algebra forgetful functor creates split coequalizers. The same properties transport through the comparison equivalence of categories, with the object-lifting convention distinguished in Question 9.
For sufficiency, suppose the three conditions hold, choose F⊣U, and let T=UF with comparison K. For each algebra (X,a), consider the pair
FTXF(a)​εFX​​​FX.
(79)
Its image has coequalizer a:TX→X. It is split: take r=μX​, s=T(a), q=a, u=ηTX​ and t=ηX​. The monad and algebra laws give
aμX​=aT(a),aηX​=1X​,μX​ηTX​=1TX​,T(a)ηTX​=ηX​a.
(80)
By hypothesis the pair has a coequalizer q:FX→DX​ and Uq is also a coequalizer. Identify UDX​ with X using the unique coequalizer isomorphism, so Uq=a. Since q is carried by K to an algebra map from the free algebra, the transported action b:TX→X satisfies
bT(a)=aμX​=aT(a).
(81)
But T(a) is a split epimorphism, with right inverse T(ηX​), so b=a. Hence (X,a)≅K(DX​): the comparison is essentially surjective.
Next, for every D∈D, put aD​=UεD​. The counit of an adjunction coequalizes
FTUDF(aD​)​εFUD​​​FUD.
(82)
This follows from naturality of the counit. The image pair has the split coequalizer aD​, as above. If q:FUD→Q is the hypothesized preserved coequalizer, the induced map h:Q→D has Uh invertible because both Uq and aD​ coequalize the same pair. Conservativity makes h invertible. Therefore εD​ itself is a coequalizer.
Let f:K(D)→K(E) be an algebra map, so faD​=aE​Tf. Set g=εE​F(f):FUD→E. Under the adjunction transpose bijection, the two composites of g with the displayed presentation pair correspond respectively to faD​ and aE​Tf.
