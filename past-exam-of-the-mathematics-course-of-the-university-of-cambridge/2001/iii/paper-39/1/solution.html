For the initial hydrogen-only photon balance, assume an ionization-bounded nebula at steady photoionization equilibrium, negligible dust absorption and ionizing-photon leakage, negligible collisional ionization, and local reabsorption of photons from direct ground-state recombinations. A ground-state recombination then causes another photoionization and produces no net removal of a proton. Net removal is counted by Case B recombination, whose coefficient is β=∑n≥2​αn​. For the hydrogen-helium mixture, define the blackbody ionizing photon function

The three relevant thresholds are approximately 13.6, 24.6 and 54.4eV, for H0, He0 and He+ respectively. Higher stellar temperature increases the relative supply of the harder photons. These cumulative photon rates cannot be assigned independently to the three species: a photon above a helium threshold can instead ionize hydrogen, and helium recombination radiation can also ionize hydrogen. The absorption cross-sections and the diffuse radiation determine the effective allocation.
A useful sharp-front model of nested hydrogen-helium ionization zones has an inner He++ sphere of radius R2​, a He+ shell ending at R1​, and an outer hydrogen-ionized region ending at RH​, with R2​≤R1​≤RH​. Let nH​,nHe​ be uniform nuclear number densities. The electron number densities in these three regions are nH​+2nHe​, nH​+nHe​ and nH​. For constant gas temperature, integrating the recombination rates gives
QHeff​QHeIeff​QHeIIeff​​=34π​βH​nH​{nH​RH3​+nHe​(R13​+R23​)},=34π​βHeI​nHe​(nH​+nHe​)(R13​−R23​),=34π​βHeII​nHe​(nH​+2nHe​)R23​.​
(8)
Here the effective budgets mean net species-specific ionizations after eliminating local ground-state recycling. Their calculation must include photon competition and any interspecies diffuse contribution; the helium coefficients label the recombining ion's emitted spectrum. Once these budgets are specified, solve the third equation for R23​, the second for R13​−R23​, and the first for RH3​.
For an isolated helium front with approximately common electron number density, the useful scaling is
(RH​RHe​​)3≃QHeff​QHeeff​​nHe​βHe​nH​βH​​.
(9)
Thus the small helium abundance partly compensates for the smaller hard-photon supply. A cooler central star produces little He++ and can have a substantially smaller helium-ionized zone. A sufficiently hot star can ionize helium throughout most of the hydrogen region. If independent radius estimates place a helium front beyond the hydrogen front, the independent photon allocations are inconsistent: the fronts and transfer must be treated together. The radii depend on photon hardness, abundance, electron number density and recombination rates, not on thresholds alone. The sharp-front equations are an approximation; they do not assert three exact independently conserved photon budgets for a real mixed nebula.
For a nonuniform nebula, an observed recombination line with effective recombination coefficient αℓeff​ has luminosity

Fνo​,∗​=πBνo​​(Ts​)d2Rs2​​.

hνℓ​Fνo​,∗​Fℓ​​αℓeff​β​=Bνo​​(Ts​)ΦE0​​(Ts​)​.​
(13)
Both Rs​ and the distance d cancel. Hydrogen and helium recombination lines probe different hardness thresholds, so their ratios and the stellar continuum constrain Ts​ and test the assumed spectrum and trapping. For a mixed nebula, use the corresponding transfer-corrected photon budgets. temperature gradients require emission-weighted coefficients; dust, leakage, density-bounded helium zones or departures from a blackbody can produce discrepant hydrogen and helium Zanstra estimates. Line fluxes alone without an appropriate budget or continuum normalization need not uniquely determine Ts​.
Finally, locate the derived Ts​ and luminosity on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram. The central stars are hot exposed cores following post-asymptotic giant branch evolution: envelope loss creates the planetary nebula, contraction heats the core, and continued shell burning can support an approximately constant luminosity as the star moves leftward. Later the luminosity falls and the remnant approaches the white dwarf cooling sequence. Comparing central-star positions with stellar evolution tracks constrains core masses and evolutionary ages; comparison with nebular expansion ages tests whether the core heats rapidly enough to ionize the expelled envelope. The central-star locus traces the transition from an envelope-losing giant to a compact stellar remnant.
