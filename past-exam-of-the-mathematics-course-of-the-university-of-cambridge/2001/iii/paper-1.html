Identify the Lie algebra g with the tangent space Te​G. For X∈g, its left-invariant vector field is XL(g)=(dLg​)e​X. Let γX​ be its integral curve with γX​(0)=e. Uniqueness of integral curves and left invariance give γX​(s+t)=γX​(s)γX​(t) wherever initially defined. Repetition of a local curve extends it to all real times. Thus γX​ is the unique one-parameter subgroup with derivative X at zero, and the Exponential map of a Lie group is defined by
expX=γX​(1),exp(tX)=γX​(t).​
(1)
Smooth dependence of solutions of differential equations on their initial data and parameters makes this map smooth.
The derivative at zero is particularly simple:
(dexp)0​(X)=dtd​​t=0​exp(tX)=X.
(2)
It is the identity linear map from g to Te​G. The inverse function theorem therefore proves that the Exponential map of a Lie group is a local diffeomorphism at zero, producing a local exponential chart. This proves the requested local assertion.
There is a necessary qualification to the other assertion: the image of every one-parameter subgroup lies in the identity component of a Lie group G∘. The correct statement without a connectedness hypothesis is
exp:(g,+)→G is a homomorphism⟺G∘ is abelian.​
(3)
Indeed, if exp is a group homomorphism, its image is an abelian subgroup. It contains an identity neighborhood by the local result. An open subgroup of a connected group is the entire group: every coset is open, so its complement is also open. Thus exp(g)=G∘, proving that this component is abelian.
Conversely, if G∘ is abelian, then exp(tX)exp(tY) is a one-parameter subgroup: its group law follows by commuting the factors. Its derivative at zero is X+Y. Uniqueness gives exp(t(X+Y))=exp(tX)exp(tY) and, at t=1, additivity. This proves the additivity of the Lie exponential map criterion. For connected G it is exactly the stated equivalence with an abelian group. For disconnected G the unrestricted claim is false: S3​×R is a nonabelian Lie group, but its exponential is the homomorphism x↦(e,x). The original PDF does not impose connectedness in that sentence, so that qualification cannot be omitted.
Now suppose G is connected and abelian, of dimension n. The exponential is a surjective group homomorphism, and its kernel Λ is discrete by local injectivity at zero. The induced map
Rn/Λ⟶G
(4)
is a bijective Lie group homomorphism and a local diffeomorphism, hence a Lie group isomorphism. To identify the quotient, take linearly independent elements λ1​,…,λb​∈Λ spanning the real linear span of Λ, and let Λ0​=∑i​Zλi​. Every coset of Λ0​ in Λ has a representative in the bounded fundamental parallelepiped of these vectors. A discrete additive subgroup has finite intersection with a compact set: otherwise differences of arbitrarily close elements would approach zero, contradicting discreteness at zero. Thus Λ/Λ0​ is finite.
It follows that Λ is a finitely generated torsion-free abelian group of rank b, hence has a Z-basis of b elements. These basis elements also span its b-dimensional real span and are linearly independent over R. Extending them to a real basis identifies Λ with Zb×{0}n−b. Consequently the classification of connected abelian Lie groups is
G≅Rn−b×(R/Z)b=Ra×Tb,a+b=n.​
(5)
The compact circle factors record the periods of the one-parameter subgroups.

The Lie algebra of SL2R is the space of traceless real two-by-two matrices. For A in this space, the Cayley-Hamilton theorem gives A2=cI, where c=−detA. Expanding the matrix exponential, if c=s2>0 then
expA=(coshs)I+ssinhs​A,tr(expA)=2coshs≥2.
(6)
If c=0, then expA=I+A and its trace is 2. If c=−s2<0, then
expA=(coss)I+ssins​A,tr(expA)=2coss∈[−2,2].
(7)
In every case tr(expA)≥−2. But
g=(−20​0−1/2​)
(8)
has determinant one and trace −5/2. Thus g∈SL2​(R) is not an exponential, and the exponential map is not surjective.

Put U=ϕ(A(t)). Applying the group homomorphism ϕ to the identity just proved shows that U is conjugate to Um2 for every positive integer m. Thus the multiset of its eigenvalues is unchanged by taking their m2th powers.
Take m=2. The fourth-power operation permutes this finite multiset. For each eigenvalue z, iterating its permutation cycle gives z4r=z for some positive integer r. Since U is unitary, z=0, so z4r−1=1: every eigenvalue is a root of unity. Choose a positive integer d divisible by all their orders. Every eigenvalue of Ud2 is then one. Because its eigenvalue multiset is also the original one, we obtain
every eigenvalue of ϕ(A(t)) equals 1.​
(11)
This is square-power rigidity of a finite nonzero spectrum. Finiteness of the dimension is essential to the permutation-cycle argument.

Every unitary matrix is unitarily diagonalizable. Part (ii) therefore proves the stronger matrix statement ϕ(A(t))=I, not merely a statement about its eigenvalues. The kernel of a group homomorphism is a normal subgroup, and it contains every upper unipotent. By part (iii) their normal closure is the entire group. Thus
ϕ(g)=Ifor every g∈SL2​(R).​

This proves the triviality of finite-dimensional unitary representations of SL2R. In fact the argument used only the algebraic homomorphism property and finite-dimensional unitarity; it did not need continuity.

For a compact Lie group G, the complex representation ring of a compact group R(G) is the Grothendieck group of finite-dimensional continuous complex group representations, with the relations [M⊕N]=[M]+[N]. Multiplication is [M][N]=[M⊗N], and the unit is the trivial one-dimensional representation. The ring of class functions is
cℓ(G)={f∈C(G,C):f(hgh−1)=f(g) for all g,h∈G},
(17)
with pointwise addition and multiplication. The character of M is χM​(g)=trρM​(g). The trace identities for direct sums and tensor products make
[M]−[N]⟼χM​−χN​
(18)
a well-defined ring homomorphism χ:R(G)→cℓ(G).
Normalize Haar measure by ∫G​dg=1. Averaging a positive definite Hermitian inner product gives
(v,w)G​=∫G​(ρ(g)v,ρ(g)w)0​dg,
(19)
which is positive definite and G-invariant. This proves unitarization of a compact-group representation. In a unitary representation the orthogonal complement of an invariant subspace is invariant. Induction on dimension therefore gives complete reducibility of compact-group representations. Hence every element of R(G) is a finite integral linear combination of irreducible classes.
We establish the needed character orthogonality for compact groups carefully. For irreducible unitary representations V,W, let G act on HomC​(W,V) by
g⋅A=ρV​(g)AρW​(g)−1.
(20)
Its average P=∫G​(g⋅)dg is a projection onto HomG​(W,V): averaging makes every image invariant, and it fixes every intertwiner. The trace of this action is χV​(g)χW​(g)​, since the inverse of a unitary matrix has conjugate trace. Taking the trace of the projection gives
∫G​χV​(g)χW​(g)​dg=dimHomG​(W,V).
(21)
For completeness, Schur's lemma follows here from invariance of the kernel and image of an intertwiner. A nonzero intertwiner between irreducibles is an isomorphism. An endomorphism of an irreducible complex representation has an eigenvalue λ; the noninvertible intertwiner A−λI must vanish. Thus the last dimension is one for isomorphic irreducibles and zero for inequivalent ones.
If a virtual character ∑V​nV​χV​ vanishes, integrating it against χW​​ gives nW​=0 for every irreducible W. The irreducible multiplicities therefore distinguish the classes, and
χ:R(G)⟶cℓ(G) is injective.​
(22)
This also proves that two finite-dimensional representations with the same character are isomorphic.
For SU(2), the irreducibles are Vk​=Symk(C2), k≥0, of dimension k+1. One can see this classification through the usual sl2​ operators H,E,F: a highest-weight vector v has Hv=kv, Ev=0, and the relations [H,E]=2E, [H,F]=−2F, [E,F]=H give, by induction,
EFjv=j(k−j+1)Fj−1v.
(23)
The torus weights are integers. If Frv=0 but Fr+1v=0, the identity at j=r+1 forces k=r≥0. The vectors v,Fv,…,Fkv span an invariant irreducible module, which in an irreducible representation is the whole space. This is the symmetric-power module. Conversely its successive monomial weight vectors are linked by E,F with nonzero coefficients, proving its irreducibility. Thus the classification of finite-dimensional representations of SU2 gives every irreducible, rather than just a list of examples.
On the maximal torus T={diag(z,z−1):∣z∣=1}, their characters are
χk​(z)=zk+zk−2+⋯+z−k.
(24)
With x=χ1​=z+z−1, multiplication gives χk+1​=xχk​−χk−1​, starting with χ0​=1. Hence each χk​ is a monic integral polynomial of degree k in x. For example χ2​=x2−1 and χ3​=x3−2x. They form a basis over Z, so
R(SU(2))≅Z[x],x=[C2].​
(25)
The character map realizes this ring as the finite integral polynomials in z+z−1, interpreted as continuous class functions. Its image is not the whole infinite-dimensional ring of continuous class functions; injectivity is the assertion being proved.

We prove uniform approximation, not just approximation in L2. The analytic facts used are: a continuous square-integrable kernel gives a compact integral operator on L2; the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators decomposes the closure of its range into its finite-dimensional nonzero eigenspaces; and continuous functions are dense in L2 for normalized Haar measure on a compact Lie group. Normalized Haar measure on a compact group is both left- and right-invariant and is preserved by inversion.
Choose a continuous nonnegative function k of integral one, supported in a sufficiently small symmetric neighborhood of the identity, with k(g−1)=k(g). Such functions are obtained from a local bump and its inverted bump followed by normalization. Define the convolution operator
(Th)(x)=∫G​h(y)k(y−1x)dy.
(26)
Its kernel is continuous on G×G, so T is compact. The symmetry of k makes it self-adjoint. It commutes with the left translations (Lg​h)(x)=h(g−1x) by a change of variable y=gz.
By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and invariance of Haar measure,
∥Th∥∞​≤∥k∥2​∥h∥2​.
(27)
The same integral formula and continuity of the kernel show Th is continuous for every h∈L2(G). Moreover, rewriting y=xz−1 gives (Tf)(x)=∫f(xz−1)k(z)dz. Uniform continuity on the compact group therefore makes ∥Tf−f∥∞​ arbitrarily small when the support of k is small. Also ∥T∥∞→∞​≤1 because k≥0 and has integral one.
Fix ε>0 and take k so ∥Tf−f∥∞​<ε/3. Then
∥T2f−f∥∞​≤∥T(Tf−f)∥∞​+∥Tf−f∥∞​<2ε/3.
(28)
Let PN​ be the orthogonal projection onto increasing finite sums of the nonzero eigenspaces of T. Since Tf lies in the closure of the range, the spectral theorem gives PN​Tf→Tf in L2. Applying the smoothing estimate gives
∥TPN​Tf−T2f∥∞​≤∥k∥2​∥PN​Tf−Tf∥2​⟶0.
(29)
Thus some h=TPN​Tf satisfies ∥h−f∥∞​<ε.
Each nonzero eigenspace is finite dimensional, invariant under Lg​, and consists of continuous functions: if Tv=λv with λ=0, then v=Tv/λ. The finite sum M containing h therefore carries the finite-dimensional unitary representation ρ(g)=Lg​∣M​. It is continuous, since translating each of its finitely many continuous basis functions depends continuously on g in the uniform and hence the L2 norm.
Let ℓ be evaluation at the identity on M, and let the dual representation on M∗ be θ(g)ℓ=ℓ∘ρ(g−1). For the linear functional Lh​ on M∗ defined by Lh​(u)=u(h),
Lh​(θ(g)ℓ)=ℓ(ρ(g−1)h)=h(g).
(30)
The rank-one endomorphism α=ℓ⊗Lh​ of M∗ satisfies tr(αθ(g))=Lh​(θ(g)ℓ). Therefore
∥f−tr(αθ(⋅))∥∞​<ε.​
(31)
This is the requested Peter-Weyl theorem, proved via the convolution proof of uniform Peter-Weyl approximation. The extra convolution after the spectral truncation is what upgrades the L2 estimate to a uniform one.
To deduce a faithful finite-dimensional representation, first note that these representations separate points. For g=e, choose a continuous function taking different values at g and e, and approximate it closely enough by a trace coefficient to retain that difference. The representation appearing in that trace must satisfy θ(g)=I.
There is an identity neighborhood U containing no nontrivial subgroup. Here is the Lie groups have no small subgroups argument: choose an injective exponential chart on a Lie-algebra ball of radius r, and take U=exp(Bδ​) with 0<δ<r/2. If a subgroup contained a nonidentity expX in U, choose the first integer m with m∥X∥≥δ. Then δ≤m∥X∥<2δ<r, so its power exp(mX) lies outside U, a contradiction. For a zero-dimensional group, take U={e}.
For every g∈/U, choose a representation nontrivial at g. The sets on which these representations are not the identity are open and cover the compact set G∖U. A finite subcover gives finitely many representations, whose direct sum has kernel contained in U. A subgroup contained in U is trivial, so this direct sum is faithful. Averaging its Hermitian form makes it unitary. Thus the finite faithful representation from point-separating representations yields
G↪Un​ for some finite n.​
(32)
Using the no-small-subgroups neighborhood is essential; G∖{e} alone need not be compact, so a finite-subcover argument on that punctured set would not be valid.

A maximal torus in a compact connected Lie group is a compact connected abelian Lie subgroup maximal among such subgroups. By the connected abelian classification it is isomorphic to a product of circles. Such subgroups exist: choose one of largest dimension among compact connected abelian subgroups; a proper inclusion of connected Lie subgroups would increase dimension. Write t for its Lie algebra.
Here are the main steps of the conjugacy assertion, with the critical arguments supplied. Average an inner product on g over the adjoint action to obtain an Ad(G)-invariant positive definite inner product. It defines a bi-invariant Riemannian metric. Compactness makes this metric complete, and the Hopf-Rinow theorem joins the identity to any g by a geodesic. For a bi-invariant metric the Levi-Civita connection on left-invariant fields is ∇X​Y=[X,Y]/2, so the geodesic starting with X is exp(tX). Consequently every g has the form expX.
The Lie-algebra centralizer of t is exactly t. If X commutes with t, its one-parameter subgroup commutes with T. The closure of the subgroup generated by T and exp(RX) is compact, connected and abelian, so it is a torus; maximality forces it to equal T, giving X∈t. The torus action on gC​ decomposes into finitely many character weight spaces. The zero-weight space is tC​. Choose H∈t outside the finitely many kernels of the nonzero weight differentials. Then the centralizer of H is exactly t.
For an arbitrary X∈g, the continuous function g↦⟨Adg​X,H⟩ attains a maximum. Put Y=Adg0​​X at a maximizer. Differentiating along exp(sZ)g0​ gives
0=⟨[Z,Y],H⟩=⟨Z,[Y,H]⟩for every Z∈g.
(33)
Hence [Y,H]=0, so Y∈t. Exponentiating gives g0​(expX)g0−1​=expY∈T. This adjoint-orbit criterion for maximal-torus conjugacy proves
every element of G belongs to a subgroup conjugate to T.​
(34)
Restriction of representations is a ring homomorphism Res:R(G)→R(T). If a virtual representation restricts to zero, its character vanishes on T. Since characters are class functions and every element is conjugate into T, it vanishes on all of G. The injectivity of the character map proved in Question 3 then gives
Res:R(G)⟶R(T) is injective.​
(35)
The Weyl group is W=NG​(T)/T. For n∈NG​(T), the operator ρ(n) intertwines the restriction of ρ with its twist by t↦ntn−1. Therefore every restricted representation, and every virtual difference of them, is fixed by W:
Res(R(G))⊆R(T)W.​
(36)
For the special orthogonal group G=SO(2m+1), take
T={diag(Rθ1​​,…,Rθm​​,1)},
(37)
where Rθ​ is a real planar rotation. It is a maximal torus: its Lie-algebra centralizer consists of the independent infinitesimal rotations in those planes. Its character lattice is Zm, so, writing zi​=eiθi​,
R(T)=Z[z1±1​,…,zm±1​].
(38)
Indeed commuting unitary torus matrices diagonalize simultaneously into one-dimensional characters, and the characters of the circle are exactly the integral powers.
The normalizer permutes the real rotation planes and can reverse each angle independently. Permutations of whole two-dimensional blocks have determinant one. A reflection in one rotation plane reverses its angle and has determinant minus one; combining it with reflection of the remaining fixed line gives an element of SO(2m+1). Thus all independent inversions occur. Conversely a normalizer must permute the distinct nontrivial torus character pairs {±ei​} and preserve the fixed line, so there are no additional actions. The kernel of this action is exactly T, giving
W≅(Z/2)m⋊Sm​,zi​↦zσ(i)±1​.​
(39)
It follows that the restriction identifies R(SO(2m+1)) with a subring of the signed-permutation-invariant Laurent polynomials. In this case we can prove equality. Invariance under individual inversions first gives
Z[z1±1​,…,zm±1​](Z/2)m=Z[x1​,…,xm​],xi​=zi​+zi−1​.
(40)
For one variable, every invariant is an integral combination of 1 and zk+z−k, and these satisfy the recurrence sk+1​=xsk​−sk−1​. Applying this one coordinate at a time proves the assertion over the other Laurent coefficient rings. Permutation invariance and the Fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials then give
R(T)W=Z[e1​(x),…,em​(x)],
(41)
where ek​ are the elementary symmetric polynomials.
Let V be the complexification of the defining (2m+1)-dimensional real representation. Its torus weights are 1,zi​,zi−1​. The characters of its exterior powers obey
∑k=02m+1​χΛkV​tk=(1+t)∏i=1m​(1+zi​t)(1+zi−1​t)=(1+t)∏i=1m​(1+xi​t+t2).
(42)
For k≤m, choosing j linear terms and r quadratic terms shows that the coefficient ck​=χΛkV​ is
ck​=∑j+2r=k​(rm−j​)ej​+∑j+2r=k−1​(rm−j​)ej​,e0​=1.
(43)
Its leading term is ek​ with coefficient one; all other terms use ej​ with j<k. By induction ek​ lies in the restriction image, because ck​ is the character of a genuine representation and all smaller ej​ already lie in the image. Hence every invariant polynomial lies there, proving the representation ring of an odd-dimensional special orthogonal group description
R(SO(2m+1))≅R(T)W=Z[e1​,…,em​]=Z[c1​,…,cm​].​
(44)
The triangular change of generators is integral, so [Λ1V],…,[ΛmV] are algebraically independent polynomial generators. For m=1, c1​=1+z+z−1 and this reduces to R(SO(3))=Z[V3​], with the three-dimensional defining representation as generator. These are genuine representations of the orthogonal group, not the half-integral spin weights of its simply connected double cover.
