Use [g] for the basis element of the group algebra A=Fp​[Zp​], so [g][h]=[g+h] and [0]=1. The kernel of the augmentation map is the augmentation ideal I. This algebra must not initially be identified with a Laurent polynomial ring: the additive group Zp​ is not abstractly generated by the integer 1.
For r≥1, map A onto Fp​[Zp​/prZp​] and call its kernel Jr​. It is generated by [g]−1 for g∈prZp​: differences of basis elements in one coset have the form [h]([g]−1). Since each such g=prh,
[g]−1=([h]−1)pr∈Ipr
(25)
by the characteristic-p binomial identity. Thus Jr​⊆Ipr.
The quotient group is a cyclic group of order pr, generated by the image of 1. Its group algebra is
Fp​[u]/(upr−1)=Fp​[T]/(Tpr),T=u−1.
(26)
The image of I is (T) and its prth power vanishes, proving the reverse inclusion. Hence
Jr​=Ipr,A/Ipr≅Fp​[T]/(Tpr)​.
(27)
This is cyclic finite quotients of the p-adic augmentation filtration.
For any n≥0, choose pr>n+1. The finite quotient then identifies In/In+1 with (Tn)/(Tn+1), a one-dimensional space generated by the image of ([1]−1)n. The degree-one symbol X of [1]−1 consequently gives a surjective graded map Fp​[X]→grI​A, and no power of X vanishes in its degree. Thus
grI​Fp​[Zp​]≅Fp​[X]​.
(28)
The same finite quotients show that the I-adic completion is Fp​[[T]], and its associated graded ring is identical. The proof therefore covers the completed group algebra interpretation too, while proving the literal ordinary-algebra statement without assuming completion. Applying part ii to a ring would require its actual completeness, not just this polynomial associated graded ring.
