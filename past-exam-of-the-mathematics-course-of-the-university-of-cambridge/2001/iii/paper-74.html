A Dedekind domain is a Noetherian integrally closed domain in which every nonzero prime ideal is maximal. The equivalent local characterization is that its localizations at nonzero prime ideals are discrete valuation rings. A field is harmless as the dimension-zero case.
Write R for the domain, F for its fraction field and I for the nonzero fractional ideal. Its proposed inverse is an R-submodule of F. Choose 0=d∈R with dI⊆R, so d∈I−1 and the inverse is nonzero. Choose also 0=a∈I. Every x∈I−1 satisfies xa∈R, hence I−1⊆a−1R. Writing a=r/s with r,s∈R gives rI−1⊆sR⊆R. Thus I−1 is a fractional ideal; the Noetherian hypothesis also makes it finitely generated.
For a nonzero maximal prime ideal p, the discrete valuation ring Rp​ makes Ip​ principal, say Ip​=ap​Rp​. We need to justify that inversion commutes with this localization. One inclusion is immediate. Conversely, if xIp​⊆Rp​, finite generation of I supplies a common s∈/p such that sxI⊆R. Therefore sx∈I−1, proving
(I−1)p​=(Ip​)−1=ap−1​Rp​.
(1)
It follows that (II−1)p​=Rp​ at every such prime ideal. Globally II−1⊆R. If this were a proper ideal, it would be contained in a maximal ideal and would remain proper after localizing there, a contradiction. In the field case every nonzero fractional ideal is the whole field. Hence in all cases
II−1=R.​
(2)
This proves that every nonzero fractional ideal of a Dedekind domain is an invertible fractional ideal, rather than presupposing its inverse exists.

For pairwise comaximal ideals I1​,…,Ir​ of R, reduction gives the Chinese remainder theorem for ideals:
R/(I1​⋯Ir​)≅i=1∏r​R/Ii​.​
(3)
Its kernel is ⋂i​Ii​. For two comaximal ideals, write 1=u+v with u∈I, v∈J. If x∈I∩J, then x=xu+xv∈IJ, showing I∩J=IJ. Induction gives the product formula: a product of ideals individually comaximal with J is still comaximal with J, by multiplying identities modulo J.
Put Ji​=∏j=i​Ij​. Since Ii​+Ji​=R, choose ei​∈Ji​ with ei​≡1(modIi​). The element ∑i​ai​ei​ has any prescribed residues ai​(modIi​), proving surjectivity. Its residue class modulo the product is unique because we already identified the kernel. In a Dedekind domain, powers of distinct nonzero prime ideals are pairwise comaximal, giving the prime-power form. The theorem requires comaximality; it is not a statement about arbitrary lists of ideals.

Let G=Gal(K/k). Each field automorphism preserves the ring of integers and the base prime ideal, so it permutes the prime ideals above p. Suppose there were more than one orbit, and fix one orbit C. By the Chinese remainder theorem for ideals, choose a∈OK​ such that
a≡0(modP)(P∈C),a≡1(modQ)(Q∈/C, Q∣p).
(4)
The field norm NK/k​(a)=∏σ∈G​σ(a) lies in Ok​. It lies in every prime ideal of C, since for a fixed member each conjugate of a is zero modulo that member. Thus its contraction lies in p. On the other hand, for Q outside C, every σ−1(Q) is also outside it, so every σ(a) equals one modulo Q. The product is consequently one modulo Q. This contradicts its membership in pOK​⊆Q. Therefore the Galois action on the primes above p is transitive. The normal extension of number fields is separable, so its conjugates are exactly the elements of G used in the product.

Let R=Ok​, S=OK​ and n=[K:k]. For each prime ideal P of S above p of R, set fP​=[S/P:R/p]. The relative norm of a fractional ideal can be specified on the unique prime ideal factorization by
NK/k​(∏P​PaP​)=∏p​p∑P∣p​fP​aP​,aP​∈Z.
(5)
Here is why this definition is the intrinsic relative ideal norm from norms of elements, and why the residue weights occur. Localize at p. The finite torsion-free Rp​-module Sp​ is free of rank n, because Rp​ is a discrete valuation ring. Multiplication by a nonzero integral a has determinant NK/k​(a). The Smith normal form gives
vp​(NK/k​(a))=lengthRp​​(Sp​/aSp​).
(6)
Decompose the quotient at the prime ideals above p. In SP​ each quotient Pj/Pj+1 is one-dimensional over S/P, so has length fP​ over Rp​. Therefore
vp​(NK/k​(a))=∑P∣p​fP​vP​(a).
(7)
For an integral ideal I, all norms of elements of I have valuations at least ∑fP​vP​(I). These minima can be attained simultaneously at the finitely many prime ideals above a fixed p. In fact the Chinese remainder theorem for ideals, applied inside the finitely generated module I, chooses an a∈I with nonzero image in each I/PI. Equivalently vP​(a)=vP​(I) at all these primes. The generated ideal of the element norms consequently has exactly the displayed valuations. Clearing a denominator extends this description to fractional ideals.
Multiplication of fractional ideals adds their prime valuations. The displayed formula therefore proves
NK/k​(IJ)=NK/k​(I)NK/k​(J).​
(8)
It also gives NK/k​((a))=(NK/k​(a)).
Finally, reduction of the free rank-n module Sp​ modulo p has dimension n over R/p. The factorization pS=∏i​Piei​​ and the Chinese remainder theorem for ideals identify this reduction with the product of the quotients by Piei​​. Their filtrations have ei​ successive quotients of dimension fi​ over R/p. Adding dimensions proves the fundamental identity for prime decomposition:
[K:k]=i∑​ei​fi​.​
(9)

Use R=Okp​​, the valuation ring of the completion of a number field at a prime ideal, with uniformizer π and residue field κ. This completeness is essential for coprime-factor Hensel lifting. Normalize the two residue factors to be monic first; if the original factors have reciprocal leading units, undo the normalization by multiplying the eventual factors by reciprocal lifts of those units.
Let the two monic residue degrees be d1​,d2​. Start with monic lifts g1​,h1​ satisfying f≡g1​h1​(modπ). Suppose monic lifts gr​,hr​ of these degrees satisfy f−gr​hr​∈πrR[X]. Put Er​=(f−gr​hr​)/πr, of degree less than d1​+d2​. Over κ, the linear map
κ[X]<d1​​⊕κ[X]<d2​​⟶κ[X]<d1​+d2​​,(u,v)⟼uϕ2​+vϕ1​
(10)
is injective: a zero image makes ϕ1​ divide u, by coprimality, forcing u=0 by its degree bound, and then v=0. The dimensions agree, so this map is a bijection. Choose lifts ur​,vr​ whose image is Er​, and set
gr+1​=gr​+πrur​,hr+1​=hr​+πrvr​.
(11)
Their product agrees with f modulo πr+1; the new cross term has factor π2r and 2r≥r+1. The degree bounds preserve monicity. Each coefficient is a Cauchy sequence in the complete discrete valuation ring R, so the limits f1​,f2​ satisfy
f=f1​f2​,f​1​=ϕ1​,f​2​=ϕ2​.​
(12)
If op​ were interpreted only as an ordinary localization rather than the completed valuation ring, the assertion would be false. For example X2−2 splits modulo seven but is irreducible over Q. The completion notation in the next part provides the intended local-field interpretation.

Set L=KP​ and F=kp​, with valuation rings S,R. Let e be the ramification index, f the residue-field degree, and p the residue characteristic. A base uniformizer π generates Pe in S, up to a unit. The free R-module S has rank ef. For an integral a∈S, its field trace is the matrix trace of multiplication by a on this module. Reduction modulo π preserves the matrix trace.
The quotient S/πS has a filtration
S/Pe⊃P/Pe⊃⋯⊃Pe−1/Pe⊃0.
(13)
Each of the e successive quotients is a one-dimensional vector space over κL​=S/P. On each, multiplication by a acts as multiplication by its residue aˉ, whose trace over κF​=R/πR is TrκL​/κF​​(aˉ). A basis adapted to the filtration makes the multiplication matrix block triangular, so its trace is the sum of these quotient traces. This proves trace reduction in a ramified local extension:
TrL/F​(a)​=eTrκL​/κF​​(aˉ).
(14)
Since the hypothesis says p∣e, the right side is zero in κF​. Thus
TrKP​/kp​​(a)∈pOkp​​.​
(15)
No normality hypothesis is needed. The original PDF only requires divisibility of e, not divisibility exactly once; the converted TeX has corrupted that condition and several local subscripts.

For R=Ok​ and S=OK​, define the inverse different, or codifferent, by the trace-dual lattice
DK/k−1​={x∈K:TrK/k​(xS)⊆R}.
(16)
The trace pairing is nondegenerate in this separable field extension, so this is a full fractional ideal of S. The relative different ideal is its inverse:
DK/k​=(DK/k−1​)−1.​
(17)
Every integral x has integral traces against S, so S⊆D−1 and D⊆S.
For k=Q, take an integral basis b1​,…,bn​ and its trace pairing matrix T=(Tr(bi​bj​)). In these coordinates the codifferent is T−1Zn, and
[D−1:S]=∣detT∣=∣dK​∣.
(18)
The ideal norm of D equals this index, by inversion of fractional ideal norms. Therefore the norm of the different ideal relation is
NK/Q​(DK/Q​)=∣dK​∣,dK​=(−1)r2​NK/Q​(DK/Q​).​
(19)
Here r2​ is the number of conjugate pairs of complex embeddings. The different is an ideal of the upper field, whereas the relative discriminant ideal is its ideal norm in the base field; over Q the ordinary signed field discriminant also records the signature.

For number fields, a prime ideal divides the different ideal exactly when it is ramified. More precisely, the local different exponent and tame ramification relation is dP​≥eP​−1, with equality for tame ramification; if e=1, the finite residue extension is separable and the exponent is zero. One can see the ramification criterion directly from the trace pairing modulo the base prime: when e>1 its quotient ring has a nonzero nilpotent ideal, whose elements pair to zero since multiplication by a nilpotent has trace zero. When e=1 the quotient is a product of finite separable fields and its trace pairing is nondegenerate. Its determinant is a unit exactly in the second case.
Let M=K1​∩K2​. Any rational prime ramifying in M must ramify in each Ki​, because ramification indices multiply in a tower. It would then divide both field discriminants, contrary to their coprimality. Thus M is unramified at every finite prime, so ∣dM​∣=1. The Minkowski lower bound for a number-field discriminant excludes this for a field of degree n>1:
∣dM​∣​≥(4π​)r2​n!nn​>1.
(23)
The last inequality follows already for the weakest signature bound r2​≤n/2: the expression starts above one at n=2 and increases with n. Consequently M=Q.
For completeness, normality converts this intersection statement into the desired degree statement. Put E=K1​K2​. Restriction injects Gal(E/K2​) into Gal(K1​/Q). The fixed field of its image in K1​ is exactly K1​∩K2​, because E/K2​ is Galois. The Galois correspondence therefore gives [E:K2​]=[K1​:M]=[K1​:Q]. This proves coprime discriminants imply linear disjointness:
[K1​K2​:Q]=[K1​:Q][K2​:Q].​
(24)

For a number field k, its ordinary Hilbert class field Hk​ is the maximal finite abelian extension unramified at every finite prime and with every real place splitting completely. The Artin reciprocity map induces
Cl(k)≅Gal(Hk​/k),[Hk​:k]=hk​.
(25)
The image of an unramified prime ideal is its Frobenius automorphism. Thus a prime splits completely in Hk​ precisely when its ideal class is trivial. This field packages ideal-class obstructions into splitting behavior. Ideals become principal in Hk​ by the principal ideal theorem. Specifying the real-place convention matters for real fields: omitting it leads instead to the narrow class-field problem. Both illustrative fields below are imaginary quadratic, so there are no real places to discuss.
First consider k=Q(−23​), whose quadratic discriminant is −23. The correspondence between ideal classes and reduced primitive positive definite binary quadratic forms lets us compute its class number without guessing a class field. A reduced form (a,b,c) obeys ∣b∣≤a≤c, b2−4ac=−23, and a≤23/3​<3; a boundary case uses b≥0. For a=1 the only class is (1,1,6); for a=2 the two possibilities are (2,1,3) and (2,−1,3). Hence hk​=3 and its ideal class group is cyclic of order three.
Take g(X)=X3−X−1. Its only possible rational roots are ±1, neither a root, so it is irreducible. Its polynomial discriminant is 4−27=−23, which is nonsquare. Thus its splitting field H has Galois group S3​ and the quadratic fixed field of A3​ is k, generated by the square root of its discriminant. Therefore H/k is cyclic of degree three and H=k(θ) for a root θ of g.
At a prime other than 23, the separable residue polynomial splits over a finite residue-field extension; Hensel's lemma puts all its roots in an unramified local extension. At 23 there is the exact factorization
g(X)≡(X−10)2(X−3)(mod23).
(26)
The simple factor lifts by Hensel's lemma. The remaining quadratic factor has discriminant valuation one, because the cubic discriminant is −23 and the resultant with the simple factor is a unit. Its local splitting field is ramified quadratic, with inertia group of order two. Its intersection with A3​ is trivial, so the relative extension H/k is unramified at 23 as well. There are no real places. An unramified abelian extension of degree hk​=3 must be all of Hk​, giving
HQ(−23​)​=Q(−23​,θ),θ3−θ−1=0.​
(27)
This is the Hilbert class field of Q of square root minus twenty-three.
The other alternative, k=Q(−30​), has quadratic discriminant −120. Reduced forms have a≤40​<7 and even b. Testing 1≤a≤6, ∣b∣≤a, and c=(b2+120)/(4a)≥a gives exactly
(1,0,30),(2,0,15),(3,0,10),(5,0,6).
(28)
Thus hk​=4. Each class is its own inverse, because replacing b by −b leaves these representatives unchanged; consequently the ideal class group is C2​×C2​.
Set H=Q(2​,−3​,5​). The square classes 2,−3,5 are independent, so [H:Q]=8. Their product gives −30​, and H/k is abelian of degree four. Its seven quadratic subfields have discriminants
8,−3,5,−24,40,−15,−120.
(29)
For a multiquadratic abelian extension, the conductor-discriminant formula takes the product of these quadratic character conductors, namely the absolute quadratic discriminants. Hence ∣dH​∣=1204. The relative discriminant tower formula gives
1204=∣dk​∣4Nk/Q​(dH/k​)=1204Nk/Q​(dH/k​).
(30)
The integral relative discriminant ideal therefore has norm one and is the unit ideal. Thus H/k is everywhere unramified, and its degree equals the class number. This proves the other explicit answer:
HQ(−30​)​=Q(2​,−3​,5​).​
(31)
It agrees with the Hilbert class field of Q of square root minus thirty. Both alternatives have been computed independently from their class numbers and ramification.

The original PDF has cube roots throughout this question; the ninth roots in the converted TeX are transcription errors. Put α=3m1​m22​​ and β=α2/m2​. The given condition excludes the trivial cube case. Both generators are integral, because
α3=m1​m22​,β3=m12​m2​.
(32)
Moreover
α2=m2​β,β2=m1​α,αβ=m1​m2​,
(33)
so A=Z+Zα+Zβ is an order in the cubic field. The field traces of α,β,α2,β2 are zero, while Tr(αβ)=3m1​m2​. Thus the trace pairing matrix of this basis is
​300​003m1​m2​​03m1​m2​0​​,disc(A)=−27m12​m22​.
(34)
We must prove that this is the maximal order, not just compute the discriminant of a suborder. The discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice restricts any index prime to those dividing 3m1​m2​. If p∣m1​, the cubic for α is Eisenstein at p, while m2​ is a unit there and A⊗Zp​=Zp​[α]. If p∣m2​, use instead the Eisenstein polynomial for β, and α=β2/m1​. In either case an Eisenstein generator is a uniformizer of the totally ramified local field and generates its full valuation ring. Indeed expansion in powers of that uniformizer using base residue representatives, and grouping powers modulo three, expresses every integral local element in its power basis.
These arguments also cover three when 3∣m1​m2​. If 3∤m1​m2​, every unit modulo nine has cube congruent to 1 or −1. Therefore the condition m1​≡±m2​(mod9) implies m1​m22​≡±1(mod9): multiply a contrary congruence by the inverse of m22​ and use m23​≡±1. Choose r∈{1,−1} with r3≡m1​m22​(mod3). The polynomial
(X+r)3−m1​m22​=X3+3rX2+3r2X+(r3−m1​m22​)
(35)
is Eisenstein at three, since its constant term is divisible by three but not nine. Its root α−r again generates the full local valuation ring. There is no index prime left. We have proved the integral basis of a cubefree pure cubic field and the requested discriminant:
OK​=Z⊕Zα⊕Zβ,dK​=−27m12​m22​.​

For K=Q(36​) write α3=6. Here OK​=Z[α] and dK​=−972. The norm of x+yα+zα2 is the determinant of its multiplication matrix, giving

π4​333!​972​=π163​​<9.

∣N(α−2)∣=2,N(3+2α+α2)=3.

The hypotheses make K a CM-field, with nontrivial automorphism c over the totally real number field k. In every complex embedding of K, this automorphism acts as complex conjugation. Write EK​=OK×​, Ek​=Ok×​ and μK​ for the finite cyclic group of roots of unity in K.
For any u∈EK​, the element u/c(u) is an algebraic integer, indeed a unit, and every conjugate has absolute value one. The Kronecker theorem on algebraic integers in the unit disk makes it a root of unity. For clarity, the essential proof is finite: the conjugates of all positive powers have modulus one, so the integer coefficients of their monic minimal polynomials are bounded by fixed binomial coefficients. There are only finitely many such polynomials and roots; two powers coincide, forcing a root of unity.
Thus
ϕ:EK​⟶μK​,ϕ(u)=u/c(u)
(42)
is a homomorphism. Its kernel is exactly Ek​, since a conjugation-fixed element lies in k and both it and its inverse are integral there. For ζ∈μK​, c(ζ)=ζ−1 and ϕ(ζ)=ζ2. Consequently ϕ induces an injection
EK​/(Ek​μK​)↪μK​/μK2​.
(43)
To check injectivity explicitly, if ϕ(u)=ζ2, then ϕ(u/ζ)=1, so u/ζ∈Ek​. The cyclic group μK​ has even order because it contains −1, and its quotient by squares has order two. Therefore the unit index of a CM-field satisfies
[EK​:Ek​μK​]∈{1,2}.​
(44)

Let Hk​ be the ordinary Hilbert class field of k. Its real places split completely, so Hk​ is totally real. Since K/k is quadratic and K is totally imaginary, Hk​∩K cannot equal K; hence it equals k. Therefore the compositum KHk​ has
[KHk​:K]=[Hk​:k]=hk​,
(45)
and is an abelian extension of K, by restriction of the Galois group to Hk​. At finite primes it is unramified because unramified extensions remain unramified under base change. The field K has no real places, so there is no infinite-place ramification condition left. Thus KHk​⊆HK​, where HK​ is the Hilbert class field of K. Since [HK​:K]=hK​, the tower degree formula proves class number divisibility for a CM extension:
hk​∣hK​.​
(46)
This argument uses the ordinary class field with split real places; replacing it silently by the narrow class field would invalidate the claim that Hk​ is totally real.
