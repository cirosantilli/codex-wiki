Let σv2​=⟨v(0)2⟩, assumed finite. The displacement is ΔX(t)=∫0t​v(s)ds. By stationarity, its two-time covariance is ⟨v(s)v(r)⟩=σv2​ρ(s−r), with ρ even for a real scalar process. Integrating over the two triangles of the square gives the Taylor turbulent dispersion identity
⟨ΔX(t)2⟩=2σv2​∫0t​(t−s)ρ(s)ds.​
(1)
A sufficient condition for ordinary long-time variance growth is ∫0∞​∣ρ(s)∣ds<∞ with ∫0∞​ρ(s)ds>0. The dominated convergence theorem then gives
2t⟨ΔX(t)2⟩​⟶D,D=σv2​∫0∞​ρ(s)ds.​
(2)
More generally the necessary asymptotic condition for a finite positive variance-based diffusion coefficient is convergence of σv2​∫0t​(1−s/t)ρ(s)ds to that coefficient. Absolute integrability is sufficient, not necessary. A zero integral does not give nondegenerate ordinary diffusion. Moreover a second-moment calculation alone does not establish a normal distribution or an entire diffusive scaling limit; those require additional probabilistic assumptions.
For the specified positive autocorrelation, direct integration gives, when α=1,2,
⟨ΔX2⟩=2σv2​[(1−α)(2−α)(1+t)2−α−1​−1−αt​].
(3)
Thus the power-law velocity-correlation dispersion has the following regimes. If α>1, the Lagrangian integral time is 1/(α−1) and ordinary variance growth holds, with D=σv2​/(α−1). At α=2 the exact expression is 2σv2​[t−log(1+t)]. If α=1,
⟨ΔX2⟩=2σv2​[(1+t)log(1+t)−t]∼2σv2​tlogt.
(4)
For 0<α<1,
⟨ΔX2⟩∼(1−α)(2−α)2σv2​​t2−α.​
(5)
The last two regimes spread faster than ordinary diffusion and have no finite long-time diffusion coefficient. Their slowly decaying velocity memory is responsible. At short times all these correlations instead give the ballistic law ⟨ΔX2⟩∼σv2​t2.

Take κ>0 and initially ω=0. Write mj​(y,t)=∫R​xjχ(x,y,t)dx, and set k=π/L, a=κk2. The advection-diffusion equation is χt​+uχx​=κ(χxx​+χyy​). Integration by parts in the unbounded coordinate gives the longitudinal moment hierarchy for shear transport
∂t​m0​=κ∂y2​m0​,∂t​m1​=κ∂y2​m1​+um0​,∂t​m2​=κ∂y2​m2​+2um1​+2κm0​.
(6)
All three moments obey Neumann boundary conditions at the walls. The initial values are m0​=L, m1​=0, m2​=L3/12. These manipulations require finite moments and vanishing boundary terms at x=±∞; diffusion of the compact initial distribution supplies the required decay for positive time.
The zeroth moment remains exactly L. Since sin(ky) is a Neumann eigenfunction, put m1​=Lg(t)sin(ky). Then g′+ag=Ucos(ωt) and g(0)=0, giving
g(t)=a2+ω2U​[acos(ωt)+ωsin(ωt)−ae−at].
(7)
For the second moment, 2um1​=LUgcos(ωt)[1−cos(2ky)]. Thus m2​=A(t)+B(t)cos(2ky), with
A′=2κL+LUgcos(ωt),B′+4aB=−LUgcos(ωt),A(0)=L3/12,B(0)=0.
(8)
The equation for B has positive damping and bounded forcing, so B remains bounded. In the equation for A, the decaying term in g contributes only a bounded accumulated transient. Over a complete nonzero-frequency cycle, ⟨cos2(ωt)⟩t​=1/2 and ⟨sin(ωt)cos(ωt)⟩t​=0. Consequently
A(t)=[2κL+2(a2+ω2)LU2a​]t+O(1),m2​(y,t)=2Ktm0​+O(1),K=κ+4(a2+ω2)U2a​.​
(9)
This proves oscillating shear dispersion in an insulating channel, including the local-in-y leading term, not just a cross-channel average. The instantaneous derivative retains bounded periodic oscillations; its cycle-averaged slope is 2Km0​. Thus 2Ktm0​ is the leading second moment, rather than a literal rate proportional to t.
The asymptotic statement holds for fixed positive diffusivity and fixed nonzero frequency, after times long compared with a−1 and the oscillation period. If ∣ω∣L2/κ≪1, transverse diffusion adjusts rapidly to the changing shear flow, and
K≃κ+4π2κU2L2​.
(10)
This is the cycle average of the instantaneous steady-shear enhancement U2cos2(ωt)/(2a). At exactly ω=0, there is no cycle average: g→U/a and K=κ+U2/(2a). Hence the zero-frequency and long-time limits do not commute. In the intermediate range a−1≪t≪∣ω∣−1, the shear is approximately steady at its initial amplitude and that latter enhancement is appropriate.

Write a=ΛT. The deformation gradient of one interval of shear flow is S(a)=(10​a1​). An initial unit material line at angle θ therefore becomes (cosθ+asinθ,sinθ). Its orientation, including the correct quadrant, is
θT​=atan2(sinθ,cosθ+asinθ),ℓ(θ)=1+asin2θ+a2sin2θ​.​
(11)
For an unoriented line the angle is taken modulo π. If Λ=0, there is no shear flow: every λN​=1 and μ=0. The fluctuation and maximizing calculations below concern nonzero shear.
To quantify the renovating shear flow, use the intended isotropic model: each shear orientation is independent and uniform modulo π. Independence by itself does not specify a probability law and cannot fix the answer. Under the uniform law, conditional on every earlier deformation, the relative angle to the next shear is again uniform. Thus the logarithmic increments ξn​=logℓ(θn​) are independent and identically distributed, even though the absolute orientation of the stretched material line need not be uniform. Since logλN​=∑n=1N​ξn​, only the one-step angular mean is needed.
Write ℓ2=A+Bcos(2θ−δ), where A=1+a2/2, B=∣a∣1+a2/4​ and A2−B2=1. The supplied angular logarithmic integral, with parameter B/A, gives
r(a):=⟨ξ⟩=21​log2A+A2−B2​​=21​log(1+a2/4).
(12)
Therefore the mean logarithmic stretching rate is
μ=2Tlog(1+Λ2T2/4)​=∣Λ∣2∣a∣log(1+a2/4)​.​
(13)
This is the mean logarithmic stretching in an isotropic renovating shear, and the strong law of large numbers gives the same almost-sure Lyapunov exponent. Taking Λ>0 without loss for this statistic puts it in the requested form ΛF(ΛT).
For b=∣Λ∣T, F(b)=log(1+b2/4)/(2b). At small b, μ=Λ2T/8+O(Λ4T3); at large b, μ∼log(b/2)/T, tending to zero when T increases at fixed shear rate. The maximizing nonzero b solves
logz=2(1−z−1),z=1+b2/4.
(14)
The difference 2(1−z−1)−logz rises from zero up to z=2 and then strictly decreases to −∞, so there is exactly one root beyond 2. Numerically,
∣Λ∣T≃3.9605826,μmax​≃0.2012∣Λ∣.​
(15)
Very rapid renovation gives little deformation per interval and cancels the first-order mean stretch. Long intervals provide strong deformation but progressively align a material line with the shear direction, so one interval stretches it only algebraically in T. Intermediate persistence combines substantial deformation with frequent reorientation, producing the finite maximum.
For the distribution, let γ=arsinh(∣a∣/2), so the singular stretches of S(a) are e±γ. In the right-singular-vector basis, ℓ2=e2γcos2φ+e−2γsin2φ. Put q=tanhγ=∣a∣/a2+4​. Factoring this expression and expanding its logarithm gives
ξ=r(a)+∑j=1∞​j(−1)j+1qj​cos(2jφ),v(a):=Varξ=21​∑j=1∞​j2q2j​.
(16)
The variance follows from angular orthogonality. These bounded independent increments imply
Nv(a)​logλN​−Nr(a)​ ⟹ N(0,1).​
(17)
Accordingly the central part of the large-N stretch distribution is approximately described by a log-normal distribution, with density [λ2πNv​]−1exp[−(logλ−Nr)2/(2Nv)]. This central limit theorem is not an assertion about arbitrarily extreme tails: the exact stretch has support inside e−Nγ≤λN​≤eNγ.
For ∣a∣≪1, the log-stretch fluctuations in an isotropic renovating shear have
r(a)=8a2​−64a4​+O(a6),v(a)=8a2​−1283a4​+O(a6).
(18)
To leading order the mean and variance of logλN​ both equal Na2/8. Typical stretch is exp(Na2/8), while its mean grows as exp(3Na2/16). These moment statements can be justified directly rather than extrapolating the central Gaussian approximation: expansion of ℓp gives
⟨ℓp⟩=1+16p(p+2)​a2+O(a4),⟨λNp​⟩=exp[16Np(p+2)a2​+O(Na4)]​
(19)
for fixed p. In particular ⟨λN2​⟩=(1+a2/2)N exactly. The leading small-a log-normal approximation is especially precise in the many-small-renovations scaling with Na2 fixed and Na4→0.
If the orientation law is not isotropic these formulas need not hold. For example, repeating a fixed shear direction gives S(a)N=S(Na), only algebraic stretch, and zero asymptotic logarithmic rate. The associated degenerate direction variables are independent, illustrating why the stated independence condition alone is insufficient to select the isotropic result.

The unperturbed sinusoidal cellular flow has
u0​=(sinxcosy,−cosxsiny),dtdψ0​​=∇ψ0​⋅u0​=0.
(20)
Its stream function is a first integral, so each trajectory lies on a level set of ψ0​. This planar autonomous Hamiltonian flow has one degree of freedom and its conserved quantity reduces motion on regular levels to a quadrature: this is its integrability. The central cell has closed streamlines; the zero level is the grid of separatrices.
Solving both components of u0​=0 gives saddle equilibria at (nπ,mπ) and center equilibria at (π/2+nπ,π/2+mπ). In the specified open square, the saddles are (0,0),(π,0),(0,π),(π,π) and the only interior center is (π/2,π/2). Centers at the outer square's corners are on its excluded boundary.
At a saddle the Jacobian matrix is diag(s,−s) with s=(−1)n+m. If n+m is even, the horizontal branches are unstable and vertical branches stable; if it is odd, those roles reverse. The branches extend along y=mπ and x=nπ and connect neighboring saddles as heteroclinic orbits. For example the bottom boundary flows from (0,0) to (π,0), the right boundary from (π,0) to (π,π), the top boundary from (π,π) to (0,π), and the left boundary returns to (0,0). A connecting branch is unstable relative to its departing saddle and stable relative to its arriving saddle.
At a center the Jacobian matrix is (0s​−s0​), where now s=sinxsiny=±1. Its eigenvalues are ±i. The local definite extremum of ψ0​ surrounds it by closed streamlines, giving a neutrally stable center, not an attracting equilibrium. Central circulation is counterclockwise and adjoining cells have alternating circulation.

Use a Poincaré map sampled once per forcing period. A hyperbolic periodic trajectory has stable manifolds containing parcels that approach it under forward iteration and unstable manifolds containing parcels that approach it under backward iteration. In the integrable limit the relevant branches coincide along a heteroclinic orbit and form a barrier between neighboring cells.
A transverse crossing in the perturbed map means that this barrier has split: a parcel on the intersection belongs to both asymptotic manifolds, and adjoining branches enclose fluid lobes transported across the former boundary. Iteration stretches and folds those lobes. In the connected heteroclinic network this creates complicated intercell transport and chaotic advection. The crossing is between invariant curves at the same forcing phase; it does not mean that different trajectories cross at one time in violation of uniqueness.
The incompressible flow gives an area-preserving map, so lobe exchange preserves parcel area. Without diffusion, an advected passive scalar also keeps its value on each trajectory. Therefore manifold intersections can generate fine filaments and efficient advective exchange but do not by themselves produce molecular homogenization, nor prove that every region of the flow becomes chaotic.

Subtracting proves the heteroclinic Melnikov function for a periodic planar flow:
M(X0​,t0​)=∫−∞∞​∇ψ0​(q0​(τ−t0​))⋅u1​(q0​(τ−t0​),τ)dτ.​
(24)
Choose a regular point on the connection where ∇ψ0​=0. The first-order signed normal distance between the perturbed invariant curves is ϵM/∣∇ψ0​∣+O(ϵ2). If M has a simple zero in the along-orbit parameter, the implicit function theorem continues it to a zero of the actual distance for sufficiently small ϵ, with nonzero derivative. The curves consequently cross transversely rather than touching tangentially.
A phase shift t0​ and a shift of the reference point along the autonomous connection are equivalent in this calculation: using X0​=q0​(s0​) changes the integrand from q0​(τ−t0​) to q0​(τ−t0​+s0​). The word “unperturbed” in the printed conclusion cannot be literal: those branches coincide before perturbation and have no transverse splitting.

On y=0, the unperturbed trajectory satisfies x˙=sinx. Put a0​=logtan(X0​/2). Integration with x(0)=X0​ gives
x(s)=2arctanes+a0​,sinx(s)=sech(s+a0​),cosx(s)=−tanh(s+a0​).

It runs from (0,0) to (π,0), so it is the appropriate heteroclinic orbit. From the perturbed stream function,

On the chosen connection, ∇ψ0​=(0,−sinx); hence the scalar product in the heteroclinic Melnikov function for a periodic planar flow is sin2xsin(ωt). Therefore
M(X0​,t0​)=∫−∞∞​sech2(τ−t0​+a0​)sin(ωτ)dτ.

Set s=τ−t0​+a0​. The odd term sech2ssin(ωs) integrates to zero, leaving the supplied even Fourier integral multiplied by sin[ω(t0​−a0​)]. Thus
M(X0​,t0​)=sinh(πω/2)πω​sin[ω(t0​−logtan2X0​​)].​
(28)
For every fixed ω=0 the amplitude is nonzero and the phase zeros are simple: ∂t0​​M there is ±πω2/sinh(πω/2)=0. At fixed phase, varying X0​ also gives simple zeros because da0​/dX0​=1/sinX0​ is nonzero for 0<X0​<π. The Melnikov splitting of a periodically perturbed cellular flow therefore produces transverse manifold intersections at sufficiently small perturbation and fluid-lobe exchange between adjacent cells. The unperturbed separatrix is no longer a material transport barrier.
At high frequency the leading splitting amplitude is asymptotic to 2π∣ω∣e−π∣ω∣/2, so fast forcing has an exponentially weak first-order effect on this connection. This is a fixed-frequency, small-ϵ result; an exponentially small leading term need not dominate higher orders in a simultaneous high-frequency limit. At exactly ω=0 the perturbation itself vanishes and M=0, so the nonzero-frequency conclusion does not apply. Manifold splitting demonstrates intercell chaotic advection locally, not a proof of uniform mixing or ordinary diffusion throughout the entire domain.
