A spherical harmonic of angular degree ℓ has horizontal wavenumber kh​=L/r, with L=ℓ(ℓ+1)​. The local acoustic dispersion relation therefore gives the radial wavenumber
kr2​=ω2/c2−L2/r2.
(25)
In the high-order regime a travelling stellar acoustic mode accumulates phase ∫kr​dr across its radial cavity. Reflection at the inner turning point and at the near-surface boundary fixes additional phases. A round trip must return the same complex amplitude, so WKB quantization gives
∫rt​ro​​ω2/c2−L2/r2​dr=(n+α)π.​
(26)
Here n counts radial oscillations, up to the choice of origin for radial order, and α incorporates both reflection phases. The outer acoustic reflection need not coincide exactly with the geometric stellar radius; replacing it by R and absorbing its correction in α is the leading asymptotic approximation. The model neglects buoyancy, perturbations of self-gravity and rapid background variations on a wavelength.
Resolved observations of the Sun measure line-of-sight surface velocity through the Doppler effect, or brightness fluctuations. Projection on spherical harmonics identifies ℓ and azimuthal order m; a temporal Fourier transform identifies the mode angular frequencies ω. The sequences of peaks of successive radial order form ridges in the frequency-degree diagram. Their ordering and comparison with a reference stellar oscillation model identify n; the surface image alone does not count inaccessible radial nodes. Fit the residual phase of the radial quantization relation across the observed mode set to estimate α. A simultaneous fit with the sound-speed profile, or a calibrated reference model, is needed: α is not determined independently by one mode, and an integer shift of the numbering of n shifts α oppositely. In a more accurate fit the surface phase depends on frequency. These steps constitute helioseismology.
Set a(r)=c(r)/r and w=ω/L. Assume a decreases outwards in the region to be inverted. With the surface approximation above, the Duvall law is
F(w)=ω(n+α)π​=∫rt​(w)R​1−a(r)2/w2​c(r)dr​.
(27)
Thus a suitably scaled mode set samples one function of w. Smooth the measured values before differentiating; inversion amplifies observational noise. At a finite outer reference point rs​, the already known contribution exterior to rs​ must be subtracted. For the remaining cavity, let ws​=a(rs​) and h(b)=−dlnr/db. Changing integration variable from radius to b=a(r) gives

∫ba​ww2−b2​a2−w2​dw​=2abπ​
(29)
follows by setting w2=b2+(a2−b2)sin2θ and then integrating 1/[b2cos2θ+a2sin2θ]. It yields the Abel inversion of stellar acoustic travel times:
lnr(a)rs​​=π2a​∫ws​a​a2−w2​wF′(w)​dw=π2a2​∫ws​/a1​1−x2​xF′(ax)​dx.​

Knowing the monotone relation r(a) then gives c=ar. Observed turning points delimit the region recoverable without extrapolation; an unknown surface contribution or nonmonotone a prevents this simple unique inversion.
There are genuine inconsistencies in the printed numerical example. Since w and w0​ have dimensions of angular frequency, F must have dimensions of time, but the displayed empirical right-hand side is dimensionless. Moreover its derivative supplies a factor 1/w0​, whereas the requested inversion supplies 1/w02​. The dimensionally repaired relation that produces the requested inversion integral is
Fc​(w)=w02​w​(1−2πw02​3w2​),Fc′​(w)=w02​1​(1−2πw02​9w2​).
(31)
Substitution in the boxed Abel transform inversion gives precisely the exponent coefficient 2a2/(πw02​), with the square-root denominator inside the integral. This repairs the first normalization but does not repair the later algebra.
Indeed, put xs​=ws​/a and b=1−xs2​​. The required elementary integrals evaluate to
∫xs​1​1−x2​xdx​=b,∫xs​1​1−x2​x3dx​=b−3b3​.
(32)
The finite-reference-point answer following from Fc​ is therefore
lnrrs​​=πw02​2a2​[b−2πw02​9a2​(b−3b3​)].​
(33)
It still depends on ws​. The later printed profile suppresses that dependence, which requires the additional surface limit ws​→0, rs​→R. Even in that limit it is not the result of this integral. To see this exactly, write L=ln(R/r) and y=a2/(πw02​). The integral gives
L=2y−6y2,c(r)=rw0​6π​(1−1−6ln(R/r)​)​.​
(34)
The minus branch is selected by a→0 at the surface. Its decreasing-radius branch extends only to ln(R/r)=1/6, where a2=πw02​/6; it cannot describe r=R/e. The stated lower bound on the largest sampled w does not fix this algebraic obstruction. In contrast, squaring and rearranging the printed closed profile would require L=18y−81y2. This differs identically from 2y−6y2, so the two printed steps cannot both follow from the same data.
The surface polytrope test can nevertheless be performed exactly, and clarifies the final discrepancy. For a plane-parallel polytropic atmosphere of index m=3, take g=GM/R2 constant and z=R−r. The stellar hydrostatic equation and p=Kρ4/3 give 4p/ρ=gz, hence the surface sound speed of a polytropic star is
c2≃4R2γGM​z,c0​=4R2γGM​​.​
(35)
The exponent in the equilibrium polytropic equation of state need not equal the perturbation adiabatic exponent γ. For the corrected inversion above, expansion at z/R≪1 gives c2≃πw02​Rz/2, so matching the surface polytrope fixes w02​=γGM/(2πR3).
Alternatively, if the later printed closed profile is adopted as a separate imposed model, its surface expansion gives c2≃πw02​Rz/18. It also has the correct square-root depth dependence, but requires
w02​=2πR39γGM​for the printed closed profile.​
(36)
At r=R/e that imposed profile then gives
c(R/e)=e1​2RγGM​​.​
(37)
The claimed coefficient π/3 is inconsistent with this substitution; 1/e=π/3. Thus the surface scaling is demonstrable, but the empirical normalization, the requested closed inversion and the final numerical coefficient cannot all be asserted as printed.
