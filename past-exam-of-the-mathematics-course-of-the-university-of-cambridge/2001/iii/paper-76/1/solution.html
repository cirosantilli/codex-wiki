The reduced denominator of k!a/q is between q/k! and q. Its approximation error is bounded by a constant depending on k times the inverse square of that denominator. Split the r-range into blocks of length a sufficiently small fixed multiple of q. Within a block the values k!θr are separated modulo one by a constant multiple of 1/q: rational differences have spacing at least 1/q, while the approximation error in a block difference is smaller than half that spacing. At most a bounded number of values occupy each distance interval of width 1/q. Summing reciprocals of their ordered distances proves the reciprocal fractional-part sum near a rational estimate

Choose ρ sufficiently small, absorb the logarithm into a further arbitrarily small power of N, and take pth roots. This proves the stated Weyl inequality.
For the recurrence assertion, a rational a=u/v gives an integer am2 with m=v. For irrational a, Weyl differencing implies that am2 is an equidistributed sequence modulo one. One way to avoid a rate issue for unusually good rational approximations is to keep a fixed differencing range H: the Van der Corput inequality for finite scalar sequences gives, for each nonzero integer j, a limsup bound limsupN​∣N−1∑m≤N​e(jam2)∣2≪1/H, because every fixed nonzero difference has irrational linear slope and its geometric average tends to zero. Let H→∞ and apply the Weyl criterion. Hence some m always enters the open ϵ-neighborhood of zero.
Finally, Um​={a∈R/Z:∥am2∥<ϵ} are open sets covering the compact circle group. A finite subcover has a largest index N. That single N works for every real a. This is uniform square recurrence on the circle; the compactness step gives existence, not an explicit quantitative bound.
