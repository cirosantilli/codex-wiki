Here is the Brownian change-of-drift form of the Girsanov theorem. Let W be a standard Brownian motion on [0,T] and θ predictable with ∫0T​θs2​ds<∞ almost surely. Suppose the stochastic exponential
Zt​=exp(−∫0t​θs​dWs​−21​∫0t​θs2​ds)
(13)
is a true martingale with EZT​=1. Define dQ=ZT​dP. Then Q is equivalent to P and
WtQ​=Wt​+∫0t​θs​ds is a standard Brownian motion under Q.​
(14)
The same statement holds in d dimensions with scalar products, squared norms and a vector drift. Novikov's condition Eexp(21​∫0T​θs2​ds)<∞ is a standard sufficient condition for the true-martingale hypothesis. Bounded θ is enough for all applications below.
For the proof, Itô formula gives dZt​=−Zt​θt​dWt​. The Itô product rule gives the cancellation
d(Zt​WtQ​)=WtQ​dZt​+Zt​(dWt​+θt​dt)+d[Z,W]t​=Zt​(1−θt​WtQ​)dWt​.
(15)
The two drift terms cancel because d[Z,W]t​=−Zt​θt​dt. Stop WQ when its absolute value reaches n, at a time τn​ capped by T. The same product calculation makes Zt​Wt∧τn​Q​ a local martingale under P. Its absolute value is at most nZt​; the density martingale is uniformly integrable over this finite horizon, so the stopped product is a true martingale. Bayes' conditional identity then gives
EQ​[Wt∧τn​Q​∣Fs​]=Zs−1​EP​[Zt​Wt∧τn​Q​∣Fs​]=Ws∧τn​Q​.
(16)
As n increases, these stopping times exhaust the horizon by continuity. Thus WQ is a continuous local martingale under Q. Adding a finite-variation drift does not change quadratic variation, so [WQ]t​=t.
To prove the Brownian conclusion rather than just assert it, for every real u apply Itô formula to Ft​=exp(iuWtQ​+u2t/2). Its differential is iuFt​dWtQ​, and its modulus is bounded on the fixed horizon, so it is a true martingale. Consequently
EQ​[eiu(WtQ​−WsQ​)∣Fs​]=e−u2(t−s)/2.
(17)
The conditional characteristic function identifies a centered normal increment with variance t−s, independent of the past. Together with continuity and W0Q​=0, this proves the Brownian assertion. For vector u, the same argument uses ∥u∥2 and gives the multivariate version. If ∣θ∣≤K, stopping Z and applying Itô formula to Z2 gives a uniform second-moment bound EZt∧τ2​≤eK2t by Gronwall inequality; this proves uniform integrability of the stopped densities and the needed true-martingale property in the bounded case.
In the Black-Scholes model with its usual augmented Brownian filtration, write dSt​=St​(αdt+σdWt​), Bt​=eρt, with σ>0. Take θ=(α−ρ)/σ, the market price of risk. Under Q, the preceding change of drift gives
dSt​=ρSt​dt+σSt​dWtQ​,
(18)
so St​/Bt​ is a martingale. The Martingale representation theorem then makes integrable Brownian-market claims attainable and gives their risk-neutral pricing as discounted conditional expectations. The physical drift α has disappeared from the price dynamics.
For the joint terminal-value and maximum law, first use the Brownian reflection principle at level a>0. Reflecting a path after its first hit of a preserves its law as a Brownian motion and sends a terminal value y<a to 2a−y>a; the symmetry and strong Markov property of the post-hit increments justify this map. Thus, with φt​(y)=(2πt)−1/2e−y2/(2t),
P(Wt​∈dy, sups≤t​Ws​<a)=[φt​(y)−φt​(2a−y)]dy(y<a).
(19)
Take the constant-drift exponential density eμWt​−μ2t/2. By Girsanov theorem, the coordinate process under this tilted measure has the law of Ws​+μs under P. Consequently, for t>0 and x≤a,
P(Wtμ​≤x,Mtμ​<a)=∫−∞x​eμy−μ2t/2[φt​(y)−φt​(2a−y)]dy.
(20)
Completing the squares gives
eμy−μ2t/2φt​(y)=φt​(y−μt),eμy−μ2t/2φt​(2a−y)=e2aμφt​(y−2a−μt).
(21)
Integrating proves
P(Wtμ​≤x,Mtμ​<a)=Φ(t​x−μt​)−e2aμΦ(t​x−2a−μt​).​
(22)
Here Φ is the standard normal distribution function. The first variable is the terminal Brownian value, distinct from its running maximum.
Set T=t0​, a=log(b/S0​)/σ>0 and μ=(ρ−σ2/2)/σ. Under the risk-neutral measure, log(Ss​/S0​)/σ=WsQ​+μs. Taking x=a in the joint law gives the probability of not hitting the upper level. Complementing it and discounting the unit payment at maturity gives the one-touch option price
V0​=e−ρT[Φ(T​μT−a​)+e2aμΦ(T​−a−μT​)].​
(23)
The exponential weight is also (b/S0​)2ρ/σ2−1. The payment occurs at T, even if the barrier was hit earlier, so its discount factor is e−ρT, not a hitting-time discount factor.
