The modular group is SL2​(Z) acting on the complex upper half-plane by
γτ=cτ+daτ+b​,ad−bc=1.
(34)
Its central element −I acts trivially on points, so the effective group is PSL2​(Z). It is generated by S:τ↦−1/τ and T:τ↦τ+1: the Euclidean algorithm reduces the bottom row of a matrix using these operations until it is a translation. The relations S2=(ST)3=1 hold in the projective group.
A standard fundamental domain of the modular group is
F={τ∈H:∣Reτ∣≤1/2, ∣τ∣≥1}.
(35)
To see that each orbit meets it, use Im(γτ)=Imτ/∣cτ+d∣2. Choose a coprime integer pair (c,d) minimizing the nonzero lattice modulus ∣cτ+d∣; a modular matrix with that bottom row maximizes the imaginary part. Translate to the central strip. If the result had modulus below one, inversion would further increase its imaginary part, a contradiction. Interior representatives are unique: for a point in the interior, ∣cτ+d∣>1 when c=0. For ∣c∣≥2 use Imτ>3​/2; for ∣c∣=1 use the strip and the strict unit-circle bound. Thus such a matrix lowers the imaginary part, which would contradict the same fact applied to its inverse if both representatives were in the interior. Matrices with c=0 are translations, and cannot move one interior strip point to another.
The vertical sides are identified by T, and the circular halves by S. The exceptional points with elliptic stabilizers of the modular group are represented by i and ρ=e2πi/3, of projective stabilizer orders two and three. The rational boundary points together with infinity form one modular cusp orbit. In the hyperbolic metric the region has area ∫−1/21/2​(1−x2)−1/2dx=π/3.
A useful subgroup is the level-two principal congruence subgroup,
Γ(2)={γ∈SL2​(Z):γ≡I(mod2)}.
(36)
Reduction modulo two is onto SL2​(F2​), which has six elements and permutes the three nonzero vectors. Hence its kernel has index six, and the quotient is S3​. In the projective action, Γ(2) is torsion-free. Indeed finite-order nonidentity modular elements have trace zero or ±1; matrices in Γ(2) have even trace, and trace zero is impossible because a,d are odd while b,c are even: ad−bc=1 with d=−a would imply bc=−a2−1≡2(mod4). The subgroup has three modular cusp classes, represented by ∞,0,1, each of width two. Parity of a primitive pair distinguishes these classes, and elementary level-two row operations reduce each class to its representative. Six translates of F supply a fundamental region. Its compactified level-two modular curve is obtained by adjoining these three modular cusp points.
Invariant holomorphic functions arise naturally from theta constants. In the radian convention of Question 4, put Θj​(τ)=θj​(0,τ), and define
λ(τ)=Θ3​(τ)4Θ2​(τ)4​.
(37)
Here Θ2​=∑n∈Z​q(n+1/2)2. The theta-constant inversion and translation laws, obtained by Gaussian Poisson summation and by reindexing, interchange Θ2​,Θ4​ under S with the common factor −iτ​, leave Θ3​ fixed up to that factor, and under T interchange Θ3​,Θ4​ while multiplying Θ2​ by eπi/4. Together with the Jacobi abstruse identity Θ34​=Θ24​+Θ44​, this gives
λ(Sτ)=1−λ(τ),λ(Tτ)=λ(τ)−1λ(τ)​.
(38)
These transformations generate the six permutations of 0,1,∞. In particular T2 and ST2S−1 fix λ; with −I, these generate Γ(2). Thus the modular lambda function is invariant under this subgroup, but not under the whole modular group. Its even theta constants do not vanish on H, so it is holomorphic there and avoids 0,1,∞. Near infinity, λ=16q+O(q2), with q=eπiτ, which is the width-two modular cusp coordinate. The two transformation laws give modular cusp values one at zero and infinity at one, with the same simple local orders. Thus λ has exactly one simple pole on the compactified level-two quotient and no pole in the half-plane. A meromorphic map with one simple pole has degree one, so it identifies that quotient with the sphere and the open quotient with C∖{0,1}. This proves the coordinate assertion and describes all three modular cusps.
Symmetrizing this subgroup invariant produces a full modular-invariant function:
j(τ)=256λ2(1−λ)2(1−λ+λ2)3​​.
(39)
Substitution shows that this rational expression is unchanged by both λ↦1−λ and λ↦λ/(λ−1), hence by S and T. It is the Klein j-invariant. Its denominator does not vanish on H, so it is holomorphic there; at the modular cusp it has j∼q−2, a simple pole in Q=e2πiτ. It therefore remains nonconstant without being a bounded holomorphic function on the compactified quotient. In fact a holomorphic invariant extending holomorphically to the compactified modular curve would be constant by the maximum principle.
This also connects invariance to elliptic curves: changing an oriented basis of the lattice Z+τZ gives precisely the modular action after a rescaling. On the compact full modular quotient, j has just the one simple modular cusp pole, so its map to the sphere has degree one. It therefore classifies the resulting homothety of complex lattices classes, while the modular lambda function retains a level-two labeling of the branch points in the Legendre model y2=x(x−1)(x−λ). Finally, a modular form of nonzero weight obeys a transformation law with a factor (cτ+d)k, not plain invariance; ratios of equal-weight forms instead give weight-zero modular functions. The distinction between forms, invariant holomorphic functions on the open half-plane, and their modular cusp behavior is essential to this construction.
