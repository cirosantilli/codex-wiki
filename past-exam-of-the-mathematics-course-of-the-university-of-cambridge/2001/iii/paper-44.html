Put r=∣x∣. In the Unscaled Papkovich–Neuber representation, choose the componentwise harmonic vector field
Φ=−21​f(r)Ω×x,f(r)=A+r3B​.
(1)
Both Ω×x and (Ω×x)/r3 are harmonic away from the origin: the latter is a linear combination of derivatives of the harmonic function 1/r. Also x⋅Φ=0, so the representation gives u=f(r)Ω×x. The no-slip boundary condition requires f(a)=1 and f(b)=0, giving
A=−b3−a3a3​,B=b3−a3a3b3​.
(2)
Thus the Rotational Stokes flow between concentric spheres is
u=b3−a3a3​(r3b3​−1)Ω×x.​
(3)
The divergence of Φ is zero: the cross-product field itself has zero divergence, and ∇f is radial and perpendicular to Ω×x. Hence p=2μ∇⋅Φ=0 in this pressure gauge.
There is also a symmetry argument. Take the rotation axis as the polar axis. Linearity of Stokes flow makes velocity and pressure perturbations change sign when the rotation reverses. Reflection in any plane containing that axis reverses the axial rotation vector. Combined with axial symmetry, this excludes meridional velocity components and forces the pressure perturbation to be both unchanged and sign-reversed by that reflection. Equivalently a purely azimuthal axisymmetric Stokes flow has no meridional component of its vector Laplacian, so its meridional pressure gradient vanishes. Thus the pressure is constant; setting that arbitrary constant to zero gives the same result without solving the radial coefficients.
For the Newtonian fluid stress tensor, let q=Ω×x. The symmetric part of the velocity gradient is
eij​=2rf′(r)​(qi​xj​+xi​qj​),
(4)
because the gradient of rigid rotation is antisymmetric. Consequently the full stress tensor, with p=0, is
σij​=−r53μB​(qi​xj​+xi​qj​).​
(5)
For Ω=Ωez​, its only nonzero spherical-coordinate components are σrϕ​=σϕr​=−3μBΩsinϑ/r3. On the inner sphere the traction exerted by the fluid, using the sphere's outward normal n, is
t=σn=−a33μB​Ω×n.
(6)
Its torque is
∫r=a​x×tdS=−3μB∫{Ω−n(n⋅Ω)}dω=−8πμBΩ,
(7)
using ∫ni​nj​dω=4πδij​/3. Therefore the applied Torque in rotational Stokes flow between concentric spheres is
G0​=b3−a38πμa3b3​Ω.​
(8)
For b≫a, the relevant speed and length are Ωa and a, so negligible inertia requires the rotational Reynolds number
μρ∣Ω∣a2​≪1.​
(9)
For a narrow gap d=b−a≪a, the viscous gradients are controlled by d, not a. A centrifugal acceleration of scale Ω2a is then compared with viscous scale μΩa/d2, rather than μΩ/a. Their ratio is ρ∣Ω∣d2/μ. This explains why the large-gap condition is not a necessary narrow-gap criterion; a thin gap greatly strengthens viscous resistance. This base-flow scaling is distinct from a criterion for stability to arbitrary short-scale disturbances.
The Minimum-dissipation theorem for Stokes flow compares a Stokes solution with all sufficiently regular incompressible trial velocities in the same fluid domain that have the same prescribed boundary velocities. With no body-force work, the dissipation functional is
D[v]=∫2μe(v):e(v)dV.
(10)
If v=u+w and w=0 on the prescribed boundary, integration by parts with ∇⋅σ(u)=0 and ∇⋅w=0 makes the cross term vanish. Thus
D[v]−D[u]=2μ∫e(w):e(w)dV≥0.
(11)
The comparison fixes velocities, rather than applied torques or forces.
Now let the particles take their actual rigid translations and rotations in the particle-containing flow. Extend the fluid velocity through each particle by its rigid velocity. No-slip boundary conditions make this extension continuous; rigid motion has zero rate-of-strain tensor and zero divergence. The extension is therefore an admissible trial field in the original particle-free annulus, and its dissipation equals that of the fluid outside the particles. Hence D≥D0​.
The Stokes boundary-work identity gives D=G⋅Ω. The outer sphere is stationary, the maintained inner sphere has zero translational velocity, and every added particle has zero total force and torque, so none of those other boundaries contributes power. Likewise D0​=G0​⋅Ω. Therefore
Ω⋅G≥Ω⋅G0​.
(12)
For nonzero rotation and a particle of positive volume the inequality is strict: equality in the dissipation identity would force the extension to be the unique original flow, but that original flow has nonzero strain on every open particle region, whereas a rigid interior has zero strain. This proves that force-free inclusions increase rotational resistance:
G⋅∣Ω∣Ω​>b3−a38πμa3b3​∣Ω∣.​
(13)
No dilute-suspension approximation or assumption about the particle shapes was used.

In a slender symmetric viscous sheet, the two broad faces have zero tangential traction. A cross-thickness variation of w on the thickness scale would produce leading shear μwx​, whereas μuz​ is smaller by the square of the aspect ratio. The leading transverse momentum balance and the shear-free conditions therefore give wx​=0: write w=w(z,t).
Incompressibility gives ux​=−wz​. Symmetry about x=0 sets the integration constant to zero, so
u=−xwz​.​
(14)
The normal traction on either nearly vertical face matches the ambient hydrostatic pressure, giving σxx​=−pa​ to leading order. The Newtonian fluid stress tensor then yields
σxx​=−p+2μux​=−p−2μwz​=−pa​,p=pa​−2μwz​,
(15)
σzz​=−p+2μwz​=4μwz​−pa​.​
(16)
The factor four is the planar extensional resistance, as in the planar viscous-sheet stretching equations, rather than the factor three for uniaxial extension with two contracting transverse directions.
The following slice diagram shows all axial-force contributions per unit y-width; the end arrows indicate positive tensile stress and reverse if that signed stress is negative.
The two end tractions have net z-component ∂z​(hσzz​)δz. The normal pressure on the inclined side faces contributes pa​hz​δz, and gravitational acceleration gives the weight ρghδz. Hence force balance is
∂z​(hσzz​)+pa​hz​+ρgh=0.
(17)
Substitute the stress expression and pa′​=ρa​g:
∂z​(4μhwz​)−hpa′​+ρgh=0,
(18)
so
h4μ​∂z​(hwz​)+(ρ−ρa​)g=0.​
(19)
Keeping the side-pressure component is essential: dropping it would retain an incorrect dependence on the arbitrary ambient pressure level. The remaining equation from conservation of mass is
ht​+(hw)z​=0,Dt​h=−hwz​.​
(20)
It follows either by integrating incompressibility across the moving faces or directly from the face kinematic boundary condition.
When effective gravitational acceleration vanishes, 4μhwz​ is independent of z. It equals the applied excess tensile force F(t), with ambient pressure removed from the end loading. Along a material element labeled by its initial position z0​,
DtDh​=−hwz​=−4μF(t)​.
(21)
Integration gives uniform material thinning under planar-sheet tension:
h(z,t)=h0​(z0​)−Δ(t),Δ(t)=4μ1​∫0t​F(s)ds.​
(22)
A material interval conserves its area: h(z,t)dz=h0​(z0​)dz0​. Thus
∂z∂z0​​=h0​(z0​)h(z,t)​,∂z0​∂z​=h0​(z0​)−Δh0​(z0​)​.​
(23)
With the specified fixed material origin, integrate the latter relation to obtain
z=z0​+∫0z0​​h0​(s)−ΔΔds​.​
(24)
These formulas hold while the material thickness remains positive.
For the quadratic initial profile, put q=H−Δ>0. If k=0, evaluating the integral at z0​=L0​ gives
L(Δ)=L0​+∣k∣Hq​Δ​tan−1(∣k∣L0​qH​​).​
(25)
For k=0 it instead gives
L(Δ)=H−ΔHL0​​.​
(26)
For a constant positive pulling force, Δ=Ft/(4μ) first reaches the minimum initial thickness H at
t∗=F4μH​.​
(27)
Both expressions for L diverge there. Write q=F(t∗−t)/(4μ). If k=0, the inverse tangent tends to π/2 and Δ→H, giving
α=−21​,A=∣k∣π​FμH​​,L∼A(t∗−t)−1/2.​
(28)
For k=0,
α=−1,A=F4μHL0​​,L∼A(t∗−t)−1.​
(29)
This is finite-time extension of a quadratically thickened sheet. With nonzero k, the dominant extension comes from the small material region ∣z0​∣=O(q/H​/∣k∣) near the thickness minimum, where the integrand is proportional to 1/(q+Hk2z02​). Its width shrinks like q​, giving an integrated divergence q−1/2. The fixed ends are asymptotically far away in those local coordinates, so A does not depend on L0​. For a uniform sheet the entire material interval has denominator q, giving the stronger q−1 divergence and retaining its length in A. The singularity is a prediction of the ideal slender-sheet model as its minimum thickness tends to zero.

For a stationary film of uniform thickness, thermal conduction gives Tzz​=0, so T=T0​+cz. The cooling boundary condition determines c:
−κc=α(ch+ΔT),T(z)=T0​−κ+αhαΔT​z.​
(30)
Consequently the surface temperature is
Ts​=T0​−ΔTκ+αhαh​=T0​−ΔTκαh​+O(ΔT(καh​)2).
(31)
This is the quasistatic temperature of a conductively cooled film, with a weak-cooling expansion when αh/κ≪1.
Let the thickness and longitudinal scales be h∗​,L, with ϵ=h∗​/L≪1. Incompressibility gives transverse speed of order ϵU, and film evolution has the kinematic time scale L/U. For the temperature variation scale Θ, longitudinal diffusion divided by transverse diffusion is h∗2​/L2=ϵ2. Each advective term, and the time derivative on that evolution scale, divided by transverse diffusion is
κΘ/h∗2​UΘ/L​=κLUh∗2​​=Pe.
(32)
Thus ϵ2≪1 and Pe≪1 make the leading thermal profile locally linear and quasistatic. Retaining the weak-cooling condition gives the same surface-temperature approximation with the local h(x,t). For any independently imposed faster time variation one would also require its thermal relaxation ratio h∗2​/(κtevol​) to be small.
Define the positive surface-tension gradient coefficient
B=−κγ′ΔTα​>0.
(33)
The weak-cooling approximation gives γs​≃γ0​+Bh, so the Marangoni stress is γs,x​=Bhx​. The Young–Laplace equation gives the leading liquid pressure p=pa​−γ0​hxx​, with constant ambient pressure. In lubrication theory, pz​=0 and μuzz​=px​. The no-slip boundary condition at the wall and the tangential surface condition μuz​(h)=γs,x​ give
u(z)=μpx​​(2z2​−hz)+μγs,x​​z.
(34)
Hence the volume flux is
Q=∫0h​udz=−3μh3px​​+2μh2γs,x​​=3μγ0​h3hxxx​​+2μBh2hx​​.
(35)
Conservation of mass gives the thermocapillary thin-film equation
ht​+∂x​(3μγ0​h3hxxx​​−2κμγ′ΔTα​h2hx​)=0.​
(36)
The replacement of surface tension by γ0​ in the capillary term needs Bh∗​/γ0​≪1. For comparable capillary and thermocapillary fluxes,
QC​QM​​∼γ0​ϵ2Bh∗​​=O(1),
(37)
so the fractional tension change is only O(ϵ2). Both the tension correction to γhxxx​ and the differentiated-tension contribution γx​hxx​ are then higher-order. Weak cooling alone would not justify that replacement if ∣γ′∣ΔT/γ0​ were allowed to grow without bound.
The capillary term smooths the film: a crest has negative curvature and higher liquid pressure, causing flow away from it. The thermal term has the opposite effect. A thicker region has a colder surface and therefore higher surface tension; Marangoni stress pulls surface fluid toward that region, reinforcing the thickness disturbance. These mechanisms give fourth-order damping and second-order growth respectively.
Use H=h/h, X=ϵx/h, and τ=t/t. The two dimensionless flux-divergence coefficients are Bϵ2t/(2μ) and γ0​ϵ4t/(3μh). Setting both equal to one gives the balanced scales for a thermocapillary film:

(38)
These choices yield Hτ​+(H2HX​)X​+(H3HXXX​)X​=0. The predicted ϵ must be small for the assumed long-wave approximation.
Linearize about H=1 using a normal mode of amplitude δ. The resulting equation is s−k2+k4=0, so the dispersion relation is

(39)
Modes with 0<∣k∣<1 grow, while ∣k∣>1 modes decay. Differentiating s gives the most unstable wavenumbers

The physical fastest wavenumber is ϵ/(2​h).

For a stationary solid skeleton, the Darcy velocity u is liquid volume transport per unit total cross-sectional area, while the pore velocity v is the intrinsic mean fluid velocity within the connected pore space. Thus
u=ϕv.​
(43)
For an incompressible liquid in a saturated region, conservation of mass is ϕt​+∇⋅u=0. With time-independent but spatially varying porosity, it becomes
∇⋅u=∇⋅(ϕv)=0,​
(44)
not generally ∇⋅v=0. Neglecting dispersion, a fluid interface is advected by the pore velocity; its mean normal speed is Vn​=v⋅n=u⋅n/ϕ.
For the thin layer on the inclined plane, the gravitational acceleration components are gsinθ downslope and −gcosθ normal to the plane. The hydrostatic approximation and constant pressure pa​ above the current give
p=pa​+ρgcosθ(h−z).
(45)
With permeability of a porous medium k(z), Darcy's law therefore gives
ux​=μρgk(z)​(sinθ−cosθhx​),uy​=−μρgk(z)​cosθhy​.
(46)
Here ux​,uy​ denote the components of the Darcy velocity. Integrate over depth, using the prescribed power laws:
V=∫0h​ϕ(z)dz=αP​hα,
(47)
Q=∫0h​(ux​,uy​)dz=βμρgK​hβ(sinθ−cosθhx​,−cosθhy​).

Far downslope let the typical height be H(X) and the cross-slope half-width be W(X). Slenderness gives W≪X and slowly varying longitudinal thickness. In a steady porous gravity current, the longitudinal spreading term is smaller than advection by the ratio H/X, while longitudinal diffusion relative to transverse diffusion is of order (W/X)2. Thus the leading balance is
∂X​hβ=∂Y​(hβhY​).
(52)
Integrating across the finite current, with zero flux through its dry side edges, makes the leading downslope volume flux constant:
∫hβdY=constant,HβW∼constant.
(53)
Balancing the two differential terms gives
XHβ​∼W2Hβ+1​,W2∼HX.
(54)
Eliminate H∝W−1/β to find the far-downslope width of a constant-flux porous current:
YN​(X)∝Xγ,γ=2β+1β​.​
(55)
The typical height is correspondingly H∝X−1/(2β+1). For every β>0, the width exponent is less than one half, so W/X→0 and H/X→0, consistent with the discarded longitudinal terms. The porosity exponent α drops out because it enters storage, which has zero time derivative in this steady problem.

For constant porosity and permeability, the equation is hT​+hX​=∇X,Y​⋅(h∇X,Y​h). Under τ=T, ξ=X−T, η=Y, the chain rule gives hT​=hτ​−hξ​ and hX​=hξ​. Thus the moving-frame porous medium equation is
hτ​=∇ξ,η​⋅(h∇ξ,η​h)=21​∇ξ,η2​(h2).​
(56)
Its conserved mass is M=∬hdξdη.
There is a source inconsistency in the printed volume formula. Actual liquid volume is V=P∬hdxdy. The stated coordinates have Jacobian determinant
dxdy=tan2θdXdY​=tan2θdξdη​,
(57)
so the volume Jacobian for downslope stretched coordinates requires
V=Pcot2θ∬hdξdη,M=PVtan2θ​.​
(58)
The printed factor tan2θ in the first expression is its reciprocal and cannot be proved with these coordinates. For example, at θ=π/6 an integral equal to one corresponds to actual volume 3P, whereas the printed formula gives P/3. The physical-volume normalization below uses the corrected Jacobian.
Let r=ξ2+η2​. For the source-type axisymmetric similarity solution, put
h=τ−af(s),s=rτ−b.

Mass conservation imposes a=2b, and balancing hτ​ against ∇⋅(h∇h) gives a+1=2a+2b. Hence a=1/2 and b=1/4. The profile equation is
