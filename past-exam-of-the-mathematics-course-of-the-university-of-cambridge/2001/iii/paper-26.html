Cramér's theorem states that the means of independent and identically distributed random variables in Rd satisfy a large deviation principle at large-deviation speed n, with good rate function equal to the Legendre-Fenchel transform of their cumulant-generating function, provided the moment-generating function is finite on a neighborhood of zero.
Here the exponential distribution has
Λ(θ)=logλ−θλ​(θ<λ),Λ(θ)=∞(θ≥λ).
(1)
For x>0, maximize θx−Λ(θ). Its derivative is x−(λ−θ)−1 and its second derivative is negative, so the maximizing parameter is θx​=λ−x−1. Therefore the rate function of an exponential sample mean is
I(x)={λx−1−log(λx),+∞,​x>0,x≤0.​​
(2)
For x≤0, letting θ→−∞ in the same supremum gives +∞, including the logarithmic divergence when x=0. The moment-generating function is finite near zero, so Cramér's theorem supplies the full large deviation principle.
The rate function is nonnegative, with its unique minimum at μ=λ−1; I′′(x)=x−2>0. It tends to infinity both as x↓0 and as x→∞. Its finite sublevel sets are closed bounded intervals contained in (0,∞), hence compact. Thus it is a good rate function.

For fixed k, independence and the product large-deviation principle give the vector (Yn​(1),…,Yn​(k)) the good rate function ∑j=1k​I(yj​). Since the minimum is a continuous map, the contraction principle for large deviations gives
J(m)=infminj​yj​=m​∑j=1k​I(yj​).
(3)
In every vector in this fibre, at least one coordinate equals m and all others are at least m. The large-deviation rate of a minimum of independent copies therefore reduces the infimum to
J(m)=I(m)+(k−1)infy≥m​I(y).
(4)
If 0<m<μ=λ−1, the other coordinates can equal μ and cost zero. If m≥μ, the rate function is increasing on [μ,∞), so every coordinate costs at least I(m) and equality is achieved when all coordinates equal m. For m≤0 the cost is infinite. Thus
J(m)={I(m),kI(m),​m<λ−1,m≥λ−1.​​
(5)
At the threshold both expressions are zero. Goodness follows from the contraction principle for large deviations. A small minimum requires only one atypically small mean; a large minimum requires all k means to be atypically large, which explains the two costs.

Varadhan's lemma states that if Un​ has a large deviation principle with good rate function K and large-deviation speed an​, then for every bounded continuous real function h,
limn​an​1​logEean​h(Un​)=supu​{h(u)−K(u)}.
(6)
For a continuous function unbounded above, the same conclusion holds under the exponential tail extension of Varadhan's lemma, for example when the weighted contribution from h>M is superexponentially negligible as M→∞.
The scalar clipping map g(m)=min(b,max(a,m)) is continuous. The rate function under scalar clipping has endpoint costs infm≤a​J(m) and infm≥b​J(m). Since a<μ<b and the costs decrease towards μ from the left and increase to its right, both infima are attained at the corresponding endpoints. Thus the good rate function of Zn​ equals J(z) on [a,b] and is infinite outside. The function logz is bounded and continuous on that compact interval, so
limn​n1​logE[Znn​]=maxa≤z≤b​{logz−J(z)}.
(7)
On [a,μ], the derivative of logz−I(z) is 2/z−λ>0, so the maximum there is logμ. On [μ,b], the derivative is (k+1)/z−kλ, with negative second derivative. Its unconstrained maximizer is
z∗​=kλk+1​.
(8)
It lies in [μ,b] whenever k≥(λb−1)−1. The right-hand objective increases initially at μ, so this maximizer dominates the whole left-hand interval. Substitution gives the limit for high moments of a clipped minimum of exponential sample means
nlim​n1​logE[Znn​]=(k+1)logkk+1​−logλ−1.​
(9)
The moment here is the expectation of the nth power. If the printed notation were instead read as (EZn​)n, its logarithmic limit would be logμ=−logλ, since boundedness and convergence in probability give EZn​→μ.

A large deviation principle for random elements XL in a Hausdorff space E, at large-deviation speed aL​→∞, has a lower semicontinuous rate function I:E→[0,∞]. For every open set G and every closed set F,
−infG​I≤liminfL​aL​1​logP(XL∈G),limsupL​aL​1​logP(XL∈F)≤−infF​I.
(10)
Equivalently, for every Borel set A, the lower and upper bounds use its interior and closure, respectively. The conventions are inf∅=+∞ and log0=−∞. A good rate function has compact sublevel sets {I≤r} for every finite r. Here take aL​=L; the different speeds in the moderate deviation principle are stated explicitly when used.

The contraction principle for large deviations says that if XL has a large deviation principle in E with good rate function I, and f:E→H is a continuous map between Hausdorff spaces, then f(XL) has the same large-deviation speed and good rate function
J(y)=inff(x)=y​I(x).
(11)
For an open set G⊂H, its inverse image is open, so the input lower bound gives
liminfL​aL−1​logP(f(XL)∈G)≥−inff−1(G)​I=−infG​J.
(12)
For a closed set F⊂H, its inverse image is closed, giving the corresponding upper bound with −infF​J.
It remains to prove goodness, including the endpoint issue in the infimum. Each fibre f−1({y}) is closed. If J(y)<∞, compact sublevel sets and lower semicontinuity make I attain its infimum on that fibre: intersect it with the compact set {I≤J(y)+1} and use the closed nested sublevels tending down to J(y). Hence for each finite r,
{y:J(y)≤r}=f({x:I(x)≤r}).
(13)
The right-hand side is compact, being a continuous image of a compact set, and is closed because the target is Hausdorff. Thus J is lower semicontinuous and good, completing the proof.

The family is exponentially tight at large-deviation speed aL​ if for every M>0 there is a compact set KM​⊂E such that
Llimsup​aL​1​logP(XL∈/KM​)≤−M.​
(14)
For this question aL​=L. The definition says that escape from an appropriate compact set can be made negligible at any prescribed exponential rate. Requiring a strict inequality instead gives the same notion, by choosing a larger M.

Use the standard Hausdorff state-space convention, so compact sets are closed. Let F be any closed set and choose KM​ from exponential tightness. The weak large deviation principle applies to the compact set F∩KM​, while
P(XL∈F)≤P(XL∈F∩KM​)+P(XL∈/KM​).
(15)
The principle of the largest exponential term gives
limsupL​aL−1​logP(XL∈F)≤max{−infF∩KM​​I,−M}≤max{−infF​I,−M}.
(16)
Let M→∞. This is the full closed-set upper bound; the open-set lower bound was already present.
For goodness, fix r<∞ and choose M>r. The complement KMc​ is open. Its lower bound and its exponential-tightness upper estimate imply
−infKMc​​I≤liminfL​aL−1​logP(XL∈/KM​)≤−M.
(17)
Thus infKMc​​I≥M and {I≤r}⊂KM​. Lower semicontinuity makes this sublevel set closed, so it is a closed subset of a compact set and therefore compact. This proves that exponential tightness upgrades a weak large deviation principle to the full principle with the same good rate function.

Write SL​=X⊕L. Its Poisson distribution has mean Lλ, since the probability-generating functions of the independent summands multiply. Define
QL​=L(1+β)/2SL​−Lλ​,aL​=Lβ.
(18)
The scaled cumulant-generating function is
ΛL​(θ)=Lβ1​logEeLβθQL​=λL1−β(eθL(β−1)/2−1−θL(β−1)/2).
(19)
A Taylor expansion of the exponential, valid for each fixed θ because β<1, gives
ΛL​(θ)⟶Λ(θ)=2λ​θ2.
(20)
The remainder is O(L(β−1)/2). The limit is finite and differentiable on all of R, hence satisfies the essential-smoothness hypotheses of the Gärtner–Ellis theorem. Its Legendre-Fenchel transform is
Iβ​(x)=θsup​{θx−λθ2/2}=2λx2​.​
(21)
This proves the Poisson moderate deviation principle with large-deviation speed Lβ and a good rate function.
For an arbitrary open set B, the lower bound uses infB​Iβ​ and the upper bound uses infB​Iβ​. These infima agree: every point of the closure is a limit of points of B and Iβ​ is finite and continuous. Thus the logarithmic probabilities of open sets for a continuous rate function give the stated logarithmic limit for every open B, not just intervals. The empty-set case follows from the usual infinite-value conventions.

The bufferless queue output is
YL=min(SL​,uL​),uL​=Lλ+CL(1+β)/2.
(24)
Put rL​=Lλ+BL(1+α)/2. Since α<β, we have rL​<uL​ for all sufficiently large L. Consequently the two queue overflow events are exactly equal eventually:
{YL>rL​}={SL​>rL​}.
(25)
Apply the Poisson moderate deviation principle with exponent α to obtain
Llim​Lα1​logP(YL>rL​)=−2λB2​.​
(26)
There is also an exponential equivalence proof, which describes the whole output law at this smaller scale. For each ε>0,
P(L(1+α)/2∣SL​−YL∣​>ε)≤P(SL​>uL​).
(27)
The right side has strictly negative logarithmic exponent at speed Lβ. Dividing instead by Lα multiplies that negative exponent by Lβ−α→∞, giving −∞. Thus the centered, scaled input and output are exponentially equivalent and have the same good rate function. This is the moderate deviations below a bufferless queue cap.

The input admits positive moderate deviations on every scale L(1+δ)/2 with 0<δ<1. The bufferless queue output retains exactly the same overflow events at thresholds below the cap, so clipping does not improve the exponents on the smaller α scale. It removes every positive burst at thresholds on the larger γ scale.
At the cap's own scale β, apply the contraction principle for large deviations to the map x↦min(x,C). The output has good rate function
Kβ​(y)={y2/(2λ),+∞,​y≤C,y>C.​
(30)
At C the cost is infx≥C​x2/(2λ)=C2/(2λ). Thus the burstiness of queue input has been reduced in its sufficiently large positive tail, while its smaller-scale rare fluctuations persist. These are the moderate deviation scales of a clipped Poisson aggregate.

Write An​ for work arriving in slot n and c for the service rate of a queue. For a work-conserving single-server queue, the Lindley recursion is Qn+1​=(Qn​+An+1​−c)+. For stable independent and identically distributed random variables with mean μ<c, its stationary workload supremum has the form
Q=dsupn≥0​(∑j=1n​Aj​−cn).
(31)
Different scaling limits of a queue concern different quantities growing large; they need not share either a probability speed or a likely path to overflow.
For large-buffer queue scaling, fix the input law and c>μ and let the buffer size of a queue b→∞. Assume finite exponential moments around a positive root θ∗​ of ΛA​(θ)=cθ. An overflow accumulated in n≃tb slots requires an average input about c+1/t, so Cramér's theorem assigns logarithmic cost btIA​(c+1/t). Optimizing the duration gives the Cramér-Lundberg workload exponent
b1​logP(Q>b)⟶−θ∗​,θ∗​=inft>0​tIA​(c+1/t).
(32)
For an interior root with ΛA′​(θ∗​)>c, Fenchel–Young inequality verifies the minimization: the lower bound is θ∗​ because IA​(x)≥θ∗​x−ΛA​(θ∗​), and equality occurs at t=[ΛA′​(θ∗​)−c]−1. The exponential workload bound from cumulative arrival moments gives the upper exponent for every θ<θ∗​; an exponentially tilted positive-drift path or the duration calculation gives the matching lower exponent. In effective bandwidth notation, ΛA​(θ∗​)/θ∗​=c. This regime measures long accumulation into a large buffer with a fixed positive stability margin.
For many-source queue scaling, superpose L independent copies of an input process, give the server capacity Lc, and give it buffer size Lb. For each lookback horizon n, the average of the L source cumulative arrivals has a large deviation principle with rate function In​. Overflow at that horizon costs LIn​(b+cn). Optimizing the lookback horizon suggests
L1​logP(QL​>Lb)⟶−V(b),V(b)=infn≥1​In​(b+cn).
(33)
For independent slots, In​(x)=nIA​(x/n), giving V(b)=infn≥1​nIA​(c+b/n). The integer horizon is important in a slotted system. For temporally dependent sources, use the cumulative-input cumulant-generating function, rather than multiplying a one-slot formula by n. The heuristic needs uniform control of the tail of the stationary supremum to justify optimizing an unbounded set of horizons; stability and suitable exponential-moment estimates provide this in the usual models. The growing quantity here is the number of independent sources, while per-source capacity and buffer remain fixed.
For moderate-deviation queue scaling, consider a bufferless queue with aggregate Poisson input of mean Lλ and capacity Lλ+CL(1+β)/2, 0<β<1. The spare capacity is much larger than the ordinary central limit theorem scale L​ but much smaller than L. The Poisson sample-mean rate function near its minimum has expansion
IP​(λ+h)=2λh2​+O(h3).
(34)
With h=CL(β−1)/2, the cost LIP​(λ+h) is asymptotic to C2Lβ/(2λ). Hence Poisson moderate deviations give logP(overflow)∼−C2Lβ/(2λ). The quadratic cost comes from a shrinking neighborhood of the mean, rather than from a fixed macroscopic overload. The regime is useful for a large aggregate whose service margin lies between its typical fluctuation scale and its mean scale.
For a heavy-traffic limit of a queue, instead hold the input law fixed with mean μ and positive finite variance σ2, and let cε​=μ+εd with d>0 and ε↓0. In t/ε2 slots the net input has mean about −dt/ε and random fluctuation of size σt​/ε. Thus the functional central limit theorem, followed by the Skorokhod reflection map on the half-line, suggests
εQε​(⌊t/ε2⌋)⇒R(t),
(35)
where R is reflected Brownian motion with negative drift −d and variance parameter σ2. The stationary law of negatively drifted reflected Brownian motion has tail P(R∞​>b)=e−2db/σ2. When passing the stationary distribution through the limit is justified, this predicts
P(Qε​>b/ε)≈e−2db/σ2.
(36)
For dependent input satisfying a suitable invariance principle, replace σ2 by its long-run variance of a stationary process. This regime is appropriate near critical load and on long time scales; a functional transient limit alone does not automatically justify the stationary-limit interchange.
A different large-service regime holds the input law fixed. With Poisson distribution of mean λ and bufferless capacity c→∞, let m=⌊c⌋+1. The single-point lower bound P(X=m) and the optimized Chernoff bound for X≥m, using Stirling's formula, give the Poisson large-service overflow asymptotic
logP(X>c)=−clog(c/λ)+c−λ+O(logc),clogclogP(X>c)​→−1.
(37)
Here the service rate grows while the input mean does not. A quadratic moderate-deviation approximation would describe the wrong part of the Poisson tail.
To choose a regime, compare the spare capacity, typical fluctuations, number of sources and buffer size before selecting a formula. Use the large-buffer exponent for a fixed stable input and a long overload episode; use the many-source variational cost when independent flows and resources grow together; use moderate deviations for an intermediate safety margin; use the heavy-traffic diffusion when the stability margin tends to zero; and use the fixed-input Poisson tail when service alone becomes very large. Check temporal dependence and exponential-moment assumptions. For heavy-tailed subexponential distributions, a single exceptionally large arrival can dominate overflow, so a finite exponential workload root and a Brownian approximation to the far tail need not apply. These comparisons explain why the same nominal queueing theory system can have different useful asymptotic descriptions.
