Write s=A(S)=4π, L=L(∂U), and a=min{A(U),A(S∖U)}. We first prove a constant-version spherical isoperimetric inequality directly, including regions with several boundary components.
If L≥1, the arithmetic bound A(U)A(S∖U)≤s2/4=4π2 already gives A(U)A(S∖U)≤4π2L2. If L=0, a region with smooth boundary is empty or the whole connected sphere up to boundary-null changes, and its area product is zero. For 0<L<1, write the smooth boundary as finitely many disjoint simple closed curves γj​ of lengths lj​. Fix a point pj​ on each curve. The shorter boundary arc from pj​ to any other point has length at most lj​/2, so γj​ is contained in the closed geodesic ball of radius lj​/2 about pj​.
The complement of that small ball is connected and lies entirely on one side of γj​. The Jordan curve theorem therefore gives a disk Ej​ bounded by γj​ contained in the ball. Its area satisfies
A(Ej​)≤2π(1−cos(lj​/2))≤4π​lj2​.

The indicator function of U and the sum ∑j​1Ej​​ have the same jumps modulo two across every boundary curve. Their difference modulo two is consequently constant throughout the sphere off the boundary. Thus either U or its complement lies in ⋃j​Ej​, up to its area-zero boundary. This also handles nested curves and disconnected regions. It follows that
a≤∑j​A(Ej​)≤4π​∑j​lj2​≤4π​L2,A(U)A(S∖U)≤sa≤π2L2.

Combining the cases, one valid universal choice is κ=4π2. Sharpness is not required.
For the inclusion estimate put x=A(V), d=A(D)>0 and y=A(V∩D). Splitting D into its portions inside and outside V gives

Because 0≤A(D∖V)≤s−x and 0≤A(D∩V)≤x, this expression lies between −x(s−x) and x(s−x). Apply the preceding spherical isoperimetric inequality to V and divide by sd:
