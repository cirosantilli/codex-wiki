Let each observation have dimension p, and define the sample mean and the divisor-n sample covariance matrix by
yˉ​=n1​∑i=1n​yi​,S=n1​∑i=1n​(yi​−yˉ​)(yi​−yˉ​)T.
(1)
The multivariate normal distribution gives the log-likelihood
ℓ(μ,V)=−2np​log(2π)−2n​logdetV−21​∑i​(yi​−μ)TV−1(yi​−μ).
(2)
Writing yi​−μ=(yi​−yˉ​)+(yˉ​−μ) makes the cross terms vanish, and the last sum becomes ntr(V−1S)+n(yˉ​−μ)TV−1(yˉ​−μ). Since V is a positive-definite matrix, its minimum over the mean is at μ​=yˉ​. Consequently
ℓp​(V):=maxμ​ℓ(μ,V)=−2n​logdetV−2n​tr(V−1S)+C.
(3)
When S is a positive-definite matrix, logdet(V−1S)=logdetS−logdetV. Thus
ℓp​(V)=2n​logdet(V−1S)−2n​tr(V−1S)+C′,
(4)
where C′ depends on the data but not on V.
For the maximum-likelihood estimator, consider the positive-definite matrix Q=S1/2V−1S1/2, with positive eigenvalues a1​,…,ap​. The part of the profile likelihood depending on V is 2n​∑j​(logaj​−aj​). The elementary inequality loga−a≤−1, with equality exactly at a=1, shows that the unique maximizing matrix is Q=I. Hence
μ​=yˉ​,V=S.​
(5)
The divisor is n, not the unbiased covariance divisor n−1. The regularity qualification matters: if S is singular, the displayed logarithm of its determinant is not finite, and no positive-definite covariance maximizes the likelihood. Sending the fitted variance in a null direction of S to zero makes the likelihood unbounded. For a nonsingular normal population, S is positive definite almost surely when n>p, which is the setting of the large-sample test.
