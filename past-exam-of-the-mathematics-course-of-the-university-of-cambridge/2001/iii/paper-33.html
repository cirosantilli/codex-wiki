Write C=∫R​f(x)dx, so 0<C<∞. The finite envelope condition means that f≤Mg almost everywhere; in particular, the proposal must cover the support of the target.
For each independent proposal Y∼g, generate an independent U∼Unif(0,1) and accept Y precisely when
U≤Mg(Y)f(Y)​.​
(1)
Discard a rejected proposal and continue. The ratio is in [0,1] and is needed only where g(Y)>0. Neither evaluation of C nor prior knowledge of the normalized target is required.
To prove the output law, for any measurable set D the joint probability of acceptance and a proposal in D is
P(Y∈D,accept)=∫D​g(y)Mg(y)f(y)​dy=M1​∫D​f(y)dy.
(2)
Dividing by the total acceptance probability C/M gives ∫D​f(y)/Cdy, the target probability. Independent proposal-uniform pairs give independent accepted observations. With a finite proposal list there can be no accepted value; with continued independent trials, acceptance eventually occurs almost surely because C/M>0.

The acceptance mass for an unnormalized rejection envelope follows by integrating the proposal-specific acceptance probability:

For normal rejection sampling with an exponential envelope, complete the square in the probability density function ratio:
g(x)h(x)​=λ2/π​​exp(−2x2​+λx)=λ2/π​​eλ2/2e−(x−λ)2/2.
(5)
The maximum occurs at x=λ, which lies in the support because λ>0. Thus
M(λ)=λ2/π​​eλ2/2=πλ22eλ2​​.​
(6)
The PDF exponent is eλ2 inside the square root; this distinction is lost in the converted TeX.
The acceptance rule simplifies to
U≤e−(Y−λ)2/2,Y∼Exp(λ).
(7)
An exponential proposal can be generated as Y=−λ−1logV from an independent uniform distribution value V. Since the target is normalized, the overall acceptance probability is 1/M(λ). To maximize it,
dλd​logM=λ−λ−1,dλ2d2​logM=1+λ−2>0.
(8)
Therefore the optimal rate is λ=1, with acceptance probability π/(2e)​.
Finally, give each accepted half-normal distribution value Y an independent fair sign R∈{−1,1} and output Z=RY. On each half-line its probability density function is half the reflected half-normal distribution probability density function:
pZ​(z)=21​h(∣z∣)=2π​1​e−z2/2.
(9)
Consequently Z∼N(0,1). The sign must be drawn independently; rejection itself does not supply it.

For the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, start at a point x with positive target probability density function. Draw Y from a proposal probability density function q(y∣x), generate an independent uniform distribution value U, and set the next state to Y if
U≤α(x,Y),α(x,y)=min{1,π(x)q(y∣x)π(y)q(x∣y)​}.​
(10)
Otherwise retain x, including that repeated state in the sample. Where a proposed forward move is possible but its reverse probability density function vanishes, its acceptance probability is zero. Unknown normalizing constants in π cancel.
For distinct states, the accepted probability flow is
π(x)q(y∣x)α(x,y)=min{π(x)q(y∣x),π(y)q(x∣y)},
(11)
which is symmetric in x,y. This detailed balance identity, together with the holding probability, proves that π is invariant. Under appropriate irreducibility and aperiodicity conditions the chain converges to π; successive states are generally dependent.
The Gibbs sampler updates coordinates from their full conditional distributions. In a systematic sweep, draw successively
Xj(r)​∼π(⋅∣X1(r)​,…,Xj−1(r)​,Xj+1(r−1)​,…,Xk(r−1)​),j=1,…,k.
(12)
Each update preserves the target joint law: integrating the old coordinate against its conditional probability density function and replacing it by an independent draw from that same conditional leaves the joint probability density function unchanged. The composition of the coordinate updates therefore also preserves π. A Gibbs sampler coordinate move can be regarded as a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm move with acceptance probability one. Systematic sweeps preserve stationarity but need not themselves be reversible. Initialization away from stationarity requires convergence before treating the draws as approximately target-distributed, and dependence must be accounted for in Monte Carlo method error estimates.

(15)
Using independent draws η1,r​,η2,r​ from the standard normal distribution, a sweep is
X1(r)​=μ1​+ρσ2​σ1​​(X2(r−1)​−μ2​)+σ1​1−ρ2​η1,r​,
(16)

(17)

Write σε2​ for the driving white noise variance and Θ(z)=1+∑ℓ=1q​θℓ​zℓ. The autoregressive polynomial factors as
Φ(z)=1−65​z+61​z2=(1−z/2)(1−z/3).
(22)
The discrete Gaussian white noise values are independent. The roots of Φ are outside the unit disc, so expanding Θ(z)/Φ(z) gives an absolutely summable causal time-series representation. Hence xt​ is a centered Gaussian process formed from driving white noise values at times at most t. In particular, for k>q, every driving white noise value εt−ℓ​ with ℓ≤q is independent of xt−k​. The autocovariance tail recurrence of a causal ARMA process follows by taking covariance of the recursion with xt−k​:
γk​−65​γk−1​+61​γk−2​=0(k>q).​
(23)
The roots of the characteristic polynomial of this tail recurrence are 1/2 and 1/3. Let k0​=max(q−1,0). There exist constants c,d such that
γk​=c2−k+d3−k(k≥k0​).
(24)
For example, put a=6γk0​+1​−2γk0​​ and b=3γk0​​−6γk0​+1​; then c=2k0​a and d=3k0​b reproduce the two starting values and therefore the whole recurrence. Choose C strictly larger than ∣c∣+∣d∣ and all the finitely many values 2k∣γk​∣ with 0≤k<k0​. Symmetry γ−k​=γk​ now gives
∣γk​∣<C2−∣k∣(k∈Z).​
(25)
For negative k this is stronger than the requested bound with 2−k. It also proves ∑k≥1​k∣γk​∣<∞.
For the Fourier calculation, take D=2m+1 positive and T=DN positive. The supplied equal-norm trigonometric identities require j≡0(modD); the usual intended range is 1≤j≤m. Because D is odd, 2j≡0(modD) at every such nonzero frequency. The geometric series of eiωt and e2iωt over T terms vanish. They imply
∑t=1T​ct​st​=0,∑t=1T​ct2​=∑t=1T​st2​=T/2,ct​=cos(ωt),st​=sin(ωt).
(26)
Other integer values of j reduce modulo D; folding a negative frequency changes the sine coefficient only by a sign. The permitted zero frequency is handled below.
Grouping the double expectation sum according to the time lag yields
E(AB)=πT1​∑t,u=1T​γu−t​ct​su​=πT1​[γ0​∑t=1T​ct​st​+∑k=1T−1​γk​(∑t=1T−k​ct​st+k​+∑t=k+1T​ct​st−k​)].
(27)
Replace the two truncated sums for lag k by full sums. Their total is
∑t=1T​ct​(st+k​+st−k​)=2cos(ωk)∑t=1T​ct​st​=0.
(28)
Each replacement adds at most k terms, each of absolute value at most one. This gives the finite-record Fourier covariance bound:
∣E(AB)∣≤πT1​k=1∑T−1​2k∣γk​∣=O(T−1)⟶0.​
(29)
Both coefficients have expected value zero, so this expectation is their covariance.
For each finite T, (A,B) has a multivariate normal distribution because it is a linear transformation of the Gaussian observation vector. If the variances converge to vA​,vB​, its characteristic function converges to
exp[−21​(vA​u2+vB​v2)],
(30)
since the cross-covariance tends to zero. This is the characteristic function of two independent centered normal variables; zero limiting variances are allowed.
We can calculate the variance limits rather than merely assume their existence. Grouping the cosine covariance sum gives
EA2=πT1​[γ0​∑t​ct2​+2∑k=1T−1​γk​∑t=1T−k​ct​ct+k​].
(31)
Over a full record, ∑t=1T​ct​ct+k​=(T/2)cos(ωk). Truncation changes this by at most k. The same statements hold for the sine products. Consequently
EA2=2πγ0​​+π1​∑k=1T−1​γk​cos(kω)+RA,T​,∣RA,T​∣≤πT2​∑k=1T−1​k∣γk​∣,
(32)
and the identical main term and bound apply to EB2. Absolute summability now gives
limEA2=limEB2=2πγ0​+2∑k≥1​γk​cos(kω)​.​
(33)
Specify the spectral convention to identify this limit. Let
f2​(ω)=2π1​∑k∈Z​γk​e−ikω,s(ω)=2f2​(ω)=πγ0​+2∑k≥1​γk​cos(kω)​.
(34)
Here f2​ is the two-sided probability density function on [−π,π], and s is the one-sided probability density function on [0,π], satisfying γk​=∫0π​s(ω)cos(kω)dω. Thus each variance limit is f2​(ω)=s(ω)/2. For this particular model, the filter representation also gives
s(ω)=πσε2​​∣1−e−iω/2∣2∣1−e−iω/3∣2∣Θ(e−iω)∣2​.

For the specified squared-sum estimator, an exact expectation calculation uses ct​cu​+st​su​=cos(ω(t−u)):

The missing tail and the k/T terms vanish; indeed the bias is O(T−1) by the exponential covariance bound. Therefore I(ω) is asymptotically unbiased for the one-sided probability density function s(ω). Under the common two-sided convention its limit in expectation is 2f2​(ω), so I(ω)/2 is the appropriately normalized estimator.
At an interior frequency with s(ω)>0, the joint Gaussian limit gives the exponential limit of a one-sided periodogram:
s(ω)I(ω)​d​2χ22​​=Exp(1).​
(37)
Moreover, for a centered Gaussian pair,

Smoothing an increasing number of nearby ordinates while shrinking the frequency bandwidth can remove the positive-spectrum variance obstruction.

This covers the zero frequency allowed by the printed inequality on j.

For the three-coordinate state realization of an ARMA(1,1) process, the observation row and transition matrix are
F=(ϕ,1,θ),G=​ϕ00​101​θ00​​.​
(40)
The first row of GSt−1​ is xt−1​ by the previous-time recursion; the second row is zero until the new white noise value is added; and the third row copies εt−1​. Hence the state equation has noise covariance
Q=Cov(wt​)=​000​0σ20​000​​.
(41)
We use the causal state model, in which the new driving noise is independent of the previous state. The Gaussian filtering with exact observations form of the Kalman filter alternates prediction through the state equation with conditioning on the next observation. Given the posterior expected value and covariance at time t−1, independence of the new white noise value gives predictive expected value at​=GSt−1​ and predictive covariance
Rt​=GPt−1​G⊤+Q.
(42)
Project through the observation row to get
xt​=Fat​,Vt​=FRt​F⊤=FGPt−1​G⊤F⊤+σ2.​
(43)
The residual vt​=xt​−xt​ is the innovation. Conditioning the joint Gaussian state-observation vector adjusts the expected value according to this residual and reduces the covariance. In this exact-observation model, the updates can be written
Kt​=Vt​Rt​F⊤​,St​=at​+Kt​vt​,Pt​=Rt​−Vt​Rt​F⊤FRt​​.
(44)
Thus FSt​=xt​ and FPt​F⊤=0: the observed linear combination of the state is now known exactly, though other state combinations remain uncertain.
The PDF's stated prediction variance omits FQF⊤=σ2. This omission cannot be used in a valid likelihood: for the AR(1) specialization below it would give zero prediction variance after the first observation. The required likelihood calculation uses the corrected innovation variance just derived.
For stationary initialization of an ARMA(1,1) state with ∣ϕ∣<1, take S0​=0 and the unconditional stationary covariance. The causal coefficients are 1 at lag zero and (ϕ+θ)ϕj−1 at lags j≥1, giving
γ0​=Var(xt​)=σ2[1+1−ϕ2(ϕ+θ)2​]=1−ϕ2σ2(1+θ2+2ϕθ)​.
(45)
Since S0​=(x−1​,ε0​,ε−1​)⊤, the cross-covariance with ε0​ is zero and that with ε−1​ is σ2. Therefore
P0​=​γ0​0σ2​0σ20​σ20σ2​​.​
(46)
Direct substitution verifies P0​=GP0​G⊤+Q. If prior state information is available, instead use its conditional expected value and covariance. A diffuse initialization is another option when the initial state is genuinely unknown, but it changes the finite-sample likelihood and should not silently replace the stationary initial law.
By the Gaussian prediction step,
xt​∣x1​,…,xt−1​∼N(xt​,Vt​).
(47)
The chain rule for probabilities for the joint probability density function therefore gives
L(ϕ,θ,σ2)=∏t=1T​(2πVt​)−1/2exp[−2Vt​(xt​−xt​)2​].
(48)
Taking minus twice its logarithm shows that maximum likelihood estimation is equivalent to minimizing
t=1∑T​[log(2π)+logVt​+Vt​(xt​−xt​)2​],​
(49)
with each Vt​ and xt​ evaluated at the candidate parameters and the specified initialization. The corrected +σ2 is essential in every Vt​.

V1​=1−ϕ2σ2​,Vt​=σ2(2≤t≤T).​
