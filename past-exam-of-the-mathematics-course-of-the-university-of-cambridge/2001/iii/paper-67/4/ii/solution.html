In deep matter domination, the scale factor is a∝τ2, so a′/a=2/τ. Neglect radiation in the gravity source, including its backreaction, to obtain
δc′′​+τ2​δc′​−τ26​δc​=0.
(34)
For a power τp the Euler-Cauchy equation becomes p(p−1)+2p−6=(p−2)(p+3)=0. Its two independent matter-era growing and decaying density modes give the full approximate solution
δc​=A(k)(τ/τi​)2+B(k)(τ/τi​)−3.​
(35)
There is no k-dependent pressure term for cold dark matter. Thus this leading equation is valid both outside and inside the horizon in the stated comoving synchronous gauge, as long as the perturbations remain linear and the omitted radiation gravity is negligible. A different time slicing would give a different density variable.
For k>0, put ω=k/3​, Cg​=A/τi2​ and Cd​=Bτi3​. The remaining radiation equation is a forced harmonic oscillator:
δr′′​+ω2δr​=34​δc′′​=38​Cg​+16Cd​τ−5.
(36)
The matter-era forced radiation response can be written exactly within the matter-background approximation as
δr​=C1​cos(ωτ)+C2​sin(ωτ)+3ω28Cg​​+ω16Cd​​∫τ∗​τ​s−5sin[ω(τ−s)]ds.
(37)
The last term is a Green's function particular solution; changing its lower limit only changes the two oscillatory constants. For the decaying source, a slowly varying particular solution obeys δr,p​≃(4/3)δc′′​/ω2, since a second time derivative is smaller than ω2δr,p​ by order (kτ)−2. Consequently the subhorizon asymptotic form is
δr​=C1​cos(kτ/3​)+C2​sin(kτ/3​)+k2τi2​8A​+k2τ548Bτi3​​+O(k4τ7Bτi3​​).​
(38)
For a pure growing mode, the constant offset and acoustic terms are an exact solution of this reduced radiation equation. In particular, the late radiation contrast does not follow the ever-growing δc​.
Before crossing, a regular growing adiabatic cosmological perturbation has both contrasts proportional to τ2, with δr​=(4/3)δc​ to leading order. Afterwards cold dark matter keeps this quadratic growth, whereas radiation undergoes subhorizon radiation acoustic oscillations about a constant forced offset. To make the requested sketch concrete, one regular leading matter-era solution on both sides is
δc​=ε(τ/τh​)2,δr​=k2τh2​8ε​[1−cos(kτ/3​)],kτh​=2π.
(39)
Its small-kτ expansion gives δr​=(4/3)δc​+O((kτ)4), and its oscillation frequency is the radiation sound frequency. The sketch plots amplitudes in units of ε; ε can be chosen small enough that the whole plotted interval remains linear. The line at τh​ uses the wavelength-to-particle-horizon convention in the question, rather than asserting that the smooth transition occurs at a sharp surface.
