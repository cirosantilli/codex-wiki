1E OurBigBook.com
A metric space is a complete metric space when every Cauchy sequence converges to a point of that space. It is a totally bounded space when, for every ε>0, finitely many open metric balls of radius ε cover the space.
With their usual distances, R is complete but not totally bounded, and (0,1) is totally bounded but not complete. The Cauchy sequence 1/n in (0,1) has its limit outside the space; no finite collection of balls of a fixed radius covers R.
For the requested continuous functions, take
f:R⟶(0,∞),f(x)=ex,
(1)
and
g:(0,1)⟶(1,∞),g(x)=x1​.
(2)
Both are continuous and onto. The domain of f is a complete metric space, but its image is not complete because 1/n tends to the missing point 0. The domain of g is a totally bounded space, but its unbounded image is not totally bounded. Uniform continuity, unlike mere continuity, would preserve total boundedness.

Let Rij​ be the components of an orthogonal matrix relating two Cartesian frames by vi′​=Rij​vj​. Repeated indices are summed from 1 to 3. The tensor component transformation law for a Cartesian second-rank tensor is
Aij′​=Rik​Rjℓ​Akℓ​​,A′=RART.

Represent a possibly randomized statistical test by a function φ(Y) taking values in [0,1], its conditional probability of rejection. Its power function is π(ρ)=Eρ​φ(Y), and its size of a statistical test is the supremum of this probability over the null hypothesis. At a prescribed significance level α, a uniformly most powerful test has at least as much power as every other level-α test at every parameter in the alternative.
Put S=∑j=1n​Yj​. For any fixed alternative 0<ρ1​<ρ0​, the likelihood ratio is
L(ρ0​)L(ρ1​)​=(ρ0​ρ1​​)ne(ρ0​−ρ1​)S,
(5)
which is strictly increasing in S. The Neyman-Pearson lemma therefore selects the upper tail S>cα​. Its cutoff is determined solely by the null distribution, not by ρ1​, so the same statistical test is most powerful against every allowed alternative and hence uniformly most powerful.
Under rate ρ, S has a gamma distribution with shape n and rate ρ. For completeness, convolution starts with ρe−ρs and, if the n-term density is ρnsn−1e−ρs/(n−1)!, convolving once more gives ρn+1sne−ρs/n!. Integrating this density by parts repeatedly gives its upper-tail probability. Consequently reject precisely when S>cα​, where
e−ρ0​cα​∑j=0n−1​j!(ρ0​cα​)j​=α,
(6)
and the power function is
π(ρ)=e−ρcα​j=0∑n−1​j!(ρcα​)j​​.
(7)
For 0<α<1 the continuous strictly decreasing null tail gives a unique positive cutoff, with exact size α and no boundary randomization.

The contraction mapping theorem says that a self-map g of a nonempty complete metric space satisfying d(gx,gy)≤kd(x,y) for a constant 0≤k<1 has exactly one fixed point. Apply it to g=fN to obtain p with fNp=p.
No continuity of f itself is needed: composition gives
fN(fp)=f(fNp)=fp.
(23)
Thus fp is also a fixed point of fN, whose uniqueness forces fp=p. Conversely any fixed point of f is fixed by fN, so it must equal p. The unique fixed point of the contracting iterate is also the unique fixed point of f. The usual nonempty-space hypothesis is necessary; an empty space has no fixed point.

Let K=O(x)​. It is nonempty, closed and bounded in finite-dimensional Euclidean space, hence compact by the Heine-Borel theorem. The strict distance inequality again makes f Lipschitz continuous. The two conditions play different roles: the covering condition supplies the whole space, while the local intersection condition supplies closure under finite intersections.

Solution

Words: 169
17F OurBigBook.com
Use an inner product linear in its first argument. The adjoint operator α∗ is the unique linear operator such that
⟨αv,w⟩=⟨v,α∗w⟩for all v,w∈V.
(57)
In a finite-dimensional inner product space, existence follows by choosing an orthonormal basis: the matrix of the adjoint is the conjugate transpose of the matrix of α. Uniqueness follows from nondegeneracy of the inner product.
If α(W)⊆W, then for w∈W and v∈W⊥,
⟨w,α∗v⟩=⟨αw,v⟩=0.
(58)
Thus α∗v∈W⊥. Conversely, if α∗(W⊥)⊆W⊥, the same equality gives ⟨αw,v⟩=0 for every v∈W⊥, so αw∈(W⊥)⊥=W. This proves the adjoint criterion for an invariant orthogonal complement.
The equality (W⊥)⊥=W uses the finite-dimensional setting appropriate here. In a general Hilbert space it holds for closed subspaces, and an adjoint is assured for bounded operators. Without closedness the converse only implies invariance of the closure. For example in ℓ2, take W to be the finite-support sequences and αv=⟨v,e1​⟩(2−j)j≥1​. Then W⊥={0} is automatically adjoint-invariant, but αe1​∈/W. Thus the finite-dimensional or closed-subspace hypothesis cannot be omitted from an infinite-dimensional reading.

Proceed by induction on dimV. The zero-dimensional case has the empty orthonormal basis. In positive dimension, the fundamental theorem of algebra gives a root of the characteristic polynomial, hence a unit eigenvector e of α, say with eigenvalue λ. On {e}⊥, the restriction of α∗ is the adjoint of the restriction of α, and the restrictions still commute. The restricted linear operator is therefore normal. By induction it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Adjoining e proves an orthonormal eigenbasis exists for V, establishing the finite-dimensional spectral theorem by construction.
