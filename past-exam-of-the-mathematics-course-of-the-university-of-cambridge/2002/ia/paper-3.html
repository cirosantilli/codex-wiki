Since AB=0, the image of a linear map represented by B lies in the kernel of A. The matrix B is nonzero, so this image of a linear map contains a nonzero vector; consequently dimkerA≥1. On the other hand, A=0 gives rankA≥1. The rank-nullity theorem in dimension two therefore gives
dimkerA=1,rankA=1.
(1)
Thus the image of A is a line through the origin, and A maps onto that line.
Reversing the order of the matrix multiplication need not give zero. For example,
A=(10​00​),B=(01​00​)
(2)
gives AB=0 but BA=B=0. This also shows why information about one composition of linear maps does not establish the corresponding property of the reversed composition.
Write A=(ac​bd​). Its rank is one, so its determinant is zero. Choose its adjugate matrix:
C=(d−c​−ba​).​
(3)
Direct matrix multiplication gives AC=CA=(ad−bc)I=0. Since the entries of C are the entries of the nonzero matrix A, rearranged with possible sign changes, C=0. This establishes both required annihilation properties.

A group G is a cyclic group if some element g∈G generates it:
G=⟨g⟩={gn:n∈Z}.
(4)
For a finite cyclic group, the order of a group element of a generator equals the number of elements of the group.
An example of a finite abelian group that is not a cyclic group is the Klein four-group, realized as
G=(Z/2Z)2
(5)
with componentwise addition modulo two. Addition commutes, so this is an abelian group of four elements. Each of its three nonzero elements satisfies g+g=0, so the cyclic subgroup it generates has just two elements; the identity generates only itself. No element generates all four elements.

Yes: every finite group of rotations about the origin in the plane is cyclic. A planar rotation is determined by an angle modulo 2π, and composition adds those angles. If G consists only of the identity, it is a cyclic group. Otherwise, finiteness allows us to choose the smallest positive representative angle α∈(0,2π) occurring in G.
Take any planar rotation in G with representative angle θ∈[0,2π). Write θ=qα+r, where q is an integer and 0≤r<α. The composition Rθ​Rα−q​ belongs to G and has angle r. Minimality of α forces r=0, so every element of G is a power of Rα​.
Similarly, write 2π=mα+r with 0≤r<α. If r>0, the planar rotation Rα−m​ has representative angle r, a contradiction. Thus α=2π/m and
G=⟨R2π/m​⟩.​
(6)
This proves finite groups of planar rotations are cyclic by an elementary angle argument.

To construct a potential for the exact differential, first integrate its dx coefficient with respect to x:
F=x3(y2+1)+g(y,z).
(8)
Matching the dy coefficient gives gy​=−2z2, hence g=−2yz2+h(z). The remaining partial derivative is then

(9)

F(x,y,z)=x3(y2+1)−2yz2+C.​
(10)
Differentiating this expression recovers all three coefficients, proving that the differential form really is an exact differential.

The divergence theorem states that, for a bounded region V with piecewise smooth boundary S, outward unit normal vector n, and a continuously differentiable vector field F on a neighborhood of V,
∫S​F⋅ndS=∫V​∇⋅FdV.
(11)
The outward orientation fixes the sign of the flux.
On the sphere of radius R, the outward unit normal vector is r/R. Thus rnr⋅n=Rn+1. Multiplying by the surface area 4πR2 gives
I=4πRn+3.​
(12)
For the second calculation, use the product rule in Cartesian coordinates:
∇⋅(rnr)=3rn+r⋅∇rn=(n+3)rn(r>0).
(13)
The field remains continuously differentiable at the origin when n>0. Indeed, for r>0 its Jacobian matrix entries are rnδij​+nrn−2xi​xj​, all tending to zero as r→0; its derivative at zero is also zero because ∣rnr∣/r=rn→0. The divergence theorem therefore applies to the entire ball. In spherical coordinates, it yields
I=4π(n+3)∫0R​rn+2dr=4πRn+3,
(14)
in agreement with the direct surface integral.

Using Einstein summation convention and the contraction of two Levi-Civita symbols,
[a×(b×c)]i​​=ϵijk​ϵkℓm​aj​bℓ​cm​=(δiℓ​δjm​−δim​δjℓ​)aj​bℓ​cm​=bi​(a⋅c)−ci​(a⋅b).​
(15)
This proves the vector triple product identity directly from the Levi-Civita symbol and Kronecker delta.
Define the scalar triple product by [a,b,c]=a⋅(b×c). Its expression ϵijk​ai​bj​ck​ is unchanged by cyclically relabeling the three indices: a three-cycle has positive sign. Thus
[a,b,c]=[b,c,a]=[c,a,b].
(16)
In particular, let Δ=[e1​,e2​,e3​]. Since the three vectors form a basis, their determinant Δ is nonzero, so their reciprocal basis vectors are well defined. Cyclic invariance and vanishing of a scalar triple product with repeated vectors give
ei​⋅ej​=δij​.
(17)
To calculate the new scalar triple product explicitly, apply the already proved vector triple product identity with A=e3​×e1​:
e2​×e3​​=Δ2(e3​×e1​)×(e1​×e2​)​=Δ2[(e3​×e1​)⋅e2​]e1​−[(e3​×e1​)⋅e1​]e2​​=Δe1​​.​
(18)
Consequently
[e1​,e2​,e3​]=Δ1​=0,​
(19)
so the reciprocal vectors also form a basis. Taking the reciprocal basis of that basis now gives
e1​=[e1​,e2​,e3​]e2​×e3​​=1/Δe1​/Δ​=e1​.
(20)
The same cyclic calculation gives e2​=e2​ and e3​=e3​, proving reciprocal basis involution.
Finally, write V=V1​e1​+V2​e2​+V3​e3​. Taking a dot product with ej​ isolates Vj​. Applying the same argument to the reciprocal basis, whose reciprocal is the original basis, gives the two sets of coordinates:
Vj​=V⋅ej​,Vj​=V⋅ej​.​
(21)
No orthonormality assumption is needed; the reciprocal basis is what makes these coordinate-extraction formulas work.

Translate one vertex of the tetrahedron to the origin and denote the other vertex vectors by a,b,c. Translation preserves edge vectors and their dot products. We may label the two known perpendicular pairs of opposite edges of a tetrahedron as the pairs represented by a,c−b and b,a−c. Their orthogonality gives
a⋅c=a⋅b,b⋅a=b⋅c.
(24)
Hence all three pairwise dot products have the same value s. The third pair satisfies
c⋅(b−a)=c⋅b−c⋅a=s−s=0,
(25)
so the third pair of opposite edges is also perpendicular.
Expanding squared lengths using the dot product gives
∣a∣2+∣c−b∣2​=∣b∣2+∣a−c∣2=∣c∣2+∣b−a∣2=∣a∣2+∣b∣2+∣c∣2−2s.​
(26)
Thus the sums of squared opposite-edge lengths are equal. These two conclusions are the basic identities for orthogonal opposite edges of a tetrahedron.

For the linear map α represented by A, a solution to the system of linear equations exists precisely when b∈imα. If x0​ is one solution, then
Ax=b⟺A(x−x0​)=0⟺x∈x0​+kerα.
(27)
Thus the affine solution space of a linear equation completely describes the possibilities: no solutions when b∈/imα; one solution when b∈imα and kerα={0}; and infinitely many solutions when b∈imα and kerα={0}. In the last case, a nonzero w∈kerα supplies the distinct solutions x0​+tw for every real t. In dimension three, the rank-nullity theorem also says that a trivial kernel is equivalent to an invertible matrix A.
For the matrix equation, AX=B means that β=α∘χ, where χ is represented by X. Necessarily imβ⊆imα. Conversely, if that containment holds, choose an α-preimage of each β(ej​) for the three standard basis vectors, and use those preimages as the columns of X. Their linear extension gives AX=B. This proves the image criterion for a matrix factorization:
AX=B is solvable⟺imβ⊆imα.​
(28)
Whenever it is solvable, every solution is X0​+N with AN=0. It is unique exactly when kerα={0}. Indeed, a nonzero w∈kerα produces a nonzero matrix N with first column w and other columns zero, so uniqueness fails for any compatible B if the kernel is nontrivial.
For the printed numerical matrix A, its determinant is −2, so the solution is unique. To calculate it, solve A(x,y,z)T=(b1​,b2​,b3​)T. The last equation gives z=b3​−3y; the second then gives x=b2​−b3​+y. Substitution into the first yields

Multiplication gives AX=​103​111​102​​, confirming the result against the printed matrix.

Choose coordinates that measure the curved boundary and the slope of a ray:
u=xy2,v=y/x.​
(43)
On the positive interior, the inverse change of variables is
x=u1/3v−2/3,y=u1/3v1/3.
(44)
The two rays become v=a and v=1, while the curve becomes u=1. The interior of S therefore maps to the rectangle 0<u<1, a<v<1. For a=0, the lower edge v=0 describes a limiting boundary, since the original region is unbounded; the origin also has no uniquely defined ratio y/x. For a=1 the region has zero area. These endpoint cases do not invalidate the change of variables on the positive interior.
For the three-dimensional integral, retain z as the third coordinate. The transformed region is
0<z<1,z<v<1,0<u<1.

In Einstein summation convention, the curl of a cross product has components
[∇×(a×b)]i​​=ϵijk​∂j​(ϵkℓm​aℓ​bm​)=∂j​(ai​bj​−aj​bi​)=ai​∂j​bj​−bi​∂j​aj​+bj​∂j​ai​−aj​∂j​bi​.​
(49)
The second line uses the contraction of two Levi-Civita symbols; the last uses the product rule. These components prove the curl of a cross product identity, including both divergence and directional-derivative terms.
Now apply that identity to m×v. On the orientable surface, m=n, m⋅v=0, and ∣m∣=1. Differentiating m⋅m=1 gives mi​∂j​mi​=0. Taking a dot product with m in the curl of a cross product formula therefore gives, on S,
n⋅∇×(m×v)​=∇⋅v−(m⋅v)∇⋅m+mi​vj​∂j​mi​−mi​mj​∂j​vi​=(δij​−ni​nj​)∂j​vi​.​
(50)
Only tangency on the surface is needed; no claim is made that the extension v is tangent away from it. The right side is the surface divergence of the tangent vector field.
With the boundary oriented according to Stokes theorem, cyclic invariance of the scalar triple product gives
(m×v)⋅dr=v⋅(dr×n)=v⋅uds,
(51)
where u is the outward conormal of a surface boundary. Thus Stokes theorem proves
∫S​(δij​−ni​nj​)∂j​vi​dS=∮C​u⋅vds.​
(52)
This is the surface divergence theorem for a tangent field.
For the disc in the z=0 plane, take n=(0,0,1) and v=r=(x,y,z). The field is tangent on the disc, and ∂j​vi​=δij​. The contraction is 3−∣n∣2=2, so the surface integral is 2πR2. On the positively oriented circular boundary, u=(cosθ,sinθ,0) and v=Ru, so the boundary flux is R(2πR)=2πR2. Both sides agree.
