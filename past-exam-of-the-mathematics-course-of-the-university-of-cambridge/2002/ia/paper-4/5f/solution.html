A set is countable if it is finite or admits an injection into the natural numbers; an infinite countable set can equivalently be listed as a sequence. Let A=⋃i≥1​Ai​ with each Ai​ countable. Choose an enumeration of each nonempty Ai​, allowing repetition if it is finite. List the pairs (i,j)∈N2 in successive finite diagonals of constant i+j and output the jth member of Ai​. This lists every member of A, so A is countable; empty sets contribute nothing, and repetitions can be removed by retaining first occurrences. This is the countable union of countable sets theorem, in the usual setting with axiom of countable choice for selecting the enumerations.
Each positive-length interval Ji​ has a nonempty interior containing a rational number by the density of the rational numbers. Fix one enumeration of the rational numbers and let qi​ be its first member in the interior of Ji​. Disjointness gives i=j⇒qi​=qj​, so i↦qi​ is an injection of I into a countable set. Hence I is countable. This proof of the countability of disjoint positive-length intervals uses a fixed enumeration and needs no arbitrary simultaneous choice of rationals.
