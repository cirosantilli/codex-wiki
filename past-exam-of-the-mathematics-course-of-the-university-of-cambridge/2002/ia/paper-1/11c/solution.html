The mean value theorem states that, if f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), with a<b, there is c∈(a,b) such that
f′(c)=b−af(b)−f(a)​.
(23)
To deduce it from Rolle's theorem, subtract the secant line: k(t)=f(t)−f(a)−b−af(b)−f(a)​(t−a). This function has the same continuity and differentiability properties and satisfies k(a)=k(b)=0. Rolle's theorem gives an interior point with k′(c)=0, which is precisely the required equality.
If h′=0 on R, apply the mean value theorem on any [x,y] with x<y. It gives h(y)−h(x)=(y−x)h′(c)=0. Hence h is constant.
For the differential equation, the product rule gives
dxd​(e−axf(x))=e−ax(f′(x)−af(x))=0.
(24)
The preceding result makes this product constant. Thus all solutions are f(x)=Ceax, and differentiation verifies every such solution.
For the integral assertion, continuity ensures that f is Riemann integrable on every finite closed interval. Use the usual orientation for an integral with negative upper endpoint. For h=0, additivity of the integral gives
hF(x+h)−F(x)​−f(x)=h1​∫xx+h​(f(t)−f(x))dt.
(25)
The absolute value of this expression is at most the supremum of ∣f(t)−f(x)∣ on the segment joining x and x+h. Continuity at x makes this bound tend to zero for either sign of h. Consequently F′(x)=f(x), proving this form of the fundamental theorem of calculus.
Finally, a differentiable solution of the integral equation is continuous, so the fundamental theorem of calculus yields g′=g; setting x=0 gives g(0)=A. The differential-equation calculation above now forces
g(x)=Aex.​
(26)
