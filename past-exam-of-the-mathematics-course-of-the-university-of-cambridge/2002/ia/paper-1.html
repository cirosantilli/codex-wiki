Place the cube vertices at (±1,±1,±1). The four vertices whose coordinate product is +1 form T; those with product −1 form T′. Within either set, two distinct vertices differ in exactly two coordinates, so every edge has length 22​ and each set is a regular tetrahedron.
A member of the cube rotation group is a signed permutation matrix of determinant one. There are 3! permutations of the coordinate axes and four choices of signs for each permutation that give determinant one, so ∣G∣=24. Such a matrix maps a coordinate-product class to a coordinate-product class. Moreover, the rotation through a quarter-turn (x,y,z)↦(−y,x,z) interchanges the classes. Thus the orbit of T consists of exactly T,T′. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, the tetrahedron stabilizer in the cube rotation group has order
∣GT​∣=24/2=12.​

The fundamental theorem of algebra says that every nonconstant polynomial with complex number coefficients has a root in the complex numbers. Repeated polynomial division by a factor of degree one therefore factors a degree-three polynomial with complex number coefficients into three factors of degree one, with algebraic multiplicities included.
For a matrix with complex number entries A of size three, its characteristic polynomial is χA​(t)=det(tI−A), and its characteristic equation is χA​(t)=0. A complex number λ is an eigenvalue exactly when λI−A is singular, equivalently when its null space contains a nonzero vector. Thus the three roots of the characteristic polynomial are the three eigenvalues counted with algebraic multiplicity.
For the specified matrix, expansion along the first row gives
χA​(t)=(t−1)det(ti​−it​)=(t−1)(t2−1)=(t−1)2(t+1).
(3)
Hence the eigenvalues are 1,1,−1. Corresponding eigenvectors are
v1​=​100​​,v2​=​0i1​​,v3​=​0−i1​​,Av1​=v1​,Av2​=v2​,Av3​=−v3​.
(4)
The matrix with these three columns has determinant 2i=0, so these eigenvectors are linearly independent. A repeated eigenvalue does not in general guarantee two independent eigenvectors; here they have been exhibited directly.

The limit of a sequence of real numbers is a if, for every ε>0, there is an integer N such that n≥N implies ∣an​−a∣<ε. It tends to positive infinity if, for every real M, there is an integer N such that n≥N implies an​>M.
If an​→+∞, choose the latter threshold with M=1/ε. Then an​>1/ε>0 eventually, so ∣1/an​∣<ε. Thus 1/an​→0. The converse is false: an​=−n has reciprocal tending to zero and tends to negative infinity. The precise sign-free statement is given by reciprocal limits and escape in absolute value: 1/an​→0 is equivalent to ∣an​∣→∞.
If an​→a=0, eventually ∣an​−a∣<∣a∣/2, and the triangle inequality gives ∣an​∣>∣a∣/2. Consequently

Here is a nested-interval proof of the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem. Put every term of the bounded real sequence in a closed interval I0​=[−M,M], with M>0. Bisect this interval and choose a closed half containing infinitely many terms. Repeating gives nested closed intervals Ij​=[lj​,rj​], each containing infinitely many sequence terms, with length 2M/2j.
By the least-upper-bound property, x∗​=supj​lj​ exists. For each j, every lower endpoint lies at most rj​, while lj​≤x∗​; hence x∗​∈Ij​. Choose increasing indices nj​ with anj​​∈Ij​, possible because each interval contains infinitely many terms. Then
∣anj​​−x∗​∣≤2M/2j⟶0.
(6)
Thus every bounded real sequence has a convergent subsequence.
For a sequence with no convergent subsequence, take an​=n: any subsequence has nj​≥j, so its terms tend to positive infinity rather than to a finite limit of a sequence. For an unbounded sequence with a convergent subsequence, take a2n​=0 and a2n−1​=n. Its odd terms are unbounded, while its even subsequence converges to zero.

Take the scalene triangle vertex set T={(0,0),(1,0),(0,2)}. Its edge lengths are 1,2,5​. Each vertex has a different unordered pair of incident edge lengths. A Euclidean isometry preserving the set must preserve these pairs, and therefore fixes every vertex.
To justify rigidity of a scalene triangle fully, Euclidean distances from an arbitrary plane point to these three fixed points determine that point uniquely: subtracting its squared Euclidean distance to the origin from the other two squared Euclidean distances determines its two coordinates. Thus an isometry fixing the three vertices fixes every plane point. Only the identity preserves T.
For the general union of group translates, let h∈G. Left multiplication by h permutes G, so
h(S)=⋃g∈G​hg(T)=⋃k∈G​k(T)=S.
(7)
Therefore G⊆H. This containment uses only the group action; the absence of symmetries of T is not needed for the containment itself.

Write a Möbius transformation as g(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d), with ad−bc=0, on the Riemann sphere. If c=0, infinity is not fixed, and its finite fixed points solve
cz2+(d−a)z−b=0.
(9)
The pole −d/c cannot solve this equation, since substitution gives (ad−bc)/c=0. The quadratic has one or two distinct complex roots, so these give exactly one or two fixed points.
If c=0, then a,d=0 and infinity is fixed. If a=d, there is one additional finite fixed point, namely b/(d−a). If a=d and b=0, the transformation is a nonzero translation, with no finite fixed point. If a=d and b=0, it is the identity and fixes the entire sphere.
Thus a nonidentity Möbius transformation has one or two fixed points; the identity has infinitely many. The examples z↦z+1, z↦2z, and z↦z realize the three possibilities respectively.

Complex conjugation fixes 0,1,∞, but sends i to −i, so it is not the identity. The preceding classification of fixed points of a Möbius transformation says that a nonidentity Möbius transformation has at most two fixed points. Since complex conjugation has at least three, it cannot be a Möbius transformation.

A vector space over the real numbers is a set V with an addition making it an abelian group and a scalar multiplication by R satisfying, for scalars a,b and vectors u,v, the identities a(u+v)=au+av, (a+b)v=av+bv, a(bv)=(ab)v and 1v=v. A vector subspace is a nonempty subset closed under addition and scalar multiplication; a proper vector subspace is one unequal to V. A spanning set is a set whose finite linear combinations give every vector of V. A basis is a linearly independent spanning set. The dimension is the number of vectors in a basis, or its cardinality when the basis is infinite.
The sum of vector subspaces and their intersection of vector subspaces are
U+W={u+w:u∈U, w∈W},U∩W={v∈V:v∈U and v∈W}.
(13)
Both are vector subspaces: addition and scalar multiplication preserve the displayed forms or simultaneous membership. The intersection of vector subspaces is never empty because 0∈U∩W.
For the two given hyperplanes, adding and subtracting their defining equations gives x1​=x2​ and x3​=x4​. Hence
U∩W={(a,a,b,b):a,b∈R}=span{b1​,b2​},b1​=(1,1,0,0),b2​=(0,0,1,1).
(14)
These two nonzero vectors are orthogonal, and consequently form an orthogonal basis of the intersection of vector subspaces. Put u=(1,−1,−1,1) and w=(1,−1,1,−1). Direct substitution gives u∈U, w∈W. Their dot products with b1​,b2​ vanish, and u⋅w=0. Each defining equation of U or W is one nonzero linear constraint in four variables, so each vector subspace has dimension three. Thus each space has the indicated orthogonal basis:
U: (b1​,b2​,u),W: (b1​,b2​,w),U+W: (b1​,b2​,u,w).​
(15)
The last four vectors are nonzero and mutually orthogonal, so they are a basis of R4. They lie in U+W, proving U+W=V. The dimension identity is therefore verified numerically:
dimU+dimW=3+3=4+2=dim(U+W)+dim(U∩W).​
(16)

Use the least-upper-bound property as the least upper bound axiom: every nonempty set of real numbers bounded above has a least upper bound in R. It implies convergence of a bounded increasing sequence: if L is the supremum of its terms, then for every ε>0 some term exceeds L−ε, and all subsequent terms lie between that term and L. A bounded decreasing sequence converges by applying this argument to its negative.
The alternating series test states that if bn​≥0, bn+1​≤bn​, and bn​→0, then ∑n≥1​(−1)n−1bn​ converges. To prove it, let SN​ denote its partial sums. The even partial sums are increasing because S2m+2​−S2m​=b2m+1​−b2m+2​≥0. The odd partial sums are decreasing because S2m+3​−S2m+1​=−b2m+2​+b2m+3​≤0. Moreover,
0≤S2m​≤S2m+1​≤b1​.
(17)
Completeness of the real numbers therefore gives a limit of a sequence for each subsequence, denoted Le​,Lo​ respectively for the even and odd subsequences. Their difference is S2m+1​−S2m​=b2m+1​→0, so Le​=Lo​. Every sufficiently late partial sum belongs to one of these two subsequences, proving convergence of the full sequence of partial sums. Multiplying the terms by −1 leaves convergence unchanged.

Let f be continuous on a closed bounded interval [a,b]. First prove that f is a bounded function. If it failed, we could choose xn​∈[a,b] with ∣f(xn​)∣>n. The Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem gives a subsequence xnj​​→x∗​. Because the interval is closed, x∗​∈[a,b]. Continuity gives f(xnj​​)→f(x∗​), contradicting ∣f(xnj​​)∣>nj​→∞.
Now let M=sup{f(x):x∈[a,b]}, which exists by completeness of the real numbers and the bound just proved. Choose yn​∈[a,b] with f(yn​)>M−1/n. A convergent subsequence has limit of a sequence y∗​∈[a,b], and continuity gives f(y∗​)=M. Applying the same argument to −f gives attainment of the infimum. This proves the extreme value theorem: the maximum and minimum both exist and are attained. The argument also covers a one-point interval, where the conclusions are immediate.

The mean value theorem states that, if f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), with a<b, there is c∈(a,b) such that
f′(c)=b−af(b)−f(a)​.
(23)
To deduce it from Rolle's theorem, subtract the secant line: k(t)=f(t)−f(a)−b−af(b)−f(a)​(t−a). This function has the same continuity and differentiability properties and satisfies k(a)=k(b)=0. Rolle's theorem gives an interior point with k′(c)=0, which is precisely the required equality.
If h′=0 on R, apply the mean value theorem on any [x,y] with x<y. It gives h(y)−h(x)=(y−x)h′(c)=0. Hence h is constant.
For the differential equation, the product rule gives
dxd​(e−axf(x))=e−ax(f′(x)−af(x))=0.
(24)
The preceding result makes this product constant. Thus all solutions are f(x)=Ceax, and differentiation verifies every such solution.
For the integral assertion, continuity ensures that f is Riemann integrable on every finite closed interval. Use the usual orientation for an integral with negative upper endpoint. For h=0, additivity of the integral gives
hF(x+h)−F(x)​−f(x)=h1​∫xx+h​(f(t)−f(x))dt.
