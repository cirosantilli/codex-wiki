Let Irr(G) denote the irreducible characters of finite-dimensional complex group representations of G. A generalized character, or virtual character, is an integral linear combination of these irreducible characters. A class function is a function constant on conjugacy classes. For class functions use the character inner product
⟨f,h⟩G​=∣G∣1​∑g∈G​f(g)h(g)​.
(1)
A p-elementary group is a direct product of groups P×C, where P is a p-group and C is a cyclic group of order coprime to p. An elementary subgroup is a subgroup of this form for some prime p. Either factor may be trivial. This notion is different from an elementary abelian group.
Brauer's characterization of characters says that a complex class function f on G is a generalized character exactly when its restriction to every elementary subgroup is a generalized character there. Equivalently,
f∈ZIrr(G)⟺⟨fE​,α⟩E​∈Z for every elementary E≤G, α∈Irr(E)​.
(2)
The character orthogonality relations make these inner products the coefficients of the restrictions in their irreducible bases. The theorem detects the integral character lattice. An ordinary character of a representation additionally requires every global irreducible coefficient ⟨f,χ⟩G​ to be nonnegative; integrality alone characterizes virtual characters, not necessarily actual representations.

If A is empty, take the zero generalized character; if B is empty, take the trivial character. Otherwise let π be the set of primes dividing the orders of elements of A. The coprimality hypothesis makes every element of B a π′-element. Thus every nonidentity element of G is either a pi-element in A or a complementary-prime element in B. In particular these sets are unions of conjugacy classes, since conjugation preserves element order.
Put m=∣G∣π​ and n=∣G∣π′​. Their greatest common divisor is one, so the Chinese remainder theorem supplies an integer d such that
d≡1(modm),d≡0(modn).
(3)
Define the class function f by f(1)=d, f=1 on A and f=0 on B. We verify Brauer's characterization of characters directly.
For an elementary subgroup E=P×C, the cyclic factor can be split into its π-part and π′-part. Attach P to the part containing its prime. This expresses E=Eπ​×Eπ′​. Both factors cannot be nontrivial: choosing a nonidentity element in each would give a commuting product of mixed prime order, whereas every element of G has order involving only one of the two prime sets. Hence E is wholly a π-group or wholly a π′-group.
Let rE​ be the character of the regular representation of E, with value ∣E∣ at the identity and zero elsewhere. In the two cases respectively,
fE​=1E​+∣E∣d−1​rE​,fE​=∣E∣d​rE​.
(4)
The coefficients are integers because ∣E∣ divides m or n. Thus both are generalized characters. The trivial subgroup causes no difficulty since d is an integer. By Brauer's characterization of characters, f is a generalized character of G.
Taking ϑ=f gives the required values.
This is a generalized character separating complementary prime elements; the freely chosen value at the identity is what makes all elementary restrictions integral.

A Frobenius group is a finite transitive group action on more than one point in which a nonidentity element fixes at most one point and a point stabilizer is nontrivial. Equivalently it has a nontrivial proper subgroup H with H∩Hg=1 for every g∈/H. The Frobenius complement is such a point stabilizer H.
The Frobenius kernel is the identity together with all fixed-point-free elements in this action, equivalently
N={1}∪(G∖⋃g∈G​Hg).
(5)
The Frobenius kernel theorem asserts that this set is a normal subgroup, acts regularly on the points, and gives G=N⋊H. In particular CN​(h)=1 for 1=h∈H.
Brauer's permutation lemma states that an automorphism of a finite group fixes equally many conjugacy classes and irreducible characters. More generally a group of automorphisms has equal permutation characters on those two sets. Indeed, the character table is invertible and intertwines the two permutation matrices: simultaneous permutation of rows and columns preserves character evaluation. Similar matrices have equal traces, and a permutation-matrix trace counts its fixed points. The fixed objects on the group side are classes, not individual elements.

Suppose first that N is the Frobenius kernel. An element g∈/N fixes a point in the Frobenius action. If n∈CN​(g), then n maps that point to another point fixed by g. Since g has only one fixed point and N acts regularly, n=1. Thus
CN​(g)=1(g∈/N).
(6)
No nonidentity conjugacy class of N can be fixed by such a g. If the class of n=1 were fixed, gng−1=vnv−1 for some v∈N, so v−1g∈/N would centralize n, a contradiction. Conjugation by g consequently fixes just the identity class. By Brauer's permutation lemma it fixes just one irreducible character, necessarily 1N​.
For every nonprincipal φ∈Irr(N), its inertia group of a character is therefore IG​(φ)=N. Since N is normal, the restriction of the induced character is the sum of its conjugates, counted over G/N:
(φG)N​=∑gN∈G/N​φg.
(7)
Frobenius reciprocity and character orthogonality give
⟨φG,φG⟩G​=⟨φ,(φG)N​⟩N​=∣IG​(φ):N∣=1.
(8)
An actual character of norm one is irreducible. Every nonprincipal irreducible character of N therefore induces irreducibly to G.

Conversely, the same induction-norm formula shows that IG​(φ)=N for every nonprincipal φ∈Irr(N). Conjugation by each g∈/N fixes only 1N​, so Brauer's permutation lemma says that it fixes only the identity conjugacy class of N. A nonidentity element centralizing g would provide another fixed class. Therefore CN​(g)=1 for every g∈/N.
We first prove that ∣N∣ and [G:N] are coprime. If a prime p divided both, a Sylow p-subgroup S of G would have 1<S∩N<S. The nontrivial normal subgroup S∩N meets Z(S) nontrivially: the class equation for the conjugation action of the p-group S on S∩N makes the number of fixed elements divisible by p, and one of them is the identity. Choose 1=z∈N∩Z(S) and s∈S∖N. Then z∈CN​(s), contradicting the previous conclusion.
Thus N is a normal subgroup that is also a Hall subgroup. The Schur-Zassenhaus theorem supplies a group complement H, meaning G=NH and N∩H=1. The theorem's existence assertion requires only normality and coprimality of order and index. Here H is nontrivial and proper by the assumptions on N.
For n∈N∖{1}, suppose 1=h∈H∩nHn−1. Write h=nh′n−1 with h′∈H. Projection to G/N gives hN=h′N, hence h=h′ because H→G/N is injective. This makes n centralize h∈/N, which is impossible. Hence H∩nHn−1=1. Writing an arbitrary element outside H as nh0​ reduces its conjugate intersection to this case. It follows that H is a Frobenius complement and G is a Frobenius group.
Moreover, for each 1=h∈H, the map v↦vhv−1 from N into the coset Nh is injective, since CN​(h)=1, and hence bijective. Thus every element outside N belongs to a conjugate of H, while no nonidentity element of N does. The Frobenius kernel is exactly N. This completes both directions of the Frobenius kernel criterion by irreducible induction.

A finite monomial group, also called an M-group, is a finite group for which every irreducible character is a monomial character: for every χ∈Irr(G) there are a subgroup H≤G and a linear character λ of H such that χ=IndHG​λ. The inducing subgroup and linear character may depend on χ.
Taketa's theorem states that every finite monomial group is solvable. Here being a solvable group means that the iterated derived subgroups eventually reach the identity.
Dade's embedding theorem for monomial groups states that every finite solvable group embeds as a subgroup of a finite monomial group. It does not say that every solvable group is itself monomial, nor that the embedding can always be taken normal. Together the results identify solvability as a necessary property of monomial groups without making monomiality closed under taking arbitrary subgroups.

Let K be the intersection under consideration. The family is nonempty since it contains G, and is invariant under conjugation: conjugating an irreducible inducing pair (H,φ) gives another such pair. Hence K is a normal subgroup of G.
For any χ∈Irr(G), monomiality gives χ=λG with λ a linear character of some H. This H belongs to the family, so K≤H. Because K is normal, K≤tHt−1 for every t∈G. In the induced representation on
C[G]⊗C[H]​Cλ​,
(9)
each coset line t⊗Cλ​ is therefore invariant under K: for k∈K,
k(t⊗v)=t⊗λ(t−1kt)v.
(10)
Thus the restriction to K is a direct sum of one-dimensional group representations. All representing matrices of K commute, and the derived subgroup K′ acts as the identity. Equivalently,
K′≤kerχfor every χ∈Irr(G).
(11)
The intersection of the character kernels is trivial: the regular representation is faithful and is a direct sum of irreducible representations. It follows that K′=1.
K is abelian.​
(12)
This proves the abelian intersection of irreducible inducing subgroups without assuming that every subgroup of an M-group is itself monomial.

Write o(θ) for the order of the determinant character of an irreducible character θ. An extension has the same degree and restricts exactly to θ, rather than just containing it as a constituent.
Extension theorem. Thompson's coprime character extension theorem says that if N◃G, θ∈Irr(N) is G-invariant, and gcd(θ(1),[G:N])=1, then θ extends to G exactly when its determinant character extends to a linear character of G. In particular, if also gcd(o(θ),[G:N])=1, there is a unique extension with determinant order coprime to the index. For a p-group quotient, the version used below gives a canonical extension whenever both the degree and the determinant order are prime to p. This theorem is an assertion of extendibility, not the false statement that every invariant irreducible character extends.
The p-nilpotency lemma. Define
sp​(G)=∑χ∈Irr(G), p∤χ(1)​χ(1)2,hp​(G)=∣G:G′∣p​,
(13)
where mp​ is the largest power of p dividing an integer m. Isaacs' character-degree criterion for p-nilpotency asserts
hp​(G)∣sp​(G),G has a normal p-complement ⟺ p∤hp​(G)sp​(G)​​.
(14)
Equivalently, sp​(G)p​=hp​(G). We prove both the divisibility and the criterion.
Let N=Op(G) be the p-residual of a finite group, the least normal subgroup with p-group quotient. It can also be described as the subgroup generated by all p-prime-order elements: every homomorphism to a p-group kills those elements, and after quotienting by their generated subgroup the image of every element has p-power order, so the quotient is a p-group by Cauchy's theorem.
The residual is p-perfect, meaning Op(N)=N. Indeed Op(N) is characteristic in N, hence normal in G, and G/Op(N) is an extension of the two p-groups N/Op(N) and G/N. Minimality of N forces equality. Consequently N/N′ has order prime to p, since otherwise it would have a quotient of order p. Every linear character of N, in particular every determinant character of N, therefore has order prime to p.
Let T consist of the G-invariant irreducible characters θ of N of degree prime to p. Each extends to an irreducible character θ of G by the extension theorem. The irreducible characters of G lying over θ are exactly
θν,ν∈Irr(G/N).
(15)
Here is the representation argument for this description. If a representation lies over the invariant θ, its N-module is copies of the module for θ. Identify it with that module tensored with its multiplicity space. Dividing the G-action by the fixed extending action on the first factor leaves an action on the multiplicity space, trivial on N. Irreducibility is equivalent to irreducibility of that quotient action, proving the displayed parametrization.
Every irreducible degree of the p-group G/N is a power of p, since it divides the group order. Thus the p-prime-degree characters lying over θ are exactly those with ν linear, and there are
∣(G/N):(G/N)′∣=∣G:NG′∣=hp​(G)
(16)
of them. For the last equality, G/NG′ is the largest abelian p-group quotient of G, hence the p-primary part of its abelianization. In particular all these characters have degree θ(1).
Conversely, for any χ∈Irr(G) of p-prime degree, Clifford theory writes its restriction to N as e times the sum of a single orbit of irreducible characters. The orbit size divides [G:N] and also divides χ(1), so it is one; the constituent θ is invariant and has p-prime degree. Hence θ∈T. The preceding extension parametrization now forces the quotient character to be linear. We have therefore obtained the exact identity
sp​(G)=hp​(G)θ∈T∑​θ(1)2​.
(17)
This proves the required divisibility without assuming p-nilpotency.
The p-group G/N acts on Irr(N) by conjugation. Every nonfixed orbit has size divisible by p, and degrees are constant on each orbit. Fixed characters whose degrees are divisible by p also contribute zero modulo p. The sum of squares of irreducible degrees consequently gives
hp​(G)sp​(G)​=∑θ∈T​θ(1)2≡∑θ∈Irr(N)​θ(1)2=∣N∣(modp).
(18)
The quotient is prime to p exactly when N has order prime to p. Since G/N is a p-group, that is exactly the assertion that N is a normal p-complement. Conversely, any normal p-complement equals Op(G) because it contains all p-prime-order elements and has p-group quotient. This finishes the lemma.
Detection from the group algebra. For finite groups G and H with isomorphic complex group algebras, the Artin–Wedderburn theorem gives
C[G]≅⨁χ∈Irr(G)​Mχ(1)​(C).
(19)
The simple matrix-block sizes and their multiplicities are determined by the algebra isomorphism. Hence G and H have identical irreducible degree multisets. They have the same order, from the degree-square sum, and the same number of linear characters, which equals ∣G:G′∣. Thus sp​ and hp​ coincide for every prime p. The lemma shows that one group is p-nilpotent exactly when the other is.
A finite group is nilpotent exactly when it has a normal p-complement for every prime dividing its order. For completeness, if all those complements exist, set Lq​=⋂p=q​Kp​, where Kp​ is the normal p-complement. The diagonal map G/Lq​→∏p=q​G/Kp​ is injective, so [G:Lq​] is prime to q. At the same time, containment in each Kp​ removes every prime other than q from ∣Lq​∣. Thus Lq​ is a normal Sylow q-subgroup. Distinct normal Sylow subgroups commute, giving the direct-product decomposition. Conversely, the direct-product decomposition of a finite nilpotent group provides all the complements. We conclude
C[G]≅C[H]⟹(G nilpotent⟺H nilpotent)​.
(20)
This is the assertion that the complex group algebra detects p-nilpotency and hence nilpotency. It needs the multiplicities of the degrees, not just their distinct values.
Normal-complement consequence. If p divides every nonlinear irreducible degree, the p-prime-degree characters are exactly the linear characters. Therefore
sp​(G)=∣G:G′∣,sp​(G)p​=hp​(G).
(21)
The lemma yields a normal p-complement immediately:
p∣χ(1) for every nonlinear χ⟹G has a normal p-complement​.
(22)
This proves Thompson's character-degree normal complement theorem, including the abelian case where the hypothesis on nonlinear characters is vacuous.

Let V afford the given group representation of H. Its induced representation is the left G-module
IndHG​V=C[G]⊗C[H]​V​.
(23)
Choose representatives t of the left cosets G/H; then it is the direct sum of the spaces t⊗V. If gt=t′h with h∈H, the action sends t⊗v to t′⊗Ψ(h)v. This is independent of the representatives up to equivalence, and its dimension is [G:H]dimV.
Only cosets fixed by g contribute to its trace. Their contribution is ψ(t−1gt), giving the induced character formula
ψG(g)=tH∈G/Ht−1gt∈H​∑​ψ(t−1gt)=∣H∣1​x∈Gx−1gx∈H​∑​ψ(x−1gx)​.
(24)
For an arbitrary class function f on H, the same averaging formula defines its induced class function on G. Equivalently, extend f by zero outside H and average ∣H∣−1∑x​f(x−1gx). This definition is linear and does not require f to be an actual character.
For a complex character ψ of a finite group G afforded by a representation ρ, its determinant character is
(detψ)(g)=detρ(g)​.
(25)
Multiplicativity of the determinant makes this a linear character; it does not mean the determinant of the scalar value ψ(g). The definition depends only on the character because characters determine the equivalence class of a finite-dimensional complex representation. In particular the determinant character is trivial on the derived subgroup G′.

Frobenius reciprocity is the adjointness of character induction and restriction: for class functions f on H and u on G,
⟨fG,u⟩G​=⟨f,uH​⟩H​​.
(26)
For characters this says that the multiplicity of an irreducible G-character in an induced character equals the corresponding multiplicity after restriction. To prove the identity directly, insert the induced class function formula and interchange the two finite sums. For each x, substitute g=xhx−1 with h∈H. Since u is constant on conjugacy classes,
⟨fG,u⟩G​​=∣G∣∣H∣1​x∈G∑​h∈H∑​f(h)u(xhx−1)​=∣H∣1​h∈H∑​f(h)u(h)​=⟨f,uH​⟩H​.​
(27)
This proves the theorem for all class functions, not just irreducible characters.
For the group-theoretic conclusion, suppose that p divides ∣G′∩Z(G)∣. A central element of order p lies in every Sylow p-subgroup: adjoining it to a Sylow subgroup gives a p-subgroup, so maximality forces it into that subgroup. Hence there is a subgroup Q≤P∩G′∩Z(G) of order p. Choose a nontrivial linear character λ of Q.
Since P is abelian, λ extends to a linear character μ of P. One direct justification is to extend from an abelian subgroup A to ⟨A,x⟩ one generator at a time: if d is the least positive exponent with xd∈A, choose a complex dth root of λ(xd) as the value at x. The formula on axj is then well defined and multiplicative. Iteration yields the claimed extension to P.
The induced character μG has degree [G:P], which is prime to p. In its decomposition into irreducible characters, at least one constituent χ must have degree prime to p; otherwise the total degree would be divisible by p. Frobenius reciprocity also gives ⟨χP​,μ⟩P​>0 for this constituent. Restriction to the central subgroup Q forces
χQ​=χ(1)λ.
(28)
Indeed, the induced representation itself restricts to Q as copies of λ, because conjugation by every coset representative is trivial on Q; alternatively this follows from Schur's lemma and the positive multiplicity after restriction.
Each q∈Q consequently acts in the representation affording χ as the scalar λ(q). Taking its determinant character gives
(detχ)Q​=λχ(1).
(29)
The left side is trivial since Q≤G′ and every linear character kills G′. The right side is nontrivial since λ has order p and p∤χ(1). This contradiction proves
p∤∣G′∩Z(G)∣​.
(30)
The argument is the central commutator has no torsion from an abelian Sylow subgroup determinant obstruction.
