First check the ordered right cosets of a convex lattice subgroup. Replacing g,h by p1​g,p2​h changes a witness g≤ph into p1​g≤p1​pp2−1​(p2​h), so the relation is independent of representatives. Reflexivity uses p=1. If g≤ph and h≤qk, two-sided invariance of the partial order gives g≤pqk, proving transitivity. If both inequalities hold in opposite directions, then
g≤ph≤pqg,1≤phg−1≤pq.
(1)
Both endpoints are in P, so convexity gives phg−1∈P and hence hg−1∈P. Thus Pg=Ph, proving antisymmetry. The join and meet formulas are Pg∨Ph=P(g∨h) and Pg∧Ph=P(g∧h). For example, if g≤pk and h≤qk, then g∨h≤(p∨q)k, giving the least-upper-bound property.
The coset order is total exactly when P is a prime convex lattice subgroup. If it is total and a,b≥1 have a∧b=1, assume Pa≤Pb. The coset meet is then Pa=P(a∧b)=P, so a∈P. Conversely, for u=gh−1, its positive and negative parts in a lattice-ordered group satisfy u+∧u−=1. Primality puts one of them in P. If u+∈P, then u≤u+ gives Pg≤Ph; if u−∈P, use u−1≤u− to obtain Ph≤Pg.
It remains to identify the stated intersection condition. If P is prime and C,D properly contain it, choose positive c∈C∖P and d∈D∖P. The cosets Pc,Pd are strictly above P. Their meet is therefore strictly above P, so c∧d∈/P. But c∧d belongs to C∩D by convexity, showing C∩D=P.
If P is not prime, choose disjoint positive a,b∈/P. The principal convex lattice subgroups A=⟨a⟩c​ and B=⟨b⟩c​ have A∩B={1}. Indeed, disjoint positive elements commute, since a∨b=a(a∧b)−1b=ab=ba, and Riesz decomposition in a lattice-ordered group shows ar∧bs=1 for every positive r,s. Now the distributivity of the lattice of convex lattice subgroups gives
(P∨A)∩(P∨B)=P∨(A∩B)=P.
(2)
Both joins properly contain P, violating the intersection condition. This proves the requested equivalence.
