A finite CW complex can be defined without assuming separation properties in advance. Start with a finite discrete set X0. Inductively form Xk from Xk−1⊔∐α​Dαk​ by identifying each boundary point with its image under a continuous attaching map aα​:Sk−1→Xk−1. Only finitely many disks are attached in total, and X=XN for some finite N. The interiors of the disks are its cells. Its weak topology of a CW complex is the final topology of the characteristic maps: a subset is closed exactly when its inverse image in every characteristic disk is closed. In this finite construction it is the topology obtained by the successive quotients. Closure-finiteness is automatic because there are only finitely many cells.
Here is a direct proof of both requested topological properties, without presupposing that arbitrary attaching maps give a triangulable space. Suppose the compact previous stage Y embeds by e:Y↪Rm, and attach one k-cell, k≥1. Extend the boundary coordinate map radially by
G(ru)=re(a(u)),u∈Sk−1,G(0)=0.
(1)
It is continuous at zero because the image of the sphere is bounded. Map the two pieces into Rm+k+1 by
y⟼(e(y),0,0),x⟼(G(x),(1−∣x∣)x,1−∣x∣).
(2)
At ∣x∣=1 these agree with the attaching identification. They therefore induce a continuous map on the quotient. Its restriction to the old stage is injective. An interior point has positive last coordinate t=1−∣x∣, and the preceding coordinate block recovers x by division by t. Hence it cannot coincide with an old point or another interior point. The induced map is injective.
The quotient is compact, being the image of a compact disjoint union. A continuous injection into Euclidean space is Hausdorff, by inverse images of disjoint separating neighborhoods; it is also a homeomorphism onto its image because closed subsets of the compact source have compact, hence closed, images in the Hausdorff target. Finite discrete X0 embeds in R, and induction over the finite list of cells proves X is Hausdorff and its weak topology is exactly an induced Euclidean subspace topology. This is Euclidean embedding by finite cell attachment; no optimal ambient dimension is needed.
For Real projective space, filter by RP0⊂⋯⊂RPn. The complement of RPk−1 in RPk is an affine k-cell. A characteristic disk is a hemisphere of Sk, whose boundary is attached by the antipodal quotient Sk−1→RPk−1. Thus there is one cell in every dimension 0,…,n.
The incidence number is the sum of the two sheet contributions. Fixing the orientation of one sheet gives degree one; the other differs by the antipodal map on Sk−1, whose degree is (−1)k. Consequently the cellular homology of real projective space has
Ck​=Z(0≤k≤n),dk​=1+(−1)k={20​k even,k odd.​
(3)
Taking kernels modulo images gives, for n≥1,
Hq​(RPn;Z)=⎩⎨⎧​ZZ/2Z0​q=0,0<q<n and q odd,q=n and n odd,otherwise.​​
(4)
Over R, multiplication by two is invertible, so
Hq​(RPn;R)={R0​q=0, or q=n with n odd,otherwise.​​
(5)
Over F2​=Z/2, every differential vanishes, giving Hq​(RPn;F2​)=F2​ for 0≤q≤n, and zero otherwise. For n=0 the space is a point and only degree-zero homology is present, with the chosen coefficient group.

A map p:E→B is a Serre fibration if every homotopy H:Dk×I→B, k≥0, with a lift of its initial slice has a lift on the whole cylinder extending that initial lift. By attaching cells, this gives the relative homotopy lifting property for CW pairs: a specified lift on the initial slice and on the subcomplex cylinder can be extended.
Give PY the compact-open topology. Suppose the initial lifting is a family of paths γx​ starting at y0​, with γx​(1)=H(x,0). Define
H(x,s)(t)={γx​((1+s)t)H(x,(1+s)t−1)​0≤t≤(1+s)−1,(1+s)−1≤t≤1.​
(6)
The two expressions coincide on their common boundary, the initial path at s=0 is exactly γx​, and the endpoint is H(x,s). The adjoint map on Dk×I×I is continuous by pasting; compactness of the path parameter gives continuity into the path space. Thus endpoint evaluation PY→Y is a Serre fibration. The variable subdivision avoids changing the initial lift by an unwanted reparametrization.
For a based pair (E,F,e0​), the long exact sequence of relative homotopy groups is
⋯→πn​(F)i∗​​πn​(E)j∗​​πn​(E,F)∂​πn−1​(F)i∗​​πn−1​(E)→⋯,
(7)
ending in π1​(E)→π1​(E,F)→π0​(F)→π0​(E). The low-dimensional end is an exact sequence of pointed sets with its usual actions, rather than an entirely abelian sequence.
The boundary sends a relative disk map to its restriction on the boundary sphere. An absolute class has constant boundary, so ∂j∗​=0. Conversely, if a relative map has null-homotopic boundary in F, choose a based null-homotopy there. Attach this null-homotopy as a collar to the disk, leaving the original map on a smaller inner disk. The resulting boundary is constant at e0​, so it defines an absolute class in πn​(E); inserting the collar is a relative homotopy, so its image is the original relative class. This proves imj∗​=ker∂. At n=1, append a path in F from the terminal point to e0​, giving the corresponding based loop and pointed-set exactness.
Now suppose F=p−1(b0​). Use a cube model in which the bottom and side faces are based and the top face maps into F. A based n-cube in B lifts from its constant bottom face, with sides held at e0​ by relative lifting. Its top face lies in F, proving surjectivity of the relative homotopy of a Serre fibration comparison p∗​:πn​(E,F)→πn​(B,b0​).
For injectivity, lift a based homotopy of two projected cubes from the first relative lift; this ends at some lift of the second base cube. Any two lifts of that same base cube are relatively homotopic: insert an extra parameter between them, prescribe the two liftings on its two ends and the constant lift on the bottom, and apply relative lifting in the remaining cube direction. The top face stays in F because its projection is b0​. Combining these homotopies proves injectivity. Therefore
p∗​:πn​(E,F)≅​πn​(B,b0​)(n≥2).​
(8)
At n=1 the same construction gives a bijection of pointed sets, and one can transport the base fundamental-group law across it. An arbitrary pair's relative π1​ has no general group law. The printed target index a is a typographical error: the map preserves degree n.
The last-column map U(n)→S2n−1 is surjective because a unit vector can be completed to a unitary basis. Its stabilizer is the block subgroup diag(U(n−1),1), so it induces a continuous bijection U(n)/U(n−1)→S2n−1. Unitary matrices form a compact set in their matrix-space topology; the quotient is compact and the sphere Hausdorff. Hence
U(n)/U(n−1)≅S2n−1.​
(9)
This quotient is a fiber bundle. Near the last standard unit vector, project the other standard vectors by I−vv∗ onto v⊥ and apply Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization; their independence persists in a small neighborhood. The resulting orthonormal columns together with v give a continuous local section. Translating these sections covers the sphere and trivializes the projection, so it is a Serre fibration.
Apply this to U(n)→U(n+1)→S2n+1. Its homotopy sequence contains
πi+1​(S2n+1)→πi​(U(n))→πi​(U(n+1))→πi​(S2n+1).
(10)
For i≤2n−1, both outer groups vanish by cellular approximation theorem. Thus πi​(U(n))≅πi​(U(n+1)) in the stated range. The component assertion also holds: diagonalizing a unitary matrix and varying its eigenvalue phases continuously connects it to the identity. This establishes the unitary sphere quotient and stability range.

Interpret the connectivity hypothesis in its usual based sense: X is (n−2)-connected, with n≥2 for the nontrivial suspension range. Form the suspension as two cones C−​X and C+​X meeting in X. Both cones are contractible. Their relative homotopy sequences give πj​(C±​X,X)≅πj−1​(X) in the relevant degrees, so both cone pairs are (n−1)-connected.
The homotopy excision theorem says that if two CW pairs meeting in a connected subcomplex are p- and q-connected, p,q≥1, the map from the first relative pair to the union relative to the second part is an isomorphism in degrees i<p+q and a surjection in degree p+q. Here p=q=n−1, giving
πi​(C+​X,X)⟶πi​(ΣX,C−​X)
(11)
as an isomorphism for i<2n−2 and a surjection for i=2n−2. Contractibility identifies the domain with πi−1​(X) and the target with πi​(ΣX). The comparison under these identifications is the suspension map: a sphere representative is coned in each half and the two cones give its suspended sphere representative. Hence the Freudenthal suspension from two cones proves
πi−1​(X)→πi​(ΣX) is onto for i≤2n−2, and is an isomorphism for i<2n−2.​
(12)
For connected CW complexes, reduced and unreduced suspension have the same based homotopy type, so either standard suspension convention gives this result. In the low-dimensional range, pointed sets are used where necessary; the displayed positive-degree homomorphism assertion concerns the usual groups.
For Sr with r≥2, cellular approximation makes every sphere map of degree below r null-homotopic: it can be homotoped into the point skeleton of the CW structure having cells only in degrees zero and r. The Hurewicz theorem therefore identifies its first possible nonzero homotopy group with homology. Its cellular chain complex gives Hr​(Sr;Z)=Z. For r=1, lifting a based loop to the universal cover R→S1 classifies it by an integer endpoint displacement, so π1​(S1)=Z; the degree-one Hurewicz map is abelianization and is an isomorphism here. Thus
πr​(Sr)≅Hr​(Sr;Z)≅Z(r≥1).​
(13)
Alternatively, after the r=2 Hurewicz base case, the suspension isomorphisms above propagate the generator to every higher sphere.

Use finitely generated right projective modules throughout. The group K0 of a ring is the Grothendieck group of their direct-sum monoid, with [P⊕Q]=[P]+[Q]. The group K1 of a ring is GL∞​(A)/E∞​(A), equivalently the abelianization of the stable general linear group. Stabilization is g↦diag(g,1); E∞​ is generated by elementary transvections. The stable elementary subgroup is the commutator subgroup: elementary transvections are commutators using a third index, and stable block interchange and elementary row identities make the quotient abelian.
Write A0​ for the lower-right ring, printed as A′ in the source. A Milnor square has unital commuting maps, is a pullback
A={(a1​,a2​)∈A1​×A2​:j1​(a1​)=j2​(a2​)},
(14)
and has at least one of j1​,j2​ surjective. Interchange the two sides if necessary so that j1​ is surjective.
We first establish the patching fact used in the proof. For projective Pi​ and an isomorphism ϕ:P1​⊗A0​→P2​⊗A0​, set
M(P1​,P2​,ϕ)={(x1​,x2​):ϕ(xˉ1​)=xˉ2​}.
(15)
For free modules denote the result by Mg​. The block matrix identity
(g0​0g−1​)=W(g)W(−I),W(g)=(I0​gI​)(I−g−1​0I​)(I0​gI​)
(16)
expresses diag(g,g−1) as elementary matrices. Their entries lift through j1​, and their elementary lifts remain invertible. A change of basis on the first side then trivializes this gluing. Thus Mg​⊕Mg−1​≅A2r, proving that Mg​ is finitely generated projective. Notice that we lifted elementary matrices, not arbitrary invertible matrices.
For general P1​,P2​, choose P1​⊕Q1​≅A1r​ and P2​⊕Q2​≅A2s​. The reductions of Q1​⊕A1s​ and Q2​⊕A2r​ are isomorphic: insert the common reduced Pi​ and reorder the two complements. Patch them by such an isomorphism. The sum with M(P1​,P2​,ϕ) now patches two free modules of equal size, so is projective by the free case, making each summand projective. The same free change of basis followed by these direct-summand projections shows that extension to Ai​ recovers Pi​. Conversely, write an A-projective as the image of an idempotent over a finite free module. Since matrices over the pullback are compatible pairs of matrices, applying the compatible idempotents to the free pullback reconstructs this projective from its two reductions. Compatible module isomorphisms patch as well. This proves Milnor patching of projective modules in the form needed below.
The diagonal maps in the sequence are extension of scalars; the two maps to A0​ are differences j1∗​−j2∗​. Define the connecting map by
∂[g]=[Mg​]−[Ar],g∈GLr​(A0​).
(17)
It is unchanged by stabilization or elementary multiplication: an elementary factor lifts through j1​ and changes a free basis in the gluing. The identity diag(g,h)=diag(gh,I)diag(h−1,h) and the preceding elementary factorization show Mg​⊕Mh​≅Mgh​⊕Ar after matching sizes. Thus ∂ is an additive homomorphism on K1​(A0​).
Exactness at K1​(A1​)⊕K1​(A2​). If the two reduced classes agree, choose stabilized representatives g1​,g2​ of the same size. Their reductions differ by an elementary matrix e over A0​. Lift e to an elementary matrix over A1​ and adjust g1​ without changing its K1​ class. Its reduction now equals that of g2​. The compatible pair and its compatible inverse give an invertible matrix over A, so the original pair of classes is in the diagonal image. The reverse containment follows from commutativity of the square.
Exactness at K1​(A0​). A gluing obtained from a change of basis over either upper ring patches a free module, so the difference-map image is killed by ∂. Conversely, ∂[g]=0 means that Mg​ becomes free after adding a common projective summand. Add a complement of that summand to obtain an actual isomorphism Mg​⊕As≅Ar+s. Its two local bases express g⊕Is​=j2​(v2​)j1​(v1​)−1. Therefore [g]=j2∗​[v2​]−j1∗​[v1​], which lies in the image of the difference map. This uses stable equality of projective classes, not cancellation of arbitrary projectives.
Exactness at K0​(A). Connecting classes reduce to zero on both sides. Conversely, write a class in the kernel as [P]−[Ar] by adding a complement to its negative projective term. Its reductions have the same classes as Air​. Equality in K0​(Ai​) becomes an isomorphism after a finite common free summand, by taking a complement to the auxiliary projective. Use one sufficiently large free summand on both sides. The stabilized P is then locally free with gluing g, so the original class is [Mg​]−[Ar+s]=∂[g].
Exactness at K0​(A1​)⊕K0​(A2​). Write a kernel pair as ([P1​]−[A1r​],[P2​]−[A2r​]), using complements and padding to make the free sizes equal. Their reduced positive classes agree. Adding a common finite free summand makes those reductions isomorphic. Patch the resulting projectives, and subtract the matching free A-module. Its restrictions are the desired pair. The reverse containment is immediate because both reductions to A0​ agree.
These arguments prove the Mayer-Vietoris sequence in algebraic K-theory
K1​A→K1​A1​⊕K1​A2​→K1​A0​∂​K0​A→K0​A1​⊕K0​A2​→K0​A0​.​
(18)
No zero is inserted at either end; this fragment does not assert injectivity at the first term or surjectivity at the last.
For the group ring of the cyclic group, take the cyclotomic Milnor square
Z[Cp​]↓Z[ζ]​⟶⟶​Z↓Fp​.​
(19)
The maps send the cyclic generator to 1 and to ζ; the lower map sets ζ=1, and the right map reduces modulo p. Since Φp​(1)=p, Z[ζ]/(ζ−1)=Fp​. To verify the pullback, represent an element by a polynomial of degree at most p−1. If both evaluations vanish, it is a constant multiple of the minimal polynomial of an algebraic element Φp​, and evaluation at one forces that constant to be zero. Conversely, represent b∈Z[ζ] by f of degree below p−1. For a compatible integer a, a−f(1) is divisible by p; replacing f by f+[(a−f(1))/p]Φp​ yields the pair (a,b). The right map is surjective, so this is a Milnor square.
The hinted cyclotomic units are uk​=1+ζ+⋯+ζk−1. If kl≡1(modp) with 1≤l<p, the geometric sum ∑j=0l−1​ζkj is their inverse, because ζkl=ζ. Their residues are k∈Fp×​, covering every unit of the common field. Gaussian elimination identifies K1​(Fp​) with Fp×​ by determinant. Thus K1​(Z[ζ])→K1​(Fp​) is surjective, and the connecting map vanishes.
The remaining exact sequence injects K0​(Z[Cp​]) into Z⊕K0​(Z[ζ]), with image {(r,x):r=rankx}. Here K0​(Z)=K0​(Fp​)=Z, and reduction of a projective over the integral domain Z[ζ] has dimension its rank: localize at (ζ−1), where the projective is free, and compare its residue-field dimension with its fraction-field dimension. Projection onto the second factor is therefore a bijection on this kernel. Hence extension along the cyclotomic evaluation gives
K0​(Z[Cp​])≅K0​(Z[ζ]).​
(20)
The essential step is reduction of cyclotomic units at the ramified prime; surjectivity of a ring map alone would not imply surjectivity on K1​.

Work in the based CW homotopy category. A homotopy-invariant functor depends on a map only through its based homotopy class. In a sequence of pointed sets, exactness at the middle means that the preceding image equals the inverse image of the distinguished point; it does not imply injectivity or surjectivity at the ends. For groups this is the familiar image-equals-kernel condition. A half-exact functor preserves such middle exactness on the appropriate elementary sequence.
For a contravariant functor F, the relevant elementary sequence is a cofibration A↪X→X/A. Half-exactness is
F(X/A)⟶F(X)⟶F(A)
(21)
exact at F(X). One should not silently turn this into a short exact sequence. In particular, arbitrary group actions and pointed sets at low degrees are not interchangeable with abelian groups.
The basic contravariant example is FY​(X)=[X,Y]∗​, often called a cohomotopy-type functor. Suppose a map f:X→Y becomes null-homotopic on A. A chosen null-homotopy extends it over the cone attached to A, giving a map from the topological mapping cone to Y. Conversely every such extension restricts to a null-homotopic map on A. Since the topological mapping cone of a subcomplex inclusion models X/A, this proves half-exactness, rather than merely asserting it.
Repeating the topological mapping cone construction gives the Puppe sequence
A→X→Ci​→ΣA→ΣX→⋯.
(22)
Contravariant mapping into Y gives
⋯→[ΣX,Y]∗​→[ΣA,Y]∗​→[Ci​,Y]∗​→[X,Y]∗​→[A,Y]∗​.
(23)
This is exact as pointed sets. An H-group structure on Y, for example a loop space with path concatenation, provides group structures; repeated looping gives abelian group structures in the usual ranges. The suspension-loop adjunction [ΣX,Y]∗​≅[X,ΩY]∗​ explains the shifted terms and their compatibility.
The covariant counterpart is HT​(Y)=[T,Y]∗​, with ordinary homotopy groups obtained from T=Sn. Its natural elementary sequence is a homotopy fiber sequence F→E→B, not an arbitrary cofibration. A map a:T→E whose composite to B is null-homotopic, together with that null-homotopy, determines a map into the homotopy fibre. Conversely a fibre map projects to such a null composite. This proves exactness of [T,F]∗​→[T,E]∗​→[T,B]∗​ at the middle. Adding the loop terms produces the homotopy long exact sequence. For a Serre fibration, its ordinary fibre supplies the same homotopy information.
This variance distinction matters. Include one circle, generated by a, in S1∨S1, with other generator b. The quotient is the second circle. The kernel of π1​(S1∨S1)→π1​(S1) is the normal closure of a, which contains bab−1. The image of the included circle is only the cyclic subgroup generated by a, and does not contain that reduced word. Thus covariant π1​ is not half-exact on this cofibration. Fibre and cofibre statements cannot be swapped just by reversing notation.
Reduced cohomology gives group-valued contravariant examples. For an abelian coefficient group G, the degree-q functor is represented by an Eilenberg–MacLane space:
Hq(X;G)≅[X,K(G,q)]∗​.
(24)
Its cofibration exact sequence continues as ⋯→Hq−1(A)→Hq(X/A)→Hq(X)→Hq(A)→⋯. Ordinary homotopy groups are instead covariant, with decreasing group degree across a fibre connecting map. These are the two complementary representable patterns, maps out of a test object and maps into a representing object.
Brown's representability theorem makes the contravariant pattern structural. A reduced contravariant homotopy functor on based CW complexes is represented by a CW complex if it converts arbitrary wedges to products and satisfies the Mayer-Vietoris weak-pullback gluing axiom. The latter says that compatible classes on two subcomplexes extend to their union. It contains more information than the single kernel statement of half-exactness. Consequently half-exactness alone is not a representability criterion. In a representing functor these axioms are natural: a map on a wedge is a family of based maps, while compatible maps on an intersection can be adjusted by a homotopy and glued across a homotopy pushout.
A reduced generalized cohomology theory with the usual wedge axiom supplies these axioms degree by degree. Its representing spaces Eq​ have Eq​≃ΩEq+1​ by suspension compatibility, assembling an Omega-spectrum. Thus generalized cohomology can be expressed as Eq(X)=[Σ∞X,ΣqE]st​, while represented homology is Eq​(X)=πq​(E∧Σ∞X). The appearance of mapping into a spectrum in one case and smashing with it in the other is another form of the same variance distinction.
There is a literal duality after stabilization and a finiteness restriction. Here a finite based CW complex is identified with its suspension spectrum. For a finite spectrum X, let DX=F(X,S) be its Spanier-Whitehead dual. Then D2X≃X and [X,Y]st​≅[DY,DX]st​ for finite spectra. Duality reverses a cofibre triangle into a fibre triangle. In particular,
Eq(X)≅π−q​(E∧DX),Eq​(X)≅[ΣqDX,E]st​.
(25)
This turns a cohomology calculation on a finite object into a homology calculation on its dual, and vice versa. It is Spanier-Whitehead duality, not a claim that the full category of ordinary spaces is self-dual.
Half-exactness is the local image–kernel property; fibres and cofibres, representability, and finite stable duality explain how that property organizes homotopy and cohomology. The distinctions between variance, pointed sets and groups, and finite stable objects and arbitrary spaces are essential to using the duality correctly.
