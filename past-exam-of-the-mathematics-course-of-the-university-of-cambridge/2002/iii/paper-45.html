The cosmological principle asserts that, after averaging over sufficiently large scales, the universe is spatially homogeneous and isotropic on constant-cosmic time slices. Homogeneity means no distinguished location; isotropy means no distinguished spatial direction. These are statistical large-scale statements, not assertions that individual galaxies, clusters or the cosmic web are uniform, and they do not require the universe to be unchanging in time.
The nearly isotropic cosmic microwave background is strong evidence for large-scale isotropy about us. Its dominant dipole is explained by our peculiar velocity; the much smaller residual fluctuations provide the seeds and record of structure formation, rather than invalidating the principle. Large-area galaxy and radio-source distributions and deep galaxy surveys support statistical isotropy and a tendency towards statistical homogeneity after smoothing beyond the strongly clustered scales. An approximately isotropic Hubble flow and broadly uniform background radiation supply complementary checks.
There is an important logical distinction: isotropy seen by one observer alone does not prove homogeneity. A spherically symmetric inhomogeneous universe centered on that observer could look isotropic. Combining the observations with the assumption that our location is not privileged, so comparable observers would see comparable large-scale isotropy, motivates the homogeneous FLRW metric. Selection effects, foregrounds, finite survey volumes and the fact that observations lie on our past light cone must be accounted for. The evidence supports a large-scale statistical principle, not exact uniformity on every scale.

Standardized supernovae test the expansion history; they do not by themselves uniquely determine every cosmological parameter.

For the Zeldovich approximation, form the Jacobian matrix of the Lagrangian map:
∂qj​∂xi​​=δij​−b∂i​∂j​ψin​.
(21)
Let λ1​≥λ2​≥λ3​ be the eigenvalues of this potential Hessian matrix, with the convention that positive λ means compression. Before shell crossing, conservation of the mass in a Lagrangian volume gives
1+δ=det(∂x/∂q)1​=(1−bλ1​)(1−bλ2​)(1−bλ3​)1​.​
(22)
If λ1​>0 and is distinct from the other eigenvalues, the first singularity occurs at b=1/λ1​. One principal length collapses while the other two remain nonzero. The resulting cosmological caustic is locally a sheet, conventionally a pancake, rather than a simultaneous spherical point collapse. Degenerate equal eigenvalues can instead give line- or pointlike special cases.
The predicted density divergence signals failure of the single-stream description. If the straight Lagrangian displacements are simply continued, particles cross the sheet and keep moving along their original displacement directions. They are not slowed and trapped by the evolved self-gravitating multistream structure. The streams therefore separate again, producing excessively broad pancakes at late times. In an N-body simulation, the changing gravitational field pulls crossing streams back, produces repeated crossings and binds matter into more compact structures. This is the limitation described by Zeldovich pancake thickness after shell crossing. The initial-displacement extrapolation is useful through the onset of collapse, but does not supply the self-consistent post-crossing gravitational dynamics or virialization. After crossing, the physical density would require summing inverse absolute Jacobians over all streams, not using one negative determinant as a density.

In an Einstein-de Sitter universe, a slightly overdense spherical region initially expands nearly with the Hubble flow, with its early physical radius proportional to t2/3. Its extra self-gravity gradually slows its expansion relative to the background. The radius reaches a maximum at spherical-collapse turnaround, where the coherent radial velocity is zero; it then contracts. The ideal pressureless top-hat would collapse to a point, but real collisionless matter undergoes crossing and relaxation into a bound system with random motions and virial equilibrium.
The bound-shell solution illustrates the stages:
R=A(1−cosθ),t=B(θ−sinθ),A3=GMB2.

At θ=π, Rm​=2A and tm​=πB; the formal cold collapse occurs at θ=2π, tc​=2tm​. For a uniform isolated sphere, let U=−3GM2/(5R). At turnaround Km​=0, whereas the final virial theorem gives 2Kv​+Uv​=0. Conservation of energy gives Um​=Kv​+Uv​=Uv​/2, hence
Rv​=Rm​/2.​

This is the potential fluctuations per logarithmic wavenumber scaling. A simple dimensional argument gives the same answer: the density amplitude on scale R is proportional to R−(n+3)/2, and its potential amplitude is of order Gρˉ​(aR)2δR​, supplying two additional powers of R.
For n=1, the Harrison-Zeldovich spectrum has scale-independent potential amplitude per logarithmic interval. If the density-spectrum amplitude grows as A(t)=b2(t)Ain​, then 4πGa2ρˉ​=C/a, so the potential amplitude evolves as b/a. It is constant in the pure growing matter mode of an Einstein-de Sitter universe, where b=a.
A literal total variance would be proportional to ∫0∞​kn−2dk. Infrared convergence requires n>1 and ultraviolet convergence requires n<1, so no index makes both ends finite. At n=1 both divergences are logarithmic. Thus cutoffs or a band specification are essential for a numerical rms; the boxed statement describes the intended scale-local fluctuations rather than claiming a finite unsmoothed potential variance.

The Press-Schechter formalism starts from a statistically homogeneous Gaussian initial density contrast with variance specified by its linear matter power spectrum. Average it on mass scale M=(4π/3)ρˉ​m0​R3, usually with a spherical top-hat. Let σ(M,t)=b(t)σ0​(M), normalizing b consistently. Assume that a region becomes a collapsed halo when its linearly extrapolated smoothed density exceeds a mass-independent linear spherical-collapse threshold δmsc​≃1.686 in the Einstein-de Sitter approximation. This extrapolates a linear Gaussian statistic to a nonlinear collapse criterion, and ignores the detailed nonspherical dynamics.
The one-scale Gaussian upper tail only assigns half the mass to overdense regions when the variance grows without limit. The conventional factor of two is inserted to account for material in larger collapsed regions even if it falls below the threshold on a smaller scale, the cloud-in-cloud issue. A first-crossing excursion-set argument can justify that factor for an ideal sharp-k filter; it is not a theorem that an arbitrary top-hat one-scale probability alone supplies it. With this prescription, the mass fraction in objects above M is
F(>M,t)=erfc(2​σ(M,t)δmsc​​).
(47)
Convert mass fraction to comoving number density by assigning mass M to each halo, using dn/dM=(ρˉ​m0​/M)∣dF/dM∣. This yields the Press-Schechter halo mass function
dMdn​=π2​​M2ρˉ​m0​​ν​dlnMdlnσ​​e−ν2/2,ν=b(t)σ0​(M)δmsc​​.​
(48)
Equivalently one can use a present-extrapolated threshold δc​(t)=δmsc​/b(t) and present variance; using both that threshold and an already grown variance would double-count growth. The model assumes approximate mass conservation, a smoothing-to-halo-mass assignment and a spherical universal barrier. It is an approximation to gravitational halo formation, not an exact partition of the real cosmic web.
In hierarchical formation, σ0​(M) decreases with mass: smaller regions become nonlinear first and are subsequently incorporated into larger haloes. Define a characteristic mass by b(t)σ0​(M∗​)=δmsc​. It increases as growth proceeds, moving the exponential cutoff towards larger masses. For a scale-free spectrum with well-defined top-hat variance, −3<n<1, σ0​∝M−(n+3)/6 and hence M∗​∝b6/(n+3). At a fixed mass, the derivative of ln(dn/dM) with respect to lnb is ν2−1: rare high-mass abundances grow, while the small-mass abundance can decline as objects merge into larger systems. Thus one must not claim that every fixed-mass bin increases forever. The growth of M∗​ is the scale-free characteristic mass in the Press-Schechter formalism.
For the temperature test, virial equilibrium and gas hydrostatic equilibrium give kB​T∼μmp​GM/R. Defining the halo radius by M=(4π/3)Δv​ρc​(z)R3 yields
T∝M2/3[Δv​ρc​(z)]1/3.
(49)
A calibrated monotone mass-temperature relation converts the mass function to dn/dT=(dn/dM)∣dM/dT∣, with temperature scatter treated by convolution when necessary. The cluster temperature function normalization is strongly sensitive to the linear fluctuation amplitude: at fixed mass and spectral shape, ∂lnn/∂lnσ8​=ν2−1. Rare hot clusters occupy the exponential tail, so even a modest amplitude change can produce a large abundance change. Here sigma eight is the rms linear density contrast today in an 8h−1Mpc top-hat, not the full spectrum.
Compare observed cluster-temperature counts and their redshift evolution with these predictions to constrain that normalization jointly with matter density and growth. The mass-temperature calibration, selection volume, temperature scatter, nonthermal support and deviations from the ideal halo mass function limit a temperature-only inference. The abundance of rare hot clusters is a sensitive amplitude probe once their mass calibration and cosmology are specified.
