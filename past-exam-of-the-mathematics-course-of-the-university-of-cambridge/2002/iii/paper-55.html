Write H=L/cp​, k=ρcp​κ and ϵ=D/κ​. The requested model neglects density differences and uses the same thermal transport coefficients in the interfacial balances. Let Cb​=C(b(t),t) and Tb​=Tm​−mCb​. With no imposed heat gradient in the fresh liquid, its temperature remains Tm​. The prescribed linear temperature in the ice is
Ti​(z,t)=Tm​+(Tb​−Tm​)b−az−a​.
(1)
The salt and temperature fields in the lower liquid satisfy diffusion equations, Ct​=DCzz​ and Tt​=κTzz​. For constant interfacial values and the proposed similarity solution, their decaying solutions are
C(z,t)Tl​(z,t)​=C0​+(Cb​−C0​)erfc(λb​)erfc[z/(2Dt​)]​,=T∞​+(Tb​−T∞​)erfc(ϵλb​)erfc[z/(2κt​)]​.​
(2)
These follow by substituting a variable z/(2χt​) into the diffusion equation, which gives U′′+2ηU′=0; integrating gives the complementary error function. Their values at b and at infinity give the required boundary data.
For the upward-moving fresh-water/ice boundary, the Stefan condition is ρLa˙=kTi,z​: negative a˙ means fresh water freezes. At the lower boundary the signed heat balance is
ρLb˙=k[Ti,z​−Tl,z​]z=b​.
(3)
Salt cannot enter the pure ice. With no bulk liquid motion its interfacial conservation law is
DCz​(b,t)=−Cb​b˙.
(4)
The derivatives and interface velocities are
a˙=λa​D/t​,b˙=λb​D/t​,Ti,z​=−2(λb​−λa​)Dt​Tm​−Tb​​,
(5)
Tl,z​(b)=−πκt​erfc(ϵλb​)(Tb​−T∞​)e−(ϵλb​)2​,Cz​(b)=−πDt​erfc(λb​)(Cb​−C0​)e−λb2​​.
(6)
Define F(x)=π​xex2erfcx. Substitution into all three balances gives
2Hϵ2λa​=−λb​−λa​Tm​−Tb​​,​
(7)
H=F(ϵλb​)Tb​−T∞​​−2ϵ2λb​(λb​−λa​)Tm​−Tb​​,F(λb​)=Cb​Cb​−C0​​.​
(8)
Together with Tb​=Tm​−mCb​, these determine the interfacial constants. In particular Cb​=C0​/[1−F(λb​)].
The physical branch has λa​<λb​<0. Since F is negative at negative arguments, the salt relation gives 0<Cb​<C0​: lower-surface melting dilutes the brine. Combining the two heat balances eliminates the ice gradient and gives
Tb​−T∞​=Hλb​λb​−λa​​F(ϵλb​)>0.
(9)
Thus Tm​>Tb​>T∞​>TL​(C0​). The interface is at local liquidus equilibrium with diluted brine, while the undiluted far-field brine remains above its own liquidus. The similarity model describes a layer starting with zero thickness, or the long-time limit when an initial finite thickness no longer sets the dominant scale.
For the small-diffusivity-ratio limit, put A=ϵλa​ and Δ=Tm​−T∞​, keeping S=H/Δ and the imposed concentrations fixed. Seek A=O(1) and λb​=O(1). Then F(ϵλb​)∼π​ϵλb​ and λb​−λa​∼−λa​. The upper heat balance gives
Tm​−Tb​∼2HA2.
(10)
In the lower balance the two terms of order ϵ−1 must cancel, giving
π​Tb​−T∞​​+2ATm​−Tb​​=0.
(11)
Consequently
Tb​−T∞​∼1−2A/π​Δ​,​
(12)
and Tb​−T∞​∼−π​HA. Adding the two temperature differences yields 2A2−π​A=1/S. The negative root is
4ϵλa​∼π​−π+8/S​.​
(13)
The resulting Cb​=(Tm​−Tb​)/m lies strictly between zero and C0​. The salt relation therefore determines a finite negative λb​, confirming its O(1) scaling. The top interface travels on the thermal scale κt​, whereas the lower melting interface travels on the shorter solutal scale Dt​. This is the brine-ice sandwich with disparate diffusion scales; S is the latent-to-sensible heat ratio, the reciprocal of the usual sensible-to-latent Stefan number convention.
The sketches show constant fresh-water temperature, a declining linear ice temperature, and a much broader thermal than solutal boundary layer in the brine. Concentration is zero in freshwater and pure ice, jumps to Cb​ across the lower interface, and rises to C0​. Accordingly the local liquidus is Tm​ on the salt-free side, jumps to Tb​ and falls rapidly to TL​(C0​). In the phase diagram, the brine starts on the liquidus at (Cb​,Tb​) and ends above it at (C0​,T∞​); the pure ice has concentration zero, and the interface tie-line joins it to the diluted brine.
Physically, the cold saline liquid permits freshwater to freeze above, but salt lowers the liquidus and allows the existing ice to melt into brine below even though that brine is colder than Tm​. Dilution raises the lower interfacial freezing temperature. Heat released by upper freezing supplies the lower melting and the conductive loss to the colder deep brine. With ∣λa​∣≫∣λb​∣, upper freezing exceeds lower melting and the ice layer thickens as b−a∝t​.

Let the surface advance downwards at speed V>0. Ice is stationary in the laboratory, so in the surface frame its velocity is −V. The steady heat equation with volumetric heating is therefore
kT′′+ρcp​VT′+q0​e−λz=0,T(0)=Ts​,T(∞)=T∞​.
(14)
Take the atmospheric heat flux to be negligible in the stated model. The upward conductive flux at the ice surface supplies the latent heat of sublimation, giving the Stefan condition kT′(0)=ρLV.
Write p=V/κ and Δ=Ts​−T∞​. For p=λ, solve the constant-coefficient equation to obtain
T(z)=T∞​+Δe−pz+kλ(p−λ)q0​​(e−λz−e−pz).​
(15)
For the resonant case p=λ, its continuous limit is
T(z)=T∞​+[Δ+kpq0​z​]e−pz.​
(16)
An especially transparent way to find V is to integrate the heat equation over the half-space. Its far-field gradient vanishes, so
−kT′(0)+ρcp​V(T∞​−Ts​)+λq0​​=0.
(17)
Using the surface heat balance gives
V=ρL+ρcp​(Ts​−T∞​)q0​/λ​.​
(18)
Absorbed radiation supplies both the latent heat and the sensible heat needed to bring incoming cold ice to the surface temperature. This is the radiatively heated sublimation profile.
The surface gradient is
G=T′(0)=kρLV​>0.
(19)
Thus temperature first rises into the solid, but ultimately falls to the colder far field: it has a subsurface maximum. For a useful dimensionless form, set x=λz, r=p/λ and S=L/(cp​Δ). Then
ΔT−T∞​​=r−1r(S+1)e−x−(rS+1)e−rx​,
(20)
with limit [1+(S+1)x]e−x when r=1. The maximum is at x∗​=[log((rS+1)/(S+1))]/(r−1), or S/(S+1) at r=1.
Melting begins below the surface if the interior maximum reaches Tm​. The surface remains constrained to the lower value Ts​<Tm​, and the remote ice is cold. Increasing absorbed heating can raise the interior maximum to the melting point, after which an all-solid steady solution is no longer valid and an internal melt region must be modeled. The maximum, rather than the far-field temperature or surface temperature alone, decides onset.
For the linear stability analysis, perturb the mean surface by η=η^​eσt+iαx, with ice on z>η. Approximate the base temperature locally by Ts​+Gz. The prescribed radiative source has no perturbation, so the perturbation heat equation is homogeneous. Choose outward curvature K≃ηxx​, so the Gibbs-Thomson effect gives local equilibrium temperature Ts​−ΓK. If the perturbation temperature is T^(z)eσt+iαx, its surface condition is
T^(0)+Gη^​=Γα2η^​.
(21)
In the quasi-static thermal limit, T^′′−α2T^=0, hence
T^(z)=(Γα2−G)η^​e−αz.
(22)
Linearizing the Stefan condition, with the base gradient taken constant, gives ρLση^​=kT^′(0). Therefore
σ≃ρLk​α(G−Γα2).​
(23)
Positive G amplifies long-wave shape perturbations, while capillarity suppresses sufficiently short waves. The unstable band is 0<α<G/Γ​, and the approximate fastest mode is α∗​=G/(3Γ)​. This is the sublimation-front capillary instability.
The qualifications on wavelength matter. Retaining the perturbation's time dependence and mean recession gives
κT^′′+VT^′−(κα2+σ)T^=0,
(24)
whose decay constant is pα​=[V+V2+4κ(κα2+σ)​]/(2κ). Replacing it by α requires
καV​≪1,κα2∣σ∣​≪1.​
(25)
Writing H=L/cp​, the second condition becomes ∣G−Γα2∣≪Hα under the approximate dispersion relation. A simple sufficient range is V/κ≪α≪H/Γ; for unstable modes the lower inequality already controls thermal time dependence. Large S alone does not justify arbitrarily small α.
Finally the local linear background is appropriate only if the perturbation penetration depth is shorter than its curvature length: α≫∣T0′′​(0)∣/G. From the steady equation this ratio is V/κ+λ(1+1/S). Thus for the actual exponentially heated profile, a sufficient local range at large S is also α≫V/κ+λ. Otherwise evaluation of the base gradient at the displaced surface contributes an omitted term kT0′′​(0)/(ρL), and the simple quoted dispersion relation need not describe those long waves.

Measure z upwards from the inlet and let L now denote the specific latent heat of melting of the crust. Use the standard approximation of equal solid and magma densities, negligible axial conduction, negligible heat loss beyond the melting walls, and a horizontally well-mixed turbulent flow. The wall is at Tm​, so newly added wall melt enters with zero excess temperature θ=T−Tm​.
For a slice of dike, total magma mass per unit height and out-of-plane length is ρb. Both melting walls add mass at rate ρbt​. Consequently mass conservation gives
ρbt​+(ρbw)z​=ρbt​,bw=Q0​.​
(26)
The growth of channel storage and mass supplied by melting cancel; omitting the right-hand source would incorrectly force the inlet flux to fill this volume without accounting for the newly melted rock. Unequal densities would instead give (bw)z​=(ρs​/ρ−1)bt​ and change the similarity model.
Let qw​=αT​ρcp​wθ be heat transfer to each wall. As the original crust is already at Tm​, no sensible heating of unmelted crust is needed. Each wall moves outwards at speed bt​/2, so its Stefan condition is
ρL2bt​​=qw​,bt​=L2αT​cp​​wθ.​
(27)
Excess sensible-heat conservation, including both walls, is
∂t​(bθ)+∂z​(bwθ)=−2αT​wθ.​
(28)
Combining these equations, with H=L/cp​, gives
bTt​+Q0​Tz​=−[H+(T−Tm​)]bt​.​
(29)
The term (T−Tm​)bt​ is the heat used to warm freshly melted wall material. It cannot simply be dropped from a conservative balance. This is melt-dilution heat balance in a dike.
At the inlet bw=Q0​ and θ=Δ0​=T0​−Tm​. The wall balance there gives b(0,t)2=b02​+4αT​Q0​t/S, where S=L/(cp​Δ0​). At late times neglect b0​ in the widened basal region and set
B(t)=(S4αT​Q0​t​)1/2,η=B(t)2αT​z​,b=B(t)f(η),θ=Δ0​ϑ(η),w=B(t)f(η)Q0​​.
(30)
The basal width and the vertical scale both grow as t​. Substituting into the wall condition gives
f(f−ηf′)=ϑ.​
(31)
Conservative heat balance first gives fϑ−η(fϑ)′+Sϑ′=−Sϑ/f. Eliminating f−ηf′ with the wall equation yields
(S−ηf)ϑ′=−(S+ϑ)fϑ​.​
(32)
The boundary conditions are f(0)=1, ϑ(0)=1, with f→0 in the outer idealized limit; the physical cooled branch also has ϑ→0. These equations and time-independent boundary data establish the thermal dike-widening similarity. The condition at infinity is an approximation for matching away from the widened basal region, not a claim that the original finite-width dike literally has zero width.
The inlet is a regular singular point of the first equation. Differentiating it and evaluating the second gives
f′(0)=ϑ′(0)=−SS+1​.
(33)
A regular expansion can still contain an undetermined higher-order coefficient, for example in f′′(0); the outer matching condition selects it. Thus specifying the inlet values does not eliminate the boundary-value aspect of the problem. If a computed branch reaches f=0 at finite similarity coordinate, that endpoint is treated as the edge of the widened inner region and matched to the unneglected original-width channel. One must not continue w=Q0​/(Bf) through zero width as a physical flow.
For numerical solution, formulate this as a nonlinear two-point boundary-value problem: start from a regular inlet expansion and adjust its free coefficient to meet the outer decay/matching condition, or solve for the profiles and boundary data together on a truncated or compactified similarity interval. Enforce nonnegative width and temperature excess, check any apparent singularity at S=ηf, and refine the outer matching location until the basal profiles are insensitive to it. No specific algorithm is required.
A useful conservation check follows by adding the latent storage to the sensible storage:
∂t​{b(H+θ)}+Q0​θz​=0.
(34)
For a vanishing outer excess-heat flux, integration of the similarity profile gives
∫f(η)[S+ϑ(η)]dη=2S​,​
(35)
where the integral runs over its active widened region. This checks the heat admitted at the base against latent and sensible storage. All factors of two refer to the two channel walls; b is the full width.
