Assume a closed population with even size N, unrelated founders, monogamous mating, nonoverlapping generations and exactly two offspring per couple. Sex labels are exchangeable for the autosomal calculation, with enough individuals of each sex to form the stipulated pairs. A randomly chosen distinct offspring pair are siblings with probability 1/(N−1): among the (2N​) pairs, exactly N/2 are within-family pairs. This fixed-family-size fact, rather than independent sampling of all parents, determines the recurrence.
First give the elementary chromosome-sharing idealization consistent with taking self-relatedness as one and ordinary sibling relatedness as 1/2. In this model each individual carries one ancestral label, passed through one randomly chosen parent. Let rG​ be mean sharing between distinct individuals, and let mG​ be the mean sharing between mates. Siblings share their parental label with probability (1+mG​)/2. The average relatedness between parents from different couples is
uG+1​=N−2(N−1)rG​−mG​​.
(50)
To see the latter count, the ordered pairs from different couples are all ordered distinct pairs minus the N ordered mating pairs. Each pair of parents from different couples contributes equally to the four possible cross-family offspring relationships. Combining sibling and nonsibling offspring gives
rG+1​=rG​+2(N−1)1−mG​​.​
(51)
With random mating, mG​=rG​ in expectation, so r0​=0 gives
rG​=1−(1−2(N−1)1​)G.​
(52)
The mean sharing tends to one, on a generation scale of order 2(N−1). It is not linear indefinitely: repeated ancestry saturates the probability. This is an exact result for the stated one-label approximation, not an exact diploid formula after substantial inbreeding has developed.
Now prohibit full-sibling matings, with N≥4 and representative sampling of allowed nonsibling mating pairs. The following closed recurrences assume that opposite-sex mating pairs have the same mean autosomal sharing as all nonsibling pairs; a sex-specific or assortative scheme requires separate mating means. In the exchangeable one-label model write uG​ for mean relatedness of a nonsibling pair, so mG​=uG​. The two required recurrences are
rG+1​=rG​+2(N−1)1−uG​​,uG+1​=N−2(N−1)rG​−uG​​,r0​=u0​=0.​
(53)
The second relation includes sharing through grandparents and more distant ancestors; setting uG​=0 forever would be wrong. For vG​=1−rG​, wG​=1−uG​, the linear recurrence matrix is
(vG+1​wG+1​​)=(1(N−1)/(N−2)​−1/[2(N−1)]−1/(N−2)​)(vG​wG​​).
(54)
Its eigenvalues are
λ±​=2(N−2)N−3±N2−4N+5​​,
(55)
both of magnitude less than one for N≥4. Thus rG​→1 even when siblings never mate. Explicitly, 1−rG​=Aλ+G​+(1−A)λ−G​, where A=[1−1/(2(N−1))−λ−​]/(λ+​−λ−​). Sibling avoidance prevents immediate close-relative matings, but it does not make the remaining population unrelated. In a fixed-two-offspring model it need not reduce average pairwise sharing at every generation: a smaller mating mean actually increases the displayed increment in rG​.
For a fully diploid formulation, self-kinship also changes with inbreeding and cannot remain artificially fixed. Let ΦG​ be mean kinship coefficient between distinct individuals, FG​ mean inbreeding coefficient, and Φm,G​ mean kinship of mates. A sibling pair has kinship (1+FG​)/4+Φm,G​/2, because parental self-kinship is (1+FG​)/2. Repeating the pair count yields finite-population chromosomal coancestry:
ΦG+1​=ΦG​+4(N−1)1+FG​−2Φm,G​​,FG+1​=Φm,G​.​
(56)
Initially Φ0​=F0​=0. For random mating take Φm,G​=ΦG​. An explicit solution is the affine matrix recurrence
(1−ΦG​1−FG​​)=(1−1/[2(N−1)]1​1/[4(N−1)]0​)G(11​).
(57)
Its characteristic polynomial is λ2−[1−1/(2(N−1))]λ−1/[4(N−1)]; its two roots have magnitude less than one. Hence ΦG​,FG​→1. The normalized relationship 2ΦG​/(1+FG​) tends to one, while unnormalized additive relationship 2ΦG​ tends to two, demonstrating why that coefficient cannot always be read literally as a probability.
With sibling avoidance replace Φm,G​ by the nonsibling mean UG​ and add
UG+1​=N−2(N−1)ΦG​−UG​​,U0​=0.
(58)
These equations compute every generation's kinship and inbreeding, including delayed consanguinity through cousins. Under random allowed mating in a connected closed population, backward ancestral gene-copy lineages have a positive chance to meet in each sufficiently long block of generations; repeated blocks force eventual coalescence. Thus sibling avoidance alone does not prevent long-term identity by descent and genetic drift. Maintaining distinct isolated breeding groups, introducing immigrants, or changing offspring-number variance gives a different model. The elementary exponential formula answers the fixed-self-sharing approximation; the diploid recurrences give the consistent inbreeding-aware answer.
