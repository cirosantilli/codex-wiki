Use the conventional additive pedigree relatedness rUV​=2ϕUV​, where ϕUV​ is the kinship coefficient, the probability that randomly selected gene copies from U and V are identical by descent. For noninbred individuals this convention gives ordinary full siblings relatedness 1/2, as required. It is important that this is not the probability of sharing at least one copy: that probability is 3/4 for ordinary full siblings.
Let the parents be M,F. They are otherwise outbred first cousins, so ϕMF​=1/16 and ϕMM​=ϕFF​=1/2. For two children C,D, independent Mendelian segregation gives
ϕCD​=41​(ϕMM​+ϕMF​+ϕFM​+ϕFF​)=329​.
(46)
Therefore the autosomal additive relatedness is
rCD​=9/16.​
(47)
The additional 1/16 beyond 1/2 comes from maternal-paternal cross-origin matches. Each child has inbreeding coefficient 1/16. The additive coefficient is a sum of descent probabilities rather than a literal bounded probability in all inbred pedigrees; a relationship normalized to self-relatedness would be rCD​/(1+F)=9/17. Likewise, identity-by-descent sharing of inbred full siblings gives a different matched-copy statistic. The exam's calibration to 1/2 supports the additive convention, but the quoted probability wording needs this qualification.
For sex chromosomes the route of cousinship matters, not merely the fact that the parents have opposite sex. Let PM​ be the mother-cousin's parent in the shared sibling pair, and PF​ the father-cousin's parent in that pair. Let FX​ be the probability that the mother's randomly transmitted X matches the father's single X by descent. With no founder inbreeding, the possibilities are
PM​malefemalemalefemale​PF​malemalefemalefemale​FX​001/83/16​​
(48)
In the first two rows the father-cousin is related through his father, whose X he does not inherit. In the third row his mother is a sister of the mother-cousin's father: the relevant brother and sister match their maternal X copies with probability 1/4, and the mother-cousin transmits her paternal X with probability 1/2, giving 1/8. In the last row the two sisters' random X gametes match with probability 3/8: their common father's X contributes 1/4, and their common mother's two X copies contribute 1/8. Transmission by the mother-cousin halves this to 3/16. This derives the sex-linked cousin kinship values.
For an unweighted comparison by chromosome pair, two daughters have sex-pair additive relatedness 3/4+FX​: their paternal X always matches, maternal X matches with probability 1/2, and the two cross-origin terms each contribute FX​. Two sons have mean sex-pair relatedness 3/4, from an X match of probability 1/2 and a common paternal Y with probability one. A daughter-son pair has sex-pair value 1/4+FX​/2, with the Y contribution zero. These calculations treat chromosomes as unrecombined blocks and weight X and Y equally; chromosome-length-weighted or locus-specific coefficients are different.
For two children with independent equally likely sexes, these three cases have weights 1/4,1/4,1/2. Their mean sex-pair relatedness is 1/2+FX​/2, so averaging the 22 autosomal pairs and the sex pair gives
rgenome​=2322(9/16)+1/2+FX​/2​.​
(49)
If the cousins' route has FX​=0, this is 103/184, slightly below 9/16. If, as an additional sampling assumption, the four parent-sex routes in the table are equally likely, EFX​=5/64 and the answer is 1653/2944. These are different legitimate numerical corrections under different specified pedigrees. There is no unique sex-chromosome correction from opposite-sex cousinhood alone. The table and formula give it once the missing pedigree and offspring-sex information is supplied.
