Use metric signature (+,−,…,−) and initially set ℏ=1. For an internal scalar line with momentum p, the Feynman propagator is i/(p2−m2+i0). An r-leg Feynman vertex, for r=3,4,6, contributes −iλr​, with all incident momenta incoming. Its 1/r! in the Lagrangian density is canceled by the permutations of its identical fields. Each vertex conserves momentum and supplies (2π)dδ(d)(∑p). Integrate each independent loop momentum with ddk/(2π)d, multiply the line and vertex factors, and divide by the Feynman-diagram symmetry factor for labeled external legs. Sum the distinct graphs and external-momentum assignments without counting the same assignment twice.
An unamputated momentum-space correlation function also contains the external propagators. Dividing the generating functional by its zero-source value removes disconnected vacuum bubbles; taking its logarithm selects connected correlation functions. Amputation of external propagators removes the external line factors when computing vertex amplitudes. Translational invariance leaves one overall momentum-conservation delta function for each connected graph.
The loop order is the number of independent graph cycles, equivalently the number of independent momentum integrations left after vertex conservation. A spanning tree through V interaction vertices contains V−1 internal edges; hence, with I internal lines,
L=I−V+1.​
(1)
Restoring ℏ in exp(iS/ℏ) gives ℏ for each internal propagator and ℏ−1 for each interaction vertex. Thus an amputated connected amplitude has
ℏI−V=ℏL−1.​
(2)
The same counting holds for a diagram in logZ[J] when the external source is coupled as Jϕ/ℏ: every external propagator brings ℏ, canceled by the source insertion's ℏ−1. This is Planck-constant loop counting with source normalization. An ordinary connected expectation of n fields, with no such source factors, instead scales as ℏL+n−1; multiplying logZ by −iℏ to form W shifts its source-kernel counting to ℏL. The normalization must be specified when applying the printed loop rule.
Let Vr​ count r-valent interaction vertices. Counting their incident half-edges gives 2I+n=3V3​+4V4​+6V6​. Substituting the loop identity yields
L−1+2n​=21​V3​+V4​+2V6​,V=V3​+V4​+V6​.
(3)
Each coefficient on the right lies between 1/2 and 2, proving the vertex-count bounds for bounded scalar valence
2V​≤L−1+2n​≤2V.​
(4)
The free two-point term, which has no interaction vertex, separately satisfies the bounds with V=L=0 and n=2.
A one-particle-irreducible Feynman diagram is connected and remains connected after any single internal line is cut. The left graph below contains two quartic vertices and two parallel internal lines: cutting either line leaves the vertices connected by the other. The right graph contains two cubic vertices joined by one internal line, whose removal disconnects it, so it is a one-particle-reducible Feynman diagram. Both have four external legs.
The quantum effective action generates the one-particle-irreducible vertices. Their ultraviolet divergences and those of their proper divergent subgraphs are removed by local counterterms. Reducible connected graphs are reconstructed by joining these vertices with full propagators; they do not introduce independent local interaction counterterms merely because they contain bridges. This makes the one-particle-irreducible Feynman diagram decomposition useful for organizing renormalization. Overall power counting still has to be supplemented by subtraction of divergent subgraphs.
For a scalar with a two-derivative kinetic term, mass dimensions obey
[ϕ]=2d−2​,[λr​]=d−r2d−2​.
(5)
The general power-counting criterion used here is that interactions of nonnegative coupling dimension can be included in a perturbatively renormalizable quantum field theory, with all symmetry-allowed required local counterterms included; negative-dimensional couplings generally require an unbounded tower of higher-dimensional counterterms. Indeed, the scalar graph power-counting identity gives
D=d−2d−2​n−∑r=3,4,6​[λr​]Vr​
(6)
for the superficial degree of divergence. Nonnegative coupling dimensions prevent this degree from increasing indefinitely with the vertex count.
The coupling dimensions are
d346​[λ3​]3/210​[λ4​]10−2​[λ6​]0−2−6​allowed displayed interactionsϕ3,ϕ4,ϕ6ϕ3,ϕ4ϕ3​​
(7)
Thus all three are power-counting renormalizable in three dimensions; cubic and quartic in four; cubic only in six. Positive-dimensional interactions are super-renormalizable, while the zero-dimensional ones are marginal by classical power counting. Usual kinetic, mass, vacuum and, when no symmetry forbids it, linear counterterms must also be admitted. This conclusion concerns perturbative ultraviolet renormalizability; it does not assert global stability of a cubic scalar potential.

A linear operator on finite-dimensional vectors acts by (Au)i​=∑j​Aij​uj​. For a field the discrete index becomes a continuous coordinate, and a suitable operator is represented by an integral kernel, possibly a distribution:
(Aϕ)(x)=∫ddyA(x,y)ϕ(y),(AB)(x,y)=∫ddzA(x,z)B(z,y).
(10)
The identity operator has kernel δ(d)(x−y). If an inverse exists on the chosen domain and with the chosen boundary prescription, its kernel satisfies
∫ddzA(x,z)A−1(z,y)=δ(d)(x−y),​
(11)
with the analogous reversed identity. A differential operator is a distributional kernel: for example, A(x,y)=(−□x​−m2)δ(d)(x−y). Its inverse is a Green function. Boundary conditions, a causal prescription and the treatment of zero modes are part of the inverse's definition, not consequences of formal matrix notation. An operator with an untreated zero mode does not have an inverse on the full field space.
The functional derivative is defined through the first variation: for arbitrary smooth test variation h,
F[ϕ+εh]−F[ϕ]=ε∫ddxδϕ(x)δF​h(x)+o(ε).
(12)
In particular,
δϕ(y)δϕ(x)​=δ(d)(x−y),δϕ(y)δ​∫ddxJ(x)ϕ(x)=J(y).
(13)
For a symmetric kernel, differentiating F[ϕ]=21​∫dxdyϕ(x)A(x,y)ϕ(y) gives δF/δϕ=Aϕ. The two terms from varying the two fields coincide; for a nonsymmetric kernel the symmetric part (A+AT)/2 appears instead. Integration by parts is understood when A is differential and the boundary variations vanish. A finite-volume, finite-mode regulator makes these statements ordinary matrix identities before a continuum limit.

The renormalization-group beta function is
β(λ)=μdμdλ​​bare parameters fixed​.​
(22)
At one-loop accuracy, integrating dλ/dlogμ=bλ2 gives
λ(μ)1​=λ(μ0​)1​−blogμ0​μ​.​
(23)
For a positive weak coupling and b>0, the coupling increases toward the ultraviolet and decreases toward the infrared. Extrapolation predicts a Landau pole at μ=μ0​exp[1/(bλ(μ0​))], where the weak-coupling approximation fails. For b<0, the positive coupling decreases toward zero as μ→∞: this is asymptotic freedom. Toward the infrared it instead grows, so perturbation theory eventually fails. The zero coupling is a fixed point. If negative couplings are allowed mathematically, their direction of motion still satisfies dλ/dlogμ=bλ2, but the side on which the pole occurs reverses: for example b>0,λ<0 approaches zero from below in the ultraviolet. Negative quartic coupling does not give a stable scalar potential.
For the loop calculation use d=4−ϵ, ϵ>0, and the PDF's vertex normalization −λϕ4/4!. A Feynman parameter combines the denominators. Shift the Euclidean loop momentum to ℓ=k+xp, and put Mx2​=m2+x(1−x)p2. The regulated integral without the vertex prefactor is

(25)
For d=4−ϵ, Γ(ϵ/2)=2/ϵ+O(1), while the other factors have finite limits. Thus the massive scalar bubble pole in four dimensions is

This pole is independent of external momentum and mass and hence is canceled by a local quartic counterterm. There are three channels, corresponding to the three pairings of four external momenta. Their poles add, and a counterterm vertex −iδλ cancels the sum when

This is the total counterterm for the three four-point diagrams specified here. The minimal subtraction scheme removes their poles only. The one-loop tadpole is momentum independent and requires a mass counterterm, but does not produce one-loop wave-function renormalization; that is why it does not alter this one-loop coupling calculation.

Absorb the gauge coupling into the connection Aμ​=Aμa​Ta​ and use Dμ​=∂μ​+Aμ​. The anti-Hermitian generators make U(x)=exp[ωa(x)Ta​] unitary for real parameters. With a matter field transforming as ψ′=Uψ, require Dμ′​ψ′=UDμ​ψ. Applying both sides to an arbitrary field gives
Aμ′​=UAμ​U−1−(∂μ​U)U−1.​
(31)
Expanding U=1+ω+O(ω2) and U−1=1−ω+O(ω2) yields the infinitesimal gauge transformation with an anti-Hermitian connection
δAμ​=[ω,Aμ​]−∂μ​ω=−Dμ​ω.​
(32)
The adjoint covariant derivative is Dμ​ω=∂μ​ω+[Aμ​,ω]. In components,
δAμa​=−∂μ​ωa−fbca​Aμb​ωc.​
(33)
The trace normalization makes the structure constants totally antisymmetric: fabc​=−Tr([Ta​,Tb​]Tc​) and cyclicity of the trace supply antisymmetry in the remaining indices. Replacing U=eω by e−ω reverses the infinitesimal parameter convention and gives δA=Dω; mixing the two conventions would change the ghost operator incorrectly. The trace normalization also gives the invariant quadratic form −Tr(Aμ​Aν​)=Aμa​Aνa​.
A gauge-invariant functional integral counts all representatives along each gauge orbit. The gauge condition F[A]=0 selects a representative, and its delta functional enforces that selection. Assume a local patch where each orbit intersects the gauge slice once and the gauge-condition derivative has no residual zero modes. Define
Mab​(x,y)=δωb(y)δFa​[Aω](x)​​ω=0​.

∫Dωδ[F(Aω)]=∣detM∣1​,1=∣detM∣∫Dωδ[F(Aω)].

Insert this Faddeev-Popov gauge-orbit identity into the unfixed path integral. The action and measure are invariant under a change A↦Aω, so integration over ω factors out as the gauge-group volume. Dividing by that redundant volume leaves the fixed integral and its Faddeev-Popov determinant. In a perturbative patch with fixed determinant sign or phase, the absolute value is replaced by the oriented determinant, up to a field-independent normalization:
Δ[A]=detM,Mab​(x,y)=−∫ddzδAμc​(z)δFa​[A](x)​Dμcb​(z)δ(d)(z−y).​
