A stellar polytrope has a spatially constant coefficient K in the relation P=Kρ1+1/n; n is its polytropic index. This relates pressure to mass density and need not describe an adiabatic gas. Combining hydrostatic equilibrium with mass conservation eliminates the enclosed mass:
r21​drd​(ρr2​drdP​)=−4πGρ.
(1)
For ρ=ρc​θn, the polytropic equation of state gives (1/ρ)dP/dr=(n+1)Kρc1/n​dθ/dr. Choose the Lane-Emden variables for a stellar polytrope with
α2=4πG(n+1)K​ρc1/n−1​,r=αξ.
(2)
Substitution then gives the dimensionless Lane-Emden equation:
ξ21​(ξ2θ′)′=−θn.​
(3)
A regular stellar centre has θ(0)=1, θ′(0)=0 and m(0)=0. In particular the regular central expansion is θ=1−ξ2/6+O(ξ4). For an isolated finite-radius stellar polytrope with negligible external pressure, the surface is the first zero ξ1​ of θ, and R=αξ1​. A core embedded in an envelope can instead end at a positive matching pressure, before this zero.
The integrated Lane-Emden equation is ξ2θ′=−∫0ξ​s2θ(s)nds. Thus mass conservation gives the Lane-Emden mass formula directly:
m(ξ)=−4πρc​α3ξ2θ′(ξ).​
(4)
For the proposed functional form, differentiation gives
θ′′+ξ2​θ′=2βγ(1+βξ2)γ−2[3+(2γ+1)βξ2].
(5)
Choosing γ=−1/2 removes the extra polynomial factor; equality to −θ5 then requires 3β=1. The regular polytrope of index five is therefore
β=31​,γ=−21​,θ=(1+ξ2/3)−1/2.​
(6)
Its enclosed mass and enclosed mean mass density are
m(ξ)=3(1+ξ2/3)3/24πρc​α3ξ3​,ρ​(ξ)=4πr33m​=ρc​(1+ξ2/3)−3/2.
(7)
There is no finite zero: its isolated radius is infinite. Nevertheless its total mass converges, since the outer mass density falls as r−5:
M=4π3​ρc​α3,R=∞,r→∞lim​ρ​(r)=0.​
(8)
The zero mean mass density is a limiting volume average, not a statement that the material has zero mass density at finite radius.
To turn this density model into a CNO cycle burning model, use gas-dominated pressure and uniform mean molecular weight. The ideal gas law and the polytropic equation of state then imply T/Tc​=θ. Without this thermal assumption a polytropic equation of state for total pressure does not alone fix the temperature profile. Since the stellar energy-generation rate is power per unit mass, its contribution to luminosity is ϵdm, giving the luminosity integral of an index-five polytrope:
L=4πϵ0​α3ρc2​Tc11​∫0∞​ξ2(1+ξ2/3)−21/2dξ.
(9)
Put u=ξ/3+ξ2​. Then ξ=3​u/1−u2​ and the dimensionless integral becomes 33​∫01​u2(1−u2)8du. Evaluation gives
A=12π3​19!8!9!217​=1.029415…,L=Aϵ0​α3ρc2​Tc11​,A∼1.​
(10)
The PDF's power of α in the proposed luminosity scaling is a genuine dimensional error: it must be three, rather than two. The volume element supplies α3; replacing it by α2 would give power divided by length, and could not be repaired by a dimensionless numerical constant.
The polytrope of index five is unlikely to model a real burning stellar core well. An embedded core has a finite boundary, and a complete isolated star cannot have this infinite radius. More importantly, strongly temperature-sensitive CNO cycle heating concentrates the luminosity centrally and tends to produce a convection zone there. A well-mixed gas-pressure-dominated convective core is closer to an adiabatic stellar polytrope of index 3/2 than to index five. A finite truncation would also change the integral defining A; its value above pertains to the full mathematical model.

In stellar homology, write r=Rx, m=Mf(x) and ρ=ρc​h(x), with common dimensionless profiles. Integrating hydrostatic equilibrium from the surface, where the pressure is negligible, gives
Pc​=RGMρc​​∫01​x2f(x)h(x)​dx.
(11)
The profile integral is common to the sequence. Similarly, mass conservation gives M=4πρc​R3∫01​x2h(x)dx. These prove the homology scaling of central stellar pressure:
ρc​∝R3M​,Pc​∝RGMρc​​∝R4GM2​.
(12)
The ideal gas law consequently gives Tc​∝μM/R, with the gravitational and gas constants absorbed into the coefficient.
The radiative diffusion in a star equation has a characteristic gradient Tc​/R. Hence L∝RTc4​/(κc​ρc​). Using Kramers' opacity law, and comparing fixed-composition homologous models, this becomes
Lrad​∝κ0​ρc2​RTc15/2​​∝κ0−1​μ15/2M11/2R−1/2.
(13)
The volume integral of the stellar energy-generation rate ϵ=ϵ0​ρTν instead gives
Lnuc​∝Mϵ0​ρc​Tcν​∝ϵ0​μνMν+2R−ν−3.
(14)
The dimensionless integrals behind these relations do not depend on M along a homologous sequence. In stellar thermal equilibrium the two luminosities agree, proving Kramers homology with a variable nuclear exponent:
Rν+5/2∝ϵ0​κ0​μν−15/2Mν−7/2.
(15)
For the specified proton–proton chain exponent ν=7/2, the mass factor cancels. Thus
R=constant,L∝M11/2.​
(16)
The model captures two important features of the lower main sequence: hydrogen burning primarily uses the proton–proton chain, and gas rather than radiation pressure supplies the principal support. A radiative interior and a Kramers' opacity law approximation are useful near the Sun. It is a simplified local model: the Sun has a convective envelope, the coolest main sequence stars become fully convective, and real opacities and burning exponents vary. In particular a literally constant radius is not a realistic law over the entire lower main sequence.
On this proton–proton chain branch Tc​∝M, because R and mean molecular weight are fixed. Normalizing to the Sun gives the upper mass estimate
M∗​≃1.5×1072×107​M⊙​=34​M⊙​.​
(17)
For the specified CNO cycle exponent ν=23/2, the homology relation instead reads R14∝M8. Substituting into the radiative luminosity relation gives
R∝M4/7,L∝M73/14,Tc​∝M3/7.​
(18)
This branch retains the deliberately idealized fully radiative assumptions, even though real CNO cycle cores are often convective.
For a Hertzsprung-Russell diagram, use the Stefan–Boltzmann law, L=4πR2σTeff4​. On the proton–proton chain branch Teff​∝M11/8, so L∝Teff4​. On the CNO cycle branch Teff​∝M57/56, giving L∝Teff292/57​. Thus the upper branch rises slightly more steeply toward the hot, luminous upper left in a logarithmic Hertzsprung-Russell diagram. The sketch below matches the approximate branches continuously at their respective burning crossovers; the true crossover has both heating terms and is smooth.
The proton–proton chain begins with reactions between hydrogen nuclei, so its specific heating scales as XH2​ρT7/2 in this approximation. The catalytic CNO cycle involves hydrogen and CNO nuclei, and scales as XH​ZCNO​ρT23/2. Here XH​ is the hydrogen mass fraction and ZCNO​ the CNO mass fraction, not the total abundance of every metal. Consequently
ϵpp​ϵCNO​​∝XH​ZCNO​​T8.
(19)
Keeping XH​ fixed while dividing ZCNO​ by 256 gives the CNO crossover temperature
T∗,poor​=2561/8(2×107K)=4×107K,M∗,poor​≃38​M⊙​.​
(20)
The mass estimate uses the unchanged proton–proton chain branch below crossover, together with the stipulated unchanged opacity and mean molecular weight.
The low-CNO proton–proton chain sequence therefore lies on the same locus, but extends to twice the transition mass. Beyond its later crossover the low-CNO CNO cycle sequence has the same logarithmic slope 292/57 but a different normalization. At a fixed mass where both sequences are CNO-dominated, lowering the nuclear coefficient by 256 gives
Rsolar​Rpoor​​=256−1/14=2−4/7,Lsolar​Lpoor​​=22/7,Teff,solar​Teff,poor​​=25/14.
(21)
Thus those low-CNO models are smaller, hotter and modestly more luminous at the same mass. At the same effective temperature, their CNO branch is lower in luminosity than the solar-composition CNO branch; these comparisons hold different quantities fixed.

A constant number star formation rate over 10Gyr produces a uniform present-age distribution. The birth-age distribution under constant star formation therefore gives
Y(t)=10Gyrt​=0.1Gyrt​(0≤t≤10Gyr).​
(22)
Outside this age interval the cumulative distribution function is zero or one. The census here retains stellar remnants as objects, as required by the population model.
Write m=M/M⊙​. The initial mass function is proportional to m−3 on m≥0.2. Normalizing the tail integral gives the power-law initial-mass-function tail coordinate:
X(m)=∫0.2∞​s−3ds∫m∞​s−3ds​=m20.04​(m≥0.2).​
(23)
For lower thresholds X=1. Because the birth mass and formation time have independence, the probability integral transform makes (X,Y) uniform on the unit square. X is an upper-tail coordinate, so larger masses have smaller X.
A red giant of current age t lies in the stellar lifetime regions in a mass-age diagram
m28​≤Gyrt​<m210​,0≤Gyrt​≤10.
(24)
There are no red giants below m=0.8​ in this model. For 0.8​<m<1 the upper boundary is the population's age limit, while for m≥1 both lifetime boundaries are present. A white dwarf lies above t/Gyr=10/m2.
In uniform probability coordinates the main-sequence boundary is Y=20X and the end of the giant phase is Y=25X. The giant region is therefore 20X≤Y<25X, clipped to the unit square: it is the triangle with vertices (0,0),(1/25,1),(1/20,1). Its area, and hence the individual giant fraction, is
Pr(G)=21​(201​−251​)=2001​=0.5%.​
(25)
The white dwarf region Y≥25X is the triangle with vertices (0,0),(0,1),(1/25,1), giving
Pr(W)=21​251​=501​=2%.​
(26)
The remaining 97.5% are on the main sequence. Boundaries have zero probability and do not affect these counts. Treating every post-giant object as a white dwarf is a stipulated toy-model assumption; real high-mass stars have other stellar remnants.
For the binary calculations, use a coeval binary population: components share their birth time even though their birth masses are independently drawn and their subsequent evolution is independent. Hence (X1​,X2​,Y) is uniform in the unit cube. At a fixed age coordinate Y=y, a component is a giant when y/25<X<y/20, and a white dwarf when 0<X<y/25. Define the conditional state fractions
g(y)=100y​,w(y)=25y​,e(y)=g(y)+w(y)=20y​.
(27)
Components have conditional independence given y; averaging over the common age must be done after forming the conditional pair probabilities. The continuous-birth binary state fractions below are consequently volumes in this cube, not products of the marginal single-star fractions.

Condition on the common age Y=y of the coeval binary population. Each component is a red giant with conditional probability g(y)=y/100, independently of the other component at that age. The conditional probability of at least one giant is 1−(1−g)2=2g−g2. Integrating over the uniform age distribution gives
Pr(at least one giant)=∫01​(1002y​−10000y2​)dy=1001​−300001​.
(28)
Thus the fraction is 299/30000=0.9966%, just below 1%. The subtracted term removes the systems counted twice because both components are red giants. Using the square of the marginal giant fraction would incorrectly ignore their shared age.

A system containing a red giant and another evolved component is either a giant–giant pair or an unordered giant–white dwarf pair. At common age y, these disjoint possibilities have conditional probabilities g(y)2 and 2g(y)w(y). The conditional evolved-companion fraction in a coeval binary population therefore has numerator
Pr(GG or GW)=∫01​(10000y2​+25002y2​)dy=300009​.
(29)
Divide by the fraction 299/30000 of systems containing at least one giant:
Pr(another evolved component∣at least one giant)=2999​=0.0301003….​
(30)
Thus just over 3% of giant-containing binaries have an evolved companion. The 0.03% numerator is a fraction of all binaries; it is not itself the requested conditional fraction.

The elements have several complementary origins: primordial light nuclei, stellar and explosive fusion products, and nuclei built by neutron capture. Chemical enrichment also requires their ejection, rather than merely their production inside a star. Nucleosynthesis and transport into the interstellar medium must therefore be considered together.
Hydrogen and much of the helium are primordial. During Big Bang nucleosynthesis, a cooling, expanding plasma assembled surviving neutrons and protons into deuterium, helium-3 and especially helium-4, with small abundances of lithium-7 or its beryllium-7 progenitor. Unstable nuclei at mass numbers five and eight, together with the rapidly falling density and short available time, obstructed sustained production of heavier nuclei. The carbon, oxygen and heavier material in later generations consequently require other sites. Cosmic rays fragment heavier interstellar nuclei and contribute importantly to the light elements lithium, beryllium and boron, which ordinary stellar burning tends to destroy.
Burning stages depend on initial stellar mass. Stellar nuclear fusion can release energy when the products have greater nuclear binding energy than the reactants. The resulting stellar thermostat links nuclear burning to hydrostatic equilibrium: when fuel exhaustion lowers heating, contraction raises the central temperature until another fuel can ignite, if the core becomes hot enough. Higher nuclear charges bring larger Coulomb barriers, so later charged-particle burning generally requires progressively higher temperatures. Whether the core reaches them depends on its mass, electron degeneracy pressure, mixing and envelope loss.
An approximately solar-composition 1M⊙​ star first spends most of its life on the main sequence, converting hydrogen into helium mainly through the proton–proton chain. The net conversion is four protons into one helium-4 nucleus, with positrons and neutrinos; the small CNO cycle contribution redistributes CNO catalyst abundances but is not a net source of carbon at this stage. Exhaustion of central hydrogen leaves a helium-rich core. A surrounding hydrogen-burning shell continues to add helium, while the core contracts and the envelope expands into a red giant.
The low-mass helium core becomes supported substantially by electron degeneracy pressure. At roughly 108K, the Triple-alpha process ignites, first making transient beryllium-8 and then carbon-12 through the Hoyle state. Because degeneracy initially weakens the usual expansion response, this ignition is a helium flash. It heats and lifts the degeneracy of the core, leading to a stable phase of core helium burning. The Triple-alpha process makes carbon; competing carbon-12 alpha capture makes oxygen:
34He⟶12C,12C(α,γ)16O.
(33)
After central helium exhaustion, the star develops a carbon-oxygen core, a helium-burning shell and a more external hydrogen-burning shell. On the Asymptotic giant branch, recurrent thermal pulses of an asymptotic-giant-branch star arise from unstable helium-shell burning. Stellar dredge-up can bring processed material toward the surface, with its efficiency depending on mass and composition. The eventual stellar wind removes the envelope; the hot exposed core can illuminate a planetary nebula, and the remnant becomes a carbon-oxygen white dwarf. A usual isolated 1M⊙​ star does not ignite carbon burning. In particular, an element synthesized in its core need not all escape: much of the carbon and oxygen remains locked in the white dwarf.
An approximately 5M⊙​ star burns central hydrogen predominantly through the CNO cycle, usually in a convective core. The CNO nuclei act as catalysts; the slow nitrogen-14 proton-capture step tends to accumulate nitrogen-14 while converting hydrogen into helium. After central fuel exhaustion, a hydrogen-burning shell surrounds the contracting helium core. Unlike the 1M⊙​ case, core helium burning normally begins before strong electron degeneracy, without a classical helium flash. The same Triple-alpha process and carbon-12 alpha capture create a carbon-oxygen core.
The 5M⊙​ star then enters the Asymptotic giant branch, with hydrogen and helium burning in shells. Repeated thermal pulses and stellar dredge-up can enrich its envelope with freshly made carbon and s-process nuclei. Hot-bottom burning can occur when the base of the convective envelope is sufficiently hot: the CNO cycle processes dredged-up carbon into nitrogen, so a massive AGB star need not become carbon-rich despite making carbon below its envelope. Helium-shell reactions on neon-22 can also provide neutrons for the s-process. For an ordinary 5M⊙​ model, strong envelope loss generally precedes carbon ignition, leaving a carbon-oxygen white dwarf. The exact threshold for carbon ignition and the size of these yields depend on metallicity, mass loss and mixing; they are not universal integers fixed solely by initial mass.
A 20M⊙​ star reaches much more advanced stages. It starts with convective-core CNO cycle hydrogen burning, followed by core helium burning. After central helium exhaustion, contraction can ignite carbon burning at temperatures approaching 109K. Carbon-fusion channels make products including neon, sodium and magnesium. Neon burning then uses photodisintegration of neon-20 to release oxygen-16 and an alpha particle, followed by capture of that alpha particle on another neon-20 nucleus; the combined network releases energy and produces magnesium-24. Oxygen burning makes silicon-group nuclei through channels such as
16O+16O⟶28Si+α.
(34)
At still higher temperature, silicon burning rearranges nuclei by photodisintegration and captures of protons, neutrons and alpha particles. It is not principally direct fusion of two silicon nuclei. Networks approach quasi-equilibrium and then nuclear statistical equilibrium, producing an iron-group core whose precise mixture depends on the electron fraction in stellar matter. Outer shells retain different stages of burning, giving an approximately layered structure. Increasing stellar neutrino energy loss makes advanced burning stages much shorter than hydrogen burning.
Near the iron group, nuclear binding energy per nucleon is close to its maximum. Further fusion cannot provide the sustained energy source required to support the core. As the core grows, electron capture reduces electron-pressure support and photodisintegration absorbs energy, assisting gravitational collapse. A successful core-collapse supernova ejects some of the envelope and processed shells, and leaves a neutron star or black hole. The shock also causes explosive burning, changing the pre-existing abundances. A 20M⊙​ initial mass does not uniquely fix the explosion or remnant: envelope loss, rotation, composition and fallback all matter, and some collapses fail to eject much material.
Ejection determines the enrichment of the interstellar medium. Low- and intermediate-mass stars return helium, CNO-processed matter, dredged-up carbon and s-process products through stellar winds and envelope expulsion. Massive-star winds can expose hydrogen- or helium-burning products before collapse. Successful core-collapse supernovae eject substantial oxygen and other alpha-capture products, together with some iron-group matter formed in hydrostatic and explosive burning. Material that falls back or remains in a stellar remnant is withheld from the interstellar medium. Subsequent mixing, cooling and star formation distribute the escaped material into new stars. The distinction between production and ejection is why an internal burning inventory alone is not a stellar yield.
Binary stars provide an additional major iron-group source. In a Type Ia supernova, a carbon-oxygen white dwarf in a binary undergoes thermonuclear disruption after an appropriate accretion or merger history. Explosive carbon and oxygen burning makes intermediate-mass and iron-group nuclei. Much initially synthesized nickel-56 decays through cobalt-56 into iron-56; this radioactive decay also powers the supernova light curve. Such explosions have delay times and yields different from those of massive-star collapse, and help explain why iron enrichment need not track oxygen enrichment exactly.
Neutron capture builds much of the material beyond iron. Charged-particle reactions encounter increasing Coulomb barriers as nuclear charge grows, but a neutral neutron does not encounter that barrier. Neutron capture raises mass number, and subsequent beta-minus decay can turn a neutron into a proton and raise atomic number. The relative capture and decay times select different paths through the nuclear chart.
In the slow neutron-capture process, most unstable intermediates decay before the next capture, so the path stays near stable nuclei. In Asymptotic giant branch stars, important neutron sources are
13C(α,n)16O,22Ne(α,n)25Mg.
(35)
The carbon-13 source operates in suitable partially mixed layers, while the hotter helium-shell pulses can activate the neon-22 source. Captures on pre-existing seed nuclei produce elements such as strontium and barium and, under suitable conditions, lead. Stellar dredge-up and stellar winds return these products to the interstellar medium. Massive stars also have a weak s-process, notably using neon-22 during helium- and carbon-burning phases; it need not produce the same abundance range as the main AGB contribution.
In the rapid neutron-capture process, very high neutron densities permit many captures before typical beta-minus decays. The path moves to neutron-rich nuclei, which later decay toward stability, producing heavy nuclei including lanthanides and actinides. Neutron-rich ejecta from neutron star mergers provide an established site: a merger can eject dynamical material and disk outflows, and radioactive decay of the newly made nuclei powers a kilonova. Special neutron-rich outflows associated with some massive-star collapses are additional candidate sites. It would be unjustified to assume that every ordinary core-collapse supernova produces the full heavy r-process pattern; the required neutron richness and expansion history are restrictive.
The two neutron-capture processes do not make every isotope beyond iron. Some proton-rich isotopes bypass their main paths. Gamma-process nucleosynthesis, for example, uses photodisintegration of pre-existing heavy seed nuclei in hot explosive material. Other proton-rich reaction paths can matter in suitable environments. Thus “heavier than iron” describes a family of production mechanisms rather than a single further ordinary fusion stage.
Binaries change both stellar evolution and the delivery of elements. Roche-lobe overflow, wind transfer and mergers can strip a donor, rejuvenate a mass-gaining star, alter core and envelope masses, and change the timing and character of an eventual explosion. Binary stripping can remove the hydrogen envelope of a massive core-collapse supernova progenitor. Accretion onto a white dwarf can cause a classical nova, ejecting hydrogen-burning products and particular isotopes while ordinarily leaving the white dwarf intact; this differs from the destructive Type Ia supernova. Compact binary stars can lose orbital energy through gravitational radiation and merge, providing the neutron star merger route to heavy r-process material. Consequently chemical enrichment cannot be inferred entirely from isolated-star tracks, even when a simplified population calculation treats binary components as evolving independently.
