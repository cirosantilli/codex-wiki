An isotropic simple fluid has local stress determined by the deformation history of the material element. A viscometric flow is one whose relative deformation history is that of steady simple shear flow, up to rigid changes of frame. At a given element the shear rate is constant along its history, although it can differ between elements. This includes the locally simple-shear kinematics used in standard viscometric measurements.
Use the component-first velocity gradient Kij​=∂vi​/∂xj​. The first two Rivlin-Ericksen tensors are
A1​=K+KT,A2​=Dt​A1​+A1​K+KTA1​.
(1)
In the axes of a steady shear vx​=sy, the relative deformation gradient over a lag q is F=I+qK, since K2=0. The material history is determined by s, and its tensor symmetries exclude xz and yz stress components. The remaining symmetric stress consists of an isotropic part, one shear component, and two independent normal-stress differences. The relevant matrices are
A1​=​0s0​s00​000​​,A12​=​s200​0s20​000​​,A2​=​000​02s20​000​​.
(2)
Thus I,A1​,A12​,A2​ span the allowed stress components. Material isotropy and reversal of the shear show that their scalar coefficients depend on ∣s∣=(A1​:A1​/2)1/2. Define the viscometric functions by
μ=sσxy​​,ψ1​=s2σxx​−σyy​​,ψ2​=s2σyy​−σzz​​.
(3)
Solving for the matrix coefficients gives
σ=−pI+μA1​+(ψ1​+ψ2​)A12​−21​ψ1​A2​.​
(4)
This is an exact steady-viscometric representation, rather than an assumption that the fluid is everywhere a second-order fluid. The shear viscosity μ measures the shear resistance, while ψ1​ and ψ2​ measure the first and second normal-stress differences generated by shear. Their zero-rate values are understood as limits when these exist. An isotropic pressure changes neither normal difference.
For a flow in the lubrication approximation with gap H, longitudinal scale ℓ≫H and speed U, the shear-rate scale is U/H. The viscous shear force per unit volume scales as μU/H2. Quadratic viscoelastic normal stresses scale as ψi​U2/H2; their longitudinal gradients, and the comparable mixed-component corrections, contribute forces of scale ψi​U2/(H2ℓ). Therefore the normal-stress smallness in lubrication criterion is
i=1,2max​μℓ∣ψi​∣U​≪1.​
(5)
Equivalently, the thin-gap aspect ratio must multiply the shear-based elastic parameter to give a small quantity: (H/ℓ)∣ψi​∣γ˙​/μ≪1. Normal stresses need not themselves be smaller than the shear stress; it is their effect on the leading force balance that matters. If the flow changes on an additional short time scale T, the Dt​A1​ term also requires ∣ψ1​∣/(μT)≪1 for its neglect. Material coefficients should be evaluated at the characteristic shear rate.
Now take positive cylinder rotation, measure θ counterclockwise from the rightward horizontal, and let y=r−a increase outward through the film. The tangent has upward component cosθ, so the tangential gravitational acceleration is −gcosθ. To leading order in h/a, neglect inertia and higher-order hydrostatic or capillary pressure gradients, and write U=Ωa. Tangential force balance and the shear-free free surface give
∂y∂σθy​​=ρgcosθ,σθy​(h)=0,
(6)
hence
σθy​(y)=ρgcosθ(y−h),0≤y≤h.​
(7)
The wall shear stress is −ρghcosθ. It opposes rotation on the uphill side and assists it on the downhill side.
For a power-law fluid, σθy​=k∣uy​∣n−1uy​. Its inversion must retain the sign:
uy​=−sgn(cosθ)(kρg∣cosθ∣​)1/n(h−y)1/n.
(8)
Integrating with the no-slip boundary condition u(0)=U yields
u(y)=U−sgn(cosθ)n+1n​(kρg∣cosθ∣​)1/n[h(n+1)/n−(h−y)(n+1)/n].
(9)
The volume flux per unit axial length is Q=∫0h​u(y)dy. The integrated bracket is [(n+1)/(2n+1)]h(2n+1)/n, so the power-law coating on a rotating cylinder has
Q=Ωah−sgn(cosθ)2n+1n​(kρg∣cosθ∣​)1/nh(2n+1)/n.​
(10)
At cosθ=0 the gravitational term vanishes by continuity. Steady mass conservation makes Q independent of θ.
On the uphill half, put D=(ρgcosθ/k)1/n and m=(2n+1)/n. The local flux is Q(h)=Uh−(D/m)hm, with
Q′(h)=U−Dh(n+1)/n,Q′′(h)<0(h>0).
(11)
It has a unique maximum at
h∗​(θ)=(ρgcosθkUn​)1/(n+1),Q∗​(θ)=2n+1n+1​Uh∗​(θ).
(12)
A positive coating must carry the same steady flux at every angle. The tightest local bound occurs at cosθ=1, not on the downhill side, where the flux increases monotonically with thickness. Consequently
Qmax​=2n+1n+1​(ρgk​)1/(n+1)(Ωa)(2n+1)/(n+1).​
(13)
For 0<Q<Qmax​ the thinner positive root on the uphill side connects through cosθ=0 to the unique downhill root, giving a periodic leading-order coating profile. At the limiting flux the two uphill roots meet at θ=0; it is the maximum flux of this lubrication model, with a possible limiting corner resolved by neglected higher-order effects. For n=1 the check is Qmax​=(2/3)UkU/(ρg)​. All these conclusions require the resulting film to remain thin enough for the lubrication approximation and for the neglected normal stresses and inertia to satisfy their scaling restrictions.

A simple fluid reduces to a linear viscoelastic fluid when its deformation and rotation over the relevant memory interval are small enough to linearize the constitutive history about an isotropic relaxed state. Small amplitude is essential; small frequency is not. For example, arbitrarily high-frequency oscillation can still be linear if its strain amplitude is sufficiently small. The relaxation modulus G(t) is the shear-stress response to a unit small step of shear strain.
Choose the harmonic convention eiωt. If the imposed shear rate is Re(γ˙​​eiωt), the corresponding shear stress is Re(η∗(ω)γ˙​​eiωt). Substitution into the constitutive convolution gives the complex viscosity:
η∗(ω)=∫0∞​G(s)e−iωsds.​
(14)
For a nondecaying elastic part this formula is understood through the limit from a positive Laplace damping parameter. The complex shear modulus is G∗=iωη∗=G′+iG′′, so G′ is the storage modulus, G′′ the loss modulus, and η∗=G′′/ω−iG′/ω in this convention.
Creep is the progressive deformation produced by a held stress. For a small step S0​H(t) of shear stress, define the creep compliance J by γ(t)=S0​J(t) from a relaxed state. Writing hats for Laplace transforms, the same constitutive law gives S=pGγ​. Since S=S0​/p, this yields
J(p)=p2G(p)1​.​
(15)
Here H is the Heaviside step function. Inversion determines the full creep response, including any instantaneous elastic strain jump. Equivalently, the causal relaxation/creep convolution includes that jump through ∫0−t​G(t−s)dJ(s)=1 for t>0.
For the Maxwell fluid, G(p)=G0​/(p+1/τ). Thus the two material responses are
η∗(ω)=1+iωτG0​τ​,J(t)=G0​1​+G0​τt​(t≥0).
(16)
The creep response is an elastic jump followed by constant-rate viscous deformation. The zero-frequency shear viscosity is η=G0​τ.
For the wall problem, take v=u(y,t)ex​ and denote the shear component of stress by S. The incompressibility constraint is satisfied, and the advective part of the material derivative vanishes because no field depends on x. With no imposed longitudinal pressure gradient, momentum conservation and the linear Maxwell law give
ρut​=Sy​,St​+τS​=G0​uy​.
(17)
The initial conditions are u=S=0 in y>0; the no-slip boundary condition is u(0,t)=UH(t), with decay as y→∞. Transforming these equations gives
S=p+1/τG0​​uy​,uyy​=q(p)2u,q(p)=G0​τρp(1+pτ)​​.
(18)
Choose the analytic square-root branch in Rep>0 with Req>0, so that the spatially increasing solution is excluded. The Maxwell start-up shear layer consequently has
u(y,p)=pU​e−q(p)y.
(19)
The Bromwich inversion formula now gives
u(y,t)=2πiU​∫b−i∞b+i∞​p1​exp​pt−yG0​τρp(1+pτ)​​​dp,b>0.​
(20)
The vertical Bromwich contour is directed upward and lies to the right of all singularities, including the origin and the branch points at 0 and −1/τ. A cut joining these branch points is a possible continuation into the left half-plane; inversion only requires the stated right-half-plane branch.
The Newtonian limit holds η=G0​τ fixed while τ→0, so G0​→∞. Then q(p)→ρp/η​. With ν=η/ρ, the inverse Laplace transform gives the complementary error function profile
u(y,t)=Uerfc(2νt​y​).​
(21)
This is diffusive momentum penetration in a Newtonian fluid, with thickness of order νt​.
The elastic limit instead holds G0​ fixed while τ→∞. Write c=G0​/ρ​. On the chosen branch, q(p)→p/c, so the Laplace transform time-shift rule yields
u(y,t)=UH(t−y/c).​
(22)
An elastic shear front travels at speed c: the material ahead is at rest, while that behind moves at the wall speed. Indeed the limiting displacement is d(y,t)=U(t−y/c)H(t−y/c), whose shear strain is dy​=−(U/c)H(t−y/c). A nonzero pointwise velocity gradient at the front is interpreted distributionally, or by smoothing the ideal wall start.
The use of linear viscoelasticity is conditional, rather than automatically justified for arbitrary U. The material derivative simplifying to a time derivative does not remove the nonlinear constitutive effects of finite shear strain. In this Maxwell problem the rapid elastic response supplies the natural strain scale U/c=Uρ/G0​​, which must be small for linearization through start-up. Away from the front, the strain accumulated over an effective memory interval must likewise be small. In the late diffusive regime its characteristic size is τU/νt​, which decreases with time. Large absolute wall displacement Ut alone is not a disqualification for a fluid with finite memory; it is relative deformation during that memory that matters. In the elastic limit, where memory does not decay, the explicit strain behind the front confirms the condition U/c≪1. Without such a small-strain restriction, the computed field is a formal linear response and need not describe an arbitrary simple fluid.

With its material state fixed by a prescribed deformation history, the differential pom-pom model is a simple-fluid constitutive law. The stress depends on the local history of a material element through its molecular stretch and orientation, without gradients of that history between neighbouring elements. A differential representation does not prevent a material from being a simple fluid.
To see the history dependence explicitly, use the component-first velocity gradient K, so the tensor evolution is Dt​A=KA+AKT−(A−I)/τ1​. Let F(t,s) be the relative deformation gradient, satisfying ∂t​F=K(t)F and F(s,s)=I. Along a material trajectory, an integrating factor gives
A(t)=e−(t−t0​)/τ1​F(t,t0​)A(t0​)F(t,t0​)T+τ1​1​∫t0​t​e−(t−s)/τ1​F(t,s)F(t,s)Tds.
(23)
Thus A is a functional of the local deformation history and its prepared initial state. If the initial A is a positive-definite matrix, it retains that property, so B=A/trA is well defined. Given this B(t), the scalar stretch solves λ˙=r(t)λ+1/τ2​, where r=B:K−1/τ2​. Its variation of constants formula again depends only on that local history:
λ(t)=λ(t0​)e∫t0​t​r(q)dq+τ2​1​∫t0​t​e∫st​r(q)dqds.
(24)
The differential pom-pom model also satisfies material frame indifference. Under an observer rotation, A and B transform as objective tensors, while λ is a scalar. The extra skew part of the transformed velocity gradient has zero contraction with symmetric B, so the stretch equation is unchanged. The tensor evolution uses an upper-convected derivative, and the final stress transforms objectively. Initial values cannot be independently forgotten when specifying a finite-past experiment: a fully specified prepared state, such as rest, is part of the history specification.

At rest the tensor equation relaxes A toward I and the stretch equation relaxes λ toward one. Thus
A0​=I,B0​=3I​,λ0​=1.​
(25)
The equilibrium nonpressure stress GI/3 is isotropic and can be absorbed into the arbitrary pressure in incompressible flow.
For the linear response, put A=I+a+O(ε2), λ=1+ℓ+O(ε2) and take the velocity gradient to be O(ε). The first-order tensor equation is
Dt​a+τ1​a​=K+KT=A1​.
(26)
Its matrix trace has no forcing, because incompressibility gives trK=0. Starting relaxed, tra=0. The first-order stretch equation is Dt​ℓ+ℓ/τ2​=(I/3):K=0, hence ℓ=0. Consequently B=I/3+a/3+O(ε2), and its anisotropic stress is Ga/3. From the relaxed remote past,
σlin=−p∗​I+3G​∫−∞t​e−(t−s)/τ1​A1​(s)ds=−p∗​I+2∫−∞t​3G​e−(t−s)/τ1​E(s)ds.
(27)
The linear and second-order response of the differential pom-pom model therefore has
Grel​(t)=3G​e−t/τ1​(t≥0),μ0​=∫0∞​Grel​(t)dt=3Gτ1​​.​
(28)
The stretch time τ2​ does not enter the linear shear relaxation modulus.
For the second-order fluid constants, additionally make the slow-flow expansion: count K as first order and Dt​ as raising the order by one. Iterating
A−I=τ1​(KA+AKT−Dt​A)
(29)
gives
A=I+τ1​A1​+τ12​(KA1​+A1​KT−Dt​A1​)+O(3).
(30)
Using the Rivlin-Ericksen tensor convention A2​=Dt​A1​+A1​K+KTA1​, the identity
KA1​+A1​KT+A1​K+KTA1​=2A12​
(31)
reduces this to A=I+τ1​A1​+τ12​(2A12​−A2​)+O(3). Since trA2​=trA12​, normalization gives
B=3I​+3τ1​​A1​+3τ12​​(2A12​−A2​)−9τ12​​tr(A12​)I+O(3).

Stretch first changes at second order. Its equation gives λ−1=τ1​τ2​tr(A12​)/6+O(3). Multiplication by λ2 therefore adds only an isotropic stress at second order; multiplying its second-order increment by the anisotropic first-order term would be third order. Absorb these isotropic corrections into p∗​ to obtain

Equivalently, in σ=−pI+μ0​A1​+α1​A2​+α2​A12​, the constants are α1​=−Gτ12​/3 and α2​=2Gτ12​/3. The general small-amplitude linear response above permits fast oscillation; the local second-order fluid expansion also requires the slow-flow ordering just used.

If x>1/2, the axial component is instead a=(2xe(2x−1)t/τ1​−1)/(2x−1) and grows exponentially without bound, while b→1/(1+x). At the boundary x=1/2, which lies between the two stated regimes, a=1+t/τ1​ grows linearly and b→2/3.
Normalization makes the molecular orientation finite even when the unnormalized conformation tensor diverges. For 0≤x<1/2, direct division by a+2b gives
B∞​=diag(3(1−x)1+x​,3(1−x)1−2x​,3(1−x)1−2x​).​

B∞​=diag(1,0,0).​

The same normalized limit holds at x=1/2, agreeing continuously with the limit of the subcritical expression. The limiting orientations have nonnegative eigenvalues and unit matrix trace, as required for the normalized molecular orientation.
