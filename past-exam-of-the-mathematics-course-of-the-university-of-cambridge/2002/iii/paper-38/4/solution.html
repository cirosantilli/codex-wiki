A statistical functional is a map T defined on a class of probability distributions. Its associated functional statistic is T(Fn​), where Fn​=n−1∑i​δXi​​ is the empirical distribution and each δXi​​ is a Dirac measure. For example, T(F)=∫xdF(x) yields the sample mean. Suppose T is differentiable in a sense strong enough to linearize it at the empirical distribution, and that its derivative in a signed direction G−F is ∫IF(x;T,F)d(G−F)(x). Averaging the directions δz​−F over z∼F gives the zero direction, so linearity implies ∫IFdF=0. The resulting asymptotic linear representation is
T(Fn​)−T(F)=n1​∑i​IF(Xi​;T,F)+op​(n−1/2).

Thus the asymptotic variance is n−1∫IF2dF. For a vector functional the integral is n−1∫IFIFTdF. Convergence of the actual finite-sample variance to this leading expression additionally requires control of second moments of the remainder, or an appropriate uniform integrability condition; convergence in distribution alone does not provide that. Existence of a contamination derivative by itself also does not guarantee the required empirical linearization.
Several complementary robustness measures follow. Gross-error sensitivity is γ∗=supz​∣IF(z)∣. Under contamination (1−ε)F+εH, the first-order bias is ε∫IFdH, whose worst absolute coefficient is γ∗; bounded influence therefore bounds infinitesimal gross-error bias. The local-shift sensitivity is λ∗=supx=y​∣IF(x)−IF(y)∣/∣x−y∣, equal to sup∣IF′∣ when the influence function is smoothly differentiable. It measures the effect of moving a contaminant a small distance. The rejection point of an influence function is ρ∗=inf{r:IF(x)=0 for ∣x∣>r}, using centered, standardized coordinates; it is infinite if there is no such cutoff. A finite cutoff completely rejects sufficiently extreme observations to first order. These measures address different aspects of robustness. The integral of squared influence controls asymptotic precision and efficiency, while breakdown point concerns finite amounts of contamination and is not determined by bounded influence alone.
For the quantile, assume 0<p<1, a unique solution q=qp​(F), and a density continuous and positive at q.
