Write the gravitational potential with spherical symmetry as Φ(r) and let n be the unit vector normal to a circular orbit, oriented in the direction of its angular velocity. Put Ω=Ωn and denote the specific angular momentum by h=hn. Circular force balance and the specific orbital energy give
Φ′(r)=rΩ2,h=r2Ω,ε=Φ(r)+21​r2Ω2.
(1)
Differentiating along the family of circular orbits,
drdε​=2rΩ2+r2ΩΩ′=Ωdrdh​.
(2)
A change in orbital orientation contributes hdn to dh, but n⋅dn=0. Consequently
dε=Ωdh=Ω⋅dh.​
(3)
This circular-orbit energy gradient accounts for radial and inclination changes together; spherical symmetry makes the orbital energy independent of the plane.
For either variable-mass particle, H=Mh and E=Mε. Thus dH=Mdh+hdM, so
dE=εdM+MΩ⋅dh=(ε−Ωh)dM+Ω⋅dH.
(4)
Conservation of mass and conservation of angular momentum impose dM2​=−dM1​, dH2​=−dH1​. Adding the two particle contributions proves
dE={(ε1​−Ω1​h1​)−(ε2​−Ω2​h2​)}dM1​+(Ω1​−Ω2​)⋅dH1​.​
(5)
To interpret the signs, label the inner orbit 1 and the outer orbit 2. For f(r)=ε−Ωh the first identity gives f′=−hΩ′. The assumed outward decrease of angular velocity therefore makes f1​<f2​, so the mass-exchange part lowers the energy when dM1​>0: mass is transferred inward. In a coplanar, co-rotating pair, Ω1​>Ω2​ and an outward angular momentum transfer has dH1​=−dHn with dH>0. Its contribution is −(Ω1​−Ω2​)dH<0.
The vector difference also identifies the inclination-reducing exchange. For a fixed small magnitude of admissible angular momentum exchange, its energy contribution is most negative when
dH1​=−s(Ω1​−Ω2​),s>0,
(6)
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. This gives dEangular​=−s∣Ω1​−Ω2​∣2. Let q=n1​⋅n2​=cosι and Hi​=∣Hi​∣, taking fixed particle masses for this exchange. Projecting dHi​ perpendicular to its own direction gives
dn1​=H1​sΩ2​​(n2​−qn1​),dn2​=H2​sΩ1​​(n1​−qn2​).
(7)
Therefore
dq=s(1−q2)(H1​Ω2​​+H2​Ω1​​)>0(0<ι<π).
(8)
The downhill exchange reduces the relative orbital inclination. Its inner angular momentum component is n1​⋅dH1​=−s(Ω1​−Ω2​q)<0; in the aligned limit the outer orbit receives that angular momentum directly. For strongly inclined orbits, part of the exchange instead cancels differently directed angular momenta as the planes align. Together these gradients explain the tendencies toward inward mass transfer, outward angular momentum transport and coplanarity.

Let M∗​ be the central mass, so Ω2=GM∗​/r3 and the specific orbital energy is ε=−GM∗​/(2r). Define the inward accretion rate M˙>0. The rate at which the gas acquires binding energy in moving from b to a is
Ba,b​=M˙{ε(b)−ε(a)}=2GM∗​M˙​(a1​−b1​).​
(9)
The vertically integrated viscous heating per unit horizontal area, including the entire column, is νˉΣ(rΩ′)2. Since rΩ′=−3Ω/2, the dissipated power in the annulus is
Da,b​=∫ab​2πrνˉΣ49​Ω2dr=23GM∗​M˙​∫ab​r21​(1−rrin​​​)dr.
(10)
Here the column integral already includes dissipation associated with both disk faces; adding another factor of two would double-count it. Performing the elementary integrals gives
Da,b​=GM∗​M˙[23​(a1​−b1​)−rin​​(a−3/2−b−3/2)].​
(11)
For the whole disk, take a=rin​ and b→∞. Both expressions give
Ddisk​=Bdisk​=2rin​GM∗​M˙​.​
(12)
If a≫rin​, the bracket 1−rin​/r​ is uniformly close to one throughout the annulus. Comparing the positive integrals shows
Ba,b​Da,b​​=3[1+O(arin​​​)],
(13)
with the correction negative. Thus far from the inner edge the viscous dissipation is approximately three times the local binding-energy acquisition.
There is no violation of conservation of energy because the viscous torque in an accretion disk also transports mechanical energy. The outward viscous torque in an accretion disk is
G=−2πνˉΣr3Ω′=M˙(GM∗​r​−GM∗​rin​​),
(14)
and its outward energy flux is ΩG=GM∗​M˙[r−1−rin​​r−3/2]. The explicit expressions satisfy
Da,b​=Ba,b​+Ω(a)G(a)−Ω(b)G(b).​
(15)
Energy transported from the inner disk supplies the extra dissipation at larger radii. The torque power vanishes at the zero-torque inner boundary condition and tends to zero at infinity, leaving global balance. This is torque transport of energy in a steady accretion disk.

The vertical hydrostatic equilibrium equation balances the gradient of the total pressure against gravity. In a thin disk with Keplerian rotation, expanding the central gravitational field about the midplane gives vertical acceleration −Ω2z, so pz​=−ρΩ2z. Disk self-gravity is neglected in this approximation.
The flux-gradient equation is local thermal balance: the upward radiative flux increases with height because differential rotation dissipates energy. For a Newtonian effective dynamic viscosity μ, the volumetric heating rate is μ(rΩ′)2, which becomes 9μΩ2/4 for Keplerian rotation. The symbol μ here is dynamic viscosity, not kinematic viscosity; the latter is ν=μ/ρ.
The radiative diffusion equation applies to an optically thick medium in local thermodynamic equilibrium. Its diffusion coefficient is c/(3κρ), and the equilibrium radiation energy density is urad​=4σT4/c. Hence
F=−3κρc​dzdurad​​=−3κρ16σT3​dzdT​.
(16)
The minus sign means energy flows down the temperature gradient. Here σ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant, κ the opacity per unit mass, and c the speed of light. Constant Thomson scattering opacity is the adopted transport approximation; local thermodynamic equilibrium is an additional assumption behind the radiation equation of state.
Finally the equation of state adds ideal gas gas pressure pg​=ρkT/(μm​mH​) and isotropic radiation pressure pr​=urad​/3=4σT4/(3c). The Boltzmann constant is k, and μm​mH​ is the mean mass per gas particle. Both contributions supply the total pressure entering vertical hydrostatic equilibrium.

In the limiting model with negligible gas pressure, p=pr​=4σT4/(3c). Its gradient is pz​=16σT3Tz​/(3c). Combining radiative diffusion and hydrostatic equilibrium gives
F=−κρc​pz​=κcΩ2​z.
(17)
Because κ and Ω are constant with height at the fixed disk radius,
dzdF​=κcΩ2​.
(18)
Equating this to the viscous heating rate 9μΩ2/4 forces
μ=9κ4c​.​
(19)
This proves constant viscosity in radiation-supported vertical balance. It fixes the local effective dynamic viscosity uniquely, independent of height, surface density and radius within this ideal limiting model. It does not fix a height-independent kinematic viscosity: ν(z)=4c/(9κρ(z)) still depends on the local mass density.

Choose a reference circular orbit at radius r0​, rotate the frame with Ω=Ω(r0​)ez​, and introduce local Cartesian coordinates x=r−r0​, y=r0​(ϕ−Ωt) and vertical coordinate z. On scales small compared with r0​, neglect curvature of the coordinate directions but retain the leading differential rotation. The background velocity relative to the rotating frame is
u0​=r0​Ω′(r0​)xey​=−2Axey​,A=−21​r0​Ω′(r0​).
(28)
Thus A=3Ω/4 for a Keplerian disk. The shearing sheet retains Coriolis acceleration and the linear tidal acceleration. The radial shearing-sheet tidal potential is −2ΩAx2, whose force 4ΩAxex​ balances the background Coriolis acceleration. If vertical tidal gravity is included, its equilibrium contribution is balanced by a background pressure p0​(z) and disappears when that equilibrium is subtracted.
Write the total velocity as u=u0​+v. In the rotating-frame magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation, background advection is (u0​⋅∇)v=−2Ax∂y​v and advection of the background by the perturbation is (v⋅∇)u0​=−2Avx​ey​. The uniform-viscosity diffusion of the linear background shear is zero. For a solenoidal magnetic field the Lorentz force per unit mass is
μ0​ρ(∇×B)×B​=μ0​ρB⋅∇B​−∇2μ0​ρB2​.
(29)
At constant density, absorb the magnetic-pressure gradient into ψ=(p−p0​)/ρ+B2/(2μ0​ρ). Subtracting the background force balance then gives the stated momentum equation, with the remaining nonlinear advection v⋅∇v, Coriolis acceleration 2Ω×v, magnetic tension and viscous diffusion.
For constant finite electrical conductivity σe​, put η=(μ0​σe​)−1. The resistive induction equation with uniform magnetic diffusivity is ∂t​B+u⋅∇B=B⋅∇u+η∇2B. Substituting the background shear supplies the term −2ABx​ey​ and the same background-advection operator as in the momentum equation. The two further conditions are
∇⋅v=0,∇⋅B=0.​
(30)
They express incompressibility and the absence of magnetic monopoles, respectively.
Now take real plane waves with common phase ϑ=k(t)⋅x and real wavevector. The solenoidal conditions become
k⋅v=0,k⋅B=0.
(31)
Every field depends on position only through ϑ. Hence for either transverse vector field P and either wave field Q, (P⋅∇)Q=(P⋅k)∂ϑ​Q=0. This proves that all the displayed quadratic advection and tension terms vanish pointwise, including products involving conjugate amplitudes. These waves are therefore exact solutions at finite amplitude, not merely a linearization.
Acting with D0​=∂t​−2Ax∂y​ on the phase yields
D0​ϑ=(k˙x​−2Aky​)x+k˙y​y+k˙z​z.
(32)
Canceling its position-dependent part requires
k˙x​=2Aky​,k˙y​=k˙z​=0,kx​(t)=kx0​+2Aky​t.​
(33)
The remaining amplitude equations are
v˙−2Avx​ey​+2Ω×v=−ikψ​−νk2v,B˙=−2ABx​ey​−ηk2B.​
(34)
Differentiating the velocity constraint determines the pressure amplitude:
ik2ψ​=4Aky​vx​−2k⋅(Ω×v).
(35)
The resistive induction equation preserves its constraint because k˙⋅B=2Aky​Bx​ cancels the corresponding shear term in k⋅B˙. The specified magnetic field has no spatially uniform part. Thus there is no background magnetic field producing an additional linear magnetic-tension coupling. The scalar ψ is the modified pressure; the physical pressure can include a constant and a second spatial harmonic from −B2/(2μ0​) while ψ retains the stipulated plane-wave form. This is an exact zero-mean magnetic shearing wave.
For decay, take the real part of the scalar product of the velocity-amplitude equation with its complex conjugate. Pressure does no work because k⋅v=0, and the real part of Coriolis work is zero. Therefore
dtd​∣v∣2=4ARe(vx​vy∗​)−2νk2∣v∣2≤(2∣A∣−2νk2)∣v∣2.
(36)
The resistive induction equation similarly gives
dtd​∣B∣2=−4ARe(Bx​By∗​)−2ηk2∣B∣2≤(2∣A∣−2ηk2)∣B∣2,
(37)
using 2∣ux​uy​∣≤∣u∣2. Integrating these differential inequalities, define
J(t)=∫0t​k(s)2ds=(kx02​+ky2​+kz2​)t+2Akx0​ky​t2+34​A2ky2​t3.
(38)
Then
∣v(t)∣2≤∣v(0)∣2e2∣A∣t−2νJ(t),∣B(t)∣2≤∣B(0)∣2e2∣A∣t−2ηJ(t).
(39)
A non-axisymmetric wave has ky​=0. For nonzero differential shear and positive ν,η, the negative cubic terms dominate the linear upper bound on shear work. Consequently
v(t)⟶0,B(t)⟶0(t→∞).​
(40)
This cubic-exponent decay of a nonaxisymmetric shearing wave allows transient energy growth before the winding to short radial wavelengths makes diffusion dominant. The magnetic amplitudes can also be written explicitly as
B(t)=e−ηJ(t)(Bx​(0), By​(0)−2AtBx​(0), Bz​(0)),
(41)
which confirms the damping independently. Positivity of the dissipative coefficients is necessary: with kz​=0, an inviscid vertical velocity amplitude can remain constant, and an ideal vertical magnetic amplitude can remain constant when η=0. The asserted decay applies to the viscous, finitely conducting model; taking an ideal limit before the long-time limit changes the conclusion.
