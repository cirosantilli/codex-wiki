Use eij​ for the expected accident count, not for the accident rate. With a design row zij​ consisting of an intercept, seven non-reference site indicators and the after-treatment indicator,
eij​=pij​exp(μ+αi​+βj​),ℓ=∑ij​{yij​(logpij​+zijT​γ)−eij​−log(yij​!)}.
(17)
Here logpij​ is a known generalized linear model offset, with coefficient fixed at one. Differentiating the Poisson regression log-likelihood gives the score function equations
ij∑​(yij​−eij​)=0,j=1∑2​(yij​−eij​)=0 (i=2,…,8),i=1∑8​(yi2​−ei2​)=0​.
(18)
These nine equations estimate the intercept, seven site contrasts and one treatment contrast, with the stated reference-level constraints.
The Fisher information is ZTWZ, where W=diag(eij​). A Fisher scoring update adds (ZTWZ)−1ZT(y−e) to the current coefficient vector. In iteratively reweighted least squares, the working response is zij∗​=logeij​+(yij​−eij​)/eij​; regress zij∗​−logpij​ on the design with weights eij​. Thus the reported four iterations solve the likelihood equations while accounting for unequal exposure periods.
The common adjusted rate ratio is obtained by exponentiating the after coefficient:
r=e−0.7806616≃0.4581​.
(19)
This estimates a 54.2% lower accident rate after the intervention, conditional on the additive site model. The standard error 0.2751810 gives normal Wald statistic −2.8369, two-sided p≃0.0046, and an approximate 95% confidence interval for the rate ratio
exp[−0.7806616±1.96(0.2751810)]≃[0.267,0.786]​.
(20)
The label t value is again a normal-reference Wald ratio under fixed Poisson dispersion, rather than an exact Student statistic.
The intercept gives the before rate at reference site one: e0.2707792≃1.311 accidents per year. The seven site coefficients are log baseline-rate ratios relative to that site. In site order two through eight, their exponentials are about 0.615,2.767,1.711,0.769,1.797,0.615,1.221. The third site has the largest estimated rate and a nominal Wald ratio about 3.118 against the reference. These are adjusted baseline comparisons, not differences in absolute accident counts. The overall unadjusted before/after rate ratio is (15/18)/(114/68)≃0.497, and it differs from 0.458 because site risks and their relative exposure weights differ.
An independent way to solve the fit is the profile likelihood for a common Poisson rate ratio with unequal exposures. Put ti​=yi1​+yi2​ and λi​=exp(μ+αi​). For fixed r, the site score gives λi​=ti​/(pi1​+rpi2​). The remaining equation is
∑i​pi1​+rpi2​ti​rpi2​​=15.
(21)
Its positive root reproduces the reported treatment estimate. Comparing the same site model with r=1 to the fitted model gives a deviance improvement about 9.750 on one degree of freedom, with nominal likelihood-ratio p≃0.0018.
There are 16 count observations and 9 fitted mean parameters, leaving 7 residual degrees of freedom. The null deviance 132.9485 on 15 degrees of freedom is for the intercept-only rate model, still including the exposure offset. The residual Poisson deviance is 16.27524 on 7 degrees of freedom. A chi-squared comparison gives approximately p=0.023, suggesting potential lack of fit of a single common treatment ratio; the extreme deviance residuals near −2.03 and 2.14 also deserve inspection. Several after-cell expected counts are below one or near two, so this absolute-fit calibration is only approximate. Site-specific treatment effects, overdispersion or temporal dependence are possible explanations to investigate, with a parametric bootstrap available to calibrate the sparse-count fit statistic. A before/after association alone does not isolate a causal effect from secular changes or regression to the mean at selected sites.
