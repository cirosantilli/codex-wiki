Spontaneous symmetry breaking occurs when the statistical Hamiltonian is invariant under a symmetry but a chosen equilibrium pure state is not. A small selecting field or a boundary condition chooses one of several states related by the symmetry. For a finite unconstrained system the symmetric ensemble generally has zero order parameter; spontaneous order is defined by taking the thermodynamic limit before removing the selecting field. A ferromagnet with a selected magnetization direction is the standard example.
If the broken symmetry is continuous, nearby symmetry-related states cost no energy for a uniform displacement along the manifold of states. Locality and a nonzero stiffness then make a slowly varying displacement cost gradient energy rather than a mass term. These gapless long-wavelength fluctuations are Goldstone modes. In the conventional relativistic internal-symmetry setting their count is the number of broken generators. Nonrelativistic dynamics can pair broken generators, as in a ferromagnet; gauge symmetries and broken space-time symmetries require additional qualifications. A discrete broken symmetry alone does not produce a Goldstone mode.
For an interface with a selected mean height, the continuous displacement is a rigid translation normal to the mean plane. The tension gives a stiffness for spatially varying translations. The following calculation concerns this static Goldstone sector. It determines the excitation energy and equilibrium fluctuations, but a frequency dispersion requires a separate inertial or dissipative dynamics. In low dimensions thermal roughness also limits the existence of a strictly localized flat interface in an infinite system.
