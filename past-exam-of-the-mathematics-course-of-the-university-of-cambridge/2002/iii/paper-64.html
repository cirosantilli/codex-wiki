a OurBigBook.com
An order parameter distinguishes thermodynamic phases. For a ferromagnet it is the magnetization; for a fluid near its liquid-gas critical point it can be the density relative to its critical value. In a system with a scalar order parameter, introduce a slowly varying real scalar field ϕ(x) whose spatial average represents that order parameter. The Landau-Ginzburg theory organizes the effective free energy as a local expansion consistent with the symmetry:
F[ϕ]=∫ddx{2K​(∇ϕ)2+2r​ϕ2+4u​ϕ4+6v​ϕ6−Hϕ}.
(1)
Here K>0 is the stiffness in the gradient energy, and H is the field conjugate to an order parameter. The even powers assume a symmetry ϕ↦−ϕ at H=0. Without that symmetry further terms, including a cubic term, may be allowed. The coefficients are smooth functions of temperature and other controls; near a mean-field transition one usually writes r=a(T−T0​) with a>0. A positive highest retained even coefficient makes the local potential bounded below.
The partition function is obtained by weighting configurations with exp[−F/(kB​T)]. The Landau approximation replaces this functional average by minimization, taking a uniform ϕ=M in a homogeneous phase. In a symmetric finite system the zero-field average can vanish even below the transition because it averages both signs of M. The spontaneous order parameter selects a pure thermodynamic phase by taking the thermodynamic limit before removing the conjugate field:
M=limH↓0​limV→∞​⟨V−1∫V​ϕ(x)ddx⟩H​.
(2)
Thus the order parameter measures the ordering of a selected thermodynamic phase, rather than the average over all symmetry-related phases.

A first-order phase transition has a discontinuity in a first derivative of the equilibrium free energy, such as entropy or the order parameter; a thermal first-order transition normally has latent heat. At a continuous phase transition the order parameter goes continuously to zero and there is no latent heat, although response functions and higher derivatives of the free energy can be singular.
For the symmetric scalar Landau free energy at zero conjugate field, minimize
V(M)=2r​M2+4u​M4+6v​M6,V′(M)=M(r+uM2+vM4).
(3)
If u>0, the sixth-order term is unnecessary near r=0. For r>0 the minimum is M=0, whereas for r<0 there are two symmetry-related minima with M2=−r/u to leading order. The order parameter vanishes continuously as r↑0. The equilibrium free-energy density changes from zero to −r2/(4u), so its first temperature derivative is continuous and its second derivative has a jump. This is the ordinary mean-field continuous phase transition.
If u<0, keep v>0 for stability. A nonzero stationary order parameter, with s=M2, obeys r+us+vs2=0. For phase coexistence with M=0 its free energy must also vanish. Substituting r=−us−vs2 into V gives
V=−4u​s2−3v​s3.
(4)
The nonzero solution of V=0 is therefore
Mt2​=−4v3u​,rt​=16v3u2​.​
(5)
The competing minima exchange stability at positive rt​, with a finite jump of the order parameter. This is a first-order phase transition. The metastability region is also visible: nonzero stationary points first appear at the spinodal r=u2/(4v), and the disordered local minimum loses stability at r=0.
For example, holding u,v fixed and taking r=a(T−T0​), the ordered phase has entropy lower than the disordered phase by aMt2​/2 at coexistence. Heating through the transition absorbs latent heat per volume Tt​aMt2​/2. More generally the temperature dependence of all coefficients contributes to this entropy difference. With no inversion symmetry, a cubic term can also produce a first-order phase transition, as comparison of the competing minima of the Landau free energy shows.

Critical exponents describe leading singular powers near a continuous phase transition. Using the reduced temperature t=(T−Tc​)/Tc​, the standard definitions include
M(t,0)∼(−t)β(t<0),χ(t,0)∼∣t∣−γ,Csing​(t)∼∣t∣−α,ξ(t)∼∣t∣−ν.
(6)
These are the order-parameter critical exponent, magnetic-susceptibility critical exponent, heat-capacity critical exponent and correlation-length critical exponent. The critical-isotherm exponent is defined by M(0,H)∼sgn(H)∣H∣1/δ. The anomalous dimension η gives the critical long-distance connected correlation function, Gc​(x)∼∣x∣−(d−2+η), when an ordinary scale-invariant critical point exists.
In the Landau approximation, minimize the Landau free energy to obtain the equation of state, differentiate it with respect to the field conjugate to an order parameter to obtain magnetic susceptibility, and differentiate the minimized free energy with respect to temperature to obtain heat capacity. These operations determine β,δ,γ,α, respectively. Expanding the Landau-Ginzburg theory about its uniform minimum gives the quadratic fluctuation kernel Kq2+V′′(M), whose Fourier transform determines the correlation length and the anomalous dimension. At the ordinary quartic critical point this gives ξ2=K/V′′(M)∝∣t∣−1 and η=0.
For u>0, r=0 gives H=uM3 and hence δ=3; the equilibrium free energy −r2/(4u) gives a finite heat capacity discontinuity, conventionally α=0. Together with the explicit calculation below, the ordinary mean-field critical exponents are
α=0,β=21​,γ=1,δ=3,ν=21​,η=0.​
(7)
These describe neglect of fluctuation corrections, rather than the exact exponents of every low-dimensional Landau-Ginzburg theory.

A tricritical point is the meeting of a line of continuous phase transitions and a line of first-order phase transitions. In the symmetric scalar Landau free energy, it occurs at r=u=0 with v>0. Two independent controls are needed to tune both coefficients. The continuous transition lies at r=0,u>0, and the tricritical three-phase line found above has r=3u2/(16v),u<0; both approach the tricritical point. At zero conjugate field the three coexisting states on this first-order line are the disordered state and the two opposite ordered states.
Setting u=H=0 makes the leading stabilizing interaction sextic. The nonzero minimum obeys M4=−r/v, so the order-parameter critical exponent along this tuned direction is βtri​=1/4. Substitution gives
fmin​=−3v​∣r∣3/2​(r<0),
(8)
and two temperature derivatives give the heat-capacity critical exponent αtri​=1/2. At r=u=0, the equation of state is H=vM5, giving the critical-isotherm exponent δtri​=5.
For magnetic susceptibility, differentiating H=rM+vM5 gives χ−1=r+5vM4. This is r above the transition and −4r below, hence γtri​=1. The quadratic fluctuation kernel still has a mass proportional to ∣r∣, giving νtri​=1/2 and ηtri​=0. Thus the mean-field tricritical exponent calculation yields
βtri​=41​,αtri​=21​,δtri​=5,γtri​=1,νtri​=21​.​
(9)
Detuning u gives tricritical crossover scaling; one must specify the approach to the tricritical point when quoting these critical exponents. The sextic interaction has upper critical dimension three, so sufficiently strong long-distance fluctuations change these mean-field predictions below three dimensions.

The relevant boundary here is the upper critical dimension: above it the critical interactions become negligible at long distances and the ordinary mean-field critical exponents apply; at it there can be logarithmic corrections. This differs from the lower critical dimension, below which a particular kind of ordering may fail to exist.
Normalize the coefficient of (∇ϕ)2 in the Landau-Ginzburg theory. Under a change of length scale x=bx′, its invariance gives the engineering dimension xϕ​=(d−2)/2. The quartic interaction then scales as
u′=bd−4xϕ​u=b4−du.
(10)
It is a relevant operator below four dimensions and an irrelevant operator above four. Thus the ordinary scalar transition has upper critical dimension dc​=4. At a tricritical point, where the quartic interaction is tuned away, the sextic coupling scales as v′=b6−2dv, giving the tricritical upper critical dimension dc​=3. This is the upper critical dimension of an even scalar interaction applied to powers four and six.
The Ginzburg criterion makes the reason for failure of the Landau approximation quantitative. In the ordered quartic theory, M2=∣r∣/u, V′′(M)=2∣r∣ and ξ2=K/(2∣r∣). The fluctuation of the order parameter averaged over a correlation volume has the scale
⟨(δϕ)2⟩ξ​≍kB​T∫∣q∣≲ξ−1​(2π)dddq​Kq2+2∣r∣1​≍KkB​T​ξ2−d.
(11)
Compared with the squared mean order parameter, the Ginzburg criterion therefore gives
M2⟨(δϕ)2⟩ξ​​≍K2kB​Tu​ξ4−d.
(12)
For d<4 this ratio grows without bound as the correlation length diverges. The assumption that fluctuations are small relative to the ordered value fails arbitrarily near the transition, and the interacting renormalization-group fixed point determines the critical exponents. For d>4 the ratio tends to zero. At d=4 scale counting is marginal, and the running quartic coupling produces logarithms.
Similarly, at the tricritical point M2≍K/v​ξ−1, so the multicritical Ginzburg ratio scales as (kB​Tv​/K3/2)ξ3−d. The Landau-Ginzburg theory remains a useful effective description below its upper critical dimension; it is its fluctuation-free exponent calculation that fails, not the possibility of including fluctuations in the theory.

Take the symmetric scalar Landau free energy with u>0, r=at for a positive constant a, and leading equation of state
H=rM+uM3.
(13)
At zero conjugate field, minimization gives M=0 for t>0 and M=±−r/u​ for t<0. Selecting the positive pure thermodynamic phase,
M=ua​​(−t)1/2.
(14)
Consequently the order-parameter critical exponent is β=1/2​.
Differentiate the equation of state at fixed temperature, within the selected phase:
χ=∂H∂M​​t​=r+3uM21​={(at)−1,(2a∣t∣)−1,​t>0,t<0.​
(15)
Both sides diverge as ∣t∣−1, so the magnetic-susceptibility critical exponent is γ=1​. The leading above-to-below magnetic susceptibility amplitude ratio is two. These are the ordinary mean-field critical exponents, valid under the fluctuation qualification explained above.

Write βT​=(kB​T)−1 for inverse temperature to distinguish it from the order-parameter critical exponent. A normalized blocking kernel Bb​(σ′,σ) is a nonnegative conditional distribution of coarse spins given the fine spins, normalized by
∑σ′​Bb​(σ′,σ)=1.
(16)
For deterministic blocking it can impose a majority rule or block average, with a Dirac delta replacing the sum constraint for continuous coarse variables. Each block occupies bD original cells, so the new lattice spacing and number of sites are
a′=ba,N′=b−DN.​
(17)
The physical volume NaD is unchanged. After p blockings, ap​=bpa and Np​=b−pDN.
Define the coarse statistical Hamiltonian by the exact Boltzmann-weight identity
∑σ​Bb​(σ′,σ)e−βT​H(u,σ)=e−βT​[H(Ru,σ′)+Ng(u)].
(18)
The operator family must include all generated interactions for this renormalization-group transformation to be exact; restricting it to a few couplings is an approximation. The scalar g(u) has energy units and is the contribution per original site that is independent of σ′. Restoring the original constant term NC gives
H(u′,σ′)+N′C′=H(Ru,σ′)+N[C+g(u)],
(19)
hence
u′=Ru,C′=bD[C+g(u)].​
(20)
This convention measures g per original site; defining a constant per new site would move a factor bD into its definition.
The identity-operator contribution to renormalization-group free energy Cp​ records the configuration-independent normalization generated by the eliminated spins. It includes their contribution to free energy, including entropy and measure factors, and need not be independent of temperature. It has no effect on normalized probabilities of the retained spins, but is indispensable for the total partition function and its temperature derivatives. Iterating its recursion gives
Cp​=bpD[C0​+∑j=0p−1​b−jDg(uj​)].
(21)
Thus Np​Cp​ is the original constant NC0​ plus the accumulated free-energy contributions of the eliminated degrees of freedom.
To obtain the additive free-energy recursion under blocking, use energy-valued free energy per current lattice site,
F(u,C)=−βT​N1​logZ(u,C,N)=C+f0​(u).
(22)
The blocking kernel normalization implies
Z(u,C,N)=Z(Ru,C′,N/bD),F(u,C)=b−DF(Ru,C′).
(23)
Substituting the recursion for C′ cancels C, giving
f0​(u)=b−Df0​(Ru)+g(u).
(24)
A second step gives f0​(u0​)=b−2Df0​(u2​)+g(u0​)+b−Dg(u1​), and induction proves
f0​(u0​)=b−pDf0​(up​)+j=0∑p−1​b−jDg(uj​).​
(25)
For dimensionless free energy −logZ/N, multiply F,f0​,g,C throughout by βT​.
The singular free-energy density must be distinguished from the exact per-site representative f0​. If a(u) is a regular background and fs​=f0​−a, then
fs​(u)=b−Dfs​(Ru)+gs​(u),gs​(u)=g(u)−a(u)+b−Da(Ru).
(26)
Thus the same displayed inhomogeneous recursion holds with fs​,gs​ after subtraction. When a suitable analytic background solves a(u)=g(u)+b−Da(Ru), it leaves a homogeneous singular recursion. Analytic subtraction of an inhomogeneous renormalization recursion explains this separation. A renormalization-group free-energy resonance can obstruct analytic subtraction and produce a logarithm. The original function g originates in the short-distance spins that were summed out, rather than in an extra external interaction.
Near a renormalization-group fixed point u∗​, linearize the transformation:
R(u∗​+δu)=u∗​+Aδu+O(∣δu∣2).
(27)
An eigenvector of A with eigenvalue byi​ defines a scaling coordinate ti​ with ti′​=byi​ti​ to leading order. A relevant operator has yi​>0: its coupling grows towards large distances, so it must be tuned to reach the fixed point. An irrelevant operator has yi​<0 and dies away; a marginal operator has yi​=0 and requires higher-order flow analysis. An operator of scaling dimension xi​ has yi​=D−xi​.
The coarse spins also require a multiplicative normalization. In continuum notation, write
x′=x/b,ϕ′(x′)=bxϕ​ϕ<​(bx′),xϕ​=2D−2+η​,
(28)
where ϕ<​ is the retained field and η is its anomalous dimension. This restores the normalization of the critical long-distance connected correlation function. Since a uniform field conjugate to an order parameter enters through ∫dDxHϕ, it has eigenvalue exponent yh​=D−xϕ​=(D+2−η)/2.
Let t be the thermal relevant coordinate with exponent yt​>0. After regular backgrounds have been removed, the leading singular free-energy density obeys
fs​(t,H)=B−Dfs​(Byt​t,Byh​H)
(29)
for a large total scale factor B=bp. Choosing B=∣t∣−1/yt​ gives the scaling hypothesis for critical phenomena
fs​(t,H)=∣t∣D/yt​Φ±​(H∣t∣−yh​/yt​).
(30)
Two temperature derivatives give the heat-capacity critical exponent; one conjugate-field derivative gives the order-parameter critical exponent; two such derivatives give the magnetic-susceptibility critical exponent. Scaling the correlation length gives ξ(t)=Bξ(Byt​t). Consequently,
α=2−yt​D​,β=yt​D−yh​​,γ=yt​2yh​−D​,ν=yt​1​.
(31)
At t=0, instead choose B=∣H∣−1/yh​; differentiating gives δ=yh​/(D−yh​). These scaling relations for critical exponents assume that irrelevant variables can be set to their fixed-point values without singular consequences. A dangerously irrelevant coupling or logarithmic correction requires keeping the corresponding dependence.
For the Gaussian field theory, integrate out the Fourier modes in Λ/b<∣q∣<Λ and retain ϕ<​. The modes are independent at zero external field, so this Gaussian thinning transformation changes the normalization by a Gaussian determinant and leaves a quadratic statistical Hamiltonian for the retained modes. Restore the cutoff with
x′=x/b,ϕ′(x′)=b(D−2)/2ϕ<​(bx′).
(32)
In the integrated statistical Hamiltonian, the volume element contributes bD, each gradient contributes b−1, and each field contributes b−(D−2)/2. Thus the coefficient κ−1 of the gradient energy is unchanged, while
(m2)′=b2m2,h′=b(D+2)/2h.
(33)
In particular yt​=2, yh​=(D+2)/2 and η=0. The Gaussian critical exponents and the Gaussian order-parameter scaling index therefore give the requested indices
α=24−D​,β=4D−2​.​
(34)
The sign convention +hϕ corresponds to the physical conjugate field H=−h and does not affect these powers.
There are physical qualifications to these Gaussian indices. The strictly quadratic Gaussian field theory is stable for positive m2 and has no stable ordered phase for negative m2; its purported spontaneous β is therefore the formal Gaussian order-parameter scaling index. A stabilizing quartic term gives an actual ordered phase. Below four dimensions that term is relevant and changes the exponents; above four dimensions it is a dangerously irrelevant coupling and gives the actual mean-field β=1/2, rather than the Gaussian hyperscaling value.
The heat capacity power can also be checked directly. Apart from constants, the zero-field Gaussian free-energy density is
f0​=2kB​T​∫∣q∣<Λ​(2π)DdDq​log(κ−1q2+m2).
(35)
If m2∝t, its singular second temperature derivative is controlled by ∫dDq(q2+κm2)−2. For 0<D<4, rescaling q=κm2​k gives a power tD/2−2, as above. At D=4 there is a logarithm, and for D>4 an analytic background dominates the heat capacity. The negative formal Gaussian α then describes the subleading singular piece, with logarithmic resonances at appropriate even dimensions. This is the Gaussian specific-heat infrared threshold, and it prevents confusing singular indices with the entire observable.

The correlation length is the distance over which fluctuations remain appreciably correlated. More precisely, in a massive phase the large-distance connected correlation function
Gc​(x)=⟨ϕ(x)ϕ(0)⟩−⟨ϕ⟩2
(36)
has an exponential factor e−∣x∣/ξ, possibly multiplied by a power. Equivalently, ξ−1 is the lowest inverse length in the long-wavelength fluctuation spectrum. In the Gaussian field theory with unit gradient coefficient, Gc​(q)∝(q2+mR2​)−1 gives ξ=1/mR​ for positive renormalized mass.
At an ordinary continuous phase transition, the restoring force for long-wavelength order-parameter fluctuations vanishes and there is no finite correlation scale. The critical connected correlation function decays as a power rather than exponentially, so ξ diverges. The correlation-function susceptibility sum rule relates magnetic susceptibility to the integral of this connected correlation function; its divergence is consistent with correlations extending over increasingly large distances. Interactions change the powers, so the renormalized mass and the bare mass need not vanish at the same temperature.
Absorb the thermal normalization into the action and set
r=u2=Λ2m2(Λ,T)​,λ=g(Λ,T)Λ−ϵ,d=4−ϵ,Kd​=(2π)dΩd​​.
(37)
Here r, although denoted u2 in the question, is a signed mass-squared coordinate, not a nonnegative square. This matters because its interacting fixed-point value is negative. Increasing b=log(Λ0​/Λ) eliminates high-momentum modes and moves towards the infrared. The initial values at b=0 are r0​=m2(Λ0​,T)/Λ02​ and λ0​=g(Λ0​,T)Λ0−ϵ​.
To derive the flow, use the momentum-shell decomposition of a scalar field ϕ=φ+χ, where χ has momenta Λe−db<∣q∣<Λ. Its Gaussian shell covariance is (q2+m2)−1. Integrating out χ while preserving low-momentum observables gives
Seff​[φ]=S0​[φ]−log⟨e−V[φ+χ]⟩>​,V=4!g​∫ddx(φ+χ)4.
(38)
The cumulant expansion of a coarse-grained free energy yields ⟨V⟩>​−21​⟨V2⟩>,c​+⋯. By Wick theorem,
⟨V⟩>​=24g​∫ddx[φ4+6φ2G>​(0)+3G>​(0)2].
(39)
The last term is field independent, while the quadratic term gives the one-loop shell mass renormalization in scalar quartic theory
δm2=2g​I1​,I1​=∫shell​(2π)dddq​q2+m21​.
(40)
This tadpole is independent of the external momentum, so no gradient renormalization occurs at this order.
For the quartic correction, the mixed vertex is (g/4)∫φ2χ2. Its connected second cumulant uses ⟨χ2(x)χ2(y)⟩c​=2G>​(x−y)2 and gives
−16g2​∫ddxddyφ2(x)φ2(y)G>​(x−y)2.
(41)
Extracting the local zero-external-momentum quartic interaction, the integral over x−y is I2​=∫shell​ddq(2π)−d(q2+m2)−2. Comparing its coefficient with gφ4/4! gives the one-loop shell quartic renormalization
δg=−23​g2I2​.
(42)
The negative sign comes from the second cumulant; the numerical factor incorporates the three quartic channels.
For the thin spherical shell, radial integration gives
I1​=Kd​1+rΛd−2​db+O(db2),I2​=Kd​(1+r)2Λd−4​db+O(db2).
(43)
The shell is stable when 1+r>0, including a small negative critical mass. Because dΛ/db=−Λ, differentiating the dimensionless couplings contributes their engineering dimensions 2 and ϵ. Together with the shell corrections this yields the renormalization-group flow
dbdr​=2r+2Kd​​1+rλ​,dbdλ​=ϵλ−23Kd​​(1+r)2λ2​.​
(44)
These are the requested equations with r=u2. They express independence of the long-distance theory from the arbitrarily chosen ultraviolet cutoff, to the retained perturbative order; higher interactions and gradient corrections enter at higher orders.
Besides the Gaussian fixed point (r,λ)=(0,0), a nonzero fixed point satisfies
λ∗​=3Kd​2ϵ​(1+r∗​)2,0=2r∗​+3ϵ​(1+r∗​).
(45)
Thus, within these truncated equations,
r∗​=−6+ϵϵ​,λ∗​=3Kd​2ϵ​(6+ϵ6​)2.
(46)
Since Ω4​=2π2 and K4​=1/(8π2), their leading epsilon expansion is
r∗​=−6ϵ​+O(ϵ2),λ∗​=316π2ϵ​+O(ϵ2).​
(47)
The rational expressions from the truncated flow are not a calculation of the genuine higher-order coefficients.
To assess stability, linearize in δr,δλ about this Wilson-Fisher fixed point. The stability matrix of a renormalization-group fixed point is
M=​2−2(1+r∗​)2Kd​λ∗​​(1+r∗​)33Kd​λ∗2​​​2(1+r∗​)Kd​​ϵ−(1+r∗​)23Kd​λ∗​​​​.
(48)
Using the fixed-point equation, its diagonal entries are 2−ϵ/3 and −ϵ, respectively. Its upper off-diagonal entry is 1/(16π2)+O(ϵ) and its lower off-diagonal entry is O(ϵ2). Hence its eigenvalues are
yt​=2−3ϵ​+O(ϵ2),yirr​=−ϵ+O(ϵ2).
(49)
The interaction perturbation along the critical surface decays towards the infrared for small positive ϵ, whereas the thermal perturbation grows. Therefore infrared attraction requires tuning to the critical surface. The source's unqualified wording cannot mean attraction in both coupling directions: the positive thermal eigenvalue is precisely what produces the divergence of the correlation length. This is the thermal relevant direction at the Wilson-Fisher fixed point.
Let τ be the thermal scaling coordinate, a linear combination of δr and δλ corresponding to the relevant left eigenvector of M. To leading order, τ=δr+[1/(32π2)+O(ϵ)]δλ is possible. Its initial value is proportional to T−Tc​ for a generic thermal path crossing the critical surface; the interaction also shifts the nonuniversal value of Tc​. In the linear regime,
τ(b)≃eyt​bτ(0).
(50)
Choose b∗​ so that ∣τ(b∗​)∣ is of order one. At this scale the dimensionless correlation length is finite. Restoring the eliminated length scale gives
ξ≍Λ0−1​eb∗​≍∣τ(0)∣−1/yt​≍∣T−Tc​∣−ν.

There is no field anomalous dimension at one loop in this scalar quartic theory, since the one-loop self-energy is momentum independent; this is consistent with η=O(ϵ2).
