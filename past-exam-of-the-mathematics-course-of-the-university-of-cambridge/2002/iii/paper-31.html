Work with the filtration relative to which the given Brownian motion or Markov jump process has its stated properties. All martingale assertions below concern every finite time interval; no infinite-horizon uniform integrability is claimed.
First put C=supx​∣σ(x)∣. The solution has the representation Xt​=∫0t​σ(Xs​)dBs​, and the Itô isometry gives
EXt2​=E∫0t​σ(Xs​)2ds≤C2t.
(1)
Thus X is a square-integrable martingale, not just a local martingale. Applying the Itô formula to its square cancels the finite-variation term and gives
Zt​=2∫0t​Xs​σ(Xs​)dBs​.
(2)
The expected squared integrand integral is bounded by
4E∫0t​Xs2​σ(Xs​)2ds≤4C2∫0t​C2sds=2C4t2.
(3)
Therefore both X and Z are true square-integrable martingales. This is the bounded diffusion coefficient gives square martingales criterion. Only bounded measurability of the coefficient and existence of the stipulated solution have been used.
For the jump process, the Lévy kernel of a Markov jump process uses displacements: a mark y sends the state x to x+y. Let Λ=supx​λ(x) and V=supx​v(x). The bounded total rate prevents explosion; the number of jumps on a finite interval is dominated by a rate-Λ Poisson process. Let N(ds,dy) record the jump displacements. Its predictable compensator of a jump measure is
ν(ds,dy)=K(Xs−​,dy)ds.
(4)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives ∫∣y∣K(x,dy)≤λ(x)v(x)​, so the zero first moment is absolutely meaningful. Since that moment vanishes,
Xt​=∫0t​∫R​y(N−ν)(ds,dy),EXt2​=E∫0t​v(Xs−​)ds≤Vt.
(5)
The compensated integral is a square-integrable martingale: its isometry follows first for bounded marks from conditional compensation and then by truncation in the squared-integrand norm.
The jump version of the square Itô formula is Xt2​=2∫0t​Xs−​dXs​+∑s≤t​(ΔXs​)2. Hence
Zt​=2∫0t​Xs−​dXs​+∫0t​∫R​y2(N−ν)(ds,dy).
(6)
The first term is square integrable because
4E∫0t​Xs−2​v(Xs−​)ds≤2V2t2.
(7)
The second is an integrable martingale: the expected absolute uncompensated jump-square sum is at most Vt, and the compensator identity, multiplied by any bounded event known at an earlier time, makes its increments conditionally centered. Values Xs​ and Xs−​ agree for Lebesgue-almost every time, so the displayed compensation is exactly the one defining Z. Thus X and Z are true martingales in the jump case as well. A fourth moment of the jumps is unnecessary; in particular we have not asserted that this Z must be square integrable. This is centered finite-rate jump kernel gives square martingales.

This proves the local martingale property up to a possible hitting of the origin. For the annulus exit time T, Mt∧T​ is bounded between logr and logR, so it is a true uniformly integrable martingale.
The exit is finite almost surely. Indeed ∣Bt​∣2−2t is a martingale, so stopping at T∧t gives 2E(T∧t)=E∣BT∧t​∣2−1≤R2−1. Letting t→∞ proves ET<∞. Bounded convergence in the logarithmic martingale now yields
EMT​=M0​=0.​
(10)
If p is the probability of reaching radius r before radius R, continuity gives
plogr+(1−p)logR=0,p=log(R/r)logR​​.
(11)
Hitting zero before reaching radius R would require reaching every positive radius r first. Its probability is bounded by p, which tends to zero as r↓0. Any finite-time path hitting zero is bounded before that time and hence hits zero before exiting some integer-radius disk. A countable union over those disks proves Bt​=0 for every t≥0, almost surely. This is planar Brownian motion avoids a fixed point; it also makes M a globally defined continuous local martingale.
To decide whether it is a true martingale, compute its expectation directly. Condition on B0​ if necessary and use rotation to take B0​=(1,0) and write Bt​=B0​+Wt​. Conditional on ρ=∣Wt​∣, the angle of Wt​ is uniform. The angular average of a logarithmic potential is
2π1​∫02π​log∣1+ρeiθ∣dθ=logmax(1,ρ).
(12)
For ρ<1 this follows by taking the real part of the convergent power series for log(1+ρeiθ): each nonconstant Fourier term integrates to zero. For ρ>1 factor out ρ and apply the same argument to 1/ρ. The equality at one follows by the integrable limiting logarithmic singularity. All interchanges are valid: the shifted Gaussian density is bounded near the origin, where ∫01​r∣logr∣dr<∞, and its Gaussian tail controls logarithmic growth.
The radius ρ has density (ρ/t)e−ρ2/(2t). Consequently, for t>0,
EMt​=∫1∞​logρtρ​e−ρ2/(2t)dρ=∫1∞​ρe−ρ2/(2t)​dρ>0.​
(13)
Since M0​=0, M is not a martingale, despite integrability at each deterministic time. This is the logarithmic radius of planar Brownian motion example.
The optional hint is consistent with this conclusion. The upper-radius exit T(2,0) is finite and never occurs at zero. After that exit the stopped square is (log2)2; before it, a radius in [1/2,2] also gives a logarithmic square no larger than (log2)2, while a radius below 1/2 contributes precisely the extra indicated term. This proves the hinted inequality without assuming that the unstopped process is a true martingale.

A simple predictable process can be written
Hs​=∑j=0m−1​ξj​1(tj​,tj+1​]​(s),0=t0​<t1​<⋯<tm​≤∞,
(14)
where each ξj​ is bounded and Ftj​​-measurable. Define its integral to start at zero:
(H⋅M)t​=∑j=0m−1​ξj​(Mt∧tj+1​​−Mt∧tj​​).
(15)
Refining the partition leaves this sum unchanged. Every summand is a martingale, by conditioning its future increment on the information at tj​. Bounded coefficients and the L2 bound on M make the integral an L2-bounded continuous martingale. Its terminal value is the same sum with M∞​ substituted at any infinite endpoint; L2 martingale convergence supplies this limit.
We need the conditional square identity, rather than assuming independent increments. The Doob L2 maximal inequality gives Esupt​∣Mt​∣2<∞. Localize the square identity for M2−[M] at level and bracket stopping times. The stopped identity and this maximal bound give E[M]∞​<∞ by monotone convergence. Now
supt​∣Mt2​−[M]t​∣≤supt​∣Mt​∣2+[M]∞​
(16)
is integrable, so localization passes to conditional expectations and M2−[M] is a uniformly integrable martingale. Expanding a squared increment and using E(Mt​∣Fs​)=Ms​ proves
E((Mt​−Ms​)2∣Fs​)=E([M]t​−[M]s​∣Fs​),s≤t≤∞.
(17)
The infinite endpoint follows from L2 convergence and integrability of the bracket. The initial value M0​ need not be zero: the integral itself is zero-starting.
In the square of the terminal integral, all cross terms vanish. For j<k, the jth increment and both coefficients in its cross term are measurable at tk​, whereas the conditional mean of the kth increment there is zero. For the diagonal terms, the preceding conditional identity can be multiplied by the bounded ξj2​. Therefore
E(H⋅M)∞2​​=j∑​E[ξj2​(Mtj+1​​−Mtj​​)2]=j∑​E[ξj2​([M]tj+1​​−[M]tj​​)]=E(H2⋅[M])∞​​.​
(18)
This proves the Itô isometry for these simple integrands. Ordered stopping-time intervals also work, replacing the conditional identity by optional stopping theorem, but deterministic intervals suffice for the approximation requested here.
For that approximation, put μ(A)=E∫01​1A​(ω,s)ds on the predictable sigma-algebra. This is a finite measure. That sigma-algebra is generated, apart from the zero-time slice of measure zero, by rectangles A×(s,t] with A∈Fs​. The indicator of every such rectangle is a bounded simple predictable process.
Here is a density proof. Let V be the closed linear span of these indicators in L2(μ), and let D contain the predictable sets whose indicators belong to V. The whole space belongs to D; complements stay in D; and disjoint countable unions do also, because their finite partial indicator sums converge in L2 under the finite measure. Thus D is a Dynkin system containing the generating rectangle pi-system. The Pi-lambda theorem makes it the entire predictable sigma-algebra. Bounded predictable simple functions therefore belong to V. Truncating any L2(μ) function and then approximating its truncated range by finitely many levels shows that every such function belongs to V. A finite sum of rectangle indicators can be rewritten on one common deterministic time partition, with the coefficient on each interval measurable at its left endpoint; it is exactly an allowed simple process.
Consequently choose Hn of this form with ∥Hn−H∥L2(μ)​<1/n. This proves
E∫01​∣Hsn​−Hs​∣2ds⟶0.​
(19)
It is density of simple predictable processes for finite measures, with a proof of the actual approximation step.
Finally apply the preceding isometry to Mt​=Bt∧1​, whose bracket is t∧1. The elementary integrals In​=∫01​Hsn​dBs​ satisfy
E∣In​−Im​∣2=E∫01​∣Hsn​−Hsm​∣2ds⟶0.
(20)
Define the Itô integral as the L2 limit of these elementary integrals. The same identity shows independence of the approximating sequence, linearity, mean zero, and E(∫01​Hs​dBs​)2=E∫01​Hs2​ds. Applying the Doob inequality to the difference processes also gives uniform-in-time L2 convergence on [0,1], with a continuous martingale version of the integral.

Write f=u+iv. The Cauchy-Riemann equations give vx​=−uy​, vy​=ux​ and Δu=Δv=0. Since the two Brownian coordinates are independent, [X]t​=[Y]t​=t and [X,Y]t​=0. The Itô formula therefore becomes
dUt​=ux​(Zt​)dXt​+uy​(Zt​)dYt​,dVt​=−uy​(Zt​)dXt​+ux​(Zt​)dYt​.
(27)
Stop in balls on which the derivatives are bounded. The stopped integrals are martingales, proving that U and V are local martingales. Their coefficient rows have equal squared length and are orthogonal. Thus
[U]t​=[V]t​=∫0t​∣f′(Zs​)∣2ds,[U,V]t​=0.​
(28)
These are quadratic covariations of an analytic Brownian image; orthogonality does not in general imply independence of the two transformed coordinates.
For the exit law, put c=2a2. Squaring sends the positive quadrant to the upper half-plane, sends a+ia to ic, and the Möbius transformation w↦(w−ic)/(w+ic) sends the upper half-plane to the unit disk with the starting point sent to zero. Its composition is the displayed conformal map. The exit time is finite: it is the minimum of the two almost surely finite one-dimensional hitting times of zero. There is no hit at the quadrant's corner, since a planar Brownian motion started away from zero avoids that point.
We can justify the uniform image exit law without assuming a conformal-time-change theorem. For each integer n≥1, the real and imaginary parts of f(Zt∧T​)n are bounded martingales obtained from harmonic functions. At the start their values are zero. The bounded convergence theorem at the finite exit time gives Ef(ZT​)n=0. The boundary image has modulus one, and conjugation gives the vanishing negative Fourier moments as well. Trigonometric polynomials are dense among continuous functions on the unit circle, so these moments characterize the uniform distribution there.
Consequently the real variable W=ZT2​ is the inverse image of a uniform point eiΘ under that Möbius transformation:
W=ic1−eiΘ1+eiΘ​=−ccot(Θ/2).
(29)
The Jacobian of this change of variable gives the centered Cauchy distribution of scale c:
pW​(w)=π(w2+c2)c​,w∈R.
(30)
Positive w corresponds to exit at w​ on the real axis; negative w corresponds to exit at i−w​ on the imaginary axis. Pulling back by w=±r2 therefore gives the complete boundary law
P(ZT​∈dr)=π(r4+4a4)4a2r​dr,P(ZT​∈idr)=π(r4+4a4)4a2r​dr(r>0).​
(31)
Here dr on each axis denotes length along that axis, not two-dimensional area measure. Each density integrates to 1/2, with no remaining atoms. Equivalently the chosen axis is a fair Bernoulli choice independent of the radius, and
P(∣ZT​∣≤r)=π2​arctan(2a2r2​),r≥0.​
(32)
This is Brownian exit law from a quadrant, obtained as a harmonic measure and not as an absolutely continuous planar density.

Here is a finite-horizon Girsanov theorem statement with the sign fixed explicitly. Let B be a Brownian motion under P, and let θ be predictable with ∫0T​θs2​ds<∞ almost surely. If
Lt​=exp(∫0t​θs​dBs​−21​∫0t​θs2​ds),0≤t≤T,
(33)
is a true martingale with ELT​=1, then the measure Q defined by dQ=LT​dP makes Bt​−∫0t​θs​ds a Brownian motion up to T. The sufficient Novikov condition is Eexp(21​∫0T​θs2​ds)<∞. Merely being a local martingale is not a sufficient density condition.
For a deterministic absolutely continuous function h with h(0)=0 and derivative g∈L2[0,T], take θ=g. Novikov's condition holds because its exponential is deterministic and finite. Under Q, the canonical process has the law of a Brownian motion plus h. Thus for every bounded measurable path functional F,
EF(B+h)=E[F(B)exp(∫0T​gs​dBs​−21​∫0T​gs2​ds)].​
(34)
This derives the Cameron-Martin theorem density formula from the Girsanov theorem, rather than assuming the two measures have the same drift convention. Replacing h by −h changes the sign of the stochastic integral.
The question's path space is the whole half-line, not just one finite horizon. When g∈L2(0,∞), the density martingale satisfies
ELt2​=exp(∫0t​gs2​ds)≤e∥g∥22​.
(35)
It has a uniformly integrable limit L∞​=exp(∫0∞​gs​dBs​−∥g∥22​/2) of mean one, strictly positive almost surely. Integrals here converge in L2 and almost surely. The finite-time identities then identify L∞​ as the density on the full path sigma-algebra, since finite-time cylinder events generate it. This is Cameron-Martin shifts on infinite Wiener path space. Finite-horizon absolute continuity of measures alone does not imply infinite-horizon absolute continuity of measures.

There is no density on the full infinite path space; the two laws are mutually singular. Under Wiener measure, Bt​/t→0 almost surely. Under the shifted law, (Bt​+t)/t→1 almost surely, so the same measurable tail event separates the laws.
For completeness the Brownian limit follows from the Gaussian bound at integers and the maximal bound on each interval [n,n+1]: for every fixed η>0, both ∑n​P(∣Bn​∣>ηn) and ∑n​P(sup0≤s≤1​∣Bn+s​−Bn​∣>ηn) are finite. The Borel-Cantelli lemma and continuity fill the gaps between integers. Intersect over positive rational η. This proves the limit and the claimed singularity, which is infinite-horizon singularity of Brownian motion with constant drift.
On every finite horizon there nevertheless is the density eBT​−T/2. Its failure to give a global density is exactly the distinction required by the printed half-line path space.
