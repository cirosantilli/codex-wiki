The Pound-Rebka experiment measures a small gravitational redshift by comparing nuclear emission and resonant absorption at different heights in Earth's field. The 14.4keV transition of iron-57 supplies the gamma-ray frequency standard. The Mössbauer effect permits recoil-free emission and absorption by nuclei bound in solids, giving a sufficiently narrow resonance. A source and absorber separated vertically by about 22.6m have a predicted fractional frequency difference of order 2.5×10−15. A calibrated Doppler effect from slow source motion measures the shift through its effect on resonant absorption. Interchanging the upper and lower arrangements reverses the gravitational contribution and helps separate it from intrinsic source-absorber offsets; temperature-dependent frequency shifts must also be controlled. The observed change agrees with the weak-field prediction.
The prediction follows either from an accelerating laboratory and the Equivalence principle, or directly from a static metric. With x0=ct, write g00​=N2≃1+2Φ/c2. The time-translation Killing vector field gives a conserved photon energy Eξ​=pa​ξa, while a static observer has four-velocity ua=ξa/N and measures hν=Eξ​/N. Thus
νemitted​νreceived​​=Nreceived​Nemitted​​≃1+c2Φemitted​−Φreceived​​.​
(11)
For an upward ray over height H, Δν/ν≃−gH/c2; downward propagation has the opposite sign. The equivalent compensating Doppler speed has magnitude gH/c, about 7.4×10−7ms−1 for this baseline. Identical clocks at rest have dτ=Ndt, so a clock at higher potential runs faster relative to the common static coordinate time. This does not mean that the nuclear transition changes its locally measured frequency with height: the comparison involves transporting a photon between different local proper-time standards.
The weak equivalence principle asserts universal free fall for test bodies. The Einstein equivalence principle adds local Lorentz invariance and local position invariance for nongravitational experiments in a freely falling laboratory. The redshift comparison tests the clock aspect of local position invariance and supports a metric description of gravity. The strong equivalence principle extends these requirements to self-gravitating bodies and local gravitational experiments, including the contribution of gravitational binding energy. Their outcomes should be independent of the external location and motion of a freely falling laboratory when tidal effects are negligible. A nuclear redshift experiment is therefore not a test of the whole strong equivalence principle, and its success does not uniquely establish the gravitational field equations.
The geometrical realization uses the Levi-Civita connection of a spacetime metric. At any event, normal coordinates make gab​=ηab​ and the Christoffel symbols vanish. Freely falling test particles follow geodesics; local nongravitational equations take their special-relativistic form at that event. The Riemann curvature tensor need not vanish, so finite laboratories can measure tidal geodesic deviation. The strong principle concerns removing a uniform external gravitational field locally, not removing curvature throughout an extended region.
For a perfect fluid, local rest-frame isotropy and the absence of heat flux or viscosity give Ta^b^=diag(ρ,p,p,p), where ρ is rest-frame energy density. With the PDF's signature (+−−−), uaua​=1 and hab=gab−uaub projects spatially. Its covariant form is
Tab=ρuaub−phab=(ρ+p)uaub−pgab.​
(12)
The pressure sign follows from the negative spatial metric; it gives positive diagonal stresses in the rest frame. Local stress-energy conservation, ∇a​Tab=0, produces the two projections
ua∇a​ρ+(ρ+p)∇a​ua=0,(ρ+p)ua∇a​ub=(gba−ubua)∇a​p.

The first is the energy equation, including pressure work; the second is the relativistic Euler equation. Its rest-frame spatial pressure force is −∇p.
Further assumptions are needed to derive the Einstein field equations. Take a local generally covariant metric equation, linear in curvature and of second differential order, and demand compatibility with stress-energy conservation. The available rank-two tensors are Rab​, Rgab​ and a constant multiple of gab​. Defining Rab​=Rcacb​, the contracted Bianchi identity gives ∇aRab​=21​∇b​R. Hence the combination Rab​+αRgab​ is identically divergence-free only when α=−21​. This determines the Einstein tensor Gab​=Rab​−Rgab​/2; a cosmological constant term remains allowed.
The coupling sign must be matched to the actual curvature convention. Here Rabcd​ represents [∇d​,∇c​], and a static weak field has R00​≃−∇2Φ/c2. With negligible pressure and cosmological term, a trial equation Gab​=CTab​ implies R00​≃Cρmass​c2/2. Comparing with the Poisson equation ∇2Φ=4πGρmass​ fixes C=−8πG/c4. Thus the Einstein equation in the sequential-derivative curvature convention is
Gab​+Λgab​=−c48πG​[(ρ+p)ua​ub​−pgab​].​
(14)
Equivalently, Rab​=Λgab​−(8πG/c4)(Tab​−Tgab​/2), with T=ρ−3p. Pressure therefore gravitates as well as supplying a material force. The cosmological constant is not fixed by the local Newtonian matching.
Setting c=1 for the action normalization, the same metric equation follows from the Einstein-Hilbert action Sg​=(2κ)−1∫−g​(R−2Λ)d4x, where κ=8πG, together with a matter action obeying δSm​=21​∫−g​Tab​δgabd4x. After the metric boundary term is removed or supplied separately, the bulk variation is proportional to Gab​+Λgab​+κTab​. This also makes the sign convention explicit. General covariance, the field equation and the contracted Bianchi identity are mutually consistent with the fluid conservation laws. The experiment motivates the local metric description; the curvature and conservation assumptions, plus the Newtonian limit, supply the additional route to the field equation.
