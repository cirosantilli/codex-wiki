Choose a reference circular orbit at radius r0​, rotate the frame with Ω=Ω(r0​)ez​, and introduce local Cartesian coordinates x=r−r0​, y=r0​(ϕ−Ωt) and vertical coordinate z. On scales small compared with r0​, neglect curvature of the coordinate directions but retain the leading differential rotation. The background velocity relative to the rotating frame is
u0​=r0​Ω′(r0​)xey​=−2Axey​,A=−21​r0​Ω′(r0​).
(28)
Thus A=3Ω/4 for a Keplerian disk. The shearing sheet retains Coriolis acceleration and the linear tidal acceleration. The radial shearing-sheet tidal potential is −2ΩAx2, whose force 4ΩAxex​ balances the background Coriolis acceleration. If vertical tidal gravity is included, its equilibrium contribution is balanced by a background pressure p0​(z) and disappears when that equilibrium is subtracted.
Write the total velocity as u=u0​+v. In the rotating-frame magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation, background advection is (u0​⋅∇)v=−2Ax∂y​v and advection of the background by the perturbation is (v⋅∇)u0​=−2Avx​ey​. The uniform-viscosity diffusion of the linear background shear is zero. For a solenoidal magnetic field the Lorentz force per unit mass is
μ0​ρ(∇×B)×B​=μ0​ρB⋅∇B​−∇2μ0​ρB2​.

For constant finite electrical conductivity σe​, put η=(μ0​σe​)−1. The resistive induction equation with uniform magnetic diffusivity is ∂t​B+u⋅∇B=B⋅∇u+η∇2B. Substituting the background shear supplies the term −2ABx​ey​ and the same background-advection operator as in the momentum equation. The two further conditions are
∇⋅v=0,∇⋅B=0.​
(30)
They express incompressibility and the absence of magnetic monopoles, respectively.
Now take real plane waves with common phase ϑ=k(t)⋅x and real wavevector. The solenoidal conditions become
k⋅v=0,k⋅B=0.
(31)
Every field depends on position only through ϑ. Hence for either transverse vector field P and either wave field Q, (P⋅∇)Q=(P⋅k)∂ϑ​Q=0. This proves that all the displayed quadratic advection and tension terms vanish pointwise, including products involving conjugate amplitudes. These waves are therefore exact solutions at finite amplitude, not merely a linearization.
Acting with D0​=∂t​−2Ax∂y​ on the phase yields
D0​ϑ=(k˙x​−2Aky​)x+k˙y​y+k˙z​z.

k˙x​=2Aky​,k˙y​=k˙z​=0,kx​(t)=kx0​+2Aky​t.​
(33)
The remaining amplitude equations are
v˙−2Avx​ey​+2Ω×v=−ikψ​−νk2v,B˙=−2ABx​ey​−ηk2B.​
(34)
Differentiating the velocity constraint determines the pressure amplitude:
ik2ψ​=4Aky​vx​−2k⋅(Ω×v).

The resistive induction equation preserves its constraint because k˙⋅B=2Aky​Bx​ cancels the corresponding shear term in k⋅B˙. The specified magnetic field has no spatially uniform part. Thus there is no background magnetic field producing an additional linear magnetic-tension coupling. The scalar ψ is the modified pressure; the physical pressure can include a constant and a second spatial harmonic from −B2/(2μ0​) while ψ retains the stipulated plane-wave form. This is an exact zero-mean magnetic shearing wave.
For decay, take the real part of the scalar product of the velocity-amplitude equation with its complex conjugate. Pressure does no work because k⋅v=0, and the real part of Coriolis work is zero. Therefore

A non-axisymmetric wave has ky​=0. For nonzero differential shear and positive ν,η, the negative cubic terms dominate the linear upper bound on shear work. Consequently
v(t)⟶0,B(t)⟶0(t→∞).​
(40)
This cubic-exponent decay of a nonaxisymmetric shearing wave allows transient energy growth before the winding to short radial wavelengths makes diffusion dominant. The magnetic amplitudes can also be written explicitly as
B(t)=e−ηJ(t)(Bx​(0), By​(0)−2AtBx​(0), Bz​(0)),

Positivity of the dissipative coefficients is necessary: with kz​=0, an inviscid vertical velocity amplitude can remain constant, and an ideal vertical magnetic amplitude can remain constant when η=0. The asserted decay applies to the viscous, finitely conducting model; taking an ideal limit before the long-time limit changes the conclusion.
