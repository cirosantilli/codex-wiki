For a categorical presheaf X:Cop→Set and A∈C, the Yoneda lemma gives a natural bijection
Nat(C(−,A),X)≅X(A),θ⟼θA​(1A​).
(1)
To construct its inverse, take x∈X(A) and set θUx​(h)=X(h)x for h:U→A. For k:V→U, the functor law gives X(k)θUx​(h)=X(hk)x=θVx​(hk), so θx is a natural transformation. Conversely, naturality of any θ at h:U→A gives θU​(h)=X(h)θA​(1A​). Thus the two constructions are inverse, proving the bijection.
The bijection is natural in X: a natural transformation t:X→Y sends the distinguished element x to tA​(x) on either side. It is natural in A: for f:A→B, precomposing a transformation C(−,B)→X with postcomposition by f sends its distinguished element x∈X(B) to X(f)x. These equations prove the claimed naturality, rather than just an objectwise correspondence. Reversing morphisms gives the covariant form Nat(C(A,−),Z)≅Z(A) for Z:C→Set.

If f:A→B is a monomorphism, each function C(U,A)→C(U,B) given by postcomposition with f is injective. Consequently, if two natural transformations into HA​ become equal after H∙​(f), their components are equal, and the transformations themselves are equal. This proves that H∙​(f) is a monomorphism in the presheaf category.
Conversely, suppose H∙​(f) is a monomorphism and u,v:U→A satisfy fu=fv. Then H∙​(f)H∙​(u)=H∙​(f)H∙​(v). Cancellation gives H∙​(u)=H∙​(v), and faithfulness of the Yoneda embedding gives u=v. Hence H∙​(f) is monic exactly when f is monic.

We first justify the relevant pointwise epimorphism in a functor category criterion. A componentwise surjective function gives an epimorphism, because equality of two composites can be cancelled at each component. Conversely, for a natural transformation t:X→Y, form the pointwise pushout P=Y⨿X​Y. Here is a precise base change of a pullback square statement. Suppose A=B×D​C is a pullback in a category and t:D′→D is a morphism. Assume the pullbacks in a category
A′=A×D​D′,B′=B×D​D′,C′=C×D​D′
(4)
exist, where A→D is the common composite. The induced square with vertices A′,B′,C′,D′ is a pullback in a category; equivalently,
(B×D​C)×D​D′≅(B×D​D′)×D′​(C×D​D′).​
(5)
Indeed, compatible morphisms T→B′ and T→C′ give morphisms b:T→B, c:T→C, and a common v:T→D′, with the composites of b,c to D both equal to tv. The original pullback in a category gives a unique morphism h:T→A with projections b,c. Its composite to D is tv, so the defining pullback in a category for A′ gives a unique morphism T→A′ above h,v. Its maps to B′ and C′ are the prescribed ones by uniqueness in those pullbacks in a category. Conversely, these projections recover b,c,v, so every possible factorization is the same. For a functor D:J→C with finite indexing category, form the finite products in a category
P=∏j∈ObJ​D(j),Q=∏u:i→j in J​D(j).
(6)
Define s,t:P⇉Q by requiring the coordinate indexed by u:i→j to be D(u)πi​ for s and πj​ for t. Let e:L→P be their equalizer, and put λj​=πj​e. The equality se=te says exactly that D(u)λi​=λj​ for every morphism u, so these morphisms form a categorical cone.
For any other categorical cone (xj​:X→D(j)), the product in a category gives a unique x:X→P with πj​x=xj​. The categorical cone equations give sx=tx, so the equalizer gives a unique xˉ:X→L with exˉ=x. This is exactly the universal property of a categorical limit. Empty indexing products are terminal objects, so the construction includes the empty diagram in a category. Thus finite products and equalizers give all finite limits.

Let 1 be a terminal object. The pullback in a category of X→1←Y has precisely the universal property of the binary product in a category X×Y: every pair of morphisms to X,Y is compatible over 1. Repeated binary products in a category, together with 1 as the empty product, give all finite products in a category.
For f,g:X⇉Y, form the pullback in a category of the categorical diagonal ΔY​:Y→Y×Y along ⟨f,g⟩:X→Y×Y. Its projection e:E→X satisfies fe=ge. If h:T→X satisfies fh=gh, the pair h,fh gives a unique morphism into this pullback in a category. Conversely any such morphism must project to that pair. Hence e is the equalizer of f,g. A precise commutation of iterated categorical limits statement is the following. For small categories I,J and F:I×J→D, if every column categorical limit and the resulting J-limit exist, then
limj​limi​F(i,j)≅lim(i,j)​F(i,j).
(10)
If every row categorical limit and the resulting I-limit also exist, then
jlim​ilim​F(i,j)≅(i,j)lim​F(i,j)≅ilim​jlim​F(i,j).​
(11)
Each isomorphism is the unique one identifying all projections to F(i,j). Indeed, the preceding part proves that each iterated categorical limit has the universal property of the whole product diagram in a category. Its uniqueness gives the canonical isomorphism. Equivalently, categorical cones with vertex X over the product diagram in a category are exactly compatible families, indexed by j, of categorical cones over F(−,j); regrouping the same compatibility equations cannot change their representing object. In a complete category all the stated existence hypotheses hold for small I,J. The theorem is about categorical limits with categorical limits, and makes no unconditional claim about commuting a categorical limit with a colimit.

A poset P becomes a category with the elements of P as objects and exactly one morphism x→y when x≤y, with no morphism otherwise. Reflexivity supplies the identity morphisms and transitivity supplies composition in a category; uniqueness of morphisms proves associativity. A functor f:P→Q is exactly a monotone function: it must send x≤y to f(x)≤f(y), and this condition suffices because all the required morphisms are unique. Antisymmetry says that isomorphic objects of this category are equal.

For the general alternative, let G:D→C be a functor between locally small categories, with D a complete category. The Freyd general adjoint functor theorem says that G has a left adjoint if and only if it preserves small categorical limits and satisfies the solution-set condition. Explicitly, for every c∈C, there must be a set of morphisms xj​:c→Gdj​ such that every x:c→Gd has the form G(h)xj​ for some j and h:dj​→d. Equivalently, each comma category (c↓G) has a weakly initial set.
We prove the initial-object lemma for complete categories with a weakly initial set. Let E be a locally small category which is complete and has a weakly initial set (wj​)j∈J​. Its product in a category p=∏j​wj​ is weakly initial: for each z, choose a morphism wj​→z and compose with the projection p→wj​. The hom-set E(p,p) is a set. Form e:i→p equalizing every endomorphism of p with 1p​, by taking the equalizer of the two morphisms
p⇉∏v∈E(p,p)​p,
(34)
whose v-coordinates are v and 1p​. Then ve=e for every endomorphism v, and e is monic. There is a morphism i→z for every z, by composing e with a weakly initial morphism out of p.
To prove uniqueness, let u,v:i⇉z and take their equalizer q:w→i. Weak initiality of p gives a:p→w. The composite eqa:p→p is an endomorphism, so eqae=e. Cancel the monomorphism e to obtain qae=1i​. Thus q is both monic and split epic, hence an isomorphism: if qk=1, cancellation from qkq=q gives kq=1. Since uq=vq, it follows that u=v. Therefore i is an initial object, proving the lemma fully.
Now assume categorical limit preservation and the solution-set condition. The comma category (c↓G) is locally small. It is complete: for a small diagram in a category (dj​,xj​), take its underlying categorical limit l in D. Since G preserves this categorical limit, the compatible xj​:c→Gdj​ give a unique x:c→Gl. The same universal property shows that (l,x) is the categorical limit in the comma category. Its weakly initial set and the proved lemma therefore supply an initial object (Fc,ηc​). For t:c→c′, its universal property defines a unique Ft:Fc→Fc′ with G(Ft)ηc​=ηc′​t. Uniqueness proves the identity morphism and composition in a category laws and the naturality of η. The same universal property gives the natural bijection D(Fc,d)≅C(c,Gd), so F⊣G.
For necessity, an adjunction F⊣G supplies the singleton weakly initial set (Fc,ηc​) at c. It also proves that G preserves categorical limits: maps c→Gd correspond to maps Fc→d, and compatible families of the latter factor uniquely through any categorical limit in D. Hence the image categorical cone under G has the required universal property. This proves both directions of the general alternative.
For the special alternative, the limit form of the special adjoint functor theorem says: if D is complete, locally small and well-powered, with a small cogenerating family (Qi​)i∈I​, and C is locally small, then G:D→C has a left adjoint exactly when it preserves small categorical limits. A cogenerating set means that unequal parallel morphisms u,v:X⇉Y are distinguished by some morphism Y→Qi​. We prove the needed solution-set condition, so that the general theorem applies.
Fix x:c→Gd. A supporting subobject of d is a monomorphism m:d′→d through whose image under G the morphism x factors. There is at least one, namely 1d​. Because D is well-powered, choose a set of representatives of all these supporting subobjects. A limit-preserving functor preserves monomorphisms: a map is monic exactly when its diagonal into its self-pullback in a category is an isomorphism, and both the pullback in a category and this isomorphism are preserved. Consequently each factorization of x through Gm is unique. Form the intersection of subobjects of the supporting representatives, as a wide pullback in a category over d. The unique lifts of x are compatible and, since G preserves that categorical limit, they give a factorization
x=G(m0​)x0​,m0​:d0​↪d,x0​:c→Gd0​.
(35)
This is a minimal supported subobject. More explicitly, if q:e↪d0​ also supports x0​, then m0​q is one of the supporting subobjects of d. The intersection therefore factors through m0​q, giving r:d0​→e with m0​qr=m0​. Monicity of m0​ gives qr=1d0​​; monicity of q then makes q invertible.
For any u,v:d0​→Qi​, if G(u)x0​=G(v)x0​, the equalizer of u,v supports x0​, because G preserves that equalizer. Minimality makes it invertible, so u=v. We have therefore obtained injective functions
D(d0​,Qi​)⟶C(c,GQi​),u⟼G(u)x0​.
(36)
Let Si​ be the realized image subset of the fixed set C(c,GQi​). Use the unique morphism corresponding to each s∈Si​ to form the evaluation embedding into cogenerator products
d0​⟶PS​=∏i∈I​∏s∈Si​​Qi​.
(37)
This morphism is monic: if two morphisms into d0​ have identical product composites, every morphism from d0​ to every Qi​ gives identical composites, and the cogenerating property makes the two morphisms equal. There is only a set of possible families of subsets (Si​), and hence a set of possible products PS​. Each has only a set of subobjects by well-poweredness, and each chosen subobject a has only a set of morphisms c→Ga by local smallness. Take all these pairs (a,z:c→Ga) as a weakly initial set. Every original (d,x) receives a morphism from a pair representing its (d0​,x0​), through m0​. Thus the set is weakly initial in (c↓G).
The general theorem now supplies a left adjoint. Necessity again follows from preservation of categorical limits by a right adjoint. This proves the special alternative, including its size argument. Using only the realized subsets Si​ avoids assuming that an arbitrary object admits a morphism into every cogenerator. Both adjoint functor theorem alternatives are proved.

A monoidal category consists of a category C, a bifunctor ⊗:C×C→C, a monoidal unit object I, and natural isomorphisms
αA,B,C​:(A⊗B)⊗C→A⊗(B⊗C),λA​:I⊗A→A,ρA​:A⊗I→A.
(52)
The associator obeys the pentagon identity for a monoidal category,
αA,B,C⊗D​αA⊗B,C,D​=(1A​⊗αB,C,D​)αA,B⊗C,D​(αA,B,C​⊗1D​),
(53)
and the unitors obey the triangle identity for a monoidal category,
(1A​⊗λB​)αA,I,B​=ρA​⊗1B​.
(54)
Composition in a category here acts from right to left. The first equation compares two paths from ((A⊗B)⊗C)⊗D to A⊗(B⊗(C⊗D)); the second compares two ways of removing the intervening unit.
It is precisely a bicategory with one object ∗. The category B(∗,∗) is C: its objects are the 1-morphisms and its morphisms are the 2-morphisms. Horizontal composition in a category is ⊗, the identity 1-morphism is I, and the associator and unitors give the weak associativity and unit constraints. Vertical composition in a category is ordinary composition in a category in C; the interchange law follows because ⊗ is a bifunctor. The bicategory pentagon and triangle axioms are exactly the two equations above. Conversely the sole hom-category of any one-object bicategory gives these data, with the convention that horizontal composition in a category uses the indicated ordering of tensor factors.

For the Category of sets, take the monoidal tensor product to be the Cartesian product, A⊗B=A×B, with unit a singleton 1. On morphisms use (f×g)(a,b)=(f(a),g(b)). Define the associator by ((a,b),c)↦(a,(b,c)) and the unitors by (∗,a)↦a and (a,∗)↦a. These are natural isomorphisms, with inverses obtained by rebracketing or adjoining ∗. Both paths in the pentagon send (((a,b),c),d) to (a,(b,(c,d))), and both paths in the triangle send ((a,∗),b) to (a,b). This proves the axioms elementwise.
There is a second structure: use disjoint union A⨿B and unit ∅. Define the associator by preserving each element and its summand while changing the nested tags, and define the unitors by removing the empty summand. These are natural and invertible. In the pentagon, either path takes an element in any of the four summands to that same element in the same summand of A⨿(B⨿(C⨿D)); the triangle removes an empty intermediate summand on either path. Thus both axioms hold.
These product and coproduct monoidal structures on sets have different units, 1 and ∅, which are not isomorphic. They are even inequivalent as monoidal categories: an equivalence of the underlying Category of sets preserves terminal objects, so it cannot take the singleton unit of the product structure to the empty unit of the coproduct structure. Hence the monoidal structure on sets is not unique.
