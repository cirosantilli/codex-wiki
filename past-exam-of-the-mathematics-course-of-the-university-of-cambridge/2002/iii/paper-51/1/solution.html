Put a=κm2, with κ>0 and m=0. The relevant homogenization of a periodic advection-diffusion equation limit is a long horizontal wavelength observed on its diffusive time scale. Introduce X=εx and τ=ε2t, retaining the fast variables y,t. Since the velocity field is independent of x, no fast horizontal cell variable is needed. Write the multiple-scale expansion

The kernel of L on this periodic cell consists of constants: multiplying Lh=0 by h and integrating over the cell gives κ⟨∣hy​∣2⟩=0, after which ht​=0. Cτ​=κe​CXX​,κe​=κ+21​⟨Ug⟩=κ+2a​⟨g2⟩.​

For the second equality, multiply g′+ag=U by g and average over a period: ⟨gg′⟩=0. In particular the enhancement of diffusivity is nonnegative. Once this solvability condition holds, the remaining zero-mean forcing admits a periodic cell solution: its nonconstant spatial Fourier modes are damped by κn2m2, while its spatially constant mode has a periodic antiderivative because its temporal average is zero. This also explains the relaxation of initial transverse inhomogeneity. The first corrector has zero spatial mean, so χˉ​=C+O(ε2).
This derivation describes the large-scale, long-time limit, after times comparable to the period and to a−1. It does not give an exact closed equation for the spatial average of arbitrary finite-time data: that average contains a correlation between the velocity field and concentration. In physical coordinates the leading long-wavelength concentration therefore obeys χˉ​t​=κe​χˉ​xx​ asymptotically. The cell expansion, its bounded periodic correctors, and the decay of transverse cell transients justify this effective diffusivity of a periodic sinusoidal shear.
For constant U0​, the periodic cell solution is g=U0​/a. Consequently

This is Taylor dispersion: molecular diffusion moves particles between different horizontal velocities and turns their accumulated horizontal displacement into large-scale diffusion.
For the impulsive flow, g jumps by U1​ at each kick and decays exponentially between kicks. If G denotes its value just after a kick, periodicity requires G=e−aTG+U1​. Thus, within one period,
G=1−e−aTU1​​,g(t)=Ge−at(0<t<T),⟨g2⟩=2aTG2(1−e−2aT)​.

κe​=κ+4TU12​​(1−e−aT)21−e−2aT​=κ+4TU12​​coth(2aT​).​
(8)
The impulsive model can also be obtained by shrinking smooth pulses of fixed integrated strength. The passive scalar undergoes the exact horizontal shear map x↦x+U1​sin(my) at a kick, while the transverse coordinate is unchanged. The cell solution above is the corresponding limit of the smooth cell solutions. Using a one-sided value in Ug instead would miss the contribution from the jump of g2.
For aT≫1,
κe​=κ+4TU12​​[1+2e−aT+O(e−2aT)].
(9)
Transverse Brownian motion substantially decorrelates the shear phase between successive kicks. A uniformly distributed phase has horizontal displacement variance U12​/2 per kick, producing an extra diffusivity Var(Δx)/(2T)=U12​/(4T).
For aT≪1,
κe​=κ+2aT2U12​​+24U12​a​+O(U12​a3T2).​
(10)
Many kicks occur before transverse diffusion changes a particle's shear phase. Their leading effect is therefore the steady shear with mean velocity amplitude U1​/T, whose Taylor dispersion coefficient is precisely U12​/(2κm2T2). The divergence as κ→0 describes a very long decorrelation time; it does not imply infinite finite-time spreading. At κ=0 the phase never changes and, for a uniform phase, n kicks give variance n2U12​/2. The spreading is then ballistic transport, rather than diffusion. The long-time diffusive limit must precede the zero-diffusivity limit, with t≫a−1.
