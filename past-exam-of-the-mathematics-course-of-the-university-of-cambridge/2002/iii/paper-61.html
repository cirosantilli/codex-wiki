Interpret convergence in C[0,1] as uniform convergence, and work first with real continuous functions. Set en,j​=Un​(pj​)−pj​, for j=0,1,2, and put M=∥f∥∞​. The given barriers have one γ≥0 independent of both t and n. A positive linear operator on continuous functions preserves non-strict inequalities, so application of Un​ and evaluation at t give
∣Un​f(t)−f(t)Un​1(t)∣≤εUn​1(t)+γUn​((⋅−t)2)(t).
(1)
Strict pointwise inequalities need not remain strict under a positive operator; non-strict ones suffice. Linearity and the cancellation of the exact moments yield
Un​((⋅−t)2)(t)=en,2​(t)−2ten,1​(t)+t2en,0​(t)≥0.
(2)
Subtracting f(t) rather than f(t)Un​1(t) therefore gives the quadratic barrier estimate for positive approximation operators:
∥Un​f−f∥∞​≤ε(1+∥en,0​∥∞​)+M∥en,0​∥∞​+γ(∥en,2​∥∞​+2∥en,1​∥∞​+∥en,0​∥∞​).
(3)
For fixed ε, all three moment errors tend uniformly to zero. Thus limsupn​∥Un​f−f∥∞​≤ε. Since ε was arbitrary,
∥Un​f−f∥∞​⟶0.​
(4)
This completes the Korovkin theorem proof. Complex-valued functions follow by applying the real result to real and imaginary parts. Uniform convergence of the three test functions is essential to this uniform conclusion; purely pointwise test convergence should not be silently substituted.

The reproduction hypothesis gives U1=1, U(⋅)(t)=t and U((⋅)2)(t)=t2. If “quadratic” were read as degree exactly two, the same identities follow by subtracting pairs of such reproduced polynomials. Hence
U((⋅−t)2)(t)=0.
(5)
Apply the same positive-barrier argument as in part (a), now with no moment errors. It gives ∣Uf(t)−f(t)∣≤ε for every t and every ε>0. Therefore
Uf=f for every f∈C[0,1],U=I.​
(6)
The identity is itself a positive linear operator on continuous functions reproducing all quadratics, so existence as well as uniqueness is established. In particular, reproducing three small test functions is much stronger for a positive operator than merely fixing one finite-dimensional polynomial space without positivity.

The relevant uniqueness is uniqueness of the node set, with n≥1. Reordering the same nodes cannot change a constraint imposed at every node, so an unsorted “different sequence” obtained only by permutation would not give the claimed counterexample. Order the genuinely different nodes as 1≥t0​>t1​>⋯>tn​≥−1 and let Li​ be their Lagrange cardinal polynomials:
Li​(x)=∏j=i​(ti​−tj​)∏j=i​(x−tj​)​.
(7)
The denominator has sign (−1)i. For 1≤k≤n, repeated differentiation of its numerator gives
dxkdk​∏j=i​(x−tj​)​x=1​=k!∑S⊂{0,…,n}∖{i}∣S∣=n−k​​∏j∈S​(1−tj​).
(8)
All products are nonnegative. At most one factor 1−tj​ is zero, and because n−k≤n−1, some product omits that factor. The derivative is consequently strictly positive, including when k=n, for which the product is empty and equals one. This proves the endpoint derivative signs of Lagrange cardinal polynomials:
(−1)iLi(k)​(1)>0(1≤k≤n).​
(9)
Choose a single Lagrange interpolation polynomial by prescribing q(ti​)=(−1)i:
q(x)=∑i=0n​(−1)iLi​(x).
(10)
It satisfies ∣q(ti​)∣=1 at every prescribed node. Its leading coefficient is ∑i​1/∣∏j=i​(ti​−tj​)∣>0, so it has degree exactly n. The endpoint sign formula gives q(k)(1)=∑i​∣Li(k)​(1)∣ simultaneously for all k.
Interpolate the Chebyshev polynomial Tn​ on the same nodes. Since ∣Tn​(ti​)∣≤1,
q(k)(1)−Tn(k)​(1)=∑i=0n​∣Li(k)​(1)∣(1−(−1)iTn​(ti​))≥0.
(11)
The only points at which ∣Tn​∣=1 are its n+1 extrema. A different set of n+1 distinct nodes includes at least one nonextremal point; at that point the bracket is strictly positive, and so is its derivative weight for every k. Moreover Tn(k)​(1)>0: differentiating its differential equation gives Tn(k+1)​(1)=(n2−k2)Tn(k)​(1)/(2k+1), beginning with Tn​(1)=1 and ending at k=n−1. Thus
∥q(k)∥∞​≥q(k)(1)>Tn(k)​(1)=∣Tn(k)​(1)∣(1≤k≤n).​
(12)
The same q violates every sharp derivative bound, proving uniqueness of Chebyshev derivative-norming nodes for the given Markov-Duffin-Schaeffer theorem. The result concerns node sets or consistently ordered sequences; a mere permutation is the literal ordering exception noted above.

The original PDF prints O(nα), with a positive exponent. That premise is insufficient; the intended decay rate is O(n−α). Indeed every bounded continuous periodic function has En​(f)≤∥f∥∞​=O(nα). For an explicit counterexample, on [−π,π] set
h(0)=0,h(x)=log(e/∣x∣)1​ (0<∣x∣≤1),h(x)=1 (1≤∣x∣≤π),
(13)
and extend periodically. This is continuous, but ω(h,δ)≥1/log(e/δ) for 0<δ<1, which is not O(δα) for any α>0. Thus the sign correction is required in the actual printed question, not just in its converted TeX.
For real continuous 2π-periodic f, write En​ for best uniform approximation by degree-at-most-n trigonometric polynomials and ω(f,δ)=sup∣h∣≤δ​∥f(⋅+h)−f∥∞​. A quantitative inverse theorem for trigonometric approximation is
ω(f,1/n)≤nC​ν=0∑n​Eν​(f),n≥1,​
(14)
where C is universal. Here is a direct reason for the sum. Choose best approximants pj​ and 2r≤n<2r+1. Write
p2r​=p0​+(p1​−p0​)+∑j=1r​(p2j​−p2j−1​).
(15)
The Bernstein inequality for trigonometric polynomials gives
∥p2r′​∥∞​≤2E0​+∑j=1r​2j+1E2j−1​.
(16)
For j≥2, monotonicity of best errors bounds 2j+1E2j−1​ by 8∑ν=2j−22j−1−1​Eν​. The first terms are bounded by a fixed multiple of E0​. Also 2E2r​≤4n−1∑ν=0n​Eν​. Now
ω(f,1/n)≤2E2r​+n1​∥p2r′​∥∞​
(17)
proves the theorem, for example with a sufficiently large absolute constant C=20.
Suppose, as intended, Eν​(f)≤Aν−α for ν≥1, after enlarging A to include any finite exceptional indices, where 0<α<1. Since ∑ν=1n​ν−α≤n1−α/(1−α), the inverse theorem gives
ω(f,1/n)≤C(E0​/n+1−αA​n−α)≤Cf​n−α.
(18)
For 0<δ≤1 take n=⌊1/δ⌋. Then 1/(n+1)<δ≤1/n, so monotonicity of the modulus of continuity and n−α≤2αδα give ω(f,δ)≤2αCf​δα. For δ>1, the separate bound ω(f,δ)≤2∥f∥∞​≤2∥f∥∞​δα covers the remaining scales. Therefore the corrected conclusion is
En​(f)=O(n−α)⟹ω(f,δ)=O(δα),0<α<1.​
(19)
Now put β=log2/log3. The given best approximant to the Weierstrass function leaves the uniformly convergent tail ∑k=m+1∞​2−kcos(3kx). Its supremum norm is exactly 2−m, attained at x=0; hence
En​(g)=2−m(3m≤n<3m+1),En​(g)≍n−β.
(20)
The corrected inverse theorem gives ω(g,δ)=O(δβ). To show that this order is sharp, set hm​=π/3m+1. Every term of g(0)−g(hm​) is nonnegative, and for k≥m+1 the cosine equals −1 because 3k−m−1 is odd. Thus
g(0)−g(hm​)≥2∑k=m+1∞​2−k=21−m=4π−βhmβ​.
(21)
Given small δ>0, select m with hm​≤δ<3hm​. Then ω(g,δ)≥4(3π)−βδβ. Combining the estimates gives the precise small-scale order
ω(g,δ)≍δlog2/log3(δ↓0).​
(22)
The exact approximation error can also be verified independently of the supplied fact: at xj​=jπ/3m+1 the tail has alternating values (−1)j2−m. There are enough such points in one period for the trigonometric Chebyshev alternation theorem, since n<3m+1. This is the positive lacunary trigonometric series mechanism behind the best partial sums.

Use the standard partition normalization of B-splines, with order k meaning degree k−1. The Cox-de Boor recurrence on arbitrary increasing spline knots is
Ni,k​(x)=ti+k−1​−ti​x−ti​​Ni,k−1​(x)+ti+k​−ti+1​ti+k​−x​Ni+1,k−1​(x),
(23)
starting with Ni,1​(x)=1[ti​,ti+1​)​(x). Terms with zero denominator are interpreted as zero in the usual repeated-knot convention. For the uniform knots here, ti​=i, this reduces to
Ni,k​(x)=k−1x−i​Ni,k−1​(x)+k−1i+k−x​Ni+1,k−1​(x),k≥2.​
(24)
The index of the second lower-order B-spline is i+1, not i−1. Both lower-order supports combine to give [i,i+k].

For real continuous f, the Chebyshev alternation theorem says that p∈Pn​ is the unique best uniform approximation to f precisely when there are n+2 ordered points 0≤x0​<⋯<xn+1​≤1 and a sign σ∈{−1,1} such that
f(xj​)−p(xj​)=σ(−1)j∥f−p∥∞​.
(31)
Here Pn​ means degree at most n. Best approximants exist: a minimizing sequence is bounded in the finite-dimensional polynomial space, and finite-dimensional equivalence of norms gives a convergent coefficient subsequence. When f=p, the zero error is treated separately; the derivative-sign hypothesis below excludes that case.
Let pn​ be a best degree-at-most-n approximant and suppose for contradiction that En​(f)=En+1​(f). Since f(n+1)>0, f is not a degree-at-most-n polynomial and its error is nonzero. The same pn​ would be best in Pn+1​, so the Chebyshev alternation theorem at that degree supplies n+3 alternating error extrema. The intermediate value theorem gives at least n+2 distinct zeros of e=f−pn​, one between each consecutive pair of extrema.
Repeated Rolle's theorem then forces a zero of e(n+1). But pn(n+1)​=0, whereas e(n+1)=f(n+1)>0 everywhere. This contradiction proves
En​(f)>En+1​(f).​
(32)
The argument proves strict decrease of best polynomial approximation under a derivative sign; an everywhere negative (n+1)st derivative gives the same strict conclusion after replacing f by −f.

The ordinary interpolation formulation is unambiguous for strictly increasing knots; repeated knots require specified confluent interpolation or a limiting convention. First take distinct knots and k≥2, and for fixed t write Ft​(u)=(u−t)+k−1​. The two degree-at-most-k−1 Lagrange interpolation polynomials ℓi​(⋅,t) and ℓi+1​(⋅,t) agree at the k−1 shared knots ti+1​,…,ti+k−1​. Their difference is therefore
ℓi+1​(x,t)−ℓi​(x,t)=ci​(t)∏r=1k−1​(x−ti+r​)=ci​(t)ωi​(x).
(33)
This remains valid if their difference has lower degree: the coefficient is then zero. The leading coefficient of a degree-at-most-k−1 interpolant is its order-(k−1) divided difference. Comparing leading coefficients and using the divided-difference recursion gives
ci​(t)​=[ti+1​,…,ti+k​]Ft​−[ti​,…,ti+k−1​]Ft​=(ti+k​−ti​)[ti​,…,ti+k​]Ft​=Ni​(t).​
(34)
Consequently the Lee interpolation identity holds for every real x,t:
ωi​(x)Ni​(t)=ℓi+1​(x,t)−ℓi​(x,t).​
(35)
No differentiability of the truncated power in its argument was needed for this distinct-node proof.
Sum the identity over i=1,…,n. The right side telescopes, giving ∑i​ωi​(x)Ni​(t)=ℓn+1​(x,t)−ℓ1​(x,t). When tk​<t<tn+1​, all the nodes for ℓ1​ lie below t, so its interpolation data are zero and ℓ1​≡0. All the nodes for ℓn+1​ lie above t, where Ft​(u)=(u−t)k−1 is already a polynomial of the admissible degree. Uniqueness of polynomial interpolation gives ℓn+1​(x,t)=(x−t)k−1. Therefore the Marsden identity is
(x−t)k−1=i=1∑n​ωi​(x)Ni​(t),tk​<t<tn+1​,x∈R.​
(36)
For order one the empty product is one, the interpolants are constants and a consistent half-open interval convention for (u−t)+0​ gives the same telescoping proof. Nondecreasing repeated-knot sequences can be treated by the appropriate confluent or limiting convention wherever that definition is valid; merely specifying repeated data values does not uniquely determine the interpolation polynomials. The basic open knot interval in the Marsden identity avoids endpoint convention ambiguity.

Write r=x−u∗. If u∗ is a best approximation in the finite-dimensional linear subspace, then for every v in it and every real t,
0≤∥r−tv∥2−∥r∥2=−2tRe(r,v)+t2∥v∥2.
(37)
A nonzero real linear coefficient would make this negative for sufficiently small t of the appropriate sign. Hence Re(r,v)=0. In a complex inner product space, apply the argument to iv too; the imaginary part also vanishes. Thus (x−u∗,v)=0 for every v in the subspace.
Conversely, if that orthogonality holds, every other candidate has the form u∗+v, and the Pythagorean theorem in the inner product norm gives
∥x−u∗−v∥2=∥x−u∗∥2+∥v∥2≥∥x−u∗∥2.
(38)
Equality requires v=0. Therefore
u∗ is the unique best approximant ⟺ x−u∗⊥Un​.​
(39)
The next part constructs this orthogonal projection explicitly. No completeness of the whole inner product space is required for projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace.

Take the inner product linear in its first argument; for a real space no conjugation convention is needed. Insert u∗=∑j=1n​aj​uj​ into the orthogonality condition against each basis vector ui​. The normal equations are
j=1∑n​(uj​,ui​)aj​=(x,ui​),1≤i≤n.​

Equivalently Ha=b, where Hij​=(uj​,ui​) and bi​=(x,ui​). This orientation of the Gram matrix matches the stated inner-product convention. It is Hermitian and positive definite: for a nonzero coefficient vector a,
a∗Ha=​∑j​aj​uj​​2>0

Use the standard L∞​-oriented partition normalization of B-splines, in contrast to the unit-integral normalization. Thus Ni​≥0 and ∑i​Ni​(t)≤1 on [0,1], with equality for a complete basis on its basic knot interval. This terminology does not mean that each basis function's maximum is exactly one; for example an order-three cardinal spline has maximum 3/4. Assume the basis has positive support on the integration interval and is continuous, as is needed for a projection taking values in C[0,1].
Let
Hij​=∫01​Ni​(t)Nj​(t)dt,di​=∫01​Ni​(t)dt>0,D=diag(di​).
(42)
The ordinary Gram matrix H is positive definite by linear independence. The appropriate row-normalized, or mixed-normalization spline Gram matrix, is
G=D−1H,Gij​=∫01​Mi​(t)Nj​(t)dt,Mi​=Ni​/di​.​
(43)
Here Mi​ is a unit-integral nonnegative spline. Unlike H, G need not be symmetric. If the full support lies inside the interval, di​=(ti+k​−ti​)/k; otherwise use the actual interval integral rather than that full-support formula.
The normal equations for the L2​ orthogonal projection PS​f=∑i​ai​Ni​ say Ha=c, with ci​=∫fNi​. Divide each row by di​ to obtain
Ga=b,bi​=∫01​f(t)Mi​(t)dt.
(44)
Nonnegativity and unit integral give ∣bi​∣≤∥f∥∞​. The subpartition of unity for B-splines then gives
∣PS​f(t)∣≤∑i​∣ai​∣Ni​(t)≤∥a∥ℓ∞​≤∥G−1∥ℓ∞​∥f∥∞​.
(45)
Taking the supremum over t and then over nonzero f proves the uniform norm bound for B-spline orthogonal projection:
∥PS​∥C[0,1]→C[0,1]​≤∥G−1∥ℓ∞​.​
(46)
The row normalization is essential; replacing G with the ordinary Gram matrix would not be the same asserted estimate. The operator is defined on C[0,1] and maps into its finite-dimensional spline subspace, onto S, not onto all of C[0,1] as one phrase in the PDF says. Its range follows both from the formula and from reproducing every s∈S.
If “L∞​-normalized” were instead read literally as individually peak-one splines, their sum can exceed one. The basis-dependent factor K=∥∑i​Ni​∥∞​ must then be retained: ∥PS​∥∞​≤K∥(D−1H)−1∥ℓ∞​. Equivalently the displayed bound without an outside factor uses G=K−1D−1H. The standard partition-normalized convention has K≤1 and yields the sharper bound proved above.
