Give O(U) the compact-open topology. Choose a finite polygonal cycle Γ in U∖σA​(x) whose winding number is one on the Banach algebra spectrum and zero outside U. One can take the oriented boundary of a finite union of small squares surrounding the compact Banach algebra spectrum and contained in U. Define
Θx​(f)=2πi1​∫Γ​f(z)(z1−x)−1dz.

Here is a direct verification of the algebra properties and uniqueness using the allowed Runge theorem. The version required is that every holomorphic function on an open plane set is a locally uniform limit of rational functions with poles outside that set, allowing a polynomial part. A rational function r=p/q of this kind has the unambiguous value r(x)=p(x)q(x)−1, since the roots of q avoid the Banach algebra spectrum. These rational values form a unital algebra homomorphism.

Differentiating with respect to a gives the analogous result for every power (z−a)−k, and partial fractions give it for all the rational functions under consideration.
If rn​→f and sn​→g locally uniformly, the contour estimate makes their evaluated sequences converge. Since rn​sn​→fg locally uniformly and multiplication in A is continuous, their limits satisfy Θx​(fg)=Θx​(f)Θx​(g). Also Θx​(1)=1 and Θx​(Z)=x. It therefore agrees on rational functions and then, by Runge density and continuity, on all of O(U). This proves existence and uniqueness of the holomorphic functional calculus.

There is a simple polygonal arc from zero through its unbounded complementary component to outside a disk containing K, continued by a ray to infinity and avoiding K. A C-star algebra is a complex Banach algebra with a conjugate-linear involution satisfying (ab)∗=b∗a∗ and ∥a∗a∥=∥a∥2. The inequality ∥a∥2≤∥a∗∥∥a∥, and then the same inequality for a∗, show that the involution is isometric. In a nonzero unital C-star algebra, the identity has norm one.
For a Hermitian algebra element h, the exponential eith is unitary for real t and has norm one. If λ∈σ(h), then eitλ∈σ(eith): factoring eitz−eitλ=(z−λ)q(z) with entire q proves this, since the evaluated factors commute. Hence ∣eitλ∣≤1 for all positive and negative t, forcing Imλ=0. Thus Hermitian algebra elements have real Banach algebra spectrum.
For a normal C-star element a, commutation of a,a∗ gives
∥a2∥2=∥(a∗)2a2∥=∥(a∗a)2∥=∥a∗a∥2=∥a∥4.
(12)
The middle equality of norms uses the C-star identity on the Hermitian algebra element a∗a. Iterating along powers 2n and applying the spectral radius formula proves r(a)=∥a∥.
Now suppose A is commutative and unital. Every maximal ideal is closed: if it were dense, it would contain an element within distance less than one of the identity, hence an invertible element by the Neumann series, which is impossible for a proper ideal. Its quotient is a complex Banach division algebra and is therefore C by the Gelfand-Mazur theorem. Maximal ideals thus give characters. Conversely, every character φ satisfies φ(a)∈σA​(a), because applying it to an inverse of a−φ(a)1 would give a contradiction. Characters are consequently contractive. The converse spectral inclusion follows by placing the proper ideal generated by a−λ1 in a maximal ideal. We have proved
σA​(a)={φ(a):φ∈ΦA​}.
(13)
The character space ΦA​ is a weak-star closed subset of the dual unit ball, characterized by φ(1)=1 and φ(ab)=φ(a)φ(b). The Banach-Alaoglu theorem makes it compact Hausdorff. Define the Gelfand transform by a(φ)=φ(a). For Hermitian h, its values are real; writing a=h+ik with both terms Hermitian shows a∗=a. In the commutative algebra every element is normal, so
∥a∥∞​=r(a)=∥a∥.
(14)
The transform is therefore an isometric unital star-homomorphism and has closed range in C(ΦA​). Its range separates characters by their definition and is closed under complex conjugation. The complex Stone-Weierstrass theorem makes that range dense, hence equal to C(ΦA​). This proves the Commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem:
A≅C(ΦA​)isometrically as a star algebra.​
(15)
For a nonunital C-star algebra, its C-star unitization can be constructed without assuming an operator representation on a Hilbert space. Represent A by left multiplication on itself, which is isometric because testing La​ on a∗/∥a∥ gives ∥La​∥=∥a∥. Adjoin the identity operator and set ∥z∥=∥Lz​∥. For z=a+λ1 and b∈A, the C-star identity in A gives ∥zb∥2=∥b∗z∗zb∥≤∥b∥∥Lz∗z​b∥. Consequently ∥Lz​∥2≤∥Lz∗z​∥≤∥Lz∗​∥∥Lz​∥. Applying this also to z∗ proves that the new involution is isometric and that its C-star identity holds. The extension by the identity is complete, since the isometric image of A is closed and one adjoins just one dimension. Apply the unital result to a commutative A+; the character ∞ given by a+λ1↦λ has kernel A. Under the isomorphism, this kernel consists of the continuous functions vanishing at ∞. Thus A≅C0​(ΦA​) in the nonunital case, with locally compact character space ΦA​=ΦA+​∖{∞}.
For a Hermitian h in a possibly noncommutative algebra, apply this result to the commutative unital C-star subalgebra C∗(1,h) in the unitization. Its Banach algebra spectrum at h equals the ambient Banach algebra spectrum: both Banach algebra spectra are real, the complement of the ambient compact real Banach algebra spectrum is connected, and the resolvent-component result of Question 1 retains that whole complement in the smaller algebra. Characters of this subalgebra are determined by their values at h, so its character space is homeomorphic to σ(h). We obtain the continuous functional calculus C(σ(h))→C∗(1,h).
Interpret R+ here as [0,∞). If σ(h)⊆[0,∞), apply this calculus to t↦t​, obtaining a Hermitian k with k2=h. In a nonunital algebra this element remains in A because the defining scalar function vanishes at zero. Conversely, if h=k2 with k=k∗, spectral mapping theorem and the real Banach algebra spectrum of k give σ(h)={t2:t∈σ(k)}⊆[0,∞). Hence nonnegative Banach algebra spectrum is equivalent to being the square of a Hermitian algebra element. A strict-positive interpretation would exclude h=0, and would not be equivalent to the assertion about an arbitrary Hermitian square.
Finally, use the two continuous real functions t↦max(t,0)​ and t↦max(−t,0)​ on σ(x). Their calculus values u,v are Hermitian, belong to A also in the nonunital case, and satisfy
x=u2−v2.​

This follows from the commutative C-star theorem applied to C∗(I,T,T∗), together with spectral permanence for C-star subalgebras, which is among the permitted C-star results.
Take the Hilbert space inner product linear in its first variable. For a vector ξ, let Hξ​={π(f)ξ:f∈C(K)}​. This cyclic subspace is invariant under every π(f) and its adjoint, so it is reducing. The functional f↦⟨π(f)ξ,ξ⟩ is positive because f≥0 implies π(f)=π(f​)∗π(f​). It has, by the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem, a finite regular positive Borel measure μξ​ on K. A representation of a Banach algebra is a complex-linear multiplicative map π:A→EndC​(X). A nonzero irreducible action of a unital algebra automatically has π(1)=I: the image of the idempotent π(1) is a nonzero invariant subspace and hence is all of X. An algebraically irreducible representation of a Banach algebra is a nonzero action if it has no nonzero proper invariant submodule, whether closed or not. A normed representation of a Banach algebra has π(a) bounded for each a on a preassigned normed vector space X; continuity of π as an operator-norm-valued map is a further conclusion. This must not be confused with topological irreducibility, which excludes only closed invariant subspaces. Unitizing the algebra extends a nonunital action by π(a+λ1)=π(a)+λI, so it is enough to discuss the unital case.
For ξ=0, irreducibility gives Aξ=X. The annihilator Lξ​={a:π(a)ξ=0} is a maximal left ideal: an intermediate left ideal gives an intermediate submodule of X. It is closed by the Neumann-series argument for maximal left ideals. Consequently A/Lξ​ transports a complete quotient norm to X, and left multiplication is contractive in this norm. Every algebraically irreducible representation of a Banach algebra can therefore be realized continuously on a Banach space. This alone does not prove continuity for its preassigned norm.
The kernel of π is the intersection of the closed left ideals Lξ​ and is a closed primitive ideal. The Jacobson radical is the intersection of all primitive ideals, equivalently the intersection of maximal left ideals. Thus these quotient representations detect semisimplicity. Equivalent algebra representations are related by a bijective module homomorphism.
The algebra of module endomorphisms is exactly CI. Indeed, if a module endomorphism D sends a fixed cyclic vector ξ to aξ, then D(bξ)=baξ. It is induced by bounded right multiplication by a on A/Lξ​, so is bounded for the quotient norm on an irreducible Banach module. Every nonzero module endomorphism is injective and surjective by irreducibility, and its inverse is bounded by the same argument. This commutant of an operator algebra is a closed division subalgebra of the bounded operators on this Banach module; the Gelfand-Mazur theorem makes it CI.
The Jacobson density theorem now says that for finitely many linearly independent ξ1​,…,ξn​ and arbitrary η1​,…,ηn​, one element of A sends all ξj​ to ηj​. Here is the induction behind it. The one-vector assertion is Aξ1​=X. Assuming the first n−1 coordinates can be prescribed, set L={a:aξj​=0 (j<n)}. Its image Lξn​ is an invariant subspace. If it is X, it adjusts the last image without changing the first ones. If it is zero, the rule (aξ1​,…,aξn−1​)↦aξn​ is a well-defined module map Xn−1→X. Each coordinate map is scalar by the module endomorphism result, forcing ξn​ to be a linear combination of the earlier vectors, a contradiction. This proves the assertion.
We can now give Johnson's continuity argument with its crucial construction. If X is finite dimensional, the closed kernel has finite codimension and A/kerπ is finite dimensional, so π is continuous. Suppose instead that X is infinite dimensional. Let
Y={ξ∈X:a↦π(a)ξ is continuous}.
(20)
This is an invariant submodule, because the orbit map of π(b)ξ is the orbit map of ξ composed with the bounded map a↦ab. Thus either Y=X or Y=0.
To rule out Y=0, choose linearly independent unit vectors ξ0​,ξ1​,…. There are elements an​∈A such that, with cn​=an​⋯a1​,
π(cm​)ξn​=0(m>n),{π(cn​)ξj​:j≥n} is linearly independent.
(21)
For completeness, construct them in the canonical quotient-norm Banach module. At stage n the current tail vectors are independent. In the closed left annihilator of its first vector, density permits arbitrary images of any finite list of the remaining vectors. The condition that finitely many such images are independent is open in this Banach subspace and dense: perturb any element by t times an element prescribing independent images; a suitable determinant is a nonzero polynomial in t, with only finitely many exceptional values. The Baire category theorem intersects these open dense conditions for all initial finite tail lists. The resulting an​ kills the first current vector and retains independence of the entire remaining tail. This is the countable annihilation sequence in an irreducible Banach module.
The gliding-hump continuity principle supplies the other ingredient: if E,F are Banach spaces, S:E→F is linear, Tj​:E→E and Un​:F→Fn​ are bounded, and Un​ST1​⋯Tm​ is continuous whenever m>n, then Un​ST1​⋯Tn​ is continuous eventually. The product form of this principle is recorded in Proposition 1.3 of Thomas's paper. Here is the contradiction mechanism. Rescale so ∥Tj​∥,∥Un​∥≤1 and put Pm​=T1​⋯Tm​. If infinitely many diagonal maps are discontinuous, choose increasing nj​ and successively tiny xj​ so
∥Unj​​SPnj​​xj​∥>j+1+∑i<j​∥Unj​​SPni​​xi​∥.
(22)
Also choose ∥xj​∥≤2−j/(1+maxi<j​∥Uni​​SPni​+1​∥). The convergent sum x=∑j​Pnj​​xj​ has, after its jth term, a tail factoring through Pnj​+1​. Applying that continuous composite bounds the tail contribution by one. The display forces ∥Unj​​Sx∥>j, contradicting ∥Unj​​Sx∥≤∥Sx∥. Only finite decompositions and the continuous tail composites are used; no unjustified interchange of a discontinuous map with an infinite sum occurs.
Apply this principle with E=A, F=B(X), where X is the completion in the original norm, and S(a) the bounded extension of π(a). Set Tj​(a)=aaj​ and Un​(R)=Rξn​. Then
Un​ST1​⋯Tm​(a)=π(a)π(am​⋯a1​)ξn​.
(23)
For m>n this is zero, hence continuous. For m=n it is the orbit map of a nonzero vector, hence discontinuous if Y=0. This contradicts the principle. Therefore Y=X.
Finally, apply the Uniform boundedness principle on the Banach space A to the continuous maps a↦π(a)ξ indexed by ∥ξ∥≤1. They are pointwise bounded because each individual π(a) is bounded in the original norm. It gives
∥π(a)∥≤C∥a∥(a∈A).​
(24)
This proves Johnson's continuity theorem for irreducible normed representations, including when the original normed vector space is incomplete. The quotient norm, countable density construction, and gliding hump explain the substantive steps rather than assuming the desired continuity in advance.

It suffices first to treat unital algebras and a unital homomorphism. A surjective homomorphism between unital algebras automatically preserves the identity. If B=0, continuity is immediate. In the nonunital case, extend to the forced unitizations by T+(a+λ1)=T(a)+λ1. This is still surjective and the unitization of a semisimple algebra is semisimple: its radical projects to zero in its scalar quotient, and its remaining intersection with the original algebra is its original radical. Continuity of the extension implies continuity of the original map.
An algebra homomorphism decreases the spectral radius, even without any continuity assumption, because it takes an inverse of λ1−a to an inverse of λ1−T(a). Suppose an​→0 in A and T(an​)→b in B. Fix any c∈B and choose u,v∈A with T(u)=b, T(v)=c. Put
w=vu,wn​=van​,d=T(w)=cb,dn​=T(wn​)⟶d.

Consider the polynomial qn​(z)=T((1−z)wn​+zw)=(1−z)dn​+zd. Its value at one is exactly d. On the outer circle ∣z∣=R,
rB​(qn​(z))≤∥qn​(z)∥≤∥d∥+(1+R)∥dn​−d∥.

rB​(cb)2≤R∥cb∥∥vu∥​.

This holds for every R>1, so rB​(cb)=0 for every c∈B.
This forces b into the Jacobson radical. To see the criterion directly, if b were outside a maximal left ideal L, then L+Bb=B, so 1=l+cb for some l∈L, c∈B. The equality rB​(cb)=0 makes 1−cb invertible, but this element equals l∈L, impossible in a proper left ideal. Hence b lies in every maximal left ideal. Since B is semisimple, b=0.
