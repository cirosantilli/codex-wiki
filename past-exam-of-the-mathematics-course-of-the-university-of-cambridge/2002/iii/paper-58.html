Eliminating A near O, for fixed nonzero λ1​, gives B˙=λ2​B−(a2​a3​/λ1​)B3+⋯: this pitchfork is supercritical for λ1​>0 and subcritical for λ1​<0. On λ2​=8λ1​/5, the mixed pair meets P. Writing A=−λ1​/a+u and η=λ2​−8λ1​/5, elimination gives B˙=ηB+(a2​a3​/λ1​)B3+⋯. This pitchfork is subcritical for λ1​>0 and supercritical for λ1​<0. A supercritical pitchfork here need not produce a stable mixed state: its other eigenvalue can already be positive. The negative mixed Jacobian determinant settles the full stability question. There is no Hopf bifurcation in this quadratic truncation.
Along λ2​=λ1​−Δ, the axial curves are A=0 and A=−λ1​/a. The mixed pair has the same plotted amplitude for its two signs of B:
AM​=−8a5(λ1​−Δ)​,B2=256a225(λ1​−Δ)(3λ1​+5Δ)​.

A=0: λ1​<min(0,Δ);A=−λ1​/a: λ1​>max(0,−5Δ/3).​
(11)

For Δ<0, O loses stability at λ1​=Δ and P becomes stable only at λ1​=−5Δ/3. Between these points none of the equilibria is stable. This local statement does not prescribe a bounded attractor of the quadratic truncation. All mixed portions are unstable.

Use a real physical field, so the three positive wavevectors are accompanied by their complex-conjugate modes. Modulo lattice translations, the effective symmetry group is G=T2⋊C6​. Its translation group action is
Tθ1​,θ2​​(A,B,C)=(eiθ1​A,eiθ2​B,e−i(θ1​+θ2​)C).
(12)
A 120∘ rotation cyclically permutes the amplitudes; a half-turn κ acts by simultaneous complex conjugation. Together these generate the sixfold rotation group. These formulas specify the group action independently of the choice of orientation of the three wavevectors.
For a nonzero real hexagon (h,h,h), its stabilizer subgroup is the rotation group C6​. A translation fixes it only when all three phase factors equal one. Cyclic permutation forces a fixed vector to have equal components, and the half-turn forces those components to be real. Therefore Fix(C6​)={(a,a,a):a∈R}. For a roll (r,0,0) with nonzero real r, the stabilizer subgroup consists of the translations T0,θ​ and the half-turn, with κT0,θ​κ=T0,−θ​. It is S1⋊C2​, isomorphic to O(2) as an abstract group even though spatial reflections are absent. The circle eliminates B,C from its fixed-point subspace of a group action, and the half-turn makes A real. Thus
Fix(Hhex​)=R(1,1,1),Fix(Hroll​)=R(1,0,0).​
(13)
If unreduced physical translations are used, the full lattice kernel is included in both stabilizer subgroups. The real representation on the six critical components is absolutely irreducible: translations distinguish the three Fourier pairs, rotations permute them, and the half-turn rules out a complex scalar in the commutant. The equivariant branching lemma consequently supplies generic steady branches of both axial types at a transverse crossing of the common linear eigenvalue. It guarantees existence, not stability or exhaustiveness of the possible branches.
Translation equivariance severely restricts the monomials in A˙. Through cubic order they are A, BC, ∣A∣2A, ∣B∣2A and ∣C∣2A: each has the same translation weight as A, using k1​+k2​+k3​=0. The half-turn forces their coefficients to be real; the 120∘ rotation gives the two cyclic counterpart equations. A spatial reflection would interchange B,C and require equal cross-couplings, but it is not a symmetry here. After a real amplitude rescaling makes the generic nonzero quadratic coefficient one, the rotational hexagon amplitude equations are
A˙=μA+BC−ν1​∣A∣2A−(ν2​+δ)∣B∣2A−(ν2​−δ)∣C∣2A,
(14)
with the other two equations obtained by cyclic permutation. All four coefficients are real; δ records the absence of spatial reflection. The normalization assumes the resonant quadratic coefficient is nonzero; its vanishing would be an additional degeneracy.
For the roll, r2=μ/ν1​. The radial eigenvalue is −2μ and the phase of the active mode is a neutral translation. The transverse variables (b,c) have matrix
(μ−(ν2​−δ)r2r​rμ−(ν2​+δ)r2​).
(15)
Its two real eigenvalues, each occurring twice in the full real linearization, are −(ν2​−ν1​)r2±δ2r4+r2​. Define d=ν2​−ν1​ and Dr​=d2−δ2. Strict attraction modulo translation is therefore equivalent to
ν1​>0,μ>0,d>0,Dr​>0,μ>Dr​ν1​​.​
(16)
Indeed the larger transverse eigenvalue is negative precisely when dr2>δ2r4+r2​, and squaring is legitimate only after requiring d>0. If δ2>d2, that inequality is impossible. Equality δ2=d2 is also insufficient because of the additional positive term r2. Any nonzero roll that exists with ν1​<0 already has a positive radial eigenvalue; the degenerate case ν1​=0 has no ordinary isolated nonzero roll branch.
For hexagons put N=ν1​+2ν2​. A nonzero real amplitude h satisfies μ=Nh2−h, or h=(1±1+4Nμ​)/(2N) when N=0. Separate real and imaginary disturbances. The real Jacobian matrix is cyclic, with diagonal −h−2ν1​h2 and off-diagonal entries h−2(ν2​±δ)h2. Its eigenvalues are
λs​=h−2Nh2,λ±​=−2h+2dh2±2i3​δh2.
(17)
The imaginary Jacobian matrix has every entry equal to −h, giving eigenvalues −3h,0,0. The two zero modes translate the hexagonal pattern. Thus the general strict stability conditions for any nonzero root h are
h>0,2Nh>1,dh<1,μ=Nh2−h.​
(18)
These conditions state orbital stability transverse to translations, not asymptotic convergence to one prescribed spatial phase. Equalities require a nonlinear bifurcation calculation.
When ν2​>ν1​>0, both d,N are positive. The upper positive hexagon branch is radially stable after its saddle-node bifurcation at μ=−1/(4N), h=1/(2N); it remains stable until h=1/d. At this second threshold the pair λ±​ crosses the imaginary axis with nonzero frequency if δ=0. The crossing is transverse, since dh/dμ=1/(2Nh−1) and the derivative of −2h+2dh2 with respect to h is two there. Hence, subject to the usual nonzero cubic coefficient, this is a Hopf bifurcation:
μH​=(ν2​−ν1​)22ν1​+ν2​​,ωH​=(ν2​−ν1​)223​∣δ∣​.​
(19)
For δ=0 the same threshold has two zero real eigenvalues, so it is not a Hopf bifurcation.
A possible phase portrait when neither steady branch is stable has an attracting periodic oscillation surrounding the unstable hexagon, with the three amplitudes waxing and waning cyclically. This is realized, for example, by ν1​=1, ν2​=2, δ=2 just above μH​=4. Rolls are unstable for every positive μ because δ2=4>d2=1. At the hexagon Hopf point h=1, the real spectrum is −9,±4i3​. Evaluation of the cubic Hopf normal form with a unit-norm critical eigenvector gives ReG21​=−64/27<0, so this example has a supercritical Hopf bifurcation and an attracting small limit cycle in the real invariant subspace. The figure shows such a cycle at μ=4.2 and an approaching trajectory; the positive octant is forward invariant because an amplitude's derivative at zero is the product of the other two. This is a possible portrait, rather than a claim that every parameter set with unstable rolls and hexagons has the same attractor. Stability asserted for the periodic orbit here is within the stipulated real subspace.

Locking within its existence range still requires an initial relative position in the stable root's basin; beyond the unstable root the front escapes instead.
These positional stability conclusions also describe the leading weakly perturbed front. Indeed v=A0′​>0 has no zeros, and L=(∂ξ​+v′/v)(∂ξ​−v′/v) is nonpositive in the L2 inner product. The limiting coefficients f′(0)=−3α2/16 and f′(q​)=−3α2/4 are negative. Thus the unperturbed shape modes are damped, with the neutral translation mode singled out by the solvability condition; the defect determines its stability at first order.
If c=0 and the locking inequality fails, ZT​ has no finite zero and has the sign of c. The separation grows without bound, the localized forcing of the front decays exponentially, and XT​→0: the defect passes the front, which approaches a stationary position in laboratory coordinates. The same escaping behavior occurs outside the locking basin when roots exist. If c=0 and K=0, the stationary defect drives the front away from it with ever decreasing speed; Z grows in magnitude logarithmically rather than tending to a finite locked position. Finally, when K=0, the front remains stationary for every c; when also c=0, every front position is a neutral equilibrium. These degenerate cases must not be included by dividing by K.

Near a spatially extended Hopf bifurcation, a critical oscillatory normal mode has a slowly varying complex amplitude. For a selected travelling-wave branch, a method of multiple scales expansion takes the form
u(x,t)=ε{A(X,T)v0​ei(k0​x−ω0​t)+complex conjugate}+O(ε2),X=ε(x−vg​t),T=ε2t.
(32)
Here the distance from onset is of order ε2, v0​ is the critical eigenvector, and vg​ is the group velocity. The comoving coordinate removes the first spatial derivative of the envelope. Projection at the first resonant order gives AT​=ρA+D0​AXX​−G0​∣A∣2A. Spatial translation and the temporal phase of the Hopf oscillation give a constant-phase symmetry A↦eiθA, which permits the cubic term ∣A∣2A but forbids a generic A2 term. The coefficients are complex because the envelope has both growth and frequency detuning. For a supercritical branch with positive real diffusion, real rescalings and a uniform phase rotation give the complex Ginzburg–Landau equation
AT​=A+(1+ib)AXX​−(1+ic)∣A∣2A,b,c∈R.​
(33)
A subcritical Hopf branch requires higher saturation terms instead. If spatial reflection makes opposite travelling waves critical together, two coupled amplitude equations are required initially; the scalar equation applies to a selected single-wave branch when its competing amplitude is stable.
Distant boundaries can still determine which travelling pattern is observed. They can select its phase and wavenumber, reflect a travelling disturbance, inject a competing wave, or let a growing wave packet leave the domain. In particular, amplification in a comoving frame can be convective wave-packet instability rather than absolute wave-packet instability at a fixed laboratory point. A finite-domain outcome then need not reflect intrinsic bulk modulation dynamics. Periodic boundary conditions are the appropriate idealization when studying bulk instabilities without end selection or reflected-wave forcing: they eliminate physical end layers and make the allowed envelope Fourier modes discrete. They do not assert that real distant boundaries are always negligible. Choose a periodic length compatible with the carrier, fix the phase winding, and compare perturbations within that same periodic problem. On an open physical domain the group velocity and actual boundary conditions must be restored.
The optimum carrier wavenumber corresponds to the spatially uniform envelope Af​=e−icT, often called a flat state. Its phase is arbitrary. Write A=e−icT(1+r)eiϕ with real small amplitude and phase disturbances. To first order,
rT​=−2r+rXX​−bϕXX​,ϕT​=−2cr+ϕXX​+brXX​.
(34)
A Fourier mode eλT+ikX therefore has matrix and dispersion relation
M(k)=(−2−k2−2c−bk2​bk2−k2​),λ2+(2+2k2)λ+2(1+bc)k2+(1+b2)k4=0.
(35)
The trace is negative. The amplitude mode at k=0 has eigenvalue −2, while the phase mode is neutral because constant phase shifts preserve the solution. Put D=1+bc. For every nonzero real k, the determinant is positive if D≥0, whereas D<0 makes it negative for 0<k2<−2D/(1+b2). Thus
1+bc>0: flat state stable modulo phase;1+bc<0: long-wave modulational instability.​
(36)
This is the Benjamin-Feir stability condition. At D=0, nonzero modes still decay, but the leading long-wave decay is fourth order rather than diffusive. On a periodic envelope interval of length L, the smallest nonzero wavenumber is 2π/L, so strict stability of all allowed nonzero modes requires
2D+(1+b2)(2π/L)2>0.
(37)
A sufficiently short periodic domain can therefore exclude the unstable band even when the infinite-domain Benjamin-Feir stability condition fails.
The amplitude is damped while the phase is slow. For a slowly varying phase, the leading slaved amplitude is r=−21​{bϕXX​+(ϕX​)2}+⋯. Substitution in the phase equation gives the diffusion term DϕXX​ and the nonlinear frequency shift (c−b)(ϕX​)2. The latter also follows directly from a constant phase gradient: an envelope with gradient q has amplitude squared 1−q2 and frequency c+(b−c)q2. Constant phase symmetry rules out undifferentiated ϕ; translation invariance gives constant coefficients. The leading comoving Ginzburg-Landau equation also has spatial parity, so odd linear derivatives are absent at this order. Its neutral phase eigenvalue expands as
λphase​(k)=−Dk2−Ek4+O(k6),E=2b2(1+c2)​.
(38)
This coefficient follows by inserting a power series for λ in the quadratic dispersion relation. Close to D=0, it becomes E=(1+b2)/2>0 at leading order. The long-wave phase reduction of the complex Ginzburg-Landau equation is consequently
ϕT​=DϕXX​−EϕXXXX​+g(ϕX​)2+⋯,g=c−b.
(39)
The fourth derivative regularizes negative phase diffusion. Terms omitted here have higher order in the long-wave expansion. First-order advection has been removed by the group-velocity frame; higher odd derivatives in a more general travelling-wave problem are beyond this leading amplitude approximation.
Take D=−d with d>0 small. At the instability boundary c=−1/b, so g=−(1+b2)/b is nonzero. Set ξ=d/E​X, τ=d2T/E, and ϕ=(d/g)Φ. At leading order the phase dynamics become the potential Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation,
Φτ​=−Φξξ​−Φξξξξ​+(Φξ​)2.
(40)
With u=−2Φξ​ this is
uτ​+uuξ​+uξξ​+uξξξξ​=0.​
(41)
The variable u is a phase-gradient disturbance, not the original physical field. Periodicity of the phase fixes ∫0P​udξ=0 for the zero-winding branch; the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation conserves this mean. The scaled period is P=Ld/E​.
At fixed period P, the flat phase has growth rates λn​=kn2​−kn4​, kn​=2πn/P. Its first loss of stability occurs at P=2π. To find what replaces it, put k=2π/P and consider P just above 2π. After choosing an origin by translation, expand a small steady modulation as u=asin(kξ)+b2​sin(2kξ)+⋯. Projection of −uuξ​ onto the first two harmonics yields
a˙=λ1​a+2k​ab2​+⋯,b˙2​=λ2​b2​−2k​a2+⋯,λj​=(jk)2−(jk)4.
(42)
Since λ2​=−12 at onset, this harmonic is slaved to b2​=ka2/(2λ2​)+⋯. The fundamental amplitude obeys
a˙=λ1​a+4λ2​k2​a3+⋯=λ1​a−48a3​+⋯(k≃1).
(43)
The primary periodic bifurcation of the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation is therefore supercritical. For 0<P−2π≪1,
a2=−k24λ1​λ2​​+O(λ12​),b2​=2λ2​ka2​+O(a4).​
(44)
The radial eigenvalue of this nonzero branch is −2λ1​+O(λ12​)<0; all higher harmonics remain damped and the modulation's spatial phase is neutral. Thus the small finite-amplitude modulation is stable in the sense of orbital stability, at fixed mean and fixed period, despite the flat state's instability. The two signs of a represent translations of the same modulation. In the potential formulation the spatial mean of Φ advances at ⟨(Φξ​)2⟩: the original wave acquires a nonlinear frequency correction rather than requiring a time-independent absolute phase.
For larger P, further linear modes become active and the small-amplitude argument no longer determines stability. A periodic modulation uˉ must then be tested with the operator vτ​=−vξξ​−vξξξξ​−∂ξ​(uˉv) on mean-zero periodic disturbances. Its translation mode is always neutral, and secondary oscillatory or more complicated modulation can occur. Moreover, stability against perturbations of the same period does not prove stability against disturbances of a larger period; those require a separate sideband or Floquet theory calculation. The controlled conclusion near the first threshold is the existence of a stable small modulation family, not universal stability of all periodic Kuramoto-Sivashinsky solutions.
