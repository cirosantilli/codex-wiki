A regular category has finite limits, factorizations of every arrow as a regular epimorphism followed by a monomorphism, and pullback-stable regular epimorphisms. Equivalently one may require coequalizers of kernel pairs and pullback stability of the resulting quotients. A regular epimorphism is a coequalizer of some parallel pair; an extremal epimorphism is an epimorphism e such that e=mh, with m monic, forces m to be invertible.
First, every regular epimorphism is extremal without assuming regularity of the whole category. Suppose e:A→B coequalizes r,s and e=mh. Since m is monic, hr=hs, so the universal property gives k:B→dom(m) with ke=h. Then mke=e; cancellation of the epimorphism e gives mk=1B​. Monicity of m now gives km=1, so m is an isomorphism. Conversely, in a regular category, factor an extremal e as mq with q regular epic and m monic. Extremality makes m invertible, so e is regular epic. Thus covers and regular epimorphisms coincide.
For a counterexample, work in the category of small categories, which has finite limits constructed on objects and arrows. Let C be two disjoint walking arrows au​b and cv​d. Let D be the walking isomorphism, with two objects 0,1 and inverse arrows p:0→1, q:1→0. Define F:C→D by a,d↦0, b,c↦1, u↦p, v↦q. These images generate all of D, so two functors agreeing after F agree everywhere; thus F is epic.
A monic functor is injective on objects and faithful, as tested by functors from the terminal category and from the walking arrow. If F factors through such a functor m:D′→D, its image contains both objects and both arrows p,q. Faithfulness forces their lifted composites to be the identities, so the image contains every arrow of D and m is an isomorphism. Hence F is extremal.
It is not regular. Its kernel pair identifies the endpoint pairs a,d and b,c but has no relation forcing the composites of the images of u,v to be identities. Concretely, send all four objects to the one-object category with endomorphism monoid {1,e}, e2=e=1, and send both u,v to e. This functor equalizes the kernel pair: corresponding identities agree, and the only nonidentity arrow pairs in the kernel pair are an arrow paired with itself. It cannot factor through D, because images of p,q would have to be inverse, whereas e2=1. In a category with kernel pairs, every regular epimorphism is the coequalizer of its kernel pair: its original coequalized pair factors through that kernel pair, giving the universal property. Therefore F is an extremal but nonregular epimorphism, the promised nonregular extremal epimorphism in the category of categories.

For a small category C, write C=[Cop,Set]. Its objects are categorical presheaves and its arrows natural transformations. The terminal presheaf is the constant singleton, and products and equalizers are computed objectwise. Their restriction maps are induced by the corresponding set operations, so the objectwise universal properties are natural and give finite limits in C.
For presheaves F,G, define the exponential object by
(GF)(A)=Nat(yA×F,G),yA=C(−,A).
(1)
For u:B→A, restriction precomposes with yu×1F​. Evaluation sends (α,x) at A to αA​(1A​,x). Given h:H×F→G, its transpose sends z∈H(A) to the transformation whose component at B is
(v:B→A,x∈F(B))⟼hB​(H(v)z,x).
(2)
Naturality of h proves this is a natural transformation and that the assignment is natural in A. Conversely evaluation reconstructs h. These constructions are inverse, proving the exponential universal property explicitly.
For the subobject classifier, let Ω(A) be the set of sieves on a category on A and let Ω(u) take inverse image of sieves on a category. The truth map 1→Ω selects the maximal sieve on a category at every stage. A monomorphism of presheaves is objectwise injective, so identify it with U⊆F. Define
χU​(x)={v:B→A:F(v)x∈U(B)},x∈F(A).
(3)
This is a sieve on a category, and restriction gives precisely inverse image, so χU​ is natural. Its value is maximal exactly when x∈U(A), by testing the identity arrow. Thus U is its pullback of truth. Uniqueness follows since membership of v is detected by whether the restriction to v is true. Smallness of C makes all the displayed components and natural-transformation sets genuine sets. Hence C is an elementary topos.
The same constructions work componentwise under the weaker slice hypothesis. Indeed,
Nat(yA×F,G)≅Nat(F∘dom,G∘dom)C/A​.
(4)
A component on v:B→A is simply a map F(B)→G(B), with the naturality conditions for triangles in C/A. Restricting to a small skeleton of an essentially small slice therefore makes this a set; the result determines the maps on all isomorphic slice objects. Likewise a sieve on a category on A is a full downward-closed collection of objects of C/A, determined by its intersection with that small skeleton, so the collection of sieves on a category is a set. The restriction, evaluation and classifier proofs above are unchanged. This establishes the slice-small presheaf construction.
For an example, take a discrete category with one object for every ordinal and no arrows other than identities. Each slice is the terminal category, but there is a proper class of pairwise nonisomorphic objects, so it is not equivalent to a small category.
The large-category assertion needs a universe convention. The constructions give all elementary-topos axioms in a larger universe in which those diagrams and their transformations are admitted. They do not imply local smallness in the original universe: for the discrete example, transformations from the constant singleton to the constant two-element presheaf are arbitrary ordinal-indexed binary families and do not form a set there. If “topos” is required to be locally small relative to that original universe, the unrestricted large-category assertion is false. With the usual larger-universe interpretation, the explicit component constructions prove the intended result; no assumption that the original category itself is essentially small is needed.

Put P(A)=ΩA, the power object, with P(f) acting by inverse image of predicates. Transposing a predicate on A×B gives natural bijections
E(A,PB)≅Sub(A×B)≅E(B,PA).

This unit is monic: two generalized elements having the same evaluation on all predicates agree by testing the singleton predicate of the first, or equivalently its graph in the relevant parameter product.
We verify a sufficient form of Beck's monadicity theorem: a right adjoint is monadic if it reflects isomorphisms and its domain has, and it preserves, coequalizers of reflexive pairs. Suppose Pf is invertible. Then PPf is invertible, and naturality of the monic unit shows that f is monic. Regard it as the subobject A↪B. Its characteristic predicate and the constantly true predicate have the same restriction to A, so injectivity of Pf makes them equal. Thus this subobject is all of B, and f is invertible. Hence P reflects isomorphisms.

Pf∃f​=1PB​,Pg∃f​=∃e​Pe,Pe∃e​=1PE​.
(6)

This proves the identity for parameterized predicates, not merely for global elements. If h:PB→Z satisfies hPf=hPg, composing with ∃f​ gives h=h∃e​Pe. Thus it factors through Pe, and the last displayed identity gives uniqueness. This proves that power objects turn coreflexive equalizers into coequalizers. All hypotheses of the monadicity criterion have now been checked, so P is monadic, with
Eop≃EPP.​
(7)
A logical functor L:E→F preserves finite limits, exponentials and the classifier, hence gives compatible natural isomorphisms LP≅PL and LPP≅PPL. They respect units and multiplications, because these are defined by evaluation and transposition. Under the displayed equivalences, Lop is therefore the induced functor on double-power Eilenberg-Moore categories. Their forgetful functors create finite limits, and the underlying L preserves them. Consequently the induced functor preserves finite limits, so Lop does too. Reversing arrows proves every logical functor preserves finite colimits.
For the first counterexample, the constant-terminal functor F:Set→Set has the constant-empty functor as left adjoint: both sets of maps in the adjunction are singletons. It preserves exponentials, since F(BA)=1=11=(FB)FA with the canonical comparison. It does not preserve the initial object, because F(0)=1. Thus preservation of exponentials alone is insufficient.
For the second, take the fixed-point functor Γ:G-Set→Set for G=Z/2. Its left adjoint gives a set the trivial action. The classifier of G-sets is the two-element set with trivial action, so Γ preserves it and its truth map. Let X be the free two-element G-set. The coequalizer of its identity and its swapping automorphism is the singleton G-set. Applying Γ gives two maps 0⇉0 followed by 1, whereas their coequalizer in sets is 0. Thus Γ preserves the classifier but not all finite colimits. These examples also explain why both requirements in logicalness matter.

A localic geometric morphism p:D→F presents D, over F, as sheaves on an internal locale of F. Equivalently, subobjects of inverse images p∗X form a separating family. A hyperconnected geometric morphism h has a full faithful inverse image h∗ whose essential image is closed under subobjects. In particular, it induces bijections
SubF​(X)≅SubD​(h∗X).
(8)
For this definition, subobject-closed means every subobject of h∗X is isomorphic, over h∗X, to the inverse image of a subobject of X.
Here is the construction and proof of the hyperconnected-localic factorization. Given f:E→F, its right adjoint gives natural bijections
F(X,f∗​ΩE​)≅E(f∗X,ΩE​)≅SubE​(f∗X).
(9)
Let L be the internal locale whose frame of opens is f∗​ΩE​. The frame operations are determined by these bijections: intersections are intersections of represented subobjects; the join of a family is its image under the projection from the parameter-indexed family. For an internal index object I, evaluation of the family over X×I becomes a subobject of f∗X×f∗I, and its direct image to f∗X represents the join. Pullback stability of images makes this natural in X, and distributivity follows from the corresponding subobject identity in E. This explicitly constructs the internal frame rather than asserting it from the existence of f∗​ alone.
Set D=ShF​(L), with its localic projection p to F. We construct h∗:D→E. A basic open family is a map u:X→f∗​ΩE​; its represented object in E is the subobject Au​↪f∗X corresponding to u. Meets and covering joins of open families become intersections and jointly epic families of these subobjects.
An internal sheaf on L is presented by its local sections with their open domains and restrictions. Pull these domains back to the corresponding Au​ and identify two sections wherever their restrictions agree. This is an effective equivalence relation in E, so its quotient defines h∗ of the sheaf. More concretely, if S is the object of sections and d:S→O(L) gives their domains, start with Ad​⊆f∗S. For two sections, the join of the opens on which they agree gives an L-valued relation on S×S, hence a subobject of f∗(S×S). Restricted to Ad​×Ad​, this is the germ equivalence relation used for the quotient. The restriction and sheaf axioms guarantee reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity. This construction is functorial. Products and equalizers are obtained by meeting domains and equalizing restrictions, so it preserves finite limits. A right adjoint is obtained by taking, over each open family u, the parameterized maps Au​→Y for Y∈E. These maps satisfy the sheaf condition because a covering join gives an effective gluing of the Au​; their parameter objects are supplied by the original adjunction f∗⊣f∗​ and exponentials. The map bijection on basic open families, followed by gluing, proves this is right adjoint to h∗. Thus h is a geometric morphism.
We must check hyperconnectedness, not just construct a factorization. A map Au​→Av​ has a graph which is a subobject of f∗(X×Y). The represented-subobject bijection turns it into an L-valued relation on X×Y. Its totality and single-valuedness say exactly that this relation is a map between the corresponding basic sheaf objects: totality uses the covering join, and single-valuedness uses intersections and the diagonal of Y. Thus maps between basic open families are precisely their maps in E. Applying this to the local sections of arbitrary sheaves, compatible maps glue uniquely on both sides. Hence h∗ is full and faithful. Likewise a subobject of h∗S pulls back to subobjects of its basic Au​; each is again represented by an open family, and their compatibility glues to a subsheaf of S. The essential image is therefore subobject-closed, proving that h is hyperconnected.
The inverse image p∗X is the constant family over the whole locale. The construction sends it to f∗X, so h∗p∗≅f∗; taking right adjoints gives the required factorization
Eh​ShF​(L)p​F.​
(10)
This argument uses the usual effective quotient and local-section constructions in a topos; it also explains why the relevant internal opens are relative subobjects of f∗X, not just global subobjects of the terminal object.
For uniqueness, suppose f=p′h′ with h′ hyperconnected and p′ localic. Then for every X,
SubE​(f∗X)≅SubD′​(p′∗X).
(11)
If D′ is sheaves on an internal locale L′, the right side is represented by its frame O(L′). These identifications preserve intersections, joins and parameter change, so the representing internal frames are isomorphic: O(L′)≅f∗​ΩE​. The locale and its sheaf topos are therefore equivalent over F. Under this equivalence the basic open families have the same images in E, and their local-section presentations determine all other images and maps. Thus the two hyperconnected parts agree up to the same equivalence. The factorization is unique up to equivalence.
Finally suppose f=hi with i:E↪D a geometric embedding and h:D→F hyperconnected. Let j be the local operator on D presenting the inclusion. For a subobject A↪X of F, take the j-closure of h∗A in h∗X. Hyperconnectedness makes it the inverse image of a unique subobject of X. This defines a closure operation on F; its extensivity, idempotence, intersection preservation and pullback stability descend from the j-closure. Let k be its local operator.
Hyperconnectedness also gives h∗​ΩD​≅ΩF​, by the represented-subobject bijections. The direct image i∗​ΩE​ is the closed-truth object Ωj​, and hence
f∗​ΩE​=h∗​Ωj​≅Ωk​.
(12)
This follows either by taking the equalizer of j and the identity or by representing closed subobjects in the preceding closure construction. The internal locale with frame Ωk​ is the sublocale of the terminal locale determined by k: covering joins are exactly closure by k. Its sheaves are the k-sheaves in F, and its projection is their geometric inclusion. Applying the just-proved factorization to f therefore gives
Ehyperconnected​shk​(F)inclusion​F.​
(13)
This supplies the requested reordered factorization.

This algebra category is Cartesian closed. For nonempty A=B×C and D=E×F, its exponential is EB×FC with rectangular multiplication: the usual set adjunction in each factor gives the universal property. For the empty cases, D0=1 and 0A=0 when A is nonempty; these formulas also give 00=1. They verify the adjunction against empty test objects as well. Thus the algebra category is Cartesian closed.
It is not a topos. If a classifier existed, its global elements would correspond to the two subobjects of the terminal singleton algebra, so its underlying set would have two elements. A two-element rectangular band is either left-zero or right-zero: its two nonempty factors have sizes (2,1) or (1,2). A homomorphism from the four-element band {0,1}×{0,1} to a left-zero classifier depends only on the first coordinate, so the preimage of truth cannot be a single point; for a right-zero classifier the same failure occurs in the second coordinate. Yet a single point is a subalgebra and its inclusion is monic. Pullbacks of algebra maps are computed on underlying sets, so this subobject could not be classified. Therefore there is no subobject classifier and the algebra category is not a topos, despite its cartesian closure.

A dense sieve, also called a persistently nonempty sieve, is one whose every pullback sieve on a category is nonempty: a member factoring through f has the form fh, corresponding to a member h of f∗R. The maximal sieve on a category clearly has this property. If R is persistently nonempty, then u∗R is too: for any v into the domain of u, apply density to uv to find an extension uvh∈R, giving vh∈u∗R.
For transitivity, suppose R is dense on n and S is a sieve on a category such that r∗S is dense for each r∈R. Given f:m→n, choose h with fh∈R. Density of (fh)∗S, tested on its identity arrow, supplies k with fhk∈S. Thus S is dense. These are the maximality, pullback and transitivity axioms of a Grothendieck topology. Therefore the specified sieves on a category form the coverage J, namely the double-negation topology. The empty sieve on a category is never covering, because its identity pullback is empty.

We prove Booleanity directly from the dense-cover description. Let B be a subsheaf of a sheaf F. Its pseudocomplement N consists of sections x∈F(c) such that no restriction along any arrow into c belongs to B. This is a subpresheaf. It is also a sheaf: if a section of F restricts into N on a dense covering sieve on a category, any hypothetical restriction into B has a further restriction in that cover; it would then belong to both B and N, impossible. Thus the unique amalgamation in F of an N-matching family lies in N.
For any x∈F(c), the sieve on a category of arrows on which its restriction lies in B or N is dense. Given an arrow u:d→c, either some further restriction belongs to B, supplying the required member of that sieve on a category, or none does, in which case the restriction along u itself belongs to N. Therefore B∪N is locally all of F, so their join in the sheaf topos is F. Their intersection is empty. Every subsheaf consequently has a complement, proving the topos is Boolean.
For two-valuedness, let U↪1 be a nonempty subterminal sheaf. At some stage m, its value is the singleton. For every k≥m there is an arrow k→m, so restriction makes U(k) the singleton as well. At any stage n, the sieve on a category of arrows into n whose domains are at least m is dense: after an arrow with domain l, choose a further domain k≥max(l,m) and use the existence of arrows downwards. On this covering sieve on a category the unique terminal section has a matching family in U, so the sheaf condition gives U(n)=1. Hence U=1. Since the empty sieve on a category never covers, the empty presheaf is an initial sheaf distinct from 1. Thus
Sub(1)={0,1},​
(23)
and the topos is two-valued, in the distinct global sense of a two-valued topos.

Its unit image is dense by the preceding plus-construction argument, and density is stable under pullback, so the resulting domain U⊆yn​ is dense. It gives exactly a partial map U→yn+1​ of the prohibited kind. Such a domain cannot be empty: at the section 1n​ of yn​(n), density would otherwise require the empty sieve on a category to cover. Thus there is no map yn​→An+1​.
Any sheaf map An​→An+1​, composed with yn​↪An​, would be such a presheaf map. Hence
HomSh(C,J)​(An​,An+1​)=∅.​
(26)
The stronger intermediate statement also says An+1​(n)=∅, by the Yoneda correspondence. It will be useful for the last clause.

The sheaf An​ is noninitial: it contains yn​, whose value at n includes 1n​. In a two-valued topos its support, the image of An​→1, can therefore only be 1. An image equal to 0 would force An​=0, since the initial object of a topos is strict. Consequently every An​→1 is an epimorphism.
Products of sheaves on a small site exist and are computed as products of the underlying presheaves, since a product of unique matching-family amalgamations again uniquely amalgamates. For the countable product P=∏n∈N​An​, its value at stage m is ∏n​An​(m). The factor with n=m+1 is empty by the stronger statement proved in the preceding part. Thus P(m)=∅ at every stage m, with the unique empty restriction maps. It is the empty presheaf, already an initial sheaf because no empty sieve on a category covers. Hence
An​↠1 for every n,n∈N∏​An​≅0.​
(27)
This proves the final PDF-only request and exhibits the separating tower site with vanishing product. It is a genuine failure of countable choice in this Boolean two-valued sheaf topos; it does not contradict the classical external construction of the site.
