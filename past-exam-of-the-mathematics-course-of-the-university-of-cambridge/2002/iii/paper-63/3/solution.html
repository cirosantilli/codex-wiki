For a Cartan-Weyl basis, the Jacobi identity gives
[Hi​,[Eα​,Eβ​]]=[[Hi​,Eα​],Eβ​]+[Eα​,[Hi​,Eβ​]]=(αi​+βi​)[Eα​,Eβ​].
(16)
Thus the bracket has joint Cartan weight α+β. If β=−α, it belongs to the zero-weight space, which is the Cartan subalgebra, and so is a linear combination αi​Hi​. If α+β is a nonzero root, its root space is one-dimensional and the bracket is proportional to Eα+β​. If that nonzero sum is not a root, the bracket vanishes. This proves the asserted forms without assuming every pair of roots has a root sum.
With the stated normalization, put Hα​=2αi​Hi​/α2. The commutators become
[Hα​,Eα​]=2Eα​,[Hα​,E−α​]=−2E−α​,[Eα​,E−α​]=Hα​.
(17)
The adjoint relations make J3​=Hα​/2, J+​=Eα​, J−​=E−α​ the usual SU(2) Lie algebra generators. Hence every finite-dimensional SU(2) irreducible component has J3​ eigenvalues m=−j,−j+1,…,j, with 2j an integer. Therefore Hα​ has integer eigenvalues 2m, including in the finite-dimensional Adjoint representation. Algebraically this is the sl2 subalgebra associated with a root.
If [Hα​,Xλ​]=λXλ​, another use of the Jacobi identity gives
[Hα​,[Eα​,Xλ​]]=(λ+2)[Eα​,Xλ​].
(18)
The bracket is therefore zero or lies in the eigenvalue-λ+2 space. Within one irreducible SU(2) ladder it is proportional to the next chosen vector Xλ+2​. If the full eigenspace has multiplicity, the weight statement is the general one; proportionality to one named vector presupposes the ladder choice. Similarly E−α​ lowers this eigenvalue by two, and the ladder weights run from −n to n in steps of two.
For a root vector Eβ​, its Hα​ weight is
mαβ​=α22α⋅β​.
(19)
The adjoint SU(2) ladder therefore proves
α22α⋅β​∈Z.​
(20)
For nonparallel α,β, raising and lowering Eβ​ move its joint Cartan weight through the root string β+kα. The finite irreducible ladder has weights symmetric about zero. Starting at mαβ​, reaching its partner −mαβ​ requires k=−mαβ​. The corresponding nonzero vector lies in the root space of β−mαβ​α, proving the Weyl reflection property:
sα​(β)=β−α22α⋅β​α is a root.​
(21)
There is no zero joint root in this string when α,β are nonparallel. Its one-dimensional root spaces prevent two irreducible ladders with the same weight parity from overlapping, so the ladder argument applies to the entire string. For β=±α, reflection simply interchanges the two opposite roots, which already exist by the adjoint relation.
For angle and length restrictions, set p=2α⋅β/α2 and q=2α⋅β/β2. These Cartan integers have the same sign when nonzero and obey
pq=4cos2θ,β2α2​=pq​.
(22)
For nonparallel, nonorthogonal roots, pq is an integer strictly between zero and four. The possibilities are consequently:
  • pq=1: ∣p∣=∣q∣=1, equal lengths, and θ=π/3 or 2π/3.
  • pq=2: {∣p∣,∣q∣}={1,2}, squared-length ratio 2 or 1/2, and θ=π/4 or 3π/4.
  • pq=3: {∣p∣,∣q∣}={1,3}, squared-length ratio 3 or 1/3, and θ=π/6 or 5π/6.
Orthogonal roots have p=q=0 and θ=π/2. Parallel roots have pq=4; the integer pairs would allow only length multiples 1,2,1/2. The stated reducedness condition excludes the latter two, so parallel roots are equal or opposite, with angles zero or π and equal lengths. Thus
θ∈{0,π/6,π/4,π/3,π/2,2π/3,3π/4,5π/6,π}.​
(23)
Not every root system realizes every listed angle.
The orthogonal pair alone gives no ratio through q/p. To finish the length restriction for a simple Lie algebra, use irreducibility of its root system. Let U span the Weyl group orbit of α. Both U and U⊥ are invariant under the orthogonal root reflections. If a root had nonzero projections in both, reflection of its projection in U would have a nonzero U⊥ component, violating invariance. Hence every root is entirely in one of these subspaces. A proper nonzero U would split the root system into two orthogonal parts, contradicting irreducibility. This proves that the Weyl orbit spans an irreducible root space.
In particular, some orbit root γ is nonorthogonal to any chosen β, and γ2=α2. Applying the previous integer-pair restriction to γ,β therefore also handles orthogonal α,β:
β2α2​∈{1,2,1/2,3,1/3}.​
(24)
There can be at most two different root lengths: three lengths would have ratios two and three relative to the shortest, but then the longest-to-middle squared-length ratio would be 3/2, which is forbidden. A reducible collection of orthogonal rank-one factors could have independently chosen scales; the simple-algebra assumption excludes that exception.
Choose a generic linear functional to define positive roots. The simple roots are those positive roots which cannot be written as sums of two positive roots; they form a basis in which all root coefficients are integers of one sign. Distinct simple roots have nonpositive inner product: if their inner product were positive, their root string would contain their difference, and whichever difference is positive would decompose one of them. In rank two, irreducibility excludes the orthogonal case. The allowed simple-root angles are therefore 120∘,135∘,150∘, giving the three irreducible diagrams in the classification of rank-two root systems: A2 root system, B2 root system and G2 root system. Reflections in the two simple roots generate their respective six, eight and twelve roots.
Here are explicit simple-root choices and positive roots; adding their negatives gives each complete diagram.
  • For A2 root system, take α1​=(1,0) and α2​=(−1/2,3​/2). The positive roots are α1​,α2​,α1​+α2​; all have squared length one.
  • For B2 root system, take the long α1​=(1,−1) and short α2​=(0,1). The positive roots are α1​,α2​,α1​+α2​,α1​+2α2​. These give the four axis roots and four diagonal roots, with squared lengths one and two. The rank-two C2​ Lie algebra is isomorphic to this one; changing the long/short ordering changes its Cartan presentation.
  • For G2 root system, take short α1​=(1,0) and long α2​=(−3/2,3​/2). The positive roots are α1​,α2​,α1​+α2​,2α1​+α2​,3α1​+α2​,3α1​+2α2​. They give two hexagons rotated by 30∘, with squared lengths one and three.
The nonnegative coefficients in each positive-root list verify the chosen simple roots explicitly. The orthogonal four-root system A1​×A1​ is rank two but reducible, so it is not a diagram for the simple algebra assumed here.
Define the Cartan matrix in the column-coroot convention:
Cij​=⟨αi​,αj∨​⟩=αj2​2αi​⋅αj​​,[Hαj​​,Eαi​​]=Cij​Eαi​​.
(25)
Using the ordered roots above gives
CA2​​=(2−1​−12​),CB2​​=(2−1​−22​),CG2​​=(2−3​−12​).​
(26)
The alternative row-coroot convention transposes these matrices. Stating the convention and root ordering avoids a spurious disagreement in the unequal-length cases.
