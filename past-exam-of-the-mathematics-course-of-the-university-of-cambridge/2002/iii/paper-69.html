Spontaneous symmetry breaking occurs when the statistical Hamiltonian is invariant under a symmetry but a chosen equilibrium pure state is not. A small selecting field or a boundary condition chooses one of several states related by the symmetry. For a finite unconstrained system the symmetric ensemble generally has zero order parameter; spontaneous order is defined by taking the thermodynamic limit before removing the selecting field. A ferromagnet with a selected magnetization direction is the standard example.
If the broken symmetry is continuous, nearby symmetry-related states cost no energy for a uniform displacement along the manifold of states. Locality and a nonzero stiffness then make a slowly varying displacement cost gradient energy rather than a mass term. These gapless long-wavelength fluctuations are Goldstone modes. In the conventional relativistic internal-symmetry setting their count is the number of broken generators. Nonrelativistic dynamics can pair broken generators, as in a ferromagnet; gauge symmetries and broken space-time symmetries require additional qualifications. A discrete broken symmetry alone does not produce a Goldstone mode.
For an interface with a selected mean height, the continuous displacement is a rigid translation normal to the mean plane. The tension gives a stiffness for spatially varying translations. The following calculation concerns this static Goldstone sector. It determines the excitation energy and equilibrium fluctuations, but a frequency dispersion requires a separate inertial or dissipative dynamics. In low dimensions thermal roughness also limits the existence of a strictly localized flat interface in an infinite system.

The renormalization group compares descriptions at successively longer length scales. First eliminate short-wavelength degrees of freedom, then rescale coordinates and fields to restore the reference cutoff. This changes the couplings, including interactions and additive free-energy terms generated during elimination. A renormalization-group fixed point is a scale-invariant description. Linearized directions growing under coarse graining are relevant, shrinking directions are irrelevant, and zero-eigenvalue directions are marginal and require higher-order analysis.
A diverging correlation length means that many coarse-graining steps can approach a renormalization-group fixed point before a relevant perturbation becomes large. Systems with different microscopic details but the same renormalization-group fixed point and relevant directions share universal critical behavior. For the quadratic model below, Fourier modes decouple, so eliminating a high-momentum shell produces a Gaussian determinant but does not couple the remaining modes. The anisotropic gradient kernel requires different length rescalings in the parallel and transverse directions. This is an anisotropic renormalization group, not an isotropic rescaling of all d coordinates.

The scaling hypothesis for critical phenomena describes the singular part of the equilibrium free-energy density, rather than its analytic background. For an isotropic system in spatial dimension D, with thermal scaling field t and conjugate field h, write
fs​(t,h)=b−Dfs​(byt​t,byh​h).
(23)
This follows from coarse graining near a renormalization-group fixed point: a correlation volume is enlarged by bD, while the relevant thermal and field perturbations grow with their eigenvalues. Choosing b=∣t∣−1/yt​ gives
fs​(t,h)=∣t∣2−αΦ±​(h/∣t∣Δ),2−α=D/yt​,Δ=yh​/yt​.
(24)
The two functions describe the two sides of the transition. Nonuniversal metric factors multiplying t,h,fs​ must be allowed when comparing microscopic models. Exponents and suitably normalized scaling functions or amplitude ratios characterize a universality class.
Differentiating the free-energy density gives the order parameter M=−∂h​fs​, magnetic susceptibility χ=∂h​M and singular heat capacity proportional to −∂t2​fs​. Thus
βmag​=yt​D−yh​​,γ=yt​2yh​−D​,α=2−yt​D​.
(25)
The subscript distinguishes the order-parameter exponent from inverse temperature. At t=0, choose b=∣h∣−1/yh​ to obtain M∼sgn(h)∣h∣1/δ, with δ=yh​/(D−yh​). These derivatives imply the Rushbrooke scaling relation α+2βmag​+γ=2 and the Widom scaling relation γ=βmag​(δ−1). Equivalently, the equation of state has h=∣t∣ΔH±​(M/∣t∣βmag​), with Δ=βmag​δ=βmag​+γ.
The correlation length scales as ξ∼∣t∣−ν, ν=1/yt​. At criticality the connected correlation function behaves as G(r)∼r−(D−2+η), giving yh​=(D+2−η)/2. Integrating G over a correlation volume gives χ∼ξ2−η and hence the Fisher scaling relation γ=(2−η)ν. The hyperscaling relation 2−α=Dν expresses one singular free-energy contribution per correlation volume.
These laws organize measurements: plotting M/∣t∣βmag​ against h/∣t∣Δ collapses near-critical curves onto the appropriate scaling function. A finite system replaces the divergent length by its size L; the corresponding prediction is fs​=L−DF(tLyt​,hLyh​) under the same scaling assumptions. Finite size rounds a sharp thermodynamic singularity.
The hypothesis has a domain of validity. At a renormalization-group fixed point one generally needs fs​=b−Dfs​(byt​t,byh​h,byu​u,…), including other scaling fields. Ordinary irrelevant couplings can be set to zero in a nonsingular limiting scaling function. A dangerously irrelevant coupling cannot: above the upper critical dimension of scalar quartic theory, the quartic term is irrelevant at the Gaussian point but still stabilizes the ordered phase. Consequently naive hyperscaling fails there even though other exponent relations can remain valid. Marginal variables can produce logarithms, anisotropic systems require the appropriate volume exponent, and the Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless transition has an essential correlation-length singularity instead of a finite power-law ν. Scaling theory relates observables through a small number of fixed-point dimensions; it does not make analytic backgrounds, marginal logarithms or dangerously irrelevant variables disappear.

The Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless transition is a defect-unbinding transition of a two-dimensional system with a compact continuous phase, such as the short-range XY model. At long wavelengths, smooth spin waves have energy
Fsw​=2ρs​​∫d2x∣∇θ∣2,
(26)
where ρs​ is the phase stiffness. Gaussian averaging gives
⟨[θ(r)−θ(0)]2⟩=πρs​kB​T​log(r/a)+O(1),⟨ei[θ(r)−θ(0)]⟩∼(a/r)η,η=2πρs​kB​T​.
(27)
Thus smooth fluctuations already destroy true nonzero magnetization in an infinite two-dimensional system, in accordance with the Mermin-Wagner theorem. The low-temperature phase instead has quasi-long-range order, with algebraically decaying correlations. The stiffness in its asymptotic exponent is the renormalized long-distance stiffness, not merely the microscopic coupling.
The phase is periodic modulo 2π, so it also permits topological defects with integer winding n. A phase vortex has ∣∇θ∣=∣n∣/r, and integration gives
En​=πρs​n2log(L/a)+Ecore​.
(28)
A singly charged phase vortex can be placed in roughly (L/a)2 locations, giving entropy 2kB​log(L/a). This energy-entropy balance suggests that isolated vortices become favorable when the effective stiffness falls to 2kB​T/π. Below the transition, vortex-antivortex binding predominates; above it their unbinding screens the logarithmic interaction and produces a finite correlation length. A single-vortex estimate identifies the mechanism but does not include screening by smaller pairs.
The dilute-vortex vortex-pair renormalization flow incorporates this screening. With K=ρs​/(kB​T) and dimensionless core fugacity y, one conventional leading-order normalization gives
dℓdK−1​=4π3y2,dℓdy​=(2−πK)y.​
(29)
The first equation says polarizable phase vortex pairs reduce stiffness. The second compares the phase vortex's position scaling dimension two with its logarithmic energy cost πK. Numerical coefficients in y depend on its definition, but the zero-fugacity threshold K=2/π is invariant. For K>2/π, sufficiently small fugacity flows to zero, leaving a line of Gaussian spin-wave renormalization-group fixed points with varying algebraic exponent. On the other side of the separatrix, fugacity grows and the dilute approximation eventually ceases to apply.
Near the endpoint set X=2−πK and Y=4πy. To leading order X′=Y2 and Y′=XY, so X2−Y2 is invariant. The critical separatrix approaches (0,0). Just on the disordered side this invariant is −ε2, proportional to the reduced temperature deviation for a generic path through the transition. Hence X′=X2+ε2, whose integration gives a time in logarithmic scale of order 1/ε. Stopping when the fugacity becomes order one yields
ξ∼aexp(B/τ​),τ=(T−Tc​)/Tc​↓0+,​
(30)
with nonuniversal B>0. This derives the essential singularity and explains why assigning a finite ordinary correlation-length exponent is inappropriate.
At the transition the limiting stiffness from below has the universal stiffness jump
ρs​(Tc−​)=π2kB​Tc​​,η(Tc​)=1/4.​
(31)
Above Tc​, the thermodynamic long-distance stiffness vanishes and correlations decay exponentially. The singular free-energy contribution is of order ξ−2 and is exponentially small in 1/τ​, so this infinite-order transition is unlike a Landau transition with a discontinuous order parameter or an ordinary power-law heat-capacity singularity. The renormalization mechanism is developed in Kosterlitz's original vortex analysis. The transition separates bound vortices and algebraic order from unbound vortices and short-range order, while neither finite-temperature phase has conventional spontaneous XY magnetization.

The Landau-Ginzburg theory for a scalar order parameter supplements a local symmetry-allowed potential with a gradient penalty. For a Z2​ symmetry at zero field, a stable quartic model is
F[ϕ]=∫dDx[2K​∣∇ϕ∣2+2r​ϕ2+4u​ϕ4−hϕ],K,u>0,
(32)
with r=a(T−Tc​) near a mean-field transition. Equilibrium fluctuations have weight e−F/(kB​T). In the Landau approximation, minimize the functional. A uniform stationary state obeys rϕ+uϕ3=h. At zero field the minimum is ϕ=0 for r>0 and ϕ=±−r/u​ for r<0. The latter pair is spontaneous symmetry breaking of the discrete symmetry.
The uniform free-energy density at its minimum is zero above the transition and −r2/(4u) below, up to regular backgrounds. Differentiating the equation of state gives χ+​=1/r and χ−​=1/(2∣r∣). At r=0, ϕ=(h/u)1/3. Gaussian fluctuations around the minimum have inverse covariance (Kq2+r)/(kB​T) above and (Kq2+2∣r∣)/(kB​T) below. Their correlation lengths are ξ+​=(K/r)1/2 and ξ−​=(K/(2∣r∣))1/2. These results give the usual mean-field critical exponents
α=0,βmag​=1/2,γ=1,δ=3,ν=1/2,η=0.​
(33)
The heat capacity has a finite jump rather than a power-law divergence. Negative quartic coefficient requires higher stabilizing terms and can produce a first-order transition; a vanishing quartic coefficient requires a tricritical analysis. Thus the assumed positivity of u matters.
The Ginzburg criterion tests whether this saddle description is self-consistent. Below the transition compare fluctuations of the order parameter averaged over a correlation volume with its squared mean-field value ϕ02​=∣r∣/u. Modes with q≲ξ−1 dominate this coarse-grained fluctuation estimate:
⟨(δϕ)2⟩ξ​≃kB​T∫q≲ξ−1​(2π)DdDq​Kq2+2∣r∣1​=AD​KD/2kB​T​∣r∣D/2−1,
(34)
where the positive dimensionless AD​ depends on the averaging window and the factor of two used in ξ. Dividing by ϕ02​ gives
G(r)=AD​KD/2kB​Tu​∣r∣(D−4)/2≪1.​
(35)
It is the fluctuation of a correlation-volume average, not the full cutoff-dominated microscopic variance, that should be compared with the mean-field order parameter.
For D<4, G diverges as r→0, so the asymptotic critical region is fluctuation dominated. Away from it, mean-field theory can still work when
∣r∣≫(AD​kB​Tu/KD/2)2/(4−D).
(36)
For D>4, the long-wavelength ratio tends to zero near criticality, consistently with Gaussian control and mean-field thermodynamic exponents. At D=4, quartic coupling is marginal; scale-dependent logarithms require a renormalization-group analysis rather than interpreting a constant bare ratio as a proof of no corrections. This identifies four as the upper critical dimension of the short-range scalar quartic model.
The Landau-Ginzburg theory is valuable because symmetries restrict a manageable list of local operators, the gradient term describes correlations and interfaces, and the Ginzburg criterion identifies when neglected fluctuations become decisive. Below the upper critical dimension an interacting renormalization-group fixed point, rather than the bare saddle, determines universal exponents. For continuous order parameters the manifold of ordered minima also has Goldstone modes, and lower-critical-dimension fluctuations require separate attention. Mean-field minimization supplies an approximation; the Ginzburg ratio decides whether its neglect of fluctuations is justified.

For a finite open chain the inverse has off-diagonal entries −ρ/[J(1−ρ2)], interior diagonal entries (1+ρ2)/[J(1−ρ2)] and endpoint diagonal entries 1/[J(1−ρ2)]. Accordingly the displayed homogeneous potential acquires the endpoint correction ρ(m12​+mN2​)/[J(1+ρ)]. These boundary terms do not change the bulk thermodynamic conclusion. Stating infinite-chain or periodized boundary conditions is necessary if the translation-invariant expression is asserted exactly.

With lattice spacing set to one and a smoothly varying auxiliary field, the discrete action becomes
S[m]=∫0L​dx[2M​(dxdm​)2+U(m)],M=Jsinhκ1​.
(44)
This is precisely the Euclidean path integral of a quantum particle with ℏ=1, mass M and potential U. With fixed endpoints it gives the transition amplitude ⟨mf​∣e−LHQ​∣mi​⟩, while periodic classical boundary conditions give its trace. The corresponding operator is
HQ​=−2Jsinhκ​dm2d2​+U(m),Zperiodic​∝Tre−LHQ​.​
(45)
This is classical Ising chain as a quantum particle. It is the continuum approximation to the exact positive transfer kernel, so its numerical spectrum is accurate when the relevant auxiliary configurations vary slowly. The transfer-kernel reasoning and the domain-wall argument below also support the qualitative conclusion without treating a continuum approximation as an exact finite-lattice identity.
At zero field write a=tanh(κ/2)/J>0. Expansion gives
U(m)=−log2+(a−2)m2+34​m4+O(m6).
(46)
A double well exists when a<2; the stationary points satisfy am=tanh(2m). For a≥2 there is a single minimum at zero, with the quadratic curvature marginal at equality. Thus a double well is the low-effective-temperature regime of the model, not an unconditional property for every J. The exact potential remains confining because U(m)=am2−2∣m∣+O(1) at large ∣m∣.
Even when two wells exist, a quantum particle with finite mass and a finite barrier tunnels between them. Its one-dimensional confining Schrödinger operator has a unique, strictly positive ground-state wavefunction. At h=0 reflection symmetry makes that state even, so every odd observable has zero expectation. An antisymmetric first excited state is split from it by a positive gap. A spectral expansion of an odd-observable correlation has factors e−x(En​−E0​); the smallest contributing gap therefore gives a finite classical correlation length. The original spin observable, after decoupling, is tanh(2m+h), which is odd at zero field. Hence
⟨σ⟩=0 at h=0 for finite J and fixed κ>0,⟨σ0​σr​⟩→0.​
(47)
A deep well can give an exponentially small quantum tunnelling gap and an extremely long correlation length, which can mimic order in a finite sample, but it does not produce true long-range order.
The classical counterpart is the finite cost of a domain wall. Because the pair sum is doubled, a sharp wall has dimensionless energy
Ewall​=4J∑r≥1​re−κr=(1−e−κ)24Je−κ​<∞.
(48)
At any finite temperature such walls have nonzero fugacity, divide a long chain into domains and destroy spontaneous magnetization. The range is large for κ≪1 but finite. The zero-temperature limit and an infinite-range limit are singular and must not be interchanged casually with the thermodynamic limit; an infinite-range interaction also requires an extensive normalization before comparing models.
A nonzero h tilts the potential: to first order Uh​(m)=U0​(m)−htanh(2m)+O(h2). A positive field favors the positive well and produces positive magnetization. For fixed finite parameters the ground state and classical free energy vary smoothly, so there is no zero-field discontinuity due merely to a double-well bare potential. A discrete broken-symmetry state appears only in an appropriate singular limit, not by ignoring quantum tunnelling.
More generally a local d-dimensional classical statistical model can be sliced along one coordinate and represented by a transfer operator. That coordinate becomes imaginary time; the other d−1 coordinates become the spatial coordinates of a quantum system. Infinite extent in the transfer direction corresponds to its ground-state, or zero-quantum-temperature, limit. The classical Ising model is thus related to a quantum Ising system in d−1 spatial dimensions, with transverse dynamics generated by the transfer operator. For d=1 this is quantum mechanics of finitely many local variables, where quantum tunnelling restores symmetry. For d≥2 the quantum system has infinitely many spatial degrees of freedom and can have distinct symmetry-broken ground states, allowing a classical finite-temperature ordering transition. Locality and anisotropy of the mapping determine the quantum couplings; the dimension correspondence alone does not fix them.
