The required theorem is Cochran's theorem: for an isotropic normal distribution, projections onto orthogonal subspaces are independent, and the squared norm on a rank-r subspace divided by σ2 has a chi-squared distribution with r degrees of freedom. Here the null makes the projected mean in col(X) zero, so (R0​−R)/σ2∼χp2​, independently of R/σ2∼χν2​.

P2​=X2​(X2T​X2​)−1X2T​,R2​=YT(I−P0​−P2​)Y.
(3)

F1​=R/ν(R2​−R)/p1​​∼Fp1​,ν​when β1​=0​.

Again reject in the upper tail. The difference of the nested fitted-value projections has rank p1​, is orthogonal to the full residual projection, and annihilates the null mean, which gives the same independent chi-squared argument. If a block has no columns, the corresponding test is vacuous.
The phrase orthogonal statistical parameters refers to zero cross-block Fisher information, not to the numerical parameter vectors being perpendicular. In this model the cross-block information is X1T​X2​/σ2, so the orthogonal coefficient blocks in a centered normal linear model condition is
X1T​X2​=0​.
(5)
Then the two fitted subspaces are orthogonal, the block least-squares estimators have zero covariance and are independent because they are jointly Gaussian, and each block's extra sum of squares is unchanged by including the other block. For logistic regression, set ηi​=xiT​β and pi​=(1+e−ηi​)−1. Its Bernoulli log-likelihood, score function and Fisher information are
ℓ(β)=∑i​{yi​ηi​−log(1+eηi​)},U(β)=XT(y−p),I(β)=XTWX,
(9)
where W=diag{pi​(1−pi​)}. Solve U(β)=0 by Fisher scoring or iteratively reweighted least squares. One scoring step is
βnew=β+(XTWX)−1XT(y−p)​.
(10)
Equivalently regress the working response zi​=ηi​+(yi​−pi​)/[pi​(1−pi​)] on X using weights pi​(1−pi​). Iterate until the likelihood and coefficients stabilize, with rank and convergence checks. Separation in logistic regression can prevent a finite maximum: if a predictor direction strictly separates the zeros and ones, moving indefinitely along it improves the likelihood rather than producing a finite root.
Under the unrestricted model, each individual probability is estimated independently as p​i​=yi​. Every observed binary outcome then has probability one, so, using boundary limits,
0≤pi​≤1max​ℓ(p1​,…,pn​)=0​.
(11)
The resulting residual deviance is D=−2ℓ(β​). It is a legitimate likelihood contrast and can be used to compare fitted models. However, individual Bernoulli deviance need not have a chi-squared calibration: the saturated model has one boundary-valued parameter for each single observation, and its dimension grows with n. The fixed-dimensional regularity behind Wilks theorem does not justify a χn−p2​ reference here.
For a concrete demonstration, let the true model contain only an intercept and have success probability 1/2. Its fitted probability is yˉ​, and
nD​=−2{yˉ​logyˉ​+(1−yˉ​)log(1−yˉ​)}⟶2log2,
(12)
whereas a χn−12​ variable divided by n converges to one. Thus treating the raw Bernoulli residual deviance as an ordinary absolute goodness-of-fit statistic can reject a correct model systematically. In contrast, deviance differences between fixed-dimensional nested logistic models have their usual asymptotic chi-squared reference under the relevant regularity assumptions. For absolute fit use meaningful replicated covariate groups and grouped-binomial logistic regression, or assess residual patterns and prediction on held-out data; a model-specific parametric bootstrap can calibrate a chosen statistic.

The first approach specifies marginal means, variances and an exchangeable correlation for each student's repeated counts. It is a moment model suitable for a generalized estimating equation; equality of the mean and variance alone does not specify a full Poisson joint distribution. Different students supply independent sampling clusters, while the three counts within a student are dependent.
The second approach is a Poisson generalized linear mixed model with a positive shared random multiplier Ui​=ebi​. Conditional on it, the three counts are independent Poisson variables; integrating over it gives a fully specified dependent joint law. The gamma random-intercept Poisson model uses Gamma shape τ2/θ and rate τ/θ, so EUi​=τ and Var(Ui​)=θ. Treat the covariates as fixed, with the same multiplier distribution across covariate/treatment groups, as required by this model.
Let aij​=exp(β0​+βTxij​) and mij​=EYij​. The law of total expectation, law of total variance and law of total covariance give
mij​=τaij​,Var(Yij​)=τaij​+θaij2​,Cov(Yij​,Yik​)=θaij​aik​(j=k)​.
(25)
Thus each marginal is a negative binomial distribution by the Poisson-gamma mixture, rather than generally a Poisson variable. Put κ=θ/τ2. Its variance is mij​+κmij2​, and the marginal correlation is
Corr(Yij​,Yik​)=(1+κmij​)(1+κmik​)​κmij​mik​​​​.
(26)
It is positive but usually depends on the two marginal means, so it need not be the common exchangeable correlation proposed in the first approach. The first method focuses on population mean effects with a chosen working correlation matrix; the second models latent student heterogeneity and separates conditional Poisson variation from additional marginal variation. Correctly specified likelihood inference for the second uses more distributional assumptions, while the first can retain valid mean inference despite a wrong working covariance by using a cluster-level sandwich variance.

For the marginal mean model, eβ0​ is the expected term count for a student with all recorded covariates at their reference or zero values. If the treatment indicator is one for the new therapy and zero for the reference treatment, then eβ1​ is the ratio of population mean counts under the two treatments, holding the remaining covariates fixed. Values below one mean a reduced expected count; 100(1−eβ1​)% is the corresponding percentage reduction.
For the random-intercept model, eβ0​ is the conditional reference count at bi​=0, or multiplier Ui​=1; the conditional baseline for student i is Ui​eβ0​. It is not automatically the mean-population baseline, which is τeβ0​. The conditional treatment rate ratio for students with the same multiplier is eβ1​. Because the multiplier has the same distribution in the treatment groups and the mean uses a logarithmic link function, that is also the marginal treatment rate ratio. This is the property that marginal and conditional slopes agree for an independent log-link random intercept.
The parameter θ is the variance of the positive student multiplier, not a Poisson sampling variance or a treatment coefficient. Greater θ at fixed τ means greater heterogeneity in underlying propensity and larger between-term covariance. Its extra contribution to the count variance is θaij2​. If τ=1 is imposed, θ is also the relative multiplier variance; otherwise the relative heterogeneity is θ/τ2.
There is a necessary identifiability qualification: if the multiplier mean τ is also unrestricted, scale identifiability in a gamma random-intercept Poisson model shows that replacing
(Ui​,β0​,τ,θ)⟼(cUi​,β0​−logc,cτ,c2θ)
(27)
leaves the response law unchanged. Consequently only β0​+logτ and θ/τ2, together with the slopes, are identified from these counts without a scale convention. Fixing the multiplier mean, usually to one, is needed to interpret the conditional intercept and heterogeneity scale separately.

The assumed Poisson marginal variance and constant correlation generally fail under this random-effect law. The simple unchanged-slope result relies on the random multiplier distribution not changing with covariates, rather than on a universal equivalence of marginal and conditional regression models.

For independent Poisson counts, the log-likelihood at mean vector μ is
ℓ(μ)=∑i​{yi​logμi​−μi​−log(yi​!)}.

The saturated model maximizes each cell separately at μ​i​=yi​, including the boundary value zero. If ei​ is the fitted mean under the log-linear regression, twice the saturated-minus-fitted log-likelihood is the full Poisson deviance
D=2∑i​{yi​logei​yi​​−yi​+ei​},
(35)
with 0log(0/ei​)=0 understood by continuity. The fitted intercept has score function ∂ℓ/∂μ=∑i​(yi​−ei​), since differentiating the logarithmic mean with respect to that intercept gives one. D=2i∑​yi​log(yi​/ei​)​.
