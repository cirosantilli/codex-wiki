Write C=σ∨ for the dual cone, S=C∩M, and Uσ​=SpecC[S] for the affine toric variety. This is the variety denoted Xσˇ​ in the source; the subscripts there refer to the cone of exponents, whereas Uσ​ refers to the cone in NR​.
For a face of a polyhedral cone τ⪯σ, define τ∗=C∩τ⊥. If v is in the relative interior of τ, nonnegativity on σ shows that a functional in C vanishes on v exactly when it vanishes on all of τ. Hence τ∗=C∩v⊥ is a face of C. Conversely a face γ of C gives γ∗=σ∩γ⊥. Every polyhedral face is exposed: the supporting functionals for its containing facets can be added, since they are nonnegative. If τ=σ∩μ⊥, then μ∈τ∗, so
τ⊆σ∩(τ∗)⊥⊆σ∩μ⊥=τ.
(1)
The same argument applied to C proves (γ∗)∗=γ, using the closed-cone identity C∨=σ. That identity follows from separation of a point outside a closed convex cone. Finally, enlarging τ makes its annihilator smaller. Thus these maps are inverse order-reversing bijections, proving face duality for polyhedral cones even when σ is not full-dimensional.
For the given integral exposing functional μ, we claim
τ∨∩M=S+Z≥0​(−μ).
(2)
One inclusion is immediate since μ vanishes on τ. For the other, let m be nonnegative on τ and test it on finitely many generating rays of σ. Where μ vanishes, those rays belong to τ, so m is already nonnegative. On every other ray μ is positive, so choosing a sufficiently large integer k makes m+kμ nonnegative on all the rays. This proves the claim. The corresponding semigroup algebra identity is
C[τ∨∩M]=C[S]χμ​,Uτ​=D(χμ)⊂Uσ​.​
(3)
Thus the face chart is an open subvariety.
For a face γ⪯C, the span of monomials χm with m∈/γ is an ideal: if m+n∈γ with m,n∈C, the face property forces both into γ. Its quotient is C[γ∩M], defining the closed subvariety Xγ​. If μ∈/γ, this subvariety lies in V(χμ).
Conversely, at a point p∈Uσ​, the exponents with χm(p)=0 form a semigroup face: a product is nonzero exactly when both factors are. These exponents are precisely the point of the toric lattices of a face γ of C. To see the geometric assertion, choose finitely many monoid generators by the Gordan lemma. The nonzero generators span a cone. If that cone were not a face, a rational nonnegative relation would express a sum of its generators using an omitted generator with a positive coefficient. Clearing denominators and evaluating the corresponding monomials would equate a nonzero product to zero. Conversely, any point of the toric lattice in their cone has a positive integral multiple expressible using them, so its monomial must also be nonzero. This proves the monomial support face of a toric point assertion. If χμ(p)=0, then μ∈/γ and every monomial outside γ vanishes at p, placing p in Xγ​. Therefore, as reduced closed subvarieties,
Uσ​∖Uτ​=γ⪯Cμ∈/γ​⋃​Xγ​.​
(4)
The assertion concerns the closed set with its reduced structure; a monomial's principal zero subscheme can have multiplicities.
For the intersection assertion, choose integral exposing functionals μi​ for τi​. Both are nonnegative on σ, so τ1​∩τ2​=σ∩(μ1​+μ2​)⊥. Localization then gives
Uτ1​​∩Uτ2​​=D(χμ1​)∩D(χμ2​)=D(χμ1​+μ2​)=Uτ1​∩τ2​​.​
(5)
This uses the dual of the intersection, as printed in the PDF; the TeX's perpendicular symbol in that position is not the correct transcription.
For a general toric fan, the points with a fixed monomial support face form a torus orbit. The nonzero evaluations extend to a character of the lattice generated by that face, and restriction from the ambient algebraic torus is surjective over C. Thus the affine calculation yields the orbit-cone correspondence: Uσ​ contains exactly the orbits O(ν) for faces ν⪯σ. The orbit associated to τ has character lattice M∩τ⊥, so it is the source's Xτ⊥​. The same monomial ideal calculation, chart by chart, shows that its closure is
Fτ​=⋃ν∈Σ, τ⪯ν​O(ν).
(6)
Indeed, inside a chart containing τ, the closure has exponent face σ∨∩τ⊥, and its smaller support faces correspond exactly to cones containing τ.
Fix σ∈Σ, as required for its affine chart. If τ is not contained in σ, none of the larger cones ν⊇τ can be contained in σ, so Fτ​ is disjoint from Uσ​. Conversely every orbit outside Uσ​ has a cone ν not contained in σ, and it lies in Fν​. Consequently the global complement is exactly
XΣ​∖Uσ​=τ∈Σ, τ⊆σ⋃​Fτ​.​
(7)

The smoothness criterion for a toric variety says that every cone must be regular: its primitive ray generators must be part of a lattice basis of N. Such a d-dimensional cone gives the chart Ad×(C∗)n−d; all charts are smooth precisely under this criterion.
Here is a terminating construction of a smooth fan subdivision. First subdivide compatibly into simplicial polyhedral cones. One method is the barycentric subdivision: choose a rational interior ray in each nonzero cone and perform the star subdivisions in decreasing dimension. Its cones correspond to chains of faces, hence are simplicial. For a simplicial cone with primitive generators vi​, let its multiplicity be the index of their integer span in the saturated lattice N∩spanR​σ.
If a cone is not regular, choose a nonregular face τ minimal under inclusion. Its half-open fundamental parallelepiped contains a nonzero point of the toric lattice w=∑i​αi​vi​, 0≤αi​<1. Every coefficient is positive: otherwise w lies in the span of a regular proper face, whose generators extend to a lattice basis, forcing all the remaining coefficients to be integers and hence zero. Replace w by the primitive vector w′ on its ray; its coefficients βi​ still satisfy 0<βi​<1. Star-subdivide the entire fan at this ray. In each cone containing τ, the new maximal cones replace one of these vi​ by w′, so multilinearity of the determinant makes the new multiplicity βi​ times the old one. It is a positive integer strictly smaller than the old multiplicity. Other cones are unchanged. Select τ inside a maximal cone of largest multiplicity. The number of maximal cones having that largest multiplicity decreases, and once it reaches zero the largest multiplicity decreases. This lexicographic integer descent terminates at multiplicity one. It proves every finite rational fan has a smooth subdivision, not merely a simplicial one; this is multiplicity descent in toric desingularization.
For the specified threefold, abbreviate the primitive rays by
a=(1,0,0),b=(1,1,1),c=(0,2,1),d=(1,1,0).
(8)
Its boundary two-dimensional faces are ab,bc,cd,da. For example their supporting normals, chosen inward, are (0,1,−1),(1,1,−2),(1,−1,2),(0,0,1); each is zero on the named pair and positive on the other two rays. These normals are primitive. Equivalently the greatest common divisor of the two-by-two minors for each ray pair is one, so these faces are regular; the rays themselves are primitive as well. The full cone is three-dimensional with four extremal rays, hence not simplicial and not regular. The orbit-cone correspondence now shows the closed torus-fixed point is its only singular point: every other orbit belongs to the smooth chart of a proper face.
Define the first subdivision by its maximal cones
Σ1max​={abc,acd}.​
(9)
Here a string of letters denotes their nonnegative span; all faces are included. This is the triangulation along diagonal ac of a positive cross-section. Both determinants have absolute value one, so it is smooth. The subdivision has the same support as the original cone, hence its toric morphism is proper and birational and is a resolution. No new rays were added, so it has no exceptional surfaces. Its internal wall ac gives a compact exceptional curve isomorphic to P1, by the two-ray quotient fan at that wall. The only proper coarsening is the original nonregular cone, so this resolution is minimal in the stated fan sense.
For a second resolution, set
e=a+c=(1,2,1)=21​(b+c+d),Σ2max​={abd,bce,cde,dbe}.​
(10)
Start with the other diagonal bd. The cone abd is regular, but bcd has determinant of absolute value two. Its primitive interior point e divides it into the three regular cones shown, each with determinant of absolute value one. Therefore Σ2​ is again a smooth resolution and is different from Σ1​.
This fan has exactly one new ray, so its only exceptional surface is Ee​≅P2. To identify it, quotient N by Ze. The three neighboring rays are bˉ,cˉ,dˉ, their sum is zero, and consecutive pairs are bases because bce,cde,dbe are regular. Their quotient fan is the simplex fan of the projective plane. In fact its normal bundle is OP2​(−2): the chart Ubcd​ before subdivision is A3/{±1} since its index-two lattice extension is generated by (b+c+d)/2, and the new radial parameter is the square of the ordinary radial parameter of O(−1). It has the transition factors of O(−2).
For minimality, the ray e cannot be deleted in a smooth coarsening. Deleting an interior ray in this triangulation merges all three incident cones into bcd, which is nonregular, or merges further into the original nonsimplicial cone. If e is retained, deleting a wall between its three incident cones gives a nonconvex union unless all three are merged, again giving bcd. The remaining internal wall bd separates abd and bde; their union has four extremal rays a,b,d,e, since a+e=b+d, and is not regular. Thus no proper coarsening is smooth. These are the two minimal toric resolutions of a four-ray threefold cone.
The factorization can be made completely explicit. Starting with Σ1​, add e=a+c in the wall ac, producing a smooth fan ΣA​ with maximal cones abe,bce,ade,cde. Next add
w=a+e=(2,2,1)=b+d
(11)
in the wall ae. The resulting common fan has maximal cones
abw,bwe,adw,dwe,bce,cde.
(12)
Starting instead with Σ2​, the star subdivision at w=b+d in the wall bd gives exactly the same six cones. Each insertion is the sum of the generators of a smooth two-dimensional cone. Such a star subdivision is the toric blowup along an orbit closure of its smooth invariant curve: in local coordinates the center has ideal (x1​,x2​), and the two blowup charts with ratios x2​/x1​ and x1​/x2​ give the two new cones. We have the zigzag of blowup morphisms
XΣ1​​ ⟵ XΣA​​ ⟵ XΣB​​ ⟶ XΣ2​​.​
(13)
The required birational map is the composite of the inverses of the first two morphisms with the last. This supplies individual smooth toric blowups, rather than appealing to an abstract factorization theorem.

A singular two-dimensional affine toric variety in the normal fan construction must come from a full-dimensional pointed cone; cones of smaller dimension give smooth charts with a torus factor. Let its primitive boundary generators be v0​,vt+1​. Choose an oriented lattice basis with v0​=(1,0), and write the other generator as (a,r), r>0. Primitivity gives gcd(a,r)=1. Changing the second basis vector by a multiple of the first lets us write the cone as
σ=R≥0​(1,0)+R≥0​(−q,r),0<q<r,gcd(q,r)=1.
(14)
Here r>1 because determinant one would give a smooth chart. The sublattice N′=Z(1,0)+Z(−q,r) has index r, and its affine chart is C2. The quotient N/N′ is cyclic, generated by (0,1), whose coordinates in the two boundary generators are (q/r,1/r). Thus the kernel of the corresponding finite map of tori acts on the two affine coordinates as (x,y)↦(ζqx,ζy). More explicitly, invariant monomials are exactly those xiyj with qi+j≡0(modr), which are the elements of the original dual lattice in the first quadrant. Their ring is C[σ∨∩M]. Hence the singularity is the cyclic quotient
Uσ​≅C2/μr​=Xr,q​.​
(15)
The choice of the two boundary axes is unordered; exchanging them replaces q by its inverse modulo r.
The Hirzebruch–Jung resolution is computed by the negative Euclidean algorithm. Put r0​=r, r1​=q and recursively set
bi​=⌈ri​ri−1​​⌉,ri+1​=bi​ri​−ri−1​.
(16)
The nonzero remainders strictly decrease, 0≤ri+1​<ri​, and their greatest common divisor stays one; the process terminates with rt​=1, rt+1​=0. All bi​≥2, and unwinding the recursion gives the negative continued fraction
qr​=[b1​,…,bt​]−:=b1​−b2​−⋯−1/bt​1​1​.
(17)
Start with v0​=(1,0) and v1​=(0,1) and define vi+1​=bi​vi​−vi−1​. The determinant of every consecutive pair is one, by induction. Moreover
det(vi​,(−q,r))=ri​,
(18)
again by the same recursion and the initial values r,q. Each intermediate ray is therefore inside σ. To check the ordering explicitly, the first coordinates after v1​ are negative, the second coordinates positive, and successive determinants are positive. At the last step the determinant with the endpoint is zero. The vector vt+1​ is primitive, points in the same direction as (−q,r), and hence equals it. Subdivide by all the intermediate rays v1​,…,vt​. Every new cone is regular, and the subdivision has the same support, so it gives a proper smooth resolution.
Each internal ray has a compact invariant curve Ei​≅P1, because the quotient fan at it has the two opposite primitive rays. The relation vi−1​+vi+1​=bi​vi​ gives its self-intersection Ei2​=−bi​. This can also be checked directly: on the chart for (vi−1​,vi​) use coordinates (x,y) with Ei​={y=0}; on the next chart the coordinates are (u,v)=(xbi​y,x−1), with Ei​={u=0}. The normal-coordinate transition is that of OP1​(−bi​), proving the self-intersection. Neighboring exceptional curves meet transversely once and nonneighbors do not meet.
Since bi​≥2, there is no exceptional minus-one curve. Combinatorially, deleting vi​ gives a cone of determinant bi​>1. Deleting a longer consecutive block cannot give a regular cone either: determinants of successively more distant rays satisfy the same recursion with coefficients at least two and grow from 0,1 to integers at least two. Thus no proper smooth coarsening of this subdivision exists. This proves the minimality of the minimal resolution of a cyclic quotient surface singularity; equivalently its exceptional chain has no curve that can be blown down to a smooth point. The number of exceptional curves is the length t of the negative continued fraction.
If t=1, then r/q=b1​ is an integer, so coprimality gives q=1. Conversely q=1 gives the single coefficient [r]− and one curve. If t=2, then
qr​=b1​−b2​1​=b2​b1​b2​−1​.
(19)
The numerator and denominator on the right are coprime, so r=b1​b2​−1 and q=b2​≥2. Therefore q>1 and q∣r+1. Conversely, if these conditions hold, then b1​=(r+1)/q≥2, b2​=q≥2 give exactly this two-term expansion. Hence the requested equivalences are
t=1⟺q=1,t=2⟺q>1 and q∣r+1.​
(20)
Now consider a complete fan in dimension two with three rays and at most one singularity. Its three two-dimensional cones are the consecutive ray pairs. At least two cones are regular, and they share a ray. After an oriented lattice change, take their consecutive generators to be v1​=(1,0), v2​=(0,1) and v3​=(−1,b): the first cone is a basis, and det(v2​,v3​)=1 forces the first coordinate −1. Completeness forces b<0, because the third pair must span the missing sector with positive determinant det(v3​,v1​)=−b. Write b=−r, r≥1. The unique positive primitive relation is v1​+rv2​+v3​=0, so the fan is that of the weighted projective plane P(1,r,1), or P(1,1,r) after permuting weights. For r=1 this is P2; for r>1 its remaining cone has type Xr,1​. This proves the full three-ray classification
XΣ​≅P(1,1,r),r≥1.​
(21)
These are the complete three-ray toric surfaces with at most one singular point, including the smooth case.
For four rays and exactly the specified singularity, place the three regular cones consecutively. Normalize the middle one to have generators (1,0),(0,1). Its neighboring regular cones then force the cyclic ray list
v1​=(a,−1),v2​=(1,0),v3​=(0,1),v4​=(−1,b).
(22)
The remaining cone has determinant det(v4​,v1​)=1−ab=r, so ab=1−r. Thus a,b have opposite signs, and s=∣a∣ is a positive divisor of r−1. In the oriented lattice basis v4​,(0,−1),
v1​=−av4​+r(0,−1),
(23)
so the singular cone has quotient parameter amodr. It follows that its parameter is congruent to ±s. If identification with the specified singularity exchanges the two boundary rays, the parameter is inverted; this does not change the criterion, since for st=r−1 we have s−1≡−t(modr), and t is the complementary positive factor. The same identity with a minus sign handles (−s)−1.
Conversely suppose s∣r−1 and s≡εq(modr), ε∈{1,−1}. Choose a=εs and b=−ε(r−1)/s in the displayed four rays. They are primitive and cyclically ordered, with all four gaps less than a half-plane; the opposite signs of a,b ensure completeness. Three consecutive determinants are one, and the fourth is r. Its quotient parameter is a≡q(modr), so the only singular point has the required type. We have proved the four-ray completion criterion for a cyclic quotient surface singularity:
∃ such complete four-ray surface⟺∃s>0: s∣r−1, s≡±q(modr).​
(24)

Let vi​ be the primitive lattice generator of the ray τi​. In the toric divisor class sequence, the first map sends a torus character exponent m∈M to its principal divisor on a toric variety:
m⟼div(χm)=∑i​⟨m,vi​⟩Fi​.
(25)
The second map sends an invariant Weil divisor to its class modulo principal divisors. Completeness makes the rays span NR​, so the first map is injective. These are the maps in the given sequence; its exactness need not be reproved.
For D=∑i​ri​Fi​, let
PD​={m∈MR​:⟨m,vi​⟩≥−ri​ for all i}.
(26)
The torus characters χm with m∈PD​∩M form a basis of L(D)={f∈C(X):div(f)+D≥0}∪{0}. This works for Weil divisors, not only for Cartier divisors. It is the character basis associated to the lattice polytope of a toric divisor.
For a Cartier divisor, the toric ampleness criterion is as follows. Each maximal cone σ has local Cartier data mσ​∈M satisfying ⟨mσ​,vi​⟩=−ri​ on its rays. The divisor is ample exactly when
⟨mσ​,vj​⟩>−rj​for every ray τj​⊆σ.
(27)
Equivalently the support function is strictly concave, with these linear pieces. For a Q-Cartier Weil divisor use rational mσ​ and clear their denominators; the criterion means that a positive multiple is ample Cartier. An arbitrary Weil divisor need not be Q-Cartier, so this qualification cannot be omitted.
Take positive weights q=(q0​,…,qn​), reduced so their common greatest common divisor is one. A toric definition of weighted projective space uses
N=Zn+1/Zq,vi​=ei​,Σ={cone(vi​:i∈I):I⊊{0,…,n}}.​
(28)
If the weights are well-formed, meaning gcd(q0​,…,qi​​,…,qn​)=1 for every i, the vi​ are primitive: the quotient N/Zvi​ has torsion of order that omitted-weight greatest common divisor. The lattice N itself is free because q is primitive. The positive relation ∑i​qi​vi​=0 implies that proper subsets generate pointed cones and that they meet along common faces. Completeness can be seen directly: represent a vector by real coordinates ai​ and subtract mini​(ai​/qi​) times q. The resulting coordinates are nonnegative and at least one is zero, placing the vector in one of the displayed cones. Their common-face property follows from this unique normalization of each class modulo Rq. This gives the fan of the well-formed weighted projective space.
The dual lattice is
M={m∈Zn+1:q⋅m=0}.
(29)
Its map in the divisor sequence is simply inclusion in Zn+1, so the homomorphism a↦q⋅a identifies the cokernel with Z. It is onto by coprimality of all the weights. Thus
Cl(X)≅Z,[Fi​]=qi​H.​
(30)
Choose integers ai​ by the Bezout identity ∑i​qi​ai​=1. Then D=∑i​ai​Fi​ explicitly represents the positive generator H.
To verify ampleness in the Weil/Q-Cartier sense, put ℓ=lcm(q0​,…,qn​). On the maximal cone omitting ray j, define
mj​=qj​ℓ​ej​−ℓa∈M.
(31)
This is integral and has q⋅mj​=ℓ−ℓ=0. Its pairings with the included rays are −ℓai​, while on the omitted ray they equal −ℓaj​+ℓ/qj​>−ℓaj​. Consequently ℓD is Cartier and ample by the numerical criterion. This is an explicit class-group generator of a well-formed weighted projective space.
The generator is generally ample Weil, not Cartier. If the source's word “ample” is interpreted as requiring a Cartier divisor, its generator request is false in general: on P(1,1,2), take D=F0​. On the chart omitting the weight-two ray, the Cartier equations force m=(−1,0,1/2), which is not in the integral dual lattice. Twice this datum is integral, so the class-one divisor has Cartier index two and no Cartier divisor generates its class group. With the usual ample-Q-Cartier-Weil interpretation, the construction above proves exactly the requested result. This distinction also explains the use of the divisorial spaces L(rD) rather than assuming an invertible sheaf O(D).
Finally let an ample divisor represent the class-group generator. Its class is the positive generator; the negative generator cannot be ample because a positive Cartier multiple has negative degree on the curves where the positive generator has positive degree. After replacing by a linearly equivalent invariant divisor, write it as D=∑ai​Fi​ with ∑qi​ai​=1. A character basis element of L(rD) has exponent m∈M with mi​+rai​≥0. Set bi​=mi​+rai​. Then b∈Z≥0n+1​ and ∑qi​bi​=r. Conversely every nonnegative exponent vector of weighted degree r gives the unique m=b−ra∈M. Therefore
χm∈L(rD)⟼X0m0​+ra0​​⋯Xnmn​+ran​​
(32)
is a bijection of bases in each degree. Adding m and r under multiplication adds these nonnegative exponent vectors, so it preserves products and the unit. A different linearly equivalent representative changes the basis by the corresponding rational-function power and gives the same graded-ring isomorphism. Hence the divisorial section ring is the weighted polynomial ring:
r≥0⨁​L(rD)≅k[X0​,…,Xn​],degXi​=qi​.​
(33)
The character construction is defined over the ground field k, so this is an isomorphism of graded k-algebras. It proves the divisorial section ring of a well-formed weighted projective space assertion even when its ample generator is not Cartier.
