(10)

A local passage near the saddle equilibrium contributes a power sδ to the return map. Because δ<1, its derivative becomes large as the orbit approaches the separatrix. The periodic orbits meeting the loops are therefore unstable, and their periods diverge logarithmically. On the side between the homoclinic and subcritical Hopf curves there are two unstable single-well periodic orbits, surrounding the two sink equilibria. On crossing the double-loop curve downward, they join into one unstable outer periodic orbit surrounding both wells. The unstable outer periodic orbit must subsequently meet that attracting outer periodic orbit in a fold of periodic orbits. This extra curve is required to obtain the six-region diagram. Write F=κfold​(λ), G=κhom​(λ) and H=−λ for λ<0, so 0<F<G<H near the double-zero point. The six open regions are:
  • I: λ>0,κ<0. The origin is the only equilibrium and is attracting; there is no periodic orbit.
  • II: λ>0,κ>0. The origin is repelling and is surrounded by an attracting periodic orbit, born at its supercritical Hopf bifurcation.
  • III: λ<0,κ<F. The origin is a saddle equilibrium and the two nonzero equilibria are sink equilibria. Its stable manifold separates their basins; there is no periodic orbit.
  • IV: λ<0,F<κ<G. There are two sink equilibria and the saddle equilibrium, an unstable periodic orbit surrounding all three, and a larger attracting periodic orbit. Initial conditions inside the unstable periodic orbit go to one of the sink equilibria, according to the saddle equilibrium's basin boundary; those outside it approach the attracting periodic orbit.
  • V: λ<0,G<κ<H. The saddle equilibrium and two sink equilibria remain, but each sink equilibrium is surrounded by its own unstable periodic orbit. A larger attracting periodic orbit surrounds the entire configuration. Each unstable inner periodic orbit bounds its sink equilibrium's basin.
  • VI: λ<0,κ>H. The two nonzero equilibria are repelling. The saddle equilibrium's stable manifold still leads to the saddle equilibrium, while generic trajectories approach the attracting outer periodic orbit.
The changes III–IV, IV–V and V–VI are respectively a fold of periodic orbits, the double homoclinic event, and the two subcritical Hopf bifurcations. Thus the unstable periodic orbits from the subcritical Hopf bifurcations, the expanding saddle equilibrium passage, and the attracting periodic orbit from the other Hopf bifurcation fit into one consistent portrait.

Here μ>μH​=λ(λ−1)>0. The origin is a saddle equilibrium, P+​ is repelling, and P−​ lies in u<0. Thus there is no saddle equilibrium in the interior of u>0 to which this periodic orbit could become homoclinic. The origin cannot supply the missing loop: the line u=0 is invariant and is precisely its local stable manifold. An orbit in u>0 cannot lie on that stable manifold, so the positive periodic orbit cannot close into a homoclinic orbit to the origin either.
The outgoing trajectories do not escape to infinity in this regime. To see the large-amplitude turning mechanism, put w=v/u once v≥0. Direct differentiation gives
w˙=u(w2−w+λ)−(λ+μ)w,u˙=u[μ+(1−w)u].
(18)
Since λ>1, the quadratic w2−w+λ is uniformly positive. At large u while 0≤w≤1, w˙ is bounded below by a positive multiple of u, whereas u˙/u≤μ+u. Thus w rises by a fixed amount per bounded increase in logu: the trajectory reaches w>1 and then w>1+μ/u, where u˙<0, before u can diverge. The same estimate bounds repeated outgoing excursions; large positive v also decreases when v>u2. Initially negative v increases until it becomes nonnegative. These nullcline and ratio estimates give bounded forward excursions.
The repelling upper equilibrium is therefore surrounded by recurrent trajectories, and the Poincaré-Bendixson alternative supplies periodic motion rather than a saddle equilibrium connection. The supercritical attracting periodic orbit persists in the displayed regime, with trajectories spiralling away from the upper equilibrium and approaching it. The last panel of the portrait figure shows this behavior; the boundary stable manifold of the origin is kept separate.

A global bifurcation joins two mechanisms: a long passage near a hyperbolic equilibrium point and a finite excursion returning the unstable manifold to its neighborhood. Eigenvalue ratios control the local passage, while the geometry of the global return decides reinjection, folding and trapping. In the real symmetric case there are two returning branches and a power-law cusp. In the saddle-focus case rotation adds infinitely many oscillations to that cusp. The following maps display both mechanisms explicitly.
For the Lorenz configuration, enter on z=h at (x,y,h) and leave on x=sh, where s=sgn(x). Solving the linear flow gives a flight time
T=λ+​1​log∣x∣h​.
(24)
Put γ=−λ/λ+​>δ=−λ−​/λ+​. The outgoing coordinates are
xout​=sh,yout​=y(∣x∣/h)γ,zout​=h(∣x∣/h)δ.
(25)
The smooth global map from each outgoing section to the incoming section has, to leading order,
xnew​=s[−μ+azout​]+O(yout​,zout2​),ynew​=sν+O(zout​,yout​),
(26)
up to the choice of which unstable branch carries the sign of ν. Reflection symmetry makes the offsets opposite and the leading coefficients equal. Since the stable transverse coordinate is multiplied by the higher power ∣x∣γ, it contributes only subleading terms to the leading scalar map. Absorbing powers of h into a positive constant gives the Lorenz power return map
xnew​=fL​(x)=sgn(x)(−μ+A∣x∣δ).​
(27)
The line x=0 lies on the stable manifold and has no finite return; the discontinuity is therefore a genuine missing return, not an arbitrary value to assign at zero.
If δ>1, the derivative Aδ∣x∣δ−1 tends to zero. For μ<0, there are two attracting fixed points, x=±s with s=−μ+Asδ≃−μ. They describe two symmetry-related one-lobe attracting flow periodic orbits. For μ>0, the orbit alternates between s and −s, where s=μ−Asδ≃μ; its multiplier is (Aδsδ−1)2<1. This represents one attracting flow periodic orbit traversing both lobes. At μ=0 the two homoclinic loops meet: this is the contracting gluing scenario, with diverging flow period at the connection.
If δ<1, the local derivative is unbounded, and the small periodic orbits are repelling in the expanding return direction. For μ>0, the small positive fixed point has s≃(μ/A)1/δ, with a multiplier tending to infinity; there is a symmetry-related negative fixed point as well. More complex motion follows from the two branches, not merely from a large derivative at one fixed point. Choose I=[−cμ,cμ] with 0<c<1. The inverse branches are
g+​(y)=((μ+y)/A)1/δ,g−​(y)=−((μ−y)/A)1/δ.
(28)
For sufficiently small positive μ, both lie inside I, their images are disjoint, and their derivatives are O(μ1/δ−1)→0. Nested inverse images for every infinite sign sequence therefore define a Cantor invariant set. Iterating the forward map shifts the sequence. This constructs periodic itineraries of every symbolic period and nonperiodic itineraries; transverse contraction in the full return turns the expanding two-strip mechanism into a horseshoe.
This is the local chaotic Lorenz mechanism. A robust attracting Lorenz set additionally needs a global trapping region and the appropriate return properties. The scalar asymptote and δ<1 alone do not prove that every nearby orbit is attracted to one chaotic set; periodic windows, escaping orbits and other global returns must be distinguished. Symmetry is crucial to obtaining the two homoclinic branches together in a single parameter variation.

Corrections from the returned radial deviation enter at order x2δ, smaller than the fixed-point term x in this range. Only positive returned x on the chosen branch belong to the scalar return domain.
For δ>1,
fS′​(x)=Axδ−1[δcos(qlogx+Φ)−qsin(qlogx+Φ)]⟶0.

It ends in the homoclinic connection at μ=0; for μ>0 there is no sufficiently small positive fixed point. The oscillations alone do not create expanding chaos when their derivative tends to zero.
For 1/2<δ<1, there is both net volume contraction and a positive saddle equilibrium value λ+​+λ−​>0. At the connection, fixed points satisfy
cos(qlogx+Φ)=Ax1−δ​.
(34)
The right side tends to zero, while the phase runs through infinitely many rotations. There are therefore infinitely many positive fixed points accumulating at zero near successive cosine zeros. Their successive size ratios tend to e−π/q and their derivative magnitudes diverge. In the full return they are saddle equilibrium periodic orbits: one multiplier expands and the thin transverse direction contracts. Their flow periods grow as λ+−1​log(h/x).
The oscillatory graph also supplies the complicated itineraries. Take a sufficiently small positive return interval. Near successive zero crossings, narrow monotone subintervals map across that interval with arbitrarily large slope. Two or more inverse branches contract into it, so repeated inverse choices give symbolic invariant sets just as in part (a); lifting the strips to the thin two-dimensional return gives horseshoes and infinitely many periodic orbits. This explains the Shilnikov chaotic mechanism rather than only naming it.
For nonzero splitting, the graph shifts vertically. Fixed-point tangencies with slope +1 create saddle-node bifurcations of periodic orbits; crossings of slope −1 give period doubling. Near an oscillatory extremum the derivative can be small, allowing stable periodic windows. Such thresholds accumulate geometrically toward the homoclinic parameter, interspersed with expanding returns. Hence the unfolding can contain complicated switching, arbitrarily long periods and chaos, rather than a single attracting periodic branch. This is log-periodic accumulation of Shilnikov cycles. The conclusion concerns invariant sets and possible attractors allowed by the return geometry; it does not assert that a homoclinic connection alone makes all nearby trajectories chaotic.

For μ>0, the cubic equation has the same sign as a everywhere away from zero: small positive or negative disturbances grow without saturation, and the cubic truncation even predicts finite-time blow-up. An amplitude of convection cannot grow indefinitely under that local approximation. The conclusion is that additional nonlinear physics and higher-order terms are needed, not that the small-amplitude reduction is valid at arbitrarily large amplitude.
For the modified equation put η=C1​μ and C=C2​>0. It can be written
a′=a[η−a+Ca2−a4].
(53)
The quintic term points inward for sufficiently large ∣a∣, so all forward solutions are bounded and approach equilibria in this scalar problem. The quadratic term breaks a↦−a symmetry. Besides a=0, the equilibrium diagram is
η=g(a)=a−Ca2+a4,a nonzero equilibrium is stable iff ag′(a)>0.​
(54)
This stability criterion follows from f′(a)=−ag′(a) on the equilibrium curve, where g′=1−2Ca+4a3. Near zero the new branch is a=η+O(η2), crossing the trivial branch transcritically. It is unstable for small negative η and stable for small positive η; the trivial branch has the opposite stability.
The negative-amplitude part of g has exactly one minimum. Above its negative fold value but below zero, a stable finite-amplitude negative state coexists with the stable origin, separated by an unstable negative equilibrium. This gives hysteresis and finite-amplitude triggering of convection. For positive η the origin is unstable and stable nonzero states provide saturation. Additional positive-amplitude folds depend on C: g′ has two positive roots for C>3/2, a double root at C=3/2 and none for C<3/2, so further multistability can occur. The right-hand figure illustrates C=1, without those extra positive folds; it does not assume that every possible C2​ gives that same detailed diagram. These are the qualitative changes in quintic saturation of a symmetry-broken pitchfork.
