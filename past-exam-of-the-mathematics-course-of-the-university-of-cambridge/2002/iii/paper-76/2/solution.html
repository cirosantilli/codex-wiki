A fixed-point combinator is a closed lambda term Z such that ZF≡β​F(ZF) for every lambda term F. It suffices to check this for a fresh free variable f, since capture-avoiding substitution then gives every instance. Take
G=λzf.f(zf).​
(4)
If GZ≡β​Z, applying both sides to f gives Zf≡β​f(Zf). Conversely suppose Z is a fixed-point combinator. Its application to a fresh f has a head normal form, because it is beta equivalent to f(Zf). Since Z is closed, head reduction of Zf must first expose a lambda abstraction in Z itself. Thus Z→β∗​λu.P, and
λf.Zf≡β​λf.P[u:=f]≡α​λu.P≡β​Z.
(5)
This establishes the needed abstraction equality by beta reduction, without assuming eta conversion. Abstracting Zf≡β​f(Zf) now gives Z≡β​λf.f(Zf)≡β​GZ.
Two examples are the Curry fixed-point combinator and the Turing fixed-point combinator:
Y=λf.(λx.f(xx))(λx.f(xx)),D=λxf.f(xxf),Θ=DD.
(6)
Writing Af​=λx.f(xx), we have Yf→β​Af​Af​→β​f(Af​Af​)≡β​f(Yf), while Θf→β2​f(Θf). Different displayed syntax alone would not prove that the two combinators are distinct under beta equivalence. Here is a reduct invariant that does. Every finite reduct of Y is λf.fn(Af​Af​), up to alpha equivalence: Af​ is beta-normal and the one remaining beta-redex is its self-application. No such reduct contains the closed subterm DD.
Every finite reduct of Θ, in contrast, retains a closed subterm DD. The contraction DD→λf.f(DDf) recreates one. The only other redexes created by these expansions are abstractions applied to variables; contracting them substitutes a variable, so preserves any closed DD inside their bodies. This describes all redexes inductively: expansion inserts only a variable-headed application and a copy of DD, and a variable substitution preserves that description. Thus every finite reduct contains DD. A common reduct of Y and Θ is impossible, so the Church-Rosser theorem proves Y≡β​Θ.
Finally put P=ZK, with K=λxy.x. The fixed-point combinator equation gives
P≡β​KP≡β​λy.P,
(7)
where y is fresh. If P had a head normal form with n leading abstractions, then λy.P would have one with n+1. A reduct of a head normal form preserves its initial abstraction count and variable-headed spine: only its argument subterms can still contract. These two head normal forms therefore cannot have a common reduct, contradicting the Church-Rosser theorem. Hence ZK is unsolvable.
