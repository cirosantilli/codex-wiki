Use the exponent convention EeiuXt​=etψ(u). A real Lévy process starts at 0, has stationary increments and independent increments, is stochastically continuous, and is taken in its càdlàg version. Its characteristic exponent of a Lévy process satisfies ψ(0)=0 and is continuous. This convention has the opposite sign from the alternative e−tΨ(u), so ψ=−Ψ.
For real u, the exponential martingale of a Lévy process is integrable because ∣Mtu​∣=e−tReψ(u) is deterministic and finite. For s≤t, its conditional expectation with respect to the natural filtration is
E[Mtu​∣Fs​]=eiuXs​−tψ(u)Eeiu(Xt​−Xs​)=eiuXs​−tψ(u)e(t−s)ψ(u)=Msu​.
(1)
The second equality uses both independent increments and stationary increments. Thus this is a complex martingale, meaning that its real and imaginary parts are martingales.
A probability distribution is an infinitely divisible distribution when, for every positive integer n, it is the distribution of a sum of n independent and identically distributed random variables. Their common distribution is allowed to depend on n. For a Lévy process,
X1​=∑j=1n​(Xj/n​−X(j−1)/n​),
(2)
and these summands are independent with the common law of X1/n​. This proves the required infinite divisibility directly.
The Lévy–Khintchine theorem states that a real infinitely divisible distribution has characteristic function eψ(u), where, for a unique triplet with the following fixed truncation,
ψ(u)=ibu−2a​u2+∫R∖{0}​(eiuy−1−iuy1{∣y∣<1}​)K(dy),​
(3)
with b∈R, a≥0, K a positive measure, K({0})=0, and ∫(1∧y2)K(dy)<∞. Conversely every such triplet gives an infinitely divisible distribution and a Lévy process, unique in law, whose time-t characteristic function is etψ(u). The Lévy measure K need not be finite near zero. Taylor expansion makes the compensated integrand O(y2) there, while the measure is finite away from zero, so the integral is well defined. The drift parameter depends on the truncation: using ∣y∣≤1 instead can alter b by the contribution of atoms at ±1. Here a denotes Gaussian variance, not drift.
We now prove that continuity forces K=0, using the suggested truncation rather than merely quoting a path classification. For ε>0, λε​=K(∣y∣≥ε) is finite. The truncated exponent is that of the Compound Poisson process
Ytε​=∑j=1Nt​​Jj​−t∫ε≤∣y∣<1​yK(dy),
(4)
where N has rate λε​ and the marks have law K(dy)1{∣y∣≥ε}​/λε​ when the rate is positive. The remaining exponent ψ−ψε​ also has an admissible Lévy–Khintchine formula triplet. Hence construct independently a Lévy process Zε with that exponent. The sum Yε+Zε has the same finite-dimensional distributions as X, and hence the same law on càdlàg path space. Continuity is therefore an almost-sure property of the sum if it is one of X.
But independent compound Poisson jumps cannot cancel: conditional on the path of Zε, its jump times are countable; the independent Poisson jump times have continuous distributions and avoid them almost surely. At each jump of Yε, the sum has the same nonzero jump, of size at least ε. If λε​>0, there is positive probability of such a jump on any fixed positive-length time interval, contradicting almost-sure continuity. Thus K(∣y∣≥ε)=0 for every ε>0, and taking ε=1/n gives K=0.
The characteristic function is now exp[t(ibu−au2/2)]. If a>0, the process Bt​=(Xt​−bt)/a​ has continuous paths and independent centered normal increments of variance t−s, so it is standard Brownian motion on the original probability space. If a=0, the increments are deterministic, and continuity together with equality at all rational times gives Xt​=bt simultaneously for all t almost surely. A Brownian motion on an enlarged space can be used in the zero-variance representation if necessary. Therefore
Xt​=bt+a​Bt​,a≥0.​
(5)

In discrete time, the bounded-time optional stopping theorem says that an integrable supermartingale X and bounded stopping times S≤T satisfy E[XT​∣FS​]≤XS​. For a martingale there is equality. The assertion at a possibly unbounded, almost-surely finite stopping time T also holds with S=0 if {XT∧n​:n≥0} is uniformly integrable: then XT​ is integrable and EXT​≤EX0​, with equality for a martingale. Boundedness of T or the stated integrability condition cannot in general be omitted.
For the increment hypothesis here, ET<∞ implies T<∞ almost surely, and telescoping gives
∣XT∧n​∣≤∣X0​∣+KT,∣XT​∣≤∣X0​∣+KT.
(6)
The right-hand side is an integrable random variable. Applying bounded-time optional stopping to T∧n and then the dominated convergence theorem proves
XT​∈L1,EXT​≤EX0​.​
(7)
This is bounded-increment optional stopping with integrable time; the same reasoning gives equality for a martingale.
Put q=1−p and first assume 0<p<1. Let τ be the first occurrence time of the specified seven-letter word. Its probability in a prescribed block is r=p4q3>0. Disjoint seven-flip blocks are independent, and a match in one forces τ to have occurred by its end. Consequently P(τ>7m)≤(1−r)m and Eτ≤7/r<∞; this establishes the integrability needed for stopping before computing its value.
Start a new gambler with unit capital immediately before each flip. A gambler bets all current capital on the next required letter of the word, receives capital divided by p for a correct head or by q for a correct tail, and receives zero on failure. After completing all seven bets, the gambler retains the winnings without further betting. Each individual bet preserves its conditional expected capital. If Cn​ is the total capital of all gamblers who have entered through flip n, then Cn​−n is a martingale.
At any time at most seven gamblers are still betting, and each one's capital is at most L=1/(p4q3), since probabilities of shorter prefixes are at least the full-word probability. Completed winnings do not change. Thus the increments of Cn​−n have a deterministic bound, for example 14L+1. The preceding optional-stopping argument therefore gives E(Cτ​−τ)=0.
At the first full match, no earlier gambler has completed the word. A surviving gambler who has placed k successful bets corresponds to a suffix of the completed word which is also its length-k prefix, namely a border of a word. The only such lengths are 3 and 7: the shorter common string is HHT. Their capitals are 1/(p2q) and 1/(p4q3), respectively. Hence Cτ​ is this deterministic sum and
Eτ=p4(1−p)31​+p2(1−p)1​.​
(8)
For a fair coin this is 136 flips. This is a waiting time for a word in independent nonuniform symbols; the overlap contribution is essential. If p=0 or p=1, the word cannot occur because it contains both types of flip, so the expected waiting time is infinite.

For real λ, the normal moment-generating formula gives Eeλ(Bt​−Bs​)=eλ2(t−s)/2. Using independent increments and conditioning on the Brownian filtration,
E[eλBt​−λ2t/2∣Fs​]=eλBs​−λ2t/2eλ2(t−s)/2=eλBs​−λ2s/2.
(9)
All terms are integrable by the same normal formula. Thus the displayed process is an Exponential martingale for Brownian motion.
For the interval exit time, we first show both almost-sure finiteness and finite expectation. On {T>n}, the position at time n lies in (−b,a). If Xn+1​−Xn​≥a+b, the continuous path must exit by time n+1. This increment is independent of Fn​ and has law N(μ,1), so the conditional probability of this event is a fixed c>0. Therefore
P(T>n+1)≤(1−c)P(T>n),P(T>n)≤(1−c)n.

This geometric tail bound from a uniform escape probability implies T<∞ almost surely and ET<∞.

Solving yields
P(XT​=a)=1−e−2μ(a+b)1−e−2μb​.​

As a check, letting μ↓0 gives the driftless formulas v→b/(a+b) and ET→ab. The expectation calculation uses a justified stopping limit, rather than assuming that optional stopping holds at every unbounded time.

Standard one-dimensional Brownian motion is a real process with B0​=0, continuous paths almost surely, and increments Bt​−Bs​∼N(0,t−s) independent of the past at time s, for 0≤s<t. These conditions determine its entire finite-dimensional distribution family.
The Wiener theorem asserts that such a process exists, equivalently that there is a unique Wiener measure on C0​([0,∞),R) under which the coordinate process has these properties. To construct it, prescribe centered multivariate normal laws with covariance EBs​Bt​=min(s,t). This covariance is positive semidefinite because
∑i,j​ci​cj​min(ti​,tj​)=∫0∞​(∑i​ci​1[0,ti​]​(u))2du≥0.
(14)
The laws are consistent under removing coordinates, so the Kolmogorov extension theorem constructs the process. Normal increments have fourth moment E∣Bt​−Bs​∣4=3∣t−s∣2. The Kolmogorov continuity theorem therefore supplies a continuous modification, retaining all the prescribed finite-dimensional laws and hence the independent normal increments. Apply this on every compact time interval, taking consistent versions; continuity and equality at rational times make them agree on overlaps. The resulting law on continuous-path space is unique because evaluations at rational times generate its Borel sigma-algebra. This is a proof sketch of the existence theorem, independent of the random-walk limit in Question 5.

For every positive real r, a centered normal variable of variance t−s has absolute rth moment cr​∣t−s∣r/2, where cr​=E∣N(0,1)∣r<∞. Fix 0<α<1/2 and choose r large enough that α<1/2−1/r. Then
E∣Bt​−Bs​∣r=cr​∣t−s∣1+(r/2−1).
(15)
The Kolmogorov continuity theorem gives a modification with Hölder continuity of every exponent less than (r/2−1)/r=1/2−1/r, in particular exponent α, on each compact interval. This modification and the given continuous Brownian motion agree almost surely at all rational times; continuity makes them agree everywhere simultaneously. Thus the property holds for the given trajectories, not just for another version.
Take a countable increasing sequence of positive exponents approaching 1/2, and intersect their probability-one events for every integer time horizon. Hölder continuity of a larger exponent implies that of every smaller positive exponent on a compact interval, after adjusting the constant. We have therefore proved the simultaneous assertion
Almost surely, B is locally α-Ho¨lder for every 0<α<21​.​
(16)
The compact-interval qualification matters: a single uniform Hölder constant on the entire half-line is not being asserted.

We use dyadic quadratic variation of Brownian motion. For fixed rational 0≤u<v, set ℓ=v−u and let Qn​ be the sum of the squares of the 2n Brownian increments on its equal-length partition. Independence and the normal fourth moment give
EQn​=ℓ,Var(Qn​)=2ℓ22−n.
(17)
For every η>0, Chebyshev's inequality bounds P(∣Qn​−ℓ∣>η) by 2ℓ2η−22−n, a summable sequence. The Borel-Cantelli lemma implies Qn​→ℓ almost surely. Intersect over positive rational η and all rational intervals to obtain one probability-one event on which every such interval has this positive quadratic-variation limit.
If a path were Hölder of exponent α>1/2 on a nonempty open interval, choose a compact rational subinterval [u,v] within it and a finite Hölder constant C there. Every partition increment would have absolute value at most C(ℓ2−n)α, so
Qn​≤C2ℓ2α2n(1−2α)⟶0.
(18)
This contradicts Qn​→ℓ>0. The same pathwise event excludes every exponent greater than 1/2, without needing an uncountable intersection of events. Therefore
Almost surely, no nonempty interval supports a Ho¨lder exponent >21​.​
(19)
This is the quadratic variation obstruction to Hölder regularity. It rules out a continuously differentiable trajectory on any interval, since a continuous derivative is bounded on smaller compact intervals and gives a Lipschitz bound. By itself it does not rule out differentiability at an isolated point. The stronger nowhere differentiability of Brownian motion can also be seen directly as follows.
On a mesh of width h=2−n in [0,M], the probability that any specified three consecutive increments all have absolute value at most Kh is at most CK3h3/2: each increment is N(0,h) and the three are independent. There are at most M/h possible blocks, so the probability of any such block is at most CMK3h1/2, summable in n. By Borel-Cantelli lemma, for every pair of positive integers M,K there are eventually no such blocks. But a finite derivative at some time t<M would imply ∣Bs​−Bt​∣≤Ct​∣s−t∣ near t. The three mesh increments beginning at the mesh point immediately to the left of t would then all be at most 6Ct​h for all sufficiently fine meshes. Choosing an integer K≥6Ct​ contradicts the preceding event. Thus finite derivatives do not exist anywhere almost surely, including a finite right derivative at 0. The Hölder estimates below 1/2 express continuity with a roughness scale; they do not imply differentiability.

Write Zt​=⟨κt​⟩, where the angle brackets average only over walk paths, and use E only for the environment. Every nearest-neighbor path of length t has probability (2d)−t, so the finite path sum is exactly the walk expectation. It depends only on environment layers through time t, and hence is Ft​-measurable. Its factors lie in [1−ε,1+ε], so Zt​ is positive and integrable at each fixed time.
For any fixed path through time t+1, the environment variable h(t+1,ξt+1​) is independent of Ft​ and has mean zero. Conditional expectation therefore removes its last factor. Summing over the 2d possible last steps of each length-t path cancels the extra factor 2d in the path probability. Thus
E[Zt+1​∣Ft​]=Zt​,Z0​=1,EZt​=1.​
(27)
This is the positive random-environment path-weight martingale. It is nonnegative and bounded in L1, so the martingale convergence theorem gives a finite limit ζ≥0 almost surely. At this stage one can conclude Eζ≤1 by Fatou's lemma, but not yet equality; the second-moment bound in part (b) will prevent loss of mean.
