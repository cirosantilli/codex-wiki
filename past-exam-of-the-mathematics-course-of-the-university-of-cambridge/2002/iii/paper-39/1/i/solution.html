Work with a nonsingular multivariate normal distribution, so V is positive-definite. The claimed finite maximum-likelihood estimates also require the centered sample covariance matrix to be nonsingular; the exceptional case is discussed below.
Independence gives, up to a constant, the log-likelihood
ℓ(μ,V)=−2n​logdetV−21​∑i=1n​(Yi​−μ)TV−1(Yi​−μ).
(1)
Let Yˉ=n−1∑i​Yi​ and S=n−1∑i​(Yi​−Yˉ)(Yi​−Yˉ)T. The centered sum is zero, and expansion therefore gives
∑i​(Yi​−μ)(Yi​−μ)T=nS+n(Yˉ−μ)(Yˉ−μ)T.
(2)
For every fixed positive-definite matrix V, the extra quadratic term in the log-likelihood is −n(Yˉ−μ)TV−1(Yˉ−μ)/2, with unique maximum at μ=Yˉ.
Assume S is positive-definite. Maximizing over V now means minimizing logdetV+tr(V−1S). Put A=S1/2V−1S1/2, another positive-definite matrix. Then
logdetV+tr(V−1S)=logdetS−logdetA+trA.
(3)
If a1​,…,ap​>0 are its eigenvalues, the variable part is ∑j​(aj​−logaj​). The scalar inequality a−loga≥1, with equality only at a=1, shows that this is minimized uniquely at A=I, or V=S. Thus
μ​=Yˉ,V=S=n1​i∑​(Yi​−Yˉ)(Yi​−Yˉ)T.​
(4)
The divisor is n because this is maximum likelihood estimation, not the unbiased covariance estimate with divisor n−1.
The nonsingularity condition matters. The centered scatter has rank at most n−1. If S is singular, choose μ=Yˉ, retain positive covariance eigenvalues on its range and let a covariance eigenvalue on its null space tend to zero. The quadratic likelihood term remains finite, while −logdetV→+∞. This is singular covariance and nonexistence of a Gaussian maximum likelihood estimate: there is no finite maximizer over nonsingular V. Under a nonsingular Gaussian population, S is positive definite almost surely when n>p; for n≤p it is necessarily singular. The asserted estimate and the inverse in the next part have their usual meaning under n>p.
