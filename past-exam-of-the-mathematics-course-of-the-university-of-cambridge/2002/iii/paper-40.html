i OurBigBook.com
For a target probability density function π(θ1​,…,θp​), one systematic sweep of the Gibbs sampler updates coordinates successively:
θj(m+1)​∼π(θj​∣θ1(m+1)​,…,θj−1(m+1)​,θj+1(m)​,…,θp(m)​),j=1,…,p.
(20)
Each update leaves the target invariant: keeping the other coordinates with their target marginal and replacing one coordinate by its conditional distribution reconstructs the target joint probability density function. Therefore composition of the coordinate updates also preserves π. It defines a Markov chain whose consecutive draws are generally dependent. Under irreducibility, aperiodicity and the usual recurrence conditions it converges to the target law from an arbitrary valid start; a stationary start gives exact target marginals immediately. Invariance alone does not prove these ergodicity conditions, and a systematic full sweep need not itself be reversible.

At temperature T>0, target a probability density function proportional to exp(f(θ)/T) on a parameter region where it is normalizable, possibly with a specified proper base measure. A Metropolis–Hastings algorithm proposal q(θ,θ′) has acceptance probability
min{1,exp[Tf(θ′)−f(θ)​]q(θ,θ′)q(θ′,θ)​}.
(33)
For a symmetric proposal, improving moves are accepted and a loss Δ is accepted with probability e−Δ/T. Run updates while gradually lowering T toward zero. This simulated annealing scheme initially permits exploration and increasingly favors high objective values. Appropriate cooling and mixing conditions are needed for a general global convergence theorem; a finite arbitrary schedule does not guarantee finding a global maximum.
To maximize a positive likelihood function, take f=logL, which has the same maximizers. The resulting likelihood-power annealing target is L1/T, and available Gibbs sampler conditional distributions can replace Metropolis proposals.

Q=∑j​nj(m)​logαj​−2n​logv−2v1​∑i,j​wij(m)​(xi​−μj​)2.
(44)
Maximizing the first term with α1​+α2​=1 gives α(m+1)=n1(m)​/n. Differentiating in each μj​ gives μj(m+1)​=∑i​wij(m)​xi​/nj(m)​. After these updates, maximizing in the shared variance gives
v(m+1)=n1​i=1∑n​j=1∑2​wij(m)​(xi​−μj(m+1)​)2.​

For a weakly stationary process with finite second moments, let μ=EXt​ and γ(h)=Cov(Xt+h​,Xt​), independent of t. If γ(0)>0, its autocorrelation function is ρ(h)=γ(h)/γ(0). Given observations x1​,…,xN​, one usual sample autocovariance function is
γ​(h)=N1​∑t=1N−h​(xt​−xˉ)(xt+h​−xˉ),ρ​(h)=γ​(0)γ​(h)​.
(46)
The correlogram plots ρ​(h) against lag h. The partial autocorrelation function at lag h is the correlation between Xt​ and Xt−h​ after linearly projecting each onto the intervening observations. Its sample version can be calculated by solving the Yule-Walker regression equations
Rh​ϕ​h​=(ρ​(1),…,ρ​(h))T,(Rh​)ij​=ρ​(∣i−j∣),
(47)
then taking the last coefficient ϕ​h,h​. Equivalently, use the corresponding residual correlation from the sample regressions, with a consistent covariance convention.
For a minimal causal autoregressive model of order p, the population partial autocorrelation function is zero after lag p, while the autocorrelation function follows an exponentially decaying or damped oscillatory tail. For a moving-average model of order q, its autocovariance and hence autocorrelation are zero after lag q, because observations farther apart share no driving-noise terms; its partial correlations usually tail off. These order identification by autocorrelation cutoffs patterns suggest AR or MA candidate orders. Sample cutoffs are approximate and should be judged with sampling uncertainty and residual diagnostics; the familiar white-noise bands near ±1.96/N​ are a null approximation, not universal confidence limits for a dependent series.
For the trend problem, write a symmetric estimator as
Tt​=a(Xt−2​+Xt+2​)+b(Xt−1​+Xt+1​)+cXt​.
(48)
Reproducing arbitrary constant and quadratic trends requires 2a+2b+c=1 and 8a+2b=0; the linear-trend condition holds automatically by symmetry. These constraints follow by expanding Tt+j​=Tt​+j(b0​+2c0​t)+c0​j2 for a trend Tt​=a0​+b0​t+c0​t2. Thus b=−4a, c=1+6a. Since the errors are uncorrelated and the estimator is unbiased, its mean squared error is
σ2(2a2+2b2+c2)=σ2(70a2+12a+1).
(49)
The strictly convex quadratic is minimized at a=−3/35, giving the five-point symmetric quadratic trend smoother
Tt​=35−3Xt−2​+12Xt−1​+17Xt​+12Xt+1​−3Xt+2​​,MSE=3517​σ2.​
(50)
This weighted moving average allows negative coefficients; a convex average could not cancel the quadratic moment.
For three points, symmetry gives weights (a,b,a). Unbiasedness for every quadratic requires 2a+b=1 and 2a=0, so the only solution is (0,1,0). Thus there is no nontrivial three-point quadratic-preserving smoother. The literal claim that there is no unbiased estimator at all is false, since Tt​=Xt​ is unbiased with error variance σ2. This is the three-point quadratic reproduction forces the identity filter distinction.
For seasonal extraction by a centred moving average, use the additive decomposition Xt​=Tt​+St​+εt​, where St​ has period m and its values over one period sum to zero. An average covering a full period removes the seasonal component and approximately retains a slowly changing trend. If m is odd, take the centred length-m average; if m is even, centre it by averaging two adjacent length-m averages, giving half weights at the two endpoints. Subtract this trend estimate from the data, average residuals within each seasonal phase over different cycles, and centre the resulting phase estimates to sum to zero. For a multiplicative decomposition, use ratios and normalize the phase estimates to average one.
Successive moving averages compose by convolution of their weights. Even an initially uncorrelated noise series becomes correlated: for weights wj​, the filtered noise has covariance σ2∑j​wj​wj+h​. Further smoothing changes this covariance again and can turn random variation into apparently persistent patterns while obscuring genuine short-scale features. Spectrally, repeated use of a transfer function H multiplies the spectrum by ∣H∣2r after r applications, so repeated smoothing is not harmless repetition of the same estimate.
In simple exponential smoothing, choose 0<α<1 and update ℓt​=αXt​+(1−α)ℓt−1​. Iteration gives ℓt​=α∑j=0t−1​(1−α)jXt−j​+(1−α)tℓ0​, so older observations receive exponentially decreasing weights. Forecast Xt+h∣t​=ℓt​ for every h≥1. This is appropriate for a locally stable level with no persistent trend or seasonality.
For a local linear trend, double exponential smoothing supplies level and slope. Holt's linear trend method uses
ℓt​=αXt​+(1−α)(ℓt−1​+bt−1​),bt​=β(ℓt​−ℓt−1​)+(1−β)bt−1​,Xt+h∣t​=ℓt​+hbt​.​
(51)
Another standard convention is Brown's double exponential smoothing: smooth twice with the same gain, st(1)​=αXt​+(1−α)st−1(1)​ and st(2)​=αst(1)​+(1−α)st−1(2)​, then put at​=2st(1)​−st(2)​, bt​=α(st(1)​−st(2)​)/(1−α) and forecast at​+hbt​. These variants handle a nonseasonal trend; seasonal effects need separate modelling, and a long-horizon linear extrapolation assumes the current local slope persists.

A linear filter of a stationary time series with real coefficients (aj​)j∈Z​ satisfying ∑j​∣aj​∣<∞ is Yt​=∑j​aj​Xt−j​. Its filter generating function is the Laurent series A(z)=∑j​aj​zj, absolutely convergent on ∣z∣=1, and its filter transfer function is HA​(λ)=A(e−iλ). For a one-sided filter this is a power series; a general two-sided filter need not converge on a larger annulus.
The filter theorem states that if X is second-order stationary, then Y exists in mean square, is second-order stationary, and has mean and covariance
EYt​=μ∑j​aj​,γY​(h)=∑j,k​aj​ak​γX​(h−j+k).
(52)
Its spectral measure of a stationary time series is dFY​(λ)=∣HA​(λ)∣2dFX​(λ). In particular, if X has a time-series spectral density in the convention γX​(h)=∫−ππ​eihλfX​(λ)dλ, then
fY​(λ)=∣A(e−iλ)∣2fX​(λ).​
(53)
To prove existence, the L2 norm of a tail of the filter is at most ∥X0​∥2​ times the sum of the omitted ∣aj​∣, which tends to zero. Hence finite partial sums converge in L2. Expectations and covariances pass to the limit, and the covariance double sum is absolutely convergent because ∣γX​(h)∣≤γX​(0). Its formula depends only on h, proving stationarity. Insert the spectral representation of γX​(h−j+k) and interchange sums and integration; the bound ∑j,k​∣aj​ak​∣FX​([−π,π])<∞ justifies the interchange. The resulting factor is ∑j​aj​e−ijλ∑k​ak​eikλ=∣A(e−iλ)∣2. This proves the spectral statement, including the spectral density transformation under a linear filter without assuming that the input autocovariances are absolutely summable.
For an autoregressive moving-average model, use real polynomials normalized by ϕ0​=θ0​=1 and first work with the reduced noise-driven representation. If ϕ has no zero on the unit circle, the rational function C(z)=θ(z)/ϕ(z) has a Laurent expansion in an annulus containing that circle, with absolutely summable coefficients. Filtering the white noise with it gives a stationary solution. Its time-series spectral density is
fX​(λ)=2πσ2​∣ϕ(e−iλ)∣2∣θ(e−iλ)∣2​,−π≤λ≤π.​
(54)
Conversely, for coprime ϕ,θ, a unit-circle zero of ϕ would make this ratio nonintegrable, since the numerator does not vanish there; thus there is no finite-variance stationary noise-driven solution. A stationary solution can use a bilateral filter when autoregressive roots lie inside the unit circle. The stronger causal condition, meaning dependence only on present and past noise, requires every zero of ϕ strictly outside the unit disk. Common factors must be handled before applying these conditions; a cancelled unit-root factor can also allow additional deterministic stationary components, which are outside the minimal noise-driven convention.
Identifiability means that different admissible parameters cannot produce the same stationary process law. In this zero-mean Gaussian setting, its law is determined by its covariance, equivalently its spectrum. Unrestricted ARMA coefficients are not identifiable from that law: if a moving-average factor has root r inside the disk, then on ∣z∣=1,
∣1−z/r∣2=∣r∣−2∣1−rz∣2.
(55)
Reflecting the root outside and multiplying the innovation variance by ∣r∣−2 preserves the spectrum, and therefore the Gaussian law. Common polynomial factors and arbitrary overall scaling give further redundancy. A regular canonical identifiable parameterization requires constant coefficients one, coprime polynomials of their stated minimal orders, all zeros of both ϕ and θ outside the closed unit disk, and σ2>0.
Here is a proof of canonical identifiability of Gaussian ARMA models. Suppose two such models have equal spectra and write their innovation standard deviations as σ,τ>0. The ratio R(z)=σθ(z)ϕ​(z)/[τθ(z)ϕ(z)] and its reciprocal are analytic on a neighbourhood of the closed unit disk, with modulus one on its boundary. The maximum modulus principle applied to both makes ∣R∣=1 throughout the disk, so R is constant. Its value at zero is the positive number σ/τ, forcing R=1 and σ=τ. Therefore θϕ​=θϕ; coprimality and the normalization at zero force equality of both polynomials. This proves the claimed parameter uniqueness within that canonical class.
In this class, D(z)=ϕ(z)/θ(z) is analytic on a disk of radius greater than one. Its Taylor series coefficients are absolutely summable, so
εt​=D(B)Xt​=j≥0∑​dj​Xt−j​.​
(56)
This is an invertible time-series representation, proved by composition of the stable filters. Causality makes εt​ orthogonal to past observations; invertibility makes past noises recoverable from past observations. Their closed past linear spans agree, so this noise is the linear innovation and the one-sided expansion Xt​=∑j≥0​cj​εt−j​ is the Wold representation. Mere stationarity without the stated canonical restrictions does not imply invertibility of the originally written noise representation.
For this one-sided canonical representation, C(z)=θ(z)/ϕ(z)=∑j≥0​cj​zj. Comparing coefficients gives the ARMA coefficient recursions
∑r=0min(p,k)​ϕr​ck−r​=θk​,cj​=0 (j<0),θk​=0 (k>q).
(57)
Thus for exactly the range asked for,
ck​+ϕ1​ck−1​+⋯+ϕp​ck−p​=0(k>max(p−1,q)).​
(58)
The characteristic rates are reciprocals of the zeros of ϕ. Partial fractions therefore express the tail as sums of Pr​(k)rk, where ∣r∣<1 and the degree of Pr​ is at most one less than the corresponding root multiplicity. Hence the coefficients decay exponentially, with possible polynomial and damped oscillatory factors. If p=0, they instead vanish after q. For a noncausal or noninvertible original specification, the Wold coefficients refer to the canonical spectral factor; using the original driving noise as the Wold innovation without checking these restrictions would be incorrect.
The autocovariance is γ(h)=σ2∑j≥0​cj​cj+∣h∣​. Analyticity of C on a disk of radius R>1 gives ∣cj​∣≤MR−j for a slightly smaller radius than its first pole, and consequently ∣γ(h)∣≤σ2M2R−∣h∣/(1−R−2). Moreover, multiply the ARMA equation at time t by Xt−h​ and take expectations. For h>q, all noises εt−s​ with 0≤s≤q are later than Xt−h​ and orthogonal to it. For h>max(p−1,q) this gives
γ(h)+ϕ1​γ(h−1)+⋯+ϕp​γ(h−p)=0.​
(59)
The covariance tail therefore has the same characteristic exponential rates and multiplicity patterns as the filter coefficients, subject to possible cancellation of amplitudes. Pure moving averages have an exact covariance cutoff.
For the final process, factor the polynomials:
ϕ(z)=1−3z​−92z2​=(1−32z​)(1+3z​),θ(z)=1−32z​.
(60)
The common factor has its zero at 3/2, so it can be cancelled as a stable filter. Explicitly, let Yt​=(1+B/3)Xt​−εt​; the original equation gives Yt​=(2/3)Yt−1​.
