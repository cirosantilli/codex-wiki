Optical cluster observations give galaxy positions and line-of-sight redshifts. Subtract the mean recession to obtain the line-of-sight velocity dispersion σlos​. If the tracers are in approximate virial equilibrium with isotropic velocities, ⟨v2⟩=3σlos2​ and K≃3Mσlos2​/2. Write the gravitational energy as UG​=−αGM2/R, with α determined by the density profile and radius convention. The virial theorem gives
M≃αG3Rσlos2​​.​
(37)
For a uniform sphere α=3/5, this becomes 5Rσlos2​/G. Projected separations are commonly used to estimate a gravitational radius rather than literally assume a uniform sphere. Interlopers, substructure, anisotropy, tracer bias and a nonzero surface term affect the estimate. The inferred mass is total gravitating mass, not merely the galaxies' stellar mass.
For X-ray observations, the hot intracluster gas emits thermal bremsstrahlung and atomic lines. Spectroscopy determines its temperature, and the surface brightness, after deprojection, determines a gas-density profile. Under spherical hydrostatic equilibrium with thermal gas pressure, dPg​/dr=−ρg​GM(<r)/r2 and Pg​=ρg​kB​T/(μmp​). Consequently
M(<r)=−Gμmp​kB​T(r)r​(dlnrdlnρg​​+dlnrdlnT​).​
(38)
The order-of-magnitude virial version is kB​T∼μmp​GM/R. X-ray gas temperature is therefore a second measure of the depth of the gravitational potential. Nonthermal pressure, mergers, clumping and temperature gradients must be considered before equating the thermal estimate to the true mass.
For dense clusters the vacuum force is small compared with their internal gravity. If retained in the hydrostatic equation, it changes GM/r2 to GM/r2−Λr/3 and adds Λr3/(3G) to the mass formula above. The optical virial relation likewise uses the separate vacuum contribution derived in part (c). Comparing optical and X-ray estimates tests the dynamical assumptions and exposes a gravitating mass much larger than the luminous stellar component.
