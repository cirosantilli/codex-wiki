A linear filter of a stationary time series with real coefficients (aj​)j∈Z​ satisfying ∑j​∣aj​∣<∞ is Yt​=∑j​aj​Xt−j​. Its filter generating function is the Laurent series A(z)=∑j​aj​zj, absolutely convergent on ∣z∣=1, and its filter transfer function is HA​(λ)=A(e−iλ). For a one-sided filter this is a power series; a general two-sided filter need not converge on a larger annulus.
The filter theorem states that if X is second-order stationary, then Y exists in mean square, is second-order stationary, and has mean and covariance
EYt​=μ∑j​aj​,γY​(h)=∑j,k​aj​ak​γX​(h−j+k).
(52)
Its spectral measure of a stationary time series is dFY​(λ)=∣HA​(λ)∣2dFX​(λ). In particular, if X has a time-series spectral density in the convention γX​(h)=∫−ππ​eihλfX​(λ)dλ, then
fY​(λ)=∣A(e−iλ)∣2fX​(λ).​
(53)
To prove existence, the L2 norm of a tail of the filter is at most ∥X0​∥2​ times the sum of the omitted ∣aj​∣, which tends to zero. Hence finite partial sums converge in L2. Expectations and covariances pass to the limit, and the covariance double sum is absolutely convergent because ∣γX​(h)∣≤γX​(0). Its formula depends only on h, proving stationarity. Insert the spectral representation of γX​(h−j+k) and interchange sums and integration; the bound ∑j,k​∣aj​ak​∣FX​([−π,π])<∞ justifies the interchange. The resulting factor is ∑j​aj​e−ijλ∑k​ak​eikλ=∣A(e−iλ)∣2. This proves the spectral statement, including the spectral density transformation under a linear filter without assuming that the input autocovariances are absolutely summable.
For an autoregressive moving-average model, use real polynomials normalized by ϕ0​=θ0​=1 and first work with the reduced noise-driven representation. If ϕ has no zero on the unit circle, the rational function C(z)=θ(z)/ϕ(z) has a Laurent expansion in an annulus containing that circle, with absolutely summable coefficients. Filtering the white noise with it gives a stationary solution. Its time-series spectral density is
fX​(λ)=2πσ2​∣ϕ(e−iλ)∣2∣θ(e−iλ)∣2​,−π≤λ≤π.​
(54)
Conversely, for coprime ϕ,θ, a unit-circle zero of ϕ would make this ratio nonintegrable, since the numerator does not vanish there; thus there is no finite-variance stationary noise-driven solution. A stationary solution can use a bilateral filter when autoregressive roots lie inside the unit circle. The stronger causal condition, meaning dependence only on present and past noise, requires every zero of ϕ strictly outside the unit disk. Common factors must be handled before applying these conditions; a cancelled unit-root factor can also allow additional deterministic stationary components, which are outside the minimal noise-driven convention.
Identifiability means that different admissible parameters cannot produce the same stationary process law. In this zero-mean Gaussian setting, its law is determined by its covariance, equivalently its spectrum. Unrestricted ARMA coefficients are not identifiable from that law: if a moving-average factor has root r inside the disk, then on ∣z∣=1,
∣1−z/r∣2=∣r∣−2∣1−rz∣2.
(55)
Reflecting the root outside and multiplying the innovation variance by ∣r∣−2 preserves the spectrum, and therefore the Gaussian law. Common polynomial factors and arbitrary overall scaling give further redundancy. A regular canonical identifiable parameterization requires constant coefficients one, coprime polynomials of their stated minimal orders, all zeros of both ϕ and θ outside the closed unit disk, and σ2>0.
Here is a proof of canonical identifiability of Gaussian ARMA models. Suppose two such models have equal spectra and write their innovation standard deviations as σ,τ>0. The ratio R(z)=σθ(z)ϕ​(z)/[τθ(z)ϕ(z)] and its reciprocal are analytic on a neighbourhood of the closed unit disk, with modulus one on its boundary. The maximum modulus principle applied to both makes ∣R∣=1 throughout the disk, so R is constant. Its value at zero is the positive number σ/τ, forcing R=1 and σ=τ. Therefore θϕ​=θϕ; coprimality and the normalization at zero force equality of both polynomials. This proves the claimed parameter uniqueness within that canonical class.
In this class, D(z)=ϕ(z)/θ(z) is analytic on a disk of radius greater than one. Its Taylor series coefficients are absolutely summable, so
εt​=D(B)Xt​=j≥0∑​dj​Xt−j​.​
(56)
This is an invertible time-series representation, proved by composition of the stable filters. Causality makes εt​ orthogonal to past observations; invertibility makes past noises recoverable from past observations. Their closed past linear spans agree, so this noise is the linear innovation and the one-sided expansion Xt​=∑j≥0​cj​εt−j​ is the Wold representation. Mere stationarity without the stated canonical restrictions does not imply invertibility of the originally written noise representation.
For this one-sided canonical representation, C(z)=θ(z)/ϕ(z)=∑j≥0​cj​zj. Comparing coefficients gives the ARMA coefficient recursions
∑r=0min(p,k)​ϕr​ck−r​=θk​,cj​=0 (j<0),θk​=0 (k>q).
(57)
Thus for exactly the range asked for,
ck​+ϕ1​ck−1​+⋯+ϕp​ck−p​=0(k>max(p−1,q)).​
(58)
The characteristic rates are reciprocals of the zeros of ϕ. Partial fractions therefore express the tail as sums of Pr​(k)rk, where ∣r∣<1 and the degree of Pr​ is at most one less than the corresponding root multiplicity. Hence the coefficients decay exponentially, with possible polynomial and damped oscillatory factors. If p=0, they instead vanish after q. For a noncausal or noninvertible original specification, the Wold coefficients refer to the canonical spectral factor; using the original driving noise as the Wold innovation without checking these restrictions would be incorrect.
The autocovariance is γ(h)=σ2∑j≥0​cj​cj+∣h∣​. Analyticity of C on a disk of radius R>1 gives ∣cj​∣≤MR−j for a slightly smaller radius than its first pole, and consequently ∣γ(h)∣≤σ2M2R−∣h∣/(1−R−2). Moreover, multiply the ARMA equation at time t by Xt−h​ and take expectations. For h>q, all noises εt−s​ with 0≤s≤q are later than Xt−h​ and orthogonal to it. For h>max(p−1,q) this gives
γ(h)+ϕ1​γ(h−1)+⋯+ϕp​γ(h−p)=0.​
(59)
The covariance tail therefore has the same characteristic exponential rates and multiplicity patterns as the filter coefficients, subject to possible cancellation of amplitudes. Pure moving averages have an exact covariance cutoff.
For the final process, factor the polynomials:
ϕ(z)=1−3z​−92z2​=(1−32z​)(1+3z​),θ(z)=1−32z​.
(60)
The common factor has its zero at 3/2, so it can be cancelled as a stable filter. Explicitly, let Yt​=(1+B/3)Xt​−εt​; the original equation gives Yt​=(2/3)Yt−1​. The second moments of Yt​ are uniformly bounded because those of Xt​ and εt​ are. Iterating gives Yt​=(2/3)mYt−m​, whose L2 norm tends to zero as m→∞, so Yt​=0 in mean square. Thus
Xt​=−31​Xt−1​+εt​=∑j≥0​(−31​)jεt−j​.
(61)
Summing the covariance series gives
γ(h)=89σ2​(−31​)∣h∣,h∈Z.​
(62)
The process is a causal stationary autoregressive process of order one, with alternating-sign geometric correlations, and is invertible. The displayed ARMA(2,1) description is nonminimal because of its common factor; its minimal identifiable class is ARMA(1,0).
