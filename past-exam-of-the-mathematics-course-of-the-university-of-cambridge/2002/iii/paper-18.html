The displayed torus-only Weyl integration formula needs φ to be a class function, meaning φ(xgx−1)=φ(g). This hypothesis is implicit in the usual formula but is not printed here. We prove the general version for a continuous function, then specialize:
∫G​φ(g)dg=∣W∣1​∫T​∫G/T​φ(xtx−1)dμ(xT)∣Δ(t)∣2dt.
(1)
Here dg and dt are probability Haar measures; dμ is the invariant probability measure on the homogeneous space G/T; and W=NG​(T)/T is the finite Weyl group.
We use the following standard maximal-torus facts, as permitted: every element of a connected compact Lie group lies in some maximal torus; all maximal tori are conjugate; two elements of a fixed T are conjugate in G exactly when they are in the same W-orbit; and the complexified Lie algebra decomposes into tC​ and one-dimensional root spaces. Opposite roots of a root system give real two-dimensional planes in t⊥. At an element t for which no root character has value one, the identity component of its centralizer is T.
Choose a positive root system Φ+. Regard each root α as a character α:T→U(1) through the Adjoint representation. A convenient globally defined denominator is
Δ0​(t)=∏α∈Φ+​(1−α(t)−1).
(2)
The usual Weyl denominator additionally has a unit-modulus factor eρ(t), with ρ half the sum of positive roots. Where that half-weight is not a global torus character, it may be interpreted on a covering torus; the squared modulus is nevertheless unambiguous. In either convention,
∣Δ(t)∣2=∣Δ0​(t)∣2=∏α∈Φ+​∣1−α(t)∣2.
(3)
Average an inner product on g over the Adjoint representation to make it invariant. It defines a bi-invariant Riemannian metric on G and the quotient metric on G/T. Consider the conjugation map
q:G/T×T→G,q(xT,t)=xtx−1.
(4)
At (T,t), identify the source tangent directions with X∈t⊥ and Y∈t, and translate the target tangent vector to the identity by t−1. Differentiating gives
dq(T,t)​(X,Y)=(Ad(t−1)−I)X+Y.
(5)
The two summands are orthogonal. On a real root plane, Ad(t−1) is a rotation with complex eigenvalues α(t)−1,α(t). Thus the absolute real Jacobian determinant on this plane is ∣1−α(t)∣2. The torus direction contributes one. Therefore the conjugation Jacobian for a compact Lie group is
Jq​(t)=∣Δ(t)∣2.​
(6)
This is also the Jacobian for the normalized measures: the Riemannian submersion has fibers isometric to T, so vol(G)=vol(G/T)vol(T); the probability-volume normalization factors cancel.
Let Treg​ be the elements for which Jq​=0. The restriction of q is a local diffeomorphism onto the regular elements of G. It is ∣W∣-to-one. Indeed, fixing t0​∈Treg​, any pair with xtx−1=t0​ has xTx−1=T, because conjugation identifies the identity components of the two centralizers. Thus x∈NG​(T), and there is exactly one pair (xT,x−1t0​x) for each coset in NG​(T)/T. This argument does not require the full centralizer of t0​ to be connected.
The nonregular locus has measure zero. In T it is a finite union of sets α(t)=1 of smaller dimension; their conjugation images are images of smaller-dimensional manifolds under q, and hence have zero volume in G. The change-of-variables formula for the regular covering therefore gives
∣W∣∫G​φ(g)dg=∫T​∫G/T​φ(xtx−1)dμ(xT)∣Δ(t)∣2dt.
(7)
For a class function, the inner integral is φ(t), proving the required formula with its correct hypothesis.
Without conjugation invariance, the torus-only expression can be false. For G=SU(2) with diagonal T, take φ(g)=∣g12​∣2. It vanishes on T, whereas its group integral is 1/2: the two squared coordinates of a Haar-distributed unit column have equal means and sum to one. Thus the conjugacy average cannot simply be dropped.

Let V=Cn and m≥0. Identify the symmetric power SymmV with the degree-m part of the symmetric algebra on the basis vectors e1​,…,en​, using formal variables xi​ for these vectors. Its monomial basis is
xa=x1a1​​⋯xnan​​,ai​≥0,∑i​ai​=m.
(8)
The diagonal maximal torus of the unitary group acts on this monomial by the character z1a1​​⋯znan​​. Distinct tuples give distinct characters, so each weight space is one-dimensional.
Suppose M is a nonzero invariant complex subspace. For a nonzero vector w∈M, select a monomial with nonzero coefficient and extract it by torus averaging:
∫T​z1−a1​​⋯zn−an​​t⋅wdt.
(9)
Fourier orthogonality makes this a nonzero scalar multiple of xa. It belongs to M, so M contains a monomial.
Differentiating the unitary group action makes M invariant under u(n) and hence under its complexification gln​(C). The matrix unit Eij​ replaces an occurrence of ej​ by ei​, so its action on the symmetric algebra is
Eij​⋅xa=xi​∂xj​​xa=aj​xa+ei​−ej​.
(10)
Whenever aj​>0, this transfers one unit of degree from coordinate j to coordinate i with a nonzero coefficient. Starting with any monomial, such transfers can produce every other tuple of nonnegative integers summing to m. Thus M contains every monomial and equals SymmV.
Consequently
SymmCn is irreducible for every m≥0.​
(11)
For m=0 this is the one-dimensional trivial representation, and for n=1 every symmetric power is also one-dimensional. This weight-transfer proof of irreducibility of symmetric powers uses neither the Weyl character formula nor the Weyl dimension formula, so no unproved dimension formula enters the argument.

Write the diagonal maximal torus of U(n) as t=diag(z1​,…,zn​), ∣zi​∣=1, and let δ=(n−1,n−2,…,0). Irreducible complex representations are indexed by dominant integer tuples λ1​≥⋯≥λn​, their highest weights. The Weyl character formula in integer-exponent form is
χλ​(t)=det(zin−j​)i,j=1n​det(ziλj​+n−j​)i,j=1n​​.​
(12)
The ratio initially uses distinct zi​, and extends to repeated eigenvalues by continuity. Every unitary matrix is conjugate to such a diagonal matrix, so this determines the character on the entire group. The shift δ differs from the usual Weyl vector ρ=((n−1)/2,(n−3)/2,…,(1−n)/2) by a multiple of (1,…,1); the corresponding determinant factors cancel between numerator and denominator.
We first justify the highest-weight information needed in the proof. Restriction to the torus splits a representation into integer weight spaces. In an irreducible representation choose a lexicographically largest weight λ and a nonzero vector v of that weight. A matrix unit Eij​ with i<j raises the weight by ei​−ej​, so it annihilates v. The character is invariant under permutations of diagonal coordinates, because permutation matrices conjugate the torus. Hence permutations of λ are also weights, and lexical maximality forces λ1​≥⋯≥λn​.
Irreducibility implies that v generates the representation under the complexified Lie algebra. Indeed, a Lie-algebra invariant subspace is preserved by exponentials of skew-Hermitian matrices, which generate U(n). Reorder any product of matrix units into lower-triangular units, diagonal units and upper-triangular units, using
[Eij​,Ekl​]=δjk​Eil​−δli​Ekj​.
(13)
Induction on length and on the number of out-of-order pairs gives the required spanning version of the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem. Upper-triangular units kill v, and diagonal units act by scalars. Every nonempty product of lower-triangular units lowers its weight by a nonzero sum of positive roots. Therefore the top weight space is exactly Cv, and its multiplicity is one. This supplies the leading coefficient without assuming a character formula.
For completeness, every dominant integer tuple occurs. Put dj​=λj​−λj+1​≥0 and form the representation
(det)λn​⊗⨂j=1n−1​(ΛjCn)⊗dj​.
(14)
Its lexicographically highest weight is λ, with one-dimensional highest space, represented by the tensor products of e1​∧⋯∧ej​. A negative determinant exponent is a legitimate one-dimensional unitary-group character. Averaging an inner product over normalized Haar measure gives invariant orthogonal complements, so the tensor representation splits into irreducible summands. One of them has highest weight λ. Uniqueness will also follow from the formula and Schur orthogonality relations.
Define the alternant Aκ​(z)=det(ziκj​​) for a strictly decreasing integer tuple κ. The denominator
Aδ​(z)=∏i<j​(zi​−zj​)
(15)
is the Vandermonde determinant, and its modulus is the denominator modulus in the Weyl integration formula. The Laurent polynomial χλ​Aδ​ is alternating. Any alternating Laurent polynomial decomposes as a finite sum
χλ​Aδ​=∑κ​cκ​Aκ​:
(16)
monomials with repeated exponents have zero coefficient, and the remaining permutation orbits are indexed uniquely by strictly decreasing tuples.
The lexicographically highest monomial in the product is zλ+δ with coefficient one. Indeed, the highest monomial of the character is zλ with coefficient one, and the unique lexical maximum among permutations of δ is δ itself. It follows that cλ+δ​=1.
Fourier orthogonality on the torus, whose probability measure is ∏i​dθi​/(2π), gives alternant orthogonality on a unitary torus:
∫T​Aκ​Aη​​dt=n!δκη​.
(17)
Expand both determinants: an integral survives exactly when the two exponent tuples are identical up to permutation. Strict decreasing order then forces κ=η, and its n! matching terms each contribute one.
Finally, Schur orthogonality relations give ∫U(n)​∣χλ​∣2=1. Applying the allowed Weyl integration formula to this class function gives
1=n!1​∫T​∣χλ​Aδ​∣2dt=∑κ​∣cκ​∣2.
(18)
Since the coefficient cλ+δ​ already equals one, every other coefficient vanishes. Thus χλ​Aδ​=Aλ+δ​, proving the formula. If two irreducible representations had the same highest weight, their characters would now coincide; the Schur orthogonality relations imply that they are isomorphic. Apparent singularities at repeated diagonal eigenvalues are removable because the quotient equals the actual finite Laurent-polynomial character. No dimension formula has been assumed.

Let v1,+​=e1​+ie2​, v1,−​=e1​−ie2​, v2,+​=e3​+ie4​, v2,−​=e3​−ie4​ and v0​=e5​. Direct multiplication by the printed rotation blocks gives their torus eigenvalues
eix1​, e−ix1​, eix2​, e−ix2​, 1.
(19)
Thus the differentiated weights of a representation are ix1​,−ix1​,ix2​,−ix2​,0, with this sign convention fixed by the given matrix blocks.
A wedge of two distinct eigenvectors has the product eigenvalue, so its differentiated weight is the sum. The ten vectors in a basis of the exterior square split into three groups:
vectorsv1,σ​∧v2,τ​vj,σ​∧v0​v1,+​∧v1,−​, v2,+​∧v2,−​​weightsi(σx1​+τx2​), σ,τ=±1iσxj​, j=1,2, σ=±10​number442​
(20)
The eight nonzero weights are distinct, so each has multiplicity one; the zero weight space has dimension two. The multiplicities add to (25​)=10, accounting for the entire representation.

For a real inner product space V=R5, define
Φ(u∧v)(w)=⟨v,w⟩u−⟨u,w⟩v.
(21)
This map takes values in the Special orthogonal Lie algebra: its matrices are skew-symmetric. It sends ei​∧ej​ to Eij​−Eji​, so it is an isomorphism between the two ten-dimensional spaces. For g∈SO(5), invariance of the inner product gives
Φ(gu∧gv)=gΦ(u∧v)g−1.
(22)
This proves the exterior square realization of the orthogonal adjoint representation, including equivariance, rather than merely matching dimensions.
After complexification, part (a) is therefore the weight decomposition of the Adjoint representation. Its zero weight space is the complex centralizer of t. The two zero-weight wedges are nonzero multiples of e1​∧e2​ and e3​∧e4​, which map under Φ to the two infinitesimal rotation generators of T. Hence
Zso(5)​(t)=t.
(23)
If a larger torus contained T, its Lie algebra would commute with t and therefore lie in t. Equality of Lie algebras and connectedness of both tori force equality of the groups. Thus T is a maximal torus.
Let εj​ denote the real coordinate functional xj​. In the real weight convention, with the common factor i removed from the differentiated torus weights, the root system is
Φ={±ε1​,±ε2​,±(ε1​+ε2​),±(ε1​−ε2​)}.​
(24)
These are the roots of a root system of type B2 root system. In the imaginary-valued compact Lie-algebra convention each displayed functional is multiplied by i. Every root space is one-dimensional, as part (a) explicitly shows.

The root hyperplanes are x1​=0, x2​=0, x1​=x2​ and x1​=−x2​. Their reflections are coordinate sign changes, coordinate interchange and a signed interchange. They generate all signed permutations of the two coordinates:
W≅(Z/2Z)2⋊S2​,∣W∣=8.​
(25)
Geometrically this is the dihedral symmetry group of a square, with order eight. It preserves both the four short roots and the four long roots of the B2 root system.
Representatives in SO(5) can be given explicitly. The matrix
D1​=diag(1,−1,1,1,−1)
(26)
has determinant one and conjugates the first rotation block to its inverse, giving (x1​,x2​)↦(−x1​,x2​). The matrix D2​=diag(1,1,1,−1,−1) similarly reverses x2​. Flipping the fifth coordinate compensates for the determinant of the planar reflection.
The permutation matrix
P=​00100​00010​10000​01000​00001​​
(27)
swaps the two coordinate planes. Its permutation is (1 3)(2 4), so its determinant is also one. Conjugation gives (x1​,x2​)↦(x2​,x1​), the reflection in x1​=x2​. These elements normalize T, and P,D1​ generate all eight Weyl actions. For example, PD1​ induces (x1​,x2​)↦(x2​,−x1​), a quarter-turn, and D1​D2​ induces the half-turn. The representatives supply the specified reflections and their typical products inside the actual special orthogonal group.

Choose positive roots
Φ+={ε1​−ε2​,ε2​,ε1​,ε1​+ε2​}.
(28)
The corresponding simple roots are ε1​−ε2​ and ε2​. The vector v1,+​∧v2,+​ has weight λ=ε1​+ε2​. Adding any positive root to this weight produces a weight absent from part (a), so every positive root vector annihilates it. It is consequently a highest-weight vector with highest weight λ.
A representation of a compact Lie group is completely reducible: Haar averaging makes an inner product invariant, and orthogonal complements of invariant subspaces remain invariant. Therefore the exterior square contains an irreducible summand Lλ​ of this highest weight. This can also be seen by projecting the displayed highest-weight vector into irreducible summands; a nonzero projection remains annihilated by all positive root vectors and has the same weight. Its multiplicity is one because the weight space at λ is one-dimensional.
Use the Weyl dimension formula, expressly allowed in this part:
dimLλ​=∏α∈Φ+​⟨ρ,α⟩⟨λ+ρ,α⟩​.
(29)
Here Lλ​ is the irreducible highest-weight representation, Φ+ is the chosen positive root system, ρ is half its sum, and the pairing is the invariant Euclidean inner product on the real weight plane, with ε1​,ε2​ orthonormal. Using coroots instead gives the same ratio because the length factor cancels for each root. In this convention,
ρ=23​ε1​+21​ε2​,λ+ρ=25​ε1​+23​ε2​.
(30)
The four factors, in the listed positive-root order, are respectively
11​,1/23/2​,3/25/2​,24​.
(31)
Their product is 10. Since dimΛ2C5=(25​)=10, this irreducible summand occupies the entire exterior square. Hence
Λ2C5 is irreducible as an SO(5)-representation.​
(32)
The proof uses the dimension formula only where it is allowed, not in the symmetric-power argument of Question 2. Its B2 adjoint representation weight diagram is exactly the eight roots and the double zero weight obtained above.
