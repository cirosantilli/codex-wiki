Because s is arc length, ∣dx/ds∣=1. Thus y=n/α, where n is the unit tangent to the elastic ray. In an isotropic medium this is also the wavefront normal. Consequently y is the vector slowness, of magnitude 1/α; locally y=∇T=k/ω, with T the travel time and k the wavevector.
For depth-dependent wave speed α(z), the ray equation gives dyx​/ds=dyy​/ds=0. The horizontal component of dx/ds therefore has a fixed direction, and the elastic ray lies in the vertical plane determined by that direction and its initial point. A vertical elastic ray is the limiting case. If i is the angle to the vertical, its conserved horizontal slowness gives
α(z)sini​=pflat​.​
(1)
At a horizontal interface, matching the tangential wavevector at fixed frequency gives the same invariant on both sides: this is Snell's law.
For radial wave speed α(r), take the cross product of position with slowness. The ray equations give
dsd​(x×y)=αy×y+x×∇(1/α)=0,
(2)
since the second vector is radial. A nonzero constant L=x×y is perpendicular to every position on the elastic ray, so the ray lies in a fixed plane through the centre. Radial rays obey the same conclusion with a nonunique plane. The spherical elastic ray invariant is therefore
p=∣L∣=α(r)rsini​,​
(3)
where i is measured from the radial direction. Across a spherical interface the radius is common and tangential slowness is continuous, so this form of Snell's law survives an interface discontinuity.
In a homogeneous sphere a P wave follows the straight chord between the two surface points. If their principal angular separation is 0≤Δ≤π, its length is 2Rsin(Δ/2). For constant P wave speed cP​,
TD​(Δ)=cP​2R​sin2Δ​.​
(4)
Now put a=R′ and denote the mantle and core P wave speeds by cm​>cc​. Equal incidence and reflection angles put the core-boundary reflection point on the angular bisector. Each mantle leg has length L given by L2=R2+a2−2Racos(Δ/2). Hence the core-reflected travel time is
TR​(Δ)=cm​2​R2+a2−2Racos(Δ/2)​,0≤Δ≤Δg​=2arccos(a/R).​
(5)
The stated domain is essential. At the reflection point the incoming line approaches from the mantle only if Rcos(Δ/2)≥a. Otherwise it has already crossed the core boundary. The mantle-only direct chord also exists only for Δ≤Δg​, with TD​=2Rsin(Δ/2)/cm​. In this interval,
L2−R2sin2(Δ/2)=[Rcos(Δ/2)−a]2≥0,
(6)
so the reflected arrival is later. Its intercept is 2(R−a)/cm​, whereas the direct intercept is zero. At grazing incidence the branches meet at T=2R2−a2​/cm​, and both have slope dT/dΔ=a/cm​. A continuation of the homogeneous-sphere direct formula beyond this endpoint is not a mantle-only arrival in the modified model.
The core-transmitted elastic rays consist of three straight legs. Their impact distances in mantle and core are bm​=pcm​ and bc​=pcc​, respectively. At core entry,
cm​sinim​​=cc​sinic​​=ap​,siniR​=Rpcm​​,0≤p≤a/cm​.
(7)
Since cc​<cm​, the elastic ray bends towards the normal on entry and away from it on exit. The mantle leg subtends im​−iR​ at the centre, and the core chord subtends π−2ic​. Thus the total angular sweep along the transmitted path is 2(im​−iR​)+π−2ic​. This sweep need not equal the principal separation if it exceeds π; the diagram uses a path with sweep below π.

An elastic deformation is reversible: after removal of the deforming loads the material returns to its reference state, and its instantaneous stress is determined by its current deformation rather than a plastic or viscous history. In the infinitesimal approximation the displacement field ui​ gives the infinitesimal strain tensor eij​=(ui,j​+uj,i​)/2. The Cauchy stress tensor is defined by traction ti​=σij​nj​ on a plane of unit normal n; absence of body couples makes σij​=σji​.
Let E(e) be the recoverable elastic energy per unit mass. The local mechanical power per unit volume is
σij​∂j​u˙i​=σij​e˙ij​,
(8)
because the symmetric Cauchy stress tensor annihilates the antisymmetric part of the velocity gradient. Conservation of energy during the deformation gives ρE˙=σij​e˙ij​. Since E˙=(∂E/∂eij​)e˙ij​ for arbitrary symmetric strain rates,
σij​=ρ∂eij​∂E​.​
(9)
Here the derivative is the gradient on the space of symmetric tensors, with the double-contraction inner product. This convention accounts for the two occurrences of each off-diagonal entry; treating six independent coordinates without their corresponding factors would give an erroneous factor of two in shear components.
Take a stress-free reference state of mass density ρ0​. The Taylor series of the elastic energy starts quadratically:
E(e)=E(0)+2ρ0​1​cijkl​eij​ekl​+O(∣e∣3).
(10)
Using ρ=ρ0​+O(∣e∣) gives σij​=cijkl​ekl​+O(∣e∣2), the relation of linear elasticity. If the reference state has initial stress, the first-order expansion also retains that constant stress. Symmetry of stress and strain gives the minor symmetries cijkl​=cjikl​=cijlk​, while equality of the mixed second derivatives of elastic energy gives the major symmetry cijkl​=cklij​. Thus the elastic stiffness tensor is a symmetric map on a six-dimensional strain space: there are 6⋅7/2=21 independent coefficients.
For twofold elastic symmetry, choose the symmetry axis as x3​. Rotation through π is Q=diag(−1,−1,1), and transforms the infinitesimal strain tensor into QeQT. Components e11​,e22​,e33​,e12​ are even and e23​,e13​ are odd. Invariance of elastic energy excludes every coupling between these parity classes. In the engineering strain ordering (e11​,e22​,e33​,2e23​,2e13​,2e12​), with corresponding stress ordering (σ11​,σ22​,σ33​,σ23​,σ13​,σ12​), the symmetric stiffness matrix has the form
C=​C11​C12​C13​00C16​​C12​C22​C23​00C26​​C13​C23​C33​00C36​​000C44​C45​0​000C45​C55​0​C16​C26​C36​00C66​​​.
(11)
Its symmetric even block has ten independent entries and its symmetric odd block has three. Hence a twofold axis leaves 13 independent coefficients. No invariance under other rotation angles has been assumed.

For a homogeneous lossless elastic solid, insert the plane wave ui​=Re{Uai​ei(k⋅x−ωt)} into the equations of linear elasticity. Put k=κn, ∣n∣=1, and v=ω/κ. The resulting eigenvalue problem is
Γik​(n)ak​=ρv2ai​,Γik​=cijkl​nj​nl​.
(12)
The minor and major symmetries make this acoustic tensor a real symmetric matrix. For a stable material it is positive-definite, so its three positive eigenvalues give three real positive phase velocities and therefore three slownesses sa​=1/va​. Coincident eigenvalues are possible, so there need not be three distinct speeds. For distinct eigenvalues the displacement polarization are orthogonal; in an anisotropic solid they are generally quasi-longitudinal and quasi-transverse rather than exactly longitudinal and transverse. Stability, or at least strong ellipticity in elasticity, is required for the assertion about positive real speeds.
The elastic slowness surface is the locus of the three vectors p=n/va​(n)=k/ω. Its equation is
det[cijkl​pj​pl​−ρδik​]=0.
(13)
For each wavefront normal there are three radial sheets, which may meet at degeneracies. To obtain the energy flux directly, multiply the elastic equation by u˙i​. For
W=21​ρu˙i​u˙i​+21​cijkl​eij​ekl​,
(14)
the symmetry of stress gives ∂t​W=∂j​(σij​u˙i​). Thus the instantaneous elastic-wave energy flux is Fj​=−σij​u˙i​. With a real branch polarization a, harmonic averaging gives
⟨Fj​⟩=21​ω∣U∣2cijkl​kl​ai​ak​=21​ω2∣U∣2cijkl​pl​ai​ak​.
(15)
Now differentiate cijkl​pj​pl​ak​=ρai​ along a regular slowness surface sheet and contract with ai​. The terms containing da cancel by symmetry and the original eigenvalue equation, leaving
2cijkl​ai​ak​pl​dpj​=0.
(16)
Every tangent dp is consequently perpendicular to ⟨F⟩: the energy flux is normal to the slowness surface. At a degenerate point this statement is made on the limiting branches, rather than assuming a unique normal.
For completeness, equality of the mean kinetic and strain energies gives ⟨W⟩=ρω2∣U∣2∣a∣2/2. Differentiating the acoustic tensor equation in the wavevector variables similarly gives
gj​=∂kj​∂ω​=ρω∣a∣2cijkl​kl​ai​ak​​,⟨F⟩=⟨W⟩g.​
(17)
Thus the group velocity is the elastic energy velocity. The homogeneous elastic dispersion relation is degree one in k, so differentiating ω(λk)=λω(k) at λ=1 gives p⋅g=1.
The elastic wave surface is the unit-time locus of these group velocities. From an impulsive point source, the propagating wavefronts at time t are its sheets scaled to x=tg. A particular source may fail to excite a mode in some directions, and its amplitudes require the source polarization and geometrical spreading; the wave surface determines the arrival geometry. It does not specify all near-field motion between the radiative fronts. Geometrically it is the envelope of the planes p⋅x=1: tangent variation gives dp⋅x=0, so x is the appropriately normalized normal to the slowness surface.
In a symmetry-plane cross-section write p=r(θ)n, n=(cosθ,sinθ) and t=(−sinθ,cosθ). The two envelope conditions give
g=rn​−r2r′​t=vn+v′t,g′=(v+v′′)t=r3r2+2r′2−rr′′​t.
(18)
The numerator is the signed curvature numerator of the slowness surface cross-section. At a simple inflection point, h=v+v′′=0 and h′=0. Then g′′=h′t and g′′′=h′′t+2h′t′ are linearly independent. These are precisely the local conditions for an ordinary cusp. The slowness curvature and wave-surface cusps relation explains why a smooth nonconvex slowness surface can generate a cusped wavefront and multiple ray arrivals.
The example below uses a positive orthotropic stiffness in normalized units: ρ=1, C11​=C22​=C33​=4, C44​=C55​=1.2, C66​=1, and all normal off-diagonal stiffnesses zero. In the symmetry plane n3​=0, its two in-plane acoustic tensor eigenvalues are v±2​=(5±5+4cos4θ​)/2, and its out-of-plane shear speed is 1.2​. The in-plane shear sheet is nonconvex and produces the marked cusps; the construction uses an actual stable elastic tensor.

Let Ω be the solid exterior to the cavity, and choose n outward from the solid, hence into the cavity on its boundary S. Subtraction of the incident solution from the total solution gives the scattered displacement field equations
ρu¨is​=∂j​σijs​,σijs​=cijkl​ekls​,ekls​=21​(uk,ls​+ul,ks​),x∈Ω.
(19)
The traction-free cavity condition for the total field gives σijs​nj​=−σij0​nj​ on S. The scattered field has zero initial displacement and initial velocity before the pulse reaches the cavity and satisfies an outgoing radiation condition, excluding an additional incoming elastic wave. Equivalently one seeks the causal response to this prescribed boundary traction. Although the incident plane pulse extends across an infinite plane, the causal scattered field from the bounded cavity has finite propagation distance at each finite time.
To prove energy uniqueness for traction-driven elasticity, take the difference w of two solutions with the same initial conditions, body force, boundary traction and outgoing condition. It satisfies
ρw¨i​=∂j​σij​(w),σij​(w)nj​=0 on S,w(⋅,t0​)=w˙(⋅,t0​)=0.
(20)
For a large enclosing ball BL​, multiply by w˙i​ and integrate over Ω∩BL​. Integration by parts gives
∫Ω∩BL​​ρw¨i​w˙i​dV=∫∂(Ω∩BL​)​w˙i​σij​(w)nj​dS−∫Ω∩BL​​σij​(w)∂j​w˙i​dV.
(21)
Symmetry of stress replaces the last derivative by e˙ij​(w), and major symmetry of the elastic stiffness tensor makes that integral the derivative of the strain energy. Therefore
dtd​21​∫Ω∩BL​​[ρ∣w˙∣2+cijkl​eij​(w)ekl​(w)]dV=∫∂(Ω∩BL​)​w˙i​σij​(w)nj​dS.

The cavity contribution is zero. On any fixed finite time interval choose L beyond the causal support of the scattered difference; the outer contribution is then zero as well. This gives conservation of its nonnegative elastic energy. Positive mass density and positive strain energy imply that zero initial energy forces w˙=0 everywhere; the zero initial displacement field then gives w=0. The same argument works for finite-energy outgoing solutions by taking the limit of enclosing surfaces and excluding incoming energy. Hence the causal scattered field us is unique. Specifying the incident field and cavity traction alone without the initial and outgoing conditions would not establish uniqueness.

Here the elastic stiffness tensor has its minor and major symmetries, and the physical body force per unit volume is qi​=ρfi​. The retarded elastodynamic Green tensor Gi j​(x,ξ,t) is the displacement in direction i due to a unit physical force impulse in direction j at ξ:
Lik​Gk j​=δi j​δ(x−ξ)δ(t),Gi j​=0(t<0).

It satisfies the actual Earth's homogeneous boundary conditions, including zero traction on the free surface and continuity of displacement and traction at bonded internal interfaces. Integration through t=0 gives ρ(x)∂t​Gi j​(0+)=δi j​δ(x−ξ), with G(0+)=0. No spherical symmetry is required.
For zero initial conditions, form the convolution
ui​(x,t)=∫0t​dτ∫V​Gi j​(x,ξ,t−τ)ρ(ξ)fj​(ξ,τ)dVξ​.​

Applying L to this expression uses the defining Dirac delta function to give ρ(x)fi​(x,t). Causality gives zero initial conditions, and each Green column supplies the required boundary conditions. The positive-energy uniqueness argument then identifies this expression with the physical response. The source factor is ρ(ξ) because f is force per unit mass, whereas the Green tensor was normalized to unit force.
A force dipole is a limiting pair of opposite point forces separated by a small vector. Put +Fj​ at ξ+εd/2 and −Fj​ at ξ−εd/2, and hold Djk​=εFj​dk​ fixed as ε→0. Expansion of the two Dirac delta functions gives

For the surface source choose a normal n from the minus side to the plus side and define
bi​=[ui​]=ui+​−ui−​,[ti​]=(σij+​−σij−​)nj​.
(27)

If the elastic coefficients are continuous through the surface, the distributional gradient of a piecewise smooth displacement is ∂j​ui​=(∂j​ui​)reg​+bi​nj​δΣ​. Its stress therefore has a singular part cijkl​bk​nl​δΣ​. Taking the divergence includes the regular stress jump, so a field satisfying the source-free equations on both sides has equivalent source

Integration by parts cancels the two temporal terms, with zero endpoint terms; the causal supports subsequently reduce the representation to 0≤τ≤t. The Green impulse contributes −ua​(x,t) on the left. The two outward face normals are n and −n, so their sum on the right is −[ui​]ti​(v)+vi​[ti​]. On a bonded material interface the Green displacement and common Green traction are continuous: [ui​]ti​(v) is then the unambiguous version of the dipole term, evaluated with the matching one-sided coefficients. External free-surface terms vanish because both tractions vanish.
