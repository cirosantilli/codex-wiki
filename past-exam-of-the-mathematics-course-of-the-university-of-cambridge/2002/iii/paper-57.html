Assume well-mixed populations, exponential prey growth without density regulation, predator mortality without food, and bilinear encounters. The Lotka-Volterra predator-prey model is
X˙=X(a−bY),Y˙=Y(cX−d),a,b,c,d>0.
(1)
Its coexistence equilibrium is X∗​=d/c, Y∗​=a/b. The Jacobian matrix there has eigenvalues ±iad​, so it is a center equilibrium rather than an attracting equilibrium. More globally,
H(X,Y)=cX−dlogX+bY−alogY
(2)
is a first integral: substituting the two equations into H˙ gives zero. It has a strict minimum at coexistence, diverges at the boundary and at infinity, and its other positive-quadrant level sets are closed periodic orbits. Small oscillations have frequency ω=ad​. In scaled variables U=d​(X−X∗​)/X∗​ and V=a​(Y−Y∗​)/Y∗​, the linearization is U˙=−ωV, V˙=ωU.
Model an annual fixed quota as a pulse removing h>0 prey, X+=X−−h, Y+=Y−, with h<X−. Between pulses the original equations apply. In logarithmic coordinates the flow has zero divergence, but the pulse has determinant X−/(X−−h)>1. Therefore a periodic annually harvested trajectory has return map determinant greater than one, and cannot have both multipliers inside the unit circle. It is unstable, even when a leading rotation-plus-translation approximation looks neutral. Near a nonresonant small-quota forced orbit this expansion destabilizes the original center equilibrium; resonant pulse timing can add secular effects. Quotas larger than the remaining prey are not biologically realizable and require stopping the model. This is fixed-quota harvesting of Lotka-Volterra populations.
The common continuous approximation to an annual quota gives X˙=X(a−bY)−h. Its positive equilibrium is X∗​=d/c, Yh​=(a−h/X∗​)/b, requiring h<aX∗​. Its Jacobian matrix has trace h/X∗​>0 and determinant bcX∗​Yh​>0, so this approximation also destabilizes coexistence. At small h it is an unstable focus. A quota is a larger per-capita mortality when prey are scarce, which is the adverse feedback responsible for the result.
For abundance-triggered pulses, retain the same fixed prey removal and specify the crossing direction. The exact event-triggered harvesting by conserved energy calculation is
ΔH=−ch−dlog(1−h/X).
(3)
If prey abundance triggers the event at X=Xc​, this increment is the same at every event. A sufficiently high prey threshold with a modest quota gives ΔH<0: the orbit's oscillation energy decreases until it no longer crosses the threshold, after which harvesting stops. If Xc​≤X∗​, then ΔH>0 and harvesting increases oscillations. At the balanced threshold satisfying ch=dlog[Xc​/(Xc​−h)], energy is unchanged and the ideal orbit is neutral. Thus the useful rule is harvesting at high prey abundance, not simply using any fixed threshold; the equality case supplies no asymptotic restoration.
A predator threshold Y=Yc​ does not hold prey abundance fixed. On a rising-predator crossing, X>X∗​, so a sufficiently small harvest initially lowers H; on a falling-predator crossing X<X∗​, it raises H. Nevertheless the rising-predator rule is not a robust stabilizer in this conservative model. Its balanced return orbit has preharvest prey abundance
Xh​=1−e−h/X∗​h​,Xh​−h<X∗​<Xh​,
(4)
obtained by equating cX−dlogX before and after the pulse. On the return section the derivative is
dXdXnext​​​Xh​​=c−d/Xh​c−d/(Xh​−h)​=−z−1+e−zez−1−z​,z=h/X∗​.
(5)
Its magnitude exceeds one because ez−e−z>2z for z>0. The balanced predator-triggered cycle is consequently unstable. At leading small-oscillation order its multiplier is −1, explaining a marginal alternation rather than stable damping. If the trigger is crossed in both directions, or a variable rather than fixed quota is used, a different return rule results and must be analyzed separately.
For conservation, the ideal model shows why fixed removals can be dangerous and why prey-based high-abundance feedback can be safer than a calendar quota or an unspecified predator threshold. These conclusions depend on the chosen pulse rule and on the absence of density dependence, refuges and stochasticity. They do not justify a universal numerical safe quota. A harvest policy needs a stopping threshold and the correct phase-dependent feedback, not merely a small nominal annual catch.

Use a basal resource X1​, herbivore X2​ and predator X3​, with positive encounter, conversion and mortality parameters:
X˙1​=X1​(r−a1​X2​),X˙2​=X2​(b1​X1​−d2​−a2​X3​),X˙3​=X3​(b2​X2​−d3​).
(6)
Only the resource has intrinsic positive growth; the consumers die without food. There is no resource carrying capacity, immigration or feeding on nonadjacent levels. These assumptions define the simplest food chain with Lotka-Volterra equations.
A positive equilibrium would require both X2​=r/a1​ and X2​=d3​/b2​. Hence three-level coexistence is possible only on the special balance κ=b2​r/a1​−d3​=0. The exact identity
X1b2​​X3a1​​=Cexp[(b2​r−a1​d3​)t]​
(7)
follows by combining b2​dlogX1​/dt and a1​dlogX3​/dt. It gives a useful classification rather than incorrectly postulating a generic coexistence fixed point.
If κ<0, the top predator cannot invade the underlying resource-herbivore cycle: its average per-capita growth there is κ. This extinction statement can be strengthened. The boundary equilibrium has X1∗​=d2​/b1​, X2∗​=r/a1​, X3∗​=0. With weights w1​=1, w2​=a1​/b1​, w3​=a1​a2​/(b1​b2​), define
V=∑i=12​wi​[Xi​−Xi∗​−Xi∗​log(Xi​/Xi∗​)]+w3​X3​.
(8)
Direct cancellation of paired trophic terms gives V˙=w3​κX3​<0. Its level sets bound the first two species above and away from zero and bound X3​. Integration and uniform continuity imply X3​→0; the limiting dynamics are the usual resource-herbivore closed orbits, not generally an attracting equilibrium.
If κ=0, there is a line of positive equilibria: X2​=r/a1​, b1​X1​−a2​X3​=d2​. Choosing any positive point on that line gives a conserved coercive version of V with all three relative-entropy terms. Together with the product integral, it confines generic trajectories to closed curves, with neutral oscillations around the equilibrium line. All three can persist, but only under this finely balanced parameter condition.
If κ>0, the product identity grows without bound, so at least one of X1​,X3​ must become unbounded in the unregulated model. There is no bounded persistent three-species attractor. Finite-time blow-up is excluded by a weighted total-abundance bound whose encounter terms cancel and whose derivative is at most a constant times that abundance. The unbounded long-term behavior signals the missing biological resource regulation. The simplest odd chain does not generically permit bounded full coexistence; adding resource self-limitation changes this conclusion.

Assume the equilibrium in part (a) is strictly positive and start with all four species positive. Choose weights recursively by wi+1​=wi​ai​/bi​ and define
V=∑i=14​wi​[Xi​−Xi∗​−Xi∗​log(Xi​/Xi∗​)].
(11)
The weighted interaction matrix is skew-symmetric matrix: for every adjacent pair wi​ai​=wi+1​bi​. On differentiating V, the equilibrium removes the constant growth terms, while paired products cancel. Thus V˙=0. Each summand is nonnegative and diverges as its positive argument approaches zero or infinity. A fixed level of V therefore bounds every abundance above and away from zero. This proves genuine persistence of all four species, although their dynamics need not converge to a fixed point.
Integrate each per-capita equation over [0,T] and divide by T. Since logXi​(T) is bounded, the left side tends to zero. Writing Xi​(T)=T−1∫0T​Xi​(t)dt, the limiting balances give
T→∞lim​Xi​(T)=Xi∗​(i=1,2,3,4),​
(12)
with the explicit abundances listed in part (a). The limit exists because the invertible four-level interaction matrix solves the averaged linear equations, whose residuals tend to zero. Periodicity or an ergodic assumption is not needed.
The same proof works for any even nearest-neighbor chain with a feasible positive equilibrium. It does not make all even-chain parameters feasible. Odd chains generically lack the required positive balance, and in their exceptional balanced cases the averaged equations do not uniquely fix every abundance. Resource density dependence and more general feeding interactions alter both the conservation law and the simple parity classification. Coexistence and the stated mean abundances hold under positivity of the equilibrium, not as an unconditional rule for every food chain.

The basic reproduction number is the expected value of secondary infections caused by one typical infectious individual introduced into an otherwise susceptible population. For a heterogeneous model it is the dominant eigenvalue of the next-generation operator, so the appropriate infected-type distribution is part of “typical.” A small introduction grows when R0​>1 and decays when R0​<1, under the usual linear invasion assumptions.
The asymptotic susceptible level depends on the model. In a homogeneous SIS model with transmission rate β and recovery rate γ, the positive endemic equilibrium has S∗​=1/R0​ when R0​=β/γ>1. In a closed SIR model, however, the final size relation for an epidemic is
log(S∞​/S0​)=−R0​(R∞​−R0recovered​).
(27)
For an infinitesimal seed in a wholly susceptible population this becomes −logS∞​=R0​(1−S∞​). The epidemic peak occurs at S=1/R0​, and a nontrivial outbreak leaves S∞​<1/R0​. Thus 1/R0​ is an endemic or instantaneous invasion threshold, not a universal final susceptible fraction.
For cooperative infections, let s,x,y,z be the fractions carrying neither infection, only A, only B, and both, with sum one. Let λA​=βA​(x+z), λB​=βB​(y+z), assume each infection independently recovers at rate γ, and multiply susceptibility to the second infection by q>1. One suitable pair of SIS models is
x˙y˙​z˙s˙​=λA​s−qλB​x−γx+γz,=λB​s−qλA​y−γy+γz,=qλB​x+qλA​y−2γz,=−(λA​+λB​)s+γ(x+y).​
(28)
Recovery of one infection from z leads to the other single-infection class, not directly to s. The probability simplex is invariant. At the disease-free equilibrium the eigenvalues are βA​−γ, βB​−γ and −2γ. Therefore failure of both isolated and low-density invasion requires RA​=βA​/γ<1, RB​=βB​/γ<1. Enhancement of secondary susceptibility does not alter these linear thresholds because coinfection is second order at introduction.
For completeness, a joint positive equilibrium must have
x=qRB−1​−s​,y=qRA−1​−s​,z=1−s−x−y,
(29)
and
(q−1)s2−qs+RA​RB​1​=0.
(30)
To obtain these, divide the equilibrium equations for total A and total B prevalence by their positive prevalences, yielding s+qy=RA−1​ and s+qx=RB−1​. Substitution into the s balance gives the quadratic. Its discriminant must be nonnegative and the resulting x,y,z positive. These conditions test equilibrium feasibility for unequal rates; stability must also be checked rather than inferred from the mere presence of a root.
A fully explicit Allee effect results in the symmetric permitted case βA​=βB​=β, R=β/γ. The joint equilibria have
s±​=2(q−1)q±q2−4(q−1)/R2​​,x=y=qR−1−s±​​.
(31)
Two feasible positive equilibria coexist with the stable disease-free state exactly in the strict range
q>2,q2q−1​​<R<1.​
(32)
Indeed at the fold the two roots coalesce, and at R=1 the smaller-prevalence branch reaches the disease-free boundary. For q≤2, roots at R<1 do not give positive singly infected fractions. Strict inequalities exclude these marginal endpoints.
Here is the stability check underlying the cooperative SIS coinfection threshold. On the symmetric subspace put v=x+z=y+z and c=z. Then
v˙=γv{R[1+(q−2)v−(q−1)c]−1},c˙=2γ[qRv(v−c)−c].
(33)
At a positive equilibrium c=qRv2/(1+qRv). Its two-dimensional Jacobian matrix has
trJ=−γ[Rv(q+2)+2],detJ=1+qRv2γ2Rv​[(1+qRv)2−(q−1)].
(34)
The higher-prevalence branch has positive determinant and negative trace, while the lower-prevalence branch is a saddle equilibrium. The remaining antisymmetric mode, from x−y, has eigenvalue −qβv<0. Thus the smaller susceptible root s−​ is a stable joint endemic equilibrium, separated from the stable zero state by a finite seeding threshold. Neither infection can persist alone in this range. Strong cooperation can sustain both infections after a sufficiently large joint introduction even though neither invades from rarity. The numerical condition belongs to the specified secondary-susceptibility model; alternative cooperation mechanisms need their own threshold calculation.

Let a,b,e be fractions of patches occupied by the two competitors and empty, with a+b+e=1. Assume well-mixed propagule dispersal, equal independent fire extinction rate m>0, colonization constants cA​>cB​>0, and net replacement advantage q=kB​−kA​>0 for the poorer colonizer. The Levins metapopulation model with replacement is
a˙=a(cA​e−m−qb),b˙=b(cB​e−m+qa),e˙=m(a+b)−e(cA​a+cB​b).
(35)
Replacement fluxes kB​ab and kA​ab cancel in the total occupancy equation. At every face the outward flux is zero or points into the patch probability simplex, so these equations preserve valid fractions. The constants include both propagule arrival and establishment probability; the model does not require unequal fire susceptibility.
Put Δc=cA​−cB​. Solving the two occupied-patch balances and their sum constraint gives
e∗​=q+Δcq​,a∗​=qm​−q+ΔccB​​,b∗​=q+ΔccA​​−qm​.​
(36)
Thus strict coexistence is equivalent to
q+ΔcqcB​​<m<q+ΔcqcA​​.​
(37)
Too little disturbance lets the better competitor exclude the colonizer; too much disturbance favors colonization or destroys occupancy. A positive equilibrium automatically has m<cA​. If the poorer colonizer cannot survive alone, coexistence can nevertheless be possible because it repeatedly replaces established patches of the better colonizer.
These bounds also have an invasion interpretation. The A-only equilibrium is a=1−m/cA​ when cA​>m; the growth rate of rare B there is q−m(q+Δc)/cA​, positive at the upper-bound condition above. At a feasible B-only equilibrium the rare-A growth is −q+m(q+Δc)/cB​, giving the lower bound. At coexistence the occupied-patch Jacobian matrix has trace −cA​a∗​−cB​b∗​<0 and determinant a∗​b∗​q(q+Δc)>0, proving local asymptotic stability. The Bendixson-Dulac criterion with multiplier 1/(ab) gives divergence −cA​/b−cB​/a<0 in the interior, excluding periodic competition cycles.
To specify selection, a monotone tradeoff alone is insufficient: one must say how two different trait values determine replacement. A natural model uses a trait x with increasing competitive ability B(x) and decreasing colonization c(x), and net replacement B(y)−B(x) by a mutant y against a resident x. The resident metapopulation occupies 1−m/c(x), requiring c(x)>m. The rare-mutant per-capita growth, or invasion fitness, is
f(y,x)=m[c(x)c(y)​−1]+[B(y)−B(x)][1−c(x)m​].
(38)
It is zero at y=x. Differentiating with respect to the mutant trait gives the selective pressure
g(x)=∂y​f(x,x)=c(x)mc′(x)​+[1−c(x)m​]B′(x).​
(39)
Higher colonization is valuable in empty habitat; higher competitive ability is valuable where the resident already occupies patches. This is colonization-competition tradeoff invasion fitness.
At an interior candidate for a local evolutionarily stable strategy, g(x∗​)=0. A strict, nondegenerate local evolutionarily stable strategy follows if
∂yy​f(x∗​,x∗​)=c(x∗​)mc′′(x∗​)​+[1−c(x∗​)m​]B′′(x∗​)<0,
(40)
since nearby distinct mutants then have negative invasion fitness. For a strict global evolutionarily stable strategy, every feasible distinct mutant must satisfy
mc(y)+[c(x∗​)−m]B(y)<mc(x∗​)+[c(x∗​)−m]B(x∗​)(y=x∗​).​
(41)
This is the supporting-line condition on the tradeoff curve. At a trait boundary replace the vanishing-gradient condition by the appropriate one-sided inequality. Equality against another mutant, or zero local curvature, needs the second condition of evolutionary stability or higher-order terms; the strict tests above are sufficient and are the usual nondegenerate conditions, not a claim that every tie is unstable.
For evolutionary approach to a singular point, the selection gradient must also point toward it, locally g′(x∗​)<0. This is logically distinct from testing mutant fitness. In this particular additive-ability model with c′<0<B′, negative mutant curvature implies negative g′ because
g′(x)=∂yy​f(x,x)+c(x)2mc′(x)[B′(x)−c′(x)]​.
(42)
If replacement instead has a general kernel κ(y,x), replace B(y)−B(x) in f by that kernel and differentiate it; monotonicity does not justify importing the displayed additive formula without that modeling assumption. The tradeoff shape, disturbance rate and replacement law together determine selection and local or global uninvadability.

Take “cousins” to mean first cousins with unrelated, outbred founders and unrelated spouses in the intervening generation. Interpret a homozygous chromosome as two copies identical by descent, and first use an idealization of independent, nonrecombining chromosome blocks. Ordinary sequence homozygosity is a different quantity: unrelated copies can have the same allele, so its probability also requires allele frequencies. Similarly, identity of an entire recombining chromosome requires a genetic map, not just a pedigree.
At a single autosomal locus, the probability of autozygosity in their child is the parents' kinship coefficient. Each of the two common grandparents contributes a path of length two from each parent, with probability (1/2)2+2+1. Thus the first-cousin inbreeding coefficient is
F=2(1/2)5=1/16.
(43)
If there are L independent autosomal blocks, the probability that none is autozygous is (1−F)L. Hence the usual 23-block idealization gives
Pr(at least one autozygous block)=1−(15/16)23.​
(44)
The exponent describes the stated independent-block approximation; it is not a claim that all 23 human chromosome pairs inherit like autosomes.
Humans have 22 autosomal pairs. A son has one X and one Y and cannot have two homologous X copies. A daughter can have an autozygous X, with probability FX​ determined by the sex-linked cousin kinship calculation in part (b). Thus a sex-aware version is
Pr(at least one autozygous paired block)=1−(15/16)22(1−pD​FX​),​
(45)
where pD​ is the probability of a daughter, or is one or zero for a specified daughter's or son's sex. The independent assortment approximation applies between the autosomes and the X. Opposite-sex parentage alone does not determine FX​: the parents may be cousins through their mothers, their fathers or one of each. Without these pedigree and chromosome-block assumptions, the wording does not specify a unique probability of an entire homozygous human chromosome.

Use the conventional additive pedigree relatedness rUV​=2ϕUV​, where ϕUV​ is the kinship coefficient, the probability that randomly selected gene copies from U and V are identical by descent. For noninbred individuals this convention gives ordinary full siblings relatedness 1/2, as required. It is important that this is not the probability of sharing at least one copy: that probability is 3/4 for ordinary full siblings.
Let the parents be M,F. They are otherwise outbred first cousins, so ϕMF​=1/16 and ϕMM​=ϕFF​=1/2. For two children C,D, independent Mendelian segregation gives
ϕCD​=41​(ϕMM​+ϕMF​+ϕFM​+ϕFF​)=329​.
(46)
Therefore the autosomal additive relatedness is
rCD​=9/16.​
(47)
The additional 1/16 beyond 1/2 comes from maternal-paternal cross-origin matches. Each child has inbreeding coefficient 1/16. The additive coefficient is a sum of descent probabilities rather than a literal bounded probability in all inbred pedigrees; a relationship normalized to self-relatedness would be rCD​/(1+F)=9/17. Likewise, identity-by-descent sharing of inbred full siblings gives a different matched-copy statistic. The exam's calibration to 1/2 supports the additive convention, but the quoted probability wording needs this qualification.
For sex chromosomes the route of cousinship matters, not merely the fact that the parents have opposite sex. Let PM​ be the mother-cousin's parent in the shared sibling pair, and PF​ the father-cousin's parent in that pair. Let FX​ be the probability that the mother's randomly transmitted X matches the father's single X by descent. With no founder inbreeding, the possibilities are
PM​malefemalemalefemale​PF​malemalefemalefemale​FX​001/83/16​​
(48)
In the first two rows the father-cousin is related through his father, whose X he does not inherit. In the third row his mother is a sister of the mother-cousin's father: the relevant brother and sister match their maternal X copies with probability 1/4, and the mother-cousin transmits her paternal X with probability 1/2, giving 1/8. In the last row the two sisters' random X gametes match with probability 3/8: their common father's X contributes 1/4, and their common mother's two X copies contribute 1/8. Transmission by the mother-cousin halves this to 3/16. This derives the sex-linked cousin kinship values.
For an unweighted comparison by chromosome pair, two daughters have sex-pair additive relatedness 3/4+FX​: their paternal X always matches, maternal X matches with probability 1/2, and the two cross-origin terms each contribute FX​. Two sons have mean sex-pair relatedness 3/4, from an X match of probability 1/2 and a common paternal Y with probability one. A daughter-son pair has sex-pair value 1/4+FX​/2, with the Y contribution zero. These calculations treat chromosomes as unrecombined blocks and weight X and Y equally; chromosome-length-weighted or locus-specific coefficients are different.

The table and formula give it once the missing pedigree and offspring-sex information is supplied.

Assume a closed population with even size N, unrelated founders, monogamous mating, nonoverlapping generations and exactly two offspring per couple. Sex labels are exchangeable for the autosomal calculation, with enough individuals of each sex to form the stipulated pairs. A randomly chosen distinct offspring pair are siblings with probability 1/(N−1): among the (2N​) pairs, exactly N/2 are within-family pairs. This fixed-family-size fact, rather than independent sampling of all parents, determines the recurrence.
First give the elementary chromosome-sharing idealization consistent with taking self-relatedness as one and ordinary sibling relatedness as 1/2. In this model each individual carries one ancestral label, passed through one randomly chosen parent. Let rG​ be mean sharing between distinct individuals, and let mG​ be the mean sharing between mates. Siblings share their parental label with probability (1+mG​)/2. The average relatedness between parents from different couples is
uG+1​=N−2(N−1)rG​−mG​​.
(50)
To see the latter count, the ordered pairs from different couples are all ordered distinct pairs minus the N ordered mating pairs. Each pair of parents from different couples contributes equally to the four possible cross-family offspring relationships. Combining sibling and nonsibling offspring gives
rG+1​=rG​+2(N−1)1−mG​​.​
(51)
With random mating, mG​=rG​ in expectation, so r0​=0 gives
rG​=1−(1−2(N−1)1​)G.​
(52)
The mean sharing tends to one, on a generation scale of order 2(N−1). It is not linear indefinitely: repeated ancestry saturates the probability. This is an exact result for the stated one-label approximation, not an exact diploid formula after substantial inbreeding has developed.
Now prohibit full-sibling matings, with N≥4 and representative sampling of allowed nonsibling mating pairs. The following closed recurrences assume that opposite-sex mating pairs have the same mean autosomal sharing as all nonsibling pairs; a sex-specific or assortative scheme requires separate mating means. In the exchangeable one-label model write uG​ for mean relatedness of a nonsibling pair, so mG​=uG​. The two required recurrences are
rG+1​=rG​+2(N−1)1−uG​​,uG+1​=N−2(N−1)rG​−uG​​,r0​=u0​=0.​
(53)
The second relation includes sharing through grandparents and more distant ancestors; setting uG​=0 forever would be wrong. For vG​=1−rG​, wG​=1−uG​, the linear recurrence matrix is
(vG+1​wG+1​​)=(1(N−1)/(N−2)​−1/[2(N−1)]−1/(N−2)​)(vG​wG​​).
(54)
Its eigenvalues are
λ±​=2(N−2)N−3±N2−4N+5​​,
(55)
both of magnitude less than one for N≥4. Thus rG​→1 even when siblings never mate. Explicitly, 1−rG​=Aλ+G​+(1−A)λ−G​, where A=[1−1/(2(N−1))−λ−​]/(λ+​−λ−​). Sibling avoidance prevents immediate close-relative matings, but it does not make the remaining population unrelated. In a fixed-two-offspring model it need not reduce average pairwise sharing at every generation: a smaller mating mean actually increases the displayed increment in rG​.
For a fully diploid formulation, self-kinship also changes with inbreeding and cannot remain artificially fixed. Let ΦG​ be mean kinship coefficient between distinct individuals, FG​ mean inbreeding coefficient, and Φm,G​ mean kinship of mates. A sibling pair has kinship (1+FG​)/4+Φm,G​/2, because parental self-kinship is (1+FG​)/2. Repeating the pair count yields finite-population chromosomal coancestry:
ΦG+1​=ΦG​+4(N−1)1+FG​−2Φm,G​​,FG+1​=Φm,G​.​
(56)
Initially Φ0​=F0​=0. For random mating take Φm,G​=ΦG​. An explicit solution is the affine matrix recurrence
(1−ΦG​1−FG​​)=(1−1/[2(N−1)]1​1/[4(N−1)]0​)G(11​).
(57)
Its characteristic polynomial is λ2−[1−1/(2(N−1))]λ−1/[4(N−1)]; its two roots have magnitude less than one. Hence ΦG​,FG​→1. The normalized relationship 2ΦG​/(1+FG​) tends to one, while unnormalized additive relationship 2ΦG​ tends to two, demonstrating why that coefficient cannot always be read literally as a probability.
With sibling avoidance replace Φm,G​ by the nonsibling mean UG​ and add
UG+1​=N−2(N−1)ΦG​−UG​​,U0​=0.
(58)
These equations compute every generation's kinship and inbreeding, including delayed consanguinity through cousins. Under random allowed mating in a connected closed population, backward ancestral gene-copy lineages have a positive chance to meet in each sufficiently long block of generations; repeated blocks force eventual coalescence. Thus sibling avoidance alone does not prevent long-term identity by descent and genetic drift. Maintaining distinct isolated breeding groups, introducing immigrants, or changing offspring-number variance gives a different model. The elementary exponential formula answers the fixed-self-sharing approximation; the diploid recurrences give the consistent inbreeding-aware answer.
