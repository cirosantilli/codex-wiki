Choose a fixed prime p≥k, and put s=2p−2. We first derive the polynomial step: a graph H on N vertices and M>(p−1)N edges, with maximum degree at most 2p−1, contains a nonempty p-regular subgraph. Use a Boolean variable xe​ for each edge and work over Fp​. Define
R(x)=∏v∈V(H)​[1−(∑e∋v​xe​)p−1]−∏e∈E(H)​(1−xe​).
(28)
The first product has degree at most (p−1)N<M, while the full squarefree monomial ∏e​xe​ has coefficient (−1)M+1=0 in the second term with its minus sign. The Combinatorial Nullstellensatz, with every grid {0,1}, gives a nonzero value of R. Since R(0)=0, this is a nonempty edge selection. The second product is then zero, so the first is nonzero. Fermat's little theorem makes each selected vertex degree zero modulo p. The maximum-degree bound implies that every positive selected degree is precisely p. This proves the prime-regular subgraph from Boolean polynomial constraints.
We next turn density into an almost-regular bipartite subgraph, keeping the logarithmic loss explicit. Let the original graph have n≥2 vertices and m>0 edges. Some bipartition retains at least m/2 edges: choose the sides independently with probability 1/2 and average the number crossing. Its average degree is at least m/n. Repeatedly delete a vertex of degree at most half the current average degree. Such a deletion cannot lower that average, since
N−12(e−d(v))​≥N2e​⟺d(v)≤e/N.
(29)
The process cannot delete the last positive-density graph, because the maintained average is positive. At termination the minimum degree is an integer σ>m/(2n).
Choose the larger side A of this remaining bipartite graph, with opposite side B. Keep exactly σ edges at each vertex of A; choices at different vertices of A do not conflict. Remove any now-isolated vertices of B. Thus ∣A∣≥∣B∣, and every vertex of A has degree σ.
Such a half-regular graph contains a half-regular restriction with a perfect matching. Choose a minimal nonempty X⊆A satisfying ∣N(X)∣≤∣X∣, which exists because the whole side A satisfies this inequality. In fact equality holds: if it were strict, deleting a vertex of X would retain the inequality, contradicting minimality; the singleton case has at least one neighbor. Every proper subset Y⊂X satisfies ∣N(Y)∣≥∣Y∣. Hall's marriage theorem therefore supplies a perfect matching on X∪N(X). All σ edges incident with each vertex of X remain inside that restriction.
Delete this perfect matching and repeat the restriction argument. The left degrees decrease by one at each step. We obtain σ edge-disjoint perfect matchings M0​,…,Mσ−1​ on nested nonempty vertex sets, with sizes
n=N−1​≥N0​≥N1​≥⋯≥Nσ−1​≥2.
(30)
For any i with i+s≤σ−1, the union of s+1=2p−1 consecutive matchings in a graph has maximum degree at most 2p−1 and at least (s+1)Ni+s​/2 edges on Ni​ vertices. If
Ni+s​>s+1s​Ni​,
(31)
it has more than (p−1)Ni​ edges, so the polynomial step yields a p-regular bipartite subgraph. Every regular bipartite graph decomposes into perfect matchings: for a set in one part, counting incident edges gives Hall's inequality, so a perfect matching exists; remove it and repeat. Choosing k of these p matchings in a graph gives a nonempty k-regular subgraph. This is the nested matching criterion for a regular subgraph.
If no k-regular subgraph exists, every displayed size ratio must therefore satisfy the reverse inequality. Iterating at indices 0,s,2s,… gives, with r=⌊(σ−1)/s⌋,
2≤Nrs​≤n(s+1s​)r.
(32)
Consequently r≤logn/log((s+1)/s) and
σ≤1+s(1+log((s+1)/s)logn​).
(33)
Combining with σ>m/(2n) proves the required edge bound. For example the finite constant
ck​=2(log21+s​+log((s+1)/s)s​),s=2p−2, p≥k fixed prime,
(34)
works for every n≥2, and the case n=1 has no edges. Thus
m≤ck​nlogn.​
(35)
The logarithm comes from how often a nested matching in a graph vertex set can shrink by a fixed factor before becoming empty; it is not supplied by the polynomial step alone.
