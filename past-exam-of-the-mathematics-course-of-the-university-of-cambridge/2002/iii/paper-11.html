Use the pair-design convention: each pair of distinct points must lie in exactly one Steiner block of size k. With this convention the printed endpoint k=1 is false for every v≥2, since a singleton contains no pair. Thus the intended assertion requires k≥2. For k=2, take all pairs as Steiner blocks for any v≥2. We now give a finite-field construction for every fixed k≥3, without assuming the desired design-existence theorem.
Put m=(2k​) and take an odd prime power q≡1(mod2m). By cyclicity of Fq×​, it has a subgroup H of index m; its order (q−1)/m is even, so −1∈H. We will find a k-set A={a1​,…,ak​} such that its m unordered pair differences occupy the m distinct cyclotomic classes of a finite field modulo H. The following argument supplies that set for every sufficiently large compatible q.
First establish the required two-class intersection estimate by elementary character sums. Let χ be a multiplicative character of a finite field of order m, with χ(γ)=ζm​ for a generator γ. Extend every power χj, including the trivial character, by zero at zero. For Ci​=γiH,
1Ci​​(x)=m1​∑j=0m−1​ζm−ij​χj(x).
(1)
For a nontrivial additive character ψ of Fq​ and a nontrivial multiplicative character η, its Gauss sum of a finite-field character satisfies
∣G(η)∣2=∑t=0​η(t)∑y=0​ψ((t−1)y)=(q−1)−∑t=0,1​η(t)=q.
(2)
Here the inner sum equals q−1 for t=1 and −1 otherwise, by additive-character orthogonality; multiplicative-character orthogonality gives the last equality. Such a nontrivial additive character exists by identifying the additive group with a vector space over its prime field and choosing a nonzero linear functional into that prime field.
If η,ξ,ηξ are nontrivial, substituting x=uz, y=(1−u)z in the product of their Gauss sums proves
G(η)G(ξ)=J(η,ξ)G(ηξ),J(η,ξ)=∑u​η(u)ξ(1−u).
(3)
The terms with x+y=0 sum to zero because ηξ is nontrivial. Hence ∣J∣=q​. If exactly one character is trivial, subtracting the excluded zero and one terms gives absolute value one. If both are nontrivial but their product is trivial, the substitution u/(1−u) gives J(η,η−1)=−η(−1), again of absolute value one. If both are trivial, the sum is q−2. Expanding the two cyclotomic indicators and scaling a nonzero translate to one therefore gives, uniformly in i,j and t=0,
∣Ci​∩(t+Cj​)∣=m2q​+Om​(q​).
(4)
All constants depend only on the fixed m.
To extend a prescribed difference pattern on r coordinates, fix desired classes D1​,…,Dr​ for the new differences x−ai​ and set
M(a)=#{x:x−ai​∈Di​ for all i},a∈Fqr​.
(5)
Average over all qr tuples, allowing repeated coordinates just for this average. With f=(q−1)/m, counting choices for each ai​ independently gives
EM=q(f/q)r=q/mr+Om,r​(1).
(6)
For the second moment, sum over the two potential extensions x,y. The x=y terms contribute qfr/qr. For x=y, the choices of ai​ number q/m2+Om​(q​) each, by the preceding intersection estimate. Thus
EM2=q2/m2r+Om,r​(q3/2),VarM=Om,r​(q3/2).
(7)
The Chebyshev inequality shows that the proportion of tuples with M(a)<q/(2mr) is Om,r​(q−1/2). Inductively, any fixed compatible difference pattern on r distinct coordinates is realized by at least cr​qr tuples, with cr​>0 independent of sufficiently large q. The assertion starts with r=1. Almost all tuples are outside the exceptional set, so at least half the realizing tuples have at least q/(2mr) extensions. Each extension is distinct from the old coordinates because its differences belong to nonzero classes. This proves the assertion for r+1. This cyclotomic pattern extension by a second-moment bound avoids the false assumption that every partial choice can be extended.
Prescribe a different one of the m cosets to each pair aj​−ai​, i<j. Since −1∈H, changing an orientation does not change its coset, so the prescriptions are compatible. The preceding induction supplies A.
Choose S⊂H containing one element from each pair {h,−h}. Form the Steiner blocks
B={sA+t:s∈S, t∈Fq​}.
(8)
For two distinct points x,y, their difference identifies exactly one unordered pair {ai​,aj​} of A having the same H-coset. Exactly one of (x−y)/(ai​−aj​) and its negative lies in S. Choose the orientation a,b of that pair making s=(x−y)/(a−b)∈S, and then set t=x−sa. This yields x=sa+t, y=sb+t. Both the oriented base pair and (s,t) are unique, so precisely one Steiner block contains the pair. In particular different Steiner block parameters cannot produce repeated Steiner blocks. Equivalently, the Steiner blocks sA form a difference family for a Steiner 2-design, and translation gives the cyclotomic construction of a Steiner 2-design.
There are infinitely many suitable field orders: choose one odd prime ℓ not dividing 2m, and take q=ℓφ(2m)u for integers u≥1. Euler's congruence gives q≡1(mod2m), and these orders become arbitrarily large. Consequently
For every k≥2, there are 2-(v,k,1) designs for infinitely many v.​
(9)
The qualification at k=1 is necessary for the standard pair-design definition.

At any vertex, each complete bipartite graph Kk,k​ containing it contributes exactly k incident edges. Since the copies partition all incident edges of the complete graph, n−1 is a multiple of k. In particular gcd(n,k)=1.
If there are b copies, counting their edges gives
2n(n−1)​=bk2,n(n−1)=2bk2.
(10)

Let G be a finite undirected d-regular graph on N vertices, with adjacency matrix A and normalized random walk matrix P=A/d. Define edge expansion and normalized conductance by
h(G)=min0<∣S∣≤N/2​∣S∣e(S,Sc)​,Φ(G)=h(G)/d.
(12)
An expander family has orders tending to infinity, uniformly bounded maximum degree, and a uniform positive lower bound on h. For a regular graph the constant vector is an eigenvector of P with eigenvalue one. Write its eigenvalues as 1=μ1​≥μ2​≥⋯≥μN​≥−1, and let δ=1−μ2​. This spectral gap measures the energetic cost of departing from a constant function.
For every mean-zero vector f, the spectral theorem and the graph's energy identity give
d1​∑{u,v}∈E​(f(u)−f(v))2=⟨f,(I−P)f⟩≥δ∥f∥22​.
(13)
Apply this to f=1S​−∣S∣1/N. Its norm squared is ∣S∣(1−∣S∣/N) and its energy is e(S,Sc)/d. Therefore
e(S,Sc)≥dδ∣S∣(1−∣S∣/N),Φ(G)≥δ/2.​
(14)
Thus a uniform spectral gap gives uniform edge expansion when d is fixed. This also bounds exterior vertex expansion: each exterior vertex accounts for at most d boundary edges, so ∣N(S)∖S∣≥e(S,Sc)/d.
For the converse, take a mean-zero eigenvector f for μ2​ and choose its sign so that its positive support has at most N/2 vertices. Put g=f+, which is nonzero. Truncation decreases energy in the following precise sense: for any two real numbers a,b,
(a+−b+)2≤(a+−b+)(a−b).
(15)
Summing over edges yields ⟨g,(I−P)g⟩≤⟨g,(I−P)f⟩=δ∥g∥22​. For the threshold sets St​={v:g(v)2>t}, every nonempty set lies in that small positive support. Integrating its boundary count gives the layer-cake identity
∑{u,v}∈E​∣g(u)2−g(v)2∣=∫0∞​e(St​,Stc​)dt≥h(G)∥g∥22​.
(16)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality bounds the left side by
(∑E​(g(u)−g(v))2)1/2(∑E​(g(u)+g(v))2)1/2≤d2δ​∥g∥22​,
(17)
because the first sum is at most dδ∥g∥22​ and the second at most 2d∥g∥22​. Cancelling proves the Cheeger inequality in both directions:
21−μ2​​≤Φ(G)≤2(1−μ2​)​.​
(18)
Hence bounded-degree regular families have uniform edge expansion exactly when their second-largest normalized eigenvalue stays uniformly below one.
Absolute eigenvalues give additional information. Put ρ=maxi≥2​∣μi​∣. Centering the indicators of S,T and applying the operator-norm bound on the mean-zero subspace proves the expander mixing lemma:
​e(S,T)−Nd∣S∣∣T∣​​≤dρ∣S∣(1−∣S∣/N)∣T∣(1−∣T∣/N)​.
(19)
Here e(S,T)=1ST​A1T​, so internal edges are counted as oriented incidences. A small ρ makes edge counts resemble uniformly distributed edges. It also gives rapid random walk mixing: starting at a vertex, Pt contracts the mean-zero component of its point mass by at most ρt in Euclidean norm; Cauchy-Schwarz inequality then gives total variation distance at most 21​N​ρt from the uniform distribution. A bipartite graph has eigenvalue −1 and its non-lazy walk alternates sides, even if it has good edge expansion. The lazy walk (I+P)/2 has nonconstant eigenvalues between zero and 1−δ/2, resolving this periodicity. It is important to distinguish the gap 1−μ2​ controlling cuts from the absolute bound controlling non-lazy mixing.
Here is a fully specified deterministic construction of an infinite family. Its degree is large but fixed. Set
d=216,D=d2=232,W=F264​,∣W∣=D2.
(20)
Choose the lexicographically first ordered list a1​,…,ad​∈W satisfying
​d1​∑j=1d​(−1)u⋅aj​​≤101​for every u∈W∖{0}.
(21)
This finite deterministic search terminates. To prove existence, choose the list uniformly and independently. For each nonzero u, its signs are independent unbiased signs; the exponential-moment estimate cosht≤et2/2 and Markov inequality give an exceptional probability at most 2e−d/200. A union bound gives total failure probability at most
2(264−1)e−216/200<1.
(22)
Thus at least one qualifying list exists. Fix it once and for all.
Let H be the Cayley graph on W whose jth neighbor of x is x+aj​. It is an undirected d-regular port-labeled graph, allowing repeated edges and loops in this intermediate construction. Its characters x↦(−1)u⋅x form an orthogonal eigenbasis, with the displayed averages as normalized eigenvalues. Therefore ρ(H)≤1/10. Each loop counts as one transition port; degrees here mean row sums of the adjacency matrix.
We next define the zig-zag product and prove the bound used for iteration. A port labeling of a D0​-regular undirected graph F gives an involution RotF​(v,a)=(w,b), recording the endpoint and reverse port. If a d-regular graph H0​ has vertex set [D0​], the product has vertices (v,a)∈V(F)×[D0​]. For each ordered pair of small-graph ports, move from a to a′ in H0​, follow the big edge RotF​(v,a′)=(w,b′), and move from b′ to b in H0​. The endpoint is (w,b); reversing the steps reverses the two small-graph port labels. The product is undirected, has ∣V(F)∣D0​ vertices, and has degree d2.
Write B=I⊗PH0​​ and let R be the permutation matrix of RotF​. The normalized product operator is BRB. For a mean-zero unit vector, decompose f=u+v into its cloud-constant part and its cloud-orthogonal part. Then Bu=u, ∥Bv∥≤ρ(H0​)∥v∥, and ∥R∥=1. The compression of R to cloud-constant vectors is exactly PF​, so ∣⟨u,Ru⟩∣≤ρ(F)∥u∥2. Expanding the quadratic form and applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
∣⟨f,BRBf⟩∣≤ρ(F)∥u∥2+2ρ(H0​)∥u∥∥v∥+ρ(H0​)2∥v∥2≤ρ(F)+2ρ(H0​)+ρ(H0​)2.
(23)
The operator is symmetric, so its mean-zero operator norm is bounded by this same quantity. No unproved product spectral estimate is needed.
Start with the loop-inclusive complete graph G0​ on D vertices, whose normalized matrix is J/D and whose nonconstant eigenvalues are all zero. Given a D-regular Gt​, its two-step graph Gt2​ has degree D2 and normalized operator PGt​2​. Label a two-step edge by its ordered pair of ports; reversal reverses the path and its reverse ports. These D2 labels are the vertex set of the fixed graph H. Define
Gt+1​=Gt2​zigzagH.
(24)
It has degree d2=D, and its number of vertices is Nt+1​=Nt​D2, hence Nt​=D2t+1. By the proved bound,
ρ(Gt+1​)≤ρ(Gt​)2+10021​.
(25)
Starting from zero, induction gives ρ(Gt​)≤1/2, since (1/2)2+21/100=46/100<1/2. The cut-energy argument therefore gives multiplicity-counted edge expansion at least D/4 for every Gt​.
To obtain ordinary simple graphs, delete loops and merge parallel edges. The maximum degree is at most D, and any one unordered vertex pair had multiplicity at most D. Thus every cut retains at least 1/D of its multiplicity-counted boundary, giving
∣V(Gt​)∣=D2t+1⟶∞,Δ≤D,h≥1/4.​
(26)
These simple graphs form an expander family. Every choice is deterministic: the finite base search is fixed independently of t, and the recursive edge rules determine all subsequent adjacency lists. Once that base is fixed, adjacency lists can be generated successively using the previous lists, with a constant amount of work per output edge. This is squaring and zig-zag iteration yields bounded-degree expanders. The spectral argument supplies the uniform expansion guarantee and explains why squaring improves the eigenvalues while the product restores a constant degree.

The coefficient form of the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz is as follows. Let F be a field, let P∈F[x1​,…,xr​] have total degree at most t1​+⋯+tr​, and suppose the coefficient of x1t1​​⋯xrtr​​ is nonzero. For finite subsets Si​⊂F with ∣Si​∣>ti​, there is a point of S1​×⋯×Sr​ where P is nonzero.
For completeness, restrict to ∣Si​∣=ti​+1. The coefficient identity is
[x1t1​​⋯xrtr​​]P=∑ai​∈Si​​∏i​∏b∈Si​∖{ai​}​(ai​−b)P(a1​,…,ar​)​.
(27)
By Lagrange interpolation, the one-variable weighted sum on the right kills powers below ti​ and equals one on the power ti​. Any monomial of total degree at most ∑ti​, other than the specified one, has some exponent below ti​ and therefore contributes zero; the specified monomial contributes its coefficient. Every denominator is nonzero in the field. Hence a nonzero coefficient forces a nonzero grid value. The two applications below use this coefficient form.

Choose a fixed prime p≥k, and put s=2p−2. We first derive the polynomial step: a graph H on N vertices and M>(p−1)N edges, with maximum degree at most 2p−1, contains a nonempty p-regular subgraph. Use a Boolean variable xe​ for each edge and work over Fp​. Define
R(x)=∏v∈V(H)​[1−(∑e∋v​xe​)p−1]−∏e∈E(H)​(1−xe​).
(28)
The first product has degree at most (p−1)N<M, while the full squarefree monomial ∏e​xe​ has coefficient (−1)M+1=0 in the second term with its minus sign. The Combinatorial Nullstellensatz, with every grid {0,1}, gives a nonzero value of R. Since R(0)=0, this is a nonempty edge selection. The second product is then zero, so the first is nonzero. Fermat's little theorem makes each selected vertex degree zero modulo p. The maximum-degree bound implies that every positive selected degree is precisely p. This proves the prime-regular subgraph from Boolean polynomial constraints.
We next turn density into an almost-regular bipartite subgraph, keeping the logarithmic loss explicit. Let the original graph have n≥2 vertices and m>0 edges. Some bipartition retains at least m/2 edges: choose the sides independently with probability 1/2 and average the number crossing. Its average degree is at least m/n. Repeatedly delete a vertex of degree at most half the current average degree. Such a deletion cannot lower that average, since
N−12(e−d(v))​≥N2e​⟺d(v)≤e/N.
(29)
The process cannot delete the last positive-density graph, because the maintained average is positive. At termination the minimum degree is an integer σ>m/(2n).
Choose the larger side A of this remaining bipartite graph, with opposite side B. Keep exactly σ edges at each vertex of A; choices at different vertices of A do not conflict. Remove any now-isolated vertices of B. Thus ∣A∣≥∣B∣, and every vertex of A has degree σ.
Such a half-regular graph contains a half-regular restriction with a perfect matching. Choose a minimal nonempty X⊆A satisfying ∣N(X)∣≤∣X∣, which exists because the whole side A satisfies this inequality. In fact equality holds: if it were strict, deleting a vertex of X would retain the inequality, contradicting minimality; the singleton case has at least one neighbor. Every proper subset Y⊂X satisfies ∣N(Y)∣≥∣Y∣. Hall's marriage theorem therefore supplies a perfect matching on X∪N(X). All σ edges incident with each vertex of X remain inside that restriction.
Delete this perfect matching and repeat the restriction argument. The left degrees decrease by one at each step. We obtain σ edge-disjoint perfect matchings M0​,…,Mσ−1​ on nested nonempty vertex sets, with sizes
n=N−1​≥N0​≥N1​≥⋯≥Nσ−1​≥2.
(30)
For any i with i+s≤σ−1, the union of s+1=2p−1 consecutive matchings in a graph has maximum degree at most 2p−1 and at least (s+1)Ni+s​/2 edges on Ni​ vertices. If
Ni+s​>s+1s​Ni​,
(31)
it has more than (p−1)Ni​ edges, so the polynomial step yields a p-regular bipartite subgraph. Every regular bipartite graph decomposes into perfect matchings: for a set in one part, counting incident edges gives Hall's inequality, so a perfect matching exists; remove it and repeat. Choosing k of these p matchings in a graph gives a nonempty k-regular subgraph. This is the nested matching criterion for a regular subgraph.
If no k-regular subgraph exists, every displayed size ratio must therefore satisfy the reverse inequality. Iterating at indices 0,s,2s,… gives, with r=⌊(σ−1)/s⌋,
2≤Nrs​≤n(s+1s​)r.
(32)
Consequently r≤logn/log((s+1)/s) and
σ≤1+s(1+log((s+1)/s)logn​).
(33)
Combining with σ>m/(2n) proves the required edge bound. For example the finite constant
ck​=2(log21+s​+log((s+1)/s)s​),s=2p−2, p≥k fixed prime,
(34)
works for every n≥2, and the case n=1 has no edges. Thus
m≤ck​nlogn.​
(35)
The logarithm comes from how often a nested matching in a graph vertex set can shrink by a fixed factor before becoming empty; it is not supplied by the polynomial step alone.

Write a=∣A∣ and b=∣B∣, so p≥a>b≥1. To cover both the unsaturated and saturated cases with the same coefficient calculation, set
b′=min{b,p+2−a},r=a+b′−2=min{p,a+b−2}.
(36)
Choose B′⊆B of size b′. These parameters satisfy 1≤b′<a≤p and 1≤r≤p.
Assume, for a contradiction, that the restricted sumset C has fewer than r elements. Extend it to a subset D⊆Fp​ of size r−1, and form
F(x,y)=(x−y)∏z∈D​(x+y−z).
(37)
On A×B′, this polynomial vanishes: when x=y the first factor vanishes, and when x=y the sum belongs to C⊆D. Its total degree is r=(a−1)+(b′−1). Only the leading homogeneous part (x−y)(x+y)r−1 contributes to the coefficient of xa−1yb′−1. That coefficient is
(a−2r−1​)−(a−1r−1​)=(a−b′)(a−1)!(b′−1)!(r−1)!​.

The equality includes b′=1, with an out-of-range binomial coefficient interpreted as zero. All factorial arguments are between zero and p−1, and 0<a−b′<p, so this coefficient is nonzero in Fp​. ∣C∣≥min{p,∣A∣+∣B∣−2}.​
(39)
