An interval covering relation I→J means F(I)⊇J. The associated directed graph has one vertex for each interval and an edge i→j for each covering. Its transition matrix is Aij​=1 when that edge exists, and zero otherwise. Matrix multiplication counts walks; thus tr(An)​ counts closed length-n itineraries, with a distinguished starting vertex.
To see how itineraries force periodic orbits, a continuous map covering a closed interval has a closed subinterval mapping onto it: choose successive first/last crossings of its endpoints. Pull these subintervals back along a closed walk. This yields K⊆Ii0​​ with Fn(K)⊇Ii0​​⊇K. At the two preimages of the ends of K, Fn(x)−x has opposite weak signs; the intermediate value theorem gives a fixed point of Fn.
A graph alone does not give an exact count of periodic points for a general continuous map: extra oscillations or a fixed interval can create additional points. For example on I=[0,1], both F(x)=x and F(x)=1−x have matrix A=(1); the first has infinitely many fixed points and the second only one. Distinct itineraries give distinct points when their interval interiors are disjoint and the periodic orbit avoids shared endpoints. In that setting, tr(An) is a guaranteed lower bound, not a universal equality. Likewise, the primitive-word count (1/n)∑d∣n​μ(d)tr(An/d) counts guaranteed least-period itineraries under this separation condition. With overlapping intervals, even distinctness needs qualification. This is the scope of counting cycles in an interval covering graph.
For the first five-cycle, put Ij​=[xj​,xj+1​], 0≤j≤3. The graph and matrix are
0→1,1→2,2→3,3→0,1,2,3,A=​0001​1001​0101​0011​​.
(35)
The four traces are 1,3,7,15. Shared endpoints have least period five, so they do not spoil the distinctness argument for the requested periods. Primitive cyclic itineraries are: 3 for period one; (3,2) for period two; (3,1,2) and (3,3,2) for period three; (0,1,2,3), (3,3,1,2) and (3,3,3,2) for period four. Consequently the monotone five-cycle interval covering pattern guarantees
at least 1,1,2,3 cycles of least periods 1,2,3,4.​
(36)
These words specify the successive intervals containing their points; cyclic rotations denote the same orbit.
There is a horseshoe for an interval map for F2: both I2​ and I3​ cover I2​∪I3​ under that iterate, and their interiors are disjoint. Repeated binary choices produce at least 2n separated length-n itineraries for the second iterate, hence positive topological entropy, at least (log2)/2 for F. Thus the dynamics are chaotic in this standard interval-map sense. No conclusion of global transitivity on all of R follows.
A one-step horseshoe is not guaranteed if that is the intended definition. For example the unimodal continuous map F(x)=x+1 for x≤3 and F(x)=16−4x for x≥3 has cycle 0,1,2,3,4. A left-branch interval cannot cover itself, since its image is shifted to the right. Two right-branch intervals cannot each cover their union because that branch is injective. If one interval straddles the maximum and covers itself, its right endpoint must map below its left endpoint; every disjoint interval farther to the right then maps below it and cannot self-cover. A disjoint interval to the left also cannot self-cover. So this example has no one-step two-branch horseshoe, although its second iterate does. It also proves that F can be unimodal.
For the second order, write J0​=[x3​,x1​], J1​=[x1​,x0​], J2​=[x0​,x2​], J3​=[x2​,x4​]. Endpoint images give
0→3,1→1,2,2→0,3→0,1,B=​0011​0101​0100​1000​​.
(37)
The traces are 1,3,1,7. The guaranteed primitive words are 1, (0,3) and (1,2,0,3), giving periods 1,2,4 but not necessarily 3​. To prove the last qualification, take ordered points 0,1,2,3,4 and linearly interpolate the map values 4,3,1,0,2 on [0,4], extending by G(x)=4−x for x<0 and G(x)=2 for x>4. Both exterior regions enter [0,4] after at most two steps, so they introduce no extra periodic points. Its only closed length-three itinerary is 111; on J1​ it is x↦5−2x, whose third iterate has only the same fixed point. Endpoints have least period five, so this example has no three-cycle.
The distinct fourth-iterate return walks 1111 and 1203 yield two subintervals of J1​ that return over J1​, giving a horseshoe for G4 and positive entropy at least (log2)/4. Thus G too must have chaotic interval dynamics and a horseshoe in some iterate. The affine example has no one-step horseshoe: its increasing branch lies strictly below the diagonal, while on the decreasing branch injectivity prevents two self-covering intervals; a minimum-straddling self-covering interval excludes a self-covering interval on either side by the same endpoint argument. The distinction between a map and its iterate is essential for both patterns.
