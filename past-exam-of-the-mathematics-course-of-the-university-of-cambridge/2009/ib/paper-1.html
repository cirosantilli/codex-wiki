Under the standard full-column-rank assumption on the design matrix, the Gaussian log-likelihood for the normal linear model, up to terms independent of β, is −∥Y−Xβ∥2/(2σ2). In a principal ideal domain, an ascending chain of ideals stabilizes: its union is an ideal (d), and its generator belongs to one member, which then contains the union. This proves the needed ascending chain condition. If a nonzero nonunit had no finite irreducible factorization, split it into two nonunits and select a factor that still has no such factorization. Continuing would give a strictly ascending chain of principal ideals, because a=bc with c a nonunit implies (a)⊊(b). This contradicts the chain condition, so factorization exists.
An irreducible element p generates a maximal ideal: any intermediate ideal is (d) with p=dc, and irreducibility forces d or c to be a unit. Therefore p is a prime element. In any two irreducible factorizations, the first prime factor divides a factor of the other, hence is associate to it. Cancel and continue. This proves every principal ideal domain is a unique factorization domain.
In Z[−3​], the multiplicative field norm is N(a+b−3​)=a2+3b2. The only units are ±1. There is no element of norm two, so every element of norm four is irreducible: a factorization into nonunits would require norms two and two. Consequently
4=2⋅2=(1+−3​)(1−−3​)​
(13)
is a pair of genuinely different irreducible factorizations. Neither 1+−3​ nor 1−−3​ is associate to 2.
Take the Eisenstein integers R=Z[ω], where ω=(−1+−3​)/2 and ω2+ω+1=0. Since −3​=1+2ω, the embedded subring is Z+2Zω, whose additive index in R is two. Its larger ring has Eisenstein-integer norm N(a+bω)=a2−ab+b2 and is Euclidean. Indeed, write a complex quotient in the real basis 1,ω and round both coordinates to integers. The coordinate errors u,v satisfy ∣u∣,∣v∣≤1/2, giving ∣u+vω∣2=u2−uv+v2≤3/4<1. Thus division leaves a remainder of smaller norm. A Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain, because division by a nonzero element of least norm in an ideal leaves zero remainder. This gives the requested index-two embedding into a principal ideal domain.

A critical region C is the set of sample outcomes on which a test rejects its null hypothesis. Its power function is β(θ)=Pθ​(X∈C); its size of a statistical test is supθ∈Θ0​​β(θ). Randomized tests replace the indicator of C by a rejection probability ϕ(X)∈[0,1].
The Neyman-Pearson lemma states that for two simple hypotheses with densities p0​,p1​, the test ϕ∗ that is one where p1​>kp0​, zero where p1​<kp0​, and randomized on equality to have null rejection probability α, maximizes power among tests of size at most α. Here k≥0. To prove it, let ϕ be any competing test. Pointwise, (ϕ∗−ϕ)(p1​−kp0​)≥0, since both factors have the same sign off the equality set. Integrating gives
E1​ϕ∗−E1​ϕ≥k(E0​ϕ∗−E0​ϕ)≥0.
(37)
This proves the lemma, including equality-set randomization and points where p0​=0.
Take the exponential parameter to be its rate: pλ​(x)=λe−λx for x≥0. For T=∑i​Xi​, the joint likelihood ratio is (λ1​/λ0​)ne−(λ1​−λ0​)T, decreasing in T. Hence for 0<α<1 the most powerful test is

Read the maze as two four-cycles: outer vertices Oj​, inner vertices Ij​ for j modulo four, edges Oj​Oj+1​, Ij​Ij+1​, and Oj​Ij​, together with edges from every Ij​ to the target T. The starting vertex is an outer vertex. Outer degrees are three and inner degrees are four.
For the ordinary random walk on a graph, symmetry gives a common expected hitting time O from an outer vertex and I from an inner one. First-step conditioning gives
O=1+32​O+31​I,I=1+41​O+21​I.
(41)
Thus O=I+3 and I=2+O/2, yielding I=7 and the requested starting mean O=10 moves.
For the non-backtracking random walk, the current vertex alone does not form a Markov chain. Use states (a,b) recording previous and current vertices. Before hitting T, its transitions are (a,b)→(b,c) with probability 1/(degb−1) for each neighbor c=a. Add a starting state with no preceding vertex, choosing its first neighbor with probability 1/3, and an absorbing hit state. This specifies the full Markov chain.
Its directed edges aggregate into types OO,OI,IO,II, determined by the two vertex layers. From OO the next type is OO or OI with probability 1/2 each. From OI it is II with probability 2/3 and the absorbing state with probability 1/3. From IO it is always OO. From II it is II, IO or the absorbing state with probability 1/3 each. Every nonabsorbing type has a positive uniformly bounded chance of hitting within at most three more moves, so its mean time is finite.
Let their remaining mean times be A,B,C,D in that order. The first-step equations are
A=1+21​A+21​B,B=1+32​D,C=1+A,D=1+31​C+31​D.
(42)
Solving gives A=13/2, B=9/2, C=15/2, D=21/4. The first move from the initial outer vertex reaches type OO with probability 2/3 and type OI with probability 1/3, so the intelligent starting mean is
1+32​213​+31​29​=641​ moves.​
(43)
This hitting a hub from two four-cycles calculation reduces the mean from 10 to 41/6, saving 19/6 moves. Excluding immediate reversal removes wasteful two-step returns, although it does not guarantee that the next move is toward the target.
