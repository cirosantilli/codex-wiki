The polynomial ring F[X] is an integral domain, since the product of two nonzero polynomials has nonzero leading coefficient. Let I be an ideal. If I=0, it is generated by zero. Otherwise choose a nonzero polynomial g∈I of minimum degree. For any f∈I, the polynomial division algorithm over the field gives f=qg+r with r=0 or degr<degg. Since r=f−qg∈I, minimality forces r=0. Hence every element of I is a multiple of g, and I=(g). Thus F[X] is a principal ideal domain.
The nonprincipal coordinate ideal in two variables provides the contrast:
(X,Y)⊂F[X,Y] is not principal.​
(1)
If it were (g), then g would divide X and Y. Total degrees add when nonzero polynomials over a field are multiplied. A nonconstant divisor of X consequently has degree one, its cofactor is constant, and it is a nonzero scalar multiple of X. Such a polynomial cannot divide Y, as setting X=0 shows. Thus g would be a nonzero constant and the ideal would contain one. But all elements of (X,Y) vanish at (0,0), so this is impossible.

True. Fix a point x0​ of a nonempty connected open set and let P be its path-connected component. Each point of the open set has a small Euclidean ball contained in it. A ball is convex and path-connected, so each such ball lies entirely in one path component. All path components are therefore open, and the complement of P is open as well. If the complement were nonempty, these two open sets would disconnect the original set. Thus P is the whole set. In fact the joining paths may be chosen polygonal.

False. The rational numbers Q with their subspace topology from R are Hausdorff. If a subset contains two distinct rationals a<b, choose an irrational c between them; intersecting the subset with (−∞,c) and (c,∞) gives a separation into two nonempty relatively open sets. Thus every nonempty connected subset of Q is a singleton, so it is totally disconnected. Yet no rational is isolated, because every interval around it contains another rational. Use the standard commutative-ring convention with identity and a nonempty multiplicative subset. An ideal disjoint from that subset is proper. Suppose ab∈I but a,b∈/I. Maximality among disjoint ideals means that both I+(a) and I+(b) meet S. Thus s=i+ra∈S and t=j+ub∈S for some i,j∈I, r,u∈R. Their product belongs to S by multiplicative closure, but
st=ij+iub+jra+ruab∈I,

a contradiction. Consequently I is prime. This is prime ideal avoiding a multiplicative subset.
For an integral domain, form its field of fractions from pairs (a,b) with b=0, identifying (a,b) and (c,d) when ad=bc. Write the class as a/b and define addition and multiplication by
ba​+dc​=bdad+bc​,ba​dc​=bdac​.

For a polygonal cell decomposition of the sphere, Euler characteristic gives
V−E+F=2.​
(25)
Count incidences between faces and edges. Each edge borders two faces, and each face has at least three sides, so 2E≥3F. If every vertex had valence at least six, counting edge endpoints would also give 2E≥6V. Hence V≤E/3 and F≤2E/3, implying V−E+F≤0, contrary to Euler's formula. Thus some vertex has valence less than six.
There is a missing qualification in the printed affine-line conclusion. Two distinct parallel lines have no intersection point, so its intersection condition holds vacuously, but they do not meet at a finite point. Even three or more distinct parallel lines give the same counterexample. Consequently that conclusion is false literally without a restriction on parallel lines.
Here is the intended spherical argument when the lines are pairwise nonparallel. Lift each line in the plane z=1 to the plane through it and the origin in R3; intersect these planes with the unit sphere to obtain great circles. A common axis of all these planes meets z=1 in the required common point. Suppose no such axis exists. Their circle arrangement then decomposes the sphere into polygonal faces with at least three edges. To see why a two-sided face is impossible, its two great-circle edges would have antipodal endpoints. Every defining hemisphere containing that face would contain both endpoints and would therefore have its boundary plane through their axis, contradicting the absence of a common axis.
Every circle-arrangement vertex corresponds to an intersection of lifted planes. Because no two affine lines are parallel, this axis meets z=1, so the vertex corresponds to a finite intersection of the original lines. By the assumed multiplicity, at least three circles pass through it, giving valence at least six. This contradicts the preceding sphere count, proving concurrence in the nonparallel case. It is the ordinary vertex in a nonconcurrent great-circle arrangement argument.
Alternatively the corrected projective statement allows parallel lines, but requires the intersection condition at infinity as well, and concludes concurrence at a possibly infinite projective point. The same great-circle proof applies; concurrence at infinity represents a parallel family. The printed condition about finite affine intersections alone must not be used to assert a finite common point for such a family.
