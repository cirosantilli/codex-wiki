The monomials xiyj with i,j≥0 and i+j≤n form a basis of the given vector space. There are k+1 of total degree k, so
dimV=k=0∑n​(k+1)=2(n+1)(n+2)​.​
(1)
On that basis the linear operator is diagonal:
S(xiyj)=(i(i−1)+j(j−1))xiyj.
(2)
Both summands of the eigenvalue are nonnegative integers. Their sum is zero precisely when i,j∈{0,1}. Thus the kernel is the span of those members of 1,x,y,xy whose total degree is at most n. In particular it is span{1,x,y} for n=1 and span{1,x,y,xy} for n≥2; for n=0 it is the constants. The image of a linear map is the span of all the other permitted monomials, since each has a nonzero diagonal eigenvalue and hence is the image of its reciprocal multiple. No excluded monomial can occur in an image because its diagonal coefficient is zero.
The trace is the sum of these eigenvalues. At degrees zero and one all contributions vanish. At degree two the contributions are 2,0,2, summing to four; at degree three they are 6,2,2,6, summing to sixteen; at degree four they are 12,6,4,6,12, summing to forty. Therefore
trS=0,4,20,60for n=1,2,3,4 respectively.​
(3)
This is the diagonal monomial action of a second-order Euler operator.

2F OurBigBook.com
For a finite group of order pam with p prime and p∤m, the Sylow theorems assert that a subgroup of order pa exists; every subgroup whose order is a power of p is contained in one such Sylow subgroup; all Sylow subgroups for that prime are conjugate; and their number np​ divides m and is congruent to one modulo p. A unique Sylow subgroup is a normal subgroup, since conjugation cannot move it to another one.
Here 56=7⋅8, so n7​ divides eight and is congruent to one modulo seven. Hence n7​=1 or 8. In the first case the Sylow subgroup of order seven is normal. In the second case, any two distinct order-seven subgroups intersect only at the identity, because a nontrivial intersection would generate both prime-order subgroups. Their nonidentity elements therefore account for 8⋅6=48 elements.
Exactly eight elements of the group remain. Every Sylow subgroup of order eight consists entirely of these remaining elements: a nonidentity element in it has order a power of two and cannot be one of the order-seven elements already counted. Each such Sylow subgroup must consequently equal the remaining eight-element set. It is unique and hence normal. Thus a group of order 56 has a normal subgroup of order seven or of order eight.

The contraction mapping theorem states that a contraction mapping T:X→X on a nonempty complete metric space has a unique fixed point. Here contraction means that some 0≤q<1 satisfies d(Tx,Ty)≤qd(x,y) for all x,y.
For a proof, choose x0​ and iterate xr+1​=Txr​. Induction gives d(xr+1​,xr​)≤qrd(x1​,x0​). For m>n, the triangle inequality and a geometric series give
d(xm​,xn​)≤∑r=nm−1​qrd(x1​,x0​)≤1−qqn​d(x1​,x0​).
(4)
Thus the iterates form a Cauchy sequence, and completeness supplies a limit a∈X. A contraction mapping is continuous, so Ta=limTxn​=limxn+1​=a. If b is another fixed point, d(a,b)=d(Ta,Tb)≤qd(a,b), forcing d(a,b)=0. This proves existence and uniqueness.
For the exponential problem, use h=f∘f on the complete metric space R. Its derivative is
h′(x)=e−xe−e−x.
(5)
Putting t=e−x>0, the function te−t has maximum 1/e at t=1, as its derivative is (1−t)e−t. By the mean value theorem, ∣h(x)−h(y)∣≤e−1∣x−y∣, so h is a contraction mapping on all of R. Let a be its unique fixed point. The compositions commute, so h(f(a))=f(h(a))=f(a); uniqueness of the fixed point of h forces f(a)=a. Conversely every fixed point of f is fixed by h. Hence there is exactly one real solution of x=e−x. It lies in (0,1) because a=e−a>0 and then e−a<1. This uses the iterated contraction argument without claiming that f itself contracts on all of R.

A Hausdorff space is a topological space in which any two distinct points have disjoint open neighborhoods. Let K be a compact subset of a Hausdorff space and x∈/K. For each y∈K, choose disjoint open neighborhoods Uy​ of y and Wy​ of x. The Uy​ cover K, so compactness gives a finite subcover Uy1​​,…,Uym​​. Their paired intersection W=⋂r=1m​Wyr​​ is an open neighborhood of x disjoint from K. Thus every point of its complement has an open neighborhood in the complement, proving every compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed. The empty set case is immediate.
In the cocountable topology on an uncountable set, any two nonempty open sets intersect: their combined complements are countable and cannot exhaust X. Thus the space is not Hausdorff.
Nevertheless every compact subset is closed here. In fact only finite subsets are compact. To prove this, suppose K is infinite and choose distinct points d1​,d2​,…∈K. The open sets
Un​=X∖{dm​:m≥n}
(6)
form an increasing open cover of X, and hence of K. Any finite selection is contained in UN​ for its largest index N, which misses dN​∈K. So K is not compact. Finite sets are compact in any topological space, and in this cocountable topology they are closed because their complements are open. This proves the claimed behavior of compact subsets of a cocountable space.

Label the towns L,M,R from left to right, and the treatment plants A,B,C,D as upper-left, upper-right, lower-left and lower-right. Model each treatment capacity as a sink arc to the sea. Incoming untreated sewage can be forwarded, so the numbers inside the plant circles constrain treatment, not total throughput.
The sum of treatment capacities is 16+20+14+12=62, so no feasible flow network can handle more than 62. Here is a feasible routing attaining that upper bound. Send town inflows
L→A:6,L→C:9;M→A:5,M→B:7,M→C:10,M→D:3;R→B:11,R→D:11.
(25)
Every town-to-plant flow respects its directed pipe capacity. Send an additional five units from C to A through their bidirectional pipe, whose capacity is twelve, and two from D to B through their bidirectional pipe, whose capacity is fourteen. Use neither horizontal plant-to-plant pipe.
Now A receives 6+5+5=16 and treats all of it. Plant C receives 9+10=19, forwards five and treats fourteen. Plant B receives 7+11+2=20 and treats all of it. Plant D receives 3+11=14, forwards two and treats twelve. Thus flow conservation holds at every plant and all treatment capacities are saturated. The routing proves the upper bound is achievable:
maximum total=62,(L,M,R)=(15,25,22).​
(26)
This is one optimal assignment, not a uniqueness assertion. The total bound comes from the cut consisting of all treatment-to-sea arcs; the explicit routing supplies a matching maximum flow certificate.

The centre of a group is Z(G)={z∈G:zg=gz for every g∈G}. It is a normal subgroup. For ∣G∣=pr with r≥1, let G act on itself by conjugation. The orbit-stabilizer theorem makes the size of the conjugacy class of g equal to [G:CG​(g)], a power of p. A class has size one exactly when g∈Z(G); every other class size is divisible by p. The class equation therefore gives
∣G∣=∣Z(G)∣+∑noncentral classes​[G:CG​(g)],
(29)
so p divides ∣Z(G)∣. Since the identity belongs to the centre of a group, its size is a positive multiple of p. This proves the nontrivial center of a finite p-group.
If G/Z(G) is cyclic, choose a representative a of a generator. Every element is amz for an integer m and z∈Z(G). Any two such elements commute, since their central factors commute with everything and their remaining factors are powers of the same element. Thus G is an abelian group, Z(G)=G, and the cyclic quotient by the centre of a group is trivial.
For a non-abelian group of order p3, the centre of a group has order p, p2 or p3 by the proved result and Lagrange's theorem. The last would make the group abelian. The middle would make G/Z(G) have prime order and hence cyclic, also making G abelian by the preceding argument. Therefore its centre has order p.

Project a point P=(X,Y,Z)=N along the line joining it to N; adjoin z=∞ for the north pole to identify the whole sphere with the Riemann sphere. A point of that line is N+t(P−N). The vacuum Maxwell equations are ∇⋅E=∇⋅B=0, ∇×E=−Bt​ and ∇×B=Et​/c2. Taking the curl of Faraday's equation and using ∇×(∇×E)=∇(∇⋅E)−∇2E gives −∇2E=−Ett​/c2. The same calculation for Ampère's equation gives ∇2B=Btt​/c2. Hence each Cartesian component satisfies the electromagnetic wave equation.
Model the walls as a perfect conductor, with no oscillating fields in its interior. The tangential electric field is zero at the surface, otherwise it would drive unbounded conductor currents. The normal magnetic field is zero, since the divergence-free field has a continuous normal component and the interior oscillating field is zero. Thus n×E=0 and n⋅B=0. Normal E and tangential B may be nonzero because surface charge and surface current support their jumps.
