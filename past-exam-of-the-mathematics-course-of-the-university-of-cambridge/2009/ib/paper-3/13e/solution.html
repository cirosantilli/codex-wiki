A function is Fréchet differentiable at x when there is a linear map A:Rn→Rm such that
f(x+h)=f(x)+Ah+r(h),∣h∣∣r(h)∣​⟶0.
(26)
This linear map, which is unique, is the derivative Df(x).
For the chain rule, if f is differentiable at x and g is differentiable at f(x), then g∘f is differentiable at x and D(g∘f)(x)=Dg(f(x))Df(x). Indeed write f(x+h)−f(x)=Ah+r(h)=v(h) and g(f(x)+v)=g(f(x))+Bv+s(v), where r(h)=o(∣h∣), s(v)=o(∣v∣). Since v(h)=O(∣h∣), substitution gives
g(f(x+h))−g(f(x))=BAh+Br(h)+s(v(h))=BAh+o(∣h∣),
(27)
which proves the assertion, including paths on which v(h)=0.
For g1​, write q=x2−y2. Away from the two diagonal lines, q=0 and the formula is a composition of smooth functions, hence differentiable. At the origin, ∣g1​(x,y)∣≤∣x2−y2∣≤x2+y2, so its ratio to the displacement length tends to zero. Its derivative there is therefore zero: this is quadratic damping restores differentiability at the origin.
At a nonzero point (a,b) with a=±b, necessarily a=0. Along the x direction,
tg1​(a+t,b)−g1​(a,b)​=(2a+t)sin2at+t21​.
(28)
The denominator inside the sine is locally an invertible function of t, with nonzero derivative 2a. Choose sequences tending to zero for which its reciprocal equals 2πn+π/2 and 2πn+3π/2. The quotients tend to 2a and −2a. Thus even this directional derivative fails to exist, and the function is not differentiable there. Consequently
Diff(g1​)={(x,y):x=y, x=−y}∪{(0,0)}.​
(29)
For g2​, the formula is smooth away from zero. At zero, ∣g2​(x,y)∣≤x2+y2, so the same remainder estimate gives zero derivative. Hence
Diff(g2​)=R2,Dg2​(0,0)=0.​
(30)
No limit of the sine factor itself is needed for either derivative at the origin.
