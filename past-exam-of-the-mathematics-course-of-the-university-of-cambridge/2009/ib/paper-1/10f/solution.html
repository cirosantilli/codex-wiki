In a principal ideal domain, an ascending chain of ideals stabilizes: its union is an ideal (d), and its generator belongs to one member, which then contains the union. This proves the needed ascending chain condition. If a nonzero nonunit had no finite irreducible factorization, split it into two nonunits and select a factor that still has no such factorization. Continuing would give a strictly ascending chain of principal ideals, because a=bc with c a nonunit implies (a)⊊(b). This contradicts the chain condition, so factorization exists.
An irreducible element p generates a maximal ideal: any intermediate ideal is (d) with p=dc, and irreducibility forces d or c to be a unit. Therefore p is a prime element. In any two irreducible factorizations, the first prime factor divides a factor of the other, hence is associate to it. Cancel and continue. This proves every principal ideal domain is a unique factorization domain.
In Z[−3​], the multiplicative field norm is N(a+b−3​)=a2+3b2. The only units are ±1. There is no element of norm two, so every element of norm four is irreducible: a factorization into nonunits would require norms two and two. Consequently
4=2⋅2=(1+−3​)(1−−3​)​
(13)
is a pair of genuinely different irreducible factorizations. Neither 1+−3​ nor 1−−3​ is associate to 2.
Take the Eisenstein integers R=Z[ω], where ω=(−1+−3​)/2 and ω2+ω+1=0. Since −3​=1+2ω, the embedded subring is Z+2Zω, whose additive index in R is two. Its larger ring has Eisenstein-integer norm N(a+bω)=a2−ab+b2 and is Euclidean. Indeed, write a complex quotient in the real basis 1,ω and round both coordinates to integers. The coordinate errors u,v satisfy ∣u∣,∣v∣≤1/2, giving ∣u+vω∣2=u2−uv+v2≤3/4<1. Thus division leaves a remainder of smaller norm. A Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain, because division by a nonzero element of least norm in an ideal leaves zero remainder. This gives the requested index-two embedding into a principal ideal domain.
