The contraction mapping theorem states that a contraction mapping T:X→X on a nonempty complete metric space has a unique fixed point. Here contraction means that some 0≤q<1 satisfies d(Tx,Ty)≤qd(x,y) for all x,y.
For a proof, choose x0​ and iterate xr+1​=Txr​. Induction gives d(xr+1​,xr​)≤qrd(x1​,x0​). For m>n, the triangle inequality and a geometric series give
d(xm​,xn​)≤∑r=nm−1​qrd(x1​,x0​)≤1−qqn​d(x1​,x0​).
(4)
Thus the iterates form a Cauchy sequence, and completeness supplies a limit a∈X. A contraction mapping is continuous, so Ta=limTxn​=limxn+1​=a. If b is another fixed point, d(a,b)=d(Ta,Tb)≤qd(a,b), forcing d(a,b)=0. This proves existence and uniqueness.
For the exponential problem, use h=f∘f on the complete metric space R. Its derivative is
h′(x)=e−xe−e−x.
(5)
Putting t=e−x>0, the function te−t has maximum 1/e at t=1, as its derivative is (1−t)e−t. By the mean value theorem, ∣h(x)−h(y)∣≤e−1∣x−y∣, so h is a contraction mapping on all of R. Let a be its unique fixed point. The compositions commute, so h(f(a))=f(h(a))=f(a); uniqueness of the fixed point of h forces f(a)=a. Conversely every fixed point of f is fixed by h. Hence there is exactly one real solution of x=e−x. It lies in (0,1) because a=e−a>0 and then e−a<1. This uses the iterated contraction argument without claiming that f itself contracts on all of R.
