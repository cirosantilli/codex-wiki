Let S be a multiplicative subset, containing one and closed under products. On A×S, use
(a,s)∼(b,t)⟺u(ta−sb)=0 for some u∈S.
(4)
The relation is reflexive and symmetric. For transitivity, if u(ta−sb)=0 and v(rb−tc)=0, then multiplication and addition give uvt(ra−sc)=0. Thus it is an equivalence relation. Write a/s for the equivalence class. The localization of a ring AS​=S−1A has operations
sa​+tb​=stat+bs​,sa​tb​=stab​,−sa​=s−a​.
(5)
They are well defined. For example, replacing a/s by b/t in its sum with c/r gives cross-numerator difference r2(ta−sb), and in its product with c/r gives rc(ta−sb). The same annihilating element u kills both differences. Replacing the other input works identically. Associativity and distributivity then follow by putting all terms over the product of their denominators and using the ring identities in A. Addition and multiplication are commutative, with identities 0/1 and 1/1, and the displayed negative is an additive inverse. Hence S−1A is a commutative ring. If 0∈S, the fraction relation identifies all elements, producing the zero ring.
The canonical ring homomorphism ι:A→S−1A, a↦a/1, makes each s∈S a unit, with inverse 1/s. The universal property of localization states that any ring homomorphism h:A→C sending S into the units factors uniquely through ι. Define the factor by
h(a/s)=h(a)h(s)−1.​
(6)
If u(ta−sb)=0, then h(u) is invertible, so h(t)h(a)=h(s)h(b), proving well-definedness. The fraction operations prove that h is a ring homomorphism. Its value is forced by a/s=ι(a)ι(s)−1, so it is unique. This property characterizes the localization up to a unique compatible isomorphism.
For a prime ideal p, its complement is a multiplicative subset, and the localization at a prime ideal is Ap​=(A∖p)−1A. Fractions with numerator outside p are units; those with numerator in p form the unique maximal ideal pAp​. Thus it is a local ring.
For the nilpotent question, suppose a=0 and an=0. Its annihilator is a proper ideal, so it lies in some maximal ideal m. The fraction criterion says a/1=0 in Am​ only if sa=0 for some s∈/m, which is impossible by the choice of m. Thus a/1 is a nonzero nilpotent element in that localization. This proves reducedness is detected by prime localizations: if every prime localization is reduced, A has no nonzero nilpotent elements.
For the domain question take A=k×k with k a field. Its only prime ideals are p1​=0×k and p2​=k×0: a prime ideal must contain one of the orthogonal idempotents (1,0),(0,1), and the corresponding quotient is k. Localizing at p1​ makes (1,0) invertible and kills (0,1), giving Ap1​​≅k; similarly Ap2​​≅k. But (1,0)(0,1)=0 with both factors nonzero. Therefore domain prime localizations do not force A to be an integral domain, as expressed by domain prime localizations do not imply a domain.
