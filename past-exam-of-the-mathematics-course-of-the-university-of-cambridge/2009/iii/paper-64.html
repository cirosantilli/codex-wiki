Let x′=x−vt, t′=t for a constant relative velocity v. The Galilean transformation gives ∇′=∇ and ∂t′​=∂t​+v⋅∇. In the magnetic Galilean limit of the pre-Maxwell equations, take
B′=B,J′=J,E′=E+v×B.​
(1)
The Ampère's law without displacement current and the solenoidal constraint are immediately unchanged. For Faraday's law, the constant-velocity vector identity is ∇×(v×B)=v∇⋅B−(v⋅∇)B. Thus
−∇′×E′=−∇×E+(v⋅∇)B=∂t′​B′.
(2)
These are transformations of the magnetic nonrelativistic approximation. The exact charge-current transformation also contains the convective term −ρe​v in J′; ignoring it requires the quasineutral magnetic limit. They are not exact transformations of the full Maxwell equations.
Since u′=u−v, the combination E′+u′×B′ equals E+u×B. For an ideally conducting fluid the rest-frame electric field vanishes, so the ideal moving-conductor Ohm law is E+u×B=0. Substitution in Faraday's law gives
∂t​B=∇×(u×B),∇⋅B=0.​
(3)
The ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation requires nonrelativistic fluid motion, negligible displacement current, and negligible nonideal terms in the electric-field relation. In particular, magnetic diffusion must be slow compared with advection: the magnetic Reynolds number Rm=UL/ηm​≫1, where ηm​=(μ0​σ)−1 is the magnetic diffusivity. The single-fluid moving-conductor Ohm law must apply on the scales considered, with corrections to that relation negligible; viscosity is not itself excluded by this induction equation.
For a smooth ideal evolution, magnetic flux freezing states that the magnetic flux through every material surface is conserved and that magnetic field lines are carried with the fluid. Their connectivity is preserved: ideal smooth advection does not reconnect them. These implications are stated here without proof, as requested.

The axial magnetic flux is πbR02​, and the toroidal magnetic field supplies the twist. If a=0 the field is untwisted; if b=0 it consists of azimuthal circles rather than helical lines carrying axial flux.
A convenient globally regular magnetic vector potential has AR​=0 and
Aϕ​Az​​={bR/2,bR02​/(2R),​R<R0​,R>R0​,​={C−aR2/2,C−aR02​/2,​R<R0​,R>R0​.​​

Work in a gas region with ρ,p>0 and physical adiabatic exponent γ>1. With ξ=x/t, each time derivative is −ξ/t times differentiation in ξ, and each spatial derivative is 1/t times it. The continuity equation, Euler momentum equation and the entropy advection equation for pressure reduce to
(u−ξ)ρ′+ρu′=0,(u−ξ)u′+p′/ρ=0,(u−ξ)p′+γpu′=0.
(29)
Their coefficient determinant is (u−ξ)[(u−ξ)2−c2], where the adiabatic sound speed is c=γp/ρ​. If the determinant is nonzero, all derivatives vanish and the solution is uniform.
The apparent third characteristic u=ξ does not give a smooth fan: on such an interval u′=1, whereas continuity requires ρu′=0, impossible for ρ>0. It cannot support an isolated smooth entropy variation either. Subtracting γ times the logarithmic density equation from the logarithmic pressure equation gives (u−ξ)(ln(p/ργ))′=0. A nonzero continuous entropy derivative would force u=ξ on an interval and give the same contradiction. Thus the specific entropy is constant throughout each smooth branch.
Every nonconstant branch therefore lies in one acoustic characteristic family. Let σ=±1 denote it, with ξ=u+σc, so u−ξ=−σc. The constant entropy means p=Kργ, K>0, and c′/c=(γ−1)ρ′/(2ρ). Continuity gives u′=σcρ′/ρ=2σc′/(γ−1). Differentiating ξ=u+σc then proves
u′=γ+12​,c′=σγ+1γ−1​,s=s0​+cv​lnK=constant.​
(30)
In particular, u−2σc/(γ−1)=J is a constant Riemann invariant. All nonconstant smooth local solutions have the explicit form
u(ξ)=γ+12ξ+(γ−1)J​,c(ξ)=γ+1σ(γ−1)(ξ−J)​,ρ=(γKc2​)1/(γ−1),p=Kργ,​
(31)
on an interval where c>0. Along the left-going family (σ=−1) sound speed decreases with ξ; along the right-going family (σ=+1) it increases. These are the two self-similar ideal-gas rarefaction branches. Together with arbitrary uniform positive states they exhaust the smooth local solutions. A global solution can join them piecewise at fan edges, where derivatives generally jump, and can also have discontinuities; those do not constitute additional smooth branches.

The initial-value problem is a Riemann problem. Both the one-dimensional Euler equations and the two constant initial states are unchanged under the simultaneous scaling (x,t)↦(λx,λt) for λ>0. There is no imposed length or time scale. Rescaling a physically admissible entropy solution therefore gives another solution with exactly the same initial data. Uniqueness of the physical Riemann solution makes the two coincide, so
(ρ,u,p)(x,t)=(ρ,u,p)(x/t).​
(32)
The smooth pieces are consequently those in part (a), while shocks and contacts lie on rays x=st. Across shocks one uses the conservative mass, momentum and total-energy equations and the entropy admissibility condition, rather than extending the smooth entropy-advection equation through the discontinuity. Scale invariance by itself, without selecting the unique admissible solution, would not justify the uniqueness step.

A single rarefaction wave enforces two matching equalities: equal specific entropy and one common Riemann invariant. The inequality uR​>uL​ does not enforce either. If the entropies differ, one smooth isentropic fan cannot connect them; even if they agree, arbitrary end velocities and pressures need not satisfy either acoustic-family invariant.
The general positive-gas Riemann problem has a left acoustic wave, a middle contact discontinuity, and a right acoustic wave. Across the contact the two intermediate states have the same velocity u∗​ and pressure p∗​, but can have different mass densities and entropies. Each acoustic wave is a rarefaction wave or a shock wave, depending on the required intermediate pressure relative to its adjacent initial state. Shocks satisfy the conservative jump conditions and increase entropy. A sufficiently large pressure contrast can produce a shock on one side even when the initial velocities obey uR​>uL​.
For sufficiently rapid separation, the two rarefactions instead exhaust the gas and create vacuum between them. Their vacuum-edge velocities are uL​+2cL​/(γ−1) and uR​−2cR​/(γ−1), so a finite vacuum gap occurs when
uR​−uL​>γ−12(cL​+cR​)​.​
(36)
Equality is the limiting case in which the vacuum edges meet. Thus the additional features can be two acoustic waves, a contact carrying an entropy jump, shocks, or a vacuum region, rather than one fan joining the two original uniform states.

The equilibrium quantities ρ,p,B,Φ are real, and the gravitational Hessian Φ,ij​=∂i​∂j​Φ is symmetric. The crucial tensor property is
Vijkl​=Vklij​.​
(37)
This follows directly from the displayed expression for V: the products δij​δkl​ and δil​δjk​ are unchanged by interchanging the pairs (i,j) and (k,l); Bj​Bl​δik​ is also unchanged; and the two terms involving Bi​Bj​δkl​ and Bk​Bl​δij​ exchange with each other. All coefficients are real.
Multiply Fξ by ρη∗ and integrate by parts over all space. For admissible displacement fields, the boundary contribution at infinity vanishes: the fluid pressure and density are confined to the body, and the magnetic stress decays in the surrounding conducting medium. The conducting exterior is included in the integration. For a piecewise description, the usual displacement and traction matching conditions cancel the two opposite interface contributions; alternatively, take a smooth transition and then its limit. Thus
⟨η,Fξ⟩=−∫ρηi∗​Φ,ij​ξj​dV−∫(∂j​ηi∗​)Vijkl​(∂l​ξk​)dV.

The negligible-density exterior can be treated as a small-positive-density conducting limit when defining this weighted pairing; its magnetic contribution is retained even as its inertia becomes negligible.

Scaling the initial displacement and velocity down leaves α unchanged, so arbitrarily small initial perturbations display the same linear growth mechanism. It proves that a negative-energy direction gives exponential growth in a self-adjoint wave equation, without assuming a discrete spectrum or quoting a trial-function variational theorem.
Positive W on nonzero displacements gives energetic stability; a negative direction gives instability. For mathematical precision, a uniform bound on the mass-weighted displacement norm follows if W≥21​ωmin2​∥ξ∥ρ2​ for some ωmin​>0, giving ∥ξ∥ρ2​≤2E/ωmin2​. Strict positivity alone in an infinite-dimensional space need not supply such a uniform spectral gap; the standard magnetohydrodynamic energy principle asserts energetic stability and absence of growing modes. Cases with zero directions are marginal and require separate consideration.
