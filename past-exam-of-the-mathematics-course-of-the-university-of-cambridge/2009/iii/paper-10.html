A completely regular Hausdorff space is a Hausdorff space in which each point x outside a closed set F is separated from F by a continuous function u:X→[0,1] with u(x)=1 and u∣F​=0. Put E=Cb​(X) and define the evaluation character by δx​(g)=g(x). The weak-star topology on E′ is the topology of pointwise convergence on E: its coordinate maps p↦p(g) are continuous, and generate the topology. It is Hausdorff, and the dual unit ball is compact in it by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem.
For nonempty X, ∥δx​∥=1 by the supremum norm bound and the constant function 1. All the coordinates of x↦δx​ are continuous, so δ is continuous. Complete regularity separates distinct points, proving injectivity. To prove continuity of the inverse onto its image, let x∈O with O open in X. Separating x from X∖O gives a bounded continuous u with
δx​∈{p:p(u)>1/2},δ−1{p:p(u)>1/2}⊆O.
(11)
Such neighborhoods prove that δ is a homeomorphism onto δ(X). This qualification is essential: it is not onto the whole dual, since the zero functional is not an evaluation on a nonempty space.
Define the Stone-Čech compactification to be
βX=δ(X)​w∗⊆BE′​.​
(12)
The Banach-Alaoglu theorem makes this a compact Hausdorff space, with X identified with its dense evaluation copy. For g∈Cb​(X), the coordinate function g​(p)=p(g) is a continuous extension. Density gives ∥g​∥∞​=∥g∥∞​. Conversely any h∈C(βX) restricts to an element g of Cb​(X), and h=g​ because they agree on the dense subset X. Restriction and coordinate extension are inverse linear isometries, hence
C(βX)≅Cb​(X)isometrically.​
(13)
They also preserve products: the corresponding continuous functions agree on X and hence everywhere.
For the universal extension property, let f:X→K be continuous and K a compact Hausdorff space. Pullback defines a bounded linear operator T:C(K)→Cb​(X) by Tg=g∘f. Its dual map T′:E′→C(K)′ is weak-star continuous, since (T′p)(g)=p(Tg). Evaluation δK​:K→C(K)′ is a homeomorphism onto a compact, thus closed, subset: a compact Hausdorff space is completely regular, by the Urysohn lemma. Now T′δx​=δK​(f(x)), so
T′(βX)⊆δK​(f(X))​⊆δK​(K).
(14)
Therefore f​=δK−1​∘T′∣βX​ is the required continuous extension. Two continuous maps into the Hausdorff space K agreeing on the dense subset X agree everywhere, proving uniqueness.
If X is open in βX, then it is a locally compact space: around any x, regularity of the compact Hausdorff space βX gives an open neighborhood whose closure is compact and lies in X. Conversely, suppose X is locally compact. Choose an open neighborhood U of x in X with compact closure C⊆X. There is an open O⊆βX with O∩X=U. Density of X implies O⊆UβX, while compactness of C makes it closed in βX, so UβX⊆C. Thus x∈O⊆X. This proves
X is open in βX⟺X is locally compact.​
(15)
The empty-space cases are immediate.
The final printed assertion, for an arbitrary subset of βX, is false. Take the infinite discrete space X=N. It is not compact, so its compactification has a point p∈βN∖N. The singleton {p} is closed, hence equals its own closure. It is not open, since every nonempty open subset of βN meets the dense copy of N. This is a counterexample.
The valid result holds for subsets D⊆X, and more generally for open subsets of βX. For D⊆X, its indicator is bounded continuous because X is discrete. Its extension 1D​​ is still idempotent by density, so it takes only the values zero and one. The set H={1D​​=1} is clopen and has H∩X=D. Density then gives H=DβX. If O is open in βX, the set O∩X is dense in O, so O=O∩X is clopen by the result just proved. Consequently βX is extremally disconnected, which means the closure of every open set is open. This is the discrete Stone-Čech compactifications are extremally disconnected theorem, with the essential open-set qualification.

The Gelfand-Mazur theorem says that a nonzero complex unital Banach algebra in which every nonzero element is invertible is isomorphic to C by the scalar map λ↦λ1. With the usual normalization ∥1∥=1, that isomorphism is isometric. Commutativity need not be assumed in the theorem; it follows from the conclusion.
Every complex Banach division algebra is the scalar field. We use complex scalars throughout this question, including for C1[0,1]; the complex-algebra character and spectrum conclusions depend on that convention.

The assertion requires a proper ideal. If I=A, no proper ideal can contain it, so the literal statement without that restriction is false.
Let I be proper, and consider the partially ordered collection of proper ideals containing I. It is nonempty. The union of a chain is an ideal, because any two of its elements belong together to some member of the chain. It is still proper: if it contained 1, some member of the chain would contain 1 and equal A. Thus every chain has an upper bound in the collection. The Zorn lemma gives a maximal member, and any proper ideal containing it still belongs to the collection. Hence this is a maximal ideal of A.
Therefore every proper ideal is contained in a maximal proper ideal. This algebraic argument only needs a nonzero unital commutative ring; the Banach algebra assumptions become important for closedness of maximal ideals in part (c).

A character of an algebra is a nonzero multiplicative complex linear functional ϕ:A→C. Nonzeroness and multiplicativity force ϕ(1)=1, so it is onto C. Its kernel is an ideal, and the quotient is the field C, making the kernel maximal.
Continuity is automatic. The element a−ϕ(a)1 cannot be invertible, since applying ϕ to an inverse equation would give 0=1. On the other hand a−λ1 is invertible whenever ∣λ∣>∥a∥, by the Neumann series. Hence ∣ϕ(a)∣≤∥a∥, the automatic continuity of characters bound.
Conversely let M be a maximal ideal. Its closure is an ideal, because multiplication is continuous. That closure is proper: otherwise there would be m∈M with ∥1−m∥<1, making m invertible by the Neumann series and forcing M=A. Maximality gives M=M. The quotient A/M is consequently a Banach algebra. It is a division algebra: for any a∈/M, the larger ideal M+Aa equals A, so the class of a has an inverse. By the Gelfand-Mazur theorem, A/M≅C, and its quotient map gives a continuous character of an algebra with kernel M.
If two algebra characters have the same kernel, then a−ϕ(a)1 lies in the second kernel, giving ψ(a)=ϕ(a) for every a. Thus
ΦA​⟶MA​,ϕ⟼kerϕ​
(32)
is a bijection. This proves that maximal ideals of a commutative complex unital Banach algebra are character kernels.
