The Hall theorem for soluble groups says that for every prime set π, a finite soluble group has Hall π-subgroups; they are all conjugate, and every π-subgroup is contained in one. A Hall π-subgroup has only primes in π in its order and no primes in π in its index.
For a proof outline, induct on the group order and choose a minimal normal subgroup N. In a finite soluble group this is elementary abelian of some characteristic p: its derived subgroup is characteristic and solvability forces it to be trivial; primary components and the subgroup of elements of order dividing p are then characteristic, so minimality gives an elementary abelian p-group. Choose a Hall π-subgroup of G/N and let U be its preimage. If p∈π, U is itself a Hall π-subgroup. If p∈/π, U/N has order prime to p and coprime splitting over an elementary abelian normal subgroup supplies a complement to N in U, of the required Hall order.
Here the complement fact has a short averaging proof. Choose a section of U/N=H and write its multiplication defect as f(h,k)∈N, in additive notation. Associativity gives
f(h,k)+f(hk,t)=hf(k,t)+f(h,kt).
(9)
Since ∣H∣ is invertible on N, averaging over t expresses the defect as f(h,k)=b(h)+hb(k)−b(hk); adjusting the section by −b gives a homomorphic section. Two such complements differ by d(hk)=d(h)+hd(k). Averaging this identity gives d(h)=b−hb, so they are conjugate by b∈N. Inductive conjugacy in the quotient followed by this complement conjugacy proves Hall conjugacy. To embed an arbitrary π-subgroup Q, first put its quotient inside the chosen quotient Hall subgroup. In the coprime case Q and the corresponding subgroup of a complement are both complements to N inside NQ, hence are N-conjugate. In the other case Q≤U directly. This proves Hall conjugacy and embedding in finite soluble groups as well as existence.
Now prove the assertion about maximal subgroups by induction on ∣G∣. Suppose a nontrivial normal subgroup K is contained in A. If K≤B, maximality gives KB=G, and since K≤A this gives AB=G. If K≤B, both A/K and B/K are maximal in G/K; induction yields either their product equal to G/K or their conjugacy, lifting respectively to AB=G or to conjugacy of A,B. The same applies with their roles reversed.
It remains to suppose that no nontrivial normal subgroup of G lies in either maximum. Let K be minimal normal, an elementary abelian p-group. Since K lies in neither maximum, KA=KB=G. The intersection K∩A is normalized by A and by the abelian group K, hence by G; it is therefore trivial. Likewise K∩B=1. Thus both A and B complement K. Their action on K is irreducible, since an invariant subspace would be a proper nontrivial normal subgroup of G. It is also faithful: CA​(K) is normalized by A and centralized by K, so is a normal subgroup of G contained in A and must be trivial.
If G/K=1, these assumptions force A=B=1 and G to have prime order. Otherwise choose L>K normal in G with L/K minimal normal in G/K. It is an elementary abelian r-group. Under A≅G/K, its copy R=A∩L is a nontrivial normal subgroup of A. The prime r differs from p: a p-group acting on the characteristic-p vector space K has nonzero fixed points, by counting its orbits on K modulo p. Its fixed subspace would be A-invariant, hence all of K by irreducibility, contrary to faithfulness of R.
Therefore A∩L and B∩L are Sylow r-subgroups of L=K(A∩L)=K(B∩L). They are conjugate in L. Conjugate B so that these intersections coincide as R. Since R⊲A, NG​(R)≥A. It cannot equal G, since R would then be a nontrivial normal subgroup contained in A. Maximality gives NG​(R)=A, and the same argument gives NG​(R)=B. The conjugated maxima are equal. Hence
G=ABorA and B are conjugate in G.​
(10)
This is the factorization-or-conjugacy property of maximal subgroups of a finite soluble group.
