The canonical anticommutation relations realize the internal states as a fermionic Fock space Λ∙C3. Choosing a Clifford vacuum ∣0⟩ annihilated by all ψa​, the states are functions multiplying ∣0⟩, ψa†​∣0⟩, ψa†​ψb†​∣0⟩ with a<b, or ψ1†​ψ2†​ψ3†​∣0⟩. Thus the four fermion-number sectors in supersymmetric quantum mechanics are
Hn​=L2(R3)⊗ΛnC3,n=0,1,2,3,​
(1)
with internal dimensions 1,3,3,1. The fermion number operator N=∑a​ψa†​ψa​ has value n on Hn​. The sectors n=0,2 have even fermion parity, and n=1,3 have odd fermion parity.
Write q=∑a​ψa†​Aa​, so that the supercharge is Q=q+q†. The commutator [Aa​,Ab​] is zero because the two mixed partial derivatives of χ agree. The canonical anticommutation relations then imply q2=(q†)2=0 and
H=qq†+q†q,[H,N]=[H,q]=[H,q†]=[H,Q]=0.
(2)
The Hamiltonian operator preserves each sector, while q raises and q† lowers its degree by one. For a state in its operator domain, ⟨v,Hv⟩=∥qv∥2+∥q†v∥2≥0, so the spectrum is nonnegative. If Hv=Ev with E>0, then Qv/E​ is a state of the same energy and opposite fermion parity, and Q(Qv/E​)=E​v. This is an odd involution pairing bosonic and fermionic states and proves equal even and odd multiplicities at every positive eigenvalue.
There is also a more precise adjacent-sector pairing. On the positive-energy eigenspace,
v=E−1qq†v+E−1q†qv.
(3)
The two summands are orthogonal since q2=(q†)2=0. A state u in the image of q† obeys q†u=0, q†qu=Eu, so q/E​ maps it to a normalized partner in the next sector, with inverse q†/E​. Thus positive-energy doublets lie in H0​⊕H1​, H1​⊕H2​, or H2​⊕H3​. In particular, every positive-energy state of H0​ has a partner in H1​, and every one of H3​ has a partner in H2​; the remaining middle-sector states pair with each other. There is no requirement that all four individual sector spectra coincide. Zero-energy states satisfy qv=q†v=Qv=0 and can be unpaired. No confinement assumption was given: a continuous spectrum is possible, with the same even/odd pairing on its positive spectral subspaces.
For real χ, the adjoint operator is Aa†​=−∂a​−∂a​χ. In H0​, every ψa†​ψb​ vanishes; in the one-dimensional internal space of H3​, it equals δab​. The two scalar Hamiltonian operators are consequently
H0​=a∑​Aa†​Aa​=−Δ+∣∇χ∣2+Δχ,H3​=a∑​Aa​Aa†​=−Δ+∣∇χ∣2−Δχ.​
(4)
These are the extreme sectors of the gradient superpotential Hamiltonian in supersymmetric quantum mechanics.
For radial χ and a normalized state v0​=f(r)∣0⟩ with E>0, q†v0​=0 and Aa​f=(xa​/r)(f′−χ′f). The radial superpartner in three-dimensional supersymmetric quantum mechanics therefore has the simple form
v1​=E​f′(r)−χ′(r)f(r)​ψr†​∣0⟩,ψr†​=a∑​rxa​​ψa†​.​
(5)
It belongs to H1​; Hv1​=Ev1​ follows from [H,Q]=0, and its wavefunction normalization follows from ∥Qv0​∥2=⟨v0​,Hv0​⟩=E. Also Qv1​=E​v0​. At the origin the formula is interpreted by regular extension; its degree-one components carry the radial unit vector rather than being three independent radial scalars.
For a zero mode in the fermion-vacuum sector, the factorization of H0​ gives 0=∑a​∥Aa​f∥2. Hence ∂a​f=(∂a​χ)f, or ∂a​(e−χf)=0. The requested wavefunction is
f(r)=Ceχ(r),∣C∣−2=4π∫0∞​r2e2χ(r)dr.​
(6)
A nonzero normalizable wavefunction exists only if this integral is finite. The positive sign in the exponential is fixed by the given convention Aa​=∂a​−∂a​χ.

Odd elements of a Grassmann algebra anticommute, and in particular ψi2​=0. For variations with fixed endpoints, move each odd variation to the left before applying integration by parts. For example,
δ(ψψ˙​)=δψψ˙​+ψδψ˙​⟹δ∫ψψ˙​dt=2∫δψψ˙​dt.
(7)
The interaction varies as δψ1​​(ψ1​ψ2​)=δψ1​ψ2​ and δψ2​​(ψ1​ψ2​)=−δψ2​ψ1​. Thus variation of the action gives the Euler-Lagrange equations
x¨=UU′+U′′ψ1​ψ2​,ψ˙​1​=U′ψ2​,ψ˙​2​=U′ψ1​.​
(8)
All products of U and its derivatives commute with the odd variables. These signs are essential for this variant of Grassmann-valued classical supersymmetric mechanics.
The fermion bilinear is constant along a solution:
dtd​(ψ1​ψ2​)=ψ˙​1​ψ2​+ψ1​ψ˙​2​=U′(ψ22​+ψ12​)=0.
(9)
For the energy, direct differentiation now gives
E˙=x˙(x¨−UU′−U′′ψ1​ψ2​)−U′dtd​(ψ1​ψ2​)=0.
(10)
Similarly the given supercharge, denoted Q1​, satisfies
Q˙​1​​=x¨ψ1​+x˙ψ˙​1​−U′x˙ψ2​−Uψ˙​2​=(x¨−UU′)ψ1​=U′′ψ1​ψ2​ψ1​=0.​
(11)
A second conserved supercharge is
Q2​=x˙ψ2​−Uψ1​,​
(12)
since Q˙​2​=(x¨−UU′)ψ2​=U′′ψ1​ψ2​ψ2​=0. The two charges are independent functions on the unrestricted phase space: their coefficient matrix on (ψ1​,ψ2​) has determinant x˙2−U2, and is invertible wherever its even body is nonzero. They can become dependent on a restricted family of trajectories.
Under x˙=U and ψ2​=−ψ1​, the bilinear ψ1​ψ2​ vanishes. Differentiating the first-order equation gives x¨=U′x˙=UU′, which is the bosonic Euler-Lagrange equation. The first fermionic equation becomes ψ˙​1​=−U′ψ1​; differentiating ψ2​=−ψ1​ gives ψ˙​2​=U′ψ1​, so the second fermionic equation holds too. This establishes the first-order zero-energy solution in Grassmann supersymmetric mechanics without imposing an additional equation.
Let x0​=x(0) and ψ10​=ψ1​(0). For U=1+cx and c=0, solve d(x+1/c)/dt=c(x+1/c) and ψ˙​1​=−cψ1​ to obtain
x(t)=ectx0​+cect−1​,ψ1​(t)=e−ctψ10​,ψ2​(t)=−e−ctψ10​.​
(13)
For c=0, the nonsingular limiting answer is x(t)=x0​+t, ψ1​(t)=ψ10​, ψ2​(t)=−ψ10​​. These formulas also hold for even initial positions in the Grassmann algebra by ordinary polynomial evaluation. Finally, U(x(t))=(1+cx0​)ect, so the conserved quantities are
E=0,Q1​=2(1+cx0​)ψ10​,Q2​=−2(1+cx0​)ψ10​.​
(14)
The individual odd supercharges generally do not vanish, although their sum vanishes on this restricted solution.

Use left Grassmann derivatives and define the supersymmetric covariant derivatives D±​=∂θ±​−iθ±∂±​. Multiplication in the indicated order gives exactly the printed differential operator:
iD−​D+​=i∂θ−​∂θ+​+θ−∂θ+​∂−​−θ+∂θ−​∂+​−iθ−θ+∂−​∂+​.
(15)
Thus the equation is iD−​D+​Φ=W(Φ) in two-dimensional N=(1,1) superspace.
Under a Lorentz boost, take x±↦e±ωx± and θ±↦e±ω/2θ±. The derivatives ∂±​ have boost weights ∓1, and D±​ have weights ∓1/2. Consequently D−​D+​ has weight zero, and is a Lorentz scalar. Both Φ and the ordinary function W(Φ) are scalar superfields, so the equation is Lorentz invariant.
For supersymmetry, {∂θ​,θ}=1 and anticommutation of distinct odd coordinates give
{Ds​,Qt​}=0(s,t∈{+,−}),Q±2​=i∂±​,{Q+​,Q−​}=0.
(16)
Moving either supercharge through the two odd derivatives introduces two minus signs, hence [Qt​,D−​D+​]=0. If δΦ=ϵ+Q+​Φ+ϵ−Q−​Φ, with odd constant parameters, the chain rule for an even scalar superfield gives
δ(iD−​D+​Φ−W(Φ))=∑t=±​ϵtQt​(iD−​D+​Φ−W(Φ)).
(17)
Therefore a solution is mapped to a solution, proving supersymmetry invariance.
To compute the supersymmetric Liouville equation, order the odd coordinates as θ−θ+ and anticommute the fermionic component fields with both coordinates. In particular, ∂θ+​(θ−θ+)=−θ−. Direct application of the operator gives
iD−​D+​Φ=F+iθ−∂−​ψ−​−iθ+∂+​ψ+​−iθ−θ+∂−​∂+​ϕ.
(18)
Let N=Φ−ϕ. Only the two products of its degree-one terms survive in N2; for example, (iθ−ψ+​)(iθ+ψ−​)=θ−θ+ψ+​ψ−​. Hence N2=2θ−θ+ψ+​ψ−​ and N3=0. The exponential function expands exactly as
eΦ=eϕ[1+iθ−ψ+​+iθ+ψ−​+θ−θ+(iF+ψ+​ψ−​)].
(19)
Equating the constant terms fixes the auxiliary field, F=eϕ​. The linear and quadratic terms then give the requested component field equations of motion:
∂−​ψ−​=eϕψ+​,∂+​ψ+​=−eϕψ−​,∂−​∂+​ϕ=−e2ϕ+ieϕψ+​ψ−​.​
(20)
The signs here follow from the printed operator, the printed component expansion, and left Grassmann derivatives; no convention change has been made in eliminating the auxiliary field.

a OurBigBook.com
A two-component Weyl spinor is a complex spinor transforming in one of the two inequivalent group representations (1/2,0) and (0,1/2) of the Lorentz spinor double cover SL(2,C). An undotted left-handed field χα​ transforms by a matrix M∈SL(2,C); its complex conjugate χˉ​α˙​ transforms by M∗. Dotted and undotted indices distinguish the two chirality representations. Raising a dotted index uses the invariant epsilon tensor and converts the complex conjugate transformation into the equivalent inverse-adjoint form. Rotations act by the same SU(2) generators on the two chiralities, while boosts have opposite generators, so the two complex representations cannot be identified by a complex-linear Lorentz invariant map.
The antisymmetric invariant tensors ϵαβ​ and ϵα˙β˙​​ raise and lower spinor indices; MTϵM=ϵ proves invariance of the contracted bilinear χψ=χαψα​. The components of a fermionic Weyl spinor are odd Grassmann variables, so this bilinear is symmetric under exchange of the two spinors: the antisymmetric epsilon sign cancels the Grassmann sign. In particular, choosing ϵ12​=1 and ϵ12=−1 gives χχ=2χ1​χ2​, generally nonzero. This Weyl spinor bilinear exchange identity permits a Majorana mass term for a single suitable Weyl spinor. With mostly-minus metric signature and σˉμ=(I,−σ), the free massless kinetic term is iχ†σˉμ∂μ​χ, and its field equation of motion is σˉμ∂μ​χ=0​.

Use mostly-minus metric signature, η=diag(1,−1,−1,−1). The Weyl sigma matrices are σμ=(I,σ) and σˉμ=(I,−σ), with the three Pauli matrices
σ1​=(01​10​),σ2​=(0i​−i0​),σ3​=(10​0−1​).
(21)
The Pauli matrix multiplication law σi​σj​=δij​I+iϵijk​σk​ immediately gives σμσˉν+σνσˉμ=2ημνI: the 00 case is 2I, the mixed cases cancel, and the spatial cases give −2δij​I. The spinor indices of σαβ˙​μ​ express the correspondence between a Lorentz four-vector and a Hermitian 2×2 matrix. For X=x0I+xiσi​, detX=(x0)2−∣x∣2; X↦MXM† preserves this determinant for M∈SL(2,C), yielding the Lorentz spinor double cover.
In four-dimensional N=1 supersymmetry, the odd supercharges Qα​ and Qˉ​α˙​=Qα†​ transform as conjugate Weyl spinors. Their Super-Poincaré algebra is
{Qα​,Qˉ​β˙​​}=2σαβ˙​μ​Pμ​,{Qα​,Qβ​}={Qˉ​α˙​,Qˉ​β˙​​}=0,[Pμ​,Qα​]=[Pμ​,Qˉ​α˙​]=0.​
(22)
The ordinary translation and Lorentz generators satisfy the Poincare algebra, and the Lorentz generators act on the supercharges by the corresponding group representations on Weyl spinors. Tracing the mixed anticommutator gives P0​=41​∑α​{Qα​,Qα†​}, proving energy positivity in global supersymmetry. For a massive rest-frame state, Pμ​=(M,0,0,0), the rescaled charges Qα​/2M​ obey the canonical anticommutation relations. Acting with their adjoints builds a supermultiplet containing states of alternating fermion parity.

For two independent left-handed Weyl spinors χ and η, a Dirac mass term is
LD​=−mχαηα​−m∗χˉ​α˙​ηˉ​α˙.​
(23)
The four-component Dirac field ΨD​=(χα​,ηˉ​α˙)T has independent particle and antiparticle degrees of freedom; after a phase redefinition making m real, the same term is −mΨˉD​ΨD​. The two left-handed spinors can carry opposite charges under a conserved U(1) symmetry, χ↦eiqωχ, η↦e−iqωη. A gauge-invariant Dirac mass term contracts conjugate gauge group representations.
For one left-handed Weyl spinor, a Majorana mass term instead has the form
LM​=−21​(mχαχα​+m∗χˉ​α˙​χˉ​α˙).​
(24)
Its self-contraction need not vanish, by the Weyl spinor bilinear exchange identity. The factor 1/2 avoids double counting identical fields in variation. The associated four-component Majorana fermion ΨM​=(χα​,χˉ​α˙)T satisfies ΨMc​=ΨM​, so its particle is its own antiparticle. A Majorana mass term breaks continuous fermion-number phase symmetry for χ, and is allowed only if its gauge indices admit an invariant symmetric contraction, as for a neutral field or a real gauge group representation. An isolated field of nonzero U(1) charge cannot have it. More generally, the mass matrix of several left-handed Weyl spinors is symmetric, since χi​χj​=χj​χi​.

The barred notation denotes the antichiral supersymmetric covariant derivative. Write barred spinor indices as dotted indices, and use the stated plus-sign convention with left Grassmann derivatives:
Dˉα˙​=∂θˉα˙​+iθβσβα˙μ​∂μ​,Dα​=∂θα​+iσαβ˙​μ​θˉβ˙​∂μ​.
(25)
Their supercovariant derivative algebra with left derivatives gives {Dˉα˙​,Dˉβ˙​​}=0 and {Dα​,Dˉβ˙​​}=2iσαβ˙​μ​∂μ​. The constraint Dˉα˙​Φ=0​ defines a chiral superfield, also called an L-superfield.
To solve the constraint, put yμ=xμ+iθσμθˉ. For a left Grassmann derivative, ∂θˉα˙​(θσμθˉ)=−θβσβα˙μ​. Therefore Dˉα˙​yμ=0 and Dˉα˙​θβ=0, so Dˉ becomes ordinary differentiation with respect to θˉ at fixed y. Its general solution is the chiral-superfield component expansion
Φ=Φ(y,θ)=A(y)+2​θαχα​(y)+θθF(y).​
(26)
Here A is a complex scalar, χ a Weyl spinor, and F a complex auxiliary field. Before imposing field equations of motion this is four real bosonic and four real fermionic components. Chirality means independence of θˉ at fixed y, rather than at fixed x. The differential supercharges Qα​=∂θα​−iσαβ˙​μ​θˉβ˙​∂μ​ and Qˉ​α˙​=∂θˉα˙​−iθβσβα˙μ​∂μ​ anticommute with both covariant derivatives, so a supersymmetry transformation preserves the chiral constraint.

Here L-superfield means a left-chiral superfield: Dˉα˙​Φ=0, and its complex conjugate is an antichiral superfield, Dα​Φ∗=0. Interpret the two barred derivatives as the contracted scalar Dˉ2=Dˉα˙​Dˉα˙. The key point is chiral projection by squared supercovariant derivatives. Since the two components of Dˉ anticommute, any product of three barred derivatives repeats a component and is zero. Thus
Dˉα˙​Dˉ2X=0
(27)
for every superfield X, even when X is antichiral. In particular, Dˉ2Φ∗ is chiral.
The right side is chiral too. Because Φ is even, the graded derivation rule gives Dˉα˙​(Φ2)=(Dˉα˙​Φ)Φ+Φ(Dˉα˙​Φ)=0. Constant m and λ do not contribute derivatives. Consequently
Dˉα˙​(Dˉ2Φ∗)=Dˉα˙​(4mΦ+4λΦ2)=0,​
(28)
so applying the chiral constraint to the equation imposes no additional incompatible condition. This is the holomorphic closure of chiral superfields; a generic nonholomorphic right side would not satisfy it.
Both sides have zero Grassmann parity and are Lorentz scalars: the two dotted derivative indices are contracted with the invariant epsilon tensor, and Φ is a scalar superfield. Moreover, each covariant derivative anticommutes with every supercharge, so its squared contraction commutes with the supercharges. Hence the equation transforms covariantly under supersymmetry. It is the chiral field equation of motion of the Wess–Zumino model with superpotential W(Φ)=mΦ2/2+λΦ3/3, for which W′=mΦ+λΦ2, using the normalization in the PDF. With mass dimensions [Φ]=1, [Dˉ]=1/2, [m]=1 and [λ]=0, each side has dimension two. No component field equations of motion are needed for this consistency argument.
