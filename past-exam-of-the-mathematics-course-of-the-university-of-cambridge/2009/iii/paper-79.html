Assume small-amplitude, nonrotating, inviscid Boussinesq approximation motion about a fluid at rest, with stable background stratification. In either uniform layer let b=−gρ′/ρ∗​ be the buoyancy perturbation and N2=−(g/ρ∗​)ρ0′​(z)>0. The linear equations are

ω2=k2+m2N2k2​.​

A failed dam launches a rarefaction wave back into the reservoir and an advancing current into the initially dry downstream channel. Take the failed dam as x=0, initial depth h0​ and zero velocity for x<0, and a dry bed for x>0. Locally assume a flat, prismatic triangular section, S=Ch2. The early flow is governed by
ht​+uhx​+2h​ux​=0,ut​+uux​+ghx​=0,c=gh/2​.
(28)

ϕ(z)=V(z)(1−z/Hp​)ϕ0​V0​​,gbubbles′​≃gϕ(z).​

Neglect bubble rise relative to the water when the bubbles' terminal slip speed is much smaller than the thermal rise speed, their response time is short compared with a/W, and they remain well dispersed and retained. Pressure must remain positive and the hydrostatic expression is used only below the ocean surface.

The Boussinesq approximation retains small density differences only in the gravitational buoyancy force and uses a constant reference density in inertia and volume conservation. The entrainment coefficient α specifies the inward velocity of ambient fluid relative to the thermal boundary, ue​=αW. Turbulent interface motions engulf ambient water and mix it into the coherent rising volume.
For a spherical buoyant thermal, V=4πa3/3. To leading order in the dilute gas fraction, expansion of the gas does not dominate the total-volume balance, so fluid entrainment gives
dtdV​=4πa2αW.
(35)
But dV/dt=4πa2da/dt and dz/dt=W. Therefore, wherever the thermal is rising,
dzda​=α,a=a0​+αz,2a=2a0​+2αz.​
(36)
This growth law for a dilute bubbly thermal is independent of stratification under the constant-α model. Stratification changes the speed and eventual stopping height; it does not enter this volume-per-distance relation. In real flows the entrainment coefficient itself can vary with stratification and flow structure.

For a high-Reynolds number thermal, buoyancy force scales as ρ0​g′a3 and inertial resistance scales as ρ0​W2a2. Their balance gives W2∝g′a. It is therefore natural to write a diameter-based Froude number closure

Let E(k) be the one-dimensional turbulent energy spectrum, normalized so that 21​⟨∣u′∣2⟩=∫0∞​E(k)dk. Large energy-containing eddies receive energy from the forcing. In the inertial range, nonlinear transfer passes this energy towards smaller scales at a mean rate ϵ per unit mass. Dimensional analysis of Kolmogorov 1941 theory gives
E(k)=CK​ϵ2/3k−5/3,k0​≪k≪kd​,kd​∼(ϵ/ν3)1/4.​
(50)
The dissipation range begins near the inverse of the Kolmogorov length scale, where kinematic viscosity removes the transferred kinetic energy. The large-scale spectrum depends on the forcing; the rising low-k branch in this sketch is illustrative, rather than a universal power law.
The tube has neither homogeneous nor isotropic large-scale turbulence: gravity selects a vertical direction, its walls constrain the motion, and its density gradient varies with height. The width W bounds the transverse size of the energy-containing eddies. At sufficiently high Reynolds number, much smaller eddies may become approximately locally isotropic and exhibit an inertial range, even though the full tube flow does not satisfy the homogeneous-isotropic assumptions.
To obtain the Reynolds stress, write ui​=Ui​+ui′​ and p=P+p′, with ⟨ui′​⟩=0. Averaging the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations uses ⟨ui​uj​⟩=Ui​Uj​+⟨ui′​uj′​⟩ and gives the Reynolds-averaged momentum equation
∂t​Ui​+Uj​∂j​Ui​=−ρ0​1​∂i​P+ν∇2Ui​−∂j​⟨ui′​uj′​⟩+⟨b⟩δiz​.
(51)
Here the final term is the mean buoyancy force, with the hydrostatic reference removed. Thus averaging the quadratic momentum flux introduces the covariance ⟨ui′​uj′​⟩; multiplied by ρ0​, it has the dimensions of a stress. Its divergence transfers momentum between the mean flow and the fluctuations.
An eddy viscosity model represents this Reynolds stress by
⟨ui′​uj′​⟩=32​kT​δij​−2νT​Sij​,kT​=21​⟨ui′​ui′​⟩,Sij​=21​(∂i​Uj​+∂j​Ui​).
(52)
The isotropic term can be absorbed into the mean pressure, while the deviatoric term gives a diffusion of mean momentum with turbulent viscosity νT​∼u∗​ℓ, where u∗​ and ℓ are representative turbulent speed and mixing length. Unlike kinematic viscosity, this coefficient depends on the flow. The closure assumes a local relation between stress and mean strain; anisotropic, buoyancy-driven large eddies may require a more detailed Reynolds stress model.

This self-similar solution deliberately ignores the end conditions: its nonzero end flux cannot satisfy impermeable ends. Moreover, the Boussinesq approximation requires FC∣z∣5≪1, which fails before the formal divergence. A closed tube has only finite available potential energy, so it cannot sustain indefinitely growing turbulent kinetic energy or the assumed instantaneous flux law. End effects, changing eddy statistics and decay of the unstable stratification must eventually replace this local model. The conflicting endpoint labels in the source do not enter the calculation because end conditions have been expressly excluded.

Let U=(u,v), ζ=vx​−uy​ and D/Dt=∂t​+U⋅∇. The inviscid shallow water equations are
DtDU​+fz^×U=−g∇(h+H),DtDh​=−h∇⋅U.
(61)
Taking the vertical curl of the momentum equation, with constant f, gives Dζ/Dt=−(f+ζ)∇⋅U. Combining this with the depth equation proves
P=hf+ζ​,DtDP​=0.​
(62)
Thus shallow-water potential vorticity is materially conserved: changes in absolute vorticity are exactly compensated by stretching or compression of the layer depth.
The relation to a Bernoulli function presupposes steady flow. In that case define B=g(h+H)+21​∣U∣2. The vector identity (U⋅∇)U=∇(21​∣U∣2)+ζz^×U gives
∇B=−(f+ζ)z^×U.
(63)
Steady volume conservation allows a transport stream function with hU=(ψy​,−ψx​). Hence z^×(hU)=∇ψ, so
∇B=−P∇ψ,dψdB​=−P.​
(64)
In particular, U⋅∇B=0: the Bernoulli function is constant along each streamline. Where the flow permits a single-valued dependence on the stream function, the displayed derivative describes how that constant changes between neighbouring streamlines. Material conservation of shallow-water potential vorticity carries P=0 from the reservoir into the channel. At leading order in a long, slowly varying channel, v and vx​ are negligible, so ζ≃−uy​. Zero absolute vorticity therefore implies uy​=f. Integration from y=0, followed by cross-channel geostrophic balance, gives the zero-potential-vorticity rotating channel flow:
u(x,y)=u1​(x)+fy,h(x,y)=h1​(x)−gf​[u1​(x)y+21​fy2].​
(66)
In particular, u2​=u1​+fb and h2​=h1​−(fb/g)(u1​+fb/2). The velocity increases towards the shallower wall. Differentiating the depth-based specific energy confirms
∂y​(h+2gu2​)=hy​+guuy​​=−gfu​+gfu​=0.
(67)
This cross-channel constancy is also the P=0 consequence of ∇B=−P∇ψ.
For a reservoir of finite depth, exact P=0 requires relative vorticity ζ=−f, an anticyclonic rotation cancelling the planetary vorticity. A reservoir at rest in the rotating frame has ζ=0 and P=f/h, so it does not have exactly zero shallow-water potential vorticity. A very deep, nearly quiescent reservoir can nevertheless supply the approximation P≃0 because f/h is small. The idealization must describe vanishing absolute vorticity or this deep-reservoir limit, rather than ordinary finite-depth rest in the rotating frame.

A hydraulic control of a zero-PV rotating channel is a critical section separating the upstream branch, where long-wave disturbances can propagate upstream, from the downstream branch, where they cannot. In the steady specific-energy description it is a stationary point of E with respect to depth at fixed volume flux and local width. The control therefore fixes the discharge for a given upstream Bernoulli function and channel geometry; an arbitrary downstream condition cannot generally be imposed independently on the upstream reservoir.
Evaluate all quantities at x=0 and keep Q,b fixed while differentiating. With Δ=fb, the preceding branch gives
∂h1​∂u1​​=Δg​(1−h2​h1​​),∂h1​∂E​=1+Δu1​​(1−h2​h1​​).
(72)
At the hydraulic control of a zero-PV rotating channel, this derivative vanishes. Using h1​−h2​=Δv/g reduces it to u1​v=gh2​, where v=u1​+Δ/2. Equivalently,
v(v+2Δ​)=gh1​,vc​=gh1​+16Δ2​​−4Δ​.
(73)

Qc​=gb​[gh1​+16b2f2​​−4bf​]3.​
(74)
