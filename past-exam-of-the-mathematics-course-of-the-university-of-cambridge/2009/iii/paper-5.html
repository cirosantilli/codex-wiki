A topological group is a group equipped with a topology for which multiplication G×G→G and inversion G→G are continuous maps. The product uses the product topology. Translation and inversion are therefore homeomorphisms. Hausdorffness is sometimes included in the definition; the two open-subgroup arguments below do not require it.
For the inverse system, take a directed set I, topological spaces Xi​, and continuous maps fij​:Xj​→Xi​ for i≤j, with fii​=id and fik​=fij​fjk​. An inverse limit is a space X with compatible continuous maps πi​:X→Xi​ such that every compatible family qi​:Y→Xi​ factors uniquely as qi​=πi​q through a continuous map q:Y→X. Existence follows from the concrete construction
X={(xi​)∈∏i∈I​Xi​:fij​(xj​)=xi​ whenever i≤j},
(1)
with the subspace topology of the product topology. The only possible map q is q(y)=(qi​(y))i​. Compatibility puts its image in X, and coordinatewise continuity makes it continuous. This proves the universal property of an inverse limit, even when X is empty. Given two inverse limits, their universal properties supply maps each way; the composites have the same coordinates as the identity, so uniqueness makes them identities. The inverse limit exists and is unique up to the unique homeomorphism respecting its projections.
A profinite group can be defined as an inverse limit of finite discrete groups, or as a compact Hausdorff space that is a totally disconnected space, equipped with continuous group operations. For the first implication, use the Tychonoff theorem. The compatibility equalities define a closed subgroup of the compact Hausdorff product. Two distinct points are distinguished by a finite discrete coordinate, so no connected subset contains both. Consequently the inverse limit is a compact Hausdorff totally disconnected topological group.
For the converse, use the topological result that a compact Hausdorff totally disconnected space has a basis of clopen sets. Choose a clopen set U containing 1 inside any specified identity neighbourhood. For each u∈U, continuity gives an identity neighbourhood Vu​ and an open neighbourhood Wu​ of u with Vu​Wu​⊆U. Finitely many Wu​ cover the compact set U. Intersect their Vu​ and its inverse to obtain a symmetric identity neighbourhood V with VU⊆U. Symmetry also gives V−1U⊆U, and hence vU=U for every v∈V. The set
H={g∈G:gU=U}
(2)
is a subgroup containing V, so it is open. It is contained in U, since 1∈U and h=h⋅1∈hU=U. By part (ii), H has finite index. Its normal core of a subgroup is a finite intersection of conjugates of H, hence an open normal subgroup N contained in U. This proves the open normal subgroup basis of a profinite group.
The natural group homomorphism
G⟶lim​N⊴o​G​G/N
(3)
is injective because the intersection of these N is {1}. For a compatible family of cosets, any finitely many have nonempty intersection: their intersection subgroup is another indexing subgroup, and its prescribed coset lies in each. The finite intersection property and compactness give a point belonging to all the cosets, proving surjectivity. Finally a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism. The two definitions of profinite group are equivalent.

The cosets of an open subgroup H form an open cover of the compact space G. A finite subcover exists. Since distinct cosets are disjoint, that subcover must contain every coset. Thus [G:H] is finite. Neither normality nor Hausdorffness is needed for this argument.

For a profinite group, its profinite Frattini subgroup is
Φ(G)=⋂{M:M is a maximal proper open subgroup of G}.
(5)
Use G for an empty intersection. This is a closed normal subgroup, invariant under continuous group automorphisms, since these automorphisms permute the maximal open subgroups. Let H=⟨X⟩​. If H=G, its image certainly topologically generates G/Φ(G). Conversely, suppose H=G. For g∈/H, the open normal subgroup basis of a profinite group gives N⊴o​G with g∈/HN: choose a sufficiently small neighbourhood gN disjoint from the closed subgroup H. The proper subgroup HN/N of the finite group G/N lies in a maximal proper subgroup. Its preimage M is a maximal proper open subgroup of G, contains H, and contains Φ(G). Since HΦ(G) is compact and therefore closed, density of the image of X in G/Φ(G) would force HΦ(G)=G, contradicting HΦ(G)≤M<G. Generation can therefore be tested modulo the profinite Frattini subgroup.
For a finite p-group, every maximal proper subgroup is normal and has index p. One proof uses the normalizer condition for finite p-groups: maximality forces its normalizer to be the whole group, and its simple p-group quotient has order p. Therefore every such maximal subgroup contains powers and group commutators. Conversely, the quotient by the algebraic Gp[G,G] is an elementary abelian p-group; its nonzero elements are separated by linear maps to Fp​. Their kernels give maximal subgroups. This proves the finite Frattini subgroup formula.
Now take a pro-p group and put D=Gp[G,G]​, retaining the closure of the algebraically generated subgroups. A maximal open subgroup is the preimage of a maximal subgroup of a finite p-group quotient, so it contains D. If g∈/D, some finite quotient of the profinite group G/D detects gD. That quotient is elementary abelian, and a linear functional nonzero at gD gives a continuous group homomorphism G→Fp​ whose kernel is a maximal open subgroup missing g. Hence
Φ(G)=Gp[G,G]​.​
(6)
The closure bar is present in the original PDF; the converted TeX fraction is an extraction error.
For the finite-index assertion, first pass from the arbitrary abstract subgroup K to its normal core of a subgroup C. The action on the finitely many cosets of K shows [G:C]<∞, without assuming that K or C is closed. Write m=[G:C]=prq with (p,q)=1, and let Xm​={hm:h∈G}. By Lagrange theorem, Xm​⊆C. For N⊴o​G, the quotient G/N is a finite p-group of order, say, ps. Choose an integer a with aq≡1(modps). Then
(ga)mN=gprN,
(7)
so gpr∈Xm​N for every such N. The set Xm​ is the continuous image of the compact space G, hence is closed. Consequently gpr∈⋂N​Xm​N=Xm​⊆C. Every element of the abstract finite quotient G/C thus has order dividing pr. By Cauchy theorem for groups, no prime other than p divides its order. Therefore [G:C], and its divisor [G:K], are powers of p. This proof does not assume continuity of the finite quotient map.
To prove openness under finite generation, first establish commutator collection with fixed generators in nilpotent groups. If a nilpotent group P is generated by a1​,…,ad​, every element of P′ is
[x1​,a1​]⋯[xd​,ad​].
(8)
Induct on the nilpotency class c. The assertion is trivial for c=1. Apply the induction hypothesis in P/γc​(P); the remaining error lies in the central subgroup γc​(P)=[γc−1​(P),P]. Since commutators in this last layer are central, collecting against words in the ai​ expresses the error as ∏i​[yi​,ai​] with yi​∈γc−1​(P). Also [P′,γc−1​(P)]≤γc+1​(P)=1. Thus replacing each xi​ by xi​yi​ multiplies the desired product by precisely that central error, proving the formula.
Let a1​,…,ad​ now topologically generate G. Every finite continuous quotient is a nilpotent group, so the formula holds there with these fixed generators. For z∈[G,G]​, the sets of tuples (x1​,…,xd​)∈Gd solving z≡∏i​[xi​,ai​](modN) are nonempty closed subsets of a compact space. They have the finite intersection property, by replacing finitely many N by their intersection. A common tuple solves the equality in G. It follows that the algebraic commutator subgroup is the compact image of Gd under this product map, and is closed.
Its quotient A=G/[G,G] is a topologically finitely generated abelian pro-p group. The continuous map Zpd​→A taking (λi​) to ∏i​aˉiλi​​ is onto, because its compact image contains a dense generating subgroup. Therefore A/pA is generated as an elementary abelian group by at most d elements, and pA is compact. The preimage Gp[G,G] of pA is closed of finite index, hence open: its complement is a finite union of closed cosets. The finite-index result and the maximal-subgroup property of finite p-groups show that any abstract index-p subgroup of G is normal. It contains this open subgroup, so is itself open.
Finally, a finite-index normal subgroup C has finite p-group quotient. If [G:C]>1, choose a normal index-p subgroup in that quotient and call its preimage H. The preceding argument makes H open. An open subgroup of a finitely generated pro-p group is finitely generated: intersect a finitely generated dense abstract subgroup with H, use the Schreier lemma, and observe that the intersection is dense in the open subgroup H. Induction on [G:C] now makes C open in H, hence in G. Returning to the normal core of a subgroup, K is a union of open cosets of C. Every finite-index subgroup of a finitely generated pro-p group is open.

The correspondence concerns uniform pro-p groups and powerful Lie lattices, with their natural morphisms. Put ϵ=1 for odd p and ϵ=2 for p=2. A powerful pro-p group is a pro-p group whose closed commutator subgroup lies in its closed pϵ-power subgroup. It is uniform when it is topologically finitely generated and all successive indices in its lower p-series are equal. Equivalently, a finitely generated powerful pro-p group is uniform precisely when it is a torsion-free group. A powerful Lie algebra over p-adic integers means a Lie algebra over Zp​ that is free of finite rank as a module and satisfies
[L,L]⊆pϵL.
(18)
Here a Lie algebra over a ring is a module with an alternating bilinear Lie bracket satisfying the Jacobi identity. Finite freeness is an essential part of the lattice convention. The zero-bracket module Fp​ satisfies the bracket inclusion but has torsion, and its additive group is not uniform. Thus allowing arbitrary torsion modules would make the categorical assertion false.
The basic structural facts behind the construction can be seen from the power layers. Write Pn​=Gpn with P0​=G. Collection for a powerful pro-p group gives
[Pr​,Ps​]⊆Pr+s+ϵ​,Pn​={gpn:g∈G}.
(19)
For odd p, the power lifting is exactly the successive-correction argument of Question 3, followed by compactness; at p=2 the stronger fourth-power commutator condition makes the corresponding collection corrections work. The maps between successive layers are surjective linear maps. Uniformity makes their finite dimensions equal, so these maps are isomorphisms. Comparing the first layer in which two elements differ now shows that the power map Pn​→Pn+1​ is injective, while the power-set formula makes it surjective. Thus all the roots used below exist uniquely in the indicated layer. If g1​,…,gd​ lift a basis of G/P1​, the images of gipn​ form a basis of Pn​/Pn+1​. Successively choosing their coefficients modulo p gives the unique ordered expression
g=g1λ1​​⋯gdλd​​,λi​∈Zp​.
(20)
Existence follows by lifting one layer at a time and taking a limit; uniqueness follows by comparing the first nonzero coefficient layer. In particular [G:Pn​]=pnd and these layers form a neighbourhood basis. These are the power-layer and coordinate facts needed in the proof, rather than an assumption that an arbitrary pro-p group has unique roots.
On the underlying set of G, set the additive zero to 1, the additive negative of x to x−1, and define
λ⋅L​x=xλ,x+L​y=limn→∞​(xpnypn)p−n,[x,y]L​=limn→∞​[xpn,ypn]p−2n.
(21)
The p-adic integers act through limits of integer powers in the closed cyclic subgroup generated by x. In the second formula the product belongs to Pn​; in the third the group commutator belongs to P2n+ϵ​. The symbols p−n and p−2n denote the unique group roots, not unrestricted division in a group. We next justify convergence and the Lie algebra laws together.
A useful concrete tool is a complete normed group algebra containing G. In its completed group algebra, put bi​=gi​−1. Ordered-coordinate uniqueness gives unique expansions ∑α∈Nd​cα​b1α1​​⋯bdαd​​ with cα​∈Zp​. To see why, first work modulo p and Pn​: the ordered products of the gi​ with exponents below pn form the regular basis, and the change to the corresponding products of gi​−1 is triangular with diagonal entries one. Take inverse limits and then lift the coefficient digits successively in powers of p. Define the weight
v(∑α​cα​bα)=infα​{vp​(cα​)+ϵ∣α∣}.
(22)
It is a genuine separated, submultiplicative weight. Indeed reordering bj​bi​ produces a group-commutator correction in Pϵ​; the binomial expansion of a pϵth power gives that correction weight at least 2ϵ, so collection never decreases total weight. Also v(pc)=1+v(c). Inverting p and completing therefore gives a Banach algebra B over Qp​. For g∈G, ordered coordinates show
v(g−1)=ϵ+max{n:g∈Pn​},
(23)
with value infinity at g=1. Thus the induced topology on G is exactly its original topology. This construction supplies the ambient algebra without presupposing the uniform pro-p group and powerful Lie lattice correspondence. The weighted construction, including the case p=2, is described in Equivariant D-modules on rigid analytic spaces, Section 6.5.
Since ϵ>1/(p−1), the p-adic logarithm and p-adic exponential converge on the relevant balls in B:
log(1+b)=∑k≥1​k(−1)k+1bk​,exp(a)=∑k≥0​k!ak​.
(24)
The estimates vp​(k!)≤k/(p−1) and vp​(k)≤logp​k show that term weights tend to infinity. Formal substitution is legitimate there and proves that the two maps are inverse. They preserve the leading weight. In particular log embeds the compact set G homeomorphically into B, and log(gpn)=pnlogg.
Put a=logx and b=logy. Expanding the convergent Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula in B gives
p−nlog(xpnypn)⟶a+b,p−2nlog([xpn,ypn])⟶ab−ba.

The first expansion has linear part pn(a+b) and all other terms have degree at least two in pna,pnb. The second has leading term p2n(ab−ba) and all remaining terms have degree at least three. After division by the displayed powers of p, the extra factor of pn forces those errors to zero. The normalized expressions are logarithms of the unique roots already specified. Since logG is compact and closed in B, both limits belong to logG. Scalar multiplication also remains in logG by continuity of powers. Consequently logG is a module over Zp​, closed under the associative-algebra Lie bracket ab−ba. Bilinearity, alternation and the Jacobi identity follow by expanding products in the associative algebra B. Transporting these operations back gives exactly LG​ above.
The commutator-layer inclusion places every normalized bracket root in Pϵ​. Since logPϵ​=pϵlogG and this set is compact, passage to the limit proves [LG​,LG​]⊆pϵLG​. A nontrivial relation among the ei​ could be divided by the smallest power of p among its coefficients, contradicting their independence modulo p. Hence LG​ is free of rank d, proving that it is a powerful Lie lattice.
Conversely, let L be a powerful Lie lattice. In L⊗Zp​​Qp​, define

More precisely, the degree-m component can be expressed as an integral Lie polynomial divided by a number of p-adic valuation at most ⌊(m−1)/(p−1)⌋. One way to obtain this estimate is to bound the associative coefficients of log(expXexpY) and use an integral ordered basis of the free Lie algebra. The denominator bound vp​(m!)+⌊logp​sp​(m)⌋, where sp​(m) is the base-p digit sum, is at most ⌊(m−1)/(p−1)⌋. A precise primary reference for that denominator estimate is Smallest common denominators for the homogeneous components of the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff series. A length-m Lie bracket in L lies in pϵ(m−1)L. Thus the degree-m term belongs to
pϵ(m−1)−⌊(m−1)/(p−1)⌋L.
(27)
These exponents are nonnegative and tend to infinity; for m≥2 they are at least one. This proves integral convergence, continuity and x∗y≡x+y(modpL). Associativity follows from the formal identity exp(BCH(X,Y))=expXexpY in the completed universal enveloping algebra. Substitution of three elements is justified by the same valuation estimates, so the two associative expressions agree. The identity is 0, the inverse is −x, and x∗k=kx because every nonlinear term involving only x vanishes.
For powerfulness, formal conjugation gives
exp(−x)exp(−y)exp(x)=exp(−e−adxy).
(28)
The difference δ=y−e−adxy lies in pϵL: its kth term has valuation at least kϵ−vp​(k!)≥ϵ for k≥1. The group commutator is therefore (−y+δ)∗y. Its linear term is δ. Every nonlinear Lie term contains δ, since it vanishes when δ=0, and the extra divisibility makes all those terms lie in pϵL too. Hence [(L,∗),(L,∗)]⊆pϵL. The ideals pnL are open normal subgroups for ∗, and their finite quotients have order pnd. The group is consequently a pro-p group. Its power sets are exactly pnL, its lower-series indices are constantly pd, and a basis of L topologically generates it by the same successive digit lifting. The BCH group (L,∗) is uniform.
To check the inverse constructions, powers in the Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula group are ordinary multiples in L. Therefore
p−nBCH(pnx,pny)⟶x+y,
(29)
and the similarly normalized group commutator tends to [x,y]. The limit constructions recover the original Lie algebra exactly. In the other direction, the ambient Banach algebra construction gives log(xy)=BCH(logx,logy), so the recovered operation is the original multiplication of G.
Finally a continuous group homomorphism between uniform pro-p groups preserves integer powers and powers with exponents in the p-adic integers, preserves the unique roots in the power layers, and commutes with their limits. It therefore becomes a linear Lie algebra homomorphism. Conversely a linear Lie algebra homomorphism between finite free modules is continuous and preserves every term of the convergent Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula, hence is a continuous group homomorphism for ∗. The constructions preserve identity maps and composition, and the inverse identifications are natural. They are mutually inverse functors, giving the stated category correspondence with the finite-free lattice convention.

A topological group module A is a topological abelian group with a jointly continuous action of G by group automorphisms. Its continuous cochains with topological coefficients are
C0(G,A)=A,Cn(G,A)=Cont(Gn,A)(n≥1),

(dc)(g1​,…,gn+1​)=​g1​c(g2​,…,gn+1​)+i=1∑n​(−1)ic(g1​,…,gi​gi+1​,…,gn+1​)+(−1)n+1c(g1​,…,gn​).​

For n=0, this means (da)(g)=ga−a. Continuity of multiplication and of the action ensures that d preserves continuous cochains. Expanding d(dc) gives cancellation in pairs: the two orders of multiplying neighbouring arguments agree by associativity, and the two ways of applying the action agree by its action law. Thus d2=0. The cocycles are Zn=ker(d:Cn→Cn+1) and the coboundaries are Bn=im(d:Cn−1→Cn), with B0=0. Define
Hn(G,A)=Zn(G,A)/Bn(G,A).​
(32)
For general topological coefficients this is an algebraic quotient of the continuous cochains; its optional quotient topology need not be Hausdorff. For discrete coefficients it is the usual continuous cohomology of a profinite group. In degree zero it is the subgroup of invariant elements.
Give the discrete prime field Fp​ its trivial G-action. The one-cocycle condition becomes c(gh)=c(g)+c(h) and every one-coboundary is zero. Hence
H1(G,Fp​)=Homcont​(G,Fp​)=Homcont​(G/Φ(G),Fp​).
(33)
Every such group homomorphism kills powers and group commutators, hence their closure Φ(G), and every continuous functional on the quotient pulls back. If G is topologically finitely generated, V=G/Φ(G) is a finite elementary abelian group. To verify finiteness without assuming it, the closure of a subgroup generated by r elements of this abelian exponent-p quotient has at most pr elements; a finite subgroup is already closed. Thus V is a finite-dimensional vector space. Question 2 says that a generating family of G is minimal precisely when its images give a basis of V. The dual space has the same finite dimension. This proves
d(G)=dimFp​​H1(G,Fp​)for finitely generated G.​
(34)
There is a genuine missing hypothesis if the printed assertion is read for arbitrary pro-p groups and d(G) means the least cardinality of any dense generating family. Here is an explicit infinite-rank failure of the first-cohomology generator formula. Let I={0,1}N and G=∏r∈I​Fp​. Enumerate the finite binary strings as s1​,s2​,… and define gn​(r)=1 if sn​ is a prefix of r, and 0 otherwise. For finitely many distinct sequences r1​,…,rk​, choose long enough prefixes to distinguish each individually. The corresponding gn​ project onto the coordinate unit vectors of Fpk​, so ⟨gn​:n≥1⟩ surjects onto every finite coordinate projection and is dense in the product topology. No finite family can generate densely, since a finitely generated abelian exponent-p subgroup is finite and closed. Thus d(G)=ℵ0​. On the other hand, continuity of a linear functional to Fp​ means its kernel contains a basic neighbourhood, so it depends on finitely many coordinates. The coordinate projections are consequently a basis of the continuous dual space, giving
dimFp​​H1(G,Fp​)=∣I∣=2ℵ0​>ℵ0​.
(35)
The unrestricted infinite-rank formula is false. A convention requiring a generating basis tending to the identity is different from the printed unrestricted topological generator count; the finite-rank identity proved above has no such ambiguity.
For finite X, a free pro-p group Fp​(X) is characterized by the universal property that every map X→P to a pro-p group extends uniquely to a continuous group homomorphism Fp​(X)→P. It is the pro-p completion of the abstract free group on X: each map to a finite p-group factors through one of the defining finite quotients, and passing to the inverse limit gives the extension to any pro-p group; uniqueness follows from density. This unrestricted universal property can also define the completion on an arbitrary set X. When using an infinite basis required to tend to the identity, one instead imposes the universal property only for maps tending to the identity. Only the finite-rank version is needed for the following argument.
A finite pro-p presentation is an isomorphism
G≅Fp​(x1​,…,xa​)/⟨R1​,…,Rt​⟩Fp​​.
(36)
There are finitely many generators and finitely many relators, and the denominator is their closed normal closure. The relators are elements of the free pro-p group; they may be limits of ordinary words. That distinction is important when writing conjugation relations below.
Now suppose K⊴o​G has a finite pro-p presentation with generators k1​,…,ka​ and relators R1​,…,Rt​. The quotient Q=G/K is a finite p-group. Choose a finite finite pro-p presentation Q=⟨yˉ​1​,…,yˉ​b​∣S1​,…,Sc​⟩. Such a presentation exists, for example by using its entire finite multiplication table. Lift the quotient generators to yj​∈G. For every i,j, choose elements Wij​,Vij​ of the free pro-p group on the xi​ that map respectively to yj−1​ki​yj​ and yj​ki​yj−1​ in K. Choose Uℓ​(x) mapping to Sℓ​(y), which lies in K. Form the finite finite pro-p presentation
P=⟨x1​,…,xa​,y1​,…,yb​∣​Rh​(x)=1,yj−1​xi​yj​=Wij​(x),yj​xi​yj−1​=Vij​(x),Sℓ​(y)=Uℓ​(x)⟩pro-p​.​
(37)
All listed relations hold in G, giving a continuous surjective group homomorphism P→G: its compact image contains K and maps onto Q.
Let X be the closed subgroup of P topologically generated by the xi​. Both conjugation directions were included, so each yj​ normalizes X. The xi​ also normalize it, and its closed normalizer contains the generators of P. Thus X⊴P. The relations Rh​ give a continuous surjection K→X. Its composite with X→K induced by P→G fixes every ki​, and hence is the identity. Therefore the surjection K→X is injective as well, so X≅K. On quotienting by X, the conjugation and kernel relations disappear, and the remaining presentation is exactly ⟨y1​,…,yb​∣S1​,…,Sc​⟩pro-p​=Q. Thus P/X→G/K is an isomorphism. The kernel of P→G lies in X and meets X trivially, so is trivial. Consequently P≅G, proving
K⊴o​G finitely presented⟹G finitely presented.​
(38)
