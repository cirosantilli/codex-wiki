In this part identify T with the complex unit circle by t↦eit. Write the finite abelian group G additively and let G=Hom(G,T), with pointwise multiplication, be the character group of a finite abelian group of G. A linear character has modulus one, since its values have finite order. We first prove the extension and counting facts needed for Fourier analysis on a finite abelian group.
If H≤G, χ∈H, and x∈/H, let d be the least positive integer with dx∈H. Choose z∈T with zd=χ(dx) and define
χ​(h+jx)=χ(h)zj.
(7)
This is well-defined: two representations differ by j−j′=qd and h−h′=−qdx, whose contributions cancel. It is a group homomorphism and extends χ. Conversely every extension has this form, and the equation for z has exactly d distinct roots. Since [H+⟨x⟩:H]=d, successive adjunctions prove that each linear character of H has exactly [G:H] extensions. Taking H={0} gives ∣G∣=∣G∣​. This is the extension of a character across a cyclic quotient, proved here without a structure theorem. Extending the linear character sending a nonzero element g of order d to e2πi/d also proves that linear characters separate points of G.
Use the normalized inner product ⟨f,g⟩=∣G∣−1∑x∈G​f(x)g(x)​. For a nontrivial linear character χ, choose a with χ(a)=1. Translation of the sum gives ∑x​χ(x)=χ(a)∑x​χ(x), so that sum is zero. Applying this to χψ​ gives
⟨χ,ψ⟩=1{χ=ψ}​.​
(8)
There are ∣G∣ such orthonormal vectors in the ∣G∣-dimensional space CG, so they form an orthonormal basis. Define the Fourier coefficients by f​(χ)=∣G∣−1∑x​f(x)χ(x)​. Expanding in that basis proves Fourier inversion on a finite group and the Parseval identity on a finite group:
f(x)=χ∈G∑​f​(χ)χ(x),⟨f,g⟩=χ∈G∑​f​(χ)g​(χ)​.​
(9)
For normalized convolution (f∗g)(x)=∣G∣−1∑y​f(y)g(x−y), changing the finite summation variables yields f∗g​(χ)=f​(χ)g​(χ). Translation f(x)↦f(x+a) multiplies its Fourier coefficient by χ(a). These formulas fix all normalization and conjugation conventions.
The PDF's double-hat identification is canonical Pontryagin duality: define e:G→G by ex​(χ)=χ(x). This is a group homomorphism and is injective because linear characters separate points. Applying the already proved character-counting result to G shows that both groups have size ∣G∣, so
G≅Gcanonically by evaluation.​
(10)
The TeX loses one hat. For completeness, the single-dual isomorphism G≅G also holds, but is generally noncanonical. Here is a proof including the needed finite cyclic decomposition. Choose an element a of maximal order d. Every element order divides d: for two elements, extract their prime-power components by integer multiples: if an element has order d and pα is the exact power of p in d, multiplying it by d/pα gives order pα. Then combine the components with the larger order for each prime. Components of coprime orders have a sum of product order, because their cyclic subgroups have trivial intersection. This constructs an element of order the least common multiple of the two original orders, which cannot exceed maximal d; hence each order divides d.
Extend the primitive linear character of ⟨a⟩ to χ:G→T. All its values are dth roots because all element orders divide d. Define ρ(g)=ja when χ(g)=e2πij/d. This is a group homomorphism fixing ⟨a⟩, and g=ρ(g)+(g−ρ(g)) shows G=⟨a⟩⊕kerρ. Induction on group size decomposes G into finite cyclic groups. A cyclic group of order dj​ has precisely the linear characters j↦e2πimj/dj​ and hence a character group of a finite abelian group isomorphic to itself. The dual of a finite direct product of groups is the product of its factor duals, since a linear character is determined by its restrictions. This proves G≅G after choices of cyclic factors and generators, without using an unproved finite-abelian-group structure theorem.
