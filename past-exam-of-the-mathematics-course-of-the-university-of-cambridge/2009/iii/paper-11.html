On any compact set avoiding the integers, write
z−n1​+n1​=n(z−n)z​.
(1)
For all sufficiently large ∣n∣, uniformly on that compact set this is O(∣n∣−2). The summands in the higher series are O(∣n∣−k) for k≥2. The Weierstrass M-test therefore gives locally uniform convergence away from the integer poles and permits termwise differentiation. Near an integer m, isolate its singular summand; all the others converge uniformly on a small neighborhood. Thus the first function has a simple pole of residue one at each integer, and the kth function has a pole of order k there with principal part (z−m)−k. There are no other singularities, so all are meromorphic functions.
Pairing positive and negative indices is legitimate because the regularized first series converges absolutely. It becomes
ε1​(z)=z1​+∑n=1∞​z2−n22z​.
(2)
Here is a direct derivation of its cotangent partial-fraction expansion. Fix z∈/Z and integrate F(ζ)=πcot(πζ)/(ζ2−z2) around the square with real and imaginary coordinates ±R, R=N+1/2, taking N large enough to enclose ±z. On the vertical sides ∣cotπζ∣≤1, and on the horizontal sides it is at most coth(πR). Thus the cotangent is uniformly bounded and ∣ζ2−z2∣≥R2−∣z∣2, so the contour integral is O(R−1)→0.
The residues at integers are 1/(n2−z2); the two residues at z and −z sum to πcot(πz)/z. The residue theorem and absolute convergence of the integer residue sum consequently give
0=zπcotπz​+∑n∈Z​n2−z21​,ε1​(z)=πcotπz.
(3)
Using the exponential definitions of sine and cosine now yields, for w=e2πiz,
φ1​(w)=πiw−1w+1​,ε1​(z)=φ1​(e2πiz).​
(4)
The same locally uniform convergence gives εk′​=−kεk+1​, including k=1. Hence if εk​(z)=φk​(e2πiz), the chain rule gives
φk+1​(w)=−k2πi​wφk′​(w).​
(5)
This is a rational function whenever φk​ is, proving the requested descent for every k by induction, with
φ2​(w)=−(w−1)24π2w​.​
(6)
In particular periodicity is a consequence of the explicit descent, rather than an assumption about the summation order.
Finally, for ∣z∣<1 expand each paired summand as a geometric series:
z2−n22z​=−2∑j=0∞​n2j+2z2j+1​.
(7)
On ∣z∣≤r<1 the double series converges absolutely, since its absolute sum is at most 2r∑n≥1​n−2/(1−r2). Interchanging the sums and using the Riemann zeta function gives the Laurent series
ε1​(z)=z1​−2j=0∑∞​ζ(2j+2)z2j+1,0<∣z∣<1.​
(8)
Thus the z−1 coefficient is one; the positive odd coefficients are −2ζ(2j+2), and all other coefficients vanish.

Zeros are counted with their prescribed finite multiplicities, and the function is not identically zero. The criterion for zero sets in the unit disc is local finiteness: no point of the disc is an accumulation point of the zero sequence, and no point has infinite multiplicity. Necessity follows from the identity theorem for holomorphic functions. For sufficiency, factor a finite number of zeros at zero as zm. For an infinite sequence of remaining zeros an​, local finiteness implies ∣an​∣→1. With Weierstrass elementary factors
En​(t)=(1−t)exp(∑j=1n​jtj​),
(9)
use f(z)=zm∏n​En​(z/an​). On each compact subdisc, eventually ∣z/an​∣≤q<1 and the logarithm of the tail factor is −∑j>n​(z/an​)j/j, bounded in modulus by qn+1/((n+1)(1−q)). These bounds are summable, so infinite product convergence from logarithmic tails gives locally uniform convergence and nonvanishing away from the listed zeros. Its finite initial factors give exactly their multiplicities. For a finite sequence an ordinary polynomial suffices.
Use the curvature-minus-one hyperbolic metric, with length element 2∣dz∣/(1−∣z∣2). Integrating radially gives
ρ(0,a)=log1−∣a∣1+∣a∣​,21​(1−∣a∣)≤e−ρ(0,a)=1+∣a∣1−∣a∣​≤1−∣a∣.
(10)
Therefore the printed sum condition is equivalent to the Blaschke condition ∑n​(1−∣an​∣)<∞.
To prove necessity for bounded f, first suppose f(0)=0 and choose a radius r<1 with no zero on its circle. Remove the finitely many factors (z−an​) for ∣an​∣<r. The logarithm of the modulus of the remaining zero-free function is harmonic on the closed disc. Its mean value property, together with the mean of log∣reiθ−a∣ being logr for ∣a∣<r (expand the logarithm in powers of a/(reiθ)), proves Jensen's formula
∑∣an​∣<r​log∣an​∣r​=2π1​∫02π​log∣f(reiθ)∣dθ−log∣f(0)∣≤log∥f∥∞​−log∣f(0)∣.

Let r↑1 through such radii. Each summand increases to −log∣an​∣, and −logt≥1−t for 0<t≤1 by integration of 1/t≥1. Consequently ∑n​(1−∣an​∣)<∞. If f has order m at zero, apply the argument to f/zm, which is still bounded: outside ∣z∣=1/2 it is bounded by 2m∥f∥∞​, and inside use the maximum modulus principle. Add the finite contribution of the zeros at zero.
For sufficiency use a Blaschke product. For a=0 its normalized Blaschke factor is
ba​(z)=a∣a∣​1−aza−z​,1−ba​(z)=(1−∣a∣)1−az1+(∣a∣/a)z​.
(12)
On ∣z∣≤R<1, ∣1−ba​(z)∣≤(1−∣a∣)(1+R)/(1−R). The Blaschke condition thus makes the product converge locally uniformly; outside the specified zeros, the tail logarithms converge absolutely and the product is nonzero. Each factor has modulus at most one in the disc, so the limit is bounded by one. A factor zm supplies any finite multiplicity at zero. This proves
A nonzero bounded holomorphic function with exactly these zeros exists⟺n∑​e−ρ(0,an​)<∞.​
(13)
For the elementary inequality, put H(t)=log((1+t)/(1−t))−2t. Then H(0)=0 and
H′(t)=1−t22​−2=1−t22t2​≥0,
(14)
so log((1+t)/(1−t))≥2t​ for 0≤t<1.
For any point w not among the zeros, the pseudohyperbolic distance formula gives
∣ba​(w)∣=​1−aww−a​​=tanh2ρ(w,a)​=1+e−ρ(w,a)1−e−ρ(w,a)​.
(15)
Apply the proved inequality with t=e−ρ(w,a) to obtain log∣ba​(w)∣≤−2e−ρ(w,a). Sum over finite products and pass to the limit, including factors at zero. This gives
∣B(w)∣≤exp(−2n∑​e−ρ(w,an​)).​
(16)
The sum is finite: the triangle inequality gives e−ρ(w,an​)≤eρ(0,w)e−ρ(0,an​). If w is itself a zero, B(w)=0 and the same bound is immediate, so no substitution at the excluded value t=1 is needed. A unimodular constant in B has no effect.
If “zeros at the points” is interpreted as a set without assigned multiplicities, first delete repeated entries throughout. Otherwise infinitely repeating a single zero would invalidate the claimed necessity of the series condition; the standard zero-sequence convention counts multiplicities.

The prescribed-pole form of Runge theorem is as follows. Let K⊂C be compact, let f be holomorphic on a neighborhood of K, and let S⊂C∖K meet every connected component of that complement. Then, for every ϵ>0, there is a rational function R, all of whose poles belong to S, with supK​∣f−R∣<ϵ. A pole at infinity means a polynomial part is allowed. In particular, if the complement is connected, choose S={∞} and obtain the polynomial Runge theorem.
First approximate using poles anywhere off K. Choose a bounded polygonal neighborhood V of K whose closure lies in the neighborhood on which f is holomorphic, and whose boundary is disjoint from K. Such a V comes from a sufficiently fine finite grid covering a small closed neighborhood of K. Orient the outer boundary positively and boundaries of holes negatively. The Cauchy integral formula gives
f(z)=2πi1​∫∂V​ζ−zf(ζ)​dζ,z∈K.
(17)
Because ∂V and K have positive distance, the integrand is uniformly continuous on their product, separately along each boundary edge. Its Riemann sums converge uniformly for z∈K. Those sums are rational functions ∑j​cj​/(ζj​−z) with poles ζj​∈/K. Thus arbitrary exterior poles suffice.
To move them to the prescribed set, let A be the uniform closure on K of rational functions with poles only in S. It is a closed unital algebra; it contains the coordinate function when ∞∈S. Define
E={a∈C∖K:(z−a)−1∈A}.
(18)
This set is open relative to C∖K. Indeed, if a∈E and ∣b−a∣<dist(a,K), the uniformly convergent expansion
z−b1​=∑j=0∞​(z−a)j+1(b−a)j​
(19)
places the new reciprocal in A, using its algebra and closedness properties. It is also relatively closed: if am​∈E tends to a∈/K, then (z−am​)−1 tends uniformly on K to (z−a)−1. Each bounded component contains a finite selected pole and hence meets E. The unbounded component also meets E if it contains a finite selected pole; if its selected pole is infinity, take ∣a∣>supK​∣z∣ and use
z−a1​=−a1​∑j=0∞​(az​)j,
(20)
a uniform polynomial expansion. Being both open and closed and meeting every component, E is all of C∖K. Every initial Cauchy-sum reciprocal therefore belongs to A, and so does f. This proves Runge theorem, including its prescribed-pole assertion, rather than merely polynomial approximation on a special compact set.
For the requested pointwise polynomial approximation of a half-plane sign, take n≥2 and
Kn+​={x+iy:∣x∣≤n, 1/n≤y≤n},Kn0​=[−n,n],Kn−​={x+iy:∣x∣≤n, −n≤y≤−1/n},
(21)
These three compact sets are disjoint, and their union Kn​ has connected complement: there are horizontal gaps above and below the segment, and every complementary point can be joined through these gaps or around a rectangle to the exterior of [−n,n]2. Define hn​ to be 1,0,−1 on disjoint open neighborhoods of these three sets. It is holomorphic on their union. By the polynomial Runge theorem, choose Pn​ with supKn​​∣Pn​−hn​∣<1/n.
For every fixed point with positive imaginary part, both coordinate bounds and 1/n≤Imz eventually hold, so it lies in Kn+​ for all large n; the reflected assertion holds for negative imaginary part. Every real point eventually lies in Kn0​. Consequently
n→∞lim​Pn​(z)=sgn(Imz),sgn(0)=0.​
(22)
The discontinuity of the limit does not contradict holomorphicity of the approximating functions: this convergence is pointwise, and is not locally uniform near the real axis.
Finally, identify K∗ as the holomorphic convex hull relative to Ω. If U is a component of C∖K lying entirely in Ω, it cannot contain infinity and is bounded. Its boundary lies in K, and its closure is compact in Ω. For any holomorphic f on Ω, the maximum modulus principle on U gives ∣f(w)∣≤sup∂U​∣f∣≤supK​∣f∣. Thus U⊆K∗, as is K itself.
Conversely let w∈Ω∖K lie in a complementary component U that is not entirely in Ω. If it contains a finite point a∈/Ω, form the uniform algebra on K generated by constants and (z−a)−1. The same open-and-closed reciprocal argument above, now only on the connected component U, shows that (z−w)−1 is in its uniform closure. Its approximants have poles only at a and are holomorphic on Ω. If the available point outside Ω is infinity, use the polynomial algebra and its large-pole expansion instead. Hence in either case choose h∈O(Ω) with
supz∈K​​z−w1​−h(z)​<2maxz∈K​∣z−w∣1​.
(23)
The function F(z)=1−(z−w)h(z) is holomorphic on Ω, has F(w)=1, and satisfies supK​∣F∣<1/2. It separates w from the hull, so w∈/K∗. Combining the two inclusions proves
K∗=K ∪U component of C∖KU⊂Ω​⋃​U.​
(24)
Here P denotes the Riemann sphere; using it ensures that the unbounded component, which contains infinity, is not accidentally filled. The empty compact set has empty hull by the usual convention; the separation argument above concerns nonempty K.

The Schwarz-Pick lemma says that every holomorphic self-map F of the unit disc contracts pseudohyperbolic distance and therefore hyperbolic distance:
​1−F(w)​F(z)F(z)−F(w)​​≤​1−wzz−w​​,ρ(F(z),F(w))≤ρ(z,w).
(25)
Its infinitesimal form is
1−∣F(w)∣2∣F′(w)∣​≤1−∣w∣21​.​
(26)
Equality for distinct points in the distance inequality, or at one point in the derivative inequality, holds exactly when F is a disc Möbius transformation.
For completeness, first prove the Schwarz lemma. If H:D→D is holomorphic and H(0)=0, the quotient H(z)/z extends holomorphically at zero. On ∣z∣=r<1 its modulus is at most 1/r. The maximum modulus principle, followed by r↑1, gives ∣H(z)∣≤∣z∣ and ∣H′(0)∣≤1. Equality at a nonzero point or in the derivative forces that quotient to be a constant of modulus one, again by the maximum modulus principle; hence H(z)=eiθz.
Now let ϕa​(z)=(z−a)/(1−az) and apply this result to H=ϕF(w)​∘F∘ϕw−1​, which fixes zero. The modulus inequality is exactly the displayed distance inequality, and ρ=log((1+δ)/(1−δ)) is increasing in δ, giving hyperbolic contraction. Also ∣ϕa′​(a)∣=(1−∣a∣2)−1 and ∣(ϕw−1​)′(0)∣=1−∣w∣2, so the chain rule proves the derivative inequality. The equality statement follows from the rotation case of the Schwarz lemma after undoing the two automorphisms. Conversely a disc automorphism preserves the distance and attains equality.
There is a maximum of the derivative modulus in the given annulus family, which is nonempty because it contains the constant function one. Indeed the functions are bounded by e, and Cauchy estimates bound their derivatives at zero. Take a sequence whose derivative moduli approach the finite supremum. By Montel theorem it has a subsequence converging locally uniformly to a holomorphic function f, with f(0)=1 and e−1≤∣f∣≤e. The upper equality cannot occur at an interior point, because the maximum modulus principle would force a constant of modulus e, contrary to f(0)=1. The lower equality is excluded by applying the same argument to 1/f, which is holomorphic because ∣f∣≥e−1. Thus the limit still maps into the open annulus. Locally uniform convergence gives convergence of derivatives by the Cauchy integral formula, so this limit attains the supremum.
To compute it and all extremizers, the nonvanishing function f on the simply connected disc has a holomorphic logarithm h with h(0)=0. One can construct it by integrating f′/f from zero; differentiation shows eh/f is constant, hence one. Its real part obeys −1<Reh<1. The map
T(h)=tan4πh​
(27)
is a biholomorphism from this vertical strip onto the disc. To check the inverse explicitly, for ∣z∣<1 the ratio (1+iz)/(1−iz) has positive real part, so its logarithm with value zero at zero has imaginary part in (−π/2,π/2). Therefore
s(z)=π4​arctanz=πi2​log1−iz1+iz​
(28)
has real part between minus one and one, and direct substitution gives T(s(z))=z. The strip restriction removes the period ambiguity of the tangent, so these are inverse maps.
Applying the Schwarz lemma to T∘h gives (π/4)∣h′(0)∣≤1. Since f′(0)=h′(0), the exact maximum is
f∈Fmax​∣f′(0)∣=π4​.​
(29)
It is attained by f(z)=exp((4/π)arctanz). The equality case requires T(h(z))=eiθz, and hence all maximizing functions are
fθ​(z)=exp(π4​arctan(eiθz)),θ∈R.​
(30)
Their derivatives are (4/π)eiθ, so the extremizer is not unique: there is exactly this circle of distinct functions, with the parameter considered modulo 2π. This is the extremal derivative of a disc map into a symmetric annulus for L=1.

Represent a transformation by
M(u,v)=(uv​vu​),detM=∣u∣2−∣v∣2=1.
(31)
Multiplication preserves this form and determinant, and the inverse has the same form with parameters (u,−v). To check the congruence condition, complex conjugation is the identity modulo 2Z[i]. Thus u+v≡1 says exactly that M fixes the column (1,1)T modulo two. Products and inverses preserve this property. The identity belongs to the set, and changing the common sign of (u,v) does not change either the transformation or its congruence. Hence these transformations form a group.
Because ∣u∣>∣v∣, the denominator has no zero in the disc. Direct calculation gives
1−∣g(z)∣2=∣vz+u∣21−∣z∣2​,∣g′(z)∣=∣vz+u∣21​.
(32)
The inverse also maps the disc into itself, so g is a disc automorphism, and these identities show that it preserves the hyperbolic metric 2∣dz∣/(1−∣z∣2).
The action is properly discontinuous. If C⊂D is compact, put R=maxz∈C​ρ(0,z). If gC∩C=∅, choose z,w∈C with w=gz. Then
ρ(0,g0)≤ρ(0,w)+ρ(w,g0)=ρ(0,w)+ρ(z,0)≤2R.
(33)
Since g0=v/u and ∣u∣2−∣v∣2=1, its distance bound gives ∣v/u∣≤tanhR<1 and therefore ∣u∣2=1/(1−∣v/u∣2)≤cosh2R. There are only finitely many Gaussian integers u,v with these bounds, so only finitely many g satisfy gC∩C=∅. This proves the required properly discontinuous group action directly.
If g0=0, then v=0 and u is one of the Gaussian units 1,−1,i,−i. The congruence u−1∈2Z[i] leaves only u=±1. Both give the identity transformation. Thus the point stabilizer of zero is trivial.
The closed Dirichlet region centered at zero is the set of z with ρ(z,0)≤ρ(z,g0) for every g. For a nonidentity element, v=0. Write
N=∣v∣2∈Z≥1​,uv=m+in,q=g0=∣u∣2uv​=N+1m+in​.
(34)
The distance inequality is equivalent, by pseudohyperbolic distance, to ∣z∣≤∣(z−q)/(1−q​z)∣. Squaring and subtracting yields
∣z−q∣2−∣z∣2∣1−q​z∣2=(1−∣z∣2)[∣q∣2(1+∣z∣2)−2Re(zq​)].
(35)
Since ∣z∣<1 and ∣q∣2=N/(N+1), the bisector half-plane is exactly
2(mx+ny)≤N(1+x2+y2),z=x+iy.
(36)
For u=1+i or 1−i and v=i or −i, all the group conditions hold and the products uv are the four numbers ±1±i, with N=1. Their four inequalities force
2(∣x∣+∣y∣)≤1+x2+y2.
(37)
Conversely, for any group element m2+n2=∣uv∣2=N(N+1)<(N+1)2. As m,n are integers, ∣m∣,∣n∣≤N. Thus every point satisfying those four inequalities satisfies every other one:
2(mx+ny)≤2N(∣x∣+∣y∣)≤N(1+x2+y2).
(38)
This proves, with no missing bisectors, the Gaussian-integer disc group with an ideal-square Dirichlet domain formula
DG​(0)={x+iy:x2+y2<1, 2(∣x∣+∣y∣)≤1+x2+y2}.​
(39)
Equivalently it is the part of the unit disc outside all four open circles of radius one centered at 1+i,1−i,−1+i,−1−i. Its sides are the inward arcs of those circles. They meet the unit circle orthogonally, and their ideal vertices are 1,i,−1,−i​; these vertices themselves lie outside the open disc.
The origin has trivial point stabilizer and the group orbit is locally finite by the discontinuity proof, so this closed bisector intersection has translates covering the disc with disjoint interiors, as in the definition of a Dirichlet domain. Side identifications are allowed on its boundary.
