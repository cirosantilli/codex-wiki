The dominant balance for algebraic roots separates one small root of a polynomial from three large ones. For the small root of a polynomial, write z=ε+aε3+O(ε5) in z=ε(1+z2)2. Comparing coefficients gives a=2, so
zsmall​=ε+2ε3+O(ε5).​
(1)
This is a real analytic function of real ε near zero, since the derivative with respect to z of z−ε(1+z2)2 is one at (0,0).
For the large roots of a polynomial, use the real cube root s=sgn(ε)∣ε∣1/3 and set z=w/s. The transformed equation is
w=(w2+s2)2.
(2)
Its three nonzero leading roots of a polynomial are w=ω, with ω3=1. Writing w=ω+as2+O(s4) gives a=4a+2ω2, hence a=−2ω2/3. Therefore
zω​=sω​−32​ω2s+O(s3),ω=1,e2πi/3,e−2πi/3.​
(3)
The choice ω=1 is the second real root of a polynomial; the other two form a complex conjugate pair for either sign of ε. The nonzero transformed roots of a polynomial are simple, so their Taylor series in s2 exist locally and exhaust the three large roots of a polynomial. Together with the small one these give all four roots of a polynomial for sufficiently small nonzero ε. At ε=0 itself the rational equation has only the finite root zero; the other three escape to infinity. Multiplying by (1+z2)2 has not introduced roots at z=±i, since those points do not solve the multiplied equation.

A calculator needs elementary arithmetic and its built-in sine and cosine, together with stored approximation coefficients. A convenient global construction uses compactified approximation from endpoint asymptotics. Define the exact real tail functions by
Q+iP=e−iλ2(A(1+i)−C−iS),u=1+λλ​.

(12)
extend continuously to the compact interval 0≤u≤1, with p(1)=1/2 and q(1)=1/4. Choose stored polynomial approximations pN​,qN​ on that interval, constrained by the small-argument Taylor series and large-argument asymptotic expansions, and calibrated across the intervening interval. The resulting elementary formula is
Ccalc​(λ)=A+1+λpN​(u)​sinλ2−(1+λ)3qN​(u)​cosλ2.​
(13)
Uniform errors δp​,δq​ in the stored polynomial approximations give ∣Ccalc​−C∣≤δp​+δq​ for every positive argument. Existence of arbitrarily accurate such approximations follows from the Weierstrass approximation theorem. A Padé approximant with a denominator having no positive zero is an alternative implementation. Matching the two end expansions alone is not an error guarantee: the middle interval must also be controlled. For very small arguments, switching to the short Taylor series avoids cancellation. Ordinary finite-precision limitations in evaluating λ2 and reducing its trigonometric phase remain those of the calculator itself.

Apply the method of multiple scales with independent fast time t and slow time T=εt, and write x=x0​(t,T)+εx1​(t,T)+⋯. The chain rule gives d/dt=∂t​+ε∂T​. At leading order,
(∂t2​+1)x0​=0,x0​=R(T)cosθ,θ=t+ϕ(T).
(14)
At first order,
(∂t2​+1)x1​=f(Rcosθ,−Rsinθ)+2RT​sinθ+2RϕT​cosθ.
(15)
A forcing component at the natural frequency produces a secular term in x1​. Thus the solvability condition in the method of multiple scales is orthogonality to both sinθ and cosθ. Define the fast-phase average, holding R,ϕ,T fixed, by
⟨h⟩=2π1​∫02π​h(θ;R,T)dθ.
(16)
Since ⟨sin2θ⟩=⟨cos2θ⟩=1/2 and ⟨sinθcosθ⟩=0, the amplitude-phase equations are
RT​=−⟨fsinθ⟩,RϕT​=−⟨fcosθ⟩.​
(17)
They are leading averaged equations, not exact equations for a purely sinusoidal solution of a general nonlinear oscillator.
For the cubic conservative force, f=−R3cos3θ, odd symmetry gives ⟨cos3θsinθ⟩=0, while ⟨cos4θ⟩=3/8. Hence
R(T)=R0​,ϕ(T)=ϕ0​+83​R02​T.​
(18)
If x(0)=x∗​ and x′(0)=v∗​, leading initial matching gives
R0​=x∗2​+v∗2​​,ϕ0​=atan2(−v∗​,x∗​),
(19)
and therefore
x(t)=R0​cos(t+ϕ0​+83​εR02​t)+O(ε)
(20)
on bounded slow time intervals for fixed bounded initial data. The positive frequency shift agrees with the increased restoring force of x′′+x+εx3=0. The zero initial state is the exact zero solution, with irrelevant phase. If a first waveform correction is desired, the remaining first-order forcing is −R03​cos3θ/4, so x1​=R03​cos3θ/32 plus a homogeneous correction chosen to match the initial data at the next order. This exhibits explicitly why the leading harmonic ansatz is approximate.

For the oscillator coupled to slow damping feedback, expand y=Y(T)+εy1​(t,T)+⋯, alongside the method of multiple scales oscillator expansion. The leading equation ∂t​Y=0 makes Y independent of fast time. The oscillator force evaluated on x0​=Rcosθ is 2YRsinθ. The amplitude-phase equations therefore give
RT​=−YR,ϕT​=0.
(21)
The next equation for the damping variable is
∂t​y1​+YT​=g(Rcosθ).
(22)
A bounded periodic y1​ exists only when the mean of its right side vanishes. Thus the full leading averaged system is
RT​=−YR,ϕT​=0,YT​=⟨g(Rcosθ)⟩.​
(23)
Set R0​=x∗2​+v∗2​​, ϕ0​=atan2(−v∗​,x∗​), and Y0​=y∗​. These specify the arbitrary leading initial conditions.
For g(x)=x, the phase average is zero. Consequently
Y(T)=Y0​,R(T)=R0​e−Y0​T,ϕ(T)=ϕ0​.​
(24)
Thus x(t)=R0​e−Y0​εtcos(t+ϕ0​)+O(ε) and y(t)=Y0​+O(ε). Positive Y0​ damps the oscillator, negative Y0​ amplifies it, and zero Y0​ leaves the leading amplitude unchanged. The fast correction y1​=Rsinθ plus an initial-matching constant confirms that y can oscillate slightly even though Y is constant. For R0​=0, the exact solution is x=0, y=y∗​.
For the logarithmic feedback, assume R0​>0. Its phase singularities are integrable, and the supplied integral gives
⟨21​log(R2cos2θ)⟩=logR−log2=log(R/2).
(25)
Writing L=log(R/2) turns the nonlinear amplitude-phase equations into the linear pair
LT​=−Y,YT​=L,
(26)
so LTT​+L=0. With L0​=log(R0​/2),
R(T)Y(T)ϕ(T)​=2exp(L0​cosT−Y0​sinT),=L0​sinT+Y0​cosT,=ϕ0​.​​
(27)
The first integral L2+Y2=L02​+Y02​ provides a direct check, and the amplitude remains positive on every finite slow time interval. At the retained averaged order, the physical approximation is x≃R(εt)cos(t+ϕ0​), y≃Y(εt).
The logarithmic case needs an interpretation at oscillator zeros. The original right side is undefined at x=0, so a globally classical solution through each crossing, or a solution from x∗​=v∗​=0, cannot be asserted. At a transverse crossing, log∣x∣∼log∣t−t∗​∣ is locally integrable; y can be interpreted as absolutely continuous, with its equation holding away from the crossings and almost everywhere. In that integrable interpretation the displayed averaged formulas apply formally for nonzero leading amplitude. The standard smooth-forcing averaging theorem cannot be invoked without addressing this singularity. The phrase arbitrary initial conditions must exclude the identically zero state for this logarithmic problem.

With the streamfunction convention (u,v)=(ψy​,−ψx​), disturbance vorticity is ω=−Δψ. The basic Couette flow has constant vorticity −1. Linearizing the two-dimensional Euler equations for an inviscid fluid in vorticity form gives
(∂t​+y∂x​)ω+u′⋅∇(−1)=0.
(41)
The second term vanishes, so
(∂t​+y∂x​)Δψ=0.​
(42)
This derivation uses incompressibility and an inviscid homogeneous fluid, without introducing a viscous decay term.
For the initial streamwise Fourier mode, write ψ=eiαxa(y,t). Its transported Laplacian has amplitude
(∂y2​−α2)a=F(y)e−iαyt,F(y)=ϕ′′(y)−α2ϕ(y).
(43)
The decaying Green function on the line is
G(y,s)=−2αe−α∣y−s∣​,F(s)=ϕ′′(s)−α2ϕ(s).​
(44)
This is the negative of the one-dimensional modified Helmholtz Green function for −∂y2​+α2: it is continuous and its first derivative with respect to y has jump one at y=s. Consequently (∂y2​−α2)G=δ(y−s), and inversion yields
ψ(x,y,t)=∫R​F(s)G(y,s)eiα(x−st)ds.​
(45)
Under the usual decay of the initial profile and its derivatives, the initial integral returns ϕ. There is no nonzero homogeneous solution of a′′−α2a=0 decaying at both infinities.
For the large-time calculation, put q=αt and H(s)=G(y,s)F(s), keeping the observation point y fixed. Assume sufficient localization to justify the following integrations by parts; for example, F may be a Schwartz function. The function H is continuous, but its derivative in s has jump
[Hs​]s=y​=F(y),
(46)
since Gs​(y,y+)−Gs​(y,y−)=1. The Fourier decay from a Green-function derivative jump gives
∫H(s)e−iqsds=−q21​[F(y)e−iqy+∫Hreg′′​(s)e−iqsds].
(47)
Here the regular second derivative is computed separately on either side of s=y. If it is integrable, its Fourier transform tends to zero by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma. Hence
ψ(x,y,t)=−α2t2F(y)​eiα(x−yt)+o(t−2).​
(48)
The cusp in the Green function, rather than a stationary point of the linear phase, supplies this leading contribution.
To obtain the streamwise velocity, differentiate the integral itself. This avoids an unjustified differentiation of a pointwise remainder. The kernel Gy​ has a jump of −1 as a function of s at s=y, so one integration by parts gives
u(x,y,t)=eiαx∫Gy​(y,s)F(s)e−iqsds=αtiF(y)​eiα(x−yt)+o(t−1).
(49)
The transverse velocity follows exactly from v=−iαψ, while the Laplacian equation gives vorticity without an asymptotic approximation:
u(x,y,t)v(x,y,t)ω(x,y,t)​=αtiF(y)​eiα(x−yt)+o(t−1),=αt2iF(y)​eiα(x−yt)+o(t−2),=−F(y)eiα(x−yt).​​
(50)
Thus the generic orders are u=O(t−1), v=O(t−2), and ω=O(1), at each fixed y. If F(y)=0, the leading coefficients vanish and faster decay can occur. For real physical disturbances, take the real part of these complex Fourier modes. The vorticity generally keeps oscillating rather than approaching a pointwise limit, even while the velocity decays. This is linear inviscid damping in unbounded Couette flow caused by phase mixing.
For arbitrary initial vorticity, the characteristics satisfy x˙=y, y˙​=0, and ω˙=0. Therefore the exact solution is
ω(x,y,t)=ω0​(x−yt,y).​
(51)
Putting ω0​=−F(y)eiαx recovers the preceding nondecaying mode. In general the shear is area preserving, so its transport preserves every finite Lp norm of vorticity. Local pointwise behavior for arbitrary ω0​ depends on its spatial profile: an initially localized profile can move away from a fixed point. There is no universal pointwise nonzero limit or t−2 decay of vorticity for arbitrary initial data.
The stated smoothness and decay of ϕ alone do not guarantee the claimed algebraic rates. The intended integration by parts calculation requires control of derivatives and tails; convergence of the displayed integral alone is insufficient. Here is a counterexample to the literal unrestricted rate, with α=1. Choose a nonnegative smooth function r supported in (−1/4,1/4) with ∫r=1, and a sufficiently rapidly increasing sequence tn​. Set
F(s)=∑n≥1​tn​es​r(s−n)eitn​s,ϕ=G∗F,G(s)=−21​e−∣s∣.
(52)
Choose ∑en/tn​<∞. Then F is smooth and integrable, ϕ is smooth and tends to zero at both infinities, and every evolution integral converges absolutely. At the observation point (x,y)=(0,0), all supports have s>0, so
ψ(0,0,t)=−21​∑n≥1​tn​1​e−i(t−tn​)nr(t−tn​),r(ξ)=∫r(s)e−iξsds.
(53)
At t=tn​, the nth summand is −1/(2tn​). The Fourier transform of r decays faster than any inverse power. Inductively choose tn​ so large that all earlier summands at tn​ together have modulus less than 1/(8tn​), and choose subsequent times so that ∑m>n​1/tm​<1/(4tn​). The later summands then also have modulus less than 1/(8tn​). Thus ∣ψ(0,0,tn​)∣≥1/(4tn​), contradicting O(t−2). This construction meets the printed assumptions; what it lacks is uniform derivative localization.
