The Lyman-alpha forest is the collection of absorption lines blueward of a distant quasar's own Lyman-alpha line. Foreground neutral hydrogen removes photons at rest wavelength λα​≃1215.67 angstroms, observed at λ=(1+z)λα​ because of cosmological redshift. Gas at many different foreground redshifts therefore produces many lines. The weak forest traces diffuse, mostly ionized intergalactic medium, rather than a population of fully neutral clouds; much stronger, self-shielded absorbers are a separate regime. Madau's account of quasar absorption systems distinguishes these column-density regimes.

For highly ionized hydrogen in photoionization equilibrium, balance the photoionization rate against radiative recombination:
nHI​ΓHI​=α(T)ne​nHII​≃α(T)nH2​.
(20)
Thus the neutral fraction is nHI​/nH​≃α(T)nH​/ΓHI​ and the neutral column density across a proper path L is
NHI​≃ΓHI​α(T)nH2​L​.
(21)
For an illustrative diffuse gas with T∼104K, α∼4×10−13cm3s−1, ΓHI​∼10−12s−1, and nH​∼10−5cm−3, the neutral fraction is only 4×10−6. A proper path of 100 kiloparsecs then gives NHI​∼1013cm−2, enough to produce a weak Lyman-alpha absorption feature even though the gas is almost entirely ionized.
This hydrogen number density is of the order of the cosmic mean at redshift a few. Since the weak forest is common along most high-redshift lines of sight, substantial path length passes through such diffuse gas. Much denser material would give much larger column density for comparable path lengths and ionizing flux. These arguments support near-mean or modestly overdense intergalactic medium as the origin of weak forest lines, not the claim that every absorber has exactly the cosmic mean density: path length, temperature, and ionizing background must also be constrained.

A radial null geodesic in the FLRW metric obeys dχ=c∣dt∣/a. The redshift-time relation dt=−dz/[(1+z)H(z)], with a0​=1, therefore gives
Δχ=c∫z1​z2​​H(z)dz​≃H(zˉ)cΔz​​,zˉ=2z1​+z2​​.

The quasar proximity effect is weaker Lyman-alpha forest absorption near a quasar's emission redshift. Extra photons from the quasar increase the photoionization rate and reduce the neutral fraction without necessarily reducing the total gas density.
For isotropic specific intensity Jν​ and a steady, isotropically radiating quasar with spectral luminosity Lν​, the background and local rates are
Γbg​=4π∫ν0​∞​hνJν​σν​​dν,ΓQ​(r)=∫ν0​∞​4πr2hνLν​σν​​dν.
(24)
Here r is a proper separation small enough that attenuation and expansion across it can initially be neglected, and σν​ is the hydrogen photoionization cross-section. At fixed density and temperature, photoionization equilibrium makes the neutral column density, and hence a given line's optical depth, scale as
τfield​τ(r)​≃Γbg​+ΓQ​(r)Γbg​​.​
(25)
The observed quasar luminosity supplies ΓQ​(r); the distance at which the absorption changes significantly estimates where ΓQ​∼Γbg​. Adopting a background spectral shape then converts the inferred rate to its specific intensity, often quoted near the hydrogen ionization energy threshold.
Important uncertainties include an enhanced gas density contrast around the host, the unobserved ionizing spectrum, anisotropic emission, source lifetime and variability, the systemic redshift and continuum placement, attenuation between source and absorber, and fluctuations between lines of sight. Higher gas density enhances recombination and can partially hide the effect, biasing a simple background estimate upward. Dall'Aglio, Wisotzki and Worseck's 2008 measurements illustrate the observed sightline scatter and the roles of variability and clustering.

The ionizing background is produced mainly by hot stars in star-forming galaxies and by accretion-powered quasars. The stellar contribution depends on star formation and the ionizing photon escape fraction; the quasar contribution depends on its evolving luminosity function. Radiative transfer through the intergalactic medium, including absorption, recombination emission, and cosmological redshifting, determines the radiation actually available for photoionization.
In the picture relevant to this 2009 paper, increasingly rare quasars cannot by themselves explain the high-redshift hydrogen ionizing field, so galaxies become especially important above z∼3. Harder quasar radiation is important for doubly ionizing helium around z∼3. Hydrogen reionization must be largely accomplished by z∼6. At lower redshift the evolution reflects declining source emissivity together with the increasing propagation distance of ionizing photons; near reionization, short absorption lengths and spatially uneven ionized regions prevent a simple uniform-background description. The background rate need not follow the quasar population alone: a roughly flat hydrogen photoionization rate over 2≲z≲4 is compatible with a changing mixture of galaxies and quasars. Faucher-Giguère and collaborators' 2009 background calculation and their preceding opacity analysis give this interpretation. These redshifts describe the broad historical picture, rather than precise universal transition times.

The comoving particle horizon is the maximum radial distance a photon could have travelled since the initial Big Bang, expressed in comoving coordinates. It describes possible past causal contact, whereas a cosmological event horizon concerns photons that can reach an observer in the future.
For a radial null geodesic in the FLRW metric, dr/1−kr2​=cdt/a. Spatial flatness sets k=0, so with a0​=1,
rp​(t)=c∫0t​a(t′)dt′​=c∫0a(t)​a′2H(a′)da′​.

The plot uses comoving wavenumber in Mpc−1. Using the inverse comoving particle horizon from part (ii) gives 1/rp,eq​≃9×10−3Mpc−1. The conventional matter-radiation equality scale is instead
keq​=caeq​Heq​​≃cH0​​aeq​2Ωm,0​​​≃1.1×10−2Mpc−1.​
(34)
These are consistent order-one conventions for locating the turnover near 10−2Mpc−1. The horizon-entry condition is k/(aH/c)∼1; equating an entire Fourier wavelength 2π/k to the particle horizon would attach a different numerical factor.
For adiabatic initial conditions, the asymptotic cold-dark-matter power spectrum has P(k)∝kns​T(k)2, where the scalar spectral index is ns​≃1 and T is the cosmological transfer function. Modes below keq​ enter during matter domination and have little relative suppression, giving approximately P∝k. Modes above keq​ enter during radiation domination, when logarithmic growth of matter perturbations during radiation domination is much slower. Their transfer is approximately T∝ln(k/keq​)/k2, giving P∝k−3ln2(k/keq​) well above equality. This explains the broad turnover; baryon acoustic oscillations add structure, and nonlinear evolution modifies sufficiently small scales.

The comoving distance to the Cosmic microwave background last-scattering surface is of order the present horizon, χ∗​∼1.3×104Mpc. At recombination the particle horizon is much smaller, so replacing χ∗​ by today's horizon is adequate for the requested estimate. A transverse Fourier mode projects approximately to angular multipole ℓ∼kχ∗​. If an angular feature's width is estimated by θ∼1/ℓ, the resolution θmin​∼10−3 gives
kmax​∼χ∗​θmin​1​∼0.08Mpc−1.
(35)
Using the common half-wavelength convention θ∼π/ℓ instead gives ℓmax​∼3000 and kmax​∼0.2--0.3Mpc−1. These express the same order-of-magnitude resolution estimate with different angular-scale conventions. On the graph, a useful indicative band is therefore from about 10−4Mpc−1 to order 10−1Mpc−1, potentially a few times 10−1; it is not a sharp instrumental boundary. The lowest useful multipoles are of order a few, with substantial cosmic variance.
The cosmic microwave background constrains the matter power spectrum indirectly through the primordial spectrum and radiation/matter cosmological transfer functions. Beam resolution, Cosmic microwave background diffusion damping, foreground emission, and projection of three-dimensional fluctuations into angular anisotropy limit the recoverable small-scale information. Resolving a given angle alone does not guarantee a precise matter-spectrum measurement there.

c OurBigBook.com
A survey depth R∼400Mpc makes wavelengths substantially larger than its volume poorly measurable. A characteristic inverse depth is 1/R≃2.5×10−3Mpc−1; a Fourier wavelength that fits across a dimension L instead requires k≳2π/L. For L between R and 2R, this is roughly 0.008--0.016Mpc−1. A limited sky patch can have smaller transverse dimensions, so its survey window function raises the practical minimum in those directions. An indicative useful band for a broad survey is around 10−2Mpc−1 and above, including scales near equality if its volume is large enough.
There is no upper wavenumber determined by depth alone. Mean separation of observed galaxies, galaxy power spectrum shot noise, angular/redshift resolution, and nonlinear clustering set it. Straightforward linear inference is typically restricted to roughly k≲0.1Mpc−1 at low redshift, with smaller scales requiring modelling. The magnitude limit does not specify the galaxy comoving number density, so a more precise sampling cutoff cannot be deduced from these data.
The two measurements are complementary. The cosmic microwave background samples a large volume at an early epoch when relevant perturbations are mostly linear, and is free of galaxy bias; its matter-spectrum inference is indirect and depends on projection, cosmological parameters, foregrounds, and cosmic variance. A galaxy survey gives three-dimensional late-time information and can reach smaller scales, but observes biased, discrete tracers: galaxy bias, nonlinear gravitational growth, redshift-space distortions, selection effects, and galaxy power spectrum shot noise must be controlled. Combining both constrains the initial spectrum and its subsequent growth more strongly than either alone, while their overlapping modes test the inferred evolution.
