When the hydrostatic spreading term is small, the depth equation reduces to ht​+chx​=0, where c=u/ϕ. Its characteristic curves have x=c(t−t0​), and the depth is constant along each characteristic. At the inlet the imposed flux gives uh(0,t0​)=Qe−t0​/τ. Therefore h0​=h(0,0)=Q/u in this advective approximation and

(6)

Let X(t) denote the polymer–oil material interface, measured from the injection well, and let U(t) be the Darcy velocity. Incompressibility and uniform cross-sectional area make U independent of x in this planar model. Integrating Darcy's law through the injected layer and the oil layer gives

(15)
The material interface moves at the pore velocity, so X′=U/ϕ. Put a=μ−μh​ and A=μh​L. Then (A+aX)X′=kΔP/ϕ, which integrates from X(0)=0 to
AX+2a​X2=ϕkΔP​t.
(16)
Thus the fixed-pressure planar Darcy displacement has
X˙(t)=ϕ(μh​L)2+2(μ−μh​)kΔPt/ϕ​kΔP​,U(t)=ϕX˙(t).​
(17)
For a=0, X=[A2+2akΔPt/ϕ​−A]/a; its continuous a=0 limit is X=kΔPt/(ϕμh​L). Positive layer dynamic viscosities ensure the resistance stays positive until breakthrough, which occurs at tb​=ϕL2(μ+μh​)/(2kΔP). The formulas stop there, since the two-layer geometry then changes.

Let Y(t) be the porous thermal front and X(t) the polymer front. With the literal stated convention for Darcy velocity,
Y′=ΓU,X′=U/ϕ,Y(0)=X(0)=0.
(18)
Therefore Y=βX with β=Γϕ. This is thermal-front retardation in Darcy flow. The region 0<x<Y is cold injected fluid of dynamic viscosity μ; Y<x<X is heated injected fluid of dynamic viscosity bμ; X<x<L is oil of dynamic viscosity μh​. The pressure drop is consequently
ΔP=kU​[μY+bμ(X−Y)+μh​(L−X)]=kU​[μh​L+(μeff​−μh​)X],
(19)
where μeff​=μ[β+b(1−β)]. Apply the preceding fixed-pressure planar Darcy displacement calculation with μ replaced by this effective dynamic viscosity. The polymer-front speed is
X˙=ϕ(μh​L)2+2(μeff​−μh​)kΔPt/ϕ​kΔP​,μeff​=μ[b−(b−1)Γϕ].​
(20)

At the porous thermal front, the cold polymer solution behind has dynamic viscosity μ, while the hot solution ahead has dynamic viscosity bμ>μ. Thus a more mobile region pushes a less mobile one. A forward protrusion locally reduces the length of high-resistance hot fluid and concentrates Darcy flux into itself; it advances faster, amplifying the protrusion. This is the mechanism of the Saffman–Taylor instability.
The leading polymer–oil interface can nevertheless be stable when the hot polymer dynamic viscosity is at least the oil dynamic viscosity. Stability of that leading interface does not guarantee stability of the trailing porous thermal front. Slow thermal conduction makes the latter sharp and offers weak short-wave smoothing; in practice it is the adverse dynamic viscosity contrast across this front that can invalidate the assumed planar stable-flow calculation.

Take constant Darcy velocity U and work locally near a planar porous thermal front moving at VT​=ΓU. Let ξ=x−VT​t and perturb its position by η=η0​eσt+iay, with wavelength λ=2π/∣a∣. Neglect thermal conduction and regard the adjoining layers as semi-infinite on this disturbance scale. In each region, Darcy's law and incompressibility imply that the pressure perturbation is harmonic, so the bounded normal modes are
p−′​=A−​e∣a∣ξeσt+iay,p+′​=A+​e−∣a∣ξeσt+iay.
(21)
Here the minus side is cold with dynamic viscosity μ, and the plus side is hot with dynamic viscosity bμ. The base pressure gradients are p−,x0​=−μU/k and p+,x0​=−bμU/k. Linearizing pressure continuity at the displaced front yields
A−​−A+​=−η0​k(b−1)μU​.
(22)
Continuity of the perturbed normal Darcy velocity gives
δU=−μk∣a∣​A−​=bμk∣a∣​A+​,
(23)
so A+​=−bA−​. Therefore δU=U∣a∣(b−1)η0​/(b+1). The thermal-front kinematic law is ση0​=ΓδU, proving the thermal-front viscous-fingering dispersion relation
σ(λ)=λ2πΓU​b+1b−1​=λ2πVT​​b+1b−1​.​
(24)
It is positive for b>1, zero for b=1, and negative for b<1. Without thermal conduction the model has no finite fastest wavelength: the positive growth rate increases without bound as λ→0. A resolved thermal transition and diffusion regularize that short-wave limit.
For a material interface the kinematic coefficient would be 1/ϕ rather than Γ. Thus, if the interface of interest were instead the hot polymer–oil interface, the same calculation would give σ=2π(U/ϕ)(μh​−bμ)/[λ(μh​+bμ)], the usual planar viscous-fingering dispersion relation. This distinguishes the two interfaces and their speeds. The boxed thermal result uses the local planar approximation; distant wells or the other front modify the pressure eigenfunctions for wavelengths comparable to those separations.

Here k is turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass, k=21​u′2+v′2+w′2​, rather than the permeability of a porous medium used in the porous-flow questions. The K-epsilon turbulence model represents the high-Reynolds-number transport of this energy and its turbulent kinetic energy dissipation rate ϵ.
In the k equation, d[(νT​/σk​)ky​]/dy is the divergence of modeled turbulent diffusion, P is mean-shear production, and −ϵ is molecular conversion of fluctuation energy into heat. Statistical stationarity and fully developed flow have removed storage and streamwise advection from this reduction.

For Ri>Ric​, the fixed-correlation equilibrium model for stratified turbulence has Rmin​>1. Thus buoyancy destruction plus dissipation exceeds shear production for every q>0, and the local kinetic-energy budget predicts decay rather than any positive statistically steady turbulent equilibrium. The stable and unstable turbulent branches have disappeared together at the critical point.
The implied outcome within this closure is suppression or decay of sustained turbulence. It is not a proof that every physical stratified shear flow above this value must be permanently laminar: energy transport, external forcing, intermittent mixing or variable correlations and length-scale ratios can violate the model assumptions. As turbulence decays, the original fully turbulent high-Reynolds-number approximation itself ceases to apply. The critical Richardson number is consequently a prediction of the stated local closure, not a universal nonlinear stability bound.
