Let a be the projective-line loop and b the core loop of the Möbius band. The boundary of the Möbius band goes twice around its core. Its boundary inclusion is a cofibration, so replacing the gluing map by a homotopic cellular degree-n map preserves the homotopy type. More precisely, the band is a mapping cylinder of the boundary double covering onto the core circle. After attaching its other end and collapsing the connecting tree, the resulting space has a homotopy-equivalent CW complex with one vertex, edges a,b, and two two-cells with attaching words
a2,a−nb2.
(3)
The first is the projective-plane face; the second records equality of the attached boundary loop an and the band boundary loop b2. Taking exponent sums gives the cellular chain complex
0⟶Z2D​Z20​Z⟶0,D=(20​−n2​).
(4)
Since detD=4, its kernel is zero. The first invariant factor in the Smith normal form is the greatest common divisor of its entries, d=gcd(2,n), and the second is 4/d. Hence
H0​(X;Z)=Z,H1​(X;Z)={Z/4,(Z/2)2,​n odd,n even,​Hj​(X;Z)=0 (j≥2).​
(5)
This includes n=0 and negative degrees. The fundamental group after attaching a Möbius band is ⟨a,b∣a2=1, b2=an⟩, consistent with the computed abelianization. The calculation is an example of homology after attaching a Möbius band to the real projective plane.

The space is the mapping torus of the antipodal map A:Sn→Sn. For n≥1, its mapping degree is δ=(−1)n+1: A is the restriction of −I on Rn+1, whose determinant is (−1)n+1. The Wang sequence gives
0⟶coker(1−A∗​:Hj​(Sn)→Hj​(Sn))⟶Hj​(TA​)⟶ker(1−A∗​:Hj−1​(Sn)→Hj−1​(Sn))⟶0.
(6)
The kernel terms are free abelian, so these short exact sequences split as groups. On H0​(Sn) the map is the identity, and on Hn​(Sn) it is multiplication by δ. For n≥2 this gives
Hj​(TA​;Z)=⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z,Z/2,0,​j=0,1,n odd and j=n,n+1,n even and j=n,otherwise.​​

At n=1, both contributions to degree one are present, giving H0​=H2​=Z and H1​=Z2, with all other groups zero. At n=0, the antipodal map exchanges the two points of S0, and the two cylinder intervals join into a single circle; thus H0​=H1​=Z and higher groups vanish. These are the homology of the antipodal sphere mapping torus cases.

For the triangle quotient, the universal coefficient theorem for homology gives Hj​(X;F2​)=0 for every j>0, because its only positive integral homology is Z/3. A connected positive-dimensional closed manifold has a nonzero top mod-two fundamental class, independently of orientability. Thus this quotient cannot be homotopy equivalent to such a manifold. A connected zero-dimensional closed manifold is a point, which its nonzero H1​(X;Z) also excludes.
For the attached-band space, the same universal coefficient theorem for homology gives nonzero H2​(X;F2​) and zero homology above degree two. A connected closed manifold homotopy equivalent to it would consequently have dimension two. Its Euler characteristic would be one. The classification theorem for surfaces says that a closed connected surface of Euler characteristic one is the real projective plane. But its integral H1​ is Z/2, differing from both possibilities computed in part 2. Thus this space is never homotopy equivalent to a closed manifold.
The antipodal mapping torus is already a closed manifold: gluing the two boundary components of Sn×[0,1] by a diffeomorphism gives a smooth (n+1)-manifold without boundary. For n≥1, it is orientable exactly when (−1)n+1=1, namely when n is odd. In that case −I on Rn+1 is isotopic to the identity through rotations, so the mapping torus is diffeomorphic to Sn×S1. The n=0 case is a circle as above. Only the third family can be homotopy equivalent to a closed manifold.

For finite-dimensional Complex projective space CPr, use the filtration by its linear projective subspaces. The difference CPj∖CPj−1 is Cj, so there is one topological cell in every even dimension 0,2,…,2r, and none in odd dimensions. Every cellular boundary vanishes for degree reasons. The integral cohomology is therefore Z in those even degrees and zero elsewhere.
For the cohomology ring, let x∈H2(CPr;Z) be the Poincare dual of a projective hyperplane, using the complex orientation. Generic hyperplanes meet transversely, and a collection of j of them intersects in CPr−j. The geometric interpretation of the cup product identifies xj with the dual of that intersection. Restricting to a linear CPj, the j hyperplanes meet in one point, with positive sign because the transverse normal directions are complex lines. Hence
⟨xj,[CPj]⟩=1.
(8)
Thus xj is a primitive generator in degree 2j, not merely a nonzero multiple. There are no classes beyond degree 2r, so
H∗(CPr;Z)≅Z[x]/(xr+1),∣x∣=2.​
(9)
The point case r=0 has ring Z. Taking the union of the finite projective spaces gives H∗(CP∞;Z)=Z[x], since each fixed cohomological degree stabilizes. This proves the cohomology ring of complex projective space computation.
For the rank bound for a nonsingular complex bilinear map, write a=dimC​U, b=dimC​V, R=imϕ, and s=dimC​R. Assume a,b≥1. Injectivity with either nonzero factor fixed implies ϕ(u⊗v)=0 whenever u,v=0. Therefore
F:CPa−1×CPb−1⟶CPs−1,([u],[v])⟼[ϕ(u⊗v)]
(10)
is well defined and continuous. Scaling either vector only scales the output, and in local projective charts the formula is given by bilinear coordinates with no simultaneous zero. Let h be the target's degree-two generator and x,y the pullbacks of the two factor generators. The Künneth theorem gives
H∗(CPa−1×CPb−1;Z)=Z[x,y]/(xa,yb).
(11)
Fixing [v] or [u] makes F a projective linear embedding induced by an injective linear map. Such an embedding pulls back the hyperplane class to the factor's hyperplane class. Since the two restrictions determine H2 of the product, F∗h=x+y, with the generator of a point factor interpreted as zero.
Put d=a+b−2. In the source cohomology ring, every term in (x+y)d vanishes except the one with exponents a−1,b−1, and
F∗(hd)=(x+y)d=(a−1a+b−2​)xa−1yb−1=0.
(12)
The binomial coefficient is a positive integer and the final monomial generates the top integral cohomology. If s≤d, the class hd in CPs−1 would be zero. This contradiction proves
rankϕ≥dimC​U+dimC​V−1.​
(13)
When a=b=1, the nonzero pure-tensor image already gives s≥1. A positivity convention is genuinely necessary: if U=0, V=C2, and W=0, all restrictions to the zero tensor subspaces are injective, but the literal bound would read 0≥1. The displayed theorem is therefore the nonzero-factor version. If a nonzero factor is infinite-dimensional, either factor restriction already bounds the image dimension below by that factor dimension; the sum of two positive cardinal dimensions, with at least one infinite, is their maximum, giving the corresponding cardinal bound.

Use cohomology with coefficients in F2​=Z2​. The cellular homology of real projective space has one cell in degrees zero, one and two, and its boundary coefficients become zero modulo two. Hence there is a one-dimensional cohomology group in each of those degrees. Let x∈H1(P;F2​) be the Poincare dual of a projective line. Two distinct projective lines intersect transversely in one point, so ⟨x2,[P]2​⟩=1. Thus
H∗(P;F2​)=F2​[x]/(x3),∣x∣=1.​
(14)
This is the mod-two cohomology ring of real projective space in dimension two. The Künneth theorem over a field makes the cross product an isomorphism of graded rings. Writing x,y for the factor pullbacks gives
H∗(P×P;F2​)=F2​[x,y]/(x3,y3).​
(15)
There are no additional signs over F2​.
The obstruction to a graph avoiding the diagonal is the mod-two diagonal class of the real projective plane. Its dual class D∈H2(P×P;F2​) is determined by
⟨D⌣z,[P×P]2​⟩=⟨i∗z,[P]2​⟩,
(16)
where i:P→P×P is the diagonal embedding. Test z=x2,xy,y2. Each diagonal pullback is x2, with evaluation one. Since x2y2 is the product's top generator, comparison of coefficients gives
D=x2+xy+y2.
(17)
If the submanifold were the graph of a smooth map f:P→P, its parametrization would be j=(id,f). For some ε∈F2​, f∗x=εx, and therefore
j∗D=(1+ε+ε2)x2=x2=0.
(18)
On the other hand, the Poincare dual of the diagonal comes from its relative Thom class in H2(P×P,(P×P)∖Δ;F2​). It restricts to zero on the complement. A graph disjoint from the diagonal would factor through that complement and force j∗D=0, a contradiction. Such a submanifold cannot be the graph of a smooth map P→P. The relative Thom-class argument also applies to continuous self-maps.

Take CP3. It is a closed smooth manifold and is simply connected: its CW complex structure has no one-cells, and attaching cells of dimension at least two to a point cannot create a fundamental group. Its cohomology ring is Z[x]/(x4) with ∣x∣=2, so x3=0.
The theorem used is the Lusternik–Schnirelmann critical point theorem: every smooth real-valued function on a closed connected smooth manifold has at least its unnormalized Lusternik–Schnirelmann category many distinct critical points, without a nondegeneracy assumption. Here category is the minimum number of open subsets covering the manifold whose inclusions are null-homotopic. The cup length bound gives category at least four. To justify that bound, a cover by three categorical sets would make each positive-degree class vanish on its assigned set. The long exact sequence of each pair lifts three copies of x to relative classes, and their relative cup product would lie in H6(M,M;Z)=0, contradicting x3=0. Thus every smooth function has at least four critical points, including functions with degenerate critical points. The critical-point theorem in this formulation is described in Conley pairs in geometry—Lusternik–Schnirelmann theory and more.

Under the intended convention that the finite group is nontrivial, take CP2. It is simply connected by its CW complex structure. The Lefschetz fixed-point theorem says that a continuous self-map of a compact triangulable space with nonzero rational Lefschetz number has a fixed point.
For any continuous f:CP2→CP2, write f∗x=dx for the integral degree-two generator, with d∈Z. Preservation of the cup product gives f∗(x2)=d2x2. Only degrees zero, two and four contribute to the rational Lefschetz number, so
L(f)=1+d+d2=(d+21​)2+43​>0.
(19)
Consequently every such map has a fixed point. In a free group action, each nonidentity group element must act by a fixed-point-free homeomorphism. This is impossible here, so no nontrivial finite group can act freely. This is the fixed-point property of even-dimensional complex projective space for complex dimension two. If the trivial group is included, the literal request has no example, since the trivial group acts freely on every space.

Take the Möbius line bundle over S1,
L=([0,1]×R)/((1,t)∼(0,−t)).
(20)
A continuous section of a vector bundle corresponds to a continuous real-valued function s:[0,1]→R with s(1)=−s(0). If s(0)=0, it already vanishes. Otherwise its endpoint values have opposite signs, so the intermediate value theorem gives s(t0​)=0 for some t0​∈(0,1). Thus this vector bundle has no nowhere-zero section. Every continuous section must vanish.

Take the Hopf map η:S3→CP1≅S2, given by η(z0​,z1​)=[z0​:z1​]. The domain has positive-dimensional homology only in degree three, while H3​(S2;Z)=0. In degree two the source group is zero, and all remaining positive-degree source groups are zero too. Hence the induced map on homology is zero in every positive degree.
For nontriviality, use the Hopf fibration S1→S3η​S2. It is a fiber bundle: phase multiplication gives each fibre, and choosing a normalized representative on either standard projective chart gives a local trivialization. The theorem invoked is the long exact sequence of homotopy groups of a fibration. Since the universal cover of S1 is contractible, π2​(S1)=π3​(S1)=0. Its exact sequence consequently gives an isomorphism
η∗​:π3​(S3)⟶π3​(S2).
(21)
The identity class of S3 is nonzero, since an identity map cannot be null-homotopic while inducing the identity on H3​(S3;Z). Its image under η∗​ is the class of η, so that class is nonzero. The Hopf map is not homotopic to a constant despite inducing zero on all positive-degree homology.

False. Take M=CP2#CP2 and N=S2×S2, with their standard product and connected-sum orientations. They are closed simply connected four-manifolds. For the connected sum, removing a four-ball leaves the fundamental group unchanged, and the Seifert-van Kampen theorem across the separating three-sphere preserves simple connectivity. The Mayer–Vietoris sequence computes integral cohomology Z,Z2,Z in degrees zero, two and four, and zero in other degrees. The Künneth theorem gives the same groups for S2×S2. Thus their Betti numbers agree and all their integral cohomology is torsion-free.
Their cohomology rings nevertheless differ. In M, let u,v come from the two projective summands and let z generate degree four. The cohomology ring of the connected sum of two complex projective planes has
u2=v2=z,uv=0.
(22)
These relations follow by using cycles in the separate summands and the degree-one pinch projections onto them. In N, the sphere-factor generators x,y satisfy
x2=y2=0,xy=z.
(23)
Consequently every degree-two class of N has square
(ax+by)2=2abz,
(24)
an even multiple of the top generator, whereas u2 in M is a primitive generator. An integral graded-ring isomorphism cannot change this property, since it sends a generator of H4 to its positive or negative. A homotopy equivalence induces a graded-ring isomorphism, so none exists. Equivalently, their intersection forms are diag(1,1) and (01​10​), an odd form and an even form respectively.

False. Take M=S4 and N=S3. Both are closed oriented simply connected manifolds and 4>3. A cohomological obstruction rules out even a topological fiber bundle.
Suppose a fiber bundle F→S4→S3 existed. The long exact sequence of homotopy groups of a fibration, including its component terms, makes F path-connected. A local trivialization over a small open ball in S3 identifies its preimage, an open subset of S4, with F×B3. Projection onto F×{0} gives a retraction. An open smooth four-manifold has the homotopy type of a CW complex of dimension at most four, so its rational cohomology vanishes in degrees above four. A retract's cohomology injects into that of the ambient space. Therefore
Hj(F;Q)=0(j>4).
(25)
Use the multiplicative cohomological Serre spectral sequence with rational coefficients. Since S3 is simply connected, there is no nontrivial coefficient action, and only columns zero and three occur on E2​. Thus E3​=E2​, and the only possibly nonzero differential is d3​. Let u be the degree-three base generator. It cannot survive, because the sequence converges to H∗(S4;Q) and that ring has zero degree-three part. It has no outgoing differential and its only possible incoming differential is from E30,2​. Hence there is a∈H2(F;Q) with
d3​a=u.
(26)
The class a is nonzero and, by the degree bound above, has a first vanishing power ar=0 for some r≥2. Multiplicativity says that d3​ is a graded derivation. Since ∣a∣=2 is even,
0=d3​(ar)=ruar−1.
(27)
But E33,2r−2​=H3(S3;Q)⊗H2r−2(F;Q), in which uar−1=0 by minimality of r; also r is nonzero over Q. This is a contradiction. Thus no such fiber bundle exists. This is the special case of a sphere cannot fibre over a lower-dimensional odd sphere required here.

α⌣β=±ωM​∈Hm(M;Z).

f∗ωM​=±f∗α⌣f∗β=0.
(29)
On the other hand, the definition of degree three gives f∗ωM​=3ωSm​=0. This contradiction proves the nonzero degree from a sphere obstructs manifold products assertion. The product with a zero-dimensional point factor is the excluded trivial case.

True: every integer occurs. The simple-connectivity assumptions in parts 1 and 2 are local to those statements and are not imposed here. For a connected closed oriented four-manifold, Poincare duality gives
χ(M)=1−b1​+b2​−b3​+1=2−2b1​+b2​,
(30)
since b3​=b1​. This expression has no fixed sign or parity.
Use χ(CP2)=3, from its three even-dimensional cells, and χ(S1×S3)=0, by Euler characteristic of a product. The Euler characteristic of a connected sum in dimension four is
χ(A#B)=χ(A)+χ(B)−2.
(31)
It follows either by Mayer–Vietoris sequence after deleting balls, or by the intermediate-degree cohomology splitting and the single top and bottom generators. Therefore a connected sum of r projective planes and s copies of S1×S3 has
χ=2+r−2s.
(32)
Given an integer h, choose an integer s≥max{0,⌈(2−h)/2⌉} and put r=h−2+2s≥0. This produces a connected closed oriented four-manifold of Euler characteristic h. When r=s=0, use S4. Thus

Work with integral cohomology. For a frame v=(v1​,…,vk​) in the complex Stiefel manifold, define
Ev​={w∈Cn:⟨w,vi​⟩=0 for every i}.
(34)
These fibres form the complement bundle on a complex Stiefel manifold, a complex vector subbundle of the trivial rank-n bundle. Indeed
Pv​=I−∑i=1k​vi​vi∗​
(35)
is a smooth orthogonal projector of constant complex rank n−k; locally projecting suitable fixed basis vectors and applying Gram-Schmidt process provides bundle frames. The underlying real vector bundle has rank r=2(n−k) and is oriented by its complex structure: a complex change of frame has real determinant equal to the positive number ∣detC​A∣2.
A point of its sphere bundle is (v1​,…,vk​,w) with ∥w∥=1 and w orthogonal to the frame. Appending w gives a diffeomorphism
S(E)≅Vk+1​(Cn),​
(36)
whose inverse forgets the last vector while retaining it as the unit vector in the complement. Thus the asserted homotopy equivalence is actually an identification of sphere bundles, for 0≤k<n.
Start the induction at V0​(Cn)={point}, with cohomology the exterior algebra on no generators. Suppose
H∗(Vk​(Cn);Z)=ΛZ​(a2n−1​,a2n−3​,…,a2n−2k+1​).
(37)
This is a free abelian group in every degree, and for k≥1 its smallest positive degree is 2n−2k+1=r+1. Hence Hr(Vk​;Z)=0. This vanishing holds at k=0 as well, since the base is a point and r=2n>0. The Euler class e(E) belongs to this zero group and is therefore zero.
For this oriented rank-r vector bundle, the Gysin sequence of a sphere bundle has segment
Hj−r(Vk​)⌣e(E)​Hj(Vk​)π∗​Hj(Vk+1​)π!​​Hj−r+1(Vk​)⌣e(E)​Hj+1(Vk​).
(38)
Because e(E)=0, this becomes
0⟶Hj(Vk​)π∗​Hj(Vk+1​)π!​​Hj−r+1(Vk​)⟶0.
(39)
In particular H∗(Vk+1​;Z) is torsion-free. At j=r−1, choose a class a∈Hr−1(Vk+1​;Z) with π!​a=1. Its restriction to the sphere fibre is an integral generator. The projection formula for sphere bundle integration, with the conventional fibre-integration sign, is
π!​(π∗b⌣a)=b⌣π!​a=b.
(40)
It can be obtained from the relative cohomology of the disk/sphere pair and the Thom isomorphism theorem. Consequently every cohomology class of Vk+1​ is uniquely expressible as π∗c+π∗b⌣a: subtract the second term using its fibre integral, then use exactness to identify the remaining term; injectivity of π∗ and the projection formula prove uniqueness. This establishes an additive splitting by the old monomials and the old monomials multiplied by a.
To establish the ring, ∣a∣=r−1 is odd, so graded commutativity of the cup product gives a2=−a2, and hence 2a2=0. The torsion-freeness just proved now gives a2=0. Graded commutativity also gives the required anticommutation with each old odd generator, and the old relations persist under the injective ring map π∗. The additive splitting proves that there are no additional relations. This is exactly Gysin ring splitting for an odd-dimensional sphere bundle, and supplies a new exterior generator of degree
r−1=2n−2k−1.
(41)
Induction therefore proves the full ring calculation
H∗(Vk​(Cn);Z)≅ΛZ​(a2n−1​,a2n−3​,…,a2n−2k+1​),0≤k≤n.​
(42)
Over Z, graded commutativity by itself only kills twice an odd square; torsion-freeness is the essential extra step in this integral cohomology of a complex Stiefel manifold proof.
Finally an ordered orthonormal n-frame is a unitary matrix, so Vn​(Cn)=U(n). Each exterior generator may be included once or omitted from a basis monomial. The rank of degree j is therefore the number of subsets of the generator degrees 1,3,…,2n−1 summing to j. Multiplication of their two-term generating factors gives
j≥0∑​bj​(U(n))tj=i=0∏n−1​(1+t2i+1).​
(43)
The highest exponent is 1+3+⋯+(2n−1)=n2, agreeing with the real dimension of the unitary group, and the total rank is 2n.
