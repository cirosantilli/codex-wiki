i OurBigBook.com
For a fixed fluid domain, consider an incompressible Newtonian Stokes flow with prescribed velocities on its boundaries and prescribed far-field behaviour. Conservative body forces, such as gravity, may be absorbed into the pressure. Among all sufficiently regular divergence-free trial velocity fields with exactly those boundary velocities and far-field behaviour, the Stokes solution minimizes the viscous dissipation
D[u]=2μ∫D​eij​(u)eij​(u)dV,eij​=21​(∂i​uj​+∂j​ui​).
(1)
To see this Minimum-dissipation theorem for Stokes flow, write a trial field as v=u+w, with ∇⋅w=0 and homogeneous velocity boundary data. The Newtonian fluid stress tensor satisfies ∇⋅σ=0, so integration by parts gives
2μ∫e(u):e(w)dV=∫σ:∇wdV=0.
(2)
Therefore
D[v]−D[u]=2μ∫e(w):e(w)dV≥0.​
(3)
Equality requires the difference to be a rigid motion, eliminated by the fixed wall and far-field data. The theorem varies the velocity field in one fixed geometry; it does not minimize over particle positions. If a nonconservative body force is prescribed, the appropriate variational functional also includes its work, rather than being the dissipation alone.

ii OurBigBook.com
Neglect tube-end effects and use the localized flow with no imposed throughflow, as implied by the sphere being the only cause of motion. Extend the fluid velocity through the sphere by its rigid-body velocity. This extended field is continuous across the surface by the no-slip boundary condition and divergence-free both inside and outside. The divergence theorem therefore makes its total flux through a complete tube section independent of height. In sections far from the sphere the fluid is quiescent, so this total flux is zero.
At height z cutting the sphere, let As​(z) be the area of its circular slice and take z upward. The translation contributes Vz​As​. The vertical component of Ω×(x−xc​) integrates to zero over that circle because its first moments about the centre vanish. Hence the zero total flux for localized tube Stokes flow gives
Qf​(z)=−Vz​As​(z)>0,​
(4)
where Vz​<0 for a falling sphere. Thus the net fluid flux is upwards in every section intersecting the sphere's interior, even though some fluid locally moves downwards. At a tangential section As​=0 and the flux vanishes. The zero-total-flux condition is important: an imposed throughflow or appreciable open-tube end effects would change this argument.

The Minimum-dissipation theorem for Stokes flow compares fields with fixed geometry and boundary velocities. Moving the sphere changes the geometry, while sedimentation prescribes its effective weight rather than its velocity. It therefore supplies no rule for migrating toward a position of least dissipation. Indeed, at fixed force W, the power is WV, and a lower drag resistance gives a larger settling speed and larger power, rather than a smaller power. This is a fixed-force comparison of minimum viscous dissipation issue.
The proposed migration is itself false in ideal Stokes flow. Reflect the uniform vertical tube in a horizontal plane through the sphere centre. The sphere's lateral position is unchanged, but the applied axial force reverses. Reflection leaves a transverse translational velocity unchanged. By Linearity of Stokes flow, however, reversing the force reverses that transverse velocity. It must therefore be zero. This proves no lateral migration of a single sphere in a uniform tube in Stokes flow:
a single rigid sphere retains its distance from the tube centreline.​
(5)
Rotation and a wall-dependent vertical speed are allowed. Inertial lift, particle deformation or tube-end effects belong to different problems and do not follow from the dissipation theorem.

Let downward velocity be positive in this part, and let W>0 be the dense sphere's weight minus buoyancy. For the original centreline configuration, write its six-component translation-and-rotation velocity as q, and its diagonal positive hydrodynamic resistance matrix as R0​. Its single-sphere dissipation is
D0​(q)=qTR0​q=Rzz​Vz2​+∑β=z​Rββ​qβ2​.
(6)
With only axial force and no torque, the original velocity is V0​=W/Rzz​.
Now add the neutral sphere. In its actual Stokes flow, extend the velocity rigidly through the new sphere's interior. This extension is a valid incompressible trial field for the domain without that sphere, with exactly the dense sphere's actual six boundary velocities; its strain and dissipation inside the inserted ball are zero. The Minimum-dissipation theorem for Stokes flow and extra dissipation due to a rigid inclusion therefore give
D≥D0​(q)≥Rzz​Vz2​.
(7)
The neutral sphere has zero net external force and torque, and the dense sphere has only the axial force W. The dissipation equals total external power, so D=WVz​. Positivity gives Vz​>0, and the comparison yields Vz​≤W/Rzz​ without assuming that either sphere translates only vertically or that either angular velocity vanishes.
For a finite nonoverlapping placement, the first inequality is strict. Equality would make the extended field the old Stokes solution and thus make that solution rigid throughout the added ball. Analyticity and connectedness then force its strain to vanish throughout the original fluid domain; the stationary tube wall and quiescent far field would force the whole motion to vanish, contradicting Vz​>0. Hence the force-free inclusion reduces axial mobility of a centred settling sphere:
0<Vz​<V0​.​
(8)
The difference tends to zero as the added sphere recedes infinitely far away. The lateral and rotational contributions are nonnegative in the resistance comparison, which is precisely why they cannot invalidate the bound.

Let δ≪a be the minimum gap as the spheres approach, and w=−δ˙>0 their normal relative approach speed. For smooth equal-radius spheres the gap has local form δ+O(s2/a), so the thin-gap region has lateral scale ℓ∼(aδ)1/2. Incompressibility drives a radial escape speed of order wℓ/δ. The lubrication pressure therefore scales as
p∼μδ3wℓ2​∼δ2μaw​,Fn​∼pℓ2∼δμa2w​.
(9)
The available gravitational forcing remains finite. Thus lubrication resistance gives w=O(Wδ/(μa2)): the normal approach becomes proportionally slower as the gap closes. In particular, for a bounded coefficient C, δ˙≥−Cδ, so δ(t)≥δ(t1​)e−C(t−t1​)>0 at every finite time. Equivalently the estimated time to reach zero contains ∫0δ1​​dδ/δ, which diverges. This is how lubrication prevents finite-time collision of smooth spheres.
The small nonzero initial lateral offset breaks the exactly collinear configuration; the spheres can deflect sideways and pass with a positive gap rather than touch. The condition R>2a leaves space for a side-by-side passage. Smooth surfaces, continuum hydrodynamics and the no-slip boundary condition are essential to the noncontact conclusion; the argument is not a model of surface roughness or molecular contact.

Choose time tc​ when the passing sphere centres are at the same height, and let S reflect positions in that horizontal plane. For each instantaneous configuration, Stokes flow determines particle velocities uniquely and linearly from the fixed external forces. Time reversal reverses all velocities and the dense sphere's applied force; reflecting in the horizontal plane reverses that force again. The neutral sphere remains force-free under both operations. Thus the reflected time-reversed motion obeys the same original forcing.
At tc​, reflection fixes both particle centres. Since the spheres have no orientational shape variables and there is no contact, uniqueness of the particle-trajectory initial-value problem makes the reflected backward motion coincide with the forward motion:
rj​(tc​+τ)=Srj​(tc​−τ),j=1,2.​
(10)
This reflection symmetry of a two-sphere passing trajectory fixes the transverse coordinates and reverses the relative axial separation. Consequently, at matching large separations above and below, both spheres return to their original distances from the centreline. Their intermediate lateral excursions are undone. This uses kinematic reversibility of Stokes flow, geometric reflection symmetry and the noncontact result, not a dissipation-minimizing trajectory. It assumes the spheres pass one another; a perfectly collinear approach would not supply the side-by-side symmetry event.

When both spheres have the same effective downward weight, the second sphere adds a force-driven disturbance instead of being merely a force-free rigid inclusion. The power now contains both WV1z​ and WV2z​, so the one-forced-sphere bound from part (iv) no longer applies.
For a≪R, place the spheres in a tandem configuration with a≪s≪R. Locally the flow around the second sphere is dominated by its downward Stokeslet. Its axial incident velocity at the first sphere is downward, so the first sphere falls faster to leading order. By contrast, the zero total flux for localized tube Stokes flow requires compensating upward flow away from the downward-moving particle core in sections cutting the sphere. There are therefore positions in the return flow where the disturbance acts upwards.
One can choose the second sphere in such an upward-flow region generated by the first. The Lorentz reciprocal theorem makes the axial cross coefficients of the hydrodynamic mobility matrix equal, so the downward force on the second sphere then produces an upward contribution to the first sphere's velocity. With small well-separated spheres, this force-driven interaction dominates the higher-order self-resistance change from simply inserting the other sphere. In mobility notation, with downward positive,
V1z​≃V0​+WM12zz​,
(11)
and the cross coefficient can have either sign in the tube. Thus
V1z​>V0​ in a downward entraining configuration,V1z​<V0​ in a return-flow configuration.​
(12)
The distinction is the sign of the incident flow, not whether adding a second geometrical obstacle lowers a fixed-velocity dissipation.

One normalization of the Papkovich–Neuber representation uses a harmonic vector Φ and harmonic scalar ψ:
u=Φ−21​∇(x⋅Φ+ψ),p=−μ∇⋅Φ,∇2Φ=0,∇2ψ=0.​
(13)
These formulae give ∇⋅u=0 because ∇2(x⋅Φ)=2∇⋅Φ, and give μ∇2u=∇p. Rescaling the potentials gives the other common normalizations of the representation.
A point force requires a decaying, rotationally covariant field linear in F, with no length scale. Thus choose the harmonic monopole Φ=cF/r, ψ=0, for r>0. Differentiating gives
u=2c​(rF​+r3(F⋅x)x​),p=μcr3F⋅x​.
(14)
The Newtonian fluid stress tensor is σij​=−pδij​+μ(∂i​uj​+∂j​ui​), which simplifies to σij​=−3μc(F⋅x)xi​xj​/r5. Its traction resultant on a sphere, using an outward normal from the origin, is −4πμcF. Momentum balance with the force Fδ(x) therefore fixes c=1/(4πμ). The resulting Stokeslet is
ui​=8πμ1​(rFi​​+r3(F⋅x)xi​​),p=4πr3F⋅x​,σij​=−4π3​r5(F⋅x)xi​xj​​.​
(15)
An arbitrary constant pressure may be added. The stress has purely radial traction on concentric spheres, and its integrated traction −F supplies the required distributional point-force normalization.

b OurBigBook.com
Take the upward direction as ez​, with polar angle θ measured from it. The liquid is quiescent at infinity. On a translating clean inviscid bubble, the kinematic boundary condition is (u−Uez​)⋅n=0, and the stress-free boundary condition is zero tangential traction. The normal traction must balance uniform gas pressure and any constant-curvature surface-tension jump.
For a vertical Stokeslet of strength F, the radial velocity is ur​=Fcosθ/(4πμr). Its traction on a concentric sphere is purely normal, so zero tangential traction is automatic. The kinematic condition at r=a gives
F=4πμaU.​
(16)
The singularity is only a device for generating the exterior solution; there is no liquid point force inside the actual bubble. The integrated dynamic traction on the bubble is −Fez​, giving the clean-bubble Stokes drag. The upward buoyancy is 4πρga3/3, hence force balance gives
U=3μρga2​.​
(17)
The normal stress also shows that surface tension is not required for this particular spherical solution. Let the ambient hydrostatic pressure at the bubble centre be pc​, so on its surface ph​=pc​−ρgacosθ. Adding the Stokeslet's dynamic normal traction gives
σnntotal​=−pc​+(ρga−4πa23F​)cosθ=−pc​,
(18)
where the terminal buoyancy value of F cancels the dipole. This is the spherical clean bubble without surface tension balance: an initially spherical bubble can remain spherical in this ideal steady Stokes solution even when surface tension is zero. With uniform surface tension γ, choose gas pressure pb​=pc​+2γ/a; with γ=0, pb​=pc​. This proves compatibility of the spherical shape, not stability of arbitrary deformations or validity when inertia matters.

The force scale is F∼ρga3. At the distant free surface, the Stokeslet's pressure and viscous normal-stress scales are F/d2. Gravity restores a displaced surface by a pressure change ρgh, so
h∼ρgd2F​=O(d2a3​).​
(19)
Thus h/d=O((a/d)3)≪1, consistent with treating the boundary as approximately flat and impermeable at leading order. This uses the stipulated gravity-dominated restoring balance.
The liquid-air interface has negligible tangential traction. The stress-free image of a normal Stokeslet places an opposite point force at the reflection point, a distance 2d above the bubble centre. Across the flat surface the combined normal velocity is odd and the tangential velocity is even, so the normal velocity and tangential stress vanish there. A same-radius inviscid drop of density 2ρ has downward excess weight 4πρga3/3, exactly opposite the bubble's upward buoyancy. Its exterior Stokeslet therefore supplies this image, giving the asserted full-space comparison to leading order in a/d.
The image's incident axial velocity at the bubble is
uimage​=−4πμ(2d)F​=−8πμdF​.
(20)
To first reflection, the bubble translates with this locally uniform incident velocity plus its isolated rise speed F/(4πμa). Hence the bubble rise near a distant stress-free free surface is
Ub​=4πμaF​−8πμdF​=U(1−2da​+o(a/d)).​
(21)
Only the first relative correction is retained; the image's spatial variation and additional reflections affect higher terms.
The customary capillary-controlled small-deformation condition is a small capillary number,
Ca=γμU​=3γρga2​≪1,​
(22)
equivalently a small Bond number Bo=ρga2/γ. This is a sufficient condition, not a necessary condition for the special isolated spherical solution in part (b). More precisely, the image's strain is O(F/(μd2)), so its deformation-producing normal stress compared with γ/a is O(Bo(a/d)2). When exploiting the exact isolated solution, that smaller combination controls the leading boundary-induced distortion. If capillarity at the distant free surface is also retained, the gravity-dominated deflection scaling requires γ/(ρgd2)≪1; a≪γ/(ρg)​≪d allows both the usual spherical-bubble criterion and gravity-dominated surface restoration.

Let the undisturbed water surface be z=0, the oil's upper surface z=η(x,t) and its lower surface z=b(x,t), so h=η−b. Leading hydrostatic pressure in the nearly inviscid water is pw​=−ρw​gz relative to atmospheric pressure; in the oil it is pH​=ρg(η−z). Equality at the interface gives ρgh=−ρw​gb, hence
b=−ρw​ρ​h,η=ρw​ρw​−ρ​h.
(23)
Thus the horizontal hydrostatic driving gradient is
pH,x​=ρgηx​=ρg′hx​,g′=ρw​ρw​−ρ​g.​
(24)
This reduced gravity accounts for most of the oil thickness being below the undisturbed water level.
Because the oil is much more viscous than the water and air, tangential tractions on both faces are negligible. In a thin layer, the leading horizontal velocity is consequently independent of depth: the floating extensional viscous gravity current is a plug, rather than the parabolic profile of a no-slip gravity current. Integrating incompressibility ux​+wz​=0 between the moving material boundaries, and using their kinematic boundary condition, gives conservation of oil volume,
ht​+(hu)x​=0,ht​+uhx​+hux​=0.​
(25)
To derive the factor 4 in the extensional balance, incompressibility gives wz​=−ux​. The Newtonian fluid stress tensor has σzz​=−p−2μux​. Normal-stress balance relative to the hydrostatic part therefore requires p=pH​−2μux​. The horizontal stress then satisfies
σxx​=−p+2μux​=−pH​+4μux​.
(26)
Thus the longitudinal tensile force in an oil slice, relative to its hydrostatic pressure, is 4μhux​ per unit width. Balancing its difference across the slice with the horizontal driving force −hpH,x​dx yields
4μ(hux​)x​=hpH,x​,h4μ​(hux​)x​=pH,x​.​
(27)
The coefficient is the planar extensional Trouton ratio, arising from both the direct horizontal strain and the pressure correction required by the transverse normal-stress condition.
Using the pressure gradient above, integration in x gives
4μhux​=21​ρg′h2+C(t).
(28)
The stated nose stress condition sets C(t)=0, not an arbitrary imposed inlet tension. Consequently
ux​=kh,k=8μρg′​.
(29)
For a material slice injected at time t0​, let τ=t−t0​ be its age. The thickness equation along its Lagrangian trajectory is dh/dt=−hux​=−kh2, with h=h0​ at injection. Define
T=kh0​1​=ρg′h0​8μ​.
(30)
Then h=h0​/(1+τ/T). The slice injected during dt0​ has volume h0​u0​dt0​ per unit width, so at fixed observation time t,
−h∂t0​∂x​=h0​u0​,∂τ∂x​=u0​(1+Tτ​).
(31)
Since a newly injected slice has x(t,t)=0, integration and subsequent differentiation along a material slice give the constant-flux floating extensional gravity current:
h(t;t0​)=1+(t−t0​)/Th0​​,u(t;t0​)=u0​(1+Tt−t0​​),x(t;t0​)=u0​[(t−t0​)+2T(t−t0​)2​].​
(32)
The oldest slice forms the nose. Measuring time from the start of injection, t0​=0 there, so
xN​(t)=u0​t+16μρg′h0​u0​​t2,hN​(t)=1+t/Th0​​.​
(33)
Its speed x˙N​=u0​(1+t/T) is exactly the material velocity at the nose, as required.
For a fixed position x behind the nose, the age needed to reach it is determined by x/u0​=τ+τ2/(2T), independently of the observation time. Hence both thickness and velocity become stationary there once the nose has passed. Eliminating τ gives
h(x)=h0​(1+4μu0​ρg′h0​x​)−1/2,u(x)=u0​(1+4μu0​ρg′h0​x​)1/2.​
(34)
Their product is h0​u0​, so the steady profile carries precisely the inlet volume flux; integrating it to xN​(t) gives the total injected volume h0​u0​t.
The length
ℓ=ρg′h0​μu0​​​
(35)
is the horizontal distance over which reduced-gravity driving causes an order-one change in inlet speed and thickness by viscous extension. The associated age is T=8ℓ/u0​; the exact profile contains x/(4ℓ). It is an extensional adjustment length, not a shear-diffusion scale. The result applies while the stated thin-layer and negligible-inertia approximations remain valid.

Take positive x in the snail's direction of travel, and let y measure height above the rigid plane. The wave moves at laboratory velocity U−V. In the wave frame the plane's horizontal velocity is V−U and the foot's is V. The foot is stationary as a shape, so its material velocity is tangent to y=h(x): to leading lubrication theory order its components are (V,Vhx​). Apply the no-slip boundary condition to both surfaces.
The leading Stokes flow equations are py​=0 and μuyy​=px​. Their solution is the local Couette-Poiseuille flow
u(x,y)=V−U+h(x)Uy​+2μpx​​y(y−h).​
(36)
The normal-velocity condition on each steady surface and incompressibility make the wave-frame flux independent of x. Integrating the velocity gives
q=h(V−2U​)−12μh3px​​.
(37)
Put C=V−U/2. Periodicity of pressure gives 0=∫0L​px​dx=12μ(CI2​−qI3​), and hence
q=CI3​I2​​,px​=12μ(h2C​−h3q​),Ij​=∫0L​h−jdx.​
(38)
The shear traction exerted by the fluid on the plane, positive in the x direction, is
τ0​=μuy​(x,0)=hμU​−2h​px​=hμ(4U−6V)​+h26μq​.​
(39)
The snail is force-free horizontally: its gait consists of internal motion and it has no externally applied horizontal drive. Uniform locomotion therefore requires zero net horizontal fluid force on the foot per wavelength. The stresses on opposite periodic fluid faces cancel, so horizontal momentum balance also makes the net force on the plane zero. Thus
0=∫0L​τ0​dx=μ(4U−6V)I1​+6μqI2​.
(40)
Substitution of q proves the lubrication model of a crawling snail:
U=4−3α6(1−α)​V,α=I1​I3​I22​​.​
(41)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to h−1/2 and h−3/2 gives 0<α≤1, with equality exactly for a uniform gap. Thus the denominator is positive, and a nonuniform waveform gives forward motion; the flat gap gives U=0.
For the sinusoidal shape, the given integrals yield
I1​=h0​1−A2​L​,I2​=h02​(1−A2)3/2L​,I3​=h03​(1−A2)5/2L(1+A2/2)​.
(42)
Therefore α=1/(1+A2/2) and
U=1+2A23A2​V.​
(43)
For small amplitude, U∼3A2V: the leading locomotion is quadratic, and there is no propulsion from a flat foot. As A→1−, U→V, so the wave becomes nearly stationary in the laboratory frame while the snail advances relative to it. However, the minimum gap tends to zero; finite speed in this limit does not imply finite dissipation, and A=1 itself is excluded by the noncontact model.
For the general waveform, use the mechanical work identity for viscous dissipation. Pressure does no boundary work because the boundary motion is tangential in the wave frame. The plane's velocity is constant, and its total force is zero, so it contributes zero net work. The upper boundary supplies, at leading lubrication order,
Φ=μV∫0L​uy​(x,h)dx=V(μUI1​+21​∫0L​hpx​dx).

The zero plane-force relation is μUI1​=21​∫hpx​dx. Consequently
Φ=2μUVI1​.​
(45)
As a direct check, integrating the squared shear rate gives Φ=∫0L​[μU2/h+h3px2​/(12μ)]dx=μI1​[U2+12C2(1−α)], which reduces to the same result using the force-free speed relation. Zero horizontal force on the foot does not mean zero gait work: the tangential material velocity along its sloping surface includes vertical motion.
For prescribed speed U>0 and sinusoidal shape, eliminate V=U(1+2A2)/(3A2) to obtain
Φ(A)=3h0​2μLU2​A21−A2​1+2A2​.
(46)
Writing y=A2, the logarithmic derivative of the amplitude-dependent factor is
1+2y2​−y1​+2(1−y)1​=0⟺2y2+3y−2=0.
(47)
The only root in 0<y<1 is y=1/2. The dissipation diverges at both endpoints, so this is the global minimum and gives the optimal sinusoidal waveform for viscous snail locomotion:
Aopt​=2​1​,Vopt​=34​U,Φmin​=382​​h0​μLU2​.​

The magically driven flat glider has simple Couette flow u=Uy/h0​ in the laboratory frame, and dissipation Φglide​=μLU2/h0​ per wavelength. Hence
Φmin​/Φglide​=82​/3.​

The comparison is at the same U, mean thickness h0​ and wavelength L; the flat glider is externally propelled, whereas the snail must power its internal waveform.
