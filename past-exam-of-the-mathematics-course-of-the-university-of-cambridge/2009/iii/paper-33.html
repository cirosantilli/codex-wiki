(3)
The strong law of large numbers applies to every integrable endpoint because the sample consists of independent and identically distributed random variables. For this finite collection the maximum tends to zero on a common probability-one event. Apply the argument for ε=1/m, m=1,2,…, and intersect those countably many events. On the resulting event the limsup is at most 1/m for every m, proving the uniform strong law from finite L1 bracketing. This pathwise argument also avoids assuming without justification that an arbitrary uncountable supremum is measurable.
For the Glivenko-Cantelli theorem, take ht​(x)=1{x≤t}​. Here is a finite bracketing construction which also handles atoms of the distribution. Choose 0<δ<ε and divide the possible values of F(t) into finitely many bins [rδ,(r+1)δ), including the final value one. For each nonempty parameter set Tr​={t:rδ≤F(t)<(r+1)δ}, set lr​=inft∈Tr​​ht​ and ur​=supt∈Tr​​ht​ pointwise. These functions are indicators of rays, possibly with an open endpoint, so they are measurable. Monotonicity of ht​ and monotone limits at the extreme thresholds give Plr​=inft∈Tr​​F(t) and Pur​=supt∈Tr​​F(t). Hence P(ur​−lr​)≤δ<ε. No continuity of F is required. Applying the proved uniform law gives

(4)
For an uncountable class of continuous functions, use H={ha​(x)=asinx:0≤a≤1}. The functions are distinct, bounded and integrable for every probability law. Moreover sup0≤a≤1​∣Pn​ha​−Pha​∣=∣Pn​sin−Psin∣→0 by the strong law of large numbers. This supplies the requested example without additional compactness machinery.

For a normalized kernel for density estimation K, with ∫K=1, the kernel density estimator is
f​n,h​(x)=nh1​i=1∑n​K(hx−Xi​​).​
(5)
It replaces each atom of the empirical measure by a translated, scaled copy of K: averaging these small probability-density bumps gives a smooth approximation to the sampling density. Integral one is part of the probability-kernel convention; without it, the estimator generally targets f(x)∫K.
The printed bandwidth assumption is insufficient for the claimed normal limit. The additional condition nhn​→∞ is needed at points with positive density. For a counterexample, take a normal distribution density f, x=0, the unit-width box kernel and hn​=n−2. Then nhn3​→0, but the probability that any observation lies in the window is at most n∥f∥∞​hn​→0. On the complementary event the estimator is zero, and nhn​​(f​n,hn​​(0)−f(0))=−f(0)/n​→0. The limit in probability is therefore zero, whereas the stated normal limit has positive variance.
We now prove the intended pointwise central limit theorem for a kernel density estimator under h=hn​→0, nh→∞ and nh3→0. The first condition also follows from the last one. A mean value theorem bound, with L=∥f′∥∞​, gives the bias of a kernel density estimator
∣Ef​n,h​(x)−f(x)∣=​∫K(u)(f(x−hu)−f(x))du​≤Lh∫∣u∣K(u)du.

Thus the bias multiplied by nh​ tends to zero.
The Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem says that independent centered variables in each row of a triangular array have sum converging in distribution to N(0,σ2) if the sum of their variances tends to σ2>0 and, for every η>0, the sum of their truncated second moments E[Zn,i2​1{∣Zn,i​∣>η}​] tends to zero. The sum is exactly nh​(f​n,h​(x)−Ef​n,h​(x)). Combining the centered limit with the negligible bias by the Slutsky theorem proves

Let ϕ be the scaling function and ψ an orthonormal wavelet, with ϕj,k​(x)=2j/2ϕ(2jx−k) and ψj,k​(x)=2j/2ψ(2jx−k). For f∈L2(R), the wavelet series at a coarse level j0​ is
f=∑k∈Z​⟨f,ϕj0​,k​⟩ϕj0​,k​+∑j≥j0​​∑k∈Z​⟨f,ψj,k​⟩ψj,k​.
(10)
The series converges in the L2 norm. Its coarse part captures broad variation and successive wavelet levels add finer detail. Truncating at levels j<J produces the orthogonal projection onto VJ​; Parseval identity makes its squared approximation error the sum of the omitted squared coefficients. Merely square-integrable functions need not have pointwise convergence.
For the Haar wavelet, use the cell convention Ij,k​=(k2−j,(k+1)2−j] and let Ij​(x) be the unique such interval containing x. The Haar scaling functions are 2j/21Ij,k​​, so the Haar projection is
Kj​f(x)=2j∫Ij​(x)​f(t)dt.
(11)
For a merely locally integrable function this cell-average formula remains defined, even if a global L2 orthogonal projection is unavailable. If ∥f′∥∞​=L, the mean value theorem gives Lipschitz continuity and, since ∣t−x∣≤2−j on the cell,
∣Kj​f(x)−f(x)∣≤2j∫Ij​(x)​L∣t−x∣dt≤L2−j.
(12)
Thus the requested bound holds with c=∥f′∥∞​​, by the Haar projection error for a Lipschitz function.
To estimate a density, replace each scaling or wavelet coefficient ∫fϕj,k​ or ∫fψj,k​ by the sample average of that basis function. The finite-resolution Haar density estimator is therefore
f​jW​(x)=∑k​(n1​∑i=1n​ϕj,k​(Xi​))ϕj,k​(x)=n2j​∑i=1n​1Ij​(x)​(Xi​).
(13)
It is the histogram on the dyadic cells, is nonnegative, and integrates to one. Its expectation is Kj​f(x). A density with bounded derivative is bounded: the Lipschitz density height bound gives f(x)2≤L by integrating the triangular lower envelope (f(x)−L∣t−x∣)+​. The case L=0 cannot be a probability density on the whole real line. Hence B=∥f∥∞​<∞.
The cell probability is pj​(x)=∫Ij​(x)​f≤B2−j. The sample count has a binomial distribution, so
Var(f​jW​(x))=n22j​pj​(x)(1−pj​(x))≤nB2j​.
(14)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality bounds the mean centered absolute deviation by the square root of this variance. Adding the projection bias gives
E∣f​jW​(x)−f(x)∣≤L2−j+nB2j​​.
(15)
Choose jn​=⌊(log2​n)/3⌋, so 2jn​≍n1/3. The two terms have the same order, and
E∣f​jn​W​(x)−f(x)∣=O(n−1/3).​
(16)
This is a bias-variance tradeoff: refining the cells decreases the averaging bias but increases the sampling variance.
The Haar projection averages this normal distribution density on each cell; the finer cells follow its variation more closely. A Haar density estimator replaces these exact cell probabilities by empirical frequencies and therefore also introduces sampling noise.

This is the absolute error bound for a ratio estimator with a controlled denominator; it does not require the numerator and denominator to be independent.
