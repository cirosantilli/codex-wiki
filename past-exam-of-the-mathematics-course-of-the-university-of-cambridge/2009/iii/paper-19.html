An almost complex structure is a smooth real bundle endomorphism J:TM→TM satisfying J2=−I. In particular the real dimension is even, say 2m. After complexifying, the type decomposition of the complexified tangent bundle is
TC​M=T1,0M⊕T0,1M,T1,0M=ker(J−i),T0,1M=ker(J+i).
(1)
The corresponding differential forms of type (p, q) are smooth sections of Λp(T1,0M)∗⊗Λq(T0,1M)∗, regarded as alternating forms by the wedge product of differential forms. A form of type (p,q) vanishes unless its arguments include p vectors of type (1,0) and q of type (0,1).
Besides vanishing of the Nijenhuis tensor, two equivalent descriptions of an integrable almost complex structure are: the smooth sections of T0,1M are closed under Lie bracket; and every point has complex coordinates in which J is multiplication by i, with holomorphic changes of coordinates. The equivalence with existence of these coordinates is the Newlander-Nirenberg theorem for smooth J. A further equivalent condition is that the exterior derivative has only the two type components d=∂+∂ˉ, with no components of bidegree (2,−1) or (−1,2). Indeed evaluation of d of a (1,0)-form on two (0,1) vector fields detects the (1,0) component of their bracket; complex conjugation detects the other component.
For the almost complex structure induced by a complex atlas, write zj=xj+iyj and put J∂xj​=∂yj​, J∂yj​=−∂xj​. The differentials of holomorphic transition maps are complex linear, so these local definitions agree. In these coordinates J has constant coefficients and all coordinate vector fields commute. The expression defining the Nijenhuis tensor is tensorial, so evaluating it on this frame proves it is zero everywhere. Thus the induced structure is integrable.
Here is a direct proof for the smooth submanifolds with invariant complex tangent spaces are complex assertion. At a point of Y, its tangent space is a complex-linear subspace, of real dimension 2k. Make a complex-linear change of ambient holomorphic coordinates so that this tangent space is Ck×0. Projection onto the first k coordinates has invertible real differential on Y. The real inverse function theorem states that a smooth map between equal-dimensional manifolds with invertible differential is a diffeomorphism on sufficiently small neighbourhoods. It therefore writes Y locally as a smooth graph w=F(z) over an open set in Ck.
A tangent vector to this graph is (v,dF(v)). Its image under J is (iv,idF(v)). Tangent-space invariance implies
dF(iv)=idF(v),
(2)
so F satisfies the Cauchy-Riemann equations. Smooth functions satisfying these equations are holomorphic: restrict to each coordinate complex line, use the one-variable criterion, and apply the iterated Cauchy integral formula on a small polydisc to get jointly holomorphic coefficients. Hence the graph parametrization is holomorphic. Restrictions of ambient holomorphic coordinates supply holomorphic transition maps between these graph charts. The restricted structure on Y is an integrable complex structure. This also proves the induced structure agrees with the original restriction, rather than merely producing some complex structure on Y.
A nonsingular analytic subvariety is a closed analytic subset which near each of its points is the zero set of a holomorphic submersion of constant rank; equivalently, it is locally a coordinate subspace in holomorphic coordinates. The holomorphic implicit function theorem says that if a holomorphic map to Cr has differential of rank r at a zero, its nearby zero set is a complex submanifold of codimension r.
The homogeneous equation defines a subset of Complex projective space because multiplying a vector by λ=0 multiplies its quadratic value by λ2. Its gradient on C4 is 2Qz, which is nonzero for z=0. More explicitly, in an affine chart set zi​=1. If all derivatives of the restricted equation vanished at one of its zeros, all components of 2Qz except possibly the ith would vanish. The Euler homogeneous function theorem gives ∑j​zj​∂j​p=2p=0, forcing the remaining component to vanish as well. This contradicts invertibility of Q. The holomorphic implicit function theorem thus proves S is a smooth complex surface.
To see its geometry, reduce the nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form to diagonal form over C. There is a vector of nonzero self-pairing, since otherwise polarization would make the entire form zero. Normalize such a vector using a square root of its self-pairing. Its orthogonal complement remains nondegenerate, so induction gives an orthonormal basis. Apply the same argument to the nondegenerate form w0​w3​−w1​w2​: the two forms are equivalent under an invertible linear change of variables. The allowed projective linear transformation therefore reduces S to
Q0​={[w0​:w1​:w2​:w3​]:w0​w3​−w1​w2​=0}.
(3)
View its coordinates as the nonzero rank-one matrix A=(w0​w2​​w1​w3​​)=uvT. At [A], holding [u] fixed and varying [v] gives a projective line, and holding [v] fixed and varying [u] gives another. They are distinct and meet at [A]. In fact these are the only lines through it: if a second rank-one matrix is abT, the entire pencil has determinant zero only if det(u,a)det(v,b)=0, so one projective factor is fixed. These are the two rulings of a smooth quadric surface.
The Segre embedding is the holomorphic map
Φ:CP1×CP1⟶Q0​,([u0​:u1​],[v0​:v1​])⟼[u0​v0​:u0​v1​:u1​v0​:u1​v1​].
(4)
Every nonzero rank-one matrix has such a factorization, unique up to reciprocal rescaling, so Φ is bijective. Its inverse is holomorphic on each open set where a matrix entry aij​=0: the projective column [a0j​:a1j​] recovers [u], and the projective row [ai0​:ai1​] recovers [v]. These formulas agree on overlaps. Thus the Segre description of a smooth quadric surface gives the promised stronger conclusion
S≅CP1×CP1biholomorphically.​
(5)

On a complex manifold, the exterior derivative splits by type as d=∂+∂ˉ. The conjugate Dolbeault operator ∂ raises holomorphic degree by one and the Dolbeault operator ∂ˉ raises antiholomorphic degree by one. Their identities are
∂2=∂ˉ2=0,∂∂ˉ+∂ˉ∂=0.
(6)
Use the convention dc=i(∂ˉ−∂) for the d c operator; then ddc=2i∂∂ˉ. The alternative convention with a factor 1/2 changes this last factor only. Complex conjugation of differential-form type interchanges ∂ and ∂ˉ without reversing the wedge order. Thus, for a real form η,
i∂∂ˉη​=−i∂ˉ∂η=i∂∂ˉη.
(7)
The resulting form is real, independently of the degree or type components of η.
The Dolbeault cohomology is
Hp,q(X)=im(∂ˉ:Ap,q−1→Ap,q)ker(∂ˉ:Ap,q→Ap,q+1)​.
(8)
In degree (0,0) there is no incoming image, and the kernel consists of global holomorphic functions. On a compact connected X, ∣f∣ attains a maximum. In a coordinate polydisc around a maximizing point, the one-variable maximum modulus principle applied successively along the coordinate directions makes f constant on a smaller polydisc. Alternatively the same argument shows the maximum set is open; it is closed and nonempty, so connectedness makes it all of X, and the local constancy then makes f globally constant. Consequently
H0,0(X)≅C.​
(9)
A holomorphic p-form on a complex manifold is a form of type (p,0) with holomorphic coordinate coefficients, equivalently one killed by ∂ˉ. For degree n=dimC​X, it is d-closed: its ∂ˉ vanishes by holomorphicity and its ∂ has impossible bidegree (n+1,0). Its conjugate is also closed.
The key positivity behind nonzero holomorphic top forms are not exact is explicit. If α=fdz1​∧⋯∧dzn​, complex orientation gives
in2α∧αˉ=2n∣f∣2dx1​∧dy1​∧⋯∧dxn​∧dyn​.
(10)
This is nonnegative everywhere and positive on some open set when α≡0, so its integral on the compact manifold is strictly positive. If α=dγ, closedness of αˉ and the Stokes theorem would give
∫X​α∧αˉ=∫X​d(γ∧αˉ)=0.
(11)
Here Stokes is used on a compact oriented manifold without boundary, and complex-valued forms are handled by linearity. The contradiction proves a nonzero holomorphic top form is not d-exact. For dimension zero the same nonexactness follows directly from the absence of degree −1 forms.
Now take a compact complex surface, with no Kähler metric assumed. For a holomorphic one-form α, the form β=dα=∂α has type (2,0) and is holomorphic, since ∂ˉ∂α=−∂∂ˉα=0. It is exact. The top-form result forces β=0, proving closedness. This establishes holomorphic forms on a compact complex surface are closed by an argument which does not require a Kähler identity.
If a holomorphic one-form were exact, write α=df for a global smooth complex function. Comparing types gives ∂ˉf=0. The preceding compactness argument makes f constant on every connected component, hence α=0. Holomorphic two-forms are closed and nonexact unless zero by the top-form result. Holomorphic zero-forms are constant on each component, and a nonzero zero-form is never exact because there are no negative-degree forms. Degrees above two contain only zero forms. Therefore every holomorphic form on a compact complex surface is closed, and a nonzero one is never exact.

An irreducible complex analytic hypersurface is a closed analytic subset of pure complex codimension one which cannot be expressed as the union of two proper closed analytic subsets. Here a local defining function of a complex analytic hypersurface means a reduced local equation: a holomorphic germ generating its vanishing ideal, with each local branch occurring once. The word reduced matters. Merely prescribing the zero set would allow both f and f2, and the requested uniqueness would then be false.
The local facts used are these: in holomorphic coordinates the local ring OX,p​ is C{z1​,…,zn​}, a regular local ring and a unique factorization domain; its units are exactly the germs nonzero at p; and the analytic Nullstellensatz identifies the zero-set ideal of a germ f with (f)​. A reduced germ is a product of distinct irreducible factors. Unique factorization makes its principal ideal radical: if f∣hr, every irreducible factor of f divides h, so f∣h. Thus two reduced germs with the same hypersurface germ have (f)=(g). Write g=uf, f=vg. The local ring is a domain, so uv=1. Hence u is a holomorphic unit and u(p)=0. This proves reduced local defining functions differ by a holomorphic unit. Global irreducibility does not imply local irreducibility at a singular point; the same argument uses the product of all local branches and therefore covers that case too.
A divisor on a complex manifold is a locally finite integer combination of irreducible analytic hypersurfaces. On a compact manifold only finitely many have nonzero coefficients. Choose local meromorphic equations fi​ for a divisor D, by products of powers of reduced local equations. Their ratios are holomorphic units on overlaps. For the holomorphic line bundle associated to a divisor, choose frames satisfying
ei​=fi​fj​​ej​.
(12)
The ratios obey the cocycle identity and so define a holomorphic line bundle [D]. This convention makes the local meromorphic sections fi​ei​ agree, with divisor D. Changing the equations by units changes frames and gives an isomorphic bundle.
For hyperplanes H1​,H2​ in Complex projective space, choose nonzero linear equations ℓ1​,ℓ2​. The degree-zero quotient r=ℓ1​/ℓ2​ is a global meromorphic function and has divisor H1​−H2​. A principal divisor on a complex manifold gives a trivial associated bundle: using the same global meromorphic equation on every chart makes all frame transitions equal to one. The allowed tensor-product rule now gives
[H1​]⊗[H2​]−1≅[H1​−H2​]≅O,[H1​]≅[H2​].​
(13)
If the hyperplanes coincide the same conclusion is immediate. Equivalently, their common bundle is the hyperplane bundle O(1).
Locally at the centre of a blowup of a complex manifold at a point, replace a coordinate neighbourhood in Cn by
U={(z,[ℓ])∈U×CPn−1:zi​ℓj​=zj​ℓi​ for all i,j},σ(z,[ℓ])=z.
(14)
Away from zero the direction is uniquely [z], so this map is an isomorphism there; glue it to X∖{p}. In the chart ℓj​=0, write zj​=u and zi​=uti​ for i=j. These are smooth holomorphic coordinates on the blowup. The exceptional divisor is u=0, globally E=σ−1(p)≅P(Tp​X).
The canonical bundle formula for a point blowup is
KX​≅σ∗KX​⊗[E]⊗(n−1).​
(15)
The local chart also explains the exponent: pulling back a coordinate volume form gives ±un−1du∧⋀i=j​dti​, so the Jacobian section has divisor (n−1)E.
Since Y is smooth at the centre, choose coordinates with Y={z1​=0}. Its strict transform is a genuine analytic hypersurface. In a blowup chart j=1, it is the closed set t1​=0: off E, the original equation ut1​=0 reduces to t1​=0, and taking the closure restores the points with u=0. In chart j=1, there are no such off-exceptional points, and the strict transform is empty there. These descriptions agree on overlaps and show analyticity, with Y∩E≅CPn−2. The total transform has the extra factor u exactly once, so
σ∗Y=Y+E,[−Y]≅σ∗[−Y]⊗[E].
(16)
Under the given anticanonical assumption, this yields
[−Y]≅KX​⊗[E]⊗(2−n).​
(17)
To prove the only-if direction, one must rule out an accidental trivial power of [E]. The local blowup is the tautological line bundle over CPn−1, with E its zero section. Its normal bundle is therefore OE​(−1). The normal bundle of a smooth analytic hypersurface identifies [E]∣E​ with this bundle. If n≥2, restrict to a projective line inside E; the restriction of [E]⊗(2−n) has degree n−2. A trivial line bundle has degree zero, so it is trivial only if n=2. Conversely the displayed tensor factor is literally trivial when n=2. This is the anticanonical strict transform under a point blowup criterion:
[−Y]≅KX​ ⟺ n=2.​
(18)
For completeness, dimension one cannot satisfy the premise in this compact setting: an irreducible analytic hypersurface is one point, and [−Y] has degree −1 on its curve component, whereas KX​ has degree 2g−2. Thus no exceptional dimension-one case invalidates the equivalence.

A holomorphic line bundle is a complex rank-one bundle locally trivialized over holomorphic coordinate opens, with nowhere-zero holomorphic transition functions. A local holomorphic section has a holomorphic coefficient in a holomorphic local frame. Let h be a smooth positive Hermitian metric on its fibres. A Chern connection is a connection compatible with h whose (0,1) part is the bundle's Dolbeault operator.
Fix the Hermitian-product convention linear in the first variable and a nonvanishing holomorphic local frame e. Put he​=h(e,e)>0 and write ∇e=Ae. Compatibility with the holomorphic structure forces A0,1=0. Metric compatibility gives
dhe​=(A+Aˉ)he​,
(19)
so taking its (1,0) part determines A uniquely:
A=∂loghe​.​
(20)
Define the connection by this formula. To check it exists globally, change frame to e′=ge for a nonzero holomorphic function g. Then he′​=∣g∣2he​ and
∂loghe′​=∂loghe​+g−1dg,
(21)
which is exactly the connection transformation law obtained from ∇(ge)=dge+g∇e. Hence the local connections glue. The formula and its conjugate give metric compatibility, and A0,1=0 gives the required holomorphic structure. Since either condition already forced this unique formula in every frame, existence and uniqueness are proved.
For a line bundle, the curvature form of a connection is F=∇2=dA, as the scalar one-form satisfies A∧A=0. The local formula for the Chern connection on a line bundle therefore gives
Fh​=∂ˉ∂loghe​=−∂∂ˉloghe​.​
(22)
This formula uses a nowhere-zero local holomorphic section; arbitrary sections with zeros are not frames there. It has type (1,1), is d-closed and satisfies Fˉh​=−Fh​, so iFh​ is a real (1,1)-form. The transformation calculation makes its frame independence explicit.
For two metrics, their ratio r=h1​(e,e)/h2​(e,e) is a global positive smooth function, since the common frame factors cancel. The curvature difference of two Chern connections is
Fh1​​−Fh2​​=∂ˉ∂logr,iFh1​​−iFh2​​=∂ˉ(i∂logr).
(23)
Each curvature is ∂ˉ-closed, and the difference has a global (1,0) primitive for ∂ˉ. Thus
[iFh1​​]=[iFh2​​] in H1,1(X).​
(24)
This is equality in Dolbeault cohomology, rather than merely equality in de Rham cohomology; the displayed primitive proves precisely that distinction.
Finally let s be a nowhere-zero smooth section of L^. In this global smooth trivialization write ∂ˉL^​s=a⊗s with a global (0,1)-form a. The holomorphic structure has ∂ˉL^2​=0. Its Leibniz rule gives
0=∂ˉL^​(a⊗s)=(∂ˉa−a∧a)⊗s=(∂ˉa)⊗s,
(25)
since a scalar one-form wedges with itself to zero. The hypothesis H0,1(X)=0 makes a=∂ˉf for a global smooth complex function f. Then
∂ˉL^​(e−fs)=e−f(a−∂ˉf)⊗s=0.
(26)
The exponential never vanishes. Therefore e−fs is a nowhere-zero holomorphic section, proving smoothly trivial holomorphic line bundles when H01 vanishes constructively, without a compactness assumption.

Let g be the real Riemannian metric underlying the Hermitian metric, and let J be the complex structure. The fundamental form of a Hermitian manifold is
ω(u,v)=g(Ju,v).
(27)
The identity g(Ju,Jv)=g(u,v) implies g(Ju,v)=−g(u,Jv), so this is an alternating real two-form. Moreover ω(Ju,Jv)=ω(u,v). A two-form with this invariance has type (1,1): on two vectors of type (1,0), applying J to both multiplies its value by i2=−1, forcing that value to vanish; the same argument applies to type (0,1). Hence ω is a real (1,1)-form.
Use the complex-linear convention for the complex Hodge star operator, extending the real star. The complex orientation and metric define dV=ωn/n!, and
α∧∗βˉ​=⟨α,β⟩g​dV,∗:Ap,q⟶An−q,n−p,∗2=(−1)r(2n−r) on total degree r.
(28)
Here the Hermitian inner product is linear in the first argument. With this convention, on the even-dimensional underlying real manifold the formal adjoints are d∗=−∗d∗, ∂ˉ∗=−∗∂∗ and ∂∗=−∗∂ˉ∗. A conjugate-linear star is another convention, but then the adjoint formulas must change accordingly.
The Hodge Laplacian and Dolbeault Laplacian are
Δd​=dd∗+d∗d,Δ∂ˉ​=∂ˉ∂ˉ∗+∂ˉ∗∂ˉ.
(29)
Also write Δ∂​=∂∂∗+∂∗∂. They are nonnegative, since for example
(Δ∂ˉ​α,α)L2​=∥∂ˉα∥L22​+∥∂ˉ∗α∥L22​.
(30)
The requested Dolbeault Hodge decomposition on a compact Hermitian manifold is a compactness statement: on a compact Hermitian manifold without boundary,
Ap,q=H∂ˉp,q​⊕⊥∂ˉAp,q−1⊕⊥∂ˉ∗Ap,q+1,H∂ˉp,q​=kerΔ∂ˉ​.
(31)
The harmonic space is finite-dimensional, and taking the harmonic representative induces an isomorphism H∂ˉp,q​≅H∂ˉp,q​(X). Compactness is essential to this formulation; it is not asserted on an arbitrary noncompact Hermitian manifold.
Now suppose X is compact and Kähler, so dω=0. The permitted Kähler Laplacian identity is Δd​=2Δ∂ˉ​=2Δ∂​, making the three harmonic spaces identical in each bidegree. A d-closed pure-type form has ∂α=∂ˉα=0, since these two terms have different types. We will construct a Green operator potential for ddbar exactness, rather than invoking the ddbar lemma as the conclusion to be proved.
Let G be the inverse of Δ∂ˉ​ on the orthogonal complement of its harmonic forms, zero on the harmonic space. It is twice the Green operator of the Hodge Laplacian. The Hodge decomposition theorem and elliptic inversion give Δ∂ˉ​G=I−H, with H harmonic projection. It preserves bidegrees. The operators ∂ and ∂ˉ commute with Δ∂ˉ​: the first follows from Δ∂ˉ​=Δ∂​ and ∂2=0, and the second directly from ∂ˉ2=0. They and their formal adjoints annihilate harmonic forms, so they preserve the orthogonal complement of the harmonic space. Consequently they commute with G, by uniqueness of the inverse on the harmonic complement. Thus u=Gα and v=G2α are killed by both differential operators.
The assumed orthogonality makes Hα=Hu=0. Applying the two Laplacians gives
α=Δ∂ˉ​u=∂ˉ∂ˉ∗u,u=Δ∂​v=∂∂∗v.
(32)
We also need the mixed anticommutator, which follows from the permitted Laplacian identity rather than an extra unproved lemma. Expand Δd​ using d=∂+∂ˉ and d∗=∂∗+∂ˉ∗. Since Δd​=Δ∂​+Δ∂ˉ​, its mixed terms sum to zero. Their two bidegrees (1,−1) and (−1,1) are distinct, so each vanishes separately, in particular ∂ˉ∗∂+∂∂ˉ∗=0. Hence
α=∂ˉ∂ˉ∗∂∂∗v=−∂ˉ∂∂ˉ∗∂∗v=∂∂ˉ(∂ˉ∗∂∗v).
(33)
We have obtained the explicit answer
α=∂∂ˉβ,β=∂ˉ∗∂∗G2α∈Ap−1,q−1.​
(34)
The positive assumptions on p,q ensure these degrees are available. Conversely such a form is orthogonal to harmonic forms by integration by parts, so the same proof also explains the harmonic orthogonality criterion for ddbar exactness.
For injectivity of the Lefschetz operator on a Hermitian manifold on real one-forms, work at one point in a metric-orthonormal coframe (e1,f1,…,en,fn) with ω=∑j​ej∧fj. If a=0, a unitary change of coframe makes a=ce1, c=0. Then
a∧ω=c∑j=2n​e1∧ej∧fj=0(n>1),
(35)
since the displayed basis three-forms are linearly independent. Therefore L(a)=a∧ω has zero kernel. Injectivity on complex one-forms follows by taking real and imaginary parts, since ω is real. This pointwise proof of injectivity of powers of the Lefschetz operator for degree one does not require the hard Lefschetz theorem.
Closedness of ω gives ∂ˉL=L∂ˉ. Using the permitted Kähler identities, specifically [∂ˉ∗,L]=i∂, gives
[Δ∂ˉ​,L]=∂ˉ[∂ˉ∗,L]+[∂ˉ∗,L]∂ˉ=i(∂ˉ∂+∂∂ˉ)=0.
(36)
Multiplying by two proves [Δd​,L]=0. In particular the Lefschetz operator preserves harmonic forms and the injective map on one-forms restricts to an injection Hd1​↪Hd3​. The de Rham Hodge theorem identifies these real harmonic spaces with the corresponding real cohomology groups. Taking dimensions yields
b3(X)≥b1(X).​
(37)
