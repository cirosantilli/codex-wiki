The axisymmetric magnetic mode spans a one-dimensional real representation of axial rotations, so its signed amplitude is a real variable B. An equatorial dipole has two real horizontal components. Rotation through an angle ϑ rotates that pair; encoding the components by one complex amplitude gives A↦eiϑA, and rotation through π gives A↦−A. The velocity mode has the same rotational representation, so write it as V↦eiϑV. Magnetic reversal acts as
(B,A,V)⟼(−B,−A,V).
(1)
Thus the two relevant symmetries are axial rotation and magnetic reversal. Reflection symmetry has not been assumed, so complex coupling coefficients are permitted.
The equations for B,A must be linear in the magnetic variables at fixed velocity. Combining this with rotational equivariance, their general local form is
B˙A˙​=Bf(∣V∣2)+A∗Vg(∣V∣2)+AV∗g(∣V∣2)∗,=Ah(∣V∣2)+BVj(∣V∣2)+A∗V2ℓ(∣V∣2),​
(2)
where f is real valued and g,h,j,ℓ are complex valued. These functions can be understood through their local Taylor expansions; the symmetry argument does not require a convergent infinite series. Reality of B˙ explains its conjugate pairing.
For the velocity equation, a monomial BpAq(A∗)rVs(V∗)t must have the same rotational weight as V and be even under magnetic reversal. Its selection rules are
q−r+s−t=1,p+q+r is even.
(3)
A formal sum of all such monomials with complex coefficients is the most general local velocity evolution. At degree one there is V; at degree two there is AB; at degree three there are V∣V∣2,V∣A∣2,V∗A2,VB2. Expanding the magnetic equations similarly gives the equivariant three-mode dynamo normal form
B˙A˙V˙​=μ1​B+c1​A∗V+c1∗​AV∗−e∣V∣2B,=(μ2​+iω2​)A+c2​VB−d2​∣V∣2A−d3​V2A∗,=(μ3​+iω3​)V+c3​AB−d4​V∣V∣2−d5​V∣A∣2−d6​V∗A2−d7​VB2.​​
(4)
The μi​,ωi​,e are real, and the remaining coefficients are complex. The minus signs on cubic coefficients are a convention, not an assumption about their signs. Terms such as B3 and A∣A∣2 are forbidden in the magnetic equations by magnetic linearity, even though symmetry alone would allow some of them.
For the normalization of nonzero dynamo coupling coefficients, assume c1​c3​=0. Make the variable changes
B=B,A=qeiχA​A,V=veiχV​V,q=∣c1​c3​∣​1​,v=∣c1​∣∣c3​∣​​,χV​−χA​=−argc1​.
(5)
The transformed coefficients are c1​=qvei(χV​−χA​)c1​=1 and c3​=(q/v)ei(χA​−χV​)c3​, of modulus one. Thus
c1​=1,c3​=eiβ,β=arg(c1original​c3original​).​
(6)
The remaining phase is genuine; the two complex coefficients cannot both generally be made positive real. A zero coupling cannot be normalized to one, so the printed normalization presupposes this generic nonzero-coupling case.
Now impose the specified simplification, in the normalized variables, and write A=reiθ, V=seiϕ with r,s>0 and signed real B. Put ψ=ϕ−θ. Separating real and imaginary parts gives
B˙r˙s˙θ˙ϕ˙​ψ˙​​=(μ1​−es2)B+2rscosψ,=μ2​r+c2​sBcosψ,=(μ3​−d4​s2)s+rBcos(β−ψ),=ω2​+rc2​sB​sinψ,=ω3​+srB​sin(β−ψ),=ω3​−ω2​+srB​sin(β−ψ)−rc2​sB​sinψ.​
(7)
Constant moduli require the first three right sides to vanish. Since rs=0, the first equation also fixes cosψ, so a continuously evolving phase difference must be constant. Hence the last right side must vanish as well. Both phases then advance at one common frequency, giving a relative equilibrium. These four algebraic conditions and the displayed common-frequency equations govern all the requested nonzero constant-modulus solutions, without needing to solve them.
The pure-velocity branch is
B=A=0,V=s0​ei(ω3​t+ϕ0​),s02​=d4​μ3​​>0.
(8)
This presumes d4​=0; the usual saturated branch has μ3​,d4​>0. Near it, set A=ei(ω3​t+ϕ0​)(u+iv), B=b, and define Δ=ω2​−ω3​ and κ=μ1​−es02​. Magnetic feedback in the velocity equation is quadratic, so the first magnetic linearization is
dtd​​buv​​=​κc2​s0​0​2s0​μ2​Δ​0−Δμ2​​​​buv​​.
(9)
A branch of the stated relative equilibria has a zero growth rate in this rotating frame. Its determinant therefore gives the magnetic onset on a rotating velocity branch condition
(μ1​−ed4​μ3​​)[μ22​+(ω2​−ω3​)2]=2c2​μ2​d4​μ3​​.​
(10)
For μ22​+Δ2=0, the zero-mode relation is u+iv=−c2​s0​b/(μ2​+iΔ), so a nonzero b gives a nonzero A when c2​=0. With a transverse eigenvalue crossing and nondegenerate nonlinear saturation this is the generic magnetic bifurcation surface; magnetic reversal pairs its two signs. Degenerate cases require separate treatment. For example, if μ2​=Δ=0 and c2​=0, determinant zero is automatic but the stationary u equation forces b=0, so that zero mode does not establish the requested branch with both magnetic amplitudes nonzero.

The six critical Fourier modes are at ±k1​,±k2​,±k3​, with k1​+k2​+k3​=0. Reality of the pattern pairs each positive-wavevector coefficient with the conjugate coefficient at the negative wavevector. The leading critical part of the extended centre manifold parametrization is consequently
u(x,t)=∑i=13​Ai​(t)eiki​⋅x+complex conjugate+slaved higher harmonics.
(11)
The displayed six-mode expression is its leading-order projection; nonlinear harmonics on the full centre manifold need not vanish identically.
A translation d acts by Ai​↦eiki​⋅dAi​. Each monomial in the equation for Ai​ must therefore carry wavevector ki​. Since −ki+1​−ki+2​=ki​, the allowed quadratic term is Ai+1∗​Ai+2∗​. At cubic order the allowed terms are Ai​∣Ai​∣2,Ai​∣Ai+1​∣2,Ai​∣Ai+2​∣2. Rotation through 60 degrees acts, with one choice of orientation, as
(A1​,A2​,A3​)⟼(A2∗​,A3∗​,A1∗​).
(12)
Its square cyclically permutes the amplitudes, equating the coefficients across the three equations. Its cube conjugates every amplitude; equivariance under this half-turn makes all coefficients real. It does not interchange the two cross-coupling coefficients. A reflection would do that and force b=c, but that symmetry is expressly absent. Thus the chiral hexagonal amplitude equations are
A˙i​=μAi​+αAi+1∗​Ai+2∗​−Ai​(a∣Ai​∣2+b∣Ai+1​∣2+c∣Ai+2​∣2),​
(13)
at the retained order, with cyclic indices and real coefficients. The missing opening modulus bar in the last cubic term of the printed formula must be supplied; phase equivariance requires ∣Ai+2​∣2.
Write Ai​=ri​eiϕi​, with all three amplitudes nonzero, and let Φ=ϕ1​+ϕ2​+ϕ3​. Separating real and imaginary parts gives
r˙i​=μri​+αri+1​ri+2​cosΦ−ri​(ari2​+bri+12​+cri+22​),ϕ˙​i​=−αri​ri+1​ri+2​​sinΦ.
(14)
Hence the phase locking of a resonant hexagonal triad obeys
Φ˙=−α(r1​r2​r3​​+r2​r3​r1​​+r3​r1​r2​​)sinΦ.
(15)
For α>0, Φ=0 is phase stable and Φ=π is phase unstable. Two independent translation coordinates remove two phases; since the wavevectors sum to zero, the remaining phase is exactly Φ. Thus a stable fully active phase-locked pattern can be represented by three positive real amplitudes after choosing the spatial origin. The statement is modulo translations, not a restriction on every representative. Boundary states must also be retained: the zero solution is stable for μ<0, and rolls have two zero amplitudes. These are the necessary qualifications to a literal claim that all stable solutions are strictly positive.
In the real nonnegative subspace, a roll is
(A1​,A2​,A3​)=(r,0,0),r2=μ/a,μ>0,​
(16)
or a cyclic permutation. For hexagons let s=a+b+c, which is positive under the assumptions. The equal-amplitude branches solve μ+αr−sr2=0, giving
r±​=2sα±α2+4sμ​​,​
(17)
with only positive roots retained. The upper branch exists for μ≥−α2/(4s); the lower branch is positive for −α2/(4s)≤μ<0. They meet at a fold.
For a purported rectangle (p,p,q) with p,q>0, subtract its first two equilibrium equations after dividing each by its own amplitude. The quadratic terms agree, and the difference is (c−b)(p2−q2)=0. Since (b−a)(c−a)<0 implies b=c, it forces p=q. A state with exactly two nonzero amplitudes is also impossible because the quadratic term drives the zero third amplitude. Thus there are no nontrivial rectangles.
In fact no other unequal positive equilibria are possible under the straddling cross-couplings. Here is the uniqueness of positive hexagon equilibria with straddling cross-couplings argument. Put xi​=ri2​, p=r1​r2​r3​>0. The equilibrium equations imply
axi​+bxi+1​+cxi+2​−xi​αp​=μ.
(18)
Consider b>a>c, write B=b−a>0, C=c−a<0, and relabel cyclically so x1​ is maximal. If x2​≥x3​, subtracting equations one and two gives
B(x2​−x3​)+C(x3​−x1​)=αp(1/x1​−1/x2​).
(19)
The left side is positive unless all three are equal, while the right side is nonpositive. If x2​≤x3​, subtract equations two and three instead:
B(x3​−x1​)+C(x1​−x2​)=αp(1/x2​−1/x3​).
(20)
Now the left side is negative unless all are equal and the right side is nonnegative. Both cases force equality. For c>a>b, reverse the cyclic orientation and exchange b,c. Thus the only nonzero equilibria in this subspace are the stated rolls and hexagons.
At a roll the active radial eigenvalue is −2μ. The two inactive amplitudes have real-part linearization
(μ(1−c/a)αr​αrμ(1−b/a)​).
(21)
Its determinant is μ2(a−c)(a−b)/a2−α2μ/a<0. It therefore has a positive eigenvalue: the rolls are unstable for every μ>0, which is stronger than the large-μ conclusion. In the complex system there is also a neutral translation phase, and the imaginary transverse block has the same determinant.
At a positive hexagon the real-amplitude Jacobian is circulant, with diagonal d=−2ar2−αr and off-diagonals p=αr−2br2, q=αr−2cr2. Its uniform eigenvector (1,1,1) has eigenvalue
λ∥​=d+p+q=αr−2sr2.
(22)
The other two eigenvectors lie in the zero-sum subspace; using the two nontrivial cube roots of unity gives
λ±​=(b+c−2a)r2−2αr ±i3​(b−c)r2.​
(23)
For sufficiently large μ, r∼μ/s​, and the positive term (b+c−2a)r2 dominates the quadratic-coupling term 2αr. Thus the hexagons are unstable at large μ, while the uniform radial direction on the upper branch is stable. The two translation phases are neutral and the total phase has eigenvalue −3αr, so the amplitude instability is genuine.
More precisely, the upper branch is amplitude stable between its fold and the chiral hexagon Hopf threshold
rH​=b+c−2a2α​,μH​=(b+c−2a)22α2(4a+b+c)​.
(24)
At this threshold the conjugate pair crosses with nonzero frequency because b=c, permitting a Hopf bifurcation. The linear calculation locates the crossing; the nonlinear normal form decides the stability and direction of its periodic branch.
The dynamics are bounded in the retained model. For S=r12​+r22​+r32​ in the positive phase-locked subspace,
S˙=2μS+6αr1​r2​r3​−2aS2−2(b+c−2a)∑i<j​ri2​rj2​.
(25)
Since r1​r2​r3​≤(S/3)3/2, the negative quadratic-in-S term dominates at large amplitude. For large positive μ, the origin, rolls and hexagons are all unstable, so generic bounded dynamics cannot end at an asymptotically stable steady pattern. The chiral difference b−c favors successive changes in dominant roll orientation, in the spirit of the Küppers–Lortz instability. Oscillatory competition or periodic switching is the natural behavior near the Hopf transition; linear stability alone does not establish a unique global limit cycle. With α=0, coordinate planes are invariant and heteroclinic roll switching is possible. For α>0 a missing third mode is regenerated when two others are present, so that exact boundary-plane heteroclinic picture is broken. Finally, sufficiently large μ is a conclusion of the cubic truncation; far from onset, omitted higher-order terms can change the physical pattern dynamics.
