Past exam of the mathematics course of the University of Cambridge / 2009 / iii / Paper 35 / 5 / Solution

A Nash equilibrium is a profile in which each player's strategy is a best response to all the others: no unilateral change increases that player's payoff. In this routing game the payoff can be taken as minus delay. A pure strategy is a single route; a mixed strategy is a probability distribution over routes, with different players randomizing independently. A non-zero-sum game does not require the players' payoffs to add to a fixed total. Nash's theorem guarantees a mixed Nash equilibrium for every finite game with finitely many pure strategies. Pure equilibria need not exist in general; the congestion game here does have them, since a unilateral delay change equals the change in its finite congestion potential.
Put x=n1​+n3​ and y=n2​+n3​. The three current delays, in the order ABD,ACD,ABCD, are
C1​=3+100x​,C2​=3+100y​,C3​=49​+100x+y​.
(22)
For a player using the first route, switching to the second makes the new second-route load y+1, while switching to the third retains that player's use of AB and adds one user to CD. The two comparisons are therefore
3+100x​≤3+100y+1​,3+100x​≤49​+100x+y+1​.
(23)
They reduce to x≤y+1 and y≥74. Reversing the two outer routes gives y≤x+1 and x≥74 for each second-route user. A third-route user who switches to the first retains their own use of AB, and one who switches to the second retains their own use of CD. The corresponding comparisons give y≤75 and x≤75. Thus the necessary and sufficient conditions, together with nonnegative integer counts summing to n, are
n1​>0n2​>0n3​>0​⟹x≤y+1 and y≥74,⟹y≤x+1 and x≥74,⟹x≤75 and y≤75.​​
(24)
They are sufficient because each used route has been compared with both alternatives, and necessary because each comparison is an available unilateral deviation. The unit changes in loads matter in a finite-player congestion game.
For n=100, the counts (25,25,50) give x=y=75 and every player's delay is 15/4. They satisfy all the conditions. To find every pure Nash equilibrium, first note that n3​=0 is impossible: if either outer route is empty, its other route users fail the lower-load condition; if both are used, x,y≥74 contradict x+y=100. Thus n3​>0, so x,y≤75. Since x=100−n2​ and y=100−n1​, this forces n1​,n2​≥25. Both are positive, so the remaining conditions force x,y≥74 and hence n1​,n2​≤26. Their differences are at most one, so every such pair works. The complete equilibrium count vectors are
(25,25,50),(25,26,49),(26,25,49),(26,26,48).​
(25)
Each vector also represents many pure profiles obtained by choosing which labeled players use each route. Therefore the displayed equilibrium is not unique, even as an aggregate count vector.
A coordinated allocation with fifty players on each outer route and nobody on ABCD gives every player delay 7/2, strictly better than 15/4. It is not a Nash equilibrium: a first-route user can switch to ABCD and obtain delay
49​+10050+51​=50163​<27​.
(26)
This exhibits the conflict between coordinated welfare and unilateral incentives. In fact 7/2 is below every user's delay in all four pure equilibrium count vectors above.
For a symmetric Nash equilibrium when n=100, let each player independently choose the two outer routes with probability q each and the middle route with probability 1−2q. Conditional on choosing the first route, that player certainly uses AB, while each of the other 99 players uses it with probability 1−q. The expected delays of the first and third routes are consequently
EC1​=3+1001+99(1−q)​=4−10099q​,EC3​=49​+1002+198(1−q)​=417​−100198q​.
(27)
The second has the same expected delay as the first by symmetry. Equalizing these gives
Pr(ABD)=Pr(ACD)=9925​,Pr(ABCD)=9949​.​
(28)
All three probabilities are positive and all three pure routes have expected delay 15/4, so every route is a best response to the other players' common mixture. This proves a symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium. Using probabilities 1/4,1/4,1/2 would omit the player's own-load correction and would not make all three expected delays equal.
For completeness, the same calculation works for every positive number of players. If n≤75, every player choosing ABCD is a symmetric pure Nash equilibrium. For 75<n<149, a symmetric mixed equilibrium has
Pr(ABD)=Pr(ACD)=n−1n−75​,Pr(ABCD)=n−1149−n​.
(29)
For n≥149, each player choosing either outer route with probability one half is a symmetric mixed equilibrium. In that last regime the unused middle route has expected cost at least that of the outer routes, since their difference is −3/4+(n+1)/200≥0. These formulas agree at the regime boundaries.
