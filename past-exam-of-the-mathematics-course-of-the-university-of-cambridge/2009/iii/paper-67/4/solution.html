A planetary magnetic field is evidence about electrical conductivity, fluid motion and thermal evolution, but its presence alone does not identify a present planetary dynamo. One must separate internal global fields, crustal remanent magnetization and currents driven by the solar wind. The following discussion uses the observational picture available by 2009, including the early MESSENGER flybys and the Voyager, Galileo and Cassini results.
Outside a planet's internal electric currents, the internal component is approximately a potential magnetic field. Its spherical harmonic of degree ℓ decreases as r−(ℓ+2). Measurements at different positions and altitudes can therefore distinguish a deep, low-degree source from localized crustal anomalies and external magnetospheric currents. Repeated observations, radio emissions, aurorae and particle measurements supply complementary evidence for a magnetosphere, while slow secular variation and records of past reversals test the internal field's evolution.
For Earth, ground observatories and satellite measurements reveal a predominantly dipolar field of several tens of microtesla at the surface, tilted relative to the rotation axis and changing with time. Magnetic minerals in rocks record a much older field and repeated geomagnetic reversals. These observations, together with seismic evidence for a liquid outer core, support an operating planetary dynamo in conducting iron alloy. Core temperatures exceed the relevant Curie temperatures, so ordinary permanent remanent magnetization cannot explain the main field. Thermal convection, cooling and compositional convection associated with inner-core growth supply buoyancy; the Coriolis force organizes the motion. The observed nonaxisymmetric components and time dependence are consistent with a dynamic source, rather than a fixed bar magnet.
Mariner 10 and the first two MESSENGER flybys established a weak but coherent intrinsic field at Mercury, with roughly dipolar structure closely aligned with its rotation axis. It is much weaker than Earth's surface field, but its global character favors a deep source over an explanation solely by localized crustal patches. A planetary dynamo in a fluid part of Mercury's large iron-rich core is the natural interpretation, supported by evidence for a molten core component. Light elements can depress the alloy's freezing temperature, and thermal or compositional buoyancy can maintain motion. Slow rotation is not enough to rule out dynamo action. The limited pre-orbital measurements do not uniquely determine the detailed core structure or the mechanism responsible for the field's weakness.
For Venus, spacecraft including Pioneer Venus found no appreciable internally generated global field. Nevertheless its ionosphere interacts with the solar wind, producing magnetic draping, a bow shock and an induced magnetosphere. Those external signatures are not evidence for an internal planetary dynamo. Venus is comparable to Earth in size and may possess a substantial conducting core; the absence of a detectable dynamo therefore need not mean that all of its core is solid. Insufficient core heat loss or stable stratification can prevent the required convection. Its slow rotation can affect flow organization, but rotation rate by itself is not a sufficient explanation of the contrast with Earth or Mercury.
For Mars, Mars Global Surveyor found strong, spatially localized crustal anomalies, especially in ancient southern terrain, rather than an appreciable present global dipole. This is remanent magnetization, indicating that an ancient field magnetized the crust while it formed and cooled. The distribution of anomalies and weakly magnetized major impact basins constrain when that ancient planetary dynamo operated; crustal magnetization is a record of past activity, not a present global dynamo. Modern interaction with the solar wind also produces an induced magnetosphere. Cooling and changes in core heat extraction can explain the cessation of dynamo-driving convection; the observations alone do not prove complete solidification of the core.
For Jupiter, radio emissions, its intense radiation belts and measurements by Pioneer, Voyager and Galileo demonstrate a strong global intrinsic field and an enormous magnetosphere. The field is broadly dipolar but tilted and contains higher multipoles. It is generated in a deep conducting fluid rather than by a permanent solid magnet: the principal conducting region is metallic hydrogen in the high-pressure interior. Cooling and gravitational energy release maintain convection, while rapid rotation organizes the flow. Plasma supplied by Io and currents in the magnetosphere strongly modify the observed external field, but cannot account for the internally sourced planetary component. Jupiter's large magnetic moment also reflects its large size; a moment ratio should not be confused with the ratio of local surface field strengths.
For Saturn, Pioneer, Voyager and Cassini measurements likewise show an intrinsic field and a magnetosphere, with a dipole strikingly close to the rotation axis. A planetary dynamo in conducting metallic hydrogen is supported by the same broad interior argument as for Jupiter. Interior cooling and differentiation can provide power, including energy associated with helium separation. The near alignment does not invalidate the Cowling anti-dynamo theorem: that theorem excludes an exactly axisymmetric isolated dynamo field, not a predominantly axisymmetric exterior with undetected nonaxisymmetric structure in its source region. A stably stratified conducting layer above the active region is one possible way to attenuate the observable nonaxisymmetric components; it is a model, not a uniquely established consequence of the observations.
For Uranus, Voyager 2 in 1986 observed an intrinsic field with a dipole tilted by about 59∘ to the rotation axis and significantly displaced from the centre. Higher multipoles are substantial, so a centred dipole is an inadequate description. The field and associated magnetosphere indicate an active planetary dynamo, despite the planet's small intrinsic heat flux. That low luminosity constrains its thermal evolution and may indicate inhibited deep mixing; it does not demonstrate an absence of all conducting-fluid motion. A conducting, volatile-rich fluid shell is a plausible source region. The term ice giant describes bulk composition, not a claim that the dynamo region is ordinary frozen ice.
For Neptune, Voyager 2 in 1989 also found a highly non-dipolar, displaced intrinsic field, with a dipole tilt near 47∘. Its field, radio emissions and magnetosphere imply another active planetary dynamo. Its larger intrinsic heat flux than Uranus supplies evidence for substantial continuing interior energy transport. Electrically conducting water-ammonia-rich mixtures at high pressure, with ionic or otherwise mobile charges, can supply the conducting region without requiring the metallic hydrogen layer characteristic of gas giants. Numerical dynamos in convecting shells surrounding more stably stratified interiors can produce strongly nonaxisymmetric, multipolar fields resembling those of the two ice giants. Such models show that their unusual field geometry is compatible with dynamo action, but field measurements do not uniquely identify the shell's composition or thickness.

A conducting fluid needs a sufficiently large magnetic Reynolds number and a flow geometry that regenerates magnetic flux rather than merely amplifying one component temporarily. Three-dimensional stretching and twisting can couple poloidal and toroidal fields; strictly planar regeneration and a strictly axisymmetric magnetic field are restricted by the relevant anti-dynamo theorems. Rotation influences this regeneration through the Coriolis force, but does not provide an inexhaustible power source merely by being present.

Thermal cooling, latent heat release, compositional convection and differentiation supply that mechanical power. Magnetic feedback eventually changes the flow and saturates the field. Strong conductivity without an adequate energy source is not sufficient for a dynamo.
The 2009 observations support active global dynamos in Mercury, Earth, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune; Venus has no detected appreciable intrinsic dynamo field, and Mars preserves evidence of an extinct earlier dynamo in its crust. Induced fields and surviving remanence explain why planets without a current global dynamo can still display magnetic phenomena. The diversity of field geometries and strengths constrains, rather than uniquely solves, the diversity of planetary conducting regions and their convective histories.
