Put p=1−R, b=μ−r1, A=σσT and q=R+ε. First assume the covariance matrix A is invertible and initial portfolio wealth is positive. Introduce the accumulated fee process
yt​=awt​exp(−2ε​∫0t​πsT​Aπs​ds).
(1)
The Itô formula and the self-financing portfolio equation give
yt​dyt​​=(r+bTπt​−2ε​πtT​Aπt​)dt+πtT​σdWt​.
(2)
This converts the path-dependent payment into a terminal-utility problem with state y. The volatility-penalized terminal fee changes its drift as well as its spot volatility.
Let J(t,y) be the manager's value function. Its Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation, with terminal condition J(T,y)=yp/p, is
0=Jt​+ryJy​+supπ​{yJy​bTπ+21​(y2Jyy​−εyJy​)πTAπ}.
(3)
The homogeneity of CRRA utility suggests J(t,y)=f(t)yp/p. Since yJy​=f(t)yp and y2Jyy​=−Rf(t)yp, the portfolio-dependent expression is
f(t)yp(bTπ−2q​πTAπ).
(4)
Its coefficient is positive even when p<0. Completing the square gives
bTπ−2q​πTAπ=2qbTA−1b​−2q​(π−π∗)TA(π−π∗),π∗=R+εA−1(μ−r1)​​.
(5)
Thus the stock fractions are constant. Substitution into the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation yields
f(t)=exp[p(r+2qbTA−1b​)(T−t)],J(0,aw0​)=p(aw0​)p​exp[p(r+2qbTA−1b​)T].
(6)
In particular, multiplying the fee by a does not affect the optimal investment portfolio.
To prove global optimality rather than merely solve a formal Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation, write vt​=σTπt​, v∗=σTπ∗ and let y∗ be the fee under the proposed constant policy. Then bTπ=qv∗⋅v. Define a probability Q by
dPdQ​=exp(pv∗⋅WT​−21​p2∣v∗∣2T).
(7)
This density is a true martingale because v∗ is constant. By the Girsanov theorem, WtQ​=Wt​−pv∗t is a Brownian motion under Q. The geometric Brownian motion formula for the fee ratio gives
logyT∗​yT​​=∫0T​(vs​−v∗)⋅dWsQ​−21+ε​∫0T​∣vs​−v∗∣2ds.
(8)
For admissible, locally square-integrable exposures with finite accumulated quadratic variation, set
ZT​=exp((1+ε)∫0T​(vs​−v∗)⋅dWsQ​−2(1+ε)2​∫0T​∣vs​−v∗∣2ds).
(9)
This stochastic exponential is a positive local martingale, so EQ​ZT​≤1. Moreover,
(yT∗​yT​​)p=ZTp/(1+ε)​,EP​[yTp​]=EP​[(yT∗​)p]EQ​[ZTp/(1+ε)​].
(10)
For p>0, the exponent p/(1+ε) lies between zero and one. The Jensen inequality for this concave function gives EQ​[ZTp/(1+ε)​]≤1. For p<0, the power is convex and decreasing, so the Jensen inequality gives EQ​[ZTp/(1+ε)​]≥(EQ​ZT​)p/(1+ε)≥1; an infinite moment only makes this inequality stronger. Dividing by p, with its corresponding sign, proves E[U(yT​)]≤E[U(yT∗​)] in both cases. Equality is achieved by the constant policy.
For an ordinary terminal-wealth agent with relative risk aversion q, the same Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation calculation, with no fee penalty, maximizes bTπ−qπTAπ/2. It therefore gives exactly the same optimal investment portfolio. For q=1, the preceding stochastic exponential and Jensen inequality verification applies with penalty zero and exponent 1−q, proving global optimality for this agent too. The spot volatility penalty adds ε to effective relative risk aversion. If q=1, the printed expression for U0​ has a zero denominator; its appropriate limit, after subtracting an irrelevant constant, is U0​(x)=logx, which still gives π∗=A−1b. In this logarithmic case, the terminal log-wealth difference from the candidate equals logZT​ for a positive stochastic exponential Z, so E[logZT​]≤logE[ZT​]≤0 proves its optimality.
If A is singular, the same conclusion holds in the absence of arbitrage, provided b lies in the image of a linear map x↦Ax: replace A−1 by the Moore-Penrose inverse, and add any vector in the kernel of A to π∗. These additions change neither returns nor Brownian portfolio exposures. If b has a nonzero component in that kernel, a zero-volatility position has a nonzero excess return. Scaling its profitable sign gives arbitrage, so a finite attained optimum as claimed requires this degeneracy to be excluded.

Write p=1−R and, for a constant bank rate s, define
B(s)=s+2Rσ2(μ−s)2​,γ(s)=Rρ−pB(s)​.

If γ0​≤0, the unconstrained Merton consumption-investment problem has value +∞ for 0<R<1 and −∞ for R>1. The remaining calculation therefore uses γ0​>0.

Interpret the exponential distribution change time as an exogenous time independent of the stock's Brownian motion, or, equivalently for this calculation, as having constant conditional intensity λ while the rate remains high. Its marginal distribution alone would not imply that conditional intensity if the change time carried information about stock returns. Write r1​=r+ε, p=1−R, B1​=B(r1​), and let V1​ be the prechange value function.
Over a short interval dt, a rate change occurs with probability λdt+o(dt) and changes continuation value from V1​(w) to V0​(w). The dynamic programming equation is consequently
0=supc>0,π​{pcp​+[r1​w+πw(μ−r1​)−c]V1′​(w)+21​σ2π2w2V1′′​(w)−ρV1​(w)+λ[V0​(w)−V1​(w)]}.
(13)
This jump term distinguishes the exponential interest-rate switch from a permanent higher bank rate.
By the homogeneity of CRRA utility, put V1​(w)=K1​wp/p, with K1​>0. Differentiating the strictly concave expressions in c and π gives
c=K1−1/R​w=γ1​w,π1∗​=Rσ2μ−r1​​.
(14)
The optimized consumption terms contribute RK11−1/R​wp/p, and the optimized investment terms contribute K1​B1​wp. With K0​=γ0−R​, the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation reduces to
D1​K1​=RK11−1/R​+λK0​,D1​=ρ+λ−pB1​.
(15)
Equivalently,
Rγ1​+λ(γ0​γ1​​)R=D1​,V1​(w)=1−Rγ1−R​w1−R​.​
(16)
The left side is continuous and strictly increasing for γ1​>0, with limits zero and infinity. Thus D1​>0 gives one and only one positive root. This scalar equation is an explicit characterization for arbitrary real R; elementary closed forms are not generally available. For example, when R=2,
γ1​=λγ02​​(1+γ02​λD1​​​−1).
(17)
Use (π1∗​,γ1​) until the rate changes, and (π0∗​,γ0​) afterwards. If the two rates coincide, the root is γ1​=γ0​; as λ tends to infinity, it also tends to γ0​.
For a direct attainability check, use the proposed prechange constant fractions. The geometric Brownian motion moment is E[wtp​]=wpexp[p(B1​−γ1​)t] on the prechange evolution. Conditioning on the independent exponential distribution time therefore gives
E[∫0τ​e−ρtp(γ1​wt​)p​dt+e−ρτV0​(wτ​)]=pwp​D1​+pγ1​γ1p​+λK0​​=pK1​wp​.
(18)
The denominator is positive because the scalar equation gives D1​+pγ1​=γ1​+λK0​/K1​>0. Hence the candidate policy actually achieves the stated value.
One can also verify the upper bound for both signs of p without relying on a sign-sensitive terminal-value argument. Let jt​ be the current regime, w∗ the candidate portfolio wealth, and
Mt​=e−ρtKjt​​(wt∗​)−R=e−ρt(ct∗​)−R.
(19)
If Nt​=1{τ≤t}​ and Nt​=Nt​−λ(t∧τ), the Itô formula and the scalar equation give
Mt−​dMt​​=−rjt​​dt−σμ−rjt​​​dWt​+(K1​K0​​−1)dNt​,
(20)
where the jump term stops after the switch and continuous coefficients are understood predictably. Thus M is a positive martingale deflator: for any admissible portfolio wealth w, the process Mt​wt​+∫0t​Ms​cs​ds is a nonnegative local martingale. It follows that
E∫0∞​Mt​ct​dt≤M0​w0​.
(21)
For the candidate policy, E[Mt​wt∗​]→0. Indeed its surviving prechange term decays at rate D1​+pγ1​>0, and its postchange terms decay at rate γ0​>0; integrating over the switch time preserves convergence to zero. The finite-time moment computations also make its budget equality exact. Consequently E∫0∞​Mt​ct∗​dt=M0​w0​. Apply the concave supporting-tangent inequality
U(ct​)≤U(ct∗​)+(ct∗​)−R(ct​−ct∗​)
(22)
and integrate in time and expectation. The budget inequality proves that no admissible policy exceeds the candidate. This verifies the value function, including when R>1.
Both γ0​>0 and D1​>0 are needed for a finite prechange answer. If the postchange value is already infinite, that problem persists after the almost surely finite switch. If γ0​>0 but D1​≤0, then for p>0 a small constant prechange consumption fraction makes the expected utility function before the switch diverge, or makes its supremum diverge when D1​=0. For p<0, the optimal no-consumption terminal moment before the switch is wpepB1​t; withdrawals only increase this negative-power moment. The expected magnitude of the negative postchange continuation utility function therefore contains the divergent integral λK0​wp∫0∞​e−D1​tdt/∣p∣. Thus these cases yield respectively +∞ or −∞, rather than a positive-root solution.

Choose the utility function primitive U(c)=c1−R/(1−R). Any other primitive with the same derivative differs by a constant C; this adds C/ρ to the value function and does not alter the policy. Put p=1−R and let πt​ be the stock fraction. The self-financing portfolio equation, including consumption withdrawals, is
dwt​=[rwt​+πt​wt​(μ−r)−ct​]dt+σπt​wt​dWt​.​
(23)
For a time-independent infinite-horizon value function V, the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation is
ρV(w)=supc>0,π​{pcp​+[rw+πw(μ−r)−c]V′(w)+21​σ2π2w2V′′(w)}.
(24)
Scaling initial portfolio wealth and all consumption rates by the same positive number preserves admissibility and scales utility function by its pth power. This homogeneity gives V(w)=Kwp/p. In particular,
V′(w)=Kw−R,V′′(w)=−RKw−R−1.
(25)
The consumption first-order condition c−R=V′(w) and stock first-order condition w(μ−r)V′(w)+σ2πw2V′′(w)=0 give
πM​=Rσ2μ−r​,c=K−1/Rw.
(26)
The second derivatives of the optimized expressions are negative, so these are their global maximizers. Writing γM​=K−1/R and substituting into the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation gives
ρ=RγM​+(1−R)(r+2Rσ2(μ−r)2​).
(27)
Therefore
πM​=Rσ2μ−r​,γM​=Rρ+(R−1)(r+2Rσ2(μ−r)2​)​,ct∗​=γM​wt​.​
(28)
The given inequality is precisely γM​>0, so the Merton consumption-investment problem value is V(w)=γM−R​w1−R/(1−R). This derives the requested policy; no additional verification argument is needed here.

Define the positive constant
k=ρ+(R−1)(α−21​Rσ2).

μtotal​=α+k.
(30)
This is the drift to use in the Merton consumption-investment problem; using only capital appreciation would omit an actual source of portfolio wealth.
At the proposed bank rate,
μtotal​−r∗=α+k−[ρ+Rα−21​(1+R)Rσ2]=Rσ2.

Thus the optimal stock fraction from part (i) is
πM​=1.​
(32)

(33)

Treat the two-point prior for α as independent of the driving Brownian motion. On a partition 0=t0​<⋯<tn​=t, conditionally on α=±a, the increments of X are independent normal distributions with means ±aΔtj​ and variances Δtj​. Their likelihood ratio is
L−​L+​​=∏j=1n​exp[2Δtj​(ΔXj​+aΔtj​)2−(ΔXj​−aΔtj​)2​]=exp(2a∑j=1n​ΔXj​)=e2aXt​.

Thus every partition containing t gives the same Bayesian posterior, depending only on the endpoint. Refine partitions through a dense set of observation times. Continuity makes their increasing observation fields generate the full path field Xt​, and convergence of bounded conditional expectations preserves that Bayesian posterior. Equal prior probabilities and the Bayes' theorem consequently give
qt​=P(α=a∣Xt​)=eaXt​+e−aXt​eaXt​​.​
(38)
Its conditional mean drift is

Use the primitive U(c)=c1−R/(1−R), as in Question 3, and the usual positive spot volatility convention σ>0. An additive utility function constant shifts both agents' value functions equally and does not affect their policies or the comparison. Set
h(x)=atanh(ax),b(x)=μ−r+σh(x).
(44)
The Itô formula applied to the stock price and the binary Brownian drift filter gives
St​dSt​​=σdWt​+[μ+σh(Xt​)]dt.
(45)
Let θt​ denote the dollar stock holding. The separated state equations are
dwt​=[rwt​+θt​b(Xt​)−ct​]dt+σθt​dWt​,dXt​=h(Xt​)dt+dWt​.
(46)
Both states use the same observed Brownian motion. In particular, their quadratic covariation is d[w,X]t​=σθt​dt.
Let J(w,x) be the Bayesian value function. Its Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation is
ρJ=supc>0,θ​{1−Rc1−R​+(rw+θb(x)−c)Jw​+h(x)Jx​+21​Jxx​+21​σ2θ2Jww​+σθJwx​}.

21​K′′+hK′+(pr−ρ)K+RK1−1/R+2Rσ2Kp(bK+σK′)2​=0.

For the comparison, now suppose 0<R<1 and μ>r. Write m=μ−r>0, b+​=m+σa, and
γ+​=Rρ−(1−R)(r+2Rσ2b+2​​)​.
