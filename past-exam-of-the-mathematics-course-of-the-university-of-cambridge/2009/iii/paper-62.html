Build the spherical system by bringing successive thin spherical shells in from infinity. The interior mass is M(r)=4π∫0r​s2ρ(s)ds, and the next shell has dM=4πr2ρ(r)dr. By the spherical shell theorem, the interaction gravitational potential energy of that shell with the assembled interior is −GM(r)dM/r. Each pair of mass elements is counted exactly once in this construction. Integrating gives
W=−∫0∞​rGM(r)​dM(r)=−4πG∫0∞​rρ(r)M(r)dr.​
(1)
The infinitesimal shell's own energy is of second order in its mass and is accounted for by the limiting pairwise assembly.
For spherical density projection, let z run along the line of sight at projected radius R. Spherical symmetry gives
Σ(R)=∫−∞∞​ρ(R2+z2​)dz.
(2)
On z>0, use r=R2+z2​, so dz=rdr/r2−R2​. The two halves of the line of sight contribute equally, yielding
Σ(R)=2∫R∞​r2−R2​ρ(r)r​dr.​
(3)
To derive the inverse Abel transform, set u=R2, v=r2, F(u)=Σ(u​) and g(v)=ρ(v​). The projection becomes F(u)=∫u∞​g(v)(v−u)−1/2dv. Compose this with the same square-root kernel and exchange the convergent integrals:
∫t∞​u−t​F(u)​du​=∫t∞​g(v)[∫tv​(u−t)(v−u)​du​]dv=π∫t∞​g(v)dv.​
(4)
The inner integral equals π, as follows from u=t+(v−t)sin2χ with 0<χ<π/2. Write the left side as ∫0∞​F(t+s)s−1/2ds before differentiating; this avoids differentiating a singular moving endpoint. Under sufficient regularity and decay,
∫t∞​u−t​F′(u)​du=−πg(t).
(5)
Finally put t=r2,u=R2. Since F′(u)=Σ′(R)/(2R), this gives the spherical Abel deprojection
ρ(r)=−π1​∫r∞​R2−r2​Σ′(R)​dR.​
(6)
Next, the strip density of a spherical system is the integral across the projected coordinate perpendicular to the strip:
S(x)=∫−∞∞​Σ(x2+y2​)dy.
(7)
For x≥0, the substitution R=x2+y2​ on y>0 yields
S(x)=2∫x∞​R2−x2​Σ(R)R​dR.​
(8)
There is a particularly simple way to compose these two projections: S(x) integrates the original mass density over the whole plane of coordinates y,z. Polar coordinates in that plane, with q2=y2+z2, give
S(x)=2π∫0∞​ρ(x2+q2​)qdq=2π∫x∞​ρ(r)rdr.
(9)
Differentiation now proves
S′(x)=−2πxρ(x),ρ(x)=−2πxS′(x)​(x>0).​
(10)
The central density, if finite, is obtained by taking the limit as x→0.
To obtain gravitational binding energy from strip density, use S′=−2πxρ in the shell-assembly result, and note also that M′=4πx2ρ=−2xS′. Two integrations by parts then give
W​=2G∫0∞​M(x)S′(x)dx=−2G∫0∞​S(x)M′(x)dx=4G∫0∞​xS(x)S′(x)dx=−2G∫0∞​S(x)2dx.​
(11)
Here [MS]0∞​=[xS2]0∞​=0; the calculation assumes convergent binding energy and sufficient regularity and decay for these boundary terms to vanish. Thus
W=−2G∫0∞​[S(x)]2dx.​
(12)
Only the nonnegative half-axis is integrated, since the strip density of a spherical system is even. Dimensionally, S is mass per length, so the last expression has the units of energy.

Write ui​=⟨vi​⟩ and use summation over repeated Cartesian indices. A mass-normalized galactic distribution function obeys the Collisionless Boltzmann equation
∂t​f+vi​∂xi​​f−(∂xi​​ϕ)∂vi​​f=0.
(13)
Assume f decays in velocity sufficiently fast that the necessary boundary terms vanish. Its zeroth velocity moment gives the continuity equation
∂t​ρ+∂xi​​(ρui​)=0.
(14)
For the first velocity moment, multiply by vj​ and integrate. In the gravitational term, integration by parts gives ∫vj​∂vi​​fd3v=−δij​ρ. Therefore
∂t​(ρuj​)+∂xi​​(ρ⟨vi​vj​⟩)=−ρ∂xj​​ϕ.
(15)
The velocity-dispersion tensor is the centered second moment, so ⟨vi​vj​⟩=ui​uj​+σij2​. Expanding the streaming terms, the factor multiplying uj​ cancels by the continuity equation:
∂t​(ρuj​)+∂xi​​(ρui​uj​)=ρ∂t​uj​+ρui​∂xi​​uj​.
(16)
Hence the Jeans equation is
ρ(∂t​uj​+ui​∂xi​​uj​)=−ρ∂xj​​ϕ−∂xi​​(ρσij2​).​
(17)
Its fluid analogue is the momentum equation with pressure tensor Pij​=ρσij2​. For isotropic random motion, Pij​=Pδij​, and this becomes the Euler equations for an inviscid fluid:
ρ(∂t​u+(u⋅∇)u)=−ρ∇ϕ−∇P.​
(18)
The tensor form allows anisotropic stellar stresses. In a fluid an equation of state can provide a pressure closure; the Jeans equation alone does not determine the velocity-dispersion tensor of a collisionless stellar system.

Use relative energy E=ψ−v2/2, with ψ=−ϕ. If the galactic distribution function depends on E alone, rotations and sign reversals in velocity space leave it unchanged. Thus all mean velocities vanish, and the three component second moments are equal. The anisotropy term in the Spherical Jeans equation cancels. With constant radial velocity dispersion σ0​, it reduces to
σ02​drdρ​=−ρdrdϕ​=ρdrdψ​.
(19)
Integration gives the isothermal collisionless tracer density
ρ(r)=ρ0​exp(σ02​ψ(r)​).​
(20)
The constant ρ0​ changes if a constant is added to the potential; this has no effect on the velocity distribution.
Apply the given untruncated Eddington inversion, retaining its lower limit −∞. Define its integral before differentiation by I(E). With ψ=E−σ02​s2, the Gaussian integral gives
I(E)​=∫−∞E​dψdρ​E−ψ​dψ​=σ0​2ρ0​​eE/σ02​∫0∞​e−s2ds=σ0​ρ0​π​​eE/σ02​.​
(21)
Differentiating and multiplying by 1/(8​π2) yields
f(E)=(2πσ02​)3/2ρ0​​eE/σ02​.​
(22)
Indeed, at a fixed position this is
f(r,v)=(2πσ02​)3/2ρ(r)​exp(−2σ02​v2​),
(23)
whose integral over all velocities is ρ and whose component second moments are σ02​. This checks both the normalization and the velocity dispersion.
Let w be the line of sight component of velocity. Integrate this Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution over the two transverse components:
∫R2​f(r,v)d2v⊥​=2π​σ0​ρ(r)​e−w2/(2σ02​).
(24)
At projected radius R, integrating along the line of sight simply replaces ρ by the projected surface density Σ(R). Dividing by Σ(R) gives the spectroscopist's normalized Gaussian line-of-sight velocity distribution:
p(w∣R)=2π​σ0​1​e−w2/(2σ02​).​
(25)
It is a zero-mean normal distribution with width σ0​, independent of projected radius. This is the untruncated model specified by the lower limit in the inversion formula: no escape-energy cutoff has been imposed. A population restricted to positive binding energy would have truncated velocity tails and would not obey this exact Gaussian density law in a finite escape potential.

For an isolated collisionless stellar system, define the scalar second mass moment and total kinetic energy by
I(t)=∫ρ(r,t)r2d3r,T=21​∫f(r,v,t)v2d3rd3v.
(26)
Take moments of the Collisionless Boltzmann equation, with vanishing surface fluxes. For any time-independent phase-space function A, integration by parts gives
dtd​∫fAd3rd3v=∫f(v⋅∇r​A−∇ϕ⋅∇v​A)d3rd3v.
(27)
Apply this first to A=r2 and then to A=r⋅v. The result is
I˙=2∫fr⋅vd3rd3v,21​I¨=2T−∫ρr⋅∇ϕd3r.
(28)
The potential is self-consistent Newtonian gravity, so
ϕ(r)=−G∫∣r−r′∣ρ(r′)​d3r′,W=−2G​∬∣r−r′∣ρ(r)ρ(r′)​d3rd3r′.
(29)
Symmetrize the force integral under r↔r′:
∫ρr⋅∇ϕd3r​=2G​∬ρρ′∣r−r′∣3(r−r′)⋅(r−r′)​d3rd3r′=2G​∬∣r−r′∣ρρ′​d3rd3r′=−W.​
(30)
Thus the dynamical virial theorem is I¨/2=2T+W. For a steady system, I¨=0, and consequently
2T+W=0.​
(31)
For bounded statistically steady motion, averaging instead gives 2⟨T⟩+⟨W⟩=0 provided [I˙(τ)−I˙(0)]/τ→0. Collisionlessness alone does not imply instantaneous virial equilibrium: a collapsing system retains the I¨ term. The displayed derivation assumes a finite second mass moment, or a limiting version with vanishing boundary terms, and no confining boundary-pressure contribution.
In virial equilibrium, the total energy is Etot​=T+W=−T. Define the kinetic temperature Θ at fixed particle number N by T=3NkB​Θ/2. Along an equilibrium sequence,
dΘdEtot​​=−23​NkB​<0.​
(32)
This is the negative heat capacity of a virialized gravitational system: removing total energy makes the kinetic temperature rise. A collisionless stellar system need not be in thermal equilibrium, so without that additional assumption this is a kinetic-temperature interpretation of heat capacity, rather than an assertion that every stellar distribution has a thermodynamic temperature.

To distinguish the moment quantity from the gravitational constant, write it as G=∑i​pi​⋅ri​. Newton's equations give p˙​i​=Fi​ and r˙i​=pi​/mi​. Differentiating proves
dtdG​=i∑​mi​pi2​​+i∑​Fi​⋅ri​=2T+i∑​Fi​⋅ri​.​
(33)
Average from 0 to τ, divide by τ, and apply the supplied long-time boundary condition. The result is
0=2⟨T⟩+⟨∑i​Fi​⋅ri​⟩,2⟨T⟩=−⟨i∑​Fi​⋅ri​⟩.​
(34)
The PDF's intermediate formula omits this minus sign. It cannot follow from the preceding derivative identity: an attractive inverse-square circular orbit already has F⋅r<0 and T>0, contradicting the printed positive-sign version. The later homogeneous-potential relation is consistent with the corrected sign.
Interpret W as the total pair potential energy, with each pair counted once, for example W=∑i<j​C∣ri​−rj​∣p+1. The printed pair sum leaves one particle index unspecified; an ordered-pair convention with the corresponding factor absorbed into C has the same degree of homogeneity. Under a common dilation of all positions,
W(λr1​,…,λrN​)=λp+1W(r1​,…,rN​).
(35)
Differentiating with respect to λ at one is the Euler theorem for homogeneous functions. Since Fi​=−∇i​W, it gives
∑i​Fi​⋅ri​=−(p+1)W.
(36)
Substitution into the correctly signed averaged identity proves the virial theorem for a homogeneous pair potential:
2⟨T⟩−(p+1)⟨W⟩=0.​
(37)
For p=−1, the conserved total energy can be evaluated using these averages:
Etot​=⟨T⟩+⟨W⟩=p+1p+3​⟨T⟩.
(38)
Use the kinetic temperature convention ⟨T⟩=κΘ, where κ=3NkB​/2>0 for three-dimensional thermalized particles, or the same positive coefficient as a kinetic-temperature definition. At fixed particle number and interaction parameters,
dΘdEtot​​=κp+1p+3​.
(39)
Thus the equilibrium heat capacity is negative precisely when
−3<p<−1,​
(40)
subject to existence of the bounded equilibrium sequence assumed by the virial theorem. At p=−3 the virial energy is zero and this derivative vanishes. At p=−1 the written power-law potential is constant, its force vanishes, and the bounded-motion virial condition excludes nonzero kinetic energy; division by p+1 is not permissible. The Newtonian case p=−2 recovers Etot​=−⟨T⟩.

Write μ=GM⊙​, and interpret the specified radial force field as acceleration, or force per unit test-particle mass. Since it is a central force, the specific angular momentum h=r×v is conserved: h˙=r×F=0. For h=∣h∣=0, motion is confined to the fixed plane perpendicular to h. Choose the orientation of the polar angle so that h=r2θ˙>0.
To derive the Binet equation here, let u=1/r and differentiate with respect to θ. Then r˙=−hu′ and r¨−rθ˙2=−h2u2(u′′+u). Equating this to the radial acceleration gives
−h2u2(u′′+u)=−μu2+Cu3,u′′+(1+h2C​)u=h2μ​.
(41)
Put K=h2+C and α=K/h2​. For K>0, the solution is
u=Kμ​[1+ecosα(θ−θ0​)].
(42)
The radial potential energy per unit mass is V(r)=−μ/r+C/(2r2), so the conserved specific orbital energy is
E=2r˙2​+2r2K​−rμ​.
(43)
Substitute the displayed u and r˙=−hu′ to obtain E=μ2(e2−1)/(2K). Therefore define
a=−2Eμ​,e2=1+μ22EK​,α=1+h2C​​.​
(44)
For regular bound motion, K>0 and −μ2/(2K)≤E<0, giving a>0 and 0≤e<1. Since K/μ=a(1−e2), choose the angular origin at a pericentre, so θ0​=0, and obtain
r=1+ecosαθa(1−e2)​.​
(45)
The turning radii are rp​=a(1−e) and ra​=a(1+e). Thus a is the arithmetic mean of the turning radii; when the orbit precesses it should not be interpreted as the semimajor axis of a fixed ellipse. If K≤0, the centrifugal barrier needed for a regular two-turning-point bound orbit is absent, and this bound-orbit parametrization does not apply.
For α=1, one has C=0 and the orbit is a closed Kepler orbit: an ellipse of eccentricity e with the Sun at a focus. In addition to E and the three components of h, the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector
A=v×h−μr
(46)
is conserved. Indeed,
dtd​(v×h)=−r3μ​r×(r×v)=μ(rv​−r3r(r⋅v)​)=μdtdr​,
(47)
so A˙=0. It points toward the fixed pericentre. The constraints A⋅h=0 and A2=μ2+2Eh2 leave five generically independent isolating integrals. One may take
E, hx​, hy​, hz​, Aj​,​
(48)
choosing a component Aj​ whose differential is independent of the other four on the local region under consideration. The extra integral fixes the apsidal direction in the already fixed orbital plane.
For α=1, successive pericentres are separated by 2π/α in polar angle. The generic bound orbit is a rosette in that same plane. Its perihelion advance per radial cycle is
Δϖ=2π(α1​−1).​
(49)
For α<1 this is prograde; for α>1 it is retrograde. If α is rational, the orbit closes after a finite number of radial cycles; if it is irrational and 0<e<1, its successive radial phases densely fill the accessible annulus. A generic independent set of single-valued isolating integrals is
E, hx​, hy​, hz​.​
(50)
The apsidal direction is no longer fixed. For irrational frequency ratio, any continuous integral of motion must be constant on the whole two-dimensional orbital torus, because one orbit is dense there; there is no further independent continuous integral specifying a fixed apsidal direction. Exceptional closed resonant orbits do not provide a fifth global integral on an open region of phase space. Circular and radial orbits are degenerate cases of these generic counts.
The plotted precession is enlarged for visibility. Both examples have e=0.5 and the same turning radii; the second has α=0.9​.
