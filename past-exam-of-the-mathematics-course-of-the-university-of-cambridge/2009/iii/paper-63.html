Use stellar homology: dimensionless density, temperature and luminosity profiles are taken to have the same shape, so the dimensional scale relations follow from the structure equations. Mass conservation and hydrostatic support give
ρc​∝R3M​,Pc​∝R4GM2​.
(1)
The ideal gas equation then gives Tc​∝μM/R, with the dimensional constants held fixed. The specific stellar energy-generation rate has the prescribed hydrogen factor and temperature exponent, so
Lnuc​∝ϵc​M∝ϵ0​Xρc​Tc13​M∝ϵ0​Xμ13M15R−16.
(2)
Independently, radiative diffusion in a star gives Tc​/R∝κρc​L/(R2Tc3​), hence
Lrad​∝κρc​RTc4​​∝κμ4M3​.
(3)
Equating the nuclear supply and radiative transport establishes composition-dependent CNO homology:
R∝[Xκμ9]1/16M3/4,L∝μ4κ−1M3.​
(4)
At one fixed composition, these reduce to R∝M3/4 and L∝M3 as required.
The Stefan–Boltzmann law gives Te4​∝L/R2∝M3/2, so Te​∝M3/8. Thus the logarithmic Hertzsprung-Russell diagram slope along this zero-age main sequence is
dlogTe​dlogL​=8.​
(5)
Increasing mass moves upward and leftward on the conventional diagram, whose temperature axis is reversed.
For composition changes, neglect the small heavy-element contribution to the mean molecular weight and set Y≃1−X. Then μ−1=(3+5X)/4. The fixed trace catalyst abundance is absorbed into ϵ0​; this is not an assertion that an exactly metal-free star can undergo CNO burning. Substitution into the homology laws gives
L∝M3(1+X)−1(3+5X)−4,​
(6)
R∝M3/4X1/16(1+X)1/16(3+5X)−9/16.​
(7)
For a retained, nonnegligible fixed metallicity Z, the corresponding mean-molecular-weight factor is 3+5X−Z instead. The displayed requested formula uses the trace-metal approximation.
At fixed mass, differentiating with respect to X at the formal reference X=1 gives
dXdlogL​=−1+X1​−3+5X20​ ⟶ −3,
(8)
dXdlogR​=16X1​+16(1+X)1​−16(3+5X)45​ ⟶ −12833​.
(9)
Consequently
dXdlogTe​​=41​(dXdlogL​−2dXdlogR​) ⟶ −256159​,
(10)
and the initial fully mixed hydrogen-burning evolutionary track has slope
dlogTe​dlogL​​M,X=1​=53256​≃4.83.​
(11)
Hydrogen depletion has dX<0, so both luminosity and effective temperature initially increase. The star therefore moves upward and leftward, on a shallower logarithmic track than the mass sequence. The figure uses dimensionless luminosity and temperature relative to its initial reference star; the track is computed for declining X at constant mass.
Ordinary cluster main-sequence turn-offs instead evolve toward brighter, cooler, more extended configurations and eventually the giant region. Normal stars are therefore not mixed throughout their entire mass during hydrogen burning: the core composition changes while the envelope retains hydrogen, and composition and entropy gradients develop. Core exhaustion and subsequent shell burning then produce very different structural evolution. Complete mixing is a special evolutionary scenario, not the standard explanation of ordinary giant formation.
These hydrogen-burning scalings must not be extrapolated literally to X=0. Their vanishing nuclear coefficient would make the inferred radius tend to zero and the effective temperature singular; in reality hydrogen exhaustion requires a different structural and energy-generation model. A homogeneous remnant may be helium-rich, but its later structure is not described by the same CNO law. Also, the cover-sheet radiation-pressure expression has a temperature-power typo: the physical term is aT4/3. Radiation pressure was neglected in this derivation, so that typo does not enter the calculation.

In the radiative layer, take the enclosed mass to be M1​, the transported luminosity to be L, and the mean molecular weight to be constant. The envelope's own gravity is neglected. Divide the hydrostatic and radiative temperature equations, and then eliminate density using the gas equation of state:
dTdP​=3κL16πacGM1​​T3=BPnT3+m+n​,B=3κ0​L16πacGM1​​(μR​)n.
(12)
Put p=n+1 and b=4+m+n. For p,b=0, integration gives
Pp=bpB​Tb+KP​.
(13)
Defining Cp=pB/b and K=KP​/Cp yields
P=C(Tb+K)1/p,C=[3κ0​L16πacGM1​​(μR​)n4+m+nn+1​]1/(n+1).​
(14)
In the relevant positive-exponent case, writing K=T0b​ produces the stated form whenever this integration constant is nonnegative. The exceptional values excluded above lead to logarithms: p=0 gives logP=BTb/b+const, b=0 gives Pp=pBlogT+const, and both zero give logP=BlogT+const.
For radiative-envelope convection matching, compute the logarithmic gradient of the pressure relation:
∇rad​=dlogPdlogT​=bp​(1+TbK​).
(15)
At the marginal boundary, the Schwarzschild criterion equates this with the ideal-gas adiabatic gradient ∇ad​=(γ−1)/γ. Hence
K=Tbb​[pb​γγ−1​−1],T0​=Tb​[γγ−1​n+14+m+n​−1]1/(4+m+n).​
(16)
The positive real T0​ notation requires a nonnegative bracket; a general signed K is the safer integration constant otherwise. For the later case n=1,m=3 and a monatomic gas, this bracket is 3/5, so T0​ is of the same order as Tb​.
Deep below the convective boundary, with the temperature-power term dominating K, the pressure law becomes P≃CTb/p. Differentiating this approximation and using hydrostatic balance gives
pb​TP​drdT​=−r2GM1​​RTμP​,drdT​=−RbμGM1​p​r21​.
(17)
The general temperature is a term proportional to 1/r plus a boundary-dependent constant. Well inside the boundary radius, where that term dominates, it reduces to
T(r)≃R(4+m+n)rμGM1​(n+1)​.​
(18)
The inverse-radius profile is an inner approximation, not an assertion that the boundary term vanishes everywhere.
Now take n=1,m=3, so p=2, b=8. Then P≃CT4, ρ≃(μC/R)T3, and define AT​=μGM1​/(4R) so that T≃AT​/r. The luminosity supplied by the thin burning layer is
L​≃4π∫R1​rb​​r2ρϵdr=4πC2ϵ0​(Rμ​)2AT16​∫R1​rb​​r−14dr=134π​C2ϵ0​(Rμ​)2AT16​(R1−13​−rb−13​).​
(19)
Because the integral is strongly concentrated near R1​ and rb​≫R1​, the outer term is negligible. Thus
L≃134π​C2ϵ0​(Rμ​)2(4RμGM1​​)16R113​1​.​
(20)
The factor ρϵ, rather than merely ϵ, is essential since the specified energy-generation rate is per unit mass. This integration uses the deep radiative profile as a leading thin-shell approximation.
Crucially, C is not independent of luminosity. Its defining relation gives
C2=3κ0​L4πacGM1​​μR​∝LM1​​.
(21)
Substitution into the shell integral therefore yields L2∝M117​R1−13​. The degenerate-core relation gives R1​∝M1−1/3​, so
L2∝M117+13/3​=M164/3​,L∝M132/3​.​
(22)
This degenerate-core shell-burning mass-luminosity relation is specific to the adopted opacity, burning exponent and structural approximations.
Removing some envelope mass while retaining the hydrogen-burning shell and the deep-envelope regime leaves the leading luminosity approximately unchanged at fixed core mass. The envelope can instead change the radius and effective temperature. This independence is not valid for arbitrarily complete stripping: removing the fuel or the pressure-confined shell structure quenches or modifies shell burning, and the assumed giant model then fails.

Write n56​ and n54​ for the number densities of nickel-56 and iron-54. Both nuclei contain 28 neutrons, while their proton numbers differ by two. Dividing the positive-exponential nuclear statistical equilibrium formulas printed in the PDF therefore gives the nickel–iron nuclear equilibrium ratio
n54​n56​​=Knp2​T−3exp(kTΔQ​),ΔQ=Q(56,28)−Q(54,26).​
(23)
The neutron-density factors cancel. K contains the isotope-dependent statistical and translational mass factors suppressed in the supplied proportionality; it is constant in this simplified calculation. The TeX conversion incorrectly reverses the exponential sign, so the PDF sign is essential here.
In the specified restricted composition, equality of total proton and neutron numbers means
28n56​+26n54​+np​=28n56​+28n54​,np​=2n54​.​
(24)
Consequently
F(T)=n543​n56​​=4KT−3eΔQ/(kT).
(25)
Differentiation, with the suppressed prefactors held constant, gives
dTdlogF​=−T3​−kT2ΔQ​.
(26)
Using the paper's supplied signed energy difference produces
Tmax​=−3kΔQ​=9.0×109 K.​
(27)
With that negative value, F tends to zero at both temperature endpoints, increases below this temperature and decreases above it. At the stationary point d2logF/dT2=−3/T2<0, so it is indeed a maximum for the printed toy data.
There is a genuine physical-data qualification: the stated negative energy difference is inconsistent with interpreting Q as the conventional positive total nuclear binding energy of these actual isotopes. Using atomic masses, whose electron masses cancel in this reaction,
ΔB≃[2m(1H)+m(54Fe)−m(56Ni)]c2≃+12.23 MeV>0.
(28)
The masses used are 1.00782503223u, 53.93960899u and 55.94212855u, from NIST hydrogen masses, NIST iron masses and NIST nickel masses. Small electronic binding corrections do not change the sign. With a positive ΔB, the simplified F(T) above is strictly decreasing for T>0 and has no finite maximum. Thus the numerical maximum follows from the supplied negative parameter, not from consistent physical binding energies; changing the TeX sign to recover a maximum would not repair the PDF's data.
Nuclear statistical equilibrium is appropriate when the relevant forward and inverse strong or electromagnetic reactions are fast compared with changes of temperature, density and composition. It is useful in sufficiently hot late silicon-burning material and in the hottest explosive burning regions of massive stars. Some burning zones reach only quasi-equilibrium, and cooling or expansion eventually freezes the abundances out; an equilibrium expression cannot by itself predict the full ashes when these timescale conditions fail. Temperature, density, conserved baryon number and the electron fraction in stellar matter must all be specified. Negligible free-particle contributions in bulk bookkeeping are not the same as exactly zero free densities in the equilibrium formula.
Strong and electromagnetic reactions preserve total charge and baryon number, so they conserve the proton-to-neutron ratio. Weak reactions need not: electron capture and beta-plus decay convert protons into neutrons and lower it. Such conversions occur in hydrogen-burning networks and in the nitrogen-to-neon chain during helium burning. At high density in advanced stellar cores and core collapse, electron capture becomes particularly important; neutrino emission also affects energy and lepton loss. Beta-minus decay changes the ratio in the opposite direction and can compete during later relaxation. Nuclear equilibrium for strong reactions does not automatically imply weak equilibrium or a fixed proton-to-neutron ratio throughout all stellar evolution.

Use f=X22​ for the neon-22 mass fraction, temporarily keeping it distinct from the earlier metallicity. Let nb​=ρ/mu​ be the baryon density, ignoring small nuclear mass defects. Equal masses of carbon and oxygen give
nC​=241−f​nb​,nO​=321−f​nb​,n22​=22f​nb​.
(29)
Carbon-12 and oxygen-16 each have equal proton and neutron numbers, while neon-22 has ten protons and twelve neutrons. The total proton and neutron numbers per baryon are consequently
Ye​=6241−f​+8321−f​+1022f​=21​−22f​,
(30)
1−Ye​=21​+22f​.
(31)
Hence
⟨N⟩⟨Z⟩​=1−Ye​Ye​​=11+f11−f​.​
(32)
The equal carbon/oxygen mass split cancels from this result: any mixture of these two symmetric nuclei has the same electron fraction. Taking the paper's metallicity symbol to mean f gives its requested expression.
The literal pre-helium-burning nitrogen interpretation, however, needs a correction. Each initial nitrogen-14 nucleus receives two alpha particles, including eight additional baryons, before becoming neon-22; the intervening beta-plus decay also emits a neutrino. Thus, if the entire original metallicity is nitrogen-14 with mass fraction Zinitial​, conservation of the number of seed nuclei gives
X22​=1422​Zinitial​,⟨N⟩⟨Z⟩​=7+Zinitial​7−Zinitial​​.​
(33)
This is neon-22 neutron excess from nitrogen-14. The two expressions are compatible only when their arguments are distinguished: the displayed (11−Z)/(11+Z) law uses the final neon mass fraction, not literally the earlier nitrogen mass fraction. Treating those fractions as equal is an extra simplifying approximation, not a consequence of the stated capture chain.
A radioactively powered Type Ia supernova light curve rises to a broad maximum over roughly weeks as energy deposited in expanding ejecta diffuses out, then declines over the following weeks and months. Its power comes predominantly from the nickel-56 decay chain, first nickel to cobalt and then cobalt to iron. Gamma rays and charged decay products deposit heat, which is reprocessed into optical and infrared radiation. At later times, declining cobalt decay power and increasing gamma-ray escape shape the tail. It is not sustained nuclear fusion in the expanding ejecta. The amount of radioactive nickel, photon diffusion time and deposition efficiency all matter, so an uncorrected fixed luminosity is only an idealization.
For the requested two-isotope yield calculation, assume the same total processed iron-group mass, namely 1M⊙​ as inferred from the zero-neon reference. Treat the final freeze-out ashes as nickel-56 and iron-54, neglecting residual light particles and additional weak neutronization. If w is the nickel mass fraction, conservation of electron fraction gives
2w​+(1−w)5426​=21​−22f​.
(34)
Since 26/54=1/2−1/54, this reduces to the metallicity dependence of nickel-56 yield
w=1−1127​f.​
(35)
Under the paper's intended identification f=Z=1/50,
M56​=(1−55027​)M⊙​=550523​M⊙​≃0.95M⊙​(two significant figures).​
(36)
If instead Z=1/50 denotes the earlier nitrogen mass fraction, retaining the actual capture stoichiometry gives w=1−27Z/7=323/350 and M56​≃0.92M⊙​. The same temperature and density do not alone fix a total burned mass; equality of that mass is also implicit in the comparison. The two-isotope assumption is a bulk-ash approximation, not a complete equilibrium network with all free particles identically absent.
For otherwise comparable explosions, approximate the peak luminosity by a quantity proportional to the radioactive nickel mass. Let d be the true distance to an extremely low-neon event, and use the solar calibration L⊙​/L0​=523/550. Its measured flux is F=L0​/(4πd2), but the inferred standard-candle distance is
ddest​​=L0​L⊙​​​=550523​​≃0.975.​
(37)
The source is intrinsically brighter than the calibration, so the distance is underestimated by about 2.5%, conventionally described here as about 3%. Equivalently, d/dest​=550/523​≃1.0255, consistent with the supplied rounded factor 1.03. Using the literal nitrogen metallicity instead gives about a 4% underestimate. These are conditional composition effects in this simplified model; real Type Ia distance estimation also standardizes light-curve shape and color.

RL​R˙L​​=(2q−35​)M2​M˙2​​,R2​R˙2​​=M2​M˙2​​.​

The second relation uses the supplied equilibrium donor sequence. As the donor loses mass, M˙2​<0. If q>4/3, its Roche lobe shrinks fractionally faster than this donor radius, so the excess filling increases and drives more transfer: it is positive feedback. If q<4/3, infinitesimal loss instead reduces overfill on that assumed sequence, so some continuing driver is needed to retain contact.
There are two limits to the literal dynamical interpretation. The given initial range is q<1, so q>4/3 is an extrapolated instability test, not a state inside that range. Also, a true dynamical stability of binary mass transfer test needs the donor's adiabatic stellar radius response exponent ζad​, rather than its thermal-equilibrium exponent. In general,
dtd​logRL​R2​​=(ζ∗​−2q+5/3)M2​M˙2​​,qcrit​=2ζ∗​+5/3​.

One continuing driver is magnetic braking of a binary star. A magnetized wind carries away the donor's spin angular momentum; tides that maintain near-synchronous rotation replenish that spin from the orbit, removing orbital angular momentum. With an external J˙<0, the lobe response becomes
RL​R˙L​​=2JJ˙​+(2q−35​)M2​M˙2​​.

M2​M˙2​​=4/3−qJ˙/J​.​
(46)
A negative torque therefore drives mass loss while maintaining contact for q<4/3, subject to the chosen donor response and braking assumptions.

To obtain the specified expansion, use the usual nova-ejection prescription: the escaping material is expelled isotropically from the white dwarf and carries its specific orbital angular momentum, with no additional torque. This is isotropic re-emission from a binary star. Circularity alone does not determine the angular momentum carried by the ejecta; the prescription is an additional physical assumption.
The accretor's orbital radius is a1​=aM2​/M, so its specific angular momentum satisfies
j1​=a12​Ω,Jj1​​=M1​MM2​​=Mq​.
(47)
During ejection, take δM1​=−δm, δM2​=0 and δM=−δm, where δm>0. Consequently
JδJ​=−Mqδm​.
(48)
Differentiate J=M1​M2​Ga/M​ to first order:
−Mqδm​=−M1​δm​+21​aδa​+21​Mδm​.
(49)
Using M/M1​=1+q yields
aδa​=Mδm​.​
(50)
The same lobe law now has changing total mass but unchanged donor mass, so
RL​δRL​​=aδa​+31​(M2​δM2​​−MδM​)=3M4δm​.​
(51)
The donor radius is unchanged on the short eruption timescale, whereas its lobe expands. It therefore falls inside the lobe and Roche-lobe overflow stops in this idealized model. This is nova-induced binary detachment.
The need for the ejection prescription can also be seen algebraically. If the ejecta instead have mean specific angular momentum jej​=ηJ​J/M, the same circular-orbit differentiation gives
aδa​=(1+2q−2ηJ​)Mδm​.
(52)
The requested coefficient is obtained only when ηJ​=q. Other prescriptions can produce a different expansion or a contraction; the stated circularity condition does not rule them out. The ejection may be short compared with the transfer time while remaining slow compared with an orbit, consistent with circularity. If truly impulsive, an eccentricity is generally excited unless an additional circularization assumption is made.
Now put Γ=−J˙>0 for the constant external torque. Keep the masses, mass ratio and torque coefficients fixed to leading order over one small-δm cycle; corrections in those coefficients affect the calculated times only at the next order. −M˙2​=M˙1​=J(4−3q)3ΓM2​​.

Here the given q<1 ensures a positive denominator. It describes an idealized nova cycle; extra frictional loss, irradiation-driven donor changes or a different ejection angular momentum would change the ratio.
