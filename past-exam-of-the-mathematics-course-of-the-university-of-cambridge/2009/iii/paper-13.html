Put eN​(t)=e2πit/N and use normalized averages Ex​=N−1∑x∈Z/NZ​. Our convention for Fourier coefficients on a finite abelian group is
f​(r)=Ex​f(x)eN​(−rx),r∈Z/NZ.​
(1)
The underlying orthogonality of complex exponentials is Ex​eN​(rx)=1 for r=0 and zero otherwise. For nonzero r this is the finite geometric series with ratio eN​(r)=1; its sum vanishes because that ratio has Nth power one. The identical orthogonality statement holds when averaging over r instead of x.
Using this orthogonality to sum the definition over r proves Fourier inversion on a finite group:
∑r​f​(r)eN​(rx)=Ey​f(y)∑r​eN​(r(x−y))=f(x).
(2)
Indeed Efg​=∑r​g​(r)​Ex​f(x)eN​(−rx)=∑r​f​(r)g​(r)​. This proves the Parseval identity on a finite group and its squared-norm case:
Ex​f(x)g(x)​=∑r​f​(r)g​(r)​,∑r​∣f​(r)∣2=Ex​∣f(x)∣2.
(3)
For normalized convolution on a finite group, write (f∗g)(x)=Ey​f(y)g(x−y). Changing variables x=y+z in its transform gives
f∗g​(r)=Ey,z​f(y)g(z)eN​(−r(y+z))=f​(r)g​(r),
(4)
the convolution theorem on a finite group. Linearity of the transform follows directly from its definition. Translation τt​f(x)=f(x+t) gives τt​f​(r)=eN​(rt)f​(r) by changing variables. Complex conjugation gives f​​(r)=f​(−r)​; for f​(x)=f(−x)​, the corresponding identity is f​​(r)=f​(r)​. Finally f​(0)=Ef and ∣f​(r)∣≤E∣f∣ by the triangle inequality. These identities establish the basic normalized Fourier analysis on a finite abelian group needed below, with all normalizations fixed.
For completeness, pointwise multiplication has the dual convolution formula fg​(r)=∑s​f​(s)g​(r−s): insert the inversion formula for g into the defining average and collect its frequencies. If c=0 modulo N, the change of variables u=cx gives f(c⋅)​(r)=f​(rc−1). These also follow directly from the same additive character orthogonality.

Assume A is nonempty, so 0<α≤1, and put a=1A​. By the Parseval identity on a finite group, ∑r​∣a(r)∣2=α. Select the nonzero frequencies
R={r=0:∣a(r)∣≥α3/2/2}.
(5)
Then ∣R∣α3/4≤α, giving ∣R∣≤4α−2. Outside R∪{0}, the fourth Fourier moment has the bound
∑r∈/R∪{0}​∣a(r)∣4≤4α3​∑r​∣a(r)∣2=4α4​.
(6)
The fourfold normalized convolution on a finite group H=a∗a∗1−A​∗1−A​ has transform ∣a(r)∣4, so Fourier inversion on a finite group gives H(x)=∑r​∣a(r)∣4eN​(rx). It is real and nonnegative, and H(x)>0 implies x∈2A−2A because the convolution counts representations, with a positive normalization.
Use the Bohr set in phase-distance convention
B(R,1/10)={x:∥rx/N∥R/Z​≤1/10 for every r∈R}.
(7)
On this set all retained additive characters have positive real part. The zero-frequency contribution is a(0)4=α4, and the total absolute contribution of the discarded frequencies is at most α4/4. Hence
H(x)≥α4−α4/4>0(x∈B(R,1/10)).
(8)
Therefore
B(R,1/10)⊆A+A−A−A,∣R∣≤4/α2.​
(9)
This is an explicit Bogolyubov lemma. In the chord-distance convention for a Bohr set, the phase-radius 1/10 corresponds to chord radius 2sin(π/10)>1/10; alternatively the same positivity proof works directly at chord radius 1/10. Thus the requested lower bound on width holds in either convention. If R is empty, the Bohr set is the whole group. Nonemptiness of A is necessary for the density statement.

Count a three-term arithmetic progression as an ordered triple (x,x+d,x+2d), allowing d=0. Inserting Fourier inversion on a finite group into its normalized count and using additive character orthogonality in x,d gives
T=Ex,d​a(x)a(x+d)a(x+2d)=∑r​a(r)2a(−2r).
(10)
Indeed the two frequency constraints are r+s+t=0 and s+2t=0, so the surviving frequencies are (r,s,t)=(t,−2t,t). The term at zero is α3.
For odd N, let f=a−α be the balanced indicator function of a finite subset, and put M=maxs=0​∣f​(s)∣. Its zero coefficient is zero, and its other coefficients equal those of a. Since −2r=0 when r=0, the Parseval identity on a finite group gives
∣T−α3∣≤M∑r=0​∣a(r)∣2≤Mα.
(11)
The assumed deficit T<α3/2 therefore forces
s=0max​∣f​(s)∣>2α2​.​
(12)
Thus c=1/2 and C=2 suffice. This is the large Fourier coefficient from a deficit of three-term progressions estimate; no generalized von Neumann theorem is needed. For the remaining prime N=2, the count is T=Ex,d​a(x)a(x+d)=α2, so the deficit hypothesis is impossible. The zero common difference was included throughout.

Write e(t)=e2πit. We first prove the needed cancellation of quadratic exponential sums, rather than invoke equidistribution without proof. The number 2​ is irrational: writing it as a reduced fraction would make its numerator even from u2=2v2, and then its denominator even as well, a contradiction. Thus 2hr2​ is irrational for nonzero integers h,r.
Fix h=0 and set zn​=e(h2​n2) for 1≤n≤L, extending it by zero otherwise. For integers L≥H≥1, each term appears exactly H times in the shifted sum, so
H∑n=1L​zn​=∑n=1L+H−1​∑j=0H−1​zn−j​.
(13)
For any complex numbers w1​,…,wJ​, nonnegativity of ∑j​∣wj​−J−1∑i​wi​∣2 gives ∣∑j​wj​∣2≤J∑j​∣wj​∣2. Apply this special Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the displayed outer sum, and then expand the inner squared modulus. This gives
​∑n=1L​zn​​2≤H2L+H−1​[HL+2∑r=1H−1​(H−r)​∑n=1L−r​zn+r​zn​​​].
(14)
For fixed H and large L, each correlation is the geometric series
e(hr22​)∑n=1L−r​e(2hr2​n),

whose absolute value is at most 2/∣1−e(2hr2​)∣, independently of L. This follows by multiplying the geometric sum by 1−e(2hr2​); its denominator is nonzero by irrationality. Divide the inequality by L2 and let L→∞, keeping H fixed. It gives a limit superior at most 1/H. Taking arbitrarily large H proves

To turn cancellation into the particular approximation, put ε=10−8 and choose an integer M>2/sin2(πε). The Fejér kernel is the explicit finite trigonometric polynomial

The expansion follows by counting pairs of indices with difference h. Its constant coefficient is one, so the cancellation just proved gives
L1​∑n=1L​FM​(n22​)⟶1.
(18)
If ∥t∥R/Z​≥ε, the geometric-series formula gives
FM​(t)=Msin2(πt)sin2(πMt)​≤Msin2(πε)1​<21​.

For all sufficiently large L, the displayed average exceeds 1/2. Consequently at least one n∈{1,…,L} satisfies
∥n22​∥R/Z​<10−8.​
(20)
It is a positive integer, as required. Every auxiliary cancellation and kernel identity used in this existence proof has been derived; a smooth cutoff or an unproved Weyl criterion is unnecessary.

A Freiman 2-isomorphism between subsets of abelian groups is a bijection ϕ such that
a1​+a2​=a3​+a4​⟺ϕ(a1​)+ϕ(a2​)=ϕ(a3​)+ϕ(a4​).
(21)
Both directions matter: an injective homomorphism on individual elements may still create new pair-sum relations. More generally a Freiman s-isomorphism preserves equalities of sums of s elements in both directions. For a nonempty finite set, its doubling constant is σ[A]=∣A+A∣/∣A∣. A Freiman 2-isomorphism preserves this constant, since a+b↦ϕ(a)+ϕ(b) is a well-defined bijection of the two sumsets.
First let A⊆Z have n elements and doubling at most K. The permitted Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality gives ∣2A−2A∣≤K4n. We will obtain the half-size prime cyclic Freiman model for every prime p>4K4n. Choose an auxiliary prime q>p larger than the absolute value of every member of 2A−2A. Such a prime exists: for any integer bound M, every prime divisor of M!+1 exceeds M.
Choose λ uniformly from the nonzero residues modulo q. For each nonzero d∈2A−2A, the residue λd is uniform among the nonzero residues. Put
T={jp(modq):0<∣jp∣<q}.
(22)
There are at most 2(q−1)/p such residues, and therefore the probability that any of the nonzero differences is dilated into T is at most
∣2A−2A∣p(q−1)2q​≤p4K4n​<1.
(23)
The first cardinal estimate is an upper bound even if some listed residues coincide; the slightly looser probability bound is convenient. Thus there exists λ with no such bad difference.
For this λ, let ta​∈{0,…,q−1} represent λa. Split these representatives into the two intervals [0,(q−1)/2] and [(q+1)/2,q−1]. Take A′ from the more populated interval, so ∣A′∣≥n/2. For any four elements of A′, put D=ta1​​+ta2​​−ta3​​−ta4​​. Their common half-interval gives ∣D∣<q, and
D≡λ(a1​+a2​−a3​−a4​)(modq).
(24)
If the original difference is zero, then D is a multiple of q of absolute value less than q, so D=0. If the original difference is nonzero, D is nonzero, and it cannot be a multiple of p: otherwise its residue belongs to T, contrary to the choice of λ. Therefore a↦ta​(modp) preserves pair-sum relations in both directions. It is injective as well, by applying the relation test to a+a0​=b+a0​ with any fixed a0​∈A′. Hence
∣A′∣≥∣A∣/2,A′ has a Freiman 2-model in Z/pZ for every p>4K4∣A∣.​
(25)
This proves the stated polynomial bound, including the prescribed prime rather than just an unspecified cyclic modulus.
Now let A⊆F2∞​. Finiteness of A places it in some finite-dimensional coordinate space, so models exist. Choose a Freiman 2-isomorphism A→C⊆F2m​ with m minimal. The doubling constant of C is at most K.
If a nonzero v did not belong to 4C, projection onto F2m​/⟨v⟩ would preserve all pair-sum relations in both directions: a new relation would have original difference v, whereas in characteristic two every such difference lies in 4C. Projection is also injective on C, since C+C⊆4C by adding any element of C twice. This would give a model in dimension m−1, contradicting minimality. Consequently every nonzero vector belongs to 4C; zero belongs as well, by adding one element four times. Thus 4C=F2m​.
Using the Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality once more gives
2m=∣4C∣≤K4∣C∣=K4∣A∣.​
(26)
This is the minimal binary Freiman models have full fourfold sumset argument. It also covers a singleton, with m=0.

We take α,β>0, as is necessary for the assertion. The nonvacuous cases have 0<α≤1/2 and 0<β≤1. Put n=∣A∣ and
K=maxb∈B​n∣A+bA∣​.
(27)
We prove a lower bound for K by transferring small sumset growth through algebraic operations on the scalars. This gives a self-contained polynomial skew-sumset expansion over a prime field proof using the permitted Ruzsa triangle inequality, Ruzsa covering lemma and Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality.
Since ∣B∣>1, choose b0​∈B∖{0}. The bound ∣b0​A+A∣≤K∣b0​A∣ and the mixed-set Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality give
∣rA−sA∣≤Kr+sn(r,s nonnegative integers).
(28)
In particular ∣A+A∣≤K2n and ∣3A−2A∣≤K5n. Here repeated-set sums such as 3A allow independent choices from each copy.
For a scalar t, write D(t)=∣A+tA∣/n, and put Q=A−A for the difference set. The Ruzsa covering lemma gives tA⊆Tt​+Q with ∣Tt​∣≤D(t). Indeed choose a maximal family of disjoint translates u+A, with u∈tA; counting their union bounds the family size, and maximality forces every remaining translate to meet one of them.
For any r,s, the containments A+(r+s)A⊆A+rA+sA and A+rsA⊆rTs​+A+rQ give, after covering each copy of rA,sA,
D(r+s)≤K5D(r)D(s),D(rs)≤K5D(r)2D(s).
(29)
For the second bound, specifically rQ=rA−rA⊆Tr​−Tr​+2Q, and A+2Q=3A−2A. The Ruzsa triangle inequality also gives ∣A−rA∣≤∣A+A∣∣A+rA∣/n, so D(−r)≤K2D(r). These are the closure bounds for small skew-sumset scalars.
We next prove a growth lemma for the scalar sets. For S⊆Fp​ with m=∣S∣≥2, let
R={c−da−b​:a,b,c,d∈S, c=d}.
(30)
If R=Fp​, then R+1 is not contained in R: otherwise equal cardinality would make R invariant under addition of one, which generates the entire prime field. Choose r=1+(a−b)/(c−d)∈/R. The map (x,y)↦x+ry on S2 is injective. Indeed a collision with different second coordinates would express r as a ratio of two differences from S, while equal second coordinates force equal first coordinates. Clearing the nonzero denominator puts its image inside
(c−d)S+(c−d+a−b)S⊆3SS−3SS,
(31)
so the latter set has at least m2 elements. Here SS is the product set, and 3SS−3SS denotes three independent product terms minus three others, not multiplication by the scalar three.
If R=Fp​, let Et​ count the collisions x1​+ty1​=x2​+ty2​ with all four variables in S. Summing over t gives
∑t∈Fp​​Et​=pm2+m3(m−1)≤pm2+m4.
(32)
The first term comes from equal second coordinates, which force equal first coordinates. For unequal second coordinates, each quadruple determines exactly one scalar. Some t consequently satisfies Et​≤m2+m4/p. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on the representation counts gives
∣S+tS∣≥Et​m4​≥1+m2/pm2​≥21​min(p,m2).
(33)
Representing t as a ratio from R and clearing its denominator embeds a dilate of this sumset in 2SS−2SS⊆3SS−3SS, padding with the same product in the positive and negative sums. We have proved the quadratic growth of a sixfold product difference set estimate
∣3SS−3SS∣≥21​min(p,∣S∣2).​
(34)
Starting from S0​=B, define Sj+1​=3Sj​Sj​−3Sj​Sj​. Choose
k=1+⌈log2​(1/β)⌉.
(35)
For p≥max(4,22/β), the growth lemma implies ∣Sk​∣≥p/2. To see the constants explicitly, put vj​=∣Sj​∣/2. Then v0​≥pβ/2 and vj+1​≥min(p/4,vj2​). Induction gives vj​≥min(p/4,pβ2j−1), and the chosen k makes the exponent at least one.
Every t∈S0​ has D(t)≤K. If D(t)≤KLj​ on Sj​, the closure bounds show that each product has exponent at most 3Lj​+5, its negative at most 3Lj​+7, and a sum of six such terms has exponent at most 18Lj​+67. Thus, with
L0​=1,Lj+1​=18Lj​+67,
(36)
we have ∣A+tA∣≤KLk​n for every t∈Sk​. The integer Lk​ depends only on β.
Now apply the collision count to A instead of S. Summing over all scalars gives at most pn2+n4. Since ∣Sk​∣≥p/2, some t∈Sk​ has collision count at most 2n2+2n4/p. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality therefore gives
KLk​n≥∣A+tA∣≥2+2n2/pn2​≥41​min(n2,p).
(37)
It follows that
KLk​≥41​min(n,p/n)≥41​pα.
(38)
For p≥42/α this gives K≥pα/(2Lk​)≥nα/(2Lk​), proving the desired positive exponent for all sufficiently large primes.
For completeness, the same conclusion with a possibly smaller exponent holds for the remaining primes. Choose an integer
P0​≥max(4,22/β,42/α),cα,β​=min(2Lk​α​,logP0​log(1+1/P0​)​)>0.
(39)
If p<P0​, choose any nonzero b∈B. We have 1<n<p. If ∣A+bA∣=n, all its translates A+u for u∈bA would coincide. Two distinct such u would make A invariant under a nonzero translation, forcing A=Fp​, a contradiction. Thus ∣A+bA∣≥n+1≥n1+cα,β​ by the definition of the second exponent. Together with the large-prime case this proves
∃b∈B: ∣A+bA∣≥∣A∣1+cα,β​.​
(40)
Positivity of the parameters cannot be dropped: at α=0, taking A=Fp​ prevents any expansion; at β=0, the allowed set B={0} prevents expansion of any proper A with more than one element. No external sum-product theorem was invoked: the scalar growth and transfer arguments were proved above.
