Use the defining property of the Frobenius complement: H∩Hx={1} whenever x∈/H. The induced character formula, extended linearly to any class function, is
θG(g)=∣H∣1​∑x∈G: x−1gx∈H​θ(x−1gx).
(1)

θG(1)=[G:H]θ(1)=0=θ(1).
(2)
Together these give
(θG)H​=θ.​
(3)
The zero identity value is essential: at nonidentity elements the intersection argument already gives the equality, whereas induction multiplies the identity value by the subgroup index.

⟨ϑG,ϑG⟩G​=⟨ϑ,(ϑG)H​⟩H​=⟨ϑ,ϑ⟩H​=1+d2,⟨ϑG,1G​⟩G​=−d.

Consequently

(6)

Its squared norm is ∑aχ2​, so norm one makes it χ or −χ for one irreducible χ. Positive degree rules out the negative sign. Thus Ψη​ is an irreducible character, an instance of the fact that positive-degree norm-one virtual characters are irreducible.
If x∈N∖{1}, no conjugate of x lies in H, so the induction formula gives ϑG(x)=0. At 1 it is also zero. Hence Ψη​(x)=d for all x∈N. Its trace equals its dimension only when all its unit-modulus eigenvalues are 1. Thus the kernel of a character is {g:Ψη​(g)=Ψη​(1)}, and N⊆kerΨη​.
Conversely, if g∈/N, then g is conjugate to some h∈H∖{1}. The character of the regular representation of H is

Because N is an abelian group, every φ∈Irr(N) is a linear character, hence a homomorphism to C×. Its inertia group of a character contains N, whose inner conjugations act trivially on N. Write an arbitrary g∈G as g=nh with n∈N and h∈H, using G=NH. Conjugation by n does not affect φ, so g stabilizes φ if and only if h does.
Suppose h=1 stabilizes φ. Then φ(h−1yh)=φ(y) for every y∈N, and its multiplicativity gives
φ([h,y])=φ(h−1y−1hy)=φ(h−1yh)−1φ(y)=1.
(13)

IG​(φ)=N(φ=1N​).​
(14)
The argument uses both abelianness, to make φ multiplicative, and the fixed-point-free commutator bijection.

We use complex representations. By Maschke's theorem, restriction to any finite subgroup is a semisimple module, so it has a canonical isotypic decomposition. In particular, the θ-isotypic component Wθ​ is the sum of all copies of the irreducible N-module with character θ. The central character idempotent
eθ​=∣N∣θ(1)​∑n∈N​θ(n−1)n
(15)
projects onto this component. These projections preserve every N-submodule, which consequently decomposes as the direct sum of its intersections with the isotypic components.
Let W afford ξ∈Irr(T∣θ). The component Wθ​ is nonzero. Since T=IG​(θ) fixes θ, it preserves Wθ​. Irreducibility of W as a T-module therefore gives W=Wθ​: its restriction to N consists entirely of copies of θ.
Form the induced representation
V=C[G]⊗C[T]​W=⨁g∈R​g⊗W,
(16)
where R represents the left cosets G/T. Because N◃G, for n∈N we have
n(g⊗w)=g⊗(g−1ng)w.
(17)
Thus g⊗W is N-isotypic of type θg, where θg(n)=θ(g−1ng). These types are distinct for distinct cosets gT, precisely by the definition of the inertia group of a character T.
Let Y be a nonzero G-submodule of V. Its isotypic decomposition as an N-module shows that it meets some g⊗W nontrivially. Acting by g−1 gives Y∩(1⊗W)=0. This intersection is a T-submodule of the irreducible W, hence equals 1⊗W. The G-translates of that component span V, so Y=V. The induced representation is therefore irreducible, and its restriction contains θ. Its induced character satisfies
ξG∈Irr(G∣θ).​
(18)
This proves the irreducibility assertion directly from isotypic components; no form of the correspondence being proved has been assumed.

An M-group is a finite group for which every irreducible character is induced from a linear character of some subgroup. Let L◃K have both L and K/L abelian, and fix an irreducible K-module V with character χ. Since L is abelian, VL​ has a linear constituent. Choose, among all pairs (A,φ) with L⊆A⊆K and a linear character φ occurring in χA​, one for which ∣A∣ is maximal.
Every subgroup containing L is normal in K: its image in the abelian quotient K/L is normal, and it is the full preimage of that image. Thus A◃K, so the inertia group of a character IK​(φ) is defined and contains A.
Suppose x∈IK​(φ)∖A. The nonzero φ-isotypic component Vφ​ is preserved by x, and every a∈A acts there as the scalar φ(a) because φ(1)=1. The finite-order operator representing x is diagonalizable over C; choose an eigenvector 0=v∈Vφ​. Then Cv is invariant under both A and x, hence under D=⟨A,x⟩. This line affords a linear character ψ of D with ψA​=φ. Since it is a subrepresentation of VD​, it is a constituent of χD​. But D strictly contains A and still contains L, contradicting maximality.
Therefore IK​(φ)=A. Apply the Clifford correspondence with normal subgroup A. Its inertia group is A itself, and Irr(A∣φ)={φ}, so the unique irreducible K-character lying over φ is φK. In particular,
χ=φK,φ(1)=1.​
(22)
Since χ was arbitrary, every irreducible character is a monomial character, proving that finite metabelian groups are monomial:
Every finite metabelian group is an M-group.​
(23)

For integer partitions λ,μ of n, padded by zero parts, the dominance order on partitions is
λ⊵μ⟺i=1∑r​λi​≥i=1∑r​μi​for every r≥1.​
(24)
A λ-tableau fills the Young diagram of λ with 1,…,n, each once. A tabloid [t] remembers the sets of entries in each row, ignoring their order within that row. The Young permutation module Mλ=CXλ​ has these tabloids as basis, with Sn​ acting by permuting the entries.
The row and column stabilizers Rt​,Ct​ independently permute entries within the rows and columns of t. The column antisymmetrizer is κt​=∑c∈Ct​​sgn(c)c, and et​=κt​[t] is the associated polytabloid. Since Cgt​=gCt​g−1 and conjugation preserves permutation sign,
κgt​=gκt​g−1,egt​=κgt​[gt]=gκt​[t],get​=egt​.​
(25)
Thus the Specht module is the submodule
Sλ=spanC​{et​:t is a λ-tableau}⊆Mλ.​
(26)
Every tableau is gt for a fixed t, so any single et​ generates it under Sn​. Also Ct​∩Rt​={1}, since a permutation preserving both rows and columns must fix every cell. The tabloids [ct] for c∈Ct​ are consequently distinct, and et​ has coefficient 1 at [t]; in particular et​=0.
We use the following precise column antisymmetrizer facts. If some row of a μ-tableau s contains two entries from one column of t, the transposition interchanging them pairs equal tabloids with opposite signs in κt​[s], so κt​[s]=0. Otherwise each row of s meets each column of t at most once. The first r rows of s therefore contain at most min(r,λj′​) entries from column j of t, whence
∑i=1r​μi​≤∑j​min(r,λj′​)=∑i=1r​λi​.
(27)
This states and explains dominance from a nonzero column antisymmetrizer: κt​[s]=0 implies λ⊵μ.
When μ=λ, the nonzero column antisymmetrizer criterion says more: either κt​[s]=0, or a column permutation takes [s] to [t], and κt​[s]=±et​. Thus κt​Mλ=Cet​. This follows by matching the row positions within each column when all intersections have size at most one; the equal row and column sizes force the Ferrers incidence pattern.
For the inner product, use the positive Hermitian extension of the orthonormal tabloid basis, linear in its first argument. It has the supplied self-adjointness of κt​. Its orthogonal complement of Sλ agrees with that obtained from the complex tabloid bilinear form, since the spanning polytabloids have real coefficients. Because the coefficient of [t] in et​ is 1, self-adjointness and the one-dimensional image give the useful identity
κt​u=⟨u,et​⟩et​(u∈Mλ).​
(28)
Indeed, the scalar multiplying et​ is the [t]-coefficient of κt​u, which is ⟨κt​u,[t]⟩=⟨u,κt​[t]⟩=⟨u,et​⟩.

The preceding parts give a simple Specht module for each partition and show that different partitions give nonisomorphic simples. The conjugacy classes of Sn​ are indexed by partitions of n, namely cycle types. We use the standard finite-group character theorem: the number of irreducible complex characters equals the number of conjugacy classes, and they form an orthonormal basis of complex class functions. Consequently the χλ are the complete list of irreducible characters of Sn​.
By Maschke's theorem, the Young permutation module Mμ decomposes as a direct sum of these simple modules. For a semisimple complex module, Schur lemma makes the multiplicity of a simple module equal to the dimension of the homomorphism space from that simple; character orthogonality identifies the same number with the character inner product. Hence
Mμ≅⨁λ⊢n​(Sλ)⊕aλμ​,aλμ​=dimHomCSn​​(Sλ,Mμ)=⟨ξμ,χλ⟩∈Z≥0​.
(36)
Part (ii) makes aλμ​=0 unless λ⊵μ, and gives aμμ​=1. Taking characters proves
ξμ=λ⊵μ∑​⟨ξμ,χλ⟩χλ,⟨ξμ,χμ⟩=1.​
(37)
Thus the permutation-character decomposition is triangular in the dominance order on partitions, with diagonal multiplicity one. These are the dominance and diagonal-multiplicity consequences of Young's rule, obtained here without assuming its full multiplicity formula.

Take g=(1 2 ⋯ n). Since n≡3(mod4) is odd, its sign is (−1)n−1=1, so g∈An​. Define s to fix 1 and reverse the other cyclic labels: it interchanges 2 with n, 3 with n−1, and so on. Then sgs−1=g−1, while
sgn(s)=(−1)(n−1)/2=−1.
(40)
The centralizer of an n-cycle in Sn​ is ⟨g⟩: a commuting permutation is determined by the image of one point, and that image fixes the corresponding power of the cycle. All its elements are even, because g is even. Any other conjugator taking g to g−1 differs from s by a centralizer element and therefore remains odd. Thus g and g−1 are not conjugate in An​. This is the parity obstruction described by inversion of an odd cycle in an alternating group.
For any complex character of a finite group, a unitary realization gives
θ(g−1)=θ(g)​.
(41)
If every θ∈Irr(An​) had real value at g, all these characters would take equal values on g and g−1. But irreducible characters separate conjugacy classes: they form a basis of class functions, so equality of every irreducible value would also give equality of the class indicator functions. The two distinct conjugacy classes cannot have that property. Consequently
There is θ∈Irr(An​) with θ((1 2 ⋯ n))∈/R.​
(42)
In other words, these alternating groups are not ambivalent groups, even though all symmetric-group irreducible characters are integer-valued.

In character theory, a p-elementary group is a finite group isomorphic to P×C, where P is a p-group and C is cyclic of order coprime to p. Either factor may be trivial. An elementary group is p-elementary for at least one prime p; it need not be an elementary abelian group.
A generalised character means an integer linear combination of irreducible complex characters. Brauer's characterisation of characters states that a complex class function f on a finite group G is a generalised character if and only if fE​ is a generalised character for every elementary subgroup E≤G. Equivalently,
f∈ZIrr(G)⟺⟨fE​,α⟩E​∈Z for every elementary E≤G and α∈Irr(E).​
(43)
An ordinary character additionally requires nonnegative coefficients in its global irreducible decomposition. We use the virtual-character criterion just stated.
For the extension construction, set d=θ(1). The determinant character detθ is linear. Since its extension μ has the same value 1 at the identity, μ is also a linear character. For any g∈G, the subgroup Jg​=N⟨g⟩ has cyclic quotient Jg​/N, hence a solvable quotient. The invariant character θ has degree coprime to [Jg​:N], since this index divides [G:N], and μJg​​ extends its determinant. The permitted coprime-degree determinant extension theorem therefore gives a unique character χg​∈Irr(Jg​) with
(χg​)N​=θ,detχg​=μJg​​.
(44)
Define a function on G by f(g)=χg​(g). We next verify that these individually defined values form a compatible class function.
If x∈G, conjugation carries Jg​ to Jxgx−1​. Transporting χg​ by this conjugation gives a character extending θ, because θ is G-invariant, and with determinant μ restricted to the conjugate subgroup, because μ is a class function. Uniqueness identifies the transported character with χxgx−1​. Consequently f(xgx−1)=f(g).
Now take an elementary subgroup E≤G and let J=NE. A finite p-group is solvable, a cyclic group is solvable, and a direct product of solvable groups is solvable. Thus E is solvable, and so is J/N≅E/(E∩N). Again the index [J:N] divides [G:N], so the same extension theorem gives χJ​∈Irr(J) extending θ with determinant μJ​.
For g∈E, we have Jg​≤J. The restriction (χJ​)Jg​​ is irreducible: an invariant subspace for Jg​ would be invariant for N, whose restriction already affords the irreducible θ. Its determinant is μJg​​. By uniqueness on Jg​, it equals χg​. Therefore
fE​=(χJ​)E​,
(45)
an ordinary character and hence a generalised character of E. Brauer's characterisation of characters now makes f a generalised character of G. If n∈N, then Jn​=N and χn​=θ, so f(n)=θ(n). We have proved, by gluing determinant-normalized character extensions,
χ:=f is a generalised character of G,χN​=θ.​
(46)
Only the intermediate quotients Jg​/N and NE/N were required to be solvable; no solvability assumption on G/N has been added.
For the prime-set assertion, let E=P×C be p-elementary, and split the cyclic group as C=Cπ​×Cπ′​, where each factor has the indicated prime divisors in its order. If p∈π, put Eπ​=P×Cπ​ and Eπ′​=Cπ′​. If p∈/π, put Eπ​=Cπ​ and Eπ′​=P×Cπ′​. In both cases the factors commute, have trivial intersection and have orders with disjoint prime spectra. This proves the prime-set decomposition of elementary groups
E=Eπ​×Eπ′​,Eπ​ a π-group,Eπ′​ a π′-group.​
(47)
Here a pi-group has order divisible only by primes in π, and a pi-element has such an element order; the identity qualifies for both complementary prime sets.
Finally, suppose K satisfies the given element-order condition. The sets A,B are unions of conjugacy classes and are disjoint, because membership in both would force every prime divisor of the element order into π∩π′=∅, hence force order 1. Let E≤K be elementary and use E=Eπ​×Eπ′​. If both factors were nontrivial, choose a=1 in the first and b=1 in the second. They commute and have coprime orders, so ab has order ∣a∣∣b∣, containing primes from both π and π′. It would belong to neither A nor B nor {1}, contrary to the hypothesis. Thus every elementary subgroup is entirely a pi-group or entirely a complementary-prime group.
Write m=∣K∣π​ and r=∣K∣π′​ for the two prime parts of ∣K∣, so ∣K∣=mr and gcd(m,r)=1. The Chinese remainder theorem supplies an integer D satisfying
D≡1(modm),D≡0(modr).
(48)
Define the class function
f(1)=D,f(a)=1 (a∈A),f(b)=0 (b∈B).
(49)
For an elementary π-subgroup E, its order divides m. Let ρE​ be the character of its regular representation, equal to ∣E∣ at 1 and zero elsewhere. Then
fE​=1E​+∣E∣D−1​ρE​,
(50)
with integral coefficient because D≡1(mod∣E∣). For an elementary π′-subgroup, its order divides r, and
fE​=∣E∣D​ρE​
(51)
again has integral coefficient. The trivial subgroup satisfies either formula. Every elementary restriction is therefore a generalised character. A second application of Brauer's characterisation of characters proves
ξ:=f is a generalised character of K,ξ(a)=1 (a∈A),ξ(b)=0 (b∈B).​
(52)
The freely chosen identity value, fixed by the two congruences, makes the restrictions integral. This construction also covers the cases π=∅, π′=∅, or K={1}, using modulus-one congruences where appropriate.
