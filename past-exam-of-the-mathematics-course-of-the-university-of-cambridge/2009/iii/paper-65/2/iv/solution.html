Measure the logarithmic surface brightness slope in resolved radial annuli after accounting for instrumental blurring and foreground emission. Between a halo core and its outer boundary, dark-matter decay radiation predicts dlnI/dlnR=−1, whereas dark-matter annihilation radiation predicts −3; doubling radius therefore reduces the respective surface brightness by factors 2 and 8. These are quantitative templates to compare with independently mapped stellar and gaseous components.
An exponential galactic disk has I∗​(R)∝e−R/Rd​, so dlnI∗​/dlnR=−R/Rd​ and I∗​(2R)/I∗​(R)=e−R/Rd​. At R=5Rd​ the latter is about 0.0067, much faster decline than either halo template, and the projected emission should also be flattened along the galactic disk. A galactic bulge described by a Sérsic profile has dlnI∗​/dlnR=−(bn​/n)(R/Re​)1/n; for n=4 and b4​≃7.67, this is about −3.4 at 10Re​ and continues to steepen, whereas a pure halo power law keeps a fixed slope.
For a stellar halo with outer luminosity density proportional to r−p∗​, the projection of a spherical power-law emissivity gives I∗​∝R1−p∗​. Illustratively, p∗​=3--4 gives projected slopes −2--−3: then Id​/I∗​∝Rp∗​−2 rises strongly outward, but annihilation can resemble the steeper stellar template. Gas-related gamma emission must be compared with observed gas columns and the cosmic ray distribution; it is not always proportional to optical light.
A shallow, extended, approximately spherical R−1 component is particularly distinctive; an R−3 component needs additional evidence. Fit the stellar, gas and halo templates together over a broad radial range, including morphology and emission spectra. A measured slope alone cannot uniquely identify dark matter, since stellar or gas profiles can coincide with a halo slope over a limited interval.
