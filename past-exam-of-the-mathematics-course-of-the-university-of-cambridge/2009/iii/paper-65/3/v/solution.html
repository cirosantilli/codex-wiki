First specify the dynamical estimate: assume an initially isolated virialized system, stars of different masses well mixed with the same velocity distribution, and ejecta escaping rapidly compared with a stellar crossing time, without imparting kicks to surviving stars. If f is the retained stellar mass fraction, the original virial theorem gives 2T+W=0, where T is kinetic energy and W<0 is Newtonian gravitational potential energy. Immediately after loss, positions and velocities are unchanged, so
T′=fT,W′=f2W,E′=fT+f2W=∣W∣f(21​−f).
(26)
Thus impulsive disruption by stellar mass loss makes the energy of the whole remaining system nonnegative when at least half the initial mass is removed.
For the power-law initial mass function, the fraction removed is a mass fraction, not a number fraction:
F(α)=∫0.1100​x1−αdx∫10100​x1−αdx​=1002−α−0.12−α1002−α−102−α​(α=2),F(2)=ln1000ln10​=31​.
(27)
For integer slopes, F(0)≃0.990, F(1)≃0.901, F(2)=0.333, and F(3)≃0.009. Interpolating between α=1 and 2 puts the threshold around 1.7--1.8. Solving F=1/2 more accurately gives
2102−α=1002−α+0.12−α,αcrit​≃1.7910.​
(28)
The rearranged equation has a spurious root at α=2 introduced when the vanishing denominator was cleared; the logarithmic limit F(2)=1/3 excludes it. Increasing α shifts the mass distribution towards lower masses, so the top-heavy side, α<αcrit​, has nonnegative remnant energy in the impulsive model; equality is marginal. One can see strict monotonicity from the covariance obtained by differentiating F: F′=−Cov(1x>10​,lnx)<0 under the normalized mass-weighted distribution.
The initial mass function alone does not guarantee complete disruption. If losses occur slowly, the adiabatic mass-loss expansion law gives expansion rather than the instantaneous half-mass threshold, and positive total energy can still leave a bound core after selective escape. The stated value is therefore the conventional global impulsive estimate, with no external confining Newtonian gravitational potential.
