Put θ0​=1 and define the autoregressive polynomial and moving-average polynomial by
Φ(z)=1−∑k=1p​ϕk​zk,Θ(z)=∑k=0q​θk​zk.
(1)
For a minimal, coprime ARMA representation, the usual simultaneous causality and invertibility root criteria for an ARMA model are
Φ(z)=0 and Θ(z)=0for all ∣z∣≤1.​
(2)
The autoregressive condition makes Θ/Φ a stable one-sided filter and gives the causal second-order stationary process. The moving-average condition makes its inverse a stable one-sided filter, so the white noise can be reconstructed from present and past observations. If the two polynomials have a common factor, first use common factor cancellation in an ARMA model; necessary-and-sufficient root statements apply to the reduced noise-driven representation. Stationarity here is second-order stationarity; weak white noise alone does not assert strict stationarity of all finite-dimensional laws.
For p=1, write ϕ=ϕ1​, so ∣ϕ∣<1. Expanding (1−ϕz)−1=∑r≥0​ϕrzr gives the Wold representation
Xt​=∑j≥0​cj​ϵt−j​,cj​=k=0∑min(j,q)​θk​ϕj−k.​

The state-space model as printed supplies only uncorrelated white noise. That is insufficient for the claimed normal conditional distributions. For example, take S0​ normal, independent process noises wt​ taking values ±W​ equally often, and independent Gaussian observation noises. With G=1 and no previous observations, S1​=S0​+w1​ is a two-component shifted mixture distribution, not normal for W>0. Its characteristic function is eiμs−P0​s2/2cos(W​s), which has zeros, whereas a normal characteristic function has none. Similarly a non-Gaussian observation noise prevents the asserted conditional normal law for Xt​.
The intended scalar Gaussian Kalman recursion requires wt​∼N(0,W) and vt​∼N(0,V) independent of each other and of the previous state and observation history. Assume these Gaussian independence conditions, V>0, W≥0, and the given normal filtering law. Conditional on the past, GSt−1​+wt​ is a sum of independent normals, hence
St​∣Ft−1​∼N(mt​,Rt​),mt​=GSt−1​,Rt​=G2Pt−1​+W.​
(8)
The new observation noise is independent of St​ and the past, so Xt​∣St​,Ft−1​∼N(FSt​,V). Write St​=mt​+ηt​ and Xt​=Fmt​+Fηt​+vt​. The independent normal pair (ηt​,vt​) shows directly that
(St​Xt​​)​Ft−1​∼N((mt​Fmt​​),(Qt​FRt​​FRt​Rt​​)),Qt​=F2Rt​+V.
(9)
The covariance FRt​ comes from the common ηt​ term. Applying the conditional multivariate normal distribution formula with Xt​ as the conditioned coordinate gives the Kalman filter update
St​=mt​+Qt​FRt​​(Xt​−Fmt​),Pt​=Rt​−Qt​F2Rt2​​=Qt​Rt​V​.​
(10)
The final expression verifies nonnegativity of the posterior variance.
For F=G=1, define
αt​=Pt−1​+W+VPt−1​+W​.​
(11)
The recursions become St​=(1−αt​)St−1​+αt​Xt​ and Pt​=αt​V. If Pt​→P, then αt​→α=P/V and, with c=W/V,
α=α+c+1α+c​⟹α2+cα−c=0.
(12)
The nonnegative solution is the steady-state local-level Kalman gain:
α∞​=2c2+4c​−c​,P=2W2+4WV​−W​.​
(13)
For c=0 the limit is zero; for c>0 it lies strictly between zero and one. These are exact conditional-law results under the additional Gaussian assumptions, not consequences of the weaker printed noise description alone.

Ah​={(u,v):u>0, u2≤h(v/u)}.

area(Ah​)=∫R​∫0h(x)​​ududx=2Z​.

The region Ah​ usually has a curved boundary, so sampling uniformly inside it directly is inconvenient. Rejection sampling replaces it by an easily sampled envelope, usually a rectangle. Choose finite bounds a,b−​,b+​ with
a≥supx​h(x)​,b−​≤min(0,infx​xh(x)​),b+​≥max(0,supx​xh(x)​).
(17)
Then Ah​⊆(0,a)×(b−​,b+​) up to null boundaries. A general rectangle algorithm uses only independent uniform deviates: draw U1​,U2​ on (0,1), set u=aU1​, v=b−​+(b+​−b−​)U2​, and return v/u if u2≤h(v/u); otherwise repeat. If a numerical generator returns u=0, discard that draw before division. No knowledge of Z is required to run the method. Its acceptance rate is the ratio of the two areas:
r=area(B)area(Ah​)​=2a(b+​−b−​)Z​.​
(18)
More generally the same area ratio applies to any finite-area envelope B sampled uniformly. A change in the proportionality constant of h scales both ratio coordinates and their areas consistently.
Finite rectangle bounds are a real condition, not a consequence of integrability alone. For instance h(x)=(1+∣x∣)−3/2 is integrable, but ∣x∣h(x)​ is unbounded. A finite-area envelope for a ratio-of-uniforms region gives a general extension: choose a finite-area open envelope containing Ah​, decompose it up to null boundaries into countably many disjoint rectangles, and select a rectangle with probability proportional to its area using a uniform draw and cumulative probabilities. Use two more uniforms to sample within it, then apply the same acceptance test. This samples the envelope uniformly and has acceptance Z/(2area(B)). Such envelopes exist for every finite-area measurable Ah​; obtaining usable bounds or an effective rectangle decomposition is density-specific. The finite-rectangle version above is the usual practical implementation.

For a target probability density function π and a proposal distribution with density q(x′∣x), the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm starts in the target support and repeats: propose x′∼q(⋅∣x), draw an independent uniform U, and move to x′ if
U≤α(x,x′),α(x,x′)=min(1,π(x)q(x′∣x)π(x′)q(x∣x′)​).​
(36)
Otherwise retain x. A zero reverse-proposal density makes the acceptance zero. Unknown multiplicative constants of π cancel. For distinct states,
π(x)q(x′∣x)α(x,x′)=min{π(x)q(x′∣x),π(x′)q(x∣x′)},
(37)
which is symmetric in the states. Together with the probability of holding at the current state, this proves detailed balance and invariance of π. Successive values form a dependent Markov chain; convergence from arbitrary initial states additionally needs the appropriate irreducibility and aperiodicity, not just this invariant-density identity.
The Gibbs sampler updates a coordinate using its full conditional distribution while keeping the others fixed. For coordinate i, write r=x−i​ and π(x)=π−i​(r)πi​(xi​∣r). Its proposal replaces xi​ by xi′​∼πi​(⋅∣r). The MH ratio, interpreted on this one-coordinate proposal space, is
π−i​(r)πi​(xi​∣r)πi​(xi′​∣r)π−i​(r)πi​(xi′​∣r)πi​(xi​∣r)​=1.
(38)
Thus every Gibbs coordinate update is an MH update with acceptance one. A random choice of coordinate gives a mixture of these invariant kernels; a deterministic sweep gives their composition. Both preserve π. The latter composition need not be reversible, so the special-case statement is about the coordinate kernels, not a claim that every entire systematic sweep is one reversible MH kernel.

At σ=1, complete the square:
x2+y2−2ρxy=(x−ρy)2+(1−ρ2)y2.
(39)

X∣Y=y∼N(ρy,1−ρ2),Y∣X=x∼N(ρx,1−ρ2).​

These are the full conditional distributions of the standard bivariate normal distribution with correlation ρ.
A systematic Gibbs sampler starts at a chosen (x0​,y0​) and, at each sweep, draws independent standard normals Z1,n​,Z2,n​ and sets
xn+1​=ρyn​+1−ρ2​Z1,n​,yn+1​=ρxn+1​+1−ρ2​Z2,n​.

Use the random-walk proposal specified by its centre and covariance: for the current state z=(x,y) draw Z1​,Z2​ independently standard normal and set
x′=x+σq​Z1​,y′=y+σq​Z2​,σq​>0.
(43)
Its properly normalized density of the proposal distribution is
q(z′∣z)=2πσq2​1​exp(−2σq2​(x′−x)2+(y′−y)2​).
(44)
It is symmetric: q(z′∣z)=q(z∣z′). This is a centred-at-the-current-state proposal, rather than an unshifted draw from the density notation in the source. The Random-walk Metropolis algorithm consequently needs only the target ratio. Put H(x,y)=x2+y2−2ρxy. Then
α(z,z′)=min{1,exp(−2(1−ρ2)H(x′,y′)−H(x,y)​)}.​
(45)
An independent uniform decides the move; on rejection both coordinates remain at their previous values. A proposal with lower H is always accepted. This construction uses the joint density directly, without needing either conditional distribution. Its continuous positive proposal density supplies communication over the target support, and the holding probability avoids a deterministic periodic transition pattern.

Tune the proposal scale using pilot runs on a grid of candidate σq​ values. After allowing for initial transients, compare the effective sample size of a Markov chain per unit computation time for representative functions, such as both coordinates and a quadratic function. Equivalently inspect their integrated autocorrelation time, using ESS≈n/(1+2∑k≥1​Corr(h(Z0​),h(Zk​))). Choose a useful scale and keep it fixed for the production run. Another pilot criterion is the average squared accepted displacement, possibly in coordinates standardized by the target covariance, which balances move size and rejection.
Acceptance alone is not an efficiency criterion. As σq​↓0, proposals are almost the current state, so acceptance tends to one while exploration becomes arbitrarily slow. Very large scales propose mostly low-density states and produce long sequences of identical values. The proposal scale and random-walk Metropolis efficiency trade-off requires both movement and acceptance; neither maximizing nor merely reporting the acceptance probability guarantees a useful sample. With strong correlation, an isotropic proposal also struggles with the target's narrow direction; covariance-scaled proposals can improve this geometry beyond tuning one scalar variance.

The observed-data likelihood function integrates out the latent variable:
L(x∣θ)=∫f(x,z;θ)dz.
(46)
Choose an initial parameter θ(0) with positive likelihood. At iteration t, the E-step of the EM algorithm forms the entire expected complete-data log-likelihood
Q(θ∣θ(t))=∫ht​(z)logf(x,z;θ)dz,ht​(z)=L(x∣θ(t))f(x,z;θ(t))​.
(47)
The expectation is under the conditional missing-data law at the old parameter, while the argument θ remains free. The M-step then chooses
θ(t+1)∈θargmax​Q(θ∣θ(t)).​
(48)
The observed data x stay fixed throughout. This is not obtained merely by substituting the conditional mean of z into a nonlinear log-likelihood: the expectation of all required complete-data terms must be taken. A generalized EM step only requires Q(θ(t+1)∣θ(t))≥Q(θ(t)∣θ(t)), which suffices for the monotonicity proved next. Exact maximization assumes that an admissible maximizer exists.

EM likelihood monotonicity makes EM an ascent method, not a global-optimization theorem. If the log-likelihood is bounded above along the iterates, its nondecreasing values converge. That alone does not show that the parameters converge, nor that any limiting parameter is a global maximum-likelihood estimator. Under additional regularity, accumulation points can be stationary; local maxima, other stationary points or flat sets can depend on initialization. Multiple starting values and comparison of the achieved observed likelihoods are therefore useful.
The upper-bound hypothesis is substantive. In a finite mixture model with a freely shrinking normal-component variance, placing one component at an observed value can make the likelihood unbounded while other components cover the remaining observations. In that situation monotonicity provides no finite maximum to converge to. EM may also improve very slowly, and a small change in likelihood alone is not proof of an accurate global solution. The guarantee in part (b) is nondecrease, not strict increase at every iteration.

Both EM algorithm and data augmentation exploit a latent completion z, but they use it differently. EM takes a conditional expectation of the complete-data log-likelihood and then maximizes over the parameter, producing a deterministic sequence of point estimates when the M-step is uniquely specified.
In Bayesian data augmentation, choose a prior distribution p(θ) and target
p(θ,z∣x)∝f(x,z;θ)p(θ).
(53)
Alternate the Gibbs sampler updates
z(t+1)∼p(z∣x,θ(t)),θ(t+1)∼p(θ∣x,z(t+1)).
(54)
These are stochastic draws from full conditional distributions, not an expectation followed by maximization. Their invariant law is the augmented joint posterior, and discarding z gives posterior inference for θ, including uncertainty rather than only an MLE. A sampled completion followed by maximization would instead be a stochastic EM variant. Individual likelihood values along a data-augmentation chain need not increase: the chain is designed to sample a posterior, not to ascend the likelihood.
