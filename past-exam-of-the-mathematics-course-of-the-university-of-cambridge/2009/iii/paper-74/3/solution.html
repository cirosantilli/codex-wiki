Let the undisturbed water surface be z=0, the oil's upper surface z=η(x,t) and its lower surface z=b(x,t), so h=η−b. Leading hydrostatic pressure in the nearly inviscid water is pw​=−ρw​gz relative to atmospheric pressure; in the oil it is pH​=ρg(η−z). Equality at the interface gives ρgh=−ρw​gb, hence
b=−ρw​ρ​h,η=ρw​ρw​−ρ​h.
(23)
Thus the horizontal hydrostatic driving gradient is
pH,x​=ρgηx​=ρg′hx​,g′=ρw​ρw​−ρ​g.​
(24)
This reduced gravity accounts for most of the oil thickness being below the undisturbed water level.
Because the oil is much more viscous than the water and air, tangential tractions on both faces are negligible. In a thin layer, the leading horizontal velocity is consequently independent of depth: the floating extensional viscous gravity current is a plug, rather than the parabolic profile of a no-slip gravity current. Integrating incompressibility ux​+wz​=0 between the moving material boundaries, and using their kinematic boundary condition, gives conservation of oil volume,
ht​+(hu)x​=0,ht​+uhx​+hux​=0.​
(25)
To derive the factor 4 in the extensional balance, incompressibility gives wz​=−ux​. The Newtonian fluid stress tensor has σzz​=−p−2μux​. Normal-stress balance relative to the hydrostatic part therefore requires p=pH​−2μux​. The horizontal stress then satisfies
σxx​=−p+2μux​=−pH​+4μux​.
(26)
Thus the longitudinal tensile force in an oil slice, relative to its hydrostatic pressure, is 4μhux​ per unit width. Balancing its difference across the slice with the horizontal driving force −hpH,x​dx yields
4μ(hux​)x​=hpH,x​,h4μ​(hux​)x​=pH,x​.​
(27)
The coefficient is the planar extensional Trouton ratio, arising from both the direct horizontal strain and the pressure correction required by the transverse normal-stress condition.
Using the pressure gradient above, integration in x gives
4μhux​=21​ρg′h2+C(t).
(28)
The stated nose stress condition sets C(t)=0, not an arbitrary imposed inlet tension. Consequently
ux​=kh,k=8μρg′​.
(29)
For a material slice injected at time t0​, let τ=t−t0​ be its age. The thickness equation along its Lagrangian trajectory is dh/dt=−hux​=−kh2, with h=h0​ at injection. Define
T=kh0​1​=ρg′h0​8μ​.
(30)
Then h=h0​/(1+τ/T). The slice injected during dt0​ has volume h0​u0​dt0​ per unit width, so at fixed observation time t,
−h∂t0​∂x​=h0​u0​,∂τ∂x​=u0​(1+Tτ​).
(31)
Since a newly injected slice has x(t,t)=0, integration and subsequent differentiation along a material slice give the constant-flux floating extensional gravity current:
h(t;t0​)=1+(t−t0​)/Th0​​,u(t;t0​)=u0​(1+Tt−t0​​),x(t;t0​)=u0​[(t−t0​)+2T(t−t0​)2​].​
(32)
The oldest slice forms the nose. Measuring time from the start of injection, t0​=0 there, so
xN​(t)=u0​t+16μρg′h0​u0​​t2,hN​(t)=1+t/Th0​​.​
(33)
Its speed x˙N​=u0​(1+t/T) is exactly the material velocity at the nose, as required.
For a fixed position x behind the nose, the age needed to reach it is determined by x/u0​=τ+τ2/(2T), independently of the observation time. Hence both thickness and velocity become stationary there once the nose has passed. Eliminating τ gives
h(x)=h0​(1+4μu0​ρg′h0​x​)−1/2,u(x)=u0​(1+4μu0​ρg′h0​x​)1/2.​
(34)
Their product is h0​u0​, so the steady profile carries precisely the inlet volume flux; integrating it to xN​(t) gives the total injected volume h0​u0​t.
The length
ℓ=ρg′h0​μu0​​​
(35)
is the horizontal distance over which reduced-gravity driving causes an order-one change in inlet speed and thickness by viscous extension. The associated age is T=8ℓ/u0​; the exact profile contains x/(4ℓ). It is an extensional adjustment length, not a shear-diffusion scale. The result applies while the stated thin-layer and negligible-inertia approximations remain valid.
