First use the algebraically closed field convention. Every line bundle on a smooth projective curve has a nonzero rational section of a line bundle: trivialize it on a nonempty open set and take its generic generator. The orders of its zeros and poles give a finite divisor D, and the local trivializations identify the line bundle with O(D). For a degree-zero line bundle write
D=∑p​np​p,∑p​np​=0,
(12)
since every closed point is a k-point. Therefore
D=∑p​np​(p−p0​),O(D)≅⨂p​O(p−p0​)⊗np​.
(13)
Only finitely many factors occur, and negative tensor powers mean dual line bundles. This proves
Pic0(X)=⟨α(X(k))⟩.​
(14)
For genus zero the group is trivial, so the conclusion remains valid.
For a general field, a closed point need not be rational, and its contribution to degree is weighted by its residue degree. Consequently rational points need not generate the degree-zero Picard group of a curve. Here is an explicit counterexample to that stronger reading. Let C be the smooth projective curve over F2​ obtained by completing the affine equation
y2+y=x5+x+1.
(15)
The polynomial is irreducible over F2​(x): a function r2+r has even pole order at infinity when it has a pole there, while the right side has pole order five. Its affine model is smooth because the partial derivative with respect to y is one. The normalized projective completion is therefore a geometrically integral smooth projective curve.
There is exactly one point p∞​ above infinity, and it is rational. Indeed the map x:C→P1 has degree two. At a point above infinity put v(x)=−e, where the ramification index e is at most two. The equation forces v(y)<0 and 2v(y)=5v(x)=−5e. Hence e=2 and v(y)=−5. The degree formula then leaves exactly one point of residue degree one. Thus x and y have pole orders two and five there. There are no affine rational points: for either x∈F2​, the right side is one, while y2+y=0 for every y∈F2​. Hence C(F2​)={p∞​} and the image of its Abel map of a pointed smooth projective curve is trivial.
The equations y=0, x2+x+1=0 define a degree-two closed point D, since x5+x+1 vanishes modulo x2+x+1. The divisor D−2p∞​ has degree zero but is not principal. To prove this, a function with poles bounded by 2p∞​ is regular on the affine curve and hence has a unique expression A(x)+B(x)y. The possible pole orders of its two terms are 2degA and 2degB+5, of opposite parity, so they cannot cancel. The pole bound forces B=0 and degA≤1. But no nonzero a+bx over F2​ vanishes at D, whose residue class of x has irreducible quadratic minimal polynomial. Thus no function has principal divisor D−2p∞​, proving its class is nonzero. This establishes the rational points need not generate the degree-zero Picard group qualification with a complete obstruction, while the generation proof above supplies the intended algebraically closed-field answer.
