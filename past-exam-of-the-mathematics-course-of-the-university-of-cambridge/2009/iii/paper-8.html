A bounded operator T:H→H is a Fredholm operator when its range is closed and both kerT and ran(T)⊥=kerT∗ have finite dimension. Its Fredholm index is
indT=dimkerT−dimkerT∗.
(1)
For index zero, choose an isomorphism J:kerT→kerT∗ and extend it by zero on (kerT)⊥. The restricted map T:(kerT)⊥→ranT is a bounded isomorphism, by the bounded inverse theorem. The finite-rank operator J fills exactly the missing kernel-to-cokernel block. Thus T+J is invertible and T=(T+J)−J is an invertible operator plus a compact operator.
Conversely suppose T=A+K with A invertible and K compact. The Atkinson theorem proved in part (b) makes every A+tK, 0≤t≤1, Fredholm: its quotient image is always [A]. The block argument in part (c) proves local constancy of the Fredholm index, so its index is constant along this interval and equals indA=0. This invokes the actual parametrix and block proofs below, not an unproved perturbation assertion. The claimed equivalence holds.

For a Fredholm operator T, define B to invert T:(kerT)⊥→ranT and to vanish on the range complement. The bounded inverse theorem makes B bounded, and
BT=I−PkerT​,TB=I−PkerT∗​.
(2)
The errors are finite rank, so T is invertible in the Calkin algebra.
Conversely suppose BT=I−K and TB=I−L for bounded B and compact K,L. On kerT, K is the identity. An infinite-dimensional closed kernel would contain an orthonormal sequence, whose images under K have no convergent subsequence, contradicting compactness. Thus the kernel is finite-dimensional.
There is a positive lower bound for T on (kerT)⊥. Otherwise choose unit vectors xj​ there with Txj​→0. Since xj​=BTxj​+Kxj​, compactness gives a norm-convergent subsequence with unit limit x. The limit lies in the kernel complement and has Tx=0, a contradiction. This lower bound proves closed range: if Txj​ converges, remove each kernel component; the remaining xj​ form a Cauchy sequence and converge to a preimage of the limit.
Finally adjointing TB=I−L gives B∗T∗=I−L∗. The same kernel argument makes kerT∗ finite-dimensional. Therefore T is Fredholm. T is Fredholm⟺[T] is invertible modulo compacts​, the Atkinson theorem. The finite-dimensional case also satisfies the parametrix formulation directly, though its quotient algebra is the zero algebra.

T+R=(AC​BD​),
(3)
where A:X→Y remains invertible by the Neumann series. Invertible triangular row and column operations reduce this matrix to
(A0​0D−CA−1B​).
(4)
The second block maps the finite-dimensional space N into M. Hence the perturbed operator has closed range, finite kernel and cokernel, and
ind(T+R)=dimN−dimM=indT,
(5)
since the rank of that finite block cancels from the difference. Thus the Fredholm set is open and its index is locally constant, hence norm continuous.

0→kerT→kerSTT​kerSq​cokerTS​cokerST→cokerS→0.

Here q is reduction modulo ranT, the induced S-map sends [x] to [Sx], and the last map is the quotient by the larger range ranS. These maps are well-defined. The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators says that a compact self-adjoint A has an orthogonal decomposition
H=kerA⊕⨁​λ=0​ker(A−λI),
(10)
with real nonzero eigenvalues of finite multiplicity. Only finitely many eigenvalues, counting multiplicity, have absolute value at least any given ε>0. Thus the nonzero eigenvalues form a finite list or a sequence tending to zero, and A=∑λ=0​λPλ​ in operator norm. The kernel may have arbitrary Hilbert dimension.
For existence of an extremal eigenvector, self-adjointness gives ∥A∥=sup∥x∥=1​∣⟨Ax,x⟩∣. One elementary proof of the nontrivial inequality uses polarization: if the quadratic form is bounded in absolute value by m∥x∥2, its polarized real part is bounded by m on two unit vectors; adjusting a phase bounds ∣⟨Ax,y⟩∣ by m. Choose unit xj​ with quadratic values tending to λ=∥A∥ or −∥A∥. Then
∥(A−λ)xj​∥2≤2∥A∥2−2λ⟨Axj​,xj​⟩⟶0.
(11)
If A=0, compactness supplies a convergent subsequence of Axj​, and the last estimate supplies a convergent unit subsequence of xj​, with eigenvalue λ.
Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal, since A=A∗. An infinite orthonormal sequence in one nonzero eigenspace would violate compactness. More generally an infinite orthonormal family with eigenvalues bounded away from zero has images separated in norm, again impossible. Take all nonzero eigenspaces and let M be their closed span. Its complement reduces A; if the restriction there were nonzero, the extremal-eigenvector argument would produce another nonzero eigenvector, a contradiction. Thus M⊥=kerA. The finite truncations of the eigenvalue sum have remainder norm tending to zero, proving the asserted norm expansion.

The Von Neumann double commutant theorem states that for a unital star-subalgebra A⊆B(H),
ASOT=AWOT=A′′.​
(13)
The commutant of an operator algebra consists of operators commuting with every element. Each commutation equation is closed in the weak operator topology, so A′′ is weakly closed and contains both indicated closures.
Let T∈A′′ and fix ξ1​,…,ξm​∈H. On Hm let A act diagonally and put ξ=(ξ1​,…,ξm​). The subspace K={(aξ1​,…,aξm​):a∈A}​ reduces the diagonal star-algebra. Its orthogonal projection P therefore commutes with every diagonal a. Writing P as an m×m operator matrix shows that each entry belongs to A′. Since T∈A′′, the diagonal T commutes with P. Unitality gives ξ∈K, so Pξ=ξ and P(Tξ)=Tξ. Thus Tξ∈K. By definition of K, for any ε>0 a single a∈A satisfies ∑j​∥(T−a)ξj​∥2<ε2. These are precisely the finite-vector neighbourhoods of the strong operator topology. Hence T belongs to the strong closure, completing the proof. Unitality, or the corresponding nondegeneracy hypothesis, is essential to the theorem's stated form.

Pass to B=A∥⋅∥. Closed invariant subspaces and intertwining maps are unchanged by this closure, and BH is still dense. Because B is a star-algebra, every closed invariant subspace reduces it.
Every nonzero nondegenerate compact-operator representation contains an irreducible reducing subspace. Indeed choose a nonzero positive compact element b; an isolated nonzero eigenvalue gives a nonzero finite-rank spectral projection e∈B by continuous functional calculus, using a function vanishing at zero. The finite-dimensional corner eBe has a minimal nonzero projection p, with pBp=Cp. Choose a unit vector v∈pH and set K=Bv. Then pK=Cv, since pbv=pbpv is scalar times v. If Q is the projection onto a reducing subspace of K, it commutes with B, and Qv=pQv∈Cv. Its projection property makes Qv either zero or v. Cyclicity then makes Q=0 or IK​. Thus K is irreducible.
Take a maximal orthogonal family of such irreducible reducing subspaces, by the axiom of choice. If their orthogonal complement were nonzero, its restricted representation would be nondegenerate: a vector annihilated by all restricted B would, by taking adjoints, be orthogonal to BH and hence zero. The image of B on this complement is a norm-closed C-star algebra of compact operators, so the preceding construction gives another irreducible summand, contradicting maximality. The family therefore exhausts H.
Finally, suppose one irreducible equivalence class occurred infinitely often. Choose some b∈B acting nontrivially on that class, and a unit vector v with ∥bv∥>0. Transport v by unitary intertwiners into distinct equivalent summands. These vectors are orthonormal; their images under b are orthogonal and all have the same nonzero norm. That contradicts compactness of b. The decomposition has finite multiplicity for every irreducible type, as claimed in nondegenerate representations by compact operators.

The finite-dimensional Krein-Milman theorem says that a nonempty compact convex set K is the convex hull of its extreme points. A point is extreme if a proper convex combination of two points of K equals it only when both points equal it.
Prove the theorem by induction on the dimension of the affine hull. Dimension zero is immediate. In positive dimension work inside that affine hull, where K has interior. Every boundary point x lies on a supporting hyperplane. To see this, choose exterior points yj​→x and nearest points pj​∈K. Convexity and the minimum-distance condition give (yj​−pj​)⋅(z−pj​)≤0 for z∈K. A subsequence of the normalized nonzero vectors yj​−pj​ converges to a unit vector v, while pj​→x, giving v⋅(z−x)≤0. The resulting face F={z∈K:v⋅(z−x)=0} has smaller affine dimension. Its extreme points are extreme in K, since equality in the supporting inequality forces both endpoints of any decomposition to lie in F. Induction expresses x as a convex combination of extreme points of K.
Every interior point is a convex combination of the two endpoints of a line segment through it maximal in K; compactness gives the endpoints, and both are boundary points. Combining their convex decompositions completes the proof. In the generalization requested, a nonempty weak-star compact convex subset K of the dual unit ball of a separable Banach space satisfies
K=conv(extK)​w∗.​
(20)
The closure is now essential; separability makes this compact weak-star setting metrizable but the general Krein-Milman theorem holds more broadly in locally convex spaces.

For a bounded self-adjoint T, nonnegative spectrum is equivalent to ⟨Tx,x⟩≥0. Here is a useful elementary justification of the nontrivial direction. Let a=inf∥x∥=1​⟨Tx,x⟩ and S=T−aI, so S has nonnegative quadratic form. Cauchy-Schwarz for that positive form gives ∥Sx∥2≤∥S∥⟨Sx,x⟩. Taking unit vectors approaching the infimum makes Sxj​→0, hence a∈σ(T). Thus nonnegative spectrum forces a≥0. The reverse direction follows from the lower bound and adjoint argument for T−λ when λ<0, together with reality of the self-adjoint spectrum.
If T=0 its positive square root is zero. Otherwise set M=∥T∥ and C=I−T/M, so 0≤C≤I and ∥C∥≤1. Write the scalar binomial series
1−z​=1−∑n≥1​an​zn,an​>0,∑n≥1​an​=1.
(21)
The last equality follows by taking z↑1 in this positive-coefficient series. Consequently
B=M​(I−∑n≥1​an​Cn)
(22)
converges absolutely in operator norm. Each partial sum is positive, because 0≤Cn≤I and the sum of its coefficients is at most one. The limit is positive and self-adjoint. Multiplying the absolutely convergent series gives B2=M(I−C)=T.
For uniqueness let R be another positive square root. It commutes with T=R2, hence with the polynomial-norm-limit B. Thus (R−B)(R+B)=0. The kernel of R+B is kerR∩kerB: zero quadratic form for a positive operator implies it annihilates the vector, by positive-form Cauchy-Schwarz. The difference R−B vanishes there and on ran(R+B); the latter is dense in the kernel complement because R+B is self-adjoint. Therefore R=B. The positive square root of an operator exists and is unique.

Let X be the character space of an algebra, consisting of nonzero multiplicative complex linear functionals on A. We construct the required Gelfand transform and prove its properties.
Characters exist. A maximal proper ideal exists by the axiom of choice and is closed: if it were dense it would contain an element within distance one of the identity, hence an invertible element, contradicting properness. The quotient is a complex unital Banach division algebra, and the Gelfand-Mazur theorem identifies it with C, producing a character. The same argument applied to the proper ideal generated by a−λ proves
σA​(a)={χ(a):χ∈X}.
(23)
Every character has norm one, since its value belongs to the spectrum and is bounded by ∥a∥, while χ(1)=1. The character equations are weak-star closed in the dual unit ball. Thus Banach-Alaoglu theorem makes X compact Hausdorff; separability of A makes it metrizable.
The algebra is inverse-closed in B(H). If a∈A is invertible there, a∗a≥cI for some c>0. For M=∥a∗a∥, the Neumann series for (a∗a)−1=M−1[I−(I−a∗a/M)]−1 converges in A, since the positive contraction in brackets has norm at most 1−c/M<1. Therefore a−1=(a∗a)−1a∗∈A. This proves spectral permanence for C-star algebras in the present concrete setting. In particular a self-adjoint element has real spectrum in A, so characters take real values on it. Decomposing a into self-adjoint real and imaginary parts gives χ(a∗)=χ(a)​.
Define a(χ)=χ(a). This is a unital star-homomorphism to C(X). Every element of the abelian star-algebra is normal, and the C-star identity gives ∥a2j∥=∥a∥2j. The spectral radius formula therefore gives rA​(a)=∥a∥. The displayed spectrum identity now yields ∥a∥∞​=∥a∥, proving isometry and injectivity. Its image is closed because A is complete, contains the constants, is closed under conjugation, and separates distinct characters. The Stone-Weierstrass theorem makes that image dense in C(X), hence equal to it. A≅C(X)​ isometrically as a star-algebra.

The Weyl-von Neumann theorem states that on a separable Hilbert space a self-adjoint operator is a diagonal self-adjoint operator plus a compact self-adjoint operator. For a bounded operator T, the perturbation can in fact be chosen Hilbert-Schmidt with arbitrarily small Hilbert-Schmidt norm. The separable-space formulation is also recorded in Kadison's Corollary B.
Here is a constructive proof. The bounded self-adjoint spectral theorem decomposes a separable H into countably many cyclic reducing spaces, on each of which T is multiplication by x in L2(μ) for a finite measure supported in a bounded real interval. First work on one such space. Partition the interval into N intervals of length at most δ, and repeatedly bisect them. Let Ej​ be the finite-dimensional space of functions constant on the level-j intervals. These spaces are nested and their union is dense, since dyadic step functions approximate continuous functions and continuous functions are dense in L2(μ).
Choose normalized interval indicators as a basis of E0​, and choose orthonormal bases of Ej​⊖Ej−1​ within each parent interval. This is possible because the refinement splits into orthogonal parent blocks. Each new basis vector at level j≥1 is supported in an interval of length at most δ2−(j−1), and there are at most N2j−1 such vectors. For every basis vector e choose a real λe​ in its supporting interval and define the diagonal operator De=λe​e. Then
∥(T−D)e∥≤length of its supporting interval,
(25)
so
∥T−D∥HS2​=∑e​∥(T−D)e∥2≤Nδ2+∑j≥1​N2j−1δ22−2(j−1)=3Nδ2.
(26)
One may take N=O(δ−1), so this bound tends to zero as δ→0. The error is Hilbert-Schmidt, hence compact; it is self-adjoint because both T and D are.
On the countably many cyclic summands choose error bounds εj​ with ∑j​εj2​<ε2. Combining their orthonormal bases and diagonal operators gives T=D+K with ∥K∥HS​<ε, proving the assertion. For an unbounded self-adjoint operator, first split by spectral intervals [j,j+1), apply the bounded construction on each, and choose summable error bounds. The resulting total K is bounded compact, and D=T−K has the same domain as T; the diagonal domain condition follows from the boundedness of K.
Separability matters. Take an uncountable orthogonal sum of copies of multiplication by x on L2[0,1]. The range closure of a compact operator is separable, and a separable subspace of this direct sum is supported in only countably many summands: choose a countable dense set and unite its countable supports. A self-adjoint compact error therefore vanishes on every remaining summand, each of which reduces the proposed diagonal operator D=T−K. The restriction of a diagonal self-adjoint operator to a reducing subspace has pure point spectrum, whereas multiplication by x on L2[0,1] has no eigenvectors. This is a contradiction, qualifying the theorem if the unspoken separability convention is dropped.

(27)
The equality follows from the Atkinson theorem. The quotient is a unital complex Banach algebra when H is infinite-dimensional. Its invertible set is open by the Neumann series, so the spectrum is closed, and it lies in the disk ∣λ∣≤∥[T]∥.
For nonemptiness, suppose the quotient resolvent R(λ)=(λ1−[T])−1 existed for every complex λ. It is analytic everywhere, and outside a sufficiently large disk its Neumann expansion gives ∥R(λ)∥≤1/(∣λ∣−∥[T]∥). Continuity bounds it on the remaining compact disk. Every continuous linear functional applied to R is therefore a bounded entire scalar function, constant by Liouville's theorem and zero because it tends to zero at infinity. The Hahn-Banach theorem, or separation by the dual, forces R(λ)=0, contradicting its inverse identity. The essential spectrum is nonempty and closed.
Infinite dimension is necessary: every operator on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space is Fredholm, so its essential spectrum in this convention is empty. The proof works on nonseparable infinite-dimensional spaces as well; it needs neither self-adjointness nor a sequence basis.

Interpret limit points as subsequential limits, including a value repeated infinitely often. Let L be the common nonempty compact cluster set. Boundedness implies dist(an​,L)→0 and likewise for bn​: otherwise a subsequence staying a fixed distance away would have a convergent further subsequence whose limit both belongs to L and stays away from it.
Construct a matching of indices in alternating steps. At step 2j−1 choose the least unused index m in the first sequence. Choose a nearest point ℓ∈L to am​ and an unused index n in the second sequence with ∣bn​−ℓ∣<1/j. Such indices exist infinitely often because ℓ is a subsequential limit. Pair m with n; their values differ by at most dist(am​,L)+1/j. At step 2j choose the least unused second-sequence index and match it to an unused first-sequence index within 1/j of a nearest point of L. This gives the analogous bound with bn​.
Every index is used exactly once: no step reuses an index, and by step 2j at least the first j indices in each sequence have been used. Hence the produced lists are permutations satisfying
k∈{m1​,…,m2k​}∩{n1​,…,n2k​}.​
(28)
The indices actively chosen as least unused tend to infinity on their respective odd and even steps. Their distances to L therefore tend to zero, and 1/j→0. The two matching estimates prove ∣amk​​−bnk​​∣→0​. The cluster-set convention is important here; accumulation points of the set of distinct values would ignore infinite repetitions.

Work with bounded self-adjoint operators on separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, the setting in which the classification holds. If UTU∗−S is compact for a unitary U, their images in the Calkin algebra have identical spectra, by compact perturbation invariance and unitary conjugation. Thus their essential spectra coincide.
Conversely apply the Weyl-von Neumann theorem to write T=Da​+Ka​ and S=Db​+Kb​ in orthonormal bases, with bounded real diagonal sequences and compact errors. The essential spectrum of a diagonal operator is exactly its sequence cluster set. Indeed outside that cluster set all but finitely many entries stay a positive distance from λ, so the diagonal has an inverse on a finite-codimensional subspace and is Fredholm. At a cluster point, infinitely many distinct basis vectors have (D−λ)ej​→0; these are weakly null unit vectors, contradicting any Fredholm parametrix. This also handles a value with infinite multiplicity.
The two diagonal sequences therefore have the same cluster set. Part (c) provides permutations with amk​​−bnk​​→0. Send the mk​th basis vector for T to the nk​th basis vector for S. Under this unitary, the diagonal difference has entries tending to zero and is compact, by finite-rank truncation. Adding the two conjugated compact errors gives UTU∗−S compact​.
Without separability, equality of essential spectra need not suffice, even on the same Hilbert space. Let H have uncountable dimension and let T,S be projections with infinite-dimensional kernels, but with range dimensions respectively countable and uncountable. Both essential spectra are {0,1}. After any unitary conjugation the range of T is still separable. If its difference from S were compact, the range of S would lie in the sum of that separable range and the separable closure of the compact error's range, which is impossible. Finite-dimensional spaces also need a separate dimension convention, since essential spectra are then empty. These are genuine qualifications to a statement made without the standard separable infinite-dimensional setting.

The irreducible finite-dimensional unitary representations of SU(2) are En​=Symn(C2), one for each n≥0, with dimension n+1. Equivalently they are spin-j representations with j=n/2. (30)

The kernel and cokernel of an equivariant Fredholm operator are finite-dimensional representations. Define its equivariant Fredholm index in the representation ring of a compact group by
indG​S=[kerS]−[cokerS].​

Use the round unit sphere with its complex orientation and unique spin structure. Its chiral spinor bundles are S+=O(−1) and S−=Λ0,1⊗O(−1), the latter a smooth line bundle of degree +1. The Dirac operator is
D=c∘∇S=(0D+​D−0​),D+=2​∂O(−1)​,D−=(D+)∗.
(33)
Here Clifford multiplication has c(v)2=−∣v∣2, and D acts self-adjointly on the L2 spinors with first-order Sobolev domain. The geometric spin connection is invariant under the lifted SU(2) action, hence D is equivariant.
We justify the spectral properties needed for its phase. The Peter-Weyl theorem and Frobenius reciprocity for compact groups give the decomposition of sections of an isotropy-weight-q line bundle: En​ occurs once exactly when n≥∣q∣ and n≡q(mod2), because its torus weights are n,n−2,…,−n. The spinor isotropy weights are +1,−1, so each chirality contains E1​,E3​,E5​,…, each once. The horizontal connection Laplacian is the total Casimir minus the vertical generator square, ∇∗∇=C−1/4 on these weight-one bundles. Expanding the Dirac square in a normal orthonormal frame gives the diagonal connection-Laplacian terms and the off-diagonal curvature contraction. The Clifford relations contract spin curvature to Scal/4, the Lichnerowicz formula for a twisted Dirac operator with no twisting bundle. Since the scalar curvature here is two,
D2=∇∗∇+21​=C+41​,D2∣En​​=4(n+1)2​I.
(34)
Thus there is no kernel, the eigenvalues of D are ±1,±2,… with finite multiplicities, and the resolvent is compact by spectral truncation. These round-sphere spectral facts agree with the explicit calculation in Abrikosov's paper.
Define the chiral phase of the round-sphere Dirac operator
V=D+(D−D+)−1/2:H+​⟶H−​,H±​=L2(S±).​
(35)
It is unitary because both chiral kernels vanish; equivariance follows from that of D. The full phase is F=D∣D∣−1=(0V​V∗0​).

F=π2​∫0∞​D(D2+t2)−1dt.

supx​∥u(x)−I−P(u(x)−I)P∥<ε.

The straight segment from u to g stays invertible; taking its unitary polar factors supplies a homotopy in UK​, since inverse square root depends continuously on the positive invertible factors and preserves identity modulo compacts.
