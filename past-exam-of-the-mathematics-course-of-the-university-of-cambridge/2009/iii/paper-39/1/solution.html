Dividing by p, with its corresponding sign, proves E[U(yT​)]≤E[U(yT∗​)] in both cases. Equality is achieved by the constant policy.
For an ordinary terminal-wealth agent with relative risk aversion q, the same Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation calculation, with no fee penalty, maximizes bTπ−qπTAπ/2. It therefore gives exactly the same optimal investment portfolio. For q=1, the preceding stochastic exponential and Jensen inequality verification applies with penalty zero and exponent 1−q, proving global optimality for this agent too. The spot volatility penalty adds ε to effective relative risk aversion. If q=1, the printed expression for U0​ has a zero denominator; its appropriate limit, after subtracting an irrelevant constant, is U0​(x)=logx, which still gives π∗=A−1b. In this logarithmic case, the terminal log-wealth difference from the candidate equals logZT​ for a positive stochastic exponential Z, so E[logZT​]≤logE[ZT​]≤0 proves its optimality.
If A is singular, the same conclusion holds in the absence of arbitrage, provided b lies in the image of a linear map x↦Ax: replace A−1 by the Moore-Penrose inverse, and add any vector in the kernel of A to π∗. These additions change neither returns nor Brownian portfolio exposures. If b has a nonzero component in that kernel, a zero-volatility position has a nonzero excess return. Scaling its profitable sign gives arbitrage, so a finite attained optimum as claimed requires this degeneracy to be excluded.
