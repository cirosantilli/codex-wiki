Take M˙>0 to mean inward accretion rate, and let S count loss through both faces per unit projected area of the accretion disk. An annulus of width dR loses 2πRSdR to the wind. In steady state, its incoming mass flux at its outer edge equals the inward flux at its inner edge plus this loss. Hence mass conservation gives
dRdM˙​=2πRS.​
(1)
If a loss rate is instead defined per individual face, it must be doubled before substituting for S here.
Use Keplerian rotation Ω2=GM/R3. The vertically integrated viscous dissipation, summed over both disk faces, is
Q+=νΣ(RdRdΩ​)2=49​νΣΩ2.
(2)
Here ν is the kinematic viscosity, and Σ is the surface density of a disk; for height-dependent viscosity the product means ∫ρνdz. Material already in a circular orbit has specific orbital energy vK2​/2−GM/R=−GM/(2R). Giving it zero total binding energy therefore costs an additional GM/(2R) per unit mass. The total launch speed is the escape velocity 2GM/R​, but one must not charge the wind for the orbital kinetic energy it already possesses. Equating wind power to fQ+ gives
f49​νΣR3GM​=S2RGM​.
(3)
Combining this with mass conservation yields the wind-powered mass loss from a steady accretion disk relation
νΣ=9f2SR2​=9πfR​dRdM˙​.​
(4)
Define the outward viscous torque in an accretion disk as
G=−2πR3νΣdRdΩ​=3πνΣh,h=R2Ω=GMR​.
(5)
The net inward angular momentum flux is M˙h−G. Because each unit mass lost to the wind carries the local specific angular momentum h, annular conservation of angular momentum reads
dRd​(M˙h−G)=hdRdM˙​,G′=M˙h′=2RM˙h​.
(6)
In the wind region, G=RhM˙′/(3f). For constant f, differentiation and cancellation of h gives
RM˙′′+23​M˙′=2R3f​M˙.
(7)
Thus the requested Cauchy-Euler differential equation is
R1/2dRd​(R3/2dRdM˙​)=23​fM˙.​
(8)
For f=1/3, a power-law trial M˙∝Rp gives p2+p/2−1/2=0, with roots p=1/2,−1. Therefore M˙=AR​+B/R in R∗​≤R≤R0​. A zero inner viscous torque in an accretion disk implies M˙′(R∗​)=0, so B=AR∗3/2​/2. There is no point mass sink at R0​, so the accretion rate is continuous there and equals M˙0​. The complete result is
M˙(R)=⎩⎨⎧​M˙0​R0​​+R∗3/2​/(2R0​)R​+R∗3/2​/(2R)​,M˙0​,​R∗​≤R≤R0​,R≥R0​.​​
(9)
Its derivative is nonnegative throughout the wind region, as required for a nonnegative loss rate S. The derivative can jump to zero outside R0​: the energy relation applies only inside the wind zone and does not impose M˙′(R0​)=0 on its inner limit. The viscous torque in an accretion disk remains continuous and extends outside via G′=M˙0​h′.
At the inner boundary the exact accreted fraction is
M˙0​M˙(R∗​)​=1+21​(R∗​/R0​)3/223​R∗​/R0​​​≃23​(R0​R∗​​)1/2(R∗​≪R0​).​
(10)
The last expression is a leading-order asymptotic equality, not the exact finite-radius fraction. Most of the supplied mass is expelled before reaching the star when R∗​/R0​ is small.

A passive disk is heated predominantly by intercepted stellar radiation, rather than by its own viscous dissipation. A rough luminosity criterion, ignoring geometric factors, is
Lacc​∼R∗​GMM˙​≪L∗​.​
(11)
The intrinsic accretion disk luminosity is of order GMM˙/(2R∗​), with additional accretion energy potentially released at the stellar surface. The local test is more precise: absorbed irradiation must dominate the viscous flux at the radius being considered. For an ordinary steady Keplerian accretion disk, sufficiently far outside its inner edge,
Fvis​≃8πR33GMM˙​,Firr​≃4πR2L∗​α​,Firr​Fvis​​≃2L∗​Rα3GMM˙​≪1,
(12)
where α is the irradiation grazing angle of a disk. Reflection, very small incidence angles and shadowing can invalidate a simple total-luminosity comparison. The following calculation assumes an illuminated, absorbing surface.
At a point (R,zs​) on the upper surface, the outward unit normal in the meridional plane is proportional to (−zs′​,1), and a light ray from the point source is directed along (R,zs​). Its flux through the upper face is the incident inverse-square flux times minus their scalar product:
Fd​=4π(R2+zs2​)L∗​​R2+zs2​​1+(zs′​)2​Rzs′​−zs​​.
(13)
For a thin disk with small surface slope this becomes
Fd​≃4πR2L∗​​(zs′​−Rzs​​)=4πRL∗​​dRd​(Rzs​​).​
(14)
The tangent slope and ray slope differ by α≃zs′​−zs​/R. Positive incidence requires disk flaring, d(zs​/R)/dR>0, and an unobstructed ray from the star. A negative value of the formula would mean that direct illumination of this face is absent, not that it receives a negative heating flux. The hierarchy zs​≫R∗​ makes the finite angular size of the star negligible here.
Let a=R/μ, so the ideal gas equation of state gives the isothermal sound speed cs2​=aTc​. In the stellar vertical gravitational potential, hydrostatic equilibrium has vertical acceleration −ΩK2​z, where ΩK2​=GM/R3. Integration of cs2​∂z​ρ=−ρΩK2​z gives a Gaussian atmosphere with disk scale height
H2=GMaTc​R3​.
(15)
Consequently q=zs​/R=fH/R satisfies Tc​=GMq2/(af2R). Substitute this into the stipulated Stefan–Boltzmann law Fd​=σTc4​ and the irradiation flux:
dRdq​=CR3q8​,C=L∗​f84πσ​(aGM​)4.​
(16)
Here f specifies surface height in units of disk scale height and is unrelated to the wind-energy fraction in the preceding solution. This separable differential equation integrates exactly to
q−7=2R27C​+B=R2R02​​+B,R02​=L∗​f814πσ​(R/μGM​)4.​
(17)
If the thin-disc solution is formally matched to q(RM​)=1, then B=1−R02​/RM2​, giving
q(R)=[1+R02​(R21​−RM2​1​)]−1/7=(R0​R​)2/7[1+R02​R2​−RM2​R2​]−1/7.
(18)
It follows that the flaring passive disk asymptotic is
Rzs​​≃(R0​R​)2/7,H∝R9/7,Tc​∝R−3/7.​
(19)
This applies in the thin intermediate region R≪min(R0​,RM​), in addition to the point-source and stellar-gravity assumptions. The corrections are of relative order R2/R02​+R2/RM2​. In particular, the printed hierarchy R≪RM​ must still be accompanied by the initial thin-disc condition q≪1: if RM​≫R0​, radii larger than R0​ cannot satisfy that approximation. The matching at q=1 is an extrapolation used to set an integration constant; the derivation is not a valid treatment of a thick surface at RM​ itself.
Identify the stellar surface temperature with its effective radiating temperature, so that luminosity of a spherical blackbody gives L∗​=4πR∗2​σT∗4​. The given stellar surface scale height has H∗2​=aT∗​R∗3​/(GM), hence GM/(aT∗​R∗​)=(R∗​/H∗​)2. Substituting into R0​ yields
R∗​R0​​=27​​(fH∗​R∗​​)4≫1.​
(20)
Thus a small stellar surface scale height leaves a wide interval above R∗​ and below R0​ in which the thin disk flaring law can apply. The point-source approximation imposes the stronger lower cutoff obtained from zs​≫R∗​:
R≫R∗7/9​R02/9​.
(21)
This cutoff is still much smaller than R0​ when R0​≫R∗​. The full interval is therefore R∗7/9​R02/9​≪R≪min(R0​,RM​), whenever these bounds overlap. An assumed thickening radius must lie above the lower cutoff for all of the original approximations to hold simultaneously. The stellar luminosity-temperature relation is essential to the final comparison; independent arbitrary values of L∗​ and T∗​ would not imply it.

In a thin disk dominated by a central stellar mass, the vertical gravitational potential is harmonic to leading order, so its vertical acceleration is −Ω02​z. For a locally isothermal background, p=cs2​ρ with cs​ independent of height. Vertical hydrostatic equilibrium therefore gives
cs2​dzdρ​=−ρΩ02​z,ρ(R,z)=ρ(R,0)exp(−2H2z2​),H=Ω0​cs​​.​
(22)
This is the isothermal vertical structure in stellar gravity. On the upper half of the disk the downward gravity magnitude is g=Ω02​z>0; the density profile extends evenly through the midplane.
For an adiabatically displaced parcel kept in pressure equilibrium, the parcel's logarithmic density gradient is γ−1dlogp/dz, whereas the environmental density gradient is dlogρ/dz. A small upward displacement η consequently produces a density contrast δρ/ρ=[γ−1dlogp/dz−dlogρ/dz]η. Its buoyancy acceleration is −gδρ/ρ=−N2η. Thus the Brunt–Väisälä frequency is
N2=g(γ1​dzdlogp​−dzdlogρ​).
(23)
Both background gradients equal −z/H2 in the isothermal atmosphere, so
N2=(1−γ1​)H2z2​Ω02​.​
(24)
An isothermal background is therefore stably stratified for adiabatic perturbations with γ>1, but not buoyant for isothermal perturbations. In applying the adiabatic equation of state to a stratified atmosphere, its proportionality relates Lagrangian parcel changes: Δp=(γp/ρ)Δρ. Eulerian perturbations generally obey p′+ηpz​=(γp/ρ)(ρ′+ηρz​) instead. Thus the printed simple proportionality must be interpreted as a parcel relation for the buoyancy calculation. For γ=1 in this isothermal background, the gradient terms cancel and the Eulerian proportionality holds too.
Now take isothermal perturbations, so γ=1 and N2=0. The two supplied wave equations reduce to
ξz​=ω2uz​,(uz​)z​−H2z​uz​+(cs2​1​−ω2−Ω02​k2​)ξ=0.
(25)
For the nonsingular oscillatory case ω=0 and ω2=Ω02​, substitute uz​=ξz​/ω2. With x=z/H, the resulting Hermite differential equation is
ξxx​−xξx​+αξ=0,α=Ω02​ω2​(1−ω2−Ω02​k2cs2​​).​
(26)
The exceptional frequencies must be treated through the original velocity-pressure equations rather than division by these factors.
The physical vertical condition is finite perturbation energy when integrated with the Gaussian density. In particular the pressure contribution is proportional to ∫e−x2/2∣ξ∣2dx for nonzero frequency. Generic nonterminating solutions have an exponentially growing component at at least one vertical end and fail this condition; acceptable modes are the terminating Hermite polynomials. This is a finite-energy condition, not pointwise boundedness of the velocity in the vanishing-density atmosphere.
To prove the allowed values of α, let the nonzero polynomial have degree n and leading coefficient an​. The highest power in ξxx​−xξx​+αξ has coefficient (α−n)an​. Hence α=n∈{0,1,2,…}. Conversely, writing ξ=∑j​aj​xj gives the recurrence
aj+2​=(j+2)(j+1)j−α​aj​.
(27)
For α=n, choose the parity of n and iterate until the series terminates at degree n. Thus all allowed Hermite vertical modes of an isothermal disk have
α=n,ξ=CHen​(z/H),​
(28)
where Hen​ is the Probabilists' Hermite polynomial. In particular, He0​=1 and He1​=x.
Let K=kH and s=ω2/Ω02​. Eliminating the denominator for regular modes gives
(s−n)(s−1)=K2s,(ω2−nΩ02​)(ω2−Ω02​)=k2cs2​ω2.
(29)
For n=0, the nonzero-frequency solution is
ω2=Ω02​(1+K2)=Ω02​+k2cs2​.​
(30)
Its pressure variable is independent of height and uz​=0. These are inertial-acoustic waves, restored by epicyclic motion and pressure; at short radial wavelength they become ordinary acoustic waves. The multiplied dispersion polynomial also has s=0. This is not an extra oscillatory solution represented by nonzero ξ, since ξ=iωp′/ρ vanishes at zero frequency for finite pressure perturbation. The full horizontal equations have a zero-frequency balanced mode of an axisymmetric disk, with no radial velocity and azimuthal Coriolis acceleration balancing a radial pressure gradient. It must be recovered without dividing by ω, rather than counted as a propagating wave. At k=0, the propagating branch limits to an epicyclic oscillation at ∣ω∣=Ω0​, another value at which the eliminated form must be interpreted by a limit.
For n=1, solve (s−1)2=K2s. The two branches are
ω±2​=Ω02​(1+2K2​±2∣K∣​K2+4​)=4Ω02​​(K2+4​±∣K∣)2.​
(31)
Both signs of frequency occur on each squared-frequency branch. The upper branch is acoustic in the large-∣K∣ limit, ∣ω+​∣∼∣k∣cs​; the lower branch is an inertial wave with ∣ω−​∣∼Ω0​/∣K∣. At ∣K∣≪1, both frequencies approach Ω0​, reflecting the equality of the radial and vertical epicyclic frequencies in a Keplerian disk. Here ξ∝z, so uz​=ξz​/ω2 is independent of height: these modes have a coherent vertical displacement of the disk column, rather than a vertical breathing motion.
For a local non-axisymmetric disturbance proportional to ei(kR+mϕ−ωt), the material frequency is ω=ω−mΩ0​. In the same local radial-wave approximation, replace ω by ω. For n=1,m=1, this gives
[(ω−Ω0​)2−Ω02​]2=k2cs2​(ω−Ω0​)2.​
(32)
The four frequencies can equivalently be written ω/Ω0​=1±(K2+4​±∣K∣)/2, with independent choices of the two signs. This is a local dispersion relation, not a claim that Doppler substitution supplies an exact global non-axisymmetric solution in a differentially rotating disk. The radial-wave ordering neglects azimuthal gradients compared with radial gradients; ∣k∣R≫1 can coexist with ∣k∣H≪1 in a thin disk.
Write w=ω/Ω0​. For the two low inertial-frame frequency branches, ∣w∣≪1, while the material frequency remains near −Ω0​. Expanding the dispersion relation gives
(w2−2w)2=K2(w−1)2,4w2=K2+higher-order terms.
(33)
Therefore
Ω0​ω​=±21​kH+O((kH)2),dkdω​≃±2cs​​.​
(34)
For signed k, the two leading branches are conventionally labelled by ±kH/2. These are bending waves of a Keplerian disk: an m=1 vertical displacement varies once around an annulus and corresponds to a small tilt of its orbital plane. A radial variation of the tilt is a warp. The waves transport the warp radially at group speeds cs​/2 in opposite directions. At zero radial wavenumber their low-frequency limit is a stationary rigid tilt; the apparent slow pattern does not mean that the orbiting gas has slow vertical motion in its own frame.
