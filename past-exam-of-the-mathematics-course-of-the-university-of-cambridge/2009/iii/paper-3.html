Throughout, rings are commutative with identity and ring homomorphisms preserve identity. The spectrum of a commutative ring is SpecA={p⊂A:p is a prime ideal}. For an ideal I, put V(I)={p:I⊆p}; these are the closed sets of the Zariski topology. Its axioms follow from
V(0)=SpecA,V(A)=∅,⋂λ​V(Iλ​)=V(∑λ​Iλ​),V(I)∪V(J)=V(IJ).
(1)
For the last equality, a prime ideal containing IJ must contain I or J: if it contained neither, choose a∈I∖p and b∈J∖p, contradicting ab∈p. The other equalities follow directly from containment. Thus arbitrary intersections and finite unions of closed sets are closed, proving that this is a topology.
The Zorn lemma says that a nonempty partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound has a maximal element. For A=0, apply it to proper ideals ordered by inclusion. The zero ideal is proper. The union of a nonempty chain is an ideal and is proper, because containing one would put one in a member of the chain; the empty chain has the zero ideal as an upper bound. There is therefore a maximal ideal m. If a∈/m, then m+(a)=A, so the class of a in A/m is invertible. This quotient ring is a field, making m a prime ideal. Hence A=0⟹SpecA=∅.
For a ring homomorphism f:A→B, define f∗:SpecB→SpecA by f∗(q)=f−1(q). The inverse image is proper because f(1)=1, and primality follows from f(ab)=f(a)f(b). Moreover
(f∗)−1(VA​(I))=VB​(IB),
(2)
where IB is the ideal generated by f(I). This proves continuity. Inverse images also give (g∘f)∗=f∗∘g∗ and id∗=id. Thus Spec is a contravariant functor from rings to topological spaces.
For the specified inclusion, the fundamental theorem of algebra gives the points 0 and (x−z) of SpecC[x]. Over R, the nonzero prime ideals are generated either by x−r with r∈R, or by (x−a)2+b2 with a∈R, b>0; all irreducible real polynomials have degree one or two. Contraction gives the spectrum map for the complexification of a real polynomial ring:
0↦0,(x−z)↦{(x−z),((x−Rez)2+(Imz)2),​z∈R,z∈/R.​​
(3)
Indeed, a real polynomial vanishing at a nonreal z also vanishes at zˉ, and is divisible by their quadratic factor; the converse is immediate. This map is surjective. Its fibers contain one point over a real linear factor, two conjugate points over an irreducible quadratic factor, and only the generic point over zero. It identifies complex-conjugate pairs. Since each proper Zariski-closed set in either one-variable polynomial ring is a finite set of closed points, images of closed sets are closed; hence the Zariski topology on the target is the quotient topology for this identification.

Let S be a multiplicative subset, containing one and closed under products. On A×S, use
(a,s)∼(b,t)⟺u(ta−sb)=0 for some u∈S.
(4)
The relation is reflexive and symmetric. For transitivity, if u(ta−sb)=0 and v(rb−tc)=0, then multiplication and addition give uvt(ra−sc)=0. Thus it is an equivalence relation. Write a/s for the equivalence class. The localization of a ring AS​=S−1A has operations
sa​+tb​=stat+bs​,sa​tb​=stab​,−sa​=s−a​.
(5)
They are well defined. For example, replacing a/s by b/t in its sum with c/r gives cross-numerator difference r2(ta−sb), and in its product with c/r gives rc(ta−sb). The same annihilating element u kills both differences. Replacing the other input works identically. Associativity and distributivity then follow by putting all terms over the product of their denominators and using the ring identities in A. Addition and multiplication are commutative, with identities 0/1 and 1/1, and the displayed negative is an additive inverse. Hence S−1A is a commutative ring. If 0∈S, the fraction relation identifies all elements, producing the zero ring.
The canonical ring homomorphism ι:A→S−1A, a↦a/1, makes each s∈S a unit, with inverse 1/s. The universal property of localization states that any ring homomorphism h:A→C sending S into the units factors uniquely through ι. Define the factor by
h(a/s)=h(a)h(s)−1.​
(6)
If u(ta−sb)=0, then h(u) is invertible, so h(t)h(a)=h(s)h(b), proving well-definedness. The fraction operations prove that h is a ring homomorphism. Its value is forced by a/s=ι(a)ι(s)−1, so it is unique. This property characterizes the localization up to a unique compatible isomorphism.
For a prime ideal p, its complement is a multiplicative subset, and the localization at a prime ideal is Ap​=(A∖p)−1A. Fractions with numerator outside p are units; those with numerator in p form the unique maximal ideal pAp​. Thus it is a local ring.
For the nilpotent question, suppose a=0 and an=0. Its annihilator is a proper ideal, so it lies in some maximal ideal m. The fraction criterion says a/1=0 in Am​ only if sa=0 for some s∈/m, which is impossible by the choice of m. Thus a/1 is a nonzero nilpotent element in that localization. This proves reducedness is detected by prime localizations: if every prime localization is reduced, A has no nonzero nilpotent elements.
For the domain question take A=k×k with k a field. Its only prime ideals are p1​=0×k and p2​=k×0: a prime ideal must contain one of the orthogonal idempotents (1,0),(0,1), and the corresponding quotient is k. Localizing at p1​ makes (1,0) invertible and kills (0,1), giving Ap1​​≅k; similarly Ap2​​≅k. But (1,0)(0,1)=0 with both factors nonzero. Therefore domain prime localizations do not force A to be an integral domain, as expressed by domain prime localizations do not imply a domain.

A Noetherian module satisfies the ascending chain condition on its submodules; equivalently every submodule is a finitely generated module. If the latter condition holds, the union of an ascending chain has a finite generating set lying in one member, so the chain stabilizes. Conversely, a submodule with no finite generating set allows successive choices outside the submodule generated so far, producing a strictly ascending chain. A Noetherian ring is a ring that is Noetherian as a module over itself, equivalently one whose ideals are all finitely generated.
The formal power series ring consists of sequences written ∑i≥0​ai​xi, with coefficientwise addition and the Cauchy product:
(∑i≥0​ai​xi)(∑j≥0​bj​xj)=∑n≥0​(∑i=0n​ai​bn−i​)xn.
(7)
Every coefficient uses a finite sum; no analytic convergence is involved. Evaluation at zero gives A[[x]]/(x)≅A. A quotient ring of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian, because its ascending chains of ideals lift to such chains upstairs. Thus one direction of Noetherianity of a formal power series ring is immediate.
For the other direction suppose A is Noetherian, and let I⊆A[[x]] be an ideal. Define the coefficient ideals of a formal power series ideal
Ir​={[xr]g:g∈I∩xrA[[x]]}⊆A.
(8)
Each is an ideal, and multiplication by x gives Ir​⊆Ir+1​. Choose N with IN​=IN+1​=⋯. For 0≤r≤N, choose generators ar1​,…,armr​​ for Ir​, and choose series grj​∈I∩xrA[[x]] whose xr coefficients are those generators. Empty generating sets are allowed for zero ideals.
These finitely many series generate I. To prove this without assuming that I is closed, start with g∈I and cancel its coefficients successively. If the residual hr​ lies in I∩xrA[[x]], its xr coefficient lies in Ir​. Put s=min(r,N); since Ir​=Is​ when r≥N, write that coefficient as ∑j​crj​asj​ and subtract ∑j​crj​xr−sgsj​. The new residual belongs to I∩xr+1A[[x]].
For s<N, the generators gsj​ are used only at the single step r=s. For s=N, their accumulated multipliers are the genuine formal power series ∑r≥N​crj​xr−N. Thus the construction yields the exact coefficientwise identity
g=∑s<N​∑j​csj​gsj​+∑j​(∑r≥N​crj​xr−N)gNj​.
(9)
Every coefficient of the residual vanishes after finitely many steps. This is an ordinary finite generating expression in A[[x]], not merely an approximation by elements of a smaller ideal. Therefore every ideal is finitely generated, proving
A is Noetherian⟺A[[x]] is Noetherian.​
(10)
Finally, for the surjective module endomorphism f, the kernels kerf⊆kerf2⊆⋯ stabilize because M is a Noetherian module. Take N with kerfN=kerfN+1. If f(m)=0, surjectivity of fN gives u with fN(u)=m. Then fN+1(u)=0, so u∈kerfN and m=0. Hence f is injective as well as surjective; its inverse is automatically A-linear. This proves f is an isomorphism, the statement that Noetherian modules are Hopfian.

An algebraic line bundle over A is an invertible module: a finitely generated projective module L such that Lp​≅Ap​ for every prime ideal p. Define the Picard group of a ring as the set of its isomorphism classes, with
[L][K]=[L⊗A​K].
(11)
The tensor product of modules of two finite projective modules is finite projective: write each as a direct summand of a finite free module and tensor the two decompositions. Localization gives (L⊗A​K)p​≅Ap​, so this multiplication stays within the set of invertible modules. It is well defined on isomorphism classes. The associativity and symmetry isomorphisms of the tensor product give associativity and commutativity, and [A] is the identity.
Let L∗=HomA​(L,A) be the dual module. A finite direct-summand presentation shows that L∗ is finite projective and that localization commutes with this dual. Since Lp​ is free of rank one, so is (L∗)p​. The evaluation homomorphism
L⊗A​L∗⟶A,ℓ⊗λ⟼λ(ℓ)
(12)
is an isomorphism after every prime localization. Its kernel and cokernel are therefore zero, by exactness of localization and localization detects zero elements for modules. Thus [L]−1=[L∗], proving that Pic(A) is an abelian group.
For a ring homomorphism f:A→B, tensoring a split presentation L⊕L′≅Ar gives (B⊗A​L)⊕(B⊗A​L′)≅Br, so B⊗A​L is finite projective. For q∈SpecB put p=f−1(q). Every element of A∖p becomes a unit in Bq​, and
(B⊗A​L)q​≅Bq​⊗Ap​​Lp​≅Bq​.
(13)
This proves base change of invertible modules. Moreover, the canonical map
(B⊗A​L)⊗B​(B⊗A​K)⟶B⊗A​(L⊗A​K),(b⊗ℓ)⊗(b′⊗k)⟼bb′⊗(ℓ⊗k)
(14)
is an isomorphism, with inverse b⊗(ℓ⊗k)↦(b⊗ℓ)⊗(1⊗k). It shows that [L]↦[B⊗A​L] is a group homomorphism. Identity maps induce the identity, and for A→B→C, the associativity isomorphism C⊗B​(B⊗A​L)≅C⊗A​L proves composition compatibility. Therefore Pic is a covariant functor to abelian groups.
The polynomial ring C[x] is a principal ideal domain by division with remainder. A submodule N of a finite free module over a principal ideal domain is free: induct on the ambient rank, project onto the first coordinate, and note that the image is a principal ideal, hence either zero or a free rank-one module. This gives a split exact sequence with that image as quotient; the kernel lies in a free module of one smaller rank, completing the induction. A finite projective module is such a submodule, and so is free. An invertible module has rank one after passing to the fraction field, so its free rank is exactly one. Hence every algebraic line bundle over C[x] is trivial and
Pic(C[x])=0.​
(15)

Here the usual homological algebra convention is that the right-exact functor F is additive. Module categories have enough projective modules, because any module is a quotient of a free module. Choose a projective resolution
⋯d3​​P2​d2​​P1​d1​​P0​⟶M⟶0.
(16)
Apply F to the unaugmented chain complex and define the left derived functors by
Li​F(M)=Hi​(F(P∙​))=imF(di+1​)kerF(di​)​(i>0),L0​F(M)=cokerF(d1​).​
(17)
Additivity ensures that F preserves zero maps, sums of maps and homotopy identities, so the transformed differentials still compose to zero.
For a module homomorphism u:M→N, lift it to a chain map between chosen projective resolutions. In degree zero, lift through Q0​↠N using projectivity of P0​. In each higher degree, the already constructed map lands in the next kernel; exactness of Q∙​ and projectivity of Pi​ supply the next lift. Any two lifts are chain homotopic: their degree-zero difference lands in ker(Q0​→N)=imd1Q​, so it lifts to h0​:P0​→Q1​. Inductively lift the remaining difference after subtracting the preceding homotopy term. This gives
ui​−vi​=di+1Q​hi​+hi−1​diP​,h−1​=0.
(18)
Applying F preserves this identity, so the lifts induce the same homology map. Identity lifts and composable lifts show that Li​F is a functor. Comparison lifts between two resolutions over the identity of M are inverse up to chain homotopy; hence the construction is independent of the chosen resolution up to its canonical natural isomorphism.
Right exactness identifies L0​F(M)=coker(F(P1​)→F(P0​)) naturally with F(M). For a projective module P, the length-zero resolution gives Li​F(P)=0 for i>0. For a short exact sequence 0→M′→M→M′′→0, the horseshoe lemma builds a degreewise split short exact sequence of projective resolutions. Concretely, lift the projective generators covering M′′ into M and combine them with generators covering M′; repeat on the kernels. The middle projective term in each degree is the direct sum of the two outer ones. An additive functor preserves these split sequences, and lifting cycles, taking their differentials and returning to the subcomplex produces the connecting maps in
⋯→L2​F(M′′)→L1​F(M′)→L1​F(M)→L1​F(M′′)→F(M′)→F(M)→F(M′′)→0.
(19)
This is the natural long exact derived-functor sequence. In particular, if 0→K→P→M→0 has P projective, then Li​F(M)≅Li−1​F(K) for i≥2, and L1​F(M)=ker(F(K)→F(P)). These are dimension-shifting formulas. Positive degrees can be killed by the surjection P→M. If F is exact, it preserves every resolution's exactness and all its positive left derived functors vanish. Conversely, if L1​F vanishes on all modules, the displayed long exact sequence makes F exact. A natural transformation between additive right-exact functors induces transformations on the left derived functors by applying it termwise.
For extension of scalars F(M)=B⊗A​M, these derived functors are the Tor functors ToriA​(B,M). When B=A/(a) and a is a non-zero-divisor, there is the length-one free resolution
0⟶A a ​A⟶B⟶0.
(20)
Although the construction above resolves M, one may instead use this resolution of B. To justify that calculation, tensor a free resolution of M with the two-term resolution of B to form a double complex. Taking homology first along the resolution of B leaves B⊗A​P∙​; taking it first along the resolution of M leaves Ma​M. The free terms preserve exactness under tensoring. The double complex is first-quadrant with finitely many terms on each total diagonal, so both filtrations compute the same total homology. This proves the required balancing directly.
The kernel and cokernel of multiplication by a now give Tor for a principal non-zero-divisor quotient:
L0​F(M)=M/aM,L1​F(M)={m∈M:am=0},Li​F(M)=0(i≥2).​
(21)
These are naturally B-modules. For M=A, the degree-one group is zero; for M=B, multiplication by a is zero and both degrees zero and one are B. Thus, when B=0, degree one explicitly detects the failure of this base-change functor to be exact.

Choose a surjection P0​=Ar0​↠M. Since A is a Noetherian ring, a finite free module is a Noetherian module, and its kernel K1​ is a finitely generated module. Choose a finite free surjection P1​↠K1​ and repeat. This constructs a resolution by finitely generated free modules
⋯⟶P2​⟶P1​⟶P0​⟶M⟶0,
(22)
whose terms are, in particular, finitely generated projective modules. It may be infinite; the request is finite generation of every term, not finite length.
Now suppose the nonzero finitely generated module M has finite projective dimension n. For n≥1, its nth syzygy Kn​ in this free resolution is projective. Indeed, dimension shifting along the successive kernel sequences gives
ExtA1​(Kn​,N)≅ExtAn+1​(M,N)=0
(23)
for every module N. The vanishing follows by computing the latter Ext functor from any length-n projective resolution. Vanishing of Ext1(Kn​,−) makes every short exact sequence ending in Kn​ split: apply the long exact sequence of Ext groups to a free surjection onto Kn​ to lift its identity. Thus Kn​ is a projective module. It is finitely generated by the preceding construction. Truncating gives
0⟶Pn​dn​​Pn−1​⟶⋯⟶P0​⟶M⟶0
(24)
with every term finite projective.
Apply the Hom functor HomA​(−,A) and write Pi∗​=HomA​(Pi​,A). At the top degree,
ExtAn​(M,A)=coker(dn∗​:Pn−1∗​→Pn∗​).
(25)
If this group were zero, dn∗​ would be surjective. The dual module of a finite projective module is finite projective, so this surjection has a section s:Pn∗​→Pn−1∗​. Finite projective modules identify with their double dual modules: the evaluation map is an isomorphism for a finite free module and passes to its direct summands. Dualizing dn∗​s=id therefore gives s∗dn​=idPn​​. Hence dn​ splits, and Pn−1​/dn​(Pn​) is projective. Replacing the last two terms by this quotient shortens the projective resolution to length n−1, contradicting minimality. This proves top Ext detects finite projective dimension:
ExtAn​(M,A)=0.​
(26)
For n=0, M is a nonzero finite projective module and embeds as a summand of Ar. Some coordinate projection restricts nontrivially to M, proving HomA​(M,A)=0 as required.
For infinite projective dimension, take the dual-number algebra A=k[ε]/(ε2) and M=k=A/(ε). Multiplication by ε has image and kernel both equal to (ε), giving the periodic resolution over dual numbers
⋯ε​Aε​Aε​A⟶k⟶0.

Applying HomA​(−,k) makes every differential zero, so ExtAi​(k,k)≅k for every i≥0. A finite projective resolution would force all sufficiently high Ext functors to vanish. Thus pdk[ε]/(ε2)​k=∞.
The finite-generation hypothesis is essential if the middle sentence's “a module” is interpreted as an unrestricted new module. A concrete vanishing top Ext without finite generation occurs for A=k[[t]] and K=A[1/t]=k((t)). This Noetherian ring has the exact free presentation
0⟶⨁i≥0​Aei​d​⨁i≥0​Aei​⟶K⟶0,d(ei​)=ei​−tei+1​,ei​⟼t−i.

The map onto K is surjective because every Laurent series has a lower bound on its exponents.
