Use the determinant-normalized slash operator and the usual Hecke operator normalization. A normalized eigenform is a nonzero cusp form f=∑r≥1​ar​qr with a1​=1 and Tn​f=λ(n)f for every n. We first establish the operator facts needed here, rather than assume the Hecke multiplication relations.
Integer matrices of determinant n have the determinant-n matrix representatives for Hecke operators γa,b,d​=(a0​bd​), with ad=n and 0≤b<d. Integer row operations give these representatives: the Euclidean algorithm reduces the first column to (a,0), and a shear reduces b modulo d. Right multiplication by the modular group permutes the left cosets, so Tn​f=nk/2−1∑ad=n​∑bmodd​f∣k​γa,b,d​ is modular. Rational changes of coordinate preserve cusp vanishing, so it remains a cusp form. Substituting its Fourier expansion of a modular form and summing e2πirb/d gives
am​(Tn​f)=∑d∣gcd(m,n)​dk−1amn/d2​(f).
(1)
In particular a1​(Tn​f)=an​(f), and the normalization gives λ(n)=an​(f) for every n.
For coprime u,v, applying this coefficient formula twice amounts to choosing independently a divisor of gcd(m,u) and one of gcd(m,v). Their product runs exactly once through the divisors of gcd(m,uv), with the same factor dk−1 and coefficient index. Thus Tu​Tv​=Tuv​. For a prime p, put P=pk−1 and ν=vp​(m). The coefficient of qm in Tpe​f is
Ae​(m)=∑j=0min(e,ν)​Pjapem/p2j​.
(2)
The coefficient in Tp​Tpe​f is Ae​(pm)+PAe​(m/p), the second summand being zero if p∤m. Expanding the two finite sums and matching their endpoints gives Ae+1​(m)+PAe−1​(m) for e≥1. Equality of all coefficients proves Tp​Tpe​=Tpe+1​+pk−1Tpe−1​. Applying these identities to the nonzero eigenvector f yields
λ(uv)=λ(u)λ(v)(gcd(u,v)=1),λ(pe+2)=λ(p)λ(pe+1)−pk−1λ(pe).​
(3)

For a prime number p, the defining finite sum for the Hecke operator simplifies to
Tp​f=pk−1f(pz)+p1​∑b=0p−1​f((z+b)/p).

First the eigenvalues of Hecke operators are real. Here is the needed Petersson inner product argument: unfold the finite coset sum defining Tn​ into its correspondence domain, then change variables by each matrix. The determinant-normalized slash action preserves ykdxdy/y2 in the paired integrand, and transfers a representative to its inverse. Multiplying that inverse by the positive scalar n gives its adjugate without changing the slash action. Adjugation reverses the integer determinant-n correspondence, so the resulting sum is again Tn​. Consequently ⟨Tn​f,g⟩=⟨f,Tn​g⟩. Taking g=f proves reality of λ(n), since ⟨f,f⟩>0.
Fix one prime p and set x=λ(p)/p(k−1)/2 and be​=λ(pe)/pe(k−1)/2. The Hecke multiplication relations proved in (a) give b0​=1, b1​=x and be+2​=xbe+1​−be​. The distinct-root assumption excludes x=±2.
If ∣x∣<2, write x=2cosθ, 0<θ<π. Solving the linear recurrence gives be​=sin((e+1)θ)/sinθ. If θ/(2π) is rational, infinitely many e satisfy (e+1)θ≡θ(mod2π), hence be​=1. If it is irrational, density of the corresponding irrational rotation gives infinitely many e in the open arc where sin((e+1)θ)>sinθ/2.
If ∣x∣>2, let β,β−1 be the real roots of X2−xX+1, choosing ∣β∣>1. Then be​=(βe+1−β−e−1)/(β−β−1). Its even-index terms are positive and tend to infinity, whether β is positive or negative. Thus the prime-power coefficients of a level-one eigenform are often large and positive, and in either case
λ(pe)≥21​pe(k−1)/2 for infinitely many e.​
(5)

A weight-2n meromorphic modular form satisfies f(γz)=(cz+d)2nf(z), while d(γz)=(cz+d)−2dz. The factors cancel in f(z)(dz)n, giving a Γ-invariant meromorphic n-differential on the complex upper half-plane.
Away from elliptic points and cusps, descend this differential through local inverse branches of the quotient map. At an elliptic point of effective stabilizer order e, choose a coordinate t in which the stabilizer acts by t↦ζt, and use w=te downstairs. If the lifted coefficient has order r, invariance forces r+n to be divisible by e. The descended differential has integer order
ordw​ω(f)=er+n​−n.​
(6)
Indeed F(w)(dw)n pulls back to entn(e−1)F(te)(dt)n. At a modular cusp of width h, the coordinate qc​=e2πiz/h gives dz=(h/(2πi))dqc​/qc​. Therefore
ordc​ω(f)=vc​(f)−n.​
(7)
Both formulas provide meromorphic extensions over the omitted points, producing the unique differential with the prescribed pullback on the compactified modular curve.
For the character-valued question, write X=X0​(N) and let f be the chosen nonzero meromorphic form. The quotient of two forms with the same weight and Dirichlet character is a meromorphic function on X. At an interior point P with effective elliptic order eP​ (one at ordinary points), let rP​ be the vanishing order of the lifted f. At a cusp let vc​ be its order in the width coordinate; this can be fractional because of the character multiplier. Multiplying by a meromorphic function φ changes these orders by eP​ordP​φ and ordc​φ, respectively. Interior holomorphy requires a nonnegative order, while cusp vanishing requires a strictly positive order. The exact divisor is consequently
D(f)=∑P interior​⌊eP​rP​​⌋P+∑c cusp​(⌈vc​⌉−1)c.
(8)
Only finitely many coefficients are nonzero. The order inequalities are equivalent to div(φ)+D(f)≥0. Division by f proves the reverse inclusion, so the Riemann-Roch space gives
Sk​(Γ1​(N),χ)=fL(D(f)).​
(9)
This extends the cusp-form divisor presentation to elliptic points and character multipliers.
Choose a nonzero meromorphic one-differential on X, and let g be its weight-two pullback. For ft​=fgt, the interior order is rP​(f)+trP​(g) and the cusp order is vc​(f)+tvc​(g). The elliptic orders are one, two or three, so adding six to t changes each interior floor by 6rP​(g)/eP​, and each cusp ceiling by 6vc​(g). The valence formula for the modular group for g says ∑P​rP​(g)/eP​+∑c​vc​(g)=d/6, where d=[SL2​(Z):Γ0​(N)]. Thus
degD(ft+6​)−degD(ft​)=d,degD(ft​)=dt/6+B(tmod6).
(10)
The degrees tend to infinity. For sufficiently large t, the Riemann-Roch theorem therefore gives
dimSk+2t​(Γ1​(N),χ)=6dt​+A(tmod6)>0,A=B+1−g(X).​
(11)
The periodic formula obtained this way is an eventual formula. For small t, the exact answer includes the Riemann-Roch correction ℓ(KX​−D(ft​)); it should not silently be discarded without the large-degree hypothesis.

Let T=(10​11​) and S=(01​−10​). For γ=(ac​bd​) in the modular group, left multiplication by powers of T and by S performs subtraction and exchange on its first-column entries. The Euclidean algorithm reduces (a,c) to (±1,0), since the determinant condition makes them coprime. The resulting matrix is ±Tm, and −I=S2 is already generated. Hence S,T generate SL2​(Z).
The standard fundamental domain of the modular group is D={z∈H:∣Rez∣≤1/2, ∣z∣≥1}, with the vertical sides identified by T and the circular sides by S. To prove every orbit meets it, choose a coprime integer pair (c,d) minimizing ∣cz+d∣. A minimum exists because only finitely many pairs have this quantity below any fixed bound: ∣c∣Imz≤∣cz+d∣ bounds c, then d. Complete the pair to a determinant-one matrix. Its image has maximal imaginary part in the orbit, since Im(γz)=Imz/∣cz+d∣2. Translate its real part into [−1/2,1/2]. If its modulus were below one, inversion would increase its imaginary part, a contradiction. This is the reduction to the standard modular region.

The normalized Eisenstein series satisfy E4​=1+240∑m≥1​σ3​(m)qm and E6​=1−504∑m≥1​σ5​(m)qm. Their weights are four and six. The valence formula for the modular group makes the normalized weight-twelve cusp form Δ have one simple cusp zero and no zero in H. Comparing its first coefficient gives Δ=(E43​−E62​)/1728.
Odd weights vanish because −I acts by (−1)k. Weight two vanishes by the valence formula for the modular group: the forced zero at i alone contributes 1/2>2/12. For every remaining nonnegative even weight, except two, choose a,b≥0 with 4a+6b=k. Subtract the constant coefficient of f times E4a​E6b​. The remainder is a cusp form; division by Δ produces a holomorphic modular form of weight k−12. The cusp and interior orders justify this division. Induction on the weight proves
M∗​(SL2​(Z))=C[E4​,E6​].​
(12)
The integral triangular basis of level-one modular forms is Fj​=ΔjE4aj​​E6bj​​, with 4aj​+6bj​=k−12j≥0 and k−12j=2. The induction just given shows these span, and their distinct leading terms qj show linear independence. Every coefficient is integral by the hypothesis on Δ and the displayed Eisenstein expansions. In f=∑j​cj​Fj​, compare coefficients successively: c0​=a0​, then c1​ is a1​ minus an integer combination of already integral coefficients, and similarly for every j. The largest permitted j is at most ⌊(k+1)/12⌋, so the stipulated initial coefficients make every cj​ integral. Therefore all Fourier coefficients of f are integers.

Set C=AB=(01​−12λ​). It fixes z=−λ+i1−λ2​ because z2+2λz+1=0. Write λ=cosθ, 0<θ<π/2; the eigenvalues of C are e±iθ.
The stabilizer of an interior point of H in PSL2​(R) is conjugate to the compact rotation group. Its intersection with a discrete subgroup is finite: otherwise compactness gives accumulating elements, and their quotients accumulate at the identity. Thus C has finite order in the projective group. Its eigenvalues then satisfy e2imθ=1 for some positive integer m, forcing θ/π∈Q. Hence λ=cos(πy) for some y∈Q​. This is a necessary condition only.

Use the normalized Fricke involution WN​=f↦f∣k​(0N​−10​), and let Dℓ​=(ℓ0​01​). Choose integers m,n with mℓ−Nn=1. The matrix γ=(1N​nmℓ​) lies in Γ1​(N), and γ′=(ℓN​nm​) has determinant one and lies in Γ0​(N), with m≡ℓ−1(modN). The displayed identity in the PDF reads Dℓ​γ−1=diag(1,ℓ)γ′. Taking double cosets therefore identifies the reversed correspondence with the diamond operator ⟨ℓ−1⟩ times the original one. Conjugation by WN​ reverses the two diagonal entries, so
WN​Tℓ​WN−1​=⟨ℓ−1⟩Tℓ​.​
(13)
The same finite-correspondence change of variables in the Petersson inner product transfers a matrix to its inverse and yields Tℓ∗​=⟨ℓ−1⟩Tℓ​. The diamond operators commute with Tℓ​, so Tℓ​Tℓ∗​=Tℓ∗​Tℓ​. Thus Tℓ​ is a normal operator. Multiplying WN​ by a nonzero scalar does not change the conjugation formula.

The commuting normal operators Tℓ​, together with the finite-order diamond operators, admit simultaneous diagonalization for the Petersson inner product. Their restrictions to the new subspace of cusp forms have one-dimensional common eigenspaces by the newform multiplicity-one theorem. This additional theorem is the essential newform input: normality alone would allow higher multiplicities. Since every Up​ commutes with the good-prime operators and preserves the new space, it preserves each such line and is scalar on it. Normalize its nonzero first Fourier coefficient to one to obtain a newform. This gives the requested simultaneous eigenbasis of the new space.
By definition, every old form is a linear combination of lower-level oldforms by argument dilation. Repeatedly split each lower-level cusp space into its old space and its Petersson inner product orthogonal new space; induction on the level shows that the specified dilations of newforms span the full space. To prove independence, separate distinct primitive eigenforms by their good-prime eigenvalues, using the newform multiplicity-one theorem. For a fixed normalized form, its dilations f(tz) are independent: order the distinct positive integers t increasingly, and the coefficient of the least occurring qt kills its coefficient in any vanishing linear combination. Repeat for the next least t. Thus these dilations are indeed a basis.
For trivial character, part (a) shows that WN​ commutes with all the good-prime operators, so it preserves the one-dimensional primitive line. Also WN2​=(−1)k for the determinant-normalized slash operator; a nonzero trivial-character form has even k. Therefore
WN​f=εf,ε∈{1,−1}.​
(14)
The power of N in an unnormalized Fricke eigenvalue depends on the convention. The plain slash action (cz+d)−kf(γz) gives εN−k/2, while the Hecke-style action (detγ)k−1(cz+d)−kf(γz) gives εNk/2−1. The PDF prints ±Nk−1; that value requires the deliberate rescaling wN​=Nk−1WN​ and does not follow under either usual convention. Under that rescaling its displayed eigenvalue is εNk−1; all the preceding eigenspace and conjugation statements are unchanged.

Let fj​(z)=f(pjz) for 0≤j≤r, and write a=ap​(f) and WM​f=εf. The Hecke operator Up​ acts on the Fourier expansion by ∑an​qn↦∑apn​qn. Since p∣M, the primitive relation is apn​=ap​an​. Hence Up​f0​=af0​ and Up​fj​=fj−1​ for j≥1. Substituting the argument directly in the Fricke involution gives

Thus, in the basis ej​=pjk/2fj​, put b=pk/2 and let R be the reversal matrix, Rej​=er−j​. The matrices are
[Up​]=​a0⋮00​b0⋯⋯​0b⋱00​⋯⋯⋱00​00⋮b0​​,[WN​]=εR.​

If the exam's rescaling wN​=Nk−1WN​ is used, multiply the second matrix by Nk−1.
Here is an explicit irreducibility argument for the prime-power old block at a bad prime. Any nonzero common invariant complex subspace V contains an eigenvector of the restricted Up​. Its eigenvalue is either a or zero. If a=0, the a-eigenspace is Ce0​; containing it gives er​ by reversal, then every ej​ by successive powers of Up​. The kernel of Up​ is C(e1​−(b/a)e0​). If V instead contains this vector, reversal and Upr​ give
Upr​R(e1​−(b/a)e0​)=br−1(a−b2/a)e0​.
(17)
This is nonzero: the bad-prime eigenvalue of a trivial-character newform satisfies a2=pk−2 when p∥M, and a=0 when p2∣M, whereas b2=pk. If a=0, the kernel is already Ce0​, and the same reversal-and-shift argument works. Therefore the block has no nonzero proper subspace invariant under Up​ and the Fricke operator, and hence none invariant under the full ring in the question.

For cusps α,β∈P1(Q), the modular symbol {α,β} is the oriented path class in H1​(X(Γ),C;Z), where C is the finite cusp set. Reversing paths changes the sign, concatenating paths gives {α,β}+{β,γ}={α,γ}, and the group identifies translated paths. A continued fraction expansion subdivides any rational-endpoint path into determinant-one edges. Representatives of the finitely many cosets therefore supply Manin symbols [g]={g0,g∞}. With inversion S and translation T, reversing an edge and taking the boundary of an ideal triangle give
[g]+[gS]=0,[g]+[gST]+[g(ST)2]=0.
(18)
These relations give a finite rational linear algebra presentation. Integral computation additionally removes the elliptic torsion in this naive presentation; the actual relative homology group of the compact surface is torsion-free.
The boundary of {α,β} is [β]−[α] in H0​(C). Its kernel is H1​(X;Q) by the long exact sequence of a pair. A Hecke operator acts by summing the images of each path under its finite determinant-n correspondence. Rational endpoints are again rational, so continued fractions reduce each image back to Manin symbols. Finite row reduction produces rational matrices for the relative symbols and for the boundary kernel. Integration of a weight-two cusp form against these absolute cycles is compatible with the Hecke action. The resulting decomposition
H1​(X;C)≃S2​(Γ)∗⊕S2​(Γ)​∗
(19)
explains the relation between these matrices and cusp forms. Strictly, a rational matrix on absolute homology is not automatically a rational matrix on the holomorphic summand in an arbitrary complex basis. For subgroups preserved by real conjugation, the conjugation eigenspaces provide rational models of the cusp-form representation. More generally one should specify a compatible rational model or use the doubled homological representation. This distinction matters in interpreting the essay's rationality claim for an arbitrary subgroup; the algorithm on homology is always rational.
For the supplied subgroup, use P=(1736)(254) for translation and Q=(17)(26)(34) for inversion, following the PDF's naming. Translation has two cycles, so there are two cusps of widths four and three. Inversion fixes just coset five, giving one elliptic orbit of order two. The order-three product acts with cycles (1)(253)(467) in one of the two equivalent composition conventions, giving one elliptic orbit of order three. Since Q2=1, the central −I acts trivially and the effective index is seven. The genus formula for a modular curve gives
g=1+127​−41​−31​−22​=0.
(20)
The long exact sequence of a pair now gives
rankH1​(X(Γ),C;Z)=2g+∣C∣−1=1.​
(21)
One can see this directly from the symbols. Over Q, Q gives m7​=−m1​, m6​=−m2​, m4​=−m3​ and m5​=0. The fixed order-three coset gives m1​=0, and the other triangle relations give m3​=−m2​. Thus (m1​,…,m7​)=(0,t,−t,t,0,−t,0). After removing elliptic torsion the integral group is free of rank one, agreeing with the surface calculation. Since g=0, the absolute boundary kernel and S2​(Γ) both vanish: the cusp-form Hecke matrices for this particular example are the empty 0×0 matrices.
