The equal carbon/oxygen mass split cancels from this result: any mixture of these two symmetric nuclei has the same electron fraction. Taking the paper's metallicity symbol to mean f gives its requested expression.
The literal pre-helium-burning nitrogen interpretation, however, needs a correction. Each initial nitrogen-14 nucleus receives two alpha particles, including eight additional baryons, before becoming neon-22; the intervening beta-plus decay also emits a neutrino. Thus, if the entire original metallicity is nitrogen-14 with mass fraction Zinitial​, conservation of the number of seed nuclei gives
X22​=1422​Zinitial​,⟨N⟩⟨Z⟩​=7+Zinitial​7−Zinitial​​.​
(33)
This is neon-22 neutron excess from nitrogen-14. The two expressions are compatible only when their arguments are distinguished: the displayed (11−Z)/(11+Z) law uses the final neon mass fraction, not literally the earlier nitrogen mass fraction. Treating those fractions as equal is an extra simplifying approximation, not a consequence of the stated capture chain.
A radioactively powered Type Ia supernova light curve rises to a broad maximum over roughly weeks as energy deposited in expanding ejecta diffuses out, then declines over the following weeks and months. Its power comes predominantly from the nickel-56 decay chain, first nickel to cobalt and then cobalt to iron. Gamma rays and charged decay products deposit heat, which is reprocessed into optical and infrared radiation. At later times, declining cobalt decay power and increasing gamma-ray escape shape the tail. It is not sustained nuclear fusion in the expanding ejecta. The amount of radioactive nickel, photon diffusion time and deposition efficiency all matter, so an uncorrected fixed luminosity is only an idealization.
For the requested two-isotope yield calculation, assume the same total processed iron-group mass, namely 1M⊙​ as inferred from the zero-neon reference. Treat the final freeze-out ashes as nickel-56 and iron-54, neglecting residual light particles and additional weak neutronization. If w is the nickel mass fraction, conservation of electron fraction gives
2w​+(1−w)5426​=21​−22f​.
(34)
Since 26/54=1/2−1/54, this reduces to the metallicity dependence of nickel-56 yield
w=1−1127​f.​
(35)
Under the paper's intended identification f=Z=1/50,
M56​=(1−55027​)M⊙​=550523​M⊙​≃0.95M⊙​(two significant figures).​
(36)
If instead Z=1/50 denotes the earlier nitrogen mass fraction, retaining the actual capture stoichiometry gives w=1−27Z/7=323/350 and M56​≃0.92M⊙​. The same temperature and density do not alone fix a total burned mass; equality of that mass is also implicit in the comparison. The two-isotope assumption is a bulk-ash approximation, not a complete equilibrium network with all free particles identically absent.
For otherwise comparable explosions, approximate the peak luminosity by a quantity proportional to the radioactive nickel mass. Let d be the true distance to an extremely low-neon event, and use the solar calibration L⊙​/L0​=523/550. Its measured flux is F=L0​/(4πd2), but the inferred standard-candle distance is
ddest​​=L0​L⊙​​​=550523​​≃0.975.​
(37)
The source is intrinsically brighter than the calibration, so the distance is underestimated by about 2.5%, conventionally described here as about 3%. Equivalently, d/dest​=550/523​≃1.0255, consistent with the supplied rounded factor 1.03. Using the literal nitrogen metallicity instead gives about a 4% underestimate. These are conditional composition effects in this simplified model; real Type Ia distance estimation also standardizes light-curve shape and color.
