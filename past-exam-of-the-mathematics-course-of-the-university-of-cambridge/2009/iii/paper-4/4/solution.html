In character theory, a p-elementary group is a finite group isomorphic to P×C, where P is a p-group and C is cyclic of order coprime to p. Either factor may be trivial. An elementary group is p-elementary for at least one prime p; it need not be an elementary abelian group.
A generalised character means an integer linear combination of irreducible complex characters. Brauer's characterisation of characters states that a complex class function f on a finite group G is a generalised character if and only if fE​ is a generalised character for every elementary subgroup E≤G. Equivalently,
f∈ZIrr(G)⟺⟨fE​,α⟩E​∈Z for every elementary E≤G and α∈Irr(E).​
(43)
An ordinary character additionally requires nonnegative coefficients in its global irreducible decomposition. We use the virtual-character criterion just stated.
For the extension construction, set d=θ(1). The determinant character detθ is linear. Since its extension μ has the same value 1 at the identity, μ is also a linear character. For any g∈G, the subgroup Jg​=N⟨g⟩ has cyclic quotient Jg​/N, hence a solvable quotient. The invariant character θ has degree coprime to [Jg​:N], since this index divides [G:N], and μJg​​ extends its determinant. The permitted coprime-degree determinant extension theorem therefore gives a unique character χg​∈Irr(Jg​) with
(χg​)N​=θ,detχg​=μJg​​.
(44)
Define a function on G by f(g)=χg​(g). We next verify that these individually defined values form a compatible class function.
If x∈G, conjugation carries Jg​ to Jxgx−1​. Transporting χg​ by this conjugation gives a character extending θ, because θ is G-invariant, and with determinant μ restricted to the conjugate subgroup, because μ is a class function. Uniqueness identifies the transported character with χxgx−1​. Consequently f(xgx−1)=f(g).
Now take an elementary subgroup E≤G and let J=NE. A finite p-group is solvable, a cyclic group is solvable, and a direct product of solvable groups is solvable. Thus E is solvable, and so is J/N≅E/(E∩N). Again the index [J:N] divides [G:N], so the same extension theorem gives χJ​∈Irr(J) extending θ with determinant μJ​.
For g∈E, we have Jg​≤J. The restriction (χJ​)Jg​​ is irreducible: an invariant subspace for Jg​ would be invariant for N, whose restriction already affords the irreducible θ. Its determinant is μJg​​. By uniqueness on Jg​, it equals χg​. Therefore
fE​=(χJ​)E​,
(45)
an ordinary character and hence a generalised character of E. Brauer's characterisation of characters now makes f a generalised character of G. If n∈N, then Jn​=N and χn​=θ, so f(n)=θ(n). We have proved, by gluing determinant-normalized character extensions,
χ:=f is a generalised character of G,χN​=θ.​
(46)
Only the intermediate quotients Jg​/N and NE/N were required to be solvable; no solvability assumption on G/N has been added.
For the prime-set assertion, let E=P×C be p-elementary, and split the cyclic group as C=Cπ​×Cπ′​, where each factor has the indicated prime divisors in its order. If p∈π, put Eπ​=P×Cπ​ and Eπ′​=Cπ′​. If p∈/π, put Eπ​=Cπ​ and Eπ′​=P×Cπ′​. In both cases the factors commute, have trivial intersection and have orders with disjoint prime spectra. This proves the prime-set decomposition of elementary groups
E=Eπ​×Eπ′​,Eπ​ a π-group,Eπ′​ a π′-group.​
(47)
Here a pi-group has order divisible only by primes in π, and a pi-element has such an element order; the identity qualifies for both complementary prime sets.
Finally, suppose K satisfies the given element-order condition. The sets A,B are unions of conjugacy classes and are disjoint, because membership in both would force every prime divisor of the element order into π∩π′=∅, hence force order 1. Let E≤K be elementary and use E=Eπ​×Eπ′​. If both factors were nontrivial, choose a=1 in the first and b=1 in the second. They commute and have coprime orders, so ab has order ∣a∣∣b∣, containing primes from both π and π′. It would belong to neither A nor B nor {1}, contrary to the hypothesis. Thus every elementary subgroup is entirely a pi-group or entirely a complementary-prime group.
Write m=∣K∣π​ and r=∣K∣π′​ for the two prime parts of ∣K∣, so ∣K∣=mr and gcd(m,r)=1. The Chinese remainder theorem supplies an integer D satisfying
D≡1(modm),D≡0(modr).
(48)
Define the class function
f(1)=D,f(a)=1 (a∈A),f(b)=0 (b∈B).
(49)
For an elementary π-subgroup E, its order divides m. Let ρE​ be the character of its regular representation, equal to ∣E∣ at 1 and zero elsewhere. Then
fE​=1E​+∣E∣D−1​ρE​,
(50)
with integral coefficient because D≡1(mod∣E∣). For an elementary π′-subgroup, its order divides r, and
fE​=∣E∣D​ρE​
(51)
again has integral coefficient. The trivial subgroup satisfies either formula. Every elementary restriction is therefore a generalised character. A second application of Brauer's characterisation of characters proves
ξ:=f is a generalised character of K,ξ(a)=1 (a∈A),ξ(b)=0 (b∈B).​
(52)
The freely chosen identity value, fixed by the two congruences, makes the restrictions integral. This construction also covers the cases π=∅, π′=∅, or K={1}, using modulus-one congruences where appropriate.
