Bn(r)​(f,x)=(n)r​j=0∑n−r​Δ1/nr​f(j/n)pn−r,j​(x),(n)r​=(n−r)!n!​.​

(9)
All sampled arguments lie in [0,1], since the largest is (n−r)t/n+r/n≤1. The rescaling is essential: the sampling meshes are 1/n and 1/(n−r), and the function gn,r​ depends on n. For n=r, the derivative is the constant n!Δ1/nn​f(0), interpreted as a degree-zero Bernstein polynomial.
Now fix r and assume f∈Cr[0,1]. The supplied uniform limit of normalized finite differences, together with (n)r​/nr→1 and uniform continuity of f(r), shows that

∥Bm​h−h∥∞​≤ω(h,δ)+2mδ2∥h∥∞​​.

(12)

Use the standard partition normalization of the B-splines, so Nj​≥0 and the subpartition of unity for B-splines gives ∑j=1n​Nj​(t)≤1. Write the interpolating spline as Px​f=∑j​cj​Nj​ and the sampled values as yi​=f(xi​). The interpolation equations are Ax​c=y, hence c=Ax−1​y whenever the stated interpolation map is well defined. For every t,
∣Px​f(t)∣≤∑j​∣cj​∣Nj​(t)≤∥c∥ℓ∞​∑j​Nj​(t)≤∥c∥ℓ∞​.
(13)
The sampling map also satisfies ∥y∥ℓ∞​≤∥f∥∞​. Taking supremum norms and then the operator norm therefore proves
∥Px​∥L∞→L∞​≤∥Ax−1​∥ℓ∞→ℓ∞​.​
(14)
This is the B-spline interpolation operator norm bound, and it applies on any interval containing the sampling sites on which these normalized B-splines are used.
There is a small qualification to the printed hypotheses: diagonal positivity alone does not ensure uniqueness unless the usual site assumptions are supplied. For example, with two quadratic B-splines on knots 1,2,3,4,5 and x1​=x2​=3, both diagonal values equal 1/2, but Ax​ has two identical rows and is singular. The intended increasing sites x1​<⋯<xn​ with Ni​(xi​)>0 satisfy the Schoenberg–Whitney theorem, making Ax​ invertible; alternatively invertibility is implicit in the premise that Px​ is the unique interpolation map. The displayed bound is proved under that premise, rather than inferring invertibility from diagonal positivity alone.

Let u∈U, r=f−u, M=∥r∥∞​, and E={x∈[0,1]:∣r(x)∣=M}. The Kolmogorov criterion for uniform approximation says
u is a best approximant⟺∀v∈U,x∈Emin​r(x)v(x)≤0.​
(23)
Equivalently, no available perturbation v has strictly the same sign as the nonzero residual at every extremal point. This statement concerns a candidate u in the linear subspace; it does not assume every subspace has an attained best approximation. If M=0, u=f is already exact and the criterion is automatic.
To see sufficiency, for any competitor u+v, the criterion supplies an x∈E with r(x)v(x)≤0. Then ∣f(x)−u(x)−v(x)∣≥∣r(x)∣=M, so the competitor cannot have smaller supremum norm error.
For necessity, suppose M>0 and some v∈U has r(x)v(x)>0 on E. Since E is compact, this product has a positive minimum there. By continuity, a neighborhood O of E has rv≥η>0. For sufficiently small ε>0,
∣r−εv∣2=r2−2εrv+ε2v2<M2on O.
(24)
On the compact complement of O, ∣r∣ has a strict gap below M, and taking ε∥v∥∞​ smaller than this gap preserves it. Hence u+εv improves the uniform error everywhere, contradicting best uniform approximation.

For a real continuous function f, the Chebyshev alternation theorem asserts that a polynomial p∈Pn​ is a best uniform approximation exactly when either it is exact or its residual r=f−p has n+2 ordered alternating extrema:
0≤x0​<⋯<xn+1​≤1,r(xj​)=s(−1)j∥r∥∞​,s∈{1,−1}.​
(25)
Here Pn​ means degree at most n, as required for a linear subspace.
For sufficiency, if a polynomial perturbation v∈Pn​ violated the Kolmogorov criterion for uniform approximation, then r(xj​)v(xj​)>0 at every displayed point. Thus v(xj​) would alternate signs, forcing a distinct zero in each of the n+1 intervals (xj​,xj+1​) by the intermediate value theorem. A nonzero polynomial of degree at most n cannot have that many zeros; the zero polynomial does not violate the criterion either.
For necessity, assume M=∥r∥∞​>0 and no such alternating sequence exists. The two compact sets E+​={r=M} and E−​={r=−M} are disjoint and, when both are nonempty, have positive separation. Hence the extremal set has finitely many successive sign runs: infinitely many alternations would contradict that separation on the bounded interval. If there are m changes of sign between those runs, then selecting one point from each run gives an alternating sequence of length m+1, so m≤n. Choose a point zj​ in the gap between each pair of consecutive opposite-sign runs, and set
v(x)=C∏j=1m​(x−zj​).
(26)
Choose the sign of C so that v agrees with r on the first run. The roots lie outside the extremal set and each changes the sign, so rv>0 at every extremal point. Since degv=m≤n, this contradicts the Kolmogorov criterion for uniform approximation. Therefore a best polynomial must have the required alternation.
Existence also holds: a minimizing sequence has bounded supremum norms, hence bounded coefficients by evaluating at n+1 fixed distinct points and inverting the resulting Vandermonde matrix. A convergent subsequence of coefficients yields a minimizer. Finally, if p and q are both best, their average is best; at its alternating extrema both individual residuals must equal the same extremal value. Consequently p−q has at least n+2 zeros and is identically zero. The best polynomial exists, is unique, and is characterized by alternation.

Fix t and let Ft​(u)=(u−t)+k−1​. Initially assume distinct spline knots, so the ordinary polynomial interpolation definitions are unambiguous. The two interpolants ℓi​(⋅,t) and ℓi+1​(⋅,t) agree with Ft​ at their k−1 shared nodes ti+1​,…,ti+k−1​. Their difference has degree at most k−1 and all these roots, hence
ℓi+1​(x,t)−ℓi​(x,t)=ci​(t)∏r=1k−1​(x−ti+r​)=ci​(t)ωi​(x).
(27)
For k=1 the product is empty and the same statement concerns constants. The leading coefficient of an interpolating polynomial is its highest-order divided difference, so the divided difference recurrence gives
ci​(t)​=[ti+1​,…,ti+k​]Ft​−[ti​,…,ti+k−1​]Ft​=(ti+k​−ti​)[ti​,…,ti+k​]Ft​=Ni​(t).​
(28)
This proves Lee's formula:
ℓi+1​(x,t)−ℓi​(x,t)=ωi​(x)Ni​(t).​
(29)
Summing over i telescopes to ℓn+1​(x,t)−ℓ1​(x,t). If tk​<t<tn+1​, the first interpolation nodes t1​,…,tk​ are all smaller than t, so their truncated-power data vanish and ℓ1​=0. The last nodes tn+1​,…,tn+k​ are larger than t, so their data are values of the polynomial (u−t)k−1 and uniqueness gives ℓn+1​(x,t)=(x−t)k−1. Thus the telescoped identity is
(x−t)k−1=i=1∑n​ωi​(x)Ni​(t),tk​<t<tn+1​,​
(30)
the Marsden identity, valid for every real x.
If repeated spline knots are allowed, ordinary value interpolation at repeated nodes does not specify ℓi​ uniquely. The standard extension uses confluent divided differences and Hermite interpolation, or limits of distinct knots: the same polynomial identity then persists wherever these quantities are defined. At a maximally repeated knot where the truncated power lacks the required derivative, the appropriate one-sided spline convention must be fixed; an unspecified classical derivative is not part of the proof. For distinct knots, the printed identity requires no such qualification.

For f∈C(T), define Ej​(f)=infT∈Tj​​∥f−T∥∞​, where Tj​ is the space of degree-at-most-j trigonometric polynomials. The first inverse theorem for trigonometric approximation states that a universal constant C satisfies
ω(f,1/n)≤nC​j=0∑n​Ej​(f),n≥1.​
(33)
For perspective, take best approximants at dyadic degrees and telescope their differences. Each difference between consecutive approximants has supremum norm at most twice the earlier error, and the Bernstein inequality for trigonometric polynomials multiplies that bound by its degree when differentiating. The resulting dyadic sum is bounded by the displayed sum because Ej​ decreases with j; the remaining approximation error is also bounded by an average of earlier errors.
Now suppose Ej​(f)≤Kj−α for large j, absorbing finitely many earlier terms into a constant. If 0<α<1, comparison with an integral gives ∑j=1n​j−α=O(n1−α). If α=1, the same comparison gives ∑j=1n​j−1=O(1+lnn). Substitution proves the requested two rates:
ω(f,1/n)={O(n−α),O(lnn/n),​0<α<1,α=1(n≥2).​​
