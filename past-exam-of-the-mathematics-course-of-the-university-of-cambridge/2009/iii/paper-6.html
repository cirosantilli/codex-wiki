First consider finite-dimensional Lie algebra representations. The operators representing an abelian Lie algebra commute. They have a common eigenvector: take an eigenspace for the first operator of a basis of the algebra, observe that every remaining operator preserves it, and repeat inside this nonzero space. The resulting line is an invariant subspace, so an irreducible representation must be one-dimensional. Its action has the form xv=λ(x)v for a linear functional λ. Conversely, every such functional defines a Lie algebra representation, since both the Lie brackets in the algebra and the commutators of its scalar operators vanish. Distinct functionals give nonisomorphic representations. Thus the irreducibles are precisely the characters λ∈g∗.
Even without a finite-dimensional assumption on the module, this conclusion for a finite-dimensional abelian Lie algebra follows from the Weak Hilbert Nullstellensatz: its universal enveloping algebra is the polynomial algebra Sym(g). A simple module over a commutative algebra is its quotient by a maximal ideal, and every maximal ideal of this polynomial algebra over C has residue field C.
Indecomposable does not imply irreducible. For the one-dimensional abelian Lie algebra Cx, let x act on C2 by the Nilpotent Jordan block
N=(00​10​).
(1)
The line spanned by the first coordinate vector is an invariant subspace, so the module is reducible. If it were a direct sum of two nonzero submodules, both would have dimension one; nilpotence would force N to act as zero on both, contrary to N=0. Thus it is an indecomposable representation.
For a finite-dimensional module V, put d=dimV and define its generalized weight spaces intrinsically by
Vλ={v∈V:(ρ(x)−λ(x)I)dv=0 for every x∈g}.
(2)
To obtain the decomposition, choose a basis x1​,…,xr​ of g, split into generalized eigenspaces of ρ(x1​), and successively split each piece by ρ(x2​),…,ρ(xr​). All the pieces are submodules because the acting operators commute. On one resulting piece, ρ(xj​) has a unique eigenvalue λj​. The commuting operators can be simultaneously upper triangularized by repeating the common-eigenvector argument on quotients. Hence on this piece every ρ(xj​)−λj​I is strictly upper triangular. Any linear combination is also strictly upper triangular, and its dth power vanishes. Extending λj​ linearly therefore identifies the piece with Vλ. Different tuples give disjoint pieces; if a component has a different tuple from λ, at least one ρ(xj​)−λ(xj​)I is invertible there. Consequently
V=λ∈g∗⨁​Vλ,​
(3)
with only finitely many nonzero summands. Since the definition uses every x rather than a chosen basis, this generalized-weight decomposition for a nilpotent Lie algebra is canonical. Its summands need not be irreducible, as the Jordan block example already shows.

The derived series of a Lie algebra is
b(0)=b,b(j+1)=[b(j),b(j)].
(4)
The algebra is a solvable Lie algebra if b(m)=0 for some m. Here a bracket of subspaces means the linear span of their Lie brackets.
The Lie theorem states that a nonzero finite-dimensional representation of a complex solvable Lie algebra has a common eigenvector; equivalently, its operators can be simultaneously upper triangularized. Applied to an Irreducible Lie algebra representation, the common eigenvector gives a nonzero invariant line, which must be the entire module. A scalar action ρ(x)=λ(x) respects the Lie bracket exactly when λ([x,y])=0. Thus, in the finite-dimensional setting,
irreducibles are Cλ​,λ∈(b/[b,b])∗.​
(5)
Every functional displayed gives a one-dimensional irreducible, and two such modules are isomorphic exactly when their functionals agree.
The finite-dimensional qualification is essential here, although the printed wording does not repeat it. For example, let [x,y]=y and act on C[t] by xf(t)=tf(t) and yf(t)=f(t−1). These operators satisfy [x,y]f=yf. A nonzero submodule stable under x is an ideal p(t)C[t]. Stability under y forces p(t)∣p(t−1), so the two polynomials, having the same degree and leading coefficient, are equal. In characteristic zero a polynomial invariant under a nonzero shift is constant. The module is therefore an infinite-dimensional simple module for the two-dimensional affine Lie algebra. It rules out extending the one-dimensional classification to unrestricted representations.

Let B be the given invariant bilinear form on a Lie algebra. Choose a basis x1​,…,xd​ and its B-dual basis x1,…,xd, so B(xi​,xj)=δij​. The Casimir element is
ΩB​=i∑​xi​xi∈U(g).​
(6)
The tensor ∑i​xi​⊗xi is the inverse tensor of B, so it is independent of the chosen basis. Multiplication in the universal enveloping algebra therefore makes ΩB​ independent of that choice as well.
For z∈g, write [z,xi​]=∑j​aji​xj​. Invariance means B([z,x],y)+B(x,[z,y])=0, whence [z,xi]=−∑j​aij​xj. Therefore
[z,ΩB​]=∑i,j​aji​xj​xi−∑i,j​aij​xi​xj=0,
(7)
by interchanging i,j in the second sum. Since g generates its universal enveloping algebra, ΩB​ is central. This argument does not require adding a symmetry assumption to the given bilinear form.
For the sl2 Lie algebra, take
e=(00​10​),f=(01​00​),h=(10​0−1​),B(X,Y)=tr(XY).
(8)
The cyclic identity for the trace gives B([Z,X],Y)+B(X,[Z,Y])=0. The form is nondegenerate, since B(e,f)=1, B(h,h)=2, and all the other pairings except B(f,e) are zero. The dual of (e,f,h) is (f,e,h/2), so
ΩB​=ef+fe+21​h2.
(9)
By the classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations, an irreducible of dimension n has a highest-weight vector v with ev=0 and hv=mv, where m=n−1. Since ef=fe+h, one obtains
ΩB​v=(m+21​m2)v.
(10)
Centrality and the Schur lemma then give the answer on the entire representation:
ΩB​=2n2−1​I.​
(11)
This Casimir eigenvalue for sl2 depends on normalization. Choosing the Killing form instead, K=4B, scales the dual basis and the Casimir element by 1/4, giving (n2−1)I/8.

Let U be a four-dimensional symplectic vector space, with a symplectic basis e1​,f1​,e2​,f2​. Its invariant inverse-form bivector may be chosen as η=e1​∧f1​+e2​∧f2​. Put vol=e1​∧f1​∧e2​∧f2​, so η∧η=2vol. Define a symmetric bilinear form on the six-dimensional exterior square by
ξ∧ζ=B(ξ,ζ)vol.
(21)
It is symmetric because both factors have degree two. It is nondegenerate: in the six-element wedge basis, every ea​∧eb​ pairs nontrivially with the wedge of the complementary pair and with no other basis vector. Since B(η,η)=2, the orthogonal complement
W=η⊥⊂Λ2U
(22)
has dimension five and inherits a nondegenerate bilinear form. It is the primitive exterior square, equivalently the kernel of the symplectic contraction of an exterior square.
The symplectic Lie algebra preserves η. It also preserves vol, since its elements have trace zero and the induced action on Λ4U is multiplication by that trace. The exterior-power Lie algebra representation therefore preserves B and W, giving a Lie algebra homomorphism
Φ:sp4​⟶so(W,B).
(23)
To show injectivity, suppose A acts as zero on W. It already acts as zero on the invariant line Cη, so it kills all of Λ2U=W⊕Cη. For any basis u1​,…,u4​, this means
Aui​∧uj​+ui​∧Auj​=0(i=j).
(24)
If k=i, choose j distinct from i,k; the coefficient of uk​∧uj​ forces Aki​=0. Thus A is diagonal. The same identity gives Aii​+Ajj​=0 for every pair. Applying this to three distinct indices forces each diagonal entry to vanish. Hence A=0 and Φ is injective.
Finally dimsp4​=2(2⋅2+1)=10 and dimso(W,B)=5⋅4/2=10. Injectivity between these equal-dimensional vector spaces gives surjectivity. Every nondegenerate complex symmetric bilinear form on a five-dimensional space is equivalent to the standard one. We have therefore constructed the exceptional isomorphism between sp4 and so5:
sp4​≅so5​.​
(25)

Identify the Cartan subalgebra with the coordinate space (t1​,…,tn​) used above. The root reflection in any root of a root system fixes the perpendicular hyperplane. For every root of the Cn root system, its complete coordinate action is
α±(εi​−εj​)±(εi​+εj​)±2εi​​sα​(t)ti​↔tj​(ti​,tj​)↦(−tj​,−ti​)ti​↦−ti​​​

Every unlisted coordinate is fixed, and changing the sign of the root leaves its reflection unchanged. These formulas follow directly from sα​(t)=t−α(t)α∨, with the coroot regarded as a vector of the Cartan subalgebra.
The Cn Weyl group is the signed symmetric group: its elements permute the n coordinates and independently change their signs. The Weyl reflections associated with the simple roots interchange consecutive coordinates for i<n and negate the last coordinate for i=n. Thus
W(Cn​)≅(Z/2Z)n⋊Sn​,∣W∣=2nn!.​

Use the ordered alphabet
A=(1,2,…,n,nˉ,n−1​,…,1ˉ),
(28)
with weights wt(i)=εi​ and wt(iˉ)=−εi​. The crystal of the defining symplectic representation is the chain
11​22​⋯n−1​nn​nˉn−1​⋯2​2ˉ1​1ˉ.
(29)
The label of an arrow is its simple root color; a Kashiwara operator f​i​ follows an arrow of color i and lowers the weight by αi​.
Here is a grid description of the entire tensor product of crystals, valid for every n. Put one vertex a⊗b at row a and column b, in the alphabet order above. Let Pi​ be the set of chain positions with outgoing color i, and Qi​ the positions with incoming color i:
Pi​={i,2n−i},Qi​={i+1,2n−i+1}(i<n),Pn​={n},Qn​={n+1}.
(30)
Their indicators are φi​ and εi​. Use the crystal tensor-product rule
f​i​(a⊗b)={f​i​(a)⊗b,a⊗f​i​(b),​φi​(a)>εi​(b),φi​(a)≤εi​(b).​
(31)
An arrow is omitted if the selected factor has no outgoing arrow of that color. Thus the complete grid has a downward color-i arrow when a∈Pi​ and b∈/Qi​, and a rightward color-i arrow when b∈Pi​ and a∈/Pi​. There are no other arrows. These two coordinate rules specify every vertex and every edge of the requested general-rank drawing. The following figure displays the full grid at n=3, including all components, together with the standard chain; increasing n uses exactly the same grid construction.
For completeness, highest vertices can be read from the raising rule, which selects the first factor when φi​(a)≥εi​(b). If a has an incoming edge of color i, then φi​(a)=0; either the raising operator acts nontrivially on a, or εi​(b)=1 and it acts nontrivially on b. Thus a highest vertex must have a=1. At a=1, all φi​(a) vanish except φ1​(a)=1. To be highest, the incoming color of b, if any, must consequently be 1. For n≥2, the possibilities are exactly
1⊗1,1⊗2,1⊗1ˉ.
(32)
Their weights are 2ε1​=2ω1​, ε1​+ε2​=ω2​, and zero. Each occurs once. Connected highest-weight components of the crystal basis describe the irreducible representations in the tensor product of Lie algebra representations, so
V⊗V≅L(2ω1​)⊕L(ω2​)⊕L(0)(n≥2).​
(33)
This is the tensor-square decomposition of the defining symplectic representation. The symmetric square is L(2ω1​); the exterior square is the direct sum of its invariant inverse-form line and the primitive exterior square L(ω2​). Their dimensions are respectively n(2n+1), n(2n−1)−1, and one, summing to 4n2=dim(V⊗V). At n=1, the chain has two vertices and the highest tensor vertices are only 1⊗1 and 1⊗1ˉ: the decomposition is L(2ω1​)⊕L(0), of dimensions three and one.

Let g be a complex semisimple Lie algebra, choose a Cartan subalgebra and positive root system R+, and put ρ=21​∑α∈R+​α. For a dominant integral weight λ, let L(λ) be the finite-dimensional highest-weight representation with highest weight λ. The Weyl dimension formula is
dimL(λ)=α∈R+∏​⟨ρ,α∨⟩⟨λ+ρ,α∨⟩​.​
(34)
Here α∨=2α/(α,α) is the coroot, the pairing is between a weight and a coroot, and ρ is the Weyl vector. The inner product can be any Weyl group invariant normalization on each simple factor: its scaling cancels from the ratios. The dominance condition means that ⟨λ,αi∨​⟩ is a nonnegative integer for each simple root.

Use the generators e,f,h of the sl2 Lie algebra with [h,e]=2e, [h,f]=−2f, and [e,f]=h. For a finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation, h is diagonalizable with integral weights. Its formal character of a weight module is
chV=m∈Z∑​(dimVm​)qm,Vm​={v:hv=mv}.​
(40)
Here q is a formal variable; equivalently the expression is trV​(qh), or the formal sum ∑m​(dimVm​)em with em=qm. This records every weight multiplicity, rather than only the dimension.
The classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations says that Ld​, the irreducible of highest weight d≥0, has the weight string d,d−2,…,−d, each with multiplicity one. Hence
chLd​=qd+qd−2+⋯+q−d=q−q−1qd+1−q−(d+1)​.
(41)
The quotient denotes the displayed Laurent polynomial and has the removable value d+1 at q=1. Formal characters are additive on direct sums, so these formulas determine the character of every finite-dimensional module.

Choose a Cartan subalgebra, simple roots α1​,…,αr​, and root vectors ei​,fi​ normalized by [ei​,fi​]=hi​=αi∨​. Let
ρ∨=21​∑α∈R+​α∨,h=2ρ∨=∑i​ci​hi​,e=∑i​ei​,f=∑i​ci​fi​.
(42)
Here the coroots lie in the Cartan subalgebra, and αi​(ρ∨)=1. This last identity is the Weyl vector identity applied to the dual root system. For distinct simple roots, αi​−αj​ is not a root, hence [ei​,fj​]=0. Consequently
[e,f]=∑i​ci​hi​=h,[h,e]=2e,[h,f]=−2f.
(43)
These are exactly the sl2 Lie algebra relations. For nonzero g the resulting homomorphism from sl2​ is nonzero and therefore injective, since sl2​ is simple. Its image is a Principal sl2 subalgebra. Equivalently its raising element is a principal nilpotent element, a nilpotent element with Lie algebra centralizer of dimension equal to the rank. The construction works for a semisimple Lie algebra with several simple factors by taking the triple in every factor, giving one diagonally embedded sl2​. This is the construction of a principal sl2 triple.
For a representation V, restrict to this subalgebra and take its formal character. If Vμ​ is the weight space for the original Cartan subalgebra, the resulting q-character of a highest-weight representation, or the analogous q-character for any finite-dimensional representation, is
chq​V=trV​(q2ρ∨)=μ∑​dim(Vμ​)qμ(2ρ∨).​
(44)
It is independent of conjugating the chosen principal triple. In particular a root β=∑i​ki​αi​ has grading β(h)=2∑i​ki​. Some conventions use qh/2 instead; that convention divides all exponents here by two. The normalization above agrees with the ordinary sl2 Lie algebra character in the preceding part.
