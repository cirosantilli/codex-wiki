The allowed algebraic characterization is the Boone-Higman theorem:
G finitely generated has soluble word problem ⟺ G↪S↪P,S simple,P finitely presented.​
(10)
The simple group S need not itself be finitely presented. The primary statement is Theorem I of An algebraic characterization of groups with soluble word problem. In particular, a finitely generated group with insoluble word problem for a group cannot embed in any finitely presented simple group: otherwise take that group as both S and P in the characterization.
Use the following effective form of the permitted Messuage lemma for groups. Given a finite group presentation of K and a word w, one can algorithmically write a finite presentation Kw​ such that w=1 in K implies Kw​=1, while w=1 implies that K embeds in Kw​. The construction is performed without first deciding whether w is trivial. A collapse-and-embedding lemma with these properties is also stated as Lemma 5.13 in Combinatorial Group Theory. No proof of that allowed lemma is needed here.
Fix a finitely presented group U with insoluble word problem for a group, whose existence is allowed. For each input word w in its generators, construct Uw​ by the lemma and then form the finite presentation of
Pw​=Uw​∗C2​.
(11)
If w=1, then Pw​≅C2​, which is a nontrivial simple group. If w=1, the lemma embeds U in Uw​, and the normal form theorem for a free product embeds Uw​ in Pw​. If Pw​ were simple, it would be a finitely presented simple group containing U, contradicting the characterization. Hence
Pw​ is simple ⟺ w=1 in U.​
(12)
An algorithm deciding simplicity from arbitrary finite presentations would therefore solve the insoluble word problem for a group in U. This proves simplicity of finitely presented groups is algorithmically undecidable. Using C2​ avoids any convention about whether the trivial group is called simple.
