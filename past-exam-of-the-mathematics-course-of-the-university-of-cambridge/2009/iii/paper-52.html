The target specifies the desired output; the controller selects a control input; an actuator realizes it on the system; and the sensor returns a measured output. The environment supplies additional interactions and disturbances. A simple loop is:
For a quantum sensor, a measurement in quantum mechanics has statistical outcomes and generally changes the state through measurement backaction. For example, outcome m of an instrument with operator Km​ has probability and conditional state
pm​=Tr(Km​ρKm†​),ρm​=pm​Km​ρKm†​​.
(1)
A single unknown quantum state cannot generally supply its complete instantaneous state vector to a controller. Noncommuting observables cannot be read simultaneously with arbitrary precision, and reliable quantum state tomography normally needs many reproducible preparations. A conditional state estimate can instead be updated from a continuous record, with its disturbance included in the model.
Quantum actuators change a Quantum Hamiltonian through applied electromagnetic fields, tunable interactions or couplings to auxiliary systems. A closed-system actuator implements unitary operators, so it preserves the spectrum and purity of a density operator; it cannot arbitrarily overwrite a state as a classical assignment operation could. Dissipative actuators or measurement in quantum measurements can change these invariants, but their dynamics must be accounted for.
The environment can entangle with the system, causing decoherence and dissipation when unobserved degrees of freedom are traced out. It is consequently part of the dynamical model, rather than just an additive classical disturbance. Conversely, Markovian reservoir engineering uses such coupling constructively to prepare and stabilize states.
In open-loop control the applied waveform is prescribed without using measurements from the current evolution. In closed-loop control it depends on the available measurement record or inferred state. Measurement-based quantum feedback is one realization of the latter; experimental adaptation across repeated preparations is another. The measurement record is classical information, but its production has quantum backaction.

Three standard objectives are state preparation, observable optimization and process implementation. All optimizations are over admissible control inputs and a specified final time T.
For state preparation, steer an initial density operator to a target ρd​. An exact goal is ρf​(T)=ρd​, or one may minimize the squared Hilbert-Schmidt distance
Jstate​(f)=21​Tr[(ρf​(T)−ρd​)2].​
(2)
For a pure target ρd​=∣ψd​⟩⟨ψd​∣, maximizing ⟨ψd​∣ρf​(T)∣ψd​⟩ is a useful alternative. The target must be physically attainable: Hamiltonian engineering alone preserves the initial spectrum of the density operator.
For an observable objective, choose a Hermitian operator O and maximize its expected final value:
JO​(f)=Tr[Oρf​(T)]to be maximized.​
(3)
Population transfer is a special case with O the projector onto the desired level or subspace; suppressing an unwanted population reverses the sign or minimizes this quantity.
For coherent process engineering, implement a target unitary operator V on every input, rather than merely arranging one state transfer. If the propagator is Uf​(T), a phase-insensitive error is
Jgate​(f)=1−N2∣Tr[V†Uf​(T)]∣2​.​
(4)
The error vanishes precisely when Uf​(T)=eiϕV: equality in the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product Cauchy-Schwarz inequality bound makes the two operators proportional. For an open-system process, the analogous objective compares the implemented quantum channel with the target channel. These objectives can be supplemented by pulse-energy, amplitude, bandwidth and duration constraints in quantum optimal control.

A reachable set consists of states obtainable from one specified initial state using admissible inputs and allowed durations. Controllability means that every target in the declared state space is reachable from every initial state there; the declared space and time convention are essential.
For a closed quantum system, let Uf​ solve the controlled Schrödinger equation with Uf​(0)=I. The propagator and state reachable sets are
RU​={Uf​(T):f admissible, T allowed},Rρ​(ρ0​)={Uρ0​U†:U∈RU​}.
(5)
A pure-state version acts on normalized vectors up to global phase. Pure-state controllability makes every pure target accessible; unitary operator controllability permits every desired coherent process, with global phase treated according to the definition.
For mixed states, density operator controllability means transitivity on each unitary orbit of a density operator, not access to all density operators regardless of spectrum. The Dynamical Lie algebra generated by −iH0​,−iH1​,… describes the accessible connected unitary transformations under the usual unconstrained-time control assumptions. Conserved quantities or restricted controls can shrink the reachable set. Thus successful preparation of one state is weaker than implementation of an arbitrary gate on all inputs, and coherent controllability does not imply purification.

Hamiltonian engineering chooses accessible interactions so that their actual or effective Quantum Hamiltonian produces the desired evolution. A typical model is Hf​(t)=H0​+∑k​fk​(t)Hk​; the controls may change frequencies, amplitudes, phases or coupling strengths. Three standard model-based open-loop control approaches illustrate different mechanisms.
In resonant quantum control, select a transition with a near-resonant field. In a rotating frame, after neglecting rapidly oscillating terms in a justified regime, an effective two-level drive can be Heff​(t)=Ω(t)σx​/2. When its axis is fixed,
U(T)=exp[−2iσx​​∫0T​Ω(t)dt].
(6)
The pulse area sets the rotation angle and the drive phase selects the transverse axis. A resonant pulse of area π swaps the two level populations. This requires knowledge of transition frequencies, couplings and the validity of the selective-drive approximation.
In adiabatic quantum control, design a slowly varying Quantum Hamiltonian whose desired instantaneous eigenstate connects the initial and final states. For a nondegenerate eigenstate separated by a gap, the quantum adiabatic theorem explains why sufficiently slow evolution follows that state up to a phase. The relative size of matrix elements of H˙ and squared spectral gaps controls the approximation; degeneracies or rapid changes invalidate simple following. This engineers a state path rather than a prescribed resonant rotation.
In average Hamiltonian engineering, apply a predetermined sequence of fast pulses Pj​ and let the system evolve for intervals τj​ in the corresponding frames. For cycle length Tc​=∑j​τj​, the leading effective generator is
H(0)=Tc​1​∑j​τj​Pj†​H0​Pj​.
(7)
Choose the sequence to cancel unwanted terms or retain desired couplings. For instance, equal intervals under H0​=ωσz​/2 and its σx​ conjugate cancel the leading generator because σx​σz​σx​=−σz​. Noncommuting terms produce higher-order commutator corrections, so fast cycling, finite pulse errors and the accuracy of the model matter. All three methods determine a waveform or sequence before its execution; none automatically corrects an unknown mismatch during that run.

Replace evaluation in a possibly inaccurate dynamical model by an experimental objective. Parametrize an admissible pulse as f(t;a), with a collecting time-slice amplitudes or basis-function coefficients. Prepare the same initial state, apply the pulse, measure performance, and update a before the next run. This is adaptive experimental quantum control: the loop closes through experimental results, even though each individual pulse is applied open loop.
For example, minimize
Jexp​(a)=1−Tr[ρd​ρa​(T)]+η∫0T​∥f(t;a)∥2dt,a∈A,​
(8)
for a pure target ρd​, with a nonempty closed convex set A enforcing amplitude and bandwidth bounds. The overlap is an experimentally estimable probability. An observable objective can use its measured expectation instead; a gate objective generally needs more input states and measurement settings. The true dynamics constrain the physically produced ρa​(T), but no accurate formula for those dynamics is required to evaluate this objective.
A direct search compares measured scores for candidate pulses, accepts improvements and refines the search region. Alternatively estimate a gradient experimentally by finite differences,
∂ak​​J​=2εJ(a+εek​)−J(a−εek​)​,an+1​=ΠA​(an​−γn​∇J),
(9)
where ΠA​ is the Euclidean projection onto a convex set. Population-based searches can also explore several candidates in parallel. These methods use experimental data rather than simulated propagation or a model-derived adjoint gradient.
Each score needs enough repeated preparations to manage measurement noise; uncertainty in score differences should determine replication and stopping decisions. Excessively small finite-difference steps amplify that noise. A model-based pulse can provide a useful starting point, but adaptation corrects its systematic mismatch using the device itself. This mitigates model uncertainty, not arbitrary fluctuations: reproducible preparation, calibrated measurements and sufficiently slow drift remain necessary. Nor does a local numerical search guarantee a global optimum.

A basis of Hermitian matrices is a real basis for the Hermitian subspace, but it also supplies a complex basis for all matrices. Indeed every operator has the decomposition
A=B+iC,B=2A+A†​,C=2iA−A†​,
(10)
with B,C Hermitian. Expand each in the given real basis, then combine their coefficients to expand A. Orthogonality for the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product determines those complex coefficients uniquely:
Tr(σj​A)=∑k​ak​Tr(σj​σk​)=aj​.
(11)
Consequently
A=k=1∑N2​Tr(σk​A)σk​.​
(12)
If A is Hermitian, cyclicity of the trace gives
aj∗​=Tr[(σj​A)†]=Tr(Aσj​)=Tr(σj​A)=aj​.
(13)
Thus its coordinate vector is real. The product σj​A need not itself be Hermitian; trace cyclicity is what justifies the conclusion.

A density operator is Hermitian, positive semidefinite and has trace one. Part (a) therefore makes all its coordinates real, and the identity basis vector fixes the final coordinate:
rN2​=Tr(ρI/N​)=1/N​.​
(14)
The remaining basis vectors are traceless by orthogonality to I/N​. The Generalized Bloch representation is consequently
ρ=NI​+∑k=1N2−1​sk​σk​.
(15)
Using orthonormality to expand the squared Hilbert-Schmidt norm gives the purity bound for generalized Bloch vectors:
Tr(ρ2)=∑k=1N2​rk2​=N1​+∥s∥2.
(16)
If p1​,…,pN​ are its nonnegative eigenvalues, then ∑j​pj​=1 and
1−Tr(ρ2)=(∑j​pj​)2−∑j​pj2​=2∑j<k​pj​pk​≥0.
(17)
It follows that
∥s∥≤1−1/N​.​
(18)
Equality requires every pairwise product pj​pk​ to vanish, so exactly one eigenvalue equals one and all others are zero. Conversely, a rank-one projector has precisely that spectrum and attains equality. Thus equality holds exactly for a pure state. This normalization is different from the commonly used unit-radius qubit Bloch vector.

For N=2, take the normalized Pauli operators as the traceless basis. The matrix associated with a vector s is ρ=I/2+s⋅σ, where these basis matrices are the usual Pauli matrices divided by 2​. Their multiplication relations give
(s⋅σ)2=2∥s∥2​I,specρ={21​+2​∥s∥​, 21​−2​∥s∥​}.
(19)
At radius 1/2​, these eigenvalues are one and zero. Every sphere point therefore gives a positive rank-one projector. Any other orthonormal Hermitian basis merely changes the real coordinates by an orthogonal transformation, so the result is basis independent.
For every N>2, choose a rank-one projector P and consider the Hermitian trace-one matrix
A=N2​I−P.
(20)
Its eigenvalues are 2/N−1 once and 2/N with multiplicity N−1. They sum to one and satisfy
Tr(A2)=(N2​−1)2+(N−1)N24​=1.
(21)
By orthonormality its traceless coordinate vector has squared length 1−1/N, so it is on the specified sphere. But 2/N−1<0, so A is not a density operator. Thus for N>2, the pure states occupy a proper subset of the sphere; positivity excludes some of its points. In fact, this example is the antipodal Bloch vector to that of P.
The usual assertion presumes N≥2. If the trivial dimension N=1 is admitted, the radius-zero sphere consists of the single coordinate point, also corresponding to the single pure state, so that dimension is an additional trivial exception.

For a Hermitian Quantum Hamiltonian, [σm​,σn​]†=−[σm​,σn​]. Consequently
Lmn∗​=−iTr([σm​,σn​]†H)=iTr(H[σm​,σn​])=Lmn​.
(28)
Swapping m,n changes the sign of the commutator, so Lnm​=−Lmn​. The generator is therefore real and antisymmetric. Its last column vanishes because the identity commutes with H, so c=0 and the traceless block satisfies AT=−A.
For constant H, the Bloch vector evolves by s(t)=R(t)s(0) with R(t)=eAt. Direct differentiation gives
dtd​(RTR)=RT(AT+A)R=0,R(0)=I.
(29)
Hence RTR=I. Its determinant is continuously connected to one and cannot change between the two possible values ±1, so detR=1. This proves that the evolution is a rotation in the special orthogonal group. A time-dependent Hermitian Quantum Hamiltonian has the same conclusion from R˙=A(t)R.
These Hamiltonian rotations of Bloch vectors preserve the Euclidean norm and purity of a density operator. For N>2, not every rotation of the ambient sphere is a physical unitary conjugation: the higher-dimensional positivity constraints and full spectrum of the density operator impose further restrictions.

Density operator controllability cannot defeat unitary invariants: it grants access to the unitary orbit of a density operator, whose members have the same eigenvalues, rather than to every density operator. In the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product orthonormal normalization of this question,
∥s0​∥2=2(0.5)2+(0.5)2​=41​,∥s1​∥2=21​.
(30)
Quantum Hamiltonian evolution preserves this norm, so the target is not reachable from the stated initial state. Equivalently, their values of purity of a density operator are 1/2+1/4=3/4 and 1/2+1/2=1: the initial state is a mixed state and the target a pure state. Dissipation, an appropriate measurement protocol or an auxiliary system with additional resources would be needed to change the spectrum; freely controlling the closed-system Quantum Hamiltonian alone does not suffice.

A steady state is a time-independent physical state: its derivative vanishes. With n=N2−1, the Affine Bloch equation therefore requires
As∗​=−c.​
(31)
As a real linear equation, it has a solution exactly when rankA=rank[A∣−c]. If sp​ is one solution, all algebraic solutions are sp​+kerA, an affine set of dimension n−rankA. Physical steady states are its intersection with the set of Bloch vectors corresponding to positive trace-one density operators. An arbitrary affine equation need not have a steady state: A=0, c=0 is a counterexample.
The equation here comes from a finite-dimensional time-independent Lindblad equation, however, so it always has at least one physical steady state. To prove this, start from any density operator and average its trajectory:
ρ​T​=T1​∫0T​ρ(t)dt.
(32)
These averages remain positive with trace one. That set is a compact set, so some sequence has a limit ρ∗​. If L is the time-independent generator, then
L(ρ​T​)=Tρ(T)−ρ(0)​⟶0,
(33)
because density operators are bounded. Continuity gives L(ρ∗​)=0. This proves directly that Finite-dimensional Lindbladians have stationary states.
If A has full rank, its algebraic solution s∗​=−A−1c is unique and existence makes that solution physical. For these Lindblad dynamics the converse also holds, even for a boundary steady state. If A is singular, choose a nonzero traceless Hermitian stationary direction X. The two parts of its positive-negative decomposition have the same trace τ>0, so X=τ(ρ+​−ρ−​) for two density operators. Time-average their trajectories along a common convergent subsequence. Their limits are stationary by the preceding argument, and their difference remains X/τ, since X itself is stationary. The limits are therefore distinct physical stationary states.
Thus the finite-dimensional physical Bloch equation has a unique steady state exactly when rankA=n. These steady states of an affine Bloch equation must be distinguished from convergence to that steady state, which requires the additional spectral condition in (b). The possibly nonorthonormal coordinate basis does not affect existence or uniqueness, since it still gives an invertible parametrization of the traceless Hermitian space.

Subtract a steady state to obtain e(t)=s(t)−s∗​ with e˙=Ae, hence e(t)=eAte(0). The steady state attracts every initial condition exactly when
Reμ<0for every eigenvalue μ of A.​
(34)
In other words, A must be a Hurwitz stable matrix. To see sufficiency without assuming diagonalizability, each block in its Jordan normal form contributes terms of the form tkeμt with finite k. Negative real part makes every such term tend to zero, so the whole matrix exponential tends to zero.
For necessity, an eigenmode with positive real part grows, and a mode with zero real part persists or oscillates instead of decaying. For a complex eigenvalue of a real matrix, take the real and imaginary parts of its eigenvector to obtain real nondecaying solutions. Thus any eigenvalue in the closed right half-plane prevents convergence of all deviations. Physical density operators span the trace-one affine space, so convergence from all physical initial states also forces decay on every traceless direction.
This gives global asymptotic stability for the affine linear system and uniqueness of its steady state. A merely negative-semidefinite symmetric part proves only nonincrease of a particular norm, not this necessary-and-sufficient condition.

σ†σy​=iσ†σ,σy​σ=−iσ†σ.

First fix a source convention needed for physical coordinates. The PDF's displayed definition of σz​ omits a factor of i: half the commutator of the two Hermitian transverse matrices is anti-Hermitian and cannot supply a real Bloch coordinate. The Hermitian choice consistent with the supplied dynamical matrix is
σz​=2i[σx​,σy​]​=(−10​01​).
(39)
With this convention the three Pauli expectation values use the unit-radius normalization, unlike the orthonormal coordinates of Question 2. For example, writing K=(0b​a0​) with a=1+λ, b=−λ, the dissipator gives rates −(a−b)2/2 for x, −(a+b)2/2 for y, and population equation z˙=b2−a2−(a2+b2)z. The Quantum Hamiltonian adds 2αz to x˙ and −2αx to z˙. This independently reproduces the supplied real Bloch dynamics.

q2x∗​−4αz∗​=0,4αx∗​+(q2+1)z∗​=−2q.
