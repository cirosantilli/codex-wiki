Lagrange's theorem says that for a subgroup H of a finite group G,
∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣,∣H∣∣∣G∣.​
(4)
To prove it, the left cosets gH partition G. Indeed every element lies in its own coset; if gH and kH intersect, write gh=kh′ and deduce g=kh′h−1, so gH=kH. Multiplication by g is a bijection from H to gH, and each coset therefore has ∣H∣ elements. Summing over the [G:H] distinct cosets proves the formula.
For the failure of the converse to Lagrange's theorem, take the alternating group A4​, of order 4!/2=12, and the divisor 6. Suppose it had a subgroup H of order six. Its index would be two. An index-two subgroup is normal: for g∈/H, both gH and Hg are the complement of H, while for g∈H they are H. Hence A4​/H would be a group of order two.
Every three-cycle in A4​ has order three. Its image in the quotient has cube equal to the identity and, since the quotient has order two, must itself be the identity. Thus all three-cycles lie in H. There are eight of them: choose the omitted point in four ways and one of two cyclic orders on the other three points. Together with the identity this would give at least nine elements of H, contradicting ∣H∣=6. Therefore A4​ has no subgroup of order six, although six divides its order.
