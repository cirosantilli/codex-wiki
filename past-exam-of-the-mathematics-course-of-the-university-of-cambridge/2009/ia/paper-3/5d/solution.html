For a group action of a finite group G on a set X, let Gx​={g:gx=x} be the stabilizer subgroup and Gx={gx:g∈G} the orbit. The orbit-stabilizer theorem is
∣Gx∣=[G:Gx​],∣G∣=∣Gx∣∣Gx​∣.​
(14)
To prove it, the map from left cosets to the orbit, gGx​↦gx, is well-defined because multiplying g on the right by an element fixing x does not change gx. It is surjective by the definition of the orbit. If gx=hx, then h−1g∈Gx​, so gGx​=hGx​; hence it is injective. The cardinality identity now follows from Lagrange's theorem.
Place the octahedron's vertices at ±e1​,±e2​,±e3​. A symmetry fixes their centre, so it is an orthogonal map and must take an opposite pair of vertices to another opposite pair. Its matrix is thus a signed permutation matrix. Conversely every signed permutation of these coordinate directions preserves the vertices, edges and faces. Therefore the full octahedral symmetry group has
∣G∣=3!23=48.​
(15)
These same signed permutation matrices preserve the cube with vertices (±1,±1,±1), so the group is also the symmetry group of a cube. The opposite-vertex lines are the three coordinate axes, so ∣D∣=3. The action on them is transitive, giving a line stabilizer of order 48/3=16.
For the line Re3​, the precise axis stabilizer in the full octahedral symmetry group consists of the block matrices
(A0​0ε​),A a signed 2×2 permutation matrix,ε∈{1,−1}.
(16)
The eight possible A are the symmetries of a square in the perpendicular plane, a dihedral group of order eight; the independent sign ε reverses the selected axis. Thus
GRe3​​≅D8​×C2​.​
(17)
Here D8​ denotes the order-eight square-symmetry group. The selected line is unoriented: its two endpoints may be exchanged. Fixing an individual endpoint instead would give only the order-eight subgroup with ε=1.
