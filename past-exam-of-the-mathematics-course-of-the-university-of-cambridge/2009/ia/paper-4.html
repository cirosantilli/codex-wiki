Write ΔA​={(x,x):x∈A} and regard each binary relation as a subset of A×A. Then R=Q∪ΔA​ is the reflexive closure of Q: every x∈A satisfies xRx because x=x. The relation S=R∪R−1 is its symmetric closure. It is a reflexive relation since it contains R, and xSy means either xRy or yRx, either of which also gives ySx.
For T, a one-step chain (x,x) is allowed because xSx, so xTx. If (x0​,…,xn​) witnesses xTy, the reversed chain (xn​,…,x0​) witnesses yTx, since S is a symmetric relation. Finally, concatenate a chain from x to y with a chain from y to z to obtain a positive-length chain from x to z. Hence T is a transitive relation as well, and
R is reflexive,S is reflexive and symmetric,T is an equivalence relation.​
(1)
Thus T is the transitive closure of a relation applied to S.
Now suppose E is an equivalence relation containing Q. Its reflexivity implies ΔA​⊆E, hence R⊆E. Its symmetry then implies R−1⊆E, hence S⊆E. Along a chain witnessing xTy, every consecutive pair is E-related. Induction on the chain length using transitivity gives xEy: the one-step case is S⊆E, and the last step joins xExn−1​ to xn−1​Ey. Therefore
T⊆E for every equivalence relation E⊇Q.​
(2)
Together with Q⊆T, this proves that T is exactly the equivalence closure of Q. The reasoning also covers an empty A, where all assertions about elements are vacuous.

A left inverse of f is a function ℓ:B→A satisfying ℓ∘f=idA​. If such ℓ exists and f(a)=f(a′), applying ℓ gives a=a′, so f is an injection. Conversely, suppose f is an injection and fix a0​∈A, possible because A is nonempty. Define
ℓ(b)={a,a0​,​b=f(a) for some a∈A,b∈/f(A).​
(17)
The first value is unique by injectivity, and ℓ(f(a))=a for every a. Thus
f is injective⟺f has a left inverse.​
(18)
No choice from a family of nonempty sets is needed here: each preimage in the first case is unique, and the second case uses one fixed element.
A right inverse is a function r:B→A with f∘r=idB​. Its existence makes every b the image of r(b), proving that f is a surjection. Conversely, if f is a surjection, every fiber of a function f−1({b}) is nonempty. Using the usual axiom of choice for arbitrary sets, choose r(b)∈f−1({b}) for each b. Then f(r(b))=b, giving
f is surjective⟺f has a right inverseassuming choice.​
(19)
The converse in this second equivalence is the right-inverse characterization of the axiom of choice; it is not an unrestricted choice-free theorem about arbitrary sets.

Suppose all infinite binary sequences could be listed as ϵ(1),ϵ(2),…, where ϵ(j)=(ϵ1(j)​,ϵ2(j)​,…). Define another binary sequence by dj​=1−ϵj(j)​. It differs from the jth listed sequence at its jth entry, so it is absent from the list. This contradiction is Cantor's diagonal argument, proving that
{0,1}N is uncountable.​
(31)
To transfer this result to [0,1], map a binary sequence to
f(ϵ)=∑j=1∞​3j2ϵj​​.
(32)
This lies in [0,1] because ∑j≥1​2/3j=1. If two sequences first differ at index j, with ϵj​=1 and ηj​=0, then
f(ϵ)−f(η)≥3j2​−∑n=j+1∞​3n2​=3j1​>0.
(33)
Thus f is an injection, avoiding the ambiguous expansions that would arise from unrestricted binary digits in base two. An injection from an uncountable set into [0,1] proves that [0,1] is uncountable​.
For Σ(Z), a nondecreasing sequence bounded above can take only finitely many integer values, all lying between its first term and an integer upper bound. Every strict increase raises its value by at least one, so only finitely many strict increases occur. It is therefore eventually constant. Every such sequence has the form
(a1​,…,aN​,aN​,aN​,…)
(34)
for some finite list of integers. The set of finite integer lists is a countable set: at stage j, list the tuples of length at most j whose entries have absolute value at most j, omitting earlier duplicates. Each stage is finite and every finite tuple occurs at some stage. Constant sequences give an injection of Z into the collection, so
Σ(Z) is countably infinite.​
(35)
This is the discrete-order phenomenon that bounded nondecreasing integer sequences are eventually constant.
For Σ(Q), use a different encoding, into rational partial sums:
sn​(ϵ)=∑j=1n​3jϵj​​.
(36)
Each sn​ is a rational number, the sequence is nondecreasing, and sn​≤1/2. If two binary inputs first differ at j, their jth partial sums differ by 3−j, so their output sequences differ. This defines an injection of the uncountable binary-sequence set into Σ(Q). Thus bounded nondecreasing rational sequences are uncountable. Finally, every such rational sequence is also an admissible real sequence, so inclusion into Σ(R) is injective. Consequently
Σ(Q) and Σ(R) are both uncountable.​
(37)
Boundedness and monotonicity force eventual constancy for integer values, but not for rational or real values; countability of the set of possible individual terms does not imply countability of all infinite sequences of those terms.

(57)

(58)
The normal force balances the perpendicular component of gravity because the centre remains at a constant distance from the plane. Although the annulus accelerates downhill, friction must act uphill to supply the torque that increases its rolling angular speed. For a plane with 0≤α<π/2, the assumed no-slip motion requires a coefficient of static friction satisfying μs​≥F/N=(5/13)tanα. With that condition, contact friction does no work because the instantaneous contact point is stationary; it redistributes the gravitational energy between translational and rotational kinetic energy.
