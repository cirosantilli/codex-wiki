A branch of the complex logarithm must be specified to obtain a unique locus. First use the principal complex logarithm. Away from the negative real axis, write z=reiθ with r>0 and −π<θ<π. Then the complex logarithms of z and its complex conjugate are logr+iθ and logr−iθ. Equating real and imaginary parts gives logr=θ. Consequently the principal-branch locus is the open segment of a logarithmic spiral
z=e(1+i)θ,−π<θ<π.​
(5)
The origin is excluded because its complex logarithm does not exist. If the principal value is assigned argument π on the negative real axis, a point there equals its own complex conjugate, so its principal complex logarithm would have to equal i times itself. That forces the complex logarithm to be zero and cannot hold there; the endpoints remain excluded.
If the printed equation instead means that some values of the multivalued complex logarithms satisfy it, write those values as logr+it and logr+iu, with eit=z/r and eiu=z/r. Equality gives logr=−u=t, hence u=−t, which is compatible with conjugation. Every real t is allowed. Under this interpretation the locus is the entire logarithmic spiral z=e(1+i)t, t∈R. The PDF does not choose between these branch conventions.

The intermediate value theorem says that if f:[a,b]→R is a continuous function, every value between f(a) and f(b) is attained at some point of [a,b].
For a proof, endpoint values are immediate, and replacing f by −f reduces the remaining case to f(a)<y<f(b). The set S={x∈[a,b]:f(x)≤y} is nonempty and bounded above; let c=supS by the least-upper-bound property. Continuity and the strict endpoint inequalities ensure a<c<b: near a there are points in S, and near b there are none. If f(c)>y, continuity gives a neighborhood of c containing no points of S, contradicting the defining approximation property of the supremum. If f(c)<y, continuity gives points to the right of c still in S, contradicting its being an upper bound. Hence f(c)=y, proving the intermediate value theorem.
For the distance problem, define the continuous function
d(x)=(x−a)2+(f(x)−b)2​.
(56)
It satisfies d(x)≥∣x−a∣, so it is a coercive function: d(x)→∞ as ∣x∣→∞. Choose R>∣a∣+d(0)+1. Outside [−R,R], the distance is greater than d(0). On [−R,R], the extreme value theorem gives a minimizer x0​, with A=d(x0​)≤d(0). It is therefore a global minimizer and A≥0.
For any y>A, choose X>x0​ so large that d(X)>y. Apply the intermediate value theorem to d on [x0​,X] to obtain a point whose distance is y. The distance A itself is attained at x0​, and no smaller value is possible. This is the range of a continuous coercive real function. Thus the distance set is exactly
[A,∞),A=x∈Rmin​(x−a)2+(f(x)−b)2​.​
(57)
In particular A=0 precisely when the fixed point lies on the graph.

Rolle's theorem states that a continuous function f:[a,b]→R, differentiable on (a,b) with a<b and f(a)=f(b), has f′(c)=0 for some c∈(a,b). If f is constant this is immediate. Otherwise the extreme value theorem supplies a maximum or minimum with value different from the common endpoint value, so one such extremum occurs in (a,b). At an interior maximum, the difference quotients for positive increments are nonpositive and those for negative increments are nonnegative. Their common limit, the derivative, must be zero. At a minimum the inequalities reverse, with the same conclusion. This proves Rolle's theorem.
For the two-function identity, write Δf=f(b)−f(a) and Δg=g(b)−g(a), and set ϕ(x)=g(x)Δf−f(x)Δg. This is a continuous function on the closed interval and is differentiable inside it. Its endpoint values agree:
ϕ(a)=ϕ(b)=g(a)f(b)−f(a)g(b).
(58)
They need not be zero, but Rolle's theorem only requires equality. At the resulting point c,
0=ϕ′(c)=g′(c)Δf−f′(c)Δg,
(59)
which proves the Cauchy mean value theorem identity
f′(c)(g(b)−g(a))=g′(c)(f(b)−f(a)).​
(60)
For the final endpoint limit, first state the denominator convention explicitly. In the usual zero-over-zero version of L'Hopital rule, g′(x)=0 on some whole interval (0,δ); this is understood if the displayed derivative quotient is to be a function defined throughout a deleted right neighborhood of zero. Define f(0)=g(0)=0, making both functions continuous at zero. For 0<x<δ, Rolle's theorem implies g(x)=0, since otherwise g would have equal zero values at 0 and x and a zero derivative between them. Apply the proved Cauchy mean value theorem on [0,x] to obtain cx​∈(0,x) with

No continuity of the derivatives is needed.

A Riemann integrable function on a closed interval is bounded and has equal upper and lower Darboux integrals. More explicitly, for a partition of an interval P:a=x0​<x1​<⋯<xm​=b, define the Darboux sums
L(f,P)=∑j=1m​inf[xj−1​,xj​]​f(xj​−xj−1​),U(f,P)=∑j=1m​sup[xj−1​,xj​]​f(xj​−xj−1​).
(65)
Then Riemann integrability means
Psup​L(f,P)=Pinf​U(f,P),​
(66)
and this common value is the Riemann integral. Equivalently, the Riemann integrability criterion requires that for every ε>0 some partition of an interval satisfies U(f,P)−L(f,P)<ε. Indeed, the common partition refinement of two partitions increases each lower Darboux sum and decreases each upper Darboux sum, so equality of the two extremal values is equivalent to arbitrarily small gaps.

Let K be the set where the indicator function f equals one. Fix a positive integer N. Each permitted length-N decimal prefix w=(w1​,…,wN​)∈{2,7}N gives the interval
Iw​=[tw​,tw​+10−N],tw​=∑j=1N​wj​10−j.
(69)
There are 2N such intervals, each of length 10−N, and they cover K. Every point of K is strictly inside the interval for its prefix, since its remaining tail lies between 2/(9⋅10N) and 7/(9⋅10N). Thus none of the interval endpoints belongs to K. The intervals are disjoint and separated: their distinct prefix integers cannot be consecutive because their final digits are only 2 and 7.
The complement consists of finitely many closed intervals on which f=0, of total length 1−2N10−N=1−5−N. Part (b) therefore already proves Riemann integrability, since this length tends to one.
To compute the Riemann integral directly, make a partition of an interval using 0,1 and all the endpoints of the Iw​. On complementary cells the upper Darboux sum contribution is zero, and on the Iw​ it is at most their total length. Moreover every nonempty cell contains a terminating decimal, which is not in K: neither its terminating expansion with a zero tail nor its alternative expansion with a nine tail consists entirely of twos and sevens. Such decimals are dense, so every cell has infimum zero. Therefore
0=L(f,PN​)≤∫01​f(x)dx≤U(f,PN​)≤5−N.
