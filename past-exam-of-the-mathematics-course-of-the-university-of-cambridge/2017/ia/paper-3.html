Use the Riemann sphere convention for evaluating a Möbius transformation at a pole and at infinity. When the three prescribed points are finite, take
f(z)=(w3​−w2​)(z−w1​)(w3​−w1​)(z−w2​)​.
(1)
Its zero is w2​, its pole is w1​, and substitution at w3​ gives one. The remaining three cases are
point at infinityw1​=∞w2​=∞w3​=∞​f(z)(z−w2​)/(w3​−w2​)(w3​−w1​)/(z−w1​)(z−w2​)/(z−w1​).​​​
(2)
All coefficient determinants are nonzero because the points are distinct. There is only one such Möbius transformation: the composite of any two candidate maps, one inverted, fixes ∞,0,1; fixing infinity makes it affine, and fixing zero and one makes it the identity.
For this question use the cross-ratio normalization
[w1​,w2​,w3​,w4​]=f(w4​).​
(3)
In the finite case this is (w3​−w1​)(w4​−w2​)/[(w3​−w2​)(w4​−w1​)], with the corresponding limits if a point is infinite. It is finite and distinct from zero and one, since w4​ is distinct from the other three points and f is a bijection. Ordering conventions for the cross-ratio differ; the definition here is fixed by the specified images, rather than by importing another ordering formula.
A generalized circle means either a circle or a straight line completed by infinity. Let K be the unique generalized circle through the first three points. Its image under f is the unique generalized circle through ∞,0,1, namely R∪{∞}. Therefore w4​∈K exactly when f(w4​) is real. Conversely a real f(w4​) lies on that extended real line, whose inverse image is K. This proves the real cross-ratio criterion for a generalized circle in both directions.

A subgroup H of a group G is a normal subgroup when gHg−1=H for every g∈G, equivalently gH=Hg. The quotient group G/H has the left cosets as elements, with (gH)(kH)=gkH, identity H and inverse (gH)−1=g−1H. Normality makes this multiplication independent of representatives.
The first isomorphism theorem for groups says that for a group homomorphism φ:G→K, its kernel of a group homomorphism is normal and
G/kerφ≅imφ,gkerφ⟼φ(g).
(4)
The image is taken with the induced group operation; surjectivity onto all of K is not required.

The quotient theorem for Cartesian tensors here says: if contracting Tij​ with every vector vj​ gives the components wi​=Tij​vj​ of a vector in every right-handed orthonormal basis, then T obeys the second-order tensor transformation law. Conversely that transformation law guarantees the contraction is a vector. One may equivalently test that ui​Tij​vj​ is a scalar for every pair of vectors u,v.
By part (a), v′=Rv and w′=Rw. The contraction property in the new basis says
T′Rv=w′=Rw=RTv.
(12)
Since this holds for every test vector, T′R=RT. Using R−1=RT gives
T′=RTRT,Tij′​=Rik​Rjℓ​Tkℓ​.​
(13)
For the converse, T′v′=RTRTRv=RTv=Rw, exactly the vector law. For the equivalent scalar formulation, invariance of uTTv for every u,v gives uTRTT′Rv=uTTv, forcing the same matrix identity.
The quantifier “every” matters: an arbitrary nonzero matrix can annihilate one fixed vector, so a single successful contraction cannot establish tensoriality. The stated bases test proper rotations; transformation under orthogonal reflections would require an additional hypothesis if that were wanted. “Second-order” counts tensor indices, rather than the matrix rank of a particular component matrix.

Restrict the quotient projection π:G→G/N to H. This is a group homomorphism with
ker(π∣H​)=H∩N.
(14)
Its image is a nontrivial subgroup of the prime-order quotient group, because H is not contained in N. By Lagrange's theorem for finite groups, the image has order p and is all of G/N. The first isomorphism theorem for groups therefore gives
H∩N⊴H,[H:H∩N]=p.​
(15)
For the second assertion, partition C into conjugacy classes for N. Because N is normal, conjugation by G permutes these smaller classes: gClN​(x)g−1=ClN​(gxg−1). This group action is transitive since any two elements of C are conjugate in G. Elements of N fix every N-class, so the action factors through G/N.
If m is the number of smaller classes, the orbit-stabilizer theorem for this transitive action gives m∣∣G/N∣=p. Thus
C is one N-class, or a disjoint union of p such classes.​
(16)
Distinct conjugacy classes are disjoint because they are orbits of a group action. This proves conjugacy class splitting in a prime-index normal subgroup without assuming all G-conjugations are already conjugations by N.

Lagrange's theorem for finite groups states that for a subgroup H of a finite group G, ∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣. For x∈G, two powers among 1,x,…,x∣G∣ coincide, giving xm=1 for some positive m. The least such exponent d is the order of a group element, and the cyclic subgroup generated by x consists of the d distinct powers 1,x,…,xd−1. Apply Lagrange's theorem for finite groups to obtain d∣∣G∣.
Cauchy's theorem for finite groups states that if a prime p divides the order of a finite group, the group contains an element of order p.
The classification of a group of order eight is
C8​,C4​×C2​,C2​×C2​×C2​,D8​,Q8​.​
(17)
Here D8​ denotes the dihedral group of order eight, not a group of order sixteen. To prove exhaustiveness, every element order is 1,2,4 or 8. An element of order eight generates the whole group, giving C8​.
If no element has order four or eight, every nonidentity element is an involution. Since (ab)−1=ab while a−1=a and b−1=b, we have ab=ba. Choose an involution a, another b outside ⟨a⟩, and then c outside the four-element subgroup ⟨a,b⟩. The factors commute and each added factor has trivial intersection with the preceding subgroup. The internal direct product theorem gives G≅C23​, an elementary abelian group.
Otherwise choose a of order four and set A=⟨a⟩. It has index two, so is normal. Choose b∈/A. The two cosets show that a,b generate G and that b2∈A. There is no element of order eight, so b2 is either 1 or a2.
If G is abelian and b2=1, the internal direct product theorem yields G=A×⟨b⟩≅C4​×C2​. If instead b2=a2, let c=a−1b; commutativity gives c2=1 and c∈/A, producing the same direct product.
If G is nonabelian, conjugation by b is a nonidentity group automorphism of A: otherwise a,b would commute and hence so would all of G. Thus bab−1=a−1. With b2=1 these are the relations of D8​; with b2=a2 they are the relations of the quaternion group Q8​. In either presentation every word reduces to aibj, 0≤i<4, 0≤j<2. The standard eight-element model maps onto G, and equal orders make that group homomorphism an isomorphism.
Finally group isomorphisms preserve element orders. The following numbers of elements distinguish all five possibilities:
GroupOrder 1Order 2Order 4Order 8
C8​1124
C4​×C2​1340
C23​1700
D8​1520
Q8​1160
Thus the list is both exhaustive and pairwise non-isomorphic.

Centre the cube at the origin with faces having normals ±e1​,±e2​,±e3​. Every rotational symmetry fixes the centre and permutes these normals, so its rotation matrix is a signed permutation matrix of determinant one. Conversely any such matrix permutes the cube’s coordinates up to signs and preserves the cube. For each of 3! coordinate permutations, four of the eight choices of signs give determinant one. Consequently the rotational symmetry group of a cube has
∣H∣=3!22=24.​
(21)
This counts the rotations directly, without an unverified stabilizer calculation. The TeX’s 2k is damaged transcription: the PDF prints 24.
Let X be the 36 face colourings. A colouring fixed by a rotation must be constant on each permutation cycle of faces, giving 3c fixed colourings if the rotation has c face cycles. The rotation types are:
RotationNumberFace cycle typeFixed colourings
Identity11636
Quarter-turn about opposite face centres612433

Third-turn about opposite vertices83232
There are three face axes, each with two quarter-turns and one half-turn; six edge axes, each with one half-turn; and four vertex axes, each with two nonidentity third-turns. These rotations are distinct and, with the identity, total 24, so the table is exhaustive. Face-axis rotations fix their two axial faces; the remaining four faces cycle or pair. With rightmost-first composition, a permutation cycle satisfies
(a1​ a2​ ⋯ am​)=(a1​ am​)(a1​ am−1​)⋯(a1​ a2​).
(23)
Tracing each aj​ verifies the equality, and every other letter is fixed. Thus every cycle is a product of transpositions; decomposing a permutation into disjoint permutation cycles proves that every element of Sn​ is such a product. The identity is the empty product.
To establish that parity is well defined, use the permutation matrix Pσ​ defined by Pσ​ei​=eσ(i)​. Its determinant is ±1, and Pστ​=Pσ​Pτ​. A transposition swaps two columns of the identity matrix and has determinant −1. Therefore if σ is expressed as a product of r transpositions,
detPσ​=(−1)r.
(24)
The left side depends only on σ, so any two decompositions have the same parity. Define
sgn(σ)=detPσ​=(−1)r.​
(25)
Multiplicativity of the determinant makes this the sign homomorphism. Its kernel of a group homomorphism is the alternating group
An​={σ∈Sn​:sgn(σ)=1}.​
(26)
It is a normal subgroup consisting of the even permutations. For n≥2 the sign map is surjective because a transposition has sign −1, so [Sn​:An​]=2. For n=1 both groups are trivial.

Write c=(1 2 ⋯ n), with n≥2. Conjugation relabels a transposition, so
cj(1 2)c−j=(1+j 2+j),
(27)
where labels are read modulo n. In particular the subgroup contains (1 2),(2 3),…,(n−1 n). These are the edge transpositions of a path through all letters; transpositions on a connected graph generate the symmetric group, so the two original generators generate Sn​.
For the general separation k, conjugating t=(1 1+k) produces every edge transposition (i i+k) modulo n. The components of this graph are precisely the residue classes modulo d=gcd(n,k): moving along an edge adds or subtracts k, and the subgroup of Z/nZ generated by k consists of multiples of d. Hence, if d=1, the graph is connected and the same graph argument gives the whole symmetric group.
For completeness, the graph argument can be proved without assuming a generating-set theorem. On a simple path v0​,…,vℓ​, put tj​=(vj−1​ vj​). Then
(v0​ vℓ​)=t1​⋯tℓ−1​tℓ​tℓ−1​⋯t1​.
(28)
This is a conjugate of the last edge transposition; connectivity therefore supplies every transposition, and part (a) supplies every permutation.
If d>1, there are d residue blocks of size n/d>1, since 1≤k<n. The cycle c permutes these blocks, and t swaps two letters in the same block. Both preserve this block system, so their generated subgroup does too. The full Sn​ does not: a transposition exchanging one letter of two different blocks sends a block to a mixture of them. Thus the subgroup is proper. This proves
⟨(1 1+k),(1 2 ⋯ n)⟩=Sn​⟺gcd(n,k)=1.​
(29)
Thus a cycle and a transposition generate the symmetric group exactly at coprime separation. The obstruction is preservation of a partition, not failure of transitivity: the n-cycle already acts transitively even when d>1.

For an arc-length parametrization, the unit tangent vector is t=r′(s), with ∣t∣=1. Its derivative is perpendicular to t. The curvature of a space curve is κ=∣t′∣; where κ>0, its principal normal vector is n=t′/κ, so t′=κn. The binormal vector b=t×n completes the positively oriented Frenet frame.

dsd​(b⋅t)=0⟹b′⋅t=−b⋅t′=−κb⋅n=0.
(43)
Thus b′ has only an n component. Define the torsion of a space curve by τ=−b′⋅n, giving b′=−τn.
Next, n′⋅n=0, and differentiating the other inner products gives n′⋅t=−κ and n′⋅b=τ. Hence the Frenet-Serret formulas are
t′=κn,b′=−τn,n′=−κt+τb.​

If κ=0, the unit tangent vector is still defined, but the standard principal normal vector, binormal vector and torsion of a space curve need not be; their existence cannot be inferred just from smoothness of the curve.

Put h=u−ϕ−C. Then h=0 on S, and the constant does not change a gradient, so ∇u=∇ϕ+∇h. Expand the unnormalized Dirichlet energy:
∫V​∣∇u∣2dV=∫V​∣∇ϕ∣2dV+2∫V​∇ϕ⋅∇hdV+∫V​∣∇h∣2dV.
(52)
The cross term is zero by Green's first identity:
∫V​∇ϕ⋅∇hdV=∫S​h∂n​ϕdS−∫V​hΔϕdV=0.
(53)
Thus the useful stronger identity is
∫V​∣∇u∣2dV−∫V​∣∇ϕ∣2dV=∫V​∣∇(u−ϕ−C)∣2dV≥0.​
(54)
Equality holds exactly when h has zero gradient. It is then constant on every connected component and zero on that component’s boundary, hence h=0. Therefore equality holds precisely when u=ϕ+C throughout V. This is the Dirichlet principle; an additive boundary constant does not alter the minimizing energy. The energy here omits the optional factor 1/2, which does not affect minimizers or the inequality.

Let E(t)=∫V​∣∇w∣2dV, using real-valued smooth w and a fixed bounded region. Differentiation under the integral sign and Green's first identity yield
E′(t)​=2∫V​∇w⋅∇wt​dV=2∫S​wt​∂n​wdS−2∫V​wt​ΔwdV.​
(55)
The boundary term vanishes because wt​=0 on S, and the heat equation gives wt​=Δw in V. Consequently
E′(t)=−2∫V​(Δw)2dV≤0.​
(56)
The integrand is continuous and nonnegative. Thus at any fixed time, equality is equivalent to Δw=0 everywhere in V, also giving wt​=0 there at that time. This proves Dirichlet energy dissipation for the heat equation.
The boundary condition fixes the boundary temperature in time; it is not a zero normal derivative condition. No sign condition on ∂n​w is used. The stated smoothness licenses the time derivative, spatial integration by parts and boundary trace; without that regularity an appropriate weak energy argument would be required.
