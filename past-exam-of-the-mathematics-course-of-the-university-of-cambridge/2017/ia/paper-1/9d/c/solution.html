Set h(x)=f(x)−yx. Then
h′(a)=f′(a)−y≤0,h′(b)=f′(b)−y≥0.
(69)
The extreme value theorem gives a minimum of h on [a,b]. If it occurs in (a,b), the two-sided difference quotient gives h′(c)=0. If it occurs at a, either h′(a)=0 or a negative right derivative contradicts minimality; the endpoint b is analogous. Hence some c∈[a,b] satisfies
f′(c)=y​.
(70)
This proves the Darboux theorem for derivatives in the stated case without assuming that f′ is continuous.
Because a differentiable function is continuous, define
F(x)=f(x)+∫ax​f(t)dt.
(71)
The fundamental theorem of calculus gives F′(x)=f′(x)+f(x). At the endpoints,
F′(a)=f′(a)+f(a)≤f′(a)≤y,
(72)
while
F′(b)=f′(b)+f(b)≥f′(b)≥y.
(73)
Applying the result just proved to F yields d∈[a,b] with
f′(d)+f(d)=y​.
(74)
