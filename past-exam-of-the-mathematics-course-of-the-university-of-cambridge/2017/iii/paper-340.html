Use the Fourier transform convention f​(ξ)=∫R​f(x)e−ixξdx. An orthonormal multiresolution analysis is a family of closed vector subspaces Vj​⊂L2(R), indexed by integers, with Vj​⊂Vj+1​, ⋃j​Vj​​=L2(R), and ⋂j​Vj​={0}. Its dilation condition is f∈Vj​ if and only if f(2⋅)∈Vj+1​. The space V0​ is invariant under integer function translations and admits a scaling function φ whose integer function translations form an orthonormal basis. Consequently φj,k​=2j/2φ(2j⋅−k) form an orthonormal basis of Vj​.
Since φ∈V1​, expansion in that orthonormal basis gives the scaling refinement equation
φ(x)=2​∑k∈Z​hk​φ(2x−k),hk​=⟨φ,φ1,k​⟩.
(1)
The series converges in the L2 norm. The associated low-pass filter of a multiresolution analysis is the periodic Fourier series symbol
m(ξ)=2​1​k∈Z∑​hk​e−ikξ,φ​(2ξ)=m(ξ)φ​(ξ).​
(2)
Initially the symbol is defined almost everywhere. Orthonormal integer function translations give the quadrature mirror filter identity ∣m(ξ)∣2+∣m(ξ+π)∣2=1 almost everywhere. For a Lebesgue integrable scaling function, its Fourier transform is continuous and ∣φ​(0)∣=1; choosing a constant phase makes φ​(0)=1 and the continuous representative near zero has m(0)=1. Different conventions absorb 2​ into the refinement coefficients; the displayed convention fixes that ambiguity.

A wavelet with p vanishing moments satisfies xkψ∈L1 and ∫xkψ(x)dx=0 for 0≤k<p. The moment differentiation of the Fourier transform theorem states that these weighted integrability hypotheses make ψ​∈Cp−1, with ψ​(k)(ξ)=∫(−ix)kψ(x)e−ixξdx. Hence ψ​(k)(0)=0. The MRA projection Fourier identity, together with density of the approximation spaces and continuity of φ​ at zero, gives ∣φ​(0)∣=1. These are the two analytic theorems needed in addition to the quadrature mirror filter construction.
For the ordinary derivative conclusion, assume also that the MRA low-pass filter is Cp−1 near π and φ​ is Cp−1 near zero. These hypotheses hold, for example, for a compactly supported scaling function with a finite refinement filter. The wavelet identity gives
m(π+t)​=φ​(t)eitψ​(2t)​.
(4)
The denominator is nonzero near zero. Its reciprocal and the exponential are Cp−1, so the product rule and the zero Taylor polynomial of ψ​ give the smooth-mask vanishing-moment criterion
m(k)(π)=0(0≤k<p),under the stated smoothness hypotheses.​
(5)
The printed integrability hypothesis alone does not guarantee ordinary higher derivatives. It gives only a continuous φ​. Without added smoothness, Taylor theorem for ψ​ still gives m(π+t)=o(∣t∣p−1) for the representative defined by the quotient: a Peano zero. This establishes the value at π, and for p≥2 the first derivative there, but it does not automatically establish iterated derivatives.
Here is a lacunary scaling-phase regularity counterexample for p≥3. Start with a compactly supported Daubechies wavelet having p vanishing moments, with scaling function φ0​ and finite MRA low-pass filter m0​. Define the periodic phase
θ(t)=∑n≥0​2−nsin(2nt),a(t)=eiθ(t),φ​(t)=a(t)φ​0​(t).
(6)
The Fourier coefficients of θ are absolutely summable. The Wiener algebra is closed under multiplication and the exponential series, so a and a−1 have absolutely summable Fourier coefficients. Thus φ is an absolutely summable combination of integer function translations of φ0​, belongs to L1∩L2, and generates the same V0​. Unimodularity preserves orthogonality and unit norm of its integer function translations; the inverse phase preserves their complete span. The same approximation spaces therefore give a multiresolution analysis.
The identity θ(2t)=θ(t)+θ(t+π) gives a(2t)=a(t)a(t+π), so the periodic phase change of a scaling function produces m(t)=a(t+π)m0​(t). In the high-pass formula the phases cancel:
e−itm(t+π)​φ​(t)=e−itm0​(t+π)​φ​0​(t).
(7)
The associated wavelet is therefore exactly the original one, with unchanged vanishing moments.
At any dyadic point t0​=2πk/2j, the difference quotient of the terms with j≤n≤log2​(1/∣h∣) is 1+O((2nh)2) per term. The sum of these errors and the remaining tail divided by h are bounded; the finitely many earlier terms have finite limits. Hence (θ(t0​+h)−θ(t0​))/h=log2​(1/∣h∣)+O(1), which has no finite limit. The exponential has the same failure of differentiability. Dyadic points are dense, and m0​(π+t) is nonzero for sufficiently small t=0. Thus m is not differentiable on any neighborhood of π, so an ordinary second derivative at π, understood as the derivative of a locally defined first derivative, need not exist. The Peano zero remains valid. This separates the intended smooth-mask theorem from what the literal assumptions establish.

Under the smoothness hypotheses in the smooth-mask vanishing-moment criterion, write any nonzero integer j as j=2rℓ, where r≥0 and ℓ is odd, possibly negative. Iterating the scaling refinement equation exactly r+1 times gives
φ​(2πj+t)=[∏n=1r+1​m(2n2πj+t​)]φ​(2r+12πj+t​).
(8)

If the remaining factors are Cp−1 at the displayed arguments, the product rule forces every derivative of order less than p of the product to vanish. In particular compact support and a finite MRA low-pass filter supply all these hypotheses. Therefore the integer-frequency zeros of a scaling function are
φ​(k)(2πj)=0(j=0, 0≤k<p),with the stated regularity.​
(9)

Thus the same regularity qualification is necessary here.

The strict null space property of order s is
∥vS​∥1​<∥vSc​∥1​for every 0=v∈kerA and every ∣S∣≤s.​
(21)
Here vS​ agrees with v on S and is zero elsewhere. Suppose a nonzero null vector had at most 2s nonzero entries. Split its support of a vector into disjoint sets S,T of size at most s. Applying the null space property to both sets would give ∥vS​∥1​<∥vT​∥1​ and ∥vT​∥1​<∥vS​∥1​, a contradiction. Empty parts cause the same contradiction. Thus no such nonzero null vector exists.
For an s-sparse x, the feasible vector x has ∥x∥0​≤s, where the zero-subscript quantity counts nonzero entries and is not a norm. Any feasible competitor z with ∥z∥0​≤∥x∥0​ also has at most s nonzero entries. The difference z−x∈kerA has at most 2s nonzero entries, so z=x. Competitors with larger support have strictly larger objective. Therefore every s-sparse vector is the unique sparsest feasible vector. This is sparse injectivity; for s=0 the only sparse vector is zero and the conclusion is immediate.

αJ(v)+21​∥v−g∥22​−αJ(u)−21​∥u−g∥22​≥α⟨q,v−u⟩+⟨u−g,v−u⟩+21​∥v−u∥22​=21​∥v−u∥22​.

On the entire plane, the printed global BV domain is not closed in the L2 geometry. The usual BV(R2) definition includes an L1 condition. For 1<a≤2, f(x)=(1+∣x∣)−a belongs to L2, has finite distributional variation 2πa∫0∞​r(1+r)−a−1dr, and fails to belong to L1. The truncated fT​=fχB(0,T)​ lies in BV∩L2 and tends to f in L2. The dual definition therefore gives ⟨q,v⟩≤J(v) for every finite-penalty v, and trivially for all other v.

If 0<α<R/2, choose c=1−2α/R; then (g−cχB​)/α=q. If α≥R/2, choose u=0; then g/α=(R/(2α))q belongs to ∂J(0), since multiplying the dual bound by a number in [0,1] preserves it. The previous optimality criterion proves
u(x)=(1−R2α​)+​χB(0,R)​(x).​
(35)
The quadratic fidelity is strictly convex, so this minimizer is unique, including the threshold α=R/2. The disk retains its radius on the positive branch and disappears on the zero branch; this is total variation denoising of a disk.

Write D=∇ and use the real pixelwise inner products. Let D∗ be the adjoint operator, defined by ⟨Du,p⟩=⟨u,D∗p⟩. For each nontrivial grid direction its one-dimensional formula is
(Dx+∗​p1)i,j​=⎩⎨⎧​−p1,j1​,pi−1,j1​−pi,j1​,pN−1,j1​,​i=1,1<i<N,i=N,​
(36)
with the analogous formula in j for Dy+∗​p2. Their sum is D∗p; unused components pN,j1​ and pi,N2​ do not contribute. For N=1, D=D∗=0. If discrete divergence is defined, its sign convention is div=−D∗, as in the adjoint of a discrete forward gradient.
Define the compact convex set Pλ​={p∈X2:∣pi,j​∣2​≤λ} and its image C=D∗Pλ​. The image is compact and convex, hence closed, and contains zero. The finite-dimensional Euclidean duality formula λ∣a∣2​=max∣p∣2​≤λ​a⋅p, applied independently at every pixel, gives
λ∥Du∥2,1​=maxp∈Pλ​​⟨Du,p⟩=maxw∈C​⟨u,w⟩=σC​(u).
(37)
Thus the penalty is the support function σC​. Its convex conjugate is the indicator functional of a constraint set ιC​, equal to zero on C and infinity elsewhere. For w∈C, the conjugate supremum is zero; for w∈/C, the separation theorem supplies a direction u with ⟨u,w⟩>σC​(u), and scaling that direction makes the supremum infinite.
The proximal operator is proxσC​​(g)=argminu​{σC​(u)+21​∥u−g∥22​}. It exists uniquely because the objective is continuous, coercive and strictly convex. The Moreau decomposition for a proper closed convex function gives proxσC​​(g)+proxιC​​(g)=g. The second term is the Euclidean projection onto a convex set, so the projection residual for discrete total variation is
u=g−PC​g,C=D∗{p:∣pi,j​∣2​≤λ}.​
(38)
Alternatively, the variational characterization of convex projection says w=PC​g exactly when ⟨g−w,z−w⟩≤0 for every z∈C. Hence w attains the support function at u=g−w, giving w∈∂σC​(u) and the same optimality condition. This supplies a direct verification of the Moreau decomposition step in this instance.
The printed wording needs “the residual after a projection”, rather than “the projection itself”. In general this denoising map is not a projection onto any fixed closed convex set. A metric projection onto a closed convex set is idempotent, while denoising a sufficiently large jump twice shrinks it twice. Concretely, for N=2 and data with rows (0,0) and (6λ,6λ), the minimizer has rows (λ,λ) and (5λ,5λ). Reapplying the map gives rows (2λ,2λ) and (4λ,4λ), so it is not idempotent. These formulas have a global certificate: take p1,j1​=λ, all other dual entries zero, giving D∗p with rows (−λ,−λ) and (λ,λ); it saturates every positive row difference and yields the primal optimality condition. The qualification is therefore not merely a consequence of a restricted two-level ansatz.

Computing PC​g is equivalent to minimizing F(p)=21​∥g−D∗p∥22​ over Pλ​, since D∗p ranges over precisely C. Differentiating with the adjoint operator gives ∇F(p)=D(D∗p−g), whose Lipschitz constant is L=∥DD∗∥=∥D∥2. Starting with any p0∈Pλ​, for example zero, the projected-gradient dual total variation algorithm is
rk=pk+τD(g−D∗pk),pi,jk+1​=max(1,∣ri,jk​∣2​/λ)ri,jk​​,uk=g−D∗pk.​
(39)
The block formula is the Euclidean projection onto a convex set Pλ​, since it clips each two-dimensional block to a Euclidean ball of radius λ. Both components must be clipped together for the isotropic block mixed norm.
For the unscaled forward differences, ∥Dx+​u∥22​≤4∥u∥22​ and likewise in the other direction, so L≤8. More exactly, for N>1 the one-dimensional path difference has eigenvalues 4sin2(kπ/(2N)), 0≤k<N, and the square-grid operator sums two such spectra. Thus L=8cos2(π/(2N)). Finite-dimensional projected gradient descent for a convex objective with an L-Lipschitz gradient converges from every feasible starting point to a dual minimizer for fixed 0<τ<2/L. The theorem assumes a nonempty closed convex set, a convex differentiable objective with globally Lipschitz gradient, and a nonempty minimizer set. All hold here: Pλ​ is compact and convex, and F is a continuous convex quadratic. Finite-dimensional convergence is of the full sequence, rather than only its objective values.
Consequently any fixed step with 0<τ<1/4 is safe on this grid; the more conservative 0<τ≤1/8 is also safe. The dual minimizer need not be unique because D∗ has a null space, but D∗pk→PC​g and uk→u uniquely. For N=1, the gradient penalty vanishes and u=g, so no positive-L step restriction is needed.

Absolute values are missing in the printed formula. For signed real blocks, the intended block mixed norm must be
∥v∥q,1​=∑i,j​(∑r=1d​∣vi,jr​∣q)1/q,q≥1.
(40)
Without them, at q=1 a block (−1,1) has value zero although it is nonzero, and a negative scalar has negative value. For nonintegral q, a negative component may not even have a real power. The literal expression therefore is not a norm and does not define the claimed general convex problem. Even integer exponents do not fix all other q in the stated range.
With the corrected norm, let q′ be the Holder conjugate exponent: q′=q/(q−1) for q>1 and q′=∞ for q=1. Blockwise Holder inequality, with equality from a norming block, gives
λ∥Au∥q,1​=supp∈Pλ,q′​​⟨Au,p⟩,Pλ,q′​={p∈Xd:∥pi,j​∥q′​≤λ for all i,j}.
(41)
The duality of block mixed norms identifies the closed compact convex set and the projection residual from the Moreau decomposition
C=A∗Pλ,q′​,u=g−PC​g.​
(42)
For q=2, the dual blocks are Euclidean balls; for q=1, they are cubes given by componentwise bounds ∣pi,jr​∣≤λ. No injectivity or surjectivity of A is required: compactness of the block product makes its linear image compact, and the strictly convex fidelity still gives a unique primal minimizer.
