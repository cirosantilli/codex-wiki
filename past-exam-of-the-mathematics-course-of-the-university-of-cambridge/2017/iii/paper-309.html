Put D=diag(a1​,…,an+1​). The invertible linear map x↦u=Dx takes the ellipsoid to the unit sphere Sn. Write u=(v,s) with v∈Rn, and let N=(0,1) and S=(0,−1) be its two poles. The following two stereographic projections define manifold charts on the ellipsoid:
UN​=Qn​∖{D−1N},χN​(x)=1−sv​,US​=Qn​∖{D−1S},χS​(x)=1+sv​.
(1)
Their ranges are Rn. With r2=∣w∣2, their inverses, followed by D−1, are
χN−1​(w)=D−1(1+r22w​,1+r2r2−1​),χS−1​(w)=D−1(1+r22w​,1+r21−r2​).
(2)
These are continuous and smooth functions, and directly satisfy the defining constraint. On the overlap, the smooth transition map is
χS​∘χN−1​(w)=∣w∣2w​(w=0).​
(3)
It is its own inverse and is smooth away from zero. The two domains are open in the subspace topology and cover the ellipsoid. Its subspace topology is inherited from Euclidean space, so the ellipsoid is Hausdorff and has a second-countable space topology. Thus these charts form a smooth atlas and make it an n-dimensional smooth manifold, with an explicit diffeomorphism to Sn. For the degenerate dimension n=0, each chart consists of a single point with range R0, and their overlap is empty; the same conclusion holds.

At p∈Q, the differential of a smooth map defines the pushforward of a vector field by
(ψ∗​X)p​=dψp​(Xp​),((ψ∗​X)p​f)=Xp​(f∘ψ),f∈C∞(N).​
(4)
This is a tangent vector at ψ(p), and varies smoothly as a section of the pullback tangent bundle. In local coordinates its components are Xi(p)∂i​ψa(p).
For a general smooth map between manifolds, this is a vector field along a map, rather than an intrinsic vector field on all of N. A vector field Y on the image can be defined only if these vectors agree whenever two points have the same image, and the resulting field must also be smooth; a projectable vector field must meet that requirement. For a diffeomorphism, there is no ambiguity and Yq​=dψψ−1(q)​Xψ−1(q)​. For example, ψ(x)=x2 on R and X=∂x​ give 2x∂y​, with opposite nonzero values at the two preimages of y>0. Thus the source's notation must be read with this qualification.

The induced metric is obtained from the ambient Euclidean metric by the pullback of a Riemannian metric operation. Differentiating x2+a2ρ2=1 on a branch with x=0 gives dx=−a2ρdρ/x. Also dy2+dz2=dρ2+ρ2dϕ2. Consequently
g=E(ρ)dρ2+ρ2dϕ2,E(ρ)=1+1−a2ρ2a4ρ2​=1−a2ρ21+a2(a2−1)ρ2​.​
(7)
The assumption that (ρ,ϕ) are coordinates already excludes the equator x=0 as well as the poles ρ=0. On such a chart, 0<ρ<1/∣a∣ and E>0.
The conformal flattening of a surface of revolution supplies the particularly simple conformal factor
Ω=ρ−1,Ω2g=ρ2E(ρ)​dρ2+dϕ2=dσ2+dϕ2,σ(ρ)=∫ρ∗​ρ​rE(r)​​dr.​
(8)
Since σ′>0, these are isothermal coordinates with a flat rescaled metric. An angular coordinate is understood on a local branch; the flat metric may equally be viewed locally as a cylinder metric. For a timelike tangent v=dx/du, put L=−g(v,v)​>0 and L=L2/2=−g(v,v)/2, and use the Euler-Lagrange equations. For the quadratic geodesic Lagrangian, they reduce to
dud​(gμν​vν)−21​∂μ​gαβ​vαvβ=0,∇v​v=0.
(9)
Here the first equation has been multiplied by an irrelevant minus sign. For the length Lagrangian, its derivatives are L−1 times those of L, and differentiating that factor yields instead
∇v​v=LL˙​v.​
(10)
This is the unparametrized geodesic equation, the key to why timelike length and energy have the same geodesic images. Define proper time locally by dτ/du=L>0 and put w=dx/dτ=v/L. The product rule then gives ∇w​w=L−2[∇v​v−(L˙/L)v]=0 and g(w,w)=−1. Conversely an affinely parametrized timelike geodesic has constant L, by metric compatibility, and therefore satisfies both equations.
Thus the two variational problems have the same oriented timelike geodesic images, after reparametrization. They do not have exactly the same parametrized solutions with an arbitrary fixed u: in Minkowski spacetime, t(u)=u2, x=0, u>0, satisfies the length equation but not the quadratic equation. The timelike assumption excludes L=0, where this argument and the length derivative would fail.

Use spatial indices i,j=1,2,3, set ϕi​=∂i​ϕ, and assume ϕ is smooth. The inverse metric has gtt=−c−2e−2ϕ/c2 and gij=δij. The Christoffel symbols of the Levi-Civita connection follow from Γαβγ​=gαδ(∂β​gγδ​+∂γ​gβδ​−∂δ​gβγ​)/2:
Γtti​=Γtit​=c2ϕi​​,Γitt​=e2ϕ/c2ϕi​.​
(11)
All other components vanish. Although gtt​=−c2−2ϕ+O(c−2) diverges, its affine connection has a smooth limit on compact subsets:
Γ(∞)itt​=ϕi​,all other limiting components=0.​
(12)
This is the Newtonian connection from an exponential lapse. It is torsion-free, and its geodesic equation, using t as an affine parameter when t˙=0, is d2xi/dt2=−∂i​ϕ. Thus ϕ has the role of a Newtonian gravitational potential. There is no finite limiting nondegenerate Lorentzian metric in these fixed coordinates; −g(c)/c2 tends to dt2, a rank-one tensor. The limiting connection preserves dt and the contravariant spatial tensor with components hij=δij, htμ=0, which describe the degenerate temporal/spatial structures of this limit.

Fix the Riemann curvature tensor convention
Rαβμν​=∂μ​Γανβ​−∂ν​Γαμβ​+Γαμλ​Γλνβ​−Γανλ​Γλμβ​,Rβν​=Rαβαν​.
(13)
For the limiting affine connection, every product of two nonzero connection coefficients is zero, because its only nonzero coefficients have spatial upper index and both lower indices t. Its only possibly nonzero curvature components, apart from antisymmetry in the last two indices, are
Ritjt​=∂j​∂i​ϕ.
(14)
The Ricci tensor is therefore
Ric(∇(∞))=(Δϕ)dt⊗dt.​
(15)
All other components vanish, so the limiting connection is a connection with vanishing Ricci tensor exactly when ϕ satisfies the Laplace equation on spatial Euclidean space. This is Ricci-flatness, not necessarily vanishing curvature: ϕ=x2−y2 is a harmonic function but has nonzero Hessian matrix and hence a nonflat limiting connection. Reversing the curvature sign convention reverses the displayed Ricci sign but leaves the equivalence unchanged.

Use x0=t and the Levi-Civita symbol ϵ123​=1. Define a smooth affine connection by
Γi00​=−Ei,Γi0j​=Γij0​=ϵijk​Bk,Γ0ab​=Γijk​=0.​

x¨i=−Γi00​−2Γi0j​x˙j=Ei+2ϵikj​Bkx˙j.
(17)
The last term is 2(B×x˙)i, with the wedge in the source interpreted as the three-dimensional cross product. Conversely a geodesic with nonzero constant dt/ds can be parametrized by t and obeys this particle equation; purely spatial geodesics with dt/ds=0 are not such trajectories. For a smooth vector field X, solve the initial-value problem dϕt​(p)/dt=X(ϕt​(p)), ϕ0​(p)=p. Local existence, uniqueness and smooth dependence for ordinary differential equations give a local flow. Uniqueness yields ϕt+s​(p)=ϕt​(ϕs​(p)) whenever both sides are defined, and ϕ−t​ is the inverse of ϕt​ on the corresponding domains. Thus each local flow map is a diffeomorphism between open subsets.
A group of diffeomorphisms of the entire manifold for every real t requires a complete vector field. This follows, for example, on a compact manifold without boundary: local existence times can be made uniform on a finite cover, and repeated continuation prevents finite-time escape. Without completeness, the source's group is only local. The vector field x2∂x​ on R has flow x↦x/(1−tx) and blows up at t=1/x for x>0, so smoothness alone cannot imply a global one-parameter group.

Define the Lie derivative of a differential form by LX​α=(d/dt)t=0​ϕt∗​α, using the local flow. The pullback of a differential form respects the wedge product of differential forms and commutes with the exterior derivative. Therefore LX​ is a derivation of an algebra of degree zero and commutes with d.
Let DX​=ιX​d+dιX​, where ιX​ is the interior product of a differential form. The graded product rules for d and ιX​ show that their anticommutator DX​ is also a degree-zero derivation of an algebra: the two mixed terms cancel. Since d2=0, it commutes with d. On a function f, ιX​f=0 and DX​f=ιX​df=Xf=LX​f. Hence DX​(df)=d(Xf)=LX​(df).
In a manifold chart, every differential form is a sum of functions times products dxi1​∧⋯∧dxip​. The two derivations agree on its generators f and dxi, and therefore agree on every such form. This proves Cartan's magic formula, including the case p=0:
LX​α=ιX​dα+d(ιX​α).​

An electromagnetic field tensor obeys the homogeneous Maxwell equations dF=0. Together with its invariance under the Killing vector field V, Cartan's magic formula gives d(ιV​F)=LV​F−ιV​dF=0. The local potential of a closed differential one-form construction, the one-form Poincaré lemma, therefore supplies a scalar field Φ on a sufficiently small contractible neighborhood with
ιV​F=dΦ.​
(25)
For an explicit local proof, write ιV​F=αi​(x)dxi on a coordinate ball centered at zero and take Φ(x)=∫01​xiαi​(sx)ds. Since dα=0, differentiating under the integral gives ∂j​Φ=∫01​[αj​(sx)+sxi∂i​αj​(sx)]ds=αj​(x). There need not be a global potential when this closed one-form has nonzero periods. The homogeneous Maxwell equation is essential; an arbitrary invariant two-form would not suffice.
Use proper time for the massive particle, let u be its tangent, and assume m=0. The Killing equation makes uaub∇a​Vb​=0. The Lorentz force equation then implies
dτd​(Va​ua)=mq​Va​Fab​ub=mq​VaFab​ub=mq​dτdΦ​.
(26)
Consequently the local conserved quantity is
mVa​ua−qΦ=constant.​
(27)
The sign follows from contracting F in its first argument, exactly as in the source. Adding a constant to Φ merely shifts the conserved quantity. This is a charged-particle conserved quantity from a Killing symmetry.

Choose signature (−,+,+,+), with e0 timelike. The orthonormal coframe in spacetime reconstructs the Lorentzian metric as
g=−(e0)2+(e1)2+(e2)2+(e3)2=−dt2+A(t−z)2dx2+B(t−z)2dy2+dz2.​
(28)
Because A and B are nowhere zero on the domain, the coframe is invertible and the metric nondegenerate; either sign of each function is allowed. In retarded and advanced null coordinates u=t−z, v=t+z the metric is −dudv+A(u)2dx2+B(u)2dy2, a diagonal example of Rosen coordinates for a plane gravitational wave. Multiplication by −1 would give the opposite overall signature convention; all subsequent signs here use the displayed one.

ω01​=ω13​=−pe1,ω02​=ω23​=−qe2,α=β=−AA′​,γ=δ=−BB′​.​

Use linearization around Minkowski spacetime after constant rescaling of x,y: set A=1+εa(u), B=1+εb(u), u=t−z. To first order,
g=η+2εa(u)dx2+2εb(u)dy2+O(ε2),a′′+b′′=0.
(35)
Hence s=a+b is affine in u, while h=a−b is the freely propagating profile. The affine transverse trace in a diagonal linearized plane wave is pure gauge: it has zero linearized curvature and is locally a coordinate artifact. To see this without imposing extra boundary conditions, use a first-order linearized coordinate gauge transformation hμν​↦hμν​−∂μ​ξν​−∂ν​ξμ​, with
ξx​=2εsx​,ξy​=2εsy​,ξt​=−4εs′(x2+y2)​,ξz​=4εs′(x2+y2)​.
(36)
The tx,ty,zx,zy changes cancel and the remaining time/longitudinal changes are proportional to s′′=0. The transverse perturbation becomes
hxx​=εh(t−z),hyy​=−εh(t−z),hxy​=0,​
(37)
with all time and longitudinal components zero. It is a transverse-traceless gauge plane gravitational wave travelling in the positive z-direction, with plus polarization and no cross polarization in these transverse axes.
For freely falling nearby particles, geodesic deviation gives opposite transverse tidal accelerations, since Rtxtx​=−εa′′ and Rtyty​=−εb′′=+εa′′ in vacuum. Thus the transverse geodesic deviation eigenvalues have opposite signs; the stretching and compressing roles interchange when the curvature profile changes sign. If h′′=0 as well, there is no linearized tidal wave: that special profile is also locally flat to this order. The exact diagonal metric describes a fixed transverse polarization through its opposite vacuum curvature eigenvalues; the linearized calculation identifies it as plus relative to the chosen axes.
