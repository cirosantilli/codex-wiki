An equivalence of categories consists of functors F:C→D and G:D→C together with invertible natural transformations GF≅1C​ and FG≅1D​. A strict isomorphism of categories instead requires a functor with an inverse whose composites are literally identity functors.
First suppose F belongs to an equivalence of categories. The isomorphisms FGD≅D give essential surjectivity. If Ff=Fg, apply G and conjugate by GF≅1C​ to obtain f=g, so F is a faithful functor. Similarly G is a faithful functor. Writing α:GF∼​1C​, any h:FA→FB has a preimage
f=αB​G(h)αA−1​.
(1)
Indeed naturality of α gives GF(f)=G(h), and faithfulness of G gives F(f)=h. Thus F is a full and faithful functor.
Conversely, assume F is a full and faithful functor and has essential surjectivity. Using the axiom of choice, for each D choose an object GD and an isomorphism θD​:F(GD)→D. Define G on a morphism h:D→E by the unique lift
F(Gh)=θE−1​hθD​.
(2)
The full and faithful functor property makes G preserve identities and composition, and makes θ:FG≅1D​ a natural transformation. For A∈C, lift θFA​:FGFA→FA uniquely to αA​:GFA→A. Lifting its inverse shows that αA​ is invertible. The naturality of θ and faithfulness of F imply the naturality of α. This proves
F is an equivalence⟺F is full, faithful and essentially surjective.​
(3)
For large categories, this choice argument is understood in a fixed universe, or with the corresponding class-choice convention; ordinary set-sized axiom of choice suffices for small categories.
For the category of partial functions, let X+​=X⨿{∗}, with a tagged new element as basepoint. Send a partial function f:X⇀Y to the basepoint-preserving total function
f+​(x)={f(x),∗,​x∈domf,x∈/domf,​f+​(∗)=∗.
(4)
Undefined composition is sent to the basepoint, so this is a functor (−)+​:Part→Set∗​. Restriction away from the basepoint recovers each partial function uniquely; hence it is a full and faithful functor. Every pointed set (Y,y0​) is isomorphic to (Y∖{y0​})+​, so there is an equivalence of categories. This particular equivalence can also be constructed explicitly, without choice, by deleting and adjoining the basepoint.
These actual categories are equivalent but not isomorphic. In Part the empty set is the only zero object: if X is nonempty, its identity differs from its nowhere-defined endomorphism, so it cannot be initial or terminal. In Set∗​ every singleton pointed set is a zero object, and distinct singleton underlying sets give distinct objects. An isomorphism of categories is a bijection on objects preserving zero objects; it cannot take one such object onto several. This uses the categories of all actual sets, as in the paper, rather than chosen skeletal categories of representatives.
A skeletal category has no distinct isomorphic objects. If an equivalence F:C→D joins two skeletal categories, essential surjectivity becomes surjectivity on objects. If FA=FB, lift the identity of that object and its inverse using full and faithful to obtain A≅B, so A=B. Thus F is bijective on objects and on every hom-set. Its inverse on objects and morphisms is a strictly inverse functor, proving that it is an isomorphism of categories.
Under the axiom of choice, choose one object from each isomorphism class of a small category. The full subcategory on those objects is a skeleton of a category, and its inclusion is a full and faithful functor with essential surjectivity, hence an equivalence of categories.
For the converse, form the small category which is a groupoid with objects (i,a) for a∈Ai​, and exactly one morphism (i,a)→(j,b) when i=j, with no morphisms when i=j. Suppose it is equivalent to a skeletal category S, with quasi-inverse functors H:C→S and K:S→C. For each i, the objects H(i,a) for a∈Ai​ are isomorphic, hence all equal to a uniquely determined si​. The isomorphism KH(i,a)≅(i,a) ensures that K(si​)=(i,ai​) for some ai​∈Ai​. The rule i↦ai​ is a choice function. In particular, no representative in Ai​ had to be chosen to define si​, since it is unique. Consequently
Every small category has a skeletal equivalent⟺the axiom of choice.​
(5)

A balanced category is one in which every morphism that is both a monomorphism and an epimorphism is an isomorphism. A faithful functor reflects both of these cancellation properties: for instance, fu=fv implies FfFu=FfFv, so monicity of Ff and faithfulness imply u=v; the dual argument applies to epimorphisms. If Ff is invertible, it is both monic and epic. Thus a faithful functor from a balanced category reflects isomorphisms.
For the adjunction F⊣G, the unit and counit of an adjunction satisfy
εFA​FηA​=1FA​,GεB​ηGB​=1GB​.
(6)
If F is a faithful functor and ηA​u=ηA​v, applying F and composing with εFA​ gives Fu=Fv, hence u=v. Thus each ηA​ is a monomorphism. Conversely, if every ηA​ is monic and Fu=Fv for u,v:X→A, naturality gives
ηA​u=GFuηX​=GFvηX​=ηA​v,
(7)
so u=v. This proves the faithful left adjoint criterion.
Now assume both η and ε are pointwise monic. The first triangle identity makes εFA​ a split epimorphism; since it is also a monomorphism, it is invertible and FηA​ is its inverse. The faithful left adjoint criterion says that F is faithful, and the balanced category argument above then reflects the invertibility of FηA​ to that of ηA​.
For completeness, an invertible unit makes F a full and faithful functor. For h:FA→FA′, set k=ηA′−1​G(h)ηA​. The triangle identity gives FηA​=εFA−1​, so naturality of ε yields
F(k)=εFA′​FG(h)εFA−1​=h.
(8)
Faithfulness was already proved.
Let q:FA→B be a strong epimorphism, and put
t=FG(q)εFA−1​:FA→FGB.
(9)
Then naturality gives εB​t=q. The square with left edge q, right edge the monomorphism εB​, top edge t, and bottom edge 1B​ has a diagonal s:B→FGB by the defining lifting property of a strong epimorphism. Thus εB​s=1B​. A monic split epimorphism is invertible, so B≅FGB. The essential image of F is therefore closed under strong quotients.

Assume F is a full and faithful functor and its essential image is closed under strong quotients. First its unit is invertible. To prove this directly, fullness gives k:GFA→A with Fk=εFA​. The triangle identity and faithfulness give kηA​=1A​. Naturality of η at k and the other triangle identity give
ηA​k=GFkηGFA​=GεFA​ηGFA​=1GFA​.
(10)
Thus ηA​ is an isomorphism, and εFA​=(FηA​)−1 is also an isomorphism.
Factor εB​ as a strong epimorphism followed by a monomorphism. By closure under strong quotients, the intermediate object can, after transport along an isomorphism, be written as FA:
FGBe​FAm​B,εB​=me.
(11)
Fullness gives e=Fk for k:GB→A. Taking the transpose of εB​=mFk under the adjunction, whose transpose is 1GB​, gives
1GB​=GmGFkηGB​=GmηA​k.
(12)
Hence k is a split monomorphism, so e=Fk is a monomorphism. A strong epimorphism which is monic is invertible: apply its lifting property to the square with that same map on both sides and identity top and bottom edges. Thus e is invertible and εB​=me is monic. We have proved the pointwise-monic unit-and-counit criterion:
η,ε monic⟺F fully faithful with image closed under strong quotients.​
(13)
For a counterexample without the balanced hypothesis, use the pointwise-monic adjunction over a non-balanced poset. Let C={0<1} and let D have one object and only its identity. The unique F:C→D is left adjoint to G selecting 1, since both D(Fc,∗) and C(c,1) are singletons. All morphisms in a poset viewed as a category are monomorphisms, so the unit and counit are monic. However F is not full: the identity F1→F0 has no preimage 1→0. The arrow 0→1 is both monic and epic but not invertible, so C is not a balanced category. The balanced hypothesis cannot be dropped.

A diagram in a category of shape J is a functor D:J→C. A categorical cone with vertex X is a family γj​:X→Dj satisfying D(a)γj​=γk​ for every a:j→k. A morphism between categorical cones from (X,γ) to (Y,δ) is a morphism u:X→Y with δj​u=γj​ for all j. A categorical limit is a terminal object in this category of cones: each cone has a unique such morphism to the limiting cone.
For a finite J, form the products in a category
P=∏j∈obJ​Dj,Q=∏a:j→k​Dk.
(14)
Define s,t:P⇉Q with a-coordinates D(a)πj​ and πk​. The equalizer e:L→P imposes exactly the cone equations. Therefore πj​e gives a categorical limit of D, since maps into P encode families of legs and factoring through e encodes their compatibility. The empty product in a category is the terminal object, covering the empty diagram. This is the construction of small limits from products and equalizers, restricted to finite shapes.
Now take an initial functor F:I→J and a categorical cone δi​:X→DFi over DF. For each j and each object (i,u:Fi→j) of the comma category (F↓j), consider D(u)δi​. A morphism a:(i,u)→(i′,u′) there satisfies u′F(a)=u, so
D(u′)δi′​=D(u′)D(Fa)δi​=D(u)δi​.
(15)
Since (F↓j) is a nonempty connected category, this common value is independent of the object. Define it to be γj​. This does not require choosing representatives: the value is uniquely determined.
For b:j→k, replacing (i,u) by (i,bu) proves D(b)γj​=γk​. Taking (i,1Fi​) proves γFi​=δi​. Conversely, extending a restricted cone recovers its original legs, since γj​=D(u)γFi​. A vertex morphism commuting with every δi​ also commutes with each γj​=D(u)δi​, and the converse follows by restriction. Thus extension and restriction are strictly inverse functors, not merely an equivalence of categories. This proves cone restriction along an initial functor.
If C has all categorical limits of shape I, transport a terminal object in the cone category of DF across this isomorphism to obtain a categorical limit of D. Uniqueness of the induced comparison, and its compatibility with natural transformations of diagrams, gives
Jlim​≅Ilim​∘F∗.​
(16)
For the converse use the representable test for initial functors. For j∈J, the representable presheaf J(−,j):Jop→Set is equivalently a diagram in a category Dj​:J→Setop. Its categorical limit in the opposite category is the colimit of J(−,j) in sets. Elements of that colimit are connected components of the category of elements, equivalently of the opposite category of the slice J↓j. This slice has terminal object (j,1j​), so that colimit is a singleton.
For the restricted presheaf J(F(−),j), the same description identifies its colimit with the connected-component set of (F↓j). Indeed a relation identifying u′:Fi′→j with u′F(a):Fi→j is exactly a generating edge of the zigzag relation in this comma category. The assumed isomorphism of limit functors forces this set to be a singleton. Therefore (F↓j) is nonempty and connected for every j, proving
F is initial.​
(17)

A monad on C is an endofunctor T with natural transformations η:1→T and μ:T2→T satisfying
μTμ=μμT,μTη=1T​=μηT.
(18)
An algebra for a monad is (A,α:TA→A) with αηA​=1A​ and αTα=αμA​. A morphism of algebras for a monad f:(A,α)→(B,β) satisfies fα=βTf. These form the Eilenberg-Moore category CT. Its free algebra functor is FX=(TX,μX​), and its adjunction to the forgetful functor U has the explicit bijection
CT(FX,(C,γ))≅C(X,C),h⟼hηX​,u⟼γTu.
(19)
Write S=A+B, V=TA+TB, and let ν1​,ν2​ be the coproduct in a category injections. Set
r=F(α+β),s=μS​Fκ:FV⇉FS,κ=[Tν1​,Tν2​].
(20)
Here μS​:FTS→FS is an algebra morphism, with underlying multiplication T2S→TS. For an algebra (C,γ), an algebra morphism h:FS→(C,γ) corresponds to u:S→C, and the transposes of hr,hs are respectively
u(α+β),hκ=γTuκ.
(21)
For the second formula, (μS​Tκ)ηV​=κ by naturality of η and the monad unit law. Thus hr=hs exactly when, writing u=[a,b],
aα=γTa,bβ=γTb.
(22)
These say precisely that a and b are morphisms of algebras for a monad. Consequently any coequalizer q:FS→Q of r,s represents pairs of algebra morphisms out of (A,α) and (B,β), proving the coproduct presentation for monad algebras:
Q≅(A,α)+(B,β)in CT.​
(23)
Its two algebra injections have underlying morphisms qηS​ν1​ and qηS​ν2​; the equivalence just proved verifies both the algebra equations and their universal property.
The displayed pair is a reflexive pair, with common section
t=F(ηA​+ηB​):FS→FV.
(24)
Indeed rt=1FS​ by the algebra unit laws, while
κ(ηA​+ηB​)=ηS​,st=μS​FηS​=1FS​.
(25)
Now suppose C has all finite colimits and T preserves reflexive coequalizers. The permitted creation theorem gives reflexive coequalizers in CT: the underlying pair is reflexive and its coequalizer exists and is preserved by T. The preceding construction therefore gives binary algebra coproducts in a category. The initial object is F0, since the free algebra functor is a left adjoint and 0 is initial in C.
For any algebra pair a,b:X⇉Y, the augmented pair
[a,1Y​],[b,1Y​]:X+Y⇉Y
(26)
is a reflexive pair, with common section the injection of Y, and has exactly the same coequalizing morphisms as a,b. Hence all coequalizers exist in CT. The construction of finite colimits from coproducts and reflexive coequalizers now gives
CT has all finite colimits.​
(27)

A regular category has finite categorical limits, coequalizers of kernel pairs, and regular epimorphism–monomorphism image factorizations whose regular epimorphisms are stable under every pullback in a category. Equivalently, finite limits, pullback-stable regular epimorphism–monomorphism factorizations suffice. In particular, every strong epimorphism in a regular category is regular.
For the weaker hypotheses here, interpret an image factorization as f=me with m:I↪B the least subobject through which f factors, and e:A→I a strong epimorphism. With pullback in a category constructions, the least-subobject characterization itself implies that e is strong: in a lifting square against a monomorphism, pull that monomorphism back to I; since e factors through this pullback, image minimality forces its mono into I to be invertible, giving the required diagonal. Thus the two descriptions of images agree under the stated hypotheses.
For a:A′↪A, define ∃f​(A′) as the image factorization subobject of fa:A′→B. For b:B′↪B, the pullback in a category universal property and image minimality give
∃f​(A′)≤B′⟺fa factors through b⟺A′≤f∗(B′).
(28)
Hence
∃f​⊣f∗.​
(29)
This is an adjunction between the posets of subobjects.
For Frobenius reciprocity for subobjects, factor fa=me through its image m:I↪B, and put P=I×B​B′. Its mono into B represents ∃f​(A′)∩B′. The pullback in a category of e along the monomorphism P→I has domain canonically A′×B​B′, which represents A′∩f∗(B′). If strong epimorphisms are stable under pullback along monomorphisms, that pulled-back arrow is strong, so its composite with P↪B is an image factorization. This gives
∃f​(A′∩f∗B′)=∃f​(A′)∩B′.​
(30)
Equality here is equality of subobjects, hence an isomorphism of their representatives.
Conversely, let e:X→Y be a strong epimorphism and b:B′↪Y be monic. Its image is all of Y: in any image factorization of e, the lifting property makes the mono invertible. Apply Frobenius reciprocity for subobjects to f=e and A′=X. The induced morphism p:X×Y​B′→B′ has image all of B′, because the image of bp in Y is B′. Its image factorization therefore has invertible mono, so p is a strong epimorphism. This proves the converse.
We now use the glued ring categories counterexample to regularity. All rings and ring homomorphisms are unital; the zero ring, where 0=1, is allowed. It is the terminal object of Rng, and there is no unital ring homomorphism from it to a nonzero ring. Denote the common zero ring by Z, and the newly adjoined strict initial object by ⊥. Nonzero objects in different copies have no morphisms between them. There is one morphism from every object to Z and from ⊥ to every object, and no morphism into ⊥ except its identity.
Finite products in a category and equalizers can be described explicitly. The terminal object is Z. The product in a category of two nonzero objects in one copy is their ordinary ring product; the product in a category of nonzero objects in different copies is ⊥, since only ⊥ can map to both. A product with Z is the other factor, and one with ⊥ is ⊥. For parallel morphisms between nonzero objects in the same copy, the ordinary ring equalizer works; it is nonzero because its subring contains distinct 0 and 1. All other parallel pairs are equal, and their equalizer is the identity of the domain. Thus the construction of small limits from products and equalizers gives all finite categorical limits.
Within either ring copy, monomorphisms are precisely injective ring homomorphisms: maps from Z[x] detect unequal elements. The only monos from outside a copy are the maps ⊥→X, and the only subobjects of Z are ⊥ and Z. Indeed a nonzero ring R is not subterminal, since Z[x]→R can send x to 0 or to 1.
Ordinary ring-image factorizations remain image factorizations in the glued category. Their surjective parts remain strong epimorphisms: a lifting square into a nonzero ring stays in one copy; a square into Z with mono ⊥→Z would require a nonexistent map from a noninitial domain to ⊥. Maps with domain ⊥ are already monic and have identity strong part. Conversely, a strong epimorphism within a ring copy has surjective ring image, since its mono image part must be invertible. A map ⊥→X with X=⊥ is monic but noninvertible, and therefore cannot be strong. This classifies the strong epimorphisms as the identities of ⊥ and the surjective homomorphisms in the copies, including maps to Z.
Pullback along a monomorphism within a copy preserves those surjections, using the given regularity of Rng. Pullback along ⊥→X gives 1⊥​, and these exhaust the additional monos, including those into Z. Thus strong epimorphisms are stable under pullback along monos, so Frobenius reciprocity for subobjects holds.
However, let e:ZD​→Z be the surjective ring homomorphism in the first copy, and pull it back along Z[x]E​→Z in the other copy. The resulting arrow is
⊥⟶Z[x]E​.
(31)
It is not even an epimorphism, since the distinct evaluation ring homomorphisms x↦0 and x↦1 to ZE​ agree after composing with ⊥→Z[x]E​. Since e is strong, it would be a regular epimorphism in a regular category, whose pullbacks must be regular, in particular epic. Therefore the glued category has finite limits and images and satisfies Frobenius reciprocity, but is not regular.

A pointed category has a zero object, both initial and terminal. Factoring through that object gives a zero morphism 0A,B​ between any two objects. In this paper a semi-additive structure means commutative-monoid enrichment: every hom-set is a commutative monoid with additive zero, and composition distributes over addition in both variables. No existence of finite products in a category is included in this last definition. This convention matters for the final one-object example; under the stronger convention requiring finite biproducts, that example would not be a semi-additive category.
Let cA,B​:A+B→A×B be the given canonical isomorphism. Transfer the coproduct injections across cA,B​, so A×B is also a coproduct in a category, with injections
i1​=⟨1A​,0⟩,i2​=⟨0,1B​⟩.
(32)
Write ∇B​:B×B→B for the morphism whose composites with i1​,i2​ are both 1B​. For f,g:A→B, define the biproduct-induced addition of morphisms by
f+g=∇B​⟨f,g⟩=[1B​,1B​]cB,B−1​⟨f,g⟩.​
(33)
The zero is the existing zero morphism. We verify the laws from the universal properties, without assuming addition in advance.
All finite canonical maps from coproducts to products are isomorphisms, by induction from the binary ones and the zero object. Thus both (B×B)×B and B×(B×B) are ternary coproducts in a category. The two iterated fold maps to B agree on each of the three injections, hence are equal. Applying this to ⟨f,g,h⟩ gives associativity. The interchange B×B→B×B swaps the two injections, so its composite with ∇B​ is ∇B​, giving commutativity. Finally ⟨f,0⟩=i1​f, so f+0=f, and similarly 0+f=f.
Precomposition is additive because ⟨f,g⟩u=⟨fu,gu⟩. For v:B→C, the morphisms v∇B​ and ∇C​(v×v) agree on both injections, so they agree; consequently v(f+g)=vf+vg. Composition with a zero morphism is zero. This proves the commutative-monoid enrichment.
It is unique. In any such enrichment, the additive zero morphisms agree with the pointed ones, since each map to or from the zero object belongs to a singleton hom-set. The projections of i1​p1​+i2​p2​ from A×B are respectively p1​,p2​ by bilinearity, so
i1​p1​+i2​p2​=1A×B​.
(34)
For A=B, composing on the left by ∇B​ and on the right by ⟨f,g⟩ forces the displayed formula for f+g. Thus the biproducts determine exactly one such enrichment.
For the last part, the underlying one-object category has endomorphism monoid (N,⋅), including 0, and identity 1. Its usual addition gives a commutative-monoid enrichment. Any permutation σ of the prime numbers extends, by unique prime factorization, to a multiplicative monoid automorphism ϕσ​:N→N, fixing 0,1. Transport addition by
a⊕σ​b=ϕσ−1​(ϕσ​(a)+ϕσ​(b)).​
(35)
This is a commutative monoid operation with zero 0, and multiplication distributes over it: applying ϕσ​ reduces each distributive law to the ordinary one in N. Thus each operation supplies a semi-additive structure on the same fixed composition law.
For each odd prime p, take σ to interchange 2 and p, fixing every other prime. Then
1⊕σ​1=ϕσ−1​(2)=p.
(36)
Different choices of p give different additions, and there are infinitely many prime numbers. Hence there are infinitely many distinct semi-additive structures, even though these transported structures are isomorphic as enriched categories. The underlying one-object category has no terminal object, because its endomorphism set is not a singleton, so it indeed has no finite products in a category.
