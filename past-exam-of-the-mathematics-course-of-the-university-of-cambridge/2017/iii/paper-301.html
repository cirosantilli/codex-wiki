Use natural units ℏ=c=1 and the Minkowski metric η=diag(1,−1,−1,−1) throughout. An Abelian gauge transformation is Aμ​↦Aμ​+∂μ​χ. For a sufficiently regular χ, commuting the partial derivatives gives Fμν​↦Fμν​, so the Maxwell Lagrangian is invariant pointwise.
Vary the action with respect to the electromagnetic four-potential, using variations of compact support or vanishing on the boundary. The antisymmetry of the electromagnetic field tensor gives
δL=−21​FμνδFμν​=−Fμν∂μ​δAν​.
(1)
After integration by parts, δS=∫d4x(∂μ​Fμν)δAν​. The principle of stationary action therefore gives the source-free Maxwell equations,
∂μ​Fμν=0⟺□Aν−∂ν(∂μ​Aμ)=0.​
(2)
In the Lorenz gauge, ∂μ​Aμ=0, this becomes □Aν​=0 with □=∂μ​∂μ. The PDF's name “Lorentz gauge” denotes the usual Lorenz gauge, named after Lorenz. Residual gauge transformations preserve it when □χ=0; this condition is not itself a complete removal of the gauge freedom.

Write D=∂μ​Aμ. The additional gauge fixing variation is δ(−D2/2)=−D∂μ​δAμ. Integration by parts gives the Euler-Lagrange field equation ∂μ​Fμν+∂νD=0, hence □Aν=0. This equation follows from the gauge-fixed density without imposing D=0 as a separate operator identity.
Direct differentiation of the density as printed gives the canonical momentum components conjugate to the lower-index fields:
πν=∂A˙ν​∂L​=−F0ν−η0νD,π0=−D,πi=A˙i​−∂i​A0​.​
(3)
There is a boundary-term convention to reconcile with part (c). The Feynman-gauge Maxwell kinetic density after a boundary-term subtraction is
L′=−21​∂μ​Aν​∂μAν,L=L′+∂μ​Kμ,Kμ=21​(Aν​∂νAμ−AμD).
(4)
It has the same Euler-Lagrange field equations, but its canonical momentum components are π′ν=−A˙ν. The mode expansion supplied in part (c) is the expansion of these latter momenta. Thus it is valid after the stated boundary-term subtraction; it is not the direct derivative of the original density. For example, a time-independent spatially varying A0​ with Ai​=0 gives πi=−∂i​A0​ but π′i=0.
The boundary-term shift of canonical field momenta is a canonical transformation. If spatial boundary terms vanish, B=∫d3xK0=∫d3xA0​∂i​Ai​, and πν=π′ν+δB/δAν​. This explains why the two conventions have the same dynamics while their momentum formulas differ.

Use the supplied oscillator expansion, with the boundary-term convention for momentum stated in part (b). Use a real polarization vector basis, as in the supplied unconjugated polarization completeness relation. Raise its second field index to obtain
∑λ,λ′​ϵμλ​ϵνλ′ηλλ′=δμ​ν.
(5)
The mixed commutator contains only the annihilation-creation and creation-annihilation terms. Their signs are both positive after combining the minus sign in the momentum expansion with the negative Minkowski metric oscillator commutator. Setting r=x−y and using the momentum Dirac delta distribution gives
[Aμ​(x),πν(y)]=2i​δμ​ν∫(2π)3d3p​(eip⋅r+e−ip⋅r)=iδμ​νδ3(r).
(6)

[Aμ​(x),Aν​(y)]=−ημν​∫(2π)3d3p​2∣p∣eip⋅r−e−ip⋅r​=0,
(7)
because the integrand is odd under p↦−p. The momentum-momentum commutator is proportional to the same odd difference, now weighted by ∣p∣/2, and also vanishes. Therefore the equal-time canonical commutation relations are
[Aμ​(x),πν(y)]=iδμ​νδ3(x−y),[Aμ​,Aν​]=[πμ,πν]=0.​
(8)
These are identities of operator-valued distributions, understood after smearing. The extra indices in the TeX polarization relation are transcription defects; the PDF has the ordinary two-polarization completeness contraction used above. The direct momenta of part (b) also satisfy the canonical commutation relations after their boundary-generated shift, although they do not have the unmodified mode expansion printed here.

d OurBigBook.com
The oscillator-generated covariant photon Fock space has an indefinite Hermitian form, not a positive Hilbert space inner product. For a normalizable one-photon wave packet of polarization λ, its squared norm is proportional to −ηλλ∫d3p∣f(p)∣2. Thus λ=0 gives a negative-norm photon state. The divergent δ3(0) of an unsmeared momentum eigenstate is a separate normalization issue, avoided by the wave packet.
Choose contravariant polarization vectors ϵ0μ=(1,0), ϵ3μ=(0,p​), and two ϵrμ=(0,er​) with er​⋅p=0 for r=1,2. The third spatial polarization is longitudinal polarization; the first two are transverse polarization. The timelike photon polarization is distinct from the longitudinal one.
The Gupta-Bleuler quantization condition sets the divergence of the positive-frequency part of a quantum field to zero on physical states:
(∂μ​Aμ)(+)∣Ψ⟩=0.
(9)
Restore the factors e−i∣p∣t to the annihilation terms. Since pμ​ϵ0μ=∣p∣ and pμ​ϵ3μ=−∣p∣, Fourier transform gives the equivalent condition
(ap0​−ap3​)∣Ψ⟩=0for every nonzero momentum, distributionally.​
(10)
Its sign depends on the chosen sign of the longitudinal polarization vector; the covariant condition does not.
To see its content for a general Fock state, temporarily discretize momentum and decompose one unphysical oscillator sector as ∣Ψ⟩=∑n,m≥0​∣n,m⟩0,3​⊗∣χnm​⟩⊥​, where ∣n,m⟩=(a0†)n(a3†)m∣0⟩/n!m!​. The temporal oscillator obeys a0∣n,m⟩=−n​∣n−1,m⟩, while a3∣n,m⟩=m​∣n,m−1⟩. The condition therefore becomes
n+1​∣χn+1,m​⟩+m+1​∣χn,m+1​⟩=0.
(11)
Equivalently, the allowed finite-particle states use the transverse creation operators and only b†=a0†−a3† in the unphysical sector. Indeed the constraint acts on a polynomial of the two unphysical creation operators as −∂a0†​−∂a3†​, whose kernel consists of polynomials in their difference. Also [b,b†]=−1+1=0, and a state containing b† is orthogonal to every constrained state because b annihilates every such state. This argument applies mode by mode and extends by smearing to continuum momentum.
For one photon, c0​a0†∣0⟩+c3​a3†∣0⟩ is constrained only when c3​=−c0​, producing a null state. The Gupta-Bleuler null-state quotient removes these null directions. The condition excludes negative-norm physical states; quotienting its null states leaves the two positive-norm transverse photon polarizations. The condition alone gives a positive semidefinite Hermitian form, not yet a positive definite Hilbert space.

The gamma matrices represent the spacetime Clifford algebra. With the Minkowski metric convention above,
{γμ,γν}=γμγν+γνγμ=2ημνI4​.​
(12)
The braces denote the anticommutator. Consequently (γ0)2=I4​, (γi)2=−I4​, and distinct gamma matrices anticommute. The identity matrix is essential: the right side is a matrix equality, not a scalar equality. These signs agree with the explicit Dirac representation of the gamma matrices in the next part.

A Dirac-to-Weyl gamma-matrix similarity transform with the required signs is
U=2​1​(I2​I2​​−I2​I2​​),γμ=Uγ​μU−1.​
(16)
It is a unitary matrix, with U−1=U†=2−1/2(I2​−I2​​I2​I2​​). Multiplication gives

(17)

A similarity transformation preserves every Clifford algebra relation, so no new consistency check is needed for the transformed representation.

Use a local first-derivative Lagrangian density L(ϕ,∂μ​ϕ,x) with sufficient differentiability, and let variations have compact support or vanish at the spacetime boundary. If the phrase “a function of the field” were read literally as forbidding derivatives, the equation below would reduce to ∂L/∂ϕ=0; a propagating field requires derivative dependence. Varying the action gives
δS=∫d4x(Lϕ​δϕ+Πμ∂μ​δϕ),Πμ=∂(∂μ​ϕ)∂L​.
(29)
Integration by parts and arbitrary interior variations yield the Euler-Lagrange field equation,
E(ϕ):=Lϕ​−∂μ​Πμ=0.​
(30)
The boundary condition is part of this derivation; if boundary variations are allowed, their separate boundary equations must also be imposed.
A variational symmetry of a Lagrangian density is an infinitesimal field change δϕ=εX for which δL=ε∂μ​Kμ off shell. Equality to zero is sufficient but is not necessary: a divergence changes only the boundary contribution to the action. For fixed-coordinate field variations,
εδL​=E(ϕ)X+∂μ​(ΠμX).
(31)
Equating the two expressions proves the scalar-field form of the Noether theorem:
jμ=ΠμX−Kμ,∂μ​jμ=−E(ϕ)X=0on shell.​
(32)
Thus every differentiable one-parameter variational symmetry gives a conserved current, and its Noether charge Q=∫d3xj0 is constant when the spatial boundary flux vanishes. This is the Noether current for a first-derivative scalar field; the same identity applies to several real components by summing over them. Identically conserved improvement terms can change its local expression without changing the charge under the same boundary assumptions.
For an active spacetime translation, ϕ′(x)=ϕ(x−a) and δϕ=−aν∂ν​ϕ. If the Lagrangian density has no explicit coordinate dependence, δL=−aν∂ν​L, so Kμ=−aμL. The Noether current is jμ=−aνTμν​, where
Tμν​=Πμ∂ν​ϕ−δμν​L,∂μ​Tμν​=0.​
(33)
These four translation currents are the canonical stress-energy tensor, also called the energy-momentum tensor. The sign in j=−aT follows from the chosen active translation; the translation charges themselves can be labelled by Pν​=∫T0ν​d3x.
For the free real scalar field, take the standard kinetic term and mass term. Its Lagrangian density and Klein-Gordon equation are
L=21​∂μ​ϕ∂μϕ−21​m2ϕ2,(□+m2)ϕ=0.​
(34)
Here Πμ=∂μϕ, so Tμν=∂μϕ∂νϕ−ημνL. Raising the charge index gives the four-momentum of a free real scalar field,
E=P0=21​∫d3x(ϕ˙​2+∣∇ϕ∣2+m2ϕ2),P=−∫d3xϕ˙​∇ϕ.​
(35)
The first is total energy, and the three components of the second are physical spatial momentum. With signature (+,−,−,−), Pi​=−Pi; this explains the opposite sign if the conserved quantities are instead written with lower spatial indices. For example, a plane wave proportional to cos(Et−k⋅x) has momentum density along k, confirming the sign. All charges require convergence of the integrals and vanishing boundary flux, or periodic boundary conditions in a finite box.
For the complex scalar field, treat ψ and ψ∗ as independent variables when varying, equivalently use their two real components. The global phase symmetry of a complex scalar field is ψ↦e−iαψ, ψ∗↦eiαψ∗ for constant α. Both the kinetic term and V(∣ψ∣2) are invariant. This is a global internal symmetry of a classical field theory with circle group U(1); a spacetime-dependent phase would require a gauge connection.
Choosing this orientation for the phase parameter gives the Noether charge of a complex scalar field:
jμ=i(ψ∗∂μψ−ψ∂μψ∗),Q=i∫d3x(ψ∗ψ˙​−ψψ˙​∗).​
(36)
To check conservation directly, the Euler-Lagrange field equations are □ψ+V′(∣ψ∣2)ψ=0 and their complex conjugates, assuming a real differentiable potential. Hence ∂μ​jμ=i(ψ∗□ψ−ψ□ψ∗)=0. Reversing the phase-parameter orientation reverses the current and charge, which is merely a convention. For a scalar carrying electric charge q, the physical electric charge is qQ after electromagnetic coupling. In a neutral theory the same global charge can instead label an internal conserved quantum number; the density alone does not identify it automatically with electricity.

For ordinary bosonic operators at distinct times, time ordering places the later operator to the left:
T{O1​(t1​)O2​(t2​)}={O1​(t1​)O2​(t2​),O2​(t2​)O1​(t1​),​t1​>t2​,t2​>t1​.​​
(37)
Equivalently, multiply these two products by the corresponding Heaviside step functions. For two odd fermionic operators the second product receives a minus sign; more generally a graded interchange has sign (−1)F1​F2​. This sign is the fermionic sign convention for time-ordered field products, not a minus sign for arbitrary bosonic operators. No equal-time convention is needed for the two distinct times in this clause.

ΔF​(τ,r)=∫(2π)3d3p​2Ep​eip⋅r​(θ(τ)e−iEp​τ+θ(−τ)eiEp​τ).

gE​(τ)=limϵ↓0​∫2πdp0​(p0)2−E2+iϵie−ip0τ​.

Here the residue step uses E>0; the massless zero-momentum point is interpreted through the smeared distribution limit, not as an isolated normalized oscillator. The prescription in this formula supplies the pole convention left unspecified in the printed display; an unprescribed ordinary real-axis integral would not be well-defined. With this normalization the derivative jump of a free scalar time-ordered two-point function gives (□+m2)ΔF​=−iδ4(x−y), an independent check of both the numerator and the sign.
