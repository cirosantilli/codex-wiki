Use the Fourier transform convention φ​(ξ)=∫e−ix⋅ξφ(x)dx and Dj​=−i∂j​, so P(D)u​=P(ξ)u. All distribution pairings use complex bilinearity, and the formal transpose is P(−D), without complex conjugation of the coefficients. If Dj​ instead denotes ∂j​, the Fourier multiplier is P(iξ); the argument below is unchanged after this substitution.
For a nonempty compact convex set K⊂Rn, let HK​(η)=supx∈K​x⋅η be its support function. The Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem says that the Fourier transform is a bijection between distributions supported in K and entire functions F on Cn for which
∣F(ζ)∣≤C(1+∣ζ∣)NeHK​(Imζ)​
(1)
for some C>0 and nonnegative integer N. In particular, support in BR​ is equivalent to the bound with eR∣Imζ∣. Convexity is essential in the precise support formulation: the support function of a set equals that of its convex hull.
Suppose first that u is a compactly supported distribution with support in K. Define F(ζ)=⟨u,e−ix⋅ζ⟩, inserting any cutoff function equal to one near K when viewing u on test functions. A fixed cutoff and the finite order of a distribution estimate permit differentiation of the pairing in each complex variable, giving ∂ζα​F=⟨u,(−ix)αe−ix⋅ζ⟩. The exponential function's power series converges with every required derivative on a fixed compact set; hence F is an entire function. On real frequencies it agrees with the Fourier transform of a compactly supported distribution.
The exact exponential indicator needs a shrinking cutoff function, rather than one fixed outside K. Choose χε​=1 near K, with support in K+B2ε​ and ∥∂αχε​∥∞​≤Cα​ε−∣α∣ for 0<ε≤1. Such a cutoff is obtained by convolving the indicator of K+Bε​ with a mollifier supported in Bε/2​. On one fixed ball containing all these supports, the finite order of a distribution bound is
∣⟨u,θ⟩∣≤C0​max∣α∣≤m​∥∂αθ∥∞​.
(2)
The Leibniz rule applied to θ=χε​e−ix⋅ζ therefore gives
∣F(ζ)∣≤C1​ε−m(1+∣ζ∣)meHK​(Imζ)+2ε∣Imζ∣.
(3)
Taking ε=(1+∣ζ∣)−1 proves the required bound, with N=2m and a fixed additional factor at most e2.
Conversely, suppose F is an entire function with the displayed bound. Its real restriction has polynomial growth, so its inverse Fourier transform defines a tempered distribution by
⟨u,φ⟩=(2π)−n∫Rn​F(ξ)φ​(−ξ)dξ,φ∈S.
(4)
Rapid decay of the Schwartz function φ​ makes this integral absolutely convergent and continuous in the Schwartz space topology.
To determine the support of a distribution, take a test function supported in a half-space x⋅ω≥HK​(ω)+δ, where ∣ω∣=1 and δ>0. The contour-shift proof of the Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem gives, for each t≥0,
⟨u,φ⟩=(2π)−n∫Rn​F(ξ+itω)φ​(−ξ−itω)dξ.
(5)
Here is the estimate that justifies both the shift and its limiting use. Since φ​(−ξ−itω)=∫eix⋅ξe−tx⋅ωφ(x)dx, repeated integration by parts with 1−Δx​ yields
∣φ​(−ξ−itω)∣≤CL​(1+t)2Le−t(HK​(ω)+δ)(1+∣ξ∣2)−L.
(6)
For fixed t, the same estimate is uniform over the imaginary strip from 0 to tω. Rotate coordinates so ω is the first coordinate direction, apply the Cauchy integral theorem on truncated rectangles, and let their real edges tend to infinity. If 2L>N+n, the boundary integrals vanish and the horizontal integrals converge absolutely; thus the claimed shift is valid. Using HK​(tω)=tHK​(ω) now bounds the pairing by
C′(1+t)N+2Le−δt∫Rn​(1+∣ξ∣)N−2Ldξ,
(7)
which tends to zero. A separating hyperplane exists at every point outside the compact convex set K. A finite partition of unity on the support of any exterior test function reduces it to such half-spaces, so suppu⊆K. Fourier inversion gives uniqueness; the entire transform of this u agrees with F on Rn and hence everywhere, by applying the one-variable identity theorem successively in each coordinate. This completes both directions.
Now apply this theorem to compact support solvability for a constant-coefficient ordinary differential equation. If P is a nonzero constant, its kernel is zero and u=v/P is the unique solution. Otherwise write
P(ζ)=am​∏j=1m​(ζ−αj​),αj​=αk​(j=k).
(8)
The solution space of P(−D)φ=0 is
N=span{e−iα1​x,…,e−iαm​x}.
(9)
Indeed these exponentials solve the equation, their initial derivative vectors have a nonzero Vandermonde determinant, and uniqueness for the order-m ordinary differential equation makes them a basis. Pairing P(D)u=v with any such solution proves necessity:
⟨v,φ⟩=⟨u,P(−D)φ⟩=0.
(10)
For sufficiency, choose R with suppv⊆[−R,R] and let V(ζ)=⟨v,e−ixζ⟩. The assumed annihilation is precisely V(αj​)=0 for every j. Since all roots of a polynomial are simple, U=V/P has removable singularities at every root and is an entire function. Outside one disk, ∣P(ζ)∣≥c(1+∣ζ∣)m. Inside that disk, the extended U is bounded. The polynomial division preservation of exponential type therefore gives
∣U(ζ)∣≤C′(1+∣ζ∣)N′eR∣Imζ∣.
(11)
The Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem produces a compactly supported distribution u with transform U, supported in the same interval. Then P(D)u​=PU=V, so Fourier inversion gives P(D)u=v. Thus
v∈P(D)E′(R) ⟺ ⟨v,φ⟩=0 for every φ∈N.​
(12)
The compactly supported solution is unique: Pu=0 forces the entire transform to vanish off the finitely many roots and hence everywhere. With repeated roots, the corresponding condition would also require derivatives V(k)(α)=0, or pairing with xke−iαx, up to one less than each multiplicity.

For multi-indices α,β, define
pα,β​(φ)=supx∈Rn​∣xα∂βφ(x)∣,S(Rn)={φ∈C∞:pα,β​(φ)<∞ for all α,β}.
(13)
These seminorms define the Fréchet space topology of the Schwartz space: φj​→φ exactly when every pα,β​(φj​−φ) tends to zero. An equivalent increasing family is
qm​(φ)=max∣β∣≤m​supx​(1+∣x∣)m∣∂βφ(x)∣.
(14)
A tempered distribution is a continuous linear functional on this space. Equivalently, ∣⟨T,φ⟩∣≤Cqm​(φ) for some C,m. The usual weak convergence of tempered distributions means ⟨Tj​,φ⟩→⟨T,φ⟩ for every fixed Schwartz function. The strong dual topology instead requires uniform convergence on every subset of the Schwartz space that is a bounded set in a topological vector space; the Fourier maps below are continuous in both topologies.
Using φ​(ξ)=∫e−ix⋅ξφ(x)dx, differentiation under the integral and integration by parts give
​ξα∂ξβ​φ​(ξ)​≤​∂xα​(xβφ)​L1​.
(15)
The omitted coefficient has modulus one. By the Leibniz rule, every integrand is a finite sum of a polynomial times a derivative of φ. Inserting the integrable weight (1+∣x∣)−n−1 bounds its L1 norm by finitely many Schwartz space seminorms. In particular qm​(φ​)≤Cm​qm+n+1​(φ). Thus the Fourier transform maps S continuously into itself.
For completeness, the Fourier inversion theorem follows here by Gaussian regularization. The inverse transform of e−t∣ξ∣2 is gt​(x)=(4πt)−n/2e−∣x∣2/(4t). The Fourier transform of a Gaussian and Fubini's theorem show
(2π)−n∫eix⋅ξe−t∣ξ∣2φ​(ξ)dξ=(gt​∗φ)(x).
(16)
As t↓0, the right side tends to φ(x) by the approximate identity property, while the left side tends to the undamped inverse integral by dominated convergence theorem, since φ​∈L1. Hence
F−1ψ(x)=(2π)−n∫eix⋅ξψ(ξ)dξ,F2φ(x)=(2π)nφ(−x).​
(17)
The inverse is (2π)−n times reflection composed with the continuous Fourier transform, and is therefore continuous on the Schwartz space. This proves a continuous linear isomorphism with continuous inverse.
Define the Fourier transform of a tempered distribution by
⟨T,φ⟩=⟨T,φ​⟩.
(18)
The continuous map on Schwartz space makes this a tempered distribution; the transpose of F−1 supplies its inverse. Pointwise convergence of pairings proves weak continuity. For the strong dual topology, the transform of a bounded set of Schwartz functions is bounded, so uniform convergence of pairings on bounded sets proves continuity of both Fourier maps there too.
Now let R∈SO(n) be a rotation matrix in the special orthogonal group and write ρR​T=T∘R. A change of variables with unit Jacobian gives
φ∘Rt​(ξ)=φ​(Rtξ).
(19)
The rotation equivariance of the Fourier transform on tempered distributions follows by duality:
⟨ρR​T​,φ⟩​=⟨T,φ​∘Rt⟩=⟨T,φ∘Rt​⟩=⟨T,φ∘Rt⟩=⟨ρR​T,φ⟩.​
(20)
Therefore ρR​T​=ρR​T. Applying this identity and the inverse Fourier transform gives the two directions:
T∘R=T for every R∈SO(n) ⟺ T∘R=T for every R∈SO(n).​
(21)
This is precisely preservation of radial tempered distributions. For n>1, SO(n) acts transitively on spheres, so an invariant smooth function is an ordinary radial function.

The multiplication of a distribution by a smooth function is ⟨ψT,φ⟩=⟨T,ψφ⟩. If ψ is a Schwartz function, the Leibniz rule shows that φ↦ψφ is a continuous map S→S, so ψT is a tempered distribution. When both ψ and T are radial, ψ∘Rt=ψ, and
⟨(ψT)∘R,φ⟩=⟨T,ψ(φ∘Rt)⟩=⟨T,(ψφ)∘Rt⟩=⟨T,ψφ⟩.
(22)
Thus Multiplication by a radial Schwartz function preserves radial tempered distributions.
For the convolution of a tempered distribution with a Schwartz function, set
(T∗ψ)(x)=⟨Ty​,ψ(x−y)⟩.
(23)
Translations of ψ depend smoothly on x in the Schwartz space, so this is a smooth function with ∂α(T∗ψ)(x)=⟨Ty​,∂αψ(x−y)⟩. The bound for T by finitely many seminorms, together with 1+∣y∣≤(1+∣x∣)(1+∣x−y∣), proves
∣∂α(T∗ψ)(x)∣≤Cα​(1+∣x∣)m.
(24)
Thus the function also defines a tempered distribution. For a radial function ψ, put gx​(y)=ψ(x−y). Then ψ(Rx−y)=ψ(x−Rty)=gx​(Rty), so
(T∗ψ)(Rx)=⟨T,gx​∘Rt⟩=⟨T∘R,gx​⟩=(T∗ψ)(x).
(25)
Hence Convolution with a radial Schwartz function preserves radial tempered distributions.
Smoothing alone need not give a Schwartz function: for the constant tempered distribution T=1 and a Schwartz function with integral one, T∗ψ=1. The radial Schwartz approximation of tempered distributions therefore combines smoothing with a large-radius cutoff. Choose a nonnegative radial mollifier ρ∈Cc∞​, supported in the unit ball with integral one, and a radial cutoff function χ∈Cc∞​ equal to one on the unit ball. Put
ρε​(x)=ε−nρ(x/ε),χj​(x)=χ(x/j),fj​(x)=χj​(x)(T∗ρ1/j​)(x).​
(26)
Each fj​ is a smooth function of compact support, hence a Schwartz function, and the two invariance calculations above make it radial.
It remains to prove convergence, including the simultaneous changes of both scales. With ρˇ​(x)=ρ(−x), the distributional convolution pairing is
⟨fj​,φ⟩=⟨T,ρˇ​1/j​∗(χj​φ)⟩.
(27)
For every fixed m, the Leibniz rule, rapid decay outside the radius-j ball, and the chain rule for χ(x/j) give
qm​((χj​−1)φ)≤jCm​​qm+1​(φ).
(28)
Convolution by ρˇ​ε​ is uniformly bounded in qm​ for 0<ε≤1, because its shifts have size at most one. The mean value theorem applied to φ(x−y)−φ(x) similarly gives
qm​(ρˇ​ε​∗φ−φ)≤Cm​εqm+1​(φ).
(29)
Splitting the error into the convolved cutoff error and the approximate identity error proves
qm​(ρˇ​1/j​∗(χj​φ)−φ)≤jCm′​​qm+1​(φ).
(30)
If ∣⟨T,θ⟩∣≤Cqm​(θ), this yields
∣⟨fj​−T,φ⟩∣≤jC′′​qm+1​(φ).
(31)
Consequently
fj​⟶Tin S′​
(32)
weakly, and even in the strong dual topology, since qm+1​ is uniformly bounded on every subset of the Schwartz space that is a bounded set in a topological vector space. No assertion that T∗ρ1/j​ itself is rapidly decreasing is needed.

Again use Dj​=−i∂j​ and distribution dual pairings with complex bilinearity. The Malgrange–Ehrenpreis theorem asserts that every nonzero constant-coefficient differential operator defined by a polynomial has a fundamental solution of a linear differential operator:
for P=0 there is E∈D′(Rn) with P(D)E=δ0​.​
(33)
If P=a=0 is constant, take E=δ0​/a. For positive degree m, write Pm​ for the highest homogeneous part. A nonzero polynomial cannot vanish on all of Rn, so choose a real unit vector e with Pm​(e)=0. A change of coordinates by an orthogonal matrix makes it the last coordinate direction. In these coordinates,
P(ξ′,z)=azm+∑k<m​ak​(ξ′)zk,a=Pm​(e)=0.
(34)
Crucially the leading coefficient a is constant and never vanishes as ξ′∈Rn−1 varies.
The finite-height polynomial root avoidance construction gives the needed Hörmander staircase. Set hℓ​=3ℓ for 0≤ℓ≤m. For each fixed ξ′, the fundamental theorem of algebra supplies m roots z1​,…,zm​, with multiplicity. A root's imaginary part can be within distance strictly less than one of at most one candidate height, since those heights are separated by three. The pigeonhole principle therefore leaves at least one height with ∣hℓ​−Imzj​∣≥1 for all roots. At that height, for every real s,
∣P(ξ′,s+ihℓ​)∣=∣a∣∏j=1m​∣s+ihℓ​−zj​∣≥∣a∣.
(35)
No continuous labelling of the roots is required. To select the heights measurably, define
Bℓ​=⋂q∈Q​{ξ′:∣P(ξ′,q+ihℓ​)∣≥∣a∣},Aℓ​=Bℓ​∖⋃k<ℓ​Bk​.
(36)
Each Bℓ​ is closed, since the coefficients are continuous in ξ′. Continuity in s makes the rational test equivalent to the bound for all s∈R. The Aℓ​ are therefore disjoint Borel sets covering Rn−1. The union of horizontal fibres
Σ=⋃ℓ=0m​{(ξ′,s+ihℓ​):ξ′∈Aℓ​, s∈R}
(37)
is a Hörmander staircase, with bounded imaginary height and a uniform nonzero denominator. For n=1, the transverse space has one point, and this is simply the choice of one horizontal line.
For example, P(ξ1​,z)=ξ12​+z2 has roots z=±iξ1​. One may select h=0 for ∣ξ1​∣≥1, and h=3 for ∣ξ1​∣<1. Then the first region has ∣P∣≥1, while in the second each root is at least two units from the chosen height. The figure shows the height projection of these horizontal fibres, rather than additional connecting contours.
Define a linear functional on test functions by
⟨E,φ⟩=(2π)−n∑ℓ=0m​∫Aℓ​​∫R​P(ξ′,s+ihℓ​)φ​(−ξ′,−s−ihℓ​)​dsdξ′.
(38)
To establish that this is a distribution, fix a compact set containing the support of φ, inside a radius-R ball. The Fourier decay in a bounded complex strip estimate follows by applying (1−Δ)L to e−hℓ​xn​φ(x):
∣φ​(−ξ′,−s−ihℓ​)∣≤CR,L​max∣α∣≤2L​∥∂αφ∥∞​(1+∣ξ′∣2+s2)−L,0≤hℓ​≤3m.
(39)
The finite bound on the heights absorbs all exponential factors and powers of hℓ​ into CR,L​. Taking 2L>n and using ∣P∣≥∣a∣ proves absolute convergence and the required finite-order continuity on each fixed support set. Hence E∈D′.
Now the formal transpose identity and the Fourier derivative identity give
P(−D)φ​(−ξ′,−z)=P(ξ′,z)φ​(−ξ′,−z).
(40)
The polynomial denominator cancels in the pairing for P(D)E. For each fixed real ξ′, the remaining function of z is entire and rapidly decreasing in its real part throughout 0≤Imz≤3m. The Cauchy integral theorem therefore allows each horizontal fibre to be shifted to the real axis; its truncated vertical end integrals tend to zero. Absolute convergence justifies Fubini's theorem and the finite sum over the measurable partition. Thus
⟨P(D)E,φ⟩​=(2π)−nℓ=0∑m​∫Aℓ​​∫R​φ​(−ξ′,−s)dsdξ′=(2π)−n∫Rn​φ​(−ξ)dξ=φ(0)=⟨δ0​,φ⟩.​
(41)
The last equality is Fourier inversion. Transforming back from the orthogonal coordinates preserves δ0​ and gives the required fundamental solution of a linear differential operator for the original operator. The staircase is allowed to jump: after cancellation the shift is performed on each one-dimensional fibre, so no assertion that Σ is a smooth contour is used. This construction proves a distributional fundamental solution, without claiming a tempered-growth estimate.
For f∈D(Rn), use the smoothing convolution with a test function
u0​=E∗f,P(D)u0​=(P(D)E)∗f=δ0​∗f=f.​
(42)
Indeed u0​(x)=⟨Ey​,f(x−y)⟩ is a smooth function, with ∂αu0​(x)=⟨Ey​,∂αf(x−y)⟩. On each compact set of x values, all translated test functions have supports in one fixed compact set, so differentiation is justified by the continuity of the distribution. Compact support of f makes the convolution well defined even when E is not tempered. It does not imply compact support of u0​.
Finally the affine solution space of a linear equation is
{u:P(D)u=f}=u0​+kerP(D).
(43)
If every homogeneous solution is represented by a smooth function, so is every u=u0​+v. Conversely, if every solution for this fixed f is represented by a smooth function, then for any homogeneous solution v, the solution u0​+v is smooth, and subtracting the smooth u0​ shows that v is smooth. Hence All inhomogeneous solutions are smooth functions exactly when all homogeneous solutions are smooth functions. This is a global statement on Rn for the stated data; no additional local regularity theorem is being assumed.
