Use the characteristic polynomial in the convention p(λ)=det(λI−A):
p(λ)=(λ+1)(λ−α)(λ−1)−ϵ(3λ−1).
(1)
For fixed α>0 with α=1, all three limiting eigenvalues are simple. Expanding this polynomial at each root, or applying first-order perturbation of a simple eigenvalue, gives
λ−1​λα​λ1​​=−1−α+12ϵ​+O(ϵ2),=α+(α+1)(α−1)(3α−1)ϵ​+O(ϵ2),=1−α−1ϵ​+O(ϵ2).​​
(2)
These are fixed-α asymptotic expansions, not uniform through α=1. At α=1/3 the displayed correction to λα​ vanishes because λ=1/3 is an exact root for every ϵ; there is no second nonzero term for that branch.
The two roots near 1 interact when the correction ϵ/(α−1) is comparable to their unperturbed gap α−1. Thus the distinguished limit is α=1+dϵ​, with bounded real d. Set h=ϵ​ and λ=1+hL+h2N+⋯. The polynomial becomes
(2+δ)δ(δ−dh)−h2(2+3δ)=0,δ=λ−1.
(3)
At orders h2 and h3,
L(L−d)=1,N(2L−d)=L.
(4)
With D=d2+4​, the two interacting eigenvalues are
λ±​=1+2h​(d±D)+2h2​(1±Dd​)+O(h3),λ−1​=−1−ϵ+O(ϵ3/2).​
(5)
The h2 correction to the pair is included to resolve the next perturbative term as well as the leading splitting. In particular the previously excluded fixed case α=1 is d=0:
λ±​=1±ϵ​+2ϵ​+O(ϵ3/2),λ−1​=−1−ϵ+O(ϵ2).​
(6)
The half-integer powers reflect square-root splitting of a defective double eigenvalue: the limiting matrix has a nontrivial Jordan block at 1.
For matching, write Δ=α−1 and take the overlap ϵ​≪∣Δ∣≪1. For d→+∞, L+​=d+d−1+O(d−3), N+​=1+O(d−2), while L−​=−d−1+O(d−3), N−​=O(d−2). Consequently the distinguished branches reduce to

The regular coefficient satisfies (3α−1)/[(α+1)(α−1)]=1/Δ+2/(2+Δ)=1/Δ+1+O(Δ), proving agreement. For d→−∞ the labels + and − exchange roles and the same matching holds. The isolated negative branch also matches because −2/(α+1)=−1+O(Δ). As a spectral check, positive diagonal symmetrization of a matrix transforms this tridiagonal matrix to a real symmetric matrix with off-diagonal entries 2ϵ​ and ϵ​; its eigenvalues are real for positive ϵ.

Apply the method of multiple scales with independent fast time t and slow time T=ϵ2t. A preliminary slow time ϵt produces no resonant forcing at first order, so its leading amplitudes are constant; the first nontrivial modulation is at T. Write x=x0​+ϵx1​+⋯, with
x0​=A(T)cost+B(T)sint.
(8)
At order ϵ, the inhomogeneous linear differential equation is (∂t2​+1)x1​=−costx0​. Absorbing its homogeneous part into the amplitudes gives
x1​=−2A​+6A​cos2t+6B​sin2t.
(9)
At order ϵ2, the forcing is −2∂t​∂T​x0​−kx0​−costx1​. Its resonant Fourier series coefficients are
[−2BT​+(125​−k)A]cost+[2AT​−(k+121​)B]sint.
(10)
The solvability condition in the method of multiple scales sets both coefficients to zero, giving the amplitude equations. Define
a=2k+1/12​,b=25/12−k​,M=(0b​a0​).
(11)
For bounded initial conditions x(0)=x∗​, x˙(0)=v∗​, the requested leading approximation is
x(t)=A(ϵ2t)cost+B(ϵ2t)sint+O(ϵ),(A(T)B(T)​)=eTM(x∗​v∗​​),β=2.​
(12)
This holds on bounded intervals of T, hence t=O(ϵ−2), with the constant in the error allowed to depend on the bounded T interval. If desired, retain the displayed ϵx1​ and adjust A(0),B(0) by O(ϵ) to match initial conditions at that order. No particular initial conditions were supplied.
Since M2=abI, the matrix exponential is Icosh(σT)+(M/σ)sinh(σT) when σ2=ab>0. When ab<0, put ω=−ab​ and replace this with Icos(ωT)+(M/ω)sin(ωT). At ab=0 it is I+TM. Thus the leading second instability tongue of a weak Mathieu oscillator has exponentially growing solutions for −1/12<k<5/12, and the leading modulation is bounded for every initial condition precisely when
k<−121​ork>125​.​
(13)
At either displayed endpoint, M is a nonzero nilpotent operator; some initial conditions give a secular term proportional to T. This leading calculation alone must not be mistaken for an exact finite-ϵ stability decision at the endpoints.
For an exact small-ϵ interpretation, put t=2s. The standard Mathieu equation parameters are aM​=4+4kϵ2 and q=−2ϵ. Its two relevant Mathieu characteristic values are obtained by projecting −d2/ds2+2qcos2s onto even and odd Fourier series modes. Near the unperturbed value 4, the even modes 1,cos2s,cos4s,cos6s have diagonal entries 0,4,16,36 and adjacent couplings 2​q,q,q in an orthonormal basis; the odd modes sin2s,sin4s,sin6s have diagonal entries 4,16,36 and couplings q,q. Substituting 4+c2​q2+c4​q4 into their characteristic polynomials gives
a2​(q)=4+125​q2−13824763​q4+O(q6),b2​(q)=4−121​q2+138245​q4+O(q6).
(14)
Higher modes of the Fourier series first contribute at higher order. Therefore the fourth-order edges of the second Mathieu instability tongue are
k−​(ϵ)=−121​+34565​ϵ2+O(ϵ4),k+​(ϵ)=125​−3456763​ϵ2+O(ϵ4).
(15)
Floquet theory gives boundedness of every solution outside the closed gap, k<k−​(ϵ) or k>k+​(ϵ), in this fixed-k=O(1) neighborhood. At the actual gap edges the second independent solution is generically unbounded. In particular, if k is fixed exactly at −1/12 or 5/12, it lies just outside the true gap and is stable for each sufficiently small positive ϵ. The strict boxed criterion is the robust leading-order, uniformly separated criterion; the endpoints require this higher-order refinement, and their amplification bounds need not remain uniform as ϵ→0.

The small denominator lies near x=0 when γ approaches 1. Its two contributions balance when γ−1=O(ε) and x=O(ε). Let
γ=1+εη,x=εs,L=log2,
(17)
with fixed real η. Then 1−γ+γx=ε(s−η)+O(ε2) and log(2−x)=L+O(εs) in the contributing region. The endpoint transition of a regularized interior pole gives
I(1+εη;ε)∼εL1​[2π​+arctan(Lη​)].​
(18)
Indeed, the leading scaled integral is ε−1∫0∞​[(s−η)2+L2]−1ds. The upper endpoint can be sent to infinity at leading order because the omitted tail contributes only O(1) to the original integral. At γ=1 the result is π/(2εlog2): only one half of the narrow peak lies in the domain.
This formula provides the required matching. For η→−∞, π/2+arctan(η/L)∼−L/η, giving I∼1/(1−γ). For η→+∞ it tends to π, giving I∼π/(εlog2), the γ↓1 limit of the interior-peak approximation. These are overlap regions with ε≪∣γ−1∣≪1, not assertions that the fixed-η remainder is uniform to arbitrarily large η.

For fixed γ>1, the linear term 1−γ+γx vanishes at x∗​=1−1/γ. Put L∗​=log(1+1/γ)>0 and resolve its neighborhood using x=x∗​+(εL∗​/γ)s. The integral is dominated by the resulting narrow peak:
I∼γεL∗​1​∫−∞∞​1+s2ds​=γεlog(1+1/γ)π​.​
(19)
The local Taylor expansion of the logarithm changes it by a vanishing relative amount on this width, while both endpoints are many peak widths away for fixed γ>1.
There is no additional breakdown when γ≫1: the peak approaches x=1, but its distance from that endpoint is d=1/γ, whereas its width is w=εL∗​/γ∼ε/γ2. Thus w/d=εL∗​∼ε/γ≪1. The relative logarithm variation over a width is O(ε/γ), also improving as γ grows. For γ≥1+c with fixed c>0 these separation conditions hold uniformly, including arbitrarily large γ. In particular
I∼επ​(γ≫1).​
(20)
The only endpoint transition requiring a new distinguished limit is the earlier one near γ=1, where the peak approaches x=0. Moving toward an endpoint is not by itself a reason for nonuniformity; the peak width must be compared with its endpoint distance.

First make explicit the usual method of steepest descent hypotheses: the saddle point is simple, G′(z0​)=0 and g2​=G′′(z0​)=0; a contour deformation leads to the two descent arms with one clockwise half-indentation around the pole; and endpoints or other contour portions contribute smaller terms. The sign of the exponential matters: ∣e−iλG∣=eλImG, so decay is toward valleys of ImG.
Use a local analytic function w(z) as coordinate. Nondegeneracy allows an analytic square root after factoring the double zero of G−G(z0​), so the coordinate can be chosen with
G(z)−G(z0​)=−2i​w2,z−z0​=cw+dw2+O(w3),c2=−g2​i​.
(21)
The sign of c is selected by the orientation of the contour integral, with w running from negative to positive real values. Matching cubic coefficients gives d/c=−cG′′′(z0​)/(6g2​). The transformed amplitude therefore has the Laurent series
z−z0​F(z)​dwdz​=wF(z0​)​+B+O(w),B=c[F′(z0​)−6G′′(z0​)F(z0​)G′′′(z0​)​].
(22)
The Cauchy principal value contribution of the pole on the two real arms is zero by oddness. Its clockwise semicircular indentation contributes −iπF(z0​), since ∫dw/w=−iπ there. The regular term contributes the Gaussian integral B2π/λ​. Consequently the simple saddle coinciding with a simple pole has the additive expansion
J(λ)=e−iλG(z0​)[−iπF(z0​)+Bλ2π​​+O(λ−3/2)].​
(23)
The remainder written here assumes the other contour contributions are negligible to that order. Otherwise the local result has to be supplemented by them. The next local correction is generically O(λ−1/2) times the displayed exponential. If B=0, it can vanish; odd Taylor terms integrate to zero between the symmetric arms. When F(z0​)=0, the leading term gives the printed asymptotic equivalence. When F(z0​)=0, asymptotic equivalence to zero is not meaningful: the additive formula is still valid and a regular saddle-point approximation may lead instead.
Nondegeneracy is necessary and is not explicitly printed. For a counterexample to the unrestricted reading, take F=1, z0​=0 and G(z)=−iz4. There is one critical point, but it is degenerate. Choose the contour from +i∞ to +∞, with a clockwise quarter-circle avoiding zero. Both rays are decaying valleys for e−λz4. Their straight-ray contributions cancel, and the indentation gives J=−iπ/2, not −iπ. Thus the requested coefficient requires the simple saddle point and contour deformation assumptions above; the mere existence of one critical point is insufficient.

The three distinguished limits are the outer expansion at x=O(1), an intermediate asymptotic region at x=O(ϵ), and an inner expansion at x=O(ϵ2). The middle scale resolves the coefficient change from x to x+ϵ; the narrower scale resolves the unmet left boundary condition. The original equation is understood on x>0 with a continuous extension to the boundary. As explained below, imposing a classical second derivative at x=0 would be too strong for this degenerate ordinary differential equation.
In the outer expansion, set y=y0​+ϵy1​+⋯. The leading equation is x(x+1)y0′​−xy0​=0. Enforcing the right boundary condition gives y0​=x+1. Because y0′′​=0, the next equation is particularly simple:
x(x+1)y1′​−xy1​=−(x+1),y1​(1)=0.
(24)
An integrating factor, or the substitution y1​=(x+1)u, gives u′=−1/[x(x+1)]. Thus
yout​(x)=(1+x)[1+ϵlog2x1+x​]+O(ϵ2),x=O(1).​
(25)
The error statement is for x bounded away from zero. The left limiting form is 1+x−ϵlog(2x) through the terms needed for matching. A logarithm in the correction makes a pure integer-power inner ansatz inadequate.
For the intermediate asymptotic region, put x=ϵs and y=V(s). The transformed equation is
ϵ​s​(s+1)ϵ(s+1)sin[ϵ(s+1)]​V′′+(s+1)(1+ϵs)V′−ϵ(s+ϵ)V=0.
(26)
The leading V0​ is a constant fixed to 1 by matching. There is no order-ϵ​ forcing, so that homogeneous constant is zero after matching. At order ϵ, (s+1)V1′​=s, giving V1​=s−log(1+s)+C(ϵ). Matching for 1≪s≪1/ϵ with the outer expansion fixes C=−log(2ϵ). Hence
ymid​(ϵs)=1+ϵ[s−log(1+s)−log(2ϵ)]+o(ϵ),s=O(1), s>0.​
(27)
The switchback term −ϵlogϵ is larger than an ordinary O(ϵ) constant correction and must be retained. The highest derivative term first changes this middle solution at order ϵ3/2, so it does not alter the displayed result.
To locate the inner expansion, compare the derivative terms for x≪ϵ. Their ratio at a layer width δ is ϵ/δ​, so the square-root-degenerate endpoint layer has δ=ϵ2. Put x=ϵ2X and y=U(X). After dividing by the common leading factor, the equation through order ϵ is
X​(1+ϵX)U′′+(1+ϵX)U′+O(ϵ2)=0.
(28)
Both orders therefore have the same homogeneous operator X​d2/dX2+d/dX. Its derivative mode is proportional to e−2X​. Using U(0)=0 and the supplied elementary integral, define
H(X)=2∫0X​e−2q​dq=1−(1+2X​)e−2X​.
(29)
The intermediate asymptotic region gives the overlap value 1−ϵlog(2ϵ) as s→0. Therefore
yin​(ϵ2X)=[1−ϵlog(2ϵ)][1−(1+2X​)e−2X​]+o(ϵ),X=O(1).​
(30)
It satisfies the left boundary condition exactly to the retained orders, and its large-X limit matches the middle result in 1≪X≪1/ϵ. The outer and middle limits match with ϵ≪x≪1. These three formulae include all terms through O(ϵ), including the logarithmic switchback; the omitted terms in the middle and inner regions need not be O(ϵ2).
For a check on that last point, continue the middle equation by one order. Its ϵ3/2 term obeys
V3/2′​=−(1+s)2s​​,V3/2​(s)=∫s∞​(1+r)2r​​dr=2π​−arctans​+1+ss​​.
(31)
The integration constant is selected by the outer limit. Its value at s=0 is π/2, so the inner matching amplitude has a next correction πϵ3/2/2. This independently explains why an o(ϵ) remainder is appropriate.
If a single additive composite expansion is useful, combine the three formulae and subtract their two common overlaps. Writing A=1−ϵlog(2ϵ) and interpreting xlogx=0 at zero gives
ycomp​=1+x+ϵ[(1+x)log21+x​−xlogx−log(x+ϵ)]−A(1+ϵ2x​​)e−2x​/ϵ.
(32)
It is zero at x=0, gives 2+O(ϵ2) at x=1, and reproduces all three retained expansions. It does not assert an O(ϵ2) error uniformly through the nested regions.
Finally, near zero H(X)=2X−38​X3/2+O(X2). Thus the solution has a finite nonzero first derivative but generally a second derivative diverging like x−1/2. This is compatible with the equation for x>0: the vanishing coefficient of y′′ multiplies that singular derivative to give a finite limit. If one instead required y∈C2[0,1] and the differential equation pointwise at 0, the left condition would force y′(0)=0. The normalized equation's other coefficients have only integrable x−1/2 singularities, so uniqueness for the initial value problem with y(0)=y′(0)=0 would give the zero solution, contradicting the right condition. Explicitly, write the normalized equation as a first-order system for (y,y′) with an integrable coefficient matrix; its integral equation and the Gronwall inequality force a solution with zero initial vector to vanish. The continuous-endpoint interpretation is therefore essential.
