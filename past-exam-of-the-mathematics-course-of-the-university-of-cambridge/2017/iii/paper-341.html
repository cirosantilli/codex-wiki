The first characteristic polynomials of a linear multistep method is
ρ(ζ)=ζ2−(1+α)ζ+α=(ζ−1)(ζ−α).
(11)
The root condition for a multistep method requires ∣α∣≤1 and excludes α=1, where the root of a polynomial at one is double. At α=−1, the two unit-modulus roots of a polynomial are distinct, so zero-stability holds. For α=1, the first-order consistency of a numerical method identity is ρ′(1)=1−α, equal to the coefficient of hf.
Using the permitted Dahlquist equivalence theorem in this multiderivative multistep method setting gives
−1≤α<1​.
(12)
This means convergence on every fixed finite time interval where the smooth exact solution of a differential equation exists, with consistent starting values and the nearby branch of each implicit solve. More specifically, the convergence of a zero-stable multiderivative method gives order p when the starting errors are O(hp) and both f and f′f are locally uniformly Lipschitz continuous on the relevant region.
The exclusions are genuine. For y′=0 and ∣α∣>1, an arbitrarily small parasitic starting error is amplified as αn. At α=1, take y0​=0, y1​=h, also for y′=0. The exact solution is zero, but the numerical recurrence gives yn​=nh, an error of order one at fixed positive time. This proves failure even though the starting values converge.

A Runge-Kutta method advances y′=f(t,y) through stages
Yi​=yn​+h∑j​aij​Fj​,Fj​=f(tn​+cj​h,Yj​),yn+1​=yn​+h∑i​bi​Fi​.
(50)
The Butcher tableau records A,b,c, normally with c=Ae. Linear absolute stability tests the amplification of a linear decay mode; nonlinear B-stability compares distances between two solutions of a dissipative vector field. Both describe propagation of perturbations, but they impose different conditions.
For the Dahlquist test equation, elimination of stages gives
R(z)=1+zbT(I−zA)−1e,z=hλ.
(51)
The linear stability domain consists of z for which the step is defined and ∣R(z)∣≤1. Poles or singular stage systems must be excluded, even if an output formula has a formal cancellation. For a scalar test equation, the proof is yn​=R(z)ny0​: the powers are bounded exactly when ∣R(z)∣≤1. For y′=Ly, the update is R(hL). If L is a normal matrix, unitary diagonalization of a normal matrix makes the operator norm equal to the largest scalar amplification modulus. For a diagonalizable matrix, powers are bounded by the condition number of the eigenvector matrix times the maximal scalar power. These constants must remain uniform for mesh-dependent systems. A defective unit-modulus eigenvalue can instead produce a growing Jordan block; the scalar spectral test alone does not control a general matrix.
An A-stable method accepts the entire closed left half-plane. The Explicit Euler method has R(z)=1+z and a disk of stability ∣1+z∣≤1, so it is not A-stable. No nonconstant polynomial stability function can be A-stable, since it is unbounded on the negative real axis; this excludes all consistent explicit Runge-Kutta methods. The Backward Euler method has R(z)=1/(1−z), and ∣1−z∣≥1 when Rez≤0, proving A-stability. It is also L-stable, because R(z)→0 for large left-half-plane z.
The implicit midpoint rule and the trapezoidal rule have the same scalar stability function,
R(z)=1−z/21+z/2​.
(52)
The identity ∣1−z/2∣2−∣1+z/2∣2=−2Rez proves A-stability. On the imaginary axis their amplification has unit modulus, and along the negative real axis it tends to −1. Thus neither is L-stable; very stiff decay can persist as alternating numerical values. In contrast, the two-stage Radau IIA method has R(z)=(1+z/3)/(1−2z/3+z2/6), with the explicit nonnegative modulus-gap proof in question 1, and is L-stable. This illustrates why L-stability matters beyond A-stability for a stiff differential equation.
For nonlinear problems, assume a real or complex Euclidean inner product and the dissipativity hypothesis
Re⟨f(t,u)−f(t,v),u−v⟩≤0
(53)
for all relevant states at each common time. Exact solutions satisfy
dtd​∥u−v∥2=2Re⟨f(t,u)−f(t,v),u−v⟩≤0.
(54)
A B-stable Runge-Kutta method reproduces this nonexpansion of distances whenever its stages exist. Put d=yn​−y​n​, Di​=Yi​−Yi​, and Gi​=f(tn​+ci​h,Yi​)−f(tn​+ci​h,Yi​). Then Di​=d+h∑j​aij​Gj​. Expansion of the output squared norm, followed by substituting d=Di​−h∑j​aij​Gj​ in its linear terms, proves the Runge-Kutta contractivity identity
∥dnew​∥2−∥d∥2=2h∑i​bi​Re⟨Di​,Gi​⟩−h2∑i,j​mij​Re⟨Gi​,Gj​⟩,mij​=bi​aij​+bj​aji​−bi​bj​.
(55)
If bi​≥0 and M=(mij​) is a positive semidefinite matrix, the first sum is nonpositive by dissipativity, and the second is nonnegative: expand each coordinate of the stage vectors to express it as a sum of nonnegative quadratic forms. These are exactly the conditions for algebraic stability of a Runge-Kutta method. Hence algebraic stability implies B-stability. Applying B-stability to the linear dissipative problem y′=λy, or its two-dimensional real form, also proves A-stability whenever stages are well defined.
The Backward Euler method has b=A=1, hence M=1; the implicit midpoint rule has b=1, A=1/2, hence M=0. Both are algebraically stable and B-stable. For the two-stage Radau IIA method,
M=161​(1−1​−11​),
(56)
a positive semidefinite matrix, while both weights are positive. It therefore combines third-order accuracy, B-stability, and L-stability. The distinction between stability and solvability is essential: for Lipschitz continuous f, the stage map is a contraction when hLmaxi​∑j​∣aij​∣<1, so the contraction mapping theorem proves small-step unique solvability. The preceding algebraic stability proof is an estimate for existing stages; it is not a theorem asserting stage existence for arbitrary step sizes and domains.
Linear A-stability is insufficient for nonlinear B-stability. The trapezoidal rule fails B-stability, even though its scalar stability function is identical to that of the B-stable implicit midpoint rule. Its Runge-Kutta method coefficients are
A=(01/2​01/2​),b=(1/2,1/2)T,M=(−1/40​01/4​).
(57)
Failure of this sufficient algebraic stability test alone would not prove failure of B-stability. An actual counterexample does: take f(y)=−y3 and h=2. Dissipativity follows from
(f(u)−f(v))(u−v)=−(u−v)2(u2+uv+v2)≤0.
(58)
The unique step map T obeys T+T3=y−y3, because the left-hand side is strictly increasing and onto. At y=1, T=0, and implicit differentiation gives
T′(1)=1+3T(1)21−3(1)2​=−2.
(59)
Nearby initial states expand in distance. This proves a true nonlinear failure rather than merely failure of a coefficient criterion.
Finally, stability of a numerical method on finite intervals connects these contractivity properties to accuracy. If a one-step map is nonexpansive and its exact one-step defect has norm at most Chp+1, then the error satisfies ∥en+1​∥≤∥en​∥+Chp+1, hence ∥en​∥≤∥e0​∥+CThp for nh≤T. A Lipschitz step bound 1+Kh gives the same order using the discrete Gronwall inequality. For a stiff differential equation, the value of B-stability is that the propagation estimate need not grow with a large negative dissipative rate. The accuracy constant still needs appropriate smoothness and uniform derivative bounds; stability alone does not establish a mesh-uniform error order.

For a fully discrete linear partial differential equation evolution, write Un+1=Ch,k​Un in a specified discrete norm. Finite-time stability of a numerical method means
∥Ch,kn​∥≤CT​whenever nk≤T​,
(60)
with CT​ independent of the allowed meshes. For a multistep method, augment the state with its time-history values and include a stable starter. Bounds may grow as eωT when the continuous problem has growth; demanding decay for all time would be a stronger assertion. The treatment of initial data, forcing, Dirichlet boundary conditions, and the norm is part of the hypothesis, not a detail supplied by an interior calculation.
For constant coefficients on an infinite or periodic uniform grid, von Neumann stability analysis substitutes the Fourier mode Ujn​=G(θ)neijθ. The discrete Fourier transform and the Parseval identity turn a uniform multiplier estimate into a discrete L2 norm estimate. In a one-step scalar scheme, ∣G(θ)∣≤1 proves contractivity, while ∣G(θ)∣≤1+ωk gives finite-time stability. In a multilevel scheme, every root of a polynomial in its amplification polynomial of a multilevel finite difference scheme matters, as does uniform control of the associated companion matrix.
For the Forward Euler diffusion scheme with μ=k/h2,
G(θ)=1−4μsin2(θ/2).
(61)
Thus ∣G∣≤1 for all frequencies exactly when
0≤μ≤21​​.
(62)
Sufficiency follows since G∈[−1,1]; necessity follows from the highest-frequency mode θ=π on even periodic grids. This gives the usual parabolic mesh restriction k≤h2/2. For the Backward Euler diffusion scheme,
G(θ)=1+4μsin2(θ/2)1​,
(63)
so every μ≥0 is stable. The Crank-Nicolson method has
G(θ)=1+2μsin2(θ/2)1−2μsin2(θ/2)​,
(64)
again of modulus at most one for all μ≥0, but stiff modes approach −1 rather than zero. These are PDE manifestations of the A-stability and L-stability distinctions for time integration.
For advection ut​+aux​=0, assume a>0 and set ν=ak/h. The upwind finite difference scheme is Ujn+1​=(1−ν)Ujn​+νUj−1n​, with
∣G(θ)∣2=1−2ν(1−ν)(1−cosθ).
(65)
It is contractive when 0≤ν≤1. This also has a direct maximum-norm proof: each newest value is a convex combination of two old values. The resulting Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition expresses that the numerical domain of dependence covers the physical one. For negative a, the upwind direction must be reversed. By contrast, Forward Euler method time stepping with the centered first finite difference has G=1−iνsinθ and ∣G∣>1 for nonzero νsinθ. Under fixed nonzero ν refinement it is unstable, since a fixed nontrivial mode grows geometrically over T/k steps. This is not a claim of instability under every imaginable mesh coupling: k=O(h2) instead bounds its spurious finite-time growth, since log∣G∣≤a2k2/(2h2).
The leapfrog advection scheme illustrates a multilevel subtlety. Its amplification polynomial of a multilevel finite difference scheme is
ξ2+2iνsinθξ−1=0.
(66)
For ∣ν∣<1, both roots of a polynomial have unit modulus and separation at least 21−ν2​. A uniformly conditioned eigenbasis of the companion matrix then proves power boundedness of a two-level Fourier scheme for arbitrary history data. At ∣ν∣=1 and a grid admitting θ=π/2, the roots coincide on the unit circle, and a Jordan block produces nξn growth. Thus the endpoint fails the ordinary arbitrary-history root condition for a multistep method; checking only that both moduli equal one misses the instability. Restricting the starter or filtering the parasitic mode is an additional hypothesis.
The energy method handles variable coefficients and finite boundaries for which Fourier stability analysis may be unavailable. For the Backward Euler diffusion scheme with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, define ∥U∥h2​=h∑j​∣Uj​∣2. The discrete summation by parts identity is
⟨U,Lh​U⟩h​=−∥D+​U∥h2​.
(67)
Take the inner product of Un+1−Un=kLh​Un+1 with 2Un+1. The elementary identity 2Re⟨x−y,x⟩=∥x∥2−∥y∥2+∥x−y∥2 gives
∥Un+1∥h2​−∥Un∥h2​+∥Un+1−Un∥h2​+2k∥D+​Un+1∥h2​=0.
(68)
This proves unconditional contractivity including the actual boundary treatment. More generally, a dissipative operator Lh​ gives (I−kLh​)−1 of operator norm at most one: with V=(I−kLh​)U, dissipativity implies Re⟨V,U⟩≥∥U∥2, and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives ∥U∥≤∥V∥. In finite dimensions this also proves invertibility. For the second-order backward differentiation formula, the BDF2 discrete energy identity used in question 4 controls both time levels and proves unconditional diffusion stability. A repeated root strictly inside the disk is harmless here; the energy argument supplies the uniform bound without a singular eigenvector formula.
A second technique is eigenvalue stability analysis of a finite difference method. Under the method of lines, a time integrator advances U′=Lh​U by R(kLh​). If Lh​ is a normal matrix, the scalar linear stability domain criterion on all kλj​(Lh​) controls its powers exactly in the corresponding L2 norm. For the centered Dirichlet discrete Laplacian, its eigenvalues lie between −4/h2 and zero; intersecting this interval with a time integrator's stability interval determines its mesh restriction. A uniform bound on the diagonalization of a matrix is needed for nonnormal diagonalizable systems. Eigenvalues alone can be misleading. For example,
Ch,k​=(1−k0​k/h1−k​)
(69)
has both eigenvalues inside the disk for 0<k<1, but the upper-right entry of Ch,kn​ is nk(1−k)n−1/h. Taking k=h, nk→T>0, this grows like Te−T/h. Hence stable scalar eigenvalues do not give mesh-uniform stability. Boundary closures can introduce precisely the extra growth that an interior Fourier symbol overlooks, so boundary stability of a finite-difference method must be checked separately.
For nonlinear spatial discretizations, a useful replacement for Fourier stability analysis is a convexity argument. If the Forward Euler method map Ek​(U)=U+kF(U) is nonexpansive in a chosen norm for k≤kFE​, the second-order strong stability preserving Runge-Kutta method
Y=Ek​(U),Unew​=21​U+21​Ek​(Y)
(70)
is also nonexpansive under the same restriction. Indeed, for two inputs, the triangle inequality gives ∥Unew​−Unew​∥≤21​∥U−U∥+21​∥Ek​(Y)−Ek​(Y)∥≤∥U−U∥. Its Taylor expansion is U+kF(U)+k2F′(U)F(U)/2+O(k3), proving order two. Such strong stability preserving Runge-Kutta methods transfer suitable forward-step bounds without relying on a linear spectral calculation.
Finally, consistency of a numerical method explains what stability buys. For a linear Hadamard well-posed problem, the Lax equivalence theorem equates convergence of a consistent discretization with its stability, under the stated approximation-space and norm hypotheses. Directly, if the error satisfies en+1=Ch,k​en+kτn, iteration gives the discrete Duhamel principle
en=Ch,kn​e0+k∑j=0n−1​Ch,kn−1−j​τj,∥en∥≤CT​(∥e0∥+Tmaxj​∥τj∥).
(71)
Thus vanishing normalized local truncation error and initial error yield convergence. With a nonlinear Lipschitz step estimate, the discrete Gronwall inequality plays the same role. A stable but inconsistent stencil, such as the literal defective formulas in questions 3 and 4 under their usual refinement, is not rescued by any amplification bound. The decisive checks are a mesh-uniform evolution bound, a valid boundary closure, and consistency with the actual PDE.
