Take 0<r1​<r2​ and positive mean kinematic viscosity νˉ. The steady state of the supplied viscous evolution of an accretion disk equation satisfies F′=−2πrS(r). The outer no-flux condition therefore gives
F(r)=2π∫rr2​​vS(v)dv.
(1)
Positive S produces positive inward accretion rate in this sign convention. Set Y=r1/2νˉΣ. In a Keplerian disk, the viscous torque in an accretion disk is proportional to Y, so the zero-torque inner boundary condition gives Y(r1​)=0. Since Y′=F/(6πr1/2), a second integration yields
Σ(r)=3νˉ(r)r1/21​∫r1​r​u−1/2[∫ur2​​vS(v)dv]du.​
(2)
This is the steady viscous disk supplied by a radial source. It does not require νˉ to be independent of radius: the equation directly determines νˉΣ. If viscosity depends on the local thermodynamic state, the displayed formula is an implicit relation completed by a separate closure. For nonnegative integrable S it gives nonnegative surface density of a disk, and the inner accretion rate equals the total supplied mass per unit time, 2π∫r1​r2​​rS(r)dr. Differentiating both nested integrals recovers the flux and the differential equation.

Interpret the outer Dirac delta function as a boundary source carrying its full mass, or as the limit of a narrow source lying just inside r2​. Then ∫ur2​​vS(v)dv=r2​S0​ for r1​<u<r2​, and
Σ(r)=3νˉ(r)2r2​S0​​(1−rr1​​​)=3πνˉ(r)M˙​(1−rr1​​​),M˙=2πr2​S0​.​
(3)
Thus the interior is the usual steady Keplerian accretion disk with a constant inward accretion rate. The product νˉΣ vanishes at the stress-free inner edge, and the surface density of a disk does too if the viscosity has a finite nonzero boundary value; far from that edge νˉΣ approaches M˙/(3π). Its density need not itself be constant because the viscosity may vary.
The outer-edge source of a viscous disk requires care about the boundary trace. The flux just inside the injecting edge is F(r2−​)=M˙, while the no-flux value outside the source is F(r2+​)=0; the source produces the intervening jump. A simultaneously continuous interior condition F(r2−​)=0 would contradict nonzero steady injection. A symmetrically regularized delta integrated only up to its center can instead give half its full weight; that would mean a different supplied mass, not a different interior solution for the same M˙. The physically normalized one-sided limiting source removes this convention ambiguity.

In the vertical equations, μ denotes dynamic viscosity, while μm​ is the mean molecular weight in units of the proton mass mp​. The symbol k denotes the Boltzmann constant. For the intended hydrostatic equilibrium calculation, put P=Kργ with K>0, γ>1, and set R=k/(μm​mp​). Dividing the hydrostatic equation by ρ and integrating from the symmetric midplane gives
γ−1γK​ργ−1=γ−1γK​ρmγ−1​−2Ω2z2​.
(4)
The specific enthalpy vanishes at the free surface. Hence the polytropic vertical structure in stellar gravity is
ρ(z)=ρm​(1−H2z2​)1/(γ−1),H2=(γ−1)Ω22γKρmγ−1​​=(γ−1)Ω22γRTm​​.​
(5)
On ∣z∣<H, the ideal gas relation gives T=Tm​(1−z2/H2) and P=Pm​(1−z2/H2)γ/(γ−1); the vacuum extension sets ρ=P=0 outside. The formula needs γ>1 for a finite positive semi-thickness and assumes the harmonic vertical gravity remains valid through this height.
There is a genuine inconsistency if all four printed structure equations are imposed literally with F=0 and finite positive opacity: radiative diffusion then requires dT/dz=0, whereas the polytropic temperature above has a nonzero gradient away from the midplane. The intended result is valid for the hydrostatic polytropic equation of state with the radiative closure replaced or neglected, for example an adiabatic opaque limit. It is not an exact simultaneous solution of the stated finite-opacity radiative system. With that radiative system and a nonvacuum ideal gas, γ=1 instead gives an isothermal solution
ρ=ρm​exp[−2RTm​Ω2z2​],
(6)
which has no finite free surface. For γ=1, constant T and constant K would force constant density, contradicting hydrostatic balance on a nontrivial vertical interval. This is the zero-radiative-flux obstruction for a polytropic ideal gas.

Assume the vertical dynamical timescale of a disk is shorter than the thermal timescale of an accretion disk, which is itself shorter than the viscous timescale. This ordering is controlled in a thin disk with α≪1: tdyn​∼Ω−1, tth​∼(αΩ)−1 and tν​∼(r/H)2tth​. Therefore compare heating and cooling at fixed surface density of a disk, radius, α and composition. Vertical hydrostatic equilibrium adjusts on the vertical dynamical timescale of a disk, giving H∝T1/2 and ρ∝T−1/2. Consequently the stipulated negative hydrogen ion opacity varies as κ∝T10−1/6=T59/6. The rates per disk face then scale as
Q+∼αΣRΩT∝T,Q−∼κΣσT4​∝T−35/6.​
(11)
Thus heating increases while cooling decreases after a positive temperature perturbation. At equilibrium Q+=Q−=Q0​, a small change gives δ(Q+−Q−)=(41/6)(Q0​/T)δT, which drives the perturbation further from equilibrium for positive effective heat capacity. A negative temperature perturbation likewise increases the net cooling. This proves thermal instability from negative hydrogen ion opacity within the local closure; it is the temperature-slope test of thermal stability of an accretion disk. Holding volume density rather than surface density of a disk fixed would give a different cooling exponent, but still the same qualitative instability; the fixed-column comparison is the relevant thin-disk thermal test.

Let K=ρ0​∣u∣2/2 be the kinetic energy per unit volume. Dot the momentum equation with ρ0​u. The Coriolis acceleration does no work because u⋅(ez​×u)=0. Incompressibility turns the advection and pressure terms into divergences, yielding
∂t​K+∇⋅[(K+P)u]=ρ0​Ω2(3x−2qz)ux​.​
(12)
Thus F=(K+P)u is the energy flux and S=ρ0​Ω2(3x−2qz)ux​ is the source in the requested kinetic-energy form.
To decide about conservation of energy, absorb the ordinary radial shearing-sheet tidal potential Φt​=−3Ω2x2/2 into the density. Its material derivative cancels the 3x work term:
∂t​(K+ρ0​Φt​)+∇⋅[(K+P+ρ0​Φt​)u]=−2ρ0​Ω2qzux​.​
(13)
For q=0 this is the usual conservative Jacobi energy in a shearing sheet, subject to vanishing boundary energy flux. For q=0, the extra body force has nonzero curl, ∇×[−2Ω2qzex​]=−2Ω2qey​, so no scalar potential can absorb it. A potential 2Ω2qxz would necessarily introduce a vertical force −2Ω2qxez​, absent from the model. Therefore this vertical-shear model does not generically conserve the standard total mechanical energy: its maintained shear/irradiation background can supply or remove energy. This is the energy balance of the incompressible vertical-shear model.

Write the proposed background as U=U(x,z)ey​, U=−3Ωx/2+qΩz. It satisfies incompressibility, and U⋅∇U=U∂y​U=0. A constant pressure has zero gradient. The Coriolis acceleration is −2Ωez​×U=2ΩUex​=−(3x−2qz)Ω2ex​, which exactly cancels the prescribed body force. Thus it is a steady state.
The background is ordinary Keplerian radial shear together with vertical shear: ∂x​U=−3Ω/2 and ∂z​U=qΩ. At a given radius, different heights have different azimuthal velocities. The small q in this question measures vertical shear; it is distinct from the orbital shear parameter, whose radial value here remains 3/2. The vertical shear instability draws on this height-dependent differential rotation.

Eliminate pressure by multiplying the radial equation by kz​ and subtracting kx​ times the vertical equation. For kz​=0, incompressibility gives u~z​=−(kx​/kz​)u~x​, and the pressure-free relation is s(kx2​+kz2​)u~x​/kz​=2Ωkz​u~y​. Substitution in the azimuthal equation yields
s2=Ω2kx2​+kz2​2qkx​kz​−kz2​​=−Ω2k2kz2​​(1−2qkz​kx​​).​
(16)
Thus the vertical shear instability occurs when 2qkx​kz​>kz2​, or 2q(kx​/kz​)>1 for kz​=0. Otherwise the nonzero modes oscillate; equality is marginal in the exponential-growth sense. With q>0, growing disturbances have kx​/kz​>1/(2q) and are strongly inclined in wavevector space.
Put R=kx​/kz​. Maximizing (2qR−1)/(1+R2) gives qR2−R−q=0. For q>0, the unstable maximizing root is
Rmax​=2q1+1+4q2​​,smax​=Ω21+4q2​−1​​=Ωq[1+O(q2)].​
(17)
More precisely its bracket is 1−q2/2+O(q4), and Rmax​∼q−1. This is the maximum growth rate of the vertical shear instability. For negative q, the maximum uses the corresponding negative root and is asymptotically Ω∣q∣. At q=0 there is no exponentially growing mode. The undivided formula handles kz​=0: s2=0, not an exponential instability; an allowed vertical velocity can instead force a secular azimuthal change. The excluded k=0 is a spatially uniform disturbance and does not belong to the stipulated nonconstant one-phase family.

The razor-thin disk approximation uses vertically integrated pressure and surface density of a disk. To close the printed equations, interpret the barotropic equation of state as P=P(Σ), or supply an equivalent fixed vertical conversion from the printed ρ to Σ. Without that conversion, the literal P=P(ρ) does not close this two-dimensional system. Set cs2​=(dP/dΣ)0​>0, and write Σ=Σ0​+σ, u=u0​+u′, h′=P′/Σ0​=cs2​σ/Σ0​. Axisymmetric flow here means ∂y​=0, so background advection vanishes. The background shear enters through u′⋅∇u0​=−3Ωux′​ey​/2. The complete linearized equations are
∂t​ux′​=2Ωuy′​−∂x​h′,∂t​uy′​=−2Ω​ux′​,∂t​σ=−Σ0​∂x​ux′​,h′=Σ0​cs2​​σ.​
(18)
These are the compressible Keplerian shearing sheet equations for an inertial-acoustic wave. No perturbed self-gravity is present in the given force law, so no gravitational potential perturbation is to be added. This closure is the barotropic closure of a razor-thin disk.

For a normal mode proportional to eikx−iωt, the linear equations give
−iωUx​=2ΩUy​−ikΣ0​cs2​​σ^,−iωUy​=−2Ω​Ux​,−iωσ^=−ikΣ0​Ux​.
(19)
For ω=0, eliminate Uy​=−iΩUx​/(2ω) and σ^=kΣ0​Ux​/ω. The density-wave dispersion relation in a non-self-gravitating disk is
ω2=Ω2+cs2​k2.​
(20)
The Ω2 term is the radial epicyclic frequency squared for Keplerian rotation: Coriolis force and background shear restore an orbital displacement. The cs2​k2 term is acoustic restoration by a pressure gradient. Long wavelengths are mainly epicyclic motion; short wavelengths are mainly acoustic waves. The given model contains no disk self-gravity term.
For completeness, the full three-variable determinant is proportional to ω(ω2−Ω2−cs2​k2). Its additional ω=0 branch has Ux​=0 and 2ΩUy​=ikcs2​σ^/Σ0​. This is a stationary pressure/Coriolis balance, the zero-frequency balanced mode of an axisymmetric disk, rather than a propagating density wave. At k=0 it reduces to a uniform surface-density change. Dividing by ω is legitimate only for the wave branches just derived.

ux′​=ucos(kx−ωt),uy′​=2ωΩu​sin(kx−ωt).

The pressureless dust fluid in a shearing sheet has no pressure restoration and is driven by drag force toward the prescribed gas velocity; gas backreaction is omitted in the given model. After linearizing about the common background shear, axisymmetry gives
(∂t​+ϵΩ)vx′​−2Ωvy′​=ϵΩux′​,(∂t​+ϵΩ)vy′​+2Ω​vx′​=ϵΩuy′​.
(23)
Apply Dϵ​=∂t​+ϵΩ to the first equation and use the second to eliminate vy′​. The exact linear scalar equation is
[∂t2​+2ϵΩ∂t​+(1+ϵ2)Ω2]vx′​=ϵΩ∂t​ux′​+ϵ2Ω2ux′​+2ϵΩ2uy′​.
(24)
For the supplied leading-order epicycle, set θ=kx−Ωt. Since ∂t​ux′​=Ωusinθ and uy′​=usinθ/2, this becomes
∂t2​vx′​+2ϵΩ∂t​vx′​+(1+ϵ2)Ω2vx′​=2ϵΩ2usinθ+ϵ2Ω2ucosθ.​
(25)
The final cosine term is missing from the printed equation. Dropping it is justified at leading order in 0<ϵ≪1, recovering the intended forcing, but the printed equation retains an order-ϵ2 frequency term on the left and is not an exact derivation at that order. A direct countercheck is v′=u′: the drag vanishes and the pair solves both dust equations exactly, while vx′​=ucosθ leaves residual ϵ2Ω2ucosθ in the printed scalar equation. This is the forced dust epicycle with aerodynamic drag; its damping time is (ϵΩ)−1.

Let Σd0​ be the uniform dust background and put δd​=Σd′​/Σd0​. The linearized continuity equation is ∂t​δd​=−∂x​vx′​ because background shear has no divergence or axisymmetric advection. Using the late entrained velocity vx′​=ucosθ gives
∂t​δd​=kusinθ,δd​=Ωku​cosθ+C(x).​
(29)
Thus the coherent travelling wave has maximum fractional excess ∣ku∣/Ω, and its crest density is Σd0​[1+∣ku∣/Ω] when the stationary part is absent. This is the density contrast of an entrained dust wave, and requires ∣ku∣/Ω≪1 in the present linear approximation. Its fractional oscillating density amplitude is the same as the gas's long-wavelength amplitude, so the coherent wave produces no additional dust-to-gas concentration ratio at this order.
Velocity damping alone does not erase the time-independent C(x); initial density and the history of the forcing are needed to fix it. The printed data therefore determine the travelling-wave contrast, rather than a unique total late-time concentration for arbitrary initial histories. As an explicit example, switch on the prescribed gas epicycle at t=0 with initially uniform dust and v′=0. The corrected velocity pair gives vx′​=u(1−e−ϵΩt)cosθ. Integrating continuity then leaves
C(x)=−Ωku​1+ϵ2ϵ2cos(kx)+ϵsin(kx)​
(30)
at late times. It is smaller by order ϵ in the stipulated weak-coupling regime, and confirms the stated leading wave-crest estimate while showing why arbitrary stationary density structure is not removed by the velocity transient.
