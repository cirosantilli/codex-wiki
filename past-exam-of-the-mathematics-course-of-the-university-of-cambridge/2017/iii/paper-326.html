A forward problem evaluates a known model at a state: given u and K, compute f=Ku. The associated inverse problem takes the data f and the model K and seeks a state u with Ku=f.
In the sense of Hadamard well-posedness, a problem is well posed if a solution exists for every admissible datum, is unique, and depends continuously on the datum in the specified input and output topologies. An inverse problem is ill-posed if at least one of these requirements fails. Thus ill-posedness means failure of existence, uniqueness or stability. The topologies and the admissible data set matter: restricting the data to the range restores existence, but need not restore continuous dependence.

For a bounded linear operator K:U→V between Hilbert spaces, a least-squares solution of data f is a minimizer of the residual norm:
uˉ∈argminu∈U​∥Ku−f∥V2​.
(1)
The minimum-norm least-squares solution is the one of smallest U-norm among these minimizers. Equivalently it is the unique least-squares solution in N(K)⊥. If the equation is consistent, this is the smallest-norm exact solution.
Let P be the orthogonal projection onto R(K)​. The orthogonal decomposition by a closed subspace gives
∥Ku−f∥2=∥Ku−Pf∥2+∥(I−P)f∥2.
(2)
The first term has infimum zero by the definition of closure; that infimum is attained exactly if Pf∈R(K). This proves the least-squares existence criterion:
least-squares solutions exist⟺f∈R(K)⊕R(K)⊥.​
(3)
They exist for every datum precisely when the range is closed. Once a solution exists, all solutions differ by elements of the closed null space, and projecting onto its orthogonal complement gives the unique minimum-norm solution.
For a counterexample to existence, take K:ℓ2→ℓ2, (Ku)j​=uj​/j, and fj​=1/j. The range is dense, so the least residual infimum is zero; finite truncations realize residuals tending to zero. But an exact preimage would have every coordinate equal to one and would not belong to ℓ2. Thus no least-squares minimizer exists for this datum.

Put M=N(K)⊥ for a bounded linear operator between Hilbert spaces. Its restriction K∣M​:M→R(K) is bijective. Define the Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator by
D(K†)=R(K)⊕R(K)⊥,K†f=(K∣M​)−1PR(K)​​f.​
(4)
The inverse vanishes on R(K)⊥. For each datum in this domain, K†f is the minimum-norm least-squares solution; all least-squares solutions are K†f+N(K). On their natural domains,
K†K=PM​,KK†=PR(K)​​.
(5)
The equivalent continuity condition is
K† is continuous in the ambient norms⟺R(K) is closed.​
(6)
If the range is closed, the inverse restriction is bounded by the bounded inverse theorem. Conversely, if ∥K†f∥≤C∥f∥ on its domain and Kun​ converges, replace un​ by PM​un​. The inequality makes these preimages a Cauchy sequence, whose limit maps to the limiting datum; the range is therefore closed. Boundedness here uses the data norm, not a graph norm that would conceal instability.

The range of the Volterra integration operator is {g∈H1(0,1):g(0)=0}, using the representative of a Sobolev space element that is an absolutely continuous function. Indeed Ku has weak derivative u and zero initial trace; conversely the fundamental theorem of calculus reconstructs such a g from its derivative. The given step is in L2, and its value at the single midpoint is immaterial. It cannot be the image of an L2 function: such an image is continuous, whereas no continuous representative agrees almost everywhere with zero on the left half and one on the right half. f∈R(K)​∖R(K).​

A regularization of an inverse problem is a family of continuous maps Rα​:V→U, α>0, such that Rα​f→K†f for each f∈D(K†) as α↓0. A linear regularization requires each Rα​ to be a bounded linear operator.
A regularization parameter choice assigns α=α(δ,fδ)>0 from the noise bound and possibly the measured data. A convergent regularization of an inverse problem satisfies, for every fixed admissible exact datum,
∥g−f∥≤δsup​∥Rα(δ,g)​g−K†f∥⟶0(δ↓0).​
(16)
In the usual definition the chosen parameter also tends to zero uniformly over this noise ball. For a linear regularization with exact-data consistency, the conditions α(δ)→0 and δ∥Rα(δ)​∥→0 are sufficient.
For example, Tikhonov regularization with identity penalty has Rα​=(K∗K+αI)−1K∗ and ∥Rα​∥≤1/(2α​). Its spectral filter converges to the Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator on its domain. Taking α(δ)=δ for small positive noise levels gives both required limits.

For a real Banach space X and a proper convex function E:X→R∪{+∞}, the convex conjugate is the extended-valued function on the continuous dual space
E∗(p)=x∈Xsup​{⟨x,p⟩−E(x)},p∈X∗.​
(31)
Here proper means that E never takes −∞ and is finite somewhere. The pairing is the duality pairing, or the inner product after identification in a Hilbert space. The conjugate is convex and lower semicontinuous because it is a supremum of continuous affine functions. Its definition gives the Fenchel–Young inequality E(x)+E∗(p)≥⟨x,p⟩; the Fenchel-Moreau theorem recovers E as E∗∗ under the stated closed-convex hypotheses.

In an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, every bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence, but it need not have a norm-convergent one. For example an orthonormal sequence en​ has pairwise distance 2​ and therefore no strongly convergent subsequence, although en​⇀0 by Bessel inequality.
More generally, weak sequential compactness of bounded sequences in a reflexive Banach space ensures a weakly convergent subsequence with its limit in that space. Conversely, this property for all bounded sequences characterizes reflexivity, using the Eberlein-Šmulian theorem and weak compactness of the closed unit ball. Thus reflexivity replaces finite-dimensional norm compactness by weak sequential compactness.
In a nonreflexive Banach space there is no such general weak-subsequence guarantee. The coordinate unit vectors in ℓ1 give an example: by the Schur property, weak convergence would imply norm convergence, impossible for their pairwise distance two. Banach-Alaoglu theorem supplies weak-star compactness of a dual unit ball, which gives convergent subnets, not automatically convergent subsequences. For a separable predual, the weak-star compact dual ball is metrizable and subsequences do suffice. For an arbitrary Banach space one can also regard a bounded sequence in its bidual and extract a weak-star convergent subnet there; the limit need not belong to the original space. These qualifications distinguish weak from weak-star compactness.

proxE​(q)=argxmin​{E(x)+21​∥x−q∥2}.​

∂J(v)={p∈U∗:J(u)≥J(v)+⟨u−v,p⟩ for all u∈U}.​

Use the standard inverse-problem setting of a bounded linear K:ℓ2→ℓ2 and exact data having a finite-penalty solution. Let u† denote the unique J-minimizing exact solution for J(u)=∥u∥1​+β∥u∥22​, β>0. This is the limiting target of elastic net regularization in Hilbert sequence space; it need not be the Moore–Penrose minimum-norm solution unless additional hypotheses identify them, for example injectivity of K.

The direct method on the reflexive l2 sequence space gives existence, and strict convexity gives uniqueness. The formulas also cover λ=0, where exact reconstruction occurs at the first update. The subgradient conditions are sufficient for global minimization because each objective is convex.
There is a genuine uniqueness issue in the printed claim about arbitrary iterates. Strict convexity of data fidelity in Ku ensures that any two minimizers at a given step have the same image under K. Induction therefore makes every minimizer branch have the same dual variables and attain exact data at the stated finite index. It does not make their states equal to the specified γuλ​ if K has a null space.
For a counterexample take U=R2, V=R, K(x,y)=x, J(x,y)=∣x∣, uλ​=(1,0), and λ=1. Every step leaves y completely unconstrained. Choosing the minimizing state's second coordinate to be k gives valid updates and subgradients but never reaches (γ,0) and does not converge as a state sequence. Thus finite exact-data recovery holds for all minimizer branches; exact recovery of the stated vector needs uniqueness or the aligned branch. If K is injective, equal reconstructed data identify the state, and the literal vector conclusion follows.
