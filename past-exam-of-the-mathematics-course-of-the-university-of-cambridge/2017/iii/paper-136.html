A suitable version of the Hensel lemma is this: if R is a complete discrete valuation ring, h∈R[X], and a is a root of h with h′(a)=0, there is a unique a∈R reducing to a with h(a)=0. Starting with any lift, Newton iteration over a valued field aj+1​=aj​−h(aj​)/h′(aj​) keeps the derivative a unit and doubles the error valuation, giving convergence. Uniqueness follows by factoring h(u)−h(v) when two roots have the same residue.
The field in the rest of this question is not assumed complete, so that version cannot simply be applied to it. Instead use the stated uniqueness of the extension of an absolute value. Let αi​,αj​ be any two roots of a polynomial f, which is irreducible. The field embedding sending αi​ to αj​ identifies K(αi​) with K(αj​). Pulling the latter field's absolute value back gives an extension on K(αi​), which must coincide with the given one. More generally, for every h∈K[X],
∣h(αi​)∣=∣h(αj​)∣.
(1)
This proves equal absolute values of algebraic conjugates without assuming separability or a transitive action of a Galois group; repeated roots cause no difficulty.
Every root of the monic f∈OK​[X] has absolute value at most one. Indeed, if ∣α∣>1, then each lower term satisfies ∣cj​αj∣≤∣α∣j<∣α∣n. The ultrametric inequality makes the leading term dominate their sum, contradicting f(α)=0. Thus
∣αi​∣=∣α1​∣≤1for all i.​
(2)
All roots lie in the valuation ring OM​, so their residues are defined in the residue field kM​. Let φ∈kK​[X] be the monic minimal polynomial of an algebraic element of α1​; this residue is algebraic because it satisfies the monic f​. Choose a coefficientwise lift Φ∈OK​[X]. Then ∣Φ(α1​)∣<1, and the conjugacy argument gives ∣Φ(αi​)∣<1 for every i. Hence every αi​ is a root of φ.
Reduction of the splitting-field factorization, with multiplicities, gives
f​(X)=∏i=1n​(X−αi​)in kM​[X].
(3)
Every monic irreducible factor of f​ over kK​ has a root among these residues. That root also satisfies φ, so its minimal polynomial is φ. Unique factorization therefore proves the pure-power reduction of a monic irreducible polynomial:
f​=φmfor some m≥1.​
(4)
This does not assert that φ is separable or that the reduction is square-free.
Finally, factor the monic g over K as g=∏j​hjej​​ with distinct monic irreducible polynomials hj​. The same leading-term argument bounds every root of g by one. Coefficients of any hj​ are elementary symmetric polynomials in some of these roots, so they lie in OK​. By the preceding result each hj​ is a power of a single monic irreducible residue polynomial φj​.
Since the prescribed g​1​,g​2​ are coprime, every φj​ occurs on exactly one side. Assign the entire factor hjej​​ to that side and form the two products. Comparing irreducible-factor multiplicities in the reduction proves the coprime factor lifting from valuation-extension uniqueness:
g=g1​g2​,gi​∈OK​[X] monic,gi​​=g​i​.​
(5)
Empty products are one, covering constant prescribed factors. The construction proves the required factor-lifting form of the Hensel lemma directly from extension uniqueness, without adding completeness as an unstated hypothesis.

On continuous functions on the p-adic integers, define the forward difference operator and the Mahler coefficients by
(Δf)(x)=f(x+1)−f(x),an​(f)=(Δnf)(0)=∑j=0n​(−1)n−j(jn​)f(j).
(6)
Writing Sf(x)=f(x+1), the explicit iterate follows from Δn=(S−I)n. The ultrametric inequality and integral binomial coefficients imply ∥Δf∥∞​≤∥f∥∞​ and ∣an​(f)∣p​≤∥f∥∞​, where the supremum norm is taken over Zp​.
The Mahler theorem states that every such f has a unique expansion with uniform convergence
f(x)=n=0∑∞​an​(f)(nx​),an​(f)⟶0.​
(7)
Conversely, every sequence in Qp​ tending to zero yields a continuous function by this expansion. The binomial polynomials form an orthonormal expansion in the non-Archimedean sense: ∥f∥∞​=supn​∣an​(f)∣p​.
Here is the requested proof under the permitted coefficient-decay assumption. For n≥1, the binomial polynomial Bn​(x)=x(x−1)⋯(x−n+1)/n! is a continuous function on Zp​. Its values on nonnegative integers are integral, and those integers are dense in the p-adic integers. Since Zp​ is a closed set in Qp​, Bn​(Zp​)⊆Zp​. Also Bn​(n)=1, so ∥Bn​∥∞​=1, including B0​=1.
If an​→0, the ultrametric inequality gives the uniform tail bound
​∑n=NM​an​Bn​​∞​≤maxN≤n≤M​∣an​∣p​⟶0.
(8)
Completeness of Qp​ gives a uniform limit F, and the uniform limit theorem makes F continuous. At any nonnegative integer m, all Bn​(m) with n>m vanish. Finite binomial inversion gives
∑n=0m​(nm​)an​=∑j=0m​f(j)(jm​)∑n=jm​(−1)n−j(n−jm−j​)=f(m),
(9)
since the inner sum is (1−1)m−j. Hence F=f on a dense subset and therefore on all of Zp​. The values at m=0,1,2,… recover each coefficient recursively because Bm​(m)=1; this proves uniqueness. The expansion bounds ∥f∥∞​ by sup∣an​∣p​, and the earlier coefficient inequality proves equality. This also proves the converse statement.
Although the problem allows us to assume decay, it can be established independently. By compactness and uniform continuity, approximate f uniformly by h constant on residue classes modulo pr. On this finite-dimensional space, Spr=I and
(S−I)pr≡Spr−I=0(modp).
(10)
The matrix of Δpr has entries divisible by p, so its operator norm is at most p−1; consequently ∥Δjpr+sh∥∞​≤p−j∥h∥∞​ for 0≤s<pr. Thus an​(h)→0. Since ∣an​(f)−an​(h)∣p​≤∥f−h∥∞​, arbitrary uniform approximation proves automatic decay of Mahler coefficients.
For the last claim, construct the discrete antidifferentiation on the p-adic integers
G(x)=∑n=0∞​an​(f)(n+1x​).
(11)
Its coefficient sequence is 0,a0​,a1​,…, still tending to zero, so it is continuous. By Pascal's identity, ΔBn+1​=Bn​. The boundedness of Δ permits applying it to the uniform limit, giving ΔG=f. For the stated linear map, invariance under translation by one now gives
T(f)=T(ΔG)=T(G(⋅+1))−T(G)=0.
(12)
Thus translation-invariant linear forms on p-adic continuous functions vanish:
T=0.​
(13)
No continuity of T has been assumed or used. In particular, one must not justify this by applying T termwise to an infinite Mahler expansion; it is the existence of a continuous discrete antiderivative that makes the conclusion valid.

Let L/K be a finite Galois extension of non-Archimedean local fields, let G=Gal(L/K), and normalize the discrete valuation by vL​(L×)=Z. The lower ramification numbering is
G−1​=G,Gi​={σ∈G:vL​(σ(x)−x)≥i+1 for every x∈OL​},i≥0.
(14)
Here G0​ is the inertia group and G1​ is the wild inertia group. For real s≥−1, put Gs​=G⌈s⌉​; thus Gs​=G0​ for −1<s≤0. Define the Herbrand function and its inverse by
φ(s)=∫0s​[G0​:Gt​]dt​,ψ=φ−1,
(15)
extending φ(s)=s on [−1,0]. The upper ramification numbering is Gu=Gψ(u)​ for u≥−1.
Let αp−α=t1−p over K=Fp​((t)), with t its uniformizer. The Artin–Schreier polynomial has derivative −1 in characteristic p, so all its roots are distinct. If α is one root and β another, then (β−α)p=β−α, so β−α∈Fp​. Conversely every α+a, a∈Fp​, is a root. Thus L=K(α) contains all roots and is a splitting field of a separable polynomial; it is Galois. Every automorphism has the form σa​(α)=α+a, and its Galois group embeds in the additive group of Fp​.
There is no root in K. If vK​(z)≥0, then zp−z cannot have negative valuation. If vK​(z)<0, the ultrametric inequality gives vK​(zp−z)=pvK​(z), divisible by p, whereas vK​(t1−p)=1−p is not. The Galois group is consequently nontrivial. Its order divides the prime p, so
[L:K]=p,G≅(Fp​,+).​
(16)
Let e=e(L/K) be the ramification index. The equation forces vL​(α)<0, and hence
pvL​(α)=(1−p)e.
(17)
As gcd(p,p−1)=1, p∣e. Since e≤[L:K]=p, we get e=p, residue-field degree one, and vL​(α)=1−p. Thus this is a totally ramified extension, and
πL​=tαhas vL​(πL​)=p+(1−p)=1.
(18)
This is a uniformizer. The uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups states that for a totally ramified Galois extension, σ∈Gi​ exactly when vL​(σ(πL​)−πL​)≥i+1. Here every nonidentity automorphism satisfies
σa​(πL​)−πL​=at,vL​(at)=p.
(19)
Therefore the ramification break of an Artin–Schreier pole occurs at p−1, and the lower groups are
Gi​=G(−1≤i≤p−1),Gi​={1}(i≥p).​
(20)
This includes p=2, where the break is one. There is no off-by-one shift: the condition is vL​(σπL​−πL​)≥i+1.
For the upper groups, compute the Herbrand function explicitly:
φ(s)=⎩⎨⎧​s,p−1+ps−(p−1)​,​0≤s≤p−1,s≥p−1.​
(21)
Hence the upper break is also p−1 and, with the stated real-index convention,

(22)

Fix a non-Archimedean local field K, its valuation ring R=OK​, a uniformizer π, and a residue field with cardinality q. The construction below applies both in mixed and equal characteristic. A one-dimensional commutative formal group law is F(X,Y)∈R[[X,Y]] with identity, associativity and commutativity, and F(X,Y)=X+Y+ higher-degree terms. It has a unique formal inverse. Its morphisms are series h(T)∈TR[[T]] with h(F(X,Y))=G(h(X),h(Y)); a unit linear coefficient makes such a morphism invertible by recursive coefficient comparison. Evaluating its integral series on the maximal ideal of a finite extension of K is legitimate because the series converge there.
Choose a Lubin–Tate series f(T) satisfying
f(T)=πT+O(T2),f(T)≡Tq(modπ).
(23)
A useful choice is f(T)=πT+Tq. The Lubin–Tate functional equation lemma constructs a unique series with any prescribed linear form ℓ and satisfying
f(H(X1​,…,Xr​))=H(f(X1​),…,f(Xr​)).
(24)
Here is its coefficient argument. If terms below degree d have been found, the unknown homogeneous degree-d term appears with multiplier π−πd. The already known error has every coefficient divisible by π: after reduction, the equation becomes H(X)q=H(X1q​,…,Xrq​), valid because the residue coefficients lie in Fq​. Since π−πd is π times a unit for d≥2, the next term is uniquely determined and integral. Recursion proves existence and uniqueness.
Use ℓ=X+Y to construct Ff​, and ℓ=aT to construct [a]f​ for every a∈R. Uniqueness gives Ff​(X,0)=X and Ff​(0,Y)=Y. Comparing [a]f​(Ff​(X,Y)) with Ff​([a]f​(X),[a]f​(Y)), which satisfy the same functional equation and have the same linear form, proves that [a]f​ is an endomorphism of the law. The same uniqueness applied to three variables proves associativity: Ff​(Ff​(X,Y),Z) and Ff​(X,Ff​(Y,Z)) have the same linear form and functional equation. It also proves commutativity and the identities
[a+b]f​(T)=Ff​([a]f​(T),[b]f​(T)),[ab]f​=[a]f​∘[b]f​,[1]f​(T)=T,[π]f​=f.
(25)
Thus this Lubin–Tate formal group is equipped with an action of the whole ring R, not only its integers. For a unit a, the inverse of [a]f​ is [a−1]f​. The same recursion, with two series for the fixed π, gives a unique strict Lubin–Tate change of series. Its integral series and inverse converge on torsion points, so it identifies their torsion fields. We may compute with f(T)=πT+Tq without losing the general construction for that uniformizer.
Write f0​(T)=T and fn​=f∘n. The Lubin–Tate torsion of level n is
Λn​={x algebraic over K:∣x∣<1, fn​(x)=0}.
(26)
For n≥1, its primitive points are the roots of
Φn​(T)=fn−1​(T)fn​(T)​=π+fn−1​(T)q−1.
(27)
This is a monic polynomial of degree Dn​=(q−1)qn−1. Modulo π it is TDn​, and its constant coefficient is exactly π. Thus the Eisenstein layers of Lubin–Tate torsion are Eisenstein polynomials. A root λn​ generates a totally ramified extension of degree Dn​, and has vK​(λn​)=1/Dn​ in the uniquely extended valuation. Also fn−1​(λn​)q−1=−π=0, so it has exact level n.
The map
R/πnR⟶Λn​,a⟼[a]f​(λn​)
(28)
is well-defined and injective. If a=πru with r<n and u a unit, then [a]λn​=[u]fr​(λn​)=0, because [u] is invertible and λn​ is primitive. Conversely every multiple of πn kills it. The image therefore contains qn distinct roots of the degree-qn polynomial fn​, so it is all of Λn​. This proves, without merely assuming the torsion cardinality,
Λn​≅R/πnRas an R-module.​
(29)
Its primitive generators are exactly [a]λn​ with a∈(R/πnR)×.
Define the Lubin–Tate extension Kπ,n​=K(Λn​). Every integral endomorphism series evaluated at λn​ converges in the complete field K(λn​), so all torsion points already lie there. Hence
Kπ,n​=K(λn​),[Kπ,n​:K]=Dn​.
(30)
It is the splitting field of fn​, which has qn distinct roots by the module calculation. Thus it is Galois. Every K-automorphism preserves the uniquely extended absolute value on a field, hence is continuous and commutes with limits of the integral endomorphism series. Its automorphisms therefore commute with the endomorphism action and send a primitive generator to a primitive generator. There is a unique unit class aσ​ such that σ(λn​)=[aσ​]λn​. The Lubin–Tate Galois action on primitive torsion gives an injective homomorphism
Gal(Kπ,n​/K)⟶(R/πnR)×.
(31)
Both sides have order Dn​=(q−1)qn−1, so it is an isomorphism. In particular every finite layer is abelian and totally ramified.
For an example, take K=Qp​, π=p, and the equally admissible series f(T)=(1+T)p−1. Its formal group law is the formal multiplicative group F(X,Y)=X+Y+XY, with [a](T)=(1+T)a−1 for a∈Zp​. The binomial polynomials make its coefficients integral. Its level-n torsion points are ζ−1 with ζpn=1. Thus the Lubin–Tate cyclotomic example gives Kp,n​=Qp​(μpn​) and Galois action ζ↦ζa. The order formula includes p=2,n=1, when the first field and its Galois group are trivial over Q2​.
Finally choose primitive points compatibly, [π]λn+1​=λn​. They exist by taking a root of f(T)−λn​ in the algebraic closure; its roots have positive valuation and exact level n+1. Then Kπ,n​⊆Kπ,n+1​, and restriction of Galois automorphisms corresponds to reduction of unit classes. The Lubin–Tate tower Kπ​=⋃n​Kπ,n​ consequently has the topological Galois group
Gal(Kπ​/K)≅nlim​​(R/πnR)×≅R×.​
(32)
The final isomorphism uses completeness of the discrete valuation ring R. This computes the finite-layer and infinite-tower Galois groups directly from the formal action and the primitive Eisenstein polynomials.
