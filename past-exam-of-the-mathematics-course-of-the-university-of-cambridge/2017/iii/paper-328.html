Use a time Laplace transform and a spatial Green function. The first-order transport term must be retained: the Airy resolvent kernel for ∂x3​+p alone would solve a different equation. The governing linear partial differential equation is the linear dispersive Stokes equation, distinct from the viscous-fluid Stokes equation.
For the time interval of interest, extend the given boundary signal by zero after T and put
G(p)=∫0T​e−psg1​(s)ds,Rep>0.
(1)
The possible discontinuity at T does not affect a solution at t<T. Equivalently, use any sufficiently regular continuation beyond T: the resulting causal Green function produces the same earlier solution. We seek the usual solution with spatial decay and a time transform, rather than an unrestricted spatially growing solution on the half-line.
Writing Q(x,p) for the time Laplace transform, the Laplace transform of a derivative gives
Qxxx​+Qx​+pQ=q0​(x),Qx​(0,p)=G(p).
(2)
The corresponding characteristic roots are the three roots of a polynomial
Pp​(r)=r3+r+p.
(3)
For Rep>0, label the unique root of negative real part by r−​(p), and the two roots of positive real part by r1​(p),r2​(p). This spatial root splitting for the dispersive Stokes resolvent is easily checked: if r=ik, then p=−i(k−k3) is imaginary, so no root crosses the imaginary axis as p varies in the right half-plane. For real p>0 the cubic is strictly increasing on the real line and has one negative real root, while its conjugate pair has positive real parts. The root count remains the same throughout that connected half-plane. A repeated root would satisfy 3r2+1=0 and again put p on the imaginary axis. All roots are therefore simple there, and r−​(p) is a nonzero holomorphic function.
Set dj​=3rj2​+1, including j=−. Define the Stokes resolvent kernel with advection
Rp​(s)=⎩⎨⎧​er−​s/d−​,−j=1∑2​erj​s/dj​,​s≥0,s<0.​
(4)
The sign on the negative half-line is essential. To verify the Green function, use the partial fraction decomposition
Pp​(r)1​=∑j∈{−,1,2}​dj​(r−rj​)1​.
(5)
Its expansion at infinity yields the reciprocal-polynomial root identities
∑j​dj​1​=0,∑j​dj​rj​​=0,∑j​dj​rj2​​=1.
(6)
Consequently Rp​ and Rp′​ are continuous at zero, and Rp′′​ has jump one. Each piece solves the homogeneous ordinary differential equation, so the jump conditions for a third-order Green function imply, in the sense of distributional derivatives,
(p+∂s​+∂s3​)Rp​=δ0​.
(7)
Both pieces decay in their respective spatial directions.
Now define
V(x,p)=∫0∞​Rp​(x−y)q0​(y)dy.
(8)
It satisfies the transformed inhomogeneous equation. The difference Q−V solves the homogeneous linear ordinary differential equation; spatial decay leaves only er−​x. Imposing the Neumann boundary condition fixes its coefficient, giving the half-line Stokes solution with a boundary derivative
Q(x,p)=V(x,p)+r−​er−​x​[G(p)−Vx​(0,p)].
(9)
No division by a vanishing root is involved, since r−​=0 would require p=0. Moreover the remaining boundary trace is explicitly known from q0​:
Vx​(0,p)=−∑j=12​dj​rj​​∫0∞​e−rj​yq0​(y)dy.
(10)
Thus introduce the completely explicit initial-data kernel
Kp​(x,y)=Rp​(x−y)−r−​er−​x​Rp′​(−y)=Rp​(x−y)+r−​er−​x​∑j=12​dj​rj​e−rj​y​.
(11)
For any upward Bromwich contour Rep=σ>0 to the right of any transform singularities, the requested integral representation is
q(x,t)=2πi1​∫σ−i∞σ+i∞​ept[∫0∞​Kp​(x,y)q0​(y)dy+r−​(p)er−​(p)x​∫0T​e−psg1​(s)ds]dp.​
(12)
The contour integral is understood in the standard Bromwich inversion formula sense, with truncation limits if it is not absolutely convergent. Every term is expressed in the supplied data and algebraic characteristic roots; there are no unknown Dirichlet or second-derivative boundary traces.
The transformed equation verifies the linear partial differential equation and the initial term, while taking the spatial derivative gives Qx​(0,p)=G(p). Bromwich inversion formula therefore supplies the prescribed derivative for 0<t<T and the initial value for x>0. For zero initial and boundary data, the transformed solution is a decaying homogeneous mode with zero boundary derivative, hence vanishes; this proves uniqueness in the stated transform/decay class. A change of the boundary continuation after T has transform e−pT times another causal transform and contributes only after T, by the Laplace transform time-shift rule. The printed dot on q0​ is interpreted as its derivative in its sole variable x, so the stated corner compatibility is q0′​(0)=g1​(0). Additional differentiability at the corner requires the corresponding higher-order compatibility, but is not needed for the representation at positive x,t.

The drift makes the natural measure weighted. Let
A=∂x2​+β∂x​=e−βx∂x​(eβx∂x​),w(x)=eβx.
(13)
Under homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, the differential operator is self-adjoint in the weighted inner product ⟨u,v⟩w​=∫0L​wuvdx. Indeed integration by parts gives
⟨u,Av⟩w​=−∫0L​wu′v′dx
(14)
when the derivative boundary terms vanish. In particular its eigenvalues are nonpositive and it has the constant stationary eigenfunction. We first construct the weighted Neumann heat kernel with constant drift, then incorporate the two prescribed coordinate derivatives.
Put b=β/2. The Robin gauge transform for constant drift, ϕ=e−bxψ, turns Aϕ=−λϕ into
ψ′′+(λ−b2)ψ=0,ψ′(0)=bψ(0),ψ′(L)=bψ(L).
(15)
These are coordinate-derivative Robin boundary conditions, with the same algebraic sign at both endpoints. The left condition for an oscillatory solution of wavenumber k gives ψ=cos(kx)+(b/k)sin(kx). The right condition then reduces to (k+b2/k)sin(kL)=0, giving kn​=nπ/L. There is also a separate mode λ=0, ψ=ebx, which must not be discarded by restricting to oscillatory k. For 0≤λ<b2, write k=iη: the endpoint determinant reduces to λsinh(ηL)=0, leaving only that zero mode. At λ=b2, the linear solution ψ=A+Bx satisfies both endpoint conditions only if it vanishes, because b>0. The complete Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion therefore uses
ϕ0​(x)=1,λ0​=0,ϕn​(x)=e−bx[cos(kn​x)+kn​b​sin(kn​x)],λn​=kn2​+b2,n≥1.
(16)
Their squared norms in the weighted measure are
N0​=βeβL−1​,Nn​=2L​(1+kn2​b2​),n≥1.
(17)
The cancellation of the weight against e−2bx makes the latter formula an ordinary trigonometric integral. Define
Kβ​(x,y,t)=∑n=0∞​Nn​e−λn​tϕn​(x)ϕn​(y)​.
(18)
For t>0 this kernel is smooth in the interior. It integrates initial values against w(y)dy, not against unweighted Lebesgue measure. It is symmetric in x,y as a weighted kernel, even though the drift operator does not look symmetric in an unweighted inner product.
To determine the boundary forcing, set an​(t)=⟨ϕn​,q(⋅,t)⟩w​/Nn​. Two applications of integration by parts leave
Nn​an′​(t)=−λn​Nn​an​(t)+eβLϕn​(L)h1​(t)−ϕn​(0)g1​(t).
(19)
The minus sign at the left endpoint is the sign of the boundary evaluation [⋅]0L​; the supplied g1​ is qx​(0,t), not an outward normal derivative. Solving this linear differential equation by an integrating factor and recombining the modes gives the requested space-time integral representation:
q(x,t)=∫0L​Kβ​(x,y,t)eβyq0​(y)dy+∫0t​[eβLKβ​(x,L,t−s)h1​(s)−Kβ​(x,0,t−s)g1​(s)]ds.​
(20)
This is the Neumann boundary-forcing formula. The zero mode also gives the useful consistency check
dtd​∫0L​eβxq(x,t)dx=eβLh1​(t)−g1​(t).​
(21)
Ordinary unweighted mass instead obeys d∫0L​qdx/dt=h1​−g1​+β[q(L,t)−q(0,t)]. Replacing the weighted measure by dy or deleting the constant mode would therefore give an incorrect solution.
There is a subtle point when checking nonzero derivative data: every homogeneous eigenfunction has zero endpoint derivative, but differentiating the boundary-forcing sum and its time integral term by term at an endpoint is not valid. The kernel is singular as t−s↓0, and the prescribed derivatives are interior limits of the full solution. An explicit Laplace transform form verifies those limits without this interchange and gives an alternative purely contour-integral version.
For Rep>0, set κ=p+b2​ using the principal square root, and define
up​(x)=e−bx[cosh(κx)+κb​sinh(κx)],
(22)
vp​(x)=e−b(x−L)[cosh(κ(L−x))−κb​sinh(κ(L−x))].
(23)
Both solve (p−A)f=0, with up​(0)=vp​(L)=1, up′​(0)=vp′​(L)=0. Their weighted Wronskian is the nonzero constant
Dp​=eβx(up′​vp​−up​vp′​)=ebLκp​sinh(κL).
(24)
Hence the resolvent kernel for Neumann advection-diffusion on an interval is
Rp​(x,y)=Dp​up​(min(x,y))vp​(max(x,y))​.
(25)
The first derivative in x has jump −e−βy, giving (p−Ax​)Rp​=e−βyδy​ in the sense of distributional derivatives. If G(p)=∫0T​e−psg1​(s)ds and H(p)=∫0T​e−psh1​(s)ds, an equivalent transformed solution is
Q(x,p)=∫0L​Rp​(x,y)eβyq0​(y)dy+Dp​eβLup​(x)H(p)−vp​(x)G(p)​.
(26)
In fact vp′​(0)=−Dp​ and up′​(L)=e−βLDp​, so Qx​(0,p)=G(p) and Qx​(L,p)=H(p) exactly. The inverse Bromwich contour integral of eptQ(x,p)/(2πi) is an alternative final answer. Its poles at p=0 and p=−λn​ reproduce the constant and decaying modes of Kβ​, respectively; apparent square-root singularities in the normalized hyperbolic factors are removable. This also independently checks the kernel normalization and boundary signs.
As in question 1, the printed dot on q0​ denotes its spatial derivative: the two corner conditions are q0′​(0)=g1​(0) and q0′​(L)=h1​(0). The initial eigenfunction expansion converges to q0​ in the weighted space and, for the stated smooth compatible data, has the appropriate classical initial and boundary limits. Differences of solutions with zero data satisfy 21​d∥q∥w2​/dt=−∫0L​w∣qx​∣2dx, proving uniqueness in the usual regular solution class. In the limit β→0, N0​→L, ϕn​→cos(nπx/L), and the ordinary Neumann heat kernel on an interval and its boundary signs are recovered.

Interpret the malformed printed domain inequality as the first quadrant x>0,y>0, as indicated by the two axis boundary conditions. There is also a genuine missing hypothesis: decay of the boundary data does not require the harmonic function itself to be bounded. For zero data, both q=0 and q=xy satisfy the Laplace equation and both edge values, but the Wirtinger derivative of the latter is
qz​=21​(qx​−iqy​)=21​(y−ix)=−2i​z=0.
(27)
Thus the data do not determine an arbitrary solution's derivative without a growth restriction. We derive the integral representation for the bounded solution, which is also the standard decaying solution when the continuous edge data decay. Any other admissible unbounded solution can differ by a zero-boundary harmonic function.
The conformal map w=z2 sends the quadrant bijectively to the complex upper half-plane: the positive real axis maps to s>0, and the positive imaginary axis maps to s<0. Define its real-axis boundary function by
f(s)={g2​(s​),g1​(−s​),​s>0,s<0,​f(0)=g1​(0)=g2​(0).
(28)
The compatibility at the corner makes f continuous there. The Dirichlet Poisson integral in a quadrant follows from the upper-half-plane Poisson kernel:
q(x,y)=π1​∫R​∣s−z2∣2Im(z2)f(s)​ds.
(29)
It is bounded by the supremum of the bounded edge data, and the kernel's approximate identity property supplies both Dirichlet boundary conditions. More explicitly, for z2=a+ic, where a=x2−y2 and c=2xy>0, this integral is
q(x,y)=π2c​∫0∞​r[(r2−a)2+c2g2​(r)​+(r2+a)2+c2g1​(r)​]dr.
(30)
It displays directly which part of the real-line boundary corresponds to each original axis.
To obtain its derivative, first take real data and use the Schwarz integral formula. A holomorphic function whose real part is the Poisson solution in the w-plane is
F(w)=πi1​∫R​f(s)[s−w1​−1+s2s​]ds.
(31)
The regularizing subtraction is independent of w and affects only the imaginary normalization; it permits bounded data without an artificial integrability assumption. Then q=ReF(z2). By the chain rule and the Wirtinger derivative,
qz​=zF′(z2)=πiz​∫R​(s−z2)2f(s)​ds.
(32)
The factor is z, not 2z: taking the real part contributes the factor 1/2 in the complex derivative. Differentiation under the integral sign is justified uniformly on compact interior subsets because the denominator stays away from the real boundary and decays quadratically at infinity. Splitting the real integral at zero and substituting s=r2 or s=−r2 gives the holomorphic derivative of a quadrant Dirichlet solution:
qz​(z)=πi2z​∫0∞​r[(r2−z2)2g2​(r)​+(r2+z2)2g1​(r)​]dr,x,y>0.​
(33)
This converges for bounded data, and in particular for the stated sufficiently decaying data. No interior pole is encountered since Im(z2)=2xy>0. For complex-valued data the same formula holds by applying the real-data construction separately to their real and imaginary parts and using linearity.
An equivalent form, often convenient when the data's derivatives decay, comes from integration by parts. Since
(r2−z2)22r​=−drd​r2−z21​,(r2+z2)22r​=−drd​r2+z21​,
(34)
the two endpoint terms are −g2​(0)/z2 and +g1​(0)/z2. They cancel by the specified compatibility, leaving
qz​(z)=πiz​[∫0∞​r2−z2g2′​(r)​dr+∫0∞​r2+z2g1′​(r)​dr].​
(35)
This is the harmonic quadrant solution from tangential boundary derivatives applied to the actual derivatives g1′​,g2′​; the supplied g1​,g2​ in this question are boundary values, not derivatives. Omitting those primes would solve a different boundary problem.
For completeness, bounded Dirichlet uniqueness in a quadrant selects the solution just constructed. The difference of two bounded solutions is zero on both axes. Odd reflection across each axis gives a bounded harmonic extension to the punctured plane; the removable singularity for a bounded harmonic function fills the corner. The harmonic Liouville theorem makes the extension constant, and oddness makes it zero. Decaying data give a decaying Poisson extension: split the real-axis integral into a fixed compact interval and a tail with arbitrarily small data; the compact contribution tends to zero as ∣z2∣→∞, while the tail is bounded by that small supremum.
As literally printed without boundedness or a growth condition, the most one can assert is
qz​=(qbounded​)z​+vz​,Δv=0,v∣x=0​=v∣y=0​=0.​
(36)
The zero-boundary harmonic growth in a quadrant includes v=cxy and more generally v=Im(z2m). This is an actual nonuniqueness of the printed problem, not a defect in the integral for the normalized bounded solution.
