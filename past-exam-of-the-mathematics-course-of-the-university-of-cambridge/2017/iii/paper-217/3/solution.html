The Cameron-Martin space of a Gaussian random variable in a Banach space is most conveniently defined using the first Gaussian chaos. Work over the real separable Banach space B, with continuous dual space B∗. For a centered Gaussian random variable in a Banach space, define
G={ℓ(X):ℓ∈B∗}​L2(Ω),Sg=E[Xg](g∈G).
(20)
The set inside the closure is already a linear subspace. Every g∈G is a centered Gaussian random variable. The Bochner integral defining S exists: Fernique's theorem states that for some a>0, Eea∥X∥B2​<∞, and then Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives E∥Xg∥B​<∞.
The map S is injective. Indeed, Sg=0 implies E[gℓ(X)]=ℓ(Sg)=0 for all ℓ∈B∗. Density of these observations in G then implies Eg2=0. Define the Reproducing kernel Hilbert space by
H=SG⊂B,⟨Sg,Sv⟩H​=E[gv],∥Sg∥H​=∥g∥2​.
(21)
The injectivity makes the inner product unambiguous, and the completeness of G makes H a Hilbert space. The embedding into B is continuous since
∥Sg∥B​≤(E∥X∥B2​)1/2∥g∥2​.
(22)
For ℓ∈B∗ put Qℓ=S(ℓ(X)). The reproducing property is
⟨h,Qℓ⟩H​=ℓ(h),⟨Qℓ,Qm⟩H​=E[ℓ(X)m(X)].
(23)
This defines the Banach-space Gaussian RKHS even for a degenerate Gaussian measure.
For h=Sg∈H, write h(X)=g. There is a measurable coordinate h on B, obtained as a limit in L2 space of continuous linear observations, and h(X) has normal distribution N(0,∥h∥H2​). The Cameron-Martin theorem for a Gaussian measure, in its real separable Banach space form, states that if μ is the probability law of X, then the probability law μh​ of X+h is an equivalent probability measure to μ precisely when h∈H. For such a shift,
dμdμh​​(x)=exp(h(x)−21​∥h∥H2​)for μ-almost every x.
(24)
For h∈/H the two laws are mutually singular measures. The coordinate h need not be a continuous functional on B, and the sample X need not belong to H.
Apply this theorem to the shift −h. With a=∥h∥H2​/2, it gives
P(X−h∈C)=e−aE[1C​(X)e−h(X)].
(25)
Choose the measurable coordinate so that h(−x)=−h(x) on a symmetric set of full μ-measure. This is possible by taking an almost-surely convergent subsequence of the approximating linear observations and intersecting its convergence set with its negative. Alternatively the joint law of (X,h(X)) is invariant under simultaneous negation, directly from those observations and their mean-square convergence. Since C=−C and the centered Gaussian measure is symmetric, the two exponential integrals with signs + and − agree. Consequently
P(X−h∈C)=e−aE[1C​(X)coshh(X)]≥e−aP(X∈C),
(26)
because coshv≥1. The symmetric Gaussian translation lower bound is therefore
P(X−h∈C)≥e−∥h∥H2​/2P(X∈C).​
(27)
Only symmetry and Borel measurability of C are required; convexity is not needed.
There is one degenerate flaw in the last printed claim. The separable Banach space B={0} with X=0 has H={0} dense in B, and all positive-radius balls have probability one. Nevertheless Φ(t)=1 for every t>0. Thus the statement needs the additional hypothesis B={0}. If the course convention excludes the zero space, this hypothesis is already implicit.
Under this necessary hypothesis, density of H implies that H contains a nonzero vector. Given 0<a<b, scale that vector to obtain h∈H with ∥h∥B​=(a+b)/2, and choose 0<δ<(b−a)/2. The triangle inequality gives
∥x−h∥B​≤δ⟹a<∥x∥B​<b.
(28)
The centered closed ball Cδ​={x:∥x∥B​≤δ} is a symmetric Borel set. Apply the symmetric Gaussian translation lower bound to get
Φ(b)−Φ(a)​=P(a<∥X∥B​≤b)≥P(∥X−h∥B​≤δ)≥e−∥h∥H2​/2Φ(δ)>0.​
(29)
Hence the Gaussian norm distribution is strictly increasing when B is nonzero. This proves the strict increase of a Gaussian norm distribution without assuming that its distribution has a density or that spheres have zero probability.
