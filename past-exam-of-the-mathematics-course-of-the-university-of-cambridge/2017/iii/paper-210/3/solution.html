(22)
We use 0log(0/q)=0 and assign infinite Kullback-Leibler divergence if Pot≪Q, where ≪ denotes absolute continuity of measures. Common domination alone also suffices for the inequalities with the extended-value convention. The negative part of the logarithmic integral is integrable: on p<q, plog(q/p)≤q−p≤q. Thus the extended-value integral defining the Kullback-Leibler divergence is well-defined. These expressions do not depend on which common dominating measure is chosen. The Kullback-Leibler divergence is generally asymmetric and therefore fails a defining property of a metric.
For completeness, the integral formula for total variation distance follows by taking A={p≥q}: since ∫(p−q)dν=0, the positive and negative parts of p−q have the same integral, and this set attains the supremum.
Put a=∫pq​dν≤1, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. A second application gives

This total variation–Hellinger–relative entropy inequality requires the stated Hellinger distance normalization for its first constant. Here is a testing form of the Le Cam two-point lemma. Suppose two parameter values θ0​,θ1​ in a metric space have separation Δ=d(θ0​,θ1​)>0, and let P0​,P1​ be the corresponding observation laws. Every estimator T satisfies
j=0,1max​Pj​{d(T,θj​)≥Δ/2}≥21−TV(P0​,P1​)​.​
(27)
To prove it, classify T by the closer of the two parameters, breaking a tie in favour of one. Let A={d(T,θ1​)≤d(T,θ0​)}. On A, the triangle inequality implies d(T,θ0​)≥Δ/2, while on Ac it implies d(T,θ1​)>Δ/2. Hence the sum of the two probabilities in the display is at least
P0​(A)+P1​(Ac)=1−{P1​(A)−P0​(A)}≥1−TV(P0​,P1​).
(28)
At least one is at least half the sum, proving the claim. It also gives a lower bound Δ(1−TV)/4 for the maximum expected metric loss.
For absolute-error loss, or any loss given by a metric, there is a stronger expectation form. Take densities p0​,p1​ relative to m=P0​+P1​ and use the triangle inequality under their overlap:
E0​d(T,θ0​)+E1​d(T,θ1​)​≥∫min(p0​,p1​){d(T,θ0​)+d(T,θ1​)}dm≥Δ∫min(p0​,p1​)dm=Δ{1−TV(P0​,P1​)}.​
(29)
Therefore the Le Cam lower bound under absolute-error loss is
j=0,1max​Ej​∣T−θj​∣≥2∣θ1​−θ0​∣​{1−TV(P0​,P1​)}.​
(30)
Both arguments are valid for randomized estimators as well, by adjoining the independent randomization to the observation; doing so does not change total variation distance.
For the normal distribution model, assume the usual nondegenerate convention σ>0 and use the full product observation laws. Choose
μ0​=0,μ1​=2n​σ​.
(31)
For one observation, its Gaussian log likelihood ratio is
logp1​(X)p0​(X)​=2σ2(X−μ1​)2−(X−μ0​)2​.
(32)
Under the first law, E0​(X−μ1​)2=σ2+(μ1​−μ0​)2 and E0​(X−μ0​)2=σ2. Thus the Kullback-Leibler divergence between normal distributions is
KL(N(μ0​,σ2),N(μ1​,σ2))=2σ2(μ1​−μ0​)2​.
(33)
For the independent and identically distributed random variables, the joint log likelihood ratio is the sum of the individual ones, so the Kullback-Leibler divergence of the two full statistical samples is n(μ1​−μ0​)2/(2σ2)=1/4. The comparison just proved implies TV(P0​,P1​)≤1/2. Applying the expectation form of the Le Cam two-point lemma gives
μ∈Rsup​Eμ​∣μ​−μ∣≥42n​σ​=n​c​,c=42​σ​>0.​
(34)
This is a Gaussian location minimax lower bound under absolute-error loss. The constant may depend on the fixed known σ, but neither on the estimator nor on n. If a signed nonzero scale were used, the same result holds with ∣σ∣ in place of σ. If zero noise were allowed, X1​=μ would estimate μ exactly, so a positive lower bound would be false; positive variance is necessary.
