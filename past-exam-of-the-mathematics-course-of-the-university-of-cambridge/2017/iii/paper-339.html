For x in the nonnegative orthant, the positive semidefinite matrix P satisfies xTPx≥0. The nonnegative matrix N satisfies
xTNx=∑i,j​Nij​xi​xj​≥0,
(3)
since every summand is nonnegative. Adding these inequalities proves
xT(P+N)x≥0(x≥0)​.
(4)
Consequently A=P+N is a copositive matrix. The symmetry of N follows from that of A and P; entrywise nonnegativity alone need not imply positive semidefiniteness.
This gives the inclusion of the positive-semidefinite-plus-nonnegative cone in the copositive cone. It is only a sufficient construction; the argument does not claim that every copositive matrix has this decomposition in arbitrary dimension.

For each fixed x in the nonnegative orthant, the map A↦xTAx is a continuous linear function on the vector space of real symmetric matrices. Thus
K=⋂x≥0​{A:⟨A,xxT⟩F​≥0}
(5)
is an intersection of closed half-spaces, using the Frobenius inner product ⟨A,B⟩F​=tr(AB) on this space. Arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed, so K is closed.
If A,B∈K and s,t≥0, then xT(sA+tB)x=sxTAx+txTBx≥0 for every x≥0. Therefore sA+tB∈K, including the zero matrix when s=t=0. Hence
K is a closed convex cone​.
(6)
The copositive cone is defined in the real space of symmetric matrices; symmetry is needed later to recover every matrix entry from these quadratic forms.

A pointed cone has no nonzero line through the origin: equivalently K∩(−K)={0}. If A∈K∩(−K), its quadratic form vanishes on the nonnegative orthant. Testing the coordinate vectors gives Aii​=0. Testing ei​+ej​ then gives
0=(ei​+ej​)TA(ei​+ej​)=2Aij​.
(7)
Since A is a symmetric matrix, every entry is zero. Thus K∩(−K)={0}​.
The identity matrix gives an interior point. For a symmetric perturbation E with operator norm ∥E∥2​<1, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
xT(I+E)x≥(1−∥E∥2​)∥x∥22​>0(x=0).
(8)
This open norm ball around I lies even in the positive semidefinite cone, hence in K. Consequently I∈intK​ and the interior is nonempty.
More generally, the interior of the copositive cone consists exactly of strictly copositive matrices. Positivity on the compact nonnegative unit sphere has a positive minimum and persists under small perturbations; a zero there is destroyed by an arbitrarily small negative multiple of I.

For a convex cone D in an inner product space, use the nonnegative-pairing convention
D∗={B:⟨A,B⟩≥0 for every A∈D}.
(9)
Here the pairing is the Frobenius inner product on real symmetric matrices. Let C0​ be the conic hull of the nonnegative rank-one matrices xxT, and let C=C0​​. For A∈K, ⟨A,xxT⟩F​=xTAx≥0. This extends to conic combinations, and by continuity to their limits. Hence C⊆K∗.
For the converse, if B∈/C, separation from a closed convex cone supplies a symmetric H with
⟨H,B⟩F​<0,⟨H,Z⟩F​≥0(Z∈C).
(10)
In particular xTHx≥0 for every x≥0, so H∈K. The negative pairing then excludes B from K∗. Therefore
K∗=cone{xxT:x≥0}​​.
(11)
The same generator test gives C∗=K. This is the duality of copositive and completely positive cones; the next argument removes the closure.

The real vector space of symmetric matrices has dimension N=n(n+1)/2. By the conic Carathéodory theorem, every member of C0​ is a conic combination of at most N generators. Absorb each nonnegative coefficient into its vector through λxxT=(λ​x)(λ​x)T.
If Bℓ​∈C0​ converges to B, write, padding with zero vectors if necessary,
Bℓ​=∑j=1N​xℓj​xℓjT​,xℓj​≥0.
(12)
The matrix trace satisfies
trBℓ​=∑j=1N​∥xℓj​∥22​.
(13)
The left side is bounded because Bℓ​ converges. Thus the finite tuple (xℓ1​,…,xℓN​) is bounded. The Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem gives a subsequence on which every vector converges, say xℓj​→xj​≥0. Continuity of the outer product now gives B=∑j=1N​xj​xjT​∈C0​. Hence
C0​ is closed,K∗=C=C0​​.
(14)
This proves closedness of the completely positive cone. The uniform bound on the number of factors and the matrix trace bound are both essential: an arbitrary conic hull of a closed generating set need not be closed.

Let U={x≥0:∥x∥2​=1}. This set is nonempty and compact for n≥1, and the quadratic form is continuous. Its minimum α is therefore attained.
For every nonzero y in the nonnegative orthant, normalizing y gives
yT(Q−λI)y=∥y∥22​[(∥y∥2​y​)TQ(∥y∥2​y​)−λ].
(15)
The zero vector imposes no further condition. It follows that Q−λI is a copositive matrix exactly when λ≤α. Thus the feasible scalar set is (−∞,α], and
α=max{λ:Q−λI∈K}​.
(16)
Both optima are attained. This copositive reformulation of an orthant Rayleigh minimum follows directly from homogeneity; it does not require invoking a duality theorem for the original nonconvex constraint.
The restriction to x≥0 matters. For the matrix in part (a), this minimum is 1 whereas the unrestricted smallest eigenvalue is −1.

For X in the dual cone C, copositivity of Q−λI gives ⟨X,Q−λI⟩F​≥0. An upper-bounding Lagrangian for the maximization is therefore
L(λ,X)=λ+⟨X,Q−λI⟩F​=⟨Q,X⟩F​+λ(1−trX).
(17)
Its supremum over unrestricted λ is finite precisely when trX=1. Minimizing that upper bound gives the conic program
X∈Cmin​ ⟨Q,X⟩F​subject to trX=1​.
(18)
This is completely positive optimization, not simply semidefinite programming: C is the completely positive cone.
There is also a direct equality certificate. Every feasible X=∑j​uj​ujT​, uj​≥0, has ∑j​∥uj​∥22​=1. Its objective is a weighted average of the nonnegative-sphere Rayleigh quotients, hence is at least α. For a minimizing unit vector x∗​ from part (g), X∗​=x∗​x∗T​ is feasible and has objective α. Thus X∗​=x∗​x∗T​​ attains the dual and both values agree, without relying on unverified regularity assumptions.

The Gram matrix representation and Yii​=1 give ∥vi​∥2​=1. For a standard Gaussian random vector Z, each ⟨vi​,Z⟩ is a standard normal scalar, so the zero event has probability zero. Choose either sign convention at zero; the resulting vector is almost surely a feasible sign vector. Consequently yTAy≤v∗ almost surely.
The Gaussian sign-correlation identity gives
E[yTAy]=∑i,j​Aij​E[yi​yj​]=π2​∑i,j​Aij​arcsin(Yij​)=π2​tr(Aarcsin[Y]).
(30)
The last equality uses symmetry of the real matrices. Hence
v∗≥E[yTAy]=π2​tr(Aarcsin[Y])​.
(31)
The inverse sine is applied entrywise, not through spectral matrix functional calculus.
For completeness, the correlation coefficient identity has a geometric proof. If the angle between two unit vectors is θ, the isotropic Gaussian random vector direction in their two-dimensional span gives opposite signs on angular sectors with probability θ/π. Thus the sign product has expectation 1−2θ/π=(2/π)arcsin(cosθ). Parallel and antiparallel pairs give the endpoint values 1 and −1 directly. This is Gaussian hyperplane rounding, and needs no independence between the rounded coordinates.

On the unit circle, z−1=z, so
p(z)=2+z+z=(1+z)(1+z)=∣1+z∣2.
(35)
Thus the trigonometric polynomial is nonnegative, with a zero at z=−1, and
q(z)=1+z,p(z)=∣q(z)∣2(∣z∣=1)​.
(36)
Equivalently, writing z=eiθ gives p(z)=2+2cosθ=4cos2(θ/2). The factor has polynomial degree one as required. Multiplication of q by any constant of modulus one leaves the factorization unchanged; uniqueness of q is not claimed.
This is the simplest instance of the Fejér–Riesz theorem, where nonnegativity of a trigonometric polynomial on the unit circle admits a polynomial modulus-square factorization.

Since p is real on the unit circle,
∑k=−dd​pk​zk=p(z)​=∑k=−dd​pk​​z−k=∑k=−dd​p−k​​zk.
(37)
Multiply the difference by zd. It is an ordinary polynomial of polynomial degree at most 2d vanishing at every point of the unit circle. A nonzero polynomial has only finitely many roots of a polynomial, so all its coefficients vanish. This proves the conjugate symmetry of trigonometric polynomial coefficients:
p−k​=pk​​(0≤k≤d)​.
(38)
For arbitrary nonzero complex z, coefficient substitution now gives precisely
p(z−1)=p(z)​​.
(39)
Equivalently p(1/z)=p(z)​. The complex conjugation on the right applies to the whole value, including the coefficients; it cannot simply be discarded away from the circle.
The PDF additionally assumes pd​=0. Then p−d​=pd​​=0, so the ensuing polynomial P=zdp has polynomial degree exactly 2d and nonzero constant term. These clauses and this subpart are absent from the damaged TeX, but present in the PDF.

For z=0, the coefficient symmetry yields
z2dP(1/z)​=z2d(1/z)dp(1/z)​=zdp(z)=P(z).
(40)
Thus P equals its reversed-conjugate polynomial. If ζ=0 is a root of a polynomial, with P(ζ)=0, this identity gives P(1/ζ​)=0, proving
ζ a root ⟹ 1/ζ​ a root​.
(41)
The reciprocal-conjugate root pairing also preserves the multiplicity of a root: reversal and complex conjugation take each factor associated with ζ to the corresponding factor associated with 1/ζ​, with the same exponent.

The fundamental theorem of algebra and the leading coefficient pd​ give P(z)=pd​∏i=1d​(z−ζi​)(z−1/ζi​​). None of the selected ζi​ is zero.
On the unit circle, the identity
z−ζi​​1​=−ζi​​z​(z−ζi​​)
(42)
turns P(z)/zd into
p(z)=c∏i=1d​(z−ζi​)(z−ζi​​)=c∏i=1d​∣z−ζi​∣2,c=∏i​ζi​​(−1)dpd​​.

q(z)=c​i=1∏d​(z−ζi​),p(z)=∣q(z)∣2(∣z∣=1)​.
