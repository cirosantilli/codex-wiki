For this stellar population, let m=M/M⊙​ denote initial mass in units of the solar mass, and let τ=t/Gyr denote present stellar age. Constant formation of equal numbers of stars per unit time makes the stellar age a uniform distribution on [0,10] Gyr, so Y=τ/10 is uniform on [0,1]. The normalized initial mass function has probability density function f(m)=0.1m−2 for m>0.1, since ∫0.1∞​m−2dm=10. Consequently
Y(t)=10Gyrt​,X(M)=∫0.1∞​u−2du∫m∞​u−2du​=m0.1​.​
(1)
These formulae have the stated age and mass domains; outside them the relevant cumulative fractions saturate at zero or one. The probability integral transform makes X uniform on [0,1]: m=0.1/X gives f(m)∣dm/dX∣=1. The time-independent initial mass function and constant number formation rate give independence of X and Y. All fractions here count objects, including white dwarfs, using the stipulated stellar evolution law.
A red giant has
m9​<τ<m10​,0≤τ≤10.
(2)
Thus the mass boundaries are m=9/τ and m=10/τ for positive τ. Equivalently, for 0.9<m<1 the red giant region runs from 9/m to the age cap 10, and for m≥1 it runs from 9/m to 10/m. There are no red giants with m≤0.9. Boundaries have zero probability and their endpoint convention does not affect the fractions. In the uniform (X,Y) square the red giant region is 9X<Y<10X, with triangle vertices (0,0), (1/10,1) and (1/9,1). The white dwarf region is the triangle 0<X<Y/10.
At fixed Y=y, define the conditional probabilities of a red giant, white dwarf and main sequence star by g(y), w(y) and s(y). Their interval widths are
g(y)=90y​,w(y)=10y​=9g(y),s(y)=1−9y​.
(3)
Integration over the uniform distribution of age gives the individual-star fractions
pG​=∫01​g(y)dy=1801​,pW​=∫01​w(y)dy=201​.​
(4)
The systems form a coeval binary population: the two binary star components have the same age. Their masses are independent, so (X1​,X2​,Y) has uniform probability density function on [0,1]3 and their states have conditional independence given Y. Unconditional independence of their states would be incorrect: older systems make both evolved states more likely. The law of total probability now gives
p≥1G​=∫01​[1−(1−g(y))2]dy=901​−243001​=24300269​.​
(5)
The subtraction removes the double counting of systems containing two red giants.

A system with a red giant and a second evolved component is either a pair of red giants, with conditional probability g(y)2, or a red giant and a white dwarf, with conditional probability 2g(y)w(y). The factor two counts which component is the white dwarf; a pair of red giants is counted only once. Since w=9g, the unconditional probability of this event is
pG+evolved​=∫01​(g2+2gw)dy=19∫01​8100y2​dy=2430019​.

(7)
In particular the denominator is a fraction of systems, not the individual-star fraction 1/180.

Neglecting spin, the angular momentum of a circular orbit is J=M1​M2​Ga/M​. Between classical novae the transfer conserves total mass, so M˙=0 and M˙1​=−M˙2​. Its logarithmic derivative gives
JJ˙​=(1−q)M2​M˙2​​+21​aa˙​,RL​R˙L​​=2JJ˙​+(2q−35​)M2​M˙2​​.

In particular, for conservation of angular momentum,
RL​R˙L​​=(2q−35​)M2​M˙2​​,R2​R˙2​​=M2​M˙2​​.​
(15)
The source PDF has J˙=0 here; the TeX's J=0 is a transcription defect. For q>4/3, the Roche-lobe radius response exponent exceeds the equilibrium stellar radius response exponent 1. Since M˙2​<0, the Roche lobe then shrinks faster than the donor star; the overfill increases and drives more transfer, giving positive feedback. Equality at q=4/3 is marginal in this linear response test.
Two qualifications matter. First, q>4/3 is a formal extrapolation outside the stated q<1 range, and the supplied Roche lobe approximation need not remain accurate there. Second, true dynamical stability of binary mass transfer compares ζL​ with the adiabatic stellar radius response exponent ζad​, not ζeq​=1. The stellar thermal equilibrium argument supplies the displayed feedback threshold under the imposed radius law, rather than a necessary and sufficient physical dynamical threshold. For example, the polytropic mass-radius relation for a fully convective adiabatic stellar polytrope with index 3/2 gives ζad​=−1/3; within this same Roche lobe approximation its dynamical threshold is q=2/3. Thus q<4/3 by itself does not guarantee dynamical stability.
Gravitational-wave emission from a binary system removes orbital energy and angular momentum. Magnetic braking of a binary star provides another important loss: a magnetized stellar wind carries away donor spin, while tidal locking couples that spin to the orbit. Tides alone redistribute angular momentum and are not an external sink. Matter expelled from the system can also carry orbital angular momentum, although appreciable continuous mass loss would require nonconservative modifications of the preceding equations. Setting R˙L​/RL​=R˙2​/R2​ gives the binary mass-transfer contact equation

During the classical nova, the ejecta carry the white dwarf's specific angular momentum, not zero angular momentum. With Ω2=GM/a3 and the accretor's distance from the centre of mass a1​=aM2​/M, isotropic escape gives

This idealization neglects changes in the donor from irradiation or interaction with ejecta; those are not supplied in the model.

For a slowly evolving circular orbit, there is no preferred orbital phase at which to excite a persistent eccentricity vector. More precisely, the rotating mass quadrupole moment emits at twice the orbital frequency, with angular harmonic number two; its gravitational-wave energy and angular-momentum balance is E˙=ΩJ˙. The circular Kepler orbit sequence has J=μGMa​ and dE/dJ=Ω, so this loss is tangent to the sequence. At leading adiabatic order it preserves zero secular orbital eccentricity. This is a circular gravitational-wave inspiral, with a small radial drift rather than an exactly fixed-radius Newtonian circle. The energy flux alone would not establish circularity without this symmetry and angular momentum balance.
To leading radiation order use r=a, u=0 and v2=GM/a in the loss formula. Combining it with E=−GMμ/(2a) gives

This formal coalescence time displays the strong a04​ dependence: gravitational-wave emission from a binary system matters far more for close binary stars than for wide ones. The orbital period decreases as a3/2 and the gravitational-wave frequency increases, producing a chirp. A detached binary can be driven into Roche-lobe overflow; further evolution then depends on mass-transfer stability. A sufficiently close compact pair may merge. The weak-field, slow-motion, point-mass approximation ceases to apply before literal a=0; finite stellar radii or strong relativistic effects determine the final interaction.
