The −μψ term means that the generator is the grand potential, rather than just the physical energy. With boundary variations vanishing, the Hamiltonian functional is
H[ψ,ψ∗]=∫[2mℏ2​∣∇ψ∣2+2g​∣ψ∣4−μ∣ψ∣2]ddx.​
(1)
Its functional derivative is
δψ∗δH​=−2mℏ2​∇2ψ+(g∣ψ∣2−μ)ψ,
(2)
so the Gross–Pitaevskii equation is iℏψt​=δH/δψ∗. Treating ψ and ψ∗ as independent variables in this variation gives the factor g, not 2g.
For repulsive interactions g>0 and μ>0, complete the square in the local energy:
2g​n2−μn=2g​(n−gμ​)2−2gμ2​.
(3)
The gradient contribution is nonnegative, so the homogeneous ground state has arbitrary constant complex argument and
n0​=gμ​,ψ0​=n0​​eiθ0​.​
(4)
The infinite-volume constant may be subtracted to define a relative grand potential; it does not affect the functional derivative. At fixed particle number one instead minimizes the physical energy H+μN, with μ as its Lagrange multiplier. For g>0, μ≤0 the grand-potential minimum over n≥0 is the vacuum n=0. For g<0 the displayed unconstrained functional is unbounded below; the positive-density stable uniform state assumed in the following parts requires repulsion.

Use the nonzero homogeneous number density n0​=μ/g from the previous part, with g,μ>0. Set
ψ​=n0​​ψ​,x=ℓh​x​,ℓh​=2mμ​ℏ​,t=ℏ2μ​t.​
(5)
The length ℓh​ is the conventional healing length in this normalization. In the dimensional Gross–Pitaevskii equation, ℏ2/(2mℓh2​)=μ and gn0​=μ. Substitution and division by μn0​​ therefore give
2i∂t​ψ​=−∇2ψ​+(∣ψ​∣2−1)ψ​,
(6)
or equivalently the requested −2i form. The factor two in the time scale is necessary: using μt/ℏ instead would leave a coefficient −i. The normalized number density is ∣ψ​∣2=n/n0​.

An impenetrable hard wall imposes the Dirichlet boundary condition ψ(0)=0. Choose the bulk complex argument to be zero and write a stationary solution as ψ(y)=n0​​f(y) with f→1 as y→∞. The dimensional Gross–Pitaevskii equation becomes
ℓh2​f′′+(1−f2)f=0,f(0)=0,f(∞)=1.
(7)
Multiplication by f′ and use of the bulk limit yield the first integral
2ℓh2​​(f′)2+2f2​−4f4​=41​,f′=2​ℓh​1−f2​
(8)
for the increasing wall profile. Integrating gives the hard-wall condensate healing profile
f(y)=tanhξy​,ξ=2​ℓh​=mμ​ℏ​,n(y)=gμ​tanh2ξy​.​
(9)
The PDF's expression without the factor μ/g is correct for the normalized number density n/n0​, not for the dimensional number density defined at the start of the paper. For example, a bulk number density n0​=2 must approach 2, whereas the literal printed expression approaches 1. If both number density and coordinates in this part are understood to have already been normalized, the same result reads n=tanh2(y​/2​) and the dimensionless width is 2​. These are two descriptions of the same profile, not different physical healing lengths.

Suppress tildes in the normalized equation and let X=x−vt. A Gross–Pitaevskii solitary wave stationary in these translating coordinates obeys
∇2ψ+(1−∣ψ∣2)ψ−2iv∂X​ψ=0,ψ(X,y)→1 as X2+y2→∞.​
(10)
The boundary fixes the bulk complex argument and describes a localized disturbance with zero net vortex winding number; a vortex pair is possible. No extra plane-wave factor is needed when only the coordinates are changed. A full Galilean transformation of the field would additionally change the bulk flow and its complex argument convention.
Subtract the uniform background from the grand potential. A convenient dimensionless energy and the renormalized momentum of a condensate are
E=21​∫R2​[∣∇ψ∣2+21​(1−∣ψ∣2)2]dXdy,​
(11)
p=2i1​∫R2​[(ψ∗−1)∇ψ−(ψ−1)∇ψ∗]dXdy.​
(12)
The subtraction in p removes the troublesome background complex argument gradient. In particular, for the usual subsonic localized far field ψ−1=O(r−1), ∇ψ=O(r−2), and 1−∣ψ∣2=O(r−2), the energy density is O(r−4) and the momentum integrand is O(r−3), both integrable in two dimensions. Merely writing ψ→1 without sufficient decay is not by itself a convergence proof. The common solitary-wave branch is subsonic: linearizing this normalization gives ω2=k2/2+k4/4 and speed of sound 1/2​.
Integration by parts, with decaying variations and their boundary terms vanishing, gives
δψ∗δE​=−21​∇2ψ+21​(∣ψ∣2−1)ψ,δψ∗δpx​​=−i∂X​ψ.
(13)
Thus the translating equation is precisely δ(E−vpx​)=0. Along a differentiable family ψλ​ with fixed bulk normalization, evaluate this stationarity on ∂λ​ψ to obtain
dλdE​=vdλdpx​​,dpx​dE​=v​
(14)
where dpx​/dλ=0. This energy–momentum slope of a solitary wave does not require differentiating the Lagrange multiplier v inside the field variation. At a turning point the parametrized identity remains true, while E need not be a single-valued function of px​ locally. The chosen factors make iψt​=δE/δψ∗, consistent with the normalized equation; changing the normalization of E alone would change the claimed slope.

For repulsive interactions g>0, the Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate neglects the density-gradient kinetic energy away from boundaries and vortex cores. The stationary Gross–Pitaevskii equation then gives μ=Vext​(r)+gn(r) wherever the condensate is present. At the axis μ=gn0​, hence
n(r)=n0​(1−Rr2​r2​)+​,Rr​=mωr2​2gn0​​​.​
(15)
Here (q)+​=max(q,0) and the support is also restricted to the physical bucket r≤R0​. The next parts' explicit outer cutoff Rr​ assumes Rr​≤R0​; if R0​<Rr​ the wall truncates the parabola and the corresponding integrals must use R0​, not the natural trap radius. A hard-wall boundary layer or the smooth trap-edge healing region is beyond the Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate. For an untruncated radial profile, the total particle number is N=πH0​n0​Rr2​/2.

Take a singly quantized vortex of positive circulation. The superfluid velocity is u=(ℏ/m)∇S, where S is the complex argument of the condensate. Since S=θ, quantized circulation gives uθ​=ℏ/(mr). Insert the radial Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate into the cylindrical volume integral, excluding the core r<a0​:
Ekin​=mπH0​ℏ2n0​​∫a0​Rr​​(1−Rr2​r2​)rdr​.
(16)
Integrating the logarithmic and quadratic terms separately gives
Ekin​=mπH0​ℏ2n0​​[loga0​Rr​​−21​+2Rr2​a02​​].​
(17)
The logarithm is the familiar long-range flow contribution of a quantum vortex; the −1/2 comes from the declining trapped number density. If the bucket truncates the cloud, put R=R0​<Rr​ and the same integration gives Ekin​=πH0​ℏ2n0​[log(R/a0​)−(R2−a02​)/(2Rr2​)]/m, provided a0​<R. A vortex of integer winding number q has uθ​=qℏ/(mr) and multiplies this flow energy by q2. The core radius acts as a cutoff of order the local healing length; its internal gradient and interaction energies are not computed by this shell estimate.

The axial angular momentum per unit volume is mn(r)ruθ​(r)=ℏn(r) for a singly quantized positive quantum vortex. Thus every particle in the prescribed shell contributes ℏ:
Lz​=2πH0​ℏn0​∫a0​Rr​​(1−Rr2​r2​)rdr.
(18)
This yields
Lz​=πH0​ℏn0​[2Rr2​​−a02​+2Rr2​a04​​]=2πH0​ℏn0​Rr2​​(1−Rr2​a02​​)2.​
(19)
This is ℏ times the shell particle number. For a wall-truncated cloud with R=R0​<Rr​, the corresponding result is Lz​=πH0​ℏn0​[R2−a02​−(R4−a04​)/(2Rr2​)]. Reversing the quantized circulation reverses Lz​ without changing the flow kinetic energy. For winding number q, Lz​ is multiplied by q.

In a rotating reference frame at angular frequency Ω, the relevant energy difference is approximately Ekin​−ΩLz​. The vortex-free state has zero circulation and zero corresponding angular momentum. For a0​/Rr​≪1, the preceding shell estimates reduce to
Ekin​≃mπH0​ℏ2n0​​[loga0​Rr​​−21​],Lz​≃2πH0​ℏn0​Rr2​​.
(20)
Therefore the thermodynamic vortex-nucleation frequency for the first positive quantum vortex is
Ωc​≃mRr2​2ℏ​[loga0​Rr​​−21​],Ω>Ωc​ ⇒ Ekin​−ΩLz​<0.​
(21)
The common factors H0​,n0​ cancel explicitly, although the trap radius and core scale still depend on number density. In the strict leading-logarithm limit, Ωc​∼2ℏlog(Rr​/a0​)/(mRr2​). The finite constant −1/2 is the one obtained from the stated Thomas–Fermi shell integral; a resolved vortex core and readjustment of the number density at fixed particle number can change the nonlogarithmic constant. This is an energetic threshold, not a dynamical guarantee of nucleation: a surface barrier can delay entry. Higher positive winding numbers have flow energy proportional to q2 and angular momentum proportional to q, so their analogous threshold is larger in this approximation.

i OurBigBook.com
Let n=∣Ψ∣2. Two independent approximations are required for the reservoir reduction of a polariton condensate. First the exciton reservoir must follow the changing condensate number density rapidly: after its initial transient, its relaxation time [γR​+RR​n]−1 must be small compared with the local density-evolution time. A sufficient modewise condition is that the relevant number density frequencies and growth rates are much smaller than γR​+RR​n; pump variations must be comparably slow. There is no reservoir diffusion in the given model. Adiabatic elimination then gives
R≃γR​+RR​nP(r)​.
(22)
Second, to obtain the particular cubic equation by expansion about the empty condensate, require ε=RR​n/γR​≪1. Then
R=γR​P​(1−γR​RR​​n)+O(γR​P​ε2).
(23)
Keeping the full denominator instead gives a saturable-gain equation, not exactly the requested cubic complex Ginzburg–Landau equation. A near-threshold condensate is one natural regime in which the second condition holds. Rapid reservoir relaxation alone does not justify this number density expansion.
Multiplying the condensate equation by −i and inserting the expanded reservoir separates real gain from the conservative frequency shift:
αg​=21​(γR​RR​P​−γC​),=U0​−γR2​gR​RR​P​,​βs​=2γR2​RR2​P​,=−γR​gR​P​.​​
(24)
These are local functions if the pump is spatially varying. Their signs are important: the reservoir blueshift gives negative s in the printed convention +isΨ, while reservoir depletion subtracts from the effective interaction g. Positive rates and pump give β>0; the effective g need not be positive merely because the original interactions are repulsive.

For a spatially uniform pump and β>0, a nonzero homogeneous solution has constant number density determined by gain saturation. Its complex argument rotates uniformly:
Ψ∞​(t)=n∞​​e−iμt+iθ0​,n∞​=βα​,μ=gn∞​−s.​
(25)
This uniform pumped polariton condensate requires α>0, equivalently
P>Pth​=RR​γC​γR​​.
(26)
In the cubic approximation its number density is
n∞​=RR​γR​​(1−PPth​​).
(27)
It is not the exact number density of the original two-field equations, which would be (P−Pth​)/γC​: the two coincide to first order in distance above threshold. At or below threshold there is no positive nonzero branch of this form; the vacuum remains a solution. This algebra establishes existence, not stability for every possible sign of g.
Choose θ0​=0 and seek a time-independent normalized profile through
Ψ(r,t)=n∞​​e−iμtψ(r).
(28)
Using βn∞​=α and μ+s=gn∞​, its equation becomes
0=α(1−∣ψ∣2)ψ+i[∇2+gn∞​(1−∣ψ∣2)]ψ.
(29)
Division by i therefore yields
[∇2+ξ(1−∣ψ∣2)]ψ=0,ξ=(g−iβ)n∞​=gn∞​−iα.​
(30)
For a localized zero-winding disturbance the bulk condition is ψ→1. Here ξ is a complex coefficient with inverse-length-squared dimensions in the scaled equation, not the real wall-healing length denoted by the same letter in Question 1. The constant far-field phase excludes net vortex winding lemma explains why the next part requires interpreting the vortex boundary differently.

For a straight singly quantized quantum vortex, use cylindrical distance r from the line and write
ψ=f(r)ei[θ+χ(r)],f≥0,k(r)=χ′(r),ξ=ξR​+iξI​.
(31)
There is no dependence along the vortex line. Substitution into the normalized stationary equation gives, before separating real and imaginary parts,
f′′+rf′​−r2f​−fk2+i(2f′k+fk′+rfk​)+ξ(1−f2)f=0.
(32)
Consequently the wave amplitude and phase-gradient equations are
f′′+rf′​−(r21​+k2)f+ξR​(1−f2)fk′+(r1​+2ff′​)k​=0,=−ξI​(1−f2).​​
(33)
The second equation can also be written (rf2k)′=−ξI​rf2(1−f2), which remains convenient at the core.
To specify what is meant by radial velocity, use the condensate number current. The time-dependent equation has kinetic operator −∇2 and therefore number current j=2∣Ψ∣2∇S. Its hydrodynamic superfluid velocity in these units is u=2∇S, so ur​=2k and uθ​=2/r. In that convention the requested pair is
f′′+rf′​−r2f​−4ur2​​f+ξR​(1−f2)fur′​+(r1​+2ff′​)ur​​=0,=−2ξI​(1−f2).​​
(34)
If instead velocity is defined as the complex argument gradient itself, the preceding k pair is the corresponding convention; the distinction matters because the printed kinetic coefficient differs from that of the normalized conservative equation in Question 1.
Regularity of a unit-charge core gives f(r)=ar+O(r3) with a>0. Integrating the current equation from the origin, with no point source or singular radial flux, gives
rf2ur​=−2ξI​∫0r​sf(s)2(1−f(s)2)ds.
(35)
The integral is a2r4/4+O(r6), so the radial flow near a driven vortex core is
ur​(r)=−2ξI​​r+O(r3)=2α​r+O(r3),ur′​(0)=2α​.​
(36)
For the phase-gradient convention, k′(0)=−ξI​/4=α/4. Gain exceeds loss in the depleted core, hence this regular flow is outwards for α>0. The real wave amplitude equation also gives f=ar−ξR​ar3/8+O(r5), independently consistent with the linear core behaviour. A nonzero integration constant in the radial-current identity would create a singular ur​∼r−3 and is excluded.
Finally, the literal boundary ψ→1 from the preceding part cannot apply to a multiplicity-one vortex: its complex argument changes by 2π around a large circle. Even f(r)eiθ with f→1 has different limits along different rays. The usual intended vortex condition concerns the wave amplitude approaching its bulk value, with the winding complex argument retained; it is not a constant complex-field limit. The local equations and core slope derived here do not establish a global stationary vortex satisfying that literal boundary. In a driven system the far-field radial flow and oscillation frequency may also require selection, so no global zero-flow vortex is asserted.
