dtd​H−1=−a˙2a¨​=3a˙24πGaρ​(1+3ω)=2aΩ​(1+3ω).

(7)
For an expanding universe, the same factor 1+3ω determines whether a small departure from flatness grows and whether the comoving Hubble radius grows. Ordinary matter with ω>−1/3 therefore has both signs associated with the conventional flatness problem and horizon problem. Scales whose physical wavelengths now exceed the Hubble radius were even farther outside it, in relative terms, earlier in such an era, rather than being brought inside for causal equilibration. Accelerated expansion with ω<−1/3 reverses both signs.
There is a qualification to the word “always”: an actual horizon problem depends on the complete past history, not just this local sign. In a flat, constant-ω hot Big Bang model with ω>−1/3, a∝(t−tB​)p with p=2/[3(1+ω)]<1, so the comoving particle horizon ∫tB​t​dt′/a(t′) is finite. The conventional causal problem then accompanies the flatness instability. An earlier accelerated era or a different past boundary can change the causal conclusion even when the present-era comoving Hubble radius is growing. Thus the requested correspondence is valid in that usual expanding hot Big Bang setting, not a universal logical equivalence between global horizons and local stability. At ω=−1/3 both local effects are marginal.

Relativistic pressure gives the radiation fluid a sound speed cs2​=1/3. Well inside the sound horizon, k≫H, radiation linear cosmological density perturbations undergo rapid pressure-supported oscillations instead of sustained gravitational growth. Their potential decays. For an ideal radiation fluid with negligible scalar anisotropic stress, the regular Newtonian gauge in cosmology solution during radiation domination illustrates this explicitly:
Φr​(k,τ)=3Φp​u3sinu−ucosu​,u=3​kτ​.
(15)
For u≫1 its amplitude is O(u−2). Thus radiation in cosmology remains essential to the background Friedmann equation, while its rapidly oscillating gravitational forcing can be smoothed out when following the slowly varying cold dark matter density mode. This is the approximation behind the Mészáros equation.
The important limitation is that “subhorizon” alone is not a pointwise theorem that ∣Φr​∣≪∣Φm​∣. Radiation can dominate the instantaneous potential during early radiation domination, and the forcing near horizon entry seeds the later logarithmic matter mode. We neglect that transient and rapidly oscillating forcing in the subsequent coarse-grained growth equation, not radiation's contribution to the expansion rate. Close to horizon entry, or for accurate oscillatory evolution, the coupled radiation and matter perturbation equations must be retained. For the slow matter potential, the metric time derivatives in part (a) are also suppressed relative to its spatial Laplacian by O((H/k)2). The approximation smooths radiation perturbations while retaining radiation in cosmology in the background cosmic expansion.

The Mészáros effect suppresses the growth of modes which enter the horizon during radiation domination. Modes with comoving wavenumber k≫keq​ enter earlier, but gain only a logarithmic growth factor until matter-radiation equality; modes entering during matter domination grow as a after entry. Consequently small-scale amplitudes are reduced relative to an extrapolation of the unsuppressed large-scale cosmological density power spectrum, creating a turnover near the matter-radiation equality scale keq​=Heq​ in units c=1.
More quantitatively, for adiabatic primordial fluctuations with scalar spectral index ns​, the late-time linear spectrum can be written, up to a time-dependent normalization,
Pm​(k)∝kns​T2(k).
(20)
The cold-dark-matter transfer function approaches a constant at k≪keq​, whereas logarithmic small-scale matter transfer gives T(k)∝(keq​/k)2log(k/keq​) at large k. Thus
Pm​(k)∝{kns​,kns​−4log2(k/keq​),​k≪keq​,k≫keq​.​​
(21)
For approximately scale-invariant initial curvature fluctuations, ns​≃1, the large-k scaling is k−3log2k, rather than k. This discussion ignores baryonic acoustic structure and nonlinear clustering. The Mészáros equation solution basis includes both a constant and a logarithmic branch during radiation domination; the physical logarithmic growth is selected by matching the earlier radiation forcing, so not every mathematical solution grows logarithmically.

Take a homogeneous comoving cell of physical volume Vc​=a3V0​ and total entropy S=sVc​. At vanishing chemical potential, the reversible first law of thermodynamics reads
TdS=d(ρVc​)+PdVc​=Vc​dρ+(ρ+P)dVc​.
(22)
For an extensive equilibrium system, the thermodynamic Euler relation is E=TS−PVc​+μN. At μ=0 this gives
s=Tρ+P​.​
(23)
The supplied pressure identity verifies consistency directly: dP=(ρ+P)dT/T implies
d[T(ρ+P)Vc​​]=TVc​dρ+(ρ+P)dVc​​=dS.
(24)
The thermodynamic Euler relation fixes the additive normalization that a differential identity alone would leave undetermined.
For adiabatic cosmological expansion, the cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation gives ρ˙​+3H(ρ+P)=0, while V˙c​=3HVc​. Hence
TS˙=Vc​[ρ˙​+3H(ρ+P)]=0,sa3=constant.​
(25)
This proves cosmological entropy conservation in the stated perfect-fluid approximation. Kinetic equilibrium alone does not guarantee adiabatic evolution: entropy injection, dissipative stresses or out-of-equilibrium reactions would require additional source terms. Those effects are implicitly excluded in this calculation.

When the number-changing reaction is in chemical equilibrium, its chemical balance condition is 2μϕ​=3μϕ​. Therefore zero chemical potential from number-changing reactions gives μϕ​=0​. Elastic scattering maintains kinetic equilibrium but imposes no independent condition on μϕ​. The conclusion requires the number-changing rate to remain large compared with the Hubble parameter; merely listing an allowed reaction does not guarantee this after freeze-out.
Before thermal decoupling in cosmology, the sectors have a common temperature T. For relativistic species at that temperature their entropy densities are
sSM​=452π2​g∗sSM​(T)T3,sϕ​=452π2​g∗sϕ​(T)T3,
(26)
so
ξ(T>Td​)=g∗sϕ​(T)g∗sSM​(T)​.​
(27)
These are the effective number of relativistic degrees of freedom for entropy. The paper's g∗​ agrees with g∗s​ for relativistic components at a common temperature. For one real scalar field, g∗sϕ​=1. This assumes no late inter-sector transfer or entropy injection.

The initial field values are ϕi​/MPl​=122n​, approximately 15.6, 19.1 and 22.1 for the three powers. A simple monomial inflation potential over this super-Planckian field range needs theoretical protection. Generic Planck-suppressed corrections to inflaton potentials, such as cp​ϕp/MPlp−4​, need not be small there; they can change the slope and curvature and spoil slow-roll inflation. Radiative corrections and possible couplings to other particles likewise require control. A symmetry, such as an approximate scalar-field shift symmetry, or a specified ultraviolet completion could supply that protection, but it is not part of the bare monomial model.
A large field value is not by itself a proof that the energy density is Planckian: a sufficiently small λ can keep V≪MPl4​. The issue is control of the effective field theory and stability of the flat potential over its field range, rather than simply comparing the field value with a mass scale.
There is also a global potential issue for n=3: V=λϕ3 continued over all real ϕ is unbounded below and has no stable minimum at zero. Restricting to ϕ>0 does not specify what happens when the field reaches that boundary, so a completion is needed for post-inflationary evolution and reheating. The even powers have a stable minimum but still need interactions that transfer the inflaton energy to a hot bath. These interactions and the resulting reheating history also affect the mapping between a pivot scale and the assumed 60 number of e-folds. Therefore the concise theoretical concerns are control of large-field corrections, a consistent stable completion, and a specified reheating mechanism.
