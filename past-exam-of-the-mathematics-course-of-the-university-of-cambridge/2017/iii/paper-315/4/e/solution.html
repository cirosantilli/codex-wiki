The three parameter groups are local temperature, local pressure, and elemental composition. The elemental composition includes both atmospheric metallicity of a giant planet and atmospheric carbon-to-oxygen ratio; these are independent compositional controls, so a specification by T and P alone is incomplete. Given these inputs, thermochemical equilibrium minimizes Gibbs free energy with element conservation.
Three useful chemical observables in a hydrogen-rich hot Jupiter are:
  • The carbon monoxide-to-methane partition. For CO+3H2​⇌CH4​+H2​O, cooler gas and, at fixed mole fractions, larger pressure favour the side with fewer molecules, whereas hotter gas generally favours CO. Thus relatively hot photospheres commonly show CO near 2.3 and 4.6μm, while cooler conditions favour methane bands near 3.3μm. The exact boundary depends on composition and pressure; carbon monoxide–methane quenching can invalidate an equilibrium diagnosis.
  • Water absorption, for example around 1.4 and 2.7μm, traces the oxygen left after CO forms. In oxygen-rich gas with C/O<1, water is often prominent; carbon-rich gas can have much less water and relatively more methane or hydrogen cyanide. Condensation and high-temperature dissociation are exceptions to a simple elemental bookkeeping argument.
  • Carbon dioxide, with a strong band near 4.3μm, is sensitive to enrichment and oxidation chemistry. In a hydrogen-dominated trace-species regime, CO+H2​O⇌CO2​+H2​ gives xCO2​​∝xCO​xH2​O​/xH2​​ at fixed T, so CO2 can grow roughly quadratically with heavy-element enrichment before the trace approximation fails.
CO/CH4, H2O, and CO2 bands provide complementary temperature and elemental-abundance diagnostics, but their observed strengths also depend on the atmospheric pressure-temperature profile, exoplanet clouds and observing geometry. A spectral feature is not by itself a direct equilibrium abundance.
