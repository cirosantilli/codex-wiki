Use the ratio definition of cutoff from part (a), and write Tn​=tmix(n)​(1/4). In the reversible Markov chain setting of the preceding parts let γn​=1−λ2,n​ and γ∗,n​=1−maxj≥2​∣λj,n​∣. In particular γ∗,n​≤γn​.
If γn​Tn​ did not tend to infinity, there would be a subsequence and B>0 for which γn​Tn​≤B. Along it part (c) implies, for every fixed 0<ε<1/2,
Tn​tmix(n)​(ε)​≥(γ∗,n​Tn​1​−Tn​1​)log2ε1​≥(B1​−Tn​1​)log2ε1​.
(19)
Since Tn​→∞, choose fixed ε so small that log(1/(2ε))>B. The lower limit of this ratio is then greater than one, contradicting cutoff for Markov chains. Thus the Peres product condition holds:
γn​Tn​⟶∞.​
(20)
In fact the argument with γ∗,n​ proves the stronger absolute-gap product condition. Part (d) does not repeat the reversible Markov chain hypothesis; the ordinary real ordering of eigenvalues above uses that hypothesis. If arbitrary chains are intended, define the spectral gap explicitly: the eigenvalue lower bound for total variation mixing works for complex eigenfunctions too, and proves the same assertion for γ∗​ or for 1−maxj≥2​Reλj​, which is at least γ∗​. No real ordering of complex eigenvalues is implicit.
For the requested counterexample take nonlazy simple random walk on the complete graph Kn​, n≥3. Let Π have every row equal to the uniform distribution on a finite set, and let a=−1/(n−1). Then
P=Π+a(I−Π),Pt=Π+at(I−Π),dn​(t)=(1−n1​)(n−1)−t.​
(21)
Thus dn​(0)→1 and dn​(1)=1/n→0: every fixed-level mixing time is eventually one. This is a bounded-time jump satisfying the ratio definition of cutoff. The ordinary spectral gap is n/(n−1) and the absolute one is (n−2)/(n−1), so both products with Tn​=1 tend to one, not infinity. The divergence assumption on Tn​ is therefore essential under this convention.
