Fix z0​∈U. We prove the Riemann mapping theorem in this case by maximizing a normalized derivative. Choose a∈/U. Since U is a simply connected domain and z−a never vanishes, it has a holomorphic square root g. This g is injective, and g(U) is disjoint from −g(U): equality up to sign would first force the original points to coincide. Choose b∈g(U) and r>0 with B(b,r)⊂g(U). The ball B(−b,r) is omitted, so 1/(g+b) is a bounded univalent function. Scaling and composing with an automorphism of the unit disk produces an injective holomorphic function ϕ:U→D with ϕ(z0​)=0 and ϕ′(z0​)>0.
Let F be the family of all such normalized univalent functions. A Cauchy estimate in a small disk about z0​ bounds their derivatives, so M=supϕ∈F​ϕ′(z0​) is finite and positive. Choose a maximizing sequence in this normal family. Montel theorem gives a subsequence converging uniformly on compact subsets to ϕ. Its derivative at z0​ is M>0. The maximum modulus principle puts its image in D, and Hurwitz's theorem implies that a nonconstant limit of injective holomorphic functions is injective. Thus the maximum is attained.
If a1​∈D is omitted, then a1​=0. Put Ta​(w)=(w−a)/(1−aw). A holomorphic square root v of Ta1​​∘ϕ exists, is injective, and takes values in D. Compose v with Tv(z0​)​ and a rotation to normalize it. Writing s=∣a1​∣∈(0,1), the new derivative has magnitude
M2s​(1−s)1−s2​=M2s​1+s​>M,
(1)
a contradiction. Therefore ϕ maps onto D. The Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk now gives
f(z)=i1−ϕ(z)1+ϕ(z)​:U∼​H,Imf(z)=∣1−ϕ(z)∣21−∣ϕ(z)∣2​>0.​

Interpret the printed congruence entrywise in the integer lattice:
a,d∈1+2Z,b,c∈2Z.
(3)
It then defines the usual level-two principal congruence subgroup of SL2​(Z), despite the PDF's ambient SL2​(R). A literal ideal congruence inside R would be vacuous because 2R=R, and would make the asserted conclusion false. The integer-lattice interpretation is essential.
Pass to Γ(2)​=Γ(2)/{±I}, which has exactly the same action. Reduction modulo two maps the modular group onto SL2​(F2​), a group of order six: the reductions of S and T generate it. Its kernel is Γ(2)​, so the index of a subgroup is six. The standard fundamental domain of the modular group has hyperbolic area π/3, and hence the quotient has hyperbolic area 2π.
There are no nonidentity elliptic Möbius transformations in Γ(2)​. An integral matrix representing an elliptic Möbius transformation has trace 0 or ±1. Here the trace is even, excluding ±1; trace zero would give d=−a and 1=−a2−bc≡−1(mod4), impossible. Thus the effective action is a free properly discontinuous group action, and the quotient is a Riemann surface.
A cusp of a modular group is represented by a rational boundary point. Their orbits correspond to
SL2​(F2​)/⟨T⟩,
(4)
which has 6/2=3 elements. They are represented by ∞,0,1, or by the three nonzero parity vectors of a primitive numerator-denominator pair. Each width of a cusp is two. A union of six copies of the standard fundamental domain of the modular group gives a fundamental region for this subgroup. Removing small horocycle neighbourhoods of its cusps leaves a compact core. Adding one point at each cusp of a modular group, using the local parameter eπiz after moving that cusp to infinity, gives a compact Riemann surface X.
For a finite-area hyperbolic surface of genus g with r cusps, the Gauss-Bonnet theorem gives area 2π(2g−2+r). Thus 2π=2π(2g−2+3) and g=0. A compact genus-zero Riemann surface is the Riemann sphere, by the uniformization theorem. A Möbius transformation sends the three added points to 0,1,∞. Restricting it gives
H/Γ(2)≅P1∖{0,1,∞}=C∖{0,1}.​
(5)

Use the Gaussian curvature −1 normalization
ds2=y2dx2+dy2​
(6)
of the Poincaré half-plane model. This is a complete conformal metric, invariant under real Möbius transformations. Its quotient by the free effective principal congruence subgroup is again complete: a geodesic lifts to the complete covering plane and extends there for all time. Transporting the quotient metric through the biholomorphism supplies the required complete conformal metric.
A nonconstant closed geodesic corresponds to a hyperbolic Möbius transformation A of Γ(2), and its hyperbolic translation length is 2arcosh(∣trA∣/2). Indeed its eigenvalues have magnitudes λ,λ−1 with λ>1, and its axis quotient has length 2logλ. For A=(ac​bd​) in Γ(2), bc≡0(mod4) implies ad≡1(mod4). The odd numbers a,d therefore have the same residue modulo four, and
trA=a+d≡2(mod4).
(7)

(10​21​)(12​01​)=(52​21​).
(8)
Consequently
ℓmin​=2arcosh3=2log(3+22​).​

First use the nonzero loci, as required by the standard SL2R action on differentials. A nonzero holomorphic one-form has, away from its zeros, flat coordinates
w=∫ω,ω=dw,
(10)
whose changes of coordinate are translations. A nonzero holomorphic quadratic differential similarly has local flat coordinates w=∫q​, with q=dw2 and changes of coordinate wj​=±wi​+cij​. These are respectively translation surfaces and half-translation surfaces.
Identify a flat coordinate with a vector in R2. For A∈SL2​(R), replace every flat coordinate by W=Aw. Since A preserves orientation and commutes with multiplication by −1, the new changes of coordinate are
Wj​=Wi​+Acij​,orWj​=±Wi​+Acij​.
(11)
They are holomorphic in the new coordinates, and so define a new complex structure. Define ωA​=dW or qA​=dW2 in that structure. The forms glue because translations preserve dW, and the extra signs preserve dW2.
The zeros also extend. A zero of order m of a holomorphic one-form has cone angle 2π(m+1); a zero of order m of a holomorphic quadratic differential has cone angle (m+2)π. The real-linear deformation preserves the corresponding winding multiplicity. Filling the cone in a local coordinate ζ gives W=ζm+1 in the first case, or a local branch of W=ζ(m+2)/2 in the second. Thus the resulting forms are constant multiples of ζmdζ or ζmdζ2 and have the same zero orders. This verifies extension across the missing points, rather than merely producing an atlas on the punctured surface.
An isomorphism preserving the original differential identifies its flat coordinates up to the permitted translations or signs; applying A identifies the deformed atlases too. Hence the construction descends to the corresponding moduli spaces. Applying B after A replaces w by BAw, so
B⋅(A⋅(X,ω))=(BA)⋅(X,ω),B⋅(A⋅(X,q))=(BA)⋅(X,q).​
(12)
The area of a quadratic differential, and the analogous area of a holomorphic one-form, are preserved because detA=1.
For q=ω2, the flat coordinates obtained from ω already give the required half-translation surface atlas for q. The same replacement w↦Aw therefore constructs both deformations, and
A⋅(X,ω2)=(XA​,ωA2​)=s(A⋅(X,ω)).​
(13)
The printed sets include identically zero differentials. They have no flat coordinates, so the customary geometric group action is defined on the nonzero loci. One can obtain a set-theoretic action on the displayed entire sets by declaring A⋅(X,0)=(X,0); the same equivariance identity then holds at zero. This extension is generally not continuous: as tω→0, the deformed underlying surface is the same XA​ for every real t>0, and can differ from X. Thus a claim about the standard continuous geometric group action requires the nonzero convention.

The analogous flat-coordinate construction does not define a full SL2​(R) action on arbitrary cubic differentials. Away from zeros, a nonzero holomorphic cubic differential has flat coordinates
w=∫c1/3,c=dw3.
(14)
Their changes of coordinate are wj​=ζwi​+bij​ with ζ3=1. A branch change is therefore a rotation through 2π/3, not merely a sign. Applying a real-linear map A changes its linear part to ARA−1, where R is that rotation. In general this is not a complex-linear map, so the proposed changes of coordinate are not holomorphic and cannot define the required deformed complex structure.
For example, take A=diag(2,1/2) and R=(cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​) with θ=2π/3. Then
ARA−1=(cosθ41​sinθ​−4sinθcosθ​),
(15)
whose off-diagonal entries fail the condition for a complex-linear map. This dependence on the choice of cube-root coordinate is the obstruction even when considering descent from the locus c=ω3: the three possible roots need not lead to the same deformation of the cubic pair.
The real matrices preserving orientation that normalize the order-three rotations are precisely the matrices of complex-linear maps; intersecting with SL2​(R) leaves SO(2). Indeed a nonreal rotation determines its complex structure, and conjugation to its inverse would reverse that structure's orientation. Thus there is a natural rotation action,
Rθ​⋅(X,c)=(X,e3iθc).​
(16)
The conclusion concerns the geometric group action analogous to that for translation surfaces and half-translation surfaces; it does not rule out artificial group actions unrelated to these atlases.

Label the polygon vertices v0​,…,v2n−1​ cyclically. Translation pairing of opposite sides identifies
vi​∼vi+n+1​,vi+1​∼vi+n​,
(17)
with indices modulo 2n. The vertex classes are consequently the cosets of the subgroup generated by n−1 in Z/(2n), and their number is
V=gcd(2n,n−1)={1,2,​n even,n odd.​
(18)
The quotient is a compact Hausdorff space. An interior point has a disk neighbourhood, a paired-side point has two half-disks joined to a disk, and each vertex class has its incident sectors cyclically joined to a cone, topologically a disk. Thus it is a connected closed surface with an orientation. Its Euler characteristic is V−n+1, since there are n paired edges and one face. Hence
g(Xn​)=2n+1−V​=⌊2n​⌋.​
(19)
The polygon interior and paired-side charts are translation surface charts with ωn​=dz. Each corner angle is (n−1)π/n. For even n, all 2n corners meet, giving cone angle 2π(n−1); for odd n, each of the two classes contains n corners, giving cone angle (n−1)π. Both are integral multiples of 2π. A cone of angle 2π(k+1) has the local uniformizing coordinate ζ with w=ζk+1; the holomorphic one-form is (k+1)ζkdζ. Filling the vertices therefore supplies the Riemann surface structure and the holomorphic one-form, with
n even:n odd:​one vertex class of zero order n−2,two vertex classes, each of zero order (n−3)/2.​​
(20)
An order-zero entry denotes a regular point, not an actual zero: for n=2,3 the surface is a torus and the form is nowhere zero. The zero orders otherwise sum to 2g−2, as a check against the degree of the canonical bundle.
For n=4, the form has one double zero and lies in the stratum of holomorphic one-forms H(2); for n=5, it has two simple zeros and lies in H(1,1). The SL2R action on differentials preserves zero multiplicities, as the local cone argument shows. Therefore
(X4​,ω4​) and (X5​,ω5​) are not in the same SL2​(R) orbit.​
(21)
Their equal genus and area do not distinguish the orbits; their different strata of holomorphic one-forms do.

A generalized conformal metric is locally dsρ​=ρ(z)∣dz∣, where ρ is nonnegative and measurable and transforms as a length density under a holomorphic change of coordinate. Its area is Aρ​=∫X​ρ2dxdy. Use the usual convention of using locally rectifiable paths for a path family Γ, and put Lρ​(Γ)=infγ∈Γ​∫γ​ρ∣dz∣. Then
λ(Γ,X)=ρ: 0<Aρ​<∞sup​Aρ​Lρ​(Γ)2​.​
(22)
This is extremal length. Zeros and isolated singularities of an admissible density are allowed; requiring a smooth strictly positive Riemannian metric would unnecessarily restrict the definition. Line integrals have their extended nonnegative values; if an arbitrary family is supplied, use its members that are locally rectifiable paths. An empty path family has infinite infimal length, whereas a family containing a constant path has extremal length zero.
Both numerator and denominator scale quadratically when ρ is multiplied by a positive constant. The coordinate transformation of the area element makes the quotient unchanged under conformal equivalence.
The normalization relevant later is worth deriving. On the conformal cylinder (R/Z)×(0,M), let Γ contain the loops going once around it. For the horizontal loop at height t, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
(∫01​ρ(x,t)dx)2≤∫01​ρ(x,t)2dx.
(23)
Integrating in t shows Lρ​(Γ)2≤Aρ​/M. The constant density achieves equality, since every winding-one loop has Euclidean length at least one. Hence
λ(Γ,cylinder)=M1​.​
(24)
Here M is the height divided by circumference, the conformal modulus of an annulus; the reciprocal is used for the family joining its boundary components.

Use the closed genus g≥2 version of Teichmüller's uniqueness theorem. Let f0​:X→Y be a Teichmüller map: for a nonzero holomorphic quadratic differential q, normalized by ∫X​∣q∣=1, and 0<k0​<1,
μf0​​=k0​q∣q∣​,K0​=1−k0​1+k0​​.
(29)
The value at a zero of q is irrelevant to the Beltrami coefficient, which is defined almost everywhere. For every quasiconformal map f:X→Y preserving orientation and homotopic to f0​, the conclusion is
K(f)≥K0​,K(f)=K0​ ⟹ f=f0​.​
(30)
The normalized q is also uniquely determined when k0​>0.
We use the following analytic input, stated with its hypotheses. The Reich–Strebel inequality says that if f0​ has the displayed Beltrami coefficient and a competitor f has the same target and homotopy class, then, for μ=μf​,
K0​≤∫X​1−∣μ∣2∣1+μq/∣q∣∣2​∣q∣.
(31)
This is the standard fundamental inequality for an integrable holomorphic quadratic differential; it also holds on a finite-type punctured surface with the homotopy fixing the punctures. Its formulation is given in Gardiner and Hu, §5. We quote this analytic inequality as the lecture result used in the proof.
Put k=∥μ∥∞​. Pointwise, away from the isolated zeros of q,
1−∣μ∣2∣1+μq/∣q∣∣2​≤1−∣μ∣2(1+∣μ∣)2​=1−∣μ∣1+∣μ∣​≤1−k1+k​.
(32)
Integrating against the probability density function ∣q∣ proves K0​≤K(f). If K(f)=K0​, all these inequalities are equalities almost everywhere. The strictly increasing last function forces ∣μ∣=k0​ almost everywhere; equality in the triangle inequality then forces μq/∣q∣=k0​. Therefore
μf​=μf0​​almost everywhere.
(33)
Two quasiconformal maps with the same Beltrami coefficient differ by postcomposition with a biholomorphism, by the local chain rule for the Beltrami equation. Consequently h=f∘f0−1​ is a biholomorphism from Y to itself homotopic to the identity.
For completeness, such a biholomorphism is the identity when g≥2. Apply the uniformization theorem and choose the lift of the homotopy to H starting at the identity. Its endpoint lift h commutes with every deck transformation. It is a real Möbius transformation. The compact quotient has no parabolic Möbius transformations in its deck group, and freeness excludes elliptic Möbius transformations. Two distinct hyperbolic axes exist: a discrete free group preserving just one axis would be cyclic, contradicting the fundamental group of a closed surface of genus at least two. Commutation with two deck transformations represented by hyperbolic Möbius transformations having distinct axes makes it fix their boundary endpoints individually; there are at least three such endpoints. A Möbius transformation fixing three points is the identity. Hence h=id and f=f0​.
If q1​ is another unit-area differential for the same map, then ∣q1​∣/q1​=∣q∣/q, so q1​/q is positive real wherever defined. This meromorphic function is constant by the open mapping theorem, and normalization makes the constant one. Without normalization, positive multiples of q describe the same map. At k0​=0, uniqueness of the map still holds in genus at least two, but there is no distinguished q. In genus one, translations supply nontrivial biholomorphisms homotopic to the identity, so equality determines the map only up to those biholomorphisms; the genus hypothesis cannot be omitted.

For fixed g≥2, Mumford's compactness theorem states that
Mgε​={X∈Mg​:syshyp​(X)≥ε} is compact for every ε>0.​
(34)
Here Mg​ is the unmarked moduli space of Riemann surfaces, with its usual topology, and the hyperbolic systole is the shortest nonconstant closed hyperbolic geodesic, in Gaussian curvature −1. Equivalently, a subset of Mg​ is relatively compact exactly when its hyperbolic systoles have a common positive lower bound.
We use two standard structural results. The Bers pants decomposition theorem provides a constant Bg​ such that every closed genus-g hyperbolic surface has a pants decomposition with all 3g−3 cuff lengths at most Bg​. For a fixed topological pants decomposition, the Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates identify Teichmüller space with
(0,∞)3g−3×R3g−3.
(35)
The length coordinates are ℓi​, and the twist coordinates τi​ are measured in length units: a full Dehn twist changes τi​ by ℓi​. Reconstruction from these coordinates is continuous; locally the marked metrics can be chosen to vary smoothly on a fixed reference surface, and the quotient by the mapping class group is the Hausdorff moduli space of Riemann surfaces.
Take any sequence in Mgε​. The pants decompositions supplied by the Bers pants decomposition theorem have cuff lengths in [ε,Bg​]. There are finitely many topological types of pants decomposition: their dual graphs have 2g−2 vertices and 3g−3 edges, with loops and multiple edges allowed, giving finitely many finite graphs. Choose a subsequence of one type, and choose markings carrying each decomposition to a fixed reference one. Compose these markings with Dehn twists so that 0≤τi​≤ℓi​. The resulting points of Teichmüller space lie in the compact box
[ε,Bg​]3g−3×[0,Bg​]3g−3.
(36)
They therefore have a convergent subsequence inside Teichmüller space; its continuous projection gives a convergent subsequence in the moduli space of Riemann surfaces. If ε>Bg​ the thick set is empty, which is already compact. Equivalently, using all the finitely many reference decompositions gives a finite union of compact projected boxes containing the whole thick set.
Finally the hyperbolic systole is continuous. Nearby marked hyperbolic surfaces admit metric comparisons with bi-Lipschitz distortion tending to one; the length of every loop, and hence the infimum over all essential loops, obeys the same multiplicative comparison. Therefore Mgε​ is closed in that compact union and is compact. A compact subset has a positive minimum hyperbolic systole, proving the converse characterization of relative compactness. This theorem concerns the unmarked quotient: repeated Dehn twists can give an unbounded sequence in Teichmüller space while leaving the underlying surface and its hyperbolic systole unchanged.
