Take z upwards and write ζ=z+Wt, so that the unperturbed interface is ζ=0, with carbon dioxide above and water below. Use the sharp-interface, incompressible flow version of Darcy's law, with constant porosity and permeability of a porous medium, no capillary pressure, and effectively unbounded layers. The distinction between Darcy flux and interface speed matters: the base flux is qz​=−ϕW, since the printed W is the speed of the interface. Thus the base pressure gradients satisfy
pj,z0​=kμj​ϕW​−ρj​g,j=c,w.
(1)
The mass density ordering is stabilizing, whereas injection of the less viscous fluid into the more viscous fluid produces the Saffman–Taylor instability.
For linear stability analysis, displace the interface by η=η​eσt+iαx. Taking the divergence of Darcy's law shows that the perturbation pressure satisfies the Laplace equation. Decay away from the interface gives, for aα​=∣α∣>0,
pc′​=Ae−aα​ζeσt+iαx,pw′​=Beaα​ζeσt+iαx.
(2)
The kinematic boundary condition and continuity of normal Darcy flux give
ϕση​=μc​kaα​A​=−μw​kaα​B​.
(3)
Meanwhile pressure continuity, evaluated on the displaced interface, gives
A−B=−η​(pc,z0​−pw,z0​)=η​[kϕW(μw​−μc​)​−g(ρw​−ρc​)].
(4)
Eliminating A,B yields the buoyancy-modified Darcy fingering dispersion relation:
σ(α)=ϕ(μw​+μc​)∣α∣​[ϕW(μw​−μc​)−kg(ρw​−ρc​)].​
(5)
Every nonzero wavenumber decays when W<Wc​, grows when W>Wc​, and is neutral at equality, where
Wc​=ϕ(μw​−μc​)kg(ρw​−ρc​)​.​
(6)
The zero wavenumber is a uniform displacement and is neutral. Without a short-scale regularization such as surface tension, this model has no fastest-growing finite wavenumber: on the unstable side its growth rate increases without bound as ∣α∣ increases. Finite layer depths would change the dispersion relation; none are specified here.
Taking g=9.81ms−2 and a year of 365.25 days, the data give
ϕWc​=3.27×10−6ms−1≃103.2myr−1,Wc​≃ϕ103.2​myr−1.​
(7)
The numerical paragraph supplies no porosity, so it cannot determine a unique numerical interface speed. If W were instead intended to mean Darcy velocity, the numerical critical value would be 103.2myr−1, and the interface would move at W/ϕ. These two velocity conventions must not be conflated.

Use the usual saline Stefan problem approximation: no bulk flow, salt-free ice with negligible salt transport, constant properties, equal phase densities, and the same thermal conductivity kT​ and thermal diffusivity κ in both phases. Write cp​ for specific heat capacity, Lf​ for latent heat per unit mass, and kT​=ρcp​κ. These thermal symmetries are needed for the arithmetic-mean interface temperature requested in the paper; unequal phase conductivities would give a weighted balance instead.
Set η=x/(2κt​), ξ=x/(2Dt​), ϵ=D/κ​ and a(t)=2λDt​. The heat equation and salt diffusion equation reduce to f′′+2sf′=0. Their similarity solutions, in terms of the complementary error function, are
Ts​(x,t)Tl​(x,t)C(x,t)​=T−∞​+(Ti​−T−∞​)erfc(−ϵλ)erfc(−η)​,=T∞​+(Ti​−T∞​)erfc(ϵλ)erfc(η)​,=C0​+(Ci​−C0​)erfc(λ)erfc(ξ)​,​​x<a(t),x>a(t),x>a(t).​
(8)
These have the required interface values and far-field limits. At each fixed x=0 they recover the initial data as t↓0. The liquidus condition is Ti​=−mCi​.
Salt rejection and the Stefan condition give, with gradients evaluated on the appropriate sides of the interface,
−DCx​(a+,t)=a˙Ci​,ρLf​a˙=kT​[Ts,x​(a−,t)−Tl,x​(a+,t)].
(9)
For example, salt rejection is obtained by differentiating the total salt on a moving liquid interval: the moving lower endpoint removes a˙Ci​, which must be supplied by diffusive transport away from the salt-free solid. Substitution, including the salt-rejection function for a saline Stefan front, gives the complete algebraic system for the diffusion-controlled iceberg growth and ablation:
Ci​[1−π​λeλ2erfc(λ)]Ti​cp​Lf​​ϵλ​=C0​,=−mCi​,=π​e−ϵ2λ2​[erfc(−ϵλ)Ti​−T−∞​​−erfc(ϵλ)T∞​−Ti​​].​​
(10)
The sign of λ distinguishes freezing from melting; neither sign should be excluded in the general similarity solution.
For λ=O(1), ϵ≪1, and fixed Lf​/cp​ and far-field temperatures, the thermal equation has leading right-hand side (2Ti​−T−∞​−T∞​)/π​, while its left-hand side is O(ϵ). Hence
Ti​=2T∞​+T−∞​​+O(ϵ).​
(11)
This is a leading-order balance, not an exact cancellation of latent heat. The resulting Ci​=−Ti​/m and the first algebraic equation determine the leading λ. A physical finite-λ branch requires Ci​>0; if the mean temperature is positive, this salt-diffusion scaling cannot describe the leading solution. Likewise, a latent-to-sensible heat ratio diverging as 1/ϵ changes the leading thermal balance.
Define θ−​=−mC0​−T−∞​ and θ+​=T∞​+mC0​. Put T0​=−mC0​ and Δθ=θ−​−θ+​. Then Ti​=T0​−Δθ/2+O(ϵ), which will determine the freezing and constitutional supercooling conditions below.

Let E(λ)=π​λeλ2erfc(λ). The salt equation in the saline Stefan problem gives Ci​−C0​=E(λ)Ci​. Since the complementary error function is positive on the real axis and Ci​>0, the signs of Ci​−C0​ and λ agree. Thus freezing is equivalent to Ci​>C0​, or Ti​<T0​=−mC0​. Using the leading thermal balance,
Ti​−T0​=2θ+​−θ−​​+O(ϵ),λ>0⟺θ−​>θ+​​
(12)
at leading order away from a vanishing temperature difference. In fact the exact onset is also θ−​=θ+​: at λ=0 the salt balance forces Ci​=C0​, and the Stefan condition then forces T−∞​+T∞​=2T0​. The solution with λ=0 has a stationary interface and balancing conductive heat fluxes, despite nontrivial diffusion of the initial temperature discontinuity. For completeness, the exact freezing criterion follows without relying on that local crossing. By the salt-rejection function for a saline Stefan front, Ti​(λ) decreases continuously from T0​ to −∞ as positive λ increases. If T−∞​<T0​, let λs​ be where Ti​=T−∞​. On [0,λs​], the solid-side thermal term decreases, the liquid-side term increases, and the latent-heat term increases: the factors e−ϵ2λ2/erfc(−ϵλ) and e−ϵ2λ2/erfc(ϵλ) decrease and increase respectively. These monotonicities follow from the positive integral representation of ev2erfcv. The thermal residual starts at (θ−​−θ+​)/π​ and is negative at λs​, so a positive root exists uniquely precisely when θ−​>θ+​. Beyond λs​ both thermal contributions to freezing are negative, so no further root is possible. If T−∞​≥T0​, the same sign argument excludes every positive root.

For freezing, the liquid concentration decreases away from the interface, so the liquidus TL​=−mC increases there. Constitutional supercooling means Tl​<TL​ somewhere ahead of the interface. Since these temperatures agree at the interface, its local onset is
Tl,x​(a+,t)<−mCx​(a+,t).
(13)
For the similarity solution and salt rejection condition, this is exactly
π​erfc(ϵλ)ϵ(T∞​−Ti​)e−ϵ2λ2​<mλCi​.​
(14)
It is also the global criterion for this freezing solution when D<κ and T∞​>Ti​: the ratio TL′​(x)/Tl′​(x) is a positive constant times exp[−x2(1/D−1/κ)/(4t)]. For x≥a≥0 this ratio decreases with x. If the ratio is at most one at the interface, Tl​−TL​ cannot become negative; if it is greater than one there, a supercooled interval appears immediately ahead. Equality marks onset, not a finite supercooled interval.
For fixed positive Δθ and λ=O(1)>0, the left side of the inequality is O(ϵ) and the right side is positive of order one, so freezing is constitutionally supercooled for sufficiently small ϵ. To locate the boundary between the two regimes, resolve the smaller scale Δθ=O(ϵ), λ=O(ϵ). The salt equation and liquidus then give
Ci​−C0​=π​C0​λ+O(λ2),mλCi​=2π​Δθ​+O(ϵ2).
(15)
Here ϵλ=O(ϵ2), so the thermal equation improves to Ti​=(T∞​+T−∞​)/2+O(ϵ2); this justifies the second expansion including latent heat. Also T∞​−Ti​=θ+​+O(ϵ) and the left side of the exact criterion is ϵθ+​/π​+O(ϵ2). Therefore the small-diffusivity constitutional-supercooling threshold is
Δθ>2ϵθ+​+O(ϵ2),θ−​>θ+​(1+2ϵ) to first order in ϵ.​
(16)
This printed condition is an asymptotic onset condition, rather than an exact finite-ϵ inequality. In its O(ϵ2) transition window the complete algebraic system and exact gradient criterion must be used.

Introduce specific heat capacity cp​, latent heat per unit mass Lf​, and ΔT=Tm​−Ts​>0. Write the prescribed convective heat flux as F=K(T−Tm​), where
K=λk(kκμρ0​gαQ​)1/5,k=ρ0​cp​κ.
(17)
There is a dimensional convention to specify: with Q measured in Wkg−1 and dimensionless λ, this printed expression makes K a heat transfer coefficient only if μ is kinematic viscosity. If μ denotes dynamic viscosity, the numerator needs ρ02​gαQ instead. The question says only “viscosity”; use the consistent kinematic interpretation, or equivalently the corrected dynamic formula. The following balances apply to either convention once K has units Wm−2K−1.
For a thin lava lake crust with a linear temperature profile, Fourier's law gives the outward conductive flux kΔT/a. The Stefan condition balances it against upward thermal convection and freezing, while the well-mixed liquid loses the convective flux and gains radiogenic heating. To leading order in a/H the radiogenically heated lava-lake crust model is
ρ0​Lf​dtda​=akΔT​−K(T−Tm​),ρ0​cp​HdtdT​=ρ0​QH−K(T−Tm​).​
(18)
The fixed surface temperature already incorporates radiative cooling, so no extra radiative loss is added to the interior balance. The linear conductive profile is interpreted as a quasi-steady approximation, so sensible-heat storage within the crust is not evolved. Thinness alone does not establish this approximation: rapid thermal adjustment in the crust is also needed, for example a small Stefan number during the initial conduction-driven growth. Replacing the liquid depth by H−a and retaining the moving liquid-volume enthalpy would introduce thin-crust corrections: the requested constant-temperature branch refers to the leading model above. A transient thermal profile in a thick or newly nucleating crust would require the full Stefan problem, rather than this linear-profile closure.
Using the thermal diffusion time H2/κ, define
τ=H2κt​,h=Ha​,θ=ΔTT−Tm​​,B=kKH​,R=cp​κΔTQH2​,S=Lf​cp​ΔT​.
(19)
The three independent dimensionless parameters are the convective-conductive coefficient B, the heating parameter R, and the Stefan number S. For positive B,R,S the evolution equations become
θ′=R−Bθ,h′=S(h1​−Bθ),​
(20)
where prime denotes d/dτ. Temperature has the exact solution
θ(τ)=θ∗​+(θ0​−θ∗​)e−Bτ,θ∗​=R/B.​
(21)
Consequently the unique constant-temperature initial condition is
T(0)=T∗​:=Tm​+Kρ0​QH​.​
(22)
At this temperature, the convective flux equals the column's radiogenic heat production. Starting with no crust, the constant-temperature lava-crust growth law is obtained by separating h′=S(1/h−R):
τ=SR2−Rh−log(1−Rh)​,0≤h<1/R.​
(23)
The crust grows towards h∗​=1/R, or a∗​=kΔT/(ρ0​QH), where conduction carries all the radiogenic input. The logarithm makes reaching this equilibrium take infinite time. For an initial thickness h0​>0, subtract [−Rh0​−log(1−Rh0​)]/(SR2) on the same side of the equilibrium; the corresponding antiderivative uses log∣1−Rh∣ on either branch. The thin-crust description through equilibrium requires R≫1.
For general initial temperature, the full early and intermediate history is specified by the exact temperature above and one scalar ordinary differential equation. A particularly convenient integral formulation for an initially bare surface is
y(τ)=h(τ)2,y′=2S[1−(R+B(θ0​−θ∗​)e−Bτ)y​],y(0)=0.
(24)
This removes the infinite initial derivative of h. The right-hand side points into y>0 at zero, and is decreasing in y when θ0​≥0; two nonnegative solutions therefore cannot separate, which gives uniqueness. Its solution, together with the temperature formula, determines the entire model evolution and can be computed without a phase-boundary search. The early expansion follows by substitution:
θh​=θ0​+(R−Bθ0​)τ+O(τ2),=2Sτ​−32​SBθ0​τ+O(τ3/2).​​
(25)
The dimensional leading crust thickness is a∼2kΔTt/(ρ0​Lf​)​. Thus hotter liquid reduces the first correction to conductive crust growth, whereas colder liquid permits more rapid thickening.
If θ0​>θ∗​, the temperature decreases and the instantaneous stationary lava-crust thickness he​(τ)=1/[Bθ(τ)] increases towards h∗​. Starting from zero, the crust always remains below this rising curve: at a first contact h′=0 but he′​>0, so it cannot cross from below. Hence hot-start crust grows monotonically. It also stays thinner than the constant-temperature solution: the early expansion puts it below, and at a proposed first crossing their derivative difference is −SB(θ0​−θ∗​)e−Bτ<0, preventing crossing from below. If 0≤θ0​<θ∗​, the temperature increases and the instantaneous stationary lava-crust thickness decreases. The crust stays thicker than the constant-temperature solution by scalar comparison, but it may cross the decreasing he​, reach a maximum and subsequently melt back towards h∗​. Such overshoot is possible, not inevitable; the two relevant relaxation rates are B and SR2. The numerical plot uses B=4, R=20, S=0.1, so crust adjustment is faster than temperature relaxation and the cool-start example does overshoot:
The curves use θ0​/θ∗​=0.5,1,1.5 and initially h=0. They solve the complete reduced time-dependent equations, rather than replacing the moving stationary thickness by the actual crust. At late times, set h=h∗​+v, δ=θ0​−θ∗​; linearization gives v′=−SR2v−SBδe−Bτ. For SR2=B,
v=Ce−SR2τ−SR2−BSBδ​e−Bτ+higher-order terms.
(26)
At equal rates the forced term is −SBδτe−Bτ. In particular, slow warming with B<SR2 approaches equilibrium from a thicker crust, explaining the plotted remelting. These long-time expressions describe the linearized response; nonlinear corrections can have their own faster exponential rates. All predictions are restricted to a/H≪1, nonnegative liquid superheat, and the assumed vigorous-convection flux law. If crust growth violates those conditions, the reduced model must be replaced rather than extrapolated to total freezing.

Let z increase upwards, so the bed is at z=−b(x), the surface is at z=h(x,t), and H=h+b. In the shallow-shelf approximation the incompressible flow has nearly depth-uniform horizontal speed u and vertical strain wz​=−ux​. The Newtonian fluid stress tensor, together with the hydrostatic approximation, then gives
σzz​=−ρg(h−z),σxx​=−ρg(h−z)+4μux​.
(27)
The factor four includes both horizontal extension and the pressure correction required by vertical compression. Integrating horizontal force balance over depth, using zero surface shear and the bed traction, gives the membrane-stress derivative ∂x​(4μHux​) and the gravitational driving force −ρgHhx​. The thin subglacial till layer is idealized by Newtonian till lubrication and undergoes Couette flow, with resisting basal stress λμu/l. Hence force balance and mass conservation yield
4μ∂x​(Hux​)−ρgHhx​−λμlu​=0,Ht​+∂x​(Hu)=0.​
(28)
Since b is fixed, Ht​=ht​. No accumulation or ablation is included.
At the grounding line, the first boundary condition is ice-sheet flotation:
ρHG​=ρw​bG​,HG​=ρρw​​αxG​.​
(29)
For an unbuttressed ice shelf, the second is the extensional stress required to balance the difference between the integrated ice and seawater hydrostatic pressures. The ice contribution is ρgHG2​/2, while the water contribution is ρw​gbG2​/2. On using ice-sheet flotation, their difference is ρg′HG2​/2, with g′=g(1−ρ/ρw​)>0. Equivalently, shelf force balance integrates to 4μHux​−ρg′H2/2=constant; absence of a buttressing force sets this constant to zero. Thus the unbuttressed Newtonian grounding-line stress condition is
4μHG​ux,G​=21​ρg′HG2​,ux,G​=8νg′HG​​,ν=μ/ρ.​
(30)
Here ν is kinematic viscosity; it appears in the printed target formula without an explicit definition.
The ratio of longitudinal stress divergence to till drag is of order 4lH/(λL2). The given small-parameter limit therefore yields the friction-dominated bulk relation
u=−λl​νg​Hhx​,ht​=λl​νg​∂x​(H2hx​).​
(31)
The coefficient depends on the basal lubrication, and hx​<0 gives seaward motion. This is a bulk reduction: longitudinal stress has been neglected in the differential equation but its boundary traction remains specified. A complete uniformly valid approximation close to the grounding line could require a membrane-stress boundary layer. In the remainder, use the pointwise friction closure and retain the shelf stress condition as the boundary data, as requested in the paper.
Differentiate the ice-sheet flotation condition along the moving grounding line:
Ht​+Hx​x˙G​=αρρw​​x˙G​,(αρρw​​−Hx​)x˙G​=Ht​=−uHx​−Hux​.
(32)
Now Hx​=hx​+α. Substitution of the friction-dominated speed and the shelf stress boundary condition gives the friction-dominated grounding-line evolution law:
(αρρw​​−Hx​)x˙G​=λl​νg​Hhx​(hx​+α)−8νg′​H2at x=xG​(t).​
(33)
The coefficient of x˙G​ is the spatial derivative of the flotation deficit, up to sign. If it vanishes, the implicit equation remains the correct compatibility condition but division to determine a finite speed is invalid; tangential contact needs separate analysis.
In a steady state, volume flux per unit width is constant: Hu=q0​. The friction relation gives
hx​=−lgH2λνq0​​,Hx​=α−lgH2λνq0​​.
(34)
Differentiating Hu=q0​ and using ux​=g′H/(8ν) at the grounding line gives
Hq0​​Hx​+8νg′H2​=0,8νg′H5​=q0​(lgλνq0​​−αH2).
(35)
Put β=l/λ, H=H/β and Q=νq0​/(gβ3). Substitution and cancellation of gβ5/ν yield the steady friction-dominated grounding-line thickness relation:
8gg′​H5=Q(Q−αH2).​
(36)
For q0​>0 and a bed deepening seaward (α≥0), the polynomial (g′/8g)H5+αQH2−Q2 is strictly increasing for H>0, starts negative and tends to infinity, so it has exactly one positive root. At that root Q>αH2 and Hx​<0, as required by steady outward extension. The relation belongs to the specified friction closure; it is not a flux law for arbitrary till rheology or arbitrary membrane-stress matching.
