Write the exponential dispersion family in the convention
f(z;θ,ϕ)=exp{ϕzθ−b(θ)​+c(z,ϕ)},ϕ>0.
(1)
Assume that the support does not depend on the parameters, that θ is in the interior of the natural parameter space, and that differentiation can pass under the normalizing integral or sum. Differentiating ∫f=1 at fixed dispersion parameter gives
0=∫ϕz−b′(θ)​f(z;θ,ϕ)dz,
(2)
so the expectation is b′(θ). A second differentiation gives
0=E[ϕ2(Z−b′(θ))2​−ϕb′′(θ)​].
(3)
Consequently
EZ=b′(θ),Var(Z)=ϕb′′(θ).​
(4)
Here b is the cumulant function of an exponential family; the same identities follow by differentiating logEetZ=[b(θ+ϕt)−b(θ)]/ϕ. For a known observation weight w, replace ϕ by ϕ/w. These regularity assumptions for differentiation under the integral sign matter: differentiation of a parameter-dependent support can introduce additional boundary terms.

For fixed known r>0, take the natural parameter of an exponential family
θ=logr+λλ​<0,b(θ)=−rlog(1−eθ).
(5)
The negative binomial distribution has log-likelihood
logf(y)=yθ−b(θ)+logΓ(y+r)−logΓ(r)−logΓ(y+1).
(6)
Thus this is a negative binomial exponential family, with dispersion parameter ϕ=1 in the convention of part (a). The carrier contains the Gamma function and depends on the fixed size r, which need not be an integer. Differentiating the cumulant function of an exponential family gives
b′(θ)=1−eθreθ​=λ,b′′(θ)=(1−eθ)2reθ​=λ+rλ2​.
(7)
Therefore
EY=λ,Var(Y)=λ+λ2/r.​
(8)
The size r controls overdispersion relative to a Poisson distribution; it is different from the unit dispersion parameter used by this fixed-size generalized linear model. Another common convention applies to the scaled response Z=Y/r: then b(θ)=−log(1−eθ) and ϕ=1/r. Keeping track of the response scale prevents confusing these two representations. At λ=0 the law is concentrated at zero and is obtained as a boundary limit, rather than by a finite natural parameter of an exponential family.

For EM for zero-inflated negative binomial regression, at iteration k apply Bayes' theorem to each zero in the zero-inflated negative binomial model. The E step computes the conditional expectation of its latent indicator:
ti(k)​=E[Ai​∣yi​]=⎩⎨⎧​π(k)+(1−π(k))(r/(r+λi(k)​))rπ(k)​,0,​yi​=0,yi​>0.​​
(17)
Treating these values as fixed, the M step maximizes the expected complete-data log-likelihood. Its two parameter blocks separate:
π(k+1)=n1​i∑​ti(k)​,β(k+1)=argβmax​i∑​wi(k)​logfNB​(yi​;r,exiT​β),wi(k)​=1−ti(k)​.​
(18)
The second block is a weighted negative binomial regression with fixed size r. Writing ηi​=xiT​β and λi​=eηi​, its parameter-dependent objective is
Qβ​=∑i​wi​{yi​ηi​−(yi​+r)log(r+eηi​)}.
(19)
Its score function and negative Hessian matrix are
U(β)=∑i​wi​r+λi​r(yi​−λi​)​xi​,−∇2Qβ​=∑i​wi​(r+λi​)2rλi​(yi​+r)​xi​xiT​.
(20)
The objective is concave, so a finite maximizer, when it exists, can be found by Newton method or iteratively reweighted least squares. The latter uses working response ηi​+(yi​−λi​)/λi​ and weights wi​rλi​/(r+λi​). This is not an unweighted regression on only the positive counts: zeros retain fractional weight in the count component.
Start with 0<π<1 and finite coefficients, alternate the two steps, and monitor the observed log-likelihood. Exact maximization makes the expectation-maximization algorithm nondecreasing in that likelihood, although the mixture may have multiple stationary points. All-zero data can lead to boundary fits and poor identification; multiple initializations and explicit checks for boundary parameters are useful.

With K≥2 distinct knots κ1​<⋯<κK​, a natural cubic spline is a C2 function on the real line, cubic on each interval between consecutive knots and affine outside the two outer knots. The latter condition gives m′′(κ1​)=m′′(κK​)=0.
There are 4(K−1) coefficients for the interior cubic pieces. Matching values and the first two derivatives at the K−2 interior knots imposes 3(K−2) independent constraints, and the two natural endpoint conditions impose two more. The affine continuations are then determined by endpoint values and slopes. The dimension of a vector space is therefore
4(K−1)−3(K−2)−2=K.​
(29)
One explicit basis uses 1,x and dj​(x)−dK−1​(x) for j=1,…,K−2, where
dj​(x)=κK​−κj​(x−κj​)+3​−(x−κK​)+3​​,u+​=max(u,0).
(30)
The cubic and quadratic terms cancel in each difference above κK​, making it affine there; below κ1​ the truncated powers vanish. The changes in third derivative at individual knots show independence of these basis functions. This count uses the linear independence of the spline constraints and counts the listed outer knots as boundary knots. If a convention instead lists K interior knots and two additional boundaries, the dimension is K+2. With only one listed knot and linearity on both sides, the C2 condition forces a globally affine function, so the exceptional dimension is two.

Choose knots independently of the eventual validation responses, and let b(x)=(b1​(x),…,bK​(x))T be a natural cubic spline basis as in part (a). A regression spline writes m(x)=b(x)Tβ. Define the design matrix Bij​=bj​(Xi​). Under the stated normal linear model, maximum likelihood estimation for β is the same as ordinary least squares:
β​=argminβ​∥Y−Bβ∥2.
(31)
Differentiation gives the normal equations BTBβ​=BTY. If the design has full column rank,
m(x)=b(x)T(BTB)−1BTY.​
(32)
If it does not, a Moore-Penrose inverse gives the fitted values, but not every coefficient or off-sample prediction is identifiable. Merely choosing distinct knots does not guarantee full rank for an arbitrary set of observed predictor values.
More knots allow greater curvature and reduce approximation bias at the cost of larger estimation variance. Too many or poorly located knots can cause unstable fits. Equally spaced knots are simple; empirical predictor quantiles put more flexibility where observations are dense; subject knowledge can concentrate knots near likely changes. Both the number and positions can be compared using cross-validation or an appropriate penalized model-selection criterion. Adaptive knot selection must be included within validation, since treating a data-selected basis as prespecified understates its flexibility. Boundary knots should cover the region where inference is intended, and natural linear tails do not by themselves justify far-out extrapolation.

For λ>0, minimize the smoothing spline objective over the Sobolev space of functions with square-integrable second derivative on the observed interval:
J(m)=∑i​{Yi​−m(Xi​)}2+λ∫Xmin​Xmax​​{m′′(x)}2dx.
(33)
The roughness penalty measures curvature; its null space consists of affine functions. To see why the minimizer is a natural cubic spline, let g be the natural spline interpolating the values of any competitor m at its distinct observation locations, and put h=m−g. Integration by parts twice on each knot interval gives ∫g′′h′′=0: g′′′′=0 on each interval, h vanishes at the knots, g′′ is continuous, and g′′ vanishes at the outer endpoints. Hence
∫(m′′)2=∫(g′′)2+∫(h′′)2≥∫(g′′)2,
(34)
while both functions have the same residual sum of squares. This proves the reduction to a finite-dimensional spline space.
For distinct Xi​ and n≥2, use its n-dimensional basis, write Bij​=bj​(Xi​), and define the positive semidefinite matrix
Ωjk​=∫bj′′​(x)bk′′​(x)dx.
(35)
The penalized least squares problem is ∥Y−Bβ∥2+λβTΩβ, so
mλ​(x)=b(x)T(BTB+λΩ)−1BTY.​
(36)
Use linear continuation outside the observation interval. The fitted-value matrix is Sλ​=B(BTB+λΩ)−1BT; its trace gives the effective degrees of freedom. As λ decreases to zero, the natural spline approaches interpolation, with trace n; as it grows without bound, the fit approaches ordinary least squares on 1,x, with trace two. For λ=0 the criterion alone does not determine values between observations; the natural interpolant is the limiting convention. Repeated observation locations are combined by using distinct knots and their multiplicities as weights, rather than pretending the spline design has n independent interpolation values.

There is a genuine syntax error in the printed command: random=list(...) must be a separate argument, following a comma rather than a plus. The intended call is
model2 <- gamm(npres ~ s(age, bs="cr") + s(wbc, bs="cr"), random=list(doctor=~age))
With the default Gaussian family, this generalized additive mixed model is
Yij​=α+s1​(aij​)+s2​(wij​)+b0j​+b1j​aij​+εij​.​
(38)
Here (b0j​,b1j​)T∼iidN2​(0,D) across doctors, D is an estimated covariance matrix, and the Gaussian errors have variance σ2 and are independent of the random effects. The formula ~age includes a random intercept as well as a random slope; it does not force these two effects to be uncorrelated.
The random intercept allows different prescribing baselines, and the random slope allows doctor-specific linear modifications to the population age curve. Conditional on these effects the observations are independent under the model, whereas two patients of the same doctor have additional covariance (1,aij​)D(1,akj​)T. This accounts for clustered data and avoids treating doctor variation as independent patient-level noise. The population smooth and doctor-specific predictions are different targets. With only four doctors, estimates of the random-effect covariance can be imprecise; the Gaussian support issue for counts also remains. The new model accounts for doctor-specific baselines and age slopes.

Let X have columns 1,t, and let Z have one column per incubator, with entry ti​ in the column for observation i's incubator and zeros elsewhere. Integrating the Gaussian random effects gives
Y∼N(Xβ,V),V=σ2I+τ2ZZT.
(42)
Consequently its marginal log-likelihood is
ℓ(β,σ2,τ2)=−21​{nlog(2π)+logdetV+(Y−Xβ)TV−1(Y−Xβ)}.
(43)
The likelihood-ratio test of H0​:τ2=0 against τ2>0 uses
T=2[β,σ2>0,τ2≥0sup​ℓ−β,σ2>0sup​ℓ(β,σ2,0)].​
(44)
For each covariance, the optimizing coefficients are the generalized least squares estimator (XTV−1X)−1XTV−1Y. Both fits should use the same marginal likelihood convention, for example fitting the alternative with update(fly.model, REML=FALSE) and the null as a Gaussian linear model. The displayed alternative REML criterion alone cannot supply T.
The variance component is constrained to be nonnegative, and zero lies on the boundary. Thus the interior-parameter hypothesis of Wilks theorem fails; the usual χ12​ approximation is inappropriate. A 21​δ0​+21​χ12​ limit sometimes applies to a single variance component under additional asymptotic conditions, but four incubators do not give a persuasive large-number-of-groups approximation.
Use a parametric bootstrap under the null: estimate β,σ2 there; simulate independent Gaussian errors with that fitted variance at exactly the observed times and grouping labels; refit both models by maximum likelihood for each simulated sample; and calculate T∗. The upper-tail proportion, conventionally (1+#{T∗≥T})/(B+1), estimates the p-value. Boundary fits with estimated τ2=0 must remain in the simulation distribution. A restricted-likelihood ratio and a correspondingly calibrated bootstrap are another option when the fixed-effect design is identical, but one must not mix ordinary and restricted likelihoods in a single statistic.

For a weakly stationary process, every observation has the same finite expectation μ. Linearity of expectation, without any assumption of independence, gives
EY=n1​i∑​EYi​=μ.​
(48)
The variance is a sum of all pairwise covariances:
Var(Y)=n21​∑i,j=1n​γ(i−j).
(49)
For a real-valued weakly stationary process, the autocovariance is even. There are n pairs at lag zero and n−h pairs in each direction at positive lag h. Therefore
Var(Y)=nγ(0)​+n22​h=1∑n−1​(n−h)γ(h).​
(50)
Positive autocovariances inflate the uncertainty relative to the independent-observation formula; negative terms may reduce it. An unbiased estimator need not have variance tending to zero: a process consisting of the same random variable at every time is weakly stationary, but averaging cannot reduce its variance.

There is a literal domain error in the printed formula. A semivariogram indexed by a signed displacement must satisfy γ(h)=γ(−h), since reversing the two observations leaves the squared increment unchanged. For positive σ2 or τ2, the displayed positive-lag value is nonzero whereas the displayed negative-lag value is zero. Thus the claim on all of R is false as written.
Interpret the argument as a nonnegative distance, or use the even extension of the Gaussian semivariogram. For σ2,τ2≥0 and ϕ>0, the intended model is
γ(h)=τ21{h=0}​+σ2(1−e−ϕ2h2).
(62)
It admits the covariogram
C(h)=σ2e−ϕ2h2+τ21{h=0}​.​
(63)
The Gaussian kernel is a positive-definite kernel, as can be seen from the Fourier transform of a Gaussian: in one dimension
e−ϕ2h2=∫R​eiωh2ϕπ​e−ω2/(4ϕ2)​dω,
(64)
and the nonnegative spectral density makes every finite covariance quadratic form nonnegative. In higher dimensions use the product Gaussian density. Adding independent Gaussian white noise of variance τ2 adds the diagonal nugget covariance. Thus a zero-mean stationary Gaussian random field exists with this covariance, and C(0)−C(h) gives the corrected semivariogram.
For nonzero structured variance, the nugget effect is the right limit τ2 at zero, the sill of a semivariogram is τ2+σ2, and the range of a semivariogram is infinite if defined as the distance at which the sill is exactly reached. A 95-percent practical range solves 1−e−ϕ2h2=0.95:
h0.95​=log20​/ϕ.​
(65)
The scale 1/ϕ is sometimes called the range parameter; it is neither the exact range nor the 95-percent practical range. If σ2=0, there is no nontrivial structured range.
The fact that a semivariogram does not determine stationarity is a further distinction: a semivariogram specifies increment variation, not a unique process or absolute covariance. Adding an independent random constant adds a constant to C without changing γ. More strongly, subtracting W(0) from a stationary field W(s) preserves its semivariogram but generally makes its variance depend on s. The corrected statement is that this semivariogram admits a second-order stationary model, with the displayed covariance using the usual convention C(h)→0 at large distance; it does not force every compatible process to be stationary.
