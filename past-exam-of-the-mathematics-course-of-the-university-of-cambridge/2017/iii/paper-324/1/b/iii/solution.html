Apply the same positive-exponent quantum Fourier transform to each of the two quantum registers. The amplitude at (c1​,c2​) of the coset state is
MM​e2πik0​c1​/M​∑b=0M−1​e2πib(yc1​+c2​)/M.
(7)
The finite geometric series is M when yc1​+c2​≡0(modM) and zero otherwise. Therefore
c2​≡−yc1​(modM),P(c1​,c2​)=M1​ on this support.​
(8)
There are M allowed pairs, with c1​ following the uniform distribution on a finite set. The factor e2πik0​c1​/M changes the probability amplitudes by phases depending on the output label c1​, but has no effect on their probabilities. Using an inverse rather than forward quantum Fourier transform on both quantum registers gives the same support relation.
A modular inverse of c1​ exists precisely when gcd(c1​,M)=1. In that case the discrete logarithm is
y≡−c2​c1−1​(modM).​
(9)
It cannot generally be determined from every allowed pair. Put d=gcd(c1​,M). The supported linear congruence has d solutions modulo M, and determines only y modulo M/d; (0,0) gives no information when M>1. For instance p=5 and (c1​,c2​)=(2,2) are compatible with both y=1 and y=3. One may verify a candidate by modular exponentiation, checking gy=x. Repeated discrete-logarithm Fourier sampling finds an invertible c1​ with per-trial probability φ(M)/M, which has the same totient lower bound from the Mertens product as before. When p=2, the group is trivial and x=1,y=0 are determined without Fourier samples.
