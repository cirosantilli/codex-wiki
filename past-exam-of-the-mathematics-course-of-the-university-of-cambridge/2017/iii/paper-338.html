Use azimuth measured from north toward west, and positive hour angle westward. These conventions are forced by the signs in the coordinate formulae. In the diagram the observer is at O, the local vertical meets the celestial sphere at the zenith Z, and the north celestial pole is P. The astronomical horizon is perpendicular to OZ; the celestial equator is perpendicular to OP. Their intersections with the celestial meridian give the north horizon point and the upper equatorial meridian point.
The north celestial pole has altitude θ; thus the angle between OP and OZ is 90∘−θ. Project X along its vertical great circle onto the astronomical horizon at H: the arc HX is the altitude γ, and the westward horizon arc from north to H is the azimuth η. Project X along its hour circle onto the celestial equator at Q: the arc QX is the declination δ, and the westward equatorial arc from the upper celestial meridian to Q is the hour angle h. Equivalently the triangle on the celestial sphere PZX has sides PZ=90∘−θ, PX=90∘−δ and ZX=90∘−γ. All five angles refer to arcs on their specified reference circles, not arbitrary angles in the projected drawing.

Let n,w,u be orthonormal unit vectors north, west and upward. A direction of altitude γ and westward azimuth η is
x=cosγcosηn+cosγsinηw+sinγu.
(1)
The north celestial pole and the upper equatorial meridian direction are
p=cosθn+sinθu,e=−sinθn+cosθu.
(2)
In the equatorial coordinate system, the same unit vector is x=sinδp+cosδ(coshe+sinhw). Taking dot products with p,w,e respectively gives
sinδcosδsinhcosδcosh​=sinγsinθ+cosγcosθcosη,=sinηcosγ,=sinγcosθ−cosγsinθcosη.​​
(3)
This is an orthogonal transformation between two bases, so it preserves the length of the direction vector. Recover h from both its sine and cosine to keep the correct quadrant. At the zenith, azimuth itself is undefined; the vector equations remain meaningful by continuity. Using the more usual eastward azimuth changes the sign of the second equation.

Write Tsid​ for one sidereal day in minutes and Ωsid​=2π/Tsid​. An equatorial star at an equatorial site rises along a vertical great circle, passes through the zenith, and descends on the opposite side. Its azimuth changes by π at the crossing. The restricted altitude travel prevents following it continuously by rotating over the zenith.
The maximum azimuth rate is Ωaz​=2πω radians per minute, so the shortest half-turn takes Δt=π/Ωaz​=1/(2ω) minutes. During that lost-track interval the star moves through
ϕfull​=Ωsid​Δt=ωTsid​π​ radians=ωTsid​180∘​.​
(5)
For Tsid​≃1436.07 minutes this is approximately 0.12534∘/ω; using a 24-hour day gives 0.125∘/ω. If the quoted zenith blind spot size means the angular radius of a symmetrically placed crossing gap, it is ϕfull​/2, approximately 0.06267∘/ω.
This derives the ideal crossing-gap size with instantaneous acceleration and an available altitude drive. It does not uniquely define a circular forbidden region for all nearby trajectories. For example, at an equatorial site a star of small nonzero declination δ has maximum azimuth rate Ωsid​∣cotδ∣ at transit: its minimum offset is constrained by ∣tanδ∣≥Ωsid​/Ωaz​. That rate contour and the half-turn crossing gap are different definitions of a zenith blind spot. Actual tracking footprints also depend on acceleration and the reacquisition strategy, as discussed in the telescope designers' tracking discussion.

A star of declination δ=θ crosses the zenith. Its direction rotates around the celestial pole at Ωsid​, but its actual angular velocity on the celestial sphere is Ωsid​cosθ. Consequently the small-angle crossing-gap estimate becomes
ϕfull​≃ωTsid​πcosθ​,ϕradius​≃2ωTsid​πcosθ​.​
(6)
For clarity, the factor cosθ is the radius of the star's daily circle on a unit celestial sphere; it does not modify the sidereal hour angle rate.
The estimate assumes the required slew is nearly 180∘. A more exact ideal symmetric reacquisition calculation is possible. Let the endpoints have hour angles −H,+H and suppose 0<θ<π/2, H small enough that both endpoints are above the astronomical horizon. The horizontal direction components are n=sinθcosθ(1−cosH) and w=±cosθsinH. Their shortest azimuth separation is
Δη(H)=2arctan(sinθcot(H/2)​).
(7)
The minimum ideal gap satisfies 2HΩaz​/Ωsid​=Δη(H); its angular separation is ϕfull​=2arcsin(cosθsinH). Expanding for a fast drive gives the boxed expression. At an equatorial site Δη=π exactly. At a geographic pole the direction with δ=θ is stationary, so the crossing argument is inapplicable and the limit is zero. A full near-zenith rate-limited footprint still requires specifying the trajectory and drive model.

For thin contacting components, optical powers add. At wavelength λ the total optical power is Φ(λ)=ρ1​[n1​(λ)−1]+ρ2​[n2​(λ)−1]. An achromatic lens has ΦF​=ΦC​, hence

Vd​ is the Abbe number, a dimensionless inverse measure of optical dispersion. Large Vd​ means weak dispersion. The Abbe diagram below spans a representative visible-light optical glass range, roughly 1.45≲nd​≲2.0 and 20≲Vd​≲95. The shaded families are schematic, not exact catalogue boundaries; modern compositions overlap and include high-index crown glasses.
For a usual positive objective, choose component 1 as a higher-Vd​ crown glass and component 2 as a lower-Vd​ flint glass. Then f1d​>0, f2d​<0, and ∣f2d​∣/f1d​=V1d​/V2d​>1: the crown glass is converging, the flint glass diverging, and their dispersive powers cancel while leaving positive net power. The Abbe numbers alone do not set their absolute focal lengths; the specified total focal length does. As a representative pair, V1d​=64.17, V2d​=36.37 gives f1d​≃0.4332fd​, f2d​≃−0.7644fd​. Representative index and dispersion data are given in SCHOTT's optical-glass data.

The optical telescope focuses the sky onto an optical slit; the collimator turns the selected light into a beam incident on the reflection diffraction grating; the optical camera images each diffracted direction onto the photodetector. The drawing also shows the monochromatic optical slit image for uniform optical slit illumination in the geometric, slit-limited approximation.
Near the chosen wavelength λ, a small wavelength increment moves the image by qΔλ, where q is the magnitude of the linear spectral dispersion. An optical slit image of physical width p therefore has an apparent spectral width p/q. With one optical slit width as the adopted separation criterion,
Δλ=qp​,R=pλq​.​
(13)
Uniform illumination gives a top-hat monochromatic profile of width p; an unresolved stellar image need not illuminate the optical slit uniformly, so its profile follows the actual illumination convolved with the instrument response. Negligible optical slit diffraction and optical camera optical aberrations do not remove the finite diffraction grating diffraction width. The formula is the slit-limited result when that width is small compared with p, rather than a universal exact line-profile criterion.

The number of spaxels is M=(β/θ)2. To obtain definite scaling exponents, keep the spectral resolving power, wavelength interval, diffraction grating angles and groove spacing, input focal ratio, and photodetector sampling of a spectral resolution element fixed. A fixed photodetector then accommodates a fixed number M0​ of spectra, so N≃M/M0​ (rounded up in an actual instrument). The corresponding etendue per spaxel scales as θ2. Angular ratios are independent of whether both angles are in arcseconds; optical invariant equations use radians.
Write b for the collimated beam diameter. The diffraction grating result RθD=W(sinα+sinβ) gives b∝W∝θ. A fixed collimator focal ratio gives fcoll​∝b∝θ. With a fixed image width p in photodetector detector pixels, the invariant θD=pAcam​/fcam​ and Acam​∝θ instead imply fcam​ is constant: the optical camera focal ratio increases as 1/θ.
In a simple on-axis beam-envelope model, each collimator and collimated disperser space has area proportional to b2 and length proportional to b, whereas the optical camera cone has area proportional to b2 and fixed length. Hence
Vcoll​∝θ3,Vgrat​∝θ3,Vcam​∝θ2,Vtotal​≃M(aθ3+b0​θ2).​
(20)
The constants include 1/M0​ and fixed design parameters. At fixed field size, the combined collimator and diffraction grating volumes scale as Mθ3∝θ∝M−1/2, while the combined optical camera beam-cone volume scales as Mθ2∝1.
These are integral-field spectrograph volume scaling laws for the shrinking beam, not a claim that the whole apparatus can shrink without a floor. A photodetector of fixed transverse size d0​ needs space for its field: an optical camera envelope interpolating between optical pupil width b and photodetector width d0​ has volume proportional to fcam​(b2+bd0​+d02​), rather than just fcam​b2. Replication then adds terms scaling as Mθ and M, which can grow as the spaxels shrink. Other field clearances and mechanical margins also change the asymptote. Without the fixed spectral/design assumptions above, the information in the question does not determine unique physical-volume exponents. The role of fixed photodetector size and beam spread is discussed in Allington-Smith's instrument scaling model.

The fundamental angular floor is the diffraction limit of a telescope, of order λ/D. The ideal diffraction grating has Rg​=mW/d, whereas its slit-limited resolving power has RθD=mWλ/d. Requiring R≤Rg​ gives
θD≳λ.​
(21)
The coefficient depends on the adopted line-resolution and aperture conventions. Below this scale, a narrower spaxel oversamples the same spatial mode rather than creating a new independently resolved element. Equivalently a spatial optical mode occupies etendue of order λ2.
There is also an engineering floor before arbitrary shrinkage: the optical camera focal length, photodetector size and support clearances do not shrink, while the number of photodetectors increases as θ−2. Thus the shrinking-beam model's constant combined optical camera volume is eventually overtaken by fixed per-camera overheads. For fixed brightness, smaller spaxels receive fewer photons, so read noise and photon shot noise can constrain useful sampling before the formal optical floor. Diffraction limits independent spatial information; fixed photodetector and mechanical dimensions limit practical volume reduction.

Let β0​ be the direction of the optical camera optical axis. The grating equation on that axis is
mλm​=d(sinα+sinβ0​)=K.
(22)
The central wavelengths in adjacent diffraction orders are therefore λm​=K/m and λm+1​=K/(m+1), with separation
Scentres​=λm​−λm+1​=m(m+1)K​,K=d(sinα+sinβ0​).​
(23)
K is the fixed order-times-wavelength product directed onto the optical camera axis, or the optical path difference between adjacent grooves in that direction. In a Littrow configuration, K=2dsinα. This adjacent-central-wavelength spacing is a conventional free spectral range of an echelle grating; at high order it is approximately λm​/m.
There is a qualification in the wording of the printed definition. The exact interval for which order m lies closest to the y axis is not, in general, identical to the spacing of two adjacent order centres. For a concrete counterexample take β0​=0, 0<α<π/2, and a photodetector mapping x=fcam​tanβ. Equality of the distances from the axis for orders m,m+1 means βm​=−βm+1​, so their common boundary is λ=K/(m+1/2). The other boundary with order m−1 is K/(m−1/2). Consequently the literal closest-order interval has width
Snearest​=m−1/2K​−m+1/2K​=m2−1/4K​,​
(24)
for m≥2 and accessible non-grazing orders. This differs from the printed K/[m(m+1)]. For a nonzero β0​, the exact boundary is determined by βm​+βm+1​=2β0​ and need not even have the simple half-order form. The two definitions agree to leading order K/m2 when m is large. Thus the printed expression is correct as an adjacent-centre convention or a high-order coverage estimate, rather than an exact consequence of the literal nearest-axis definition. Gap-free coverage should be checked using the actual photodetector mapping and neighbouring order endpoints.

The optical pupil mapped onto the deformable mirror has actuator pitch approximately s=D/N. A sinusoidal wavefront error must have at least two actuator intervals per period to avoid aliasing, by the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem. Thus
Λmin​≃2s=N2D​.​
(25)
If the N actuator centres include both endpoints of the optical pupil diameter, the literal pitch is D/(N−1) and the corresponding expression is 2D/(N−1); the paper uses the customary large-N pitch convention. At the exact Nyquist boundary one quadrature can be poorly sampled, and real influence functions and actuator coupling reduce useful correction near the cutoff. The result is an ideal sampling bound, not a guarantee of arbitrary correction at its endpoint.

Write spatial frequency in cycles across the optical pupil as u=D/Λ. The previous result gives a maximum axial spatial frequency N/2. If one counts one representative per positive-quadrant integer lattice point inside an isotropic disk ux2​+uy2​≤(N/2)2, the continuum area estimate is
Mquadrant​≃41​π(2N​)2=16πN2​.​
(26)
This is the geometric counting convention producing the printed formula. Lattice boundaries, the zero-frequency piston and axis points give lower-order corrections, so the formula is not an exact integer count.
The mode definition in the PDF is D=jΛ/2, hence u=j/2 and jmax​=N. It does not itself specify a two-dimensional lattice, its orientation, its independent wave phase components or a circular rather than square spatial frequency cutoff. Full-cycle integer frequencies correspond only to the even values of j in that definition. A general real wavefront error also needs both sine and cosine quadratures, and distinct oblique orientations cannot all be identified with a single positive quadrant: the Fourier transform of a real wavefront has conjugate coefficients at u and −u, rather than identifying every independent sign change of a component.
For comparison, a circular spatial frequency disk has approximately πN2/4 signed full-cycle lattice points, or πN2/8 conjugate pairs; two real wave phase components per pair restore approximately πN2/4 real degrees of freedom. A square spatial frequency support gives a different count. The circular physical optical pupil similarly has only about πN2/4 illuminated actuators. The printed M is a quadrant area estimate under an extra counting convention; it is not a uniquely determined count of all independently correctable wavefront modes from the stated one-dimensional mode condition.

The speckle contrast of a sinusoidal wavefront error gives a peak modal wave amplitude h0​=(λ/π)C​. If all M residual modes have that wave amplitude, their coefficient norm is Hcoeff​=M​h0​. Substituting the printed quadrant count gives the intended expression
Hcoeff​=4π​NλC​​.​
(27)
There is a normalization issue if this quantity is called physical spatial root mean square wavefront error. For h(r)=∑k​h0k​cos(2πuk​⋅r/D+ψk​), the actual pupil-averaged value is
hrms2​=∑k,l​h0k​h0l​⟨sk​sl​⟩pupil​,sk​=cos(2πuk​⋅r/D+ψk​).
(28)
On an orthogonal full-period basis, ⟨sk2​⟩=1/2 and cross terms vanish. Alternatively, independent uniform random wave phases give this result after wave phase averaging, even on a circular optical pupil. With the same M and equal contrasts the physical RMS is then
Eψ​hrms2​​=2M​​h0​=42π​NλC​​.​
(29)
A single full-period cosine already demonstrates the issue: its RMS is h0​/2​, not h0​. On the actual circular optical pupil, any nonconstant unit-peak cosine also has mean square strictly less than one; averaging over its wave phase gives exactly 1/2. On a finite circular optical pupil, phase-dependent mode overlaps can additionally matter. If a constant piston is removed before computing RMS, replace the mode Gram matrix by its centred optical pupil covariance. The printed value can be recovered as a modal coefficient norm, or by counting 2M orthogonal peak-amplitude quadratures in the physical RMS; neither convention is specified by the quoted mode definition. This is the distinction between modal amplitude norm versus wavefront RMS, not a correction silently made to the PDF. Unequal residual contrasts require Eψ​hrms2​=λ2∑k​Ck​/(2π2), so one speckle's C alone cannot determine the total RMS. The weak-aberration approximation also requires the total wave phase error to stay small, not merely each coefficient separately.

An optical pupil ripple of spatial period Λ diffracts starlight into two sidebands displaced by θ≃λ/Λ along the ripple's spatial frequency vector. Substituting the axial Nyquist period gives the deformable-mirror control radius
θmax​=2DNλ​.​
(30)
The square actuator lattice has component cutoffs ∣ux​∣,∣uy​∣≤N/2, hence an ideal square corrected region with this half-width in each photodetector direction. Its diagonal reaches 2​ times the axial radius; an isotropic conservative restriction is the inscribed disk. Using λ/D as the scale of a spatial resolution element, the square is about N elements on a side and N2 in area; the disk has about πN2/4 elements. These are angular area counts, not the quadrant coefficient count in part (ii).
A circular illuminated optical pupil has fewer active actuator degrees of freedom than the complete square array, and influence functions alter the usable boundary. Moreover one real wave phase deformable mirror produces conjugately related corrections at opposite speckles: arbitrary complex-field correction of both independent wave amplitude and wave phase errors generally needs additional control, or a restricted half-plane. The square therefore describes ideal spatial-frequency access, rather than a guarantee that every intensity element inside it can be independently set to zero. The Fourier-domain square control region is derived in Bordé and Traub's speckle-nulling analysis.

Use the ideal detection convention of one collected Electron per detected photon; otherwise replace photon totals by detected Electron totals using the quantum efficiency. Let A be the summed measurement on the star patch and B′ the independent background-only patch. After additive offsets are removed, their means are Q+B and B. Independent Poisson distribution counts have variance equal to their mean, and a single read of n detector pixels contributes nr2 to each patch's variance. Therefore
E(A−B′)=Q,Var(A−B′)=Q+2B+2nr2,Z=Q+2B+2nr2​Q​.​
(31)
The two factors of two arise from the independently measured background and the second patch's read noise, not from doubling the stellar signal. The formula assumes equal background means, independent detector pixels and patch measurements, one read per patch, and negligible dark current or other noise. Several exposure reads or correlated detector pixels require their own variance and covariance terms.

If spatial illumination is stable between the two sums, it acts just like the sensitivity factor: the mean ratio remains one but each detector pixel has a different shot-noise variance. A whole-image standard deviation is disproportionately influenced by faint detector pixels, so substituting a global arithmetic mean signal generally underestimates detector conversion gain. If the illumination pattern differs between the sums, their noise-free ratio is not constant; its structure adds apparent variance and causes a further systematic error.
Use locally uniform signal bins or subregions, remove offsets, mask defective or saturated detector pixels, and propagate the local shot and read variances. In the stable-pattern case a useful alternative to pooling raw ratios is the normalized difference
Ui​=Ai​+Bi​​Ai​−Bi​​.
(38)
At high signal and negligible read noise, treating the denominator at its mean gives Var(Ui​)≃(2Ni​/g)/(2Ni​)=1/g, independent of the illumination. Thus a variance fit or g≃1/Var(U) can use a spatially varying flat without the arithmetic/harmonic mismatch. Measured noisy denominators cause higher-order corrections, so independent or smoothed signal estimates can be preferable. Fit and subtract a genuine changing illumination pattern before noise analysis, using sufficient smoothing not to fit away the random noise. Arbitrary flat-field normalization without propagating its noise does not by itself recover the original gain.
