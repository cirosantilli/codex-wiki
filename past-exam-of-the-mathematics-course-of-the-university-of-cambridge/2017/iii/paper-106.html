The Hahn-Banach theorem for bounded linear functionals says that a bounded linear functional on any linear subspace of a normed vector space extends to the whole space with its norm unchanged. No closedness or completeness of the subspace is required. Question 1 uses real-valued ℓ∞​(Γ), so its operator assertions are read over the real scalar field. The analogous complex statements use complex-valued indexed functions.
For x=0, define g(tx)=t∥x∥ on its one-dimensional span. This is a norm-one functional, so Hahn-Banach theorem supplies an extension f∈X∗ with
∥f∥=1,f(x)=∥x∥.​
(1)
The canonical embedding into the bidual is Jx(f)=f(x). It is linear and ∥Jx∥≤∥x∥. The bounded linear functional just constructed gives the reverse inequality for nonzero x, and the zero case is immediate. Thus ∥Jx∥=∥x∥​ and J is injective.
For the coordinate functional representation of an operator into bounded indexed functions, let eγ​ evaluate a coordinate and put fγ​=eγ​T. If T is bounded and linear, then fγ​∈X∗ and ∥fγ​∥≤∥T∥. Conversely, if M=supγ​∥fγ​∥<∞, the formula (Tx)(γ)=fγ​(x) defines a bounded scalar function for each x, is linear, and satisfies ∥Tx∥∞​≤M∥x∥. Combining the two estimates gives
T∈B(X,ℓ∞​(Γ))⟺γsup​∥fγ​∥<∞,∥T∥=γsup​∥fγ​∥.​
(2)
For an empty index set both spaces/families have norm bound zero; the supremum of the empty nonnegative family is taken as zero.
Choose Γ=BX∗​ and Tx=(f(x))f∈BX∗​​. The Hahn-Banach theorem makes ∥Tx∥∞​=∥x∥, so this is a linear isometric embedding into bounded scalar functions on an index set.
If X is nonzero and separable, choose a dense sequence un​ in its unit sphere and supporting functionals fn​ with ∥fn​∥=fn​(un​)=1. For a unit vector u and ϵ>0, some un​ satisfies ∥u−un​∥<ϵ, whence ∣fn​(u)∣≥1−ϵ. Thus (fn​) is a countable norming family and x↦(fn​(x)) is an isometry into the l-infinity sequence space. Completeness of X is not needed.
If instead X=E∗ for a separable normed vector space E, choose a dense sequence vn​ in BE​. The evaluations fn​(x)=x(vn​) lie in BX∗​ and continuity of x∈E∗ gives supn​∣x(vn​)∣=∥x∥. This again gives Γ=N, even when E∗ is not separable. If X={0}, use zero coordinates throughout.
To prove 1-injectivity, take T:Y→ℓ∞​(Γ) on a subspace of Z. Extend every coordinate fγ​∈Y∗ to f​γ​∈Z∗ by Hahn-Banach theorem, keeping its norm. Their uniform bound defines Tz=(f​γ​(z)), and the coordinate norm identity yields
T∣Y​=T,∥T∥=∥T∥.​
(3)
Selection of these extensions uses the usual choice convention for arbitrary index sets. This verifies the lambda-injective normed space definition with λ=1.
For the final retraction characterization of lambda-injectivity, suppose first that X is lambda-injective and i:X→Z is a linear isometry. The inverse i(X)→X has norm one when X=0; extend it to P:Z→X with ∥P∥≤λ. Then Pi=IX​, and iP is a bounded projection onto i(X). For the zero space take P=0.
Conversely, suppose every linear isometry out of X has such a left inverse. Fix an isometry j:X→ℓ∞​(Γ) as above and a left inverse P with ∥P∥≤λ. For any T:Y→X, extend jT to S:Z→ℓ∞​(Γ) by 1-injectivity. Then T=PS extends T, and ∥T∥≤λ∥S∥=λ∥T∥. Therefore X is lambda-injective exactly when every isometric embedding i:X→Z admits a bounded map P:Z→X satisfying

The weak topology σ(X,X∗) is the coarsest topology making every bounded linear functional continuous. A neighbourhood basis at x consists of sets {y:∣fj​(y−x)∣<ϵ, 1≤j≤m} for finite families in X∗.
Mazur theorem says that for any convex subset C of a real or complex normed vector space,
Cw=C∥⋅∥.​
(5)
The norm closure is convex. If x is outside it, the Hahn-Banach separation theorem gives a bounded linear functional f and a real a with Ref(x)>a≥supy∈C​Ref(y). In the real case omit the real part. Thus x has a weak neighbourhood missing C, proving that the weak closure lies in the norm closure. The other inclusion follows because the weak topology is weaker than the norm topology. In the complex case real separation is converted to a complex functional by f(z)=h(z)−ih(iz).
The sequential formulation, Mazur lemma, follows as well. If xn​⇀x, then x lies in the weak closure of each tail and hence in the norm closure of its convex hull. Select a finite convex combination of the nth tail at norm distance less than 1/n from x.
A weakly bounded set D satisfies supx∈D​∣f(x)∣<∞ for every f∈X∗. Regard J(D) as a pointwise bounded family of functionals on X∗. The completeness of the dual space holds even if X is incomplete: a norm-Cauchy sequence of functionals has a pointwise bounded linear limit and then converges uniformly on the unit ball. The Uniform boundedness principle therefore gives supx∈D​∥Jx∥<∞, and ∥Jx∥=∥x∥ proves norm boundedness.
One can see the precise Baire argument here. The closed sets Fn​={f∈X∗:supx∈D​∣f(x)∣≤n} cover the Banach space X∗. The Baire category theorem makes some Fn​ contain a ball f0​+rBX∗​, after shrinking the radius. For ∥g∥≤r, both f0​ and f0​+g lie in Fn​, so supD​∣g(x)∣≤2n. Scaling and the dual norm formula yield supD​∥x∥≤2n/r. A weakly compact set is weakly bounded because each functional has compact, hence bounded, image. Consequently it is norm bounded.
For a Banach space, let J:X→X∗∗ be its canonical isometry. We use two explicitly stated weak-star facts: Banach-Alaoglu theorem makes a dual unit ball weak-star compact, and Goldstine theorem makes J(BX​) weak-star dense in BX∗∗​. The weak topology on X is carried by J to σ(X∗∗,X∗) on its image, since their coordinates are the same evaluations f(x).
If X is a reflexive Banach space, J(BX​)=BX∗∗​ and Banach-Alaoglu proves weak compactness. Conversely, if BX​ is weakly compact, its image is weak-star compact and hence closed in the Hausdorff space X∗∗. Goldstine density then forces J(BX​)=BX∗∗​, which implies JX=X∗∗. This proves the weak compactness characterization of reflexivity:
X is reflexive⟺BX​ is weakly compact.​
(6)
Finally let K⊂X be weakly compact and let (fn​)⊂BX∗​ separate points. If K is empty there is nothing to prove; otherwise define
d(x,y)=n=1∑∞​2−n1+∣fn​(x−y)∣∣fn​(x−y)∣​.​
(7)
The summands are bounded by 2−n and separation makes d(x,y)=0 only for x=y. The coordinate maps are weakly continuous, so the series, being uniformly convergent, makes the identity from weak K to metric K continuous. A continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism. Thus d induces precisely the weak topology on K, the countable separating family metrizes a weakly compact set result. No norm-density of the separating family in X∗ is claimed or required.

The weak-star topology σ(X∗,X) is the coarsest topology making f↦f(x) continuous for every x∈X. Its basic neighbourhoods of zero impose finitely many inequalities ∣f(xj​)∣<ϵ.
Suppose first that zero had a countable neighbourhood base (Un​) in the entire dual. Choose a basic neighbourhood Vn​⊂Un​ controlled by a finite set Fn​⊂X, and let En​=span(F1​∪⋯∪Fn​). Given x∈X, the neighbourhood Wx​={f:∣f(x)∣<1} contains some Un​, hence Vn​. If x∈/spanFn​, the Hahn-Banach theorem supplies a bounded linear functional h which vanishes on Fn​ but has h(x)=0. Indeed, the finite-dimensional span is closed, and the functional taking e+tx to t on its sum with Fx is bounded because dist(x,spanFn​)>0. Every scalar multiple of h lies in Vn​, contradicting Vn​⊂Wx​. Thus x∈En​, and X=⋃n​En​ has countable Hamel dimension.
If X is infinite-dimensional, every finite-dimensional En​ is closed and has empty interior. Completeness rules out their union. For clarity, the Baire category theorem needed here has a direct nested-ball proof: inside a starting open ball choose a closed ball missing E1​, then inside its interior choose a closed ball missing E2​, and continue with positive radii tending to zero. The centres are Cauchy; their limit belongs to every ball and to none of the En​, a contradiction. A metric topology would have a countable ball base at zero. Hence
σ(X∗,X) is not metrizable on X∗.​
(11)
This is the weak-star topology on an entire infinite-dimensional Banach dual is not metrizable result. It does not contradict weak-star metrizability of the dual ball for a separable predual: that assertion concerns a bounded subset.
The Banach-Alaoglu theorem states that BX∗​ is compact for σ(X∗,X) for every normed vector space X, whether complete or not. Embed it in the product ∏x∈X​{a∈F:∣a∣≤∥x∥} by its evaluations. Each factor is compact, so Tychonoff theorem makes the product compact Hausdorff. The equations expressing additivity and scalar homogeneity define a closed subset of the product. Its elements are exactly the bounded linear functionals of norm at most one, since the coordinate bounds give ∣f(x)∣≤∥x∥. The induced product topology is precisely weak-star, proving the theorem.
The theorem printed as “Goldstein” is the standard Goldstine theorem: J(BX​) is weak-star dense in BX∗∗​ for the canonical embedding into the bidual. To prove it, fix Φ∈BX∗∗​ and f1​,…,fm​∈X∗. The set S={(f1​(x),…,fm​(x)):x∈BX​} is convex. If b=(Φ(f1​),…,Φ(fm​)) were outside its closure, finite-dimensional Hahn-Banach separation theorem would supply coefficients aj​ such that, for f=∑j​aj​fj​,
ReΦ(f)>supx∈BX​​Ref(x)=∥f∥.
(12)
The real case omits real parts; in the complex case every real-linear separating functional on Cm has the displayed form. The supremum equals the dual norm by multiplying vectors by signs or unimodular scalars. The strict inequality contradicts ∥Φ∥≤1. Thus b∈S, which is exactly approximation on every prescribed finite family of weak-star coordinates.
Now take K=BX∗​ with its compact Hausdorff weak-star topology. For each x∈X, the evaluation x:f↦f(x) is continuous on K, and Hahn-Banach theorem gives ∥x∥∞​=∥x∥. Therefore
X⟶C(K),x⟼x​
(13)
is the isometric evaluation embedding into continuous functions on a compact dual ball, over the same scalar field as X.
For the final sequential assertions, choose a norm-dense sequence (fj​) in the separable dual X∗ and suppose ∥xn​∥≤M. Each bounded scalar sequence fj​(xn​) has a convergent subsequence: divide a bounding interval or square into finitely many smaller closed pieces, repeatedly retain one containing infinitely many terms, and let the diameters tend to zero. Successive extraction for j=1,2,…, followed by a diagonal subsequence (yn​), makes every fj​(yn​) converge.
If M=0 the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise, for any f∈X∗ and ϵ>0, choose j with ∥f−fj​∥<ϵ/(4M). For sufficiently large n,m,
∣f(yn​)−f(ym​)∣≤2M∥f−fj​∥+∣fj​(yn​)−fj​(ym​)∣<ϵ.
(14)
Thus φ(f)=limn​f(yn​) exists for every f. Taking limits proves linearity, and ∣φ(f)∣≤M∥f∥ proves
φ∈X∗∗,∥φ∥≤M.​
(15)
This diagonal argument includes the continuity estimate rather than appealing to a sequential compactness theorem.
If X is not reflexive, choose Φ∈BX∗∗​∖JX, scaling a bidual element outside JX if necessary. The Goldstine theorem proved above supplies xn​∈BX​ with ∣fj​(xn​)−Φ(fj​)∣<1/n for j≤n. Norm density and the bound ∥Jxn​−Φ∥≤2 extend this convergence to every f∈X∗. Hence Jxn​→Φ weak-star. Every subsequence has the same coordinate limits, so a weakly convergent subsequence with limit x∈X would give Jx=Φ, a contradiction. This sequential Goldstine approximation for a separable dual proves
∥xn​∥≤1,(xn​) has no weakly convergent subsequence in X.​
(16)
The construction uses the Goldstine proof already given, not an unproved weak sequential compactness result.

A character of an algebra is a nonzero complex-linear multiplicative map φ:A→C. The character space of an algebra ΦA​ carries the Gelfand topology, namely pointwise convergence on A, equivalently the subspace weak-star topology in A∗.
For a unital complex Banach algebra, φ(1)=1: multiplicativity and nonzeroness force this. If a−φ(a)1 were invertible, applying φ to its product with its inverse would give 0=1. Thus φ(a)∈σA​(a). The Neumann series shows σA​(a)⊂{∣z∣≤∥a∥}, so ∣φ(a)∣≤∥a∥ and the character of an algebra is automatically bounded. The same conclusion for a nonunital algebra follows by extending φ to its unitization of an algebra via φ​(a,t)=φ(a)+t and using the standard norm ∥(a,t)∥=∥a∥+∣t∣.
In the algebra R(K) let u(z)=z and fix φ∈ΦR(K)​. Write λ=φ(u). If λ∈/K, the rational function (u−λ)−1 belongs to R(K), which is impossible because a character of an algebra cannot vanish on an invertible element. Thus λ∈K. For a rational function r=p/q without poles in K, choose a reduced denominator having no zeros in K. Multiplicativity gives φ(r)=p(λ)/q(λ)=r(λ). Density of these rational functions and boundedness of φ then yield φ(h)=h(λ) for every h∈R(K).
Conversely, evaluation at each λ∈K is a character of an algebra. The map λ↦δλ​ is continuous in the Gelfand topology because every h∈R(K) is continuous; its inverse is φ↦φ(u), also continuous. Hence the character space of R(K) is
ΦR(K)​≅K,φ=δλ​.​

The identification includes the topology, not merely a bijection of sets.

The prescribed-pole form of Runge theorem is this: let K⊂C be compact, and let E⊂C∖K meet every connected component of the complement in the Riemann sphere. If f is holomorphic on a neighbourhood of K, then for every ϵ>0 there is a rational function with all its poles in E whose uniform distance from f on K is less than ϵ. A polynomial is regarded as having its only possible pole at infinity. The empty compact set is trivial, so assume K=∅.
First suppose ∞∈E. Let A⊂C(K) be the uniform rational approximation algebra with prescribed poles, the uniform closure of rational functions whose finite poles lie in E. It is a commutative unital Banach algebra, and contains u(z)=z. Set
S={λ∈C∖K:(u−λ1)−1∈A},
(25)
where the inverse in this definition is the pointwise continuous function on K. The set S is relatively open: if the inverse at λ belongs to A, the Neumann series gives inverses at nearby points. It is relatively closed: when λn​→λ∈/K, the corresponding scalar functions converge uniformly on K, and A is closed.
Each bounded complementary component meets E in a finite point a, and (u−a1)−1 is an allowed rational function. The unbounded component meets S because for ∣λ∣>∥u∥ the geometric series for (u−λ1)−1 belongs to A. Being both open and closed in the complement, S therefore contains every component, so it is the entire complement. Conversely, evaluation at any z∈K prevents u−z1 from being invertible. Hence
σA​(u)=K.​
(26)
Apply the holomorphic functional calculus in A to f near K. For every z∈K, the evaluation character of an algebra and the contour formula from (b) give f(u)(z)=f(z). Thus f∣K​∈A. By the definition of this uniform closure, the required rational approximations exist.
If ∞∈/E, choose a finite point a∈E in the component containing infinity. The Möbius transformation w=(z−a)−1 sends K to a compact subset K′ of the plane and sends E to a set E′ containing infinity. It preserves complementary components, so E′ meets every component of the complement of K′. The transformed function F(w)=f(a+1/w) is holomorphic near K′, since 0∈/K′. The case already proved approximates F by rational functions with poles in E′. Pulling them back gives rational approximations to f on K with poles only in E: a pole at infinity in the w-plane becomes a pole at a, and every other pole has its prescribed preimage. In particular the pullbacks do not acquire a pole at the original infinity, since 0∈/E′.
This proves the full prescribed-pole theorem, including the case where a pole at infinity is forbidden. If C∖K is connected, take E={∞} to obtain the polynomial Runge theorem as a corollary.

The Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem identifies the dual of the complex space of continuous functions on a compact space C(K) with finite regular complex Borel measures on K: each bounded linear functional has a unique representation
L(f)=∫K​fdμ,∥L∥=∣μ∣(K).​
(27)
Here ∣μ∣ is the variation measure; positive functionals correspond exactly to positive regular measures. This is the measure representation theorem, rather than the Hilbert-space representation of a functional by a vector.
For the spectral construction use the convention that the Hilbert inner product is linear in its first argument. A unital subalgebra of B(H) is understood to contain IH​: an algebra whose abstract identity is a smaller projection could not give a resolution normalized by P(K)=IH​. On a compact Hausdorff space, a resolution of the identity is understood to be a normalized regular projection-valued measure: its scalar measures are regular, its values are orthogonal projections, and it is countably additive in the strong operator topology. Regularity is part of the usual convention needed for the uniqueness assertion; the statement is interpreted in this sense.
The Commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem makes the Gelfand transform an isometric unital star-isomorphism A→C(K), where K=ΦA​ is compact Hausdorff. Denote its inverse by π:C(K)→A⊂B(H). For x,y∈H, the bounded linear functional f↦⟨π(f)x,y⟩ has norm at most ∥x∥∥y∥. Riesz-Markov-Kakutani gives a regular complex measure μx,y​ such that
⟨π(f)x,y⟩=∫K​fdμx,y​,∣μx,y​∣(K)≤∥x∥∥y∥.
(28)
Uniqueness makes these measures sesquilinear in x,y. For f≥0, the identity π(f)=π(f​)π(f​)∗ shows positivity, so μx,x​ is positive with total mass ∥x∥2.
For every Borel set B, the bounded sesquilinear form (x,y)↦μx,y​(B) determines an operator P(B) by Hilbert-space Riesz representation theorem, with
⟨P(B)x,y⟩=μx,y​(B).
(29)
In particular 0≤P(B)≤I and P(B) is self-adjoint. We now verify the projection identity rather than assume it.
For continuous f, uniqueness of the representing measure applied to continuous test functions gives
μπ(f)x,y​=fμx,y​,μx,π(f​)y​=fμx,y​.
(30)
These identities imply P(B)π(f)=π(f)P(B). Testing once more against a continuous g gives
⟨π(g)P(B)x,y⟩=∫B​gdμx,y​.
(31)
The restriction 1B​μx,y​ of a finite regular Borel measure is still regular, so uniqueness in Riesz-Markov-Kakutani yields μP(B)x,y​=1B​μx,y​. Consequently, for any Borel C,
⟨P(C)P(B)x,y⟩=μP(B)x,y​(C)=μx,y​(B∩C),P(C)P(B)=P(B∩C).​
(32)
Taking C=B proves that P(B) is an orthogonal projection. Also P(K)=I and P(∅)=0. For disjoint Bj​, these projections are orthogonal, and scalar countable additivity gives weak countable additivity. If B=⋃j​Bj​, then RN​=P(B)−∑j≤N​P(Bj​) is the projection of the remaining union and
∥RN​x∥2=μx,x​(⋃j>N​Bj​)⟶0.
(33)
Thus countable additivity holds in the strong operator topology. The scalar measures are the regular measures already constructed. This completes the scalar-measure construction of a projection-valued measure.
By the integral theorem permitted in the question, continuous f satisfies ⟨(∫fdP)x,y⟩=∫fdμx,y​=⟨π(f)x,y⟩, and hence ∫fdP=π(f). For T∈A, this proves the spectral theorem for a commutative operator algebra:
T=∫K​TdP.​
(34)
Any other regular resolution giving these integrals has the same scalar integrals on all of C(K); Riesz-Markov-Kakutani uniqueness forces the same scalar measures and therefore the same projections on every Borel set.
For nonempty open U⊂K, compact Hausdorff normality supplies a nonzero continuous function f supported in U. If P(U)=0, the stated squared-norm identity for spectral integrals gives π(f)=∫fdP=0, contradicting the isometry of π. This proves the full support of a faithful spectral measure property
P(U)=0for every nonempty open U⊂K.​
(35)
Faithfulness of the representation is essential here.
The spectral theorem for normal operators says that a bounded normal operator T on a nonzero complex Hilbert space has a unique regular projection-valued measure E on σ(T) such that T=∫zdE(z). Its support is all of σ(T), and bounded Borel functions have the associated Borel functional calculus for a normal operator.
For the proof sketch, A=C∗(I,T) is commutative because T commutes with T∗; polynomials in these two operators commute, as do their norm limits. The map ΦA​→σ(T), φ↦φ(T), is onto by the character of an algebra formula and spectral permanence for C-star algebras. It is one-to-one because a character of an algebra preserves the star operation and its values on T,T∗ determine it on their dense polynomial algebra. It is therefore a homeomorphism from compact ΦA​ to the Hausdorff spectrum. Transport the resolution just constructed through this homeomorphism. It gives the formula for T and full support; conversely a regular resolution for T gives the same integrals for polynomials in T,T∗, hence by density the same continuous functional calculus and the same resolution. This argument works without separability of H.
Finally choose disjoint nonempty relatively open sets U,V⊂σ(T) around two distinct spectral points, and put Q=E(U). Full support gives Q=0 and E(V)=0, while QE(V)=0, so Q=I. The spectral integral, or multiplicativity of its Borel calculus with 1U​, gives QT=TQ and also QT∗=T∗Q. Thus Y=QH is closed, nonzero and proper, and T(Qx)=Q(Tx)∈Y. The spectral projection gives a reducing subspace conclusion is
Y=E(U)H is a nontrivial closed invariant subspace of T.​
(36)
In fact it is a reducing subspace, since it is also invariant under T∗.
