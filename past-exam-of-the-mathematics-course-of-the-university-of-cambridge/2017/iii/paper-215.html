Let R interchange the two complete graphs, sending vi​ to vi′​ and fixing w. This involution preserves the transition matrix of the simple random walk. A mirror coupling under an involution starts X at vi​, keeps Yt​=R(Xt​) until the hitting time τ of w, and then makes the two walks move identically. Each coordinate has the correct transition probabilities, and they coalesce precisely at τ. The coupling inequality for total variation gives
∥Pt(vi​,⋅)−Pt(vi′​,⋅)∥TV​≤Pvi​​(τ>t).​
(6)
There is a genuine error in the printed expected hitting time bound, as well as an unspecified initial law. For n≥2, write a=Ev1​​τ and b=Evi​​τ for i=1. From every clique vertex there is a positive chance of reaching w within two steps, uniformly over the finite state space for each fixed n, so τ has finite expected value. First-step analysis, using the degree of a vertex n at v1​ and n−1 at the other clique vertices, gives
a=1+nn−1​b,b=1+n−1a+(n−2)b​.
(7)
Solving these linear equations yields
a=n2−n+1,b=n2,Ew​τ=0.​
(8)
In particular even the attachment vertex exceeds the printed bound by one. The valid uniform replacement is maxx​Ex​τ≤n2, the hitting time of the bridge vertex between two cliques formula.
We must also handle arbitrary starting states, including w, before applying the mirror argument. Identify related vertices and consider the lumped Markov chain Z on {1,…,n,w}. Its transition matrix Q has Q(1,j)=1/n for j=1, Q(1,w)=1/n, Q(i,j)=1/(n−1) for i=1 and j=i, and Q(w,1)=1, with all other entries zero. Here the row for i=1 only ranges over j∈{1,…,n}.
For n≥3 and j∈{2,…,n}, direct two-step calculation gives
Q2(w,j)Q2(i,i)Q2(i,j)​=1/n,=(n−2)/(n−1)2+1/(n(n−1)),=(n−3)/(n−1)2+1/(n(n−1)),​Q2(1,j)i≥2,i,j≥2, i=j.​=(n−2)/(n(n−1)),
(9)
All these entries are at least 1/(2n). Thus Q2(z,⋅)≥αν(⋅) for every z, where ν is the uniform distribution on a finite set {2,…,n} and α=(n−1)/(2n)≥1/3. This is a Doeblin condition. At each two-step block couple the two quotient endpoints to the same sample from ν with probability α, using the residual probability distributions otherwise. The first successful block has expected value at most 1/α, so the alignment time A has EA≤6. Lift each endpoint coupling of probability distributions to the original walks by their conditional two-step path laws; this preserves every marginal path law. The success test uses fresh block randomness and so permits restarting the walks with their original transition matrix at the aligned endpoints.
At alignment the original walks are equal or related. Use identical transitions in the first case and the mirror coupling under an involution in the second. The resulting coalescing coupling has coalescence time T satisfying ET≤6+n2 for every pair of initial states. Therefore Markov inequality and the pairwise mixing diameter imply
d(t)≤dˉ(t)≤supx,y​Px,y​(T>t)≤tn2+6​,tmix​(1/4)≤⌈4(n2+6)⌉=O(n2).​
(10)
For the hint's particular starting states, the quotient one-step laws from two different clique indices have a common component of mass at least 1−2/n: for two ordinary indices it is (n−2)/(n−1); for one attachment index it is (n−2)/n. A maximal coupling therefore aligns those indices with probability 1−O(1/n), as suggested. The two-step argument above additionally covers w.
The nonlazy walk is an aperiodic Markov chain for n≥3, because the connected graph contains a triangle. For n=1,2 it is a periodic Markov chain on a bipartite graph; each bipartition class has stationary mass 1/2, and the walk stays on a single class at each time. Hence d(t)≥1/2 and the 1/4 mixing time is infinite. The asymptotic conclusion is valid for n≥3.

For a finite irreducible Markov chain that is an aperiodic Markov chain, set
dn​(t)=maxx​∥Pnt​(x,⋅)−πn​∥TV​,tmix(n)​(α)=min{t∈Z≥0​:dn​(t)≤α}.​
(11)
Here πn​ is its unique stationary distribution, and the distance is total variation distance. Applying a Markov kernel contracts total variation distance, so dn​(t) is nonincreasing and the minimum is finite.
Use the ratio definition of cutoff: a family has cutoff for Markov chains if, for every fixed 0<ε<1/2,
tmix(n)​(1−ε)tmix(n)​(ε)​⟶1,​
(12)
with the denominator positive eventually. Thus the transition between any two fixed distance levels takes negligible time compared with the mixing time scale. By monotonicity and squeezing, this also gives tmix(n)​(α)/tmix(n)​(β)→1 for any fixed α,β∈(0,1).
Some definitions of cutoff for Markov chains additionally require the mixing time to diverge. That extra requirement cannot be built into the definition here: the final request in part (d) explicitly asks for a bounded-time counterexample. The ratio convention is the one consistent with that request.

Use the ratio definition of cutoff from part (a), and write Tn​=tmix(n)​(1/4). In the reversible Markov chain setting of the preceding parts let γn​=1−λ2,n​ and γ∗,n​=1−maxj≥2​∣λj,n​∣. In particular γ∗,n​≤γn​.

Tn​tmix(n)​(ε)​≥(γ∗,n​Tn​1​−Tn​1​)log2ε1​≥(B1​−Tn​1​)log2ε1​.

Since Tn​→∞, choose fixed ε so small that log(1/(2ε))>B. The lower limit of this ratio is then greater than one, contradicting cutoff for Markov chains. Thus the Peres product condition holds:
γn​Tn​⟶∞.​
(20)
In fact the argument with γ∗,n​ proves the stronger absolute-gap product condition. Part (d) does not repeat the reversible Markov chain hypothesis; the ordinary real ordering of eigenvalues above uses that hypothesis. If arbitrary chains are intended, define the spectral gap explicitly: the eigenvalue lower bound for total variation mixing works for complex eigenfunctions too, and proves the same assertion for γ∗​ or for 1−maxj≥2​Reλj​, which is at least γ∗​. No real ordering of complex eigenvalues is implicit.
For the requested counterexample take nonlazy simple random walk on the complete graph Kn​, n≥3. Let Π have every row equal to the uniform distribution on a finite set, and let a=−1/(n−1). Then
P=Π+a(I−Π),Pt=Π+at(I−Π),dn​(t)=(1−n1​)(n−1)−t.​
(21)
Thus dn​(0)→1 and dn​(1)=1/n→0: every fixed-level mixing time is eventually one. This is a bounded-time jump satisfying the ratio definition of cutoff. The ordinary spectral gap is n/(n−1) and the absolute one is (n−2)/(n−1), so both products with Tn​=1 tend to one, not infinity. The divergence assumption on Tn​ is therefore essential under this convention.

On a finite state space with at least two states, as in the surrounding questions, for a real function write π(f)=∑x​π(x)f(x) and Varπ​(f)=∑x​π(x)(f(x)−π(f))2. The Poincare inequality for a reversible Markov chain with constant C is
Varπ​(f)≤CE(f,f)for every f,​
(22)
where the Dirichlet form of a Markov chain is
E(f,f)=21​∑x,y​π(x)P(x,y)(f(x)−f(y))2=⟨f,(I−P)f⟩π​.
(23)
The associated spectral gap is the ordinary gap γ=1−λ2​, with variational formula
γ=Varπ​(f)>0inf​Varπ​(f)E(f,f)​.​
(24)
Equivalently minimize E(f,f) subject to π(f)=0 and ⟨f,f⟩π​=1. Thus the Poincare inequality for a reversible Markov chain holds precisely for C≥1/γ, and the optimal constant is 1/γ. It controls the ordinary rather than absolute spectral gap, so negative eigenvalues do not invalidate it. If the unspecified state space in this part is infinite, the Poincare inequality for a reversible Markov chain and the variational infimum still make sense on nonconstant functions in L2(π), using the self-adjoint operator on that Hilbert space; the resulting spectral gap need not be represented by an actual second eigenvalue. The finite-state formula above is the intended specialization.

For a finite irreducible Markov chain satisfying detailed balance, define the directed-edge capacity Q(u,v)=π(u)P(u,v). For every ordered pair (x,y) choose a path ηxy​ from x to y using only positive-capacity edges; take the empty path for x=y. Let Ne​(η) count occurrences of the directed edge e. The canonical paths Poincare bound says that
ρ=maxe:Q(e)>0​Q(e)1​∑x,y​π(x)π(y)∣ηxy​∣Ne​(ηxy​)
(25)
is a valid Poincare inequality for a reversible Markov chain constant. Simple paths give Ne​∈{0,1}; allowing repetitions requires the multiplicities shown.
To prove the theorem, expand the variance using two independent samples from π:
Varπ​(f)=21​∑x,y​π(x)π(y)(f(x)−f(y))2.
(26)
Telescoping along ηxy​ and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
(f(x)−f(y))2≤∣ηxy​∣∑e​Ne​(ηxy​)(f(e−)−f(e+))2.
(27)
Interchange the finite sums, then use the definition of ρ:
Varπ​(f)​≤21​e∑​(f(e−)−f(e+))2x,y∑​π(x)π(y)∣ηxy​∣Ne​(ηxy​)≤2ρ​e∑​Q(e)(f(e−)−f(e+))2=ρE(f,f).​

The lazy Markov chain has holding probability 1/2 and transition probability 1/(2n) to each neighbour in the hypercube graph. Its stationary distribution is π(x)=2−n by symmetry.
For (x,y) choose the path changing the differing coordinates in increasing order. Its length is at most n. Fix a directed edge (u,u(k)) that flips coordinate k. A path uses this edge exactly when xk​=uk​, yk​=−uk​, yj​=uj​ for j<k, and xj​=uj​ for j>k. The coordinates xj​ for j<k and yj​ for j>k are free. Thus exactly 2n−1 ordered pairs use it, while
Q(u,u(k))=2n2−n​,∑(x,y):e∈ηxy​​π(x)π(y)=2n−12−2n=2−n−1.
(29)
The unweighted load divided by capacity equals n; multiplying by the maximum length yields ρ≤n2. The canonical paths Poincare bound therefore proves the slightly stronger Varπ​(f)≤n2E(f,f), and in particular the requested
Varπ​(f)≤2n2E(f,f),γ≥2n21​.​
(30)
One can keep the actual lengths: among these 2n−1 pairs, coordinate k always differs, and each of the other n−1 coordinates differs for half the pairs. Their mean path length is (n+1)/2, giving exact congestion ρ=n(n+1)/2. This refinement still has order n2.

For each subset J⊆{1,…,n}, take the Walsh character fJ​(x)=∏j∈J​xj​, including f∅​≡1. Under the uniform distribution on a finite set these functions are an orthonormal basis: fJ​fK​=fJ△K​ has mean zero unless J=K, since the coordinates are independent fair signs. There are 2n such functions, the full dimension of the function space.
Flipping a coordinate in J changes the sign, while flipping one outside J leaves the value unchanged. The total probability of a sign change is ∣J∣/(2n), so
PfJ​=(1−n∣J∣​)fJ​.
(31)
The spectrum of lazy random walk on the hypercube is therefore
λk​=1−nk​with multiplicity (kn​),0≤k≤n.​
(32)
All eigenvalues are nonnegative and the second largest is 1−1/n. Hence both the ordinary spectral gap and the absolute spectral gap equal γ=1/n​. The requested bound in part (c) is smaller than the exact gap by a factor 2n; even the refined increasing-coordinate canonical paths Poincare bound loses a factor (n+1)/2. The true optimal Poincare inequality for a reversible Markov chain constant is n.

The symmetric group has n! elements, not the printed order n. For n≥2 the identity and the n−1 adjacent transpositions have probabilities 1/n, so the specified transition matrix is normalized. Its stationary distribution is uniform and it satisfies detailed balance, since each transposition is its own inverse. Adjacent transpositions generate the symmetric group, making this an irreducible Markov chain; the positive holding probability makes it an aperiodic Markov chain.
For a fixed label i, right multiplication swaps positions, so f(σ)=σ−1(i) is the label's position. Under the uniform distribution on a finite set it is uniform on {1,…,n}. Therefore
π(f)=2n+1​,Varπ​(f)=12n2−1​.
(36)
For each generator sj​=(j,j+1), the squared change [f(σsj​)−f(σ)]2 is one if the label occupies either of the two positions, and zero otherwise. Its stationary expected value is 2/n, giving
E(f,f)=21​∑j=1n−1​n1​n2​=n2n−1​.
(37)
Thus the tagged-label Rayleigh quotient for adjacent transpositions gives the corrected bound
γ≤n2(n+1)12​=n312​(1+o(1)).​
(38)
The printed factor six cannot be obtained: the hint's variance of a uniform distribution is wrong. Directly, Var(U)=∫01​u2du−(∫01​udu)2=1/12. Bounded convergence in distribution of Un​ does imply convergence of its first two moments, hence convergence of its variance, but the limit is 1/12.
More strongly, the claimed asymptotic bound itself is false for this kernel. The Aldous spectral gap theorem states that the interchange process on a finite connected weighted graph has the same spectral gap as the single-label random walk with identical edge rates; see Theorem 1.1 and its proof. Here P−I is that interchange process generator on a path graph with edge rates 1/n. For the tagged-label generator, direct substitution of gℓ​(k)=cos(ℓπ(k−1/2)/n) gives eigenvalues −2(1−cos(ℓπ/n))/n, 0≤ℓ<n, including the reflecting endpoint equations. These n distinct eigenvalues exhaust the tagged-label function space. Consequently the theorem yields
γ=n2​(1−cosnπ​)∼n3π2​,​

which exceeds 6/n3 asymptotically. This use of a substantial external theorem is only to certify the printed claim's failure; the corrected factor-twelve upper bound above follows directly from the requested test function and the variational formula.

The reference kernel printed in the hint has total mass 1/n+(2n​)/(2n​)=1+1/n, so it is not a Markov kernel. We explicitly replace it by the normalized random transposition shuffle: choose two positions independently and uniformly and swap them. Its probabilities are
p​(id)=1/n,p​((i,j))=2/n2(i<j).
(40)
These sum to one, and its ordinary spectral gap is γ​=2/n, the value intended in the hint. This value can also be certified by the Aldous spectral gap theorem: the single-label generator has rate 2/n2 between distinct positions, so on centered functions it acts as multiplication by −2/n. Both shuffles have the same uniform distribution on a finite set as stationary distribution.
We use the Canonical paths comparison theorem in the following precise form. For two finite reversible Markov chains with the same positive stationary distribution π, route each directed transition (x,y) of P along a positive-capacity path ηxy​ of P. If
A=maxe:Q(e)>0​Q(e)1​∑x,y​π(x)P(x,y)∣ηxy​∣Ne​(ηxy​),Q(u,v)=π(u)P(u,v),
(41)
then E(f,f)≤AE(f,f) and γ≥γ​/A. Indeed, telescope each difference along its path, apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and sum with weights π(x)P(x,y)/2. The load definition gives the Dirichlet form of a Markov chain inequality; the common variance denominator then gives the spectral gap inequality. Identity transitions use empty paths.
Put sk​=(k,k+1). A transposition (i,j) is routed using the word
si​si+1​⋯sj−1​sj−2​⋯si​,
(42)
of length Lij​=2(j−i)−1≤2n. Each generator occurs at most twice; write its occurrence count as Nk​(i,j)≤2. For a fixed directed adjacent edge (σ,σsk​) and each occurrence of sk​ in a word, there is exactly one starting permutation whose translated word crosses this edge at that occurrence. This follows because right multiplication by the prefix is a bijection of the symmetric group. Hence the uniform stationary factors cancel in the comparison load, leaving
A=maxk​n2​∑i<j​Lij​Nk​(i,j).
(43)
The crude bounds Lij​≤2n, Nk​(i,j)≤2, and (2n​)≤n2/2 imply A≤4n2. Therefore
γ≥4n22/n​=2n31​.​
(44)
Only pairs with i≤k<j contribute, and there are k(n−k)≤n2/4 of these. Keeping this restriction gives the stronger A≤2n2 and γ≥1/n3. Neither comparison bound needs the exact adjacent-shuffle spectral gap; the normalization repair of the reference kernel is essential.
