Use the Fourier transform convention φ​(ξ)=∫e−ix⋅ξφ(x)dx and Dj​=−i∂j​, so P(D)u​=P(ξ)u. All distribution pairings use complex bilinearity, and the formal transpose is P(−D), without complex conjugation of the coefficients. If Dj​ instead denotes ∂j​, the Fourier multiplier is P(iξ); the argument below is unchanged after this substitution.
For a nonempty compact convex set K⊂Rn, let HK​(η)=supx∈K​x⋅η be its support function. The Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem says that the Fourier transform is a bijection between distributions supported in K and entire functions F on Cn for which
∣F(ζ)∣≤C(1+∣ζ∣)NeHK​(Imζ)​
(1)
for some C>0 and nonnegative integer N. In particular, support in BR​ is equivalent to the bound with eR∣Imζ∣. Convexity is essential in the precise support formulation: the support function of a set equals that of its convex hull.
Suppose first that u is a compactly supported distribution with support in K. Define F(ζ)=⟨u,e−ix⋅ζ⟩, inserting any cutoff function equal to one near K when viewing u on test functions. A fixed cutoff and the finite order of a distribution estimate permit differentiation of the pairing in each complex variable, giving ∂ζα​F=⟨u,(−ix)αe−ix⋅ζ⟩. The exponential function's power series converges with every required derivative on a fixed compact set; hence F is an entire function. On real frequencies it agrees with the Fourier transform of a compactly supported distribution.
The exact exponential indicator needs a shrinking cutoff function, rather than one fixed outside K. Choose χε​=1 near K, with support in K+B2ε​ and ∥∂αχε​∥∞​≤Cα​ε−∣α∣ for 0<ε≤1. Such a cutoff is obtained by convolving the indicator of K+Bε​ with a mollifier supported in Bε/2​. On one fixed ball containing all these supports, the finite order of a distribution bound is
∣⟨u,θ⟩∣≤C0​max∣α∣≤m​∥∂αθ∥∞​.
(2)
The Leibniz rule applied to θ=χε​e−ix⋅ζ therefore gives
∣F(ζ)∣≤C1​ε−m(1+∣ζ∣)meHK​(Imζ)+2ε∣Imζ∣.
(3)
Taking ε=(1+∣ζ∣)−1 proves the required bound, with N=2m and a fixed additional factor at most e2.
Conversely, suppose F is an entire function with the displayed bound. Its real restriction has polynomial growth, so its inverse Fourier transform defines a tempered distribution by
⟨u,φ⟩=(2π)−n∫Rn​F(ξ)φ​(−ξ)dξ,φ∈S.
(4)
Rapid decay of the Schwartz function φ​ makes this integral absolutely convergent and continuous in the Schwartz space topology.
To determine the support of a distribution, take a test function supported in a half-space x⋅ω≥HK​(ω)+δ, where ∣ω∣=1 and δ>0. The contour-shift proof of the Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem gives, for each t≥0,
⟨u,φ⟩=(2π)−n∫Rn​F(ξ+itω)φ​(−ξ−itω)dξ.
(5)
Here is the estimate that justifies both the shift and its limiting use. Since φ​(−ξ−itω)=∫eix⋅ξe−tx⋅ωφ(x)dx, repeated integration by parts with 1−Δx​ yields
∣φ​(−ξ−itω)∣≤CL​(1+t)2Le−t(HK​(ω)+δ)(1+∣ξ∣2)−L.
(6)
For fixed t, the same estimate is uniform over the imaginary strip from 0 to tω. Rotate coordinates so ω is the first coordinate direction, apply the Cauchy integral theorem on truncated rectangles, and let their real edges tend to infinity. If 2L>N+n, the boundary integrals vanish and the horizontal integrals converge absolutely; thus the claimed shift is valid. Using HK​(tω)=tHK​(ω) now bounds the pairing by
C′(1+t)N+2Le−δt∫Rn​(1+∣ξ∣)N−2Ldξ,
(7)
which tends to zero. A separating hyperplane exists at every point outside the compact convex set K. A finite partition of unity on the support of any exterior test function reduces it to such half-spaces, so suppu⊆K. Fourier inversion gives uniqueness; the entire transform of this u agrees with F on Rn and hence everywhere, by applying the one-variable identity theorem successively in each coordinate. This completes both directions.
Now apply this theorem to compact support solvability for a constant-coefficient ordinary differential equation. If P is a nonzero constant, its kernel is zero and u=v/P is the unique solution. Otherwise write
P(ζ)=am​∏j=1m​(ζ−αj​),αj​=αk​(j=k).
(8)
The solution space of P(−D)φ=0 is
N=span{e−iα1​x,…,e−iαm​x}.
(9)
Indeed these exponentials solve the equation, their initial derivative vectors have a nonzero Vandermonde determinant, and uniqueness for the order-m ordinary differential equation makes them a basis. Pairing P(D)u=v with any such solution proves necessity:
⟨v,φ⟩=⟨u,P(−D)φ⟩=0.
(10)
For sufficiency, choose R with suppv⊆[−R,R] and let V(ζ)=⟨v,e−ixζ⟩. The assumed annihilation is precisely V(αj​)=0 for every j. Since all roots of a polynomial are simple, U=V/P has removable singularities at every root and is an entire function. Outside one disk, ∣P(ζ)∣≥c(1+∣ζ∣)m. Inside that disk, the extended U is bounded. The polynomial division preservation of exponential type therefore gives
∣U(ζ)∣≤C′(1+∣ζ∣)N′eR∣Imζ∣.
(11)
The Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem produces a compactly supported distribution u with transform U, supported in the same interval. Then P(D)u​=PU=V, so Fourier inversion gives P(D)u=v. Thus
v∈P(D)E′(R) ⟺ ⟨v,φ⟩=0 for every φ∈N.​
(12)
The compactly supported solution is unique: Pu=0 forces the entire transform to vanish off the finitely many roots and hence everywhere. With repeated roots, the corresponding condition would also require derivatives V(k)(α)=0, or pairing with xke−iαx, up to one less than each multiplicity.
