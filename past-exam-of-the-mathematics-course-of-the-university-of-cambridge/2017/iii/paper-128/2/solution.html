A right Noetherian ring satisfies the ascending chain condition on right ideals; equivalently, every right ideal is a finitely generated module.
Here is a noncommutative Hilbert basis theorem proof adapted to the stated hypothesis. Set F−1​=0 and
Fn​=∑i=0n​Axi=∑i=0n​xiA.
(5)
The equality follows inductively from A+xA=A+Ax, by moving one coefficient past one x at a time. These spaces form an exhaustive filtered algebra structure on B, with Fn​Fm​⊆Fn+m​. This does not assert uniqueness of the displayed expressions or the existence of a coefficient-moving automorphism.
For a right ideal I⊆B, define
Ln​={a∈A:axn∈I+Fn−1​}.
(6)
Each Ln​ is a right ideal of A. In fact, if a∈Ln​ and c∈A, write cxn=xnb+u with b∈A, u∈Fn−1​; then acxn=(axn)b+au∈I+Fn−1​. Also Ln​⊆Ln+1​ by right multiplication by x. Since A is a right Noetherian ring, this ascending chain stabilizes at some LN​.
Choose finite generators anj​ for each Ln​, 0≤n≤N, and choose fnj​∈I with fnj​−anj​xn∈Fn−1​. These finitely many elements generate I as a right ideal. To see this, induct on m for f∈I∩Fm​. Write f=axm+u with u∈Fm−1​; then a∈Lm​. Put d=min(m,N) and express a=∑j​adj​cj​. Write cj​xd=xdbj​+uj​ with uj​∈Fd−1​. The difference
f−∑j​fdj​bj​xm−d
(7)
lies in I∩Fm−1​, so the induction applies. At m=0 the remainder is zero. Hence B is right Noetherian.
For the quantum torus, take q∈k× and the convention YX=qXY. Begin with the polynomial ring k[X], which is Noetherian by the Hilbert basis theorem. Adjoining X−1 preserves the hypothesis because it commutes with k[X], and gives k[X±1]. Adjoin Y next. The relation Yf(X)=f(qX)Y and its inverse coefficient-moving relation give A+YA=A+AY for A=k[X±1]. Finally adjoin Y−1 to k[X±1][Y;σ], where σ(X)=qX. On a monomial, Y−1XiYj=q−iXiYj−1; when j=0 this is in AY−1, and when j>0 it is in A. The reverse inclusion follows by the same relation. The preceding argument applies at each step, proving the quantum torus is right Noetherian. Nonzero q is required for this notation.
For a noncommutative ring, a prime ideal of a noncommutative ring means a proper two-sided ideal P such that UV⊆P for two-sided ideals U,V implies U⊆P or V⊆P. Equivalently, aAb⊆P implies a∈P or b∈P. This definition does not require A/P to be a noncommutative domain.
Retain the right Noetherian ring hypothesis for the last assertion. More generally, the ascending chain condition on two-sided ideals suffices. We claim that every proper two-sided ideal I contains a product of finitely many prime ideals of a noncommutative ring, each containing I. If not, choose a maximal counterexample I. It cannot be a prime ideal of a noncommutative ring. Thus there are two-sided ideals U,V strictly containing I with UV⊆I: add I to the two witnesses for failure of the defining condition for a prime ideal of a noncommutative ring. By maximality, both U and V contain products of finitely many prime ideals of a noncommutative ring containing them. Concatenating these products gives a product inside UV⊆I, a contradiction.
Apply the claim to I=0 in a nonzero A, obtaining P1​⋯Pr​=0. Every prime ideal of a noncommutative ring Q contains one of the Pi​, by repeated application of the definition of a prime ideal of a noncommutative ring. For the prime radical of a noncommutative ring N, it follows that
N=P1​∩⋯∩Pr​,Nr=0.​
(8)
Indeed, N⊆Pi​ gives Nr⊆P1​⋯Pr​=0, and ⋂i​Pi​⊆Q for every Q gives equality of the intersections. If A=0, the empty intersection is the whole zero ring and the conclusion is immediate.
The final assertion is false for arbitrary algebras without the preceding chain condition. For example, in the commutative ring k[z1​,z2​,…]/(zi2​:i≥1) the nilradical is (z1​,z2​,…), its only prime ideal, but the product of any number of distinct zi​ is nonzero. Thus this nilradical is not a nilpotent ideal.
