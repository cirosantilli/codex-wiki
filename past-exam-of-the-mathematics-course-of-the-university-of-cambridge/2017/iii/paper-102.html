A simple Lie algebra is a nonabelian Lie algebra whose only Lie algebra ideals are zero and the whole algebra. Assume n≥2; sl1​=0 is not simple. We prove the assertion by the matrix-unit extraction lemma for special linear ideals, avoiding any classification theorem.
Let I be a nonzero ideal in the special linear Lie algebra and 0=A∈I. If an off-diagonal entry Aji​ is nonzero, two commutators with the matrix unit Eij​ give
[Eij​,[Eij​,A]]=−2Aji​Eij​∈I,
(1)
so Eij​∈I. If A is diagonal and nonscalar, choose unequal diagonal entries; [Eij​,A]=(Ajj​−Aii​)Eij​ again supplies a matrix unit. A nonzero scalar matrix cannot have zero trace over C, so these cases cover every nonzero A.
Once Eij​∈I, its bracket with Eji​ gives H=Eii​−Ejj​, and [H,Eji​]=−2Eji​ puts the reverse unit in I. Bracketing with the other off-diagonal matrix units generates every off-diagonal unit: for a third index k, [Eij​,Ejk​]=Eik​ and [Eki​,Eij​]=Ekj​; using the reverse unit fills the remaining row and column, then [Eki​,Eiℓ​]=Ekℓ​ fills the others. Opposite pairs generate every trace-zero diagonal matrix. These vectors span sln​, so
sln​(C) is simple for n≥2.​
(2)

In field characteristic p, the identity matrix has trace p=0, and hence belongs to slp​. Its span is a nonzero proper central ideal, so slp​ is not a simple Lie algebra. In fact its center of a Lie algebra is precisely kIp​: commuting with every off-diagonal matrix unit forces a matrix to be scalar.
For p>2, the matrix-unit extraction lemma for special linear ideals still works because two is invertible. Any ideal containing a nonscalar matrix contains every off-diagonal matrix unit and every trace-zero diagonal matrix, hence equals slp​. Therefore an ideal of the quotient Lie algebra pulls back either to the center or to the whole algebra. The quotient is nonabelian, since [E12​,E23​]=E13​ remains nonzero modulo the center. Consequently
slp​/kIp​ is simple for p>2.​
(3)
No separation of diagonal root spaces is needed, so this proof also handles p=3 without assuming their weights are distinct.
For p=2, every trace-zero two-by-two matrix has the form aI+bE12​+cE21​. The only nonzero bracket between these basis vectors is [E12​,E21​]=I, which is central. Thus
sl2​/kI is a two-dimensional abelian Lie algebra in characteristic two.​
(4)

For the finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation specified in the PDF, a Lie-invariant bilinear form satisfies
B(xv,w)+B(v,xw)=0(x∈g, v,w∈V).
(5)
The dual Lie algebra representation has action (xϕ)(w)=−ϕ(xw), so the map TB​:V→V∗ defined by TB​(v)=B(v,−) is an intertwining operator. If V is irreducible and B=0, its kernel is zero. Since V,V∗ have equal finite dimension, TB​ is an isomorphism, and B is a nondegenerate bilinear form.
For B1​=0, the composition TB1​−1​TB2​​ is a representation endomorphism. Over an algebraically closed field, the Schur lemma makes it scalar, proving
B2​=λB1​.​
(6)
Transposing B gives another Lie-invariant bilinear form. For B=0, write BT=μB; transposing twice gives μ2=1. In field characteristic different from two, this means μ=1 or μ=−1. Thus the form is a symmetric bilinear form or an alternating bilinear form, respectively. The zero form has both properties. In the second case B(v,v)=−B(v,v) forces B(v,v)=0, using the same characteristic assumption.

Use the classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations. In the irreducible representation V(m), choose vj​=fjv0​, 0≤j≤m, so
hvj​=(m−2j)vj​,fvj​=vj+1​,evj​=j(m−j+1)vj−1​,
(7)
with vectors beyond the endpoints interpreted as zero. The invariant form on an irreducible sl2 module is
B(vj​,vk​)={(−1)j,0,​j+k=m,j+k=m.​​
(8)
Its anti-diagonal entries are nonzero, so it is nondegenerate. Lie-invariant bilinear form invariance under h follows from the sum of the two weights. For f, the two potentially nonzero terms are (−1)j+1 and (−1)j. For e, their coefficients coincide when j+k=m+1, and their signs are opposite. These checks prove invariance under the generators of the sl2 Lie algebra.
Interchanging j,k multiplies the form by (−1)m, giving
B is symmetric for even m,alternating for odd m.​
(9)
Equivalently it is a symmetric bilinear form in odd integer dimension and an alternating bilinear form in even number dimension. The Weyl complete reducibility theorem expresses any finite-dimensional representation as a direct sum of these irreducibles. Give each summand the displayed form and make different summands orthogonal. The resulting form is invariant and nondegenerate. On a reducible representation with both parities, this orthogonal sum need not itself be symmetric or alternating; the dichotomy in the preceding part required irreducibility.

Let U be a four-dimensional symplectic vector space. The symplectic contraction of an exterior square splits Λ2U into its five-dimensional primitive exterior square W and the invariant line spanned by the inverse symplectic bivector Ω. Choose vol=Ω∧Ω/2. Exterior multiplication defines a symmetric bilinear form on Λ2U by ξ∧η=B(ξ,η)vol; it is symmetric because both degrees are two. In a symplectic basis, Ω∧Ω=2vol=0, and W=Ω⊥, so the restriction to W is nondegenerate.
The symplectic Lie algebra sp4​ preserves this form, giving a homomorphism sp4​→so(W). It is injective: an element acting trivially on W also acts trivially on Ω, hence on all of Λ2U. In a four-vector basis, the identities Aei​∧ej​+ei​∧Aej​=0 first force every off-diagonal coefficient of A to vanish, then force Aii​+Ajj​=0 for every pair. Over C these equalities imply A=0.
Both Lie algebras have dimension ten: dimsp4​=4⋅5/2 and dimso5​=5⋅4/2. The injective map is therefore an isomorphism, proving the exceptional isomorphism between sp4 and so5:
sp4​(C)≅so5​(C).​
(15)

Identify the diagonal Cartan subalgebra with coordinates (h1​,…,hn​). The root reflection sα​ is h↦h−2(h,α)α/(α,α) in the standard Euclidean identification. For the Bn root system, this gives
sεi​​:sεi​−εj​​:sεi​+εj​​:​ hi​↦−hi​, (hi​,hj​)↦(hj​,hi​), (hi​,hj​)↦(−hj​,−hi​).​​
(16)
All unlisted coordinates stay fixed, and s−α​=sα​. These transformations are coordinate permutations and independent sign changes. They generate exactly the signed symmetric group, so
W≅(Z/2Z)n⋊Sn​,∣W∣=2nn!.​
(17)
Here Sn​ permutes the n factors in the semidirect product.

The PDF uses V=C2n+1 for so2n+1​; the TeX incorrectly turns this into a power of two. The crystal of the defining odd-orthogonal representation is the chain with weights ε1​,…,εn​,0,−εn​,…,−ε1​. Label its vertices 1,…,n,0,nˉ,…,1ˉ. Its directed edge colors are
1,2,…,n−1,n,n,n−1,…,2,1.
(18)
In particular the short-root string n→0→nˉ has two edges of color n.
For a full drawing of the tensor product of crystals in any rank, take every ordered pair of chain vertices (a,b). Let εi​(b) count incoming edges of color i along its string, and let φi​(a) count outgoing ones. We use the convention
f​i​(a⊗b)={f​i​(a)⊗b,a⊗f​i​(b),​φi​(a)>εi​(b),φi​(a)≤εi​(b),​
(19)
omitting an edge when the indicated operator vanishes. This crystal tensor-product rule specifies every vertex and every edge; the raising rule uses ≥ for its first-factor branch. The picture shows the chain and all 25 tensor vertices for n=2, with connected components distinguished by node color.
For n≥2, the three highest-weight vectors of the tensor crystal are 1⊗1, 1⊗2 and 1⊗1ˉ. They have weights 2ε1​, ε1​+ε2​ and zero. There are no others: a highest tensor vertex must have highest first factor, and the string inequalities then restrict its second factor to these three vertices. Therefore
V⊗V=L(2ε1​)⊕L(ε1​+ε2​)⊕L(0).​
(20)
The summands are the traceless symmetric square, the exterior square (the Adjoint representation), and the invariant scalar line. Their dimensions are (2n+1)(n+1)−1, n(2n+1) and one, summing to (2n+1)2. For n≥3 the first two highest weights are 2ω1​,ω2​; for n=2 they are 2ω1​,2ω2​. For n=1, the three tensor highest vertices are 1⊗1,1⊗0,1⊗1ˉ, giving L(4ω1​)⊕L(2ω1​)⊕L(0) of dimensions 5,3,1.

Choose a positive root system R+ for a complex semisimple Lie algebra, with Weyl group W and Weyl vector ρ=21​∑α∈R+​α. For a dominant integral weight λ, let L(λ) denote its finite-dimensional irreducible representation, and use formal symbols with eμeν=eμ+ν. Its formal character of a weight module is chL(λ)=∑μ​dimL(λ)μ​eμ. The Weyl character formula says
chL(λ)=∑w∈W​det(w)ewρ∑w∈W​det(w)ew(λ+ρ)​.​
(21)
Here det(w)=(−1)ℓ(w), the sign of the product of simple root reflections. The Weyl denominator formula is
w∈W∑​det(w)ewρ=eρα∈R+∏​(1−e−α).​
(22)
The quotient is initially interpreted in the fraction field of the group algebra of the weight lattice; the theorem makes it a finite character.

Take an integer k≥0, so kρ is a dominant integral weight. Apply the dilation eμ↦e(k+1)μ to the Weyl denominator formula:
∑w∈W​det(w)e(k+1)wρ=e(k+1)ρ∏α∈R+​(1−e−(k+1)α).
(23)
This is exactly the numerator of the Weyl character formula for L(kρ). Divide by its denominator and use a finite geometric series in each root direction:
chL(kρ)=ekρα∈R+∏​1−e−α1−e−(k+1)α​=ekρα∈R+∏​(1+e−α+⋯+e−kα).​
(24)
This character of a Weyl-vector multiple also gives dimL(kρ)=(k+1)∣R+∣ by evaluating every formal exponential at one. This evaluation is safe in the final polynomial expression, without dividing by a zero denominator.

Number the G2 root system with α1​ short and α2​ long, normalized by (α1​,α1​)=2, (α2​,α2​)=6 and (α1​,α2​)=−3. Its positive roots are α1​,α2​,α1​+α2​,2α1​+α2​,3α1​+α2​,3α1​+2α2​, and the negatives complete the twelve-root system. The short roots form one hexagon, and the long roots form a rotated hexagon whose radius is 3​ times larger. The picture also supplies the crystal requested in the next part.
The fundamental weights are ω1​=2α1​+α2​, ω2​=3α1​+2α2​, and ρ=ω1​+ω2​. The six positive coroots, in coordinates relative to α1∨​,α2∨​, are (1,0),(0,1),(1,3),(2,3),(1,1),(1,2). Applying the Weyl dimension formula to λ=n1​ω1​+n2​ω2​ therefore gives the G2 dimension polynomial
dimL(λ)=120(n1​+1)(n2​+1)(n1​+n2​+2)(n1​+2n2​+3)(n1​+3n2​+4)(2n1​+3n2​+5)​.​
(25)
The denominator is the product 1⋅1⋅2⋅3⋅4⋅5 of the corresponding pairings with ρ. As checks, the weights ω1​,ω2​,2ω1​ give dimensions 7,14,27 respectively.

Choose the chain basis v0​,…,v6​ from the preceding part, normalized by v1​=f1​v0​, v2​=f2​v1​, v3​=f1​v2​. The associated sl2 Lie algebra strings give e1​v1​=v0​, e1​v3​=2v2​ and e2​v2​=v1​; other raising actions used below vanish because their proposed weights do not occur.
In the exterior square, v0​∧v1​ is a highest-weight vector of weight 2ω1​−α1​=ω2​: e1​ produces v0​∧v0​=0, and e2​ kills both factors. Also
w=v0​∧v3​−2v1​∧v2​
(28)
is a nonzero highest-weight vector of weight ω1​. Its two e1​ terms cancel, and its e2​ image is zero. The Weyl complete reducibility theorem supplies irreducible summands with those weights; their dimensions 14+7=21 exhaust dimΛ2V. Thus
Λ2V=L(ω2​)⊕L(ω1​).​
(29)
For the symmetric square, v0​⊗v0​ is a highest-weight vector of weight 2ω1​, giving a 27-dimensional summand. The longest Weyl-group element of G2​ acts as minus the identity, so the highest weight of a dual representation is −w0​ω1​=ω1​: V is self-dual. A nonzero intertwiner V→V∗ gives a nondegenerate Lie-invariant bilinear form. It is symmetric, since an alternating bilinear form cannot be nondegenerate in odd dimension. Its inverse is a nonzero invariant vector in S2V, supplying the scalar summand. Since 27+1=28=dimS2V,
S2V=L(2ω1​)⊕L(0).​
(30)
These are the exterior and symmetric squares of the seven-dimensional G2 representation; all highest-weight and dimension claims are specified rather than relying on an unstated cross-product identity.
