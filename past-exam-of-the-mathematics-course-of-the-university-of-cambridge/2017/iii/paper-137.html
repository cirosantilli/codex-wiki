Use the Fourier transform normalization f​(ξ)=∫R​f(x)e−2πixξdx. A precise version of the Poisson summation formula is that, for every Schwartz function f∈S(R),
n∈Z∑​f(n)=m∈Z∑​f​(m).​
(1)
Both series have absolute convergence. Here being a Schwartz function means being smooth with supx​∣xaf(b)(x)∣<∞ for all nonnegative integers a,b. This hypothesis makes the sums and the termwise operations below legitimate; the formula is not being asserted for arbitrary integrable functions.
Form the periodization of a Schwartz function Pf​(x)=∑n​f(x+n). Every differentiated series has uniform convergence on [0,1], by rapid decay, so Pf​ is a smooth periodic function of period one. Its mth Fourier coefficient is
∫01​Pf​(x)e−2πimxdx=∑n​∫nn+1​f(u)e−2πimudu=f​(m).
(2)
The interchange follows from absolute convergence uniformly on this interval. Integration by parts shows that these Fourier coefficients decrease faster than every inverse power of ∣m∣. Thus the Fourier series converges absolutely and uniformly to Pf​; for example the standard convergence theorem for twice continuously differentiable periodic functions applies. Evaluating at zero proves the Poisson summation formula.
For t>0, scaling the given Gaussian Fourier transform gives
e−πtx2(ξ)=t−1/2e−πξ2/t.
(3)
Applying the Poisson summation formula gives the real-parameter Jacobi theta function transformation
θ(t)=n∈Z∑​e−πn2t=t−1/2θ(1/t).​
(4)
For Res>1, termwise application of the Mellin transform is justified by integrating absolute values and gives
Λ(s):=π−s/2Γ(s/2)ζ(s)=21​∫0∞​(θ(t)−1)ts/2−1dt.
(5)
Each positive n contributes Γ(s/2)(πn2)−s/2, and the factor one half removes the equal positive and negative terms. This is the Mellin representation of the completed Riemann zeta function.
Split the integral at one. On (0,1), the Jacobi theta function transformation writes θ(t)−1=t−1/2−1+t−1/2(θ(1/t)−1). Integrating the first two terms and substituting u=1/t in the third yields the pole-subtracted theta integral for the completed zeta function:
Λ(s)=s−11​−s1​+21​∫1∞​(θ(t)−1)(ts/2−1+t(1−s)/2−1)dt.
(6)
The remaining integral is an entire function of s: θ(t)−1=O(e−πt), and on each compact set of s this exponential dominates all powers of t and all factors arising from differentiation. It therefore supplies a meromorphic continuation of Λ to the whole plane, with simple poles at one and zero, of residues 1 and −1, respectively. Its expression is unchanged by s↦1−s.
The reciprocal Gamma function is entire, with simple zeros at its nonpositive integer arguments. Consequently
ζ(s)=πs/2Γ(s/2)−1Λ(s)
(7)
is holomorphic everywhere except for a simple pole at s=1, of residue one. The apparent pole at s=0 cancels; in fact Γ(s/2)−1∼s/2 gives ζ(0)=−1/2. This proves the analytic continuation of the Riemann zeta function. The completed functional equation of the Riemann zeta function is
π−s/2Γ(s/2)ζ(s)=π−(1−s)/2Γ((1−s)/2)ζ(1−s).​
(8)
These are equalities of meromorphic functions; at apparent singularities they are interpreted by continuation. Equivalently, ξ(s)=21​s(s−1)Λ(s) is an entire function with ξ(s)=ξ(1−s).

Extend the Dirichlet character χ periodically to all integers, putting χ(n)=0 when (n,N)>1. Its Dirichlet L-function, initially on Res>1, is
L(χ,s)=n≥1∑​χ(n)n−s=p∤N∏​(1−χ(p)p−s)−1.​
(9)
The Euler product follows from unique prime factorization and absolute convergence; a Dirichlet character is completely multiplicative on this extension. Write χ0​ for the principal Dirichlet character, equal to one on units and zero elsewhere.
For a nonprincipal Dirichlet character χ, character orthogonality gives ∑a=1N​χ(a)=0. Explicitly, multiplication by a unit u with χ(u)=1 permutes the unit residues and multiplies this sum by χ(u), forcing it to vanish. For t>0 set
Hχ​(t)=∑n≥1​χ(n)e−nt=1−e−Nt∑a=1N​χ(a)e−at​.
(10)
The numerator is O(t) at zero and the denominator is Nt+O(t2), so Hχ​ is bounded, indeed analytic, near zero; it decays exponentially at infinity. Initially for Res>1, absolute convergence justifies
Γ(s)L(χ,s)=∫0∞​Hχ​(t)ts−1dt.
(11)
This Mellin transform integral is holomorphic for Res>0, locally uniformly in s, and division by the Gamma function proves the requested analytic continuation to the left of the line one.
In fact, the same argument proves Mellin continuation of a nonprincipal Dirichlet L-function to the entire plane. If Hχ​(t)=∑r=0M​cr​tr+O(tM+1) at zero, subtract this Taylor polynomial on (0,1) and add its explicit integrals:
Γ(s)L(χ,s)=∫1∞​Hχ​(t)ts−1dt+∑r=0M​s+rcr​​+∫01​(Hχ​(t)−∑r=0M​cr​tr)ts−1dt.
(12)
The last integral is holomorphic on Res>−M−1. Its possible simple poles at nonpositive integers cancel against zeros of 1/Γ(s). Letting M increase shows that L(χ,s) is an entire function, without any primitivity assumption.
For real s>1, use the absolutely convergent Euler product logarithm
Bχ​(s)=∑p∤N​∑m≥1​mpmsχ(p)m​=Fχ​(s)+Rχ​(s),eBχ​(s)=L(χ,s).
(13)
The higher-power remainder has the uniform estimate
∣Rχ​(s)∣≤∑p​∑m≥2​p−m=∑p​p(p−1)1​≤∑n≥2​n(n−1)1​=1.
(14)
For a nonprincipal Dirichlet character χ, invoke the allowed nonvanishing of a nonprincipal Dirichlet L-function at one. Its holomorphy and nonvanishing give a holomorphic logarithm on a small disk about one. On the connected real interval 1<s<1+η, Bχ​(s) differs from this logarithm by a fixed element of 2πiZ: the difference is continuous with exponential one. Thus the prime-character sum near one is bounded. This branch argument is needed for complex-valued Dirichlet characters.
For χ0​,
L(χ0​,s)=ζ(s)∏p∣N​(1−p−s).
(15)
The finite product has a positive limit as s↓1, and the residue-one pole of the Riemann zeta function gives
Fχ0​​(s)=logs−11​+O(1)⟶+∞,Fχ​(s)=O(1)(χ=χ0​).​
(16)
Here O(1) for nonprincipal Dirichlet characters means bounded complex magnitude.
Finally, for a residue class a coprime to N, Orthogonality of Dirichlet characters gives
∑p≡a(modN)​p−s=φ(N)1​∑χmodN​χ(a)​Fχ​(s)=φ(N)1​logs−11​+O(1).
(17)
Only primes not dividing N occur, so the character orthogonality applies to every term. This sum diverges as s↓1. A finite collection of primes would give a bounded sum, a contradiction. Every reduced residue class contains infinitely many primes. This is the Dirichlet theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions; the coprimality hypothesis is essential.

Use the valence formula for the modular group: for a nonzero modular form of weight k on SL2​(Z),
v∞​(f)+21​vi​(f)+31​vρ​(f)+∑z=i,ρ​vz​(f)=12k​,ρ=e2πi/3.

The sum contains one representative of each other modular group orbit of zeros. The orders are nonnegative because f is holomorphic on the complex upper half-plane and at infinity. The half and third weights account for the elliptic stabilizers of the modular group.
In weight two, the transformation under S=(01​−10​) at its fixed point gives f(i)=i2f(i)=−f(i). Thus any nonzero f would have vi​(f)≥1. The displayed definition uses iterated Eisenstein summation in weight two: the sum in n is evaluated before the sum in m, with the single (0,0) term omitted. This order is essential. The two-dimensional lattice series does not have absolute convergence, so arbitrary rearrangement would not be justified.
For noninteger w, the cosecant partial-fraction identity is
∑n∈Z​(w+n)21​=π2csc2(πw).
(20)
For completeness, apply the residue theorem to πcot(πζ)/(ζ−w)2 on squares with large half-integer sides. The cotangent is bounded on the contours and the integral is O(R−1). Its residues at the integers are (n−w)−2 and its residue at w is −π2csc2(πw), proving the formula. If Imw>0, the geometric-series expression cot(πw)=−i(1+2∑r≥1​e2πirw), differentiated termwise, gives the cotangent partial-fraction Fourier kernel
π2csc2(πw)=−4π2∑r≥1​re2πirw.
(21)
Put q=e2πiz with z in the complex upper half-plane. For positive m, this gives −4π2∑r≥1​rqmr. Negative m gives the same value, by replacing n with −n in its inner sum. The m=0 row is ∑n=0​n−2=π2/3, by the Basel problem. The resulting series in m,r does have absolute convergence, locally uniformly in z, so collecting the coefficient at qℓ is legitimate:
G2​(z)=3π2​−8π2∑m,r≥1​rqmr=3π2​−8π2∑ℓ≥1​σ1​(ℓ)qℓ.
(22)
The coefficient is the sum-of-divisors function, since r runs over the positive divisors of ℓ. Thus
G2​(z)=3π2​E2​(z).​
(23)
There is no conflict with vanishing of weight-two level-one modular forms: the Eisenstein series of weight two has an anomalous transformation term, so it is not a weight-two modular form.

First establish rational conjugation of finite-index modular subgroups without assuming that Γ is a congruence subgroup. Multiply γ by a positive integer to obtain an integral matrix A, and let D=detA>0. Conjugation is unchanged by this scalar. If h=I+DB∈Γ(D), then
AhA−1=I+ABadj(A)∈SL2​(Z).
(28)
Thus the principal congruence subgroup Γ(D) is contained in H=SL2​(Z)∩γ−1SL2​(Z)γ. It has finite index because reduction modulo D has finite image. Inside H, pullback under conjugation of Γ has relative index at most [SL2​(Z):Γ]. Consequently
[SL2​(Z):Γ′]<∞,Γ′=SL2​(Z)∩γ−1Γγ.​
(29)
This argument does not assert that an arbitrary finite-index subgroup contains a principal congruence subgroup.
Use the determinant-normalized slash operator
(f∣k​γ)(z)=(detγ)k/2(cz+d)−kf(cz+daz+b​),γ=(ac​bd​),detγ>0.
(30)
The positive real power of the determinant is used; on SL2​(Z) this reduces to the usual slash operator for modular forms. The automorphy factor identity gives the right-action rule (f∣k​γ)∣k​η=f∣k​(γη).
A modular form on a finite-index subgroup of integer weight k is a holomorphic function on the complex upper half-plane, invariant under this weight-k action of Γ, and holomorphic at a cusp at each of its cusps. A cusp of a modular group is a Γ orbit in P1(Q). If σ∈SL2​(Z) carries infinity to its representative, choose a positive integer h with σThσ−1∈Γ. Such h exists by finite index. Then f∣k​σ is periodic and has a convergent expansion in qh​=e2πiz/h near zero; holomorphy means no negative exponents, and being a cusp form means zero constant term. Using an actual translation period avoids possible signs if a smaller width of a cusp is defined only modulo the center, particularly in odd weights.
For cusp holomorphy under rational slash operators, choose σ∈SL2​(Z) with σ∞=γ∞, possible by completing a primitive integer pair to a determinant-one matrix. Then σ−1γ=(a0​bd​), with a/d>0. Up to a nonzero constant factor,
f∣k​γ(z)=(f∣k​σ)((a/d)z+b/d).
(31)
The imaginary part of the argument tends to infinity with that of z, so this remains bounded by the cusp expansion of f∣k​σ. It tends to zero if f is a cusp form. Moreover f∣k​γ is invariant under Γ′: for η∈Γ′, γηγ−1∈Γ and the right-action rule applies. Finite index gives a translation period for Γ′, so boundedness is a removable singularity at zero in that periodic parameter. This proves holomorphy at infinity. For every other cusp, apply the same argument to the rational matrix γρ, with ρ∈SL2​(Z). Thus all cusp conditions hold, and
f∣k​γ∈Mk​(Γ′).​
(32)
For the character twist by rational translations of a cusp form, put Bj​=(10​j/N1​) and K=Γ1​(N)∩Γ0​(N2). For η=(ac​bd​)∈K, direct conjugation gives
Bj​ηBj−1​=(a+jc/Nc​b+j(d−a)/N−j2c/N2d−jc/N​)∈SL2​(Z).
(33)
Indeed N2∣c and a≡d≡1(modN). Every f∣k​Bj​=f(z+j/N) is therefore K-invariant and vanishes at all its cusps by the preceding rational-translate argument. Their finite weighted sum is a cusp form, for every Dirichlet character:
fχ​∈Sk​(Γ1​(N)∩Γ0​(N2)).​
(34)
There is, however, a missing primitivity hypothesis in the printed final expansion claim. The exact Fourier expansion of a modular form is always
fχ​(z)=n≥1∑​an​(f)Sχ​​(n)qn,Sχ​​(n)=j∈(Z/NZ)×∑​χ(j)​e2πinj/N.​
(35)
Values of a Dirichlet character on units have modulus one, so χ(j)−1=χ(j)​. For unit n, substitution gives Sχ​​(n)=χ(n)g(χ​), where g is the Gauss sum of a Dirichlet character. For nonunit n, this vanishing formula requires a primitive Dirichlet character.
Here is its proof in that case. Choose a prime p∣(n,N). Primitivity supplies a unit u≡1(modN/p) with χ(u)=1: otherwise the character would factor through the surjective reduction to units modulo N/p. Surjectivity follows by lifting a unit and, if needed, adjusting the lift to avoid the additional prime p, using the Chinese remainder theorem. Multiplication by u fixes e2πinj/N because p∣n, but multiplies the character factor by a nontrivial constant. Hence Sχ​​(n)=0. The finite Fourier transform of a primitive Dirichlet character now gives the corrected formula
fχ​=g(χ​)n≥1∑​χ(n)an​(f)qnif χ is primitive modulo N.​
(36)
The constant is nonzero: finite exponential orthogonality gives ∑nmodN​∣Sχ​​(n)∣2=Nφ(N), whereas the proved formula makes this φ(N)∣g(χ​)∣2. Thus ∣g(χ​)∣=N​.
For a concrete counterexample to the printed unrestricted claim, take N=2, the principal Dirichlet character, and f=Δ. The translation sum is Δ(z+1/2)=∑n​(−1)nτ(n)qn, whose q2 coefficient is −24. Any constant multiple of the proposed odd-index-only series has q2 coefficient zero. Thus the general modularity conclusion is proved, while the claimed simplification is false without the stated extra hypothesis.

Write Γ=SL2​(Z) and G=GL2​(Q)+. The double-coset Hecke algebra consists of Γ-bi-invariant complex functions on G supported on finitely many double cosets, with convolution
(h1​∗h2​)(g)=∑Γx∈Γ\G​h1​(gx−1)h2​(x).
(40)
Each double coset has finitely many orbits under left multiplication by Γ, by rational conjugation of finite-index modular subgroups. Thus the sum is finite and independent of representatives. The characteristic functions of the double cosets form its basis, and its right action on invariant modular forms is f∗h=∑Γx​h(x)f∣k​x, using the determinant-normalized slash operator.
For a positive integer n, let Mn​ be all integral two-by-two matrices of positive determinant n. It is Γ-bi-invariant. Define T(n) to be its indicator function, equivalently the sum of the distinct double cosets it contains, each with coefficient one. This is the convention consistent with the requested formula. For composite n, it need not be the single double coset of diag(1,n): for instance 2I∈M4​ cannot lie in that double coset, since multiplying by unimodular integral matrices preserves the greatest common divisor of the entries.
Prove all the needed subgroup facts directly. For a finite-index subgroup L⊆Z2, take a to be the least positive first coordinate appearing in L and d the least positive second coordinate on its intersection with the second axis. Euclidean division then shows that the first-coordinate projection is aZ and L∩({0}×Z)={0}×dZ. Positivity follows, for example, because the finite quotient group kills a nonzero multiple of each coordinate vector. Choose (a,b)∈L and reduce b modulo d to 0≤b<d. Every vector of L has first coordinate a multiple of a, and subtracting that multiple of (a,b) leaves a multiple of (0,d). Therefore these two vectors form a basis of L. The parameters a,d,b are unique. Reducing the first coordinate modulo a and then the second modulo d gives precisely ad quotient representatives, so [Z2:L]=ad.
Apply this row Hermite normal form in rank two to the row lattice of an integral matrix A with detA=n. Its row lattice contains nZ2, since adj(A)A=nI, so it has finite index. Its two rows and the displayed two rows are bases of the same row lattice. The two inverse change-of-basis matrices have integer entries, so their determinants are integers whose product is one. Thus the change-of-basis matrix has determinant ±1; since both orientations are positive, its determinant is one. Thus each orbit under left multiplication by Γ has exactly one of the determinant-n matrix representatives for Hecke operators
(a0​bd​),a,d>0,ad=n,0≤b<d.
(41)
There are ∑d∣n​d such representatives, proving finiteness as well as the formula. The subgroup argument is a proof of the relevant Hermite normal form, not an invocation of an unproved lattice classification.
Consequently the normalized Hecke operator is
Tn​f=nk/2−1ad=n∑​b=0∑d−1​f∣k​(a0​bd​).​
(42)
It preserves Mk​(Γ): right multiplication by Γ permutes the left-multiplication orbits in Mn​, giving invariance, and cusp holomorphy under rational slash operators gives the holomorphy of each term at every cusp.
For a triangular representative the determinant-normalized slash operator is f∣k​A=nk/2d−kf((az+b)/d). Summing the Fourier expansion of a modular form over b kills every index not divisible by d, by finite exponential orthogonality. Thus
Tn​f=nk−1∑ad=n​d1−k∑ℓ≥0​adℓ​(f)qaℓ.
(43)
The Fourier coefficients of a composite-index Hecke operator are therefore
ar​(Tn​f)=a∣gcd(n,r)∑​ak−1anr/a2​(f)(r≥1),a0​(Tn​f)=σk−1​(n)a0​(f).​
(44)
In particular a1​(Tn​f)=an​(f). If Tn​f=λf, comparison of the q coefficients gives
an​(f)=λa1​(f).​
(45)
Finally suppose a0​(f)=0 and f is a simultaneous eigenfunction. Constant coefficients force λn​=σk−1​(n), so an​(f)=a1​(f)σk−1​(n) for all n≥1. Weight two cannot occur, by vanishing of weight-two level-one modular forms. For even k≥4, use the normalized Eisenstein series with its Fourier expansion of a normalized Eisenstein series
Ek​=1+ck​∑n≥1​σk−1​(n)qn,ck​=−Bk​2k​=0,
(46)
where Bk​ is the Bernoulli number. Then h=f−a0​(f)Ek​ is a cusp form with coefficients (a1​(f)−a0​(f)ck​)σk−1​(n). The Fourier coefficient bound for a cusp form bounds these by O(nk/2), but at arbitrarily large primes p their magnitude is ∣a1​−a0​ck​∣(1+pk−1). Since k−1>k/2, the coefficient factor must vanish. All coefficients of h then vanish, including its constant coefficient, so h=0 by its cusp expansion and the identity theorem. This proves the noncuspidal level-one Hecke eigenform characterization:
f=a0​(f)Ek​(k≥4).​
