Assume real bounded coefficients, with a principal part defining a uniformly elliptic operator, say aijξi​ξj​≥λ∣ξ∣2 for some λ>0, and assume c≤0. No smoothness of the boundary is needed for this classical weak maximum principle for elliptic operators. Its conclusion is
Ωsup​u≤max{0,∂Ωsup​u}.​
(1)
If c=0, the zero may be omitted. Only the symmetric part of the principal coefficients contributes to the Hessian matrix contraction.
For a strict inequality Lu>0, a positive interior maximum is impossible: there Du=0, D2u is negative semidefinite, and cu≤0, so Lu≤0. To reduce the non-strict case to this one, let q(x)=eκx1​. Boundedness of b1,c permits choosing κ so large that
Lq=q(κ2a11+κb1+c)≥q(λκ2−∥b1∥∞​κ−∥c∥∞​)>0.
(2)
Thus L(u+εq)>0. The preceding maximum argument and continuity on the compact closure give
supΩ​(u+εq)≤max{0,sup∂Ω​(u+εq)}.
(3)
Let ε↓0; the exponential is bounded on the bounded domain. This is the exponential perturbation proof of the weak maximum principle with drift, with the zeroth-order term included. For c=0, any interior maximum, of either sign, is impossible for the perturbed function, giving the sharper boundary bound. The sign restriction on c is essential: a positive first Dirichlet Laplacian eigenfunction violates the conclusion for L=Δ+λ1​.

Assume an interior sphere condition at y, and assume the coefficients are bounded and the principal part defines a uniformly elliptic operator near its tangent Euclidean ball. Shrinking the Euclidean ball while keeping it tangent if necessary, arrange that BR​(z)​∖{y}⊂Ω. Write ν=(y−z)/R, the outward radial direction. The conclusion of the Hopf boundary point lemma in the regularity stated in the question is
t↓0liminf​tu(y)−u(y−tν)​>0.​
(8)
If the one-sided normal derivative exists, this says ∂ν​u(y)>0. For a C1 boundary its outward normal agrees with the tangent Euclidean ball's radial normal. Continuity alone does not guarantee that the derivative exists, so its existence must be added when asserting a finite derivative.
On the annulus R/2<r=∣x−z∣<R, use the barrier
h(x)=e−kr2−e−kR2>0.
(9)
The operator has no zeroth-order term, and differentiation gives
Lh=e−kr2(4k2aij(xi​−zi​)(xj​−zj​)−2ktra−2kbi(xi​−zi​))>0
(10)
for sufficiently large k: the quadratic-in-k term is at least k2λR2, while the remaining terms are bounded multiples of k.
On the inner sphere, compactness and strict inequality give a positive minimum of u(y)−u(x). Choose ε>0 small enough that u−u(y)+εh≤0 there. The same inequality holds on the outer sphere since h=0. The weak maximum principle for elliptic operators yields u−u(y)+εh≤0 throughout the annulus. Along the inward radius,
tu(y)−u(y−tν)​≥εte−k(R−t)2−e−kR2​⟶2εkRe−kR2>0.
(11)
This proves the lemma. The interior sphere condition supplies precisely the geometry needed for the barrier; no global boundary regularity is used.

Here a domain is connected. Suppose both a negative point and a zero point exist. The open set D={v<0} then has a boundary point q inside Ω. Choose z∈D close enough to q that the Euclidean ball of radius R=dist(z,Z) stays compactly inside Ω; it is contained in D and touches Z at some y. Attainment of this distance follows by restricting to a compact neighbourhood of q.
On that Euclidean ball v is twice differentiable and
Δv=−v>0,v(y)=0>v(x)(x∈BR​(z)).
(12)
The Hopf boundary point lemma gives a strictly positive outward radial derivative at y. But y is an interior maximum of the C1 function v on Ω, so Dv(y)=0. This contradiction proves the Hopf dichotomy with classical regularity away from the zero set:
v≡0orv<0 throughout Ω.​
(13)
For the requested weaker-regularity counterexample take Ω=(−1,1)n and
v(x)=−max{sinx1​,0}.​
(14)
It is a locally Lipschitz function and nonpositive. Its negative set is x1​>0, where v=−sinx1​ is smooth and Δv+v=0. On x1​≤0 it is zero. The normal derivative jumps from zero to minus one across x1​=0, so it is not C1. All other hypotheses hold; imposing the equation also across that interface would be an additional condition absent from the question.

The global Schauder estimate on a bounded C2,α domain is
∥u∥C2,α(Ω)​≤C(∥u∥C0(Ω)​+∥f∥C0,α(Ω)​+∥ψ∥C2,α(Ω)​).​
(15)
Here C depends on n,α, the domain's boundary regularity and ∥c∥C0,α​, but not on the particular solution or data. The C0 term is needed before uniqueness has been established. The given ψ is an extension of the boundary data to the closure.
For c≤0, choose d with Ω⊂{∣x1​∣<d} and set M=sup∂Ω​∣ψ∣, F=supΩ​∣f∣. The hinted function
b(x)=M+(e2d−ex1​+d)F
(16)
is nonnegative, dominates ∣ψ∣ on the boundary, and satisfies
(Δ+c)b=−ex1​+dF+cb≤−F,
(17)
since x1​+d>0. Therefore (Δ+c)(u−b)=f−(Δ+c)b≥0 and (Δ+c)(−u−b)=−f−(Δ+c)b≥0. Both functions are nonpositive on the boundary. The weak maximum principle for elliptic operators gives ∣u∣≤b, hence the supremum norm barrier for an elliptic Dirichlet problem:
∥u∥∞​≤M+e2dF.​
(18)
Unlike the full Schauder estimate, this last constant depends only on a slab containing Ω, and is independent of how negative c is.

Let L=Δ+c with real c, and let
N={z∈C2,α(Ω):Lz=0, z∣∂Ω​=0}.
(19)
The Fredholm alternative for an elliptic Dirichlet problem says that N is finite-dimensional. If N=0, all the prescribed data admit a unique solution. In general the exact boundary compatibility in the self-adjoint Fredholm alternative is
∫Ω​fzdx+∫∂Ω​ψ∂ν​zdS=0for every z∈N.​
(20)
These conditions are necessary and sufficient; when they hold, all solutions form u0​+N. Thus in the second branch some data are incompatible, while compatible data have nonunique solutions. The outward normal derivative fixes the displayed sign.
To prove the alternative, put X={w∈C2,α(Ω):w∣∂Ω​=0} and Y=C0,α(Ω). The standard zero-boundary Poisson equation theorem makes Δ:X→Y an isomorphism. Multiplication by c, followed by Δ−1, is a compact operator K:X→X: the embedding C2,α↪C0,α is compact, and multiplication is bounded. Writing u=ψ+w gives
(I+K)w=Δ−1F,F=f−Δψ−cψ.
(21)
The Fredholm alternative for a compact operator implies finite kernel and cokernel of equal dimension, and invertibility exactly when the kernel vanishes.
For the explicit compatibility conditions, use the self-adjoint Dirichlet realization of L on L2. It has compact resolvent: a sufficiently large positive shift of −L is coercive, its inverse gains two derivatives, and the Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorem makes that inverse compact. The Fredholm solvability condition for a self-adjoint operator is F⊥N. Standard boundary elliptic regularity upgrades the resulting weak solution to C2,α for these data and boundary. Thus it gives the same kernel and solvability as the preceding Hölder-space formulation.
Finally Green second identity, with z=0 on the boundary and Lz=0, gives
∫Ω​zLψdx=−∫∂Ω​ψ∂ν​zdS.
(22)
Consequently ∫zF=∫zf+∫ψ∂ν​z, proving both necessity and sufficiency with the stated sign. If using complex data and real c, replace z by z in the pairings.

Use the paper's classical norm convention: ∣u∣2;U​ is the derivative supremum norm through order two, not an L2 norm. Write H(u;U)=[D2u]α;U​ and Q(u,f)=∣u∣2;B1​​+∣f∣0,α;B1​​, where the latter is the full Hölder norm of the forcing.
Fix 0<δ<1. If the asserted estimate failed, rescaling the functions by their global Hessian matrix Hölder seminorm would produce uj​,fj​ with
H(uj​;B1​)=1,H(uj​;B1/2​)>δ+jQ(uj​,fj​).
(26)
In particular Q(uj​,fj​)→0. Choose xj​,yj​∈B1/2​ for which
∣D2uj​(yj​)−D2uj​(xj​)∣>2δ​∣yj​−xj​∣α.
(27)
The supremum norm of D2uj​ tends to zero, so rj​=∣yj​−xj​∣→0. Let Pj​ be the quadratic Taylor polynomial of uj​ at xj​, and define
Uj​(z)=rj2+α​uj​(xj​+rj​z)−Pj​(xj​+rj​z)​.
(28)
The domains contain B1/(2rj​)​, and Uj​,DUj​,D2Uj​ vanish at zero. Moreover
[D2Uj​]α​≤1,ΔUj​(z)=rj−α​(fj​(xj​+rj​z)−fj​(xj​)).
(29)
The right side is bounded by [fj​]α​∣z∣α and tends to zero locally uniformly. The normalized Hessian matrix bound controls ∣D2Uj​(z)∣≤∣z∣α; integration along line segments then bounds DUj​,Uj​ on every compact Euclidean ball. Arzelà-Ascoli theorem and a diagonal subsequence argument give convergence in C2 on compact subsets to an entire harmonic function U, with [D2U]α;Rn​≤1 and D2U(0)=0.
After a further subsequence zj​=(yj​−xj​)/rj​→z∗​ with ∣z∗​∣=1. The normalization ensures ∣D2U(z∗​)∣≥δ/2. But each second derivative of U is harmonic and has finite global Hölder seminorm with exponent below one. The supplied Liouville theorem makes each derivative constant; it is the globally Hölder case of the polynomial-growth Liouville theorem for harmonic functions. Its value at zero makes it zero, a contradiction. This blow-up compactness proof of an interior Schauder estimate establishes
H(u;B1/2​)≤δH(u;B1​)+Cn,α,δ​Q(u,f).​
(30)
To absorb the term on the larger Euclidean ball, rescale this estimate to Euclidean balls contained in Bs​. For 1/2≤r<s<1, pairs separated by less than (s−r)/2 are controlled in a Euclidean ball centred at their first point; farther pairs are controlled directly by ∥D2u∥∞​. Thus
H(u;Br​)≤δH(u;Bs​)+Cδ​(s−r)−2−αQ(u,f).
(31)
This is the covering step in the Simon absorption lemma. Take rj​=1−2−j−1 and fix δ<2−2−α. Iterating makes the remainder δjH(u;Brj​​) tend to zero, since the global seminorm is finite; the error terms form a convergent geometric series. Consequently
[D2u]α;B1/2​​≤Cn,α​(∣u∣2;B1​​+∣f∣0,α;B1​​).​
(32)
For the requested failure of the weaker estimate, take um​=m−2sin(mx1​) and fm​=−sin(mx1​). The derivative supremum norm ∣um​∣2​ and ∥fm​∥∞​ remain bounded, but comparison at 0 and πe1​/(2m), both in B1/2​ for large m, gives
[D2um​]α;B1/2​​≥(2m/π)α⟶∞.​
(33)
This proves the necessity of Hölder forcing for Schauder estimates. Every member of the sequence is smooth; failure comes from the increasing frequency, not from any lack of individual regularity.

Adopt the sign convention that a weak supersolution for Lu=Di​(aijDj​u) satisfies Lu≤0, or ∫aijDj​uDi​φ≥0 for every nonnegative compactly supported test function. For a nonnegative supersolution, the Weak Harnack inequality gives some q>0 and CH​≥1, depending only on n,λ,Λ, such that
(∣B1/2​∣1​∫B1/2​​uqdx)1/q≤CH​B1/2​essinf​u.​
(34)
Here ∣E∣−1∫E​ denotes the average over E; equivalently the right side gives a lower bound for the essential infimum. One may take a sufficiently small positive exponent, so no assumption q≥1 is needed. The inequality is unchanged under translations and dilations of the Euclidean balls.
Since a Sobolev space element is defined up to changes on a null set, the infimum is initially essential. After choosing a continuous representative it is the ordinary infimum. Without choosing such a representative, an arbitrary pointwise infimum cannot be bounded by an integral.
