Linearity of Stokes flow and rotational symmetry, including reflection in mathematics invariance of the spherical geometry, determine the possible rigid motions before any calculation. Translation is a polar vector; its linear dependence on a single vector A must be cA. A traceless second-rank tensor B cannot produce a polar vector by an isotropic linear map: tensor contraction with the identity matrix vanishes and tensor contraction with the totally antisymmetric tensor vanishes because B is a symmetric second-rank tensor. Angular velocity is an axial vector. Neither A nor a symmetric B can produce one by an isotropic linear map. Products such as A×BA are excluded by Linearity of Stokes flow. Thus Ω=0.
Use the Unscaled Papkovich–Neuber representation, with harmonic functions Φ and χ:
u=∇(x⋅Φ+χ)−2Φ,p−p∞​=2μ∇⋅Φ.
(1)
Indeed ∇⋅u=0 and μ∇2u=∇p because each potential is a harmonic function. Put r=∣x∣ and S=x⋅Bx. The decaying harmonic functions with the necessary angular dependence are A⋅x/r3, Bx/r3 and S/r5. The last is a harmonic function precisely because trB=0. A 1/r Stokeslet is excluded by the force-free condition, and a rotlet by the torque-free condition. Try
Φ=dr3Bx​,χ=cr3A⋅x​+er5S​.
(2)
The resulting Stokes flow is
u=c(r3A​−r53(A⋅x)x​)−3dr5Sx​+e(r52Bx​−r75Sx​).
(3)
At r=a, the coefficient of n(A⋅n) fixes c=a3/3; the remaining constant vector gives U=−2A/3. In the B mode, matching Bn−(n⋅Bn)n gives e=a4/2 and d=−a2/2. Therefore the complete two-mode tensorial squirmer flow is
u(x)p−p∞​​=3r3a3​[A−3(A⋅x)x]+2r53a2Sx​+2a4​(r52Bx​−r75Sx​),=3μa2r5S​,U=−32​A,Ω=0.​​
(4)
The boundary condition is satisfied in the laboratory frame, so the Stokes flow tends to zero at infinity. The potential dipole in the A mode gives an irrotational flow and decays as r−3; the leading B mode is a stresslet, decaying as r−2. Neither carries a net force or torque. Direct surface integral averaging with the surface slip velocity formula gives the same translation, providing an independent check. Uniqueness of Stokes flow then identifies this decaying solution.

Let e=X/R, s=e⋅Be, and A∥​=A⋅e. Applied forces and couples below are forces and torques on the solid; by force balance their values are also the strengths exerted by that solid on the fluid. This fixes the signs in Faxén's first law and Faxén's rotational law. To hold the passive sphere fixed, set its translation and angular velocity to zero in those laws. The incident two-mode tensorial squirmer flow gives
Fh​=−6πμa(u(X)+6a2​∇2u(X)),Gh​=−4πμa3ω(X).
(5)
Only the stresslet part has vorticity:
ω(x)=3a2r5(Bx)×x​.
(6)
Consequently, for fixed A,B as a/R→0,
Fh​Gh​​=−R29πμa3​se−R32πμa4​(A−3A∥​e)+O(R4μa5∥B∥​+R7μa8∣A∣​),=−R512πμa5​(BX)×X+higher reflections.​​
(7)
The leading force is the radial stresslet force. Its next correction, O(μa4∣A∣/R3), comes from the incident potential dipole. The next B correction, O(μa5∥B∥/R4), contains both the swimmer's r−4 field and the Laplacian term in Faxén's first law. If the relevant coefficient vanishes, these next terms must be retained instead of calling the vanished term a nonzero leading approximation. Further exchanges in the method of reflections for Stokes flow are smaller still.
The passive sphere reflects primarily a Stokeslet with strength Fh​. At the swimmer, its velocity is (I+ee)Fh​/(8πμR). The unchanged surface slip velocity adds the free swimming velocity to the incident-flow contribution in Faxén's first law. Thus
δU​=−4R39a3​se−4R4a4​(A−5A∥​e)+O(R5a5∥B∥​+R6a6∣A∣​).​​
(8)
The leading change is O(a3∥B∥/R3); its next correction is the displayed O(a4∣A∣/R4) term. Finite-radius Faxén's first law corrections, the passive sphere's reflected stresslet, and its rotlet contribute at order a5∥B∥/R5 to translation.
For a Stokeslet at the origin,
uF​(x)=8πμ1​(rF​+r3(F⋅x)x​).
(9)
The curl of the first term is F×x/r3; that of the second is also F×x/r3, before multiplying by 1/(8πμ). Hence ωF​=F×x/(4πμr3). At the swimmer use displacement −X. The leading B force is parallel to X, so its Stokeslet has zero vorticity there. This eliminates the putative O(a3∥B∥/R4) rotation. The potential dipole itself has no vorticity, but the holding force it induces is not radial. Applying Faxén's rotational law to its reflected Stokeslet yields
Ω=21​4πμR3Fh,A​×(−X)​=4R6a4​A×X+O(R6a5∥B∥​+R7a6∣A∣​).​
(10)
The passive sphere's rotlet and reflected stresslet can affect that last order, so they cannot restore the larger missing rotation. When A×X=0, the boxed coefficient is zero and the higher-order terms determine any rotation.

Take z positive downwards. The leading axial velocity of a slender viscous thread is independent of radius. Incompressible flow then gives ur​=−rw′/2, while steady mass conservation gives (a2w)′=0. The Newtonian fluid stress tensor has σrr​=−p−μw′ and σzz​=−p+2μw′. The Young–Laplace equation, neglecting axial curvature at slender order, gives σrr​=−pe​−γ/a and hence p=pe​+γ/a−μw′.
The axial cut transmits both the integrated Newtonian fluid stress tensor and the circumferential surface tension. After subtracting the ambient pressure, its tensile force is
T=πa2(3μw′−γ/a)+2πγa=3πμa2w′+πγa.
(11)
The coefficient three is the Trouton ratio for axial extension. The last term is essential: the capillary tensile force of a slender thread is πγa, not 2πγa, because the capillary pressure subtracts half the interfacial line contribution. Axial force balance is T′+πρga2=0. Together the governing equations are
(a2w)′=0,3μ(a2w′)′+ρga2+γa′=0.​
(12)
Define the dimensionless variables
ℓ=ρgμw0​​​,Z=ℓz​,W=w0​w​,Γ=μa0​w0​γℓ​=ρga0​ℓγ​.
(13)
Then a=a0​W−1/2 and the axial equation becomes
3dZd​(WWZ​​)+W1​+ΓdZd​W−1/2=0.
(14)
For the increasing power-law branch, all three terms have the same dependence when α=2. Substitution leaves 1−6k2−Γk=0. Thus
W=(1+kZ)2,α=2,k=12Γ2+24​−Γ​.​
(15)
This remains valid at Γ=0, when k=1/6​. The other root is negative and describes a decreasing local branch up to its singular endpoint, not a thread accelerating downwards indefinitely. Positive surface tension decreases k, so it slows the fall at a given height on this branch. Thinning reduces the capillary tensile force πγa. Its negative axial derivative supplies an upward capillary force on a thread segment, balancing part of the weight. The viscous tensile force therefore supplies a smaller remaining balance; on the quadratic family this reduces the stretching rate.
The condition W(0)=1 alone does not select a unique solution of this second-order ordinary differential equation. The boxed power law is the requested verified branch, with tensile force tending to zero at large height; an additional nozzle tension or downstream condition is required to select it physically. The slender, negligible-inertia approximation must remain valid over the height range considered.

Write D=Re​−Ri​, S=Re​+Ri​, A=Re2​−Ri2​=DS, and Δp=pi​−pe​. Incompressible flow with axial strain rate E gives
ur​=−2E​r+rC(t)​.

Its radial Stokes equation gives pr​=0, because ur,rr​+ur,r​/r−ur​/r2=0. The radial Newtonian fluid stress tensor component is σrr​=−p−μE−2μC/r2. The outer and inner Young–Laplace equation conditions have opposite curvature signs:
σrr​(Re​)=−pe​−Re​γ​,σrr​(Ri​)=−pi​+Ri​γ​.
(17)
Subtracting and eliminating C gives
C=2μARi2​Re2​​[Δp−γ(Ri​1​+Re​1​)],p=pe​−AΔpRi2​​+AγS​−μE.​
(18)
In particular, the boxed pressure is equivalent to the printed identity for (−p−μE)A. For equal gas pressures, C=−γRi​Re​/(2μD), so surface tension draws both interfaces inward in addition to the imposed extension.
The kinematic boundary condition is R˙j​=−ERj​/2+C/Rj​ at either interface. Taking the difference, and then the difference of squared radii, gives
D˙=−2E​D+2μγ​−2μSΔpRi​Re​​,A˙=−EA,A(t)=A(0)e−Et.​
(19)
The cancellation of C in the area equation is mass conservation: axial stretching reduces the liquid area while radial redistribution changes the hole size. For E=0, equal pressures, and Ri​(0)=b, Re​(0)=2b, one has A=3b2 and D=b+γt/(2μ). Since S=A/D,
Ri​=21​(DA​−D),Re​=21​(DA​+D).
(20)
Closure occurs at D=A​=3​b, so
t∗​=γ2μb​(3​−1).​
(21)
This capillary collapse of an annular viscous cylinder has finite closure time for γ>0 and b>0. For γ=0 it does not close in this case. The annular formula is used only up to closure; the singular cylindrical inner curvature is not a boundary condition on the subsequent solid cylinder.

Use A=Re2​−Ri2​, S=Re​+Ri​, D=Re​−Ri​, and Q=Aw=A(0)w0​. Locally replace E in the preceding annular viscous extension calculation by w′(z). Its leading pressure is
p=pe​−A(pi​−pe​)Ri2​​+AγS​−μw′.
(22)
The axial liquid stress relative to ambient pressure, together with the two circumferential surface tension forces, transmits a cut force
Tcut​/π=3μAw′+(pi​−pe​)Ri2​+γS.
(23)
It would be incorrect simply to differentiate this and retain an extra gas-pressure term. The sloping inner gas interface also exerts an axial pressure force −π(pi​−pe​)(Ri2​)′ per unit height, after subtracting the common ambient pressure contribution. For constant gas pressures these terms cancel. The resulting capillary tensile force of a hollow slender thread therefore gives
3μ(Aw′)′+ρgA+γS′=0,Aw=Q.​
(24)
Thus the printed axial equation holds even for unequal constant gas pressures; their influence remains in the radial evolution.
For equal pressures, the kinematic boundary condition from the previous calculation becomes wD′=−w′D/2+γ/(2μ). Multiplying by the appropriate integrating factor gives
dzd​(Dw​)=2μw​γ​,D(z)w(z)​=D(0)w0​​+2μγ​∫0z​w(ζ)​dζ​.
(25)
At closure Ri​=0, so D=A​ and Dw​=Q​. Hence
2μw0​​γ​∫0z∗​​w(ζ)​dζ​=A(0)​−Re​(0)+Ri​(0).​
(26)
The right side is positive for a genuine annulus. On the gravity-driven quadratic family from the previous part, w1/2 grows linearly with height and this integral diverges logarithmically. It must then reach the finite positive threshold at a finite height. This explains the expected capillary closure of a falling hollow thread on that freely falling branch without requiring its full coupled profile.
There is a real boundary-data limitation to that expectation. Neither the printed nozzle speed nor the local equations alone guarantee finite closure for every imposed nozzle tension. To see this within the same leading model, define T=3μQw′/w+γS, so T′=−ρgQ/w. For equal pressures and w′>0, both radii decrease and S≤S0​. If
c=3μQT0​/2−γS0​​>0,cw0​ρgQ​<2T0​​,
(27)
then a bootstrap gives T>T0​/2 and w≥w0​ecz: the maximum accumulated weight is smaller than the assumed tension margin. Consequently the closure integral, including its prefactor, is at most γ/(μcw0​). If this is smaller than A0​​−D0​, closure never occurs at finite height. For example, in consistent units choose μ=ρg=w0​=1, Ri​(0)=λ, Re​(0)=2λ, γ=λ, T0​=100λ2. Then c=47/9, the weight bound is 27λ2/47<50λ2, and 9λ/47<(3​−1)λ. Small λ makes the initial geometry slender. Thus finite closure is the expected freely falling branch behaviour, not a theorem following from nozzle speed alone. If γ=0 there is no capillary closure by this mechanism.

First supply the introductory scaling and geometry. The leading tangential Stokes equation in a thin gap balances P/L against μU/H2. The shear stress scale is τ∼μU/H, so lubrication pressure dominates shear stress:
P∼H2μUL​,Pτ​∼LH​≪1.​
(28)
The outer circle, viewed along a ray r(cosθ,sinθ) from the inner centre, satisfies r2−2αΔrsinθ+α2Δ2=(a+Δ)2. Expanding its positive root to first order in Δ/a gives r=a+Δ+αΔsinθ+O(Δ2/a), hence h=Δ(1+αsinθ).
Take positive Ω to mean increasing θ, and put s=aθ, y=r−a, U=Ωa. With no-slip boundary conditions at y=0,h, lubrication theory gives
uθ​=U(1−hy​)+2μps​​y(y−h),q=2Uh​−12μh3​ps​.
(29)
Steady mass conservation makes q independent of θ. Single-valued pressure requires ∫02π​pθ​dθ=0. Write In​=∫02π​(1+αsinθ)−ndθ and Dα​=1+α2/2. It follows that
q=2ΩaΔ​I3​I2​​=2ΩaΔ​Dα​1−α2​,ps​=h26μΩa​−h312μq​.​
(30)
The tangential fluid tractions on the inner and outer solids, measured in the same increasing-θ direction, are respectively
τi​=μuθ,y​(0)=h26μq​−h4μΩa​,τo​=−μuθ,y​(h)=h26μq​−h2μΩa​.​
(31)
This sign convention distinguishes the traction on a wall from the corresponding stress component in the fluid.
To leading order the holding force balances the pressure traction on the inner circle. Integration by parts gives
Fx​=a∫02π​pcosθdθ=−a∫02π​pθ​sinθdθ.
(32)
Use pθ​=aps​ and ∫sinθ/(1+αsinθ)ndθ=(In−1​−In​)/α, with the continuous limit at α=0. Substituting the boxed flux yields
Fx​=−Δ26πμΩa3​1−α2​Dα​α​,Fz​=0.​
(33)
The zero vertical component also follows from reflection in mathematics in the vertical line: this leaves the geometry and vertical force unchanged, reverses Ω, and Linearity of Stokes flow then requires Fz​=0.
Pressure gives no moment about either circular wall's own centre. Integrating the viscous shear torque with lever arm a to leading order gives the fluid couples
Ci​=−Δ1−α2​2πμΩa3​Dα​1+2α2​,Co​=ΔDα​2πμΩa31−α2​​.​
(34)
Indeed Ci​=a2∫τi​dθ and Co​=a2∫τo​dθ at this order. Applied holding or driving couples are the negatives of these fluid couples. They need not cancel because they are moments about different axes. The fluid force on the outer solid equals the holding force Fx​ on the inner one. Transferring its moment to the inner axis adds −αΔFx​, so angular momentum balance about a common axis is
Ci​+Co​=αΔFx​.​
(35)
For α=0 the usual equal and opposite concentric couples are recovered. All these formulas are leading thin-gap expressions at fixed ∣α∣<1; taking the contact limit requires maintaining the local slender-gap assumptions.

Put the fixed outer centre at the origin, and let the moving inner centre be d=(x,z). This reverses the sign of the vertical offset used in the previous parts: now h=Δ−d⋅n. At concentricity rotation produces no force and translation has isotropic resistance R0​=12πμa3/Δ3. Balancing the external load gives
x˙(0)=0,z˙(0)=−12πμa3FΔ3​.​
(39)
There is no inertial acceleration in this Stokes flow model; velocity is determined instantaneously by force balance.
For the subsequent motion, the full-film journal bearing assumption matters: pressure is single-valued around the whole annulus, with no specified cavitation boundary. Let r=∣d∣, e=r/Δ, ϕ=arg(x+iz), and er​,eϕ​ be its radial and tangential unit vectors. The squeeze resistance of an eccentric journal bearing has radial and tangential coefficients
Rr​=(1−e2)3/2R0​​,Rt​=1−e2​(1+e2/2)R0​​.
(40)
For completeness, rotate coordinates until d points along x, so h=Δ(1−ecosθ). Radial translation gives squeezing flux q=avr​sinθ and resistance proportional to ∫sin2θ/(1−ecosθ)3=π/(1−e2)3/2. Tangential translation gives q=avt​[−cosθ+3e/(2+e2)]; the constant enforces periodic pressure. Its resistance uses
∫(1−ecosθ)3cos2θ​dθ−I3​[∫cosθ/(1−ecosθ)3dθ]2​=1−e2​(1+e2/2)π​.
(41)
These expressions supply both translation directions; retaining only the vertical squeezing formula would miss the moving axle's horizontal dynamics.
The rotational fluid force from the earlier calculation points along eϕ​, with magnitude Rt​Ωr/2. Superposing rotation and translation by Linearity of Stokes flow, and resolving the load, gives
Rr​r˙=−Fsinϕ,Rt​r(ϕ˙​−2Ω​)=−Fcosϕ.​
(42)
For positive Ω the axle initially moves down, then to the right because the rotation-generated force is perpendicular to the downward eccentricity. The equilibrium is displaced horizontally to the right, at the same height as the outer axis. Its eccentricity 0<e∗​<1 is the unique solution
x∗​=Δe∗​,z∗​=0,F=Δ26πμΩa3​1−e∗2​​(1+e∗2​/2)e∗​​.​
(43)
The rotational force then points upwards and balances the load. For negative Ω the displacement is to the left, using ∣Ω∣ in the magnitude equation. Uniqueness follows because the logarithmic derivative of e/[1−e2​(1+e2/2)] has positive numerator 1−e2/2+e4 after a common positive denominator. If Ω=0 and F>0, no separated static equilibrium exists in this ideal model. If F=0, concentricity remains a solution.
One should not assert that this full-film model necessarily settles to the equilibrium. It predicts full-film journal-bearing whirl. For F,Ω>0, eliminate time from the two boxed evolution equations and put K=6πμΩa3/Δ2. The orbit starting at concentricity obeys
ded​x+2(1−e2)3e​x=2FΩΔ2R0​​(1−e2)3/2e​,
(44)
so, with its zero initial horizontal displacement,
Δx​=5F2K​[(1−e2)−1/2−(1−e2)3/4],z=±Δe2−(x/Δ)2​.​
(45)
The orbit begins on the lower branch, turns at z=0 before contact, and returns along the upper branch, forming a closed orbit through concentricity. The ratio of the right-hand side to e increases from zero to infinity, giving a unique turning eccentricity below one. Linearizing at equilibrium likewise gives δr˙=−(F/Rr,∗​)δϕ and δϕ˙​=c∗​δr with c∗​>0: the eigenvalues are imaginary, not damped. Thus the equilibrium position exists, but the initially concentric axle circulates around it in the stated ideal full-film approximation. Cavitation or additional mechanical constraints would change the model; none were specified.
