The general-order Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem applies to analytic data on a non-characteristic hypersurface. In local coordinates (t,z), a real analytic system of order m in an unknown vector U is in normal form when
∂tm​U=F(t,z,{∂tj​∂zα​U:j<m, j+∣α∣≤m}),
(1)
with F real analytic near the initial jet. Analytic data ∂tj​U(0,z)=hj​(z) for 0≤j<m determine a unique real analytic solution near each initial point. More general systems, including different orders for the components, have the same conclusion after solving for the highest normal derivatives: the corresponding coefficient matrix, or highest-derivative Jacobian for a nonlinear system, must be invertible. This is the system's non-characteristic hypersurface condition. The uniqueness asserted by the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem is initially uniqueness in the real analytic class.
A real analytic coordinate change flattens a real analytic initial hypersurface. Non-characteristicity permits solving for its highest normal derivatives by the real analytic implicit function theorem. Introduce all derivatives through order m−1 as additional unknowns. Their normal derivatives are either another jet variable, a tangential first derivative of a jet variable, or the right-hand side of the original equation. This gives a first-order real analytic system. The derivative-compatibility identities have zero initial data and are preserved by the system; equivalently, its real analytic coefficient recurrence reproduces the derivatives of U. This explains both reductions, rather than assuming arbitrary real analytic hypersurfaces are already flat.
For the first example, the principal symbol of the Laplace equation at the conormal dx1​ is 1, so the plane is a non-characteristic hypersurface. The theorem therefore applies to real analytic u∣x1​=0​ and ∂1​u∣x1​=0​:
analytic local existence and analytic uniqueness hold for Laplace Cauchy data.​
(2)
This does not give Hadamard well-posedness in smooth or Sobolev space norms. For n≥2, the harmonic functions
uN​(x)=Ne−N​​sinh(Nx1​)cos(Nx2​)
(3)
have zero value data and normal derivative data e−N​cos(Nx2​) tending to zero with every fixed tangential derivative. At any fixed x1​=δ>0, however, uN​(δ,0) diverges. Thus the elliptic Cauchy problem for a partial differential equation is unstable despite real analytic solvability. The exceptional case n=1 is the elementary affine ordinary differential equation and has no such tangential high-frequency instability.

For the wave equation, the principal symbol is −τ2+∣ξ∣2, so its value on the conormal dt is −1. Consequently the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem applies to real analytic displacement and velocity data on this non-characteristic hypersurface:
the theorem applies to the wave equation at t=0.​

For the heat equation, viewed as a second-order equation in all (t,x) variables, the principal symbol is ∣ξ∣2. It vanishes on dt, so t=0 is a characteristic hypersurface and
the ordinary Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem does not apply at t=0.​
(5)
Writing ut​=Δx​u does not repair its hypotheses: the right side has second spatial derivatives, exceeding the first-order normal-form allowance. Indeed real analytic initial data need not produce a jointly real analytic solution through time zero. In one dimension, u0​(x)=(1+x2)−1 would force
∂tk​u(0,0)=∂x2k​u0​(0)=(−1)k(2k)!.
(6)
The time Taylor coefficients (−1)k(2k)!/k! have zero radius of convergence.
Nevertheless the forward heat equation has Hadamard well-posedness in standard L2 or Sobolev space classes for t≥0; its Fourier multiplier e−t∣ξ∣2 gives stable smoothing. Backward evolution has the growing multiplier e∣t∣∣ξ∣2 and is unstable. Neither forward stability nor backward instability is decided simply by whether the real analytic theorem applies.

P∗v=∣α∣≤m∑​(−1)∣α∣∂α(aα​v).​

u(t,x)=u0​(A(t,x)).​
(40)
It is C1, and the chain rule makes its value constant along every characteristic curve. The same calculation proves classical uniqueness. No boundedness of u0​ is needed for this classical statement.
For u0​∈L∞, a bounded weak solution on the positive half-line is defined by
∫0∞​∫Rd​u[φt​+divx​(Fφ)]dxdt+∫Rd​u0​(x)φ(0,x)dx=0
(41)
for every compactly supported C1 test function on [0,∞)×Rd. The analogous backward-time identity defines the solution on all real time. The udivF term is essential: this is transport, not the conservative equation ut​+div(Fu)=0.
The same inverse-flow formula defines the unique weak solution, with ∥u(t)∥∞​=∥u0​∥∞​, equality almost everywhere, and a representative continuous into L1 on each compact spatial set. For a direct uniqueness argument, change variables x=X(t,a) in the weak identity and put w(t,a)=u(t,X(t,a)). Using Jt​=(divF)(t,X)J gives
∫w∂t​(Jζ)dadt+∫u0​(a)ζ(0,a)da=0,
(42)
initially for C1 compactly supported ζ. Since J>0 and J,Jt​ are continuous on compact sets, approximation in the spatial parameter permits ζ=η/J: both ζ and ζt​ are uniformly approximable there, which are the only derivatives left in this identity. Hence ∫wηt​+∫u0​η(0)=0 and w=u0​ almost everywhere. This also verifies existence by reversing the argument, and avoids assuming second derivatives of the C1 field. Local L1 continuity follows by approximating u0​ by continuous functions on compact flow tubes.
This is the weak transport solution under characteristic coordinates. The weak-strong uniqueness principle says that a weak solution in the specified class agrees with a classical solution having the same data for as long as that classical solution exists. Here it holds globally in the bounded class whenever the classical initial data are also bounded.

For a bounded C1 classical solution and a smooth convex entropy η, let its entropy flux for a scalar conservation law satisfy ψ′=f′η′. The chain rule gives
∂t​η(u)+∂x​ψ(u)=η′(u)(ut​+f′(u)ux​)=0.

Approximate a convex piecewise C1 entropy by smooth convex functions on the bounded state range. Their values and flux antiderivatives converge uniformly, so the integrated equality passes to the limit. Therefore every bounded classical solution is an entropy solution. The bounded qualifier is required by the definition's L∞ membership; an arbitrary unbounded C1 solution would not satisfy that membership condition.
