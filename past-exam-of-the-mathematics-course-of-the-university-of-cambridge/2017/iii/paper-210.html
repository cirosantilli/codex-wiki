For a kernel for density estimation K with integral one and a smoothing bandwidth h>0, the univariate kernel density estimator is
Kh​(u)=h1​K(u/h),f​h​(x)=n1​i=1∑n​Kh​(x−Xi​).​
(1)
Its expected value is
Ef​h​(x)=n1​∑i=1n​∫R​Kh​(x−y)f(y)dy=∫R​Kh​(x−y)f(y)dy=(Kh​∗f)(x).
(2)
This is the convolution identity. It holds wherever the integral is defined, in particular everywhere for the bounded kernel used below. For a nonnegative kernel for density estimation, Tonelli theorem also gives the identity as an extended nonnegative integral. A signed kernel for density estimation instead requires absolute integrability for this interchange.
For the centred unit-width uniform kernel, substitute u=(x−y)/h:
Ef​h​(x)=∫−1/21/2​f(x−hu)du.
(3)
Put M=supz​∣f′′(z)∣. The Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder gives
f(x−hu)=f(x)−huf′(x)+R(x,u),∣R(x,u)∣≤2Mh2u2​.
(4)
The odd function term integrates to zero, while ∫−1/21/2​u2du=1/12. Hence the bias of a kernel density estimator obeys
​Ef​h​(x)−f(x)​≤24Mh2​.​
(5)
This is a second-order pointwise bias bound for kernel density estimation. Existence and boundedness of the second derivative suffice for the stated remainder; continuity of that derivative is not an extra assumption.
For concentration, write
Bi​=1{∣Xi​−x∣≤h/2}​,p=P(∣X1​−x∣≤h/2).
(6)
The Bi​ are independent and identically distributed random variables with a Bernoulli distribution, and f​h​(x)=(nh)−1∑i​Bi​. The required concentration bound follows from the Hoeffding lemma. We prove its Bernoulli case directly. With
ψ(s)=logEes(B1​−p),
(7)
we have ψ(0)=ψ′(0)=0 and ψ′′(s)=ps​(1−ps​)≤1/4, where ps​=pes/(1−p+pes) is the exponentially tilted success probability. Therefore ψ(s)≤s2/8 for every real s. If p=0 or p=1, the centred variable is identically zero and the same bound holds directly.
Independence and the exponential form of the Markov inequality now give, for s>0,
P(∑i​(Bi​−p)≥nht)≤exp(−snht+8ns2​).
(8)
Choose s=4ht. Applying the same argument to the lower tail and adding the probabilities proves the Hoeffding inequality
P(​f​h​(x)−Ef​h​(x)​≥t)≤2e−2nh2t2.​
(9)
To obtain the requested variance constant, use the additional bound that a probability is at most one. Set Z=f​h​(x)−Ef​h​(x) and a=2nh2. The tail integral formula for moments, justified by Tonelli theorem, yields
Var(f​h​(x))​=EZ2=∫0∞​P(Z2>u)du≤∫0∞​min{1,2e−au}du=alog2​+∫log2/a∞​2e−audu=a1+log2​.​
(10)
Thus
Var(f​h​(x))≤2nh21+log2​.​
(11)
The clipping at one is essential for this integration argument to give that constant. It is an instance of a second moment bound from a clipped Gaussian tail. The exact Bernoulli distribution calculation also gives Var(f​h​(x))=p(1−p)/(nh2)≤1/(4nh2), an even stronger bound.

Choose a Borel measurable function version of the posterior success probability η:Rd→[0,1]. Conditional on X=x, predicting zero has error probability η(x) and predicting one has error probability 1−η(x). A Bayes classifier for zero-one loss is therefore
CBayes(x)=1{η(x)≥1/2}​,R∗=Emin{η(X),1−η(X)}.​
(12)
Either decision is optimal when η(x)=1/2. The displayed Bayes risk also equals ∫min{η(x),1−η(x)}dPX​(x) and is unaffected by changing η on a PX​-null set.
For any measurable set A⊆Rd, independence and the uniform distribution of U1​ give
P(X1​∈A,Y1​=1)​=∫A​P(U1​≤η(x))dPX​(x)=∫A​η(x)dPX​(x)=P(X∈A,Y=1).​
(13)
Likewise the probability with label zero is ∫A​(1−η(x))dPX​(x). These sets determine the joint probability distribution, so
(X1​,Y1​) =d (X,Y).​
(14)
The same construction makes all training pairs independent and identically distributed random variables.
Fix a feature-measurable nearest-neighbour tie-breaking rule, for example increasing original index for observations at equal Euclidean norm distance from the query. This is important: the ordering may depend on the features, but not on the Ui​ or labels. Let Y(j)​(x) be the label attached to the jth ordered feature. The classifier from the K-nearest neighbors algorithm is
Cnknn​(x)=1{k−1∑j=1k​Y(j)​(x)≥1/2}​.​
(15)
The displayed rule resolves a tied vote in favour of one; any fixed vote convention would also define the method. When k=1, this is the one-nearest-neighbour classifier Cn1nn​(x)=Y(1)​(x).
Write In​(x) for the selected original index. Conditional on all features X1​,…,Xn​, it is fixed, and UIn​(x)​ is still uniform on [0,1]. The two decisions being compared are
Cn1nn​(x)=1{UIn​(x)​≤η(XIn​(x)​)}​,Cn1nn​(x)=1{UIn​(x)​≤η(x)}​.
(16)
The latter is an oracle comparison rule: its relabeling is recomputed at each query x, rather than being a fixed relabeling of the training data for all queries. For a variable U with the uniform distribution on [0,1], the two threshold indicators at r,s∈[0,1] differ exactly when U lies between them, an interval of length ∣r−s∣. This Bernoulli coupling by a shared uniform random variable proves
P{Cn1nn​(x)=Cn1nn​(x)}=E∣η(X(1)​(x))−η(x)∣.​
(17)
Take the test pair (X,Y) independent of the training data and its auxiliary uniforms. Let Tn​=σ((Xi​,Yi​,Ui​):1≤i≤n), and interpret L(C) as conditional misclassification risk given Tn​. The tower property of conditional expectation gives EL(C)=P(C(X)=Y). Conditional on the test feature X=x and all training features, Cn1nn​(x) and Y are independent variables with a Bernoulli distribution of parameter η(x). Consequently, for every n,
EL(Cn1nn​)=E[2η(X){1−η(X)}].​
(18)
This is an exact finite-sample equality, not merely a limit.
The two error indicators can differ only if the two classifiers disagree, so
​EL(Cn1nn​)−EL(Cn1nn​)​​≤P{Cn1nn​(X)=Cn1nn​(X)}=E∣η(X(1)​(X))−η(X)∣.​
(19)
The permitted nearest-neighbour approximation theorem states that the last expected value tends to zero for the feature-based selection rule. This establishes the one-nearest-neighbour asymptotic risk
n→∞lim​EL(Cn1nn​)=n→∞lim​EL(Cn1nn​)=E[2η(X){1−η(X)}].​
(20)
For r∈[0,1], put a=min(r,1−r)∈[0,1/2]. Then 2r(1−r)=2a(1−a), and a≤2a(1−a)≤2a. Integrating gives the Bayes risk bound for one-nearest-neighbour classification
R∗≤n→∞lim​EL(Cn1nn​)≤2R∗.​
(21)
Thus a one-nearest-neighbour classifier need not achieve the Bayes risk: for constant η=1/4, its limiting misclassification risk is 3/8, while R∗=1/4.
Feature-based neighbour tie-breaking is required. The convention cannot be dropped for arbitrary PX​. For a counterexample, take Xi​=X=0 almost surely and constant η=1/4, and choose among the coincident neighbours the one with the smallest Ui​. Then the predicted label is one with probability 1−(3/4)n, so its misclassification risk tends to 3/4, exceeding the claimed upper bound 2R∗=1/2. The corrected theorem uses ties determined independently of the uniforms and labels, as above.

Let p=dP/dν and q=dQ/dν be Radon-Nikodym derivatives. Use the following normalizations of the Kullback-Leibler divergence, total variation distance and unnormalized Hellinger distance:
KL(P,Q)TV(P,Q)h(P,Q)2​=∫plog(p/q)dν,=A∈Asup​∣P(A)−Q(A)∣=21​∫∣p−q∣dν,=∫(p​−q​)2dν.​​
(22)
We use 0log(0/q)=0 and assign infinite Kullback-Leibler divergence if Pot≪Q, where ≪ denotes absolute continuity of measures. Common domination alone also suffices for the inequalities with the extended-value convention. The negative part of the logarithmic integral is integrable: on p<q, plog(q/p)≤q−p≤q. Thus the extended-value integral defining the Kullback-Leibler divergence is well-defined. These expressions do not depend on which common dominating measure is chosen. The Kullback-Leibler divergence is generally asymmetric and therefore fails a defining property of a metric.
For completeness, the integral formula for total variation distance follows by taking A={p≥q}: since ∫(p−q)dν=0, the positive and negative parts of p−q have the same integral, and this set attains the supremum.
Put a=∫pq​dν≤1, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. A second application gives
TV(P,Q)​=21​∫∣p​−q​∣(p​+q​)dν≤2h(P,Q)​(∫(p​+q​)2dν)1/2=2h​2+2a​≤h.​
(23)
The logarithmic inequality in the hint implies logt≤t−1 for t>0. Apply it to t=q/p​ on {p>0}:
−log(q/p)≥2(1−q/p​).
(24)
Multiplying by p and integrating proves
KL(P,Q)≥2−2∫pq​dν=h(P,Q)2.
(25)
If q=0<p on a set of positive P-measure, the right side is finite but the Kullback-Leibler divergence is infinite, so the inequality is immediate. Thus
TV(P,Q)≤h(P,Q)≤KL(P,Q)​.​
(26)
This total variation–Hellinger–relative entropy inequality requires the stated Hellinger distance normalization for its first constant. With the alternative convention hˉ2=21​∫(p​−q​)2, the correct comparison is TV≤2​hˉ and hˉ≤KL/2​. In particular, P=Bernoulli(0.6) and Q=Bernoulli(0.4) have TV=0.2>hˉ≈0.1421.
Here is a testing form of the Le Cam two-point lemma. Suppose two parameter values θ0​,θ1​ in a metric space have separation Δ=d(θ0​,θ1​)>0, and let P0​,P1​ be the corresponding observation laws. Every estimator T satisfies
j=0,1max​Pj​{d(T,θj​)≥Δ/2}≥21−TV(P0​,P1​)​.​
(27)
To prove it, classify T by the closer of the two parameters, breaking a tie in favour of one. Let A={d(T,θ1​)≤d(T,θ0​)}. On A, the triangle inequality implies d(T,θ0​)≥Δ/2, while on Ac it implies d(T,θ1​)>Δ/2. Hence the sum of the two probabilities in the display is at least
P0​(A)+P1​(Ac)=1−{P1​(A)−P0​(A)}≥1−TV(P0​,P1​).
(28)
At least one is at least half the sum, proving the claim. It also gives a lower bound Δ(1−TV)/4 for the maximum expected metric loss.
For absolute-error loss, or any loss given by a metric, there is a stronger expectation form. Take densities p0​,p1​ relative to m=P0​+P1​ and use the triangle inequality under their overlap:
E0​d(T,θ0​)+E1​d(T,θ1​)​≥∫min(p0​,p1​){d(T,θ0​)+d(T,θ1​)}dm≥Δ∫min(p0​,p1​)dm=Δ{1−TV(P0​,P1​)}.​
(29)
Therefore the Le Cam lower bound under absolute-error loss is
j=0,1max​Ej​∣T−θj​∣≥2∣θ1​−θ0​∣​{1−TV(P0​,P1​)}.​
(30)
Both arguments are valid for randomized estimators as well, by adjoining the independent randomization to the observation; doing so does not change total variation distance.
For the normal distribution model, assume the usual nondegenerate convention σ>0 and use the full product observation laws. Choose
μ0​=0,μ1​=2n​σ​.
(31)
For one observation, its Gaussian log likelihood ratio is
logp1​(X)p0​(X)​=2σ2(X−μ1​)2−(X−μ0​)2​.
(32)
Under the first law, E0​(X−μ1​)2=σ2+(μ1​−μ0​)2 and E0​(X−μ0​)2=σ2. Thus the Kullback-Leibler divergence between normal distributions is
KL(N(μ0​,σ2),N(μ1​,σ2))=2σ2(μ1​−μ0​)2​.
(33)
For the independent and identically distributed random variables, the joint log likelihood ratio is the sum of the individual ones, so the Kullback-Leibler divergence of the two full statistical samples is n(μ1​−μ0​)2/(2σ2)=1/4. The comparison just proved implies TV(P0​,P1​)≤1/2. Applying the expectation form of the Le Cam two-point lemma gives
μ∈Rsup​Eμ​∣μ​−μ∣≥42n​σ​=n​c​,c=42​σ​>0.​
(34)
This is a Gaussian location minimax lower bound under absolute-error loss. The constant may depend on the fixed known σ, but neither on the estimator nor on n. If a signed nonzero scale were used, the same result holds with ∣σ∣ in place of σ. If zero noise were allowed, X1​=μ would estimate μ exactly, so a positive lower bound would be false; positive variance is necessary.

Use the natural cubic spline convention appropriate to the interior design knots: the spline is linear on [a,x1​] and [xn​,b]. We also need n≥2. These points will be important for uniqueness.
A cubic spline is a function in C2[a,b] whose restriction to each of [a,x1​], [x1​,x2​], through [xn​,b] is a polynomial of degree at most three. A spline knot is a junction between consecutive pieces. For a natural cubic spline, the two exterior pieces are affine functions; equivalently, one uses the natural endpoint conditions g′′(x1​)=g′′(xn​)=0 on the spline over [x1​,xn​] and then extends linearly with matching derivatives. Merely prescribing g′′(a)=g′′(b)=0 on otherwise arbitrary exterior cubic pieces is a different condition and does not provide the unique interpolant invoked in this question.
The natural cubic spline interpolant Nv to values v=(v1​,…,vn​)T is the natural cubic spline satisfying (Nv)(xi​)=vi​. Its existence and uniqueness for n≥2 are the interpolant fact allowed in the PDF. The interpolation map N is a linear map: a linear combination of interpolants satisfies the same spline, boundary and value conditions, so uniqueness identifies it with the interpolant to the corresponding linear combination of value vectors.
Let g=Nv, let g​∈C2[a,b] have the same values, and put r=g​−g. Then r(xi​)=0. We prove the orthogonality
∫ab​g′′r′′dx=0.
(35)
Apply integration by parts twice on each polynomial interval. On an interior interval, g′′′′=0, and
∫xi​xi+1​​g′′r′′dx=[g′′r′−g′′′r]xi​xi+1​​.
(36)
The g′′′r terms vanish because r vanishes at the knots. The g′′r′ terms cancel at internal knots because g′′ and r′ are continuous, and vanish at x1​,xn​ because g′′ is zero there. On the exterior intervals, g′′=0 identically. Summing therefore gives the claimed orthogonality without assuming that g′′′ is continuous across knots.
Expanding the square now gives the minimum roughness property of the natural cubic spline interpolant
∫ab​(g​′′)2dx=∫ab​(g′′)2dx+∫ab​(r′′)2dx≥∫ab​(g′′)2dx.​
(37)
Equality forces r′′=0 everywhere, since r′′ is continuous. Thus r is an affine function. It vanishes at two distinct design points, so r=0, proving equality if and only if g​=g.
For the penalized fit, take the allowed spline roughness penalty matrix Γ to be a symmetric matrix with zTΓz≥0; thus it is a positive semidefinite matrix. If its supplied representative were not symmetric, its symmetric part defines the same quadratic form and is all that is needed. For any g​, let v=(g​(xi​))i=1n​. Replacing it by Nv preserves every fitted value and can only decrease the second derivative roughness penalty. Therefore it is enough to minimize
Qλ​(v)=∥Y−v∥22​+λvTΓv
(38)
over Rn. The matrix Aλ​=In​+λΓ is a positive-definite matrix, since
zTAλ​z=∥z∥22​+λzTΓz>0(z=0).
(39)
Completing the quadratic or differentiating gives the unique solution
vλ​=(In​+λΓ)−1Y,g​λ​=Nvλ​.​
(40)
This is the cubic smoothing spline. More explicitly, the normal equations are (In​+λΓ)vλ​=Y, and
Qλ​(v)−Qλ​(vλ​)=(v−vλ​)TAλ​(v−vλ​).
(41)
For an arbitrary function with fitted vector v, the earlier orthogonality also gives
Sλ​(g​)−Sλ​(g​λ​)=(v−vλ​)TAλ​(v−vλ​)+λ∫ab​(g​′′−(Nv)′′)2dx.
(42)
Both terms are nonnegative. Equality forces v=vλ​ and, by the proved interpolation equality case, g​=Nv=g​λ​. This proves both existence in C2[a,b] and uniqueness over the entire stated function class, not just over splines.
The argument is deterministic for each observed Y. The fixed-design nonparametric regression and homoscedasticity assumptions motivate the squared-error loss, but Gaussian errors are not required.
At least two distinct design points are required for uniqueness. Question 4 does not explicitly restate n≥2, and this hypothesis is necessary. With one design point, every function g​(x)=Y1​+c(x−x1​) has zero residual and zero second derivative roughness penalty, for any real c. Thus the printed unrestricted uniqueness conclusion would be false for n=1. The corrected theorem assumes at least two distinct knots and the stated linear-tail convention. At λ=0, uniqueness over all of C2[a,b] also fails; the stipulated λ>0 eliminates that degeneracy.
