A real positive-definite kernel is a symmetric function k:X×X→R such that, for every finite collection x1​,…,xn​ and every a∈Rn,
∑i,l=1n​ai​al​k(xi​,xl​)≥0.
(1)
Thus every kernel matrix is a positive semidefinite matrix; “positive definite” here does not require strict positivity. A Reproducing kernel Hilbert space is a Hilbert space H of functions on X in which every point evaluation is a continuous linear functional. Its reproducing property is f(x)=⟨f,kx​⟩H​ with kx​=k(⋅,x)∈H, and k(x,y)=⟨ky​,kx​⟩H​. For a sum of positive-definite kernels, the quadratic form of its kernel matrix is a sum of nonnegative quadratic forms. Symmetry also survives summation. Consequently k=j∑​kj​ is a positive-definite kernel.​
For the representer theorem, put V=span{kx1​​,…,kxn​​}. This is a closed finite-dimensional vector space. The orthogonal decomposition by a closed subspace gives f=fV​+f⊥​, where fV​∈V and f⊥​⊥V. The reproducing property implies f⊥​(xi​)=0 for every observed input, and therefore
Q1​(f)=Q1​(fV​)+λ∥f⊥​∥H2​.
(2)
Since λ>0, any minimizer must lie in V. For fα​=∑i​αi​kxi​​, the reproducing property gives the evaluation vector Kα and ∥fα​∥H2​=αTKα. Hence Q1​(fα​)=M(α). If f​ minimizes Q1​, its representation yields a minimizing α. Conversely, if α minimizes M, then, for any f∈H, representing fV​=fα​ gives Q1​(f)≥M(α)≥M(α). Thus
f​=i∑​αi​k(⋅,xi​),α∈α∈Rnargmin​M(α).​
(3)
This is an equivalence of minimizers, not an existence or uniqueness assertion: the loss function is arbitrary. A singular kernel matrix also allows nonunique coefficients, since differences in kerK represent the zero function. Strict convexity of the loss was not used.
Now use the stated minimum-norm description of a sum of reproducing-kernel Hilbert spaces. Let f​=∑j​f​j​. If the minimizing tuple did not attain the minimum total squared norm among decompositions of f​, replacing it by that unique minimum-norm tuple would preserve the loss function and strictly lower the penalty. This contradicts optimality. Therefore
Q2​(f​1​,…,f​p​)=Q1​(f​).
(4)
For an arbitrary f∈H, take its minimum-norm decomposition (f1​,…,fp​). Then Q1​(f​)=Q2​(f​1​,…,f​p​)≤Q2​(f1​,…,fp​)=Q1​(f), proving that f​ minimizes Q1​.
Choose α from the representer theorem for this minimizing sum, and define gj​=∑i​αi​kj​(⋅,xi​). Write Kj​ for the component kernel matrices. Then
∑j​gj​=f​,∑j​∥gj​∥Hj​2​=∑j​αTKj​α=αTKα=∥f​∥H2​.
(5)
By uniqueness of the minimum-norm decomposition, gj​=f​j​ for every j. This proves the common representer coefficients for a sum of kernels conclusion:
f​j​(⋅)=i=1∑n​αi​kj​(⋅,xi​)for every j,α∈argminM.​
(6)
The same coefficient vector works simultaneously. Even when K is singular, changing that vector by a∈kerK leaves every component unchanged: 0=aTKa=∑j​aTKj​a forces each nonnegative squared component norm to vanish.

Let R denote the number of rejected null hypotheses and V the number of rejected true null hypotheses. The familywise error rate is Pr(V≥1), whereas the false discovery rate is E[V/(R∨1)], with a zero contribution when R=0. A valid true-null p-value satisfies Pr(pi​≤t)≤t for 0≤t≤1.
For the closed testing procedure, choose a level-α local test of every nonempty intersection HI​=⋂i∈I​Hi​. Reject Hi​ only if every intersection containing it is rejected by its local test. If any true null hypothesis is rejected, the local test of HI0​​ must reject. Since that intersection is true, its rejection probability is at most α. If I0​ is empty there can be no false rejection. This proves strong closed-testing control of the familywise error rate, under arbitrary dependence, provided each local test is valid under its whole intersection null.
For the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, order the p-values and set
R=max{r∈{1,…,m}:p(r)​≤αr/m},
(17)
with maximum zero if the set is empty; reject the first R hypotheses. To prove its false discovery rate bound, fix a true-null index i. Replace pi​ by zero and let Ri​ be the modified rejection count. This is a function of the other p-values and satisfies Ri​≥1.
The Benjamini-Hochberg leave-one-out identity is
R∨11{i rejected}​=Ri​1{pi​≤αRi​/m}​.
(18)
Here is its deterministic justification. If i was rejected, lowering its value cannot decrease R, and leaves all ordered values of ranks greater than R unchanged. No such rank can newly satisfy its threshold, so Ri​=R. Conversely, if Ri​=r and pi​≤αr/m, at least r−1 other values and pi​ are at most this threshold. Therefore the original procedure has R≥r. Monotonicity under decreasing pi​ also gives R≤Ri​=r, so R=r and i is rejected. This establishes the identity; harmless deterministic tie-breaking also covers the artificial zero introduced in the modified data.
The assumed independence makes pi​ independent of Ri​. Conditional on Ri​=r, validity of the p-value implies
E[Ri​1{pi​≤αRi​/m}​​Ri​=r]≤r1​mαr​=mα​.
(19)
Sum over i∈I0​ to obtain
FDR≤mm0​​α≤α.​
(20)
If true-null p-values are exactly uniform rather than merely valid, the first inequality is equality. No restriction on dependence among the false-null p-values is needed beyond their joint independence from the true-null values.
For the requested Simes inequality, apply this result to the m0​ true-null p-values alone, for m0​>0. Every rejection is now false, so V=R and the false discovery rate is the probability of any rejection. The event of at least one Benjamini-Hochberg procedure rejection is exactly
{min1≤r≤m0​​rp(r,I0​)​​≤m0​α​}.
(21)
Its probability is at most α. This holds for α∈[0,1]; with no true nulls the written minimum and division by m0​ are undefined and the error-control statement is instead vacuous.
The final step-up rule is the Hochberg procedure. Use Simes tests as the local tests for a closed testing procedure: reject HI​ when minr​p(r,I)​/r≤α/∣I∣. Under any true intersection all its p-values are valid and independent, so the Simes inequality proves level α for its local test. The implication supplied in the question says that whenever the Hochberg procedure rejects H(i)​, every intersection containing (i) passes its local Simes test. Thus every such rejection is also a closed-testing rejection. Consequently
FWER(Hochberg)≤FWER(closed Simes tests)≤α.​
(22)
The true-null independence assumption is essential to this particular proof of local-test validity; the arbitrary-dependence guarantee of closed testing does not create valid local tests automatically.

The global Markov property for a directed acyclic graph requires, for all pairwise disjoint vertex sets A,B,C, that D-separation of A and B by C in G implies ZA​⊥ZB​∣ZC​. Two Markov equivalent directed acyclic graphs encode exactly the same such D-separations. The structural characterization is that they have the same skeleton of a directed graph and the same unshielded colliders. A distribution has faithfulness of a directed acyclic graph when its conditional independences are exactly those encoded by D-separation; this includes the global Markov implication as well as its converse.
The population PC algorithm uses an exact conditional independence oracle. Start with the complete undirected graph, and conditioning-set size ℓ=0. At each size, examine ordered adjacent pairs (u,v). Test subsets C⊆Adj(u)∖{v} of size ℓ until independence Zu​⊥Zv​∣ZC​ is found or all such subsets have been examined. On finding it, delete u−v and record a symmetric separating set Sep(u,v)=Sep(v,u)=C. Ordered pairs ensure that subsets from either endpoint's neighbours are considered. Increase ℓ until no adjacent ordered pair has enough remaining neighbours to supply a set of that size. Rechecking eligibility after deletion, or freezing the neighbours at each level and then deleting, gives the same population skeleton under the hypothesis below.
Next, for each unshielded triple u−v−w with u,w nonadjacent, orient u→v←w precisely when v∈/Sep(u,w). Propagate directions that are forced without introducing a directed cycle or an additional unshielded collider. In particular, a→b−c with a,c nonadjacent forces b→c; an undirected edge a−b with a directed path from a to b forces a→b; and a−b,a−c,a−d,c→b,d→b with c,d nonadjacent forces a→b. The first avoids a new unshielded collider, the second a directed cycle. For the third, b→a would force c→a and d→a to avoid cycles, creating the prohibited unshielded collider c→a←d.
For a fully specified exact completion, list all acyclic orientations of the recovered skeleton which extend the collider arrows and have exactly the recorded unshielded colliders. Direct an edge if and only if all listed graphs orient it the same way; otherwise leave it undirected. This finite enumeration implements the definition of the completed partially directed acyclic graph; orientation propagation is the usual efficient way to obtain the same completion. The equivalence-class proof below uses the exact completion description and does not need an unproved completeness claim about a chosen subset of propagation rules.
To prove correctness, assume faithfulness of a directed acyclic graph to G0. A true edge u→v cannot be D-separated by any set excluding its endpoints: its length-one path is always active. Thus a true edge is never deleted. Conversely, take nonadjacent u,v and choose their order so that u is earlier than v in a topological ordering of G0. Then u is a nondescendant and nonparent of v. Its parent set Pa(v) gives D-separation of u and v. To see this local graphical fact, a path beginning v←q is blocked by the conditioned parent q, a noncollider. A path beginning with an arrow out of v must encounter a first collider before reaching the nondescendant u. That collider is a descendant of v, and neither it nor any of its descendants can be a parent of v in an acyclic graph. Hence conditioning on Pa(v) does not open it.
All parent edges survive. If u−v has not already been deleted, Pa(v) remains among v's neighbours excluding u. It is therefore tested by size ∣Pa(v)∣, and faithfulness forces deletion. The size loop cannot stop prematurely while this set remains eligible. This proves exact recovery of the skeleton of a directed graph.
For an unshielded triple u−v−w, if it is a noncollider in G0, its length-two path remains active unless v is conditioned on. Thus every separator of u,w contains v. If it is a collider, conditioning on v opens the length-two path, so no separator can contain v. Faithfulness turns these graphical statements into the recorded separator test. Therefore the collider phase recovers precisely all unshielded colliders. By the skeleton and collider characterization of Markov equivalence, the acyclic extensions with exactly these colliders are exactly the Markov equivalence of directed acyclic graphs class of G0. In particular that set is nonempty, and the completion returns its CPDAG. This proves population PC correctness under faithfulness.
For the four-variable example, the only removable pairs are 2,4, with separator ∅, and 1,3, with separator {2,4}. The remaining skeleton has edges 1−2,1−4,2−3,3−4. The unshielded triples 2−1−4 and 2−3−4 both have center outside the separator of 2,4, so both are unshielded colliders. Every remaining edge is thereby oriented:
G0:2⟶1⟵4,2⟶3⟵4.​
(23)
The other two unshielded triples are noncolliders, consistent with the separator {2,4} of 1,3. There is no undirected edge left, so the equivalence class consists of this unique Directed acyclic graph. This identification is about the given faithful observational distribution; general PC algorithm outputs need not identify a unique causal direction.

For λ>0 and labels Yi​∈{−1,1}, with squared-norm penalty normalized as λαTKα, the kernel soft-margin support vector machine solves
μ∈R,α∈Rnmin​{n1​i=1∑n​(1−Yi​(μ+KiT​α))+​+λαTKα}.​
(24)
Here (u)+​=max(u,0) is the hinge loss building block, and Ki​ is column i of the symmetric kernel matrix. The prediction is
Y(x)=sgn(μ​+i∑​αi​k(x,xi​)),​
(25)
with a fixed class choice, for example +1, when the score is zero. Other positive rescalings of the penalty change numerical coefficients in the optimality equation but not the zero-coefficient conclusion.
For a finite convex function f:Rn→R, its subdifferential at x is
∂f(x)={g∈Rn:f(y)≥f(x)+gT(y−x) for every y∈Rn}.
(26)
For f(x)=h(cTx+b), affine composition preserves the defining convexity inequality: for 0≤t≤1,
f(tx+(1−t)y)=h(t(cTx+b)+(1−t)(cTy+b))≤tf(x)+(1−t)f(y).
(27)
If v∈∂h(u), the subgradient inequality gives f(y)≥f(x)+vcT(y−x), proving cv∈∂f(x).
Conversely, let g∈∂f(x) and first suppose c=0. For every z∈kercT, the function is constant along x+tz. Applying the subgradient inequality for both signs of t shows gTz=0. Thus g is in the span of c, or g=cv with v=gTc/∥c∥22​. Substituting y=x+(a−u)c/∥c∥22​ gives h(a)≥h(u)+v(a−u) for every real a. Hence v∈∂h(u). If c=0, f is constant and ∂f(x)={0}; since finite convex h on all of R has a nonempty subdifferential at b, c∂h(b)={0} as well. This handles the case in which the suggested orthogonal projection would be undefined. Therefore
∂f(x)=c∂h(cTx+b).​
(28)
This proves the subdifferential under scalar affine composition identity with all its degenerate cases.
Let mi​=Yi​(μ​+KiT​α) be a fitted support-vector-machine margin. The subdifferential of the hinge loss yields choices
ti​=⎩⎨⎧​1[0,1]0​mi​<1,mi​=1,mi​>1.​
(29)
Applying the affine-composition identity and the subdifferential sum rule to the convex objective, its optimum satisfies
2λKα=n1​∑i​Yi​ti​Ki​,∑i​Yi​ti​=0.
(30)
The second equation is the intercept condition. Since K is invertible, multiplying the first by K−1 gives the kernel support-vector coefficient from hinge activity formula
αi​=2nλYi​ti​​.
(31)
In particular mi​>1 ⟹ αi​=0.​ Invertibility is needed for this statement about the chosen coefficients: for K=11T with two positive labels, μ​=2 and α=(1,−1)T give zero objective and both margins equal to two, while both coefficients are nonzero. A singular kernel matrix allows coefficient changes in the null space without changing the fitted function.
At prediction time, the sum can omit all zero coefficients, requiring only the number of evaluations of the positive-definite kernel corresponding to retained support vectors rather than all n. This need not remove the cost of forming or solving with a dense training kernel matrix. Every misclassified point has mi​≤0<1, including a score-zero tie assigned to the opposite class, so ti​=1 and αi​=Yi​/(2nλ)=0. Thus many classification errors imply many active support vectors and little prediction-time sparsity benefit. Correctly classified points inside the unit margin can also have nonzero coefficients; a point exactly on the margin need not have a nonzero coefficient.
