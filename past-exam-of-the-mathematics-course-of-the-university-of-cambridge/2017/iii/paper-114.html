Use integer coefficients. The mapping-torus homology from Mayer–Vietoris works for every continuous map f:X→X, without assuming that the mapping torus is a fiber bundle. Project to the time circle and choose two open arcs, one containing the seam and one away from it. The inverse image U of the seam arc is a mapping cylinder neighborhood with a deformation retraction to its target copy of X; the inverse image V of the other arc is a product with a deformation retraction to X. Their intersection has two components, each with a deformation retraction to X. Label the upper component first. With the convention that the second inclusion in the Mayer–Vietoris sequence has a minus sign, its homology map is
Hq​(X)⊕Hq​(X)⟶Hq​(X)⊕Hq​(X),(a,b)⟼(a+f∗​b,−a−b).
(1)
Indeed, the upper component includes into the seam neighborhood by the identity, while retracting the lower component across the seam applies f. Put u=a+b and v=b, and replace target coordinates (p,q) by (p+q,−q). The map becomes ((f∗​−id)v,u). Negating v gives a direct sum of id−f∗​ and an isomorphism. Canceling the latter summands in the long exact sequence yields
⋯⟶Hq​(X)id−f∗​​Hq​(X)⟶Hq​(Tf​)⟶Hq−1​(X)id−f∗​​Hq−1​(X)⟶⋯.​
(2)
In particular, there is a short exact sequence
0⟶coker(id−f∗​∣Hq​​)⟶Hq​(Tf​)⟶ker(id−f∗​∣Hq−1​​)⟶0.
(3)
No splitting of this short exact sequence is assumed at this stage.
For the torus, take the coordinate loops c1​(t)=[(t,0)] and c2​(t)=[(0,t)] as the basis of H1​. Their lifts to R2 have endpoint displacements e1​,e2​. Applying A changes those displacements to its columns, so
(fA​)∗​[cj​]=∑i​Aij​[ci​].
(4)
This also follows from the identification of the first homology group with the abelianization of the fundamental group. The Künneth theorem gives H2​(X)=Z, with generator the product orientation class. If α,β are the dual degree-one cohomology classes, then α⌣β evaluates to one on this generator, and
fA∗​(α⌣β)=(A11​α+A12​β)⌣(A21​α+A22​β)=det(A)α⌣β.
(5)
Thus the degree-two homology map is multiplication by detA, as in homology of an integer torus endomorphism. The claim that the torus map is a homeomorphism requires A to be a unimodular matrix, equivalently an integer inverse or detA=±1; nonsingularity over R alone would only give a covering map of finite degree. The particular matrix here is unimodular.
For this matrix, detA=1 and
I−A=(11​−11​).
(6)
Its kernel is zero and its Smith normal form is diag(1,2), so its cokernel is Z/2. The maps id−f∗​ on H0​ and H2​ are zero. Substituting into the short exact sequences, and splitting the degree-one extension because its quotient Z is free, gives
Hq​(TfA​​;Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z⊕Z/2,0,​q=0,2,3,q=1,otherwise.​​
(7)

For the sphere homology calculation use singular homology, homotopy invariance of homology, and the reduced Mayer–Vietoris sequence, not cellular homology. For a point, its chain group for singular homology is Z in every nonnegative degree, and its boundary operator is multiplication by ∑i=0q​(−1)i, hence alternately zero and one in positive degrees. Its homology is therefore Z in degree zero and zero above. Singular chains of a disjoint union split into the summand chain complexes. Start with S0, the disjoint union of two points: H0​(S0;Z)=Z2, all positive groups vanish, and H0​(S0;Z)=Z. For n≥1, remove the north and south poles to obtain an open cover U,V of Sn. Each member is contractible, while U∩V has a deformation retraction to Sn−1, including the disconnected intersection when n=1. The reduced exact sequence therefore supplies
Hq​(Sn;Z)≅Hq−1​(Sn−1;Z)(q≥1).
(8)
Also Sn is connected, so its reduced degree-zero group vanishes. Induction gives
Hq​(Sn;Z)≅{Z,0,​q=0,n,q∈/{0,n},​n≥1.​
(9)
The separate S0 calculation avoids conflating its two degree-zero generators.
Choose an orientation of Sn, with n≥1, and let [Sn] be its fundamental class. The mapping degree is the integer determined by f∗​[Sn]=deg(f)[Sn]. To define the local degree at x0​, set y0​=f(x0​) and require x0​ to be isolated in f−1(y0​). Choose an open neighborhood B containing no other point of that fiber. Then f defines a map of pairs
(B,B∖{x0​})⟶(Sn,Sn∖{y0​}).
(10)
The degree-n relative homology groups on both sides are Z, by a manifold chart and excision. The local orientation of a manifold chooses their generators. The induced map multiplies these generators by an integer, denoted degx0​​f. Excision and naturality show independence of the choice of B. Differentiability is unnecessary; if f is smooth and Dfx0​​ is invertible, the inverse function theorem gives degx0​​f=signdetDfx0​​ relative to the orientations. Without isolation in the fiber this pointwise definition need not apply.
For the degree as a sum of local degrees, assume y∈Sn has finite fiber F={x1​,…,xs​}. Disjoint coordinate neighborhoods and excision identify
Hn​(Sn,Sn∖F;Z)≅⨁j=1s​Hn​(Sn,Sn∖{xj​};Z).
(11)
The absolute fundamental class maps to the tuple of local orientation generators. Under the relative map induced by f, the jth generator goes to degxj​​f times the generator at y, and the resulting map from the direct sum adds these contributions. On the other hand, naturality says that first applying the absolute map f∗​ and then passing to the local group at y gives deg(f) times that generator. Thus
deg(f)=x∈f−1(y)∑​degx​f.​
(12)
If the fiber is empty the sum is zero: the map factors through the contractible punctured sphere and has degree zero. If every point of a fiber is isolated, compactness makes that closed fiber finite, so the theorem applies. In particular, a regular value of a smooth map gives the signed count of its inverse images. For n=0, define a mapping degree on the reduced generator [p+​]−[p−​]: the identity has mapping degree one, the swap of the two points minus one, and either constant map zero. With local orientation signs positive at p+​ and negative at p−​, the same sum formula holds. The connected-sphere convention above is the standard positive-dimensional one.

The real tautological line bundle has total space E={(ℓ,v):ℓ∈RPn, v∈ℓ} and projection (ℓ,v)↦ℓ. On the open chart Ui​={[x]:xi​=0}, the vector si​([x])=x/xi​ is a continuous nowhere-zero section of a vector bundle, independent of the representative of [x]. The explicit local trivialization is
Ui​×R⟶E∣Ui​​,([x],t)⟼([x],tsi​([x])),
(13)
with inverse ([x],v)↦([x],vi​). Both maps are continuous and linear on each fiber. The charts cover the base, proving the existence of the required local trivializations.
Let E→B be a rank-r vector bundle oriented over a commutative ring R, with r≥1. An R-orientation is a coherent choice of generator of each fiber's top relative cohomology, or equivalently a Thom class uE​. The Thom isomorphism theorem identifies Hq−r(B;R) with Hq(D(E),S(E);R) by a↦π∗a⌣uE​. The disk bundle retracts to B, and the relative-to-absolute map becomes multiplication by the Euler class e(E). The pair's long exact sequence therefore becomes the Gysin sequence of a sphere bundle:
⋯⟶Hq−r(B;R)⌣e(E)​Hq(B;R)p∗​Hq(S(E);R)⟶Hq−r+1(B;R)⌣e(E)​Hq+1(B;R)⟶⋯.
(14)
With real coefficients, the bundle must be oriented in the usual sense. For the computation here use R=F2​, over which every real vector bundle is oriented, including the nonorientable real tautological line bundle when n≥1.
The unit sphere bundle of γn​ is Sn: a unit vector determines its line. Put a=e2​(γn​)=w1​(γn​)∈H1(RPn;F2​). For n≥1, the pullback H0(RPn;F2​)→H0(Sn;F2​) is an isomorphism, so the next connecting homomorphism is zero and multiplication by a is injective on H0. Since the sphere's cohomology vanishes strictly between degrees zero and n, exactness makes multiplication by a an isomorphism Hq−1→Hq for 1≤q<n, and injective for q=n. For q=1 these assertions also use the just-noted vanishing of the connecting homomorphism from H0(Sn).
The standard n-dimensional CW complex structure of Real projective space gives Hq=0 for q>n. Consequently the segment Hn(Sn;F2​)→Hn(RPn;F2​)⌣a​Hn+1(RPn;F2​)=0 makes Hn at most one-dimensional. The preceding injectivity makes it exactly one-dimensional, generated by an. This includes n=1; n=0 is a point separately. We have derived the multiplication, not just the dimensions:
H∗(RPn;F2​)=F2​[a]/(an+1),∣a∣=1.​
(15)
An odd map between spheres descends to fˉ​:RPn→RPm. The odd maps pull back the real tautological line bundle argument gives γn​≅fˉ​∗γm​: with x a unit vector the fiber map is tx↦tf(x), unchanged when (x,t) is replaced by (−x,−t). Naturality of the first Stiefel–Whitney class gives fˉ​∗b=a. If n>m, the relation bm+1=0 would imply am+1=0 in a ring where this power is nonzero. Hence n≤m​. The case n=0 is immediate, and the same argument rules out m=0<n.
For the map with separate oddness, pass to gˉ​:RPn×RPn→RPn. Let a,b be the degree-one classes from its two domain factors and c the target class. There is an isomorphism
gˉ​∗γn​≅p1∗​γn​⊗p2∗​γn​,
(16)
given on fibers by (sx)⊗(ty)↦stg(x,y); flipping either unit representative leaves this map well defined. The First Stiefel–Whitney class of a tensor product of real line bundles gives gˉ​∗c=a+b. By the Künneth theorem,
H∗(RPn×RPn;F2​)=F2​[a,b]/(an+1,bn+1).
(17)
Pulling back cn+1=0 shows that (a+b)n+1=0. The two end monomials already vanish, while every interior monomial ajbn+1−j, 1≤j≤n, is a distinct nonzero basis element. Therefore all interior binomial coefficients in row N=n+1 are even. To identify such rows, write N=∑i∈I​2i. In F2​[t] repeated squaring gives (1+t)N=∏i∈I​(1+t2i). If I has at least two elements, the coefficient of t2minI is one and its exponent is strictly between zero and N, a contradiction. Thus N is a power of two, as in binomial coefficients with even interior terms, and
n=2k−1for some k≥0.​
(18)
This is the necessary cohomological obstruction to separately odd sphere multiplication; it does not assert existence in every dimension of this form.

Use F2​ coefficients throughout. Write h=c1​(O(1))mod2 on Complex projective space and a=w1​(γd​) on Real projective space. We use the standard cohomology ring of complex projective space F2​[h]/(hd+1), with ∣h∣=2, and the real projective calculation from the preceding solution. The complex projective calculation follows, for example, from its one cell in each even dimension together with the fact that transverse projective hyperplanes represent the powers of h; d such hyperplanes have one intersection point.
For the standard inclusion i:RPd↪CPd, the restricted complex tautological line is γd​⊗R​C. As a real vector bundle it is γd​⊕γd​, whose total Stiefel–Whitney class is (1+a)2=1+a2. The Stiefel–Whitney class of the underlying real bundle of a complex line identifies its degree-two class with its First Chern class reduced modulo two. Passing to the dual line defining h changes only a sign, which disappears modulo two. Thus mod-two restriction from complex to real projective space gives i∗h=a2. In particular the answer for dimension two is the ring map
i∗:F2​[h]/(h3)⟶F2​[a]/(a3),h⟼a2.​
(19)
It sends 1 to 1, is an isomorphism in degree two, and sends h2 to zero.
Now assume k≥1 and put M=CP2k, N=RP2k, U=M∖N, with inclusions i:N↪M and j:U↪M. The real dimensions are 4k and 2k, respectively. A tubular neighborhood and excision identify the cohomology of (M,U) with that of the normal disk bundle relative to its sphere bundle. Every real normal bundle is oriented over F2​, so the Thom isomorphism theorem gives
Hq(M,U)≅Hq−2k(N).
(20)
Under this identification the relative-to-absolute map is the cohomological Gysin map of an embedding i!​. The exact sequence of the pair is therefore
⋯⟶Hq−2k(N)i!​​Hq(M)j∗​Hq(U)δ​Hq+1−2k(N)i!​​Hq+1(M)⟶⋯.
(21)
Here δ denotes the ordinary connecting homomorphism followed by the inverse of the isomorphism supplied by the Thom isomorphism theorem.
To compute i!​, use mod-two Poincare duality and its evaluation formula
⟨i!​b⌣z,[M]2​⟩=⟨b⌣i∗z,[N]2​⟩.
(22)
For 0≤r≤k, take b=a2r and z=hk−r. The right side is ⟨a2k,[N]2​⟩=1. Since the target group is generated by hk+r and ⟨h2k,[M]2​⟩=1, this proves
i!​(a2r)=hk+r.
(23)
Odd-degree classes have zero image because M has no odd-degree cohomology. Exactness now gives
Hq(U)≅{F2​,0,​q even and 0≤q≤4k−2,otherwise.​
(24)
For precision, below degree 2k−1 the restriction map is an isomorphism. In degree 2k−1 the next map is i!​:H0(N)→H2k(M), an isomorphism, so the complement group is zero. In even degrees 2k+2r, 0≤r<k, the preceding i!​ is an isomorphism and the following odd-degree i!​ is zero; consequently δ:H2k+2r(U)→H2r+1(N) is an isomorphism. The intervening odd groups and the top group H4k(U) vanish.
It remains to establish multiplication. Put x=j∗h. Since hk=i!​1, exactness gives xk=0. Choose y∈H2k(U) with δy=a, which is possible by the just-computed isomorphism. Naturality of the relative cup product, and the module property of the Thom isomorphism theorem, give
δ(j∗z⌣v)=i∗z⌣δv.
(25)
Thus δ(xry)=a2r+1 for 0≤r<k. Each class xry is nonzero and generates its upper-half group, while 1,x,…,xk−1 generate the lower-half groups. Also y2=0 because H4k(U)=0. These classes account for every group, so there are no further relations:
H∗(U;F2​)≅F2​[x,y]/(xk,y2),∣x∣=2,∣y∣=2k.​
(26)
By the field-coefficient Künneth theorem this is precisely the cohomology ring of CPk−1×S2k. For k=1 the relation x=0 leaves the sphere's ring. The calculation identifies rings and does not claim a homotopy equivalence of the spaces; k=0 is outside the expression involving CPk−1.

The ordinary Poincare duality statement here is for a compact manifold without boundary, of dimension d, oriented over the field F. Its fundamental class [M]F​ induces isomorphisms
Hq(M;F) a↦[M]F​⌢a ​Hd−q​(M;F)
(27)
for every q. For a manifold with boundary the appropriate statement is Poincare-Lefschetz duality with relative groups; the ordinary pairing need not be nondegenerate. Over a field, the universal coefficient theorem for cohomology identifies Hd−q(M;F) with the full dual of Hd−q​(M;F). The cap-cup evaluation identity and duality therefore make
Hq(M;F)×Hd−q(M;F)⟶F,(a,b)⟼⟨a⌣b,[M]F​⟩
(28)
a perfect pairing. Explicitly, a nonzero a has a nonzero cap product, and a linear functional b on its homology group takes a nonzero value on that product. The same argument in the other variable proves nonsingularity. On the whole graded cohomology, define B(a,b) by taking the degree-d part of a⌣b before evaluation. For a nonzero component aq​ choose b homogeneous of degree d−q to pair nontrivially with it. All other components contribute zero in degree d. This proves that the Poincare duality pairing is a nondegenerate bilinear form on H∗(M;F); it need not be symmetric on all degrees.
Put r=2n+1, so r is positive and odd, and orient each Sr×Sr by its product orientation. The Künneth theorem gives integral cohomology Z in degrees zero and 2r, Z2 in degree r, and zero elsewhere. Its two degree-r generators α,β have α2=β2=0, αβ equal to its top orientation class, and βα=−αβ.
For a connected sum of oriented manifolds, excision and the long exact sequence for deleting a ball show that deleting a ball removes the top homology class and leaves all lower positive homology groups unchanged. The boundary sphere represents zero in the punctured manifold: it is the boundary of its relative fundamental chain. In the Mayer–Vietoris sequence for the two punctured pieces joined along the separating sphere, the orientation class of the connected sum maps onto that sphere's class. The intermediate positive groups are consequently the direct sums of the groups of the two original manifolds. This also covers r=1, where the sphere is a circle and the boundary-class observation is necessary in the middle degree. Iterating and applying the universal coefficient theorem for cohomology gives
Hq(Wg​;Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z2g,0,​q=0,2r,q=r,otherwise.​​
(29)
Here g≥1; under the conventional extension W0​=S2r the same formula holds with a zero middle group.
Let ω be the top cohomological orientation class. The connected-sum pinch map to the wedge of the g sphere products gives degree-one projections to each summand. Pull back the two factor classes from summand i to obtain αi​,βi​. Their product is ω, since the projection has degree one. Classes from different wedge summands have zero positive-degree cup products, and graded commutativity of the cup product supplies the reversed sign. Thus the full cohomology ring of a connected sum of odd-dimensional sphere products is the graded free abelian group just displayed with multiplication
αi​αj​=βi​βj​=0,αi​βj​=δij​ω,βj​αi​=−δij​ω.​
(30)
The unit is 1, and ω times any positive-degree class is zero by dimension. This describes all products, including ω2=0.
The smooth involution is a diffeomorphism, since it is its own inverse. Its fixed set is closed; discreteness and compactness therefore make it finite. The supplied positivity of det(I−Dfx​) makes every fixed point nondegenerate with local index +1. The Lefschetz-Hopf fixed-point theorem then gives
#Fix(f)=L(f)=2−trT,T=f∗:Hr(Wg​;R)→Hr(Wg​;R).
(31)
Indeed the degree-zero and top-degree traces are both one, because the manifold is connected and f preserves its orientation; the middle degree is odd.
On V=Hr(Wg​;R), the form Ω(a,b)=⟨a⌣b,[Wg​]⟩ is a nondegenerate alternating bilinear form by Poincare duality and the oddness of r. Thus it is a symplectic vector space of dimension 2g. Naturality and orientation preservation show that T preserves Ω, and T2=I. For the eigenspaces of a symplectic involution, write V=V+​⊕V−​: the polynomial (t−1)(t+1) has distinct roots over R. If v+​∈V+​ and v−​∈V−​, then Ω(v+​,v−​)=Ω(Tv+​,Tv−​)=−Ω(v+​,v−​), so the two vector subspaces are orthogonal. Each restricted form is nondegenerate, since a vector annihilating its own vector subspace also annihilates the other and hence all of V. Their dimensions are therefore even, say dimV+​=2a, dimV−​=2b, with a+b=g. Consequently
trT=2a−2b=2g−4b,#Fix(f)=2−2g+4b.
(32)
This proves the fixed-point congruence for an involution on an odd-sphere connected sum:
#Fix(f)≡2−2g(mod4).​
(33)
