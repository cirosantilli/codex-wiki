Take h positive downwards and write B for the filament bending modulus. Under the material-modulus interpretation of the printed A as Young's modulus, the circular cross-section gives the second moment of area and bending modulus
I=∫section​y2dS=4πa4​,B=AI=4Aπa4​.
(1)
Indeed a small curvature κ gives axial strain −yκ, so integrating Ay2κ2/2 over the section gives bending energy Bκ2/2 per unit length. Keeping this distinction is essential to the powers of radius in the answer.
After allowing for buoyancy, the effective downward force per unit length is
w=(ρf​−ρ)πa2g>0.
(2)
The filament loses potential energy as it moves down; the displaced fluid's hydrostatic contribution is included through the density difference. For an inextensible filament with arc-length coordinate s, the geometric bending and gravitational energy is ∫0L​[Bκ(s)2/2−wh(s)]ds, up to a constant. For the small-slope Monge representation this reduces to
E[h]=∫0L​[2B​(hxx​)2−wh]dx,B=4Aπa4​,w=(ρf​−ρ)πa2g.​
(3)
The upward-positive convention reverses the sign of h and the gravitational term together. There is no stretching contribution at this order when bending a slender filament without imposed axial tension. The wording does not explicitly distinguish a material elastic modulus from a flexural modulus: if A instead denotes the filament bending modulus in the intended convention, set B=A throughout; then the πa4/4 factor is already included in A.

Use the downward-positive displacement and the filament bending modulus B and load density w defined above. Two applications of integration by parts give the first variation
δE=∫0L​(Bhxxxx​−w)δhdx+B[hxx​δhx​−hxxx​δh]0L​.
(4)
Thus the elastic-plus-gravitational force density is −δE/δh=−Bhxxxx​+w. In resistive-force theory, transverse motion has drag per unit length −ζ⊥​ht​. Neglecting filament and fluid inertia, instantaneous force balance gives the forced small-slope elastohydrodynamic filament equation
ζ⊥​ht​=−Bhxxxx​+w.​
(5)
Clamping fixes position and tangent, while the free end has zero bending moment and zero transverse shear force. Therefore
h(0,t)=hx​(0,t)=0,hxx​(L,t)=hxxx​(L,t)=0.​
(6)
The last two conditions also follow as natural boundary conditions from the variation, since the free-end values of δh and δhx​ are arbitrary. Distributed weight does not add a concentrated force or moment at the tip.
At steady state, Bhs′′′′​=w. Integrating first from the free end gives hs′′′​=(w/B)(x−L) and hs′′​=(w/2B)(x−L)2. Integrating twice more with the clamp conditions gives the uniform-load bending of a clamped filament:
hs​(x)=24Bw​x2(x2−4Lx+6L2).​
(7)
In particular the free-end displacement is δ=hs​(L)=wL4/(8B) and the total buoyancy-corrected gravitational load is F=wL. Thus the tip stiffness of a uniformly loaded cantilever is
F=keff​δ,keff​=L38B​=L32πAa4​.​
(8)
The last expression uses A as Young's modulus; if A itself is flexural rigidity, the same result reads keff​=8A/L3. This is Hooke's law for the specified loading pattern. It is not the stiffness for a point force at the tip, which would be 3B/L3; replacing a distributed force by its resultant does not preserve its bending moment distribution.
For dimensional analysis, [A]= force/length squared, [I]= length to the fourth power, hence [B]= force times length squared. Consequently [B/L3]= force/length, as required for a spring constant. Also [ζ⊥​]= force times time/length squared makes ζ⊥​ht​, Bhxxxx​ and w all forces per unit length. Neither viscosity nor density enters the stiffness, although viscous drag controls the rate of approach to the steady shape. The small-slope approximation requires wL3/B≪1, since hs′​(L)=wL3/(6B).

ϕ′′′′=α4ϕ,Γ=ζ⊥​L4Bα4​.
(9)
The two clamp conditions reduce the general hyperbolic/trigonometric solution to
ϕ(s)=C[cosh(αs)−cos(αs)]+D[sinh(αs)−sin(αs)].
(10)
The two free-end conditions have matrix
(coshα+cosαsinhα−sinα​sinhα+sinαcoshα+cosα​)(CD​)=0.
(11)
Its determinant is 2(1+coshαcosα). Hence the clamped--free bending mode values satisfy
cosαn​coshαn​=−1,α1​=1.8751040687…,α2​=4.6940911330….
(12)

An objective time derivative transforms as a physical tensor under a time-dependent rigid change of observer. If x∗=Q(t)x+c(t), with Q orthogonal and A∗=QAQT, objectivity requires
(DtDA​)∗=QDtDA​QT.​
(17)
This means the constitutive equation makes the same physical prediction in translating and rotating frames, rather than requiring individual tensor components to be invariant.
The printed plus commutator uses the derivative-index-first velocity gradient, Gij​=∂i​uj​. This is the transpose of the component-index-first convention Lij​=∂j​ui​ often used for the velocity gradient. In this notation ω=G−GT, and the rate is the Jaumann derivative
DtDA​=Dt​A+21​(ωA−Aω),Dt​=∂t​+u⋅∇.
(18)
To check the covariance of the Jaumann derivative, let R=Q˙​QT. Direct transformation gives
Dt∗​A∗=Q(Dt​A)QT+RA∗−A∗R,ω∗=QωQT−2R.
(19)
The observer-rotation terms cancel in the displayed corotational rate. Equivalently, in the L convention the physical spin tensor is W=(L−LT)/2=−ω/2, and the same rate is Dt​A−WA+AW. One must transpose the gradient and reverse the commutator together; using L in the printed plus-sign expression would not be objective.
Another example is the upper-convected derivative, written consistently in the L convention as
A▽=Dt​A−LA−ALT.​
(20)
The extra terms in L∗=QLQT+R cancel those in Dt∗​A∗, so this also transforms covariantly. In contrast, the uncorrected material derivative Dt​A of a second-rank tensor is not objective: its transformation contains RA∗−A∗R. For instance a constant anisotropic tensor in a stationary fluid has zero material derivative in its original frame, but its components have a nonzero commutator derivative in a rotating observer's frame. The ordinary partial time derivative is likewise not an objective tensor rate.

Linearize about a motionless, isotropic, relaxed state by taking velocity, deformation and extra stress to be first order in a small amplitude. The advection u⋅∇σ and rotational commutator ωσ−σω are then second order. The corotational Maxwell fluid therefore reduces to the linear Maxwell fluid:
σ+λ∂t​σ=μγ˙​,γ˙​=2e,​
(21)
where e is the rate-of-strain tensor. This is the linear Maxwell viscoelasticity model, mechanically a spring and dashpot in series.
For one shear component, dividing by μ gives γ˙​=σ/μ+(λ/μ)σ˙. Comparing with a dashpot of viscosity μ in series with a spring of shear modulus G identifies
μ=zero-frequency shear viscosity,λ=stress relaxation time,G=μ/λ.​
(22)
In particular a held strain with zero subsequent shear rate has σ(t)=σ(0)e−t/λ. At long times under steady small shear, σ=μγ˙​, whereas a sufficiently rapid deformation probes the elastic modulus G.
In linear simple shear, γ˙​ has only off-diagonal xy components. The diagonal stresses have no forcing and, from a relaxed initial state, stay zero. Any pre-existing diagonal stress decays homogeneously. Thus both normal-stress differences vanish in the linear response to shear. The finite-shear normal-stress differences generated by products of shear rate and stress are quadratic in deformation amplitude and have been discarded by this linearization. This conclusion is about simple shear, not about every possible extensional deformation.

Let the flow direction be x, the gradient direction y, and the vorticity direction z, with simple shear flow u=(sy,0,0) and signed shear rate s=γ˙​. Use the gradient convention established in part (a):
ω=​0s0​−s00​000​​,γ˙​=​0s0​s00​000​​.
(23)
At steady homogeneous shear the ordinary time derivative and advection vanish. Put X=σxx​, Y=σyy​ and S=σxy​. The corotational Maxwell fluid law then gives
X−λsS=0,Y+λsS=0,S+2λs​(X−Y)=μs.
(24)
The zz equation gives σzz​=0, and the unforced xz,yz equations force those components to zero. Solving the three displayed equations gives the steady shear of a corotational Maxwell fluid:
S=1+λ2s2μs​,X=1+λ2s2μλs2​,Y=−X.
(25)
The deviatoric stress is indeed traceless. Its isotropic pressure contribution is irrelevant to either shear viscosity or a normal-stress difference.
Consequently the steady shear viscosity is
ηsh​(s)=sS​=1+λ2s2μ​,ηsh​(0)=μ.​
(26)
For s>0, dηsh​/ds=−2μλ2s/(1+λ2s2)2<0 when μ,λ>0; it decreases with ∣s∣ and tends to zero at large rate. This proves shear thinning directly, without confusing a decreasing viscosity with a necessarily decreasing shear stress.
With the standard definitions N1​=σxx​−σyy​ and N2​=σyy​−σzz​,
N1​=1+λ2s22μλs2​>0,N2​=−1+λ2s2μλs2​=−21​N1​<0.​
(27)
At zero shear both vanish; at small shear they are quadratic, consistently with part (b). The signs and the fact ∣N2​∣<N1​ agree qualitatively with many polymeric-fluid experiments. However the fixed ratio ∣N2​∣/N1​=1/2 often overestimates the measured second difference and cannot capture variation of that ratio with material and shear rate. For example, measurements on polyethylene show a smaller, rate-dependent relative second normal-stress difference. Thus the qualitative normal-stress signs are reasonable, but the predicted relative magnitude is not generally quantitatively satisfactory; these signs are not a universal statement about every non-Newtonian fluid.

Take the physical diffusion and consumption constants D,k>0. Use the travelling wave coordinate z=x−vt, with v>0. Then ∂t​=−vd/dz and ∂x​=d/dz. The original PDF has the bacterial drift flux χbcx​: the TeX transcription's χb is erroneous. Substituting the logarithmic chemotactic sensitivity χ=α/C gives
−vB′=DB′′−α(CBC′​)′,vC′=kB.​
(28)
Primes here denote differentiation with respect to z. The second equation already shows that nutrient increases towards the front whenever the bacterial density is positive. For the positive band, C>0 at every finite z, so division by C is legitimate, despite its zero limit far behind.
Integrating the first equation once gives
DB′+vB−αCBC′​=J,
(29)
with constant J. For a localized band with no bacterial flux at infinity, B and its flux terms vanish ahead, so J=0. Equivalently the laboratory flux −DB′+αBC′/C equals vB. The Keller--Segel model then has the useful first-order reduction
BB′​=Dα​CC′​−Dv​,C′=vk​B.​
(30)
The far-field conditions choose this zero-flux integration constant; it should not be imposed for an arbitrary nonlocalized travelling solution. We solve its positive localized branch in the next part.

Put μ=α/D>1; here μ is the dimensionless chemotactic ratio, unrelated to the fluid viscosity in the other questions. Integrating the first-order relation yields
B=KCμe−vz/D,K>0.
(31)
Substitute into vC′=kB and separate variables. With ξ=vz/D,
dξdC​=v2kDK​Cμe−ξ,C1−μ=C∞1−μ​+v2(μ−1)kDK​e−ξ.
(32)
The first integration constant is fixed by the positive nutrient concentration ahead. The second positive constant can be written C∞1−μ​A0​, and a translation of the wave origin sets A0​=1. Indeed A0​e−vz/D=e−v(z−z0​)/D with z0​=(D/v)logA0​. With that origin choice the nutrient-consuming chemotactic travelling band is
C(z)=C∞​(1+e−ξ)−1/(μ−1),B(z)=kD(μ−1)v2C∞​​e−ξ(1+e−ξ)−μ/(μ−1).​
(33)
The second formula also follows by differentiating the first and using B=vC′/k, so its prefactor is fixed, not a freely chosen independent amplitude.
As ξ→+∞, C→C∞​ and B∼[v2C∞​/(kD(μ−1))]e−ξ→0. As ξ→−∞,
C∼C∞​eξ/(μ−1),B∼kD(μ−1)v2C∞​​eξ/(μ−1)→0.
(34)
Thus all four required limits hold. The singular sensitivity causes no finite-z problem, and C′/C tends to the finite value v/[D(μ−1)] behind. Since C′=kB/v>0, C is strictly monotone increasing.
For the bacterial peak, differentiate its logarithm:
dξdlogB​=−1+μ−1μ​1+e−ξe−ξ​.
(35)
This strictly decreases from 1/(μ−1)>0 to −1<0, so it vanishes exactly once. The peak values are
ξ∗​=−log(μ−1),C∗​=C∞​μ−1/(μ−1),Bmax​=kDv2C∞​​μ−μ/(μ−1).​
(36)
Accordingly the nutrient curve is a smooth rising front, and the bacterial curve is a positive pulse, with generally different exponential decay lengths ahead and behind. The nutrient concentration C∞​ is the undepleted nutrient density in the fresh liquid ahead of the migrating band. Behind the band the nutrient has been consumed. The plot shows the front and pulse for several admissible chemotactic ratios; at μ=2 the bacterial pulse is symmetric about ξ=0.
The condition μ>1 is sufficient for this smooth exponential-tail family. The derivation does not claim to classify all limiting or weak solutions at other parameter values.

Let a denote tube cross-sectional area in this question, not a filament or sphere radius. The bacterial density is uniform across that section in this one-dimensional Keller--Segel model, so the total bacterial number is
N=a∫−∞∞​B(z)dz.
(37)
Integrating the nutrient balance vC′=kB across the complete band gives
v[C(+∞)−C(−∞)]=k∫−∞∞​B(z)dz=akN​.
(38)
Hence the speed of a nutrient-consuming chemotactic band is
v=aC∞​Nk​.​
(39)
This is a nutrient budget: the whole population consumes at rate Nk, while advancement of the band at speed v brings fresh nutrient at rate avC∞​. The conservation of mass equation for bacteria ensures N stays constant when there is no flux at infinity and no cell birth or death.
At fixed total bacterial number, the speed grows linearly with N and the per-cell consumption rate k, and decreases with tube area and nutrient concentration ahead. More nutrient takes longer to deplete, giving the inverse dependence on C∞​. Although D and α determine the width, skewness, peak density and admissibility of the smooth band, they do not appear in this speed when N,k,a,C∞​ are fixed. This independence relies on neglecting nutrient diffusion and using the specified concentration-independent consumption law; it is not a general prediction for all chemotaxis models. Dimensionally Nk is nutrient amount per time and aC∞​ nutrient amount per length, so their ratio is a velocity.

For the two Stokes flow solutions take the same incompressible Newtonian fluid, with constant viscosity μ, no body forces, and
∇⋅u=∇⋅u=0,∇⋅σ=∇⋅σ=0,σ=−pI+2μe,σ=−p​I+2μe.
(40)
Here e and e are the symmetric rate-of-strain tensors. The equality of viscosities is part of comparing two solutions in the same fluid; symmetry of an arbitrary constitutive stress alone would not suffice.
In components, with derivatives ∂j​ui​, use the product rule to compute the divergence of the cross-work flux:
∂j​(ui​σij​)=(∂j​ui​)σij​=−p∂i​ui​+2μeij​∂j​ui​=2μeij​eij​.
(41)
The pressure term vanishes by incompressibility; the antisymmetric part of the velocity gradient has zero contraction with the symmetric stress. Interchanging the hatted and unhatted fields gives exactly the same scalar. Thus
∇⋅(σ⋅u−σ⋅u)=0.
(42)
Integrate over V and apply the divergence theorem. In its usual form the normal is outward from the fluid volume, whereas the printed n points into the fluid. Reversing the normal multiplies both integrals by the same minus sign, leaving the Lorentz reciprocal theorem for Stokes flow:
∫S​u⋅σ⋅ndS=∫S​u⋅σ⋅ndS.​
(43)
This proof applies to smooth fields or the corresponding finite-energy weak formulation. For an exterior domain one first truncates at a large sphere, applies the same identity, and takes the limit when the outer cross-work terms vanish, as for the decaying fields used below.

Let n point from the sphere into the exterior fluid, so the surface position relative to the centre is r=an. The physical surface velocity is
u∣S​=Ω×an+u′.
(44)
Free rotation means the actual body is torque-free: the total hydrodynamic torque T=∫S​an×(σ⋅n)dS vanishes. Use the auxiliary rotating sphere in Stokes flow with arbitrary Ω, boundary velocity u=Ω×an and the given traction σ⋅n=−3μΩ×n. The auxiliary flow is the decaying exterior rotation of a sphere, not a rigid rotation of the entire infinite fluid.
The left-hand cross-work in the Lorentz reciprocal theorem for Stokes flow is
∫S​u⋅σ⋅ndS=Ω⋅T=0.
(45)
The other cross-work becomes
0=−3μ∫S​(Ω×an+u′)⋅(Ω×n)dS.
(46)
The spherical integral identities are
∫S​ni​nj​dS=34πa2​δij​,∫S​dS=4πa2.
(47)
Using (Ω×n)⋅(Ω×n)=Ω⋅Ω−(Ω⋅n)(Ω⋅n), the rotational contribution is −8πμa3Ω⋅Ω. The slip contribution is −3μΩ⋅∫S​n×u′dS. Since Ω is arbitrary, the torque-free rotation of a spherical squirmer satisfies
Ω=−8πa33​∫S​n×u′dS.​
(48)
The viscosity cancels, as expected for a kinematically prescribed slip in a linear viscous problem. If the freely moving sphere also has a translation U, its added cross-work is zero because the auxiliary rotating sphere has zero total force, ∫S​σ⋅ndS=0. Thus translation does not alter this rotational formula. Without the torque-free assumption an external-torque contribution would have to be retained.

(53)
