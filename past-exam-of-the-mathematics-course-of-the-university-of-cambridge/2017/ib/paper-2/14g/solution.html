For unit u, put A=I−2uuT. Then AT=A and A2=I, so A is an orthogonal matrix. The affine reflection in a hyperplane is R(x)=Ax+2cu. Hence R(x)−R(y)=A(x−y) preserves the Euclidean norm and distance, and R(x)=x whenever u⋅x=c. Thus it is an Euclidean isometry fixing the hyperplane pointwise.
For p=q, any reflection exchanging them must have normal parallel to p−q, and its fixed hyperplane must contain their midpoint. These requirements determine the perpendicular bisector uniquely:
u=∥p−q∥p−q​,c=u⋅2p+q​,R(p)=q.​
(40)
Changing both signs of u,c leaves the same reflection. It belongs to the orthogonal group O(n) exactly when its translation term vanishes, that is, c=0. Since c=(∥p∥2−∥q∥2)/(2∥p−q∥),
R∈O(n)⟺∥p∥=∥q∥.​
(41)
To prove the finite reflection decomposition of a Euclidean isometry, first note that a distance-preserving map g fixing zero preserves inner products by the polarization identity. Its values on an orthonormal basis ei​ form an orthonormal basis, and g(x)⋅g(ei​)=xi​ shows g(x)=∑i​xi​g(ei​). Thus g is orthogonal.
Every orthogonal transformation is a product of at most n linear reflections: if g(e1​)=e1​, reflect g(e1​) to e1​ using the hyperplane normal g(e1​)−e1​, which passes through zero. The resulting orthogonal transformation fixes e1​, and its restriction to e1⊥​ is handled inductively in dimension n−1. If g(e1​)=e1​, no initial reflection is necessary. Reflections on the complement extend by fixing e1​; the induction starts in dimension zero.
For an arbitrary isometry f, if f(0)=0, first reflect f(0) to zero across its perpendicular bisector with zero. Composing this one affine reflection with f gives an orthogonal transformation. If f(0)=0, skip the initial reflection. Therefore
f is a product of at most n+1 hyperplane reflections.​
(42)
The glide reflection (x,y)↦(x+1,−y) cannot use fewer than three: its determinant sign is negative, excluding zero or two reflections, while absence of fixed points excludes a single reflection. Three suffice by reflecting successively in x=0, x=1/2, and y=0.
