1F OurBigBook.com
For a linear map α:U→V with U a finite-dimensional vector space, the rank-nullity theorem is
dimU=dimkerα+dimimα.​
(1)
Choose a basis u1​,…,uk​ of the kernel of a linear map and extend it to a basis u1​,…,uk​,v1​,…,vr​ of U. The vectors α(vj​) span the image of a linear map, because the kernel basis contributes zero. If ∑j​cj​α(vj​)=0, then ∑j​cj​vj​ belongs to the kernel and is a combination of the ui​; independence of the extended basis makes every cj​=0. Thus the image basis has r elements and dimU=k+r, proving the theorem.
For a rank-two map on R3, the direct sum occurs for α(x,y,z)=(x,y,0): its kernel is Re3​ and its image is span(e1​,e2​). It fails for β(x,y,z)=(y,z,0): the kernel is Re1​, contained in the two-dimensional image span(e1​,e2​). The rank-nullity theorem fixes the sum of the dimensions, but does not imply zero intersection.

A unique factorization domain is an integral domain in which every nonzero nonunit is a finite product of irreducible elements, uniquely up to reordering and multiplication of factors by a unit in a ring. A principal ideal domain is an integral domain in which every ideal is generated by one element.
Gauss lemma for polynomials says that over a unique factorization domain D, the product of two primitive polynomials is primitive, and the polynomial content satisfies c(fg)=c(f)c(g) up to a unit. In particular a primitive polynomial of positive degree is irreducible over D exactly when it is irreducible over the fraction field of D. Consequently a polynomial ring over a unique factorization domain is itself a unique factorization domain.
The Eisenstein criterion says that if f(X)=an​Xn+⋯+a0​∈D[X] has n≥1 and there is a prime element p with p∤an​, p∣aj​ for every j<n, and p2∤a0​, then f is irreducible over the fraction field. If f is primitive, it is also irreducible in D[X]. Over Z one may take p to be an ordinary prime number. The primitivity qualification prevents a nonunit constant content from being overlooked.
The defining distinction is unique irreducible factorization versus generation of every ideal by one element.

Take R=Q[X]​ and, for the next clause, its subring S=Z[X]. Division with remainder makes R a Euclidean domain, with the degree as Euclidean size for nonzero polynomials. For completeness, a nonzero ideal of R contains a polynomial g of least degree; division of any member by g leaves a smaller-degree remainder in the ideal, so that remainder must vanish. For the same pair, S=Z[X]⊂Q[X]​. Since Z is a unique factorization domain, Gauss lemma for polynomials proves that S is a unique factorization domain.
To prove that S is not a principal ideal domain, consider the ideal I=(2,X). A generator g would divide 2 and X. Divisibility of 2 forces g to be a nonzero constant integer; divisibility of X then forces that integer to divide its coefficient 1, so g=±1. This would make I=S. ∀ϵ>0 ∃δ>0:d(x,y)<δ⟹e(f(x),f(y))<ϵ.​

A 100γ% confidence interval is a pair of statistics L(X)≤U(X) whose random interval satisfies
Pθ​{L(X)≤θ≤U(X)}≥γfor every allowed θ.​
(16)
An exact confidence interval has equality throughout. The probability refers to repeated sampling at a fixed unknown parameter; the endpoints are random before observation. It is not a posterior probability assigned to the parameter after seeing the data. Here 0<γ<1.

For a vector equality constraint, take the Lagrangian function in constrained optimization
L(x,λ)=f(x)+λT(b−g(x)),x∈X,λ∈Rm.​
(21)
The sign of the multiplier is a convention; equality multipliers are unrestricted. The Lagrange sufficiency theorem says that if x∗∈X is feasible and, for some λ∗, globally minimizes L(⋅,λ∗) over X, then x∗ globally minimizes the constrained objective.
Indeed for any feasible x, f(x)=L(x,λ∗)≥L(x∗,λ∗)=f(x∗). Thus x∗ is a global primal minimizer​. The theorem requires a global Lagrangian minimum, not merely a stationary point. It needs neither differentiability nor convexity when stated in this form; those hypotheses can be used separately to establish the required minimum.

Let Sk​=∑ℓ=1k​Yℓ​, S0​=0, be the epochs of the renewal process. The definition uses the next epoch at or after n, so Xn​=0 at an epoch. If Xn​=i≥1, no renewal occurs before n+i and necessarily Xn+1​=i−1.
