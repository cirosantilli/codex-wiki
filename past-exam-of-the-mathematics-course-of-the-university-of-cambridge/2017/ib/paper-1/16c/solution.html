In a source-free vacuum, Maxwell equations are
∇⋅E=0,∇⋅B=0,∇×E=−∂t​B,∇×B=μ0​ϵ0​∂t​E.
(60)
Taking a curl of the last two equations and using ∇×(∇×A)=∇(∇⋅A)−∇2A gives the electromagnetic wave equations
Ett​=c2∇2E,Btt​=c2∇2B,c=μ0​ϵ0​​1​​.
(61)
Substitution of the complex plane wave amplitudes into Maxwell equations, then taking real parts, gives
k⋅e=k⋅b=0,k×e=ωb,k×b=−c2ω​e.
(62)
For a nonzero propagating wave these imply the dispersion relation ω2=c2∣k∣2​. Conversely choose any nonzero transverse complex e and set b=k×e/ω to obtain such a wave. The static uniform-field sector at ω=∣k∣=0 is separate from propagation.
At a perfect conductor, the oscillatory boundary conditions are n×E=0 and n⋅B=0. Surface charge and surface current allow nonzero normal electric and tangential magnetic fields. The magnetic condition here concerns the time-dependent wave field; a perfect conductor can retain a pre-existing static magnetic field.
Let R=I−2nnT be the orthogonal reflection across the boundary plane, so detR=−1. The proposed reflected amplitudes are k′=Rk, e′=−Re, and b′=Rb. For an orthogonal reflection, (Ra)×(Rd)=−R(a×d). Hence
k′×e′=ωb′,k′×b′=−c2ω​e′,k′⋅e′=k′⋅b′=0.
(63)
Also ∣k′∣=∣k∣, so the reflected wave obeys the same dispersion relation. At n⋅x=0, the incident and reflected phases coincide because k′−k is parallel to n. Their summed amplitudes satisfy
e+e′=2(n⋅e)n,b+b′=2[b−(n⋅b)n].
(64)
The first has zero tangential part and the second zero normal part, proving both boundary conditions. Finally k′⋅n=−k⋅n>0, as required for reflection into the vacuum.
