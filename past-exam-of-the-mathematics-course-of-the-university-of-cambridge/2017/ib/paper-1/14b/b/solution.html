For smooth rapidly decaying initial data, apply the Fourier transform in x and differentiate under the integral. The Fourier transform of a derivative turns the wave equation into
vtt​+k2v=0,v(k,0)=f​(k),vt​(k,0)=g​(k).
(51)
Solving this constant-coefficient ordinary differential equation gives
v(k,t)=f​(k)coskt+g​(k)ksinkt​​.
(52)
At k=0 the quotient means its continuous limit t, so v(0,t)=f​(0)+tg​(0).
Since coskt=(eikt+e−ikt)/2, the Translation property of the Fourier transform inverts the first term as (f(x+t)+f(x−t))/2. Also Kt​(x)=21​1[−t,t]​(x) has Fourier transform sinkt/k, including its limiting value at zero. The convolution theorem therefore inverts the second term as
(Kt​∗g)(x)=21​∫−tt​g(x−s)ds=21​∫x−tx+t​g(ξ)dξ.
(53)
This proves the D'Alembert formula
v(x,t)=2f(x−t)+f(x+t)​+21​∫x−tx+t​g(ξ)dξ​.
(54)
The formula itself extends beyond the rapidly decaying class: f∈C2 and g∈C1 suffice for a classical solution, verified by differentiation and the two initial conditions. The decay assumption above justifies the ordinary transform derivation without distributional qualifications.
