1E OurBigBook.com
If m1​,…,mn​ generate the module M, define the R-module homomorphism
T:Rn⟶M,(r1​,…,rn​)⟼∑j=1n​rj​mj​.
(1)
This is a surjection by the definition of generation. Conversely, an R-module homomorphism that is a surjection sends the standard basis vectors of the free module Rn to a generating family, since every vector in Rn is their linear combination. Thus
M is finitely generated⟺M is a quotient of some finite free module Rn.​
(2)
This is the finite generation as a quotient of a finite free module criterion. The zero module causes no exception: it is generated by the empty family, or by one zero element.
For the requested example, take M=(Z/2Z)2, with the usual integer scalar action. Reducing both coordinates modulo two gives an R-module homomorphism that is a surjection Z2→M with nonzero kernel 2Z2, so it is not an isomorphism. Every image of Z is a cyclic module, whereas every element of M has additive order at most two and M has four elements. Hence M=(Z/2Z)2​ admits no such surjection from Z.

A metric space is a complete metric space when every Cauchy sequence converges to a point of that space: for each ε>0, the condition d(xm​,xn​)<ε for all sufficiently large m,n must imply existence of x with d(xn​,x)→0.
A Cauchy sequence in [0,1] is also Cauchy in R, so it converges there by the assumed completeness. Its limit remains between zero and one, since inequalities are preserved under limits. Therefore
[0,1] with the Euclidean metric is complete.​
(3)
This is an instance of the fact that a closed subset of a complete metric space is complete with its induced metric.

The product topology has as a basis of a topology all sets U×V, where U is open in X and V is open in Y. Open sets are arbitrary unions of these rectangles. The projection maps are p(x,y)=x and q(x,y)=y. For any open U⊂X and V⊂Y,
p−1(U)=U×Y,q−1(V)=X×V.​
(6)
These are open rectangles, including the empty case. Taking inverse images of open sets therefore proves that both projection maps are continuous functions.

If X is Hausdorff, every pair x=y has disjoint open neighborhoods U and V. The rectangle U×V is an open neighborhood of (x,y) disjoint from the diagonal. Hence every point of its complement is interior, so the diagonal is a closed set.
Conversely, if the diagonal is closed, its complement is open in the product topology. For x=y, the rectangle-basis property gives open U∋x and V∋y with U×V disjoint from the diagonal. If z∈U∩V, then (z,z) lies in that rectangle, a contradiction. Thus U∩V=∅, giving the Hausdorff separation condition. Consequently
X is Hausdorff⟺ΔX​ is closed in X×X.​
(7)
The argument includes spaces with zero or one point, for which both conditions hold.

A reversible Markov chain has a stationary law π for which every finite path has the same distribution as its time-reversed path:
(X0​,…,Xk​)=d(Xk​,…,X0​)for every k≥0.​
(25)
For a transition matrix P, the equivalent detailed balance condition is πi​Pij​=πj​Pji​ for all i,j. It implies πP=π by summing over i. Multiplying the balance identities along a path shows equality of its forward and reverse probabilities when X0​∼π; conversely, taking paths of length one gives those identities.

For the usual undirected graph with at least two vertices, a simple random walk chooses a neighbor uniformly at each step, so Pij​=1/di​ when i and j are adjacent and zero otherwise, where di​ is the degree of a vertex. The handshaking lemma gives ∑i​di​=2∣E∣. Thus the stationary distribution of a graph random walk is
πi​=2∣E∣di​​.​
(26)
For adjacent i,j, πi​Pij​=1/(2∣E∣)=πj​Pji​, and for nonadjacent pairs both sides vanish. By detailed balance, this is an invariant distribution and the walk started from it is a reversible Markov chain. Connectedness makes the finite walk an irreducible Markov chain, giving uniqueness of its invariant distribution. No aperiodicity assumption is needed for uniqueness, so a bipartite graph is allowed.
If the connected graph has a single isolated vertex, the usual neighbor-choice formula is undefined. With the natural absorbing convention P11​=1, its unique invariant distribution is π1​=1 and it is reversible. The degree-normalization formula presupposes at least one edge.

Associate to f the symmetric bilinear form B(u,v)=21​(f(u+v)−f(u)−f(v)). If f is identically zero, every basis is diagonal. Otherwise choose v with f(v)=0. Every w has the decomposition
w=B(v,v)B(w,v)​v+(w−B(v,v)B(w,v)​v),
(27)
whose second term lies in v⊥. Thus V=Rv⊕v⊥, with no cross term in f. Induction on dimension gives a diagonal basis. Rescaling its nonzero entries reduces the form to
f=∑i=1p​xi2​−∑j=1q​yj2​,
(28)
with k additional zero coordinates. Define the rank of a quadratic form r=p+q and its signature s=p−q. Rank is intrinsic, since it is the rank of v↦B(v,⋅), equivalently n−dimradB. Also p is the greatest dimension of a subspace on which the restriction of f is a positive-definite quadratic form: any such subspace intersects the negative-plus-zero coordinate space only at zero, so its dimension is at most p, while the positive coordinate space attains p. The corresponding negative statement identifies q. This proves the basis independence of r and s, the Sylvester's law of inertia conclusion.
For a totally isotropic subspace W on which f vanishes, polarization identity also makes B vanish on W×W. Quotient by the radical of a bilinear form. In the resulting nondegenerate form, projection of an isotropic subspace to either the positive or negative coordinate space is injective, so its dimension is at most min(p,q). Consequently dimW≤k+min(p,q). The radical of a bilinear form together with the vectors ei+​+ei−​, 1≤i≤min(p,q), attains this bound. The maximum dimension of a totally isotropic subspace is therefore
dimWmax​=n−r+2r−∣s∣​=n−2r+∣s∣​.​
(29)
The criterion for membership in a diagonal basis follows similarly: a nonzero vector can belong to a diagonal basis if it has nonzero quadratic value, by the same orthogonal splitting, or if it lies in the radical of a bilinear form, by choosing a radical basis containing it. An isotropic vector in a diagonal basis must be orthogonal to every basis vector, and hence must lie in that radical. For the specified form, its radical is the z-axis, giving exactly
v=(x,y,z) qualifies iff (x2=y2) or (x=y=0 and z=0).​
(30)

A Euclidean domain is an integral domain R with a function ν:R∖{0}→N such that for each a∈R and b=0 there are q,r∈R with a=qb+r and either r=0 or ν(r)<ν(b). Some conventions add a multiplicative monotonicity condition; it is unnecessary for this implication.
Let I be a nonzero ideal. Choose d∈I∖{0} minimizing ν(d), possible because the values lie in the nonnegative integers. For a∈I, division by d gives a=qd+r, with r=a−qd∈I. A nonzero remainder would contradict minimality, so r=0 and a∈(d). Since d∈I, the reverse inclusion also holds. The zero ideal is (0). Thus
every ideal is principal, so every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain.​
(31)

A contraction mapping satisfies d(Tx,Ty)≤qd(x,y) for all x,y, with one fixed 0≤q<1. The contraction mapping theorem states that such a map on a nonempty complete metric space has a unique fixed point and that every sequence of iterates converges to it.
Choose x0​ and put xn+1​=Txn​. Iteration gives d(xn+1​,xn​)≤qnd(x1​,x0​), so for m>n,
d(xm​,xn​)≤∑j=nm−1​qjd(x1​,x0​)≤1−qqn​d(x1​,x0​).
(36)
Thus the iterates form a Cauchy sequence and converge by completeness to x∗​. The Lipschitz bound gives continuity of T, hence Tx∗​=x∗​. Two fixed points satisfy d(x∗​,y∗​)≤qd(x∗​,y∗​), forcing equality of the points. Passing m→∞ also gives the usual geometric error estimate. For q=0, convergence occurs after one application.
If fk is a contraction mapping, let x∗​ be its unique fixed point. The commutation identity fkf=ffk gives fk(fx∗​)=fx∗​, so uniqueness implies fx∗​=x∗​. Any fixed point of f is a fixed point of fk, giving uniqueness, without even assuming f continuous. On the complete discrete metric space {0,1,2}, the map f(0)=f(1)=0, f(2)=1 has constant second iterate but is not a contraction: d(f2,f0)=d(2,0)=1.

In a matrix game, entry aij​ is the row player's payoff, and the column player's payoff is −aij​. A mixed strategy is a probability vector over the available rows or columns. With strategies p,q, the row payoff is pTAq. Its optimal mixed strategy maximizes its guaranteed payoff, while the column player minimizes the largest row payoff:
p∈Δm​max​q∈Δn​min​pTAq=q∈Δn​min​p∈Δm​max​pTAq=v.​
(77)
The equality is the finite minimax theorem. Equivalently the row player maximizes minj​(pTA)j​, and the column player minimizes maxi​(Aq)i​. Each optimal strategy guarantees its corresponding bound against every opponent strategy; for the column player's own negative payoff this is also a maximin strategy.
