A Lie point symmetry is a local one-parameter family of diffeomorphisms of (t,x) that takes solution graphs of the differential equation to solution graphs wherever the transformed graph remains a graph over time. Its infinitesimal generator is Y=τ(t,x)∂t​+∑i​ηi​(t,x)∂xi​​. Its first prolongation is
pr(1)Y=Y+∑i​(Dt​ηi​−x˙i​Dt​τ)∂x˙i​​,Dt​=∂t​+∑i​x˙i​∂xi​​.
(119)
For a regular first-order equation the infinitesimal criterion is pr(1)Y(Δ)=0 on Δ=0; it says that the prolonged vector field is tangent to the equation submanifold.
With the constant nondegenerate canonical matrix J, define the Poisson bracket by {f,g}=(∇f)TJ∇g and the Hamiltonian vector field by Vf​=J∇f. Our convention gives Vf​[h]={h,f}. Applying the Jacobi identity to a test function h,
[Vf​,Vg​]h={{h,g},f}−{{h,f},g}=−{h,{f,g}},
(120)
so
[Vf​,Vg​]=−V{f,g}​​.
(121)
A first integral F obeys dF/dt={F,H}=0. Consequently [VF​,VH​]=0, and the local flows commute. The time-independent transformation generated by VF​, with t unchanged, therefore maps integral curves of a vector field for VH​ to integral curves of a vector field: it is a Lie point symmetry.
Conversely, such a time-independent symmetry gives [VF​,VH​]=0, hence V{F,H}​=0. Nondegeneracy of J makes {F,H} constant on each connected component. At a fixed point x0​, J∇H(x0​)=0 implies ∇H(x0​)=0, so that constant is zero on its component. Thus the converse holds on a connected phase space containing a fixed point, or on each component that contains one.
The printed global converse needs this connectedness convention: a fixed point on one component does not control the constants on other components. For example take one component with H=(q2+p2)/2, F=0, and another with H=p, F=q. The Hamiltonian vector fields commute everywhere and there is a fixed point on the first component, but F is not a first integral on the second. Even on a connected space the fixed-point condition matters: H=p, F=q commute but F˙=1.
