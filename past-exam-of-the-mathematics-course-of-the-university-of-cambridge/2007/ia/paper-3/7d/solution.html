Allow an eigenvector v=0 to have complex entries, since real matrices can initially have complex eigenvalues. Write v∗ for its conjugate transpose. Reality of A gives A∗=AT; symmetry then gives A∗=A. Thus A is a Hermitian matrix. The scalar v∗Av equals its complex conjugate, because (v∗Av)∗=v∗A∗v=v∗Av. If Av=λv, then
λ=v∗vv∗Av​∈R,
(14)
since v∗v>0. This proves that all eigenvalues are real. The equality A∗=AT is precisely where reality was used; a complex symmetric matrix need not be Hermitian, as iI illustrates.
If Au=λu and Av=μv, then u∗Av=μu∗v, but also u∗Av=(Au)∗v=λu∗v=λu∗v. Consequently (μ−λ)u∗v=0. Distinct eigenvalues force u∗v=0. For real eigenvectors this is ordinary Euclidean orthogonality. These arguments establish real symmetric spectral orthogonality without assuming a diagonalization in advance.
A real orthogonal matrix satisfies PTP=I, equivalently P−1=PT. If A is symmetric,
(P−1AP)T=(PTAP)T=PTATP=PTAP=P−1AP,
(15)
so orthogonal similarity preserves symmetry. An arbitrary real invertible change of basis need not do so. For example,

The final matrix is not symmetric. Orthogonal similarity preserves real symmetry; general similarity does not.
