In the normalized second-order linear differential equation, an ordinary point z0​ is one at which both p and q are holomorphic functions in a neighbourhood of z0​. A singular point of a differential equation is a point that is not ordinary. A singular point z0​ is a regular singular point if (z−z0​)p(z) and (z−z0​)2q(z) extend analytically to z0​. Equivalently, p has at most a simple pole and q at most a double pole there. If a singular point fails this test, it is an irregular singular point. The normalized form matters: zeros of a leading coefficient must first be assessed after division by that coefficient.

Multiply the first-order linear differential equation by the integrating factor et/α. Then
dtd​(et/αy)=α1​et/αf(t).
(16)
If y→0 in the remote past, then et/αy→0 there. Integration from −∞ gives the necessary formula
y(t)=α1​∫−∞t​e−(t−s)/αf(s)ds.​
(17)
When this integral exists and tends to zero as t→−∞, differentiating verifies the equation and the past condition. Two such solutions differ by Ce−t/α, which diverges at −∞ unless C=0, proving uniqueness. This is the causal response of a first-order relaxation equation, a convolution with its decaying impulse response.
The phrase “general function” implicitly requires compatibility with the past condition: it does not guarantee existence for arbitrary f. A sufficient condition is bounded forcing with f(t)→0 as t→−∞; after setting r=t−s, the integral is bounded by the supremum of ∣f∣ before t. For example, f≡1 gives y=1+Ce−t/α, so no solution has the stated past limit. Both forcings below vanish in the past and meet the intended causal condition, the impulse being interpreted distributionally.

The forcing is a unit pulse on 0<t<1. For t<0, the homogeneous equation and continuity with y(0)=0 give y=0. On 0<t<1, integration with y(0)=0 gives y=1−e−t. At the end of the pulse its value is 1−e−1, and the subsequent homogeneous decay must start at that value. Thus
y(t)=⎩⎨⎧​0,1−e−t,(1−e−1)e−(t−1),​t≤0,0≤t≤1,t≥1.​​
(21)
Equivalently it is the difference of two translated step responses, (1−e−t)H(t)−(1−e−(t−1))H(t−1). The solution is continuous, rises concavely to its maximum 1−e−1 at t=1, and then decays exponentially to zero. Its derivative has jumps at the pulse edges; those are permitted because the right-hand side has step discontinuities.

For a nonnegative integer-valued random variable, its expected value is EN=∑r=0∞​rpr​, allowing the value +∞. Since r=∑n=1r​1, rearranging nonnegative summands gives the tail-sum formula for expectation:
EN=∑r=0∞​∑n=1r​pr​=∑n=1∞​∑r=n∞​pr​=n=1∑∞​P(N≥n).​
(25)
No integrability assumption is needed for the rearrangement. The same formula holds for an extended nonnegative integer-valued variable by the monotone convergence theorem, using N=∑n≥1​1{N≥n}​.
For the first ascent in independent continuous observations, continuity makes ties probability zero. By order symmetry of independent random variables, the first m observations have each of their m! strict orderings with equal probability. For n≥2, N≥n means exactly that the first n−1 observations are decreasing, so
P(N≥1)=1,P(N≥n)=(n−1)!1​(n≥2).
(26)
Subtracting consecutive tails gives
P(N=r)=(r−1)!1​−r!1​=r!r−1​,r≥2,​

EN=1+n=2∑∞​(n−1)!1​=e.​
(28)
The first observation cannot itself end the run; this is why the tail includes the separate term P(N≥1)=1.

Let Θ=∠AOB and let the sphere have radius R. By rotational invariance, condition on A and place it at the north pole. A band at polar angle θ has surface area 2πR2sinθdθ, while the sphere has area 4πR2. Thus the central angle between independent uniform sphere points has density
fΘ​(θ)=21​sinθ(0<θ<π),​
(39)
with density zero outside that interval.
Condition now on A,B with their angle fixed at θ. For ∠AOC and ∠BOC to be acute, the unit position vector of C must have positive dot products with both unit vectors of A and B. Hence C must lie in the intersection of the two corresponding open hemispheres. The great circles bounding them meet at the two antipodes perpendicular to the plane of A,B. Their common hemisphere region is a spherical lune of opening π−θ: when A=B it is a hemisphere of opening π, and as the two normals separate, that opening decreases by θ. More explicitly, take the common boundary antipodes as poles. The longitude conditions each allow an interval of length π, with their centres separated by θ, so the intersection has longitude width π−θ.
Integrating the spherical area element R2sinφdφdλ over that lune gives area 2R2(π−θ). Thus the conditional probability that the two angles involving C are acute is (π−θ)/(2π). The remaining angle Θ must itself be acute, so the desired probability is
P=∫0π/2​2ππ−θ​2sinθ​dθ.
(40)
Integration by parts gives ∫0π/2​θsinθdθ=1, while ∫0π/2​sinθdθ=1. Therefore
P=4ππ−1​.​
(41)
This calculates pairwise acute central angles of three uniform sphere points by conditional area, rather than incorrectly treating the three angle events as independent. Boundary right angles have probability zero, so choosing open rather than closed hemispheres does not change the result.
