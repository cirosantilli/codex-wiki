Define the binomial coefficient (kn​) as the number of k-element subsets of an n-element set. Equivalently it is n!/[k!(n−k)!]: there are n(n−1)⋯(n−k+1) ordered lists of k distinct elements, and each subset gives k! such lists. The empty subset gives (0n​)=1.

(kn−1​)+(k−1n−1​)=(kn​).​

Take upward velocity as positive, and consider the closed material system consisting of the rocket and the fuel it ejects during a short interval dt. Initially its mass and momentum are m and mv. Let δm>0 be the ejected mass, so the rocket's mass change is dm=−δm. At the end its velocity is v+dv, while the ejected gas has inertial velocity v−u to first order; its downward relative velocity need not be downward in the inertial frame.
The final total momentum, to first order, is
(m−δm)(v+dv)+δm(v−u)=mv+mdv−uδm.
(8)
Neglect air resistance and take the gravitational acceleration g constant. The external gravitational impulse on this material system is −mgdt to first order. Newton's second law therefore gives mdv−uδm=−mgdt, or
mdtdv​=−udtdm​−gm.​
(9)
The positive thrust is −um˙ because fuel loss makes m˙<0. Applying d(mv)/dt to the rocket alone without accounting for the escaping momentum would give the wrong equation.
For constant u and positive mass, divide by m and integrate from release at t=0:
v(t)=−ulnm0​m(t)​−gt.
(10)
Thus the gravity-corrected rocket equation is equivalent to
m(t)=m0​exp[−ugt+v(t)​].​
(11)

The potential factors as V=x2(3−2x), so its zeros are 0 and 3/2, with a double zero at the origin. Its derivatives are V′=6x(1−x) and V′′=6−12x. Thus there is a local minimum (0,0), a local maximum (1,1) and an inflection at (1/2,1/2). The potential tends to +∞ on the far left and −∞ on the far right. These features determine the left-hand sketch.
Put v=x˙. Newton's second law gives the phase plane system x˙=v, v˙=−V′=6x(x−1). Its conserved mechanical energy and trajectories are
E=21​v2+3x2−2x3,v=±2[E−V(x)]​.​
(12)
The linearization at (0,0) has eigenvalues ±i6​, and the surrounding energy curves are closed, so it is a center equilibrium. At (1,0) the eigenvalues are ±6​, giving a saddle equilibrium. The figure shows the cubic potential barrier phase portrait; arrows point right when v>0 and left when v<0.
For 0<E<1, there are three turning-point roots of V(x)=E. The two leftmost enclose a closed orbit around the origin: the particle oscillates between them. There is also a separate open component to the right of the third root, where an incoming particle turns and escapes to +∞. The closed orbits run clockwise in the (x,v) plane.
For E=1, the factorization 1−V(x)=(x−1)2(2x+1) gives the separatrix
v2=2(x−1)2(2x+1).
(13)
The branch with −1/2≤x<1 is a homoclinic orbit: it leaves the saddle asymptotically, turns at x=−1/2 and returns asymptotically. On x>1, one branch approaches the saddle and the other departs towards infinity. Approaching the saddle takes infinite time. The saddle itself is also an equilibrium trajectory.
For E>1, the particle can cross the barrier. There is one left turning point below −1/2; an incoming particle passes through the well, turns on the left and escapes to the right. For E<0, only a right-hand open component exists, with its turning point beyond 3/2. At E=0, the stationary centre coexists with a right-hand open trajectory turning at 3/2. Thus negative energy does not imply trapping here: the cubic potential is unbounded below on the right.

We use these countability facts: the integers are countable; a finite Cartesian product of countable sets is countable; a countable union of countable sets is countable; and every subset of a countable set is countable. Finite products can be enumerated by listing index tuples in increasing maximum coordinate, and countable unions by diagonal enumeration of pairs of indices.
Fix d≥1. For each degree bound D, there are finitely many monomials X1α1​​⋯Xdαd​​ with α1​+⋯+αd​≤D. An integer polynomial of total degree at most D is specified by an integer coefficient for each of them, so these polynomials form a finite Cartesian product of copies of Z and are countable. Every polynomial has some finite degree bound. Taking the union over D=0,1,2,… proves the countability of integer polynomial rings:
Z[X1​,…,Xd​] is countably infinite.​
(18)
It is infinite because it contains all integer constant polynomials.
For d=1, each nonzero polynomial has only finitely many real roots: repeated application of the factor theorem bounds their number by its degree. The real algebraic numbers are the union of these finite root sets over the countably many nonzero integer polynomials. Thus they are countable. If the real transcendental numbers were countable too, their union with the algebraic numbers would make R countable, contrary to the allowed uncountability assumption. Hence there are uncountably many real transcendental numbers.
To construct an algebraically independent real sequence, choose x1​ outside the algebraic numbers. Suppose x1​,…,xd​ already satisfy the required nonvanishing condition. For each nonzero P∈Z[X1​,…,Xd​,T], write
P(X1​,…,Xd​,T)=∑j=0s​Pj​(X1​,…,Xd​)Tj.
(19)
At least one coefficient polynomial Pj​ is nonzero, and the inductive hypothesis makes its value at (x1​,…,xd​) nonzero. Consequently P(x1​,…,xd​,T) is a nonzero one-variable polynomial and has only finitely many real roots. There are countably many choices of P, so the union of all these forbidden roots is countable. Choose xd+1​ outside it, which is possible because R is uncountable. This extends the nonvanishing condition to d+1. Recursion therefore gives
f(x1​,…,xd​)=0for every d≥1 and nonzero f∈Z[X1​,…,Xd​].​
(20)
In particular the chosen numbers are distinct, since a relation Xi​−Xj​ is forbidden. Clearing rational denominators shows that every finite initial segment consists of algebraically independent elements over Q.

A real sequence xn​ converges to l if, for every ε>0, there is an integer N such that ∣xn​−l∣<ε for every n≥N. A convergent series ∑n=1∞​xn​ is one whose partial sums sN​=∑n=1N​xn​ converge to a finite real limit.
If sN​→s, then xN​=sN​−sN−1​→s−s=0. This necessary condition is the term test for divergence. Its converse fails: the harmonic series has terms 1/n→0 but diverges. For a direct proof, each block from n=2j−1+1 to 2j contains 2j−1 terms each at least 2−j, so contributes at least 1/2. Infinitely many such blocks make the partial sums unbounded.
For the positive sequences in the final request, the inequality makes yn​ decreasing and bounded below by zero. The bounded monotone sequence theorem gives yn​→l≥0. Summing the decrements gives
21​∑n=1N​min(xn​,yn​)≤y1​−yN+1​≤y1​.
(21)
Suppose l>0. Since yn​≥l, this implies
∑n=1N​min(xn​,l)≤2y1​for every N.
(22)
But clipping preserves divergence of a positive series: if infinitely many xn​≥l, the clipped series has infinitely many terms equal to l; otherwise its tail equals the divergent tail of ∑xn​. Either case contradicts the displayed bound. Hence the only possible limit is
yn​⟶0.​
(23)
This proves the minimum-decrement convergence criterion without assuming that the divergent positive sequence xn​ itself tends to zero.

If the prime p does not divide x, then gcd(p,x)=1. The Bezout identity gives integers a,b with ap+bx=1. Multiplying by y gives apy+bxy=y. If p∣xy, both terms on the left are divisible by p, so p∣y. Thus Euclid lemma holds:
p∣xy⟹p∣x or p∣y.​
(24)
For completeness, existence in the Fundamental theorem of arithmetic follows by strong induction on a positive integer n>1. If n is prime there is nothing to prove; otherwise n=ab with 1<a,b<n, and the inductive prime factorizations of a,b give one for n. The integer 1 has the empty factorization.
For uniqueness, suppose p1​⋯pr​=q1​⋯qs​ are two prime factorizations. Repeated application of Euclid lemma shows that p1​ divides some qj​. Both are primes, so they are equal. Reorder the second list, cancel this equal factor, and repeat. All factors must be matched, including their multiplicities. Thus prime factorization exists and is unique up to the order of its factors. For nonzero negative integers one also records the sign.

The Fermat-Euler theorem states that, for a positive integer q and an integer a coprime to q, aφ(q)≡1(modq), where the Euler totient function φ(q) counts residue classes coprime to q. The case q=1 is trivial. For q>1, let r1​,…,rφ(q)​ be a reduced residue system. Multiplication by a permutes it: equality of two resulting residues can be cancelled using the modular inverse of a, and the products remain coprime to q. Multiplying the residues before and after the permutation gives
aφ(q)r1​⋯rφ(q)​≡r1​⋯rφ(q)​(modq).
(25)
Every factor on the right is a unit, so its product can be cancelled. This proves
aφ(q)≡1(modq).​
(26)
The integer 359 is prime: none of the primes 2,3,5,7,11,13,17 up to 359​<19 divides it. Therefore φ(359)=358. Apply the theorem to 60 and use the supplied congruence:
10179≡(602)179=60358≡1(mod359).
(27)
To connect this to individual digits rather than merely assert periodicity, use long-division remainders r0​=1 and
10rn−1​=359an​+rn​,0≤rn​<359.
(28)
Induction gives rn​≡10n(mod359). Since 10 is coprime to 359, no remainder is zero. The congruence above therefore implies rn+179​=rn​ for every n≥0, using the unique representative in 1,…,358. In particular
an+179​=⌊35910rn+178​​⌋=⌊35910rn−1​​⌋=an​.
(29)
Hence
an+179​=an​(n≥1).​
(30)
This is the mechanism of decimal period from multiplicative order: the period divides 179.
