Let p be a permutation of the finite set. Starting from a, repeatedly apply p. Some iterate repeats; because p is invertible, the first repeat closes the sequence at a. More explicitly, if pi(a)=pj(a) with 0≤i<j, applying p−i gives pj−i(a)=a. If r is the least positive return time, the elements a,p(a),…,pr−1(a) are distinct and form one permutation cycle. If the set is not exhausted, start again at an unused element. Two such sets cannot overlap: an overlap lets an inverse iterate express either starting point as an iterate of the other, making the sets identical. Thus this process partitions the set into disjoint permutation cycles. On each part its cycle acts exactly as p and the other cycles fix the elements. Their product therefore equals p. This proves existence of the cycle decomposition of a permutation, including one-cycles for fixed points.
Use the composition convention that the rightmost permutation acts first. For the specified product, the images are
1↦2↦3↦1,4↦5↦4,6↦7↦8↦6.
(1)
Hence
στ=(123)(45)(678),ord(στ)=6.​
(2)
The order of a group element here is the least positive exponent making every cycle return to the identity. It must be divisible by each cycle length, and any common multiple does make every cycle return. The least common multiple of 3,2,3 is six.

A group isomorphism f:G→H is a bijection satisfying f(xy)=f(x)f(y) for all x,y∈G. It sends the identity to the identity and satisfies f(xk)=f(x)k. Applying its inverse shows that it preserves the order of a group element exactly.
The cyclic group C8​ has an element of order eight. Every element of the direct product of groups C4​×C2​ has fourth power equal to the identity, and a generator of the first factor paired with the identity of the second has order four. Every nonidentity element of C2​×C2​×C2​ has order two. Thus their maximum element orders are respectively 8,4,2, which an isomorphism would preserve. The three groups are pairwise nonisomorphic.
For a fourth example take the dihedral group of symmetries of a square. It consists of four rotations and four reflections, so has order eight. With r a quarter-turn and s a reflection, srs=r−1=r, showing sr=rs. This group is nonabelian, whereas all three displayed direct products of groups are abelian groups. Since a group isomorphism preserves commutativity, the square's dihedral group is isomorphic to none of them. Its order is eight regardless of whether a notation convention calls it D4​ or D8​.

The order of a group element x is the least positive integer r with xr=e, or infinity if none exists. In a finite group, some two powers among e,x,…,x∣G∣ coincide; cancellation then gives a positive power equal to e, so r exists. The generated cyclic group H=⟨x⟩ consists of the distinct elements e,x,…,xr−1 and has size r.
We now prove the needed divisibility directly by cosets. Multiplication by g bijects H with the left coset gH, so every left coset has r elements. If gH and hH intersect, write ga=hb with a,b∈H. Then h−1g=ba−1∈H, which implies gH=hH. Thus distinct left cosets are disjoint, and every element of G belongs to its own left coset. The finite set G is partitioned into, say, k such cosets. Hence ∣G∣=kr and
ord(x)∣∣G∣.​
(11)
This supplies the counting argument underlying Lagrange's theorem, rather than appealing to it or to an unproved orbit formula.
A divisor of ∣G∣ need not occur as an element order. Use G=C2​×C2​×C2​, of order eight, and d=4<8. Every element has square equal to the identity, so there is no element of order four. Thus the proposed converse is false, even for an abelian group.
For the direct product of groups Cm​×Cn​, let a,b generate the factors. The least k>0 for which (a,b)k=(e,e) is the least common multiple of m,n. If m,n are coprime, this is mn, the size of the whole product, so (a,b) generates every element. Conversely, for any pair (u,v) its order is lcm(ordu,ordv) and divides lcm(m,n). If gcd(m,n)>1, that bound is mn/gcd(m,n)<mn, so no element can generate the product. Therefore
Cm​×Cn​ is cyclic exactly when gcd(m,n)=1.​
(12)
This is the cyclicity of a product of two finite cyclic groups; the general pair-order criterion is the order of a direct-product element.

A subgroup H is a normal subgroup of G if gHg−1=H for every g∈G, equivalently if gH=Hg for every g. In the symmetric group S3​, the subgroup A3​={e,(123),(132)} is normal: conjugation relabels a three-cycle as a three-cycle, so preserves this set. By contrast, {e,(12)} is not normal because conjugating (12) by (23) gives (13) outside the subgroup.
If H is normal, define a product on its left cosets by (gH)(kH)=(gk)H. To check that this quotient group operation is well-defined, take different representatives gh1​ and kh2​, with h1​,h2​∈H. Their product is
gh1​kh2​=gk(k−1h1​k)h2​.
(13)
Normality puts the last two factors in H, so its coset is exactly gkH. Associativity follows from that in G, the identity coset is H, and the inverse of gH is g−1H. Thus all the group axioms hold, and the projection g↦gH is a group homomorphism.

Work on the Riemann sphere, including zero and infinity. For j(z)=1/z, two iterations give the identity and one does not, so ord(j)=2. For k(z)=1/(1−z), direct composition gives
k2(z)=zz−1​,k3(z)=z.
(21)
Neither k nor k2 is the identity; in particular 0↦1↦∞↦0. Thus
ord(z↦1/z)=2,ord(z↦1/(1−z))=3.​
(22)
To prove the Möbius conjugacy normal forms, write a Möbius transformation as f(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d) with ad−bc=0. A finite fixed point satisfies cz2+(d−a)z−b=0. If c=0, this quadratic has two roots counting multiplicity, and infinity is not fixed. If c=0, the transformation is affine, infinity is fixed, and there is one further finite fixed point unless its slope is one. A nonidentity slope-one affine map is a nonzero translation with only infinity fixed. Thus any nonidentity Möbius transformation has one or two distinct fixed points.
If there are two, say p,q, choose a Möbius transformation h taking them to zero and infinity. For finite p,q one can use h(z)=(z−p)/(z−q); the cases involving infinity use an affine map or reciprocal. The conjugate hfh−1 fixes zero and infinity. Fixing infinity sets its denominator's linear coefficient to zero, and fixing zero sets its numerator's constant term to zero; hence it is z↦μz with μ=0.
If there is only one fixed point, send it to infinity. The conjugate has the affine form az+b. If a=1, b/(1−a) would be another fixed point, which is impossible; therefore a=1,b=0. Conjugating this translation by z↦z/b yields z↦z+1. The identity is already the scaling μ=1. This proves the classification, and invertibility excludes μ=0.
Conjugation carries fixed-point sets bijectively. The translation z+1 has exactly one fixed point, infinity. A nonidentity scaling has the two fixed points zero and infinity, while the identity fixes every point. Therefore z+1 is not conjugate to any scaling.
Finally let f have finite positive order n. A nonzero translation has infinite order, since its kth iterate is z+k after normalization. Thus f is conjugate to z↦μz, where μ has multiplicative order exactly n. For n>1, zero and infinity each form a singleton orbit. At a finite nonzero z, an iterate returns precisely when μkz=z, equivalently μk=1; the least positive return time is n. Conjugacy preserves orbit sizes. Hence the finite-order Möbius orbit sizes are
1 and n(n>1),only 1(n=1).​
(23)
For n>1 exactly two points have size-one orbits; every other point has orbit size n.

Assume P,Q are continuously differentiable on a neighbourhood of a bounded region with a positively oriented piecewise smooth simple boundary. To prove Green theorem, first take a region between the graphs y=f(x) and y=g(x), a≤x≤b, with f≤g. Counterclockwise orientation traverses the lower graph to the right and the upper graph to the left; vertical end pieces contribute nothing to Pdx. Thus the fundamental theorem of calculus gives
∮∂R​Pdx=∫ab​[P(x,f(x))−P(x,g(x))]dx=−∫ab​∫f(x)g(x)​Py​dydx.
(26)
Likewise, for a region between graphs x=u(y) and x=v(y), the right boundary is traversed upwards and the left one downwards. Horizontal end pieces contribute nothing to Qdy, so
∮∂R​Qdy=∫cd​[Q(v(y),y)−Q(u(y),y)]dy=∫cd​∫u(y)v(y)​Qx​dxdy.
(27)
For a polygonal region, triangulate its interior. Each triangle is of both graph types with piecewise linear bounds, so adding the formulae gives the result: the double integrals add and the integrals on internal edges cancel because the adjacent triangles traverse them in opposite directions. For a regular piecewise smooth simple boundary, choose inscribed simple polygonal approximations to its piecewise smooth parametrization. Their line integrals converge to the original line integral, since the parametrizations converge uniformly and their piecewise constant tangent vectors converge in the integral norm on each smooth arc. Their regions converge in area: the symmetric difference lies in a shrinking neighbourhood of the original boundary, which has planar measure zero. Boundedness of the continuous integrand then gives convergence of the area integrals. Passing to the limit proves the formula for the stated boundary class. Combining the two identities gives
∮∂R​(Pdx+Qdy)=∬R​(Qx​−Py​)dxdy.​
(28)
Positive orientation is essential for the sign.
Choose P=−y/2 and Q=x/2, for which Qx​−Py​=1. The boundary formula for planar area is
area(R)=21​∮∂R​(xdy−ydx).​
(29)
For the ellipse, take a,b>0 so the given increasing-angle parametrization is counterclockwise. Then dx=−asinθdθ and dy=bcosθdθ, so
area(R)=21​∫02π​ab(cos2θ+sin2θ)dθ=πab​.
(30)
If signed nonzero parameters are allowed, the unsigned area is π∣ab∣, with orientation corrected accordingly.

For a Cartesian second-rank tensor, write
Pij​=21​(Pij​+Pji​)+21​(Pij​−Pji​).
(45)
The first term is a symmetric second-rank tensor and the second is an antisymmetric second-rank tensor. Taking the trace of the symmetric term and subtracting it gives the scalar, symmetric-traceless and axial tensor decomposition:
P=31​Pkk​,Sij​=21​(Pij​+Pji​)−31​Pkk​δij​,Ak​=21​ϵkij​Pij​.​
(46)
Here Sij​=Sji​ and Sii​=0. To check reconstruction of the antisymmetric part, contract the Levi-Civita symbols:
ϵijk​Ak​=21​ϵijk​ϵklm​Plm​=21​(δil​δjm​−δim​δjl​)Plm​=21​(Pij​−Pji​).
(47)
Thus Pij​=Pδij​+Sij​+ϵijk​Ak​, and the displayed contractions also prove uniqueness. The vector associated with the antisymmetric part is an axial vector under reflections, and an ordinary vector under proper rotations.
For the specified isotropic tensor law, contract the Kronecker deltas first:
Pij​=αδij​Tkk​+βTij​+γTji​.
(48)
Write Tij​=Tδij​+Wij​+ϵijk​Vk​, where T=Tkk​/3 and W is symmetric and traceless. Transposition preserves its scalar and symmetric-traceless parts and reverses its antisymmetric part. Therefore
Pij​=(3α+β+γ)Tδij​+(β+γ)Wij​+(β−γ)ϵijk​Vk​.
(49)
Uniqueness of the decomposition gives the isotropic elasticity on tensor components relations. Provided their respective coefficients are nonzero, the requested inverses are
T=3α+β+γP​,Wij​=β+γSij​​,Vk​=β−γAk​​.​
(50)
If one coefficient vanishes, that strain component has zero stress response: the corresponding stress component must be zero for a solution to exist, and that strain component is not uniquely recoverable. Isotropy by itself does not guarantee invertibility.
If Tij​ is symmetric then V=0, hence A=0 and the stress is symmetric. Equivalently, direct transposition gives Pij​−Pji​=(β−γ)(Tij​−Tji​)=0. Restricting to this symmetric-strain case gives
Pij​=λδij​Tkk​+μTij​,λ=α,μ=β+γ.​
(51)
This final two-parameter expression uses the symmetry assumption from the preceding request. For a general nonsymmetric array, the transpose term cannot be absorbed into the same coefficient: it acts with β+γ on the symmetric-traceless part and with β−γ on the antisymmetric part. The stated coefficient μ is the coefficient of Tij​ itself; if one instead writes the conventional elastic law with 2μ multiplying the strain, that shear-modulus convention is half this coefficient.
