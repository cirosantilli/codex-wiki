A real-valued function f on an interval I is uniformly continuous when
∀ε>0 ∃δ>0 ∀x,y∈I:∣x−y∣<δ ⟹ ∣f(x)−f(y)∣<ε.

a OurBigBook.com
Every point has arbitrarily small path-connected open neighbourhoods. Indeed, in a coordinate chart homeomorphic to Rn, take the inverse image of a sufficiently small open Euclidean ball. The ball is convex, hence path-connected, and its inverse image is path-connected by the homeomorphism. If the supplied neighbourhood is not itself open, first take an open neighbourhood within it and a ball contained in its chart image. This proves that X is a locally path-connected space.
Fix x0​∈X and let A consist of the points joined to x0​ by a path. For x∈A, a path-connected open neighbourhood Ux​ is contained in A: concatenate a path from x0​ to x with one from x to any point of Ux​. Hence A is open. For x∈/A, the same kind of neighbourhood cannot meet A, since a meeting point would provide a path to x. Hence X∖A is also open. Since X is a connected space and A is nonempty, A=X. This proves X is path-connected, including the general principle that connected locally path-connected spaces are path-connected. The empty-space case, if allowed, is vacuous.

Every nonempty open set in the given topology contains 1. Suppose a continuous map f took distinct values f(m) and f(n). Choose disjoint open intervals U,V⊂R containing these respective values. By continuity, f−1(U) and f−1(V) are nonempty open sets in N, and therefore both contain 1. This is impossible because the inverse images of disjoint sets are disjoint. Thus every such continuous map is constant. Conversely, constant maps are continuous. This is the continuous real maps from the initial-segment topology argument, using the Hausdorff separation of distinct real points.

For a differentiable equality constraint, the Lagrange multiplier method finds candidate extrema by solving
∇f=λ∇g,g=c.
(12)
The condition requires ∇g=0 at the point: every tangent direction to the constraint is perpendicular to ∇g, and at a extremum the directional derivative of f vanishes in every such direction. Thus ∇f is parallel to ∇g. One must then classify candidates and check any singular or boundary cases.

The row rank of a matrix is the dimension of the linear span of its row vectors. Its column rank is the dimension of the linear span of its column vectors, equivalently the dimension of the image of the corresponding linear map. The equality of row rank and column rank states that these two numbers are equal for every matrix. Their common value is the rank of a matrix.

An entire function that is doubly periodic is bounded: its absolute value has a finite maximum on the compact square [0,1]+i[0,1], and periodic translations carry every point of C into that square. The Liouville theorem therefore proves every such analytic function is constant.
For the zero-pole count, suppose the meromorphic function is not identically zero; otherwise isolated zero multiplicities are not defined. It has only finitely many zeros and poles modulo the lattice Λ=Z+iZ, because a compact square can be covered by finitely many local meromorphic neighbourhoods. Choose a translated unit square with no zeros or poles on its boundary. The logarithmic derivative f′/f is doubly periodic, so integrals on opposite sides cancel, with their opposite orientations. The argument principle gives
N−P=2πi1​∮f(z)f′(z)​dz=0.​
(42)
Both counts include multiplicities. Each half-open fundamental parallelogram contains exactly one representative of each lattice orbit, and translation by a lattice vector preserves the multiplicity of a zero or pole. Consequently the counts agree in the prescribed half-open square too, even if that particular square has boundary zeros or poles. Nonzero constants have both counts zero.
For the series, let Λ=Z+iZ and write it as the Weierstrass elliptic function ℘(z). On a compact set avoiding Λ, choose R with ∣z∣≤R. For ∣w∣>2R,
(z−w)21​−w21​=w2(z−w)22wz−z2​,​(z−w)21​−w21​​≤∣w∣3CR​​.

The Weierstrass M-test proves absolute locally uniform convergence of the tail, and the finite remaining terms define holomorphic functions off their poles. It follows that ℘ is a holomorphic function off Λ. Near each w0​∈Λ, separate the term (z−w0​)−2; the remaining terms converge locally uniformly to a holomorphic function. Thus ℘ is meromorphic on C, with a double pole at each lattice point and no other poles.
It remains to prove the required periods, rather than merely assert them from the lattice. Termwise differentiation, justified by locally uniform convergence of these holomorphic series, gives
℘′(z)=−2∑w∈Λ​(z−w)31​.
(44)

The original convergent series is even: reindexing w↦−w gives ℘(−z)=℘(z). At z=−ω/2, which is not a lattice point for either generator, evenness gives

Conservation of circulation around every material curve therefore makes the line integral of the velocity zero around every closed curve at the current time, not just small contractible loops. For the Lagrangian sufficiency theorem, write a maximization problem as maxx∈D​f(x) subject to gi​(x)≤ci​. Here D may already incorporate simple constraints such as nonnegativity. Define the Lagrangian in optimization
L(x,λ)=f(x)−∑i​λi​(gi​(x)−ci​),λi​≥0.
(77)
The theorem states that a feasible point x∗​ is a global maximizer if there are such multipliers for which x∗​ globally maximizes L(⋅,λ) on D and complementary slackness holds: λi​(gi​(x∗​)−ci​)=0 for every i. To prove it, for any feasible x observe that
f(x)≤L(x,λ)≤L(x∗​,λ)=f(x∗​).
(78)
The first inequality uses feasibility and nonnegative multipliers, the second uses global maximization of the Lagrangian in optimization, and the last uses complementary slackness. Thus x∗​ is globally optimal. No convexity hypothesis is needed for this implication; concavity is useful when establishing the required global maximization of L from its gradient.
For the particular problem, put d=ec2​−1≥0 and R=c1​−2d≥0. The logarithmic constraint is equivalent to x1​≥d. The objective increases strictly with either variable, so at a maximum the resource constraint is tight: otherwise x1​ could be increased. Hence
