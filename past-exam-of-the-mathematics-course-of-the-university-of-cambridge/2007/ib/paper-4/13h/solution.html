The contraction mapping theorem says that if (X,d) is a nonempty complete metric space and T:X→X satisfies d(Tx,Ty)≤qd(x,y) for a constant 0≤q<1, then T has exactly one fixed point.
For the proof, start at x0​∈X and set xn​=Tnx0​. Iterating the contraction bound gives d(xn+1​,xn​)≤qnd(x1​,x0​). The triangle inequality then gives, for m>n,
d(xm​,xn​)≤∑k=nm−1​qkd(x1​,x0​)≤1−qqn​d(x1​,x0​).
(34)
Thus (xn​) is a Cauchy sequence and has a limit x∗​ by completeness. Since the contraction is continuous, Tx∗​=limTxn​=limxn+1​=x∗​. If y∗​ is another fixed point, then d(x∗​,y∗​)≤qd(x∗​,y∗​), forcing distance zero. This proves existence and uniqueness.
For the integral map, the fundamental theorem of calculus shows that Tf is continuous whenever f is continuous. In the supremum norm,
∣Tf(x)−Tg(x)∣≤3∥f−g∥∞​​∫0x​∣t∣dt​=23​x2∥f−g∥∞​.
(35)
Hence ∥Tf−Tg∥∞​≤23​max(a2,b2)∥f−g∥∞​. We can choose
a=−21​,b=21​,q=83​.​
(36)
The space C[a,b] with this supremum norm is complete: a uniformly Cauchy sequence has pointwise real limits, the Cauchy bound passes to those limits to give uniform convergence, and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. The contraction mapping theorem therefore gives a unique continuous y with y(x)=1+∫0x​3ty(t)dt. The fundamental theorem of calculus makes this y differentiable, with y′=3xy and y(0)=1. Conversely every solution of that initial-value problem obeys the integral equation, so uniqueness transfers to the ordinary differential equation. Explicitly y(x)=e3x2/2 on [−1/2,1/2], as direct differentiation confirms.
