OurBigBook.com Words: 231
First, the orbit-stabilizer theorem applied to a face gives ∣G∣=20⋅3=60: its stabilizer subgroup consists of the identity and the two rotations through 120∘ about the axis through the centres of that face and its opposite. A rotation preserving a face must preserve its outward normal and cyclically permute its three vertices, so there are no further stabilizing rotations.
The conjugacy classes are as follows:
  • The identity: size 1.
  • Half-turns about axes through midpoints of opposite edges: size 15.
  • Rotations through ±2π/3 about opposite-face axes: size 20.
  • Rotations through ±2π/5 about opposite-vertex axes: size 12.
  • Rotations through ±4π/5 about opposite-vertex axes: size 12.
There are 30/2=15 edge axes, 20/2=10 face axes, and 12/2=6 vertex axes. They give respectively 15, 2⋅10=20, and 4⋅6=24 distinct nonidentity rotations, already accounting for all 59 nonidentity elements.
To justify the class assertions, conjugation by a rotation carries the rotation axis to its image and preserves the rotation angle about the transported oriented axis. The transitive group action on edges puts all half-turns in one conjugacy class. The transitive group action on faces puts the 120∘ rotations defined using outward face normals in one conjugacy class; opposite faces encode opposite signs about the same unoriented axis. The same reasoning with outward vertex directions gives a class of twelve 72∘ rotations and a class of twelve 144∘ rotations, including their inverses. These last classes cannot merge: the matrix trace of a three-dimensional rotation is 1+2cosθ, which differs for θ=2π/5 and 4π/5. Thus the list is complete.
