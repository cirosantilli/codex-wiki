Each endomorphism has three distinct eigenvalues in a three-dimensional complex vector space. Choose a nonzero eigenvector for each eigenvalue; these vectors are linearly independent and hence form an eigenbasis. Both operators are therefore diagonalizable with diagonal matrix D=diag(1,2,3).
Their characteristic polynomials are
χS​(t)=χT​(t)=(t−1)(t−2)(t−3).​
(1)
If a polynomial p annihilates either operator, applying it to each eigenvector gives p(1)=p(2)=p(3)=0. Thus its minimal polynomial must contain all three factors. Conversely, their product annihilates the diagonal matrix, so
mS​(t)=mT​(t)=(t−1)(t−2)(t−3).​
(2)
Finally, send an ordered eigenbasis of S to the correspondingly ordered eigenbasis of T. The resulting invertible map C satisfies CS=TC, and hence T=CSC−1: the two endomorphisms are conjugate.

A Euclidean domain is an integral domain R with a function δ:R∖{0}→Z≥0​ such that, for every a∈R and nonzero b∈R, there are q,r∈R satisfying a=bq+r, with either r=0 or δ(r)<δ(b).
For the integers, take δ(b)=∣b∣. Given a and b=0, put m=∣b∣ and choose the integer q0​=⌊a/m⌋. Then a=mq0​+r with 0≤r<m. Setting q=q0​ when b>0 and q=−q0​ when b<0 gives a=bq+r. A nonzero remainder has δ(r)=r<∣b∣=δ(b), and the integers are an integral domain. Thus Z is a Euclidean domain with Euclidean function ∣b∣.

In a steady state, Maxwell's equations imply ∇×E=0, since the magnetic field is time independent. The divergence of the steady Ampère equation gives ∇⋅j=0. With uniform electrical conductivity, Ohm's law is j=σE, so
∇×j=0,∇⋅j=0.
(10)
For the usual straight-wire model, the cylindrical side is insulating and the two flat end faces are equipotentials. Put E=−∇V. The electric potential solves Laplace's equation, with constant end values and zero normal derivative on the side. The linear axial potential V(z)=V(0)−ΔVz/l satisfies all these conditions. It is unique: the difference h of two solutions has zero end values and zero side normal derivative, and integration by parts gives ∫∣∇h∣2dV=0. Thus
j=lσΔV​ez​=πa2I​ez​.​
(11)
This proves uniform current in a straight homogeneous wire with the usual contact conditions. Integrating the electric current density over a cross-section and using Ohm's law then gives
R=σπa2l​,Q˙​=∫j⋅EdV=σπa2I2l​=I2R.​
(12)
The second expression is the rate of Ohmic heating.
Strictly, steady current and uniform conductivity alone do not force uniformity; the contact conditions are implicit in the wire model. For example, let χ be a nonconstant transverse Neumann eigenfunction on the disk, satisfying Δ⊥​χ=−λ2χ and zero normal derivative at its edge. Then V=−E0​z+Cχcosh(λ(z−l/2)) is harmonic and preserves an insulating cylindrical side, but its nonconstant end potentials produce nonuniform current. Its added axial current has zero cross-sectional mean. This supplies a counterexample if arbitrary contacts are permitted.
A bend generally redistributes the electric current density: the inner side of the bend carries more current than the outer side, as illustrated by jθ​∝1/r in an annular conductor. Exact resistance and local Ohmic heating can therefore change. For a slender, gently curved wire the straight-wire formulas remain the leading approximation; the identity Q˙​=I2R remains valid for the total steady dissipation.

The row player maximizes the payoff and the column player minimizes it. Row three is weakly dominated by row one, so the row player can discard it. On the remaining two rows, column two is weakly dominated by column three, so the column player can discard column two. The reduced zero-sum game has payoff matrix
(23​42​).
(22)
If the first row is chosen with probability r, the expected payoffs against its two columns are 3−r and 2+2r. Equalizing them gives r=1/3 and value 8/3. If the first column is chosen with probability s, the payoffs of the two rows are 4−2s and 2+s. Equalizing them gives s=2/3.
Restoring the original indexing, the optimal mixed strategies and value of a zero-sum game are
r=(1/3,2/3,0),s=(2/3,0,1/3),v=8/3.​
(23)
To verify these against every original strategy, the row mixture gives the three column payoffs (8/3,3,8/3), each at least v. The column mixture gives the three row payoffs (8/3,8/3,7/3), each at most v. Thus each mixed strategy guarantees the stated value, including against the discarded strategies. The strict inequalities for column two and row three also force those probabilities to be zero in any optimal strategy, and the remaining equalities determine the displayed mixtures uniquely.

A field, for example Q, is a Noetherian ring: its only ideals are (0) and the whole field, both finitely generated.
For a non-Noetherian example, take R=Q[x1​,x2​,…], the polynomial ring in countably many variables, each individual polynomial using only finitely many variables. The ascending chain of ideals
(x1​)⊊(x1​,x2​)⊊(x1​,x2​,x3​)⊊⋯
(30)
is strictly increasing. Indeed, if xn+1​ belonged to (x1​,…,xn​), setting the first n variables to zero would give the impossible equality xn+1​=0. The chain never stabilizes, contradicting the ascending chain condition. Thus Q is Noetherian, while Q[x1​,x2​,…] is not.

Hilbert's basis theorem: if R is a commutative Noetherian ring with identity, then the polynomial ring R[x] is Noetherian. Iteration gives the same assertion for every finite number of variables.
Here is a bounded-degree coefficient proof of Hilbert basis theorem. Let I be any ideal of R[x]. For n≥0, define Jn​⊆R to be the coefficients of xn in the polynomials of I of degree at most n, including the zero coefficient. Closure under addition and scalar multiplication makes Jn​ an ideal of R. Multiplication of a polynomial by x shows Jn​⊆Jn+1​. By the ascending chain condition, there is N such that JN​=JN+1​=⋯.
Each Jn​ for 0≤n≤N is finitely generated. Choose generators an,1​,…,an,rn​​ and corresponding polynomials fn,j​∈I of degree at most n whose coefficient of xn is an,j​. There are only finitely many chosen polynomials. We prove they generate I by induction on degree.
Take a nonzero f∈I of degree d and leading coefficient a. If d≤N, express a=∑j​cj​ad,j​ and subtract ∑j​cj​fd,j​. If d>N, use a∈Jd​=JN​, express a=∑j​cj​aN,j​, and subtract ∑j​cj​xd−NfN,j​. In both cases the leading coefficient cancels, leaving a polynomial in I of smaller degree. For degree zero the remainder is zero, and the induction completes the claim.
Thus every ideal of R[x] is finitely generated, proving the Hilbert basis theorem. The proof uses no cancellation of nonzero elements of R, so it remains valid when R has zero divisors.

In the position representation p=−iℏ∇. For a radial wavefunction, ∇Ψ0​=Ψ0′​(r)x/r, parallel to x, so
LΨ0​=−iℏx×∇Ψ0​=0.​

The Neyman-Pearson lemma concerns a simple hypothesis with density f0​ against a simple alternative with density f1​. Allow a randomized test 0≤φ≤1. Choose a threshold c≥0 and, if necessary, randomize on the equality set so that
φ∗​=1where f1​>cf0​,φ∗​=0where f1​<cf0​,E0​φ∗​=α.
(63)
Then φ∗​ is a most powerful test among all tests of size at most α. To prove it, let E0​φ≤α. Pointwise, (φ∗​−φ)(f1​−cf0​)≥0: above threshold both factors are nonnegative; below threshold both are nonpositive; on the threshold their product vanishes. Therefore
E1​φ∗​−E1​φ≥c(E0​φ∗​−E0​φ)≥0.
(64)
This proves the lemma, including regions with f0​=0, where a positive alternative density must be rejected. It also shows that ties in the likelihood ratio are the only discretionary region when the inequality is strict off the threshold.
For the stated family, R=∣X∣ has the Gamma distribution with shape θ and unit rate. At θ=1 it has the exponential distribution, and the likelihood ratio for θ=2 against θ=1 is
f(x∣1)f(x∣2)​=∣x∣,
(65)
because Γ(1)=Γ(2)=1. The most powerful test therefore rejects for large ∣X∣. Since Pr1​(∣X∣>c)=e−c, its exact size-α critical value and rejection rule are
c=−logα,reject H0​ if ∣X∣>−logα.​
(66)
The distribution is continuous, so boundary randomization is unnecessary. Under θ=2, integration by parts gives the power function of a statistical test
2Pr​(∣X∣>c)=∫c∞​re−rdr=(c+1)e−c=α(1−logα).​
(67)
For every fixed θ>1, the likelihood ratio against the same null is ∣x∣θ−1/Γ(θ), strictly increasing in ∣x∣. The size constraint still selects exactly the same critical value c=−logα, independent of the alternative. The Neyman-Pearson lemma therefore makes this single test most powerful against each θ>1 separately. Hence it is uniformly most powerful for the composite alternative θ>1.

Let G0​,G1​,… be independent with Pr(Gn​=j)=qpj for j≥0. The transition law is realized by the geometric-arrival single-service queue
Xn+1​=max(Xn​−1,0)+Gn​.
(68)
The chain is an irreducible Markov chain: repeated zero arrivals reach zero from every state, and any state can be reached from zero in one step. It is an aperiodic Markov chain because the zero state has self-transition probability q>0.
Starting with one customer, serve one customer per step and regard the arriving customers as that customer's offspring. The number of steps until the queue empties is the total progeny of a Galton-Watson process with offspring probability generating function
g(s)=1−psq​,m=g′(1)=qp​.
(69)
Here is the extinction argument needed for recurrence. The probability of extinction by generation n is g∘n(0): condition on the first generation and iterate. These probabilities increase to the smallest fixed point in [0,1]. The fixed-point equation s=q/(1−ps) has roots 1 and q/p. Thus the queue started at one hits zero almost surely when p≤q, but with probability only q/p<1 when p>q.
If p≤q, every finite initial queue empties almost surely, since it represents finitely many family trees. Starting from zero, the first step gives a finite G0​, so the return probability to zero is one. If p>q, the first step has positive probability of producing one customer, whose probability of never emptying is positive; thus the return probability is below one. Communication in this irreducible Markov chain then makes every state a transient state.
For p≤q, the expected total progeny from one customer is
∑n=0∞​EZn​=∑n=0∞​mn,
(70)
since conditioning on a generation gives EZn+1​=mEZn​. Nonnegativity justifies interchanging the expectation and sum. For p<q this equals 1/(1−m). After the first step from zero the mean number of initial customers is EG0​=m, so the mean return time is
E0​τ0+​=1+1−mm​=q−pq​<∞.
(71)
The chain is therefore positive recurrent. At p=q=1/2 extinction still occurs almost surely, but the expected progeny, and hence the mean return time, is infinite. The chain is null recurrent.
For the stationary distribution in the positive recurrent case, let Π(z)=∑j≥0​πj​zj. Stationarity and the transition realization imply
Π(z)=1−pzq​[π0​+zΠ(z)−π0​​].
(72)
Multiplying through and factoring gives
(z−1)(q−pz)Π(z)=qπ0​(z−1),Π(z)=q−pzqπ0​​.
(73)
Normalization Π(1)=1 yields π0​=(q−p)/q. Expanding the geometric series gives the complete classification and invariant probabilities:
0<p<1/2:p=1/2:1/2<p<1:​positive recurrent,πj​=(1−p/q)(p/q)j,j≥0;null recurrent;transient.​​
(74)
For p≥q the ratio p/q cannot define a summable probability distribution, consistently with the absence of a stationary probability law. The displayed recurrence proof distinguishes the critical null recurrent case from the transient case; failure of normalizability alone would not do so.
