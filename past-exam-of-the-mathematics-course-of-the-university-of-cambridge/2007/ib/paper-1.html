For a geodesic spherical triangle on a sphere of radius R, the Gauss-Bonnet theorem gives
Area=R2(α+β+γ−π),​
(4)
where α,β,γ are its interior angles. Indeed, its geodesic sides have zero geodesic curvature, its Gaussian curvature is R−2, and its three exterior turning angles sum to 3π−(α+β+γ).
Choose a point strictly inside a full-dimensional convex polyhedron and radially project its boundary onto the unit sphere. Triangulate each projected face by diagonals, using only its existing vertices. Convexity ensures that the resulting spherical triangles cover the sphere once, with disjoint interiors. A face with k edges gives k−2 triangles, so their number is T=∑faces​(k−2)=2E−2F, since every original edge belongs to two faces.
Sum the spherical excess formula over all triangles. The total area is 4π, and the angles incident to each projected vertex sum to 2π. No additional vertices were introduced by the face triangulations. Hence
4π=2πV−πT=2πV−π(2E−2F),F−E+V=2.​
(5)
This is the spherical-triangle proof of the polyhedron Euler formula.

For a finite directed flow network with nonnegative finite edge capacities, a source s and a distinct sink t, the max-flow min-cut theorem states that the largest feasible flow value equals the smallest capacity of a cut separating s from t. A feasible flow has 0≤fe​≤ce​ on each directed edge and flow conservation at all other vertices. Its value is the net outflow at s.
For any cut S containing s but not t, sum conservation over vertices of S. Internal edges cancel, giving
∣f∣=∑e:S→Sc​fe​−∑e:Sc→S​fe​≤∑e:S→Sc​ce​=cap(S).
(20)
Thus every flow value is bounded by every cut capacity.
A maximum flow exists: the feasible set is nonempty, closed and bounded in a finite-dimensional edge-coordinate space, hence compact, and the flow value is continuous. For such a flow, form its residual network: each original edge has forward residual capacity ce​−fe​ and backward residual capacity fe​. Keep these as edge-labelled arcs if the original network already has edges in both directions. If a directed positive-residual path joined s to t, increasing the flow along its forward arcs and decreasing it along its backward arcs by the path's positive bottleneck capacity would preserve feasibility and increase the value. That contradicts maximality.
Let S be the vertices reachable from s by positive residual arcs. Then t∈/S. Every original edge from S to Sc is saturated, otherwise its forward residual arc would extend reachability. Every original edge from Sc to S has zero flow, otherwise its backward residual arc would extend reachability. Therefore
∣f∣=∑e:S→Sc​ce​=cap(S).
(21)
Together with the bound for every flow and cut, this proves
fmax​∣f∣=S:s∈S,t∈/Smin​cap(S).​
(22)
The compactness step makes the proof valid for arbitrary real capacities; it does not assume that a particular augmenting-path algorithm terminates after finitely many augmentations.

Sylvester's law of inertia states that a real quadratic form can be brought by an invertible real change of coordinates to
Q=∑j=1p​uj2​−∑j=p+1p+q​uj2​,
(23)
with r remaining zero coordinates, and that the integers (p,q,r) are uniquely determined. To prove existence, represent it by a symmetric matrix. The allowed orthogonal diagonalization yields real eigenvalues, and scaling each nonzero eigen-coordinate by the square root of its absolute eigenvalue changes its coefficient to +1 or −1.
For uniqueness, p is the largest dimension of a subspace on which the form is positive definite. The positive coordinate subspace attains p. Any subspace of dimension greater than p intersects the (q+r)-dimensional span of the negative and zero coordinates nontrivially, by the dimension formula. A nonzero vector in that intersection has nonpositive quadratic value, contradicting positive definiteness. Similarly, q is the maximal dimension of a negative-definite subspace. These descriptions are independent of coordinates, and r=dimV−p−q is then fixed. This proves existence and uniqueness, including degenerate forms.
For the final application, nonsingularity gives p+q=2m. In diagonal coordinates write v=(v+​,v−​), with Q(v)=∣v+​∣2−∣v−​∣2. If v∈U projects to zero in the positive coordinates, Q(v)=0 forces v−​=0, so projection U→Rp is injective. The same argument applies to the negative coordinates. Hence m≤p and m≤q, and their sum is 2m. Therefore
p=q=m.​
(24)
The inertia signature is (m,m), or signature difference p−q=0 if that convention is used. This is the isotropic dimension bound from real inertia.

ii OurBigBook.com
A nonzero polynomial of degree n over a field has at most n distinct roots in that field. For a proof, the factor theorem gives (t−a)∣f(t) whenever f(a)=0. Distinct linear factors are coprime in K[t], a unique factorization domain, so for distinct roots a1​,…,ak​ their product divides f. Its degree is k, giving k≤n. One may equivalently remove one linear factor at a time and argue by induction.
If the field has at least n elements, this bound is attained by a product of n distinct linear factors. For a fixed finite field with fewer than n elements, the largest possible number of distinct roots is min(n,∣K∣) instead; multiplicities do not create more distinct roots. The zero polynomial is excluded since it has no finite degree.

The product topology on X×Y has as a basis the rectangles U×V, where U is open in X and V is open in Y. Equivalently, it is the coarsest topology making both coordinate projections continuous.
To show that the product of two Hausdorff spaces is Hausdorff, take distinct points (x1​,y1​) and (x2​,y2​). If x1​=x2​, choose disjoint open neighborhoods U1​,U2​ in X; the rectangles U1​×Y and U2​×Y separate the product points. If the first coordinates agree, the second coordinates differ, and the same argument uses disjoint open neighborhoods in Y. Thus every pair of distinct product points has disjoint open neighborhoods.
The three implications below prove the requested equivalence without assuming metrizability.
