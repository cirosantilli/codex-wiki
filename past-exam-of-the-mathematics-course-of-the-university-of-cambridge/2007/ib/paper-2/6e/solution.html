In a steady state, Maxwell's equations imply ∇×E=0, since the magnetic field is time independent. The divergence of the steady Ampère equation gives ∇⋅j=0. With uniform electrical conductivity, Ohm's law is j=σE, so
∇×j=0,∇⋅j=0.

For the usual straight-wire model, the cylindrical side is insulating and the two flat end faces are equipotentials. Put E=−∇V. The electric potential solves Laplace's equation, with constant end values and zero normal derivative on the side. The linear axial potential V(z)=V(0)−ΔVz/l satisfies all these conditions. It is unique: the difference h of two solutions has zero end values and zero side normal derivative, and integration by parts gives ∫∣∇h∣2dV=0. Thus

The second expression is the rate of Ohmic heating.
Strictly, steady current and uniform conductivity alone do not force uniformity; the contact conditions are implicit in the wire model. For example, let χ be a nonconstant transverse Neumann eigenfunction on the disk, satisfying Δ⊥​χ=−λ2χ and zero normal derivative at its edge. Then V=−E0​z+Cχcosh(λ(z−l/2)) is harmonic and preserves an insulating cylindrical side, but its nonconstant end potentials produce nonuniform current. Its added axial current has zero cross-sectional mean. This supplies a counterexample if arbitrary contacts are permitted.
A bend generally redistributes the electric current density: the inner side of the bend carries more current than the outer side, as illustrated by jθ​∝1/r in an annular conductor. Exact resistance and local Ohmic heating can therefore change. For a slender, gently curved wire the straight-wire formulas remain the leading approximation; the identity Q˙​=I2R remains valid for the total steady dissipation.
