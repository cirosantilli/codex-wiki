The discrete valuation identities v(xy)=v(x)+v(y) and v(x+y)≥min(v(x),v(y)) make R closed under products and sums; it contains 0,1 and additive inverses. As a subring of the field K, it is an integral domain.
Its units are exactly the elements of valuation zero: x−1∈R if and only if v(x)=0. The remaining elements form the ideal m={x:v(x)>0}, and every proper ideal is contained in it, since an ideal containing a unit is the whole ring. Hence R is a local ring with this unique maximal ideal.
For a nonzero ideal I, the set of valuations of its nonzero elements is a nonempty subset of Z≥0​ and has a least value. Choose x∈I attaining it. Every y∈I satisfies v(y/x)≥0, so y/x∈R and I=(x). The zero ideal is also principal. This proves that R is a discrete valuation ring, in particular a principal ideal domain.
Choose a uniformizer π, with v(π)=1. For z∈K, some n≥0 makes πnz∈R, and z=(πnz)/πn is a quotient of elements of R. Thus K is the field of fractions of R.

Let n0​=v(x) and y0​=π−n0​x∈R×. Choose an0​​ to represent the residue of y0​, and set y1​=(y0​−an0​​)/π∈R. Continue by choosing an0​+j​ representing yj​ and putting yj+1​=(yj​−an0​+j​)/π. The first digit is not in m, because y0​ is a unit. After r steps,
x=∑j=0r−1​an0​+j​πn0​+j+πn0​+ryr​.
(1)
The error has valuation at least n0​+r, so the partial sums converge to the existing element x. This proves the digit expansion in a discretely valued field without any completeness assumption.
The leading exponent is forced to be v(x). Reduction of π−n0​x modulo m forces the first digit, and the recursive quotient then forces every later digit. Thus the expansion is unique for the chosen representatives. No assumption that the representative of the zero residue is literally zero is needed.
Arbitrary series of this form need not converge in K. Their partial sums are Cauchy, so they do converge if K is complete. For a counterexample take K=Q with the three-adic valuation, π=3, and A={0,1,2}. Hensel lemma applied to T2−10 at the simple residue root 1 gives a square root of 10 in Q3​. Its three-adic digits form such a series, but its sums cannot converge to an element of Q, since that would give a rational square root of 10. Convergence to an already given element and convergence of every possible digit sequence are different assertions.

A simple-root version of Hensel lemma is: if R is a complete discrete valuation ring, f∈R[T], f(a1​)≡0(modπ), and f′(a1​) is a unit, then there is a unique root a∈R congruent to a1​ modulo π.
Suppose f(an​)∈πnR. The derivative remains a unit, so choose a residue cn​ satisfying
πnf(an​)​+cn​f′(an​)≡0(modπ),
(2)
and put an+1​=an​+cn​πn. Taylor expansion gives f(an+1​)≡f(an​)+cn​πnf′(an​)(modπn+1), because 2n≥n+1. Thus f(an+1​)∈πn+1R. The sequence is a Cauchy sequence and converges by completeness; continuity of polynomial evaluation makes its limit a root.
If roots a,b have the prescribed residue, factor f(a)−f(b)=(a−b)Q(a,b). Modulo π, the second factor is f′(a1​), so it is a unit. Therefore a=b. This is the simple-root Hensel lifting by successive residues.

For odd p, eighth roots of unity have order prime to the residue characteristic. The total ramification criterion for local cyclotomic extensions shows that they generate an unramified extension of degree f=ord8​(p). More directly, a primitive eighth root in Fpf​ lifts uniquely by Hensel lemma, since the derivative of T8−1 is a unit at each nonzero root. Its residue has exact order eight, and the Frobenius automorphism acts by ζ8​↦ζ8p​. Consequently f=1 when p≡1(mod8) and f=2 for the other three odd residue classes.
At p=2, the translated cyclotomic polynomial is
Φ8​(1+T)=(1+T)4+1=T4+4T3+6T2+4T+2.
(3)
It is Eisenstein at two, so the extension has degree four. All primitive eighth roots are odd powers of ζ8​ and already lie in the field; the extension is Galois, and its automorphisms give the full group (Z/8Z)×≅C2​×C2​. Thus

Any extending absolute value is also non-Archimedean. Indeed its values on integers are the given base-field values, bounded by one, so the integer criterion for a non-Archimedean absolute value applies. For the criterion's sufficiency, the binomial theorem gives ∣x+y∣n≤(n+1)max(∣x∣,∣y∣)n; taking nth roots yields the ultrametric inequality.
Fix a K-basis of L and its coordinate maximum norm. Every extending absolute value is a vector-space norm satisfying ∥av∥=∣a∣∥v∥. The finite-dimensional non-Archimedean norm equivalence over a complete field makes it equivalent to that coordinate norm. Here is the relevant proof: induct on dimension. The span W of all but the last basis vector e is complete by the induction hypothesis, hence closed in the given norm. Therefore δ=infw∈W​∥e−w∥>0. For v=w+ae, one has ∣a∣≤∥v∥/δ and ∥w∥≤max(∥v∥,∣a∣∥e∥). The induction bound on W bounds all its coordinates by a constant times ∥v∥. The reverse bound follows from the triangle inequality. This proof applies to every complete base field here, without a local compactness assumption.
Thus two extending absolute values satisfy c∣x∣1​≤∣x∣2​≤C∣x∣1​ for fixed positive constants. Apply this to xn and take nth roots:
c1/n∣x∣1​≤∣x∣2​≤C1/n∣x∣1​.
(5)
Letting n→∞ proves equality. The bounded comparison forces equality of multiplicative absolute values argument gives the required unique extension of an absolute value to a finite extension, without proving existence.

Put α=pp​ and λ=ζp​−1. The polynomial Tp−p is Eisenstein, so [Qp​(α):Qp​]=p, while the preceding part gives degree p−1 for E. Both numbers divide [K:Qp​] by the tower law, and the compositum degree is at most their product. Coprimality forces [K:Qp​]=p(p−1). Since K contains all the roots ζpa​α of Tp−p, it is the splitting field of a separable polynomial and is Galois.
Extend vp​ with vp​(p)=1. Then vp​(α)=1/p, vp​(λ)=1/(p−1), and
ϖ=pp​ζp​−1​,vp​(ϖ)=p(p−1)1​.​
(10)
The value group therefore forces the ramification index to be at least p(p−1); it cannot exceed the extension degree. Thus K/Qp​ is totally ramified and ϖ is a uniformizer for the normalized valuation vK​=p(p−1)vp​.
Write its automorphisms as σa,b​(α)=ζpa​α and σa,b​(ζp​)=ζpb​, with a∈Fp​, b∈Fp×​. The degree count makes all p(p−1) pairs occur, giving G≅Fp​⋊Fp×​. Their action on the uniformizer is
ϖσa,b​(ϖ)​=ζp−a​ζp​−1ζpb​−1​.
(11)
If b=1, the residue of this ratio is b, so subtracting one gives a unit and vK​(σa,b​(ϖ)−ϖ)=1. If b=1 and a=0, the ratio is ζp−a​, giving displacement valuation 1+vK​(ζp−a​−1)=p+1. The identity has infinite displacement valuation.
By the uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups, σ∈Gi​ precisely when that displacement valuation is at least i+1. Hence the ramification groups of the splitting field of Xp minus p over the p-adic numbers have sizes
∣Gi​∣=⎩⎨⎧​p(p−1),p,1,​i=−1,0,1≤i≤p,i≥p+1.​​
(12)
For 1≤i≤p the group itself is {σa,1​:a∈Fp​}≅Cp​. The lower wild ramification break is p.
