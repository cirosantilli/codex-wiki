We prove the positive state-price density alternative using finite-dimensional convex cones. Put w=(1+∥A1​∥)−1 and define
D={E[wfA1​]:f bounded, measurable and nonnegative},K=D,L=spanK.
(1)
All displayed expectations are finite, since w∥A1​∥≤1. The set D is a convex cone, so K is a closed convex cone. A vector x is nonnegative on K exactly when x⊤A1​≥0 almost surely. Indeed, one direction follows by taking every nonnegative bounded test weight f; for the other direction, if x⊤A1​<0 on a set of positive probability, take its indicator as f to obtain a strictly negative expectation. This also identifies K as the closed cone generated by the support of a probability distribution of A1​.
The three cases A0​∈/K, A0​∈K∖riK, and A0​∈riK are treated below. They give respectively a negative-cost separating portfolio, a supporting zero-cost portfolio with a strictly positive payoff on an event of positive probability, or the required positive pricing weight. Thus the alternatives are exhaustive.
They are mutually exclusive. If A0​=E[νA1​] with ν>0 and a portfolio has nonnegative payoff, then x⊤A0​=E[νx⊤A1​]≥0. A negative initial cost is impossible. If the payoff is strictly positive with positive probability, this expectation is strictly positive, so nonpositive initial cost is impossible too. The given weighted integrability permits taking the expectation of this scalar product.
In a one-period market, x⊤A0​ is the portfolio's initial cost and x⊤A1​ its terminal value. The first two cases are investment-consumption arbitrage: nonnegative wealth can be taken out at both dates, with a strict benefit at one of them. If a strictly positive bank account is traded, any negative initial cost can be invested in it, converting the opportunity into a zero-cost arbitrage with nonnegative terminal payoff and positive payoff with positive probability.
In the third case, ν is a strictly positive state-price density. If a traded numéraire has price B0​>0 and terminal value B1​>0, its pricing identity is E[νB1​]=B0​. Consequently
dPdQ​=B0​νB1​​,B0​A0​​=EQ​[B1​A1​​].​
(2)
The density has mean one and is strictly positive, so Q is equivalent to the physical measure. Thus discounted asset prices are martingales in the one-period sense. This is the fundamental theorem of asset pricing formulation of absence of arbitrage; normalization to a probability measure uses the positive traded numéraire, not an unsupported assumption that Eν=1.

Suppose A0​ lies on the relative boundary of K in its span L. There is a nonzero x∈L with x⊤y≥0 for every y∈K and x⊤A0​=0. Here is a direct construction of the supporting vector. Choose points zn​∈L∖K converging to A0​, let pn​ be their nearest points in K, and set xn​=(pn​−zn​)/∥pn​−zn​∥. The minimization inequality for a nearest point gives xn⊤​y≥0 on K and xn⊤​pn​=0. A subsequence of the unit vectors converges to a unit vector x∈L. Since 0≤xn⊤​A0​≤∥A0​−zn​∥, it has the asserted support properties.
The test-weight characterization of K now gives x⊤A1​≥0 almost surely. This random payoff cannot vanish almost surely: otherwise x would annihilate every element of D, hence all of L, contradicting x∈L and ∥x∥=1. Therefore
x⊤A0​=0,x⊤A1​≥0 a.s.,P(x⊤A1​>0)>0.​
(3)
This supplies the positive-terminal-payoff alternative. If K has zero-dimensional span, its relative boundary is empty, so that case requires no supporting vector.

Suppose A0​∈riK. We first justify the finite-dimensional fact riK⊆D, since D itself need not be closed. Around any relative-interior point z, choose a small simplex in L whose vertices lie in K and which contains z strictly inside. Approximate its vertices by points of D, possible because D=K. For close enough approximations, the barycentric coordinates of z remain strictly positive; hence z is a convex combination of points of D and belongs to D. If L={0}, the assertion is immediate.
Let m=E[wA1​]∈L. Relative openness lets us choose ε>0 such that A0​−εm∈riK⊆D. There is therefore a bounded nonnegative measurable f satisfying A0​−εm=E[wfA1​]. Set
ν=w(f+ε)>0.​
(6)
Then E[νA1​]=A0​ and E∥νA1​∥≤∥f∥∞​+ε<∞. This proves existence with strict positivity even when the unweighted asset vector is not integrable.
It also proves the strictly positive barycentre cone lemma. Every strictly positive weighted barycentre must lie in riK: otherwise the supporting vector constructed for the relative boundary would have zero value at that barycentre while its strictly positive weighted payoff had positive expectation. Thus the relative interior is exactly the set of such barycentres.

Consider a binomial market with dates 0,…,n, bank account Bk​=bk for b>0, and stock price Sk+1​=Sk​u or Sk​d, where 0<d<u. Both moves have positive physical probability, and the information at date k is the history of the first k moves. Absence of arbitrage requires and is implied by d<b<u. If b≤d, borrowing cash to buy stock has nonnegative payoff with a strict gain in one state; if b≥u, the reverse trade does. In the interior range, the risk-neutral probability of an up move is
q=u−db−d​∈(0,1).​
(7)
It makes EQ​[Sk+1​∣Fk​]=bSk​, so Sk​/Bk​ is a martingale. This proves absence of arbitrage by pricing discounted gains under the equivalent measure.
At a node with stock price s, let the desired next-period portfolio values be Vu​,Vd​. Solve the two replication equations Δsu+βBk+1​=Vu​ and Δsd+βBk+1​=Vd​. The replicating portfolio in a binomial market has
Δk​=s(u−d)Vu​−Vd​​,βk​Bk​=b(u−d)uVd​−dVu​​.​
(8)
Its current value is
Vk​=Δk​s+βk​Bk​=bqVu​+(1−q)Vd​​.​
(9)
Rebalance at the next node using exactly the value supplied by the previous holdings, so the strategy is self-financing. Backward induction from any terminal contingent claim H gives its hedge and the unique price
Vk​=Bk​EQ​[H/Bn​∣Fk​].
(10)
Thus the finite binomial market is a complete market. The construction works for path-dependent claims on the full history tree, even when their values do not recombine merely as functions of the current stock price. For H=f(Sn​), independence under Q gives the explicit time-zero price
V0​=b−nj=0∑n​(jn​)qj(1−q)n−jf(S0​ujdn−j).​
(11)
The stock hedge is a difference quotient of successor values, the discrete analogue of the delta hedge.

Suppose under the physical measure P the up indicators are independent with probability p∈(0,1). If Nk​ is the number of up moves through date k, a particular history has probability pNk​(1−p)k−Nk​ under P and qNk​(1−q)k−Nk​ under Q. Thus the binomial-market probability density, the Radon-Nikodym derivative on Fk​, is
Lk​=dPdQ​​Fk​​=(pq​)Nk​(1−p1−q​)k−Nk​.​
(12)
It is strictly positive. Its next multiplier has conditional expectation p(q/p)+(1−p)((1−q)/(1−p))=1, so Lk​ is a mean-one martingale and Lk​=EP​[Ln​∣Fk​]. These facts verify normalization and equivalence of the two probability measures.

Vk​=Lk​Bk​​EP​[Bn​Ln​H​ ​ Fk​].

In a complete market, terminal-wealth optimization can first be solved over terminal payoffs and then implemented by replication. Let initial wealth be x0​>0, and take an increasing differentiable strictly concave utility function U on (0,∞) satisfying the Inada conditions. The state-price budget constraint for a terminal wealth X is EP​[ζn​X]≤x0​. Monotonicity spends the whole budget at the optimum.
Let I=(U′)−1 be the inverse marginal utility. Choose y>0 to satisfy EP​[ζn​I(yζn​)]=x0​. In a finite tree with positive state probabilities, this left side is continuous and strictly decreasing from infinity to zero, so y exists uniquely. The complete-market terminal utility optimizer is
X∗=I(yζn​).​
(14)
To prove optimality, concavity gives U(X)≤U(X∗)+U′(X∗)(X−X∗)=U(X∗)+yζn​(X−X∗). Taking expectations and using the budget proves EU(X)≤EU(X∗). Strict concavity gives uniqueness of the optimal terminal wealth. The finite-market setting avoids additional infinite-state existence and integrability issues.
Replicate X∗ using the backward recursion of part (i). Its wealth process is Vk​=Bk​EQ​[X∗/Bn​∣Fk​], and the two-successor difference quotient gives its stock holdings. This self-financing strategy starts with x0​ by the budget equality and remains positive at every node, since it is the discounted conditional expectation of a strictly positive terminal wealth.
For logarithmic utility, U(x)=logx, I(z)=1/z, the multiplier is y=1/x0​ and X∗=x0​/ζn​=x0​Bn​/Ln​. For CRRA utility with risk aversion γ>0, γ=1, the normalized payoff is
X∗=EP​[ζn1−1/γ​]x0​ζn−1/γ​​.​
(15)
Constraints such as prohibiting short sales change the attainable terminal-payoff set and require a constrained optimization; the unconstrained complete-market formula uses the stated assumptions.

qh​=eσh​−e−σh​eρh−e−σh​​=21​+2σρ−σ2/2​h​+O(h3/2).
(16)
Each stock log-increment is ±σh​. Its mean under Q is (ρ−σ2/2)h+O(h2) and its variance is σ2h+O(h2). Independent increments, their vanishing maximum size, and the Donsker invariance principle for this triangular array give the path limit
logSt(n)​ ⟹ logS0​+(ρ−σ2/2)t+σWtQ​.​
(17)
Exponentiation gives the geometric Brownian motion of the Black-Scholes model, and the bank account tends to eρt.
The physical model is matched by taking ph​=(eμh−d)/(u−d). Its corresponding limit has log drift μ−σ2/2, so dSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWtP​. The likelihood ratios from part (ii) have the matching continuous-time limit. With λ=(μ−ρ)/σ, a Taylor expansion of their two possible log-multipliers gives
logLn​=−λWT(n)​−21​λ2T+o(1),WT(n)​=∑k=1n​εk​h​−σμ−σ2/2​T,
(18)
where εk​ is +1 or −1. Thus the limiting Radon-Nikodym derivative is the Girsanov theorem density exp(−λWTP​−λ2T/2), turning the physical stock drift into ρ.
The binomial risk-neutral pricing formula converges to e−ρTEQ​[f(ST​)] for bounded continuous terminal payoffs by convergence in distribution. For ordinary call and put payoffs, uniform stock second-moment bounds give the required uniform integrability and hence convergence despite unboundedness. Bounded payoffs with discontinuities only at events of zero limiting probability are handled by the same weak-convergence criterion. Corresponding continuous path functionals can be treated at the process level. Arbitrary discontinuous path claims do not follow from weak convergence without further conditions. In the smooth limiting model, the discrete hedging difference quotient becomes the delta hedge Vx​, and its value satisfies the Black-Scholes equation.

Use a risk-neutral measure Q, and let Ps,t​ be the price at time s of a zero-coupon bond paying one at its maturity t. The information available at s is the filtration Fs​. For positive differentiable bond prices, the instantaneous forward rate is
f(s,t)=−∂t​logPs,t​,Ps,t​=exp(−∫st​f(s,u)du),
(43)
with Pt,t​=1. The short rate is Rs​=f(s,s). The continuous-time bank account Bs​=exp(∫0s​Ru​du) is the discounting numéraire.
If Ps,t​/Bs​ is a true martingale for each fixed maturity, then its terminal value is Bt−1​ and
Bs​Ps,t​​=EQ​[Bt−1​∣Fs​].
(44)
Multiplication by the Fs​-measurable Bs​ gives
Ps,t​=EQ​[e−∫st​Ru​du∣Fs​].​
(45)
Conversely this conditional-expectation formula makes the discounted bond a martingale by the tower property of conditional expectation. The equivalence uses integrability and true martingales, rather than merely local martingales.
Forward-rate modelling specifies the stochastic evolution of f(s,t) as s varies at fixed maturity. For example, with one Brownian driver, write df(s,t)=α(s,t)ds+β(s,t)dWsQ​. Differentiation of the integral representation and Itô formula give the bond's relative volatility −∫st​β(s,u)du and drift Rs​−∫st​α(s,u)du+21​(∫st​β(s,u)du)2. The discounted-bond condition therefore imposes
∫st​α(s,u)du=21​(∫st​β(s,u)du)2,

or, after differentiation in maturity, α(s,t)=β(s,t)∫st​β(s,u)du. This illustrates how the no-arbitrage condition constrains forward-rate drift.

∫st​Ru​du=ΔRs​+2θ​Δ2+σ∫st​(t−v)dWvQ​.
(47)
The last stochastic integral is Gaussian with mean zero and variance Δ3/3, by the Itô isometry. Its Gaussian moment-generating function therefore gives
Ps,t​=exp[−ΔRs​−2θ​Δ2+6σ2​Δ3],bs,t​=Δ,as,t​=−2θ​Δ2+6σ2​Δ3.​
(48)
When σ=0 and the initial rate is deterministic, the Brownian and short-rate filtrations coincide because WsQ​=(Rs​−R0​−θs)/σ. If σ=0, the same bond formula holds deterministically, while the asserted filtration equivalence is unnecessary.
For a deterministic drift function θv​, the same calculation gives
Ps,t​=exp[−(t−s)Rs​−∫st​(t−v)θv​dv+6σ2​(t−s)3].
(49)
Let the observed initial curve be P0,tobs​>0, normalized by P0,0obs​=1, and define f0​(t)=−dtd​logP0,tobs​. For a differentiable initial forward curve choose
R0​=f0​(0),θt​=f0′​(t)+σ2t.​
(50)
Indeed the model's initial forward rate is R0​+∫0t​θv​dv−σ2t2/2=f0​(t), so integrating it gives exactly the observed bond curve. This is forward-curve calibration of a shifted Brownian short rate, with Rt​=f0​(t)+σ2t2/2+σWtQ​. Every finite collection of positive bond quotes can first be fitted by a smooth positive curve; an arbitrary entire curve requires the stated smoothness or an appropriate weaker integral interpretation. The freedom is in the deterministic drift, and the initial short rate must match the initial forward rate.
