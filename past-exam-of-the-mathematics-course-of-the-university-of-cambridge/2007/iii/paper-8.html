The Baire category theorem states that, in a nonempty complete metric space, a countable intersection of open dense sets is dense. Equivalently, a nonempty open set cannot be covered by countably many nowhere dense sets.
For the Uniform boundedness principle, let X be a Banach space, Y a normed vector space, and (Tα​) a family of bounded linear operators for which supα​∥Tα​x∥<∞ for every x∈X. Put
Fn​={x∈X:∥Tα​x∥≤n for every α}.
(1)
Each Fn​ is closed, being an intersection of inverse images of closed balls, and X=⋃n​Fn​. By the Baire category theorem, some Fn​ has interior. Choose a closed ball B(x0​,r)⊆Fn​ with r>0. For ∥h∥≤r, both x0​ and x0​+h belong to Fn​, and linearity gives ∥Tα​h∥≤2n. Scaling yields
αsup​∥Tα​∥≤r2n​<∞.​
(2)
Completeness is needed for the domain, not the codomain.
We next prove the Silverman-Toeplitz theorem. Write c for the real convergent sequence space with the supremum norm. It is a Banach space: the l-infinity sequence space is complete by coordinatewise passage to limits of norm-Cauchy sequences, and c is closed in it. Indeed, if x(m)→x uniformly and each x(m) converges, approximate x within ε/3 by one x(m) and make that sequence's tail oscillation smaller than ε/3. Then the tail of x is Cauchy and therefore converges in R.
Suppose A is a regular summability matrix. Fix a row i and consider the finite bounded linear functionals
Si,N​(x)=∑j=1N​aij​xj​(x∈c).
(3)

Their operator norms are exactly ∑j≤N​∣aij​∣: the upper bound is the triangle inequality, and the finite sign sequence xj​=sgn(aij​) for j≤N, zero afterwards, attains it. Applying the Uniform boundedness principle to this row shows ∑j​∣aij​∣<∞.
Consequently the full row functional Ai​:c→R is continuous and has norm ∑j​∣aij​∣, using the same finite sign tests and then letting N→∞. Regularity makes (Ai​(x))i​ convergent, hence bounded, for every x∈c. A second application of the Uniform boundedness principle gives a common row bound M. Testing regularity on a coordinate vector ej​ and on the constant-one sequence gives, respectively, convergence to zero down each column and convergence of row sums to one. Thus the necessary conditions are
supi​∑j​∣aij​∣≤M<∞,aij​⟶0 (j fixed),si​:=∑j​aij​⟶1.

Given ε>0, choose N such that ∣xj​−L∣<ε/(M+1) for j>N. The tail of the first sum has modulus at most Mε/(M+1)<ε, uniformly in i. Its finite head tends to zero by columnwise convergence, and L(si​−1)→0. Since ε is arbitrary, Ai​(x)→L. The three conditions are necessary and sufficient for regularity.

This is subsequence selection as regular matrix summability; it reverses the quantifier order of the impossibility result for a fixed matrix.

Let C=∥T∥. We construct the extension by a deterministic recursion over the given dense list, using the one-dimensional dominated extension of a real linear functional. Neither Zorn's lemma nor any selection of a family of extensions is needed.
Suppose f:W→R is already a linear functional satisfying ∣f(w)∣≤C∥w∥, and v∈/W. An extension to W+Rv must have the form
fv​(w+tv)=f(w)+ta.
(16)
Define
Lv​=supw∈W​(f(w)−C∥w−v∥),Uv​=infw∈W​(C∥w+v∥−f(w)).
(17)
For any w,z∈W, the bound on f and the triangle inequality give
f(w)+f(z)=f(w+z)≤C∥w+z∥≤C∥w−v∥+C∥z+v∥.
(18)
Thus every quantity in the supremum defining Lv​ is at most every quantity in the infimum defining Uv​. Taking w=0 and z=0 gives finite bounds, specifically
−C∥v∥≤Lv​≤Uv​≤C∥v∥.
(19)
The completeness of the real numbers defines these endpoints uniquely. Set a=Lv​; this is an explicit rule, not an arbitrary choice from a nonempty interval.
For t>0, the upper-endpoint inequality with w/t yields f(w)+ta≤C∥w+tv∥. For t<0, write t=−s with s>0 and use the lower-endpoint inequality with w/s to obtain the same bound. For t=0 it is the original bound. Applying the upper bound also to the negative of the vector gives
∣fv​(w+tv)∣≤C∥w+tv∥.
(20)
The representation w+tv is unique because v∈/W, so fv​ is a well-defined bounded linear functional extending f.
Start with W0​=U and f0​=T. At step n, if yn​∈Wn−1​ leave the functional unchanged; otherwise use exactly the supremum-endpoint rule with v=yn​. Put Wn​=U+span{y1​,…,yn​}. This defines compatible functionals fn​ on Wn​ with the common bound C. Their union f∞​ on W∞​=⋃n​Wn​ is linear and bounded by C. The subspace W∞​ is dense in V because it contains every yn​.
To make the final passage to the closure equally free of the axiom of choice, for v∈V let jn​(v) be the least positive integer with ∥yjn​(v)​−v∥<2−n. Density ensures that this integer exists, and leastness specifies it uniquely. Define
T(v)=limn→∞​f∞​(yjn​(v)​).
(21)
The limit exists because
∣f∞​(yjn​(v)​)−f∞​(yjm​(v)​)∣≤C(2−n+2−m).
(22)
Any other approximating sequence in W∞​ gives the same limit, by the same norm bound. Applying this independence to sums and scalar multiples of approximating sequences proves linearity of T. It also shows T=f∞​ on W∞​ and ∣T(v)∣≤C∥v∥. Restriction to U therefore gives
T∣U​=T,∥T∥=∥T∥.​
(23)
The upper bound follows from construction and the lower bound from restriction to U. This also covers C=0, when all extensions are zero. No completeness of V or closedness of U was assumed. The argument establishes the choice-free Hahn-Banach extension in a separable space by uniquely specified operations at every stage.

A point x of a set S is an extreme point if x=(1−t)y+tz, with y,z∈S and 0<t<1, forces y=z=x. For a convex set it is enough to test midpoint decompositions: a nontrivial segment containing x in its interior contains a smaller segment with midpoint x. The nonempty convex set R2 has no extreme points, since every x is the midpoint of x+e1​ and x−e1​.
For the real l-infinity sequence space, a vector in the closed unit ball is extreme precisely when every coordinate is 1 or −1. If ∣xj​∣<1 for some j, choose 0<δ≤1−∣xj​∣. The distinct vectors x±δej​ remain in the unit ball and have midpoint x, so x is not extreme. Conversely suppose all coordinates have modulus one and x=(y+z)/2, with ∥y∥∞​,∥z∥∞​≤1. In each real interval [−1,1], an endpoint can be a midpoint only if both entries equal that endpoint. Thus yj​=zj​=xj​ for every j and y=z=x. Therefore
extBℓ∞​={x:xj​∈{−1,1} for every j}.​
(24)
For the real absolutely summable sequence space, any point with ∥x∥1​<1 is nonextreme: choose a nonzero coordinate perturbation of norm less than 1−∥x∥1​. If ∥x∥1​=1 and two coordinates xj​,xk​ are nonzero, take 0<δ<min(∣xj​∣,∣xk​∣) and put
h=δ(sgn(xj​)ej​−sgn(xk​)ek​).
(25)
Both perturbations preserve the signs of those two coordinates. One adds δ to one absolute value and subtracts it from the other, so ∥x+h∥1​=∥x−h∥1​=1. Hence such an x is not extreme. The only remaining candidates are ±ej​. If ej​=(y+z)/2 with ∥y∥1​,∥z∥1​≤1, its jth coordinate forces yj​=zj​=1. That consumes the entire norm allowance, forcing all other coordinates to vanish. The argument for −ej​ is identical. Thus
extBℓ1​={ej​,−ej​:j≥1}.​
(26)
For the real l2 sequence space, interior points of the unit ball again permit sufficiently small opposite perturbations and are not extreme. If ∥x∥2​=1 and x=(y+z)/2, write y=x+h, z=x−h. The parallelogram law gives
2∥y∥22​+∥z∥22​​=∥x∥22​+∥h∥22​=1+∥h∥22​.
(27)
The left side is at most one, so h=0 and y=z=x. Consequently
extBℓ2​={x:∥x∥2​=1}.​
(28)
These are the extreme points of real sequence-space unit balls: signs in ℓ∞, coordinate spikes in ℓ1, and the whole sphere in ℓ2. The restriction to real scalars matters for the first description; the complex supremum ball has arbitrary unit-modulus coordinates instead.

The Gelfand-Mazur theorem says that a nonzero complex unital Banach algebra in which every nonzero element is invertible is the complex field itself: a↦λ where a=λ1. This is an isometric isomorphism under the usual normalization ∥1∥=1; without that normalization its norm is simply ∥λ1∥=∣λ∣∥1∥. We first prove the required spectral and resolvent facts rather than assume them.
For any element a of a complex unital Banach algebra A, define its spectrum of an element by
σA​(a)={λ∈C:λ1−a is not invertible}.
(29)
If ∥b∥<1, the partial sums of ∑n≥0​bn converge in the complete algebra. Multiplying a partial sum by 1−b on either side gives 1−bN+1→1, proving the Neumann series inverse. For ∣λ∣>∥a∥ this gives
R(λ,a)=(λ1−a)−1=∑n=0∞​λn+1an​.
(30)
Thus the spectrum is bounded. Under ∥1∥=1, ∥R(λ,a)∥≤(∣λ∣−∥a∥)−1; for an unnormalized identity the same estimate with numerator ∥1∥ suffices. Also λR(λ,a)→1 as ∣λ∣→∞.
If λ0​ is outside the spectrum and R0​=R(λ0​,a), then for ∣h∣∥R0​∥<1,
R(λ0​+h,a)=∑n=0∞​(−h)nR0n+1​.
(31)
This follows by factoring (λ0​+h)1−a=(λ0​1−a)(1+hR0​) and applying the just-proved series. The series converges locally in norm, proving that the resolvent set is open, the spectrum is closed, and the resolvent of an element is holomorphic. Subtracting inverse equations also gives the resolvent identity
R(λ,a)−R(μ,a)=(μ−λ)R(λ,a)R(μ,a),
(32)
consistent with the derivative R′(λ,a)=−R(λ,a)2 from the local series.
The spectrum is nonempty. If it were empty, the resolvent would be an entire algebra-valued function tending to zero at infinity. Choose a bounded linear functional ℓ:A→C with ℓ(1)=1, extending the functional on C1 by the complex Hahn-Banach theorem. Then ℓ(R(λ,a)) is an entire function, bounded outside a disk by the Neumann estimate and bounded inside by continuity and compactness. The Liouville theorem makes it constant, and its decay makes that constant zero. But λℓ(R(λ,a))→ℓ(1)=1, a contradiction. We have proved that every spectrum is a nonempty compact subset of the disk ∣λ∣≤∥a∥.
Now assume every nonzero element of A is invertible. For any a, choose λ∈σA​(a). The noninvertible element a−λ1 must be zero. Thus every element is a scalar multiple of the identity, uniquely because 1=0, and
A=C1≅C.​
(33)
No commutativity assumption was needed for this part; it is forced by the conclusion. Complex scalars are essential to the spectral argument.
For the maximal ideal space and its correspondence with characters of an algebra, now take A to be a commutative complex unital Banach algebra. Every proper ideal lies in an algebraic maximal ideal by Zorn's lemma: a chain's union is an ideal not containing 1. Every proper ideal also has proper closure. Otherwise it contains an element m with ∥1−m∥<1, which is invertible by the Neumann series, forcing the ideal to contain 1. A maximal ideal I is therefore closed, since its closure is a proper ideal containing it.
The quotient A/I, with its quotient norm, is a complex Banach algebra. Completeness follows from closedness of I, and submultiplicativity follows by taking infima over representatives. It is a division algebra: if a∈/I, maximality gives I+Aa=A, so ba+i=1 for some b∈A, i∈I. Hence a+I is invertible. By the proved Gelfand-Mazur theorem, A/I≅C, with the identity mapping to 1. Composing this isomorphism with the quotient map produces a nonzero multiplicative complex linear functional ϕI​ with kernel I.
Conversely let ϕ:A→C be a nonzero multiplicative complex linear functional, without assuming continuity. Nonzeroness and multiplicativity give ϕ(1)=1, hence ϕ(λ1)=λ and surjectivity onto C. Its kernel is a maximal ideal. If a−ϕ(a)1 were invertible, applying ϕ to its product with an inverse would give 0=1. Therefore ϕ(a)∈σA​(a), so
∣ϕ(a)∣≤∥a∥.
(34)
This proves automatic continuity of characters; with normalized identity their operator norm is one. Finally a kernel determines its character uniquely: for every a, its class in A/I=C1 is a unique scalar multiple of the unit, and every character with kernel I must take that scalar value. We have obtained the bijection
{maximal ideals of A}⟷{nonzero multiplicative complex linear functionals on A},I↔ϕI​.​
(35)
The commutativity in this correspondence cannot be dropped: Mat2​(C) has the maximal two-sided ideal 0, but has no scalar-valued character, since such a character would have zero kernel and give an impossible injective linear map into C.
For use in the next question, the spectrum equals character values in a commutative Banach algebra. We have proved one inclusion. For the other, if a−λ1 is noninvertible, its generated ideal is proper, hence lies in a maximal ideal. The corresponding character takes a to λ. Thus
σA​(a)={ϕ(a):ϕ∈MA​}.
(36)
Give MA​ the Gelfand topology, namely the weak-star topology of pointwise convergence on A through these characters. It is Hausdorff and compact: the characters lie in the dual unit ball and form a weak-star closed subset, described by the closed equations ϕ(1)=1 and ϕ(ab)=ϕ(a)ϕ(b). Apply the Banach-Alaoglu theorem. The Gelfand transform a↦a, where a(ϕ)=ϕ(a), is consequently a contractive unital algebra homomorphism into C(MA​); it separates distinct characters. This argument proves compactness and continuity, but does not assert injectivity or isometry for an arbitrary Banach algebra.

The first assertion needs the C-star identity; a Banach-algebra involution alone is insufficient. For an explicit counterexample, take the dual-number algebra
B=C[ε]/(ε2),∥a+bε∥=∣a∣+∣b∣,(a+bε)∗=aˉ+bˉε.
(37)
This is a commutative unital Banach algebra, since it is finite-dimensional and
∥(a+bε)(c+dε)∥=∣ac∣+∣ad+bc∣≤(∣a∣+∣b∣)(∣c∣+∣d∣).
(38)
Its involution is conjugate-linear, multiplicative, involutive and isometric. Every character of an algebra kills ε, because ϕ(ε)2=0. Thus there is just one character, a+bε↦a, and the Gelfand transform sends the nonzero norm-one element ε to zero. It is neither injective nor norm preserving. In particular ∥ε∗ε∥=0=∥ε∥2, identifying the missing norm axiom.
Even the claimed compatibility with the involution is not automatic. On C2 with the maximum norm and pointwise multiplication, define (a,b)∗=(bˉ,aˉ). This is another isometric involution, but evaluation at the first coordinate takes (1,0)∗ to zero rather than to the conjugate of its value 1 at (1,0). These examples establish involution alone does not imply an isometric Gelfand transform.
We now prove the intended assertion for a commutative complex unital C-star algebra. Let M be its maximal ideal space, identified with the compact character space of an algebra as in Question 3. The C-star identity and submultiplicativity give
∥a∥2=∥a∗a∥≤∥a∗∥∥a∥,
(39)
and applying the same inequality to a∗ proves ∥a∗∥=∥a∥. Thus the involution is continuous. For a self-adjoint element h, the exponential series defines ut​=exp(ith) in norm. Applying the involution term by term gives ut∗​=exp(−ith), and multiplying the absolutely convergent series shows ut∗​ut​=1. Hence ∥ut​∥=1. Every character of an algebra is contractive by Question 3, and so
∣eitϕ(h)∣=∣ϕ(ut​)∣≤1(t∈R).
(40)
Taking both signs of t forces ϕ(h) to be real. Writing
a=h+ik,h=(a+a∗)/2,k=(a−a∗)/(2i),
(41)
with h,k self-adjoint, proves
a∗=a.​
(42)
This proves that characters of a C-star algebra respect the involution.
To prove the norm assertion, we also justify the spectral radius formula used here. Put r(a)=maxλ∈σ(a)​∣λ∣. By spectrum equals character values in a commutative Banach algebra,
r(a)n=supϕ∈M​∣ϕ(an)∣≤∥an∥.
(43)
For R>∥a∥, integrating the uniformly convergent Neumann series on ∣z∣=R gives
an=2πi1​∫∣z∣=R​zn(z1−a)−1dz.
(44)
Only the coefficient of z−1 survives. The resolvent is holomorphic outside the spectrum by Question 3, so the Cauchy integral theorem deforms this contour to any circle of radius R>r(a). For algebra-valued integrals the same identity follows by applying every bounded linear functional and then using Hahn-Banach separation. Taking norms gives
∥an∥≤Rn+1max∣z∣=R​∥(z1−a)−1∥.
(45)
The maximum is finite by resolvent continuity on the circle. Taking nth roots and then R↓r(a) proves limn​∥an∥1/n=r(a).
Every element is normal because B is commutative. For self-adjoint h, the C-star identity gives ∥h2∥=∥h∥2. Therefore, for arbitrary a,
∥a2∥2=∥(a2)∗a2∥=∥(a∗a)2∥=∥a∗a∥2=∥a∥4.
(46)
Iterating yields ∥a2n∥1/2n=∥a∥, so the proved spectral radius formula gives
∥a∥∞​=r(a)=∥a∥.​
(47)
The Gelfand transform is thus isometric and injective. Its range is closed, since B is complete and an isometry takes norm-Cauchy sequences back to norm-Cauchy sequences. It contains the constants, separates the characters, and is closed under complex conjugation by the proved involution identity. The Stone-Weierstrass theorem says that such a unital self-adjoint point-separating subalgebra of C(M) is uniformly dense: apply its real version to real and imaginary parts. Closedness then makes the range all of C(M). Hence the Gelfand transform is an isometric star-isomorphism under the C-star hypothesis, establishing the Commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem in the form required.
For the finite-dimensional operator application, assume first V=0 and let A be the unital algebra generated by α,β,α∗,β∗. Their pairwise commutation makes A commutative; it is closed under the adjoint and norm closed because it is finite-dimensional. With the operator norm it is a C-star algebra. Indeed inner product duality gives ∥T∗∥=∥T∥, and
∥T∥2=sup∥v∥=1​⟨T∗Tv,v⟩≤∥T∗T∥≤∥T∗∥∥T∥=∥T∥2.
(48)
Thus the missing hypothesis in the first paragraph really does hold for this algebra, independently of any spectral theorem.
The proved result identifies A with C(MA​). This character space is finite. For if it had N distinct points, point separation would give, for each pair, a continuous function taking values 1 and 0 at the chosen two points. Products of these functions give N functions with Kronecker-delta values on the selected points, hence N linearly independent functions. Taking N>dimA is impossible. Write MA​={ϕ1​,…,ϕm​}, a finite Hausdorff and therefore discrete space.
Let pj​ be the indicator of {ϕj​} and let πj​ be its inverse Gelfand transform in A. Since pj2​=pj​, pˉ​j​=pj​, pj​pk​=0 for j=k, and ∑j​pj​=1, the injective star-isomorphism gives
πj2​=πj​=πj∗​,πj​πk​=0 (j=k),∑j​πj​=IV​.
(49)
Thus the πj​ are mutually orthogonal linear projections, and in particular commute. Put λj​=ϕj​(α) and μj​=ϕj​(β). The pointwise identities α=∑j​λj​pj​ and β​=∑j​μj​pj​ pull back to
α=j=1∑m​λj​πj​,β=j=1∑m​μj​πj​.​
(50)
The ranges are simultaneous eigenspaces: on πj​V, the two operators act as the scalars λj​,μj​. This proves that finite commuting normal operators have joint spectral projections. If V=0, the same conclusion holds with empty sums.
