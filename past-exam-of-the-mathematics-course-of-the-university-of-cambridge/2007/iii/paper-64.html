Use the spatial-potential convention of this paper and assume ρˉ​+Pˉ=0. A passive time displacement changes the spatial scalar by Ψ−Ψ=NˉHξ0 and changes the energy density by
δρ​−δρ=−ρˉ​˙​ξ0=3NˉH(ρˉ​+Pˉ)ξ0.
(9)
The two contributions to the uniform-density curvature perturbation therefore cancel:
ζ​−ζ=−NˉHξ0+3(ρˉ​+Pˉ)3NˉH(ρˉ​+Pˉ)ξ0​=0.
(10)
A spatial scalar displacement changes E but not this combination. Hence ζ is gauge invariant at linear order. Pure cosmological-constant matter, for which ρˉ​+Pˉ=0, does not supply a energy density clock and this formula is not defined for that component alone.
Now take the definite constant-w barotropic equation of state and use physical-time differentiation D=Nˉ−1∂t​. Put A=ρˉ​+Pˉ. Background stress-energy conservation gives DA=−3H(1+w)A. Differentiating ζ=−Ψ+δρ/(3A), then substituting the two supplied perturbation equations, gives
Dζ​=−DΨ+3ADδρ​+H(1+w)Aδρ​=HΦ−3κ​−3Δχ​−HAδρ+δP​+3κ​−HΦ−3AΔu​+H(1+w)Aδρ​=−AH​(δP−wδρ)−31​Δ(χ+Au​).​
(11)
Thus the lapse and expansion perturbations cancel without imposing a gauge. In terms of the non-adiabatic pressure perturbation δPnad​=δP−wδρ,
Nˉζ˙​​=−ρˉ​+PˉHδPnad​​−31​Δ(χ+ρˉ​+Pˉu​).​
(12)
For the stipulated barotropic equation of state, δP=wδρ, so the first term vanishes. For a Fourier mode, Δ=−k2/a2. For regular long-wavelength solutions the remaining gradients are suppressed when k≪aH. Consequently ζ˙​≃0 on superhorizon scales, with exact conservation in the leading zero-gradient limit. It is not an exact finite-k identity. Entropy perturbations or a singular gradient expansion can invalidate that conclusion; the supplied adiabatic assumption is what removes the unsuppressed pressure source.
This superhorizon conservation of uniform-density curvature is useful because inflationary fluctuations leave the horizon during cosmic inflation and may re-enter only much later. In an adiabatic single-clock evolution, the conserved uniform-density curvature perturbation transports their initial amplitude through reheating and the later radiation in cosmology and matter eras without solving every short-distance process. It then determines the initial conditions for large-scale structure and Cosmic microwave background anisotropy. Extra fluctuating fields or fluids can introduce isocurvature perturbations and convert them into curvature, so conservation must be checked rather than assumed during such transitions.

Let H=a′/a and θN​=ik⋅vN​. Choose the synchronous gauge rest frame of cold dark matter, so θC​=0. Its continuity equation then gives h′=−2δC′​. Substitution in the trace Einstein equation gives
δC′′​+HδC′​−23​H2(ΩC​δC​+2ΩR​δR​)=0.​
(13)
For radiation in cosmology the continuity and Euler equations are δR′​+4θR​/3+2h′/3=0 and θR′​=k2δR​/4. Differentiate the first, eliminate θR′​ and h′′, and obtain
δR′′​+3k2​δR​−34​δC′′​=0.​
(14)
radiation in cosmology pressure supplies the acoustic restoring term while its energy density contributes twice as strongly to the trace gravitational source through 1+3wR​=2.
Deep in radiation domination, a∝τ, H=1/τ, ΩR​≃1 and ΩC​≃0. At long wavelengths kτ≪1, the adiabatic initial conditions require δC​=3δR​/4. The cold-matter equation reduces to
δC′′​+τ1​δC′​−τ24​δC​=0.
(15)
A power τp gives p2−4=0. The regular growing solution therefore has
δC​=A(k)τ2,δR​=34​A(k)τ2,kτ≪1.​
(16)
The other adiabatic solution diverges as τ−2 and is discarded for the regular primordial growing mode. The relation between the density contrasts fixes their relative entropy to zero, not their subsequent equality inside the acoustic horizon.
One can also solve the leading radiation-era equations explicitly, which proves that the same regular mode develops logarithmic cold-matter growth after horizon entry. Put x=kτ/3​. The first equation gives δR​=(x2δC,xx​+xδC,x​)/3. Substituting in the second gives
x2δC,xxxx​+5xδC,xxx​+x2δC,xx​+xδC,x​=0.
(17)
Define
F(x)=∫0x​t1−cost​dt+xsinx​−x21−cosx​−21​.
(18)
Its useful derivative is F′=1/x−2sinx/x2+2(1−cosx)/x3. Direct differentiation verifies the fourth-order equation and gives the associated radiation in cosmology solution:
δC​=K(k)F(x),δR​=3K​[−2cosx+x4sinx​−x24(1−cosx)​].
(19)
At zero, F=x2/8+O(x4) and δR​=Kx2/6+O(x4), so this is precisely the regular adiabatic radiation-era cold-dark-matter transfer solution with K=24A/k2. The other indicial behaviors of the fourth-order equation are 1,x,x−2; they are either nonadiabatic at leading order or singular and do not add another regular growing adiabatic mode.
For x≫1, the integral is γ+lnx−Ci(x), where γ is the Euler's constant and Ci the cosine integral. Its oscillatory tail cancels the explicit leading sinx/x term, giving
δC​=K[lnx+γ−21​+O(x−2)],δR​=−32K​cosx+O(x−1).
(20)
Thus the requested late sound-horizon behavior is
δC​≃B(k)ln(τ/τ∗​),δR​≃C(k)cos(kτ/3​)+D(k)sin(kτ/3​).​
(21)
An additive constant in δC​ is absorbed into the reference τ∗​; the regular adiabatic solution fixes the acoustic phase, with D=0 in the explicit time-origin convention above. A general acoustic mixture has both terms. The physical expansion parameter is kτ/3​; comparisons with 2π/k describe the same long- and short-wavelength regimes up to fixed numerical factors.
The contrast grows much more slowly after entry during radiation domination: radiation in cosmology undergoes pressure-supported acoustic oscillations and cold matter only the logarithmic Mészáros effect. Modes entering well before matter-radiation equality consequently have suppressed late amplitudes relative to modes that stay outside the horizon until near equality. After matter domination begins, pressureless growing modes can grow as a. This scale-dependent history produces the turnover and small-scale suppression of the matter transfer function and affects the initial conditions for large-scale structure of the universe. The early subhorizon limit requires both kτ≫1 and τ≪τeq​; it is not applicable to a mode that enters only in the matter domination.

Before decoupling, rapid Thomson scattering makes the photon distribution nearly isotropic in the local photon-fluid rest frame. A local blackbody temperature perturbation changes its energy density by δγ​=4δT/T. A first-order boost by the fluid velocity adds a temperature photon dipole n⋅v; it does not generate a photon quadrupole until second order in that boost. Thus the photon brightness perturbation at instantaneous decoupling is
Δ∗​=δγ∗​+4n⋅v∗​.
(40)
Higher angular moments are suppressed by the photon mean-free-path to perturbation-length ratio in the tight-coupling approximation. The idealized equilibrium assumption neglects those finite-mean-free-path corrections, polarization effects and finite thickness of the last-scattering surface. It is not an assertion that collisionless photons remain isotropic after decoupling. The distinction between a fluid treatment and the subsequent hierarchy is discussed in the primary synchronous and Newtonian perturbation treatment.
Let μ=k^⋅n and W(τ)=eikμ(τ−τ0​). Multiplication of the synchronous photon brightness equation by its integrating factor gives
(eikμτΔ)′=−2eikμτhij′​ninj.
(41)
Integration from τ∗​=τdec​ to τ0​ and division by four yield
Θ(k,μ,τ0​)=(4δγ∗​​+n⋅v∗​)e−ikμ(τ0​−τ∗​)−21​∫τ∗​τ0​​W(τ)hij′​ninjdτ,Θ=TΔT​.​
(42)
For the real-space observation point x0​, the corresponding background ray is x(τ)=x0​−n(τ0​−τ). Fourier inversion gives the synchronous Sachs-Wolfe line-of-sight formula
Θ(x0​,n,τ0​)=41​δγ​(x∗​,τ∗​)+n⋅v(x∗​,τ∗​)−21​∫τ∗​τ0​​hij′​(x(τ),τ)ninjdτ,x∗​=x(τ∗​).​
(43)
Every source is evaluated at its appropriate point on the ray, rather than at the observation position. Here n is photon propagation direction, opposite to the outward observer-to-source sky direction; changing that convention changes the written sign of the Doppler projection consistently.
The first term is the intrinsic photon temperature at last scattering. On wavelengths larger than the sound horizon, it retains primordial adiabatic information; inside that horizon the photon-baryon fluid has acoustic energy density oscillations, producing the acoustic temperature peaks. The second term is the Doppler effect from the fluid's bulk motion at emission. A regular long-wavelength velocity is gradient-suppressed; the Doppler contribution is important near and below the acoustic horizon, with its acoustic phase displaced from that of the energy density oscillation.
The integral is the gravitational energy shift produced by the changing spatial metric tensor along the photon path. In synchronous gauge it includes endpoint gravitational redshifts as well as evolving-potential effects. It must not be identified solely with the Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect: in Newtonian gauge the same total separates into the ordinary last-scattering Sachs-Wolfe effect, local observer terms, and an integral of time-varying potentials. The ordinary contribution is important on scales outside the sound horizon at decoupling. A late integrated contribution is strongest on large angular scales when dark energy or curvature changes the potentials; evolution around matter-radiation equality also contributes near the first acoustic scales.
The relevant comoving sound scale is rs​(τ∗​)=∫τ∗​cs​(τ)dτ, while the projection distance is D∗​≃τ0​−τ∗​. Modes project roughly to photon temperature multipole ℓ∼kD∗​, and the acoustic scale is ℓ∼D∗​/rs​, with successive peak spacing of order πD∗​/rs​. At much shorter wavelengths Silk damping and the finite last-scattering thickness suppress energy density and velocity anisotropies; the instantaneous-decoupling approximation alone does not model that cutoff. These scale distinctions explain why the three terms cannot be assigned one common range of important wavelengths.
