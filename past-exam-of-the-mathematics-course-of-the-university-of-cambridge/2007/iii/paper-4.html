All brackets belong to the line Cc, and [p,q]=c shows this line is exactly the first derived algebra. Since c is central, the derived series of a Lie algebra is
g(0)=g,g(1)=Cc,g(2)=0.​
(1)
The lower central series of a Lie algebra is likewise
γ1​g=g,γ2​g=Cc,γ3​g=0.​
(2)
For completeness, the center of a Lie algebra is Cc: an element ap+bq+dc commutes with p only if b=0, and with q only if a=0. The upper central series of a Lie algebra is therefore 0⊂Cc⊂g, because the quotient by Cc is abelian. Thus the Heisenberg Lie algebra is nilpotent of class two and solvable of derived length two. These properties are not mutually exclusive.

Let V be a nonzero finite-dimensional Irreducible Lie algebra representation. Since c is central, its action is a scalar λI by the Schur lemma. Taking the trace of [ρ(p),ρ(q)]=ρ(c) gives 0=λdimV, so λ=0.
The actions of p,q now commute. Choose an eigenvalue a of ρ(p). Its nonzero eigenspace is preserved by ρ(q), which has an eigenvector there, with some eigenvalue b. The span of this common eigenvector is invariant under all of g. Irreducibility forces it to be the whole representation. Consequently
V=C,ρ(p)=a,ρ(q)=b,ρ(c)=0,(a,b)∈C2.​
(3)
Conversely each such assignment is a one-dimensional Lie algebra representation and hence irreducible. Distinct pairs give nonisomorphic representations. This classifies all finite-dimensional irreducible representations; none is faithful because all annihilate c.

Use the Polynomial representation of the Heisenberg Lie algebra on V=C[x]:
ρ(p)=dxd​,ρ(q)f=xf,ρ(c)=I.​
(4)
The product rule gives [d/dx,x]f=f, verifying the required bracket, and the identity commutes with both operators.
For irreducibility, let W be a nonzero invariant subspace and choose a nonzero polynomial of degree d in it. Its dth derivative is a nonzero constant, so 1∈W. Repeated multiplication by x then puts every monomial in W, giving W=C[x].
For faithfulness, suppose aρ(p)+bρ(q)+dI=0. Applying this operator to 1 gives bx+d=0, so b=d=0. Applying what remains to x gives a=0. The representation is therefore faithful and irreducible. It is necessarily infinite-dimensional, by the finite-dimensional classification just proved.

We construct the exceptional isomorphism between sp4 and so5. Let W=C4 have a nondegenerate alternating form Ω and symplectic basis a1​,b1​,a2​,b2​. Define the symplectic contraction of an exterior square
κ:Λ2W→C,κ(u∧v)=Ω(u,v),E=kerκ.
(13)
It is equivariant for the symplectic Lie algebra, and dimE=5. Identify Λ4W with C using a1​∧b1​∧a2​∧b2​. Wedge product then gives a symmetric bilinear form B(ξ,η)=ξ∧η on Λ2W. It is nondegenerate, as the wedge-basis vectors pair with their complementary pairs.
The bivector τ=a1​∧b1​+a2​∧b2​ is invariant under sp(W), satisfies B(τ,τ)=2, and has B(ξ,τ)=κ(ξ). Consequently E=τ⊥ and B∣E​ is nondegenerate. The exterior-power Lie algebra representation preserves wedge product and the volume form, so restriction gives a homomorphism
Φ:sp4​⟶so(E,B).
(14)
We check injectivity directly. If X acts trivially on E, it also annihilates the invariant line Cτ, hence acts trivially on all of Λ2W. In any basis w1​,…,w4​, the equation X(wi​∧wj​)=0 forces every off-diagonal coefficient Xki​ to vanish: choose j distinct from i,k and inspect the coefficient of wk​∧wj​. Thus X is diagonal. Its diagonal entries xi​ satisfy xi​+xj​=0 for every pair, which forces them all to be zero. Therefore Φ is injective.
A symplectic matrix has infinitesimal block form (AC​B−AT​) with B,C symmetric, so dimsp4​=4+3+3=10. Also dimso(E,B)=5⋅4/2=10. Injectivity and equal dimensions make Φ an isomorphism. Every nondegenerate complex symmetric form in dimension five is equivalent by a change of basis to the given antidiagonal form, so
so5​≅sp4​.​
(15)

Each arrow of color i subtracts the simple root αi​. The two arrows through zero form the length-two short-root string.
Here is a complete grid description of the tensor product of crystals, valid for arbitrary n. Place the vertex a⊗b in row a, column b, in the above chain order; there are (2n+1)2 vertices. Let εi​(a) and φi​(a) count incoming and outgoing steps along the color-i string. For i<n, the two strings are i→i+1 and i+1​→iˉ, each of length one. For i=n, the string is n→0→nˉ, with pairs (εn​,φn​) equal to (0,2),(1,1),(2,0). Other vertices have both counts zero.
Using the crystal tensor-product rule, draw every color-i edge by
f​i​(a⊗b)={f​i​(a)⊗b,a⊗f​i​(b),​φi​(a)>εi​(b),φi​(a)≤εi​(b).​​
(20)
The first case is a downward arrow to the next row and the second a rightward arrow to the next column; omit an arrow when its indicated factor has no outgoing edge. Thus the rule specifies every vertex and every arrow of the general tensor-square diagram, including the boundary nodes. The figure displays the resulting entire grid for n=3, alongside the vector crystal; the generator can draw any positive rank.
The raising rule chooses the first factor when φi​(a)≥εi​(b) and the second otherwise. For n≥2, its only highest vertices are

Indeed a highest tensor must have highest first factor, hence a=1. For colors i>1, the second factor cannot have an incoming edge. For color one, its incoming count must be at most φ1​(1)=1. Inspecting the strings leaves precisely b=1,2,1ˉ.

Let g be a complex semisimple Lie algebra, choose a Cartan subalgebra and a positive root system R+, and let λ be a dominant integral highest weight. Write L(λ) for the corresponding finite-dimensional irreducible representation, α∨=2α/(α,α) for a coroot, and ρ=21​∑α∈R+​α for the Weyl vector. The Weyl dimension formula is
dimL(λ)=α∈R+∏​⟨ρ,α∨⟩⟨λ+ρ,α∨⟩​.​
(23)
The bracket denotes the natural pairing of weights with coroots. The normalization of an invariant inner product cancels from each ratio, so the formula can equivalently use roots instead of coroots in both numerator and denominator.

Number the simple roots of the G2 root system so that α1​ is short and α2​ is long. In the Euclidean plane choose

Together with their negatives these form the twelve-root diagram: two regular hexagons of radii one and 3​, rotated by 30 degrees relative to one another. Solving the fundamental weight equations gives
ω1​=2α1​+α2​=(1/2,3​/2),ω2​=3α1​+2α2​=(0,3​).​

In particular ω1​ is the highest short root and ω2​ the highest long root. The root diagram below marks both weights.
To evaluate the Weyl dimension formula, put a=n1​, b=n2​ and λ=aω1​+bω2​. The Weyl vector is ρ=ω1​+ω2​. In the order of positive roots listed above, their coroots have simple-coroot coefficient pairs

Give g its Adjoint representation and use the tensor product of Lie algebra representations. Define the action map of a Lie algebra representation
a:g⊗V⟶V,a(x⊗v)=xv.​
(29)
For y∈g, the action on the source is y(x⊗v)=[y,x]⊗v+x⊗yv. The defining representation identity then gives
a(y(x⊗v))=[y,x]v+x(yv)=y(xv)=ya(x⊗v).
(30)
Thus a is a Lie algebra representation homomorphism.
If V is nontrivial and simple, the action is not identically zero, so the image of a is a nonzero submodule. Simplicity forces a to be surjective. For a finite-dimensional V and a semisimple Lie algebra, the Weyl complete reducibility theorem provides an invariant complement U to kera in g⊗V. The restriction a∣U​ is then an isomorphism onto V, giving
g⊗V≅kera⊕V.​
(31)
This proves the requested summand assertion in the finite-dimensional setting.
If finite dimensionality is not implicit, the final assertion needs that hypothesis: the action summand can fail for an infinite-dimensional simple module. Here is an explicit counterexample. Take g=sl2​ with [h,e]=2e, [h,f]=−2f, [e,f]=h, and the Verma module of highest weight −2:
V=⨁m≥0​Cvm​,fvm​=vm+1​,hvm​=(−2−2m)vm​,evm​=−m(m+1)vm−1​.
(32)
The lowering coefficient never vanishes for m>0, so an invariant subspace, using the distinct h-weights to isolate a basis vector and then raising, contains v0​ and hence all of V. Thus V is simple.
In sl2​⊗V, a vector of weight −2 is Ae⊗v1​+Bh⊗v0​. Applying e gives −2(A+B)e⊗v0​, so its highest vectors are precisely the multiples of w=e⊗v1​−h⊗v0​. But w=f(e⊗v0​), and e⊗v0​ has weight zero, absent from V. Every homomorphism r:g⊗V→V therefore kills this vector and then kills w. Any embedded copy of V would send its highest vector to a nonzero multiple of w, so it cannot admit a projection back to V. This rules out a direct summand, not merely a splitting of the particular action map.

Let h be the standard Cartan element of the sl2 Lie algebra, with [h,e]=2e and [h,f]=−2f. For a finite-dimensional representation, its formal character records its weight multiplicities:
χV​(q)=j∈Z∑​(dimVj​)qj,Vj​={v:hv=jv}.​
(33)
The classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations and the Weyl complete reducibility theorem give V=⨁m≥0​am​Lm​, with finitely many nonzero am​. Each Lm​ has weights m,m−2,…,−m, each of multiplicity one. Therefore
χV​(q)=∑m≥0​am​(qm+qm−2+⋯+q−m),cj​=∑m≥∣j∣m≡j (2)​​am​.
(34)
In particular cj​=c−j​, and for j≥0 one has cj​−cj+2​=aj​≥0. This proves parity unimodality of an sl2 character: within each parity, the coefficients increase towards zero and decrease away from zero. For a representation whose weights have a single parity, deleting the intervening zero coefficients gives a symmetric unimodal sequence.
The parity convention is necessary for the printed assertion. If unimodality means the full coefficient sequence at every consecutive integer exponent, it is false: the standard two-dimensional representation has character q+q−1, whose coefficients at exponents −1,0,1 are 1,0,1. The statement established above is unimodality on cosets of the root lattice for sl2​, and is the version needed in part ii. Infinite-dimensional representations need not even have Laurent-polynomial characters.

Assume 0≤k≤n with n,k integers, as required for the quantum binomial expression. Let Ln−1​ be the n-dimensional irreducible sl2 Lie algebra representation, with one-dimensional weight spaces of weights n−1−2j, 0≤j<n. The exterior-power Lie algebra representation on ΛkLn−1​ has character
Cn,k​(q)=∑0≤j1​<⋯<jk​<n​qk(n−1)−2(j1​+⋯+jk​).
(35)
This is already a Laurent polynomial with nonnegative integer coefficients.
To identify it with the requested quotient, introduce t=q−2 and the Gaussian binomial coefficient
Gn,k​(t)=∑0≤j1​<⋯<jk​<n​tj1​+⋯+jk​−k(k−1)/2.
(36)
Splitting the subsets according to whether n−1 is included proves
Gn,k​=Gn−1,k​+tn−kGn−1,k−1​,Gn,0​=Gn,n​=1.
(37)
The product ∏j=1k​(1−tn−k+j)/(1−tj) satisfies the same recurrence: after factoring common numerator and denominator terms, the needed identity is (1−tn−k)+tn−k(1−tk)=1−tn. Induction on n therefore proves the product identity, including its polynomiality.
Since the quantum integer is [a]q​=qa−1(1−q−2a)/(1−q−2), taking the quotient of the products gives
[nk​]q​=qk(n−k)Gn,k​(q−2)=Cn,k​(q)=χΛkLn−1​​(q).​
(38)
Thus this is the symmetric quantum binomial coefficient. All its weights have parity k(n−k), because the exponent in the exterior-power sum differs from k(n−k) by an even integer. Applying part i to this finite-dimensional representation proves symmetric unimodality in steps of two. Equivalently, the ordinary polynomial Gn,k​(t) has a unimodal coefficient sequence at consecutive powers of t.
Again, the full integer-exponent coefficient sequence in q, including the absent parity, need not be unimodal: for n=2,k=1 the answer is q+q−1. This counterexample makes precise the convention under which the claimed quantum unimodality holds. The cases k=0 or k=n give the constant one.
