A comoving coordinate labels a position relative to the homogeneous expansion. For two nearby comoving positions separated by Δχ, their proper distance in cosmology at time t is a(t)Δχ. Thus a fixed comoving separation expands physically with the scale factor, while a fixed proper separation corresponds to a shrinking comoving separation. Normalize a0​=1, so a(z)=1/(1+z).
At a common cosmological redshift, a transverse proper size l subtends θ≃l/DA​, where DA​ is the angular diameter distance. At z=3, a 5 comoving Mpc separation has proper size 5/(1+3)=1.25 Mpc. Hence
θ5proper Mpc​=4θ5comoving Mpc​.​
(1)
The proper-size object has the larger angular size, by a factor of four. This comparison does not require a numerical angular diameter distance, since it cancels.

On an expanding branch with a˙>0, minimizing a˙ is equivalent to minimizing its square. Differentiating the preceding Friedmann equation with respect to a gives
dada˙2​=H02​(−a2Ωm,0​a03​​+2ΩΛ,0​a).
(5)
For positive matter density and a positive cosmological constant, it vanishes uniquely at
amin​=a0​(2ΩΛ,0​Ωm,0​​)1/3.​
(6)
The second derivative is H02​[2Ωm,0​a03​/a3+2ΩΛ,0​]>0. Thus this is the minimum expansion speed in a matter-Lambda universe. The matter and vacuum densities satisfy ρm​=2ρΛ​ there, precisely the transition from deceleration to acceleration in the Friedmann acceleration equation.
The conclusion concerns an expanding branch that actually reaches this value of a. A sufficiently closed model can recollapse before reaching it; positive Λ alone does not rule that out. In flat or open models with the stated positive densities the minimum is reached. The degenerate zero-speed limiting case requires separate treatment and does not describe a finite-time passage through a positive-speed minimum.

In a spatially flat FLRW metric, a radial null geodesic satisfies dχ=cdt/a(t). Normalize a0​=1. The comoving particle horizon at time t is therefore rh​=c∫0t​dt′/a(t′), using the initial singularity as the time origin. The cosmological redshift relation 1+z=1/a gives
dtdz​=−(1+z)H(z),a(t)dt​=−H(z)dz​.
(10)
For matter and a cosmological constant in a spatially flat model, the Friedmann equation gives H(z)=H0​[Ωm,0​(1+z)3+ΩΛ,0​]1/2. The initial singularity corresponds to z→∞, so reversing the integration limits yields
rh​(z)=H0​c​∫z∞​[Ωm,0​(1+z′)3+ΩΛ,0​]1/2dz′​.​
(11)
This is a comoving particle horizon, not a distance to the source currently observed at redshift z, and not a future-directed cosmological event horizon. The corresponding proper horizon radius at that epoch is rh​(z)/(1+z).

Use the stipulated common normalization A=H0​Ωm,0​ and plot R=Arh​/c. The Einstein-de Sitter universe has H0,E​=A, whereas the (Ωm,0​,ΩΛ,0​)=(0.3,0.7) model has H0,Λ​=A/0.3. Their comoving particle horizons are therefore
RE​(z)=1+z​2​,RΛ​(z)=0.3∫z∞​0.3(1+z′)3+0.7​dz′​.
(14)
Both decrease with increasing z, or increase with cosmic time. The Einstein-de Sitter universe curve diverges at z=−1 and has RE​(0)=2, RE​(2)=2/3​. The matter-plus-vacuum curve remains finite at z=−1: the integrand approaches a constant there, and its high-redshift tail is integrable. Moreover it lies below the Einstein-de Sitter curve for this particular normalization, since, with u=1+z′>0,
0.3u3+0.7​0.3​<u3/21​.
(15)
The high-redshift ratio of the two curves tends to 0.3​, not one. Holding H0​Ωm,0​​ fixed would give a different comparison; it is H0​Ωm,0​ that the original question specifies.
A positive cosmological constant eventually makes the scale factor grow exponentially. The future integral ∫t∞​cdt′/a(t′) then converges, so the remaining conformal time is finite. The comoving particle horizon approaches a finite limiting visibility radius; it does not shrink. The remaining distance to that limit is the comoving radius of the cosmological event horizon. Thus a signal emitted sufficiently far away today can never reach us, even though the proper particle-horizon radius continues to grow. This is the total conformal lifetime of a flat matter-Lambda universe.

For a radial null geodesic in a spatially flat universe, the comoving radial distance increment is dχ=cdz/H(z). If the cloud and quasar redshifts differ only slightly, take H at their common approximate epoch z∗​, giving
Δχ≃H(z∗​)cδz​,H(z∗​)=H0​Ωm,0​(1+z∗​)3+ΩΛ,0​​.
(18)
Multiplying by the scale factor at that epoch gives the local proper distance in cosmology relevant to the quasar's radiation flux:
dcq​≃H0​(1+z∗​)Ωm,0​(1+z∗​)3+ΩΛ,0​​cδz​.​
(19)
If distance is instead expressed in comoving units, it is (1+z∗​)dcq​. Equivalently the small rest-frame velocity difference is δv=cδz/(1+z∗​), and dcq​≃δv/H(z∗​). This quasar-cloud redshift separation assumes the redshift difference is due to homogeneous expansion, with negligible peculiar velocities. Since the two observations lie on a light cone, the proper separation is understood as the short separation on a nearly common cosmic-time slice; specifying the epoch matters at higher order.

The Lyman-alpha forest is the multitude of Lyman-alpha absorption lines blueward of the broad emission line in a distant quasar spectrum. Neutral hydrogen along the line of sight absorbs at its rest wavelength λα​≃1216A˚, so each absorber appears at λobs​=(1+zc​)λα​. Much of the ordinary forest traces modest overdensities in the mostly ionized intergalactic medium, rather than compact objects made primarily of neutral gas. Strong self-shielded systems form a distinct absorption regime. Line strengths constrain neutral-hydrogen column density, while line widths contain thermal and velocity information.
The redshift evolution of the Lyman-alpha forest is normally described, for a fixed line selection, by an incidence dN/dz=A(1+z)γ. At redshifts of a few the line incidence and mean absorption increase rapidly with redshift; typical selected populations have γ of order 2–3, not a universal exponent. Low-redshift evolution is appreciably flatter. Geometry alone cannot explain the full change: for a fixed comoving abundance and fixed proper cross section one obtains dN/dz∝(1+z)2/H(z), only (1+z)1/2 in matter domination. Density evolution, thermal evolution and the cosmic ionizing background change how many structures exceed the chosen absorption threshold. In optically thin photoionization equilibrium,
nHI​ΓHI​=ne​np​α(T),
(23)
so for roughly fixed overdensity the rising cosmic density alone makes nHI​ scale approximately as α(T)(1+z)6/ΓHI​. At low redshift, the declining ionizing radiation partly offsets the decrease in gas density. Near the end of reionization the forest becomes very opaque and the simple individual-line description ceases to suffice.
The quasar proximity effect is the statistically reduced absorption near the quasar's own redshift, contrary to the general increase of absorption towards higher redshift. It results from additional ionizing radiation from the nearby quasar. At fixed density and temperature the local photoionization rate becomes Γbg​+ΓQ​. Accordingly
NHI​=1+ωNHI(0)​​,ω=Γbg​ΓQ​​,
(24)
where NHI(0)​ is the value with the background alone. Lines weaken and some fall below the detection threshold; the absorbers need not have been physically removed. This gives the proximity-effect estimator of the ionizing background.
For an approximately isotropic quasar and a nearby cloud at proper distance d, calculate its flux from Fν,Q​≃Lν,Q​/(4πd2), allowing for absorption and relative redshift if necessary. The quasar and isotropic background ionization rates are
ΓQ​=∫ν0​∞​hP​νFν,Q​σHI​(ν)​dν,Γbg​=4π∫ν0​∞​hP​νJν​σHI​(ν)​dν,
(25)
where ν0​ is the hydrogen ionization threshold, hP​ is the Planck constant and Jν​ is the background specific intensity. Establish the ordinary absorption statistics far from quasars, then fit their reduction as a function of distance and known quasar luminosity. The scale at which ω∼1 gives the background rate; an assumed spectral shape converts this to an intensity near the ionization threshold. For matching spectral shapes, ω≃Lν0​,Q​/(16π2d2Jν0​​). A column-density distribution proportional to N−p gives a line-incidence suppression (1+ω)1−p above a fixed threshold, by shifting that threshold to (1+ω)Nmin​ in the unperturbed distribution. This is the physical measurement principle established in adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1988ApJ...327..570B .
The inference is statistical. Quasars live in overdense environments, which enhance absorption and can mask extra ionization. Emission-redshift errors smear the effect, as part (iii) demonstrates. Finite source lifetimes, anisotropic emission, variability and departures from ionization equilibrium can also alter the radial profile. These effects must be modeled rather than attributed entirely to the cosmic ionizing background.
The background is produced mainly by ionizing radiation from accretion-powered quasars and hot massive stars in star-forming galaxies. Their relative importance changes with redshift. Quasars have hard spectra and can ionize helium as well as hydrogen; stellar photons depend strongly on their ionizing photon escape fraction from galaxies. Integrating source luminosities over the luminosity function and propagating radiation through absorbing gas gives the background. At high redshift the observed quasar population alone need not supply the required hydrogen ionization, so star-forming galaxies can be essential, as discussed in arxiv.org/abs/0807.4177 . Diffuse recombination radiation can contribute, but a residual primordial radiation field is not an adequate ionizing source.

A complete Einstein ring requires an axisymmetric gravitational lens and exact alignment of the source with the lens centre and observer: β=0. A finite aligned circular source gives a ring of finite width. The background source must lie behind the lens, so the lensing distance factor is positive.
With β=0 the thin gravitational lens equation gives

Choose a signed angular coordinate along the source-lens line. The singular isothermal sphere lens equation is
β=θ−θE​sgnθ.
(33)
For β>0, the positive image is θ+​=β+θE​. A negative image would require θ−​=β−θE​<0, which is possible only for β<θE​. Here β=4 arcmin and θE​=1 arcmin, so there is only one image, at θ=5 arcmin. Formally using the negative-image formula would produce a positive value, inconsistent with the branch from which it was obtained.
The paper defines its vector deflection as source minus image angle, αpaper​=(β−θ)θ^. This is the negative of the usual reduced deflection in β=θ−αstandard​. Therefore its relation to lensing convergence is
κ=−21​∇θ​⋅αpaper​.​

For the SIS, αpaper​=−θE​θ^. The two-dimensional polar-coordinate divergence gives
∇θ​⋅αpaper​=θ1​dθd​(−θE​θ)=−θθE​​,κ=2θθE​​.​

Using a plus sign with the printed inward deflection would incorrectly give negative convergence.
For a small intrinsically circular source, differentiate the thin gravitational lens equation. The radial and tangential source-image Jacobian matrix eigenvalues are
λr​=1,λt​=1−θθE​​.
(36)
Thus the image is stretched tangentially around the lens centre, with no radial stretch in this idealized model. At θ=5 arcmin, λt​=4/5; the image is an ellipse with tangential-to-radial axis ratio 5/4. It is not a complete ring. This local description assumes a source small enough that the Jacobian is nearly constant across it.

Cosmic shear is the small, statistically correlated distortion of distant galaxy images by weak gravitational lensing from intervening large-scale matter inhomogeneities. An individual galaxy has an unknown intrinsic shape, so the signal is measured through ensemble shape correlations rather than by assuming every source is circular. The angular and redshift dependence of these correlations probes the projected matter fluctuations, their growth and the angular diameter distance relation, constraining dark matter, cosmological density parameters and dark energy. Gravitational lensing responds to all gravitating matter rather than just luminous tracers. Intrinsic alignments and shape-measurement biases must be modeled separately from the lensing signal; the physical and statistical basis is described in arxiv.org/abs/1411.0115 .
