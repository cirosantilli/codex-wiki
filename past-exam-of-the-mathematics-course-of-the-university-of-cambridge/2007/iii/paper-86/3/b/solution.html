Keep the coordinates and operator Dk​ from part (a). The attenuation f is known, and the source g is to be reconstructed. Put F(ρ,τ,θ)=∫τ∞​f(scosθ−ρsinθ,ssinθ+ρcosθ)ds and S(ρ,θ)=Rf=∫R​fdτ. The directed forward attenuated Radon transform, for a detector at the positive end of the line, is
Af​g(ρ,θ)=Af​(ρ,θ)=∫−∞∞​e−F(ρ,τ,θ)g(τcosθ−ρsinθ,τsinθ+ρcosθ)dτ.​
(38)
This weight accounts for absorption from each emission point to the detector. It is also derived by solving the real transport equation ∂τ​u+fu=g with zero incoming intensity: its outgoing limit is Af​. In particular reversing the line direction generally changes the measurement. The pair reconstructs g from full directed data and known f; it does not infer both unknown functions from the same data.
We must specify the sign convention for the projectors. Use the Hilbert transform Hv(ρ)=π−1PV∫R​v(s)/(ρ−s)ds and the signed Cauchy boundary operators
P±v=±21​v+2i​Hv.
(39)
Then P+−P−=I, and the positive idempotent projectors are P+ and −P−. This is the signed boundary-value convention in the given limits of M. To express everything with ordinary complementary projectors, one may instead write Π+​=P+, Π−​=−P−. The given limits become
M+=−P−S−F=:a+​−F,M−=P+S−F=:a−​−F.
(40)
Both a±​ depend on the transverse coordinate and direction but are constant along τ.
The equation Dk​M=f supplies an integrating factor:
Dk​(eMμ)=eMg,μ=e−MTk​(eMg),
(41)
where Tk​ is the inverse of unattenuated complex transport, normalized to vanish at spatial infinity. To see the normalization and analyticity rather than assume them, let w=x1​+ix2​. The complexified transport Cauchy kernel is
Ek​(w)=πsgn(1−∣k∣2)​w−k2wˉk​,Tk​h=Ek​∗h,0<∣k∣=1.
(42)
In the rotated coordinates, Ek​=[2π∣B∣(τ+iAρ/B)]−1. The identity ∂zˉ​(1/(πz))=δ and the Jacobian A/∣B∣ prove Dk​Ek​=δ with exactly this normalization. The denominator cannot vanish for nonzero real w off the unit circle. The kernel is analytic separately inside and outside that circle, is O(k) at zero and O(k−1) at infinity. Thus M=Tk​f and the displayed μ have the corresponding spectral behavior for the smooth decaying data. In particular μ=O(k−1) at infinity and is bounded, indeed O(k), at zero.
The limiting unattenuated inverse on any smooth decaying input h is
T+​h=−P−Rh−∫τ∞​hds,T−​h=P+Rh−∫τ∞​hds.
(43)
One may verify this directly in the rotated Fourier variables: Dk​ has multiplier iAξ−Bη, so its inverse has a pole at ξ=−iBη/A. As B→0+, the positive transverse frequencies have the negative tail primitive and the negative transverse frequencies have the positive past primitive; for B→0− these allocations reverse. Splitting with the Fourier projectors Π±​ yields exactly the two formulas above. Applying them to f reproduces the assumed limits of M and checks the signs.
Now apply the same limiting inverse to h=eM±g. Because a±​ are independent of τ, its line integral is ea±​Af​, while its tail is ea±​∫τ∞​e−Fgds. Hence
μ+μ−​=−eF−a+​P−(ea+​Af​)−eF∫τ∞​e−Fgds,=eF−a−​P+(ea−​Af​)−eF∫τ∞​e−Fgds.​
(44)
The unknown incomplete line integral cancels in their difference. All remaining quantities are obtained from the measurements and known attenuation:
μ+−μ−=−J,J=eF[eP−SP−(e−P−SAf​)+e−P+SP+(eP+SAf​)].
(45)
Each projection acts in ρ at fixed θ, before evaluating ρ=−x1​sinθ+x2​cosθ and τ=x1​cosθ+x2​sinθ. Pulling a transverse-dependent exponential outside a projector would be incorrect.
The sectionally analytic μ therefore solves an additive Riemann-Hilbert problem on the counterclockwise unit circle, with the plus value inside. The normalized Cauchy integral formula is
μ(x,k)=−2π1​∫02π​eiθ−keiθJ(x,θ)​dθ.
(46)
The Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem gives its jump; boundedness at zero and decay at infinity make it unique by the Liouville theorem. Its expansion at infinity is
μ(x,k)=2πk1​∫02π​eiθJ(x,θ)dθ+O(k−2).
(47)
Substitute into the original equation. The k∂x1​​−ik∂x2​​ part contributes at order one, the reciprocal derivative and fμ vanish at that order. This derives the projection formula for inverse attenuated Radon transform:
g(x1​,x2​)=4π1​(∂x1​​−i∂x2​​)∫02π​eiθJ(x1​,x2​,θ)dθ.​
(48)
Together with the boxed forward transform this is the requested pair. The derivation uses no small-attenuation approximation. For smooth compactly supported data the operations above are justified directly; sufficient integrable decay permits the same limiting construction.
As a normalization check, set f=0. Then S=F=0, Af​=Rg and J=(P−+P+)Rg=iHRg. Since the line transform is independent of τ and eiθ(∂x1​​−i∂x2​​)=∂τ​−i∂ρ​, the formula reduces to
g(x)=4π1​∫02π​∂ρ​HRg(ρ,θ)​ρ=x⋅(−sinθ,cosθ)​dθ,
(49)
the full-angle inverse Radon transform. For the radial Gaussian g=e−∣x∣2, Rg=π​e−ρ2 and ∂ρ​HRg(0)=2, so the reconstructed value at the origin is 1, checking the 1/(4π) factor.
In single-photon emission computed tomography, g represents the internal emission density, f the known absorption coefficient and Af​ the directed detector counts. Each path integral weights a source point by its survival probability to the detector. Thus the attenuated Radon transform is the forward measurement model and its derived inverse reconstructs the emission distribution, providing the mathematical basis of SPECT.
