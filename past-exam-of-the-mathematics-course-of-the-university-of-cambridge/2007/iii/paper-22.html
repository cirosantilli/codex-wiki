An almost complex structure is a smooth real vector bundle endomorphism J:TM→TM satisfying J2=−I. In particular, M has even real dimension 2n. Extend J complex-linearly to TC​M=TM⊗R​C. Its minimal polynomial divides (u−i)(u+i), whose roots are distinct, so the type decomposition of the complexified tangent bundle is
TC​M=T1,0M⊕T0,1M,T1,0M=ker(J−iI),T0,1M=ker(J+iI).
(1)
The smooth projections are (I−iJ)/2 and (I+iJ)/2. Complex conjugation exchanges the two rank-n eigenbundles. A (1,0)-covector annihilates T0,1M and a (0,1)-covector annihilates T1,0M. Thus the differential forms of type (p, q) are the smooth sections of
Λp,qT∗M=Λp(T1,0M)∗⊗Λq(T0,1M)∗,ΛrTC∗​M=⨁p+q=r​Λp,qT∗M.
(2)
Here the tensor product is identified with its image under the wedge product of differential forms. For a real tangent vector v, the vectors v−iJv and v+iJv exhibit the two types directly.
We now construct the almost complex structure from a decomposable volume form. Locally write Ω=θ1​∧⋯∧θn​. The nonvanishing of Ω∧Ω says that the 2n covectors θ1​,…,θn​,θ1​,…,θn​ form a complex coframe. In particular, the span of the θj​ has the intrinsic description
Wx​={α∈Tx∗​M⊗C:α∧Ωx​=0}.
(3)
Indeed, expansion in that coframe shows that every coefficient of a conjugate factor must vanish if α∧Ωx​=0. Therefore this span is independent of the chosen local factorization. The local coframes show that W is a smooth rank-n vector subbundle, and
T∗M⊗C=W⊕W.
(4)
Define JΩ∗​ to be multiplication by i on W and by −i on W. It commutes with complex conjugation, so is the complexification of a real smooth bundle endomorphism, whose dual defines JΩ​ on TM. It has square −I, and its (1,0)-covectors are exactly W. Conversely, any such almost complex structure must have this same action on the entire coframe. Thus JΩ​ exists globally and is unique, and every factor in every allowed decomposition is a (1,0)-form.
Finally suppose dΩ=0. Since θj​∧Ω=0, the exterior derivative product rule gives
0=d(θj​∧Ω)=dθj​∧Ω−θj​∧dΩ=dθj​∧Ω.
(5)
Decompose dθj​ into types (2,0), (1,1) and (0,2) using JΩ​. The first two wedge products with the (n,0)-form Ω vanish by dimension. On (0,2)-forms, wedging with Ω is injective: the monomials Ω∧θa​∧θb​ are linearly independent. For n=1 the (0,2) space is zero, so the statement still holds. Hence every dθj​ has no (0,2) component. For an arbitrary (1,0)-form α=∑j​aj​θj​, the extra terms daj​∧θj​ likewise have only types (2,0) and (1,1). The bracket and differential-form criteria for integrability proved below now give
dΩ=0 ⟹ JΩ​ is integrable.​
(6)
Local decomposability has been used essentially; the nonvanishing top-degree product alone does not provide the required coframe.

These are local criteria: multiplying by a smooth bump function extends any local test field or form to a global one without changing it near a chosen point. Assume the Lie bracket of any two sections of T1,0M again lies in T1,0M. Because the almost complex structure is real, conjugating this condition shows that the Lie bracket of any two sections U,V of T0,1M lies in T0,1M.
For a (1,0)-form α, the exterior derivative formula is
dα(U,V)=U(α(V))−V(α(U))−α([U,V]).
(7)
The first two terms vanish because α annihilates T0,1M, and the last vanishes by bracket closure. The (0,2) component is exactly the restriction of dα to two arguments in T0,1M. Thus it is zero. Since the only types of a two-form are (2,0), (1,1) and (0,2), the bracket condition implies the stated differential-form condition. This argument uses neither holomorphic coordinates nor an integrability theorem as an unproved intermediate step.

Conversely, suppose the exterior derivative of every (1,0)-form has no (0,2) part. For sections U,V of T0,1M, the same exterior derivative formula gives
0=dα(U,V)=−α([U,V])for every (1,0)-form α.

On a complex manifold, holomorphic coordinates give the decomposition d=∂+∂ˉ, with
∂:Ap,q→Ap+1,q,∂ˉ:Ap,q→Ap,q+1.
(9)
For α=∑I,J​aIJˉ​dzI∧dzˉJ, the conjugate Dolbeault operator ∂ and the Dolbeault operator ∂ˉ are explicitly
∂α=∑I,J,k​∂zk∂aIJˉ​​dzk∧dzI∧dzˉJ,∂ˉα=∑I,J,k​∂zˉk∂aIJˉ​​dzˉk∧dzI∧dzˉJ.
(10)
These are the type components of the intrinsic exterior derivative, so are independent of coordinates. The identity d2=0, separated by type, gives ∂2=∂ˉ2=0 and ∂∂ˉ+∂ˉ∂=0. A holomorphic p-form on a complex manifold is a (p,0)-form whose coefficients are holomorphic functions; equivalently it is a (p,0)-form killed by the Dolbeault operator.
A rank-r holomorphic vector bundle has local trivializations E∣Ui​​≅Ui​×Cr whose changes are (x,v)↦(x,gij​(x)v), with gij​:Ui​∩Uj​→GLr​(C) holomorphic. These transition functions of a vector bundle satisfy gii​=I and gij​gjk​=gik​. Equivalently, for local frames we use the convention ej​=ei​gij​. A holomorphic section is a section having holomorphic coordinate functions in every such holomorphic local frame; the condition is unchanged by the holomorphic invertible transitions.
The holomorphic cotangent bundle has local frames dz1,…,dzn. Under another holomorphic coordinate system w, the chain rule gives
dwa=∑b​∂zb∂wa​dzb.
(11)
The Jacobian matrix is holomorphic and invertible, with holomorphic inverse supplied by the inverse coordinate change. These are precisely holomorphic bundle transitions. A section written as ∑b​fb​(z)dzb is holomorphic exactly when every fb​ is holomorphic, so
H0(X,ΩX1​)={α∈A1,0(X):∂ˉα=0},​
(12)
the space of holomorphic one-forms. In particular, being a holomorphic section is not the same as being a d-closed one-form on an arbitrary complex manifold.
The complex tautological line bundle is
O(−1)={([Z],v)∈CPn×Cn+1:v∈CZ}.
(13)
On Ui​={Zi​=0} use the nowhere-zero frame si​([Z])=Z/Zi​. Its components are holomorphic affine coordinates and the constant one in position i. On overlaps
sj​=Zj​Zi​​si​,
(14)
so the frame changes are nowhere-zero holomorphic functions. This proves that O(−1) is a holomorphic line bundle, and its inclusion into the trivial bundle is a holomorphic bundle map.
A global holomorphic section composed with that inclusion has n+1 component holomorphic functions on compact connected Complex projective space. Each is constant: its absolute value attains a maximum; in a coordinate ball the one-variable maximum modulus principle along complex lines makes it locally constant, and connectedness propagates that constant globally. Thus the section is a fixed vector v∈Cn+1 lying in every fibre, namely every line CZ. For n≥1 the intersection of all these lines is zero. Hence
H0(CPn,O(−1))=0(n≥1).​
(15)
The positive-dimension convention is necessary: CP0 is one point and this section space is C.

An irreducible analytic subvariety of a complex manifold is a closed subset locally defined by finitely many holomorphic functions which cannot be expressed as the union of two proper closed analytic subsets. In a complex n-manifold, codimension k means pure complex dimension n−k. A divisor on a complex manifold is a locally finite formal integer combination
D=∑ν​mν​Yν​
(16)
of irreducible analytic hypersurfaces. Compactness makes the set of components in this sum finite. Negative coefficients are permitted; a local equation for such a divisor on a complex manifold is generally meromorphic rather than holomorphic.
We use these precise local facts. In holomorphic coordinates at x, the local ring is OX,x​≅C{z1​,…,zn​}, a Noetherian regular local ring and a unique factorization domain. Each analytic hypersurface germ has finitely many irreducible local branches; their prime ideals have height one and are generated by irreducible holomorphic germs. A holomorphic germ is a unit exactly when its value at x is nonzero. Consequently a nonzero meromorphic germ with order zero on every local hypersurface branch is a holomorphic unit, by cancelling its irreducible factors in numerator and denominator. These are the auxiliary local-ring properties required below.
After shrinking a neighbourhood Ui​ around each point, choose local branch equations hiνa​, and put
fi​=∏ν,a​hiνamν​​.
(17)
Its zero and pole orders give exactly the local divisor on a complex manifold D∣Ui​​. A globally irreducible hypersurface may have several branches locally; all of them must be included with the indicated multiplicity. On an overlap, fi​/fj​ has zero order along every branch, so the local-ring facts show that it extends to a nowhere-zero holomorphic function. These germwise extensions patch uniquely to a holomorphic unit on the overlap. This describes a local defining function of D and proves why multiplicities, not just zero sets, matter.
Set gij​=fi​/fj​ and glue line-bundle frames by ej​=gij​ei​. The identities gij​gjk​=gik​ give a holomorphic line bundle associated to a divisor, denoted [D]. Equivalently, the frame ei​ can be represented by 1/fi​ in the sheaf of meromorphic functions, so [D]=OX​(D). If alternative equations are fi′​=ui​fi​, where ui​ are holomorphic units, the corresponding frames are identified by ei′​↦ui−1​ei​. This respects the changed transitions. Refining the cover gives the same identifications, proving that [D] is well-defined up to holomorphic bundle isomorphism. The expressions fi​ei​ glue to its canonical meromorphic section, whose divisor is D.
For two divisors on a complex manifold use a common cover with equations fi​ and hi​. Their sum has equation fi​hi​, and the tensor product of vector bundles has frame transitions
fj​fi​​hj​hi​​=fj​hj​fi​hi​​.
(18)
Sending the sum-divisor frame to the tensor product of the two frames gives
[D1​+D2​]≅[D1​]⊗[D2​].​
(19)
This also yields [−D]≅[D]∗.
For a nonsingular complex analytic hypersurface Y, choose reduced local equations fi​ with dfi​ nonzero along Y. The holomorphic conormal bundle is
NY/X∗​=ker(ΩX1​∣Y​⟶ΩY1​),
(20)
whose fibre is the annihilator of T1,0Y. It has local frame dfi​∣Y​. Here this restriction is as a covector in the ambient cotangent bundle along Y, not its pullback to ΩY1​, where it would vanish. The line bundle [−Y] has local frame ai​=fi​, and if fj​=uji​fi​, then
aj​=uji​ai​,dfj​∣Y​=uji​∣Y​dfi​∣Y​,
(21)
since fi​duji​ vanishes along Y. The frame identifications ai​∣Y​↦dfi​∣Y​ therefore patch and are fibrewise nonzero. This proves the conormal line of a smooth analytic hypersurface formula
[−Y]∣Y​≅NY/X∗​.​
(22)
Equivalently it identifies OX​(−Y)∣Y​=IY​/IY2​ with the analytic conormal sheaf.

Choose holomorphic coordinates centered at the point p and a polydisc U⊂Cn about the origin. The local blowup of a complex manifold at a point is the incidence manifold
U={(z,[ℓ])∈U×CPn−1:zi​ℓj​=zj​ℓi​ for all i,j},σ(z,[ℓ])=z.
(23)
Away from z=0 the direction is forced to be [ℓ]=[z], giving a biholomorphism with U∖{0}. Glue this punctured part to X∖{p}. The charts below make the result a complex manifold. The projection is proper near p, since it is the restriction of projection from a closed subset of U×CPn−1; globally the modification is proper and is an isomorphism off p. Its exceptional divisor is
E=σ−1(p)≅P(Tp​X)≅CPn−1.
(24)
On ℓi​=0, let t=zi​ and uj​=ℓj​/ℓi​ for j=i. Then
zi​=t,zj​=tuj​ (j=i).
(25)
These are coordinates on the open domain where t and all tuj​ lie in the original polydisc; at t=0 all finite uj​ are allowed. Their inverse reads the direction ratios and zi​ from the incidence point. In this chart E is the nonsingular hypersurface t=0. On overlap with chart k=i, the holomorphic coordinate changes are
t′=tuk​,ui′​=1/uk​,uj′​=uj​/uk​(j=i,k).
(26)
They are holomorphic with holomorphic inverses where uk​=0, and the n charts cover every point of E. Intrinsically, changing the original coordinates by F lifts via [ℓ]↦[A(z)ℓ], where F(z)=A(z)z, A(0)=dF0​, and A is holomorphic and invertible near zero. One can take A(z)=∫01​dFsz​ds on a sufficiently small polydisc. Away from the origin the lift is uniquely forced; this proves coordinate-independence of the gluing near E.
The canonical bundle is KX​=ΛnΩX1​. In the ith blowup chart the top-form Jacobian is
σ∗(dz1​∧⋯∧dzn​)=(−1)i−1tn−1dt∧⋀j=i​duj​,
(27)
where the uj​ occur in increasing order. Every term with a second dt vanishes in the wedge product of differential forms. Thus the differential gives a map σ∗KX​→KX​ vanishing to order n−1 along E and nowhere else. To express this through the supplied meromorphic-section hypothesis, take a nonzero meromorphic section s of KX​ and put D=div(s). Locally s=f(z)dz1​∧⋯∧dzn​, so
div(σ∗s)=σ∗D+(n−1)E.
(28)
Here σ∗D is the total divisor pullback, including any additional exceptional multiplicity from f; it is not merely the strict transform. A line bundle with a nonzero meromorphic section is isomorphic to the line bundle of that section's divisor, as its local coefficient ratios are its frame transitions. Pullback of divisor line bundles and additivity of analytic divisor line bundles therefore give the canonical bundle formula for a point blowup:
KX​≅σ∗KX​⊗OX​((n−1)E).​
(29)
For n=1 the blowup is already an isomorphism, consistently with the zero exponent.
For the projective-plane construction, an invertible linear change of homogeneous coordinates puts x=[1:0:0]. Lines through x are parametrized by [a:b]∈CP1, with equation bZ1​−aZ2​=0. Thus the required map on the punctured plane is
f([Z0​:Z1​:Z2​])=[Z1​:Z2​].
(30)
It is well-defined under rescaling and holomorphic on the covering sets Z1​=0 and Z2​=0, where its target coordinates are Z2​/Z1​ and Z1​/Z2​. These sets cover the complement of x.
The graph closure is
S={([Z0​:Z1​:Z2​],[a:b])∈CP2×CP1:Z1​b=Z2​a}.
(31)
Over the complement of x it is the graph of f. In the affine chart Z0​=0, its equation is exactly the two-dimensional local incidence model for the blowup of a complex manifold at a point, so its first projection identifies it with the required blowup globally. The second projection is a holomorphic map f​:S→CP1 extending f∘σ. Explicitly, the two blowup charts give
(z1​,z2​)=(t,tu): f​=[1:u],(z1​,z2​)=(tv,t): f​=[v:1].
(32)
These formulas agree when v=1/u and remain holomorphic at t=0. Hence the pencil extends over the exceptional divisor, where it records the projective tangent direction. This is the projective pencil resolved by a point blowup.

Let n=dimC​X, and use the complex orientation. Write g for the real Riemannian metric underlying the Hermitian metric. Its fundamental form of a Hermitian manifold is
ω(u,v)=g(Ju,v),ω=2i​∑a,b​habˉ​dza∧dzˉb,
(33)
where g=Re(∑habˉ​dza⊗dzˉb) in this convention. It is real and of type (1,1), since g(Ju,Jv)=g(u,v). Choose an adapted real orthonormal frame Ej​,Fj​=JEj​ and its dual coframe ej,fj. Then ω=∑j​ej∧fj, giving the Riemannian volume form
volg​=e1∧f1∧⋯∧en∧fn=n!ωn​.​
(34)
We use the complex-linear Hodge star operator, the extension of the real metric star characterized on equal-degree forms by
α∧∗β​=⟨α,β⟩g​volg​.
(35)
It maps (p,q)-forms to (n−q,n−p)-forms and satisfies ∗∗α=(−1)p+qα. It is distinct from the conjugate-linear map α↦∗α. In the adapted coframe the star of ej∧fj is the wedge product of all the other two-dimensional factors, with positive sign. Summing proves the Hodge star of the fundamental Hermitian form formula
∗ω=(n−1)!ωn−1​.​
(36)
This computation uses no closedness assumption on ω.
Use the integrated Hermitian inner product (α,β)=∫X​α∧∗β​. The formal adjoints d∗ and ∂ˉ∗ are defined by (dα,β)=(α,d∗β) and (∂ˉα,β)=(α,∂ˉ∗β); compact manifolds here have no boundary. The Hodge Laplacian and Dolbeault Laplacian are
Δd​=dd∗+d∗d,Δ∂ˉ​=∂ˉ∂ˉ∗+∂ˉ∗∂ˉ.
(37)
The Dolbeault Hodge decomposition on a compact Hermitian manifold states that
Ap,q(X)=H∂ˉp,q​⊕∂ˉAp,q−1(X)⊕∂ˉ∗Ap,q+1(X)
(38)
is an orthogonal direct sum, with finite-dimensional H∂ˉp,q​=kerΔ∂ˉ​. Each Dolbeault cohomology class
H∂ˉp,q​(X)=ker(∂ˉ:Ap,q→Ap,q+1)/∂ˉAp,q−1
(39)
has a unique representative in H∂ˉp,q​. A form is harmonic exactly when both ∂ˉα and ∂ˉ∗α vanish, since its Laplacian inner product is the sum of their squared norms.
Now let Lα=ω∧α be the Hermitian Lefschetz operator and let Λ be its pointwise adjoint. For β of total degree r=p+q and α of degree r−2, the defining inner products give
(Lα)∧∗β​=α∧ω∧∗β​=α∧∗Λβ​.
(40)
The wedge pairing is nondegenerate, so ∗Λβ​=L∗β​. Apply another star to the degree-(r−2) form and use that the star and L commute with conjugation. This yields
Λβ=(−1)r−2∗L∗β=(−1)p+q∗L∗β.​
(41)
For degrees below two both sides vanish. The argument also identifies its bidegree as (−1,−1) and does not require the Kähler condition.
Assume now that dω=0, so the metric is a Kähler metric. Start with the supplied Kähler identities relation [Λ,∂]=i∂ˉ∗. Since Λ is real, conjugation gives [Λ,∂ˉ]=−i∂∗, hence
∂ˉ∗=−i[Λ,∂],∂∗=i[Λ,∂ˉ].
(42)
The commutators are ordinary commutators because Λ has even degree. Using ∂2=0 we obtain, by direct expansion,
∂∂ˉ∗+∂ˉ∗∂=−i(∂Λ∂−∂2Λ+Λ∂2−∂Λ∂)=0.
(43)
Conjugation gives ∂ˉ∂∗+∂∗∂ˉ=0 as well. Expanding the two unmixed Laplacians and using ∂∂ˉ=−∂ˉ∂ gives the same operator:
Δ∂​​=i(∂Λ∂ˉ−∂ˉΛ∂−∂∂ˉΛ−Λ∂∂ˉ)=Δ∂ˉ​.​
(44)
Finally expand d=∂+∂ˉ and d∗=∂∗+∂ˉ∗; the mixed terms just proved zero disappear. This derives the Kähler Laplacian identity
Δd​=Δ∂​+Δ∂ˉ​=2Δ∂ˉ​.​
(45)
The Dolbeault Laplacian preserves bidegree. Thus the complex d-harmonic forms of total degree r split into their harmonic (p,q) parts. The allowed real Hodge theorem, followed by complexification, gives
br(X)=∑p+q=r​hp,q,hp,q=dimC​H∂ˉp,q​.
(46)
The real operator Δd​ commutes with conjugation, which exchanges (p,q) and (q,p). Therefore hp,q=hq,p. For odd r, no summand has p=q, so they pair off in equal dimensions. This proves that all odd Betti numbers are even.
For 1≤k≤n, the Kähler form power ωk is closed. If it were exact, say ωk=dη, then
∫X​ωn=∫X​d(η∧ωn−k)=0
(47)
by Stokes theorem. But ωn=n!volg​ has strictly positive integral on the nonempty compact manifold. Hence the powers of a compact Kähler form have nonzero cohomology classes, and
b2k−1(X)∈2Z≥0​,b2k(X)≥1(1≤k≤n).​
(48)
For an example take the Hopf surface
H=(C2∖{0})/⟨z↦2z⟩.
(49)
The dilation action is free and properly discontinuous: on a compact subset the radii are bounded above and away from zero, so only finitely many dilations can meet it. It is holomorphic, giving a complex manifold quotient; the annulus 1≤∥z∥≤2 covers the quotient, proving compactness. The map
[z]⟼(z/∥z∥, log∥z∥modlog2)
(50)
is a diffeomorphism H≅S3×S1. The Künneth theorem gives b1(H)=1. This violates the necessary odd-degree parity, so this compact complex surface admits no Kähler metric.
