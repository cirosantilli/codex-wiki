Assume X is nonempty, as is necessary for a probability measure to exist. Give its isometry group IX​ the uniform metric
D(g,h)=supx∈X​d(gx,hx).
(1)
Every distance-preserving self-map of a compact metric space is onto. Indeed, if x0​∈/gX, then δ=d(x0​,gX)>0, and for n>m the iterates satisfy d(gmx0​,gnx0​)=d(x0​,gn−mx0​)≥δ. This infinite separated set contradicts compactness. Consequently IX​ is a group under composition, with the identity and inverses again isometries.
The metric D is invariant under both left and right multiplication: left invariance uses preservation of distances, and right invariance uses surjectivity. Inversion is an isometry for D, and D(gh,g′h′)≤D(g,g′)+D(h,h′) proves continuity of multiplication. Moreover, IX​ is compact. A sequence of isometries has a subsequence converging at every point of a countable dense subset of X, by a diagonal argument. Their common Lipschitz constant 1 and finite epsilon-nets of X make this subsequence uniformly Cauchy. Its uniform limit preserves distances and is onto by the preceding argument. Thus IX​ is a compact metrizable topological group. A transitive group action means that for every x,y∈X some g∈IX​ has gx=y.
We construct the invariant Borel probability measure directly. The Hall marriage theorem says that a finite bipartite graph with equal-sized vertex classes A,B has a perfect matching precisely when every subset S⊆A has at least ∣S∣ neighbours in B. Its following consequence is useful. If A,B are ε-separated subsets of maximum cardinality in a compact metric space, join points whose distance is less than ε. If some S⊆A had fewer than ∣S∣ neighbours, replacing those neighbours in B by S would produce a larger ε-separated set. This contradicts maximal cardinality. Hence there is a bijection between A and B moving each point by less than ε.
Choose εn​↓0 and maximum-cardinality εn​-separated sets An​⊂IX​. Such sets exist: a finite covering by balls of radius less than εn​/2 bounds every separated set's cardinality. Fix x0​∈X and define the empirical probability measures
μn​=∣An​∣1​∑a∈An​​δax0​​.
(2)
For f∈C(X) let ωf​(r)=supd(x,y)≤r​∣f(x)−f(y)∣, which tends to zero with r by uniform continuity. For every h∈IX​, left invariance of D makes hAn​ another maximum-cardinality separated set. Match it to An​ as above. Then
​∫fdμn​−∫f(hx)dμn​(x)​≤ωf​(εn​).
(3)
The allowed compactness of probability measures on a compact metric space supplies a subsequence converging to μ in the weak-star topology. Passing to that limit for each fixed h,f gives ∫fdμ=∫f∘hdμ. The same subsequence works for every h,f, since the displayed estimate held for all of them before taking the limit.
For uniqueness, put Fn​(x)=∣An​∣−1∑a∈An​​f(ax). For x=hx0​, right invariance of D lets us match An​h with An​. Transitivity therefore gives the uniform estimate ∣Fn​(x)−Fn​(x0​)∣≤ωf​(εn​) for every x∈X. If ν is any invariant Borel probability measure, then
∫fdν=∫Fn​dν,​∫fdν−Fn​(x0​)​≤ωf​(εn​).
(4)
Two invariant probability measures consequently differ on the integral of f by at most 2ωf​(εn​), hence agree on every continuous function. Continuous functions determine Borel probability measures on a compact metric space, so the invariant probability measure exists and is unique.

B2d​⊆K⊆d​B2d​,∥x∥E​≤∣x∣≤d​∥x∥E​​.

∑j​cj​uj​⊗uj​=I,∑j​cj​=d,∣⟨uj​,x⟩∣≤∥x∥E​.

The last inequality follows because the supporting hyperplane of K at uj​ must also support the contained Euclidean unit ball, hence has normal uj​. For completeness, the decomposition follows from maximal volume as follows. If I were not in the convex cone generated by u⊗u at contact points, finite-dimensional separation would give a symmetric matrix A with trA>0 and ⟨Au,u⟩≤0 at every contact. Subtract δI for small δ>0, retaining positive trace and obtaining strict negative values. For small t>0, the support function ∣(I+t(A−δI))v∣ is less than 1 near all contact directions. On the compact complement of those neighbourhoods, hK​(v)−1 has a positive minimum, so the perturbed ellipsoid remains inside K there too. The cone is closed: its generating contact matrices form a compact set of trace 1, so bounded traces bound their nonnegative coefficient sums. For a d-dimensional normed vector space, with unit ball K and John ellipsoid E, its volume ratio is
vr(E)=(volEvolK​)1/d.
(21)
It is at least 1 and invariant under invertible linear coordinate changes.
For ℓ1n​, the unit ball has volume 2n/n!: its intersection with each orthant is the standard simplex of volume 1/n!. Its John ellipsoid is n−1/2B2n​. To verify maximum volume, let AB2n​ be any centered inscribed ellipsoid. For each sign vector ε∈{−1,1}n, containment gives ∣ATε∣≤1. Averaging over independent Rademacher random variables yields tr(AAT)≤1. The arithmetic-geometric mean inequality for its squared singular values gives ∣detA∣≤n−n/2. The scalar matrix n−1/2I attains this bound because ∥x∥1​≤n​∣x∣. Centered ellipsoids suffice: if a+AB2n​ is contained in the symmetric convex unit ball, averaging it with −a+AB2n​ gives the centered copy with the same volume.
Using the stated Euclidean ball volume and n=2k,
vr(ℓ12k​)2k=(2k)!πk22k(2k)kk!​≤(π8​)k,
(22)
since (2k)!/k!=∏j=k+12k​j≥kk. Therefore vr(ℓ12k​)≤8/π​ uniformly in k.
The subsequent assertion for an arbitrary 2k-dimensional space is false as printed. Here is a counterexample independent of any asymptotic section theorem. Take E=ℓ∞2k​. Its John ellipsoid is B22k​: an inscribed AB22k​ has every row of A of Euclidean length at most 1, so the Hadamard inequality gives ∣detA∣≤1, attained by A=I. Let F be any k-dimensional subspace and f1​,…,fk​ an orthonormal basis of it. The random unit vector
x=k​1​∑j=1k​εj​fj​
(23)
has ∣x∣=1. Since ∑j​fj​(i)2≤1 for each coordinate i, the moment-generating function bound for independent Rademacher random variables gives
Eetxi​≤et2/(2k),P(∥x∥∞​>r)≤4ke−kr2/2.
(24)
Choose r=2log(8k)/k​; the latter probability is at most 1/2, so some unit vector in F has ∥x∥∞​≤r. The proposed section inequality would therefore force
L≥2log(8k)k​​⟶∞​.
(25)
Thus even one half-dimensional section cannot have the printed universal constant.
The valid orthogonal splitting with bounded volume ratio is that L may depend on V=vr(E), and L=32V2 suffices. This supplies a dimension-independent constant for the preceding ℓ12k​ case, or whenever V is bounded uniformly. We prove the complete qualified assertion, including its epsilon-net ingredient.
In John position, ∥x∥E​≤∣x∣. Polar integration of the radial function ρ(u)=1/∥u∥E​ gives
V2k=volB22k​volK​=∫S2k−1​∥u∥E−2k​dσ(u),σ{∥u∥E​≤r}≤(Vr)2k,
(26)
where σ is normalized spherical measure.
A maximal ε-separated subset of Sk−1 is an epsilon-net. Balls of radius ε/2 about its points have disjoint interiors and lie in the ball of radius 1+ε/2. Comparing volumes bounds its cardinality by (1+2/ε)k. Finite existence follows from this packing bound, and maximality proves the net property without assuming a separate covering theorem.
Choose two orthogonal k-dimensional subspaces F0​,F0⊥​ and one such net on each unit sphere. Rotate both by a common uniformly distributed orthogonal matrix U. This distribution can be obtained from Question 1 applied to the compact orthogonal group with its Frobenius metric; left and right multiplication are transitive isometries. Consequently Uv is uniformly distributed on S2k−1 for every fixed unit vector v. The union bound shows that the probability that some rotated net point has norm less than 2ε is at most
2(1+2/ε)k(2Vε)2k=2[4V2(ε2+2ε)]k.
(27)
Set ε=(32V2)−1. Because V≥1, the bracket is at most 1/4+1/256, and twice its kth power is less than 1 for every k≥1. Some rotation therefore makes every point of both nets have norm at least 2ε.
The triangle inequality and ∥x∥E​≤∣x∣ imply that ∥⋅∥E​ is 1-Lipschitz for the Euclidean metric. Every unit vector of UF0​ or UF0⊥​ is within ε of a good net point and thus has norm at least ε. By homogeneity, on both orthogonal subspaces,
∥x∥E​≤∣x∣≤32vr(E)2∥x∥E​​.
(28)

Use the Lipschitz constant convention ∥f∥L​=supx=y​∣f(x)−f(y)∣/d(x,y) in the final quantity; it is a seminorm, with constants having value zero. Also interpret the coupling space as P(X×X). The printed P(X,Y) has an undefined Y. The third quantity has a duplicated printed label; it is placed in part iii here, with the fourth in part iv.
First prove weak optimal transport duality. For any feasible continuous pair f,g and any coupling of probability distributions π with marginals P,Q,
∫fdP+∫gdQ=∫X×X​(f(x)+g(y))dπ(x,y)≤∫X×X​d(x,y)dπ(x,y).
(29)
Taking the supremum over pairs and infimum over couplings gives md​(P,Q)≤W(P,Q). These quantities are finite: P⊗Q is a coupling and the continuous cost d is bounded on the compact metric space X×X. The zero pair is feasible, so 0≤md​≤W≤diamX.
The reverse inequality is established in part ii by finite approximation and a proof of the required finite linear programming duality. Parts iii and iv then show that all the dual formulations agree.

Fix ε>0 and a finite epsilon-net x1​,…,xN​ in X. Choose a Borel map r:X→{x1​,…,xN​} with d(x,r(x))≤ε, for example the nearest point with ties broken by index. Its cells Ai​ give discrete probability measures with masses pi​=P(Ai​) and qi​=Q(Ai​). Let
Wε​=min{∑i,j​d(xi​,xj​)πij​:πij​≥0, ∑j​πij​=pi​, ∑i​πij​=qj​}.
(30)
The feasible set is nonempty and compact, so the minimum exists. Pushing any coupling forward by (r,r) changes its cost by at most 2ε, hence Wε​≤W+2ε. Conversely, lift a discrete coupling by the measure
∑i,j:pi​qj​>0​πij​pi​P∣Ai​​​⊗qj​Q∣Aj​​​.
(31)
Terms with zero row or column masses vanish. This lift has marginals P,Q, and its cost differs by at most 2ε. Therefore
∣W−Wε​∣≤2ε.
(32)
Here is the finite linear programming duality proof. Write dij​=d(xi​,xj​) and consider the convex cone
C={((∑j​πij​)i​,(∑i​πij​)j​,∑i,j​dij​πij​+s):πij​≥0, s≥0}.
(33)
It is closed and finitely generated. One elementary justification of closedness is to reduce each nonnegative representation to linearly independent generators, eliminating a coefficient along any linear dependence; after choosing a subsequence using the same subset of generators, their independent coordinates have bounded, convergent coefficients whenever the represented vectors converge. For η>0, the point (p,q,Wε​−η) lies outside C. Separation from this closed convex cone gives coefficients (a,b,τ) with
τ≥0,ai​+bj​+τdij​≥0,∑i​ai​pi​+∑j​bj​qj​+τ(Wε​−η)<0.
(34)
If τ=0, summing the middle inequalities against any feasible π contradicts the last one. Thus τ>0, and fi​=−ai​/τ, gj​=−bj​/τ obey fi​+gj​≤dij​ with dual objective greater than Wε​−η. The weak inequality already proved gives the reverse bound. Letting η↓0 proves finite strong duality.
Reduce this discrete dual to a single Lipschitz function. For a feasible pair put ϕi​=minj​(dij​−gj​). The triangle inequality gives ∣ϕi​−ϕl​∣≤dil​, while fi​≤ϕi​ and gi​≤−ϕi​. Consequently
∑i​pi​fi​+∑i​qi​gi​≤∑i​(pi​−qi​)ϕi​.
(35)
Conversely, any 1-Lipschitz vector ϕ gives a feasible pair (ϕ,−ϕ). Adding a constant leaves its objective unchanged, so impose ϕ1​=0. The resulting feasible set is compact, and its maximum is Wε​.
For a maximizing vector, the McShane extension theorem has the following explicit proof in this situation. Define
ϕ(x)=mini​{ϕi​+d(x,xi​)}.
(36)
The triangle inequality proves that this extension is 1-Lipschitz, and the discrete Lipschitz condition makes ϕ(xi​)=ϕi​. Thus (ϕ,−ϕ) is a continuous feasible pair. Because ∣ϕ(x)−ϕ(r(x))∣≤ε,
md​≥∫ϕdP−∫ϕdQ≥Wε​−2ε≥W−4ε.
(37)
Letting ε↓0, together with part i, proves md​(P,Q)=W(P,Q). The infimum defining W is also attained: the set of couplings is a closed subset of the compact space of probability measures on X×X, and integrating the continuous cost is continuous for the weak-star topology.

Since Lipschitz functions are continuous, the feasible class here is smaller, giving mL​≤md​. To prove the reverse inequality, take any continuous feasible pair f,g and perform Lipschitz regularization of transport potentials:
h(x)=miny∈X​{d(x,y)−g(y)}.
(38)
The minimum exists by compactness. For every x,x′ the triangle inequality gives h(x)≤d(x,x′)+h(x′); interchanging x,x′ proves ∣h(x)−h(x′)∣≤d(x,x′). Thus h has Lipschitz constant at most 1.
The original constraint yields f(x)≤h(x), and evaluating the minimum at y=x gives h(x)≤−g(x). Moreover, h(x)−h(y)≤d(x,y), so (h,−h) is a feasible Lipschitz pair with
∫fdP+∫gdQ≤∫hdP−∫hdQ≤mL​.
(39)
Taking the supremum over the original continuous pairs proves mL​(P,Q)=md​(P,Q)=W(P,Q). Notice that this is an exact regularization, with no density or approximation assumption about the initial pair.

Every function h with Lipschitz constant at most 1 yields a feasible pair (h,−h), and replacing h by −h reverses the objective. Hence
γ(P,Q)=sup∥h∥L​≤1​(∫hdP−∫hdQ)≤mL​(P,Q).
(40)
Conversely, the Lipschitz regularization of transport potentials in part iii dominates every feasible pair by (h,−h) with ∥h∥L​≤1, so mL​≤γ. Together with the preceding parts this proves the Kantorovich–Rubinstein theorem here in all four requested forms:
md​(P,Q)=W(P,Q)=mL​(P,Q)=γ(P,Q)​.
(41)
The seminorm convention is essential. If instead ∥h∥L​ meant ∥h∥∞​+Lip(h), the equality would fail: on a two-point space at distance 2, with point masses at the two different points, W=2 whereas the latter unit-ball supremum is 1. Indeed, an oscillation a needs supremum norm at least a/2 and Lipschitz constant a/2, so the sum norm bounds a by 1, attained by values −1/2,1/2. This distinguishes the Lipschitz seminorm used above from a full bounded-Lipschitz norm.
