Use opposite boundary-basis orientations on the two sides, so that a determinant-one coefficient matrix describes an orientation-reversing gluing map. Work in the smooth or piecewise-linear category; embedded spheres and disks are locally flat. A closed connected orientable three-manifold is an irreducible three-manifold when every embedded S2 bounds an embedded three-ball. It is a prime three-manifold when M≅P#Q forces P≅S3 or Q≅S3. A separating sphere gives such a connected sum after capping its two sides with balls. Irreducibility therefore implies primeness, but a prime manifold can still have a nonseparating sphere.
A geometrizable three-manifold is one whose prime summands, after sphere splitting and capping, can be cut along disjoint essential tori into pieces whose interiors admit complete geometric structures modeled on the eight Thurston geometries:
S3,R3,H3,S2×R,H2×R,SL2​(R),Nil,Sol.
(12)
Here a geometric structure is a locally homogeneous quotient structure modeled on the stated simply connected space. The original manifold need not itself carry one geometry before decomposition.
A Heegaard splitting is M=H1​∪ϕ​H2​, where H1​,H2​ are handlebodies with their boundaries identified. Its genus is the common boundary genus. The Heegaard genus gH​(M) is the least possible genus of such a splitting. Thus “genus g” as a minimum requires both a genus-g splitting and the nonexistence of one of smaller genus; an arbitrary genus-g splitting only proves gH​(M)≤g.

The sphere {p}×S2 in S1×S2 is nonseparating: its complement is (S1∖{p})×S2, which is connected. A sphere bounding a ball separates, so this product is not an irreducible three-manifold.
To show it is prime, suppose a separating sphere cuts it into compact pieces V,W with one spherical boundary each. The Seifert-van Kampen theorem gives
Z≅π1​(S1×S2)≅π1​(V)∗π1​(W).
(13)
A free product of two nontrivial groups is nonabelian: choose nonidentity elements in the two factors; their products in the two orders are distinct reduced words. Hence one factor, say π1​(V), is trivial. The inclusion of this simply connected compact piece lifts to the universal cover R×S2≅R3∖{0}. The lift is an embedding: equality of lifted points would imply equality of their projections in V. Its image V is compact with boundary the lifted sphere.
Use the three-dimensional Schoenflies theorem: a locally flat embedded sphere in R3 bounds a ball, and its compact side is that ball. The interior of V is both open and closed in the corresponding complement component, and compactness rules out the unbounded component. Thus V is the bounded ball, and V itself is a ball. Capping V gives S3, so every connected-sum decomposition is trivial. Therefore S1×S2 is prime but not irreducible.
Now let a prime closed connected orientable M fail irreducibility. A sphere not bounding a ball cannot separate: if it did, primeness would make one capped side S3, and the three-dimensional Schoenflies theorem would make its uncapped side a ball. Thus there is a nonseparating sphere S. Its two-sided neighborhood is S2×[−1,1]. Since the complement is connected, join its two boundary spheres by an embedded arc in that complement and thicken the arc. The union is a punctured S2×S1: the thickened arc joins the two ends of the sphere-cylinder, and the remaining boundary is one sphere. This is the nonseparating sphere gives a sphere-circle summand construction and yields
M≅(S1×S2)#N.
(14)
The product is not S3, since its fundamental group is Z. Primeness forces N≅S3, so
M≅S1×S2.​
(15)
The background results used are van Kampen, the explicit universal cover of the product, and the locally flat three-dimensional Schoenflies theorem; no Poincaré-conjecture argument is needed.

Let Dα​,Dβ​ be the disjoint complete meridian-disk system of H1​, and let Dγ​,Dδ​ be that of H2​. The identification ϕ(α)=γ joins Dα​ and Dγ​ into an embedded sphere S in M. Each disk is nonseparating in its handlebody: cutting along it leaves a genus-one handlebody, and cutting along the other then gives a ball. After cutting M along S, the two cut handlebodies are connected and are still joined along the complement of the common boundary circle. Hence S is nonseparating.
Cap the two new sphere boundaries with balls. The resulting closed manifold N inherits a genus-one Heegaard splitting: its two sides are the solid tori obtained by cutting the original handlebodies along their common disks. Their meridians are the surviving curves β and δ. The hypothesis that ϕ(β) avoids γ makes it descend to the compressed torus, and its single intersection with δ remains a single intersection there.
A genus-one splitting whose two meridians intersect once is S3. To see this directly, choose the target torus basis (μ,λ) with μ its meridian. The other meridian is aμ±λ. A meridional Dehn twist, extending across the target solid torus, removes the integer a, leaving the longitude. Gluing a second solid torus with meridian equal to that longitude is the standard splitting of S3⊂C2 into the neighborhoods of its two coordinate circles. Therefore N≅S3.
By the common nonseparating disks in a Heegaard splitting construction and part (b), reversing the cut and caps gives
M≅(S1×S2)#S3≅S1×S2.​
(16)
The product metric on S2×R is homogeneous, and quotienting by an integer translation in the second factor gives S2×S1. Thus M carries this Thurston geometry and is a geometrizable three-manifold.
There is a wording qualification: if “Heegaard genus two” means the minimum defined in part (a), the disk data contradict it. The product splits as S1×D2 on each hemisphere of S2, giving a genus-one splitting, while genus zero gives only S3, so gH​(S1×S2)=1. The genuine construction is a genus-two splitting of a genus-one manifold; the geometrizability conclusion remains valid. The proof used the complete disk systems, the common-sphere cut-and-cap construction, and the explicitly justified genus-one meridian-intersection criterion.

For tame or smooth embeddings f0​,f1​:S1↪S3, ambient isotopy means there is a continuous family Ft​:S3→S3, 0≤t≤1, of homeomorphisms, with F0​=id and F1​∘f0​=f1​. Equivalently the map (x,t)↦(Ft​(x),t) is a homeomorphism of S3×[0,1] preserving the time coordinate. In the smooth category one uses diffeomorphisms and a smooth isotopy. If only unparametrized images are compared, the last condition is F1​(f0​(S1))=f1​(S1); for oriented knots it must also preserve the strand orientation.
A link is an embedding of a finite disjoint union of circles. Choose a point of S3 outside it and identify the complement of that point with R3. A generic planar projection gives an immersion with only finitely many transverse double points, no triple points and no singularities of the projected strands. A link diagram is this planar immersed union together with the over/under information at each double point, recording which strand has the larger height. An oriented link diagram also records each component's orientation. The crossing data are indispensable: the planar immersion alone does not specify the link.

τc​(e2πiθ,s)=(e2πi(θ+ρ(s)),s),​

Since a full turn is the identity on the boundary circle, this map is the identity near both boundary components of the annulus and extends by the identity outside it. The Lickorish-Dehn theorem says that every orientation-preserving homeomorphism of a compact connected orientable surface, fixing its boundary pointwise, is isotopic relative to the boundary to a finite product of Dehn twists and their inverses about simple closed curves. Boundary-parallel curves are permitted. For a closed surface of genus g≥1, a finite collection of curves suffices to generate its mapping class group. If boundary components are allowed to be permuted, twists alone do not express those permutations; the boundary condition is part of the precise statement.
The fundamental theorem of Dehn surgery asserts that every closed connected orientable three-manifold is integer surgery on a finite framed link in S3. To prove it, first choose a Heegaard splitting of M of genus g. Existence of such splittings is a standard background result: a triangulation, with spanning trees collapsed in its primal and dual one-skeletons, gives complementary regular neighborhoods of finite graphs, hence two handlebodies. Choose a standard genus-g splitting of S3, formed by stabilizing its genus-zero splitting. Identify the two pairs of handlebodies. If f0​ is the sphere's gluing map and f is the gluing map for M, then f0−1​f is an orientation-preserving self-homeomorphism of the common surface. The Lickorish-Dehn theorem writes it, up to isotopy, as a product τc1​ϵ1​​⋯τcm​ϵm​​, ϵi​=±1.
Here is the local surgery mechanism. Place c on an intermediate level of a product collar of the surface. Its tubular neighborhood has longitude λ given by that surface and meridian μ around the normal disk. Remove the neighborhood and fill with meridian slope μ±λ. Cut the remaining annular neighborhood of c along a transverse arc. Going around the new meridian now makes one extra positive or negative turn in the longitude direction. On gluing the cut annulus back, the two collar levels are therefore identified by (θ,s)↦(θ±ρ(s),s), precisely the Dehn twist from part (a). Outside this annulus the identification is unchanged. This proves unit surface-framed surgery realizes a Dehn twist; its surgery coefficient is +1 or −1 relative to the surface framing, with sign fixed by the chosen normal convention.
Put the curves ci​ on distinct collar levels and order those levels to realize the indicated composition. They are disjoint as curves in the three-manifold even if their surface projections intersect. Performing the prescribed surgeries replaces f0​ by f, up to isotopy. An isotopy of a gluing map extends across a collar, so the filled manifold is homeomorphic to M.
Finally each surface framing differs from the preferred longitude framing in S3 by an integer number of twists. Thus its coefficient ±1 becomes an integer coefficient in the usual knot longitude convention. These finitely many disjoint curves form the required framed link L:
M≅SL3​ by integer Dehn surgery.​
(35)
The ingredients used were Heegaard-splitting existence, the stated twist-generation theorem, and the explicit local unit-surgery construction.

A Kleinian group is a discrete subgroup of the orientation-preserving isometry group of H3, identified with PSL2​(C). For an abstract finitely generated group this means a faithful representation into PSL2​(C) whose image is discrete; the chosen image is the geometric realization. Its boundary action is by Möbius transformations on S∞2​≅C∪{∞}.
Discreteness means the identity is isolated in the group topology, and for a subgroup it also excludes an infinite sequence of distinct elements converging anywhere in the isometry group. Torsion-freeness is not part of this definition: elliptic Möbius transformations of finite order are allowed. Therefore a Kleinian quotient need not be a smooth hyperbolic manifold.

Fix an interior point o∈H3. The limit set of a Kleinian group is the set of boundary accumulation points of its orbit, or
Λ(Γ)=ΓoH3∪S∞2​∩S∞2​.​

A discrete isometry group has only finitely many orbit points in each compact interior region: isometries moving o into a compact set form a compact family after accounting for the compact rotation stabilizer, and infinitely many distinct group elements there would contradict discreteness. The stabilizer of o is finite for the same reason. Thus an infinite group has an infinite orbit escaping every compact interior set and a nonempty boundary accumulation set. A finite group has empty limit set. This also shows the displayed intersection does not introduce ordinary interior accumulation points.

(41)
If γj​o→ξ∈S∞2​, bounded distance implies
∣γj​o−γj​o′∣≤sinh(C/2)(1−∣γj​o∣2)(1−∣γj​o′∣2)​⟶0.
(42)
So γj​o′→ξ as well. Exchanging o,o′ proves equality of the two accumulation sets. Hence the definition is independent of the interior base point.
It is closed as an intersection of the compact orbit closure with the closed boundary, and it is Γ-invariant because multiplying an orbit by an element of Γ permutes that orbit. Changing ball coordinates or conjugating the geometric realization transports the set by the induced boundary homeomorphism. Different nonconjugate representations of an abstract group are not asserted to have the same subset of one fixed sphere. None of these arguments requires finite generation.

Let F⊆S∞2​ be nonempty, closed and Γ-invariant. We first show it cannot be a singleton when Γ is a non-elementary Kleinian group.
Suppose every element fixes one boundary point, conjugated to ∞. Each boundary map then has the affine form z↦az+b. If all elements have ∣a∣=1, their action in upper half-space keeps the height of o fixed. An orbit sequence escaping compact interior sets must have its horizontal coordinate tending to infinity along any convergent boundary subsequence, so its only possible boundary limit is ∞. If some element has ∣a∣=1, move its second fixed point to 0, making it f(z)=az. For any g(z)=cz+d, its conjugates are fngf−n(z)=cz+and. Choose n→+∞ or −∞ so an→0. If d=0, these are distinct group elements converging to z↦cz, contradicting discreteness. Thus d=0 for every group element: all fix both 0 and ∞, and their only possible interior-orbit boundary limits are these two points. In both cases the Kleinian limit set has at most two points. This contradicts non-elementarity. Therefore F has at least two distinct points.
Use the Klein ball model, in which hyperbolic geodesics are straight chords, and take the closed Euclidean convex hull C of F in the closed ball. It contains an interior point o, since the open chord between two distinct points of F lies in the open ball. The interior part of this hull is hyperbolically convex and invariant under Γ: isometries preserve geodesic segments, so they preserve the hyperbolic convex hull of the invariant set. Thus Γo⊆C.
The only points of C on the boundary sphere are the points of F. Indeed, if ξ∈S∞2​∖F, compactness of F gives ξ⋅z≤c<1 for every z∈F, because ξ⋅z=1 on the unit sphere only when z=ξ. The same linear inequality holds throughout C, excluding ξ from it. Conversely F⊂C, so C∩S∞2​=F.
Every boundary accumulation point of Γo consequently lies in F. Base-point independence from part (b) identifies that accumulation set with Λ(Γ), proving minimality of the Kleinian limit set:
Λ(Γ)⊆F.​
(43)
This proves the stated containment for every nonempty closed invariant set, not merely for sets containing an arbitrarily chosen orbit on the boundary.

Write N=Γ′. As a subgroup of a discrete group, it is a Kleinian group, even if it is not finitely generated. Since it is infinite, part (b) gives a nonempty closed Kleinian limit set Λ(N). For γ∈Γ, conjugation and base-point independence give
γΛ(N)=Λ(γNγ−1).
(44)
One can see this directly by applying γ to No: the image is the orbit of γo under the conjugate subgroup, and the choice of interior point does not affect its limit set. Normality makes the conjugate subgroup equal to N, so Λ(N) is Γ-invariant. Applying part (c) yields Λ(Γ)⊆Λ(N).
Conversely No⊆Γo, so every boundary accumulation point of the smaller orbit is one of the larger orbit: Λ(N)⊆Λ(Γ). Therefore the limit set of an infinite normal Kleinian subgroup satisfies
Λ(Γ′)=Λ(Γ).​
(45)
Infinitude supplies nonemptiness, normality supplies invariance, and non-elementarity supplies minimality. Properness of the subgroup is not needed, and no finite-generation assumption on it has been inserted.

Take Γ=PSL2​(Z) as a subgroup of PSL2​(C). It is discrete because representatives have integer entries, so a bounded set of matrices contains only finitely many such representatives, modulo the two choices of sign. It is finitely generated by
S(z)=−1/z,T(z)=z+1.
(46)
The Euclidean algorithm on the first column of an integer determinant-one matrix reduces it using these two transformations, proving the generation statement.
It is non-elementary. The powers of T move an interior upper-half-space point towards ∞, so ∞∈Λ(Γ). Conjugating T by S gives a parabolic with fixed point 0, and conjugating that parabolic by T gives one with fixed point 1. Their interior orbits approach those respective points. Thus 0,1,∞ are three distinct points in the Kleinian limit set.
However S2=1 in the projective group, and S is a nonidentity elliptic Möbius transformation fixing the hyperbolic geodesic with boundary endpoints i,−i. It is a half-turn around that axis. Near an ordinary point of the axis, quotienting identifies the transverse angle modulo π, giving cone angle π instead of 2π. The elliptic stabilizer obstruction to a hyperbolic manifold quotient therefore gives
H3/PSL2​(Z) is a hyperbolic orbifold, not a smooth hyperbolic 3-manifold with its quotient metric.​
(47)
The obstruction is the nonfree action, not finite generation or elementary behavior. An underlying topological quotient can still be a manifold; the conical quotient metric is what prevents this example from being a hyperbolic manifold quotient.
