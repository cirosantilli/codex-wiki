A topological group is a group with a topology for which multiplication G×G→G and inversion G→G are continuous. A profinite group has either of the following equivalent descriptions: a compact Hausdorff totally disconnected topological group, or an inverse limit of finite discrete groups. Totally disconnected means that every connected component is a singleton.
For clarity, an inverse system over a directed set I consists of groups Gi​ and maps ϕij​:Gj​→Gi​ for i≤j, with ϕii​=1 and ϕik​=ϕij​ϕjk​. Its inverse limit is
lim​Gi​={(gi​)∈∏i​Gi​:ϕij​(gj​)=gi​ whenever i≤j},
(1)
with coordinatewise multiplication and the subspace topology. Equivalently, it is a group with compatible continuous projections such that every other compatible family of maps into the Gi​ factors uniquely through it.
The displayed limit is a closed subgroup of the compact Hausdorff product of the finite groups, and is totally disconnected, since two different tuples are separated by a clopen coordinate condition. Conversely a compact Hausdorff totally disconnected group has a neighbourhood basis of open normal subgroups. One way to see the group part of this standard clopen-basis fact is to choose a clopen identity neighbourhood U inside a prescribed neighbourhood. Compactness of U and continuity give a symmetric identity neighbourhood V with VU⊆U. The stabilizer H={g:gU=U} contains V, is open, and is contained in U because 1∈U. Its index is finite by part (ii); its normal core is the intersection of finitely many conjugates and is open and normal. The canonical map
G⟶lim​N⊴o​G​G/N
(2)
is injective because these N separate points, and surjective because compatible cosets have the finite intersection property and G is compact. It is a homeomorphism by compactness and the Hausdorff property. This explains the equivalence of the two definitions.
The profinite Frattini subgroup is Φ(G)=⋂M, where M ranges over all maximal proper open subgroups; for the trivial group the empty intersection is G. For a pro-p group,
Φ(G)=Gp[G,G]​.​
(3)
Here powers and group commutators mean generated subgroups and the bar supplies closure. Every maximal open subgroup in a pro-p group is normal of index p, as follows by passing to the finite p-group quotient by its normal core. Such a subgroup therefore contains all powers and group commutators. Conversely the quotient by D=Gp[G,G]​ is an abelian pro-p group of exponent p. If an element of this quotient is nontrivial, some finite elementary abelian quotient detects it; a linear functional to Fp​ then detects it as well. Its group homomorphism kernel pulls back to a maximal open subgroup of G. Thus the intersection of these kernels is exactly D.
We also use the topological generation criterion: a subset generates G topologically if and only if its image generates G/Φ(G) topologically. To prove the nontrivial direction, a proper closed generated subgroup K lies in a proper open subgroup KN for a suitably small open normal N, and hence in a maximal open subgroup obtained from the finite quotient G/N. Since that maximal subgroup contains Φ(G), the image of K cannot generate the Frattini quotient.
Now suppose G is a nontrivial procyclic pro-p group. If it had distinct maximal open subgroups M1​,M2​, the homomorphism to Cp​×Cp​ induced by the two quotient maps would be onto: its projections are onto, and a proper such subgroup would be a one-dimensional graph, forcing the two kernels to coincide. Hence G/(M1​∩M2​)≅Cp2​, contradicting procyclicity. Thus there is exactly one maximal open subgroup M, and Φ(G)=M. Any g∈/M generates G/Φ(G)≅Cp​, so the criterion gives
G=⟨g⟩​.​
(4)
For the trivial group use its identity as generator. The additive group Zp​ is an infinite example, topologically generated by 1: its finite continuous quotients are cyclic Z/pnZ.
