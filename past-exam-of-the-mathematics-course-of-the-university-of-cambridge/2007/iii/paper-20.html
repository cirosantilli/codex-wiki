Use the nonnegative convention for the Laplace-Beltrami operator. For a smooth function f on a Riemannian manifold (M,g), three intrinsic definitions are
Δf=d∗df=−div(gradf)=−trg​(∇df)​.
(1)
Here d is the exterior derivative, d∗ its formal L2 adjoint for the Riemannian volume density, gradf the Riemannian gradient, and ∇ the Levi-Civita connection. The bilinear form ∇df is the Riemannian Hessian. The equally common nonpositive convention negates all three definitions simultaneously.
In local coordinates let gij be the inverse metric matrix and ρ=det(gij​)​. By the defining relation g(gradf,V)=df(V),
(gradf)i=gij∂j​f.
(2)
The divergence of a Riemannian vector field can be defined by LV​(dVg​)=(divV)dVg​. Differentiating the coordinate volume density gives divV=ρ−1∂i​(ρVi). Thus the second definition becomes
−div(gradf)=−ρ1​∂i​(ρgij∂j​f).
(3)
For compactly supported smooth h, coordinate integration by parts shows
∫M​h[−ρ−1∂i​(ρgij∂j​f)]dVg​=∫M​gij(∂i​h)(∂j​f)dVg​=⟨dh,df⟩L2​=⟨h,d∗df⟩L2​.
(4)
Since this holds for every such h, the first and second definitions agree. This argument also works on a nonorientable Riemannian manifold, using its volume density; no global orientation is needed.
For the third definition,
(∇df)ij​=∂i​∂j​f−Γijk​∂k​f.
(5)
The Christoffel symbols satisfy Γiki​=∂k​logρ. This follows by tracing their formula and using ∂k​logdetg=tr(g−1∂k​g). Metric compatibility gives
∂i​gij+Γiki​gkj=−Γikj​gik.
(6)
Substitute both identities into the coordinate divergence expression to obtain
−ρ−1∂i​(ρgij∂j​f)=−gij∂i​∂j​f+gijΓijk​∂k​f=−trg​(∇df).
(7)
This proves all three definitions equivalent. In geodesic normal coordinates at a point p, the common value is −∑i​∂i2​f(p). Finally, on a closed manifold,
∫M​fΔfdVg​=∫M​∣df∣g2​dVg​≥0,
(8)
confirming the chosen positive Laplace-Beltrami operator sign convention.

We construct the two domains explicitly from seven congruent triangles. Let a scalene reference triangle have sides a,b,c opposite its three labelled vertices. Choose its three side lengths in a small open neighbourhood of (1,1,1), with a fixed strict ordering. Thus the three angles are distinct and lie between π/4 and π/2. Each copy keeps these side labels.
For the first assembly glue the following tile pairs, reflecting across the indicated side:
sideabc​ΩP​(2,3),(6,7)(4,6),(5,7)(1,5),(3,7)​ΩL​(1,3),(5,7)(2,6),(3,7)(4,5),(6,7)​​
(9)
Each coloured adjacency graph is a tree with central tile 7 and three two-tile arms. Place tile 7 in the plane and obtain every other tile by the prescribed reflections. At the equilateral reference shape the copies have disjoint interiors and form a polygonal disk; the same holds throughout a sufficiently small neighbourhood, since the only contacts between tiles are the prescribed edges and their incident vertices. In particular each assembly is a bounded connected planar domain.
To prove equality of spectra, index tiles by the seven nonzero vectors x∈F23​, using x=(x1​,x2​,x3​) with label x1​+2x2​+4x3​. Let
sa​=I+E12​,sb​=I+E23​,sc​=I+E31​.
(10)
These involutions act on points by x↦ss​x and on nonzero covectors by y↦ss−T​y=ssT​y. Their swaps are exactly the two columns of the table. Write Ps​,Qs​ for the corresponding permutation matrices. The Fano plane incidence matrix
By,x​=1y⋅x=0​
(11)
satisfies BPs​=Qs​B, since incidence is preserved by a linear transformation and its inverse transpose. Each point lies on three lines, while two different points have one common line. Therefore
BTB=2I+J,
(12)
where J is the all-ones matrix, proving that B is invertible.
For the Dirichlet boundary condition, a glued side has a positive swap and an exposed side has diagonal entry −1. Both trees have the same bipartition, with sign matrix
D=diag(1,1,−1,1,−1,−1,1).
(13)
Their signed reflection matrices are Ms​=−DPs​D and Ns​=−DQs​D. On a glued edge its endpoint signs are opposite, so the off-diagonal entries are +1; on an exposed edge the entry is −1. Consequently the invertible matrix C=DBD satisfies
CMs​=Ns​C(s=a,b,c).
(14)
We spell out why this gives isospectrality. Pull each function back from the seven tiles to the reference triangle. Its seven boundary values along side s form a vector us​ satisfying Ms​us​=us​: this imposes equal values at glued edges and zero values at exposed edges. Outward normal derivatives of a smooth eigenfunction satisfy Ms​vs​=−vs​, giving opposite normal derivatives on glued edges. The identities above preserve both conditions under u↦Cu. Since C is constant, it also commutes with the scalar Laplacian on each triangle.
One can include all weak eigenfunctions without any corner regularity issue by orthogonalization of a transplantation matrix. The matrix CTC commutes with the symmetric Ms​, so O=C(CTC)−1/2 is orthogonal and still intertwines Ms​,Ns​. On the disjoint tiles, O preserves the L2 norm and the sum of the Dirichlet energies; it maps the trace-matching form domain H01​(ΩP​) bijectively to H01​(ΩL​). Thus it is a unitary equivalence of the Dirichlet Laplacians, proving equality of every eigenvalue with its multiplicity.
Finally, the only reentrant vertices of either polygon are the three vertices of tile 7, with interior angles four times the corresponding reference-triangle angles. All other boundary angles are less than π. These three distinct angles force any hypothetical isometry to map each labelled central vertex to its counterpart. An isometry between connected open planar domains is a restriction of a rigid motion: its derivative is orthogonal, and preservation of the flat Levi-Civita connection makes that derivative constant. After aligning the two central triangles, this rigid motion fixes three noncollinear points and is the identity. But the two domains do not coincide: the tile beyond side a of the central triangle has its next attachment along b in the point assembly and along c in the line assembly. These occupy different open regions. This contradiction proves nonisometry.
Therefore the three freely varying side lengths give a three-parameter family of nonisometric Dirichlet-isospectral bounded planar domains. The parameters include scale; no congruence normalization removes it.

On a closed compact Riemannian manifold, the heat kernel for the positive Laplace-Beltrami operator is the smooth kernel KM​(t,x,y) of e−tΔM​ for t>0. It solves (∂t​+Δx​)KM​=0 and tends to the delta kernel as t↓0, so that
u(t,x)=∫M​KM​(t,x,y)f(y)dVM​(y),u(0,x)=f(x).
(15)
For an orthonormal eigenbasis with eigenvalues repeated according to multiplicity,
KM​(t,x,y)=∑j=0∞​e−tλj​ϕj​(x)ϕj​(y)​,ZM​(t)=Tr(e−tΔM​)=∫M​KM​(t,x,x)dVM​(x).
(16)
The exponential factors and elliptic regularity ensure smooth convergence for positive time. If a boundary is present, the same construction uses a specified boundary condition preserved by the covering; the closed-manifold case is the one needed below.
Let p:N→M=N/U be the finite normal locally isometric covering map. The heat equation for f∘p on N is the lift of the one for f on M. Integrate the lifted solution over a fundamental domain for the deck transformation group U, splitting the integral over N into its translates. This gives
KM​(t,px,py)=u∈U∑​KN​(t,x,uy)​.
(17)
There is no averaging factor in this kernel formula: each translate accounts for one lift of the integration variable. To verify it directly, the sum solves the lifted heat equation and its integral against f converges to f(px); uniqueness of the heat solution identifies the kernel. Replacing either lift x or y only permutes the sum, since isometries preserve the heat kernel.
Integrating the diagonal and using the covering degree gives the heat trace formula
ZM​(t)=∣U∣1​u∈U∑​Ft​(u)​,Ft​(u)=∫N​KN​(t,x,ux)dVN​(x).
(18)
Suppose U is contained in a finite group T of isometries of N. For s∈T, change variables x=sy and use invariance of both volume and the heat kernel. Then
Ft​(sus−1)=∫N​KN​(t,sy,suy)dVN​(y)=Ft​(u).
(19)
Thus Ft​ is a class function on T, and for its conjugacy classes C,
ZN/U​(t)=∣U∣1​∑C⊂T​∣U∩C∣Ft​(cC​).
(20)
Consequently, if U1​,U2​≤T are Gassmann equivalent and act freely, their orders are equal and the displayed class-by-class sums give ZN/U1​​(t)=ZN/U2​​(t) for every t>0. Equal heat traces determine equal eigenvalues with multiplicities: the least eigenvalue at which the multiplicities differed would give a nonzero leading exponential in their trace difference as t→∞, a contradiction. Hence the two smooth quotient manifolds are isospectral. This proves the Sunada theorem, with the free-action hypothesis ensuring that the quotients are manifolds rather than orbifolds. The theorem alone makes no nonisometry claim.

Let V=F23​. Since F2×​={1} and the scalar centre is trivial,
G=PSL(3,2)=GL(3,2),∣G∣=(8−1)(8−2)(8−4)=168.
(21)
Choose U1​ to stabilize a nonzero vector and U2​ to stabilize a two-dimensional subspace. There are seven choices of either object, so both subgroups have index 7 and order 24.
Their coset permutation characters agree. The number of nonzero vectors fixed by g is 2dimker(g−I)−1. A two-dimensional subspace is the kernel of a unique nonzero covector, so its stabilizer action is the dual action g−T. Its fixed-point count is
2dimker(g−T−I)−1=2dimker(g−I)−1,
(22)
using equality of the matrix ranks of a matrix and its transpose. For a subgroup H, counting the representatives of fixed cosets gives
χG/H​(g)=∣H∣∣CG​(g)∣​∣H∩[g]G​∣.
(23)
Thus the equal characters and subgroup orders give equal intersection counts with every conjugacy class. This proves U1​,U2​ are Gassmann equivalent.
They are not conjugate. For example, the stabilizer of e1​ contains every matrix (10​bB​) with b∈F21×2​ and B∈GL(2,2). An invariant plane containing e1​ would give an invariant line in V/⟨e1​⟩, impossible for the full GL(2,2) action. An invariant plane not containing e1​ would be a complement to that line, but the arbitrary shears b do not preserve any such complement. Hence a point stabilizer preserves no plane, whereas every conjugate of U2​ does. The two subgroups are nonconjugate.
We now use a cone-torus construction of genus-four Sunada surfaces. Let B be a hyperbolic torus with one cone point of angle 2π/7. Its orbifold fundamental group has presentation
Γ=⟨a,d∣[d,a]7=1⟩.
(24)
Map a,d to the supplied generators A,D of G. Their commutator has exact order 7, so this gives a surjection Γ→G with torsion-free kernel K: every finite-order element of Γ is conjugate to a power of the cone generator, and no nonidentity such power lies in K. Thus N=H2/K is a smooth closed hyperbolic surface with a G-action. Its quotients by U1​,U2​ are smooth too, since order-24 subgroups contain no element of order 7 and therefore meet no cone stabilizer.
The orbifold Euler characteristic of B is −(1−1/7)=−6/7. Each quotient cover has degree 7, so
χ(Si​)=7χorb​(B)=−6=2−2g(Si​),g(S1​)=g(S2​)=4​.
(25)
The Sunada theorem proved in Question 3 makes S1​,S2​ isospectral for every choice of the base hyperbolic metric.
Here is an explicit supply of base metrics. A hyperbolic trirectangle has three right angles and a fourth angle α=π/14. If its sides adjacent to the opposite right-angle vertex have lengths r,s, the trirectangle identity is sinhrsinhs=cosα. Thus r>0 varies freely, with s=arsinh(cosα/sinhr). Reflecting four copies about their two perpendicular centre lines gives a quadrilateral with four angles π/14 and opposite sides of equal length. Identifying opposite sides produces a cone torus: all four corners give total angle 2π/7. Its two centre-line geodesics intersect once and have lengths 2r,2s. Cut along the shorter chosen geodesic d and reglue with a small twist τ; this retains the cone angle and allows an additional metric parameter. The remaining cut surface has two equal-length geodesic boundaries and the cone point.
We give a concrete intersection test for nonisometric finite covers, rather than relying on subgroup nonconjugacy alone. Choose ℓ(d)=ε sufficiently small. The trirectangle geometry gives an embedded collar of width log(1/ε)+O(1) about d. The shortest perpendicular joining the two boundary components after cutting has length δ=2log(1/ε)+O(1). At zero twist it closes to a, the unique shortest closed geodesic crossing d once. Uniqueness follows from uniqueness of the shortest perpendicular; all other once-crossing classes have a positive length gap, since only finitely many closed geodesics have bounded length. For sufficiently small twists, the same marked class a remains the unique shortest once-crossing geodesic. A geodesic crossing d at least twice traverses the collar at least twice, so for small ε its length is greater than ℓ(a).
Geodesics disjoint from d remain entirely in the cut surface and have twist-independent lengths. In a bounded window around ℓ(a) they supply only finitely many possible length values, including lengths of iterates. Meanwhile ℓ(a) varies nontrivially with τ by the hyperbolic seam identity: its hyperbolic cosine has a positive multiple of cosh(τ/2). Choose a small twist away from those finitely many coincidences. Then the only base geodesic of length ℓ(a) is a, up to orientation. By taking ε smaller if necessary, the only base geodesics of length at most 2ε are powers of d: those crossing d are long, and the nonperipheral geodesics of the limiting two-cusped cone pair of pants have lengths bounded away from zero.
In each cover, primitive lift components of a base geodesic are indexed by cycles of its monodromy permutation, with length equal to the cycle size times its base length. The permutation of A has a unique singleton {0} in each action, so each Si​ has a uniquely length-identified primitive lift αi​ of length ℓ(a). The permutation of D has one two-cycle: it is {0,3} for U1​ and {2,5} for U2​. Hence each Si​ has a unique primitive lift βi​ of length 2ε; the iterate of the singleton lift is not primitive. At the single base intersection of a,d, intersections of their lifted components are exactly their common sheet labels. Therefore
i(α1​,β1​)=1,i(α2​,β2​)=0​.
(26)
An isometry would have to preserve the uniquely identified primitive lengths and their geometric intersection numbers. This contradiction proves that S1​ and S2​ are not isometric, even allowing orientation reversal.
Finally, let ε range over a sufficiently small positive interval, choosing a permissible twist for each value. The singleton cycle of D makes the hyperbolic systole of each cover equal to ε. Distinct values therefore give distinct isometry classes. We obtain uncountably many pairs of nonisometric, isospectral genus-four Riemann surfaces.

Throughout the hyperbolic discussion take curvature −1 and g≥2. A Y-piece is a pair of pants with a hyperbolic metric and three geodesic boundary components. Three labelled positive boundary lengths ℓ1​,ℓ2​,ℓ3​ determine it up to isometry. To see existence and uniqueness, cut along the three shortest perpendicular seams. The two resulting right-angled hyperbolic hexagons have alternating side lengths ℓi​/2, which determine each hexagon. Conversely, any three positive alternating lengths produce such a hexagon, and doubling along the other sides produces the desired Y-piece. In particular the seam opposite ℓi​ satisfies
coshsi​=sinh(ℓj​/2)sinh(ℓk​/2)cosh(ℓi​/2)+cosh(ℓj​/2)cosh(ℓk​/2)​.
(27)
An X-piece is a four-holed hyperbolic sphere with geodesic boundaries, obtained by joining two Y-pieces along one pair of equal-length boundaries. For a chosen separating curve, it is determined by
ℓ1​,ℓ2​,ℓ3​,ℓ4​>0,s>0,τ​,
(28)
where s is the common gluing length and τ is the relative arclength twist, measured between chosen seam endpoints. The identification reverses boundary orientation so that the surface is oriented. For an unmarked gluing, τ is periodic modulo s; a marking retains τ∈R, because a full Dehn twist changes the marking. These six parameters describe the X-piece with its chosen separating curve; different curve choices or boundary symmetries can describe the same unmarked surface.
Choose a pants decomposition of a closed oriented genus-g surface. Each Y-piece has Euler characteristic −1, and gluing boundary circles does not change the total Euler characteristic. Thus there are 2g−2 pieces. Their 3(2g−2) boundary circles are paired, giving 3g−3 gluing curves. Choose their disjoint geodesic representatives, then prescribe each cuff length and its relative twist. The Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates are
(ℓi​,τi​)i=13g−3​∈(0,∞)3g−3×R3g−3​.
(29)
They determine the marked hyperbolic surface and hence its unmarked isometry class, with 6g−6 real parameters. One must also fix the combinatorial gluing pattern of the chosen pants decomposition. Full twists and other changes of marking identify some parameter sets in unmarked moduli space of Riemann surfaces. The genus restriction matters: a sphere and a torus do not admit a closed curvature-−1 metric or such a decomposition into geodesic Y-pieces.
For a fixed oriented topological surface Sg​, Teichmüller space Tg​ consists of marked Riemann surfaces (X,f:Sg​→X). Two markings represent the same point when a conformal isomorphism between the surfaces carries one marking to the other up to homotopy. By the uniformization theorem, for g≥2 these are equivalently marked curvature-−1 metrics. The displayed Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates identify Tg​ with a cell of real dimension 6g−6; the mapping class group quotient gives the unmarked moduli space of Riemann surfaces.
The spectral version of the Wolpert generic spectral rigidity theorem says that outside a closed proper real-analytic exceptional subset of Tg​, the Laplace spectrum determines the hyperbolic surface up to isometry. The exceptional subset has positive codimension and is invariant under changes of marking. Orientation reversal must be allowed, since the Laplacian cannot detect orientation. Equivalently, a generic surface is determined by its unmarked length spectrum, counted with multiplicities. This is a generic uniqueness statement, not a claim that every isospectral pair is isometric; the construction in Question 4 lies in the exceptional locus.
Three major ingredients and their roles are as follows.
  • The Selberg trace formula converts the Laplacian spectrum into the lengths and multiplicities of closed geodesics, and conversely. It moves the problem from eigenvalues to geometric length data, where pants and twists can be used.
  • Finite length coordinates for hyperbolic surfaces and real-analytic length functions reduce candidate spectral matchings to finitely many local analytic systems. The cuff lengths fix the Y-pieces; two transverse curves at each cuff fix its twist, giving 9g−9 labelled length functions. Hyperbolic holonomy gives 2cosh(ℓγ​/2)=∣trρ(γ)∣, so these lengths obey analytic trace relations. Discreteness of bounded length data, compactness in the thick part, and finite determination of those trace relations control the locally possible unlabelled matchings. A matching that is not an identity therefore lies in a proper analytic zero set.
  • Geometric decoding of the persistent identities uses the collar lemma and variations of Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates. Pinching a cuff makes crossing curves long while disjoint ones remain bounded; twisting controls the transverse length relations. These behaviours recover the pants incidence and the twist data, and show that a matching persisting on an open parameter set comes from a change of marking, possibly with orientation reversal. Thus any genuinely nonisometric matching is confined to the lower-dimensional exceptional analytic locus.
For completeness, a related finiteness statement sometimes grouped with this theorem is that a fixed closed hyperbolic surface has only finitely many isospectral isometry classes. Its short proof uses three particularly concrete tools: a common discrete length spectrum; Mumford's compactness theorem, applied to the common positive systole; and finitely many determining marked lengths. Compactness would give an accumulation point if an isospectral family were infinite. Nearby markings make each determining length converge, while discreteness of the fixed spectrum makes it eventually constant. The determining lengths then force eventual equality of the marked surfaces, a contradiction. This finiteness of isospectral hyperbolic surfaces statement does not replace the stronger generic uniqueness assertion above.
