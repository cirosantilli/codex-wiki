Both alternatives can be proved using the same smallness mechanism. We first establish the initial-object lemma for complete categories with a weakly initial set, then obtain the two adjoint functor existence theorems and their applications.
Let E be a locally small category with all small categorical limits and a weakly initial set (Wi​)i∈I​. Its product in a category W=∏i​Wi​ is weakly initial: given any object, choose a map from some Wi​ and compose with the corresponding projection. Take the simultaneous equalizer e:E→W of every endomorphism of W and its identity; local smallness makes this a small categorical limit. Then E is also weakly initial. If a,b:E→X, form their equalizer j:Y→E and choose t:W→Y. Since ejt is an endomorphism of W, the construction of e gives ejte=e. Cancelling the monomorphism e yields jte=1E​. Thus j is both a monomorphism and a split epimorphism, hence invertible, and a=b. There is therefore exactly one arrow from E to every object: E is initial.
The Freyd general adjoint functor theorem says that for a functor U:A→B between locally small categories, with A complete, a left adjoint exists exactly when U preserves all small categorical limits and satisfies the solution-set condition. That condition requires, for each B, a set of arrows ui​:B→UAi​ such that every u:B→UA factors as U(f)ui​ for some i and f:Ai​→A.
For sufficiency, the comma category (B↓U) is locally small and complete: form a diagram's categorical limit in A, and use categorical limit preservation to obtain its arrow from B. The solution set is a weakly initial set in this comma category. The lemma provides an initial object (LB,ηB​:B→ULB). Its universal property gives
A(LB,A)≅B(B,UA),f⟼U(f)ηB​.
(1)
For h:B→B′, define Lh by U(Lh)ηB​=ηB′​h. Uniqueness proves the identity and composition laws and naturality of the displayed bijections, hence L⊣U. Conversely, an adjunction makes U preserve categorical limits, since the corresponding hom-set functors turn a categorical limit cone into a categorical limit of sets. The single arrow ηB​ is a solution set. This proves both directions of the theorem.
Now let F:C→D have small domain and codomain. The functor categories [D,Set] and [C,Set] are locally small, and their categorical limits are pointwise. Thus the precomposition functor F∗ preserves categorical limits and its domain is complete. To verify its solution-set condition, fix X:C→Set and an arrow x:X→F∗Y. Let Y0​⊆Y be the subfunctor generated by all elements xc​(a), where c∈C and a∈X(c). Explicitly,
Y0​(d)={Y(g)(xc​(a)):g:Fc→d, a∈X(c)}.
(2)
These sets are stable under every Y(h). If κ is an infinite cardinal bounding ∣MorD∣ and ∣∐c​X(c)∣, every Y0​(d) has cardinal at most κ. Replace each such set by a subset of a fixed set of size κ. There is only a set of choices of these subsets, of their action maps for the set of arrows of D, and of maps from X to their restrictions. These coded pairs form a solution set: x factors through F∗Y0​ and then the inclusion Y0​↪Y. The general theorem now yields
LanF​⊣F∗.​
(3)
Its values may also be written LanF​X(d)=colim(Fc→d)​X(c), the usual left Kan extension formula.
For the other alternative, the categorical limit form of the Special adjoint functor theorem says that a limit-preserving functor U:A→B has a left adjoint if A is locally small, complete and well-powered, has a cogenerating set (Qi​)i∈I​, and B is locally small. A cogenerating set means that maps into its members distinguish any two parallel arrows. The dual theorem says that a colimit-preserving functor from a locally small, cocomplete, well-copowered category with a small generating family has a right adjoint. These conclusions are also necessary in their preservation clauses.
Here is a proof of the special theorem rather than an invocation of the general theorem without its required solution set. Given (A,x:B→UA), intersect all subobjects m:M↪A through which x factors after application of U. There are only a set of them by well-poweredness. Their intersection is a wide pullback, preserved by U, so gives a smallest supporting pair (A0​,x0​). If p,q:A0​→Qi​ satisfy U(p)x0​=U(q)x0​, their equalizer is a supporting subobject. Minimality makes that equalizer invertible, whence p=q. Consequently
A(A0​,Qi​)⟶B(B,UQi​),p⟼U(p)x0​,
(4)
is injective. The cogenerating property makes the evaluation arrow from A0​ into the product in a category of the Qi​, one factor for each map A0​→Qi​, a monic arrow. Its indexing set at each i is identified with a subset of the fixed set B(B,UQi​). Only a set of such products in a category can occur. Each has only a set of subobjects, and each possible subobject has only a set of arrows from B into its image under U. These pairs give a weakly initial set in (B↓U), since every original pair receives the arrow from its minimal supporting pair. The complete-category lemma now gives the adjoint. Reversing all arrows proves the colimit form of the special theorem.
Apply that dual form to F∗:[D,Set]→[C,Set]. Its domain is cocomplete and locally small, and F∗ preserves pointwise colimits. The set of covariant representable functors D(d,−) generates the domain: by the Yoneda lemma, maps from these representables probe every element at every object and hence distinguish distinct natural transformations. Finally, epimorphisms in this functor category are pointwise surjective. Indeed, their pointwise images form subfunctors; if an image is proper, the two inclusions into the pointwise pushout of two copies of the target over that image distinguish the original map, contradicting epicity. Quotients of a fixed functor are therefore specified by compatible equivalence relations on its sets of values, and form a set. Thus the domain is a well-copowered category. All special-theorem hypotheses hold, giving
F∗⊣RanF​.​
(5)
Here the Right Kan extension can be written RanF​X(d)=Nat(D(d,F−),X). In particular both requested adjoints exist, although the two proofs use different smallness hypotheses.
