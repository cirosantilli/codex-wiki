A central series is a finite chain of normal subgroups
1=G0​≤G1​≤⋯≤Gr​=G
(1)
with Gi​/Gi−1​≤Z(G/Gi−1​) for every i≥1. Equivalently, writing [x,y]=x−1y−1xy and [A,B] for the subgroup generated by these group commutators, the condition is [Gi​,G]≤Gi−1​. Centrality is relative to the whole quotient group, not just to the next subgroup in the chain.
A group is nilpotent if it admits a finite central series. The trivial group admits a series of length zero. For a nontrivial nilpotent group, the minimum possible length is its nilpotency class.

Use induction on the order of the finite p-group. The trivial group is nilpotent. For G=1, the permitted center theorem gives Z=Z(G)=1, so the quotient group G/Z is a smaller p-group. By induction it has a central series
1=H0​≤H1​≤⋯≤Hs​=G/Z.
(2)
Let Hi​ be the inverse image of Hi​ under the quotient map, so H0​=Z and Hs​=G. Centrality in the quotient gives [Hi​,G]≤Hi−1​ for i≥1: the image of every such group commutator lies in Hi−1​. Also [Z,G]=1. Therefore
1≤Z=H0​≤H1​≤⋯≤Hs​=G​
(3)
is a central series of G. This proves every finite p-group is nilpotent, including the case Z=G, when the quotient is trivial and the series is simply 1≤G.

Let 1=A0​≤⋯≤Ar​=A and 1=B0​≤⋯≤Bs​=B be central series. Pad the shorter chain by repeating its terminal group, so both have length t=max(r,s). In the direct product of groups, group commutators are coordinatewise, hence
[Ai​×Bi​,A×B]=[Ai​,A]×[Bi​,B]≤Ai−1​×Bi−1​.
(7)
The equality holds because commutators supported in either coordinate generate the corresponding factor independently. Therefore
1=A0​×B0​≤⋯≤At​×Bt​=A×B​
(8)
is a central series, proving that a direct product of two nilpotent groups is nilpotent. More precisely, the coordinatewise recurrence for the lower central series gives Γj​(A×B)=Γj​(A)×Γj​(B), so the nilpotency class of a direct product is the maximum of the two factor classes.

Define the lower central series by Γ1​(G)=G and Γj+1​(G)=[Γj​(G),G]. Its terms are characteristic normal subgroups and form a descending chain. Define the upper central series by Z0​(G)=1 and
Zi+1​(G)/Zi​(G)=Z(G/Zi​(G)).
(9)
Equivalently, Zi+1​(G)={x∈G:[x,G]⊆Zi​(G)}. These are characteristic normal subgroups in an ascending chain, and successive factors are central in the appropriate quotient.
For any length-r central series 1=G0​≤⋯≤Gr​=G, prove the two comparisons independently. Starting at Γ1​(G)=Gr​, if Γr−i+1​(G)≤Gi​, then
Γr−i+2​(G)=[Γr−i+1​(G),G]≤[Gi​,G]≤Gi−1​.
(10)
Descending induction gives the lower containment. Starting at G0​=Z0​(G)=1, if Gi−1​≤Zi−1​(G), then [Gi​,G]≤Gi−1​≤Zi−1​(G), which by definition gives Gi​≤Zi​(G). Thus the central-series comparison theorem yields
Γr−i+1​(G)≤Gi​≤Zi​(G)(0≤i≤r).​
(11)
In particular, a nilpotent group satisfies Γr+1​(G)=1 and Zr​(G)=G.
Conversely, if Γn​(G)=1, reverse the finite lower chain to obtain 1=Γn​≤Γn−1​≤⋯≤Γ1​=G. The defining commutator recurrence makes it a central series. If Zn​(G)=G, the finite upper chain is already a central series. This proves
G nilpotent ⟺ Γn​(G)=1 for some n ⟺ Zn​(G)=G for some n.​
(12)
Finally, for an integer c>0, termination at Γc+1​=1 gives a central series of length c and hence Zc​=G by the comparison. Conversely, Zc​=G gives a central series of length c and hence Γc+1​=1. Therefore
Γc+1​(G)=1⟺Zc​(G)=G.​
(13)
Repeated terms cause no problem; the least such c for a nontrivial group is its nilpotency class.

Choose a representative Sylow subgroup with generators
r=(1 2 3 4),s=(1 3),t=(5 6).
(14)
On the first four points, r4=s2=1 and srs=r−1, so ⟨r,s⟩ is the dihedral group of order eight. The disjoint transposition t commutes with it and generates an independent factor of order two. Thus G=⟨r,s,t⟩≅D8​×C2​ has order 16. Since the power of two dividing 7! is 2⌊7/2⌋+⌊7/4⌋=24, this is a Sylow 2-subgroup of S7​. Any other choice is conjugate to it and has conjugate series.

Γ1​(G)=G,Γ2​(G)=⟨(1 3)(2 4)⟩,Γ3​(G)=1.​

Identify G with a subgroup of Sym(Ω) and adjoin one new point ∞, fixed by every element of G. A one-point extension of a permutation group is a transitive subgroup G+≤Sym(Ω∪{∞}) whose point stabilizer at ∞ is exactly this embedded copy of G. Equivalently, one may use an isomorphic permutation representation with a distinguished point whose stabilizer acts on the remaining points as the original group.
Since G is transitive on Ω, the extension is doubly transitive group action. The orbit-stabilizer theorem gives the useful necessary order condition
∣G+∣=(∣Ω∣+1)∣G∣.​
(17)

Suppose an extension H existed. Its degree would be p, and its order would be 2p(p−1) by the orbit-stabilizer theorem. A Sylow p-subgroup P consequently has order p, and the Sylow theorems imply that its number np​ divides 2(p−1) and is congruent to one modulo p. Since 2(p−1)<2p, the only possibilities are 1 and p+1. The latter would require p+1∣2(p−1) and hence p+1∣4, impossible for p≥5. Thus P is a normal subgroup of H.
A nonidentity element of P is a p-cycle, so P acts regularly on the p points. Label them by Fp​ so that P consists of translations. A permutation normalizing P must have the form x↦ax+b: its conjugation sends translation by one to translation by some a=0, and its value at zero determines b. Therefore the normalizer of P in Sp​ has order p(p−1). Normality would place H inside this normalizer, contradicting ∣H∣=2p(p−1). Hence the specified dihedral permutation group has no one-point extension.

Here is an involution form of the double-coset criterion for a one-point extension. Embed G in Sym(Ω∪{∞}) by fixing ∞, choose a∈Ω, and put H=Ga​. The necessary and sufficient condition is the existence of an involution t such that
t(∞)=a,t(a)=∞,tHt=H,tgt∈GtG for every g∈G∖H.​
(18)
Products in this part are compositions of maps, with the rightmost map applied first. The criterion is a finite test on permutations of the enlarged set; it does not presuppose that a larger group has already been found.
For sufficiency, put K=G∪GtG. Multiplying a double coset by an element of G on either side leaves it unchanged. A product of two elements of GtG reduces to g1​tgtg2​. If g∈H, then tgt∈H≤G; otherwise the last condition puts it in GtG. Hence K is closed under multiplication. It contains the identity and is closed under inverses, since t−1=t, so it is a group.
Every element g1​tg2​ sends ∞ to g1​(a)∈Ω, whereas elements of G fix ∞. Thus K∞​=G. Moreover the orbit of ∞ contains a and then all of Ω, so K is transitive and is the desired one-point extension of a permutation group. For the order check, G∩tGt=H: an element of the intersection fixes both ∞ and a, and the reverse containment follows from tHt=H. Consequently ∣GtG∣=∣G∣2/∣H∣=∣Ω∣∣G∣, as required.
For necessity, let K be an extension. It is doubly transitive group action, of order ∣Ω∣(∣Ω∣+1)∣Ga​∣, which is even. By Cauchy theorem for groups it contains a nontrivial involution, and a pair interchanged by this involution can be carried to (∞,a) by double transitivity. Conjugating gives an involution t interchanging these two points. Their common point stabilizer is H, so tHt=H. Finally G has just two orbits on the enlarged set, namely {∞} and Ω, giving K=G∪GtG. More explicitly, for k∈K∖G, choose g∈G with g(a)=k(∞); then tg−1k fixes ∞ and belongs to G. If g∈/H, the element tgt moves ∞, so it belongs to GtG, completing necessity.

A nonidentity transvection is a linear automorphism τ=I+N with rankN=1 and N2=0. Equivalently, it fixes a hyperplane pointwise and its displacement has image in a line lying in that hyperplane. The notation T∖{1} allows the identity as a degenerate transvection; use that convention here, with N=0 also allowed.
For a nonidentity transvection, choose d=0 spanning the image of N. There is a unique nonzero linear functional f such that N(v)=f(v)d. Since N2(v)=f(v)f(d)d, we have f(d)=0, so d∈kerf. Conversely, this condition makes (df)2=0 and gives inverse I−df. The identity is represented by f=0 and any d=0. Thus
τf,d​(v)=v+f(v)d,d=0,f(d)=0.​
(20)
When f(d)=f′(d)=0, the two displacement maps have zero product, giving
τf,d​τf′,d​=τf+f′,d​,τf,d−1​=τ−f,d​.​
(21)
For clarity use left actions of linear maps. Applying g−1, then τf,d​, then g gives
gτf,d​g−1=τf∘g−1,g(d)​.​
(22)
With the alternative conjugation notation τg=g−1τg, the answer is τf,dg​=τf∘g,g−1(d)​. In either convention the transformed direction lies in the kernel of the transformed functional.

Write xij​(a)=I+aEij​ for i=j. For a=0 these are transvections, and for a=0 they are the identity. Left multiplication by xij​(a) adds a times row j to row i. We show that these elementary transvection matrices generate the special linear group.
Apply Gaussian elimination using only row additions. If a pivot is zero, invertibility ensures some lower entry in its column is nonzero; adding that row produces a nonzero pivot without interchanging or scaling rows. Clear entries below each pivot, and then above pivots in reverse order. This reduces any invertible matrix to a diagonal matrix D=diag(d1​,…,dn​), with all di​=0, preserving determinant throughout. For a determinant-one matrix, ∏i​di​=1.
A two-coordinate diagonal block is itself a product of elementary transvection matrices. On those two coordinates put
w(a)=x12​(a)x21​(−a−1)x12​(a)=(0−a−1​a0​),w(a)w(−1)=(a0​0a−1​).
(25)
This formula is valid in characteristic two as well. For n≥2, multiply the diagonal blocks with entries di​,di−1​ on coordinates (i,n) for 1≤i<n. Their product is D, because its last entry is ∏i<n​di−1​=dn​. Thus D and the original matrix lie in the subgroup generated by transvections. Conversely every transvection has determinant one by part ii. Hence
⟨T⟩=SL(V).​

For n=1, both the special linear group and the subgroup generated by the degenerate identity transvection are trivial. The argument also establishes transvections generate the special linear group over any field.

First determine when a group automorphism can carry a transposition to another type of involution. An involution in Sn​ is a product of k disjoint transpositions, and its conjugacy class has size
2kk!(n−2k)!n!​.
(30)
An automorphism preserves element orders and conjugacy-class sizes. Equality with the transposition-class size n(n−1)/2 requires
(n−2k)!(n−2)!​=2k−1k!.
(31)
For k=2 this says (n−2)(n−3)=4, which has no integral solution n≥4. For k=3, the minimum value of the left side, at n=6, is 4!=24=22⋅3!; it increases strictly for larger n. For k≥4, even its minimum (2k−2)! exceeds 2k−1k!: at k=4 this is 720>192, and the ratio of these two quantities increases at the next step by (2k)(2k−1)/(2(k+1))>1. Thus only in degree six can the transposition class be exchanged with a different involution class, namely the triple transpositions.
We next prove that any automorphism preserving transpositions is an inner automorphism. Regard a transposition as an edge of the complete graph on the n letters. Two distinct transpositions fail to commute exactly when their edges meet. Any family of pairwise intersecting edges is contained in a vertex star or a triangle: after choosing {a,b} and {a,c}, an edge not containing a must be {b,c}, which precludes any fourth edge. For n≥5, the stars are therefore distinguished as the maximal such families of size n−1≥4. An automorphism permutes stars, inducing a permutation π of the letters; their pairwise intersections show that (i j) is sent to (π(i) π(j)). Since transpositions generate Sn​, this is conjugation by a permutation.
For n=4, stars and triangles both have three edges, but a star generates S4​ while a triangle generates a subgroup S3​. An automorphism preserves the order of the generated subgroup and hence still distinguishes stars. For n=3, every permutation of the three transpositions is induced by conjugation in S3​, since its conjugation action on them is faithful and has order six. The groups S1​,S2​ have trivial automorphism groups. This completes the small cases.
It follows that all automorphisms for n=6 are inner. In degree six, an automorphism permutes the two involution classes of size 15; the kernel of this action consists precisely of the inner automorphisms. Therefore the automorphisms of the symmetric group satisfy
Out(Sn​)=1 (n=6),∣Out(S6​)∣≤2.​
(32)

A duad is an unordered pair of letters, a syntheme is a partition of the six letters into three duads, and a total of synthemes is a set of five synthemes partitioning the 15 duads. There are 6!/(23⋅3!)=15 synthemes. Fix one, M. Each of its three duads lies in three synthemes, and two distinct duads of M can occur together only in M itself. Thus precisely 1+3⋅2=7 synthemes meet M in a duad, leaving eight disjoint from it.
For any syntheme M′ disjoint from M, their union as edges in K6​ is a six-cycle: the two perfect matchings alternate, and no two-cycle is possible in a simple graph. Its complement is a triangular prism, namely two triangles joined by three corresponding edges. The prism has four perfect matchings. One consists of all three joining edges; each of the other three consists of one joining edge and the remaining edge in each triangle. To partition the prism into three perfect matchings, one cannot use the all-joining matching, since what remains is two odd triangles. The other three do partition it. Hence two disjoint synthemes extend to a unique synthematic total.
There are eight choices for M′ and four choices of M′ within each synthematic total containing M. Thus M lies in exactly two synthematic totals. Counting incidences of synthemes and synthematic totals now gives the counting synthematic totals formula
#{totals}=515⋅2​=6.​
(33)
Two distinct synthematic totals share at most one syntheme, since sharing two would force them to be the unique synthematic total extending that pair. Each syntheme gives a pair of synthematic totals containing it, and the 15 synthemes account for all (26​)=15 pairs. Therefore every pair of synthematic totals shares exactly one syntheme.
This also explains why the action on the six synthematic totals defines an automorphism of S6​. A permutation fixing all synthematic totals fixes their pairwise intersections, hence every syntheme. Each duad is the intersection of two distinct synthemes containing it, so every duad is then fixed. Intersecting two duads through a point shows that every point is fixed. The action is faithful and, by equality of orders, maps S6​ onto the full symmetric group of the synthematic totals. Part iii shows that a transposition acts as a triple transposition, establishing that this is an outer automorphism.

A Steiner system S(5,6,12) consists of 12 points and a collection of six-element blocks such that every five-element subset lies in exactly one block. The blocks are often called hexads. Necessarily their number is (512​)/(56​)=132. We give the synthematic construction of the small Witt design explicitly and prove this defining property.
Write X={1,…,6} and Y={T1​,…,T6​}. For an unordered pair P={Ti​,Tj​}⊂Y, let S(P) denote the unique syntheme shared by its two synthematic totals. A duad D⊂X belongs to three synthemes. The corresponding three pairs P partition Y: every synthematic total contains D in exactly one of its synthemes, and each syntheme lies in exactly two synthematic totals. Thus the incidence D∈S(P) gives a perfect matching of the six synthematic totals for each duad.
To describe the blocks with three points in each half, take an unordered partition X=A⊔Ac into triples. A syntheme is cross if each of its three duads joins A to Ac. Form a graph on Y by declaring P an edge exactly when S(P) is cross. This graph is the union of two disjoint triangles. Indeed a synthematic total partitions the nine cross duads; every syntheme has either one or three cross duads, so if k of its five synthemes are cross, then 3k+(5−k)=9, giving k=2. Each vertex therefore has degree two. The stronger assertion about its components follows directly for A={1,2,3}, when the two triangles are

The complete block collection is given by the following three rules:
  • Include X and Y.
  • For each P⊂Y of size two and each duad D∈S(P), include D∪(Y∖P) and its complement (X∖D)∪P.
  • For each unordered triple partition A⊔Ac and its two components B,Bc, include A∪B, A∪Bc, Ac∪B, and Ac∪Bc.
There are 2+15⋅3⋅2+10⋅4=132 distinct blocks. The first two rules have different intersection sizes with X from the third; within the third, a block determines its triple A and its component. We next verify the Steiner system property, rather than relying solely on this count.
We need one further consequence of the triangle construction. The three synthemes attached to the three pairs within B are pairwise disjoint, since any two belong to their common synthematic total, and together they are all nine cross duads of the complete bipartite graph between A and Ac. Thus B determines the unordered partition A⊔Ac uniquely: it is the bipartition of this connected graph. There are 10 such partitions, each giving two different components, hence 20 different triples in Y. Since (36​)=20, each triple in Y corresponds to exactly one triple partition of X. The same reasoning applies to Bc.
Let F be any five-point set and put r=∣F∩X∣. If r=5, only X can contain it; if r=0, only Y can contain it, because no other block has five points in either half.
If r=4, set D=X∖F and write F∩Y={T}. The three pairs attached to D partition Y, so exactly one contains T; its block (X∖D)∪P is the unique block through F. If r=1, put P=Y∖F and write F∩X={x}. The three duads of S(P) partition X, so exactly one contains x; this gives the unique block D∪(Y∖P).
If r=3, write A=F∩X and P=F∩Y. A syntheme has either one or three duads crossing A⊔Ac. In the one-cross case, S(P) has exactly one duad D internal to Ac, and the unique block is (X∖D)∪P. No block with three points in each half is possible, since P is then not an edge of either triangle. In the three-cross case, P is an edge of exactly one triangle B, giving the unique block A∪B; no duad of S(P) is internal to Ac, so a block with four points in X is impossible.
Finally let r=2, write D=F∩X and B=F∩Y, and use the uniquely associated partition X=A⊔Ac just proved. If D crosses this partition, the three synthemes belonging to the pairs inside B contain each cross duad exactly once. Thus the matching of Y attached to D has exactly one pair internal to B. It has exactly one pair P internal to Bc as well, since a matching must leave equal numbers of vertices in the two halves for cross pairs. The unique block containing F is then D∪(Y∖P); D is not contained in either A or Ac, so no block of the third rule applies. If D is internal to A or Ac, none of its attached pairs lies inside B or Bc, since the synthemes attached to these internal pairs are cross. Thus the second rule gives no block. The third rule gives exactly one, namely A∪B or Ac∪B, according to which part contains D.
All possibilities have now been exhausted, proving
