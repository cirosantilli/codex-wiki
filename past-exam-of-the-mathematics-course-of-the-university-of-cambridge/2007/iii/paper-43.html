This is an analysis of covariance: achievement is the response, aptitude is a quantitative covariate, and teaching method is a categorical explanatory variable. The usual normal linear model assumes independent errors with common variance σ2.
read.table reads the data with a header row; attach makes its columns directly accessible, and the two printed vectors confirm their ordering. rep(1:3, each=7) supplies the three group labels, and factor makes them categorical. The contrast option selects treatment coding for unordered factors, with method A as the first-level reference. The polynomial option would apply to ordered factors and has no role here.
The formula using aptitude * Group includes both main effects and their interaction. Writing IB​,IC​ for the method indicators, its fitted mean has the form
E(Yi​)=β0​+β1​xi​+τB​IB,i​+τC​IC,i​+γB​xi​IB,i​+γC​xi​IC,i​.
(1)
Thus A, B and C have separate intercepts and slopes. There are six coefficients and 21−6=15 residual statistical degrees of freedom. The analysis of variance table uses sequential sums of squares: adding aptitude to an intercept-only model removes 36.57548 from the residual sum of squares; subsequently adding the two group indicators removes 16.93200; adding the two slope interactions removes only 0.66714. The remaining sum of squares is 9.63490, giving mean square 9.63490/15=0.64233.
Each displayed F-test divides the added term's sum of squares by its statistical degrees of freedom and then by this full-model residual mean square. In particular the interaction test is
F=9.63490/150.66714/2​=0.51932,p=0.60524.
(2)
There is no evidence that the slopes differ, so the common-slope model is preferable among the fitted models. The small group p-value concerns differences after including aptitude, whereas the first aptitude row is sequential and does not adjust for Group. It should not be interpreted as the covariate test after treatment adjustment.
The additive formula gives the parallel-lines model
E(Yi​)=β0​+β1​xi​+τB​IB,i​+τC​IC,i​.
(3)
It has four coefficients and 17 residual statistical degrees of freedom. Removing the interaction raises the residual sum of squares to 9.63490+0.66714=10.30204, but lowers the residual mean square to 10.30204/17=0.60600. The sequential aptitude and group sums of squares are unchanged. The adjusted group test is now F=(16.93200/2)/0.60600=13.97024 on (2,17) statistical degrees of freedom, with p=0.0002579: after allowing for initial aptitude, the methods are not all equally effective.
summary(..., cor=F) displays the fitted coefficients, their standard errors, the individual t statistics, and two-sided p-values; it omits the coefficient-correlation matrix. The intercept 2.8367 is the fitted A mean at aptitude zero, which is outside the observed range and is mainly a parametrization device. The common slope 0.7429 predicts about 0.74 extra achievement units per aptitude unit, holding method fixed. Its adjusted t test is 5.2267 on 17 statistical degrees of freedom, with p about 0.0001. The method coefficients are adjusted differences from A, not the B and C intercepts themselves. The three fitted lines are
y​A​=2.8367+0.7429x,y​B​=5.0245+0.7429x,y​C​=4.6979+0.7429x.​
(4)
The B-minus-A estimate is 2.1878 with standard error 0.4545 and t=4.8139; the C-minus-A estimate is 1.8612 with standard error 0.4240 and t=4.3901. Both comparisons are strongly significant.
The B-minus-C comparison needs its own treatment contrast; its significance cannot be read off either comparison with A. Reconstructing the coefficient covariance from the design gives
τB​−τC​=0.32653,se=0.42831,t=0.7624,p≃0.4563.
(5)
Thus there is no detectable difference between B and C. This is consistent with pooling their effects in a further reduced model, but does not establish exact equality.
The residual standard error is 0.60600​=0.7785. The five-number residual summary shows deviations on both sides of zero, with the largest magnitude about 1.74; it does not by itself verify constant variance or normality. The coefficient of determination R2=0.8386 means about 84% of the achievement variation about its overall mean is explained. The overall F=29.43 compares all three nonintercept coefficients with zero, on (3,17) statistical degrees of freedom; its p-value is 5.889×10−7. Printed coefficient p-values rounded to 0.0000 are small, not literally zero.
The following original sketch shows the separate-slope model and the preferred parallel-line fit.
At the same initial aptitude, B and C give higher predicted achievement than A, with no convincing difference between B and C. Interpreting this adjusted association as a causal teaching effect additionally relies on the experimental allocation and error assumptions.

For an exponential dispersion family, differentiating the normalization integral with respect to θ gives an expected score of zero. The score is (Y−b′(θ))/ϕ, hence E(Y)=b′(θ). Differentiating again, the expected squared score is b′′(θ)/ϕ; it is also Var(Y)/ϕ2. Thus
μi​=b′(θi​),Var(Yi​)=ϕb′′(θi​).​
(6)
The canonical link function sets the linear predictor equal to the natural parameter: g(μ)=θ=(b′)−1(μ). The variance function is V(μ)=b′′((b′)−1(μ)), so Var(Yi​)=ϕV(μi​); the dispersion parameter ϕ is separated from the mean-dependent factor.
The saturated statistical model has a separate mean for each response and fits μ​i​=yi​, with boundary parameters interpreted by limits. At the same fixed dispersion, its log likelihood and the fitted model's log-likelihood define the scaled deviance
D∗=2{ℓ(θ;ϕ)−ℓ(θ;ϕ)}.
(7)
The unscaled deviance is
D=ϕD∗=2∑i​{yi​(θi​−θi​)−b(θi​)+b(θi​)}.
(8)
Keeping dispersion fixed in this definition is essential; it is not a comparison with a separately estimated zero variance in a Gaussian saturated fit.
For the normal distribution, expanding its density exponent gives
−2σ2(y−μ)2​−21​log(2πσ2)=σ2yμ−μ2/2​−2σ2y2​−21​log(2πσ2).
(9)
Consequently
θi​=μi​,b(θ)=θ2/2,ϕ=σ2,c(y,ϕ)=−y2/(2ϕ)−21​log(2πϕ).​
(10)
Here V(μ)=1 and the canonical link is the identity, so μi​=βTxi​. The saturated natural parameter is θi​=yi​. Substitution into the deviance formula yields
D=∑i​(yi​−μ​i​)2,D=RSS,D∗=RSS/σ2.​
(11)
For the cholesterol fits, all formulae include an intercept. Model 1 has age and BMI with three coefficients; models 2 and 3 have one of these covariates with two coefficients; model 4 has only the intercept. Their residual statistical degrees of freedom are respectively 27,28,28,29. Their reported deviances are residual sums of squares, because these are Gaussian linear models.
The relevant hypothesis is H0​:βBMI​=0 in the full model, against H1​:βBMI​=0, with age already present. Thus compare model 2 with model 1 using the partial F-test. Its extra sum of squares is 31.64−27.10=4.54, and the common variance is estimated from model 1 by σ2=27.10/27. Therefore
F=27.10/2731.64−27.10​=4.52325∼F1,27​under H0​.
(12)
Since 4.52325>4.210, reject at 5%; the two-sided coefficient-test equivalent has p≃0.0427. BMI is associated with cholesterol after adjustment for age, under the linear Gaussian model. The RSS values alone do not identify the sign of its coefficient or the size of the association.
For comparison, the marginal BMI test would compare model 4 with model 3, giving F=13.88/(35.82/28)=10.8498 on (1,28) statistical degrees of freedom, with p≃0.00268. This does not answer the adjusted question and makes the BMI evidence appear stronger than the conditional test. Age also contributes after BMI: comparing model 3 with model 1 gives F=8.72/(27.10/27)=8.6878 on (1,27) statistical degrees of freedom, with p≃0.00653. Thus retaining both predictors is supported at the stated level.
A raw deviance difference must not simply be compared with the supplied χ12​ cutoff: Gaussian dispersion is unknown and the differences here are unscaled. If σ2 were known, the scaled difference 4.54/σ2 would have a χ12​ law. Estimating it from the full fit gives the exact finite-sample F comparison above, with the appropriate denominator statistical degrees of freedom.

The experimental unit for comparing the treatments is the pot: its two plants form a pair. Let Di​=Ci​−Si​ be cross-fertilised minus self-fertilised height in pot i, for i=1,…,15. The paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test assumes independent differences with a continuous distribution symmetric about a location δ. Its null is δ=0, with the one-sided formulation δ≤0, against δ>0. Under this location model it asks whether cross-fertilisation produces a positive shift within a pot. The location is a median, and also a mean if it exists; a hypothesis merely about a median without symmetry does not justify the signed-rank null distribution.
Test 1 uses this paired test and its exact distribution. Form the fifteen differences, omit any zero differences, rank their absolute values, and sum the statistical ranks belonging to positive differences. That positive-rank sum is the reported V=96. Tied absolute differences receive average statistical ranks. Under the symmetric zero-location null, conditional on their magnitudes, the signs are independent fair signs. The exact upper-tail probability is obtained from all sign allocations to the observed statistical ranks: with fifteen nonzero differences there are 215 equiprobable allocations. For untied absolute differences the rank weights are 1,…,15; ties would require the corresponding conditional rank weights. The reported probability of a positive-rank sum at least as large as observed is 0.0206. No normal approximation is used.
Test 2 makes exactly the same paired comparison and has the same null and alternative hypotheses. exact=F replaces the exact distribution by a normal approximation with a continuity correction. Under an untied null the positive-rank sum has
E(V)=4n(n+1)​,Var(V)=24n(n+1)(2n+1)​.
(24)
More generally, conditional on the nonzero absolute statistical ranks ri​, its variance is 41​∑i​ri2​. The upper-tail correction places the boundary half a lattice unit below the observed statistic. Standardizing gives the reported Z=2.0163, and its approximate p-value is 1−Φ(Z)=0.0219. Thus the slight difference from Test 1 reflects approximation, not a different scientific question.
Test 3 omits paired=T, so it is the unpaired Wilcoxon rank sum test. Its usual distribution-free null is that two independent samples have the same continuous distribution. In a common-shape location model this is a zero location difference, against a positive cross-minus-self shift. More generally “greater” directs the test towards the cross distribution being shifted upwards; it does not turn the test into a general comparison of means or medians for arbitrary unequal shapes.
To form its statistic, pool the thirty heights, rank them together with averaged statistical ranks for ties, and add the cross-group statistical ranks to get RC​. Equivalently use U=RC​−nC​(nC​+1)/2. Under independent untied samples,
E(U)=2nC​nS​​,Var(U)=12nC​nS​(nC​+nS​+1)​.
(25)
If the pooled ties have sizes th​ and N=nC​+nS​, the variance becomes
12nC​nS​​{N+1−N(N−1)∑h​(th3​−th​)​}.
(26)
Subtract the null mean and the upper-tail continuity correction, then divide by this null standard deviation. This procedure gives the reported rank-sum Z=3.0105. Its approximate one-sided probability is 1−Φ(Z)=0.0013. This describes how the statistic and probability are obtained without reconstructing the unprinted heights.
For the statistician, use the paired analysis, preferably the exact Test 1 for these fifteen pairs. The independent units are the pots, while the two heights within a pot share growing conditions and cannot be treated as independent samples. Test 2 is a reasonable approximation to the same paired test and agrees closely. Test 3 discards the matching and uses a null variance that assumes independence between the samples, so its smaller p-value is not a reason to prefer it. The appropriate paired result gives evidence at 5%, but not at 1%, that cross-fertilised plants have a positive height shift relative to their paired self-fertilised plants, subject to the symmetry assumption for differences.

A Gaussian generalised additive model with the identity link specifies
Yi​=β0​+j=1∑p​fj​(xij​)+εi​,εi​ independent N(0,σ2).​
(27)
The fj​ are unknown smooth functions estimated from the data, rather than necessarily straight lines. The model is additive in their effects, so it does not automatically contain interactions between covariates. To identify the intercept separately from the smooths, impose a constraint such as ∑i​fj​(xij​)=0 for each j. Linear terms can also be included alongside smooth terms. More generally a specified link function can make g(E(Yi​)) additive; for the normal identity-link model the mean itself has the displayed form. Smoothness is typically controlled through a basis and a roughness penalty, preventing an unrestricted smooth from interpolating all the noise.

A natural cubic spline interpolant s satisfies s(xi​)=yi​, is twice continuously differentiable, and is a polynomial of degree at most three on each interval [xi​,xi+1​]. Its natural boundary conditions are
s′′(x1​)=s′′(xn​)=0.​
(28)
If defined outside the data interval, it continues linearly on both exterior intervals. Thus function values and the first two derivatives match at the knots, but the third derivative may jump.
For an explicit construction, let hi​=xi+1​−xi​>0 and Mi​=s′′(xi​). Set M1​=Mn​=0 and solve
hi−1​Mi−1​+2(hi−1​+hi​)Mi​+hi​Mi+1​=6{hi​yi+1​−yi​​−hi−1​yi​−yi−1​​},2≤i≤n−1.
(29)
On [xi​,xi+1​], put
s(x)=​6hi​Mi​(xi+1​−x)3+Mi+1​(x−xi​)3​+(yi​−6Mi​hi2​​)hi​xi+1​−x​+(yi+1​−6Mi+1​hi2​​)hi​x−xi​​.​
(30)
This formula interpolates the values and matches second derivatives; the tridiagonal equations enforce first-derivative matching. The homogeneous system has only the zero solution: in a row with maximal nonzero ∣Mi​∣, its diagonal coefficient strictly exceeds the sum of its neighbour coefficients, making cancellation impossible. Thus the natural interpolant exists uniquely.

With the convention that the residual term is a sum rather than an average, the penalised least squares criterion is
Jλ​(g)=i=1∑n​{yi​−g(xi​)}2+λ∫x1​xn​​{g′′(x)}2dx,λ>0.​
(31)
Minimize over functions whose first derivative is absolutely continuous and whose second derivative is square-integrable, that is, the usual Sobolev space H2[x1​,xn​]. The cubic smoothing spline g​λ​ is its minimizer, with natural boundary conditions and linear continuation outside the data range. It is a natural cubic spline with possible knots at the observed xi​, but usually does not interpolate the noisy yi​.
To see why the minimizer is a natural spline, for any g take the natural cubic spline interpolant s through its values g(xi​) and write h=g−s. Then h(xi​)=0. Integrating ∫s′′h′′ by parts on every knot interval twice gives zero: the terms involving h vanish at knots, the terms involving h′ cancel because s′′ is continuous there, and the outer terms vanish by the natural boundary conditions. Hence
∫(g′′)2=∫(s′′)2+∫(h′′)2≥∫(s′′)2,
(32)
while their residual sums of squares agree. Replacing g by s never increases the criterion. This proves the minimum roughness property of the natural cubic spline interpolant needed here.
For fitted values ui​=s(xi​), write ∫(s′′)2=uTKu, where K is positive semidefinite and has the sampled affine functions as its two-dimensional nullspace. The finite-dimensional minimization becomes ∥y−u∥2+λuTKu. Its strictly positive definite Hessian is 2(I+λK), so it has the unique solution
u=Aλ​y,Aλ​=(I+λK)−1.​
(33)
This is the roughness-matrix formula for a natural cubic smoothing spline. Larger λ penalizes curvature more strongly. As λ↓0 the fit approaches interpolation; as λ→∞ it approaches the ordinary least-squares affine line.

Let A=Aλ​ be the influence matrix, so y​=Ay. Its trace is the effective degrees of freedom, accounting for shrinkage of the fitted smooth rather than just counting basis coefficients. The generalised cross-validation score is
GCV(λ)={1−tr(Aλ​)/n}2∥y−Aλ​y∥2/n​={n−tr(Aλ​)}2n∑i​(yi​−y​i​)2​.​
(34)
For a fixed smoothing parameter, the exact leave-one-out residual of this penalized linear fit is (yi​−y​i​)/(1−Aii​), obtained by removing its observation's contribution to the normal equations. Thus Leave-one-out cross-validation uses the average of their squared values. GCV replaces the individual leverages Aii​ by their average tr(A)/n. This avoids separate refits and gives a criterion balancing the small training residuals of a flexible fit against its larger complexity.
Evaluate the score across positive λ, or optimize it numerically, and choose λ minimizing GCV. Then refit or retain the spline at this value. In the full natural smoothing-spline family the trace decreases from n towards 2 as smoothing increases. The selected parameter is intended to give good prediction, rather than necessarily to pass through every point.
