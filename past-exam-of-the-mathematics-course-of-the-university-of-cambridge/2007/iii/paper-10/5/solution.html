The Fredholm index measures the finite-dimensional obstruction to inverting an operator. For a bounded operator T:H1​→H2​ between Hilbert spaces, the Fredholm conditions are that its range of a bounded linear operator is closed and its kernel and cokernel are finite-dimensional. The orthogonal complement of the range is kerT∗, so
indT=dimkerT−dimkerT∗.​
(26)
An invertible linear map has Fredholm index zero; the converse needs an additional finite-dimensional correction, since an operator can have equally large kernel and cokernel.
First construct an inverse up to finite-rank operators. On (kerT)⊥, the restriction of T is a bounded bijection onto its closed range, with bounded inverse by the bounded inverse theorem. Extend that inverse by zero on (ranT)⊥, obtaining S:H2​→H1​. Then
ST=I−PkerT​,TS=I−PkerT∗​.
(27)
Conversely, suppose ST−I and TS−I are compact operators. On kerT, the first identity expresses the identity as a compact operator, so this kernel is finite-dimensional. If T were not bounded below on (kerT)⊥, there would be unit vectors xj​ in that complement with Txj​→0. Write ST=I+K. Compactness of K gives a subsequence for which Kxj​ converges, hence xj​=STxj​−Kxj​ converges to a unit vector x in (kerT)⊥ with Tx=0, a contradiction. This lower bound makes the range closed. Applying the analogous argument to T∗, using S∗T∗=I+K′∗, proves that its kernel is finite-dimensional. This proves the Atkinson theorem: a bounded operator is Fredholm exactly when it has an inverse modulo compact operators. For operators on one Hilbert space this says that its image in the Calkin algebra is invertible.
The Fredholm index is stable. Decompose the domain as N⊕N⊥, where N=kerT, and the target as C⊕R, where C=kerT∗ and R=ranT. A sufficiently small norm perturbation has block matrix
T+E=(ac​bD​),D:N⊥⟶R invertible.
(28)
Multiplying by invertible triangular block matrices reduces this matrix to the direct sum of D and the Schur complement a−bD−1c:N→C. The latter's kernel dimension minus cokernel dimension is dimN−dimC, whatever its rank. Therefore the Fredholm set is open and the index is locally constant. If K is compact, the same parametrix works modulo compact operators for every T+tK, 0≤t≤1. Local constancy along this path gives
ind(T+K)=indT.
(29)
For Fredholm operators A:H1​→H2​ and B:H2​→H3​, multiplication of parametrices shows that BA is Fredholm. Its index is additive. Here is a direct proof, rather than an assertion of additivity. There is an exact sequence of finite-dimensional spaces
0⟶kerA⟶kerBAA​kerB⟶cokerAB​cokerBA⟶cokerB⟶0.
(30)
The map from kerB takes a vector to its class modulo ranA; the next takes the class of y to that of By; the last is the quotient map. For example, a class y+ranA lies in the kernel of the middle map exactly when By=BAx for some x, so y−Ax∈kerB. This verifies the only less immediate exactness assertion; the others follow directly from their definitions. Alternating the dimensions gives
ind(BA)=indB+indA.
(31)
Also indT∗=−indT, and indices add under direct sums. For I+K with K compact, the index is zero, so injectivity is equivalent to surjectivity. More generally Tx=f is solvable exactly when f is orthogonal to kerT∗: these are the finitely many compatibility conditions in the Fredholm alternative.
Toeplitz operators exhibit how this analytic index detects a topological winding. Let H2⊂L2(S1) be the Hardy space of the circle, spanned by 1,z,z2,…, and let P be its orthogonal projection. For a continuous symbol g, define Tg​=PMg​∣H2​, where Mg​ is multiplication by g. Then ∥Tg​∥≤∥g∥∞​. The product defect is
Tf​Tg​−Tfg​=−PMf​(I−P)Mg​∣H2​.
(32)
For trigonometric polynomials the crossing of negative and nonnegative Fourier modes has finite rank. Uniform approximation of continuous symbols by trigonometric polynomials, together with the displayed norm estimate, therefore makes this defect a compact operator for all continuous f,g. If g is nowhere zero, 1/g is continuous and T1/g​ is a two-sided parametrix modulo compact operators. Thus Tg​ is Fredholm.
The converse is instructive. If g(ζ)=0 at ζ∈S1, consider the normalized Hardy kernels
kr,ζ​(z)=1−rζ​z1−r2​​,0<r<1.
(33)
Their norms are one, their individual Fourier coefficients tend to zero as r↑1, and hence they converge weakly to zero. Their squared moduli are the Poisson kernel, so continuity of g gives
∥Tg​kr,ζ​∥22​≤∥gkr,ζ​∥22​=∫S1​∣g(z)∣2∣kr,ζ​(z)∣22π∣dz∣​⟶0.
(34)
A Fredholm operator is bounded below off its finite-dimensional kernel. The projection of these weakly vanishing vectors onto that kernel tends to zero, contradicting this lower bound. Thus a continuous-symbol Toeplitz operator is Fredholm exactly when its symbol never vanishes. Applying the same argument to g−λ gives its essential spectrum as g(S1), with essential spectrum defined by failure of the Fredholm property.
For nonvanishing g, let m=wind(g,0). Lifting a continuous argument of g(eit) on 0≤t≤2π gives an argument change 2πm. After division by zm, its argument has matching endpoints, so there is a continuous function h:S1→C with g(z)=zmeh(z). The nonvanishing-symbol homotopy gt​=zmeth reduces the index to that of Tzm​. For m≥0 this is the mth power of the unilateral shift operator, whose kernel is zero and whose range has codimension m. For m<0 it is the (−m)th power of the adjoint shift, whose kernel has dimension −m and whose range is all of H2. Consequently
indTg​=−wind(g,0).​
(35)
The minus sign comes from counting missing nonnegative Fourier modes. This is a concrete bridge between the Fredholm index, stability under compact perturbations, and the winding number of a loop.
