Expand the derivative by the product rule and insert the integrable weight (1+∣x∣)−n−1; the right side is bounded by finitely many Schwartz seminorms of f. This proves continuity F:S→S. The identical argument with the opposite sign proves continuity of G.
For surjectivity and injectivity an inversion proof is needed. If f∈S, multiply Ff(ξ) by e−ε∣ξ∣2/2. Fubini's theorem and the elementary Gaussian integral give

F:S(Rn)⟶S(Rn) is a continuous bijection, with inverse G.​
(51)
Its inverse is continuous by the seminorm estimate already proved. This establishes the requested bijection without assuming Fourier inversion as an unexplained theorem.
Inversion gives F2f(x)=f(−x) and the Parseval identity. Indeed, inserting the integral for Ff into ∫FfFg​ and using f,Fg∈L1 gives ∫fg​. Density of Schwartz functions in L2 extends F to a unitary operator on L2, the Plancherel theorem. Translation of f becomes multiplication by a phase, modulation becomes frequency translation, and
F(f∗g)=(2π)n/2(Ff)(Fg),F(∂j​f)=iξj​Ff.

These formulas explain why the Fourier transform diagonalizes constant-coefficient differential operators. By transpose it also defines a bijection of the tempered distributions: with the complex-linear distributional pairing, ⟨FT,ϕ⟩=⟨T,Fϕ⟩, since the Fourier kernel is symmetric in its variables.
The connection with creation and annihilation operators is especially transparent for the harmonic oscillator. On the common invariant dense domain S, set
aj​=2​xj​+∂j​​,aj†​=2​xj​−∂j​​.
(53)
Integration by parts shows that these are formal Hilbert-space adjoints. They are closable unbounded operators; commutators below are computed on S, avoiding undefined products on arbitrary L2 vectors. The identity [∂j​,xk​]=δjk​I gives the canonical commutation relations
[aj​,ak†​]=δjk​I,[aj​,ak​]=[aj†​,ak†​]=0.

It also gives
H=−Δ+∣x∣2=2∑j​aj†​aj​+nI.

These same relations show that the Hermite functions are orthonormal: unequal occupations are orthogonal eigenvectors of one of the commuting symmetric number operators aj†​aj​, and the ladder relations recursively give norm one.
For completeness, let g∈L2 be orthogonal to every hα​. The Hermite polynomials have nonzero leading monomials and triangular lower-degree terms, so these orthogonality relations say that all polynomial moments of h(x)=g(x)e−∣x∣2/2∈L1 vanish. Its Fourier integral extends to an entire function of ζ∈Cn:
F(ζ)=∫g(x)e−∣x∣2/2e−ix⋅ζdx.
(58)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, with the Gaussian absorbing every exponential linear weight and every polynomial, justifies differentiation uniformly on compact sets of ζ. Every Taylor coefficient at zero is a vanishing moment, so the entire function is zero. Fourier uniqueness on L1 follows here without circularity: if an L1 function has zero Fourier transform, convolving it with ρε​ gives zero by the Gaussian integral and Fubini, and the approximate-identity limit in L1 gives the original function zero. Thus h=0 and g=0. The Hermite functions form an orthonormal basis of L2.
Finally, the elementary Gaussian integral gives Fh0​=h0​. The derivative and multiplication identities above imply
Faj​=iaj​F,Faj†​=−iaj†​F,
(59)
where the operators on the right act on the frequency variable. Hence
Fhα​=(−i)∣α∣hα​.​
(60)
Thus creation and annihilation organize both the oscillator's spectral decomposition and the four possible Fourier eigenvalues. On Schwartz functions the oscillator powers control weighted derivatives, and Hermite coefficients decay faster than every power of 1+∣α∣; conversely this rapid coefficient decay implies all Schwartz seminorms are finite. The Fourier phases preserve this decay, providing another explanation of the Fourier automorphism of Schwartz space.
