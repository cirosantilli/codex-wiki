On the torus Tn=(R/2πZ)n, choose normalized Haar measure, so that ek​(x)=eik⋅x, k∈Zn, is an orthonormal basis of L2. A periodic Sobolev space is defined, for any real s, by
Hs(Tn)={u:∑k∈Zn​(1+∣k∣2)s∣u(k)∣2<∞},∥u∥Hs2​=∑k​(1+∣k∣2)s∣u(k)∣2.
(36)
For negative s, the coefficients describe distributions rather than necessarily functions. For a nonnegative integer s, the weight is comparable to ∑∣α∣≤s​∣kα∣2, so this norm is equivalent to the sum of the squared L2 norms of all weak derivatives through order s. Fourier truncation proves that smooth trigonometric polynomials are dense. The L2 pairing identifies the continuous dual of Hs with H−s, giving Sobolev duality.
Two Fourier estimates explain the usefulness of these spaces. If s>m+n/2, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
∑k​∣k∣m∣u(k)∣≤∥u∥Hs​(∑k​(1+∣k∣2)s∣k∣2m​)1/2<∞.
(37)
Uniform convergence of the differentiated Fourier series proves the Sobolev embedding Hs↪Cm. If δ>0, a bounded subset of Hs+δ has uniformly small high-frequency tails in Hs:
∑∣k∣>R​(1+∣k∣2)s∣u(k)∣2≤(1+R2)−δ∥u∥Hs+δ2​.
(38)
Finite-dimensional Fourier truncation therefore proves the compact embedding Hs+δ↪Hs, the periodic version of Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorem. In addition, for s>n/2 multiplication is continuous Hs×Hs→Hs, making it a Sobolev algebra. Indeed the weighted coefficient of a product is bounded by the sum of the convolutions of ⟨k⟩s∣u(k)∣ with ∣v(k)∣ and of ∣u(k)∣ with ⟨k⟩s∣v(k)∣; Cauchy-Schwarz puts unweighted coefficients in ℓ1, and the ℓ1∗ℓ2→ℓ2 case of Young's convolution inequality finishes the estimate.
For eigenfunction expansions, distinguish ellipticity from self-adjointness. An arbitrary non-self-adjoint elliptic operator need not have an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions. A useful definite setting is
Lu=−∑i,j​∂i​(aij​(x)∂j​u)+V(x)u,
(39)
with smooth real periodic coefficients, aij​=aji​, real V, and uniform ellipticity
∑i,j​aij​(x)ξi​ξj​≥c∣ξ∣2(c>0).
(40)
Take the self-adjoint realization in L2 with domain H2. Its construction and spectral properties can be seen from the associated form on H1,
b(u,v)=∫(∑i,j​aij​∂j​u∂i​v​+Vuv).
(41)
For a sufficiently large constant C, bC​=b+C(⋅,⋅) is a positive coercive form: bC​(u,u)≥c1​∥u∥H12​, and L+C≥I. The Lax-Milgram theorem supplies the unique weak solution of (L+C)u=f for f∈H−1; the form is Hermitian, which gives symmetry of its inverse on L2.
The regularity input is the periodic elliptic estimate
∥u∥Hs+2​≤Cs​(∥Lu∥Hs​+∥u∥Hs​).
(42)
Here is why an elliptic operator gains two derivatives. With coefficients frozen at a point, its principal symbol ∑aij​ξi​ξj​ dominates c∣ξ∣2, and the estimate follows by multiplying Fourier coefficients by that symbol. On a sufficiently small coordinate patch, the difference of the leading coefficients from these constants has arbitrarily small supremum norm. In the integer-order differentiated estimate, this absorbs the highest derivatives into the left side; derivatives falling on coefficients contribute only lower-order terms. A partition of unity reduces the variable-coefficient problem to such patches. Its commutators with L have order one, controlled by
∥u∥Hs+1​≤ε∥u∥Hs+2​+Cε​∥u∥Hs​,
(43)
which follows by splitting low and high Fourier modes. Absorbing these terms gives the displayed estimate. For a weak H1 solution, the same localization with difference quotients first bounds its second weak derivatives for f∈L2; differentiating and repeating then gives f∈Hs⇒u∈Hs+2 for nonnegative integer s. Interpolation and duality extend the scale to real orders. In particular (L+C)−1:L2→H2 is bounded.
Let R=(L+C)−1. Compactness of H2↪L2 makes R a compact operator. Symmetry of the form makes R self-adjoint, and positivity of L+C makes R positive and injective. Its range is dense because kerR∗=kerR=0. The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators consequently gives an orthonormal basis ϕj​ with
Rϕj​=μj​ϕj​,μj​>0,μj​⟶0,Lϕj​=λj​ϕj​,λj​=μj−1​−C⟶+∞.
(44)
Elliptic regularity bootstraps every ϕj​ to a smooth function. Each eigenvalue has finite multiplicity. Thus a self-adjoint uniformly elliptic second-order operator on the torus has a complete smooth orthonormal eigenfunction expansion with discrete eigenvalues tending to infinity.
Writing uj​=(u,ϕj​), every u∈L2 has u=∑j​uj​ϕj​ in L2, with ∥u∥22​=∑j​∣uj​∣2. Iterating the elliptic estimate shows that the graph norm of (L+C)m is equivalent to the H2m norm. Interpolation and duality give the more general spectral description
∥u∥Hs2​≍j∑​(λj​+C)s∣uj​∣2.​
(45)
The comparison constants depend on the operator and s. In particular smooth functions have coefficients decaying faster than every power of λj​+C, and their expansions converge in every Sobolev norm and hence in every Cm norm.
This gives a practical way to solve elliptic equations. The equation Lu=f is solvable if and only if fj​=0 whenever λj​=0; then one may take uj​=fj​/λj​ on the other eigenspaces and add an arbitrary vector in kerL. The Sobolev description proves the two-derivative gain and the Fredholm alternative, rather than leaving them as formal series manipulations. Similarly the solution of the heat equation ∂t​u+Lu=0 is
u(t)=∑j​e−tλj​uj​(0)ϕj​.
(46)
For every t>0 the exponential decay at high eigenvalues places this solution in every Sobolev space, hence it is smooth.
For the Laplacian −Δ the eigenfunctions are precisely eik⋅x with eigenvalues ∣k∣2. The number with ∣k∣2≤R is the number of lattice points in a radius-R​ ball, asymptotic to its volume by comparison with unit cubes. For the general self-adjoint operator above, bounds on its form above and below by positive multiples of ∥∇u∥22​ plus constants, together with the Courant–Fischer min-max principle, imply λj​+C≍j2/n. Thus the growth reflects dimension and the two-derivative order. Compactness of the torus is essential: on Rn, translated bumps prevent the compact embedding, and the Laplacian instead has continuous spectrum described by the Fourier transform.
