Write C=σˇ=σ∨, S=C∩M, and Uσ​=Xσˇ​=Speck[S]. The semigroup algebra has basis χm=xm, m∈S, with χmχm′=χm+m′. The vertex assumption means that σ is strongly convex, so its dual cone C has full dimension. By the Gordan lemma, S is finitely generated: choose integral toric cone generators and subtract integer parts of their nonnegative coefficients; the remainder lies in a bounded parallelepiped with only finitely many lattice points. This argument also applies when C has lineality space, by including generators in both directions there.
Choose an integral interior point u∈C. For every m∈M, both tu and tu+m belong to S for all sufficiently large integers t. Thus S−S=M. Inverting all monomial generators of k[S] gives the group algebra k[M], which becomes k[z1±1​,…,zn±1​] after a basis of M is chosen. This proves the dense torus of an affine toric variety assertion:
XM​=Speck[M]≅(k∗)n is a dense open subset of Uσ​.​
(1)
Density follows because the localization of a ring is of a domain. The algebra map χm↦χm⊗χm from k[S] to k[M]⊗k[S] defines the extended algebraic torus action; on XM​ it is ordinary multiplication.
For a face of a polyhedral cone ν⪯C, set Iν​=(χm:m∈S∖ν). The convex face property ensures that this is an ideal, and quotienting leaves k[ν∩M]. Hence Xν​=Speck[ν∩M] is a closed subvariety of Uσ​. Its open algebraic torus consists of points where precisely the monomials with exponent in ν are nonzero.
To see that these algebraic tori exhaust the points, let p be a point of Uσ​ and put Sp​={m∈S:χm(p)=0}. Multiplicativity gives m+m′∈Sp​ if and only if both m,m′∈Sp​. Thus Sp​ is a semigroup face. It is S∩ν for a unique polyhedral convex face ν: among a finite generating set of S, take the toric cone spanned by generators nonzero at p. A relation expressing a sum with a generator outside this toric cone as a combination of generators inside would, after clearing rational denominators, contradict the displayed multiplicativity property. Therefore the toric cone is a convex face. Conversely, if m∈S lies in that convex face, a positive multiple of m is an integral sum of those generators, so χm(p) is nonzero as well. This also proves uniqueness.
The point p lies in XM​ exactly when its convex face is all of C. Therefore
Uσ​∖XM​=ν≺C⋃​Xν​.​
(2)
One can equivalently write this as the disjoint union of the open algebraic tori of the proper convex faces. For μ∈S, the same description shows that χμ(p)=0 exactly when μ is not in the convex face supporting p. On every Xν​ with μ∈/ν the monomial vanishes identically. Consequently the monomial zero locus in an affine toric variety is
V(χμ)red​=ν⪯Cμ∈/ν​⋃​Xν​.​
(3)
This is an equality of reduced subvarieties, or of their underlying closed sets. The principal subscheme itself may have multiplicity, as x2=0 already shows on A1.
The face duality for polyhedral cones is the order-reversing pair of maps
τ⪯σ ⟼ C∩τ⊥,ν⪯C ⟼ σ∩ν⊥.​
(4)
Their values are convex faces because the relevant pairings are nonnegative and a sum pairs to zero only if each summand does. More explicitly, finitely many nonnegative exposing functionals can be summed to expose the indicated intersection. For a convex face τ, choose m0​∈C exposing it: τ=σ∩m0⊥​. Then m0​∈C∩τ⊥, so σ∩(C∩τ⊥)⊥⊆τ, while the reverse inclusion is immediate. Apply the same argument to a convex face ν of C, using C∨=σ, to prove the other composite is the identity. Inclusion reversal follows directly from annihilators. This proves the bijection, not just the existence of the maps.
For τ⪯σ, write ν=C∩τ⊥ and L=M∩τ⊥. An exposing functional m0​ for τ is strictly positive on every toric ray outside τ. Small perturbations of m0​ within τ⊥ preserve those finitely many strict inequalities, so ν has nonempty relative interior in τ⊥ and spans it. Therefore ν∩M generates L, by the interior-point argument used above within that subspace. Thus the open algebraic torus of Xν​ is Speck[L]=Xτ⊥​. Here Xτ⊥​ uses the lattice in the whole annihilator, rather than the toric cone semigroup. The sublattice L is a saturated sublattice of M and consequently a direct summand; restriction Hom(M,k∗)→Hom(L,k∗) is surjective. The extended algebraic torus action is multiplication on this open algebraic torus and hence transitive. All other monomials vanish there. It follows that
O(τ)=Xτ⊥​,Fτ​=O(τ)​=XC∩τ⊥​.​
(5)
This is the torus orbit-cone correspondence and its orbit closure in a toric variety description. Under convex face duality, Fγ​=⋃η⪰γ​O(η).
Finally, choose integral mτ​∈C exposing τ. Localizing k[C∩M] at χmτ​ gives k[τ∨∩M]: for m∈τ∨∩M, adding a sufficiently large multiple of mτ​ makes it nonnegative on every toric ray of σ outside τ, while preserving nonnegativity on τ. Hence Xτˇ​=Uτ​=D(χmτ​) is the usual affine open chart inside Uσ​. An orbit O(γ) belongs to it exactly when γ⪯τ. The requested complement of an affine toric face chart is therefore
Xσˇ​∖Xτˇ​=γ⪯σγ⋠τ​⋃​Fγ​.​
(6)
All sets on the right are closed, and if γ is not contained in τ, neither is any convex face containing γ. This justifies replacing the excluded orbits by their whole closures.

First suppose Δ has full dimension in MR​. Form the toric cone over it in MR​⊕R and the graded semigroup algebra
RΔ​=⨁l≥0​ ⨁m∈lΔ∩M​kχmtl,deg(χmtl)=l,
(7)
where degree zero contains only k. The lattice semigroup is finitely generated by the Gordan lemma. The projective toric variety of a lattice polytope is PΔ​=ProjRΔ​. Using all lattice points in all dilates avoids any assumption that the degree-one lattice points generate the ring.
For each vertex v of Δ, the degree-zero part after inverting χvt is
(RΔ​[(χvt)−1])0​=k[cone(Δ−v)∩M].
(8)
Indeed its exponents are m−lv with m∈lΔ. Conversely, every lattice point of the tangent toric cone has this form for sufficiently large integral l. These charts cover the Proj construction: each point of a dilate is a rational convex combination of vertices, so some power of its monomial is a product of vertex monomials. Their dual cones are the vertex toric cones of the inward normal fan of a polytope. The normal toric fan is complete because every linear functional on Δ attains its minimum on a convex face. This describes PΔ​ as a complete toric variety. Translating Δ by m0​∈M gives the graded isomorphism (m,l)↦(m+lm0​,l), so containing the origin is unnecessary. If Δ is not full-dimensional, translate a lattice vertex to zero and use M∩spanR​(Δ−Δ); the construction is then complete of dimension dimΔ.
For the simplex in question put Q=∏i​qi​. Its inequalities are xi​≥0 and ∑i​qi​xi​≤Q, so its inward normal toric rays are R+​ei​ and R+​(−q1​,…,−qn​). Here the ei​ in the dual space denote the standard dual basis. Apply the real linear map A(ei​)=ei​/qi​. It carries these toric rays and their convex face incidences to the standard simplex fan, with toric rays e1​,…,en​,−∑i​ei​. The transported lattice is
N′=i=1⨁n​qi​1​Zei​⊇Zn,PΔ​≅XΣ,N′​.​
(9)
With gcd(q1​,…,qn​)=1, the primitive vectors of this toric fan in N′ are vi​=ei​/qi​ and v0​=−∑i​ei​. The latter is primitive because λ∑i​ei​∈N′ requires each λqi​ to be integral, whose smallest positive solution is λ=1. Their unique primitive relation is
v0​+q1​v1​+⋯+qn​vn​=0.
(10)
Thus mutual coprimality beyond the displayed common gcd condition is not needed.
For a general complete toric variety, introduce one variable zρ​ for each toric ray. Its toric homogeneous coordinate ring, or Cox ring, is
S=k[zρ​:ρ∈Σ(1)],degzρ​=[Fρ​]∈Cl(XΣ​).​
(11)
The toric divisor class sequence is 0→M→ZΣ(1)→Cl(XΣ​)→0, where the first map is m↦(⟨m,vρ​⟩)ρ​. Thus the graded piece Sα​ has basis the monomials ∏zρbρ​​ with bρ​≥0 and ∑bρ​[Fρ​]=α. For an invariant divisor D=∑aρ​Fρ​, monomials in degree [D] correspond to algebraic torus characters χm through bρ​=aρ​+⟨m,vρ​⟩≥0. This also identifies that graded piece with the global sections of O(D).
In our example M′=(N′)∗=⨁i​qi​Zei∗​, and its map to Zn+1 sends ∑i​qi​bi​ei∗​ to (−∑i​qi​bi​,b1​,…,bn​). The quotient is therefore Z, via (c0​,…,cn​)↦c0​+∑i​qi​ci​. In particular degz0​=1 and degzi​=qi​, giving the explicit graded isomorphism
S ∼ ​k[Y0​,Y1q1​​,…,Ynqn​​],z0​↦Y0​,zi​↦Yiqi​​.​
(12)
The target has the grading inherited from ordinary total degree in k[Y0​,…,Yn​]. Its displayed generators are algebraically independent, which proves injectivity as well as surjectivity onto that subring.
To obtain the quotient, the toric irrelevant ideal is generated by ∏ρ∈/σ(1)​zρ​. Here each maximal toric cone omits exactly one toric ray, so it is (z0​,…,zn​), and the open set is An+1∖{0}. The diagonalizable algebraic group with algebraic torus character group Cl(X)=Z is k∗. Its action on the Cox coordinates is dictated by their degrees, namely
λ⋅(y0​,…,yn​)=(λy0​,λq1​y1​,…,λqn​yn​),PΔ​≅P(1,q1​,…,qn​).​
(13)
Here the yi​ name the independent Cox coordinates; the graded-subring realization above does not change their weights. More concretely, the quotient chart where yi​=0 has coordinate ring (S[yi−1​])0​, the invariant ring for this action. These charts glue to ProjS and to the specified toric fan charts. Positive weights make each orbit closed inside the punctured affine space, and in characteristic zero the finite stabilizers cause no failure of this geometric quotient. This is the weighted projective space construction.

Let u,v,w be the consecutive primitive toric ray vectors around the compact invariant curve F=Fv​. Smoothness gives oriented determinants det(u,v)=det(v,w)=1, hence u+w=bv for an integer b. Choose an algebraic torus character m with ⟨m,u⟩=0 and ⟨m,v⟩=1. The principal divisor on a toric variety has coefficient one at F, coefficient b at Fw​, and zero at Fu​. The other invariant curves do not meet F, while each adjacent one meets it transversely once. Intersecting this principal divisor with the proper curve F gives 0=F2+b. This proves the self-intersection formula for a toric surface divisor even when the ambient surface is not complete.
If F2=−1, then b=1 and v=u+w. The vectors u,w form a lattice basis since det(u,w)=1, and their toric cone is the union of the two toric cones adjacent to v. Deleting v is consequently a smooth toric fan coarsening. Its inverse is precisely the toric blowup at a torus-fixed point, obtained by inserting the sum of the two primitive basis vectors. Conversely, that insertion gives u+w=v, and hence algebraic self-intersection −1. Thus the toric contraction of a minus-one curve satisfies
F contracts torically to a smooth point ⟺ F2=−1.​
(14)
For the cyclic quotient description, first take a strongly convex toric cone of dimension two. Write its primitive boundary vectors as a,b and put N0​=Za+Zb, r=[N:N0​]. Since a is primitive, extend it to a basis of N and write b=ua+rc with gcd(u,r)=1. Thus N/N0​ is cyclic of order r. In the lattice N0​, the toric cone is smooth and its toric variety is A2. Passing to the larger lattice N takes the finite quotient whose invariant monomials are precisely those in the smaller dual lattice M=N∗⊂N0∗​. Relative to the two boundary coordinates, c=(b−ua)/r, so a generator acts with weights (−u,1) modulo r. Replacing that generator by a suitable power makes the first weight one and the second some q with 0<q<r and gcd(q,r)=1. Therefore, for the singular case,
Uσ​≅A2/μr​,ζ⋅(x,y)=(ζx,ζqy).​
(15)
The cyclic quotient surface singularity can equivalently be described by the first-quadrant toric cone in
L=Z2+Zr(1,q)​,L∗={(i,j)∈Z2:i+qj≡0(modr)}.
(16)
Indeed its coordinate ring is k[xiyj:i,j≥0, i+qj≡0(modr)]=k[x,y]μr​. Characteristic zero ensures the stated ordinary finite-group quotient interpretation.
The restriction to singular toric cones of dimension two is necessary for r>1. A smooth toric cone has r=1 and gives A2 with the trivial quotient. A toric cone of dimension one gives A1×k∗, and the zero toric cone gives (k∗)2. These latter surfaces cannot be a quotient of the displayed kind, since they have nonconstant invertible regular functions, whereas k[x,y]μr​ has only constant units. Thus the blanket affine-surface wording requires these separate cases.
For the singular case, compute the negative continued fraction by the ceiling form of the Euclidean algorithm. Start with a0​=r, a1​=q, and, while ai​>0, set
bi​=⌈ai​ai−1​​⌉,ai+1​=bi​ai​−ai−1​.
(17)
Then 0≤ai+1​<ai​, so the process terminates at as+1​=0. The gcd is unchanged at each step, hence as​=1. Since ai−1​>ai​>0, every bi​≥2. Rearranging the recurrence gives
qr​=[b1​,…,bs​]−=b1​−b2​−⋯−1/bs​1​1​.​
(18)
Now define lattice vectors
v0​=(0,1),v1​=r(1,q)​,vi+1​=bi​vi​−vi−1​.
(19)
To verify this Hirzebruch–Jung resolution algorithm, write vi​=(pi​,ai​)/r with p0​=0, p1​=1 and pi+1​=bi​pi​−pi−1​. Induction gives ai​≡qpi​(modr), so vi​∈L. It also gives pi​ai+1​−ai​pi+1​=−r. Thus every adjacent pair has absolute determinant 1/r, the covolume of L, and is a basis of L. The pi​ increase strictly, while the ai​ decrease to zero, so these toric rays occur in order inside the first quadrant. At termination, as​=1 and the determinant identity gives ps+1​=r, hence vs+1​=(1,0). All boundary and inserted vectors are primitive because they occur in lattice bases.
Subdivide the quadrant by v1​,…,vs​. Every resulting toric cone is smooth by the smoothness criterion for a toric variety, and the fan subdivision gives a proper birational toric morphism, hence a resolution. Each interior toric ray has a complete one-dimensional star, so its exceptional curve of a surface resolution is P1. The recurrence yields
Fi2​=−bi​≤−2,Fi​⋅Fi+1​=1,Fi​⋅Fj​=0 (∣i−j∣>1).​
(20)
This proves minimality: no exceptional curve of a surface resolution can be contracted to a smooth point by the Castelnuovo contraction criterion. There is also a direct toric fan check. For fixed i, the determinants of vi​ with vi+h​, measured in units of the lattice covolume, satisfy the same recurrence with coefficients at least two and initial values zero and one. They are at least h. Omitting any intervening toric ray therefore leaves a toric cone of determinant at least two, so no nontrivial coarsening is smooth. Thus this is the minimal resolution of a cyclic quotient surface singularity.
If all exceptional algebraic self-intersections are −2, all bi​=2 and induction gives [2,…,2]−=(s+1)/s. Since r,q are coprime, this forces r=s+1, q=s. Conversely r/(r−1) has exactly r−1 coefficients equal to two. Therefore
Fi2​=−2 for every exceptional curve ⟺ q=r−1.​
(21)
For the final toric fan, the minimal smooth fan subdivision of the toric cone from (0,1) to (r,1) inserts the toric rays (j,1) for 1≤j<r. Adjacent determinants have absolute value one, and (j−1,1)+(j+1,1)=2(j,1), so its inserted curves have algebraic self-intersection −2 and it is the minimal fan subdivision just described. The second original toric cone, from (r,1) to (1,0), is already smooth.
Write vj​=(j,1) and e=(1,0). In the resolved toric fan, the rightmost interior toric ray obeys vr−1​+e=vr​, so its curve has algebraic self-intersection −1 and can be blown down to a smooth point. After deleting it, the same relation vr−2​+e=vr−1​ applies to the new rightmost interior toric ray. Continue deleting vr​,vr−1​,…,v1​ in that order. Each deletion is the inverse of a toric blowup at a torus-fixed point. The last toric fan has only the quadrant toric cone and defines A2. Consequently
XΣ′​⟶A2 is the composite of exactly r smooth-point toric blowdowns.​
(22)
All maps induce the identity on the dense algebraic torus, so this composite is the same morphism as XΣ′​→XΣ​→A2. The intermediate smooth toric fans need not include the singular toric fan Σ.

The dense algebraic torus has coordinate ring k[M], a Laurent polynomial ring and hence a unique factorization domain. Its divisor class group is therefore zero. Restrict an arbitrary Weil divisor D to the algebraic torus and choose a rational function on an algebraic variety f whose divisor there is D∣T​. Then D−div(f) is supported on the algebraic torus boundary. The prime divisors in that boundary are precisely the Fj​ corresponding to the toric rays, by the torus orbit-cone correspondence. Consequently
D∼j=1∑d​aj​Fj​for some aj​∈Z.​
(23)
Since the variety is smooth, every Weil divisor is Cartier. This gives invariant representatives for all divisor classes, without assuming that the original divisor was invariant.
Now use such a representative. The associated vector space is
L(D)={f∈k(X)∗:div(f)+D≥0}∪{0}=H0(X,OX​(D)).
(24)
The principal divisor on a toric variety formula gives div(χm)=∑j​⟨m,ej​⟩Fj​. Define the lattice polytope of a toric divisor
PD​={m∈MR​:⟨m,ej​⟩≥−aj​ for every j}.
(25)
For a maximal toric cone σ, its basic toric ray vectors are a basis of N, so there is a unique integral mσ​ with ⟨mσ​,ej​⟩=−aj​ on its toric rays. The algebraic torus character χmσ​ is a local frame for O(D) on Uσ​. Every global section restricts on the algebraic torus to a finite Laurent polynomial ∑m​cm​χm. Relative to this frame, regularity on Uσ​ means ∑m​cm​χm−mσ​∈k[σ∨∩M]. Since distinct algebraic torus characters are independent, this holds exactly when each exponent with nonzero coefficient satisfies the inequalities for the toric rays of σ. Taking all charts proves
L(D)=m∈PD​∩M⨁​kχm.​
(26)
Completeness makes PD​ bounded when nonempty: a nonzero recession vector would be nonnegative on every toric ray of a complete toric fan, and therefore on all of NR​, which is impossible. The displayed space is consequently finite-dimensional.
At the torus-fixed point pσ​ in the smooth chart Uσ​≅An, a section χm has local monomial χm−mσ​. Its value is nonzero exactly when this monomial is constant, namely when m=mσ​. Thus if the complete linear system of a divisor has no base point, some section is nonzero at pσ​, forcing mσ​∈PD​. Conversely, if mσ​∈PD​ for every maximal toric cone, its algebraic torus character is a global section whose local expression on Uσ​ is one. It therefore vanishes nowhere on that whole chart, and these charts cover X. This proves the toric basepoint-free criterion:
∣D∣ is basepoint-free ⟺ ∀σ∈Σ(n), ⟨mσ​,ej​⟩≥−aj​ for all j, with equality on σ.​
(27)
The analogous toric ampleness criterion, stated without proof, replaces the inequalities off σ by strict ones:
D is ample ⟺ ∀σ∈Σ(n), ⟨mσ​,ej​⟩=−aj​ on σ,⟨mσ​,ej​⟩>−aj​ off σ.​
(28)
Equivalently, the normal fan of a polytope PD​ is exactly Σ.
For the final fan subdivision, take n≥2 so that e~ is a new toric ray, as the construction requires. The vectors e1​,…,en​ are a lattice basis, so their sum is primitive. Replacing any one basis vector by this sum gives another basis. Thus the star subdivision gives a smooth toric blowup at a torus-fixed point π:XΣ′​→XΣ​, with algebraic exceptional divisor E=F~. On the old chart, a local equation of D is χ−mσ​, whose order on the new toric ray is −⟨mσ​,e~⟩=∑i=1n​ai​=a. All old toric ray coefficients are unchanged. Therefore
D′=π∗D.​
(29)
We prove ampleness of Dc​=cD′−E directly by inequalities, giving the ample divisor after blowing up a torus-fixed point result.
Let u1​,…,un​ be the basis of M dual to e1​,…,en​. On the new maximal toric cone σi​, which omits ei​, the required local algebraic torus character is
mi,c​=cmσ​+ui​.
(30)
Indeed its pairings on ej​, j=i, are −caj​, while its pairing on e~ is −ca+1, exactly the negative of the new coefficient ca−1. Its inequality on the omitted old toric ray has strict gap one:
⟨mi,c​,ei​⟩+cai​=1.
(31)
For any toric ray ej​ outside the original toric cone, put δj​=⟨mσ​,ej​⟩+aj​. The original ampleness gives positive integers δj​, and the new strict gaps are
⟨mi,c​,ej​⟩+caj​=cδj​+⟨ui​,ej​⟩>0
(32)
for all sufficiently large c.
For any unchanged maximal toric cone η=σ, the local algebraic torus character is cmη​. All strict inequalities on old toric rays follow from those for D. Its inequality on the new toric ray has gap
⟨cmη​,e~⟩+ca−1=cϵη​−1,ϵη​=∑i=1n​(⟨mη​,ei​⟩+ai​).
(33)
Each summand is nonnegative, and at least one is positive because η=σ cannot contain all its toric rays. Hence ϵη​ is a positive integer, making this gap positive for c≥2. There are only finitely many remaining inequalities. For example, any integer satisfying
c>max{1, max1≤i≤nj>n​​δj​−⟨ui​,ej​⟩​}
(34)
works, with the inner maximum omitted when its index set is empty. The toric ampleness criterion now gives
cD′−F~ is ample for every sufficiently large integer c.​
(35)
The new-ray assumption is essential to the printed construction. In dimension one, e~=e1​ and the fan subdivision is unchanged, so the two purported distinct divisors coincide. If the displayed definition of D′ is applied literally on P1 with coefficients a1​=−2, a2​=3, then D has degree one but D′ has degree −1, contradicting the conclusion. The argument above applies to the intended blowup in dimension at least two.
