Use the real vector space of Hermitian matrices and its Hilbert-Schmidt inner product ⟨A,B⟩=Tr(AB). A Hermitian N-by-N matrix has N real diagonal entries and two real parameters for each off-diagonal pair, hence real dimension N+2(2N​)=N2. Imposing zero trace removes one real dimension. Each supplied generalized Pauli matrix is Hermitian and traceless, and there are N(N−1) off-diagonal matrices and N−1 diagonal matrices, totaling N2−1.
Their assumed orthonormality makes them linearly independent. Since this equals the dimension of the traceless space, they are an orthonormal basis of the traceless Hermitian matrices. To extend the family, the required additional generator is σ0​=I/N​, which is implicit in the indexing of the question. It has unit Hilbert-Schmidt norm and is orthogonal to every traceless generator, so the extended family is an orthonormal basis of all Hermitian matrices.

Take any rank-one orthogonal projection P and its generalized Bloch vector s. The antipodal vector −s is on the same sphere, but its corresponding trace-one Hermitian matrix is
ρ−​=NI​−(P−NI​)=N2​I−P.
(5)
On the range of P, this matrix has eigenvalue 2/N−1, which is negative for N>2. It is therefore not a positive semidefinite matrix and cannot be a density operator. Hence the pure states do not cover the sphere when N>2. This also shows directly why the purity bound for generalized Bloch vectors does not by itself guarantee a physical quantum state.

Write the unitary conjugation as ρ(t)=U(t)ρ(0)U(t)†. The identity component is unchanged, and the generalized Bloch vector transforms by
sk​(t)=∑ℓ​Okℓ​(U(t))sℓ​(0),Okℓ​(U)=Tr(σk​Uσℓ​U†).

The individual rates describe population relaxation: γ12​ transfers population from level one to level two, and γ21​ transfers it back. With p1​+p2​=1 and the conventional qubit coordinate rz​=p1​−p2​,
p˙​1​=−γ12​p1​+γ21​p2​,r˙z​=−(γ12​+γ21​)rz​+(γ21​−γ12​).
(10)
Thus T1−1​=γ12​+γ21​ is the longitudinal relaxation rate. The parameter Γ=T2−1​ is the transverse relaxation rate: it damps the off-diagonal coherence, or the x,y components of the Bloch vector. In the usual completely positive two-level model,
Γ=21​(γ12​+γ21​)+γϕ​,γϕ​≥0,
(11)
so Γ includes both the coherence loss caused by population relaxation and additional pure dephasing.
There is a normalization switch in the displayed qubit Affine Bloch equation. Its constant term is appropriate to the conventional Pauli matrices and unit-radius coordinate rk​=Tr(ρσkPauli​). With the orthonormal generators used earlier, s=r/2​, the same physical rates would instead give a constant term (γ21​−γ12​)/2​. The drift matrix is unchanged. We interpret the supplied qubit equation in its conventional r coordinates.
With zero controls and g=γ12​+γ21​>0, Γ>0, the steady state is unique:
r∗​=(0,0,(γ21​−γ12​)/g),ρ∗​=diag(γ21​/g,γ12​/g).​
(12)

The formal case g>0, Γ=0 permits extra transverse equilibrium coordinates, but is excluded by the completely positive rate bound above.

A real Lie algebra is a real vector space with a bilinear antisymmetric bracket satisfying the Jacobi identity. For matrices, the bracket is the commutator [X,Y]=XY−YX. The Dynamical Lie algebra is
g=LieR​{iH0​,iH1​,…,iHm​}⊆u(N).
(14)
It is found by repeatedly adjoining commutators and taking real linear spans until no new independent generators appear. Let G be its connected dynamical Lie group. Under the standard assumptions of freely timed controls with unrestricted real amplitudes, the following are the relevant Lie criteria.
For pure-state controllability, G must act transitively on normalized state vectors, or on their rays when global phase is disregarded. The standard finite-dimensional classification is, up to a unitary change of basis,
g=u(N),su(N),or, for N=2n,sp(n),sp(n)⊕RiI.
(15)
Here sp(n) is the compact symplectic Lie algebra in its defining complex 2n-dimensional representation. Equivalently, the infinitesimal action spans the tangent directions to the whole pure-state orbit. The symplectic cases are important because pure-state reachability alone need not give complete mixed-state reachability.
For density operator controllability, the action must be transitive on every unitary orbit of a density operator, meaning on every fixed-spectrum class. The criterion is g=su(N) or u(N). This concerns all density operators, not merely one unusually degenerate spectrum.
For exact unitary operator controllability, the criterion is g=u(N). If only the physical gate up to global phase matters, it is enough that the image after removing the scalar direction is su(N), equivalently that g+RiI=u(N). A traceless system can implement all special-unitary gates while lacking independent control of the overall phase.

Let P0​,P1​ be any two rank-one orthogonal projections. Choose 0<q<1/N and p=1−(N−1)q, so p>q, and form the mixed density operators
ρj​=qI+(p−q)Pj​.
(23)
They have the same spectrum. If density operator controllability holds, a reachable U obeys Uρ0​U†=ρ1​, and subtraction of qI gives UP0​U†=P1​. Thus density operator controllability implies pure-state controllability, even if its definition is phrased using only mixed density operators.
The converse is false in general. For N=4, the full compact symplectic group Sp(2) is transitive on the complex unit sphere: identify C4 with H2, extend any quaternionic unit vector to an orthonormal quaternionic basis, and map one such basis to another. This gives pure-state controllability. Yet the invariant in part (d) prevents it from connecting the indicated isospectral mixed density operators, so it fails density operator controllability. This counterexample uses the full symplectic group, not an assertion that every parameter choice in part (c) generates it.
For N=2, the converse is true. Every density operator has the form ρ=qI+(p−q)P for a rank-one P, with p+q=1. If p=q, pure-state control of P controls its entire fixed-spectrum unitary orbit of a density operator. If p=q=1/2, the orbit contains only I/2. Therefore the two controllability notions coincide for a qubit. The Lie classification gives the same conclusion, since Sp(1)=SU(2).

The claimed universality is false for the particular gate specified in part (d). Every allowed single-qubit gate is local, and Cphase​=−Z⊗Z is local too. Products of these gates remain of the form eiχA⊗B. They preserve all product states and cannot, for example, implement the CNOT gate that sends ∣+⟩⊗∣0⟩ to (∣00⟩+∣11⟩)/2​. An always-on Ising coupling produces evolution during every nominally local pulse, and generally does not commute with the applied x,y Quantum Hamiltonians. A naive product of single-qubit rotations therefore accumulates unwanted conditional phases and can entangle the qubits. Idling is also no longer an identity operation.
For a pulse of duration τ, neglecting the coupling requires ∣J12​∣τ≪1 in units ℏ=1, or ∣J12​∣τ/ℏ≪1 in ordinary units. For order-one rotation angles, this is the strong-local-drive regime ∣B∣≫∣J12​∣. Large drive amplitude alone does not justify neglect over a long total sequence: the accumulated interaction time or an appropriate error bound must also be small.
If it cannot be neglected, include it in the quantum optimal control model, or refocus it. A π pulse P=Rx(1)​(π) obeys P(Z⊗Z)P†=−Z⊗Z, giving the ideal Ising spin echo
e−iHI​τ/2Pe−iHI​τ/2P†=I.
(35)
This cancellation assumes the refocusing pulses are instantaneous relative to 1/∣J12​∣, or that the coupling during finite pulses is compensated. It explains how fast local controls can suppress the fixed coupling without mistaking it for a controllable zero interaction.

An open-loop control is computed from a dynamical model and the initial preparation, then applied without changing it in response to measurements during that run. Controllability determines which transformations are possible in principle; field design must also account for duration, available control generators, amplitude and bandwidth limits, and calibration errors.
Three useful model-based strategies are the following. Resonant quantum control chooses carrier frequencies and phases to select transitions, and uses quantum pulse area or elementary rotation gates to compile the desired transformation. Adiabatic quantum control chooses a slowly varying path of Quantum Hamiltonians whose relevant eigenstate connects the initial and target states, maintaining a sufficiently large spectral gap. Quantum optimal control parametrizes or varies the fields and maximizes a fidelity or observable objective, while penalizing resources and enforcing the dynamical equations. These can all produce predetermined laboratory waveforms; experimental feedback is a separate design choice.
For a detailed variational construction, let
H(u,t)=H0​+∑k=1m​uk​(t)Hk​,iℏψ˙​=H(u,t)ψ,ψ(0)=ψ0​.
(36)
Choose a positive target observable O, for example O=∣ψd​⟩⟨ψd​∣ for pure-state transfer. Maximize
J[u]=⟨ψ(T)∣O∣ψ(T)⟩−21​∑k​λk​∫0T​uk​(t)2dt,λk​>0.
(37)
The terminal term is the target probability or observable expectation, and the quadratic integral penalizes field energy. A costate χ(t) enforces the Schrödinger equation through the real augmented functional
J=J−2Re∫0T​⟨χ​ψ˙​+ℏi​H(u,t)ψ⟩dt.
(38)
Variation in χ recovers the state equation. Integrating the variation in ψ˙​ by parts gives the terminal boundary term 2Re⟨Oψ(T)−χ(T)∣δψ(T)⟩ and the interior adjoint equation. Thus the Euler-Lagrange conditions are
iℏψ˙​=Hψ,ψ(0)=ψ0​;iℏχ˙​=Hχ,χ(T)=Oψ(T).​
(39)
The costate obeys the same Hamiltonian equation but is propagated backward from its terminal value. The field variation is
δJ=∑k​∫0T​{ℏ2​Im⟨χ(t)∣Hk​∣ψ(t)⟩−λk​uk​(t)}δuk​(t)dt.
(40)
Hence the variational costate gradient for Hamiltonian quantum control is
gk​(t)=δuk​(t)δJ​=ℏ2​Im⟨χ(t)∣Hk​∣ψ(t)⟩−λk​uk​(t).
(41)
At an unconstrained stationary field,
uk​(t)=ℏλk​2​Im⟨χ(t)∣Hk​∣ψ(t)⟩.​
(42)
These equations couple forward and backward boundary data, so this is not a single initial-value integration, nor a direct feedback law for an unknown experimental state.
A practical direct-adjoint looping algorithm starts from an admissible trial field. Propagate ψ forward and store its trajectory; set χ(T)=Oψ(T) and propagate χ backward; compute gk​ along the two trajectories; then update uknew​=uk​+ηgk​ for a positive step size η. A line search evaluates the actual objective and decreases η until the ascent condition is met. Project into the admissible amplitude range or optimize a finite Fourier or pulse basis to enforce bandwidth constraints. Repeat until the projected gradient and change in objective are small. Repeated initializations help explore different local optima; the stationarity equations alone do not guarantee a global optimum.
For a piecewise constant discretization, define Hj​=H0​+∑k​uk,j​Hk​ and Uj​=e−iΔtHj​/ℏ. Forward multiplication gives the state trajectory; backward multiplication gives the costate. The exact matrix-exponential derivative is
∂uk,j​∂Uj​​=−ℏi​∫0Δt​e−i(Δt−s)Hj​/ℏHk​e−isHj​/ℏds.
(43)
This supplies accurate discrete gradients even when Hj​ and Hk​ do not commute. The small-step expression ∂Uj​/∂uk,j​≃−iΔtHk​Uj​/ℏ is only an approximation. Forward/backward factorization is the basis of gradient ascent pulse engineering. For a full-gate objective, propagate all basis columns or U(t) itself and use a terminal objective such as the phase-insensitive unitary gate error; optimizing one state alone does not implement a prescribed operation on every input.
To implement the result, convert the optimized envelope and quadratures into the calibrated electric or magnetic drive fields. An arbitrary-waveform source can set time-domain quadratures; optical spectral pulse shaping applies a complex mask to the available pulse spectrum,
Eout​(ω)=M(ω)Ein​(ω),
(44)
using, for example, a 4f pulse shaper and spatial light modulator. Finite spectral support, mask resolution and actuator limits must be included in the optimization: smoothing an unconstrained optimum afterward may spoil it. Simulating the calibrated final pulse, optionally over an ensemble of detunings or calibration errors, checks its predicted fidelity and robustness. The computed fields are then executed as open-loop control, with all measurements used for later calibration rather than an assumed instantaneous state readout.

Hcouple​=i∫dωκ(ω)(a+a†)⊗(bω†​−bω​),
(45)
with real coupling amplitude κ. The free Quantum Hamiltonian is H0​=ωc​a†a+∫ωbω†​bω​dω. In the interaction picture, the two photon-exchange products abω†​ and a†bω​ acquire phases with frequency ω−ωc​, whereas a†bω†​ and abω​ oscillate at ω+ωc​.

HI​(t)=i∫dωκ(ω){a⊗bω†​ei(ω−ωc​)t−a†⊗bω​e−i(ω−ωc​)t}.

Under the Markov approximation, a short incoming time bin is independent of the earlier input and commutes with adapted cavity operators. Write its quantum noise increment as dBin​(t)=∫tt+dt​b(τ)dτ; this is the increment denoted Bin​(t) in the short-time definition, not an accumulated integral from zero.
During this bin, use the cavity operator at its left endpoint. Integrating the cavity-reservoir interaction and applying U=e−i∫HI​dτ yields the bin propagator
UI​(t+dt,t)=exp[γ​{a(t)⊗dBin†​(t)−a†(t)⊗dBin​(t)}].​
(48)
The exponent is anti-Hermitian, so the bin evolution is unitary. This is the short-time stochastic form: the time-bin limit, or equivalently the time-ordered product of these adapted propagators, gives continuous evolution. The quantum noise increments scale as dt​; therefore one must retain the square of this exponent, rather than treating its integral as an ordinary order-dt bounded Hamiltonian.

For a specified negative-feedback loop with return ratio L(s), let P be its number of open-right-half-plane poles. Let Ncw​ be the signed clockwise encirclement count of −1 by the standard Nyquist stability criterion contour. Provided the contour does not hit a singularity or the critical point, the number of right-half-plane closed-loop poles is Z=P+Ncw​. Thus closed-loop stability requires Ncw​=−P and no closed-loop pole on the imaginary axis, with the usual well-posedness and absence of hidden unstable cancellations. For an already stable return ratio, there must be no encirclement of −1 and no passage through it.
The open cavity itself, for γ>0, has state eigenvalue and transfer-function pole −γ/2. Its initial transient decays and its impulse response is a direct-feedthrough term plus a decaying exponential. Hence the open-loop cavity is asymptotically stable and its transfer function is BIBO stable. The right-half-plane zero at +γ/2 does not make the open cavity unstable. On the frequency axis,
G(iω)=ω2+(γ/2)2ω2−(γ/2)2+iγω​,∣G(iω)∣=1.
(59)
Its unit-circle plot passes through −1 at ω=0. A Nyquist stability criterion is a feedback criterion; this passage is not by itself a test of open-plant stability.
If the intended interpretation is a unit negative-feedback loop with L=G, then
1+G(s)=s+γ/22s​,1+G(s)G(s)​=2ss−γ/2​.
(60)
That loop has a pole at zero, so unit negative feedback is marginal rather than asymptotically stable, and the critical-point passage correctly prevents a strict Nyquist stability conclusion. Without a specified feedback interconnection, the unambiguous conclusion is the stable open cavity, not that particular closed loop.
