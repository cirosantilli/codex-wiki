In this static scalar problem, a classical field-theory soliton is a nontrivial smooth localized finite-energy field configuration which is static in its rest frame and stable against admissible small perturbations. Moving examples are obtained by a Lorentz boost. The relevant static energy is
E[ϕ]=T+V,T=21​∫RD​∣∇ϕ∣2dDx,V=∫RD​U(ϕ)dDx.
(1)
Use the usual vacuum normalization U≥0, with zero energy at a vacuum, and impose finite-energy vacuum behavior at spatial infinity. These assumptions, together with the unconstrained real target and the canonical two-derivative kinetic term, are the hypotheses of the following Derrick theorem argument.
For Derrick scaling ϕλ​(x)=ϕ(λx), change variables to obtain
E(λ)=λ2−DT+λ−DV.
(2)
A static solution must be stationary under this admissible scale variation. The Derrick virial identity is
(2−D)T−DV=0.
(3)
For D>2, both terms are nonpositive and the gradient term is strictly negative for a nonconstant field, so no nontrivial static solution is possible. At D=2, the identity forces V=0, but the gradient term is scale invariant and a further argument is needed.
Since U≥0, V=0 implies U(ϕ(x))=0 everywhere. Every zero of a differentiable nonnegative potential has U′=0. The static Euler-Lagrange field equation Δϕ=U′(ϕ) therefore makes ϕ a harmonic function. Each derivative ∂i​ϕ is harmonic and belongs to L2 space by finite energy. Its mean-value property and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on a ball of radius R imply
∣∂i​ϕ(x)∣2≤volBR​1​∫BR​(x)​∣∂i​ϕ∣2dDy≤volBR​2T​.
(4)
Letting R→∞ gives ∂i​ϕ=0. This proves the two-dimensional flat-target Derrick obstruction. Thus a nontrivial static real-scalar soliton in this class can occur only for D=1. The argument does not apply unchanged to curved-target sigma models or gauge-field energies.
In one dimension the scale identity is T=V, and the second scale derivative is 2V>0 for a nontrivial solution; scale variation does not rule it out. To derive the Bogomolny equations, write U=21​W′(ϕ)2 on the field interval of interest. Completing the square gives
E=21​∫−∞∞​(ϕ′∓W′)2dx±[W(ϕ(+∞))−W(ϕ(−∞))].
(5)
Therefore the Bogomolny bound and its saturation equation are
E≥∣ΔW∣,ϕ′=±W′(ϕ).​
(6)
Equivalently, for a monotone field connecting adjacent vacua, ϕ′=±2U(ϕ)​, with the sign chosen for its direction. Differentiating the first-order equation gives ϕ′′=W′W′′=U′, verifying the static Euler-Lagrange field equation. Saturation minimizes energy within the fixed-boundary topological sector.
For the given sextic potential, factorization gives U(ϕ)=ϕ2(ϕ2−4)2, so the vacua are −2,0,2. Choose
W′(ϕ)=2​ϕ(4−ϕ2),W(ϕ)=2​(2ϕ2−4ϕ4​).
(7)
This choice satisfies U=W′2/2. On 0<ϕ<2, the increasing kink in a phi-six model satisfies ϕ′=2​ϕ(4−ϕ2). Setting y=ϕ2 gives y′=22​y(4−y). Separation gives
log4−yy​=82​(x−x0​),
(8)
and hence
ϕ0→2​(x)=1+e−82​(x−x0​)​2​,E=42​.​
(9)
The endpoints are zero and two, and the mass follows from W(2)−W(0)=42​. The other increasing elementary scalar-field kink is
ϕ−2→0​(x)=−1+e82​(x−x0​)​2​,E=42​.​
(10)
It uses the opposite sign of W′ and has the same mass. Reflecting x−x0​ yields the two decreasing antikinks; x0​ is the free center.
There is no single finite-width static kink directly connecting −2 to 2. Multiplying the static equation by ϕ′ gives the first integral ϕ′2/2−U=0 for a finite-energy vacuum-to-vacuum solution. At the intermediate vacuum ϕ=0, this forces ϕ′=0. Uniqueness for the smooth second-order field equation makes data (ϕ,ϕ′)=(0,0) stay at that vacuum, so a nontrivial solution cannot cross it at finite x. This is the intermediate-vacuum obstruction to a kink; the two elementary transitions can only separate by an infinite interval.

A nonlinear sigma model describes a field taking values in a Riemannian target rather than an unconstrained linear space. Its static configurations are harmonic maps, critical points of the Dirichlet energy. The standard example for sigma-model lumps is the O3 nonlinear sigma model in two spatial dimensions: a unit vector n(x1​,x2​)∈S2 has
E[n]=21​∫R2​(∣∂1​n∣2+∣∂2​n∣2)d2x.
(11)
The same target is the complex projective line, giving the CP1 nonlinear sigma model description. A lump is a localized finite-energy static solution, whose existence and minimum energy are governed by the degree of its field map.
Impose a fixed angle-independent value n∞​ at spatial infinity and require the field to extend smoothly there. The one-point compactification of the plane is a sphere, so the configuration becomes a map S2→S2. Its topological degree is the integer
N=4π1​∫R2​n⋅(∂1​n×∂2​n)d2x.
(12)
This is the degree charge of an O3 sigma-model lump: the integrand is the pullback of the oriented unit-sphere area form, normalized by its area 4π. Equivalently, the degree counts inverse images of a regular target point with orientation signs. It is unchanged under smooth homotopy with the prescribed boundary value. Consequently fields of different degree belong to distinct topological sectors.
Topology also determines an energy lower bound. The derivatives are tangent to the sphere, so ∣n×∂2​n∣=∣∂2​n∣. Since
∂1​n⋅(n×∂2​n)=−n⋅(∂1​n×∂2​n), square completion gives
E=21​∫∣∂1​n±n×∂2​n∣2d2x±4πN.
(13)
Choosing the sign of N proves the Bogomolny degree bound for the O3 sigma model
E≥4π∣N∣.​
(14)
For positive degree, equality holds precisely when ∂1​n+n×∂2​n=0; for negative degree the opposite sign applies. These are the Bogomolny equations for the lump. A saturating field minimizes energy in its degree sector and hence solves the static field equation. Explicitly, that equation is Δn+∣∇n∣2n=0, obtained by varying under n2=1.
The first-order equations become especially simple in stereographic projection. Use z=x1​+ix2​ and
n=1+∣w∣2(2Rew,2Imw,1−∣w∣2)​,∂z​=21​(∂1​−i∂2​),∂zˉ​=21​(∂1​+i∂2​).
(15)
The round target metric is 4dwdwˉ/(1+∣w∣2)2. In the one-half energy normalization used here, the stereographic energy of the O3 sigma model and degree are
E=4∫(1+∣w∣2)2∣wz​∣2+∣wzˉ​∣2​d2x,N=π1​∫(1+∣w∣2)2∣wz​∣2−∣wzˉ​∣2​d2x.
(16)
Thus E−4πN=8∫∣wzˉ​∣2/(1+∣w∣2)2. Positive-degree lumps are holomorphic maps, wzˉ​=0, and negative-degree lumps are antiholomorphic maps, wz​=0.
A holomorphic sphere map is meromorphic in a stereographic coordinate and hence a rational map w=p(z)/q(z) with coprime polynomials. To see why the rational form suffices, its finitely many coordinate poles can be removed by subtracting their principal parts, leaving a holomorphic function on the compact sphere and therefore a constant. The degree of a rational map of the Riemann sphere is
N=max(degp,degq)​
(17)
after canceling common factors. A generic target value c has inverse images solving p−cq=0, with the root at infinity counted in homogeneous coordinates when necessary; the number is N, and holomorphic local degrees are positive. Poles of w do not make the physical field singular: the reciprocal target coordinate 1/w is regular there. Antiholomorphic rational maps have the opposite topological degree and the same minimum energy.
For example, the unit sigma-model lump and size modulus is
w(z)=z−z0​ρ​,ρ=0,E(z)=(∣z−z0​∣2+∣ρ∣2)24∣ρ∣2​.
(18)
Its degree is one and radial integration gives E=4π. The complex center z0​ supplies two position parameters, while the magnitude and phase of ρ give size and internal orientation. More generally, fixing w(∞)=0 allows a monic degree-N denominator and a numerator of degree at most N−1, with no common roots. Their 2N complex coefficients give the based rational-map moduli of sigma-model lumps.
Two-dimensional Derrick scaling leaves this pure-gradient energy unchanged, so degree fixes the minimum energy but not a preferred size. As ρ→0, the unit-lump energy concentrates and the field limit is singular at its center. A degree cannot change through a smooth boundary-preserving deformation, but concentration can leave that smooth configuration space. Thus topological degree protects the smooth sector and determines the Bogomolny energy, while not by itself stabilizing the lump size. There is no contradiction with the scalar obstruction in Question 1: a curved-target harmonic map obeys a nonlinear constrained field equation, rather than having harmonic unconstrained components.
The kink panel uses the normalization of Question 1. The lump panel has z0​=0 and ∣ρ∣=1 in the unit sigma-model lump and size modulus; changing its size redistributes the density while keeping its total energy 4π.

Use anti-Hermitian su(2) matrices, with invariant positive inner product ⟨X,Y⟩=−2tr(XY). On an adjoint-valued field Ξ, define the adjoint covariant derivative and gauge curvature by
Di​Ξ=∂i​Ξ+[Ai​,Ξ],Fij​=∂i​Aj​−∂j​Ai​+[Ai​,Aj​].​
(19)
Equivalently, F=dA+A∧A and [Di​,Dj​]Ξ=[Fij​,Ξ]. Put Bi​=21​ϵijk​Fjk​, with ϵ123​=1. The monopole Bogomolny equations are Bi​=Di​Φ.
For a smooth SU(2) gauge transformation g(x), use
Aig​=gAi​g−1−(∂i​g)g−1,Φg=gΦg−1.
(20)
Differentiating a transformed adjoint field shows Dig​(gΞg−1)=g(Di​Ξ)g−1: the derivatives of g cancel the inhomogeneous term in Aig​. Applying the commutator of these derivatives gives Fijg​=gFij​g−1, and therefore Big​=gBi​g−1. Both sides of Bi​=Di​Φ transform identically, establishing gauge invariance of the solution condition. Individual matrix components are covariant, rather than invariant numbers.
The monopole boundary condition is a nonzero asymptotic Higgs norm,
∣Φ(x)∣⟶v>0(∣x∣→∞),
(21)
together with finite three-dimensional Yang-Mills-Higgs energy. Standard monopole asymptotics have DΦ=O(r−2) and F=O(r−2). The normalized Higgs field at infinity defines a map S∞2​→SU(2)/U(1)≃S2, whose topological degree is the magnetic charge. A nonzero charge gives a genuine non-Abelian magnetic monopole; the nonzero Higgs boundary value alone also allows the zero-charge vacuum. Thus the relevant nontrivial solutions are Bogomolny-Prasad-Sommerfield monopoles in the zero-Higgs-potential limit.
Now form the four-dimensional connection with τ-independent components. Its gauge curvature has
Fij​=Fij​,Fiτ​=∂i​Φ−∂τ​Ai​+[Ai​,Φ]=Di​Φ=Bi​.
(22)
The self-duality sign requires an orientation. Choose the Euclidean metric and orientation dτ∧dx1∧dx2∧dx3. For cyclic (i,j,k), the Hodge star operator satisfies
∗(dxj∧dxk)=dτ∧dxi,∗(dτ∧dxi)=dxj∧dxk.
(23)
Using Fjk​=ϵjki​Bi​ and dxi∧dτ=−dτ∧dxi, the curvature is
F=∑cyclic​Bi​(dxj∧dxk−dτ∧dxi).
(24)
Each term has negative star eigenvalue, so
∗F=−F.​
(25)
This proves the Anti-self-dual Yang-Mills equations in the stated convention. The orientation of a monopole lift matters: with dx1∧dx2∧dx3∧dτ instead, the same connection is self-dual. With that opposite orientation, the anti-self-dual lift would use −Φdτ.
To fix the action normalization, take the Euclidean Yang-Mills action
S4​=4gYM2​1​∫R4​∑μ,ν​∣Fμν​∣2d4x=−2gYM2​1​∫∑μ,ν​tr(Fμν​Fμν​)d4x.
(26)
Both sums are over ordered index pairs. The component identity
∑j,k​∣Fjk​∣2=2∑i​∣Bi​∣2 gives
∑μ,ν​∣Fμν​∣2=∑j,k​∣Fjk​∣2+2∑i​∣Di​Φ∣2=2∑j,k​∣Fjk​∣2.
(27)
Hence the four-dimensional action density in terms of the three-dimensional curvature is
L4​=2gYM2​1​j,k∑​∣Fjk​∣2=gYM2​1​i∑​∣Bi​∣2=−gYM2​1​j,k∑​tr(Fjk​Fjk​).​
(28)
A different overall action normalization rescales all three equal expressions together.
A Yang-Mills instanton is a smooth finite-Euclidean-action self-dual or anti-self-dual connection in pure Yang-Mills theory, with the usual asymptotic pure-gauge behavior. Physically the term generally refers to a nontrivial field; its asymptotic gauge winding labels an instanton number. The Bianchi identity DF=0, together with ∗F=−F, implies D∗F=0, so the first-order equations already imply the second-order Yang-Mills equations. Finite action is a separate requirement.
Here L4​ is independent of τ. If the monopole has nonzero curvature, the density is positive on an open set in R3, and integrating its infinite τ-extent gives
S4​=∞.​
(29)
For a finite-energy monopole this can also be written as its positive action per unit length times ∫R​dτ. Thus a nontrivial monopole lift is not a Yang-Mills instanton on R4. The flat solution Ai​=0, Φ=Φ0​ constant is the zero-action exception: it is a vacuum, or a trivial charge-zero instanton if that terminology includes flat connections. It is not a counterexample to the nontrivial-monopole assertion.
More generally, finite Yang-Mills action forbids nontrivial translation symmetry, even when the translation preserves a connection only up to gauge. Such a symmetry would preserve its positive gauge-invariant action density. A nonflat field has a ball on which this density is bounded below by some positive constant. Repeated translations by a nonzero symmetry vector produce infinitely many disjoint copies of a sufficiently small such ball, all with the same positive action contribution, contradicting finiteness. Continuous translation invariance is a special case. Therefore a nonflat ASDYM instanton is localized in all four Euclidean directions and has no nonzero translational symmetry. Translating an instanton still produces another instanton with a shifted center; these position parameters belong to the instanton moduli space, rather than being invariances of one localized solution.

Let [Z0​:Z1​] be homogeneous coordinates on the complex projective line. The complex tautological line bundle O(−1) has fibre the line C(Z0​,Z1​)⊂C2 over that point. Its dual is the hyperplane line bundle O(1). Define the tensor powers of the hyperplane line bundle by O(n)=O(1)⊗n for n>0, the trivial line bundle for n=0, and the appropriate power of O(−1) for n<0.
An explicit holomorphic line bundle construction uses the two charts U0​={Z0​=0} and U∞​={Z1​=0} with coordinates z=Z1​/Z0​ and w=Z0​/Z1​=1/z. Choose holomorphic local frames related on the overlap by
e∞​=zne0​.
(30)
This is consistent with the tautological frames (1,z) and (w,1)=z−1(1,z) for O(−1). A holomorphic section of O(n) is represented by entire chart functions satisfying
f0​(z)e0​=f∞​(1/z)e∞​,f∞​(w)=wnf0​(1/w).
(31)
Expand f0​(z)=∑k≥0​ak​zk. Holomorphicity of f∞​ at w=0 requires every power wn−k with n−k<0 to vanish. Thus for n≥0, f0​ is exactly a polynomial of degree at most n. Conversely every such polynomial gives an entire f∞​. Its coefficients supply the explicit isomorphism
H0(CP1,O(n))≃Cn+1(n≥0).​
(32)
Equivalently, the global holomorphic sections are the homogeneous polynomials ∑k=0n​ak​Z0n−k​Z1k​. For n<0, the same chart argument gives no nonzero global section.
For the first cohomology, use the sheaf of holomorphic sections of a vector bundle and its Čech cohomology on this two-chart cover. A zero-cochain is a pair of entire chart functions (h0​,h∞​). A one-cochain is a function g(z) holomorphic on C∗, multiplied by e0​. There are no alternating two-cochains on a two-set cover, so every one-cochain is a cocycle. The coboundaries are
(δh)(z)=znh∞​(1/z)−h0​(z).
(33)
Consequently the holomorphic Laurent-series cohomology of twists on the projective line is
H1(CP1,O(n))=O(C)+znO(C)(1/z)O(C∗)​.
(34)
Here the last term means the set of functions znh(1/z) with h entire. The charts and their intersection are Stein manifolds; Cartan theorem B makes this cover acyclic for the line-bundle section sheaf, so this Čech group computes the sheaf cohomology.
Every g on C∗ has a Laurent series ∑k∈Z​ak​zk converging normally on compact annuli. Its nonnegative-power tail is an entire function of z and can be removed by a finite-chart coboundary. Its tail with k≤n can be removed by znh∞​(1/z): the series ∑k≤n​ak​wn−k is entire in w, because the inner Laurent radius is zero. Assign any overlapping removable powers to just one tail. The only possible remaining powers satisfy n<k<0.
If n≥−1, there is no such integer, proving the requested vanishing:
H1(CP1,O(n))=0(n>−2).​
(35)
For completeness, if n≤−2, the remaining classes have basis zn+1,…,z−1 and dimension −n−1. None can be a coboundary, by uniqueness of Laurent coefficients. This also explains exactly why −2 is the threshold.
Finally, a global holomorphic section of O(1)⊕O(1) is a pair of linear homogeneous polynomials:
s([Z0​:Z1​])=(aZ0​+bZ1​, cZ0​+dZ1​).
(36)
It vanishes at a projective point precisely when the nonzero vector (Z0​,Z1​)T belongs to the kernel of the coefficient matrix M=(ac​bd​). If M has rank two there is no zero; if M=0 the section vanishes everywhere. Exactly one projective zero occurs precisely when the kernel is a one-dimensional line. Thus the rank-one sections of two hyperplane line bundles are characterized by
ad−bc=0,(a,b,c,d)=(0,0,0,0).​
(37)
Equivalently, s=vℓ, where v∈C2 is a fixed nonzero vector and ℓ is a nonzero section of O(1). Its single simple zero is the zero of ℓ. This includes the case when one component is identically zero and the other is a nonzero linear form.
