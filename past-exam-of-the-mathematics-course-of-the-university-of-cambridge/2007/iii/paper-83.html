Assume a steady, incompressible, inviscid flow with a nearly uniform velocity u(x) across each water section. The channel varies slowly enough for vertical accelerations to be negligible, pressure to be hydrostatic and the water surface to be nearly horizontal across the width. Atmospheric pressure is uniform; neglect surface tension, wall friction and energy losses. The hydraulic equations apply away from jumps and short nonhydrostatic regions.
The Euler equation for horizontal momentum, with hydrostatic pressure, gives
udxdu​=−gdxd(H+h)​.
(1)
Integrating gives a constant Bernoulli equation head B=H+h+u2/(2g). Conservation of mass gives the constant volume discharge Q=bhu. Thus the shallow-water specific energy, measured above the local bed, is
E(h;x)=h+2gb(x)2h2Q2​,H(x)+E(h;x)=B.​
(2)
Define the Froude number by F=u/gh​ for the positive flow direction. Then
E=h(1+F2/2),∂h∂E​​Q,b​=1−F2.​
(3)
At fixed discharge and width, energy tends to infinity at both zero and infinite depth. It has a unique minimum at
hc​=(gb2Q2​)1/3,F=1,Ec​=23​hc​.​
(4)
Indeed Ehh​=3Q2/(gb2h4)>0. Above the minimum there are two depth branches: the shallower one is supercritical flow and the deeper one is subcritical flow.
The local long-wave propagation speeds from the shallow water equations are u±gh​. In subcritical positive flow, one wave family travels upstream and one downstream. In supercritical flow both travel downstream. At critical flow the upstream-directed speed is zero, so the flow can provide hydraulic control separating upstream adjustment from downstream conditions. This statement concerns long waves in the slowly varying hydraulic approximation, rather than all short-wave disturbances.

In the same idealized momentum model, write S=−∣S∣. The discharge now increases downstream. The admissible maximum of Q/b moves to xc​>0, beyond the geometric throat, because added discharge initially offsets the widening channel. The formula xc​=−SL2/(Q0​+D) remains valid, and the critical depth depends on S2 through D. The positive-flow reach is now restricted upstream by Q0​+∣S∣x>0, rather than downstream by exhaustion of the discharge. The same critical-head and boundary-connection conditions are still needed.
Actual injection introduces a further physical issue: newly added fluid need not have the stream's axial velocity. Let its axial velocity be ua​ and let A=bh. The momentum correction for lateral mass addition is
uux​=−g(h+H)x​+AQx​​(ua​−u),Bx​=gAQx​(ua​−u)​.
(15)

For example, flat-bed addition at rest gives (1−F2)hx​=F2h[bx​/b−2Qx​/Q]. Its regular-control compatibility is bx​/b=2Qx​/Q, rather than the co-moving condition bx​/b=Qx​/Q; in the parabolic channel it gives the different candidate xc​=−SL2/Q0​. Thus the sign reversal moves the ideal control downstream, but its quantitative prediction depends on the momentum carried by the injected water. The source specifies the volume rate only, so an injection-velocity assumption must accompany any definite constant-energy answer.

The layer equations describe long-wave adjustment behind the nose, but do not resolve the short region where the dense fluid pushes aside the ambient. A finite-height front cannot be determined by simply adjoining a vacuum solution of the one-layer momentum equation. The ambient pressure and turning flow provide additional nose physics.
In the nose frame, the material condition makes the adjacent current velocity equal to the front speed in the laboratory frame. The two interior characteristic speeds relative to that front are then ±cf​: one characteristic brings information from the body and one brings a boundary condition from the nose. This is why a gravity-current front condition is needed. With a deep ambient, fixed channel shape and negligible viscosity, the local buoyancy depth supplies the velocity scale g′hf​​. A dimensionless closure therefore has the form
uf​=Fg′hf​​,​
(21)
where a constant F is an idealization for a fixed geometry and chosen nose model. Dimensional analysis identifies the form but does not determine the coefficient.
To determine an appropriate coefficient theoretically, take a control volume moving with the nose and bounded by sections far enough ahead and behind for hydrostatic pressure and approximately uniform section velocities to apply. Use the actual triangular wetted areas and integrated pressures. Conservation of fluid volume fixes the ambient return-flow velocities; integrated horizontal momentum balances the incoming and outgoing momentum fluxes against the pressure forces. A Bernoulli relation on an ambient streamline, with an explicit nose-loss assumption if needed, supplies the pressure or energy closure. The density-anomaly pressure contribution of the lower layer contains the integral g′khf3​/3 derived above. Solving those balances gives uf2​/(g′hf​) and hence F2.
Alternatively, measured front speeds and consistently defined adjacent heights in a geometrically similar experiment can calibrate the closure. A rectangular-channel numerical coefficient must not be transferred to a triangular channel without checking those balances. The height definition and dissipative nose structure also matter; a primary study of gravity-current front conditions explains why different height conventions can lead to different inferred coefficients. No numerical value of F is required for the following solution.

Write ν=μ/ρ and average the horizontal Navier-Stokes equation, with zero-mean fluctuations and incompressible flow. The mean flow is stationary, has no vertical velocity and has no horizontal pressure gradient. The mean advection of U(z) is therefore zero. Incompressibility rewrites the averaged nonlinear fluctuation term as a divergence:
uj′​∂j​u′​=∂j​uj′​u′​.
(31)
These correlations transport mean momentum and produce Reynolds stress. Horizontal statistical homogeneity makes the horizontal derivatives of the averaged correlations vanish. It does not require the instantaneous turbulent derivatives themselves to vanish. Only the vertical divergence remains, giving
0=νUzz​−dzd​u′w′.​
(32)
Multiplying by density and integrating once gives constant-stress Reynolds-averaged wall flow:
τd​=μUz​−ρu′w′,dzdτd​​=0.​
(33)
Here τd​ is physical shear stress. Positive mean shear commonly has u′w′<0, representing downward transport of positive horizontal momentum. The Reynolds term then contributes positively to the total stress.
The printed target formula has an extra vertical derivative in the Reynolds contribution. That term has dimensions of stress per length, while μUz​ has dimensions of stress, so the formula as printed is inconsistent. The derivative belongs in the stress-divergence equation above; the boxed integrated stress is the corrected result. Part b also uses a kinematic rather than physical stress, so the density factor must be tracked explicitly.
Define the friction velocity u∗​=τd​/ρ​ for positive wall shear. In the near-wall region turbulence is suppressed and molecular viscosity carries the stress. With no slip, U≃u∗2​z/ν. The viscous wall coordinate z+=zu∗​/ν becomes order one at the characteristic viscous sublayer length
δ∼u∗​ν​.​
(34)
The numerical extent of viscosity dominance depends on the crossover convention and wall condition; the scaling is the required viscous length.

Particles settle toward the bed under their submerged weight. A concentration increasing downward gives an upward turbulent diffusive flux, which can oppose that settling. If turbulence cannot sustain the particles, they enter the weakly mixed near-wall region and can deposit. Sediment resuspension requires bed forces strong enough to entrain grains, not merely a specified diffusivity far above the bed.
For noncohesive grains, an appropriate criterion uses the Shields parameter
Θ=(ρs​−ρ)gdτd​​>Θc​,​
(42)
where d is grain size, ρs​ its density and Θc​ the sediment entrainment threshold expressed dimensionlessly. The threshold depends on grain geometry, contacts and viscous effects; cohesion changes the criterion. Sustained suspension additionally requires sufficiently vigorous upward turbulent motions relative to Ws​. Bed motion and well-mixed suspension are therefore not identical conditions.
At sedimentation-resuspension equilibrium, the total vertical particle flux is constant. Set κT​=Kz with K=C/2. The stationary equation gives
J=−Ws​ϕ−Kzϕz​=constant,ϕ(z)=Az−Ws​/K−Ws​J​.
(43)
Since Ws​>0 and ϕ→0 at infinity, necessarily J=0. Settling and upward diffusion then balance pointwise. Taking a reference height z1​=1 in the specified length units gives the equilibrium settling-diffusion profile
ϕ(z)=ϕ1​(z1​z​)−P,P=KWs​​=αu^2Ws​​,z1​=1.​
(44)
It is nonnegative and decays to zero. The profile describes the region z≫δ; it does not assert that this power law continues through the viscous layer down to the physical bed. The supplied reference concentration summarizes the bed exchange and near-wall physics that the outer diffusion closure does not resolve.

The Batchelor entrainment hypothesis makes the inward entrainment velocity proportional to the local plume speed:
ue​=α∣w∣,​
(45)
where α>0 is the dimensionless entrainment coefficient. For an upward plume w>0, this is ue​=αw. The hypothesis represents turbulent ingestion of ambient fluid; it is a closure rather than a consequence of mass conservation.
Let A=b2, suppressing the common factor π from cross-sectional integrals. In the Boussinesq approximation, use a constant reference density ρr​ in the leading-order mass conservation equation. Dividing that equation by ρr​ gives volume conservation:
At​+(Aw)z​=2bue​.​
(46)
The factor two is the ratio of the perimeter 2πb to the suppressed area factor π. Density differences are retained in buoyancy, even though they are neglected in the leading inertial and volume terms.
To derive the density-deficit balance, first leave the small density differences explicit in the supplied mass equation. Because the ambient density ρ0​(z) is time independent, the product rule gives
∂t​[(ρ0​−ρ)gA]+∂z​[(ρ0​−ρ)gAw]​=gρ0​[At​+(Aw)z​]+gρ0z​Aw−g[(ρA)t​+(ρAw)z​]=gρ0​(2bue​)+gρ0z​Aw−g(2ρ0​bue​)=gρ0z​Aw.​
(47)
Thus the ambient mass entrained through the perimeter cancels exactly against its contribution to the volume budget, yielding the required buoyancy balance. It is understood to the accuracy of the underlying Boussinesq approximation; the volume equation is not an additional exact constraint on a fully variable-density fluid.
For later use define reduced gravity g′=g(ρ0​−ρ)/ρr​ and the squared buoyancy frequency N2=−gρ0z​/ρr​. With B=Ag′ and Q=Aw, the balance is
Bt​+(Awg′)z​=−N2Q.​
(48)
A stably stratified ambient has N2>0, so upward motion consumes the plume's positive density deficit; an unstable ambient reverses this sign.

The last relation gives s=p+1 and the first r=2p−2. Substitution into the middle relation yields 4p−5=2p+1, hence
p=3,r=s=4.
(57)
The amplitude equations are
3q=2αm​,4m=mfq​,4f=G2q.
(58)
From the first relation m=9q2/(4α2), while the last two give m2=G2q2/16. Taking positive amplitudes gives m=Gq/4, and consequently
Q=9α2G​z3,M=36α2G2​z4,F=36α2G3​z4.​
(59)
Reconstructing the radius, speed and reduced gravity gives the top-hat plume in constant unstable stratification:
b=M​Q​=32αz​,w=QM​=4Gz​,g′=QF​=4G2z​.​
(60)
As a direct check, Mz​=α2G2z3/9=FQ/M and Fz​=α2G3z3/9=G2Q, while Qz​=α2Gz2/3=2αM​.
The corresponding ambient gradient is ρ0z​=ρr​G2/g. Thus ρ(z)=ρ0​(z)−ρr​G2z/(4g); for a linear ambient this is ρ0​(0)+3ρr​G2z/(4g). The plume is locally lighter than the ambient even though both densities increase upward in this prescribed unstable stratification.
The coordinate z can be measured from a virtual origin. This exact power law branch has fluxes tending to zero there and describes buoyancy generated by the maintained unstable ambient; it does not impose arbitrary nonzero source fluxes at that origin. The given idealization holds the ambient quiescent despite its static instability. Finally, the Boussinesq approximation limits the modeled height range to small relative density changes, here G2z/g≪1. Within these assumptions, the displayed positive branch proves the requested existence.
