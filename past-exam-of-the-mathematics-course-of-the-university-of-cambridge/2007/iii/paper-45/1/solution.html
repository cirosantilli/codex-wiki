Condition on the claim count distribution. If μ=EX1​ and v=Var(X1​), then independence of the count and the independent and identically distributed random variables gives
E[S∣N]=Nμ,Var(S∣N)=Nv.
(1)
The empty sum is zero. The law of total expectation and law of total variance therefore prove the random sum of independent claims formulas
ES=ENEX1​,Var(S)=ENVar(X1​)+Var(N)(EX1​)2.​
(2)
For the variance calculation assume the count and severity have finite second moments; the expectation identity needs the corresponding integrability. Nonnegative claims also permit the expectation identity with extended nonnegative values.
Represent each policy payment as Si​=Bi​Yi​, where Bi​ is a Bernoulli random variable of parameter qi​, independent of its severity. Equivalently, Yi​ specifies the payment distribution conditional on a claim. Then
ESi​=qi​μi​,ESi2​=qi​(σi2​+μi2​),Var(Si​)=qi​σi2​+qi​(1−qi​)μi2​.
(3)
Independence of the policies makes their variances add. Put
Q=∑i​qi​,M=∑i​qi​μi​,A=∑i​qi​(σi2​+μi2​),C=∑i​qi2​μi2​.
(4)
The heterogeneous individual claims model consequently has
ET=M,Var(T)=A−C=i∑​[qi​σi2​+qi​(1−qi​)μi2​].​
(5)
For the Poisson approximation, define the severity mixture distribution
F=∑i​Qqi​​Fi​.
(6)
Let φi​(t)=E[eitYi​] be the individual characteristic functions. Conditioning on each Poisson count and then multiplying transforms of the independent policy payments gives
E[eitT]​=i∏​exp{qi​(φi​(t)−1)}=exp{Q[i∑​Qqi​​φi​(t)−1]}.​
(7)
This is Poisson superposition of insurance portfolios. By the uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions,
T =d j=1∑K​Zj​,K∼Poisson(Q),Zj​∼iidF,​
(8)
with count and severities independent. Its compound Poisson distribution has distribution function
P(T≤x)=e−Q∑k=0∞​k!Qk​F∗k(x),
(9)
where F∗k is the convolution distribution of k independent severities and F∗0 is the distribution concentrated at zero. Characteristic functions avoid assuming that severity moment-generating functions exist. Since EZ=M/Q and EZ2=A/Q, the random-sum formulas give
ET=M,Var(T)=A.​
(10)
Use TB​ to distinguish the second, compound-binomial approximation from the Poisson one. It has KB​∼Bin(n,p) with p=Q/n and the same severity law F. Thus EKB​=Q and Var(KB​)=Q(1−p). Applying the random sum of independent claims identities again gives
Var(TB​)​=Q[QA​−(QM​)2]+Q(1−p)(QM​)2=A−nM2​.​
(11)
Hence
ETB​=M,Var(TB​)=A−M2/n.​
(12)
Both approximations match the exact expected value. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives M2≤nC, proving the variance comparison for compound portfolio approximations:
Var(T)≤Var(TB​)≤Var(T).​
(13)
The excess variances are respectively C−M2/n and C. The compound-binomial model is therefore closer in variance; equality with the exact variance occurs when all qi​μi​ are equal. This variance comparison is not a general ordering of approximation errors or tail probabilities.
The Poisson replacement is useful when all claim probabilities are small: it replaces the individual restriction of at most one claim by an unrestricted Poisson count, and the variance error is of second order in those probabilities. The compound-binomial replacement retains a count bounded by n, but pools both frequencies and severity laws and can lose policy-specific information. If all qi​ and all Fi​ agree, it is exactly the original portfolio law. In a heterogeneous portfolio, agreement of means, or even variances, does not make either approximate law exact. Neither approximation is guaranteed accurate merely by having many policies.
