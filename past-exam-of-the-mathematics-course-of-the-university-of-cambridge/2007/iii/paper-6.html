Use unital rings, and distinguish left and right Noetherianity. A useful form of the noncommutative Hilbert basis theorem is the following: if a ring B is generated by a subring A and an element x, if A+xA=A+Ax, and if A is a right Noetherian ring, then B is a right Noetherian ring. The corresponding left-handed statement holds as well. In particular, a central polynomial ring over a Noetherian ring is Noetherian, and a skew polynomial ring of an automorphism preserves either side's Noetherianity.
Here is a proof of the general right-handed statement. Put F−1​=0 and
Fn​=A+Ax+⋯+Axn=A+xA+⋯+xnA.
(1)
The equality follows inductively from A+xA=A+Ax. These groups form an exhaustive ascending filtration, stable under multiplication by A on either side, with Fj​Fk​⊆Fj+k​. For any b∈A and n≥0, moving coefficients one step at a time also gives
bxn=xnc+v(c∈A, v∈Fn−1​).
(2)
Fix a right ideal I of B and define coefficient sets
Ln​={a∈A:axn∈I+Fn−1​}.
(3)
Each Ln​ is a right ideal of A. Additive closure is immediate. If a∈Ln​ and b∈A, use the preceding transport identity to obtain
abxn=(axn)c+av∈I+Fn−1​.
(4)
Here (axn)c has this membership because I is a right ideal and Fn−1​ is stable under right multiplication by A. Also Ln​⊆Ln+1​, by multiplying axn∈I+Fn−1​ on the right by x. The ascending chain condition in A yields N with Ln​=LN​ for n≥N.
For each 0≤k≤N, choose finite right generators akj​ of Lk​ and lifts fkj​∈I with fkj​−akj​xk∈Fk−1​. These finitely many lifts generate I as a right ideal of B. To prove this, let f∈I∩Fd​ and write f=axd+v, v∈Fd−1​. Then a∈Ld​. Put k=min(d,N) and write a=∑j​akj​bj​. Transport each coefficient as bj​xd=xdcj​+vj​ with vj​∈Fd−1​. Consequently
f−∑j​fkj​xd−kcj​∈I∩Fd−1​.
(5)
Induction on d, with F−1​=0, expresses f in the right ideal generated by the lifts. Every right ideal is therefore finitely generated, proving the theorem. Passing to the opposite ring proves the left-handed version. This proof does not require unique coefficient normal forms.
A poly-(cyclic or finite) group has a finite subnormal series
1=G0​◃G1​◃⋯◃Gr​=G
(6)
whose factors Gi​/Gi−1​ are cyclic or finite. Normality is required between consecutive terms. We show inductively that each group ring Z[Gi​] is both a left Noetherian ring and a right Noetherian ring.
The starting ring Z is Noetherian, since every ideal is principal. The other module fact we use is that a finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring on the relevant side is a Noetherian module: it is a quotient of a finite direct sum of regular modules, and the ascending chain condition passes to finite sums and quotients. For a finite sum this follows by induction, comparing the intersections of a chain with one summand and its images in the quotient by that summand.
If H◃K has finite index, choosing coset representatives shows that Z[K] is finite free as a left and as a right Z[H]-module. By finite-module extension preserves Noetherianity, every one-sided ideal of Z[K] is finitely generated already as a Z[H]-module, and those same generators generate it as a Z[K]-ideal. Thus either chain condition passes through a finite factor.
If K/H is infinite cyclic, choose t∈K lifting its generator. Every element of K has a unique form htn, and conjugation by t gives an automorphism σ of Z[H]. Hence
Z[K]≅Z[H][t,t−1;σ],ta=σ(a)t.
(7)
For the polynomial subring C=Z[H][t;σ], the condition A+tA=A+At follows from invertibility of σ, so the proved noncommutative Hilbert basis theorem makes C Noetherian on both sides. A right ideal J of the skew Laurent polynomial ring is generated by J∩C: for any f∈J, choose m large enough that ftm∈C, and recover f=(ftm)t−m. Finite generators of J∩C over C therefore generate J over the Laurent ring. Left multiplication by a large power of t proves the left-handed statement. These are the Laurent-extension facts used in the induction.
Every factor of the subnormal series is covered by one of these two cases. Therefore
Z[G] is left and right Noetherian.​
(8)
The argument proves the more general result that group rings of poly-(cyclic or finite) groups are Noetherian over any coefficient ring satisfying both chain conditions.

For a commutative ring R and a multiplicative subset S containing 1, the universal property of localization specifies a pair (RS​,ι) with ι:R→RS​ a unital ring homomorphism, such that every ι(s), s∈S, is a unit, and any unital ring homomorphism f:R→T into a commutative ring that sends S to units factors uniquely through ι. Thus there is a unique f​:RS​→T satisfying f​ι=f. The property determines the pair up to a unique compatible isomorphism.
For existence, take pairs (r,s)∈R×S and declare
(r,s)∼(r′,s′)⟺u(s′r−sr′)=0 for some u∈S.
(9)
The extra multiplier is essential when R has zero divisors. This is an equivalence relation: for transitivity, multiply the two cross-multiplication equations by appropriate denominators and add them, using the product of their annihilating multipliers. Write the class as r/s and define
sr​+s′r′​=ss′rs′+r′s​,sr​s′r′​=ss′rr′​,ι(r)=1r​.
(10)
Multiplying the defining equivalence equations verifies that these operations are well defined. They give a commutative ring, and s/1 has inverse 1/s. The required map is necessarily
f​(r/s)=f(r)f(s)−1.
(11)
It is well defined because applying f to u(s′r−sr′)=0 and cancelling the units f(u),f(s),f(s′) equates the two images. Its addition and multiplication laws follow directly from the fraction formulas. This establishes existence and the universal property of localization. If 0∈S, this construction gives the zero ring, which is allowed as a localization.
For a prime ideal P, its complement S=R∖P is multiplicatively closed, and localization at a prime ideal means RP​=S−1R. The map
RP​⟶Frac(R/P),r/s⟼r/s
(12)
is well defined and surjective, with kernel of a ring homomorphism PRP​: a numerator has zero image exactly when it belongs to P. Since R/P is an integral domain, its field of fractions is a nonzero field, so PRP​ is a proper maximal ideal. Every fraction r/s with r∈/P is a unit, with inverse s/r. Every fraction with r∈P belongs to this proper ideal and cannot be a unit. Thus the nonunits are exactly PRP​, and every proper ideal lies within it. Consequently
RP​ is a local ring with unique maximal ideal PRP​.​
(13)
Now suppose each RP​ is reduced. If x∈R is nilpotent, its image x/1 is nilpotent in every RP​, hence is zero. If x=0, its annihilator AnnR​(x) is a proper ideal, so choose a maximal ideal P containing it. Maximal ideals in a commutative ring are prime ideals. The vanishing criterion in a module localization would give sx=0 for some s∈/P. But then s∈AnnR​(x)⊆P, a contradiction. Hence R is reduced. This is reducedness is detected by prime localizations; no finiteness hypothesis is needed.
The stronger local-domain condition does not force R to be an integral domain. Take R=k×k for a field k. Its only prime ideals are P1​=0×k and P2​=k×0: a prime ideal must contain one of the orthogonal idempotents (1,0) and (0,1), and the remaining quotient is the field k. At P1​, inverting (1,0) kills the second component, so RP1​​≅k; similarly RP2​​≅k. Both localizations are integral domains, but
(1,0)(0,1)=0
(14)
is a product of nonzero elements in R. Therefore the answer is no, as illustrated by domain prime localizations do not imply a domain.

Assume R is a nonzero unital ring and all its modules are unital. Order the proper left ideals by inclusion. A chain has an upper bound given by its union, which is still a proper left ideal: if it contained 1, one member of the chain would contain 1 and be all of R. The family is nonempty because it contains 0. The Zorn lemma gives a maximal proper left ideal L. The nonzero quotient R/L has no proper nonzero submodules, because its submodules correspond to left ideals between L and R. Thus R/L is a simple left module. The nonzero-ring qualification is necessary: the zero ring has no nonzero unital simple module.
Define the Jacobson radical J(R) as the intersection of all maximal left ideals. It is equivalently the intersection of the ideals AnnR​(S) over all simple modules, where Ann is the annihilator of a module. To verify this equivalence, for a nonzero vector v in a simple module S, the surjection R→S, r↦rv, has a maximal left-ideal kernel. Thus x∈J(R) implies xv=0 for every v in every simple module. Conversely, if x annihilates all simple modules, it annihilates 1+L in every R/L, so x∈L. An annihilator of a module is a two-sided ideal; this description therefore proves that J(R) is a two-sided ideal.
For the forward implication of the unit criterion for the Jacobson radical, first let y∈J(R). If R(1−y) were a proper left ideal, it would lie in a maximal one L. Both y and 1−y would belong to L, an impossibility. Hence c(1−y)=1 for some c∈R. A left inverse alone does not yet establish that 1−y is a unit in a general noncommutative ring. However, c=1+cy, with cy∈J(R), so the same argument gives dc=1 for some d. Multiplying c(1−y)=1 by d on the left gives d=1−y. Therefore (1−y)c=dc=1 as well. This proves that 1−y is a unit.
For x∈J(R), its two-sided-ideal property gives axb∈J(R) for all a,b, so 1−axb is a unit. Conversely, if x∈/J(R), choose a maximal left ideal L with x∈/L. Then L+Rx=R, so l+ax=1 for some l∈L, a∈R. Hence 1−ax=l∈L is not a unit: if it were invertible, multiplying it on the left by its inverse would put 1 in L. Taking b=1 contradicts the given unit property. We have proved
J(R)={x∈R:1−axb is a unit for every a,b∈R}.​
(15)
Finally let R be commutative and I an ideal. The set S=1+I contains 1, and
(1+i)(1+j)=1+(i+j+ij)∈1+I,
(16)
so it is a multiplicative subset. In the localization of a module IS​⊆RS​, any element is i/s with i∈I and s∈S. For any r/t∈RS​,
1−tr​si​=tsts−ri​.
(17)
Both ts and ts−ri lie in S, since ts∈1+I and ri∈I. The fraction is consequently a unit in RS​. In a commutative ring, the product of any two coefficients is again a single coefficient, so the proved unit criterion for the Jacobson radical applies to all expressions 1−a(i/s)b. Thus
IS​⊆J(RS​).​
(18)
This proves localization away from one plus an ideal lies in the Jacobson radical. If I=R, then 0∈S, RS​=0, and the inclusion holds trivially.

The ring form of the Artin–Wedderburn theorem states that the following conditions on a unital ring R are equivalent: R is a semisimple ring; R satisfies the descending chain condition on left ideals and J(R)=0; and
R≅i=1∏s​Mni​​(Di​),​
(19)
where s is finite, each ni​≥1, and each Di​ is a division ring. The factors are unique up to permutation and isomorphism. The zero ring may be included as the empty product. This theorem also implies the corresponding right-handed statements.
First prove the implication from the chain condition and zero Jacobson radical. Among finite intersections of maximal left ideals, choose a minimal one, say L=L1​∩⋯∩Lt​. Such a minimal intersection exists by the descending chain condition. Intersecting with any other maximal left ideal cannot make L smaller, so L is contained in every maximal left ideal. Thus L⊆J(R)=0. It follows that the map of left modules
R⟶⨁j=1t​R/Lj​
(20)
is injective. Each quotient is a simple module.
For completeness, a submodule N of a finite direct sum V=⨁j​Tj​ of simple modules is itself a finite direct sum of simple modules and is a direct summand of V. Choose a sum W of coordinate summands, maximal among those with N∩W=0. For any omitted coordinate summand Tj​, simplicity gives Tj​∩(N+W)=0 or Tj​⊆N+W. In the first case N∩(W⊕Tj​)=0, contradicting maximality; hence every coordinate summand lies in N+W. Therefore V=N⊕W. Projection onto N shows N is a finite sum of images of the Tj​, each image zero or simple. Add these images successively, discarding any image already in the sum: simplicity makes each new intersection zero. This yields a direct-sum decomposition. Applying this argument to the displayed injection proves that the left regular module is a finite sum of simple modules.
Conversely, if the regular module is a direct sum of simple modules, that sum is finite because 1 lies in a finite subsum and generates the whole regular module. Every left ideal, being a submodule, has the preceding decomposition and splitting property. Its chain lengths are bounded by the number of simple summands, so the descending chain condition holds. The Jacobson radical annihilates every simple summand of the regular module, hence annihilates 1 and is zero. This identifies the first two conditions.
Write the finite decomposition using pairwise nonisomorphic simple left modules:
R​R≅⨁i=1s​Sini​​.
(21)
Every simple module is a quotient of R, by sending 1 to any nonzero vector, so this list includes every simple isomorphism class. The Schur lemma gives HomR​(Si​,Sj​)=0 for i=j, and Ei​=EndR​(Si​) is a division ring: any nonzero endomorphism has zero kernel and full image by simplicity. Consequently
EndR​(R​R)≅∏i=1s​Mni​​(Ei​).
(22)
Every endomorphism of the left regular module is right multiplication by its value on 1. Composition reverses multiplication of those values, so the left side is Rop. Taking the opposite ring, and using transpose to identify Mn​(E)op with Mn​(Eop), gives the theorem's decomposition with Di​=Eiop​. This keeps the left/right conventions explicit.
Conversely, the standard column module Dn over Mn​(D) is simple: a nonzero coordinate of a vector can be inverted to construct a matrix sending it to any prescribed vector. The left regular module decomposes as the sum of its n column ideals, each isomorphic to Dn. A finite product of these matrix rings is therefore a semisimple ring. This proves all implications. For uniqueness, the isomorphism classes of simple modules and their multiplicities in the regular module determine the numbers ni​ and the division rings Eiop​. The multiplicity can also be recovered as the dimension of HomR​(Si​,R) as a right Ei​-module, so it does not depend on the chosen decomposition.
Now let A=C[G] for the finite group G. It has dimension ∣G∣, with the elements of G as a basis, so its left ideals satisfy the descending chain condition by their dimensions. The permitted assumption J(A)=0 makes the Artin–Wedderburn theorem applicable. Its decomposition is a complex-algebra decomposition
A≅∏i​Mni​​(Di​),
(23)
with each Di​ finite-dimensional over the central copy of C. Each such division algebra is just C. Indeed, for d∈Di​, finite dimensionality gives a nonzero polynomial relation p(d)=0 with complex coefficients. Factor p into linear factors over C. Their product at d is zero, so the absence of zero divisors forces d−λ=0 for some scalar root λ. Thus every element is scalar.
The pairwise nonisomorphic simple modules of ∏i​Mni​​(C) are the column modules Cni​, with all other factors acting as zero. Hence dimC​Si​=ni​. Taking complex dimensions gives
i=1∑k​(dimC​Si​)2=i=1∑k​ni2​=dimC​C[G]=∣G∣.​
(24)

An almost commutative algebra over C has an exhaustive nonnegative increasing multiplicative ascending filtration
0=F−1​R⊆F0​R⊆F1​R⊆⋯,1∈F0​R,Fi​RFj​R⊆Fi+j​R,
(25)
for which the associated graded ring
grR=⨁n≥0​Fn​R/Fn−1​R
(26)
is a commutative finitely generated complex algebra. This includes the customary degree filtration with F0​R=C, finitely many degree-one generators, and commutators lowering degree. The finite-generation clause is part of the convention used here and is necessary for the conclusion.
A finitely generated commutative complex algebra is a quotient of a polynomial ring C[X1​,…,Xd​]. Repeated application of the central-variable case of the Hilbert basis theorem makes that polynomial ring Noetherian; quotients preserve the ascending chain condition by lifting ideal chains. Thus grR is Noetherian.
We prove ascending filtered-graded transfer of Noetherianity directly. Give a left ideal I⊆R the induced ascending filtration I∩Fn​R. Its associated graded module embeds into grR as a homogeneous ideal, denoted grI. Choose finitely many homogeneous generators u1​,…,um​ of this ideal, of degrees d1​,…,dm​, and lifts uj​∈I∩Fdj​​R. Homogeneous generators may be chosen by taking homogeneous components of any finite generating set.
For u∈I∩Fn​R, its degree-n symbol can be expressed as
u=∑j​aj​uj​,degaj​=n−dj​,
(27)
with terms of negative coefficient degree omitted. Lift each coefficient to aj​∈Fn−dj​​R. Then
u−∑j​aj​uj​∈I∩Fn−1​R.
(28)
Induction on n, ending at F−1​R=0, expresses u in the left ideal generated by the uj​. Hence every left ideal is finitely generated. Applying the same argument to right ideals, or to the opposite ring, proves
R is left and right Noetherian.​
(29)
This degree-lowering proof terminates in finitely many steps and requires no completeness assumption on the filtered ring. Without finite generation of the graded algebra, a counterexample would be the commutative polynomial ring in infinitely many variables, whose ideals (X1​)⊊(X1​,X2​)⊊⋯ do not stabilize.
For the universal enveloping algebra U(g), use the degree ascending filtration inherited from the tensor algebra of the finite-dimensional Lie algebra g. For x,y∈g, the defining relation is xy−yx=[x,y]∈F1​U(g), whereas the product xy has filtration degree at most 2. Thus the degree-one symbols commute in grU(g). Those symbols generate the graded algebra, so it is a quotient of
Sym(g)≅C[X1​,…,Xd​],d=dimC​g.
(30)
This already proves the required almost-commutative property. The Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem gives the stronger identification grU(g)≅Sym(g), but injectivity of this map is not needed for the chain-condition argument. Therefore
U(g) is left and right Noetherian.​
(31)

Since M is a finitely generated module, choose a surjection from a finite free module, F0​↠M. A finite free module over a left Noetherian ring is a Noetherian module, so the kernel K1​ is finitely generated. Choose a finite free module F1​ surjecting onto K1​, then compose its map with K1​↪F0​. Its kernel is again finitely generated. Repeating constructs an exact sequence
⋯⟶F2​⟶F1​⟶F0​⟶M⟶0,​
(32)
with all Fi​ finite free modules, hence finitely generated projective modules. Thus the requested projective resolution exists, without asserting that it has finite length.
Assume now that M=0 and pdR​M=n<∞. We may arrange a length-n projective resolution with all terms finite projective. Here is why the preceding construction gives one. Set K0​=M, with short exact sequences 0→Kj+1​→Fj​→Kj​→0. Applying the Ext functor, whose positive degrees vanish on a projective first argument, gives by repeated dimension shifting
ExtR1​(Kn​,N)≅ExtRn+1​(M,N)=0
(33)
for every left module N. The final equality follows from the existence of any length-n projective resolution. Vanishing of these degree-one groups means that every short exact sequence ending in Kn​ splits: its extension class is zero. In particular a free surjection onto Kn​ splits, so Kn​ is a projective module. It is finitely generated by the Noetherian kernel construction. Use Pn​=Kn​ and Pj​=Fj​ for j<n to obtain
0⟶Pn​dn​​Pn−1​⟶⋯⟶P0​⟶M⟶0.
(34)
For a left finite projective module P, put P∗=HomR​(P,R), a right module under (fr)(p)=f(p)r. It is a right finite projective module, and its natural evaluation map
P⟶P∗∗=HomRop​(P∗,R),p⟼(f⟼f(p)),
(35)
is an isomorphism. These claims hold for a finite free module by coordinates, and pass to direct summands, proving them for every finite projective module without any right-Noetherian hypothesis.
Suppose n≥1 and ExtRn​(M,R)=0. Computing the Ext functor from this resolution shows that its last dual map
dn∗​:Pn−1∗​⟶Pn∗​
(36)
is surjective. Since Pn∗​ is projective as a right module, this map has a right-module section h:Pn∗​→Pn−1∗​, with dn∗​h=1. Dualizing gives h∗dn∗∗​=1. Under the natural double-dual identifications, dn∗∗​ is dn​, so this gives a left-module retraction of dn​.
Therefore Pn−1​=dn​(Pn​)⊕Q for a finite projective module Q. The next differential has kernel dn​(Pn​), so restricting it to Q gives an exact resolution with Q replacing Pn−1​ and the initial Pn​ removed. This has length n−1, contradicting the definition of projective dimension. For n=0, the nonzero M is a finite projective module and a summand of some Rd. A nonzero vector in that summand has a nonzero coordinate, so one coordinate projection restricts to a nonzero map M→R. Thus the degree-zero Hom functor is nonzero as well. We have proved
ExtRn​(M,R)=0.​
(37)
This is top Ext detects finite projective dimension over a left Noetherian ring. The nonzero-module convention matters when n=0: if one assigns projective dimension zero to the zero module, then HomR​(0,R)=0 is an exception. With the convention pdR​0=−∞, it is outside the asserted finite nonnegative case.
For an example of infinite projective dimension, take the dual numbers R=k[ε]/(ε2) over a field k, and M=k=R/(ε). The periodic resolution over dual numbers is
⋯ε​Rε​Rε​R⟶k⟶0.
(38)
It is exact because both the kernel and image of multiplication by ε are the ideal (ε), and that is also the kernel of R→k. Applying HomR​(−,k) gives a copy of k in every degree and zero differentials, since ε acts as zero on k. Consequently
ExtRj​(k,k)≅k=0for every j≥0.
(39)
A finite projective resolution would make these groups zero above its length, so
pdk[ε]/(ε2)​k=∞.​
