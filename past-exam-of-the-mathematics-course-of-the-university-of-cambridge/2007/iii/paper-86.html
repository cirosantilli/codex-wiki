Extend q by zero to x<0. Its ordinary Fourier transform is precisely the half-line transform q​, so Fourier inversion gives the real-axis term for every interior point x>0. At x=0 the zero extension can have a jump; the interior inversion is the assertion needed here.
Write k=u+iv. The contour is the upper branch of a hyperbola,
v=2α+α2+4u2​​≥α,
(1)
with u increasing from −∞ to ∞. In the region above it, ν(k)=iα−k has nonpositive imaginary part. Therefore q​(ν(k)) is analytic there: its defining integral has factor eiux−(v−α)x and converges for a sufficiently decaying q. Smoothness and integration by parts also give q​(ν)=O(∣ν∣−1) away from bounded spectral sets.
For an interior spatial point x>0, the factor eikx decays exponentially as Imk→+∞. Truncate the hyperbolic contour and close it above, using the Cauchy integral theorem; the closing segments vanish by this decay and the transform estimate. No poles occur in the enclosed region. Consequently the half-line drift null contour identity is
∫L​eikxq​(iα−k)dk=0,x>0.​
(2)
Adding any constant multiple of zero to Fourier inversion proves the proposed inversion, for every c. Oscillatory real-axis inversion may be understood by a Gaussian cutoff followed by its usual limit. The same contour argument allows a spectral multiplier analytic above L with polynomial growth, because the exponential still controls the closing paths. We will need this slight extension for derivative boundary data.

The modified Helmholtz closed spectral one-form is exact in the simply connected quadrant. Writing its primitive as μE gives the compatible first-order equations
μz​−ikμ=qz​+ikq,μzˉ​−ikλ​μ=−qzˉ​−ikλ​q.

μ0​(z,k)μx​(x,y,k)μy​(x,y,k)​=H(z,k)∫0z​W,=−i∫x∞​e−ia(k)(s−x)[b(k)q(s,y)−qy​(s,y)]ds,=−∫y∞​eb(k)(s−y)[iqx​(x,s)−a(k)q(x,s)]ds.​
(21)
Path independence follows from closedness. The two infinite primitives exist in the lower and left spectral half-planes respectively. In their common third quadrant they coincide, because the global relation makes the difference of the two basepoint integrals zero.
Choose a sectionally analytic spectral function by using μ0​ in quadrant I, μy​ in quadrant II and μx​ in quadrants III and IV. It has no jump across the negative axes. Let ℓx​=(0,∞) and ℓy​=(0,i∞), both oriented outward. With plus denoting the left side of an oriented ray, the two remaining jumps are
μ+​−μ−​=Hρx​on ℓx​,μ+​−μ−​=Hρy​on ℓy​.
(22)
For example, on ℓx​ the jump is μ0​−μx​, the primitive around the bottom boundary; on ℓy​ it is μy​−μ0​, the left boundary traversed downward. This fixes both orientations and signs of the Riemann-Hilbert problem.
Integration by parts in the infinite primitive formulas, or in the finite primitive with its decaying basepoint contribution, gives
μ=−q+O(k−1)(k→∞),μ=q+O(k)(k→0).
(23)
For instance the horizontal formula has leading value −bq/a, which tends to −q at infinity and q at zero; the vertical formula has leading value −aq/b with the same limits. These expansions hold in closed subsectors, with the boundary limits obtained by oscillatory regularization. Along ℓx​, H damps with y>0 at both spectral ends; along ℓy​, it damps with x>0. Thus the ray integrals converge in the interior and small and large spectral circles can be removed.
Solve the additive Riemann-Hilbert problem by a Cauchy integral formula, normalized at infinity:
μ(k)=−q+2πi1​[∫ℓx​​s−kH(s)ρx​(s)​ds+∫ℓy​​s−kH(s)ρy​(s)​ds].
(24)
The Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem gives the stated jumps. The difference between this expression and the constructed primitive is analytic across both rays, bounded at zero and vanishes at infinity; the Liouville theorem makes that difference zero. Evaluate at zero, where μ(0)=q. The difference between the two spectral normalizations is 2q, hence the quadrant modified Helmholtz spectral reconstruction is
q(x,y)=4πi1​[∫0∞​eia(k)x−b(k)yρx​(k)kdk​+∫0i∞​eia(k)x−b(k)yρy​(k)kdk​].​

The factor 1/(4πi) is essential. A useful independent normalization check is to write p=a(k) on the real ray, so b=p2+4λ​=B(p) and dk/k=dp/B. On the imaginary ray write k=ir, p=r−λ/r and a=iB(p). The formula becomes

In single-photon emission computed tomography, g represents the internal emission density, f the known absorption coefficient and Af​ the directed detector counts. Each path integral weights a source point by its survival probability to the detector. Thus the attenuated Radon transform is the forward measurement model and its derived inverse reconstructs the emission distribution, providing the mathematical basis of SPECT.
