The displayed Cauchy-Schwarz argument supplies the needed corrected identity independently.
Under the null, let Z=n​(Xˉ−μ0​) and C=(n−1)S. Then Z∼Np​(0,Σ), C∼Wp​(n−1,Σ), and Z,C are independent. To see these sample properties, apply an orthogonal transformation to the n observation indices with first row n−1/2(1,…,1). Its first centered row is Z; its other n−1 rows are independent Np​(0,Σ) residual contrasts, independent of the first. Their outer products sum to C, establishing the Wishart distribution and independence.
Since T2=(n−1)ZTC−1Z, use the supplied Gaussian-Wishart quadratic-form law with k=n−1 to obtain
p(n−1)n−p​T2∼Fp,n−p​under H0​.​
(5)
Thus a level-η test rejects when this scaled statistic exceeds the (1−η) quantile of the F-distribution. The Gaussian-Wishart law requires independence; it holds here because of the orthogonal sample decomposition. Its printed use of X instead of the introduced Z is a notational slip. A fixed-direction Student law alone would not calibrate the maximized statistic, because its maximizing direction depends on the sample.

Transform each observation to Yj​=ATXj​. If AT has full row rank m, the transformed sample is multivariate normal with mean ATμ and positive-definite covariance matrix ATΣA. Its sample mean is ATXˉ and its unbiased sample covariance matrix is ATSA. The Hotelling test of linear hypotheses therefore uses
TA2​=n(ATXˉ)T(ATSA)−1(ATXˉ),m(n−1)n−m​TA2​∼Fm,n−m​ under H0​.​
(6)
Here n>m is required. Reject for a large scaled statistic using the relevant upper F-distribution quantile. If the rows of AT are dependent, choose a basis for their row space, use that basis as the contrast matrix, and replace m by its rank r. The null hypothesis is unchanged, but redundant contrasts must not be treated as independent dimensions. Rank zero gives no restrictions and hence no nontrivial test.

At equal spacing h, the adjacent increments of an affine mean profile are all βh. Their differences are therefore zero. Define the (p−2)×p matrix B whose row associated with the interior time i has coefficients 1,−2,1 in columns i−1,i,i+1. Then
(Bμ)i−1​=μi−1​−2μi​+μi+1​=0.
(7)
Conversely these equations make all adjacent first differences equal, so μi​ is affine in i, equivalently affine in the equally spaced times. Thus the restriction is exactly Bμ=0, not just a necessary condition. Its null space has basis 1 and t=(t1​,…,tp​)T, so B has rank r=p−2.
For p≥3 and n>r, the second-difference test of a linear mean profile is the Hotelling test of linear hypotheses with
TB2​=n(BXˉ)T(BSBT)−1(BXˉ),r(n−1)n−r​TB2​∼Fr,n−r​.​
(8)
Reject the affine-profile hypothesis when the scaled statistic exceeds its level-η upper F-distribution critical value. This removes the nuisance intercept and slope without estimating them separately.
For unequal ordered distinct times, put hi​=ti+1​−ti​>0 and replace each second-difference row by the adjacent-slope contrast
hi​μi+1​−μi​​−hi−1​μi​−μi−1​​.
(9)
Its three coefficients are 1/hi−1​, −(1/hi−1​+1/hi​) and 1/hi​. Vanishing means all adjacent slopes agree, again giving null space span(1,t) and rank p−2. Use this new B in the same statistic and null distribution. For p≤2, every mean profile at distinct times is affine, so there is no lack-of-linearity restriction to test.

For an integrable real function g, let R−​={x:g(x)<0}. For every measurable region R,
∫R​g−∫R−​​g=∫R∖R−​​g−∫R−​∖R​g≥0.
(10)
The first term is nonnegative because g≥0 there, and the integral subtracted in the second term is nonpositive. Hence the negative set minimizes the integral. Including or excluding any points where g=0 has no effect; changes on null sets do not matter either. This is the minimum-integral decision region principle. Integrability ensures the subtraction is defined, and it holds automatically for the weighted differences of probability density functions used below.

Principal component analysis replaces correlated measurements by uncorrelated linear compounds ordered by decreasing variance. Keeping the leading compounds gives a low-dimensional summary that preserves as much variance as possible under orthonormal projection. It is an unsupervised description of variation, rather than a guarantee that the high-variance directions are best for classification or prediction.
Write Xc​=X−μ. For a unit loading vector q, the variance of qTXc​ is qTΣq. The spectral theorem gives an orthonormal eigenbasis q1​,…,qp​ with eigenvalues λ1​≥⋯≥λp​≥0. If q=∑j​bj​qj​ and ∑j​bj2​=1, then
qTΣq=∑j​λj​bj2​≤λ1​.
(20)
Equality is attained at q1​. Requiring the next loading to be orthogonal to the first excludes its eigendirection, so the same argument gives q2​ and variance λ2​, and continues inductively. Repeated eigenvalues permit any orthonormal basis of the tied eigenspace. These are the population principal components.
With Q=(q1​,…,qp​), set Y=QTXc​. Then
Cov(Y)=Λ=QTΣQ=diag(λ1​,…,λp​),Σ=QΛQT.​
(21)
In particular the components are uncorrelated. They need not be independent unless, for example, X is multivariate normal. Finally
j=1∑p​Var(Yj​)=j=1∑p​λj​=tr(Σ)=j=1∑p​Var(Xj​).​
(22)
The trace equality follows from QTQ=I and cyclicity of trace, so the rotation preserves total variance. The explained variance of a principal component is its eigenvalue divided by this total.

Covariance-based PCA uses centered protein measurements in their original gram units. Its total variance is 218.4. Correlation-based PCA first divides each centered food measurement by its sample standard deviation, giving nine unit-variance variables and total variance nine. Thus it is principal component analysis on a correlation matrix, not an equivalent rotation of the original unscaled measurements.
The variances of cereals and milk together account for 170.9/218.4≈78.3% of the original total. Consequently those variables have considerable influence on covariance-based PCA. Its first component is dominated by a cereal coefficient −0.86 opposed to milk 0.42, broadly a cereal-versus-milk contrast. Its second is dominated by milk 0.83, with cereals 0.40 and fish −0.29. Its third is chiefly white meat 0.80 versus fish −0.52, with a smaller negative milk term. Component signs may all be reversed without changing the analysis; the important features are relative signs and magnitudes.
After standardization, eggs and starch can contribute as strongly as large-variance foods. The first correlation-based component gives positive weight to meat, eggs, milk and starch, opposed principally to cereals and pulses/nuts; eggs now has coefficient 0.43 despite its small raw variance. The second emphasizes fish and fruit/vegetables negatively, opposed especially to starch and white meat. The third contrasts white meat and fruit/vegetables with milk, fish and red meat. Thus standardization changes both the emphasis and the directions, rather than just the displayed units of the same components.
The explained variance of a principal component must use the appropriate total:
analysiscovariancecorrelation​PC171.1%44.6%​PC214.1%18.2%​PC37.1%12.6%​first three92.3%75.3%.​​
(26)
Raw and standardized percentages describe different variance objectives and must not be compared as if one method were always superior.
Standardize when relative patterns across variables, rather than their absolute gram-scale variation, should have equal marginal weight. Correlation-based PCA is also insensitive to changing a variable's measurement unit, which covariance-based PCA is not. Here every food group already has the same units, so differing units do not force standardization. If large fluctuations in actual protein amounts are scientifically important, covariance-based PCA is appropriate. If dietary composition across all food groups is the focus, correlation-based PCA can be more informative. Standardization can nevertheless amplify noise in nearly constant variables; the decision should follow the scientific purpose and measurement reliability.

For rows xi​,xj​, three useful choices are Euclidean distance, standardized Euclidean distance, and Mahalanobis distance:
dE​(i,j)=∑r=1p​(xir​−xjr​)2​,dS​(i,j)=∑r=1p​sr2​(xir​−xjr​)2​​,dM​(i,j)=(xi​−xj​)TS−1(xi​−xj​)​.
(29)
The Euclidean choice is simple, rotation invariant and appropriate when the original coordinate scales carry comparable meaning. It is sensitive to scale changes, may be dominated by a high-variance variable and gives correlated or duplicate variables repeated influence. Squared Euclidean dissimilarity is sometimes used algorithmically, but removing the square root generally loses the triangle inequality.
The standardized Euclidean choice removes marginal units using fixed positive sample scales sr​. It gives equal marginal weight to variables, but ignores correlations and can magnify noise when an estimated variance is small. Constant variables require omission or an explicitly chosen positive external scale.
The Mahalanobis choice incorporates the full positive-definite sample covariance S, so it is Euclidean distance after whitening. It accounts for both scale and redundancy and is invariant under an invertible change of coordinates when S is transformed consistently. However estimating S requires enough observations and can be sensitive to extreme values; inversion is unstable near singularity and unavailable when S is singular. With fixed positive scales or positive-definite S, all three square-root expressions are genuine metrics on the measurement space. Their different geometries encode different notions of which individuals should be considered similar.

In agglomerative hierarchical clustering, start with eight singleton groups. Single-linkage clustering uses the smallest distance between cross-pairs; complete-linkage clustering uses the largest. Updating these cluster distances after each merge, one valid order of the tied merges gives
step1234567​single clusterBDACEGABCDEGHEFGHABCDEFGH​height0.40.60.60.60.91.01.9​complete clusterBDACEGFHABCDEFGHABCDEFGH​height0.40.60.61.01.41.53.8​​
(34)
The merges at height 0.6 may be reordered; they give the same requested three-cluster cuts. For example, the single-link distance from AC to BD is min(1.2,1.4,0.6,0.8)=0.6, while its complete-link distance is their maximum 1.4. After forming EG, its single-link distance to H is min(0.9,1.2)=0.9. The complete-link distance between EG and FH is max(1.2,0.9,1.5,1.2)=1.5. Finally the closest cross-pair between ABCD and EFGH is CG at 1.9, while the farthest is BF at 3.8.
At three groups the partitions are therefore
single linkage: {A,B,C,D}, {E,G,H}, {F};​
(35)
complete linkage: {A,B,C,D}, {E,G}, {F,H}.​
(36)
Two groups, {A,B,C,D} and {E,F,G,H}, are more strongly supported by the merge heights. For single linkage the three-to-two merge is at 1.0, close to the preceding height 0.9, whereas the two-to-one merge is delayed until 1.9. For complete linkage the three-to-two merge at 1.5 is close to 1.4, whereas the final merge jumps to 3.8. Both methods agree on the two-group partition but split its second group differently when forced to return three groups. A three-group choice is possible if required substantively, but is not particularly compelling from these dissimilarities alone.
