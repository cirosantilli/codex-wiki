Let T be the set of three-vertex subsets and let IA​ indicate that A forms a triangle. Then
X=∑A∈T​IA​,pA​=EIA​=p3,μn​=(3n​)p3⟶α.
(21)
In particular p∼(6α)1/3/n. We will use the Stein-Chen method with edge-sharing dependency neighborhoods, rather than wrongly treating triangle indicators as mutually independent.
Here is the needed Poisson approximation with dependency neighborhoods, together with its proof. For indicators IA​ with means pA​, let BA​ contain A and suppose IA​ is independent of the entire family of indicators outside BA​. With μ=∑A​pA​ and W=∑A​IA​, define
b1​=∑A​∑C∈BA​​pA​pC​,b2​=∑A​∑C∈BA​∖{A}​E(IA​IC​).
(22)
The total variation distance convention is dTV​(P,Q)=supE​∣P(E)−Q(E)∣. We claim
dTV​(L(W),Poisson(μ))≤b1​+b2​.
(23)
For an indicator test function g on the nonnegative integers, solve the Poisson Stein equation
μf(k+1)−kf(k)=g(k)−Eg(Z),Z∼Poisson(μ),
(24)
with ∥Δf∥∞​≤1. This elementary Stein factor can be proved as follows. Take the immigration--death process with immigration rate μ and per-particle death rate one. Starting at k, its time-t distribution is
Ztk​=dBinomial(k,e−t)+Poisson(μ(1−e−t)),
(25)
with independent summands. Its stationary law is that of Z. Coupling the initial particles with a stationary Poisson initial population makes the centered expectations decay at least as a constant times (k+μ)e−t, so
u(k)=−∫0∞​(Eg(Ztk​)−Eg(Z))dt
(26)
is finite. Its generator equation is μ(u(k+1)−u(k))+k(u(k−1)−u(k))=g(k)−Eg(Z), obtained by integrating the semigroup derivative. Set f(k)=u(k)−u(k−1) for k≥1; choose f(0)=f(1), which does not affect the equation. To bound Δf(k), couple the processes starting at k−1,k,k+1 by adding two independent initial particles. Both survive to time t with probability e−2t, and otherwise their second difference cancels in expectation. Since ∣g(j+2)−2g(j+1)+g(j)∣≤2,
∣u(k+1)−2u(k)+u(k−1)∣≤∫0∞​2e−2tdt=1.
(27)
This proves the Stein factor for k≥1, and the chosen f(0) handles k=0.
Put WA​=W−∑C∈BA​​IC​. Independence gives E[IA​f(WA​+1)]=pA​Ef(WA​+1). Inserting this identity into the Stein equation yields
Eg(W)−Eg(Z)=​A∑​pA​E[f(W+1)−f(WA​+1)]+A∑​E[IA​(f(WA​+1)−f(W))].​
(28)
The first sum in absolute value is at most b1​, since the two arguments differ by ∑C∈BA​​IC​. In the second sum, on IA​=1 the arguments differ by ∑C∈BA​∖{A}​IC​, giving bound b2​. Taking the supremum over event indicators g proves the claimed total variation distance bound.
For triangle A, choose BA​ to consist of A itself and all triangles sharing an edge with A. There are exactly 1+3(n−3) such triangles. Every outside triangle uses none of the three edges of A, even if it shares one vertex, so IA​ is independent of their entire indicator family. For distinct neighboring triangles, their union has five edges and E(IA​IC​)=p5. Therefore
b1​=(3n​)(1+3(n−3))p6=O(n−2),b2​=(3n​)3(n−3)p5=O(n−1).
(29)
The Stein-Chen method gives dTV​(L(X),Poisson(μn​))→0. Coupling Poisson distributions of two means by adding an independent Poisson variable gives
dTV​(Poisson(μn​),Poisson(α))≤1−e−∣μn​−α∣≤∣μn​−α∣⟶0.
(30)
This proves the Poisson limit for triangles in a binomial random graph:
dTV​(L(X),Poisson(α))⟶0.​
(31)
Applying the same bound to the event {1,2,…} gives
P(X≥1)⟶1−e−α.​
(32)
For finite n this is an approximation, not an exact formula: the error from 1−e−μn​ is at most b1​+b2​, and the error from 1−e−α is at most b1​+b2​+∣μn​−α∣.
