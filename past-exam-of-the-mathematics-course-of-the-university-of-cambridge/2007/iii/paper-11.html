For a continuous boundary function g(eiθ), its Poisson integral on the unit disk is
V(z)=2π1​∫02π​g(eiθ)∣eiθ−z∣21−∣z∣2​dθ,∣z∣<1.

The kernel is positive and has integral 2π: expand the holomorphic fraction as 1+2∑n≥1​zne−inθ and integrate. As z approaches a boundary point, the mass outside any fixed arc around that point tends to zero. Uniform continuity of g therefore shows that V extends continuously with boundary values g.
Apply this with g=u∣∂D​. The maximum principle for harmonic functions applied to the harmonic difference u−V, which vanishes on the boundary, gives
u(z)=2π1​∫02π​u(eiθ)∣eiθ−z∣21−∣z∣2​dθ.​
(9)
At boundary points the formula is understood through its continuous interior limit.
Now suppose a continuous function has the mean value property and has a local maximum M=u(z) at z. Shrink the neighborhood so that u≤M there and all sufficiently small centered circles satisfy the mean value property. On each such circle the average is M. If one value were smaller than M, continuity would make it smaller on an arc of positive length, contradicting this average. Thus every sufficiently small circle, and hence an entire neighborhood of z, has value M.
A harmonic function has the mean value property. Indeed, if m(r) is its average on a circle centered at z, the divergence theorem gives
m′(r)=2πr1​∫∣w−z∣<r​Δu(w)dA(w)=0.
(10)
As r↓0, m(r)→u(z), giving the asserted equality.
Conversely, suppose u is continuous and has the mean value property. On an arbitrary closed disk compactly contained in its domain, form the harmonic Poisson integral V with boundary values u. The difference w=u−V is continuous, has the mean value property, and vanishes on the boundary. If its maximum were positive, it would be attained inside. The set attaining that maximum is relatively closed and, by the local argument already proved, relatively open. Connectedness of the disk makes w constant, contradicting its boundary value. Thus w≤0. Applying the same argument to −w gives w≥0. Therefore u=V on every such disk, proving
u has the mean value property ⟺ u is harmonic.​

The Blaschke product here is taken over distinct points of the orbit; its existence is part of the hypothesis. It is not necessary to assert that every discrete orbit satisfies the Blaschke condition.
The modulus of a Blaschke factor is the pseudohyperbolic distance. Thus, away from the orbit,
∣B(z)∣=∏a∈G(0)​​1−azz−a​​=∏a∈G(0)​tanh2ρ(z,a)​.
(20)
The logarithms converge absolutely at such a point, by the compact convergence estimate for the Blaschke product. Every T∈G preserves the hyperbolic metric and permutes the orbit. Reindexing this convergent product yields ∣B(Tz)∣=∣B(z)∣.
The functions B∘T and B have the same simple zero set, since T is a biholomorphism. Their quotient therefore extends holomorphically and without zeros across the orbit. It has modulus one everywhere. The open mapping theorem makes it a constant, denoted χ(T). Finally
B(STz)=χ(S)B(Tz)=χ(S)χ(T)B(z).
(21)
Since B is not identically zero, cancellation gives
χ(ST)=χ(S)χ(T),∣χ(T)∣=1,B(Tz)=χ(T)B(z).​
(22)
This is the automorphy character of an orbit Blaschke product. The equal-modulus argument is essential: equality of zero sets alone would not make the quotient of two bounded holomorphic functions constant.

A representative of an element of G is a determinant-one matrix (ab​ba​) over the Gaussian integers, considered up to its common sign. It acts by a Möbius transformation of the disk. Since T(0)=b/a and ∣a∣2−∣b∣2=1, we have
∣a∣2=1−∣T(0)∣21​.
(23)
Only finitely many Gaussian-integer pairs (a,b) can have T(0) in a fixed compact subdisk. More generally, if T carries some point of a compact set K into K, and K lies in the hyperbolic ball of radius R about zero, the triangle inequality gives ρ(0,T0)≤2R. Thus there are only finitely many such T. This proves the properly discontinuous group action, and in particular discreteness. The specified A,B belong to G, so their subgroup H is discrete as well.
The fixed-point equations reduce to −i(z−1)2=0 for A and i(z+1)2=0 for B. Thus their unique fixed points are respectively 1 and −1 on the ideal boundary. Neither transformation is the identity; their displayed determinant-one representatives have trace two. They are therefore parabolic Möbius transformations.
To find the Dirichlet domains, use the Möbius transformation
C(z)=i1−z1+z​,C(0)=i.
(24)
It takes the disk to the upper half-plane, and direct substitution gives
CAC−1(w)=w−2,CBC−1(w)=2w+1w​.
(25)
For w=x+iy and v in the upper half-plane, the hyperbolic distance obeys coshρ(w,v)=1+∣w−v∣2/(2yImv). Since C(Ak0)=i−2k, the distance inequalities are equivalent to
x2+(y−1)2≤(x+2k)2+(y−1)2(k∈Z).
(26)
These reduce to kx+k2≥0 for all integers k. The cases k=1,−1 imply −1≤x≤1, which also suffices for every other k. Therefore the cyclic Dirichlet domain becomes the strip ∣Rew∣≤1.
The two vertical hyperbolic geodesics pull back to arcs joining 1 to i and 1 to −i. In disk coordinates they are the circles ∣z−(1+i)∣=1 and ∣z−(1−i)∣=1, restricted to the disk. Thus
DA​={z∈D:∣z−(1+i)∣≥1, ∣z−(1−i)∣≥1}.​
(27)
Conjugating by z↦−z gives B, so its two hyperbolic geodesics join −1 to i and −1 to −i, and
DB​={z∈D:∣z−(−1+i)∣≥1, ∣z−(−1−i)∣≥1}.​

For any z0​, the orbit points satisfying ρ(0,Tz0​)≤ρ(0,z0​) form a nonempty finite set, by proper discontinuity and compactness of closed hyperbolic balls. Choose a closest one, q=Tz0​. For every integer k, its minimality gives
ρ(0,q)≤ρ(0,A−kq)=ρ(Ak0,q),
(29)
and the same inequality with B. Hence q lies in the ideal hyperbolic quadrilateral
P=DA​∩DB​={z∈D:∣z−(ε+iη)∣≥1 for ε,η∈{1,−1}}.​
(30)
Its ideal vertices are 1,i,−1,−i.
For the quotient identification, it remains to justify that this covering region is a fundamental polygon. The four open corner caps excluded by P are pairwise disjoint inside the disk. The transformation A carries the exterior of the lower-right cap into the upper-right cap; A−1 reverses the pairing. Similarly B carries the exterior of the upper-left cap into the lower-left cap, and B−1 reverses this. These assertions follow either from the upper-half-plane formulas or by mapping their boundary circles and testing zero: A0=(1+i)/2 and B0=(−1−i)/2.
Apply a reduced word in A±1,B±1 from right to left to a point in the interior of P. The first letter puts it into its target cap. Every subsequent letter can do the same, since its inverse cap is distinct from the preceding target cap unless the word cancels. Thus the final point lies in the target cap of the leftmost letter, never in the interior of P. This ping-pong lemma argument proves freeness and disjointness of distinct translated interiors. Together with orbit coverage it makes P a fundamental polygon. The corresponding argument on closed caps shows that boundary identifications are generated by the stated side pairings.
The generator A pairs the side from 1 to −i with the side from 1 to i, fixing the ideal endpoint 1 and sending −i to i. The generator B pairs the side from −1 to i with the side from −1 to −i, fixing −1 and sending i to −i. There are three ideal-vertex classes: {1}, {−1} and {i,−i}. The first two give parabolic ends from A,B; the third does too, since AB is a nonidentity parabolic transformation fixing i. Adding one point at each end produces an oriented closed surface with one face, two edges and three vertices. Its Euler characteristic is 1−2+3=2, so it is a sphere. Removing the three added points gives a thrice-punctured sphere, proving
D/H is homeomorphic to a thrice-punctured sphere.​

On a connected Riemann surface R, a Perron family of subharmonic functions is a nonempty family P of real continuous subharmonic functions closed under pairwise maxima and under harmonic lifting on every relatively compact coordinate disk. A harmonic lifting of a continuous subharmonic function replaces its values inside such a disk by the harmonic Poisson integral of its boundary values, leaving the function unchanged outside. Harmonic comparison shows that the lift dominates the original function. The lift is continuous and remains subharmonic by harmonic comparison across the circle.
Write U=supv∈P​v. Because the family is nonempty and its members are finite, U cannot be minus infinity. Suppose first U(p)<∞, and choose a relatively compact coordinate disk V containing p. Choose vn​∈P approaching U(p) at p. Lift v1​ on V to get h1​, and recursively lift max(hn−1​,vn​) to get hn​. These are members of the family, harmonic on V, and increasing. Also hn​(p)→U(p).
The nonnegative harmonic functions hn​−h1​ are bounded at p. The Harnack inequality for harmonic functions, on chains of compact subdisks, bounds them uniformly on every compact subset of V. The Poisson integral then bounds their derivatives on smaller subdisks. Compact convergence and the mean value property show that their increasing limit is finite and harmonic; denote the limit of hn​ by h.
Fix an arbitrary v∈P, and let kn​ be the lift of max(v,hn​) on V. These functions increase, are harmonic on V, and satisfy hn​≤kn​≤U. They are bounded at p, so the same Harnack inequality for harmonic functions argument gives a harmonic limit k. At p, k(p)=h(p)=U(p), whereas k≥h on V. The nonnegative harmonic difference has an interior zero, so the strong maximum principle for harmonic functions gives k=h. Since v≤kn​, we have v≤h. Taking the supremum over v gives U≤h, and hn​≤U gives the opposite inequality. Thus U=h on V.
The finite locus of U is open by this argument. It is also closed: if a point is a limit of finite points, choose a coordinate disk around it and a finite point in that disk; the preceding argument applied with that finite point shows that U is finite on the entire disk. Connectedness now proves the dichotomy
U≡+∞orU is finite and harmonic on R.​
(32)
Both occur. The family of all continuous subharmonic functions bounded above by zero is a Perron family of subharmonic functions, since the maximum principle for harmonic functions keeps every lift below zero; it contains zero, so its supremum is zero. The family of constant functions with positive integer values is also a Perron family of subharmonic functions, and its supremum is everywhere infinite.
For the final application, the radius must enclose the evaluation point. For ∣z∣<r<1 set
Hr​(z)=2π1​∫02π​u(reiθ)∣reiθ−z∣2r2−∣z∣2​dθ.
(33)
This is the harmonic lifting of a continuous subharmonic function on the radius-r disk. Hence Hr​≥u there. If ∣z∣<r<s<1, Hs​≥u=Hr​ on the radius-r boundary, so harmonic comparison gives Hs​≥Hr​ throughout the smaller disk. The increasing limit is either everywhere infinite or finite and harmonic, by the preceding Harnack argument and propagation across overlapping disks. In the finite case it dominates u. Any harmonic majorant h of u dominates Hr​ by boundary comparison and therefore dominates their limit. Thus the precise conclusion is
hmin​(z)=r↑1lim​Hr​(z)=∣z∣<r<1sup​Hr​(z),​
(34)
provided a finite harmonic majorant exists. This is the least harmonic majorant by expanding disk lifts.
There are two necessary qualifications to the printed formula. First, the supremum cannot include r<∣z∣. For example, if u≡−1 and z=0, the kernel has normalized integral 1 for r>∣z∣ and −1 for r<∣z∣. The displayed integral is consequently −1 in the first case and +1 in the second. The unrestricted supremum would be +1, although the least harmonic majorant is −1; at r=∣z∣ the kernel is undefined. Second, a finite harmonic majorant need not exist. The continuous subharmonic function
u(z)=1−∣z∣21​,Δu(z)=(1−∣z∣2)34(1+∣z∣2)​>0,
(35)
has Hr​≡(1−r2)−1→+∞. If h≥u were harmonic, its centered circular means would equal h(0) and dominate (1−r2)−1 for every r, an impossibility. In this case the limit formula holds only in the extended sense, with value +∞ and no finite least harmonic majorant.

A hyperbolic Riemann surface in potential theory is a noncompact Riemann surface admitting a positive Green function on a Riemann surface. With pole p, this is a positive function harmonic off p, having local form −log∣ζ∣ plus a harmonic function in a coordinate ζ(p)=0, and obtained as the minimal such positive function by exhaustion. This is a noncircular definition for the present proof. For a simply connected surface it is equivalent to disk conformal type; for arbitrary surfaces potential-theoretic hyperbolicity and disk universal-cover type should be distinguished.
The unit disk is an example. At pole zero its function is G(z,0)=−log∣z∣: it is positive in the punctured disk, harmonic there, has the correct logarithmic singularity, and tends to zero at the boundary. If another positive function has the same singularity and is harmonic elsewhere, subtract G and apply the maximum principle for harmonic functions on disks of radius r<1; the boundary lower bound is logr. Letting r↑1 shows that the other function dominates G. Thus G is indeed the positive Green function on a Riemann surface. At a general pole it is
G(z,p)=log​z−p1−p​z​​.​
(36)
Here is a Green-function exhaustion proof of disk uniformization. The main analytic idea is to build conformal maps on bordered disks from their Green functions and pass to an extremal limit. The positive global Green function prevents that limit from becoming constant.
First exhaust the noncompact simply connected surface R by relatively compact domains Rn​ containing p, each with a smooth Jordan boundary. This topological step does not use conformal uniformization: take regular neighborhoods of finitely many coordinate disks covering successive compact sets and fill bounded complementary components. Simple connectedness and the Jordan curve theorem make the filled neighborhoods topological disks; enlarge them to be nested.
Each Rn​ has a zero-boundary Green function on a Riemann surface Gn​(⋅,p). One analytic construction, independent of conformal uniformization, is as follows. Equip the compact bordered domain with a smooth conformal metric. Multiply −log∣ζ∣ by a smooth cutoff function equal to one near p and zero near the boundary; call the result s. Distributionally
−Δs=2πδp​+k,
(37)
where k is smooth and supported away from p. Solve Δv=k with zero boundary data. This auxiliary Dirichlet problem can be obtained by minimizing 21​∫∣∇v∣2+∫kv over functions with zero boundary trace: the Poincaré inequality gives coercivity, minimization in a Hilbert space gives a weak solution, and interior and smooth-boundary elliptic regularity give a smooth solution. Then Gn​=s+v is harmonic away from p, has the logarithmic pole, and vanishes at the boundary. It is positive by the maximum principle for harmonic functions, applied after removing a sufficiently small circle around its positive pole. This construction uses ordinary linear elliptic existence, not the desired uniformization theorem. Equivalently, the same bordered-domain Green functions can be constructed by the Perron method for the Dirichlet problem using local logarithmic barriers.
On Rn​∖{p} choose local harmonic conjugates Gn∗​. Their period around the pole is −2π, and every closed loop is homologous to an integer multiple of that loop. Therefore
fn​=exp(−Gn​−iGn∗​)
(38)
is a single-valued holomorphic function. It extends across p with one simple zero and has no others. Since Gn​>0, ∣fn​∣<1; since Gn​=0 on the boundary, ∣fn​∣→1 there. Thus fn​:Rn​→D is proper. For any a∈D, choose a smooth interior contour sufficiently close to the boundary that ∣fn​∣>∣a∣ everywhere on it. The contour surrounds p and all possible preimages of a, because ∣fn​∣>∣a∣ throughout a thin boundary collar. By Rouché's theorem, fn​−a has the same number of zeros inside as fn​, namely one. Hence fn​ is onto and one-to-one, with nonzero derivative: it is a biholomorphism from Rn​ to the disk.
Write near p
Gn​=−log∣ζ∣+cn​+o(1).
(39)
Multiply fn​ by a unit constant so that, in the fixed coordinate, fn′​(p)=e−cn​>0. Domain monotonicity gives Gn​≤Gn+1​: their difference is harmonic even at p and nonnegative on ∂Rn​. The global positive Green function on a Riemann surface GR​ similarly majorizes every Gn​. Comparing the regular parts shows that cn​ increases and is bounded above by the finite regular part of GR​ at p. Consequently
fn′​(p)⟶d>0.
(40)
On each fixed compact subdomain, the fn​ are eventually bounded holomorphic functions. Montel theorem and a diagonal subsequence therefore give a locally uniform convergence limit F:R→D with F(p)=0 and F′(p)=d>0. The maximum modulus principle keeps its values strictly inside the disk. It is injective: for fixed q, the functions fn​−fn​(q) have no zeros away from q; Hurwitz's theorem implies that their nonconstant limit has no other zeros. This is the locally uniform limit of univalent functions argument.
There is also an extremality property. For any holomorphic function g:R→D with g(p)=0, the function g∘fn−1​ is a disk self-map fixing zero. The Schwarz lemma gives ∣g′(p)∣≤fn′​(p). Passing to the limit yields ∣g′(p)∣≤d=F′(p).
Finally suppose F omits a∈D. Necessarily a=0. Apply the disk Möbius transformation ϕa​(w)=(w−a)/(1−aw). The nowhere-zero function ϕa​∘F has a global holomorphic logarithm because R is simply connected, hence a holomorphic square root h. It maps into the disk and ∣h(p)∣=∣a∣​. Normalize it by setting g=ϕh(p)​∘h, so g(p)=0. Direct differentiation gives
∣g′(p)∣=2∣a∣​(1−∣a∣)1−∣a∣2​∣F′(p)∣=2∣a∣​1+∣a∣​d>d.
(41)
The strict inequality follows from (1−∣a∣​)2>0. This contradicts extremality. Therefore F omits no point, and we conclude
R is conformally equivalent to D.​
