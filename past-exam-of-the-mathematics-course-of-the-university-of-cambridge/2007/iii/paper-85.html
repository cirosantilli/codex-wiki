Local equilibrium and Hopf bifurcations do not locate the larger cycles' endpoints. In the large-s case, the stable origin cycle is confined between two symmetry-related saddles. A natural global completion is a heteroclinic cycle formed by the two separatrix connections between those saddles. The stable periodic orbit expands into that connecting cycle and disappears; the two connections occur together because (q,p)↦(−q,−p) exchanges them. This is a global transition in the positive-κ, positive-λ region where the Bendixson-Dulac criterion permits cycles. Beyond it, the cubic inverted potential allows trajectories to leave the local region; the local reduction does not specify their ultimate fate in the original four-dimensional system.
In the small-s case, the two stable cycles born around the nonzero well equilibria can encounter the central saddle simultaneously. Their limiting orbits form a symmetric figure-eight pair of homoclinic orbits. A symmetric homoclinic gluing bifurcation can then replace the two one-well cycles by one larger cycle visiting both wells. There is also an outer unstable cycle associated with the subcritical origin Hopf branch, continued into the double-well region. A saddle-node bifurcation of periodic orbits between the large stable and unstable cycles provides a possible later termination. Thus the global curves needed for the usual completion are a symmetric heteroclinic connection in the large-s case, and paired homoclinic gluing together with a fold of cycles in the small-s case.
These connections, and any resulting basin changes, are not detected by the equilibrium Jacobians. Their precise parameter curves require a global return-map or separatrix calculation, which is not part of this requested qualitative description. Symmetry forces paired connections where indicated, but the local centre manifold and Hopf calculations alone should not be presented as numerical locations or a proof of the full large-amplitude diagram.

Let a finite or compact group G act linearly on a real space V, and let x˙=F(x,μ) be a smooth equivariant dynamical system: F(gx,μ)=gF(x,μ). Assume F(0,μ)=0. The representation is an absolutely irreducible real representation when its complexification is irreducible; equivalently, every real linear operator commuting with G is a scalar multiple of the identity. Consequently the linearization at the trivial branch is Dx​F(0,μ)=α(μ)I. A generic steady-state crossing has α(0)=0 and α′(0)=0, with stable noncritical directions removed by a centre manifold if this is a reduction of a larger system.
The isotropy group of a vector x is Σx​={g∈G:gx=x}. For a subgroup Σ, its fixed-point subspace of a group action is Fix(Σ)={x:σx=x for every σ∈Σ}. Its normaliser is
NG​(Σ)={g∈G:gΣg−1=Σ}.
(31)
The normaliser preserves Fix(Σ), and NG​(Σ)/Σ describes its residual symmetry. A steady-state axial subgroup is an actual isotropy subgroup with dimR​Fix(Σ)=1.
The equivariant branching lemma states that, under the smoothness, equivariance, absolute irreducibility, and transverse scalar-eigenvalue crossing hypotheses above, each conjugacy class of axial isotropy subgroup gives a branch of nonzero equilibria in its fixed-point line through (0,0). The generic leading nonlinear coefficient determines which parameter side the branch occupies and whether it is transcritical-like or pitchfork-like; the theorem does not assert that these branches are stable or exhaust every possible branch. The usual nondegeneracy condition excludes a vanishing leading nonlinear coefficient. Conjugate subgroups produce symmetry-related copies of a branch, rather than new isotropy types.
To explain the dynamical role of the fixed-point subspaces of a group action, if x is fixed by Σ, then σF(x,μ)=F(σx,μ)=F(x,μ) for every σ∈Σ. Thus the vector field is tangent to that subspace, and uniqueness makes it flow invariant. On a fixed-point line one solves a scalar equilibrium equation and determines radial stability. Stability in the full space additionally requires all transverse eigenvalues to be stable. Higher-dimensional fixed-point spaces support their own restricted dynamics and may contain symmetry-constrained connecting orbits.
Finally, the normalizer action determines axial branch parity: if NG​(Σ)/Σ contains an element acting as x↦−x on the fixed line, its scalar vector field must be odd, so a generic cubic term gives a pitchfork bifurcation. If that residual sign reversal is absent, a quadratic term is permitted and can give a transcritical-like branch. This is why fixed-space dimension alone does not determine either the branch type or its full stability.

First there is a necessary coordinate qualification. With the literal z action obtained in part (c), the temporal weights are −1,+1 and reflection is conjugation followed by exchange. A linear term with (μ+iω) in both equations fails reflection equivariance for ω=0, since conjugation changes the sign of iω. The proposed first-component monomial zˉ1​z22​ also has temporal weight +3, rather than −1. Thus the displayed equations cannot be proved in those literal coordinates. They are the correct dihedral fourfold Hopf normal form in the common-phase Hopf coordinates (q1​,q2​)=(z1​,zˉ2​).
In these coordinates the temporal circle imposes total phase weight one on every vector-field monomial. There are no quadratic monomials with that weight. A cubic monomial has two unbarred factors and one barred factor. For the first component, rotation by ρ must multiply the monomial by i. Inspecting the six phase-compatible monomials leaves precisely
q12​qˉ​1​=q1​∣q1​∣2,q1​q2​qˉ​2​=q1​∣q2​∣2,q22​qˉ​1​.
(42)
The excluded three monomials multiply by −i instead. Reflection swaps the components, so their coefficients must be the same after swapping indices. The linear part is a scalar complex multiple by absolute irreducibility. Orient the Hopf parameter to write it as μ+iω, and name the cubic coefficients −a,−b,−c, where a,b,c are generally complex. This proves
q˙​1​q˙​2​​=(μ+iω)q1​−aq1​∣q1​∣2−bq1​∣q2​∣2−cqˉ​1​q22​,=(μ+iω)q2​−aq2​∣q2​∣2−bq2​∣q1​∣2−cqˉ​2​q12​.​​
(43)
These are cubic amplitude normal forms of a dynamical system, after the usual symmetry-compatible Hopf normalization. Returning to literal z coordinates conjugates the second equation: its linear coefficient is μ−iω, its cubic coefficients are aˉ,bˉ,cˉ, and the first coupling is −czˉ1​zˉ22​. That corrected form respects every printed generator.
On the three complex axial isotropy subgroup fixed spaces, substitute q1​=ReiΩt and q2​=0,q1​,iq1​. The radial equations are R˙=R(μ−Aj​R2), with
AR​=Rea,AA​=Re(a+b+c),AD​=Re(a+b−c).
(44)
Therefore the leading component amplitudes are
RR2​=Reaμ​,RA2​=Re(a+b+c)μ​,RD2​=Re(a+b−c)μ​.​
(45)
Each expression applies on the parameter side where it is positive, with its denominator nonzero for a nondegenerate branch. The frequencies are Ωj​=ω−Im(aj​)Rj2​, with aj​=a,a+b+c,a+b−c. The denominators are generically distinct, so coexisting branches generically have different component amplitudes. If the amplitude is instead measured by the original real-space norm, the identity ∣w1​∣2+∣w2​∣2=2(∣q1​∣2+∣q2​∣2) gives squared amplitudes 2RR2​, 4RA2​, and 4RD2​, again generically distinct.
