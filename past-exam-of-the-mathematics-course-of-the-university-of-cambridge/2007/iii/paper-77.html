In the Boussinesq approximation, density departures from a constant reference density ρ0​ are small enough to neglect in inertia and mass continuity, but their gravitational effect is retained. Write the total buoyancy acceleration as b=−g(ρ−ρ0​)/ρ0​, choose a hydrostatic background bˉ(z), and set σ=b−bˉ. Thus σ is the buoyancy perturbation, positive for a parcel lighter than the background at its present height. The squared buoyancy frequency is
N2(z)=bˉz​=−ρ0​g​ρˉ​z​.
(1)
Stable stratification has N2>0. A small vertical displacement ξ of a parcel conserving its buoyancy gives σ=−N2ξ, explaining the restoring acceleration and the name of N.
Let P=p′/ρ0​ be pressure with the background hydrostatic pressure removed, u=(u,v,w), and D/Dt=∂t​+u⋅∇ the material derivative. The ideal, nonrotating Boussinesq equations are
DtDu​=−∇P+σz^,∇⋅u=0,DtDσ​+N2(z)w=0.​
(2)
The last equation is equivalent to Db/Dt=0. There is neither viscosity nor buoyancy diffusion. The reference density occurs in the pressure normalization, while the small density anomaly occurs only through buoyancy.
For the two-dimensional disturbance, linearization about rest gives
ut​=−Px​,wt​=−Pz​+σ,σt​+N2w=0,ux​+wz​=0.
(3)
A plane internal gravity wave proportional to ei(kx+mz−ωt) therefore satisfies
w=−mk​u,P=kω​u,σ=−ωiN2​w,ω2=k2+m2N2k2​.​
(4)
These internal-wave polarization relations and the dispersion relation determine the individual responses in parts i and ii. A radiating response has no incoming internal gravity wave from x=+∞; an evanescent response is bounded there. The harmonic solutions describe the response after excluding arbitrary initial transients.
For the localized oscillatory boundary, define α=ω/N2−ω2​ and I(z)=ϵ∫0∞​u^0​(m)eimzdm=f(z)+ig(z). Both signs of vertical wavenumber must radiate into x>0. Their horizontal wavenumber is k=α∣m∣, so the positive-m contribution involves Z1​=z+αx and the negative-m contribution involves Z2​=z−αx. Consequently the complex horizontal velocity is
uc​=e−iωt(I(Z1​)+I(Z2​)∗).
(5)
Taking its real part proves
u=(f(Z1​)+f(Z2​))cosωt+(g(Z1​)−g(Z2​))sinωt.​
(6)
The positive-m internal-wave polarization has w=−αu and the negative-m one has w=+αu. Hence
w=α((f(Z2​)−f(Z1​))cosωt−(g(Z1​)+g(Z2​))sinωt).
(7)
In particular, 2f(z)=ϵu0​(z), so the boundary condition is recovered exactly. This is two-beam radiation from a localized oscillating boundary.
Take a smooth localized profile, for example u0​(z)=e−z2/H2. Its effective support is a neighbourhood of width a few H; increasing that neighbourhood makes the tails arbitrarily small. The boundary displacement is ϵu0​(z)sinωt/ω to first order, so at t=0 it is flat and moving at maximum speed. At that instant,
(u,w)=(f(Z1​)+f(Z2​), α(f(Z2​)−f(Z1​))),f(z)=2ϵ​e−z2/H2.
(8)
There are two narrow oblique beams centered on z=−αx and z=+αx. Away from their overlap, the velocity is parallel to the relevant beam, in the directions (1,−α) and (1,+α). The figure uses α=3/4 and measures velocities in units of ϵ.
At other phases the g terms contribute. For the positive-frequency convention above, g is the Hilbert transform of f; it generally has longer tails than f. Thus even when the boundary is nearly stationary, the interior need not be stationary or confined to the bright cores shown here. Half a period later all velocities reverse. The beam directions remain the same, but the instantaneous velocity profile and its quadrature component change.
A short pulse has a broad frequency spectrum. Frequencies below N produce outgoing internal gravity wave packets with frequency-dependent ray slopes ±ω/N2−ω2​; frequencies above N contribute an evanescent near-boundary response. Moreover the group velocity depends on wavelength as well as direction. The radiated packet therefore spreads into a dispersive fan or wake, with different frequencies traveling at different angles and different scales separating along those directions. After the forcing ends the propagating packet travels away, rather than maintaining the two permanently driven monochromatic beams.

For 0<ω<N, put α=ω/N2−ω2​. The dispersion relation permits k=±αm. On the positive-frequency branch, for k>0,
ω=k2+m2​Nk​,cgx​=(k2+m2)3/2Nm2​>0,cgz​=−(k2+m2)3/2Nkm​.
(9)
The radiation condition therefore selects k=αm, since energy must travel into the fluid. Substitution into the internal-wave polarization relations gives the complex fields
u=ϵei(αmx+mz−ωt),w=−αϵei(αmx+mz−ωt),σ=ωiN2αϵ​ei(αmx+mz−ωt).​
(10)
The physical fields are their real parts; v=0. The vertical component of group velocity is negative, so this positive-m plane internal gravity wave carries energy down and right, although its phase propagates in a different direction.

For ω>N, the dispersion relation gives k2=−m2ω2/(ω2−N2). Put κ=mω/ω2−N2​>0. Boundedness as x→+∞ selects k=iκ, rather than the exponentially growing root. The internal-wave polarization relations now yield
u=ϵe−κx+i(mz−ωt),w=−miκ​ϵe−κx+i(mz−ωt),σ=−mωN2κ​ϵe−κx+i(mz−ωt).​
(11)
Again take real parts and set v=0. This is an evanescent wave, decaying over distance κ−1; there is no propagating far-field internal gravity wave at this frequency. The pressure amplitude P=−iωu/κ is in quadrature with the horizontal velocity, so the time-averaged horizontal pressure-work flux vanishes. The equality ω=N is a singular limiting case and is outside the two frequency ranges considered here.

Use the streamfunction convention u=ψz​, w=−ψx​, and take v=0. With J(A,B)=Ax​Bz​−Az​Bx​, the material derivative is D=∂t​−J(ψ,⋅). This convention differs from the horizontal quasi-geostrophic streamfunction convention used below. The nonlinear Boussinesq equations are
ut​+uux​+wuz​=−Px​,wt​+uwx​+wwz​=−Pz​+σ,ux​+wz​=0.
(12)
The y component of vorticity is ζ=uz​−wx​=∇2ψ. Differentiate the first momentum equation in z and subtract the x derivative of the second. The pressure terms cancel; the derivatives of the advecting velocity cancel by ux​+wz​=0. Thus Dζ=−σx​, or
∇2ψt​+σx​=J(ψ,∇2ψ).​
(13)
Conservation of total buoyancy, with background gradient N2(z), gives
σt​−N2ψx​=J(ψ,σ).​
(14)
Both equations retain the nonlinear advection terms.
Write ψ=ψˉ​(z)+ϕ, with ψˉ​z​=U(z)=uˉ(z), and take the background buoyancy anomaly to be zero. The linear equations for (ϕ,σ) are
(∂t​+U∂x​)∇2ϕ−U′′ϕx​+σx​=0,(∂t​+U∂x​)σ−N2ϕx​=0.
(15)
For a normal mode ϕ=ψ^​(z)eik(x−ct), the second equation gives σ^=N2ψ^​/(U−c). Substitute this into the first to obtain the Taylor–Goldstein equation
ψ^​zz​+(ℓ2−k2)ψ^​=0,ℓ2=(U−c)2N2​−U−cU′′​.​
(16)
Here k=0 and the calculation is initially away from critical levels U=c. The first term is the restoring effect of stable buoyancy on vertically displaced parcels. The second comes from advection of the background vorticity gradient U′′: a displacement creates a vorticity anomaly, whose induced velocity acts back on the displacement. This is the shear-flow counterpart of a Rossby wave restoring mechanism; its sign can also permit instability, so it is not invariably stabilizing.
Rigid boundaries imply w=−ϕx​=0, hence ψ^​(0)=ψ^​(πH)=0. For a real, regular normal mode, multiply the Taylor–Goldstein equation by ψ^​, integrate, and integrate its second derivative by parts. The endpoint term vanishes, leaving
k2=∫0πH​ψ^​2dz∫0πH​(ℓ2ψ^​2−ψ^​z2​)dz​=I(ψ^​;c).​
(17)
For a smooth real dispersion branch, differentiate this identity with respect to k. The stated stationarity of I removes the term involving dψ^​/dk, so 2k=Ic​dc/dk. Direct differentiation at fixed ψ^​ gives
∂c​ℓ2=(U−c)32N2​−(U−c)2U′′​=(U−c)3N2​+U−cℓ2​.
(18)
Since the frequency is kc, the group velocity is d(kc)/dk, and therefore
cg​=c+∫0πH​((U−c)3N2​+U−cℓ2​)ψ^​2dz2k2∫0πH​ψ^​2dz​.​
(19)
This is variational group velocity of a Taylor-Goldstein mode. Singular critical levels or a vanishing denominator require separate limiting analysis; no stationarity proof is needed here.
For the sinusoidal profiles, choose c=0. In the interior,
ℓ2=U02​N02​​+H21​,ψ^​n​=sin(nz/H),kn2​=U02​N02​​+H21−n2​.​
(20)
The profile ratios extend to the endpoints in this reduced equation. Thus n=1 always gives a nonzero real k, with k12​=N02​/U02​. For this normal mode,
∫0πH​ψ^​12​dz=2πH​,∫0πH​sin(z/H)ψ^​12​​dz=2H.
(21)
The denominator in the group velocity expression is 2H(2N02​/U03​+1/(U0​H2)). It follows that
cg,1​=2πU0​​2N02​H2+U02​N02​H2​.​
(22)
Although U vanishes at the endpoints, this weighted integral is finite; the vanishing of ψ^​1​ supplies the required cancellation. The formula is the limiting derivative approached from the regular real branch with c<0, before any interior critical level is encountered.
If instead N=N0​ and U=U0​ are constant, still take c=0. The same vertical eigenfunctions satisfy the Taylor–Goldstein equation, now with
kn2​=U02​N02​​−H2n2​.​
(23)
There is no value of n≥1 guaranteed to give real k for arbitrary positive parameters: if N0​H<U0​, all these squared wavenumbers are negative. A nonzero real k exists exactly when N0​H>U0​; equality gives only the degenerate n=1, k=0 limit. For a real branch the same group velocity calculation gives cg​=kn2​U03​/N02​.
To test exact nonlinear validity, take a real normal mode ϕ=Asin(nz/H)cos(kx) and its corresponding buoyancy perturbation σ=a(z)ϕ, where a=N2/(U−c). Because
∇2ϕ=−(k2+H2n2​)ϕ,
(24)
the quadratic vorticity self-advection is zero: J(ϕ,∇2ϕ)=0. The quadratic buoyancy self-advection, however, is
J(ϕ,aϕ)=a′(z)ϕϕx​.​
(25)
This nonlinear exactness test for a stratified streamfunction mode distinguishes the cases. For constant N,U, a=N02​/U0​ is constant, so both quadratic terms vanish. The background-mode terms already satisfy the linear equations, hence the constant-profile modes are exact finite-amplitude solutions of the ideal Boussinesq equations. Pressure can be recovered from the momentum equations: the vorticity equation supplies their compatibility condition in this simply connected strip.
For the sinusoidal profiles, a=(N02​/U0​)sin(z/H) has a nonzero derivative in the interior. For every nonzero A and nonzero real k, a′ϕϕx​ is not identically zero; it produces a second horizontal harmonic that is absent from the proposed solution. Thus none of these nontrivial sinusoidal-profile modes is an exact finite-amplitude solution. A k=0 field is merely an added horizontal shear with no vertical motion and is a degenerate exception to the wave calculation, not a propagating member of it.

For the shallow-water approximation, let the vertical scale be much smaller than the horizontal scale, so vertical acceleration is negligible and pressure is hydrostatic. Assume constant density, a flat impermeable bottom, a free surface under constant atmospheric pressure, and a horizontally moving column with no leading-order vertical shear. Neglect friction. Taking the bottom at z=0, hydrostatic pressure is p=pa​+ρg(h−z), so its horizontal gradient is independent of z. The horizontal momentum equations are therefore
DtDH​u​=−ghx​,DtDH​v​=−ghy​,DtDH​​=∂t​+u∂x​+v∂y​.
(26)
Integrating incompressibility from the bottom to the free surface, using zero bottom vertical velocity and the surface kinematic condition w=ht​+uhx​+vhy​, gives
ht​+∂x​(hu)+∂y​(hv)=0.​
(27)
Together these are the nonrotating shallow water equations.
Rotation with angular velocity fz^/2 adds Coriolis force terms −fv and +fu to the left sides of the u and v equations, respectively. The centrifugal potential is absorbed into the background pressure and effective gravity. Thus
DH​u−fv=−ghx​,DH​v+fu=−ghy​.
(28)
Write ζ=vx​−uy​ and d=ux​+vy​. Taking the horizontal curl, including the derivatives of the advecting velocity, yields
DH​ζ=−(ζ+f)d.
(29)
For example the curl of the Coriolis force contribution is fd, and the curl of nonlinear advection supplies ζd. Since qa​=f+ζ and DH​h=−hd, division gives
DtDH​​(hqa​​)=h−qa​d​+h2qa​hd​=0.​
(30)
This is exact shallow-water potential vorticity conservation within the rotating shallow water equations.
For the quasi-geostrophic approximation, take a horizontal scale L, speed U, mean depth H, and slow time L/U. Require small Rossby number Ro=U/(∣f∣L)≪1 and small surface departure η=h−H. Introduce the Rossby deformation radius LR​=gH​/∣f∣ and Burger number Bu=LR2​/L2=O(1). Geostrophic balance gives gη/L∼∣f∣U, hence η/H∼Ro/Bu≪1. The Froude number is U/gH​=Ro/Bu​≪1. These assumptions select slow balanced motion, with fast gravity-wave transients absent at leading order.
Define the quasi-geostrophic streamfunction by ψ=gη/f. The leading geostrophic flow is (ug​,vg​)=(−ψy​,ψx​), which is horizontally nondivergent. Expanding the exact shallow-water potential vorticity gives
H+ηf+ζ​=Hf​+H1​(∇H2​ψ−LR2​ψ​)+higher-order terms.
(31)
The correction is smaller than f/H by O(Ro). Advecting it by the leading geostrophic flow, while the constant leading term has zero gradient, gives
Q=f+∇H2​ψ−LR−2​ψ,Qt​+Jxy​(ψ,Q)=0,Jxy​(A,B)=Ax​By​−Ay​Bx​.​
(32)
Here potential-vorticity conservation means that Q is carried unchanged by the leading horizontal trajectories. Potential-vorticity inversion means solving the elliptic equation (∇H2​−LR−2​)ψ=Q−f, with appropriate boundary or decay conditions, to recover ψ and thus the velocity. Conservation evolves the scalar field; inversion obtains its velocity field.
For a mean current U(y)=−ψˉ​y​, substitute Q=Qˉ​+Q′ and ψ=ψˉ​+ψ′ into potential-vorticity conservation. Retaining first-order terms gives
Qt′​+UQx′​+Qˉ​y​ψx′​=0.​
(33)
Let ΔQ=Qˉ​(0+)−Qˉ​(0−)>0. Differentiating the mean inversion relation gives Qˉ​y​=−U′′+LR−2​U. Away from y=0 this is zero, so decay at both infinities gives U=U0​e−∣y∣/LR​. A velocity jump would introduce a derivative of a delta function, absent from Qˉ​y​, so U is continuous. Integrating through zero now gives −[U′]=ΔQ, and therefore
U0​=2LR​ΔQ​,U(y)=2LR​ΔQ​e−∣y∣/LR​.​
(34)
For the regular interfacial normal mode ψ′=ϕ(y)eik(x−ct), the linear equation reads
(U−c)(ϕ′′−(k2+LR−2​)ϕ)+ΔQδ(y)ϕ=0.
(35)
In each region, choose zero perturbation potential vorticity, giving ϕ′′−(k2+LR−2​)ϕ=0. This supplies a regular solution even if U=c at isolated locations away from the interface. Continuity and decay require ϕ=Ae−γ∣y∣, where γ=k2+LR−2​​. Integrating the equation across zero gives
(U0​−c)[ϕ′]+ΔQϕ(0)=0.
(36)
Since [ϕ′]=−2γA, the nonzero normal mode has
c=U0​−2γΔQ​=U0​(1−k2+LR−2​LR−2​​​),ϕ=Ae−γ∣y∣.​
(37)
This potential-vorticity step edge wave is an interfacial Rossby wave. Displacing the potential vorticity jump creates alternating anomalies; their inverted velocity displaces the interface further with an intrinsic propagation opposite to the positive mean current. The mechanism requires a nonzero jump, or more generally a gradient, of the mean potential vorticity. It does not require planetary variation of f. Reversing the jump reverses the intrinsic propagation; setting the jump to zero removes this interfacial restoring mechanism.

Define the quasi-geostrophic streamfunction by p′/ρ0​=f0​ψ. Leading horizontal geostrophic balance, f0​z^×ug​=−∇H​P, gives (ug​,vg​)=(−ψy​,ψx​). The hydrostatic approximation gives Pz​=σ, hence
σ=f0​ψz​,Dg​=∂t​−ψy​∂x​+ψx​∂y​.​
(38)
Thus Dg​ is the material derivative along the leading geostrophic flow, and the vertical derivative of pressure determines the buoyancy perturbation. This also gives thermal wind: ugz​=−σy​/f0​ and vgz​=σx​/f0​.
The balanced regime is favored by small Rossby number, a shallow aspect ratio, and a Burger number N2H2/(f02​L2) of order one. Equivalently the stratified Froude number U/(NH)=Ro/Bu​ is small. On a beta plane, require βL/∣f0​∣=O(Ro), and take slow, initially balanced motion away from the equator. Under the traditional approximation, the horizontal Coriolis force generated by vertical velocity is small because vertical velocity is small, while the vertical rotational force is small compared with the leading hydrostatic pressure and gravity terms. Strong stratification and small aspect ratio justify retaining only the vertical component of Earth's rotation in this regime; this argument is not uniform near the equator.
Put A(z)=f02​/N2(z). The buoyancy equation gives w=−f0​Dg​ψz​/N2. Substitution into the vertical vorticity equation yields
Dg​∇H2​ψ+βψx​=−∂z​(ADg​ψz​).
(39)
There is an important commutator cancellation. With Jxy​(a,b)=ax​by​−ay​bx​,
∂z​(ADg​ψz​)=Dg​∂z​(Aψz​)+AJxy​(ψz​,ψz​)=Dg​∂z​(Aψz​).
(40)
Also Dg​f=βψx​ for f=f0​+βy. Adding terms therefore proves potential-vorticity conservation:
Dg​Q=0,Q=f+∇H2​ψ+∂z​(N2f02​​ψz​).​
(41)
This is the three-dimensional quasi-geostrophic potential vorticity.
At an impermeable stationary lower boundary z=b(x,y), the kinematic condition is w=uH​⋅∇H​b=Dg​b. For small elevation and slope, evaluate this at the reference level z=0 to leading order. Combining it with the buoyancy equation gives the rigid-boundary buoyancy condition for quasi-geostrophic waves
Dg​(N2f0​​ψz​+b)=0at z=0.​
(42)
The quantity in parentheses is the boundary displacement plus its buoyancy-equivalent displacement.
Now take constant N,f and the mean quasi-geostrophic streamfunction ψˉ​=−(U0​+Λz)y. Its mean buoyancy perturbation is −fΛy, consistent with thermal wind, and its interior potential vorticity is constant. In the boundary condition, the perturbation velocity v′=ψx′​ advects the mean boundary buoyancy gradient. Keeping this contribution gives
(∂t​+U0​∂x​)ψz′​−Λψx′​+fN2U0​​bx​=0at z=0.​
(43)
In particular the −Λψx′​ term must not be omitted.
The linear interior potential-vorticity equation is (∂t​+U(z)∂x​)Q′=0. Starting with zero interior perturbation potential vorticity, and with forcing only through the boundary, it remains zero. For a steady solution this is also the regular zero-interior-PV choice, continued through any isolated zero of U. The inversion equation for a horizontal mode is
ψzz′​−K2ψ′=0,K=fNk2+ℓ2​​>0.
(44)
The printed convention K>0 takes f>0; for either hemisphere the decay rate is defined using ∣f∣. Boundedness aloft selects the decaying mode. Write
ψ′=A(t)cos(ℓy)eikxe−Kz
(45)
with real part understood. The linear boundary condition becomes
A′+ik(U0​+KΛ​)A=KfikϵN2U0​​.​
(46)
For KU0​+Λ=0, a steady quasi-geostrophic mountain wave is therefore
ψs′​=f(KU0​+Λ)ϵN2U0​​cos(ℓy)e−Kzeikx.​
(47)
On y=0, its real meridional velocity is
vs′​=−f(KU0​+Λ)kϵN2U0​​e−Kzsin(kx),b(x,0)=ϵcos(kx).
(48)
For the positive-parameter convention k,ϵ,f,U0​>0 and KU0​+Λ>0, the velocity minimum lies one quarter wavelength downstream of each topographic maximum; its maximum lies one quarter wavelength downstream of each topographic minimum. If the velocity prefactor changes sign, maxima and minima interchange. The condition KU0​+Λ>0 alone does not fix that prefactor if U0​ is allowed to be negative. The upper panel shows the positive-current convention.
At KU0​+Λ=0, the free boundary Eady edge wave has zero frequency in the ground frame. If kϵU0​=0, a steady amplitude cannot satisfy the forced amplitude equation. With A(0)=0, integration gives
A(t)=KfikϵN2U0​​t,ψ′(t)=KfikϵN2U0​​tcos(ℓy)e−Kzeikx.​
(49)
Consequently
ψt′​=KfikϵN2U0​​cos(ℓy)e−Kzeikx.​
(50)
The symbol multiplying i here is the horizontal wavenumber k, as in the PDF. Taking the real part and differentiating in x gives
ψreal′​=−KfkϵN2U0​t​cos(ℓy)e−Kzsin(kx),v′=−Kfk2ϵN2U0​t​cos(ℓy)e−Kzcos(kx).
(51)
For U0​=−Λ/K>0, positive ϵ,f, and t>0, the meridional-velocity minima coincide with topographic maxima, and its maxima with topographic minima. This is the lower panel. The change from quadrature to opposition is the phase of a resonantly forced topographic edge wave.
The stationary relief continuously excites a boundary Rossby wave whose intrinsic propagation exactly cancels mean advection. The forcing therefore remains coherent with that wave; with no damping or radiation loss for this trapped mode, its linear amplitude grows secularly. This is a resonant topographic quasi-geostrophic wave. Unlimited growth is a conclusion of the linear idealization: eventually the small-disturbance approximation fails.
The assertion that resonance must be unsteady needs nonzero forcing. If k=0, then bx​=0 and the boundary current does not cross the relief; if U0​=0, the forcing also vanishes. In either case the zero disturbance is a steady solution even if KU0​+Λ=0. For example, choose k=0, ℓ=0, and Λ=−KU0​; this is a concrete exception to an unconditional resonance claim. When both horizontal wavenumbers vanish, K=0 and the stated decaying-wave ansatz itself does not apply.
