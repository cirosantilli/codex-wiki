The sphere {p}×S2 in S1×S2 is nonseparating: its complement is (S1∖{p})×S2, which is connected. A sphere bounding a ball separates, so this product is not an irreducible three-manifold.
To show it is prime, suppose a separating sphere cuts it into compact pieces V,W with one spherical boundary each. The Seifert-van Kampen theorem gives
Z≅π1​(S1×S2)≅π1​(V)∗π1​(W).
(13)
A free product of two nontrivial groups is nonabelian: choose nonidentity elements in the two factors; their products in the two orders are distinct reduced words. Hence one factor, say π1​(V), is trivial. The inclusion of this simply connected compact piece lifts to the universal cover R×S2≅R3∖{0}. The lift is an embedding: equality of lifted points would imply equality of their projections in V. Its image V is compact with boundary the lifted sphere.
Use the three-dimensional Schoenflies theorem: a locally flat embedded sphere in R3 bounds a ball, and its compact side is that ball. The interior of V is both open and closed in the corresponding complement component, and compactness rules out the unbounded component. Thus V is the bounded ball, and V itself is a ball. Capping V gives S3, so every connected-sum decomposition is trivial. Therefore S1×S2 is prime but not irreducible.
Now let a prime closed connected orientable M fail irreducibility. A sphere not bounding a ball cannot separate: if it did, primeness would make one capped side S3, and the three-dimensional Schoenflies theorem would make its uncapped side a ball. Thus there is a nonseparating sphere S. Its two-sided neighborhood is S2×[−1,1]. Since the complement is connected, join its two boundary spheres by an embedded arc in that complement and thicken the arc. The union is a punctured S2×S1: the thickened arc joins the two ends of the sphere-cylinder, and the remaining boundary is one sphere. This is the nonseparating sphere gives a sphere-circle summand construction and yields
M≅(S1×S2)#N.
(14)
The product is not S3, since its fundamental group is Z. Primeness forces N≅S3, so
M≅S1×S2.​
(15)
The background results used are van Kampen, the explicit universal cover of the product, and the locally flat three-dimensional Schoenflies theorem; no Poincaré-conjecture argument is needed.
