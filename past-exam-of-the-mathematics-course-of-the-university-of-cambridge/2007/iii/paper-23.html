Use the quotient convention for projective space. To a scheme T, associate the set of pairs
(q,L),q:OT⊕(n+1)​↠L,
(1)
where L is a line bundle, modulo isomorphisms L→L′ carrying q to q′. Pullback defines the contravariant functor. On T=SpecA, this means rank-one projective module quotients of An+1; the quotient need not be free. Thus merely listing generating tuples modulo multiplication by a unit would omit points over rings with nontrivial Picard group.
This functor is represented by PZn​. For each i, the locus where q(ei​) generates L is open. These loci cover T, since a surjection onto a rank-one free stalk must have some coefficient a unit in its local ring. On this locus, trivialize by q(ei​) and write
tj​=q(ei​)q(ej​)​,j=i.
(2)
These functions specify a map to the standard chart Ui​=SpecZ[tj​:j=i]. Their transition rules are exactly those of projective space, so the chart maps glue. Conversely the universal quotient on projective space pulls back to the given pair, and the construction is natural and unique. This proves that projective space represents invertible quotients.
Write its universal exact sequence as
0⟶K⟶V⊗Oq​O(1)⟶0,V=Zn+1.
(3)
A first-order change of the quotient, with target trivialized locally, is a map δq:V⊗O→O(1). Changing the target trivialization by 1+εh changes δq by hq. Restriction to K removes this ambiguity, and every local map K→O(1) extends because the displayed sequence splits locally. Consequently the tangent sheaf of a scheme is
TPn/Z​≅Hom(K,O(1)).
(4)
This identification can also be seen on Ui​: the first-order change of tj​ is δq(ej​)−tj​δq(ei​) after setting q(ei​)=1. Thus these maps are precisely the coordinate tangent directions.
Apply Hom(−,O(1)) to the universal sequence. Since its terms are locally free and it splits locally, this gives the Euler sequence
0⟶O(X0​,…,Xn​)​O(1)⊕(n+1)⟶TPn/Z​⟶0.​
(5)
It remains exact after any base change. Taking its dual of a sheaf over a field k gives
0⟶ΩPkn​/k1​⟶O(−1)⊕(n+1)⟶O⟶0.
(6)
For n=1, the first term has rank one; taking determinants yields
ΩPk1​/k1​≅O(−2).​
(7)
Explicitly, for u=t−1 on the overlap of the two affine charts, du=−t−2dt, the same transition as O(−2) after changing one local frame's sign.

Cover the projective line by U0​=Speck[t] and U1​=Speck[t−1]. On their overlap the coordinate is invertible. Choose frames e0​,e1​ of the twisting sheaf on projective space O(n) with e1​=tne0​. The vanishing of quasi-coherent cohomology on an affine scheme applies to both charts and their intersection, so the Čech cochain complex
0⟶k[t]⊕k[t−1]δ​k[t,t−1]⟶0,δ(a,b)=tnb−a,
(8)
computes the sheaf cohomology. Its kernel identifies with k[t]∩tnk[t−1], and its cokernel is
k[t]+tnk[t−1]k[t,t−1]​.
(9)
For n≥0, the intersection has basis 1,t,…,tn; for n<0 it is zero. In the quotient, powers tj with j≥0 or j≤n disappear. The surviving basis for n≤−2 is tn+1,…,t−1; for n≥−1 none survives. Hence
h0(O(n))=max(n+1,0),h1(O(n))=max(−n−1,0),Hi(O(n))=0 (i≥2).​
(10)
This is the Čech cohomology of twists on the projective line.
By the preceding question, ω=ΩP1/k1​=O(−2). In the dt frame its degree-one Čech cohomology is one-dimensional, represented by t−1dt. Define
tr:H1(ω)⟶k,[h(t)dt]⟼[t−1]h(t).
(11)
This is well defined: differentials regular on U0​ have nonnegative powers, and those regular on U1​ have powers at most −2 when expressed using dt. Evaluation and the cup product give natural pairings
Hi(E)×H1−i(E∨⊗ω)⟶H1(ω)tr​k,i=0,1.
(12)
For E=O(n) with n≥0, the basis element ta in H0(E) pairs with t−a−1dt in H1(E∨⊗ω) and with no other element of this dual Laurent basis. The pairing matrix is a permutation matrix, hence invertible. For n≤−2 the same computation exchanges degrees zero and one. For n=−1 both sides vanish. Thus the pairings are perfect for every line bundle on P1.
Here we used the classification of line bundles on the projective line: they are O(a) for integers a. One direct justification is that every line bundle has a nonzero rational section and hence is associated with a divisor on an algebraic curve. The finite closed point defined by an irreducible polynomial p(t) is linearly equivalent to (degp)∞, using the principal divisor of p(t). Thus every divisor is equivalent to its degree times ∞, proving the classification over arbitrary k.
To extend the pairing to every locally free coherent sheaf E, induct on rank, without assuming the splitting theorem of the next question. A one-dimensional subspace of the generic fibre, saturated in E, gives an exact sequence
0⟶L⟶E⟶Q⟶0
(13)
with L a line bundle and Q a lower-rank vector bundle. This saturated line subbundle on a smooth curve exists because its stalk and its torsion-free quotient are free over the discrete valuation rings of the curve. The dual sequence, tensored with ω, is exact as well, since the original sequence splits locally.
The two long exact sequences in sheaf cohomology, after dualizing one, are compatible with the evaluation pairings. In Čech cohomology, this compatibility follows by lifting a section locally: the differences of its lifts represent its connecting class, and contracting those differences with a dual cocycle gives the transpose connecting map, with the usual sign. Thus the connecting maps are adjoint; changing one vertical map's sign gives a commuting diagram. Its rows are
00​→H0(L)→H0(E)→H0(Q)→H1(L)→H1(E)→H1(Q)→0,→H1(L∨ω)∗→H1(E∨ω)∗→H1(Q∨ω)∗→H0(L∨ω)∗→H0(E∨ω)∗→H0(Q∨ω)∗→0.​
(14)
The maps for L and Q are isomorphisms by the line-bundle calculation and induction. Exactness then makes the maps for E isomorphisms, by the Five lemma (or by comparing the relevant kernels and cokernels). All groups are finite-dimensional because the curve is projective and the sheaves coherent. Therefore residue duality on the projective line proves
Hi(P1,E)≅H1−i(P1,E∨(−2))∗,i=0,1.​
(15)
These isomorphisms are natural in E. Higher sheaf cohomology vanishes by the same two-chart complex.

We prove the Birkhoff–Grothendieck theorem by induction on the rank of the vector bundle E. Rank zero is immediate; rank one is the classification Pic(Pk1​)≅Z established above.
First there is a largest integer a with H0(E(−a))=0. To see existence and boundedness directly, trivialize E on the two standard affine charts: the associated finite projective modules over the principal ideal domains k[t] and k[t−1] are free. Let A(t)∈GLr​(k[t,t−1]) be its transition matrix, so a section of E(−a) is a pair satisfying
p(t)=t−aA(t)q(t−1),p∈k[t]r,q∈k[t−1]r.
(16)
If a exceeds the largest exponent in any entry of A, the right side has only strictly negative powers; the left side has nonnegative powers. Hence both vanish. For a sufficiently negative, take a nonzero constant vector p and put q=taA(t)−1p; all its exponents are nonpositive, so it supplies a nonzero section. Thus a largest a exists.
Such a section gives a nonzero map O(a)→E. It is injective, since it is nonzero generically and the source is torsion-free. Saturate its image. The resulting line subbundle is O(a+d) for the degree d≥0 of the effective divisor of zeros of the original map. If d>0, its inclusion in E would give a nonzero section of E(−a−d), contradicting maximality. Therefore d=0 and
0⟶O(a)⟶E⟶Q⟶0
(17)
has a locally free quotient. By induction, Q≅⨁j​O(bj​).
Twist this exact sequence by O(−a−1). The outer groups H0(O(−1)) and H1(O(−1)) are zero by the Čech cohomology of twists on the projective line. The long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology therefore gives
H0(E(−a−1))≅H0(Q(−a−1)).
(18)
The first is zero by the choice of a, so each H0(O(bj​−a−1)) vanishes. Thus bj​≤a for every j. The extension class lies in
Ext1(Q,O(a))≅⨁j​H1(O(a−bj​))=0,
(19)
since a−bj​≥0. The identification of this Ext functor with cohomology can be seen without additional duality: local splittings of a vector-bundle extension differ by a Čech cocycle in Hom(Q,O(a)), and a coboundary changes them to compatible splittings. Vanishing therefore gives a global splitting. Consequently
E≅O(a)⊕j⨁​O(bj​),​
(20)
completing the induction over any field k.
The degrees can be arranged in nonincreasing order and their multiset is unique. Indeed, for any such decomposition,
h0(E(m))−h0(E(m−1))=#{i:ai​≥−m},
(21)
so the dimensions of the twisted global sections recover the number of summands of every degree.

Write ΩC1​=ΩC/k1​. A dualizing sheaf on a smooth projective curve, for the vector bundles in this question, consists of a line bundle DC​ and a trace H1(C,DC​)→k making evaluation induce natural perfect pairings
Hi(C,E)×H1−i(C,E∨⊗DC​)⟶k,i=0,1.
(22)
In particular it represents the functor E↦H1(C,E)∗ through HomC​(E,DC​). We construct it by reducing to the proved residue duality on the projective line.
Choose a separating nonconstant rational function t on C. Such a choice exists over any field k: the function field of a smooth algebraic curve is separably generated of transcendence degree one, and a choice with dt=0 makes its finite extension of k(t) separable. The map (t:1) extends across poles using (1:t−1) in the discrete valuation ring at each point. It gives a finite generically separable morphism
f:C⟶Pk1​.
(23)
Indeed it is nonconstant, hence has finite fibres, and is proper; a proper quasi-finite morphism is finite. This finite map is flat: locally its direct-image module is finite and torsion-free over a discrete valuation ring, and hence free. The same argument works componentwise if needed.
For an affine chart SpecB⊂P1 with inverse image SpecA, form the A-module
W=HomB​(A,B),(aλ)(a′)=λ(aa′).
(24)
These modules glue to the relative trace-dual sheaf Wf​ on C. There are two algebraic identities used here. First, finite-map adjunction gives
HomA​(M,HomB​(A,N))≅HomB​(M,N).
(25)
The forward map evaluates at 1; the inverse sends g to m↦(a↦g(am)). Second, the different–differential identity for a finite generically separable map between smooth curves gives
Wf​⊗f∗ΩP1/k1​≅ΩC/k1​.
(26)
To explain its algebraic content, the field trace identifies HomB​(A,B) with the inverse different ideal, while the different is the vanishing ideal of f∗ΩP1/k1​→ΩC/k1​. These identities include wild ramification; one must not replace the different by just ramification index minus one. This is the identity allowed to be quoted in the question; the finite-map description is recorded in Stacks Project, finite morphisms, and its differential form in Stacks Project, Section 53.12.
Set DC​=Wf​⊗f∗ΩP1/k1​. The second identity shows it is a line bundle and already identifies it with ΩC1​. For a vector bundle E, the first identity gives the sheaf isomorphism
f∗​(E∨⊗DC​)≅HomP1​(f∗​E,ΩP1/k1​)=(f∗​E)∨⊗ΩP1/k1​.
(27)
The sheaf f∗​E is locally free: on an affine chart, the projective A-module defining E is a summand of Ar, and A is finite free over B. Since f is finite, affine-cover Čech cohomology identifies Hj(C,F) with Hj(P1,f∗​F) for each of these sheaves. Apply the already established duality on P1 to f∗​E. It gives
Hi(C,E)≅H1−i(C,E∨⊗DC​)∗,DC​≅ΩC/k1​,i=0,1.​
(28)
The trace is obtained by evaluating the trace-dual module at 1 and then using the trace H1(P1,ΩP1/k1​)→k. The adjunction formulas show the displayed isomorphisms are induced by evaluation against this trace and are natural in E. They thus establish the requested dualizing property, not merely the existence of an invertible differential sheaf. Uniqueness follows from the Yoneda lemma applied to the representing functor on vector bundles.

Use the finite map f:C→P1 from the preceding solution. The inverse images of the two standard affine charts and of their intersection are affine, because f is finite. The vanishing of quasi-coherent cohomology on an affine scheme therefore lets their two-term Čech cochain complex compute Hi(C,E). It has terms only in degrees zero and one, proving
Hi(C,E)=0(i≥2).​
(29)
This actually applies to every quasi-coherent sheaf on C.
For a smooth projective curve with H0(C,OC​)=k, define its genus of a smooth projective curve by
g=dimk​H1(C,OC​).​
(30)
The preceding Serre duality also gives g=dimk​H0(C,ΩC1​). The condition on constants holds in particular for a geometrically connected smooth projective curve. It makes g equal to the arithmetic genus 1−χ(OC​). We state the theorem with this usual convention and then give the formula valid without that condition.
For a divisor on an algebraic curve D and a canonical divisor K with O(K)≅ΩC1​, the Riemann-Roch theorem is
ℓ(D)−ℓ(K−D)=degk​D+1−g,ℓ(D)=h0(C,O(D)).​
(31)
The degree over an arbitrary field is weighted by residue degrees: degk​D=∑p​np​[k(p):k] when D=∑p​np​p. Equivalently, for a line bundle L,
h0(L)−h0(ΩC1​⊗L−1)=degk​L+1−g.
(32)
We now prove it via Riemann-Roch via elementary modifications.
For any closed point p and any line bundle L, the exact sequence
0⟶L(−p)⟶L⟶L∣p​⟶0
(33)
has a skyscraper sheaf as its quotient, with k-dimension [k(p):k]. This follows locally from a uniformizer of the discrete valuation ring at p: a local line-bundle generator modulo that uniformizer spans one copy of k(p). The quotient has zero higher cohomology, as it is the direct image from a finite affine scheme. The long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology and the proved higher vanishing give
χ(L)−χ(L(−p))=[k(p):k],χ(L)=h0(L)−h1(L).
(34)
Every line bundle has a nonzero rational section of a line bundle, whose local orders give a divisor on an algebraic curve D with L≅O(D). Iterating the displayed identity, adding or subtracting closed points with their multiplicities, gives
χ(O(D))=degk​D+χ(OC​).
(35)
For the constants convention above, χ(OC​)=1−g. The dualizing sheaf on a smooth projective curve gives h1(O(D))=h0(O(K−D)), which converts this Euler-characteristic identity into the stated Riemann-Roch theorem. This proves the formula over arbitrary k, without assuming the closed points are k-rational.
For vector bundles, the corresponding formula is
χ(E)=degk​(detE)+rank(E)(1−g).​
(36)
To see it, saturate a rational rank-one subspace to a line subbundle and induct on rank. Both Euler characteristic of a coherent sheaf and the degree of the determinant are additive in the resulting short exact sequence. The line-bundle formula just proved starts the induction.
Taking L=ΩC1​ and using duality gives h0(ΩC1​)=g, h1(ΩC1​)=1, and hence degk​ΩC1​=2g−2. In particular a line bundle of degree greater than 2g−2 has zero first cohomology: its dual twisted by ΩC1​ has negative degree, and a negative-degree line bundle has no nonzero section, since any section would have an effective divisor of zeros.
If the term “curve over k” is used without requiring H0(OC​)=k, the unconditional formula we proved is
χ(L)=degk​L+χ(OC​).​
(37)
Writing g=h1(OC​) then replaces 1−g by h0(OC​)−g. For example, Pk′1​ viewed as a k-curve for a finite separable extension k′/k has h0(OC​)=[k′:k], so a literal constant term 1 would be incorrect. Alternatively the arithmetic genus pa​=1−χ(OC​) gives the formula with constant 1−pa​. The constants hypothesis for the usual genus is recorded in Stacks Project, genus of a curve.

Here k is algebraically closed and C is a connected smooth projective curve. For a scheme T over k, let π:CT​=C×k​T→T. The degree-d Picard functor of a curve assigns
PicCd​(T)={L∈Pic(CT​):deg(L∣Ctˉ​​)=d for every geometric point tˉ}/π∗Pic(T).​
(38)
One can equivalently take the associated faithfully flat sheaf. It is essential to divide out by line bundles from the base: a parameter space should remember the family on C, not an independent base line bundle.
Choose p0​∈C(k). A class can be normalized as L⊗π∗(L∣p0​×T​)−1, with its canonical trivialization along p0​×T. Since π∗​OCT​​=OT​, an automorphism of a line bundle is multiplication by a base unit, and preserving this normalization forces that unit to be 1. Descent of line bundles is effective, so normalized bundles satisfy the faithfully flat sheaf condition with no automorphism ambiguity. Thus this normalized description works on families, including nonreduced bases, rather than only on k-points.
We construct a representing scheme by Picard charts from nonspecial divisors. Fix N≥2g and put r=N−g. The symmetric product of a curve C(g) represents relative effective Cartier divisors on a curve of degree g and has a universal divisor Δ. This uses the scheme symmetric product, not the naive set of unordered T-points. Locally on a smooth curve, a divisor of length m is described by a monic polynomial in a smooth coordinate; its coefficients are elementary symmetric functions, so this construction includes coincident points in any characteristic. These local descriptions give the usual effective-divisor interpretation of the symmetric product.
Let W⊂C(g) be the open set where H1(C,O(E))=0. It is open by semicontinuity theorem for coherent cohomology. The Riemann-Roch theorem gives h0(O(E))=1 there, so E is the unique effective divisor in its line-bundle class. For every fixed effective divisor B of degree r, take a copy WB​ of W, with family
LB​=O(B+Δ)
(39)
of degree N. Normalize it along p0​ as above.
This chart represents the open subfunctor defined by
H1(C,Lt​(−B))=0.
(40)
Indeed, for a family satisfying that condition, cohomology and base change for line bundles on a curve makes V=π∗​L(−B) a line bundle on T, with formation commuting with base change. The rank is one by Riemann-Roch theorem. The evaluation map
π∗V⟶L(−B)
(41)
is a nonzero section on each fibre, up to the base twist. Its zero scheme is a relative effective Cartier divisor on a curve E of degree g. To check the family assertion, source and target are flat over T and the fibre maps are injections of line bundles on an integral curve, so their cokernel is flat over T. Their zeros are Cartier, and the zero scheme is proper with finite fibres of length g, hence finite flat of degree g. It therefore defines a unique map T→WB​. Conversely this construction recovers the family O(B+E) modulo a base line bundle.
The base-change step controls infinitesimal families as well. Locally, a finite free complex computes the two cohomology groups. Vanishing of its degree-one cokernel on fibres makes the last differential surjective by the Nakayama lemma. Splitting that differential leaves a locally free degree-zero kernel of rank one and commutes with base change. This is the standard finite-complex form of the permitted cohomology semicontinuity and base-change results.
These chart subfunctors cover all degree-N families. For a geometric-fibre line bundle L of degree N, the preceding solution gives H1(L)=0 and h0(L)=N+1−g=r+1. Choose r points successively so that each imposes one independent condition on the current section space. At each stage that space is nonzero; a nonzero section has finitely many zeros, so a point outside its zero set can be chosen, also outside the finitely many previously chosen points. For their sum B,
h0(L(−B))=1,degL(−B)=g,
(42)
and Riemann-Roch theorem forces h1(L(−B))=0. The points may be chosen in C(k) even when the fibre field extends k, since C(k) is infinite and a nonzero section on the base-changed curve has only finitely many zeros. Thus fixed divisors B defined over k suffice. Upper semicontinuity gives an open neighbourhood on the base for each successful B.
It remains to glue the charts explicitly. On WB​, the locus
WBB′​={E:H1(C,O(B+E−B′))=0}
(43)
is open. The line bundle inside the braces has degree g; its unique section up to scale has an effective zero divisor E′. Base change and the evaluation construction make E↦E′ a morphism WBB′​→WB′​. It satisfies O(B+E)≅O(B′+E′). Reversing B,B′ gives its inverse. On triple overlaps the transition maps compose correctly because the residual effective divisor is unique. Hence the copies WB​ glue along these open isomorphisms to a scheme PN.
The normalized bundles LB​ glue as well: on overlaps their normalized isomorphisms exist and are unique, so they satisfy the cocycle condition. Let PN​ denote the resulting universal normalized line bundle on C×PN. For any T, the open cover where the conditions for some B hold gives compatible maps T→WB​, and thus a unique map T→PN. Pulling back PN​ reverses this procedure. We have established a natural bijection
Homk​(T,PN)≅PicCN​(T).​
(44)
This proves representability of the full functor, not just a parametrization of individual line bundles. The nonspecial-divisor construction is also recorded in Stacks Project, Picard scheme of a curve.
Finally tensoring by OC​((N−d)p0​) is a natural isomorphism PicCd​→PicCN​ for every integer d. Transporting the universal bundle back constructs its degree-d representative Pd, usually denoted PicC/kd​. Thus every degree component is represented. These identifications depend on the chosen point and twist; intrinsically the degree-zero component is a group scheme and each degree component is its torsor.
