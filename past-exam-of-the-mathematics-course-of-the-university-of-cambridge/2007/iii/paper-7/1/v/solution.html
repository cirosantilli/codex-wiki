Define the lower central series by Γ1​(G)=G and Γj+1​(G)=[Γj​(G),G]. Its terms are characteristic normal subgroups and form a descending chain. Define the upper central series by Z0​(G)=1 and
Zi+1​(G)/Zi​(G)=Z(G/Zi​(G)).
(9)
Equivalently, Zi+1​(G)={x∈G:[x,G]⊆Zi​(G)}. These are characteristic normal subgroups in an ascending chain, and successive factors are central in the appropriate quotient.
For any length-r central series 1=G0​≤⋯≤Gr​=G, prove the two comparisons independently. Starting at Γ1​(G)=Gr​, if Γr−i+1​(G)≤Gi​, then
Γr−i+2​(G)=[Γr−i+1​(G),G]≤[Gi​,G]≤Gi−1​.
(10)
Descending induction gives the lower containment. Starting at G0​=Z0​(G)=1, if Gi−1​≤Zi−1​(G), then [Gi​,G]≤Gi−1​≤Zi−1​(G), which by definition gives Gi​≤Zi​(G). Thus the central-series comparison theorem yields
Γr−i+1​(G)≤Gi​≤Zi​(G)(0≤i≤r).​
(11)
In particular, a nilpotent group satisfies Γr+1​(G)=1 and Zr​(G)=G.
Conversely, if Γn​(G)=1, reverse the finite lower chain to obtain 1=Γn​≤Γn−1​≤⋯≤Γ1​=G. The defining commutator recurrence makes it a central series. If Zn​(G)=G, the finite upper chain is already a central series. This proves
G nilpotent ⟺ Γn​(G)=1 for some n ⟺ Zn​(G)=G for some n.​
(12)
Finally, for an integer c>0, termination at Γc+1​=1 gives a central series of length c and hence Zc​=G by the comparison. Conversely, Zc​=G gives a central series of length c and hence Γc+1​=1. Therefore
Γc+1​(G)=1⟺Zc​(G)=G.​
(13)
Repeated terms cause no problem; the least such c for a nontrivial group is its nilpotency class.
