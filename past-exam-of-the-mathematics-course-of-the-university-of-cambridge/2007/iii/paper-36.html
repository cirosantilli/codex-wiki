Take a complete, right-continuous filtration (Ft​), an increasing family of sigma-algebras. Adapted means that Xt​ is Ft​-measurable at each t; càdlàg means right-continuous with left limits. A local martingale is an adapted càdlàg process X, with integrable initial value, for which there are stopping times τn​↑∞ almost surely such that each stopped process Xtτn​​=Xt∧τn​​ is a true martingale. Such a sequence is a localizing sequence. A true martingale is integrable at each time and satisfies E(Xt​∣Fs​)=Xs​ for s≤t.
The necessary and sufficient condition is that X is a Class DL process:
X is a martingale ⟺ {Xτ​:τ≤T a stopping time} is uniformly integrable for every finite T.​
(1)
A family Z is uniformly integrable if its members are integrable and supZ∈Z​E(∣Z∣1{∣Z∣>K}​)→0 as K→∞. A stopping time τ satisfies {τ≤t}∈Ft​ for all t.
For necessity, on [0,T] the optional stopping theorem gives Xτ​=E(XT​∣Fτ​); conditional expectations of one integrable variable form a uniformly integrable family. For sufficiency, Xt∧τn​​→Xt​ almost surely, and Class DL upgrades this convergence to L1 on each finite horizon. Pass to the limit in E(Xt∧τn​​∣Fs​)=Xs∧τn​​ to obtain the martingale identity. Requiring uniform integrability of all times on the entire half-line would be stronger than necessary; Brownian motion is already a counterexample to that stronger requirement.

The Lévy characterization of multidimensional Brownian motion says that a continuous adapted Rd-valued process Z, with Z0​=0, is standard Brownian motion relative to the given filtration if and only if its coordinates are local martingales and
[Zi,Zj]t​=δij​t(1≤i,j≤d).​
(2)
For Brownian motion, independent centered Gaussian increments give the martingale property of each coordinate and of Zti​Ztj​−δij​t. The defining uniqueness of quadratic covariation consequently gives the bracket condition.
Conversely, suppose the local martingale and bracket conditions hold. For θ∈Rd, the Itô formula gives
Et​=exp(iθ⋅Zt​+21​∣θ∣2t),dEt​=iEt​θ⋅dZt​,
(3)
since the bracket term cancels the time derivative. On [0,T], ∣Et​∣≤e∣θ∣2T/2, so its real and imaginary parts are bounded local martingales and hence true martingales. Thus, for s≤t,
E[eiθ⋅(Zt​−Zs​)∣Fs​]=e−21​∣θ∣2(t−s).
(4)
This is the characteristic function of N(0,(t−s)Id​) and is independent of Fs​. Uniqueness of characteristic functions shows that Zt​−Zs​ has that Gaussian distribution and is independent of the past. Iterating over ordered times gives independent Gaussian increments; together with continuous paths and Z0​=0, these are precisely the defining properties of standard Brownian motion.

For every zero-starting continuous local martingale M, there is a unique, up to indistinguishability, continuous adapted increasing process [M], with [M]0​=0, such that
Mt2​−[M]t​ is a local martingale.​
(14)
This is its quadratic variation. It also satisfies, for deterministic partitions whose mesh tends to zero,
[M]t​=lim∑j​(Mtj+1​∧t​−Mtj​∧t​)2,
(15)
with convergence uniformly on compact time intervals in probability. The theorem includes existence and finiteness on each compact interval; monotonicity is understood pathwise outside one null set.
Uniqueness follows because the difference of two candidate increasing continuous processes is both a finite-variation process and a continuous local martingale, hence is identically zero from its zero initial value. For a continuous semimartingale X=X0​+M+A, its quadratic variation is [X]=[M], since continuous finite-variation terms have zero quadratic variation. Polarization defines the quadratic covariation by [M,N]=([M+N]−[M−N])/4.

For an elementary previsible integrand, the integral is the increment sum ∑j​Hj​(Xt∧tj+1​​−Xt∧tj​​). This expression uses only H and X, so is identical under the two measures, irrespective of how the local martingale and finite-variation parts change.
We may use the stochastic dominated convergence theorem: if bounded predictable Hn​ converge pointwise to H under a common deterministic bound, their integrals against a semimartingale converge uniformly on compact intervals in probability. Also convergence in P-probability implies convergence in Q-probability when Q≪P. To see the latter directly, let D=dQ/dP. For any event E,
Q(E)=EP​(D1E​)≤KP(E)+EP​(D1{D>K}​),
(19)
and let first P(E)→0, then K→∞. Apply this to events involving the supremum on a finite horizon for the uniform-in-probability statement.
Let C be the bounded predictable integrands for which the two integral processes agree up to Q-indistinguishability. It is a vector space containing elementary rectangle indicators generating the predictable sigma-algebra. It is closed under uniformly bounded pointwise limits: stochastic dominated convergence theorem gives convergence of the integrals under each measure, and the preceding transfer makes the P limit also a Q limit. Uniqueness of limits in probability makes the two limiting integrals equal. The functional monotone-class theorem therefore puts every bounded predictable integrand in C.
Finally, for locally bounded H, stop on intervals where it has a deterministic bound. Such stopping times tend to infinity under P and hence also under Q. Apply the bounded result to the stopped integrands and use the stopping property of the integral. This proves the stochastic integral under an absolutely continuous measure change identity
(H⋅X)P=(H⋅X)QQ-indistinguishably.​
(20)
The decompositions H⋅M+H⋅A and H⋅N+H⋅B thus represent the same process under Q, although their individual components need not agree. With only absolute continuity, equality is asserted outside a Q-null set, not necessarily outside a P-null set for a chosen Q version.

A weak solution of a stochastic differential equation consists of a choice of probability space, filtration, driving Brownian motion and adapted solution with the specified initial law. The driving noise and the space are part of what may be chosen.
A strong solution of a stochastic differential equation is constructed for a prescribed Brownian motion and initial value, as a nonanticipating measurable functional of that data; for deterministic initial value it is adapted to the usual augmentation of the Brownian filtration. No further independent randomness is required.
Uniqueness in law means that any two weak solutions of a stochastic differential equation with the same initial distribution have the same law as solution processes. Pathwise uniqueness means that two solutions on the same filtered space, driven by the same Brownian motion and with the same initial value almost surely, are indistinguishable: P(Xt​=Xt​ for every t)=1. The latter concerns equality of paths on a common space, whereas uniqueness in law concerns equality of probability distributions.

A Markov jump process relative to (Ft​) is an adapted càdlàg process with piecewise-constant paths and finitely many jumps on each bounded time interval, whose conditional future law given Ft​ depends only on its current state. For a time-homogeneous process on a countable state space, write q(x,y)≥0 for its transition rates and q(x)=∑y=x​q(x,y)<∞. In state x, the holding time is exponential of rate q(x) and the next state is y with probability q(x,y)/q(x); a zero rate makes the state absorbing. The generator is Af(x)=∑y=x​q(x,y)(f(y)−f(x)). Nonexplosion is included here so that the process is defined at every finite time.
Here is a finite-jump, bounded-rate form of the Kurtz fluid limit theorem.
