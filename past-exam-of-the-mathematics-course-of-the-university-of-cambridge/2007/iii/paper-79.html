Take the principal complex logarithm and deform the interval upwards, where eixt decays for x>0. The small indentation at zero contributes nothing because rlogr→0; the distant horizontal segment vanishes exponentially. The two vertical edges therefore give the exact representation
g(x)=i∫0∞​(logs+2iπ​)e−xsds−ieix∫0∞​log(1+is)e−xsds.
(6)
This is an endpoint steepest descent contour, with distinct contributions from the singular endpoint and the smooth endpoint. Setting u=xs in the first integral and using the given logarithmic moment gives
i∫0∞​(logs+2iπ​)e−xsds=x−π/2−i(logx+γ)​.
(7)
For the second edge, the Taylor series at s=0 is log(1+is)=∑n≥1​(−1)n+1(is)n/n. Watson's lemma integrates it term by term as an asymptotic expansion, using ∫0∞​sne−xsds=n!/xn+1. Consequently
g(x)∼x−π/2−i(logx+γ)​−eixk=0∑∞​(ix)k+2k!​.​
(8)
Here γ is the Euler--Mascheroni constant. The first smooth-endpoint terms are
g(x)∼x−π/2−i(logx+γ)​+eix(x21​−x3i​−x42​+x56i​+⋯).
(9)
For every fixed N≥1, retaining k=0,…,N−1 leaves O(x−N−2). The factorial series is an asymptotic series, not a convergent sum. Treating the logarithmic endpoint by an ordinary smooth-endpoint integration formula would lose the leading logarithm; this is the logarithmic endpoint Fourier asymptotics mechanism.

The linear damped harmonic oscillator has characteristic exponents −ϵ±i1−ϵ2​. Imposing both initial conditions gives, for ∣ϵ∣<1,
x(t)=e−ϵtωsin(ωt)​,ω=1−ϵ2​.​
(10)
For completeness, at ∣ϵ∣=1 its continuous limiting form is te−ϵt; for ∣ϵ∣>1 replace sin(ωt)/ω by sinh(ϵ2−1​t)/ϵ2−1​. The initial derivative is one, as the original PDF specifies; the converted TeX loses this derivative and corrupts the time derivatives in both oscillator equations.
The separate nonlinear oscillator has quadratic drag. Introduce the slow time T=ϵt and use the method of multiple scales,
x=x0​(t,T)+ϵx1​(t,T)+⋯,x0​=A(T)sin(t+ϕ(T)).
(11)
The order-ϵ equation is
(∂t2​+1)x1​=−2∂t​∂T​x0​−∣∂t​x0​∣∂t​x0​.
(12)
For positive amplitude, write ϑ=t+ϕ. Its right side is −2AT​cosϑ+2AϕT​sinϑ−A2∣cosϑ∣cosϑ. The cosine coefficient of the last angular function is
π1​∫02π​∣cosϑ∣cos2ϑdϑ=3π8​,
(13)
while its sine coefficient is zero. The solvability condition in the method of multiple scales removes the resonant harmonics, giving
AT​=−3π4​A2,ϕT​=0.
(14)
The leading initial conditions select A(0)=1, ϕ(0)=0. Therefore the quadratically damped oscillator has
x(t)≃1+4ϵt/(3π)sint​.​
(15)
The same sign follows from the exact energy balance d[(x˙2+x2)/2]/dt=−ϵ∣x˙∣3. Averaging ∣cosϑ∣3 gives 4/(3π), consistent with the amplitude equation.
For ϵ>0, the amplitude decays algebraically and stays at most one. On each fixed interval 0≤ϵt≤T∗​, the omitted displacement correction is O(ϵ), so this approximation is valid through times of order ϵ−1. The effective drag strength ϵA decreases rather than increases, and the envelope predicts A∼3π/(4ϵt) at later times. There is no finite-time pole in the positive-drag approximation. A uniform error assertion for arbitrarily long intervals is stronger than the finite-slow-time expansion just derived; the stated O(ϵ) estimate applies to the specified ϵt=O(1) range.
For ϵ<0, write δ=1−4∣ϵ∣t/(3π). The amplitude grows as 1/δ, with a formal pole at
t∗​=4∣ϵ∣3π​.
(16)
On fixed slow-time intervals strictly before that pole, the displacement error remains O(∣ϵ∣). This estimate is not uniform as δ→0: nonresonant corrections have size comparable to ∣ϵ∣A2, and the required weak-drag condition is ∣ϵ∣A=∣ϵ∣/δ≪1. Thus the approximation necessarily fails when δ=O(∣ϵ∣), corresponding to a distance of order one in fast time from the formal pole, and may require additional error control when approaching it. The plot therefore stops short of the pole. Its position is a prediction of the leading amplitude equation, not an exact blow-up time for the original nonlinear equation; continuing the formula past it is invalid.

At fixed t, expand the exact damped harmonic oscillator solution using e−ϵt=1−ϵt+O(ϵ2), ω=1+O(ϵ2) and 1/ω=1+O(ϵ2). Its Poincaré expansion through first order is
y(ϵ,t)=(1−ϵt)sint,x(t)−y(ϵ,t)=O(ϵ2)(t bounded).​
(17)
The correction −ϵtsint is a secular term. When ϵt is order one it is not small, and the truncated amplitude eventually changes sign and grows in magnitude, instead of reproducing positive damping. This loss of ordering is why a fixed-time regular perturbation is unsuitable on the damping timescale.

Retain the exact damping factor and expand only the slowly accumulating phase. With T=ϵt bounded,
ωt=t−21​ϵ2t+O(ϵ4t),ωsin(ωt)​=sint−21​ϵ2tcost+O(ϵ2).
(18)
The resulting two-time expansion of a weakly damped linear oscillator is
z(ϵ,t)=e−ϵt(sint−2ϵ2t​cost),x−z=O(ϵ2)(ϵt=O(1)).​
(19)
In slow-time notation the second term in parentheses is −ϵTcost/2, an order-ϵ correction. Simply retaining e−ϵtsint would have order-ϵ rather than order-ϵ2 error on this scale, and would not meet the requested accuracy.
When ϵ2t=O(1), the phase shift is order one: Taylor-expanding its sine and cosine is no longer an ordered asymptotic expansion of the oscillatory factor. The exact solution and z acquire visibly different phases after removing their common exponential damping. In contrast, for ϵt=O(1) the main difference between y and z is their amplitude envelope: 1−ϵt versus e−ϵt.
The phrase “not valid” on the later scale needs an error convention. For fixed positive damping, the absolute displacement of both x and z is exponentially small when ϵ2t is a positive order-one number. Indeed Taylor's theorem gives, for small positive ϵ,
∣x−z∣≤Ce−ϵt(ϵ2+ϵ4t+ϵ4t2).
(20)
The supremum of the right side over t≥0 is O(ϵ2). Thus z loses phase accuracy on t=O(ϵ−2), but does not lose an absolute O(ϵ2) error bound there. The last plot displays eϵtx and eϵtz to show that phase failure honestly; multiplying by the damping factor would make the difference extremely small. Relative pointwise error also becomes singular near zeros, so accuracy of the oscillatory factor is the meaningful late-time comparison.

Multiply the equation by x2. Its left side is an exact derivative, yielding the first integral
ϵx2y′+(x2−2ϵ3)y=C.
(21)
For a solution continuous at the singular endpoint, the limiting value is C=−2ϵ3γ. This can also be established from the exact integrating-factor formula below, without assuming uniform bounds on derivatives near zero.
Away from zero, the outer expansion has leading term y∼C/x2. The endpoint value at one requires C∼ϵ3 if an exponentially amplified homogeneous term is to be avoided. It follows already that
γ∼−21​.​
(22)
To exhibit the matching and explain the required boundedness, first set x=ϵX, y=ϵY(X) for a representative without an order-one homogeneous contribution. The leading inner expansion obeys
Y′′+(1+X2​)Y′+X2Y​=0,X2(Y′+Y)=1.
(23)
Integration gives
Y=−X1​+e−X[Ei(X)+B],
(24)
where Ei is the exponential integral. For X→∞, Y∼X−2, matching the outer term ϵ3/x2. For X→0, Y∼−1/X, so the intermediate solution behaves as −ϵ2/x and reaches order one on a second scale x=ϵ2ξ.
On this second scale, the first integral becomes, at leading order, ξ2Y0′​−2Y0​=1. Equivalently its differentiated equation is Y0′′​+(2/ξ−2/ξ2)Y0′​=0, as in the hint. The matching solution that tends to zero as ξ→∞ is
Y0​(ξ)=21​(e−2/ξ−1).​
(25)
It has Y0​∼−1/ξ at large ξ, matching the intermediate region, and Y0​(0)=−1/2. Thus the nested layers at an essential singular endpoint connect an order-ϵ3 outer solution to an order-one endpoint value without an unbounded overshoot.
The issue of uniqueness is clarified by exact integration. Put
μ(x)=ex/ϵ+2ϵ2/x,h(x)=μ(x)−1.
(26)
For every fixed ϵ>0 and every prescribed exact value of γ, the solution satisfying the right endpoint is
y(x)=h(x)[ϵ3μ(1)+2ϵ2γ∫x1​s2μ(s)​ds].
(27)
As x→0, the integral is asymptotic to μ(x)/(2ϵ2), proving y(0)=γ. Since h(1)>0, the two exact endpoint values fix the two constants: for a prescribed exact γ, this finite-ϵ boundary-value problem is unique.
However, specifying only the leading value γ∼−1/2 and uniform order-one size does not select a unique asymptotic family. To see this constructively, choose xm​=2​ϵ3/2 and an arbitrary bounded real constant A. Write
y(x)=h(x)[A+ϵC​∫xm​x​s2μ(s)​ds],I=∫xm​1​s2μ(s)​ds.
(28)
The right endpoint fixes
C=Iϵ[ϵ3μ(1)−A]​,γ=−2ϵ3C​.
(29)
Endpoint Laplace's method gives I=ϵμ(1)[1+2ϵ+O(ϵ2)]. Therefore every bounded choice of A has
γ=−21​+ϵ+O(ϵ2),
(30)
with changes in A affecting γ only by O(ϵ−3e−1/ϵ). These solutions are uniformly order one: near zero their particular part has the finite limit γ, near one it follows the small outer solution, and the freely chosen value at xm​ is Ah(xm​)=O(1). The inner-layer homogeneous contribution is also bounded. Indeed h vanishes at zero, is order one near xm​, and is exponentially small at one.
Consequently the order-one selected value is −1/2, but algebraic asymptotic endpoint data leave an order-one interior freedom. This is beyond-all-orders boundary-value sensitivity. If “constant” were interpreted as fixing γ=−1/2 exactly and independently of ϵ, the omitted algebraic corrections would excite an exponentially large interior homogeneous term; a genuinely uniformly bounded exact family requires the higher-order tuning of γ. Constancy in x does not preclude that parameter dependence.

Use incompressible Euler equations with pressure divided by the constant reference density. Writing L=∂t​+U(y)∂x​, their linearization about the parallel shear flow is
Lu+U′v=−px​,Lv=−py​,Lw=−pz​,ux​+vy​+wz​=0.
(31)
Taking the divergence requires the commutator [∂y​,L]=U′∂x​. Both this commutator and the divergence of U′v contribute, so ∇2p=−2U′vx​. Applying the Laplacian to Lv=−py​ gives
L∇2v+2U′vxy​+U′′vx​=−∂y​∇2p=2U′vxy​+2U′′vx​.
(32)
Hence the wall-normal velocity equation is
L∇2v−U′′vx​=0.​
(33)
Taking ∂z​ of the streamwise momentum equation minus ∂x​ of the spanwise equation cancels pressure and yields the inviscid Squire equation,
Lη=−U′vz​,η=uz​−wx​.​
(34)
This is the vorticity component in the original PDF; the converted TeX corrupts its definition. Impermeable inviscid walls impose v(±1,t)=0. They do not impose a viscous no-slip condition on the tangential perturbation velocity.
For a Fourier mode, set k2=α2+β2, D=d/dy and q=(D2−k2)v. The equations become
(∂t​+iαU)q−iαU′′v=0,(∂t​+iαU)η​=−iβU′v.
(35)
For Couette flow U=λy, U′′=0, so q(y,t)=e−iαλytq0​(y) with q0​=(D2−k2)v0​. Invert this using the Dirichlet Green function of D2−k2 on [−1,1]. If y<​=min(y,s) and y>​=max(y,s), it is
Gk​(y,s)=−ksinh(2k)sinh[k(y<​+1)]sinh[k(1−y>​)]​.
(36)
It vanishes at both walls and its first derivative has jump one at y=s. Thus
v(y,t)=∫−11​Gk​(y,s)e−iαλst(Ds2​−k2)v0​(s)ds.​
(37)
At t=0, uniqueness of the Dirichlet inversion recovers the compatible initial velocity. This inviscid Couette initial-value Green function directly solves the initial-value problem without a Laplace transform.
The Squire equation is now a first-order forced equation in time. Its integrating factor gives
η​(y,t)=e−iαλytη​0​(y)−iβλ∫0t​e−iαλy(t−s)v(y,s)ds.​
(38)
For nonzero α, the velocity formula contains shearing phases and the inviscid Couette continuous spectrum. Velocity cancellation can occur even though the transported q has constant magnitude; it must not be confused with exponential modal instability.
For the separate case α=0, β=0, the Dirichlet operator D2−β2 is invertible and qt​=0. Hence v=v0​ and
η​(y,t)=η​0​(y)−iβλtv0​(y).​
(39)
Since η​=iβu in this case, u=u0​−λtv0​. This is the lift-up effect: a transverse disturbance transports base-flow momentum and creates a streamwise perturbation growing linearly in time, with perturbation kinetic energy generically growing as t2. The growth is algebraic and is possible without an exponentially unstable normal mode. For nonzero initial normal velocity and nonzero shear, nonlinear effects eventually limit the validity of the linearized calculation. The same streamwise-independent result holds with λ replaced by U′(y) for a general parallel profile.

Let V=U−c and suppose a growing normal mode has Imc=ci​>0, taking α>0 without loss of generality. Since V never vanishes on the real channel, choose a continuous square-root branch and write ϕ=V1/2H. Direct differentiation gives
ϕ′=V1/2H′+2V1/2U′​H,
(40)
ϕ′′=V1/2H′′+V1/2U′​H′+(2V1/2U′′​−4V3/2U′2​)H.
(41)
Substitution into the Taylor–Goldstein equation and division by V1/2 gives
VH′′+U′H′−α2VH−21​U′′H+VJ−U′2/4​H=0.
(42)
Thus the required transformation is
D(VDH)−(α2V+21​U′′+VU′2/4−J​)H=0.​
(43)
The impermeable walls give ϕ(±1)=0, hence H(±1)=0. Multiply by H, integrate across the channel, and use integration by parts. With the boundary term zero, the resulting power-transformed Taylor–Goldstein energy identity is
∫−11​[V(∣H′∣2+α2∣H∣2)+21​U′′∣H∣2+VU′2/4−J​∣H∣2]dy=0.
(44)
Both U′′ and J are real. Since ImV=−ci​ and Im(1/V)=ci​/∣V∣2, its imaginary part, divided by −ci​, is
∫−11​[∣H′∣2+α2∣H∣2+∣U−c∣2J−U′2/4​∣H∣2]dy=0.
(45)
If J≥U′2/4 everywhere, the integrand is nonnegative and the first two terms have positive integral for any nonzero mode. This contradicts the identity. Therefore instability requires
J(y)<41​U′(y)2somewhere in the channel.​
(46)
This is the necessary instability condition of the Miles–Howard theorem. Where U′=0, it says that the gradient Richardson number J/U′2 must be below 1/4 somewhere. It is not a sufficient condition for instability, and the conclusion concerns exponential normal-mode growth, not exclusion of the algebraic lift-up effect in part (a). The algebra uses the given nondimensional J; in dimensional notation its counterpart is the squared buoyancy frequency.
