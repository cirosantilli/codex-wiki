Adjoin P1(Q) to the complex upper half-plane. Ordinary points retain their usual neighborhoods. At a rational boundary point a=σ∞, a neighborhood basis consists of a together with σ{z∈H:Imz>Y}, with Y arbitrarily large. Different choices of σ∈SL2​(Z) change it by a translation and sign, so give the same neighborhood system. Give X(1) the quotient topology. The modular group acts properly discontinuously on H; its modular cusp neighborhoods descend to punctured neighborhoods with their modular cusp point filled in. This quotient is Hausdorff: interior orbits can be separated by proper discontinuity, and a sufficiently high modular cusp neighborhood avoids any prescribed compact interior neighborhood.
Away from fixed points of the effective group PSL2​(Z), a small disk is disjoint from its nonidentity translates, so its coordinate gives a holomorphic quotient coordinate. At an elliptic fixed point τ0​, use
u=τ−τ0​​τ−τ0​​.
(10)
Its effective stabilizer rotates u by an eth root of unity, where e=2 at i and e=3 at ρ. The quotient coordinate is w=ue, not u. All rational boundary points lie in one modular cusp class. At infinity the stabilizer acts by τ↦τ+1, and
q=e2πiτ
(11)
is a punctured-disk coordinate; adjoining q=0 fills in the modular cusp. These charts and their holomorphic transition maps define the complex structure of the compactified modular curve.
For compactness use the standard region
D={τ∈H:∣Reτ∣≤1/2, ∣τ∣≥1}.
(12)
Its part with Imτ≤Y, Y>1, is compact: the imaginary part is at least 3​/2. The quotient of its upper tail, with its two vertical sides identified and infinity added, is the closed disk ∣q∣≤e−2πY. These two compact images cover X(1), including the boundary-edge identifications. Thus X(1) is a compact Riemann surface.
The j function also extends over the modular cusp with one simple pole, since j=q−1+O(1). Its local expressions at elliptic points descend to holomorphic functions of ue. By Question 1 it maps the remaining quotient bijectively onto C, so its extension is a degree-one holomorphic map X(1)→P1. A degree-one map of compact Riemann surfaces is biholomorphic: any local ramification would already give degree at least two. Therefore X(1) is the Riemann sphere; this will also identify the base genus in Question 5.
