Use time dependence e−iωt, positive wavenumber k and aperture normal n=ex​. Write F(y′,z′)=Einc,y​(0,y′,z′) on the aperture and extend F by zero outside it. Define
F(ν,η)=∫A​F(y′,z′)e−i(νy′+ηz′)dy′dz′,κ=k2−ν2−η2​,q=(κ,ν,η).
(1)
The outgoing branch has κ≥0 for propagating components and Imκ>0 for evanescent components.
Fix the Green function normalization by G0​(r)=eik∣r∣/(4π∣r∣), so (Δ+k2)G0​=−δ. Its transverse Fourier transform satisfies (∂x2​+κ2)G0​=−δ(x). An outgoing solution on both sides is proportional to eiκ∣x∣, and its derivative jump fixes G0​=ieiκ∣x∣/(2κ). Thus the Weyl plane-wave representation for observation at x>0 is
G0​=2(2π)2i​∫R2​κeiκx+iν(y−y′)+iη(z−z′)​dνdη.
(2)
Since n×Einc​=Fez​, the given aperture integral becomes a superposition of curls of ez​eiq⋅r. Each curl supplies iq×ez​=i(ν,−κ,0). Therefore, with the unit prefactor in that integral,
E(x,y,z)=2(2π)21​∫R2​F(ν,η)(−κν​,1,0)eiκx+iνy+iηzdνdη​.
(3)
Every vector amplitude is transverse to q, as Maxwell's equations require. The polarizer specifies the incident aperture field; diffraction can regenerate an x component outside the aperture. In contrast, its z component remains zero in this representation. Evanescent plane waves are needed for the complete near field, not just the propagating disk ν2+η2≤k2.
The factor 1/2 follows explicitly from the standard outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation and the printed unit curl prefactor. If the intended kernel is the doubled half-space kernel 2G0​, or the aperture formula carries the usual compensating factor 2, delete that 1/2. The latter normalization makes the tangential output trace equal to F. Keeping the convention explicit avoids silently changing the supplied representation; the angular dependence and transversality of an electromagnetic plane wave are the same in either normalization. Reversing the specified normal also reverses the overall sign.

Let the two tangential aperture fields be Fy​(y,z) and Fz​(y,z), extended by zero off the aperture. The normal component does not enter n×Einc​, which equals −Fz​ey​+Fy​ez​. Apply the Weyl plane-wave representation and curl to both components. With the same kernel normalization as part (a),
E(r)=2(2π)21​∫R2​[Fy​(ey​−κν​ex​)+Fz​(ez​−κη​ex​)]eiq⋅rdνdη​.
(4)
This is the electromagnetic aperture angular spectrum. The two electromagnetic polarization vectors are linearly independent and both orthogonal, bilinearly, to q; they need not be orthogonal to each other. Arbitrary tangential electromagnetic polarization is therefore represented, with its outgoing longitudinal component fixed by κEx​+νEy​+ηEz​=0 rather than independently prescribed. Away from grazing components one could equally express the spectrum in a transverse-electric and transverse-magnetic basis. The same limiting outgoing branch handles grazing and evanescent components.
For each component the magnetic amplitude follows from Faraday's law as B=q×E/ω. Together with q2=k2 and transversality of an electromagnetic plane wave, this verifies the source-free Maxwell equations. If the doubled-kernel aperture convention is used, multiply the entire expression by two, exactly as in part (a).

Use ψ​sc​(ν)=∫ψsc​(x,0)e−iνxdx, inverse factor 1/(2π), and k=ω/c. Fourier transform the homogeneous Helmholtz equation gives
∂z2​ψ​sc​+(k2−ν2)ψ​sc​=0.
(5)
An upward radiating field has no incoming component from z=+∞. For q(ν)=k2−ν2​, take the nonnegative real root when ∣ν∣<k and the positive imaginary root when ∣ν∣>k. Consequently the outgoing angular spectrum is
ψsc​(x,z)=2π1​∫R​ψ​sc​(ν)eiνx+iq(ν)zdν​.
(6)
The propagating terms go upward; the evanescent terms decay with increasing z. Grazing values are understood by a radiation limit.
There is a reference-plane qualification in the question. For an arbitrary rough surface the full line z=0 need not lie in the medium: already f(x)=h>0 puts it in vacuum. In that case the stated physical trace is undefined without continuation. The exact representation is obtained instead on a plane z=z∗​>supf,
ψsc​(x,z)=2π1​∫ψ​∗​(ν)eiνx+iq(ν)(z−z∗​)dν,z≥z∗​.
(7)
One can formally set ψ​sc​=ψ​∗​e−iqz∗​, but convergence of that continued spectrum down to the rough boundary is an additional assumption. This is the reference-plane validity of a rough-surface angular spectrum. The first boxed formula is exact in an appropriate homogeneous upper half-space; it is not automatically an exact representation at every point of a completely arbitrary rough domain. Small-height perturbation theory in part (c) uses the mean plane order by order.

For a complex harmonic field use complex conjugation in its wave-field correlation: m=⟨ψsc​(x1​,z)ψsc​(x2​,z)∗⟩. Put A(ν)=ψ​sc​(ν) and let C(ν,ν′)=⟨A(ν)A(ν′)∗⟩. Direct substitution of part (a), before imposing any homogeneity, gives
⟨ψsc​(x1​,z)ψsc​(x2​,z)∗⟩=(2π)21​∬C(ν,ν′)eiνx1​−iν′x2​+i[q(ν)−q(ν′)∗]zdνdν′.
(8)
This is the general answer. It need not depend only on ξ=x1​−x2​: even a deterministic sum of two distinct outgoing plane waves has cross terms depending on the midpoint. The requested separation-only wave-field correlation therefore presumes translation-homogeneous two-point statistics.
Under that assumption define the uncentered spectrum by C(ν,ν′)=2πS(ν)δ(ν−ν′). Then the height propagation of an angular-spectrum correlation is
m(ξ,z)=2π1​∫R​S(ν)eiνξ−2Imq(ν)zdν​.
(9)
Equivalently, for the spectral measure of a stationary random field F(dν)=S(ν)dν/(2π), write m=∫eiνξ−2ImqzF(dν). This version includes coherent spectral atoms and avoids squaring Dirac delta functions. The propagating spectrum ∣ν∣≤k is independent of height, while an evanescent contribution is multiplied by e−2ν2−k2​z. At ξ=0 the formula gives the mean wave intensity.
If the brackets instead mean the spatial autocorrelation of a finite-energy deterministic field, define mL​(ξ,z)=∫ψsc​(x+ξ,z)ψsc​(x,z)∗dx. Parseval's identity gives the same integral with S(ν)=∣ψ​sc​(ν)∣2. That finite-energy formula must not be applied unmodified to an infinite stationary field or an infinite plane wave, whose transforms require a spectral-measure convention.

Write the unit-amplitude incident plane wave as ψi​=eipi​x−iqi​z, with qi​=k2−pi2​​>0. The flat Dirichlet reflected field is ψs[0]​=−eipi​x+iqi​z, so the flat total field vanishes at z=0 and has derivative ∂z​ψ[0](x,0)=−2iqi​eipi​x.
For a controlled height expansion take f=εh with fixed regular bounded shape h. Taylor expansion at the moving boundary yields
0=ψ[0](x,0)+f(x)∂z​ψ[0](x,0)+ψs[1]​(x,0)+O(ε2).
(10)
Thus the first-order scattered trace is ψs[1]​(x,0)=2iqi​f(x)eipi​x, and its transform follows by the Translation property of the Fourier transform:
ψ​s[1]​(ν)=2iqi​f​(ν−pi​),ψ​sc​(ν)=−2πδ(ν−pi​)+2iqi​f​(ν−pi​)+O(ε2)​.
(11)
The first formula is the roughness correction; the second includes the zeroth-order reflection. The small-height assumption controls the Taylor expansion for a fixed profile; arbitrarily fine surface scales can require additional derivative control, not merely a small height at fixed k.
If the mean roughness height is a constant fˉ​, as for stationary roughness, then ⟨f​(ν−pi​)⟩=2πfˉ​δ(ν−pi​). Consequently the specular first-order mean reflection is
⟨ψsc​(x,z)⟩=(−1+2iqi​fˉ​)eipi​x+iqi​z+O(ε2)​.
(12)
Its tangential wavenumber is unchanged and its normal wavenumber is reversed relative to incidence, which is precisely specular reflection. For zero mean height the first-order mean correction is zero, but the full mean reflected field is not zero: it still contains the flat reflection.
Small height alone does not imply the statistical conclusion. For example, deterministic mean height fˉ​(x)=acosKx with ∣ka∣≪1 produces first-order mean sidebands at pi​±K and is not purely specular. Constant mean is sufficient at first order; stationarity supplies it and, with suitable translation-invariant statistics, constrains higher-order coherent reflection as well.

Use positive carrier wavenumber k and retain waves traveling mainly in the positive x direction. Factor out their rapid wave phase by writing ψ=eikxE(x,z). Substitution into the Helmholtz equation is exact and gives
Exx​+2ikEx​+Ezz​=0.
(13)
For a transverse Fourier mode eiνz at a small angle, ∣ν∣/k≪1 and its forward longitudinal wavenumber is
kx​=k2−ν2​=k−2kν2​+O(ν4/k3).
(14)
Thus the envelope varies along x on scale k/ν2, much more slowly than the carrier wavelength, and ∣Exx​∣/∣kEx​∣=O(ν2/k2). Dropping this smaller term derives the parabolic wave equation,
2ikEx​+Ezz​=0,Ex​=2ki​Ezz​​.
(15)
With transform E(x,ν)=∫E(x,z)e−iνzdz, its free propagation is
E(x,ν)=E(0,ν)e−iν2x/(2k),E(x,z)=2π1​∫E(0,ν)eiνz−iν2x/(2k)dν.
(16)
This is a one-way paraxial approximation, not a replacement for backward waves or large-angle Helmholtz components. Its Fourier multiplier has unit modulus and therefore does not attenuate any admitted component.

Represent the thin layer by its wave phase jump at the exit x=0 and take the incident envelope to have amplitude one. Then E(0,z)=eiϕ(z). Stationarity makes the wave phase mean a constant μ; it need not be zero, since the question specifies only the variance. For a normal distribution marginal, write ϕ=μ+σZ with Z∼N(0,1). If F(t)=E[eitZ], integration by parts in the Gaussian density gives F′(t)=−tF(t) and F(0)=1, hence F(t)=e−t2/2. Thus its characteristic function gives
⟨E(0,z)⟩=⟨eiϕ(z)⟩=eiμ−σ2/2≡M.
(17)
The Fourier transform of this constant is 2πMδ(ν), interpreted distributionally for the infinite plane wave. Applying the propagator from part (a),
⟨E(x,ν)⟩=2πeiμ−σ2/2δ(ν)e−iν2x/(2k)=2πeiμ−σ2/2δ(ν)​.
(18)
If the wave phase has zero mean, omit eiμ. A nonunit incident amplitude simply multiplies the expression. Only the one-point Gaussian distribution is needed for this expected value; no assumption about joint Gaussian distributions at separated points is needed.

Inverse Fourier transform of the expected value gives
⟨E(x,z)⟩=eiμ−σ2/2​,
(19)
independent of both coordinates. Its squared modulus is the coherent scalar wave intensity e−σ2, which must not be confused with the mean wave intensity.
For the latter use the stationary two-point statistics. Let F0​(dν) be the uncentered spectral measure of a stationary random field of E(0,z), normalized by
⟨E(0,z+ζ)E(0,z)∗⟩=∫eiνζF0​(dν).
(20)
Since ∣E(0,z)∣=1, its total mass is ∫F0​(dν)=1. Under free paraxial propagation each spectral component is multiplied by e−iν2x/(2k); in its wave-field correlation this wave phase multiplies its complex conjugate and cancels. The stationary intensity under free paraxial propagation is consequently
⟨I(x,z)⟩=∫F0​(dν)=1​.
(21)
Stationarity is essential to this pointwise statement: it makes the spectral covariance diagonal. Conservation of integrated power alone would not show that a particular spatial point keeps the same scalar wave intensity.
The same result follows locally from Ix​+Jz​=0, where J=Im(E∗Ez​)/k. Taking expectations makes ⟨J⟩ independent of z, so ∂x​⟨I⟩=0. Thus coherent scalar wave intensity and mean wave intensity are both constant in propagation, with different values. The incoherent contribution has mean 1−e−σ2. Individual realizations can show focusing and changing scalar wave intensity even though the expected value remains constant. All these propagation statements concern the free parabolic wave equation from part (a).

Hadamard well-posedness requires existence of a solution for every admissible datum, uniqueness, and continuous dependence of the solution on the datum in the specified normed spaces. An inverse problem is ill-posed if any of these requirements fails. The choice of spaces and norms is part of this assertion: continuity in one norm does not establish continuity in a stronger norm.
For a bounded linear operator A, existence requires y∈ranA, and uniqueness requires kerA={0}. Even when both hold for exact data, inversion can be unstable. For example, if ∥xN​∥X​=1 but ∥AxN​∥Y​→0, data AxN​ approach zero while their solutions do not approach zero. Thus A−1 on its range is not continuous. Perturbed data can also leave the range entirely, so an exact solution need not exist after measurement error. These are distinct mechanisms of ill-posedness; the inverse scattering problem below exhibits unstable continuation even on data for which an outgoing continuation exists.

Write r=∣x∣, θ=x/r, and choose R so that the sphere r=R strictly encloses the scatterer. Normalize the spherical harmonics to be an orthonormal basis of L2(S2), with surface measure on the unit sphere. The outgoing spherical Hankel function is denoted here by hn(1)​, corresponding to the printed Hn(1)​. For fixed n, its large-argument behavior is
hn(1)​(kr)=(−i)n+1kreikr​(1+O(r−1)).
(26)
Consequently the coefficient of Ynm​ in the outgoing expansion is asymptotic to anm​eikr/r: the factors satisfy in+1(−i)n+1=1. With the far-field pattern defined by us​(rθ)=eikru∞​(θ)/r+O(r−2), we obtain
u∞​(θ)=n=0∑∞​m=−n∑n​anm​Ynm​(θ).​
(27)
One can first project onto each spherical harmonic and then take the large-r limit. This avoids assuming that the fixed-order asymptotic is uniform in n. The Sommerfeld radiation condition selects the outgoing spherical Hankel functions rather than their incoming counterparts.
Let g(θ)=us​(Rθ). Its coefficients are gnm​=dn​anm​, where dn​=kin+1hn(1)​(kR). Since the field is smooth on a sphere outside the scatterer, g∈L2(S2); Parseval's identity gives
∥g∥L2(S2)2​=k2∑n=0∞​∣hn(1)​(kR)∣2∑m=−nn​∣anm​∣2<∞.
(28)
The required coefficient condition uses fixed-argument growth of spherical Hankel functions, a different limit from the far-field limit. For fixed t>0,
yn​(t)∼−tn+1(2n−1)!!​,jn​(t)=O((2n+1)!!tn​),hn(1)​(t)=jn​(t)+iyn​(t).
(29)
For completeness, the series of the ordinary Bessel function gives J−n−1/2​(t)∼(t/2)−n−1/2/Γ(1/2−n). The identities yn​(t)=(−1)n+1π/(2t)​J−n−1/2​(t) and Γ(1/2−n)=(−4)nn!π​/(2n)! give the first formula; the corresponding positive-order series gives the estimate for jn​. The successive series corrections at fixed t are O(1/n). Applying Stirling's approximation to the factorial ratio yields
(2n−1)!!=2nn!(2n)!​∼2​(e2n​)n,∣hn(1)​(t)∣∼t2​​(et2n​)n.
(30)
In particular there are positive constants giving upper and lower bounds by the last weight for all sufficiently large n. The finite trace norm is therefore equivalent to the spherical far-field range condition
n=0∑∞​(ekR2n​)2nm=−n∑n​∣anm​∣2<∞,​

where the n=0 weight is defined to be one. The same conclusion holds at every larger sphere on which the outgoing field is defined.
The printed comparison with ∞ must mean a finite sum: a literal non-strict inequality allows a divergent sum. Also, the supplied one-sided big-O bound alone does not imply this necessary condition from a finite trace norm; a lower bound is needed in that direction. The two-sided asymptotic above supplies it. Conversely, its upper bound makes the weighted condition sufficient for an L2 trace at the chosen sphere. This is a condition on exterior-field continuation, not a sufficient condition that the coefficients come from a particular Dirichlet obstacle.

Use the L2(S2) data norm. The coefficients of the measurement error are enm​=bnm​−anm​, so Parseval's identity gives
∑n,m​∣enm​∣2≤δ2.
(32)
An unregularized continuation of these measurements to r=R replaces anm​ by bnm​ in the outgoing spherical harmonic expansion. Whenever this continuation has a finite trace, its error is
∥gδ−g∥2=k2∑n,m​∣hn(1)​(kR)∣2∣enm​∣2.
(33)
Choose a measurement error supported on a single normalized spherical harmonic, with eN0​=δ and all other coefficients zero. The data error has norm exactly δ, but
∥gδ−g∥=δk∣hN(1)​(kR)∣⟶∞(N→∞).​
(34)
This perturbation theory is a finite expansion, so it defines an actual outgoing solution of the Helmholtz equation outside any sphere of positive radius. It is not merely an example of nonconvergent formal coefficients. Taking instead eN0​=∣dN​∣−1 gives data errors tending to zero and trace errors of norm one, which directly proves discontinuity of inversion. General square-summable measurement noise can even violate the spherical far-field range condition, leaving no L2 continuation to the chosen sphere.
For obstacle reconstruction one would continue the field toward the unknown boundary and look for a surface on which ui​+us​=0, the Dirichlet boundary condition. The unbounded spherical far-to-near-field continuation shows why small far-field errors can cause arbitrarily large errors in the field used to estimate that surface. Locally, a differentiable normal displacement η of a candidate boundary must obey the linearized condition Δus​+η∂n​(ui​+us​)=0. Thus a large continued-field error, or a small normal derivative, defeats an uncontrolled boundary estimate.
There is a necessary qualification to the wording about errors in the scatterer itself. The calculation proves an unbounded field-continuation error. An arbitrary perturbed outgoing field need not correspond to any Dirichlet obstacle, and no admissible class or metric on obstacle shapes is specified. It therefore does not by itself prove an arbitrarily large geometric error in every constrained reconstruction method. Such a claim requires a shape model and an analysis of the boundary-extraction step. The instability displayed here is exactly the mechanism that an unregularized reconstruction must control.

To illustrate Tikhonov regularization precisely, take the unknown to be the trace g on a chosen enclosing sphere of radius R. Use Hilbert spaces X=Y=L2(S2) and the forward linear operator
(Ag)nm​=dn​gnm​​,dn​=kin+1hn(1)​(kR).
(35)
At real positive kR, dn​=0, because the real Spherical Bessel functions jn​,yn​ have a nonzero Wronskian and cannot vanish simultaneously. Moreover ∣dn​∣→∞. Thus A is an injective compact operator: truncating to finitely many harmonic orders approximates it in operator norm, since the remaining diagonal entries tend uniformly to zero. Its inverse is unbounded. This is a linear field reconstruction; estimating the obstacle from the reconstructed field remains a further nonlinear step.
Given coefficients bnm​ of measured data yδ, minimize the quadratic functional
∥Ag−yδ∥2+α∥g∥2,α>0.
(36)
Varying g in every direction gives (A∗A+αI)gαδ​=A∗yδ. The operator A∗A+αI is bounded below by αI, so it is invertible and the minimizer is unique. In coefficients the minimization is separable: A∗y has coefficient bnm​/dn​​ and A∗A has multiplier ∣dn​∣−2. Hence the Tikhonov regularization of spherical far-field continuation is
(gαδ​)nm​=1+α∣dn​∣2dn​bnm​​.​
(37)
The implied regularized far-field coefficients are bnm​/(1+α∣dn​∣2). This suppresses precisely the high-order coefficients that make backward continuation unstable.
Let g† be the exact trace and y=Ag†, with ∥yδ−y∥≤δ. The noise-bias decomposition for linear regularization is
gαδ​−g†=(A∗A+αI)−1A∗(yδ−y)−α(A∗A+αI)−1g†.
(38)
For a singular value s=∣dn​∣−1, the noise multiplier is s/(s2+α). Since s2+α≥2sα​, its supremum is at most 1/(2α​). Consequently
∥gαδ​−g†∥≤2α​δ​+B(α),B(α)=​α(A∗A+αI)−1g†​.​
(39)
This Tikhonov stability bound measures the trace norm, not a distance between obstacle boundaries. The bias coefficient is α/(∣dn​∣−2+α) times the exact coefficient. It tends to zero for each fixed n,m, is bounded by one, and ∑∣gn†,m​∣2<∞. Splitting off a finite sum and bounding its tail therefore proves B(α)→0 as α→0.
A convergent regularization parameter choice thus satisfies
α(δ)→0,α(δ)​δ​→0.​
(40)
In nondimensionalized variables, α=δp with 0<p<2 is one such rule. Without a smoothness assumption on the exact trace there is no universal bias rate and no universal optimal value of α. For a concrete normal-operator source condition, suppose g†=A∗Aw and ∥w∥≤C. Each bias multiplier on w is αs2/(s2+α)≤α, so B(α)≤Cα. Minimizing the resulting bound gives, for C>0,
dαd​(Cα+2α​δ​)=C−4α3/2δ​,α=(4Cδ​)2/3,∥gαδ​−g†∥=O(C1/3δ2/3).​
(41)
This optimizes the displayed worst-case bound under that additional source condition, rather than guaranteeing an exact optimum for every datum.
If only the effect of measurement error is minimized, the multiplier bound decreases when α increases. Indeed ∥(A∗A+αI)−1A∗∥≤∥A∥/α→0 as α→∞. But the reconstruction then approaches zero and its bias approaches ∥g†∥. Suppressing noise alone does not optimize reconstruction: α must balance noise amplification against regularization bias. A geometrically accurate obstacle estimate additionally needs justified shape constraints and a stable procedure for extracting a boundary from the regularized field.
