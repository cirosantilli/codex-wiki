An ultrafilter on N={1,2,…} is a family p⊆P(N) satisfying the proper filter axioms: N∈p, ∅∈/p, closure under finite intersections, and upward closure under inclusion. In addition, for every A⊆N, exactly one of A and Ac belongs to p. Equivalently, an ultrafilter is a maximal proper filter. The principal ultrafilter at n consists of all subsets containing n; an ultrafilter not of this form is a nonprincipal ultrafilter.
To prove existence, start with the cofinite filter and order the proper filters containing it by inclusion. The union of a chain is still a proper filter: finitely many of its members lie in one filter of the chain, and no constituent contains the empty set. Thus Zorn lemma supplies a maximal such filter p. If A∈/p, adjoining A cannot generate a proper filter; otherwise maximality fails. Consequently some B∈p has B∩A=∅, so Ac∈p by upward closure. This proves the ultrafilter alternative. No finite A can belong to p, because Ac is cofinite and already belongs to p. In particular p contains no singleton and is a nonprincipal ultrafilter. The same argument, starting from any proper filter, proves the ultrafilter lemma used below.
Define βN to be the set of all ultrafilters on N, with basic open sets
A={p∈βN:A∈p},A⊆N.
(18)
They form a topological basis because N=βN and A∩B=A∩B. Moreover (A)c=Ac, so they are clopen sets. This is the compact Hausdorff topology on ultrafilters. If p=q, some A belongs to p but not q, and then A and Ac are disjoint open neighbourhoods of the two points. Thus βN is Hausdorff.
For compactness, take an open cover and refine it by basic open sets Aλ​​. If no finite subfamily covers, then for every finite collection of indices the intersection ⋂Aλc​ is nonempty. Indeed, if that intersection were empty, the corresponding Aλ​ would cover N, and every ultrafilter would contain at least one of them by the finite ultrafilter alternative. The complements therefore have the finite intersection property and generate a proper filter. Extend it to an ultrafilter q by the proved ultrafilter lemma. Then Aλc​∈q for every λ, so q belongs to none of the covering Aλ​​, a contradiction. A finite basic subcover lies inside a finite subcover of the original cover. Hence
βN is compact and Hausdorff.​
(19)
The map n↦pn​, where pn​ is the principal ultrafilter at n, identifies the discrete natural numbers with a dense subset: any nonempty A has A=∅ and contains pn​ for n∈A.
Hindman theorem states that every finite colouring of the positive integers admits an increasing sequence x1​<x2​<⋯ whose finite-sums set
FS(x1​,x2​,…)={∑i∈F​xi​:∅=F⊆N finite}
(20)
is monochromatic. We now give the Idempotent-ultrafilter proof of Hindman's theorem. For A⊆N, write A−x={y∈N:x+y∈A}. The addition on βN is defined by
A∈p+q⟺{x∈N:A−x∈q}∈p.
(21)
Take an idempotent ultrafilter on the natural numbers p, whose existence is allowed, so p+p=p. It is a nonprincipal ultrafilter: addition of principal points is pm​+pn​=pm+n​, whereas no positive integer satisfies 2m=m.
Exactly one colour class A belongs to p. Define
A∗=A∩{x:A−x∈p}.
(22)
By idempotence, A∗∈p. The idempotent-ultrafilter star-set lemma needed for the recursion is that A∗−x∈p whenever x∈A∗. To prove it, put D=A−x∈p. Idempotence again gives
D∗=D∩{y:D−y∈p}∈p.
(23)
For y∈D∗, we have x+y∈A and A−(x+y)=D−y∈p, hence x+y∈A∗. Thus D∗⊆A∗−x, proving the lemma by upward closure.
Choose x1​∈A∗. Suppose Fn​=FS(x1​,…,xn​)⊆A∗ has been constructed. Every factor of
A∗∩⋂s∈Fn​​(A∗−s)∩{z:z>∑i=1n​xi​}
(24)
belongs to p: the translate factors do by the just-proved lemma, and the last factor does because it is cofinite and p is a nonprincipal ultrafilter. The finite intersection is therefore nonempty. Choose xn+1​ in it. Then xn+1​ and every s+xn+1​ with s∈Fn​ lie in A∗, so Fn+1​⊆A∗. This constructs a strictly increasing sequence, indeed xn+1​>∑i≤n​xi​, and proves Hindman theorem.
Finally apply Hindman theorem to obtain a monochromatic finite-sums set FS(y1​,y2​,…). We construct a divisibility chain inside a finite-sums set using disjoint successive blocks of indices. Start with x1​=y1​. If xi​=m, take 2m unused terms after all indices used so far, and split them into two groups of m consecutive terms. In each group its m+1 partial sums, including 0, contain two in the same congruence class modulo m by the pigeonhole principle. Their difference is a nonempty consecutive sum divisible by m, and positivity makes it at least m. Let xi+1​ be the sum of these two selected sums. Then m∣xi+1​ and xi+1​≥2m>m.
The index set defining xi+1​ lies strictly after all previous index sets. Discard the other terms in these two groups before continuing. Because the chosen index sets are disjoint, every finite sum of distinct xi​ is a finite sum of distinct yj​, retaining the same colour. Therefore
x1​<x2​<⋯,xi​∣xi+1​ for every i,FS(x1​,x2​,…) is monochromatic.​
(25)
