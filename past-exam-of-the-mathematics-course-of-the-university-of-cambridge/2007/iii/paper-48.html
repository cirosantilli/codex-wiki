Define the autoregressive polynomial Φ(z)=1−∑k=1p​ϕk​zk and the moving-average polynomial Θ(z)=1+∑k=1q​θk​zk. The standard causality and invertibility root criteria for an ARMA model are
Φ(z)=0 and Θ(z)=0for every ∣z∣≤1.​
(1)
In the usual minimal autoregressive moving-average model, the two polynomials have no common roots. Under that convention, these are the necessary and sufficient root conditions for a causal and invertible representation with the given noise. Without minimality they remain sufficient; shared factors must be considered before claiming necessity.
To see why the conditions work, both ratios C(z)=Θ(z)/Φ(z) and D(z)=Φ(z)/Θ(z) are analytic on a disc of radius greater than one. Their power series coefficients therefore decay geometrically and are absolutely summable. The backshift operator BXt​=Xt−1​ then gives a well-defined mean-square convergent causal time-series representation
Xt​=C(B)ϵt​=∑j≥0​cj​ϵt−j​.
(2)
Filtering white noise by these fixed coefficients gives a weakly stationary process. The identity Φ(B)C(B)=Θ(B) verifies the original equation. Multiplication by the absolutely summable inverse of Φ(B) also shows uniqueness among finite-variance stationary solutions. Similarly D(B)Xt​=ϵt​ gives the invertible time-series representation.
Comparing coefficients in Φ(z)C(z)=Θ(z) derives the first of the ARMA coefficient recursions:
cj​=θj​+k=1∑p​ϕk​cj−k​(j≥0).​
(3)
Here cj​=0 for negative j, θ0​=1, and θj​=0 for j>q, so in particular c0​=1. For the inverse filter, compare coefficients in Θ(z)D(z)=Φ(z). With a0​=1, aj​=−ϕj​ for 1≤j≤p, aj​=0 for j>p, and dj​=0 for negative indices, this gives
dj​=aj​−k=1∑q​θk​dj−k​(j≥0),d0​=1.​
(4)
The minus sign in the inverse recursion comes from moving the nonconstant moving-average terms to the other side.
For the first-order model, the standard uncancelled conditions are ∣ϕ∣<1 and ∣θ∣<1. The geometric power series expansions give
1−ϕz1+θz​=1+(ϕ+θ)∑j≥1​ϕj−1zj,1+θz1−ϕz​=1−(ϕ+θ)∑j≥1​(−θ)j−1zj.
(5)
Thus the ARMA(1,1) causal and inverse coefficients are
c0​=d0​=1,cj​=(ϕ+θ)ϕj−1,dj​=−(ϕ+θ)(−θ)j−1(j≥1).​
(6)
At j=1 these give c1​=ϕ+θ and d1​=−(ϕ+θ), including zero-valued parameters without any negative powers. If θ=−ϕ, the filtered process reduces to Xt​=ϵt​ and all later coefficients vanish. This common factor cancellation in an ARMA model is why the root inequalities should not be advertised as necessary for every nonminimal parameterization. The stated inequalities still ensure uniqueness for the original equation as well as the desired causal and invertible filters.

For 0<u<1 define the generalized inverse, or quantile function, by
Q(u)=inf{x∈R:F(x)≥u}.
(13)
It is finite because the cumulative distribution function tends to zero and one at the two infinities. Monotonicity and right continuity of F give the key equivalence
Q(u)≤x⟺u≤F(x).
(14)
For the forward implication, choose points in the defining set approaching its infimum from above. Right continuity gives F(Q(u))≥u, and then monotonicity gives F(x)≥u whenever Q(u)≤x. The reverse implication follows immediately because x then belongs to the defining set. Consequently, for a continuous uniform distribution draw U,
P(Q(U)≤x)=P(U≤F(x))=F(x).​
(15)
This proves inverse transform sampling for discontinuous as well as continuous distribution functions.
There is a minor endpoint defect in the printed codomain. The true quantile endpoint at probability one is Q(1)=inf{x:F(x)=1}, which equals +∞ for an unbounded-above normal distribution. Thus it need not be real at one. Use the extended-real value there, or assign an arbitrary finite value at this single probability level. Since P(U=1)=0, either choice gives the same sampled distribution; the latter supplies a real-valued version on all of (0,1]. The endpoint has no effect on the inverse-transform law.
For rejection sampling, choose a proposal probability density function g that can be sampled and a finite constant M with f(y)≤Mg(y) wherever the target has positive mass. Generate independent pairs (Yj​,Uj​), with Yj​ of density g and Uj​ uniform. Accept Yj​ if Uj​≤f(Yj​)/(Mg(Yj​)); otherwise discard the pair and repeat with fresh independent draws. At points where both densities vanish the acceptance rule may be defined arbitrarily. For any measurable set A,
P(Yj​∈A, acceptance)=∫A​g(y)Mg(y)f(y)​dy=M1​∫A​f(y)dy.
(16)
Thus one trial accepts with probability 1/M, and the conditional density given acceptance is f. More explicitly, summing over the index of the first accepted proposal gives
P(output∈A)=∑j≥1​(1−1/M)j−1M1​∫A​f(y)dy=∫A​f(y)dy.
(17)
The probability of never accepting is zero, since M<∞, and the expected number of proposals is M. The algorithm terminates almost surely and samples the target density exactly.

Both the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm and the Gibbs sampler construct a Markov chain with a specified target distribution, usually a posterior distribution known only up to normalization. For a proposal density q(y∣x) and target density π, the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability is
a(x,y)=min{1,π(x)q(y∣x)π(y)q(x∣y)​}.
(32)
After drawing y, move there with this probability; otherwise stay at x. The unknown normalizing constant of π cancels. The original Metropolis algorithm uses a symmetric proposal, q(y∣x)=q(x∣y), so the proposal-density ratio disappears. The general Metropolis–Hastings algorithm permits nonsymmetric proposals and needs that correction.
A Gibbs sampler instead chooses a coordinate or block and draws directly from its full conditional distribution, leaving the other coordinates fixed. It is exactly a conditional Metropolis–Hastings update whose proposal is that full conditional. For a change from xi​ to yi​ with x−i​ fixed, the acceptance ratio is
π(xi​,x−i​)π(yi​∣x−i​)π(yi​,x−i​)π(xi​∣x−i​)​=1.
(33)
Thus a Gibbs update is an always-accepted Metropolis–Hastings update with the full conditional as proposal. This statement concerns each update: a systematic sweep of several Gibbs kernels preserves the target but need not itself be reversible.
When the full conditional distributions are easy to sample, a Gibbs sampler avoids proposal tuning and rejection, and is particularly convenient for conjugate priors and data augmentation. When a conditional cannot be sampled directly, a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm can use only its evaluable density; such an update can also be inserted into a Gibbs sweep. Block or joint Metropolis–Hastings proposals can move efficiently along strongly correlated directions for which coordinate-wise Gibbs updates mix slowly. Conversely, a badly chosen Metropolis–Hastings proposal can reject most moves or make only tiny moves. Acceptance probability one by itself does not guarantee rapid exploration. Both methods require appropriate irreducibility and convergence checks before their long-run averages are useful posterior estimates.

Both data augmentation and the expectation-maximization algorithm introduce latent variables Z that make the complete-data model easier to handle. The observed-data likelihood is obtained by summing or integrating p(x,z∣θ) over the missing data, and both methods use the conditional law of Z given the observations and current parameters.
In data augmentation for Bayesian inference, one alternates random draws
Z(r+1)∼p(z∣x,θ(r)),θ(r+1)∼p(θ∣x,Z(r+1)).
(42)
These are Gibbs sampler updates on the joint posterior distribution; marginal parameter draws explore the whole posterior, including uncertainty, provided the chain converges appropriately. The parameter conditional incorporates its prior.
The expectation-maximization algorithm is instead a deterministic optimization procedure. Its E step forms
Q(θ∣θ(r))=EZ∣x,θ(r)​[logp(x,Z∣θ)],
(43)
and its M step maximizes this function over θ. Exact steps do not decrease the observed-data likelihood function. Their aim is a likelihood stationary point, often a local maximum, rather than draws from a parameter distribution. Adding a log prior to the maximization gives a posterior-mode version, which still does not sample the posterior.
Data augmentation samples conditional missing data and parameters; EM averages the complete-data log likelihood and optimizes parameters. The E step is an expectation of the log likelihood, not the log of an averaged likelihood, and generally is not equivalent to merely replacing each missing value by its conditional mean. A Monte Carlo E-step approximation is a further variant of EM, not automatically the same procedure as a data augmentation chain.

Fit the finite Gaussian mixture with a common variance for candidate values k=1,…,K, using several initializations of the expectation-maximization algorithm for each, and retain the best likelihood value found. Maximized likelihood alone cannot select the number of components: a smaller mixture embeds in a larger one by giving an added component zero weight, so its likelihood cannot decrease.
There are k−1 free mixture weights, k means and one common variance, hence
dk​=(k−1)+k+1=2k.
(51)
A standard working choice is the Bayesian information criterion:
k=1≤k≤Kargmin​{−2ℓ(θk​)+2klogN}.​
(52)
The penalty balances fit against parameter count. The Akaike information criterion instead uses −2ℓ(θk​)+4k and is another possible comparison, particularly when predictive fit is the goal. The parameter count would differ if component variances were separate, but that is not this model.
Likelihood fitting must first be made well posed. Even a shared variance does not prevent all degeneracy: if k=N, choose μj​=xj​, αj​=1/N and let the common variance tend to zero. Each observed density is at least 1/(N2πσ2​), so the full log-likelihood function tends to +∞. The same construction works when k reaches the number of distinct observed values. Thus one must not feed infinite fitted likelihoods into an information criterion. Restrict candidate sizes to a sensible range below that degeneracy, or impose a fixed positive variance floor and use that same constrained model in every comparison. Under a variance floor ε, the M-step variance becomes max{ε,R/N}.
There is also nonregular mixture model selection: at a missing component its weight is on a boundary and its mean is unidentified, while permuting labels leaves the distribution unchanged. Consequently an ordinary chi-squared calibration for a likelihood-ratio test between successive component counts is not generally justified. Information criteria are practical comparisons, not an automatic universal proof that the true number has been recovered. Predictive cross-validation or a suitably constructed parametric bootstrap comparison can supplement them. Select the component count by a penalized, well-defined mixture fit, rather than by unpenalized likelihood increase.
