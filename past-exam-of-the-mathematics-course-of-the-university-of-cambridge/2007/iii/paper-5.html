A topological group is a group with a topology for which multiplication G×G→G and inversion G→G are continuous. A profinite group has either of the following equivalent descriptions: a compact Hausdorff totally disconnected topological group, or an inverse limit of finite discrete groups. Totally disconnected means that every connected component is a singleton.
For clarity, an inverse system over a directed set I consists of groups Gi​ and maps ϕij​:Gj​→Gi​ for i≤j, with ϕii​=1 and ϕik​=ϕij​ϕjk​. Its inverse limit is
lim​Gi​={(gi​)∈∏i​Gi​:ϕij​(gj​)=gi​ whenever i≤j},
(1)
with coordinatewise multiplication and the subspace topology. Equivalently, it is a group with compatible continuous projections such that every other compatible family of maps into the Gi​ factors uniquely through it.
The displayed limit is a closed subgroup of the compact Hausdorff product of the finite groups, and is totally disconnected, since two different tuples are separated by a clopen coordinate condition. Conversely a compact Hausdorff totally disconnected group has a neighbourhood basis of open normal subgroups. One way to see the group part of this standard clopen-basis fact is to choose a clopen identity neighbourhood U inside a prescribed neighbourhood. Compactness of U and continuity give a symmetric identity neighbourhood V with VU⊆U. The stabilizer H={g:gU=U} contains V, is open, and is contained in U because 1∈U. Its index is finite by part (ii); its normal core is the intersection of finitely many conjugates and is open and normal. The canonical map
G⟶lim​N⊴o​G​G/N
(2)
is injective because these N separate points, and surjective because compatible cosets have the finite intersection property and G is compact. It is a homeomorphism by compactness and the Hausdorff property. This explains the equivalence of the two definitions.
The profinite Frattini subgroup is Φ(G)=⋂M, where M ranges over all maximal proper open subgroups; for the trivial group the empty intersection is G. For a pro-p group,
Φ(G)=Gp[G,G]​.​
(3)
Here powers and group commutators mean generated subgroups and the bar supplies closure. Every maximal open subgroup in a pro-p group is normal of index p, as follows by passing to the finite p-group quotient by its normal core. Such a subgroup therefore contains all powers and group commutators. Conversely the quotient by D=Gp[G,G]​ is an abelian pro-p group of exponent p. If an element of this quotient is nontrivial, some finite elementary abelian quotient detects it; a linear functional to Fp​ then detects it as well. Its group homomorphism kernel pulls back to a maximal open subgroup of G. Thus the intersection of these kernels is exactly D.
We also use the topological generation criterion: a subset generates G topologically if and only if its image generates G/Φ(G) topologically. To prove the nontrivial direction, a proper closed generated subgroup K lies in a proper open subgroup KN for a suitably small open normal N, and hence in a maximal open subgroup obtained from the finite quotient G/N. Since that maximal subgroup contains Φ(G), the image of K cannot generate the Frattini quotient.
Now suppose G is a nontrivial procyclic pro-p group. If it had distinct maximal open subgroups M1​,M2​, the homomorphism to Cp​×Cp​ induced by the two quotient maps would be onto: its projections are onto, and a proper such subgroup would be a one-dimensional graph, forcing the two kernels to coincide. Hence G/(M1​∩M2​)≅Cp2​, contradicting procyclicity. Thus there is exactly one maximal open subgroup M, and Φ(G)=M. Any g∈/M generates G/Φ(G)≅Cp​, so the criterion gives
G=⟨g⟩​.​
(4)
For the trivial group use its identity as generator. The additive group Zp​ is an infinite example, topologically generated by 1: its finite continuous quotients are cyclic Z/pnZ.

Suppose (L,(πi​)) and (L′,(πi′​)) are inverse limits of the same system, in the universal-property sense. The compatible family (πi′​) induces a unique continuous homomorphism u:L′→L with πi​u=πi′​; similarly there is a unique v:L→L′ with πi′​v=πi​. Then uv and 1L​ both induce the projections (πi​), so uniqueness gives uv=1L​. Likewise vu=1L′​. Thus the limits are uniquely isomorphic by an isomorphism preserving every projection. In particular the inverse maps are continuous, so this is an isomorphism of topological groups, not just abstract groups.

For odd p, a subgroup N is powerfully embedded in G if [N,G]≤Np, where Np=⟨np:n∈N⟩. The condition implies normality since ng=n[n,g]∈N. The group G is a powerful p-group if [G,G]≤Gp, equivalently if G itself is powerfully embedded. For a finite p-group, the Burnside basis theorem gives d(Q)=dimFp​​Q/Φ(Q), and Φ(Q)=Qp[Q,Q]. Thus for powerful Q, d(Q)=dimQ/Qp.
We prove d(H)≤d(G) by induction on ∣G∣, with the trivial group immediate. The supplied embedding result applied to N=G makes Gp powerfully embedded, and hence powerful. It is a proper subgroup when G=1, because Gp=Φ(G) is proper in a nontrivial finite p-group. Set
K=H∩Gp,V=G/Gp,W=Gp/Gp2,A=HGp/Gp≤V.
(6)
All the displayed quotients are elementary abelian. The supplied onto homomorphism θ:V→W, xGp↦xpGp2, is therefore linear. Write v=dimV=d(G), w=dimW=d(Gp), r=dimA, and t=dimθ(A).
Since H/K embeds in V, Φ(H)≤K. Also
Φ(K)≤Φ(H),Φ(K)=Kp[K,K]≤(Gp)p[Gp,Gp]=Gp2.
(7)
Here (Gp)p=Gp2 is the iterated-power convention, valid for powerful groups. Hence the natural map K/Φ(K)→W is defined. The image under this map of the subspace Φ(H)/Φ(K) contains θ(A), because for every h∈H its power hp lies in Φ(H)∩K. It follows that
dimK/Φ(H)≤d(K)−t.
(8)
The exact sequence 1→K/Φ(H)→H/Φ(H)→H/K→1 consists of elementary abelian groups, and dimH/K=r. By induction inside Gp, d(K)≤d(Gp)=w. Therefore
d(H)=r+dimK/Φ(H)≤r+w−t=w+dimker(θ∣A​)≤w+dimkerθ=v.
(9)
This proves d(H)≤d(G), including subgroups H that are not themselves powerful.
The rank of a profinite group is rk(P)=sup{d(K):K≤P closed}, where d(K) is its least topological generator number. If P has finite rank then every closed subgroup, including the open subgroup H, has no larger rank. Conversely suppose H has rank r<∞. Its normal core C=⋂g∈P​Hg is an open normal subgroup: there are finitely many conjugates because H has finite index. It has rank at most r.
For every closed K≤P, the subgroup K∩C is normal in K and has at most r topological generators. The quotient K/(K∩C) embeds in the finite group P/C. A finite group of order at most M=[P:C] has at most ⌊log2​M⌋ generators: in a minimal sequential generating list every strict enlargement at least doubles the subgroup order. Lift such quotient generators and adjoin generators of K∩C. Their closed generated subgroup contains the group homomorphism kernel and maps onto the quotient, so equals K. Consequently
rk(P)≤r+⌊log2​[P:C]⌋<∞.​
(10)
Thus an open subgroup has finite rank exactly when the whole profinite group does.

For a pro-p group, the lower p-series is the descending sequence of closed characteristic subgroups
P1​(G)=G,Pi+1​(G)=Pi​(G)p[Pi​(G),G]​.​
(11)
The bar means closure of the subgroup generated by the displayed powers and group commutators. Throughout this question p is odd. For a finitely generated powerful pro-p group, group commutator collection gives Pi​(G)=Gpi−1, each Pi​ is powerful, and Φ(Pi​)=Pi+1​. All these subgroups are open. A uniform pro-p group is such a group with all subgroup indices [Pi​:Pi+1​] equal to [G:P2​]. We use the standard powerful-group torsion criterion: a finitely generated powerful pro-p group is uniform exactly when it is a torsion-free group. For a uniform group of generator number d, the indices are pd, and x↦xpa is a homeomorphism from G onto Gpa.
We record explicitly the collection facts used for the operations below. With Ub​=Gpb=Pb+1​, one has
[Ua​,Ub​]≤Ua+b+1​,x≡y(modUb​) ⟺ xpa≡ypa(modUa+b​).
(12)
The second statement is the power-congruence property: the pth-power map is an isomorphism on adjacent uniform layers, and collection gives (yu)p≡ypup(modUb+2​) for u∈Ub​. Thus the first nonzero layer of a difference moves down exactly one step under a pth power. Iterating gives the asserted equivalence and also justifies taking roots of congruences. These facts follow from powerful-group group commutator collection, using divisibility by p of (2p​) and the successive-layer power isomorphisms; they are the uniform-group structure results being used here.
The transported addition on a uniform pro-p group is
x+n​y=(xpnypn)p−n(n≥1).​
(13)
The product is in Un​ and has a unique pnth root in G. For the comparison at n=1 define x+0​y=xy. The numbered proofs below establish associativity, approximate commutativity and compatibility of these operations.
The intrinsic Lie algebra of a uniform pro-p group has underlying set G, zero equal to the group identity, additive inverse x−1, and operations
x+L​y=n→∞lim​(xpnypn)p−n,[x,y]L​=n→∞lim​[xpn,ypn]p−2n,λx=xλ (λ∈Zp​).​
(14)
Here the group commutator is [x,y]=x−1y−1xy. For λ∈Zp​, xλ is the limit of xmj​ for integers mj​→λ; the pro-p topology makes it well defined. The addition sequence is Cauchy by the compatibility proved below. The group commutator estimate puts [xpn,ypn] in U2n+1​, so its displayed root exists and lies in U1​. Collection of group commutators shows that successive bracket approximants differ in layers tending to the identity, giving the other limit. The group commutator identities give additivity of the limiting bracket, and the Hall-Witt identity gives its Jacobi identity. Approximate commutativity makes limiting addition commutative. The resulting lattice LG​ is a free module over Zp​ of rank d(G), and [LG​,LG​]≤pLG​.
A powerful Lie algebra over p-adic integers, in the convention relevant here, is a Lie algebra L whose underlying module is finite free over Zp​ and whose bracket satisfies [L,L]≤pL. For p=2 the condition is instead [L,L]≤4L. For odd p, define multiplication on its underlying set by the convergent Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula
x∗y=BCH(x,y)=x+y+21​[x,y]+121​([x,[x,y]]+[y,[y,x]])+⋯.
(15)
An iterated bracket of degree m lies in pm−1L. The BCH coefficient bound loses at most ⌊(m−1)/(p−1)⌋ powers of p, so all higher-degree terms are integral, and their valuations tend to infinity. This explains both convergence and why multiplying a lattice with small bracket stays inside the lattice. Formal associativity of BCH passes to the convergent series, the identity is 0, the inverse is −x, and every group power satisfies x∗m=mx. The quotients by paL are finite p-groups. Hence the resulting compact group is a finitely generated torsion-free group and a pro-p group, and its group commutator lies in pL. Thus (L,∗) is a uniform pro-p group, with Pi​(L,∗)=pi−1L and successive index prankZp​​L.
The standard uniform-group/Lie-lattice correspondence identifies these constructions as mutually inverse: L(L,∗)​≅L and (LG​,∗)≅G. To see why the inverse limits recover the lattice operations, the addition approximant for a BCH group is p−nBCH(pnx,pny): its linear term is x+y and every higher term tends to zero. Similarly its group commutator approximant has limiting term [x,y]. In the other direction, a local logarithm in the complete normed group algebra identifies the intrinsic operations with algebra addition and commutator and identifies group multiplication with BCH; the uniform power maps extend the identification from a sufficiently small power subgroup to all of G. Continuous group homomorphisms respect powers, unique roots and limits, while Lie algebra homomorphisms respect every BCH term, so the equivalence also preserves morphisms.

The objective is a continuous faithful representation of a uniform pro-p group G in some GLN​(Zp​). The proof proceeds from the intrinsic Lie lattice to a small matrix group, then extends the resulting representation to all of G. This last step matters: a representation defined only on an open subgroup does not yet establish linearity of the original group.
Let L=LG​ be the Lie algebra of a uniform pro-p group and put g=Qp​⊗Zp​​L. The uniform-group/Lie-lattice correspondence identifies the group law on L with BCH. The substantial finite-dimensional Lie-theoretic input is the Ado theorem: every finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a characteristic-zero field admits a faithful finite-dimensional representation. Apply it to obtain
ρ:g↪EndQp​​(V),ρ([x,y])=ρ(x)ρ(y)−ρ(y)ρ(x).
(24)
An Adjoint representation would not suffice here, since it kills the centre; Ado supplies injectivity even in central directions.
Choose a p-adic lattice Λ spanning V. Since L has finitely many lattice generators, their representing matrices have bounded denominators. There is therefore an integer a≥0 such that
ρ(paL)⊆pϵEndZp​​(Λ),ϵ={1,2,​p=2,p=2.​
(25)
On this matrix lattice the series expX=∑n≥0​Xn/n! and log(1+Y)=∑n≥1​(−1)n+1Yn/n converge and are inverse. For example vp​(n!)≤n/(p−1), so the valuations of the exponential terms tend to infinity with this choice of ϵ; the logarithm terms do as well. They take values in 1+pϵEnd(Λ) and in pϵEnd(Λ) respectively. The formal identity exp(BCH(X,Y))=expXexpY holds for the convergent series.
Under the correspondence, U=Gpa has Lie lattice paL and is an open characteristic subgroup. Define
η:U⟶GL(Λ),η(u)=exp(ρ(logG​u)),
(26)
where logG​ denotes the identification of U with paL. A Lie homomorphism preserves BCH, so the exponential identity proves η(uv)=η(u)η(v). If η(u)=I, the matrix logarithm gives ρ(logG​u)=0; faithfulness of ρ gives u=1. Thus η is a continuous faithful integral representation of U.
To extend it, take the module
W={f:G→Λ:f(ux)=η(u)f(x) for all u∈U, x∈G}.
(27)
Values on a transversal of U\G determine f, so W is a free module over Zp​ of rank [G:U]dimV. These functions are continuous, since on each open coset they are given by the continuous representation η. Define (Tg​f)(x)=f(xg). The equivariance condition is preserved, and Tg​Th​=Tgh​. In a transversal basis each Tg​ is a permutation of blocks followed by matrices from η(U), so it belongs to GL(W) and depends continuously on g.
Faithfulness can be checked directly. If g∈/U, right multiplication moves the coset U to a different coset, so Tg​ moves a function supported on one block and cannot be the identity. If g∈U and Tg​=1, evaluation at 1 gives f(g)=f(1) for every f, whereas equivariance gives f(g)=η(g)f(1). Since f(1) is arbitrary, η(g)=I, and hence g=1. Therefore
G↪GL[G:U]dimV​(Zp​).​
(28)
Compactness of G makes this continuous injection a homeomorphism onto its closed image. This proves the linearity of a uniform pro-p group for the full group, including its centre, and explains why taking a sufficiently small power lattice permits matrix integration before finite-index induction restores the whole group.

Write k=Fp​. The Nottingham group N is the set of formal series f(t)=t+∑i≥2​ai​ti over k, with ordinary composition f∘g as multiplication and the t-adic topology. The identity is t. A compositional inverse exists uniquely by solving its coefficients successively, since the linear coefficient is 1.
Equivalently N is the group of continuous k-automorphisms of k((t)) whose action on (t)/(t2) is the identity. Such an automorphism is determined by the image of t, which is t+O(t2). To identify this with ordinary series composition without reversing multiplication, send f to the automorphism h(t)↦h(f−1(t)). Its composite with the automorphism for g is the one for f∘g.
The depth filtration is
Nm​={f:f(t)≡t(modtm+1)},m≥1.
(29)
Each Nm​ is open and normal, and the coefficient of tm+1 identifies Nm​/Nm+1​ with (k,+). Thus [N:Nm​]=pm−1, and N is the inverse limit of its finite p-group truncations.
Here is a local-field proof that every finite p-group embeds in N. We first establish the realization step rather than assume the Nottingham embedding itself. Put F=k((t)) and Γ=Gal(Fsep/F), and let I⊴Γ be the inertia subgroup, the group homomorphism kernel of the action on the algebraic closure of the residue field. We use the standard additive normal basis theorem consequence Hj(Γ,Fsep)=0 for j>0. It follows by taking direct limits of the finite-Galois normal-basis modules, whose additive cohomology vanishes. The Artin-Schreier sequence
0⟶Fp​⟶Fsepz↦zp−z​Fsep⟶0
(30)
then gives
H1(Γ,Fp​)=F/{zp−z:z∈F},H2(Γ,Fp​)=0.
(31)
The classes t−m for positive m prime to p are linearly independent: a nonzero finite linear combination has a most negative valuation prime to p, whereas a negative valuation of zp−z is divisible by p. Thus there are infinitely many independent continuous characters Γ→Cp​.
Induct on the order of a finite p-group E. Choose a central subgroup C≅Cp​ and put Q=E/C. By induction there is an onto map ϕ:Γ→Q with ϕ(I)=Q, corresponding to a totally ramified Q-extension. The central extension 1→C→E→Q→1 is specified by a two-cocycle. Its pullback along ϕ is a coboundary because H2(Γ,Fp​)=0. Correcting a set-theoretic section by that coboundary gives a continuous homomorphism ρ0​:Γ→E lifting ϕ.
We must make the lift onto on inertia, not merely onto on the whole Galois group. Let A=I∩kerϕ. On A, the lift ρ0​ takes values in C. Characters vanishing on A form a finite-dimensional space: Γ/A has a finite subgroup I/A≅Q and procyclic quotient Γ/I≅Z, so a character is determined by finitely many values on generators of these two pieces. The infinite-dimensional character space therefore has infinitely many distinct restrictions to A. Choose a character χ:Γ→C whose restriction to A does not cancel ρ0​∣A​, and set ρ(γ)=χ(γ)ρ0​(γ). Centrality of C makes this another homomorphism lifting ϕ. Its image on A is nontrivial and hence all of C, while its image on I maps onto Q. Therefore ρ(I)=E. The fixed field of kerρ is the required totally ramified finite Galois extension K/F with group E. This proves the realization step for every finite p-group.
Since the residue field of K is still k, choosing a uniformizer u identifies K with k((u)). Indeed successive subtraction of its residue coefficient and division by u gives each element of its valuation ring a unique convergent expansion ∑i≥0​bi​ui with bi​∈k, and fractions give the Laurent expansions. Each element of E preserves the valuation and fixes k, so sends u to a1​u+a2​u2+⋯. The linear coefficient is a homomorphism E→k×. Since E is a p-group and ∣k×∣=p−1, that homomorphism is trivial. Thus the faithful Galois action lies in N(k), completing the embedding proof.
An infinite profinite group is hereditarily just infinite when every open subgroup is infinite and every nontrivial closed normal subgroup of each such open subgroup is open. We sketch the normal-subgroup argument, giving the depth calculation in odd characteristic explicitly. For series of depths r,s,
f=t+atr+1+⋯,g=t+bts+1+⋯⟹[f,g]=t+ab(r−s)tr+s+1+O(tr+s+2),
(32)
where [f,g]=f−1∘g−1∘f∘g. The coefficient follows by comparing f∘g and g∘f in degree r+s+1: their cross terms are ab(r+1) and ab(s+1).
Let H be open and 1=K⊴H closed. Choose m with Nm​≤H and choose f∈K of depth r. For every j≥m with j≡r(modp), commutation with t+btj+1 supplies an element of K of depth r+j, with any prescribed nonzero leading coefficient by varying b. Thus every sufficiently high layer except possibly depths congruent to 2r modulo p occurs in K.
When p is odd, choose one available depth u≥r+m with u≡2r,r(modp). Such a residue exists even for p=3. An element h∈K of that depth can be commuted with t+btk−u+1 for every sufficiently large missing depth k≡2r(modp). The new leading coefficient contains 2u−k≡2(u−r)=0, so those missing layers occur too. Hence K contains an element with any chosen coefficient in every depth k≥M, for some M.
Successive coefficient correction now proves NM​≤K: match the coefficient of an arbitrary target in depth M by an element of K, correct the error in depth M+1, and continue. The partial products converge t-adically to the target and remain in K, which is closed. Thus K is open. This proves hereditary just infiniteness for odd p.
For characteristic two, the leading coefficient alone misses even depths. The standard characteristic-two normal-closure collection lemma supplies the needed extra input: for every m≥1 and every nonidentity f∈N(F2​), the closed subgroup generated by the conjugates fg with g∈Nm​ contains NM​ for some M. Its proof retains the next coefficients in pairs of group commutators to fill the parity gaps, then uses the same successive coefficient correction. Apply this lemma to f∈K and Nm​≤H: every such conjugate lies in K, so K contains the tail and is open. This states the additional collection result used in the characteristic-two sketch; the odd-prime leading-term argument is not being applied there. Since every open subgroup contains a tail and is infinite, the Nottingham group is hereditarily just infinite for every prime p.

The first alternative in the supplied analytic-group theorem would give a faithful representation of N in some GLd​(Zp​) or GLd​(Fp​[[t]]). The embedding proved above supplies cyclic subgroups of order pa for arbitrarily large a. Such orders are impossible in either fixed matrix dimension.
In characteristic zero, a matrix A of order pa is diagonalizable over an algebraic closure, because its minimal polynomial divides the separable polynomial Xpa−1. At least one eigenvalue must be a primitive path root of unity; otherwise Apa−1=I. The cyclotomic polynomial Φpa​ is irreducible over Qp​, since Φpa​(1+X) satisfies the Eisenstein criterion at p. Consequently that eigenvalue has degree φ(pa)=pa−1(p−1) over Qp​, which must be at most the matrix dimension d. This bounds a.
In characteristic p, pass from the power-series ring to its fraction field. A matrix of p-power order satisfies Xpa−1=(X−1)pa, so it is unipotent. Writing A=I+B, nilpotence gives Bd=0. For pe≥d,
Ape=I+Bpe=I.
(33)
Thus its order is at most p⌈logp​d⌉, again a fixed bound. Therefore the linear alternative is impossible in both coefficient rings.

We next bound the closed derived series. For odd p, the depth calculation yields
[Nm​,Nm​]=N2m+1​.
(34)
For the inclusion from left to right, two series of depth at least m commute modulo N2m+1​: at depth 2m the only possible leading pair has equal depths and its coefficient vanishes. For the reverse inclusion, every depth k≥2m+1 can be written r+s=k with r,s≥m and r≡s(modp). Start with r=m,s=k−m; if their difference vanishes modulo p, replace them by r=m+1,s=k−m−1, which is allowed except at k=2m+1, where the original difference is already nonzero. The difference changes by 2, nonzero for odd p. Commutators thus supply every high leading coefficient, and closed successive coefficient correction fills the tail.
With N(0)=N1​, induction on n gives
N(n)=N2n+1−1​,logp​[N:N(n)]=2n+1−2.
(35)
For p=2 use the standard characteristic-two derived-series collection bound, explicitly stated as an additional result:
log2​[N:N(n)]≤Cκn,κ=21+17​​<3,
(36)
for a fixed constant C. One can take a fixed enlarged constant from the tail estimate N(n)⊇N⌈4κn⌉+1​. The more involved two-coefficient collection is necessary in characteristic two; the exact odd-prime tail formula is not asserted there.
In either case there are constants C′>0 and λ<3 such that logp​[N:N(n)]≤C′λn. If an analytic structure existed, its dimension d would be positive, since a zero-dimensional analytic pro-p group is discrete and compact, hence finite. The second alternative of the supplied theorem would require
logp​[N:N(n)]≥d3n−s
(37)
for all sufficiently large n, for some fixed s. Combining the bounds gives d3−s≤C′(λ/3)n, impossible as n→∞.
The root solution proved hereditary just infiniteness, so the supplied theorem applies. Part (i) excluded its linear alternative, and this calculation excludes its growth alternative. Hence the Nottingham group is not analytic over any pro-p ring.
