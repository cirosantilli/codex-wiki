A deduction from premises Γ in propositional logic is a finite sequence of formulas, each an allowed logical axiom instance, a member of Γ, or obtained from earlier formulas by an inference rule, such as modus ponens. For example a Hilbert system can use the schemas p→(q→p), [p→(q→r)]→[(p→q)→(p→r)], and (¬q→¬p)→(p→q), with modus ponens as its rule. Its last formula is the conclusion. A finite deduction uses only finitely many premises. The completeness theorem for propositional logic says Γ⊨v implies Γ⊢v; together with soundness, syntactic consistency is equivalent to existence of a satisfying valuation.
For compactness, if Γ had no model, completeness would give a deduction of a contradiction. That deduction uses a finite subset of Γ, which therefore also has no model. Contrapositively, if every finite subset has a model, so does Γ.
Fix the given valuation on Q and let Lv​ be its collection of true literals. Suppose {s}∪Lv​ were inconsistent. A finite subset of its literals, with conjunction ℓ∈L(Q), would satisfy s⊢¬ℓ by the deduction theorem and classical logic. But ¬ℓ∈U whereas the given valuation makes ℓ true, contradicting that it satisfies all of U. Hence {s}∪Lv​ is consistent.
If U∪{¬t} had a model, restrict that valuation to Q and apply the preceding consistency result. Completeness supplies an extension to P∪Q making s true and preserving those Q values. Combine its P values with the original model's Q∪R values. Disjointness of P,Q,R makes this a well-defined valuation satisfying s and ¬t, contradicting soundness of s⊢t. Thus U∪{¬t} is inconsistent.
