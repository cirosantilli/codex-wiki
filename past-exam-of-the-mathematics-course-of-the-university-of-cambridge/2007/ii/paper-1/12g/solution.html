Using the diameter normalization, define Hδd​(E)=inf{∑j​(diamUj​)d:E⊆⋃j​Uj​, diamUj​≤δ} and the Hausdorff measure Hd(E)=limδ↓0​Hδd​(E). The Hausdorff dimension is inf{d:Hd(E)=0}, equivalently sup{d:Hd(E)=∞}.
The Cantor set is the intersection of the sets left by successively removing open middle thirds from [0,1]. At level k it has 2k Cantor cylinders of diameter 3−k. For s=log2/log3, their total s-cost is 2k3−ks=1, so Hs(C)≤1.
The random series ξ=∑n≥1​Xn​3−n converges absolutely, and its ternary digits are all zero or two. Thus ξ∈C and its law is the Cantor Bernoulli measure. Each level-k cylinder has probability 2−k. Distinct such intervals have separation at least 3−k; a set U of smaller diameter meets at most one. If 3−(k+1)≤diamU<3−k, then
P(ξ∈U)≤2−k=2(3−(k+1))s≤2(diamU)s.

Sets of diameter zero have probability zero, since the measure has no measure atoms; diameters at least one give the bound trivially. For arbitrary nonmeasurable sets use outer probability. Any countable cover of C therefore satisfies 1≤∑j​P∗(ξ∈Uj​)≤2∑j​(diamUj​)s. Hence
21​≤Hs(C)≤1,dimH​C=log3log2​.​

The last implication follows by multiplying the s-costs by powers of the cover diameter: measures above s vanish and those below s are infinite.
