Replace φ by φ+ϵη, with η vanishing on the boundary, and differentiate the action at zero. Integrate the derivative of η by parts:
δS=∫[∂φ∂L​η+∂φ,a​∂L​∂a​η]d4x=∫[∂φ∂L​−∂a​∂φ,a​∂L​]ηd4x.
(105)
The coefficient of η is the functional derivative δS/δφ.
For the electromagnetic action, δFab​=∂a​δAb​−∂b​δAa​. Antisymmetry combines these terms and integration by parts gives
δS=∫[μ0−1​∂a​Fab−Jb]δAb​d4x.
(106)
Thus the inhomogeneous Maxwell equations are ∂a​Fab=μ0​Jb. The definition of F also gives the homogeneous equations ∂[a​Fbc]​=0, equivalently ∂a​Fab=0. These are the two four-vector Maxwell systems.
Unrestricted variation of the alternative component-gradient action instead gives □Ab=μ0​Jb. This is equivalent to the first Maxwell system only when supplemented by the Lorenz gauge ∂a​Aa=0, because
∂a​Fab=□Ab−∂b(∂a​Aa).
(107)
The alternative action is not by itself gauge-invariant; the additional condition must be supplied consistently, not silently omitted.
Expand the tensor square: FabFab​=2[∂bAa∂b​Aa​−∂bAa∂a​Ab​]. Hence
S−S=2μ0​1​∫∂bAa∂a​Ab​d4x.
(108)
Integrating this cross term by parts twice expresses its integral as a boundary term plus ∫(∂a​Aa)2d4x. Under Lorenz gauge it is therefore purely a boundary term. With fixed or vanishing boundary contributions, the two actions have the same variational equations once the additional gauge condition is included. This is the Lorenz-gauge reduction of the electromagnetic action.
