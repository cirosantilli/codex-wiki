For a Euclidean circle with centre c and radius r, two distinct finite points are inverse when they lie on the same ray from c and their distances from c have product r2. Points on the circle are fixed, and c and infinity are inverse to each other. In complex coordinates this uniquely defines inversion in a circle by
IΓ​(z)=c+z−cr2​.
(5)
Uniqueness follows from the ray and product conditions, including the specified centre–infinity pair; the formula also gives IΓ2​=id. A circle through infinity is a straight line in the complex plane; its inversion is reflection in that line, fixing infinity. For a line through c with direction angle θ, its formula is c+e2iθz−c​.
Thus every inversion is an anti-Möbius transformation, of the form (az+b)/(cz+d) with nonzero determinant. If J(z)=z, write such an inversion as M∘J with M a Möbius transformation. Since J∘M∘J is the Möbius transformation with conjugated coefficients, the product of two inversions is M1​∘(J∘M2​∘J), hence Möbius. Pairing consecutive inversions proves every composition of an even number of inversions is a Möbius transformation.
