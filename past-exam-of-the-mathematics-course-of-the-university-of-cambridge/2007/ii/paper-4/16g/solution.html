A binary relation R on a set is a well-founded relation if every nonempty subset has an R-minimal member, that is, a member with no predecessor inside that subset. Here write xRy when x∈f(y).
Suppose R is well-founded and fix any g:P(b)→b. Call a partial function h:D→b consistent if D is downward closed under R and h(y)=g({h(x):x∈f(y)}) for y∈D. Two consistent partial functions agree on their common domain: otherwise the set of points where they disagree has a minimal member, all of whose predecessors are in both domains and agree, so applying g gives agreement at that member as well, a contradiction. Thus the union of all consistent partial functions is itself a function h:D→b, on a downward-closed domain, and remains consistent. This union is a set, because every such graph is a subset of a×b.
If D=a, choose a minimal member y of a∖D. All predecessors of y lie in D, so extend h to y by the prescribed value g({h(x):x∈f(y)}). The enlarged domain remains downward closed and consistent, contradicting the union's maximality. Hence D=a. The same minimal-disagreement argument proves uniqueness. This establishes the required recursion directly, without invoking an unproved well-founded recursion theorem.
Conversely, if R is not well-founded, choose a nonempty S⊂a such that each y∈S has a predecessor in S. Let T be all points reachable from S by a finite chain of upward R steps, including S. Then
y∈T⟺f(y)∩T=∅.
(45)
For a point reached by a nonempty chain, its preceding point is in T; for a point of S, use its predecessor in S. The converse follows by appending the final step to a chain. Choose b={0,1} and g(B)=1 if 1∈B, zero otherwise. Both h0​≡0 and h1​=1T​ satisfy the required recursion, and they differ because S=∅. This contradicts recursiveness. Therefore f is recursive exactly when its predecessor relation is well-founded, the characterization of a recursive powerset mapping.
