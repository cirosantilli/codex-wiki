Use the riskless financial asset as numéraire, normalized to have initial value one and terminal value R>0. Write S0​∈Rs for the initial risky prices and S1​ for the finite, measurable terminal price random vector. Define the discounted gain vector X=S1​/R−S0​. A risky holding h financed by the cash holding −h⋅S0​ has zero initial cost and discounted terminal gain h⋅X. An arbitrage is such a portfolio with h⋅X≥0 almost surely and P(h⋅X>0)>0. Allowing nonpositive initial cost is equivalent: invest any initial surplus in the riskless financial asset to obtain a zero-cost arbitrage.
An equivalent martingale measure is a probability measure Q with exactly the same null events as the physical probability measure P, under which discounted asset prices are integrable and satisfy EQ​[S1​/R]=S0​. Equivalently, EQ​X=0. Such a measure rules out arbitrage: every zero-cost portfolio has mean discounted gain zero, whereas a nonnegative gain positive with positive P-probability is also positive with positive Q-probability and has strictly positive Q-expectation.
For the converse, the probability space need not be finite, and the payoffs need not be integrable under P. Here is a direct Gaussian-damped martingale density construction. Set
N={h∈Rs:h⋅X=0 almost surely},L=N⊥,F(h)=EP​e−∣X∣2−h⋅X(h∈L).
(1)
The set N is a linear subspace. Choosing a finite basis of N shows that X∈L almost surely: intersect the probability-one events on which each basis vector is orthogonal to X. If h∈L∖{0}, then h⋅X is not almost surely zero. Absence of arbitrage implies both P(h⋅X<0)>0 and P(h⋅X>0)>0, by applying the definition to h and −h.
All the analytical steps in minimizing F can be checked directly. On ∣h∣≤H the integrand is bounded by eH2/4, since −∣X∣2+H∣X∣≤H2/4. Its first two directional derivatives are bounded uniformly in X by constants depending only on H and the direction, because the functions rje−r2+(H+1)r are bounded for j=0,1,2. The mean-value bound therefore proves continuity of F, and the Taylor theorem remainder bound, integrated over P, proves
Dv​F(h)=−EP​[(v⋅X)e−∣X∣2−h⋅X],v∈L.
(2)
Thus differentiation under the integral does not require an unproved physical moment assumption.
To prove coercivity, suppose ∣hk​∣→∞ in L. A subsequence of hk​/∣hk​∣ converges to a unit vector v∈L. This elementary finite-dimensional compactness follows by successively taking convergent subsequences of the bounded coordinates. Since P(v⋅X<0)>0, some event A={v⋅X≤−ε, ∣X∣≤K} has positive probability: the negative-gain event is the countable union of such events with ε=1/j and integer K. Eventually (hk​/∣hk​∣)⋅X≤−ε/2 on A, and hence
F(hk​)≥P(A)e−K2e∣hk​∣ε/2⟶∞.
(3)
If coercivity failed, an unbounded sequence with bounded F would have just such a contradictory subsequence. Consequently a minimizing sequence for F is bounded and has a convergent subsequence; continuity makes its limit h∗​ a minimizer. If L={0}, simply take h∗​=0. In every case the directional derivatives vanish at h∗​. The vector EP​[Xe−∣X∣2−h∗​⋅X] lies in L and is orthogonal to L, so it is zero. Therefore
dPdQ​=EP​e−∣X∣2−h∗​⋅Xe−∣X∣2−h∗​⋅X​​
(4)
is a positive mean-one density defining an equivalent martingale measure. Integrability of X under Q follows from the same Gaussian bound with j=1. This establishes absence of arbitrage if and only if an equivalent martingale measure exists, without quoting a financial duality theorem or assuming a finite state space.
Now suppose the probability space is finite. Discard null states and enumerate its positive-probability atoms by 1,…,m. Every measurable terminal payoff is a vector in Rm. Let A be the m×(s+1) matrix whose columns are the discounted terminal payoffs of the traded financial assets, with first column 1, and let p=(1,S0​) be their initial price vector. A portfolio with holding vector z replicates the discounted payoff Az, and an equivalent martingale measure is exactly a vector q satisfying
qj​>0,ATq=p.
(5)
The first pricing equation includes ∑j​qj​=1. A complete market means imA=Rm. If q,q′ both price the financial assets, then (q−q′)⋅Az=0 for every z. Completeness allows Az to be each coordinate vector, so q=q′.
Conversely, if the financial market is incomplete, there is a nonzero vector v with ATv=0. To see this without invoking a duality theorem, perform row elimination on the homogeneous system: its number of pivots is rankA<m, leaving a free variable and hence a nonzero solution. The constant payoff column gives ∑j​vj​=0. Existence of an equivalent martingale measure was already proved, so choose one strictly positive vector q. For
0<ε<minvj​=0​∣vj​∣qj​​,
(6)
both q+εv and q−εv are distinct strictly positive probability vectors satisfying AT(q±εv)=p. Thus uniqueness fails. On a finite arbitrage-free market, completeness is equivalent to uniqueness of the equivalent martingale measure.

Let the bank account be Br​=Rr, where R>0 is the one-period riskless gross return. In the binomial market, Sr+1​ is either uSr​ or dSr​, with 0<d<1<u and both successors having positive physical probability. The information at time r consists of the first r moves. A trading strategy chooses its holdings using only this information. A self-financing portfolio funds rebalancing from its current value; there is no external contribution.
Absence of arbitrage requires d<R<u. If R≤d, borrowing to buy the stock yields a nonnegative gain, strictly positive on the up branch; if R≥u, shorting the stock and investing its price yields the reverse arbitrage. When d<R<u, define the risk-neutral probability in a binomial market
q=u−dR−d​∈(0,1),qu+(1−q)d=R.
(7)
Giving each successive up move conditional probability q defines an equivalent martingale measure Q, since every finite path has positive probability and EQ​[Sr+1​∣Fr​]=RSr​.
For a contingent claim with successor values Vu​,Vd​ at a node of current stock value s, solve the two replication equations. The number of shares and current cash value are
Δ=s(u−d)Vu​−Vd​​,C=R(u−d)uVd​−dVu​​.
(8)
Then Δus+RC=Vu​ and Δds+RC=Vd​, so the replicating strategy costs
V=Δs+C=R−1{qVu​+(1−q)Vd​}.
(9)
Hold C/Br​ bank-account units over that period. Starting with the terminal payoff and applying these equations backwards supplies an adapted self-financing strategy replicating every path-dependent contingent claim. This proves market completeness. A different claim price would permit an arbitrage by buying the cheaper claim or replica and selling the dearer one. Iterating the backward pricing relation gives the unique risk-neutral pricing formula
Vr​=R−(n−r)EQ​[H∣Fr​].
(10)
The physical up probability affects real-world frequencies but does not enter this replication price.
For a terminal payoff f(Sn​), write k=n−r. Its current price is the restriction to the available nodes of the function
gr​(s)=R−k∑j=0k​(jk​)qj(1−q)k−jf(sujdk−j),s>0.
(11)
If f is convex, each summand is convex in s and each weight is positive; summing their defining convexity inequalities proves that gr​ is convex. This provides an explicit convex extension between the actual stock nodes, so “convex on the nodes” is unambiguous. If f is strictly convex, each positive scaling preserves strict convexity, so gr​ is strictly convex as well.
For the replicating portfolio in a binomial market, the stock holding is
Δr​(s)=s(u−d)gr+1​(us)−gr+1​(ds)​.
(12)
For r+1<n, let F=gr+2​. The next down and up holdings are the secant slopes of F on the adjacent intervals with endpoints d2s,uds,u2s:
Δr+1​(ds)=ds(u−d)F(uds)−F(d2s)​,Δr+1​(us)=us(u−d)F(u2s)−F(uds)​.
(13)
For any convex function and a<b<c, its defining inequality at b=(1−t)a+tc rearranges to (F(b)−F(a))/(b−a)≤(F(c)−F(b))/(c−b). The inequality is strict for a strictly convex function. Applying this to the three stock values orders the two next holdings. Meanwhile the backward pricing relation gives, by subtracting its values at us and ds,
Δr​(s)=Rqu​Δr+1​(us)+R(1−q)d​Δr+1​(ds).
(14)
The coefficients are strictly positive and sum to one. Therefore the current share holding lies between the next two holdings:
Δr+1​(us)≥Δr​(s)≥Δr+1​(ds).​
(15)
Both inequalities are strict for a strictly convex payoff. For an affine payoff the share holding is constant, explaining the required weak interpretation. These are numbers of shares, not the fraction of portfolio wealth invested in the stock.

A useful finite-horizon form of the Girsanov theorem is as follows. Let W be a Brownian motion and η an adapted, progressively measurable process with ∫0T​ηs2​ds<∞ almost surely. Suppose its stochastic exponential
Zt​=exp(−∫0t​ηs​dWs​−21​∫0t​ηs2​ds)
(16)
is a true martingale with mean one. The Novikov condition Eexp(21​∫0T​ηs2​ds)<∞ is a sufficient condition. Under the equivalent probability measure dQ=ZT​dP, the process WtQ​=Wt​+∫0t​ηs​ds is a Brownian motion.
For the proof sketch, the Itô formula gives dZ=−ZηdW. Integration by parts cancels the drift in d(ZWQ), since Zηdt from dWQ cancels d[Z,WQ]=−Zηdt. After localization, the Bayes formula for conditional expectation makes WQ a continuous local martingale under Q. Its quadratic variation is still t, because adding a finite-variation process does not change quadratic variation. One can see the Brownian conclusion directly: for real z, the Itô formula makes exp(izWtQ​+z2t/2) a local martingale. Its modulus is bounded on the fixed horizon, so it is a true martingale. Thus
EQ​[eiz(WtQ​−WsQ​)∣Fs​]=e−z2(t−s)/2.
(17)
This is the conditional characteristic function of a centered normal distribution with variance t−s, independent of the past. Continuity and these independent normal increments give the defining properties of Brownian motion.
Now put ϕt​(x)=(2πt)−1/2e−x2/(2t) and Φ(z)=∫−∞z​ϕ1​(x)dx, the standard normal distribution function. At zero drift the endpoint density of paths staying below a is
P(Wt​∈dx, sups≤t​Ws​<a)={ϕt​(x)−ϕt​(2a−x)}dx,x<a.
(18)
Indeed, reflect the portion of a path after its first hitting time of a. By continuity and the independent symmetric Brownian motion increments after this stopping time, reflection preserves its probability law and maps a crossing path ending at x<a to a path ending at 2a−x>a. This subtracts exactly ϕt​(2a−x) from the unrestricted endpoint density; it is the Brownian reflection principle.
Taking the constant drift tilt dQ/dP=eμWt​−μ2t/2 in the Girsanov theorem makes W under Q have the same law as W+μs under P. Therefore the desired finite-horizon maximum of Brownian motion with drift has probability
∫−∞a​eμx−μ2t/2{ϕt​(x)−ϕt​(2a−x)}dx.
(19)
Completing squares gives eμx−μ2t/2ϕt​(x)=ϕt​(x−μt) and eμx−μ2t/2ϕt​(2a−x)=e2μaϕt​(x−2a−μt). Hence
P(s≤tsup​(Ws​+μs)≤a)=Φ(t​a−μt​)−e2μaΦ(t​−a−μt​).​
(20)
The maximum has no atom at a positive level, so strict and weak barrier inequalities agree. This also follows from continuity of the displayed distribution function. At μ=0 the formula reduces to 2Φ(a/t​)−1.
For the up-and-out power claim, write T=t0​>0 and use the risk-neutral measure for the Black-Scholes model, under which
St​=S0​exp(σWt​+(ρ−21​σ2)t),A=σlog(c/S0​)​,m0​=σρ−σ2/2​,
(21)
with σ>0. The barrier survives exactly when supt≤T​(Wt​+m0​t)<A. Its risk-neutral pricing value is
V0​=S02​e(ρ+σ2)TEQ​[e2σWT​−2σ2T1{supt≤T​(Wt​+m0​t)<A}​].
(22)
The factor inside the expectation is a mean-one Exponential martingale for Brownian motion. Under the probability tilted by it, Wt​−2σt is a Brownian motion, so the barrier process has drift m=m0​+2σ=(ρ+3σ2/2)/σ. Applying the preceding survival formula yields
V0​=S02​e(ρ+σ2)T[Φ(T​A−mT​)−e2mAΦ(T​−A−mT​)].​
(23)
As c→∞, the bracket tends to one and the price becomes the discounted second moment S02​e(ρ+σ2)T. As c↓S0​, it tends to zero. If σ=0, use the deterministic path instead: the price is S02​eρT when S0​max(1,eρT)<c, and zero otherwise. At T=0, with c>S0​, the price is simply S02​.

Write T=t0​ and b(t)=e−ρ(T−t) for the price of the unit zero-coupon bond maturing at T, so db=ρbdt. Let the stock satisfy dSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​ with σ>0. Assume the value function f(x,t)=xg(x,t)+b(t)h(x,t) is C2,1 and the holdings have enough smoothness for the calculations below. The self-financing portfolio condition is the gain identity
df(St​,t)=g(St​,t)dSt​+h(St​,t)db(t).
(24)
The Itô formula expresses the left side as
(ft​+μxfx​+21​σ2x2fxx​)dt+σxfx​dWt​.
(25)
Matching the diffusion terms gives fx​=g, or xgx​+bhx​=0. Differentiating fx​=g gives fxx​=gx​. Also ft​=xgt​+bht​+ρbh. Matching the remaining drift terms consequently gives the two necessary conditions
xgx​+bhx​=0,xgt​+bht​+21​σ2x2gx​=0.​
(26)
They are sufficient because substituting them into the Itô formula restores exactly the gain identity. Necessity first holds along the stock process; its positive lognormal density for every t>0, together with continuity of the coefficient functions, gives the displayed identities for all x>0 and 0<t<T, and continuity extends them to the endpoints.
Equivalently, the smooth value of any self-financing portfolio satisfies the Black-Scholes equation
ft​+ρxfx​+21​σ2x2fxx​−ρf=0.​
(27)
Indeed, the gain identity after imposing g=fx​ has drift μxfx​+ρ(f−xfx​), which gives this equation. Conversely, if f satisfies this equation, take
g(x,t)=fx​(x,t),h(x,t)=b(t)f(x,t)−xfx​(x,t)​.
(28)
The Itô formula then verifies that these holdings form a self-financing portfolio of value f. This converse asserts existence of the correct holdings. It does not certify an arbitrary decomposition of f into stock and bond holdings: for example, f=0, g=1, h=−x/b solves the value equation but fails g=fx​. The Black-Scholes value equation needs delta-compatible holdings precisely for this reason.
For a constant-proportion portfolio, the stock value must satisfy xg=γf. Since g=fx​, integration of xfx​=γf gives f(x,t)=C(t)xγ. Substituting into the Black-Scholes equation gives
C′(t)=(1−γ)(ρ+21​γσ2)C(t).
(29)
Thus, for initial portfolio wealth V0​ and stock price S0​, the complete explicit solution is
Vt​=V0​(S0​St​​)γe(1−γ)(ρ+γσ2/2)t,gt​=St​γVt​​,ht​=b(t)(1−γ)Vt​​.​
(30)
Here gt​ counts shares and ht​ counts maturity-T bonds; the initial cost is V0​. Directly, the self-financing gain equation is dVt​/Vt​=[ρ+γ(μ−ρ)]dt+γσdWt​, which also verifies the displayed value. Constant proportions concern the values of the holdings, not constant numbers of shares or bonds. The nondegenerate volatility assumption is necessary for the preceding diffusion matching; with σ=0, one only obtains a gain condition along the deterministic stock path.

Let Ps,t​>0 denote the time-s price of a zero-coupon bond paying one at maturity t, with Pt,t​=1. Information available at s is the sigma-algebra Fs​ in the market filtration. For a curve differentiable in maturity, the instantaneous forward rate and its diagonal short rate are
f(s,t)=−∂t​logPs,t​,Rs​=f(s,s),Ps,t​=exp(−∫st​f(s,u)du).
(48)
The last identity follows by integrating the maturity derivative and using Ps,s​=1. The continuous-time bank account reinvesting at the short rate is Bs​=exp(∫0s​Ru​du), and its reciprocal is the discount factor. Modelling forward rates means specifying a random maturity-indexed curve with an evolution consistent with the same pricing measure for every bond. The expectation below is under that money-market risk-neutral measure, not an unspecified physical measure.
For fixed maturity t, if Ps,t​/Bs​ is a true martingale on 0≤s≤t, its terminal value is 1/Bt​. Consequently
Bs​Ps,t​​=EQ​[Bt−1​∣Fs​],Ps,t​=EQ​[e−∫st​Ru​du∣Fs​].​
(49)
Conversely, this representation gives Ps,t​/Bs​=EQ​[Bt−1​∣Fs​], a conditional-expectation martingale by the tower property. For example, for r<s, conditioning the right side on Fr​ returns EQ​[Bt−1​∣Fr​]. These claims assume EQ​Bt−1​<∞. A positive discounted local martingale alone need not equal the conditional expectation of its terminal value, so the word “martingale” cannot be weakened without an extra integrability argument.
In the Vasicek model, the Brownian motion W and coefficients in dRs​=α(β−Rs​)ds+σdWs​ must be understood under this pricing measure. Multiplying by eαs and integrating gives, conditionally on Fs​,
Ru​=β+(Rs​−β)e−α(u−s)+σ∫su​e−α(u−v)dWv​,u≥s.
(50)
Assume first α=0, and put τ=t−s and B(τ)=(1−e−ατ)/α. Integrating the explicit solution over s≤u≤t yields
∫st​Ru​du=Rs​B(τ)+β{τ−B(τ)}+σ∫st​B(t−v)dWv​.
(51)
The interchange of the stochastic integrals is justified by their deterministic square-integrable kernels on the finite triangular domain; equivalently approximate the kernels by step functions, interchange finite sums, and pass to the limit using the Itô isometry. The last integral conditionally has a centered normal distribution independent of Fs​, with variance
σ2J(τ),J(τ)=∫0τ​B(v)2dv=α21​[τ−2B(τ)+2α1−e−2ατ​].
(52)
A deterministic Brownian integral is Gaussian because its step approximations are sums of independent normal increments; their variances converge by the Itô isometry. Completing the square in the normal density proves Ee−Y=e−m+v/2 for a normal variable of mean m and variance v. Hence the zero-coupon bond price is exponential-affine:
Ps,t​=eas,t​−bs,t​Rs​,bs,t​=B(τ),as,t​=−β{τ−B(τ)}+2σ2​J(τ).​
(53)
An equivalent familiar form is
as,t​=(β−2α2σ2​)(B(τ)−τ)−4ασ2​B(τ)2.
(54)
At t=s, both coefficients are zero and the bond value is one. These formulas are valid for negative as well as positive nonzero α on finite horizons, though a genuinely mean-reverting Vasicek model has α>0. For α=0, direct integration gives B(τ)=τ, J(τ)=τ3/3 and
bs,t​=τ,as,t​=σ2τ3/6.​
(55)
All the exponential moments used above are finite, so the prices defined by the conditional expectation do give true discounted martingales.
To fit an initial bond curve, let its observed prices p0​(t) be positive, satisfy p0​(0)=1, and have a sufficiently smooth instantaneous forward rate f0​(t)=−dlogp0​(t)/dt. Set R0​=f0​(0); this compatibility is forced by the infinitesimal-maturity bond price. Replace β by a deterministic function θ(t), with α=0, so that
dRt​=α{θ(t)−Rt​}dt+σdWt​.
(56)
Its mean m(t)=EQ​Rt​ satisfies m′=α(θ−m), m(0)=R0​. The variance of the integrated rate remains σ2J(t), since changing deterministic drift does not change the centered Gaussian process. Therefore
−logP0,t​=∫0t​m(u)du−2σ2​J(t),−∂t​logP0,t​=m(t)−2σ2​B(t)2.
(57)
The required mean is consequently m(t)=f0​(t)+σ2B(t)2/2. Solve its mean equation for θ:
θ(t)=m(t)+αm′(t)​=f0​(t)+αf0′​(t)​+2σ2​B(t)2+ασ2​B(t)e−αt.
(58)
Combining the last two terms gives the explicit Hull-White model calibration in the question's parameter convention:
θ(t)=f0​(t)+αf0′​(t)​+2α2σ2​(1−e−2αt).​
(59)
With R0​=f0​(0), the specified mean solves the same linear differential equation and initial condition as the model mean, so it is the actual mean. The resulting model forward curve equals f0​; integrating and using P0,0​=p0​(0)=1 proves P0,t​=p0​(t) for every maturity. If the drift is instead written θ(t)−αRt​, its calibration parameter is θ=αθ, not θ itself.
The printed allowance of arbitrary constants needs a qualification for this final claim. If α=0, replacing β by θ(t) changes no coefficient at all: Rt​=R0​+σWt​ regardless of θ. Its initial curve is fixed at P0,t​=exp(−R0​t+σ2t3/6). With σ=0, the compatible smooth observed curve p0​(t)=e−R0​t is already a counterexample to the unconditional fitting claim. Thus arbitrary compatible smooth positive initial curves can be fitted by the stated replacement when α=0; it cannot fit arbitrary curves when α=0. In that degenerate case one can instead introduce an independent deterministic drift k(t) in dRt​=k(t)dt+σdWt​, choosing k(t)=f0′​(t)+σ2t to recover the desired initial curve. Likewise an externally fixed R0​=f0​(0) is incompatible with exact fitting, and an arbitrary nonsmooth set of prices need not define the differentiable forward curve assumed in the model.
