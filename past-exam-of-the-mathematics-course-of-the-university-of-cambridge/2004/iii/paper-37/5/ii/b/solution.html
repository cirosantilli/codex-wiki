For a homogeneous continuous-time Markov chain, the holding time in a living state r before the next transition has survival probability e−λr​t, where λr​=∑s=r​qrs​=−qrr​. Indeed, conditional on still occupying that state, the probability of leaving in the next dt is λr​dt+o(dt), so its survival function satisfies S′(t)=−λr​S(t) and S(0)=1. Integrating gives the mean holding time from a transition intensity matrix:
τr​=∫0∞​e−λr​tdt=λr​1​=−qrr​1​.​

This is the mean time until any allowed exit from that state, not necessarily the patient's remaining lifetime.
For confidence limits, use the supplied standard error sr​ of the corresponding diagonal generator estimate. Changing its sign does not change its standard error, so it is also the standard error of λr​. The delta method, with derivative d(1/λ)/dλ=−1/λ2, gives
se(τr​)≈λr2​sr​​,τr​±1.96λr2​sr​​.

The diagonal standard errors already incorporate the covariance among outgoing rates. They should not be replaced by the square root of a sum of squared off-diagonal standard errors, which would silently assume those rate estimates independent.
Alternatively construct a positive rate confidence interval and apply the confidence interval for an inverse rate transformation. Here every normal rate interval has a positive lower endpoint, giving
[λr​+1.96sr​1​,λr​−1.96sr​1​].
(49)
These approximate intervals are, respectively, (10.863,12.830), (4.497,7.161), (2.397,3.117) and (1.548,2.631) years. They are asymmetric because inversion is nonlinear; positive log-rate or likelihood-based intervals could also be inverted. The symmetric delta intervals and the inverted-rate intervals are two legitimate first-order approaches and need not have numerically identical endpoints.
The death state has zero exit rate, so its holding time is infinite. There is no finite mean-sojourn estimate or ordinary finite confidence interval for this structurally absorbing state. All intervals above concern the mean parameter, not a prediction interval for one patient's realized holding time.
