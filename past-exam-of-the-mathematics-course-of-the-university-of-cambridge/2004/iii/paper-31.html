Write an​=n2(1−H) and bn​=n2(1−G). Then an​/bn​=n2(G−H)→∞. The large deviation principle on the whole space forces infI=0; compact sublevel sets and lower semicontinuity make that infimum attained. The unique possible zero is therefore mu, and I(μ)=0.
If a closed set C contains mu, its new infimum rate is zero and the claimed upper bound follows from probabilities being at most one. If it excludes mu, then η=infC​I>0, possibly infinite: otherwise a sequence of rates tending to zero would have a convergent subsequence in a compact sublevel set, with limit in C and zero rate, contradicting uniqueness. This is the fact that a good rate function is separated from zero away from its minimizer.
Choose any 0<c<η. The original upper bound gives logP(Xn​∈C)≤−can​ for all sufficiently large n. Thus
limsupn​bn−1​logP(Xn​∈C)≤limn​−cbn​an​​=−∞=−infC​I′.
(8)
Together these cases prove the closed-set upper bound at the slower speed, with rate zero only at mu. Its finite sublevels are the compact singleton {μ}, so it is a good rate function.

Let IX​,IY​ be the original rates and μX​,μY​ their unique zero points. Suppose first that H<G. At speed bn​=n2(1−G), the preceding argument gives X the rate zero at μX​ and infinity elsewhere, while Y retains IY​. Independence allows the product large-deviation principle, and addition is continuous, so the contraction principle for large deviations gives the sum rate
K(z)=infx+y=z​{IX′​(x)+IY​(y)}=IY​(z−μX​).
(10)
This is good, has unique zero μX​+μY​, and inherits a strictly positive finite value by translation from IY​. Thus the sum has Hurstiness G. The case G<H is identical with the roles exchanged.
If G=H, the same product and contraction results apply to the original rates at their common speed, giving their infimal convolution
K(z)=infx+y=z​{IX​(x)+IY​(y)}.
(11)
It is good. Every finite fiber infimum is attained: a minimizing sequence lies in a product of compact sublevel sets and has a subsequence converging in the closed addition fiber. Consequently its only zero is μX​+μY​. Choose x^ with 0<IX​(x^)<∞ and set z^=x^+μY​. Then z^ differs from the zero point and 0<K(z^)≤IX​(x^)<∞. The nondegeneracy requirement is therefore satisfied here too. In all cases,
Hurstiness(Xn​+Yn​)=max(H,G).​
(12)
The general results used are the independent product large-deviation theorem and the continuous contraction principle. Checking the zero set and a positive finite value is necessary in addition to obtaining an LDP.

Let ρ=λ/C<1 and measure payload in kilobytes. A unit-mean exponential payload requires an exponential service time of rate C. Headers remain until service is complete, so Q counts all unfinished packets, including the packet in service. Its process is an M/M/1 queue with birth rate lambda and death rate C whenever nonempty.
The detailed balance equations give πk+1​=ρπk​, and normalization gives πk​=(1−ρ)ρk. In the stationary infinite-space approximation,
P(Q≥q)=ρq,q=0,1,2,….​
(13)
For a noninteger positive threshold replace q by its ceiling. At capacity 1000 the requested tail estimate is ρ1000; literal exceedance of that capacity uses the adjacent integer threshold 1001. We compare the stated capacity-tail proxies consistently below.

Let U be the total payload arriving in one slot. Conditional on its Poisson packet count, use the exponential moment of each independent payload to get, for θ<1,
EeθU=exp{λδ(1−θ1​−1)}.
(14)
For net work Z=U−Cδ, its cumulant-generating function is
logEeθZ=δ(1−θλθ​−Cθ).
(15)
The mean net work is δ(λ−C)<0. Under the arrivals-before-service slot convention the Lindley recursion gives stationary queue workload Rδ​=dsupm≥0​∑j=1m​Zj​. The positive root of the exponential-moment equation is θ∗​=1−ρ. Thus exp(θ∗​∑j=1m​Zj​) is a nonnegative martingale. Stop it at the first crossing of r or a finite horizon; its value on the crossing event is at least eθ∗​r. Increasing the horizon proves
P(Rδ​≥r)≤e−(1−ρ)r,r>0.
(16)
This is an infinite-horizon bound, not a one-slot Chernoff estimate.
A useful refinement identifies both the exponential rate and the small-slot prefactor. Couple the random walk to the continuous compound Poisson input U(t), and write W=supt≥0​(U(t)−Ct) and Wδ​=supk≥0​(U(kδ)−Ckδ). Their laws are the continuous and slot-boundary stationary queue workloads. Monotonicity of U implies the grid-sampled compound Poisson workload bound: Wδ​≤W≤Wδ​+Cδ, by rounding each t up to the next grid point.
The continuous queue workload law can be obtained directly from the stationary packet count. Conditional on Q=k, the k remaining payloads are independent unit-mean exponentials: unserved payloads have not affected earlier service completions, and the in-service residual uses the memoryless property. Hence
Ee−sW=∑k≥0​(1−ρ)ρk(1+s)−k=1+s−ρ(1−ρ)(1+s)​=(1−ρ)+ρ1−ρ+s1−ρ​.
(17)
Thus W has mass 1−ρ at zero, and its positive part is exponential with rate 1−ρ. Combining this stationary workload of an exponential-payload queue with the grid bound gives the explicit estimate
ρe−(1−ρ)(r+Cδ)≤P(Rδ​≥r)≤ρe−(1−ρ)r,r>0.​
(18)
In particular limr→∞​r−1logP(Rδ​≥r)=−(1−ρ), and for small delta the estimate is ρe−(1−ρ)r. The exact continuous-time tail is that latter expression. Another slot convention may shift queue workload by the within-slot input/service timing; its convention must be specified before asserting a prefactor, while this exponential tail rate is unchanged.

At a common large threshold b, the header tail has exponent −logρ, whereas the payload tail has exponent 1−ρ. Since −logρ>1−ρ for 0<ρ<1, the payload queue has the heavier tail and dominates overflow on the large-buffer exponential scale at fixed load. The inequality follows from the strict convexity of minus the logarithm, or from integrating 1/u>1 between rho and one.
The explanation is that a header always consumes one slot, while a payload can be unusually large. A rare accumulation of work can therefore overflow the byte buffer without accumulating as many as 1000 packets. Replacing every payload by its mean would lose precisely this source of variability.
For the actual capacity 1000, retaining the exact continuous-time prefactors gives a qualification that a tail-exponent comparison alone cannot detect. The two infinite-buffer proxies are pH​=ρ1000 and pP​=ρe−1000(1−ρ), and
logpH​pP​​=999(−logρ)−1000(1−ρ).​
(19)
Thus payload overflow is more likely under this proxy exactly when the displayed expression is positive. For ordinary loads it is positive; for example it is positive at ρ=0.9. At ρ=0.999 it is approximately −0.00050017, so the header proxy is slightly larger. Consequently, without a numerical load, there is no universal exact ordering for the fixed capacity, although the large-buffer exponential ordering is unambiguous. Very near full utilization both 1000-unit buffers have substantial tail probabilities, so equal numerical capacities alone do not establish that the design is sensible. These are the requested infinite-space estimates, rather than an exact loss model for the interacting finite buffers.

Use the stated continuity of the infinite-buffer and finite-buffer queue workload maps on the input path space. The contraction principle for large deviations gives, at the same speed as the input sequence,
J(x)=inf{I(a):q(a)=x},Jˉ(x)=inf{I(a):qˉ​(a)=x}.​
(20)
Empty fibers have infimum infinity. Both rates are good because they are continuous contractions of the good rate function I. In particular, at speed L the infinite-buffer queue workload satisfies, for every Borel set E,
−infE∘​J≤liminfL​L−1logP(q(AL)∈E)≤limsupL​L−1logP(q(AL)∈E)≤−infE​J,
(21)
and the identical bounds with bars give the finite-buffer queue workload LDP. This states the entire principles, not just an informal tail approximation.

Put g=Λ∗ and γ=infv>C​g(v)/(v−C), allowing gamma to be infinity if no rate above C has finite cost. We prove the linear workload rate for a local convex action, which in particular proves monotonicity on the physical domain [0,∞).
For an absolutely continuous finite-cost path and any interval of length t with positive net work z(t), its average arrival rate is C+z(t)/t. Nonnegativity of g and Jensen inequality give
I(a)≥∫−t0​g(a˙s​)ds≥tg(C+tz(t)​)≥γz(t).
(23)
If q(a)=x>0, choose intervals with z(t) approaching its supremum x, obtaining I(a)≥γx. If gamma is infinite, the existence of any positive net interval instead forces infinite cost, giving the same extended-value conclusion.
Conversely, choose any v>C of finite cost, use arrival rate v during the last t=x/(v−C) time units and the typical rate mu before that. This constant-rate burst path has queue workload exactly x: the lookback net input increases to x over the burst and then decreases with slope μ−C<0. Its action is tg(v)=xg(v)/(v−C). Taking the infimum over v proves the matching upper bound. The typical-rate path has zero queue workload and zero cost. Therefore
J(0)=0,J(x)=γx (x>0),J(x)=∞ (x<0).​
(24)
Strict convexity and g(μ)=0 ensure γ>0 whenever it is finite. Indeed, choose a finite-cost v>C and some u∈(μ,C); then g(u)>0, and convexity gives g(v)/(v−C)≥g(u)/(u−μ) for every finite-cost v>C. Thus J is increasing on nonnegative queue workloads, and the preceding fiber bound yields Jˉ(x)≥J(x) for x≥0​. Negative values are impossible for both queue workloads and have infinite rate. No monotonicity is asserted across that impossible negative domain.
