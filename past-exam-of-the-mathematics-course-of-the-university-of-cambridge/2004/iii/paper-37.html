Let X be the n×p design matrix with row xiT​, let y=(yi​), and write μi​(β)=exp(xiT​β). Independence gives the Poisson regression likelihood function
L(β)=∏i=1n​yi​!e−μi​μiyi​​​,ℓ(β)=∑i​{yi​xiT​β−exiT​β−log(yi​!)}.
(1)
The score function and negative Hessian matrix are
U(β)=XT(y−μ),I(β)=−∇2ℓ(β)=XTWX,W=diag(μi​).​
(2)
Because EYi​=μi​, the score has mean zero; independence and the Poisson distribution variance give VarU=XTWX. Thus the observed and expected Fisher information matrices are the same here.
Assume X has full column matrix rank p. For a nonzero vector a, aTIa=∑i​μi​(xiT​a)2>0. The concavity of the Poisson regression likelihood therefore makes any finite solution of XT(y−μ​)=0 the unique maximum-likelihood estimator. If the design is rank deficient, parameters differing by a vector in kerX give the same distribution, so only identifiable combinations can be estimated. Full rank alone does not ensure a finite maximizer: for an intercept-only model with all observations zero, ℓ=−neβ approaches its supremum as β→−∞. More generally, directions that decrease only fitted means attached to zero counts can produce boundary fits. A useful sufficient case for a finite fit is full rank with every count positive: each summand yi​ηi​−eηi​ tends to −∞ in either tail, and some ∣ηi​∣ diverges whenever ∥β∥ diverges.
For an interior fit, Newton's method and Fisher scoring give the same update:
β(k+1)=β(k)+[XTW(k)X]−1XT(y−μ(k)).
(3)
This is also iteratively reweighted least squares. Define the working response zi​=ηi​+(yi​−μi​)/μi​, with η=Xβ(k), and regress z on X using weights W(k):
β(k+1)=(XTW(k)X)−1XTW(k)z.
(4)
A numerical implementation solves these linear equations rather than explicitly forming the inverse, checks convergence of the score and likelihood, and can shorten a step when necessary.
Under the usual fixed-design regularity conditions, with growing information and no single observation dominating it, a score Taylor expansion about the true parameter gives
β​−β0​≈I(β0​)−1U(β0​),β​ ∼˙ Np​(β0​,I(β0​)−1).​
(5)
Estimate the covariance matrix by [XTdiag(μ​i​)X]−1; its diagonal square roots are the coefficient standard errors. This justifies large-sample Wald confidence intervals and Wald tests for specified coefficients or contrasts. Finite-sample unbiasedness is not being asserted. A likelihood-ratio test compares nested fits through twice their maximized log-likelihood difference. The Poisson deviance against the saturated fit is
D=2∑i​{yi​logμ​i​yi​​−(yi​−μ​i​)},
(6)
with 0log0=0. Residual diagnostics and a check of overdispersion assess whether the assumed Poisson variance is adequate; the variance correction required when it is not is derived in the next part.

The first output uses the equal-variance, pooled Student's t-test, as shown by its seven statistical degrees of freedom. Its exact model assumes two independent samples from normal distributions, with means μX​,μY​ and a common unknown variance σ2. The hypotheses are H0​:μX​=μY​ against H1​:μX​<μY​. The sample means are xˉ=4.35 and yˉ​=9.88, and the unbiased within-sample sample variances are
sX2​=4.356667,sY2​=8.822.
(22)
The pooled sample variance and its resulting standard error are
sp2​=73sX2​+4sY2​​=6.908286,se(Xˉ−Yˉ)=sp​41​+51​​=1.763159.
(23)
Hence
T=1.7631594.35−9.88​=−3.136416.​
(24)
To justify its reference distribution, under the null (Xˉ−Yˉ)/(σ1/4+1/5​) is standard normal, while (3sX2​+4sY2​)/σ2 is χ72​, independently of the two sample means. Dividing the first variable by the square root of the second divided by seven gives the exact pooled two-sample t statistic, with Student t-distribution t7​. Its one-sided p-value is the lower tail
Pr{t7​≤−3.136416}=0.0082307.​
(25)
The associated one-sided 95% confidence interval for μX​−μY​ is
(−∞,xˉ−yˉ​+t7,0.95​sp​1/4+1/5​)=(−∞,−2.189557).​
(26)
The printed missing lower bound means that this is a one-sided upper confidence limit, not that a finite lower endpoint should be estimated. With such small samples the normality and common-variance assumptions matter; a Welch analysis would use a different variance estimate and generally different degrees of freedom.
The second output is the Wilcoxon rank sum test for two independent continuous samples. Under a common-distribution null hypothesis, labels are exchangeable conditional on the pooled values, so the four ranks assigned to the first sample form a uniformly chosen four-element subset of {1,…,9}. The first sample receives ranks 2,1,3,5, giving raw rank sum
WX​=2+1+3+5=11.​
(27)
Equivalently the Mann–Whitney U test statistic is UX​=WX​−4⋅5/2=1, the number of cross-sample pairs with an X observation larger than a Y observation. This output reports the raw rank sum, rather than the shifted statistic used by some software.
A first sample tending to be smaller has a small rank sum. Under the null, all (49​)=126 rank allocations have equal probability. Only {1,2,3,4} and {1,2,3,5} have rank sum at most 11. By the exact rank-sum tail one above the minimum, the exact lower-tail p-value is
Pr(WX​≤11)=1262​=0.0158730.​
(28)
No large-sample normal approximation or continuity correction is involved; the absence of ties makes this simple enumeration valid. For a common-shape location-shift family, the test's alternative is a negative location shift of X relative to Y. Without that model, the null is equality of distributions and the rank comparison is not simply a test of equality of means or medians.
Both tests reject at the 5% level in the direction of smaller X observations; only the pooled t-test rejects at 1%. The t-test uses the numerical spacing and targets a mean difference under its normal model. The rank-sum test uses ordering and an exact exchangeability calculation, avoiding the normality and common-normal-variance assumptions. Their different p-values therefore reflect both different statistics and different modelling assumptions, not a numerical contradiction.

The diagonal standard errors already incorporate the covariance among outgoing rates. They should not be replaced by the square root of a sum of squared off-diagonal standard errors, which would silently assume those rate estimates independent.
Alternatively construct a positive rate confidence interval and apply the confidence interval for an inverse rate transformation. Here every normal rate interval has a positive lower endpoint, giving

The symmetric delta intervals and the inverted-rate intervals are two legitimate first-order approaches and need not have numerically identical endpoints.
The death state has zero exit rate, so its holding time is infinite. There is no finite mean-sojourn estimate or ordinary finite confidence interval for this structurally absorbing state. All intervals above concern the mean parameter, not a prediction interval for one patient's realized holding time.

The transition matrix over five years is P(5)=e5Q. Its first row conditions on starting in state 1. The displayed approximate probabilities mean that, five years later, about 65% remain disease-free, 6% have local recurrence only, 10% have distant metastasis only, 2% have both, and 16% have died. They are probabilities of the states occupied at five years; they are not the probabilities of the next jump destinations or annual transition intensities. In particular the positive probability of state 4 does not require a direct 1→4 arrow: the paths 1→2→4 and 1→3→4 contribute to it. The probability of death includes deaths after any allowed sequence of intermediate states.
The printed row sums to 0.99 rather than one because the entries are approximate; an exact stochastic transition row sums to one. Exponentiating the displayed three-decimal generator gives approximately (0.65377,0.06490,0.09775,0.02532,0.15826), illustrating that the edited, low-precision table should not be treated as an exact matrix exponential of its separately rounded rates.
State 5 is an absorbing state, so once entered it can never be left. Its generator row is zero, and the last row of every positive-time transition matrix is therefore
(0,0,0,0,1).​
