The emission-density term is the intrinsic temperature fluctuation: blackbody radiation has δργ​/ργ​=4ΔT/T, hence the factor 1/4. Adiabatic compression and rarefaction of the photon-baryon fluid create coherent density extrema at last scattering. Their projection produces acoustic peaks, with angular scale set by the sound horizon divided by the distance to last scattering. In a familiar nearly flat cosmology the first prominent temperature peak is around degree scales, ℓ of order a few hundred; precise positions depend on the background.
The velocity term is a Doppler effect from the emitting plasma's line-of-sight motion. The acoustic velocity is approximately a quarter-cycle out of phase with the density oscillation. Its projected contribution is strongest on acoustic and smaller angular scales and interleaves with density extrema, with projection broadening its features. Both sources are suppressed on sufficiently small scales by Silk damping and the finite thickness of last scattering.
The metric tensor integral describes photon-energy shifts during propagation through the changing perturbed geometry. In synchronous coordinates its split from the intrinsic endpoint term is gauge dependent. Rewriting the observable in Newtonian variables produces the usual emission Sachs-Wolfe effect and the Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect from evolving potentials, with the appropriate endpoint terms. The ordinary large-scale Sachs-Wolfe combination gives a low-multipole plateau for nearly scale-invariant initial curvature power. Late-time evolving potentials chiefly affect large angles, often ℓ≲20; evolution near radiation-matter equality also drives an early integrated contribution near the first acoustic peak. Static matter-era potentials do not produce that Newtonian integrated contribution.
All emission quantities are evaluated at the last-scattering endpoint and the metric tensor source along the unperturbed photon ray, not at a single fixed spatial point throughout the integral. Observer monopole/dipole conventions do not change this physical interpretation.
The sketch uses arbitrary units and representative scales. It displays source auto-power shapes, not measured data or an assertion that the total power is their simple sum. The terms are correlated; their cross-power contributions and the gauge-consistent endpoint combination are essential in an actual angular-spectrum calculation.

For the printed spacetime, yes. The surfaces T=constant, equivalently t=constant, are Cauchy surfaces in Minkowski spacetime. Every inextendible causal curve intersects each once; if its monotone time coordinate had a finite endpoint, the bound ∣dx/dT∣≤1 would give a finite spatial endpoint too, allowing continuation. Causal diamonds are closed and bounded in (T,x) and hence compact. Therefore the printed spacetime is globally hyperbolic.
Initial data on a Cauchy slice determine unique causal evolution for the usual well-posed hyperbolic field equations, without boundary data at spatial infinity at finite time. The time reparametrization preserves that property.
For the qualified AdS interpretation, no. On the universal cover, choose two central events with enough time separation for a null ray to approach a strip edge and return. Their causal diamond contains a sequence with ρ→π/2 but no limit in the physical spacetime, so it is noncompact. A constant-t surface fails to meet every inextendible causal curve arriving from timelike conformal infinity. Thus even the cover is not globally hyperbolic. Field evolution needs appropriate boundary conditions, such as a reflecting condition compatible with the field theory, as well as initial data. If AdS time itself is periodically identified, closed timelike curves give an additional and more immediate obstruction. These are consequences of the alternative spatial-lapse geometry, not of the printed flat metric tensor.

At distances and elapsed times much smaller than R, propagation probes the local covering space and resolves the compact direction. Formally R−1∑n​ becomes ∫dkw​/(2π) when many nearby compact momenta participate, giving the massless five-dimensional retarded Green function. Equivalently, the compact Green function is a sum of covering-space images at Δw+jR. Before a signal can travel to any nonzero winding image, only the direct image contributes. Short-scale physics is five-dimensional.
At low frequency and long wavelength, ∣ω∣,∣k∣≪2π/R, massive modes cannot be excited as propagating particles, and the massless zero mode dominates at long noncompact range. Then G≃R−1G(4,0), with higher-mode corrections and the corresponding effective source normalization. Low-energy long-scale physics is four-dimensional.
A precise static illustration avoids ambiguities of pointlike retarded signals. At equal compact positions and zero frequency, the Yukawa potential sum is
Gstat​(r,0)=4πRr1​[1+2∑n=1∞​e−2πnr/R]=4πRr1​coth(Rπr​).
(61)
This static Green function across a circle compactification has limits
Gstat​∼4π2r21​(r≪R),Gstat​∼4πRr1​(r≫R).​
(62)
They are the Coulomb kernels in four and three spatial dimensions respectively, with exponentially small massive corrections at large r.
For an unsmeared point-source retarded distribution, coordinate separations alone are not enough to impose a low-energy approximation. A high-frequency pulse can still excite massive modes and resolve winding signals at arbitrarily long travel distance/time; its image fronts satisfy Δt2=r2+j2R2. Thus large separation does not impose a Kaluza-Klein low-energy limit. The usual large-separation four-dimensional statement means low-energy sources or coarse-grained measurements, or a static long-distance limit. It is not a universal pointwise limit erasing all massive oscillations from a sharply resolved retarded pulse.
