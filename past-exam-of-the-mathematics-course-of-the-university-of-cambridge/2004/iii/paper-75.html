A characteristic curve emitted at time s carries constant u=Usinωs and h=h(0,s) and follows
x=v(s)(t−s),v(s)=c0​+23​Usin(ωs).
(5)
Increasing boundary velocity produces faster following characteristic curves, steepening the advancing side of a crest; decreasing boundary velocity spreads the curves and produces a rarefaction wave. A trough has reduced depth and smaller propagation speed. Once the compressive curves intersect, the single-valued simple wave must be replaced by an admissible hydraulic bore, with mass conservation and momentum conservation across it and loss of mechanical energy. In a very weak or dispersive wavetrain an undular bore can replace the ideal discontinuity.
For a bore of speed sb​ entering undisturbed depth H, the Rankine-Hugoniot condition gives sb​[h]=[hu] and sb​[hu]=[hu2+gh2/2]. Vertical turbulence carries particles upward as well as downward. Its mixing time h/u′ should be short relative to the settling time h/Vs​; hence u′≫Vs​ is a useful well-mixed criterion, subject also to the eddy correlation time and particle response time. A more resolved description balances turbulent diffusion against downward settling velocity. A nearly uniform interior concentration does not eliminate the downward drift.
At an absorbing bed, particles crossing the boundary settle and do not return unless sediment resuspension is separately supplied. With well-mixed concentration ϕ, the particle volume deposition flux is Vs​ϕ per unit horizontal area. Thus stirring can preserve an almost uniform suspension while its total particle content decreases. The present model neglects sediment resuspension and any significant change of bulk fluid volume due to the dilute particles.

Here the speed is measured relative to the resting deep ambient, and hf​,bf​ are the depth and reduced gravity immediately behind the head. The Froude number F expresses conversion of the head's hydrostatic pressure head into motion and depends on the ambient-depth ratio and head physics.

In stable density stratification, ambient density decreases with height. A rising buoyant thermal encounters progressively lighter surroundings and entrains denser fluid from below, so its net buoyancy decreases. It reaches neutral buoyancy, overshoots through its remaining momentum, and eventually stops rising, spreads as a buoyant intrusion or excites internal gravity waves. The finite rise is a consequence of this restoring effect, not just drag.

W(z)=ikUη0​sin(mH)sin[m(H−z)]​​.
(44)

A reflecting lid reverses the vertical energy direction while retaining horizontal wavenumber and laboratory frequency. In the terrain frame the incident and reflected energy rays therefore travel downstream while zigzagging between the boundaries. The velocity field has nodes at the lid and a vertically standing pattern; the sketch marks this ray geometry, rather than falsely drawing the reflected ray as a specular optical ray.
The intrinsic group velocity makes an angle θ with vertical satisfying tanθ=∣cgxfluid​∣/cgz​=m/k. Consequently the denominator vanishes when

(46)

The Batchelor entrainment hypothesis assumes that the mean ambient inflow normal to the edge of a turbulent turbulent plume is proportional to its representative axial velocity, ve​=αw. With top-hat plume model fields and circumference 2πb, the added volume per height is 2πbαw=2παM​. It is an integral turbulent mixing closure; it does not assert that every instantaneous eddy has the same inward speed.
