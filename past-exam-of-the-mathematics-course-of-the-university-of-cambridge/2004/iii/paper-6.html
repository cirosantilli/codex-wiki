We first prove the stronger compact-square form, in which all the one-variable functions are continuous. There are five fixed pairs of continuous functions aq​,bq​ on the unit interval such that every real f∈C([0,1]2) has a representation
f(x,y)=q=1∑5​Gq​(aq​(x)+bq​(y)),​
(1)
with continuous one-variable Gq​. We construct the inner functions and then prove convergence of the outer functions, rather than assume the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem.
Constructing separating inner functions. Work in the Banach space X=C([0,1])10 with the maximum of the ten supremum norms. For each positive integer n, let Un​ consist of tuples (aq​,bq​)q=15​ for which there are, for each q, finite families of closed intervals Iqi​ and Jqj​ of length less than 1/n, satisfying two conditions. Each point of the unit interval belongs to at least four of the five unions ⋃i​Iqi​, and likewise for the J families. Within each fixed q, the compact sets
Kqij​=aq​(Iqi​)+bq​(Jqj​)
(2)
are pairwise disjoint for distinct pairs (i,j). Each Kqij​ is a closed interval, by continuity and the intermediate value theorem. These are grid-separated additive coordinates.
The set Un​ is open: for a tuple and its finitely many witnessing intervals, the distinct compact image intervals have a positive minimum separation. A sufficiently small uniform change in the inner functions preserves that separation, while the interval-cover conditions do not change.
To prove density, start with any ten continuous functions and any positive approximation tolerance. Choose a common fine partition so that each original function oscillates by less than a small fraction of the tolerance on a partition cell and its immediate neighbours; choose its mesh smaller than 1/(2n). Near every internal division point put five small, pairwise disjoint open gaps, one for each q. Use these gaps to divide the interval into closed towns for family q, including the two endpoints in towns. Gaps from different families are disjoint, so any point misses at most one family; every town has length less than 1/n if the gaps are sufficiently small. Do this for both coordinates.
Approximate aq​ and bq​ by functions which are constant on their respective towns, and interpolate linearly across the gaps. The towns lie near single partition cells, so uniform continuity makes these approximations as close to the original functions as desired. For each q, perturb the finitely many town values αqi​,βqj​ arbitrarily slightly so that all sums αqi​+βqj​ are distinct. This is possible because each unwanted equality between distinct pairs is a proper affine hyperplane in the finite-dimensional vector space of values; finitely many such hyperplanes cannot fill any open ball. Interpolating the perturbed values still approximates the original functions, and the rectangle images are now distinct singleton sets. Thus Un​ is dense.
By the Baire category theorem, choose one tuple in ⋂n≥1​Un​. To recall why completeness suffices here, start inside any open ball and successively choose closed balls of positive radii tending to zero, each contained in the preceding ball and in the next dense open Un​. Their centers form a Cauchy sequence, and its limit belongs to every ball, hence every Un​. Fix this tuple once and for all: it does not depend on f.
A quantitative approximation step. Let a continuous residual r have supremum norm M>0. Choose n large enough that the oscillation of r on any rectangle of side lengths below 1/n is at most M/20. For each rectangle Iqi​×Jqj​ choose a sample point (xqi​,yqj​). Define a one-variable hq​ to have constant value r(xqi​,yqj​)/3 on Kqij​. The image intervals are disjoint, so interpolate linearly in their gaps and extend constantly beyond the outermost ones. Then hq​ is continuous on the real line and
∥hq​∥∞​≤M/3.
(3)
At a given (x,y), at most one family fails to contain x in an I town and at most one fails to contain y in a J town. Thus the point belongs to rectangles in k families, where 3≤k≤5. For each of these families the summand differs from r(x,y)/3 by at most M/60. Each remaining summand has absolute value at most M/3. Consequently
​r(x,y)−q=1∑5​hq​(aq​(x)+bq​(y))​​≤(3k−3​+35−k​)M+60kM​≤32​M+121​M=43​M.​
(4)
This proves a strict contraction uniformly over the square, including points in gaps.
Summing the corrections. Set r0​=f and apply that step repeatedly, subtracting the five summands at each stage. The residuals satisfy ∥rm​∥∞​≤(3/4)m∥f∥∞​, and the corresponding corrections satisfy ∥hqm​∥∞​≤(3/4)m∥f∥∞​/3. If a residual is zero, take all subsequent corrections to be zero. The series
Gq​=∑m=0∞​hqm​
(5)
converges uniformly on the real line to a continuous function. The residuals tend uniformly to zero, proving the boxed representation. An affine change of each coordinate treats any compact rectangle.
Removing a restriction to a compact domain. For a continuous real function f on the whole plane, set
M(t)=maxx2+y2≤t​∣f(x,y)∣(t≥0).
(6)
Choose a positive continuous one-variable A by linear interpolation of A(n)=(n+2)(1+M(n+1)) at nonnegative integers. Since these values increase, for n≤t≤n+1 we have A(t)≥(n+2)(1+M(n+1))≥(1+t)(1+M(t)). Therefore
b(x,y)=A(x2+y2)f(x,y)​satisfies∣b(x,y)∣≤1+x2+y21​.
(7)
Let σ(x)=21​+π−1arctanx. Transport b to the open square by b(s,t)=b(tan(π(s−1/2)),tan(π(t−1/2))), and set it to zero on the square's boundary. The displayed decay estimate proves continuity at every boundary point, including the corners. Apply the compact-square representation to b and substitute s=σ(x), t=σ(y) to obtain b using continuous one-variable functions and addition.
Finally multiply this expression by A(x2+y2). Multiplication itself uses only addition and one-variable functions, because
uv=4(u+v)2−(u−v)2​.
(8)
Squaring, negation and scaling are one-variable operations, as are A and σ. Thus the whole-plane reduction for continuous superposition gives a finite expression of the required kind for every continuous f on the plane, without a boundedness assumption. For a complex-valued f, apply the argument to its real and imaginary parts and combine them using addition and the one-variable map z↦iz. All the constituent one-variable functions can be taken continuous.

The Tychonoff theorem states that an arbitrary product of compact spaces, with the product topology, is compact; no Hausdorff hypothesis is necessary. We prove it using Zorn's lemma and the axiom of choice.
First, every proper filter on a set extends to an ultrafilter. Order its proper extensions by inclusion. The union of a chain is again a proper filter: any finite collection of its members occurs in one member of the chain, and the empty set occurs in none. Zorn's lemma gives a maximal proper filter U. If A∈/U, adjoining A must destroy properness, so some F∈U has F∩A=∅, and hence X∖A∈U. This proves the decision property of an ultrafilter.
Next prove the ultrafilter characterization of compactness. In a compact space the closed sets F, for F∈U, have the finite intersection property, so their intersection contains a point x. Every open neighbourhood O of x belongs to U: otherwise its closed complement belongs to U and contains x, a contradiction. Thus the ultrafilter converges to x. Conversely, if an open cover has no finite subcover, the closed complements have the finite intersection property and generate a proper filter. Extend it to an ultrafilter. A limit point lies in some member O of the cover, so both O and its complement would belong to the ultrafilter. This is impossible. These arguments also prove the closed-set form of compactness directly by taking complements.
Now let X=∏j∈J​Xj​. If a factor is empty, the product is empty and compact. Otherwise the axiom of choice makes X nonempty. For an ultrafilter U on X, its coordinate image
Uj​={A⊆Xj​:πj−1​(A)∈U}
(9)
is an ultrafilter. Compactness of Xj​ supplies at least one limit xj​; use the axiom of choice to choose one for every j. A basic open neighbourhood of x=(xj​)j​ restricts finitely many coordinates. Each corresponding inverse image belongs to U, so their finite intersection also does. Hence U converges to x in the product topology. The ultrafilter criterion proves the theorem. The explicit choices and the maximal-filter argument are exactly the places where choice enters.
The Banach-Alaoglu theorem states that the closed unit ball of the continuous dual space V∗ of a real or complex normed vector space V is compact in the weak-star topology σ(V∗,V). Completeness of V is not required.
For each v∈V, let Dv​={z∈K:∣z∣≤∥v∥}, a compact scalar disk or interval. By the Tychonoff theorem, P=∏v∈V​Dv​ is compact. Inside P, impose the equations
zu+v​=zu​+zv​,zαv​=αzv​(u,v∈V, α∈K).
(10)
Each equation defines a closed subset, since it concerns continuous coordinate projections into a Hausdorff scalar field. Their intersection K is therefore compact. A point in K defines a linear functional ℓ(v)=zv​, and the coordinate bounds give ∣ℓ(v)∣≤∥v∥. Conversely, every ℓ∈V∗ with ∥ℓ∥≤1 gives such a point. Thus K is exactly the image of the dual unit ball under ℓ↦(ℓ(v))v∈V​. The induced product topology is precisely pointwise convergence on V, which is the weak-star topology. This proves
{ℓ∈V∗:∥ℓ∥≤1} is weak-star compact.​
(11)

This proof uses neither the axiom of choice nor a preliminary choice of points in all the given sets. For each nonempty Aj​, form a disjoint union Xj​=Aj​⊔{∞j​} with a distinguished added point, and give it the topology
τj​={∅,Aj​,{∞j​},Xj​}.
(12)
The original set Aj​ has the indiscrete subspace topology, and is clopen. Any open cover of Xj​ either contains Xj​, or contains both Aj​ and {∞j​}, so it has a subcover of at most two members. Thus Xj​ is compact by a proof requiring no choices. No Hausdorff restriction is imposed in this question, and these generally non-Hausdorff spaces are allowed.
The product X=∏j≥1​Xj​ has the explicitly given all-infinity point, so it is nonempty without choice. By the assumed countable-product compactness it is compact. For each j let
Fj​={x∈X:xj​∈Aj​}.
(13)
The coordinate projection is continuous and Aj​ is closed in Xj​, so Fj​ is closed. Any finite intersection of the Fj​ is nonempty: pick an element of each of those finitely many nonempty sets and put ∞k​ in every other coordinate. Finite choice follows by induction in ordinary set theory without assuming the axiom of choice.
Compactness and the finite intersection property now give ⋂j≥1​Fj​=∅. Choose one point x from this single nonempty set. The function f(j)=xj​ satisfies f(j)∈Aj​ for every j, proving the axiom of countable choice.
The implication holds in choice-free set theory: countable product compactness implies countable choice. The printed lower index zero is inconsistent with the stated positive-integer index set; indexing from one merely relabels this argument and changes no conclusion.

sup∥x∥≤1​∥Lx​y∥=sup∥x∥≤1​∥Ry​x∥≤∥Ry​∥<∞.
(14)
The Uniform boundedness principle therefore gives C<∞ such that ∥Lx​∥≤C whenever ∥x∥≤1. Scaling yields
∥xy∥≤C∥x∥∥y∥.
(15)
For completeness, the relevant Uniform boundedness principle follows from the Baire category theorem as follows. For a pointwise bounded family T of bounded linear operators on a Banach space, the closed sets Em​={y:supT∈T​∥Ty∥≤m} cover the space. One Em​ contains an open ball B(y0​,r). Subtracting the bounds for y0​ and y0​+h, with ∥h∥<r, gives supT​∥Th∥≤2m. Applying this to h=(r/2)u, ∥u∥≤1, bounds every operator norm by 4m/r.
Define the new norm by
∥x∥∗​=∥Lx​∥.​
(16)
It is a norm because Lx​e=x, so Lx​=0 implies x=0. The triangle inequality and scalar homogeneity follow from those of the operator norm. Moreover,
∥e∥∥x∥​≤∥Lx​∥≤C∥x∥,
(17)
so it is equivalent to the original norm and remains complete. By associativity, Lxy​=Lx​Ly​, and the submultiplicativity of the operator norm proves
∥xy∥∗​≤∥x∥∗​∥y∥∗​.​
(18)
This is renorming a separately continuous Banach algebra. It even gives ∥e∥∗​=1. If the algebra is the zero space, the conclusion is immediate separately.

A complex Banach algebra is an associative complex algebra with a complete norm satisfying submultiplicativity, ∥ab∥≤∥a∥∥b∥. A field has a nonzero identity e, and every nonzero element is invertible. We derive the Gelfand-Mazur theorem from these facts.
If ∥z∥<1, the series e+z+z2+⋯ converges in the complete algebra. Multiplying its partial sums by e−z and passing to the limit proves the Neumann series inverse. In particular, for any x∈B and ∣λ∣>∥x∥,
R(λ)=(λe−x)−1=λe​+∑n=1∞​λn+1xn​.
(19)
This resolvent of an element tends to zero in norm at infinity; the estimate follows from the geometric bound on ∥xn∥.
We next prove that the spectrum of an element cannot be empty. Suppose every λe−x were invertible. At each λ0​, write
λe−x=(λ0​e−x)(e+(λ−λ0​)R(λ0​)).
(20)
The Neumann series on a small disk about λ0​ proves that R is analytic there. Consequently, for each continuous linear functional ℓ:B→C, the function λ↦ℓ(R(λ)) is entire. It is bounded outside a large disk by the estimate above and bounded on that disk by continuity. The Liouville theorem makes it constant; its zero limit at infinity makes the constant zero.
Continuous complex linear functionals separate points of B. Here is the needed consequence of the Hahn-Banach theorem: for z=0, define g(tz)=t∥z∥ on the real span of z and extend it as a bounded real linear functional of norm one. Then ℓ(w)=g(w)−ig(iw) is complex linear and continuous, and Reℓ(z)=∥z∥=0. The real extension theorem is proved explicitly in Question 4(i) below. Thus ℓ(R(λ))=0 for every ℓ forces R(λ)=0, contradicting (λe−x)R(λ)=e=0. This proves the nonemptiness of the Banach-algebra spectrum.
Choose λ∈σB​(x). Since B is a field, the noninvertible element x−λe must be zero. Hence every x∈B is a scalar multiple of e. The map
Φ:C⟶B,Φ(λ)=λe​
(21)
is therefore a bijective unital complex-algebra homomorphism. It and its inverse are bounded, since ∥λe∥=∣λ∣∥e∥. Thus it is an isomorphism of Banach algebras. If the usual normalization ∥e∥=1 is included in the definition, it is an isometry; without that normalization, it is still the required continuous algebra isomorphism.

∥f∗g∥1​≤∫s,u≥0, s+u≤1​∣f(s)∣∣g(u)∣dsdu≤∥f∥1​∥g∥1​.

B=C⊕A,(α,f)(β,g)=(αβ,αg+βf+f∗g),∥(α,f)∥=∣α∣+∥f∥1​.

Let p be the Minkowski functional of E:
p(v)=inf{t>0:v∈tE}.
(29)
The contained ball makes E absorbing, so p is finite, nonnegative and satisfies p(v)≤∥v∥/ϵ. It is positively homogeneous. To check subadditivity, if v∈sE and w∈tE, the defining property of a convex set gives (v+w)/(s+t)∈E, whence p(v+w)≤s+t; let s,t decrease to the respective infima. Thus p is a sublinear functional. We have p(e)≤1 for every e∈E. Also p(x)≥1: otherwise x∈tE for some 0<t<1, which would put x in E because E is a convex set and 0∈E.
We prove the dominated form of the Hahn-Banach theorem needed here. Suppose g is linear on a subspace M and g≤p there. To extend it to M+Rv with v∈/M, choose the value c at v between
supm∈M​{g(m)−p(m−v)}andinfn∈M​{p(n+v)−g(n)}.
(30)
Every lower candidate is at most every upper candidate, because
g(m)+g(n)=g(m+n)≤p(m+n)≤p(m−v)+p(n+v).
(31)
Taking m=n=0 among the candidates shows the two endpoints are finite: the lower supremum is at least −p(−v) and at most p(v), and the upper infimum lies between them and p(v). Define g​(m+tv)=g(m)+tc. For t>0, the upper inequality applied to m/t gives domination by p(m+tv). For t<0, apply the lower inequality to m/(−t) and multiply by −t. At t=0 domination already holds. Hence this is a dominated one-dimensional extension.
Order all dominated extensions by inclusion of their domains. Chain unions preserve linearity and domination, so Zorn's lemma gives a maximal extension. The one-dimensional argument shows its domain is the whole vector space. This proves the requisite dominated Hahn-Banach theorem, rather than invoking an unproved separation form.
On the line Rx, start with g(tx)=tp(x). For t≥0 this equals p(tx); for t<0 it is nonpositive and therefore at most p(tx). Extend it to T≤p on V. Then
Tx=p(x)≥1,Te≤p(e)≤1(e∈E).
(32)
Applying domination to v and −v also gives
∣Tv∣≤ϵ∥v∥​.
(33)
Thus T is a continuous linear functional, and
Tx≥1≥Tefor every e∈E.​
(34)
This proves separation from an absorbing convex set with no closedness assumption on E. The proof also gives the norm-preserving real extension theorem used earlier: take p(v)=C∥v∥ when the original functional has norm at most C.

λj​≥0,j=1∑N​λj​=1,​j=1∑N​λj​fj​​<ϵ.​
