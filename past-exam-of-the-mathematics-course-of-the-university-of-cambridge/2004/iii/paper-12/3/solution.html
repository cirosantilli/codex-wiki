For a rational matrix A with columns a1​,…,an​, partition regularity means that every finite colouring of the positive integers admits a positive monochromatic vector x with Ax=0. Repeated coordinates are allowed. The columns condition is an ordered partition {1,…,n}=B1​⊔⋯⊔Bs​ into nonempty blocks such that
∑i∈B1​​ai​=0,∑i∈Bj​​ai​∈spanQ​{ai​:i∈B1​∪⋯∪Bj−1​}(j≥2).
(9)
Rado's theorem says that A is partition regular if and only if it satisfies the columns condition. We prove both directions.
For necessity, clear denominators so the columns are integer vectors. For every pair of disjoint index sets I,B with B=∅ for which ∑i∈B​ai​ is not in the rational linear span of the I-columns, choose an integer linear functional ℓI,B​ that vanishes on those I-columns but not on that sum. Such a functional exists by finite-dimensional linear algebra; clear its rational denominators. There are only finitely many pairs, so choose a prime number p dividing none of the nonzero integers
ℓI,B​(∑i∈B​ai​).
(10)
If there are no offending pairs, any prime number suffices.
Colour a positive integer x=pvp​(x)u, p∤u, by umodp. This is the last nonzero digit coloring, using p−1 colours. Partition regularity gives a positive monochromatic solution. Group its coordinate indices by their distinct P-adic valuations e1​<⋯<es​, writing Bj​={i:vp​(xi​)=ej​}. All units xi​/pej​ have the same nonzero residue α modulo p.
For each j, let I=B1​∪⋯∪Bj−1​. If its block sum failed the required span condition, apply the chosen ℓI,Bj​​ to ∑i​ai​xi​=0. The earlier terms vanish exactly, not just modulo a power of p. Divide by pej​; terms with later valuations are still divisible by p. Reduction modulo p then yields
0=αℓI,Bj​​(∑i∈Bj​​ai​)(modp),
(11)
contrary to the choice of p. Thus every required span relation holds, and with I=∅ the first block sum is zero. This is the finite separating-functional proof of the columns condition; it avoids an unjustified passage from congruences to rational equality.
For sufficiency, assume the columns condition. Choose rational coefficients λij​, for j≥2 and i∈B1​∪⋯∪Bj−1​, such that
∑i∈Bj​​ai​+∑i∈B1​∪⋯∪Bj−1​​λij​ai​=0.
(12)
Choose a positive integer c clearing every denominator, and a positive integer p≥max∣cλij​∣, taking p=1 if the list is empty. By the allowed monochromatic m-p-c set theorem, there are positive generators z1​,…,zs​ such that all numbers
czh​+∑j>h​μj​zj​,μj​∈Z,∣μj​∣≤p,
(13)
are positive and have one colour. This is a full m-p-c set; positivity is part of its definition, not a rule discarding negative expressions. For i∈Bh​, put
xi​=czh​+∑j>h​cλij​zj​.
(14)
These coordinates all belong to that monochromatic m-p-c set. Collecting coefficients of zj​ gives
∑i​ai​xi​=cz1​∑i∈B1​​ai​+∑j=2s​czj​(∑i∈Bj​​ai​+∑i∈B1​∪⋯∪Bj−1​​λij​ai​)=0.
(15)
Thus the Rado solution inside an m-p-c set proves partition regularity and completes the theorem.
To obtain the Finite sums theorem from Rado's theorem, introduce a positive variable yF​ for every nonempty F⊆{1,…,k} and impose
yF​−∑i∈F​y{i}​=0(∣F∣≥2).
(16)
Partition the columns of this system into Bj​={F:minF=j}. The B1​-column sum is zero: in a row indexed by F containing 1, the contributions of yF​ and y{1}​ cancel, and other rows receive neither. For j>1, the Bj​-column sum is
−∑F:minF<jj∈F​​aF​,
(17)
where aF​ is the column of yF​. Those columns lie in earlier blocks. Hence the columns condition holds. A monochromatic positive solution gives xi​=y{i}​ and yF​=∑i∈F​xi​, proving
FS(x1​,…,xk​) is monochromatic.​
(18)
For k=1 the system has no rows and the conclusion is immediate. The construction is also visible directly in the sufficiency proof: a monochromatic (k,1,1)-set contains every nonempty sum of its generators, classified by the smallest chosen index.
