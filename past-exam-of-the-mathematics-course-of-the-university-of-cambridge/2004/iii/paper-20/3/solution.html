The Lie group–Lie algebra correspondence separates local differential structure from global topology. For a Lie group G, define LG=Te​G as a real vector space. Extend each X∈Te​G to the left-invariant vector field
XL(g)=de​Lg​(X).
(20)
The commutator of two such vector fields is again left-invariant. Set
[X,Y]=[XL,YL](e).​
(21)
The vector-field commutator is bilinear, antisymmetric and satisfies the Jacobi identity, so this makes Te​G a Lie algebra. For a matrix group this bracket is XY−YX.
For a smooth Lie group homomorphism ϕ:G1​→G2​, its differential at the identity is the linear map
Lϕ=de1​​ϕ:g1​⟶g2​.
(22)
The identity ϕ∘Lg​=Lϕ(g)​∘ϕ shows that the corresponding left-invariant fields are ϕ-related. Brackets of related vector fields are related, hence
Lϕ([X,Y])=[Lϕ(X),Lϕ(Y)].​
(23)
The chain rule also gives L(ψ∘ϕ)=Lψ∘Lϕ and L(id)=id. Thus differentiation is a functor from Lie groups and smooth homomorphisms to real Lie algebras and bracket-preserving linear maps.
To see what information the differential retains, apply ϕ to a one-parameter subgroup. Its initial tangent is Lϕ(X), so uniqueness yields
ϕ(expX)=exp(Lϕ(X)).​
(24)
If G1​ is connected, a small exponential neighbourhood generates it. Every g∈G1​ is a finite product expX1​⋯expXk​, and therefore
ϕ(g)=exp(Lϕ(X1​))⋯exp(Lϕ(Xk​)).
(25)
For an already existing homomorphism this expression is independent of the chosen product because it equals ϕ(g). Two homomorphisms with the same differential agree on that neighbourhood and hence everywhere. This proves that a Lie group homomorphism determined by its differential requires a connected source.
On a disconnected source only the restriction to G1∘​ is determined. For instance a nontrivial finite discrete group has zero Lie algebra, so its identity and trivial endomorphisms have the same zero differential but are different maps. The discussion of all components must therefore include additional global data. The remaining parts explain the corresponding existence and topology conditions.
