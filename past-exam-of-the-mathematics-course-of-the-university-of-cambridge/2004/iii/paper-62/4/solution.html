A solar-type main sequence star has a radiative interior and an outer convection zone. Rotating convection and differential rotation continually regenerate its magnetic field by dynamo action. The field is therefore not simply an initially stored field undergoing passive magnetic diffusion. In an alpha-Omega dynamo, the Omega effect winds a poloidal magnetic field into a toroidal magnetic field, while the alpha effect associated with helical motions can regenerate poloidal flux. In a solar dynamo, the Babcock-Leighton mechanism provides another poloidal source through the emergence and dispersal of tilted active regions. Shear near the tachocline and transport through the convection zone help organize the cycle; no single dynamo model is needed to claim that rotation and convection are essential ingredients.
Rotation acts on convective motions through the Coriolis force, altering their correlations and helicity. A useful activity measure is the stellar activity Rossby number, Ro∗​=Prot​/τc​, where τc​ is a convective turnover time. At a given stellar structure, faster rotation ordinarily increases rotational influence and produces greater magnetic activity in the unsaturated regime. Young solar-type main sequence stars commonly rotate rapidly and show strong sunspots, flares, and chromospheric and coronal emission, although their initial rotation rates have substantial scatter. Very rapid rotators enter a saturated regime, so activity does not increase without limit as rotation increases. The saturation mechanism is not uniquely fixed by the correlation itself. The observed unsaturated correlation and saturated regime are documented in primary rotation–activity measurements.
The reverse interaction is wind-driven magnetic braking of a solar-type star. An ionized stellar wind is coupled to the star's open magnetic field. Matter carries angular momentum, and the Maxwell stress tensor also transmits a torque. Magnetic coupling provides an effective lever arm comparable to the Alfvén radius, rather than only the stellar radius. Writing M˙w​>0 for the outward mass-loss rate, a schematic angular-momentum equation is
dtd​(IΩ)=−M˙w​ΩRA2​,
(33)
where a geometry factor can be absorbed into an effective RA​. This lever-arm interpretation follows from the angular-momentum invariant of a magnetized wind; it is the central result of the original magnetized solar-wind calculation. Magnetic braking can be effective despite a small mass-loss rate, because RA​ can greatly exceed the stellar radius.

Ω−2(t)=Ω−2(t0​)+I2K​(t−t0​).

Thus Ω∝t−1/2,Prot​∝t1/2​ in the late-time part of this approximation. This Skumanich rotation law captures the classic observed secular decline of rotation and magnetic activity; its observational basis is the original rotation and chromospheric-emission analysis. It is a scaling model, not an exact trajectory for every star. With a saturated field, the same simplified wind model instead gives a torque approximately proportional to −Ω, so initially rapid rotation can decay more nearly exponentially until the unsaturated regime is reached.
Over the main sequence lifetime, wind loss therefore generally makes the star rotate more slowly, increases its stellar activity Rossby number, and reduces its unsaturated mean activity. Later changes of moment of inertia, wind properties and open-field topology alter the simple power law. Internal transport of angular momentum can also feed the braked envelope from the radiative interior, so surface rotation is not automatically a measure of the entire star's angular momentum. In a star with weak braking, this secular history differs from the ideal cubic-torque model; an age law alone is not a fundamental dynamo equation.
The stellar rotation-activity feedback also acts on shorter time scales. A growing magnetic field exerts a Lorentz force that changes differential rotation and convective motions, extracting kinetic energy and limiting further growth through dynamo quenching. Conversely, varying shear and poloidal regeneration change the magnetic cycle. The present solar-like regime can sustain repeated solar cycles, with polarity reversals and activity modulation superposed on the much slower rotational evolution. Rotation promotes dynamo activity; dynamo-generated fields brake rotation through the wind and react back on the motions that generate them. This coupled feedback explains the broad progression from young, rapidly rotating, strongly active stars toward older, more slowly rotating, less active solar-type stars, while allowing cycles, irregular modulation and different initial histories.
