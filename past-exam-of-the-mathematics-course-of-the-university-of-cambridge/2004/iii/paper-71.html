If polynomials with integer monomial coefficients converge uniformly to f, their values at zero and one are integers converging to f(0) and f(1). A convergent integer sequence is eventually constant: once all its terms lie in an interval of length less than one they must agree. Hence both endpoint values are integers.
Conversely assume those values are integers. The rounded Bernstein polynomial is an integer-coefficient polynomial, because its displayed scalar coefficients are integers and each xk(1−x)n−k expands with integer coefficients. Part b gives its vanishing distance from Bn​f. For completeness, the remaining uniform convergence Bn​f→f follows directly from uniform continuity. Let X have the binomial distribution with parameters n,x; then Bn​(f,x)=Ef(X/n), E(X/n)=x, and Var(X/n)=x(1−x)/n≤1/(4n). Splitting into ∣X/n−x∣≤δ and its complement, and applying Chebyshev's inequality, gives
∣Bn​(f,x)−f(x)∣≤ω(f,δ)+2∥f∥∞​4nδ21​,
(6)
where ω is the modulus of continuity. First choose δ to make the first term small uniformly, then take n large. The triangle inequality now gives ∥Bn∗​f−f∥∞​→0. We have proved the integer-coefficient polynomial approximation characterization
f∈Z[x]​∥⋅∥∞​⟺f(0),f(1)∈Z.​
(7)

A strictly convex normed space is a normed vector space whose unit sphere contains no nontrivial line segment. Equivalently, for distinct unit vectors x,y,
​2x+y​​<1.
(8)
Suppose u,v∈U are both elements of best approximation in a normed space to f, and let d=infw∈U​∥f−w∥. If d=0 then u=v=f. If d>0 and u=v, the vectors (f−u)/d and (f−v)/d are distinct unit vectors. Strict convexity gives
​f−2u+v​​=d​2(f−u)/d+(f−v)/d​​<d.
(9)
But (u+v)/2∈U, contradicting the definition of d. Thus there is at most one best approximant. This proves uniqueness of best approximation in a strictly convex space, and does not assert existence for an arbitrary, possibly nonclosed, linear subspace.

Take n≥1 and 1<p<∞. The Lp space is strictly convex because z↦∣z∣p is strictly convex: for distinct unit functions its pointwise midpoint inequality is strict on a set of positive measure, hence integration gives norm strictly below one. The finite-dimensional linear subspace Tn−1​ has a best approximant: any minimizing sequence is bounded by ∥t∥p​≤∥f−t∥p​+∥f∥p​, and finite-dimensional compactness gives a convergent subsequence whose limit minimizes the continuous error. Uniqueness follows from part 1.
Write that unique minimizer as t∗ and its residual as F=f−t∗. Translation on the circle preserves the Lp norm. Since f(x+2π/n)=f(x), the translated polynomial t∗(x+2π/n) is another minimizer. Uniqueness forces
t∗(x+2π/n)=t∗(x),F(x+2π/n)=F(x).
(10)
Also f(x+π/n)=−f(x). Therefore the polynomial −t∗(x+π/n) has residual −F(x+π/n), with the same norm as F, and is again a minimizer. Hence
t∗(x)=−t∗(x+π/n),F(x)=−F(x+π/n).
(11)
To extract the consequence, write the trigonometric polynomial as t∗(x)=∑∣ℓ∣≤n−1​cℓ​eiℓx. The first identity gives (e2πiℓ/n−1)cℓ​=0. Only ℓ=0 in the indicated frequency range is a multiple of n, so t∗ is constant. The second identity makes this constant its own negative and therefore zero. Thus the lower-frequency Lp approximation to a single harmonic is
tn−1∗​≡0,En−1,p​(f)=∥acosnx+bsinnx∥p​.​
(12)
For n=1 the space already consists only of constants, and the negative-translation argument gives the same result.

For a real continuous 2π-periodic function f, the trigonometric Chebyshev alternation theorem says that t∗∈Tn​ is its best uniform approximation if and only if there are 2n+2 distinct cyclically ordered points in one period,
x0​<x1​<⋯<x2n+1​<x0​+2π,
(13)
and a sign ε∈{1,−1} such that
f(xj​)−t∗(xj​)=ε(−1)j∥f−t∗∥∞​(0≤j≤2n+1).​
(14)
The best approximant exists and is unique. Alternation is cyclic: the last and first displayed extrema also have opposite signs across the closing interval. If the error is zero, f=t∗ and the characterization holds trivially.
The sufficiency can be seen from zero counting. A strictly improving trigonometric polynomial would have a difference from t∗ with the alternating nonzero signs at these points, forcing at least 2n+2 zeros on the circle. A nonzero member of Tn​ has at most 2n zeros: multiplying its Laurent expression by einx gives an ordinary polynomial of degree at most 2n in eix. This contradiction is the alternation mechanism used in the next part.

Let y=(f(x1​),…,f(xn​))T and write the interpolating spline as Px​f=∑j=1n​cj​Nj​. The interpolation equations are Ax​c=y, so c=Ax−1​y. Since Px​ is defined as a unique interpolant for arbitrary data, its B-spline collocation matrix is invertible. Ordered sites satisfying diagonal positivity ensure this by question 7. Positivity alone without admissible ordering does not: two equal sites in overlapping basis supports could give two identical rows. The norm argument below uses the existence of the operator stipulated here.
Standard partition-normalized B-splines satisfy Nj​≥0 and ∑j=1n​Nj​≤1. The latter is a subpartition of unity for B-splines: extending the knot sequence gives a full partition of unity, and discarding basis functions cannot increase its sum. Hence
∣Px​f(t)∣≤∑j​∣cj​∣Nj​(t)≤∥c∥ℓ∞​≤∥Ax−1​∥ℓ∞​∥y∥ℓ∞​≤∥Ax−1​∥ℓ∞​∥f∥∞​.
(19)
Taking the supremum over t and then over unit-norm f gives the B-spline interpolation operator norm estimate
∥Px​∥L∞​≤∥Ax−1​∥ℓ∞​.​
(20)
Here the matrix norm is the induced maximum-row-sum norm, not the maximum of its individual entries. The proof only needs nonnegative basis functions, their sum at most one, and invertible interpolation.

Use the standard knot-domain convention in which the spline space contains every polynomial of degree at most k−1 on [a,b], with completed or clamped endpoint knots. The De Boor–Fix functional extracts a local spline coefficient, and its Hahn-Banach theorem extension is linear on continuous local data with the stated bound. Only that bound and spline reproduction are needed here.
For x in a knot cell [tj​,tj+1​], support and Gram bandwidth of B-splines implies that only indices
j+1−k≤i≤j
(38)
can contribute, restricted also to 1≤i≤n. Every corresponding local support [ti​,ti+k​] lies inside
Jj​=[tj+1−k​,tj+k​].
(39)
Nonnegativity and subpartition of unity for B-splines therefore give
∣Qf(x)∣≤∑i​∣λi​(f)∣Ni​(x)≤ck​∥f∥C(Jj​)​∑i​Ni​(x)≤ck​∥f∥C(Jj​)​.
(40)
Taking the supremum over the cell proves the local stability estimate for spline quasi-interpolation:
∥Qf∥C[tj​,tj+1​]​≤ck​∥f∥C[tj+1−k​,tj+k​]​.​
(41)
Endpoint limits follow by continuity for k≥2; the order-one version uses the usual one-sided interval convention.
Let h=maxi​∣ti+1​−ti​∣ and f∈Ck[a,b]. Choose any point yj​ in the stencil and form its degree-k−1 Taylor polynomial pj​. The Taylor theorem remainder gives
∥f−pj​∥C(Jj​)​≤k!diam(Jj​)k​∥f(k)∥C(Jj​)​≤k!(2k−1)khk​∥f(k)∥∞​.
(42)
The polynomial is itself a spline, so Qpj​=pj​. By linearity and the local stability estimate,
∥f−Qf∥C[tj​,tj+1​]​≤∥f−pj​∥C[tj​,tj+1​]​+∥Q(f−pj​)∥C[tj​,tj+1​]​≤(1+ck​)∥f−pj​∥C(Jj​)​.
(43)
Taking the maximum over cells yields
∥f−Qf∥∞​≤k!(1+ck​)(2k−1)k​hk∥f(k)∥∞​=O(hk).​
(44)
The constant depends only on k and the supplied functional bound, not on mesh ratios or the number of knots.
The endpoint convention is essential to the global assertion. The domain can be the basic knot interval, with any needed exterior data extended smoothly, or the full approximation interval can use clamped endpoint knots. If one instead takes a finite, unclamped collection on the entire outer support interval [t1​,tn+k​], every order-k≥2 basis function vanishes at t1​, so Q1(t1​)=0 and uniform approximation to the constant one there is impossible. This does not affect the local estimate; it explains the usual boundary completion implicit in the global spline approximation statement.

First take k≥2, so the simple-knot B-splines are continuous, vanish at both support endpoints, and are strictly positive inside their supports. Thus the diagonal condition is ti​<xi​<ti+k​. We prove both directions of the Schoenberg–Whitney theorem, deriving the weighted zero-count fact needed for sufficiency rather than relying on an ambiguous coefficient-free hint.
For necessity, suppose Ni​(xi​)=0. If xi​≤ti​, then for every row r≤i and column j≥i we have xr​≤xi​≤ti​≤tj​, hence Nj​(xr​)=0. The first i rows have nonzero entries in at most i−1 columns and are linearly dependent. If instead xi​≥ti+k​, then for r≥i and j≤i we have xr​≥xi​≥ti+k​≥tj+k​, so those entries vanish. The last n−i+1 rows have nonzero entries in at most n−i columns and are again dependent. In either case the B-spline collocation matrix is singular. Thus invertibility implies the diagonal positivity condition.
We establish two elementary spline facts for the converse. For local linear independence of B-splines, extend the strictly increasing knots in both directions. On a cell (tj​,tj+1​) the k active basis functions have indices j−k+1,…,j. Their knot polynomials are ψi​(y)=∏ℓ=1k−1​(y−ti+ℓ​). The Cox-de Boor recurrence proves Marsden's identity
(y−x)k−1=∑i=j−k+1j​Ni​(x)ψi​(y).
(45)
Here is the induction behind that identity. For order one the indicator functions sum to one. On passing from order k−1 to k, collect the coefficient of each lower-order basis function. With u=ti​, v=ti+k−1​, its extra factor is
v−u(x−u)(y−v)+(v−x)(y−u)​=y−x.
(46)
Thus the identity at one order multiplies by y−x at the next.
The k polynomials ψi​ are independent: evaluate successively at y=tj​,tj−1​,…,tj−k+1​. The first evaluation isolates ψj​; after its coefficient has been eliminated the next isolates ψj−1​, and so on, with nonzero diagonal factors because the knots are distinct. Comparing powers of y in the identity then shows that the active Ni​ span all polynomials of degree at most k−1 on the cell. There are k of them, so they are independent. In particular, a spline vanishes on a whole open cell exactly when all its active coefficients vanish. Padding the finite coefficient vector with zeros makes this fact apply also near the outer knots.
Next let s=∑i=pq​ci​Ni​ be a real spline on I=(tp​,tq+k​), with no identically zero knot cell there. The simple-knot B-spline regularity gives s∈Ck−2, and its derivatives through order k−2 vanish at both endpoints because it is supported on the closed interval. Suppose it has Z distinct interior zeros. Its zero set initially has Z+2 connected components, counting the endpoints; the zeros are isolated because no cell vanishes identically.
A useful form of Rolle's theorem counts zero components, including any zero intervals that arise after differentiation. Between two successive zero components a real differentiable function is nonzero and has an interior extremum, supplying a zero of its derivative. These derivative-zero components are distinct: a zero interval connecting two such extrema would make the original function constant across an intervening zero and nonzero gap, which is impossible. If its derivative also vanishes at both endpoints, those supply two further distinct components. Thus differentiation increases the zero-component count by at least one. Apply this successively through derivative order k−2; every required endpoint derivative vanishes. No derivative in this process can vanish identically: that would make s a global polynomial of degree below k−1, with all its endpoint derivatives through that degree zero, forcing s=0. The final derivative has at least Z+k zero components.
That final derivative is continuous and piecewise affine on q−p+k knot cells. Each closed affine cell can meet at most one zero component: two different zeros would force the whole cell to vanish, joining them into one component. Every zero component meets a cell, so there are at most q−p+k of them. For k=2 this argument applies directly without differentiation. We have proved the compact-support spline zero count
Z(s;I)≤q−p.​
(47)
This proof also accommodates zero intervals of the derivatives; counting them as infinitely many distinct zeros would invalidate a naive repeated-Rolle argument.
Now assume Ni​(xi​)>0 for all i and suppose Ax​c=0 for a nonzero coefficient vector. Since Ax​ is real, a nonzero real null vector can be chosen. Set s=∑i​ci​Ni​, so s(xi​)=0 for all i. Consider one connected component of the union of the open supports of the terms with ci​=0. If its first and last indices are p,q, the component is exactly I=(tp​,tq+k​). On this interval s equals s0​=∑i=pq​ci​Ni​. Each knot cell in I has at least one active nonzero coefficient, so local independence ensures s0​ does not vanish on any whole cell.
For every p≤i≤q, diagonal positivity gives ti​<xi​<ti+k​, and this places xi​ inside I. Thus s0​ has the q−p+1 distinct zeros xp​,…,xq​, contradicting the upper bound q−p. There is no nonzero null vector, proving sufficiency. Together the two directions give
Ax​ is invertible⟺Ni​(xi​)>0 for every i.​
(48)
For completeness, order k=1 consists of disjoint interval indicators, with the usual left-closed, right-open convention. An invertible ordered collocation requires exactly one site in each interval; ordering makes this equivalent to Ni​(xi​)>0, and the matrix is then the identity. This handles possible sites at left knots without incorrectly replacing positivity by a strict interior inequality for order one.
