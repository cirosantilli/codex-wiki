Write Biα​=A˙iα​ and assume that the material constraint is regular, so F,A​=0. Differentiating the constraint shows that admissible rates of the deformation gradient satisfy F,Aiα​​Biα​=0. The given stress-power identity says that W,A​−NT annihilates this tangent hyperplane. Its orthogonal complement is the one-dimensional span of F,A​, so a Lagrange multiplier gives the constraint reaction in hyperelastic stress:
Nαi​=W,Aiα​​+qF,Aiα​​.​
(1)
The sign of q is a convention; the virtual work identity determines no constitutive value for this reaction. Regularity matters: a singular equation describing the same constraint need not have a nonzero gradient.
For incompressibility, take F=detA−1. Differentiating the expression using the Levi-Civita symbol gives
∂Aiα​∂detA​=21​ϵαβγ​ϵijk​Ajβ​Akγ​=(detA)(A−1)αi​.
(2)
The middle expression is the corresponding entry of the cofactor matrix. Since detA=1, the nominal stress is therefore
Nαi​=W,Aiα​​+q(A−1)αi​.​
(3)
For the neo-Hookean solid, W,Aiα​​=μAiα​, so in the material-first convention N=μAT+qA−1. The identity matrix satisfies the prescribed dead loading with q=T−μ, proving that the undeformed cube is always an equilibrium.
To obtain the homogeneous tensile bifurcation of a neo-Hookean cube, first seek A=diag(λ,λ,λ−2), with λ>0. This has unit determinant. The two distinct diagonal stress equations are μλ+q/λ=T and μλ−2+qλ2=T. Eliminating q gives
(λ3−1)[μ(λ+λ−2)−T]=0.
(4)
Thus the nontrivial branch is
t=λ+λ−2,λ3−tλ2+1=0,q=μ/λ,t=T/μ.​
(5)
The function h(λ)=λ+λ−2 decreases on (0,21/3) and increases on (21/3,∞), because h′=1−2λ−3. Its minimum is h(21/3)=3/22/3. Above this threshold there are two positive roots λ−​<21/3<λ+​. For t=2, neither root is one, and the three choices of the exceptional coordinate for each root give six distinct diagonal deformation gradients, in addition to I.
At t=2, however, λ−​=1 and the three smaller-root matrices coincide with I: it would be incorrect to count those as three new deformations. The requested existence of seven distinct homogeneous deformations nevertheless holds even at this value. For any unit vector n, put
A(n)=λ+​I+(λ+−2​−λ+​)nnT.
(6)
Its positive eigenvalues are λ+​,λ+​,λ+−2​, and its determinant is one. Since A(n) is an orthogonal conjugate of the diagonal solution, the same q=μ/λ+​ gives μA(n)T+qA(n)−1=TI. Choose the six directions
e1​, e2​, e3​, 2​e1​+e2​​, 2​e1​+e3​​, 2​e2​+e3​​.
(7)
Their outer products are distinct, and λ+​>21/3>1, so the six A(n) are distinct from one another and from I. Constant nominal stress satisfies equilibrium and the prescribed surface tractions. Hence seven distinct homogeneous equilibria exist whenever T/μ>3/22/3. This is an existence count, not an exhaustive classification: rotated principal axes actually give a continuous family. The conventional seven axis-aligned positive-stretch branches coalesce to four distinct matrices at T/μ=2.

Work in the fixed reference domain Ω0​, and write vi​=δxi​. For the unconstrained hyperelastic material considered here, the material-first nominal stress is Nαi​=W,Aiα​​. The increment of the deformation gradient and the incremental elastic moduli are
δAiα​=vi,α​,cαiβj​=∂Aiα​∂Ajβ​∂2W​,δNαi​=cαiβj​vj,β​.
(14)
All coefficients are evaluated at the base deformation. The Hessian symmetry is cαiβj​=cβjαi​; minor interchange symmetry is unnecessary. Linearizing the momentum equation gives
(cαiβj​vj,β​),α​+ρ0​δgi​=ρ0​v¨i​in Ω0​.​
(15)
The reference mass density and reference outward unit normal vector are fixed, so the incremental boundary conditions are
να​cαiβj​vj,β​=δti0​orvi​=δxi0​.​
(16)
In particular, no variation of a current surface normal belongs in this material-coordinate traction condition. If a material constraint were imposed, one would additionally linearize its constraint reaction in hyperelastic stress and its admissibility equation; the present moduli formula is the unconstrained one.

For a uniform material and an affine static base deformation, the deformation gradient, incremental elastic moduli and reference mass density are constant. With zero incremental body force, the equation reduces to cαiβj​vj,βα​=ρ0​v¨i​. Let ν be a unit material propagation direction, m a constant polarization and s=t−να​ξα​/c. Direct differentiation of the plane wave gives
vi​=mi​f(s),v¨i​=mi​f′′(s),vj,βα​=c2να​νβ​​mj​f′′(s).
(17)
Thus every twice differentiable profile f satisfies the incremental equation if
Qij​(ν)mj​=ρ0​c2mi​,Qij​(ν)=cαiβj​να​νβ​.​
(18)
This is the acoustic tensor eigenvalue problem. The acoustic tensor is a real symmetric matrix: interchange the index pairs in the incremental elastic moduli and then interchange the dummy material indices to obtain Qji​=Qij​. Its eigenvectors give the displacement polarizations. A positive eigenvalue gives a real nonzero speed c; arbitrary-profile propagation imposes precisely this algebraic condition. Boundary conditions determine which combinations of these local plane waves are allowed in a finite body.

For a static base state under dead loading, put vi​=ui​(ξ)eiωt and take zero incremental body force and traction. The admissible squared frequencies are the eigenvalues of the weighted boundary value problem
−(cαiβj​uj,β​),α​=ρ0​ω2ui​in Ω0​,να​cαiβj​uj,β​=0on ∂Ω0​.
(19)
Multiply by ui​​ and integrate over the reference body. The boundary term in integration by parts vanishes, giving the elastic normal-mode energy identity
ω2=∫Ω0​​ρ0​ui​​ui​dV∫Ω0​​ui,α​​cαiβj​uj,β​dV​.​
(20)
For a nonzero mode and positive mass density, the denominator is real and positive. The numerator is real by major symmetry of the incremental elastic moduli. Explicitly, if u=p+iq with real p,q, the two imaginary cross terms cancel, leaving the sum of the quadratic energies of ∇p and ∇q. Consequently every admissible ω2 is real. A negative value, when allowed by indefinite moduli, corresponds to exponential growth or decay rather than an oscillation with real frequency.
If the moduli quadratic form is strictly positive on every nonzero real matrix, that same decomposition shows that the numerator is positive whenever ∇u is nonzero. Thus ω2>0 for every nontranslation mode. Under the usual assumptions of a bounded connected body, regular coefficients and uniform positivity, the Rayleigh quotient defines a self-adjoint operator with discrete positive squared frequencies on the mass-weighted mean-zero displacement space. Its eigenfunctions give the nontrivial small harmonic oscillations.
There is an unavoidable zero-frequency qualification for the pure traction problem as printed: ui​=bi​, for any nonzero constant vector b, has zero gradient, zero incremental nominal stress and ω2=0. These three constant translations obey all the homogeneous incremental boundary conditions. Under the strict quadratic-form hypothesis they are the entire zero eigenspace on a connected body. Therefore the unrestricted conclusion is
ω2≥0;ω2=0 precisely for translations.​
(21)
Strict positivity for every nonzero displacement requires excluding these modes, for example by imposing ∫Ω0​​ρ0​udV=0, or by adding a displacement constraint. If harmonic perturbations are defined to have nonzero frequency from the outset, the requested positivity holds for all those perturbations.

The fixed-displacement boundary value problem now has u=0 on the whole boundary. The elastic normal-mode energy identity still holds, because its boundary term contains u. Define
Bαiβj​=Kkγ​ϵαβγ​ϵijk​,Hαiβj​=cαiβj​+Bαiβj​.
(22)
For real constant K, B has major symmetry: both Levi-Civita symbols change sign upon exchanging the two index pairs. Its quadratic density is a quadratic minor null Lagrangian. Indeed,
∫Ω0​​ui,α​​Bαiβj​uj,β​dV​=∫∂Ω0​​να​ui​​Bαiβj​uj,β​dS−∫Ω0​​ui​​Kkγ​ϵαβγ​ϵijk​uj,βα​dV=0.​
(23)
The boundary integral vanishes by the Dirichlet boundary condition, and the volume integral vanishes because mixed derivatives are symmetric in α,β whereas ϵαβγ​ is antisymmetric. Constancy of K is essential: otherwise its derivatives would produce extra terms. This calculation initially applies to smooth fields and extends to fields with zero Sobolev boundary trace by density.
It follows that the Rayleigh quotient numerator can be replaced by the quadratic form with H:
ω2∫Ω0​​ρ0​∣u∣2dV=∫Ω0​​ui,α​​Hαiβj​uj,β​dV.
(24)
The assumed positivity of H on all nonzero real matrices also gives positivity on nonzero complex matrices by splitting real and imaginary parts. A nonzero u satisfying u=0 on the boundary cannot have identically zero gradient. Hence the right side is strictly positive, and
ω2>0for every nonzero fixed-boundary mode.​
(25)
This works even when the original moduli quadratic form is not pointwise positive on all matrices: its integrated energy on fixed-boundary gradients agrees with a positive density after adding the quadratic minor null Lagrangian.

At a fixed material point, freeze the incremental elastic moduli at their local values and fix a unit propagation direction ν. For an arbitrary nonzero polarization m, the rank-one matrix aiα​=mi​να​ annihilates the added quadratic minor null Lagrangian, since
Kkγ​ϵαβγ​ϵijk​mi​να​mj​νβ​=0.
(26)
The assumed positivity therefore gives
mi​Qij​(ν)mj​=aiα​Hαiβj​ajβ​>0.
(27)
Thus the acoustic tensor is positive-definite; this is strong ellipticity in elasticity. Major symmetry also makes it a real symmetric matrix. By the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices, it has three real positive eigenvalues q1​,q2​,q3​ and an orthonormal basis of real polarizations m(1),m(2),m(3). Therefore
cr2​=qr​/ρ0​>0,r=1,2,3.​
(28)
Each polarization gives a real plane wave profile through the equation of part (a), in either sign of the propagation speed. Repeated eigenvalues still admit three independent polarizations; the claim is not that the three speeds must be distinct. For an inhomogeneous base deformation these are local, frozen-coefficient wave modes, rather than exact plane waves throughout the entire body.

For antiplane shear, the infinitesimal displacement is (0,0,w(x1​,x2​)). The isotropic linear elasticity constitutive law gives σ13​=μw,1​ and σ23​=μw,2​, together with the symmetric entries σ31​,σ32​. All other stress tensor components vanish. Static force balance without body force becomes μ(w,11​+w,22​)=0, so w satisfies the Laplace equation.
Locally choose a harmonic conjugate ϕ by ϕ,1​=w,2​ and ϕ,2​=−w,1​. Compatibility follows from the Laplace equation. These are the Cauchy-Riemann equations for F=ϕ+iw, and hence
F′(z)=ϕ,1​+iw,1​=w,2​+iw,1​,σ23​+iσ13​=μF′(z).​
(29)
Thus the complex stress potential for antiplane shear supplies both stresses and, by integration, the displacement. A global potential must have the displacement periods appropriate to the cracked body; these periods also help fix homogeneous solutions.
For the additional crack-induced field, set G=μF′ and p(t)=σ23A​(t,0). Only p loads the prospective faces, since σ13A​ is tangential to their normal. Denote upper and lower boundary values by G+,G−. The imposed cancellation of face tractions is ReG±=−p. The traction data and geometry are invariant under the displacement reflection w(x1​,x2​)↦−w(x1​,−x2​). For the induced correction with no independently added loading, uniqueness up to a rigid translation allows the odd displacement choice. Then w,2​ is even, w,1​ is odd, and G−=G+ on the real axis. Therefore the Hilbert problem for an antiplane crack is
G++G−=−2p on cracks,G+−G−=0 on intact portions.​
(30)
The second condition expresses continuation through uncracked material. There G is real and the additional σ13​ vanishes. This scalar Riemann-Hilbert problem is supplemented by at most inverse-square-root stress singularities at the crack tips and the usual correction with no additional remote resultant or imposed dislocation. Without a far-field or period normalization, the face conditions alone permit homogeneous terms. The formulas below select this normalized induced field and explicitly identify the excluded terms.

In the reflected real-loading class C is real, and its 1/z term gives a nonzero displacement period around the crack. It is excluded for the induced, dislocation-free correction. A nonvanishing constant stress term would change the remote applied field and is likewise excluded.
