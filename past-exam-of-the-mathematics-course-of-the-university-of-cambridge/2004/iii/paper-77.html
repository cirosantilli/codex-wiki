The Lighthill elongated-body theory approximation treats each cross-section of a slender swimmer as a locally two-dimensional potential flow problem. The transverse dimension is small compared with the length and the scale over which cross-sections vary. The displacement and slope are small enough to linearize the impermeability condition about a straight body. The Reynolds number is sufficiently large for lateral loading to be dominated by fluid inertia; viscosity, axial friction and higher-order end corrections are neglected at this order. This reactive approximation must be distinguished from the viscous resistive-force theory used for a flagellum.
In coordinates translating with the swimmer, the undisturbed fluid passes in the positive x direction at speed U. Applying the impermeability condition to the moving centre plane gives the transverse fluid velocity
vy​=ht​+Uhx​=Dh,D=∂t​+U∂x​.
(1)
The local added mass m(x) is defined so that the transverse fluid hydrodynamic impulse per unit axial length is Py​=m(x)vy​. A fixed body-coordinate interval receives an impulse flux UPy​ at its leading end and loses the corresponding flux at its trailing end. Its rate of change of impulse plus the net outward impulse flux is therefore
∂t​Py​+U∂x​Py​=D(mDh).
(2)
This is the transverse force exerted by the body on the fluid. By Newton's third law, the transverse force exerted by the fluid on the body has the opposite sign:
Ffluid​=D(mDh),Fbody​=−D(mDh).​
(3)
The distinction is essential here: the printed positive expression is labelled as the force on the fish, so that label and expression are inconsistent. For example, take a uniform cross-section and a rigid lateral displacement h=H(t). The swimmer must accelerate fluid of added mass m, which reacts on it with force −mH′′, not +mH′′. Thus the positive expression is recovered exactly with the force-on-fluid convention; the physically consistent force on the fish is the negative expression. In the following parts, Fy​ denotes Fbody​ so that the beam balance uses a genuine external force on the fish.

Choose the internal transverse force S(x,t) so that the net internal transverse force on a slice is [S(x+dx,t)−S(x,t)]. Its linear momentum balance is then
mb​(x)htt​dx=Sx​dx+Fy​dx.
(4)
The coordinate x labels body slices, so their lateral material acceleration is htt​; the fluid transport operator D must not be substituted for this body acceleration.
Let G be the total internal bending moment, including both passive elastic and active muscular contributions. With the compatible moment orientation, the net couple on the slice is (Gx​−S)dx. The cross-sectional rotary inertia is smaller than the translational inertia by the square of the transverse-to-longitudinal length ratio, and is neglected in this slender-beam approximation. Slice angular momentum balance therefore gives S=Gx​. Eliminating S from the slice linear momentum balance proves
Gxx​=mb​htt​−Fy​.​
(5)
This equation is the balance law for an active bending beam for a swimming fish; a constitutive relation for the passive bending moment or the muscle actuation is not needed to derive it. With the force-on-body convention established above it becomes
Gxx​=mb​htt​+D(mDh).
(6)
As a check, constant m and rigid lateral acceleration give the effective inertia mb​+m. Combining the printed positive force in part (i) with the minus sign in the beam equation would instead give mb​−m. That is the same sign defect, rather than a negative hydrodynamic added mass. If the positive quantity in part (i) is called Fy​ as a force on the fluid, the beam equation must contain +Fy​.

A freely swimming body has neither an imposed end couple nor an imposed end transverse force. Its end bending moment is therefore zero, and the transverse force S=Gx​ is also zero at both ends. These are the four free-end bending boundary conditions; they do not require the end displacement or slope to vanish.
Put R=mb​htt​+D(mDh), using the physically consistent sign. Integrating Gxx​=R with the two leading-end conditions gives
Gx​(x,t)=∫0x​R(s,t)ds,G(x,t)=∫0x​(x−s)R(s,t)ds.
(7)
The two trailing-end conditions consequently require
∫0L​Rdx=0,∫0L​xRdx=0.​
(8)
These free-end compatibility for a swimming beam conditions are total transverse force and torque balances. They explain why an arbitrary plausible prescribed h does not suffice: two integration constants can satisfy only two end conditions unless the forcing already satisfies both compatibility integrals.
The missing freedom is the swimming recoil correction. An observed or prescribed deformation hd​ relative to the body must be supplemented by a lateral translation Y(t) and a small yaw Θ(t):
h(x,t)=hd​(x,t)+Y(t)+Θ(t)(x−xc​),xc​=∫0L​mb​dx∫0L​xmb​dx​.
(9)
To identify Y with the actual centre of mass displacement, first remove the body-mass-weighted mean from hd​. One may also remove its body-mass-weighted first moment about xc​ to define the yaw reference uniquely. This changes the decomposition of the observed motion, not the physical displacement.
For ξ=x−xc​, the recoil contribution to the forcing is
Rrec​=(mb​+m)(Y′′+ξΘ′′)+2UmΘ′+Um′Y′+Um′ξΘ′+U2m′Θ.
(10)
Insert R=Rd​+Rrec​ into the two compatibility integrals, using 1 and ξ as weights. This gives two coupled ordinary differential equations for the two recoil motions. Their principal acceleration matrix is
(∫0L​(mb​+m)dx∫0L​ξ(mb​+m)dx​∫0L​ξ(mb​+m)dx∫0L​ξ2(mb​+m)dx​).
(11)
For a nondegenerate positive mass distribution it is a positive-definite matrix: its quadratic form at (a,b) is ∫0L​(mb​+m)(a+bξ)2dx>0 unless a=b=0. Thus initial translation and yaw determine the recoil through these equations, and the corrected h supplies the two compatibility conditions. The four free-end conditions determine the bending moment together with two rigid recoil motions; they do not overdetermine a physically complete swimming motion.

In resistive-force theory, the flagellum is slender and moves at a small Reynolds number, so local viscous resistance is linear in its velocity and fluid and body inertia are neglected. Normal and tangential resistance coefficients are treated as positive constants, usually κN​>κT​. The approximation neglects distant hydrodynamic interactions along the filament, head-tail interactions, nearby boundaries and self-contact. The centreline is an inextensible filament with prescribed shape. Its local resistance tensor is
K=κT​tt+κN​(I−tt).
(12)
Since the filament velocity relative to distant fluid is −w, the fluid force on a filament element is Kwds. The equal and opposite force on the fluid has total axial component T. Projecting onto i gives
−T=∫0L​i⋅Kwds=(κT​−κN​)∫0L​(w⋅t)(t⋅i)ds+κN​∫0L​w⋅ids.
(13)
This establishes the thrust expression with its force-on-fluid convention.
For a uniform travelling wave, write the moving centreline as r(s,t)=rwave​(s−ct)+(V−U)ti, where s is material arc length and ∣rwave′​∣=1. Differentiating at fixed material label gives rt​=(V−U)i−ct. Hence
w=(U−V)i+ct.​
(14)
Let q=t⋅i=Xs​, α=⟨q⟩ and β=⟨q2⟩, where these brackets are contour-length averages. The axial wavelength divided by contour wavelength is α=V/c. For a filament comprising whole wave periods, or after the usual period averaging, c∫0L​qds=VL. This averaging is needed to avoid end-phase corrections for an arbitrary fraction of a wavelength. Substitution of w into the thrust expression now gives
T=(V−U)[(κT​−κN​)βL+κN​L]−κT​VL.
(15)
For γ=κT​/κN​ and r=1−γ, this is T/(κN​L)=Vr(1−β)−U(1−rβ). The swimming body is force-free, so the reaction thrust on the head-tail assembly balances its head drag. Thus T=δκN​LU, and linear resistive-force propulsion of a travelling filament gives
U=V1+δ−(1−γ)β(1−γ)(1−β)​.​
(16)
We henceforth assume 0<γ<1, δ≥0 and 0<β<1. Isotropic resistance, γ=1, gives no propulsion in this model.
The flagellar contribution to viscous dissipation is the positive quadratic form Pf​=∫w⋅Kwds. Expanding it, and using c⟨q⟩=V, gives
Pf​=κN​L{(V−U)2[1−(1−γ)β]−2γV(V−U)+γc2}.
(17)
There is also head dissipation δκN​LU2. Equivalently, the work of the flagellar deformation relative to the translating head is Pf​+UT; the force balance makes UT precisely the head dissipation. Consequently, with α=V/c,
κN​LU2E​=(UV​−1)2(βγ+1−β)−2γUV​(UV​−1)+α2U2γV2​+δ.​
(18)
This derives the rate of working, rather than merely inferring it from the thrust.
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives α2=⟨q⟩2≤⟨q2⟩=β. Equality requires q to be constant almost everywhere. For a planar periodic filament this is an ideal sawtooth with alternating transverse tangent and fixed axial tangent, or a limit with negligibly rounded corners. It is not an identity for a general sinusoidal wave, and the ideal calculation omits the elastic cost of sharp corners.
For the stipulated equality α2=β, put d=1+δ and eliminate V at fixed U using V/U=(d−rβ)/[r(1−β)]. Direct simplification yields
F(β):=κN​LU2E​=r2β(1−β)(d−rβ)(γd+δrβ)​.
(19)
The derivative is
F′(β)=r2β2(1−β)2r(δ2−γ)β2+2γd2β−γd2​.
(20)
Writing A=r(δ2−γ) and B=γd2, the numerator is Aβ2+2Bβ−B. It is negative at zero and equals (δ+γ)2>0 at one. Its derivative is positive throughout (0,1): this is immediate if A≥0; if A<0, its minimum derivative is 2(A+B)=2(δ+γ)2>0. Since F diverges at both endpoints, the unique stationary point is the global fixed-speed energy optimum for a planar flagellum:
β∗​=B+B2+AB​B​,A=(1−γ)(δ2−γ),B=γ(1+δ)2.​
(21)
This form includes the case A=0, when β∗​=1/2, without a removable division by zero. With no head drag it reduces to β∗​=1/(1+γ​).
The printed final optimality formula does not follow from the preceding thrust and power equations. For a concrete check, γ=1/2, δ=0 gives F=(2−β)/[β(1−β)]. The derived minimum is at β=2−2​. The printed formula instead gives β2=6​−2, hence β≈0.67044, where F′=0 and the expenditure is greater. Thus the thrust and working formulas can both be established as above, but the last printed conclusion must be replaced by the derived optimum.

Neglecting cell rotational inertia, the light and viscous torque sum to zero. Therefore the angular velocity is Ω=ω/2+(p×L)/α. A material orientation evolves by p˙​=Ω×p. The vector triple-product identity gives
p˙​=21​ω×p+αL−(p⋅L)p​.​
(22)
The last term is the projection of L/α onto the tangent plane to the unit sphere. In particular, p⋅p˙​=0, as required to preserve the unit orientation. It aligns the swimmer towards the light; the vorticity rotates that orientation.
For rotational diffusion coefficient Dr​, the phototactic orientation Fokker-Planck equation balances deterministic orientation drift with diffusion on the sphere. Its density is normalized with respect to solid angle, not dθdϕ. When ε=0, there is no azimuthal drift and the zero-current condition is f0,θ​=−λsinθf0​. Thus f0​=ηeλcosθ, and normalization gives
1=2πη∫0π​eλcosθsinθdθ=4πηλsinhλ​,η=4πsinhλλ​.​
(23)
The limiting density at λ=0 is 1/(4π).
To justify the angular dependence of the first correction, define the operators
A=∇p2​+λ(sinθ∂θ​+2cosθ),R=sinϕ∂θ​+cotθcosϕ∂ϕ​.
(24)
The steady Fokker-Planck equation is Af=−εRf. Its regular expansion f=f0​+εf(1)+O(ε2) therefore gives
Af(1)=−Rf0​=ληsinθeλcosθsinϕ.
(25)
The coefficients of A are independent of ϕ, so each azimuthal Fourier harmonic is preserved. The forcing has only the sine harmonic with azimuthal number one. There can be no extra unforced harmonic in a smooth normalized first correction: writing a homogeneous solution as f0​h gives A(f0​h)=∇p​⋅(f0​∇p​h). Multiplication by h and integration by parts on the sphere gives ∫f0​∣∇p​h∣2dΩ=0, hence h is constant. Normalization ∫f(1)dΩ=0 removes that multiple of f0​. It follows that
f=ηeλcosθ+εηsinϕg(θ)+O(ε2).​
(26)
No explicit calculation of g is needed. Its dependence is on θ and the parameter λ, and its behavior at the poles must make the density smooth.
In the stated spherical coordinates, p=cosθex​+sinθcosϕey​+sinθsinϕez​. Azimuthal integration makes the leading y,z components and the first-order x,y corrections vanish. The surviving coefficients are
K0​=2πη∫0π​cosθeλcosθsinθdθ,K1​=πη∫0π​g(θ)sin2θdθ.
(27)
Thus the small-vorticity phototactic orientation response is
⟨p⟩=K0​ex​+εK1​ez​+O(ε2).​
(28)
Both coefficients depend only on λ. In particular, K0​ is the Langevin function of λ and is positive for positive light strength; its integral representation above suffices.

The cell conservation equation equates the change of cell number density n to minus the divergence of its total flux. The flux nu is advection by the water, nVs​⟨p⟩ is the mean swimming flux, and −D⋅∇n is the dispersal flux. The tensor D describes effective translational diffusion, whereas Dr​ in part (i) describes rotational diffusion; they have different dimensions. This conservation law neglects birth, death and sources at the walls.
The equation ∇⋅u=0 expresses incompressible flow. In the momentum equation, ρ is the reference suspension density, ρ[∂t​u+(u⋅∇)u] is material acceleration times density, −∇pe​ is the pressure force after subtracting the pure-water hydrostatic pressure, and μ∇2u is the viscous force for constant dynamic viscosity μ in a Newtonian fluid. Since n counts cells per volume, g′ in −g′nez​ is excess downward weight per cell; it is not an acceleration. The small cell-fluid density difference permits the Boussinesq approximation: retain that difference in buoyancy while using constant ρ elsewhere. Diluteness permits neglect of cell-cell interactions, changes of viscosity, and active cell stresses. Any separate settling speed is assumed negligible or already incorporated in the specified mean drift. The light strength and swimming speed are uniform.
Consider the intended long-channel bulk approximation. At the sidewalls impose zero normal cell flux and the no-slip boundary condition. The closed ends require zero total fluid flux through each horizontal section, so ∫−aa​wdx=0; localized turning regions are omitted from the bulk equations. The mean concentration is ∫−aa​ndx=2an0​. The final paragraph below distinguishes these bulk conditions from exact closed-end cell conservation.
For u=w(x)ez​, the vorticity is −w′(x)ey​. Hence ε=−w′/(2Dr​), and the leading horizontal mean swimming direction is K0​ex​. With D=DI and n=n(x), steady cell conservation equation says that Jx​=Vs​K0​n−Dn′ is constant. Its wall value is zero, so n′=βn with β=Vs​K0​/D. The phototactic concentration in a vertical channel is therefore
β=DVs​K0​​,n(x)=n1​eβx,n1​=sinh(aβ)n0​aβ​.​
(29)
This result neglects the O(ε2) correction to horizontal swimming. For positive phototaxis, β>0 and cells accumulate towards the illuminated wall x=a.
The steady inertial terms vanish for w=w(x), since u⋅∇=w∂z​. The horizontal momentum equation gives ∂x​pe​=0. The vertical momentum equation then requires a constant excess pressure gradient G=∂z​pe​ and gives μw′′=G+g′n1​eβx. Twice integrating produces
w(x)=Ceβx+2μG​x2+Ax+B,C=μβ2g′n1​​.
(30)
Set b=aβ. The two no-slip boundary conditions give A=−Csinhb/a and B=−Ccoshb−Ga2/(2μ). The zero fluid-flux condition follows by integrating this expression:
0=2C(βsinhb​−acoshb)−3μ2Ga3​.
(31)
Consequently the remaining constants are
G=b23g′n0​​(1−bcothb),A=−μβg′n0​​,B=−μβ2g′n1​coshb​−2μGa2​.​
(32)
The resulting velocity can also be written without separate polynomial constants as
w=C[eβx−coshb−ax​sinhb]+2μG​(x2−a2).​
(33)
Here G is the zero-flux pressure gradient in a cell-driven channel. It is negative for b>0: pressure decreases upwards, creating the upward pressure force that balances the excess cell weight and drives the return flow. Its magnitude is determined by zero net fluid transport, not by prescribing pressure independently. In the zero-light limit, the continuous result is n=n0​, G=−g′n0​ and w=0.
For the requested large-b sketch, cells occupy a boundary layer of thickness β−1 near x=a. Put ξ=x/a and W=g′n0​a2/(μb). The exact dimensionless profile above is
Ww​=sinhbebξ​−cothb−ξ+23​(cothb−b1​)(1−ξ2).
(34)
Away from the illuminated-wall layer it tends to (1+ξ)(1−3ξ)/2. There is broad upward return flow for −1<ξ<1/3 and downward flow on the illuminated side. The outer maximum is 2W/3 at ξ=−1/3. Near ξ=1, the exponential term is approximately 2e−b(1−ξ) and restores the no-slip boundary condition from the negative outer velocity to zero at the wall. The finite-b profile shown below retains that layer. The small-vorticity assumption must still be enforced, for example by sufficiently weak excess weight or a sufficiently dilute suspension; b≫1 alone does not ensure ∣w′∣/(2Dr​)≪1.
There is a genuine global qualification to the bulk construction. For an exactly steady channel with impermeable ends as well as impermeable sidewalls, the closed-channel cell-flux constraint also requires ∫Jz​dx=0 at every height. In this bulk ansatz the first-order orientation closure gives
Jz​=n(w−2Dr​Vs​K1​​w′),∫−aa​Jz​dx=(1+2Dr​Vs​βK1​​)∫−aa​nwdx,
(35)
since integration by parts, n′=βn and w(±a)=0 imply ∫nw′=−β∫nw. Moreover, multiply μw′′=G+g′n by w and integrate. The wall values and zero fluid flux give
g′∫−aa​nwdx=−μ∫−aa​(w′)2dx<0
(36)
for a nontrivial profile. Thus zero axial cell flux generally fails unless the extra factor 1+Vs​βK1​/(2Dr​) vanishes, or the flow is trivial. A complete steady closed-channel solution generically needs axial concentration variation. The explicit profile solves the stated bulk equations, sidewall conditions, concentration normalization and zero fluid-flux condition; it is not, for arbitrary parameters, an exact globally closed steady cell distribution. Ignoring end regions gives the intended bulk approximation, but does not by itself prove the stronger global existence claim.
