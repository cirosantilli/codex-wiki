A chain complex consists of abelian groups Cq​ and homomorphisms ∂q​:Cq​→Cq−1​ with ∂q−1​∂q​=0. Its groups of chain cycles and chain boundaries are Zq​=ker∂q​ and Bq​=im∂q+1​; the identity ensures Bq​⊆Zq​. Its homology groups are
Hq​(C∗​)=Zq​/Bq​.
(1)
For a nonnegative complex, take Cq​=0 for q<0.
The singular chain group Cq​(X) is the free abelian group on continuous maps σ:Δq→X, called singular simplices. With δi​ the inclusion of the face opposite vertex i, define
∂σ=∑i=0q​(−1)iσ∘δi​.
(2)
Deleting any two vertices in the two possible orders gives the same face with opposite signs, proving ∂2=0. This defines the singular chain complex and its singular homology. A continuous map f induces the chain map f#​(σ)=f∘σ; it commutes with the boundary because composition commutes with restriction to faces.
To prove homotopy invariance of homology, let F:X×I→Y have endpoints f,g. Triangulate Δq×I by the q+1 simplices with ordered vertices
(v0​,0),…,(vi​,0),(vi​,1),…,(vq​,1),0≤i≤q.
(3)
Let pi​:Δq+1→Δq×I be their affine parameterizations. The singular prism operator is
Pq​(σ)=∑i=0q​(−1)iF∘(σ×1I​)∘pi​.
(4)
On taking boundaries, shared internal faces of consecutive prism simplices cancel. The top and bottom faces give g#​σ−f#​σ, and the remaining faces are −Pq−1​(∂σ) with their induced signs. Thus
∂P+P∂=g#​−f#​.
(5)
For a chain cycle z, g#​z−f#​z=∂Pz is a chain boundary, so f∗​=g∗​ on homology. If f is a homotopy equivalence, its homotopy inverse therefore induces an inverse to f∗​, proving that singular homology depends only on homotopy type.
Use the standard CW complex for an orientable genus-three surface: one vertex, six edges a1​,b1​,a2​,b2​,a3​,b3​, and a two-cell whose attaching word is ∏j=13​[aj​,bj​]. The cellular homology theorem identifies this cellular calculation with singular homology. The boundary of every edge is zero, and each edge's total exponent in the attaching word is zero, so the two-cell boundary is zero. Hence
Hq​(Σ3​;Z)=⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z6,0,​q=0,2,q=1,otherwise.​​
(6)
Choose C1​ to be the nonseparating curve represented by a1​, and choose C2​ as a small circle bounding a disc in a surface patch. Then [C1​] is a nonzero primitive homology class, whereas the inclusion of C2​ induces zero on H1​. If a homotopy equivalence carried C1​ onto C2​, its restriction would factor through C2​ and kill [C1​], contradicting injectivity on first homology. This is the homology obstruction to carrying surface curves onto one another.
There is nevertheless such a continuous map. Map the edge a1​ homeomorphically onto S1 and all other edges to its base point. The two-cell attaching word then has winding number zero in S1, hence is null-homotopic and the map extends to r:Σ3​→S1. Compose r with a homeomorphism S1→C2​ and the inclusion of C2​ in the surface. This gives g(C1​)=C2​. Thus an arbitrary map can carry the first curve onto the second, but a homotopy equivalence cannot.
For an unambiguous drawing, realize Σ3​ as the double of a manifold formed from a disc with three holes. Its two copies are glued along all four boundary circles. The red curve below follows an arc from the outer boundary to the first hole on the front copy and returns along the matching arc on the back copy; it is a nonseparating curve. The blue circle is entirely in a disc patch on the front copy and is contractible.
