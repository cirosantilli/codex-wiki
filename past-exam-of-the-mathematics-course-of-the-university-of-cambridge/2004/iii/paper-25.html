The hypotheses for Kummer theory are that n≥1 is prime to the characteristic of K and that all nth roots of unity lie in K. Under these hypotheses, adjoining radicals produces abelian extensions of exponent dividing n, and conversely every such finite extension is obtained this way. The roots-of-unity hypothesis matters: without it, adjoining one radical need not give a Galois extension.
We first prove Hilbert's theorem 90, including its cocycle form. Distinct field automorphisms are linearly independent as functions into the field. Indeed, in a shortest nontrivial relation ∑i​ci​τi​(y)=0, choose x with τ1​(x)=τr​(x). Subtract τr​(x) times the relation at y from the relation at xy. This eliminates the last term while leaving the first nonzero, contradicting minimality. This is linear independence of distinct field embeddings.
Let L/K be finite Galois with group G, and suppose aσ​∈L∗ satisfy aστ​=aσ​σ(aτ​). By independence of the automorphisms, choose c∈L for which
b=∑τ∈G​aτ​τ(c)=0.
(1)
Then
σ(b)=∑τ​σ(aτ​)στ(c)=aσ−1​∑τ​aστ​στ(c)=aσ−1​b.
(2)
Thus every multiplicative cocycle is a coboundary:
aσ​=b/σ(b),H1(G,L∗)=0.​
(3)
For a cyclic group generated by σ of order m, an element a of norm one defines the cocycle aσi​=∏j=0i−1​σj(a); the condition at i=m is exactly NL/K​(a)=1. Hence
NL/K​(a)=1⟺a=b/σ(b) for some b∈L∗.​
(4)
The reverse implication follows by telescoping the product of conjugates.
Now let Δ be a finite subgroup of K∗/K∗n and put L=K(nΔ​). Finitely many representatives generate it; since μn​⊂K, the polynomials Xn−a split in L and are separable. Thus L/K is finite Galois. For σ∈G and [a]∈Δ, choose αn=a and define the Kummer pairing
⟨σ,[a]⟩=σ(α)/α∈μn​.
(5)
Changing the root or multiplying a by an nth power does not change this value. It is multiplicative in both arguments. Since the radicals generate L, it embeds G in Hom(Δ,μn​). Consequently G is abelian of exponent dividing n.
For completeness, establish perfection rather than just this injection. Let ΔL​ consist of all classes in K∗/K∗n having an nth root in L. The same construction gives an injective map ΔL​→Hom(G,μn​): the character is trivial precisely when the root lies in K. It is surjective by Hilbert 90. Given χ:G→μn​, apply the cocycle theorem to aσ​=χ(σ)−1; it gives σ(b)/b=χ(σ), so bn∈K and its class has the required character. A finite abelian group of exponent dividing n has as many μn​-characters as elements, by decomposition into cyclic factors. Therefore
∣G∣≤∣Δ∣≤∣ΔL​∣=∣G∣.
(6)
It follows that Δ=ΔL​ and
[L:K]=∣Δ∣,G≅Hom(Δ,μn​).​
(7)
Conversely, for any finite abelian L/K of exponent dividing n, Hilbert 90 supplies eigenvectors for every character. Their nth powers lie in K, and the intersection of their character kernels is trivial, so they generate L. This proves the Kummer classification and the inclusion-reversing duality between subgroups and intermediate extensions.
The simplest case is a cyclic extension of degree m∣n: it has a generator α with αm∈K, and its automorphisms multiply α by mth roots of unity. Quadratic extensions in characteristic different from two are the case n=2. The cohomological summary is the exact sequence 1→μn​→Ks∗​z↦zn​Ks∗​→1 together with Hilbert 90, giving H1(GK​,μn​)≅K∗/K∗n.

Normalize each finite-place valuation to have image Z. For a radical class [a], the residue of v(a) modulo n is well-defined, because replacing a by abn adds nv(b). A suitable finite set is
S=S∞​ ∪ {v<∞:v(n)>0} ∪ {v<∞:v(a)≡0(modn) for some [a]∈Δ}.​
(8)
One can compute the last union from any finite generating set of Δ. Each representative has only finitely many nonzero valuations, so this indeed defines a finite set depending only on n and the classes, not on a choice of representatives. Including all infinite places safely allows possible real-to-complex ramification.
Fix v∈/S and work over the completion Kv​. A representative has v(a)=nk, so a=πnku with u a unit. Adjoining an nth root of a is the same as adjoining an nth root of u. Since n is invertible in the residue field, Xn−uˉ is separable: its derivative nXn−1 has no common zero with it.
Take a finite extension of the finite residue field containing all roots of all these finitely many reduced polynomials. The corresponding finite unramified extension of Kv​ exists by the classification proved in Question 2. Hensel's lemma lifts each simple residue root to a root of the original polynomial in that unramified extension. Thus all the required radicals, and every conjugate root, lie in an unramified local extension. Every completion of the radical field at v is therefore unramified. This proves finite ramification support for radical extensions.
The local argument does not assume μn​⊂K: adjoining all roots proves the stronger unramified assertion for the splitting field, and hence for any field obtained by choosing the radicals. For n=1 the radical extension is trivial; the displayed set is still valid.

v(f(ar+1​))≥2v(f(ar​)),v(ar+1​−ar​)=v(f(ar​)).
(9)

f(a)−f(b)=(a−b)(f′(b)+(a−b)h(a,b)).
(10)

In the absolute-value description, a place of a number field is an equivalence class of nontrivial absolute values, where equivalent absolute values give the same topology, or differ by a positive power. More generally this defines the rank-one absolute-value places used in weak approximation for a field. For a number field, finite places correspond to nonzero prime ideals of its ring of integers, while infinite places correspond to real embeddings and pairs of conjugate complex embeddings. The completion at v is denoted Kv​.
A valuation place can also be described by a valuation ring Ov​⊂K and its residue map into kv​, extended by sending elements outside that ring to infinity. The finite places above are the discrete-valuation instances of this description.
The weak approximation theorem states that for finitely many pairwise inequivalent nontrivial absolute values v1​,…,vr​, the diagonal image of K is dense in ∏i​Kvi​​. Equivalently, for arbitrary ai​∈Kvi​​ and ϵi​>0, there is a∈K such that
∣a−ai​∣vi​​<ϵi​(1≤i≤r).​
(17)
The places must be distinct: two requirements at the same completion need not be compatible. In the number-field setting this permits simultaneous prescribed finite congruences and archimedean approximations.

The idele group is the restricted product
JK​=∏v′​Kv∗​,
(18)
with respect to Ov∗​ at the finite places. Thus a=(av​) is an idele if all components are nonzero and av​ is a unit at all but finitely many finite places. Its topology has basic open sets which equal the full unit subgroup outside finitely many places; it is not just the subspace topology of the unrestricted product. The group is locally compact, and the diagonal K∗ consists of the principal ideles. The idèle class group is CK​=JK​/K∗.
The finite valuations give the idele-to-ideal homomorphism
(av​)⟼∏v<∞​pvv(av​)​.
(19)
Its kernel is the product of the finite unit groups and the infinite multiplicative groups; principal ideles map to principal fractional ideals. Thus further quotienting gives the ideal class group. The product of normalized local moduli defines ∣a∣J​; at complex places the normalized modulus is the square of the ordinary absolute value. The product formula says that principal ideles have modulus one. These constructions package ideals, units and archimedean conditions into one group.

One can explicitly connect this idelic formulation with the classical ideal law without assuming an idelic law in advance. A modulus of a number field records finitely many nonnegative exponents at finite places and a selected subset of real places. For a modulus m, let Um​ have factors 1+pvmv​​ at finite places dividing the modulus, full units at other finite places, positive real numbers at specified real places, and full infinite multiplicative groups otherwise. A central simple algebra over K is a finite-dimensional associative unital K-algebra A with no nonzero proper two-sided ideals and center exactly K. Its dimension is a square, dimK​A=n2. The Artin–Wedderburn theorem writes it as Mr​(D) for a uniquely determined central division algebra D up to isomorphism. One obtains this structure by taking a minimal left ideal and its commuting division endomorphism ring; the simple algebra acts as all endomorphisms over that ring. Over a suitable finite splitting field it becomes a full matrix algebra.
The Brauer group identifies two central simple algebras if their division-algebra parts agree, equivalently if tensoring each with suitable matrix algebras makes them isomorphic. The product is tensor product, the identity is the class of K, and the inverse of [A] is [Aop]. Indeed
A⊗K​Aop⟶EndK​(A),(a⊗b)(x)=axb
(25)
is an isomorphism for a central simple algebra, so the product class is split. This also explains why matrix size is irrelevant to the Brauer class. The order of the class is its period, while the degree of its division-algebra representative is its index; these are different notions in general.
For the cyclic algebra construction, take a cyclic extension L/K of degree m, a generator σ and a∈K∗. Set
A=(L/K,σ,a)=⨁i=0m−1​Lui,uℓ=σ(ℓ)u,um=a.
(26)
The multiplication is obtained by moving coefficients past u and replacing every um by a. It is associative: reducing a product of three monomials gives the same total exponent, the same power of a and the same iterated automorphisms in either order. The scalar a is fixed by σ and σm=1, so reduction is consistent.
An element commuting with every ℓ∈L has only its u0 coefficient nonzero, because σi=1 for 0<i<m. Commuting with u then forces that coefficient into K, proving that the center is K. To prove simplicity, choose a nonzero element of a nonzero ideal with the fewest nonzero monomial coefficients. Since u is invertible and each nonzero coefficient in L is invertible, normalize one term to be the constant one. If another term remains, its automorphism differs from the identity; commuting with a suitable element of L eliminates the constant term but not that other term. This gives a shorter nonzero element, a contradiction. The minimal element is therefore a unit, and the ideal is all of A. Thus A is central simple of dimension m2.
It splits over L. For example, over L represent ℓ by the diagonal matrix with entries ℓ,σ(ℓ),…,σm−1(ℓ), and let u shift the standard basis down one place, sending the first vector to a times the last. These matrices satisfy the defining relations. A primitive element of the separable extension L/K has distinct conjugates; the Vandermonde matrix of their powers is invertible. Thus the diagonal images span all diagonal matrices after scalar extension to L. Multiplying the diagonal matrix units by powers of the shift gives every matrix unit, proving A⊗K​L≅Mm​(L).
The particularly useful splitting criterion is
(L/K,σ,a) is split⟺a∈NL/K​L∗.​
(27)
If a=N(b), let the algebra act on L by multiplication for ℓ and by the semilinear operator bσ for u. Its mth power is multiplication by N(b)=a, so this gives an isomorphism with EndK​(L), by simplicity and dimension. Conversely, a split algebra acts on an m-dimensional K-space V. Restriction to the subfield L makes V one-dimensional over L, and u is a nonzero σ-semilinear operator. In an L-basis it is bσ, so um=a forces a=N(b). More generally the relative Brauer group split by a cyclic L/K is K∗/NL/K​L∗, with multiplication of parameters corresponding to addition of classes.
For a finite field, a finite division algebra is commutative by Wedderburn's little theorem. Therefore every central simple algebra is a matrix algebra and Br(Fq​)=0​. The cyclic-algebra viewpoint gives the same conclusion for cyclic classes because the norm between finite fields is surjective on their cyclic multiplicative groups.
For a nonarchimedean local field, the local Brauer invariant gives
Br(K)≅Q/Z.​
(28)
An unramified cyclic extension of degree m, with arithmetic Frobenius generator, gives invariant vK​(a)/m to its cyclic algebra. Question 2 shows that all units are norms; norm valuations are multiples of m. Thus the relative classes are Z/mZ, represented by powers of a uniformizer. Allowing all m realizes all invariants. At the infinite local fields, Br(C)=0, while Br(R)≅Z/2Z, with the nonzero class represented by Hamilton's quaternions and invariant 1/2.
For a number field, the Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorem supplies the exact sequence
0⟶Br(K)⟶⨁v​Br(Kv​) ∑v​invv​ ​Q/Z⟶0.
(29)
Hence a global algebra is determined by its finitely many nonzero local invariants, whose sum is zero; it is split precisely when all its local classes vanish. Every division algebra over a number field is cyclic, so cyclic algebras provide representatives for the global classes as well. Their norm parameters translate splitting into local norm conditions and the reciprocity constraint on the sum of invariants. The local invariant theorem, Wedderburn's little theorem and the global invariant theorem are the standard structural results used here; their role in the requested computation is explicit.

The existence theorem of global class field theory, together with reciprocity, gives an inclusion-reversing bijection between finite abelian extensions L/K inside a fixed algebraic closure and open finite-index subgroups of the idèle class group CK​. The subgroup for L is NL/K​CL​, and
CK​/NL/K​CL​≅Gal(L/K).​
(30)
Equivalently, for each modulus m, subgroups between the ray-principal group PK​(m) and the ideal group IK​(m) classify abelian extensions whose conductor divides m. Their quotients are identified with the corresponding Galois groups by the Artin map. Changing the modulus gives the same extension when the induced congruence subgroup is the same.
The conductor criterion says that a finite place has conductor exponent zero exactly when the extension is unramified there; a real place is absent from the conductor exactly when it stays real. Thus conductor one means unramified at every finite place and no real-to-complex ramification.
Apply the existence theorem to the open subgroup of CK​ which is the image of
U=∏v<∞​Ov∗​×∏v∣∞​Kv∗​.
(31)
The idele-to-ideal map identifies the quotient by this subgroup with the ordinary ideal class group, which is finite. Therefore its class field H exists and satisfies
Gal(H/K)≅Cl(K),[H:K]=hK​.​
(32)
Its conductor is one, so it is everywhere unramified. Conversely any everywhere-unramified abelian extension has conductor one; its Artin map kills U, hence factors through this quotient and corresponds to an extension contained in H. This proves that H is exactly the Hilbert class field, the maximal abelian everywhere-unramified extension. Here the ordinary ideal class group is appropriate because the infinite factors include all Kv∗​, rather than only the positive real factors used in a narrow ray class group.

Hence representation by x2+xy−57y2 is equivalent to p splitting in K with trivial Artin symbol in HK​/K, or to complete splitting of p in HK​/Q.
Let τp​∈S3​ be its rational Frobenius, defined up to conjugacy. Its restriction to the discriminant quadratic field is its sign. The splitting criterion for that quadratic field is
p splits in K⟺(p/229)=1.
(45)
For odd p this follows by quadratic reciprocity from the character of discriminant 229; for p=2 both criteria are negative since 229≡5(mod8). The polynomial is separable modulo p, and the degrees of its factors are the cycle lengths of τp​. Thus a root modulo p means that τp​ fixes a root. An even permutation of three letters with a fixed point is the identity: a nonidentity three-cycle has none. Therefore the two stated conditions together are exactly complete splitting in HK​, and
p=x2+xy−57y2 for some x,y∈Z⟺(p/229)=1 and X3−4X−1 has a root modulo p.​
(46)
This is prime representation by the norm form of Q of square root 229, with the norm sign and the prime two explicitly covered.
