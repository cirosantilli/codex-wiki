Choose depth z positive downwards and harmonic time dependence e−iωt. At normal incidence, P waves and the two polarizations of S waves are independent scalar problems: a P wave uses the longitudinal modulus ρα2, and an S wave uses the shear modulus ρβ2. In a uniform layer with wave speed c and seismic impedance q=ρc, write velocity and compressive traction as
V=Aeiωz/c+Be−iωz/c,P=−T=q(Aeiωz/c−Be−iωz/c).
(1)
At a perfectly bonded interface the velocity and traction are continuous. For incident velocity wave amplitude one from medium 1 into medium 2, these two conditions are 1+rv​=tv​ and q1​(1−rv​)=q2​tv​. Thus
rv​=q1​+q2​q1​−q2​​,tv​=q1​+q2​2q1​​.​
(2)
Reflection is determined by seismic impedance, not by speed alone. A step to larger seismic impedance reverses the reflected velocity/displacement field waveform; the pressure reflection coefficient has the opposite sign. Its transmitted pressure coefficient is tp​=2q2​/(q1​+q2​). Equal seismic impedance eliminates the reflection even when speed and wavelength change. At a free surface traction vanishes and velocity reflects with coefficient +1; at a rigid surface velocity reflects with coefficient −1.
The mean elastic-wave energy flux is 21​Re(PV∗)=q(∣A∣2−∣B∣2)/2 for positive real q. Therefore the reflected and transmitted energy fractions are
R=∣rv​∣2,T=q1​q2​​∣tv​∣2,R+T=1.​
(3)
A displacement field transmission coefficient greater than one is compatible with conservation of energy; its flux must be weighted by the receiving seismic impedance.
For many layers, use the seismic layer transfer matrix. A layer of thickness hj​ has wave phase thickness δj​=ωhj​/cj​ and
(VP​)z+hj​​=Mj​(VP​)z​,Mj​=(cosδj​iqj​sinδj​​isinδj​/qj​cosδj​​),detMj​=1.
(4)
This follows either by eliminating A,B between the two faces or by integrating V′=iωP/(ρc2) and P′=iωρV. For the complete stack put M=MN​⋯M1​=(AC​BD​). With upper and lower half-space impedances qa​,qb​, an outgoing lower wave satisfies Pb​=qb​Vb​. Solving this condition gives the input seismic impedance
Zin​=D−qb​Bqb​A−C​,R=qa​+Zin​qa​−Zin​​,T=qa​D+qb​A−C−qa​qb​B2qa​​.​
(5)
Here T is lower-face velocity divided by upper-face incident velocity, including the propagation wave phase. For lossless layers and real frequency, ∣R∣2+(qb​/qa​)∣T∣2=1. This supplies a sensitive check on any spreadsheet or numerical implementation; it is not correct simply to multiply the individual interface transmissions and discard the elastic waves.
The physical reason is repeated wave interference. In one layer between media 0 and 2, let rij​,tij​ denote the velocity coefficients above and δ=ωh/c1​. The first transmitted wave has wave amplitude t01​t12​eiδ. Every extra round trip supplies r10​r12​e2iδ. Summing the geometric series gives
T=1−r10​r12​e2iδt01​t12​eiδ​,R=r01​+1−r10​r12​e2iδt01​t10​r12​e2iδ​.
(6)
In the time domain the successive arrivals are delayed by 2h/c1​, producing reverberation. In the frequency domain the same delayed terms produce resonant maxima and minima. For a layer between identical half-spaces of impedance q0​,
T=[1+41​(q0​q1​​−q1​q0​​)2sin2δ]−1.​
(7)
At δ=nπ the layer transmits all the incident energy: the internally elastic waves cancel the direct reflection. Halfway between these resonances, a large impedance contrast gives strong reflection. For unequal exterior impedances, a quarter-wave layer with q12​=qa​qb​ transforms its lower load to Zin​=q12​/qb​=qa​, again giving zero reflection at its design frequency. This matching is narrow-band.
Periodic alternation produces a periodic seismic multilayer stop band. For a cell of two layers with total thickness d, the cell matrix has reciprocal eigenvalues e±iKd, so
cosKd=21​tr(M2​M1​)=cosδ1​cosδ2​−21​(q2​q1​​+q1​q2​​)sinδ1​sinδ2​.
(8)
When the absolute value of this expression exceeds one, the infinite periodic medium has no propagating cell mode; a long finite stack has exponentially small transmission. The energy is reflected, not absorbed. Pass bands instead contain oscillatory transmission peaks, becoming more numerous as the stack length increases. Disorder removes the exact cell periodicity but not multiple scattering or frequency-selective interference.
For a concrete reproducible investigation, take qH​/qL​=4, equal one-way travel times τ in both layer types, exterior impedance qL​, and frequency coordinate ν=2ωτ/π. At ν=1 each layer is a quarter wavelength; one cell has diagonal matrix entries −4,−1/4. For N cells,
T(1)=(4N+4−N)24​.
(9)
It is about 0.22145 for one cell, 6.10×10−5 for four cells and 9.31×10−10 for eight cells. The infinite-cell stop band is 2arcsin(0.8)/π<ν<2−2arcsin(0.8)/π. The accompanying calculation also shows the first transmitted arrivals for a single high-impedance slab: their wave amplitudes are 0.64(0.36)n at times (2n+1)τ. Their sum is one, consistent with unit zero-frequency transmission. These calculations illustrate both the spectrum and the pulse train without assuming access to the course's spreadsheet.
Finally, as ω→0 for a fixed finite stack, every Mj​→I, so the response approaches that of a direct interface between the exterior half-spaces. Equal exterior impedances give R→0,T→1. For wavelengths much longer than a repeating cell, expansion of Mj​ to first order instead gives an effective medium with averaged mass density ρeff​=∑hj​ρj​/d and harmonic-mean modulus Meff−1​=∑hj​/Mjmod​/d, where Mjmod​=ρj​cj2​. Its speed is Meff​/ρeff​​, not generally the arithmetic mean of the layer speeds. Attenuation makes the energy balance an inequality, while for a fixed oblique horizontal slowness the scalar SH calculation uses qj​=μj​pβ,j​ and δj​=ωpβ,j​hj​. Oblique P-SV waves require coupled displacement field and traction matrices because of mode conversion at a planar elastic interface. Thick evanescent layers require scattering or impedance recursion rather than exponentially ill-conditioned transfer-matrix products.

Work in a meridional R,z plane and denote the interface height by Z(R), to distinguish it from an observation coordinate. Put
s=z0​(α−αf​),d=α2−αf2​​,D(R)=s2+d2R2​.
(10)
The interface is Z=(αs+αf​D)/d2, so Z′=αf​R/D. Its unit normal into the fluid is proportional to (−Z′,1). Consequently the angle between this normal and the vertical satisfies
tanθz​=Z′=Dαf​R​.​
(11)
The outgoing source P wave has ray direction proportional to (R,Z), tilting outwards by arctan(R/Z) from the vertical, while the normal tilts inwards. Thus θP​=arctan(R/Z)+θz​. The tangent addition formula gives
tanθP​=Z−RZ′R+ZZ′​.
(12)
Direct substitution simplifies its denominator to s(αD+αf​s)/(d2D) and its numerator to αR(αD+αf​s)/(d2D). Hence
tanθP​=sαR​=z0​(α−αf​)αR​.​
(13)
The denominator is positive, so these are the physical incidence angles in [0,π/2); the transmitted P wave reaches the fluid rather than propagating back into the solid.

The angles from part (i) have sines
sinθP​=s2+α2R2​αR​,sinθz​=s2+α2R2​αf​R​.
(14)
By Snell law for elastic and acoustic waves, sinθf​/αf​=sinθP​/α. Both angles lie in [0,π/2), so θf​=θz​. The outgoing fluid ray tilts away from the inward-tilted normal by exactly its tilt from vertical; it therefore travels vertically upwards. This is hyperbolic interface collimation of P-waves.
The source-to-interface distance satisfies
r(R)=R2+Z(R)2​=d2αD+αf​s​.
(15)
One can verify this by squaring, using D2−s2=d2R2. Consequently
αr​−αf​Z​=−ααf​s​=αz0​​−αf​z0​​.
(16)
Adding the vertical fluid travel time from the interface to height z gives
τ(R,z)=αr(R)​+αf​z−Z(R)​=αz0​​+αf​z−z0​​.​
(17)
Thus surfaces of equal arrival time in the fluid are horizontal planes. Their leading wave amplitude may vary with R: a planar wavefront does not imply a spatially uniform plane-wave solution at every order.

Use local coordinates with the interface z=0, solid below and fluid above, and choose the P-SV displacement potentials convention ux​=ϕx​−ψz​, uz​=ϕz​+ψx​. Let the reflected P and SV potential wave amplitudes be A,B, while the transmitted P wave amplitude is TP​. All four waves have the same tangential slowness
p=αsinθP​​=βsinθS​​=αf​sinθf​​,qP​=αcosθP​​,qS​=βcosθS​​,qf​=αf​cosθf​​.
(18)
The incident and transmitted retarded times have negative qP​z,qf​z, while elastic waves have positive qP​z,qS​z. At the interface all waveform derivatives therefore have the same argument. displacement field continuity gives
qP​(1−A)+pB=qf​TP​.(1)
(19)
With Lamé parameters μ=ρβ2 and λ=ρ(α2−2β2), the shear and normal stresses follow directly from differentiation:
σxz​=μ(2ϕxz​+ψxx​−ψzz​),σzz​=λΔϕ+2μ(ϕzz​+ψxz​).
(20)
The fluid has no shear traction, so the coefficient of f′′ in σxz​ must vanish:
α2sin2θP​​(1−A)−β2cos2θS​​B=0.(2)
(21)
The coefficient of f′′ in the solid stress is ρ[cos2θS​(1+A)−sin2θS​B]; in the fluid it is ρf​TP​. Thus
ρ[cos2θS​(1+A)−sin2θS​B]=ρf​TP​.(3)
(22)
These are the three required matching conditions; tangential displacement field need not be continuous at a solid–inviscid-fluid boundary.
Put C=cos2θS​ and X=1−A. From (2), B=(β2/α2)(sin2θP​/C)X. In (1), the coefficient of X simplifies because C+2(β2/α2)sin2θP​=1. Hence X=(αCcosθf​)/(αf​cosθP​)TP​. Substitute this into (3) to obtain
2ρC=[ρf​+αf​cosθP​ραcosθf​​(C2+α2β2​sin2θP​sin2θS​)]TP​.
(23)
Therefore the solid-fluid P-wave transmission coefficient is
TP​=ρf​αf​cosθP​+ρα[cos22θS​+(β2/α2)sin2θP​sin2θS​]cosθf​2ραf​cos2θS​cosθP​​.​
(24)
Division by C was only an elimination device; the final expression extends continuously to C=0. The normal-incidence value is 2ραf​/(ρα+ρf​αf​). This is a potential coefficient; longitudinal displacement field transmission additionally multiplies it by α/αf​. The original PDF's bracket lies in the denominator and contains β2/α2 and cosθf​. The converted TeX misplaces the bracket, substitutes θ2 for β2, and loses the final cosine.

The leading source displacement field is
uinc​=−αrr​f′(t−r/α)−r2r​f(t−r/α).
(25)
The second term is one derivative smoother at the leading arrival. At each surface point, use its tangent plane, the angles from (i)–(ii), and the local solid-fluid P-wave transmission coefficient. The incident potential coefficient is 1/r(R), so the transmitted leading potential coefficient is
Aϕ​(R)=r(R)TP​(R)​.
(26)
Transport this coefficient along the vertical fluid rays. Their transverse area does not change with height, so ray-tube conservation for a P-wave jump gives no further geometrical spreading. The leading displacement field coefficient is consequently
uzlead​(R,z,t)=−Au​(R)f′(t−τ),Au​(R)=αf​r(R)TP​(R)​.​
(27)
This is the transmitted jump at a collimating solid-fluid interface. Radial derivatives of Aϕ​ multiply f, and therefore do not contribute to the first derivative jump carried by f′. A varying transverse wave amplitude does generate smoother later terms, so this construction is a leading-front result, not a complete global plane-wave field.
Since the source is zero before t=0 and is C2 there, f(0)=f′(0)=f′′(0)=0. Thus displacement field itself does not jump. If J=[f(3)(0)] exists and is the first nonzero jump, the leading discontinuity is the acceleration jump [∂t2​u]=−Au​(R)Jez​. More generally, a first jump in f(m) gives [∂tm−1​u]=−Au​(R)[f(m)]ez​. The supplied regularity alone does not guarantee a nonzero discontinuity: a source smooth to all orders may have none. The geometrical transfer factor Au​ is nevertheless determined.
At R=0, r=z0​ and all three angles vanish. Hence
Aϕ​(0)=z0​(ρα+ρf​αf​)2ραf​​,Au​(0)=z0​(ρα+ρf​αf​)2ρ​.​
(28)
For large R, put C∞​=1−2β2/α2 and retain s=z0​(α−αf​), d=α2−αf2​​. The relevant limits are
r=dαR​+O(1),cosθP​=αRs​+O(R−3),cosθf​=αd​+O(R−2),cos2θS​=C∞​+O(R−2).
(29)
When C∞​=0, the square-bracket factor in the denominator of TP​ is C∞2​+O(R−1), so
TP​=αdC∞​R2αf​s​+O(R−2),Aϕ​(R)=α2C∞​R22αf​s​+O(R−3),Au​(R)=α2C∞​R22s​+O(R−3).​
(30)
These are signed waveform coefficients; their absolute values give wave amplitude magnitudes. The leading displacement field decays as R−2 rather than R−1 because grazing incidence adds a small transmission factor to spherical spreading. The leading coefficient is independent of fluid and solid mass densities, although the exact wave amplitude and its higher-order terms depend on them.
The assumptions do not exclude β2=α2/2, where C∞​=0 and the preceding division is invalid. In this special case cos2θS​=cos2θP​ exactly, and the square-bracket factor is cosθP​+O(cos3θP​). Keeping both denominator terms gives
Au​(R)=α3(ρf​αf​+ρd)R32ρs2d​+O(R−4),Aϕ​=αf​Au​.​
(31)
It has no R−2 term. This exceptional case matters when requesting an asymptotic formula without an additional restriction on the elastic moduli.

Write ω=u+iv, with v>0, and a chosen square root kβ​=a+ib. Squaring gives
a2−b2=β2u2−v2​−k2,ab=β2uv​.
(32)
One cannot have b=0: the second relation would force u=0, and then the first would require the square of a real number to be negative. Consequently
Repβ​=Reu+iva+ib​=∣ω∣2au+bv​=∣ω∣2v​(b+β2bu2​).
(33)
The bracket has the sign of b, proving the causal vertical wavenumber branch equivalence
Imkβ​>0⟺Repβ​>0.​
(34)
This selects the square root analytically in the upper frequency half-plane. At ω=iv it is kβ​=iv2/β2+k2​; continuation supplies the outgoing branch for other frequencies. The expression β−2−p2​ must use this inherited branch rather than an independently chosen square root. For positive real frequency its limit is positive real for a propagating wave and positive imaginary for an evanescent wave.

Let the shear-horizontal wave displacement field be U(z)eikx−iωt. Its velocity is V=−iωU, and its traction is T=μU′. The scalar shear-wave equation gives
(μU′)′+(ρω2−μk2)U=0.
(35)
With p=k/ω and pβ2​=β−2−p2, the first-order velocity–traction system is
V′=−μiω​T,T′=−iωμpβ2​V.
(36)
These equations remain valid for depth-dependent μ,ρ; differentiating μU′ is essential and one must not incorrectly use μU′′ when μ varies.
Where V=0, define the SH input impedance Z=−T/V. Differentiating T=−ZV and substituting the two equations gives the SH impedance Riccati equation:
Z′=−iω(μZ2​−μpβ2​),V′=iωμZ​V.​
(37)
The second relation is a one-way scalar equation once the complete SH input impedance is known. It does not discard reflections: Z describes the actual superposition of both wave directions. At a zero of V, Z may have a pole while the velocity–traction system stays regular.

Use the quadratic spectral flux Sz​=−21​Re(V∗T). For positive real material mass density and shear modulus, differentiation of the velocity–traction system gives
Sz′​=−21​Re[μiω∗​∣T∣2−iωμpβ2​∣V∣2].
(38)
Because k is real, μpβ2​=ρ−μk2/ω2. If v=Imω>0, then Re(iω∗)=v and Re(−iωμpβ2​)=v(ρ+μk2/∣ω∣2). This proves complex-frequency SH flux monotonicity:
Sz′​=−2v​[μ∣T∣2​+(ρ+∣ω∣2μk2​)∣V∣2]<0.​
(39)
Strictness holds for a nontrivial wavefield: if V,T vanished together at one point, uniqueness of the first-order system would make the entire solution zero. The zero solution is the necessary exception to the literal strict inequality. Across a bonded material boundary V,T, and hence Sz​, are continuous; integrating the negative derivative gives strict decrease across any positive-depth interval as well.
Where the SH input impedance is finite, Sz​=21​ReZ∣V∣2. If ReZ(z1​)>0, then Sz​(z1​)>0 and all shallower fluxes are larger and positive. They cannot have V=0, since that would make Sz​=0. Thus ReZ(z)>0 for every z<z1​. Likewise, negative real SH input impedance at z0​ gives negative flux throughout z>z0​, excludes velocity zeros there and implies ReZ(z)<0 for every z>z0​.
In a uniform region let q=μpβ​ on the causal vertical wavenumber branch. The constant Riccati solutions are Z=±q, and integrating the one-way equation gives
V(z)∝e±iωpβ​z=e±ikβ​z.​
(40)
Since Req>0, the plus solution carries positive downward flux and the minus solution negative upward flux. Writing kβ​=a+ib with b>0, their moduli are proportional to e−bz and ebz respectively: each decays in its direction of energy flow.
For complex frequency the displayed quadratic form is the spectral flux diagnostic specified in the question; a genuinely periodic time average is recovered on the real-frequency axis. The decrease here comes from the upper-half-plane convergence parameter, not from material dissipation. At real frequency in a lossless layer the corresponding flux is constant.

Let zr​ be either endpoint at which Zr​ is prescribed, and put dz​=z−zr​, q=μpβ​ and κ=ωpβ​. Integrating the constant-coefficient velocity–traction system gives
(V(z)T(z)​)=(cosκdz​−iqsinκdz​​−isin(κdz​)/qcosκdz​​)(Vr​−Zr​Vr​​).
(41)
Taking the ratio −T/V yields the uniform-layer SH impedance update and its accompanying velocity:
Z(z)=cosκdz​+i(Zr​/q)sinκdz​Zr​cosκdz​+iqsinκdz​​,V(z)=Vr​[cosκdz​+iqZr​​sinκdz​].​
(42)
The initial value Vr​ sets an arbitrary normalization; specifying impedance alone cannot specify the overall wave wave amplitude. Positive dz​ propagates data from z0​, while negative dz​ propagates a specified load from z1​, so the same expressions solve both requested initial-value problems.
Expanding sine and cosine gives
V(z)=2Vr​​(1+qZr​​)eiκ(z−zr​)+2Vr​​(1−qZr​​)e−iκ(z−zr​).​
(43)
Absorbing the reference-point phases into the two constants gives the requested forward/backward exponential superposition. Equivalently, wherever the ratios are defined,
Z(z)+qZ(z)−q​=Zr​+qZr​−q​e−2iκ(z−zr​).
(44)
The exceptional values Zr​=q,−q give the pure directional solutions. If a denominator in the impedance formula vanishes, propagate the finite pair (V,T) through that point; this is an impedance-chart pole, not a failure of the physical solution. The upper-half-plane assumptions ensure q=0.

In the lower uniform half-space the causal vertical wavenumber branch has Imkβ,b​>0. The downward factor eikβ,b​(z−zb​) decays as z→+∞, whereas the upward factor grows there and represents an incoming wave from the lower infinity. The outgoing radiation condition therefore retains only the downward factor and gives
Z(zb​)=qb​=μb​pβ,b​.​
(45)
For each uniform layer j of thickness hj​, work upwards from its known bottom load Zj,b​. With qj​=μj​pβ,j​ and δj​=ωpβ,j​hj​, part (iv) gives
Zj,t​=cosδj​−i(Zj,b​/qj​)sinδj​Zj,b​cosδj​−iqj​sinδj​​.​
(46)
velocity and traction are continuous across a bonded interface, so this top impedance becomes the bottom load of the next shallower layer. Repeat up to za​ to obtain Zin​=Z(za​). Poles can be handled by carrying (V,T) or the reciprocal ratio. Since the lower load has positive real part, part (iii) actually ensures that the outgoing whole-stack impedance stays finite with positive real part at all shallower depths in the upper frequency half-plane.
The upper half-space has directional impedance qa​=μa​pβ,a​. At za​, an incident velocity V0​ and reflected velocity RV0​ give
V(za​)=V0​(1+R),T(za​)=−qa​V0​(1−R).
(47)
Taking their ratio and solving establishes reflection from a layered SH impedance:
Zin​=qa​1+R1−R​,R=qa​+Zin​qa​−Zin​​.​
(48)
Thus no individual deeper reflection coefficients are needed after Zin​ is known: it contains all underlying interfaces and their coherent multiples. A matched load gives R=0; the real-frequency limits of zero traction and zero velocity give R=+1 and R=−1. For real frequency and propagating upper and lower half-space waves, the usual energy-flux balance provides a further check on the computed reflection and transmission. A stable implementation for thick layers uses the ratio update or its decaying-exponential version instead of multiplying growing transfer matrices.
