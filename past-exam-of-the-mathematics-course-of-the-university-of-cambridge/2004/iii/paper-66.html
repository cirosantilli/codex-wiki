The separated disk modes. Away from the plane of a razor-thin disk, there is no volume source, so the Newtonian gravitational potential obeys the Laplace equation in cylindrical coordinates. Separation of variables gives
RJ(RJ′)′​=−ZZ′′​=−k2,J′′+R1​J′+k2J=0,Z′′−k2Z=0.
(1)
Here k>0 is a radial wavenumber, with units of inverse length. Regularity on the axis selects the Bessel function of the first kind J0​(kR) rather than the singular second solution. Decay away from the disk, continuity across it and reflection symmetry select Z=e−k∣z∣. These conditions apply to each nonzero-wavenumber mode; a uniform sheet is a separate zero-mode problem.
Integrate the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity across the disk to obtain the jump condition
Φz​(R,0+)−Φz​(R,0−)=4πGΣ(R).
(2)
For the unit-amplitude separated mode, the jump is −2kJ0​(kR). Thus
Φk​=e−k∣z∣J0​(kR),Σk​=−2πGk​J0​(kR).​
(3)
An individual basis density is signed; positive physical surface densities are built by superposition.
An arbitrary axisymmetric surface density. Define its order-zero Hankel transform by
Σ(k)=∫0∞​Σ(R′)J0​(kR′)R′dR′,Σ(R)=∫0∞​Σ(k)J0​(kR)kdk.
(4)
Matching the coefficients of the mode densities gives the Hankel representation of thin-disk gravity,
Φ(R,z)=−2πG∫0∞​Σ(k)J0​(kR)e−k∣z∣dk.​
(5)
The boundary conditions exclude added external fields. For sufficiently decaying surface density, this is the isolated-disk potential; for infinite scale-free disks, only potential differences or regularized forces need be finite.
The Mestel disk. Put C=Σ0​R0​. The Bessel function integral ∫0∞​J0​(kR′)dR′=1/k, understood with a decaying regulator, gives Σ=C/k. Differentiate the potential before removing the regulator and use J0′​=−J1​:
ΦR​(R,0)=2πGC∫0∞​J1​(kR)dk=R2πGC​.
(6)
The last integral is 1/R, since ∫0∞​J1​(u)du=[−J0​(u)]0∞​=1. Therefore the circular speed and enclosed mass are
vc2​=RΦR​=2πGΣ0​R0​=:v02​,M(R)=2π∫0R​Σ(R′)R′dR′=2πΣ0​R0​R.​
(7)
Consequently vc2​=GM(R)/R. This equality follows from the specific Mestel disk calculation; the spherical shell theorem cannot be applied to general disks. Its total mass and absolute potential referenced to infinity diverge. A convenient finite midplane reference is Φ(R,0)=v02​log(R/R0​).
The rotating distribution function. Use specific energy E=(vR2​+vϕ2​)/2+Φ and Lz​=Rvϕ​. For the printed positive exponential, take ε=−E and s:=σ>0; s has units of squared velocity. If instead ε means ordinary total energy and σ>0, the velocity integral diverges, so that literal convention cannot define the requested galactic distribution function. Equivalently one can retain ordinary energy and choose the printed σ negative.
With the binding-energy convention, write the amplitude as A to distinguish it from the function:
f=A(Rvϕ​)qexp[−2svR2​+vϕ2​​](R/R0​)−v02​/s,vϕ​>0.
(8)
Integration over both radial velocities and positive azimuthal velocities gives
Σ(R)​=ARq(R/R0​)−v02​/s2πs​2(2s)(q+1)/2​Γ(2q+1​)=ARq(R/R0​)−v02​/sπ​2q/2s(q+2)/2Γ(2q+1​).​
(9)
The half-line integral follows by setting u=vϕ2​/(2s) in the defining Gamma function integral and requires q>−1. Matching its radial power and normalization to the Mestel disk yields the rotating Mestel disk distribution function parameters
q=sv02​​−1,F=A=π​2q/2s(q+2)/2Γ((q+1)/2)Σ0​R0−q​​.​
(10)
Since v02​,s>0, the required integrability condition is automatic. The amplitude uses the stated potential reference; changing the additive potential constant changes A without changing the physical distribution.

First velocity moment. Assume a stationary, spherical, nonstreaming collisionless stellar system with constant mass-to-light ratio Υ. Use a luminosity-weighted galactic distribution function, with luminosity density λ. Multiplying the Collisionless Boltzmann equation by vi​ and integrating over velocities, with its velocity-space boundary terms vanishing, gives
∂j​(λ⟨vi​vj​⟩)=−λ∂i​Φ.
(11)
Spherical symmetry makes the stress tensor
Tij​=qt​δij​+(q−qt​)r2xi​xj​​,q=λσr2​,qt​=λσt2​.
(12)
Here σt2​ is one tangential component, as in the paper; the total tangential variance is 2σt2​. Its radial divergence is q′+2(q−qt​)/r, since the divergence of the radial unit vector is 2/r. With the velocity-anisotropy parameter β=1−σt2​/σr2​, and enclosed mass M(r)=ΥL(r), this gives the Spherical Jeans equation
q′+r2βq​=−λΦ′(r)=−γr2λL(r)​,γ=GΥ.​
(13)
This uses a constant ratio of total gravitating mass to luminosity, rather than an arbitrary independent dark halo.
Projection and elimination of anisotropy. Write R for projected radius and r for spherical radius. The Abel transform of the luminosity density gives
μ(R)=2∫R∞​r2−R2​λ(r)rdr​,
(14)
whose inverse is the displayed density inversion in the paper. At a point on this line of sight, sin2θ=R2/r2. The line-of-sight second stellar velocity moment is σr2​cos2θ+σt2​sin2θ=σr2​(1−βR2/r2). Therefore the measured quantity J(R)=μ(R)σp2​(R) satisfies
J(R)=2∫R∞​(1−β(r)r2R2​)r2−R2​q(r)rdr​.
(15)
The Spherical Jeans equation gives βq=−rq′/2−γλL/(2r). Substitution yields
J(R)=2∫R∞​r2−R2​q(r)rdr​+R2∫R∞​r2−R2​q′(r)dr​+γR2∫R∞​r2r2−R2​λ(r)L(r)dr​.
(16)
The inner γL printed in the PDF's definition of p must therefore be λL: the literal expression has an extra gravitational factor and lacks the density. Both this derivation and dimensional consistency require the correction. Define the projected spherical Jeans residual by
p(R)=J(R)−γR2∫R∞​r2r2−R2​λ(r)L(r)dr​.
(17)
To express it entirely in terms of the radial stress, put
A(R)=∫R∞​r2−R2​q(r)rdr​=∫0∞​q(R2+z2​)dz.
(18)
Differentiating the nonsingular z representation gives A′(R)=R∫R∞​q′(r)/r2−R2​dr. Hence
p(R)=2A(R)+RA′(R)=R1​dRd​[R2A(R)].​
(19)
If R2A vanishes at zero and infinity, then ∫0∞​6πRp(R)dR=6π[R2A]0∞​=0. These are the absence-of-boundary-stress conditions needed for the global result.
The virial relation, term by term. Interchange the order in the residual integral and use
∫0r​r2−R2​R3dR​=r3∫0π/2​sin3θdθ=32​r3.
(20)
It follows that
∫0∞​6πRp(R)dR=6π∫0∞​RJ(R)dR−4πγ∫0∞​rλ(r)L(r)dr.
(21)
The first term is 2K/Υ: spherical averaging assigns one third of the integrated velocity-square to any fixed viewing direction, giving K=3πΥ∫RJdR. The gravitational potential energy is
W=−∫0∞​rGM(r)​dM(r)=−4πGΥ2∫0∞​rλ(r)L(r)dr.
(22)
Thus the integrated residual is exactly (2K+W)/Υ, and its vanishing is the virial theorem. Solving for γ gives the global projected virial estimate of a mass-to-light ratio,
γ=2∫0∞​rλ(r)L(r)dr3∫0∞​Rμ(R)σp2​(R)dR​.​
(23)
It is independent of the radial anisotropy profile when the full aperture and the stated boundary conditions are used. A finite aperture retains a boundary term.

The factor called N in the formula must be the stellar number density n, not a dimensionless count. Indeed σ3/(G2m2) has dimensions time per volume, so a density in the denominator is essential. If N∗​ is the population in a representative volume V, then n=N∗​/V.
For a weak encounter between equal-mass stars, let u be the asymptotic relative speed and b the impact parameter. In the straight-line impulse approximation, one star's transverse kick is
Δv⊥​=∫−∞∞​(b2+u2t2)3/2Gmbdt​=bu2Gm​.
(24)
The other receives the opposite kick, so the relative-velocity change is Δu⊥​=4Gm/(bu). Encounters in (b,b+db) occur at rate 2πbdbnu. Independent random kicks add in squared magnitude, giving
Dv​:=dtd⟨∣Δv∣2⟩​=u8πnG2m2​∫bmin​bmax​​bdb​=u8πnG2m2lnΛ​,Du​=4Dv​.
(25)
This is the random-walk derivation of stellar relaxation. The Coulomb logarithm in stellar dynamics is lnΛ=ln(bmax​/bmin​). The upper cutoff is the size or inhomogeneity scale beyond which independent local encounters are inappropriate. The lower cutoff is of order the strong-deflection scale G(2m)/u2, or a larger stellar-size/softening scale if applicable. A self-gravitating system commonly has Λ of order its total number of stars; that population count is distinct from n in the diffusion rate.
To state the numerical convention explicitly, use representative encounter speed u=2​σ and define the relaxation estimate from the relative-velocity diffusion rate with reference squared velocity 3σ2. This gives
TR​:=Du​3σ2​=16π2​3​nG2m2lnΛσ3​.​
(26)
This recovers the printed coefficient under a specified convention. There is no convention-independent exact numerical prefactor determined by a representative one-dimensional velocity alone. Using the single-star diffusion rate with the same 3σ2 reference instead gives four times this time; averaging over a full velocity distribution also changes the prefactor. The relaxation time conventions in stellar dynamics must therefore distinguish single-star and relative kicks. The physical scaling is TR​∝σ3/(nG2m2lnΛ), and the printed N must be interpreted as number density.

Use μ=γm for the gravitational parameter, to distinguish it from the Delaunay action called G. The displayed actions are per unit reduced mass. Accordingly use specific canonical momenta; with physical relative momenta, all three actions acquire a common reduced-mass factor, which leaves the eccentricity fraction below unchanged.
The canonical transformation. In spherical coordinates, separation of the Kepler Hamilton-Jacobi equation gives
pϕ​=H,pθ2​=G2−sin2θH2​,pr2​=2E+r2μ​−r2G2​,E=−2L2μ2​.
(27)
A local type-two generating function, continued across turning points with the appropriate momentum branches, is
W=Hϕ+∫θG2−H2/sin2θ′​dθ′+∫r−μ2/L2+2μ/r′−G2/r′2​dr′.
(28)
The old momenta are ∂W/∂(r,θ,ϕ) and the new angles are l=∂L​W, g=∂G​W, h=∂H​W. This defines a canonical transformation, since its generating differential is dW=pr​dr+pθ​dθ+pϕ​dϕ+ldL+gdG+hdH. With standard choices of angle origins, the resulting Delaunay variables are
L=μa​,G=L1−e2​,H=Gcosi,
(29)
with l the mean anomaly, g the argument of periapsis, and h the longitude of ascending node. In particular, the signed H includes retrograde orbits; the squared equation in the PDF must not be read as H≥0.
For bound noncollision Kepler orbits, the independent ranges are
0<L<∞,0<G<L,−G<H<G,0≤l,g,h<2π.​
(30)
The limiting circular, radial and coplanar cases sit on boundaries where some angle charts degenerate. They have zero measure in the generic phase-volume calculation. The ranges are constrained as above, rather than a Cartesian box with three unconstrained actions.
The eccentricity count. A canonical transformation preserves phase space volume, so
dN=F(E(L))dLdGdHdldgdh.
(31)
Integrating the three angles contributes (2π)3, and integrating H gives 2G. At fixed L, the condition that the orbital eccentricity be less than e0​ is G>L1−e02​​. Thus
∫L1−e02​​L​2GdG=L2e02​,∫0L​2GdG=L2.
(32)
Any energy weight cancels between these integrals. If the total population is finite, or an explicit finite action band is used,
N(e<e0​)=e02​Ntot​,P(e<e0​)=e02​,fe​(e)=2e.​
(33)
This is the Delaunay phase-volume proof of the thermal eccentricity distribution. The source's e2 law is a cumulative count, not a differential eccentricity density. Also, pointwise finite F(E) does not alone imply finite Ntot​: F=1 gives ∫0∞​L2dL=∞. The finite normalization of an energy-only Kepler distribution is the additional condition needed for a global probability statement; the fraction at every fixed energy is unaffected.
Why the observation does not imply relaxation or equilibrium. No Boltzmann distribution was used: every normalizable energy-only phase-space distribution function has the same eccentricity marginal, irrespective of collisional thermalization. An even stronger counterexample is a genuinely time-dependent collisionless distribution
f(L,G,H,l,g,h,t)=F(E(L))[1+ηcos(l−ν(L)t)],0<∣η∣<1,ν(L)=L3μ2​.
(34)
Kepler motion has l˙=ν(L) and constant actions and other angles, so ∂t​f+ν∂l​f=0: this is an exact nonstationary solution of the Collisionless Boltzmann equation. Integrating l removes the cosine and leaves precisely the same e2 cumulative count at every time. An eccentricity marginal alone cannot establish thermal relaxation, a relaxed age, or even a stationary distribution.

An isolating integral is a single-valued integral of motion I(x,v) whose fixed value confines an orbit to a lower-dimensional level set of phase space. Along the flow, dI/dt=0; for an autonomous Hamiltonian, this means {I,H}=0. Several isolating integrals are independent when their gradients are linearly independent on the regular region considered. Counting them requires a specified domain: merely local orbit labels in a flow box do not establish global single-valued integrals. The following cases concern regular globally meaningful orbit constraints.

One integral can occur in an autonomous system with a fully chaotic region and no additional continuous orbit constraints. The specific orbital energy is conserved in a time-independent gravitational potential. If a chaotic stellar orbit is dense in a connected region of its energy hypersurface, every continuous integral of motion is constant on that region. Thus it cannot supply a new independent constraint there, and energy is the only independent isolating integral. A generic nonaxisymmetric potential can contain such regions; not every orbit in such a potential is necessarily chaotic.

No global isolating integral is possible in a sufficiently general time-dependent system without surviving symmetries or other invariants. For
E=21​v2+Φ(x,t),dtdE​=∂t∂Φ​,
(35)
even energy is then not conserved, and nonsymmetric forcing can destroy all global constraints on the orbit. Time dependence alone does not imply this result: a central time-dependent force still conserves angular momentum, and a rigidly rotating potential can conserve a Jacobi integral of a rigidly rotating potential. The case of no integrals requires absence of such additional structure. A fixed point or locally defined flow labels are not the global orbit family considered here.

More than three integrals occur when additional symmetry or degeneracy restricts motion beyond a generic three-frequency invariant torus. In a time-independent central gravitational potential, energy and the three components of specific angular momentum are four generically independent isolating integrals. They fix the orbital plane as well as the radial-motion parameters. A nonclosed planar rosette typically has two-dimensional orbit closure, rather than a three-dimensional invariant torus.
The Kepler orbit is a stronger example. In addition to E and L, the conserved Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector
A=v×L−μrr​,μ=GM,
(36)
fixes the apsidal direction. Its constraints A⋅L=0 and A2=μ2+2EL2 leave five independent integrals among E,L,A. It is a maximally superintegrable Hamiltonian system, with closed nondegenerate bound orbits.
More than three integrals need not form a set of Poisson-commuting functions; the three-integral limit applies to an independent commuting set, rather than to every conserved orbit constraint. At a nonstationary point in six-dimensional phase space, integral gradients annihilate the nonzero flow vector, so at most five are independent. This explains the isolating integrals and orbital closure dimension count. A resonance on one isolated orbit does not by itself prove an additional smooth global integral on a neighborhood.

Use M for the mass parameter throughout; the PDF uses m in its formulas and M in the subsequent prose for the same quantity. Set a=rj​>0. The Jaffe model density simplifies to
ρ(r)=4πr2(r+a)2Ma​.
(37)
Its enclosed mass is
M(r)=4π∫0r​ρ(s)s2ds=Ma∫0r​(s+a)2ds​=r+aMr​​.
(38)
It tends to M as r→∞, verifying the total mass. It tends to zero at the center, so the cusp carries no extra point mass.
Spherical symmetry gives Φ′(r)=GM(r)/r2=GM/[r(r+a)]. Integrating with Φ(∞)=0 gives
Φ(r)=−GM∫r∞​s(s+a)ds​=aGM​logr+ar​​.
(39)
This also directly satisfies the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity: differentiating r2Φ′=GMr/(r+a) gives 4πGr2ρ(r). The circular speed is
vc2​(r)=rΦ′(r)=r+aGM​.​
(40)
For r≪a, vc​=GM/a​[1−r/(2a)+O((r/a)2)] is approximately constant. For r≫a, vc​=GM/r​[1−a/(2r)+O((a/r)2)]∝r−1/2. Thus the finite-mass model has an inner nearly flat galaxy rotation curve and an outer Keplerian decline.
