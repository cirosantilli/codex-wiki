A 5M⊙​ star of roughly solar stellar composition begins on the zero-age main sequence with a hot convective core, a mostly radiative envelope, and hydrogen burning dominated by the CNO cycle. The strong temperature dependence of the CNO cycle concentrates the stellar energy-generation rate near the centre, helping produce the convective core. Core mixing replenishes hydrogen within that region; it does not homogenize the entire star. The nuclear fuel supply gives a stellar nuclear timescale of order 108 years. For a numerical comparison, Table 2 of Ekström et al.'s solar-metallicity models gives core hydrogen burning lifetimes of about 88 and 109 million years for nonrotating and rotating 5M⊙​ models, respectively.
After central hydrogen exhaustion, an inert helium core contracts and heats through Kelvin-Helmholtz contraction, while a hydrogen-burning shell supplies the growing luminosity. The envelope expands and cools: the track crosses the Hertzsprung gap toward the red giant region. Its deepening convective envelope causes first dredge-up, bringing material altered by hydrogen burning to the surface. The rapid structural crossing has a thermal timescale, roughly 105–106 years for a star of this mass, rather than the full stellar nuclear timescale; an estimate follows from the Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time GM2/(RL) with the evolving radius and luminosity.
The helium core reaches ignition before becoming strongly supported by electron degeneracy pressure. Thus a normal 5M⊙​ star starts core helium burning without the helium flash characteristic of lower-mass stars. The Triple-alpha process produces carbon-12, and carbon-12 alpha capture produces oxygen-16; these reactions build a carbon-oxygen core. There is a convective core and continued hydrogen-burning shell activity. Core helium burning lasts order 107 years: the same model table gives approximately 19 and 18 million years. The changing core and envelope structure can produce a blue loop in the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram; its extent depends on stellar composition, mixing and stellar rotation.
After central helium exhaustion, the carbon-oxygen core contracts and becomes increasingly supported by electron degeneracy pressure. A helium-burning shell forms above it, beneath the hydrogen-burning shell. The star returns to a cool, luminous Asymptotic giant branch configuration. On this early branch, helium-burning shell activity is an important energy source; the overlying hydrogen-burning shell can weaken during readjustment and resume later. At this mass, a deepening convective envelope can cause second dredge-up. This shell-supported evolution to the first AGB thermal pulse takes order 106 years, with model-dependent duration. As the helium-burning shell becomes sufficiently thin, the thin-shell instability ends steady shell burning and marks the onset of AGB thermal pulses.
The original sketch shows the direction of stellar evolution, with higher effective temperature to the left. It illustrates a possible blue loop; its coordinates and loop shape are schematic, rather than a computed stellar model. The stages before the first AGB thermal pulse are the requested track; the later remnant evolution is described below.
In the usual final evolution, stellar winds and an AGB superwind remove the envelope before the carbon-oxygen core can ignite substantial carbon burning. Once shell fuel is exhausted, the exposed core contracts and heats, potentially ionizing the expelled gas as a planetary nebula, and finally becomes a carbon-oxygen white dwarf that loses its stored thermal energy by cooling. The relevant competition is between the AGB envelope-loss and core-growth timescales, not just the total initial mass.
Much higher late-stage mass loss strips the envelope sooner, shortens the Asymptotic giant branch phase, and allows less core growth, usually producing a smaller carbon-oxygen white dwarf. Much lower mass loss prolongs shell burning and permits greater core growth. In an extreme hypothetical case, retention of the envelope could let a degenerate carbon-oxygen core approach the Chandrasekhar mass and undergo explosive carbon burning: the proposed type 1.5 supernova channel. This is not an inevitable fate for a 5M⊙​ star. Doherty et al.'s evolutionary calculations emphasize the uncertainty of that channel and find that their adopted mass loss removes carbon-oxygen envelopes before such ignition. If carbon burning instead proceeds without disruption, the possible further evolution depends on the resulting core and continued envelope retention; lowering mass loss alone does not establish an electron-capture supernova outcome.
