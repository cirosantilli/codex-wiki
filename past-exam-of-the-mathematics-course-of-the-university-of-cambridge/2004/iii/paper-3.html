The derived series of a Lie algebra is defined recursively by g(0)=g and g(r+1)=[g(r),g(r)], where the bracket denotes the linear span of all brackets of pairs of elements. A solvable Lie algebra is one whose derived series of a Lie algebra terminates at zero:
g is solvable⟺g(m)=0 for some m≥0.​
(1)
The solvable radical radg of a finite-dimensional Lie algebra is its largest solvable ideal of a Lie algebra. A semisimple Lie algebra has no nonzero solvable ideals of a Lie algebra, equivalently
g is semisimple⟺radg=0.​
(2)
These are definitions; identifying semisimplicity with a direct sum of simple ideals additionally uses the structure theorem in characteristic zero. The calculations and representation theorems below are over C, as in the complex representation setting of this paper.

Tr(LA​LB​)Tr(LA​RB​)​=ntr(AB),=trAtrB,​Tr(RA​RB​)Tr(RA​LB​)​=ntr(BA),=trAtrB.​
(3)
For example, the coefficient of Eij​ in AEij​B is Aii​Bjj​; summing over i,j proves the mixed-trace identity. Expanding the product of the two adjoint operators therefore gives the Killing form of the general linear Lie algebra:
TrMn​​(adAadB)=2ntr(AB)−2trAtrB.
(4)
For A,B in the special linear Lie algebra, both individual traces vanish. Furthermore Mn​(C)=sln​⊕CI, and every adjoint operator kills the scalar summand, so its product has the same trace on Mn​ and on sln​. Consequently the Killing form of the special linear Lie algebra is
(A,B)ad​=2ntr(AB),λ=2n1​(n≥2).​
(5)
No uniqueness theorem for invariant bilinear forms on a Lie algebra is needed: this calculation establishes the constant directly. For the degenerate case n=1, the Lie algebra is zero, both forms vanish, and proportionality does not determine a unique constant.

Define a symmetric bilinear form B by pairing c with d and p with q, each with value one, and setting all other pairings of basis vectors to zero. Its Gram matrix in the order (c,d,p,q) is
[B]=​0100​1000​0001​0010​​.​
(7)
The determinant is one, so this is a nondegenerate bilinear form.
For invariance, put T(x,y,z)=B([x,y],z). The nonzero values on distinct basis triples are generated by
T(d,p,q)=B(p,q)=1,T(p,q,d)=B(c,d)=1,T(q,d,p)=B(q,p)=1
(8)
and the reversed triples have value minus one. Every value involving c, or a repeated basis vector, vanishes. Thus T is an alternating trilinear form, explicitly d∗∧p∗∧q∗. In particular T(x,y,z)=T(y,z,x). By symmetry of B, this reads B([x,y],z)=B(x,[y,z]), the defining identity for an invariant bilinear form on a Lie algebra.
Suppose a finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation ρ realized this form as B(x,y)=tr(ρ(x)ρ(y)). The Lie theorem says that every finite-dimensional representation of a complex solvable Lie algebra admits a basis in which all representing matrices are upper triangular. A commutator of upper triangular matrices has zero diagonal, hence is strictly upper triangular. Therefore ρ([g,g]) is strictly upper triangular, and its product with any representing matrix still has zero diagonal. It follows that
tr(ρ(x)ρ(y))=0(x∈[g,g], y∈g).
(9)
This is the general fact that trace forms of solvable Lie algebras annihilate the derived algebra. Here c∈[g,g], so such a trace form would have B(c,d)=0, contradicting the constructed value one. Thus the nondegenerate invariant form exists, but no finite-dimensional representation has it as its trace form. Finite dimensionality is implicit in the ordinary representation trace; no trace on arbitrary infinite-dimensional endomorphisms is being assumed.

In the defining representation V=C2n, the basis vectors vi​ and viˉ​ have weights εi​ and −εi​, respectively, each with multiplicity one. Thus its formal character is
chV=i=1∑n​(eεi​+e−εi​),highest weight ε1​.​
(21)
The vector v1​ is killed by every positive root space, since all positive-root matrices are upper triangular, and is a highest-weight vector. Any nonzero invariant subspace splits into weight spaces and therefore contains one of the natural weight vectors, since those weight spaces are one-dimensional. The root matrices connect that vector to all the others, so this representation is irreducible for n≥2. In terms of the fundamental weights of Dn, its highest weight is ω1​ for n≥3 and ω1​+ω2​ for n=2.
The flip of the two tensor factors is equivariant, hence
V⊗V=Sym2V⊕Λ2V.
(22)
Use the invariant symmetric bilinear form Q(u,v)=uTJv. Contraction C(u⊗v)=Q(u,v) is an equivariant map to the trivial Lie algebra representation. Its inverse-form tensor
τ=∑i=1n​(vi​⊗viˉ​+viˉ​⊗vi​)
(23)
is invariant and satisfies C(τ)=2n. Consequently
Sym2V=Cτ⊕Sym02​V,Sym02​V=ker(C∣Sym2V​).
(24)
The traceless summand contains the highest-weight vector v1​⊗v1​ of weight 2ε1​. The Weyl complete reducibility theorem states that every finite-dimensional complex semisimple Lie algebra representation is a direct sum of irreducible representations, so the submodule generated by this highest vector is L(2ε1​). The Weyl dimension formula verifies that it is the entire traceless space: only positive roots involving ε1​ change their factors, and
dimL(2ε1​)=∏j=2n​j−1j+1​2n−j−12n−j+1​=2n(n+1)​n2(2n−1)​=(n+1)(2n−1).

This equals dimSym2V−1=n(2n+1)−1. Thus the traceless symmetric square of the defining even orthogonal representation is irreducible, including n=2.
For the other summand, the exterior square of the defining orthogonal representation has the equivariant identification
u∧v⟼[w⟼Q(v,w)u−Q(u,w)v]∈so(V,Q).
(26)
The image is skew relative to Q, and in a basis making Q the identity the images are the independent elementary skew matrices. The map is therefore an isomorphism onto the Adjoint representation of a Lie algebra. For n≥3, the connected Dn Dynkin diagram gives a simple Lie algebra (with D3​=A3​); its adjoint module is irreducible because its invariant subspaces are exactly its ideals. Its highest weight is ε1​+ε2​, and v1​∧v2​ is a highest vector. The tensor-square decomposition of the defining even orthogonal representation is consequently

Let g be a finite-dimensional complex semisimple Lie algebra, choose a Cartan subalgebra t and a set R+ of positive roots, and write W for the Weyl group. Let λ be a dominant integral weight and L(λ) the finite-dimensional irreducible highest-weight representation with that highest weight. If L(λ)μ​ denotes its weight space of weight μ, its formal character is chL(λ)=∑μ​dimL(λ)μ​eμ. The symbols eμ belong to the group algebra of the weight lattice and multiply by eμeν=eμ+ν; they are formal symbols, not matrix exponentials.
Put ρ=21​∑α∈R+​α, the half-sum of positive roots, and let ℓ(w) be the Coxeter length of w, the least number of simple reflections expressing it. Then (−1)ℓ(w)=det(w) in the real reflection representation. The Weyl character formula states
chL(λ)=w∈W∑​(−1)ℓ(w)ewρw∈W∑​(−1)ℓ(w)ew(λ+ρ)​.​
(29)
By the Weyl denominator formula, the denominator is eρ∏α∈R+​(1−e−α), giving the equivalent expression
chL(λ)=α∈R+∏​(1−e−α)w∈W∑​det(w)ew(λ+ρ)−ρ​.​
(30)
The quotient can be interpreted in the fraction field of the formal group algebra; the theorem says it simplifies to the finite formal character above. In particular the apparent denominator does not mean that the representation has infinitely many weights.

Use the standard sl2 Lie algebra relations [h,e]=2e, [h,f]=−2f and [e,f]=h. The classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations says that every finite-dimensional complex module is a direct sum of L(m), m∈Z≥0​, where L(m) has weights m,m−2,…,−m. The raising operator e and lowering operator f move weights by two and minus two. On L(m) each therefore has (m+1)st power zero. There is consequently one integer N such that eN=fN=0 on all of V.
The matrix exponentials are thus finite polynomials in nilpotent operators:
exp(e)=∑j=0N−1​j!ej​,exp(−f)=∑j=0N−1​j!(−f)j​.
(34)
Each is a well-defined endomorphism, and its inverse is obtained by negating the argument. Their product is therefore a linear automorphism,
s=exp(e)exp(−f)exp(e)∈GL(V).​
(35)
This Weyl reflection lift in an sl2 representation is algebraically defined by finite sums; no choice of topology or convergence of an infinite series is needed.

Let v0​ be the highest vector of the Verma module M(n) and set vk​=fkv0​. The Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem implies that (vk​)k≥0​ is a basis, so none of these vectors vanishes. The actions are
fvk​=vk+1​,hvk​=(n−2k)vk​,evk​=k(n−k+1)vk−1​.
(36)
The raising operator is a locally nilpotent operator: on every vector, repeated application of e eventually removes all its finitely many basis terms. Thus its algebraic exponential of a locally nilpotent operator is defined, and exp(e)v0​=v0​. But the middle factor in the proposed product would have to send this vector to
exp(−f)v0​=∑k≥0​k!(−1)k​vk​,
(37)
which has infinitely many nonzero, linearly independent basis coefficients. An element of the algebraic Verma module is a finite linear combination of the vk​, so this expression is not in M(n). In particular the lowering operator is not locally nilpotent.
Even for integral n≥0, fn+1v0​ is nonzero in the Verma module; it becomes zero only in its finite-dimensional irreducible quotient L(n). Hence s is not defined as the displayed composition of endomorphisms of the algebraic Verma module. Working in a completion would be a different problem and is not part of the module specified here.
