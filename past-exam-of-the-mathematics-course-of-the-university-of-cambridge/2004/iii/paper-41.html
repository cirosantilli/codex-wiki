Let Λ=∑i=1m​λi​. For Λ>0, the resulting compound Poisson distribution has
count parameter Λ,F(x)=i=1∑m​Λλi​​Fi​(x).​
(1)
Thus the merged claim size probability distribution is a rate-weighted mixture distribution, not an equally weighted mixture distribution unless the rates agree.
To prove this without any moment-generating function assumption, let χi​(t) be the characteristic function of a claim of type i. Conditioning on its Poisson distribution count gives
EeitSi​=exp{λi​(χi​(t)−1)}.
(2)
Independence of the aggregate risks therefore gives
EeitS=∏i​exp{λi​(χi​(t)−1)}=exp{Λ(∑i​Λλi​​χi​(t)−1)}.
(3)
The weighted characteristic function inside is precisely that of the displayed mixture distribution. This is the transform of a sum of independent claims with independent count Pois(Λ), proving the claim by uniqueness of characteristic functions. This is Poisson superposition of insurance portfolios. If Λ=0, all risks make no claims almost surely and S=0; the severity law is then irrelevant.

Use the preceding construction with type-i claims of deterministic size ai​. For Λ=∑i​λi​>0, the answer is
N∼Pois(Λ),P(X=ai​)=Λλi​​,FX​(x)=i:ai​≤x∑​Λλi​​.​
(4)
The characteristic function verifies this directly:
EeitT=∏i​exp{λi​(eitai​−1)}=exp{Λ(EeitX−1)}.
(5)
Hence T has the stated compound Poisson distribution. Distinctness of the ai​ makes the atomic probabilities unambiguous.
A zero coefficient is allowed. It corresponds to zero-sized claims in this representation and contributes nothing to T. If a representation using only positive claims is preferred, remove that category: its count parameter becomes Λ+​=∑i:ai​>0​λi​, with probabilities λi​/Λ+​ on the positive values. The zero claim sizes in a compound Poisson representation explain why the count parameter need not be unique. If Λ=0, or if all positive-size categories have zero rate, the aggregate is identically zero.

Define Ni​=∑k=1N​1{Xk​=ai​}​. Grouping equal claim sizes gives the pathwise identity
T=i=1∑m​ai​Ni​.​
(6)
Conditional on N=n, the n independent claims receive category labels with probabilities pi​. There are n!/(n1​!⋯nm​!) assignments producing prescribed category counts, and every assignment has probability ∏i​pini​​. Thus the conditional probability distribution is a multinomial distribution:
P(N1​=n1​,…,Nm​=nm​∣N=n)=∏i​ni​!n!​∏i​pini​​

when the nonnegative counts sum to n, and is zero otherwise.
For 0≤zi​≤1, condition first on N and use the probability generating function of a Poisson distribution:
E∏i​ziNi​​=E(∑i​pi​zi​)N=exp{λ(∑i​pi​zi​−1)}=∏i​exp{λpi​(zi​−1)}.
(8)
This factorization is the joint probability generating function of independent Poisson distribution variables. Hence
Ni​∼Pois(λpi​)independently.​
(9)
A category with pi​=0 has zero count almost surely. The categorical thinning of a Poisson claim count produces independence after averaging over the random total, even though the counts are constrained to sum to n conditional on that total.

Let MX​(t)=EetX. Conditioning on N∼Pois(λ) gives
EetS=E[MX​(t)N]=exp{λ(MX​(t)−1)},KS​(t)=λ(MX​(t)−1).​
(10)
Where the moment-generating function is finite around zero, differentiating the cumulant-generating function gives
κj​(S)=KS(j)​(0)=λE[Xj].​
(11)
In particular ES=λEX and VarS=λEX2. These compound Poisson cumulants use raw claim moments. The first two formulas also follow by conditioning: E[S∣N]=NEX and Var(S∣N)=NVarX, so
VarS=E[N]VarX+VarN(EX)2=λEX2.
(12)
This is the law of total variance. They therefore remain valid with finite first two moments even when no positive exponential moment exists.
Under per-claim excess of loss reinsurance, put Y=min(X,M) and let W=(X−M)+​ be the positive part of the excess. The insurer and reinsurer totals are sums of these payments over the same Poisson distribution count. Write F(x)=P(X>x). Using the tail integral formula for moments on the tail probabilities of Y and W gives
ESI​ESR​​=λ∫0M​F(x)dx,=λ∫M∞​F(x)dx,​VarSI​VarSR​​=2λ∫0M​xF(x)dx,=2λ∫M∞​(x−M)F(x)dx.​​
(13)
For example, EY2=∫0∞​P(Y2>t)dt=2∫0M​xF(x)dx after t=x2, and shifting by M gives the reinsurer formula. The moment formulas are finite whenever the corresponding claim moments are finite.
For the specified Pareto distribution, with shape three and minimum d>0,
F(x)={1,d3/x3,​0≤x≤d,x≥d,​EX=3d/2,EX2=3d2.
(14)
If 0≤M≤d, every claim exceeds the reinsurance retention. Therefore
ESI​ESR​​=λM,=λ(3d/2−M),​VarSI​VarSR​​=λM2,=λ(3d2−3dM+M2).​
(15)
If M≥d, the tail integrals instead give
ESI​ESR​​=λ(23d​−2M2d3​),=2M2λd3​,​VarSI​VarSR​​=λ(3d2−M2d3​),=Mλd3​.​
(16)
Consequently the requested sum of variances is
V(M)=λ{3d2−3dM+2M2,3d2−d3/M,​0≤M≤d,M≥d.​
(17)
On the first branch, V′(M)=λ(4M−3d); it decreases until M=3d/4 and then increases. On the second branch V′(M)=λd3/M2>0. For λ>0 the minimum is unique; if λ=0, all variances are zero and every reinsurance retention minimizes them. Thus
M∗=43d​,V(M∗)=815​λd2.​
(18)
The curve starts at 3λd2, reaches this minimum, passes through V(d)=2λd2 with matching slope λd on both branches, and then increases concavely toward the horizontal asymptote 3λd2. The variance-minimizing retention for shape-three Pareto claims is shown below.
The two aggregate payouts are dependent. Conditioning on the common count gives covariance λCov(Y,W)+λEYEW=λE[YW]=λMEW, so V(M)+2Cov(SI​,SR​)=3λd2 for every reinsurance retention, as required by SI​+SR​=S. The covariance of two components of a compound Poisson sum accounts for this distinction between a sum of variances and the variance of a sum.
There is a moment domain qualification for this heavy-tailed example. For every t>0, ∫d∞​etx3d3x−4dx=∞, and moments of order j≥3 also diverge. Thus its positive moment-generating function is not finite and higher finite cumulants cannot be obtained by differentiating around zero. Its mean and variance calculations above are valid by conditioning or by the first two right derivatives at zero of the Laplace transform of a nonnegative random variable.

For nontrivial positive claim sizes in the classical risk model and λ>0, the adjustment coefficient is the unique positive solution
λ(MX​(R)−1)=cR,0<R<r∞​.​
(19)
Indeed, h(r)=λ(MX​(r)−1)−cr has h(0)=0, h′(0)=λμ−c<0, and is strictly convex. It becomes positive near the right endpoint of the moment-generating function domain. For a finite endpoint this follows from the assumed divergence; for an infinite endpoint, P(X≥ε)>0 for some ε>0 gives an exponential lower bound on MX​(r), which eventually exceeds any linear function. Convexity gives existence and uniqueness of the positive crossing and λMX′​(R)−c>0.
Put q=λμ/c and F=1−F. Substituting φ=1−ψ into the supplied survival renewal equation for a classical risk model, and using ∫0∞​F(x)dx=μ, yields
ψ(u)=cλ​∫u∞​F(x)dx+cλ​∫0u​ψ(u−x)F(x)dx.
(20)
Multiplying by eRu gives the proper renewal-type equation
Z(u)=gR​(u)+∫0u​Z(u−x)kR​(x)dx,​kR​(x)=cλ​eRxF(x),gR​(u)=cλ​eRu∫u∞​F(x)dx.
(21)
The exponential tilt of the ruin renewal kernel turns a defective kernel into a probability density function, because the tail integration identity gives
∫0∞​kR​(x)dx=cλ​RMX​(R)−1​=1.
(22)
This identity follows also by integrating eRX−1=R∫0X​eRxdx and applying Tonelli theorem. The tilted probability density function defines a nonarithmetic distribution, even if the claim size probability distribution itself has atoms.
Differentiate (MX​(R)−1)/R to calculate its mean:
mR​=∫0∞​xkR​(x)dx=cλ​R2RMX′​(R)−MX​(R)+1​=cRλMX′​(R)−c​.
(23)
It is finite and positive because R is inside the finite moment-generating function domain. Next, reversing the order of integration gives
∫0∞​gR​(u)du​=cλ​∫0∞​F(x)∫0x​eRududx=cRλ​(RMX​(R)−1​−μ)=cRc−λμ​.​
(24)
The forcing function is directly Riemann integrable. Choose ε>0 with MX​(R+ε)<∞. Markov's inequality bounds F(x) by MX​(R+ε)e−(R+ε)x, so gR​(u) is bounded by a constant times e−εu. It is continuous; on finite intervals continuity controls its Riemann sums, and the exponential envelope controls the tails. These are sufficient conditions for direct Riemann integrability.
Iterating the renewal equation by convolution gives Z=gR​∗UR​, where UR​=∑n≥0​kR∗n​ is the renewal measure. The remainder after n iterations is bounded on each fixed interval by a constant times the chance that a sum of n positive interarrivals remains in that interval, which tends to zero. The key renewal theorem, applicable to this nonarithmetic distribution of finite mean and the directly integrable forcing, now gives the interior adjustment coefficient ruin prefactor:
A=u→∞lim​eRuψ(u)=mR​∫0∞​gR​(u)du​=λMX′​(R)−cc−λμ​.​
(25)
Both numerator and denominator are positive, so 0<A<∞.
For the unit-rate shape-two Erlang distribution, μ=2, MX​(r)=(1−r)−2 and MX′​(r)=2(1−r)−3. Write δ=λ/c, so positive loading requires 0<δ<1/2. Dividing the adjustment coefficient equation by its nonzero root gives δ(2−R)=(1−R)2. Thus
R=1−2δ+δ2+4δ​​,A=2δ(1−R)−3−11−2δ​=3−R(1−R)(3−2R)​.​
(26)
The last simplification uses δ=(1−R)2/(2−R); it is the Erlang shape-two ruin prefactor. Its value depends on the premium-to-arrival ratio, which is not specified numerically.
For the final exponential representation, evaluating at zero in the survival renewal equation for a classical risk model gives λμ/c=ψ(0)=a+b. If the slower term is present, necessarily a>0 and euψ(u)→a. Comparing with the finite positive asymptotic constant just proved yields
R=1,A=a,λμ/c=a+b(a>0).​
(27)
A smaller adjustment coefficient would make the limit zero and a larger one would make it infinite. Here the leading exponential term determines a ruin adjustment coefficient. The coefficient b need not be assumed positive to identify the leading term.
The wording does not specify that a is nonzero. If a=0 and b>0, the only surviving term instead gives R=6, A=b and λμ/c=b. This case is realizable: claims with an exponential distribution of rate β=6+λ/c have ultimate ruin probability (λ/(cβ))e−6u. Negative a would make the ultimate ruin probability negative for large u, and both coefficients zero are incompatible with a nontrivial positive claim rate and mean. These observations specify the coefficient qualification needed for the usual R=1 answer.

Use the shape-rate convention for the gamma distribution θ∼Gamma(α,β), with prior distribution probability density function proportional to θα−1e−βθ. The observations are independent conditional on the common parameter θ. Unconditionally a common nondegenerate prior distribution induces covariance α/β2 between distinct observations, so the Bayesian inference model requires conditional independence.
For unit-exposure counts, the likelihood is proportional to θ∑i​xi​e−nθ. Multiplying by the prior distribution gives
θ∣x∼Gamma(α+∑i=1n​xi​, β+n).
(28)
The future count has conditional expectation θ, so posterior distribution averaging gives
E[Xn+1​∣x]=β+nα+∑i​xi​​=n+βn​x+n+ββ​βα​.​
(29)
This is an exact Bayesian credibility estimate, with credibility factor Z=n/(n+β) and collective mean α/β.
For the insurance exposure and claims inflation calculation put cj​=c(1+r)j, assuming c>0, 1+r>0 and positive policy counts nj​. Let Kj​ be the total claim count in year j. Conditional independence of the policy counts gives Kj​∣θ∼Pois(nj​θ), and the observed average amount satisfies
Yj​=nj​cj​Kj​​,kj​=cj​nj​yj​​.
(30)
For possible observations, the recovered kj​ are nonnegative integers. If they are not, the data have zero likelihood under the model and no conditional posterior distribution is defined for those impossible values.
Write W=∑j=1n​nj​ and K=∑j=1n​kj​. Since Yj​ and Kj​ determine each other, conditioning on the observed average amounts is equivalent to conditioning on these counts. The independent yearly count likelihood is
∏j=1n​e−nj​θkj​!(nj​θ)kj​​∝θKe−Wθ.
(31)
The Poisson-gamma conjugacy with unequal exposures therefore gives
θ∣y∼Gamma(α+K,β+W).​
(32)
For the future year, E[Yn+1​∣θ]=cn+1​θ; the number of future policies cancels because Yn+1​ is a per-policy average. Hence
E[Yn+1​∣y]=cn+1​β+Wα+K​=Zm(y)+(1−Z)m,​
(33)
with
Zmm(y)​=W+βW​,=c(1+r)n+1βα​,=cn+1​WK​=W1​j=1∑n​nj​(1+r)n+1−jyj​.​​
(34)
The collective mean m is the prior distribution expected claim amount per policy in the future year's monetary units. The experience mean m(y) first inflates each past per-policy amount to that same future year and then averages with policy-exposure weights. Equivalently, it estimates annual claim frequency by total observed claims divided by total past insurance exposure and converts that frequency to the future claim cost. This is inflation-adjusted Poisson-gamma credibility.
For fixed prior distribution parameters, Z increases strictly with total past insurance exposure: dZ/dW=β/(W+β)2>0, and Z→1 as W→∞. The prior distribution weight decreases to zero; β acts as equivalent prior distribution insurance exposure. With no past insurance exposure the appropriate weight is zero and the prediction is m, without defining an empirical experience mean.
