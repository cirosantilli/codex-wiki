The Frankl-Wilson theorem is a striking example of the polynomial method in combinatorics: an intersection restriction creates a family of linearly independent low-degree polynomials, whose dimension bounds the set family. Let p be a prime number, let L⊆Fp​ have s elements, and let F be a set family such that member sizes modulo p avoid L while every distinct pair has intersection size in L modulo p. Its general modular form gives
∣F∣≤∑j=0s​(jn​).
(30)
If all members have the same size k and s≤k, the uniform refinement gives ∣F∣≤(sn​).
For the proof, work over the finite field Fp​ and associate to A∈F the modular intersection polynomial
PA​(x)=∏λ∈L​(∑i∈A​xi​−λ).
(31)
At the characteristic vector of a set B, this equals zero for B=A and is nonzero for B=A. A linear relation among the evaluation functions therefore has every coefficient zero. The multilinear reduction on the Boolean cube, replacing xia​ by xi​ for a≥1, preserves these evaluations and the degree bound s. There are ∑j=0s​(jn​) possible square-free monomials, proving the general form.
For uniform members, the low-degree evaluation rank on a uniform layer supplies the sharper dimension bound. To verify this without division modulo p, form the integer incidence matrix with rows indexed by subsets S of size at most s, columns indexed by k-subsets B, and entries 1S⊆B​. Over the rational numbers, a row with ∣S∣=j is (s−jk−j​)−1 times the sum of all size-s rows containing S. Its matrix rank is at most (sn​). Every larger integer minor is consequently zero and remains zero modulo p, giving the same bound over Fp​. The nonzero diagonal evaluation matrix of the PA​ then forces ∣F∣≤(sn​).
The theorem, proved by Frankl and Wilson in 1981, extends the theme of the Ray-Chaudhuri–Wilson theorem from ordinary intersection sizes to residues. Modular restrictions can be weaker than exact intersection restrictions yet still force unexpectedly small families. Here are three consequences from one explicit modular intersection graph.
Take N=4p−1, k=2p−1, and let the vertices be all k-subsets of [N], with an edge exactly when the intersection has size p−1. An independent set avoids the only possible off-diagonal intersection congruent to k modulo p, so applying the Frankl-Wilson theorem with L=Fp​∖{p−1} gives
α(G)≤B:=(p−1N​).
(32)
A clique has one exact intersection size. Apply the uniform theorem modulo a prime larger than k, with L={p−1}, to get ω(G)≤N. Since there are v=(kN​) vertices, this gives the explicit asymmetric Ramsey number bound R(N+1,B+1)>v. It illustrates how algebraic constructions produce graphs with simultaneously restricted cliques and independent sets.
For a geometric application, map each vertex to xA​=1A​/2p​ in RN. Then ∣xA​−xB​∣2=(k−∣A∩B∣)/p, so edges have unit Euclidean distance. Every proper colouring of Euclidean space therefore colours this finite graph, giving
χ(RN)≥χ(G)≥(p−1N​)(2p−1N​)​.
(33)
The Stirling formula gives the ratio as exp((log2−H(1/4)+o(1))N), where H(a)=−aloga−(1−a)log(1−a) is the binary entropy function in natural-log units. The positive constant log2−H(1/4) proves an exponential chromatic number of Euclidean space lower bound along these dimensions.
Finally, a quadratic embedding converts this same forbidden intersection into a forbidden diameter pair. Set uA​=21A​−1 and QA​=uA​uAT​. The quadratic sign-vector diameter formula follows from the Frobenius inner product:
∥QA​−QB​∥F2​=2(N2−(uA​⋅uB​)2),uA​⋅uB​=4∣A∩B∣−4p+3.
(34)
For distinct members the smallest possible absolute inner product is one, attained exactly when the intersection is p−1. Thus maximum-distance pairs are exactly edges of G. The matrices have common diagonal; using their off-diagonal coordinates, multiplied by 2​, realizes them isometrically in D=(2N​) dimensions. They are distinct because equal outer products require uA​=±uB​, and complements have size 2p, not k.
Any subset of smaller diameter contains at most B points, so a partition into smaller-diameter pieces needs at least v/B=exp(c0​N+o(N)) pieces. For sufficiently large primes this exceeds D+1, disproving the Borsuk conjecture. This is the mechanism behind the Kahn-Kalai counterexample to the Borsuk conjecture: the number of required pieces grows exponentially in the original set-system parameter while dimension grows quadratically. Their 1993 paper establishes the resulting high-dimensional counterexample.
