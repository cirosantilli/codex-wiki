The determinant-one cyclic quotient of the affine plane has the closed embedding
A2/μn​≅{(u,v,w)∈A3:uv=wn},π(x,y)=(xn,yn,xy).​
(1)
Here μn​ denotes the cyclic group generated by the given diagonal matrix. We establish the presentation of the polynomial invariant ring and verify that its fibres really are the group orbits.
On the coordinate ring A=C[x,y], the inverse-pullback action multiplies xayb by ζb−a. Since these monomials are a basis, a polynomial is invariant precisely when each of its nonzero monomials satisfies a−b≡0(modn). If a≥b, such a monomial is (xn)(a−b)/n(xy)b; if b≥a, it is (yn)(b−a)/n(xy)a. Therefore
Aμn​=C[xn,yn,xy].
(2)
These generators give a surjective homomorphism C[u,v,w]→Aμn​, and uv−wn lies in its kernel of a ring homomorphism. To prove there are no further relations, reduce any monomial using uv=wn. The result is a linear combination of
wj,uiwj,viwj(i≥1, j≥0).
(3)
Their images are respectively xjyj, xni+jyj and xjyni+j. All have distinct exponent pairs, so they are linearly independent in A. Any polynomial in the kernel of a ring homomorphism has zero reduced expression and hence belongs to (uv−wn). Consequently
Aμn​≅C[u,v,w]/(uv−wn).
(4)
This proves the claimed closed subvariety embedding of the affine quotient.
For surjectivity of π, if u=0 choose xn=u and set y=w/x. Then yn=wn/u=v. If u=0, the relation forces w=0; choose x=0 and any nth root of v for y. If two points have the same image and x=0, equality of their nth powers gives x′=ζkx and equality of xy gives y′=ζ−ky. If x=0, both first coordinates vanish, and the same argument using yn gives the orbit relation; the case x=y=0 has only that one point in its fibre. Thus every fibre is exactly one group orbit.
The map is finite: x and y satisfy the monic equations Tn−u=0 and Tn−v=0, and the finitely many monomials xayb, 0≤a,b<n, span A as a module over its polynomial invariant ring. A finite morphism is closed when it is surjective as here and therefore gives the quotient topology. For a principal open subset defined by an invariant h, its regular functions pull back to Ah​ and
(Ah​)μn​=(Aμn​)h​.
(5)
Indeed, because h is invariant, an invariant fraction with denominator hk has invariant numerator. These principal open subsets cover the affine hypersurface, proving the required local equality of invariant regular functions. Together with the orbit fibres, this verifies the geometric quotient, not merely a bijection of underlying sets.

Using the printed convention that the projective points represent lines of binary quartics, the locus of GIT stable points is
{[f]:f has four distinct roots in P1}={[f]:Disc(f)=0}.​
(6)
The root at infinity is included. We determine stability by calculating every relevant algebraic one-parameter subgroup weight.
Use the natural action (g⋅f)(X,Y)=f(g−1(X,Y)). Every nontrivial algebraic one-parameter subgroup of SL2​ is conjugate to λm​(t)=diag(tm,t−m) for some integer m>0: its two integral weights of a representation sum to zero, and a basis of weight vectors can be rescaled to have determinant one. Write
f(X,Y)=∑j=04​aj​X4−jYj,λm​(t)⋅X4−jYj=tm(2j−4)X4−jYj.
(7)
If r=min{j:aj​=0}, then r is the multiplicity of a root at the projective point [1:0]: in the local coordinate Y/X, the polynomial begins with a nonzero multiple of (Y/X)r.
The Hilbert-Mumford criterion for projective stability, with μ=−min(active weights), now gives
μ([f],λm​)=m(4−2r).
(8)
Being a GIT stable point requires this number to be strictly positive for every nontrivial subgroup. Conjugating the subgroup lets [1:0] range over the entire projective line. Thus the root-multiplicity criterion for stable binary forms becomes rp​(f)<2 at every point p. A nonzero degree-four form over C has four projective roots counted with multiplicity, so this means precisely four distinct roots. This is equivalently nonvanishing of its discriminant.
The boundary cases show why a weak inequality would give the wrong answer. A triple or quadruple root can be moved to [1:0], leaving only strictly positive weights; its affine representative tends to zero under that subgroup, so it is unstable. If all root multiplicities are at most two, all Hilbert-Mumford values are nonnegative and the point is a GIT semistable point. A double root gives a zero value for a suitable subgroup, so never represents a GIT stable point. More concretely, after putting that double root at [1:0] one has
f=Y2(aX2+bXY+cY2),a=0,
(9)
and λ1​(t)⋅f→aX2Y2. When there are three distinct roots, this limiting form, representing a GIT semistable point with two distinct roots, is outside the original group orbit, so that group orbit is not closed in the semistable locus. When there are two double roots, a change of coordinates gives X2Y2, which has the positive-dimensional diagonal torus as stabilizer. Neither case represents a GIT stable point.
For a form with four distinct roots, the projective stabilizer is finite: it permutes the four roots, and a Möbius transformation fixing three distinct points is the identity. Thus its image in PGL2​ injects into the finite permutation group on the roots. The kernel of a group homomorphism for SL2​→PGL2​ is {±I}, also finite. This agrees with the finite-stabilizer condition in geometric invariant theory. The weight calculation already supplies the required strict stability condition for every conjugate subgroup.

Put A=C[V]=⨁d≥0​Ad​ and R=AG. The linear action preserves polynomial degree, so R=⨁d≥0​Rd​ with R0​=C. We prove Hilbert finite generation for reductive invariants by combining finite ideal generation in A with a degree-lowering argument in R.
Over C, representations of a reductive algebraic group are completely reducible. On each finite-dimensional Ad​, project its isotypic component for the trivial representation onto itself and its nontrivial summands onto zero. These projections together form a degree-preserving Reynolds operator R:A→R, with R∣R​=id. The crucial additional property is
R(ah)=aR(h)(a∈R, h∈A).
(10)
Here is why it holds rather than being an arbitrary projection property. For a homogeneous invariant a, multiplication by a is a G-equivariant linear map Ad​→Ad+dega​. It sends invariant vectors to invariant vectors. On a nontrivial irreducible summand, its composition with projection onto the target's trivial summand is zero, since there is no nonzero equivariant map from a nontrivial irreducible representation to the trivial representation. It therefore commutes with the Reynolds operators. Summing the homogeneous components of a and h proves the displayed module property in general.
Let R+​=⨁d>0​Rd​ and form the ideal I=AR+​ in the ambient polynomial ring. The Hilbert basis theorem makes A a Noetherian ring, so I is generated by finitely many homogeneous positive-degree invariants f1​,…,fs​. To justify choosing the generators from this particular set, start with any finite ideal generating set; each of its members is a finite sum of multiples of homogeneous members of R+​, and the union of those finitely many invariants then generates I. If I=0, there are no positive-degree invariants and the answer is simply R=C.
Otherwise consider a homogeneous invariant f of degree d>0. Since f∈I, write
f=∑i=1s​hi​fi​,hi​∈Ad−degfi​​,
(11)
with hi​=0 when that degree is negative. Taking the degree-d component of any ideal expression gives exactly such an expression. Apply the Reynolds operator and its module property:
f=R(f)=∑i=1s​R(hi​)fi​.
(12)
Each coefficient R(hi​) is invariant and has degree strictly smaller than d, because every fi​ has positive degree. Induction on d, starting from R0​=C, now shows that every homogeneous invariant lies in C[f1​,…,fs​]. An arbitrary invariant is a finite sum of invariant homogeneous components, so
C[V]G=C[f1​,…,fs​].​
(13)
This proves finite generation as an algebra. No Noetherian property of the polynomial invariant ring was assumed in obtaining the generators.

Let C be the smooth algebraic curve. A nonzero regular section of a line bundle has an effective zero divisor, so its degree of a line bundle is nonnegative. Degrees add under tensor products and change sign on taking the dual. We use genuine line subbundles, so their quotients in E are locally free. Both bounds can be proved constructively.
For the upper bound, take a rational frame of E∗ over the function field of C. Its two members are rational sections with only finitely many poles. Choose an effective divisor D large enough to bound the pole orders of both rational sections. They become global sections of E∗⊗OC​(D) and define a sheaf map
E⟶OC​(D)⊕OC​(D)
(14)
which is an isomorphism at the generic point. It is therefore injective as a sheaf map: a section in its kernel would vanish generically in a locally free sheaf, which is a torsion-free sheaf. For any line subbundle L⊂E, at least one of its two component maps into OC​(D) is nonzero. Such a map is a nonzero global section of OC​(D)⊗L−1. Its zero divisor on an algebraic curve is effective, so
degL≤degD.​
(15)
This upper degree bound for line subbundles on a smooth curve depends only on a choice made for E, and applies to every one of its line subbundles.
For the lower-bound question we will actually construct subbundles OC​(−mp0​) for every sufficiently large integer m, where p0​ is a fixed point. First prove the globally generated vector bundle property rather than assuming that negative-degree inclusions automatically are subbundles.
Apply the upper-bound argument to the rank-two vector bundle F=E∗⊗KC​, where KC​ is the canonical bundle. Let B be its upper degree bound. Choose m with m−1>B. For any point p∈C, a nonzero global section of F(−mp0​+p) would give a nonzero map
OC​(mp0​−p)⟶F.
(16)
Its image can be saturated to a saturated line subbundle on a smooth curve M⊂F. Locally the local ring of a smooth algebraic curve is a discrete valuation ring: dividing out the common power of a uniformizer makes the image a primitive rank-one summand, and its quotient is free. Thus the saturation is indeed a subbundle. The original map gives a nonzero global section of M⊗OC​(−mp0​+p) and consequently degM≥m−1, contradicting the upper bound. Hence H0(F(−mp0​+p))=0 for every p. By Serre duality,
H1(E(mp0​−p))≅H0(E∗⊗KC​(−mp0​+p))∗=0.
(17)
The exact sequence
0⟶E(mp0​−p)⟶E(mp0​)⟶E(mp0​)∣p​⟶0
(18)
and its long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology show that evaluation H0(E(mp0​))→E(mp0​)∣p​ is onto for every p. This proves the global generation of positive twists on a smooth curve with one bound on m that works at every point.
Write W=H0(E(mp0​)) and N=dimW. The evaluation map W⊗OC​→E(mp0​) is surjective with kernel bundle of rank N−2. Consider the incidence subset
Z={(p,s)∈C×W:s(p)=0}.
(19)
It is the total space of this kernel bundle, so dimZ=1+(N−2)=N−1. Its projection into W is closed because C is proper, and its image has dimension at most N−1<N. Choose s outside this proper closed subset. It vanishes nowhere. In a local trivialization at any point, one component of s is a unit, so it can be completed to a basis. Therefore OC​s​E(mp0​) is a subbundle with a locally free rank-one quotient. Twisting back gives
OC​(−mp0​)⊂E,degOC​(−mp0​)=−m⟶−∞.​
(20)
This proves the unbounded negative degrees of line subbundles. The nowhere-vanishing construction guarantees a subbundle at every point, including the zeros that might otherwise have occurred in a generic meromorphic inclusion. If the curve has several connected components, apply these constructions on each component and add the degrees; the same upper-bound and no-lower-bound conclusions follow.
