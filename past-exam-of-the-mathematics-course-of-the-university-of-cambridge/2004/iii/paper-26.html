Write Γ=SL2​(Z), H={τ:Imτ>0} and q=e2πiτ. The space Mk​ consists of holomorphic functions on the complex upper half-plane satisfying
f(cτ+daτ+b​)=(cτ+d)kf(τ)((ac​bd​)∈Γ)
(1)
and holomorphic at the cusp: their Fourier expansion of a modular form is f=∑n≥0​an​qn. The subspace Sk​ of cusp forms has a0​=0. Since all cusps are equivalent at level one, this condition treats every cusp. The element −I forces odd-weight forms to vanish.
Use the permitted properties of the normalized Eisenstein series and modular discriminant:
E4​=1+240∑n≥1​σ3​(n)qn,E6​=1−504∑n≥1​σ5​(n)qn,Δ=q∏n≥1​(1−qn)24.
(2)
They have integer Fourier coefficients and weights 4,6,12. For even k≥0, the assumed dimension formula gives d=⌊k/12⌋+1 except when k≡2(mod12), when d=⌊k/12⌋. For 0≤j<d, the remaining weight k−12j is nonnegative and is not two. Choose bj​∈{0,1} and aj​≥0 with 4aj​+6bj​=k−12j. Every allowable remaining weight admits such a choice. The forms
Fj​=ΔjE4aj​​E6bj​​=qj+O(qj+1)
(3)
give an integral triangular basis of level-one modular forms: their distinct first exponents prove independence, and their number d proves that they span.
Now take f with integral coefficients. Subtract its constant coefficient times F0​. The remainder still has integral coefficients and has no constant term. Subtract its coefficient at q times F1​, then its current coefficient at q2 times F2​, and continue. Every subtraction uses an integer. After d steps the remainder's first d coefficients vanish; triangularity and the basis property force that remainder to be zero. Consequently
f=j=0∑d−1​bj′​ΔjE4aj​​E6bj​​,bj′​∈Z.​
(4)
This proves integral polynomial generation without dividing by 1728. The relation E43​−E62​=1728Δ explains why the additional integral generator is useful, although over C two generators suffice. For k=0 the basis is the constant one; when Mk​=0, including weight two and odd weights, the claim is vacuous.

Start from the integral triangular basis of level-one modular forms. Proceed downwards in j. Set gd−1​=Fd−1​; once gj+1​,…,gd−1​ are constructed, subtract from Fj​ its coefficients at qj+1,…,qd−1 times these respective forms. The already constructed rows have a single nonzero coefficient among those initial positions, so this removes every unwanted initial coefficient without changing the coefficient one at qj. All subtractions use integers. The resulting integral echelon basis of level-one modular forms therefore has
gj​=qj+n≥d∑​cn​(j)qn,cn​(j)∈Z.​
(5)
Its first d coefficient vectors are the standard coordinate vectors, hence it is a basis. If another form had the same first d coefficients as gj​, their difference would have those coefficients all zero; the triangular basis proves that difference vanishes. This proves uniqueness of every gj​, not merely uniqueness up to basis change.
For any f∈Mk​, its expansion in this basis is f=∑j=0d−1​aj​(f)gj​, since its first d coefficients determine the basis coordinates. Comparing subsequent coefficients gives
an​(f)=j=0∑d−1​cn​(j)aj​(f),n≥d.​
(6)
The coefficients of f need not be integral for this last identity. When needed at earlier indices, extend the notation by cn​(j)=δnj​ for 0≤n<d. This agrees with the actual coefficients of the basis forms and is the convention needed for the next part's all-index wording.

Use the standard normalization of the Hecke operator, for which
am​(Tn​f)=∑r∣gcd(m,n)​rk−1amn/r2​(f),a1​(Tn​f)=an​(f).
(7)
The Hecke multiplication relations make these operators commute; a coefficient-based proof is included in 3(i). For a cusp form, a0​(f)=0, so the preceding coefficient relation says
a1​([Tn​−∑j=1d−1​cn​(j)Tj​]f)=0
(8)
for every f∈Sk​. Denote the bracketed operator by Dn​. A vanishing first coefficient alone would not prove Dn​=0. Instead, for every m≥1, commute it past Tm​ and apply the same relation to the cusp form Tm​f:
am​(Dn​f)=a1​(Tm​Dn​f)=a1​(Dn​Tm​f)=0.
(9)
Its constant coefficient is zero too. Thus every Fourier coefficient of Dn​f vanishes, and holomorphy gives Dn​f=0. We have proved
Tn​=j=1∑d−1​cn​(j)Tj​on Sk​.​
(10)
For n≥d these are the integers constructed in (ii); for 1≤n<d, the extension cn​(j)=δnj​ makes the formula tautological. Without that implicit extension the symbols in the printed all-index assertion are undefined. The j=0 term is excluded because its coefficient functional is zero on cusp forms. If Sk​=0, the operator statement is vacuous. This also exhibits the perfect integral Hecke pairing: the first d−1 coefficient functionals on the cusp space are dual to T1​,…,Td−1​.

For a period lattice Λ, define the Weierstrass elliptic function by
℘(z)=z−2+∑ω∈Λ∖{0}​((z−ω)−2−ω−2).
(11)
The summands on a compact set away from the lattice are O(∣ω∣−3), and that lattice sum converges. Thus the series and its termwise derivative converge normally. The function is even, and ℘′=−2∑ω∈Λ​(z−ω)−3 is odd and periodic by reindexing its absolutely convergent series. Hence ℘(z+ωj​)−℘(z) is constant; evaluation at −ωj​/2 and evenness make this constant zero for each basis period. The only poles of wp are double lattice poles, with principal part (z−ω)−2.
Let Gr​=∑ω=0​ω−r for the convergent weights used here. Expanding around zero gives
℘(z)=z−2+3G4​z2+5G6​z4+O(z6),℘′(z)=−2z−3+6G4​z+20G6​z3+O(z5).
(12)
Therefore
(℘′)2−4℘3=−60G4​z−2−140G6​+O(z2).
(13)
Put g2​=60G4​ and g3​=140G6​. The elliptic function (℘′)2−4℘3+g2​℘+g3​ has a removable singularity at zero and hence at every lattice point. It is entire and periodic, thus bounded on the plane and constant by the Liouville theorem. Its zero constant term makes that constant zero. This proves the Weierstrass elliptic differential equation
℘′(z)2=4℘(z)3−g2​℘(z)−g3​.​
(14)
The three classes ωi​/2, with ω3​=−ω1​−ω2​, are the three distinct nonzero two-torsion classes. Oddness and periodicity give ℘′(ωi​/2)=−℘′(ωi​/2), so they are zeros of the derivative. Their half-period values of the Weierstrass elliptic function ei​ are therefore roots of 4X3−g2​X−g3​.
They are distinct. If ei​=ej​=e with i=j, the elliptic function ℘−e would have a zero of multiplicity at least two at each of those distinct point classes, since its derivative vanishes there. It has only one pole on the torus, of order two. The argument principle on a fundamental cell equates its total zero and pole orders, contradicting at least four zeros. Thus e1​,e2​,e3​ are distinct, and the cubic factors as
℘′(z)2=4i=1∏3​(℘(z)−ei​).​
(15)
For the monic cubic X3−(g2​/4)X−g3​/4, the polynomial discriminant is (g23​−27g32​)/16=∏i<j​(ei​−ej​)2. Hence the nonvanishing discriminant of a complex lattice is
Δ(Λ)=g23​−27g32​=16i<j∏​(ei​−ej​)2=0.​
(16)
Here Δ(Λ) is the lattice discriminant, not the normalized q-series of Question 1. For Λ=Z+Zτ, the normalizations give g2​=(4π4/3)E4​ and g3​=(8π6/27)E6​, so Δ(Λ)=(2π)12Δ(τ).

Fix an even weight and use the determinant-normalized slash operator for modular forms
(f∣k​A)(τ)=det(A)k/2(cτ+d)−kf(Aτ),A∈GL2+​(Q).
(17)
It is unchanged by positive scalar multiples of A and satisfies (f∣A)∣B=f∣AB. The determinant-n matrix representatives for Hecke operators are
Πn​={(a0​bd​):a,d>0, ad=n, 0≤b<d}.
(18)
Indeed, integer row operations with determinant one send the first column of an integral determinant-n matrix to (a,0)t, with a>0; the second diagonal entry is d=n/a>0, and a row shear reduces b modulo d. This gives unique representatives of the left modular-group orbits.
Define the Hecke operator by
Tn​f=nk/2−1A∈Πn​∑​f∣k​A=nk−1ad=n∑​d−kb=0∑d−1​f(daτ+b​).​
(19)
The set of integral matrices of determinant n is stable under right multiplication by the modular group. Right multiplication therefore permutes its left orbits, proving the modular transformation law for Tn​f. Each summand is holomorphic in the upper half-plane. Its imaginary argument tends to infinity at the cusp, so the formula preserves cusp holomorphy and maps Mk​ to itself and Sk​ to itself.
Expand each summand in q. The finite sum over b vanishes unless the Fourier index is divisible by d, when it equals d. The coefficient at qm then receives a contribution for each a∣gcd(m,n), giving the Fourier coefficients of a composite-index Hecke operator formula
am​(Tn​f)=a∣gcd(m,n)∑​ak−1amn/a2​(f).​
(20)
For m=0 interpret the sum over all divisors of n, so the constant coefficient is σk−1​(n)a0​(f). In particular a1​(Tn​f)=an​(f) and integral coefficients are preserved.
Here is a useful direct derivation of the Hecke multiplication relations. On formal Fourier series put Up​f=∑m​apm​qm and Vp​f=f(qp). They obey Up​Vp​=1, although Vp​Up​ is generally not the identity. The coefficient formula gives
Tpr​=∑j=0r​pj(k−1)Vpj​Upr−j​.
(21)
Multiplying by Tp​=Up​+pk−1Vp​ and using Up​Vp​=1 separates the terms with j=0 from the others and yields
Tp​Tpr​=Tpr+1​+pk−1Tpr−1​(r≥1).
(22)
For distinct primes, all the relevant shift operators commute. Thus Tm​Tn​=Tmn​ when gcd(m,n)=1, and each prime-power operator is a polynomial in its prime operator. This proves commutativity. Induction on the smaller of two prime exponents, using the displayed recurrence, gives Tpr​Tps​=∑j=0min(r,s)​pj(k−1)Tpr+s−2j​. Factoring general indices into primes then proves
Tm​Tn​=a∣gcd(m,n)∑​ak−1Tmn/a2​.​
(23)
The auxiliary shifts need not themselves preserve the level-one form space; they are used only to prove identities of coefficient sequences.
For cusp forms, the Petersson inner product is
⟨f,g⟩=∫Γ\H​f(τ)g(τ)​yky2dxdy​.
(24)
It converges by exponential cusp decay, is positive-definite, and makes the Hecke operators self-adjoint. For the change-of-variable argument, the determinant-normalized slash action compensates exactly for the change in yk, while hyperbolic area is invariant. Integrating a correspondence term in the opposite direction replaces its matrix by its inverse. The adjugate A#=nA−1 is again an integral matrix of determinant n; it has the same slash action as A−1, because scalar matrices act trivially. Summing over the two sets of representatives, equivalently over the common finite covering domains of the correspondence, interchanges f and g and proves ⟨Tn​f,g⟩=⟨f,Tn​g⟩. This explains Hecke operators are self-adjoint for the Petersson inner product with the present normalization.
Commuting self-adjoint operators on the finite-dimensional cusp space have a common orthonormal eigenbasis: diagonalize one, restrict the others to its invariant eigenspaces and repeat. If f is a nonzero common Hecke eigenform, an​(f)=a1​(Tn​f)=λn​a1​(f). The first coefficient cannot vanish, since then every coefficient would vanish. Normalize it to one. The eigenvalue is then λn​=an​(f), and the common eigenspace is one-dimensional because all its normalized Fourier coefficients are fixed. The eigenvalues are real by self-adjointness and are algebraic integers, since the Hecke operators act by integer matrices on the integral cusp-form lattice from Question 1. The integral echelon basis and the operator identity there also show that T1​,…,Td−1​ are an integral basis of the integral Hecke algebra of level-one cusp forms; independence follows by evaluating their first-coefficient functionals on g1​,…,gd−1​.
The eigenform's coefficient relations are
am​an​=∑a∣gcd(m,n)​ak−1amn/a2​,apr+1​=ap​apr​−pk−1apr−1​.
(25)
They give the Euler product of a Hecke eigenform
L(f,s)=∑n≥1​nsan​​=∏p​(1−ap​p−s+pk−1−2s)−1
(26)
in a sufficiently far right half-plane. The Mellin transform of a cusp-form L-function completes this to Λ(f,s)=(2π)−sΓ(s)L(f,s)=∫0∞​f(iy)ys−1dy. Splitting at one and using f(i/y)=ikykf(iy) gives
Λ(f,s)=∫1∞​f(iy)(ys−1+ikyk−s−1)dy.
(27)
Exponential decay makes this an entire function of s, and the formula proves Λ(f,s)=ikΛ(f,k−s).
On the noncuspidal line in positive even weights k≥4, the normalized Eisenstein series has eigenvalues σk−1​(n), as follows from the same divisor formula for its coefficients; Mk​=CEk​⊕Sk​. As a cuspidal example, S12​ is spanned by the normalized modular discriminant. Its coefficients τ(n) are the Hecke eigenvalues, and the recurrence gives τ(4)=τ(2)2−211=−1472 from τ(2)=−24. Thus the Hecke theory converts analytic forms into multiplicative arithmetic data.

State the characteristic convention explicitly: for real α,β, define the theta function with characteristics by
θ[α,β](z,τ)=n∈Z∑​exp(πi(n+α)2τ+2πi(n+α)(z+β)),Imτ>0.​
(28)
Binary subscripts ϑab​ often denote this function with (α,β)=(a/2,b/2), a,b∈{0,1}. On compact subsets of the (z,τ) domain, a summand and any derivative are bounded by a polynomial in ∣n∣ times e−cn2+C∣n∣. Normal convergence therefore gives an entire function of z, holomorphic dependence on τ, and legitimate termwise differentiation. In particular the heat equation for theta functions with characteristics is
4πi∂τ​θ=∂z2​θ.​
(29)
Characteristics are defined modulo integers with a phase: θ[α+1,β]=θ[α,β] and θ[α,β+1]=e2πiαθ[α,β]. Shifting the summation index proves the spatial quasi-periods
θ(z+1)=e2πiαθ(z),θ(z+τ)=e−πiτ−2πi(z+β)θ(z).
(30)
These multipliers describe a degree-one line bundle on the complex torus with periods 1,τ, rather than an ordinary elliptic function. The logarithmic derivative is unchanged under one and decreases by 2πi under tau. Pairing opposite sides of a fundamental cell therefore makes its contour integral 2πi. The argument principle counts exactly one zero in a cell, with multiplicity.
The characteristic function is a translate of the unshifted theta function:
θ[α,β](z,τ)=eπiα2τ+2πiα(z+β)θ[0,0](z+ατ+β,τ).
(31)
At z=(1+τ)/2, the unshifted series cancels in pairs n,−n−1. Hence the zeros of theta functions with characteristics occur exactly and simply at
z=(1/2−α)τ+(1/2−β)(modZ+Zτ).​
(32)
Reindexing also gives θ[α,β](−z)=θ[−α,−β](z). For half-integer characteristics the parity is e4πiαβ, so only (1/2,1/2) is odd and vanishes at zero; the other three theta constants are nonzero.
The modular transformations of theta characteristics are obtained from Gaussian Poisson summation and elementary phase rearrangement. With the holomorphic branch of −iτ​ positive at τ=i,
θ[α,β](z/τ,−1/τ)=−iτ​eπiz2/τ+2πiαβθ[β,−α](z,τ),​
(33)
θ[α,β](z,τ+1)=e−πiα(α−1)θ[α,β+α−1/2](z,τ).​
(34)
For the first formula, apply Poisson summation to F(t)=eπiτ(t+α)2+2πi(t+α)(z+β). Completing the square in its Fourier transform gives
F(m)=(−iτ)−1/2e2πimα−πi(z+β−m)2/τ.
(35)
Rearranging ∑n​F(n)=∑m​F(m) proves the stated inversion law. For translation by one, use eπin2=eπin in the defining series; the remaining characteristic phase is precisely the displayed factor.
Write Q=eπiτ, so the modular-form variable of Question 1 is q=Q2, and put Θ2​=θ[1/2,0](0,τ), Θ3​=θ[0,0](0,τ) and Θ4​=θ[0,1/2](0,τ). Inversion interchanges Θ2​,Θ4​ and fixes Θ3​, each with factor −iτ​. Translation interchanges Θ3​,Θ4​ and multiplies Θ2​ by eπi/4. The Jacobi triple product gives
Θ2​=2Q1/4∏m≥1​(1−Q2m)(1+Q2m)2,
(36)
Θ3​=∏m≥1​(1−Q2m)(1+Q2m−1)2,Θ4​=∏m≥1​(1−Q2m)(1−Q2m−1)2.
(37)
It also gives the conventional odd theta function θ1​=−θ[1/2,1/2] its product 2Q1/4sin(πz)∏m≥1​(1−Q2m)(1−2Q2mcos(2πz)+Q4m). Differentiate at zero and multiply the three even products to obtain the Jacobi derivative formula
θ1′​(0,τ)=πΘ2​Θ3​Θ4​.​
(38)
In the constant-product simplification, ∏m​(1+Q2m)(1−Q4m−2)=1, by separating even and odd positive exponents. The products prove nonvanishing of all three even constants as well.
The Jacobi abstruse identity can be proved directly, without assuming it from the products. Splitting pairs of summation indices according to whether their sum and difference are even or odd gives
Θ3​(τ)2=Θ3​(2τ)2+Θ2​(2τ)2,Θ4​(τ)2=Θ3​(2τ)2−Θ2​(2τ)2,
(39)
Θ2​(τ)2=2Θ2​(2τ)Θ3​(2τ).
(40)
For example, u=(n+m)/2,v=(n−m)/2 are either both integers or both half-integers, and n2+m2=2u2+2v2. The alternating signs give the second identity; shifting both original indices by one half gives the third. Squaring the first two and subtracting then proves Θ34​=Θ24​+Θ44​.
These identities connect theta theory to modular forms and elliptic curves. The fourth powers are weight-two forms on the principal level-two congruence subgroup. The modular lambda function λ=Θ24​/Θ34​ obeys λ(−1/τ)=1−λ(τ) and λ(τ+1)=λ(τ)/(λ(τ)−1); the characteristic permutation kernel is Γ(2). It parametrizes an elliptic curve with its ordered two-torsion in Legendre form y2=x(x−1)(x−λ). The corresponding Klein j-invariant is 256(1−λ+λ2)3/[λ2(1−λ)2]. Finally the same products give
Δ(τ)=2−8(Θ2​Θ3​Θ4​)8.​
(41)
Indeed Θ2​Θ3​Θ4​=2Q1/4∏m​(1−Q2m)3, whose eighth power divided by 256 is q∏m​(1−qm)24. This recovers the normalized modular discriminant of Question 1 and displays the bridge between Gaussian theta sums and the lattice discriminant of Question 2.
