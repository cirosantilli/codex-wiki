Use rational coefficients and finite singular chains. For an N-dimensional stratified pseudomanifold with filtration Xj​, the lower middle perversity is
mˉ(k)=⌊2k−2​⌋(k≥2).​
(1)
A singular simplex σ:Δi→X is an allowable singular simplex when, for every k≥2, σ−1(XN−k​) lies in the (i−k+mˉ(k))-skeleton of Δi; negative-dimensional skeletons are empty. Let Ai​ be the span of these simplices in Si​(X;Q). The intersection chains are
ISi​(X)={c∈Ai​:∂c∈Ai−1​}.
(2)
The condition on ∂c is imposed after combining equal simplices and cancelling coefficients: the faces of an allowable simplex need not themselves be allowable. If c∈ISi​, then ∂c∈Ai−1​ and ∂(∂c)=0, so ∂c∈ISi−1​. Thus IS∗​ is a chain subcomplex of S∗​, and IHi​(X)=Hi​(IS∗​) defines intersection homology.
Write v for the vertex of cY. Its codimension is 2n, so the vertex condition for an allowable i-simplex is
σ−1(v)⊆(Δi)(i−n−1).
(3)
In particular all simplices of degree at most n avoid v. For i<n, both the degree-i chains and the degree-(i+1) chains that can bound them lie in cY∖{v}=Y×(0,1). The two chain complexes therefore have the same cycles and boundaries in degree i, proving the low-degree isomorphism.
For i≥n, let z be an allowable i-cycle. Cone every simplex of z radially to v, using the cone on Δi as Δi+1, and write the resulting chain as cz. At the vertex, coning an old inverse image in the (i−n−1)-skeleton gives a subset of the (i−n)-skeleton, and the new cone vertex also lies in this skeleton because i−n≥0. For every other singular stratum, coning the inverse image increases its permitted skeleton dimension by one, exactly as increasing the simplex dimension does. Thus cz is allowable. The usual cone identity is ∂(cz)=z−c(∂z)=z; since z is allowable, the boundary condition for intersection chains holds as well. Every cycle is consequently a boundary. This proves the intersection homology cone formula here:
IHi​(cY)={IHi​(Y×(0,1)),0,​i<n,i≥n.​​
(4)
The assumption dimY=2n−1 entails n≥1, so no augmented degree-zero exception arises in this coning argument.
For the suspension of a topological space, the two endpoints are collapsed separately, giving two distinct vertices. Use the following standard computational facts for intersection homology: a product with an interval has the same intersection homology as the original stratified space, via the projection and its stratum-preserving homotopy inverse; and an open cover has a Mayer–Vietoris sequence whose overlap map is the signed pair of inclusion maps. Cover the suspension by its two open cones. Their overlap is Y×(−ε,ε). Below degree n, the overlap map is, under the interval identifications, a↦(a,−a) in IHi​(Y)⊕IHi​(Y), hence is injective with cokernel IHi​(Y). In degree n both cone groups vanish and the next overlap map, in degree n−1, is still injective. Above degree n both adjacent cone groups vanish. Exactness therefore gives
IHi​(SuspY)=⎩⎨⎧​IHi​(Y),0,IHi−1​(Y),​i<n,i=n,i>n.​​
(5)
This differs from ordinary reduced suspension homology: below the cutoff the cycles cannot use either vertex, and in the middle degree coning kills them. If Y is a compact oriented Witt space, its Poincare duality pairs IHi​(Y) with IH2n−1−i​(Y). The displayed groups of the suspension are correspondingly paired in complementary degrees i and 2n−i, while its middle group is zero. The new vertices have even codimension 2n and thus impose no additional Witt space condition; the suspension is again Witt. Its middle intersection form has zero rank and, when its dimension is divisible by four, zero signature.

Put B=∂M and let v be the cone vertex. Specify the convention away from the Witt case: first take intersection cohomology to mean the hypercohomology of the intersection complex ICmˉ​, normalized in degree zero on the regular stratum. The lower middle perversity at the vertex is mˉ(2m+1)=m−1. The standard local cone calculation for this convention says
IHmˉi​(U)={Hi(B;Q),0,​i<m,i≥m,​
(6)
and restriction to U≃B is the identity below the cutoff. Here this can also be read directly from the construction ICmˉ​=τ≤m−1​Rj∗​Q, with j:M∖{v}↪M: punctured conical neighbourhoods retract onto B, and the truncation discards the degrees above m−1.
Define relative intersection cohomology as the hypercohomology of the homotopy fibre of restriction, namely RΓ(M,U;IC)=Cone(RΓ(M;IC)→RΓ(U;IC))[−1]. Restricting to the pair (M,U) gives a map to the corresponding ordinary relative cohomology, since IC∣M​=QM​. The induced map of exact triangles gives the requested commutative diagram:
⋯→⋯→​IHi(M,U)↓Hi(M,U)​→→​IHi(M)↓Hi(M)​→→​IHi(U)↓Hi(U)​→→​IHi+1(M,U)↓Hi+1(M,U)​→⋯→⋯.​
(7)
Both rows are long exact cohomology sequences of a pair. Commutativity includes the connecting homomorphisms, because they arise from the same map of restriction cones, with a common sign convention. The relative vertical maps are isomorphisms: excision removes the common conical neighbourhood, leaving precisely the manifold pair outside it, where IC is constant. Also the collar retracts onto B, so H∗(M,U)≅H∗(M,B).
The only possible nonmanifold stratum is v, with link B of dimension 2m. Since intersection homology of a manifold is ordinary homology, the exact necessary and sufficient condition is
M is Witt⟺Hm(B;Q)=0.​
(8)
The equivalence with the defining Hm​(B;Q)=0 follows from the universal coefficient theorem for cohomology over Q. In the Witt case the cone restriction is an isomorphism through degree m, since both degree-m groups are zero. Comparing the two exact rows, or applying the Five lemma to each five-term segment, gives IHi(M)≅Hi(M) for i≤m. For i>m, both IHi−1(U) and IHi(U) vanish, so the top row identifies the absolute group with the relative one. Hence
IHi(M)≅{Hi(M;Q),Hi(M,B;Q),​i≤m,i>m.​​
(9)
Without the Witt hypothesis, comparison still gives IHmˉi​(M)≅Hi(M) for i<m. At degree m the upper row reads
Hm−1(B)δ​Hm(M,B)⟶IHmˉm​(M)⟶0.
(10)
The lower row identifies the quotient by imδ with the image of the relative-to-absolute map. In higher degrees both cone terms vanish. Thus the full lower-middle Deligne answer is
IHmˉi​(M)≅⎩⎨⎧​Hi(M),im(Hm(M,B)→Hm(M)),Hi(M,B),​i<m,i=m,i>m.​​
(11)
For clarity, if IHi is instead defined as the linear dual of the lower-middle intersection chains of question 1, it corresponds to the complementary upper middle perversity Deligne sheaf. Its cone group retains Hm(B) as well. The same diagram then gives
IHnˉi​(M)≅⎩⎨⎧​Hi(M),im(Hm+1(M,B)→Hm+1(M)),Hi(M,B),​i≤m,i=m+1,i>m+1.​​
(12)
Equivalently the exceptional group is Hm+1(M,B)/im(Hm(B)δ​Hm+1(M,B)). These conventions agree under the stated Witt condition, explaining why that condition removes the ambiguity.
Take the solid torus M=S1×D2, whose boundary is the two-dimensional torus B=T2. Its ordinary cohomology is H0(M)=H1(M)=Q, zero otherwise; Poincare duality for a manifold with boundary gives H2(M,B)=H3(M,B)=Q and all other relative groups zero. The lower-middle Deligne formula gives
dimIHmˉi​(M)=(1,0,1,1)for i=0,1,2,3.​
(13)
Thus degrees one and two have different dimensions, so there can be no three-dimensional Poincare duality. In the dual-lower-chain convention the dimensions are (1,1,0,1), which likewise fail duality. Both computations exhibit the obstruction H1(T2)=Q2=0.

All cohomology below has rational coefficients. First describe the incidence space geometrically. The image of g is P(W)≅CP1, inside Gr(1,4)≅CP3. For a line U⊂W, choosing a plane V containing U is equivalent to choosing the line V/U in C4/U. If L is the tautological line bundle on P(W), then
FW​≅P((P(W)×C4)/L).
(14)
This is the projective bundle of a rank-three quotient bundle, with fibre CP2. Local frames of the quotient bundle give local product charts. In particular FW​ is an oriented real six-dimensional manifold.
For V∈SW​∖{W}, the intersection V∩W is exactly a line, so f−1(V) is a single point and the inverse map is V↦(V∩W,V). The intersection line varies continuously, indeed holomorphically, wherever its dimension is constant: in local Grassmannian charts it is the kernel of a matrix of constant rank, whose kernel bases can be chosen locally. Thus f is an isomorphism over the open stratum. At the remaining point,
f−1(W)=P(W)≅CP1.​
(15)
Set K=Rf∗​QFW​​, a derived direct image of sheaves. The proper base change for sheaves theorem identifies its derived stalks with the cohomology complexes of the fibres. Consequently K is the constant rational sheaf in degree zero on SW​∖{W}, and
Ha(iW∗​K)={Q,0,​a=0,2,a=0,2.​
(16)
The cohomology sheaves are therefore finite-rank local systems on the two strata, with no other nonzero degrees. This proves that K is a constructible complex of sheaves and verifies normalization. The open stratum has real dimension six, so the isolated point has codimension six. Its middle perversity is mˉ(6)=2, and the intersection-complex support and cosupport axioms require stalk vanishing in degrees above two. The fibre calculation verifies the support axiom, as well as vanishing in negative degrees.
For an oriented real six-dimensional manifold, Verdier duality gives DFW​​QFW​​≅QFW​​[6]. For a proper map, Verdier duality commutes with derived direct image. Applying these statements here yields
DSW​​K≅Rf∗​DFW​​QFW​​≅Rf∗​QFW​​[6]=K[6],Σ−6DSW​​K≅K.​
(17)
The duality identity iW!​DSW​​K≅D{W}​iW∗​K converts this into
Ha(iW!​K)≅H6−a(iW∗​K)∗.
(18)
Here iW!​K is the costalk, and dualization of rational complexes reverses degrees. Thus its only nonzero groups are Q in degrees four and six. In particular they vanish for a≤3, which is precisely the cosupport axiom at this isolated six-dimensional singularity. The uniqueness characterization by constructibility, normalization, support and cosupport now identifies K with the unshifted intersection complex of SW​. Taking hypercohomology, and using the composition of derived global sections with Rf∗​, gives
IH∗(SW​)≅H∗(SW​,K)≅H∗(FW​).​
(19)
Finally apply the fibration result allowed in the question to g:FW​→P(W). Its base is the simply connected complex projective line, its fibre is the Complex projective plane, and the projective-bundle charts above verify the fibration hypothesis. Hence, as graded rational vector spaces,
IH∗(SW​)≅H∗(CP1)⊗H∗(CP2).​
(20)
The resulting Betti numbers and Poincaré polynomial are
dimIHi(SW​)=⎩⎨⎧​1,2,0,​i=0,6,i=2,4,otherwise,​PIH​(t)=1+2t2+2t4+t6.​
(21)
This uses H∗(CPr)=Q[h]/(hr+1) with degh=2. No assertion that an arbitrary projective fibration has the product cohomology ring is needed.

An N-dimensional stratified pseudomanifold has a closed filtration ∅=X−1​⊆X0​⊆⋯⊆XN​=X with each nonempty Xs​∖Xs−1​ an s-dimensional manifold, dense top stratum, and XN−1​=XN−2​. Each point of an s-dimensional stratum has a stratum-preserving conical neighbourhood Rs×cL, where L is a compact (N−s−1)-dimensional stratified pseudomanifold. The definition is inductive, starting with zero-dimensional spaces. There are no singular codimension-one strata; connectedness of links is not required. One may work in the usual triangulable category used for Witt bordism.
A rational Witt space is such a stratified pseudomanifold for which every stratum of odd codimension 2r+1 has link L satisfying IHrmˉ​(L;Q)=0. No extra condition is imposed at even codimension. A Witt space with collared boundary has Witt interior and Witt boundary, and a stratum-preserving collar ∂X×[0,ε) with the product stratification. An orientation means an orientation of the regular stratum, inducing the boundary orientation on its collar.
A three-pronged graph, with its central vertex and open edges as strata, is a stratified space but not a one-dimensional pseudomanifold: the central vertex is a singular codimension-one stratum, and deleting it leaves three local branches instead of the two branches of a one-manifold. For a non-Witt pseudomanifold, take the suspension of T2. Its two vertices have codimension three and link T2, whose middle group is IH1​(T2;Q)=H1​(T2;Q)=Q2. It violates the Witt space condition.
The rational Witt bordism classification in dimensions divisible by four states that, for every k≥1, the middle intersection form induces an isomorphism
Ω4kWitt​ ∼ ​W(Q),[X]⟼[IH2k(X;Q),λX​].​
(22)
Here W(Q) is the Witt group of a field, so a nonsingular symmetric form represents zero precisely when it is metabolic, that is, when it has a half-dimensional totally isotropic subspace. The Complex projective plane gives a nonzero example: its middle group is generated by the degree-two class h, with ⟨h2,[CP2]⟩=1. Its form is ⟨1⟩, of signature one; a metabolic form has signature zero. Thus [CP2]=0 in Ω4Witt​.
For a closed oriented Witt space X of dimension 2k+1, take the closed cone
Y=(X×[0,1])/(X×{0}),∂Y=X×{1}.
(23)
Away from its vertex it is a stratified product with an interval, so all old Witt space conditions persist. At the new vertex the codimension is 2k+2, which is even; hence its odd-dimensional link X creates no new Witt condition. The collar X×(1−ε,1] is explicit, and the product orientation may be chosen to induce the given orientation on X. This is a compact oriented Witt space with collared boundary, proving
Ω2k+1Witt​=0.​
(24)
Now let dimX=2. Its singular strata are zero-dimensional. Their links are compact one-dimensional pseudomanifolds, which have no singular strata and are therefore nonempty finite disjoint unions of circles. A single circle is allowed, since a chosen stratum need not be intrinsically singular. The Witt condition is automatic in dimension two, because singular strata have even codimension.
Construct the normalization of a two-dimensional pseudomanifold concretely. Remove disjoint small conical neighbourhoods of the finitely many zero-stratum points. The remainder is a compact oriented surface with boundary, with one boundary circle for each local branch. Cap every such circle with a separate disk. The result X is a closed compact oriented topological surface. Map each new disk homeomorphically onto its branch of the original cone, sending all the disk centres belonging to one old singular point to that point. Along the disk boundaries this agrees with the identification with the remainder, so it defines a continuous surjection π:X→X. It is a homeomorphism off these finitely many centres, in particular over the prescribed open stratum. Every fibre is a point except over a zero-stratum point, where it is the finite set of its branch centres. Thus every fibre is zero-dimensional, as required.
Each component of X bounds an oriented handlebody: a surface of genus g is the boundary of a three-ball with g one-handles attached. Let H be the disjoint union of these handlebodies, oriented so that ∂H=X. Form
Z=H∪π​(X×[0,1]),
(25)
gluing x∈∂H to (π(x),0). The quotient is compact and Hausdorff, and its only remaining boundary is X×{1}, with an explicit product collar. Near regular points of the gluing locus, two half-ball neighbourhoods glue to a three-ball; the induced orientations match after choosing the collar orientation in the usual boundary-gluing convention.
It remains to check the singular links, rather than merely assert that this quotient is a Witt space. If an old point p has r circle branches, the new one-dimensional stratum {p}×(0,1) has link the disjoint union of those r circles. This has codimension two and imposes no Witt condition. At (p,0), each branch supplies a half-three-ball from H and another from the collar. The halves glue to a full three-ball; the different branches share only the positive collar axis. Intersecting with a small link therefore gives r copies of S2 identified at one common point. This is a two-dimensional pseudomanifold with zero middle intersection homology: an allowable one-cycle avoids the common point, so decomposes into cycles in the r punctured spheres, each homeomorphic to R2; each such cycle bounds there. Consequently IH1​(⋁a=1r​S2;Q)=0. This is exactly the Witt condition at the new codimension-three stratum. The boundary has the required product stratification. Hence Z is a three-dimensional Witt space with collared boundary with ∂Z≅X, proving
Ω2Witt​=0.​
(26)
For the final product assertion, “product” means a nontrivial product with at least two positive-dimensional factors; a single four-dimensional factor would allow the preceding CP2 counterexample. State the standard rational intersection-homology product fact used here: products of Witt spaces are Witt, their intersection cohomology satisfies the Künneth theorem, and the complementary-degree intersection pairing on elementary tensors is the product of the factor pairings, with the sign (−1)degβdegα′ for α⊗β and α′⊗β′. Terms whose degrees in a factor do not add to that factor's dimension pair to zero. Regrouping factors reduces total dimension four to cases 1+3 or 2+2.
In the 1+3 case, write X=A×B. The middle intersection cohomology is
IH2(X)=(IH0(A)⊗IH2(B))⊕(IH1(A)⊗IH1(B)).
(27)
Each summand is totally isotropic, because the degrees in A are respectively 0+0 and 1+1, neither equal to one. They pair perfectly with one another by factor Poincare duality. Thus either summand has half the total dimension and is a Lagrangian for the middle symmetric form.
For dimensions 2+2, the middle group is the direct sum
(IH0(A)⊗IH2(B))⊕(IH1(A)⊗IH1(B))⊕(IH2(A)⊗IH0(B)).
(28)
The outside summands are isotropic and pair perfectly with one another. Each factor's degree-one pairing is a nonsingular skew-symmetric form, so choose a Lagrangian subspace LA​⊂IH1(A). Inside the central summand, LA​⊗IH1(B) is isotropic and has half its dimension. It is orthogonal to both outside summands by their degrees. Therefore
(IH0(A)⊗IH2(B))⊕(LA​⊗IH1(B))
(29)
is an isotropic subspace of half the full middle dimension. In both cases the form is metabolic. The Witt bordism classification now proves the desired [X]=0 in Ω4Witt​. This also proves the assertion for more factors, using the regrouping just described.

For a cochain complex E, define the shift of a cochain complex by
(ΣE)i=Ei+1,dΣEi​=−dEi+1​.​
(30)
For a cochain map a:E→F, put (Σa)i=ai+1. This is again a cochain map, so the construction is a functor. The sign ensures the standard cone identities; the shifted differential squares to zero. Iteration gives E[r]i=Ei+r and differential (−1)rdE​, and Hi(E[r])=Hi+r(E).
For ϕ:E→F, its algebraic mapping cone is
C(ϕ)i=Fi⊕Ei+1,dC​(f,e)=(dF​f+ϕe,−dE​e).​
(31)
Applying this differential twice gives first component dF2​f+dF​ϕe−ϕdE​e=0, since ϕ is a cochain map, and second component dE2​e=0. The maps
u:F→C(ϕ),u(f)=(f,0),v:C(ϕ)→ΣE,v(f,e)=e
(32)
are cochain maps: dC​u(f)=(dF​f,0)=u(dF​f) and v(dC​(f,e))=−dE​e=dΣE​v(f,e). They are maps of complexes of sheaves, because every displayed operation is a sheaf morphism in each degree.
The bounded below derived category of sheaves D(X)=D+(X) can be constructed by first quotienting the category of bounded below cochain complexes by cochain homotopy and then formally inverting all quasi-isomorphisms. A morphism may be represented by a roof E∼​E′→F. Alternatively, take injective resolutions of sheaves and use homotopy classes of maps between the resulting injective complexes. The cohomology sheaf functors descend to the localization, because they already identify homotopic maps and turn quasi-isomorphisms into isomorphisms. If E≅0 in D(X), applying these functors gives Hi(E)=0 for every i. Conversely, if all these sheaves vanish, the unique map E→0 is a quasi-isomorphism, which becomes invertible in D(X). Hence
E≅0 in D(X)⟺Hi(E)=0 for every i.​
(33)
An exact triangle in a derived category is a triangle isomorphic to a cone triangle
Eϕ​Fu​C(ϕ)v​ΣE.
(34)
In particular the last arrow is part of the structure; a mere sequence of three objects with successive composite zero does not suffice.
Suppose 0→Eϕ​Fψ​G→0 is degreewise exact. Define
r:C(ϕ)→G,r(f,e)=ψ(f).
(35)
Then r(dC​(f,e))=ψ(dF​f)+ψϕ(e)=dG​ψ(f)=dG​r(f,e), so r is a cochain map. It is natural under maps of short exact sequences. It is degreewise surjective as a sheaf map, and its kernel can be identified with Ei⊕Ei+1 using (a,e)↦(ϕ(a),e). In these coordinates the kernel differential is
d(a,e)=(dE​a+e,−dE​e).
(36)
There is a contracting homotopy hi(a,e)=(0,a) in degree i−1:
dh(a,e)=(a,−dE​a),hd(a,e)=(0,dE​a+e),dh+hd=id.
(37)
Thus the kernel has zero cohomology sheaves. The cohomology-sheaf exact sequence is the sequence of the short exact sequence of complexes 0→kerr→C(ϕ)→G→0, or equivalently its ordinary complex-cohomology sequence at every stalk. It follows that r induces isomorphisms of all cohomology sheaves, proving
C(ϕ)∼​G is a natural quasi-isomorphism.​
(38)
Under this isomorphism the cone triangle becomes the exact triangle
Eϕ​Fψ​Gδ​ΣE,
(39)
where δ=v∘r−1 in the derived category.
For the final construction write U=S2∖S1 and set A=i!​QU​, B=QS2​, C=j∗​QS1​, each initially placed in degree zero. The stated sequence 0→A→B→C→0 is exact on stalks: on U its first map is an isomorphism and its last sheaf is zero; on the equator its first sheaf is zero and the second map is an isomorphism. Take its connecting morphism
μ=δ:C⟶A[1]in D(S2).​
(40)
The source has only H0(C)=C, whereas the target has only H−1(A[1])=A. Therefore every induced map Hi(μ) is zero: in any degree at least one of its source and target is zero. This is a ghost morphism in a derived category.
To prove the derived morphism itself is nonzero, apply derived global sections, giving the hypercohomology sequence. The extension by zero identifies H∗(S2;A) with the compactly supported cohomology of U, and closed direct image identifies H∗(S2;C) with H∗(S1;Q). The complement U is two open disks, each homeomorphic to R2, so
Hcq​(U;Q)={Q2,0,​q=2,q=2.​
(41)
The degree-one part of the resulting exact sequence is
0=H1(S2;Q)⟶H1(S1;Q)=Qμ∗​​Hc2​(U;Q)=Q2⟶H2(S2;Q)=Q⟶0.
(42)
Thus μ∗​ is injective, in particular nonzero. With the hemispheres oriented from S2, the last map is (a,b)↦a+b, so an appropriately oriented equator generator maps to (1,−1). A zero morphism in the derived category of sheaves would induce a zero map after derived global sections and taking cohomology. Consequently μ=0, although Hi(μ)=0 for every i. This establishes explicitly that the collection of cohomology-sheaf functors detects zero objects but is not faithful on morphisms.
