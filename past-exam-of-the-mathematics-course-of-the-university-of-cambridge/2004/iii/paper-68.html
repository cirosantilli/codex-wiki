In cylindrical coordinates the particle's radial and vertical equations are r¨−rϕ˙​2=−Φr​ and z¨=−Φz​. Axisymmetry gives conservation of angular momentum, with specific angular momentum h=r2ϕ˙​. Eliminating the cyclic azimuthal motion therefore gives
r¨=−∂r​(Φ+2r2h2​),z¨=−∂z​(Φ+2r2h2​).
(1)
Thus Φ1​=Φ+h2/(2r2)​ governs the meridional motion. Its conserved energy is 21​(r˙2+z˙2)+Φ1​. The additional term is the centrifugal barrier appropriate to fixed angular momentum.
For a point mass, Φ=−GM/r2+z2​. If h=0, the vertical derivative vanishes only at z=0, and the radial stationary condition there gives r0​=h2/(GM). This is the sole stationary point with r>0, at energy −GM/(2r0​). Its Hessian matrix is
∇r,z2​Φ1​​(r0​,0)​=r03​GM​(10​01​).
(2)
Consequently the local contours are closed, approximately circular curves about a strict minimum. Finite negative-energy contours above the minimum remain closed; the centrifugal barrier prevents approach to the axis and the negative energy prevents escape to infinity. The zero contour satisfies z2=4r4/r02​−r2, while positive-energy contours can be unbounded. The left panel illustrates these features; portions of the outer contours extend outside the plotting window.
Each panel uses its own circular radius as length unit and GM/r0​ as potential unit. Linearizing near the minimum gives
δr¨=−ΩK2​δr,δz¨=−ΩK2​δz,ΩK2​=r03​GM​.
(3)
The radial and vertical epicyclic frequencies both equal the circular orbital frequency. Circular Kepler orbits are stable to small meridional perturbations of a freely moving particle. With no dissipation the oscillations persist rather than decay. A change in h shifts the circular radius to a nearby orbit; azimuthal phase itself is neutral by rotational symmetry. If h=0, there is no stationary point or centrifugal barrier and this circular-orbit stability discussion does not apply.

The imposed constraint now fixes ϕ˙​=ω, while a torque allows h=r2ω to vary as the particle moves radially. The meridional equations become r¨=−Φr​+ω2r and z¨=−Φz​. Hence Φ2​=Φ−21​ω2r2​ is the appropriate effective potential. One must not substitute a variable h into the fixed-angular-momentum potential and then differentiate as though h were constant.
For a point mass and nonzero ω, the stationary point is (rc​,0) with rc​=(GM/ω2)1/3. Its meridional Hessian matrix is
∇r,z2​Φ2​​(rc​,0)​=ω2(−30​01​).
(4)
It is a saddle, at Φ2​=−3GM/(2rc​). Near it the contours are hyperbolas, with the saddle contour locally tangent to z=±3​(r−rc​), as shown in the right panel. On the equatorial plane the potential tends downward both toward the point mass and at large cylindrical radius; at fixed r it increases as ∣z∣ increases. The radial perturbation obeys δr¨=3ω2δr, while the vertical perturbation obeys δz¨=−ω2δz. Thus the radial motion has growing and decaying exponential solutions and the vertical motion is oscillatory. For ω=0 no such stationary point exists.
This saddle is the basis of magnetocentrifugal acceleration. A sufficiently strong, open poloidal magnetic field can enforce approximate corotation with its disc footpoint while allowing motion along the field. If its outward tangent makes angle ϑ with the vertical, a small distance l along it has δr=lsinϑ, δz=lcosϑ. Its leading potential change at a Keplerian rotation footpoint is
ΔΦ2​=21​ω2l2(cos2ϑ−3sin2ϑ)+O(l3)=21​ω2l2(1−4sin2ϑ)+O(l3).
(5)
The thirty-degree magnetocentrifugal launching criterion follows: cold local launching has a downhill quadratic direction when ϑ>30∘​ from the vertical. Equality is marginal at quadratic order and depends on higher terms and field-line geometry. The field transmits the torque needed to increase the material's angular momentum; a freely moving particle has no such constraint and instead sees the stable minimum in part (a). The local criterion does not by itself prove global escape or determine the mass loading of a complete magnetohydrodynamic wind.

The centripetal acceleration of the purely rotating barotropic fluid is −rΩ2er​. For p=Kρ2, the pressure force satisfies ρ−1∇p=2K∇ρ, so its barotropic enthalpy function is w=2Kρ. The equilibrium equation is therefore
∇(w+Φ)=rΩ2(r,z)er​.
(6)
Taking its curl gives ∂z​(rΩ2)=0. Thus Ω2 is independent of z; for a continuous, consistently signed rotation law, so is Ω. This proves the barotropic cylindrical rotation theorem. Integrating the remaining radial force yields
2Kρ+Φ3​=C,Φ3​=Φ−∫rΩ2(r)dr.​
(7)
The constant C is common to a connected fluid region.
Interpret the semi-thickness as a free surface with ρ(r,±H)=0, taking the exterior pressure to be negligible. At H=ϵr, the gravitational potential on this surface is Φs​=−GM/[r1+ϵ2​]. The first integral evaluated at the surface says C=Φs​(r)−∫rΩ2dr. Differentiating along it gives
0=r21+ϵ2​GM​−rΩ2,
(8)
and therefore Ω2=(1+ϵ2)−1/2GM/r3​. Radial pressure support makes this slightly slower than Keplerian rotation for nonzero thickness.
Subtracting the free-surface first integral from its value inside the disc gives the complete density profile
ρ(r,z)=2KGM​[r2+z2​1​−r1+ϵ2​1​],∣z∣≤ϵr.
(9)
It is nonnegative for K>0. Integrating over height, with y=z/r, gives
Σ(r)=KGM​∫0ϵ​[1+y2​1​−1+ϵ2​1​]dy=KGM​[arsinhϵ−1+ϵ2​ϵ​]​.
(10)
Thus the surface density is independent of r in this quadratic-polytrope disk with constant aspect ratio. The factor 1/r in density is canceled by the scale r in the vertical extent. The free-surface assumption is essential to this determination; a prescribed nonzero boundary pressure would require its own boundary data. No radial edges are specified, so the formula describes the local equilibrium rather than a finite-mass disc extended to all radii.

Use outward radial mass flux F=2πrΣur​ and outward stress-carried angular-momentum flux G. The PDF includes a second conservation equation omitted from the converted TeX:
∂t​(Σh)+2πr1​∂r​(Fh+G)=−T.
(11)
Together with mass conservation, Σt​+(2πr)−1Fr​=−S, it fixes the signs of the wind terms. Subtract h times the mass equation, noting that the prescribed h(r) is independent of time. One obtains
Fdrdh​+∂r∂G​=2πr(Sh−T).
(12)
The viscous torque in an accretion disk in this outward-flux convention is G=−2πνˉΣr3Ω′. Thus, where h′=0,
F=2π(h′)−1[∂r​(νˉΣr3Ω′)+r(Sh−T)].
(13)
Putting this result back into mass conservation gives
Σt​=−r1​∂r​{(h′)−1[∂r​(νˉΣr3Ω′)+r(Sh−T)]}−S.​
(14)
The derivation requires a fixed rotation law; it would acquire additional terms if h evolved independently in time.
For Keplerian rotation, h=GMr​, h′=h/(2r), and r3Ω′=−(3/2)h. With T=qSh, the radial flux becomes
F=−6πr​∂r​(r​νˉΣ)−4π(q−1)r2S.
(15)
Consequently the wind-loaded Keplerian disk evolution equation is
Σt​=r3​∂r​[r​∂r​(r​νˉΣ)]+r1​∂r​[2(q−1)r2S]−S.​
(16)
The final term removes mass locally. The extra flux term describes the disc's radial response when the outflow removes more or less than the local specific angular momentum. In particular, q=1 cancels this additional transport term without canceling the local mass sink.

Set S=T=0 and choose νˉ(r)=ν0​r​, with ν0​>0 constant. Define y=r​ and g=r3/2Σ. Then r​νˉΣ=ν0​g, and ∂r​=(2y)−1∂y​. Directly transforming the Keplerian viscous diffusion equation gives
Σt​=4r3/23ν0​​gyy​,gt​=43ν0​​gyy​.
(17)
The heat-equation transform for linear-radius disk viscosity is completed by x=2y/3ν0​​:
x=23ν0​r​​,g=r3/2Σ,gt​=gxx​.​
(18)
Equivalently one can use g=r​νˉΣ, which differs only by a constant. This is the viscosity law making the transformed diffusivity constant under this square-root-radius reduction. Initial and boundary conditions transform too; the change of variables does not supply unspecified disc-edge data.
The physical diffusion arises from angular momentum transport. Differential rotation creates a viscous shear stress, which transfers angular momentum between neighboring annuli. Gas that loses angular momentum moves inward, while gas that gains it can move outward. Combining conservation of angular momentum with mass conservation turns the stress gradient into a radial mass flux and its divergence into a second radial derivative. Positive kinematic viscosity smooths the appropriately weighted profile; matter need not all move in one direction, and an accreting disc can simultaneously spread outward. Viscosity dissipates orbital energy but redistributes angular momentum internally except for boundary losses.

Put β=2(q−1)>0. With zero viscosity and S=f(r)Σ, the wind-loaded Keplerian disk evolution equation becomes
Σt​=r1​∂r​(βr2fΣ)−fΣ=βrfΣr​+[β(2f+rf′)−f]Σ.
(19)
On an interval with f>0, introduce the inward travel-time coordinate and weighted density
x′(r)=∫rβuf(u)du​,g′(r,t)=f(r)r2−1/βΣ(r,t).​
(20)
Primes on x′ and g′ denote the new variables, not differentiation. To verify the transformation, let W=fr2−1/β and w=βrf. Its logarithmic derivative satisfies
wWWr​​=βrf′+(2β−1)f=β(2f+rf′)−f.
(21)
Since ∂x′​=w∂r​, multiplication of the density equation by W gives exactly
∂t​g′=∂x′​g′.​
(22)
This establishes inward transport by an angular-momentum-enhanced disk wind, including the amplitude weighting rather than only a characteristic speed.
The general local advection equation solution is g′=F(x′+t); an initial profile fixes F on the interval swept out by its characteristics. A constant wave phase therefore obeys dx′/dt=−1, or
dtdr​=−βrf(r)<0,Σ(r,t)=f(r)r2−1/βF(x′(r)+t)​.​
(23)
Thus the transformed wave propagates inward. The physical surface density is not a rigid profile in r: its weighting accounts for radial compression and loss of mass into the outflow. The same velocity follows independently from the mass flux in part (a), ur​=F/(2πrΣ)=−βrf. If f vanishes, the coordinate change applies only on the neighboring positive-loss intervals; at a loss-free point this wind-driven velocity vanishes.
A magnetocentrifugal acceleration mechanism can produce q>1. A poloidal field anchored to the disc transmits a torque to the outflow and enforces approximate corotation below its Alfvén surface. The wind's total specific angular momentum is Ω0​rA2​, where rA​ is its cylindrical Alfvén radius, compared with the footpoint value h0​=Ω0​r02​. Hence q=(rA​/r0​)2>1 for a lever arm beyond the footpoint. Magnetic stresses remove the surplus angular momentum and drive accretion without an effective internal viscosity.

The additional conditions are ∇⋅u=0,∇⋅B=0​: incompressible flow and the solenoidal magnetic field constraint. The scalar Ψ combines pressure per unit density with magnetic pressure per unit density and any conservative gravitational and centrifugal potentials. In particular, the Lorentz force identity separates magnetic tension from −∇(B2/2μ0​ρ). Adding this latter term to the rotating-flow pressure potential gives the pressure appearing in the stated equation; the Coriolis force remains explicit.
Work in magnetic velocity units B/μ0​ρ​, and write U=−2Axey​ for the background shearing sheet flow. Its advective acceleration is zero, and 2Ωez​×U=4ΩAxex​=−∇(−2ΩAx2), so the stated quadratic background pressure potential gives an exact balance. A spatially uniform mean magnetic field must evolve as
bˉ˙=−2Abˉx​ey​,bˉx​,bˉz​=constant,bˉy​(t)=bˉy​(0)−2Abˉx​t.​
(24)
This is stretching of radial field into azimuthal field by differential rotation.
For the shearing wave phase Θ=k(t)⋅x, advection by the background gives
(∂t​+U⋅∇)Θ=k˙⋅x−2Axky​.
(25)
It vanishes identically if
k˙=2Aky​ex​,kx​(t)=kx​(0)+2Aky​t,ky​,kz​=constant.​
(26)
The changing wavevector describes the shear of initially planar wavefronts. The mean-field stretching and wavevector evolution cancel in the Alfvén frequency:
dtd​(k⋅bˉ)=2Aky​bˉx​−2Aky​bˉx​=0.
(27)
Hence ωA​=k⋅bˉ is constant​.
These solutions are exact finite-amplitude disturbances, not merely a linearization. To see this, let u′=Re(veiΘ) and B′=Re(beiΘ) in magnetic velocity units. The divergence constraints give k⋅v=k⋅b=0. Thus both real disturbances are pointwise perpendicular to k, whereas all their spatial derivatives are along k. Every quadratic term u′⋅∇u′, B′⋅∇B′, u′⋅∇B′ and B′⋅∇u′ vanishes, including terms involving conjugate amplitudes. This extends the exact incompressible shearing wave to an exact magnetic shearing wave. The gas pressure can compensate quadratic magnetic-pressure terms even though the combined Ψ contains only its stated background and wave harmonic.
The remaining wave terms are therefore precisely
v˙−2Avx​ey​+2Ωez​×vb˙k⋅v​=−ikψ+iωA​b−νk2v,=−2Abx​ey​+iωA​v−ηk2b,=k⋅b=0.​​
(28)
The shear terms arise from perturbations acting on ∇U, the magnetic terms from the uniform field acting on the wave, and the last terms from diffusion. Pressure enforces the moving-wavevector velocity constraint: differentiating it gives
ik2ψ=4Aky​vx​−2Ωk⋅(ez​×v).
(29)
Using this pressure leaves k⋅v=0 invariant. Differentiating k⋅b in the induction equation similarly leaves it zero. Thus the evolution is consistent with both constraints for initially transverse amplitudes.
Now take positive Ω, so the stipulated condition implies 0<A<2Ω, and take magnetic Prandtl number ν/η=1, with ν=η>0. For a vertical wavevector k=kez​, use the equal-diffusivity magnetorotational channel form
v=(V,V,0),b=(B,−B,0),ψ=0.
(30)
The two horizontal momentum equations, after adding them, give V˙=(A−νk2)V. Either component then gives iωA​B=(A−2Ω)V. The first induction equation gives (∂t​+νk2)B=iωA​V. These equations are simultaneously solved by
ωA2​=A(2Ω−A),B=AiωA​​V,V(t)=V0​eγt,γ=A−νk2.​
(31)
The second induction component gives the same relation, since By​=−Bx​. Exponential growth occurs when νk2<A​. The quarter-cycle phase between magnetic and velocity perturbations is required by the factor i in the Fourier equations.
For Keplerian rotation, expanding the local shear gives 2A=−(rΩ′)=3Ω/2, hence A=3Ω/4. Therefore ωA2​=15Ω2/16. With vAz​=Bˉz​/μ0​ρ​ and ωA​=kvAz​, the growth condition for this prescribed polarization becomes
vAz2​>ν(2Ω−A)=45​νΩ,∣Bˉz​∣>45​μ0​ρνΩ​.​
(32)
A weaker field would require a shorter wavelength to retain this mode's selected Alfvén frequency, making diffusive damping too strong. The required wavelength must also fit any actual vertical domain; no vertical boundary conditions are specified here.
The field bound is for the requested equal-component mode, rather than a universal necessary condition for all magnetorotational instability. This distinction matters if all vertical wavelengths are admitted. Indeed, let s=γ+νk2 for a general horizontal polarization. Eliminating its two magnetic amplitudes gives (s+ωA2​/s)vx​−2Ωvy​=0 and (s+ωA2​/s)vy​+[2(Ω−A)−2AωA2​/s2]vx​=0. Their determinant gives
(s2+ωA2​)2+κ2s2−4ΩAωA2​=0,κ2=4Ω(Ω−A).
(33)
For a Keplerian disk, κ2=Ω2. As a concrete counterexample to a universal reading of the bound, in consistent dimensionless units take Ω=1, A=3/4, ν=η=1, vAz​=0.1, and k=0.01. The positive root gives s≃0.00173205 and γ=s−0.0001>0, even though vAz2​=0.01<1.25. This mode has a different polarization and a much longer wavelength. Real finite-height discs restrict such wavelengths; the PDF supplies no such restriction.
Finally, the horizontally averaged radial flux of azimuthal momentum is the sum of Reynolds stress and magnetic tension stress:
Txy​=ρ⟨ux′​uy′​⟩−μ0​1​⟨Bx′​By′​⟩=2ρ​(∣V∣2+∣B∣2)>0.
(34)
Multiplication by the local radius gives an outward angular-momentum flux. Equal radial and azimuthal velocities give the largest positive product at fixed horizontal kinetic energy, while opposite magnetic components give the largest positive −Bx​By​ at fixed horizontal magnetic energy. Both follow from 2∣ab∣≤∣a∣2+∣b∣2. This stress-maximizing magnetorotational channel polarization therefore makes both contributions reinforce outward angular momentum transport. It extracts shear energy at rate −Txy​∂x​Uy​=2ATxy​>0, explaining why these correlations are especially effective in driving accretion.
