We derive the required Calderón–Zygmund decomposition and apply the preceding two parts. Use the Fourier transform convention f​(ξ)=∫e−ix⋅ξf(x)dx, and put A=∥K∥∞​. The Plancherel theorem gives
∥Tf∥2​≤A∥f∥2​.
(28)
For completeness this starts with smooth rapidly decreasing inputs, where K∗f​=Kf​, and extends by L2 density. It agrees with the pointwise convolution in the question: if fj​→f in L2, then ∥K∗(fj​−f)∥∞​≤∥K∥2​∥fj​−f∥2​. Thus there is no need to assume that K itself is integrable.
Fix f∈L1(Rd), let F=∥f∥1​, and choose λ>0. Select the maximal dyadic cubes Q for which ∣Q∣−1∫Q​∣f∣>λ. They exist above every qualifying cube because averages over increasingly large ancestors tend to zero. They are disjoint, their total volume is at most F/λ, and maximality gives
λ<∣Q∣1​∫Q​∣f∣≤2dλ.
(29)
Outside their union, dyadic differentiation gives ∣f∣≤λ almost everywhere. One can obtain this differentiation fact from the allowed martingale convergence theorem: on each fixed dyadic cube the averages are conditional expectations of the integrable restriction, their almost-everywhere limit exists, and approximation by dyadic step functions shows in L1 that this limit is f.
Let fQ​=∣Q∣−1∫Q​f, define g=f outside the selected cubes and g=fQ​ on Q, and set bQ​=(f−fQ​)1Q​. This constructs

∥g∥∞​≤2dλ,∥g∥1​≤F,∥g∥22​≤2dλF,∑Q​∥bQ​∥1​≤2F.
