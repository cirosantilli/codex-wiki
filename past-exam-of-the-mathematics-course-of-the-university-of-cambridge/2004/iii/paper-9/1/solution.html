With determinant-one normalization, the necessary and sufficient condition for sphere rotations as special-unitary Möbius transformations is
c=−b,d=a,∣a∣2+∣b∣2=1.​
(1)
Equivalently, the matrix M=(ac​bd​) belongs to the special unitary group SU(2). The two determinant-one representatives M and −M induce the same Möbius transformation, and both satisfy this condition.
Use north-pole stereographic projection in the direction from the plane to the sphere:
φ(z)=1+∣z∣2(2Rez,2Imz,∣z∣2−1)​,φ(∞)=(0,0,1).
(2)
Its chord-distance identity is ∣φ(z)−φ(w)∣=2χ(z,w), where χ is the chordal metric. Direct subtraction, using ad−bc=1, gives
χ(g(z),g(w))=H(z)H(w)​∣z−w∣​,H(z)=∣az+b∣2+∣cz+d∣2.
(3)
This is first calculated away from poles and then extended continuously to the Riemann sphere. A sphere rotation preserves chord distances, so for distinct finite nonpole points
1+∣z∣2H(z)​1+∣w∣2H(w)​=1.
(4)
Using three distinct points shows all these positive ratios equal one. Hence H(z)=1+∣z∣2 everywhere. Comparing its coefficients gives
∣a∣2+∣c∣2=1,∣b∣2+∣d∣2=1,ab+cd=0.
(5)
Thus M is a unitary matrix. Comparing M−1=M∗ with M−1=(d−c​−ba​) proves the boxed relations.
Conversely, these relations give H(z)=1+∣z∣2, so the induced sphere map T=φgφ−1 preserves all Euclidean chord distances. Such a bijection of the unit sphere is the restriction of an orthogonal matrix: let vj​=T(ej​); preservation of distances makes these an orthonormal basis, and T(p)⋅vj​=p⋅ej​ forces T(p)=∑j​pj​vj​. Finally the Möbius transformation is holomorphic with positive real Jacobian at every ordinary coordinate point. Conjugating by stereographic projection preserves orientation, so this orthogonal matrix has determinant +1. It is therefore a genuine rotation in three dimensions, proving sufficiency rather than just spherical isometry.
For the circle assertion, a nondegenerate generalized circle has an equation
A∣z∣2+Bz+Bz+C=0,A,C∈R,∣B∣2−AC>0.
(6)
For A=0 this is an ordinary circle, and for A=0 it is a line with infinity included. On the sphere write coordinates (p1​,p2​,p3​), with z=(p1​+ip2​)/(1−p3​) and ∣z∣2=(1+p3​)/(1−p3​). Multiplication by 1−p3​ gives the plane
2ReBp1​−2ImBp2​+(A−C)p3​+(A+C)=0.
(7)
If n=(2ReB,−2ImB,A−C) and h=−(A+C), then ∣n∣2−h2=4(∣B∣2−AC)>0. Thus the plane intersects the unit sphere in a proper circle. It contains the north pole exactly when A=0, accounting for the point at infinity on a line.
Conversely, a circle on the sphere is the intersection with a plane n⋅p=h, ∣h∣<∣n∣. Set A=(n3​−h)/2, C=(−n3​−h)/2 and B=(n1​−in2​)/2. The preceding equation is recovered and has ∣B∣2−AC=(∣n∣2−h2)/4>0. Its inverse stereographic image is an ordinary circle or a line with infinity attached. This proves both directions of the circle-plane relation under stereographic projection.
