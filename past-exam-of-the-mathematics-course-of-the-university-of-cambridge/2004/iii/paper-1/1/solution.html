The Hilbert basis theorem says that if B is a commutative Noetherian ring, then B[t] is Noetherian; iteration treats any finite number of variables. Here is a complete leading-coefficient proof. For an ideal I⊆B[t], let Ld​ be the set of degree-d coefficients of its polynomials of degree at most d, including zero coefficients. These are ideals of B, and multiplication by t gives Ld​⊆Ld+1​. The ascending chain condition makes the chain stationary from some N onward. Choose finite generators for L0​,…,LN​ and lift them to polynomials fdj​∈I of degree at most d. If f∈I has degree m, set d=min(m,N) and express its leading coefficient as ∑j​adj​bj​, where adj​ is the chosen degree-d coefficient. Then f−∑j​bj​tm−dfdj​ has smaller degree. Induction proves that the finitely many lifts generate I. This proves the theorem, including coefficient rings with zero divisors.
Put A=CG and L=C[X,X−1]. The infinite dihedral group has normal forms Xm and XmY. The relations move every Y to the right and reduce its exponent modulo two; the action X:n↦n+1, Y:n↦−n on Z shows that these forms are distinct. Thus
A=L⊕LY,Yf(X)=f(X−1)Y.
(1)
The Hilbert basis theorem makes C[X] Noetherian, and its localization L is Noetherian: an ideal of a localization is generated by the images of the finite generators of its contraction. Since A is free of rank two over L on the left, it is a Noetherian module over L. Every left ideal of A is an L-submodule, so all its ascending chains stabilize. Therefore CG is left Noetherian.
For the center of the infinite dihedral group algebra, write an element as f(X)+g(X)Y. Commuting it with X gives g(X)(X−1−X)=0 in the Laurent integral domain, forcing g=0. Commuting f(X) with Y then gives f(X)=f(X−1). A symmetric Laurent polynomial is a linear combination of 1 and Xj+X−j, j≥1. With z=X+X−1, the recursion
S0​=2,S1​=z,Sj+1​=zSj​−Sj−1​
(2)
expresses Sj​=Xj+X−j as a polynomial in z. Conversely every polynomial in z is central. The highest Laurent exponent of a nonzero polynomial in z shows that z is transcendental. Hence
R=Z(A)=C[z],z=X+X−1.​
(3)
The identities X−1=z−X and X2−zX+1=0 give L=R+RX. Independence follows by applying X↦X−1 to a relation f(z)+Xg(z)=0 and subtracting: (X−X−1)g(z)=0, so g=f=0. Consequently
A=R⊕RX⊕RY⊕RXY​
(4)
is a free R-module of rank four.
Let M be a nonzero simple module on the left. For any m=0, M=Am, so it is finite over R. Every central r∈/P=AnnR​(M) acts bijectively: its kernel and image are A-submodules, and simplicity forces kernel zero and image M. To prove that P is maximal, not merely prime, put B=R/P; then M is a faithful finite B-module. For 0=b∈B, bM=M. If m1​,…,md​ generate M, write mi​=b∑j​cij​mj​. The adjugate matrix identity for I−bC shows that det(I−bC) annihilates every generator and hence M. Faithfulness makes this determinant zero in B. Its constant term is one, so it has form 1−bq, proving bq=1. Thus B is a field, and P is a maximal ideal of R. This proves the general principle for simple modules over an algebra finite over its center in this instance.
Every maximal ideal of C[z] is (z−λ) for some λ∈C, by factorization of complex polynomials. The module M=Am is therefore a quotient of the four-dimensional algebra A/(z−λ)A. In particular dimC​M≤4<∞, as required.
The actual simple dimensions are at most two. If λ=±2, choose u=±1 with u+u−1=λ. The matrices X=diag(u,u−1) and Y=(01​10​) give a representation of A/(z−λ)A onto M2​(C): polynomial functions of X give the two diagonal matrix units, and multiplication by Y gives the off-diagonal ones. Both algebras have dimension four, so this is an isomorphism. If λ=±2, the element X∓1 has square zero and is conjugated to its negative by Y; its generated two-sided ideal is square zero. It annihilates every simple module, since its action on a simple module is either zero or the whole module, and the latter contradicts its square being zero. The remaining quotient is C[C2​], whose two simple modules have Y=±1. Thus the generic simple modules have dimension two, and the four exceptional one-dimensional characters have X=±1, Y=±1.
