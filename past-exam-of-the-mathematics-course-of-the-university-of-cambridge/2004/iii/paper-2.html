The Frattini subgroup is the intersection of the maximal subgroups of G, with Φ(1)=1. A useful equivalent property is
HΦ(G)=G⟹H=G(H≤G).
(1)
Indeed, a proper subgroup H lies in a maximal subgroup, which also contains Φ(G). The Frattini subgroup is a characteristic subgroup, since automorphisms permute the maximal subgroups.
The Fitting subgroup is the largest nilpotent normal subgroup. To prove its existence, first form the p-core Op​(G): the product of all normal p-subgroups. For two such subgroups, their product is normal and has order ∣AB∣=∣A∣∣B∣/∣A∩B∣, a power of p. There are only finitely many subgroups, so Op​(G) exists. For distinct primes p,r,
[Op​(G),Or​(G)]≤Op​(G)∩Or​(G)=1.
(2)
Thus the product of the p-cores is a nilpotent normal subgroup, a direct product of groups of its Sylow subgroups. Conversely, a finite nilpotent normal subgroup K has unique Sylow subgroups, each a characteristic subgroup of K. Each is normal in G and lies in the corresponding p-core. Consequently the required largest subgroup exists, and
F(G)=p∣∣G∣∏​Op​(G).​
(3)

Put Φ=Φ(G) and choose a Sylow subgroup P of Φ. Since Φ is normal, the Frattini argument gives G=ΦNG​(P): for g∈G, the Sylow subgroups Pg and P of Φ are conjugate by an element of Φ, leaving an element of NG​(P). The non-generator property of the Frattini subgroup proved above forces NG​(P)=G. Thus every Sylow subgroup of Φ is normal. Distinct such Sylow subgroups commute because their group commutators lie in their trivial intersection. Hence Φ is a nilpotent normal subgroup, and

Write F=F(G), C=CG​(F) and D=C∩F=Z(F). Both C and D are normal in G. Suppose C>D. Choose a minimal normal subgroup K/D of G/D contained in C/D. In a finite soluble group, such a minimal normal subgroup is elementary abelian: the last nontrivial term of its derived series is an abelian characteristic subgroup, so minimality makes the whole subgroup abelian; its primary components and subgroup of elements killed by p then force one prime and exponent p.
It follows that K′≤D. Moreover K≤C centralizes D≤F, so K′≤Z(K). Therefore K is a nilpotent group of class at most two. Since K is normal in G, the definition of the Fitting subgroup gives K≤F. But then K≤C∩F=D, a contradiction. This proves the Fitting subgroup is self-centralizing in soluble groups:
CG​(F(G))≤F(G),CG​(F(G))=Z(F(G)).​

It suffices to centralize each minimal normal subgroup M. The subgroup M∩F(G) is normal in G, so it is either 1 or M. In the first case
[M,F(G)]≤M∩F(G)=1.
(6)
In the second case M is a nilpotent group. Its nontrivial group center is a characteristic subgroup of M, hence normal in G, so minimality makes M abelian. The primary components, which are characteristic subgroups, and the subgroup killed by a prime show that M is elementary abelian of exponent p. Thus M≤Op​(G).
The finite p-group Op​(G) acts by conjugation on M. Its orbits have prime-power sizes; hence the number of fixed elements is congruent to ∣M∣=0 modulo p. The identity is fixed, so there is a nonidentity fixed element. Therefore M∩Z(Op​(G))=1. This intersection is normal in G, and minimality makes it M. The other p-cores commute with Op​(G), and hence with M. Thus every factor of F(G) centralizes M. Since the group socle is generated by these minimal normal subgroups,
F(G)≤CG​(Soc(G)).​

A soluble group is a group whose derived series G(0)=G, G(i+1)=[G(i),G(i)] terminates at 1. Equivalently, it has a finite subnormal series with abelian factors. For the reverse implication, if G/N has derived length r, then G(r)≤N; if N has derived length s, then G(r+s)=1. Iterating this observation through the series proves the equivalence. For a finite group, refining the abelian factors to composition factors says that solubility is equivalent to all composition factors being cyclic of prime order.
Subgroups and quotient groups of soluble groups are soluble, since their derived series lie in, or are images of, the original series. The same calculation proves closure under group extensions. Finite p-groups are nilpotent groups, hence soluble: their nontrivial centers allow induction to construct a central series. Every finite nilpotent group is a direct product of groups of its Sylow subgroups. The converse fails: S3​ has the abelian-factor series 1<C3​<S3​, but its order-two Sylow subgroups are not normal. The symmetric group S4​ is also soluble, with series 1<V4​<A4​<S4​ and abelian factors. A finite soluble group has elementary abelian minimal normal subgroups, by the argument in 1(c). In contrast, a nonabelian simple group is a perfect group and is not soluble.
The Fitting subgroup collects the normal nilpotent structure. For a nontrivial finite soluble group it is nontrivial, since it contains an elementary abelian minimal normal subgroup. Part 1(c) proves CG​(F)≤F, so conjugation embeds G/CG​(F) in Aut(F). This shows why controlling a soluble group through normal abelian or nilpotent layers is effective, even when the whole group is not a nilpotent group.
For a set of primes π, a Hall subgroup of type π has order divisible only by primes in π and index divisible only by primes outside π. The Hall theorem for soluble groups says that a finite soluble group has such subgroups, any two are conjugate, and every subgroup of π-order is contained in one. These assertions generalize the Sylow theorems, which treat a single prime. Here is a proof of all three assertions.
First we need coprime splitting over an elementary abelian normal subgroup. Suppose V⊴E is an elementary abelian p-group and Q=E/V has order h prime to p. A section s:Q→E defines an action on V and an additive factor set f(x,y) by s(x)s(y)=f(x,y)s(xy). Associativity says
f(x,y)+f(xy,z)=xf(y,z)+f(x,yz).
(10)
Summing over z∈Q and dividing by h in V gives f(x,y)=b(x)+xb(y)−b(xy), where b(x)=h−1∑z​f(x,z). Replacing s(x) by −b(x)s(x) removes the factor set, giving a complement. Two complements differ by a map d satisfying d(xy)=d(x)+xd(y). Averaging over y gives d(x)=b−xb, so the complements are conjugate by an element of V. This proves both splitting and conjugacy, without assuming that an arbitrary extension splits.
Induct on ∣G∣, taking an elementary abelian minimal normal subgroup V of exponent p. Choose a quotient Hall subgroup Hˉ≤G/V and let K be its preimage. If p∈π, then K itself is a Hall subgroup. If p∈/π, the preceding splitting argument gives a complement H to V in K, and that complement is a Hall subgroup of G.
For conjugacy, the images of two Hall subgroups are quotient Hall subgroups, so induction allows them to have the same image and lie in the same K. If p∈π, every Hall subgroup contains V: its product with V is a π-subgroup and cannot be larger by the subgroup-order formula. Both are then K. If p∈/π, both are complements to V in K, so the averaging argument conjugates them.
Finally let U≤G be any π-subgroup. By induction its image lies in a quotient Hall subgroup, so U≤K. If p∈π, K is already a Hall subgroup containing U. Otherwise write K=V⋊H. In VU, both U and the inverse image in H of the image of U are complements to V. Complement conjugacy therefore places U in a conjugate of H. This proves embedding and completes the induction.
Thus existence, conjugacy and containment all hold for every prime set in a finite soluble group. The solubility hypothesis matters: A5​ has no Hall subgroup for π={2,5}. Such a subgroup would have index three; its coset action would give a nontrivial group homomorphism A5​→S3​, impossible because A5​ is a simple group and has order 60. This example also distinguishes a Hall subgroup from a merely arbitrary subgroup whose order uses the chosen primes.

Since the two-transitive group action is primitive, the orbits of any nontrivial normal subgroup form only the single block Ω. Thus N is transitive. If N is regular, identify Ω with N by x↦xα. Then G=NGα​, N∩Gα​=1, and conjugation by Gα​ gives automorphisms of N. The two-transitive group action makes these automorphisms transitive on N∖{1}. All nonidentity elements therefore have the same order, which must be a prime p: a suitable power of an element of composite order would have a smaller nontrivial order. Cauchy's theorem for finite groups now shows ∣N∣=pd.
The center of a group of prime-power order is nontrivial and is a characteristic subgroup. The transitive conjugation action on nonidentity elements therefore forces Z(N)=N. Hence N is an elementary abelian group, which we identify with the additive vector space Fpd​. Conjugation by Gα​ is linear on this vector space. It is faithful: an element fixing α and centralizing N fixes every xα, hence is the identity permutation. Consequently
n=pd,G=N⋊Gα​≤AGLd​(p).​
(11)
Here the general affine group acts by translations followed by invertible linear maps.

The orbits of Nα​⊴Gα​ on Ω∖{α} have a common size r, because Gα​ is transitive there. Suppose N has a block system of block size b, 1<b<n. Since r∣n−1 and b∣n, gcd(r,b)=1. Let B be the block of α and C=B another block. The union Nα​C is a union of blocks and of r-element point-orbits, so its size is divisible by br; it is at most br. Equality means each of the b point-orbits meets C once. Thus Nαβ​ fixes C pointwise for β∈C. Interchanging α,β also makes it fix B. All two-point stabilizers have order ∣Nα​∣/r. Therefore Nαβ​=Nαγ​ for every γ∈B∖{α}. Varying β outside B shows this same subgroup fixes every point, so it is trivial. We have proved uniform subdegrees force an imprimitive action to be Frobenius: no nonidentity element of N fixes two points.
Write ∣Nα​∣=h. The distinct point stabilizers meet only at 1, and ∣N∣=nh. Thus the set D of fixed-point-free elements of N has size
∣D∣=nh−(1+n(h−1))=n−1.
(12)
Count triples (α,β,x) with x∈D and xα=β: there are n(n−1), exactly the number of ordered distinct pairs. Conjugation by the two-transitive group makes every pair occur, hence each pair has a unique such x. It follows that G conjugates all elements of D transitively.
Also h∣n−1, since Nα​ acts freely on the remaining points. For each prime p∣n, Cauchy's theorem for finite groups supplies an order-p element of N; it cannot fix a point, since p∤h. Conjugacy of D forces all these primes to coincide, so n=pd and every element of D has order p. A Sylow subgroup P of N has order n, because p∤h. Every nonidentity element of P is fixed-point-free, so P={1}∪D. This uniquely described subgroup is normal in G. Minimal normality of N gives P=N, and ∣N∣=n. Therefore N is regular. The proof establishes closure through a Sylow subgroup; it does not assume that a set of derangements is automatically a subgroup.

A finite minimal normal subgroup is a characteristically simple group, and has the direct-product structure of a finite minimal normal subgroup
N=T1​×⋯×Tk​
(13)
with isomorphic simple groups Ti​. For completeness, choose a minimal nontrivial normal subgroup T of N. Its distinct G-conjugates are minimal normal subgroups of N, so they centralize one another. Their product is normal in G, hence equals N. If T is abelian the product is abelian and minimal primary characteristic subgroups make N elementary abelian. Otherwise each T has trivial center: its center is a characteristic subgroup of T, hence normal in N, and minimality would make T abelian. Each new conjugate then meets the product of previous conjugates trivially, because it centralizes that product and is centerless. The product is direct. Every normal subgroup of a direct factor is normal in N, so minimality of that factor makes it simple.
Assume now that N acts primitively and nonregularly. Any nontrivial normal subgroup of this faithful primitive action is transitive. Thus each Ti​ is transitive. If k≥2, the transitive commuting groups T1​ and T2​ are regular: an element of one fixing α commutes with the other and hence fixes its entire orbit. But the centralizer of a regular group has order n, as its elements are determined by the image of α. Therefore all remaining factors lie in the order-n centralizer of T1​, and the transitive group T2​ already fills it. There can be only two factors, with ∣N∣=n2. Now ∣Nα​∣=n, whereas its common nontrivial subdegree r divides both n and n−1. Hence r=1, making Nα​ trivial, a contradiction. Thus k=1, and N is simple. An abelian transitive group is regular, so N is nonabelian.
Finally CG​(N) is normal in the primitive group G. If nontrivial it would be transitive, and commuting with transitive N would force N regular. Thus CG​(N)=1, and conjugation embeds G in Aut(N). The centerless simple group N is identified with its inner automorphism group. Therefore
N is nonabelian simple,N≤G≤Aut(N).​
(14)

Let S=SLn​(F) and let Z be its scalar center. Its action on the lines of Fn has kernel exactly Z: a linear map fixing every line is diagonal in a basis, and fixing the lines spanned by ei​+ej​ makes all its diagonal entries equal. The action is two-transitive. Indeed an ordered pair of distinct lines can be represented by two independent vectors and extended to a basis. A linear map between two such bases can be adjusted to determinant one by scaling one target basis vector; this preserves both target lines. Therefore S/Z has a faithful primitive group action.
Fix L=Fv. The maps
AL​={I+vf:f∈(Fn)∗, f(v)=0}

form an abelian subgroup: all products of the rank-one terms vanish. It is normal in the line stabilizer, since conjugation replaces v by a scalar multiple and transports f. Its conjugates contain every transvection, and hence generate S by 4(b). The same statements hold for its image in S/Z.

Tv,c​(x)=x+cB(x,v)v.
(24)

If B(u,v)=0 and u=v, choose w with both B(u,w) and B(w,v) nonzero: the union of two proper hyperplanes does not exhaust a vector space, even over F2​. Two transvections then suffice. Thus one can make a form-preserving linear map fix the first basis vector e. Next align its partner f, while keeping e fixed. Two partners have difference in e⊥, so the preceding single-transvection construction uses a direction in e⊥ whenever their mutual pairing is nonzero. If it is zero, the intermediate partner f+ae, a=0, has nonzero pairing with both. All these transvections fix e. Once both e,f are fixed, recurse on their nondegenerate symplectic orthogonal complement. This proves generation over every field.
Use the action on the points of projective space, whose kernel is the scalar center Z={λI:λ2=1}. For m=1 this is the SL2​ case. For m≥2 the action is primitive, although it is generally not two-transitive. The stabilizer of a line L has two orbits on other lines: those orthogonal to L and those not orthogonal to L. This follows by extending a hyperbolic pair, or an independent isotropic pair, to a symplectic basis. A block containing L and a second line M contains the entire corresponding orbit of the stabilizer of L. Let V have dimension 2m over the finite field Fq​, with a nondegenerate alternating bilinear form B. The symplectic group over a finite field is
Sp(V,B)={g∈GL(V):B(gx,gy)=B(x,y) for all x,y}.

Represent a subset by the coordinate vector of its indicator function in F2n​. Symmetric difference then becomes coordinatewise addition, and the proposed pairing is the dot product
B(x,y)=∑i=1n​xi​yi​.
(41)
It is a symmetric bilinear form, and it is nondegenerate because its Gram matrix in the singleton basis is the identity. Coordinate permutations preserve the sum, so the natural Sn​ action preserves the bilinear form.

(42)
is the even-weight subspace. For even n, B(j,j)=n=0 in F2​, so j∈W. Moreover B(x,x)=∑i​xi2​=∑i​xi​=0 for x∈W, making the restricted bilinear form alternating. Nondegeneracy on the ambient space implies W⊥=⟨j⟩, so the radical of B∣W​ is exactly
