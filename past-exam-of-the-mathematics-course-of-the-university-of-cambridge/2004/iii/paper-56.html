Use units c=1 and take the initially expanding branch with ordinary positive matter or radiation density. In the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric, k=+1,0,−1 respectively describe positive, zero and negative spatial curvature. Separately conserved pressureless matter has ρM​∝a−3 and radiation in cosmology has ρR​∝a−4, so either dominates the curvature and vacuum terms sufficiently far back on a hot Big Bang branch.
With negligible cosmological constant, a spatially flat radiation universe has a∝t1/2 and a flat matter universe has a∝t2/3; both expand forever while decelerating. An open universe also expands forever and eventually becomes approximately curvature dominated, with a∝t. A closed universe containing only ordinary matter/radiation reaches a maximum size and recollapses. For example, dust gives a˙2=C/a−k with C>0, so only k=+1 admits the positive turning point a=C.
Curvature alone does not determine the fate when the cosmological constant is retained. Positive Λ can cause late accelerated expansion and prevent even a closed universe from recollapsing; whether a closed expanding branch turns around depends on the relative matter and vacuum terms. Negative Λ can cause recollapse even for flat or open spatial sections. These statements follow by inspecting a˙2=C/a−k+Λa2/3 for dust; the curvature sign is a geometrical classification, not by itself a universal destiny criterion.
Define the critical density ρc​=3H2/(8πG) and cosmological density parameters ΩM​=ρM​/ρc​, ΩR​=ρR​/ρc​, ΩΛ​=Λ/(3H2) and Ωk​=−k/(a2H2). The Friedmann equation gives ΩM​+ΩR​+ΩΛ​+Ωk​=1. Dividing the Friedmann acceleration equation by −H2 gives
q=3H24πG​(ρ+3P)−ΩΛ​=21​ΩM​+ΩR​−ΩΛ​.
(1)
Consequently
q=21​ΩM​−ΩΛ​for dust plus vacuum,q=ΩR​−ΩΛ​for radiation plus vacuum.​
(2)
The deceleration parameter measures the sign and size of the acceleration relative to aH2: q>0 means deceleration, q<0 accelerated expansion and q=0 instantaneously zero acceleration. In particular a falling Hubble parameter alone does not imply deceleration, since q=−1−H˙/H2.
For conformal time dη=dt/a, the conformal Hubble parameter is H=aH. Using matter conservation and differentiating ΩM​∝ρM​/H2,
ΩM​Ω˙M​​=−3H−2HH˙​=H(2q−1),ΩM′​=HΩM​(ΩM​−1−2ΩΛ​).​
(3)
This is the matter density flow with a cosmological constant. Neglecting ΩΛ​ during matter domination reduces it to the requested relation
ΩM′​=HΩM​(ΩM​−1).​
(4)
That last equation is not exact with nonzero Λ. For example, a flat dust-plus-vacuum state with (ΩM​,ΩΛ​)=(0.9,0.1) gives ΩM′​/H=−0.27, whereas the matter-only expression gives −0.09.
For the flatness problem, let Ωtot​=ΩM​+ΩR​+ΩΛ​. Curvature satisfies Ωtot​−1=k/(aH)2, so
dlogadlog∣Ωtot​−1∣​=2q.
(5)
In decelerating near-flat radiation and matter eras, deviations therefore grow as a2 and a respectively. Maintaining small curvature at a late epoch requires very small early deviations unless a prior mechanism suppresses them. Cosmic inflation, with q≃−1, drives the deviation down approximately as a−2. If vacuum energy is present, flatness means Ωtot​=1, not ΩM​=1.

Work in natural units kB​=ℏ=c=1, initially neglecting helium and excited hydrogen states. The reaction p+e−↔H+γ is in chemical equilibrium when μp​+μe​=μH​, since equilibrium photons have zero chemical potential. Substitution of the nonrelativistic Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution for the three densities gives
nH​np​ne​​=gH​gp​ge​​(2πmH​mp​me​T​)3/2exp[−Tmp​+me​−mH​​].
(6)
For ground-state hydrogen use ge​=gp​=2, gH​=4, and mH​≃mp​ in the translational prefactor. Writing its Hydrogen binding energy as I, this becomes (me​T/(2π))3/2e−I/T. Charge neutrality gives np​=ne​=Xe​nB​, while baryon conservation gives nH​=(1−Xe​)nB​. Hence the hydrogen-only Saha equation is
Xe2​1−Xe​​=nB​(me​T2π​)3/2eI/T.​
(7)
The photon Bose-Einstein distribution supplies nγ​=2ζ(3)T3/π2, where ζ(3) is the usual Riemann zeta function value in the thermal number integral. With baryon-to-photon ratio ηγ​=nB​/nγ​, substitution yields
Xe2​1−Xe​​=π22ζ(3)​ηγ​(me​2πT​)3/2eI/T.​
(8)
This reproduces the requested numerical coefficient, with the printed ξ(3) interpreted as the thermal ζ(3) value. There is a normalization ambiguity in the PDF: it writes η=nB​/s without defining s. To reproduce this coefficient, s must mean photon number density. If s has its usual entropy density meaning, the Saha baryon-abundance normalization instead gives
s=452π2​g∗s​T3,ηs​=snB​​,Xe2​1−Xe​​=452π2​g∗s​ηs​(me​2πT​)3/2eI/T.​
(9)
The two forms agree through ηγ​=[π4g∗s​/(45ζ(3))]ηs​, with the entropy sector specified. A baryon-to-entropy ratio cannot simply be inserted into the photon-normalized expression.
As the universe cools, the Boltzmann factor favors neutral atoms and cosmological recombination sharply lowers Xe​. For an order-one ionized fraction the photon-normalized equation gives the balance
TI​≃log[2ζ(3)ηγ​π2​(2πTme​​)3/2]
(10)
up to an order-one ionization-fraction factor. The small baryon abundance means that there are very many photons per baryon: even a small high-energy photon tail can ionize most atoms. Thus the recombination temperature is far below the microscopic binding scale I≃13.6eV, typically a few tenths of an eV for the standard abundance, rather than of order I.
Photon decoupling is a related but distinct kinetic event. The Thomson scattering rate ne​σT​ falls relative to H as free electrons disappear, and photons begin free streaming. The precise last-scattering epoch depends on integrated optical depth, not only the local equality of rates. At still lower density the effective recombination rate per electron also drops below the expansion rate. Residual electron freeze-out leaves a small nonzero ionized fraction, larger than the vanishingly small equilibrium Saha prediction; radiative atomic bottlenecks mean the entire history cannot be obtained from equilibrium alone.
The temperature comparison in the last request needs an interpretation. If “reionisation temperature” means the microscopic thermal ionization scale, the large photon-to-baryon ratio explains why cosmological recombination requires T≪I. If it means later cosmic reionization, there is no universal thermal threshold: stars or other sources supply ionizing photons and can reheat gas. The cosmological photon background is actually colder at that later epoch, because Tγ​∝a−1. Its temperature therefore cannot literally be higher than the background temperature at recombination merely because hydrogen is reionized.

For a homogeneous canonical inflaton, ρϕ​=ϕ˙​2/2+V and Pϕ​=ϕ˙​2/2−V. Vacuum domination by a scalar field requires positive potential energy to dominate both kinetic energy and any competing material components. Then Pϕ​≃−ρϕ​ and the expansion is nearly vacuum-like. Exact scalar-driven acceleration requires ρϕ​+3Pϕ​<0, equivalently ϕ˙​2<V; potential domination is a stronger condition.

together with approach to the slow-roll attractor. The reduced equations are H2≃8πV/(3mpl2​) and 3Hϕ˙​≃−V′.

Assume standard radiation-dominated expansion, negligible chemical potential, one neutrino helicity and one antineutrino helicity, and a stable species X with no later entropy injection. Use natural units and unreduced Planck mass mpl​≃1.22×1019GeV. The relativistic energy density is ρR​=π2g∗​T4/30. The Friedmann equation then gives
H=454π3g∗​​​mpl​T2​≃1.66g∗​​mpl​T2​.

For thermal decoupling in cosmology, equate the interaction rate GA2​TD5​ to H(TD​):
TD​≃(GA2​mpl​1.66g∗​​​)1/3≃1.1×102GeV.​
(19)
This order-of-magnitude estimate assumes the supplied rate law remains applicable there.
A decoupled relativistic species cools as TX​∝a−1, while annihilations heat the coupled bath. Treat the supplied high-temperature count 100 as the remaining bath's entropy count, approximately equal to its energy count. Cosmological entropy conservation down to ordinary neutrino decoupling gives
Tν​TX​​=(10010.75​)1/3.
(20)

Tγ​TX​​=(10010.75​114​)1/3=(110043​)1/3≃0.339.​
(21)
The ordinary Cosmic neutrino background has Tν​/Tγ​=(4/11)1/3. If the rounded count 100 instead includes the new two helicities, the remaining bath count is 98.25; this changes the answer only slightly, to about 0.341. Keeping the extra species' entropy separate is essential in either convention.
For the two relativistic fermion states, g∗s,X​=(7/8)2=7/4. Photon and three standard neutrino species have present entropy degrees of freedom, referred to Tγ​,
g∗s,std​=2+87​6(114​)=1143​.
(22)
Writing θ=TX​/Tγ​, the fractional entropy of a relativistically decoupled species is
stotal​sX​​=43/11+(7/4)θ3(7/4)θ3​=100+7/47/4​≃0.0172.​

Thus approximately 1.7% of the thermal particle entropy is in the new neutrinos and antineutrinos. If 100 counts all states before the new decoupling, the fraction is 1.75%. These are the same rounded estimate. The question concerns the radiation/relic thermal entropy budget; it does not supply a model for entropy in later black holes or other gravitational structures.
If X has a small mass but decoupled relativistically, its comoving particle number is still conserved. The relativistic-neutrino number-density ratio gives
nγ​nX​​=43​θ3≃0.0293,nν,one standard species​nX​​=10010.75​=0.1075.
(24)
Once nonrelativistic, ρX​≃mX​nX​. The density bound on a relativistically decoupled massive relic is therefore
mX​≤nX​ΩX,max​ρc​​.​
(25)
Using the standard present photon abundance and critical-density normalization, this is approximately ΩX​h2≃mX​/(9×102eV), where h=H0​/(100kms−1Mpc−1) is the present Hubble normalization, not a magnetic field. A stable relic must not exceed the permitted matter density; for example, if ΩM​h2=0.15, even assigning all matter to it gives only an order-102eV mass ceiling.
A stronger restriction can follow from neutrino free streaming and the observed formation of large-scale structure of the universe: a light thermal population acts as hot rather than cold dark matter, erasing structure below its streaming scale. If at most a fraction fmax​ of the matter may be in this population, replace the overclosure ceiling by mX​≲9×102fmax​ΩM​h2eV. The allowed fraction is observational input and is not supplied, so the paper does not determine a unique tighter mass limit. A species still relativistic today is radiation rather than matter; its contribution to the effective number of neutrino species is ΔNeff​=(TX​/Tν​)4≃0.051, and must instead satisfy the radiation-density constraints. These conclusions assume stable thermally populated states; decay or nonstandard entropy histories change the bounds.
