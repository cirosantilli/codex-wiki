A kernel for density estimation is an integrable function K of unit integral, ordinarily nonnegative and centered. Square integrability is needed for integrated variance; a symmetric second-order kernel for density estimation also has ∫uK(u)du=0 and finite nonzero second moment μ2​(K). Its scaled form is Kh​(u)=h−1K(u/h), and the kernel density estimator is
f​h​(x)=n1​∑i=1n​Kh​(x−Xi​).
(1)
Higher-order signed kernels are possible, but need not give a nonnegative probability density function estimate.
The mean integrated squared error is E∫(f​h​(x)−f(x))2dx. Put gh​=Kh​∗f. Independence gives the pointwise mean gh​ and variance n−1[(Kh2​∗f)−gh2​]. Integrating, use ∫f=1 and Fubini to obtain ∫(Kh2​∗f)=R(Kh​). The bias-variance decomposition proves the exact formula
MISE(f​h​)=R(Kh​∗f−f)+n1​{R(Kh​)−R(Kh​∗f)}.​
(2)
Equivalently this is R(f)−2∫f(Kh​∗f)+(1−n−1)R(Kh​∗f)+R(K)/(nh). These identities assume the displayed integrals are finite; they are exact, rather than a small-smoothing bandwidth expansion.
For a symmetric second-order kernel for density estimation, Taylor expansion gives leading bias h2μ2​(K)f′′/2, and the leading integrated variance is R(K)/(nh). Thus the asymptotic mean integrated squared error balances inverse smoothing bandwidth against fourth-power bias, with optimal smoothing bandwidth [R(K)/(nμ2​(K)2R(f′′))]1/5. Its appearance of the integrated squared density curvature motivates the scale comparison.
For fa​(x)=af(ax), a>0, two differentiations give fa′′​(x)=a3f′′(ax). A change of variables therefore gives R(fa′′​)=a5R(f′′), which tends to zero as a↓0 if the original curvature integral is finite. The distribution with probability density function fa​ is that of X divided by a, so σ(fa​)=σ(f)/a. Consequently
D(fa​)=σ(fa​)5R(fa′′​)=σ(f)5R(f′′)=D(f).​
(3)
This scale-invariant density curvature measures shape without dependence on the units used to measure X.
For the final minimization, write c=35/32 and use the triweight kernel f0​=c(1−x2)3 on (−1,1), zero outside. Integration gives total mass one, mean zero and variance 1/9. Its value and its first two derivatives vanish at both endpoints, so its extension is twice continuously differentiable. On the interior,
f0′′​=c(−6+36x2−30x4),f0′′′​=c(72x−120x3),f0(4)​=c(72−360x2).
(4)
In particular f0′′′​(1)=−48c and f0′′′​(−1)=48c.
Put e=h−f0​. The mass and moment constraints give ∫e=∫x2e=0, since both means vanish and their variances match. If R(h′′)=∞ the claim is immediate; otherwise expand the squared norm and integrate its cross term twice by parts over [−1,1]. The first endpoint term vanishes because f0′′​(±1)=0, but the next one must be retained:
∫e′′f0′′​​=−[ef0′′′​]−11​+c∫−11​(72−360x2)e(x)dx=48c{h(1)+h(−1)}+c∫∣x∣>1​(360x2−72)h(x)dx≥0.​
(5)
For the second equality use the zero mass and second moment of e and e=h≥0 outside the support. All terms in the last expression are nonnegative, including the endpoint values of a probability density function. It follows that
R(h′′)=R(f0′′​)+R(e′′)+2∫e′′f0′′​≥R(f0′′​)=35.​
(6)
This proves that the triweight density minimizes integrated squared curvature under the specified constraints. Equality forces e′′=0; a globally affine integrable difference of probability density functions is zero, so equality occurs only at h=f0​.

The empirical distribution function is Fn​(x)=n−1∑i​1Xi​≤x​. For continuous F, the probability integral transform gives independent uniform variables Ui​=F(Xi​). Strict increase of F is unnecessary. More explicitly, generate the observations by Xi​=F−1(Ui​) with the generalized quantile inverse. Then 1Xi​≤x​=1Ui​≤F(x)​, so Fn​(x)=Gn​(F(x)), where Gn​ is the uniform empirical distribution.
Continuity and the limits of F ensure that its range contains (0,1). The endpoint discrepancies for Gn​ are zero. Hence
Dn​=d0≤u≤1sup​∣Gn​(u)−u∣,​
(7)
which depends only on n. Equivalently it is max1≤i≤n​{i/n−U(i)​,U(i)​−(i−1)/n}, a function of uniform order statistics. This proves the distribution-free property of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic even when F has flat portions.
Choose cn,α​ from this common distribution so that P(Dn​≤cn,α​)=1−α. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov confidence band is
max(0,Fn​(x)−cn,α​)≤F(x)≤min(1,Fn​(x)+cn,α​)for all x.​
(8)
The event that all these inequalities hold is exactly the event used to choose the critical value, giving simultaneous coverage 1−α. The Glivenko-Cantelli theorem states that for an independent identically distributed sample from any distribution function, continuous or not, supx​∣Fn​(x)−F(x)∣→0 almost surely.
For an integrable r-argument U-statistic kernel, replace it by hs​(x1​,…,xr​)=(r!)−1∑π​h(xπ(1)​,…,xπ(r)​). Every permutation has the same expectation by exchangeability, so this symmetrization of a U-statistic kernel preserves the target functional. For a symmetric U-statistic kernel h and n≥r, the U-statistic is
Un​=(rn​)−1∑i1​<⋯<ir​​h(Xi1​​,…,Xir​​).
(9)
Each summand has expectation theta, hence the statistic is unbiased.
For the population variance, use h(x,y)=(x−y)2/2. Independence gives E(X1​−X2​)2=2Var(X1​) when the second moment is finite. The corresponding statistic simplifies using ∑i<j​(Xi​−Xj​)2=n∑i​(Xi​−X)2:
Un​=n(n−1)1​i<j∑​(Xi​−Xj​)2=n−11​i∑​(Xi​−X)2.​
(10)
Thus variance as a U-statistic is precisely the usual unbiased sample variance, for n≥2.

Let the summands have mean mu, variance σ2>0, and cumulants κj​. For the standardized sum Zn​=(Sn​−nμ)/(σn​), expansion of its log-characteristic function gives a normal leading term and successively smaller cumulant terms. Fourier inversion turns these terms into Probabilists' Hermite polynomials. Under sufficient moments and a smooth nonlattice condition, the Edgeworth expansion begins
pZn​​(z)=ϕ(z)[1+6σ3n​κ3​​(z3−3z)+O(n−1)]
(11)
for bounded z. Higher orders include the fourth cumulant and products of lower cumulants. This is a central expansion in powers of n−1/2; it need not remain positive, normalized, or accurate in far tails after truncation.
For a saddlepoint density approximation, require the summand cumulant-generating function K to be finite on an interval about zero. At a sum value s=ny, find the interior saddle t satisfying K′(t)=y. Exponential tilting makes the tilted mean y, so the tilted sum is approximated locally by its normal probability density at its mean. Undoing the tilt gives
p​Sn​​(s)=2πnK′′(t)​exp{nK(t)−ts}​.
(12)
Equivalently this follows by deforming the transform-inversion contour through its stationary point. Suitable analytic and nonlattice regularity gives relative error O(n−1) at fixed interior y. This approximation incorporates a nonquadratic tail exponent and is positive, while an Edgeworth truncation uses a normal probability density times a polynomial. It uses more information from the distribution and stronger exponential-moment assumptions. Its integral may differ from one by a higher-order amount, so positivity does not imply exact normalization.
For the centered Laplace distribution, K(t)=t2+t4/2+⋯, hence κ2​=2 and κ3​=0. Its skewness vanishes, so the order-n−1/2 Edgeworth correction is zero. With z=s/2n​, the requested expansion, omitting terms of order n−1 in the standardized correction, is
pSn​E​(s)=4πn​1​e−s2/(4n).​
(13)
For bounded z the exact probability density function is this expression times 1+O(n−1). The prefactor is n−1/2 because this is the probability density function of the unstandardized sum; that change of units should not be confused with the orders inside the Edgeworth expansion.
For the saddlepoint, K′(t)=2t/(1−t2). Write y=s/n and d=1+y2​. The root in (−1,1) is
t=1+dy​,1−t2=1+d2​,yt=d−1,K′′(t)=d(1+d).
(14)
These formulas include s=0 without a division by y. Substitution yields
pSn​SP​(s)=2πnd(1+d)​exp{n[log21+d​−d+1]}​,d=1+(s/n)2​.​
(15)
This is the leading saddlepoint approximation, without its order-n−1 relative correction. Expanding d near one recovers the same central normal approximation, while retaining d captures the Laplace-sum large-deviation exponent away from the center.

Assume a symmetric second-order probability density function kernel for density estimation and enough smoothness for the displayed derivative functionals. The benchmark smoothing bandwidth is h0​=[R(K)/(nμ2​(K)2R(f′′))]1/5, balancing R(K)/(nh) against h4μ2​(K)2R(f′′)/4. Three widely used selection methods are as follows.
For least-squares density cross-validation, expand integrated squared error as R(f​h​)−2∫f​h​f+R(f). Estimate the middle integral by leaving out the observation at which the estimate is evaluated, and minimize
CV(h)=R(f​h​)−n2​∑i​f​h,−i​(Xi​),f​h,−i​(x)=n−11​∑j=i​Kh​(x−Xj​).
(16)
The left-out observation is independent of its estimator, so the second term has expectation −2∫(Kh​∗f)f. Thus ECV(h)=MISE(h)−R(f) exactly. The subtraction of the self-contribution is essential. Computation uses R(f​h​)=n−2∑i,j​(Kh​∗Kh​)(Xi​−Xj​), where Kh​(u)=Kh​(−u). This method directly targets integrated error without estimating a curvature functional first. Under standard smoothness and a suitable search interval its selected smoothing bandwidth is ratio-consistent with the MISE optimum, and hence with h0​. Relative fluctuations are ordinarily Op​(n−1/10), so the selected values can be quite variable in finite samples.
For biased density cross-validation, estimate curvature from the second-derivative probability density function estimator and subtract its diagonal variance term:
Ah​=R(f​h′′​)−nh5R(K′′)​,BCV(h)=nhR(K)​+4μ2​(K)2h4​Ah​.
(17)
Differentiating the kernel for density estimation estimator and applying the integrated-variance identity gives the exact expectation ER(f​h′′​)=R(K′′)/(nh5)+(1−n−1)R(Kh​∗f′′). Under the derivative regularity assumptions, R(Kh​∗f′′)=R(f′′)+O(h2), so EAh​=R(f′′)+O(h2)+O(n−1). Minimize BCV over a reasonable smoothing bandwidth range. It is a smoothed criterion directed at AMISE; the name reflects its finite-sample bias rather than an exact unbiased-risk identity. Standard conditions again give ratio consistency and relative fluctuation order n−1/10. It often has a smoother objective than ordinary least-squares cross-validation, though numerical behavior and relative variance depend on the probability density function and kernel for density estimation.
For plug-in bandwidth selection, obtain an independent pilot scale g, estimate A=R(f′′), then set
hPI​=[nμ2​(K)2AR(K)​]1/5.​
(18)
The pilot should be positive and stable; consistent positive estimates make this expression ratio-consistent by continuity. A simple normal-reference initialization uses R(f′′)=3/(8π​σ5) under a normal reference model, substituting a sample scale for sigma. For a Gaussian density kernel this gives h≈1.06σn−1/5. A pure normal-reference rule is quick but is asymptotically correctly calibrated only when its assumed curvature matches the actual probability density function; it is useful as the first stage of a data-driven plug-in procedure.
A more adaptive fourth-derivative pilot estimate of density curvature uses R(f′′)=∫ff(4), obtained by two integrations by parts, and estimates it by
Rg(2)​=n1​∑i​f​g(4)​(Xi​)=n21​∑i,j​Kg(4)​(Xi​−Xj​).
(19)
The diagonal expectation contributes K(4)(0)/(ng5); the leading smoothing bias is −g2μ2​(K)R(f′′′)/2. Balancing these terms produces the stated pilot scale g≍R(f′′′)−1/7n−1/7, with another pilot or reference model supplying R(f′′′). Under sufficient derivatives and tail control, its error is bounded by
Op​(g2+ng51​+n−1/2+ng9/21​)=Op​(n−2/7)
(20)
at that pilot scale. Taylor expansion of the fifth-root formula gives hPI​/h0​−1=−(A−A)/(5A)+op​(∣A−A∣), so it inherits that bound. A calibrated cancellation of leading pilot bias can give a faster rate. This illustrates why an appropriately constructed plug-in selector can converge more quickly than cross-validation, while requiring more smoothness and pilot choices. For each ratio-consistent method, its attained AMISE divided by the minimal AMISE tends to one.

A marginal statistical likelihood uses a statistic whose marginal sampling distribution depends on the parameter of interest but does not involve the nuisance parameter. It integrates out other observed coordinates. A profile likelihood instead keeps the full data and, for every fixed interest parameter, fits the nuisance parameter by maximizing the full likelihood. Profiling supplies a likelihood function but usually not a normalized sampling probability density function. Here the marginalization is of data coordinates; Bayesian model evidence averages parameters against a prior and is a separate construction.
Use shape-rate notation for the gamma distribution. The independent sums have X∼Gamma(m,ψλ) and Y∼Gamma(n,λ), so their joint probability density for x,y>0 is
fX,Y​(x,y)=Γ(m)Γ(n)ψmλm+n​xm−1yn−1e−λ(ψx+y).
(21)
For x=tu, y=u the absolute Jacobian determinant is u. Therefore
fT,U​(t,u)=Γ(m)Γ(n)ψmλm+n​tm−1um+n−1e−λ(1+ψt)u,t,u>0.
(22)
Integrate u using the gamma integral ∫0∞​um+n−1e−budu=Γ(m+n)b−(m+n). The lambda factors cancel, leaving
fT​(t)=Γ(m)Γ(n)Γ(m+n)​(1+ψt)m+nψmtm−1​,t>0.​
(23)
Thus T gives a nuisance-free marginal likelihood. Discarding only terms independent of psi, its logarithm is
ℓm​(ψ;t)=mlogψ−(m+n)log(1+ψt).​
(24)
The factor t in the logarithm is required, as in the original PDF.
The full log-likelihood is mlogψ+(m+n)logλ−λ(ψx+y)+c(x,y). Its lambda derivative vanishes uniquely at
λψ​=ψx+ym+n​.​
(25)
The second derivative in lambda is negative. Substituting this maximum gives ℓp​(ψ)=mlogψ−(m+n)log(ψx+y)+c′(x,y). Since ψx+y=y(1+ψt), it agrees with ℓm​(ψ;t) up to a psi-independent constant. This proves the Gamma-ratio marginal and profile likelihood identity. Their common maximizer is ψ​=m/(nt)=my/(nx), if an interest-parameter estimate is also wanted.

In a regular model let ℓN​(θ) be the log-likelihood, UN​(θ)=∇ℓN​(θ) its score, IN​(θ) its total Fisher information, and θ the unrestricted maximum-likelihood estimator. For a point null θ=θ0​ in a p-dimensional parameter space, the standard statistics are
WN​SN​LN​​=(θ−θ0​)TIN​(θ)(θ−θ0​),=UN​(θ0​)TIN​(θ0​)−1UN​(θ0​),=2{ℓN​(θ)−ℓN​(θ0​)}.​
(26)
These define the Wald test, score test and likelihood-ratio test. With an interior true parameter, increasing information, suitable differentiability and asymptotic normality, all have asymptotic chi-squared distribution with p degrees of freedom under the null. Observed information can replace expected information when it is asymptotically equivalent.
For q smooth independent restrictions g(θ)=0, replace the Wald statistic by g(θ)T[GIN​(θ)−1GT]−1g(θ), where G is the derivative of g at the estimate; compare with chi-squared q. The likelihood-ratio statistic uses the difference between unrestricted and null-restricted maxima. The score statistic is computed at the restricted maximum and uses efficient information for the tested coordinates: with nuisance coordinates lambda this is Iψψ​−Iψλ​Iλλ−1​Iλψ​. Inverting the acceptance inequalities for point nulls produces approximate coverage-1−α confidence regions; for example the Wald region consists of theta satisfying (θ−θ)TIN​(θ)(θ−θ)≤χp,1−α2​. Analogous score and likelihood-ratio inequalities give their confidence regions.
For the Poisson family, put Y−1​=1 to include the initial observation, and define
Sn​=∑i=0n​Yi​,Tn​=1+∑i=0n−1​Yi​.
(27)
Multiplying the initial and conditional Poisson probabilities gives, on the supported observation sequences,
ℓn​(θ)=Sn​logθ−θTn​+c(Y0​,…,Yn​).​
(28)
The parameter-free term is ∑i=0n​[Yi​logYi−1​−log(Yi​!)], interpreting 0log0 as zero. A positive count after a zero preceding count is outside the support.
For Sn​>0, strict concavity gives unconstrained maximum θ=Sn​/Tn​, and the upper endpoint restriction gives
θ=min(Tn​Sn​​,1)when Sn​>0.​
(29)
There is an endpoint qualification in the printed question. With probability e−θ, Y0​=0 and all later counts are zero. Then Sn​=0, Tn​=1 and the likelihood is e−θ, which has no maximum on the stated space 0<θ≤1. The formula produces zero only as an extended estimate on the closure [0,1]. This is a likelihood supremum at an excluded boundary, and zero cannot be called an actual maximum in the original space.
The observed negative second derivative is Sn​/θ2. Conditional expectation gives EY0​=θ and EYi​=θEYi−1​=θi+1. Therefore for 0<θ<1,
in​(θ)=θ2ESn​​=θ(1−θ)1−θn+1​≤θ(1−θ)1​.​
(30)
This is the bounded information in a subcritical Poisson branching process. Observing more generations of one family does not provide unbounded information. Indeed P(Yn​>0)≤EYn​=θn+1→0, and zero is absorbing, so extinction occurs at a finite generation almost surely. The total observed progeny S∞​ is finite; eventually Sn​=S∞​, Tn​=1+S∞​, and the extended estimate equals S∞​/(1+S∞​), a discrete nondegenerate random variable rather than a consistent increasingly precise estimate.
Here is a direct proof of the failure of a chi-squared Wald limit which does not rely solely on the information bound. Under any interior null θ0​, the event Y0​=1, Y1​=0 has probability θ0​e−2θ0​>0 and forces every later count to be zero. On it the genuine constrained estimate is 1/2 for every n≥1, so the usual expected-information Wald statistic is
Wn​=in​(θ)(θ−θ0​)2=4(1−2−(n+1))(1/2−θ0​)2.
(31)
Thus it has a persistent atom tending to 4(1/2−θ0​)2. The fact that atoms obstruct a continuous distributional limit rules out a chi-squared limit, including when θ0​ equals one half and that atom is at zero. Any definition adopted for the separate all-zero sample event leaves this positive-count counterexample intact. Hence the Wald statistic does not have the usual asymptotic chi-squared null law for this increasing observation horizon.
