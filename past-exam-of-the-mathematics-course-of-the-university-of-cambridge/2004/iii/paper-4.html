An inverse system of topological spaces over a directed set I consists of spaces Xi​ and continuous maps fij​:Xj​→Xi​ for i≤j, with fii​=1 and fik​=fij​fjk​. Its inverse limit is a space X with compatible continuous projections πi​ such that every compatible family of continuous maps ui​:Y→Xi​ factors uniquely through a continuous map u:Y→X. To construct it, take
X={(xi​)∈∏i∈I​Xi​:fij​(xj​)=xi​ whenever i≤j}
(1)
with the subspace topology inherited from the product topology. The map u(y)=(ui​(y)) has values in X and is continuous by the definition of the product topology; its coordinates force uniqueness. If X′ is another inverse limit, the two universal properties give continuous maps X→X′ and X′→X whose composites have all the same projections as the identity. Uniqueness makes these composites the identity. The inverse limit exists and is unique up to a unique projection-preserving homeomorphism. An arbitrary inverse limit may be empty; nonemptiness requires additional hypotheses.
Use the usual Hausdorff convention for a topological group. An inverse limit of finite discrete groups is a closed subgroup of their product: each compatibility condition is closed because the finite factors are Hausdorff spaces. The Tychonoff theorem gives compactness, and distinct tuples are separated by a clopen coordinate cylinder, so the limit is totally disconnected. Coordinatewise multiplication and inversion are continuous, giving a profinite group.
Conversely, let G be a compact Hausdorff space and a totally disconnected topological group. We use the standard topological fact that a compact Hausdorff space that is totally disconnected has a basis of clopen sets. Given an identity neighbourhood, choose a clopen identity neighbourhood U inside it. Compactness of U and continuity of multiplication give a symmetric identity neighbourhood V with VU⊆U: cover U by finitely many neighbourhoods on which multiplication by a sufficiently small identity neighbourhood stays inside U, then intersect those identity neighbourhoods. Symmetry gives vU=U for v∈V. Consequently H={g:gU=U} is a subgroup containing V, hence an open subgroup, and H⊆U since 1∈U. Its open cosets cover the compact group, so there are finitely many. The normal core N=⋂g∈G​gHg−1 is a finite intersection of open subgroups, hence an open normal subgroup contained in U. Thus the open normal subgroups form an identity-neighbourhood basis and have trivial intersection.
The canonical group homomorphism G→lim​N​G/N is continuous and injective. For a compatible tuple of quotient elements, its inverse-image cosets in G are closed and have the finite intersection property: a finite family is refined by the quotient for the intersection of its normal subgroups. Compactness gives an element in all those cosets, proving surjectivity. A continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism. The compact totally disconnected and inverse-limit definitions of a profinite group agree.
Index the finite nonempty sets P(N) by open normal subgroups, with the transition from M to N when M⊆N given by image under G/M→G/N. Images of Sylow subgroups under surjections of finite groups are Sylow subgroups. These transitions are surjective: if S≤G/N is a Sylow subgroup, choose a Sylow subgroup in its full inverse image in G/M. It maps onto S, and its order has the full p-part of ∣G/M∣, so it is a Sylow subgroup of G/M too. The transitions respect composition, giving an inverse system of finite sets.
Its inverse limit is nonempty. Indeed, finitely many compatibility conditions can be satisfied by choosing one Sylow subgroup in a quotient refining all the involved quotients and taking its images; compactness of the product of the finite sets then gives a compatible family (PN​)N​. Define
P=⋂N​πN−1​(PN​).
(2)
This is a closed subgroup of G. Its image in G/N is exactly PN​: for a prescribed s∈PN​, each finite collection of conditions defining P together with πN​(g)=s is satisfiable in a common refining quotient, because the transitions on the chosen Sylow subgroups are surjective. The finite intersection property again supplies g∈P. Hence P≅lim​N​PN​ is a pro-p group and PN/N=PN​ for every N.
Finally, if a pro-p subgroup Q contains P, its image in every finite quotient is a p-group containing the Sylow subgroup PN​, so that image equals PN​. Thus every q∈Q belongs to every πN−1​(PN​), whence Q⊆P. The constructed P is a maximal pro-p subgroup.

A topological group is a group with a topology for which (x,y)↦xy and x↦x−1 are continuous; we use the Hausdorff convention. Translations and inversion are then homeomorphisms.
For the final generator-extraction assertion, use the profinite Frattini subgroup
Φ(G)=Gp[G,G]​.
(3)
For a finitely generated pro-p group, the Burnside basis theorem says that G/Φ(G) is a finite-dimensional vector space over Fp​ and a subset topologically generates G precisely when its image spans that vector space. In particular its dimension is the minimal number d(G) of topological generators. A given d-element generating set shows d(G)≤d. The image of any other generating set spans the same finite-dimensional vector space, so it contains a basis of d(G) elements. Choose their preimages in that generating set and apply the Burnside basis theorem again. Every generating set contains a generating subset of size d(G)≤d. This argument also applies to an infinite generating set.

The proof of the linearity of a uniform pro-p group has three distinct steps: pass to a finite-dimensional Lie algebra, integrate a faithful representation on a sufficiently small open subgroup, and extend it to the entire group. Working first on a small subgroup is essential because a faithful Lie algebra representation need not send the original integral lattice into the convergence domain of the matrix exponential.
Let G be a uniform pro-p group and let LG​ be its intrinsic powerful Lie lattice. Then
g=Qp​⊗Zp​​LG​
(41)
is a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero. The Ado theorem, the classical Lie-theoretic ingredient in this proof, supplies a faithful finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation
ρ:g↪gl(V),dimQp​​V=m<∞.
(42)
One must use a faithful representation rather than just the Adjoint representation, which annihilates the center of a Lie algebra.
Choose a p-adic lattice Λ⊂V. Since LG​ is finitely generated as a module, the entries of the representing matrices of a module basis have bounded denominators. Hence for sufficiently large k,
ρ(pkLG​)⊆pEndZp​​(Λ).
(43)
For odd p, the matrix exponential and p-adic logarithm converge and are inverse between pEnd(Λ) and 1+pEnd(Λ). Put H=Gpk; under the uniform pro-p group and powerful Lie lattice correspondence it is the BCH group on pkLG​. Define
τ:H⟶GL(Λ),τ(expG​X)=exp(ρ(X)).
(44)
Here expG​ denotes the inverse of the intrinsic logarithm identification, not an assumed matrix representation of G. The Lie algebra homomorphism ρ respects the convergent Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula, so
exp(ρ(X∗Y))=exp(ρ(X))exp(ρ(Y)).
(45)
Thus τ is a continuous group homomorphism. If τ(expG​X)=1, taking the matrix logarithm gives ρ(X)=0, whence X=0. Therefore τ is faithful and all its matrices preserve Λ. This proves linearity of the open normal subgroup H.
To finish, use the extension of a faithful representation from an open normal subgroup. Choose representatives t1​=1,t2​,…,tr​ of the finite left cosets G/H, where r=[G:H]. On the finite free module W=⨁j=1r​Λ, define the action of g∈G by writing uniquely
gtj​=ti​hij​,hij​∈H,
(46)
and mapping the j-th block to the i-th block by τ(hij​). If g′tj​=tℓ​h′ and gtℓ​=ti​h, then gg′tj​=ti​hh′; this proves the representation identity because τ(hh′)=τ(h)τ(h′). Each block matrix is invertible over Zp​, and the map is continuous because the finite coset permutation is locally constant and the remaining matrix entries are obtained continuously from τ.
If g∈/H, its permutation of G/H moves the identity coset, so its block representation is not the identity. If g∈H and its block representation is the identity, the block corresponding to t1​=1 is τ(g)=1, and faithfulness of τ gives g=1. Thus this induced representation is faithful. Choosing a basis of W proves
G↪GLmr​(Zp​)​.
(47)
The continuous injection is a topological embedding, since G is compact and the target is Hausdorff. The trivial group is immediate. For uniform groups at p=2, the same proof uses the usual stronger powerfulness condition and the convergence ball 4End(Λ) instead; the preceding questions concern odd p.
