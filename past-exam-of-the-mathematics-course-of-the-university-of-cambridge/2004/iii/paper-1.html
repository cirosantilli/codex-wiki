The Hilbert basis theorem says that if B is a commutative Noetherian ring, then B[t] is Noetherian; iteration treats any finite number of variables. Here is a complete leading-coefficient proof. For an ideal I⊆B[t], let Ld​ be the set of degree-d coefficients of its polynomials of degree at most d, including zero coefficients. These are ideals of B, and multiplication by t gives Ld​⊆Ld+1​. The ascending chain condition makes the chain stationary from some N onward. Choose finite generators for L0​,…,LN​ and lift them to polynomials fdj​∈I of degree at most d. If f∈I has degree m, set d=min(m,N) and express its leading coefficient as ∑j​adj​bj​, where adj​ is the chosen degree-d coefficient. Then f−∑j​bj​tm−dfdj​ has smaller degree. Induction proves that the finitely many lifts generate I. This proves the theorem, including coefficient rings with zero divisors.
Put A=CG and L=C[X,X−1]. The infinite dihedral group has normal forms Xm and XmY. The relations move every Y to the right and reduce its exponent modulo two; the action X:n↦n+1, Y:n↦−n on Z shows that these forms are distinct. Thus
A=L⊕LY,Yf(X)=f(X−1)Y.
(1)
The Hilbert basis theorem makes C[X] Noetherian, and its localization L is Noetherian: an ideal of a localization is generated by the images of the finite generators of its contraction. Since A is free of rank two over L on the left, it is a Noetherian module over L. Every left ideal of A is an L-submodule, so all its ascending chains stabilize. Therefore CG is left Noetherian.
For the center of the infinite dihedral group algebra, write an element as f(X)+g(X)Y. Commuting it with X gives g(X)(X−1−X)=0 in the Laurent integral domain, forcing g=0. Commuting f(X) with Y then gives f(X)=f(X−1). A symmetric Laurent polynomial is a linear combination of 1 and Xj+X−j, j≥1. With z=X+X−1, the recursion
S0​=2,S1​=z,Sj+1​=zSj​−Sj−1​
(2)
expresses Sj​=Xj+X−j as a polynomial in z. Conversely every polynomial in z is central. The highest Laurent exponent of a nonzero polynomial in z shows that z is transcendental. Hence
R=Z(A)=C[z],z=X+X−1.​
(3)
The identities X−1=z−X and X2−zX+1=0 give L=R+RX. Independence follows by applying X↦X−1 to a relation f(z)+Xg(z)=0 and subtracting: (X−X−1)g(z)=0, so g=f=0. Consequently
A=R⊕RX⊕RY⊕RXY​
(4)
is a free R-module of rank four.
Let M be a nonzero simple module on the left. For any m=0, M=Am, so it is finite over R. Every central r∈/P=AnnR​(M) acts bijectively: its kernel and image are A-submodules, and simplicity forces kernel zero and image M. To prove that P is maximal, not merely prime, put B=R/P; then M is a faithful finite B-module. For 0=b∈B, bM=M. If m1​,…,md​ generate M, write mi​=b∑j​cij​mj​. The adjugate matrix identity for I−bC shows that det(I−bC) annihilates every generator and hence M. Faithfulness makes this determinant zero in B. Its constant term is one, so it has form 1−bq, proving bq=1. Thus B is a field, and P is a maximal ideal of R. This proves the general principle for simple modules over an algebra finite over its center in this instance.
Every maximal ideal of C[z] is (z−λ) for some λ∈C, by factorization of complex polynomials. The module M=Am is therefore a quotient of the four-dimensional algebra A/(z−λ)A. In particular dimC​M≤4<∞, as required.
The actual simple dimensions are at most two. If λ=±2, choose u=±1 with u+u−1=λ. The matrices X=diag(u,u−1) and Y=(01​10​) give a representation of A/(z−λ)A onto M2​(C): polynomial functions of X give the two diagonal matrix units, and multiplication by Y gives the off-diagonal ones. Both algebras have dimension four, so this is an isomorphism. If λ=±2, the element X∓1 has square zero and is conjugated to its negative by Y; its generated two-sided ideal is square zero. It annihilates every simple module, since its action on a simple module is either zero or the whole module, and the latter contradicts its square being zero. The remaining quotient is C[C2​], whose two simple modules have Y=±1. Thus the generic simple modules have dimension two, and the four exceptional one-dimensional characters have X=±1, Y=±1.

If M=0, all its localizations are zero. Conversely, take 0=m∈M. Its annihilator I=AnnR​(m) is a proper ideal, so it is contained in a maximal ideal P, which is prime. The zero criterion for module localization says that m/1=0 in MP​ only if some s∈/P satisfies sm=0. This would put s∈I⊆P, a contradiction. Therefore MP​=0, proving
M=0⟺MP​=0 for every prime ideal P.​
(5)
In fact maximal ideals suffice, and neither finite generation nor Noetherianity is needed for this implication.

Set C=N/φ(M). The natural map NP​→CP​ is onto. Its kernel is the image of φP​: a fraction n/s maps to zero precisely when tn∈φ(M) for some t∈/P. If tn=φ(m), then n/s=φP​(m/(ts)). This proves the localized cokernel identity directly:
coker(φP​)≅CP​.
(6)

Let S=R∖P. If Q⊆P is a prime ideal, then Q∩S=∅. Its extension is
QRP​={q/s:q∈Q, s∈S}.
(7)
It is proper: 1=q/s would give u(s−q)=0 for some u∈S, and hence us=uq∈Q, impossible because u,s∈/Q. If a product (a/s)(b/t) lies in QRP​, clearing a denominator gives uab∈Q for some u∈S. Primeness and u∈/Q imply a∈Q or b∈Q. Thus QRP​ is prime. Its contraction is Q, since r/1∈QRP​ implies ur∈Q for some u∈S, forcing r∈Q.
Conversely, contract a prime ideal q of RP​ to Q={r:r/1∈q}. This is a prime ideal of R. It contains no element of S, since such an element becomes a unit and cannot belong to the proper ideal q. Hence Q⊆P. Also r/s∈q exactly when r/1∈q, because s/1 is a unit, so q=QRP​. These extension and contraction operations are inverse, giving the prime ideal correspondence for localization:
Spec(RP​)⟷{Q∈SpecR:Q⊆P},Q↦QRP​.​
(8)

An associated prime of a module is an annihilator of a nonzero element. Suppose Q=AnnR​(m) is prime and Q⊆P. Then m/1=0 in MP​: a denominator s∈/P killing m would belong to Q⊆P. Moreover
AnnRP​​(m/1)=QRP​.
(9)
Indeed, if (a/s)(m/1)=0, some t∈/P has tam=0, so ta∈Q; primeness and t∈/Q imply a∈Q. The reverse containment is immediate. This proves one inclusion in the localization of associated primes formula.
For the other, let q=AnnRP​​(m/s) be an associated prime of MP​ and contract it to Q. By part (iii), Q is prime, Q⊆P and QRP​=q. Since s is invertible, q is also the annihilator of m/1. The Noetherian ring hypothesis makes Q=(a1​,…,ar​) finitely generated. For each ai​, the equality (ai​m)/1=0 supplies ti​∈/P with ti​ai​m=0. Put t=∏i​ti​, taking t=1 for the zero ideal. Then Q annihilates tm, while tm/1=0 because t/1 is a unit and m/1=0.
If b(tm)=0, then b/1 annihilates m/1, so b∈Q. Therefore AnnR​(tm)=Q, and Q∈AssR​(M). We conclude
AssRP​​(MP​)={QRP​:Q∈AssR​(M), Q⊆P}.​
(10)
The single common denominator t is the key point: one must turn a localized annihilator into the annihilator of one actual nonzero element of M, not merely assert that annihilators commute with localization.

Use generators x,D with Dx−xD=1. The ordered monomials xiDj form a basis: construct the Weyl algebra as the differential Ore extension of C[x] with Df=fD+f′, which has unique ordered polynomial expressions. Giving both generators degree one produces the Bernstein filtration, whose associated graded ring is C[x,ξ]. If a,b=0, their nonzero leading symbols have nonzero product in this polynomial integral domain. Thus ab has nonzero leading symbol and cannot vanish. Hence A1​(C) has no nontrivial zero divisors.
Here is the left-handed Ore theorem. Let S be a multiplicative set of regular elements of a ring, containing 1 and excluding 0. There is an injective Ore localization whose elements are left fractions s−1a if and only if
for every a∈A,s∈S, there are t∈S,b∈A with ta=bs.​
(11)
This is the left Ore condition. For a noncommutative domain, taking S=A∖{0} gives a division ring exactly when this condition holds. With zero-divisor denominators a further left reversibility condition is needed; the regular-denominator statement is the one used here.
Necessity follows by writing as−1=t−1b in the quotient and multiplying by t and s, giving ta=bs in the embedded original ring. For a construction proving sufficiency, use the left Ore fraction construction: pairs (s,a) represent s−1a, and (s,a) and (t,b) are equivalent when
us=vt=w∈S,ua=vb
(12)
for suitable u,v∈A. Reflexivity and symmetry are immediate. For transitivity, combine us=vt=w and u′t=v′q=w′ by a common left multiple hw=kw′∈S. Then (hv−ku′)t=0, so regularity gives hv=ku′, which combines the numerator equalities and proves transitivity.
Addition uses the common denominator w, with numerator ua+vb. To multiply s−1a by t−1b, choose v∈S,c∈A with va=ct; set
(s−1a)(t−1b)=(vs)−1cb.
(13)
The Ore condition supplies a common refinement whenever a representative or an Ore relation is changed, and regularity cancels the shared denominator, proving independence of choices. The same refinements verify the ring axioms; the formulas are simply addition and multiplication after clearing left denominators. The map a↦(1,a) is injective, since equivalence to zero would give ua=0 with u∈S. Each s∈S becomes invertible. A homomorphism inverting S extends uniquely by (s,a)↦f(s)−1f(a), giving the universal property. For all nonzero denominators in a noncommutative domain, a nonzero fraction has inverse (s−1a)−1=a−1s, so the quotient is a division ring. This is the requested proof sketch, with the fraction equivalence and multiplication made explicit.
It remains to check the left Ore condition for A1​(C). Its graded polynomial ring is Noetherian by the Hilbert basis theorem. Lifting homogeneous generators of a graded left ideal and successively cancelling leading symbols proves that the filtered algebra is a left Noetherian ring: each subtraction lowers the nonnegative degree, so it terminates.
In any left Noetherian noncommutative domain, nonzero principal left ideals intersect. Otherwise Ab∩Aa=0 with a,b=0 would make
Ab⊕Aba⊕Aba2⊕⋯
(14)
an infinite direct sum of nonzero left ideals. To verify directness, reduce a finite relation modulo Aa to make its first summand zero, then cancel a on the right and repeat. Its increasing finite partial sums contradict the ascending chain condition. A nonzero element of Aa∩As therefore has the form ta=bs=0, with t=0, proving the required condition. Consequently A1​(C) has a classical ring of left quotients, and it is a division ring.

For n≥1, the Weyl algebra A=An​(C) is generated by x1​,…,xn​,D1​,…,Dn​ with [Di​,xj​]=δij​ and the other generator commutators zero. Iterating the differential Ore extension construction gives the ordered basis xαDβ. In the Bernstein filtration, both kinds of generator have degree one, and
Fj​A=span{xαDβ:∣α∣+∣β∣≤j},dimFj​A=(2nj+2n​),grA≅C[x1​,…,xn​,ξ1​,…,ξn​].
(15)
Leading symbols show that A is a noncommutative domain. The Hilbert basis theorem and homogeneous-generator lifting by decreasing degree make it both left and right Noetherian.
It is also a simple ring. In a nonzero two-sided ideal, choose a nonzero element and a monomial xα0​Dβ0​ of maximal total degree with nonzero coefficient c. Applying (adD)α0​(adx)β0​ eliminates every lower-degree term and every different term of that maximal degree. The result is the nonzero scalar (−1)∣β0​∣α0​!β0​!c. Thus the ideal contains 1. Here the scalar extraction by iterated Weyl commutators uses characteristic zero. Despite simplicity, A has no nonzero finite-dimensional modules: in such a representation [D1​,x1​]=I would have trace 0=dimM, impossible over C. Its interesting module theory is necessarily infinite-dimensional.
For a finitely generated left module M, choose a finite-dimensional generating subspace M0​ and set Mj​=Fj​AM0​. This is a good filtration of a module. Its associated graded module is finite over the graded polynomial ring, so its cumulative Hilbert function is eventually a polynomial,
dimMj​=d(M)!e(M)​jd(M)+O(jd(M)−1)(M=0),
(16)
with positive integer multiplicity e(M). These are the Bernstein growth dimension of a Weyl algebra module and its multiplicity of a filtered module. Two good filtrations bound each other after fixed shifts: lift finite homogeneous generators in one filtration, bound their degrees in the other, and use compatibility with Fj​A; then reverse the roles. The growth degree and leading coefficient therefore do not depend on the good filtration of a module. The degree is also the Gelfand–Kirillov dimension of a module and the dimension of the characteristic variety of a Weyl algebra module, the support of grM. Since M is a quotient of a finite sum of copies of A, d(M)≤2n.
The essential lower bound is the Bernstein inequality for Weyl algebra modules:
M=0 finitely generated⟹d(M)≥n.​
(17)
Here is a full proof. Multiplication gives a linear map Fj​A→HomC​(Mj​,M2j​). Prove it injective by induction on j. At j=0 a scalar killing M0​=0 must vanish. If a∈Fj​A kills Mj​, then for every generator z, its commutator [a,z]∈Fj−1​A kills Mj−1​: both azm and zam are zero for m∈Mj−1​. Induction gives [a,z]=0 for all generators. In the ordered basis, [Di​,a] differentiates the xi​ exponents and [xi​,a] differentiates the Di​ exponents with a minus sign. Vanishing of all these commutators in characteristic zero forces a to be a scalar. It then vanishes because it kills M0​. This proves the faithful finite-step action of a Weyl algebra and hence
(2nj+2n​)≤(dimMj​)(dimM2j​)≤(dimM2j​)2.
(18)
Comparison of degrees gives 2n≤2d(M), proving the inequality. Equally, without invoking eventual polynomiality, this estimate directly bounds the Gelfand–Kirillov dimension of a module below by n. Right modules satisfy the same inequality through the filtration-preserving isomorphism A≅Aop, xi​↦xi​, Di​↦−Di​.
A nonzero finitely generated module with d(M)=n is a holonomic Weyl algebra module; the zero module is conventionally included. This minimal-growth class is stable under submodules, quotient modules and extensions. For 0→N→M→Q→0, take the induced and quotient filtrations. Their graded sequence is exact; Noetherianity of grA makes the induced filtrations good. Thus the Hilbert functions add and
d(M)=max(d(N),d(Q));
(19)
if both nonzero terms have the top dimension, their multiplicities add. For a holonomic Weyl algebra module, every nonzero subquotient has dimension n, by the upper bound inherited from M and Bernstein's lower bound. Each nonzero factor in a strict chain therefore consumes positive integral multiplicity. This proves the multiplicity bounds the length of a holonomic Weyl module:
lengthA​(M)≤e(M).​
(20)
In particular a holonomic module is both Noetherian and Artinian and has finite length, although its vector-space dimension is infinite. This distinction separates finite-dimensional representation theory from finite-length Weyl module theory.
The polynomial module C[x1​,…,xn​], with xi​ acting by multiplication and Di​ by differentiation, is generated by 1. Its Mj​ consists of polynomials of degree at most j, with dimension (nj+n​), so d=n and e=1. It is simple: differentiating a maximal-degree monomial of a nonzero polynomial isolates a nonzero constant, and multiplication by polynomials then generates the whole module. A second basic example is the delta module
A/∑i​Axi​.
(21)
Using the reverse ordered basis Dβxα, its vector space is C[D1​,…,Dn​]; Di​ acts by multiplication and xi​ by negative differentiation. The same argument proves simplicity and holonomicity. These two examples are exchanged by the automorphism xi​↦Di​, Di​↦−xi​. Free modules, in contrast, have growth dimension 2n and are not holonomic. Thus the central organizing invariants are good filtration of a module, growth dimension, characteristic support and multiplicity, with Bernstein's estimate explaining why dimension n is the natural smallest nonzero size.

Specify a commutative ground ring k and a unital associative k-algebra R. For an R-bimodule M, the Hochschild cochain complex has
Cq(R,M)=Homk​(R⊗k​q,M),C0(R,M)=M,
(22)
and coboundary
(δf)(a1​,…,aq+1​)=​a1​f(a2​,…,aq+1​)+i=1∑q​(−1)if(a1​,…,ai​ai+1​,…,aq+1​)+(−1)q+1f(a1​,…,aq​)aq+1​.​
(23)
The bimodule identities and associativity cancel the terms of δ2 in pairs. The Hochschild cohomology is HHkq​(R,M)=kerδq​/imδq−1​; the paper's Hq(R,R) uses the regular bimodule as coefficients.
Over a field, the bar resolution of an associative algebra explains its homological meaning. Put Re=R⊗k​Rop. The degree-q bar term is R⊗k​(q+2), with the outer factors supplying the Re action, boundary obtained by multiplying adjacent factors with alternating signs, and augmentation multiplication R⊗R→R. Inserting 1 at the left gives a k-linear contracting homotopy, proving exactness. The terms are free Re-modules after choosing a k-basis for the inner tensor factors. Applying HomRe​(−,M) gives precisely the displayed cochain complex and therefore
HHkq​(R,M)≅ExtReq​(R,M).
(24)
This ground-ring qualification in Hochschild cohomology matters for an arbitrary ring: one can take k=Z, but the bar terms need not be projective over Re. The ordinary cochain complex then computes relative Hochschild cohomology; the absolute Ext identification needs, for example, R projective over k. Likewise the extension classification below applies to ground-ring-linearly split extensions. For algebras over a field these qualifications are automatic.
In degree zero, (δm)(a)=am−ma, so HH0(R,R)=Z(R). In degree one the cocycle equation is
ad(b)−d(ab)+d(a)b=0,
(25)
exactly the Leibniz rule for a derivation of an algebra. The coboundaries are the inner derivations a↦am−ma. Thus the First Hochschild cohomology as outer derivations is
HH1(R,R)=Derk​(R)/Inn(R).​
(26)
Its significance is infinitesimal automorphisms modulo inner ones. Over the dual numbers k[ε]/(ε2), the map a↦a+εd(a) is multiplicative precisely when d satisfies the Leibniz rule. Conjugation by 1+εm gives a↦a+ε(ma−am), an inner derivation with the opposite sign convention. The quotient therefore measures infinitesimal symmetry not arising from conjugation.
Degree two controls extensions and changes of multiplication. A k-linear split square-zero extension of an algebra with prescribed kernel bimodule M can be written as E=R⊕M. Choose a unital section and let f(a,b) be the difference between its product and its value on ab. Then
(a,u)(b,v)=(ab,av+ub+f(a,b)).
(27)
Computing both parenthesizations of a triple gives the associativity condition
af(b,c)−f(ab,c)+f(a,bc)−f(a,b)c=0,
(28)
namely δf=0. A unital product has f(1,a)=f(a,1)=0, so one uses the normalized Hochschild cochain complex. Changing section by g:R→M gives f↦f+δg. Conversely the displayed product for any normalized two-cocycle constructs such an extension, and a section change gives the corresponding extension isomorphism. This proves that second Hochschild cohomology classifies split square-zero extensions:
HH2(R,M) classifies these extensions, up to equivalence fixing R,M.​
(29)
The zero class is the split algebra extension with no extra product term. With M=R, this gives one meaning of the requested H2(R,R).
For a first-order associative deformation, retain the underlying k-module and put a∗b=ab+εf(a,b). Expanding (a∗b)∗c=a∗(b∗c) modulo ε2 gives exactly δf=0. A coordinate change T=1+εg transports the product to T−1(T(a)∗T(b)), whose first-order coefficient is f+δg. Hence
HH2(R,R) is the space of inequivalent first-order changes of multiplication.​
(30)
This classification is of infinitesimal deformations, not a guarantee that all classes extend to every order. For a∗b=ab+tf(a,b)+t2μ2​(a,b) modulo t3, the next associativity equation is
δμ2​(a,b,c)=f(f(a,b),c)−f(a,f(b,c)).
(31)
Substitution of δf=0 shows the right-hand side is a three-cocycle. Its class in HH3(R,R) is the second-order obstruction to an associative deformation. It must vanish for a second-order extension to exist. At higher orders the same expansion gives further obstruction cocycles. If HH2(R,R)=0, every first nonzero coefficient of a formal associative deformation is a coboundary and can be removed by a coordinate change. Repeating this order by order gives formal rigidity from vanishing second Hochschild cohomology, with the transformations converging in the t-adic topology. Vanishing of HH3 instead removes these extension obstructions; these are distinct roles.
Examples make the two low degrees concrete. For R=k[x] over a field, inner derivations vanish and every derivation is h(x)∂x​, determined by its value on x. The bimodule resolution
0⟶k[x]⊗k[x]x⊗1−1⊗x​k[x]⊗k[x]⟶k[x]⟶0
(32)
is exact: the middle tensor ring is the polynomial integral domain k[u,v], multiplication identifies its quotient by u−v, and multiplication by u−v is injective. Applying bimodule Hom gives zero differential. Consequently the Hochschild cohomology of a polynomial algebra in one variable is
HH0(k[x])=k[x],HH1(k[x])=k[x]∂x​,HHq(k[x])=0(q≥2).
(33)
This algebra has many infinitesimal automorphisms, while its ordinary associative multiplication is formally rigid.
For R=k[x,y] in characteristic zero, the bilinear map f(a,b)=(∂y​a)(∂x​b) is a two-cocycle: substitution in δf and the two product rules cancel its four terms. It is not a coboundary, because every δg on a commutative algebra is symmetric in its two inputs, whereas f(y,x)−f(x,y)=1. This class integrates to the Weyl deformation of a polynomial algebra in two variables, with yx−xy=t. The differential Ore extension construction supplies the ordered basis xiyj over k[t], so the t-adic completion has the fixed underlying module k[x,y][[t]]. Its first multiplication correction is exactly this f. This displays a genuinely noncommutative formal associative deformation detected by HH2.
Finally, the Hochschild cup product (f⌣g)(a1​,…,ap+q​)=f(a1​,…,ap​)g(ap+1​,…,ap+q​) descends to the graded cohomology algebra, and the Gerstenhaber bracket organizes the interaction of derivations and formal associative deformation cocycles. The calculations above explain why the first two positive degrees are especially useful: HH1 measures outer infinitesimal symmetries, while HH2 measures split extensions and infinitesimal associative structures, with degree three detecting the first obstruction to continuing them.
