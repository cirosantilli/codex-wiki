A chain complex consists of abelian groups Cq​ and homomorphisms ∂q​:Cq​→Cq−1​ with ∂q−1​∂q​=0. Its groups of chain cycles and chain boundaries are Zq​=ker∂q​ and Bq​=im∂q+1​; the identity ensures Bq​⊆Zq​. Its homology groups are
Hq​(C∗​)=Zq​/Bq​.
(1)
For a nonnegative complex, take Cq​=0 for q<0.
The singular chain group Cq​(X) is the free abelian group on continuous maps σ:Δq→X, called singular simplices. With δi​ the inclusion of the face opposite vertex i, define
∂σ=∑i=0q​(−1)iσ∘δi​.
(2)
Deleting any two vertices in the two possible orders gives the same face with opposite signs, proving ∂2=0. This defines the singular chain complex and its singular homology. A continuous map f induces the chain map f#​(σ)=f∘σ; it commutes with the boundary because composition commutes with restriction to faces.
To prove homotopy invariance of homology, let F:X×I→Y have endpoints f,g. Triangulate Δq×I by the q+1 simplices with ordered vertices
(v0​,0),…,(vi​,0),(vi​,1),…,(vq​,1),0≤i≤q.
(3)
Let pi​:Δq+1→Δq×I be their affine parameterizations. The singular prism operator is
Pq​(σ)=∑i=0q​(−1)iF∘(σ×1I​)∘pi​.
(4)
On taking boundaries, shared internal faces of consecutive prism simplices cancel. The top and bottom faces give g#​σ−f#​σ, and the remaining faces are −Pq−1​(∂σ) with their induced signs. Thus
∂P+P∂=g#​−f#​.
(5)
For a chain cycle z, g#​z−f#​z=∂Pz is a chain boundary, so f∗​=g∗​ on homology. If f is a homotopy equivalence, its homotopy inverse therefore induces an inverse to f∗​, proving that singular homology depends only on homotopy type.
Use the standard CW complex for an orientable genus-three surface: one vertex, six edges a1​,b1​,a2​,b2​,a3​,b3​, and a two-cell whose attaching word is ∏j=13​[aj​,bj​]. The cellular homology theorem identifies this cellular calculation with singular homology. The boundary of every edge is zero, and each edge's total exponent in the attaching word is zero, so the two-cell boundary is zero. Hence
Hq​(Σ3​;Z)=⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z6,0,​q=0,2,q=1,otherwise.​​
(6)
Choose C1​ to be the nonseparating curve represented by a1​, and choose C2​ as a small circle bounding a disc in a surface patch. Then [C1​] is a nonzero primitive homology class, whereas the inclusion of C2​ induces zero on H1​. If a homotopy equivalence carried C1​ onto C2​, its restriction would factor through C2​ and kill [C1​], contradicting injectivity on first homology. This is the homology obstruction to carrying surface curves onto one another.
There is nevertheless such a continuous map. Map the edge a1​ homeomorphically onto S1 and all other edges to its base point. The two-cell attaching word then has winding number zero in S1, hence is null-homotopic and the map extends to r:Σ3​→S1. Compose r with a homeomorphism S1→C2​ and the inclusion of C2​ in the surface. This gives g(C1​)=C2​. Thus an arbitrary map can carry the first curve onto the second, but a homotopy equivalence cannot.
For an unambiguous drawing, realize Σ3​ as the double of a manifold formed from a disc with three holes. Its two copies are glued along all four boundary circles. The red curve below follows an arc from the outer boundary to the first hole on the front copy and returns along the matching arc on the back copy; it is a nonseparating curve. The blue circle is entirely in a disc patch on the front copy and is contractible.

A CW complex is built by attaching q-dimensional discs to the (q−1)-skeleton along maps of their boundary spheres, with the closure-finite and weak-topology conditions. Write Xq for its q-skeleton and X−1=∅. The cellular chain group is
Cqcell​(X)=Hq​(Xq,Xq−1)≅⨁q-cells​Z.
(7)
The cellular boundary is the composite of the connecting map and the relative quotient map:
Hq​(Xq,Xq−1)δ​Hq−1​(Xq−1)⟶Hq−1​(Xq−1,Xq−2).
(8)
Consecutive maps in the relevant exact sequences compose to zero, so these maps make a cellular chain complex. Equivalently, a boundary coefficient is the degree of the attaching sphere followed by collapse onto the sphere of a selected lower cell. Its cellular homology is kerdq​/imdq+1​. The relative groups of successive skeleta vanish off their cell dimension; their exact sequences identify this homology with singular homology, the cellular homology theorem.
For k≥1, the sphere Sk has a zero-cell and a k-cell with zero boundary. The Complex projective space CPn has one cell in each dimension 0,2,…,2n and no odd cells, so all cellular boundaries vanish. Consequently
Hq​(Sk)={Z,0,​q=0,k,otherwise,​Hq​(CPn)={Z,0,​q∈{0,2,…,2n},otherwise.​​
(9)
At k=0, the sphere consists of two points and H0​(S0)=Z2; at n=0, projective space is a point.
Put a=2n−2 and Z=Sa×S3. Its product CW complex has cells in dimensions 0,3,a,a+3=2n+1. For n>3, these positive dimensions are distinct and never consecutive, so all cellular boundaries vanish. Thus
Hq​(Z)={Z,0,​q∈{0,3,2n−2,2n+1},otherwise.​​
(10)
Its 2n-skeleton is Sa∨S3. Collapsing it gives the quotient
q:Z⟶Sa∧S3≅S2n+1.
(11)
It induces an isomorphism on top homology, with degree +1 after choosing the quotient orientation. Let p:S2n+1→CPn be the Hopf fibration and set ϕ=pq, the Hopf projection of a product smash quotient.
On reduced homology, the collapse kills the two lower-dimensional classes, and the remaining top class maps to zero because projective space has no odd-dimensional homology. Therefore ϕ∗​=0 in every reduced degree. On homotopy groups, πj​(Z)≅πj​(Sa)⊕πj​(S3) for j≥2, with summands induced by the two factor inclusions. Both inclusions are collapsed by q, so q∗​ and hence ϕ∗​ vanish on both summands. The domain is simply connected and connected, which also handles the lower groups.
It remains to prove that ϕ is not null-homotopic. The Hopf fibration has fibre S1 and has the homotopy lifting property for the CW domain Z: a homotopy downstairs and a lift of its initial map have a lift with that specified initial value. If ϕ were homotopic to a constant, lift this homotopy starting at q. The ending map would land in the single fibre S1. Every map Z→S1 is null-homotopic, since the simply connected domain permits its lift to the contractible universal cover R→S1. Thus q would be null-homotopic in S2n+1, contradicting its nonzero top homology map. Hence
ϕ∗​ vanishes on all homotopy groups and reduced homology, but ϕ≃∗.​
(12)

An orientation of a topological n-manifold without boundary is a choice of generator μx​ of each local homology group Hn​(M,M∖{x};Z)≅Z, compatible in coordinate neighbourhoods. Equivalently, local orientations of coordinate discs are chosen so their overlap changes have degree +1. This trivializes the orientation local system.
Here the printed Hctn​ denotes compactly supported cohomology, which we write Hcn​. We prove the cyclicity assertion without assuming Poincare duality. By the compactly supported cohomology of Euclidean space, an open n-disc has one copy of Z in degree n and zero in all other degrees. The compact-support Mayer–Vietoris sequence for open sets contains
Hcn​(U∩V)⟶Hcn​(U)⊕Hcn​(V)⟶Hcn​(U∪V)⟶Hcn+1​(U∩V).
(13)
Use the finite good cover allowed in the paper. Induction on the number of discs shows that any union of its discs has zero compact-support cohomology above degree n: at each step the intersection with the new disc is itself a union of fewer discs with a good cover, so the displayed sequence and its higher-degree terms apply.
The same induction presents Hcn​(M) by a generator ui​ for each cover disc, with overlap relations ui​=ϵij​uj​, where ϵij​=±1 is its orientation transition sign. Indeed Hcn​ of the intersection is generated by its overlap discs; extension by zero sends each generator to the two disc generators with these signs. Passing through the compact-support Mayer–Vietoris sequence imposes exactly those relations. This is top compactly supported cohomology from orientation signs.
Since M is connected, the overlap graph is connected: a disconnection would split the union into two disjoint nonempty open subsets. A spanning tree expresses every generator as u or −u, so Hcn​(M) is cyclic. Additional overlap relations can only impose 2u=0. If M is oriented, use its compatible local orientations; every relation becomes ui​=uj​, and the presentation is exactly one free generator. Thus
Hcn​(M) is cyclic;Hcn​(M)≅Z if M is oriented.​
(14)
In fact a nonorientable connected manifold has an orientation-reversing loop in this transition graph and gives Z/2. In dimension zero the connected manifold is a point and the assertion is immediate.
For a closed oriented n-manifold, Poincare duality says that its fundamental class [M]∈Hn​(M;Z) gives isomorphisms by cap product
Hq(M;Z) ∩[M] ​Hn−q​(M;Z)for all q.
(15)
For an oriented manifold that is not necessarily compact, the corresponding ordinary-homology statement is Hcq​(M;Z)≅Hn−q​(M;Z), using the locally finite fundamental class. The version without compact supports on cohomology pairs with locally finite homology instead. Orientability and the absence of boundary are essential to these untwisted formulas; for a manifold with boundary one needs Poincare-Lefschetz duality, and for a nonorientable manifold one uses the orientation local system.
For the following cases, H0​=Z forces connectedness. The universal coefficient theorem for cohomology gives
H1(M;Z)≅Hom(H1​(M;Z),Z),
(16)
since the Ext term from H0​=Z vanishes. Combining with duality yields the third homology of a closed oriented four-manifold constraint H3​≅Hom(H1​,Z).

For an oriented real vector bundle π:E→X of rank d, the Thom isomorphism theorem gives a unique Thom class uE​∈Hd(D(E),S(E);Z) restricting to the chosen generator in each fibre, and isomorphisms
Hq(X;Z) a↦π∗a⌣uE​ ​Hq+d(D(E),S(E);Z).
(19)
Equivalently use the pair (E,E∖X), identifying X with the zero section. After forgetting relative supports, pull the Thom class back along the zero section s to define the Euler class:
e(E)=s∗uE​∈Hd(X;Z).​
(20)
In this expression the relative class is first mapped to absolute cohomology.
A compact embedded submanifold equipped with a coorientation Y⊂X has an oriented normal bundle νY​ of rank d. A tubular neighborhood and excision identify its Thom class with a class uY​∈Hd(X,X∖Y). Its image in Hd(X) is the cohomology class of a cooriented submanifold εY​. In particular it restricts to zero on X∖Y. If the ambient manifold is oriented, orient Y by the normal-first convention. The class is its Poincare dual, characterized by
⟨εY​⌣α,[X]⟩=⟨α∣Y​,[Y]⟩
(21)
for the appropriate complementary degree. This is the stated integration identity in cohomological notation.
First prove the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem for an oriented closed n-manifold X. Orient its diagonal by the identification Δ≅X, and choose the normal orientation so this agrees with the normal-first convention in the product. This produces the integral class εΔ​. To compute the part that contributes to the trace, tensor with Q: the trace on a finitely generated integral cohomology group means its trace on the free quotient, equivalently on rational cohomology; torsion contributes nothing.
Choose a basis ep,j​ of Hp(X;Q) and its Poincare duality pairing dual basis ep,j∨​∈Hn−p(X;Q), normalized by ⟨ep,i​⌣ep,j∨​,[X]⟩=δij​. The rational image of the cohomology class of the diagonal is
εΔ​=p,j∑​(−1)npep,j∨​×ep,j​.​
(22)
Indeed, pairing this expression against α×β with degrees p,n−p picks out the p-summands. The cross-product multiplication contributes (−1)p2 and moving α past ep,j∨​ contributes (−1)p(n−p). Together with (−1)np their product is +1. The result is ⟨α⌣β,[X]⟩, exactly the diagonal's defining identity. The rational Künneth theorem and nondegeneracy of the duality pairing determine the class. An integral torsion component, if present, is not determined by this rational expression and does not affect any evaluation below.
For the graph map Γf​=(1,f):X→X×X, write f∗ep,j​=∑i​Aij​ep,i​. Pulling back the expression and evaluating gives the graph-diagonal formula for the Lefschetz number:
⟨Γf∗​εΔ​,[X]⟩​=p,j∑​(−1)np⟨ep,j∨​⌣f∗ep,j​,[X]⟩=p∑​(−1)np+p(n−p)trAp​=p∑​(−1)ptr(f∗:Hp(X;Q)→Hp(X;Q))=L(f).​
(23)
If f has no fixed point, its graph lies in (X×X)∖Δ. The relative Thom class restricts to zero there, so Γf∗​εΔ​=0 and L(f)=0. The contrapositive proves the required fixed-point assertion, without requiring smoothness of f or transversality of its graph.
The printed hypothesis does not require X to be orientable. For a nonorientable X, the diagonal's normal bundle is TX, so an ordinary integral coorientation cannot be assumed. We extend the proved theorem as follows, rather than apply the untwisted diagonal computation incorrectly. We first construct a smooth embedding in Euclidean space using finitely many charts χi​:Ui​→Rn and smooth functions ρi​ supported in those charts, with at least one ρi​(x)>0 at every point. The map Ψ(x)=(ρi​(x),ρi​(x)χi​(x))i​, with zero extensions, is injective: a positive first coordinate identifies a chart in which its remaining coordinates recover x. Its differential is injective as well, since dρi​(v)=0 and d(ρi​χi​)(v)=0 force dχi​(v)=0 in a chart with ρi​>0. Compactness makes this injective immersion a homeomorphism onto its image. This finite-chart Euclidean embedding of a compact smooth manifold, enlarged by zero coordinates if necessary, has positive codimension. A compact tubular disc neighbourhood W is an oriented N-manifold with boundary, inheriting its orientation from the ambient Euclidean space. Its double of a manifold M is closed and oriented. Projection of each copy of W to X agrees on their common boundary, giving r:M→X; inclusion into the first copy gives i:X→M with ri=1X​.
Set F=ifr. A fixed point of F must lie in i(X), and then is exactly the image of a fixed point of f. Moreover F∗=r∗f∗i∗, and cyclicity of trace for maps between finite-dimensional rational cohomology groups gives
tr(r∗f∗i∗)=tr(f∗i∗r∗)=tr(f∗)
(24)
in each degree, because i∗r∗=1. Thus L(F)=L(f). The oriented theorem for M now gives a fixed point of F, hence of f, whenever L(f)=0. This oriented manifold retract for the Lefschetz theorem proves the full printed result. The double construction also works componentwise if necessary.

For a sphere self-map, the Lefschetz number is L(f)=1+(−1)ndegf. At degree zero this is 1, so the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem supplies x with f(x)=x.
Let A:Sn→Sn be the antipodal map, A(z)=−z, of degree (−1)n+1. The composite Af also has degree zero, hence Lefschetz number one, so it has a fixed point y. The equation A(f(y))=y is f(y)=−y. Consequently
∃x,y∈Sn: f(x)=x,f(y)=−y.​
(25)
The two points are distinct, since a unit vector is never its own negative.

Use the standard integral cohomology ring H∗(CP2)=Z[u]/(u3), ∣u∣=2. Any self-homeomorphism h sends u to u or −u, and hence sends u2 to u2. Its Lefschetz number is therefore 1±1+1, which is either 3 or 1. By the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem, every such homeomorphism has a fixed point.
Now CP2 is simply connected: its CW structure has no one-cells and only two- and four-cells. If it covered a connected space with degree d≥2, it would be the universal cover. The usual covering space hypotheses hold for the base because it is locally homeomorphic to this manifold. Its deck transformation group would act transitively on every fibre and have d elements. A nonidentity deck transformation has no fixed point: uniqueness of lifts makes one fixing a point the identity everywhere on the connected cover. This contradicts the preceding calculation. Thus
CP2 is not the total space of a covering of degree at least two.​
(26)
Thus the complex projective plane has no nontrivial covering quotient.

Over the Grassmannian Grk​(CN), the complex tautological bundle on a Grassmannian is
γkN​={(W,v):W∈Grk​(CN), v∈W},(W,v)⟼W.
(27)
Its fibre at W is the k-plane W itself. A neighbourhood of a fixed plane consists of graphs of linear maps A:W→W⊥; (A,w)↦(graphA,w+Aw) gives a local trivialization. The inclusions of coordinate spaces give the infinite Grassmannian and its tautological rank-k bundle γk∞​.
Use the customary compact Hausdorff base convention for the classification of complex vector bundles by a Grassmannian; equivalently the argument uses finite subordinate partitions of unity. Give a rank-k complex vector bundle E→X a continuous fiber metric given by Hermitian inner products. Choose a finite trivializing cover with local orthonormal coordinates ti​:E∣Ui​​→Ui​×Ck, and a subordinate partition of unity ρi​ with support contained in Ui​. Define
Jx​(v)=(ρi​(x)​ti,x​(v))i​∈Ckr,
(28)
extending each component by zero off its support. This is continuous and satisfies ∥Jx​(v)∥2=∑i​ρi​(x)∥v∥2=∥v∥2, so it is a fibrewise embedding. The image planes vary continuously: in local frames, their orthogonal projection matrix is J(J∗J)−1J∗. Thus they define a map cE​:X→Grk​(Ckr), and v↦(x,Jx​v) identifies E with cE∗​γk∞​. This proves that every bundle is a tautological pullback vector bundle, by the finite-dimensional embedding of a complex vector bundle.
We next prove the two independence assertions that turn this construction into a bijection. First, homotopic maps have isomorphic pullback vector bundles. Pulling back along a homotopy gives a bundle over compact Hausdorff X×I; embed that bundle in a finite trivial vector bundle by the construction just given. Its image has a continuous family of rank-k orthogonal projection matrices P(x,t). Compactness gives a subdivision 0=t0​<⋯<ts​=1 with
∥P(x,tj+1​)−P(x,tj​)∥<1for every x.
(29)
Restriction of P(x,tj+1​) to the image at tj​ is injective: if it killed v, then v=(P(x,tj​)−P(x,tj+1​))v, impossible by the strict norm bound unless v=0. Equal ranks make it an isomorphism, with continuous local inverses. Composing these bundle maps identifies the endpoint vector bundles. The fact that close orthogonal projections identify their image bundles therefore makes the pullback assignment well-defined on homotopy classes.
Conversely, suppose two classifying maps give isomorphic vector bundles. The infinite Grassmannian has its standard weak CW topology; the compact-subset property of CW complexes puts the compact images of the two maps in finite stages. Their tautological pullback vector bundles therefore give two finite-dimensional embeddings J0​:E→CN0​ and J1​:E→CN1​ of the same bundle, after using the vector bundle isomorphism. In orthogonal coordinate blocks interpolate by
Jt​(v)=(cos2πt​J0​(v), sin2πt​J1​(v)).
(30)
At least one coefficient is nonzero, so every Jt​ is injective and its image planes give a continuous Grassmannian homotopy. One endpoint uses the first block and the other the second block. Moving the latter coordinate copy to the standard one is a fixed unitary change of coordinates, connected to the identity by a path in the unitary group. Such a path exists by diagonalizing the unitary matrix and continuously varying its eigenvalue phases. Hence the original two maps are homotopic in the infinite Grassmannian. This also proves independence of all embedding and metric choices. Together the two directions establish
VectCk​(X)⟷[X,Grk​(C∞)],[f]⟼[f∗γk∞​].​
(31)
For arbitrary non-Hausdorff compact spaces without the required numerability, that usual bundle-classification statement needs additional hypotheses; the construction above specifies precisely the conventional setting being used.
Write B=Grk​(C∞). For the assumed fiber bundle Uk​→ν→B with contractible total space, the long exact sequence of homotopy groups of a fibration has segment
0=πq​(ν)⟶πq​(B)∂​πq−1​(Uk​)⟶πq−1​(ν)=0.
(32)
For q≥2 this gives group isomorphisms. For q=1, the relevant pointed-set part and connectedness of Uk​ give π1​(B)=0, corresponding to the single component π0​(Uk​). Thus the claimed formula includes its endpoint:
πq​(B)≅πq−1​(Uk​)(q≥1),π1​(B)=0.​
(33)
Finally the determinant gives a fiber bundle SU2​→U2​→S1. Since π3​(S1)=π4​(S1)=0, its exact sequence yields π3​(U2​)≅π3​(SU2​). The given identification SU2​≅S3 and the degree calculation π3​(S3)=Z therefore give
[S4,BU2​]=π4​(BU2​)≅π3​(U2​)≅Z.
(34)
The equality of free and based homotopy classes uses the simple connectedness just proved. Hence
VectC2​(S4)≅Z.​
(35)
The classification of rank-two complex vector bundles over the four-sphere is a bijection of isomorphism classes, labelled by the integer clutching function homotopy class after choosing a generator. It is not an assertion that Whitney sum gives a group operation on bundles of fixed rank two.
