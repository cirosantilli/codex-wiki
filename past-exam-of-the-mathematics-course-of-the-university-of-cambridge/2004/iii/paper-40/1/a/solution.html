For a weakly stationary process with mean m and nonzero variance, put γ(h)=Cov(Xt+h​,Xt​) and ρ(h)=γ(h)/γ(0). The function ρ is its autocorrelation function. For observations x1​,…,xN​, one common sample autocorrelation function is
γ​(h)=N1​∑t=1N−h​(xt​−x)(xt+h​−x),ρ​(h)=γ​(0)γ​(h)​.
(1)
A correlogram plots these estimates against lag; the population analogue plots ρ(h).
The lag-h partial autocorrelation function measures the correlation between Xt​ and Xt−h​ after removing their linear projections on the intervening observations. Its sample counterpart can be calculated by the Yule-Walker equations: solve
Rh​ϕ​h​=rh​,(Rh​)ij​=ρ​(∣i−j∣),(rh​)i​=ρ​(i),
(2)
and take α(h)=ϕ​h,h​, assuming the matrix is nonsingular. This defines a standard sample partial autocorrelation function.
For a minimal causal autoregressive model of order p, the autocorrelation typically decays geometrically, possibly with damped oscillations, whereas the partial autocorrelation function is zero beyond lag p: after those p preceding values have been projected out, the innovation is orthogonal to older values. For a moving-average model of order q, nonoverlapping sets of driving white noise give γ(h)=0 for ∣h∣>q; its partial autocorrelation function generally tails off when the representation is invertible. Thus an ACF cutoff suggests an MA order; a PACF cutoff suggests an AR order. An ARMA model generally has neither cutoff. These are population properties; sample noise makes cutoffs approximate, so fitted residuals and uncertainty should also be inspected. Under independent white noise, fixed-lag sample correlations have approximate standard error N−1/2, giving a rough diagnostic band rather than a universal simultaneous test.
