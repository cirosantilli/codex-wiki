Let M=Rd/Λ be a flat torus, where Λ is a full-rank Euclidean lattice, and use the nonnegative Laplace-Beltrami operator Δ=−∑k=1d​∂k2​. The dual lattice is
Λ∗={w∈Rd:⟨w,v⟩∈Z for every v∈Λ}.
(1)
For w∈Λ∗, the function ϕw​(x)=vol(M)−1/2e2πi⟨w,x⟩ is periodic under Λ, so it defines a function on M, and direct differentiation gives Δϕw​=4π2∣w∣2ϕw​. Integrating characters on a fundamental parallelepiped proves that these functions are orthonormal. After choosing a lattice basis, the ordinary Fourier series theorem on Rd/Zd proves completeness, so there are no additional eigenvalues. Thus the spectrum of a flat torus is
Spec(ΔM​)={4π2∣w∣2:w∈Λ∗},​
(2)
with multiplicity equal to the number of dual vectors of the given length; the zero eigenvalue occurs once.
It remains to show that a two-dimensional Euclidean lattice is determined up to an orthogonal transformation by its vector-length multiset. This is the two-dimensional lattice reconstruction from vector lengths. Let v be a shortest nonzero vector, of length a. It is primitive: v=ku with integer ∣k∣>1 would give a shorter nonzero vector. The lattice vectors on its line are therefore precisely the integer multiples of v. Subtract two occurrences of each length ka, k=1,2,…, from the length multiset. This removes exactly one primitive lattice line, even when other vectors happen to have the same lengths. The smallest remaining length b is the length of a shortest vector w independent of v.
These two vectors form a basis of the lattice. Otherwise a nonzero coset of Zv+Zw has a lattice representative u=sv+tw with ∣s∣,∣t∣≤1/2. It cannot have t=0, since primitivity of v would then force u=0. It is therefore independent of v, while
∣u∣≤∣s∣a+∣t∣b≤21​(a+b)≤b.
(3)
The inequality is strict: if both coefficients are nonzero, the vectors are not parallel and the triangle inequality is strict; if s=0, then ∣u∣≤b/2. This contradicts the choice of w.
The covolume A is also determined by the length multiset. If N(R) counts lattice vectors in a radius-R disk, then
N(R)=AπR2​+O(R),A=limR→∞​N(R)πR2​.
(4)
To justify the estimate, tile the plane by a bounded fundamental parallelogram. Only cells meeting a fixed-width neighbourhood of the disk boundary contribute an error; their total number is O(R). The lattice basis v,w has Gram matrix
(a2c​cb2​),a2b2−c2=A2.
(5)
Thus ∣c∣=a2b2−A2​ is determined. Replacing w by −w makes c≥0, so the Gram matrix is determined entirely. Equal Gram matrices give an orthogonal map between the lattice bases and hence between the lattices.
Two isospectral two-dimensional flat tori consequently have orthogonally equivalent dual lattices. Taking duals shows that their original lattices are orthogonally equivalent too, and the orthogonal map descends to an isometry of the quotient tori. Two isospectral flat tori of dimension two are isometric.

Use the positive Laplace-Beltrami operator Δ=−div∇. For the global discrete spectral statements, take M to be a closed manifold with a smooth Riemannian metric. The Riemannian heat kernel is the integral kernel H(t,x,y) of the heat semigroup e−tΔ:
(e−tΔf)(x)=∫M​H(t,x,y)f(y)dVy​,(∂t​+Δx​)H=0,H(0,x,y)=δy​(x).
(6)
The initial condition is distributional with respect to the Riemannian volume form. Equivalently, an orthonormal basis of Laplacian eigenfunctions gives the spectral expansion of the Riemannian heat kernel
H(t,x,y)=∑j​e−tλj​ϕj​(x)ϕj​(y)​.
(7)
The usual compact elliptic spectral theorem supplies this basis; polynomial elliptic regularity bounds and exponential decay prove convergence with every space derivative on t≥ε>0. These analytic results may be used as clearly stated external ingredients. We will also construct the kernel locally and prove its short-time expansion.
On a sufficiently small neighbourhood of the diagonal, let r=d(x,y) and write x=expy​ξ in geodesic normal coordinates, with dVx​=J(y,ξ)dξ. The heat kernel expansion has the form
H(t,x,y)∼(4πt)−m/2e−r2/(4t)∑j≥0​tjuj​(x,y),m=dimM.
(8)
The heat invariants are the integrated diagonal coefficients aj​=∫M​uj​(x,x)dVx​, so
Tr(e−tΔ)∼(4πt)−m/2∑j≥0​aj​tj.
(9)
In particular
a0​=vol(M),a1​=61​∫M​RdV,a2​=3601​∫M​(5R2−2∣Ric∣2+2∣Rm∣2)dV.
(10)
Here R is scalar curvature, Ric is the Ricci tensor and Rm is the Riemann curvature tensor. The last expression is the integrated second scalar heat coefficient; a local divergence term integrates to zero on a closed manifold. The exponent of the leading heat-trace term determines dimension, and these coefficients determine volume, total scalar curvature, and the displayed integrated curvature combination. On a surface R=2K, where K is Gaussian curvature, so a1​=31​∫KdA=32π​χ(M) by the Gauss-Bonnet theorem, and a2​=151​∫K2dA. Thus the Euler characteristic, and the genus of a connected orientable closed surface, are determined. The first three coefficients also detect constant Gaussian curvature, since they determine ∫(K−K)2dA. These conclusions do not claim that the pointwise curvature function or the metric is determined.
Here is the local construction. Put gt​=(4πt)−m/2e−r2/(4t). The Gauss lemma and the radial expression for the geometric Laplacian give, for a smooth amplitude u,
(∂t​+Δx​)(gt​tju)=gt​[tj−1(r∂r​u+2r​∂r​logJu+ju)+tjΔx​u].
(11)
The t−2 terms cancel because ∣∇r∣=1. Define D=r∂r​+(r/2)∂r​logJ. The singular term disappears with u0​=J−1/2, normalized by u0​(y,y)=1. For j≥1, cancel the remaining powers of t by the heat-kernel transport equations
(D+j)uj​=−Δx​uj−1​,
(12)
which have the explicit solution
uj​(expy​ξ,y)=−J(y,ξ)−1/2∫01​sj−1J(y,sξ)1/2(Δx​uj−1​)(expy​(sξ),y)ds.
(13)
This formula is obtained by differentiating rjJ1/2uj​ along each radial geodesic. It proves smoothness jointly in x,y, including the diagonal, by induction: the integrand is smooth on a fixed compact integration interval. A homogeneous solution of (D+j)u=0 has rjJ1/2u constant along a ray, and smoothness at r=0 forces this constant to vanish for j>0. Thus the smooth transport coefficients are unique. In particular uj​(y,y)=−(Δx​uj−1​)(y,y)/j. Expanding J=1−Ricab​(y)ξaξb/6+O(∣ξ∣3) gives u1​(y,y)=R(y)/6, checking the sign convention.
Choose a smooth cutoff η(x,y) that is one near the diagonal and supported where these normal coordinates exist, and form the heat parametrix
PN​(t,x,y)=η(x,y)gt​(x,y)∑j=0N​tjuj​(x,y).
(14)
It tends to the identity kernel as t↓0: in normal coordinates the Gaussian is an approximate identity, and u0​J=J1/2→1 at the centre. Its residual RN​=(∂t​+Δx​)PN​ is ηgt​tNΔx​uN​ plus cutoff-derivative terms supported a positive distance from the diagonal. Those latter terms are smaller than every power of t. Hence RN​=O(tN−m/2) uniformly. Every prescribed finite number of derivatives has the same kind of estimate after increasing N; differentiating a Gaussian costs only finitely many powers of t.
For N large enough that the residual is continuous and bounded up to time zero, use the Volterra parametrix correction. For kernels define
(F∗G)(t,x,y)=∫0t​∫M​F(t−s,x,z)G(s,z,y)dVz​ds.
(15)
Set Q=−RN​+RN​∗RN​−RN​∗RN​∗RN​+⋯. A bounded residual with bound C gives
∣RN∗k​(t,x,y)∣≤Ckvol(M)k−1(k−1)!tk−1​,
(16)
so this series converges and Q+RN​+RN​∗Q=0. Because PN​ has identity initial kernel, differentiating the convolution gives (∂t​+Δx​)(PN​∗Q)=Q+RN​∗Q. Thus
H=PN​+PN​∗Q​
(17)
is an exact kernel with the correct initial condition. Taking N sufficiently large gives any required finite regularity; uniqueness of the heat equation makes the kernels obtained from different N equal. For uniqueness, the difference applied to smooth initial data has zero initial value, and the energy identity dtd​∥v∥22​=−2∥∇v∥22​ forces v=0. Parabolic regularity then gives smoothness for positive time.
The Gaussian part of PN​ has uniformly bounded spatial L1 norm, so the correction is O(tN+1−m/2). This proves, uniformly near the diagonal,
H(t,x,y)−gt​(x,y)∑j=0N​tjuj​(x,y)=O(tN+1−m/2),
(18)
with corresponding derivative estimates on increasing the truncation order. In particular on every parabolic neighbourhood r≤Bt​ it is the stated Gaussian expansion with relative remainder O(tN+1), and on the diagonal it yields the complete heat trace expansion. The standard off-diagonal Gaussian version on compact subsets of a sufficiently small convex normal neighbourhood follows from the same convolution estimates with the Gaussian factor retained: the inequality
t−sd(x,z)2​+sd(z,y)2​≥td(x,y)2​
(19)
localizes the convolution to the unique joining geodesic; its transverse Hessian is positive, and Gaussian integration gives the successive transport orders. The additive estimate above already specifies an appropriate uniform near-diagonal remainder without asserting a relative estimate far from the diagonal on the basis of a crude bound.
If M is noncompact, use the minimal Friedrichs extension heat semigroup; if there is a boundary, specify boundary conditions. The same construction is local in the interior. One may extend a relatively compact coordinate neighbourhood to a closed auxiliary manifold; localization errors are supported a positive distance away and are exponentially small near the smaller diagonal neighbourhood. Thus the local coefficients remain the same. Finite integrated heat invariants and a discrete spectrum require the global hypotheses used above.

The Sunada theorem is the following. Let X→M0​ be a finite normal Riemannian covering of a connected closed Riemannian manifold, with finite deck transformation group T. If U1​,U2​≤T satisfy Gassmann equivalence, meaning
∣U1​∩C∣=∣U2​∩C∣for every conjugacy class C of T,
(20)
then U1​\X and U2​\X have the same scalar Laplacian spectrum, with multiplicities. Equivalently, an epimorphism π1​(M0​)→T yields isospectral covers corresponding to the inverse images of U1​,U2​.
Here is the representation-theoretic proof. Every finite-dimensional Laplacian eigenspace Eλ​ of X is a group representation of T, since isometries commute with the Laplace-Beltrami operator. Eigenfunctions on Ui​\X identify with EλUi​​. Averaging gives the projection Pi​=∣Ui​∣−1∑u∈Ui​​u, so
dimEλUi​​=∣Ui​∣1​∑u∈Ui​​χλ​(u).
(21)
The two subgroup orders agree, and the character of a representation χλ​ is constant on conjugacy classes. The displayed multiplicities are therefore equal for every λ. This proof also applies when T has fixed points on X, provided the two subgroups Ui​ act freely, so their quotients are smooth manifolds. We shall use this extension for an orbifold base in Question 4.
The precise geometric test for an isometry uses the full isometry group of a common universal cover. Equip the simply connected cover X of M0​ with the lifted metric and let Γi​ be the subgroups of its deck transformation group corresponding to the two covers. Then
Γ1​\X≅Γ2​\X isometrically⟺FΓ1​F−1=Γ2​ for some F∈Isom(X).​
(22)
Indeed, such an F descends to an isometry of the quotients. Conversely an isometry of the quotients lifts to their simply connected covers; lift its inverse with compatible basepoints to see that the lift is bijective. Since it preserves the lifted metrics locally, it is a global isometry, and equivariance of the covering maps shows that it conjugates the deck subgroups. This proves the criterion, rather than assuming that an isometry must preserve the map to M0​. In particular, nonconjugacy inside T alone does not establish nonisometry; the full covering space may have additional isometries. Nonisomorphic fundamental groups are a sufficient topological obstruction, and length-identified geometric configurations provide another.
For a concrete nonisomorphic Gassmann equivalent regular subgroups example, fix an odd prime p and take T=Sp3​. Embed E=(Cp​)3 and B=UT3​(Fp​) through their regular permutation actions. Both have order p3. Every nonidentity element of E has order p. For B, write its elements as I+N with N3=0; then
(I+N)p=I+pN+(2p​)N2=I
(23)
over Fp​, including p=3, so its nonidentity elements also have order p. In a regular action, each nonidentity order-p element has cycle type pp2. Thus each subgroup meets the identity class in one element, the class of that cycle type in p3−1 elements, and every other conjugacy class in zero elements. They are Gassmann equivalent. They are not isomorphic: E is abelian, whereas the Heisenberg group B has [I+E12​,I+E23​]=I+E13​=I.
To obtain arbitrarily many examples in dimension five, choose r distinct odd primes p1​,…,pr​ with 2r≥n. For each prime use the preceding pair Ej​,Bj​≤Spj3​​. Set
T=∏j=1r​Spj3​​,Uε​=∏j=1r​Hj,εj​​,Hj,0​=Ej​, Hj,1​=Bj​,ε∈{0,1}r.
(24)
A conjugacy class in a direct product is a product of conjugacy classes, and its intersection count with Uε​ is the product of the individual counts. Thus all 2r subgroups are Gassmann equivalent. They are pairwise nonisomorphic: the unique Sylow pj​-subgroup of Uε​ is abelian precisely when εj​=0, and an abstract group isomorphism preserves this property at each prime.
We use the closed-manifold realization of a finitely presented fundamental group in dimension five. Its construction here is short. From a finite presentation of T with s generators, start with the connected sum of s copies of S1×S4. Its fundamental group is free on those generators. Represent the finitely many relators by disjoint embedded circles, which is possible by general position for curves in a manifold in dimension five. Their oriented normal bundles are trivial. Perform framed circle surgery, removing S1×D4 and inserting D2×S3. The Seifert-van Kampen theorem shows that each surgery kills exactly the normal closure of the corresponding relator: the attaching circle bounds the new disk, and the new S3 factor contributes no fundamental group. The resulting closed connected five-manifold M0​ has π1​(M0​)=T.
Choose any smooth Riemannian metric on M0​ and lift it to its universal cover X. Since T is finite, X is a compact simply connected five-manifold on which T acts freely and isometrically. The Sunada theorem makes all Uε​\X isospectral, while
π1​(Uε​\X)≅Uε​
(25)
shows that they are pairwise nonhomeomorphic, hence nonisometric. Taking any n members proves for every positive integer n, there are n pairwise nonisometric closed five-manifolds with the same Laplacian spectrum.

Put T=PSL(3,2)=GL(3,F2​), of order (8−1)(8−2)(8−4)=168. It acts on the seven points and seven lines of the Fano plane. Take U1​ to be a point stabilizer and U2​ to be a line stabilizer; both have index seven and order twenty-four. The fact that point and hyperplane stabilizers are almost conjugate follows by comparing fixed-point counts: for a matrix g, the numbers of fixed nonzero vectors and fixed nonzero covectors are both
2dimker(g−I)−1,
(26)
because g−I and its transpose have the same rank, and the dual action g−T has the same fixed space as gT. Over F2​, nonzero vectors represent points and nonzero covectors represent lines without any further scalar quotient. The two permutation characters therefore agree. The coset-character formula
χT/Ui​​(g)=∣Ui​∣∣CT​(g)∣​∣Ui​∩[g]T​∣
(27)
then proves Gassmann equivalence. They are not conjugate: a point stabilizer has orbits of sizes one and six on points and hence preserves no three-point line. Its conjugates are again point stabilizers, whereas U2​ preserves its line. The point and line stabilizers form the required nonconjugate Gassmann pair.
We construct a family using the Lambert quadrilateral construction of a cone torus. Let α=π/14. We use the elementary hyperbolic tri-rectangle fact that a quadrilateral with three right angles, remaining angle α, and side lengths x,y adjacent to the right-angle vertex opposite α exists uniquely for
sinhxsinhy=cosα,x,y>0.
(28)
Here x,y are the sides at the opposite right-angle corner, not the sides incident to α. Given any x>0, choose y=arsinh(cosα/sinhx). Reflect four copies around the opposite right-angle vertex. The result is a symmetric hyperbolic quadrilateral with all four corner angles α and opposite sides of equal lengths. Pair opposite sides by the axis translations. The quotient is an oriented torus with one cone point, whose total angle is 4α=2π/7. Its two central axis loops a,d are embedded closed geodesics, of lengths 2x,2y, intersecting once at right angles.
Cut this cone torus along a. The result is a hyperbolic pair of pants with two geodesic boundaries of length ℓ=2x and the cone point. Reglue those equal boundaries with a Fenchel–Nielsen twist τ∈R. This gives a two-parameter family Bℓ,τ​, with the same cone angle, and marked simple classes a,d meeting once. Near any zero-twist member their geodesic representatives remain embedded, transverse and disjoint from the cone point. The length of a remains ℓ, and the right-angle matrix product gives
cosh2ℓd​(ℓ,τ)​=coshy(ℓ)cosh2τ​.
(29)
For example, the zero-twist holonomies are diag(ex,e−x) and (coshysinhy​sinhycoshy​); multiply the latter by diag(eτ/2,e−τ/2) to obtain the twist formula. Their commutator has trace 2−4sinh2xsinh2y=−2cos(π/7), confirming exact elliptic order seven. The side-pairing construction supplies discreteness; the trace computation alone would not do so.
The orbifold fundamental group is
Γ=⟨a,d∣[d,a]7=1⟩.
(30)
Send a to the supplied generator A and d to D. Their commutator C=[D,A] has order seven, so this defines an epimorphism ρ:Γ→T. Its kernel avoids all nontrivial cone stabilizers, which are conjugates of powers of [d,a]; therefore it is torsion-free. It defines a smooth closed regular hyperbolic cover Xℓ,τ​→Bℓ,τ​ with deck group T. The subgroups Ui​, of order twenty-four, also avoid every order-seven cone stabilizer. Thus
Si​(ℓ,τ)=Ui​\Xℓ,τ​
(31)
are smooth closed seven-sheeted covers of the cone torus. The orbifold Euler characteristic is χorb​(B)=−(1−1/7)=−6/7, giving
χ(Si​)=7(−6/7)=−6,g(Si​)=4.​
(32)
Equivalently there is one preimage of the cone point, with local degree seven, and its lifted angle is 2π. The Sunada theorem, in the free-subgroup-action form proved above, makes S1​(ℓ,τ) and S2​(ℓ,τ) isospectral for every parameter.
Nonisometry requires further work. A cycle of length r in the monodromy permutation of a simple base geodesic gives a primitive simple lifted geodesic of length r times the base length. From the supplied A action, each cover has one lift of a of length ℓ and two of length 3ℓ. From the supplied D action, each has one lift of d of each length ℓd​,2ℓd​,4ℓd​. Let βi​ be the unique two-cycle lift of d, and let αi,1​,αi,2​ be the two three-cycle lifts of a. The fact that cycle intersections count intersections of lifted curves gives their intersection numbers: the seven crossings above the one base crossing are labelled by the sheets, so a crossing belongs to both lifted components exactly when its sheet lies in both cycles. Consequently
{i(α1,1​,β1​),i(α1,2​,β1​)}={0,1},{i(α2,1​,β2​),i(α2,2​,β2​)}={0,2}.
(33)
Indeed, in the first cover the two three-cycles have supports {1,2,5},{3,6,4} and the two-cycle has support {0,3}; in the second they have supports {1,4,3},{2,5,6} and {2,5}. The lifted curves intersect only over the single base crossing, so these are actual intersection counts of the geodesics, not merely abstract sheet-incidence numbers.
To make this a test against every isometry, we must identify these curves intrinsically, rather than require the isometry to respect the covering map. We use generic isolation of lifted simple geodesics, and give the needed genericity argument. Lengths of closed geodesics are real-analytic functions of the cone-torus length and twist parameters. For a primitive hyperbolic class w and an integer 1≤r≤7, consider the equalities
rℓw​(ℓ,τ)=3ℓorrℓw​(ℓ,τ)=2ℓd​(ℓ,τ).
(34)
Apart from w=a,r=3 or w=d,r=2, none is an identity on the full two-parameter family. To see this for the first equality, pinch the simple curve a while keeping the cone angle fixed. The collar lemma makes every class crossing a long. A class disjoint from a lies in the cut pair of pants; its limiting holonomy has positive translation length unless it is a power of one of the new cusp loops, which are conjugate to a in the torus group. Cone-peripheral classes are elliptic and do not represent closed geodesics. Thus the only primitive class with length tending to zero in this degeneration is a, up to inversion and conjugacy. This excludes an identity with a positive multiple of ℓ. Pinching d proves the corresponding assertion for ℓd​. The argument uses the usual hyperbolic collar and cusp degeneration facts, obtainable by the right-angle quadrilateral formulas; the cone angle 2π/7<π remains fixed. Analytic continuation on the connected length-twist parameter space shows that the same identities cannot hold on a small open neighbourhood of a zero-twist member.
There are countably many class equations, and each nontrivial real-analytic equation has a closed zero set with empty interior. The Baire category theorem therefore gives a parameter (ℓ0​,τ0​) in that small neighbourhood at which both target lengths are attained only by the prescribed lift components. This isolation persists on a smaller open neighbourhood. Indeed, near a fixed compact hyperbolic metric there are uniformly only finitely many primitive geodesic classes below a fixed length bound, by discreteness of the covering group and a compact fundamental region; nearby metrics are uniformly bilipschitz. Only finitely many classes can therefore approach the two fixed finite target lengths. Their strict inequalities persist under a sufficiently small parameter change.
Now keep τ=τ0​ and let ℓ vary in a sufficiently small interval I around ℓ0​. For each ℓ∈I, the only primitive geodesics of length 3ℓ in either cover are the two α components, and the only primitive geodesic of length 2ℓd​ is β. An isometry would have to preserve these length classes and their intersection-count multiset, contradicting {0,1}={0,2}. Hence
(S1​(ℓ,τ0​),S2​(ℓ,τ0​)),ℓ∈I,​
(35)
is an interval-parametrized family of isospectral nonisometric hyperbolic Riemann surfaces of genus four. Shrink I again so a small length window around 3ℓ0​ contains only those two components for every parameter. Their common length 3ℓ varies strictly, so members at distinct parameters cannot all be the same surface in different markings. The spectra agree within each pair; this construction does not assert a nontrivial continuously isospectral deformation of one hyperbolic surface.
