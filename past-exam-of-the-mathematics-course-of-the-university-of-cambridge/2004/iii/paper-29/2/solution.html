For r>0, let κr​ be the probability law of a seed displacement from its tree. For the literal circular support, it is normalized arclength on {u:∣u∣=r}. The conditional mean measure of a point process is
ΛΠ​(A)=μz∈Π∑​κr​(A−z).​
(10)
We prove the conditional Poisson assertion directly by joint probability generating functions.
Fix disjoint bounded measurable sets A1​,…,Ak​. For a tree at z, put qj​(z)=κr​(Aj​−z) and q0​(z)=1−∑j​qj​(z). Conditional on its seed count, the allocation among these sets and their complement is multinomial distribution. Averaging over the Poisson seed count gives
E[∏j=1k​sjNz​(Aj​)​​Π]=exp[μ(q0​(z)+∑j​qj​(z)sj​−1)]=∏j=1k​exp(μqj​(z)(sj​−1)).
(11)
Thus this tree contributes independent Poisson counts of means μqj​(z) to the disjoint sets. Different trees contribute independently. Only trees within distance r of the bounded union of the Aj​ can contribute, and there are almost surely finitely many of them. Multiplying their probability generating functions gives
E[∏j​sjΠ∗(Aj​)​​Π]=∏j​exp(ΛΠ​(Aj​)(sj​−1)).
(12)
This is precisely the joint probability generating function of independent Poisson counts with the displayed means. Also ΛΠ​ is locally finite, because each bounded set can receive seeds from only finitely many trees. This proves the conditional Poisson point process assertion and the Poisson offspring clusters directed by their parent process construction.
In arclength notation the directing measure is ΛΠ​=μ∑z∈Π​σz,r​/(2πr), where σz,r​ is arclength on the circle centered at z. If “circle” is used to mean the filled disc instead, take κr​(du)=1{∣u∣≤r}​du/(πr2); then
ΛΠ​(dx)=πr2μ​∑z∈Π​1{∣x−z∣≤r}​dx.
(13)
The generating-function proof and the following unconditional conclusion hold for either reading. The circular version has a singular conditional directing measure, giving the random directing measure of a Cox process formulation.
Unconditionally this is a Cox process, specifically a Poisson-offspring Neyman-Scott process. Its mean measure of a point process is nevertheless spatially uniform. With qA​(z)=κr​(A−z), the Campbell first-moment formula and translation invariance give
EΛΠ​(A)=λμ∫R2​qA​(z)dz=λμ∣A∣,
(14)
since ∫qA​(z)dz=∫κr​(du)∫1A​(z+u)dz=∣A∣. Uniform mean alone does not establish a Poisson law.
For a bounded set A of positive area, the conditional count has mean and variance ΛΠ​(A). The law of total variance gives
VarΠ∗(A)=EΛΠ​(A)+VarΛΠ​(A).
(15)
A Poisson integral of a deterministic function q has variance λ∫q2dz: for a simple function on disjoint sets this follows from independent Poisson counts, and square-integrable approximation gives the general formula. Here 0≤qA​≤1 and it has bounded support, so
VarΠ∗(A)=λμ∣A∣+λμ2∫R2​qA​(z)2dz>λμ∣A∣=EΠ∗(A).​
(16)
The strict inequality holds when λ,μ>0, because ∫qA​=∣A∣>0 implies ∫qA2​>0. A Poisson random variable has equal mean and variance. Hence the unconditional process is not a Poisson process in the nontrivial model. If λ=0 or μ=0, there are no seeds and the process is the degenerate empty Poisson process. The overdispersion of nondegenerate mixed Poisson counts quantifies the additional clustering induced by the random parents.
