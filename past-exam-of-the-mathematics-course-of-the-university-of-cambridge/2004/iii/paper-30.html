Let N be the zero-starting continuous local martingale, and let Vt​ be the total variation of its path on [0,t]. Stop when ∣N∣ or V reaches n, and also at time n. The stopped process is bounded, hence a true square-integrable martingale by the bounded local martingale criterion, and its total variation is bounded by n.
For a deterministic partition 0=t0​<⋯<tm​=t, martingale increments are orthogonal in L2, so
E(Nt∧τn​2​)=∑j​E(Ntj​∧τn​​−Ntj−1​∧τn​​)2.
(1)
The sum inside the expectation is at most the largest absolute increment times the stopped total variation. As the mesh tends to zero, path continuity makes the largest increment tend to zero. The product is bounded by 2n2, so the dominated convergence theorem makes the displayed expectation zero. Thus the stopped process vanishes at every fixed time. Use rational times, continuity and τn​↑∞ to conclude
Nt​=0 for all t≥0 outside one null set.​
(2)
This proves that a continuous finite-variation local martingale is constant.
Write a normalized semimartingale decomposition as X=X0​+N+A, with both N0​=A0​=0, N a continuous local martingale and A of finite variation. The difference of two such martingale parts is also the difference of two finite-variation parts, and hence vanishes by the result just proved. The other parts coincide too. The normalized continuous semimartingale decomposition is unique. Fixing the initial constants is necessary: without normalization a constant can be transferred between the two parts.

For each fixed horizon t, the stipulated property is the Class D process condition on [0,t], and imposing it for every finite horizon gives a Class DL process.
First suppose M is a true martingale. For every stopping time T≤t, the bounded-time optional sampling theorem gives
MT​=E(Mt​∣FT​).
(6)
The conditional expectations of one integrable variable form a uniformly integrable family. To see the uniformity explicitly, put V=E(U∣H). For any r,K>0, conditional Jensen and Markov inequalities give
E[∣V∣1{∣V∣>K}​]≤E[∣U∣1{∣U∣>r}​]+KrE∣U∣​.
(7)
Choose r large and then K large. The bound does not depend on the conditioning sigma-algebra. This proves the required uniform integrability. Bounded-time optional sampling here can also be justified directly: approximate T from above by finite-grid stopping times, use discrete optional sampling with terminal value Mt​, and use this same conditional-expectation uniform integrability and right continuity to pass to the limit.
Conversely, take a localizing sequence τn​ for the local martingale. For fixed s≤t, both families Mt∧τn​​ and Ms∧τn​​ are uniformly integrable, because their times are bounded by t. They converge almost surely to Mt​ and Ms​, and therefore converge in L1. The stopped martingale identity
E(Mt∧τn​​∣Fs​)=Ms∧τn​​
(8)
passes to the limit by L1 contractivity of conditional expectation. Thus E(Mt​∣Fs​)=Ms​, and all time values are integrable. Hence a local martingale is a true martingale exactly when it is of Class DL. This is a finite-horizon criterion, not a claim of uniform integrability over all times simultaneously.

For continuous semimartingales U,V, the semimartingale integration by parts formula is
Ut​Vt​=U0​V0​+∫0t​Us​dVs​+∫0t​Vs​dUs​+[U,V]t​.​
(9)
Indeed each partition increment satisfies
Δ(UV)=Uleft​ΔV+Vleft​ΔU+ΔUΔV.
(10)
Summing telescopes the left side. The first two sums converge to the stochastic integrals by left-endpoint approximation, and the last sum converges to the quadratic covariation by polarization of the quadratic-increment formula. All limits are in uniform convergence on compacts in probability. Hence the equality holds at every time outside one null set, using continuity. In differential notation it is the Itô product rule d(UV)=UdV+VdU+d[U,V].
For a vector of continuous semimartingales X=(X1,…,Xd) and F∈C1,2, the multidimensional Itô formula is
F(t,Xt​)=F(0,X0​)​+∫0t​∂s​F(s,Xs​)ds+i∑​∫0t​∂i​F(s,Xs​)dXsi​+21​i,j∑​∫0t​∂ij​F(s,Xs​)d[Xi,Xj]s​.​​
(11)
One route from the product formula is to apply it repeatedly to powers and products of the coordinates, obtaining this expression for polynomials; polarization identifies the mixed second derivatives. Time is another coordinate of finite variation, so it has no quadratic-covariation contribution. Localize to compact ranges, approximate a C1,2 function and its indicated derivatives by smooth polynomial approximations, and pass to the limit using stochastic-integral continuity and the variation bounds on the finite-variation terms. Equivalently, second-order Taylor expansion along partitions gives the same limit, with integration by parts accounting for every quadratic term.
