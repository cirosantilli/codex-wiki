Take c=1 and normalize the Big Bang to t=0. The cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation for pressureless matter makes C=8πGρm​a3/3 a positive constant. The flat Friedmann equation consequently reads
a˙2=aC​+3Λa2​.
(1)

a(t)=⎩⎨⎧​(3C/Λ)1/3sinh2/3(3Λ​t/2),(9C/4)1/3t2/3,(3C/∣Λ∣)1/3sin2/3(3∣Λ∣​t/2),​Λ>0,Λ=0,Λ<0.​​

First fix a genuine normalization error in the original PDF: the Gaussian exponent uses σ2, so σ must be the standard deviation, the variance is σ2, and the normalized probability density function is
pM​(δ)=2π​σ(M,z)1​exp(−2σ2(M,z)δ2​).
(20)
The printed prefactor has only σ under its square root; its integral is σ​ rather than one. Use the normalized normal distribution above.
The one-scale probability of exceeding the linear spherical-collapse threshold is PM​=21​erfc[δc​/(2​σ)]. In the Press-Schechter formalism, the mass fraction above M is assigned to
F(>M,z)=2PM​=erfc(2​σ(M,z)δc​​).
(21)
The factor two is necessary to recover all the mass in the hierarchical limit σ→∞: the uncorrected one-point upper tail tends to only one half. A precise justification is available in the excursion-set description of halo formation with a sharp-k smoothing filter. The smoothed Gaussian random field then becomes Brownian motion in its variance S. Reflecting a trajectory after its first crossing of δc​ pairs trajectories which crossed but end below the threshold with those which end above it. The Brownian reflection principle therefore gives Pr(maxs≤S​δ(s)>δc​)=2Pr(δ(S)>δc​).
This multiscale mass assignment is an extra modelling prescription: a Gaussian one-point probability and a collapse threshold alone do not mathematically determine a halo mass function. The “all matter” normalization additionally assumes arbitrarily large small-scale variance; a finite variance cutoff leaves an uncollapsed fraction.
Differentiate at fixed z and fixed δc​:
∂M∂F​=π2​​σ2δc​​exp(−2σ2δc2​​)∂M∂σ​.
(22)
For decreasing σ(M,z), a mass interval contains the positive fraction −F′(M)dM. Its comoving mass is ρm,0​[−F′(M)]dM, so division by halo mass gives the Press-Schechter halo mass function:

The density is comoving, so the prefactor uses today's matter mass density, rather than the physical density at redshift z.

Write n=n0​+yLn0​+O(y2), with n0​=(ex−1)−1 and L=x−2∂x​(x4∂x​). Differentiating the Planck photon distribution gives
n0′​=−(ex−1)2ex​,Ln0​=(ex−1)2xex​[xex−1ex+1​−4].
(27)
Dividing by n0​ proves the first-order thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect:
n0​Δn​=yex−1xex​[xcoth(x/2)−4].​
(28)
In the Rayleigh-Jeans law limit, xex/(ex−1)→1 and xcoth(x/2)→2, so Δn/n0​→−2y. In the Wien approximation, the prefactor approaches x and the bracket approaches x−4, so Δn/n0​∼yx(x−4)∼yx2. The last limit is an asymptotic description of the first-order coefficient: a perturbative prediction at large x still requires yx2≪1.
Hot electrons transfer energy to the cosmic microwave background, removing low-frequency photons and enhancing the high-frequency tail. The first-order distortion crosses zero where xcoth(x/2)=4, at x≃3.830; for today's CMB this is near 217GHz. Photon conservation follows directly from the boundary term
∫0∞​x2Δndx=y[x4n0′​]0∞​=0.
(29)
In contrast, integration by parts gives ∫x3Δndx=4y∫x3n0​dx, so radiation gains a fraction 4y of its original energy. The thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect is thus a spectral distortion, not simply a new blackbody temperature.
A galaxy cluster with bulk peculiar velocity along the line of sight additionally produces the kinetic Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect. Define v∥​>0 for recession and τ=∫ne​σT​dℓ. To first order in velocity and optical depth, ΔT/T=−τv∥​/c: receding gas gives a decrement and approaching gas an increment. Its occupation number perturbation is (ΔT/T)xex/(ex−1)2, the spectrum of a small blackbody temperature change. It remains nonzero at the thermal null and therefore complicates measurements near that frequency.

Real galaxy clusters are neither uniform nor generally spherical. The inferred depth depends on the ratio of line of sight depth to transverse extent; elongation and orientation selection therefore bias the distance. density clumps enhance X-ray emission, which weights ne2​, more than the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect, which weights ne​. An unmodelled gas clumping factor greater than one makes the inferred distance too small and the inferred Hubble constant too large in this simple model. Temperature gradients and multiple thermal components bias the X-ray spectral temperature relative to the pressure-weighted Compton y parameter. Metals alter the cooling spectrum, and finite X-ray bands require a spectral and redshift correction to the bolometric approximation. Relativistic thermal corrections, the kinetic Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect, radio sources and primary Cosmic microwave background anisotropy contaminate the signal. Finally, calibration, angular-profile fitting and low-redshift peculiar velocities affect the observables and the relation between measured redshift and expansion distance.

A useful description of the low-column Lyman-alpha forest at z≃3 is a power law fX​(N,z)=A(z)N−β, with β around 1.4–1.5 over the ordinary unsaturated forest range. The same column dependence holds for fz​ at a fixed redshift. It is not a universal unbroken power law: saturation, increased neutral fraction and changing absorber populations produce breaks. Lyman limit systems become optically thick at N≃1017.2cm−2 because Nσ912​≃1 with σ912​≃6.3×10−18cm2. Damped Lyman-alpha systems have N≳2×1020cm−2 and strong radiative damping wings. They are rare in number but contain substantial neutral mass. The highest-column distribution steepens or cuts off rather than extending indefinitely.
This sketch shows representative shapes and transition scales, not an observational fit; its ordinate is distribution per unit column, not counts per logarithmic bin. In an Einstein-de Sitter universe, H=H0​(1+z)3/2, so a fixed comoving population with fixed physical cross-section above a fixed column threshold has dN/dz∝(1+z)1/2. Thus
(dN/dz)z=1.5​(dN/dz)z=4​​=(2.55​)1/2=2​≃1.414.​
(38)
The observed redshift evolution of the Lyman-alpha forest is much stronger for commonly selected forest lines. For example, Kim, Cristiani and D'Odorico's absorption survey finds an index about 2.19 in dN/dz∝(1+z)γ for 1013.64<N/cm−2<1016 over 1.5<z<4, corresponding to an increase by approximately 22.19≃4.56. This number is selection-dependent. Expansion changes the gas density, and the evolving cosmic ionizing background changes its neutral fraction through photoionization equilibrium, moving systems across a fixed neutral-column threshold. Structure also evolves. A non-evolving population with fixed physical cross-section is therefore not an adequate physical model of the observed forest.

No measured normalization of the neutral-hydrogen column-density distribution, ionizing field or gas temperature is supplied, so the paper's formulas do not determine numerical densities on their own. With observational data and suitable ionization corrections, the high-redshift Lyman-alpha forest gives a total gas density of order a few 10−2 relative to today's critical density, comparable to the cosmological baryon density Ωb​∼0.04–0.05 for H0​∼70kms−1Mpc−1. The conclusion is that a large fraction of baryons at z≃3 reside in diffuse ionized gas, rather than that every baryon has been individually accounted for. Schaye's analysis and Kim and collaborators' survey support that interpretation under their stated gas and ionizing-background assumptions.
The neutral-gas reservoir traced predominantly by damped Lyman-alpha systems is much smaller, of order 10−3 in the same convention. It is broadly comparable in order of magnitude to today's luminous stellar matter, whose cosmic stellar mass density is a few 10−3, but exact comparisons depend on cosmology, helium correction and which stellar components are counted. Storrie-Lombardi, McMahon and Irwin's neutral-gas census finds especially close comparison with present stellar disks. Most high-redshift gas is ionized; the smaller neutral reservoir has a mass of roughly the same order as today's visible stellar population. This comparison does not imply that the measured neutral reservoir alone simply becomes all present-day stars: gas accretion, recycling, ionization changes and outflows continue during galaxy evolution.
The neutral gas density from an absorption distribution is comparatively robust for high columns where the hydrogen is mostly neutral, but saturated lines, dust-obscured quasars, incomplete surveys and small numbers of rare high-column systems affect the integral. The ionized gas density from an absorption distribution has additional systematic uncertainty in the cosmic ionizing background, temperature, absorber depth, helium treatment and small-scale density inhomogeneity. At low columns, line blending and incompleteness matter; at high columns, attenuation makes an optically thin ionization correction invalid. A single power law extrapolated outside the measured column range can also produce a misleading mass integral. Hot gas, molecular gas and gas missed by the selected absorption population are not included automatically. These are observational and modelling qualifications to a baryon census, not failures of the column-mass accounting itself.
