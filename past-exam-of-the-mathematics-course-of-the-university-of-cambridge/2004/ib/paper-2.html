We prove Simplicity of the alternating group A5 by finding all its conjugacy classes. The alternating group has group order ∣A5​∣=5!/2=60. Its even permutations have cycle types 1, (abc), (ab)(cd) and (abcde). There are (35​)⋅2=20 three-cycles, 5⋅3=15 products of disjoint transpositions, and 4!=24 five-cycles.
For a three-cycle, a commuting permutation must rotate its three-element support and may interchange the two fixed points. Its centralizer in S5​ has order six, and only the three rotations are even. Thus its conjugacy class in A5​ has size 60/3=20 and contains every three-cycle. The centralizer of (12)(34) in S5​ has order eight: one may interchange the two pairs and independently interchange the elements within each pair. It contains the odd element (12), so exactly half of these commuting elements are even. The conjugacy class in A5​ consequently has size 60/4=15. A permutation commuting with a five-cycle is determined by the image of one point and must be a power of that cycle. All five powers are even, so each such conjugacy class has size 60/5=12. The 24 five-cycles form exactly two classes.
A normal subgroup contains the identity and is a union of whole conjugacy classes. Its possible orders therefore lie in
1+{0,12,24}+{0,15}+{0,20}={1,13,16,21,25,28,33,36,40,45,48,60}.
(2)
By Lagrange's theorem, its order must divide 60. Only 1 and 60 in this list do so. Hence A5​ has no proper nontrivial normal subgroup and is a simple group.

Any two nonempty open sets in the cofinite topology on N intersect: their complements have finite union, which cannot exhaust N. Thus this is a hyperconnected space. Suppose a continuous map f took two distinct values a and b. The usual real line is a Hausdorff space, so there are disjoint open intervals U and V containing a and b. ∇⋅B=0,∇×B=μ0​J.
(9)

For the first sampling design, treat the fixed groups of 60 and 90 people as two independent, representative samples from the respective populations. Within each sample, assume independent individuals with common eye-colour probabilities and mutually exclusive, exhaustive categories. This gives two independent multinomial distributions. The null hypothesis is that the two populations have the same eye-colour probabilities; its estimated common probabilities are 40/150, 70/150 and 40/150.
The expected counts under the null hypothesis are
E=(1624​2842​1624​).
(18)
The Pearson chi-squared test of homogeneity uses
T=∑i,j​Eij​(Oij​−Eij​)2​=21616​+2864​+22416​+4264​=750​≃7.143.
(19)
There are 2(3−1)=4 unrestricted category parameters and 3−1=2 under the null hypothesis, so the asymptotic chi-squared distribution has two degrees of freedom. All expected counts exceed five, making the usual large-sample approximation reasonable. Reject equality at the 5% level, but not at the 1% level: 5.99<T<9.21. The approximate p-value is P(χ22​≥T)=e−25/7≃0.0281. Independence and representative sampling, rather than merely the count sizes, are essential to population inference; the result does not establish a causal explanation.
In the second design, the total sample size is fixed but both row and column classifications are observed for each sampled person. Assume an independent, representative sample with a common joint distribution of handedness and eye colour. The six cell counts then have one multinomial distribution. The null hypothesis is independence of the two classifications, so joint probabilities factor into row and column probabilities. Their maximum-likelihood estimates are the observed marginal proportions, again giving exactly E above.
The unrestricted joint distribution has five free parameters; independence leaves (2−1)+(3−1)=3. The Pearson chi-squared test of independence again has two degrees of freedom, the same statistic, and the same significance conclusion. The numerical analysis is unchanged, but the sampling model changes from two fixed-row samples to one joint sample. If handedness groups had instead been deliberately sampled with fixed row sizes, the first, homogeneity formulation would apply to those groups too.

For a Markov chain started in its stationary distribution, reversibility means that for every n, the joint distribution of (X0​,…,Xn​) equals that of (Xn​,…,X0​). More generally, stationarity then makes every finite segment invariant under reversal of its time order.
If the Markov chain is reversible, take n=1. Comparing the events (X0​,X1​)=(i,j) and (j,i) gives the detailed balance equations
πi​Pij​=πj​Pji​(i,j∈S).​
(20)
Conversely, an irreducible Markov chain with an invariant probability distribution has πi​>0 for every state. Under detailed balance, a transition is positive exactly when its reverse is positive. For a path with positive probability, substitute Pij​=πj​Pji​/πi​ in each factor to obtain
πi0​​∏r=0n−1​Pir​ir+1​​=πin​​∏r=0n−1​Pir+1​ir​​.
(21)
The factors of π telescope. If a path has a zero transition, its reverse does too, so the equality still holds. These are the respective forward and reversed path probabilities. Summing over any collection of paths proves equality of the joint distributions, hence a reversible Markov chain. The first factor in the PDF's balance equation is πi​; the converted TeX incorrectly changes it to πj​.

Suppose K=kerφ for a group homomorphism φ:G→H. Its identity belongs to K, and for k,l∈K we have φ(kl−1)=1, so it is a subgroup. Moreover, for g∈G and k∈K,
φ(gkg−1)=φ(g)φ(k)φ(g)−1=1.
(26)
Hence gKg−1⊆K; applying this also to g−1 gives equality, so K is a normal subgroup.
Conversely, if K is a normal subgroup, multiplication of cosets in the quotient group G/K is well-defined. The map g↦gK is a group homomorphism and has kernel of a group homomorphism exactly K. This proves both directions without requiring φ to be injective or surjective.
For the matrix application, reduce all entries modulo p:
ρ:SL2​(Z)⟶SL2​(Fp​),A⟼A.
(27)
Reduction respects matrix multiplication and the determinant, so this is a group homomorphism. Its kernel of a group homomorphism consists exactly of matrices congruent to the identity modulo p, which are the given matrices. Therefore K=kerρ is a normal subgroup, the level-p principal congruence subgroup. Surjectivity of reduction is not needed for this argument.

The complete metric space R supplies a counterexample: An​=[n,∞) are descending nonempty closed sets, but no real number belongs to them all.
Now assume shrinking diameters. Choose xn​∈An​. Given ε>0, choose N so that diam(AN​)<ε. Whenever m,n≥N, both chosen points lie in AN​, so d(xm​,xn​)<ε. Thus (xn​) is a Cauchy sequence. For a sequence ϵ, let Cn​(ϵ) be its prefix cylinder set, consisting of sequences agreeing with ϵ in the first n coordinates. The specified collection consists exactly of unions of such cylinder sets.
The empty set meets the defining condition vacuously, and X meets it because every cylinder set is contained in X. For a union ⋃λ​Yλ​, a point lies in one Yλ​ and has a prefix cylinder set contained in that set, hence contained in the union. For a finite intersection, a point has a cylinder neighbourhood in every constituent. Taking the largest of the finitely many prefix lengths gives one cylinder set contained in all of them. Therefore the collection contains the empty and whole sets, is closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections, and is a topology on X, the prefix topology of Cantor space.

This argument concerns eigenvalues and does not assert a strictly positive bound on an arbitrary continuous spectrum.

A simple hypothesis specifies one probability distribution. Let the null hypothesis and alternative have probability density functions f0​,f1​ with respect to a common measure. A possibly randomized statistical test is a measurable function φ(x)∈[0,1], its conditional probability of rejecting the null hypothesis. Its size of a statistical test is E0​φ, and its statistical power at the alternative is E1​φ. A most powerful test at level α maximizes power among tests with size at most α.
The Neyman-Pearson lemma says to choose k≥0 and a test φ∗​ of size α with φ∗​=1 where f1​>kf0​ and φ∗​=0 where f1​<kf0​, choosing values on the equality set to reach the specified size. Equivalently, threshold the likelihood ratio, treating positive alternative density with zero null density as an infinite ratio. Randomization on a threshold atom ensures exact size when needed. If k=0, all remaining null-only mass can be used to reach size without changing power.
For any test φ of size at most α, the signs of the two factors give
(φ∗​−φ)(f1​−kf0​)≥0
(50)
pointwise. Integrating proves
E1​φ∗​−E1​φ≥k(E0​φ∗​−E0​φ)≥0.
(51)
This is the required optimality proof. If k>0 and another test is equally powerful, both inequalities must be equalities. It must have size α and agree with φ∗​ wherever f1​=kf0​, apart from null sets for the relevant densities. Thus freedom is confined to the equality set and sets null under both hypotheses; at k=0, additional size need not improve power. This also explains the nonuniqueness below.
For the unit-scale Laplace distribution, put δ=θ1​−θ0​>0. Its likelihood ratio is
L(x)=f(x∣θ0​)f(x∣θ1​)​=⎩⎨⎧​e−δ,e2x−θ0​−θ1​,eδ,​x≤θ0​,θ0​<x<θ1​,x≥θ1​.​
(52)
It is nondecreasing, so an upper-tail critical region has the Neyman-Pearson lemma form, even if the cutoff lies inside a constant-ratio tail. Solving Pθ0​​(X>cα​)=α gives the most powerful test for a Laplace location shift,
reject H0​ when X>cα​,cα​={θ0​−log(2α),θ0​+log(2(1−α)),​0<α≤1/2,1/2<α<1.​​
(53)
Continuity of the distribution makes this nonrandomized test exactly size α. It need not be the unique most powerful test: within a flat extreme likelihood ratio region, other selections having the same null probability are equally good.
Its statistical power is
β(θ1​)={21​eθ1​−cα​,1−21​ecα​−θ1​,​cα​≥θ1​,cα​<θ1​.​
(54)
Here β denotes power, not the probability of a type-II error. For α=0.05, cα​=θ0​+log10. Thus the power is 0.05eδ for δ≤log10, and 1−5e−δ otherwise. The first branch is at most 1/2. On the second branch, power at least 0.95 is equivalent to 5e−δ≤0.05. Therefore the required pairs are exactly θ1​−θ0​≥log100, with no restriction on the common location.

Read the arrows in the original diagram as follows: 0 moves to 1′ with probability one; from an unprimed n≥1, probability q leads to n−1 and probability p leads to (n+1)′; from n′ the corresponding destinations are n and (n+1)′. This is the two-lane Markov chain with a deterministic restart. It is irreducible: every state can reach zero by a finite path of q transitions, and zero can reach every primed state by outward p transitions and then every unprimed state by a q transition. Thus all states have the same recurrence classification.
Let an​ and bn​ be the hitting probabilities of zero starting from n and n′, with a0​=1. First-step conditioning gives
an​=qan−1​+pbn+1​,bn​=qan​+pbn+1​(n≥1).
(55)
Subtracting yields bn​=(1+q)an​−qan−1​. Eliminating bn+1​ gives
p(1+q)an+1​−(1+pq)an​+qan−1​=0.
(56)
Its characteristic roots are 1 and r=q/[p(1+q)]=q/(1−q2). Put qc​=(5​−1)/2, so r=1 precisely when q=qc​.
For q>qc​, the general solution is an​=A+Brn. The bound 0≤an​≤1 and r>1 force B=0, and a0​=1 then gives an​=bn​=1. For q=qc​, the general solution is an​=A+Bn; boundedness again gives an​=bn​=1. In both cases, the return probability from zero is b1​=1, so the Markov chain is recurrent.
For q<qc​, 0<r<1, and the pair
an​=rn,bn​=q(1+q)rn
(57)
is a nonnegative bounded solution of the first-step equations. To identify it as the actual hitting probability, use that hitting probability is the minimal nonnegative harmonic extension. This fact follows directly by iteration: a nonnegative solution with boundary value one at zero dominates the probability of hitting zero within N steps, for every N. The actual bounded solution has form an​=A+(1−A)rn, where nonnegativity as n→∞ requires A≥0. Minimality bounds it above by rn, while this form bounds it below by rn. Hence it is exactly the displayed solution. Starting at zero, the first return time has finite-return probability b1​=q2/p<1. The Markov chain is therefore transient.
It remains to distinguish positive and null recurrence. Write un​=πn​ and vn​=πn′​ for invariant probabilities. Balance at zero and at 1′ gives π0​=qu1​ and v1​=π0​. For the finite cut consisting of zero and both lanes up to level n, invariance equates its outward and inward probability fluxes:
p(un​+vn​)=qun+1​.
(58)
Balance at the unprimed state n also gives un​=q(un+1​+vn​). Combining these identities yields
vn​=qun​,un+1​=tun​,t=qp(1+q)​=q1−q2​.
(59)
Therefore every invariant probability distribution would necessarily have un​=(π0​/q)tn−1 and vn​=π0​tn−1. Conversely, these weights satisfy all individual balance equations, including vn+1​=p(un​+vn​), so their summability is the only remaining condition.
When q>qc​, t<1, and normalization gives
π0​=q(q+2)q2+q−1​,πn​=qπ0​​(q1−q2​)n−1,πn′​=π0​(q1−q2​)n−1(n≥1).​
(60)
An irreducible Markov chain has an invariant probability distribution exactly when it is positive recurrent; equivalently its mean return time is finite and π0​ is its reciprocal. Thus this case is positive recurrent. At q=qc​, t=1 and the nonzero invariant weights cannot be summed to one. Irreducibility would force π0​>0 in any invariant probability distribution. Since recurrence was already proved but no such distribution exists, this case is null recurrent.
The complete classification is therefore transient for 0<q<qc​, null recurrent at q=qc​, and positive recurrent for qc​<q<1. A drift argument using only the unprimed arrows would miss the primed lane and give the wrong threshold.
