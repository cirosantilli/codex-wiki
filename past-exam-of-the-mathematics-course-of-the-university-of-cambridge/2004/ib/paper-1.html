The normalizer is NG​(H)={g∈G:gHg−1=H}. It is a subgroup: the identity normalizes H, products of normalizing elements normalize it, and conjugating the equality by g−1 proves inverse closure. The map

(2)

By the permitted conjugacy assertion for Sylow subgroups, the number np​ of Sylow p-subgroups equals [G:NG​(P)] for one such subgroup P. Lagrange's theorem makes this divide ∣G∣, and P⊆NG​(P) also makes it divide ∣G∣/∣P∣.
For order 72=8⋅9, a Sylow 3-subgroup has order nine, so n3​ divides eight. To obtain the required congruence without assuming an additional Sylow counting theorem, let P act by conjugation on the set of Sylow 3-subgroups. Its orbit sizes are powers of three. A fixed subgroup Q is normalized by P, so PQ is a subgroup and its order ∣P∣∣Q∣/∣P∩Q∣ is a power of three. Since nine is the largest power of three dividing 72, this forces PQ=P=Q. 6B OurBigBook.com
In three dimensions, the general rank-two isotropic tensor is aδij​ and the general rank-three tensor invariant under proper rotations is bϵijk​. Here δ is the Kronecker delta and ϵ the Levi-Civita symbol. The latter is orientation-sensitive: an ordinary third-rank tensor invariant under all orthogonal transformations is zero instead. This distinction does not change the stress conclusion.
For an isotropic material, the constitutive tensor must consequently have Aijk​=bϵijk​ in the proper-rotation convention. Hence σij​=bϵijk​Bk​=−σji​. Mechanical stress in the model is also symmetric, so σij​=−σij​ and every component vanishes. Thus nonzero stress linear in the field requires anisotropic material coefficients. This is the isotropic linear magnetostriction vanishes restriction, not a claim that all higher-order magnetostriction is impossible in isotropic materials.

Assume independent samples X1​,…,Xn​ and Y1​,…,Ym​, each consisting of independent normal random variables, with respective means μ1​,μ2​ and a common unknown variance σ2>0. Take n,m≥2. Normality, independence and equal variances are the assumptions giving the exact pooled Student's t-test; unequal variances would require a different test. We test H0​:μ1​=μ2​ against an unrestricted difference.
Put N=n+m and
W=∑i​(Xi​−Xˉ)2+∑j​(Yj​−Yˉ)2,B=Nnm​(Xˉ−Yˉ)2.
(18)
The normal likelihood is proportional to (σ2)−N/2exp[−S/(2σ2)], where S is the residual sum of squares. Under the unrestricted model, the maximizing means are Xˉ,Yˉ and S=W. Under H0​, the maximizing common mean is (nXˉ+mYˉ)/N and decomposition about the sample means gives S=W+B. For either model, maximizing over variance gives σ2=S/N. Thus the generalized likelihood-ratio test statistic is
Λ=(W+BW​)N/2=(1+N−2T2​)−N/2,T=sp​1/n+1/m​Xˉ−Yˉ​,sp2​=N−2W​.
(19)
Small Λ is therefore equivalent to large ∣T∣.
Under the null, Z=(Xˉ−Yˉ)/[σ1/n+1/m​] is standard normal. In each sample an orthogonal change of Gaussian coordinates separates the sample-mean coordinate from the centered residual coordinates; those coordinates are independent. Consequently V=W/σ2 has a chi-squared distribution with N−2 degrees of freedom and is independent of Z. Therefore T=Z/V/(N−2)​ has the Student's t-distribution with N−2 degrees of freedom. 11H OurBigBook.com
Irreducibility and the presence of another state imply Pii​<1: otherwise i would be absorbing and could not reach that other state. Hence the proposed matrix has nonnegative entries and row sums ∑j=i​Pij​/(1−Pii​)=1.
Observe the original Markov chain only at times when it changes state. Starting from i, the next different state is j=i with probability
∑r=0∞​Piir​Pij​=1−Pii​Pij​​.
(20)
Each holding run has a finite geometric length almost surely. The Strong Markov property at the successive change times therefore identifies the observed process as the discrete-time jump chain with transition matrix P.
Any positive-probability path of the original chain gives a path of this new chain after repeated consecutive states are deleted. Every remaining edge has positive P probability, so P is irreducible. For recurrence, starting at any state i, repeated application of the Markov property at return times shows that the original recurrent chain visits i infinitely often almost surely. A single sojourn cannot account for infinitely many visits, because every sojourn is finite almost surely. There must therefore be infinitely many separate visits to i in the observed chain too. Thus P is recurrent, proving that recurrence survives deletion of self-transitions.

The Fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups states that every such group is isomorphic to
Zr⊕Z/d1​Z⊕⋯⊕Z/ds​Z,1<d1​∣d2​∣⋯∣ds​,

In the upper half-plane model, a hyperbolic line is either a vertical line or a Euclidean semicircle perpendicular to the real axis. For the vertical line x=a, set RL​(z)=2a−zˉ. For the circle ∣z−a∣=r with real a, set
RL​(z)=a+zˉ−ar2​.​
(25)
The vertical formula preserves y and Euclidean speed. For the circular formula, ImRL​(z)=r2Imz/∣z−a∣2, while its local Euclidean length multiplier is r2/∣z−a∣2. Thus both preserve ∣dz∣/Imz, the hyperbolic metric, and consequently all curve lengths and distances. Each is an involution preserving the half-plane; each fixes its specified line pointwise and moves points off that line. This establishes the required hyperbolic reflection explicitly.
To factor an arbitrary isometry g, first recall why a bisector reflection exchanges any distinct points P,Q. The hyperbolic distance formula is
coshd(x+iy,a+ib)=2yb(x−a)2+y2+b2​.
(26)
Equating the distances to P and Q yields a vertical line or a circle with centre on the real axis, hence a hyperbolic line. Substitution in the reflection formulas above shows that its reflection exchanges P and Q. Choose a base point P. If gP=P, compose g on the left with their bisector reflection, obtaining h that fixes P; otherwise take h=g.
Choose Q=P. The points hQ,Q have the same distance from P, so their bisector contains P. If they differ, its reflection corrects hQ while retaining P. We obtain an isometry k fixing both P,Q after at most two reflections.
An isometry fixing two points is either the identity or reflection in their joining line. To see this directly, a point with prescribed distances from P,Q lies at the intersection of two hyperbolic circles; these are Euclidean circles in this model and have at most two intersections. When there are two, reflection in the line PQ interchanges them. Pick a third point off that line. Either k fixes it, or composing k with that line reflection fixes it. An isometry fixing three noncollinear points is the identity: the first two distances leave only the reflected pair, and the distance to the third point distinguishes that pair. Thus k is the identity or one additional reflection. Reversing the compositions proves every isometry is a product of at most three hyperbolic reflections.

W=2ϵ0​AV2​(H+a1​+H−a1​)=H2−a2ϵ0​AV2H​.​
(36)
The two faces of the middle plate carry charges Q−​=ϵ0​AV/(H+a) and Q+​=ϵ0​AV/(H−a). Its total charge is their sum; the grounded plates have zero potential and contribute zero to 21​∑Qj​ϕj​. The charge-potential expression therefore gives W=21​V(Q−​+Q+​), exactly the same result. Since H2−a2 is largest at a=0, the energy at fixed V is minimized when the middle plate is centered, with value ϵ0​AV2/H.
This is the electrostatic energy of a three-plate capacitor in the intended uniform-field model. Finite circular plates have fringing corrections; no small-gap-to-radius ratio is explicitly stated in the source, so the simple formula should not be described as an exact finite-disc solution.

The Rao-Blackwell theorem states that if an estimator D has finite second moment and T is a sufficient statistic, then D∗(T)=E[D∣T] can be chosen without knowing the parameter. It has the same expectation as D and no larger variance. In particular an unbiased estimator remains unbiased and its squared-error risk cannot increase. Sufficiency makes the conditional distribution, hence the function of T used here, parameter-independent.
The tower property gives ED∗=ED. The law of total variance gives
Var(D)=Var(E[D∣T])+E[Var(D∣T)],
(48)
so the variance decreases, with equality exactly when D is already a function of T almost surely. Since the bias is unchanged, the same identity proves the squared-error risk claim. More generally the corresponding convex-loss inequality follows from conditional Jensen's inequality.
For the given geometric distribution, the joint mass is (1−p)np∑xj​−n on positive-integer sample vectors. Thus T=∑j​Xj​ is a one-dimensional sufficient statistic. Directly, conditional on T=t, every positive-integer composition of t into n parts has the same probability; there are (n−1t−1​) such compositions, independently of p.
The simple estimator D=1{X1​>1}​ is unbiased for p, since Pr(X1​>1)=p. For n≥2, compositions with X1​>1 are counted by subtracting one from their first part, giving (n−1t−2​) when t>n. Consequently the Rao-Blackwell estimator of a geometric failure probability is
p​=E[D∣T]=T−1T−n​,n≥2.​
(49)
At T=n its value is zero, as it must be when all observations are one. For the possible one-observation case, use p​=1{T>1}​; the displayed fraction would otherwise be undefined at T=1. The conditional-expectation argument proves unbiasedness without needing to sum the negative-binomial mass explicitly.
