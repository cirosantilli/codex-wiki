Number the natural numbers from one. If the power set could be enumerated as A1​,A2​,…, form D={j:j∈/Aj​}. For each j, membership of j in D is opposite to its membership in Aj​, so D=Aj​. This contradicts completeness of the enumeration. Thus the Cantor diagonal argument proves that the power set is an uncountable set.
For a finite subset A, define b(A)=∑j∈A​2j−1. This is a nonnegative integer and the map is injective: if two finite subsets differ, let k be their largest differing element. Its contribution 2k−1 exceeds the sum 1+2+⋯+2k−2 of all smaller possible contributions, so their codes differ. There are infinitely many singleton subsets. Hence the countability of finite subsets of a countable set here is countable infinitude.
To treat all bijections, associate to every subset A⊆N the permutation that swaps 2j−1 and 2j when j∈A, and fixes both when j∈/A. These disjoint pair swaps define a bijection of N. Different subsets give different permutations, so the map from the power set into X is injective. Therefore X is an uncountable set.
Every finitely supported permutation fixes all elements above some k. A bijection fixing that tail must permute {1,…,k}: it cannot send a smaller element into the fixed tail without violating injectivity. There are exactly k! such permutations. Thus
Y=⋃k≥1​{σ:σ(j)=j for every j>k}
(15)
is a countable union of finite sets. Enumerate first by k, then by one of the finitely many permutations at that k; duplicates can be omitted. Infinitely many distinct pair swaps belong to Y, so Y is countably infinite. The full family is uncountable, while the finitely supported family is countably infinite.

For a prime number p and an integer x coprime to p, multiplication by x permutes the nonzero residue classes modulo p. Indeed, ax≡bx(modp) implies a≡b(modp), and none of the products is zero. Taking the product over the p−1 classes gives
xp−1(p−1)!≡(p−1)!(modp).
(16)
The factorial is coprime to p and can be cancelled, proving Fermat little theorem: xp−1≡1(modp)​.
Now n=mp and x is coprime to n, so it is coprime to p. Since (n−1)−(m−1)=m(p−1), the proved theorem gives
xn−1=xm−1(xp−1)m≡xm−1(modp).
(17)
Thus the two specified integer congruences modulo p are equivalent. In this particular step the additional hypothesis (m,p)=1 is not needed; it will hold for the squarefree factorization used next.
If n is a squarefree integer, its distinct prime factors are pairwise coprime. Consequently n divides xn−1−1 exactly when every prime factor does. Applying the preceding equivalence with m=n/p proves
xn−1≡1(modn)⟺x(n/p)−1≡1(modp) for every p∣n.​
(18)
Finally retain this squarefree hypothesis. If p−1 divides n−1, write n−1=kp​(p−1). Fermat little theorem gives xn−1=(xp−1)kp​≡1(modp) for every prime factor. Their pairwise coprimality then gives xn−1≡1(modn)​. For composite n this is the sufficient direction of the Korselt criterion for a Carmichael number; primality is not excluded by the requested implication.

The formulas apply up to the positive return time tb​=2V/b. Substitution into the signed velocity and angular velocity gives
v(tb​)=−V,speed at return=V,ω(tb​)=aV​sinα−μcosαsinα−5μcosα​.​
(57)
The return spin can have either sign and vanishes at tanα=5μ. It reverses when 3μ<tanα<5μ, although the contact point still slips downhill. The unchanged centre speed does not mean that no energy was dissipated: the rotational kinetic energy has decreased. Indeed integrating the frictional power Fw<0 gives
K(0)−K(tb​)=(sinα−μcosα)22μmV2cosα(sinα−3μcosα)​≥0,
(58)
which agrees with the difference of the initial and final rotational kinetic energies.
