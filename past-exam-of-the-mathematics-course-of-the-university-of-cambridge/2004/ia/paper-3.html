1D (Algebra and Geometry)

Words: 201 Articles: 1

Solution

Words: 201
1D OurBigBook.com
For a subgroup H of a finite group G, Lagrange's theorem states ∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣. Thus a subgroup's order, and in particular the order of a group element, divides ∣G∣.
For ∣G∣=10, if every element had order one or two, the order of a finite group of exponent two would make ∣G∣ a power of two, a contradiction. Hence an element has order five or ten. In the latter case G is the cyclic group C10​. Otherwise choose a of order five. Its cyclic subgroup H=⟨a⟩ has index two and is therefore a normal subgroup.
In the noncyclic case an element b outside H has order two: its order divides ten, it cannot have order ten, and an element of order five has trivial image in the order-two quotient group G/H. Every element of G is then aj or ajb. Conjugation by b restricts to an automorphism of H, say bab−1=ar. Since b2=1, r2≡1(mod5), so r=1 or −1 modulo five.
For r=1, the generators commute and ab has order ten, yielding the cyclic case. For r=−1, these are the relations of the dihedral group of the pentagon. The classification of groups of order ten is therefore exactly C10​ and D10​, where the subscript on D10​ denotes its order. They are not isomorphic because one is Abelian and the other is not.

2D OurBigBook.com
The Möbius group consists of maps z↦(az+b)/(cz+d) with ad−bc=0, acting on the Riemann sphere C=C∪{∞}. Proportional matrices represent the same map. Thus it is PGL2​(C), equivalently PSL2​(C) after normalizing the determinant. A zero denominator gives the image ∞, while ∞ maps to a/c when c=0 and stays at infinity when c=0.
Fixing both zero and infinity forces b=c=0, leaving z↦λz with λ=0. Composition multiplies the parameters, giving a bijective group homomorphism from the multiplicative group of nonzero complex numbers to this pointwise stabilizer.
For the other pair, take T(z)=z/(1−z), which sends zero to zero and one to infinity. Conjugate the scaling subgroup by this Möbius transformation:
T−1(λT(z))=1+(λ−1)zλz​,λ∈C∗.​
(1)
Every map fixing zero and one arises this way. Composition again multiplies λ, proving both subgroups are isomorphic to C∗. This is the Möbius pointwise stabilizer of two points; conjugation changes the selected fixed points without changing its group structure.

For a regular space curve parametrized by arc length s, its curvature is κ=∥dT/ds∥, where T=dx/ds is the unit tangent. Differentiating the given parametrization gives
x′(t)=2et​(cost−sint, sint+cost, 2​),∥x′(t)∥=et.
(6)
The curve approaches the origin as t→−∞; the origin is a limiting endpoint. Its arc length measured from that endpoint is s=∫−∞t​eudu=et, so
x(s)=(2s​coslogs, 2s​sinlogs, 2​s​),s>0.​
(7)
This is a logarithmic conical helix. Its unit tangent and derivative are
T(s)=21​(coslogs−sinlogs, sinlogs+coslogs, 2​),T′(s)=2s1​(−sinlogs−coslogs, coslogs−sinlogs, 0).
(8)
Consequently,
κ(s)=2​s1​.​
(9)
The infinitely many turns near the limiting origin have finite total arc length, while the curvature diverges there.

Use D12​ for the dihedral group of order twelve and write each element uniquely as gj or gjh, 0≤j<6. The classification of subgroups of a dihedral group organizes the complete list: rotational subgroups are cyclic, and a subgroup containing a reflection is generated by its rotational part and one reflection. The individual lists and quotients follow below.

i OurBigBook.com
The involutions in an even dihedral group are the half-turn g3 and the six reflections gjh. Thus the seven order-two subgroups are
⟨g3⟩,⟨gjh⟩(j=0,1,2,3,4,5).​
(10)
The half-turn is central, so only ⟨g3⟩ is normal. Indeed g(gjh)g−1=gj+2h, a different reflection; an order-two subgroup has only one nonidentity element, so each reflection subgroup fails the normal subgroup condition.

ii OurBigBook.com
The remaining proper subgroups are
{1},⟨g2⟩,⟨g⟩,⟨g3,gjh⟩ (j=0,1,2),⟨g2,gjh⟩ (j=0,1).​
(11)
Their orders are respectively one, three, six, four and six. The order-four groups are Klein four-groups; the last two are dihedral groups of order six, isomorphic to S3​.
For completeness, if H contains a reflection, write H∩⟨g⟩=⟨gd⟩ with d∣6. Any other reflection differs from a chosen gjh by an element of this rotational subgroup, so H=⟨gd,gjh⟩ with j specified modulo d. The choices d=1,2,3,6 give respectively the whole group, the two order-six groups, the three order-four groups and the six reflection groups already listed. This proves there are no omitted subgroups.
Thus there are eight remaining proper subgroups. The printed count nine includes one too many; nine would be correct if the whole group were included with these remaining subgroups. Altogether G has sixteen subgroups, fifteen proper.

iii OurBigBook.com
The proper normal subgroups and their quotient groups are as follows. Here the trivial subgroup is included as a proper subgroup.
  • N={1} gives G/N≅D12​.
  • N=⟨g3⟩ gives G/N≅S3​: the rotation has order three and the reflection inverts it.
  • N=⟨g2⟩ gives G/N≅C2​×C2​: the two generators now have order two and commute.
  • N=⟨g⟩ gives G/N≅C2​.
  • N=⟨g2,h⟩ or ⟨g2,gh⟩ gives G/N≅C2​.
The rotational subgroups are preserved by reflection conjugation, and the last three order-six subgroups are normal by index two. The three order-four groups are not normal, since conjugation by g changes the reflection exponent by two, which is nonzero modulo three. Part solution excluded the reflection subgroups. These observations, together with the complete subgroup list, prove the normal-subgroup list is exhaustive.

n=5​1​(1,2,0)T​

cosθ=−32​.​

7D OurBigBook.com
Let Az=λz with z=0, allowing complex eigenvectors. A real symmetric matrix is Hermitian, so z∗Az is real and λ=z∗Az/(z∗z) is real. For eigenvectors u,v of distinct real eigenvalues λ,μ, self-adjointness gives λu∗v=u∗Av=μu∗v, hence u∗v=0. Thus the distinct eigenspaces are orthogonal.
Here A=3I−J, where J is the all-ones matrix. Since J(1,1,1)T=3(1,1,1)T and J vanishes on the plane of coordinate sum zero,
λ=0: E0​=span{(1,1,1)T};λ=3: E3​=span{(1,−1,0)T,(1,1,−2)T}.​
(14)
Every nonzero vector in the indicated eigenspace is an eigenvector. The repeated eigenvalue three has a two-dimensional eigenspace.
A complex symmetric nilpotent matrix provides the requested counterexample:
N=(1i​i−1​),N=0,NT=N,N2=0.​
(15)
Its characteristic polynomial is λ2, so its only eigenvalue is zero. It is not diagonalizable: a diagonalizable matrix with only zero eigenvalues would itself be zero. Complex symmetry NT=N is weaker than the Hermitian condition N∗=N.

8D OurBigBook.com
Expanding the characteristic polynomial along the first column gives
χA​(λ)​=(λ−3)[(λ−4+s)(λ−4s+1)+4(s−1)2]=(λ−3)2(λ−3s)​.​
(16)
For s=1, solving the two nullspace equations gives
E3​=span{(1,0,0)T,(0,2,1)T},E3s​=span{(1,s−1,2s−2)T}.​
(17)
These are all the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, with nonzero vectors understood. The three displayed spanning vectors form an eigenbasis, so A is diagonalizable.
At s=1, the sole eigenvalue is three, with algebraic multiplicity three but the same two-dimensional E3​. Thus A is diagonalizable exactly when s=1. Indeed, at the exceptional parameter, A=3I+N with N=0 and N2=0, so its Jordan normal form has one block of size two and one of size one. This illustrates how an eigenvalue collision can destroy diagonalizability without changing the two-dimensional eigenspace.

Solution

Words: 149
9C OurBigBook.com
Set r=x2+y2​. The bound ∣xy∣≤r2/2 and ∣x2−y2∣≤r2 give ∣f(x,y)∣≤r2/2. Hence f→0 at the origin, proving continuity, and ∣f(x,y)∣/r→0, proving Fréchet differentiability there with derivative equal to the zero linear map.
Along the coordinate axes, direct difference quotients give
fx​(0,y)=−y,fy​(x,0)=x.​
(18)
These formulas include the origin. Therefore the two mixed partial derivatives differ:
∂y​(∂x​f)(0,0)=−1,∂x​(∂y​f)(0,0)=1.​
(19)
The explicit operator order removes any ambiguity about the notation for mixed derivatives. Also fxx​(0,0)=fyy​(0,0)=0, since the first derivatives restricted to their own axes vanish identically.
All four second-order partial derivatives are discontinuous at the origin. Away from it,
fxx​(x,y)=(x2+y2)34xy3(−x2+3y2)​,fyy​(x,y)=−(x2+y2)34yx3(−y2+3x2)​.
(20)
Along x=y=0 these are one and minus one, rather than their zero values at the origin. Meanwhile ∂y​∂x​f(x,0)=1 for x=0, whereas its value at the origin is minus one; and ∂x​∂y​f(0,y)=−1 for y=0, whereas its origin value is one. This gives a concrete unequal mixed partial derivatives example despite differentiability of the original function.

10C OurBigBook.com
An exact differential is a one-form equal to dΦ for a single-valued scalar function Φ on the domain. In Euclidean coordinates this means its vector coefficients form ∇Φ. Integrating the first component of F with respect to x, then matching the other components, gives
Φ(x,y,z)=z3(ex−ey)+x3(ey−ez)+C.​
(21)
Differentiating checks all three components. Since the domain R3 is connected, two such potentials differ only by a constant, so this is the most general potential.
At both specified endpoints the nonconstant terms vanish. The fundamental theorem for line integrals gives zero for the line integral along every path joining the two points.
For the planar field, choose a continuous lift θ(t) of the polar angle along a curve avoiding the origin. Differentiating the angle gives
G⋅dx=x2+y2−ydx+xdy​=dθ.
(22)
On the closed curve the angle increases by 2π times its winding number. Thus
∮C​G⋅dx=2π.​
(23)
The angle differential is locally exact but not globally exact on the punctured plane: the angle lift need not return to its initial value. This is the nonexact angular one-form, whose nonzero closed-path integral obstructs a global potential.

Choose zero gravitational potential at infinity. Apply superposition to a uniform sphere of positive density and the two voids represented by spheres of negative density. The shell theorem makes every component act as a point mass outside the large sphere. With c2​=(1/2,0,0) and c3​=(−1/4,0,0),
Φ(x)=−34πGρ​[∣x∣1​−∣x−c2​∣1/8​−∣x−c3​∣1/64​],∣x∣>1.​
(24)
The volume factors are the cubes of the three radii.
Inside the smaller void, the large sphere and the removed small sphere have interior fields −Cx and +C(x−c3​), where C=4πGρ/3. They combine into the uniform gravitational field in an off-center spherical cavity, −Cc3​=C(1/4,0,0). The point is outside the other void's sphere, whose removed mass contributes a repulsive field. Thus
g(x)=C[41​e1​+81​∣x−c2​∣3x−c2​​].
(25)
An equilibrium must lie on the line through the two centers. Write x=qe1​ with −1/2<q<0. Its equation is 1/4−1/[8(1/2−q)2]=0, giving
x∗​=(1/2−1/2​,0,0).​
(26)
This point is inside the smaller void, but it is not stable. Let d=∣x∗​−c2​∣=1/2​. The field's Jacobian matrix has eigenvalues
−4d3C​,8d3C​,8d3C​.
(27)
The axial displacement is restoring, while transverse displacements accelerate away from equilibrium. Therefore there is no point in that void where a test particle remains stably at rest. The cavity equilibrium destabilized by a second void is a saddle; existence of a zero force is insufficient for stability.

For a continuously differentiable vector field on a volume and its boundary, the divergence theorem equates ∫V​∇⋅FdV to the outward flux ∫∂V​F⋅ndS. For the given field, ∇⋅F=5(x2+y2+z2)=5r2, so
∫V​∇⋅FdV=4π∫0R​5r4dr=4πR5.
(28)
On the sphere, n=(x,y,z)/R, and
F⋅n=Rx4+y4+z4+2x2y2+2y2z2+2z2x2​=Rr4​=R3.
(29)
Thus the surface flux is also 4πR5, verifying the theorem by explicit calculation.
Apply the product rule to ∇⋅(ϕ∇ψ)=ϕΔψ+∇ϕ⋅∇ψ, then apply the divergence theorem. This proves Green's first identity:
∫V​(ϕΔψ+∇ϕ⋅∇ψ)dV=∫S​ϕ∂n​ψdS.
(30)
Swap ϕ and ψ and subtract. The gradient products cancel, giving Green's second identity:
∫V​(ϕΔψ−ψΔϕ)dV=∫S​(ϕ∂n​ψ−ψ∂n​ϕ)dS.
(31)
For uniqueness, set w=ϕ1​−ϕ2​. It is a harmonic function with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions. Under the stated smoothness assumptions, Green's first identity with ϕ=ψ=w gives ∫V​∣∇w∣2dV=0. Thus w is constant on each connected component, and its boundary value makes every constant zero. The two solutions agree throughout V. This is Dirichlet uniqueness for the Poisson equation. For classical solutions merely continuous up to the boundary and twice continuously differentiable in the interior, the maximum principle for harmonic functions gives the same conclusion without requiring boundary derivatives.
