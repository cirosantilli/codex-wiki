For a straight-line solution y=mx, substitution into the differential equation gives m=(1−2m)/(2+m). Thus m2+4m−1=0, and the two possible slopes are
m±​=−2±5​.​
(1)
These straight lines solve the differential equation on either side of the origin. At the origin itself the right-hand side is undefined.
An isocline of slope k has equation (1−2k)x−(2+k)y=0. For k=−2 it is the straight line y=(1−2k)x/(2+k); for k=−2 it is x=0, excluding the origin. In particular, y=x/2 is the zero-slope isocline. The line y=−2x is excluded from the domain of the differential equation; slopes tend to infinity on approaching it. Draw the flow vectors proportional to (1,(x−2y)/(2x+y)), with positive horizontal component. This specifies orientation by increasing x, rather than by an auxiliary time variable.
To integrate, put y=xu where x=0. Then
xu′=2+u1−4u−u2​,∫1−4u−u22+u​du=∫xdx​.
(2)
The left-hand integral is −21​log∣1−4u−u2∣, so the nonconstant-u solutions have the first integral
y2+4xy−x2=C.​
(3)
The constant-u solutions omitted by separation are exactly the two straight lines already found, corresponding to C=0. Alternatively, differentiating this first integral gives (2y+4x)y′+4y−2x=0, which recovers the original differential equation wherever 2x+y=0 and also verifies continuation through x=0 when y=0.
The first integral describes hyperbolas with the two straight-line solutions as asymptotes. For C=1, the upper branch crosses the vertical axis at (0,1) and exists for every real x. For C=−1, each branch is confined to ∣x∣≥1/5​ and reaches a vertical tangent on the excluded line y=−2x; the corresponding classical solution intervals exclude those endpoints. These give the two distinct types of curves requested in the sketch.
The initial condition selects C=1 and the upper branch, hence

Here 2x+y=5x2+1​>0, so this really is a global solution of the initial value problem, and y′(0)=−2 agrees with the right-hand side.

For random variables with finite second moments, their covariance is
cov(X,Y)=E[(X−EX)(Y−EY)]=E[XY]−EXEY.​
(8)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality ensures the product is integrable under this hypothesis. More generally the definition works whenever the displayed expectations exist. Assertions about arbitrary random variables below are understood on this domain: without suitable moments a covariance need not be defined at all.

Linearity of expectation gives E(X+Y)=EX+EY. Expand the centered product and use linearity of expectation again:
cov(X+Y,Z)​=E[((X−EX)+(Y−EY))(Z−EZ)]=cov(X,Z)+cov(Y,Z).​

Thus the assertion is true whenever the covariances are defined. Neither independence nor identical distributions are required.

The covariance is symmetric, because multiplication of real random variables is commutative. Expanding with the bilinearity just proved gives
cov(X+Y,X−Y)​=var(X)−cov(X,Y)+cov(Y,X)−var(Y)=var(X)−var(Y).​

Introduce the velocity variable v=x˙. The second-order differential equation becomes the autonomous planar system
x˙=v,v˙=f(x,v).​
(25)
A critical point of a dynamical system is a point where both components of the vector field vanish: here v=0 and f(x,0)=0. It is a stationary solution in the phase plane. The Jacobian matrix at each such point gives the local linear stability of a planar equilibrium. For the equations below, a conserved energy also determines the nonlinear phase portrait; this is important because purely imaginary eigenvalues alone do not generally prove that an equilibrium is a center equilibrium.

In a forced linear differential equation, resonance in a differential equation occurs when exponential forcing excites a mode with the same exponent as its eigenvalue, producing a polynomial factor such as t multiplying that exponential. Merely matching an exponent is not enough: its projection onto that mode must be nonzero. Here teλj​t still decays in absolute magnitude because the real part of λj​ is negative; resonance refers to this extra factor, not necessarily to unbounded growth.

For events with P(B)>0, the conditional probability is
P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A∩B)​.​
(46)
Use the sample space Ω={1,2,3,4}×{0,1}4, where the first coordinate is the chosen coin and the other coordinates record which of its two physically distinguished faces lands upwards on each toss. There are 4⋅16=64 equally likely elementary outcomes. Label the double-headed coin by one. For that coin both face indices mean heads; for each other coin only face index one means heads. In particular, strings of observed heads and tails are not equally likely outcomes.
Let E be the event that the first three tosses show heads. With coin one all sixteen face-index strings are permitted. For each ordinary coin the first three indices must be one and the fourth is free, giving two strings. Thus ∣E∣=16+3⋅2=22. Of these outcomes, sixteen from the double-headed coin and one from each ordinary coin also have a fourth head. Hence
P(fourth head∣E)=2216+3​=2219​.​
(47)
Equivalently, Bayes' theorem gives conditional probability 8/11 of having selected the double-headed coin after E; the fourth-head probability is then 8/11+(3/11)(1/2)=19/22. The tosses are independent conditional on the selected coin, while mixing over the same coin explains their unconditional dependence.

Because the events are pairwise disjoint events, their count can only be zero or one. The event that the count is zero is the complement of an event of their union. Finite additivity therefore gives
P(N=0)=1−P(i=1⋃r​Ai​)=1−i=1∑r​P(Ai​).​
(53)
Now use N≥1 for the fixed number of independently located ships. Normalize the radius to one and write their positions as unit vectors a1​,…,aN​. A point x on the sphere is reached by ship i precisely when ai​⋅x>0, so each ship reaches an open hemisphere. Coverage fails exactly when there is a unit vector x with ai​⋅x≤0 for every i. Equivalently, all ship positions lie in some closed hemisphere.
With probability one no pair of ship vectors is antipodal and no three are linearly dependent: the exceptional choices lie on sets of zero surface area. For such a configuration, containment in a closed hemisphere implies containment in an open hemisphere. To see this, choose its inward normal u, so u⋅ai​≥0. At most two vectors lie on its boundary. If there are two, their sum has positive dot product with each, since they are not antipodal; if there is one, use that vector itself. Perturb u slightly in this direction. The boundary dot products become positive while all already positive dot products remain positive. Thus the strict and non-strict containment conditions have the same probability here.
Condition on the unoriented axes {ai​,−ai​}. Choose representatives bi​ by any fixed sign convention. By uniform sampling and independence, the actual positions are ai​=εi​bi​ with N independent fair signs εi​∈{−1,1}. Conditional on these axes, each of the 2N sign choices has probability 2−N.
The great circles bi​⋅u=0 partition the sphere into RN​=N(N−1)+2 open regions. In each region the sign vector σ(u)=(sgn(bi​⋅u))i​ is constant. No sign vector labels two different regions: its strict inequalities define a convex cone, and normalized line segments within that cone connect any two points of its spherical section. The regions of a general-position great-circle arrangement therefore yield exactly RN​ realized sign vectors.
The chosen positions lie in an open hemisphere if and only if some u has u⋅ai​>0 for every i, that is, εi​=sgn(bi​⋅u) for every i. Failure of coverage thus corresponds exactly to one of the RN​ realized sign choices. These choices are mutually exclusive events, each of conditional probability 2−N, so the first part applies:
P(failure∣axes)=2NRN​​.
(54)
The result is independent of the axes. Averaging gives the coverage of a sphere by random open hemispheres:
P(every point is reached)=1−2NN(N−1)+2​,N≥1.​
(55)
For N=1,2,3 this is zero; for N=4 it is 1/8. If no ship lands, coverage has probability zero separately. The counting uses equiprobable orientation signs, not an assumption that the spherical regions have equal area.
