The Noncommutative Ruzsa triangle inequality says that nonempty finite subsets B,C,D of a group satisfy
∣B∣∣CD−1∣≤∣CB−1∣∣BD−1∣.
(2)
The Ruzsa covering lemma says that if ∣CD∣≤K∣D∣, then some X⊆C with ∣X∣≤K satisfies
C⊆XDD−1.
(3)
Now let A be a symmetric subset of a group, so A−1=A and 1∈A. Apply the triangle inequality with the middle set A to obtain, for m≥4,
∣A∣∣Am∣≤∣Am−1∣∣A3∣.
(4)
The hypothesis therefore gives ∣Am∣≤K∣Am−1∣. Starting from ∣A3∣≤K∣A∣ proves
∣Am∣≤Km−2∣A∣(m≥3).​
(5)
In particular ∣A5∣≤K3∣A∣. Apply the covering lemma to C=A4 and D=A. There is X⊆A4 with ∣X∣≤K3 such that
A4⊆XAA−1=XA2.
(6)
The set A2 is symmetric and contains the identity element, so this inclusion is exactly the covering condition showing that
A2 is a K3-approximate group.​
(7)
Small doubling alone is insufficient in a noncommutative group. Let H be a finite group, let G=H∗⟨x⟩ be its free product with an infinite cyclic group, and put
A=H∪{x,x−1}.
(8)
Then A is symmetric and ∣A2∣≤5∣H∣+4=O(∣A∣), while A3 contains the double coset HxH. Distinct pairs (h1​,h2​)∈H2 give distinct reduced words h1​xh2​, so ∣HxH∣=∣H∣2. Letting ∣H∣→∞ proves the small doubling does not control tripling in a noncommutative group phenomenon.

By the Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality,
∣4A∣≤K4∣A∣.
(9)
Apply the Ruzsa covering lemma to 3A and A. Since A=−A, there is a set X⊆3A with ∣X∣≤K4 such that
3A⊆X+A−A=X+2A.
(10)
Adding A and reusing this inclusion inductively gives
mA⊆(m−2)X+2A(m≥3).​
(11)
A sum of m−2 members of the fixed set X depends only on the multiplicity of each member. The number of possible multiplicity vectors is at most m∣X∣, and therefore
∣(m−2)X∣≤m∣X∣≤mK4.
(12)
Since ∣2A∣≤K∣A∣≤Km∣A∣, it follows that
∣mA∣≤KmmK4∣A∣.​
(13)
For fixed K, this differs from the Plünnecke–Ruzsa bound Km∣A∣ only by a polynomial factor in m, so both have the same leading exponential function factor Km.

Let π:G→G/[G,G] be the abelianization map. Its image π(A) is again a K-approximate group. Apply the large-progression form of the Freiman-Green-Ruzsa theorem to π(A). It gives a finite subgroup H, elements x1​,…,xr​, and lengths L1​,…,Lr​, with
r≤KO(1),HP(x1​,…,xr​;L1​,…,Lr​)⊆π(A4),
(19)

∣HP∣≥exp(−KO(1))∣π(A)∣.
(20)

​(A16∩π−1(H))∏i=1r​(A22∩π−1(⟨xi​⟩))​≥exp(−KO(1))∣A∣.

Briefly, choose a section of π on π(A6). Multiplication by that section is multiplicative up to A12∩[G,G]; lifting successively the subgroup part and each progression direction therefore places every element of A6∩π−1(HP) in the displayed product. The fiber-counting lemma for a quotient map gives ∣A6∩π−1(HP)∣≥exp(−KO(1))∣A∣, which proves the estimate.
Set
A0​=A16∩π−1(H),Ai​=A22∩π−1(⟨xi​⟩)(1≤i≤r).
(22)
The intersection of an approximate group power with a subgroup shows that each Ai​ is a KO(1)-approximate group contained in AO(1). The preimage of a cyclic subgroup of G/[G,G] has step less than s. The same is true of the preimage of the finite subgroup H because G is a torsion-free group. Consequently each ⟨Ai​⟩ has step less than s, and the displayed estimate is the required conclusion.

Fix ε>0 and set d=2/ε. Suppose, towards a contradiction, that
D=diamS​(G)>max{∣G∣ε,λ},
(59)
where λ will absorb constants depending only on ε. Put n=⌊∣G∣ε⌋. Once λ is large enough, n≥N(d), n<D, and
∣Sn∣≤∣G∣≤nd∣S∣.
(60)
Part (a) yields H⊴C<G with [G:C]=Oε​(1), H⊆S⌊n/2⌋, and C/H nilpotent of class Oε​(1).
The subgroup core ⋂g∈G​gCg−1 is normal in G and has index at most [G:C]!. Since G is a simple group, the core is either {1} or G. In the first case ∣G∣≤[G:C]!=Oε​(1), which is excluded by increasing λ. Hence the core is G, so C=G.

A nontrivial finite nilpotent group has nontrivial center of a group; simplicity would force that center to be all of G, making G Abelian. This contradicts the assumption that G is non-abelian.
