Let x,y∈Hn(Sn×Sn;Z) be the pullbacks of the two orientation classes. The cohomology ring of a product of two spheres has
x2=y2=0,xy=(−1)nyx,
(1)
and xy generates H2n≅Z. Write
f∗x=ax+by,f∗y=cx+dy.
(2)
The matrix A=(ac​bd​) lies in GL2​(Z) because a homeomorphism induces a cohomology ring automorphism.
If n is even, graded commutativity gives
0=(f∗x)2=2abxy,0=(f∗y)2=2cdxy.
(3)
Thus ab=cd=0. Invertibility forces A to be diagonal or anti-diagonal, and its two nonzero entries must each be ±1. Hence there are exactly eight possible actions: the signed permutation matrices.
Evenness is necessary. For n=1, the product is the torus, and every A∈GL2​(Z) is induced by an integral linear automorphism of the torus. For example, the infinitely many matrices (10​m1​) give distinct actions on H1.

The cellular chain complex for either space has one cell in dimensions 0,2,3,4, with the only nonzero differential equal to multiplication by p from degree three to degree two. The universal coefficient theorem for cohomology therefore gives, for both X and Y,
Hj(−;Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z/p,0,​j=0,4,j=3,otherwise.​
(4)
Every product of positive-degree classes vanishes for dimensional reasons, so
H∗(X;Z)≅H∗(Y;Z) as graded rings.​
(5)
Their modulo-p cohomology rings distinguish them. Let u∈H2(X;Fp​) be the class restricting to the standard generator on CP2. The attaching map has degree p, so its cellular coboundary vanishes modulo p; the classes in degrees two and four restrict isomorphically to those of CP2. Hence u2=0 in H4(X;Fp​). In Y=M(Z/p,2)∨S4, the degree-two class comes from the three-dimensional Moore space, so its square is zero; products between distinct wedge summands also vanish. The mod-p cup-square obstruction to a homotopy equivalence now proves
X≃Y.​
(6)

The standard cellular chain complex of the Klein bottle gives
H0​(K;Z)=Z,H1​(K;Z)=Z⊕Z/2,H2​(K;Z)=0.
(7)
The universal coefficient theorem for cohomology therefore yields H0=Z, H1=Z, and H2=Z/2. The degree-one generator is pulled back from the base circle in the circle-bundle description of K, so its square is zero. Thus every positive-degree product vanishes. The same calculation for RP2∨S1 gives the integral cohomology ring of the wedge of the real projective plane and a circle, and hence
H∗(K;Z)≅H∗(RP2∨S1;Z).​
(8)
There is no map Y→K inducing this isomorphism. The Klein bottle is an aspherical space, and its fundamental group is torsion-free. Any map RP2→K therefore induces the trivial homomorphism Z/2→π1​(K) and is null-homotopic. Its pullback on H2 is zero, while the circle summand has no degree-two cohomology. Consequently every map Y→K is zero on H2(K;Z) and cannot be a cohomology isomorphism.

A direct system of abelian groups over the directed poset A consists of groups Ga​ and maps ϕab​:Ga​→Gb​ for a≤b, satisfying ϕaa​=1 and ϕbc​ϕab​=ϕac​. Its direct limit is the quotient of ⨁a​Ga​ by the relations ga​∼ϕab​(ga​).
For the displayed sequence, put P0​=1 and Pn​=a0​a1​⋯an−1​. Map the copy of Z at stage n to Q by
m⟼Pn​m​.
(9)
This is compatible with the next transition because an​m/Pn+1​=m/Pn​. The universal property of the direct limit therefore identifies it with
n≥0⋃​Pn​1​Z.​
(10)
In reduced form, these are exactly the rationals whose denominator divides one of the finite products Pn​. This is the sequential direct limit of multiplication maps on the integers.

Every singular simplex has compact image, and a singular chain is a finite sum of simplices. The image of any chain is therefore compact and lies in some Xa​. Directedness puts any finite collection of chains into one common Xb​, so
C∗​(X;Z)=lim​a​C∗​(Xa​;Z).
(11)
Because filtered colimits of abelian groups are exact, kernels and images commute with this colimit. Taking homology gives the homology of a directed union:
Hi​(X;Z)≅alim​​Hi​(Xa​;Z).​
(12)
For an open U⊆RN, use the directed family of finite unions of closed rational cubes contained in U. Every compact subset of U lies in one such finite polyhedron, and each polyhedron has finitely generated cellular homology. There are only countably many of them, so their direct limit is countable. Thus every Hi​(U;Z) is countable.
Cohomology behaves differently because Hom turns a direct sum into a direct product. The connected open set
U=R2∖{(n,0):n≥1}
(13)
has one independent loop around each puncture, so H1​(U;Z)≅⨁n≥1​Z. Since H0​(U;Z)=Z, the universal coefficient theorem for cohomology gives
H1(U;Z)≅Hom(n≥1⨁​Z,Z)≅n≥1∏​Z,​
(14)
which is uncountable. This is the first cohomology of the countably punctured plane.

An R-orientation of a rank-d real vector bundle E→B is a locally coherent choice of generator of Hd(Eb​,Eb​∖{0};R) in every fibre. Equivalently, it is represented by a Thom class uE​∈Hd(D(E),S(E);R) restricting to the chosen generator on each fibre pair. If e(E)∈Hd(B;R) is the Euler class, the Gysin sequence of its unit sphere bundle contains
⋯→Hq−d(B;R)⌣e(E)​Hq(B;R)→Hq(S(E);R)→Hq−d+1(B;R)⌣e(E)​Hq+1(B;R)→⋯.
(18)
The diagonal quotient defining L(p) is the three-dimensional lens space as a circle bundle S1→L(p)→S2 with Euler class p times a generator. With integral coefficients, the only nontrivial Euler-class map is multiplication by p:H0(S2;Z)→H2(S2;Z). Exactness gives
Hj(L(p);Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z/p,0,​j=0,3,j=2,otherwise.​​
(19)
Modulo p, the Euler class vanishes, so the same Gysin sequence gives
Hj(L(p);Fp​)≅Fp​(0≤j≤3),​
(20)
with all other groups zero.
For the coefficient sequence 0→Zp​Z→Fp​→0, the long exact sequence from a coefficient sequence contains
0=H1(L(p);Z)→H1(L(p);Fp​)δ​H2(L(p);Z)p​H2(L(p);Z).
(21)
The last map is zero and both middle groups have order p, so δ is an isomorphism. Reduction H2(L(p);Z)→H2(L(p);Fp​) is likewise an isomorphism. Their composite is the Bockstein isomorphism for a three-dimensional lens space
β:H1(L(p);Fp​)∼​H2(L(p);Fp​).​
(22)
If a′=na is another generator, linearity of the Bockstein homomorphism and bilinearity of the cup product give
t(a′)=n2t(a).​
(23)
Moreover t(a)=0: β(a) is nonzero, and the Poincare duality pairing H1×H2→H3≅Fp​ is nondegenerate. If h:L(p)→L(p) is an orientation-reversing homotopy equivalence and h∗a=na, naturality gives
n2t(a)=t(h∗a)=⟨h∗(a⌣βa),[L(p)]⟩=⟨a⌣βa,h∗​[L(p)]⟩=−t(a).
(24)
Cancelling the nonzero t(a) yields n2≡−1(modp). Thus −1 must be a quadratic residue modulo p.

Let m=dimM. Choose a homogeneous basis eq,i​ of Hq(M;Q) and its Poincare dual basis eq,i∗​∈Hm−q(M;Q), normalized by
⟨eq,i​⌣eq,j∗​,[M]⟩=δij​.
(25)
With the product orientation, the cohomology class of the diagonal is
εΔ​=q,i∑​(−1)mqeq,i∗​×eq,i​.​
(26)
Indeed, multiplying this class by α×γ and evaluating on [M×M] gives ⟨α⌣γ,[M]⟩, which characterizes the Poincare dual of Δ.
Pulling back along the graph map (1,f):M→M×M and evaluating gives the graph-diagonal formula for the Lefschetz number:
⟨(1,f)∗εΔ​,[M]⟩=∑q​(−1)qtr(f∗∣Hq(M;Q))=L(f).
(27)
If f has no fixed point, its graph is disjoint from Δ. Represent εΔ​ with support in a tubular neighbourhood disjoint from the graph; its pullback is zero, so L(f)=0. Contrapositively, L(f)=0 implies that f has a fixed point, the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem.
Now let M be three disjoint circles. A homeomorphism permutes their three components. Its action on H1(M;Z)≅Z3 is a signed permutation matrix. A component fixed setwise contributes to the trace by the degree of the corresponding circle homeomorphism. An orientation-reversing circle homeomorphism has a fixed point, so fixed-point-freeness forces that degree to be +1. Nonfixed components contribute zero. The trace is therefore the number of fixed points of a permutation of three objects, and
tr(f∗∣H1(M;Z))∈{0,1,3}.​
(28)
For a compact manifold N with boundary, let D(N)=N+​∪∂N​N−​ be its double and define F by applying f on both copies. The Mayer–Vietoris sequence for this decomposition is natural under F. Alternating traces in a finite-dimensional exact sequence sum to zero, so the two copies of N contribute twice and their intersection contributes with the opposite sign:
L(F)=2L(f)−L(f∣∂N​).​
(29)
This is the Lefschetz number of a doubled map.
Finally let N be a pair of pants. If f is fixed-point-free, so are its double F and its boundary restriction. The Lefschetz fixed-point theorem and the displayed identity give L(F)=L(f∣∂N​)=0, hence L(f)=0. Since N is connected and Hi(N;Q) is nonzero only for i=0,1,
tr(f∗∣H1(N;Q))=1.
(30)
Suppose the boundary permutation σ had a fixed component. The restriction there is a fixed-point-free circle homeomorphism and thus has degree +1, forcing f to preserve the surface orientation. The homology action of a pair-of-pants homeomorphism would then have trace #Fix(σ)−1, which is 2 for the identity permutation and 0 for a transposition. Neither is 1. Therefore σ has no fixed component; a permutation of three objects with no fixed point is a three-cycle. Thus
f cyclically permutes the three boundary components.​
(31)
