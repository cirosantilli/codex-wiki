Write Q=N˙ion​ for the constant hydrogen-ionizing photon production rate. Assume an initially neutral gas of hydrogen atoms, a sharp spherical ionization front, complete ionization inside it, and a fixed gas density. The Electron and proton number densities inside are then both n. Take the recombination coefficient α to be constant, with the usual Case B recombination interpretation: photons from recombination directly to the ground state are absorbed locally. Neglect absorption by dust, pressure-driven gas motion, and light-travel delays.
The total rate of Case B recombination within radius R is
R(R)=∫0R​αne​np​4πr2dr=34π​αn2R3.
(1)
At photoionization equilibrium, Q=R(RS​), giving the Strömgren radius
RS​=(4παn23Q​)1/3.​
(2)
The square of the density appears because recombination requires encounters between an Electron and a proton.
During Ionization-front growth of a Strömgren sphere, each photon either replaces an ion lost through Case B recombination or ionizes an additional atom. There are Ni​=(4π/3)nR3 ions inside the ionization front, so photon counting gives
dtdNi​​=Q−αnNi​,4πnR2dtdR​=Q−34π​αn2R3.
(3)
The recombination time is trec​=(αn)−1. Solving this first-order differential equation with R(0)=0 gives
Ni​(t)=Qtrec​(1−e−t/trec​),RS​R(t)​=(1−e−t/trec​)1/3.​
(4)
For an initial radius R0​, the corresponding result is R3(t)=RS3​+(R03​−RS3​)e−t/trec​. Initially, the idealized photon-counting solution has R≃[3Qt/(4πn)]1/3; at late times, R/RS​≃1−31​e−t/trec​. The initially divergent front speed in this approximation is a consequence of neglecting light-travel delays; the formula describes the growth once that approximation is applicable.
For a power law density, specify a reference radius r0​ so that n(r)=n0​(r/r0​)−β. With an inner cutoff rin​>0, the recombination integral for a power-law nebula is
R(R;rin​)=4παn02​r02β​⎩⎨⎧​3−2βR3−2β−rin3−2β​​,lnrin​R​,​β=23​,β=23​.​
(5)
Equivalently, a logarithmic interval in radius contributes dR/dlnr=4παn02​r02β​r3−2β. For β<3/2, the central integral converges as rin​→0, and the recombination rate grows as R3−2β. At β=3/2, each logarithmic interval contributes equally and the centre gives a logarithmic divergence. For β>3/2, the unmodified profile gives a power divergence at the centre, while its outer recombination integral converges. The change at β=3/2 is set by the competition between the spherical volume element and the squared density.
To regularize the centre, choose the following explicit cored power-law density profile, taking r0​=rc​ and n0​ as the core density:
n(r)={n0​,n0​(r/rc​)−β,​0≤r≤rc​,r>rc​.​
(6)
For R≤rc​, R(R)=(4π/3)αn02​R3. For R>rc​, put u=R/rc​ and integrate the core and envelope separately:
R(R)=4παn02​rc3​⎩⎨⎧​31​+3−2βu3−2β−1​,31​+lnu,​β=23​,β=23​.​
(7)
This rate is continuous and strictly increasing with R. If β>3/2, it has a finite supremum, the critical ionizing photon rate of a cored power-law nebula:
Qcrit​=4παn02​rc3​(31​+2β−31​)=3(2β−3)8παβn02​rc3​​.​
(8)
For 0<Q<Qcrit​ there is a unique finite radius at photoionization equilibrium. At Q=Qcrit​ the equality is approached only as R→∞; for Q>Qcrit​ even ionizing the whole profile cannot consume all photons through Case B recombination, so no equilibrium radius exists. For β≤3/2, the envelope integral grows without bound and there is no finite critical rate in this infinite-extent model.
For a concrete choice, take any n0​>0, rc​>0, and β=2. Then Qcrit​=16παn02​rc3​/3. In the envelope, defining q=Q/(4παn02​rc3​) gives R=rc​/(4/3−q) for 1/3<q<4/3, explicitly showing the divergence as the critical rate is approached. A different core shape changes the numerical coefficient of Qcrit​, while the outer-slope condition β>3/2 remains the same.

Use a stationary, non-streaming tracer population with spherical symmetry in its full velocity distribution. In particular, the mean velocities vanish, the mixed covariances vanish, and the two tangential components have equal velocity dispersions. Write
σr2​=σrr2​,σt2​=σθθ2​=σϕϕ2​,β(r)=1−σr2​σt2​​.
(9)
Here σt2​ is the variance of one tangential component. In the alternative convention using the sum of both tangential variances, the same velocity-anisotropy parameter is 1−(σθ2​+σϕ2​)/(2σr2​). These definitions agree; they must not be mixed by inserting an extra factor of two.
The time derivative and mean-velocity terms in the supplied Jeans equation vanish, as do its angular mixed-moment terms. Its remaining geometric term is 2ν(σr2​−σt2​)/r. Thus the Spherical Jeans equation becomes
drd(νσr2​)​+r2β(r)​νσr2​=−νdrdΦ​.​
(10)
The no-streaming assumption excludes a steady radial flow; stationarity of the density alone would not remove its advective terms.
By the shell theorem, the gravitational potential of a spherical mass distribution satisfies dΦ/dr=GM(<r)/r2. Expanding the derivative above, dividing by ν, and using logarithmic derivatives gives
M(<r)=−Grσr2​​[dlnrdlnν​+dlnrdlnσr2​​+2β(r)].​
(11)
The tracer density ν need not be the density of the gravitating matter. The circular speed is Vc2​=rdΦ/dr=GM(<r)/r, whether or not the tracers themselves follow circular orbits.
Now let β be constant, ν=ν0​(r/r0​)−γ, and Vc2​=V02​(r/r0​)2α. With y=σr2​, the Spherical Jeans equation reads
y′+r2β−γ​y=−rVc2​(r)​.
(12)
For a power-law ansatz y=KVc2​, y′=2αy/r, so (γ−2β−2α)y=Vc2​. Therefore the scale-free spherical Jeans solution is
σr2​(r)=γ−2β−2αVc2​(r)​,γ−2β−2α>0.​
(13)
The positivity condition is necessary for this particular solution to be a physical velocity dispersion.
There is a boundary condition implicit in selecting this solution. The integrating factor r2β−γ gives, when D=γ−2β−2α=0,
y(r)=DVc2​(r)​+C(r0​r​)γ−2β.
(14)
The homogeneous term represents an additional radial-pressure boundary contribution. It is removed, for example, by requiring r2βνσr2​→0 at infinity. Under that condition,
σr2​(r)=ν(r)r−2β​∫r∞​ν(s)Vc2​(s)s2βsds​,
(15)
and convergence requires D>0, giving the boxed result. If D=0, the solution instead has the logarithmic form
y(r)=(r0​r​)2α[C−V02​lnr0​r​],
(16)
so the quoted constant proportionality does not apply. A finite outer boundary can retain a homogeneous term even when D=0.
To derive the heliocentric projection of a spherical velocity dispersion, put the Sun at vector R⊙​ of length R⊙​, and a tracer at r=rr^. Its heliocentric line of sight is
ℓ^=dr−R⊙​​,d2=r2+R⊙2​−2rR⊙​u,u=r^⋅R^⊙​.
(17)
If χ is the angle between ℓ^ and the Galactocentric radial direction, then
cosχ=dr−R⊙​u​,sin2χ=r2+R⊙2​−2rR⊙​uR⊙2​(1−u2)​.
(18)
After correction for the Sun's motion, the intrinsic covariance matrix has eigenvalues σr2​,σt2​,σt2​ in the local spherical basis. Projection onto the line of sight therefore gives
σlos2​(r,u)=σr2​cos2χ+σt2​sin2χ=σr2​(r)[1−β(r)sin2χ].
(19)
At fixed r, σr​ and β are constant across the spherical shell. Averaging its squared line of sight velocity, with zero mean, gives
σ⊙​(r)=σr​(r)1−β(r)A(r,R⊙​)​,A=⟨sin2χ⟩.​
(20)
For a uniformly sampled Galactocentric shell, the solid angle measure makes u uniform on [−1,1], giving the geometric correction for heliocentric velocity dispersion
A(r,R⊙​)=2R⊙2​​∫−11​r2+R⊙2​−2rR⊙​u1−u2​du.
(21)
To evaluate it, set a=r2+R⊙2​ and b=2rR⊙​, and divide the integrand:
a−bu1−u2​=bu​+b2a​+a−bu1−a2/b2​.
(22)
The odd term integrates to zero, while ∫−11​du/(a−bu)=b−1ln[(a+b)/(a−b)]. For r>0, R⊙​>0, and r=R⊙​, this gives
A(r,R⊙​)=4r2r2+R⊙2​​−8r3R⊙​(r2−R⊙2​)2​ln​r−R⊙​r+R⊙​​​.​
(23)
The removable limit at r=R⊙​ is A=1/2. As r/R⊙​→∞, A≃2R⊙2​/(3r2), while as r/R⊙​→0, A→2/3. If R⊙​=0, A=0 and the observed velocity dispersion is exactly radial. For isotropic velocities, β=0, the correction vanishes at every radius.
The averaging measure matters. If directions are instead uniform in heliocentric solid angle at a fixed Galactocentric radius r>R⊙​, let ϑ be the angle between the line of sight and R⊙​. Geometry gives sin2χ=(R⊙​/r)2sin2ϑ, so that particular weighting gives A=2R⊙2​/(3r2) exactly. The logarithmic expression above uses uniform tracer weighting on a Galactocentric shell. For an observational sample, use its actual angular selection weights in A=⟨sin2χ⟩; the boxed dispersion relation remains valid.

Use the instantaneous recycling approximation with a well-mixed gas reservoir, constant stellar yield p, no gas inflow, and an irreversible galactic outflow. Let Ms​ be the mass locked into long-lived stars and remnants after prompt recycling, and put ψ(t)=M˙s​(t)≥0. The yield p is the newly synthesized mass of heavy elements returned to the gas per unit increase of Ms​. Thus Mh​ in Z=Mh​/Mg​ denotes the heavy-element mass in the gas, excluding metals already locked into stars.
Assume Ms​(0)=0, Mg​(0)=M0​, and Z(0)=Z0​; initially pristine gas means Z0​=0. Take the outflow rate to be αψ with constant α≥0, and assume its metallicity equals the current gas-phase metallicity Z. This is the well-mixed leaky-box model of galactic chemical evolution. The parameter α is a mass-loading factor relative to the net rate of locking mass into stars. If a gross star formation rate Ψ and a prompt returned fraction R are used instead, ψ=(1−R)Ψ and a wind rate ηΨ corresponds to α=η/(1−R).
Conservation of mass gives
M˙g​=−(1+α)ψ,Mg​(t)=M0​−(1+α)Ms​(t),
(24)
and the baryonic mass remaining in the box is Mg​+Ms​=M0​−αMs​. Newly made metals enter the gas at rate pψ, while pre-existing metals are locked into stars at rate Zψ and leave in the wind at rate αZψ. Hence
dtdMh​​=[p−(1+α)Z]ψ.
(25)
Using Mh​=ZMg​ and the product rule,
Mg​Z˙+ZM˙g​=[p−(1+α)Z]ψ⟹Z˙=Mg​pψ​.​
(26)
The cancellation expresses the fact that a well-mixed wind removes gas and metals in the same proportion, while fresh stellar production raises the abundance in the remaining gas.
Divide this equation by M˙g​=−(1+α)ψ along the evolving reservoir, or integrate in time through intervals with no star formation. The resulting gas-phase metallicity is
Z(t)=Z0​+1+αp​lnMg​(t)M0​​=Z0​+1+αp​ln[1−(1+α)Ms​(t)/M0​1​].​
(27)
Equivalently, its fully explicit dependence on a prescribed net star formation rate is
Z(t)=Z0​+1+αp​ln[M0​−(1+α)∫0t​ψ(t′)dt′M0​​],
(28)
valid while Mg​(t)>0. With pre-existing stars, replace Ms​(t) in the gas-mass relation by Ms​(t)−Ms​(0). Without specifying ψ(t) or a gas-consumption law, the model fixes Z as a function of gas mass but does not determine a unique function of time.
For the closed-box model of galactic chemical evolution, set α=0. Then Mg​=M0​−Ms​ and
Zclosed​=Z0​+plnMg​M0​​.​
(29)
At the same remaining fraction g=Mg​/M0​ of the initial gas reservoir, the leaky box has the logarithmic coefficient p/(1+α), lower than the true stellar yield by the wind factor. This coefficient is often called an effective yield, but the definition of the gas fraction must be specified.
In particular, the observable gas fraction of a galaxy is often μ=Mg​/(Mg​+Ms​), using the mass still present, rather than g. In our initially star-free model,
μ=1+αg(1+α)g​,g=1+α(1−μ)μ​.
(30)
Therefore
Zleaky​(μ)=Z0​+1+αp​ln[μ1+α(1−μ)​],Zclosed​(μ)=Z0​+plnμ1​.​
(31)
For 0<μ<1 and α>0, the leaky-box enrichment is smaller at the same μ: ln[1+α(1−μ)]≤α(1−μ)<αln(1/μ). If effective yield is defined observationally as (Z−Z0​)/ln(1/μ), it is consequently not simply the constant p/(1+α) at all μ.
As an explicit time example, choose a linear gas-consumption law ψ=ϵMg​, with constant ϵ>0. The mass and metallicity equations give
Mg​=M0​e−(1+α)ϵt,Ms​=1+αM0​​(1−e−(1+α)ϵt),Z(t)=Z0​+pϵt.​
(32)
The closed box with the same ϵ has the same metallicity at a given time, while the leaky box exhausts its gas faster. Thus a comparison at fixed time depends on the chosen star formation rate, even though the comparison at fixed gas fraction above is unambiguous.
Finally, the assumption about wind metallicity is essential. If the expelled gas has abundance Zw​ instead, its metal-loss rate is αZw​ψ, and the same bookkeeping yields
Mg​Z˙=[p+α(Z−Zw​)]ψ.
(33)
A wind enriched relative to the ambient gas lowers the enrichment rate; the logarithmic solution derived above applies to Zw​=Z.
