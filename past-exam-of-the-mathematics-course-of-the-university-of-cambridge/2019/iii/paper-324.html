The hidden subgroup problem is to recover a subgroup K≤G, usually by giving generators of a group, from an oracle machine providing f:G→Y with the promise
f(g)=f(h)⟺gK=hK.​
(1)
Thus f is constant on each left coset and distinguishes different left cosets. The input includes an effective representation of elements of the group and access to the group operations; the desired efficiency is measured against the number of bits describing an element, rather than necessarily against ∣G∣.

Write the finite abelian group additively. Its group shift operator is
U(h)∣g⟩=∣g+h⟩.​
(2)
These unitary operators form the regular representation and commute. The representation-theoretic facts we use are that every irreducible representation of a finite abelian group is one-dimensional, there are ∣G∣ such characters of a representation, and their character orthogonality gives an orthonormal basis of functions on G. Each linear character here is a group homomorphism χ:G→U(1), with ∣χ(g)∣=1.
For χ∈G, the character group of a finite abelian group, put
∣vχ​⟩=∣G∣​1​∑g∈G​χ(g)​∣g⟩.
(3)
Changing variables to u=g+h gives
U(h)∣vχ​⟩=∣G∣​1​∑u​χ(u−h)​∣u⟩=χ(h)∣vχ​⟩​.
(4)
The ∣G∣ vectors ∣vχ​⟩ are therefore a common eigenbasis. Using χ(g) instead of its complex conjugate in their definition reverses every eigenphase; this is merely the quantum Fourier transform sign convention.

Measure the coset state in the common eigenbasis {∣vχ​⟩} of the group shift operators. Its overlap is
⟨vχ​∣g0​+K⟩=∣G∣∣K∣​χ(g0​)​∑k∈K​χ(k).
(5)
The character-sum cancellation lemma makes this sum ∣K∣ when χ is trivial on K, and zero otherwise. Hence, with K⊥ the annihilator of a subgroup of a finite abelian group,
Pr(χ)={∣K∣/∣G∣,0,​χ∈K⊥,χ∈/K⊥.​​
(6)

(9)

Ul​∣emN/r​⟩=e−2πiml/r∣emN/r​⟩.​
(10)
Consequently measurement in this common eigenbasis returns each m with probability 1/r; the corresponding eigenvalue of U1​ is a uniformly sampled rth root of unity. A single measured phase −m/r reveals the denominator r/gcd(m,r), which need not be r. Repeated samples permit exact period recovery from a Fourier sample, for example by taking the least common multiple of their reduced denominators. The simultaneous eigenspaces and their probabilities depend on r, not on the particular labels f(j).

Let Π be the orthogonal projection onto the good linear subspace G, and let ∣ψ⟩ be a prepared unit vector with p=⟨ψ∣Π∣ψ⟩. For 0<p<1, define
sinθ=p​,∣g⟩=p​Π∣ψ⟩​,∣b⟩=1−p​(I−Π)∣ψ⟩​.
(11)
The amplitude amplification theorem states that, if the reflection operators about the initial state and the good subspace can be implemented, the iteration
Q=(2∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣−I)(I−2Π)
(12)
satisfies
Qj∣ψ⟩=sin((2j+1)θ)∣g⟩+cos((2j+1)θ)∣b⟩.​
(13)
To prove it, the two reflections preserve the linear span of ∣g⟩,∣b⟩. In this ordered orthonormal basis, direct multiplication gives
Q=(cos2θ−sin2θ​sin2θcos2θ​).
(14)
Multiplying this rotation matrix by (sinα,cosα)T replaces α by α+2θ, proving the formula by mathematical induction.
For known 0<p≤1/2, choose j to be a nearest nonnegative integer to π/(4θ)−1/2. Then ∣(2j+1)θ−π/2∣≤θ, so measurement finds the good subspace with probability at least 1−p, using O(p−1/2) iterations. For p>1/2, measurement of the initial state already has constant success probability. If p=1 success is certain; if p=0 these reflections cannot create any good component. If ∣ψ⟩=A∣0⟩, the initial-state reflection is A(2∣0⟩⟨0∣−I)A†, implemented using quantum state preparation and its inverse quantum circuit.

An injective self-map of the finite set ZN​ is a bijection. For sufficiently large N, exactly
m=⌊N1/4⌋+1
(21)
outputs satisfy the integer comparison, so there are exactly m good inputs. Prepare the uniform superposition state ∣ψ⟩=N−1/2∑x​∣x⟩, whose good probability is
p=Nm​=Θ(N−3/4).
(22)
Use the phase oracle from the previous part and the reflection 2∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣−I. The amplitude amplification theorem requires O(p−1/2) iterations; each uses two Uf​ queries, while the state preparation and its reflection are independent of f. With the nearest-integer iteration count from that theorem, the failure probability is at most p, tending to zero. Therefore the success probability eventually exceeds 0.9, with
O(N3/8) queries to Uf​.​
(23)
This is a bound on quantum query complexity; implementation costs of the known gates are not counted as oracle queries.

The printed sum contains Zn​ although the stated labels end at n−1. We use the natural periodic convention Zn​=Z0​, with n≥3. If an open chain was intended, omitting the final term gives the same asymptotic bound.
Set Hj​=Xj−1​Zj​, with indices modulo n, and implement the product-formula Hamiltonian simulation
U=(∏j=1n​e−iHj​/k)k.
(27)
Each factor acts on two qubits, so it is a two-qubit unitary operator. More explicitly, if a=j−1, b=j, and Ca→b​ is a controlled-NOT gate,
e−iδXa​Zb​=Ha​Ca→b​e−iδZb​Ca→b​Ha​,
(28)
where Ha​ is a Hadamard gate. This gives a constant number of one-qubit and two-qubit gates per factor; one-qubit gates can also be viewed as two-qubit gates tensored with the identity.
The spectral norm obeys the triangle inequality, submultiplicativity of the operator norm, and invariance under multiplication by unitary operators. In particular, the telescoping bound for products of operators gives ∥Vk−Wk∥≤k∥V−W∥ for unitary V,W. The first-order unitary product-formula error bound consequently yields
∥U−U∥≤2k1​∑j<l​∥[Hj​,Hl​]∥.
(29)
Only neighboring terms can have a nonzero commutator, because all other supports are disjoint. There are n neighboring unordered pairs, and ∥[Hj​,Hl​]∥≤2∥Hj​∥∥Hl​∥=2. Thus ∥U−U∥≤n/k. Choosing k>n/ε gives
O(n2/ε) gates,degree 2 in n for fixed ε.​
(30)
The coarser bound that counts all (2n​) pairs also proves the often-used O(n3/ε) construction. Locality improves that cubic estimate to the quadratic bound above; neither assertion is a lower bound on the best possible circuit.

The usual sufficient input promises for the HHL algorithm are efficient quantum state preparation of the nonzero vector b, efficient access to a sparse matrix A with at most poly(n) nonzero entries per row, and a condition number κ=λmax​/λmin​=poly(n) after an efficiently known normalization. Both the positions and values of the nonzero matrix elements must be computable coherently in polynomial time. More generally, efficient Hamiltonian simulation can replace the sparsity promise. We assume A is invertible; if zero eigenvalues occur, one must instead restrict b to their orthogonal complement and specify the desired inverse on that support.
With a=λmax​ and a known bound κ, choose c=a/κ≤λmin​. The HHL controlled reciprocal rotation then succeeds with probability
psucc​=c2∥A−1∣b⟩∥2≥a2c2​=κ−2​.
(35)
This is the required inverse-polynomial lower bound. In the usual approximate algorithm, inverse-polynomial requested error also gives polynomial runtime under these input promises. The original HHL paper states the dependence on sparsity, conditioning, and accuracy.
There is a qualification for a literally exact version. Representability of the eigenvalues in n bits does not itself make exact quantum phase estimation efficient: using e2πiA requires controlled powers corresponding to evolution times as large as 2π2n−1. Under the paper's idealization we may describe their exact action, but polynomial runtime for that exact circuit additionally requires efficient implementations of those controlled powers, or equivalent efficient exact spectral access. Assuming an operation executes exactly does not bound its cost. This distinction is recorded in cost of exact phase estimation on a dyadic spectrum.

Write the normalized input as ∣b⟩=∑j​βj​∣uj​⟩, where A∣uj​⟩=λj​∣uj​⟩ and λj​>0. Use a data register, an n-qubit eigenvalue register, and a flag quantum ancilla. Begin with ∣b⟩∣0n⟩∣0⟩.
Apply exact quantum phase estimation to e2πiA. Its controlled evolutions and inverse quantum Fourier transform produce
∑j​βj​∣uj​⟩∣ℓj​⟩∣0⟩,λj​=ℓj​/2n.
(36)
Apply the HHL controlled reciprocal rotation, with 0<c≤λmin​:
∣ℓj​⟩∣0⟩⟼∣ℓj​⟩(1−c2/λj2​​∣0⟩+λj​c​∣1⟩).
(37)
The function of ℓj​ is computed by quantum arithmetic; the rotation is a quantum variable rotation. On a zero eigenvalue label with no input amplitude, define any unitary action, for example no rotation.
Run the inverse phase-estimation circuit. Because each amplitude multiplier depends only on λj​, the eigenvalue register is reset to ∣0n⟩ on both flag branches. Measuring the flag and conditioning on outcome one leaves
∣ξ⟩=∑j​∣βj​∣2λj−2​​∑j​βj​λj−1​∣uj​⟩​=∥A−1b∥A−1b​.​
(38)
The successful unnormalized branch is cA−1∣b⟩, giving the probability in the preceding part. The operations independent of A,b are the Hadamard gates preparing the phase register, the inverse quantum Fourier transform, the reversible reciprocal/angle arithmetic, the controlled fixed-angle rotations, and the final flag measurement. Uncomputation removes all arithmetic workspace; the A-dependent controlled evolutions and the b-dependent state-preparation circuit supply the input-specific operations.

Use a reversible circuit T to compute an L=O(n)-bit representation of the angle into a workspace register, with all additional work bits retained:
∣x⟩∣0L⟩∣0⟩T​∣x⟩∣θx​⟩∣0⟩.
(39)
Write that representation as θx​=∑l=1L​bl​(x)αl​, with known binary place values αl​ (including the fixed scale and any integer bit). On the target, apply a controlled unitary gate Ry​(2αl​) controlled by each angle bit bl​. Rotations about the same axis add their angles, so their product is
∏l=1L​Ry​(2bl​αl​)=Ry​(2θx​),Ry​(2θx​)∣0⟩=cosθx​∣0⟩+sinθx​∣1⟩.
(40)
Finally apply T† to perform uncomputation. The workspace returns to zero while the control register and rotated target remain unchanged; the construction works coherently on every superposition of x.
A polynomial time classical calculation has a polynomial-size reversible circuit with Toffoli gates, and each Toffoli gate has a constant-size decomposition into one-qubit and two-qubit gates. The L controlled rotations are themselves two-qubit gates. Thus the total quantum circuit size is poly(n). This is the binary-angle implementation of a quantum variable rotation; the stipulated angle precision is understood.
