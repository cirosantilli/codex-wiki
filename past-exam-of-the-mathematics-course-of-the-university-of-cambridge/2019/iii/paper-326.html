A regularization of an inverse problem is a family of continuous maps Rα​:V→U, α>0, such that
Rα​f⟶A†f(α↓0)
(1)
for every f in the domain of the Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator. A linear regularization has each Rα​ bounded and linear. For example, Tikhonov regularization gives
Rα​=(A∗A+αI)−1A∗.
(2)
A nonlinear example is quartic-norm variational regularization:
Rα​f=argminu​{∥Au−f∥2+α∥u∥4}.
(3)
Its strictly convex norm penalty selects the same minimum-norm least-squares solution as α↓0, but the map from data to minimizer is generally nonlinear.
A convergent regularization of an inverse problem additionally has a parameter rule α(δ,fδ​)>0 such that, for every admissible exact f,
∥fδ​−f∥≤δsup​∥Rα(δ,fδ​)​fδ​−A†f∥⟶0(δ↓0).​
(4)
An a priori regularization parameter choice depends only on δ and fixed information about the problem.

Let u†=A†f. Linearity and the triangle inequality give the noise-bias decomposition for linear regularization
∥Rα(δ)​fδ​−u†∥≤∥Rα(δ)​(fδ​−f)∥+∥Rα(δ)​f−u†∥≤δ∥Rα(δ)​∥+∥Rα(δ)​f−A†f∥.
(5)
The first term tends to zero by the assumed noise-amplification bound. The second tends to zero because α(δ)→0 and Rα​ is a regularization of an inverse problem. The right side is independent of the particular noisy datum within its allowed ball. Its convergence therefore proves the uniform noisy-data convergence required of a convergent regularization of an inverse problem.

Use the singular system of a compact operator convention Axj​=σj​yj​ and A∗yj​=σj​xj​. Starting from zero gives no component in kerA. Write x(t)=∑j​cj​(t)xj​. The coefficient equations are
cj′​=−σj2​cj​+σj​⟨f,yj​⟩,cj​(0)=0.
(6)
Solving these scalar linear ordinary differential equations gives
cj​(t)=σj​1−e−σj2​t​⟨f,yj​⟩.
(7)
Consequently asymptotic regularization has the spectral regularization method representation
uα​=j∑​σj​1−e−σj2​/α​⟨f,yj​⟩xj​.​
(8)
The component of f orthogonal to the closure of the range of A is annihilated by A∗ and contributes nothing. The scalar filter satisfies (1−e−σ2/α)/σ≤min(σ/α,1/σ)≤α−1/2, so every fixed-α reconstruction is a bounded linear operator. Its long-time limit is the Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator on admissible data.

For a Banach space X, weak convergence uk​⇀u means
ℓ(uk​)→ℓ(u)for every ℓ∈X∗.
(9)
For its continuous dual space X∗, convergence in the weak-star topology means
ℓk​(x)→ℓ(x)for every x∈X.
(10)
Weak convergence on X∗ tests against all of X∗∗, whereas weak-star convergence tests only against the canonical image of X. Thus weak convergence always implies weak-star convergence. If X is a reflexive Banach space, that image is all of X∗∗, so the weak and weak-star topologies on X∗ coincide. Weak-star topology is defined relative to a chosen predual; it is not an extra intrinsic topology on an arbitrary Banach space without such an identification.

The Radon-Riesz property of a norm is the implication
uk​⇀u,∥uk​∥→∥u∥⟹∥uk​−u∥→0.
(11)
In a Hilbert space, expand the square using its inner product:
∥uk​−u∥2=∥uk​∥2+∥u∥2−2Re⟨uk​,u⟩.
(12)
Weak convergence gives ⟨uk​,u⟩→∥u∥2, and convergence of norms gives ∥uk​∥2→∥u∥2. Hence the right side tends to zero. This proves the Hilbert-space Radon-Riesz theorem.

The assumptions prove convergence to the set of J-minimizing solutions, and subsequential convergence to a member of that set. Convergence to a single fixed member requires uniqueness or a suitable selection rule.
Let u† be any J-minimizing solution, whose existence is granted. It is feasible for every noisy-data constraint because Au†=f and ∥f−fδ​∥≤δ. Minimality therefore gives J(uδ​)≤J(u†). All reconstructions lie in this strongly sequentially compact space sublevel set. For any sequence δk​↓0, pass to a subsequence with uδk​​→u∗​. Boundedness of A and the triangle inequality imply
∥Auδk​​−f∥≤∥Auδk​​−fδk​​∥+∥fδk​​−f∥≤2δk​,
(13)
so Au∗​=f. By sequential lower semicontinuity,
J(u∗​)≤liminfk​J(uδk​​)≤J(u†).
(14)
Thus u∗​ is a J-minimizing solution. The same compactness argument shows the distance to a set of exact minimizers tends to zero: otherwise a subsequence at a fixed positive distance would converge to an exact minimizer, a contradiction. If that minimizer is unique, every convergent subsequence has the same limit, giving the asserted full strong convergence.
For a counterexample to full convergence under the printed hypotheses alone, take U=V=R, A=0, f=fδ​=0, and J(u)=max{∣u∣−1,0}. This is nonnegative and continuous, with compact sublevel sets [−1−C,1+C] for C≥0. Every point of [−1,1] is both an exact and a residual-method minimizer. Choosing u1/k​=(−1)k gives a valid sequence of minimizers that does not converge. The reusable corrected statement is compact-sublevel convergence of the residual method.

For u∈L1(Ω), the total variation seminorm on a domain is
TV(u)=supφ∈Cc∞​(Ω;Rn)∥φ∥∞​≤1​​∫Ω​udivφdx.
(15)
Using Cc1​ instead gives the same definition. The zero test field shows nonnegativity, and TV(0)=0, so this is a proper extended-real function. Each test field defines a linear functional of u; taking their supremum proves convexity, equivalently
TV(tu+(1−t)v)≤tTV(u)+(1−t)TV(v),0≤t≤1.
(16)
It is not strictly convex: two distinct constant functions both have zero total variation, as does every convex combination of them.
The function of bounded variation on a domain space is
BV(Ω)={u∈L1(Ω):TV(u)<∞},∥u∥BV​=∥u∥L1​+TV(u).
(17)
Total variation is not coercive on BV(Ω): for uk​≡k on a domain of positive measure, ∥uk​∥BV​=k∣Ω∣→∞, whereas TV(uk​)=0. This is noncoercivity of total variation on constants; a mean-zero constraint can remove the constant obstruction.

An absolutely one-homogeneous functional satisfies
J(cu)=∣c∣J(u)for every scalar c,
(18)
with J(0)=0. The printed strictly positive codomain cannot hold at zero; the standard interpretation is a nonnegative, possibly extended-real functional.
For p∈∂J(u), apply the subgradient inequality at v=0:
0=J(0)≥J(u)−⟨p,u⟩,
(19)
so ⟨p,u⟩≥J(u). Apply it at v=2u and use one-homogeneity:
2J(u)=J(2u)≥J(u)+⟨p,u⟩,
(20)
which gives the reverse inequality. Therefore
J(u)=⟨p,u⟩.​
(21)
This is the Euler identity for a convex one-homogeneous functional; differentiability is unnecessary.

Use the interior-domain definition of total variation seminorm on a domain, so there is no boundary-extension term at ±1. For any admissible scalar test function φ,
∫−11​uR​(x)φ′(x)dx=∫−RR​φ′(x)dx=φ(R)−φ(−R)≤2.
(22)
Thus TV(uR​)≤2.
Choose 0<ε<min{R,1−R}. From the supplied smooth bump function, define
φ(x)=eωε​(x−R)−eωε​(x+R).
(23)
The two supports are disjoint and inside (−1,1). Each bump has maximum e−1, so ∥φ∥∞​≤1, while φ(R)=1 and φ(−R)=−1. This attains the upper bound, proving
TV(uR​)=2.​
(24)
Equivalently, its distributional derivative is DuR​=δ−R​−δR​, with total variation two. This is total variation of an interval indicator.

For a convex functional J and p∈∂J(v), the generalized Bregman distance is
DJp​(u,v)=J(u)−J(v)−⟨p,u−v⟩≥0.​
(25)
The inequality is exactly the subgradient inequality.
Suppose J is strictly convex and u=v, with both values finite. If the distance vanished, the supporting affine function at v would also meet J at u. At w=(1−t)v+tu, 0<t<1, the subgradient inequality would give
J(w)≥J(v)+t⟨p,u−v⟩=(1−t)J(v)+tJ(u),
(26)
contradicting strict convexity. Hence zero distance implies u=v.
Without strict convexity, take J(s)=∣s∣, v=1, and p=1∈∂J(1). For every u≥0,
DJ1​(u,1)=u−1−(u−1)=0,
(27)
including u=2=1. Thus zero Bregman distance need not identify a point. It identifies points on the same supporting face, as explained by zero Bregman distance and supporting faces.

The source condition in variational regularization at an exact solution u† is the existence of a source element μ†∈V∗ such that
A∗μ†∈∂J(u†).​
(28)
For a Hilbert data space one identifies V∗ with V. If u satisfies Au=f and A∗μ∈∂J(u), then every other exact solution v obeys the subgradient inequality
J(v)≥J(u)+⟨A∗μ,v−u⟩=J(u)+⟨μ,Av−Au⟩=J(u).
(29)
Hence u is a J-minimizing solution. This is source-condition certification of a penalty-minimizing solution.

Let u†=uJ†​, p†=A∗μ†∈∂J(u†), and rα​=Auα​−f. The subgradient inequality, the dual pairing bound, and Au†=f give
J(uα​)−J(u†)≥⟨p†,uα​−u†⟩=⟨μ†,rα​⟩≥−∥μ†∥∥rα​∥.
(30)
Minimality of uα​ in the exact penalty method gives
∥rα​∥+α[J(uα​)−J(u†)]≤0.
(31)
Combining them yields
(1−α∥μ†∥)∥rα​∥≤0.
(32)
The coefficient is strictly positive, so
Auα​=f.​
(33)
Now minimality gives J(uα​)≤J(u†), and exact feasibility plus the defining minimality of u† gives the reverse inequality. Thus uα​ is a J-minimizing solution. Finally,
DJp†​(uα​,u†)=J(uα​)−J(u†)−⟨μ†,A(uα​−u†)⟩=0.​
(34)
This exact-penalty threshold from a source condition does not require one-homogeneity; convexity and the source subgradient suffice. If μ†=0, the same argument works for every α>0, interpreting the upper threshold as infinite.
