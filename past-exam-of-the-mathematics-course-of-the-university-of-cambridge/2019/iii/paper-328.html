The Wirtinger derivative uz​=(ux​−iuy​)/2 is holomorphic, since ∂zˉ​uz​=Δu/4=0 by the Laplace equation. The conformal map w=z2 takes the quadrant to the complex upper half-plane. Integrating the prescribed tangential boundary derivatives, choose a common corner value c and define the real boundary function

(1)
The decaying data are bounded under the usual regularity at the corner, so f(s)=O(1+∣s∣​). Thus its Poisson integral converges. A holomorphic function whose real part is that Poisson integral is supplied by the regularized Schwarz integral formula:
W(w)=πi1​∫−∞∞​f(s)(s−w1​−1+s2s​)ds.

For u∗​(z)=ReW(z2), the chain rule gives (u∗​)z​=zW′(z2). Apply integration by parts to obtain

(3)
Substituting s=r2 and s=−r2 gives the requested particular integral representation:
(u∗​)z​(z)=πiz​[∫0∞​r2−z2g2​(r)​dr+∫0∞​r2+z2g1​(r)​dr].​

Both integrals converge absolutely for an interior point of the quadrant. The Sokhotski–Plemelj formula gives Re(u∗​)z​(x)=g2​(x)/2 on the horizontal edge and Im(u∗​)z​(iy)=−g1​(y)/2 on the vertical edge, verifying the prescribed derivatives.
The printed conditions alone do not determine uz​ uniquely. The general answer adds a holomorphic function H satisfying
ReH(x)=0,ImH(iy)=0,x,y>0.
(5)
Equivalently, write H(z)=ih(z2)/z, where h is holomorphic in the complex upper half-plane with real boundary values on the nonzero real axis. An antiderivative of 2H has a harmonic real part with zero tangential boundary derivatives. For example, u=xy contributes H=−iz/2 without changing either datum. If the two edge constants differ by c2​−c1​, the angular harmonic function contributes i(c2​−c1​)/(πz). The boxed expression selects the Poisson integral representative with equal edge constants; regularity and suitable growth conditions can be used to select that representative.

Use the weighted Neumann heat kernel with constant drift. The spatial operator has the self-adjoint form
Lu=uxx​+βux​=e−βx(eβxux​)x​,
(6)
so its homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions give a regular Sturm-Liouville problem in the weighted inner product
⟨f1​,f2​⟩β​=∫0L​eβxf1​(x)​f2​(x)dx.
(7)
The constant eigenfunction is ϕ0​=1, with eigenvalue λ0​=0 and squared norm N0​=(eβL−1)/β. For k≥1, put qk​=kπ/L. Substitution of ϕ=e−βx/2v in Lϕ=−λϕ gives v′′+(λ−β2/4)v=0 and the Robin boundary conditions v′=βv/2 at both endpoints. Hence
ϕk​(x)=e−βx/2[cos(qk​x)+2qk​β​sin(qk​x)],λk​=qk2​+4β2​.
(8)
Indeed,
ϕk′​(x)=−e−βx/2(qk​+4qk​β2​)sin(qk​x),Nk​=∫0L​eβxϕk​(x)2dx=2L​(1+4qk2​β2​).

Kβ​(x,y,t)=N0​1​+∑k=1∞​Nk​e−λk​t​ϕk​(x)ϕk​(y),t>0.

For a coefficient ak​(t)=⟨ϕk​,u(⋅,t)⟩β​/Nk​, two applications of integration by parts yield

u(x,t)=∫0L​Kβ​(x,y,t)eβyu0​(y)dy+∫0t​[eβLKβ​(x,L,t−s)h(s)−Kβ​(x,0,t−s)g(s)]ds.​
(12)
This depends only on the given data. In particular, the zero eigenvalue retains the changing weighted mean:
dtd​∫0L​eβxu(x,t)dx=eβLh(t)−g(t).
(13)
The endpoint derivatives of the boundary integral are understood as interior limits. Differentiating each homogeneous Neumann eigenfunction at the endpoint before summing would incorrectly discard the prescribed boundary forcing: the short-time heat kernel makes that interchange invalid. The stated compatibility conditions give the classical boundary traces.

Apply the time Laplace transform to the Airy equation. Extend g past T in any suitable way and write
U(x,p)=∫0∞​e−ptu(x,t)dt,G(p)=∫0∞​e−ptg(t)dt.
(14)
For example, setting g=0 after T makes G(p)=∫0T​e−ptg(t)dt; the final solution for t<T is independent of this extension. The Laplace transform of a derivative gives
Uxxx​+pU=u0​(x),Ux​(0,p)=G(p).
(15)
Take the principal cube root λ=p1/3 for Rep>0. The three characteristic roots of the spatial ordinary differential equation are
−λ,r1​=λeiπ/3,r2​=λe−iπ/3.
(16)
Since ∣argλ∣<π/6, only −λ has negative real part. Thus spatial decay leaves just one homogeneous exponential, which the single prescribed Neumann boundary condition determines.
The Airy resolvent kernel on the whole real line is
Rp​(s)=⎩⎨⎧​3λ2e−λs​,−3r12​er1​s​−3r22​er2​s​,​s≥0,s<0.​
(17)
It decays at both ends, is continuous together with its first derivative, and satisfies Rp′′​(0+)−Rp′′​(0−)=1. Consequently (∂s3​+p)Rp​=δ0​ as a distribution. A particular solution is the Green-function representation
V(x,p)=∫0∞​Rp​(x−y)u0​(y)dy.
(18)
Adding the decaying homogeneous mode to impose the boundary derivative gives
U(x,p)=V(x,p)+λe−λx​[Vx​(0,p)−G(p)],Vx​(0,p)=∫0∞​Rp′​(−y)u0​(y)dy.
(19)
Every term is known. If u0​(r)=∫0∞​e−ryu0​(y)dy denotes the spatial Laplace transform, then
Vx​(0,p)=−31​[r1​u0​(r1​)​+r2​u0​(r2​)​].
(20)
The Bromwich inversion formula now gives the required integral representation:
u(x,t)=2πi1​∫γ−i∞γ+i∞​ept{∫0∞​Rp​(x−y)u0​(y)dy+p1/3e−p1/3x​[∫0∞​Rp′​(−y)u0​(y)dy−G(p)]}dp.​
(21)
Here γ>0 is to the right of any singularities required by the growth of the data. The usual decay or growth hypotheses are understood for this Laplace transform construction; when absolute inversion is unavailable, the vertical integral is interpreted as the limit of truncated Bromwich contours. The transformed ordinary differential equation verifies the partial differential equation and initial condition, while differentiating at x=0 gives exactly G(p) and hence ux​(0,t)=g(t). The derivative compatibility u0′​(0)=g(0) makes the two data agree at the corner.
