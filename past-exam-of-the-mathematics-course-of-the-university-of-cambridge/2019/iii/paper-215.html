1 OurBigBook.com
Start the random-to-top card shuffle from a fixed ordering and let T be the first time every card has been selected. Once a card has been selected, its location relative to the other selected cards is determined by their most recent selection times. At T these times have a uniformly random strict order, so the deck is uniform and independent of the event {T=t}. Thus T is a strong stationary time, and
dTV​(t)≤P(T>t).
(1)
The coupon collector problem gives T=nlogn+OP​(n), so for c→∞,
dTV​(nlogn+cn)⟶0.
(2)
Before all cards have been selected, the unselected cards form the bottom block of the deck in their original relative order. At time nlogn−cn, with c→∞ sufficiently slowly, the number U of unselected cards tends to infinity in probability. Choose k→∞ with P(U≥k)→1. The bottom k cards are then in their original relative order, whereas under the uniform distribution this has probability 1/k!. Hence
dTV​(nlogn−cn)⟶1.
(3)
The random-to-top shuffle therefore has cutoff for Markov chains at nlogn with window O(n).
The top-to-random card shuffle is the time reversal of the random-to-top card shuffle under the uniform stationary distribution. Equivalently, their step distributions on the symmetric group are carried into one another by permutation inversion, which preserves total variation from uniform. Their mixing profiles agree, so top-to-random has the same cutoff at nlogn with window O(n).

Use the monotone grand coupling in every coordinate: at each step use the same attempted move for chains started from the bottom and top states, censoring moves that leave the interval. The two copies coalesce after the lower copy has accumulated enough upward drift and boundary censoring has removed their initial separation. Away from the boundary, one step has mean displacement
31​−61​=61​.
(9)
Standard exponential concentration for sums of bounded independent increments shows that for every fixed C>6 the one-coordinate coupling time τ satisfies
P(τ>Cn)≤e−cC​n.
(10)
All other initial states lie between the extremal copies. Coupling the d coordinates independently and using the union bound gives
P(some coordinate has not coupled by Cn)≤de−cC​n=o(1),
(11)
because logd=o(n). The coupling inequality for total variation therefore gives
tmix​=O(n).​

Take d=1 and start from the lower endpoint. The stationary distribution of this birth-death chain satisfies detailed balance with
π(k)π(k+1)​=1/61/3​=2,
(13)
so it is concentrated within O(1) of the upper endpoint n. Before reaching that region the walk has drift 1/6. The weak law of large numbers and exponential concentration therefore imply that its hitting time of n−O(1) is
6n+OP​(n​).
(14)
At time (6−ε)n the chain is still macroscopically below the stationary region with probability tending to one, so its total-variation distance tends to one. Under the monotone coupling from part (b), by time (6+ε)n the extremal copies have coalesced with probability tending to one, so the distance tends to zero. Therefore the family has
cutoff at 6n with an O(n​) window.​

Let P be a finite reversible Markov chain with stationary distribution π, and write Q(e)=π(u)P(u,v) for an oriented transition edge e=(u,v). For every ordered pair (x,y) choose a directed path γxy​ from x to y using positive-capacity edges, and define the congestion
ρ=maxe​Q(e)1​∑x,y:e∈γxy​​π(x)π(y)∣γxy​∣.
(22)
Then the Canonical paths comparison theorem gives the Poincaré inequality
Varπ​(f)≤ρE(f,f)​
(23)
for every real function f, and hence the spectral gap is at least 1/ρ.
Indeed,
Varπ​(f)=21​∑x,y​π(x)π(y)(f(x)−f(y))2.
(24)
Write each difference as the sum of edge differences along γxy​ and apply Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:
(f(x)−f(y))2≤∣γxy​∣∑e∈γxy​​(∇e​f)2.
(25)
Interchanging the pair and edge sums, then applying the definition of ρ, bounds the result by
2ρ​∑e​Q(e)(∇e​f)2=ρE(f,f).
(26)
