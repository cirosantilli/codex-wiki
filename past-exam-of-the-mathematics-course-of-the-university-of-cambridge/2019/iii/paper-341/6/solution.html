A Runge-Kutta method with stage matrix A, weights b and step size h is
Yi​=yn​+h∑j​aij​f(Yj​),yn+1​=yn​+h∑i​bi​f(Yi​).
(22)
Its order measures accuracy as h→0, while stability concerns how errors or stiff components propagate. Linear A-stability, strong stiff damping through L-stability, and nonlinear B-stability address different questions.
For the Dahlquist test equation y′=λy, elimination of the stages gives the stability function
R(z)=1+zbT(I−zA)−11,z=hλ.
(23)
The linear stability domain is {z:∣R(z)∣≤1}. The method is A-stable when it contains the closed left half-plane, and L-stable when it is also true that R(z)→0 as ∣z∣→∞ there. These conditions describe, respectively, stability for every non-growing scalar linear mode and damping of very rapidly decaying modes.
The Forward Euler method has R(z)=1+z, so it fails A-stability, for example at z=−3. In fact no nontrivial explicit Runge-Kutta method is A-stable: its stability function is a nonconstant polynomial, which is unbounded along the negative real axis. The Backward Euler method has R(z)=1/(1−z). For Rez≤0, ∣1−z∣≥1, and the function tends to zero at infinity, proving both A-stability and L-stability. The implicit midpoint rule has R(z)=(1+z/2)/(1−z/2). The identity ∣1−z/2∣2−∣1+z/2∣2=−2Rez proves A-stability, but its limit −1 rules out L-stability.
For nonlinear equations, a dissipative vector field satisfies ⟨f(x)−f(y),x−y⟩≤0. The corresponding exact solutions contract because the time derivative of their squared distance is twice that inner product. A B-stable method preserves this contraction for every h>0, whenever its stages are well defined.
Let d=yn​−y​n​, Di​=Yi​−Yi​, and Fi​=f(Yi​)−f(Yi​). Expanding the update's squared norm, substituting d=Di​−h∑j​aij​Fj​, and symmetrizing the double sum proves the Runge-Kutta contractivity identity
∥dn+1​∥2−∥d∥2=2h∑i​bi​⟨Di​,Fi​⟩−h2∑i,j​mij​⟨Fi​,Fj​⟩,mij​=bi​aij​+bj​aji​−bi​bj​.
(24)
If bi​≥0 and the matrix M=(mij​) is a positive semidefinite matrix, the first sum is nonpositive by dissipativity. The second double sum is nonnegative: sum the quadratic form of M over each coordinate of the Fi​. Thus algebraic stability implies B-stability. Applied to complex scalar linear equations with the real part of the Hermitian inner product, the same argument also implies A-stability, where the stages are defined.
The Backward Euler method has b1​=a11​=1, hence M=(1), and is algebraically stable. One can prove its nonlinear contraction directly: dn+1​=d+h(f(yn+1​)−f(y​n+1​)) gives ∥dn+1​∥2≤⟨d,dn+1​⟩≤∥d∥∥dn+1​∥. The implicit midpoint rule has a11​=1/2, b1​=1 and M=0, so it is B-stable even though it is not L-stable.
Linear A-stability does not by itself imply nonlinear B-stability. The ODE trapezoidal rule has the same stability function as the implicit midpoint rule, but take the dissipative scalar field f(y)=−max(y,0) and h=6. For any positive starting value, its trapezoidal update is yn+1​=yn​+3(f(yn​)+f(yn+1​))=−2yn​<0. Thus two positive starting values have their distance doubled, violating B-stability. Its algebraic stability matrix also has a negative first diagonal entry. The field is globally Lipschitz continuous, so this is a well-defined nonlinear counterexample.
These examples distinguish accuracy, scalar stiff-mode stability, nonlinear contraction, and rapid stiff-mode damping. For implicit methods, solvability of the stage equations remains an additional requirement; a formal stability function alone does not establish it for arbitrary nonlinear problems.
