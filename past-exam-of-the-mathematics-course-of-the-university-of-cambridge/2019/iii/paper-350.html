Let π0​ be the probability density function of the unknown and ρ that of the noise, both relative to Lebesgue measure. By independence of random variables, the conditional distribution of the measurement given u has density ρ(m−Au). Thus its likelihood function and the joint density are
π(m∣u)=ρ(m−Au),π(u,m)=π0​(u)ρ(m−Au).
(1)
The Bayesian model evidence is the marginal data density
Z(m)=∫Rd​π0​(v)ρ(m−Av)dv.
(2)
Bayes' theorem gives the posterior density solving this Bayesian inverse problem:
πm(u)=Z(m)π0​(u)ρ(m−Au)​.​
(3)
This formula applies when 0<Z(m)<∞. Since ∫Z(m)dm=1 by Tonelli theorem, these conditions hold almost everywhere under the marginal data law. Values of a conditional distribution at exceptional data are not determined by the joint law. The posterior mean or a maximum a posteriori estimate can then give a point estimate, while the full posterior density describes the remaining uncertainty.

A simple choice is an independent Laplace distribution at each pixel, giving a Laplace prior for sparse images:
π0​(u)=(2λ​)dexp(−λj=1∑d​∣uj​∣),λ>0.​
(20)
Its negative log density penalizes the L1 norm. Relative to a normal distribution, it combines a sharp peak at zero with heavier tails, allowing most pixels to lie near the background level while a few have appreciable intensity. Quantitatively, P(∣uj​∣>τ)=e−λτ, so the expected number of appreciable pixels is de−λτ. Choosing λτ large makes this small. This is a simple sparsity model: it does not enforce that nearby active pixels form connected objects, nor does its continuous density give exact zero pixels positive probability. Additional spatial modelling would be needed to insist on contiguous shapes.
To sample, draw independent Uj​∼U(0,1) and use inverse transform sampling:
uj​={λ−1log(2Uj​),−λ−1log(2(1−Uj​)),​0<Uj​≤21​,21​<Uj​<1.​​
(21)
The endpoint events have probability zero and can be assigned arbitrary finite values. To justify the rule, the cumulative distribution function of a centered Laplace distribution is
F(x)={21​eλx,1−21​e−λx,​x≤0,x≥0.​
(22)
The displayed rule is F−1(Uj​), so P(uj​≤x)=P(Uj​≤F(x))=F(x). Independence of random variables then gives the stated product prior distribution.

The Cameron-Martin space of a Gaussian measure μ=N(0,Σ) is
E=RanΣ1/2,⟨h,g⟩E​=⟨Σ−1/2h,Σ−1/2g⟩H​.​
(32)
The inverse is defined on the range of Σ1/2. In this infinite-dimensional setting “positive definite” must mean ⟨Σh,h⟩>0 for every nonzero h, rather than a uniform lower bound: a trace-class operator cannot be uniformly positive on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space.
By the spectral theorem for compact Hermitian operators, choose an orthonormal basis (ej​) with Σej​=λj​ej​, λj​>0, and ∑j​λj​<∞. (A strictly positive trace-class operator also forces the ambient Hilbert space to be separable.) In coordinates hj​=⟨h,ej​⟩,
E={h∈H:∑j​λj​hj2​​<∞},∥h∥E2​=∑j​λj​hj2​​.
(33)
This is a Hilbert space with its indicated norm, even though its range need not be closed in the ambient norm.
The Cameron-Martin theorem for a Gaussian measure states that the translated law μh​(B)=μ(B−h)=N(h,Σ)(B) is an equivalent probability measure to μ exactly when h∈E. For such h, its Radon-Nikodym derivative is
dμdμh​​(x)=exp(ℓh​(x)−21​∥h∥E2​),ℓh​(x)=N→∞lim​j=1∑N​λj​hj​xj​​.​
(34)
The series has mean-square convergence and converges almost surely under μ, since its independent summands have total variance ∑j​hj2​/λj​. Its law is N(0,∥h∥E2​), so the density has expected value one. It is sometimes formally written ℓh​(x)=⟨h,x⟩E​, but a typical infinite-dimensional Gaussian sample is not in E; the series interpretation is essential. If h∈/E, the two laws are mutually singular measures.

The notation P0​=N(0,I) denotes generalized Gaussian white noise over H=L2(R2), not a Gaussian measure supported on H: the identity is not a trace-class operator there. For example, realize the observation on a suitable space of tempered distributions. Its Cameron-Martin space of a Gaussian measure is H. For deterministic h∈H, the stochastic pairing Wm​(h) has normal distribution N(0,∥h∥H2​) under P0​ and is the isonormal Gaussian process indexed by H.
Interpret the stated maps between Sobolev spaces as bounded linear operators, as usual. In particular K:H→H2(R2)⊂H is bounded, so its adjoint operator K∗ is bounded on H. Hence A=K∗K maps H into H, which is the property needed here. The stronger smoothing follows by Sobolev duality: boundedness of K:H−4→H−2 gives K∗:H2→H4, and therefore A:H→H4⊂H. As Π(H)=1, the shift Au belongs to the noise Cameron-Martin space of a Gaussian measure for Π-almost every u.
The white-noise likelihood for a square-integrable shift, obtained from the Cameron-Martin theorem for a Gaussian measure in its white-noise form, is
L(u,m)=dP0​dPu​​(m)=exp(Wm​(Au)−21​∥Au∥H2​)=e−Φ(u;m),Φ(u;m)=21​∥Au∥H2​−Wm​(Au).
(35)
Here Pu​ is the law of Au+η. In an orthonormal basis (ej​) of H, with white-noise coordinates mj​, the pairing under P0​ is
Wm​(Au)=limN→∞​∑j=1N​mj​⟨Au,ej​⟩H​.
(36)
For fixed u this has mean-square convergence and converges almost surely, since ∑j​∣⟨Au,ej​⟩∣2=∥Au∥H2​. The stipulated joint measurability allows the likelihood to be used under ν0​(du,dm)=Π(du)P0​(dm). One must not replace this expression by a finite ∥m−Au∥H2​, because white noise is not H-valued. On the unbounded domain R2, membership of white noise in a global unweighted negative Sobolev space must not be assumed either; the stochastic pairing avoids that issue.
Let ν(du,dm)=Π(du)Pu​(dm) be the actual joint law. The shift formula gives ν≪ν0​ with Radon-Nikodym derivative L. Define
Z(m)=∫H​L(u,m)Π(du).
(37)
For each fixed admissible u, the moment-generating function of a normal distribution gives

By Tonelli theorem, ∫Z(m)P0​(dm)=1, so Z(m)<∞ for P0​-almost every m. The likelihood is finite and strictly positive for ν0​-almost every (u,m); Fubini's theorem then gives Z(m)>0 for P0​-almost every m. The marginal observation law is Z(m)P0​(dm) and is an equivalent probability measure to P0​.
The Bayes formula for a dominated observation model therefore defines
dΠdΠm​(u)=Z(m)1​exp(Wm​(Au)−21​∥Au∥L22​).​

Choose the common dominating probability measure ν=(μ+μ′)/2 and let p=dμ/dν, q=dμ′/dν be the Radon-Nikodym derivatives. The assumed second moments imply first-moment integrability by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Since Y is a separable Banach space, the measurable f is a strongly measurable function, and both expected values exist as Bochner integrals.
The difference of these Bochner integrals satisfies
∥Eμf−Eμ′f∥Y​​=​∫X​f(p−q)dν​Y​≤∫X​∥f∥Y​∣p​−q​∣(p​+q​)dν≤(∫X​∥f∥Y2​(p​+q​)2dν)1/2(∫X​(p​−q​)2dν)1/2.​
