A multiset C of subsets of [n] is a uniform cover of multiplicity k when each coordinate occurs in exactly k members. The uniform covers theorem states that every Euclidean body S⊆Rn satisfies
∣S∣k≤A∈C∏​∣SA​∣,​
(21)
where SA​ is the coordinate projection of a Euclidean body onto the coordinates in A.
We prove it by induction on n. Split the cover into C−, whose members omit n, and C+, whose members contain n. Exactly k members lie in C+. For a last-coordinate value x, let S(x)⊆Rn−1 be the corresponding slice. Removing n from the members of C+ and retaining the members of C− gives a k-uniform cover of [n−1]. The inductive hypothesis and Fubini's theorem give
∣S∣​=∫∣S(x)∣dx≤A∈C−∏​∣SA​∣1/k∫A∈C+∏​∣S(x)A∖{n}​∣1/kdx.​
(22)
Apply Hölder's inequality to the k factors in the integral. Since
∫∣S(x)A∖{n}​∣dx=∣SA​∣,
(23)
we obtain ∣S∣≤∏A∈C​∣SA​∣1/k, which is the result after taking the kth power. The one-dimensional base case is immediate.
The Bollobas--Thomason box theorem states that for every body S⊆Rn there is an axis-parallel box B such that
∣B∣=∣S∣and∣BA​∣≤∣SA​∣ for every A⊆[n].​
(24)
An irreducible uniform cover cannot be decomposed into two smaller uniform covers. There are only finitely many such covers of [n]: encode a cover by its multiplicity vector in N2n and apply the Dickson lemma.
Choose a componentwise minimal positive array (xA​)A⊆[n]​ satisfying
xA​≤∣SA​∣,∣S∣k≤∏A∈C​xA​
(25)
for every irreducible k-uniform cover C, together with
xA​≤∏i∈A​x{i}​.
(26)
The actual projection volumes are feasible by the uniform covers theorem, and finiteness gives a minimal array. Every uniform cover is a disjoint union of irreducible ones, so its cover inequality also holds for this array.
Minimality implies that, for each coordinate i, some tight uniform-cover inequality can be chosen whose cover contains the singleton {i}. Indeed, either such an inequality already blocks decreasing x{i}​, or a tight product inequality xA​=∏j∈A​x{j}​ does; in the latter case take a tight cover containing A and replace that occurrence of A by its singleton coordinates. Let these tight covers be Ci​, of multiplicities ki​, and let K=∑i​ki​. Their multiset union is a K-uniform cover. Removing one copy of every singleton leaves a (K−1)-uniform cover, so comparison of its cover inequality with the product of all the tight equalities yields
∏i=1n​x{i}​≤∣S∣.
(27)
The singleton cover gives the reverse inequality, hence
∏i​x{i}​=∣S∣.
(28)
For any A⊆[n], the one-uniform cover consisting of A and the singletons {i} for i∈/A now gives xA​≥∏i∈A​x{i}​. The defining product inequality gives the reverse bound. Thus all these quantities are equal. Taking the side lengths of B to be x{1}​,…,x{n}​ proves the theorem.
Finally suppose that the proper body S⊆R3 satisfies
∣S∣2=∣S12​∣∣S13​∣∣S23​∣.
(29)
This is equality in the three-dimensional Loomis--Whitney inequality. In its proof, equality must hold in both applications of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Their equality conditions force the three projection indicators to factor through one-dimensional measurable sets E1​,E2​,E3​, and force
S=E1​×E2​×E3​
(30)
up to a set of Lebesgue measure zero; this is Equality in the three-dimensional Loomis--Whitney inequality.
Because S is connected, each one-coordinate projection is connected and hence is an interval. Because S is a finite union of positive-volume axis-parallel boxes, a proper difference between S and the product of those three intervals would contain a positive-volume rectangular cell in a common finite subdivision. That would contradict equality up to measure zero. Consequently the equality is exact and
S is an axis-parallel box.​
(31)
