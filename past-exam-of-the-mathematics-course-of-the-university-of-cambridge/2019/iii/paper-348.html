For a cost c:X×Y→[0,+∞] that is a Borel measurable function, a transport map is a measurable T:X→Y whose pushforward measure satisfies
T#​μ=ν,μ(T−1(B))=ν(B)for every Borel B⊆Y.
(1)
The Monge optimal transport problem moves every source point to one destination:
T#​μ=νinf​M(T),M(T)=∫X​c(x,T(x))dμ(x).​
(2)
The Kantorovich optimal transport problem permits mass to split. Its admissible transport plans are the probability measures on X×Y with prescribed marginal distributions:
Π(μ,ν)={π:(pX​)#​π=μ, (pY​)#​π=ν},π∈Π(μ,ν)inf​K(π),K(π)=∫X×Y​cdπ.​
(3)
Thus a transport plan is a coupling of probability distributions. The set Π(μ,ν) is never empty: it contains the product measure μ⊗ν. Signed costs can also be used when their integrals are well defined, for example with an integrable lower bound of the form a(x)+b(y).
On the Polish space R, take the Dirac measures
μ=δ0​,ν=21​δ−1​+21​δ1​.​
(4)
Every measurable map satisfies T#​δ0​=δT(0)​, which cannot equal ν. A transport map cannot split an atom of a measure, whereas the transport plan 21​δ(0,−1)​+21​δ(0,1)​ can.

The intended monotone rearrangement is
T†(x)=G−1(F(x))μ-almost everywhere.​
(9)
Here G−1 is the quantile function of ν, agreeing with the ordinary inverse when G is continuous and strictly increasing. With an atomless measure μ, its cumulative distribution function F is continuous, and the probability integral transform makes F(X) uniform on (0,1) for X∼μ. Thus G−1(F(X))∼ν. The one-dimensional monotone rearrangement theorem says this transport map minimizes the cost d(x−y) for convex continuous d, whenever the cost integrals are well defined. Values at exceptional endpoints may be chosen arbitrarily.
The printed assumptions omit an essential source condition. Invertibility of G alone does not ensure an admissible transport map. For example, μ=δ0​ and ν a standard normal distribution satisfy the stated condition on G, but T#​δ0​ is always a Dirac measure. There is no solution to the Monge optimal transport problem in this example. The boxed answer therefore requires the additional assumption that μ is an atomless measure, or an equivalent condition making the displayed map admissible. For arbitrary sources the always admissible monotone transport plan is (F−1,G−1)#​U(0,1), which need not be induced by a map.

Interpret the invertibility assumption on G as continuity and strict increase on the relevant range, so that ν is an atomless measure. Suppose a non-decreasing transport map T with T#​μ=ν exists. Then μ is also an atomless measure: if μ({x})>0, the pushforward measure would give ν({T(x)})≥μ({x})>0. Thus F is continuous.
At any point x where the non-decreasing representative is defined, the definition of a monotone function gives
{z:z≤x}⊆{z:T(z)≤T(x)},{z:T(z)<T(x)}⊆{z:z<x}.
(10)
Using the pushforward measure identity and continuity of the two cumulative distribution functions, we obtain
F(x)≤G(T(x)),G(T(x))=G(T(x)−)≤F(x−)=F(x).
(11)
Consequently
T(x)=G−1(F(x))μ-almost everywhere.​
(12)
The monotone rearrangement in part (c) is optimal for the convex difference cost, so T has the same cost and solves the Monge optimal transport problem. The meaningful uniqueness is up to a μ-null set; arbitrary values away from the source do not affect transport or cost. Existence of the non-decreasing transport map supplies the source condition missing in part (c).

The dual of the Kantorovich optimal transport problem is
(u,v)∈Ac​sup​{∫X​udμ+∫Y​vdν},​
(27)
where the Kantorovich potentials are measurable representatives satisfying
Ac​={(u,v):u∈L1(μ), v∈L1(ν), u(x)+v(y)≤c(x,y) for every (x,y)∈X×Y}.
(28)
One standard form of the Kantorovich duality theorem assumes that X,Y are Polish spaces, μ,ν are probability measures defined as Borel measures, and c:X×Y→[0,+∞] is sequentially lower semicontinuous. Then
π∈Π(μ,ν)min​∫cdπ=(u,v)∈Ac​sup​(∫udμ+∫vdν).​
(29)
The primal infimum is attained; its value may be +∞. Nonnegativity can be replaced by a constant lower bound by shifting the cost. This duality for lower semicontinuous costs is also discussed in Beiglboeck, Leonard and Schachermayer's duality paper.
Equality of values does not by itself assert a dual maximum. A sufficient stronger setting for attainment on both sides is compact metric spaces X,Y and a finite continuous cost c; then continuous Kantorovich potentials attain the dual supremum. The general statement above correctly uses a supremum.

Fix any admissible pair of Kantorovich potentials (u,v) and any transport plan π∈Π(μ,ν). Its marginal distributions give
∫X​udμ+∫Y​vdν=∫X×Y​(u(x)+v(y))dπ(x,y).
(30)
The right side is well defined because u(x) and v(y) are Lebesgue integrable with respect to π. Integrating their pointwise feasibility inequality gives
∫X​udμ+∫Y​vdν≤∫X×Y​c(x,y)dπ(x,y).
(31)
Since this holds for every feasible pair and every transport plan,
(u,v)∈Ac​sup​(∫udμ+∫vdν)≤π∈Π(μ,ν)inf​K(π).​
(32)
This proves the required inequality directly from the transport constraints, without any convex optimization duality theorem. Whenever the extrema are attained, the supremum and infimum can respectively be written as a maximum and minimum. Even the Kantorovich duality theorem is unnecessary for this direction.

The Kantorovich–Rubinstein theorem concerns a Polish space (X,ρ) and probability measures μ,ν with finite first moments, meaning ∫ρ(x,x0​)dμ(x)+∫ρ(x,x0​)dν(x)<∞ for one, hence every, x0​∈X. It states
W1​(μ,ν)=π∈Π(μ,ν)inf​∫ρ(x,y)dπ=Lip(f)≤1sup​{∫fdμ−∫fdν}.​
(33)
Here Lip(f)≤1 means ∣f(x)−f(y)∣≤ρ(x,y) for every x,y. The Wasserstein distance with ground cost ρ therefore equals a supremum over functions with Lipschitz constant at most one. One may normalize f(x0​)=0, since adding a constant does not change the difference of integrals. This normalization bounds ∣f(x)∣ by ρ(x,x0​), so finite first moments ensure integrability. The supremum is unchanged if an absolute value is placed around the difference, because −f is admissible whenever f is.

For probability measures μ,ν on Rd with finite second moments, the Knott–Smith optimality criterion states that a transport plan π∈Π(μ,ν) minimizes the quadratic cost ∣x−y∣2 if and only if there is a proper convex function Φ:Rd→R∪{+∞} that is sequentially lower semicontinuous and satisfies
y∈∂Φ(x)for π-almost every (x,y).​
(38)
The subdifferential is characterized by
y∈∂Φ(x)⟺Φ(z)≥Φ(x)+y⋅(z−x)for every z∈Rd,
(39)
with Φ(x)<∞. Thus the transport plan is concentrated on the graph of the subdifferential. No absolute continuity of measures assumption on μ is needed. Multiplying the cost by 1/2 leaves the criterion unchanged. The original quadratic optimal mapping result is Knott and Smith, On the optimal mapping of distributions.

A standard sufficient form of Brenier theorem assumes μ,ν∈P(Rd) have finite second moments and μ≪Ld, that is, absolute continuity of measures with respect to Lebesgue measure. For the cost ∣x−y∣2, there exists a unique optimal transport plan, and it is induced by a transport map:
π†=(Id,∇Φ)#​μ,T†=∇Φ,(∇Φ)#​μ=ν.​
(40)
Here Φ is a proper convex function, which may be chosen sequentially lower semicontinuous, and its gradient exists μ-almost everywhere. The map ∇Φ is unique μ-almost everywhere and is the unique minimizer of the Monge optimal transport problem; its cost equals the Kantorovich optimal transport problem minimum. Equivalently, it is the unique gradient of a convex function transporting μ to ν.
The uniqueness claim concerns the map and the transport plan, not a globally unique potential. The potential may be shifted by a constant, and additional nonuniqueness away from the source can occur. No density assumption is required on ν. A primary reference is Brenier's Polar factorization and monotone rearrangement of vector-valued functions.
