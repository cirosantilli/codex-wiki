Let D/Dt=∂t​+u⋅∇ be the material derivative, and write h for specific enthalpy. The ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation and Gauss's law for magnetism give
DtDB​=(B⋅∇)u−B∇⋅u.
(1)
Dotting the momentum equation with B eliminates the Lorentz force density. Therefore the cross-helicity density satisfies
DtD(u⋅B)​​=−B⋅∇Φ−ρB⋅∇p​+B⋅∇2u2​−(u⋅B)∇⋅u.​
(2)
The first law of thermodynamics gives dh=Tds+dp/ρ for specific entropy s, without requiring uniform entropy. Consequently
∂t​hc​+∇⋅(uhc​)=−B⋅∇(h+Φ−2u2​)+TB⋅∇s.
(3)
Since ∇⋅B=0, the first term on the right is a divergence. This proves the cross-helicity conservation law, with flux
F=u(u⋅B)+B(h+Φ−2u2​).​
(4)
For the perfect gas used here, h=γp/[(γ−1)ρ].

The calculation in part (a) leaves the cross-helicity conservation law source
Qh​=TB⋅∇s.​
(5)
At positive temperature, this vanishes exactly when the magnetic field is tangent to constant-specific entropy surfaces: B⋅∇s=0. A homentropic flow is a sufficient special case; uniform entropy throughout space is not necessary. Mere advection of specific entropy, Ds/Dt=0, constrains its variation along the velocity rather than along the magnetic field, so it does not by itself eliminate this source. To conserve total cross-helicity in a volume, the net boundary flux must also vanish.

For uniform specific entropy, the material conservation of cross-helicity density criterion is
DtDhc​​=B⋅∇(2u2​−h−Φ)−hc​∇⋅u.
(6)
Thus the requested condition is
B⋅∇(2u2​−h−Φ)=(u⋅B)∇⋅u.​
(7)
In the steady state, mass conservation gives ∇⋅u=−u⋅∇logρ. An equivalent form, expressed entirely in the flow and thermodynamic variables, is
B⋅∇((γ−1)ργp​+Φ−2u2​)=(u⋅B)u⋅∇logρ.​
(8)
If “isentropic” means only constant specific entropy along individual trajectories, rather than a homentropic flow, the general criterion instead retains TB⋅∇s on the right-hand side of the material derivative equation. The distinction matters because a source-free cross-helicity conservation law need not make hc​ constant along each trajectory.

In the shock frame, integrate mass conservation, momentum conservation, and total-energy conservation across a thin interval containing the stationary normal shock wave. No mass, momentum, or energy is stored in the vanishingly thin interval, so each flux has the same value on both sides. For a perfect gas, the specific enthalpy is h=γp/[(γ−1)ρ]. The resulting Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas are
ρ1​u1​p1​+ρ1​u12​u1​(2ρ1​u12​​+γ−1γp1​​)​=ρ2​u2​,=p2​+ρ2​u22​,=u2​(2ρ2​u22​​+γ−1γp2​​).​​
(9)
The momentum flux includes both transported momentum and pressure force. The energy flux includes kinetic energy and enthalpy, the latter accounting for internal energy and the work needed to push gas through the interval. Entropy production can occur inside the normal shock wave even though total energy is conserved.

Let m=ρ1​u1​=ρ2​u2​ be the signed mass flux. Momentum conservation gives
m2(ρ1​1​−ρ2​1​)=p2​−p1​.
(10)
Dividing the energy-flux equation by m and eliminating m2 yields the pressure-density Hugoniot relation for a perfect gas:
γ−1γ​(ρ2​p2​​−ρ1​p1​​)=2p2​−p1​​(ρ1​1​+ρ2​1​).
(11)
With P=p2​/p1​ and D=ρ2​/ρ1​, this becomes
γ−12γ​(P−D)=(P−1)(D+1).
(12)
Solving for the mass density ratio gives
D=(γ−1)P+γ+1(γ+1)P+γ−1​.​
(13)
For a compressive normal shock wave, P>1 and D>1; as P→∞, the finite shock compression ratio is (γ+1)/(γ−1).

For N equal weak shocks, (1+δ)N=Ps​, hence
N=log(1+δ)logPs​​=δlogPs​​[1+O(δ)].
(19)
Adding the individual increases in specific entropy gives the entropy production in successive weak shocks
cv​Δsweak​​=12γ2γ2−1​δ2logPs​[1+O(δ)].
(20)
By contrast, a single normal shock wave with the same total pressure ratio gives
cv​Δssingle​​=logPs​−γlogD(Ps​)=logPs​−γlogγ−1γ+1​+O(Ps−1​).
(21)
Thus a single strong shock produces more entropy than the chain of sufficiently small weak shocks. At fixed Ps​, the chain approaches zero entropy production as δ→0, while the single-shock result stays positive. The gradual compression approaches reversible isentropic flow.

Align the polar axis with the magnetic dipole moment. The magnetic dipole field has
Br​=r32mcosθ​,Bθ​=r3msinθ​.
(34)
The magnetic-field-line equation gives dr/(rdθ)=2cotθ, hence the dipole magnetic-field line is r=Csin2θ. Normalize the loaded bundle by its small surface angular radius θ⋆​ at r=R⋆​. Its boundary obeys
sin2θ⋆​sin2θ(r)​=R⋆​r​,θ(r)2≃θ⋆2​R⋆​r​.
(35)
The dipolar flux-tube area for one polar cap is consequently
A(r)≃πθ⋆2​R⋆​r3​.​
(36)
Equivalently, BA is constant by conservation of magnetic flux and B∝r−3 near the axis. Two equal loaded caps double the total area. The r3 scaling is independent of the normalization; if the loading angle is specified at another radius rL​, use A(r)=πθL2​r3/rL​ instead.

In the narrow polar flux tube, the magnetic field lines are almost radial and the transverse width is much smaller than the radial scale. The Lorentz force density has no component along the magnetic field, so the longitudinal magnetically channelled accretion is hydrodynamic. Let v(r)>0 denote inward speed. Mass conservation, the Euler equations for an inviscid fluid, and the polytropic equation of state give
ρvA=M˙tube​,vdrdv​=−ρ1​drdp​−r2GM⋆​​,p=Kργ.
(37)
Using the adiabatic sound speed c2=γKργ−1 and A∝r3,
ρρ′​=−vv′​−r3​,(v−vc2​)v′=r3c2​−r2GM⋆​​.​
(38)
The Bernoulli equation is
2v2​+γ−1c2​−rGM⋆​​=γ−1c02​​,​
(39)
where the right-hand side comes from matching to a nearly stationary reservoir with negligible gravitational potential. The fixed magnetic field provides transverse confinement; neglecting magnetic forces in this one-dimensional equation concerns their longitudinal component.

A regular sonic point must make both sides of the flow equation vanish:
vs​=cs​,rs​=3cs2​GM⋆​​.
(40)
Substituting into the Bernoulli equation yields
cs2​(γ−11​−25​)=γ−1c02​​,cs2​=7−5γ2c02​​.
(41)
For γ>1, positive finite sound speed therefore requires the critical adiabatic index for dipolar accretion
1<γ<γcrit​=57​.​
(42)
To check that this gives real regular crossings, differentiate the flow equation at the sonic point and put x=rs​vs′​/cs​. The transonic accretion in a power-law tube calculation gives
(γ+1)x2+6(γ−1)x+9γ−12=0,x=γ+1−3(γ−1)±3(7−5γ)​​.

The dipole magnetic-field line geometry gives the angular radius at the sonic point as θs​≃θ⋆​rs​/R⋆​​. The sonic region must remain a narrow flux tube for the one-dimensional transonic branch equations to apply. Thus a necessary small-angle constraint is
θ⋆​≪rs​R⋆​​​=GM⋆​(7−5γ)6c02​R⋆​​​.​

c02​GM⋆​​≪rL​≪θ⋆2​R⋆​​.
