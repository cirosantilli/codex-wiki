Take γ>0 so that the short-wavelength quadratic energy is stable. For B=0, a Fourier mode with wavevector magnitude q has inverse Gaussian field theory covariance
G(q)=a+κq2+γq4=a−4γκ2​+γ(q2+2γκ​)2.
(1)
For κ<0, its minimum is at
q0​=−2γκ​​,ac​=4γκ2​.​
(2)
The zero-average constraint excludes the uniform mode but allows ϕ=Acos(q0​z). At a=ac​ the modes on the nonzero-wavevector soft-mode sphere become soft; below it they have negative quadratic energy, so the homogeneous state loses stability. A modulation along any chosen direction is the simplest smectic phase pattern. The quadratic instability selects its wavelength; with B=0, a nonlinear stabilizing interaction would still be needed to determine its amplitude.

Average the trial field over an integer number of periods. The squared-field, squared-gradient and squared-Laplacian averages are A2/2, q02​A2/2 and q04​A2/2, while ⟨ϕ2∣∇ϕ∣2⟩=A4q02​/8. Hence the single-mode smectic mean-field free energy is
VH​=4a−ac​​A2+8Bq02​​A4.​
(3)
For the physically stabilizing case B>0, minimization gives
A2={0,(ac​−a)/(Bq02​),​a≥ac​,a<ac​,​ac​=4γκ2​.​
(4)
The amplitude tends continuously to zero at ac​, so this mean-field approximation predicts a continuous phase transition. The ordered minimum has free-energy density −(ac​−a)2/(8Bq02​).
A nonzero B alone is not sufficient for this conclusion: B<0 makes the displayed quartic amplitude energy unbounded below and requires higher-order stabilization. The continuous-transition statement uses B>0 and the stipulated fixed wavevector q0​.

⟨e−(H−H0​)⟩0​≥e−⟨H−H0​⟩0​.

Choose an even positive J(q). Each independent complex Fourier mode of the real field has density (J(q)/π)e−J(q)∣ϕq​∣2, so
⟨ϕq​ϕp​⟩0​=J(q)δp,−q​​.
(7)
The quadratic part therefore has expectation ∑q+​G(q)/J(q). For the quartic part, apply Isserlis theorem to its four jointly centered Gaussian random variables, or to their real and imaginary components. Pairing the two differentiated fields together imposes q2​=−q1​, and its contribution is
VB​(∑q​J(q)q2​)(∑p​J(p)1​).
(8)
Each of the two other pairings instead gives −(B/V)∣∑q​q/J(q)∣2=0, because the summand is odd under q↦−q. Thus only the displayed contribution remains. Each full sum is twice its positive-wavevector sum for a real field; putting S0​=∑q+​1/J(q) and S2​=∑q+​q2/J(q) gives ⟨H4​⟩0​=4BS2​S0​/V.
Substitute these expectations, F0​ and ⟨H0​⟩0​ into the Feynman-Bogoliubov inequality:
F≤F[J]=q∑+​[logπJ(q)​−1+J(q)G(q)​]+V4B​S2​S0​.​
(9)
The zero mode is absent by the composition constraint. The counting uses one representative of every nonzero {q,−q} pair, so no extra factor of two is assigned to an independent complex amplitude.

In the usual three-dimensional isotropic setting, the transition is expected to be discontinuous, by the Brazovskii fluctuation-induced first-order transition mechanism.
If κˉ<0, write q∗2​=−κˉ/(2γ) and r=aˉ−κˉ2/(4γ). Then J(q)=r+γ(q2−q∗2​)2. A continuous finite-wavevector transition would require r→0 with q∗​>0. But the soft-mode shell integral behaves as
∫(2π)3J(q)d3q​∼4πγr​q∗​​,∫(2π)3J(q)q2d3q​∼4πγr​q∗3​​.
(13)
The positive fluctuation corrections in the self-consistency equations diverge in that limit. In particular κˉ=κ+2BI0​ cannot stay at a finite negative value while I0​ diverges. Thus the isotropic disordered state cannot lose stability through a zero-mass mode on a fixed nonzero shell in this approximation.
Ordering is instead expected through a free-energy crossing with a finite-amplitude smectic phase, giving a first-order phase transition. This is a reasoned fluctuation prediction for the finite-wavevector transition; the disordered Gaussian variational approximation alone does not locate the coexistence point or calculate the amplitude jump. A zero-wavevector instability or a lower-dimensional setting would require a separate analysis.

Write μj​=δF/δpj​ and ∫=∫t1​t2​​dt∫dr. Eliminating the noise gives f=p˙​+Γμ. The Onsager--Machlup path probability is therefore
PF​[p]=K[p]exp[−2σ21​∫∣p˙​+Γμ∣2],PB​[p]=K[p]exp[−2σ21​∫∣−p˙​+Γμ∣2].​
(14)
These are conditional path densities given the corresponding starting configuration. For a time-even vector order parameter, reversal reads the configurations backward, changes p˙​ to −p˙​, and keeps μ unchanged on each corresponding configuration.
The two Gaussian white noise measures have the same normalization. With a common midpoint discretization, the noise-to-path Jacobian also agrees under reversal by time-reversal invariance of a path Jacobian. The common factor K[p] may include that path-dependent Jacobian; it need not be a universal constant. This makes the shared-factor assertion precise while leaving the requested ratio unaffected.

Put uj​=p˙​j​+∂i​Wij​. The noises required by a joint trajectory are fj​=uj​+Γμj​ and Nij​=Wij​+M∂i​μj​. Their independent Gaussian white noise weights give the joint path probability of an order parameter and its flux:
PF​[p,W]=K[p,W]exp[−∫{2σ2∣u+Γμ∣2​+2σN2​∣W+M∇μ∣2​}].​
(21)
For the physically reversed path, p is time-even and the transport flux W is time-odd. Therefore u↦−u, W↦−W, and
PB​[p,W]=K[p,W]exp[−∫{2σ2∣−u+Γμ∣2​+2σN2​∣−W+M∇μ∣2​}].​
(22)
The common factor includes the two noise normalizations and the midpoint Jacobian determinant. Its calculation must include both relaxation channels. On a finite spatial grid, let D and G represent the divergence and gradient, with Δ=DG the discrete Laplacian, and let Hn​=∂μ/∂p be the Hessian matrix of F at the midpoint of time step n. Differentiating the two required noises with respect to the next configuration and the interval flux gives the Jacobian matrix
∂(pn+1​,Wn​)∂(fn​,Nn​)​=(ΔtI​+ΓHn​/2MGHn​/2​DI​).
(23)
Using the Schur complement, its Jacobian determinant, apart from the path-independent power of Δt, is
det[I+2Δt​(ΓI−MΔ)Hn​].
(24)
Reversal visits the same midpoint configurations in reverse order, so the product of these factors is unchanged. The joint time-reversal invariance of a path Jacobian therefore involves the complete relaxation operator ΓI−MΔ.
Taking the action difference gives
logPB​PF​​=−σ22Γ​∫μj​(p˙​j​+∂i​Wij​)−σN2​2M​∫Wij​∂i​μj​.​
(25)
Repeated component indices are summed. The second squared norm sums over both flux indices. This is mixed conserved and nonconserved order-parameter dynamics; reversing the order parameter history while leaving the flux unreversed would give the wrong probability ratio.

Set the noise strengths to the model A fluctuation-dissipation relation and the fluctuation-dissipation relation for a conserved flux:
σ2=2ΓkB​T,σN2​=2MkB​T.
(26)
The preceding logarithmic ratio becomes
logPB​PF​​=−kB​T1​∫[μj​p˙​j​+μj​∂i​Wij​+Wij​∂i​μj​].
(27)
The last two terms combine as ∂i​(μj​Wij​). They integrate to zero under the periodic boundary conditions, by integration by parts. The remaining term is F2​−F1​ by the functional chain rule, hence
PB​PF​​=e−(F2​−F1​)/(kB​T).​
(28)
After including the equilibrium initial weights, the forward and reversed histories have equal probability. Thus microscopic reversibility is satisfied by the two channels together, with each channel's own thermal noise strength.

Use the stable parameters b,κ>0. The bulk stationary points satisfy f′(ϕ)=aϕ+bϕ3=0. For a<0, ϕ=0 has negative curvature, while the two minima are
ϕ=±ϕB​,ϕB​=−a/b​.​
(29)
Both have f(±ϕB​)=−a2/(4b) and f′′(±ϕB​)=−2a>0. To establish minimization at fixed total composition, write
f(ϕ)=−4ba2​+4b​(ϕ2−ϕB2​)2,F≥−4bVa2​.
(30)
For mean composition ϕˉ​ with ∣ϕˉ​∣<ϕB​, a mixture of the two minima with volume fraction λ=(ϕˉ​+ϕB​)/(2ϕB​) in the positive phase attains this lower bound in the bulk. Macroscopic slabs have only a subextensive interfacial excess free energy, so the bound is attained per unit volume in the thermodynamic limit. Thus constrained minimization gives phase coexistence in the mean-field approximation; at the endpoints only one phase is needed.
The two equilibrium conditions also follow directly by constrained optimization. Introduce a Lagrange multiplier μ for λϕ+​+(1−λ)ϕ−​=ϕˉ​ in the mixture's bulk free-energy density. Varying ϕ+​ and ϕ−​ gives f′(ϕ+​)=f′(ϕ−​)=μ; varying λ gives f(ϕ+​)−μϕ+​=f(ϕ−​)−μϕ−​. These equate the chemical potentials and pressures, respectively.
Equivalently, their bulk chemical potentials are both zero and their pressures are equal:
μ=f′(±ϕB​)=0,Π=μϕ−f(ϕ)=4ba2​.​
(31)
These are precisely the conditions of the common-tangent construction for phase coexistence: the horizontal supporting tangent touches both minima. Equal chemical potentials prevent net composition exchange, and equal pressure gives mechanical equilibrium at a flat interface.

Apply force balance to one hemisphere. The pressure force is πR2(Πin​−Πout​), while the interfacial tension around its equator gives 2πRγ. The Young–Laplace equation therefore yields
Πin​−Πout​=R2γ​.​
(32)
To leading order in curvature, let the two bulk compositions be ϕ+​=ϕB​+δ+​ and ϕ−​=−ϕB​+δ−​. Their curvatures agree, α=f′′(±ϕB​)=−2a, so equal chemical potentials require αδ+​=αδ−​ and hence a common shift δ.
Since dΠ/dϕ=ϕf′′(ϕ), expansion about the flat coexistence compositions gives Π+​−Π−​=2ϕB​αδ+O(δ2). Matching the capillary pressure proves the Gibbs--Thomson relation in these variables:
δ=αϕB​Rγ​,μs​=αδ=ϕB​Rγ​.​
(33)
The pressure jump balances interfacial forces; it does not permit unequal chemical potentials. A common shift in both compositions supplies a common interfacial chemical potential while their pressures differ. The expansion assumes R large compared with the phi-four diffuse interface width.

The flat interface is an equilibrium reservoir with μ=0, while the droplet has the constant surface value μs​=γ/(ϕB​R). The exterior chemical potential satisfies the Laplace equation, tends to zero far away, and obeys these two surface values. These are exactly the electrostatic boundary conditions at a conductor for a sphere held at potential μs​ above a grounded infinite plane.
Introduce an arbitrary permittivity ϵ. In the electrostatic problem, Q=−ϵ∫SR​​∂n​μdS=C(R,h)μs​. Therefore the diffusion-capacitance analogy gives the outward current
I(R,h)=−M∫SR​​∂n​μdS=ϵM​C(R,h)μs​.
(39)
For an isolated sphere, its capacitance is C(R,∞)=4πϵR, so I(R,∞)=4πMRμs​=4πMγ/ϕB​. Dividing gives
I(R,h)=I(R,∞)c(R/h),c(R/h)=C(R,∞)C(R,h)​.​
(40)
Thus sphere-plane capacitance determines the evaporation enhancement. The assumed spherical shape gives the corresponding law R˙=−Mγc(R/h)/(2ϕB2​R2) for droplet evaporation near a planar reservoir. The local surface current is generally nonuniform, even though its total is described by a single capacitance.
