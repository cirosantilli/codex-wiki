Use the outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation
Gk​(r,r′)=4π∣r−r′∣eik∣r−r′∣​,(Δr′​+k2)Gk​=−δr​.
(1)
Let n′ point out of the obstacle D into the exterior. Apply Green second identity to the field and Gk​ in a large exterior region. Their Sommerfeld radiation conditions make the outer-sphere contribution vanish. The normal on the exterior region's inner boundary is −n′, so the Kirchhoff–Helmholtz representation is
ψ(r)=∫V​Gk​(r,r′)Q(r′)dr′+∫∂D​[ψ(r′)∂n′​Gk​−Gk​∂n′​ψ(r′)]dS′,r∈/D.​
(2)
The first integral is the incident source field ψi​. Therefore the scattered field is the surface term. Since ψi​ solves the homogeneous Helmholtz equation within the obstacle, its surface term is zero at exterior observation points; the same representation can be written using only scattered-field traces:
ψs​(r)=∫∂D​[ψs​∂n′​Gk​−Gk​∂n′​ψs​]dS′.​
(3)
The unknown boundary condition determines these traces, but the representation itself does not require choosing that condition.

The boundary identity gives a numerical route to shape recovery from a Herglotz boundary identity. First recover an admissible density g from measured far-field patterns, enforcing the supplied Herglotz pairing with an obstacle far field for available incident directions:
∫S2​f∞​(r,r0​,k)g(r)​dS=k1​.
(7)
Then evaluate its Herglotz wave function throughout a search region and find a smooth positive radial profile satisfying
vg​(h(θ)r(θ,ϕ))+kh(θ)e−ikh(θ)​=0.​
(8)
The same h(θ) must work for every azimuth ϕ, expressing the assumed axial symmetry. In practice expand g and h in basis functions, enforce these equations at collocation points, and minimize the complex residual together with the far-field-data residual. The exclusion of an interior Dirichlet eigenvalue ensures uniqueness of the interior Dirichlet problem for the trial boundary data. Use regularization of an inverse problem to control measurement error and unstable density components; neither uniqueness nor numerical stability follows merely from writing the residual equations.
For rich incident-direction data, the conjugated pairing constraints form an adjoint far-field integral equation for g. For only one fixed incidence, the displayed pairing is a single complex scalar constraint and cannot by itself determine an arbitrary density. Additional data or a joint constrained shape fit is then needed; the trace condition supplies the actual geometric information.

Let V=k02​(n2−1) be the scattering potential supported in D. The background Helmholtz equation and its outgoing kernel give the scalar Lippmann-Schwinger equation
ψ(r)=ψi​(r)+∫D​Gk0​​(r,r′)V(r′)ψ(r′)dr′.
(9)
Replacing the unknown field in the integral by the incident field gives the Born approximation for scalar wave scattering:
ψB​=ψi​+ψs,B​,ψs,B​(r)=∫D​Gk0​​(r,r′)V(r′)ψi​(r′)dr′.​
(10)
For the Rytov approximation, work where ψi​=0 and set ψ=ψi​eϕ. Dividing the wave equation by the total field and subtracting the incident equation gives
Δϕ+2∇logψi​⋅∇ϕ+(∇ϕ)2=−V.
(11)
Neglect the quadratic gradient term. If ϕ1​=ψs,B​/ψi​, direct differentiation shows Δϕ1​+2∇logψi​⋅∇ϕ1​=−V, so
ψR​=ψi​exp(ψi​ψs,B​​).​
(12)
Expanding the exponential in the potential gives ψR​=ψi​+ψs,B​+O(V2)=ψB​+O(V2). Thus both approximations have the same first-order field, although exponentiation retains a particular family of higher powers.

Φ=k0​(r−r0​⋅r),q=k0​(r0​−r),K(r′)=4πrei(Φ+q⋅r′)​.

To first order in weak contrast, V=2μk02​W+O(μ2). Its linearized part is a centered stationary Gaussian random field, with autocorrelation function of a random field CV​(s)=4μ2k04​CW​(s). The exponential moment of a Gaussian linear functional therefore gives the usual Gaussian intensity in the first Rytov approximation:
I=exp[8π2r21​∫D​∫D​CV​(r′−r′′)cos(Φ+q⋅r′)cos(Φ+q⋅r′′)dr′dr′′].​
(17)
Equivalently the exponent is E∣ϕ1​∣2+ReEϕ12​. The second term generally matters because a finite real random medium need not generate a circular complex Gaussian amplitude.
If the full stated potential V=k02​(2μW+μ2W2) is retained, it is not Gaussian and has mean mV​=μ2k02​. To retain every term through order μ2 in the intensity of this first Rytov field, the cumulant expansion instead gives
logI=mV​∫D​a(r′)dr′+21​∫D​∫D​a(r′)a(r′′)CVcov​(r′−r′′)dr′dr′′+O(μ4).​

i OurBigBook.com
For admissible data, the Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator selects the minimum-norm least-squares solution:
x†∈(kerA)⊥,∥Ax†−y∥=x∈Xinf​∥Ax−y∥,A†y=x†.​
(19)
Its domain is ranA⊕(ranA)⊥. When the equation is consistent it is the solution of Ax=y orthogonal to the kernel; for inconsistent admissible data it solves the normal equation for a linear inverse problem and projects away the component orthogonal to the range.
Take a singular system of a compact operator satisfying Avj​=σj​uj​, A∗uj​=σj​vj​. The generalized inverse is
x†=j∑​σj​⟨y,uj​⟩​vj​.​
(20)
The Picard criterion requires the squared coefficients to be summable. For an infinite-rank compact operator, σj​→0, so ∥A†uj​∥=1/σj​→∞ even though ∥uj​∥=1. Hence A† is unbounded. Finite-rank compact operators do not have this obstruction. Data failing the Picard condition need not have a least-squares minimizer at all, even when the infimum residual is zero.

A regularization of an inverse problem replaces the unbounded generalized inverse by bounded operators Rα​:Y→X satisfying Rα​y→A†y as α↓0 on admissible exact data. A spectral regularization method can be written
xα​=Rα​y=j∑​σj​fα​(σj​)​⟨y,uj​⟩vj​,​
(21)
where fα​(σ)→1 for fixed σ>0 while fα​(σ)/σ remains bounded for each fixed α. A uniform bound on fα​ supplies the usual dominated-convergence argument on exact data.
This defines fα​ as the dimensionless damping factor. In the alternative convention calling the entire inverse coefficient the spectral filter, that filter is fα​(σ)/σ. For data error bounded by δ, a sufficient regularization parameter choice also requires δ∥Rα(δ)​∥→0; consistency alone does not control noise.

Minimize the data-misfit functional J(x)=21​∥Ax−y∥2. For a perturbation h, DJ(x)[h]=Re⟨A∗(Ax−y),h⟩, so its gradient is A∗(Ax−y). Gradient descent gives Landweber iteration:
xn+1​=xn​+τA∗(y−Axn​),x0​=0,0<τ<∥A∥22​.​
(22)
On a singular component, the iteration error is multiplied by 1−τσj2​, whose modulus is less than one. Starting from zero preserves orthogonality to kerA and makes the exact-data iterates converge to x†.
For the unit-step formulas and noise bound used below, rescale so that τ=1 and ∥A∥≤1. Finite iteration count bounds amplification of small singular-value components; the stopping count provides the regularization parameter.

a OurBigBook.com
Use early stopping of Landweber iteration. For each finite n, the Landweber spectral filter gives a bounded reconstruction operator; as n→∞, it approaches the generalized inverse on exact admissible data. Taking α=1/n, a sufficient rule for a convergent regularization of an inverse problem is
n(δ)→∞,n(δ)​δ→0,​
(23)
or equivalently α(δ)→0 and δ/α(δ)​→0. For example, n(δ)=⌊δ−1⌋ satisfies both in the normalized problem. The first condition removes exact-data bias; the second prevents arbitrarily small measurement errors from being amplified by excessive iteration.
