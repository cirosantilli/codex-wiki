Put D=∂t​+U∂x​, write p for perturbation pressure divided by ρ0​, and set b=−gρ′/ρ0​. The linearized Boussinesq approximation, incompressibility and material derivative give
Du+U′w=−px​,Dw=−pz​+b,Db+N2w=0,ux​+wz​=0.
(1)
Taking the curl eliminates pressure; differentiating the resulting vorticity equation in x and using incompressibility gives D∇2w−U′′wx​=bxx​. A further application of D therefore yields
D2∇2w−U′′D∂x​w+N2∂x2​w=0.​
(2)
For a stationary nonzero horizontal Fourier mode, D=U∂x​, so cancellation of ∂x2​ gives the stationary Taylor–Goldstein equation
(∇2+U2N2​−UU′′​)w=0.​
(3)
This division requires U=0 on the interval considered: a zero of U is a critical level of an internal gravity wave. The mean profiles must be sufficiently smooth for the displayed derivatives, with background hydrostatic pressure and stable density stratification, N2>0, for propagating internal gravity waves. The horizontally uniform component is excluded from the cancellation. For a horizontal wavenumber k, local vertical propagation additionally requires N2/U2−U′′/U>k2; this is distinct from the smoothness restrictions. If stability of the background against other disturbances is needed, the Miles–Howard theorem supplies the sufficient condition N2≥(U′)2/4, rather than a necessary condition for deriving the equation.

Take U0​,N0​,k>0 and assume an initially propagating mode, k<N0​/U0​. Put s=N0​/U0​ and δ(z)=1+γz. Neglecting U′′/U as stipulated, the Scorer parameter and stationary internal-wave WKB solution are
m(z)2=δ(z)2s2​−k2,w≃Cm−1/2exp(ikx+i∫0z​m(ζ)dζ).
(4)
The positive root m>0 selects upward group velocity for the negative intrinsic frequency branch ω=−kU. The WKB approximation needs a slowly varying background relative to the local vertical wavelength, in particular ∣m′∣≪m2 and ∣m′′∣≪∣m∣3, away from a turning level or singular mean profile.
The local dispersion relation is ω=kU−Nk/(k2+m2)1/2=0. Its intrinsic and laboratory group velocities are, with K=(k2+m2)1/2,
cg,int​=K3N​(−m2,km),cg​=K2U​(k2,km).​
(5)
Thus intrinsic energy propagation is upstream and upward, while laboratory energy propagation is downstream and upward, parallel to (k,m). Constant-phase lines of an internal gravity wave have slope dz/dx=−k/m and normal spacing 2π/K; the horizontal spacing remains 2π/k.
If γ<0, m grows with height. The wavevector and laboratory energy ray become more vertical; phase lines become flatter and closer together. For finite nonzero N, the height
zc​=−1/γ​
(6)
is a critical level of an internal gravity wave: U→0 and the vertical wavenumber diverges. The laboratory group velocity tends to zero. Importantly, ∣m′∣/m2→∣γ∣/s, so the formal WKB condition need not deteriorate before this level if that ratio is small. Nevertheless the stationary equation is singular at the level, the phase has no finite limit, and the horizontal perturbation velocity grows as m1/2; the inviscid linear wave cannot be continued uniformly through it without additional physics.
If γ>0, m decreases to zero at
zt​=γs/k−1​.​
(7)
The wavevector and laboratory ray become horizontal, the phase lines become vertical, and the normal spacing increases. Above zt​, m2<0 and the disturbance is an evanescent wave. Here ∣m′∣/m2 diverges, so the WKB approximation fails in a turning region and must be replaced by a local connection solution.

Use a common factor ei(kx−ωt), with k=k0​>0 and ω=ω0​. For the upward propagating branches with 0<ω2​=ω−kU2​<N2​, define
sinθ1​=N1​ω​,sinθ2​=N2​ω2​​,mj​=−kcotθj​,0<θj​<2π​.​
(8)
These are the inclinations of upward-sloping constant-phase lines of an internal gravity wave to the horizontal. Write the incident, reflected and transmitted complex velocity amplitudes as A0​,Ar​,At​. Incompressibility gives (ui​,wi​)=A0​(cosθ1​,sinθ1​), (ur​,wr​)=Ar​(cosθ1​,−sinθ1​), and (ut​,wt​)=At​(cosθ2​,sinθ2​).
The jump conditions for stratified inviscid shear flow require a common interface displacement, not a common vertical velocity: w=−iωη on each side. Since the background mass density is continuous, pressure is continuous without a hydrostatic jump. The horizontal momentum equation gives p/ρ0​=(ω/k)u. Hence
ωsinθ1​​(A0​−Ar​)=ω2​sinθ2​​At​,ωcosθ1​(A0​+Ar​)=ω2​cosθ2​At​.
(9)
These two matching equations determine internal-wave transmission across a velocity jump:
A0​At​​=rcosθ2​/cosθ1​+r−1sinθ2​/sinθ1​2​,A0​Ar​​=rcosθ2​/cosθ1​+r−1sinθ2​/sinθ1​rcosθ2​/cosθ1​−r−1sinθ2​/sinθ1​​,r=ωω2​​.​
(10)
The amplitude formula printed in the PDF is inconsistent with these material-interface matching conditions. In particular, identical layers have At​/A0​=1 and Ar​/A0​=0, whereas its expression labelled “reflected” equals one. The displayed results above distinguish reflection from transmission and retain the intrinsic-frequency factors required by the kinematic boundary condition and pressure balance.
For equal buoyancy frequencies and the specified opposing current, θ1​=π/6, θ2​=arcsin(3/4), and
A0​At​​=4+21​8​,A0​Ar​​=21​+421​−4​.​
(11)
The incident phase lines have slope 1/3​, the reflected lines slope −1/3​, and the transmitted lines slope 3/7​: they steepen above the interface. For the other specified current, ω2​=0. The transmitted vertical wavenumber then diverges and there is no regular propagating upper-layer wave of that frequency: this is the critical level of an internal gravity wave limit. Approaching it from ω2​>0 gives Ar​/A0​→−1 and vanishing transmitted vertical energy flux; setting the intrinsic frequency to zero directly is outside the regular matching calculation.

For a dilute particle suspension, take Ws​>0 as the downward speed relative to the carrier fluid. The particle deposition flux through a horizontal absorbing bed is Ws​ϕb​, where ϕb​ is the near-bed particle volume fraction. Without stirring, settling can create a particle-free upper layer and a descending concentration front, so uniform concentration throughout the remaining fluid is not automatic. Weak turbulence can maintain a nearly uniform suspended concentration while leaving the mean settling velocity approximately equal to the isolated-particle value; this justifies using Ws​ϕ in a mixed-layer balance. Strong stirring can produce resuspension and requires a separate erosion model.
For a bed inclined at angle ϑ to the horizontal, the normal settling speed is Ws​cosϑ, giving deposition flux Ws​ϕb​cosϑ per unit actual bed area. There is also downslope particle motion, and near-wall effects or resuspension can alter this simplest absorbing-boundary model. Integrating the normal projection over a sloping bed is equivalent to integrating Ws​ϕb​ over its horizontal projection.
The mixture mass density is ρ=ρ0​+(ρp​−ρ0​)ϕ, so its reduced gravity is
g′=dϕ,d=ρ0​g(ρp​−ρ0​)​.​
(12)
The Boussinesq approximation requires (ρp​−ρ0​)ϕ/ρ0​≪1, in addition to the dilution assumption.

The shallow-water approximation requires a small depth-to-horizontal-scale ratio, slow streamwise variation of the channel and interface, small vertical acceleration, and rapid enough cross-sectional mixing for u and ϕ to be representative averages. Near the channel apex, or during the initial collapse at a sharp front, these conditions need not hold. The high Reynolds number permits neglect of leading viscous resistance; weak stirring mixes the particles without appreciable ambient fluid entrainment.
Put λ=β/H. At elevation z, the channel width is 2λxz, so the occupied area and interface width are
A=λxh2,T=2λxh.
(13)
Volume conservation and suspended-particle conservation give
At​+(Au)x​=0,(Aϕ)t​+(Auϕ)x​=−Ws​Tϕ.
(14)
The excess hydrostatic pressure is ρ0​dϕ(h−z). Its streamwise gradient averaged over the triangular section gives acceleration −dϕhx​−(dh/3)ϕx​: the weighted mean of h−z is h/3. Thus the triangular-channel shallow water equations are
ht​+uhx​+2h​ux​ut​+uux​+dϕhx​+3dh​ϕx​ϕt​+uϕx​​=−2xuh​,=0,=−h2Ws​ϕ​.​​
(15)
The quasilinear coefficient matrix in (h,u,ϕ) is
​udϕ0​h/2u0​0dh/3u​​.
(16)
Its three distinct real eigenvalues, for h,dϕ>0, establish strict hyperbolicity:
dtdx​=u,dtdx​=u±c,c=2dϕh​​.​
(17)
On the material characteristic curve, dϕ/dt=−2Ws​ϕ/h. On each of the other two characteristic curves, multiplying by the left eigenvector (±2c/h,1,±dh/(3c)) gives the compatibility ordinary differential equation
dtdu​±h2c​dtdh​±3ch​dtdg′​=∓c(xu​+3h4Ws​​),dtdx​=u±c.​
(18)
The derivatives are along the indicated characteristic, with g′=dϕ. At vanishing concentration, the gravity-wave speeds coalesce and strict hyperbolicity is lost.

Take inward fluid entrainment speed to be positive. The non-Boussinesq form of the Batchelor entrainment hypothesis is
ve​=αwρ/ρ0​​,​
(23)
where α is the entrainment coefficient. Define physical, cross-section-integrated fluxes for the top-hat plume model by
Q=πb2ρw,M=πb2ρw2,F=πb2wg(ρ0​−ρ).​
(24)
Thus Q is mass flux, M is momentum flux, and F is a density-weighted buoyancy flux. The kinematic buoyancy flux in the Boussinesq approximation is F/ρ0​. These definitions keep the factor π; conventions suppressing it simply rescale all three fluxes consistently.
For a homogeneous ambient, the sectional volume, mass and momentum balances are
∂t​(πb2)+∂z​(πb2w)=2πbve​,
(25)
∂t​(πb2ρ)+∂z​Q=2πbρ0​ve​,∂t​Q+∂z​M=πb2g(ρ0​−ρ).
(26)
The momentum source is the net upward buoyancy force; entrained ambient fluid initially supplies no vertical momentum. Subtracting the mass balance from ρ0​ times the volume balance gives conservation of the density deficit:
∂t​[πb2g(ρ0​−ρ)]+∂z​F=0.
(27)
Now w=M/Q, πb2ρ=Q2/M, and πb2g(ρ0​−ρ)=QF/M. Substitution produces the non-Boussinesq top-hat plume equations
∂t​(MQ2​)+∂z​Q=2απρ0​M​,∂t​Q+∂z​M=MQF​,∂t​(MQF​)+∂z​F=0.​
(28)
For reconstructing the fields without a Boussinesq approximation,
ρ=gQ+Fρ0​gQ​,b2=πρMQ2​,ρg(ρ0​−ρ)​=QF​.
(29)
The last expression uses the plume density in the acceleration denominator; it agrees with the ambient-density definition of reduced gravity to Boussinesq order.
