Let V=U−c and take k>0 without loss of generality. The Boussinesq approximation replaces density by ρ0​ in inertia and pressure acceleration, while retaining the density perturbation in buoyancy. Linearizing about the hydrostatic equilibrium and substituting the normal mode gives
ikVu+U′w=−ikp​/ρ0​,ikVw=−p​′/ρ0​−gρ​/ρ0​,
(1)
iku+w′=0,ikVρ​+ρ′w=0.
(2)
The last two equations give u=iw′/k and ρ​=iρ′w/(kV). The horizontal momentum equation gives
ρ0​p​​=ikVw′−U′w​.
(3)
Substituting in the vertical momentum equation yields the Taylor–Goldstein equation:
w′′−k2w−U−cU′′​w+(U−c)2N2​w=0,N2=−ρ0​g​ρ′.​
(4)
The buoyancy frequency is real for stable density stratification. The impermeable walls impose w(±L)=0.

Choose a=1/2 in the power-transformed Taylor–Goldstein energy identity and let Q=N2−(U′)2/4. The result is
∫V(∣q′∣2+k2∣q∣2)dz=∫(VQ​−2U′′​)∣q∣2dz.
(8)
Because ImV=−ci​ and Im(1/V)=ci​/∣V∣2, its imaginary part gives
ci​∫−LL​[∣q′∣2+k2∣q∣2+∣U−c∣2Q​∣q∣2]dz=0.
(9)
If 4N2>(U′)2 everywhere, the integral is strictly positive for every nonzero eigenfunction, so a mode with ci​=0 is impossible. Thus the flow is neutrally stable to these inviscid normal modes. This is the Miles–Howard theorem, expressed as a lower bound Ri>1/4 on the gradient Richardson number. It excludes exponential modal growth; it does not by itself exclude transient growth.

Choose a=1. The power-transformed Taylor–Goldstein energy identity reduces to
∫−LL​(U−c)2(∣q′∣2+k2∣q∣2)dz=∫−LL​N2∣q∣2dz.
(10)
The right side is real. For an unstable mode, ci​>0, so taking the imaginary part gives
∫−LL​(U−cr​)(∣q′∣2+k2∣q∣2)dz=0.
(11)
Consequently the phase velocity is a weighted average:
cr​=∫−LL​(∣q′∣2+k2∣q∣2)dz∫−LL​U(∣q′∣2+k2∣q∣2)dz​,Umin​<cr​<Umax​.​
(12)
The weights are nonnegative and not identically zero. For a nonconstant shear profile, equality at an extremum would force q to vanish on an interval where U differs from that extremum; uniqueness for the regular ordinary differential equation would then give the zero mode. This is the phase-speed bound for unstable stratified shear modes.

Let η​ be the displacement amplitude of an interface. Its kinematic boundary condition is ik(U−c)η​=w, on either side, so the first of the jump conditions for stratified inviscid shear flow is
[U−cw​]−+​=0.​
(13)
For zero surface tension, pressure is continuous at the displaced interface. Since the background hydrostatic pressure has derivative −gρ, linearization gives [p​−gρη​]−+​=0. Using p​=ρ0​[(U−c)w′−U′w]/(ik) and the kinematic condition gives
[(U−c)w′−U′w−ρ0​gρ​U−cw​]−+​=0.​
(14)
This derivation also works when U jumps: the displacement is shared, while the vertical velocity need not be. It avoids ambiguous products of distributions at a discontinuous velocity profile.

For large α, the coupling e−kh=e−2α is exponentially weak. If each interface were isolated, its interfacial gravity waves would have dimensional phase velocities U(z±​)±d​, or
c=1±2αJ​​,c=−1±2αJ​​.
(22)
The upper-interface wave travelling against its positive local current has speed 1−J/(2α)​. The lower-interface wave travelling against its negative current has speed −1+J/(2α)​. Their laboratory speeds match at zero when J=2α. Weak interaction then produces counterpropagating wave instability rather than two independent travelling waves.
The band has the large-wavenumber form J=2α±2αe−2α+O(αe−4α). Exactly at resonance, Imc∼e−2α/2, so
kci​∼4kΔU​e−kh.​
(23)
Thus the instability window and growth rate both become exponentially small as the interfaces become weakly coupled.

E˙=2λ1​x12​+2x1​x2​+2λ2​x22​=2xTHx,H=2L+LT​=(λ1​1/2​1/2λ2​​).

At each fixed time, the maximum over nonzero initial conditions is the optimal energy amplification of a linear system:
G(t)=maxx0​=0​∥x0​∥22​∥A(t)x0​∥22​​=σmax​(A(t))2.
(31)
Write a=eλ1​t, d=eλ2​t and b=(a−d)/(λ1​−λ2​). The symmetric matrix ATA has trace τ=a2+b2+d2 and determinant a2d2. Its largest eigenvalue is
G(t)=2τ+τ2−4a2d2​​.​
(32)
An optimal initial condition is a right singular vector of A(t) associated with its largest singular value.
If λ2​>λ1​, let α=1/(λ2​−λ1​). Then A(t)/eλ2​t→(0α​01​), so the precise long-time asymptotic statement is
G(t)∼(1+α2)e2λ2​t,α2=(λ2​−λ1​)21​.​
(33)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality shows that the unit initial conditions achieving this leading factor are
x(0)=±1+α2​1​(α1​).​
(34)
This is an adjoint eigenvector, since LT(α,1)T=λ2​(α,1)T. It differs from the right eigenvector v2​=(0,1)T: the initial condition maximizes the projection onto the slow mode, whereas the eventual state aligns with that right eigenvector. For λ2​<0, even the optimal energy ultimately decays to zero; the prefactor describes enhanced excitation of the slow mode.

Linearizing about the conductive state gives
∇2ψ′=−Rθx′​,θt′​+ψx′​=∇2θ′.

The perturbations vanish at z=0,1. For a normal mode with vertical dependence sin(nπz), put q=k2+n2π2. The two modal equations are −qψ​=−ikRθ and sθ+ikψ​=−qθ. Eliminating ψ​ gives
