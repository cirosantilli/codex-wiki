The exterior derivative is the real-linear degree-one map d:Ωr(M)→Ωr+1(M) characterized by df(X)=Xf on smooth functions, the graded Leibniz rule
d(α∧β)=dα∧β+(−1)rα∧dβ(α∈Ωr(M)),
(1)
and d2=0. In local coordinates (x1,…,xm), write α=∑I​αI​dxi1​∧⋯∧dxir​ using multi-index notation. Since dxi=d(xi) and hence d(dxi)=0, the defining rules force
dα=I,j∑​∂xj∂αI​​dxj∧dxi1​∧⋯∧dxir​.​
(2)
This proves local uniqueness, and the coordinate formulas agree on overlaps because the same rules are preserved by the pullback of a differential form. They also directly define an operator satisfying all three rules, proving existence.
An exact differential form is a form ω=dη. Let π:S2n→RP2n be the antipodal double covering map and let a(x)=−x. For a 2n-form ω on real projective space, a∗π∗ω=π∗ω, while the mapping degree of the antipodal map is −1. Therefore
∫S2n​π∗ω=∫S2n​a∗π∗ω=−∫S2n​π∗ω=0.
(3)
By the stated criterion, π∗ω=dη. The invariant primitive under a finite group action
ηˉ​=21​(η+a∗η)
(4)
still satisfies dηˉ​=π∗ω and descends to a form β on RP2n. Since pullback through a covering is injective on differential forms, π∗(dβ−ω)=0 implies dβ=ω. Thus every 2n-form on RP2n is exact; this is the top-degree differential forms on even-dimensional real projective space are exact result.
The kth de Rham cohomology is
HdRk​(M)=im(d:Ωk−1(M)→Ωk(M))ker(d:Ωk(M)→Ωk+1(M))​,
(5)
the closed differential forms modulo the exact ones. For the product, let p:M×S1→M be projection and choose a closed one-form ν on the circle with ∫S1​ν=1. Averaging differential forms over the circle is cochain-homotopic to the identity, so every class has a rotation-invariant representative; if such a representative is exact, averaging a primitive gives an invariant primitive. Every invariant k-form has a unique decomposition p∗α+p∗β∧ν, and
d(p∗α+p∗β∧ν)=p∗(dα)+p∗(dβ)∧ν.
(6)
Closedness and exactness are therefore componentwise. Hence the map
([α],[β])⟼[p∗α+p∗β∧ν]​
(7)
is a well-defined bijection HdRk​(M)⊕HdRk−1​(M)→HdRk​(M×S1), proving the de Rham cohomology of a product with a circle formula.

An immersed submanifold of M is a manifold Y equipped with an injective immersion ψ:Y→M. It is an embedded submanifold when ψ is also a homeomorphism onto its image with the subspace topology, equivalently when it is a smooth embedding. For irrational α, the irrational winding of the torus
t⟼(eit,eiαt)
(8)
is an injective immersion R→T2 with dense image, and therefore is not an embedding. If Y is compact, however, an injective immersion is a continuous bijection from a compact space to its image in the Hausdorff manifold M; its inverse is continuous. Thus the compact injective immersion is an embedding theorem makes ψ(Y) embedded.
Now let X⊂M be embedded, with dimM=m, dimX=m−k, and p∈X. Apply the constant rank theorem to its inclusion. After choosing coordinates and reordering them, there is a neighborhood U of p with coordinates (x1,…,xm) for which
U∩X={x1=⋯=xk=0}.​
(9)
This is the slice chart for an embedded submanifold.
It is false that every embedded submanifold is the inverse image of a regular value of a map to a Euclidean space. If a codimension-k submanifold is f−1(y) for a regular value of f:M→Rk, the differentials of the component functions give a global frame of its conormal bundle, so its normal bundle is trivial. The core circle of the Möbius band is embedded but has the nontrivial Möbius normal line bundle. This is the normal-bundle obstruction to being a regular level set.
Finally, an inductive spinning construction gives the requested torus. Place an embedding F:Nm↪Rm+1 in the half-space Fm+1​>0 and define
F(x,eiθ)=(F1​(x),…,Fm​(x),Fm+1​(x)cosθ,Fm+1​(x)sinθ).
(10)
The positive radius makes this map injective, and its differential is injective in both the N and circle directions; compactness then makes it an embedding. Starting with S1↪R2 and iterating proves the embedding of the n-dimensional torus in codimension one:
Tn↪Rn+1.​
(11)

A Lie group is a group and smooth manifold whose multiplication and inversion are smooth. A Lie algebra is a vector space with a bilinear alternating bracket satisfying the Jacobi identity. For a Matrix Lie group G⊂GL(m,C), a logarithmic chart of a matrix Lie group near g sends h to log(g−1h)∈g=TI​G; the matrix exponential is its local inverse, and the Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula makes the local group operations smooth.
For B1​,B2​∈g, consider the group commutator
C(s,t)=esB1​etB2​e−sB1​e−tB2​∈G.
(12)
Its logarithm takes values in the vector space g, and expansion at (0,0) gives
∂s∂t∂2​​(0,0)​logC(s,t)=B1​B2​−B2​B1​.
(13)
Thus g is closed under the commutator. Bilinearity, alternation, and the Jacobi identity follow from matrix multiplication, so this proves that the Lie algebra of a matrix Lie group has
[B1​,B2​]=B1​B2​−B2​B1​.​
(14)
A principal bundle π:P→M with structure group G is a smooth fiber bundle with a free right G-action, each fiber a single orbit, and equivariant local trivializations π−1(U)≅U×G.
For the right action of the unitary group on GL(n,C), define
q(g)=21​log(gg∗)∈H(n).
(15)
The matrix gg∗ is a positive-definite matrix and a Hermitian matrix, and q(gu)=q(g) for u∈U(n). The polar decomposition of an invertible complex matrix gives the unique factorization
g=eq(g)u(g),u(g)=e−q(g)g∈U(n).
(16)
Consequently
Φ:H(n)×U(n)⟶GL(n,C),(B,u)⟼eBu
(17)
is a diffeomorphism. If W⊂H(n) is a neighborhood of zero and N=q−1(W), then N is an open neighborhood of I, it is a union of complete orbits, and
V=⋃h∈N​R(h)=N≅W×U(n).
(18)
Moreover, two matrices have the same value of q exactly when they differ by right multiplication by a unitary matrix. Thus q induces the smooth identification
M=GL(n,C)/U(n)≅H(n),​
(19)
under which π is projection H(n)×U(n)→H(n). This is the general linear group modulo the unitary group, and π is in fact a globally trivial principal U(n)-bundle.

The Levi-Civita connection of a Riemannian manifold (M,g) is the unique connection on a vector bundle on TM that is torsion-free and compatible with the Riemannian metric. Any such connection must satisfy the Koszul formula
2g(∇X​Y,Z)=​Xg(Y,Z)+Yg(Z,X)−Zg(X,Y)−g(X,[Y,Z])+g(Y,[Z,X])+g(Z,[X,Y]).​
(20)
Nondegeneracy of g determines ∇X​Y uniquely from the right-hand side. Conversely, define ∇ by this formula. Direct substitution shows that it is C∞-linear in X, satisfies the Leibniz rule in Y, preserves g, and obeys ∇X​Y−∇Y​X=[X,Y]. It is therefore a torsion-free metric connection. This proves the existence and uniqueness of the Levi-Civita connection.
For the metric vector bundle E, choose the stated orthonormal local frame (e1​,…,em​) and write dA​ej​=Aij​ei​. Since ⟨ei​,ej​⟩=δij​, metric compatibility gives
0=d⟨ei​,ej​⟩=⟨dA​ei​,ej​⟩+⟨ei​,dA​ej​⟩=Aji​+Aij​.
(21)
Hence the connection matrix in an orthonormal frame is skew-symmetric:
Aij​=−Aji​.​
(22)
On an oriented Riemannian d-manifold, the Hodge star operator is defined by
α∧∗β=⟨α,β⟩dvolg​.
(23)
It is an orthogonal map ∗:ΛrTx∗​M→Λd−rTx∗​M and satisfies ∗2=(−1)r(d−r). In dimension d=2n on middle-degree forms, the Adjoint of the Hodge star on middle-degree forms is
∗†=∗−1=(−1)n2∗.
(24)
Thus it is self-adjoint when n is even, but it is not always self-adjoint. For n=1 on the oriented Euclidean plane,
∗dx=dy,∗dy=−dx,
(25)
so its matrix in the orthonormal basis (dx,dy) is skew-adjoint.
The Laplace-Beltrami operator on differential forms is the Hodge Laplacian
Δ=dδ+δd,
(26)
where the codifferential δ is the formal L2 adjoint of the exterior derivative. On a compact manifold without boundary, if Δω=λω for a nonzero differential form ω, then integration by parts gives
λ∥ω∥L22​=⟨Δω,ω⟩L2​=∥dω∥L22​+∥δω∥L22​≥0.
(27)
Therefore the nonnegativity of the Hodge Laplacian yields
λ≥0.​
(28)
