A Lie group is a group that is also a smooth manifold, with differentiable multiplication and inversion. A Lie algebra is a vector space with a bilinear alternating Lie bracket satisfying the Jacobi identity.
For a Matrix Lie group G, put g=TI​G. If X,Y∈g, the matrix commutator [X,Y]=XY−YX again lies in g: the group commutator etXesYe−tXe−sY lies in G, and the coefficient of ts in its matrix logarithm is [X,Y]. Bilinearity and antisymmetry are immediate, while associativity of matrix multiplication gives the Jacobi identity. Thus TI​G, with the commutator bracket, is the Lie algebra of a matrix Lie group L(G).
The special linear group SL(2,R) is the inverse image of the regular value 1 under the smooth determinant map, and multiplication and inversion are smooth. Differentiating det(I+tA)=1+ttrA+O(t2) shows that
L(SL(2,R))=sl2​(R)={(ac​b−a​):a,b,c∈R}.
(1)
The Cayley-Hamilton theorem applied to a trace-zero two-by-two matrix gives
A2=−det(A)I2​.​
(2)
The exponential map of a matrix Lie group is the matrix exponential
Exp(A)=eA=∑n=0∞​n!An​.
(3)
Since det(eA)=etrA=1, its image lies in SL(2,R). Put d=detA. The identity A2=−dI sums the series explicitly. If d>0, with r=d​,
eA=cosrI+rsinr​A,tr(eA)=2cosr≥−2.
(4)
If d=0, the trace is 2, while if d<0, with r=−d​,
eA=coshrI+rsinhr​A,tr(eA)=2coshr≥2.
(5)
Hence
tr(ExpA)≥−2.​
(6)
But diag(−2,−1/2)∈SL(2,R) has trace −5/2. It is therefore outside the image, so the exponential map is not surjective.

Let G be a simple Lie group, let Ta be Hermitian matrices for a finite-dimensional unitary representation R, and normalize
[Ta,Tb]=ifabc​Tc,tr(TaTb)=T(R)δab.
(23)
A matter field ψ transforms locally as ψ′(x)=U(x)ψ(x), where U(x)=eigϵa(x)Ta. An ordinary derivative of ψ does not transform covariantly because it differentiates U. Introduce a gauge field Aμ​=Aμa​Ta and the gauge covariant derivative
Dμ​=∂μ​−igAμ​.
(24)
Demanding Dμ′​ψ′=UDμ​ψ determines the Yang-Mills gauge transformation
Aμ′​=UAμ​U−1−gi​(∂μ​U)U−1.
(25)
To first order in ϵ,
δAμ​=∂μ​ϵ+ig[ϵ,Aμ​],δAμa​=∂μ​ϵa+gfbca​Aμb​ϵc.​
(26)
The gauge field strength is defined by [Dμ​,Dν​]=−igFμν​:
Fμν​=∂μ​Aν​−∂ν​Aμ​−ig[Aμ​,Aν​],
(27)
or, in components,
Fμνa​=∂μ​Aνa​−∂ν​Aμa​+gfbca​Aμb​Aνc​.
(28)
Covariance of the commutator gives
Fμν′​=UFμν​U−1,δFμν​=ig[ϵ,Fμν​].
(29)
The commutator term distinguishes Yang-Mills theory from an Abelian gauge theory and produces cubic and quartic gauge-boson interactions.
For a Dirac field of mass m in R, the Lagrangian is
L=−41​Fμνa​Faμν+ψˉ​(iγμDμ​−m)ψ.​
(30)
Equivalently, the gauge term is proportional to −tr(Fμν​Fμν). The cyclic property of the trace and Fμν′​=UFμν​U−1 make it invariant. Unitarity gives ψˉ​′=ψˉ​U−1, while Dμ′​ψ′=UDμ​ψ, so both the matter kinetic term and mass term are invariant. A complex scalar ϕ in a unitary representation may instead be coupled through
Lϕ​=(Dμ​ϕ)†Dμϕ−V(ϕ),
(31)
provided the scalar potential V is G-invariant.
The simplicity assumption means that the Lie algebra g is nonabelian and has no proper nonzero ideal of a Lie algebra. Its Adjoint representation is therefore irreducible, and every invariant symmetric bilinear form is proportional to the Killing form. Consequently the pure gauge kinetic term has one overall gauge coupling for a simple factor. The theory has no independent Abelian gauge direction; if the gauge algebra were a direct sum of simple and Abelian ideals, each factor could instead carry its own coupling. A simple group may still have a discrete center, but this does not add a gauge boson because gauge bosons are indexed by the Lie algebra.
