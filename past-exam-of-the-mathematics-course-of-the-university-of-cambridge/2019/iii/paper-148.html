An element t∈T is an integral element over R when it satisfies a monic equation
tn+rn−1​tn−1+⋯+r0​=0,ri​∈R.
(1)
The extension R⊆T is an integral extension when every t∈T is integral over R.
Let P∈SpecR and localize both rings at S=R∖P. The extension RP​⊆S−1T remains integral. Choose a maximal ideal n of S−1T. The contraction of a maximal ideal under an integral extension is maximal, and the local ring RP​ has unique maximal ideal PRP​, so n∩RP​=PRP​. Contracting n back to T produces Q∈SpecT with Q∩R=P. This proves the Lying-over theorem and hence the surjectivity of
π:SpecT⟶SpecR.
(2)
Suppose Q1​⊆Q2​ and both contract to P. Quotient by Q1​ and localize the resulting integral domain at the nonzero elements of R/P. The localized ring is an integral domain integral over the field Frac(R/P), and is therefore itself a field. The localization of Q2​/Q1​ must consequently be zero; since the localized ring is a domain, Q2​/Q1​=0. Thus the incomparability theorem for integral extensions gives
Q1​=Q2​.​
(3)
The Krull dimension of a ring is the supremum of the lengths of its strict chains of prime ideals. Incomparability makes the contraction of every strict prime chain in T strict, so dimT≤dimR. Conversely, start over the bottom prime of any chain in R using lying over and lift each subsequent inclusion using the going-up theorem. Hence
dimT=dimR.​
(4)
For the concrete surface, write x,y,z for the residue classes of X,Y,Z and put
u=x−z,v=y−z.
(5)
Then
xy+yz+zx=uv+2(u+v)z+3z2,
(6)
and characteristic zero allows division by three. Therefore
T≅k[u,v][z]/(z2+32​(u+v)z+31​uv).
(7)
Division by the monic quadratic shows that T is free over k[u,v] with basis 1,z. In particular, u,v are algebraically independent and the requested Noether normalization of the quadratic surface xy plus yz plus zx is
R=k[x−z,y−z]≅k[U,V].​
(8)

The set S=1+I is a multiplicative subset, since (1+a)(1+b)=1+(a+b+ab). The localization of a ring S−1R consists of fractions r/s, where
sr​=s′r′​⟺u(s′r−sr′)=0 for some u∈S.
(9)
Similarly, the localization of a module S−1M consists of fractions m/s, with the analogous equivalence relation. The canonical maps are
R⟶S−1R,r⟼r/1,M⟶S−1M,m⟼m/1.
(10)
The Localization of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian. Since a finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring is a Noetherian module, localizing a finite generating set shows that S−1M is Noetherian over S−1R.
The prime ideal correspondence for localization identifies SpecS−1R with the primes P∈SpecR disjoint from S. Here
P∩(1+I)=∅⟺P+I=R,
(11)
so the spectrum of localization away from one plus an ideal is
SpecS−1R≅{P∈SpecR:P+I=R}.​
(12)
An element m maps to zero exactly when (1+a)m=0 for some a∈I. Such an equation gives m=(−a)jm∈IjM for every j, proving one inclusion. Conversely, suppose m∈⋂j≥1​IjM. The Artin-Rees lemma applied to Rm⊆M says that for some c,
InM∩Rm=In−c(IcM∩Rm),n≥c.
(13)
Taking n=c+1 gives m∈I(Rm), so m=am for some a∈I. Then (1−a)m=0 and 1−a∈S. Thus
ker(M→S−1M)=j≥1⋂​IjM.​
(14)
For failure without Noetherianity, take
R=k[tq:q∈Q≥0​],I=(tq:q>0).
(15)
The strict chain (t)⊊(t1/2)⊊(t1/4)⊊⋯ shows that R is not Noetherian. Since tq=(tq/2)2, one has I2=I, and hence ⋂j​Ij=I=0. But R is an integral domain, so its localization map into (1+I)−1R is injective. This is the non-Noetherian failure of the intersection formula for localization.

The height of a prime ideal P is
htP=sup{n:P0​⊊P1​⊊⋯⊊Pn​=P are prime}.
(16)
The Krull principal ideal theorem states that if R is Noetherian and P is minimal among the primes containing a proper principal ideal (a), then
htP≤1.​
(17)
Suppose otherwise that P0​⊊Q⊊P. Quotient by P0​ and localize at P; it is enough to consider a Noetherian local domain whose maximal ideal P is the only prime containing (a) and which has 0⊊Q⊊P.
For n≥1, put
Q(n)=QnRQ​∩R.
(18)
This is a Q-primary ideal. Since R/(a) is a zero-dimensional Noetherian ring, it is Artinian, and the descending chain (Q(n)+(a))/(a) eventually stabilizes. Thus, for all sufficiently large n, every qn​∈Q(n) can be written
qn​=qn+1​+ra,qn+1​∈Q(n+1).
(19)
Now ra∈Q(n), while a∈/Q; Q-primaryness gives r∈Q(n). Hence
Q(n)=Q(n+1)+aQ(n).
(20)
The Nakayama lemma applied to Q(n)/Q(n+1) gives Q(n)=Q(n+1). Localizing at Q makes all sufficiently large powers of the nonzero maximal ideal QRQ​ equal. A nonzero element of the stable power then belongs to ⋂n​QnRQ​, contradicting the Krull intersection theorem. This proves the theorem.
Now let R be a Noetherian integral domain. If R is a unique factorization domain and P has height one, choose 0=x∈P and an irreducible factor p of x. In a UFD, p is prime, so
(0)⊊(p)⊆P.
(21)
Height one forces P=(p).
Conversely, suppose every height-one prime is principal. Noetherianity makes R an atomic domain. Given an irreducible element p, choose a prime P minimal over (p). The principal ideal theorem gives htP=1, so P=(q) by hypothesis. Since p=qr and p is irreducible, r is a unit; hence (p)=P is prime. Thus every irreducible is a prime element, and an atomic domain with this property is a UFD. Therefore
R is a UFD⟺every height-one prime of R is principal.​
(22)

If P is a prime ideal of the commutative Artinian ring R, then R/P is an Artinian domain. For 0=x∈R/P, the descending chain (x)⊇(x2)⊇⋯ stabilizes, so xn=xn+1a for some n; cancellation gives xa=1. Thus R/P is a field and P is maximal.
There are only finitely many maximal ideals. Otherwise, distinct maximal ideals Pi​ are pairwise comaximal and their finite products give the strictly descending chain
P1​⊋P1​P2​⊋P1​P2​P3​⊋⋯,
(23)
contradicting the Artinian condition. This proves the statement about the prime ideals of a commutative Artinian ring.
The nilradical N is the ideal of all nilpotent elements, equivalently the intersection of all prime ideals. Its powers stabilize, say Nr=Nr+1=I. If I=0, choose an ideal J minimal subject to IJ=0, then choose x∈J with Ix=0. Since I2=I, minimality gives Rx=Ix, so x=ax for some a∈I. But a is nilpotent, hence 1−a is a unit, contradicting (1−a)x=0. Therefore the nilradical of a commutative Artinian ring is nilpotent.
If the maximal ideals are P1​,…,Ps​, the Chinese remainder theorem gives
R/N≅∏i=1s​R/Pi​,
(24)
a finite product of fields. Each layer Nj/Nj+1 is an Artinian module over this product and therefore finite-dimensional. Since N is nilpotent, these finitely many layers show that every ideal of R is finitely generated. This proves the Artinian commutative ring is Noetherian theorem.
Now assume R is local with maximal ideal P and dimR/P​P/P2=1. The Nakayama lemma gives P=(x). Since P is nilpotent, its powers form a finite chain ending in zero. For a nonzero ideal J, choose the largest r with J⊆(xr) and take y∈J∖(xr+1). Write y=xru; then u∈/P, so u is a unit. Hence
J⊆(xr)=(y)⊆J,
(25)
and every ideal of R is principal.
Finally, the Artin–Wedderburn theorem says that a finite-dimensional semisimple k-algebra has the form
T≅i=1∏s​Mni​​(Di​),​
(26)
where each Di​ is a finite-dimensional division ring over k; the factors are unique up to permutation and isomorphism. By the assumed complete reducibility, write the right regular module as
TT​≅⨁i=1s​Sini​​
(27)
with pairwise nonisomorphic simple right modules Si​. The Schur lemma says that Di​=EndT​(Si​) is a division ring and that homomorphisms between distinct Si​ vanish. Left multiplication and the endomorphism ring of the displayed direct sum therefore give
T≅EndT​(TT​)≅∏i​Mni​​(Di​).
(28)
Conversely, the regular module of Mn​(D) is a direct sum of n simple column modules, proving that every algebra on the right is semisimple. Uniqueness follows from uniqueness of the simple summands and their multiplicities.

For the R-R-bimodule M, the Hochschild chain complex is
Cn​(R,M)=M⊗k​R⊗k​n,
(29)
with boundary
b(m⊗r1​⊗⋯⊗rn​)=​mr1​⊗r2​⊗⋯⊗rn​+i=1∑n−1​(−1)im⊗r1​⊗⋯⊗ri​ri+1​⊗⋯⊗rn​+(−1)nrn​m⊗r1​⊗⋯⊗rn−1​.​
(30)
Then
HHn​(R,M)=Hn​(C∙​(R,M),b).
(31)
The Hochschild cochain complex is Cn(R,M)=Homk​(R⊗k​n,M) with
(δf)(r1​,…,rn+1​)=​r1​f(r2​,…,rn+1​)+i=1∑n​(−1)if(r1​,…,ri​ri+1​,…,rn+1​)+(−1)n+1f(r1​,…,rn​)rn+1​,​
(32)
and HHn(R,M)=Hn(C∙(R,M),δ).
A derivation into a bimodule is a k-linear map d:R→M satisfying
d(rs)=rd(s)+d(r)s.
(33)
It is an inner derivation when d=dm​ for some m∈M, where dm​(r)=rm−mr. The degree-one cocycle equation is precisely the Leibniz rule, and the degree-one coboundaries are the inner derivations. Therefore the First Hochschild cohomology as outer derivations is
HH1(R,M)≅Derk​(R,M)/Inn(R,M).​
(34)
Let μ:R⊗k​R→R be multiplication and Ω=kerμ, equipped with the outer bimodule structure. The universal bimodule derivation
D:R⟶Ω,D(r)=r⊗1−1⊗r
(35)
satisfies D(rs)=rD(s)+D(r)s. Composition with D defines
HomR−R​(Ω,M)⟶Derk​(R,M).
(36)
For a derivation d, its inverse image under this map is
θd​(∑i​ri​⊗si​)=∑i​d(ri​)si​.
(37)
If ∑i​ri​si​=0, the Leibniz rule shows that this formula is both left and right R-linear. Moreover every element of Ω is ∑i​D(ri​)si​, proving existence and uniqueness.
For dm​(r)=rm−mr, the corresponding map is
θm​(i∑​ri​⊗si​)=i∑​ri​msi​.​
(38)
These are exactly the maps Ω→M which extend to bimodule maps R⊗k​R→M.

Choose homogeneous algebra generators x1​,…,xr​ of positive degrees d1​,…,dr​ for S; they exist because S is Noetherian. For a finitely generated graded module V=⨁j​Vj​, its Poincare series of a graded module is
PV​(t)=∑j​(dimk​Vj​)tj.
(39)
The Hilbert-Serre theorem states that
PV​(t)=∏i=1r​(1−tdi​)Q(t)​​
(40)
for a Laurent polynomial Q(t)∈Z[t,t−1].
We prove this by induction on r. For r=0, S=k and V is finite-dimensional, so its series is a Laurent polynomial. For x=xr​ of degree d=dr​, multiplication gives the exact sequence of graded modules
0⟶(0:V​x)(−d)⟶V(−d)x​V⟶V/xV⟶0.
(41)
Additivity of the Hilbert series yields
(1−td)PV​(t)=PV/xV​(t)−tdP(0:V​x)​(t).
(42)
Both modules on the right are finitely generated over k[x1​,…,xr−1​], so the induction hypothesis proves the formula.
For the finitely generated commutative algebra R, let Rj​ be the stated degree filtration. Its associated graded ring
grR=⨁j≥0​Rj​/Rj−1​
(43)
is a standard graded algebra generated by the initial forms of x1​,…,xn​. Since
dimk​Rj​=∑i=0j​dimk​(Ri​/Ri−1​),
(44)
Hilbert–Serre shows that the quantity dimk​Rj​ agrees with a polynomial for all sufficiently large j. The growth of a finitely generated commutative algebra has polynomial degree dimR, independently of the chosen finite generating set.
For
R=k[Y1​,Y1−1​,Y2​,Y2−1​]
(45)
with the four displayed generators, a basis of Rj​ consists of the Laurent monomials Y1a​Y2b​ satisfying ∣a∣+∣b∣≤j. There are 4r points with ∣a∣+∣b∣=r>0, and one point at the origin. Hence the growth of the two-variable Laurent polynomial algebra is
dimk​Rj​=1+4r=1∑j​r=2j2+2j+1.​
(46)
