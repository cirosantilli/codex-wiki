The Schwartz space consists of the smooth functions φ for which
pα,β​(φ)=supx∈Rn​∣xα∂βφ(x)∣<∞
(1)
for all multi-indices α,β. Its topology is generated by these seminorms; φj​→φ means convergence in every seminorm. A tempered distribution is a continuous linear functional on this space. Convergence in S′ here means weak convergence: ⟨uj​,φ⟩→⟨u,φ⟩ for every Schwartz function.
Use the unnormalized angular-frequency Fourier transform
φ​(λ)=∫Rn​e−ix⋅λφ(x)dx,⟨u,φ⟩=⟨u,φ​⟩.
(2)
The transform is a continuous automorphism of the Schwartz space, so this duality defines a tempered distribution. Put Dj​=−i∂j​, as required by the stated identities. Integration by parts and differentiation under the integral give
Dxα​φ​(x)=(−1)∣α∣λαφ​(x),Dλβ​φ​(x)=xβφ​(x).
(3)
Thus, keeping the transpose factor of D=−i∂ explicit,
⟨Dj​u​,φ⟩=i⟨u,∂xj​​φ​⟩=⟨u,λj​φ⟩,
(4)
and
⟨xj​u​,φ⟩=⟨u,xj​φ​⟩=⟨u,Dj​φ⟩=⟨−Dj​u,φ⟩.
(5)
Iterating gives the Fourier derivative identities for distributions
Dαu=λαu,xβu=(−1)∣β∣Dβu.​
(6)
The logarithm defines a tempered distribution because it is locally integrable and grows more slowly than a polynomial. Its derivative is u′(x)=x/(1+x2). The standard transform pair (1+x2)−1​=πe−∣λ∣, obtainable by the residue theorem, gives
u′(λ)=i∂λ​(πe−∣λ∣)=−iπsgn(λ)e−∣λ∣.
(7)
As u′=iλu, we have λu=−πsgn(λ)e−∣λ∣. Define
⟨T,φ⟩=−π∫R​∣λ∣φ(λ)−φ(0)​e−∣λ∣dλ.
(8)
This integral is absolutely convergent: the numerator is O(∣λ∣) near zero, and the exponential controls the tails. It defines a tempered distribution satisfying the same multiplication equation. The kernel of multiplication by a coordinate therefore gives u=T+cδ0​.
To determine c, use the supplied Gaussian Fourier transform pair with φn​(0)=1/(2π​). Then
⟨u,φn​⟩=n​∫R​21​log(1+x2)e−nx2dx=∫R​21​log(1+y2/n)e−y2dy⟶0.
(9)
Also ⟨T,φn​⟩→0 by the dominated convergence theorem: the relevant integrand is bounded by a constant times ∣λ∣e−∣λ∣ for n≥1. Consequently
c=0,⟨u,φ⟩=−πε↓0lim​∫∣λ∣>ε​∣λ∣φ(λ)−φ(0)​e−∣λ∣dλ.​
(10)
The cutoff limit agrees with the absolutely convergent integral; this particular subtraction convention fixes the otherwise ambiguous delta term. The reusable identity is Fourier transform of the logarithm of one plus x squared.

The space of test functions is D(R)=Cc∞​(R). A sequence φj​ converges to φ when its supports eventually lie in a single compact set and every derivative converges uniformly. The distribution space D′(R) is its continuous linear dual; weak convergence means ⟨uj​,φ⟩→⟨u,φ⟩ for every test function. We use ⟨u′,φ⟩=−⟨u,φ′⟩ and ⟨au,φ⟩=⟨u,aφ⟩ for multiplication of a distribution by a smooth function.

ψ(x)=∫−∞x​φ(t)dt
(11)
is another compactly supported smooth test function with ψ′=φ. Hence v′=0 implies ⟨v,φ⟩=−⟨v′,ψ⟩=0 for every such φ.
Choose a test function η with integral one. For arbitrary φ, the function φ−η∫φ has zero integral, so

The principal-value reciprocal distribution is
⟨pvx1​,φ⟩=limε↓0​∫∣x∣>ε​xφ(x)​dx.

v=pvx1​+cδ0​,c∈C.​
(14)
For real distributions the constants are real.

The distributional derivative of the Heaviside step function gives H′=δ0​, while ((x−1)H(x−1))′′=δ1​. A particular solution is therefore H(x)−2(x−1)H(x−1).
The difference from any other solution has second derivative zero. Applying a distribution with zero derivative is constant twice shows that difference is affine. Thus
u=H(x)−2(x−1)H(x−1)+ax+b,a,b∈C.​
(17)
The ramp function is continuous at x=1, and its first derivative jumps there, producing the indicated delta source in its second derivative.

The Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem in its closed-ball form says that a tempered distribution u is supported in BR​(0) if and only if its Fourier transform is the real restriction of an entire function F on Cn satisfying, for some C>0 and integer N≥0,
∣F(ζ)∣≤C(1+∣ζ∣)NeR∣Imζ∣.​
(20)
The distribution is unique by Fourier inversion. More generally a compact convex support set K replaces R∣Imζ∣ by its support function HK​(Imζ).
For the forward implication, a compactly supported distribution has finite order of a distribution, say m, on a fixed neighborhood of its support. Define
F(ζ)=⟨u,e−ix⋅ζ⟩,
(21)
using a cutoff equal to one near the support. Differentiation with respect to each complex coordinate is allowed in this pairing and gives ∂ζj​​F=⟨u,−ixj​e−ix⋅ζ⟩, so F is entire.
To obtain the exact radius R, rather than an enlarged radius, choose a cutoff function χε​ equal to one near BR​ and supported in BR+ε​, with derivatives through order m bounded by Cγ​ε−∣γ∣. Set ε=(1+∣ζ∣)−1. The finite-order bound and the Leibniz rule give
∣F(ζ)∣≤C(1+∣ζ∣)me(R+ε)∣Imζ∣≤Ce(1+∣ζ∣)meR∣Imζ∣.
(22)
This proves the required growth estimate.
For the converse, F has at most polynomial growth on real frequencies, so define its inverse tempered distribution by
⟨u,φ⟩=(2π)−n∫Rn​F(ξ)φ​(−ξ)dξ.
(23)
Let a compactly supported test function lie in a half-space x⋅ω≥R+η, with ∣ω∣=1 and η>0. By contour shifting,
⟨u,φ⟩=(2π)−n∫Rn​F(ξ+itω)φ​(−ξ−itω)dξ,t≥0.
(24)
Here is the decay needed to justify the shift and then let t grow. Repeated integration by parts, applying 1−Δx​ to e−tx⋅ωφ(x), gives for every integer M,
∣φ​(−ξ−itω)∣≤CM​(1+t)2Me−(R+η)t(1+∣ξ∣2)−M.
(25)
Choose 2M>N+n. For each fixed t, this decay makes the vertical sides of a large rectangle vanish in the one complex coordinate parallel to ω; Cauchy integral theorem then supplies the contour identity. Combining the two bounds yields
∣⟨u,φ⟩∣≤C′(1+t)N+2Me−ηt⟶0.
(26)
Thus u vanishes on such test functions. Every point outside BR​ has a neighborhood of this form. A partition of unity therefore proves suppu⊆BR​, completing the converse. The same argument with separating half-spaces proves the compact-convex-set form. This is the contour-shift proof of the Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem.
For a regular function u, the change of variables y=tx gives
∫u(tx)φ(x)dx=t−n∫u(y)φ(y/t)dy.
(27)
The map φ↦φ(⋅/t) is continuous on the Schwartz space, so duality extends the dilation of a distribution to
⟨δt​u,φ⟩=t−n⟨u,δ1/t​φ⟩.​
(28)
Its Fourier transform obeys δt​u​(ζ)=t−nF(ζ/t). If the original support is in the unit ball, the theorem bounds F by C(1+∣ζ∣)Ne∣Imζ∣. Hence
∣δt​u​(ζ)∣≤Ct​(1+∣ζ∣)Ne∣Imζ∣/t.
(29)
The converse theorem gives the support law for distributional dilation
supp(δt​u)⊆{x:∣x∣≤1/t}={x:∣tx∣≤1}.​
(30)
