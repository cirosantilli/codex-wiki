Treat each grain as an isolated compact sphere and take its wavelength-independent radiation-pressure efficiency to be Qpr​=1. At distance R, its mass and radiation-force cross-section are
md​=34​πrd3​ρd​,Ad​=πrd2​.

κd​=md​Ad​​=4ρd​rd​3​,LEdd,d​=κd​4πGMBH​c​.
(3)
To equal the Eddington luminosity based on electron-scattering opacity, κd​ must equal κes​. Therefore
rd,crit​=4ρd​κes​3​.​
(4)
For fully ionized hydrogen, κes​=σT​/mp​≃0.40cm2g−1, and the stated ρd​=1gcm−3 gives

(5)
Using 0.34cm2g−1 for a roughly solar-composition gas instead gives 2.2cm. This composition-dependent normalization should be stated because the problem does not fix it. For a different constant radiation-pressure efficiency, the critical radius is multiplied by Qpr​.
At L=LEdd,es​, the grain's radiation-pressure coefficient is
FG​Frad​​=κes​κd​​=rd​rd,crit​​.
(6)
Consequently, smaller grains experience a net outward force, larger grains remain gravitationally attracted, and critical-size grains have zero net radial force. Grains released at rest are therefore size-sorted into expelled and infalling populations. With an initial inward velocity, an outward force does not guarantee reversal before the grain reaches the hole; trajectories also depend on initial conditions. Gas drag and dust destruction have been omitted from this isolated-grain calculation. Ordinary interstellar grains are much smaller than a centimetre, so their direct radiative acceleration is very strong in this idealized model.

Use a geometrically thin spherical shell with constant mass Msh​ and assume that its dust transfers momentum efficiently to its gas. Define the ultraviolet and infrared opacities per unit total shell mass by κUV​ and κIR​. Its dust optical depths are

M˙Edd​=η0​c2LEdd,es​​=η0​κes​c4πGMBH​​≃6.6(0.1η0​​)−1(0.40cm2g−1κes​​)−1M⊙​yr−1.

M˙Edd​M˙dyn​​≃92(0.16fgas​​)(0.1η0​​)(0.40cm2g−1κes​​)(200kms−1σ​)−1.​
(32)
Thus a gas fraction of order 0.1--0.16 and the usual η0​=0.1 give roughly 60--90 times the Eddington accretion rate, below 100 at the requested dispersion. Using a crossing time R/(2​σ) instead changes the coefficient by 2​, so this is an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than a sharp universal bound. The problem does not specify fgas​ or the rate convention: without these choices, the numerical claim cannot be proved for every gas fraction. Defining the rate as LEdd​/c2 would make this ratio ten times larger.
This is extremely optimistic because it gives every gas element a direct path to the hole on a crossing time. In a real galaxy, gas must shed angular momentum, avoid conversion into stars through star formation, cool sufficiently to flow inward, and survive active-galactic-nucleus feedback. A rotating accretion disk generally transports material on a viscous timescale, much longer than the orbital crossing time. Most available gas need not reach the central hole. If the hole is supplied at approximately the Eddington accretion rate, only about one percent of this optimistic dynamical supply reaches it; the actual percentage depends on its luminosity and duty cycle, so the bound alone is not a measurement of the fuelling efficiency.
For the temperature jump, use Bondi accretion as the local hot-gas estimate:
M˙B​∝ρ∞​cs−3​.
(33)

(34)

The result illustrates thermal suppression of black-hole accretion and negative active-galactic-nucleus feedback: vigorous activity can shut off its own gas supply. The factor 10−3 is conditional on the fixed-density comparison; the previous dynamical upper bound is not itself an actual pre-heating Bondi rate.

Work in the rest frame of the hole and neglect stellar velocities compared with v. In addition to v≫σ∗​, assume the galactic encounter speed is nonrelativistic, v≪c. Treat a star as a test particle with specific angular momentum ℓ=bv. At its pericentre rmin​, its radial velocity vanishes. Conservation of energy and conservation of angular momentum give
2v2​=2rmin2​ℓ2​−rmin​GMBH​​,b2=rmin2​+v22GMBH​rmin​​.
(35)
The second term is gravitational focusing. For the rough Newtonian estimate in the question, assume capture when the pericentre reaches the Schwarzschild radius, rS​=2GMBH​/c2. It follows that
bcap2​=rS2​(1+v2c2​),bcap​≃cv2GMBH​​.​
(36)
The approximation keeps the focusing term because v≪c.
Let ρ∗​=mn∗​ be the stellar mass density. Multiplying the geometric collision cross-section by the incoming mass flux gives the collisionless black-hole capture rate
M˙∗​≃πbcap2​ρ∗​v=c2v4πG2MBH2​ρ∗​​.
(37)
For a supersonic gas with negligible sound speed, Bondi--Hoyle--Lyttleton accretion has capture radius ra​=2GMBH​/v2 and rate
M˙BHL​≃πra2​ρg​v=v34πG2MBH2​ρg​​.
(38)
Consequently
M˙BHL​M˙∗​​≃ρg​ρ∗​​(cv​)2.​
(39)
For comparable densities and v=200kms−1, the suppression is approximately 4×10−7. Gas can collide, dissipate orbital energy, and be captured from a region of size GM/v2; collisionless particles generally leave on an unbound orbit unless their angular momentum is small enough for direct capture. Chandrasekhar dynamical friction can slow the hole by transferring energy to a stellar wake, but does not mean that the stars producing that wake are swallowed.
There is an important accuracy limitation in the requested numerical coefficient. Newtonian orbital mechanics evaluated at a relativistic event horizon cannot give the exact capture boundary. In Schwarzschild spacetime, a slowly incoming massive particle has dimensionless energy E≃1 and timelike geodesic effective potential
V(r)=(1−r2rg​​)(1+c2r2ℓ2​),rg​=c2GMBH​​.
(40)
The double turning point satisfying V=1 and dV/dr=0 occurs at r=4rg​ and ℓ=4GMBH​/c. The exact low-speed boundary is therefore bcap​≃4GMBH​/(cv), twice the rough value; it makes the rate ratio 4(ρ∗​/ρg​)(v/c)2. The key suppression scaling is unchanged.
Direct capture of ordinary stars or dark matter is therefore an inefficient route to rapid growth of a supermassive black hole under these conditions. Tidal disruption events can capture stellar debris without swallowing an intact point-like star, and special dense environments can modify the supply. These qualifications do not make collisionless capture equivalent to dissipative gas accretion.

The usual Pressure-opacity zones of a Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk, in order of increasing cylindrical radius, are an inner region dominated by radiation pressure and electron-scattering opacity, a middle region dominated by gas pressure and electron-scattering opacity, and an outer region dominated by gas pressure and the Kramers' opacity law, often approximated by free-free opacity. The boundaries depend on mass, mass accretion rate, and the alpha disk viscosity parameter. At sufficiently large radius, accretion-disk self-gravity or changes in ionization can invalidate this three-zone model.

ΩK2​=R3GMBH​​,f(R)=1−RR∗​​​,F(R)=8πR33GMBH​m˙​f(R).

Analyze a local temperature perturbation at fixed radius and fixed surface density of a disk Σ. The thermal timescale of an accretion disk is shorter than its viscous timescale, so mass redistribution is negligible during the perturbation. Let Q+ be viscous heating and Q− radiative cooling, measured consistently through one face or both faces, and define net cooling by Q˙​=Q−−Q+. A temperature increase is unstable if it decreases net cooling.
With gas pressure dominant, vertical hydrostatic equilibrium and the ideal gas law give
cs2​∝Tc​,H∝Tc1/2​,ρc​∼2HΣ​∝Tc−1/2​.
(46)
For the alpha disk prescription with temperature-independent α, ν=αcs​H∝Tc​, and the Keplerian rotation heating law is
Q+=89​νΣΩK2​∝Tc​.
(47)
One must not hold the steady-state m˙ fixed while perturbing the temperature: the stress and heating respond through ν, even though Σ is temporarily fixed.
At an equilibrium temperature T0​ where Q+=Q−=Q0​, if Q−∝Tcq​ and Q+∝Tc​, then
dTc​dQ˙​​​T0​​=(q−1)T0​Q0​​.
(48)
The cooling exponent therefore decides the sign.
For hot, ionized, optically thin gas, the usual intended cooling model is thermal bremsstrahlung, whose volume emissivity scales as ρ2Tc1/2​. Integrated vertically,
Qff−​∝Hρc2​Tc1/2​∝HΣ2​Tc1/2​∝Tc0​.
(49)
Thus q=0, and
dTc​dQ˙​​=−T0​Q0​​<0:the optically thin bremsstrahlung branch is unstable.​
(50)
A hotter annulus expands vertically, lowering its density enough to offset the direct increase in bremsstrahlung emission; viscous heating still rises.
For an optically thick medium with the Kramers' opacity law, κ∝ρc​Tc−7/2​∝Tc−4​ at fixed Σ. Thermalized radiative diffusion gives
Q−∝κΣTc4​​∝Tc8​,dTc​dQ˙​​=7T0​Q0​​>0:the gas-pressure Kramers branch is stable.​
(51)
Its falling opacity lets a hotter annulus radiate much more efficiently.
For an optically thick medium with dominant electron-scattering opacity, κ is nearly independent of temperature. Provided enough true absorption is present to thermalize the radiation,
Q−∝Tc4​,dTc​dQ˙​​=3T0​Q0​​>0:the gas-pressure electron-scattering branch is stable.​
(52)
This conclusion differs from the thermal instability of a radiation-pressure-dominated alpha disk, where the heating has a different temperature dependence. The question explicitly excludes that pressure regime.
The first conclusion needs its cooling assumption stated. T>104K alone does not guarantee that thermal bremsstrahlung dominates; atomic-line cooling, ionization changes, external illumination, or energy advection can change the answer. For a general optically thin emissivity ρ2Λ(Tc​), the corresponding exponent is

(53)
Accordingly, under the same fixed-Σ alpha prescription,
optically thin thermal stability requires dlogTc​dlogΛ​>23​.​
(54)
The unstable/stable/stable classification is the standard free-free/diffusion answer, rather than a universal result specified by temperature and optical depth alone.

(63)
If the quoted 0.1M⊙​yr−1 were instead the actual gravitating mass-growth rate, the corresponding endpoint time would be ΔMBH​/M˙BH​≃1.45×109yr. The two rate conventions cannot be interchanged.
The exact extremal endpoint is an idealization. Capture of disc photons exerts a counteracting torque and gives the Thorne spin limit, a∗​≃0.998, for the standard radiatively efficient thin-disc model. Therefore physical disc accretion approaches a large spin below unity rather than producing an exactly extremal hole. Thorne's original spin-evolution calculation describes that correction. The independently integrated expressions above explain why the frozen-mass estimate is too short for the ideal endpoint.
The qualitative conclusion is robust: a hole that gains most of its mass through a persistent, coherently corotating Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk is expected to have substantial black-hole spin. Very low spins require a different angular-momentum history, such as short episodes with changing orientations, counterrotating accretion, or mergers. Coherent feeding, not merely the existence of a disc during each episode, is the decisive assumption.
The Soltan argument offers a population-level observational test. From the bolometric luminosity function of active galactic nuclei, infer the total energy radiated per comoving volume over cosmic history,
UAGN​=∫dt∫LΦ(L,t)dL.
(64)
Compare it with the increase ΔρBH​ in supermassive black hole mass density attributed to radiative accretion. For an effective radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion ηeff​,
ΔρBH​=ηeff​1−ηeff​​c2UAGN​​,ηeff​=UAGN​+ΔρBH​c2UAGN​​.​
(65)
In a zero-torque relativistic thin disc, η=1−EISCO​ rises from about 0.057 at zero spin to 0.423 in the photon-free prograde extremal limit. The measured effective efficiency therefore constrains population spin models under the thin-disc assumption. Soltan's original population argument explains how integrated quasar light constrains accumulated black-hole mass.
This is an accreted-rest-mass-weighted efficiency constraint, not a direct measurement of the arithmetic mean spin of every black hole. Obscured emission, bolometric corrections, seed masses, nonradiative growth, and disc orientation introduce uncertainty, and the efficiency-spin relation is nonlinear. These qualifications are essential when interpreting an inferred average spin for the whole population.
