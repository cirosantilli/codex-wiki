An aleph number is an infinite initial ordinal. More explicitly,
ℵ0​=ω,ℵα+1​=the least cardinal greater than ℵα​,ℵλ​=supα<λ​ℵα​
(149)
for a limit ordinal λ. By the well-ordering theorem, every set is equipotent to an ordinal and hence to a unique initial ordinal. If the set is infinite, that initial ordinal occurs in the aleph enumeration. Thus every infinite set has cardinality ℵα​ for a unique ordinal α.
We next prove the square of an infinite cardinal. Suppose otherwise and let κ be the least infinite cardinal for which κ2>κ. Well-order κ×κ by increasing
max{α,β},
(150)
breaking ties lexicographically. The predecessors of (α,β) lie inside γ×γ for some γ<κ. If μ=∣γ∣, then μ<κ; by minimality, μ2=μ when μ is infinite, while the finite case is immediate. Every proper initial segment therefore has cardinality less than κ.
Recursively assign to each pair the least ordinal below κ not already assigned to one of its predecessors. Such an ordinal always exists by the preceding bound, so this constructs an injection κ×κ→κ. The map α↦(α,0) gives the reverse injection, and the Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem yields
κ2=κ.​
(151)
For infinite X1​,…,Xn​, finitely many applications of the cardinal comparability principle let us choose Xi​ of largest cardinality κ. Then
κ≤∣X1​∪⋯∪Xn​∣≤∣X1​∣+⋯+∣Xn​∣≤nκ=κ,
(152)
where the final equality follows from infinite cardinal arithmetic. Consequently
∣X1​∪⋯∪Xn​∣=∣Xi​∣ for some i.​
(153)
For a countable family of pairwise different infinite cardinalities, the answer is yes. Regard initial ordinals as sets and take
X1​=ℵω​,Xn+2​=ℵn​(n<ω).
(154)
The cardinalities are pairwise distinct, but every Xn+2​ is a subset of X1​. Hence
j≥1⋃​Xj​=X1​,​
(155)
which is the countable family of distinct infinite cardinalities with a largest member construction.
