Use E=−∇ϕ and Gauss's law ∇⋅E=ρ/ϵ0​. The divergence theorem and the decay at infinity give
∫ρϕdV=ϵ0​∫ϕ∇⋅EdV=ϵ0​∫∇⋅(ϕE)dV−ϵ0​∫E⋅∇ϕdV=ϵ0​∫∣E∣2dV.

Multiplying by 1/2 proves that the two expressions for the electrostatic energy agree.
The uniform volume charge density in the thick shell is
ρ0​=4π(b3−a3)3Q​.
(94)
Spherical symmetry and the integral form of Gauss's law give
E(r)=⎩⎨⎧​0,4πϵ0​Q​(b3−a3)r2r3−a3​r,4πϵ0​r2Q​r,​0≤r<a,a≤r≤b,r>b.​​

Thus ∣E∣ is zero inside, rises continuously through the charged region, reaches Q/(4πϵ0​b2) at b, and then decays as r−2. Taking ϕ(∞)=0 and integrating E=−dϕ/dr gives
ϕ(r)=⎩⎨⎧​ϕ(a),4πϵ0​Q​[b1​+b3−a3(b2−r2)/2+a3(1/b−1/r)​],4πϵ0​rQ​,​r<a,a≤r≤b,r>b.​
(96)
The electric potential is continuous, constant inside, decreases smoothly across the charge, and continues as 1/r outside.
As b→a, the charge becomes a surface shell. Then
E={0,Qr/(4πϵ0​r2),​r<a,r>a,​ϕ={Q/(4πϵ0​a),Q/(4πϵ0​r),​r≤a,r≥a.​
(97)
The potential remains continuous, but the normal electric field jumps by Q/(4πϵ0​a2) across the surface charge density.
The field-energy expression gives
U=2ϵ0​​∫a∞​(4πϵ0​r2Q​)2,4πr2dr=8πϵ0​aQ2​=21​Qϕ(a).
(98)
The charge-potential expression gives the same result directly because the entire charge lies where the potential equals ϕ(a):
U=21​∫ρϕdV=21​Qϕ(a).
(99)
This is the electrostatic energy of a uniformly charged spherical shell.
Since ϕ(a)=Q/(4πϵ0​a) is proportional to Q,
δU=δ(8πϵ0​aQ2​)=ϕ(a)δQ​
(100)
to first order. This is the work required to bring the additional charge δQ from infinity to the potential created by the charge already present.
