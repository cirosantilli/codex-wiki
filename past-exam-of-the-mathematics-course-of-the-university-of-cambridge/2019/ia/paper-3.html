Conjugating a cycle merely relabels its entries:
g(a1​ a2​ ⋯ ak​)g−1=(g(a1​) g(a2​) ⋯ g(ak​)).
(1)
Thus conjugate permutations have the same cycle type. Conversely, if σ and τ have the same cycle type, match the entries of each cycle of σ bijectively and in cyclic order with those of a cycle of τ of the same length. Extending these matches to a permutation g gives gσg−1=τ.
Let CSn​​(σ) be the centralizer of σ. Its Sn​-conjugacy class remains one An​-conjugacy class exactly when CSn​​(σ) contains an odd permutation. Indeed, in that case CAn​​(σ) has index two in CSn​​(σ), so the orbit-stabilizer theorem gives
∣ClAn​​(σ)∣=∣CAn​​(σ)∣∣An​∣​=∣CSn​​(σ)∣∣Sn​∣​=∣ClSn​​(σ)∣.
(2)
If the centralizer contains only even permutations, the An​ class has half the size and the Sn​ class splits into two An​ classes.
The even cycle types in S5​ are
15,312,221,5.
(3)
The classes of the identity, a three-cycle, and a double transposition do not split: their centralizers contain an odd permutation. The centralizer of a five-cycle is its cyclic subgroup of order five, which lies in A5​, so that class splits in two. Therefore
A5​ has 1+1+1+2=5 conjugacy classes​.
(4)

The orthogonal group is
O(n)={Q∈Mn​(R):QTQ=I}.
(5)
The special orthogonal group is its determinant-one subgroup
SO(n)={Q∈O(n):detQ=1}.
(6)
Every eigenvalue of an orthogonal matrix has modulus one. A real three-by-three matrix has at least one real eigenvalue, and nonreal eigenvalues occur in a complex conjugate pair. If Q∈SO(3) has such a pair e±iθ, their product is one, so the remaining eigenvalue is detQ=1. If all three eigenvalues are real, each is ±1 and their product is one; an odd number of three signs with product one must include +1. Hence every element of SO(3) has an eigenvector of eigenvalue one and represents a rotation in three dimensions about its span.
It is false that every element of O(3) is either a rotation or a plane reflection. For example,
Q=​cosθsinθ0​−sinθcosθ0​00−1​​,0<θ<π,
(7)
is an improper orthogonal transformation combining a rotation with a reflection. Its eigenvalues are e±iθ and −1, whereas a plane reflection has eigenvalues 1,1,−1 and a rotation has determinant one.

Cauchy's theorem for finite groups states that if a prime number p divides the order of a finite group G, then G contains an element of order p.
Consider
X={(x1​,…,xp​)∈Gp:x1​x2​⋯xp​=e}.
(19)
The first p−1 entries determine the last, so ∣X∣=∣G∣p−1 is divisible by p. The cyclic group Cp​ acts on X by cyclically shifting the coordinates; the shifted tuple remains in X because x2​⋯xp​x1​=x1−1​(x1​⋯xp​)x1​=e. Every orbit has size one or p. The fixed points are exactly (x,…,x) with xp=e. Their number is therefore divisible by p. Since the identity is one fixed point, another exists; its order is p.
Now let the abelian group G have order pq for distinct primes p,q. Cauchy's theorem supplies x,y of orders p,q. Their cyclic subgroups intersect trivially, and commutativity shows that xy has order pq. Thus G is cyclic and
G≅Cpq​≅Cp​×Cq​​.
(20)
The corresponding assertion for order p2 is false: Cp2​ is not isomorphic to Cp​×Cp​, since the former has an element of order p2 while every nonidentity element of the latter has order p.

Lagrange's theorem for finite groups states that if H≤G and G is finite, then
∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣,
(21)
so ∣H∣ divides ∣G∣. Indeed, the left cosets of H partition G, and multiplication by a coset representative is a bijection from H to each coset.
Applying the theorem to the cyclic subgroup ⟨g⟩ gives
ord(g)=∣⟨g⟩∣∣∣G∣.
(22)
In C3​×C9​, an element has order dividing three exactly when its first coordinate is arbitrary and its second belongs to the unique subgroup of order three. There are 3⋅3=9 such elements, and after removing the identity the answer is 8​.

The first isomorphism theorem for groups states that for a group homomorphism f:G→Q,
G/kerf≅imf.
(26)
The map gkerf↦f(g) is well defined because two representatives differ by an element of the kernel; it is a surjective homomorphism onto the image and is injective because f(g)=e exactly when g∈kerf.
For ni​∈N and hi​∈H, normality of N gives
(n1​h1​)(n2​h2​)=n1​(h1​n2​h1−1​)h1​h2​∈NH,
(27)
and (nh)−1=h−1n−1hh−1∈NH. Hence NH is a subgroup. Also N∩H⊴H, since conjugation by H preserves both N and H, and N⊴NH because NH≤G.
Apply the first isomorphism theorem to f:H→NH/N, f(h)=hN. Its kernel is N∩H, and it is surjective because nhN=hN. Therefore
H/(N∩H)≅NH/N​.
(28)
If K,H⊴G, then KH is a normal subgroup: it is a subgroup by the calculation above, and g(KH)g−1=(gKg−1)(gHg−1)=KH. Without normality a product need not be a subgroup. In S3​, take H=⟨(12)⟩ and K=⟨(23)⟩. The set KH has four elements, so it cannot be a subgroup of the six-element group by Lagrange's theorem for finite groups.

Represent a Möbius transformation by a matrix, with nonzero scalar multiples representing the same map. Matrix multiplication reproduces composition, so θ is a group homomorphism. Every Möbius map has a representing matrix A∈GL2​(C); multiplying A by either square root of (detA)−1 gives a matrix in SL2​(C) representing the same map. Thus θ is surjective. A matrix in its kernel represents the identity and is therefore scalar; determinant one leaves
kerθ={I,−I}​.
(29)
If a nonidentity Möbius map T has two distinct fixed points, conjugate those points to 0 and ∞. The conjugated map fixes both and hence has the form S(z)=μz with μ=0,1. If T has one fixed point, conjugate it to ∞. The resulting affine map has the form az+b; having no finite fixed point forces a=1 and b=0, and conjugating by a scaling makes it S(z)=z+1.
Directly, the finite fixed points satisfy
cz2+(d−a)z−b=0.
(30)
Including ∞ when appropriate, a nonidentity Möbius map consequently has one or two distinct fixed points.
Finally suppose T has the unique fixed point z0​. With the conjugacy R above,
RTR−1(z)=z+1,(RTR−1)n(z)=z+n.
(31)
Every finite point tends to ∞ on the Riemann sphere, while ∞ itself is fixed. Applying the continuous map R−1 shows that
Tn(z)⟶z0​​
(32)
for every z∈C∪{∞}.
