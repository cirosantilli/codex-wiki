A set is countable if it is finite or admits an injection into the natural numbers; equivalently, its elements can be listed by a finite list or a sequence.
The product of two countable sets is countable: after choosing enumerations, its elements are indexed by pairs (m,n)∈N2, and these pairs can be listed diagonally. Thus an injection X→A×B followed by an injection A×B→N proves that X is countable.
The integers are listed as 0,1,−1,2,−2,…, so Z is countable. Every rational number is represented by a pair (p,q)∈Z×N>0​, so Q is countable as the image of a countable set.
Finally, if A0​,A1​,… are countable, choose listings An​={an,0​,an,1​,…}, allowing repetitions for finite sets. The diagonal listing of the pairs (n,m) lists every an,m​, proving that a countable union of countable sets is countable.

b OurBigBook.com
Fix a positive rational radius r. Distinct disjoint circles of radius r have centers more than 2r apart, so their open interior disks are pairwise disjoint. Each such disk contains a point of the countable dense set Q2. Fix an enumeration of Q2 and assign to each disk the first rational point it contains. Disjointness makes this assignment injective, so there are only countably many circles of radius r.
The positive rational numbers are countable. Taking the countable union of countable sets over all rational radii proves that the whole collection is countable.
