The intermediate value theorem states that if f:[a,b]→R is continuous and y lies between f(a) and f(b), then some c∈[a,b] satisfies f(c)=y.
Assume f(a)<y<f(b) and let
S={x∈[a,b]:f(x)≤y},c=supS.
(67)
The least-upper-bound property makes c well defined. If f(c)<y, continuity gives points immediately to the right of c in S, contradicting that c is an upper bound. If f(c)>y, continuity gives a left neighborhood of c disjoint from S, contradicting the definition of the supremum. Thus f(c)=y. The endpoint and reversed-order cases follow directly or by replacing f with −f.
The mean value theorem states that if g is continuous on [α,β] and differentiable on (α,β), then some c∈(α,β) satisfies
g′(c)=β−αg(β)−g(α)​.
(68)
For the functions in the question, the definition of the derivative makes h continuous at a and f continuous at b, with
h(a)=g′(a)<k,h(b)=b−ag(b)−g(a)​=f(a),f(b)=g′(b)>k.
(69)
If the middle secant slope equals k, take [α,β]=[a,b]. If it exceeds k, the intermediate value theorem applied to h gives β∈(a,b) with h(β)=k, and we take α=a. If it is below k, apply the theorem to f to obtain α∈(a,b) with f(α)=k, and take β=b. In every case
β−αg(β)−g(α)​=k.
(70)
The mean value theorem on this subinterval then produces c∈(α,β)⊂(a,b) with
g′(c)=k​.
(71)
This is the Darboux theorem for derivatives for the stated pair of derivative values.
