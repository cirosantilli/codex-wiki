For a nonempty set S⊆R, an upper bound in a partially ordered set is a real number u such that s≤u for every s∈S. Its supremum, or least upper bound, is an upper bound L with L≤u for every upper bound u. The least-upper-bound property asserts that every nonempty set of real numbers bounded above has a real supremum.
Let (xn​) be a bounded, increasing sequence; here increasing allows xn+1​=xn​. Apply the least-upper-bound property to its set of terms and put L=sup{xn​:n≥1}. Given ε>0, the number L−ε cannot be an upper bound, so some xN​ satisfies xN​>L−ε. The monotone sequence property then gives
L−ε<xN​≤xn​≤L(n≥N).
(4)
Hence ∣xn​−L∣<ε for all n≥N. This proves the monotone bounded sequence result, with limit xn​⟶L​.
For the two series, define their partial sums AN​=∑n=1N​an​ and BN​=∑n=1N​bn​. Positivity makes both partial-sum sequences increasing, and termwise comparison gives 0<BN​≤AN​. Since (AN​) is a convergent sequence, it is bounded above, and therefore so is (BN​). Applying the monotone bounded sequence result already proved shows that (BN​) converges. By the definition of a convergent series, ∑n=1∞​bn​ converges. This is the comparison test for series for positive terms.

In a circular orbit, r=a is constant, so the radial acceleration vanishes. The radial equation requires
h2=kan+3​.
(12)
For every a>0, this equation permits a real nonzero specific angular momentum, h=±kan+3​, and hence a circular orbit. This chooses h for the desired radius; it does not claim that one prescribed value of h permits every radius.
To test circular-orbit stability for a power-law central force, keep that chosen specific angular momentum fixed and write r=a+ρ, with ∣ρ∣≪a. The radial equation becomes
ρ¨​=(a+ρ)3h2​−k(a+ρ)n.
(13)
Expanding to first order and using h2=kan+3 gives
ρ¨​=−(a43h2​+knan−1)ρ+O(ρ2)=−(n+3)kan−1ρ+O(ρ2).
(14)
For n>−3, the linearized equation is the harmonic oscillator equation, with
Ωr2​=(n+3)kan−1>0​.
(15)
The radial disturbance undergoes bounded small oscillations, rather than growing. Equivalently, the effective potential has a strict local minimum: its second derivative at a is (n+3)kan−1>0. By the effective potential stability criterion, sufficiently small radial disturbances remain small, proving stability for n>−3. For comparison, n<−3 gives exponential growth. Suppose f:S→P(S) were a surjective function. Form the subset
D={s∈S:s∈/f(s)}.
(16)
Since D belongs to the power set, surjectivity would give D=f(d) for some d∈S. But then d∈D holds if and only if d∈/f(d)=D, a contradiction. Thus no such function is surjective, and in particular there is no bijection from S to its power set. This is Cantor theorem; the argument also applies when S is empty.
For infinite binary sequences, use the bijection
(xn​)n≥1​⟼{n∈N:xn​=1}
(17)
with P(N), taking N={1,2,…}. Conversely, a subset determines its sequence of membership indicators. The power set is infinite, since it contains all singletons, and Cantor theorem rules out a bijection with N. Hence it is an uncountable set, as is the set of infinite binary sequences. Equivalently, a proposed enumeration (xn(j)​)n≥1​ is defeated by the sequence yj​=1−xj(j)​, which differs from its jth listed sequence at position j. This is the Cantor diagonal argument.

Its exhaust has inertial velocity v(τ)−u, by the Galilean transformation of velocities. Once expelled, that material retains its velocity because it experiences no external force. In the Earth inertial frame, the return journey takes 2d/v. Bob is at rest in this frame, while Alice's proper time is reduced by the Lorentz factor γ=(1−v2/c2)−1/2. Neglecting the short turnaround interval, their increases in age are

Here arcosh is the nonnegative real inverse hyperbolic cosine. As a check, if α=coshη∗​ with η∗​>0, then Bob ages by 2t0​sinhη∗​ and Alice by 2t0​η∗​; sinhη∗​>η∗​ confirms that Bob again ages more.

Use an Earth-centred rotating reference frame, with Earth's constant angular velocity vector ω. The vectors r, v, and a are the particle's position, velocity, and acceleration measured in that rotating frame. The vector g is the gravitational acceleration due to the actual gravitational force, expressed in the same axes; it excludes the separate centrifugal acceleration. The remaining term −2ω×v is the Coriolis acceleration. A constant rotation axis and an unaccelerated Earth-centred origin account for the absence of an Euler term or a translational-origin term.
For the size estimates, Earth's angular speed is
ω=9×1042π​≈7.0×10−5 s−1.
(60)
With drop height H=20 m, unperturbed free fall gives the fall time
T=g2H​​=2 s​,
(61)
and final speed gT=20 ms−1. Thus the gravitational term has size 10 ms−2, the Coriolis acceleration is at most about 2ωgT≈2.8×10−3 ms−2, and the centrifugal acceleration is at most ω2R0​≈2.9×10−2 ms−2. These are characteristic sizes 101, 10−3, and 10−2 in acceleration units; geometric factors can reduce them, and the Coriolis term is zero at release.
For a suitable approximation to the rotational deflection of a falling body, take g constant over the short fall, start at rest relative to Earth, and use the zeroth-order velocity v(0)(t)=gt inside the small Coriolis term. In the centrifugal term replace r by R0​, because H/R0​≪1. Integrating the resulting acceleration twice, with initial position ri​, gives
r(t)≈ri​+21​gt2−31​ω×gt3−21​ω×(ω×R0​)t2.
(62)
Since ri​+gT2/2=R0​, evaluating at the unperturbed fall time yields the printed first-iterate estimate
R≈R0​−31​ω×gT3−21​ω×(ω×R0​)T2​.
(63)
The approximation keeps the leading Coriolis contribution and the leading centrifugal contribution enhanced by Earth's large radius. Feeding the Coriolis velocity correction back into the equation produces terms of size ω2gT4, smaller than the retained centrifugal displacement by a factor of order H/R0​. Replacing r by R0​ in that term has the same relative error. Here ωT≈1.4×10−4 and H/R0​≈3.3×10−6, so both approximations are well controlled. Strictly, the displayed vector is the position at time T: its small upward component is removed by the slightly longer actual fall time. That arrival-time correction changes the retained horizontal displacements only at higher order. Thus the horizontal components of the printed estimate give the landing point on the locally flat surface; its vertical component should not be interpreted as a landing above the ground.
Let e^E​,e^N​,e^U​ point east, north, and vertically upwards. They form a right-handed basis, with e^N​×e^U​=e^E​. At latitude λ, in the spherical-Earth approximation,
ω=ω(cosλe^N​+sinλe^U​),g=−ge^U​,R0​=R0​e^U​.
(64)
The required cross products are
ω×g=−ωgcosλe^E​,ω×(ω×R0​)=ω2R0​(sinλcosλe^N​−cos2λe^U​).
(65)
At λ=45∘, the northerly and easterly displacements are therefore
ΔN=−4ω2R0​T2​,ΔE=32​ωgT3​​.
(66)
The negative northerly component means a southward displacement. With the supplied values, these are approximately 2.9 cm south and 1.3 mm east. The eastward effect comes from Coriolis acceleration, while the leading southward effect here comes from centrifugal acceleration.
