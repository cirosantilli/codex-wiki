Suppose every group commutator belongs to H. For h∈H and g∈G, the particular group commutator h−1g−1hg belongs to H. Multiplying on the left by h gives g−1hg∈H. Thus g−1Hg⊆H for every g; applying the same inclusion to g−1 gives the reverse inclusion. Therefore H is a normal subgroup.
We can now form the quotient group. Its group commutators are
(aH)−1(bH)−1(aH)(bH)=(a−1b−1ab)H=H.
(1)
A group commutator is the identity element exactly when the two elements commute. Hence G/H is an abelian group. Equivalently, the hypothesis says that H contains the commutator subgroup, so the quotient factors through the abelianization.

Lagrange's theorem states that, for a finite group G and a subgroup H,
∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣.​
(5)
To prove it, define an equivalence relation by x∼y when x−1y∈H. Its classes are the left cosets xH, which therefore partition G. Left multiplication by x gives a bijection H→xH, so every coset has ∣H∣ elements. Counting this partition gives the formula; in particular ∣H∣ divides ∣G∣. Applying the theorem to the cyclic subgroup generated by an element shows that its order of a group element divides ∣G∣.
Write the given order-two normal subgroup as N={1,z}. Conjugation must preserve N and cannot send its nonidentity element to the identity, so gzg−1=z for all g∈G. Thus z is in the center of a group: a normal subgroup of order two is central.
Choose g∈/N. By Lagrange's theorem, its order of a group element is 2, p or 2p. It cannot have order two: since z is central, the four distinct elements 1,z,g,zg would form a subgroup of order four, whereas 4 does not divide 2p for odd p. If g has order 2p, it already generates G. Otherwise g has order p, commutes with z, and (gz)k=1 implies gk=z−k∈⟨g⟩∩N={1}. Hence both p and 2 divide k, so gz has order 2p. In either case
G≅C2p​.​
(6)

For a group action of a finite group G on a set and a point x, the orbit-stabilizer theorem is
∣Gx∣=[G:Gx​],∣G∣=∣Gx​∣∣Gx∣,​
(26)
where Gx​={g:gx=x} is the stabilizer subgroup. The map gGx​↦gx is well defined because elements of the same coset act identically on x. If gx=hx, then h−1g∈Gx​, so their cosets agree; every orbit point has the form gx, giving surjectivity. This is a bijection. Lagrange's theorem, ∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣ for any subgroup H, yields the counting formula.
Apply the conjugation action of the finite p-group G to its nontrivial normal subgroup N. Normality ensures the action stays inside N. The singleton orbits of a group action are exactly the elements of N∩Z(G). Every larger orbit of a group action has size a positive power of p, by the orbit-stabilizer theorem and Lagrange's theorem. Therefore
∣N∣≡∣N∩Z(G)∣(modp).
(27)
Since N is nontrivial, its order is divisible by p. The intersection contains the identity and has cardinality a positive multiple of p, so it contains a nonidentity element. This proves the central intersection property of normal subgroups of finite p-groups.
For the proper subgroup H, let H act by left multiplication on the left cosets G/H. A coset gH is fixed by every h∈H exactly when g−1Hg⊆H. Both groups have the same finite size, so this inclusion is equality; the fixed cosets are precisely those with g∈NG​(H). Hence their number is [NG​(H):H]. All non-singleton orbits of a group action have size divisible by p, so
[G:H]≡[NG​(H):H](modp).
(28)
Since H is proper, [G:H] is a positive power of p greater than one. The fixed-coset count is nonzero, because H itself is fixed, and is divisible by p. Therefore the normalizer strictly contains H:
[NG​(H):H]≥p,∃g∈G∖H: g−1Hg=H.​
(29)
This is the normalizer condition for finite p-groups. The proof also covers H={1}, whose action fixes all cosets.

The Möbius group consists of all Möbius transformations of the Riemann sphere C∞​=C∪{∞}, with operation function composition. A nonzero-determinant matrix M=(ac​bd​) represents the map z↦(az+b)/(cz+d). If c=0, its pole −d/c maps to ∞ and ∞ maps to a/c; if c=0, the map is affine and fixes ∞. Nonzero scalar multiples of M represent the same transformation.
Define ϕ(M) by this action for M∈SL2​(C). Substitution of fractional-linear expressions shows ϕ(MN)=ϕ(M)∘ϕ(N), so ϕ is a group homomorphism. Any invertible matrix can be rescaled into the special linear group: choose λ∈C× with λ2detM=1, which is possible because every nonzero complex number has a square root. This does not change the transformation, proving surjectivity. A transformation represented by M is the identity only if fixing 0, ∞ and 1 forces b=c=0 and a=d. The determinant-one condition then gives a2=1. Thus
kerϕ={I,−I},M≅SL2​(C)/{±I}.​
(30)
For the fixed-point claim, a nonidentity Möbius transformation has at least one fixed point of a Möbius transformation: when c=0 the finite fixed points solve a quadratic, and when c=0 infinity is fixed. A quadratic shows there can be no more than two distinct fixed points unless the transformation is the identity. Suppose there is exactly one. Conjugate it to infinity by a Möbius transformation. The resulting map has form z↦az+b, since it fixes infinity. If a=1 it also has the finite fixed point b/(1−a), a contradiction. Hence it is z↦z+b with b=0. Its mth power is z↦z+mb, never the identity for m>0. Conjugation preserves order, so a nonidentity finite-order Möbius transformation has exactly two fixed points.
Now let K be a finite abelian subgroup of the Möbius group. First suppose it contains an element g of order greater than two. Conjugate the two fixed points of g to 0 and ∞, so g(z)=ζz, with ζ a root of unity of order greater than two. Every element h commuting with g permutes its fixed-point set, because g(h(p))=h(g(p))=h(p). A Möbius transformation preserving each of 0,∞ is h(z)=az; one exchanging them is h(z)=a/z. In the second case hg(z)=a/(ζz) and gh(z)=ζa/z, so commuting would force ζ2=1, impossible. Thus all elements of K are scalings. A finite subgroup of a field multiplicative group is cyclic; here one can see this directly by choosing the least common multiple m of all element orders and observing that the scalars lie among the mth roots of unity, a cyclic group. Hence K is cyclic.
Otherwise every nonidentity element of K has order two. If K is nontrivial, choose g and conjugate it to z↦−z. The action of K on {0,∞} has image of order at most two. Its kernel consists of scalings z↦az, and the order-two condition forces a2=1, so the kernel has at most two elements. Hence ∣K∣≤4. Orders one and two give cyclic groups; order four with all nonidentity elements involutions gives C2​×C2​, generated by any two distinct nonidentity elements. Both possibilities occur: scalings by roots of unity give every finite cyclic group, and {z,−z,1/z,−1/z} gives the Klein four group. The classification is
K is cyclic, or K≅C2​×C2​.​
(31)

A conservative vector field on Rn is a vector field V=∇F for a globally defined smooth potential of a conservative vector field F. The chain rule gives
∫C​V⋅dx=F(endpoint)−F(startpoint),
(32)
so its line integral is path independent.
Green theorem states that, for a bounded planar region D with piecewise smooth boundary and continuously differentiable P,Q on a neighbourhood of its closure,
∮∂D​(Pdx+Qdy)=∬D​(Qx​−Py​)dxdy.​
(33)
The boundary is positively oriented: the region lies on the left while it is traversed, so outer components run counterclockwise and hole boundaries clockwise.
For the proposed potential of a conservative vector field, the fundamental theorem of calculus and differentiation under the integral sign give
Fy​(x,y)=Q(x,y),Fx​(x,y)=P(x,0)+∫0y​Qx​(x,s)ds.
(34)
Since Qx​=Py​, the last integral equals P(x,y)−P(x,0), including negative y with the usual signed-integral convention. Hence the proposed potential is global and
∇F=(P,Q)=V.​
(35)
The fact that the vector field is defined on all of R2 ensures both straight integration segments stay inside its domain; a hole in the domain could obstruct a global potential.

Integrating the oscillatory term together with the constant components gives a potential of a conservative vector field
F(x,y)=x+2y+2πsin(2π(x+y))​.​
(36)

∫C​V⋅dx=m+2n.​

B(x)=∫R3​4π∣x−y∣3J(y)×(x−y)​d3y.​
(55)
Finally, change variables z=x−y in the integral for A. This keeps the singular kernel independent of the differentiation variable:
A(x)=∫R3​4π∣z∣J(x−z)​d3z,∇⋅A(x)=∫R3​4π∣z∣(∇⋅J)(x−z)​d3z=0.
(56)
For smooth localized J, differentiation under the integral sign is legitimate: 1/∣z∣ is locally integrable in three dimensions, and on a bounded neighbourhood of x the translated sources and their derivatives have common compact support. The integral indeed satisfies Coulomb gauge.
