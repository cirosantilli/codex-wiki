A counterclockwise planar rotation matrix has the form
Rθ​=(cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​),θ∈R.
(9)
To recover this form from preservation of the Euclidean norm, let u,v be the columns of R. Applying the norm condition to the two coordinate unit vectors gives ∣u∣=∣v∣=1. Applying it to their sum gives
2=∣u+v∣2=∣u∣2+2u⋅v+∣v∣2,
(10)
so u⋅v=0. The columns are an orthonormal basis, and RTR=I: R is an orthogonal matrix.
Write u=(cosθ,sinθ)T. There are two perpendicular unit vectors available for v, namely ±(−sinθ,cosθ)T. The first gives determinant 1, the second −1. The positive determinant hypothesis selects the first. A norm-preserving planar map with positive determinant is a rotation.

A=(ac​bd​).

(12)

A complex square matrix U is a unitary matrix when U†U=I, equivalently U−1=U†. Here † denotes the adjoint matrix, the conjugate transpose. Such a matrix preserves the standard complex inner product and its Euclidean norm.
A real symmetric matrix A is a Hermitian matrix, so A†=A. For arbitrary complex x,
∣(A+iI)x∣2​=x†(A−iI)(A+iI)x=x†(A2+I)x=∣Ax∣2+∣x∣2.​
(66)
The imaginary cross terms cancel because A is Hermitian. Reversing the signs gives the same identity:
∣(A−iI)x∣2=∣(A+iI)x∣2=∣Ax∣2+∣x∣2.​
(67)
In particular, (A+iI)x=0 implies ∣x∣2=0, so A+iI has zero kernel of a linear map. It is a square matrix, and the rank-nullity theorem therefore makes it invertible. The same argument proves invertibility of A−iI.
The proposed matrix
U=(A−iI)(A+iI)−1
(68)
is consequently well-defined. For any y, put x=(A+iI)−1y. The two norm identities show directly that
∣Uy∣=∣(A−iI)x∣=∣(A+iI)x∣=∣y∣.
(69)
To verify the unitary matrix identity explicitly, take adjoints:
U†=(A−iI)−1(A+iI).
(70)
The two shifts commute, because both are polynomials in A. Hence
U†U​=(A−iI)−1(A+iI)(A−iI)(A+iI)−1=(A−iI)−1(A−iI)(A+iI)(A+iI)−1=I.​
(71)
Thus
(A−iI)(A+iI)−1 is unitary.​
(72)
The imaginary shifts are invertible, and their equal norm changes cancel in the quotient. This is the Cayley transform of a Hermitian matrix in the (A−iI)(A+iI)−1 convention.

The Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem states that every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence. We first use it to show that a continuous function on [a,b] is bounded above. Otherwise, choose xn​∈[a,b] with f(xn​)>n. A convergent subsequence xnk​​→c exists, with c∈[a,b] because the interval is closed. Continuity would give f(xnk​​)→f(c), contradicting f(xnk​​)>nk​→∞.
Let L=sup{f(x):x∈[a,b]}, which is finite. By the definition of supremum, there are yn​∈[a,b] with L−1/n<f(yn​)≤L. Another application of the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem gives a subsequence ynk​​→c∈[a,b]. By continuity,
f(c)=limk→∞​f(ynk​​)=L.
(73)
Thus the supremum is actually attained:
∃c∈[a,b] ∀x∈[a,b]: f(x)≤f(c).​
(74)
This proves the maximum part of the extreme value theorem from sequential compactness rather than assuming it.
Now suppose f′′<0 on R. Applying the mean value theorem to f′ shows that f′ is strictly decreasing. At a local maximum of a differentiable function, the left difference quotients are nonnegative and the right difference quotients are nonpositive; existence of the derivative forces f′=0. A strictly decreasing function has at most one zero. Therefore
f′′<0 ⟹ at most one local maximum.​
(75)
If that zero exists, f′ is positive to its left and negative to its right, so the local maximum is also a global maximum.
For the uniform bound by K<0, define
h(x)=f′(x)−Kx.
(76)
Its derivative is h′(x)=f′′(x)−K<0, so h is strictly decreasing by the mean value theorem. Comparing with h(0)=f′(0) gives
{f′(x)<f′(0)+Kx,f′(x)>f′(0)+Kx,​x>0,x<0.​
(77)
Because K<0, this makes f′ negative at sufficiently large positive x and positive at sufficiently large negative x. The function f′ is continuous, since it is differentiable; the intermediate value theorem supplies a zero c between such points. Its strict decrease makes this zero unique. Again using the mean value theorem for f, the derivative signs imply that f increases up to c and decreases after c:
f′′(x)<K<0 for all x ⟹ f has a unique global maximum.​
(78)
This illustrates uniform negative curvature and global maximization.
Merely requiring f′′<0 does not force existence. Take
f(x)=−e−x.
(79)
Then f′(x)=e−x>0 and f′′(x)=−e−x<0, but f is strictly increasing, approaches 0 as x→+∞, and never equals 0. The answer to the final question is no, even for a function bounded above:
Rsup​(−e−x)=0and the supremum is not attained.​
(80)
The stronger uniform curvature assumption prevents this behaviour. Here f′′(x)→0 at the end where the supremum is approached, so no uniform negative bound of that kind is available.

The Rolle theorem states that if a real function is continuous on [u,v], differentiable on (u,v), and has equal endpoint values, then its derivative vanishes somewhere in (u,v). Order the k distinct real roots as r1​<⋯<rk​. The theorem gives a root of f′ in each interval (rj​,rj+1​). These intervals are disjoint, so the obtained roots are distinct:
f′ has at least k−1 distinct real roots.​
(81)
We prove the requested bound for positive roots of a polynomial by mathematical induction on the number of nonzero terms. In fact, the proof also works with roots counted according to their multiplicity. Write V(p) for the number of sign changes and N+​(p) for the positive-root count.
A one-term polynomial a1​xd1​ has no positive roots and no sign changes. For the induction step, factor out the smallest power of x:
q(x)=x−d1​p(x)=a1​+∑j=2n​aj​xej​,ej​=dj​−d1​>0.
(82)
For x>0 this factor is nonzero, so N+​(q)=N+​(p), including multiplicities, and V(q)=V(p). Differentiation removes exactly the constant term:
q′(x)=∑j=2n​ej​aj​xej​−1.
(83)
The exponents remain strictly increasing, and the positive multipliers ej​ do not change coefficient signs. The induction hypothesis therefore gives
N+​(q′)≤V(q′),V(q′)=V(a2​,…,an​).
(84)
First suppose a1​a2​<0. Removing a1​ removes exactly one sign change, so V(q)=V(q′)+1. If q has positive roots r1​<⋯<rk​ of multiplicities m1​,…,mk​, its derivative has multiplicity mj​−1 at rj​ and, by the Rolle theorem, an additional root between each pair. Thus the Rolle root count with multiplicities gives
N+​(q′)≥∑j=1k​(mj​−1)+(k−1)=N+​(q)−1
(85)
when there is a positive root. Consequently
N+​(q)≤N+​(q′)+1≤V(q′)+1=V(q).
(86)
If there is no positive root, the desired bound is automatic.
Now suppose a1​a2​>0, so V(q)=V(q′). Again the zero-root case is immediate. Otherwise, multiplying q by −1 if necessary lets us assume a1​>0 and a2​>0 without changing roots or sign changes. For sufficiently small x>0,
q(x)>0,q′(x)>0,
(87)
since q(0)=a1​ and the lowest-power term of q′ has positive coefficient. Choose such a point u before the first positive root r1​. The mean value theorem on [u,r1​] gives a point v∈(u,r1​) with
q′(v)=r1​−uq(r1​)−q(u)​<0.
(88)
But q′(u)>0. The intermediate value theorem applied to the polynomial q′ therefore gives an extra root in (u,v), before r1​. It is distinct from the roots at rj​ and those between consecutive roots. Hence
N+​(q′)≥N+​(q),N+​(q)≤N+​(q′)≤V(q′)=V(q).
(89)
Both sign cases complete the induction:
N+​(p)≤V(p).​
(90)
An initial sign change allows one extra positive root; without that sign change, the derivative must already turn before the first positive root. This is the upper-bound part of the Descartes' rule of signs. The multiplicity claim used above follows by differentiating q(x)=(x−r)mh(x) with h(r)=0: the derivative has a factor (x−r)m−1 and its remaining factor is nonzero at r.

The stated bound is Lipschitz continuity with constant 1. For every x and every ε>0, taking δ=ε ensures ∣f(x)−f(y)∣<ε whenever ∣x−y∣<δ. The same δ works at every point, so f is both continuous and uniformly continuous.
For an integer N≥1, partition [0,1] into N equal intervals and define a step function
gN​(x)=f(j/N)for Nj​≤x<Nj+1​,j=0,…,N−1.

∣f(x)−gN​(x)∣≤N1​for all x∈[0,1].

A sufficiently fine partition gives a piecewise constant approximation uniformly over the whole interval.
