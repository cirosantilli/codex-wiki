First, the absolute values in the printed limit are an error: the correct long-run variance of a stationary process is the signed sum of autocovariances. Directly,
Var(XT​)=T21​∑s,t=1T​γX​(t−s),
(23)
so
TVar(XT​)=∑h∈Z​(1−T∣h∣​)+​γX​(h).
(24)
Each coefficient tends to 1 and has absolute value at most 1. Absolute summability and the dominated convergence theorem therefore give
TVar(XT​)⟶h∈Z∑​γX​(h)=2πfX​(0).​
(25)
The sum of absolute values is an upper bound, not the general limit. For a concrete counterexample to the printed assertion, take Xt​=ηt​−ηt−1​ with unit-variance iid noise. Then γ(0)=2, γ(±1)=−1, and other covariances vanish. The absolute sum is 4, but XT​=(ηT​−η0​)/T, so TVar(XT​)=2/T→0.
For correlated data the central limit theorem can still hold under suitable strong mixing of a stationary process and moment conditions, but the limiting variance is the long-run variance of a stationary process, rather than the one-observation variance. When it is positive,
T​(XT​−μ)⟹N(0,∑h​γX​(h)).
(26)
Positive serial dependence usually increases the standard error, while negative dependence can reduce it. The approximate effective sample size of a stationary sample is TγX​(0)/∑h​γX​(h) when that denominator is positive. A long-memory time series can require a different normalization or a different limit law. Absolute covariance summability alone does not prove a CLT: if Xt​=Vηt​ with a common independent random scale V taking values 1 and 2 with equal probabilities and iid ηt​ with the standard normal distribution, the off-diagonal covariances are zero, yet T​XT​ has the nonnormal scale-mixture law VN(0,1). The example is not an ergodic stationary process. If the long-run variance of a stationary process is zero, the usual nondegenerate square-root-T CLT is unavailable.
For the causal AR(1), ∣ϕ∣<1 and
μ=1−ϕm​,Xt​∣Xt−1​=x∼N(m+ϕx,σε2​).​
(27)
The conditional distribution follows because the current innovation is independent of the past.
For a fixed known ϕ, condition on the observed X1​ and use the n=T−1 transitions t=2,…,T. Their conditional maximum likelihood criterion is, up to constants,
−2σε2​1​∑t=2T​(Xt​−ϕXt−1​−(1−ϕ)μ)2.
(28)
Differentiating in μ yields
μ​=(T−1)(1−ϕ)∑t=2T​(Xt​−ϕXt−1​)​.​
(29)
Since Xt​−ϕXt−1​=(1−ϕ)μ+εt​,
μ​−μ=(T−1)(1−ϕ)∑t=2T​εt​​.
(30)
Thus it is an unbiased estimator, even conditionally on X1​, and
Var(μ​)=(T−1)(1−ϕ)2σε2​​.​
(31)
It has statistical consistency with mean-square convergence, and the iid-noise strong law of large numbers also gives almost-sure statistical consistency. Its conditional distribution is exactly normal with the displayed mean and variance. If X0​ is also observed and all T transitions are used, replace T−1 by T.
The fixed-ϕ qualification is necessary for the exact finite-sample claims. If ϕ is jointly estimated, conditional likelihood function is linear regression with an intercept: writing Xˉ−​=n−1∑t=2T​Xt−1​ and Xˉ+​=n−1∑t=2T​Xt​, the unconstrained estimators are
ϕ​=∑t=2T​(Xt−1​−Xˉ−​)2∑t=2T​(Xt−1​−Xˉ−​)(Xt​−Xˉ+​)​,m=Xˉ+​−ϕ​Xˉ−​,μ​=1−ϕ​m​.
(32)
This ratio is not generally an unbiased estimator and does not have the preceding finite-sample variance. Under the usual stationary regression conditions it has statistical consistency; its asymptotic variance is σε2​/(1−ϕ)2. Indeed, it differs from Xˉ+​ by ϕ​(XT​−X1​)/(n(1−ϕ​)), an asymptotically negligible endpoint term. Profiling an unknown innovation variance does not change the fixed-ϕ estimate of μ.
