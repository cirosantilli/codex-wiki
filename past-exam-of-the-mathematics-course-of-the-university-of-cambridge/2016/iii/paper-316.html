For a spherical grain of density ρ, the radiation-pressure coefficient is
β=8πcGM⋆​ρD3L⋆​⟨Qpr​⟩​.
(1)
The r−2 dependence of radiation pressure cancels that of stellar gravity. Here radiation-pressure efficiency includes absorption and the momentum-transfer part of scattering, averaged over the stellar spectrum. In the geometrical optics regime, D exceeds the important stellar wavelengths, Qpr​ is of order unity, and β∝D−1: cross-sectional area grows as D2, whereas mass grows as D3. Around the stellar wavelength, Mie scattering can produce a broad maximum and material-dependent structure. In the Rayleigh scattering regime, absorption gives Qabs​∝D, while scattering gives Qsca​∝D4. Thus small absorbing grains approach an approximately constant β, whereas nearly transparent grains have β∝D3. A universal fall to zero at small D is therefore inappropriate without specifying the optical properties. The sketch shows both possible small-grain limits; its vertical normalization is illustrative.
Assume release with negligible velocity relative to the planet, negligible planetary gravity after release, and a constant radiation-pressure coefficient. Put μ=GM⋆​ and μd​=(1−β)μ. The inherited speed and specific angular momentum are vp​=μ/ap​​ and h=ap​vp​. The new specific orbital energy is
εd​=2vp2​​−ap​μd​​=ap​μ​(β−21​).
(2)
For 0≤β<1/2, the dust orbit released from a circular parent ring is an ellipse. Using εd​=−μd​/(2ad​) and h2=μd​ad​(1−ed2​) gives
ap​ad​​=1−2β1−β​,ed​=1−ββ​​.
(3)
For β>0, release is at periapsis, since ad​(1−ed​)=ap​. At the radiation-pressure blowout threshold, β=1/2, the orbit is parabolic. For 1/2<β<1 it is hyperbolic: the signed semimajor axis is negative and ed​>1. For β≥1 the central force ceases to be attractive, so the elliptical interpretation and the positive-eccentricity formula cannot be extended unchanged.
With θd​ measured from release, it is the true anomaly. The polar equation of a Kepler orbit and conservation of specific angular momentum give
r=1+ed​cosθd​h2/μd​​=1−β+βcosθd​ap​​,θ˙d​=r2h​=np​(1−β+βcosθd​)2​.
(4)
Define the signed dust-tail angular lag by Δθ=θd​−θp​, with θp​=np​t and both angles unwrapped. For fixed θd​, expansion to first order in β gives
np​t=∫0θd​​(1−β+βcosψ)2dψ​=θd​+2β(θd​−sinθd​)+O(β2θd​),
(5)
so Δθ=2β(sinθd​−θd​)+O(β2θd​). The dust trails the planet, hence the signed lag is negative. Its slope is −4βsin2(θd​/2); it decreases monotonically, has horizontal tangents at successive release-direction passages, and has a sinusoidal ripple about −2βθd​. At the end of orbit j, θd​=2πj and Δθ≃−4πβj. At five orbits this is −0.2π for β=0.01 and −0.8π for β=0.04. The upper-β sketch remains a first-order approximation; its accumulated error is O(β2θd​).
For the subsequent positive-distance statements, set s=−Δθ=θp​−θd​≥0. The mean motion of the grain is
np​nd​​=(ad​/ap​)31−β​​=1−β(1−2β)3/2​=1−2β+O(β2).
(6)
A full relative wrap, s=2π, therefore takes twrap​≃π/(βnp​) after averaging over the orbital ripple. During this time Poynting–Robertson drag changes the semimajor axis by
ad​(0)∣ad​(twrap​)−ad​(0)∣​≃cap2​2βμ​βnp​π​=c2πvp​​​.
(7)
The signed change is negative. This estimate treats ad​≃ap​ as fixed in the rate and computes the drag accumulated over the radiation-pressure wrap time. Since vp​/c≪1, drag causes little migration over that time. For an arbitrarily tiny β, however, this small migration can itself alter the relative phase appreciably: neglecting that feedback on the wrap time additionally requires vp​/c≪β.
Rapid dust sublimation is a plausible sink near a hot planet: a grain can evaporate long before it completes a relative wrap. Destructive collisions can also remove visible grains. Poynting–Robertson drag alone, with the estimate above, does not explain immediate removal from a short tail. If the destruction time is tloss​, a short tail requires roughly 2βnp​tloss​≪2π.
For a steady dust-tail continuity equation, assume a constant injection rate N˙, grains with the same fixed β, negligible release-speed dispersion, and negligible destruction within the segment being calculated. A finite destruction lifetime can terminate that segment, or multiply its density by a survival probability. To first order,
s˙=np​−θ˙d​=4βnp​sin2(θd​/2)+O(β2),n(s)=s˙N˙​.
(8)
To obtain the explicit trigonometric profile, make the additional secular phase approximation for a dust tail, s≃2βθd​, discarding the bounded −2βsinθd​ term in the angle-to-age map while retaining the periodic instantaneous drift speed. Then
n(s)≃4βnp​N˙​sin2(s/(4β))1​​.
(9)
Since the square is even, the same written profile applies to the signed Δθ if number intervals are interpreted with positive orientation. The result is an approximate phase-substitution profile, not a uniformly valid consequence of β≪1. The consistent first-order description is instead parametric:
s=2β(θd​−sinθd​),n(s)=4βnp​sin2(θd​/2)N˙​​.
(10)
In particular, immediately after release, s∼βθd3​/3 and n∝s−2/3, whereas the phase-substitution expression would give s−2. Nor does s≪2π by itself justify neglecting the orbital ripple. The formal enhancements where s˙=0 are dust-tail caustics; a spread of radiation-pressure coefficients, finite release velocities, and destruction smooth them. With constant lifetime τ, the parametric profile acquires exp[−t(θd​)/τ]. These qualifications state precisely the extra assumptions behind the explicit profile.

The normalization of the circular restricted three-body problem implies μ1​+μ2​=1, by Kepler's third law. The two bodies have barycentric positions (−μ2​,0) and (μ1​,0). Consequently
r1​=(x+μ2​)2+y2​,r2​=(x−μ1​)2+y2​​.
(11)
Differentiate the effective potential directly:
Ux​=x−r13​μ1​(x+μ2​)​−r23​μ2​(x−μ1​)​,Uy​=y−r13​μ1​y​−r23​μ2​y​.
(12)
Since x=μ1​(x+μ2​)+μ2​(x−μ1​) and y=(μ1​+μ2​)y, these become
Ux​=μ1​(1−r1−3​)(x+μ2​)+μ2​(1−r2−3​)(x−μ1​),Uy​=μ1​(1−r1−3​)y+μ2​(1−r2−3​)y​.
(13)
On y=0, centrifugal acceleration is the straight line acf​=x. Each gravitational term points towards its source, diverges at that source, and tends to zero far away. Let f(x)=Ux​(x,0); away from the sources,
f′(x)=1+∣x+μ2​∣32μ1​​+∣x−μ1​∣32μ2​​>0.
(14)
Thus the net acceleration is strictly increasing on each of the three intervals separated by the bodies. On the left interval f runs from −∞ to +∞; on the middle interval it runs from −∞ just right of M1​ to +∞ just left of M2​; on the right interval it runs from −∞ to +∞. The intermediate value theorem and strict monotonicity give exactly three collinear Lagrange points, L3​,L1​,L2​ in increasing x order. The sketch plots gravity and centrifugal acceleration separately; their intersections with agrav​=−x locate the equilibria.
Write xL2​=μ1​+δ, so r2,L2​=δ and r1,L2​=1+δ. The exact right-hand equilibrium equation is
0=μ1​+δ−(1+δ)2μ1​​−δ2μ2​​=(1+2μ1​)δ−δ2μ2​​+O(δ2).
(15)
As μ2​→0, μ1​→1, so 3δ3≃μ2​. Therefore the distance is the leading Hill radius in units of the binary separation:
r2,L2​≃α=(3M1​M2​​)1/3​.
(16)
Replacing M1​+M2​ by M1​ changes only higher-order terms. In dimensional units the right-hand side is multiplied by the binary separation.
Use the displacement vector q=(X,Y,X˙,Y˙)T, where X=x−xL2​ and Y=y. The absolute coordinate x cannot be the first entry of a homogeneous linear system about L2​; it must be translated. At L2​, reflection symmetry gives Uxy​=0, while the Hessian matrix has
Uxx​=1+2B,Uyy​=1−B,B=(1+δ)3μ1​​+δ3μ2​​​.
(17)
To first order in ∣X∣/α and ∣Y∣/α, the Coriolis acceleration then gives
q˙​=Aq,A=​001+2B0​0001−B​100−2​0120​​.
(18)
This is the linearization at L2. The expression for B is exact at the true equilibrium; in the small-secondary limit, μ2​/δ3≃3 and μ1​/(1+δ)3≃1, so B≃4.
For an eigenmode proportional to eλt, eliminate the velocity coordinates. The characteristic polynomial is
[λ2−(1+2B)][λ2−(1−B)]+4λ2=λ4+(2−B)λ2+(1+2B)(1−B)=0.
(19)
Thus
λ2=2B−2±B(9B−8)​​.
(20)
For B≃4, the four eigenvalues are
λ=±1+27​​,λ=±i27​−1​​.
(21)
There is an exponentially growing eigenmode, so L2​ is an unstable saddle-centre equilibrium. A generic displacement has an unstable component whose distance grows as e1+27​​t. The L2 escape e-folding time is
τgrow​≃nbin​1+27​​1​≃nbin​0.399​≃0.0634Pbin​​.
(22)
This is a local exponential timescale, not the time to reach a prescribed distance from an unspecified initial offset. Starting with unstable amplitude q0​, reaching q1​ within the linear neighborhood takes approximately τgrow​log(q1​/q0​). Initial data exactly on the centre-stable subspace need not grow forward at this linear order; the existence of the growing eigenmode already proves instability.

For brevity write b1​=b3/2(1)​(α), b2​=b3/2(2)​(α), and K=GMp​α/(4ap​). The quadratic disturbing function is R=K[21​b1​e2−b2​ep​ecos(ϖp​−ϖ)]. Differentiation in Lagrange planetary equations gives
e˙=na2K​b2​ep​sin(ϖp​−ϖ),ϖ˙=na2K​[b1​−b2​eep​​cos(ϖp​−ϖ)]​.
(23)
The separate longitude of pericentre equation is singular at e=0, because a circular orbit has no distinguished periapsis. The complex eccentricity, z=eeiϖ, is regular there. Using zp​=ep​eiϖp​ and z˙=eiϖ(e˙+ieϖ˙) gives
z˙=ina2K​(b1​z−b2​zp​).
(24)
Since na2=GM⋆​a​ and np​=GM⋆​/ap3​​,
na2K​=A(α)=41​np​M⋆​Mp​​α1/2,z˙=iA(α)(b1​z−b2​zp​)​.
(25)
The approximations are planar, small-orbital eccentricity, first order in the planet-to-star mass ratio, and secular averaging away from important mean-motion resonances. The semimajor axis values remain constant at this order.
For a planet with fixed orbit, set g=Ab1​ and zf​=(b2​/b1​)zp​. Then z˙=ig(z−zf​), so z−zf​=(z(0)−zf​)eigt. With z(0)=0,
z(t)=b1​b2​​zp​(1−eigt)​.
(26)
On the Argand diagram, the complex eccentricity traces a circle centred on the forced eccentricity zf​, of radius ∣zf​∣. For an interior test particle, 0<α<1 and g>0, so the motion is counterclockwise, starting at the origin. The rotating radius z−zf​ is the free eccentricity. The orbital eccentricity ranges from zero to 2∣zf​∣, attaining its maximum at half a secular period. If zp​ is placed on the positive real axis, the initial tangent points downward, which is consistent with counterclockwise motion from the leftmost point of this circle.
Now let the planetary mass grow as Mp​(t)=Mp​t/tp​ for 0≤t≤tp​, while the planet's orbital elements and the test particle's semimajor axis remain fixed. Both the precession and forcing coefficients acquire the same factor t/tp​. Their ratio, and hence zf​, remain constant. Thus
z˙=igtp​t​(z−zf​),Φ(t)=∫0t​gtp​t′​dt′=2tp​gt2​,
(27)
and
z(t)=zf​[1−exp(2tp​igt2​)],0≤t≤tp​​.
(28)
This is the secular phase integral for proportional mass growth. The Argand diagram circle and the free eccentricity amplitude are unchanged; only the rate of traversal changes. The angular speed about zf​ starts at zero and rises linearly to g. The early displacement is quadratic in time rather than linear. Since tp​=2π/g, Φ(tp​)=π, so
z(tp​)=2zf​=2zp​b1​b2​​​.
(29)
A planet already at its final mass would instead return the particle to z=0 at tp​. After growth stops, the circle continues at rate g, with z(t)=zf​[1+eig(t−tp​)] for t≥tp​. Slow mass growth does not damp the free eccentricity here: the fixed centre of the circle and the conservative equation preserve its amplitude.
For several planets with fixed masses, Laplace-Lagrange secular theory gives z˙=iAz for the planets and
z˙=ig0​z+i∑j​νj​zj​(t)
(30)
for a massless particle, with real secular coupling coefficients νj​. Resolve planetary complex eccentricities into secular eigenmodes, zj​(t)=∑k​Ejk​eigk​t. Away from secular resonances, the test-particle solution has the form
z(t)=Ceig0​t+∑k​gk​−g0​∑j​νj​Ejk​​eigk​t.
(31)
Its Argand diagram trajectory is a superposition of rotations: generally a quasiperiodic curve with beats, rather than a circle with one fixed centre. Even if the planetary orbits are artificially held fixed, their combined forcing shifts the centre to the sum of the individual forced eccentricities. At gk​=g0​, the resonant contribution is proportional to teig0​t in linear theory; its growing amplitude eventually invalidates the small-orbital eccentricity approximation.
If one planetary mass changes, the Laplace-Lagrange secular matrix, its eigenvalues, its eigenvectors, and the particle's forced response generally change with time. Planetary modes need not remain independent. Frequencies drift and a secular resonance can sweep across the particle, changing or exciting its free eccentricity. Very slow evolution away from resonances permits an approximate adiabatic mode description; rapid evolution or a resonance passage can mix modes. The simple substitution of a phase integral works only in the special proportional-growth case above, where the entire particle equation is multiplied by one scalar and its forced centre stays fixed. It does not apply to a general many-planet system.

Write the differential power-law size distribution as n(D)=KD−α and take all bodies to have the same bulk density. In a fixed belt volume, K is proportional to Mtot​ when the size cutoffs and the shape of the distribution are fixed, because
Mtot​=6πρK​∫Dmin​Dmax​​D3−αdD.
(32)
For α=4 the integral is (Dmax4−α​−Dmin4−α​)/(4−α); at α=4 it is a logarithm. Thus no assumption that the mass is always dominated by the largest bodies is needed for this normalization step.
A size-independent catastrophic disruption threshold and collision speed imply a fixed minimum projectile-to-target diameter ratio ξ, since 21​mp​v2/mt​∼QD∗​ gives ξ∼(2QD∗​/v2)1/3. With negligible gravitational focusing, a target of size D has a catastrophic collision rate proportional to
tc​(D)1​∝∫ξDDmax​​Kd−α(D+d)2dd=KD3−α∫ξDmax​/D​u−α(1+u)2du.
(33)
For α>3 and targets well inside the cutoffs, the dimensionless integral converges at the upper end and is size independent. Consequently
tc​=AMtot−1​Dα−3​.
(34)
Here A contains the belt geometry, speed, density, cutoffs and disruption parameters. Close to a cutoff, or if ξD<Dmin​, this scaling needs modification. A finite belt without replenishment can only be in quasi-steady state over times short compared with depletion of its largest reservoir.
The mass in a logarithmic size interval is mlnD​∝D4n(D)∝D4−α. For a scale-independent fragment redistribution function, steady collisional gain and loss transfer a constant mass flux down the cascade: each logarithmic interval processes the same mass per unit time, away from boundaries. Equivalently, a normalized translation-invariant redistribution kernel acting on logarithmic bins admits constant processed mass as its steady solution. Thus
tc​mlnD​​∝D4−α−(α−3)=D7−2α
(35)
must be size independent. Therefore α=7/2, the Dohnanyi collisional cascade, and tc​=(A/Mtot​)D1/2.
The Yarkovsky effect is recoil from anisotropic thermal radiation. Finite thermal inertia shifts the hottest region away from the instantaneous substellar point. The resulting recoil has a tangential component and produces a secular change of semimajor axis; the diurnal component can drift in either direction depending on spin, and the seasonal component generally drifts inward. Migration into a dynamical escape region can remove a body from the belt. For large bodies, the intercepted luminosity scales as D2 and inertia as D3, giving a recoil acceleration roughly proportional to D−1, hence a removal time growing as D. Very small bodies become nearly isothermal when heat penetrates the whole body; the anisotropy decreases and the removal time again grows. The stated phenomenological law
ty​=BD+DC​,B,C>0
(36)
encodes these limits. Its minimum occurs at D0​=C/B​, with ty,min​=2BC​. This minimum in absolute removal time differs from the minimum relative to the collision time.
For the collision-only cascade, form the ratio
tc​ty​​=AMtot​​(BD1/2+CD−3/2).
(37)
Differentiation gives the minimum at D∗​=3C/B​=3​D0​. At this diameter,
minD​tc​ty​​=33/4A4Mtot​​B3/4C1/4=My​Mtot​​,My​=433/4​AB−3/4C−1/4​.
(38)
Thus ty​<tc​ for some sizes if and only if Mtot​<My​ for an unrestricted size interval. In a finite belt, that interval must also overlap [Dmin​,Dmax​]; the displayed inequality alone is necessary but not sufficient if all favorable sizes lie outside the belt. On logarithmic axes ty​ has slopes −1 and +1, while tc​ is a line of slope 1/2. Increasing belt mass shifts the collision line downward.
For Mtot​≫My​, collisions dominate at all sizes. The belt retains nb​(D)∝D−7/2. Let Tdyn​ be the size-independent residence time of objects after Yarkovsky removal. Their steady number distribution is

Hence nout​∝D−5/2 for D≪D0​ and nout​∝D−9/2 for D≫D0​, with a smooth change around D0​. These are differential number slopes, not cumulative-number or mass-per-logarithmic-bin slopes.
For Mtot​≪My​, first quantify the nominal crossings obtained by extending the collision-only cascade:
BD2+C=Mtot​A​D3/2.
(40)
The two roots surround D∗​, and in the well-separated limit they are
D−,0​≃(ACMtot​​)2/3,D+​≃(BMtot​A​)2​,D−,0​≪D0​≪D+​.
(41)
They mark where the undepleted distribution first becomes susceptible to Yarkovsky removal. The upper crossing remains the leading estimate for the onset of depletion, since larger bodies still constitute a collision-dominated reservoir. The lower crossing will be shifted by the depletion itself, as described below.
In the removal-dominated band, the fragment number injection spectrum is SN​(D)=JD−7/2 under the stated fragment redistribution function assumption. Balancing production against escape gives
nb​(D)≃SN​(D)ty​(D)=J(BD−5/2+CD−9/2),nout​(D)≃Tdyn​JD−7/2.

Thus the belt slopes in the depleted band are −5/2 above D0​ and −9/2 below D0​; the removed population has slope −7/2 across that band. The steep small-size side recovers towards a secondary collision-dominated cascade as escape becomes inefficient. It does not keep the −9/2 slope to zero size.
For a useful quantified sketch, approximate the collision integral by its scale-free local dependence and match adjacent asymptotic branches. Let nb​=KD−7/2 above D+​, and write its collision rate as (Mtot​/A)D−1/2. Matching at D+​ gives J≃K/(BD+​). On the small-size side of the removal band,
nb​≃KD+​D02​​D−9/2.
(43)
Its collision rate scales as (Mtot​/A)(D02​/D+​)D−3/2, rather than the undepleted D−1/2 law. Equating this rate to D/C gives the corrected lower transition
D−​∼D08/5​D+−3/5​=(BC​)4/5(ABMtot​​)6/5​.
(44)
The equality is an order-of-magnitude matching law: the collision integral has different dimensionless coefficients for slopes 7/2 and 9/2, and projectiles of size ξD spread transitions over a finite range. The powers follow from the stated scale-free model; an exact numerical lower crossing requires the collision kernel and fragment normalization. In particular, simply retaining D−,0​ as the true lower crossing silently treats the depleted projectile abundance as unchanged.
Below D−​, the secondary Dohnanyi collisional cascade has nb​≃K−​D−7/2 with matching normalization
K−​∼KD+​D−​D02​​=K(D+​D0​​)2/5≪K.
(45)
The four belt branches, in descending diameter, are therefore
nb​(D)∼⎩⎨⎧​KD−7/2,(K/D+​)D−5/2,(KD02​/D+​)D−9/2,K−​D−7/2,​D≫D+​,D0​≪D≪D+​,D−​≪D≪D0​,D≪D−​.​​
(46)
The corresponding Yarkovsky removal population has differential slopes −9/2, −7/2, −7/2, −5/2, respectively: division by ty​ cancels the bend at D0​ within the removal-dominated band. Its genuine changes of slope occur at D+​ and D−​. Multiplication by Tdyn​ changes only its normalization. If one is using the preliminary fixed-projectile approximation instead, the same four asymptotic slopes apply, with D−,0​ as its nominal lower breakpoint; that approximation omits the feedback just quantified.
All sketches presume the relevant breakpoints lie between the size cutoffs, enough time to establish the asserted steady portions, and a large-body reservoir feeding fragments. Otherwise only the branches within the actual size interval appear. Constant collision speed, size-independent strength, a self-similar redistribution law, and a size-independent external residence time are essential: changing them changes the exponents. Boundary waves and nonlocal collisions round the sharp branch joins in the sketch.
