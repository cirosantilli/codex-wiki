Use the simple-root form of Hensel lemma: if F∈Zp​[T] and t0​∈Zp​ satisfy F(t0​)≡0(modp) and F′(t0​)≡0(modp), there is a unique t∈Zp​ with F(t)=0 and t≡t0​(modp). More generally the same assertion holds over a complete discrete valuation ring, with its maximal ideal in place of (p).
Every nonzero 3-adic number has a unique expression
x=3kϵu,k∈Z,ϵ∈{1,−1},u∈U1​:=1+3Z3​.
(1)
Indeed its normalized P-adic valuation fixes k, and its unit residue modulo three fixes ϵ. Cubing is an automorphism on {1,−1}, so it remains to compute the cube map on the principal units.
For t∈Z3​,
(1+3t)3=1+9(t+3t2+3t3).
(2)
Thus U13​⊆U2​:=1+9Z3​. Conversely, given 1+9a∈U2​, apply Hensel lemma to
F(T)=T+3T2+3T3−a.
(3)
Modulo three it is T−a, and F′(T)=1+6T+9T2 is always a unit. The residue root amod3 therefore lifts, giving t with (1+3t)3=1+9a. Hence
U13​=U2​.​
(4)
This is the cube map on 3-adic principal units. Introducing T is essential: applying simple-root lifting directly to X3−u would fail because its derivative is divisible by three at every unit.
The map 1+3t↦tmod3 induces an isomorphism U1​/U2​≅(Z/3Z,+), since multiplication adds the first principal-unit digits modulo three. The valuation factor contributes Z/3Z independently, so the cube-class group of the 3-adic numbers is
Q3×​/(Q3×​)3≅Z/3Z×Z/3Z.​
(5)
Explicit generators are the classes of 3 and 4=1+3. Representatives are 3a4b with 0≤a,b<3. The class of 3 is detected by valuation modulo three, and that of 4 by its nonzero first principal-unit digit, so the two generators are independent.

Normalize the discrete valuation on L by vL​(L×)=Z, set vL​(0)=+∞, and write OL​ for its valuation ring. For a finite Galois extension of local fields, define G−1​=G=Gal(L/K) and, for integers i≥0,
Gi​={σ∈G:vL​(σ(a)−a)≥i+1 for every a∈OL​}.​
(6)
Equivalently, Gi​ is the kernel of the action on OL​/mLi+1​. These are the lower ramification numbering of the ramification groups. In particular, G0​ is the inertia group, the kernel of the action on the residue field, and G1​ is the wild inertia group. They form a decreasing sequence of normal subgroups, eventually trivial.
For a real index t≥−1, extend by Gt​=G⌈t⌉​. Define the Herbrand function by
φL/K​(t)=∫0t​[G0​:Gs​]ds​(t≥0),φL/K​(t)=t(−1≤t≤0).
(7)
It is continuous, strictly increasing and piecewise linear; denote its inverse by ψL/K​. The upper ramification numbering is
Gu=GψL/K​(u)​(u≥−1).​
(8)
The definition includes the placement of the groups at break endpoints: the group at a break is the group before the drop. Lower numbering is compatible with subgroups; upper numbering is the one compatible with quotients. In a totally ramified extension, the uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups permits testing the defining valuation inequality on one uniformizer instead of on every integral element.

Let a=33​ and let ζ be a primitive cube root of unity. The splitting field is L=Q3​(a,ζ). The polynomial X3−3 is an Eisenstein polynomial, so [Q3​(a):Q3​]=3. Meanwhile (1+2ζ)2=−3 shows that Q3​(ζ) is quadratic: −3 is not a square because its valuation is odd. A quadratic field cannot lie in the degree-three field. Consequently [L:Q3​]=6, and the action on the three roots identifies its Galois group with the symmetric group S3​.
A useful uniformizer is
π=aζ−1​.
(9)
Since (ζ−1)3=−3ζ(ζ−1), we obtain
π3=−ζ(ζ−1)=1+2ζ,π6=−3.
(10)
Thus π is a root of the Eisenstein polynomial X6+3. Its degree is six, so L=Q3​(π), the extension is totally ramified, and π is a uniformizer. With vL​(π)=1,
vL​(3)=6,vL​(a)=2,vL​(ζ−1)=3.
(11)
This is the Eisenstein sextic presentation of the splitting field of X3 minus 3 over Q3.
Let τ(a)=ζa, τ(ζ)=ζ, and let s(a)=a, s(ζ)=ζ−1. Then τ has order three, s has order two, and sτs=τ−1. Their actions on the uniformizer are
τ(π)=ζ−1π,s(π)=−ζ−1π.
(12)
The two nonidentity elements of ⟨τ⟩ therefore satisfy
vL​(τ(π)−π)=vL​(τ2(π)−π)=1+vL​(ζ−1)=4.
(13)
Each transposition sends π to −ζjπ for some j, so its displacement has valuation one: 1+ζj is a unit, reducing to 2 modulo the maximal ideal. The uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups now gives
G−1​=G0​=S3​,G1​=G2​=G3​=⟨τ⟩≅C3​,Gi​=1 (i≥4).​
(14)
For real indices, this is S3​ on [−1,0], C3​ on (0,3], and 1 for t>3. In particular the lower breaks are 0 and 3.
On (0,3] the index [G0​:Gt​] is two; beyond three it is six. Thus the Herbrand function is
φ(t)=⎩⎨⎧​t,t/2,3/2+(t−3)/6,​−1≤t≤0,0≤t≤3,t≥3.​
(15)
The positive lower break 3 becomes the upper break 3/2. Therefore
Gu=⎩⎨⎧​S3​,C3​,1,​−1≤u≤0,0<u≤3/2,u>3/2.​​
(16)
The fractional upper break is allowed because the full extension is nonabelian. As a check, the different exponent from ramification groups is 5+3⋅2=11. The derivative of the minimal polynomial X6+3 gives the same result: vL​(6π5)=6+5=11. This is the p=3 case of ramification groups of the splitting field of Xp minus p over the p-adic numbers.

Normalize vK​ by vK​(πK​)=1 and put vK​(0)=+∞. Write f(X)=∑j=0d​aj​Xj, with ad​=1 and a0​=0. In the usual coefficient-exponent convention, the Newton polygon NK​(f) is the lower boundary of the convex hull of the upward vertical rays starting at
(j,vK​(aj​))(0≤j≤d, aj​=0).​
(17)
It runs from (0,vK​(a0​)) to (d,0), with nondecreasing slopes from left to right. Zero coefficients contribute no finite point. Equivalently it is the largest convex piecewise-linear function lying below all these coefficient points.
The Newton polygon root valuation theorem says that a segment of slope s and horizontal length r accounts for exactly r roots of valuation −s, counted with multiplicity, using the extended valuation on an algebraic closure. The nonzero constant coefficient excludes a zero root.
For a short justification, use the weighted Gauss valuation wt​(P)=minj​(v(cj​)+jt) for P=∑cj​Xj. It is multiplicative: after scaling X by an element of valuation t in a valued extension, the initial nonzero residue polynomials multiply without vanishing. Rational t suffice. Factoring a monic polynomial into linear factors gives wt​(f)=∑r​min(t,v(r)). This supporting-line function changes derivative at precisely the root valuations; its derivative drops count their multiplicities. Supporting lines to the lower polygon have slope −t, proving the sign and horizontal-length assertion.
Slope convention for part (b). Reflecting the horizontal axis, by plotting (d−j,vK​(aj​)), produces the reflected Newton polygon convention; its slopes are the root valuations themselves. Part (b)'s wording with m instead of −m is correct under this positive-root-valuation convention. The proof below gives both forms explicitly, so the result does not depend on silently changing the sign.

The denominator is πnX; dividing by X removes the zero at X=0. A uniformizer of a degree-n totally ramified extension generates the field: its valuation over K is 1/n, so K(π)/K already has ramification index at least n. Hence K(π)=L, its minimal polynomial has degree n, and its conjugates are the distinct σ(π) for σ∈G.
Factor the minimal polynomial over L. The reduced ramification polynomial is
g(X)=πnXf(π(1+X))​=∏σ=1​(X−πσ(π)−π​).
(18)
Every displayed root rσ​=(σ(π)−π)/π is nonzero and integral, since two uniformizers have a difference of valuation at least one. Thus g is monic of degree n−1, has nonzero constant coefficient, and belongs to OL​[X]. For n=1 it is the constant polynomial 1, with no slopes and no ramification jumps.
Here is also a justification of the uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups in this setting. The powers 1,π,…,πn−1 are a K-basis. In an expansion x=∑aj​πj, the nonzero terms have valuations nvK​(aj​)+j, distinct modulo n; therefore there is no cancellation at the least valuation. If x is integral, all those values are nonnegative, which forces aj​∈OK​. Thus OL​=OK​[π]. For j≥1, σ(π)j−πj is divisible by σ(π)−π, with the remaining factor integral. Consequently every σ(x)−x has valuation at least vL​(σ(π)−π), and equality is attained at x=π.
For a nonidentity automorphism, write i(σ)=vL​(σ(π)−π). By the defining inequality for the lower ramification numbering,
σ∈Gm​∖Gm+1​⟺i(σ)=m+1⟺vL​(rσ​)=m.
(19)
The Newton polygon root valuation theorem therefore proves the ramification breaks from a reduced ramification polygon relation:
−m is a slope of the coefficient-exponent NL​(g)⟺Gm​=Gm+1​.​
(20)
The segment's horizontal length is exactly ∣Gm​∣−∣Gm+1​∣. Equivalently, in the reflected Newton polygon convention required for a positive-slope formulation,
m is a slope of the reflected NL​(g)⟺Gm​=Gm+1​.​
(21)
All occurring root valuations are nonnegative integers. For negative indices, extend Gm​=G; total ramification means that there are no negative-index jumps, so the reflected formulation remains true for all integers.
The sign distinction is substantive. Using the uniformizer from question 2, whose minimal polynomial is X6+3, gives
g(X)=X(1+X)6−1​=6+15X+20X2+15X3+6X4+X5.
(22)
Its coefficient valuations are (6,6,0,6,6,0), because vL​(3)=6. The usual lower hull has vertices (0,6),(2,0),(5,0) and slopes −3,0, of lengths 2,3. These correspond exactly to the drops ∣G3​∣−∣G4​∣=2 and ∣G0​∣−∣G1​∣=3. With the reflected axis the slopes are 0,3, as in the printed positive-m wording. If the coefficient-exponent definition is used throughout, the printed assertion needs the minus sign shown above.

Let K=Q(−31​). Its ring of integers of a quadratic field is Z[(1+−31​)/2], and its fundamental field discriminant is −31.
First compute the class number. The ideal-form correspondence for imaginary quadratic fields identifies the ideal classes with proper equivalence classes of primitive positive definite binary quadratic forms of discriminant −31. Each has a unique reduced positive definite binary quadratic form representative under the convention ∣b∣≤a≤c, with b≥0 when ∣b∣=a or a=c. From 31=4ac−b2≥3a2 we get 1≤a≤31/3​<4. Checking these three possible leading coefficients gives exactly
[1,1,8],[2,1,4],[2,−1,4].
(23)
For a=3, neither odd residue choice b=±1,±3 makes (b2+31)/12 integral. The listed forms are primitive and satisfy the reduction convention, so
hK​=3,Cl(K)≅C3​.​
(24)
This computes the ideal class group of Q of square root minus thirty-one.
Now take a root θ of F(T)=T3+T−1, and let H be its splitting field over Q. There is no rational root: the only candidates are ±1, and neither vanishes. Thus the cubic is irreducible. Its polynomial discriminant is
disc(F)=−4−27=−31.
(25)
Since this is nonsquare, the Galois group of an irreducible cubic is S3​. Its quadratic subfield is K: the product of pairwise root differences squares to −31 and changes sign under odd permutations. Thus H/K is cyclic of degree three, and H=K(θ). Indeed the cubic field and K have coprime degrees, so their compositum already has the full splitting-field degree six. The squarefree polynomial discriminant also shows, by the discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice, that Z[θ] is the full integer ring of the cubic field.
It remains to prove that H/K is unramified everywhere. At any rational prime ℓ=31, the reduction of F is separable. Over a finite residue extension in which it splits, all residue roots are simple and lift by Hensel lemma to the corresponding unramified extension of Qℓ​. Therefore the local splitting field is unramified over Qℓ​, and its extension over a completion of K is unramified as well.
At the only possible ramified prime, direct reduction gives
F(T)≡(T−17)2(T−28)(mod31),F′(28)≡28≡0(mod31).
(26)
The simple root lifts to r∈Q31​. Write F(T)=(T−r)Q(T), with Q monic quadratic over Q31​. The discriminant product formula gives
disc(F)=disc(Q)Q(r)2.
(27)
Here Q(r)=F′(r) is a unit, so the quadratic discriminant has square class −31. Hence the entire local splitting field is
Q31​(disc(Q)​)=Q31​(−31​)=K31​.
(28)
It is quadratic over Q31​, but its extension over the completion of K is trivial. Thus the prime over 31 splits completely in H/K; in particular it is not ramified there. This is the local splitting-field argument behind an unramified cyclic cubic extension from a prime-discriminant cubic. There are no real places of the imaginary quadratic field K, so no infinite-place ramification remains to check.
The Hilbert class field theorem identifies the maximal everywhere unramified abelian extension with an extension of degree hK​. Our cyclic unramified extension already has that degree, so it must be the whole Hilbert class field. Therefore
HK​=Q(−31​,θ),θ3+θ−1=0.​
(29)
Equivalently, it is the splitting field of T3+T−1, of degree six over Q and degree three over K. Replacing θ by θ−1 gives the alternative defining cubic T3−T2−1. This is the Hilbert class field of Q of square root minus thirty-one.
