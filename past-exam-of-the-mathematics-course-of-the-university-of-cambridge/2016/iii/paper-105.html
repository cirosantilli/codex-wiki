Non-characteristic. The first-order principal symbol is νt​−iνx​, which equals one on (0,1).
Set z=x+it. The equation is equivalent to ∂zˉ​ϕ=(ϕx​+iϕt​)/2=0. Because ϕ is continuously differentiable, the Cauchy-Riemann equations imply that it is a holomorphic function of z. Its convergent complex Taylor series restricts to a real power series along t=0, so g is a real analytic function.
For smooth-data instability of the Cauchy-Riemann Cauchy problem, choose
ϕn​(x,t)=e−n​cos(n(x+it)).​
(1)
These are entire functions and satisfy the equation. Their initial derivatives obey supx​∣∂xj​gn​∣≤nje−n​, so every finite sum tends to zero. But
supx∈R​∣ϕn​(x,t)∣=e−n​cosh(n∣t∣)⟶∞(t=0).
(2)
Thus analytic solutions can exist while continuous dependence on initial data fails for this smooth-data topology.
The Cauchy estimate follows directly from the differentiated Cauchy integral formula:
f(j)(z0​)=2πij!​∫∣z−z0​∣=r​(z−z0​)j+1f(z)​dz,∣f(j)(z0​)∣≤rjj!M​.​

The circle has length 2πr, giving the bound for every integer j≥0.
Put w=ϕ−ψ and integrate on the straight segment z=τt/∣t∣. For fixed ∣x∣<sR, a disc centred at x with radius approaching R(σ(τ)−s) stays inside ∣ξ∣≤σ(τ)R. Applying the first-derivative Cauchy estimate there gives
∣wx​(x,z)∣≤R(σ(τ)−s)sup∣ξ∣≤σ(τ)R,∣ζ∣≤τ​∣w(ξ,ζ)∣​.
(4)
Integrating its modulus and taking the supremum over ∣x∣<sR is exactly the asserted integral estimate. The source cancels from Tϕ−Tψ.
To obtain a contraction mapping, use the weighted holomorphic norm on a shrinking time domain. Its finite-norm space consists of holomorphic functions on Cα​ with zero initial value; the apparent quotient at t=0 is interpreted by a limit. Completeness follows because convergence in this norm implies locally uniform convergence of holomorphic functions, including near t=0, and the limiting pointwise bounds give convergence in the norm.
Here is the explicit contraction estimate on a shrinking holomorphic domain. Write N=∥w∥α​, r=∣t∣, A=α(1−s), and choose
σ(τ)=s+2αA−τ​(0≤τ≤r<A).
(5)
Then σ−s=(A−τ)/(2α) and α(1−σ)−τ=(A−τ)/2. The whole auxiliary polydisc lies inside Cα​, and its supremum of ∣w∣ is at most 2Nτ/(A−τ). Consequently
rA−r​∣Tϕ−Tψ∣​≤R4αN​rA−r​∫0r​(A−τ)2τ​dτ=R4αN​[1+rA−r​log(1−r/A)]≤R4αN​.​
(6)
Thus ∥Tϕ−Tψ∥α​≤(4α/R)∥ϕ−ψ∥α​. Choose 0<α<min(η,R/4). The source is bounded on the closed polydisc, say by Mf​, and ∥T0∥α​≤αMf​. Therefore T maps the Banach space into itself and is a strict contraction mapping. The Banach fixed-point theorem gives a holomorphic fixed point with ϕ(x,0)=0. Differentiating the integral identity yields ϕt​−iϕx​=f, establishing this case of the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem.
Finally, near each real initial point, a real analytic function g has a holomorphic extension G. Apply the same construction to ϕ=G(x)+v with v(x,0)=0 and source iG′(x), restricting the discs if necessary. This gives a local real analytic function of (x,t) with the prescribed initial value. Equivalently the solution is ϕ(x,t)=G(x+it)​ wherever the extension is defined.

For an integer s≥0, the Sobolev space is
Ws,p(Rd)={u∈Lp:Dβu∈Lp for every multi-index ∣β∣≤s},
(7)
where Dβ is a weak derivative. For p<∞ one may use the Sobolev norm (∑∣β∣≤s​∥Dβu∥pp​)1/p; for p=∞ use the maximum of the finitely many essential-supremum norms.
For 1≤p<d, the Sobolev inequality is ∥u∥p∗​≤Cd,p​∥∇u∥p​, with Sobolev conjugate exponent p∗=dp/(d−p). For d<p<∞, Morrey's inequality supplies a continuous representative satisfying
∣u(x)−u(y)∣≤Cd,p​∣x−y∣1−d/p∥∇u∥p​,∥u∥∞​≤Cd,p​(∥u∥p​+∥∇u∥p​).
(8)
At p=∞ the representative is Lipschitz continuous. At the critical exponent p=d>1, first-order Sobolev regularity gives every finite Lq embedding for q≥d, with an inhomogeneous norm, but generally no L∞ embedding. The one-dimensional endpoint W1,1(R)↪L∞(R) is an exception.
For the proof of Morrey's inequality, start with a smooth u and write uB(x,r)​ for its average on a ball. Averaging the fundamental theorem of calculus along a line segment and changing radial variables gives
∣u(x)−uB(x,r)​∣≤Cd​∫B(x,r)​∣x−z∣d−1∣∇u(z)∣​dz≤Cd,p​r1−d/p∥∇u∥p​.
(9)
The last step is the Holder inequality; integrability of the kernel to power p′ is exactly (d−1)p′<d. For r=∣x−y∣, translate the averaging ball along the segment from x to y. The fundamental theorem of calculus along a line segment and the Holder inequality give
∣uB(x,r)​−uB(y,r)​∣≤r∣Br​∣−1/p∥∇u∥p​.
(10)
Combining the two point-to-average bounds and this average-to-average bound proves the required Hölder estimate. The point-to-average bound with r=1, together with ∣uB(x,1)​∣≤∣B1​∣−1/p∥u∥p​, gives the supremum estimate. Density of smooth functions in a Sobolev space then gives a uniformly convergent sequence of smooth representatives, preserving both bounds. For p=∞, mollification gives the Lipschitz version.
For the decay conclusion assume d<p<∞. The representative is uniformly continuous. If ∣u(xj​)∣≥2θ>0 along points escaping to infinity, the Hölder bound gives a radius ρ>0, independent of j, on which ∣u∣≥θ. A subsequence has disjoint radius-ρ balls, each contributing at least θp∣Bρ​∣ to ∥u∥pp​, a contradiction. This is uniformly continuous integrable functions vanish at infinity.
The finite-p restriction is necessary. If the printed range includes p=∞, its decay assertion is false: u≡1 belongs to W1,∞ but does not tend to zero.

The Arzelà-Ascoli theorem says that a uniformly bounded, equicontinuous family of continuous functions on a compact metric space is relatively compact in the uniform norm. Thus every sequence in that family has a uniformly convergent subsequence, and its limit is continuous.

On a bounded Lipschitz domain Ω⊂Rd, the Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorem states that W1,p(Ω)↪Lq(Ω) is compact for 1≤p<∞ and
q1​>p1​−d1​,
(11)
with 1/q=0 for q=∞. Thus q<p∗ when p<d, every finite q is allowed when p=d, and the embedding into continuous functions with their uniform norm is compact when p>d.
For the main proof, a Sobolev extension operator puts a bounded sequence into a common compactly supported region of Rd. The Sobolev fundamental theorem of calculus on lines gives the uniform translation estimate ∥u(⋅+h)−u∥p​≤∣h∣∥∇u∥p​. Convolution with a mollifier therefore approximates the sequence uniformly in Lp space. At any fixed smoothing scale, its derivatives and supremum are uniformly bounded, so the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem gives a convergent subsequence. A diagonal choice and the uniform approximation give strong Lp space convergence. The finite measure of Ω handles q<p, while Lp interpolation inequality with the bounded Sobolev embeddings upgrades this to the larger subcritical finite Lp spaces; for p>d, Morrey's inequality gives uniform equicontinuity directly and the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem gives uniform convergence. Boundedness of the domain is essential.

Work with real-valued functions. The screened sine-Gordon energy is well defined: 0≤1−cosu≤u2/2, and fu is integrable by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. It is coercive, because
E[u]≥21​∥u∥H12​−∥f∥2​∥u∥2​≥41​∥u∥H12​−∥f∥22​.
(15)
A minimizing sequence is bounded in the Hilbert space H1. Weak sequential compactness of bounded sequences in a reflexive Banach space supplies a weakly convergent subsequence. The squared H1 space norm is weakly lower semicontinuous, the source pairing is weakly continuous, and the nonlinear term is covered by local Sobolev compactness gives lower semicontinuity of a nonnegative integral. The direct method in the calculus of variations therefore gives a minimizer ϕ.
Taking its first variation in any v∈H1 gives
∫R3​∇ϕ⋅∇v+ϕv+sinϕvdx=∫R3​fvdx.
(16)
Thus the Euler-Lagrange equation is
−Δϕ+ϕ+sinϕ=f,​
(17)
as a weak solution, equivalently in distributions when tested against smooth compactly supported functions. The derivative of the nonlinear term is justified by ∣sinϕ∣≤∣ϕ∣ and the second-order remainder bound ∣cos∣≤1.
Now G=f−sinϕ∈L2, so the supplied elliptic regularity estimate puts ϕ in H2. Applying the Sobolev inequality to ϕ and each first weak derivative gives ϕ∈W1,6(R3). Morrey's inequality and uniformly continuous integrable functions vanish at infinity prove that its continuous representative tends to zero.
The final printed supremum estimate is false in general. The maximum bound for a monotone reaction term involves h(s)=s+sins, which is odd and strictly increasing: h′=1+coss≥0, and its zeros are isolated. If M=∥f∥∞​<∞, a positive maximum m of ϕ satisfies h(m)≤f≤M; apply the same argument to −ϕ. The valid general estimate is
∥ϕ∥∞​≤h−1(M)≤M+1.​

A contraction semigroup on a Banach space X consists of bounded linear operators S(t) with S(0)=I, S(t+s)=S(t)S(s), ∥S(t)∥≤1, and S(t)x→x as t↓0 for each x∈X. Its infinitesimal generator of a semigroup is
D(A)={x:limh↓0​hS(h)x−x​ exists in X},Ax=limh↓0​hS(h)x−x​.
(20)
For density, Yosida averaging of a semigroup gives xh​=h−1∫0h​S(s)xds. Taking the difference quotient of this Bochner integral shows that xh​∈D(A) and Axh​=(S(h)x−x)/h. Strong continuity gives xh​→x, so D(A) is dense.
For closedness, the semigroup restricted to its generator domain satisfies
S(t)x−x=∫0t​S(s)Axds(x∈D(A)).
(21)
If xn​→x and Axn​→y, pass to the limit in this identity. After dividing by t, strong continuity gives (S(t)x−x)/t→y. Hence x∈D(A) and Ax=y. This proves generator of a strongly continuous semigroup is closed and densely defined.
The contraction form of the Hille-Yosida theorem states that a linear operator A generates a contraction semigroup if and only if it is densely defined and closed, every real λ>0 lies in its resolvent set, and
∥(λ−A)−n∥≤λ−n(n≥1).
(22)
Here the n=1 bound implies the others by taking powers of the same bounded resolvent.
The H1 space is H1(R)={u∈L2:u′∈L2} with weak derivative u′ and squared norm ∥u∥22​+∥u′∥22​. The displayed weighted space is the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator form domain, with inner product
⟨u,v⟩X​=∫R​uvˉ+u′v′+x2uvˉdx.
(23)
If un​ is Cauchy in X, it converges in H1 to u, and xun​ converges in L2 space to some w. On each bounded interval, multiplication by x is bounded, so w=xu there. Thus xu∈L2 and convergence holds in X, proving completeness and the Hilbert space property. Equivalently this is closedness of the multiplication operator by x.
For the Sobolev characterization by bounded difference quotients, if f∈H1 then
Dh​f=∫01​f′(⋅+θh)dθ,∥Dh​f∥2​≤∥f′∥2​,Dh​f⟶f′ in L2.
(24)
The last assertion uses continuity of translation of a function in L2 space. Conversely, if f∈L2 and the quotients are uniformly bounded for 0<∣h∣<1, take a weakly convergent subsequence as h→0. For every test function v,
∫Dh​fvdx=∫f(x)hv(x−h)−v(x)​dx⟶−∫fv′dx.
(25)
The weak limit is therefore the weak derivative f′∈L2. This proves the characterization.
