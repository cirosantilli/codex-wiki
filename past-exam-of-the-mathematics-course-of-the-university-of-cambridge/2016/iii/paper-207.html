Use the usual clinical trial sampling assumption that all patients' responses are independent, with fixed positive arm sizes and positive known variances. Marginal normal distributions alone would not specify the variance of a treatment contrast without this assumption. Put Yi​=ni−1​∑j​Yij​, vi​=σi2​/ni​, and si​=vi​+v0​​. The sample mean contrast estimates δi​ and has standard error si​. Thus the one-sided Wald statistics are
Wi​=σi2​/ni​+σ02​/n0​​Yi​−Y0​​,i=1,2.​
(1)
Large positive values of these Wald test statistics provide evidence against the respective null hypotheses.

Couple the Wald test statistics as Wi​=Zi​+δi​/si​, where the joint centered bivariate normal distribution of (Z1​,Z2​) is fixed. Increasing either δi​ enlarges, pointwise in this coupling, the event {W1​>c or W2​>c}. Its probability is consequently nondecreasing in each treatment effect.
If both null hypotheses hold, then δ1​,δ2​≤0, so this probability is maximal at the joint boundary (0,0). To check the familywise error rate across all configurations, suppose only hypothesis i is true. A Type I error then occurs only if Wi​>c, with probability at most 1−Φ(c), where Φ is the standard normal distribution function. This is no larger than the any-rejection probability at (0,0), since that joint event contains {Zi​>c}. If neither null hypothesis is true, the familywise error rate is zero. Hence the least-favourable null configuration for Type I error is
δ1​=δ2​=0,FWERmax​=1−Φ2​(c,c;ρ),​
(6)
where Φ2​ is the centered bivariate normal distribution function with correlation coefficient ρ.

Choose a group sequential design. Randomize patients among the three arms, schedule interim analyses after prespecified amounts of information, and calculate the two Wald test statistics at each look. Prespecify efficacy boundaries and a futility rule; allow early stopping of an ineffective experimental arm or of the trial when sufficiently convincing efficacy evidence is obtained. Calibrate the boundaries for both repeated looks and the two shared-control comparisons so that the familywise error rate remains controlled. An alpha-spending function is one way to allocate the error probability across looks.
Two potential benefits are lower expected recruitment and shorter duration when a treatment is clearly effective or futile, and earlier identification of benefit or lack of benefit, reducing continued exposure to an ineffective experimental treatment. These benefits concern expected performance; the maximum sample size may still be required.
Two drawbacks are the extra operational work needed for rapid, reliable outcomes and confidential interim analyses, and more complicated inference: repeated testing requires adjusted boundaries, and ordinary fixed-sample effect estimates and confidence intervals can be misleading after data-dependent stopping. Continuing two comparisons against a shared control also requires care if an arm is stopped.

Age changes during follow-up, so the generator matrix Q(s) changes with time. The transition matrix must solve ∂t2​​P(t1​,t2​)=P(t1​,t2​)Q(t2​), with P(t1​,t1​)=I. It is generally not the matrix exponential of one constant matrix; even exp(∫Q(s)ds) is not generally valid because the matrices at different times need not commute.
For example, write the age-dependent onset rate as λ(s) and keep progression rate ν constant. Then
P12​(t1​,t2​)=∫t1​t2​​λ(u)exp(−∫t1​u​λ(v)dv)e−ν(t2​−u)du.
(14)
Even when the integrated onset rate is elementary, this remaining integral need not have an elementary closed form. Special cases can have closed forms; time dependence does not by itself prove universal impossibility.
For a tractable likelihood function, freeze age at its left-endpoint value, or a midpoint value, on each observation interval [tj​,tj+1​). The genetic covariate is already constant. This piecewise-constant covariate approximation gives a constant matrix Qj​ on each interval and a product of closed-form interval probabilities:
L=j∏​[eQj​(tj+1​−tj​)]xj​xj+1​​.​
(15)
Here xj​ is the observed state at tj​, and the likelihood function is conditional on the initial state. The approximation improves when intervals are short relative to the variation in the age effect.

Let ϵ be the false positive rate for a genuinely cancer-free person, encompassing the harmless-tumour misclassification, and assume screening errors are conditionally independent given the latent states. A pre-clinical case tests positive with probability one; a clinical case is separately recognized. Thus a negative screen identifies latent state 1, whereas a positive pre-clinical screen can arise from state 1 or 2. This is a Hidden Markov model with a screening misclassification model.
Conditional on the negative baseline screen, the state at year 0 is 1. The negative screen at year 2 requires staying in state 1 for two years and then testing negative. From that state, the positive screen at year 4 can arise either with probability ϵ while still disease-free or through pre-clinical disease. Consequently the conditional likelihood is
Lcond​=e−2λ(1−ϵ)[ϵe−2λ+ν−λλ​(e−2λ−e−2ν)].​
(16)
If the study instead specifies an initially disease-free patient and includes the baseline screening result in the likelihood function, its probability contributes a further factor 1−ϵ, giving
Lall​=(1−ϵ)2e−2λ[ϵe−2λ+ν−λλ​(e−2λ−e−2ν)].​
(17)
The formulas use the positive emission for a pre-clinical screen, with no clinical diagnosis in this record. A persistent harmless-tumour subtype producing correlated screening errors would need an additional latent-state or emission specification; its likelihood is not determined by the single parameter ϵ.

In the SIR model, S,I,R count susceptible, infectious, and recovered immune individuals. The closed population has S+I+R=N+1. Under homogeneous mixing, use the paper's mass-action infection convention: each susceptible has infection hazard function βI, so the population infection rate is βSI. Each infectious individual recovers with hazard function γ, giving population recovery rate γI and mean infectious duration 1/γ.
The arrows are one-way: recovered individuals are immune, and there are no demographic entries or exits. The transition intensities attached to the arrows are population flow rates, rather than individual hazard functions.

b OurBigBook.com
The deterministic SIR model follows the ordinary differential equations
S˙=−βSI,I˙=βSI−γI,R˙=γI,(S(0),I(0),R(0))=(N,1,0).​
(18)
Use β≥0 and γ>0. The state space is S,I,R≥0, S+I+R=N+1; summing the three equations proves conservation of this population. Infection is supported only where S>0 and I>0, and recovery only where I>0. The rates vanish at the corresponding boundaries, preventing negative compartment sizes.
This convention has an unnormalized population infection rate βSI, as required by the printed threshold. A convention with infection rate βSI/(N+1) would use a differently scaled parameter.
