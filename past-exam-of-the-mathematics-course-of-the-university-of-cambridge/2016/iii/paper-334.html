Let μ be the dynamic viscosity, ui​=x˙i​, and Fi​ the signed force exerted by sphere i on the fluid. For negligible sphere inertia, this also equals the externally or internally applied force on that sphere; its hydrodynamic force is −Fi​. The Stokes drag law and the axial velocity of a Stokeslet give the leading hydrodynamic mobility matrix
(u1​u2​​)=(m1​h(ℓ)​h(ℓ)m2​​)(F1​F2​​),mi​=6πμai​1​,h(ℓ)=4πμℓ1​.
(1)
The factor 1/(4πμℓ) follows from (I+r^r^)F/(8πμℓ) when F is parallel to the line of centres. The longitudinal two-sphere mobility retains the first interaction in ai​/ℓ; finite-size and repeated-reflection corrections are higher order.
For the linked force-free pair, F2​=−F1​. Put X=(x1​+x2​)/2 and Sm​=m1​+m2​. Then
ℓ˙=(2h−Sm​)F1​,X˙=2m1​−m2​​F1​,
(2)
so
X˙=−2[Sm​−2h(ℓ)]m1​−m2​​ℓ˙​.
(3)
The coefficient depends only on the current separation. For any period T, ℓ(T)=ℓ(0), and therefore
X(T)−X(0)=∮−2[Sm​−2h(ℓ)]m1​−m2​​dℓ=0​.
(4)
This is a closed integral of a single-valued function of one real shape coordinate. Since x1​=X−ℓ/2 and x2​=X+ℓ/2, both spheres also have zero net displacement. Equal radii make X˙=0 instantaneously; unequal radii generally permit oscillatory translation, but still no mean motion. This explicit force-free two-sphere stroke is the scallop theorem: a single-parameter reciprocal deformation in a Newtonian fluid at zero inertia cannot produce net free swimming. The timing of extension and contraction cannot change that conclusion, because Stokes flow has no inertial memory.
For the externally prescribed pair, put q=ωt, ζi​=1/mi​=6πμai​, and
d(t)=ℓ(t)−ℓ0​=δ[cos(q+ϕ)−cosq],u1​=−δωsinq,u2​=−δωsin(q+ϕ).
(5)
Invert the hydrodynamic mobility matrix:
F1​=m1​m2​−h2m2​u1​−hu2​​=ζ1​u1​−ζ1​ζ2​hu2​+ζ12​ζ2​h2u1​+⋯.
(6)
In a large-ℓ0​ expansion with ai​,δ fixed,
h=4πμ1​(ℓ0​1​−ℓ02​d​)+O(ℓ0−3​).
(7)
The isolated-drag term and the constant-h interaction have zero mean. The order-ℓ0−2​ term from h2u1​ also has zero mean, since its coefficient is constant at that order. Meanwhile,
⟨du2​⟩t​=2δ2ω​sinϕ.
(8)
Consequently the externally driven two-sphere pump has the following phase-dependent mean force of an externally driven sphere pair:
⟨F1​⟩t​=8πμℓ02​ζ1​ζ2​δ2ω​sinϕ=2ℓ02​9πμa1​a2​δ2ω​sinϕ+O(ℓ0−3​)​.
(9)
Here ⟨⋅⟩t​ denotes averaging over one period. The analogous calculation gives ⟨du1​⟩t​=δ2ωsinϕ/2, so ⟨F2​⟩t​=⟨F1​⟩t​ at leading order, and
⟨F1​+F2​⟩t​=ℓ02​9πμa1​a2​δ2ω​sinϕ+O(ℓ0−3​)​.
(10)
Thus the mean fluid forcing is nonzero except at ϕ=0 or π modulo 2π. The sign changes when the phase lag is reversed. Those exceptional strokes are reciprocal: the position-space loop collapses to a line, and their mean force vanishes by reversibility, not just by this leading expansion.
There is no contradiction with the scallop theorem. The externally imposed motion is not force-free, and the actuators prescribe two phase-shifted motions; their combined motion is not reciprocal for a generic phase. The spheres return to their prescribed positions while transferring a mean force to the fluid. This is pumping by external forcing, rather than propulsion of the freely linked one-shape-coordinate system.
The asymptotic calculation requires δ/ℓ0​≪1 and persistent large separation. The minimum separation of phase-shifted sphere oscillations is
mint​ℓ(t)=ℓ0​−2δ∣sin(ϕ/2)∣.
(11)
The printed condition ℓ0​≥δ alone does not guarantee nonoverlap or the stipulated far-separated regime. The prescribed trajectories must additionally keep this minimum well above both radii. This is a compatibility qualification on the data, not a change of the phase-dependent force calculation.

Use u=∂y​ψ and v=−∂x​ψ in the frame translating with the sheet. At each instant, the incompressible Stokes equations are
−∇p+μ∇2u=0,∇⋅u=0.
(12)
Taking their curl gives the biharmonic stream function for planar Stokes flow:
∇4ψ=0​
(13)
in the fluid above the actual sheet. The no-slip boundary condition is imposed on material points, whose label is x and whose actual horizontal position is xs​. With q=x−t,
ψy​(xs​,ys​,t)=−3ϵacos3q,ψx​(xs​,ys​,t)=ϵbcosq.
(14)
The second sign follows from v=∂t​ys​=−ϵbcosq. Far above the sheet,
ψy​→U,ψx​→0,
(15)
with bounded velocity and no imposed mean pressure gradient. The positive far-field velocity arises because the sheet moves with laboratory velocity −Uex​. All fields are 2π-periodic in x; an additive function of time in ψ is a harmless gauge. These conditions formulate the Taylor swimming sheet problem with both types of travelling deformation.
Expand U=ϵU1​+ϵ2U2​+⋯ and ψ=ϵψ1​+ϵ2ψ2​+⋯. At first order the boundary can be evaluated at y=0:
∇4ψ1​=0,ψ1y​(x,0,t)=−3acos3q,ψ1x​(x,0,t)=bcosq.
(16)
The far-field conditions are ψ1y​→U1​ and ψ1x​→0. For a nonzero Fourier mode of wavenumber k, bounded velocity selects (E+Gy)e−kyeikq; the exponentially growing modes are excluded. Matching the modes k=1 and k=3 gives
ψ1​=b(1+y)e−ysinq−3aye−3ycos3q​.
(17)
An additive gauge has been set to zero. The first term is a transverse mode of a Taylor swimming sheet, and the second is a longitudinal mode of a Taylor swimming sheet. Their velocities decay at infinity. The mean first-order boundary velocity is zero, and the bounded zero Fourier mode is a constant plus a multiple of y, so U1​=0.
For the second-order Taylor-expanded no-slip boundary condition, the first-order displacement is
ξ=(asin3q,bsinq).
(18)
There is no explicit second-order material velocity in the prescribed motion. Hence, with all first-order derivatives evaluated at (x,0,t),
∇4ψ2​=0,u2​=−ξ⋅∇u1​,v2​=−ξ⋅∇v1​,
(19)
where uj​=ψjy​ and vj​=−ψjx​. Differentiation of the first-order solution gives
u1x​=9asin3q,u1y​=18acos3q−bsinq,v1x​=bsinq,v1y​=−9asin3q
(20)
on the flat reference boundary. Therefore
u2​(x,0,t)=−9a2sin23q−18absinqcos3q+b2sin2q,v2​(x,0,t)=8absinqsin3q​.
(21)
The boundary conditions for the streamfunction are ψ2y​=u2​ and ψ2x​=−v2​. Their far-field counterparts are ψ2y​→U2​ and ψ2x​→0.
Products of the two distinct first-order Fourier modes generate only the nonzero wavenumbers 2,4,6, as well as a mean. Thus the bounded general solution compatible with these data has the form
ψ2​=C0​(t)+U2​y+Re⎩⎨⎧​k∈{2,4,6}∑​(Ek​+Gk​y)e−kyeikq⎭⎬⎫​​.
(22)
The discarded zero-mode terms y2,y3 would give unbounded velocity, and the discarded nonzero modes grow exponentially. The zero-mean normal velocity in periodic half-space Stokes flow is consistent with a bounded periodic flow and no far-field vertical flux. For example, integrating ψ2x​=−v2​ along the boundary yields
ψ2​(x,0,t)=−2absin2q+absin4q+C0​(t).
(23)
This fixes the Ek​; the nonconstant part of u2​ fixes Gk​−kEk​. All coefficients are thereby determined, but none is needed to find the mean swimming speed.
Indeed, mean boundary velocity determines Taylor-sheet swimming speed. The mean Fourier mode of ψ2​ is C0​+U2​y, so its horizontal velocity is the same at the boundary and infinity. Fourier orthogonality of sheet swimming modes removes the mixed product, giving
U2​=⟨u2​(x,0,t)⟩x​=2b2−9a2​,U=2ϵ2​(b2−9a2)+O(ϵ3)​.
(24)
Here ⟨⋅⟩x​ is the average over one spatial period; a temporal average gives the same answer for these travelling waves. The laboratory swimming velocity is −Uex​. The transverse wave drives motion opposite to its direction of propagation, whereas the longitudinal wave drives it in the propagation direction. Their wavenumbers enter quadratically. Thus b/a=3 cancels the swimming speed at order ϵ2. This different-wavenumber cancellation in sheet swimming is not a claim that all higher-order swimming terms vanish.

In resistive-force theory (RFT), the hydrodynamic force per unit arclength exerted by the fluid on a slender filament is
f=−ξ∥​(u⋅t)t−ξ⊥​[u−(u⋅t)t]=−[ξ⊥​I+(ξ∥​−ξ⊥​)ttT]u​.
(25)
Here t is the unit tangent and u the local velocity relative to the background fluid. The parallel and perpendicular drag coefficients of a slender filament are positive and generally satisfy ξ⊥​>ξ∥​; their leading logarithmic ratio is about two. This local approximation assumes a small filament radius compared with length and curvature scales, negligible fluid inertia, and a Newtonian fluid. It represents drag by the local tangent direction and neglects nonlocal hydrodynamic interactions between separated filament segments. Boundaries, close approaches and end corrections may require slender-body theory or a more complete flow calculation. In this problem the background fluid is at rest, and the drag coefficients are taken uniform along the filament.
The rigid-body velocity in a deforming swimmer frame is the sum of translation, rotation and material deformation. With r=xex​+ϵgey​,
u=Uex​+Vey​+Ωez​×r+ϵgt​ey​,
(26)
so, in instantaneous swimmer-frame components,
u=(U−ϵΩg)ex​+(V+Ωx+ϵgt​)ey​​.
(27)
The reference-frame conditions g(0,t)=gx​(0,t)=0 attach the origin and orientation to the filament's base and base tangent. They prevent arbitrary shape translations or tilts from being absorbed into the definition of g.
The exact tangent and arclength element are
t=1+ϵ2gx2​​ex​+ϵgx​ey​​,ds=1+ϵ2gx2​​dx.
(28)
Since U,V,Ω=O(ϵ), the local velocity is O(ϵ). Changing the drag tensor by its O(ϵ) tangent correction therefore changes f only at O(ϵ2). The arclength correction is smaller still at this order. Also −ϵΩg=O(ϵ2). Thus t=ex​ is sufficient at order ϵ. This assumes the small-slope expansion is uniform, ϵ∣gx​∣≪1.
Put G0​(t)=⟨gt​⟩x​ and G1​(t)=⟨xgt​⟩x​, with ⟨h⟩x​=L−1∫0L​hdx. The first-order local force is
f=−ϵξ∥​U1​ex​−ϵξ⊥​(V1​+Ω1​x+gt​)ey​+O(ϵ2).
(29)
Its total hydrodynamic forces are
Fx​=−ϵξ∥​LU1​+O(ϵ2),Fy​=−ϵξ⊥​L(V1​+2L​Ω1​+G0​)+O(ϵ2)​.
(30)
Taking the moment about the swimmer-frame origin, Mz​=∫(xfy​−ϵgfx​)ds, the second term is beyond first order. Hence
Mz​=−ϵξ⊥​L(2L​V1​+3L2​Ω1​+G1​)+O(ϵ2)​.
(31)
For force-free and torque-free motion, the three leading coefficients vanish. The longitudinal force immediately gives U1​=0. The two transverse equations are
V1​+2L​Ω1​=−G0​,2L​V1​+3L2​Ω1​=−G1​.
(32)
Their determinant is L2/12>0. Solving yields the first-order free swimming of a planar filament:
V1​=−4⟨gt​⟩x​+L6​⟨xgt​⟩x​,Ω1​=L6​⟨gt​⟩x​−L212​⟨xgt​⟩x​​.
(33)
The resistive-force theory coefficient cancels because both equations use the same transverse drag. Another interpretation is the least-squares projection of filament deformation velocity: −V1​−Ω1​x is the best affine approximation to gt​ on [0,L]. Zero transverse force and torque mean that the residual is orthogonal to 1 and x.
If g is sufficiently differentiable and periodic with period T, each gt​(x,t) has zero temporal mean. More explicitly,
V1​=dtd​[−4⟨g⟩x​+L6​⟨xg⟩x​],Ω1​=dtd​[L6​⟨g⟩x​−L212​⟨xg⟩x​].
(34)
Both bracketed quantities return to their initial values over a period. Thus the first-order periodic transverse swimming velocity satisfies
⟨V1​⟩t​=⟨Ω1​⟩t​=0​.
(35)
These results concern first order and swimmer-frame components. Changes of orientation can affect laboratory displacements at second order; higher-order net swimming is not ruled out.

Use the same resistive-force theory convention as before: the force density is the fluid's force on the filament. Let φ be the usual cylindrical azimuth. Choose the orientation of a left-handed helix so that, as z increases, φ decreases. With s=sinθ, c=cosθ,
t=cez​−seφ​,u=Uez​+aΩeφ​,u⋅t=Uc−aΩs.
(36)
Reversing the orientation of the tangent leaves the local drag tensor unchanged. Define k=ξ⊥​−ξ∥​. From resistive-force theory, the relevant force-density components are
fz​=−(ξ∥​c2+ξ⊥​s2)U−kascΩ,
(37)
fφ​=−kscU−a(ξ⊥​c2+ξ∥​s2)Ω.
(38)
These scalar axial and azimuthal components are uniform along the helix. Integration over its contour length ℓ, and use of the axial torque density afφ​, give the axial resistance matrix of a slender helix:
A=ℓ(ξ∥​cos2θ+ξ⊥​sin2θ),B=C=ℓa(ξ⊥​−ξ∥​)sinθcosθ,​
(39)
D=ℓa2(ξ⊥​cos2θ+ξ∥​sin2θ)​.
(40)
Thus B=C, as also required by the Lorentz reciprocal theorem. The signs here belong to (F,L)T=−(AB​BD​)(U,Ω)T, with F,L forces and moments on the helix. The handedness reversal of helical hydrodynamic resistance reverses B,C but leaves A,D unchanged. If the positive rotation direction or handedness convention is reversed, the coupling sign reverses with it.
As a check on physical admissibility, the determinant of helical resistance in resistive-force theory is
AD−B2=ℓ2a2ξ∥​ξ⊥​>0​.
(41)
Together with A,D>0, this makes the hydrodynamic resistance matrix positive definite and the viscous power loss AU2+2BUΩ+DΩ2 positive. Isotropic local drag would have B=0, so rotating a helix would not propel it in this approximation.
For the pair, use a local additive resistive-force theory model: both helices have the same drag coefficients, and inter-helix hydrodynamic interactions and unresolved end effects are omitted. The right-handed helix has resistance entries (nA,−nB,−nB,nD). Define the signed motor motion by
Ω1​−Ω2​=ω​.
(42)
The printed relative rotation specifies a magnitude rather than which helix rotates relative to which. Choosing Ω2​−Ω1​=ω instead reverses all signed speeds below.
The common translation speed U and zero total force and torque obey
(1+n)AU+BΩ1​−nBΩ2​=0,
(43)
(1−n)BU+DΩ1​+nDΩ2​=0.
(44)
Internal motor forces and torques cancel in these totals. Introduce
D=AD(1+n)2−B2(1−n)2=(AD−B2)(1+n)2+4nB2>0.
(45)
Solving the three linear equations yields the opposite-handed counterrotating helical swimmer:
U=−D2nBD​ω,​
(46)
Ω1​=Dn[AD(1+n)+B2(1−n)]​ω,Ω2​=−DAD(1+n)−B2(1−n)​ω​.
(47)
For n>0, ξ⊥​>ξ∥​ and ω>0, B>0, so U<0, Ω1​>0 and Ω2​<0. The opposite-handed helices counterrotate but contribute thrust in the same axial direction. The formula satisfies the specified relative rotation without identifying either laboratory rotation rate with the motor rate.
At n=0, the second helix supplies no hydrodynamic resistance. The remaining helix must have F=L=0, and invertibility of its hydrodynamic resistance matrix forces U=Ω1​=0. Formally Ω2​=−ω is the rotation of a zero-resistance motor shaft or vanishing second rotor; there is no physical finite second helix to propel or to provide a reaction torque. This is the vanishing reaction rotor in a helical swimmer.
At n=1, equal-length opposite-handed helices have
Ω1​=ω/2,Ω2​=−ω/2,U=−2AB​ω​.
(48)
The translation-generated axial torques cancel between the two helices, and equal counterrotation balances the rotational torques. Their propulsive forces add, rather than cancel, because handedness and rotation both reverse. These are equal-length opposite-handed helices.
For n→∞, the large reaction helix limit is
U∼−AD−B22BD​nω​→0,Ω1​→ω,Ω2​∼−AD−B2AD+B2​nω​→0​.
(49)
The increasingly long second helix nearly anchors the whole assembly: its very small translation and rotation suffice to balance the finite force and torque generated by the first helix. The first helix rotates at almost the full motor rate, but moving the large resistive second helix makes the common translation tend to zero. These are idealized limits of the additive local model, rather than a resolution of short-helix end effects or infinitely extended interacting filaments.
