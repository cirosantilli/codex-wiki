Let e=(∇u+∇uT)/2 be the rate-of-strain tensor and σ=−pI+2μe the Newtonian fluid stress tensor. The viscous dissipation is D[u]=2μ∫V​e:edV. The Minimum-dissipation theorem for Stokes flow compares the Stokes flow with every sufficiently regular incompressible flow in the same domain having the same prescribed boundary velocity, with no body force or with a conservative body force absorbed into the fluid pressure. A trial field need not satisfy the Stokes flow equations. Write it as u+w, with ∇⋅w=0 and w=0 on the boundary. Integration by parts, the symmetry of the Newtonian fluid stress tensor, and ∇⋅σ=0 give
2μ∫V​e(u):e(w)dV=∫V​σ:∇wdV=∫∂V​w⋅σndS−∫V​w⋅∇⋅σdV=0.
(1)
Consequently the Stokes flow minimizes dissipation:
D[u+w]−D[u]=2μ∫V​e(w):e(w)dV≥0.​
(2)
Equality requires a rigid body motion of w, which the prescribed boundary eliminates. Extending the flow inside the inserted particle as its rigid body velocity produces an admissible comparison field with zero internal rate-of-strain tensor; hence the extra dissipation due to a rigid inclusion is nonnegative.
Here and below n on A points into the particle, so the traction σn represents force exerted by the particle on the fluid. Apply the Lorentz reciprocal theorem for Stokes flow in the fluid region to u and the restriction of u0​. The equal outer boundary velocities yield
∫∂V​V0​⋅(σ−σ0​)ndS=∫A​(u⋅σ0​n−u0​⋅σn)dS.
(3)
The boundary-work formula for viscous dissipation therefore gives D′=∫A​[(u−u0​)⋅σn+u⋅σ0​n]dS. The last integral is zero: σ0​ is symmetric and divergence-free throughout the particle's original volume, so its total force and torque there vanish, and u on A is a rigid body velocity. This also allows the sign of this zero-work term to be reversed, giving exactly
D′=∫A​(u⋅σ−u0​⋅σ−u⋅σ0​)⋅ndS.​
(4)
With the particle's centre as origin, define F=∫A​σndS, G=∫A​x×(σn)dS and the particle stresslet tensor S=−21​∫A​[x(σn)+(σn)x]dS. Contracting the linear background velocity with the traction gives
D′=F⋅(U−U0​)+G⋅(Ω−Ω0​)+S:E0​.​
(5)
The background rate-of-strain tensor E0​ is symmetric and trace-free; the trace of this unprojected particle stresslet tensor does not affect the contraction.
For the straight-rod resistance in a linear flow, write ΔU=U−U0​, ΔΩ=Ω−Ω0​, M=I−pp/2, and X=sp, −L≤s≤L. The slender-body force density becomes f=CM[ΔU+s(ΔΩ×p−E0​p)]. The integrals of s and s2 are 0 and 2L3/3, respectively, so
F=2CLMΔU,G=32CL3​[(I−pp)ΔΩ−p×E0​p].​
(6)
For a force-free, torque-free rod, ΔU=0 and the perpendicular component of ΔΩ is p×E0​p. Axial spin is not determined by this leading, zero-thickness slender-body theory; it has no effect on p. Put ap​=p⋅E0​p. The vector identity (p×E0​p)×p=E0​p−ap​p gives
f=−2C​sap​p,p˙​=Ω0​×p+E0​p−ap​p.​
(7)
The force-free straight-rod orientation equation combines the background rotation with the part of strain that turns the rod; removing ap​p preserves its unit length. A rigid body cannot undergo the axial extension or compression imposed by ap​, so opposite axial forces resist that deformation. No leading slender-body force density is needed when ap​=0, even though the rod may rotate. Its particle stresslet tensor and viscous dissipation are
S=3CL3​ap​pp,D′=3CL3​ap2​.​
(8)
In the simple shear flow, Ω0​=−γez​/2, (E0​)xy​=(E0​)yx​=γ/2 and ap​=γcosθsinθ. Thus the rod excess dissipation in shear is
D′(θ)=12CL3γ2​sin2(2θ),θ˙=−γsin2θ.​
(9)
The maxima at θ=π/4,3π/4 correspond to strongest axial extension and compression. The zeros at 0,π/2,π correspond to zero axial strain: a rod aligned with the velocity or its gradient needs no leading force at that instant. For γ>0, cotθ=γt with the continuous branch θ:π→0 and θ(0)=π/2. Reversing the shear flow reverses the traversal. Substitution gives
D′(t)=3CL3γ2​[1+(γt)2]2(γt)2​,∫−∞∞​D′(t)dt=6πCL3∣γ∣​.​
(10)
The t−2 tails make the total finite; the instantaneous zero at t=0 lies between two maxima.
For a dilute rod suspension, the added bulk stress is the number density times the orientation average of the particle stresslet tensor. In this ideal infinitely slender, non-interacting model, rods approach alignment with the shear flow and their excess viscous dissipation decays. A real finite-aspect-ratio rod continues to tumble; rotational diffusion and interactions also maintain a spread of orientations. One therefore expects increased effective viscosity, with alignment offering a mechanism for reduced excess viscosity at strong shear flow. This is a qualitative expectation, not a claim that a finite rod suspension has zero steady excess stress.
