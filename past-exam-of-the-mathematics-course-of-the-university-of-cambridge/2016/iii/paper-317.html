Write R=k/(μmH​) for the specific gas constant, with the printed mH​ denoting the atomic mass unit, and ar​=4σ/c for the radiation constant. In the low-mass envelope the enclosed mass is approximately M and the outward luminosity is approximately L. The stellar hydrostatic equation, stellar radiative diffusion, and ideal gas equation give
drdP​=−r2GMρ​,drdT​=−16πar​cr2T33κρL​,P=RρT.
(1)
The Kramers' opacity law becomes κ=(κ0​/R)PT−9/2. Dividing the two differential equations eliminates radius and mass density:
PdTdP​=3κ0​L16πar​cGMR​T15/2.
(2)
Integrating from a surface matching point (Ps​,Ts​) gives
P2=Ps2​+51κ0​L64πar​cGMR​(T17/2−Ts17/2​).
(3)
Deep compared with the photosphere, the surface terms are negligible. The radiative-zero white-dwarf envelope therefore has
P(T)=(51κ0​L64πar​cGMR​)1/2T17/4,ρ∝T13/4.​
(4)
This is the leading envelope approximation used in the later parts, rather than an exact atmospheric boundary condition at Teff​.
At the base, temperature, mass density and pressure match the isothermal degenerate core. Let KNR​=K1​μe−5/3​, where μe​ is the mean molecular weight per electron. Equating the gas and electron degeneracy pressure gives
Rρb​Tc​=KNR​ρb5/3​,ρb​=(KNR​RTc​​)3/2=μe5/2​(K1​R​)3/2Tc3/2​.​
(5)
Hence Pb​=μe5/2​R5/2K1−3/2​Tc5/2​. Substituting this into the envelope relation at T=Tc​ yields
ML​=51κ0​64πar​cG​μe5​R4K13​​Tc7/2​=51κ0​256πσG​μe5​K13​​(kμmH​​)4Tc7/2​.​
(6)
The matching of a white-dwarf envelope to a degenerate core thus gives L/M∝Tc7/2​, the envelope relation underlying Mestel's cooling law. It depends on pressure as well as mass density continuity at the interface; otherwise the supplied core equation would not determine the matching mass density.

In the radiative-zero white-dwarf envelope, dP/dT=(17/4)P/T=(17/4)Rρ. Substitute this derivative into the stellar hydrostatic equation:
417​RρdrdT​=−r2GMρ​,drdT​=−174​Rr2GM​.
(7)
Integrating from the idealized zero-temperature surface at R gives
T(r)=174​RGM​(r1​−R1​)=174​kμmH​​GM(r1​−R1​).​
(8)
This also gives dlogT/dlogP=4/17. A finite surface temperature adds Ts​ to the integrated profile if the pure power-law pressure relation is retained locally. Near the actual photosphere, the integration constant in the pressure law also matters; the displayed expression is the radiative-zero limit implicit in the printed result and accurate in the deep envelope when Tc​≫Teff​.

Apply the preceding temperature profile at the envelope base. Introduce the dimensionless parameter
q=417​GMRTc​R​.
(9)
Then R/rb​−1=q, so the geometrical thickness of a white-dwarf radiative envelope is
rb​=1+qR​,Rs​=1+qq​≃q,s≃417​GMRTc​R2​.​
(10)
The smallness to be established is therefore RTc​≪GM/R, up to the numerical factor 17/4.
That ordering follows from the cold, strongly electron-degenerate white dwarf model. Throughout the mass-bearing core, kTc​≪EF​, where EF​ is the Fermi energy. For nonrelativistic electron degeneracy pressure,
ρPdeg​​=5μe​mH​2EF​​.
(11)
The stellar hydrostatic equation gives a characteristic core Pdeg​/ρ of order GM/R. Consequently
GM/RRTc​​∼2μ5μe​​EF​kTc​​≪1
(12)
for fixed composition with order-one molecular-weight ratios, and s≪R. Equivalently, the matched base mass density is much lower than a characteristic core mass density, since ρb​/ρcore​∼[RTc​/(GM/R)]3/2. The degeneracy ordering applies in the bulk core; at the matching point degeneracy is becoming marginal.
The inequalities Tc​≫Teff​ and kTc​≪EF​ express different facts. The first justifies ignoring surface-temperature terms, while the second makes the envelope geometrically thin. If the core were hot enough that q were not small, the exact expression s/R=q/(1+q) would not justify a thin-layer approximation.

Compare two cooling states of the same white dwarf, holding its mass, nearly temperature-independent degenerate radius and composition fixed. The matching of a white-dwarf envelope to a degenerate core gives L∝Tc7/2​, while the geometrical thickness of a white-dwarf radiative envelope gives s∝Tc​ at leading thin-layer order. Eliminate Tc​:
s2​s1​​=Tc,2​Tc,1​​=(L2​L1​​)2/7.​
(13)
Thus a luminosity drop makes the envelope thinner, with s2​/s1​=(L2​/L1​)2/7. This is the white-dwarf envelope thickness-luminosity relation. Retaining finite thickness in the radiative-zero model gives the correction
s2​s1​​=(L2​L1​​)2/71+q1​1+q2​​,q1​/q2​=(L1​/L2​)2/7,
(14)
which reduces to the boxed result for q1​,q2​≪1.

Use stellar homology: dimensionless profiles and composition are fixed, so proportionality constants do not change along the model sequence. Scaling mass conservation and the stellar hydrostatic equation gives
ρ∗​∝R3M​,P∗​∝R4GM2​,T∗​∝ρ∗​P∗​​∝RM​,
(15)
where the last relation uses the ideal gas equation of state with fixed mean molecular weight. The specific stellar energy-generation rate is ϵ∝ρTη, so
Lnuc​∼Mϵ∗​∝Mη+2R−(η+3).
(16)
By stellar radiative diffusion, T∗​/R∼κ∗​ρ∗​L/R2T∗3​, hence
Lrad​∝κ∗​ρ∗​RT∗4​​∝M3−λ−νR3λ+ν.
(17)
Equate the generated and transported luminosity in thermal equilibrium. The homology relations for radiative stars give
R3+η+ν+3λ∝Mη+ν+λ−1,R∝MX,X=3+η+ν+3λη+ν+λ−1​.​
(18)
This division presumes a nonzero denominator. If it vanishes, the scaling balance instead imposes a condition on M or leaves the radius scaling undetermined, depending on the numerator. For later use, the corresponding mass-luminosity relation exponent is
Y=η+2−(η+3)X=3−λ−ν+(3λ+ν)X.
(19)

For fixed composition, electron-scattering opacity is effectively independent of mass density and temperature, so λ=ν=0. With the specified CNO cycle exponent η=16, the homology relations for radiative stars give
X=1915​,Y=18−19X=3.
(23)
The radiative homology with CNO burning and electron scattering is therefore
R∝M15/19,L∝M3.​
(24)
The Stefan–Boltzmann law now gives
Teff​∝M27/76,dlogTeff​dlogL​=3−30/194⋅3​=976​≃8.444.​
(25)
Equivalently Teff​∝L9/76. These slopes are illustrated with arbitrary separate reference-model normalizations:
The proton–proton/Kramers model applies to the lower-mass regime; the CNO/electron-scattering model applies to the higher-mass regime. Higher-mass cores are hotter, making the more temperature-sensitive CNO cycle efficient, and highly ionized material favors electron-scattering opacity. These are the stated idealized radiative, gas-pressure-supported sequences: a fully convective low-mass star or a radiation-pressure-dominated massive star needs a different structural model. Let S denote specific entropy, reserving s from the first question for envelope thickness. The stellar adiabatic exponents are defined at fixed composition by
Γ1​=(∂logρ∂logP​)S​,Γ2​Γ2​−1​=(∂logP∂logT​)S​.​
(26)
It is useful to introduce Γ3​−1=(∂logT/∂logρ)S​, so (Γ2​−1)/Γ2​=(Γ3​−1)/Γ1​.
Take the stellar gas to be a fully ionized, nonrelativistic monatomic ideal gas, with fixed specific gas constant R. Its mixture with thermal radiation has
P=RρT+31​ar​T4,u=23​RT+ρar​T4​.
(27)
Here u is specific internal energy, and the stellar gas-pressure fraction is β=RρT/P. The assumption of monatomic gas fixes its heat capacity; the perfect-gas pressure law alone would not determine it.
At fixed T and fixed ρ, respectively, the pressure derivatives are
χρ​=(∂logρ∂logP​)T​=β,χT​=(∂logT∂logP​)ρ​=4−3β.
(28)
For an isentropic process, the first law of thermodynamics gives du=Pdρ/ρ2. Differentiate the internal energy rather than artificially holding β fixed during the perturbation:
(23​R+ρ4ar​T3​)dT=ρ2P+ar​T4​dρ.
(29)
The parenthesis is (P/ρT)[(3/2)β+12(1−β)], giving
Γ3​−1=12−(21/2)β4−3β​=24−21β8−6β​.
(30)
Since dlogP=χρ​dlogρ+χT​dlogT,
Γ1​=β+(4−3β)(Γ3​−1)=24−21β32−24β−3β2​.​
(31)
Using the relation between the exponents gives the adiabatic exponents of a monatomic gas-radiation mixture
Γ2​=24−18β−3β232−24β−3β2​,∇ad​=Γ2​Γ2​−1​=32−24β−3β28−6β​.​
(32)
The requested values, including the adiabatic temperature gradient, are
β01/21​Γ1​4/377/545/3​Γ2​4/377/575/3​∇ad​1/420/772/5​​.​
(33)
The pure-radiation and pure-gas rows are understood as limits of the mixture.
For uniform composition, the Schwarzschild criterion for stellar convective instability is
∇≡dlogPdlogT​>∇ad​=Γ2​Γ2​−1​.​
(34)
To test whether a radiative configuration becomes unstable, use its required stellar radiative temperature gradient ∇rad​ for ∇. Since dP/dr<0, the equivalent radial condition is
−drdT​>Γ2​Γ2​−1​PT​(−drdP​).
(35)
An outward-displaced parcel then cools less than its new surroundings, remains less dense at the same pressure, and is further accelerated outward.
The requested Eddington-model convective-core mass fraction follows from the usual global constant-β closure. At the outer radiative surface, put m=M, Lr​=L, and Prad​=(1−β)P. Dividing the radiation-pressure gradient by the stellar hydrostatic equation gives
dPdPrad​​=4πcGmκLr​​,L=κ4πcGM​(1−β)=(1−β)LEdd​.
(36)
There is no energy generation outside the convective core, so use Lr​=L there. The local radiative-gradient formula is
∇rad​(m)=16πar​cGmT43κLP​=16πcGm(1−β)κL​≃4mM​.
(37)
The same representative β has been used in the global luminosity closure and at the boundary. Setting this gradient equal to ∇ad​ at m=Mc​ produces
MMc​​=4∇ad​1​=4(Γ2​−1)Γ2​​=4(8−6β)32−24β−3β2​.​
(38)
This is 1, 77/80, and 5/8 for β=0,1/2,1, respectively, with β=0 a marginal radiation-dominated limit.
There is a consistency qualification to this last model estimate. It cannot be an exact stellar solution if all the printed assumptions are enforced pointwise. An exactly uniform nonzero radiation fraction would give dPrad​/dP=1−β at every radiative point. The exact equations would then require
mLr​​=κ4πcG​(1−β).
(39)
If Lr​ is constant throughout an envelope with positive mass density, m increases with radius, so this equality cannot hold there. Equivalently uniform β fixes the actual gradient to 1/4, whereas ∇ad​>1/4 for β>0. The boxed core fraction is the intended Eddington closure and boundary estimate, which relaxes exact constancy of β in the detailed envelope. The constant-beta radiative-envelope obstruction identifies the missing approximation; it is not legitimate to silently assert exact compatibility.

For a slowly evolving spherical star, use a Lagrangian enclosed mass coordinate m. Let u be specific thermal internal energy and D/Dt follow a mass element. The stellar energy balance equation is
∂m∂L​=ϵnuc​−ϵν​−DtDu​−PDtD​(ρ1​)=ϵnuc​−ϵν​+ϵgrav​.​
(40)
The outward luminosity is energy transported through the mass shell, ϵnuc​ is nuclear rest-mass energy released per unit mass and time, and ϵν​ is escaping neutrino power per unit mass. Define the gravothermal stellar energy generation by ϵgrav​=−Du/Dt−PD(1/ρ)/Dt. At fixed composition it is −TDS/Dt by the first law of thermodynamics. During composition changes the full internal-energy derivative must use the appropriate equation of state, with nuclear rest-mass changes counted only once. If a tabulated nuclear rate already subtracts reaction-neutrino energy, that loss must not be subtracted a second time in ϵν​.
In a nearly steady main sequence star, stellar nuclear fusion supplies most of the luminosity. Before stable hydrogen ignition, Kelvin-Helmholtz contraction releases gravitational energy. In a gas-supported hydrostatic star the stellar virial theorem gives U≃−Ω/2, so about half the change in gravitational binding energy heats the star and half is available for radiation. The associated timescale is GM2/(RL). After nuclear burning ends, a white dwarf can shine by losing stored thermal energy; crystallization releases latent heat, and composition separation can add gravitational energy. These are heat and gravitational reservoirs rather than sustained hydrogen fusion.
Stellar nuclear fusion is possible because lighter nuclei can combine into products with larger nuclear binding energy. The energy per reaction is Q=(minitial​−mfinal​)c2, including the relevant particles consistently. Although thermal energies are below the Coulomb barrier, quantum tunnelling permits fusion. The thermonuclear reaction rate averages a cross-section over the thermal relative-speed distribution:
r12​=1+δ12​n1​n2​​⟨σv⟩,ϵ12​=ρr12​Qdeposited​​.
(41)
For nonresonant charged-particle reactions the energy weighting contains exp[−E/(kT)−EG​/E​]; the compromise between the thermal tail and penetration produces the Gamow peak. A resonance can greatly enhance the rate. Density, abundances and temperature therefore all affect the stellar energy-generation rate, and power laws such as T4 or T16 are local approximations.
For the proton–proton chain, first produce deuterium and helium-3:
p+p⟶2H+e++νe​,2H+p⟶3He+γ.
(42)
The initial reaction is slow because it converts a proton to a neutron through the weak interaction. It is the bottleneck that allows long hydrogen-burning lifetimes. The alternative pep reaction p+e−+p→2H+νe​ also feeds the same chain. There are three principal proton-proton chain branches. In pp I, two helium-3 nuclei terminate the chain:
3He+3He⟶4He+2p.
(43)
In pp II, helium-3 first captures an existing helium-4 nucleus, then electron capture and proton capture finish the branch:
3He+4He⟶7Be+γ,7Be+e−⟶7Li+νe​,7Li+p⟶24He.
(44)
In pp III, the beryllium-7 instead captures a proton:
7Be+p⟶8B+γ,8B⟶8Be∗+e++νe​,8Be∗⟶24He.
(45)
Relative branch weights change with temperature and composition. All branches convert four protons into a net helium-4 nucleus, with two weak conversions and two neutrinos, allowing for the electron consumed in pp II. After including positron annihilation, the atomic-mass energy budget is approximately 26.7MeV per net helium-4 nucleus, but the deposited heat is smaller by the branch-dependent escaping-neutrino energy. Near ordinary low-mass main-sequence conditions, ϵpp​∝ρXH2​T4 is a useful local approximation.
The CNO cycle provides an alternative catalyzed hydrogen burning route in hotter cores. The CNO-I cycle, also called the CN cycle, consists of
12C+p13N13C+p14N+p15O15N+p​⟶13N+γ,⟶13C+e++νe​,⟶14N+γ,⟶15O+γ,⟶15N+e++νe​,⟶12C+4He.​
(46)
The carbon seed is regenerated: the net reaction again consumes four protons and produces one helium-4 nucleus, two positrons and two neutrinos. The slow 14N(p,γ)15O reaction governs the ordinary cycle and causes nitrogen-14 to accumulate. The CNO-II cycle branches through 15N(p,γ)16O, 16O(p,γ)17F, 17F→17O+e++νe​, and 17O(p,α)14N, after which the main cycle continues. Thus CNO nuclei are catalytic in the closed cycles, although their relative abundances change. A local rate is ϵCNO​∝ρXH​XCNO​Tη with a much steeper exponent than the PP chain; the preceding homology problem specifies η=16. The concentration of this heating near the centre favors a convective core in hotter, more massive hydrogen-burning stars.
Once hydrogen is exhausted in a core, contraction can raise its temperature enough for core helium burning. The Triple-alpha process overcomes the absence of stable mass-5 and mass-8 nuclei by maintaining a tiny transient beryllium-8 population:
4He+4He⇌8Be,8Be+4He⟶12C∗⟶12C+γ rays.
(47)
The Hoyle state, an excited carbon-12 resonance near the three-alpha threshold, makes the second capture efficient enough despite beryllium-8's very short lifetime. The net conversion is 34He→12C, releasing about 7.27MeV. Because three alpha particles are required, the specific rate scales as ρ2Y3; a schematic resonant rate is
ϵ3α​∝ρ2Y3T8−3​e−44/T8​,T8​=T/(108K).
(48)
Its local temperature exponent is −3+44/T8​, of order forty near T8​=1. A degenerate core cannot expand promptly to regulate this increase, giving a helium flash; in a nondegenerate core the stellar thermostat permits stable helium burning. The competing 12C(α,γ)16O reaction determines much of the eventual carbon-oxygen mixture.
If the core becomes hot enough, carbon burning follows, for example through 12C+12C→20Ne+α and 12C+12C→23Na+p. Neon burning begins with photodisintegration, 20Ne+γ→16O+α, followed by alpha capture such as 20Ne+α→24Mg+γ. The first step consumes heat, but the combined rearrangement can release net energy. Oxygen burning includes 16O+16O→28Si+α and channels producing phosphorus and other nearby nuclei. At still higher temperatures, silicon burning proceeds through photodisintegration and particle captures in a reaction network approaching quasi-statistical equilibrium, producing iron-group nuclei. It is not simply direct silicon-plus-silicon fusion; the products depend on the electron fraction and weak-interaction timescale.
The increasing nuclear binding energy per nucleon supplies progressively less energy as products approach the iron group. Further fusion past that region cannot provide sustained net heat to support a core. Massive stars can therefore end with an unstable iron-group core; lower-mass stars do not attain all these burning stages and instead leave remnants such as carbon-oxygen white dwarfs. Heavy-element neutron captures can synthesize nuclei beyond the iron group without being the principal hydrostatic power source.
Finally, stellar neutrino energy loss competes with every heat source. Reaction neutrinos accompany the proton–proton and CNO chains. Hot or dense material also emits neutrino pairs through e−+e+→ν+νˉ, plasmon decay, e−+γ→e−+ν+νˉ, and electron-ion bremsstrahlung. These neutrinos usually escape, unlike the photons whose transport is diffusive; neutrino trapping requires much more extreme collapse conditions. Late burning has smaller fuel-energy reservoirs and strong neutrino cooling, so its duration is much shorter than core hydrogen burning. The surface luminosity is the escaping photon power, L=4πR2σTeff4​, not the sum of photons and unobserved neutrino power. Radiative diffusion in a star, convection and electron conduction redistribute energy, while the energy-balance equation distinguishes true sources, losses and storage. Accretion or mass loss adds boundary energy and mechanical work when present, rather than changing the nuclear reaction budget.
