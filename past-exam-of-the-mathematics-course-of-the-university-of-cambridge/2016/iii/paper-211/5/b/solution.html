For the intended random walk model, take S0​ to be deterministic, or independent of all the independent and identically distributed random variables Yj​=Sj​−Sj−1​. If ν is their common probability distribution, then Yt+1​ is independent of Ft​=σ(S0​,Y1​,…,Yt​). This supplies the Markov property that the backward dynamic programming argument needs. V(T,s)=f(s),V(t,s)=max{f(s),(PV(t+1,⋅))(s)}.
(48)
At any state where the conditional law is evaluated, independence gives
E[V(t+1,St+1​)∣Ft​]=(PV(t+1,⋅))(St​).
(49)
Backward induction, starting with UT​=f(ST​), proves the intended representation
Ut​=V(t,St​).​
(50)
Integrability along the actual random walk makes the recursion finite at the states used by that process, up to null sets. If finite real-valued value functions on all of R are intended, a convenient sufficient convention is statewise integrability: E∣f(s+Y1​+⋯+Yk​)∣<∞ for every s and 0≤k≤T. Indeed each stopping value is bounded in absolute value by the expectation of the sum of the absolute rewards. This convention makes the displayed recursion finite and measurable everywhere; the source only assumes integrability from the given starting process.
The printed independence of the increments alone does not suffice if S0​ can reveal future increments. Here is a bounded finite-state counterexample, also with a convex reward. Let T=2, let ε1​,ε2​ be independent fair signs, and set
S0​=ε2​,S1​=ε1​+ε2​,S2​=ε1​+2ε2​,f(s)=s+.
(51)
The two increments are exactly ε1​,ε2​, hence independent and identically distributed. But F1​ already knows both signs, so U1​=max{S1+​,S2+​}. On the two positive-probability histories with S1​=0, its values are respectively 0 and 1. Thus no deterministic V(1,0) can work. Adding independence of S0​ from the increments repairs this missing Markov property hypothesis.
