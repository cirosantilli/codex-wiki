A sufficient absolute constant is C=16​. The proof is a density increment argument for a cap set over the finite field F3​.
First work in V=F3d​, write N=3d, and let f=1A​ be the indicator function of a cap set of subset density α>0. All expectations below are uniform. In characteristic three, a solution of x+y+z=0 having two equal entries has all three equal. Consequently the normalized linear configuration count is
T=Ex,y​f(x)f(y)f(−x−y)=N2∣A∣​=Nα​.
(1)
Set ω=e2πi/3 and use Fourier analysis on a finite abelian group with
f​(ξ)=Ex​f(x)ω−ξ⋅x.
(2)
The orthogonality of roots of unity and the Parseval identity on a finite group give
T=∑ξ∈V​f​(ξ)3,f​(0)=α,∑ξ=0​∣f​(ξ)∣2=α−α2.
(3)
If Nα2≥2, then α<1 for a cap set and
2α3​≤α3−Nα​≤∑ξ=0​∣f​(ξ)∣3≤(maxξ=0​∣f​(ξ)∣)(α−α2).

Thus some nonzero finite abelian Fourier coefficient has magnitude at least α2/2.
For this ξ, let αj​ be the subset density of A on the affine subspace {x:ξ⋅x=j}, for j=0,1,2. These three affine subspaces have equal cardinality, and

Among three directions separated by 2π/3, one makes an angle at most π/3 with any given complex number. Hence maxj​(αj​−α)≥∣f​(ξ)∣. Restricting to that hyperplane gives the hyperplane density increment for cap sets

(6)
Translate the affine subspace to its underlying vector space. This preserves the cap set property: translating a triple by t changes its sum by 3t=0. The same argument can therefore be iterated.
For completeness, the density increment iteration gives an explicit uniform bound. If n<16, the hypothesis α≥16/n is impossible. Suppose n≥16 and a cap set has α0​≥16/n. For every integer 0≤t≤⌊n/2⌋, its remaining dimension is at least n/2, and its current subset density is at least 16/n. Thus
3n−tαt2​≥3n/2n2256​≥2.
(7)
The last inequality holds at n=16 and remains true as n increases: the successive ratio of 3n/2/n2 is 3​(n/(n+1))2>1 for n≥16. At each step, as long as the subset density remains at most one,
αt​1​−αt+1​1​≥2+αt​1​≥31​.
(8)
After L=⌊n/2⌋ steps this would imply
0<αL​1​≤16n​−3L​<0,
(9)
a contradiction. The endpoint α=1 already contradicts the cap set property in positive dimension. This proves the claimed existence of three distinct points and the Meshulam bound for cap sets.

Use uniform expectations on the two nonempty parts, and equip their function spaces with the inner product ⟨u,v⟩=Euv. Let H=G−γ and
(TH​v)(x)=Ey​H(x,y)v(y).
(10)
Since the bipartite graph is a biregular graph, both row and column averages of H vanish. The normalized adjacency operator of a bipartite graph therefore splits into the map between constant functions, of singular value γ, and TH​ between their orthogonal complements.
There is no singular value larger than γ. Indeed, for γ>0, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
∣Ey​G(x,y)v(y)∣2≤γEy​G(x,y)∣v(y)∣2.
(11)
Averaging in x uses the constant column degree and gives ∥θG​v∥2​≤γ∥v∥2​. For γ=0 every operator is zero. Thus the second singular value is
s=∥TH​∥op​.
(12)
If one part has a single vertex, missing singular values are interpreted as zero; the biregular graph then has H=0.
The discrepancy in (i) is exactly EH1A​1B​. Its supremum over A,B is the cut norm δ=∥H∥cut​. To pass from this bound to arbitrary bounded real-valued functions, use the positive and negative parts and the identity u+​(x)=∫0M​1{u(x)>t}​dt for ∣u∣≤M. Applying this separately to both factors gives

Use the following normalization for Fourier analysis on a finite group. Choose one unitary irreducible representation ρ:G→U(dρ​) from each equivalence class, including the trivial representation. For a scalar function f:G→C, put
f​(ρ)=Ex​f(x)ρ(x),⟨f,g⟩=Ex​f(x)​g(x).
(22)
This convention uses ρ(x), rather than ρ(x)∗, in the Fourier transform on a finite group; it makes the normalized convolution on a finite group preserve multiplication order.
The needed representation theory consists of Maschke's theorem and unitarization of a finite-group representation, together with the Schur orthogonality relations:
Ex​ρ(x)ij​σ(x)kl​​={dρ−1​δik​δjl​,0,​ρ=σ,ρ=σ.​
(23)
The regular representation contains dρ​ copies of each ρ, so ∑ρ​dρ2​=∣G∣. Thus the scaled matrix coefficients dρ​​ρ(x)ij​, and also their complex conjugates, form an orthonormal basis of all scalar functions on G. These facts imply Fourier inversion on a finite group and the Parseval identity on a finite group in the forms
f(x)=∑ρ​dρ​tr(f​(ρ)ρ(x)∗),⟨f,g⟩=∑ρ​dρ​tr(f​(ρ)∗g​(ρ)),
(24)
and hence
∥f∥22​=∑ρ​dρ​∥f​(ρ)∥HS2​.
(25)
In particular, the transform is an isomorphism onto the direct sum of the matrix algebras Mdρ​​(C), with the displayed weighted Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.
Define the normalized convolution on a finite group by
(f∗g)(x)=Ey​f(y)g(y−1x).
(26)
Substituting x=yz and using the group representation identity yields the convolution theorem on a finite group
f∗g​(ρ)=f​(ρ)g​(ρ).
(27)
Unlike normalized convolution on a finite group on an abelian group, this product need not commute. If f​(x)=f(x−1)​, then f​​(ρ)=f​(ρ)∗. For left translation of a group function and right translation of a group function La​f(x)=f(a−1x) and Ra​f(x)=f(xa),
La​f​(ρ)=ρ(a)f​(ρ),Ra​f​(ρ)=f​(ρ)ρ(a)∗.
(28)
For an abelian group, every irreducible representation is one-dimensional; this reduces to Fourier analysis on a finite abelian group with characters relabelled by their inverses. These formulas establish the basic scalar theory, with all normalizations and multiplication orders fixed.
Now suppose every nontrivial irreducible representation has dρ​≥m. If f is a mean-zero function, its component at the trivial representation is zero. The Parseval identity on a finite group gives, for each other ρ,
∥f​(ρ)∥op​≤∥f​(ρ)∥HS​≤dρ​​∥f∥2​​≤m​∥f∥2​​.
(29)
Using the convolution theorem on a finite group, the Hilbert-Schmidt norm inequality ∥AB∥HS​≤∥A∥op​∥B∥HS​, and the Parseval identity on a finite group once more gives the product mixing in a quasirandom group estimate
∥f∗g∥2​≤m−1/2∥f∥2​∥g∥2​when Ef=0.​
(30)
Write a,b,c for the subset density values of A,B,C, respectively, and let f=1A​−a, g=1B​−b be balanced subset indicators. Since both are mean-zero functions, 1A​∗1B​=ab+f∗g. Their squared norms are a(1−a) and b(1−b). Also E(f∗g)=0, so the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields
∣Ez​(1A​∗1B​)(z)1C​(z)−abc∣​=∣Ez​(f∗g)(z)(1C​(z)−c)∣≤ma(1−a)b(1−b)c(1−c)​​≤mabc​​.​
(31)
If abc>1/m, the final bound is strictly smaller than abc. Thus the normalized number of pairs (x,y)∈A×B with xy∈C is positive. Equivalently,
∣A∣∣B∣∣C∣>∣G∣3/m ⟹ AB∩C=∅.​
(32)
This is the desired conclusion for a quasirandom group; the strict inequality ensures positivity rather than merely a nonnegative lower bound.

Use c>0, the necessary parameter range for the bounds involving 1/c. The construction comes from the singular value decomposition of matrix Fourier blocks. For each inequivalent unitary irreducible representation ρ of dimension dρ​, consider the Hilbert space Hρ​=Mn×dρ​​(C) with the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product ⟨A,B⟩=tr(A∗B) and the operator
Tρ​(A)=Ex​f(x)Aρ(x)∗.
(33)
Its operator norm is at most one, since ρ(x) is a unitary matrix and ∥f(x)∥op​≤1. Thus all its singular values λρ,j​ lie in [0,1].
Two identities control their weighted moments:
S2​=∑ρ​dρ​∑j​λρ,j2​=Ex​tr(f(x)f(x)∗)≤n,
(34)
S4​=∑ρ​dρ​∑j​λρ,j4​=Exy−1zw−1=e​tr(f(x)f(y)∗f(z)f(w)∗)≥cn.
(35)
Here the expectation in S4​ is uniform on the ∣G∣3 solutions of the constraint, and the trace on Hρ​ used to compute each moment is the ordinary operator trace. We justify the identities explicitly to fix their normalizations and the noncommutative order.
The adjoint operator is Tρ∗​(A)=Ey​f(y)∗Aρ(y), so
Tρ​Tρ∗​(A)=Ex,y​f(x)f(y)∗Aρ(yx−1).
(36)
For rectangular matrices, the operator A↦LAR has trace tr(L)tr(R), as is seen on the matrix-unit basis. Squaring the previous operator therefore gives
TrHρ​​(Tρ​Tρ∗​)TrHρ​​((Tρ​Tρ∗​)2)​=Ex,y​tr(f(x)f(y)∗)χρ​(yx−1),=Ex,y,z,w​tr(f(x)f(y)∗f(z)f(w)∗)χρ​(wz−1yx−1).​
(37)
The required representation theory consists of unitarization of a finite-group representation, the Schur orthogonality relations, and the regular representation decomposition. The last gives the character expansion of the identity delta
∑ρ​dρ​χρ​(g)=∣G∣1{g=e}​.
(38)
Summing the two operator traces with weights dρ​ selects x=y in the first, and w=xy−1z in the second. Multiplication by ∣G∣ converts each independent-variable expectation to its conditional expectation. This proves S2​,S4​. In particular S4​ is real and nonnegative, despite the apparently complex summands in its original formula. Since S4​≤S2​≤n, the nonvacuous hypothesis has 0<c≤1.
Select every singular value satisfying λρ,j​≥c/2​. Index the selected pairs by p=1,…,r, set ρp​=ρ, np​=dρ​, and choose unit right singular vectors Ap​ and unit left singular vectors Bp​ with Tρp​​Ap​=λp​Bp​. Define
U(p)=np​​Ap​,V(p)=np​​Bp​.​
(39)
Both are n×np​ matrices, proving (i). Repeated group representations in this list are literally the same chosen representative, rather than different equivalent realizations.

Within each fixed unitary irreducible representation, choose the selected right singular vectors to be orthonormal. Their corresponding left singular vectors are also orthonormal, even when a singular value is repeated: choose an orthonormal basis in each eigenspace of Tρ∗​Tρ​ and put Bp​=Tρ​Ap​/λp​. Therefore, when ρp​=ρq​,
tr(U(p)∗U(q))=tr(V(p)∗V(q))=np​δpq​​.
(44)
This is (iv), with the unnormalized Hilbert-Schmidt inner product and the Kronecker delta. No orthogonality of differently shaped matrices is being asserted.
For the unheaded continuation, partition b∈Cm into blocks bp​∈Cnp​. Then
UP(x)∗b=∑p​U(p)ρp​(x)∗bp​.

The Schur averaging of rectangular matrices formula is
Ex​ρp​(x)Mρq​(x)∗={(trM/np​)Inp​​,0,​ρp​=ρq​,ρp​=ρq​,​
(46)
for any np​×nq​ matrix M. The average is an intertwiner; the Schur lemma makes it zero for inequivalent irreducible representations, and a scalar multiple of the identity for the same representative. The trace determines that scalar in the latter case.
