(1)

(2)
This is the distinction between the drift of logS and the drift of S: here the latter is zero.

First work with the filtration Gt​=FtW​∨σ(1{u≤τ}​:0≤u≤t), which records the Brownian motion and whether default has occurred. On {s>τ} all subsequent defaultable stock prices vanish. The residual survival event is independent of the future independent increments of the Brownian motion. Thus, for deterministic s<t,
E[St​∣Gs​]=1{s≤τ}​eλte−λ(t−s)E[St​∣FsW​]=1{s≤τ}​eλsSs​=Ss​.

Choose any strictly positive pricing kernel Z∈Z. The assumed componentwise expectations and the linearity of expectation give
E[Z(H⋅P)]=H⋅E[ZP]=H⋅p≤0.
(24)
The integrand is nonnegative, so its expectation is also nonnegative and hence zero. A nonnegative random variable with zero expectation vanishes almost surely. Since Z>0 almost surely, this forces H⋅P=0 almost surely. Both conclusions follow:
H⋅p=0,H⋅P=0almost surely.​
(25)
The strict positivity of the pricing kernel is essential: a kernel allowed to vanish could miss a positive payoff on its zero set. No normalization EZ=1 is assumed in this abstract market; that normalization follows only if a unit-priced unit-payoff cash asset is included.

A complete market can replicate every contingent claim in its specified payoff class: for every terminal FT​-measurable payoff X, there is an admissible self-financing portfolio whose terminal wealth equals X almost surely. Equivalently, in an arbitrage-free finite-state discrete-time model the equivalent martingale measure is unique. In notation,
∀X in the claim class,∃ self-financing θ with VTθ​=X almost surely.​
(36)
Market completeness is a replication property. The following price comparisons also use the usual absence of arbitrage, which the source leaves implicit: without it the initial cost of claim replication need not be unique. For example, in a deterministic one-period model with cash worth one at both dates and a stock worth one initially but zero finally, all terminal claims are replicable using cash. Adding any stock holding changes the initial cost without changing the terminal payoff. This is a complete model with arbitrage, so it has no well-defined unique replication price.

Backward induction makes every Ut​ adapted and integrable: the conditional expectation of an integrable random variable is integrable, and ∣max(a,b)∣≤∣a∣+∣b∣. The Snell envelope recursion directly gives Ut​≥E[Ut+1​∣Ft​], so U is a supermartingale.

Ut​=max{Zt​,E[ZT​∣Ft​]}=E[ZT​∣Ft​].
(45)

U is a supermartingale;Z submartingale ⟹ Ut​=E[ZT​∣Ft​] is a martingale.​
(46)
In the second case waiting until T attains the optimal stopping value. It is not generally true that Ut​=Zt​: that further equality holds when Z itself is a martingale.

Define the transition operator

Integrability along the actual random walk makes the recursion finite at the states used by that process, up to null sets. Take the original measure as the risk-neutral measure for this zero-interest model, or interpret S as a discounted stock price. ξt​ is the arbitrage-free value of the European contingent claim with payoff g(ST​), and πt​ is its option delta and replicating stock holding. Part (b) supplies the self-financing portfolio:
stock holding πt​=VS​(t,St​),cash holding ξt​−πt​St​.​
(63)
With the cash asset identically one, its value is ξt​ and its gains satisfy dξt​=πt​dSt​, so it exactly replicates g(ST​). This delta hedge is admissible since the assumed nonnegative V gives nonnegative wealth. In this continuous-path setting the holdings can be taken predictable.
The auxiliary change of measure in part (c) expresses the option delta as an expectation of terminal payoff sensitivity. It changes the stock drift to aa′ and is generally not the original risk-neutral measure for the zero-interest stock market. Its role is to value sensitivity under the tilted state dynamics, while the actual replicating strategy trades under the original dynamics. No general claim of market completeness is needed, including when a can vanish.
