An integral domain A is an integrally closed domain if every element of its fraction field which is an integral element over A belongs to A. Explicitly, if z∈FracA satisfies
zd+ad−1​zd−1+⋯+a0​=0,ai​∈A,
(34)
then z∈A. Thus A is integrally closed exactly when its integral closure in its fraction field is A​.
Suppose A is a unique factorization domain and z=a/b is integral, with a,b∈A, b=0, and with no common irreducible element as a factor. Multiplying its monic equation by bd gives
ad+ad−1​ad−1b+⋯+a0​bd=0.
(35)
Hence b divides ad. Every irreducible element dividing b is a prime element, so it would divide a, contrary to the choice of the fraction. Therefore b is a unit, and z∈A. We have proved
Every unique factorization domain is integrally closed.​
(36)
The going-down theorem is the following. Let A⊆B be an integral extension of integral domains with A an integrally closed domain. If
p1​⊆p2​in A,q2​∩A=p2​for a prime ideal q2​⊂B,
(37)
then
there is a prime ideal q1​⊆q2​ with q1​∩A=p1​.​
(38)
We first prove this for a module-finite ring extension and then remove that extra hypothesis. We use the Lying-over theorem, the going-up theorem, and the incomparability theorem for integral extensions: primes exist over every base prime, prime chains can be extended upwards, and distinct primes over the same base prime cannot contain one another. The going-up theorem follows by applying the Lying-over theorem to the quotient by the lower prime. These results concern arbitrary integral extensions; none assumes the going-down theorem.
For the finite case, let K=FracA and L=FracB. Since B is module-finite, L/K is a finite field extension. Take a finite normal field extension E/K containing L, obtained as a splitting field of the minimal polynomials of finitely many field generators. The extension E/K need not be a separable field extension. Let C be the integral closure of A in E. Then B⊆C, and C is integral over both A and B.
We need one auxiliary fact: the finite group G=AutK​(E) acts transitively on the prime ideals of C over any fixed prime ideal p of A. First observe that an element w∈E fixed by all of G is a purely inseparable algebraic element over K. Indeed, all K-embeddings of K(w) into an algebraic closure extend to K-automorphisms of the normal field extension E. Hence the minimal polynomial of an algebraic element w has only one distinct root. In characteristic zero this says w∈K; in characteristic p>0 it says wpe∈K for some e≥0.
Now let Q,Q′ be primes of C over p, and suppose Q is different from every σ(Q′), σ∈G. By the incomparability theorem for integral extensions, Q is contained in none of these conjugate primes. The prime avoidance lemma supplies
x∈Q∖⋃σ∈G​σ(Q′).
(39)
The product
h=∏σ∈G​σ(x)
(40)
is fixed by G, is integral over A, and lies in Q because one factor is x. Choose an exponent e0​ equal to 1 in characteristic zero, or a power of p in characteristic p, such that he0​∈K. Since A is an integrally closed domain, he0​∈A. It also lies in Q∩A=p, hence in Q′. Because Q′ is a prime ideal, some factor σ(x) lies in Q′. This means x∈σ−1(Q′), contradicting our choice. Thus Q=σ(Q′) for some σ, proving transitivity. The power e0​ accounts for the possible purely inseparable field extension and makes the argument valid in every characteristic.
Lift the prescribed q2​ to a prime Q2​ of C by the Lying-over theorem for B⊆C. Separately, choose a prime Q1′​ over p1​ and use the going-up theorem to find Q2′​⊇Q1′​ over p2​. Transitivity gives σ∈G with σ(Q2′​)=Q2​. Then
Q1​=σ(Q1′​)⊆Q2​,Q1​∩A=p1​.
(41)
Contracting to B gives q1​=Q1​∩B⊆q2​ with the required contraction. This proves the going-down theorem for the module-finite case.
For a general integral extension A⊆B, consider Bq2​​ and the ideal
J=p1​Bq2​​.
(42)
We claim that J is disjoint from A∖p1​. Otherwise, clearing the finitely many denominators in an expression for an element a∈J∩(A∖p1​) gives
sa=∑i=1h​ai​bi​,s∈B∖q2​,ai​∈p1​,bi​∈B.
(43)
The subalgebra B0​=A[s,b1​,…,bh​] is a module-finite ring extension of A, since all its generators are integral elements. Apply the finite case to q2​∩B0​: there is a prime Q1​⊆q2​∩B0​ contracting to p1​. The displayed identity puts sa in Q1​, but a∈/Q1​. Thus s∈Q1​⊆q2​, a contradiction.
Localize Bq2​​ further at the multiplicative subset A∖p1​. The extension of J is a proper ideal, by the disjointness just proved. Choose a maximal ideal containing it and contract back to a prime ideal of Bq2​​. This prime contains J and avoids A∖p1​. Its contraction q1​ to B lies inside q2​ by the prime ideal correspondence for localization, and its contraction to A is exactly p1​. This completes the proof of the going-down theorem without any finite generation assumption on B.
