Write the period lattice as Λ=Zω1​+Zω2​, with ω1​,ω2​ real-linearly independent and Im(ω2​/ω1​)>0. A meromorphic function f is an elliptic function for this lattice if f(z+λ)=f(z) for every λ∈Λ. If it has no poles, it is an entire function bounded on the closure of a fundamental parallelogram. Periodicity makes it bounded on all of C, so the Liouville theorem proves that an elliptic function without poles is constant.
Here are the two contour identities needed for counting its zeros and their sum. Choose a positively oriented fundamental parallelogram P whose boundary avoids all zeros and poles, and put h=f′/f. The argument principle gives
N−Pf​=2πi1​∫∂P​h(z)dz=0,
(1)
because h is periodic and opposite edges cancel. Here N,Pf​ count zeros and poles with multiplicity. For the weighted integral, let Ji​ be the integral of h along the edge from a vertex c to c+ωi​. Translating the opposite edges gives
2πi1​∫∂P​zh(z)dz=2πiω1​J2​−ω2​J1​​.
(2)
The endpoint values of f agree along either edge. A continuous logarithm along the edge therefore changes by 2πini​, with ni​∈Z, and Ji​=2πini​. On the other hand, the residue theorem evaluates the left side as the sum of the zero positions minus the sum of the pole positions, both with multiplicity. Thus the elliptic divisor-sum identity is
∑j​rj​zj​−∑l​ml​pl​=ω1​n2​−ω2​n1​∈Λ.
(3)
Under the stated pole hypothesis there is just one pole class, represented by zero, of order m. Consequently
N=m,j∑​rj​zj​≡0(modΛ).​
(4)
Changing representatives of the zero classes changes their weighted sum by an element of Λ, so the congruence is well defined.
The Weierstrass elliptic function is
℘Λ​(z)=z21​+∑0=ω∈Λ​((z−ω)21​−ω21​).
(5)
On a bounded set of z, the summand is O(∣ω∣−3) for large ∣ω∣. The lattice sum of ∣ω∣−3 converges in real dimension two, proving Normal convergence of the Weierstrass elliptic-function series on compact sets away from Λ. Hence ℘ is a holomorphic function there, and at zero its principal part is z−2, so it has a double pole. Replacing ω by −ω shows that ℘ is even. Differentiating the normally convergent series gives
℘′(z)=−2∑ω∈Λ​(z−ω)−3,
(6)
whose absolute convergence permits reindexing by any λ∈Λ. Thus ℘′ is periodic and ℘(z+λ)−℘(z) is constant. Substituting −z−λ for z and using evenness changes that constant to its negative, so it is zero. This proves that ℘ is an elliptic function, with double poles precisely at the lattice points.
The preceding zero count applied to ℘−a says that it has exactly two zeros modulo Λ, counted with multiplicity. Evenness pairs any zero z with −z. If these are distinct classes they must both be simple. If they coincide, 2z∈Λ, and locally ℘(z+t)=℘(z−t): the zero has even order, which the total count forces to be two. In particular such a zero cannot be a lattice point, where ℘ has a pole. Every finite fibre of the Weierstrass function consists of two opposite simple points or one double nonzero half-period point.
To obtain the rational representation, first consider an even elliptic function g. The preceding description proves that ℘:C/Λ→P1 has exactly the fibres {z,−z}, so g defines a function A of w=℘(z). Away from the branch values a local inverse of ℘ makes A meromorphic. At a half-period z0​, the local expansion is w−w0​=ct2+O(t4), with c=0 because the zero has order exactly two. The Laurent series of g(z0​+t) contains only even powers, and t2 is a holomorphic local coordinate as a function of w−w0​. Thus A is meromorphic at that value too. At zero the same argument uses 1/w=t2+O(t6), proving meromorphy at infinity. A meromorphic function on the Riemann sphere is a rational function: subtract its finitely many principal parts and use compactness to make the remainder constant. This proves that even elliptic functions are rational in the Weierstrass function.
For an arbitrary elliptic function, split f=f+​+f−​, where f±​(z)=(f(z)±f(−z))/2. The even part is A(℘). Since ℘′ is odd and not identically zero, f−​/℘′ is an even meromorphic elliptic function, including at the zeros of ℘′ where the quotient may have poles. It is therefore B(℘) for a rational function B. The elliptic function-field decomposition is
f(z)=A(℘(z))+B(℘(z))℘′(z),A,B∈C(w).​
(7)
The representation is unique: its even and odd parts determine A and B, and ℘ takes every value on the Riemann sphere.

A weight-k modular form for Γ=SL2​(Z) is a holomorphic function f on the complex upper half-plane satisfying
f(cτ+daτ+b​)=(cτ+d)kf(τ)((ac​bd​)∈Γ),
(8)
and holomorphic at a cusp. Since the full modular group has one cusp class, this last condition means that the period-one function has a convergent expansion f(τ)=∑n≥0​an​qn, q=e2πiτ, near infinity. A cusp form has a0​=0. The element −I makes a nonzero weight odd form impossible.
For weight zero, f descends to a holomorphic function on the quotient of the complex upper half-plane by the modular group. At an elliptic fixed point, invariance under its finite stabilizer makes the local Taylor series a series in the quotient coordinate. At the cusp, the q expansion extends it over q=0. The standard fundamental domain of the modular group, with its boundary identified and its cusp added, is a compact Riemann surface, the compactified modular curve X(1). The maximum modulus principle now proves
M0​(SL2​(Z))=C.​
(9)
Holomorphy at the cusp is essential to this conclusion.
For positive even k≥4, define the unnormalized Eisenstein series
Gk​(τ)=∑(m,n)∈Z2∖{(0,0)}​(mτ+n)−k.
(10)
The real-linear map (m,n)↦mτ+n is invertible, and on a compact subset of the complex upper half-plane its norm is uniformly comparable to m2+n2​. Thus the sum converges absolutely and locally uniformly for k>2, proving holomorphy. For γ=(ac​bd​),
mγτ+n=cτ+d(ma+nc)τ+(mb+nd)​.
(11)
The map (m,n)↦(ma+nc,mb+nd) is a bijection on the nonzero integer pairs, giving Gk​(γτ)=(cτ+d)kGk​(τ).
For the expansion, the cotangent partial-fraction Fourier kernel follows by differentiating the partial-fraction expansion of πcotπz and its geometric-series expansion −πi(1+2∑r≥1​e2πirz), valid for Imz>0. It gives, for even k≥2,
∑n∈Z​(z+n)−k=(k−1)!(2πi)k​∑r≥1​rk−1e2πirz.
(12)
The m=0 part of Gk​ is 2ζ(k), where ζ is the Riemann zeta function; positive and negative m contribute equally. Apply the kernel at z=mτ for m>0, and regroup the absolutely convergent sum according to N=mr. With the divisor sum σk−1​(N)=∑r∣N​rk−1, this proves
Gk​(τ)=2ζ(k)+(k−1)!2(2πi)k​N≥1∑​σk−1​(N)qN.​
(13)
The expansion has no negative powers and converges for ∣q∣<1, so it also proves holomorphy at a cusp and completes the proof of modularity.
The supplied zeta values yield the Fourier expansion of a normalized Eisenstein series
E4​=1+240S3​,E6​=1−504S5​,Sj​=∑n≥1​σj​(n)qn.
(14)
Both E43​ and E62​ are modular forms of weight twelve. Their constant terms cancel, so their difference divided by 1728 is a cusp form. Integrality requires an argument: dividing an integral series by 1728 alone would not suffice. Expanding directly gives the integrality identity for the modular discriminant
Δ=125S3​+7S5​​+100S32​+8000S33​−147S52​.
(15)
For every integer d, 5d3+7d5 is divisible by twelve. Modulo three, d3≡d5≡d; modulo four, an even d makes both powers divisible by four, while an odd d has d3≡d5≡d(mod4). Thus each coefficient (5σ3​(n)+7σ5​(n))/12 is integral. The other three series visibly have integral coefficients, proving
Δ∈S12​(SL2​(Z)),Δ∈qZ[[q]],a1​(Δ)=1.​
(16)
This proves integrality without assuming a product expansion for the modular discriminant.

Hecke operators give a commuting family of arithmetic symmetries of modular forms. They preserve the weight and cusp forms, and diagonalizing them turns the Fourier coefficients of a modular form into multiplicative arithmetic data. Odd weight spaces are zero by the action of −I; in weight zero the operators act on constants by Tn​1=σ−1​(n). For the substantive theory, fix an even positive weight k and Γ=SL2​(Z), and use the following normalization consistently.
Extend the slash operator for modular forms to GL2+​(Q) by
(f∣k​α)(τ)=(detα)k/2(cτ+d)−kf(ατ),α=(ac​bd​).
(17)
It is a right action and positive scalar matrices act trivially. Let Dn​ be the integral matrices of determinant n>0. The determinant-n matrix representatives for Hecke operators for Γ\Dn​ are
(a0​bd​),ad=n,a,d>0,0≤b<d.
(18)
Integer row operations reduce the first column to (a,0), after which a row shear reduces b modulo d; uniqueness follows from these same conditions. The Hecke operator is
Tn​f=nk/2−1α∈Γ\Dn​∑​f∣k​α=nk−1ad=n∑​d−kb=0∑d−1​f(daτ+b​).​
(19)
Right multiplication by Γ permutes the left cosets, so Tn​f is again invariant under the weight-k slash operator for modular forms. Each summand is a holomorphic function on the complex upper half-plane, and the coefficient formula below proves holomorphy at a cusp. Thus Tn​ acts on Mk​(Γ) and preserves Sk​(Γ). Geometrically, these operators sum over index-n sublattices, or equivalently the associated finite-degree correspondences between complex tori; the factors above compensate for the weight's response to lattice scaling.
Write f=∑r≥0​ar​qr. The sum over b is zero unless d∣r, in which case it is d. Substituting into the displayed definition proves the Fourier coefficients of a composite-index Hecke operator formula
ar​(Tn​f)=∑d∣gcd(n,r)​dk−1anr/d2​(f),r≥0,
(20)
with gcd(n,0)=n. In particular, a0​(Tn​f)=σk−1​(n)a0​(f) and a1​(Tn​f)=an​(f). At a prime this becomes
Tp​f=pk−1f(pτ)+p1​∑b=0p−1​f(pτ+b​),ar​(Tp​f)=apr​+pk−1ar/p​,
(21)
where ar/p​=0 if p∤r.
The Hecke multiplication relations are
Tm​Tn​=d∣gcd(m,n)∑​dk−1Tmn/d2​.​
(22)
They can be checked by applying the coefficient formula twice and regrouping the divisors. More explicitly, coprime indices factor independently, so Tm​Tn​=Tmn​ if (m,n)=1. At a prime, separating the terms in the divisor sum according to whether one more factor p is available gives
Tp​Tpr​=Tpr+1​+pk−1Tpr−1​,r≥1.
(23)
Iteration gives Tpa​Tpb​=∑j=0min(a,b)​pj(k−1)Tpa+b−2j​; combining the prime factors gives the full relation. Hence all Hecke operators commute, T1​=1, and the Hecke algebra of modular forms is generated by the Tp​ together with scalars.
The Petersson inner product on the finite-dimensional cusp form space is
⟨f,g⟩=∫Γ\H​f(τ)g(τ)​,yky2dxdy​.
(24)
The integrand is invariant and the integral converges because cusp forms decay exponentially at the cusp. The Hecke operators are self-adjoint for the Petersson inner product at level one. To see the underlying adjoint calculation, unfold the finite correspondence for a determinant-n matrix and change variable τ↦ατ. The invariant hyperbolic measure and the determinant-normalized slash factors transfer α from one side of the inner product to α−1. Scalar matrices act trivially, so one can replace α−1 by the integral adjugate nα−1, which again has determinant n. This reverses the correspondence and permutes the same collection of terms, with the same real prefactor nk/2−1. Summing gives
⟨Tn​f,g⟩=⟨f,Tn​g⟩.
(25)
Commuting self-adjoint operators on a finite-dimensional inner product space admit a common orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Thus Sk​(Γ) has a basis of simultaneous Hecke eigenforms, with real eigenvalues.
A nonzero simultaneous cuspidal Hecke eigenform has a1​=0: if Tn​f=λn​f and a1​=0, comparison of the first coefficients gives an​=λn​a1​=0 for every n≥1, so f=0. Normalize a1​=1. Then
Tn​f=an​(f)f.​
(26)
Consequently the full Fourier series is determined by the eigenvalues, and a simultaneous eigenvalue system has a one-dimensional eigenspace. The Hecke multiplication relations translate into
am​an​=∑d∣gcd(m,n)​dk−1amn/d2​,apr+1​=ap​apr​−pk−1apr−1​.
(27)
In particular the coefficients are multiplicative at coprime indices and are determined by the prime coefficients. This is the elementary multiplicity-one statement for normalized level-one Hecke eigenforms.
The Hecke eigenvalues are algebraic integers. One way to explain the arithmetic input is to use the rational structure supplied by the graded ring M∗​(Γ,Q)=Q[E4​,E6​]. The rational cusp form space has a basis with integral Fourier coefficients, for example from appropriate monomials in E4​,E6​,Δ. Its subgroup of forms whose entire expansions are integral is a full lattice: a finite initial coefficient map is injective by the valence formula for the modular group, so it embeds this subgroup into a finite-rank integer module. The coefficient formula shows that every Tn​ preserves this lattice, and therefore has a monic integral characteristic polynomial. Its eigenvalues are algebraic integers. Simultaneous diagonalization also makes their number field finite over Q; conjugating the rational coefficient data gives another eigenform, whose eigenvalues are again real by self-adjointness. Thus the coefficient field is totally real.
For a normalized cuspidal Hecke eigenform, define its L-function of a cusp form by L(f,s)=∑n≥1​an​n−s. The coefficient relations yield the Euler product of a Hecke eigenform
L(f,s)=p∏​(1−ap​p−s+pk−1−2s)−1.​
(28)
Indeed the prime-power recurrence gives ∑r≥0​apr​Xr=(1−ap​X+pk−1X2)−1, and multiplicativity assembles these factors. These identities hold analytically in an initial right half-plane, as well as formally. For instance boundedness of yk/2∣f(τ)∣ on the full complex upper half-plane gives an​=O(nk/2), so Res>k/2+1 suffices for absolute convergence.
The Mellin transform of a cusp-form L-function relates this arithmetic to the modular transformation:
Λ(f,s)=(2π)−sΓ(s)L(f,s)=∫0∞​f(iy)ys−1dy.
(29)
Splitting at one and using f(i/y)=ikykf(iy) gives
Λ(f,s)=∫1∞​f(iy)(ys−1+ikyk−s−1)dy,Λ(f,s)=ikΛ(f,k−s).​
(30)
Exponential cusp decay makes the last integral entire in s. This proves the analytic continuation and functional equation of the L-function of a cusp form, complementing its Euler product. The root number is ik=(−1)k/2.
Two examples show the scope of the theory. For even k≥4, the Eisenstein series Ek​ is an eigenform with Tn​Ek​=σk−1​(n)Ek​. This follows from its divisor-sum expansion and the same multiplicativity relations; its associated positive-index Dirichlet series is proportional to ζ(s)ζ(s−k+1). The constant coefficient gives Mk​(Γ)=CEk​⊕Sk​(Γ), so an eigenbasis of the cusp space together with Ek​ gives an eigenbasis of the whole modular-form space. The ordinary Petersson inner product above is only used on the cusp space, where it converges. In weight twelve the valence formula for the modular group gives dimS12​=1: cancellation of the first coefficients of two independent cusp forms would give a nonzero form with order at infinity at least two, exceeding 12/12. Thus the normalized modular discriminant is a Hecke eigenform; writing Δ=∑n≥1​τ(n)qn gives the Ramanujan tau function relations τ(mn)=τ(m)τ(n) for coprime m,n and τ(pr+1)=τ(p)τ(pr)−p11τ(pr−1).

For a full-rank Euclidean lattice Λ⊂Rn, its dual lattice is
Λ′={u∈Rn:⟨u,λ⟩∈Z for all λ∈Λ}.
(31)
If Λ=BZn, then Λ′=B−TZn, m(Λ)=∣detB∣ and m(Λ′)=m(Λ)−1. The characters of a real torus identify the additive dual lattice with the multiplicative character group by
u⟼χu​,χu​(x+Λ)=e2πi⟨u,x⟩.​
(32)
The map is well defined precisely because u∈Λ′, and is injective. For surjectivity, a continuous group homomorphism from the compact torus into C× has compact image. Its modulus has logarithm a homomorphism into (R,+) with compact image, hence is zero, so the image lies in the unit circle. Pull the character back to Rn. Its continuous real lift under t↦e2πit, normalized to zero at the origin, is additive: its additive defect is an integer-valued continuous function and vanishes at the origin. A continuous additive function is ⟨u,x⟩ for a unique u. Triviality on Λ says u∈Λ′, proving surjectivity.
Use the Fourier transform convention f​(u)=∫Rn​f(x)e−2πi⟨u,x⟩dx, and take f to be a Schwartz function. The periodization of a Schwartz function Pf​(x)=∑λ∈Λ​f(x+λ) is smooth and Λ-periodic. On a fundamental cell F, the coefficient of the torus character χu​ is
m(Λ)1​∫F​Pf​(x)e−2πi⟨u,x⟩dx=m(Λ)1​f​(u),u∈Λ′.
(33)
The equality follows by translating each cell and using e2πi⟨u,λ⟩=1. The rapidly convergent Fourier series can be evaluated at zero, yielding the Poisson summation formula for a Euclidean lattice
λ∈Λ∑​f(λ)=m(Λ)1​u∈Λ′∑​f​(u).​
(34)
This argument keeps track of the covolume factor rather than tacitly assuming a unit-volume lattice.
For τ in the complex upper half-plane, let gτ​(x)=eπiτ∣x∣2. Scaling the self-dual real Gaussian function gives its Fourier transform at τ=it, t>0, and holomorphic continuation in τ gives the complex Gaussian Fourier transform
g​τ​(u)=(τ/i)−n/2exp(−τπi∣u∣2​).
(35)
The branch is exp[−(n/2)Log(−iτ)] with the logarithm on the right half-plane; it is positive for τ=it. Both the integrals and the lattice sums are locally normally convergent on the complex upper half-plane. Applying the Poisson summation formula proves the lattice theta functional equation
ΘΛ​(τ)=(τ/i)−n/2m(Λ)−1ΘΛ′​(−1/τ).​
(36)
No integrality or self-duality hypothesis on the lattice is needed.
Put a=n/2, m=m(Λ) and θΛ​(t)=ΘΛ​(it). The Epstein zeta function converges absolutely for Res>a, and its Mellin transform representation is
EΛ​(s)=π−sΓ(s)EΛ​(s)=∫0∞​(θΛ​(t)−1)ts−1dt.
(37)
At infinity, θΛ​(t)−1 decays exponentially. Define the entire function
AΛ​(s)=∫1∞​(θΛ​(t)−1)ts−1dt.
(38)
On (0,1), substitute θΛ​(t)=m−1t−aθΛ′​(1/t) and then u=1/t. Isolate the two elementary terms before integrating; this gives the pole-subtracted theta integral for an Epstein zeta function
EΛ​(s)=AΛ​(s)+m−1AΛ′​(a−s)+s−am−1​−s1​.​
(39)
The formula initially holds for Res>a and continues the completed Epstein zeta function meromorphically to all C. Apply the same formula to the dual lattice at a−s, using m(Λ′)=m−1 and Λ′′=Λ. The entire terms and the two rational terms match, proving
EΛ​(s)=m(Λ)−1EΛ′​(n/2−s).​
(40)
Finally EΛ​(s)=πsEΛ​(s)/Γ(s) also continues meromorphically. Its only pole is a simple one at s=n/2, with residue πn/2/[m(Λ)Γ(n/2)]; the pole of the completed function at zero is cancelled by 1/Γ(s), and EΛ​(0)=−1. The residues and this cancellation make explicit why subtracting the constant theta term was necessary.

Work initially with Res>1, where the nonholomorphic Eisenstein series and its differentiated series converge locally absolutely. For γ=(ac​bd​)∈SL2​(Z), the identities
Im(γτ)=∣cτ+d∣2y​,mγτ+n=cτ+d(ma+nc)τ+(mb+nd)​

show that each summand at γτ becomes the summand indexed by (ma+nc,mb+nd) at τ. Reindexing the nonzero integer pairs therefore proves
G(γτ,s)=G(τ,s).​
(42)

For the nonnegative Laplace-Beltrami operator of the hyperbolic plane, Δys=−y2∂y2​ys=s(1−s)ys. If (m,n)=r(c,d) with (c,d) primitive and r>0, choose a matrix in SL2​(Z) with bottom row (c,d). The corresponding summand is
