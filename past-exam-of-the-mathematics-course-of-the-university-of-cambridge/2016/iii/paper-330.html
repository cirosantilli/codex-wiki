The WKB approximation treats the vertical wavenumber as locally constant over one vertical wavelength. Write w=ℜ{A(z)eikx+i∫zm(s)ds}, with ∣m′∣≪m2 and ∣A′∣≪∣mA∣. For the stationary internal-wave WKB solution, A∝m−1/2 away from singular or turning levels. A small terrain slope and small perturbations are also needed for Linearized Boussinesq equations.
Let p be the perturbation fluid pressure divided by reference mass density, and b the buoyancy perturbation. The steady Linearized Boussinesq equations and incompressibility condition are
Uux​+U′w=−px​,Uwx​=−pz​+b,Ubx​+N2w=0,ux​+wz​=0.
(1)
For an eikx component, elimination gives u=−w′/(ik), b=−N2w/(ikU) and p=(Uw′−U′w)/(ik). Substitution into vertical momentum gives the exact stationary Taylor–Goldstein equation
w′′+(U2N2​−UU′′​−k2)w=0.
(2)
The leading WKB approximation therefore identifies
m2=U2N2​−UU′′​−k2=(k2U2N2​−k2UU′′​−1)k2.​
(3)
The kinematic boundary condition fixes the horizontal wavenumber to k=kT​ for the sinusoidal forcing.
For explicit heights assume a constant positive buoyancy frequency N, 0<kU0​<N, and ∣S∣/N≪1. The latter is a large gradient Richardson number. The linear shear flow has U′′=0, so the propagation condition is ∣U∣<N/k.
For S<0, the first obstruction is the critical level of an internal gravity wave at
zc​=∣S∣U0​​.​
(4)
As z↑zc​, m∼N/U→∞. The critical-height approach of a stationary wave in linear shear has upward group velocity cgz​∼kU2/N, so the travel time to zc​ diverges: the height is a supremum, approached in the ideal ray model. Here ∣m′∣/m2→∣S∣/N, which can remain small; it would be incorrect to say that the short-wavelength WKB approximation necessarily fails solely because m diverges. Nevertheless w∝U1/2 and displacement w/(ikU)∝U−1/2, so a finite disturbance eventually violates linearity. Dissipation and overturning also matter near this singular level.
For S>0, the turning height of a stationary wave in linear shear is
zt​=SN/k−U0​​.​
(5)
At zt​, m=0 and the WKB approximation fails. An Airy function matching region describes reflection and an upper evanescent wave. Thus zt​ is the highest height of vertical propagation, rather than a sharp cut-off of every disturbance.
The problem initially allows N(z) to vary. Without the extra constant-N assumption, replace the explicit turning height by the first root of N(z)=k∣U0​+Sz∣ encountered on a propagating branch. A declining N(z) can cause a turning level before the negative-shear critical level. The two closed-form heights require the stated constant-stratification assumption.

Take a common constant buoyancy frequency N and put k=kT​>0. Define η to be vertical parcel displacement; the kinematic boundary condition is w=ikUj​ηj​. In either uniform region the exact Taylor–Goldstein equation gives
ηj′′​+mj2​ηj​=0,mj2​=Uj2​N2​−k2.
(6)
No WKB approximation is needed. If the earlier N(z) is genuinely nonconstant, the exact regional equation is instead ηj′′​+[N(z)2/Uj2​−k2]ηj​=0; its fundamental solutions, not plane exponentials, must be used. For example, let C1​(0)=1, C1′​(0)=0, S1​(0)=0, S1′​(0)=1, and let f2​ be an outgoing upper solution. Then η1​=h0​C1​+DS1​, η2​=Af2​, with D,A determined by h0​C1​(H)+DS1​(H)=Af2​(H) and U12​[h0​C1′​(H)+DS1′​(H)]=U22​Af2′​(H). This gives the exact arbitrary-profile field implicitly, while a simple angle-only coefficient requires further stratification assumptions.
Under the common constant-N assumption, propagating incident internal gravity waves require
0<U1​<kN​.​
(7)
For co-directed upper flow, the outgoing propagating branch exists for 0<U2​<N/k and has m2​>0. The radiation condition chooses mj​>0 because the laboratory vertical group velocity is positive on the negative intrinsic frequency branch. Define tanθj​=mj​/k, so cosθj​=kUj​/N.
At the velocity jump, the material-interface jump conditions for stratified inviscid shear flow require continuity of displacement and of fluid pressure. Vertical velocity itself is generally discontinuous: wj​=ikUj​η. In terms of displacement the pressure is pj​=Uj2​ηj′​. The displacement impedance for an internal wave at a velocity jump is Zj​=Uj2​mj​. An upward incident component of complex displacement amplitude I at H, a reflected component RI and a transmitted component TI therefore obey
1+R=T,Z1​(1−R)=Z2​T.
(8)
Consequently
R=Z1​+Z2​Z1​−Z2​​,T=Z1​+Z2​2Z1​​.​
(9)
For a one-pass incident displacement h0​, a real wave field is the imaginary part of
η1​(x,z)η2​(x,z)​=h0​eikx[eim1​z+Reim1​(2H−z)],=h0​Tei[kx+m1​H+m2​(z−H)],​​0<z<H,z>H.​
(10)
The incident vertical-displacement amplitude is h0​, the reflected amplitude is ∣R∣h0​ and the transmitted amplitude is ∣T∣h0​; the sign of R encodes a phase reversal. The other fields follow from w=ikUη, u=−w′/(ik), b=−N2η and p=U2η′. If a perfectly reflecting terrain boundary is enforced after the return wave arrives, replace the incident coefficient h0​ by h0​/[1+Re2im1​H]. The one-pass amplitudes use the permitted neglect of repeated terrain reflections.
For common N, Zj​=(N2/k)cosθj​sinθj​. The physical vertical-displacement transmission coefficient is therefore
h0​η2,vertical​​=cosθ1​sinθ1​+cosθ2​sinθ2​2cosθ1​sinθ1​​=sin(θ1​+θ2​)cos(θ1​−θ2​)2cosθ1​sinθ1​​.​
(11)
The printed coefficient has a different denominator and cannot be obtained for this standard displacement convention. For example, when U2​=U1​, there is no interface scattering and the vertical amplitude stays h0​, whereas the printed expression gives h0​/cosθ1​. A total displacement magnitude would instead be the vertical amplitude divided by cosθ2​; with unequal velocities it still does not give the printed expression. The transmission formula needs an explicit correction or a different, presently unspecified amplitude/interface model.
For U2​>N/k, choose m2​=iκ with κ=(k2−N2/U22​)1/2: the transmitted response decays as e−κ(z−H) and the same impedance equations give a complex R,T, with ∣R∣=1. Oppositely directed upper flow formally supports propagating waves when 0<∣U2​∣<N/k, but upward radiation then requires m2​<0 and signed Z2​; the positive-angle co-directed formula is not valid there. A real velocity jump can also have Kelvin-Helmholtz instability, which is excluded from this formal steady scattering calculation.

Consider the permanent stationary topographic internal gravity wave component of a localized source, with constant buoyancy frequency in each layer and positive U1​,U2​. A sufficiently broad obstacle spectrum is assumed. The dispersion relation on the upward branch in a uniform layer is
ω=kU−(k2+m2)1/2Nk​.
(12)
For a stationary wave, K=(k2+m2)1/2=N/U. Differentiating before imposing stationarity gives the laboratory group velocity
(cgx​,cgz​)=U(cos2θ,sinθcosθ),cosθ=k/K.
(13)
The vertical component is at most U/2, attained at θ=π/4. The first arrival of a stationary internal wavefront at a layer boundary is thus
T=U1​2H​,x(T)=H.​
(14)
If the obstacle spectrum lacks that wavenumber, the first arrival of its actual permanent wave packets is later. This statement concerns the stationary part; arbitrary long-wave startup transients are not bounded by this particular envelope.
In the lower layer an elapsed propagation time t gives x=U1​tcos2θ1​, z=U1​tsinθ1​cosθ1​. Elimination gives the causal envelope of stationary internal waves
(x−2U1​t​)2+z2=(2U1​t​)2,z≥0.​
(15)
Later-emitted packets fill the interior. At t=T this semicircle just touches H at x=H. At 2T its unobstructed continuation would reach 2H, but rays that hit the interface must instead be refracted or reflected.
For a lower-layer ray labelled by θ1​, its interface arrival time and point are
τ1​=U1​sinθ1​cosθ1​H​,xH​=Hcotθ1​.
(16)
The refraction of a stationary internal-wave causal envelope conserves horizontal wavenumber, so cosθ2​=(U2​N1​)/(U1​N2​)cosθ1​. If τ1​≤t and 0<cosθ2​<1, its transmitted front is parametrized by
x=xH​+(t−τ1​)U2​cos2θ2​,z=H+(t−τ1​)U2​sinθ2​cosθ2​.​
(17)
The reflected front instead has x=U1​tcos2θ1​ and z=2H−U1​tsinθ1​cosθ1​, until it reaches the ground. At 2T that first return just touches the ground. If the π/4 incident ray is evanescent upstairs, upper-layer propagating arrival is delayed until an allowed ray reaches H; this does not change the first lower-layer arrival time T.
The stationary phase of topographic internal waves also gives the phase sketch. In a uniform lower layer the Fourier transform integrand has phase kx+m(k)z. The stationary phase method requires x=kz/m, giving phase Φ=(N1​/U1​)(x2+z2)1/2. Thus the dominant permanent phase lines are downstream circular arcs centred on the obstacle. Reflected phase arcs have the image source (0,2H). Upstairs the ray arriving at (xH​,H) has phase
Φ=kxH​+m1​H+k(x−xH​)+m2​(z−H),
(18)
which is continuous at the interface and determines refracted constant-phase curves. Individual monochromatic components have straight phase lines of slope −k/mj​ instead; the arcs describe their localized-source superposition.
The schematic uses H=U1​=N=1 and U2​=U1​/2. It shows the direct front below H, the transmitted front above it and the reflected front below it; dotted arcs are permanent phase curves, not material trajectories.

Use the steady Boussinesq top-hat plume in a stratified ambient, with negligible ambient vertical momentum and no externally added heat or particles. The Batchelor entrainment hypothesis supplies inward velocity αw across circumference 2πb. Volume conservation gives πQz​=2πbαw, and bw=M​, hence
Qz​=2αM​.​
(19)
The momentum flux increases by the integrated buoyancy force. Writing g′=gΔρ/ρo​, the force per reference density and per π is g′b2. Since F=g′Q and b2=Q2/M, this gives
Mz​=g′b2=MFQ​.​
(20)
For the buoyancy flux, entrained material has the local environmental mass density. Let ρp​ be the plume's averaged density. Its scalar mass conservation is (ρp​Q)z​=ρa​Qz​. Since F=(g/ρo​)(ρa​−ρp​)Q, differentiating cancels both entrainment terms and gives
Fz​=ρo​g​[(ρa​Q)z​−(ρp​Q)z​]=Qρo​g​ρa′​.​
(21)
In stable density stratification, ρa′​<0 and a rising plume loses buoyancy flux. These are kinematic fluxes with the common factor πρo​ removed, so no additional factor of π belongs in the three differential equations.

M1/2=1+21​ϵM′+O(ϵ2),MFQ​​=1+ϵ(F′+Q′−M′)+O(ϵ2).

dξd​​Q′M′F′​​=J​Q′M′F′​​,J=​−qmf​q/2−2m0​0m−f​​.​
(30)
In particular, the second row contains −2mM′, since the reciprocal of M contributes another −M′.
For the growing mode of power-law plume similarity, the characteristic polynomial is
P(λ)=λ3+(q+2m+f)λ2+(23​qm+qf+2mf)λ+qmf.
(31)
Within the physical interval q,m>0 and f<0, P(0)<0 while P(λ)→+∞ as λ→+∞. There is a positive real eigenvalue, so the similarity equilibrium is unstable with increasing height. Perturbations behave as eλξ∝zλ; the result is not stability in physical time. Thus this exact positive-buoyancy similarity trajectory is not a generic attracting far field.
At the upper endpoint,
β→−38​:f→0−,q→35​,m→34​.​
(32)
The limiting polynomial is λ(λ+1)(λ+10/3), reproducing the three stated eigenvalues. The positive eigenvalue tends to zero, with λ∼−2f/3. The two negative modes decay as z−1 and z−10/3; the remaining mode is neutral at the limiting linear system. Its eigenvector is (1,2,3) in relative-flux coordinates.
The neutral buoyancy-flux mode of a pure plume has a simple interpretation: a constant surviving buoyancy flux fixes the amplitudes of a pure plume, with Q∝F1/3z5/3 and M∝F2/3z4/3. A small fractional change in F produces changes in Q,M in the ratio 1:2:3. It is retained rather than damped out.
There is a qualification at the exact endpoint. For fixed nonzero K and β=−8/3, taking Q∝z5/3 gives Fz​∝−z−1, not zero. The flux coefficients above also diverge as f→0. Thus the constant-flux powers are the formal limiting scaling, not an exact constant-buoyancy solution in a nonzero endpoint stratification; cumulative ambient effects can require logarithmic corrections.

For identical suspended particles, reduced gravity is proportional to particle volume fraction. If particles settle through the bed at speed vs​ and are not resuspended, the downward particle deposition flux removes a buoyancy-equivalent amount vs​g′ per unit bed area. The depth-integrated scalar mass conservation equation is
∂t​(g′h)+∂x​B=−vs​g′,B=ug′h.​
(37)
For a steady particle-laden gravity current, Bx​=−vs​B/(uh). If vs​/u≪1, with fixed positive E and drag/slope coefficients, the locally adjusted leading solution of the momentum and volume equations is u∼β∗​B1/3 and h∼h0​+E(x−x0​). The weak-settling attenuation of a sloping gravity current then gives
dxd​B1/3∼−3β∗​[h0​+E(x−x0​)]vs​​,
(38)
and hence
B(x)∼[Bo1/3​−3β∗​Evs​​ln(1+h0​E(x−x0​)​)]3.​
(39)
For an inlet at x0​=0 this has the requested logarithmic form. If the inlet is genuinely at x0​=0, the argument must contain x−x0​ in order to satisfy the specified inlet condition.
The speed factor must be the β∗​ derived in the previous part. The printed β omits the factor one-half multiplying Ecosθ and uses an undefined uppercase C instead of the governing equation's c. Taking C=c still leaves a nonzero leading momentum residual −21​E(B/u)cosθ for the printed speed; this discrepancy cannot be repaired by a correction of order vs​/u. With the given hydrostatic momentum equation, the logarithmic law is valid with β∗​, not the printed speed factor.
A controlled local approximation also needs vs​/(Eu)≪1 if E is allowed to become small. Indeed the exact volume balance is hx​=E−hux​/u; under u∼β∗​B1/3 its second term is vs​/(3u). The neglected depth and acceleration corrections become significant as B approaches zero.

For a steady no-source particle-laden gravity current, Bx​=−vs​g′<0. Simply entraining particle-free ambient fluid increases volume and dilutes particle volume fraction; it cannot create particle buoyancy flux.
A steady increase can occur if sediment resuspension or bed erosion adds more suspended particle buoyancy than settling removes, if a tributary or lateral particle source supplies a positive flux, or if the buoyancy per unit particle amount increases through a change of the surrounding fluid's density. Changes of particle properties or another buoyancy source can also alter the flux, but they go beyond the fixed-particle, fixed-reference model. In a model with a source SB​,
∂t​(g′h)+Bx​=SB​−vs​g′.
(41)
Thus the buoyancy production in a particle-laden current requires SB​>vs​g′ in a steady current. In an unsteady current, Bx​ can be positive even without external addition: local depletion has ∂t​(g′h)<−vs​g′, so more buoyancy leaves a section than enters it while stored particles are being released. An instantaneous spatial flux increase is not by itself proof of particle production.

The two bounding straight shocks initially have
V1​=−us​(1−a),V2​=us​b=2us​a​.​
(53)
The upper front's nominal meeting with the fan's upper edge occurs at
tc1​=us​aL​=5us​aH​,hc1​=H−a(1−a)L​=5aH(6a−1)​.​
(54)
The lower front meets the fan's lower edge at
tc2​=us​(1−b)zm​​=5us​(1−a/2)4H​,hc2​=1−bbzm​​=5(2−a)4aH​.​
(55)
The lower meeting is always physical in the stated range. The upper meeting need not happen before the suspension disappears; this distinction matters for sufficiently small a.
For the curved settling fronts within a rarefaction fan, the Rankine-Hugoniot condition gives z˙u​=us​(ϕ−1) at the clear-fluid front and z˙d​=us​ϕ at the deposit front. Substitution of the fan particle volume fraction and matching to the straight sections gives
zu​=zm​−us​t+2us​aLt​,zd​=zm​+us​t−2us​Dt​.​
(56)
The first curve applies after tc1​ if that meeting occurs, and the second after tc2​ until it leaves the fan or deposition ends. If both fronts meet inside the fan, their intersection is
tc​=us​(aL​+D​)2​,hc​=aL+bzm​=53​aH.​
(57)
The last surviving characteristic of a settling fan joins (zm​,0) to (hc​,tc​). It has constant particle volume fraction
ϕ∗​=aL​+D​aL​​,tc​hc​−zm​​=us​(2ϕ∗​−1).​
(58)
It is swallowed simultaneously by the clear-fluid and deposit shocks at final deposition. This requires b≤ϕ∗​≤a, which in the given geometry is equivalent to
a≥a∗​=1−32​​.​
(59)
Equality places the last characteristic curve on the upper fan edge. In the generic fan-intersection regime a>a∗​, both nominal meetings and the two curved fronts are realized.
For 0<a<a∗​ there is early extinction of a settling rarefaction fan. The deposit shock reaches the upper fan edge before the clear-fluid front does, at
te​=us​(1−a)2D​,ze​=zm​+us​(2a−1)te​.
(60)
It then travels through the remaining uniform particle volume fraction a with speed us​a. The upper front retains V1​ until the final intersection. Since the final bed height is still hc​=3aH/5, the actual stopping time is
tc​=us​(1−a)H−hc​​=us​(1−a)H(1−3a/5)​,0<a<a∗​.​
(61)
In this regime (hc1​,tc1​) is only an extrapolated intersection; for a<1/6 its extrapolated height is even negative. The line joining the initial internal interface to the final point would have a particle volume fraction greater than a, so it is not a characteristic curve emitted by that fan. The single fan-characteristic curve stopping-time construction does not apply to the entire printed interval 0<a<1/2; the missing subregime must be included.
Both panels label the actual final deposit height and time; the second shows the deposit shock leaving the fan while the upper front remains straight. Finally, the density inversion in a settling suspension puts more particles, hence denser fluid, above lighter fluid. Rayleigh-Taylor instability and overturning can accompany this profile in a real container. They are excluded by the one-dimensional quiescent kinematic sedimentation model used for these calculations.
