Use the extended real numbers R=R∪{−∞,+∞}. A proper convex function never takes −∞ and is finite somewhere. Its effective domain is
domf={x∈Rn:f(x)<+∞}​.
(1)
For a proper extended-real function, convexity means
f((1−t)x+ty)≤(1−t)f(x)+tf(y),x,y∈Rn,0<t<1.
(2)
The endpoint cases are identities; taking 0<t<1 avoids the ambiguous product 0⋅(+∞). Equivalently, its epigraph is a convex set. In particular, the effective domain is a convex set. Properness is an additional condition: it rules out the identically +∞ function and all −∞ values, rather than requiring finiteness everywhere.
For x∈domf, the subdifferential is the set of supporting slopes
∂f(x)={p∈Rn:f(u)≥f(x)+⟨p,u−x⟩ for every u∈Rn}​.
(3)
Each element is a subgradient. Define ∂f(x)=∅ outside domf. The defining subgradient inequality must hold globally, not merely in a neighborhood of x. For a differentiable finite convex function, ∂f(x)={∇f(x)}.

Write h=f□g for the infimal convolution. It is proper by the permitted hypothesis. If h(x0​) and h(x1​) are finite, choose y0​,y1​ within ε of their respective infima. For 0<t<1, put xt​=(1−t)x0​+tx1​ and yt​=(1−t)y0​+ty1​. Convexity of f and g gives
h(xt​)​≤f(xt​−yt​)+g(yt​)≤(1−t)[f(x0​−y0​)+g(y0​)]+t[f(x1​−y1​)+g(y1​)]≤(1−t)h(x0​)+th(x1​)+ε.​
(4)
Let ε↓0. If either endpoint value is infinite, the desired inequality is automatic. Thus the infimal convolution is convex, without assuming the infimum is attained.
For its convex conjugate, replace a negative infimum by a supremum and then change variables u=x−y:
h∗(p)​=xsup​[⟨p,x⟩−yinf​{f(x−y)+g(y)}]=x,ysup​{⟨p,x⟩−f(x−y)−g(y)}=u,ysup​{⟨p,u⟩−f(u)+⟨p,y⟩−g(y)}=f∗(p)+g∗(p).​
(5)
The two suprema separate because u and y are independent. Properness of f and g makes each supremum strictly greater than −∞, so this separation remains valid when one or both are +∞. Therefore (f□g)∗=f∗+g∗. This is the conjugate of an infimal convolution. The bounded-domain assumption is unnecessary for these two calculations; the assumed properness and lower semicontinuity of h will be used when applying subgradient inversion under convex conjugacy.

Use the convention
Pτ​(x)=proxτf​(x)=argminu​{f(u)+2τ1​∥x−u∥22​}.
(6)
This proximal map is also the resolvent of a monotone operator (I+τ∂f)−1. A proper lower semicontinuous convex function has an affine minorant, so the quadratic term makes this minimization coercive and strongly convex. A unique minimizer exists for every x. Its subgradient optimality condition is
τx−Pτ​(x)​∈∂f(Pτ​(x)).
(7)
The Moreau–Yosida regularisation is fτ​=f□gτ. The conjugate of an infimal convolution and the quadratic conjugate give
fτ∗​(p)=f∗(p)+2τ​∥p∥22​.
(8)
The factor τ here is essential. Apply subgradient inversion under convex conjugacy, followed by the subdifferential sum rule with the everywhere differentiable quadratic:
p∈∂fτ​(x)​⟺x∈∂fτ∗​(p)⟺x−τp∈∂f∗(p)⟺p∈∂f(x−τp)⟺x−τp=Pτ​(x).​
(9)
The last equivalence is precisely the unique proximal minimization condition. Thus
∂fτ​(x)={τx−proxτf​(x)​}​.
(10)
This proves both existence and uniqueness of the subgradient, rather than only identifying a possible element. The finite convex function fτ​ is therefore differentiable, with ∇fτ​=(I−Pτ​)/τ, the gradient of a Moreau envelope.
For completeness, monotonicity of ∂f applied to the two proximal conditions gives
⟨Pτ​(x)−Pτ​(y),x−y⟩≥∥Pτ​(x)−Pτ​(y)∥22​.
(11)
Hence Pτ​ is firmly nonexpansive. Expanding the same inequality shows that I−Pτ​ is firmly nonexpansive too. In particular, ∇fτ​ is 1/τ-Lipschitz continuous. None of this requires a bounded effective domain; the result applies to the next example as well.

For the Euclidean norm, rotational symmetry and minimizing the radial objective give the radial soft thresholding formula
proxτ∥⋅∥2​​(x)=⎩⎨⎧​0,(1−∥x∥2​τ​)x,​∥x∥2​≤τ,∥x∥2​>τ.​
(12)
Indeed, if r=∥x∥2​, an optimal u is a nonnegative scalar multiple of x; minimizing s+(r−s)2/(2τ) over s≥0 gives s=(r−τ)+​. The gradient of a Moreau envelope is consequently
∂fτ​(x)={{x/τ},{x/∥x∥2​},​∥x∥2​≤τ,∥x∥2​>τ.​​
(13)
At x=0 this is {0}, and the two expressions agree when ∥x∥2​=τ. The Moreau envelope of the Euclidean norm itself is
fτ​(x)={∥x∥22​/(2τ),∥x∥2​−τ/2,​∥x∥2​≤τ,∥x∥2​>τ.​
(14)
Thus a quadratic core replaces the nondifferentiable tip while the outer gradient remains the normalized radial direction. The Euclidean norm has an unbounded effective domain, unlike the earlier bounded-domain hypothesis; the preceding argument explicitly shows that this restriction is unnecessary here.
For convex optimization, this Moreau–Yosida regularisation permits gradient descent and other methods for functions with a Lipschitz gradient, using a gradient Lipschitz constant 1/τ. It approximates the norm uniformly: 0≤∥x∥2​−fτ​(x)≤τ/2. Smaller τ improves approximation but makes the permitted gradient steps smaller. Minimizing fτ​ alone preserves the minimizers and minimum value of f; replacing one term in a larger objective can shift the minimizer, so the smoothing parameter controls that approximation error.

For 0<α<1, an averaged operator has the form
T=(1−α)I+αR​
(15)
for some nonexpansive mapping R, meaning ∥Rz−Rw∥2​≤∥z−w∥2​ for all z,w. The word averaging refers to a convex combination with the identity. It does not mean averaging function values over a neighborhood.
An equivalent and useful averaged-operator inequality is
∥Tz−Tw∥22​≤∥z−w∥22​−α1−α​∥(I−T)z−(I−T)w∥22​​.
(16)
To see it, put d=z−w and r=Rz−Rw and expand
∥(1−α)d+αr∥22​=(1−α)∥d∥22​+α∥r∥22​−α(1−α)∥d−r∥22​.
(17)
Use ∥r∥2​≤∥d∥2​ and (I−T)z−(I−T)w=α(d−r). Conversely, substituting R=[T−(1−α)I]/α into the same expansion proves its nonexpansiveness from the displayed inequality. The strict range 0<α<1 matters for convergence of unrelaxed iterations: a general nonexpansive mapping such as T=−I can have nonconvergent alternating iterates.

A firmly nonexpansive mapping satisfies
∥Tz−Tw∥22​≤⟨Tz−Tw,z−w⟩for all z,w​.
(18)
Writing d=z−w and a=Tz−Tw, this is equivalent to
∥a∥22​+∥d−a∥22​≤∥d∥22​.
(19)
It is also equivalent to ∥2a−d∥2​≤∥d∥2​, so R=2T−I is a nonexpansive mapping. Consequently firm nonexpansiveness is exactly 1/2-averagedness. A proximal map of a proper lower semicontinuous convex function is a basic example. A 1/2-averaged operator is also α-averaged for every α∈[1/2,1), by combining its nonexpansive part with the identity; the equivalence with firm nonexpansiveness specifically uses the parameter 1/2.

The Browder convergence theorem for averaged operators states: if T:Rd→Rd is an averaged operator and its fixed-point set FixT={z:Tz=z} is nonempty, then for every starting point, zk+1=Tzk converges in norm to a fixed point of T. The theorem assumes a fixed point exists; averaging by itself does not create one.
The finite-dimensional mechanism is worth making explicit. For any fixed point p, the averaged-operator inequality gives
∥zk+1−p∥22​≤∥zk−p∥22​−α1−α​∥zk+1−zk∥22​.
(20)
This is Fejér monotonicity with respect to the fixed-point set. It makes the sequence bounded and, by summation, makes ∑k​∥zk+1−zk∥22​ finite. Hence its residual tends to zero. Choose a convergent subsequence zkj​→zˉ; continuity of the nonexpansive mapping T implies Tzˉ=zˉ. Apply Fejér monotonicity with p=zˉ. The nonincreasing distances ∥zk−zˉ∥2​ have a subsequence tending to zero, so the whole sequence converges to zˉ. This last compactness step uses finite dimension; a corresponding general Hilbert space statement usually gives weak convergence.

⟨∇f(u)−∇f(v),u−v⟩≥L1​∥∇f(u)−∇f(v)∥22​​.
(21)
First, integration of the Lipschitz gradient along a line segment proves the descent lemma:
f(w)≤f(v)+⟨∇f(v),w−v⟩+2L​∥w−v∥22​.
(22)
Indeed, subtract the linear term and integrate ⟨∇f(v+t(w−v))−∇f(v),w−v⟩ for 0≤t≤1; its absolute value is at most Lt∥w−v∥22​.
Fix u and define q(w)=f(w)−⟨∇f(u),w⟩. This is a convex function, has the same Lipschitz gradient constant, and ∇q(u)=0, so u is a global minimizer. Put d=∇f(v)−∇f(u)=∇q(v). The descent lemma at v with w=v−d/L gives
q(u)≤q(v−d/L)≤q(v)−2L1​∥d∥22​.
(23)
Therefore
f(v)−f(u)−⟨∇f(u),v−u⟩≥2L1​∥∇f(v)−∇f(u)∥22​.
(24)
Interchange u,v and add the inequalities. The function values cancel, leaving the asserted cocoercivity. This is a proof of the Baillon–Haddad theorem without assuming a second derivative. Convexity is essential: f(u)=−∥u∥22​/2 has a 1-Lipschitz gradient but its gradient is not cocoercive.

Assume argminf=∅, as is necessary for convergence to a minimizer. The printed hypotheses alone omit this condition: f(x,y)=x in scalar x is convex with an L-Lipschitz gradient for any prescribed L>0, but has no minimizer, and the update sends xk=x0−kτ to −∞.
Under existence, define the full update T(x,y)=(x+,y+). The proximal y-step is uniquely defined by strong convexity of its quadratic term and satisfies
y−y+=τ∇y​f(x,y+),x−x+=τ∇x​f(x,y+).
(25)
The partial subdifferential in this equation belongs to the scalar function y↦f(x,y); differentiating a subdifferential itself would not define the printed proximal minimization.
We prove directly that the alternating proximal-gradient operator T is averaged. For two inputs z=(x,y) and z=(x,y​), set
w=(x,y+),w=(x,y​+),p=x−x,q=y+−y​+,(a,b)=∇f(w)−∇f(w).
(26)
The input difference is (p,q+τb), the output difference is (p−τa,q), and the difference of the two update residuals is τ(a,b). Exact expansion gives
∥Tz−Tz∥22​​=∥z−z∥22​−2τ[⟨p,a⟩+⟨q,b⟩]+τ2(∥a∥22​−∥b∥22​)≤∥z−z∥22​−(L2τ​−τ2)∥a∥22​−(L2τ​+τ2)∥b∥22​≤∥z−z∥22​−(τL2​−1)∥(I−T)z−(I−T)z∥22​.​
(27)
The first inequality uses cocoercivity at the mixed points w,w. Therefore T is (τL/2)-averaged for the requested 0<τL≤1. In particular, the endpoint τL=1 is covered: T is firmly nonexpansive, not merely nonexpansive.
A fixed point has y+=y and x+=x, so the two optimality equations give ∇y​f=0 and ∇x​f=0. Conversely, at a minimizer both gradients vanish and neither step moves. By convexity,
FixT=argminf​.
(28)
The assumed existence of a minimizer supplies a fixed point. The Browder convergence theorem for averaged operators now gives
(xk,yk)⟶(x∗,y∗)∈argminf​.
(29)
No uniqueness of the minimizer is needed. The same estimate even proves averagedness for 0<τL<2 in this smooth setting, but the requested interval already suffices. The existence condition and the use of the gradient at (xk,yk+1) are both essential to the proof as presented.

Thus, with σ=τ/(1+2τ), the steps in terms of the proximal map of ϕ are

The factor 2 comes from the squared residual without a 1/2 prefactor. The proximal map can equivalently be denoted (I+σ∂ϕ)−1. Since ϕ is a finite convex function, it is continuous, and the quadratic makes the proximal minimizer unique.
A general convex ϕ may be nondifferentiable, so this last example need not satisfy the full L-smooth hypothesis of the preceding part. The formulas nevertheless remain well-defined: only the x derivative is used explicitly. If ϕ has an Lϕ​-Lipschitz gradient, one may take L=2(∥A∥22​+1)+Lϕ​ in the smooth proof, where ∥A∥2​ is the operator norm.
There is also a useful nonsmooth extension. Put h(x,y)=∥Ax+y−c∥22​, whose Lipschitz gradient constant is Lh​=2(∥A∥22​+1). For two updates, retain the mixed-point differences (p,q) above, let (a,b)=∇h(w)−∇h(w), and let d be the difference of the two selected subgradients of ϕ at y+,y​+. The input difference is (p,q+τ(b+d)) and the output difference is (p−τa,q). Monotonicity of ∂ϕ gives ⟨q,d⟩≥0. The same norm expansion now yields
∥Tz−Tz∥22​≤∥z−z∥22​−(Lh​2τ​−τ2)∥a∥22​−Lh​2τ​∥b∥22​−τ2∥b+d∥22​.
(32)
For 0<τLh​≤1, discard the nonpositive b term and use 2τ/Lh​−τ2≥τ2. The result is the firmly nonexpansive mapping inequality for the full residual τ(a,b+d). Fixed points satisfy ∇x​h=0 and 0∈∇y​h+∂ϕ, exactly the subgradient optimality condition for the convex objective. Thus the stated algorithm also converges for nonsmooth ϕ under this sufficient step bound, provided a minimizer exists; this extension uses monotonicity, not an unavailable gradient of ϕ.

Let S:Rp⇉Rq be a set-valued mapping and let vˉ∈S(uˉ). The Aubin property at (uˉ,vˉ) means there are neighborhoods U of uˉ, V of vˉ, and a finite constant κ≥0 such that
S(u)∩V⊂S(u′)+κ∥u−u′∥2​Bq​for every u,u′∈U​.
(33)
Here Bq​ is the closed unit ball. Equivalently, every solution v∈S(u) near vˉ can be matched to a solution v′∈S(u′) within κ∥u−u′∥2​. The localization is on the left-hand side: the matching point need not be in V. The property is also called the Lipschitz-like property.
For sensitivity analysis, let u represent data or perturbations and S(u) the set of feasible or optimal solutions. The Aubin property bounds how far a nearby solution can move when the data change. Taking u=uˉ, v=vˉ also guarantees a nearby solution for each sufficiently small perturbation u′. It is a stability estimate for a relation, and by itself does not imply uniqueness or differentiability. For a single-valued solution map, it reduces to local Lipschitz continuity.

Work in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces. Suppose the graph of a set-valued mapping,
gphS={(u,v):v∈S(u)},
(34)
is locally closed at (uˉ,vˉ)∈gphS. Local closedness means that its intersection with some neighborhood of this point is closed relative to that neighborhood. This assumption is part of the criterion.
For a set C and z∈C, define the Fréchet normal cone by
NC​(z)={w:limsupz′→zz′∈C, z′=z​​∥z′−z∥2​⟨w,z′−z⟩​≤0}.
(35)
At an isolated point the condition is vacuous, so all vectors are regular normals. The limiting normal cone consists of limits of these regular normals at nearby points:
NC​(z)={w:∃zk​∈C, zk​→z, ∃wk​∈NC​(zk​), wk​→w}.
(36)
It can be nonconvex. The Mordukhovich coderivative is
D∗S(uˉ∣vˉ)(v∗)={u∗:(u∗,−v∗)∈NgphS​(uˉ,vˉ)}​.
(37)
The minus sign in the output-dual component is part of the definition. For a single-valued continuously differentiable mapping s, the graph normal is (Ds(uˉ)Tv∗,−v∗), giving D∗s(uˉ∣s(uˉ))(v∗)={Ds(uˉ)Tv∗}. Thus the coderivative extends a transpose derivative, rather than the ordinary forward derivative.
The exact Mordukhovich criterion is
S has the Aubin property at (uˉ,vˉ)⟺D∗S(uˉ∣vˉ)(0)={0}​.
(38)
This is equivalent to excluding a nonzero horizontal limiting graph normal (u∗,0). One must use the limiting normal cone; simply checking regular normals at the reference point can miss normals inherited from neighboring graph pieces.
For sensitivity analysis, apply this test to a solution map or use coderivative and normal cone calculus to express its graph normals through optimality constraints. Triviality of this kernel then proves Lipschitz-like stability without solving the perturbed problem explicitly. Under these same finite-dimensional, locally closed assumptions, the exact Lipschitz modulus is the outer norm
lipS(uˉ∣vˉ)=sup{∥u∗∥2​:u∗∈D∗S(uˉ∣vˉ)(v∗), ∥v∗∥2​≤1}.
(39)
The zero-kernel condition is the qualitative criterion; the modulus quantifies the sensitivity bound. For the next problem an explicit local formula is simpler than evaluating these graph normals.

∂ℓi​(x)=⎩⎨⎧​{−ai​},{0},{−λi​ai​:0≤λi​≤1},​1−ai​x>0,1−ai​x<0,1−ai​x=0.​

This formula also covers ai​=0, if such a sample is allowed, because the loss is then constant. For a convex function, zero in the subdifferential is necessary and sufficient for global optimality. Thus x is optimal exactly when there are weights λi​ such that
αx=n1​i=1∑n​λi​ai​,λi​=⎩⎨⎧​1,0,any value in [0,1],​ai​x<1,ai​x>1,ai​x=1.​​

αnx∈∑i∈I(x)​ai​+∑i∈J(x)​[0,1]ai​.
