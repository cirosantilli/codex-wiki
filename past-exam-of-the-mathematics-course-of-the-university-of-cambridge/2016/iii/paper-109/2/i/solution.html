Identify the Boolean hypercube Qn​ with the subsets of [n]. For a family H, write N(H) for its closed vertex neighbourhood, consisting of the family and all vertices at Hamming distance one from it. Order subsets first by increasing size and, within a fixed size, use lexicographic order: at the smallest coordinate on which they differ, the set containing that coordinate comes first. This is the simplicial order on the discrete cube. Harper's theorem states that if I is its initial segment of size ∣H∣, then
∣N(H)∣≥∣N(I)∣.​
(5)
Thus simplicial initial segments minimize the external vertex boundary for each prescribed family size. The closed-neighbourhood and external-boundary formulations are equivalent because ∣N(H)∖H∣=∣N(H)∣−∣H∣. No edge boundary is involved in this assertion.
We now give the deduction Harper theorem implies the Kruskal-Katona theorem, keeping track of the two different orders. For a family F of r-sets with r≥1, adjoin every smaller set:
H=⋃j<r​(j[n]​) ∪ F.
(6)
Its closed vertex neighbourhood is exactly
N(H)=⋃j≤r​(j[n]​) ∪ ∇F,
(7)
where ∇F is the upper shadow. Applying Harper theorem to H shows that, among r-uniform families of fixed size, the lexicographic initial segment minimizes the upper shadow: all the lower-level contributions to the two neighbourhood sizes are identical.
To convert this into a statement about the lower shadow of a k-uniform family G, take r=n−k and map each member S to R([n]∖S), where R(i)=n+1−i reverses the ground-set coordinates. Complementation changes lower shadows to upper shadows, and the coordinate reversal preserves their sizes. Moreover, the resulting lexicographic order corresponds exactly to colexicographic order on the original k-sets: S precedes T in colexicographic order precisely when the largest element of S△T lies in T. After complementation and reversal, the smallest differing coordinate lies in the image of S, as required for the stated lexicographic order. Hence a colexicographic initial segment minimizes the lower shadow, which is the Kruskal-Katona theorem. The cases k=n and k=0 are immediate and need no adjoining construction.
For its usual numerical form, write the unique greedy binomial representation
m=(kak​​)+(k−1ak−1​​)+⋯+(tat​​),ak​>ak−1​>⋯>at​≥t≥1.
(8)
Then the Kruskal-Katona theorem says
∣∂G∣≥(k−1ak​​)+(k−2ak−1​​)+⋯+(t−1at​​)(∣G∣=m).​
(9)
Here is why the shadow of the colexicographic initial segment has exactly that size. Its first block consists of all k-sets in [ak​]. If any members remain, they have the form {ak​+1}∪T, where T runs through a colexicographic initial segment of (k−1)-sets. The lower shadow consists of all (k−1)-sets in [ak​], together with {ak​+1} adjoined to the lower shadow of that remainder. These two blocks are disjoint. Recursing gives precisely the displayed sum. For m=0, both the initial segment and its shadow are empty.
The Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem states that an intersecting family A⊆(r[n]​), with 1≤r≤n/2, satisfies
∣A∣≤(r−1n−1​).​
(10)
The bound is attained by all r-sets containing one fixed element. For the proof Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem from shadows, take complements to obtain an (n−r)-uniform family B of the same size, and let D be its rank-r lower shadow. Every member of D is disjoint from some member of A, so D∩A=∅. Iterating the Kruskal-Katona theorem bounds ∣D∣ below by the rank-r shadow of the colexicographic initial segment of size ∣B∣. This iteration is valid because lower shadows of colexicographic initial segments are again colexicographic initial segments, as the preceding block description shows.
If ∣A∣>(r−1n−1​)=(n−rn−1​), that initial segment contains all (n−r)-sets in [n−1] and at least one further set containing n. Since r≤n−r≤n−1, its rank-r shadow contains every r-set in [n−1] and at least one r-set containing n. Thus ∣D∣>(rn−1​), implying
∣D∣+∣A∣>(rn−1​)+(r−1n−1​)=(rn​),
(11)
contrary to their disjointness. This proves the bound. If r>n/2, all r-sets intersect and the trivial maximum is instead (rn​).
For an intersecting family in a product alphabet, label the alphabet by Z/kZ. Partition the words into the classes
{(x1​+t,…,xn​+t):t∈Z/kZ}.
(12)
Each class has k words, and any two distinct words in it disagree in every coordinate. An intersecting family therefore contains at most one word per class. There are kn−1 classes, and fixing the first coordinate gives an intersecting family of that size. Consequently the exact maximum is
kn−1(n≥1, k≥1).​
(13)
For k=1 this just says that the single word may be included.
Finally, for a weakly intersecting family in a product alphabet, associate to each word its support S(x)={i:xi​>1}. The occurring supports form an intersecting family of nonempty subsets, and the fibre over support S has (k−1)∣S∣ words. Put w=k−1. When k≥2, w≥1, and a support family can contain at most one of each complementary pair S,[n]∖S. For odd n, the larger support in each pair has size greater than n/2, so
∣A∣≤∑{S,Sc}​max{w∣S∣,wn−∣S∣}=∑j>n/2​(jn​)(k−1)j.
(14)
All supports of size greater than n/2 are mutually intersecting, so taking every word over those supports achieves this bound. Thus the required extremal family is the strict-majority support family, with size
j=(n+1)/2∑n​(jn​)(k−1)j.​
(15)
This is the weighted intersecting family bound on an odd Boolean lattice. For k>2, strict inequality between the complementary fibre weights forces this extremal family to be unique. For k=2, it is a largest family but need not be the only one; for example, all supports containing one fixed coordinate also attain the same size. For k=1, no word has a nonempty support, so the maximum is zero, again agreeing with the formula.
