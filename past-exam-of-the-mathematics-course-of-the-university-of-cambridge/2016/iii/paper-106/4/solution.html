The definitions in a unital C-star algebra are
hermitian:unitary:normal:​x∗=x,x∗x=xx∗=1,x∗x=xx∗.​​
(40)
Thus a Hermitian element of a C-star algebra is self-adjoint, a Unitary element of a C-star algebra has inverse x∗, and a Normal element of a C-star algebra commutes with its adjoint. We derive the needed C-star algebra facts directly from ∥a∗a∥=∥a∥2, as required.
First ∥1∥=∥1∥2 gives ∥1∥=1, and
∥a∥2=∥a∗a∥≤∥a∗∥∥a∥
(41)
gives ∥a∥≤∥a∗∥; applying the same argument to a∗ gives equality. Thus the involution is an isometry. For a Unitary element of a C-star algebra u,
∥u∥2=∥u∗u∥=1,∥u−1∥=∥u∗∥=1.
(42)
The general Banach algebra spectrum bound gives ∣λ∣≤1 for λ∈σ(u). Such a λ is nonzero, and the inverse spectral mapping theorem gives λ−1∈σ(u−1), so also ∣λ∣−1≤1. Hence
σ(u)⊆T={z∈C:∣z∣=1}.​
(43)
If h=h∗, the convergent exponential series and the isometric involution give (eith)∗=e−ith. These commuting exponentials multiply to 1, so eith is unitary for real t. By the exponential spectral mapping theorem, if λ∈σ(h) then eitλ∈σ(eith)⊆T. Taking t=1 gives e−Imλ=1, so
σ(h)⊆R.​
(44)
Only general Banach algebra spectral mapping theorem and the defining C-star identity were used.
Now prove spectral permanence for C-star algebras. Let B⊆A be the norm-closed C-star subalgebra with the same identity. The algebraic inclusion always gives σA​(b)⊆σB​(b). For h=h∗∈B, both spectra lie in R. If λ∈/σA​(h), choose nonreal λn​→λ. Since σB​(h)⊆R, each (λn​1−h)−1 belongs to B. By continuity of inversion in a Banach algebra, these inverses converge in A to (λ1−h)−1, which belongs to B because B is closed. Thus λ∈/σB​(h), proving equality for hermitian elements.
For the requested normal x∈B, suppose b=x−λ1 is invertible in A. The element b∗b is hermitian and invertible in A, so the equality just proved places (b∗b)−1 in B. Consequently
y=(b∗b)−1b∗∈B,yb=1.
(45)
Since b already has a two-sided inverse in A, multiplying yb=1 by that inverse gives y=b−1. Hence its inverse belongs to B, and
σB​(x)=σA​(x).​
(46)
We need one more elementary consequence of the C-star identity: the spectral radius norm equality for normal elements. If h=h∗, then ∥h2∥=∥h∥2. If a is normal, commutativity of a,a∗ gives
∥a2∥2=∥(a∗)2a2∥=∥(a∗a)2∥=∥a∗a∥2=∥a∥4.
(47)
Every power of a is a Normal element of a C-star algebra. Induction gives ∥a2n∥=∥a∥2n. The general spectral radius formula therefore implies
r(a)=limm→∞​∥am∥1/m=∥a∥.
(48)
The continuous functional calculus is the unique unital ∗-homomorphism C(K)→B(H) taking the coordinate function to T. For bounded linear operators on a Hilbert space, the adjoint identity gives ∥S∥=sup∥v∥=∥w∥=1​∣⟨Sv,w⟩∣=∥S∗∥. Therefore
∥S∥2=sup∥v∥=1​⟨S∗Sv,v⟩≤∥S∗S∥≤∥S∗∥∥S∥=∥S∥2.
(49)
This proves the C-star identity for B(H) directly; completeness and submultiplicativity come from the operator norm. Let
C={p(T,T∗):p a complex polynomial in two commuting variables}​∥⋅∥.
(50)
Because T is a normal operator, this is a commutative unital C-star algebra. Every element of C is a Normal element of a C-star algebra, so the preceding norm identity says its Gelfand transform is isometric:
∥a∥∞​=supχ∈Δ(C)​∣χ(a)∣=rC​(a)=∥a∥.
(51)
Here Δ(C) is the compact character space from the general Gelfand representation theorem for commutative Banach algebras.
Each algebra character preserves the involution. Indeed, write a=h+ik with h,k hermitian; χ(h)∈σC​(h)⊆R and similarly for k, so χ(a∗)=χ(a)​. Therefore the continuous map
Δ(C)⟶K,χ⟼χ(T)
(52)
is injective: its value determines χ(T∗) and hence its value on all the dense polynomials. It is surjective because the general character description of the spectrum gives {χ(T):χ∈Δ(C)}=σC​(T), and spectral permanence for C-star algebras identifies this with σB(H)​(T)=K. A continuous function that is a bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism, so we identify Δ(C) with K.
Under this identification, the Gelfand transform sends T to u(z)=z and T∗ to u. Its image is an isometric, and therefore closed, unital self-adjoint subalgebra of C(K). It separates points because it contains u. The complex Stone-Weierstrass theorem makes the image dense, hence equal to all of C(K). Inverting the Gelfand transform and including C into B(H) gives
Φ:C(K)⟶B(H),f⟼f(T),
(53)
an isometric unital C-star homomorphism with u(T)=T.
To prove uniqueness without assuming automatic continuity of C-star homomorphisms, let Ψ be any other such map. Since every f∈C(K) is normal, Ψ(f) is normal. A unital algebra homomorphism preserves inverses, so
σB(H)​(Ψ(f))⊆σC(K)​(f)=f(K).
(54)
Using the normal-element norm identity yields ∥Ψ(f)∥=r(Ψ(f))≤∥f∥∞​. Thus Ψ is contractive. It agrees with Φ on polynomials in u,u, since both send them to the same polynomials in T,T∗. These polynomials are dense by the Stone-Weierstrass theorem, so continuity proves Ψ=Φ. This establishes the continuous functional calculus with no unproved theorem specific to C-star algebras.
A disconnected spectrum gives a nontrivial closed invariant subspace. Write K=K1​⊔K2​ with K1​,K2​ nonempty and both open and closed in K. The indicator function f=1K1​​ is continuous on K, even though no such continuity is needed across the gap outside K. Let P=f(T). The continuous functional calculus gives
P2=P,P∗=P,PT=TP.
(55)
Its isometry gives ∥P∥=1 and ∥I−P∥=1, so P=0,I. The linear projection has closed range Y=PH=ker(I−P). Its range is nonzero and proper, and T(Px)=P(Tx)∈Y proves invariance. Since it also commutes with T∗, the same subspace is reducing. This proves disconnected spectrum gives a reducing subspace.
The figure can be realized without any eigenvalues: take H=L2(K,dA), where dA is planar area on the two closed disks, and let T multiply by z. Its adjoint multiplies by z, so it is a normal operator. An eigenvector for λ would be supported on the area-zero singleton {λ}, hence would be zero in L2. Outside K, multiplication by 1/(z−λ) is a bounded inverse to T−λI. For λ∈K, normalized indicator functions of K∩{∣z−λ∣<ε} have ∥(T−λI)h∥≤ε, excluding a bounded inverse. Thus its spectrum is exactly K. The linear projection in the figure multiplies by 1K1​​, and its range consists of functions supported on the left disk.
