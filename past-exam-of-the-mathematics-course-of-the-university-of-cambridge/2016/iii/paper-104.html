The free product G1​∗G2​ is obtained by taking disjoint copies of the elements of the two groups as generators and imposing precisely their internal multiplication relations. Equivalently, if Gi​=⟨Xi​∣Ri​⟩ with disjoint Xi​, then
G1​∗G2​=⟨X1​⊔X2​∣R1​⊔R2​⟩.
(1)
A nontrivial free product has both factors different from the trivial group.
The normal form theorem for a free product says that every element has a unique expression
g1​g2​⋯gr​,gj​∈Gϵj​​∖{1},ϵj​=ϵj+1​,​
(2)
where the empty expression represents the identity. The gj​ are called syllables in a free product. Thus nonempty alternating products cannot be the identity, and the natural maps of the factors into the free product are injective.
For existence, multiply adjacent syllables from the same factor and delete identity syllables until the word alternates. Each change uses a defining relation and decreases the number of syllables. To establish uniqueness without assuming that these reductions are confluent, let W be the set of alternating nonidentity syllable sequences, including the empty sequence. For h∈Gi​, define a permutation Lh​ of W by prepending h, multiplying into the first syllable if it belongs to Gi​, and deleting it if that product is the identity; for h=1, do nothing.
The multiplication law inside Gi​ gives Lh​Lk​=Lhk​ for h,k∈Gi​, and Lh−1​=Lh−1​. This can be checked at the first syllable: if a product deletes it, the next syllable belongs to the other factor and is treated as a new first syllable. Hence the defining relations give a group action of G1​∗G2​ on W. An alternating word g1​⋯gr​ sends the empty sequence to its own syllable sequence. Two such words representing the same group element induce the same permutation, and therefore have identical sequences. This proves the normal form theorem for a free product.
The universal property of a free product states that, for group homomorphisms fi​:Gi​→H, there is a unique group homomorphism f:G1​∗G2​→H extending both. Explicitly,
f(g1​⋯gr​)=fϵ1​​(g1​)⋯fϵr​​(gr​).​
(3)
The internal multiplication relations are respected because each fi​ is a group homomorphism, so this assignment descends from words to the presented group. Alternatively, multiplication of two normal forms is concatenation followed by precisely those internal relations, which do not change its value in H. Uniqueness holds because the two factors generate the free product.

A topological graph is a CW complex with only zero-dimensional and one-dimensional cells: its vertices form the zero-skeleton, and each edge is a copy of an open interval attached by its two endpoints to vertices, possibly the same vertex. It has the weak topology of a CW complex. A subgraph is a CW subcomplex, obtained by choosing vertices and edges and retaining the endpoints of every chosen edge. Loops and multiple edges are allowed.
A spanning tree is a connected spanning subgraph containing no cycle in a graph. To construct one in any connected topological graph Γ, consider all acyclic spanning subgraphs, ordered by inclusion; the subgraph with every vertex and no edges is one. The union of a chain is still acyclic, since any cycle uses only finitely many edges and would already occur in a member of the chain. The Zorn lemma therefore gives a maximal such subgraph Δ.
If Δ were disconnected, a finite edge path in Γ between vertices of two of its components would contain an edge joining different components. Adding that edge creates no cycle, contradicting maximality. Thus Δ is a spanning tree. Fix a root vertex. Each point of Δ has a unique finite path to the root; give every edge length one and move the point a fraction s of the way along that path at time s∈[0,1]. This defines a contraction of a topological space of Δ. On each closed edge times [0,1], the construction takes place in a finite tree and is continuous. Since the interval is a locally finite CW complex, the product with Δ has the corresponding weak topology of a CW complex, so these restrictions establish continuity globally. Consequently
Δ≃{point}.​
(5)
This argument includes infinite graphs and does not assume local finiteness of Γ.
If p:Γ→Γ is a covering map, take p−1(Γ0) as the vertices upstairs. Each component of the inverse image of an open edge is an open interval mapping homeomorphically to that edge, because an interval is simply connected. Its parametrization lifts along the closed edge to attach its endpoints to lifted vertices, by path lifting theorem. Small star neighborhoods at vertices are lifted homeomorphically by the covering map, and give exactly the neighborhoods of the resulting topological graph. Thus the cell structure has the topology of the original covering space. The total space may be disconnected, but every component is a topological graph.
The Nielsen–Schreier formula is
[Fn​:H]=d<∞⟹H≅F1+d(n−1)​.​
(6)
For a proof, realize Fn​ as the fundamental group of the wedge sum of n circles. Construct its connected covering graph with vertices the right cosets Hg, and an oriented edge labeled xi​ from Hg to Hgxi​ for each free generator xi​. There is exactly one incoming and one outgoing edge of each label at every vertex, which establishes the local covering map property. Reading a word from H ends at its coset, so the closed paths at H give exactly the words in H. The induced map of fundamental groups is injective by the homotopy lifting property of a covering map, and its image is H.
For index d, this covering graph has d vertices and dn edges. A spanning tree in it has d−1 edges. The permitted rank formula for a finite connected graph now gives
rankH=dn−(d−1)=1+d(n−1).
(7)
In particular this proves both freeness and the index formula.
If n≥2 and d>1, then 1+d(n−1)>n, so a proper finite-index subgroup cannot be isomorphic to Fn​. For n=1, F1​≅Z, and dZ is a proper index-d subgroup isomorphic to Z for every d≥2. Hence
Such a proper finite-index copy exists exactly when n=1.​
(8)

The HNN extension in the convention used here is
G∗ϕ​=⟨G,t∣tat−1=ϕ(a) for every a∈A⟩.
(9)
The new generator t is its stable letter, and A,B are its associated subgroups of an HNN extension. We prove the normal form theorem for an HNN extension, which also proves that the natural map G→G∗ϕ​ is injective.
Set C+​=A, C−​=B, ψ+​=ϕ, and ψ−​=ϕ−1. For each sign ϵ, choose a right coset transversal Rϵ​ for Cϵ​\G, containing 1 as the representative of Cϵ​. Thus every g∈G has a unique decomposition g=cr, with c∈Cϵ​ and r∈Rϵ​. A normal form is
g0​tϵ1​r1​⋯tϵk​rk​,ri​∈Rϵi​​,ri​=1 if i<k and ϵi+1​=−ϵi​,​
(10)
where g0​∈G is unrestricted and the case k=0 is just g0​.
Here is a constructive proof. Let N be the set of these formal sequences. Left multiplication by h∈G changes only g0​ to hg0​. To prepend tϵ, uniquely decompose g0​=cr using Rϵ​ and use
tϵcr=ψϵ​(c)tϵr.
(11)
If the old first stable letter is t−ϵ and r=1, cancel this pair and multiply ψϵ​(c) into the now-leading coefficient r1​. Otherwise retain the new first stable letter. Both cases give a sequence in N; after a cancellation the leading coefficient is unrestricted, so no further normalization is needed at that end. Prepending the letters of a word from right to left proves existence.
These operations define permutations of N. The base-group operations satisfy Lh​Lh′​=Lhh′​. The operations Lt+​ and Lt−​ are inverse: if prepending did not cancel, the reverse operation decomposes the new leading coefficient ψϵ​(c) with representative 1 and cancels the newly inserted letter; if it did cancel, the reverse operation decomposes ψϵ​(c)r1​ with representative r1​, restoring the original prefix. The normal-form restriction rules out an unwanted second cancellation in the latter case.
For a∈A, decomposing ag0​ uses the same representative as decomposing g0​, and changes its subgroup coefficient from c to ac. Both the cancelling and noncancelling cases therefore give
Lt​La​=Lϕ(a)​Lt​.
(12)
Thus all defining relations act identically on N, and we obtain a group action of the presented HNN extension. Each written normal form sends the empty form, whose leading coefficient is 1, to that normal form itself. Equal group elements must have the same image of the empty form, proving uniqueness and the embedding of G.
A reduced sequence in an HNN extension is a word g0​tϵ1​g1​⋯tϵk​gk​ containing no pinch in an HNN extension: no tat−1 with a∈A and no t−1bt with b∈B. Equivalently, whenever ϵi+1​=−ϵi​, one requires gi​∈/Cϵi​​.
Britton's lemma states that
a reduced sequence with k>0 is not the identity.​
(13)
More strongly it cannot represent an element of G. To prove this from the normal form theorem for an HNN extension, normalize the coefficients from right to left. Splitting gi​=cr moves ψϵi​​(c) to the coefficient immediately on its left. If the preceding stable letter has the opposite sign, this transported factor belongs to the subgroup relevant to that inverse pair; multiplying by it cannot turn a coefficient outside that subgroup into one inside it. If the signs agree, cancellation is impossible anyway. Hence no stable letter disappears during normalization of a reduced sequence. Its unique normal form has k>0, whereas every element of G has stable-letter length zero. This proves Britton's lemma. The same proof works with finitely many stable letters, with pinches requiring the same letter and its inverse.

We give a finite semigroup presentation using the convention that s0​ is blank, the tape is unbounded in both directions, and a transition δ(qi​,sj​)=(qk​,sp​,D) writes sp​ and moves in direction D. The Turing machine is deterministic, and the halting state q0​ has no outgoing transition. A finite configuration is encoded by
huqi​vh,u,v∈S∗,
(19)
with the head scanning the first symbol of v; if v is empty, it scans an implicit blank. Unrepresented cells beyond the boundary markers h are blank. An additional generator q records completed halting and erasure.
The generators of the semigroup are S⊔Q⊔{h,q}. Its relations have the following finite families. For every right-moving transition, include
qi​sj​=sp​qk​if δ(qi​,sj​)=(qk​,sp​,R).
(20)
For every left-moving transition and every tape symbol sℓ​, include
sℓ​qi​sj​=qk​sℓ​sp​if δ(qi​,sj​)=(qk​,sp​,L).
(21)
If stationary moves are part of the chosen machine model, include qi​sj​=qk​sp​ for each such move. Include the boundary-padding relations, for every state qi​,
hqi​=hs0​qi​,qi​h=qi​s0​h.
(22)
They supply a blank immediately to the left of the head when needed, or at the right boundary when the current scanned cell was implicit. Finally include
q0​=q,sj​q=q=qsj​ (0≤j≤m),hq=q=qh.
(23)
Writing RT​ for this displayed list, the answer is the explicit finite semigroup presentation
Γ(T)=⟨S⊔Q⊔{h,q}∣RT​⟩.​

All relations are between nonempty words; no group inverses or empty-word generator are used. The transition families are finite because the transition table and alphabet are finite, and the padding and erasure families are visibly finite. The declared head convention determines which side of a tape symbol carries a state letter.

If the Turing machine halts on w, follow its computation from hq1​wh. A transition at an already represented cell is one of the corresponding relations. At a boundary, first insert the required blank using a padding relation. Thus the computation gives a finite equality derivation to huq0​vh. Replace q0​ by q, erase the symbols of u,v using sj​q=q=qsj​, and erase both boundary markers. This proves hq1​wh=q in the semigroup.
For the converse, equality in a presented semigroup means a finite sequence of contextual replacements using defining relations in either direction. In any derivation from hq1​wh to q, consider the first occurrence of the special symbol q. Before it occurs, the erasure relations cannot be used in either direction. Each word therefore still has exactly two markers h, exactly one state letter, and the form huqi​vh. Both directions of a transition relation join configurations linked by one actual machine step; both directions of a boundary-padding relation merely change how much blank tape is represented.
The first occurrence of q must come from q0​=q in the forward direction. Hence the initial configuration is connected, by transitions in either direction and harmless padding, to a configuration in state q0​. For a deterministic Turing machine, the property of eventually reaching q0​ is invariant along each transition edge: if C→D is a step, then C halts if and only if D halts, since that step is the unique next step of C and C is not already in the halting state. The same property is unchanged by padding. A finite path of these edges to q0​ therefore shows that the initial configuration halts.
Consequently
w∈Ω(T)⟺hq1​wh=q in Γ(T).​
(25)
The invariance argument is necessary because equality in a semigroup presentation allows reversed transitions as well as forward computation steps.

The base free product for the group encoding of a modular machine is
K=⟨x,y,t∣xy=yx⟩≅Z2∗Z,t(r,s)=x−ry−stxrys.​
(28)
The letters x,y generate the first factor, and t generates the second. Let TK​ be the kernel of the group homomorphism K→⟨x,y⟩≅Z2 that kills t. Then
TK​=⟨t(r,s):r,s∈Z⟩
(29)
is a free group with this displayed free basis of a group. Indeed every word in K can be rewritten as a product of such conjugates followed by an element of ⟨x,y⟩, so they generate the kernel. A freely reduced product of these conjugates is nontrivial by the normal form theorem for a free product: after combining adjacent occurrences of the same conjugate, different successive indices give a nonzero intervening x,y-syllable. For clarity, a modular machine of modulus m>1 has at most one instruction for each residue pair (a,b), with 0≤a,b<m and 0≤c<m2. Its right and left transitions are respectively
(mu+a,mv+b)⟼(m2u+c,v),(mu+a,mv+b)⟼(u,m2v+c).
(30)

The factors Z2 and Z are torsion-free groups, and so is their free product K. Indeed every element of a free product is conjugate either into one factor or to an alternating word whose first and last syllables lie in different factors. A word of the latter kind has nonempty reduced powers of every positive length, so has infinite order. A nonidentity element of either factor also has infinite order.
By the finite-order result in part (b), every finite-order element of the multiple HNN extension KM​ is conjugate into the torsion-free base K, and hence is the identity. Apply the same result once more to GM​, whose base is KM​. It follows that
GM​ is torsion-free.​
(48)
Thus the failure of a word problem for a group to be decidable does not require torsion.

An isomorphism-invariant Markov property of finitely presented groups ρ has two finitely presented witnesses: a group A+​ with ρ, and a group A−​ which cannot embed in any finitely presented group having ρ. In symbols,
A+​ has ρ,A−​↪B⟹B does not have ρ.​
(61)
The second condition is an obstruction to embedding, stronger than merely saying that A−​ itself fails the property.
Let U be a fixed finitely presented group with unsolvable word problem for a group. Form the finitely presented free product P=U∗A−​. Given a word w in the generators of U, apply part (a) to this P and w, and output the finite presentation of
B(w)=A+​∗P(w).
(62)
If w=1 in U, it is also 1 in P, so P(w) is trivial and B(w)≅A+​ has ρ. If w=1 in U, its image stays nontrivial in the free product P, and P embeds in P(w). Therefore A−​ embeds in B(w), which cannot have ρ. We have the effective equivalence
B(w) has ρ⟺w=1 in U.​
(63)
An algorithm recognizing whether an arbitrary finite group presentation has ρ would decide the unsolvable word problem for a group U. This contradiction proves the Adian–Rabin theorem: no Markov property of finitely presented groups is algorithmically decidable.

The auxiliary Higman group Q has no nontrivial finite quotients of a group. One can use this permitted property directly, but there is a short verification. In any finite quotient, let ni​ be the order of the image of ai​. Since conjugation sends ai+1​ to its square, ni+1​ must be odd. If some ni​>1, choose the least prime number p dividing any ni​. It is odd. Conjugation by the preceding generator gives
2ni−1​≡1(modp).
(64)
The multiplicative order of 2 modulo p is therefore a divisor of ni−1​. It is greater than one and divides p−1 by the Fermat little theorem, so it has a prime divisor smaller than p. That prime also divides ni−1​, contradicting the choice of p. Hence every ni​=1 and the quotient is trivial.
Given any finitely presented group P, form P=P∗⟨v⟩, and apply part (a) to the word w=v. This word is nontrivial by the normal form theorem for a free product, so the output J=P(v) contains P by the embedding already proved.
