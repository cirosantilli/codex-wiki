For a bounded linear operator K:U→V between Hilbert spaces, the Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator assigns to each admissible datum its unique minimum-norm least-squares solution. Its domain is
D(K†)=ranK⊕(ranK)⊥.​
(1)
Write f=Ku+h, where u∈(kerK)⊥ and h∈(ranK)⊥=kerK∗. Then K†f=u. Restricting to (kerK)⊥ removes the ambiguity among solutions differing by an element of the kernel. The orthogonal decomposition also shows that KK†f is the orthogonal projection of f onto ranK, and K†Ku is the orthogonal projection of u onto (kerK)⊥. The inverse is defined on a dense domain and need not be a bounded linear operator when the range is not closed.

The given map is the unilateral shift operator. Its adjoint operator is the left shift operator, since
⟨Ku,f⟩=∑j≥1​uj​fj+1​​,K∗f=(f2​,f3​,…).
(2)
The shift is an isometry with trivial kernel and closed range {f:f1​=0}. Its range has orthogonal complement span{e1​}, so every square-summable datum is admissible. Consequently,
D(K†)=ℓ2,K†(f1​,f2​,…)=(f2​,f3​,…)=K∗f.​
(3)
Indeed K†K=I, while KK†f=f−f1​e1​ is the required orthogonal projection. The discarded first component is the residual of the least-squares solution, not an obstruction to the existence of the Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator.

Choose a singular system of a compact operator with Kvj​=σj​uj​, K∗uj​=σj​vj​ and σj​>0. The Picard criterion, together with range orthogonality, gives
f∈ranK⟺f⊥kerK∗ and j∑​σj2​∣⟨f,uj​⟩∣2​<∞.​
(4)
For necessity, expand a solution's component in (kerK)⊥ in the orthonormal vectors vj​; its coefficients must be ⟨f,uj​⟩/σj​. Their square sum is finite by Bessel's inequality. Conversely, the displayed square sum defines a convergent Hilbert space series
K†f=∑j​σj​⟨f,uj​⟩​vj​.
(5)
Applying the bounded linear operator K to the partial sums yields the expansion of f in ranK=(kerK∗)⊥. Thus its limit solves Ku=f. The perpendicularity condition is essential here: the Picard criterion alone characterizes the domain of the generalized inverse, which also includes components in kerK∗.

A linear regularization is a family of bounded linear operators Rα​:V→U, α>0, for which Rα​f→K†f for every f∈D(K†) as α↓0. To obtain a convergent regularization of an inverse problem for noisy data, choose a regularization parameter so that the exact-data approximation error and the amplified noise both vanish.
An example is Tikhonov regularization:
Rα​=(K∗K+αI)−1K∗.​
(6)
The positive quadratic term makes K∗K+αI invertible. The Tikhonov filter norm bound follows from the filter s/(s2+α), whose maximum for s≥0 is 1/(2α​). Therefore ∥Rα​∥≤1/(2α​). The spectral factors s2/(s2+α) tend to one on the positive spectrum; dominated convergence theorem gives consistency on D(K†). The noise-bias decomposition for linear regularization then proves convergence whenever α(δ)→0 and δ/α(δ)​→0, for example α(δ)=δ for sufficiently small positive noise levels.

The Volterra integration operator is injective and has dense range. Its range of the Volterra integration operator is {f∈H1(0,1):f(0)=0}, and its Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator is differentiation on this domain. Thus the error estimate for K†f implicitly requires f(0)=0, in addition to the printed H2 assumption. For an arbitrary H2 function the same calculation bounds error against f′, but that function need not be in D(K†).
Put a=(1−α)/2, b=(1+α)/2 and h=α/2. The three stencils are forward, central finite differences, and backward on [0,a), [a,b) and [b,1], respectively. All shifted evaluation points lie in [0,1]. For g∈L2(0,1), apply ∣A−B∣2≤2(∣A∣2+∣B∣2) and change variables in each of the six evaluation terms. The resulting integration intervals are
[α,b],[0,a],[1/2,1/2+α],[1/2−α,1/2],[b,1],[a,1−α].
(7)
Pair [0,a] with [a,1−α], [α,b] with [b,1], and the two middle intervals. Each pair has disjoint interiors, so almost every point is counted at most three times. This overlap multiplicity bound for piecewise difference operators proves
∥Rα​g∥22​≤α26​∥g∥22​,∥Rα​∥≤α6​​.
(8)
For the bias on the middle interval, write the central finite difference as an average of f′. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives
Rα​f(x)−f′(x)=∫−hh​kα​(s)f′′(x+s)ds,kα​(s)=αsgn(s)(h−∣s∣)​,
(9)
where ∥kα​∥1​=α/4. Extend f′′ by zero to the real line. Young's convolution inequality bounds the middle-interval L2 error by αc/4. Combining its squared error with the supplied outer-interval estimate gives the central-difference noise-bias bound
∥Rα​f−f′∥2​≤α2c2+α2c2/16​=417​​αc.
(10)
The triangle inequality and the operator norm estimate now give
∥K†f−Rα​fδ∥2​≤α6​δ​+417​​αc.​
(11)
For c>0, balancing noise and approximation bias minimizes this upper bound at
α(δ)=2(6/17)1/4δ/c​,∥K†f−Rα(δ)​fδ∥2​≤1021/4δc​,​
(12)
for sufficiently small δ that α<1/2. Capping the parameter at 1/4 supplies an admissible rule for all positive noise levels. More generally, α→0 and δ/α→0 suffice; α=δ​ avoids needing c or dividing by c=0.
To prove convergence for every admissible datum, not just H2 data, let f=Ku with u∈L2(0,1) and extend u by zero. Each stencil is a forward, centred or backward average of u. Translation continuity in Lp makes each averaged difference from u tend to zero in L2(R). Restricting those three bounds to their respective intervals proves Rα​Ku→u. The noise-bias decomposition for linear regularization, with δ/α→0, finishes the convergent regularization of an inverse problem.

For a proper convex function E on a real Hilbert space, its subdifferential at y is
∂E(y)={p:E(x)≥E(y)+⟨p,x−y⟩ for every x}.
(13)
In a Banach space, interpret the pairing with an element of the dual space. For a chosen subgradient p∈∂E(y), the Bregman distance is
DEp​(x,y)=E(x)−E(y)−⟨p,x−y⟩≥0.​
(14)
If also q∈∂E(x), its symmetric Bregman distance is
DEp​(x,y)+DEq​(y,x)=⟨q−p,x−y⟩.​
(15)
The subgradient inequality proves nonnegativity. These quantities depend on the chosen subgradients and generally are not metrics: they need not distinguish different points or obey the triangle inequality.

For the maximum-entropy regularization functional, differentiation of the scalar integrand gives (slogs−s)′=logs. Thus the Fréchet derivative at a positive reference function y is E′(y)=logy, and the stated differentiability result gives ∂E(y)={logy}. Consequently,
DE​(x,y)=∫Ω​[xlogyx​−x+y]dt.​
(16)
This is the generalized Kullback–Leibler divergence. When both functions integrate to the same mass, the last two terms cancel after integration; for probability density functions it becomes the usual Kullback-Leibler divergence. For two positive functions, adding the reverse distance gives
DEsym​(x,y)=∫Ω​(x−y)(logx−logy)dt.​
(17)
A sufficient setting for the differentiability argument is L∞(Ω) with y bounded away from zero. For nonnegative x, use 0log(0/y)=0 wherever appropriate. Positivity alone in L2 does not automatically provide an open domain on which the Fréchet derivative exists.

Interpret the normal-operator source condition as the displayed intersection condition: there is v∈U with w=K∗Kv∈∂J(u†). Fix any α>0 and put uˉ=u†+αv. The subgradient optimality condition for the artificial-data objective is
0∈K∗(Ku†−Kuˉ)+α∂J(u†).
(19)
It holds because its first term equals −αK∗Kv=−αw. By convexity, this is sufficient for global minimality, proving the forward implication. Conversely, if u† minimizes that objective, the same optimality condition gives
w=α1​K∗K(uˉ−u†)∈∂J(u†).
(20)
Taking v=(uˉ−u†)/α proves the intersection condition. Thus the two conditions are equivalent when the source vector is allowed to be zero.
The printed additional requirement v=0 is not equivalent in general. For K=I and J≡0, the displayed source condition holds, and choosing uˉ=u† makes u† the minimizer, but ∂J(u†)={0} forces v=0. A nonzero-source version needs an additional hypothesis, such as requiring w=0; it cannot be inferred from the printed intersection alone.

Let w=K∗Kv∈∂J(u†) from the normal-operator source condition, and set e=u−u†. Since u† is a least-squares solution, the normal equation for a linear inverse problem gives K∗(Ku†−f)=0. Expand the objective difference and use the Bregman distance definition:
Fα​(u)−Fα​(u†)​=21​∥Ke∥2+α[J(u)−J(u†)]=21​∥Ke∥2+α⟨Kv,Ke⟩+αDJw​(u,u†)=21​∥K(e+αv)∥2+αDJw​(u,u†)−2α2​∥Kv∥2.​
(21)
The cross term with the residual of the least-squares solution vanishes by the normal equation; exact data are not required. Compare the minimizing uα​ with z=u†−αv. At z, the completed square is zero. Dividing the resulting inequality by α proves the stronger shifted-comparator Bregman bound
DJw​(uα​,u†)+2α1​∥K(uα​−u†+αv)∥2≤DJw​(u†−αv,u†),w=K∗Kv.​
(22)
Dropping the nonnegative squared term gives the requested estimate. If the comparator lies outside the effective domain of J, its distance is infinite and the inequality is valid but gives no finite error bound.

The relevant nonlinear generalized singular vector relation, without imposing unit-data normalization, is
p=λK∗Ku†∈∂J(u†),u†=0.
(23)
This is a generalized eigenfunction in the forward-operator metric, rather than a distributional generalized eigenfunction of a linear operator. It supplies the normal-operator source condition with v=λu†. For a convex positively one-homogeneous functional, the subgradient inequality applied at zero and at 2u† gives ⟨p,u†⟩=J(u†). If Ku†=0, then
λ=∥Ku†∥2J(u†)​.
(24)
Assume the usual positive eigenvalue λ>0 and 0<αλ≤1. By positive homogeneity,
DJp​((1−αλ)u†,u†)=0.
(25)
The shifted-comparator Bregman bound therefore yields
DJp​(uα​,u†)=0,Kuα​=(1−αλ)Ku†.​
(26)
The vector (1−αλ)u† is itself a minimizer: p remains a subgradient along the nonnegative ray, and substitution satisfies the subgradient optimality condition. If K is injective, this also proves uα​=(1−αλ)u†. Otherwise distinct minimizers can differ in the kernel while sharing the same predicted data.
For a nonnegative convex positively one-homogeneous functional, the branch continues as (1−αλ)+​u† above the threshold, since p/(αλ)∈∂J(0) there. Nonnegativity is needed for that clipping argument; bare positive homogeneity allows signed linear functionals.
Zero Bregman distance does not imply exact recovery. For K=I, J(s)=∣s∣, u†=a>0 and 0<α<a, the minimizer is uα​=a−α=a, but DJ1​(a−α,a)=0. Both points touch the same supporting line, as described by zero Bregman distance and supporting faces.

For a proper convex function E that is lower semicontinuous on a Hilbert space, its proximal operator at scale γ>0 is
proxγE​(z)=argxmin​{21​∥x−z∥2+γE(x)}=(I+γ∂E)−1z.​
(27)
The minimizer is unique because the objective is strongly convex; existence follows from the closed proper convex functional and the coercive quadratic, using an affine lower bound. The subgradient optimality condition is 0∈x−z+γ∂E(x), which is exactly the displayed resolvent of a monotone operator relation. Setting γ=1 gives the unscaled proximity or resolvent operator requested here. This is a nonlinear resolvent of the subdifferential, distinct from the spectral resolvent of a linear operator.

Let s=Kx and assume s>0. The scalar Poisson data fidelity integrand has derivative 1−y/s. Applying the permitted interchange of differentiation and integration in a direction z gives
dεd​E(x+εz)​ε=0​=∫Σ​(1−Kxy​)Kzdt=⟨z,K∗(1−Kxy​)⟩.
(28)
Thus the ambient-space Fréchet derivative, or its Hilbert space gradient when the pairing is an inner product, is
E′(x)=K∗(1−Kxy​).​
(29)
The quotient is pointwise, and the adjoint operator transports it back from the data domain to the source domain. The chosen spaces must ensure the integrals and derivative pairing are finite; an output bounded away from zero is a useful sufficient condition.
The printed set of probability density functions and positive cone are not vector spaces, so the notation for a bounded linear map between them is understood as a positive bounded linear operator on ambient function spaces, restricted to admissible inputs. If unit mass is a constraint, admissible directions satisfy ∫Ω​z=0. The displayed gradient is then a representative modulo an additive constant; optimization with that constraint additionally includes its normal cone.

Use the scaled proximal operator proxγE​; take γ=1 for each unscaled resolvent.
For the quadratic case, write DTD=diag(d1​,…,dn​) and ξ=Qx. Since Q is an orthogonal matrix, the quadratic proximal term becomes ∥ξ−Qz∥2/2. The subgradient optimality condition gives
(I+γDTD)ξ=Qz+γDTy.
(30)
All dj​≥0, so each diagonal entry is positive, including when dj​=0. Multiplying by the inverse diagonal matrix gives
proxγE​(z)=QTdiag(1+γdj​1​)(Qz+γDTy).​
(31)
The proximal operator of Poisson data fidelity separates pointwise. At each t, the scalar optimality equation on x>0 is
x−z+γ(1−xy​)=0,x2+(γ−z)x−γy=0.
(32)
Since y>0, the two roots have opposite signs. Only the positive root is admissible in the logarithm:
proxγE​(z)(t)=2z(t)−γ+(z(t)−γ)2+4γy(t)​​.​
(33)
The negative root is outside the effective domain. The second derivative 1+γy/x2 of the proximal objective is positive, establishing the unique pointwise minimizer. The integral formula is understood on the function spaces where these quantities are admissible.
Finally, for E(x)=∣x∣, positive and negative minimizers obey x−z+γ=0 and x−z−γ=0, respectively. At zero the subdifferential condition is z∈[−γ,γ]. Combining these cases yields soft thresholding:
proxγ∣⋅∣​(z)=sgn(z)max{∣z∣−γ,0}.​
(34)
Thus γ=1 supplies all three requested closed forms.

Take F=(F∗)∗ with the usual proper closed convex assumptions, so the Fenchel-Moreau theorem identifies its conjugate with the supplied F∗. Put zk=uk−τDTvk. The first update is wk+1=proxF∗/τ​(zk/τ). By Moreau decomposition, the second update is
uk+1=zk−τproxF∗/τ​(zk/τ)=proxτF​(zk).
(36)
Also τ(DTvk+wk+1)=uk−uk+1, so the argument in the final update contains uk+1−τ(DTvk+wk+1)=2uk+1−uk. Eliminating the auxiliary variable gives
uk+1vk+1​=proxτF​(uk−τDTvk),=proxσG​(vk+σD(2uk+1−uk)).​​
(37)
This is the primal-dual hybrid gradient method with extrapolation parameter one and the primal update performed first. Its saddle point function is F(u)+⟨Du,v⟩−G(v), corresponding to the primal objective F(u)+G∗(Du).
For proper lower semicontinuous convex functions and a nonempty saddle-point set in these finite-dimensional spaces, the standard convergence of primal-dual hybrid gradient theorem gives the sufficient parameter condition
τ>0,σ>0,τσ∥D∥22​<1.​
(38)
Here ∥D∥2​ is the operator norm, or largest singular value. For D=0, one possible choice is τ=σ=η/∥D∥2​ with 0<η<1; for D=0 any positive steps satisfy the condition. The existence assumption is necessary: step sizes alone cannot guarantee convergence to a saddle point that does not exist.
