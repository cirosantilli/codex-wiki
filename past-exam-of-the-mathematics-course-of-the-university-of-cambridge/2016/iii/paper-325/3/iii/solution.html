Write the objective as Fa​(x)=αx2/2+n−1∑i​ℓi​(x) with ℓi​(x)=max{0,1−ai​x}. Each hinge loss is finite and convex, so the subdifferential sum rule applies without a qualification gap. Its subdifferential is
∂ℓi​(x)=⎩⎨⎧​{−ai​},{0},{−λi​ai​:0≤λi​≤1},​1−ai​x>0,1−ai​x<0,1−ai​x=0.​
(40)
This formula also covers ai​=0, if such a sample is allowed, because the loss is then constant. For a convex function, zero in the subdifferential is necessary and sufficient for global optimality. Thus x is optimal exactly when there are weights λi​ such that
αx=n1​i=1∑n​λi​ai​,λi​=⎩⎨⎧​1,0,any value in [0,1],​ai​x<1,ai​x>1,ai​x=1.​​
(41)
Equivalently, using I(x)={i:ai​x<1} and J(x)={i:ai​x=1},
αnx∈∑i∈I(x)​ai​+∑i∈J(x)​[0,1]ai​.
(42)
The notation [0,1]ai​ denotes the segment between 0 and ai​, also when ai​<0. This avoids reversing interval endpoints for negative-class samples.
Because α>0, the objective is strongly convex, and Fa​(x)≥αx2/2 makes it coercive. It is continuous, so a minimizer exists and is unique for every finite data vector a. The weights at exact margins need not be unique even though x is. These are the hinge-loss optimality weights for the one-dimensional support vector machine.
