Over the complex numbers, every finite-dimensional representation of a solvable Lie algebra has a basis in which every representing matrix is upper triangular. The Lie theorem is often stated first as the existence of a common eigenvector in every nonzero finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation of a solvable Lie algebra. Applying that assertion successively to quotient representations gives an invariant complete flag, and hence the upper triangular form. The same proof works over any algebraically closed field of characteristic zero.
We prove the common eigenvector assertion by induction on dimg, writing the action as av. The zero Lie algebra is immediate. If g=0 is solvable, its derived series of a Lie algebra shows that [g,g]=g. Choose a codimension-one ideal of a Lie algebra a containing [g,g], and choose x∈/a. By induction there are v=0 and a linear functional λ:a→C such that av=λ(a)v for all a∈a.
Let W be the span of v,xv,x2v,…. If m=dimW, the first m of these vectors form a basis, and W is x-invariant. We claim that for each a∈a,
axjv=λ(a)xjv+span{v,xv,…,xj−1v}.
(1)
For j=0 this is the definition of v. For the induction step, use ax=xa+[a,x] and [a,x]∈a. Applying the induction hypothesis to both a and [a,x] proves the claim. Consequently W is a-invariant, and every a∈a acts on W by an upper triangular matrix with all diagonal entries λ(a).
Since both x and a preserve W, the matrix trace of their commutator on W is zero. The claim applied to [x,a]∈a gives
0=trW​[x,a]=mλ([x,a]).
(2)
Here characteristic zero is essential: m=0 in the field, so λ([x,a])=0. Now the common weight space
Eλ​={w∈V:aw=λ(a)w for all a∈a}
(3)
is nonzero and x-invariant. Indeed, for w∈Eλ​,
a(xw)=x(aw)+[a,x]w=λ(a)xw+λ([a,x])w=λ(a)xw.
(4)
An endomorphism of a nonzero finite-dimensional complex vector space has an eigenvector, so choose an eigenvector of x in Eλ​. It is a common eigenvector for g=a+Cx. This proves the Lie theorem.
For the printed matrices in characteristic p, [x,y]=x, and there is no common eigenvector. Index the standard basis by e0​,…,ep−1​. The cyclic entry in the PDF gives
xej​=ej−1​(j modulo p),yej​=jej​.
(5)
The p diagonal eigenvalues of y are distinct in k. Thus every eigenvector of y is a scalar multiple of a single ej​. Since p≥2, xej​ is never a scalar multiple of ej​, proving the assertion even when k is not an algebraically closed field.
For 1≤j≤p−1,
[x,y]ej​=jej−1​−(j−1)ej−1​=ej−1​.
(6)
At the cyclic boundary,
[x,y]e0​=−(p−1)ep−1​=ep−1​.
(7)
Hence [x,y]=x. The two-dimensional Lie subalgebra kx+ky has derived algebra kx, whose own derived algebra is zero, so it is solvable. It nevertheless has no common eigenvector, including after extending k to its algebraic closure. This is a failure of Lie theorem in positive characteristic. In the proof above, the obstruction is precisely that dimW=p can vanish as a scalar in k.
The derived algebra of a complex solvable Lie algebra is nilpotent. First suppose g⊆gl(V). By the Lie theorem, put every element of g in upper triangular form. The diagonal of a commutator of upper triangular matrices is zero, so d=[g,g] consists of strictly upper triangular matrices. The Lie algebra of all such matrices is a nilpotent Lie algebra: if Fr​ consists of matrices whose entries vanish whenever j−i<r, then
[Fr​,Fs​]⊆Fr+s​,FdimV​=0.
(8)
Therefore the lower central series of a Lie algebra of d reaches zero. Alternatively, every element of d is a nilpotent linear map, and the Engel theorem states that a finite-dimensional Lie subalgebra consisting of nilpotent linear maps is a nilpotent Lie algebra.
For an abstract complex solvable Lie algebra g, apply the preceding result to its Adjoint representation. The Lie algebra add=[adg,adg] is nilpotent. Since ker(ad∣d​)=d∩Z(g) is central in d, some term of the lower central series of a Lie algebra of d lies in that central ideal; the next term is zero. Thus d itself is nilpotent.
Conversely, every nilpotent Lie algebra is solvable, since its derived series of a Lie algebra is contained term by term in its lower central series of a Lie algebra. If d is nilpotent, it is therefore solvable, and g/d is Abelian. More directly, the derived series of a Lie algebra of g, after its first term, is the derived series of a Lie algebra of d. Consequently the derived algebra nilpotence criterion is
g solvable⟺[g,g] nilpotent.​
(9)

The form attached to V is BV​(x,y)=trV​(xy). More generally, for a Lie algebra representation ρ, the Trace form of a Lie algebra representation is
BV​(x,y)=trV​(ρ(x)ρ(y)).
(10)

B(x,y)=trg​(adxady).
(11)
The distinction matters: a Trace form of a Lie algebra representation can be degenerate even when g is semisimple, for example on the trivial Lie algebra representation.
The Trace form of a Lie algebra representation is bilinear and symmetric, because tr(AB)=tr(BA). It is an invariant bilinear form on a Lie algebra:
BV​([x,y],z)=BV​(x,[y,z]).
(12)

BV​([x,y],z)+BV​(y,[x,z])=0.
(13)

On a complex finite-dimensional Lie algebra, the Cartan criterion for semisimplicity says that the Killing form is nondegenerate exactly when the Lie algebra is semisimple. Schur's lemma says that a nonzero intertwiner between irreducible representations is an isomorphism; over C, every endomorphism of a finite-dimensional irreducible representation is scalar. To prove the Schur lemma, let T:V→W be a intertwining operator between irreducible representations. Its kernel and image of a linear map are invariant. If T=0, irreducibility forces kerT=0 and imT=W. This proves the first assertion over any field. In particular the endomorphisms of an irreducible representation form a division ring.
When V is finite-dimensional over an algebraically closed field, an endomorphism T has an eigenvalue λ. The endomorphism T−λI has nonzero kernel. By the first assertion it must be zero, so T=λI. Consequently, for complex finite-dimensional irreducible representations, the space of intertwining operators has dimension zero for nonisomorphic representations and dimension one for isomorphic representations.
Every finite-dimensional representation of a complex semisimple Lie algebra is completely reducible. We prove the Weyl complete reducibility theorem using the allowed Casimir operator facts, without assuming a splitting in advance. For dual bases xi​,xi with respect to the Killing form, the Casimir element
Ω=∑i​xi​xi∈U(g)
(27)
is central in the universal enveloping algebra. Hence its Casimir operator CM​=∑i​ρM​(xi​)ρM​(xi) commutes with the action on every Lie algebra representation M and is compatible with subrepresentations, quotient representations, and intertwining operators. On the trivial Lie algebra representation it acts by zero. On every nontrivial finite-dimensional Irreducible Lie algebra representation it acts by a nonzero scalar.
For clarity, the last fact can be expressed by the Casimir eigenvalue formula: on an irreducible with dominant integral weight λ, the scalar is (λ,λ+2ρ), with the inner product induced by the Killing form and ρ the half-sum of positive roots. On the real span of the weights this inner product is positive definite, and the scalar is positive for λ=0. For a semisimple Lie algebra with several simple factors the scalars add, so a nontrivial representation still gives a nonzero scalar. These are properties of the Casimir operator being used here.
First establish Casimir splitting of a trivial quotient. Suppose
0⟶N⟶E⟶q​C⟶0
(28)
is a short exact sequence of finite-dimensional Lie algebra representations, with trivial quotient. The generalized eigenspaces of CE​ are invariant, so
E=E0​⊕⨁c=0​Ec​.
(29)
Each Ec​ with c=0 maps to zero under q: applying a sufficiently large power of CE​−cI and using CC​=0 gives (−c)rq(e)=0. Thus q(E0​)=C.
Take a composition series of a module of E0​. The Casimir operator is nilpotent on E0​, so its scalar on every irreducible composition factor is zero. The stated Casimir operator property makes every such factor trivial. In a basis adapted to the composition series of a module, the image of g on E0​ therefore consists of strictly upper triangular matrices, so that image is solvable. The allowed fact that a semisimple Lie algebra acts trivially on every one-dimensional representation implies that g is a perfect Lie algebra: otherwise a nonzero linear functional on g/[g,g] would define a nontrivial one-dimensional Lie algebra representation. Thus g=[g,g], and its image is consequently a perfect Lie algebra too. A perfect Lie algebra that is also a solvable Lie algebra is zero, since its derived series of a Lie algebra is constant until it vanishes. Hence g acts trivially on E0​. Choose e∈E0​ with q(e)=1. The map 1↦e is an invariant section, proving the split short exact sequence assertion.
Now let W⊆V be any nonzero subrepresentation. On the Hom representation Hom(V,W) the action is
(x⋅f)(v)=xf(v)−f(xv).
(30)
Consider the invariant subspace
E={f∈Hom(V,W):f∣W​∈CIW​}.
(31)
Restriction produces a short exact sequence
0⟶Hom(V/W,W)⟶E⟶CIW​⟶0.
(32)

V=W⊕kerp,
(33)
and kerp is invariant. The zero subrepresentation also has a complement. Repeatedly splitting off an irreducible subrepresentation now gives a direct sum of irreducibles, proving the Weyl complete reducibility theorem.
A derivation of a Lie algebra is a linear map D:g→g satisfying the Leibniz rule
D[x,y]=[Dx,y]+[x,Dy].
(34)
The space Der(g) is a vector subspace of End(g). Equip it with the commutator [D,E]=DE−ED. Expanding the Leibniz rule twice gives
DE[x,y]ED[x,y]​=[DEx,y]+[Ex,Dy]+[Dx,Ey]+[x,DEy],=[EDx,y]+[Dx,Ey]+[Ex,Dy]+[x,EDy].​
(35)
Subtracting proves that [D,E] is again a derivation of a Lie algebra. Antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity hold for the commutator in every associative endomorphism algebra, so this defines the derivation Lie algebra.
The Jacobi identity says that adx is a derivation of a Lie algebra. Moreover, for D∈Der(g),
[D,adx](y)=D[x,y]−[x,Dy]=[Dx,y],
(36)
so
[D,adx]=ad(Dx).​
(37)
This makes adg an ideal of a Lie algebra in Der(g).
Finally suppose g is semisimple. Let g act on Der(g) by x⋅D=[adx,D]. The Weyl complete reducibility theorem supplies an invariant complement M to adg. For D∈M, invariance gives [adx,D]∈M, while the ideal identity above gives [adx,D]∈adg. Their intersection is zero, so ad(Dx)=0 for every x. The center of a Lie algebra of a semisimple Lie algebra is zero, hence Dx=0 for every x, and M=0. Therefore
Der(g)=adg.​
(38)
Every derivation of a Lie algebra is inner, and the element giving it is unique because the center of a Lie algebra vanishes.

An abstract root system, in the reduced crystallographic convention, is a finite subset Φ of a finite-dimensional real Euclidean space E satisfying the following conditions:
  • 0∈/Φ and Φ spans E.
  • For each α∈Φ, Φ∩Rα={α,−α}; this is the reduced condition.
  • The root reflection sα​(v)=v−2(v,α)α/(α,α) preserves Φ.
  • Every Cartan integer ⟨β,α∨⟩=2(β,α)/(α,α) is an integer.
The root system is irreducible if it is not a union of two nonempty mutually orthogonal root systems. The integrality condition is essential for the finite angle list below; arbitrary finite reflection systems need not satisfy it.
The possible angles are 0, π/6, π/4, π/3, π/2, 2π/3, 3π/4, 5π/6, and π. For roots α,β, put
a=(α,α)2(β,α)​,b=(β,β)2(α,β)​.
(55)

(56)

Now suppose two simple roots α,β have angle 5π/6. Distinct simple roots have nonpositive inner product. Here is a proof from their defining sign property: if (δ,ϵ)>0, then sδ​(ϵ)=ϵ−nδ with a positive Cartan integer n. This would be a root whose coefficients in the root basis include both a positive and a negative coefficient, which is impossible. Therefore, after normalizing the simple roots to unit length, any nonzero off-diagonal inner product is at most −1/2, by the angle list.

Indeed, the root reflections in α,β preserve their plane and fix its orthogonal complement; the root reflections in the other simple roots preserve that complement and fix the plane. The given facts that these reflections generate the Weyl group and that every root is Weyl group-conjugate to a simple root imply that every root lies entirely in one of those two orthogonal subspaces. If there were any other simple root, this would make Φ reducible. Therefore irreducibility forces rank two.
Up to interchanging the simple roots, take α short and β long. The squared-length ratio is 3. Choose a scale and orthonormal coordinates so that
α=(1,0),β=(−23​,23​​).
(59)
The Cartan integers are ⟨β,α∨⟩=−3 and ⟨α,β∨⟩=−1. In coefficient coordinates mα+nβ, the two root reflections are
sα​(m,n)=(−m+3n,n),sβ​(m,n)=(m,m−n).
(60)
Their orbits of α and β are, respectively,
{±α,±(α+β),±(2α+β)},{±β,±(3α+β),±(3α+2β)}.
(61)
These sets are closed under both displayed reflections; for example sβ​(α)=α+β, sα​(α+β)=2α+β, sα​(β)=3α+β, and sβ​(3α+β)=3α+2β. The assumption that every root is conjugate to a simple root now determines all twelve roots, with no further ones possible.
For completeness, this twelve-element set really is a root system. It is finite, spans the plane, and is reduced. The six short roots have length 1 and directions spaced by π/3; the six long roots have length 3​ and interleaved directions, also spaced by π/3. Their mutual Cartan integers are integral: within either length class they are 0, ±1, or ±2 as applicable, and between the two length classes they are 0, ±1, or ±3. Closure under all root reflections follows by conjugating the two simple reflections, since every listed root lies in a simple-root orbit. The nonorthogonal simple roots make it irreducible. We have therefore proved existence as well as uniqueness, up to isometry and overall scale:
The unique irreducible type is the G2​ root system.​
(62)

A smooth vector field on a smooth manifold M is a smooth section of the tangent bundle: it assigns v(p)∈Tp​M to each p, smoothly in local coordinates. In a coordinate chart it has the form v=∑i​ai​∂/∂xi​, with smooth coefficients ai​. Equivalently, it acts on smooth functions as a derivation, v(fh)=v(f)h+fv(h).
For a Lie group G, write La​(h)=ah for Left translation on a Lie group. A left-invariant vector field satisfies
(dLa​)h​v(h)=v(ah)(a,h∈G).
(63)
Thus it is determined by its value at the identity. Given the printed X∈Tg​G, first translate it to the identity:
ξ=(dLg−1​)g​X∈Te​G.
(64)
The unique left-invariant vector field with value X at g is
vX​(h)=(dLh​)e​ξ=(dLhg−1​)g​X.​
(65)
The smoothness of multiplication makes this a smooth vector field, the chain rule proves left invariance, and setting h=g gives vX​(g)=X.
We prove the completeness of left-invariant vector fields: this left-invariant vector field is a complete vector field. The Picard-Lindelöf theorem, applied in a local chart, gives a unique local integral curve of a vector field u:(−ε,ε)→G through e. For every h∈G, the curve t↦hu(t) is an integral curve of a vector field through h, because
dtd​(hu(t))=(dLh​)u(t)​vX​(u(t))=vX​(hu(t)).
(66)
Crucially, the same interval (−ε,ε) works for every initial point.
Let γ be the maximal integral curve of a vector field through g, with maximal interval (a,b). If b<∞, choose t0​∈(a,b) with b−t0​<ε/2. The curve
t⟼γ(t0​)u(t−t0​)
(67)
exists on (t0​−ε,t0​+ε) and agrees with γ on the overlap by uniqueness of the local ordinary differential equation. It extends γ past b, a contradiction. The same argument at a finite a excludes that possibility. Thus (a,b)=R, and uniqueness on overlapping intervals gives uniqueness on all of R.
After establishing completeness, uniqueness also gives the one-parameter subgroup law for the global curve through e:
u(s+t)=u(s)u(t).
(68)
Both sides, as curves in t, are integral curves of a vector field through u(s) at t=0. Defining the Exponential map of a Lie group by u(t)=exp(tξ), the answer is
γg​(t)=gexp(tξ),ξ=(dLg−1​)g​X,t∈R.​
(69)
The identity component G∘ is an open normal subgroup, and every open identity neighbourhood generates it. Let G∘ be the connected component of e. The product of connected spaces is connected, so the image of G∘×G∘ under multiplication is connected and contains e. It is therefore contained in G∘. Inversion has the same property. Thus G∘ is a subgroup.
A smooth manifold is locally connected. In particular, a coordinate neighbourhood of e can be chosen homeomorphic to an open ball, so there is a connected open neighbourhood O of e contained in G∘. Its translates hO, h∈G∘, are open and lie in G∘, and cover G∘. Therefore the identity component of a Lie group is open in G. Conjugation by any a∈G is a homeomorphism fixing e, so it maps G∘ into itself; conjugation by a−1 gives the reverse inclusion. Hence G∘ is a normal subgroup.
If U is an open neighbourhood of e in G∘, let H=⟨U⟩, allowing inverses in the meaning of generated subgroup. For each h∈H, hU is open and lies in H, so H=⋃h∈H​hU is open in G∘. Every other left coset of H is also open. Thus H is both open and closed in the connected space G∘. It is nonempty, so H=G∘.
A quadratic form on V=Rn is a function Q(v)=B(v,v) for a symmetric bilinear form B. Equivalently, Q(tv)=t2Q(v) and the polarization
B(u,v)=2Q(u+v)−Q(u)−Q(v)​
(70)
is a bilinear map. In coordinates there is a unique real symmetric matrix A with Q(v)=vTAv. No assumption of nondegeneracy or positive definiteness is needed.
An element g∈GLn​(R) stabilizes Q when Q(gv)=Q(v) for every v. By the polarization identity, this is equivalent to preserving B, or in coordinates to
GQ​={g∈GLn​(R):gTAg=A}.
(71)
This is a closed Matrix Lie group. The Lie algebra of a quadratic-form stabilizer is
Te​GQ​={X∈Mn​(R):XTA+AX=0}.​
(72)
To prove necessity, differentiate g(t)TAg(t)=A along any smooth curve in GQ​ with g(0)=I, g′(0)=X. The derivative at zero is XTA+AX.
To prove sufficiency, suppose XTA+AX=0 and consider the matrix exponential g(t)=etX. Then
dtd​(etXTAetX)=etXT(XTA+AX)etX=0.
(73)
Its value at zero is A, so etX∈GQ​ for every real t. This curve has derivative X at zero, proving the claimed tangent space description even for a degenerate quadratic form.
Equivalently, the condition is B(Xu,v)+B(u,Xv)=0 for all u,v. For a nondegenerate quadratic form it is the corresponding Special orthogonal Lie algebra. To see the degenerate case explicitly, choose a basis with
A=(D0​00​),D=diag(Ir​,−Is​).
(74)

PTD+DP=0,R=0,
(75)
while S,T are arbitrary. Thus the nullspace of the quadratic form is invariant, but arbitrary infinitesimal maps into it are allowed. When A=0, the formula correctly gives GQ​=GLn​(R) and Te​GQ​=Mn​(R).
