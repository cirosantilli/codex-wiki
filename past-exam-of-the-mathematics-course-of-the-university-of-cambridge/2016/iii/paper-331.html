Let η​ be the displacement of a material interface and use [f]−+​=f+​−f−​. On each side, the kinematic boundary condition is

Take k>0, without loss for this symmetric real base state, and write s=1+J, e=e−2α, and y=c2. The given quartic becomes a real quadratic y2+Ay+B=0. Its constant term factors as
B=4α2[2α−s(1+e)][2α−s(1−e)]​.
(22)
Therefore B<0 exactly when
1+e−2α2α​<1+J<1−e−2α2α​.
(23)
A negative product means the two quadratic roots are real and of opposite sign: the discriminant is A2−4B>0. The negative root gives a pair c=±i−y−​​. One sign has positive temporal growth rate kci​ and is unstable. Using the definitions of the hyperbolic functions gives the required unstable band:
coshααeα​<1+J<sinhααeα​.​
(24)
This proves the unstable band of a three-layer stratified shear flow directly, without needing to solve all four quartic roots.
For the wave interpretation, consider either interface in isolation with decaying normal modes w=w0​e−k∣z−zi​∣. Put S=ΔU/h, Ui​=U(zi​), and v=c−Ui​, the intrinsic phase velocity. At either interface [ρ​]=−Δρ/2, while [U′]=−S at the upper interface and +S at the lower one. The jump conditions for stratified inviscid shear flow give the gravity-vorticity interface wave relation
2kv2−[U′]v−2ρ0​gΔρ​=0.
(25)
For stable density jumps, J≥0, both intrinsic branches are real. In units C=2c/ΔU, the isolated wave speeds are
Cu​=1+4α−1±1+8αJ​​,Cl​=−1+4α1±1+8αJ​​.
(26)
The upper wave travelling against its positive background current has Cu​=1−[1+1+8αJ​]/(4α). The lower wave travelling against its negative background current has the opposite speed, Cl​=−Cu​. Their counterpropagating wave resonance in a three-layer shear flow occurs at Cu​=Cl​=0, which gives
1+J=2αfor the isolated-wave resonance.​
(27)
At large wavenumber, coupling across the separation h is exponentially weak, of order e−kh=e−2α. The unstable band becomes
1+J=2α+O(αe−2α),
(28)
with full width 4αe−2α+O(αe−6α). It is a counterpropagating wave instability: the two waves propagate oppositely relative to their local currents but have almost equal laboratory phase velocities, allowing weak coupling to lock their phases and extract mean-flow energy. The unstable band of a three-layer stratified shear flow thus becomes narrowly concentrated around this resonance. For fixed J, arbitrarily large k lies outside that band; large-k resonance requires J to grow with α.

In the linear complex Ginzburg-Landau equation, take U,cd​,μ real. U gives advection, carrying the envelope downstream at speed U. cd​ gives wave dispersion: the imaginary part of the second-derivative coefficient changes wave phase without directly dissipating a real-wavenumber Fourier mode. μ gives local linear production or damping: a spatially uniform perturbation grows for μ>0 and decays for μ<0.
The real part of the diffusion coefficient is the scaled value one. It smooths the envelope and damps a real-wavenumber Fourier mode at rate k2. Thus the linear complex Ginzburg-Landau equation models competing advection, smoothing through a diffusion equation, wave dispersion and local growth, rather than nonlinear saturation.

Both eigenvalues of the linear generator, −1/R and −1/(4R), are negative. Nevertheless the non-normal matrix transfers the slowly decaying second component into the first before viscous decay wins. Modal decay coexists with energy amplification of order R2 over a time of order R.
This illustrates transient growth from non-normal modes: large Reynolds number permits much greater disturbance amplification even though the linear stability analysis finds no unstable eigenvalue. The energy-neutral rotational nonlinearity can redirect the amplified state and affect its subsequent evolution. The linear gain alone establishes neither sustained nonlinear instability nor a nonlinear transition threshold.
