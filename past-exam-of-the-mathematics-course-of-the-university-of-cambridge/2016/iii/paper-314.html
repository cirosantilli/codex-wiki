Take the primitive state vector U=(ρ,p,ux​,uy​,uz​,Bx​,By​,Bz​)T, with ρ>0. In the absence of gravity, the ideal magnetohydrodynamic equations are first order: expanding the material derivatives, pressure gradient and Lorentz force makes every term linear in a spatial derivative of U, with coefficients depending on U. Dividing momentum balance by ρ therefore gives a quasilinear partial differential equation system ∂t​U+Ai​(U)∂i​U=0 with eight-by-eight matrices. The solenoidal magnetic-field constraint is an additional constraint on initial data; the ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation preserves it because the divergence of a curl vanishes.
For a one-dimensional simple wave in magnetohydrodynamics, write U=U(q(x,t)) with a nonzero state-space tangent. Substitution gives Uq​qt​+Ax​Uq​qx​=0. A nonconstant profile requires the tangent to be a right eigenvector of Ax​:
Ax​Uq​=v(q)Uq​,qt​+v(q)qx​=0.
(1)
The chain rule then gives the wave-speed equation:
vt​+vvx​=vq​(qt​+vqx​)=0.​
(2)
This is the Inviscid Burgers equation, including the special case of constant v. Its characteristic curves are x=a+v0​(a)t, with v=v0​(a). As long as the mapping remains invertible,
vx​=1+tv0′​(a)v0′​(a)​.
(3)
Thus a region with v0′​<0 steepens: faster characteristics catch slower ones, and the first gradient catastrophe occurs at t∗​=−1/mina​v0′​(a) when the minimum is negative. A genuinely nonlinear compressive simple wave therefore forms a shock wave. Rarefactive profiles can spread instead; steepening is not inevitable for every initial profile.
To continue past this time, use weak solutions of the conservative mass, momentum, fluid total-energy equation and ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation. The Rankine-Hugoniot conditions fix the jumps and shock velocity, while physical entropy production selects admissible magnetohydrodynamic shocks. Diffusive shock wave layers are replaced by moving discontinuities, so their microscopic structure need not be explicitly resolved. In particular, the smooth adiabatic pressure equation must be replaced by total-energy conservation when imposing the ideal magnetohydrodynamic shock conditions.
For the Alfvén waves, impose the one-dimensional solenoidal magnetic-field constraint, so Bx​ is constant and δBx​=0. Put B⊥​=(By​,Bz​) and c=v−ux​. The transverse components of the linearized ideal magnetohydrodynamic equations give
−cδu⊥​−μ0​ρBx​​δB⊥​=0,−cδB⊥​−Bx​δu⊥​=0.
(4)
The longitudinal momentum equation additionally gives δp+B⊥​⋅δB⊥​/μ0​=0. The Alfvén eigenvectors have δρ=δp=δux​=0, hence their transverse polarization is perpendicular to B⊥​. For s=±1, their speed and explicit right Alfvén characteristic eigenvectors are
vs​=ux​+sμ0​ρ​Bx​​,rs​=​000hy​hz​0−sμ0​ρ​hy​−sμ0​ρ​hz​​​,By​hy​+Bz​hz​=0.​
(5)
For B⊥​=0 one may choose (hy​,hz​)=(Bz​,−By​), up to a nonzero scalar factor. If B⊥​=0, either transverse polarization is allowed and this characteristic speed is degenerate. These expressions describe the two propagating Alfvén branches for Bx​=0; if Bx​=0, they coalesce with advected, nonpropagating transverse disturbances.
Integrating along an Alfvén simple wave leaves ρ,p,ux​,Bx​ unchanged and gives du⊥​=−sdB⊥​/μ0​ρ​ and B⊥​⋅dB⊥​=0. Consequently the finite-amplitude nonlinear Alfvén wave relations are
f1​=Cy​−μ0​ρ​sf3​​,f2​=Cz​−μ0​ρ​sf4​​,f32​+f42​=B⊥2​=constant,v=vs​.​
(6)
The constant-magnitude condition is essential: otherwise a varying magnetic pressure would drive longitudinal compression. An arbitrary smooth phase profile gives an explicit family, f3​=B⊥​cosΘ(x−vs​t) and f4​=B⊥​sinΘ(x−vs​t), with f1​,f2​ as above. Direct substitution in the transverse momentum and ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation gives (ux​−vs​)u⊥′​=Bx​B⊥′​/(μ0​ρ) and (ux​−vs​)B⊥′​=Bx​u⊥′​, confirming the solution without relying on the eigenvector argument. Longitudinal momentum holds because p+B⊥2​/(2μ0​) is constant. The speed vs​ is independent of amplitude along this family: these linearly degenerate characteristic fields translate without steepening.

Let e be specific internal energy, Q the heat supplied per unit volume per unit time, and E=ρ(e+∣u∣2/2). For an inviscid perfect gas in a prescribed Newtonian gravitational potential Φ, the fluid total-energy equation is
∂t​E+∇⋅[(E+p)u]=−ρu⋅∇Φ+Q.
(7)
There is no gravitational term if no external body is present. For a time-independent Φ, adding potential energy gives the equivalent conservative form ∂t​(E+ρΦ)+∇⋅[(E+p+ρΦ)u]=Q. Subtracting the kinetic-energy equation and using mass conservation gives
ρDtDe​=−p∇⋅u+Q.
(8)
For a perfect gas with constant specific-heat ratio γ, e=p/[(γ−1)ρ]. The isothermal equation of state therefore makes e=cs2​/(γ−1) constant. Hence the required heat supply and heat loss are
Q=p∇⋅u,L=−Q=−p∇⋅u.​
(9)
Compression requires cooling; expansion requires heating. The isothermal sound speed cs​ differs from the adiabatic sound speed γ​cs​.
Define the isothermal Mach numbers by Mi​=ui​/cs​. Orient the normal to a stationary isothermal shock along the flow, so the mass flux j=ρ1​u1​=ρ2​u2​>0. Tangential velocity is continuous for this planar hydrodynamic shock. Normal momentum conservation gives
ρ1​u12​+cs2​ρ1​=ρ2​u22​+cs2​ρ2​,u1​+u1​cs2​​=u2​+u2​cs2​​.
(10)
For a genuine discontinuity u1​=u2​, factorization gives u1​u2​=cs2​. Thus the isothermal shock jump relations are
ρ1​ρ2​​=u2​u1​​=cs2​u12​​=M12​=M22​1​,M1​M2​=1.​
(11)
Across a thin shock, gravitational potential and tangential kinetic energy are unchanged. The specific enthalpy h=γcs2​/(γ−1) is the same on both sides, so the energy removed per unit area per unit time is
FE,1​−FE,2​=2j​(u12​−u22​).
(12)
Positive net cooling requires u1​>u2​, equivalently ρ2​>ρ1​ and M1​>1>M2​. Cooling across an isothermal shock permits only compression shocks; the formal expansion discontinuity requires energy supplied by the surroundings. The continuous state u1​=u2​ is not a shock.
For steady radial flow, mass conservation gives r2ρur​=constant. Differentiating it and eliminating the mass density gradient from radial momentum balance gives
(ur​−ur​cs2​​)drdur​​=r2cs2​​−r2GM​.
(13)
A regular sonic point has ∣ur​∣=cs​, so the right-hand side must vanish there as well. Therefore
rs​=2cs2​GM​.​
(14)
This is the Bondi sonic point for accretion; the same critical radius appears in an isothermal Parker wind. A general steady solution need not pass through a sonic point, but a smooth transonic one must obey both critical conditions.
Put x=r/rs​ and M=∣ur​∣/cs​ on a branch whose flow direction is fixed. The radial equation becomes
(M−M1​)dxdM​=x2​−x22​.
(15)
Integrating yields the integrated isothermal Bondi flow relation:
21​M2−lnM=x2​+2lnx+C.​
(16)
For a transonic branch, x=M=1 gives C=−3/2. Expansion about the critical point gives (M−1)2=(x−1)2+O((x−1)3), with slope −1 for inflow supplied by gas at rest at infinity and slope +1 for a transonic outflow.
For Isothermal Bondi accretion, integrate radial momentum balance once more, using ur​→0 and ρ→ρ∞​ at infinity:
21​ur2​+cs2​lnρ∞​ρ​−rGM​=0.
(17)
At the Bondi sonic point, GM/rs​=2cs2​ and ur2​=cs2​, so ρs​=e3/2ρ∞​. The positive inward isothermal Bondi accretion rate is consequently
M˙=−4πr2ρur​=4πrs2​ρs​cs​=πe3/2cs3​G2M2ρ∞​​.​
(18)
The critical mass density and rate are fixed by the regular transonic solution and the specified reservoir at infinity; an arbitrary subsonic solution does not share this accretion rate.

Use Faraday's law ∂t​B=−∇×E and the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint ∇⋅B=0. In the nonrelativistic, single-fluid approximation, neglect Hall and other nonideal electromotive terms and use the moving-conductor moving-conductor Ohm law, E+u×B=J/σ. Infinite electrical conductivity with finite current gives E=−u×B, hence
∂t​B=∇×(u×B).​
(19)
This is the ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation. For comparison, neglecting displacement current in Ampère-Maxwell equation gives J=∇×B/μ0​; with uniform finite electrical conductivity it produces magnetic diffusion ηm​∇2B, where the magnetic diffusivity is ηm​=1/(μ0​σ). The ideal approximation requires a large magnetic Reynolds number UL/ηm​. Dropping displacement current is useful for this finite-conductivity comparison, but Faraday's law and the ideal Ohm relation already suffice for the ideal induction equation.
Expanding the curl and using the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint gives the material derivative form
DtDB​=(B⋅∇)u−B∇⋅u.
(20)
Combine this with mass conservation, Dρ/Dt=−ρ∇⋅u, to obtain
DtD​(ρB​)=(ρB​⋅∇)u.​
(21)
Now parametrize a material curve by a fixed label a: X(a,t) obeys ∂t​X=u(X,t). Differentiating with respect to a shows that its tangent ℓ=∂a​X evolves by Dℓ/Dt=(ℓ⋅∇)u. This is exactly the same linear ordinary differential equation as for B/ρ. Initially parallel tangents remain parallel by uniqueness, with a label-dependent proportionality factor constant along each particle trajectory. Thus magnetic field lines are transported as material curves, wherever the field and fluid flow are smooth and the field is nonzero. This is the field-line form of magnetic flux freezing.
For the flux statement, take a material surface X(a,b,t) and let ta​=∂a​X, tb​=∂b​X. Both tangents obey Dti​/Dt=(∂j​ui​)tj​. Its oriented material surface element is A=ta​×tb​dadb. Differentiating the cross product, rather than assuming its transport rule, gives
DtDAi​​=εijk​[(∂ℓ​uj​)taℓ​tbk​+taj​(∂ℓ​uk​)tbℓ​]dadb=(∂j​uj​)Ai​−(∂i​uj​)Aj​.
(22)
Equivalently, DA/Dt=(∇⋅u)A−(∇u)TA, with (∇u)ij​=∂j​ui​. Contracting this derived rule with the induction equation gives a pointwise cancellation:
DtD​(B⋅A)=[(∇u)B−B∇⋅u]⋅A+B⋅[(∇⋅u)A−(∇u)TA]=0.
(23)
Integrating over the fixed material labels therefore proves conservation of flux through an open material surface:
dtd​∫S(t)​B⋅dS=0.​
(24)
The surface need not be closed; its boundary is carried with the fluid. The result follows from material transport, rather than from the zero flux through a closed surface.
For a homologously shrinking cloud, write V∼L3 and keep its shape factors fixed. Conserved mass gives ρ∼M/L3; conserved magnetic flux gives B∼Φ/L2. Thus the gravitational and magnetic energies scale as
Wg​=−Cg​LGM2​,EB​=CB​μ0​LΦ2​,​
(25)
where Cg​,CB​>0 are dimensionless geometry factors. Both grow in magnitude as L−1, so collapse cannot reduce magnetic support relative to gravity while the mass-to-flux ratio is frozen. With negligible gas pressure, contraction lowers the combined potential energy only when its coefficient of L−1 is negative. Consequently a necessary critical mass-to-flux ratio condition is
ΦM​>μ0​G​λ​,λ=CB​/Cg​​.​
(26)
Here Φ denotes the magnitude of the conserved threading flux. The numerical coefficient depends on geometry and boundary conditions; the scaling argument does not determine it or make the condition sufficient in the presence of other support.
For adiabatic pressure support during gravitational collapse, p∝ργ∝L−3γ. The pressure-support scale is pV∝L3−3γ, so relative to either gravity or magnetic energy,
∣Wg​∣pV​ ∝ L4−3γ.​
(27)
Pressure becomes more important as L decreases if γ>4/3, equally important in scaling if γ=4/3, and less important if γ<4/3. In particular, a monatomic perfect gas with γ=5/3 becomes increasingly pressure supported. For isothermal pressure support during gravitational collapse, the isothermal equation of state gives p∝ρ∝L−3, so pV is constant and pV/∣Wg​∣∝L: isothermal pressure becomes less important during collapse. The same comparisons hold against magnetic support because its energy has the same L−1 scaling as gravity.

For the self-gravitating incompressible slab, let the fluid occupy −H<z<H, with mass density ρ0​=Σ/(2H) and a vacuum exterior. Poisson equation and hydrostatic equilibrium give
Φ0′​(z)=4πGρ0​z,p0​(z)=2πGρ02​(H2−z2),gs​=Φ0′​(H)=2πGΣ.
(28)
A constant ambient pressure could be added without changing the result. Choose perturbations proportional to eikx−iωt, using rotational symmetry in the horizontal plane to put the wavevector along x.
For the fluid displacement, the linearized momentum equation inside the uniform slab is −ω2ξ=−∇(p′/ρ0​+ϕ′). For ω=0 it follows that ξ=∇χ, with p′/ρ0​+ϕ′=ω2χ. Incompressibility gives ∇⋅ξ=0, hence
(∂z2​−k2)χ=0.​
(29)
Neutral limits of these surface modes follow by continuity. Stationary vorticity perturbations form a separate zero-frequency sector; the nonzero-frequency surface modes are irrotational by the momentum equation itself, without an extra irrotational-flow assumption.
The Eulerian mass density perturbation vanishes in the bulk because the equilibrium mass density is uniform and the displacement is divergence free. If the two vertical surface displacements are η+​=ξz​(H) and η−​=ξz​(−H), the surface density perturbation of a displaced uniform interface gives
ρ′=ρ0​η+​δ(z−H)−ρ0​η−​δ(z+H).
(30)
Thus Poisson equation reduces to Laplace equation for ϕ′ inside and outside. The potential is continuous, while integrating through each surface gives
[∂z​ϕ′]H−H+​=4πGρ0​η+​,[∂z​ϕ′]−H−−H+​=−4πGρ0​η−​.
(31)
The perturbing potential must decay at vertical infinity. The kinematic conditions are η±​=∂z​χ(±H). The free surface pressure condition is the vanishing Lagrangian pressure perturbation, p′+η±​p0′​=0; at the top this gives p′(H)/ρ0​=gs​η+​.
Put q=kH>0. For even scalar-potential symmetry, choose
χ=Acosh(kz),ϕin′​=Ccosh(kz),ϕout′​=Ccoshqe−k(∣z∣−H).
(32)
Then η−​=−η+​: this is a thickness-changing mode, not a vertical bending mode. Write η=η+​; the top derivative jump gives −kC(coshq+sinhq)=4πGρ0​η. Since coshq+sinhq=eq,
χ(H)=kη​cothq,ϕ′(H)=−k(1+tanhq)4πGρ0​η​.
(33)
Substitute these values into ω2χ(H)=gs​η+ϕ′(H). Using 4πGρ0​=gs​/H gives the even-mode dispersion relation for the surface modes of a self-gravitating incompressible slab:
ωe2​=H2πGΣ​[qtanhq−1+cothq1​].​
(34)
The positive term comes from the restoring hydrostatic free-surface pressure in the background gravitational field. The negative term is the attraction due to the perturbed gravitational potential of the displaced surfaces, which tends to reinforce the disturbance. Both effects ultimately involve the slab's gravity, but enter the boundary condition through different terms.
For odd scalar-potential symmetry, instead take
χ=Asinh(kz),ϕin′​=Csinh(kz),ϕout′​=Csinhqsgn(z)e−k(∣z∣−H).
(35)
Now η−​=η+​, so the surfaces bend in the same direction. The jump again gives C=−4πGρ0​η/(keq), but χ(H)=ηtanhq/k and ϕ′(H)=−4πGρ0​η/[k(1+cothq)]. The same pressure condition yields
ωo2​=H2πGΣ​[qcothq−1+tanhq1​].​
(36)
The bottom kinematic, jump and pressure conditions follow with the indicated parity and give the same dispersion relation, so no boundary condition has been discarded.
To determine the gravitational instability of an incompressible slab, factor the two dimensionless expressions as
Fe​(q)=tanhq[q−21+e−2q​],Fo​(q)=cothq[q−21−e−2q​].
(37)
For the even mode the bracket is strictly increasing, with derivative 1+e−2q>0, is negative at zero and tends to positive infinity. It therefore has a unique positive zero
qc​=21+e−2qc​​≃0.6392322714.​
(38)
The even mode is unstable for 0<kH<qc​, neutral at qc​, and oscillatory for kH>qc​. Negative ωe2​ gives a growing mode with ω=i−ωe2​​ under the stated time convention. For the odd mode, 1−e−2q<2q for every q>0, so the bracket is always positive: all odd modes with positive wavenumber are stable.
At long wavelengths Fe​=−q+2q2+O(q3) and Fo​=q−2q2/3+O(q3): a thickness or column-density disturbance has a gravitational instability, whereas a bending disturbance is restored. At short wavelengths both approach q−1/2, so both surface branches are stable. The slab equilibrium is therefore linearly unstable overall, because it admits the long-wavelength even modes despite the stability of its odd sector.
