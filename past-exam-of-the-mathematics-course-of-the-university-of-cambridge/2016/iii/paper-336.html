The expansion is singular near the moving lower limit, so expanding e−ε/x throughout the original integral would lose its constant term. Introduce the symmetric exponential integral
J(ε)=∫0∞​xe−x−ε/x​dx.
(1)
Under x=ε/t, the discarded piece becomes
J−I=∫1∞​te−t−ε/t​dt=β−ε∫1∞​t2e−t​dt+O(ε2)=β−ε(e−1−β)+O(ε2).
(2)
This is a uniform Taylor expansion because t≥1. To derive the logarithmic expansion of a symmetric exponential integral without needing another special function, first exploit the same substitution to write
J=2∫ε​∞​xe−x−ε/x​dx=2E(ε​)+O(ε​)=−logε−2γ+o(1).
(3)
The estimate follows from 0≤1−e−ε/x≤ε/x. Here γ is Euler's constant and the small-argument expansion of the exponential integral fixes the constant as well as the logarithm.
Differentiate under the integral and integrate the derivative of e−x−ε/x/x over (0,∞); the exponentially small endpoint terms vanish. This gives the ordinary differential equation
εJ′′+J′=J.
(4)
For the Frobenius method at its regular singular point, write J=Alogε+B+ε(C1​logε+D)+⋯. Substitution gives C1​=A and D=B−2A. The preceding leading calculation sets A=−1, B=−2γ, hence
J=−logε−2γ+ε[−logε+2−2γ]+O(ε2∣logε∣).
(5)
Subtracting the moving-cutoff correction to a symmetric exponential integral yields the requested answer, including the logarithmically enhanced first-order term:
I(ε)=−logε−2γ−β+ε[−logε+2−2γ+e−1−β]+O(ε2∣logε∣).​
(6)
Thus the εlogε switchback term must be retained when asking for all contributions through order ε.

Let b=eiθ and use the method of steepest descents with phase Φ(z)=−z2. For z=x+iy, ReΦ=y2−x2 and ImΦ=−2xy. The simple saddle point is z=0: the real axis is its descending path and the imaginary axis its ascending path. The quadratic complex endpoint descent path from b satisfies Im(z2)=sin2θ and can be parametrized by
z(s)2=b2+s,s≥0,
(10)
with the square-root branch chosen continuously from b. Increasing s reduces the real part of the exponent by λs.
For 0≤θ<π/2, that endpoint path goes to positive real infinity. Deform the original contour into the real path from −d through the saddle point towards +∞, followed backwards along the endpoint descent path to b. The integrand is an entire function, so finite contour deformations are legitimate; connecting arcs in the far decaying sector vanish. If Tb​ denotes the oriented integral from b to that infinity, this gives
f=λπ​​−Td​−Tb​,Td​∼2​λe−λ/2​,Tb​∼2λbe−λb2​.
(11)
The last expression follows by setting z2=b2+s: dz/ds=1/(2z), so the endpoint integral is e−λb2∫0∞​e−λs/[2z(s)]ds. This derives its phase and orientation directly.
For π/2<θ≤π, the descent path from b goes to negative real infinity. Deform from −d towards that infinity and then backwards along this endpoint path to b. There is no saddle point segment, and
f=−Td​−Tb​,Tb​∼2λbe−λb2​
(12)
uses the same formula with the negative square-root branch. At θ=π/2 the contour can run from −d to zero and then up the imaginary axis. It gives a half-Gaussian contribution and the dominant imaginary endpoint term ieλ/(2λ). The change in the subdominant saddle point contribution is a Stokes phenomenon; it does not change the leading endpoint term on either side of that angle.
The Gaussian saddle and endpoint dominance by angle follows by comparing exponential sizes and gives all fixed-angle leading behaviours:
f(θ,λ)∼⎩⎨⎧​π/λ​,−2λeiθe−λe2iθ​,−λe−λ/2​[2​1​+2e5πi/6ei3​λ/2​],−2​λe−λ/2​,​0≤θ≤π/4,π/4<θ<5π/6,θ=5π/6,5π/6<θ≤π.​​
(13)
At θ=π/4, the endpoint term has magnitude O(λ−1), so the saddle point term of order λ−1/2 still dominates. In the middle interval the endpoint term grows exponentially for π/4<θ<3π/4, is oscillatory of order λ−1 at 3π/4, and decays exponentially for 3π/4<θ<5π/6. On the latter interval it nevertheless decays more slowly than the fixed-endpoint term. Equality of their exponential rates means cos2θ=1/2 on the left half-plane, hence θ=5π/6. At that angle both terms are required; their coefficient magnitudes are unequal, so their sum has no complete destructive cancellation.
These fixed-angle asymptotic expansions are not uniform if θ is allowed to vary with λ near a dominance transition. The endpoint/saddle balance near π/4 involves λ(θ−π/4)=O(logλ); the two-endpoint competition near 5π/6 has an angular scale O(λ−1).

For the matched asymptotic expansion, first determine the outer expansion. Setting ε=0 away from x=0 gives y0′​=y0​, and the right boundary condition sets y0​=ex. At first order,
y1′​−y1​=−2xex​,y1​(1)=0,
(14)
so
yout​(x)=ex[1−2ε​logx]+O(ε2),x>0 fixed.​
(15)
There is no outer ε​ term: its homogeneous coefficient would be forced to zero by the right boundary condition. The logarithmic overlap forces a switchback term in the inner expansion.
Near zero, balance εy′′ against 2xy′ to get the square-root boundary layer at a vanishing drift:
s=ε​,x=sz,Y(z)=y(sz),Y′′+2zY′−2szY=0.
(16)
For fixed z expand Y=Y0​+sY1​+s2Y2​+⋯, allowing logarithmic dependence on s in Y2​. With L0​=∂z2​+2z∂z​, the leading equation is L0​Y0​=0. Its bounded solutions are a constant plus an error function. The left boundary condition and the overlap value one fix
Y0​(z)=erfz.​
(17)
The following two solution sections complete the half-order and first-order matching; their headings retain the scaffold's organization of the supplied hints.

Write E=erfz, g=e−z2 and b=2/π​. The first correction in the inner expansion satisfies L0​Y1​=2zE. Direct differentiation verifies that zE+bg is a particular solution. Adding the constant and error function homogeneous solutions gives Y1​=zE+bg+A+BE.
The left boundary condition forces A=−b. Re-expanding the outer expansion at x=sz gives
yout​(sz)=1+sz+s2[2z2​−21​logz−21​logs]+⋯.
(18)
Therefore Y1​∼z, with no constant term in its large-z overlap. Since E→1 and g→0, this sets B=b. Thus the required half-order correction is
Y1​(z)=zerfz+π​2​[e−z2+erfz−1].​
(19)
It vanishes at zero and has the correct linear overlap. Keeping this O(ε​) term is essential even though the outer solution has no correction of that order.

H(z)=∫0z​e−t2∫0t​eu2erf(u)dudt,H(z)∼21​logz+C.

The order-s2 equation in the inner expansion is L0​Y2​=2zY1​. The preceding result decomposes its forcing into 2z2E+2bzE−2bz+2bzg. The particular solutions combine to
Y2,p​=21​z2E−H+bz(E−1)+b2g.
(21)
For example, L0​H=E follows directly by differentiating the nested integral, so the equation can also be verified without relying on the supplied particular-solution formulas. At zero Y2,p​=b2; add −b2+DE to impose the left boundary condition. As z→∞ this gives
Y2​∼21​z2−21​logz−C−b2+D.
(22)
The outer expansion in the overlap, derived in the previous section, requires the remaining constant to be −21​logs. Thus D=C+b2−21​logs. The logarithmic matching constant is an error-function logarithmic switchback.
The complete answer through order ε in the inner expansion is
yin​(ε​z)=​E+ε​[zE+b(g+E−1)]+ε[21​z2E−H(z)+bz(E−1)+b2(g+E−1)+(C−41​logε)E]+O(ε3/2∣logε∣),z=O(1).​​
(23)
Here E=erfz, g=e−z2 and b=2/π​. This expression is zero at z=0, and its large-z expansion agrees with 1+x+x2/2−(ε/2)logx. Together with yout​=ex[1−(ε/2)logx], it covers both requested regions. The overlap uses 1≪z≪ε−1/2, with the usual termwise ordering restrictions when matching higher terms.

(24)
The apparent logarithmic divergence at x=0 cancels: its residual is −(ε/2)(ex−1)logx→0. The composite therefore extends continuously to the left endpoint and satisfies the right boundary condition to the requested order.

z=ε2x−ε​,W(z)=w(ε+ε2z),(1+εz)W′′+W′+2ε3(1+εz)W=0.

Use the geometrical optics convention
ω=−θT​,k=θX​,ℓ=θY​.​
(32)
Because ∂t​=ε∂T​ and ∂x​=ε∂X​, differentiating eiθ/ε gives an order-one physical angular frequency and order-one physical wavenumbers. Absorb a constant leading amplitude phase into θ and take A0​ real; for a complex amplitude the corresponding quadratic density is ∣A0​∣2.
Substitution into the wave equation gives the eikonal equation for a variable-speed wave at leading order:
ω2=c2(k2+ℓ2).​
(33)
Choose the positive-frequency branch ω=Ω=cK, K=k2+ℓ2​; the negative branch can be treated in exactly the same way. The imaginary first-order terms give
2ωA0,T​+ωT​A0​+2c2(kA0,X​+ℓA0,Y​)+[(c2k)X​+(c2ℓ)Y​]A0​=0.
(34)
Multiplying by A0​ puts this wave-action transport for a variable-speed wave in conservation form:
(ωA02​)T​+(c2kA02​)X​+(c2ℓA02​)Y​=0.​
(35)
Its density is ωA02​ and its flux is that density times the group velocity. Independently, equality of mixed wave phase derivatives gives
kY​=ℓX​​,kT​=−ωX​,ℓT​=−ωY​.
(36)
This phase compatibility in geometrical optics is essential when converting the differentiated dispersion relation into equations along rays.
For completeness let ω=Ω(k;X,T) be a general local dispersion relation. Define ray tracing by dX/dT=cg​=∇k​Ω. Using the chain rule in ∇X​ω and the symmetry ∂Xi​​kj​=∂Xj​​ki​ gives
kT​+(cg​⋅∇X​)k=−∇X​Ω​k​.
(37)
Likewise ωT​=ΩT​+cg​⋅kT​ cancels its advective term because kT​=−∇ω. Hence the Hamiltonian ray equations for a local dispersion relation are
dTdX​=∇k​Ω,dTdk​=−∇X​Ω​k​,dTdω​=ΩT​​k​.​
(38)
The derivatives on the right hold the wavevector fixed. For this wave equation they reduce to
dTdX​=cKk​=ωc2k​,dTdk​=−K∇c,dTdω​=KcT​.
(39)
When c=c(Y), the ray invariants are k and ω, while dℓ/dT=−KcY​<0. For a monochromatic component with these fixed invariants write θ=kX−ωT+S(Y), so
ℓ=S′(Y)=c(Y)2ω2​−k2​>0.
(40)
Using the stated independence of A0​ from X,T, the wave-action transport for a variable-speed wave becomes (c2ℓA02​)Y​=0. Therefore
A0​(Y)=c(Y)ℓ(Y)​μ​.​
(41)
This is the stationary wave amplitude in a stratified wavespeed; μ is the constant for that component or ray family.
At the proposed simple turning point of a variable-speed wave, take cs​=c(Ys​)=ω/k and cs′​=cY​(Ys​)>0. On the chosen positive branch this entails k>0. Define
Q(Y)=c2ω2​−k2,q=−Q′(Ys​)=cs​2k2cs′​​>0.
(42)
As the ray approaches from below, ℓ∼[q(Ys​−Y)]1/2 and
A0​∼cs​q1/4(Ys​−Y)1/4μ​.
(43)
The amplitude diverges and the vertical group velocity c2ℓ/ω vanishes. The WKB approximation also fails its slow-variation condition ε∣ℓY​∣/ℓ2≪1 when Ys​−Y=O(ε2/3). The turning-point enhancement of wave amplitude shows that this divergence is a failure of the outer ray approximation, rather than an actual infinite solution.
To derive the inner wavefield directly, separate the conserved horizontal wave phase and frequency in the original differential equation:
φ=Ψ(Y)ei(kX−ωT)/ε+c.c.,ε2(c2ΨY​)Y​+(ω2−c2k2)Ψ=0.
(44)
Dividing by c2 gives ε2ΨYY​+2ε2(cY​/c)ΨY​+Q(Y)Ψ=0. Set Y−Ys​=δη. The second derivative is of size ε2/δ2, whereas Q∼−qδη, so the Airy scaling at a variable-speed wave turning point is
δ=(qε2​)1/3,Ψηη​−ηΨ=0 at leading order.​
(45)
The first-derivative term is smaller by O(δ), and higher coefficients in the expansion of Q give the same relative order. Thus the general leading inner profile is a linear combination of the two Airy functions Ai(η) and Bi(η). For decaying Airy continuation and ray reflection, if the physical continuation is bounded and decays into Y>Ys​, its growing Bi coefficient is zero. In that usual evanescent continuation the wavefield has the form
φ∼ε−1/6BAi(q1/3ε2/3Y−Ys​​)ei(kX−ωT+θs​)/ε+c.c.,B=O(1).​
(46)
Without a condition in the evanescent region the corresponding Bi term remains admissible locally; the given existence of a wavefield below the turning point alone does not select its coefficient.
Finally, the outer amplitude at distance δ is O(δ−1/4)=O(ε−1/6) for μ=O(1) and fixed nonzero q. The oscillatory Airy turning-point connection formula has exactly the same (−η)−1/4 envelope on the allowed side. Therefore the inner wavefield has magnitude O(ε−1/6). The decaying Airy continuation matches an incident and reflected pair of rays, so a single upward ℓ>0 branch must be supplemented by its reflected branch near the turning point; no formal coefficient matching is needed to identify this magnitude.
