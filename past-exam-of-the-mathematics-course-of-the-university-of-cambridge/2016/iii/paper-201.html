Work on a filtered probability space (Ω,F,(Fn​)n≥0​,P). A submartingale is a real-valued stochastic process satisfying the following three conditions: Yn​ is Fn​-measurable, E∣Yn​∣<∞, and
E[Yn+1​∣Fn​]≥Yn​almost surely.​
(1)
Thus it is adapted, has integrable random variables at every time, and its conditional expected next value is at least its current value. Repeated conditioning gives E[Ym​∣Fn​]≥Yn​ for every m≥n. The inequality concerns conditional expectation; individual sample paths need not increase. If no filtration is specified, the definition can be applied to the natural filtration generated by the process.

The almost sure submartingale convergence theorem says that if a submartingale (Yn​) satisfies
supn​E[Yn+​]<∞,
(2)
then there is an integrable random variable Y∞​ such that
Yn​⟶Y∞​almost surely.​
(3)
Indeed the positive-part bound also bounds the negative parts, since EYn​≥EY0​; the theorem gives a finite limit. In particular, the martingale convergence theorem applies to any martingale with supn​E∣Mn​∣<∞.
For a nonnegative martingale, EMn​=EM0​ supplies this bound automatically. Its limit obeys EM∞​≤EM0​ by the Fatou lemma. Almost sure convergence alone need not preserve the expectation or give convergence in L1. Uniform integrability is the additional condition that supplies convergence in L1 and the identity Mn​=E[M∞​∣Fn​].

Let (Fn​) be the natural filtration of the random walk on a graph. For a bounded harmonic function on a graph, the transition rule and the Markov property give
E[h(Xn+1​)∣Fn​]=deg(Xn​)1​∑u∼Xn​​h(u)=h(Xn​).
(7)
Thus h(Xn​) is a bounded martingale. The martingale convergence theorem gives a finite almost sure limit L.
On the probability-one recurrence event, every vertex v is visited along a sequence nk​→∞. Along that sequence h(Xnk​​)=h(v), while convergence of the whole sequence forces h(v)=L. Therefore every pair of vertices has the same value. This is the bounded harmonic function theorem on a recurrent graph:
h(v)=h(w)for all vertices v,w.​
(8)
Recurrence makes each vertex value a subsequential limit of the same convergent martingale. A connected locally finite graph is countable, since it is the union of its finite distance balls, so these almost sure assertions can also be combined by a countable intersection. The one-vertex case is immediate.

Write Dk​=Mk​−Mk−1​ for the martingale difference sequence. Bounded increments mean that one deterministic C<∞ satisfies ∣Dk​∣≤C for every k, almost surely. Then EDk​=0 and, for j<k,
E[Dj​Dk​]=E[Dj​E[Dk​∣Fk−1​]]=0.
(9)
The increments are uncorrelated random variables, uniformly bounded in L2. Applying the supplied strong law for uniformly L2-bounded uncorrelated random variables gives n−1∑k=1n​Dk​→0 almost surely. Also M0​/n→0, because M0​ is finite almost surely. Therefore a martingale with bounded increments has zero linear growth:
nMn​​⟶0almost surely.​
(10)
This is the strong law for martingales with bounded increments.

A standard real Brownian motion relative to a filtration (Ft​) is an adapted stochastic process with B0​=0 almost surely, continuous paths almost surely, and
Bt​−Bs​∼N(0,t−s),Bt​−Bs​ independent of Fs​(0≤s<t).​
(33)
Equivalently, it has independent increments and stationary increments, each with a normal distribution of mean zero and variance equal to the elapsed time, together with continuous paths and the stated initial value. The filtration may be its natural filtration. A Brownian motion started at z is z+Wt​ for a standard process W; multidimensional standard Brownian motion has independent standard real coordinate processes. The normalization is variance t at time t.

First take finite endpoints ℓ<r and a starting point z∈(ℓ,r). For the Brownian exit time H=inf{t:Bt​∈/(ℓ,r)}, the stopped path stays in [ℓ,r]. The process (Bt​−z)2−t is a martingale. Bounded-time optional stopping theorem gives
E(H∧t)=E(BH∧t​−z)2≤max{(r−z)2,(z−ℓ)2}.
(42)

Pz​(H<∞)=1.​
(43)
For completeness, a proper unbounded interval also has almost sure exit. Applying optional stopping theorem to BH∧t​ and then bounded convergence theorem gives the endpoint probabilities
Pz​(BH​=r)=r−ℓz−ℓ​,Pz​(BH​=ℓ)=r−ℓr−z​.

After a level is reached, the same argument from that level shows that a level strictly outside a given proper interval is eventually reached. This proves exit even when endpoints are included. A starting point already outside needs no argument. The whole real line has no exit, so the phrase “every interval” is understood to exclude that trivial exception. A real Lévy process is a stochastic process (Xt​)t≥0​ with X0​=0 almost surely, independent increments, stationary increments, and stochastic continuity. The latter means Xt​−Xs​→0 in probability as t→s. Its usual path convention is càdlàg: paths are right-continuous and have left limits. Equivalently one starts with the increment and continuity-in-probability conditions and then chooses the càdlàg version.
The Lévy characteristic exponent is specified here by
EeiθXt​=e−tψ(θ).​
(54)
Some conventions use etψ​(θ) instead, with ψ​=−ψ. The sign convention must be fixed before computing the exponent. The zero process satisfies this definition; excluding it requires an additional nondegeneracy condition.

Both Poisson processes start at zero and have càdlàg paths. On disjoint time intervals their increments are independent, and independence of the two processes makes the difference increments independent as well. Their increment laws depend only on interval length, so X has stationary increments. For a time interval of length h, the probability that either Poisson process has a jump is 1−e−h→0. This proves stochastic continuity.
