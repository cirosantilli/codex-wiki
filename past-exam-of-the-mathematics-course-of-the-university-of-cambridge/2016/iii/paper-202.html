Let V be the closure of the simple predictable processes in the Hilbert space L2(P,μ). Common refinement of deterministic partitions makes these processes a vector space. To prove density of simple predictable processes for finite measures, suppose g∈V⊥. Since μ is a finite measure, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives g∈L1(μ), so
ν(E)=∫E​gdμ
(4)
defines a finite signed measure. The indicator of each predictable generating rectangle, including each time-zero rectangle, is a simple predictable process; hence ν vanishes on these rectangles.
Finite intersections of the rectangles are again rectangles or empty. Add the whole space to this pi-system. Its ν-mass is zero because 1Ω×[0,n]​ is simple and converges to 1 in L2(μ) by the dominated convergence theorem. The zero sets of ν form a lambda-system now that its total mass is zero. The Pi-lambda theorem therefore gives ν(E)=0 for every E∈P. Taking the sets where g>0 and g<0 gives g=0 μ-almost everywhere.
Thus V⊥={0} and the orthogonal complement criterion for a closed subspace of a Hilbert space yields
SL2(P,μ)=L2(P,μ).​
(5)

A weak solution of a stochastic differential equation consists of a filtered probability space, an adapted continuous process X, and a Brownian motion B relative to that filtration, with specified initial distribution, such that for every finite t the coefficient integrals are finite and
Xt​=X0​+∫0t​σ(Xs​)dBs​+∫0t​b(Xs​)dsalmost surely for all t.​
(19)
In one dimension the integrability conditions are ∫0t​σ(Xs​)2ds<∞ and ∫0t​∣b(Xs​)∣ds<∞. The noise integral is the Itô integral. A solution on a prescribed stochastic basis with prescribed B is distinguished from this weak construction.
A strong solution of a stochastic differential equation uses the prescribed B and initial variable, and is adapted to their augmented natural filtration; thus no additional randomness is needed. For deterministic X0​=x, each Xt​ is determined by the driving Brownian motion through time t. Merely being adapted to a larger filtration is insufficient for this definition.
Uniqueness in law means that any two weak stochastic solutions with the same initial distribution have the same law of X on continuous path space. Pathwise uniqueness means that two solutions on one stochastic basis, with the same B and almost surely equal initial values, are indistinguishable stochastic processes. The latter compares paths under the same noise, rather than just comparing their probability distributions.

The Girsanov theorem construction proves a weak stochastic solution, but does not by itself establish a strong stochastic solution. It constructs X=x+W first, and then constructs a new driving Brownian motion B=W−∫b(Xs​)ds. Adaptation of X to the original filtration of W does not by itself prove adaptation to the smaller natural filtration of B. Inverting this relation requires an additional result; uniqueness in law alone is insufficient in general.
For the particular coefficients in part (b), however, the literal claim that a strong solution may fail is incorrect. The strong existence theorem for additive-noise SDEs with bounded measurable drift gives strong existence and pathwise uniqueness for bounded Borel state-dependent b and unit diffusion. Consequently every weak realization here is determined by its driving noise and initial value. This theorem is deeper than the Girsanov theorem argument and no proof is needed here. Examples with path-dependent drift or with a nonconstant diffusion coefficient do not contradict it.
The valid distinction is therefore: the argument in part (b) has established weak existence and uniqueness in law; strong existence requires an additional theorem, and in this class that theorem is available. A primary reference for the correction is On strong solutions and explicit formulas for solutions of stochastic integral equations.

The Lévy characterization of Brownian motion states that an adapted continuous real process M with M0​=0 is standard Brownian motion relative to (Ft​) if and only if it is a continuous local martingale and [M]t​=t. The multidimensional version replaces the bracket identity by [Mi,Mj]t​=δij​t.

g(Xt​)−g(X0​)−∫0t​Lg(Xs​)ds is a martingale.​
(43)
Here Cb2​ includes boundedness of the function and its derivatives through order two. This is the true-martingale version of the diffusion martingale problem. The matrix a is the diffusivity, and b is the drift. An Itô diffusion with coefficient σ has a=σσT.
A more general local martingale problem may permit unbounded coefficients and require only a local martingale. That convention is weaker and must not silently replace the true martingale property used here and in part (b). The bounded-coefficient convention guarantees all finite-horizon integrability needed below; a sufficient more general replacement is E∫0T​(∑i​∣bi​(Xs​)∣+∑i​aii​(Xs​))ds<∞ for every T.

We prove the extension from spatial test functions to time-dependent test functions for a diffusion martingale problem directly, without assuming a driving Brownian motion. Fix 0≤s<t and a deterministic partition s=t0​<⋯<tn​=t. For each j, gj​(x)=f(tj​,x) lies in Cb2​. The defining martingale problem gives
E[1A​∑j=0n−1​(f(tj​,Xtj+1​​)−f(tj​,Xtj​​)−∫tj​tj+1​​Lf(tj​,Xu​)du)]=0(A∈Fs​).
(44)
Call the sum Sn​. Telescoping separates the spatial and temporal changes:
Sn​=f(t,Xt​)−f(s,Xs​)−∑j​∫tj​tj+1​​∂u​f(u,Xtj+1​​)du−∑j​∫tj​tj+1​​Lf(tj​,Xu​)du.
(45)
The sample path X is uniformly continuous on [s,t] and has compact range. Joint continuity of ∂t​f, the spatial first derivatives, and the spatial second derivatives therefore gives, as the mesh tends to zero,
Sn​⟶f(t,Xt​)−f(s,Xs​)−∫st​(∂u​+L)f(u,Xu​)du=Mtf​−Msf​
(46)
almost surely. For bounded a,b and bounded derivatives of f, both integrals are uniformly bounded on this finite horizon, as are the two endpoint terms. The dominated convergence theorem permits passage to the limit in the expectation against 1A​. Thus Mtf​ is integrable and E[1A​(Mtf​−Msf​)]=0 for every A∈Fs​, proving
E[Mtf​∣Fs​]=Msf​.​
(47)
Adaptation and continuity follow from the formula, so Mf is a continuous martingale. Under the more general integrability condition in part (a), the same proof uses an integrable bound proportional to 1+∫st​(∑i​∣bi​(Xu​)∣+∑i​aii​(Xu​))du; positive semidefiniteness bounds every off-diagonal entry by the diagonal ones. With only local assumptions this reasoning proves a local martingale, and an integrability hypothesis is needed to upgrade that conclusion.
