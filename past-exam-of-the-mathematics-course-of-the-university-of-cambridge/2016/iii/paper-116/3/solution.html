Use the Riemann curvature tensor convention R(X,Y)Z=∇X​∇Y​Z−∇Y​∇X​Z−∇[X,Y]​Z. A Jacobi field is a smooth vector field J along γ satisfying
Dt2​J+R(J,γ˙​)γ˙​=0,​
(20)
where Dt​ is the covariant derivative along a curve for the Levi-Civita connection. Differentiating a geodesic variation gives this equation, and conversely every Jacobi field arises from a geodesic variation by varying its initial point and velocity.
Choose a parallel frame E1​(t),…,En​(t) along γ and write J=∑a​ya​Ea​. The Jacobi equation becomes the linear system
y′′(t)+K(t)y(t)=0,Kab​(t)=⟨R(Eb​,γ˙​)γ˙​,Ea​⟩.
(21)
The existence and uniqueness theorem for linear ordinary differential equations says that each pair (y(0),y′(0))∈Rn⊕Rn determines exactly one solution on [0,1]. Addition and scalar multiplication preserve the equation. Thus evaluation of initial position and covariant velocity is a linear isomorphism
Jγ​⟶Tγ(0)​M⊕Tγ(0)​M,J⟼(J(0),Dt​J(0)),
(22)
and the dimension is
dimJγ​=2n.​
(23)
For the convexity assertion, use the convex-normal meaning of a geodesically convex open set: its points are joined by a unique geodesic within the set, and the joining geodesic depends smoothly on its endpoints. Equivalently, the appropriate star-shaped restriction of expp​ is a diffeomorphism onto the set. The convex-normal-neighbourhood theorem supplies such sets around every point. Mere existence of some minimizing geodesic, without this uniqueness and normality, would not imply the assertion.
Let p=γ(0) and v=γ˙​(0). For a Jacobi field with J(0)=0 and Dt​J(0)=w, differentiating expp​(t(v+sw)) gives the standard differential-of-the-exponential map identity
J(1)=(dexpp​)v​(w).
(24)
In a convex normal neighbourhood, (dexpp​)v​ is invertible. Therefore a Jacobi field vanishing at both endpoints has w=0 and hence is identically zero. The difference of two fields with the same endpoint values consequently vanishes. More precisely, the map
Jγ​⟶Tp​M⊕Tγ(1)​M,J⟼(J(0),J(1))​
(25)
is an isomorphism: it is injective and both spaces have dimension 2n. The constant-geodesic case follows directly from Dt2​J=0. This uses the convex-normal-neighbourhood theorem, the differential-of-the-exponential map identity, and linear ordinary differential equation uniqueness.
For the special unitary group example, use Jacobi fields from conjugation at a central endpoint. Write
A=diag(2πi/3,2πi/3,−4πi/3),z=e2πi/3.
(26)
The endpoints of γ(t)=exp(tA) are I and zI. The latter is central. For every X∈su(3) consider
FX​(s,t)=esXetAe−sX.
(27)
Conjugation is an isometry for a bi-invariant Riemannian metric, and geodesics of a bi-invariant metric are one-parameter subgroups. Thus FX​ is a geodesic variation. Its Jacobi field is
JX​(t)=XetA−etAX,
(28)
which vanishes at t=0 and t=1 because both endpoints are central. Its initial covariant derivative is [X,A].
The linear map X↦JX​ has kernel equal to the centralizer of an element of a Lie algebra of A. Indeed, JX​≡0 implies [X,A]=0 on differentiating at t=0, and that commutation conversely implies JX​≡0. The repeated first two eigenvalues give
z(A)={(B0​0c​):B∈u(2), c∈iR, trB+c=0},dimR​z(A)=4.
(29)
As dimR​su(3)=8, rank-nullity theorem gives
dim{J:J(0)=J(1)=0}≥8−4=4.​
(30)
Explicit independent generators are the fields belonging to E13​−E31​, i(E13​+E31​), E23​−E32​ and i(E23​+E32​). Their initial derivatives [X,A] are independent because the eigenvalue differences in the 13 and 23 entries are 2πi. This also demonstrates directly why the endpoint-value conclusion fails along this geodesic.
