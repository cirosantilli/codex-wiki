Work with a probability measure-preserving system, so μ(X)=1. Put
Sn​f=∑j=0n−1​f∘Tj,An​f=nSn​f​.
(1)
The maximal ergodic theorem, also called the maximal ergodic lemma, says that for real f∈L1(μ),
EN​={max1≤n≤N​Sn​f>0},E={supn≥1​Sn​f>0}⟹∫EN​​fdμ≥0,∫E​fdμ≥0.​
(2)
No ergodic transformation or invertibility assumption is needed for this maximal ergodic lemma.
Here is a proof of the maximal ergodic lemma. Set M0​=0 and MN​=max(0,S1​f,…,SN​f). These are integrable: 0≤MN​≤∑j=0N−1​∣f∣∘Tj. Splitting off the first summand gives
MN​=max(0,f+MN−1​∘T),MN−1​≤MN​.
(3)
On EN​={MN​>0}, this implies f=MN​−MN−1​∘T≥MN​−MN​∘T. Outside EN​ the right side is −MN​∘T≤0. Consequently
f1EN​​≥MN​−MN​∘T.
(4)
Integrating and using the measure-preserving transformation property gives ∫EN​​fdμ≥0. Since EN​ increases to E, the dominated convergence theorem, with dominating function ∣f∣, proves the infinite-supremum assertion of the maximal ergodic lemma.
For example, applying the maximal ergodic lemma to ∣f∣−ε yields the useful maximal bound
μ{supn≥1​∣An​f∣>ε}≤μ{supn≥1​An​∣f∣>ε}≤ε∥f∥1​​(ε>0).

The pointwise ergodic theorem states that for a probability measure-preserving system and f∈L1(μ),
An​f⟶E[f∣I]almost everywhere,​
(6)
where I is the invariant sigma-algebra. The conditional expectation is invariant and has integral ∫fdμ. If T is an ergodic transformation, this conditional expectation is the constant ∫fdμ. The assertion applies to real or complex integrable functions; it is the Birkhoff ergodic theorem.
For a nonnegative measurable f, if ∫fdμ<∞, the pointwise ergodic theorem already proves the required conclusion. For the remaining case, let fK​=min(f,K), K=1,2,…. Each fK​ is integrable on the probability measure-preserving system. The pointwise ergodic theorem and ergodic transformation property give, on a single set of full measure obtained by a countable intersection,
limn→∞​An​fK​(x)=∫fK​dμfor every K.
(7)
Since f≥fK​, every such x satisfies liminfn​An​f(x)≥∫fK​dμ for all K. The monotone convergence theorem gives ∫fK​dμ↑∫fdμ=+∞. Thus the nonnegative ergodic averages with infinite integral tend to +∞, rather than merely having an unbounded subsequence. Combining the two cases,
n→∞lim​n1​j=0∑n−1​f(Tjx)=∫fdμ∈[0,+∞]almost everywhere.​
(8)
Nonnegativity is what makes truncation a lower bound. The probability normalization is also essential to the displayed value: for a finite measure of total mass c>0, the corresponding constant is c−1∫fdμ.
