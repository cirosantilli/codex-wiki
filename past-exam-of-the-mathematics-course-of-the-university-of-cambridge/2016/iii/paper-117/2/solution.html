A flat torus is the quotient Rd/Λ, where Λ is a full-rank Euclidean lattice, with the Riemannian metric induced from the Euclidean metric. Translation by a lattice vector is a Riemannian isometry, so this metric is well defined and has zero curvature. Its volume is the covolume of Λ.
The dual lattice is Λ∨={w:⟨w,v⟩∈Z for all v∈Λ}. For w∈Λ∨, the function ew​(x)=exp(2πi⟨w,x⟩) descends to the flat torus, and direct differentiation gives
Δew​=4π2∣w∣2ew​.
(8)
Conversely, write Λ=BZd and x=By. Ordinary Fourier series on Rd/Zd have frequencies n∈Zd, corresponding to w=B−Tn∈Λ∨. After normalization they form a complete orthonormal basis in L2, and the Fourier coefficients of a smooth function decay rapidly. Applying the Laplace-Beltrami operator term by term shows that a smooth eigenfunction with eigenvalue λ has nonzero coefficients only where 4π2∣w∣2=λ. Thus the spectrum of a flat torus is
Spec(Rd/Λ)={4π2∣w∣2:w∈Λ∨},m(λ)=#{w∈Λ∨:4π2∣w∣2=λ}.​
(9)
The formula counts multiplicity over either the real or complex numbers: the two frequencies w,−w give the real sine and cosine functions.
For the rigidity assertion in dimension two, it suffices to reconstruct a rank-two Euclidean lattice L=Λ∨ from its vector-length multiset. The multiset first determines its covolume A. Indeed, a bounded fundamental parallelogram, and comparison of the cells meeting a disk with slightly larger and smaller disks, give the lattice-point asymptotics by fundamental cells
NL​(R)=#{v∈L:∣v∣≤R}=AπR2​+O(R).
(10)
The spectrum determines NL​(R), including multiplicities, so it determines A.
Let a be the smallest nonzero vector length, and choose v∈L with ∣v∣=a. This vector is primitive: v=ku with ∣k∣>1 would contradict minimality. From the length multiset subtract the two vectors kv,−kv at every positive length ka, for k=1,2,…. The shortest remaining length b is exactly the length of a shortest vector w∈/Zv. This subtraction is legitimate even if several directions have length a: it subtracts just two copies, and then b=a. It also proves that b is independent of our choice of shortest direction. Replace w by w−kv to arrange
∣v⋅w∣≤a2/2.
(11)
The replacement stays outside Zv and cannot be shorter than b, so a reduced choice still has length b.
The shortest-independent-vector basis lemma says that v,w are a basis of L. To prove it, choose coordinates with v=(a,0). Because v is primitive, L/Zv≅Z; let h>0 be the smallest positive vertical coordinate in L. Write w=(x,mh), with m≥1 after changing its sign. If m≥2, a vector u of vertical coordinate h, reduced horizontally modulo v, has
∣u∣2≤a2/4+h2≤(a2+m2h2)/4≤(a2+b2)/4≤b2/2<b2.
(12)
This contradicts the definition of b, since u∈/Zv. Hence m=1 and v,w generate L.
The Gram matrix of this basis is (a2c​cb2​), with c=v⋅w, and its determinant is A2. Therefore
c2=a2b2−A2.
(13)
Changing the sign of one basis vector allows c≥0. The numbers a,b,A, all audible from the spectrum, consequently determine the Gram matrix, and hence L, up to an orthogonal transformation. Taking dual lattices gives the same conclusion for Λ. Isospectral flat two-dimensional tori are isometric. The argument uses the special basis property of rank-two Euclidean lattices, so it does not claim higher-dimensional rigidity.
