The test-function space is D(Rn)=Cc∞​(Rn). Its topology is the strict inductive limit topology of the spaces DK​ of smooth functions supported in a fixed compact set K, each with the seminorms pK,j​(φ)=max∣α∣≤j​supK​∣∂αφ∣. In particular, a sequence converges in D precisely when its supports lie in one compact set and all its derivatives converge uniformly. The indices α use multi-index notation.
The distribution space D′(Rn) consists of continuous complex-linear forms on D. Equivalently, for every compact K there are CK​ and an integer qK​≥0 such that
∣⟨u,φ⟩∣≤CK​max∣α∣≤qK​​supK​∣∂αφ∣,suppφ⊂K.
(1)
We use distributional convergence: uj​→u means ⟨uj​,φ⟩→⟨u,φ⟩ for every φ∈D. The pairings are bilinear, with no complex conjugation. A test function f is identified with its regular distribution ⟨f,φ⟩=∫fφ.
For a distribution u and a test function ψ, their smoothing convolution with a test function is
(u∗ψ)(x)=⟨uy​,ψ(x−y)⟩.​
(2)
It is a smooth function, with ∂xα​(u∗ψ)(x)=⟨uy​,∂αψ(x−y)⟩. Indeed, for x in a compact neighborhood all translated tests have support in one compact set, and their difference quotients converge in the space of test functions. Notice that u∗ψ need not be compactly supported.
Choose a nonnegative mollifier ρ∈Cc∞​(B1​) with ∫ρ=1, and write ρε​(x)=ε−nρ(x/ε). If ρ​(x)=ρ(−x), then
⟨u∗ρε​,φ⟩=⟨u,ρ​ε​∗φ⟩.
(3)
The right-hand test tends to φ in D: its supports lie in suppφ+B1​ for ε≤1, and every derivative converges uniformly by the approximate-identity argument. Thus the smooth regularizations converge to u as distributions.
To obtain actual compactly supported approximants, choose a smooth cutoff function χ equal to one on B1​ and supported in B2​, and set
fj​(x)=χ(x/j)(u∗ρ1/j​)(x)∈D(Rn).​
(4)
For each fixed test function φ, the cutoff is identically one on its support once j is large. Hence ⟨fj​,φ⟩=⟨u,ρ​1/j​∗φ⟩→⟨u,φ⟩. This proves D is dense in D′, and in fact establishes the density of test functions in distributions and gives a convergent approximating sequence for each distribution. The expanding cutoff is essential when u has noncompact support of a distribution.
For the radial limit, take a test function φ and introduce t=r2−1 in polar coordinates. The Jacobian rdr=dt/2 gives
⟨um​,φ⟩=∫−1∞​msin(m∣t∣)A(t)dt,A(t)=21​∫02π​φ(1+t​(cosω,sinω))dω.
(5)
When φ is supported away from the origin, A vanishes near t=−1 and extends to a compactly supported smooth function on the whole line. The folded sine approximation to a Dirac delta is exposed by setting B(s)=A(s)+A(−s): integration by parts gives
∫0∞​msin(ms)B(s)ds=B(0)+∫0∞​cos(ms)B′(s)ds⟶2A(0),
(6)
by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma. Therefore
um​⟶δS1​in D′(R2∖{0}),⟨δS1​,φ⟩=∫02π​φ(cosω,sinω)dω.​
(7)
This surface delta distribution is arclength measure on the unit circle. By the level-set normalization of a surface delta, it is 2δ(∣x∣2−1): the factor two cancels the gradient magnitude ∣∇(∣x∣2−1)∣=2 on the circle. It is not twice arclength measure.
For a test function that can meet the origin, the endpoint cannot be discarded. Now A(−1)=πφ(0). Its right derivative is integrable, with A′(−1+s)=(π/4)Δφ(0)+O(s) from differentiated Taylor expansion: the angular average cancels odd Taylor terms, so A(t)=πφ(0)+(π/4)(1+t)Δφ(0)+O((1+t)2) near t=−1. Applying integration by parts separately on the negative and positive intervals gives
⟨um​,φ⟩=2A(0)−A(−1)cosm−∫−10​cos(mt)A′(t)dt+∫0∞​cos(mt)A′(t)dt.
(8)
The last two integrals tend to zero by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma. Thus the radial quadratic oscillation defect in two dimensions gives the stronger oscillating point-mass defect formula
um​=δS1​−πcosmδ0​+oD′​(1).​
(9)
Choose φ supported in B1/2​ with φ(0)=1. Its pairing is −πcosm+o(1), which does not converge. For completeness, if cosm had a limit L, the recurrence cos(m+1)+cos(m−1)=2cos1cosm would force L=0, whereas the even subsequence identity cos(2m)=2cos2m−1 would then force L=−1. Consequently there is no limit in D′(R2).

Use the Fourier transform convention φ​(ξ)=∫e−ix⋅ξφ(x)dx, with inverse factor (2π)−n. The Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem can be stated with sharp convex support: for a nonempty compact convex set K⊂Rn, let its support function be HK​(η)=supx∈K​x⋅η. Then F is the Fourier transform of a unique distribution supported in K if and only if it is entire and
∣F(z)∣≤C(1+∣z∣)MeHK​(Imz)(z∈Cn)​
(10)
for some C and nonnegative integer M. For K=BR​, this is the familiar bound C(1+∣z∣)MeR∣Imz∣. Allowing some R characterizes all compactly supported distributions. Here the extension of the Fourier transform is F(z)=⟨u,e−ix⋅z⟩, interpreted with a cutoff equal to one near the support of a distribution.
First suppose suppu⊂K. Fix one smooth cutoff function equal to one near K. Pairing the resulting compactly supported exponential with u shows that F is an entire function: differentiation with respect to zj​ inserts −ixj​, and the power series converges in the test-function seminorms uniformly on compact subsets of Cn.
To keep the exponential type exactly HK​, rather than that of a fixed larger neighborhood, use a shrinking-cutoff exponential-type estimate. There are cutoffs χε​ equal to one on K+εB1​, supported in K+3εB1​, and satisfying ∣∂αχε​∣≤Cα​ε−∣α∣ for 0<ε≤1. One construction convolves the indicator of K+2εB1​ with a unit-mass mollifier supported in εB1​. Continuity of u on a fixed compact neighborhood gives a finite order of a distribution q there. By the product rule,
∣F(z)∣=∣⟨u,χε​e−ix⋅z⟩∣≤Cε−q(1+∣z∣)qexp(HK​(Imz)+3ε∣Imz∣).
(11)
Taking ε=(1+∣z∣)−1 proves the required bound with M=2q, since the extra exponential factor is at most e3. The value of the pairing is independent of the chosen cutoff because all cutoffs agree near suppu. Thus the forward direction has the exact asserted support function, without an unproved estimate on derivatives restricted only to K.
Conversely, suppose the entire F has the stated bound. Its restriction to real frequency has polynomial growth, so define a tempered distribution u by
⟨u,φ⟩=(2π)−n∫Rn​F(ξ)φ​(−ξ)dξ,φ∈S(Rn).
(12)
The Schwartz space decay makes this integral absolutely convergent and continuous; by Fourier inversion, u=F on real frequency. It remains to prove the support assertion by mollifier regularization for contour recovery of support.
Choose a nonnegative unit-mass mollifier ρ supported in B1​, and set
Fε​(z)=F(z)ρ​(εz),hε​(x)=(2π)−n∫Rn​eix⋅ξFε​(ξ)dξ.
(13)
On real frequency, Fε​ decays faster than any polynomial after enough applications of integration by parts to ρ​; hence hε​ is smooth, by differentiation under the integral sign. More generally, for every integer L there is Cε,L​ such that
∣ρ​(ε(ξ+iη))∣≤Cε,L​(1+∣ξ∣)−L(1+∣η∣)Leε∣η∣.
(14)
To obtain this estimate, write the transform of ρε​ as the real-frequency transform of ex⋅ηρε​(x) and integrate by parts; each derivative introduces at most one factor of ∣η∣.
Fix a unit vector v and take L>M+n+1. A contour-shift proof of the Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem moves the inverse-transform contour to Rn+itv:
hε​(x)=(2π)−n∫Rn​eix⋅(ξ+itv)Fε​(ξ+itv)dξ.
(15)
Here is a justification of the shift. Rotate coordinates so v is the first coordinate direction, apply the Cauchy integral theorem on a rectangle in the first complex variable, and integrate over the other real variables. For fixed t, the vertical sides at real part ±A have an integrated bound proportional to AM−L+n−1, and hence vanish as A→∞. The same decay bounds give absolute convergence on the horizontal sides. No contour shift of an unregularized polynomially growing integral is needed.
Positive homogeneity of the support function now gives
∣hε​(x)∣≤Cε,L​(1+t)M+Lexp(−t[x⋅v−HK​(v)−ε]).
(16)
If x∈/K+εB1​, the Hahn-Banach separation theorem supplies a unit vector v with x⋅v>HK​(v)+ε. Letting t→∞ proves hε​(x)=0. Therefore hε​∈Cc∞​ and supphε​⊂K+εB1​.
Since ρ​(εξ)→1 and its modulus is at most one on real frequency, the dominated convergence theorem in the formula for ⟨u,φ⟩ gives hε​→u as tempered distributions, and thus as distributions. A test function supported outside K has positive distance from K, so its pairing with hε​ is zero for all sufficiently small ε. This proves suppu⊂K. The Fourier transform of a compactly supported distribution constructed in the forward direction equals F on Rn; applying the one-variable identity theorem successively in each coordinate extends the equality to Cn. Injectivity of the Fourier transform of a tempered distribution proves uniqueness and completes both directions.
For the independence application, put gm​(z)=eiz⋅ym​fm​(z) and rm​=1/(m+1). The ball version of the Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem supplies a nonzero distribution vm​ supported in Brm​​(0) with vm​=gm​. By the Translation property of the Fourier transform,
fm​(z)=e−iz⋅ym​vm​(z)=wm​(z),wm​=τym​​vm​,suppwm​⊂Brm​​(ym​),
(17)
where ⟨τy​v,φ⟩=⟨v,φ(⋅+y)⟩. Distinct integer points are at least distance one apart. For distinct positive indices j,k, the largest possible radius sum is 1/2+1/3=5/6<1. Thus these closed balls, and hence the distribution supports, are pairwise disjoint.
If ∑m=1N​cm​fm​=0, injectivity of the Fourier transform gives ∑m​cm​wm​=0. For each j, choose a smooth cutoff function equal to one near its ball and zero near all other balls. Multiplying the distributional identity by this cutoff isolates cj​wj​=0. Since fj​ is not identically zero, wj​=0, so cj​=0. Consequently f1​,…,fN​ are linearly independent over C. This Fourier independence from disjoint distribution supports uses the quantitative exponential types to establish support separation.

With ⟨θ⟩=(1+∣θ∣2)1/2, the symbol class Sym(X,Rk;N)=S1,0N​ consists of complex-valued a∈C∞(X×Rk) such that, for every compact K⋐X and every pair of multi-indices α,β,
∣∂xα​∂θβ​a(x,θ)∣≤CK,α,β​⟨θ⟩N−∣β∣,x∈K.​
(18)
An x derivative preserves symbol order, while a θ derivative lowers it by one. The definition is local in x and imposes estimates of every derivative order.
In the homogeneous convention, a phase function is real, smooth on X×(Rk∖{0}), positively homogeneous of degree one in θ, and satisfies
(∇x​Φ,∇θ​Φ)=0(θ=0).
(19)
Only the large-frequency behavior is relevant to the regularization argument; any prescribed smooth modification near θ=0 contributes an ordinary convergent integral. If the phase is presented on all of X×Rk, homogeneity can be required outside a bounded frequency region. Equivalently for this construction, its large-frequency symbol bounds and the following uniform estimate are sufficient:
∣∇x​Φ∣2+∣θ∣2∣∇θ​Φ∣2≥cK​∣θ∣2,x∈K, ∣θ∣≥1.
(20)
For a homogeneous phase function, this follows from compactness of K×Sk−1. Nonvanishing of the total gradient is the condition; ∇θ​Φ may vanish. The displayed definition does not impose the stronger rank condition sometimes called a nondegenerate phase in Fourier-integral-operator theory.
We construct the oscillatory integral by integration by parts in both x and θ. For ∣θ∣≥1, set
q=∣∇x​Φ∣2+∣θ∣2∣∇θ​Φ∣2,L=iq1​(∇x​Φ⋅∇x​+∣θ∣2∇θ​Φ⋅∇θ​).
(21)
Then L(eiΦ)=eiΦ. The coefficients of the x derivatives have symbol order −1 and those of the θ derivatives have order zero. Use the bilinear formal transpose of a differential operator, not the Hermitian adjoint:
Ltb=−∑j​∂xj​​(iq∂xj​​Φ​b)−∑ℓ​∂θℓ​​(iq∣θ∣2∂θℓ​​Φ​b).
(22)
The symbol order reduction by a phase integration operator follows from the product rule and the symbol class estimates: Lt:S1,0s​→S1,0s−1​ locally in x. Differentiating the coefficients preserves precisely these orders because q is elliptic of degree two on each compact x set.
Let ζ∈Cc∞​(Rk) equal one on ∣θ∣≤1 and vanish on ∣θ∣≥2. The low-frequency part is an ordinary integral. For an integer r>N+k, define the high-frequency part by
⟨IΦ​(a),φ⟩=​∬eiΦζ(θ)a(x,θ)φ(x)dxdθ+∬eiΦ(Lt)r[(1−ζ(θ))a(x,θ)φ(x)]dxdθ.​
(23)
If the homogeneous phase is only defined off zero, its value at the single point zero is immaterial to the low-frequency integral; its modulus remains one. In the high-frequency term all coefficients are used away from zero. For suppφ⊂K, repeated product rules give
∣(Lt)r[(1−ζ)aφ]∣≤CK,a,Φ,r​⟨θ⟩N−rmax∣α∣≤r​supK​∣∂αφ∣.
(24)
Since N−r<−k, both terms converge absolutely. They are linear in the test function, with the continuity bound
∣⟨IΦ​(a),φ⟩∣≤CK​∣α∣≤rmax​Ksup​∣∂αφ∣.​
(25)
Thus IΦ​(a)∈D′(X), with order of a distribution at most r on each compact set.
To check that this construction is the intended oscillatory integral and does not depend on r, ζ or the large-frequency cutoff, let χ∈Cc∞​(Rk) equal one near zero and form
JR​(φ)=∬eiΦ(x,θ)a(x,θ)φ(x)χ(θ/R)dxdθ.
(26)
After r applications of integration by parts, the term without a derivative on χ tends to the absolutely convergent high-frequency term by the dominated convergence theorem. Each extra term is supported in an annulus ∣θ∣≍R. A derivative of χ(θ/R) contributes R−1, so those terms have total absolute value at most CK​RN−r+kmax∣α∣≤r​∥∂αφ∥∞​→0. The low-frequency part is unchanged for large R. Hence JR​(φ) tends to the displayed functional, independently of all cutoffs and of the permissible number of integrations. This proves the cutoff independence of an oscillatory integral as well as its continuity.
For the final distribution, the derivative convention ⟨δ′,g⟩=−g′(0) identifies it as −x2​δ′(x1​). The delta derivatives from polynomial oscillatory amplitudes identity yields
Φ(x,θ)=x1​θ,a(x,θ)=−2πi​x2​θ,u=IΦ​(a)=−2πix2​​∫R​eix1​θθdθ.​
(27)
Here k=1, a∈Sym(R2,R;1), and ∇x​Φ=(θ,0) is nonzero for θ=0. Thus the phase is admissible even though ∂θ​Φ=x1​ vanishes on the distribution's support.
For a direct proof of the sign and normalization, put g(s)=∫R​x2​φ(s,x2​)dx2​∈Cc∞​(R). The cutoff integral is
JR​(φ)=−2πi​∫θχ(θ/R)g​(−θ)dθ=2πi​∫ξχ(−ξ/R)g​(ξ)dξ.
(28)
The Fourier transform g​ is rapidly decreasing, so the dominated convergence theorem and differentiated Fourier inversion give
limR→∞​JR​(φ)=g′(0)=∫R​x2​∂x1​∂φ​(0,x2​)dx2​.
(29)
This is exactly the required pairing, so the oscillatory representation gives the prescribed distribution, not its negative or a multiple of it.
