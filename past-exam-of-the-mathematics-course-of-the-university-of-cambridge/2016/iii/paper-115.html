A smooth vector field is a smooth section of a vector bundle X:M→TM of the tangent bundle, so Xp​∈Tp​M. In a manifold chart, X=Xi∂i​ with smooth coefficients. It acts on a smooth function by Xf=Xi∂i​f. For a diffeomorphism α:M→N, the pushforward of a vector field is
(α∗​X)q​=dαα−1(q)​Xα−1(q)​.
(1)
The Lie bracket of vector fields is the commutator of their actions on smooth functions:
[X,Y]f=X(Yf)−Y(Xf),[X,Y]j=Xi∂i​Yj−Yi∂i​Xj.
(2)
The second derivatives of f cancel, so this is again a vector field. The corresponding Jacobi identity is [X,[Y,Z]]+[Y,[Z,X]]+[Z,[X,Y]]=0.
A local flow of X is a smooth map (t,p)↦ϕt​(p) on an open neighbourhood of {0}×M satisfying ϕ0​(p)=p and ∂t​ϕt​(p)=Xϕt​(p)​. On a sufficiently small neighbourhood and time interval, each ϕt​ is a diffeomorphism onto its image, with inverse ϕ−t​. Uniqueness of integral curves of a vector field gives ϕs+t​=ϕs​∘ϕt​ wherever both sides are defined. These are local statements; no assumption of a complete vector field is required.
A smooth tensor field of type (r,s) is a smooth section of (TM)⊗r⊗(T∗M)⊗s, where the factors are the tangent bundle and cotangent bundle. The flow definition of the Lie derivative of a tensor field is
LX​T=dtd​​t=0​ϕt∗​T.
(3)
Here pullback applies dϕt−1​ to each vector factor and the dual of dϕt​ to each covector factor, evaluating T at ϕt​(p). Thus all tensors being differentiated lie in the same fibre over p.
For a smooth function, ϕt∗​f=f∘ϕt​, and the chain rule gives
LX​f=df(X)=Xf​.
(4)
For a vector field Y, in local coordinates the expansions ϕt​(x)=x+tX(x)+O(t2) and dϕt​(x)=I+tdXx​+O(t2) give
ϕt∗​Y(x)=dϕt​(x)−1Y(ϕt​(x))=Y(x)+t(dYx​X(x)−dXx​Y(x))+O(t2).
(5)
Consequently
LX​Y=[X,Y]​.
(6)
Pullback preserves tensor products and tensor contractions, so differentiation makes this definition a tensor derivation. It therefore agrees on every tensor field with the Lie derivative of a tensor field determined by these two formulas.
Now put ψt​=α∘ϕt​∘α−1. The chain rule gives
∂t​ψt​(q)=dαϕt​(α−1q)​Xϕt​(α−1q)​=(α∗​X)ψt​(q)​,ψ0​(q)=q.
(7)
Thus ψt​ is the local flow of α∗​X. In the case M=N, if α∗​X=X, uniqueness of integral curves of a vector field makes ψt​=ϕt​ on their common domains, or α∘ϕt​=ϕt​∘α. Conversely, differentiating this commuting identity at zero gives dαp​Xp​=Xα(p)​, hence α∗​X=X. This proves that diffeomorphism invariance of a vector field is equivalent to commuting with its local flow, with every identity understood on the domain where its compositions exist.

In a manifold chart, write a smooth differential form as η=∑I​aI​dxi1​∧⋯∧dxip​, with I increasing. Define its exterior derivative by
dη=∑I,j​∂j​aI​dxj∧dxi1​∧⋯∧dxip​.
(8)
To check that this definition is global, change coordinates to y. The chain rule expresses dxi as ∑j​(∂j​xi)dyj. Differentiating it gives zero because the second partial derivatives of xi are symmetric and dyk∧dyj is antisymmetric. The coordinate definition satisfies the graded Leibniz rule, so differentiating the transformed expression for η leaves exactly ∑I​daI​∧dxi1​∧⋯∧dxip​. This agrees with the original formula and proves independence of the manifold chart. The same cancellation of mixed derivatives gives d2=0.
The de Rham cohomology is therefore
HdRp​(M;R)=im(d:Ωp−1(M)→Ωp(M))ker(d:Ωp(M)→Ωp+1(M))​,
(9)
the vector space of closed differential forms modulo exact differential forms.
Here is a Green-theorem proof of exactness on a disc. Identify the disc with a round disc centred at zero, and let η=Pdx+Qdy be a closed differential form. Set f(x)=∫[0,x]​η. For x and x+h in the disc, Green theorem on the oriented triangle with vertices 0,x,x+h gives zero boundary integral, since ∂x​Q−∂y​P=0. Hence
f(x+h)−f(x)=∫[x,x+h]​η=∫01​ηx+sh​(h)ds.
(10)
Taking h=εv and dividing by ε yields dfx​(v)=ηx​(v). The radial integral defining f is smooth, so η=df and
HdR1​(Δ;R)=0​.
(11)
Degenerate triangles follow by continuity. The same proof works on any star-shaped plane domain, including the whole plane.
Integration requires an orientation: take M to be an oriented compact n-dimensional smooth manifold without boundary. The Generalized Stokes theorem says ∫M​dβ=0 for every smooth (n−1)-form β. Every n-form is closed by dimension, and changing it by an exact differential form leaves its integral unchanged. Thus
[η]⟼∫M​η
(12)
is a well-defined linear map HdRn​(M;R)→R. Compactness alone does not supply the orientation omitted from the printed integration statement.
For an n-dimensional Lie group, choose a nonzero λ∈ΛnTe∗​G and define the invariant volume form on a Lie group by
ωg​(v1​,…,vn​)=λ(d(Lg−1​)g​v1​,…,d(Lg−1​)g​vn​).
(13)
Each dLg−1​ is an isomorphism, so ω is nowhere zero and defines an orientation. The composition identity L(hg)−1​∘Lh​=Lg−1​ gives Lh∗​ω=ω. Moreover, every left-invariant differential form of degree n is determined by its value at e, so the space of such forms is one-dimensional.
Under the supplied invariant-representative assumption, all top-degree de Rham cohomology classes are multiples of [ω]. For compact G, orient it so ω is positive; then ∫G​ω>0, whereas the Generalized Stokes theorem makes every exact top form have integral zero. Therefore [ω]=0 and
HdRn​(G;R)≅R​
(14)
under that assumption. For actual compact Lie groups, this argument applies when G is connected. The supplied assumption fails for disconnected groups: on S1×Z/2, the two components have independent degree-one classes, while globally left-invariant top forms form only a one-dimensional space. In general the top-degree de Rham cohomology of a compact Lie group is Rc, where c is its number of connected components.
For S2, use U=S2∖{N} and V=S2∖{S}. Stereographic projection identifies each with the plane. The disc argument gives η∣U​=dfU​ and η∣V​=dfV​ for any closed differential one-form η. The overlap U∩V is connected, so d(fU​−fV​)=0 makes the difference a constant. Adjust one potential by that constant; they glue to a global smooth function f with df=η. Thus
HdR1​(S2;R)=0​.
(15)
Finally, S3 is a compact connected Lie group: the identification
(a,b)⟼(a−b​ba​),∣a∣2+∣b∣2=1,
(16)
identifies it smoothly with the special unitary group SU(2). Matrix multiplication and inversion supply its smooth group operations. The preceding invariant-volume argument therefore gives
HdR3​(S3;R)≅R​.
(17)

A smooth vector field along a map γ:[a,b]→M is a smooth section of the pullback tangent bundle γ∗TM: it assigns V(t)∈Tγ(t)​M, with smooth coefficients in each local frame. It need not be the restriction of one ambient vector field, since a curve may return to the same point with different values of V.
An affine connection, also called a Koszul connection, induces a pullback connection on this bundle. In coordinates, write V=Vi(t)∂i​ and ∇∂j​​∂k​=Γijk​∂i​. Its covariant derivative along a curve is
Dt​V=(V˙i+Γijk​(γ(t))γ˙​jVk)∂i​.
(18)
The connection transformation law, or the Leibniz rule applied to a change of frame, makes this expression independent of the frame. In particular it is defined even when γ˙​=0; it differentiates the section's coefficients as well as the frame.
The field is parallel precisely when Dt​V=0. In a frame along a coordinate segment this is the linear ordinary differential equation
V˙i=−Aik​(t)Vk,Aik​(t)=Γijk​(γ(t))γ˙​j(t).
(19)
The Picard-Lindelöf theorem gives a unique solution with prescribed initial value. Smooth coefficients on compact subintervals are bounded, and the usual norm estimate for a linear ordinary differential equation prevents finite-time blowup there. A finite sequence of coordinate segments covers the image of the compact interval [a,b]; solve successively and use uniqueness on overlaps. This gives a unique parallel field on the whole interval for every Va​∈Tγ(a)​M.
Define parallel transport by τt​(Va​)=V(t). Linearity of the equation and uniqueness give τt​(cVa​+dWa​)=cτt​(Va​)+dτt​(Wa​). Solving along the reversed curve supplies its inverse. Therefore each τt​:Tγ(a)​M→Tγ(t)​M is a linear isomorphism.
For two affine connections, put Δ(X,Y)=∇X​Y−∇X​Y. Their rules give
Δ(fX,Y)=fΔ(X,Y),Δ(X,fY)=fΔ(X,Y),
(20)
since the two X(f)Y terms cancel. The tensoriality of this operation proves that the difference of affine connections is a tensor, of type (1,2), with smooth coordinate coefficients Γijk​−Γijk​.
A parametrized geodesic satisfies Dt​γ˙​=0, or the geodesic equation
γ¨​i+Γijk​(γ)γ˙​jγ˙​k=0.
(21)
With vi=γ˙​i, this becomes the first-order system γ˙​i=vi, v˙i=−Γijk​(γ)vjvk. Its right side is smooth, so the Picard-Lindelöf theorem gives a unique local solution for each initial point and tangent vector. This establishes uniqueness with the parametrization fixed.
The two accelerations differ by
Dt​γ˙​−Dt​γ˙​=Δ(γ˙​,γ˙​).
(22)
If Δ(v,v)=0 for every tangent vector, either acceleration vanishes exactly when the other does. Conversely, start the ∇-geodesic with arbitrary initial vector v at an arbitrary point. If it is also a ∇-geodesic with the same parameter, evaluating this identity initially gives Δ(v,v)=0. Hence
same parametrized geodesics⟺Δ(v,v)=0 for every v​.
(23)
Polarization makes the latter condition equivalent to Δ(u,v)+Δ(v,u)=0: parametrized geodesics determine the symmetric part of an affine connection. In particular torsion-free connections are determined by their parametrized geodesics, since their difference is also symmetric and must then vanish.

Let i:M↪N be the inclusion. The restriction of a connection to an embedded submanifold is the pullback connection on i∗TN=TN∣M​. Concretely, for a tangent vector field X on M and a local section S of TN∣M​, extend S smoothly off M and define ∇X​S by differentiating the extension in direction di(X). Two extensions differ by a field whose coefficients vanish on M. Their derivatives along every tangent curve in M vanish too, and the connection's coefficient terms are multiplied by the zero field. The answer is therefore independent of the extension. Dependence only on the value of the first argument follows from the connection's linearity over smooth functions.
The induced Riemannian metric is positive definite, so the fibrewise orthogonal projection π:TN∣M​→TM is smooth. For tangent fields define the projected ambient connection DX​Y=π(∇X​Y). It is real-linear and satisfies
DfX​Y=fDX​Y,DX​(fY)=X(f)Y+fDX​Y,
(24)
because πY=Y. Thus D is a Koszul connection on M.
The Levi-Civita connection is characterized by the torsion-free connection and metric connection conditions
∇X​Y−∇Y​X=[X,Y],X⟨Y,Z⟩=⟨∇X​Y,Z⟩+⟨Y,∇X​Z⟩.
(25)
These determine it uniquely. For the ambient Levi-Civita connection, the Gauss formula is ∇X​Y=DX​Y+II(X,Y), with
II(X,Y)=(I−π)∇X​Y.
(26)
Linearity over smooth functions in X is immediate. In Y, the additional X(f)Y term is tangent and is killed by I−π. Thus tensoriality makes II a bilinear map TM×M​TM→νM into the normal bundle. Its antisymmetric part is
II(X,Y)−II(Y,X)=(I−π)[X,Y]=0,
(27)
because the Lie bracket of vector fields tangent to M is tangent. This proves the symmetry of the second fundamental form in an arbitrary Riemannian ambient manifold.
Write the curvature operators as RN(v,w)y=∇v​∇w​y−∇w​∇v​y−∇[v,w]​y and RM with D in place of ∇. To match the four-slot notation in the requested identity, use
R(x,y,v,w)=⟨RN(v,w)y,x⟩,R(x,y,v,w)=⟨RM(v,w)y,x⟩.
(28)
This convention is stated because permuting the slots can change the displayed signs.
If η is a normal field and x is tangent, metric compatibility applied to ⟨η,x⟩=0 gives the tangential derivative of a normal field identity
⟨∇v​η,x⟩=−⟨η,II(v,x)⟩.
(29)
Using the Gauss formula and this identity,
⟨∇v​∇w​y,x⟩=⟨Dv​Dw​y,x⟩−⟨II(w,y),II(v,x)⟩.
(30)
Here the normal part of ∇v​(Dw​y) pairs to zero with x. Interchange v,w and subtract; the bracket term obeys ⟨∇[v,w]​y,x⟩=⟨D[v,w]​y,x⟩. It follows that
R(x,y,v,w)=R(x,y,v,w)−⟨II(w,y),II(v,x)⟩+⟨II(v,y),II(w,x)⟩.
(31)
Rearranging proves the Gauss equation in a curved ambient manifold:
R(x,y,v,w)=R(x,y,v,w)+⟨II(v,x),II(w,y)⟩−⟨II(v,y),II(w,x)⟩​.
(32)
All expressions are tensorial, so choosing local extensions of the four tangent vectors proves the pointwise identity at P. With flat Euclidean ambient space, RN=0 and this reduces to the usual Gauss equation.
