The Laurent polynomial ring is a localization of a ring:
R=k[X1​,X2​]X1​X2​​.
(1)
The Hilbert basis theorem makes k[X1​,X2​] a Noetherian ring, and Localization of a Noetherian ring preserves this property. Thus R is Noetherian​.
For separating finite Laurent support by an integer weight, take a nonzero Laurent polynomial r and let F⊂Z2 be its finite set of exponent pairs with nonzero coefficients. Choose an integer N such that
N>maxm,m′∈F​∣m1​−m1′​∣,
(2)
where the maximum is zero if F has only one member. Set (a1​,a2​)=(1,N). If m1​+Nm2​=m1′​+Nm2′​, then
N∣m2​−m2′​∣=∣m1​−m1′​∣<N,
(3)
so m2​=m2′​ and then m1​=m1′​. Consequently all powers of T in θ(1,N)​(r) are distinct. The linear independence of the Laurent monomials prevents cancellation, giving
θ(1,N)​(r)=0.​
(4)
The choice depends on the finite support of r; a single fixed substitution need not detect every nonzero element.
For Noetherianity of subalgebras of a one-variable Laurent polynomial ring, let A be a unital k-subalgebra of k[T,T−1]. If A=k, it is already a Noetherian ring. Otherwise we find a nonconstant f∈A and an ambient ring B containing A such that B is a finitely generated module over k[f].
If A⊆k[T], choose a nonconstant polynomial f=ad​Td+⋯+a0​ in A, where ad​=0. Then T is an integral element over k[f], since it satisfies the monic polynomial equation
Td+ad​ad−1​​Td−1+⋯+ad​a1​​T+ad​a0​−f​=0.
(5)
Thus B=k[T] is a finitely generated module over k[f], generated by 1,T,…,Td−1. The case A⊆k[T−1] is identical after replacing T by T−1.
In the remaining case A is contained in neither of these two polynomial rings. There is an element with a positive exponent and an element with a negative exponent. If either already has both, use it as f. Otherwise their sum has both, since the positive powers of the first and the negative powers of the second cannot cancel. We obtain
f=∑j=−pq​aj​Tj,p,q>0,a−p​aq​=0.
(6)
Multiplying this identity by Tp gives a polynomial equation for T with leading coefficient aq​:
∑j=−pq​aj​Tj+p−fTp=0.
(7)
Dividing by aq​ makes it monic. Similarly, multiplication by T−q gives a monic equation for T−1 after division by a−p​. Both generators are therefore integral elements over k[f], so B=k[T,T−1] is a finitely generated module over k[f].
In all three cases k[f] is a Noetherian ring, as a quotient of a one-variable polynomial ring. The subalgebra A⊆B is also a k[f]-submodule, so it is a Noetherian module. Every ideal of A is a k[f]-submodule of A and is therefore finitely generated over k[f], hence over A. This proves
Every k-subalgebra of k[T,T−1] is Noetherian.​
(8)
For the final request, take the following subalgebra of the two-variable Laurent polynomial ring:
A=k+X1​k[X1​,X2​]=k[X1​,X1​X2​,X1​X22​,…].​
(9)
Let J=X1​k[X1​,X2​], an ideal of A. We have
A/J≅k,J2=X12​k[X1​,X2​],J/J2≅X1​k[X2​]
(10)
as k-vector spaces. In particular, the classes of X1​X2j​, for j≥0, are linearly independent. If J had finitely many generators as an A-module, their classes would span J/J2 over A/J=k, contradicting its infinite dimension. Hence J is not finitely generated and A is not a Noetherian ring. This illustrates why the preceding result is special to one Laurent variable.

The Weak Hilbert Nullstellensatz states that if k is an algebraically closed field, every maximal ideal of k[Y1​,…,Ys​] is
(Y1​−a1​,…,Ys​−as​)for a unique (a1​,…,as​)∈ks.​
(11)
Equivalently, every proper ideal has a common zero. We prove the more general field statement, the Zariski lemma: a field finitely generated as a k-algebra is a finite field extension of k.
Write such a field as L=k[b1​,…,bs​]. Choose a maximal set of algebraically independent elements t1​,…,tr​ among these generators and denote the remaining generators by c1​,…,ch​. Each cj​ is an algebraic element over K=k(t1​,…,tr​), so L/K is a finite field extension. Clearing the denominators in their monic equations, choose a nonzero d∈k[t1​,…,tr​] such that each cj​ is an integral element over
D=k[t1​,…,tr​,d−1].
(12)
Because L is a field and d−1∈L, we have L=D[c1​,…,ch​]. Thus L is an integral extension of D.
A subring over which a field is integral must itself be a field. Indeed, for 0=a∈D, an integral equation for a−1 multiplied by am−1 expresses a−1 as an element of D. But if r>0, the ring D is not a field: choose an irreducible polynomial q(t1​)∈k[t1​] which does not divide d. There are infinitely many monic irreducible polynomials in k[t1​], by the usual product-plus-one argument, whereas only finitely many can divide the nonzero d. The prime ideal (q) stays proper after inverting d, so q has no inverse in D. Therefore r=0, proving that L/k is a finite field extension, and in particular an algebraic field extension.
Apply the Zariski lemma to the residue field k[Y1​,…,Ys​]/m of a maximal ideal. When k is an algebraically closed field, this field is k itself. The images ai​ of Yi​ then give m=(Y1​−a1​,…,Ys​−as​), proving the Weak Hilbert Nullstellensatz.
For the real Laurent polynomial ring, the same argument applies because it is the finitely generated algebra
R≅R[U1​,V1​,U2​,V2​]/(U1​V1​−1,U2​V2​−1).
(13)
Every residue field at a maximal ideal is consequently a finite field extension of R, and so is R or C. Here we use the fundamental theorem of algebra, which makes C the algebraic closure of R of degree two. The images of X1​,X2​ must be nonzero since these elements are units. It follows that the answer is
mα,β​={f∈R:f(α,β)=0},(α,β)∈(C×)2,​
(14)
with (α,β) and (α,β​) defining the same maximal ideal. If both coordinates are real, evaluation has image R; otherwise its image is C. Thus every displayed kernel is maximal, and the Zariski lemma shows that this list is exhaustive.
For explicit generating sets of an ideal, there are three cases. For real nonzero a,b,
ma,b​=(X1​−a,X2​−b).
(15)
If α=a∈R× and β=s+it with t=0,
ma,β​=(X1​−a, X22​−2sX2​+s2+t2).
(16)
If α=u+iv with v=0 and β=s+it, then
mα,β​=(X12​−2uX1​+u2+v2, X2​−s−vt​(X1​−u)).
(17)
The last two quotients are C; the nonzero coordinate condition ensures that the Laurent inverses exist in them. There are no further coincidences in the classification: equal kernels induce an R-isomorphism between the evaluation image fields. The only R-automorphisms of C are the identity and complex conjugation, so the two pairs must agree or be conjugate.
To prove the intersection assertion, fix a prime ideal P and an element f∈/P. The integral domain B=R/P has nonzero image fˉ​. Its localization of a ring B[fˉ​−1] is nonzero and still a finitely generated algebra over R, so it has a maximal ideal n. By the Zariski lemma, the field L=B[fˉ​−1]/n is finite over R.
The image of B in L is a subring containing R and is a finite-dimensional R-vector space. It is an integral domain, hence a field: multiplication by any nonzero element is an injective linear map of a finite-dimensional vector space and therefore surjective. The kernel of B→L is thus a maximal ideal which avoids fˉ​, since fˉ​ is invertible in L. Its inverse image in R is a maximal ideal containing P but avoiding f. As this works for every f∈/P,
P=m⊇Pm maximal​⋂​m.​
(18)
This is the Jacobson ring property for this Laurent polynomial ring.
For a nonzero finitely generated module M, its set of associated primes is
AssR​(M)={AnnR​(m):0=m∈M, AnnR​(m) is a prime ideal}.​
(19)
Here AnnR​(m)={a∈R:am=0} is the annihilator of the element. The Laurent polynomial ring R is a Noetherian ring, so the nonempty collection of annihilators of nonzero elements of M has a maximal member, say P=AnnR​(m). It is proper because m=0.
If ab∈P and b∈/P, then bm=0 and
P⊆AnnR​(bm),a∈AnnR​(bm).
(20)
Maximality forces AnnR​(bm)=P, hence a∈P. Thus P is a prime ideal, proving AssR​(M)=∅​. This argument in fact only needs that R is Noetherian, not the finite generation of M.
A proper ideal Q is P-primary when its radical of an ideal is P and
ab∈Q, a∈/Q ⟹ bj∈Q for some j≥1.​
(21)
Equivalently, ab∈Q and b∈/P imply a∈Q; equivalently, every zero divisor of R/Q is nilpotent. These formulations distinguish the actual primary ideal property from the weaker assertion that there is only one minimal prime ideal.
For the final example, set u=X1​−1 and v=X2​−1. Then
I=(u2,uv),P=(u).​
(22)
Since R/P≅R[X2​,X2−1​] is an integral domain, P is a prime ideal. Moreover I⊆P and u2∈I, so I​=P. Consequently P is the unique minimal prime ideal over I.
Nevertheless uv∈I, while u∈/I and no power of v belongs to I. For the first assertion, reduce modulo v: the image of I is (u2) in R[X1​,X1−1​], and u is not a multiple of u2 because u is a nonunit in this integral domain. For the second, v∈/P, so vj∈/P⊇I for every j≥1. Thus I is not a P-primary ideal: a unique minimal prime does not imply primary. In R/I, the nonzero class of u is killed by v, a zero divisor which is not nilpotent.

Put u=X1​−1 and v=X2​−1. Evaluation at (1,1) is surjective, and
R/(u,v)≅Z,
(23)
so its kernel is the prime ideal P=(u,v)​. In particular P/P2 is naturally an R/P=Z-module, generated by the classes of u,v.
For integers a,b, including negative integers, reduction modulo P2 gives
(1+u)a(1+v)b≡1+au+bv(modP2).
(24)
For negative powers, this follows from (1+u)−1≡1−u and (1+v)−1≡1−v; the mixed product uv vanishes modulo P2. Therefore a Laurent polynomial f satisfies
f≡θ(f)+D1​(f)u+D2​(f)v(modP2),Di​(f)=∂Xi​∂f​​(1,1)​∈Z.
(25)
The formal derivatives obey the product rule, so Di​(ab)=θ(a)Di​(b)+θ(b)Di​(a). If a,b∈P, both terms vanish. Hence both Di​ vanish on P2 and define a map
P/P2⟶Z2,[f]⟼(D1​(f),D2​(f)).
(26)
It sends [u] to (1,0) and [v] to (0,1), and the displayed expansion gives its inverse. Thus the conormal module has the explicit basis
P/P2≅Z2,[u],[v] a basis.​
(27)
Let A=RP​ be the localization at a prime ideal, with maximal ideal m=PRP​. Every nonzero integer lies outside P and is therefore inverted. The residue field is
A/m=Frac(R/P)=Q.​
(28)
Indeed there is an explicit identification
A≅Q[u,v](u,v)​:
(29)
inverting 1+u and 1+v has no effect in this local ring, and a polynomial denominator lies outside (u,v) precisely when its constant term is nonzero. Conversely, clearing rational denominators turns every such fraction into a fraction from RP​.
For the Krull dimension, the prime ideal correspondence for localization preserves the strict chain
(0)⊊(u)⊊(u,v)
(30)
of prime ideals in R. They are prime because the successive quotients are the integral domains R, Z[X2​,X2−1​], and Z. They remain distinct after localization at a prime ideal because they are all contained in P. Hence dimA≥2.
For the upper bound, R is a Noetherian ring by the Hilbert basis theorem and Localization of a Noetherian ring. Since P is generated by two elements, the Krull height theorem gives htP≤2. The prime ideal correspondence for localization identifies dimRP​ with htP, so
dimRP​=2.​
(31)
A Noetherian local ring (B,n) with residue field κ is a regular local ring if
dimκ​(n/n2)=dimB.​
(32)
By the Nakayama lemma, the dimension on the left is the minimal number of generators of n; this is also called the embedding dimension.
The localization of a module is exact and commutes with quotients and products of ideals. Applying it to the already computed conormal module gives the cotangent space of a local ring
m/m2≅(P/P2)⊗Z​Q≅Q2.
(33)
Its dimension is two, equal to dimA. Therefore RP​ is a regular local ring​. Notice that P/P2 has a basis over Z, whereas the localized cotangent space of a local ring has a basis over the residue field Q.

An integral domain A is an integrally closed domain if every element of its fraction field which is an integral element over A belongs to A. Explicitly, if z∈FracA satisfies
zd+ad−1​zd−1+⋯+a0​=0,ai​∈A,
(34)
then z∈A. Thus A is integrally closed exactly when its integral closure in its fraction field is A​.
Suppose A is a unique factorization domain and z=a/b is integral, with a,b∈A, b=0, and with no common irreducible element as a factor. Multiplying its monic equation by bd gives
ad+ad−1​ad−1b+⋯+a0​bd=0.
(35)
Hence b divides ad. Every irreducible element dividing b is a prime element, so it would divide a, contrary to the choice of the fraction. Therefore b is a unit, and z∈A. We have proved
Every unique factorization domain is integrally closed.​
(36)
The going-down theorem is the following. Let A⊆B be an integral extension of integral domains with A an integrally closed domain. If
p1​⊆p2​in A,q2​∩A=p2​for a prime ideal q2​⊂B,
(37)
then
there is a prime ideal q1​⊆q2​ with q1​∩A=p1​.​
(38)
We first prove this for a module-finite ring extension and then remove that extra hypothesis. We use the Lying-over theorem, the going-up theorem, and the incomparability theorem for integral extensions: primes exist over every base prime, prime chains can be extended upwards, and distinct primes over the same base prime cannot contain one another. The going-up theorem follows by applying the Lying-over theorem to the quotient by the lower prime. These results concern arbitrary integral extensions; none assumes the going-down theorem.
For the finite case, let K=FracA and L=FracB. Since B is module-finite, L/K is a finite field extension. Take a finite normal field extension E/K containing L, obtained as a splitting field of the minimal polynomials of finitely many field generators. The extension E/K need not be a separable field extension. Let C be the integral closure of A in E. Then B⊆C, and C is integral over both A and B.
We need one auxiliary fact: the finite group G=AutK​(E) acts transitively on the prime ideals of C over any fixed prime ideal p of A. First observe that an element w∈E fixed by all of G is a purely inseparable algebraic element over K. Indeed, all K-embeddings of K(w) into an algebraic closure extend to K-automorphisms of the normal field extension E. Hence the minimal polynomial of an algebraic element w has only one distinct root. In characteristic zero this says w∈K; in characteristic p>0 it says wpe∈K for some e≥0.
Now let Q,Q′ be primes of C over p, and suppose Q is different from every σ(Q′), σ∈G. By the incomparability theorem for integral extensions, Q is contained in none of these conjugate primes. The prime avoidance lemma supplies
x∈Q∖⋃σ∈G​σ(Q′).
(39)
The product
h=∏σ∈G​σ(x)
(40)
is fixed by G, is integral over A, and lies in Q because one factor is x. Choose an exponent e0​ equal to 1 in characteristic zero, or a power of p in characteristic p, such that he0​∈K. Since A is an integrally closed domain, he0​∈A. It also lies in Q∩A=p, hence in Q′. Because Q′ is a prime ideal, some factor σ(x) lies in Q′. This means x∈σ−1(Q′), contradicting our choice. Thus Q=σ(Q′) for some σ, proving transitivity. The power e0​ accounts for the possible purely inseparable field extension and makes the argument valid in every characteristic.
Lift the prescribed q2​ to a prime Q2​ of C by the Lying-over theorem for B⊆C. Separately, choose a prime Q1′​ over p1​ and use the going-up theorem to find Q2′​⊇Q1′​ over p2​. Transitivity gives σ∈G with σ(Q2′​)=Q2​. Then
Q1​=σ(Q1′​)⊆Q2​,Q1​∩A=p1​.
(41)
Contracting to B gives q1​=Q1​∩B⊆q2​ with the required contraction. This proves the going-down theorem for the module-finite case.
For a general integral extension A⊆B, consider Bq2​​ and the ideal
J=p1​Bq2​​.
(42)
We claim that J is disjoint from A∖p1​. Otherwise, clearing the finitely many denominators in an expression for an element a∈J∩(A∖p1​) gives
sa=∑i=1h​ai​bi​,s∈B∖q2​,ai​∈p1​,bi​∈B.
(43)
The subalgebra B0​=A[s,b1​,…,bh​] is a module-finite ring extension of A, since all its generators are integral elements. Apply the finite case to q2​∩B0​: there is a prime Q1​⊆q2​∩B0​ contracting to p1​. The displayed identity puts sa in Q1​, but a∈/Q1​. Thus s∈Q1​⊆q2​, a contradiction.
Localize Bq2​​ further at the multiplicative subset A∖p1​. The extension of J is a proper ideal, by the disjointness just proved. Choose a maximal ideal containing it and contract back to a prime ideal of Bq2​​. This prime contains J and avoids A∖p1​. Its contraction q1​ to B lies inside q2​ by the prime ideal correspondence for localization, and its contraction to A is exactly p1​. This completes the proof of the going-down theorem without any finite generation assumption on B.

The Artin-Rees lemma states that, for a Noetherian ring R, an ideal I, a finitely generated module M, and a submodule N⊆M, there is c≥0 such that
IjM∩N=Ij−c(IcM∩N)for every j≥c.​
(44)
Thus the filtration on N induced from the I-adic filtration of M is eventually determined by one of its terms.
For the proof, form the Rees ring and its Rees module:
R(I)=⨁j≥0​Ijtj⊆R[t],RI​(M)=⨁j≥0​IjMtj.
(45)
Since R is a Noetherian ring, write I=(a1​,…,as​). Then R(I)=R[a1​t,…,as​t], so it is a Noetherian ring by the Hilbert basis theorem. Generators of M in degree zero generate RI​(M) as a module over R(I), so this is a Noetherian module.
The graded submodule
L=⨁j≥0​(IjM∩N)tj
(46)
is therefore finitely generated. Choose homogeneous generators zα​tdα​ with dα​≤c for some c. For j≥c, taking degree-j components gives
IjM∩N=∑α​Ij−dα​zα​=Ij−c∑α​Ic−dα​zα​.
(47)
The same generators show that the final sum is exactly IcM∩N. This proves the Artin-Rees lemma. If L=0, one may simply take c=0.
Now let
K=⋂j≥1​IjM.
(48)
It is a submodule of the Noetherian module M, hence a finitely generated module. Apply the Artin-Rees lemma with N=K and j=c+1. Since K is contained in every IjM (including I0M=M), its conclusion reduces to
K=IK.
(49)
Write generators as y1​,…,yh​. There is a matrix A=(aij​) with entries in I such that yi​=∑j​aij​yj​. Multiplication by the adjugate of Ih​−A shows that
det(Ih​−A)yi​=0for all i.
(50)
The determinant is congruent to 1 modulo I, so it is 1+r for some r∈I. This determinant trick produces a single such element annihilating all of K, and in particular annihilating each m∈K. If K=0, use r=0.
Conversely, if (1+r)m=0 with r∈I, then m=−rm. Iterating gives m=(−r)jm∈IjM for every j≥1. Hence the exact description is
j≥1⋂​IjM={m∈M:some r∈I satisfies (1+r)m=0}.​
(51)
In a local ring with I contained in the maximal ideal, every 1+r is a unit, so this also yields the usual vanishing form of the Krull intersection theorem.
For a failure without the Noetherian ring hypothesis, let D=Z[1/2]≥0​ and use the semigroup algebra
R=k[tq:q∈D]=⋃h≥0​k[t1/2h].
(52)
Its elements are finite sums of formal monomials tq, with multiplication tqtq′=tq+q′. Each ring in the union is a polynomial ring and the inclusions are injective, so R is an integral domain. It is not a Noetherian ring, since
(t)⊊(t1/2)⊊(t1/4)⊊⋯.
(53)
Indeed the generator on the left is the square of the next generator, while division in the opposite direction would require a negative exponent, which is unavailable in R.
Let I be the ideal of elements with zero constant term, equivalently
I=(tq:q∈D, q>0).
(54)
It is proper because taking the constant term gives R/I≅k. Every generator tq is (tq/2)2, with tq/2∈I, so I2=I. Thus this is a nonzero idempotent ideal, with Ij=I for every j≥1. Take
m=t∈j≥1⋂​Ij.​
(55)
For every r∈I, the constant term of 1+r is 1, so 1+r=0. Since R is an integral domain and t=0, we have (1+r)t=0. This gives all three requested objects and proves that no element of the required form annihilates m.

For a finitely generated module V=⨁d​Vd​ over the graded ring S=k[X1​,…,Xn​], with each Xi​ of degree one, its Poincare series of a graded module is
PV​(t)=∑d​dimk​(Vd​)td.
(56)
This is also its Hilbert series. One usually takes d≥0; allowing an integer grading also permits finitely many negative degrees. Homogeneous generators of degrees a1​,…,ah​ give a surjection ⨁j​S(−aj​)→V, where the graded shift is defined by V(−a)d​=Vd−a​. Consequently every Vd​ is a finite-dimensional vector space and Vd​=0 for all sufficiently negative d.
The Hilbert-Serre theorem in the standard grading says
PV​(t)=(1−t)nQ(t)​,Q(t)∈Z[t,t−1].​
(57)
For a nonnegatively graded V, the numerator is in Z[t]. Equivalently, the only possible pole of this rational expression is at t=1, with order at most n.
We prove the Hilbert-Serre theorem by induction on the number of variables. For n=0, S=k and V is a finite-dimensional graded module; thus PV​(t) is a Laurent polynomial. Suppose n>0 and put
K=0:V​Xn​={v∈V:Xn​v=0},C=V/Xn​V.
(58)
The Hilbert basis theorem makes S a Noetherian ring, so K is a finitely generated module, as is C. Both are graded modules killed by Xn​, hence finitely generated over S′=k[X1​,…,Xn−1​].
The graded exact sequence
0⟶K(−1)⟶V(−1) Xn​ ​V⟶C⟶0
(59)
identifies the kernel and cokernel of multiplication by Xn​. Taking the alternating sum of the finite-dimensional degree components yields
tPK​(t)−tPV​(t)+PV​(t)−PC​(t)=0,
(60)
so
(1−t)PV​(t)=PC​(t)−tPK​(t).
(61)
By induction the right side has denominator (1−t)n−1, which proves the required denominator (1−t)n. Since (1−t)nPV​(t) has no negative powers when V is nonnegatively graded, its Laurent polynomial numerator is an ordinary polynomial in that case.
For n≥1, this also implies the usual Hilbert polynomial consequence. Writing Q(t)=∑j​qj​tj and using
(1−t)n1​=∑d≥0​(n−1d+n−1​)td
(62)
gives, for all sufficiently large d,
dimk​Vd​=∑j​qj​(n−1d−j+n−1​),
(63)
a polynomial in d of degree at most n−1. For n=0, the graded pieces are eventually zero.
For the final request, a free resolution of V is an exact sequence
⋯⟶F2​d2​​F1​d1​​F0​⟶V⟶0
(64)
with each Fi​ a free module. In the graded setting we take finite direct sums of shifts S(−a) and maps preserving degree. The resolution has length at most n if Fi​=0 for every i>n. The syzygy modules are the successive kernels which record the relations among generators, then relations among those relations, and so on.
Put m=(X1​,…,Xn​), the homogeneous maximal ideal with S/m=k. Choose a homogeneous k-basis of V/mV and lift it to V. These lifts generate V by the graded Nakayama lemma: a bounded-below graded module W satisfying W=mW must be zero, since a nonzero homogeneous element of least degree could not be a sum of variables times elements of lower degrees. Apply this to the quotient by the submodule generated by the chosen lifts. This gives a surjection F0​→V inducing an isomorphism modulo m.
Its kernel is finitely generated because S is a Noetherian ring. Repeat the construction for that kernel, then for each subsequent kernel. We obtain a minimal graded free resolution, meaning
di​(Fi​)⊆mFi−1​(i≥1).
(65)
Indeed, at each stage the free cover induces an isomorphism modulo m, so its kernel lies in m times its source. The sequence is exact by construction, though it may at first appear infinite.
The Koszul complex on X1​,…,Xn​ is
Ki​=⋀iSn≅S(−i)(in​),0≤i≤n,
(66)
with basis vectors ej​ given degree one, and differential
∂(ej1​​∧⋯∧eji​​)=∑a=1i​(−1)a−1Xja​​ej1​​∧⋯∧eja​​​∧⋯∧eji​​.
(67)
The hat means omission of that factor. Every pair of terms in ∂2 cancels with opposite signs, so this is a chain complex. The sequence X1​,…,Xn​ is a regular sequence: after quotienting by the first j−1 variables, the next variable is a non-zero-divisor in the remaining polynomial ring.
The Koszul complex is consequently exact in positive degrees and has H0​=k, so it is a Koszul resolution of k of length n. For completeness, one proves this by induction: for one variable it is 0→SX1​​S→S/(X1​)→0. Appending Xj​ constructs the mapping cone of multiplication by Xj​ on the previous chain complex. Its homology is zero in positive degrees because Xj​ acts injectively on S/(X1​,…,Xj−1​); its degree-zero homology is the further quotient. Here a mapping cone combines a chain complex with a shifted copy and adds the given multiplication map to the differential, thereby measuring its kernel and cokernel on homology.
The Tor functor ToriS​(V,k) is defined as the degree-i homology of F∙​⊗S​k for a free resolution F∙​ of V. It can equally be computed as the homology of V⊗S​K∙​, using the Koszul resolution of k. To see the equality, form the double complex F∙​⊗S​K∙​. Taking homology first along F∙​ leaves V⊗S​K∙​, since each Ki​ is a free module; taking homology first along K∙​ leaves F∙​⊗S​k, since each Fi​ is free. The finite sums along each total degree identify both with the homology of the total complex. This also explains the symmetry used in computing the Tor functor.
Since Ki​=0 for i>n, we have ToriS​(V,k)=0 for i>n. On the other hand, all differentials of the minimal graded free resolution become zero after tensoring with k=S/m, so
ToriS​(V,k)≅Fi​⊗S​k=Fi​/mFi​.
(68)
Thus Fi​/mFi​=0 for i>n, and the graded Nakayama lemma implies Fi​=0 for those i. We have proved the required Hilbert syzygy theorem:
0⟶Fn​⟶⋯⟶F0​⟶V⟶0,​
(69)
where some initial Fi​ may be zero. The argument supplies finitely generated graded free modules, which is stronger than merely giving an ungraded free resolution.
