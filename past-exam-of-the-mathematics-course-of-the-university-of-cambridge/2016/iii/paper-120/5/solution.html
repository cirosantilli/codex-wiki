A many-one reduction A≤m​B is a total computable function f:N→N such that
n∈A⟺f(n)∈B.
(29)
A Turing reduction A≤T​B is an oracle machine which, with membership oracle B, halts on every input and computes the indicator function χA​. It may make finitely many adaptive queries on each input. A many-one reduction gives a Turing reduction by a single query, but Turing reducibility allows more general procedures.
The Friedberg–Muchnik theorem states that there are computably enumerable sets A,B with incomparable Turing degrees:
A≤T​BandB≤T​A.​
(30)
Here is a finite-injury priority construction proving it. Effectively enumerate all Turing functionals (Φe​). Arrange the requirements in the priority order
R0​,S0​,R1​,S1​,…,Re​:ΦeA​=χB​,Se​:ΦeB​=χA​.
(31)
Equality would require the Turing functional to be total and give the correct bit at every input; one disagreement or one undefined input defeats it.
Start with A0​=B0​=∅ and enumerate elements without ever removing them. A strategy has a private witness, a flag recording whether it has acted, and a restraint on its oracle. Every new witness is larger than every previously assigned witness and every current higher-priority restraint. Retired witnesses are never reused. The only possible enumeration of a witness is by its owner, so an unacted witness remains outside its target set.
The strategy for Re​ chooses a witness x and waits for a stage at which the bounded simulation gives
ΦeAs​​(x)↓=0.
(32)
On seeing this, put x into B, declare the strategy acted, and impose restraint u on A, where u is the oracle use: one more than the largest queried number, or zero if there were no queries. Initialize every lower-priority strategy, discarding its current witness, acted flag and restraint. An acted strategy does nothing further until initialized. The strategy for Se​ is identical with A,B interchanged. Waiting specifically for zero is enough: if the eventual output is nonzero while the witness stays out, it already disagrees with the target indicator function.
For a completely effective schedule, at stage s visit the first s+1 strategies in priority order. Assign an unassigned witness when visited. Simulate each unacted strategy's computation for at most s steps with the current finite oracle. If some strategy can act, perform the first such action and end the stage; otherwise end after all visits. All steps are finite and algorithmic, so the limiting A,B are computably enumerable sets.
Every lower-priority enumeration respects higher-priority restraints. When its witness was chosen it lay above these restraints. Any subsequent action of a higher-priority strategy would have initialized it, so its current witness remains safe to enumerate. The initialization of all lower strategies also removes their obsolete restraints before they can obstruct a legitimate higher-priority action.
We verify finite injury by mathematical induction on priority. The first strategy is never injured and acts at most once. If all predecessors of a strategy act finitely often, there is a last stage when a higher-priority action initializes it. It is subsequently visited, chooses its permanent witness, and acts at most once. Thus it too acts finitely often and all its lower-priority strategies suffer only finitely many injuries. The scheduling cannot starve a fixed strategy: after the finitely many higher-priority actions, all sufficiently large stages visit it.
Consider the final uninjured Re​ with witness x. If it acts, then x∈B permanently, and every later lower-priority enumeration into A respects its restraint. No later higher-priority action occurs. The preserved computation is therefore
ΦeA​(x)=0=1=χB​(x).
(33)
If it never acts, x∈/B. Were ΦeA​=χB​, the final computation on x would halt with zero and have finite oracle use. The finitely many oracle bits it queries eventually stabilize, and a sufficiently late bounded stage simulation would see exactly this zero computation. The strategy would then act, a contradiction. Hence Re​ holds in this case too. The same reasoning proves every Se​.
All possible Turing reductions in both directions are excluded. This proves the Friedberg–Muchnik theorem; in particular both constructed Turing degrees are noncomputable, since a computable set is Turing reducible to every oracle.
