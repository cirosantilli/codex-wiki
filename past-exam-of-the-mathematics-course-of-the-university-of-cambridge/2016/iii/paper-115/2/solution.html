In a manifold chart, write a smooth differential form as η=∑I​aI​dxi1​∧⋯∧dxip​, with I increasing. Define its exterior derivative by
dη=∑I,j​∂j​aI​dxj∧dxi1​∧⋯∧dxip​.
(8)
To check that this definition is global, change coordinates to y. The chain rule expresses dxi as ∑j​(∂j​xi)dyj. Differentiating it gives zero because the second partial derivatives of xi are symmetric and dyk∧dyj is antisymmetric. The coordinate definition satisfies the graded Leibniz rule, so differentiating the transformed expression for η leaves exactly ∑I​daI​∧dxi1​∧⋯∧dxip​. This agrees with the original formula and proves independence of the manifold chart. The same cancellation of mixed derivatives gives d2=0.
The de Rham cohomology is therefore
HdRp​(M;R)=im(d:Ωp−1(M)→Ωp(M))ker(d:Ωp(M)→Ωp+1(M))​,
(9)
the vector space of closed differential forms modulo exact differential forms.
Here is a Green-theorem proof of exactness on a disc. Identify the disc with a round disc centred at zero, and let η=Pdx+Qdy be a closed differential form. Set f(x)=∫[0,x]​η. For x and x+h in the disc, Green theorem on the oriented triangle with vertices 0,x,x+h gives zero boundary integral, since ∂x​Q−∂y​P=0. Hence
f(x+h)−f(x)=∫[x,x+h]​η=∫01​ηx+sh​(h)ds.
(10)
Taking h=εv and dividing by ε yields dfx​(v)=ηx​(v). The radial integral defining f is smooth, so η=df and
HdR1​(Δ;R)=0​.
(11)
Degenerate triangles follow by continuity. The same proof works on any star-shaped plane domain, including the whole plane.
Integration requires an orientation: take M to be an oriented compact n-dimensional smooth manifold without boundary. The Generalized Stokes theorem says ∫M​dβ=0 for every smooth (n−1)-form β. Every n-form is closed by dimension, and changing it by an exact differential form leaves its integral unchanged. Thus
[η]⟼∫M​η
(12)
is a well-defined linear map HdRn​(M;R)→R. Compactness alone does not supply the orientation omitted from the printed integration statement.
For an n-dimensional Lie group, choose a nonzero λ∈ΛnTe∗​G and define the invariant volume form on a Lie group by
ωg​(v1​,…,vn​)=λ(d(Lg−1​)g​v1​,…,d(Lg−1​)g​vn​).
(13)
Each dLg−1​ is an isomorphism, so ω is nowhere zero and defines an orientation. The composition identity L(hg)−1​∘Lh​=Lg−1​ gives Lh∗​ω=ω. Moreover, every left-invariant differential form of degree n is determined by its value at e, so the space of such forms is one-dimensional.
Under the supplied invariant-representative assumption, all top-degree de Rham cohomology classes are multiples of [ω]. For compact G, orient it so ω is positive; then ∫G​ω>0, whereas the Generalized Stokes theorem makes every exact top form have integral zero. Therefore [ω]=0 and
HdRn​(G;R)≅R​
(14)
under that assumption. For actual compact Lie groups, this argument applies when G is connected. The supplied assumption fails for disconnected groups: on S1×Z/2, the two components have independent degree-one classes, while globally left-invariant top forms form only a one-dimensional space. In general the top-degree de Rham cohomology of a compact Lie group is Rc, where c is its number of connected components.
For S2, use U=S2∖{N} and V=S2∖{S}. Stereographic projection identifies each with the plane. The disc argument gives η∣U​=dfU​ and η∣V​=dfV​ for any closed differential one-form η. The overlap U∩V is connected, so d(fU​−fV​)=0 makes the difference a constant. Adjust one potential by that constant; they glue to a global smooth function f with df=η. Thus
HdR1​(S2;R)=0​.
(15)
Finally, S3 is a compact connected Lie group: the identification
(a,b)⟼(a−b​ba​),∣a∣2+∣b∣2=1,
(16)
identifies it smoothly with the special unitary group SU(2). Matrix multiplication and inversion supply its smooth group operations. The preceding invariant-volume argument therefore gives
HdR3​(S3;R)≅R​.
(17)
