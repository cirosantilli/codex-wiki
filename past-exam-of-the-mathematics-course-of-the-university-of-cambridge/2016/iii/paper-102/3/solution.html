Schur's lemma says that a nonzero intertwiner between irreducible representations is an isomorphism; over C, every endomorphism of a finite-dimensional irreducible representation is scalar. To prove the Schur lemma, let T:V→W be a intertwining operator between irreducible representations. Its kernel and image of a linear map are invariant. If T=0, irreducibility forces kerT=0 and imT=W. This proves the first assertion over any field. In particular the endomorphisms of an irreducible representation form a division ring.
When V is finite-dimensional over an algebraically closed field, an endomorphism T has an eigenvalue λ. The endomorphism T−λI has nonzero kernel. By the first assertion it must be zero, so T=λI. Consequently, for complex finite-dimensional irreducible representations, the space of intertwining operators has dimension zero for nonisomorphic representations and dimension one for isomorphic representations.
Every finite-dimensional representation of a complex semisimple Lie algebra is completely reducible. We prove the Weyl complete reducibility theorem using the allowed Casimir operator facts, without assuming a splitting in advance. For dual bases xi​,xi with respect to the Killing form, the Casimir element
Ω=∑i​xi​xi∈U(g)
(27)
is central in the universal enveloping algebra. Hence its Casimir operator CM​=∑i​ρM​(xi​)ρM​(xi) commutes with the action on every Lie algebra representation M and is compatible with subrepresentations, quotient representations, and intertwining operators. On the trivial Lie algebra representation it acts by zero. On every nontrivial finite-dimensional Irreducible Lie algebra representation it acts by a nonzero scalar.
For clarity, the last fact can be expressed by the Casimir eigenvalue formula: on an irreducible with dominant integral weight λ, the scalar is (λ,λ+2ρ), with the inner product induced by the Killing form and ρ the half-sum of positive roots. On the real span of the weights this inner product is positive definite, and the scalar is positive for λ=0. For a semisimple Lie algebra with several simple factors the scalars add, so a nontrivial representation still gives a nonzero scalar. These are properties of the Casimir operator being used here.
First establish Casimir splitting of a trivial quotient. Suppose
0⟶N⟶E⟶q​C⟶0
(28)
is a short exact sequence of finite-dimensional Lie algebra representations, with trivial quotient. The generalized eigenspaces of CE​ are invariant, so
E=E0​⊕⨁c=0​Ec​.
(29)
Each Ec​ with c=0 maps to zero under q: applying a sufficiently large power of CE​−cI and using CC​=0 gives (−c)rq(e)=0. Thus q(E0​)=C.
Take a composition series of a module of E0​. The Casimir operator is nilpotent on E0​, so its scalar on every irreducible composition factor is zero. The stated Casimir operator property makes every such factor trivial. In a basis adapted to the composition series of a module, the image of g on E0​ therefore consists of strictly upper triangular matrices, so that image is solvable. The allowed fact that a semisimple Lie algebra acts trivially on every one-dimensional representation implies that g is a perfect Lie algebra: otherwise a nonzero linear functional on g/[g,g] would define a nontrivial one-dimensional Lie algebra representation. Thus g=[g,g], and its image is consequently a perfect Lie algebra too. A perfect Lie algebra that is also a solvable Lie algebra is zero, since its derived series of a Lie algebra is constant until it vanishes. Hence g acts trivially on E0​. Choose e∈E0​ with q(e)=1. The map 1↦e is an invariant section, proving the split short exact sequence assertion.
Now let W⊆V be any nonzero subrepresentation. On the Hom representation Hom(V,W) the action is
(x⋅f)(v)=xf(v)−f(xv).
(30)
Consider the invariant subspace
E={f∈Hom(V,W):f∣W​∈CIW​}.
(31)
Restriction produces a short exact sequence
0⟶Hom(V/W,W)⟶E⟶CIW​⟶0.
(32)
Surjectivity follows by extending IW​ to a linear map on V. The quotient is trivial, because IW​ commutes with the action on W. The preceding Casimir splitting of a trivial quotient yields an invariant p:V→W with p∣W​=IW​. Thus
V=W⊕kerp,
(33)
and kerp is invariant. The zero subrepresentation also has a complement. Repeatedly splitting off an irreducible subrepresentation now gives a direct sum of irreducibles, proving the Weyl complete reducibility theorem.
A derivation of a Lie algebra is a linear map D:g→g satisfying the Leibniz rule
D[x,y]=[Dx,y]+[x,Dy].
(34)
The space Der(g) is a vector subspace of End(g). Equip it with the commutator [D,E]=DE−ED. Expanding the Leibniz rule twice gives
DE[x,y]ED[x,y]​=[DEx,y]+[Ex,Dy]+[Dx,Ey]+[x,DEy],=[EDx,y]+[Dx,Ey]+[Ex,Dy]+[x,EDy].​
(35)
Subtracting proves that [D,E] is again a derivation of a Lie algebra. Antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity hold for the commutator in every associative endomorphism algebra, so this defines the derivation Lie algebra.
The Jacobi identity says that adx is a derivation of a Lie algebra. Moreover, for D∈Der(g),
[D,adx](y)=D[x,y]−[x,Dy]=[Dx,y],
(36)
so
[D,adx]=ad(Dx).​
(37)
This makes adg an ideal of a Lie algebra in Der(g).
Finally suppose g is semisimple. Let g act on Der(g) by x⋅D=[adx,D]. The Weyl complete reducibility theorem supplies an invariant complement M to adg. For D∈M, invariance gives [adx,D]∈M, while the ideal identity above gives [adx,D]∈adg. Their intersection is zero, so ad(Dx)=0 for every x. The center of a Lie algebra of a semisimple Lie algebra is zero, hence Dx=0 for every x, and M=0. Therefore
Der(g)=adg.​
(38)
Every derivation of a Lie algebra is inner, and the element giving it is unique because the center of a Lie algebra vanishes.
