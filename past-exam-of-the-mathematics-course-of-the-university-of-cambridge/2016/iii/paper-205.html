Write V={1,…,p}. The global Markov property for a directed acyclic graph says that, for every three pairwise disjoint vertex sets A,B,C,
A and B are D-separated by C in G⟹ZA​⊥⊥ZB​∣ZC​.
(1)
Thus graphical D-separation implies probabilistic conditional independence. Causal minimality means that P has this global Markov property for a directed acyclic graph, but has it for no proper subgraph of G obtained by deleting edges while keeping all vertices. Faithfulness of a directed acyclic graph means that the displayed implication is an equivalence: the conditional independences of P are exactly the D-separations of G. In particular it includes the global Markov property for a directed acyclic graph.
A causally minimal distribution can nevertheless have an extra independence. Here is a causally minimal nonfaithful Gaussian model. Take three independent random variables E1​,E2​,E3​, each with the standard normal distribution, and set
Z1​=E1​,Z2​=Z1​+E2​,Z3​=Z2​−Z1​+E3​.
(2)
Use the Directed acyclic graph with edges 1→2, 1→3 and 2→3. The equations give a directed acyclic graph factorization, so the global Markov property for a directed acyclic graph holds. Alternatively this complete acyclic graph imposes no nontrivial D-separations. But Z3​=E2​+E3​ is independent of Z1​=E1​, even though the direct edge 1→3 prevents their D-separation. Hence faithfulness of a directed acyclic graph fails.
To check causal minimality explicitly, the covariance matrix is
Σ=​110​121​012​​.
(3)
The linear transformation from (E1​,E2​,E3​) to (Z1​,Z2​,Z3​) is invertible, so this is a nonsingular multivariate normal distribution. Deleting 1→2 would require Z1​⊥Z2​, whereas Cov(Z1​,Z2​)=1. Deleting 1→3 would require Z1​⊥Z3​∣Z2​, whereas the conditional covariance is
Cov(Z1​,Z3​∣Z2​)=0−21⋅1​=−21​.
(4)
Deleting 2→3 would require Z2​⊥Z3​∣Z1​, whereas its conditional covariance is 1−1⋅0=1. By Gaussian conditional independence, each nonzero conditional covariance rules out the required independence. Every proper subgraph is contained in a graph obtained by a single edge deletion, and further deletion can only add Markov restrictions. Thus no proper subgraph is Markov for P, proving causal minimality.
For the Markov blanket, use its standard convention that the focal vertex is excluded:
B=mb(k)=(pa(k)∪ch(k)∪pa(ch(k)))∖{k}.
(5)
We prove Markov blanket D-separation by examining an arbitrary path from k to v∈/B∪{k}. If its first edge points into k, its next vertex is a parent of k, hence a conditioned noncollider in B, and the path is blocked. Otherwise the path starts k→h at a child h∈B. If the next edge points out of h, then h is again a conditioned noncollider. If it points into h, the path begins k→h←u. Although conditioning on the collider h opens that local segment, u is another parent of h, hence belongs to B. The outgoing arrow u→h makes u a noncollider on the path, so conditioning on u blocks it. Since v is outside B, none of these blocking vertices can be the endpoint.
Every path is therefore blocked by B. Applying the global Markov property for a directed acyclic graph gives
Zk​⊥⊥ZV∖(B∪{k})​∣ZB​.​
(6)
This proves the requested screening property without any positivity assumption on P.
Finally assume faithfulness of a directed acyclic graph and let A have the stated screening property. Then k and every vertex outside A∪{k} must be D-separated by A. If a parent or child h of k were missing from A, the one-edge path between them would be active, a contradiction. Thus every parent and child is in A. If another parent u of a child h were missing from A, the path k→h←u would be active: its only interior vertex is the collider h, already in A. This is again a contradiction. Consequently
A⊇mb(k).​
(7)
Under faithfulness the graphical blanket is contained in every screening set. This is the minimal Markov blanket under faithfulness; the preceding argument also shows that the blanket itself is a screening set.

Use the normalization
β​λ​=argminβ∈Rp​{21​∥Y−Xβ∥22​+2λ​∥β∥22​}.​
(8)
This is ridge regression; the penalty is the squared Euclidean norm. The centering removes the need for an intercept. Differentiating gives the normal equation
(XTX+λIp​)β​λ​=XTY.
(9)
For every nonzero v, vT(XTX+λIp​)v=∥Xv∥22​+λ∥v∥22​>0. The coefficient matrix is therefore a positive-definite matrix, even if the design matrix has deficient matrix rank. The objective is a strictly convex function, so the unique minimizer is the closed-form ridge regression estimator, with fitted values
Y=X(XTX+λIp​)−1XTY.​
(10)
For the primal-dual identity for ridge regression, put K=XXT and observe
(XTX+λIp​)XT=XT(K+λIn​).
(11)
Both parenthesized matrices are positive-definite matrices. Multiplying by their inverses gives
(XTX+λIp​)−1XT=XT(K+λIn​)−1.
(12)
Thus the same fitted vector has the dual expression
Y=K(K+λIn​)−1Y.​
(13)
No inverse of XTX or K alone was needed. This is also the kernel-ridge hat matrix for the linear kernel.
Here a positive-definite kernel means a symmetric real-valued function k whose every finite kernel matrix is positive semidefinite: for all m, x1​,…,xm​∈X and a1​,…,am​∈R,
∑i,j=1m​ai​aj​k(xi​,xj​)≥0.
(14)
This is the usual positive-semidefinite kernel convention; strict positivity for distinct points is not required. In particular k(x,x)≥0. Write a=k(x,x), b=k(x′,x′) and r=k(x,x′). Positivity of the two-point kernel matrix implies
a+2tr+t2b≥0(t∈R).
(15)
If b>0, substitute t=−r/b to obtain a−r2/b≥0. If b=0, the affine expression a+2tr can be nonnegative for every t only if r=0. Both cases give the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for positive-semidefinite kernels:
k(x,x′)2≤k(x,x)k(x′,x′).​
(16)
The zero-diagonal case is essential when the positive-definite kernel is not strictly positive.
To construct a feature map, let F be the real vector space of finite formal linear combinations of symbols ex​, one for each x∈X. Define a symmetric bilinear form by
B(∑i​ai​exi​​,∑j​bj​eyj​​)=∑i,j​ai​bj​k(xi​,yj​).
(17)
The positive-definite kernel property makes this a positive semidefinite bilinear form. It need not yet be an inner product: distinct formal vectors can have zero squared length. The same quadratic-polynomial argument, now applied to B(u+tv,u+tv), proves
∣B(u,v)∣2≤B(u,u)B(v,v).
(18)
Therefore B(u,u)=0 implies B(u,v)=0 for every v. The set
N={u∈F:B(u,u)=0}={u∈F:B(u,v)=0 for every v∈F}
(19)
is the radical of a bilinear form, hence a linear subspace. Set
H=F/N,⟨u+N,v+N⟩=B(u,v).
(20)
The quotient vector space has a well-defined inner product: changing representatives by elements of N changes no pairing, and a zero-length class is exactly the zero class. It is therefore an inner product space.
Define ϕ(x)=ex​+N. By construction,
⟨ϕ(x),ϕ(x′)⟩=B(ex​,ex′​)=k(x,x′).​
(21)
Every positive-definite kernel thus has a feature representation, including degenerate kernels and the zero kernel. This is its canonical feature space of a positive-semidefinite kernel. The construction already gives the requested inner product space.

Write Ω=Σ−1 for a positive-definite matrix. Up to a constant independent of the parameters, the log-likelihood of the multivariate normal distribution is
ℓ(μ,Ω)=2n​logdetΩ−21​∑i=1n​(xi​−μ)TΩ(xi​−μ).
(38)
The identity
∑i​(xi​−μ)TΩ(xi​−μ)=∑i​(xi​−Xˉ)TΩ(xi​−Xˉ)+n(μ−Xˉ)TΩ(μ−Xˉ)
(39)
shows that, for each Ω≻0, the unique optimizing mean is μ​=Xˉ. Using the matrix trace to rewrite the first quadratic sum, the profile likelihood is
ℓprof​(Ω)=constant+2n​{logdetΩ−tr(SΩ)}.
(40)
Therefore maximum likelihood estimation of the precision matrix, whenever the maximum exists, is exactly
Ω∈argminΩ=ΩT≻0​{−logdetΩ+tr(SΩ)}.​
(41)
This is the profile likelihood for a Gaussian precision matrix. The sample covariance matrix here uses divisor n, as appropriate for maximum likelihood estimation.
There is an important rank qualification in the printed assumptions. If S≻0, differentiation gives −Ω−1+S=0, so the unique minimizer is S−1. Uniqueness follows because the negative log-determinant is a strictly convex function on the positive-definite matrices. But full column rank of the uncentered X alone does not imply S≻0: the rank of a centered sample covariance matrix depends on the centered design. For example, with n=p=2 and raw data matrix X=I2​,
S=41​(1−1​−11​),Sv=0for v=(1,1)T.
(42)
Although X has full column rank, setting Ωt​=I2​+tvvT gives objective −log(1+2t)+tr(S)→−∞. Thus an unpenalized maximizer need not exist under the literal raw-rank assumption. The likelihood reduction remains valid; existence requires full column rank of the centered data, equivalently S≻0. For the nonsingular Gaussian model this holds almost surely when n>p.
Use the entrywise-penalty convention for the Graphical Lasso:
Ωλ​=argminΩ=ΩT≻0​{−logdetΩ+tr(SΩ)+λr,s=1∑p​∣Ωrs​∣}.​
(43)
The sum is the entrywise matrix L1 norm. Its Graphical-Lasso Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions are
S−Σ+λZ=0,Zrs​∈∂∣Ωλ,rs​∣,Σ=Ωλ−1​.​
(44)
Here Z is symmetric; its nonzero-entry values are sgn(Ωλ,rs​), and its zero-entry values are any numbers in [−1,1]. These are the subgradient optimality condition for this convex optimization problem. Since a positive-definite matrix has positive diagonal, Zrr​=1 and Σrr​=Srr​+λ. Counting both off-diagonal entries in the penalty and in the trace makes the same λ appear in their stationarity equations. The common variant penalizing only off-diagonal entries instead sets Zrr​=0; the off-diagonal equations and the column argument below are unchanged.
For the given column problem, abbreviate
A=Σ−j,−j​=W2,sj​=S−j,j​,v=Σ−j,j​.
(45)
Its smooth part expands to 21​bTAb−sjT​b plus a constant, because W is symmetric and WW−1=I. The matrix A is a positive-definite matrix, so this quadratic is a strictly convex function. Adding the convex L1 norm penalty preserves strict convexity, and the objective tends to infinity as ∥b∥2​→∞. The minimizer b∗ exists and is unique. Its Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions are
Ab∗−sj​+λu=0,ur​∈∂∣br∗​∣.
(46)
We identify that minimizer directly from the precision matrix. Write Ω=Ωλ​ and define
b=−Ωjj​Ω−j,j​​.
(47)
The denominator is positive. The off-diagonal part of column j of ΣΩ=I gives
AΩ−j,j​+vΩjj​=0,hence Ab=v.
(48)
On the other hand, the off-diagonal Graphical-Lasso Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions read
sj​−v+λZ−j,j​=0.
(49)
Because Ωjj​>0, the coordinates of b have the opposite signs from those of Ω−j,j​ and vanish at exactly the same positions. The subgradient of the absolute value therefore satisfies
−Z−j,j​∈∂∥b∥1​.
(50)
It follows that
Ab−sj​+λ(−Z−j,j​)=v−sj​−λZ−j,j​=0.
(51)

Σ−j,j​=Σ−j,−j​b∗,b∗=−Ωjj​Ω−j,j​​.​

This is the Graphical Lasso column regression identity. The minus sign is essential: it converts the precision-entry subgradient into the regression-coefficient subgradient. The argument uses the inverse identity directly, so it does not require proving the supplied block inverse formula.
