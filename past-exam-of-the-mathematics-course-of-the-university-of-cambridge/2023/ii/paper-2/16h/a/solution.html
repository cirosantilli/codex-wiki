For a nonzero ordinal α, let
S={ξ:ωξ≤α}.
(140)
The set is nonempty because 0∈S, and it is an initial segment because ordinal exponentiation by ω is strictly increasing. Put β=supS.
If β is a successor, the definition of the supremum forces β∈S. If β is a limit ordinal, then continuity of ordinal exponentiation gives
ωβ=supξ<β​ωξ≤α,
(141)
so again β∈S. Thus ωβ≤α, while ωβ+1>α by the definition of the supremum. Hence β is the greatest required exponent.
Apply division by an additively indecomposable ordinal with λ=ωβ:
α=ωβq+γ,γ<ωβ.
(142)
Since α≥ωβ, the quotient q is nonzero. It must be finite: if q≥ω, then
ωβ+1=ωβω≤ωβq≤α,
(143)
contradicting the maximality of β. Writing q=n gives
α=ωβn+γ​,0<n<ω,γ<ωβ.
(144)
This is the leading-term decomposition.
