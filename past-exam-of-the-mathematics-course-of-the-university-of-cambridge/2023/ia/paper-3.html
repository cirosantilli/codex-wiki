Existence follows from the three-transitivity of Möbius transformations: one can send three distinct source points to 0,1,∞ and then send those to the three distinct target points.
For uniqueness, suppose S and T have the same values at three distinct points. Then M=T−1S fixes those points. If
M(z)=cz+daz+b​,
(1)

The Lagrange theorem states that ∣H∣ divides ∣G∣ for every subgroup H of a finite group G, with ∣G∣=∣G:H∣∣H∣. The left cosets partition G, and multiplication by a representative bijects H with each coset, proving the formula.
The intersection H∩K contains the identity and is closed under xy−1, so it is a subgroup. Its order divides both ∣H∣ and ∣K∣; if these are coprime, H∩K={e}.
The order o(x) is the least positive m with xm=e. Division k=qm+r, 0≤r<m, shows that xk=e exactly when r=0, so exactly when o(x)∣k.
If x,y commute and have coprime orders m,n, then (xy)mn=e. Conversely (xy)r=e implies xr=y−r lies in ⟨x⟩∩⟨y⟩={e}, so m,n∣r and mn∣r. Hence o(xy)=mn.
Cauchy theorem for groups says that every prime divisor p of ∣G∣ occurs as the order of an element of G. For ∣G∣=26, the Sylow 13-subgroup P≅C13​ is unique and normal, and Cauchy's theorem supplies a complement C2​. Thus G=C13​⋊C2​. The homomorphism C2​→Aut(C13​)≅C12​ is either trivial or has the unique image of order two, inversion. These give exactly C26​ and the dihedral group of order 26.

G/kerϕ≅imϕ,gkerϕ↦ϕ(g).
