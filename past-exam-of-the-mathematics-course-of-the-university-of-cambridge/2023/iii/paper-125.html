For a smooth projective curve C of geometric genus one, the Riemann-Roch theorem says
ℓ(D)−ℓ(KC​−D)=degD.
(1)
The canonical divisor has degree zero and is principal because a nonzero regular differential has no zeros. Hence KC​∼0, so equivalently
ℓ(D)−ℓ(−D)=degD.
(2)
In particular, ℓ(D)=degD when degD>0.
The group Pic0(E) is the group of degree-zero divisor classes on E, with addition induced by addition of divisors. Consider
ι:E⟶Pic0(E),P⟼[(P)−(OE​)].
(3)
For surjectivity, let D have degree zero. Since deg(D+(OE​))=1, Riemann--Roch gives ℓ(D+(OE​))=1. A nonzero element of this space makes D+(OE​) linearly equivalent to an effective divisor of degree one, necessarily (P) for some point P. Thus [D]=[(P)−(OE​)].
For injectivity, suppose (P)−(OE​) is a principal divisor. If P=OE​, its defining function would be nonconstant and would have at most one simple pole, whereas Riemann--Roch gives ℓ((OE​))=1, so every such function is constant. Therefore P=OE​, and ι is a bijection.

Embed E as a smooth plane cubic with OE​ an inflection point. A line through P,Q, using the tangent when P=Q, has a third intersection S counted with multiplicity. The chord-and-tangent group law defines P+Q=−S, where −S is the third point on the line through S and OE​.
The divisor cut out by the first line is
(P)+(Q)+(S)−3(OE​),
(4)
and the line through S and OE​ gives (S)+(−S)−2(OE​). Their quotient therefore shows
[(P)−(OE​)]+[(Q)−(OE​)]=[(P+Q)−(OE​)].
(5)
Under the bijection from part (a), the chord-and-tangent operation is exactly addition in the abelian group Pic0(E). It is consequently associative and commutative, has identity OE​, and has the geometrically defined point −P as inverse. Thus it makes E an abelian group.

The Hasse theorem for elliptic curves states that for E/Fq​,
∣#E(Fq​)−(q+1)∣≤2q​.
(11)
Let π be the Frobenius isogeny of an elliptic curve. Its fixed points are exactly E(Fq​), and 1−π is separable because its differential is the identity. Therefore
#E(Fq​)=#ker(1−π)=deg(1−π).
(12)
For every endomorphism α, the dual isogeny gives αα=[degα], and the degree satisfies the parallelogram law
deg(α+β)+deg(α−β)=2degα+2degβ.
(13)
Consequently, if a=1+q−deg(1−π) is the trace of π, then
deg([m]−[n]π)=m2−amn+qn2.
(14)
This is nonnegative for every pair of integers m,n. By approximating the minimizing real ratio m/n=a/2, the quadratic polynomial can be nonnegative for all rational ratios only if its discriminant is nonpositive. Thus a2≤4q, and the displayed point-count identity proves Hasse's theorem.
An explicit example with nonisomorphic groups occurs over F43​. Put
E1​:y2=x3+2x+4,E2​:y2=x3+20.
(15)
On E1​, the point (30,19) has order seven and its nonzero cyclic subgroup has x-coordinates 30,31,40. Vélu formulas give the quotient coefficients
v=6∑xi2​+6A=9,w=10∑xi3​+6A∑xi​+12B=10(mod43),
(16)
so the degree-seven quotient is
A′=A−5v=0,B′=B−7w=20,
(17)
which is E2​. Direct quadratic-residue counts give #E1​(F43​)=#E2​(F43​)=49. The points killed by seven on E1​ are only the displayed kernel and O, while (1,8) and (3,2) are independent seven-torsion points on E2​. Hence
E1​(F43​)≅Z/49Z,E2​(F43​)≅(Z/7Z)2.
(18)
Finally, let p<43 and suppose two curves are linked by an isogeny of degree seven. The isogeny and its dual induce inverse isomorphisms on every prime-to-seven primary subgroup, so nonisomorphic rational point groups would require 49 to divide their common order. Since this order is less than 57 by Hasse, one group would then be cyclic of order 49 and the other would contain all of E[7]. The Weil pairing on rational seven-torsion forces μ7​⊂Fp​, hence p≡1(mod7) unless p=7. The only prime below 43 congruent to one modulo seven is 29, but Hasse gives #E(F29​)<49; for p=7 the same inequality is immediate. No such pair exists below 43.

The curve has good reduction when it admits a Minimal Weierstrass equation over OK​ whose discriminant is a unit, so reduction modulo π is a nonsingular elliptic curve E/k. Write a point of E(K) in primitive projective coordinates (X:Y:Z) over OK​. Reducing all three coordinates defines
red:E(K)⟶E(k),(X:Y:Z)⟼(X:Y:Z).
(19)
Primitivity ensures that the reduced triple is not zero. The addition morphism on the smooth Weierstrass model reduces to the addition morphism on E; equivalently, this follows from the valuative criterion for properness for the smooth proper group scheme. Therefore red(P+Q)=red(P)+red(Q).

The needed Hensel lemma says that if f∈OK​[X] and a∈OK​ satisfy f(a)≡0(modπ) and f′(a)≡0(modπ), then a lifts uniquely to a root of f in OK​ with the prescribed residue. At every affine point of the smooth curve E, one partial derivative of its Weierstrass equation is nonzero. Fixing the other coordinate and applying Hensel's lemma lifts that point to E(K); OE​ lifts itself. Thus reduction is surjective.
Use the local parameters
t=−x/y,z=−1/y,
(20)
so x=t/z and y=−1/z. Substitution in a general integral Weierstrass equation gives
z=t3+a1​tz+a2​t2z+a3​z2+a4​tz2+a6​z3.
(21)
For fixed 0=t∈πOK​, the difference between the two sides, viewed as a polynomial in z, is congruent to z modulo π and has derivative congruent to one. Hensel's lemma gives a unique z∈πOK​, and therefore a unique point θ(t)=(t/z,−1/z) in the kernel of reduction of an elliptic curve. Together with θ(0)=OE​, this identifies that kernel with the parameters t∈πOK​.

Let Knr be the maximal unramified extension of K. Because p∤n, multiplication by n on the special fibre is a separable isogeny and is surjective on E(k). Choose a point Q​ with [n]Q​=P and lift it, after a finite unramified extension, to Q0​. Then R=P−[n]Q0​ lies in the kernel of reduction.
On the formal group of an elliptic curve, multiplication by n has the form
[n]F​(T)=nT+O(T2).
(22)
Since n is a unit in OK​, the invertible morphism criterion for formal group laws makes [n]F​ an automorphism of πOKnr​. Hence R=[n]S for a unique point S in the kernel of reduction, and Q=Q0​+S satisfies [n]Q=P. Moreover, good reduction makes the finite group scheme E[n] étale over OK​, so all its points are defined over an unramified extension. Every point of [n]−1P=Q+E[n] is therefore unramified, proving that K([n]−1P)/K is unramified.
Multiplication by n is already an automorphism of the formal kernel, so the exact reduction sequence shows that E(K)/nE(K) is finite. Choose representatives P1​,…,Ps​. Each becomes n-divisible over a finite unramified extension; their compositum L/K is still finite and unramified. Every class from E(K)/nE(K) then maps to zero in E(L)/nE(L).

An admissible change of Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve replaces the x-coordinate by a degree-one rational function, so the two logarithmic heights differ by O(1). Dividing this bounded difference by 4r in the defining limit shows that the canonical height is unchanged. It is intrinsic to the elliptic curve and the chosen point.

Fix a prime p. If a rational point T=(x,y) has vp​(x)<0, the integral projective Weierstrass equation forces
vp​(x)=−2m,vp​(y)=−3m
(35)
for some m>0. Hence its formal parameter t=−x/y lies in pZp​, so T belongs to the formal group of an elliptic curve at the identity. This formal kernel has no nonzero torsion for the present equation. Multiplication by an integer prime to p is a formal-group automorphism, while the formal logarithm rules out p-power torsion for odd p. For p=2, inversion sends t to −t because the equation has no xy or y term, and therefore
[2]F​(t)=2t+O(t3).
(36)
For v2​(t)≥1, its leading term has strictly smaller valuation than every higher term, so [2]F​(t) cannot vanish; iterating excludes all two-power torsion as well.
Thus a nonzero torsion point cannot have vp​(x)<0. Since this holds for every prime, x∈Z. The integral equation then makes y2 an integer; a rational number whose square is integral is itself integral, so y∈Z. This is the integrality assertion in the Lutz–Nagell theorem.
