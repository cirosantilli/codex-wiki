A strong classical simulation of a quantum circuit computes any requested output probability
p(y)=Pr(Y=y),y∈{0,1}k,
(1)
in polynomial time, to the prescribed inverse-polynomial accuracy. A weak classical simulation of a quantum circuit instead produces classical samples from the circuit's output distribution, with exact or suitably small total-variation error.
The Extended Gottesman--Knill theorem states that a unitary Clifford circuit with an arbitrary product state input and final computational-basis measurements is weakly classically simulable. It is strongly simulable when only O(logn) output qubits are measured. Indeed, each joint output projector expands into 2k Pauli operators, and Clifford conjugation maps every such operator to another Pauli operator whose expectation factors over the input qubits. The factor 2k is polynomial precisely for k=O(logn).

Choose a Clifford operation D with D∣0n⟩=∣σ⟩ and absorb D into the circuit. Push each subsequent Clifford gate forward through the computation. A computational-basis measurement made after a Clifford prefix U becomes a Pauli measurement
Zj​⟼U†Zj​U
(2)
on the initial state, because Clifford conjugation preserves the Pauli group. Adaptivity merely makes the next Pauli depend on earlier classical outcomes.
It remains to eliminate the n stabilizer qubits. Maintain their current stabilizer group. For a Pauli P to be measured, there are two cases.
  • If P commutes with every stabilizer generator, its action on the one-dimensional stabilizer sector reduces to a Pauli operator on the remaining t qubits, possibly with a known sign. Measure that effective Pauli on ∣ρ⟩.
  • If P anticommutes with some stabilizer S, its outcome λ∈{+1,−1} is uniformly random. Sample λ for an ordinary measurement, or set λ=+1 when the original measurement is postselected. The Clifford operator
Vλ​=2​I+λPS​
(3)
maps the old stabilizer sector into the λ eigenspace of P. Updating the Clifford frame by Vλ​ removes this measurement while conjugating every later Pauli to another Pauli.
Iterating this procedure leaves an adaptive Pauli-based computation on ∣ρ⟩. The same classical outcomes determine every adaptive choice and final output, so this gives a weak classical simulation. Every postselected Z outcome becomes either a fixed classical +1 branch or a postselected +1 Pauli measurement, as required.

Use the standard ancilla-assisted Pauli measurement. To measure a Pauli P, reset the ancilla to ∣0⟩, apply H, apply the controlled version of every nonidentity factor of P with the ancilla as control, apply H again, and measure the ancilla in the computational basis. The preceding calculation shows that outcome s projects the data with
Πs​(P)=2I+(−1)sP​.
(9)
First use this circuit with P1​=Z⊗I, obtaining s1​. Since the measured ancilla is ∣s1​⟩, apply the classically controlled correction Xs1​ to reset it to ∣0⟩. Reuse it to measure P2​=Z⊗X, obtaining s2​. All controlled Pauli gates and single-qubit corrections are Clifford gates. Since P1​P2​=P2​P1​, the final data state is
∣Ψout​⟩=∥Πs2​​(P2​)Πs1​​(P1​)∣A⟩⊗2∥Πs2​​(P2​)Πs1​​(P1​)∣A⟩⊗2​​.
(10)
Thus one resettable ancilla implements both measurements of the PBC without disturbing the already measured Pauli eigenvalue.

Let the spectral decomposition of the Hermitian matrix be
A=∑j​λj​∣uj​⟩⟨uj​∣,∣b⟩=∑j​βj​∣uj​⟩.
(11)
The HHL algorithm proceeds as follows.
Efficiently prepare the normalized amplitude encoding ∣b⟩.
Apply quantum phase estimation to the simulated evolution eiAt, producing an approximation of each eigenvalue:
∑j​βj​∣uj​⟩∣λj​⟩.
(12)
Add one ancilla and perform an eigenvalue-controlled rotation
∣λj​⟩∣0⟩⟼∣λj​⟩(1−λj2​C2​​∣0⟩+λj​C​∣1⟩),
(13)
where 0<C≤minj​∣λj​∣.
Uncompute the eigenvalue register. Conditional on measuring the ancilla as 1, the system register is
∣x⟩=∥A−1∣b⟩∥A−1∣b⟩​=∑j​∣βj​∣2∣λj​∣−2​∑j​βj​λj−1​∣uj​⟩​.
(14)
The postselection probability can be increased with amplitude amplification.
The ingredients used here are efficient sparse Hamiltonian simulation of eiAt and quantum phase estimation, which converts an eigenphase of that evolution into a binary approximation of λj​. For sparsity s, condition number κ, and error ϵ, the cost is polynomial in s, κ, 1/ϵ, and logN under the stated access assumptions.
Finally estimate ⟨x∣M∣x⟩ by repeated measurement of an efficient observable decomposition of M, or by a Hadamard test when M is unitary. A general efficiently block-encoded Hermitian M can similarly be measured through its block encoding. Repetition and a concentration inequality give additive sampling error O(1/R​) after R independent preparations.

The essential requirement is an efficient quantum state preparation circuit B satisfying
B∣0n⟩=∣b⟩=∥b∥1​∑j=0N−1​bj​∣j⟩.
(15)
A standard sufficient promise is that b has only poly(logN) nonzero components, with their positions and values classically computable to the required precision in poly(logN) time, and with efficiently computable normalization. More structured dense vectors are also allowed whenever cumulative weights or an equivalent data-access oracle permit amplitude encoding in polylogarithmic time. Without such a promise, merely loading N arbitrary classical entries already costs Ω(N) and removes the claimed exponential dependence on dimension.

Let ΠG​ be the orthogonal projection onto G. The two reflection operators are
I∣ψ⟩​=I−2∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣,IG​=I−2ΠG​.
(24)
The first fixes the hyperplane ∣ψ⟩⊥ and changes the sign of ∣ψ⟩; the second fixes G⊥ and changes the sign of G.
Write the normalized projections of ∣ψ⟩ as
∣ψ⟩=sinθ∣g⟩+cosθ∣b⟩,∣g⟩∈G,∣b⟩∈G⊥.
(25)
The amplitude amplification theorem states that for
R=I∣ψ⟩​IG​
(26)
one has, up to the irrelevant global sign (−1)k,
Rk∣ψ⟩=(−1)k[sin((2k+1)θ)∣g⟩+cos((2k+1)θ)∣b⟩]​.
(27)
Thus every iteration increases the angle toward the good axis by 2θ until the first overshoot.
For the proof, the plane span{∣g⟩,∣b⟩} is invariant. In its ordered basis,
IG​=(−10​01​),I∣ψ⟩​=(cos2θ−sin2θ​−sin2θ−cos2θ​).
(28)
Their product is a planar rotation through 2θ together with an overall sign. Applying that matrix k times proves the formula, while components orthogonal to this plane never enter the initial state.

If some integer k obeys (2k+1)θ=π/2, ordinary amplitude amplification already gives ∣g⟩ exactly. For a general known θ, use exact amplitude amplification. Choose k so that
θ′=4k+2π​≤θ
(29)
and put c=sinθ′/sinθ≤1. Append an ancilla and coherently arrange that its designated good value has amplitude c conditional on the original register being good. With the enlarged good subspace defined by
f(x)=1andancilla=0,
(30)
the starting state's total good amplitude is csinθ=sinθ′.
Apply the amplitude amplification theorem k times to this enlarged problem. Its final good amplitude is
sin((2k+1)θ′)=sin2π​=1.
(31)
The Boolean quantum oracle implements the reflection IG​ by phase kickback, and the known state-preparation circuit implements the reflection about the starting state by prepare--reflect--unprepare. A final computational-basis measurement therefore yields an x with f(x)=1 with certainty.

At trial k, the amplitude amplification theorem gives success probability
pk​=sin2((2k+1)θ).
(32)
Because the state is prepared afresh, the probability that the first success occurs at k is
Pr(k∗=k)=pk​j=0∏k−1​(1−pj​)=sin2((2k+1)θ)j=0∏k−1​cos2((2j+1)θ)​.
(33)
If one application of R uses a constant number of oracle calls, reaching and performing trial k costs a total of order 1+2+⋯+k=Θ(k2) calls. Equivalently,
EC=Θ(∑k≥1​k∏j=0k−1​cos2((2j+1)θ)).
(34)
For kθ≪1, use logcos2x=−x2+O(x4) and
∑j=0k−1​(2j+1)2=3k(4k2−1)​
(35)
to obtain
Pr(k∗≥k)=exp[−34​θ2k3+o(θ2k3)].
(36)
The first successful index is therefore typically k∗=Θ(θ−2/3), and
EC=Θ(θ−4/3)=Θ((n∗)4/3)​,
(37)
where n∗=Θ(1/θ) is the first near-optimal Grover iteration count.
The last requested assertion in the official paper is false as printed. In fact, for small θ, at least a constant fraction of the indices j<n∗ have (2j+1)θ≥π/4, and hence failure probability at most 1/2. Consequently
Pr(k∗≥n∗)=j=0∏n∗−1​cos2((2j+1)θ)≤2−cn∗⟶0​
(38)
for some constant c>0, rather than being bounded below by a positive constant. The reversed event Pr(k∗<n∗) does have a positive lower bound and in fact tends to one.

S1A​=X1​Z2​,S2A​=Z1​X2​Z3​,S3A​=Z2​X3​,
