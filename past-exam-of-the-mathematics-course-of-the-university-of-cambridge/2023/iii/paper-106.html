Mazur theorem says that the weak closure and norm closure of a convex subset of a real or complex normed space coincide. The norm closure is contained in the weak closure because every norm-continuous linear functional is norm-continuous. Conversely, if x is outside the norm closure of a convex set C, the Hahn-Banach separation theorem gives f∈X∗ and a real number α such that
Ref(x)>α≥supy∈C​Ref(y).
(1)
This weakly open separation shows that x is outside the weak closure.
By definition,
xn​⇀0⟺f(xn​)⟶0for every f∈X∗.
(2)
For each n, regard xn​ as the bounded functional Jxn​:X∗→F given by Jxn​(f)=f(xn​). Pointwise convergence makes the family (Jxn​) pointwise bounded on the Banach space X∗. The Uniform boundedness principle gives
supn​∥xn​∥=supn​∥Jxn​∥<∞.
(3)
Suppose first that xn​⇀0. Every subsequence indexed by an infinite set M also converges weakly to zero. Thus zero belongs to the weak closure of the convex hull of {xn​:n∈M}, and Mazur's theorem puts it in its norm closure. This gives the required finite convex combination of norm below any prescribed ε.
Conversely, if weak convergence fails, some f∈X∗ and an infinite subsequence satisfy either Ref(xn​)≥ε throughout or Ref(xn​)≤−ε throughout. Every convex combination from that subsequence then has norm at least ε/∥f∥, contradicting the stated property.
Now let the xn​∈ℓp, 1<p<∞, have pairwise disjoint supports and satisfy ∥xn​∥p​≤C. For N distinct terms,
​N1​∑j=1N​xnj​​​pp​=Np1​∑j=1N​∥xnj​​∥pp​≤CpN1−p⟶0.
(4)
The convex-combination criterion therefore proves xn​⇀0. This is weak convergence of bounded disjointly supported sequences in lp.
The final implication for a commutative unital C*-algebra is true. By the Commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem, A≅C(K) and its characters are the point evaluations. The hypotheses say that the uniformly bounded functions an​ converge pointwise to zero. Every functional on C(K) is integration against a finite regular measure by the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem; the dominated convergence theorem gives
∫K​an​dμ⟶0.
(5)
Hence an​⇀0.

The holomorphic functional calculus assigns to a in a unital complex Banach algebra and every function holomorphic near σ(a) the element
f(a)=2πi1​∫Γ​f(z)(z1−a)−1dz,
(6)
where Γ winds once around the spectrum. It is a unital algebra homomorphism, extends polynomial evaluation, is independent of the admissible contour, and obeys the spectral mapping theorem
σ(f(a))=f(σ(a)).
(7)
Let zK​∈R(K) be the coordinate function. For a character φ, put λ=φ(zK​). If λ∈/K, then (zK​−λ)−1 is one of the admitted rational functions, contradicting the fact that zK​−λ lies in the kernel of φ. Thus λ∈K. For every rational function r without poles on K,
φ(r)=r(λ).
(8)
Continuity of characters and uniform density extend this identity to every member of R(K). Conversely evaluation at every λ∈K is a character. Therefore the character space of R(K) is naturally K.
Runge approximation theorem says that if K⊂C is compact, f is holomorphic on a neighbourhood of K, and one chooses one point in every bounded component of C∖K, then f can be approximated uniformly on K by rational functions whose finite poles belong only to the chosen points.
To prove it, surround K by finitely many small rectangles contained in the domain of f and apply the Cauchy integral formula on their oriented boundaries:
f(z)=2πi1​∫Γ​ζ−zf(ζ)​dζ.
(9)
Riemann sums approximate this integral uniformly on K by rational functions with poles on Γ. If a pole b lies in a component containing the selected point a, choose a polygonal path from b to a inside that component and subdivide it finely. The resolvent identity
z−b1​−z−c1​=(z−b)(z−c)b−c​
(10)
allows the pole to be moved step by step along the path, with arbitrarily small uniform error on K. Moving every pole proves the theorem.
For an open set U, choose a compact exhaustion K1​⊂K2​⊂⋯ such that every component of C∖Kn​ meets C∖U. Runge's theorem approximates a holomorphic function on Kn​ by a rational function with all poles outside U. A diagonal choice gives convergence uniformly on every compact subset, proving that such rational functions are dense in the space of holomorphic functions O(U) with its compact-open topology.
Finally, equality of the Gelfand transforms gives
σA​(x1​)={x1​​(φ):φ∈ΦA​}={x2​​(φ):φ∈ΦA​}=σA​(x2​).
(11)
Let this common compact spectrum be K. The divided difference
g(z,w)=⎩⎨⎧​z−wf(z)−f(w)​,f′(z),​z=w,z=w​
(12)
is holomorphic near K×K. The two-variable holomorphic functional calculus for the commuting pair (x1​,x2​) gives an element u=g(x1​,x2​) satisfying
f(x1​)−f(x2​)=u(x1​−x2​).
(13)
For every character,
u(φ)=g(x1​​(φ),x2​​(φ))=f′(x1​​(φ))=0.
(14)
An element of a commutative unital Banach algebra is invertible exactly when its Gelfand transform has no zero. Thus u is invertible, and f(x1​)=f(x2​) implies x1​=x2​. This is injectivity through a holomorphic functional calculus with nonvanishing derivative.

Suppose first that X is separable, and choose a norm-dense sequence (xn​) in its unit ball. On the dual unit ball define
d(f,g)=∑n=1∞​2−n1+∣(f−g)(xn​)∣∣(f−g)(xn​)∣​.
(15)
Uniform boundedness on the unit ball and density of the xn​ show that this metric induces the weak-star topology. Conversely, if BX∗​ is weak-star metrizable, the Banach-Alaoglu theorem makes it a compact metric space. Hence C(BX∗​) is separable. The evaluation map
X⟶C(BX∗​),x⟼(f↦f(x))
(16)
is an isometry by the Hahn-Banach theorem. A subspace of a separable metric space is separable, so X is separable. This proves the weak-star metrizability criterion for a dual ball.
If X has a countable weakly dense subset D, the rational linear span of D is weakly dense. Its norm closure is a convex set, so Mazur theorem says that its weak and norm closures agree. Thus X is norm separable. The weak-star compact metric ball BX∗​ consequently has a countable weak-star dense subset, and the union of its integer dilates is weak-star dense in X∗. Therefore X∗ is weak-star separable.
It need not be weakly separable. Take X=ℓ1, whose dual is ℓ∞. A weakly separable normed space is norm separable by the preceding convex-closure argument, whereas ℓ∞ is not norm separable.
If the Banach space X is reflexive, its closed unit ball identifies with the weak-star compact ball of X∗∗, hence is weakly compact. Conversely, if BX​ is weakly compact, its canonical image J(BX​) is weak-star compact and therefore weak-star closed in X∗∗. Goldstine theorem says it is weak-star dense in BX∗∗​, so
J(BX​)=BX∗∗​.
(17)
Scaling proves that J is surjective and X is reflexive. This is the weak compactness characterization of reflexivity.
The Krein-Milman theorem says that a nonempty compact convex subset of a locally convex space is the closed convex hull of its extreme points. For reflexive X, the ball BX​ is weakly compact, so
BX​=convExt(BX​),
(18)
where weak and norm closure agree for the convex hull by Mazur's theorem.
For the final claim, let H be the set of functions Z2→[0,1] with the mean-value property. It is a compact convex subset of the product [0,1]Z2. If f is extreme, its four unit translates also lie in H, and the mean-value identity writes f as their average. Extremality forces every translate to equal f, so f is constant. Every extreme point is therefore constant. Krein--Milman now makes every member of H a limit of convex combinations of constant functions, and hence constant. This is the bounded harmonic function theorem on the integer lattice.

The useful forms of the Hahn-Banach separation theorem in a real locally convex space are:
  • if C is nonempty open convex and x∈/C, there are a continuous linear functional f and α∈R with f(c)<α≤f(x) for every c∈C;
  • if C is closed convex and x∈/C, there are f and α with supC​f<α<f(x);
  • if K is compact convex, C is closed convex, and K∩C=∅, there are f and α<β with supC​f<α<β<infK​f, after changing the sign of f if needed.
The Banach-Alaoglu theorem says that the closed unit ball of E∗ is compact in σ(E∗,E). Goldstine theorem says that the canonical image of the closed unit ball of a normed space E is weak-star dense in the closed unit ball of E∗∗.
Give Z its norm inherited from X∗ and define
J:X⟶Z∗,Jx(f)=f(x).
(19)
It is linear and contractive, and it is injective because Z separates points. Goldstine's theorem followed by restriction from X∗ to Z shows that J(BX​) is weak-star dense in BZ∗​: a functional on Z first extends norm-preservingly to X∗, and elements of BX​ approximate that extension on every finite subset of Z.
The topology induced by σ(Z∗,Z) on J(BX​) is exactly the given topology σ(X,Z). By hypothesis BX​ is compact, so J(BX​) is weak-star compact and therefore closed in the Hausdorff space BZ∗​. Density now gives
J(BX​)=BZ∗​.
(20)
Thus J is surjective and maps closed unit ball onto closed unit ball, so it is an isometric isomorphism. Hence X is a dual space. This is the compact norming dual-pair criterion.

A character of an algebra is a nonzero algebra homomorphism φ:A→C. The character space is
ΦA​={φ:A→C:φ is a character}.
(25)
For a unital algebra, φ(1)=1. Moreover φ(a)∈σA​(a), since applying φ shows that a−φ(a)1 cannot be invertible. In a Banach algebra,
∣φ(a)∣≤r(a)≤∥a∥.
(26)
Thus ∥φ∥≤1, while φ(1)=1 gives equality.
If A is commutative and λ∈σA​(a), the proper ideal generated by a−λ1 lies in a maximal ideal. The quotient by that maximal ideal is C, and its quotient map is a character taking a to λ. Therefore
σA​(a)={φ(a):φ∈ΦA​}.
(27)
Since every Banach-algebra element has nonempty spectrum, ΦA​ is nonempty.
For X=Y⊕Z, let P,Q be the complementary coordinate projections. A character must send each idempotent to zero or one, and P+Q=I forces their values to be different. Choose mutually inverse isomorphisms between Y and Z and regard them as off-diagonal operators S,T on X. Then
ST=P,TS=Q.
(28)
Multiplicativity would give φ(P)=φ(S)φ(T)=φ(T)φ(S)=φ(Q), a contradiction. Hence ΦB(X)​ is empty. This is the absence of characters on an operator algebra with isomorphic complementary summands.
If a=a∗ in a unital C*-algebra, then eita is unitary for real t. If λ∈σ(a), the spectral mapping theorem gives eitλ∈σ(eita), whose modulus is one. Varying positive and negative t forces Imλ=0, so σ(a)⊂R.
If B⊆A is a unital C*-subalgebra and x∈B is normal, spectral permanence holds. Indeed, when x−λ is invertible in A, the positive normal element
(x−λ)∗(x−λ)
(29)
has spectrum bounded away from zero. Continuous functional calculus uniformly approximates its reciprocal by polynomials, placing the reciprocal in B. It follows that (x−λ)−1∈B. Thus σB​(x)=σA​(x).
The Commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem says that the Gelfand transform is an isometric unital star-isomorphism
A≅C(ΦA​)
(30)
for every commutative unital C*-algebra. If a is positive, continuous functional calculus for the function t↦t​ on σ(a)⊂[0,∞) gives a positive b with b2=a. Pointwise uniqueness in C∗(1,a)≅C(σ(a)) gives the unique positive square root. This is the positive square root in a C*-algebra.
