Let X={v1​,…,vm​} be the vertices of a regular polygon, indexed cyclically so that vi​↦vi+1​ is an isometry. We first prove the product lemma needed for the cyclic-symmetry argument.
Suppose A⊆X has the property that, for every number of colors, a finite witness forces a copy of X on which the vertices corresponding to A are monochromatic. We claim that for every N,k there is a finite witness forcing an A-invariant copy of XN. Proceed by mathematical induction on N. The case N=1 is the assumption. For the step, choose a finite set S that forces an A-invariant XN−1 for a coloring with k∣X∣−∣A∣+1 colors, and choose T that forces an A-monochromatic X for a coloring with k∣S∣ colors.
Given a k-coloring c of S×T, color t∈T by its complete vector (c(s,t))s∈S​. In the resulting copy of X in T, that vector is the same for every member of A. Each s∈S can therefore be colored by the vector consisting of c(s,a) for one a∈A and the colors c(s,x) for x∈X∖A. Applying the choice of S gives an A-invariant copy of XN−1, and its product with the chosen copy of X in T is the desired A-invariant copy of XN. This proves the lemma rather than assuming it.
We now prove by induction on r that a finite witness forces a copy of X whose first r vertices have one color. The case r=1 is trivial. For r=2, use that a line segment is a Euclidean Ramsey set; each occurrence of a segment congruent to v1​v2​ can be extended to a copy of the regular polygon, and only finitely many extensions are needed for a finite witness.
Assume the assertion for r, and put A={v1​,…,vr​}. The product lemma lets us work inside an A-invariantly colored copy of XN, with N as large as required. For an increasing (m−1)-element subset H={a1​<⋯<am−1​} of [N] and 1≤i≤r, define a word wi​(H) by putting vi+j​ in coordinate aj​, with subscripts read modulo m, and putting v1​ in every other coordinate. Color H by the r-tuple
(c(w1​(H)),…,c(wr​(H))).
(30)
By the Finite Ramsey theorem, for sufficiently large N there are coordinates b1​<⋯<bm​ on which all (m−1)-subsets have the same tuple color.
For 1≤i≤r+1, let zi​ be the word whose entries in coordinates b1​,…,bm​ are
vi​,vi+1​,…,vi+m−1​,
(31)
cyclically, and whose other entries are v1​. For i≤r, the word zi​ differs from wi​({b2​,…,bm​}) only in coordinate b1​, where the two entries vi​,v1​ lie in A. Similarly zi+1​ differs from wi​({b1​,…,bm−1​}) only in coordinate bm​, again by two members of A. The A-invariance and the homogeneous choice of the bj​ therefore give
c(zi​)=c(wi​({b2​,…,bm​}))=c(wi​({b1​,…,bm−1​}))=c(zi+1​).
(32)
Hence z1​,…,zr+1​ have one color.
The cyclic words z1​,…,zm​ form an isometric copy of m​X: in each of the m varying coordinates the cyclic shift is an isometry, so every squared distance is multiplied by m. Rescaling the finite witness by 1/m​ gives a copy of X. The induction reaches r=m, proving that every regular polygon is a Euclidean Ramsey set.
For three consecutive vertices p0​,p1​,p2​ of a regular M-gon scaled to have side length one,
d(p0​,p1​)=d(p1​,p2​)=1,d(p0​,p2​)=2cosMπ​⟶2.
(33)
Since the polygon is Euclidean Ramsey, choosing M large enough proves that {0,1,2} is an approximately Euclidean Ramsey set.
In fact every finite X⊆Rd is approximately Ramsey. First perturb each coordinate of each point onto a sufficiently fine lattice δZ, changing all pairwise distances by less than ε/2. For each coordinate, map the finitely many required integers to consecutive vertices of a very large regular polygon, scaled so that one angular step has arc length δ. If the step angle is θ, the chord replacing a difference of q lattice steps has length
2θδ​sin2∣q∣θ​⟶∣q∣δ
(34)
as θ→0, uniformly over the finitely many differences involved. Taking the orthogonal Cartesian product of d such polygons therefore embeds the perturbed points with a further distance error below ε/2. Each polygon is Euclidean Ramsey, and their product is Euclidean Ramsey by the product theorem for Euclidean Ramsey sets. A monochromatic copy of that product contains the corresponding approximate copy of X, completing the proof.
