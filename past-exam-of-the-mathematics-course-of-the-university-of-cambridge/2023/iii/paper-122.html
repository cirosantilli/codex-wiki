In an independent Bernoulli product space, an event is increasing if changing any coordinate from 0 to 1 cannot destroy it. Harris' inequality says that two increasing events A,B satisfy
P(A∩B)≥P(A)P(B).
(1)
Equivalently, two decreasing events are positively correlated.
For Janson inequalities, let R be a random subset of a finite ground set whose elements are selected independently, let (Si​)i∈I​ be fixed subsets, and put
Ii​=1{Si​⊆R}​,X=∑i​Ii​,μ=EX.
(2)
Write i∼j when i=j and Si​∩Sj​=∅, and define the ordered dependency sum
Δ=∑i∼j​E(Ii​Ij​).
(3)
The first Janson inequality is
P(X=0)≤exp(−μ+2Δ​).
(4)
The usual extended form is
P(X=0)≤{e−μ/2,e−μ2/(2Δ),​Δ≤μ,Δ≥μ.​
(5)
There is also the complementary lower bound
P(X=0)≥∏i​(1−EIi​),
(6)
and if EIi​≤ε<1 for all i, the product is at least exp(−μ/(1−ε)).
For the proof, order I arbitrarily and expose the avoidance events one at a time. The standard Janson sequential product lemma, obtained by conditioning and using Harris' inequality on the coordinates outside Si​, gives
P(X=0)≤∏i​(1−EIi​+sumj<i:j∼i​E(Ii​Ij​)).
(7)
Using 1−x≤e−x and summing the pair terms yields e−μ+Δ/2. If Δ≤μ, this is at most e−μ/2. If Δ>μ, adjoin an independent Bernoulli coordinate of mean q=μ/Δ to each index and intersect Ai​ with the event that its new coordinate is 1. The event X=0 implies that none of these thinned events occurs, while the thinned family has mean qμ and dependency sum q2Δ. Applying the first inequality in the enlarged product space gives
P(X=0)leqexp(−qμ+2q2Δ​)=e−μ2/(2Δ).
(8)
Finally, the events Ii​=0 are decreasing, so repeated Harris' inequality proves the product lower bound; log(1−x)≥−x/(1−ε) gives its exponential version.

Let X count the triangles of the binomial random graph G(n,p). Then
μ=EX=(3n​)p3=Θ(n3p3).
(9)
Two distinct triangle indicators are dependent only when the triangles share an edge. Hence their ordered Janson dependency sum is
Δ=Θ(n4p5).
(10)
If 0<p≤n−1/2, then Δ=O(μ). The first Janson inequality gives
P(X=0)≤e−Ω(μ).
(11)
For the reverse bound, the events that individual triangles are absent are decreasing, so Harris' inequality gives
P(X=0)≥(1−p3)(3n​)=e−O(n3p3),
(12)
where p<1/2 keeps the logarithmic estimate uniform. Thus the probability is e−Θ(p3n3).
If n−1/2≤p<1/2, the extended Janson bound gives
P(X=0)≤exp(−Ω(Δμ2​))=e−Ω(pn2).
(13)
For a lower bound, fix a balanced bipartition of the vertices and require every edge inside either part to be absent. The resulting graph is bipartite, hence triangle-free, and this event has probability
(1−p)2(2⌊n/2⌋​)=e−O(pn2).
(14)
Combining the bounds proves the second regime.

Put p=Cn−3/4. For any fixed U⊆[n] with ∣U∣≥3n/5, let XU​ count copies of the cycle graph C4​ in G[U]. Then
μU​=3(4∣U∣​)p4=Θ(C4n).

A set R⊆V(G) is (s,k)-rich when every s-element subset of R has at least k common neighbours.
Choose t vertices x1​,…,xt​ independently and uniformly from V(G), allowing repetitions, and let
X=N(x1​)∩⋯∩N(xt​).
(18)
By Jensen inequality applied to the convex function z↦zt,
E∣X∣=∑v∈V(G)​(nd(v)​)t≥n(n22m​)t=n2t−1(2m)t​.
(19)
Let Y count the s-subsets S⊆X having fewer than k common neighbours in G. For each such S, the probability that S⊆X is
(n∣N(S)∣​)t<(nk​)t,
(20)
so
EY≤(sn​)(nk​)t.
(21)
The assumed inequality gives E(∣X∣−Y)≥r, so some choice has ∣X∣−Y≥r. Delete one vertex from each bad s-subset of X. The remaining set R has size at least ∣X∣−Y≥r and contains no bad s-subset, so it is (s,k)-rich. This is the basic dependent random choice argument.

Embed the part A injectively into the rich set in a graph R. List the vertices of B as b1​,…,bq​. When embedding bi​, the images of its at most s neighbours in A have at least ∣H∣ common neighbours in G: extend that image set to an s-subset of R if necessary, noting that enlarging a set can only shrink its common neighbourhood. At most ∣H∣−1 vertices have already been used, so one common neighbour remains available for bi​. Choosing it embeds every edge incident with bi​ and keeps the map injective. Continuing greedily embeds H in G. This is the rich-set embedding lemma.

Write fn​(q)=P(G(n,q)∈Pn​). This is increasing in q by monotone coupling of binomial random graphs, and fn​(p)=1/10 by hypothesis.
First let q/p→0. Choose k=⌊p/(2q)⌋, so k→∞. The union of k independent copies of G(n,q) has distribution G(n,q′), where
q′=1−(1−q)k≤kq≤p.

The triangle embedding lemma for regular pairs states that if 0<ε<1/2, the three pairs among disjoint nonempty sets A,B,C are ε-uniform, and all three densities are at least 2ε, then the graph contains a triangle with one vertex in each set.
In an ε-uniform pair (A,B) of density d, fewer than ε∣A∣ vertices of A have fewer than (d−ε)∣B∣ neighbours in B; otherwise those vertices and B would violate uniformity. Apply this observation to (A,B) and (A,C). Since 2ε<1, choose a∈A that is typical for both pairs. Then
B′=N(a)∩B,qquadC′=N(a)∩C
(43)
satisfy ∣B′∣≥ε∣B∣ and ∣C′∣≥ε∣C∣. Uniformity of (B,C) gives
d(B′,C′)≥d(B,C)−ε≥ε>0.
(44)
Thus some bc joins B′ to C′, and abc is the required triangle.
