The Karhunen–Loève expansion has the following Hilbert-space form. Let X be a square-integrable Random element of a Hilbert space H, with mean μ and covariance operator C. There are nonnegative eigenvalues λ1​≥λ2​≥⋯ and orthonormal eigenvectors ϕk​ spanning the closure of the range of C such that
Cϕk​=λk​ϕk​,X=μ+∑k≥1​ξk​ϕk​
(1)
in L2(Ω;H). The functional principal component scores
ξk​=⟨X−μ,ϕk​⟩
(2)
satisfy
Eξk​=0,E[ξj​ξk​]=λk​1{j=k}​.
(3)
If X is a Gaussian random element, the scores are independent normal random variables.
For the proof, C is positive, self-adjoint, and trace-class, with
trC=E∥X−μ∥2<∞.
(4)
It is therefore compact, so the spectral theorem for compact Hermitian operators supplies the eigenpairs. Their score covariance is
E[ξj​ξk​]=⟨Cϕj​,ϕk​⟩=λj​⟨ϕj​,ϕk​⟩.
(5)
For the residual Rm​=X−μ−∑k=1m​ξk​ϕk​, Parseval identity and the trace formula give
E∥Rm​∥2=trC−∑k=1m​λk​=∑k>m​λk​⟶0.
(6)
The centered variable's projection onto kerC has zero second moment and is therefore zero almost surely, which completes the mean-square expansion. When C has a continuous covariance kernel, Mercer's theorem additionally expands that kernel as c(s,t)=∑k​λk​ϕk​(s)ϕk​(t).

Let (λj​,ϕj​) be the ordered eigenpairs of the common covariance operator CX​, and write δ=μ−μ∗. Estimate CX​ by the pooled covariance operator
C=2n1​∑i=1n​[(Xi​−X)⊗(Xi​−X)+(Xi∗​−X∗)⊗(Xi∗​−X∗)]
(11)
and denote its first K eigenpairs by (λj​,ϕ​j​). The sign ambiguity of each eigenfunction disappears after squaring. Consider the Two-sample FPCA mean statistic
Tn,K​=2n​∑j=1K​λj​⟨X−X∗,ϕ​j​⟩2​.
(12)
Under H0​, the Hilbert-space central limit theorem gives
2n​​(X−X∗)d​G,
(13)
where G is a centered Gaussian random element with covariance CX​. The assumed eigenvalue gaps give consistency of the estimated eigenvalues and eigenfunctions, so Slutsky's theorem yields
Tn,K​d​∑j=1K​λj​⟨G,ϕj​⟩2​∼χK2​.
(14)
An asymptotic level-α test therefore rejects when Tn,K​ exceeds the (1−α)-quantile of the chi-squared distribution with K degrees of freedom.
Under a fixed alternative,
nTn,K​​p​21​∑j=1K​λj​⟨δ,ϕj​⟩2​.
(15)
The test is consequently consistent whenever the mean difference has a nonzero projection onto one of the retained principal components. A difference orthogonal to their span is invisible to this fixed-K test, so μ=μ∗ alone does not guarantee consistency. Increasing K with n can recover such alternatives, but requires additional eigenvalue and approximation control.

Let Ξik​=⟨Xi​,Bk​⟩ and define the roughness penalty matrix
Ωjk​=⟨Bj′′​,Bk′′​⟩=∫01​Bj′′​(t)Bk′′​(t)dt.
(34)
For c=(c1​,…,cK​)T, the loss is the quadratic function
L(c)=∥Y−Ξc∥22​+ρcTΩc.
(35)
Its normal equations are
(ΞTΞ+ρΩ)c=ΞTY.
(36)
Whenever ΞTΞ+ρΩ is positive definite, the unique minimizer is
c=(ΞTΞ+ρΩ)−1ΞTY,β​(t)=∑k=1K​ck​Bk​(t).
(37)
For the usual choice ρ≥0, the penalty matrix is positive semidefinite, so full column rank of Ξ suffices. Since the question permits arbitrary real ρ, a sufficiently negative value can make the quadratic form indefinite; then the loss is unbounded below and no minimizer exists. In the singular positive-semidefinite case, the Moore-Penrose inverse describes the minimum-norm solution whenever the normal equations are consistent.
