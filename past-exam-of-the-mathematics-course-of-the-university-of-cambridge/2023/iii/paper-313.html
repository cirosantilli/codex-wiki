A classical field-theory soliton is a smooth, spatially localized, finite-energy solution which retains its identity under time evolution and is stable against small perturbations, commonly because of a topological charge or a balance between energy terms with different scaling behavior.
For a static field write
E=E2​+E4​+E0​,
(1)
where
E2​=21​∫∣∇ϕ∣2dDx,E4​=κ2∫∣∇ϕ∣4dDx,E0​=∫U(ϕ)dDx.
(2)
Under the Derrick scaling ϕλ​(x)=ϕ(λx), a change of variables gives
E(λ)=λ2−DE2​+λ4−DE4​+λ−DE0​.
(3)
A static solution must be stationary under this variation, so the Derrick virial identity is
(2−D)E2​+(4−D)E4​−DE0​=0​.
(4)
All three energies are nonnegative. For D≥4, every coefficient is nonpositive and the coefficient of the strictly positive E2​ of any nonconstant field is negative. The identity is impossible. Thus, when the quartic-gradient term is available,
D≥4⟹no nontrivial static soliton.​
(5)
For D=2 or 3, the E4​ term has the opposite sign to at least one other term and the Derrick theorem does not rule out a soliton. If κ=0 from the outset, the identity reduces to (2−D)E2​−DE0​=0, recovering the stronger standard obstruction for D≥2.

The potential factorizes as
U(ϕ)=ϕ2(ϕ2−4)2.
(6)
It is even and nonnegative, with three degenerate vacua at ϕ=−2,0,2. Its two intervening maxima occur at ϕ=±2/3​ and have height 256/27.
A finite-energy static solution in one dimension satisfies
ϕ′′=U′(ϕ).
(7)
Multiplication by ϕ′ and use of the vacuum boundary conditions gives the first integral
21​(ϕ′)2=U(ϕ).
(8)
Any path from a negative vacuum to a positive vacuum must pass through the intermediate vacuum ϕ=0. There both U and ϕ′ vanish. The Picard-Lindelof theorem then forces a solution reaching (ϕ,ϕ′)=(0,0) at finite x to remain there. Equivalently, the first-order orbit approaches ϕ=0 only as ∣x∣→∞. This intermediate-vacuum obstruction to a kink means that a single kink cannot connect −2 to 2; it splits into two elementary kinks at infinite separation.

Away from a zero of the Higgs field, write
ϕ=eu/2+iχ,u=log∣ϕ∣2.
(13)
Separating real and imaginary parts of (Dx​+iDy​)ϕ=0 gives
Ax​=∂x​χ+21​∂y​u,Ay​=∂y​χ−21​∂x​u.
(14)
Thus B=−Δu/2 away from zeros. Combining this with B=(1−eu)/2 gives
Δu+1−eu=0.
(15)
If ϕ has a zero of multiplicity N at the origin, its vortex number is the winding number N and u=2Nlogr+O(1). Since Δlogr=2πδ(2)(x) as a distributional identity, the complete Taubes equation is
Δu+1−eu=4πNδ(2)(x)​.
(16)
The boundary conditions for a single N-vortex are
u(r)=2Nlogr+O(1)(r→0),u(r)→0(r→∞)​.
(17)
The latter is the finite-energy condition ∣ϕ∣→1.

Suppose u were positive somewhere. Since it tends to zero at infinity and to −∞ at its vortex zero, it would attain a positive interior maximum away from the origin. At such a maximum the second-derivative test gives Δu≤0, whereas the smooth Taubes equation gives
Δu=eu−1>0.
(18)
This contradiction with the maximum principle for subharmonic functions proves
u≤0on R2​.
(19)
Consequently the Higgs magnitude of a vortex satisfies ∣ϕ∣=eu/2≤1 everywhere.

For a conformal rescaling of a Riemannian metric h=Ω(dx2+dy2), the Taubes equation in flat coordinates is
Δv+Ω(1−ev)=4π∑r​Nr​δ(2)(z−zr​).
(24)
The first solution u has Ω=1 and vortex number N. For g​=eug, the second solution therefore obeys
Δu+eu(1−eu)=4π∑s​Ns​δ(2)(z−zs​),
(25)
where we used Δg​​=e−uΔg​ and converted the covariant delta distribution to the flat coordinate measure. Adding the equation for u gives
Δ(u+u)+1−eu+u=4π[∑r​Nr​δ(2)(z−zr​)+∑s​Ns​δ(2)(z−zs​)].
(26)
Thus u+u is again a flat-metric Taubes equation solution. Its vortex divisor is the union of the two divisors, with multiplicities added at coincident zeros, and its total vortex number is
Ntotal​=N+N​.
(27)
This is vortex composition by conformal rescaling.

On an oriented Euclidean four-space with its metric volume form, the Hodge star operator is defined by
α∧⋆β=⟨α,β⟩vol
(28)
for forms α and β of the same degree. In n Euclidean dimensions it satisfies
⋆(⋆α)=(−1)p(n−p)α(α∈Λp).
(29)
Hence on two-forms in four dimensions,
⋆⋆=1on Λ2(R4)​.
(30)
The normalization of vol as the metric volume form is essential; reversing its orientation reverses a single Hodge star but leaves its square unchanged.

Because ⋆⋆=1, the exterior algebra of two-forms has the orthogonal decomposition
Λ2=Λ+2​⊕Λ−2​,F±​=21​(F±⋆F),
(31)
where ⋆H=H for a self-dual differential form and ⋆G=−G for an anti-self-dual differential form. The Hodge star is self-adjoint, so
⟨H,G⟩=⟨⋆H,⋆G⟩=−⟨H,G⟩=0.
(32)
It follows that
H∧G=−H∧⋆G=−⟨H,G⟩vol=0​.
(33)
For an SU(n) connection, use the positive norm
∥F∥2=−∫R4​Tr(F∧⋆F)
(34)
and define the Second Chern number by
k=−8π21​∫R4​Tr(F∧F).
(35)
Orthogonality gives
∥F∥2=∥F+​∥2+∥F−​∥2,8π2k=∥F+​∥2−∥F−​∥2.
(36)
Therefore the Euclidean Yang-Mills action
SYM​=gYM2​1​∥F∥2
(37)
obeys the Yang-Mills instanton Bogomolny bound
SYM​≥gYM2​8π2​∣k∣​.
(38)
Equality holds precisely when F−​=0 or F+​=0, according to the sign of k; these are the self-dual and anti-self-dual Yang-Mills instantons. Other trace and orientation conventions may reverse k but leave the absolute-value bound unchanged.

Let Di​=∂i​+Ai​ and Dτ​=∂τ​+Aτ​. In temporal gauge, Aτ​=0, so
Fτi​=∂τ​Ai​.

Choose the orientation dx1∧dx2∧dx3∧dτ and the anti-self-duality convention matching the question. The three independent components of F=−⋆F are

Let ζ∈CP1 be the spectral parameter. Define the two covariant differential operators
L(ζ)=D1​+iD2​−ζ(D3​−iDτ​),
(42)
M(ζ)=D3​+iDτ​+ζ(D1​−iD2​).
(43)
The auxiliary system
L(ζ)Ψ=0,M(ζ)Ψ=0
(44)
is compatible exactly when [L(ζ),M(ζ)]=0 for every ζ. The constant and quadratic coefficients give
F13​−F2τ​=0,F1τ​+F23​=0,
(45)
and their equivalent conjugate equations, while the linear coefficient gives
F12​+F3τ​=0.
(46)
Since Fiτ​=−Fτi​, these are precisely
Fτ1​=F23​,Fτ2​=F31​,Fτ3​=F12​.
(47)
Thus
(L(ζ),M(ζ))​
(48)
is a Lax pair for the anti-self-dual Yang-Mills equations. In temporal gauge one simply sets Dτ​=∂τ​.
