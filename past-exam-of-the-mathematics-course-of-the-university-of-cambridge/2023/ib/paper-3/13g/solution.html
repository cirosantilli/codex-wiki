Rouché's theorem states that if g and h are holomorphic functions on a neighbourhood of the closure of a bounded domain and
∣h(z)∣<∣g(z)∣
(88)
on its positively oriented boundary, then g and g+h have the same number of zeros in the domain, counted with multiplicity.
The Open mapping theorem states that a nonconstant holomorphic function on a domain maps every open subset to an open subset. To prove it, fix z0​ and put w0​=f(z0​). By the identity theorem, the zeros of f−w0​ are isolated. Choose r>0 so that the closed disc D(z0​,r)​ lies in the domain and z0​ is the only zero of f−w0​ in that disc. Then
δ=min∣z−z0​∣=r​∣f(z)−w0​∣>0.
(89)
Whenever ∣w−w0​∣<δ, the constant w0​−w has modulus smaller than f−w0​ on the circle. The Rouche theorem therefore says that
f(z)−w=(f(z)−w0​)+(w0​−w)
(90)
has the same positive number of zeros in the disc as f−w0​. Thus every w∈D(w0​,δ) lies in the image of f, proving that the image is open.
If ∣f∣ had a local maximum at z0​, take a small open disc D on which ∣f(z)∣≤∣f(z0​)∣. The restriction of f to D cannot be constant, since the identity theorem would then make f constant on the whole domain. The complex open mapping theorem says that f(D) is an open neighbourhood of f(z0​), and such a neighbourhood contains points of modulus greater than ∣f(z0​)∣, a contradiction. This proves the maximum modulus principle from the complex open mapping theorem.
Now let Ω be bounded. Its closure is compact, so the continuous function ∣f∣ attains a maximum there. If f is nonconstant, the maximum modulus principle excludes an interior maximum; if f is constant, the claimed bound is immediate. Hence
maxz∈Ω​∣f(z)∣=maxz∈∂Ω​∣f(z)∣≤M,
(91)
which is the maximum modulus principle on a bounded domain.
Finally let Ω={z:Rez>1} and suppose ∣f∣≤K throughout Ω. Fix z0​∈Ω. The principal complex logarithm is holomorphic on the right half-plane, so for each positive integer n the function
gn​(z)=f(z)z−1/n=f(z)exp(−n1​Logz)
(92)
is holomorphic on Ω and continuous on its closure. Given ε>0, choose R>∣z0​∣ so large that
KR−1/n≤M+ε.
(93)
Apply the bounded-domain result to gn​ on Ω∩D(0,R). On the vertical part of the boundary, ∣z∣≥1 and hence ∣gn​(z)∣≤M. On the circular part,
∣gn​(z)∣≤KR−1/n≤M+ε.
(94)
It follows that
∣f(z0​)∣≤(M+ε)∣z0​∣1/n.
(95)
Letting n→∞ and then ε→0 gives ∣f(z0​)∣≤M. Since z0​ was arbitrary, this proves the bounded half-plane maximum principle.
The boundedness assumption is necessary. The function
