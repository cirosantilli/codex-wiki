1E OurBigBook.com
For a finite group G and a subgroup H, Lagrange's theorem states
∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣.
(1)
In particular ∣H∣ divides ∣G∣: the left cosets partition G, and multiplication by a representative is a bijection from H to each coset. Apply this to the cyclic subgroup ⟨g⟩. Its size is the order of a group element g, so every element order divides ∣G∣.
Under the square condition, every group element is its own inverse element. Consequently
gh=(gh)−1=h−1g−1=hg
(2)
for all g,h. This proves that a group of exponent two is abelian, including the trivial group.
For the fourth-power condition a counterexample to commutativity is the quaternion group
Q8​={±1,±i,±j,±k}.​
(3)
Here i2=j2=k2=−1 and ij=k=−ji. Thus its six elements outside {±1} have order of a group element four, while 1 and −1 have orders one and two. All fourth powers are the identity, yet ij=ji.

A permutation cycle (a1​ a2​ ⋯ ap​) sends a1​ to a2​, each successive listed point to the next, and ap​ to a1​, fixing every unlisted point. Its length is p. For any permutation h,
h(a1​ ⋯ ap​)h−1=(h(a1​) ⋯ h(ap​)).
(4)
Thus conjugate group elements in the symmetric group have the same cycle length. Conversely, two cycles of the same length are conjugate: send their entries, in cyclic order, to one another and extend this bijection arbitrarily to the unused points. Length-one cycles represent the identity and cause no exception to this conclusion.
An odd-length permutation cycle is an even permutation, since its sign is (−1)p−1. Let c,d be such cycles on p+2 points and choose h∈Sp+2​ with hch−1=d. If h is odd, let t be the transposition of the two points unused by the listed cycle c. Then tc=ct, and ht is even with
(ht)c(ht)−1=hch−1=d.
(5)
Hence all the p-cycles are conjugate within Ap+2​. The two spare letters supply the parity correction; they are the reason the same argument need not work with only one spare letter.
In A4​ the answer is no. For example, (1 2 3) and (1 3 2) are conjugated by the odd transposition (2 3) in S4​. The centralizer of (1 2 3) in S4​ is exactly its order-three cyclic subgroup: a commuting permutation fixes the unique fixed point 4 and acts as a power of the cycle on the other three points. All these centralizing elements are even. Every other conjugating element differs from (2 3) by a centralizing element, so it is also odd. There is no conjugator in A4​. Equivalently, the eight three-cycles split into two conjugacy classes of size 12/3=4, illustrating the alternating conjugacy class splitting criterion.

A differential one-form Pdx+Qdy is an exact differential if there is a differentiable function F with P=Fx​ and Q=Fy​, so the form equals dF. For continuously differentiable P,Q, equality of mixed partial derivatives gives the necessary condition
Py​=Qx​.​
(6)
A local differential condition need not by itself give a global potential on a domain with holes; no such issue arises for the explicit primitives here.
For the first form, Py​=2y=Qx​ and direct differentiation gives
y2dx+2xydy=d(xy2).​
(7)
For the second, Py​=2y but Qx​=y2. These are not equal on any nonempty open subset of the plane, so that form is not an exact differential. Nevertheless,
y2dx+xy2dy=y2(dx+xdy)=y2e−yd(xey)​.
(8)
Thus one suitable pair is f(x,y)=xey, g(y)=y2e−y. This factorization is smooth even at y=0. On regions where y=0, its reciprocal factor ey/y2 is an integrating factor which turns the original form into df.

A Cartesian second-rank tensor is an isotropic tensor if its components are unchanged under every proper rotation matrix R:
Tij​=Rik​Rjℓ​Tkℓ​,or equivalently T=RTRT.
(9)
The Kronecker delta is isotropic because Rik​Rjk​=δij​, the orthogonality relation RRT=I.
No symmetry of T needs to be assumed. A half-turn about the first axis has matrix diag(1,−1,−1); invariance under it forces T12​,T13​,T21​,T31​ to vanish. A half-turn about the second axis additionally kills T23​ and T32​. Thus T=diag(d1​,d2​,d3​). Each half-turn is the square of an allowed quarter-turn. A quarter-turn about the third axis interchanges d1​,d2​, so they are equal. A quarter-turn about the second axis interchanges d1​,d3​, so all three are equal. Consequently the complete family of isotropic second-rank tensors is
Tij​=λδij​,λ∈R.​
(10)
Conversely, every member of this family is invariant because R(λI)RT=λI.

For a group action of G on X, let Gx​={g:gx=x} be the stabilizer subgroup of x, and Gx its group orbit. The orbit-stabilizer theorem is the bijection
G/Gx​⟶Gx,gGx​⟼gx.
(11)
It is well defined because elements in the same left coset differ by an element fixing x. Conversely, gx=hx implies h−1g∈Gx​, so the cosets coincide. Surjectivity is the definition of the group orbit. For a finite group, counting the cosets gives
∣G∣=∣Gx∣∣Gx​∣.​
(12)
To obtain Cayley theorem, let G act on its own underlying set by left multiplication. The map λg​:x↦gx is a permutation, with inverse λg−1​, and λg​λh​=λgh​. If λg​ is the identity permutation, evaluating at the identity element gives g=e. Thus g↦λg​ is an injective group homomorphism. When ∣G∣=n, labeling this set by n letters identifies its image with a subgroup of Sn​. Every finite group embeds in the symmetric group on its own elements.

Choose x0​∈X with stabilizer subgroup H. Transitivity means that every point is gx0​ for some g. Its stabilizer subgroup is
Ggx0​​=gHg−1,
(13)
since a(gx0​)=gx0​ exactly when g−1ag∈H. The kernel of a group homomorphism arising from the group action is the set of elements fixing every point, hence
ker(G→Sym(X))=g∈G⋂​gHg−1=CoreG​(H).​
(14)
This is the subgroup core. A group homomorphism is injective precisely when its kernel is trivial. The desired equivalence is therefore exactly the equivalence between a trivial subgroup core and a faithful group action. No finiteness assumption is required in this part.

iii OurBigBook.com
Every nontrivial subgroup of the quaternion group contains −1. Indeed, it either contains −1 directly, or contains one of ±i,±j,±k, whose square is −1.
Suppose Q8​ acts on an n-point set with n<8. Every group orbit has size at most n<8. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, each point's stabilizer subgroup has order 8/∣Q8​x∣>1, so it contains −1. Consequently −1 fixes every point, and the group action is not faithful. An embedding in Sn​ would give a faithful group action on n letters, which is impossible. Thus
Q8​↪Sn​(n<8).​
(15)
The left-regular group action on eight points is faithful by Cayley theorem, so the minimal faithful permutation degree of the quaternion group is exactly eight. The proof allows multiple group orbits; restricting only to transitive actions would not suffice.

Write a member of the special linear group as g=(ac​bd​) with ad−bc=1. Its Möbius transformation is z↦(az+b)/(cz+d), with the usual values at a pole and at infinity. For nonreal z,
Im(gz)=∣cz+d∣2Imz​.
(16)
This follows by multiplying numerator and denominator by czˉ+d. Thus the sign of the imaginary part is preserved, while the extended real line is preserved as a set.
Fixing zero requires b=0. Therefore its stabilizer subgroup and group orbit are
G0​={(ac​0a−1​):a=0, c∈R},G0=R∪{∞}.​
(17)
Translations send zero to each finite real point, and (01​−10​) sends it to infinity.
For gi=i, comparing real and imaginary parts gives a=d and b=−c, with a2+c2=1. Exactly the same conditions follow from g(−i)=−i. Hence
Gi​=G−i​=SO(2)={(cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​):θ∈R}.​
(18)
For any x∈R and y>0, the upper triangular matrix
hx,y​=(y​0​x/y​1/y​​)
(19)
sends i to x+iy and −i to x−iy. Therefore the two group orbits are the open complex upper half-plane and the open lower half-plane respectively. Along with the extended real line they exhaust the Riemann sphere, so there are exactly three orbits, the real determinant-one Möbius orbits.
Every h∈H has the form (a0​ba−1​) and acts by z↦a2z+ab. Thus Hi is the entire complex upper half-plane, by the same explicit matrices hx,y​. Given g∈G, choose h∈H such that hi=gi. Then k=h−1g fixes i, so k∈SO(2) and g=hk. This proves the triangular-rotation factorization of real determinant-one matrices.
For uniqueness, if h1​k1​=h2​k2​, then h2−1​h1​=k2​k1−1​∈H∩SO(2). An upper triangular rotation matrix must have sinθ=0, so
H∩SO(2)={I,−I}.
(20)
There are consequently exactly two matrix factorizations, (h,k) and (−h,−k). Restricting the first diagonal entry of h to be positive makes the factorization unique. For example, if g=(ac​bd​) and q=c2+d2​, that unique branch is
h=(1/q0​(ac+bd)/qq​),k=q1​(dc​−cd​).
(21)
Angles are understood modulo 2π when counting matrices; allowing unrestricted real angle representatives gives infinitely many labels for those same two factorizations.

Use the standard implicit assumption that p is prime, so Fp​ is a finite field. The matrices in question form GL2​(Fp​), the general linear group over a finite field. Products stay in the set because determinants multiply, the identity has determinant one, and
(ac​bd​)−1=(ad−bc)−1(d−c​−ba​)
(22)
has entries in the finite field and nonzero determinant. Together with associativity this verifies the group axioms. The multiplication of matrices with column vectors is a group action because Iv=v and (gh)v=g(hv).
Let C=⟨g⟩ be the order-p cyclic subgroup. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, its group orbits on Fp2​ have size either one or p. If f is the number of fixed vectors, counting all p2 vectors gives f≡p2≡0(modp). Since the zero vector is fixed, f≥1 and hence f≥p. There is a nonzero fixed vector v.
Extend v to a basis (v,w). In this basis g has matrix (10​bd​). From gp=I we get dp=1; in the prime finite field, dp=d, so d=1. Since g has order p>1, it is not the identity, and b=0. Replace w by b−1w. Then g(b−1w)=v+b−1w, and in the new basis
g∼(10​11​).​
(23)
Thus all the order-p matrices in GL2 over the prime field form one conjugacy class. This proof also works at p=2. Primality is essential to the first assertion: for modulus four, diag(2,1) has nonzero determinant but no inverse, so the set defined using merely nonzero determinants would not be a group.

Work with x modulo p−1 and u modulo p. By Fermat's little theorem, ap−1=1 in Fp​, so the factor ay is independent of the chosen representative of y. The proposed multiplication is therefore well defined on the stated Cartesian product. Its two associative bracketings have the same second component:
az(ayu+v)+w=ay+zu+azv+w,
(24)
and both have first component x+y+z. The identity element is (0,0), and the two-sided inverse element is
(x,u)−1=(−x,−a−xu)​.
(25)
Thus the multiplication defines the twisted cyclic pair group, of order p(p−1).
If a=1, multiplication is coordinatewise addition, so the group is abelian. If a=1, then p>2 and both (1,0) and (0,1) are available; their products in opposite orders are (1,1) and (1,a). They differ. Hence
G is abelian⟺a=1.​
(26)
Both H and K contain the identity element and are closed under the multiplication and inverses: they are the cyclic groups of orders p−1 and p respectively. Conjugation gives, with all coordinates reduced in the appropriate modulus,
(x,u)(0,v)(x,u)−1=(0,a−xv),
(27)
so K is a normal subgroup. For H, the corresponding calculation is
(x,u)(y,0)(x,u)−1=(y,a−x(ay−1)u).
(28)
When a=1 this lies in H. Conversely, for a=1, take x=0, u=1, y=1: the second coordinate is a−1=0, so H is not normal. Thus H is normal precisely when a=1.
Finally, the projection
π:G⟶Z/(p−1)Z,π(x,u)=x​
(29)
is a surjective group homomorphism because the first coordinates add. Its kernel of a group homomorphism is exactly K. At p=2, a=1 is the only possibility and the first factor is trivial, consistent with every conclusion. No assumption that a generates the multiplicative group of the finite field is needed.

For an oriented smooth surface S with piecewise smooth, consistently oriented boundary, Stokes theorem gives
∫S​(∇×B)⋅ndS=∮∂S​B⋅dx​
(30)
for a continuously differentiable vector field defined on a neighborhood of S. Choose the upward normal vector here. The surface is an annular band of an elliptic paraboloid, parametrized by
r(r,θ)=(rcosθ,rsinθ,r2),1/3≤r≤1,0≤θ<2π.
(31)
Using ∂r​r×∂θ​r as the oriented vector area element gives
dS=(−2r2cosθ,−2r2sinθ,r)drdθ,dS=r1+4r2​drdθ.​
(32)
The sketch below shows the open band, not a capped solid. Its upper circle has radius one and its lower circle radius one third.
Differentiating the given vector field yields
∇×B=(0,0,3x2+3y2)=(0,0,3r2).
(33)
The surface integral is therefore
I=∫02π​∫1/31​3r3drdθ=23π​(1−3−4)=2740π​​.
(34)
For the boundary line integral, the upward normal vector induces counterclockwise traversal of the outer circle and clockwise traversal of the inner circle, as viewed from above. On a circle of radius R, parametrized counterclockwise, z is constant and
B⋅dx=R4(sin4θ+cos4θ)dθ.
(35)
Since each fourth power integrates to 3π/4, the two-circle line integral is
∮∂S​B⋅dx=23π​(1−811​)=2740π​,
(36)
confirming Stokes theorem and the Stokes flux through an annular paraboloid. Reversing the chosen orientation changes both integrals to −40π/27.

For fixed u∈(0,1), x=1−u∈(0,1) and
∂v∂y​=−(1−uv)21−u​<0.
(37)
As v runs from zero to one, y decreases continuously from one to zero. Thus the map is onto the open unit square and is one-to-one, with smooth inverse
u=1−x,v=1−(1−x)y1−y​.
(38)
Its denominator is positive in the open square. This is a rational square diffeomorphism. Its Jacobian determinant is
∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​=(−1)(−(1−uv)21−u​)=(1−uv)2x​.
(39)
Writing A=1−(1−x)y, the inverse relation gives 1−uv=x/A. Consequently
∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​=xA2​.​
(40)
The second transformation also maps the open unit square bijectively onto the open unit square: for fixed w, u=(1−t)/(1−wt) decreases strictly from one to zero as t goes from zero to one, while v=1−w independently spans (0,1). Its positive Jacobian determinant is
∂(t,w)∂(u,v)​=(1−wt)21−w​.
(41)
To express this in x,y, the composite transformation gives y=w/[x+(1−x)w] and x=t(1−w)/(1−wt). Put B=1−(1−x2)y. Solving these relations gives
w=Axy​,t=BxA​,1−w=A1−y​,1−wt=B1−y​.
(42)
The chain rule for Jacobian determinants therefore gives
∂(t,w)∂(x,y)​=xA2​A(1−y)B2​=x(1−y)AB2​.​
(43)
The requested integrand is exactly the reciprocal of this positive determinant. Apply the change of variables formula using the composite diffeomorphism:
∫R​[1−(1−x)y][1−(1−x2)y]2x(1−y)​dxdy=∫01​∫01​1dtdw=1.​
(44)
Possible singular behavior at the square's boundary does not invalidate the calculation: first integrate over the images of compact interior squares, where all transformations are smooth with nonzero Jacobian determinant, and then increase these domains to the full square. Positivity and monotone convergence theorem justify this limit.

[F×(∇×G)]i​​=ϵijk​Fj​ϵkℓm​∂ℓ​Gm​=(δiℓ​δjm​−δim​δjℓ​)Fj​∂ℓ​Gm​=Fj​∂i​Gj​−Fj​∂j​Gi​.​

Assume the continuously differentiable irrotational vector field is defined on all of R3, as in this vector calculus setting. A constructive vector-field potential with the requested negative-gradient convention is
ϕ(x)=−∫01​xj​Ej​(tx)dt.
(47)
Since zero curl means ∂i​Ej​=∂j​Ei​, differentiation under the integral gives

For the given field, the second component requires ϕy​=ye−x2z. Integration in y gives ϕ=21​y2e−x2z+C(x,z). Its x derivative is −xy2ze−x2z+Cx​, and its z derivative is −21​x2y2e−x2z+Cz​. Comparison with the other two components forces Cx​=Cz​=0. Hence

For two solutions of the Poisson equation with the same Dirichlet boundary condition, let w=u1​−u2​. Then ∇2w=0 in V and w=0 on its boundary. By Green's first identity,
∫V​∣∇w∣2dV=∫S​w∂n​wdS−∫V​w∇2wdV=0.
(50)
Thus w is constant on each connected component, and the zero boundary values make each constant zero. The Dirichlet solution is unique whenever it exists. This argument requires the regularity needed for the displayed integration by parts; it does not assert existence for arbitrary rough data.
With a Neumann boundary condition, integrating the Poisson equation and using the divergence theorem instead gives the necessary compatibility condition
∫V​ρdV=∫S​gdS.​
(51)
On a disconnected region this condition must hold on each connected component separately. If two solutions have the same normal derivative, their difference is harmonic with ∂n​w=0. The same Green's first identity again forces ∇w=0. Thus Neumann solutions are unique only up to an additive constant on each connected component. Adding a constant preserves both the equation and normal derivative, so uniqueness without a normalization cannot hold. On a connected region, prescribing the mean value removes this one-dimensional freedom.
