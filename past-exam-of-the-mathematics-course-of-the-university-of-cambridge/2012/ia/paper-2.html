The repeated-root constant-coefficient differential equation has characteristic polynomial (r+2)2. Its two fundamental solutions are
y1​=e−2x,y2​=xe−2x​.
(1)
They are linearly independent: their Wronskian is e−4x, which never vanishes. For the forced equation, exploit the repeated factor by writing y=e−2xu. Direct differentiation gives (D+2)2y=e−2xu′′, so the forcing reduces to u′′=1. The initial data imply u(0)=0 and u′(0)=0, hence u=x2/2. Therefore
y(x)=2x2​e−2x,x≥0​.
(2)
The extra factor x2 reflects resonance of the forcing with the repeated characteristic root; the exponential substitution obtains it without guessing a particular solution.

A constant orbit is a fixed point of F(u)=u(1+u)/2. Solving F(u)=u gives u(u−1)=0, hence
un​≡0andun​≡1​.
(3)
For fixed point stability for an iteration, perturb a fixed point u∗​ by vn​. Its local evolution is vn+1​=F′(u∗​)vn​+O(vn2​), where F′(u)=u+1/2. At zero, F′(0)=1/2, so perturbations contract: zero is locally asymptotically stable. For example, if ∣u∣≤r<1, then ∣F(u)∣≤(1+r)∣u∣/2; this gives an invariant neighborhood and geometric convergence to zero.
At one, F′(1)=3/2, so perturbations expand: one is unstable. More explicitly, putting un​=1+vn​ gives vn+1​=3vn​/2+vn2​/2, whose magnitude increases while a nonzero perturbation remains sufficiently small. The derivative test concerns local stability; it does not assert attraction from every real initial value.

For events with P(B)>0, the conditional probability is P(A∣B)=P(A∩B)/P(B). Applying the definition in both orders gives Bayes' theorem:
P(B∣A)=P(A)P(A∩B)​=P(A∣B)P(A)P(B)​.
(4)
Conditionally on N=n, independent fair tosses give a binomial distribution for the head count, so P(H=1∣N=n)=n2−n. The joint probability mass function is consequently
P(N=n,H=1)=n4−n.
(5)
Use the law of total probability and the differentiated geometric series, ∑n≥1​nrn=r/(1−r)2, to obtain P(H=1)=4/9. The posterior coin count after exactly one head is therefore
P(N=n∣H=1)=4n+19n​,n≥1​.
(6)
These probabilities sum to one by the same series identity. The conditioning weights possible coin counts by their likelihood of producing exactly one head.

For a nonnegative integer-valued random variable, the probability generating function is
GX​(s)=E[sX]=∑j=0∞​P(X=j)sj,
(7)
with 0≤s≤1 always allowed. Its derivative at 1 from below gives the expectation when finite.
The waiting time N has the positive-integer geometric distribution P(N=j)=pqj−1. For the capped geometric waiting time, X=j<k exactly when N=j, while X=k occurs whenever N≥k. Thus the terminal mass is qk−1, giving
GX​(s)=pj=1∑k−1​qj−1sj+qk−1sk=1−qsps+qksk(1−s)​​.
(8)
The finite sum is a polynomial; any apparent singularity of the rational expression is removable. This includes k=1, where GX​(s)=s.
Differentiating the rational form at s=1 yields
GX′​(1)=p2(p−qk)p+pq​=p1−qk​​.
(9)
As a second interpretation, the expectation is the sum of the tail probabilities P(X≥j)=qj−1 for 1≤j≤k. Their finite geometric sum gives the same answer.

Use a power-series solution of a differential equation, y=∑n≥0​cn​xn. Comparing coefficients gives
(n−1)(n−2)cn​=cn−2​,c−1​=c−2​=0.
(10)
In particular c0​=0, while c1​ and c2​ are free. The two prescribed derivative pairs select (c1​,c2​)=(a,0) and (0,b). The next coefficients are c3​=c1​/2, c4​=c2​/6, c5​=c1​/24, c6​=c2​/120. Hence
y1​(x)=a(x+2x3​+24x5​+O(x7))​,y2​(x)=b(x2+6x4​+120x6​+O(x8))​.
(11)
These are the first three nonzero terms when the respective parameter is nonzero; if a=0 or b=0, the corresponding solution vanishes identically.
For closed forms, set y=xu. Cancellation of the first-derivative terms reduces the equation, for x=0, to u′′−u=0. The analytic solutions extend across zero, and their hyperbolic cosine and hyperbolic sine expansions identify
y1​=axcoshx,y2​=bxsinhx​.
(12)
This is the hyperbolic reduction of a regular-singular differential equation: although the original leading coefficient vanishes at zero, both selected power-series solutions of a differential equation are entire.

6A OurBigBook.com
Completing squares reveals the geometry of the sheared quartic double-well gradient flow:
V=(x2−1)2+(x+y)2−1.
(13)
The critical points satisfy 2(x+y)=0 and 4x3−2x+2y=0, so they are (0,0), (1,−1) and (−1,1). The Hessian matrix is
HessV=(12x2−22​22​).
(14)
At the origin its determinant is −8, so (0,0) is a saddle point, with V=0. At either other critical point, the determinant is 16 and the upper-left entry is 10, so it is a positive-definite matrix. They are strict local minima; the completed squares show that both are global minima, with V=−1.
A contour at level c obeys
(x+y)2=c+1−(x2−1)2,y=−x±c+1−(x2−1)2​.
(15)
There are no contours for c<−1, two isolated minimum points for c=−1, and two separate closed ovals for −1<c<0. At c=0 the two lobes meet at the saddle point; near the origin their tangents are y=(−1±2​)x. For c>0 one closed contour surrounds both minima.
Along the gradient flow, the gradient-flow dissipation identity is
dtdV​=Vx​x˙+Vy​y˙​=−Vx2​−Vy2​≤0​.
(16)
At the stated initial point, V(1,−1/2)=−3/4. Its trajectory stays in the invariant sublevel set V≤−3/4. This set is compact because V is a coercive function. It also excludes every point with x=0, where V=y2≥0; continuity therefore keeps the trajectory in its positive-x component.
To justify its limit rather than merely infer it from the sketch, integrate the dissipation identity: ∫0∞​∥∇V∥2dt is finite. On this compact set the gradient and its time derivative are bounded, so ∥∇V∥2 is uniformly continuous. A nonnegative uniformly continuous function with finite integral tends to zero: otherwise separated intervals of a fixed positive height and width would force an infinite integral. Every accumulation point is consequently a critical point. In the positive-x component below this energy level the only one is (1,−1). Compactness then implies convergence, and
(x(t),y(t))⟶(1,−1)(t→∞)​.
(17)
This is a compact gradient-flow trapping criterion; the drawn trajectory illustrates, rather than replaces, the convergence proof.

Write the linear system of differential equations as
z′=t1​Az+b,A=(4−1​−25​),b=(3−9​).
(18)
A homogeneous power tλv solves this Cauchy-Euler differential system precisely when Av=λv. The eigenvalues are 3 and 6, with eigenvectors (2,1)T and (1,−1)T. The homogeneous solution is therefore Ct3(2,1)T+Dt6(1,−1)T.
For a particular solution, put zp​=tc. Then (I−A)c=b, which gives c=(3,0)T. At t=1, the two initial conditions become 2C+D+3=0 and C−D=0, so C=D=−1. Thus
x(t)=3t−2t3−t6,y(t)=t6−t3,t≥1​.
(19)
The two powers correspond to the two eigenvalues; the lower-degree particular term accounts for the constant forcing. Substitution gives both original right sides and the zero initial data.

Use the shift derivative of a step response, writing y3​(t,b)=H(s)K(s) with s=t−b. Differentiate with respect to the forcing location:

The moment-generating function is MX​(t)=E[etX] at real t for which that expectation is finite; MX​(0)=1. For independent random variables, the joint law factorizes, so
MaX+bY​(t)=E[eatXebtY]=E[eatX]E[ebtY]=MX​(at)MY​(bt)​.
(27)
This is the transform form of independence. Its ordinary finite-valued interpretation applies where both factors are finite, with the transform of a zero multiple equal to 1.

Finiteness of the moment-generating function on an open neighborhood of zero gives its differentiability there. Using the permitted interchange of differentiation and expectation,
MX′​(t)=E[XetX],MX′′​(t)=E[X2etX].

Let S=X+Y and D=X−Y. Since they are independent, applying the product rule for moment-generating functions to S+D=2X gives
M(2t)=MS​(t)MD​(t)=M(t)2M(t)M(−t)=M(t)3M(−t)​.
(30)
All factors are finite and strictly positive for every real t by the stated hypothesis. Applying the same identity to −t and dividing yields ψ(2t)=ψ(t)2, or
ψ(t)=ψ(t/2)2​,ψ(t)=M(−t)M(t)​.
(31)
The centered unit-variance Taylor expansion from part (ii) gives M(h)=1+h2/2+o(h2) and the same expansion for M(−h). Consequently ψ(h)=1+o(h2).
For the first step of dyadic rigidity of a moment-generating function, take logarithms, which are allowed because ψ>0. For each fixed t and every integer m,
logψ(t)=2mlogψ(t/2m).
(32)
Since logψ(h)=o(h2), the right side tends to zero: it is 2mo(t2/4m). Thus ψ(t)=1 for every t, and the moment-generating function is even.
The original identity now becomes M(2t)=M(t)4. Iterating this time with the correct fourth-power scaling gives
logM(t)=4mlogM(t/2m).
(33)
Near zero, logM(h)=h2/2+o(h2), so the right side tends to t2/2. Therefore
M(t)=et2/2for all real t​.
(34)
This is the moment-generating function of a standard normal variable. By the uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions, each of X and Y has the standard normal distribution. The proof establishes the Gaussian characterization by independent sum and difference directly from the two functional identities and the first two moments.

For a Galton-Watson process, condition on the number K of children of the initial individual. The descendants of these children over the next n generations are independent copies of Xn​. The probability generating function of their total, conditional on K=k, is Gn​(s)k. Averaging over the family-size law gives
Gn+1​(s)=E[Gn​(s)K]=G(Gn​(s))​,G0​(s)=s.
(41)
This is the branching-process generating-function iteration. Conditioning instead on the last generation gives Gn​(G(s)); both orders agree because Gn​ is the nth iterate of the same function G. The argument holds on the usual PGF domain ∣s∣≤1, with larger domains available when the corresponding expectations converge.

For independent random variables, {min(X,Y)>u}={X>u,Y>u} and {max(X,Y)≤v}={X≤v,Y≤v}. Factor their probabilities to obtain the distribution functions of independent minima and maxima:
FU​(u)=1−[1−FX​(u)][1−FY​(u)],FV​(v)=FX​(v)FY​(v)​.
(51)
These formulas also allow atoms; no probability density function assumption is needed.
For independent unit-rate exponential distributions, P(U>u)=e−2u for u≥0, so U∼Exp(2). To establish the independent minimum and gap of two exponential variables, set D=V−U. On either ordering branch, the change of variables is (X,Y)=(u,u+d) or (u+d,u) with unit absolute Jacobian determinant. Both contribute e−2u−d; ties have probability zero. Thus the joint probability density is
fU,D​(u,d)=2e−2u−d=(2e−2u)(e−d),u,d>0.
(52)
It factors into normalized exponential probability density functions, proving U∼Exp(2), D∼Exp(1) and their independence.
The maximum is V=U+D. A half-scaled unit-rate exponential has rate two, so the independent pair (D,U) has the same joint law as (X,Y/2). Consequently
V=dX+21​Y​.
(53)
Using the expected values, variances and independence of the summands,
EV=23​,Var(V)=45​​.
(54)
The spacing factorization explains why an order statistic can be represented as a sum of independent waiting times.
