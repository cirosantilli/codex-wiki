All the transformations below fix the origin. A planar rotation preserves lengths and angles and turns every vector through one common oriented angle. Its rotation matrix is
Rθ​=(cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​).
(3)
An orthogonal reflection in a line fixes that line and reverses its perpendicular direction; reflection in the horizontal axis has reflection matrix diag(1,−1). A uniform dilation multiplies every vector by the same positive scale s, represented by sI; for example 2I doubles lengths. A shear mapping fixes one line pointwise and shifts vectors parallel to that line by an amount proportional to their transverse coordinate. A horizontal shear mapping has matrix
Sk​=(10​k1​),(x,y)⟼(x+ky,y),
(4)
with S1​ a nontrivial example.
The first given matrix is the clockwise planar rotation through π/4, namely A=R−π/4​. Direct multiplication gives
C=AB=(10​21​)=S2​.​
(5)
Thus C is a horizontal shear mapping of strength 2. Because A−1=Rπ/4​,
B=Rπ/4​S2​.​
(6)
So B is the composition of that shear mapping followed by an anticlockwise planar rotation through π/4; order matters. It is not individually one of the four elementary types under these definitions. In particular, its determinant is 1, but it is not an orthogonal matrix, excluding both a planar rotation and an orthogonal reflection. It is not a scalar matrix, excluding a uniform dilation; its eigenvalues 2​+1 and 2​−1 exclude a pure shear mapping, whose eigenvalues are both 1. As a symmetric positive-definite matrix, B can also be described as stretching two perpendicular principal directions by these reciprocal factors, preserving area.

A continuous function at x0​ satisfies the following quantified condition:
∀ε>0 ∃δ>0 ∀x∈R:∣x−x0​∣<δ ⟹ ∣f(x)−f(x0​)∣<ε.​
(7)
The choice of δ may depend on x0​ and ε.
For the bounded example, take
f(x)=(1−x)sin(1/x),0<x≤1.​
(8)
This is a continuous function on its domain and ∣f(x)∣≤1−x<1. Along xn​=(π/2+2πn)−1 its values tend to 1, while along yn​=(3π/2+2πn)−1 its values tend to −1. Hence the supremum is 1 and the infimum is −1, and neither is attained. “Upper and lower bound” here means the least upper and greatest lower bounds.
For the nonnegative function on the whole real line, if f≡0 the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise choose x∗​ with m=f(x∗​)>0. The limits at both infinities give R>∣x∗​∣ such that f(x)<m/2 whenever ∣x∣>R. By the extreme value theorem, a continuous function on the compact interval [−R,R] has a maximum M=f(α) there. Since M≥m, it also exceeds every value outside this interval. Thus f is bounded above and attains its global maximum. Nonnegativity ensures that either the function is zero or such a positive comparison value exists; without it, a function such as −e−x2 would have an unattained supremum of zero.

∀ε>0 ∃N∈N ∀n≥N:∣xn​−ℓ∣<ε.​

Let Rf​,Rg​ be the two radii of convergence. First take ∣x∣<t<Rf​, with t>0. Absolute convergence at t bounds the terms: ∣an​tn∣≤M for all n. Thus
∣(n+1)an+1​xn∣≤tM​(n+1)(∣x∣/t)n.
(41)
At x=0 the right-hand series is finite. Otherwise it converges by the ratio test, because its limiting ratio is ∣x∣/t<1. So the differentiated power series converges absolutely at every interior point of f, proving Rg​≥Rf​. This proof also covers Rf​=∞ by choosing a finite t above any given ∣x∣; if Rf​=0, that inequality is automatic.
Conversely, if 0<∣x∣<Rg​, absolute convergence gives
∑n=1∞​∣an​∣∣x∣n=∣x∣∑m=0∞​m+1∣(m+1)am+1​∣∣x∣m​≤∣x∣∑m=0∞​∣(m+1)am+1​∣∣x∣m<∞.
(42)
The constant term does not affect convergence, and both series converge at zero. Hence Rf​≥Rg​, including the zero and infinite cases. Therefore Rf​=Rg​​. Since the common radius of convergence is infinite, the termwise differentiation of a power series theorem applies on the entire real line. For the even series, differentiation and the index change m=n−1 give
f′(x)=∑n=1∞​(−1)n(2n−1)!x2n−1​=−∑m=0∞​(−1)m(2m+1)!x2m+1​=−g(x).
(45)
The odd series similarly gives g′(x)=∑n=0∞​(−1)nx2n/(2n)!=f(x). Thus f′=−g,g′=f​ everywhere. By the product rule,
dxd​(f2+g2)=2ff′+2gg′=−2fg+2gf=0.
(46)
The mean value theorem implies that a differentiable function with zero derivative is constant on the real line. At zero, the series give f(0)=1 and g(0)=0. Consequently f(x)2+g(x)2=1 for every x∈R​. This derives the identity from the power series without assuming a trigonometric identity in advance.
