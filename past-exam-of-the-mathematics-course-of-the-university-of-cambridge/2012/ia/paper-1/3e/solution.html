A continuous function at x0​ satisfies the following quantified condition:
∀ε>0 ∃δ>0 ∀x∈R:∣x−x0​∣<δ ⟹ ∣f(x)−f(x0​)∣<ε.​
(7)
The choice of δ may depend on x0​ and ε.
For the bounded example, take
f(x)=(1−x)sin(1/x),0<x≤1.​
(8)
This is a continuous function on its domain and ∣f(x)∣≤1−x<1. Along xn​=(π/2+2πn)−1 its values tend to 1, while along yn​=(3π/2+2πn)−1 its values tend to −1. Hence the supremum is 1 and the infimum is −1, and neither is attained. “Upper and lower bound” here means the least upper and greatest lower bounds.
For the nonnegative function on the whole real line, if f≡0 the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise choose x∗​ with m=f(x∗​)>0. The limits at both infinities give R>∣x∗​∣ such that f(x)<m/2 whenever ∣x∣>R. By the extreme value theorem, a continuous function on the compact interval [−R,R] has a maximum M=f(α) there. Since M≥m, it also exceeds every value outside this interval. Thus f is bounded above and attains its global maximum. Nonnegativity ensures that either the function is zero or such a positive comparison value exists; without it, a function such as −e−x2 would have an unattained supremum of zero.
