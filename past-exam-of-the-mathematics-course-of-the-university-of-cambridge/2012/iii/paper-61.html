The first relation is vertical hydrostatic equilibrium. In a thin disc, the central object's gravity near the midplane is gz​=−Ω2z; the pressure gradient balances it. The second is an equation of state combining ideal gas pressure with isotropic radiation pressure. Write R=k/(μm​mp​), so pg​=RρT and pr​=4σT4/(3c); σ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant and μm​ the mean molecular weight in proton-mass units.
The third is local energy balance: the increase of upward radiative flux with height equals viscous dissipation. Here μ is dynamic viscosity, not mean molecular weight. A Keplerian disc has shear rdΩ/dr=−3Ω/2, so its heating rate is μ(rΩ′)2=9μΩ2/4. The fourth is optically thick radiative diffusion, with opacity per unit mass κ and diffusion directed down the temperature gradient. Midplane symmetry gives F(0)=0 and oppositely directed fluxes from the two faces. The four relations express vertical force balance, gas–radiation thermodynamics, local heating, and diffusive cooling.

Take the positive constant ratio λ=pr​/pg​. The equation of state implies
ρ=3cλR4σ​T3,p=(1+λ)RρT∝T4.
(1)
This is a polytropic equation of state of index three. Differentiating the pressure and using vertical hydrostatic equilibrium gives 4(1+λ)RρTz​=−ρΩ2z. Hence
T=T0​−8(1+λ)RΩ2z2​.
(2)
Define the semi-thickness H by T(H)=0 and put s=1−z2/H2. Then the constant gas-radiation ratio vertical disc structure is
T=T0​s,ρ=ρ0​s3,p=p0​s4,∣z∣<H,​
(3)
with
T0​=8(1+λ)RΩ2H2​,ρ0​=384cλ(1+λ)3R4σΩ6H6​,p0​=8ρ0​Ω2H2​=3072cλ(1+λ)3R4σΩ8H8​.​
(4)

420λ(1+λ)3Σ=(kμm​mp​​)4cσ​H7Ω6.​
(5)
All coefficients here are local in radial position; the derivation fixes the vertical profile rather than a global accretion rate. The zero-temperature surface is a formal boundary of this bulk model; optically thick diffusion ultimately fails in a thin photospheric layer.

For constant opacity, the ratio T3/ρ=3cλR/(4σ) cancels the height dependence in the radiative diffusion coefficient. Since Tz​=−Ω2z/[4(1+λ)R], the radiative flux is
F(z)=κc​1+λλ​Ω2z.​
(6)
Consequently Fz​=cλΩ2/[κ(1+λ)]. Equating this to the viscous heating rate gives
μ=9κ4c​1+λλ​,∣z∣<H.​
(7)
Thus the dynamic viscosity is uniform in height even though pressure and mass density decrease. Its units are mass per length per time, since c/κ has those units. A pointwise pressure-proportional alpha prescription would be incompatible with this exact profile, which is why the subsequent alpha relation is vertically averaged.

∫−HH​pdz=315256​p0​H=9ΣΩ2H2​.
(8)
The vertically averaged alpha closure for a mixed-pressure layer therefore gives
νˉ=9αH2Ω​.​
(9)
The height-integrated dynamic viscosity is 2Hμ=8cHλ/[9κ(1+λ)]. Equate it to νˉΣ to obtain
1+λλ​=8cαHΩκΣ​.​
(10)
These are integrated equalities, not an imposed pointwise relation μ(z)∝p(z).
Now keep α, the opacity and molecular constants independent of radius, and use Keplerian rotation Ω∝r−3/2. In the gas-dominated limit, λ≪1 but positive, the last equality gives λ∝HΩΣ. The column-density relation gives λΣ∝H7Ω6. Eliminating λ yields H6∝Σ2Ω−5, whence
νˉ∝Σ2/3Ω−2/3∝rΣ2/3(λ≪1).​
(11)
This is the gas-pressure branch of a mixed-pressure alpha disk. In the radiation-dominated limit, λ/(1+λ)→1, so H∝(ΩΣ)−1 and
νˉ∝Ω−1Σ−2∝r3/2Σ−2(λ≫1).​
(12)
For viscous stability of an accretion disk, linearize the Keplerian viscous diffusion equation at fixed radius: a local mass density perturbation has diffusion coefficient 3∂Σ​(νˉΣ). The gas branch has νˉΣ∝Σ5/3 and a positive derivative, so it smooths perturbations. The radiation branch has νˉΣ∝Σ−1 and a negative derivative, giving radiation-pressure viscous instability. The gas-pressure branch is viscously stable; the radiation-pressure branch is viscously unstable in this closure. This concerns radial mass-transport stability on wavelengths where the vertically averaged thin-disc model applies.

The boundary of a steady Kida vortex must be material. Put E=x2/b2+y2/a2. Requiring u⋅∇E=0 on the ellipse gives
2xy(b2α​−a2β​)=0,β=r2α.
(13)

α+β=S−ζ0​=r(r−1)S(r2+1)​.

(15)
For the usual positive orbital shear both are positive, the flow circulates clockwise and its streamlines have the same aspect ratio as the patch. The velocity coefficients in this question are unrelated to the alpha viscosity parameter in Question 1.

The steady incompressible shearing sheet equation is
u⋅∇u+2Ωez​×u=−ρ−1∇p+2ΩSxex​.

The final term follows from the local tidal potential −ΩSx2. For the given linear velocity, the advective acceleration is −αβ(xex​+yey​), while the Coriolis acceleration is 2Ωβxex​+2Ωαyey​. Equating components gives
ρpx​​=[αβ+2Ω(S−β)]x,ρpy​​=α(β−2Ω)y.
(17)
The cross derivatives vanish, so the pressure Hessian of an elliptical shearing-sheet vortex integrates to
ρp​=21​Ax2+21​By2+constant,A=(r−1)2S2​−r−12ΩS​,B=(r−1)2S2​−r(r−1)2ΩS​.​

A pressureless test particle feels tidal gravity, Coriolis acceleration and linear drag toward the prescribed gas velocity. Its planar equations are
x¨−2Ωy˙​=2ΩSx−γ(x˙−αy),y¨​+2Ωx˙=−γ(y˙​+βx).​
(19)
There is no gas-pressure force on this test particle. For (x,y)=(X,Y)eλt, the coefficient matrix is
(λ2+γλ−2ΩS2Ωλ+γβ​−2Ωλ−γαλ2+γλ​).
(20)
Nontrivial solutions require a zero determinant. Expansion gives
λ4+2γλ3+[2Ω(2Ω−S)+γ2]λ2+2Ω(α+β−S)γλ+αβγ2=0.​
(21)
Each root has a nonzero modal vector in the matrix kernel; real motions are obtained from conjugate pairs. Repeated roots may also supply polynomially weighted exponential solutions. Negative real parts make the centre linearly asymptotically attracting for trajectories governed by this interior linear flow.

Take every finite positive drag coefficient γ>0 and define
K=2Ω(2Ω−S),L=Ω(α+β−S),P=αβ.
(22)
The coefficients of the characteristic polynomial are c3​=2γ, c2​=K+γ2, c1​=2Lγ and c0​=Pγ2. The nontrivial Routh-Hurwitz criterion reduces exactly to
c3​c2​c1​−c32​c0​−c12​=4γ2[L(K−L)+γ2(L−P)]>0.
(23)
The printed strict chain K>L>P>0 makes every coefficient positive and both bracket terms positive, so it proves attraction for every positive drag coefficient.
There is, however, a boundary error in the asserted necessity. To have the bracket positive for every γ>0, its constant and slope as a function of γ2 must be nonnegative, and cannot both vanish. Together with coefficient positivity this gives the exact drag-polynomial stability for all positive stopping rates:
K≥L≥P>0,K>P.​
(24)
Necessity follows by taking arbitrarily small and arbitrarily large positive γ; if one bracket coefficient is negative, the inequality fails in the corresponding limit. Sufficiency follows because at least one coefficient is strictly positive. Thus K=L>P and K>L=P still give strict decay at each finite positive γ. They need not give a uniform stability margin in the zero- or infinite-drag limit.
In a Keplerian shearing sheet, S=3Ω/2 and
Ω2K​=1,Ω2L​=2r(r−1)3(r+1)​,Ω2P​=4(r−1)29​.
(25)
The differences are
Ω2K−L​=2r(r−1)(r−3)(2r+1)​,Ω2L−P​=4r(r−1)23(2r+1)(r−2)​.
(26)
Hence r>3 establishes the requested strict chain and trapping conclusion. At r=3, however, K=L=Ω2 and P=9Ω2/16, so
c3​c2​c1​−c32​c0​−c12​=47​Ω2γ4>0.
(27)
All coefficients remain positive. This is an explicit counterexample within the stated vortex model: r=3 also attracts particles for every finite positive drag coefficient, so the literal strict “if and only if” is false. The exact Keplerian all-positive-drag condition is r≥3. If γ=0 is included, the polynomial has zero and purely imaginary roots and there is no asymptotic attraction; “all drag coefficients” must mean positive drag. The local attraction claim presumes the particle remains in the patch, as is assured for sufficiently small perturbations of its centre.

Let Σ0​ be the uniform background surface density, u0​=−Sxey​, and write Σ=Σ0​+Σ′, u=u0​+v, Φd​=Φd0​+Φd′​. The background sheet has no horizontal gravitational gradient; its vertical field is irrelevant to the stated planar equations. Put D0​=∂t​−Sx∂y​. Keeping first-order terms gives
D0​Σ′+Σ0​(∂x​vx​+∂y​vy​)=0,​
(28)
D0​vx​−2Ωvy​=−∂x​(cs2​Σ0​Σ′​+Φd,m′​),D0​vy​+(2Ω−S)vx​=−∂y​(cs2​Σ0​Σ′​+Φd,m′​),​
(29)
∇2Φd′​=4πGΣ′δ(z),Φd,m′​=Φd′​(x,y,0,t).​
(30)
The −Svx​ contribution comes from v⋅∇u0​, while the Coriolis acceleration supplies the remaining coupling. The prescribed tidal potential has no perturbation, and its background force already balances the background Coriolis term. The isothermal pressure linearizes to cs2​∇Σ′/Σ0​.

Apply the shearing-wave ansatz to each disturbance. Acting on its phase gives
D0​ei[kx​(t)x+ky​(t)y]=i[(k˙x​−Sky​)x+k˙y​y]eik⋅x.
(31)
Thus a spatially uniform amplitude system exists when
k˙=Sky​ex​,ky​=constant,kx​(t)=kx​(0)+Sky​t.​
(32)
For a nonzero horizontal wavenumber k=∣k∣, the razor-thin disk Poisson kernel follows from (∂z2​−k2)Φd′​=4πGΣ′δ(z). The potential decays above and below the sheet as Φd,m′​e−k∣z∣. Integrating across the midplane gives the jump −2kΦd,m′​=4πGΣ′, hence
Φd,m′​=−k2πG​Σ′.
(33)
Substitute this potential and the phase evolution into the linearized equations. The barotropic shearing-wave gravitational amplitude system is
Σ˙′v˙x​−2Ωvy​v˙y​+(2Ω−S)vx​​=−iΣ0​(kx​vx​+ky​vy​),=−ikx​(cs2​−k2πGΣ0​​)Σ0​Σ′​,=−iky​(cs2​−k2πGΣ0​​)Σ0​Σ′​.​​

The self-gravity sign is attractive and opposes the pressure response. The uniform k=0 mass density shift requires its own gravitational reference and is not obtained by dividing by k.

Write B=2Ω−S, A(t)=cs2​−2πGΣ0​/k(t), and w=ikx​vy​−iky​vx​. Differentiation, including k˙x​=Sky​, gives
w˙=iSky​vy​+ikx​(−Bvx​−iky​AΣ′/Σ0​)−iky​(2Ωvy​−ikx​AΣ′/Σ0​)=−iB(kx​vx​+ky​vy​).
(35)
The two pressure/self-gravity cross terms cancel. Continuity gives Σ˙′=−iΣ0​(kx​vx​+ky​vy​), so w˙=BΣ˙′/Σ0​. Consequently
dtd​[Σ0​ikx​vy​−iky​vx​​−Σ02​(2Ω−S)Σ′​]=0.​
(36)
The conserved amplitude is linearized vortensity: the perturbation of the absolute vertical vorticity divided by surface density,
q=Σ2Ω+∂x​uy​−∂y​ux​​,q0​=Σ0​2Ω−S​.
(37)
A two-dimensional inviscid barotropic fluid materially conserves this potential vorticity. Both pressure enthalpy and gravitational forces are gradients, so they create no baroclinic vorticity source. Since q0​ is uniform, its linear perturbation obeys D0​q′=0, explaining why a shearing wave has constant vortensity amplitude.

For ky​=0 and nonzero kx​, the wavenumber is constant. Continuity gives Σ˙′=−ikx​Σ0​vx​, and the conserved vortensity relation gives
ikx​vy​=Σ0​q​′+Σ0​(2Ω−S)Σ′​.
(38)
Differentiate continuity and substitute radial momentum and this identity. The forced axisymmetric density mode with vortensity obeys
Σ¨′+ωk2​Σ′=−2ΩΣ02​q​′,ωk2​=cs2​kx2​−2πGΣ0​∣kx​∣+κ2,κ2=2Ω(2Ω−S).​
(39)
The constant forcing represents the stationary vortical or balanced component, which is lost if every mode is assumed to have nonzero frequency. Let k∗​=πGΣ0​/cs2​=κ/(Qcs​). Completing the square gives
ωk2​=cs2​(∣kx​∣−k∗​)2+κ2(1−Q−2).
(40)
For Q>1, every nonzero radial wavenumber has positive ωk2​. The solution is a constant balanced offset −2ΩΣ02​q​′/ωk2​ plus bounded sine and cosine oscillations. Pressure stabilizes short wavelengths, rotation stabilizes long wavelengths, and self-gravity is strongest at intermediate wavelengths.
For Q<1, the Toomre stability criterion fails in the band
k∗​(1−1−Q2​)<∣kx​∣<k∗​(1+1−Q2​).​
(41)
There ωk2​<0 and the homogeneous solutions grow or decay exponentially. The fastest rate is smax​=κQ−2−1​ at ∣kx​∣=k∗​. Outside the band the modes oscillate; at its edges the frequency vanishes.
At Q=1, ωk2​≥0 and it vanishes only at ∣kx​∣=κ/cs​. At that critical wavenumber,
Σ′(t)=C0​+C1​t−ΩΣ02​q​′t2.​
(42)
This Toomre marginal algebraic growth is spectrally marginal, not bounded for all data: a nonzero conserved vortensity gives quadratic secular growth, and even zero vortensity can allow the linear term. Other wavenumbers still oscillate about a balanced offset. Q>1 gives bounded axisymmetric modes, Q<1 allows exponential instability, and Q=1 has a zero-frequency marginal mode with possible algebraic growth. These statements are axisymmetric; nonaxisymmetric shearing disturbances can have transient swing behavior. For kx​=ky​=0, continuity instead fixes the uniform mass density shift, while the velocities execute epicyclic motion; no 1/k gravitational formula is used.
