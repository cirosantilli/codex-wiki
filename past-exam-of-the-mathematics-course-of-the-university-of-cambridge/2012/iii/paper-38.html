Use the one-sided convention: integrating the one-sided spectral density over [0,π] gives the variance. A standard sufficient hypothesis is absolute summability of the autocovariance sequence:
γ0​+2k=1∑∞​∣γk​∣<∞.​
(1)
For a real weakly stationary process, extend the sequence by γ−k​=γk​. The Fourier pair is
fX​(ω)=π1​k∈Z∑​γk​eikω=π1​(γ0​+2k≥1∑​γk​cos(kω)),γk​=∫0π​fX​(ω)cos(kω)dω.​
(2)
Absolute summability makes the series uniformly convergent and continuous; cosine orthogonality gives the inverse relation. Nonnegativity follows from the spectral measure of a stationary time series, or from the nonnegative Fejér approximations obtained by taking variances of finite Fourier sums.
For precision, absolute summability is sufficient, not necessary. The exact general condition is that the spectral measure be absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. For example, a density constant on [0,π/2] and zero elsewhere has γk​ proportional to sin(kπ/2)/k, which is not absolutely summable. Thus the displayed absolutely convergent Fourier formula answers the usual covariance-summability version; an arbitrary density need only have the integral inverse relation and Fejér-mean Fourier recovery in L1.

The series defining the linear filter of a stationary time series converges in L2, because the norm of a tail is at most ∥X0​∥2​ times the corresponding tail sum of ∣as​∣. Its mean is the original mean times ∑s​as​. Its covariance is time independent and equals
γkY​=∑s,u∈Z​as​au​γk−s+u​.
(3)
Under the summability assumption in (i),
∑k​∣γkY​∣≤(∑s​∣as​∣)2∑k​∣γk​∣<∞.
(4)
We may therefore interchange all the sums. Setting h=k−s+u in the Fourier series gives
fY​(ω)​=π1​s,u∑​as​au​ei(s−u)ωh∑​γh​eihω=(s∑​as​eisω)(u∑​au​e−iuω)fX​(ω).​
(5)
The second factor is the conjugate of the first because the coefficients are real. Hence
fY​(ω)=∣α(ω)∣2fX​(ω).​
(6)
This spectral density transformation under a linear filter also holds whenever the original spectral measure has a density, without covariance summability: insert its integral representation into the covariance double sum, justified by absolute summability of the filter coefficients.

In the usual causal stationary convention, a centered autoregressive moving-average model satisfies
Xt​−∑j=1p​ϕj​Xt−j​=εt​+∑j=1q​θj​εt−j​,εt​∼WN(0,σ2).
(7)
Set Φ(z)=1−∑j=1p​ϕj​zj and Θ(z)=1+∑j=1q​θj​zj. After removal of common factors, the usual causality condition is that all zeros of Φ lie outside the closed unit disk. Then Θ(z)/Φ(z) has an absolutely summable causal coefficient sequence, giving a linear-filter representation of X in terms of white noise. Invertibility of the moving-average part is a separate issue and is not needed for its covariance spectrum.
White noise has one-sided density σ2/π. Applying the filtering result, or equating the spectra of the two filtered sides of the defining equation, gives
fX​(ω)=πσ2​​1−∑j=1p​ϕj​eijω​2​1+∑j=1q​θj​eijω​2​.​
(8)
The moving-average sign agrees with the positive θ convention used later. More generally, the same quotient applies to a stationary two-sided solution when the reduced denominator has no unit-circle zeros; causality selects the past-dependent realization. This is the spectral density of an ARMA process.

1−ϕB1+θB​=1+∑j≥1​(ϕ+θ)ϕj−1Bj.

The autocovariance of an MA(1) process comes directly from shared noise terms:
γ0​=σ2(1+θ2),γ1​=γ−1​=σ2θ,γk​=0(∣k∣≥2).​
(14)
Work with the zero-mean white noise convention and σ2>0. Put Ht−1​=span(X1​,…,Xt−1​). Suppose the preceding finite-past innovations U1​,…,Ut−1​ are mutually orthogonal. Their triangular relation to the observations means that they span the same finite past. For s≤t−2, Us​ is a linear combination of X1​,…,Xs​, so Cov(Xt​,Us​)=0. Moreover,
Cov(Xt​,Ut−1​)=Cov(Xt​,Xt−1​−λt−1​Ut−2​)=γ1​.
(15)
Projection onto this orthogonal basis therefore has only its last coefficient nonzero:
λt​=φt−1​γ1​​,Xt​=λt​Ut−1​,Ut​=Xt​−Xt​.​
(16)
The residual is orthogonal to the entire preceding span, proving the induction. Expanding its variance gives
φt​=γ0​−2λt​γ1​+λt2​φt−1​=γ0​−γ1​λt​.​
(17)
The initialization is U1​=X1​, φ1​=γ0​, λ1​=0 and U0​=0. Each finite covariance matrix is a positive-definite matrix: the last observation in any nonzero finite linear combination contains a noise term absent from earlier observations. Thus all the projection denominators are positive. These are the finite-sample innovations of an MA(1) process, requiring no Gaussian assumption.
Substituting the covariances into the recursion gives
λt​=1+θ2−θλt−1​θ​.
(18)
The denominator has a positive limit when λt−1​→λ∈[−1,1]. Taking limits gives (λ−θ)(θλ−1)=0. The permitted interval selects
λ={θ,1/θ,​∣θ∣≤1,∣θ∣>1.​​
(19)
For θ=0, every coefficient is zero; at θ=±1, the roots coincide. This is the limiting MA(1) innovations coefficient. The limiting innovation variance is correspondingly σ2max(1,θ2).
For the observed numerical case, γ0​=2 and γ1​=−1. The exact innovation calculations are
λ2​=−21​,U1​=−1013​,U2​=54​−(−21​)(−1013​)=203​,φ2​=23​,λ3​=−32​.
(20)
Therefore the predictor of X3​ based on the two observations and its error variance are
X3∣2​=−101​,Var(X3​−X3∣2​)=34​.​
(21)
For X4​, the available information is still only H2​. Since its covariances with both X1​ and X2​ are zero, its orthogonal projection onto that span is zero:
X4∣2​=0,Var(X4​−X4∣2​)=2.​
(22)
This is two-step prediction for an MA(1) process. Applying the next one-step recursion would instead use an observed X3​, which is not available here.

To generate a chi-squared variable with one degree of freedom by the Marsaglia polar method, repeatedly draw independent V,W uniformly on (−1,1) until 0<S=V2+W2<1. Return
Z=VS−2logS​​,Y=Z2.
(23)
The accepted point is uniform on the unit disk; its squared radius is uniform on (0,1) and independent of angle. The radial rescaling gives a standard normal coordinate Z, hence Y∼χ12​. The same accepted pair also supplies another independent standard normal coordinate, if needed. The proof stages below follow the four printed sampler labels.

Squaring a variable with the standard normal distribution gives the required chi-squared distribution. Its two inverse images at y>0 are ±y​, so the change-of-variable formula gives
g(y)=2πy​exp(−y/2)​.
(24)
Draw the branch-selection uniform variable independently of the randomness used for the normal draw. Then the pair (Y,U) has the product law: conditioning on Y leaves U uniform. This independence is exactly what justifies interpreting the subsequent threshold as the conditional probability of selecting the smaller root.

x±​(y)=μ(1+t±t(t+2)​),x−​(y)x+​(y)=μ2.​

For 0<x<μ, only the smaller-root branch can produce a value near x. Conditional on Y=h(x), its selection probability is μ/(μ+x). The output density on that interval is therefore
p−​(x)=μ+xμ​g(h(x))∣h′(x)∣.
(28)
Use
h(x)​=μx​λ​∣x−μ∣​,∣h′(x)∣=μ2x2λ∣x−μ∣(x+μ)​.

For x>μ, the corresponding smaller root is μ2/x. The conditional probability of selecting the larger root is
1−μ+μ2/xμ​=μ+xμ​.

The algorithm always selects one of its positive roots, so these branch densities exhaust its probability distribution.

A Gibbs sampler updates one coordinate by drawing from its full conditional distribution, leaving the others unchanged and using their current values. Each coordinate kernel Ki​ preserves the target joint law P: integrate first over the unchanged coordinates, then over the old coordinate, and finally draw the new coordinate from the same conditional distribution. The resulting joint law is again P. In the discrete notation, if two states share the remaining coordinates,
P(x)Ki​(x,x′)=P−i​(x−i​)P(xi​∣x−i​)P(xi′​∣x−i​),
(35)
which is symmetric in xi​,xi′​. Thus a coordinate update satisfies detailed balance. A systematic full sweep has kernel K1​⋯Kk​ and remains invariant because each factor preserves P. A fixed-probability random-scan mixture also preserves P; the mixture is reversible, whereas a systematic composition need not be.
In the autologistic binary-image model, count each neighboring pair once. Changing one pixel from zero to one raises n1​ by one and n2​ by the number zij​ of neighboring ones. The ratio of its two conditional weights is exp(α+βzij​). Therefore
pij​=1+eα+βzij​eα+βzij​​=logistic(α+βzij​).​
(36)
For single-site Gibbs sampling, select a pixel, compute its current neighbor sum and replace it by a draw from a Bernoulli distribution with this probability. One may use random scans or complete ordered sweeps. The normalizing constant is not needed. For finite α,β, every conditional probability lies strictly between zero and one; the finite chain is irreducible and has self-transitions, so its stationary law is unique and iterating converges to it.
For checkerboard Gibbs sampling, no edge joins two black pixels or two white pixels. Conditional on the whites, the black-pixel law factorizes:
P(B=b∣W=w)=∏v∈B​pv​(w)bv​(1−pv​(w))1−bv​.
(37)
Draw all black pixels independently with these probabilities, then draw all white pixels independently conditional on the newly drawn blacks. Each color block is a full conditional update, so the two-block sweep preserves the joint target. Pixels of the same color can be updated together because their conditional distribution factorizes.
For the noisy image, the log likelihood contributes −21​∑v​(yv​−xv​)2. Since xv2​=xv​, dropping terms independent of x gives
P(x∣y) ∝ exp{∑v​(α+yv​−21​)xv​+β∑{v,w} edge​xv​xw​}.
(38)
The Gaussian-noise posterior for an autologistic image therefore has conditional probabilities
P(Xij​=1∣X−(ij)​,Y=y)=logistic(α+βzij​+yij​−21​).​
(39)
Use this probability in both single-pixel and sequential color-block schemes. The independent observation factors change only the local field, so conditional independence within each color is retained. The same invariance proof then targets the posterior distribution rather than the prior.
