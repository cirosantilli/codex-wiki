For two incompressible Stokes flows (u,σ) and (u,σ) with the same viscosity and no body forces, the Lorentz reciprocal theorem for Stokes flow states
∫∂D​u⋅σndS=∫∂D​u⋅σndS.
(1)
Indeed the divergence of their difference is zero: stress divergences vanish, pressure contracts with zero velocity divergence, and the two symmetric strain-rate contractions cancel.
Apply it to the decaying disturbance u′=u−u∞​ around the sphere and an auxiliary translating sphere of velocity V. On the body surface, u′=U+Ωs​×x−u∞​. Using normals from the body into the fluid, the given auxiliary traction is −3μV/(2a); reversing both normal conventions does not change the reciprocal equality. The background traction integrates to zero because it is regular inside the sphere. The disturbance's hydrodynamic force is consequently FH​=−F, where F is the externally applied force. The rotational term integrates to zero. Thus for arbitrary V,
−2a3μ​V⋅(4πa2U−∫Sa​​u∞​dS)=−V⋅F.
(2)
Therefore U=F/(6πμa)+⟨u∞​⟩Sa​​.
The background Stokes equations imply ∇2p∞​=0 and ∇4u∞​=0. The spherical mean of a biharmonic function gives exactly ⟨u∞​⟩=u∞​+(a2/6)∇2u∞​, evaluated at the sphere center. One can obtain it from Taylor expansion and ⟨xi​xj​⟩=a2δij​/3; every subsequent spherical average contains ∇4 and vanishes. This requires the background to be regular throughout the enclosed ball. Hence the Faxén relation is
U=6πμaF​+(1+6a2​∇2)u∞​​x=xc​​.​
(3)
The printed denominator 6πμ omits a. The given traction integrates to −6πμaV, fixing that factor; the printed force term also fails dimensional consistency unless its force were redefined, which the question does not do.

Use the Papkovich–Neuber representation in the convention
u=Φ−21​∇(x⋅Φ+χ),p=−μ∇⋅Φ,∇2Φ=0,∇2χ=0.
(4)
It gives ∇⋅u=0 and μ∇2u=−μ∇(∇⋅Φ)=∇p. Additive pressure constants are immaterial.
Let q=x⋅Ex, with E symmetric and traceless. The harmonic vector Ex/r3 is a derivative of 1/r. The scalar q/r5 is a decaying degree-two harmonic because q is a harmonic homogeneous quadratic. These Papkovich potentials for a strained sphere have precisely the strain symmetry and decay required for the disturbance, so try
Φ=Ar3Ex​,χ=Br5q​.
(5)
Substitution gives
u′=23A​r5qx​−Br5Ex​+25B​r7qx​.
(6)
At r=a, require u′=−Ex. The independent vector structures give B=a5 and A=−5a3/3. The disturbance for a sphere in a uniform straining Stokes flow is
u′=−r5a5​Ex−2r55a3​(1−r2a2​)qx,p′=−5μa3r5q​.​
(7)
It decays at infinity and satisfies no slip exactly at the sphere. Its leading far field is a stresslet, of velocity scale Ea3/r2.
A force-free sphere in a linear background translates with U0​, by Faxén's first law, and a couple-free sphere rotates with the background rigid-body rotation Ω, by Faxén's rotational law. Uniform translation and solid-body rotation can then be matched without disturbance. The remaining relative boundary velocity is exactly −Ex. The Linearity of Stokes flow and Uniqueness of Stokes flow therefore give the same disturbance. The pure strain produces no resultant force or couple, by its symmetric degree-two angular structure.

The shear has Exy​=Eyx​=C/2. The separation between centers is 2(X,Y,0), so in the leading stresslet from part b, q=4CXY, r=2R and the radial vector's y component is 2Y. Evaluating the other sphere's disturbance at the positive center gives
Y˙=−85Ca3​R5XY2​+O(Ca5/R4).​
(8)
This is the first method of reflections for Stokes flow approximation. The next multipole in part b and the receiver's Faxén Laplacian contribute at Ca5/R4; changes to the incident strain from further reflections are smaller, Ca6/R5. The hypothesis Y∞​≫a ensures well-separated spheres throughout the encounter.
The leading horizontal velocity is X˙=CY+O(Ca3/R2). Dividing and using Y∞​ on the right at first order gives
dXdY​=−85a3Y∞​​(X2+Y∞2​)5/2X​+higher orders.
(9)
Integrating from negative infinity,
Y(X)=Y∞​+24(X2+Y∞2​)3/25a3Y∞​​+O(a5/Y∞4​).
(10)
The correction increases until X=0 and decreases afterward. Thus
Ym​=Y∞​(1+24Y∞3​5a3​+O(a5/Y∞5​)).​
(11)
The expansion gives Y→Y∞​ as X→+∞. More generally, reversible scattering of two spheres in simple shear gives no net transverse shift for this smooth, noncontact, deterministic two-sphere encounter: reflection X↦−X reverses the imposed shear, and time reversal restores it. Uniqueness makes the incoming and outgoing branches mirror images. Brownian motion, contact forces or finite inertia would remove the assumptions behind this conclusion.

Take pressure positive in compression, outward normal on the upper interface, and curvature κ≃−hxx​. The external-minus-internal traction convention gives the base pressure p0​=V/h03​. For the symmetric Fourier perturbation, the linearized upper-surface conditions are
v(h0​)=sη,μ(uy​+vx​)(h0​)=0,−p′+2μvy​=(h04​3V​−γk2)η.​
(12)
Isotropic base stress cancels all terms due merely to rotating the normal. Velocity u is even and v odd in y.
Use the same Papkovich–Neuber representation as in Q1, with Φ=(0,ϕ) and symmetric harmonic potentials
ϕ=Asinh(ky)eikx,χ=Bcosh(ky)eikx.
(13)
Suppress the common exponential. They give
u=−2ik​(Aysinhky+Bcoshky),v=2A​(sinhky−kycoshky)−2kB​sinhky,p′=−μkAcoshky.
(14)
The zero tangential stress is Ah0​coshK+BsinhK=0, where K=kh0​>0. Hence B=−Ah0​cothK, v(h0​)=AsinhK/2 and the normal stress becomes μkA(coshK+K/sinhK). Eliminating A using the kinematic condition gives the Van der Waals rupture instability of a viscous sheet rate
s=μh03​3V​K(2K+sinh2K)(1−ΓK2)sinh2K​,Γ=3Vγh02​​.​
(15)
Attraction destabilizes thickness variations; curvature pressure opposes them.
For the clean-sheet long-wave rupture plateau, writing s0​=3V/(μh03​), the rate is s/s0​→1/4. Without capillarity, the curve decreases toward 1/(2K) at large K; with Γ=1 it changes sign at K=1 and behaves as −K/2. Thus short waves are weakly unstable without surface tension, but stabilized by it. The largest clean-sheet rate is approached at long wavelength. The strictly uniform k=0 thickness change is not an admissible fixed-volume Fourier mode; this is the limit of nonzero waves. Stress-free surfaces permit extensional, nearly plug-like motion whose mobility compensates the weak long-wave pressure gradient. Unsteady inertia eventually matters in that limit; finite sheet extent, drainage, gravity, surface elasticity/viscosity, surfactant transport and molecular-scale corrections can also modify wavelength selection.
For the comparison with surfactant, keep the same equilibrium tension γ0​. Flow away from a thinning neck stretches the surface, diluting its surfactant and raising tension there. Convergence into thicker regions concentrates surfactant and lowers tension. The resulting Marangoni stress points back toward the neck, opposing the draining surface motion. It therefore reduces mobility and the rupture rate relative to the clean constant-γ0​ sheet. This mechanism needs surface transport to sustain concentration gradients; instantaneous concentration equilibration would remove it.
For the supplied strong-surfactant rate, unstable waves satisfy K<Γ−1/2. When Γ≫1, all of them have K≪1. Expanding its mobility factor gives
4Kcosh2Ksinh2K−2K​=3K2​+O(K4),s=3s0​​K2(1−ΓK2)[1+O(K2)].
(16)
Maximizing in K2 yields the strong-surfactant long-wave rupture maximum
Km2​=2Γ1​[1+O(Γ−1)],smax​=12Γs0​​[1+O(Γ−1)]=4μγh05​3V2​[1+O(Γ−1)].​
(17)
Here γ is the equilibrium tension γ0​ in the soap-film comparison. Surface immobilization removes the clean long-wave plateau and selects a finite long wavelength. The growth timescale is lengthened by a factor about 3Γ compared with the clean maximum. A linear rupture-time estimate is smax−1​log(h0​/∣ηinitial​∣), not a universal bubble lifetime: drainage and changing thickness, initial defects and nonlinear rupture also matter.

For a slender viscous thread, leading axial velocity is uniform over a section. Incompressibility gives ur​=−rwz​/2. The radial and azimuthal strain rates are −wz​/2, and the axial one is wz​. The Newtonian fluid stress tensor then has σrr​=−p−μwz​. Matching the inviscid exterior normal stress requires p=pa​−μwz​, so σzz​=−pa​+3μwz​. The coefficient three is the Trouton ratio.
A short section has end-stress resultant ∂z​(Aσzz​)dz. The side's hydrostatic traction adds pa​Az​dz, and gravity adds ρgAdz. Neglecting inertia and capillarity, their sum is zero:
3μ(Awz​)z​−A(pa​)z​+ρgA=0.
(18)
Since (pa​)z​=ρa​g, the balances are
A3μ​(Awz​)z​+(ρ−ρa​)g=0,At​+(Aw)z​=0.​
(19)
The second is cross-sectional volume conservation, equivalently Dt​A=−Awz​. Small interface slope justifies the sectionwise extensional approximation; the slow-flow assumption is additionally needed to neglect inertia.

With no net body force, the tensile resultant is constant along the thread: 3μAwz​=F(t). In material coordinates z0​, volume conservation gives Dt​A=−F/(3μ). Thus
A=A0​(z0​)−Δ(t),Δ(t)=3μ1​∫0t​F(t′)dt′.​
(22)
The material Jacobian satisfies A∂z/∂z0​=A0​. Integrating with z(0,t)=0 gives
z=z0​+∫0z0​​A0​(z0′​)−ΔΔdz0′​​.​
(23)
This remains valid while Δ<minA0​. The even profile now specified ensures symmetric endpoints ±L(t).
For A0​=C(1+k2z02​) and k=0, direct integration yields
L=L0​+∣k∣C(C−Δ)​Δ​tan−1(∣k∣L0​C−ΔC​​).
(24)
For k=0, instead L=CL0​/(C−Δ). A constant tensile F>0 gives Δ=Ft/(3μ) and the finite blow-up time
t∗=F3μC​.​
(25)
Put α=F/(3μ) and δ=C−Δ=α(t∗−t). For fixed nonzero k, the arctangent tends to π/2, whereas for the uniform profile the whole length has the same thinning. Consequently the localized-neck and uniform-thread stretching exponents are
k=0:k=0:​β=−21​,B=2∣k∣π​F3μC​​,β=−1,B=F3μCL0​​.​​
(26)
A nonuniform thread's divergent integral is concentrated in a material interval ∣z0​∣∼δ/C​/∣k∣ near its quadratic minimum. Its width shrinks like δ1/2 while the Jacobian grows like δ−1, yielding δ−1/2. The remote ends no longer affect this leading coefficient, explaining independence from L0​. For k=0 every element contributes the same δ−1 stretch. The limits k→0 and t→t∗ do not commute.

Take positive Ω counterclockwise in the (x,y) plane and put W=Ωa, d=ϵa. The bottom cylinder surface moves locally at W; the wall moves at −U. In the lubrication approximation,
h=d+2ax2​=d(1+ξ2),ξ=ℓx​,ℓ=2ad​.
(27)
The leading equations are py​=0, μuyy​=px​ and incompressibility. No slip gives the Couette-Poiseuille flow in a thin gap
u=−U+hU+W​y+2μpx​​y(y−h),q=2h​(W−U)−12μh3px​​.​
(28)
The steady gap flux is independent of x. Equal leading pressures at the two matched outer ends require ∫px​dx=0. Let Jn​=∫h−ndx=ℓd−nIn​. Then J2​/J3​=4d/3, and q=32​d(W−U)​. The velocity field and flux determine the normal velocity by incompressibility and no penetration.
At the wall and cylinder, respectively,
μτ0​​=h4U−2W​+h26q​,μτh​​=h4W−2U​−h26q​.​
(29)
Here τ=σxy​; the fluid force on the cylinder has the opposite traction sign from that on the wall. Since J2​=J1​/(2d),
Fwallshear​=2μUJ1​,Fcylindershear​=−2μWJ1​,J1​=πd2a​​.​
(30)
The shear and pressure force partition in cylinder lubrication explains why these two shear forces need not balance: pressure on the sloping cylinder surface supplies an equally important tangential component. Its contribution is
Fcylinderp​=−∫ph′dx=∫hpx​dx=2μ(W−U)J1​.
(31)
Boundary terms vanish under outer matching. Thus the total cylinder force is −2μUJ1​, and it balances the wall force once pressure is included. Pressure acts normally through the circular cylinder's center and gives no torque; leading shear torque is aFcylindershear​=−2μΩa2J1​.
The effective weight per unit length is +πa2Δρg in the falling direction, with zero applied couple. Force and couple balance give U=Δρga2ϵ​/(22​μ),Ω=0 at leading order​. Finite outer-region torque can generate smaller rotation; the leading singular gap resistance is what fixes this result.
For Ω=0, q=−2dU/3 and
px​=d22μU​(1+ξ2)31−3ξ2​,p=d22μUℓ​(1+ξ2)2ξ​,τh​=d2μU​(1+ξ2)21−ξ2​.
(32)
The pressure is odd, negative for x<0 and positive for x>0 with the wall moving toward decreasing x, and tends to the common reference pressure at either end. The cylinder shear changes sign at x=±ℓ=±a2ϵ​​. For ζ=y/h, the velocity is
u/U=(1−ζ)[−1+(3−1+ξ24​)ζ].
(33)
Flow is entirely wall-directed near the neck, while a reversed layer next to the cylinder exists for ∣ξ∣>1. These changes are the separation and reattachment points shown in the stream-function sketch.
For the final Couette flow, the printed center (λϵa,0) is an offset parallel to the walls. Translational invariance removes it; the centered two-gap symmetry gives the literal answer V=0​ for every λ. A dependence on λ requires a transverse position, evidently the intended (0,λϵa). With that repaired interpretation, the lower and upper gaps are db​=ϵa(1+λ) and dt​=ϵa(1−λ).
In the translating cylinder frame the wall velocities are −U−V and U−V. Each leading tangential drag is 2μJ1​ times its wall velocity, independent of the rotation. Hence zero net force requires (U−V)Jt​−(U+V)Jb​=0, where Jt,b​=π2a/dt,b​​. For the intended transverse offset,
V=U1+λ​+1−λ​1+λ​−1−λ​​=U1+1−λ2​λ​.​
(34)
This formula assumes both gaps remain lubrication gaps, with fixed ∣λ∣<1. It gives V=0 at λ=0 and motion toward the faster nearby wall otherwise.
At λ=0, the lower-gap flow is the previous −U flow and the upper gap its reversed mirror, with fluxes ∓2dU/3. Their leading translational shear torques vanish. Any finite outer-fluid Couette torque is O(μUa), while the sum of the gap rotation torques is O(μΩa2/ϵ​). Balancing them explains Ω=O(ϵ​U/a)​. Leading gap analysis alone does not determine its prefactor.
