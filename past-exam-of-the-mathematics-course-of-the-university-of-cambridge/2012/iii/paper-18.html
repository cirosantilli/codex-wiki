Let E=C/Λ, and let Λ′ be the inverse image of the cyclic subgroup G under the quotient map. Then Λ⊂Λ′ is an inclusion of Euclidean lattices with Λ′/Λ≅Z/NZ. The Smith normal form supplies a basis u,v of Λ′ for which
Λ=Z(Nu)+Zv.
(1)
Choose the orientation of this basis so that Im(v/(Nu))>0. Dividing the coordinate on C by Nu gives an isomorphism of complex tori
E≅C/(Z+Zτ),τ=Nuv​.
(2)
Here u maps to 1/N, and its class generates Λ′/Λ. Thus the isomorphism carries G to the required cyclic subgroup. Every pair is represented: Φ is surjective. This argument avoids assuming that an arbitrary integer lift of a primitive vector modulo N is itself primitive.

Suppose τ1​=(aτ2​+b)/(cτ2​+d), with ad−bc=1, and put α=(cτ2​+d)−1. The identities
α=a−cτ1​,ατ2​=dτ1​−b
(3)
show that multiplication by α takes one period lattice isomorphically to the other: its basis matrix has determinant ad−bc=1. If N∣c, the image of 1/N is congruent to a/N modulo the target period lattice. Moreover ad≡1(modN), so a is a unit modulo N. The image therefore generates the same cyclic subgroup.
Conversely, a holomorphic group isomorphism of complex tori lifts to z↦αz, with αΛτ2​​=Λτ1​​. Indeed its lift fixing zero is additive: its failure to be additive is a continuous lattice-valued function, hence zero. An additive holomorphic map is complex linear. Write
α=a−cτ1​,ατ2​=−b+dτ1​.
(4)
The corresponding integral basis matrix has determinant 1: complex multiplication preserves the real orientation, and both bases (1,τj​) are positively oriented. Solving gives τ1​=(aτ2​+b)/(cτ2​+d). Preservation of the cyclic subgroup means α/N≡u/N(modΛτ1​​) for some unit u modulo N. Comparing the two real basis coefficients gives N∣c. Thus the matrix lies in the Gamma 0 congruence subgroup, and the classification is
E≅Γ0​(N)\H.​
(5)
This is the noncompact modular curve classification; no cusp points have been added.

Use the convention that a Hermitian form is complex linear in its first argument. In the integral Hermitian forms on the square complex torus calculation, every Hermitian form on C has the form Ht​(z,w)=tzw, where t=Ht​(1,1) is real. For λ=m+ni and μ=r+si in the period lattice,
Et​(λ,μ)=ImHt​(λ,μ)=t(nr−ms).
(6)
Consequently integrality holds precisely when t∈Z: necessity follows from Et​(1,i)=−t, and sufficiency follows from the displayed formula. Addition of Hermitian forms corresponds to addition of t. Positivity is equivalent to t>0, since Ht​(z,z)=t∣z∣2. The answer is
Ht​(z,w)=tzw(t∈Z),Ht​>0⟺t>0.​
(7)
With the conjugate-linear-first convention the formula is tzw and the sign of Et​ reverses. The positive integer parameter is unchanged.

For the intended complex unit circle, put u=α(1) and v=α(i), with ∣u∣=∣v∣=1. The complete family of semicharacters of a complex lattice is
α(m+ni)=(−1)mnumvn.​
(8)
To obtain this, the identity at zero gives α(0)=1; on each coordinate axis the alternating form vanishes, so α(m)=um and α(ni)=vn, also for negative integers. Combining the axes gives the factor (−1)mn. Conversely, for λ=m+ni, μ=r+si, the quotient of the proposed values is (−1)ms+nr, which equals eiπ(nr−ms). Hence every displayed formula is a semicharacter of a complex lattice and none are missing.
The original PDF actually puts z∈Z in its definition of C1​. Under that literal reading C1​={1,−1}, so there are exactly four solutions, given by the same formula with u,v∈{1,−1}. Under either reading the specified choice is α1​(m+ni)=(−1)mn.

The Appell–Humbert theorem identifies the positive Hermitian form H1​ with a holomorphic line bundle whose First Chern class is represented, in our linear-first convention, by
ω=2i​dz∧dz=dx∧dy.
(9)
Its integral over the square fundamental domain is 1, so degL=1. In particular the oriented Chern pairing is −E1​(1,i)=1; using E1​(1,i) without adjusting the Hermitian convention would give the wrong sign.
The complex torus has a nowhere-vanishing differential dz, so its canonical divisor is trivial and its genus is one. A nonzero section of a line bundle of negative degree would have an effective divisor of negative degree, which is impossible. Thus H0(X,L−1)=0. The Riemann-Roch theorem and Serre duality give
h0(X,L)−h0(X,L−1)=degL=1.
(10)
Therefore h0(X,L)=1​. The choice of semicharacter of a complex lattice changes the degree-zero component of the line bundle, but not this calculation.

A holomorphic group endomorphism of the complex torus lifts to multiplication by a scalar c∈C, as in Question 1. It descends exactly when cΛ⊆Λ. Applying this condition to 1 gives c∈Z[i]. Conversely any Gaussian integer c preserves Z[i], since the latter is a ring. Addition of endomorphisms is addition of scalars, and composition is multiplication of scalars. Thus, as rings,
End(X)≅Z[i].​
(11)
The wording “endomorphism algebra” here denotes this integral endomorphism ring of an elliptic curve. If it instead meant the rational algebra End0(X)=End(X)⊗Z​Q, that algebra would be Q(i).

Pulling back the factors of automorphy in the Appell–Humbert theorem by z↦cz, where c=a+bi, pulls back both the Hermitian form and the semicharacter of a complex lattice. Thus the explicit answer is
H(z,w)=(a2+b2)zw,α(λ)=α1​((a+bi)λ).​
(12)
Writing λ=m+ni makes the second formula completely explicit:
α(m+ni)=(−1)(am−bn)(bm+an)=(−1)ab(m+n)+(a2+b2)mn.
(13)
The second equality is an equality of signs, obtained by reducing the exponents modulo 2. The Hermitian form follows from H1​(cz,cw)=∣c∣2H1​(z,w). Its integer parameter equals the norm of the Gaussian integer, agreeing with the degree of this nonzero isogeny of elliptic curves. The formulas also include c=0, when the pulled-back line bundle is trivial.

The bundle is on A, so the printed, undefined target Pic(X) must be read as the Picard group Pic(A). Write this Picard group additively and set q(h)=[h∗L]. The zero morphism pulls back L to a constant one-dimensional vector space tensored with OA​, so q(0)=0. The Theorem of the Cube, pulled back along (f,g,h):A→B3, gives
q(f+g+h)−q(f+g)−q(f+h)−q(g+h)+q(f)+q(g)+q(h)=0.
(14)
This is the bilinear cross-effect of a line bundle on an abelian variety: set d(f,g)=q(f+g)−q(f)−q(g). Substitution into this identity gives
d(f+g,h)=d(f,h)+d(g,h).
(15)
The definition gives d(f,g)=d(g,f), so additivity holds in both arguments. Additivity includes negative multiples and the zero morphism. Hence DL​ is a symmetric bilinear map with values in Pic(A). The equalities refer to isomorphism classes of line bundles; without chosen rigidifications they are not asserted to be specified canonical isomorphisms of bundles.

Let m:B×B→B be addition, and let p1​,p2​ be the projections. Consider the line bundle
M=m∗L⊗p1∗​L−1⊗p2∗​L−1.
(16)
Its restriction to B×{y} has Picard group class [Ty∗​L⊗L−1]=ϕL​(y); the factor Ly−1​ is a constant line and has trivial class. Its restriction to {0}×B is also trivial. Choose a trivialization of the fiber L0​ and use it to normalize along the zero sections. The defining universal property of the normalized Poincaré line bundle then identifies
M≅(idB​,ϕL​)∗PB​.
(17)
More explicitly, both sides have the same restrictions to all fibers of the second projection, and both are normalized on {0}×B; the Seesaw theorem makes their quotient trivial. Here ϕL​:B→B is the homomorphism associated to a line bundle on an abelian variety, and B is the dual abelian variety. Pull back by (f,g) to obtain the required identity
DL​(f,g)≅(f,ϕL​∘g)∗PB​.​
(18)
The choice of fiber trivialization is immaterial to this identity in the Picard group.

For a group homomorphism h:A→B, the dual morphism h:B→A is pullback of algebraically trivial line bundles. Translations satisfy h∘Tx​=Th(x)​∘h, and therefore
ϕh∗L​(x)=h∗(Th(x)∗​L⊗L−1)=(h∘ϕL​∘h)(x).
(20)
Also ϕL⊗M​=ϕL​+ϕM​ and ϕL−1​=−ϕL​. Dualizing is additive: f+g​=f​+g​, since algebraically trivial line bundles obey the Theorem of the square. Apply these identities to the three factors defining DL​. The two diagonal terms cancel in the expansion
ϕDL​(f,g)​​=(f​+g​)ϕL​(f+g)−f​ϕL​f−g​ϕL​g=f​ϕL​g+g​ϕL​f.​
(21)
Hence ϕDL​(f,g)​=f​∘ϕL​∘g+g​∘ϕL​∘f​.

For a line bundle M on C×T, put d(t)=degMt​, and choose an ample line bundle A on C. Fix t0​. For sufficiently large q, both H1(C,Mt0​​⊗Aq) and H1(C,Mt0​−1​⊗Aq) vanish, by Serre duality and negativity of the degrees of their dual canonical twists. The semicontinuity theorem for coherent cohomology makes both groups vanish on a neighborhood of t0​. The family is flat over T: C×T→T is flat and the sheaves in question are locally free.
On that neighborhood the Riemann-Roch theorem gives
h0(C,Mt​⊗Aq)=d(t)+qdegA+1−g,h0(C,Mt−1​⊗Aq)=−d(t)+qdegA+1−g.
(22)
Upper semicontinuity of both zeroth sheaf cohomology dimensions, after shrinking again, gives d(t)≤d(t0​) and −d(t)≤−d(t0​). Hence d(t)=d(t0​). The degree is locally constant, and thus constant on connected T. With the usual convention that a variety is irreducible, this proves the assertion as printed. If disconnected varieties are allowed, the necessary qualification is constancy on each connected component: take T to be two points and the bundles OP1​ and OP1​(1) to see that a global constant degree need not exist.

The defining section of OX​(D) is nowhere zero on C, so its restriction is trivial and has degree zero. Every translated line bundle also has degree zero on C, by the preceding part. For any t∈X, either C⊆D−t or the defining section of Tt∗​OX​(D) restricts to a nonzero section on C. In the second case its zero effective divisor has degree zero, so it has no zeros. Thus a translate of D either contains all of C or misses all of C.
Take d∈D and x1​,x2​∈C. The translate D−(d−x1​) contains x1​, hence all of C. In particular d+(x2​−x1​)∈D. This proves D+(x2​−x1​)⊆D; exchanging x1​,x2​ proves the reverse inclusion. Since the prime Weil divisor D is reduced and translation is an automorphism, equality of these sets gives equality of Weil divisors. Remembering that pullback by Tt​ translates the divisor by −t, the answer is
Tx1​−x2​∗​D=D.​
(24)

The line bundle translation stabilizer is the closed subgroup
K(L)=kerϕL​={x∈X:Tx∗​L≅L}.
(25)
Each difference x1​−x2​ lies in K(L), by the equality of Weil divisors just proved. Thus the subgroup they generate lies in K(L), and closedness puts its Zariski topology closure there too. Therefore Y⊆K(L)​. The closure of a subgroup is again a subgroup: multiplication and inversion preserve its closure by continuity. In this setting it is connected as well, since C−C is connected and contains zero, and the increasing finite sums of C−C are connected with a common point. Its reduced closure is an abelian subvariety.

For the curve generation criterion for an abelian variety, first suppose Y=X and an irreducible Weil divisor D avoids C. The preceding part gives K(OX​(D))=X, so ϕOX​(D)​=0 and OX​(D)∈Pic0(X). A nonzero effective divisor cannot have this property: if H is an ample line bundle and g=dimX, then the intersection product of Cartier divisors D⋅Hg−1 is positive, whereas an algebraically trivial line bundle has zero intersection with every curve. The latter follows from constancy of degree along a connected family defining algebraic equivalence; the former is the positive projective degree of D after replacing H by a very ample power. This contradiction proves that C meets every irreducible Weil divisor.
Conversely suppose Y=X. Fix c0​∈C, so C⊆c0​+Y. Use the permitted fiber theorem to choose a morphism of varieties f:X→Z with f−1(z0​)=Y. This morphism is nonconstant because Y is proper. Choose an affine neighborhood U of z0​. The irreducible image f(X) has positive dimension, so its intersection with U does too; some regular function h on U is nonconstant on this intersection. Then F=h∘f is a nonconstant rational function on X, regular on f−1(U).
The pole divisor avoiding a fiber construction applies: its pole Weil divisor is nonzero. Indeed on the smooth, hence normal, projective variety X, a rational function with no codimension-one poles extends to a global regular function; every global regular function on a connected projective variety is constant. Every pole component D0​ lies outside f−1(U) and hence avoids Y. Therefore D0​+c0​ is an irreducible Weil divisor disjoint from c0​+Y, and in particular from C. This proves the converse using precisely the fiber fact allowed in the question, without requiring a projective target Z. The criterion is
Y=X⟺C meets every irreducible divisor of X.​
(26)

Write the projective plane as P(V) for a three-dimensional complex vector space V. A projective line is the zero set of a nonzero linear functional ℓ∈V∗. Two such functionals give the same line exactly when they differ by a nonzero scalar. Hence the parameter space of lines is the dual projective space P(V∗). A choice of basis identifies V∗ with C3, giving (P2)∨≅P2​. The description as P(V∗) is intrinsic; the identification with the original P(V) requires a choice.

Represent the smooth plane conic by vTQv=0, with Q an invertible symmetric 3×3 matrix. The tangent line at [v] has coefficients ℓ=Qv, because the differential is 2vTQ. Thus its image in the dual projective space satisfies
ℓTQ−1ℓ=0.
(27)
Conversely any nonzero ℓ satisfying this equation gives v=Q−1ℓ, a point of the original smooth plane conic whose tangent is [ℓ]. This correspondence is the restriction of an invertible projective linear transformation. The dual conic is therefore the smooth conic
C1∨​:ℓTQ−1ℓ=0.​
(28)

To analyze the genus one tangent incidence curve of two conics, choose projective coordinates in which C1​ has equation XZ−Y2=0. Its points and tangent lines are parametrized by [a:b]∈P1 as
[a2:ab:b2],ℓ[a:b]​: b2X−2abY+a2Z=0.
(29)
For x=[X:Y:Z]∈C2​, the incidence equation is a nonzero homogeneous quadratic in a,b, so the projection E→C2​ is finite of degree two. Its discriminant vanishes exactly when Y2−XZ=0, that is, at C1​∩C2​. There are four such points; Bézout theorem says their intersection multiplicities sum to four, so all four intersections are transverse.
Locally on C2​, after choosing an affine tangent-parameter chart and completing the square, the incidence equation is w2=u(s), where s is a local parameter and u has a simple zero at each intersection. This is smooth, with ramification index two there; away from those points the roots are distinct and the cover is étale. The analogous chart covers a root at infinity. Thus E is smooth everywhere, and the cover has exactly four ramification points. It is connected: the discriminant has odd valuation at each of its four zeros, so it cannot be a square in the function field of C2​. The associated quadratic extension is therefore a field, not two separate sheets.
A smooth plane conic over C is isomorphic to the projective line. Apply the Riemann-Hurwitz formula to this connected degree-two cover:
2g(E)−2=2(2⋅0−2)+4=0.
(30)
Consequently g(E)=1​, and E is a smooth projective genus one curve.

The printed construction is not well defined: the next tangent must pass through x1​, rather than x0​. If x0​=x1​ and ℓ1​=ℓ0​, a second line through x0​ cannot also pass through x1​, because the unique line through both points is ℓ0​. Here is a concrete counterexample satisfying all the conic hypotheses. In the affine chart take
C1​:x2+y2=1,C2​:x2/4+y2/9=1,
(31)
with ℓ0​:x=1, x0​=(1,33​/2) and x1​=(1,−33​/2). The second tangent through x0​ is −23x+123​y=31. It does not contain x1​: its left side there is −77. The conics meet transversely at the four complex points with x2=32/5, y2=−27/5. Thus this is a defect in the original PDF, not just in its conversion.
For the corrected construction, let ιℓ​ exchange the two points of C2​ on a fixed tangent line, and let ιx​ exchange the two tangents to C1​ through a fixed point of C2​. Both projections from E are degree-two morphisms to a smooth plane conic: for the line projection this follows from intersecting a line with C2​, which has no line component. Since E is smooth and the ground field has characteristic zero, their quadratic function-field extensions define regular involutions on the whole curve. At a ramification point “the second” point is the same point, counted with multiplicity. Each switch is an involution of a degree-two map from a genus one curve. Hence the corrected step is the everywhere-defined automorphism F=ιx​∘ιℓ​.
Choose an origin O on the genus one curve. The Abel-Jacobi map of a genus-one curve identifies E with Pic0(E) by p↦OE​(p−O). Fibers of each degree-two projection are linearly equivalent Weil divisors, since they are pullbacks of points of P1. Thus their group sums are constant: for suitable a,b∈E,
ιℓ​(p)=a−p,ιx​(p)=b−p,F(p)=p+(b−a).
(32)
This also holds at the ramification points, where 2p=a or 2p=b. Therefore the corrected step is a translation on an elliptic curve. If Fn(p0​)=p0​ for one point, then n(b−a)=0. It follows that Fn(p)=p for every p∈E. The corrected construction has the Poncelet porism: one periodic orbit implies all orbits are periodic, with the same least period. The literal printed construction fails before this conclusion; the proof establishes the intended, explicitly corrected assertion.
