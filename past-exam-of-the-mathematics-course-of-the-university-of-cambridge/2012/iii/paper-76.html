A regular perturbation admits an asymptotic expansion about the limiting problem on the relevant scale without losing its solutions or conditions. A singular perturbation changes the limiting structure, so some solutions require a different scale: a differential equation may lose an order, or a polynomial may lose its degree.
The two finite roots approach s=±2​. Set x=s+ϵa+O(ϵ2). Substitution into the polynomial gives s2−2=0 at leading order and s3+2sa=0 at the next order. Since s2=2, a=−1 for either sign. Each branch is a regular perturbation of a simple algebraic root, with
x+​=2​−ϵ+O(ϵ2),x−​=−2​−ϵ+O(ϵ2).​
(1)
The limiting quadratic has only two roots, whereas the original equation has three. The missing root is a divergent algebraic root in a singular perturbation. The dominant balance for algebraic roots sets x=ϵ−1X, giving X3+X2=2ϵ2. Its nonzero leading root is X=−1. Put X=−1+aϵ2+O(ϵ4); the left side is aϵ2+O(ϵ4), so a=2. Hence the second nonzero term occurs at order ϵ, not order one:
x3​=−ϵ−1+2ϵ+O(ϵ3).​
(2)
For small nonzero real ϵ these three branches are real and distinct; the divergent branch changes sign with ϵ. The two bounded branches are regular even though the full root problem is singular.

Use primes for differentiation with respect to X. Since X=ϵx, substituting the amplitude-phase representation into the differential equation and separating real and imaginary parts gives
ϵ2A′′+(k2−σ2)A=0,2σA′+σ′A=0.
(11)
The second equation is exactly (σA2)′=0. Thus, with a real constant C and A=0,
σA2=C,ϵ2A′′+k2A=A3C2​.​
(12)
This exact amplitude-phase equation is the Ermakov-Pinney equation for the oscillator. Conversely, any real nonvanishing A satisfying it and σ=C/A2 gives an exact complex solution. Existence is not an asymptotic assumption: choose independent real solutions u,v of the original equation, form y=u+iv, and set A=(u2+v2)1/2. Their nonzero constant Wronskian prevents simultaneous zeros. The phase derivative satisfies σA2=uvx​−ux​v, so it is constant. Complex conjugation gives a second branch, and linear combinations recover real solutions.
For the WKB approximation, work on an interval where K=∣k∣>0 is smooth and bounded away from zero. Choose the positive phase branch and an ϵ-independent positive C. The equations naturally admit even-power expansions
σ=K+ϵ2σ1​+O(ϵ4),A=A0​+ϵ2A1​+O(ϵ4).

There is no order-ϵ phase correction in this convention. Leading order gives σ0​=K and A0​=(C/K)1/2. Comparing order ϵ2 in the real equation yields
σ1​=2KA0​A0′′​​=8K33(K′)2​−4K2K′′​,A0​A1​​=−2Kσ1​​.

Assume ϵ↓0 and fixed A>0. Away from the degenerate endpoint x=0, the reduced equation is Ay0′​+y0​=0. The positive drift makes the rapidly varying homogeneous mode decay as one moves away from the left boundary, so the outer solution is selected by the right boundary condition:
yout,0​(x)=e(1−x)/A.​
(19)
It does not satisfy the left value: its limit there is L=e1/A.
If the layer width is δ, diffusion is O(ϵ/δ2), while Axy′ is O(A) because the drift vanishes linearly. Their dominant balance gives the square-root boundary layer at a vanishing drift
x=O(ϵ/A​),ξ=xA/ϵ​.​
(20)
Writing y=Y(ξ) gives Y′′+ξY′+[ϵ​/A3/2]ξY=0. The first nonzero inner expansion satisfies Y0′′​+ξY0′​=0, hence Y0′​=Be−ξ2/2. Imposing Y0​(0)=1 and matching Y0​(∞)=L gives
Y0​(ξ)=1+(e1/A−1)erf(ξ/2​).​
(21)
The overlap region is ϵ/A​≪x≪1 for fixed A. The additive composite expansion
ycomp,0​=e(1−x)/A+(1−e1/A)erfc(x2ϵA​​)
(22)
combines the two leading approximations and satisfies the left value exactly and the right value up to an exponentially small term. Only the first nonzero terms were requested; higher matching terms need not be added.
For fixed A<0, the decaying fast mode is at the other endpoint. The outer solution is now fixed by y(0)=1, giving e−x/A. At x=1 the drift does not vanish, so the right layer has width O(ϵ/∣A∣), with leading transient proportional to e−∣A∣(1−x)/ϵ. In particular its leading inner profile is e−1/A+(1−e−1/A)e−η, where η=∣A∣(1−x)/ϵ. The vanishing drift at the left endpoint does not require an order-one mismatch layer for these data.
For A=0, the reduced equation is xy0​=0, which cannot supply the nonzero endpoint values. The equation becomes an Airy ordinary differential equation: x=ϵ1/3ξ gives Y′′+ξY=0. The exact solutions are combinations of Ai(−x/ϵ1/3) and Bi(−x/ϵ1/3). For x>0 they oscillate, with local wavelength of order ϵ/x​, rather than forming a decaying exponential layer matched to a smooth nonzero outer profile. The O(ϵ1/3) region resolves the turning endpoint, but oscillations extend throughout the interval. Boundary-value resonances can occur at isolated parameter values. Thus setting A=0 is not a continuous substitution into either fixed-sign layer approximation.

Interpret the Fourier transform as a distributional Fourier transform. Exponentially regularize the Heaviside step function:
e−axH(x)​(k)=∫0∞​e−(a+ik)xdx=a+ik1​=a2+k2a​−ia2+k2k​,a>0.
(23)
As a↓0, the first term tends to πδ(k) in the tempered distribution sense, since its integral is π and it concentrates at zero. The second tends to −iPV(1/k). Therefore
H(k)=πδ(k)+PVik1​.​
(24)
The principal-value convention is essential: the printed 1/(ik) is not an ordinary integrable function at zero. This normalization also satisfies ikH=1, the Fourier transform of a derivative version of H′=δ.

The locally integrable logarithm has distributional derivative (log∣x∣)′=PV(1/x). By the Fourier transform of a derivative and the previous result,
iklog∣x∣​(k)=−iπsgnk.
(27)
For k=0, this gives −π/∣k∣. At zero the expression must be extended as a Hadamard finite-part integral, not treated as a locally integrable function. For example, define the finite-part inverse-absolute-value distribution by
⟨T,φ⟩=∫∣k∣<1​∣k∣φ(k)−φ(0)​dk+∫∣k∣≥1​∣k∣φ(k)​dk.

log∣x∣​(k)=−πPf∣k∣1​+Cδ(k).​

For a locally integrable periodic function, its Fourier series coefficients are cm​=∫01​f(x)e−2πimxdx. Window the function to one period by w(x)=H(x)−H(x−1). With the stated Fourier transform convention,
cm​=fw​(2πm).​
(30)
There is no extra factor of 2π: the Fourier coefficient already integrates over a period of length one.
For a completely arbitrary periodic distribution, multiplication by a discontinuous window need not be defined, for example at a derivative of a delta supported on a period boundary. The sharp-window assertion therefore presupposes a windowing convention or enough regularity at the endpoints. The smooth-window Fourier coefficients of a periodic distribution use a smooth compactly supported partition-of-unity window χ with ∑j∈Z​χ(x+j)=1; then cm​=χf​(2πm) in the distribution sense. The logarithmic example below is locally integrable, so the printed sharp window is legitimate and no ambiguity affects it.
For f(x)=log∣x−1/2∣, put u=x−1/2. Symmetry gives, for integer m=0,
cm​=2(−1)m∫01/2​logucos(2πmu)du.
(31)
An integration by parts has no endpoint term: ulogu→0 at zero and sin(πm)=0 at the upper endpoint. It follows that
cm​=−πm(−1)m​Si(πm),​
(32)
where the sine integral is Si(z)=∫0z​sint/tdt. Its limiting value π/2 follows from the Abel-regularized calculation in part (ii); integration by parts of its tail gives Si(πm)=π/2−(−1)m/(πm)+O(m−3) for positive integer m. Hence the Fourier coefficients of a periodic logarithmic singularity have

Take the spatial Fourier transform of the causal Green function. The transformed equation is Gt​=(μ−iUk−γk2)G+δ(t), with zero response for t<0. Integration across the impulse sets G(k,0+)=1, so
G(k,t)=H(t)eμt−iUkt−γk2t.
(34)
Inverting the Gaussian Fourier transform gives
G(x,t)=4πγt​H(t)​exp(μt−4γt(x−Ut)2​).​
(35)
This solves the homogeneous equation for t>0 and tends to the Dirac delta distribution as t↓0. Its spatial integral is eμt, as required by the zero-wavenumber growth law.
Along a ray x=vt, the exponential growth rate is
s(v)=μ−4γ(v−U)2​.
(36)
The impulse grows in the moving interval of rays U−2γμ​<v<U+2γμ​, while its peak at x=Ut grows as t−1/2eμt. At a fixed position,
G(x,t)∼4πγt​eUx/(2γ)​e(μ−U2/(4γ))t.
(37)
Thus, under the printed assumption μ>0, 0<μ<U2/(4γ) gives convective wave-packet instability, because the growing disturbance is swept away from each fixed observation point. μ>U2/(4γ) gives absolute wave-packet instability, with exponential growth at fixed position. At equality the fixed-position exponential rate is zero and the impulse decays algebraically as t−1/2, even though its moving peak grows. The two bounding rays have the same algebraic prefactor.
These statements classify the Green function and localized initial disturbances propagated by its convolution. They do not assert that every possible initial condition decays at fixed position in the convective case: spatially uniform initial data instead produce eμt. Persistent external forcing can likewise change the observed late-time response. The distinction between convective wave-packet instability and absolute wave-packet instability concerns localized disturbances in a specified frame.

ω0​=i(μ0​−4γU2​),f=(ν/γ)1/4,λ=γν​iω1​​.​
(40)
The assumed decaying solutions of the Hermite differential equation require λ=2j+1, j=0,1,…. They are proportional to e−ξ2/2Hj​(ξ), with physicists' Hermite polynomials. Therefore the global modes of a quadratically confined Ginzburg-Landau equation have
ω1​=−i(2j+1)γν​,sj​=μ0​−4γU2​−ϵ(2j+1)γν​.​
(41)
For every fixed positive ϵ, multiplying the Hermite-Gaussian by eUx/(2γ) still gives decay at both infinities. The strongest mode is j=0, so the whole-line system is globally unstable precisely when
μ0​>4γU2​+ϵγν​.​
(42)
Equality is a neutral ground mode. This spectrum is exact for the stated quadratic profile, even though the notation organizes it as an expansion in ϵ.
The homogeneous absolute-instability threshold in part (a) is μ=U2/(4γ). The global threshold approaches that value as the profile varies increasingly slowly, but at finite ϵ the quadratic confinement adds the loss ϵγν​. A locally absolutely unstable patch at the profile maximum is necessary but must be strong and broad enough to overcome that loss. Merely having positive local temporal growth μ0​>0, which can be only convective wave-packet instability, is insufficient for a growing decaying global normal mode. This connects the local impulse classification to the discrete global spectrum.
