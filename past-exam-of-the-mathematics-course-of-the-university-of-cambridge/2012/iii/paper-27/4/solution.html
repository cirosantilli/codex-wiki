The elliptic-curve discriminant of y2=x3−12x2+20x is 16⋅202⋅(144−80)=409600=21452. Thus three and eleven are primes of good reduction. Counting the reduced points gives four points over F3​ and sixteen over F11​. For example, the numbers of affine points above successive x=0,1,2 modulo three are 1,1,1. Modulo eleven, for x=0,…,10, they are 1,2,1,2,0,0,2,2,2,2,1; add the point at infinity in each case.
The reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve is injective on torsion prime to the residue characteristic. The three-primary rational torsion injects into the group of order sixteen at eleven, so is trivial; likewise the eleven-primary torsion injects into the group of order four at three. Every other primary component injects at both primes. Therefore the rational torsion subgroup has order dividing four. All four rational 2-torsion points are visible, so
E(Q)tors​={O,(0,0),(2,0),(10,0)}≅(Z/2Z)2.​
(15)
The rational point P=(1,3) lies on the curve. The tangent slope in the elliptic-curve addition formula is (3−24+20)/6=−1/6, giving 2P=(361/36,−323/216). The integral substitution X=x−4 changes the equation to y2=X3−28X−48; the doubled point has X=217/36. The Nagell–Lutz theorem says that a rational torsion point on this integral short equation has integral coordinates, so 2P, and hence P, is nontorsion. An infinite-order point is (1,3).
We prove the rank bound by two-isogeny descent. For a=−12,b=20, the two-isogeny formula gives the partner
E′:Y2=X3+24X2+64X
(16)
and maps ϕ:E→E′, ψ:E′→E with ψϕ=[2]. The two-torsion square-class homomorphism α:E(Q)→Q∗/(Q∗)2 is α(x,y)=[x] away from O,(0,0), with values 1 and [20]=[5] at those exceptional points. Its kernel is ψE′(Q). The corresponding map α′ on E′ has kernel ϕE(Q).
The prime-support bound in two-isogeny descent can be checked directly here. For a prime ℓ∤20, if vℓ​(x)>0 then x2−12x+20 is a unit, so 2vℓ​(y)=vℓ​(x) is even; if vℓ​(x)<0, the x3 term has strictly least valuation and 2vℓ​(y)=3vℓ​(x) is even. Thus the square class of x is supported only on two and five. Also x(x−2)(x−10)≥0 forces x∈[0,2]∪[10,∞), so nonzero x is positive. The possible classes are 1,2,5,10, and the four torsion points provide all of them:
α(O)=1,α((2,0))=2,α((0,0))=5,α((10,0))=10.
(17)
Consequently #α(E(Q))=4.
The same valuation argument on E′ gives possible signed classes 1,−1,2,−2, since its coefficient b′=64 has only the prime two. The identity gives class 1, and (−4,8)∈E′(Q) gives class −1. For d=±2, the two-isogeny descent quartic is
W2=dU4+24U2V2+(64/d)V4,
(18)
with integral coprime U,V after scaling a rational solution. If U is odd, its right side is 2 or 10 modulo sixteen for d=2, and 14 or 6 for d=−2, according to the parity of V. None is a square. If U=2u is even, V is odd and the right side is 32(±u4+3u2V2±V4). The bracket is odd for either parity of u, so its 2-adic valuation is five, again impossible for a square. The signed divisor-two obstruction for an isogeny covering excludes both classes. Hence α′(E′(Q))={1,−1}.
For completeness, the index correction in the two-isogeny index formula over a number field is one: kerψ={O,(0,0)} and ϕ((2,0))=(0,0), so the whole kernel lies in ϕE(Q). Thus
[E(Q):2E(Q)]=#α(E(Q))#α′(E′(Q))=4⋅2=8.
(19)
The Mordell-Weil theorem and the four rational 2-torsion points identify this index as 2r+2. Therefore 2r+2=8 and
rankE(Q)=1.​
(20)
This is the rank-one elliptic curve with roots zero two and ten. We have exhibited a point of infinite order but have not needed to prove that it generates the free part.
