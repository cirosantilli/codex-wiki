Let Vm​=det[xij−1​]i,j=1m​. It is an alternating polynomial: exchanging two variables exchanges two rows of the Vandermonde matrix and reverses its determinant. Hence Vm​ vanishes whenever xk​=xl​, so each factor xl​−xk​ divides it. The distinct linear factors are pairwise coprime polynomials, and their product therefore divides Vm​. Both have total degree 0+1+⋯+(m−1)=m(m−1)/2, so
Vm​=Cm​∏k<l​(xl​−xk​).
(1)
The coefficient of xmm−1​ in the determinant is Vm−1​, by expansion along its last row. The same coefficient in the product is ∏k<l<m​(xl​−xk​). Thus Cm​=Cm−1​, and C1​=1. Consequently the Vandermonde determinant identity is
det[xij−1​]i,j=1m​=1≤k<l≤m∏​(xl​−xk​).​
(2)
This is a polynomial identity, including repeated coordinates; no division by a possibly zero numerical Vandermonde product is needed.

Choose real monic orthogonal polynomials pj​ of degree j, j=0,1,…, for w(x)=xae−x on [0,∞), and let
hj​=∫0∞​pj​(x)2w(x)dx,ϕj​(x)=hj−1/2​pj​(x)w(x)​.
(5)
Extend each ϕj​ by zero to negative x. The resulting functions form an orthonormal set in L2(R,dx). Define the orthogonal polynomial projection kernel
Km​(x,y)=j=0∑m−1​ϕj​(x)ϕj​(y)=w(x)w(y)​j=0∑m−1​hj​pj​(x)pj​(y)​.​
(6)
The formula on the right is for nonnegative arguments; the kernel is zero when either argument is negative. With Φij​=ϕj−1​(xi​), the kernel evaluation matrix is ΦΦT. By the determinant multiplication identity and the preceding monic basis change,
det[Km​(xi​,xj​)]i,j=1m​=∏j=0m−1​hj​∏i<j​(xj​−xi​)2∏i​w(xi​)​.
(7)
Thus initially cm​=cm​∏j​hj​.
Use the symmetric labelled joint probability density on the full orthant. Expanding the two copies of detΦ into permutations and integrating all variables, orthonormality makes a term vanish unless the two permutations agree. Each of the m! surviving terms integrates to 1. Hence
fm​(x1​,…,xm​)=m!1​det[Km​(xi​,xj​)]i,j=1m​,cm​=m!∏j=0m−1​hj​1​.​
(8)
If the eigenvalues are instead listed in increasing order on a single ordered chamber, that chamber's density is m! times this symmetric density. We keep the full-orthant convention throughout.
An explicit choice is pj​(x)=(−1)jj!Lj(a)​(x) in terms of the Generalized Laguerre polynomials. To verify the normalization independently, their Rodrigues' formula gives
pj​(x)=(−1)jw(x)−1dxjdj​(xa+je−x).

for x,y≥0.

Write Dn​=det[K(xi​,xj​)]i,j=1n​ and expand it by permutations. The assumptions ensure that the indicated single-variable integrals exist; the finite-rank projection kernel constructed above satisfies them with r=m. No symmetry of a general kernel is needed for the following argument.
If a permutation fixes n, its product contains the separate factor K(xn​,xn​). Integration gives r times the term of the corresponding permutation of {1,…,n−1}. These terms contribute rDn−1​.
Otherwise n belongs to a longer permutation cycle. Let i precede n and let j follow it. The only factors containing xn​ are K(xi​,xn​)K(xn​,xj​), whose integral is K(xi​,xj​) by the reproducing assumption. Delete n from the cycle to obtain a permutation τ on n−1 letters. Inserting n back after any of the n−1 possible letters reconstructs exactly one permutation; its sign of a permutation is −sgn(τ), since increasing a cycle length by one reverses its sign. Thus the nonfixed terms contribute −(n−1)Dn−1​.
Combining these two disjoint classes proves projection kernel determinant integration:
∫R​Dn​dxn​=(r−n+1)Dn−1​.​
(11)
For n=1, use the empty determinant D0​=1, and the statement is precisely the assumed diagonal integral. For our kernel, orthonormality directly yields
∫R​Km​(x,y)Km​(y,z)dy=Km​(x,z),∫R​Km​(x,x)dx=m,
(12)
so every step of the marginal integration is justified.

For 1≤k≤m, the correlation function of a point process associated with the eigenvalues is
Rk​(x1​,…,xk​)=(m−k)!m!​∫Rm−k​fm​(x1​,…,xm​)dxk+1​⋯dxm​.
(13)
The factorial factor counts ordered selections of k distinct eigenvalues, so this is a factorial moment density, rather than the ordinary probability density of k particular labels. Equivalently, for a nonnegative measurable test function F,
E∑i1​,…,ik​ distinct​F(λi1​​,…,λik​​)=∫Rk​F(x1​,…,xk​)Rk​(x1​,…,xk​)dx.
(14)
Repeated projection kernel determinant integration, with r=m, gives a factor (m−k)! on integrating an m-by-m kernel determinant down to size k. Together with the normalizing 1/m! in fm​, the factors cancel:
Rk​(x1​,…,xk​)=det[Km​(xi​,xj​)]i,j=1k​.​
(15)
In particular R1​(x)=Km​(x,x) and ∫Rk​dx=m!/(m−k)!. Set R0​=1 and Rk​=0 for k>m. The eigenvalue configuration is thus a determinantal point process with a rank-m finite-rank projection kernel.

A function f:R→R satisfies Lipschitz continuity with bound L≥0 if
∣f(x)−f(y)∣≤L∣x−y∣for every x,y∈R.​
(16)
Here L is an admissible Lipschitz bound; the least admissible value is the Lipschitz constant. The later parts use the admissible bound 1.

The Hoffman–Wielandt inequality says that for normal matrices A,B with eigenvalues αi​,βi​, there is a permutation π such that
∑i=1N​∣αi​−βπ(i)​∣2≤∥A−B∥F2​=Tr[(A−B)∗(A−B)],
(19)
where ∥⋅∥F​ is the Frobenius norm and ∗ denotes the conjugate transpose. For Hermitian matrices, the increasingly ordered real eigenvalues may be paired directly: this ordering minimizes the sum of squared distances over all permutations, by removing crossed pairs. In particular, for the real symmetric matrices in this question,
i=1∑N​∣λi​−λi​∣2≤Tr[(XN​−XN​)2].​
(20)
The square on the right is justified by symmetry. For arbitrary matrices, the appropriate expression is the conjugate-transpose product, not the ordinary square.

Use the fixed common entry law from the printed setup: Y=N​Xij​ has mean zero and second moment 1 for each upper-triangular entry, including the diagonal. Let
ZC​=Y1{∣Y∣≥C}​,t(C)=E[Y21{∣Y∣≥C}​].
(23)
Since EY=0, the removed part after centered truncation of a Wigner matrix is
Δij​=Xij​−Xij​=N−1/2(ZC,ij​−EZC​).
(24)
Consequently EΔij2​=N−1Var(ZC​)≤N−1t(C). Symmetry gives TrΔ2=∑i,j​Δij2​. There are N diagonal terms and N(N−1) off-diagonal terms in this sum, so
E[N1​TrΔ2]=Var(ZC​)≤t(C).
(25)
Mirrored entries are counted twice, as they must be; independence of those mirrored entries is neither true nor needed for this expectation calculation.
By the preceding bound and the Markov inequality,
P{D>ε}≤P{N1​TrΔ2>ε2}≤ε2t(C)​.
(26)
The dominated convergence theorem applies to Y21{∣Y∣≥C}​≤Y2, so t(C)→0. Choose C with t(C)<ε3. Then
P{∣⟨LN​,f⟩−⟨LN​,f⟩∣>ε}<εfor every N.​
(27)
This second moment bound for spectral truncation depends only on ε and the common law of Y, and is uniform over functions with Lipschitz bound 1. No fourth-moment hypothesis is required. If the scaled entry law were allowed to vary arbitrarily with N, a uniform choice would instead require uniform decay of its second-moment tails; the intended fixed-law setting supplies exactly that condition.

Use block matrix elimination with the invertible square block A:
(I−CA−1​0I​)(AC​BD​)=(A0​BD−CA−1B​).
(28)
The left multiplier is block triangular with identity diagonal blocks, so its determinant is 1. Taking the determinant of the block-triangular product proves
detM=detAdet(D−CA−1B).​
(29)
The matrix D−CA−1B is the Schur complement of A. The proof needs A invertible, but does not assume the Schur complement or the full matrix is invertible.

Take z off the real axis, or more generally require both X−zI and X(i)−zI to be invertible. The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices guarantees these inverses for nonreal z. Move coordinate i to the first position by a simultaneous row and column permutation. With H=X(i)−zI, the permuted matrix is
(Xii​−zxi​​xiT​H​).
(30)
Solve its equation against the first coordinate vector: if its solution is (u,v)T, then the lower block equation gives v=−H−1xi​u. Substitution into the first equation gives (Xii​−z−xiT​H−1xi​)u=1. The component u is the requested diagonal entry of the matrix inverse, proving the Schur complement formula for a diagonal resolvent entry:
[(X−zI)−1]ii​=Xii​−z−xiT​(X(i)−zI)−1xi​1​.​
(31)
The product is bilinear, with transpose, because xi​ is real and X is real symmetric, even when the inverse is complex. In a complex Hermitian matrix version the row is xi∗​ instead. The final xi​ belongs inside the denominator, as in the original PDF; the converted TeX misplaces it outside the fraction.
The inverse hypotheses matter if z is real: X=(01​10​) is invertible at z=0, while its one-by-one principal minor is not. Thus existence of the full inverse alone is not sufficient to use this formula.

Work with z in the upper half-plane. Write the Stieltjes matrix resolvents and their normalized matrix traces as
G=(XN​−zI)−1,G(i)=(XN(i)​−zI)−1,g=N1​TrG,g(i)=N1​TrG(i),qi​=xiT​G(i)xi​.
(32)
The minor trace is normalized by N, not by N−1. This sign convention is the negative of the convention (zI−XN​)−1 used in the general resolvent of an operator article. Here g is the Stieltjes transform of a measure of the empirical spectral measure, with kernel (x−z)−1.
The diagonal entries of XN​ are zero, so the preceding Schur complement formula gives Gii​=−1/(z+qi​). Taking the matrix trace, subtracting the comparison value −1/(z+g), and combining fractions gives the resolvent self-consistency defect
εN​(z)​=g+z+g1​=N1​i=1∑N​(z+g1​−z+qi​1​)=N1​i=1∑N​(z+g)(z+qi​)qi​−g​​.​
(33)
The positive numerator sign is fixed by this subtraction. All denominators are nonzero in the upper half-plane, as the imaginary-part estimate in the next part shows. The identity is deterministic and does not use entry independence or moment assumptions.

Put η=Imz>0. For a real eigenvalue λ,
Imλ−z1​=(λ−Rez)2+η2η​>0.
(34)
The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices therefore gives Img>0 and Imqi​≥0, since qi​ is a sum of (λ−z)−1 with nonnegative coefficients given by squared eigenvector coordinates of xi​. This is the positive imaginary part of a Stieltjes matrix resolvent. Hence
∣z+g∣≥η,∣z+qi​∣≥η.
(35)
Apply the triangle inequality to the identity above, and add and subtract the minor trace:
∣εN​(z)∣​≤η2N1​i=1∑N​∣qi​−g∣≤η2N1​i=1∑N​∣qi​−g(i)∣+η2N1​i=1∑N​∣g(i)−g∣.​
(36)
The allowed principal minor resolvent trace bound, with the printed N normalization, bounds the second average by c/(ηN). Thus the explicit conclusion is
∣εN​(z)∣≤(Imz)2N1​i=1∑N​​xiT​(XN(i)​−zI)−1xi​−gN(i)​(z)​+(Imz)3Nc​.​
(37)
In particular, the final term is O((Imz)−3N−1) with a constant independent of N. The upper-half-plane restriction gives the displayed positive denominators; in the lower half-plane the analogous bound uses ∣Imz∣.
The chosen centering also has a probabilistic meaning. The column vector xi​ is independent of the minor, has mean zero and coordinate variance 1/N, so its conditional expectation satisfies
E[qi​∣XN(i)​]=N1​TrG(i)=gN(i)​.
(38)
This explains why the bound isolates fluctuations of a quadratic form around the minor trace. Mean centering alone is not a concentration estimate; no unrequested limiting assertion is being assumed.
