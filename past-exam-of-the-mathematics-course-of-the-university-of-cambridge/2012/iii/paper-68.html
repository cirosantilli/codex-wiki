Use Dj​=−i∂xj​​ and ⟨ξ⟩=(1+∣ξ∣2)1/2, so the Fourier transform of P(D)u is P(ξ)u(ξ). Write PN​ for the homogeneous degree-N part. An elliptic differential operator has PN​(ξ)=0 for every real ξ=0. By continuity on the unit sphere, m=min∣ω∣=1​∣PN​(ω)∣>0. The lower-degree terms are bounded by C∣ξ∣N−1 for ∣ξ∣≥1, whence
∣P(ξ)∣≥m∣ξ∣N−C∣ξ∣N−1≥2m​∣ξ∣N≥c⟨ξ⟩N
(1)
for sufficiently large ∣ξ∣. This proves the high-frequency lower bound for an elliptic polynomial. Degree zero just means a nonzero constant and has no derivative gain.
With the Fourier convention in Question 3, the Sobolev space is
Hs(Rn)={u∈S′:⟨ξ⟩su∈L2},∥u∥Hs2​=(2π)−n∫⟨ξ⟩2s∣u(ξ)∣2dξ.​
(2)
The condition includes that the weighted transform is represented by an L2 function. For open X, the Local Sobolev space consists of u∈D′(X) for which χu, extended by zero, belongs to Hs(Rn) for every χ∈Cc∞​(X). We use the elementary Sobolev multiplication by a smooth cutoff fact for every real s. It follows from Fourier convolution with the rapidly decreasing χ​, the weighted inequality ⟨ξ⟩s≲⟨ξ−η⟩∣s∣⟨η⟩s, and the L1∗L2→L2 convolution bound.
For a compactly supported distribution, continuity on test functions supported in a fixed compact neighborhood gives finite order: for some integer M,
∣⟨u,φ⟩∣≤Cmax∣α∣≤M​supK​∣∂αφ∣.
(3)
Insert a compact smooth cutoff function equal to one near the support, times e−ix⋅ξ. The resulting Fourier transform is smooth and satisfies ∣u(ξ)∣≤C′⟨ξ⟩M. The distribution is also tempered by this same finite-order bound. Thus
u∈Ht(Rn)whenever t<−M−n/2.​
(4)
Indeed the square of the weighted bound is integrable exactly when 2(t+M)<−n. This is negative Sobolev regularity of a compactly supported distribution.
We next prove local regularity by a high-frequency reciprocal parametrix kernel, including its off-diagonal smoothing property. Choose ψ∈Cc∞​(Rn) equal to one on a ball containing all real zeros of P, and put b(ξ)=(1−ψ(ξ))/P(ξ), defining it smoothly as zero in the inner ball. Differentiation of the reciprocal and the elliptic lower bound give
∣∂ξα​b(ξ)∣≤Cα​⟨ξ⟩−N−∣α∣.
(5)
The Fourier multiplier E=b(D) maps Hs to Hs+N by its zeroth-order bound, and
EP(D)=P(D)E=1−R,R=ψ(D).
(6)
The kernel K=F−1b is smooth away from the origin: for x=0, repeatedly integrate by parts using eix⋅ξ=(i∣x∣2)−1x⋅∂ξ​eix⋅ξ. After sufficiently many integrations, the differentiated symbol is integrable. For any desired x derivative, repeat the argument with the additional polynomial ξβ. A large-radius cutoff function justifies each step and its removal uniformly on compact sets away from zero. Thus convolution by K carries a compactly supported distribution to a smooth function at points separated from its support. Also R carries a compactly supported distribution to a Schwartz function, because ψu is smooth with compact support.
For a target compact set in X, choose χ∈Cc∞​(X) equal to one on a neighborhood of it. Then χu and [P(D),χ]u have compact support, and the commutator is supported where derivatives of χ occur, away from the target. The parametrix identity gives
χu=E[χP(D)u]+E[[P(D),χ]u]+R(χu).
(7)
The first term is in Hs+N since χP(D)u∈Hs. The other two terms are smooth near the target by the proved kernel property. Since the target was arbitrary,
P(D)u∈Hlocs​(X)⟹u∈Hlocs+N​(X).​
(8)
This proof does not discard the cutoff function commutator; it places its support away from the set where regularity is sought.
For the final polynomial, select a multi-index α0​ with ∣α0​∣=N and ∂α0​Q a nonzero constant. The derivative-ratio hypothesis immediately gives ∣Q(ξ)∣≥c∣ξ∣δN at large frequency. In particular Q has no real zero there. For N>0, its degree bound also forces δ≤1. Differentiating 1/Q gives products of ratios ∂βQ/Q, whose total derivative order is ∣α∣. Hence the corresponding high-frequency reciprocal satisfies
∣∂ξα​bQ​(ξ)∣≤Cα​⟨ξ⟩−δN−δ∣α∣.
(9)
Its multiplier maps Hs to Hs+δN. Its kernel is still smooth off zero: each frequency integration by parts now lowers the order by δ, so more iterations may be needed, but δ>0 supplies arbitrarily much decay. The same separated-support commutator identity applies without a change. The derivative-ratio Sobolev gain for a polynomial operator is therefore
Q(D)u∈Hlocs​(X)⟹u∈Hlocs+δN​(X).​
(10)
Simply estimating the commutator by its differential order would lose this sharper gain; the off-diagonal kernel argument uses the full derivative-ratio hypothesis.

The symbol class Sym(X,Rk;N) consists of smooth amplitudes with estimates
∣∂xα​∂θβ​a(x,θ)∣≤CK,α,β​⟨θ⟩N−∣β∣,x∈K⋐X.​
(11)
There is no loss of symbol order under an x derivative. A phase function is real and smooth for θ=0, positively homogeneous of degree one in θ, with its full differential (dx​Φ,dθ​Φ) nowhere zero there, at least on the amplitude's conic support. Homogeneous phases need not be smooth at θ=0; that bounded-frequency region is handled separately or the amplitude is cut off near it.
Choose ρ∈Cc∞​(Rk) equal to one near zero. The oscillatory integral is defined by regularization in distributions:
⟨IΦ​(a),f⟩=ε↓0lim​∫X​∫eiΦ(x,θ)a(x,θ)ρ(εθ)f(x)dθdx,f∈Cc∞​(X).​
(12)
This is an ordinary integral when decay makes it absolutely convergent. For general order, the definition is independent of the regularizer. To see the mechanism, at large ∣θ∣ set
A=∣dx​Φ∣2+∣θ∣2∣dθ​Φ∣2,L=iAdx​Φ⋅∂x​+∣θ∣2dθ​Φ⋅∂θ​​.

Its x-derivative coefficients have order −1 and its θ-derivative coefficients order zero, so each formal transpose Lt lowers the amplitude order by one. Iterating more than N+k times makes the test-function pairing integrable, and the same estimates control regularizer derivatives. This justifies the cutoff function limit and its independence. A fixed bounded-frequency integral is smooth in x.
The singular support of a distribution is the complement of the largest open set on which it is represented by a smooth function. For the support-sensitive bound one must use conic support of an oscillatory amplitude: a closed set of limiting high-frequency directions, locally in the base variable. One sufficient precise choice is
Ca​=⋂R>0​{(x,θ/∣θ∣):(x,θ)∈suppX×Rk​a, ∣θ∣≥R}​⊂X×Sk−1,
(14)
with closure taken in this product. If x0​ is outside the projection of Ca​∩{dθ​Φ=0}, compactness of the unit sphere gives a neighborhood of x0​ and a positive uniform lower bound on ∣dθ​Φ∣ wherever the large-frequency amplitude is supported. On that region use
Lθ​=i∣dθ​Φ∣2dθ​Φ⋅∂θ​​,Lθ​eiΦ=eiΦ.
(15)
Its coefficients have order zero and its transpose lowers order by one. To prove Cj regularity, first differentiate j times in x, raising the amplitude order by at most j, and then integrate by parts more than N+j+k times. The resulting integrals and their derivatives converge uniformly on compact neighborhoods. Since j is arbitrary, the stationary-direction bound for singular support follows:
singsuppIΦ​(a)⊂πX​(Ca​∩{dθ​Φ=0}).​
(16)
This is the standard conic interpretation of the support restriction in the question; directions at infinity, rather than finite-frequency stationary points, determine possible singularities.
If suppa(x,⋅) literally means the support of the restricted function, the printed inclusion can fail. There is a slice-support obstruction for oscillatory singularities. In one dimension, take Φ=xθ and a=xη(θ)/∣θ∣, where η is smooth and even, zero for ∣θ∣≤1, and one for ∣θ∣≥2. This is a symbol of order −1, and the full phase differential is nonzero for θ=0. Its regularized integral near x=0 is
I(x)=2x∫0∞​η(θ)θcos(xθ)​dθ=−2xlog∣x∣+a smooth function.
(17)
Indeed substitute u=∣x∣θ in the tail, split at u=1, and write cosu=1+(cosu−1) below one. The first term gives −log∣x∣, while the remainder has an even convergent power series in x plus a constant; the bounded-frequency correction is smooth. Thus 0 is in the singular support, but a(0,⋅)=0 has empty slice support. The joint closed conic support retains this limiting base point and makes the proved theorem valid. No nonstationarity conclusion is inferred just from the amplitude vanishing at one base point.
For the wave equation, Fourier transformation in x reduces the initial-value problem to ∂t2​E+c2∣ξ∣2E=0, with E(ξ,0)=0 and ∂t​E(ξ,0)=1. For c>0 its solution is

E(x,t)=Elow​(x,t)+IΦ+​​(a+​)(x,t)+IΦ−​​(a−​)(x,t),

Elow​=(2π)−n∫eix⋅ξχ(ξ)c∣ξ∣sin(ct∣ξ∣)​dξ,Φ±​=x⋅ξ±ct∣ξ∣,a±​=±2ic∣ξ∣(2π)−n(1−χ(ξ))​.

The high-frequency amplitudes belong to Sym(Rn+1,Rn;−1), vanish near zero, and the phases have nonzero full differential since dx​Φ±​=ξ=0. Their stationary-direction equations are x±ctξ/∣ξ∣=0. A Schwartz function is a smooth function for which every seminorm
pαβ​(φ)=supx∈Rn​∣xα∂βφ(x)∣
(22)
is finite. These seminorms define the Fréchet topology of the Schwartz space S(Rn). The tempered distributions form its continuous linear dual S′. We use bilinear distribution pairing, with no complex conjugation on the test function.
Differentiation under the Fourier integral and integration by parts give
∂ξβ​φ​=F[(−ix)βφ],ξαf​=F[Dαf],D=−i∂.
(23)
Therefore each Schwartz seminorm of φ​ is bounded by a finite sum of L1 norms of polynomially weighted derivatives of φ. Insert the integrable weight ⟨x⟩−n−1 to bound those norms by finitely many Schwartz seminorms. This proves that F:S→S is continuous.
To justify inversion without a merely formal exchange of integrals, insert e−ε∣ξ∣2/2 in the inverse integral. The Gaussian Fourier integral gives
(2π)−n∫eix⋅ξe−ε∣ξ∣2/2φ​(ξ)dξ=(φ∗gε​)(x),gε​(x)=(2πε)−n/2e−∣x∣2/(2ε).
(24)
The normalized Gaussian is an approximate identity, so the right side tends to φ(x); the left side converges by dominated convergence theorem since φ​ is integrable. It follows that
φ(x)=(2π)−n∫eix⋅ξφ​(ξ)dξ,F2φ=(2π)nφ(−⋅).​
(25)
Reflection is continuous in Schwartz seminorms, so F−1=(2π)−nRF is continuous as well. This proves the Fourier transform isomorphism of the Schwartz space.
Define the distributional Fourier transform by
⟨u,φ⟩=⟨u,φ​⟩.​
(26)
It agrees with the ordinary integral transform whenever Fubini applies. Transposing the Schwartz inverse gives an inverse on S′, and the same squared-transform identity holds. For the usual strong dual topology, a seminorm is pB​(u)=supφ∈B​∣⟨u,φ⟩∣ for a bounded set B⊂S. Since a continuous linear Schwartz map takes bounded sets to bounded sets, pB​(u)=pFB​(u) proves continuity, and similarly for the inverse. Continuity also holds in the weak dual topology. Thus the transform is a continuous isomorphism on tempered distributions, with the stated normalization.
For a real symmetric positive-definite matrix G, there is c>0 with g(x)=xTGx≥c∣x∣2. The reciprocal is locally integrable in dimension three: the radial factor near zero is r2r−2dr. At infinity, rapid decay of a Schwartz test function makes it integrable. More quantitatively,
​∫g(x)φ(x)​dx​≤Csupx​⟨x⟩2∣φ(x)∣∫R3​∣x∣2⟨x⟩2dx​<∞.

For the complex extension use a symmetric matrix A=G+iB with real symmetric B and positive real part G. Symmetry is natural for a quadratic form; a skew-symmetric part contributes nothing. The bound ∣xTAx∣≥xTGx again gives a regular tempered reciprocal.
