Work under the money-market risk-neutral measure, with r0​≥0. The zero-coupon bond price is P(0,T)=E[exp(−∫0T​rs​ds)]. For this CIR model, write p(τ,r)=A(τ)e−B(τ)r. The Feynman-Kac formula gives
∂τ​p=(a−br)∂r​p+21​σ2r∂rr​p−rp,p(0,r)=1.
(1)
Matching the constant and linear coefficients of r yields
B′=1−bB−21​σ2B2,A′/A=−aB,B(0)=0,A(0)=1.
(2)
For CIR bond pricing, solving this Riccati equation and integrating the scalar equation for A, put γ=b2+2σ2​ and D(τ)=(γ+b)(eγτ−1)+2γ. Then
B(τ)=D(τ)2(eγτ−1)​,A(τ)=[D(τ)2γe(b+γ)τ/2​]2a/σ2.
(3)
Substitution verifies both equations and their initial conditions. The discounted candidate solves the martingale pricing equation; its boundedness on finite horizons, or Feynman-Kac formula, identifies it with the conditional expectation. The answer is P(0,T)=A(T)e−B(T)r0​​. More generally the same coefficients give P(t,T)=A(T−t)e−B(T−t)rt​.
The CIR model mean reverts to a/b and has volatility σr​, so it remains nonnegative and its fluctuation size depends on the rate level. For positive initial rate, zero is inaccessible if 2a≥σ2; below this threshold zero is reachable but the process remains nonnegative. Its stationary law is a gamma distribution, of shape 2a/σ2 and rate 2b/σ2. Explicit affine bond prices and nonnegative rates are useful features. Limitations include exclusion of negative rates, restricted volatility behavior and a single stochastic factor; the time-homogeneous parameter family cannot fit an arbitrary initial term structure exactly.

A futures contract fixes a maturity and underlying quantity, but its quoted delivery price is marked to market: gains and losses from quote changes are settled through the margin account, typically daily. A newly settled position has zero contract value; the quoted futures price is not an upfront purchase price for that position. At maturity the quote equals the spot price. In the continuous-settlement idealization, discounted futures gains must be local martingales under the money-market risk-neutral measure. Thus the quote has zero pricing-measure drift. Assuming the relevant integrability makes it a true martingale, FtT​=EQ​[ST​∣Ft​]​.
This is a futures pricing formula. In contrast, an unsettled forward contract has delivery quote EQ​[DtT​ST​∣Ft​]/EQ​[DtT​∣Ft​], where DtT​=e−∫tT​ru​du. Stochastic interest rates can make the two quotes differ. Contango means the futures quote is above spot for the maturity considered, while backwardation means it is below spot; futures curves are correspondingly described as rising or falling when comparing maturities.
For the Multivariate Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, multiplication by the matrix exponential eAt gives d(eAtXt​)=eAtdBt​, hence
Xt​=e−AtX0​+∫0t​e−A(t−u)dBu​.​
(4)
For deterministic X0​=x0​, the Gaussian distribution has mean e−Atx0​ and covariance
Ct​=∫0t​e−Aue−ATudu.
(5)
Equivalently C˙=I−AC−CAT, C0​=0. No symmetry or invertibility of A is required. This covariance can also be evaluated from a single block matrix exponential: if the upper-right block of exp(t(−A0​IAT​)) is Ht​, then Ct​=Ht​e−ATt. If X0​ is random, these assertions hold conditionally on X0​; the unconditional law need not be Gaussian. A stationary Gaussian law exists when the eigenvalues of A have positive real parts, but stability is unnecessary for the finite-time formulas.
For τ=T−t, the conditional mean of XT​ is e−AτXt​ and its conditional covariance is Cτ​. The Gaussian exponential-moment formula therefore gives the explicit quote
FtT​=exp(bTe−AτXt​+21​bTCτ​b).​
(6)
It tends to eb⋅Xt​=St​ as T↓t, as required.

For centered square-integrable variables with positive variances, the correlation coefficient is
ρ(X,X′)=E[X2]E[(X′)2]​E[XX′]​.
(7)
Put v=E[X2]​ and v′=E[(X′)2]​. Then
E[(vX​−v′X′​)2]=2(1−ρ).
(8)
Thus ρ=1⟺X=(v/v′)X′ almost surely​, with positive proportionality factor. Conversely positive proportionality immediately gives correlation one. For variables with nonzero means, this criterion applies to their centered versions and yields an affine, not necessarily proportional, relationship. That distinction is essential for the positive stock prices below.

Assume positive deterministic initial stock prices and finite, positive terminal variances, as required to define the correlation coefficient. The explicit stochastic-exponential solutions with constant volatilities give
St′​St​​=S0′​S0​​exp[(σ−σ′)Bt​−21​(σ2−(σ′)2)t].
(9)
The common, possibly random, accumulated interest rate cancels. If σ=σ′, this ratio is a positive constant, so the prices are perfectly correlated.
Conversely, perfect correlation would give St​=cSt′​+d almost surely for constants c>0,d. If σ=σ′, the displayed ratio is a nonconstant variable with a lognormal distribution with full support (0,∞). If d>0, the ratio would always exceed c, and if d<0 it would always be below c, both impossible. If d=0, the ratio would be constant, also impossible. Therefore ρ(St​,St′​)=1⟺σ=σ′​, whenever the stated terminal variances exist and are positive. No deterministic-interest assumption is needed. Degenerate terminal prices, for example zero volatilities and deterministic rates, have undefined correlation and must be excluded.

The principle terminal correlation does not identify adapted stock volatility exposes a false printed assertion for the correlation coefficient defined in the introduction. Take r=0, S0​=S0′​=1, and
St​=eBt​−t/2,St′​=21​(1+St​).
(10)
Then dSt​=St​dBt​ and
dSt′​=21​St​dBt​=St′​1+St​St​​dBt​.
(11)
Hence σt​=1 and σt′​=St​/(1+St​) are bounded. Both terminal prices have positive finite variance and are perfectly correlated, because one is a positive affine function of the other. Nevertheless ∫01​(σt​−σt′​)2dt=∫01​(1+St​)2dt​>0​ on every continuous finite stock path. Equal initial prices fix the means, not the affine slope.
Here is the exact general conclusion and the intended qualified proof. Bounded volatilities make these zero-rate stochastic exponentials true square-integrable martingales. Writing s0​=S0​=S0′​, perfect terminal correlation gives S1​−s0​=c(S1′​−s0​) for some c>0. Conditional expectation therefore gives Su​−s0​=c(Su′​−s0​) for every 0≤u≤1. Comparing stochastic integrals, the Itô isometry yields
σu​Su​=cσu′​Su′​for dudP-almost every (u,ω).
(12)
This relation need not imply equality of the volatilities.
The terminal proportionality identifies bounded stock volatility criterion repairs the statement. If one additionally assumes equal terminal variances, then c=1 and the whole stock paths coincide, so positivity gives σ=σ′ almost everywhere and the requested integral is zero. The same repair follows if terminal proportionality S1​=cS1′​ is assumed: equal martingale means force c=1, after which the conditional-expectation and Itô isometry argument applies. This proves the intended conclusion under a sufficient additional hypothesis without treating centered correlation as uncentered proportionality.

For the quadratic Ornstein-Uhlenbeck state-price density, apply the Itô's formula under the reference probability measure. Since f′(x)=x and f′′(x)=1,
dζt​=e−αtσXt​dBt​−e−αt[αa−21​σ2+(λ+21​α)Xt2​]dt.
(15)
The stochastic integral is a true martingale on finite horizons by the finite moments of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. A uniform sufficient condition is α≥σ2/(2a)​, making the finite-variation drift nonpositive. This is also the condition for nonpositive drift at every state.
Use the positive state-price density ζ to price a terminal payoff H by Πt​(H)=E[ζT​H∣Ft​]/ζt​. The riskless account must cancel the drift of ζ, giving the short rate
rt​=a+Xt2​/2αa−σ2/2+(λ+α/2)Xt2​​=α+a+Xt2​/2λXt2​−σ2/2​.​
(16)
This pricing system is consistent with a money-market risk-neutral measure: with Mt​=e∫0t​ru​du, the process ζt​Mt​/ζ0​ is a stochastic exponential whose diffusion coefficient σXt​/f(Xt​) is bounded. The Novikov condition makes it a true density martingale. The original Brownian motion is for the reference measure, not automatically for this new pricing measure.
For X0​=x0​, the OU second moment is EXT2​=x02​e−2λT+σ2(1−e−2λT)/(2λ). Thus the zero-coupon bond price is
P(0,T)=e−αTa+21​x02​a+21​x02​e−2λT+4λσ2​(1−e−2λT)​.​
(17)
Conditionally at time t, replace T by T−t and x0​ by Xt​.
The short rate is nonnegative under the condition above, increases with Xt2​, and lies between α−σ2/(2a) and α+2λ. The model is tractable but imposes fixed rate bounds, identical rates for opposite values of X, and a restricted one-factor term structure. Its long-maturity yield is α. These structural restrictions, including exclusion of negative rates under the supermartingale condition, may be unsuitable for a general interest-rate fit.

For secant domination for expected utility derivatives, at each sample value x set gx​(θ)=U(θx). This is a differentiable concave function, regardless of the sign of x. Fix θ0​ and δ>0. For θ∈[θ0​−δ,θ0​+δ], concavity bounds its derivative between the two outer secant slopes:
δgx​(θ0​+2δ)−gx​(θ0​+δ)​≤gx′​(θ)≤δgx​(θ0​−δ)−gx​(θ0​−2δ)​.
(19)
Because U<0, finiteness of F means E∣U(ηX)∣<∞ at each of these four endpoints. Their absolute values divided by δ therefore give a common integrable bound on ∣gX′​(θ)∣. Difference quotients satisfy the same bound by the mean value theorem. The dominated convergence theorem permits differentiation under the expectation, and pointwise continuity of XU′(θX) with the same bound gives continuity of the derivative. Hence F′(θ)=E[XU′(θX)]​ and F∈C1(R). This argument proves absolute integrability of the displayed derivative; no unjustified differentiation assumption is needed.

A strictly increasing differentiable concave utility function on all of R has U′(x)>0 everywhere. Otherwise monotonicity and the decreasing derivative would make it constant on a right half-line. Its tangent at zero also shows U(x)→−∞ as x→−∞.
If X has only one sign, the stated second alternative already holds. Otherwise both P(X<0) and P(X>0) are positive. As θ→∞, −U(θX) tends to infinity on {X<0}; as θ→−∞, it does so on {X>0}. Since −U≥0, Fatou lemma implies F(θ)→−∞ at both ends. Continuity gives a finite maximizer θ∗​, and differentiability gives F′(θ∗​)=0.
For optimal marginal utility as a one-period pricing density, put Z=U′(θ∗​X). It is strictly positive. On {∣X∣≥1} it is bounded by ∣X∣U′(θ∗​X), whose expectation is finite by part ii. On {∣X∣<1} continuity bounds U′ on the compact interval [−∣θ∗​∣,∣θ∗​∣]. Thus Z is integrable and XZ is absolutely integrable. Consequently E[XZ]=0​. Normalizing Z by its positive expectation, if desired, makes it a probability density with the same zero pricing expectation.

On each open half-line the sample objective is a utility of a linear gain: U(θ(X−ε)) on the positive half-line, and U(θ(X+ε)) on the negative half-line. On a compact subinterval wholly inside either half-line, the secant domination argument from the frictionless part ii applies verbatim, using finiteness of G at four surrounding points. The dominated convergence theorem therefore gives the derivatives
G′(θ)={E[(X−ε)U′(θ(X−ε))],E[(X+ε)U′(θ(X+ε))],​θ>0,θ<0.​​
(21)
They are continuous on their respective half-lines. Near zero, the same outer secants of the globally concave sample objective bound its one-sided slopes. Dominated convergence consequently gives
G+′​(0)=U′(0)(EX−ε),G−′​(0)=U′(0)(EX+ε).
(22)
Here E∣X∣<∞: the frictionless derivative at zero already proves it, since U′(0)>0. Thus zero generally has a transaction-cost kink; when ε=0 the two formulas agree.

For marginal utility pricing with proportional transaction costs, if X−ε≥0 almost surely or −X−ε≥0 almost surely, the source's second alternative holds. Otherwise P(X<ε)>0 and P(X>−ε)>0. The Fatou lemma argument now gives G(θ)→−∞ at both ends, since a large positive holding loses on the first event and a large negative holding loses on the second. Let θ∗​ be a finite maximizer.
If θ∗​>0, set Z0​=U′(θ∗​(X−ε)). The derivative condition gives E[XZ0​]=εEZ0​. As before, split according to ∣X−ε∣≥1 to obtain integrability of Z0​ from the absolutely integrable derivative, with a compact-set bound on the complement. If θ∗​<0, use Z0​=U′(θ∗​(X+ε)) and obtain E[XZ0​]=−εEZ0​. In either case normalize Z=Z0​/EZ0​; then Z>0, EZ=1, and XZ is absolutely integrable.
If θ∗​=0, a concave maximum has G−′​(0)≥0≥G+′​(0), so EX∈[−ε,ε]. Choose Z=1. In every case of this first alternative, E[XZ]∈[−ε,ε]​. The normalization establishes a stronger, economically meaningful statement than the literal unnormalized condition: the expected gain under the resulting density lies inside the transaction-cost spread.

For zero drift, the reflection principle gives the known Brownian first-passage time density
h0​(t)=2πt3​a​e−a2/(2t),t>0.
(23)
To obtain a general drift, on a fixed finite horizon use the exponential martingale Mt​=ecBt​−c2t/2 as change-of-measure density. The Novikov condition holds for constant c, and the Girsanov theorem says that the coordinate process B has drift c under the new measure. Thus the hitting law of B under that measure is the law of B+ct under the original measure. Conditional expectation of MT​ at a hitting time bounded by T equals its stopped value, by the martingale optional sampling theorem. At Ha​=t this value is eca−c2t/2. The drifted Brownian first-passage density is obtained by multiplying h0​, giving hc​(t)=ae−(a−ct)2/(2t)/2πt3​​.
To verify the integrated formula, put u=(a−cT)/T​ and v=(−a−cT)/T​. The identity e2acϕ(v)=ϕ(u) for the standard normal density gives
dTd​[Φ(u)+e2acΦ(v)]=ϕ(u)2T3/2a+cT+a−cT​=hc​(T).
(24)
Both terms vanish as T↓0, so P(Ha​≤T)=Φ((a−cT)/T​)+e2acΦ((−a−cT)/T​)​. The distribution has total mass one for c≥0 and mass e2ac for c<0; in the latter case the remaining probability is an atom at infinity.

Under the Black-Scholes model pricing measure, write logSt​/σ=Bt​+ct, where c=(r−σ2/2)/σ. Brownian continuity and immediate crossing on reaching a level make the strict up-crossing time equal almost surely to the hitting time Ha​. Its finite-time law has no atom at T, so the convention before rather than at expiry does not affect the price.
For truncated discounted Brownian first passage, since payment occurs at the hitting time, discount there, not at expiry. The one-touch option price is
V0​=E[e−rHa​1{Ha​≤T}]=∫0T​e−rthc​(t)dt.
(25)
Set d=c2+2r​=∣r+σ2/2∣/σ. The expression under the square root is nonnegative for every real r, by this Black-Scholes identity. The discounted density satisfies e−rthc​(t)=ea(c−d)hd​(t). Applying part i yields the closed price
V0​=ea(c−d)Φ(T​dT−a​)+ea(c+d)Φ(T​−a−dT​).​
