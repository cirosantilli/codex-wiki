Let the fluid approach a uniform, quiescent state (ρ0​,p0​,c0​) at infinity. Use the outward unit normal vector N=∇f/∣∇f∣, pointing from the object into the fluid. Write H=H(f) for the Heaviside step function, q=ρ−ρ0​, and δS​=δ(f)∣∇f∣. With the viscous stress tensor τij​, define
Pij​=(p−p0​)δij​−τij​,Tij​=ρui​uj​+Pij​−c02​qδij​,Li​=Pij​Nj​.
(1)
Thus Tij​ is the Lighthill stress tensor, and Li​ is the surface loading exerted on the fluid. The total force on the object has the opposite sign. Extend q by zero inside the object. Since the boundary is fixed and impermeable, u⋅N=0, and the distributional mass conservation and momentum conservation equations are
∂t​(Hq)+∂i​(Hρui​)=0,
(2)
∂t​(Hρui​)+c02​∂i​(Hq)+∂j​(HTij​)=Li​δS​.
(3)
For example, the surface contribution to the momentum equation is (ρui​uj​+Pij​)Nj​δS​=Li​δS​. Differentiating the first equation in time and subtracting the divergence of the second gives the fixed-body Ffowcs Williams-Hawkings equation:
(∂t2​−c02​Δ)(Hq)=∂i​∂j​(HTij​)−∂i​(Li​δS​).​
(4)
There is no acoustic thickness noise: a fixed impermeable boundary injects no mass. Apply the retarded acoustic Green function δ(t−R/c0​)/(4πc02​R), where R=∣x−y∣. The supplied surface-delta identity converts the loading term to a surface integral. For an exterior observer, the required integral equation is
q(x,t)=4πc02​1​∂xi​​∂xj​​∫f(y)>0​RTij​(y,t−R/c0​)​d3y−4πc02​1​∂xi​​∫f(y)=0​RLi​(y,t−R/c0​)​dSy​.​
(5)
This acoustic analogy is an exact rearrangement of the fluid equations; source approximations enter later. In the distant linear acoustic region, p′=c02​q.
The acoustic far field requires observer distance r much larger than the source extent ℓ and k0​r≫1, so derivatives of the retarded phase dominate derivatives of 1/R. The acoustic compact-source approximation requires k0​ℓ≪1, so the source's differential propagation delays can be neglected. These conditions concern different lengths and neither implies the other. Put n=x/r, tr​=t−r/c0​, and define the loading and stress moments
Fi​(t)=∫S​Li​(y,t)dSy​,Qij​(t)=∫f>0​Tij​(y,t)d3y.
(6)
The leading compact acoustic far field is
p′(x,t)≃4πr1​[c0​ni​​F˙i​(tr​)+c02​ni​nj​​Q¨​ij​(tr​)].​
(7)
The surface term is an acoustic dipole; the volume term is an acoustic quadrupole. The plus sign of the loading term follows from the minus sign of its spatial divergence and the derivative of retarded time.
For the power comparison, assume low Mach number M=U/c0​, high Reynolds number, eddy size ℓ, characteristic time τ=ℓ/U, and stress magnitude ρ0​U2. Keep the prescribed turbulent region and its leading volume stress comparable when inserting the object; neglect changes of source statistics and exceptional cancellations of the leading moments. Compactness follows from ℓ/(c0​τ)=M≪1. Since Q∼ρ0​U2ℓ3 and the outgoing acoustic intensity is ⟨p′2⟩/(ρ0​c0​), the volume power is
PQ​∼c05​ρ0​U8ℓ2​=ρ0​c03​ℓ2M8.​
(8)
If the object's size is comparable to ℓ and its fluctuating resultant loading is F∼ρ0​U2ℓ2, then
PD​∼c03​ρ0​U6ℓ2​=ρ0​c03​ℓ2M6,PD​/PQ​∼M−2.​
(9)
Angular factors and dimensionless loading coefficients have been omitted. The acoustic dipole and acoustic quadrupole interference vanishes in the leading power integrated over a full sphere, by odd angular parity; individual directions can show interference.
A size threshold also requires a loading model. For a small nonseparating object of size a≪ℓ in an approximately inviscid, smoothly varying incident eddy, a spatially uniform pressure has zero resultant on its closed surface. The first surviving loading is the pressure-gradient or added mass force, F∼ρ0​a3U/τ=ρ0​U2a3/ℓ. Under these explicitly stated assumptions,
PD​∼c03​ℓ4ρ0​U6a6​,PQ​PD​​∼M2(a/ℓ)6​.
(10)
The two mechanisms become comparable at a∼ℓM1/3; for smaller objects the cases with and without the object have the same leading order of power. This is the small-body loading-noise threshold. It is not a geometry-independent size law: a separated drag force F∼ρ0​U2a2 fluctuating on the original eddy time instead gives PD​/PQ​∼(a/ℓ)4/M2 and a∼ℓM​. If both force and time are set by body-scale eddies, τ=a/U, their loading power is ρ0​U6a2/c03​ and comparison with the original volume source gives a∼Mℓ. Each estimate states which source time and force it uses.
The physical distinction is momentum conservation. Unforced turbulence in a fluid supplies internal stresses, whose net-force contribution cancels and leaves acoustic quadrupole radiation. The supported object exchanges momentum with the fluid and the external support, permitting a time-dependent resultant force and acoustic dipole radiation. One fewer retarded derivative makes the loading radiation more efficient at low Mach number.

ϕ​(k,y)=∫R​eikxϕ(x,y)dx,ϕ(x,y)=2π1​∫Γ​e−ikxϕ​(k,y)dk.
(11)
A right Half-range Fourier transform is analytic above its convergence line and a left transform below it. For the limiting absorption principle with this time convention take ω=ωr​−iε, ωr​>0, ε>0, before taking ε↓0. Set
γ(k)=k2−ω2​,Reγ(k)>0(k∈R).

Across the fluid part of y=0, the field and its normal derivative are continuous; on the sheet both sides have the same normal velocity. The outgoing Helmholtz equation solutions therefore have equal normal derivatives and opposite scattered traces:
ϕ​(k,y)={21​J(k)e−γy,−21​J(k)eγy,​y>0,y<0,​ϕ​y​(k,0)=−2γJ​.
(13)
The jump J is supported on x<0, and so is a minus transform.
The linear fluid equation iωvy​=−∂y​ρ′ and the sheet velocity vy​=iωη imply ∂y​ρ′=ω2η on either face. The incident contribution to the left derivative transform is ωsinθ0​/(k+ωcosθ0​). Writing η−=∫−∞0​eikxη(x)dx, it follows that
−2γJ​=ω2η−−k+ωcosθ0​ωsinθ0​​+d+.

η′′​−=s−k2η−,J=(mω2−Tk2)η−+Ts.
(15)
Combining these identities gives the Wiener-Hopf equation
K(k)J(k)+d+(k)=F(k),K(k)=2γ(k)​+mω2−Tk2ω2​,​
(16)
F(k)=k+ωcosθ0​ωsinθ0​​+mω2−Tk2Tω2s​.

This is the plane-wave forcing of a pinned elastic half-sheet.
Let b=ωm/T​ and assume initially that the distinguished points are distinct. Besides the branch points ±ω, K has simple poles at ±b. Its physical-sheet zeros ±ks​ solve the fluid-loaded dispersion relation
γ(ks​)(Tks2​−mω2)=2ω2.
(18)
For real positive frequency there is exactly one positive real root ks​>max(ωr​,ωr​m/T​): the left side increases strictly from zero to infinity on that interval. Equivalently, with q=γ(ks​)>0, Tq3+(T−m)ωr2​q−2ωr2​=0. Its other roots have negative real part and do not belong to this outgoing sheet. Continue ks​ with small absorption; +ω,+b,+ks​ lie below the real line and their negative partners above it. The Wiener-Hopf factorization assigns the lower branch cut and pole to K+, and the upper branch cut and pole to K−. The continued K+ has a zero at +ks​, and K− at −ks​; these are poles of their reciprocals, rather than poles of the factors. Branch cuts can be drawn away from the real line into the corresponding half-planes.
For this standard assignment a common strip of analyticity and nonvanishing is
D={k:−d<Imk<d},d=min{ε, εm/T​, −Imks​}>0.​
(19)
The strip shrinks to the real contour as absorption is removed. The factors continue meromorphically beyond their designated half-planes; the allocation of the poles and branch points is part of the radiation condition. The incident pole −ωcosθ0​ belongs above the inversion contour. For incidence with 0<θ0​<π/2 the real contour is a direct choice. Other nongrazing angles are obtained by analytic continuation with the incident pole kept on its prescribed side, deforming the contour locally if necessary. Coincident distinguished points and grazing incidence are treated by the corresponding limits, not by dividing by a coincident pole.
Divide the Wiener-Hopf equation by K+ and use pole subtraction in a Wiener-Hopf equation. With a=ωcosθ0​, the minus part of F/K+ is
B−(k)=K+(−a)(k+a)ωsinθ0​​+2bK+(−b)(k+b)ω2s​.
(20)
Indeed these are exactly the incident-pole and upper bare-sheet-pole residues. At the lower bare-sheet pole +b, the forcing pole is canceled by the pole of K+ before splitting. Thus B+=F/K+−B− is analytic on the plus side of the contour. The separated equation is
K−J−B−=B+−d+/K+=E.
(21)
With the stipulated E=0,
J=K−B−​,d+=K+B+=F−K+B−.
(22)
The scattered field is consequently
ϕ(x,y)=4πsgny​∫Γ​e−ikx−γ(k)∣y∣K−(k)B−(k)​dk,y=0.​
(23)
The full time-dependent density perturbation is the incident field plus eiωtϕ. The two traces at y=0 follow by limiting from above and below. A constant rescaling of the two factors does not change this field.
To determine the endpoint slope, reconstruct the sheet transform using the fluid condition:
η−(k)=ω21​[−2γJ​−d++k+aωsinθ0​​]=mω2−Tk2J−Ts​.
(24)
At +b the continued K+ has residue −ω2/(2TbK−(b)). Therefore the derivative transform d+ generically has the lower-plane pole
Resk=b​d+=−2bω2s​+2TbK−(b)ω2B−(b)​.
(25)
This pole produces a forbidden incoming bare-sheet wave in the reconstructed displacement: eiωt−ibx travels towards the endpoint from x=−∞. A minus transform of the specified outgoing solution must be analytic there. The incoming-pole cancellation at a pinned membrane edge is precisely
B−(b)=TsK−(b),J(b)=Ts.​
(26)
For generic parameters this determines
s=K+(−a)(b+a)[TK−(b)−4b2K+(−b)ω2​]ωsinθ0​​.​
(27)
The lower pole is removed; the upper zero −ks​ of K− can still produce the legitimate outgoing evanescent acoustic surface wave, whose phase travels to negative x. This distinction separates the spurious bare-sheet wave from the coupled fluid-loaded mode. No explicit factorization has been used.

Use the scattering potential convention V=k02​(n2−1). With time dependence e−iωt the outgoing Green function is G0​(r,r′)=eik0​∣r−r′∣/(4π∣r−r′∣) and satisfies (Δ+k02​)G0​=−δ. The total-field Lippmann-Schwinger equation is
ψ(r)=ψ0​(r)+∫D​G0​(r,r′)V(r′)ψ(r′)d3r′.
(28)
Replacing the field in the integral by the incident field gives the first Born approximation for scalar wave scattering:
ψB​=ψ0​+ψ1​,ψ1​(r)=∫D​G0​(r,r′)V(r′)ψ0​(r′)d3r′.​
(29)
For the first Rytov approximation, write ψ=ψ0​eχ on a region where the incident field is nonzero and a continuous complex logarithm can be chosen. The logarithmic wave perturbation equation is
Δχ+2∇logψ0​⋅∇χ+∇χ⋅∇χ=−V.
(30)
The last dot product is bilinear, not a squared modulus. Dropping it leaves a linear equation for χ1​. Multiplication by ψ0​ shows that ψ0​χ1​ satisfies the same outgoing inhomogeneous Helmholtz equation as ψ1​. Hence
χ1​=ψ0​ψ1​​,ψR​=ψ0​exp(ψ0​ψ1​​).​
(31)
Introduce a small multiplier λ on V. Then ψ1​=O(λ) and
ψR​=ψ0​+ψ1​+2ψ0​ψ12​​+O(λ3)=ψB​+O(λ2).
(32)
The two total-field approximations agree to first order. Exponentiating the first logarithmic wave perturbation does not supply the generally missing second-order Born series contribution.

The zero-mean fluctuation is W, not the refractive index n: ⟨n⟩=1. Also the full scattering potential has a nonzero mean and is not Gaussian:
V=2μk02​W+μ2k02​W2,V=μ2k02​.
(36)
The usual Gaussian closure requires W to be a jointly Gaussian random field, not merely to have a Gaussian distribution at each point. We first give the intended leading weak-fluctuation result with the linearized potential V1​=2μk02​W, then account for the quadratic term.
Keep the definitions q,B from the preceding solution and let χ1​=B∫D​V1​(y)e−iq⋅ydy. Because the incident field has unit modulus,
I=⟨eχ1​+χ1∗​⟩,h(y)=Be−iq⋅y+B∗eiq⋅y=2Re(Be−iq⋅y).
(37)
The exponent is a real Gaussian linear functional Z=∫D​hV1​. If CW​(y−z)=⟨W(y)W(z)⟩, the linearized potential has autocorrelation function of a random field C1​(s)=4μ2k04​CW​(s). Complete the square in the one-dimensional Gaussian density of Z: ⟨eZ⟩=exp(⟨Z⟩+VarZ/2). Here its mean is zero, so
Ilin​=exp{21​∫D​∫D​h(y)h(z)C1​(y−z)dydz}.​
(38)
This is Gaussian intensity in the first Rytov approximation. In a more explicit complex notation, put
J1​=∫D​∫D​C1​(y−z)e−iq⋅(y−z)dydz,J2​=∫D​∫D​C1​(y−z)e−iq⋅(y+z)dydz.
(39)
Then
Ilin​=exp{∣B∣2J1​+Re(B2J2​)}.​
(40)
The second term is the pseudo-covariance of a complex random variable contribution. It does not vanish for an arbitrary finite real medium. The real-kernel expression shows that the combined exponent is nonnegative, even though its two complex-form contributions need not be separately nonnegative.
For the full potential, Gaussian moment pairing theorem gives its centered covariance function
CV​(s)=Cov(V(y),V(y+s))=4μ2k04​CW​(s)+2μ4k04​CW​(s)2.
(41)
Its uncentered autocorrelation is RV​(s)=CV​(s)+μ4k04​. The first Rytov approximation is linear in V, but its intensity is an exponential average; applying a Gaussian formula exactly to this quadratic potential would be incorrect. A consistent expansion through order μ2 is
logI=2μ2k02​Re[B∫D​e−iq⋅ydy]+21​∫D​∫D​h(y)h(z)C1​(y−z)dydz+O(μ4).​
(42)
The first term is the coherent mean-potential correction. Odd powers vanish by the symmetry of the joint Gaussian field. The variance term can equally use CV​=RV​−V2 through this order. Thus the mean shift must not be discarded while retaining the order-μ2 fluctuation intensity.
An exact expression within the far-field, first-Rytov approximation model is also available when the full n2 is retained. Assume the restriction of W to D is a Gaussian element of the real Hilbert space L2(D), with covariance operator C of kernel CW​(y−z). Let H denote multiplication by h, ℓh​=2μk02​h, Q=μ2k02​C1/2HC1/2 and bh​=C1/2ℓh​. The Gaussian quadratic exponential moment is
I=det(I−2Q)−1/2exp[21​⟨bh​,(I−2Q)−1bh​⟩].​
(43)
Here the determinant is the Fredholm determinant; require I−2Q strictly positive for finiteness. Diagonalize the self-adjoint trace-class operator Q and complete the square in each Gaussian coordinate to derive the formula. On a bounded D, unit pointwise variance makes trC=∣D∣, so the covariance is trace class under the stated measurable L2 hypothesis. Small enough μ satisfies the positivity condition. Expanding the determinant and inverse recovers the preceding mean and covariance terms. For small μ this is also determined by the potential autocorrelation: since CW​∈[−1,1],
CW​(s)=μ2−1+1+CV​(s)/(2k04​)​​,CV​=RV​−μ4k04​,
(44)
using the branch near zero. This makes explicit the extra joint-Gaussian assumption behind an exact correlation-based answer.
Finally, Gaussian marginals alone are insufficient. Take X∼N(0,1) and independent random signs Sj​. The stationary sequence Wj​=Sj​X has Gaussian marginals and covariance ⟨Wi​Wj​⟩=δij​, just like independent standard Gaussian variables. However ⟨et(W1​+W2​)⟩=(1+e2t2)/2, whereas for the independent Gaussian pair it is et2. The different intensities show why the joint hypothesis is needed. The mean-zero hint is interpreted for W, and the linearized Gaussian formula is not claimed to be exact for V=k02​(n2−1).

Use the inner product convention linear in its first argument. A singular value system consists of positive singular values σn​ and orthonormal bases un​ of (kerA)⊥⊂X and vn​ of ranA⊂Y, with
Aun​=σn​vn​,A∗vn​=σn​un​.
(45)
For an infinite-rank compact operator, σn​→0; a finite-rank operator has a finite system. Applying the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators to A∗A constructs un​, with eigenvalues σn2​, and then vn​=Aun​/σn​. This solution uses the input/output naming of this problem, which is the reverse of another common singular-vector convention.
The Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator is
x†=A†y=n∑​σn​⟨y,vn​⟩​un​.​
(46)
Its domain is ranA⊕(ranA)⊥, equivalently the data satisfying the Picard criterion
∑n​σn2​∣⟨y,vn​⟩∣2​<∞.
(47)
It ignores the component in kerA∗ and lies in (kerA)⊥. The series then converges in X by orthonormality and is the minimum-norm least-squares solution. If y∈ranA, it is an exact solution; all other exact solutions are x†+z with z∈kerA. Compactness does not make the inverse bounded when there are infinitely many positive singular values.

A linear regularization is a family of bounded operators Rα​:Y→X such that Rα​y→A†y for every y in the domain of the Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator as α↓0. For noisy data ∥yδ−y∥≤δ, a convergent regularization of an inverse problem also specifies a parameter choice α(δ,yδ) giving convergence to A†y, uniformly over the allowed noise at each fixed admissible exact datum.
Expand xα​ in the input singular vectors. The Tikhonov normal equation implies
(α+σn2​)⟨xα​,un​⟩=σn​⟨y,vn​⟩.
(48)
The component in kerA is zero because α>0. Comparing with the given filter convention gives
fα​(σ)=α+σ2σ2​,Rα​y=n∑​α+σn2​σn​​⟨y,vn​⟩un​.​
(49)
The singular-system Tikhonov filter is often described by its gain gα​(σ)=σ/(α+σ2); the requested fα​ includes one extra factor of σ. For admissible data, fα​→1 and 0≤fα​≤1, so dominated convergence theorem applied to the squared Picard criterion coefficients proves exact-data convergence. Also
∥Rα​∥≤supσ≥0​α+σ2σ​=2α​1​.
(50)
By the noise-bias decomposition for linear regularization, α(δ)→0 and δ/α(δ)​→0 suffice for noisy-data convergence; for example α=δ as δ↓0. This establishes both consistency and stability of Tikhonov regularization.

For f,g∈L2(0,1), interchange the integrals in the inner product of the Volterra integration operator:
⟨Af,g⟩=∫01​∫0x​f(t)g(x)​dtdx=∫01​f(t)∫t1​g(x)dx​dt.
(51)
Thus the adjoint operator is A∗g(t)=∫t1​g(x)dx. The triangular integration kernel is square-integrable, so A is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator and hence a compact operator.
Set an​=(n−21​)π and σn​=1/an​. Direct integration gives
Aun​(x)=2​∫0x​cos(an​t)dt=σn​2​sin(an​x)=σn​vn​(x),
(52)
A∗vn​(x)=2​∫x1​sin(an​t)dt=σn​2​cos(an​x)=σn​un​(x),
(53)
since cosan​=0. Product-to-sum identities give ⟨un​,uj​⟩=⟨vn​,vj​⟩=δnj​. For completeness, g=A∗Af satisfies g′′=−f, g′(0)=0, g(1)=0. The associated Sturm-Liouville problem has precisely the complete mixed-boundary cosine basis un​; AA∗ gives the mixed-boundary sine basis vn​, with vn​(0)=0, vn′​(1)=0. Both A and A∗ are injective, since differentiation of their zero integrals recovers their inputs. Their singular vectors therefore cover the entire two Hilbert spaces, rather than just proper orthogonal complements.
The given functions form the complete singular value system, with σn​=2/((2n−1)π). This is the mixed-boundary singular system of the Volterra operator.

Substitution into the Tikhonov regularization series gives an explicit reconstruction for every y∈L2(0,1):
xα​(x)=n=1∑∞​1+αan2​an​​[∫01​y(t)2​sin(an​t)dt]2​cos(an​x),an​=(n−21​)π.​
(54)
Equivalently the summand is σn​⟨y,vn​⟩un​/(α+σn2​). The Tikhonov stability bound makes the series converge in L2 for every positive α. It suppresses high-frequency components of unstable differentiation, rather than differentiating arbitrary noisy data pointwise.
The range of the Volterra integration operator is {y∈H1(0,1):y(0)=0}. On exactly these data, A†y=y′ and the series converges to y′ as α↓0.
