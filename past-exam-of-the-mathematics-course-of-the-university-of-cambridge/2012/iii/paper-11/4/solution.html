The key is to widen the forbidden intersection while controlling a product of densities. We first derive the two ingredients, with constants explicit enough to yield the numerical base in the question.
For two nonempty set families F,G on m coordinates, suppose every cross-intersection has size greater than an integer w≥0. Their complements are separated from F by Hamming distance greater than w. Let a be the least integer with ∣F∣≤Sm​(a), where Sm​(a)=∑j=0a​(jm​). Iterating Harper inequality, and using ∣F∣>Sm​(a−1), shows that its radius-w neighbourhood has at least Sm​(a+w−1) vertices. The complementary set family is disjoint from that neighbourhood, so
∣F∣∣G∣≤Sm​(a)Sm​(m−a−w).
(22)
For a=0 the needed weaker neighbourhood bound follows simply by taking the ball around its single vertex; define Sm​(−1)=0. If a displayed index forces a full neighbourhood, the other set family would be empty, already excluded.
The provided binary entropy function bound gives
Sm​(t)≤2mexp[−m2​(m/2−t)+2​].
(23)
Indeed, for t≤m/2 this follows from H2​(1/2−v)≤1−2v2/log2, obtained by integrating H2′′​≤−4/log2; for larger t use Sm​(t)≤2m. The two nonnegative deficits in the preceding product have sum at least w. Their squared sum is at least w2/2. Thus the cross-intersection bound from cube separation is
μm​(F)μm​(G)≤e−w2/m​,μm​(F)=∣F∣/2m.
(24)
Now suppose a pair of set families forbids every cross-intersection in [a,b]⊆[0,m]. Put ρ=μm​(F)μm​(G), and fix δ=1/50. Split both set families by the last coordinate. The following three replacements preserve the indicated forbidden intervals on m−1 coordinates:
new families(F1​,G1​)(F0​,G0​∪G1​)(F1​,G0​∩G1​)​new forbidden interval[a−1,b−1][a,b][a−1,b]​​
(25)
For example, in the third row the same second set has witnesses in both sections of G, so its intersection with a first-section member must avoid both shifted intervals; their union is [a−1,b].
If the first replacement increases the density product by a factor at least 1+δ, use it. Otherwise interchange the names of the two set families if needed so that
x=μm​(F)μm−1​(F1​)​≤1+δ​.
(26)
If the second replacement gives a factor at least 1+δ, use it. If neither does, use the third. To bound its loss, put
z=μm​(G)μm−1​(G0​∪G1​)​.
(27)
We have z≥1, (2−x)z<1+δ, and consequently 1−δ<x≤1+δ​≤1+δ/2. The third replacement's product factor is
x(2−z)>h(x),h(x)=2−xx(3−δ−2x)​.
(28)
Since h′′(x)=−4(1+δ)/(2−x)3<0, its minimum on [1−δ,1+δ/2] is at an endpoint. There
h(1−δ)=1−δ,h(1+δ/2)=1−δ−1−δ/2δ2​≥1−δ−2δ2.
(29)
Thus each step either gains a factor 1+δ without increasing interval width, or widens the interval by one and loses at most a factor 1−δ−2δ2. This is the forbidden-intersection density increment, with its loss proved rather than hidden in an asymptotic error.
Start with F=G=A and a=b=ℓ. Stop when a=0 or b=m. Dimension drops at every step. The positive lower product factors ensure that a nonempty starting pair stays nonempty; it therefore cannot reach a forbidden interval containing all 0,…,m. Write v for the number of widening steps, u for the number of gain steps, and put
g=log(1.02),h=−log(0.9792),D=g+h.
(30)
The terminal density product ρ∗​ satisfies ρ∗​≥ρ0​egu−hv. Always v≤ℓ, since a widening step lowers a by one.
If the procedure stops at a=0, the terminal forbidden interval is [0,v]. Every cross-intersection is therefore greater than v, and the cross-intersection bound from cube separation gives ρ∗​≤e−v2/m. At least ℓ steps were needed to lower a to zero, so u≥ℓ−v. Since m≤n,
logρ0​≤−gℓ+Dv−v2/n≤−gℓ+D2n/4​.
(31)
If instead the procedure stops at b=m, the number of steps is at least n−ℓ, so u≥n−ℓ−v. Using ρ∗​≤1 and v≤ℓ gives
logρ0​≤−g(n−ℓ)+Dv≤−gn+(2g+h)ℓ​.
(32)
These estimates hold for every nonempty pair forbidding the original intersection, with ρ0​=(∣A∣/2n)2 in our application.
For ℓ=n/4, the two positive exponential decay rates are, respectively,
g/4−D2/4>0.00453,g−(2g+h)/4>0.00464.
(33)
Both exceed c=−2log(1.999/2)<0.001001. Hence ρ0​≤e−cn and
∣A∣≤2ne−cn/2=1.999n.​
(34)
The empty set family is immediate. This proves the numerical bound directly from Harper inequality and the supplied binary entropy function estimate.
For ℓ=⌊n/8⌋, the identical forbidden-intersection density increment applies. For n≥17, use ℓ≥n/8−7/8 in the first stopping estimate. Its decay rate is at least
g/8−D2/4−8n7g​≥g/8−D2/4−7g/136>0.001039>c.
(35)
For the second stopping estimate, ℓ≤n/8 gives the still stronger rate g−(2g+h)/8>0.01222. At n=16 the floor is exact and the first rate is g/8−D2/4>0.002058>c.
It remains to handle 1≤n≤15 without silently absorbing a constant into an exponential. Then ℓ is zero or one. Fix an ℓ-set T. The 2n−ℓ sets containing T are paired as T∪S and T∪([n]∖(T∪S)), and each pair intersects in exactly T. Since n>ℓ, these are distinct pairs, and at most one member of each belongs to A. Therefore
∣A∣≤2n−2n−ℓ−1≤43​2n<1.999n.
(36)
For n=0, the forbidden self-intersection is zero, so the set family is empty. We have proved the claimed base for all dimensions in the second case as well.
Finally, the same base is not forced by a forbidden intersection near 2n/3. Take n=2000 and every subset of size at most 1000. Every intersection has size at most 1000, below ⌊2n/3⌋=1333, including self-intersections. By pairing complementary sets this set family has more than 21999 members, whereas
1.9992000=22000(0.9995)2000<21999,
(37)
since 2000log(0.9995)<−1<−log2. Thus it is a large family avoiding a high intersection, and it contradicts the proposed bound.
