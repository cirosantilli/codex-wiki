Choose a separation SpecR=U⊔V into two nonempty clopen sets. Both are closed in the Zariski topology, so write U=V(I) and V=V(J) for ideals I,J. Their disjointness gives V(I+J)=∅. A proper ideal lies in a maximal ideal, so this forces I+J=R.
Their union is all of the spectrum of a commutative ring, and V(I)∪V(J)=V(IJ). Thus every prime ideal contains IJ, so
IJ⊆⋂p∈SpecR​p=(0)​=0.
(1)
The last equality uses that R is a reduced ring. For comaximal ideals, I∩J=IJ: if i+j=1 and r∈I∩J, then r=ri+rj∈IJ. Therefore the Chinese remainder theorem gives
R≅R/I×R/J​.
(2)
The isomorphism sends r to its two residue classes. Its kernel is I∩J=0; for surjectivity, amodI and bmodJ are obtained from aj+bi, where i∈I, j∈J and i+j=1. The two factors are nonzero because U,V are nonempty, so I,J are proper.
This is a disconnected reduced spectrum product decomposition. The elements i,j also satisfy ij=0, i2=i, j2=j, exhibiting the ring product decomposition by an idempotent. No Noetherian hypothesis is required.

Put S=RG, the ring of invariants. It is an integral domain because it is a subring of the domain R. Embed FracS in FracR in the evident way.
Suppose q∈FracS is an integral element over S. Its monic equation has coefficients in S⊆R, so q is integral over R. Since R is a normal domain, q∈R.
Write q=a/b with a,b∈S and b=0. Each ring automorphism in G extends to the fraction field by acting on numerator and denominator, and fixes this fraction. Hence q∈RG=S. We have proved that S is integrally closed in its own fraction field:
RG is a normal domain​.
(3)
This normality of a ring of invariants argument actually works for any group; finiteness is not needed for this conclusion. For finite G, there is additionally an integral extension RG⊆R, since for each r∈R the orbit polynomial under a finite automorphism group
∏g∈G​(T−g(r))
(4)
is monic, has coefficients fixed by G and vanishes at r. Neither argument divides by ∣G∣, so it is valid when the characteristic divides the group order. Here normality means integral closedness of a domain.

Use y on the target and x on the source, so the map of coordinate rings sends y to x4. A closed point of the real affine line corresponds to a maximal ideal generated either by y−a, a∈R, or by a monic irreducible quadratic q(y). The scheme-theoretic fibre over that point has coordinate algebra
Bp​=R[x]⊗R[y]​κ(p)=R[x]/(qp​(x4)).
(5)
Here κ(p) is the residue field, and qp​=y−a in the real-point case. The fibre is a scheme, so its nilpotents must be retained.
For a>0, put r=a1/4. The factors x−r, x+r, x2+r2 are pairwise coprime, with the last one irreducible over R. The Chinese remainder theorem identifies Bp​ with R×R×C, giving three irreducible components.
For a<0, put r=(−a)1/4. The exact factorization is
x4+r4=(x2+2​rx+r2)(x2−2​rx+r2).
(6)
Each quadratic has discriminant −2r2<0, and they are distinct and coprime. Thus Bp​≅C×C, giving two irreducible components.
For a=0, Bp​=R[x]/(x4). It has one prime ideal, generated by the class of x, so one irreducible component. It is not the reduced point SpecR: its class of x is a nonzero nilpotent element with nilpotency index four.
Finally write the irreducible quadratic as qp​(y)=(y−z)(y−z), with z∈/R. Over C, qp​(x4) has four roots above z and four above z. They are all simple, because z=0, and the two sets are disjoint. None is real. Conjugation pairs them into four distinct irreducible real quadratics. Hence Bp​≅C4 and this fibre has four irreducible components.
The real fourth-power fibre classification is:

All fibres in each row are isomorphic as affine schemes over R. The rows are distinct: their numbers of irreducible components already distinguish them, and the zero fibre is additionally nonreduced. Therefore
exactly four real-scheme isomorphism classes​.
(7)
These are ordinary scheme components, not geometric components counted after complexifying the base. Counting only real roots would miss both the complex-residue factors and the nonreal closed points.

Let A=C[x,y,z]. This polynomial ring is a unique factorization domain of Krull dimension three. For a height-one prime in a unique factorization domain p, choose a nonzero element of it and an irreducible factor q belonging to p. In a unique factorization domain, q is prime, so (q) is a nonzero prime ideal contained in p. Strict containment would give a chain (0)⊊(q)⊊p of length two. Thus
htp=1⟹p=(q)​.
(8)
If p has height three, it is maximal: any strictly larger proper prime would extend a length-three chain and contradict dimA=3. The Weak Hilbert Nullstellensatz over the algebraically closed field C then gives
htp=3⟹p=(x−a,y−b,z−c)​
(9)
for some a,b,c∈C. This proves both requested generator assertions.
For the monomial curve with exponents three, four and five, put
g1​=y2−xz,g2​=yz−x3,g3​=z2−x2y,J=(g1​,g2​,g3​).
(10)
All three map to zero under the given parametrization, so J⊆I=kerφ. Reduce any monomial using y2↦xz, yz↦x3 and z2↦x2y. Each reduction decreases the sum of the exponents of y,z, so it terminates. Every polynomial has, modulo J, the form
A0​(x)+A1​(x)y+A2​(x)z.
(11)
Its image is A0​(t3)+t4A1​(t3)+t5A2​(t3). These three terms occupy different exponent classes modulo three, so they cannot cancel. A zero image forces each Ai​=0. Consequently
I=(y2−xz, yz−x3, z2−x2y)​.
(12)
The quotient is the domain C[t3,t4,t5], hence I is a prime ideal. It is finite free over C[t3], with basis of a module 1,t4,t5, by the same normal-form argument. Its dimension is therefore one, using that integral extensions preserve Krull dimension. The polynomial-ring height and dimension formula gives
htI=3−dim(A/I)=2​.
(13)
For the generator obstruction, let m=(x,y,z). Since I⊆m2, we have mI⊆m3. The degree-two initial forms of g1​,g2​,g3​ are
y2−xz,yz,z2.
(14)
They are linearly independent over C in m2/m3. Thus no nonzero constant linear combination of the gi​ belongs to mI, and their classes are independent in I/mI. Since the three generators span that quotient,
dimC​I/mI=3​.
(15)
Two global generators would span it by two vectors, which is impossible. This is the initial-form lower bound for ideal generators; it shows why height two does not force two generators, even though height one and height three behave as above.

We use Zorn's lemma, and no basis or other existence theorem from linear algebra. A subset B⊆V is linearly independent if every finite relation ∑b∈F​λb​b=0 forces every coefficient to be zero.
Order the linearly independent subsets by inclusion. This partially ordered set is nonempty, because the empty subset is independent. An empty chain has the empty subset as an upper bound. For any nonempty chain, its union is independent: every finite subset of that union lies in one member of the chain. Indeed choose one chain member containing each of its finitely many vectors, then take the largest among that finite collection of comparable members. Every finite relation in the union is therefore a relation in an independent chain member. The union is an upper bound.
Zorn's lemma gives a maximal independent subset B. If v were outside its span, then B∪{v} would still be independent. In a finite relation
λv+∑b∈F​λb​b=0,
(16)
a nonzero λ could be inverted in the field k, placing v in the span of B. If λ=0, independence of B forces the remaining coefficients to be zero. This contradicts maximality, so B spans V.
Define the free module on B as the direct sum k(B)=⨁b∈B​k: its elements are coefficient families with finite support. The map
Φ:k(B)⟶V,(λb​)⟼∑b​λb​b
(17)
is a module homomorphism. Spanning proves surjectivity; independence proves injectivity. Hence the vector-space freeness from maximal independence gives
V≅b∈B⨁​k​.
(18)
Finite support is essential; this is a direct sum, not an unrestricted product. The zero vector space uses B=∅ and is free of rank zero. The proof for arbitrary vector spaces explicitly uses the axiom of choice through Zorn's lemma.

A generic linear projection over an infinite field is not enough for the stated arbitrary field k. Instead use Noether normalization by weighted substitutions to make the defining polynomial monic in one coordinate.
For n>1, choose an integer M at least every exponent occurring in a nonzero monomial of f, put B=M+1, and use the triangular polynomial change of variables
xi​=yi​+ynBi​(1≤i<n),xn​=yn​.
(19)
Here M≥1 because the irreducible polynomial is nonconstant. This is an automorphism, with inverse yi​=xi​−xnBi​, yn​=xn​. A monomial x1α1​​⋯xnαn​​ contributes a highest yn​ power
w(α)=αn​+∑i=1n−1​αi​Bi.
(20)
These weights are all distinct: the exponents are base-B digits bounded by M. All other terms in the expansion have smaller yn​ degree than that monomial's weight. Therefore the highest-degree yn​ term in the transformed f comes from exactly one original monomial and has a nonzero coefficient in k, independent of the other yi​. No assumption about the cardinality or characteristic of k enters this argument.
Multiply by the inverse of that coefficient to obtain a monic polynomial F∈A[yn​] of some degree D>0, where A=k[y1​,…,yn−1​]. The same construction for n=1 simply rescales f to be monic over A=k.
The monic polynomial quotient is finite free because division by F gives a unique representative of degree less than D. Thus
k[x1​,…,xn​]/(f)≅A[yn​]/(F)≅A⊕Ayn​⊕⋯⊕AynD−1​​
(21)
as A-modules. Uniqueness follows because any nonzero multiple of a monic F has yn​ degree at least D. In particular the map A→A[yn​]/(F) is injective.
The induced morphism
X⟶SpecA=Akn−1​​
(22)
is finite, since its coordinate algebra is a finite module, and flat, since tensoring with a finite free module is a finite direct sum of copies of the original module and preserves exactness. This is a finite flat Noether normalization of an affine hypersurface, with the explicit projection coordinates yi​=xi​−xnBi​. The construction actually applies to any nonconstant polynomial, not only an irreducible one.

The characteristic of K is either a prime p or zero. In characteristic p, it is a finitely generated algebra over Fp​. The course's Zariski lemma says that a field finitely generated as an algebra over a field is a finite algebraic extension of that field. Therefore
charK=p⟹∣K∣=pd for some finite d​.
(23)
It remains to exclude characteristic zero. Write K=Z[a1​,…,am​]. Because K is then a field containing Q, also K=Q[a1​,…,am​]. Zariski lemma makes K/Q finite algebraic. Each ai​ has a monic equation with rational coefficients. Choose a positive integer N clearing all their coefficient denominators. Each ai​ is then integral over A=Z[1/N], so A[a1​,…,am​] is integral over A. This algebra equals K: it contains the given integer algebra K, and 1/N already lies in the field K.
An integral field extension forces the base domain to be a field. To see it here, for a nonzero a∈A its inverse in K satisfies
(a−1)r+cr−1​(a−1)r−1+⋯+c0​=0,ci​∈A.
(24)
Multiplying by ar−1 gives
a−1=−(cr−1​+cr−2​a+⋯+c0​ar−1)∈A.
(25)
Thus A would be a field. But choose a prime ℓ∤N. Reduction modulo ℓ is a well-defined map Z[1/N]→Fℓ​, so the nonzero element ℓ is not invertible in A. This contradiction rules out characteristic zero. Therefore the finite-field theorem for finitely generated integer algebras yields
K is a finite field​.
(26)
This uses Zariski lemma and elementary integrality, not a claim that a field merely finitely generated as a field extension must be algebraic.

A nilpotent element belongs to every prime ideal and therefore to every maximal ideal, so
(0)​⊆Jac(R).
(27)
For the reverse containment, let r be nonnilpotent. The localization of a ring S=R[1/r] is nonzero: if 1=0 there, some power of r would annihilate 1 in R. This is still a finite-type integer algebra, because it can be presented as R[T]/(rT−1).
Choose a maximal ideal n of S. Its residue field L=S/n is a finite-type integer algebra, hence finite by part (a). Consider the image F of R in L. It is a finite integral domain containing 1, so it is a field: multiplication by a nonzero element is injective on the finite set F, hence surjective, and therefore has an inverse in F.
The kernel m of R→F is consequently maximal. The image of r is nonzero, since r became a unit in S and remains a unit in its nonzero residue field. Thus r∈/m. Every nonnilpotent element can therefore be avoided by a maximal ideal, giving
Jac(R)=(0)​​.
(28)
This radical equality for finitely generated integer algebras uses part (a) to ensure that the contraction of the localized maximal ideal is maximal. Such contraction is not generally maximal for arbitrary localization of a ring; the finite-field image is the decisive additional step. The zero ring is immediate, with the intersection of its empty set of maximal ideals understood as the whole ring. No general theorem that integer algebras are Jacobson rings is being quoted.
