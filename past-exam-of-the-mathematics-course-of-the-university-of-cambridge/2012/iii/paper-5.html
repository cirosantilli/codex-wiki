Work over F=R or C. A Fréchet space is a Hausdorff, complete, metrizable locally convex space. Its topology is generated by a countable separating family of seminorms (pn​)n≥1​. A neighbourhood base at zero consists of finite intersections
{x:pn1​​(x)<ε1​,…,pnk​​(x)<εk​}.
(1)
Replacing pn​ by maxj≤n​pj​ makes the family increasing. One compatible translation-invariant metric is
d(x,y)=∑n≥1​2−nmin{1,pn​(x−y)}.
(2)
Completeness means that a sequence Cauchy for all these seminorms converges in the space. The separating condition ensures that d(x,y)=0 only when x=y.
Let E′ denote the continuous dual space for the original topology τ. The weak topology σ(E,E′) is the coarsest topology making all f∈E′ continuous. Its basic zero-neighbourhoods are
{x:∣f1​(x)∣<ε1​,…,∣fk​(x)∣<εk​},fj​∈E′.
(3)
Equivalently, it is generated by the seminorms x↦∣f(x)∣.
To prove the Hausdorff property, fix z=0. Some continuous seminorm p has p(z)>0. On the one-dimensional span of z, define f0​(az)=ap(z). Then ∣f0​(az)∣=p(az). The real or complex Hahn-Banach theorem extends this to f:E→F with ∣f(x)∣≤p(x). Thus f is originally continuous and f(z)=0. This proves continuous-dual point separation. For distinct x,y, inverse images under such an f of disjoint small scalar neighbourhoods of f(x) and f(y) are disjoint weak neighbourhoods. Hence the weak topology is Hausdorff.
If a linear functional is originally continuous, it belongs to E′ and is weakly continuous by definition. Conversely, σ(E,E′) is coarser than τ, so every weakly continuous scalar function is originally continuous. In particular,
(E,σ(E,E′))′=E′.​
(4)
This special case of the continuous dual of a weak topology needs no completeness argument.
For the linear map T:E1​→E2​, original continuity implies that g∘T∈E1′​ whenever g∈E2′​. Each such composition is weakly continuous on E1​, so the defining property of the target weak topology makes T weak-to-weak continuous.
Conversely, suppose T is weak-to-weak continuous. Every g∘T, g∈E2′​, is then weakly continuous, and hence continuous for τ1​. We show that the graph of a linear operator Γ(T) is closed in the original product topology. If xn​→x in E1​ and Txn​→y in E2​, then
g(y)=limn​g(Txn​)=limn​(g∘T)(xn​)=g(Tx)(g∈E2′​).
(5)
By continuous-dual point separation on E2​, this forces y=Tx. The product is metrizable, so sequential closedness is closedness. The closed graph theorem for Fréchet spaces now gives original continuity of T.
The relevant version of that theorem does use completeness of both spaces: a closed graph is a Fréchet space, its first-coordinate projection is a continuous linear bijection onto E1​, and the open mapping theorem for Fréchet spaces gives a continuous inverse. Composing with the second-coordinate projection gives T. Thus
T is originally continuous⟺T is weak-to-weak continuous.​
(6)
Equality of continuous scalar functionals does not imply equality of the two topologies; the closed graph theorem for Fréchet spaces is the extra step that makes this conclusion valid.

Write a finite binary word as s=(s1​,…,sn​) and denote its prefix cylinder set by
[s]={ω:ω1​=s1​,…,ωn​=sn​}.
(7)
More generally, a cylinder set fixes finitely many coordinates, or prescribes a subset of their finite coordinate product, leaving all other coordinates free. Each coordinate factor is discrete, so finite-coordinate inverse images are both open and closed. Thus cylinders are clopen. The basic open sets of the product topology restrict only finitely many coordinates, so cylinders form a base. Any finite-coordinate cylinder is a finite union of prefix cylinders of one sufficiently long common length; the prefix cylinders therefore also form a base.
The Bernoulli space here is Cantor space, with compatible metric
d(ω,ω′)=∑j≥1​2−j∣ωj​−ωj′​∣.
(8)
It is a compact metric space: from any sequence, choose successively subsequences constant in the first, second and subsequent coordinates, and take the diagonal subsequence. Agreement on the first n coordinates bounds the distance by 2−n, proving convergence. Compactness also follows from Tychonoff theorem.
Every cylinder indicator function is continuous because the cylinder is clopen. Hence S(Ω) is a vector subspace of the space of continuous functions on a compact space. To prove density, fix f∈CR​(Ω) and ε>0. By uniform continuity, choose n so that d(x,y)≤2−n implies ∣f(x)−f(y)∣<ε. Choose a point ωs​ in each length-n prefix cylinder and put
fn​=∑s∈{0,1}n​f(ωs​)1[s]​.
(9)
Then fn​∈S(Ω) and ∥f−fn​∥∞​<ε. This proves cylinder-function density in Cantor space:
S(Ω)​∥⋅∥∞​=CR​(Ω).​
(10)
Positivity of ϕ implies monotonicity. The pointwise bounds −∥f∥∞​1≤f≤∥f∥∞​1 therefore give
∣ϕ(f)∣≤∥f∥∞​ϕ(1)=∥f∥∞​.
(11)
Evaluation on 1 attains equality, so ϕ is continuous and has norm one. This is the norm of a positive functional on C(K).
The object extended to open sets is the set function ℓ(C)=ϕ(1C​), rather than the functional ϕ itself. Positivity gives ℓ(C)≥0, and linearity gives finite additivity on disjoint cylinders. To construct its extension directly, use the disjoint cylinder decomposition of open subsets of Cantor space. For an open U, take the shortest prefixes s for which [s]⊂U. They are prefix-free, their cylinders are disjoint, and their union is U. Define
ℓ(U)=[s] in this decomposition∑​ℓ([s]).​
(12)
For U=Ω, use the empty prefix; for U=∅, use the empty sum.
This value is independent of the chosen disjoint prefix-cylinder decomposition. Indeed, compare two such decompositions (Ci​) and (Dj​). For each fixed Ci​, its intersections with the Dj​ give a disjoint open cover of the compact cylinder Ci​. Compactness reduces this to finitely many nonempty intersections. Each intersection is a prefix cylinder or is empty, so finite additivity gives ℓ(Ci​)=∑j​ℓ(Ci​∩Dj​). Sum over i and rearrange the nonnegative double sum. Doing the same with each Dj​ proves equality of the two totals.
For disjoint open sets Uk​, combine their disjoint cylinder decompositions to obtain one for ⋃k​Uk​. Rearrangement of nonnegative sums proves countable additivity on this family of open sets. The extension is unique because any countably additive extension must have the prescribed sum on every disjoint cylinder decomposition. Open sets are not themselves a sigma-algebra; countable additivity here concerns disjoint open families and their open union.
For the Borel probability measure, let A be the algebra of finite unions of prefix cylinders. It is also the algebra of clopen sets, because every clopen set is compact and has a finite cylinder cover. Define ℓ(A)=ϕ(1A​) on A. If A=⨆k≥1​Ak​ with all sets in A, compactness of A gives a finite subcover by the Ak​. Disjointness makes all remaining members empty. Thus finite additivity already establishes the premeasure condition.
The Caratheodory extension theorem gives a unique measure P on σ(A). This is the Borel sigma-algebra, since the cylinders are a countable base; P(Ω)=1. Its values on open sets agree with the extension above. For cylinder simple functions,
ϕ(fn​)=∫Ω​fn​dP.
(13)
Both sides are continuous in the supremum norm, so cylinder-function density in Cantor space gives
ϕ(f)=∫Ω​fdP(f∈CR​(Ω)).​
(14)
Any other Borel probability measure with this property agrees on every cylinder indicator function, hence on A, and uniqueness in the Caratheodory extension theorem makes it equal to P. This proves the Cantor-space representation of positive functionals without assuming the general representation theorem.
Now let h:Ω→X be the supplied continuous surjection. The pullback
J:CR​(X)⟶CR​(Ω),Jf=f∘h
(15)
is a unital isometric embedding: surjectivity gives ∥f∘h∥∞​=∥f∥∞​. On the vector subspace J(CR​(X)), define L(Jf)=ϕ(f). This is well-defined, has norm one and satisfies L(1)=1.
The real Hahn-Banach theorem extends L to L on all of CR​(Ω) with the same norm. Norm preservation alone does not automatically mean positivity, so verify it. If 0≤g≤1, then ∥1−g∥∞​≤1 and
L(g)=1−L(1−g)≥1−∣L(1−g)∣≥0.
(16)
Scaling proves positivity for every nonnegative g. This is the unital contraction positivity criterion, giving a positive extension from a unital subspace of C(K).
Represent L by the already constructed Borel probability measure P on Ω and take the pushforward measure Q=h∗​P. Continuity of h makes its Borel inverse images measurable, and Q(X)=1. The pushforward integral identity gives
ϕ(f)=L(f∘h)=∫Ω​f∘hdP=∫X​fdQ.​
(17)
This is compact-metric representation by Cantor-space pullback. The hypothesis ϕ(1)=1 already excludes an empty X. The measure is on the Borel sets; no uniqueness claim on an unspecified larger collection of subsets is needed.

Construct the densities using the Riesz representation theorem for a Hilbert space, rather than assuming the measure-decomposition conclusion. Put ρ=μ+ν, a finite dominating measure, and work in the real L2 space L2(ρ). The linear functional
L(g)=∫Ω​gdν
(18)
is well-defined on its equivalence classes because ν≤ρ. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
∣L(g)∣≤ν(Ω)1/2(∫Ω​∣g∣2dν)1/2≤ν(Ω)1/2∥g∥L2(ρ)​.
(19)
Thus the Riesz representation theorem supplies h∈L2(ρ) such that L(g)=∫ghdρ. Since ρ is finite, h is also integrable. Testing g=1A​ gives
ν(A)=∫A​hdρ,μ(A)=∫A​(1−h)dρ(A∈Σ).
(20)
The first identity and nonnegativity of ν rule out a positive-measure set where h<0; for example, test {h≤−1/n}. The second identity and nonnegativity of μ similarly rule out {h≥1+1/n}. Modify a measurable representative on a ρ-null set so that 0≤h≤1 everywhere. This is the Hilbert-space construction of dominated measure densities.
Set
B={h=1},f={h/(1−h),0,​h<1,h=1.​
(21)
Then B∈Σ and μ(B)=0. The indicator identities for μ extend to integrals of nonnegative measurable functions by approximation with simple functions and the monotone convergence theorem. Consequently,
∫Ω​fdμ=∫Bc​hdρ=ν(Bc)≤ν(Ω)<∞,
(22)
and, for every A,
∫A​fdμ=ν(A∖B).
(23)
Hence
ν(A)=∫A​fdμ+ν(A∩B),f≥0,f∈L1(μ),μ(B)=0.​
(24)
This proves Lebesgue decomposition from a sum-measure density. The argument includes the cases of zero measures; there is no division on B, where the denominator vanishes.
Absolute continuity of measures, written ν≪μ, means that μ(A)=0 implies ν(A)=0 for every A∈Σ. If ν≪μ, then ν(B)=0 in the construction above, so the singular term vanishes. Conversely, if ν(A)=∫A​fdμ with a nonnegative integrable f, that integral is zero on every μ-null set. Thus
ν≪μ⟺ν(A)=∫A​fdμ for some nonnegative f∈L1(μ).​
(25)
This is the finite-measure Radon-Nikodym theorem as a consequence of the proved construction.
Finally restrict the base measure to the sub-sigma-algebra Σ0​. For a real f∈L1(μ), write f=f+−f−, with both parts nonnegative and integrable. On Σ0​ define finite positive measures
ν±​(A)=∫A​f±dμ.
(26)
They are absolutely continuous with respect to μ∣Σ0​​. The result just proved gives nonnegative, Σ0​-measurable densities g±​ satisfying
∫A​g±​dμ=∫A​f±dμ(A∈Σ0​).
(27)
Set f0​=g+​−g−​. Since ∫(g+​+g−​)dμ=∫∣f∣dμ<∞, this is in L1(Ω,Σ0​,μ), and subtraction gives
∫A​f0​dμ=∫A​fdμ(A∈Σ0​).​
(28)
For complex f, apply the real construction separately to its real and imaginary parts and combine the two results. This is conditional expectation from finite-measure densities. The resulting conditional expectation is unique almost everywhere: for two real versions, their difference has integral zero on its positive and negative level sets in Σ0​, forcing it to vanish; apply this to both components in the complex case. No probability normalization of μ is required.

Start with a finite subcover of the compact set K. Among its remaining open balls, retain one of greatest radius and discard every ball intersecting it. Repeat until no balls remain. The retained family F is finite and pairwise disjoint. If a discarded ball B(c,r) met the retained ball B(c0​,R), then r≤R and ∣c−c0​∣<r+R. Therefore, for any y∈B(c,r),
∣y−c0​∣≤∣y−c∣+∣c−c0​∣<2r+R≤3R.
(29)
Every original ball lies in the concentric triple of one retained ball. This proves the Wiener covering lemma for the finite subcover. By Lebesgue measure scaling and subadditivity,
λd​(K)≤∑B∈F​λd​(3B)=3d∑B∈F​λd​(B).
(30)
Disjointness gives
λd​(B∈F⋃​B)≥3−dλd​(K).​
(31)
The empty compact set is covered by the empty selection.
The spherical derivative of a measure at x is the limit
Ds​μ(x)=limr↓0​λd​(B(x,r))μ(B(x,r))​=limr↓0​vd​rdμ(B(x,r))​,
(32)
when it exists, where vd​ is the unit-ball volume. We prove that this limit is zero outside a Lebesgue-null set for the singular measure.
First derive the needed maximal estimate from the selection lemma. For a finite positive Borel measure η, put
Mu​η(x)=supB an open ballx∈B​​λd​(B)η(B)​.
(33)
This is the uncentered maximal function of a finite measure. For α>0, its strict superlevel set is the union of all balls with η(B)>αλd​(B), so it is open. Cover any compact subset H of this set by such balls and apply the finite Wiener covering lemma. The selected disjoint balls give
λd​(H)≤3d∑B∈F​λd​(B)≤α3d​∑B∈F​η(B)≤α3d​η(Rd).
(34)
By inner regularity of Lebesgue measure, this proves the uncentered maximal weak-type inequality
λd​{Mu​η>α}≤α3d​η(Rd).​
(35)
Since μ and λd​ are mutually singular measures, there is a Borel set N with λd​(N)=0 and μ(Rd∖N)=0. By regularity of finite Borel measures on Euclidean space, for each ε>0 choose compact L⊂N with μ(Rd∖L)<ε. Define the remainder measure ηε​(A)=μ(A∖L).
For x∈/L, distance from x to this compact set is positive. Thus sufficiently small centered balls avoid L, and
μ(B(x,r))=ηε​(B(x,r))for all sufficiently small r.
(36)
Write Ds​μ(x) for the upper limit of the ratios. If it exceeds α, then either x∈L or Mu​ηε​(x)>α. Hence
{Ds​μ>α}⊂L∪{Mu​ηε​>α}.
(37)
The right side has Lebesgue measure at most 3dε/α, since λd​(L)=0. This even bounds the outer measure of the left side, without a separate measurability argument for the upper density. Let ε↓0, and then take the countable union over positive rational α. We obtain Ds​μ=0 Lebesgue almost everywhere. The ratios are nonnegative, so their lower limit is also zero and
Ds​μ(x)=0λd​-almost everywhere.​
(38)
This proves that the spherical derivative of a singular measure vanishes. It is important to approximate the null carrier by compact subsets: a null carrier can be dense, so points outside it need not have any neighbourhood avoiding it.
In one dimension, consider the cumulative distribution function F. For either sign of h=0, monotonicity gives a nonnegative difference quotient, and the relevant half-open interval lies inside B(x,2∣h∣). In detail, the numerator is μ((x,x+h]) for h>0, while after reversing both signs it is μ((x+h,x]) for h<0. Thus
0≤hF(x+h)−F(x)​≤∣h∣μ(B(x,2∣h∣))​=4λ1​(B(x,2∣h∣))μ(B(x,2∣h∣))​.
(39)
At each point where the spherical derivative of a measure is zero, this bound tends to zero from both sides. The enlarged open interval avoids any endpoint ambiguity from atoms. Therefore the ordinary two-sided derivative exists and
F′(x)=0λ1​-almost everywhere.​
(40)
This is why singular distribution functions have zero derivative almost everywhere without having to be constant: almost-everywhere differentiation recovers increments only under extra hypotheses such as absolute continuity of a function.
