Put V=∣v0​∣>0, take q>0 without loss of generality, and write s=R2+z2/q2. The Newtonian gravitational potential is singular at the origin; all pointwise claims below concern s>0. The zero of the Newtonian gravitational potential is the one in the paper, with an implicit fixed length unit inside the logarithm.
The Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity in cylindrical coordinates gives
4πGρ=R1​∂R​(RΦR​)+Φzz​,ΦR​=sV2R​,Φz​=q2sV2z​.
(1)
The two contributions to the Laplacian are
R1​∂R​(RΦR​)=s2V2​−s22V2R2​,Φzz​=q2sV2​−q4s22V2z2​.
(2)
Consequently the mass density of the axisymmetric logarithmic gravitational potential is
ρ(R,z)=4πGq2V2​(R2+q−2z2)2R2+(2−q−2)z2​.​
(3)
The coefficient of R2 is positive. The coefficient of z2 determines the density positivity for an axisymmetric logarithmic potential: strict positivity at every noncentral point requires q2>1/2 and v0​=0. Nonnegativity permits q2=1/2, where the mass density vanishes on the noncentral symmetry axis. For q2<1/2 it is negative near that axis. Either sign of a nonzero real q gives the same model, and q=0 is undefined. Setting v0​=0 yields vacuum away from the singular origin, not strictly positive mass density. The r−2 central cusp is locally integrable and contains no hidden point mass: the Newtonian gravitational field flux through a shrinking sphere is of order its radius.
An equatorial circular orbit requires inward gravitational acceleration equal to vc2​/R. Thus the circular speed is
vc2​=RΦR​(R,0)=V2,vc​(R)=V(R>0).​
(4)
This is a flat galaxy rotation curve. The singular center does not define an additional circular orbit at R=0.
The specific orbital energy E=21​(vR2​+vϕ2​+vz2​)+Φ is conserved because the Newtonian gravitational potential is time independent. The axial specific angular momentum Lz​=Rvϕ​ is conserved because the Newtonian gravitational potential is axisymmetric. Hence the Jeans theorem, or directly dF(E,Lz2​)/dt=0 in the Collisionless Boltzmann equation, permits a stationary two-integral galactic distribution function. Using Lz2​ makes this galactic distribution function even under reversal of the azimuthal velocity, so it describes a nonstreaming population. A general axisymmetric population can have an odd part in Lz​, or dependence on a third integral of motion; neither is required for this construction.
At each position define ρ⟨g⟩=∫gFd3v. The two-integral galactic distribution function is invariant under swapping vR​ and vz​, since only their sum of squares occurs in E. It is also even in each velocity component. Swapping integration variables proves meridional velocity isotropy of a two-integral distribution, while reflecting one variable makes each mixed integrand odd. Therefore
⟨vR2​⟩=⟨vz2​⟩,⟨vR​vz​⟩=⟨vR​vϕ​⟩=⟨vϕ​vz​⟩=0.​
(5)
All mean velocities vanish, so these are also the corresponding statements for the velocity ellipsoid and its centered covariance tensor. They do not require ⟨vϕ2​⟩ to equal either meridional second moment.
To normalize the proposed logarithmic-potential two-integral distribution, first separate the mass density into terms with the same spatial factors as the two exponentials:
ρ=4πGq2V2​[s2q2−1​+s22(1−q2)R2​].
(6)
Since e−2Φ/V2=s−1 and e−4Φ/V2=s−2, the Gaussian integral and its derivative give
∫e−v2/V2d3v=π3/2V3,∫vϕ2​e−2v2/V2d3v=82​π3/2V5​.
(7)
Thus velocity integration of the galactic distribution function yields AR2s−2π3/2V5/(82​)+Bs−1π3/2V3. Matching the independent spatial factors determines
A=π5/2Gq2V342​(1−q2)​,B=4π5/2Gq2V2q2−1​.​
(8)
This verifies both the stationary Collisionless Boltzmann equation and the required Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity. A shift Φ↦Φ+C changes the normalizations to Ae4C/V2 and Be2C/V2; the physical mass density is unchanged.
There is an important distinction between positive mass density and a nonnegative galactic distribution function. For 1/2≤q2≤1, both coefficients above are nonnegative. For a prolate model q2>1, A<0 and one must test the sum rather than reject the model just because one term is negative. On accessible phase space,
Lz2​e−2E/V2=sR2​vϕ2​e−v2/V2≤eV2​,
(9)
with equality in the equatorial plane at vR​=vz​=0, vϕ2​=V2. The nonnegativity bound for a prolate logarithmic-potential distribution is consequently
q2≤162​−2e162​−e​(q2>1).​
(10)
For larger q, the displayed algebraic expression still integrates to the positive mass density, but is negative at those equatorial circular orbit phase points and is not a physical galactic distribution function. This extra restriction is distinct from the earlier density-only answer.

Let ui​=⟨vi​⟩, σij​=⟨(vi​−ui​)(vj​−uj​)⟩, and Pij​=ρσij​. The Jeans equation obtained by taking the first velocity moment of the Collisionless Boltzmann equation is
∂t​(ρuj​)+∂k​(ρuj​uk​+Pjk​)=−ρ∂j​Φ.
(11)
For an equilibrium population multiply by xi​ and integrate. Integration by parts gives
∮xi​(ρuj​uk​+Pjk​)nk​dS−∫(ρui​uj​+Pji​)d3x=−∫ρxi​∂j​Φd3x.
(12)
Assume the integrals exist and the boundary flux vanishes, as for an isolated finite system with adequate decay. With the stellar kinetic-energy tensor, stellar pressure-energy tensor and stellar potential-energy tensor defined by
Tij​=21​∫ρui​uj​d3x,Πij​=∫ρσij​d3x,Wij​=−∫ρxi​∂j​Φd3x,
(13)
we obtain the equilibrium tensor virial theorem
2Tij​+Πij​+Wij​=0.​
(14)
The stellar kinetic-energy tensor describes ordered motion, whereas the stellar pressure-energy tensor describes random motion. In particular, Tij​=0 does not mean the stars have no kinetic energy. If a surface flux is retained, the right side of the displayed equilibrium identity is that surface tensor; it cannot be discarded for an arbitrary finite aperture. For an isolated self-gravitating population, Wij​ is symmetric by pairwise interchange in the Newtonian interaction integral, and the time-dependent identity is 21​I¨ij​=2Tij​+Πij​+Wij​ with Iij​=∫ρxi​xj​d3x.
For the spherical Newtonian gravitational potential, ∂j​Φ=Φ′(r)xj​/r. In the pressure-supported case the tensor virial theorem gives Πii​=∫ρΦ′(r)xi2​/rd3x. The notation ΠRR​ used in this question is the planar virial trace Πxx​+Πyy​, not just the integral of the local cylindrical radial velocity dispersion. In cylindrical coordinates this trace includes both vR2​ and vϕ2​. Summing the two Cartesian components therefore gives
Πzz​ΠRR​​=∫ρΦ′(r)z2/rd3x∫ρΦ′(r)R2/rd3x​.​
(15)
The distinction explains the factor of two in the spherical limit.
Use ordinary spherical coordinates, R=rsinθ, z=rcosθ. The mass density separates as ρ0​r−γh(θ), where
h(θ)=(sin2θ+q−2cos2θ)−γ/2.
(16)
Both potential-energy integrals have the same radial factor 2πρ0​∫0∞​r3−γΦ′(r)dr. When this factor is finite and positive it cancels, leaving the scale-free tracer virial ratio
Πzz​ΠRR​​=∫0π​sinθcos2θ(sin2θ+q−2cos2θ)−γ/2dθ∫0π​sin3θ(sin2θ+q−2cos2θ)−γ/2dθ​.​
(17)
The angular factor in the printed intermediate formula has its sine and cosine interchanged. Keeping its sine/cosine weights while using that printed factor would instead produce the opposite sign in the final small-flattening correction. Merely measuring θ from the equatorial plane cannot fix this: the measure and both moment weights would have to change as well.
The angular ratio is finite and positive for every fixed q>0 and finite real γ, because h is positive and bounded above and below. However, the claim that the unrestricted global virial integrals are always well-defined needs a hypothesis. In a point-mass Newtonian gravitational potential, the common radial factor is proportional to ∫0∞​r1−γdr: convergence at zero requires γ<2, while convergence at infinity requires γ>2. There is no such γ. If the global energies diverge, the boxed angular ratio is still the ratio of the two potential-energy integrals with identical spherical inner and outer cutoffs, independent of the cutoffs. It is not a quotient of two finite global stellar pressure-energy tensors; applying a finite-aperture tensor virial theorem additionally requires its surface terms. These convergence and boundary qualifications are separate from the printed angular typo.
Set ϵ=q−2−1. For modest flattening, the Taylor expansion is h=1−(γ/2)ϵcos2θ+O(ϵ2). The required elementary angular integrals are
∫0π​sin3θdθ=34​,∫0π​sin3θcos2θdθ=154​,∫0π​sinθcos2θdθ=32​,∫0π​sinθcos4θdθ=52​.
(18)
These follow directly by t=cosθ, or from the supplied gamma function identity. Hence the numerator is (4/3)[1−γϵ/10+O(ϵ2)] and the denominator is (2/3)[1−3γϵ/10+O(ϵ2)]. The flattening–anisotropy virial relation becomes
2Πzz​ΠRR​​=1+5γ​(q−2−1)+O((q−2−1)2).​
(19)
For a decreasing tracer mass density (γ>0), oblate flattening (q<1) requires more random kinetic support per in-plane direction than vertically. In a spherical Newtonian gravitational field, directional orbital anisotropy supplies the flattening rather than an anisotropic force law. A steeper radial mass density amplifies the required velocity dispersion anisotropy. A prolate tracer reverses the leading inequality. The relation constrains integrated velocity dispersions, not a unique local galactic distribution function.

For an axisymmetric Newtonian gravitational potential, the vacuum Laplace equation in cylindrical coordinates is R−1∂R​(R∂R​Φ)+∂z2​Φ=0. Use separation of variables, Φ=X(R)Z(z). Dividing by XZ and setting the separated constant to k2, with k>0, gives
Z′′=k2Z,X′′+R1​X′+k2X=0.
(20)
The first equation has solutions e±kz; with u=kR, the second is the order-zero Bessel differential equation. Regularity at the axis selects the Bessel function of the first kind J0​(kR), rather than the singular Bessel function of the second kind Y0​. Thus
Φk​(R,z)=e±kzJ0​(kR).​
(21)
There are also zero-separation-constant modes, and other boundary conditions can select other separated families. For a localized reflection-symmetric thin disk, decay away from the disk selects e−k∣z∣ on the respective half-spaces.
Apply the divergence theorem to the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity across a narrow pillbox enclosing disk area. Writing the volume mass density as Σ(R)δ(z) gives
Φz​(R,0+)−Φz​(R,0−)=4πGΣ(R).
(22)
Reflection symmetry makes Φz​(R,0+)=2πGΣ(R). A superposition of the regular vacuum modes therefore satisfies
Φ=∫0∞​S(k)J0​(kR)e−k∣z∣dk,−∫0∞​kS(k)J0​(kR)dk=2πGΣ(R).
(23)
The order-zero Hankel transform and its inversion now determine the Hankel representation of thin-disk gravity:
S(k)=−2πG∫0∞​Σ(R)J0​(kR)RdR.​
(24)
The sign is fixed by the positive derivative of an attractive Newtonian gravitational potential above the disk. For ordinary finite disks the representation uses the isolated boundary condition, fixing any extra vacuum field or additive constant. Infinite scale-free disks require suitable regularization of the potential itself.
Equatorial circular orbit balance is vc2​=RΦR​(R,0). Using J0′​(u)=−J1​(u) in the Hankel representation of thin-disk gravity gives
vc2​(R)=−R∫0∞​kS(k)J1​(kR)dk.​
(25)
Differentiation under the integral is justified by convergence for regular models, or by first working off the disk and taking the limiting radial field.
Write C=Σ0​R0​. For the Mestel disk, the supplied Bessel function integral gives S(k)=−2πGC/k for k>0. The circular speed is then
vc2​=2πGCR∫0∞​J1​(kR)dk=2πGC.
(26)
Meanwhile the interior mass is M(R)=2π∫0R​(C/s)sds=2πCR. Consequently
vc2​=2πGΣ0​R0​=RGM(R)​.​
(27)
The Mestel disk has a flat galaxy rotation curve, despite its nonspherical geometry.
Its infinite total mass prevents setting the Newtonian gravitational potential to zero at infinity. Indeed the integral for Φ diverges at k=0 because S(k)∼1/k. Subtracting the potential at a reference point removes this additive divergence. As an explicit check, the Mestel disk potential-density pair is
Φ=2πGClog(R∗​R2+z2​+∣z∣​).
(28)
It is harmonic for z=0, its vertical derivative jump is 4πGC/R, and RΦR​(R,0)=2πGC. Equivalently, an Abel regularization of an oscillatory integral with damping factor e−ak in the radial-field integral gives R∫0∞​e−akJ1​(kR)dk=1−a/a2+R2​, which tends to one as a↓0. The force is well-defined although the unreferenced potential is not.
The equality with GM(R)/R is special to the Mestel disk, not a thin-disk shell theorem. The spherical shell theorem normally makes that formula possible, but does not apply to an astrophysical disk. Exterior annuli exert outward radial gravitational acceleration inside their radii, while interior annuli do not act exactly as point masses at the origin. For this particular scale-free surface density, their combined contributions happen to produce the same result as the interior mass expression. Changing the radial surface density or truncating the disk generally destroys the equality: enclosed mass does not determine a disc rotation curve.

Let M=M1​+M2​ and α=M2​/M, with 0<α<1. In the centre of mass frame the two primary distances are r1​=αR and r2​=(1−α)R. Applying Newton's law of universal gravitation and circular centripetal acceleration to the first primary gives M1​ω2αR=GM1​M2​/R2, and hence
ω2=R3GM​.​
(29)
The same result follows from the second primary or from the relative two-body equation of motion.
A rotating-frame equilibrium has zero rotating-frame velocity, so its Coriolis force vanishes. Above the orbital plane both primaries have downward gravitational components; below it both have upward components. The centrifugal force has no z component. More explicitly,
az​=−Gz(∣r−r1​∣3M1​​+∣r−r2​∣3M2​​),
(30)
which vanishes only at z=0 for finite nonsingular points. Thus all equilibria lie in the orbital plane.
The equation of motion in a rotating frame for the dwarf is
mr¨=−∇U−2mω×r˙−mω×(ω×r),U=−∣r−r1​∣GmM1​​−∣r−r2​∣GmM2​​.
(31)
On the orbital plane the centrifugal contribution is mω2r. Thus the rotating-frame effective potential is E=U−mω2(x2+y2)/2; equilibria are exactly its stationary points. The Coriolis force affects motion about those equilibria but not their positions. This is the circular restricted three-body problem, with the dwarf treated as a test mass.
The printed energy unit has the wrong dimensions. The correct total energy unit is mGM/R, or GM/R for specific energy; GM/R2 is an acceleration. Dividing E by mGM/R and measuring lengths in units of R gives the dimensionless effective potential
E(x,y)=−2x2+y2​−(x+α)2+y2​1−α​−(x−1+α)2+y2​α​.
(32)
The primary coordinates are (−α,0) and (1−α,0). Restricting to y=0 gives F(x)=E(x,0) and
F′(x)=−x+∣x+α∣3(1−α)(x+α)​+∣x−1+α∣3α(x−1+α)​,F′′(x)=−1−∣x+α∣32(1−α)​−∣x−1+α∣32α​<0.
(33)
The absolute values are essential; dropping them would reverse the attraction on the left of a primary. On each of the intervals separated by the two primaries, F′ decreases strictly from +∞ to −∞. There is exactly one Collinear Lagrange point on each interval, a maximum along the x direction.
For the inner Lagrange point, write x=1−α−δ with δ>0. For the L2 Lagrange point, write x=1−α+δ. The respective equations are
0=−(1−α−δ)+(1−δ)21−α​−δ2α​=3δ−δ2α​+O(δ2+αδ),
(34)
0=−(1−α+δ)+(1+δ)21−α​+δ2α​=−3δ+δ2α​+O(δ2+αδ).
(35)
Balancing the leading terms gives δ=(α/3)1/3+O(α2/3). Therefore the small-mass-ratio collinear Lagrange points obey
xL1​​=1−(α/3)1/3+O(α2/3),xL2​​=1+(α/3)1/3+O(α2/3).​
(36)
The omitted −α displacement of the secondary is smaller than the stated error. The leading distance from the secondary is the dimensionless Hill radius.
For the L3 Lagrange point put x=−1+cα+O(α2). It lies to the left of both primaries, so
F′=−x−(x+α)21−α​−(x−1+α)2α​.
(37)
Expanding the two positive inverse-square factors gives (1−α)/(x+α)2=1+(2c+1)α+O(α2) and α/(x−1+α)2=α/4+O(α2). Thus F′=−(3c+5/4)α+O(α2), so
xL3​​=−1−125​α+O(α2).​
(38)
All three have y=z=0. The small-α equalities in the paper are asymptotic expressions, not exact coordinates for finite mass ratio.
For the remaining Lagrange points, introduce plane polar coordinates about the first primary: x=−α+scosθ, y=ssinθ. Its distance to the second primary is d=(s2+1−2scosθ)1/2, and
E=−21​(s2−2αscosθ+α2)−s1−α​−dα​.
(39)
The angular stationary condition is ∂θ​E=αssinθ(d−3−1)=0. For an off-axis point s>0 and sinθ=0, so d=1. The radial stationary condition becomes
∂s​E=−s+αcosθ+s21−α​+d3α(s−cosθ)​=(1−α)(s−2−s)=0.
(40)
Hence s=1, and d=1 implies cosθ=1/2. The Triangular Lagrange points are exactly
L4​=(21​−α,23​​,0),L5​=(21​−α,−23​​,0).​
(41)
Both triangles are equilateral. The collinear and off-axis calculations together exhaust the stationary points.
For the requested sketch, E tends to −∞ at either primary and at large in-plane radius. Each Collinear Lagrange point is a saddle of the planar effective potential: its x curvature is negative and its y curvature is positive. To see the latter, let d1​=∣x+α∣, d2​=∣x−1+α∣ and D=(1−α)/d13​+α/d23​. At an inner point both distances are below one, so D>1. At an exterior stationary point, use barycentric positions a1​=−α, a2​=1−α and weights 1−α,α: the equation implies x(D−1)=∑wi​ai​/∣x−ai​∣3. To the right, the weighted sum is positive because the positive primary is closer; to the left it is negative because the negative primary is closer. Thus D>1 there as well, and Eyy​=D−1>0.

Its trace is −3 and determinant 27α(1−α)/4>0, so these are local maxima. Their height is E=−3/2+α(1−α)/2.
