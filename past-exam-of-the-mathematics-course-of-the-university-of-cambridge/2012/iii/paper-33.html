Use the natural filtration Fn​=σ(X1​,…,Xn​) of the simple symmetric random walk. The event {T1​≤n}=⋃k=0n​{Sk​=1} belongs to Fn​, so T1​ is a stopping time.
For a positive integer m, set τm​=T1​∧T−m​. This exit time from the finite interval is integrable: in each block of m+1 steps there is a probability at least 2−(m+1) of all steps being positive, which forces exit if it has not already occurred. Consequently the survival probabilities have a geometric upper bound.
We use the bounded-time optional stopping theorem: if M is a martingale and σ a bounded stopping time, then EMσ​=EM0​. Apply it at τm​∧n to Sn​ and to the square-minus-time martingale of a simple symmetric random walk Sn2​−n. The stopped positions lie in [−m,1], so dominated convergence and monotone convergence give
ESτm​​=0,Eτm​=ESτm​2​.
(1)
Writing pm​=P(T1​<T−m​), the first equation becomes pm​−m(1−pm​)=0, hence pm​=m/(m+1). The second gives Eτm​=pm​+m2(1−pm​)=m. Since τm​≤T1​, its expectation satisfies ET1​≥m for every m. Therefore
ET1​=+∞.​
(2)
Nevertheless P(T1​<∞)≥pm​→1. Thus the infinite mean first passage of a simple symmetric random walk occurs despite almost sure finiteness; using unrestricted optional stopping directly at T1​ would be unjustified.

Define the stopped martingale
Mn​=1−Sn∧T1​​.​
(3)
It is nonnegative: before the first visit to 1, the nearest-neighbour random walk is at most 0, and afterwards the stopped position is 1. Each Mn​ is integrable, with ∣Mn​∣≤n+1. Moreover
Mn+1​−Mn​=−1{T1​>n}​Xn+1​.
(4)
The indicator is Fn​-measurable and the increment has conditional mean zero, so Mn​ is a martingale with EMn​=M0​=1.
Part (a) shows T1​<∞ almost surely, so every such path eventually has Mn​=0. Thus Mn​→0 almost surely. But E∣Mn​−0∣=1 for every n, so there is no convergence in L1. This is a nonnegative martingale with almost sure convergence but no convergence in L1, and hence it is not uniformly integrable.

The time T is the waiting time for two consecutive successes in independent fair trials. First note its integrability: if any one of the disjoint pairs (X1​,X2​),…,(X2k−1​,X2k​) is ++, then T≤2k. Thus P(T>2k)≤(3/4)k.
Let e0​ be the expected additional waiting time with no trailing positive step, and let e1​ be the expected additional time when the preceding step was positive. Conditioning on the next independent increment gives
e0​=1+21​e0​+21​e1​,e1​=1+21​e0​.
(6)
A negative step returns either state to state 0; a positive step moves state 0 to state 1 and completes the pattern from state 1. Substitution yields e0​=2+e1​=3+e0​/2, so e0​=6. The original process starts in state 0, and therefore
ET=6.​
(7)
Overlapping pairs are dependent, so regarding each successive pair as a fresh trial of success probability 1/4 would give the wrong expectation.

On a measurable space (E,E) with a sigma-finite measure μ, a Poisson random measure N is a random counting measure such that N(A) has the Poisson distribution with parameter μ(A) for every measurable A of finite intensity, and the counts on finitely many pairwise disjoint measurable sets are independent. The sample measures are countably additive; repeated atoms are allowed if the intensity has atoms.
When μ(A)=∞, exhaustion by finite-intensity subsets gives N(A)=∞ almost surely. Indeed their counts increase, and the Poisson distribution probability of a count at most any fixed integer tends to zero as the parameter tends to infinity. Independent Poisson counts on disjoint sets, with means prescribed by μ, characterize the Poisson random measure.

Interpret the second process as an independent copy of the unit-rate Poisson process. First, the sums are finite almost surely. On [0,1] there are finitely many arrivals, none at 0, so their inverse-square contributions are finite. On [1,∞),
E∑Zi​≥1​Zi−2​=∫1∞​z−2dz=1.
(17)
For completeness, this expectation identity follows first for nonnegative simple functions from EN(A)=∣A∣, and then for all nonnegative measurable functions by monotone convergence. Thus no unproved Poisson-integral formula is needed, and the tail sum is finite almost surely.
By (b), merging the two processes produces a rate-2 Poisson process with arrival times Vi​. Its rescaled arrivals 2Vi​ form a unit-rate Poisson process: for a measurable set A, their count is the merged count on A/2, with parameter 2∣A/2∣=∣A∣, and disjoint-set independence is preserved. Consequently
X+Y=∑i​Vi−2​=4∑i​(2Vi​)−2 =d 4X.
(18)
Therefore c=4​. This is the scaling of an inverse-power sum over Poisson arrivals with exponent 2.
The PDF does not explicitly repeat the second process's rate. If that rate were ρ rather than 1, the same count argument would give X+Y=d(1+ρ)2X. The numerical value 4 uses the intended independent-unit-rate-copy interpretation.

Use the independent-mark interpretation: the Brownian motions attached to the atoms are independent of one another and of the initial Poisson random measure. Fix t and define
pt​(x)=P(∃s∈[0,t]:∣x+ξ(s)−f(s)∣≤1).
(19)
Conditionally on the initial atoms, each atom is retained as a dangerous atom if its own path meets the target by time t. These tests are independent, with position-dependent retention probability pt​(x). The permitted Poisson thinning property makes the dangerous atoms a Poisson random measure of intensity pt​(x)dx.
Its total intensity is computed without invoking a marking or displacement theorem. The event in the definition of pt​(x) says that x belongs to
W−​(t)=⋃0≤s≤t​B(f(s)−ξ(s),1).
(20)
By the Tonelli theorem,
∫Rd​pt​(x)dx=E∫Rd​1{x∈W−​(t)}​dx=Evol(W−​(t)).
(21)
Symmetry of Brownian motion gives −ξ=dξ as processes. It therefore identifies the last expectation with Evol(W(t)), where the sign of the deterministic path f is unchanged. This swept set is a Wiener sausage with an added deterministic path.
The total intensity is finite on each bounded time interval. To see this explicitly, write Kt​=1+sups≤t​∣f(s)∣ and Rt​=sups≤t​∣ξ(s)∣. The sausage is contained in a ball of radius Kt​+Rt​. On a finite time grid, splitting according to the first crossing of a positive level u by a one-dimensional Brownian motion shows that the chance of ending above u, conditional on the crossing, is at least 1/2: the remaining independent increment is symmetric. Thus the grid maximum exceeds u with probability at most twice the final Gaussian upper tail. Dense grids and continuity give the same bound for the path maximum. Applying this to each coordinate and its negative gives
P(Rt​>u)≤4dP(N(0,t)>u/d​)≤4de−u2/(2dt)(t>0).
(22)
The last inequality follows from EeθN(0,t)=eθ2t/2 and minimizing the exponential Markov bound. Integration of this tail gives finite moments of Rt​ of every positive order, so Evol(W(t))<∞.
There are consequently only finitely many dangerous atoms on each compact time interval, almost surely. For closed balls, continuity of the paths makes each finite hitting-time infimum attained. The event T>t is therefore the absence of dangerous atoms up to time t.
The open-ball convention has the same survival probability at each fixed t. Indeed for any compact path range K, the difference between its closed and open radius-one neighborhoods is the shell {x:dist(x,K)=1}, which has zero Lebesgue measure. To see this, choose a nearest y∈K for a shell point x. For every sufficiently small ε>0, the ball of radius ε/2 centered at x−ε(x−y) lies in the open neighborhood and inside the ball of radius 3ε/2 about x. Thus the shell has a fixed proportional hole at every scale; the Lebesgue density theorem forces its measure to be zero. By the Tonelli theorem, closed-touch and open-entry retention probabilities have equal integrals. Independent thinning therefore gives no atom that touches only the boundary before this fixed horizon, almost surely. This also excludes a difference at an open-entry infimum equal to t.
The zero-count probability of a Poisson distribution is e−λ, and hence
P(T>t)=exp{−Evol(W(t))}.​
(23)
This survival among independently moving Poisson traps formula has been derived using only independent thinning and the defining Poisson zero-count probability, with the expectation/volume step supplied by Tonelli's theorem.

For a nonnegative submartingale (Mk​)0≤k≤n​ and Mn∗​=max0≤k≤n​Mk​, the weak form of the Doob maximal inequality is
uP(Mn∗​≥u)≤E[Mn​1{Mn∗​≥u}​]≤EMn​(u>0).​
(24)
To prove it, let Ak​ be the event that k is the first index with Mk​≥u. These events are disjoint, Ak​∈Fk​, and their union is A={Mn∗​≥u}. The submartingale property gives E[Mn​∣Fk​]≥Mk​. Multiplying by 1Ak​​, taking expectations and summing yields E[Mn​1A​]≥∑k​E[Mk​1Ak​​]≥uP(A). Nonnegativity gives the second inequality.
The associated Doob Lp maximal inequality, for p>1 and Mn​∈Lp, is
∥Mn∗​∥p​≤p−1p​∥Mn​∥p​.​
(25)
Indeed Mk​≤E[Mn​∣Fk​], so conditional Jensen inequality first ensures finite pth moments for all Mk​ and hence for the finite maximum. Integrating the weak inequality against pup−2du and using the Tonelli theorem gives

First fix a deterministic point y different from the initial point. By translation, work with y=0 and B0​=x=0. For R>∣x∣, hitting 0 before TR​ requires hitting every circle of radius r∈(0,∣x∣) before TR​. Part (a) gives
Px​(T0​<TR​)≤logR−logrlogR−log∣x∣​⟶0(r↓0).
(37)
Any finite-time hit of 0 lies before exit from some sufficiently large integer-radius circle, since a continuous path on a finite interval is bounded. A countable union over such radii therefore shows that this fixed point is never hit. This is the polar point for planar Brownian motion property; it is a statement about each fixed point, not about avoiding all points simultaneously.
For the initial point x, fix a deterministic ε>0. The Gaussian distribution of Bε​ has a density, so Bε​=x almost surely. Conditional on Fε​, the future is Brownian motion starting from Bε​, and the preceding fixed-point result shows that it never hits x. Taking ε=1/m and a countable union proves there is no return to the starting point at any positive time.
In contrast, neighborhoods are revisited arbitrarily late. Choose r>0 so that the closed disk centered at x of radius r lies in the given open set U. From any point z outside this disk, let R→∞ in part (a), centered at x. It gives
Pz​(Tr​<∞)≥limR→∞​logR−logrlogR−log∣z−x∣​=1.
(38)
At each deterministic integer time n, condition on Bn​. If it is in the disk there is already a visit to U at time n; if it is outside, the Markov property and the last calculation ensure a later visit to that disk, hence to U. With probability one this holds for all n simultaneously. Thus the set of visits to U is unbounded. The recurrence of planar Brownian motion concerns neighborhoods even though every fixed point is polar.

Let Mt​=max0≤s≤t​Bs​. The Brownian reflection principle gives
P(Mt​>1)=2P(Bt​>1).
(41)
Here is the reflection argument: on paths that hit 1 before t, reflect all increments after their first hitting time. The Strong Markov property gives independent Brownian motion after that time, whose sign can be reversed without changing its law. This exchanges paths ending below 1 with paths ending above 1. The latter have necessarily hit 1, and the endpoint has no atom at 1, giving the displayed equality. It also shows that Mt​ has a continuous distribution at every positive level.
Writing Φ for the standard normal distribution function, the probability of staying below the barrier is therefore
P(Bs​≤1 for all s≤t)=2Φ(t−1/2)−1.
(42)
Since Φ(h)−Φ(0)∼h/2π​ as h↓0, the Brownian barrier survival asymptotic is
α=21​,t→∞lim​t​P(Bs​≤1 for all s≤t)=π2​​.​
(43)
Any smaller exponent gives a zero limit and any larger exponent gives an infinite limit, so this is the unique exponent producing a finite positive constant.
