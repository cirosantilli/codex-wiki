An affine function preserves mixtures, so
U0​(pλ+(1−p)ν)−U0​(pμ+(1−p)ν)=p[U0​(λ)−U0​(μ)]>0.

First, equality of two U0​ values forces equality of the corresponding V0​ values: neither strict comparison holds for U0​, and the same is then true for V0​ in both directions. Thus V0​=g∘U0​ for a well-defined strictly increasing function g on J=U0​(P). Since P is a convex set and U0​,V0​ are affine functions, J is a real interval and
g(pu+(1−p)v)=pg(u)+(1−p)g(v),u,v∈J,0≤p≤1.
(4)
If J contains u<v, let a=[g(v)−g(u)]/(v−u)>0 and b=g(u)−au. The mixture identity gives g(w)=aw+b for w∈[u,v]. For w>v, express v as a convex combination of u and w and solve the same identity for g(w); for w<u, express u as a mixture of w and v. Hence the formula holds on all of J, not merely between the chosen anchors. We obtain positive affine uniqueness of affine preference representations:
V0​(λ)=aU0​(λ)+bfor every λ∈P,a>0.​
(5)
If U0​ is constant, the same comparisons force V0​ to be constant; choose a=1 and the appropriate b. If the probability-measure set is empty, the assertion is vacuous. These degenerate cases do not require distinct anchors.

The Inada conditions give U′(0+)=∞ and U′(∞)=0. Differentiability and strict concavity make U′ continuous and strictly decreasing, with range (0,∞). Hence the inverse marginal utility I=(U′)−1 exists. The unique maximizer in the utility conjugate is x=I(y), and
U(y)=U(I(y))−yI(y),U′(y)=−I(y).​
(7)
The derivative formula does not require differentiability of I: compare the optimizing values at y and y+h to squeeze the difference quotient between −I(y) and −I(y+h), and use continuity of I. Thus the dual is continuously differentiable, strictly decreasing, and strictly convex, since −I is strictly increasing.
For the requested second-derivative assertion, a curvature hypothesis is missing. Under the intended nondegeneracy U′′(x)<0 for every x>0, inverse differentiation gives
U′′(y)=−U′′(I(y))1​>0,U′′(x)U′′(U′(x))=−1.​
(8)
This proves the intended dual differentiability with nonvanishing utility curvature. If U is only twice differentiable, it gives pointwise twice differentiability of the dual; continuous second derivatives additionally follow when U∈C2.
Literal strict concavity does not imply nonvanishing curvature. An explicit Inada utility with vanishing curvature is
U(x)=logx+x1​−6x21​,U′(x)=x1​−x21​+3x31​,U′′(x)=−x4(x−1)2​.
(9)
Its derivative is positive, strictly decreasing, tends to infinity at zero and to zero at infinity. It is smooth and strictly concave, but U′′(1)=0. At y0​=U′(1)=1/3, a finite derivative of I would contradict differentiation of U′(I(y))=y, giving 0⋅I′(y0​)=1. Hence U′ is not differentiable there. As printed, the twice-differentiable-dual claim is false; it is valid with U′′<0. The remaining differentiability, monotonicity and strict-convexity conclusions above hold under the printed hypotheses.

Admissible terminal wealth must lie in (0,∞), the domain of the utility; alternatively extend U by −∞ outside that domain. A one-period state-price density has Z>0 and E[ZP1​]=P0​, componentwise. Therefore for W=H⋅P1​, E[ZW]=H⋅P0​=x.
The dual definition gives the pointwise inequality U(w)≤U(z)+zw for w,z>0. Apply it with z=yZ and take expectations on the finite state space:
EU(H⋅P1​)≤EU(yZ)+yx.​
(10)
Equality holds precisely when U′(w)=z, because that is the unique maximizing payoff in the dual definition. If the feasible portfolio H∗ has U′(H∗⋅P1​)=y∗Z∗, then
EU(H∗⋅P1​)=EU(y∗Z∗)+y∗x.
(11)
The same right side bounds every competing feasible portfolio. Thus H∗ is optimal. This is the one-period marginal-utility certificate of optimality; it does not require the erroneous second-differentiability assertion in part (a).

Let βt​ be the cash position and πt​ the risky shares chosen using information at t−1. At the start of the period the portfolio costs βt​+πt​St−1​=Xt−1​. With cash price fixed at one, its ending value is βt​+πt​St​. Rebalancing cannot add or remove wealth under a self-financing strategy, so
Xt​=Xt−1​+πt​(St​−St−1​).​
(15)
The holdings are predictable: they are chosen before the current price increment is observed. This is the discounted wealth equation in discrete time with cash as numéraire.

Take the nondegenerate case σ>0 and an integer trading horizon T. For exponential utility, maximize the negative of the exponential loss, equivalently minimize its expectation. Conditional on past information, a predictable choice π has
E[e−γπΔSt​∣Ft−1​]=exp(−γμπ+21​γ2σ2π2).
(16)
Complete the square:
−γμπ+21​γ2σ2π2=21​γ2σ2(π−γσ2μ​)2−2σ2μ2​.
(17)
Thus the minimum conditional multiplier is e−κ with κ=μ2/(2σ2). Backward induction starts with terminal loss e−γx and gives the minimal continuation loss e−γx−κ(T−t). Independence of the next increment justifies the same conditional minimization for every history, not just deterministic trading plans. Hence exponential-utility trading with Gaussian increments gives
πt∗​=γσ2μ​,1≤t≤T,V0​(x)=−exp(−γx−2σ2Tμ2​).​
(18)
These are constant numbers of shares, not constant wealth fractions. Strategies with infinite exponential loss have expected utility −∞ and cannot improve this finite value. If σ=0, the usual formula is inapplicable: with μ=0 trading has no effect, while a nonzero deterministic increment admits unbounded riskless gains and no finite maximizing position.

The marked-to-market value of the current holdings is Xt​=Ht​⋅Pt​. In a self-financing portfolio, purchases are paid for by sales within the portfolio, so rebalancing itself changes no net wealth. Asset price changes contribute Ht​⋅dPt​, and nonnegative consumption removes ct​dt from wealth. Hence
Xt​=Ht​⋅Pt​,dXt​=Ht​⋅dPt​−ct​dt.​
(24)
The holdings must be predictable and stochastically integrable. The second identity is the self-financing definition with withdrawals; it is not obtained by incorrectly setting the changes in the holdings themselves to zero.

The deflated asset prices ZP are local martingales, so predictable stochastic integrability makes M=∫H⋅d(ZP) a zero-starting local martingale. Integrating part (b) gives
Nt​:=x+Mt​=Zt​Xt​+∫0t​Zs​cs​ds≥0.
