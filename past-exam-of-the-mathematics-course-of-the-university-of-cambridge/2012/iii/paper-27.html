Write g2​=60G4​ and g3​=140G6​, with the lattice sums taken over nonzero lattice elements. Reindexing the normally convergent series shows that the Weierstrass elliptic function ℘ is an even function, and consequently ℘′ is an odd function. Expanding (z−w)−2 near zero and cancelling the odd lattice sums gives the Laurent coefficients of the Weierstrass elliptic function
℘(z)=z−2+3G4​z2+5G6​z4+O(z6),℘′(z)=−2z−3+6G4​z+20G6​z3+O(z5).
(1)
Thus the elliptic function ℘′2−4℘3+60G4​℘ has no pole at zero and its constant term is −140G6​. By periodicity it has no poles anywhere. An entire elliptic function is bounded on a fundamental parallelogram and hence everywhere, so Liouville theorem makes it constant. This proves the Weierstrass elliptic differential equation
℘′2=4℘3−g2​℘−g3​.​
(2)
We first verify that the cubic is smooth, rather than presupposing it when using its group law. There are three nonzero two-torsion points of a complex torus hj​∈(21​Λ)/Λ. Oddness and periodicity imply ℘′(hj​)=0. Since ℘′ has a triple pole and no other poles on the torus, value multiplicity of an elliptic function says that these three distinct zeros exhaust its zeros and are simple. Put ej​=℘(hj​). Each ℘−ej​ has a double zero at hj​, since ℘′(hj​)=0 and the zero of ℘′ is simple. If ei​=ej​ with i=j, this same function would have at least four zeros counted with multiplicity, although its only pole is double. Therefore the half-period values of the Weierstrass elliptic function are distinct. They are roots of 4x3−g2​x−g3​, so all its roots are distinct and the affine curve is a smooth algebraic curve. Its projective closure is
Y2Z=4X3−g2​XZ2−g3​Z3.
(3)
It has the single point O=(0:1:0) at infinity. The derivative with respect to Z of the defining homogeneous polynomial is nonzero at O, proving smoothness there too.
For any finite value a, value multiplicity of an elliptic function gives exactly two solutions of ℘(z)=a, counting multiplicities. Evenness supplies the pair z,−z. At a nonzero half-period these coincide and the zero is double; elsewhere they are distinct. An even elliptic function f therefore descends to a meromorphic function of x=℘(z) on the Riemann sphere. To justify descent at a half-period, put z=h+u: both f(h+u) and ℘(h+u) are even in u, and ℘(h+u)−℘(h) is u2 times a nonvanishing even analytic function. The local Laurent series of f is consequently meromorphic in the coordinate x−e. At zero the same argument uses 1/x=z2+O(z6). Every meromorphic function on the Riemann sphere is a rational function, proving that even elliptic functions are rational in the Weierstrass function.
For an arbitrary elliptic function f, its even part (f(z)+f(−z))/2 is A(℘). Its odd part divided by ℘′ is an even meromorphic function, hence is B(℘) with B rational. We have therefore proved the elliptic function-field decomposition
f(z)=A(℘(z))+℘′(z)B(℘(z)),A,B∈C(x).​
(4)
Uniqueness follows by taking even and odd parts. In particular the field of elliptic functions is C(℘,℘′).
Define Φ(0)=O and Φ(z)=(℘(z),℘′(z)) elsewhere on C/Λ. Periodicity makes the map well defined. Near zero, the projective chart Y=0 has coordinates X/Y=℘/℘′=−z/2+O(z5) and Z/Y=1/℘′=−z3/2+O(z7), so the extension is holomorphic. The fiber description above proves injectivity: the two candidates z,−z are distinguished by ℘′ unless they coincide at a half-period. It also proves surjectivity, since every x occurs and the two derivatives are the two allowed y values, with y=0 at a branch value.
Finally, a nonvertical line y=ax+b pulls back to the elliptic function ℘′−a℘−b, whose only pole is triple at zero. Its three zeros z1​,z2​,z3​, counted with intersection multiplicity, satisfy z1​+z2​+z3​=0 modulo Λ by the zero-pole sum of an elliptic function. On the cubic, reflection y↦−y corresponds to z↦−z. The chord-and-tangent group law therefore gives Φ(z1​)+Φ(z2​)=Φ(−z3​)=Φ(z1​+z2​). Vertical lines give inverse pairs; tangencies are included through repeated zeros, and the identity cases follow from the extension at zero. This completes the Weierstrass uniformization by half-period values: Φ is a group isomorphism.

All geometric 2-torsion is rational: its points are O,(α,0),(β,0),(γ,0). We will construct the required finite quotient directly, without assuming any theorem about finite generation or two-descent on an elliptic curve.
Choose Q∈E(K) with 2Q=P and define the halving cocycle with rational two-torsion
cP​(σ)=σQ−Q∈E[2],σ∈GK​.
(5)
Such a half exists because the nonconstant multiplication morphism [2] on a smooth projective elliptic curve is surjective over an algebraic closure. Since E[2] is rational, cP​(στ)=cP​(σ)+cP​(τ). A different half is Q+T with rational T∈E[2] and gives the same cocycle. Replacing P by P+2R, with R∈E(K), allows the half Q+R and again changes nothing. Adding chosen halves shows additivity in P. If the cocycle is zero, the half is fixed by the absolute Galois group and lies in E(K), so P∈2E(K). This proves, rather than assumes, an injection
E(K)/2E(K)↪Homcont​(GK​,E[2]).
(6)
The fixed field of kercP​ is exactly K(Q). Its Galois group is the image of cP​, so its degree is at most four. This also follows from the permitted biquadratic description.
Let S be the finite set of primes dividing 2(α−β)(α−γ)(β−γ). At a place v∈/S the displayed integral cubic has unit elliptic-curve discriminant, hence good reduction of an elliptic curve, and the residue characteristic is odd. Choose a half Q​ of the reduction of P over the algebraic closure of the residue field. Its coordinates belong to a finite residue extension; let Lv​/Kv​ be the corresponding finite unramified extension. Smoothness and the Hensel lemma lift Q​ to some Q0​∈E(Lv​). The difference P−2Q0​ reduces to zero and belongs to the formal kernel of a minimal Weierstrass equation. In its integral formal group law the doubling series is 2t+O(t2), with unit linear coefficient. The prime-to-residue-characteristic multiplication on a formal group gives a unique R in this kernel such that 2R=P−2Q0​. Thus Q0​+R is a half of P defined over an unramified local extension. Every other half differs by rational 2-torsion, so it too is unramified. We have proved the unramified halving fields for a split cubic assertion at every v∈/S.
There are only finitely many bounded-degree extensions with restricted ramification of K of degree at most four. This is the permitted number-field finiteness consequence of the Hermite–Minkowski theorem: bounded local degrees bound the discriminant exponents at the finitely many allowed ramified primes, so absolute discriminants are bounded. Each possible halving field L is Galois, and there are only finitely many homomorphisms Gal(L/K)→E[2]. The injection constructed above therefore has finite image. Consequently E(K)/2E(K) is finite. No elliptic-curve finiteness or descent theorem was used in this argument.
For K=Q, write s=#S. Each coordinate of cP​ in a basis of E[2]≅(Z/2Z)2 is a quadratic Galois character unramified outside S. Its quadratic extension has a signed square-free integer representative d=±∏p∈S​pep​, ep​∈{0,1}: an odd valuation outside S would ramify there. There are at most 2s+1 choices, including the trivial character. Hence the pair of characters gives at most 22s+2 cocycles. Each prime in S is at most 2m, because the three root differences have absolute values at most 2m and 2≤2m. Among the integers up to 2m there are at most m primes: two, together with at most m−1 odd candidates. The rational halving quotient bound from root size is therefore
#(E(Q)/2E(Q))≤22s+2≤22m+2.​
(7)

In the one-dimensional commutative convention, a formal group law over R is a formal power series F(X,Y)=X+Y+terms of degree at least two satisfying F(X,0)=X, F(0,Y)=Y, F(X,Y)=F(Y,X) and F(F(X,Y),Z)=F(X,F(Y,Z)). The formal inverse is a series I(T)=−T+O(T2) with F(T,I(T))=0. For R=Zp​ all these series converge on pZp​, which becomes a group with operation F.
Repeated formal addition gives the multiplication isomorphism of a formal group law [n]F​(T)=nT+O(T2). If p∤n, the coefficient n is a unit in the p-adic integers. We can construct an inverse H(T)=n−1T+∑j≥2​hj​Tj by coefficient recursion. At degree j, the equation [n]F​(H(T))=T has the form nhj​+known integral terms=0; dividing by the unit n keeps hj​ integral. The inverse is two-sided by uniqueness of formal composition inverses. Since integral coefficients multiplied by successive powers of t∈pZp​ tend to zero, both series converge on that set and their formal identities hold there. This proves prime-to-residue-characteristic multiplication on a formal group:
[n]F​:F(pZp​)∼​F(pZp​),p∤n.​
(8)
The argument also applies at p=2 for odd n; it does not require a logarithm or torsion-freeness.

For an integral Minimal Weierstrass equation, let E0​(Qp​) be the subgroup of points whose reduction is a smooth point of the reduced cubic. Its reduction is a homomorphism to the smooth-locus group of a singular Weierstrass cubic, or to the whole reduced elliptic curve in the good-reduction case. Define the formal kernel of a minimal Weierstrass equation by
E1​(Qp​)=ker(E0​(Qp​)⟶Ens​(Fp​)).​
(9)
It consists of the identity and the points reducing to the identity at infinity. For a nonidentity affine point this is equivalent to vp​(x)=−2r and vp​(y)=−3r for some positive integer r.
The local parameter t=−x/y, with t(O)=0, gives the group isomorphism
E1​(Qp​)≅E(pZp​),​
(10)
where E is the formal group of an elliptic curve. To see the local coordinates, put w=−1/y in the Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve. The resulting relation is w=t3+a1​tw+a2​t2w+a3​w2+a4​tw2+a6​w3, and its unit linear coefficient gives a unique integral series w(t)=t3+O(t4). Thus x=t/w and y=−1/w have valuations −2vp​(t) and −3vp​(t). The chord-and-tangent group law expressed in this parameter is precisely the integral formal group law E. This description includes bad reduction; only the smooth identity neighborhood is involved.

Direct substitution gives y2+y=x3−x+6 for both points. The displayed Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve is minimal at every prime by the permitted assumption.
For P, neither 364 nor 11737 is divisible by three or five. Therefore
v3​(x(P))=v5​(x(P))=−2,v3​(y(P))=v5​(y(P))=−3.
(11)
The formal kernel of a minimal Weierstrass equation description gives P∈E1​(Q3​)∩E1​(Q5​). Similarly, neither 2059 nor 98783 is divisible by three or seven, so Q∈E1​(Q3​)∩E1​(Q7​), with the same respective coordinate valuations −2,−3.
If a point T∈E1​(Qp​) had finite order of a group element N=pan with p∤n, the point paT would be killed by n. By prime-to-residue-characteristic multiplication on a formal group, multiplication by n is injective on this kernel, so paT=O. Its order is therefore a power of p. Applying this at two distinct primes is the two-prime formal-kernel test for nontorsion: the order of P would be both a power of three and of five, and the order of Q both a power of three and of seven. Each would have to be the identity, although both are affine points. Both P and Q have infinite order.

The elliptic-curve discriminant of y2=x3−12x2+20x is 16⋅202⋅(144−80)=409600=21452. Thus three and eleven are primes of good reduction. Counting the reduced points gives four points over F3​ and sixteen over F11​. For example, the numbers of affine points above successive x=0,1,2 modulo three are 1,1,1. Modulo eleven, for x=0,…,10, they are 1,2,1,2,0,0,2,2,2,2,1; add the point at infinity in each case.
The reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve is injective on torsion prime to the residue characteristic. The three-primary rational torsion injects into the group of order sixteen at eleven, so is trivial; likewise the eleven-primary torsion injects into the group of order four at three. Every other primary component injects at both primes. Therefore the rational torsion subgroup has order dividing four. All four rational 2-torsion points are visible, so
E(Q)tors​={O,(0,0),(2,0),(10,0)}≅(Z/2Z)2.​
(15)
The rational point P=(1,3) lies on the curve. The tangent slope in the elliptic-curve addition formula is (3−24+20)/6=−1/6, giving 2P=(361/36,−323/216). The integral substitution X=x−4 changes the equation to y2=X3−28X−48; the doubled point has X=217/36. The Nagell–Lutz theorem says that a rational torsion point on this integral short equation has integral coordinates, so 2P, and hence P, is nontorsion. An infinite-order point is (1,3).
We prove the rank bound by two-isogeny descent. For a=−12,b=20, the two-isogeny formula gives the partner
E′:Y2=X3+24X2+64X
(16)
and maps ϕ:E→E′, ψ:E′→E with ψϕ=[2]. The two-torsion square-class homomorphism α:E(Q)→Q∗/(Q∗)2 is α(x,y)=[x] away from O,(0,0), with values 1 and [20]=[5] at those exceptional points. Its kernel is ψE′(Q). The corresponding map α′ on E′ has kernel ϕE(Q).
The prime-support bound in two-isogeny descent can be checked directly here. For a prime ℓ∤20, if vℓ​(x)>0 then x2−12x+20 is a unit, so 2vℓ​(y)=vℓ​(x) is even; if vℓ​(x)<0, the x3 term has strictly least valuation and 2vℓ​(y)=3vℓ​(x) is even. Thus the square class of x is supported only on two and five. Also x(x−2)(x−10)≥0 forces x∈[0,2]∪[10,∞), so nonzero x is positive. The possible classes are 1,2,5,10, and the four torsion points provide all of them:
α(O)=1,α((2,0))=2,α((0,0))=5,α((10,0))=10.
(17)
Consequently #α(E(Q))=4.
The same valuation argument on E′ gives possible signed classes 1,−1,2,−2, since its coefficient b′=64 has only the prime two. The identity gives class 1, and (−4,8)∈E′(Q) gives class −1. For d=±2, the two-isogeny descent quartic is
W2=dU4+24U2V2+(64/d)V4,
(18)
with integral coprime U,V after scaling a rational solution. If U is odd, its right side is 2 or 10 modulo sixteen for d=2, and 14 or 6 for d=−2, according to the parity of V. None is a square. If U=2u is even, V is odd and the right side is 32(±u4+3u2V2±V4). The bracket is odd for either parity of u, so its 2-adic valuation is five, again impossible for a square. The signed divisor-two obstruction for an isogeny covering excludes both classes. Hence α′(E′(Q))={1,−1}.
For completeness, the index correction in the two-isogeny index formula over a number field is one: kerψ={O,(0,0)} and ϕ((2,0))=(0,0), so the whole kernel lies in ϕE(Q). Thus
[E(Q):2E(Q)]=#α(E(Q))#α′(E′(Q))=4⋅2=8.
(19)
The Mordell-Weil theorem and the four rational 2-torsion points identify this index as 2r+2. Therefore 2r+2=8 and
rankE(Q)=1.​
(20)
This is the rank-one elliptic curve with roots zero two and ten. We have exhibited a point of infinite order but have not needed to prove that it generates the free part.
