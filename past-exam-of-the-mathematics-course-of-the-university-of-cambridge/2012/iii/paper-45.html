It is essential to distinguish a finite-dimensional spinor matrix from its action on a family of operator components. Take the Pauli matrices and σˉμν in the supplied convention, with η=diag(1,−1,−1,−1). They give
σˉij=21​ϵijk​σk​,σˉ0i=2i​σi​.
(1)
If both displayed transformations are read literally as left multiplication on the column Qˉ​α˙, expanding the operator conjugation and comparing its linear term gives the formal answer [Mμν,Qˉ​]=+σˉμνQˉ​.
There is a sign inconsistency in that literal reading. With the usual angular momentum commutation relations, coefficient matrices Ri​ in [Ji​,qa​]=(Ri​)ab​qb​ must satisfy
[Ri​,Rj​]=−iϵijk​Rk​.
(2)
Indeed, the Jacobi identity gives [Ji​,[Jj​,q]]−[Jj​,[Ji​,q]]=(Rj​Ri​−Ri​Rj​)q, whereas [[Ji​,Jj​],q]=iϵijk​Rk​q. The formal Ri​=+σi​/2 violates this identity. For example it would give [J3​,q1​]=q1​/2, [J+​,q1​]=q2​ and [J3​,q2​]=−q2​/2; the two sides of the Jacobi identity for J3​,J+​,q1​ are then −q2​/2 and 3q2​/2.
A consistent canonical column-operator convention uses the inverse adjoint action for the displayed spinor transformation, equivalently the contragredient operator-component prescription. It gives
[Mμν,Qˉ​α˙]=−(σˉμν)α˙β˙​​Qˉ​β˙​.​
(3)
This is the convention used for the physical states below. Reversing the adjoint action is the minimum needed sign correction in the literal column reading; a dual row notation can encode the same correction by its index contractions. The formal printed-sign coefficients are also recorded below, so the distinction is explicit rather than silently changing a convention. The representation matrices themselves remain Ji​=σi​/2 and Ki​=iσi​/2 in Question 3: the minus sign here concerns commutators of operator components.

A supercharge is an odd Weyl spinor generator. The mixed anticommutator has one undotted and one dotted index, hence transforms as a Lorentz four-vector. In the minimal Super-Poincaré algebra, its only spacetime generator is the four-momentum, so covariance fixes it to cσαβ˙​μ​Pμ​. Normalizing the charges canonically fixes c=2:
{Qα​,Qˉ​β˙​​}=2σαβ˙​μ​Pμ​.​
(4)
The number two is a normalization, not something Lorentz covariance alone could fix under Q↦aQ.
For an explicit closure calculation, use left Grassmann derivatives in superspace and the differential translation convention Pμ​=i∂μ​:
Qα​=∂θα∂​−iσαβ˙​μ​θˉβ˙​∂μ​,Qˉ​β˙​​=−∂θˉβ˙​∂​+iθασαβ˙​μ​∂μ​.
(5)
Each of the two derivative-coordinate cross terms contributes iσμ∂μ​; the other terms cancel by anticommutativity. This proves the displayed coefficient. The same calculation gives {Q,Q}={Qˉ​,Qˉ​}=0 and [Pμ​,Q]=[Pμ​,Qˉ​]=0. There is no scalar central charge for a single supersymmetry label: symmetry of the anticommutator is incompatible with a term proportional only to the antisymmetric ϵαβ​.
In the massive rest frame Pμ​=(m,0,0,0), the mixed relation is {Qα​,Qβ†​}=2mδαβ​. These positive canonical anticommutation relations supply the input for the normalization and finite fermionic Fock space in the question-level continuation.

Write q1​=Qˉ​1˙ and q2​=Qˉ​2˙. In the Jacobi-consistent convention just fixed,
[Ji​,qa​]=−21​(σi​)ab​qb​.
(6)
Applying the commutator product rule twice gives
[J2,qa​]=∑i​(Ji​[Ji​,qa​]+[Ji​,qa​]Ji​)=43​qa​−∑i​(σi​)ab​qb​Ji​.
(7)
Here ∑i​σi2​=3I produces the constant term; moving the Ji​ to the right accounts for it. Consequently
[J2,q1​][J2,q2​]​=43​q1​−q2​(J1​−iJ2​)−q1​J3​,=43​q2​−q1​(J1​+iJ2​)+q2​J3​.​
(8)
Thus the physically consistent numerical coefficients are
(a1​,…,a9​)=(−21​,43​,−1,i,−1,43​,−1,−i,1).​
(9)
If one mechanically retains the literal positive-sign column transformation instead, the coefficient tuple would be (21​,43​,1,−i,1,43​,1,i,−1). It cannot describe a unitary multiplet with the supplied rotation algebra, by the explicit Jacobi counterexample in part (a).
Take the spin-zero Clifford vacuum ∣Ω⟩, normalized to one and annihilated by the two annihilation charges. This is a lowest Fock state of a positive-mass representation, not a zero-energy vacuum annihilated by every charge. For the first one-charge state,
J2q1​∣Ω⟩=43​q1​∣Ω⟩,J3​q1​∣Ω⟩=−21​q1​∣Ω⟩.
(10)
The ladder commutators are [J+​,q1​]=−q2​, [J−​,q1​]=0, [J+​,q2​]=0, [J−​,q2​]=−q1​. Therefore these two states are the j=1/2 pair. A phase choice ∣21​,+21​⟩=−Nq2​∣Ω⟩ makes the usual positive ladder coefficient hold.
Raising the dotted index only changes the oscillator basis by the unitary antisymmetric epsilon matrix, so {qa​,qb†​}=2mδab​. Since qa†​∣Ω⟩=0,
∥qa​∣Ω⟩∥2=2m,N=2m​1​,Nq1​∣Ω⟩:(j,j3​)=(21​,−21​).​
(11)
The positive convention would formally label this state with j3​=+1/2 while J+​ acts nontrivially on it, contradicting the highest-weight ladder rule. This supplies an independent state-level check of the needed sign correction.
The two-charge state is a rotation singlet. Indeed, using q12​=q22​=0 and q2​q1​=−q1​q2​, the product rule gives [Ji​,q1​q2​]=0 for every i: only the sum of the two diagonal spinor coefficients survives, and their trace is zero. Hence the requested commutators and quantum numbers are
[J2,q1​q2​]=[J3​,q1​q2​]=0,N2q1​q2​∣Ω⟩:(j,j3​)=(0,0).​
(12)
Its squared norm is (2m)2 before multiplication by N4, by the two oscillator anticommutators.
The spin-zero massive N=1 supermultiplet therefore consists of ∣Ω⟩, Nq1​∣Ω⟩, Nq2​∣Ω⟩ and N2q1​q2​∣Ω⟩. Their spins are 0,1/2,1/2,0, all with the same mass m. Starting with an even vacuum, the two spin-zero states are bosonic and the two one-charge states fermionic. They are the two real scalar degrees of freedom and the two on-shell fermion polarizations of a massive chiral multiplet. Any product of three creation charges repeats an index and vanishes; annihilation charges reduce to these four states by the canonical anticommutation relations. There are exactly four independent states.

a OurBigBook.com
Assume the standard canonical kinetic action ∫d4θΦ†Φ. A constant field redefinition Φ=Ψ+s is invertible and preserves chirality. Its kinetic action differs by sΨ†+s∗Ψ+∣s∣2, whose full superspace integrals vanish up to a boundary term: a chiral or antichiral field has no independent full-superspace component. Equivalently, spacetime derivatives of a constant are zero. There is no nontrivial Jacobian for this translation of the field. Thus physical predictions are unchanged when all parameters and the coordinate of the vacuum are transformed together.
The transformed superpotential is
W(Ψ+s)=A+(κ+ms+2g​s2)Ψ+2m+gs​Ψ2+6g​Ψ3,
(13)
where A=W(s). Removing the linear term means choosing a critical point W′(s)=0, not merely choosing s=−κ/m while ignoring the cubic term. For g=0,
s=g−m±Δ​​,Δ=m2−2gκ,M:=m+gs=±Δ​.​
(14)
The resulting superpotential is A+MΨ2/2+gΨ3/6. Its constant term has no effect in global supersymmetry, since its chiral superspace integral is zero. It would matter in supergravity, which is not the model here.
A real shift is physically allowed, but it does not always suffice to remove the linear term. With real couplings it requires Δ≥0 for g=0. For example m=0, κ=1, g=2 gives W′(s)=1+s2, which has no real root. A complex shift s=±i is still a valid redefinition of the complex chiral scalar and removes the term. Thus the printed instruction needs either the real-root hypothesis or permission for a complex shift.
If g=0 and m=0, choose s=−κ/m. If g=m=0, no shift removes a nonzero κ; if κ=0 it is already absent. These exceptional branches will also distinguish the supersymmetry-breaking possibilities below.

Eliminating the auxiliary field gives F∗=−W′(φ) and the nonnegative F-term scalar potential
V(φ)=​κ+mφ+2g​φ2​2.​
(15)
Use u=φ−s after the critical-point shift, so that
V(u)=​Mu+2g​u2​2.​
(16)
This notation keeps the original parameters distinct from the shifted mass coefficient M. For real M,g with Mg=0, the real slice is V(u)=u2(M+gu/2)2. It has zero-energy minima at u=0 and u=−2M/g, a maximum between them at u=−M/g with height M4/(4g2), and grows quartically toward both ends. These statements follow by differentiating: V′(u)=2u(M+gu/2)(M+gu).
When M=0 it becomes ∣g∣2u4/4, with one degenerate zero minimum. When g=0, it is ∣M∣2u2 after a permissible shift. For the real parameters with negative discriminant, the original real slice can have a positive minimum even though the full complex scalar potential has zero-energy vacua away from that slice. c OurBigBook.com
A supersymmetric vacuum satisfies F=0, equivalently W′(v)=0, and has V(v)=0. For g=0, the nonconstant quadratic W′ has at least one complex root, including a double root when Δ=0. Since the scalar is complex, these roots are admissible physical vacua whether or not they lie on the real slice. Thus supersymmetry is unbroken at either zero of W′. The same is true for g=0,m=0, where W′ is linear and has the root v=−κ/m.
The exceptional pure-linear model g=m=0,κ=0 has F∗=−κ everywhere and V=∣κ∣2>0. It exhibits flat F-term breaking with a linear superpotential: all scalar values are flat vacua but no state annihilates every supercharge. If also κ=0, the potential is identically zero and supersymmetry is unbroken on a flat moduli space.
Consequently the generic cubic or massive model does not spontaneously break supersymmetry; only the nonzero purely linear branch does in this single-field canonical model. The answer is independent of α.

In the original coordinate, the possible zero-energy vacuum expectation values for g=0 are
v±​=g−m±m2−2gκ​​.​
(17)
They coincide when the discriminant vanishes. With real parameters and a nonnegative discriminant these are real; otherwise the allowed complex scalar values must be retained.
In the coordinate u=φ−s used to remove the linear term, the same vacua are
⟨u⟩=0or−g2M​.​
(18)
The nonzero branch exists in this shifted coordinate when M=0. Let w=−2M/g be that branch and write u=w+φ​. Equivalently, in the original coordinate write φ=v+φ​ with v=s+w. The physical fluctuation field is the same. For g=0,m=0 there is the unique vacuum v=−κ/m; for the constant and pure-linear exceptional branches the scalar is a flat direction, with the supersymmetry status already distinguished in part (c).

First use the actual PDF boost bracket [Ki​,Kj​]=−iϵijk​Jk​; replacing the final Jk​ by Kk​, as the TeX transcription does, would be a different and incorrect algebra. Expanding the three brackets yields
[Ai​,Aj​][Bi​,Bj​][Ai​,Bj​]​=41​([Ji​,Jj​]+i[Ji​,Kj​]+i[Ki​,Jj​]−[Ki​,Kj​])=iϵijk​Ak​,=iϵijk​Bk​,=41​([Ji​,Jj​]−i[Ji​,Kj​]+i[Ki​,Jj​]+[Ki​,Kj​])=0.​
(25)
Thus
so(1,3)C​≅sl2​(C)⊕sl2​(C),J=A+B.​
(26)
The two sets have SU(2)-type brackets. Their compact real form exponentiates to SU(2)×SU(2), whereas their full complexification exponentiates to SL(2,C)×SL(2,C). This does not turn the physical real Lorentz group into a compact product. With Hermitian J,K, A†=B, and the two factors are tied by the Lorentz reality condition; the connected physical spin cover is SL(2,C).
For the right-handed spinor representation, the supplied sigma formula gives σˉij=ϵijk​σk​/2 and σˉ0i=iσi​/2. Therefore
Ji​=2σi​​,Ki​=2iσi​​,Ai​=0,Bi​=2σi​​.​
(27)
It is the (0,1/2) representation in this definition of A,B. These are finite-dimensional representation matrices; the boosts are not Hermitian for a positive-definite spinor inner product, as expected for a noncompact group. They are distinct from the operator-component commutator coefficients discussed in Question 1.
For the group map, encode a real Minkowski vector by the Hermitian matrix
X=xμ​σμ=xνσν​,detX=(x0)2−∣x∣2.
(28)
The trace identity 21​Tr(σˉμσν​)=δμν​ recovers its contravariant components. For N∈SL(2,C), X′=NXN† is Hermitian and has the same determinant. Expanding X′ in that basis gives
x′μ=Λμν​(N)xν,Λμν​(N)=21​Tr(σˉμNσν​N†).​
(29)
The coefficients are real because the trace of a product of Hermitian matrices is real. Equality of determinants for all x proves ΛTηΛ=η. The action for N1​N2​ is the composition of the two actions, proving the homomorphism law.
Since SL(2,C) is connected and the identity maps to the identity, its image has determinant +1 and preserves time orientation. More directly, positive-definite matrices representing future timelike vectors remain positive definite under X↦NXN†. The kernel is {I,−I}: if every Hermitian X is fixed, X=I first makes N unitary, and then commutation with all Hermitian matrices makes it a scalar with determinant one.
Conversely, SU(2) gives every spatial rotation, and positive Hermitian determinant-one matrices exp(b⋅σ/2) give every pure boost. Rotation-boost decomposition yields every proper orthochronous Lorentz transformation. Thus the formula gives the Lorentz spinor double cover
SL(2,C)/{±I}≅SO+(1,3).​
(30)
It is a map into SO(1,3) as asked, with precisely its identity component as image; it does not produce disconnected time-reversing transformations.

The Weyl fields have odd Grassmann parity. With the given epsilon convention, write χα​=(a,b) and ψα​=(c,d), where all four components anticommute. Raising an index gives χα=(b,−a) and ψα=(d,−c). Hence
(χψ)=bc−ad,(ψχ)=da−cb=−ad+bc.
(31)
The minus sign from the antisymmetric epsilon tensor is canceled by exchanging the two Grassmann-odd components. Therefore the Weyl spinor bilinear exchange identity is
(χψ)=(ψχ),C=1.​
(32)
For commuting numerical spinors the exchange sign would instead be negative. The answer here uses fermionic Weyl fields, as appropriate to the supersymmetric model.

Use the same four Grassmann generators and a fixed order a,b,c,d. Their squares vanish, so
(bc−ad)2=−bcad−adbc=−2abcd.
(33)
Meanwhile the two self-contractions are
(ψψ)=dc−cd=−2cd,(χχ)=ba−ab=−2ab,
(34)
and moving the pair cd past ab gives no sign, yielding (ψψ)(χχ)=4abcd. Thus the square of a Weyl spinor bilinear is
(χψ)2=−21​(ψψ)(χχ),D=−21​.​
(35)
The order of individual odd components, rather than an informal commuting-spinor substitution, fixes this sign. For commuting spinors both self-contractions vanish, so no corresponding universal factorization of the generally nonzero mixed square is possible.
