We first construct a genuinely compact Besicovitch set; a sequence of unrelated small-area sets would not suffice. The compact Kakeya construction from small projections below gives the limiting compact set together with its segments.
Let X consist of nonempty compact sets K⊂[0,1]×R whose horizontal projection is exactly [0,1]. With the Hausdorff metric this is a complete metric space. Indeed a Hausdorff-Cauchy sequence is eventually contained in one bounded closed rectangle and converges there to a nonempty compact set; horizontal projection is Lipschitz for Hausdorff distance, so its value [0,1] is preserved in the limit.
For t∈[0,1] put Lt​(x,y)=y+tx and mt​(K)=∣Lt​(K)∣. The function (K,t)↦mt​(K) is upper semicontinuous. To verify this, cover the compact set Lt​(K) by finitely many open intervals with total length less than mt​(K)+ε. Joint continuity of the compact images in the Hausdorff metric puts every sufficiently nearby image inside the same cover. This proves the required upper bound, uniformly over a compact parameter interval when a strict bound holds throughout it.
Fix q∈[0,1] and ε>0. The set
U(q,ε)={K∈X:supt∈[0,1],∣t−q∣≤ε​mt​(K)<2ε}
(33)
is open. It is also dense, by the following explicit finite approximation. Given K and tolerance h>0, partition [0,1] into N equal subintervals and choose (j/N,yj​)∈K at each left endpoint. Above that subinterval put the segment
{(x,yj​−q(x−j/N)):j/N≤x≤(j+1)/N}.
(34)
Each segment lies within 1+q2​/N of its chosen point in K. Add a finite h/2-net of points of K to their union, ensuring the reverse Hausdorff inclusion. For large N the resulting compact set K′ belongs to X and has Hausdorff distance less than h from K. The image of each segment under Lt​ has length ∣t−q∣/N, and the added points contribute zero length. Hence
mt​(K′)≤∣t−q∣≤εif ∣t−q∣≤ε,

Define
B={(t,y+tx):(x,y)∈K, 0≤t≤1}.
(37)
This is the continuous image of the compact set K×[0,1], hence compact. Its section at horizontal coordinate t is Lt​(K), of measure zero. The Fubini theorem gives ∣B∣=∫01​mt​(K)dt=0. For every slope x∈[0,1], horizontal surjectivity supplies (x,y)∈K, and B contains the segment (t,y+tx), 0≤t≤1, of length 1+x2​≥1. Four rotated copies, through angles 0,π/4,π/2,3π/4, cover all unoriented directions. Their finite union B is compact, has area zero, and contains a unit subsegment in every direction:
∣B∣=0,B compact and containing all unit-segment directions.​
(38)
The point-line dual construction is the mechanism in Körner's construction; the density calculation above supplies the needed proof, rather than assuming that small-area sets automatically admit a suitable nested limit.
For the multiplier discussion, define TD​ by TD​g​(ξ)=1{∣ξ∣≤1}​g​(ξ). The Plancherel theorem gives boundedness on L2. We explain why a putative Lp bound, 1<p<2, would contradict Kakeya packing; duality of Lp spaces then treats p>2.
First, such a bound would force the vector-valued inequality

To see the important curvature step, let TR​ project onto the radius-R disk and modulate by eiRvj​⋅x. Its conjugated symbol is 1{∣ξ+Rvj​∣≤R}​, tending almost everywhere to 1{ξ⋅vj​<0}​. Letting R→∞ gives the displayed estimate by L2 convergence and Fatou lemma, first for finitely many smooth function functions and then by approximation. The disk has a boundary normal in every direction; a fixed polygon does not.
The geometric input for this overview is the standard finite Perron tree packing used in Fefferman's original proof: for arbitrarily small η>0 one obtains long thin rectangles Rj​ with A=∑j​∣Rj​∣ and ∣⋃j​Rj​∣≤ηA, while fixed longitudinal translates Rj′​ are pairwise disjoint. This is a quantitative finite arrangement, stronger than merely having a compact null set. The splitting/sliding procedure subdivides an angular triangle into narrow triangles and overlaps their bases; the extensions along their axes separate in reverse order. Its area estimate has the form α2m+2(1−α) relative to the original total triangle area: the merged central part shrinks by α2 at each generation, and the accumulated caps contribute at most 2(1−α). Taking α close to one and then m large makes the ratio arbitrarily small. Inscribed rectangular portions retain a fixed proportion of the total area. The reflected extensions explain why the translates can be disjoint even though the original rectangles overlap strongly.

This explains both the random-sign step and the precise geometric amplification; it is not an assertion that frequency rectangles by themselves produce a logarithmic norm blow-up.
