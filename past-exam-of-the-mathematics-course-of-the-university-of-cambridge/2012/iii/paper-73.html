Work with an incompressible Newtonian fluid of constant kinematic viscosity. The vorticity equation is Dω/Dt=(ω⋅∇)u+ν∇2ω. In the axisymmetric Burgers vortex the swirl does not advect the axial vorticity, and the stretching rate is ∂z​uz​=α. Put s(t)=δ2(t) and W=ωz​. The Gaussian profile has
W∂t​W​=s˙(−s1​+s2r2​),Wur​∂r​W​=sαr2​,W∇2W​=−s4​+s24r2​.
(1)
Substitution into ∂t​W+ur​∂r​W=αW+ν∇2W matches both the constant and r2 terms precisely when
s˙+αs=4ν​.
(2)
For every finite positive initial s0​,
δ2(t)=α4ν​+(s0​−α4ν​)e−αt​,δ(t)⟶4ν/α​.
(3)
The strain compresses the cross-section and stretches its vorticity, while kinematic viscosity spreads the core. The attracting radius is the balance of these effects; its relaxation time is α−1. Total circulation stays fixed because stretching increases the vorticity as the cross-sectional area decreases.
At the steady core radius, the swirl speed is of order Γ0​/δ, and its velocity gradient tensor is of order Γ0​/δ2. Its viscous dissipation integrated over a cross-section therefore scales as
Dtube​∼ν(δ2Γ0​​)2δ2∼αΓ02​​.
(4)
This is per unit length and per unit density. More precisely, for the swirl contribution, integration by parts relates the integrated strain viscous dissipation to ν∫W2dA; the boundary term vanishes for the 1/r swirl velocity. Thus
Dtube​=2πδ2νΓ02​​=8παΓ02​​​.
(5)
Multiply by density for power per physical length. Here the azimuthal velocity's circulation Γ is Γ0​, as follows by integrating W over the cross-section. The result is finite as ν→0 with strain and circulation held fixed. Local core viscous dissipation grows like ν−1 while its area shrinks like ν. For Γ0​/ν≫1, the swirl gradients dominate the background strain within the core.
This finite quantity is the excess viscous dissipation due to the vortex. The uniform straining flow alone dissipates 3να2 per unit volume, and integrating that over an infinite cross-section diverges. The total viscous dissipation of the complete idealized flow is therefore not a finite tube power unless the background is subtracted or spatially bounded.
A Burgers vortex sheet instead smooths a fixed velocity jump U across a layer of thickness δs​∼ν/α​. Its viscous dissipation per sheet area is of order
Dsheet​∼ν(U/δs​)2δs​∼U2να​⟶0.
(6)
A tube with fixed circulation supports finite viscous dissipation in a vanishingly small core; a sheet with fixed velocity jump does not. This makes strained vortex tubes plausible intense dissipative structures at high Reynolds number. It does not assert that sheets are absent from turbulence: sheets can carry inertial range organization and become unstable, producing finer vortical structures.

The high-Reynolds-number balance gives positive mean vortex stretching. The Betchov relation therefore gives ⟨abc⟩<0. To connect this to a fixed longitudinal velocity derivative, additionally assume isotropic turbulence, or average over the direction of that derivative. For a unit vector n, the derivative is d=nTSn. Uniform directional averaging, with trS=0, gives
d2=152​trS2,d3=1058​trS3=358​abc.
(16)
For example these identities follow by averaging products of direction cosines on a sphere. Hence in isotropic turbulence
⟨(∂x​ux​)3⟩=358​⟨abc⟩=−352​⟨ωi​ωj​Sij​⟩<0,
(17)
and division by the positive derivative variance to the power 3/2 gives negative longitudinal velocity-gradient skewness. Homogeneity alone does not fix the orientation statistics and does not establish this sign for every chosen coordinate direction.
Order the principal strain rates as a≥b≥c. Except at degeneracies, a>0 and c<0, so abc<0 corresponds to b>0: two extensional directions and one compressive direction, or bi-axial strain. The negative average of abc says that such configurations dominate this cubic amplitude-weighted statistic over axial-extension configurations with b<0. In the usual physical interpretation, comparably intense strain events are therefore biased toward bi-axial extension.
An unweighted frequency claim needs more information. As a counterexample to deducing it solely from the cubic average, assign strain eigenvalues (1,1,−2) to 10% of samples and (−0.1,−0.1,0.2) to 90%, with randomly oriented eigenvectors. Then ⟨abc⟩=−0.1982<0, despite bi-axial extension occurring in only 10% of the samples. This example addresses what the moment inequality can infer, not a construction of a complete turbulent velocity field. A measured frequency preference is a further statistical observation.
The geometric significance is that a fluid element stretches in two directions and contracts in the third, becoming sheet-like while preserving volume. Distributed vorticity is swept into thin layers and its component along an extensional direction is amplified. The associated shear layers can subsequently roll up and produce vortex tubes. Thus the weighted strain bias helps explain sheet formation in the energy cascade even though the most intense dissipative structures can be tubes.

In the Townsend-Betchov cascade cartoon, bi-axial strain sweeps distributed vorticity into extended sheets at the upper part of the inertial range. These are predominantly inviscid structures: their shape and instability arise from advection and strain rather than local viscous smoothing. Strong velocity variation across a thin sheet permits Kelvin-Helmholtz instability, roll-up and three-dimensional deformation. The resulting small vortex tubes are stretched into still finer cores.
At the dissipative end, stretching and kinematic viscosity balance in Burgers-like cores. A core of strain rate s has radius of order (ν/s)1/2; taking s∼(ϵ/ν)1/2 gives the Kolmogorov length (ν3/ϵ)1/4. Circulation and shrinking cross-section allow finite integrated tube viscous dissipation. Thus sheet formation and tube formation play different roles, rather than the entire cascade being a succession of identical geometrical eddies.
The cartoon challenges a literal, uniformly self-similar, step-by-step cascade with independent interactions only between neighboring scales. Large-scale sheet formation can generate much smaller scales through an instability, and the resulting structures retain coherent spatial organization. A branching model that assumes every scale-transfer step has the same geometry and loses information about the parent flow can miss this mechanism. Closures that erase the relevant phase correlations also have difficulty representing its concentrated gradients and intermittency. The objection is to these dynamical assumptions, not to conservation of energy or the existence of a mean energy flux through scales. The cartoon alone is not a proof that all spectral transfer is nonlocal.

Internal intermittency is the uneven distribution of activity within an already turbulent fluid: intense gradients and viscous dissipation occupy relatively small, fluctuating regions separated by much quieter regions. It concerns the internal field, rather than a turbulent/non-turbulent interface intermittently passing a probe.
Integral-scale intermittency is the variability of the energy-containing eddies and their local or time-dependent energy supply. Its modulation occurs on lengths and times of order ℓ and ℓ/u, can depend strongly on forcing or initial conditions, and changes the background intensity experienced by smaller eddies.
Equilibrium-range intermittency concerns the increasing fluctuations of local transfer and viscous dissipation as the averaging scale is reduced below the integral scale of turbulence. It appears as non-Gaussian velocity-gradient statistics, fluctuating coarse-grained viscous dissipation and anomalous longitudinal structure function exponents. It includes the inertial range and its continuation into the dissipation range, where kinematic viscosity ultimately limits gradients. Large-scale modulation varies the local energy supply; small-scale intermittency concentrates and redistributes that supply. These effects can coexist, so they should not be identified simply because both broaden a distribution.

For the Landau intermittency counterexample, combine two locally homogeneous turbulent populations, with the same sufficiently wide inertial interval but different viscous dissipation rates ϵ1​ and ϵ2​. This can mean an ensemble randomly selected between two intensities, or large turbulent patches whose small-scale increments are sampled away from the patch boundaries. Suppose each population individually obeys the proposed universal coefficient βp​. If their proportions are w and 1−w, the combined longitudinal structure function is
Sp​(r)=βp​rp/3[wϵ1p/3​+(1−w)ϵ2p/3​],ϵ=wϵ1​+(1−w)ϵ2​.
(18)
Its effective coefficient referenced to the global mean viscous dissipation is therefore
βpeff​=βp​[wϵ1​+(1−w)ϵ2​]p/3wϵ1p/3​+(1−w)ϵ2p/3​​​.
(19)
For a concrete example let w=1/2 and ϵ1,2​=ϵ(1±d), with 0<d<1. Then
βp​βpeff​​=2(1+d)p/3+(1−d)p/3​.
(20)
For p=6 this is 1+d2, explicitly dependent on the strength of the large-scale modulation. For p=2 it is less than one by concavity. Thus coefficients based solely on mean viscous dissipation cannot be universal under arbitrary integral-scale intermittency. The common inertial interval can be maintained by taking both local Reynolds numbers sufficiently large.
At p=3 the ratio is exactly one. This exception is consistent with the signed third-order energy-flux law and must not be used to rescue universality of all other orders. For noninteger orders one uses absolute increments; the even-order examples already establish the counterexample without a sign convention ambiguity. The issue is the difference between a moment of the local viscous dissipation and the same power of its mean, not a failure of averaging itself.

Consider an unforced incompressible flow with localized vorticity and sufficient smoothness for integration by parts. Define the hydrodynamic impulse per unit density by I=21​∫x×ωdV, denoting the vector impulse by I to distinguish it from the later scalar L. The vorticity equation can be written
∂t​ω=∇×F,F=u×ω−ν∇×ω.
(33)
The supplied identity, integrated over all space, gives 21​∫x×∇×FdV=∫FdV, because F is localized and its surface terms vanish. Therefore
dtdI​=∫u×ωdV−ν∫∇×ωdV.
(34)
The second integral is a vanishing surface integral. The vector identity u×ω=∇(∣u∣2/2)−(u⋅∇)u and incompressibility reduce the first to surface terms proportional to the quadratic velocity. Those vanish for u=O(R−3). Thus
dI/dt=0​.
(35)
For a large spherical control volume define PR​=∫VR​​udV. The impulse relation is I=(3/2)limR→∞​PR​ for this localized field. The factor is important: impulse is not simply the integral of velocity, since an R−3 velocity tail has a nonvanishing contribution in the impulse integration by parts involving a factor of R.
Using pressure per unit density Π and the Navier-Stokes equation gives
dtdPR​​=−∫∂VR​​[u(u⋅n)+Πn−ν∂n​u],dA.
(36)
The advective flux is O(R−4), the pressure force is O(R−1), and the viscous term is O(νR−2) under the regular far-field derivative decay. All vanish as R→∞. The localized eddy cannot change its total impulse without a net external momentum input. Pressure redistributes momentum internally but its resultant force on an increasingly distant control surface tends to zero. At finite R, boundary forces need not vanish exactly; the all-space limit is the invariant statement.

(47)
At the origin smooth isotropic correlations give K=O(r) or smaller and C′=O(r), so both boundary terms vanish. At infinity the usual regular power-law decay K=O(r−4), with the corresponding derivative decay, makes the first term tend to zero. A sufficiently smooth integrable trace correlation makes r2C′→0 as well. Under these physical far-field conditions,
dL/dt=0​.
(48)
The exact required conditions are the limits of these two boundary fluxes. A bare big-O condition on K without control of its derivatives is not by itself a rigorous sufficient condition: the smooth tail K(r)=r−4sin(r2) is O(r−4), but r−1(r4K)′=2cos(r2) has no zero limit. The intended power-law assumption excludes such increasingly rapid far-field oscillations; this qualification is needed in a literal proof.
Physically, L is the large-volume momentum variance divided by volume. Internal advective and pressure forces exchange momentum among eddies but do not provide a net impulse to the whole unforced field. For a large spherical region, the variance balance is driven by its boundary momentum flux. The stated correlation decay makes that contribution negligible per unit volume as the region grows. Thus the conservation law preserves momentum-fluctuation density, not kinetic energy. kinematic viscosity can remove kinetic energy while leaving this integral invariant.

Let E=⟨∣u∣2⟩/2 be the turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass. In addition to L>0 and high Reynolds number, use the usual self-preserving large-eddy decay assumption: the normalized large-scale correlation has a fixed shape, so
L=CL​Eℓ3,ℓ=(CL​EL​)1/3,
(49)
where CL​>0 is constant. For freely decaying high-Reynolds-number turbulence, the cascade viscous dissipation law is ϵ=Cϵ​E3/2/ℓ. The kinetic-energy balance therefore gives
dtdE​=−Cϵ​CL1/3​L−1/3E11/6.
(50)
Put A=Cϵ​CL1/3​L−1/3. Integrating,
E(t)=[E(t0​)−5/6+65​A(t−t0​)]−6/5​,E∝(t−t∗​)−6/5,ℓ∝(t−t∗​)2/5​.
(51)
The constant t∗​ is a virtual time origin. This is the Saffman decay law. The energy decreases while the integral scale of turbulence grows so that the conserved large-volume momentum variance remains constant.
The exponent does not follow from the invariant alone: it also uses unforced decay, one evolving large-eddy length and an asymptotically constant cascade viscous dissipation coefficient. A changing normalized spectral shape or a confining boundary can invalidate that closure. Moreover Re∼E1/2ℓ/ν∝(t−t∗​)−1/5, so at fixed kinematic viscosity the high-Reynolds-number decay regime eventually ends; the −6/5 law is not a claim about the ultimate viscous limit.
