Assume the integrals exist and the boundary flux vanishes, as for an isolated finite system with adequate decay. With the stellar kinetic-energy tensor, stellar pressure-energy tensor and stellar potential-energy tensor defined by
Tij​=21​∫ρui​uj​d3x,Πij​=∫ρσij​d3x,Wij​=−∫ρxi​∂j​Φd3x,

The stellar kinetic-energy tensor describes ordered motion, whereas the stellar pressure-energy tensor describes random motion. In particular, Tij​=0 does not mean the stars have no kinetic energy. If a surface flux is retained, the right side of the displayed equilibrium identity is that surface tensor; it cannot be discarded for an arbitrary finite aperture. For an isolated self-gravitating population, Wij​ is symmetric by pairwise interchange in the Newtonian interaction integral, and the time-dependent identity is 21​I¨ij​=2Tij​+Πij​+Wij​ with Iij​=∫ρxi​xj​d3x.
For the spherical Newtonian gravitational potential, ∂j​Φ=Φ′(r)xj​/r. In the pressure-supported case the tensor virial theorem gives Πii​=∫ρΦ′(r)xi2​/rd3x. The notation ΠRR​ used in this question is the planar virial trace Πxx​+Πyy​, not just the integral of the local cylindrical radial velocity dispersion. In cylindrical coordinates this trace includes both vR2​ and vϕ2​. Summing the two Cartesian components therefore gives
Πzz​ΠRR​​=∫ρΦ′(r)z2/rd3x∫ρΦ′(r)R2/rd3x​.​

The distinction explains the factor of two in the spherical limit.
Use ordinary spherical coordinates, R=rsinθ, z=rcosθ. The mass density separates as ρ0​r−γh(θ), where
h(θ)=(sin2θ+q−2cos2θ)−γ/2.
(16)
Both potential-energy integrals have the same radial factor 2πρ0​∫0∞​r3−γΦ′(r)dr. When this factor is finite and positive it cancels, leaving the scale-free tracer virial ratio
Πzz​ΠRR​​=∫0π​sinθcos2θ(sin2θ+q−2cos2θ)−γ/2dθ∫0π​sin3θ(sin2θ+q−2cos2θ)−γ/2dθ​.​
(17)
The angular factor in the printed intermediate formula has its sine and cosine interchanged. Keeping its sine/cosine weights while using that printed factor would instead produce the opposite sign in the final small-flattening correction. Merely measuring θ from the equatorial plane cannot fix this: the measure and both moment weights would have to change as well.
The angular ratio is finite and positive for every fixed q>0 and finite real γ, because h is positive and bounded above and below. However, the claim that the unrestricted global virial integrals are always well-defined needs a hypothesis. In a point-mass Newtonian gravitational potential, the common radial factor is proportional to ∫0∞​r1−γdr: convergence at zero requires γ<2, while convergence at infinity requires γ>2. There is no such γ. If the global energies diverge, the boxed angular ratio is still the ratio of the two potential-energy integrals with identical spherical inner and outer cutoffs, independent of the cutoffs. It is not a quotient of two finite global stellar pressure-energy tensors; applying a finite-aperture tensor virial theorem additionally requires its surface terms. These convergence and boundary qualifications are separate from the printed angular typo.
Set ϵ=q−2−1. For modest flattening, the Taylor expansion is h=1−(γ/2)ϵcos2θ+O(ϵ2). The required elementary angular integrals are
∫0π​sin3θdθ=34​,∫0π​sin3θcos2θdθ=154​,∫0π​sinθcos2θdθ=32​,∫0π​sinθcos4θdθ=52​.
(18)
These follow directly by t=cosθ, or from the supplied gamma function identity. Hence the numerator is (4/3)[1−γϵ/10+O(ϵ2)] and the denominator is (2/3)[1−3γϵ/10+O(ϵ2)]. The flattening–anisotropy virial relation becomes
2Πzz​ΠRR​​=1+5γ​(q−2−1)+O((q−2−1)2).​
(19)
For a decreasing tracer mass density (γ>0), oblate flattening (q<1) requires more random kinetic support per in-plane direction than vertically. In a spherical Newtonian gravitational field, directional orbital anisotropy supplies the flattening rather than an anisotropic force law. A steeper radial mass density amplifies the required velocity dispersion anisotropy. A prolate tracer reverses the leading inequality. The relation constrains integrated velocity dispersions, not a unique local galactic distribution function.
