For a continuous semimartingale X, its quadratic variation is the continuous increasing zero-starting process obtained as the limit, uniformly on compact time intervals in probability, of sums of squared increments along deterministic partitions whose mesh tends to zero. If X=X0​+N+V is its continuous local-martingale/finite-variation decomposition, then [X]=[N]; finite-variation terms and their cross sums vanish.
Write D=dQ/dP. If P(En​)→0, then for every R>0,
Q(En​)≤RP(En​)+EP​[D1{D>R}​].
(11)
First send n to infinity and then R to infinity. Thus convergence in probability under P implies convergence in probability under Q, also for a supremum on a compact time interval. The same squared-increment sums converge under Q to the P version of [X]. Since X is also a semimartingale under Q, these sums converge to its Q quadratic variation. Uniqueness of limits in probability gives equality at every fixed time; continuity and countably many rational times give
[X]Q=[X]PQ-indistinguishably.​
(12)
Absolute continuity suffices; equivalence is not required. This is quadratic variation under an absolutely continuous measure change.

On a compact time interval a continuous path avoiding the origin has a strictly positive minimum radius. Thus the times S1/n​ tend to infinity almost surely. Part (a) localizes Mt​=log∣Bt​∣, so M is a continuous local martingale.
To show that it is not a martingale, compute its expectation at a positive deterministic time. In distribution Bt​=(1,0)+Wt​, where Wt​ is a centered isotropic Gaussian vector. Conditional on ρ=∣Wt​∣, its angle is uniform. The angular average of a logarithmic potential is
2π1​∫02π​log∣1+ρeiθ∣dθ=logmax(1,ρ).
(24)
For ρ<1 this follows by averaging the real part of the power series for log(1+ρeiθ); for ρ>1 factor out ρ and apply the same argument to ρ−1. The value at 1 follows by the integrable logarithmic singularity, or is irrelevant for the continuously distributed radius.
The radius has density (ρ/t)e−ρ2/(2t). The logarithm of ∣Bt​∣ is absolutely integrable: near the origin its Gaussian density is bounded and ∫01​r∣logr∣dr<∞; away from the origin its positive part is bounded by a constant plus ∣Bt​∣. Therefore conditioning is legitimate, and integration by parts yields
EMt​=∫1∞​logρtρ​e−ρ2/(2t)dρ=∫1∞​ρe−ρ2/(2t)​dρ>0(t>0).​
(25)
Since M0​=0, this contradicts preservation of expectation by a martingale. Thus the logarithmic radius of planar Brownian motion is a strict local martingale. It is not a nonnegative local martingale; its negative values allow its expectation to increase.
