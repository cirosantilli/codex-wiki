Every Non-Archimedean absolute value on Q is either the trivial absolute value, or ∣⋅∣pc​ for a unique prime p and some c>0. Here ∣p∣p​=p−1.
To prove this classification, the ultrametric inequality gives ∣n∣≤1 for all integers n. If every nonzero integer has absolute value one, multiplicativity makes the absolute value on a field trivial on all rational numbers. Otherwise some positive integer has absolute value less than one, and its prime factorization gives a prime p with 0<∣p∣<1. There cannot be two such primes: if p=q and ap+bq=1 with integers a,b, then 1≤max(∣p∣,∣q∣)<1, a contradiction. Every other prime consequently has absolute value one. Unique prime factorization now gives
∣x∣=∣p∣vp​(x)=p−cvp​(x),c=−logplog∣p∣​>0.
(1)
Conversely these are Non-Archimedean absolute values, by the valuation inequality vp​(x+y)≥min(vp​(x),vp​(y)). Thus ∣⋅∣=∣⋅∣pc​ (c>0) or the trivial value​. This is the non-Archimedean part of Ostrowski theorem, including the trivial case.

Write O={x:∣x∣≤1}. First, O is compact. A compact neighborhood of zero contains a sufficiently small closed ball tmO, since ∣t∣m→0; that ball is closed in the compact neighborhood. Scaling back proves compactness of O. Also K is complete: a Cauchy sequence has a tail in a translate of a compact neighborhood, hence a convergent subsequence, and the Cauchy condition makes the whole sequence converge. This is the completeness of locally compact nontrivially valued fields argument.
The additive subgroup tO is open: a sufficiently small ball around any of its points stays in tO by the ultrametric inequality. Thus the compact discrete quotient O/tO is finite. Choose a set A⊂O of representatives, with zero representing the zero coset. For x∈O, recursively choose an​∈A and xn+1​∈O such that xn​=an​+txn+1​, beginning with x0​=x. Then
x=∑j=0m−1​aj​tj+tmxm​,∣tmxm​∣≤∣t∣m⟶0.
(2)
For arbitrary x∈K, multiply first by a large power of t to put it in O, then divide the resulting expansion by that power. This gives the digit expansion with a nonuniformizer.
For uniqueness, take the first exponent r at which two expansions differ. After dividing their difference by tr, all the later terms belong to the closed subgroup tO, whereas ar​−br​ does not, because the representatives are distinct. The difference cannot be zero. Conversely every series with coefficients in A and only finitely many nonzero negative-index terms converges by completeness and ∣t∣<1. Every element therefore has a unique expansion x=n≥r∑​an​tn​, with the harmless initial zero coefficients understood as part of the same indexed series. No assumption that t is a uniformizer is needed.

Take the formal Laurent series field K=C((T)), with vT​ the least exponent of a nonzero term and ∣x∣=2−vT​(x). Its value group is Z, so this is a discrete valuation. A Cauchy sequence has an eventually stable coefficient at every exponent, and its tail has a common lower bound on its exponents. The stabilized coefficients therefore give a formal Laurent series field element to which the sequence converges. Hence K is complete.
Its valuation ring is C[[T]], and its residue field is the infinite field C. If K were locally compact, the previous compact-ball argument would make C[[T]] compact. Its quotient by the open ideal TC[[T]] would then be compact and discrete, hence finite, contradicting its identification with C. Thus C((T)) is complete and discretely valued but not locally compact​.

A useful version is the strong form of Hensel lemma. Let K be complete for a Non-Archimedean absolute value, let f∈OK​[X], and suppose a∈OK​ satisfies
∣f(a)∣<∣f′(a)∣2.
(4)
Then there is a unique root α in the ball ∣α−a∣<∣f′(a)∣, and ∣α−a∣≤∣f(a)/f′(a)∣.
Put h=∣f′(a)∣ and η=∣f(a)∣/h2<1. Starting with x0​=a, apply Newton iteration over a valued field xn+1​=xn​−f(xn​)/f′(xn​). Taylor expansion over the valuation ring gives
∣f(x+u)−f(x)−f′(x)u∣≤∣u∣2,∣f′(x+u)−f′(x)∣≤∣u∣
(5)
for integral x,u. Inductively ∣f′(xn​)∣=h and ∣f(xn​)∣/h2≤η2n. Indeed the Newton increment has size at most hη2n<h, so the derivative size is unchanged, and the new function value is bounded by the square of the increment. The increments tend to zero, and the ultrametric inequality makes the sequence Cauchy. Completeness supplies a root with the stated distance bound.
If b,c are distinct points of the ball, their divided difference is f′(a) plus a term of size at most max(∣b−a∣,∣c−a∣)<h, so (f(b)−f(c))/(b−c) is nonzero. Two roots in that ball are impossible. This proves the stated Hensel lemma. In particular a simple root modulo the maximal ideal lifts uniquely to a root in its residue class.

At odd residue characteristic use a unit with nonsquare residue. The square classes are open in Kv×​. Indeed the strong form of Hensel lemma applied to X2−u at 1 shows that u is a square whenever v(u−1)>2v(2). Normalize vK​ to have value group Z, and extend it to an algebraic closure. Suppose α is a root of a monic Eisenstein polynomial of degree n. A root cannot have nonpositive valuation: the leading term would then have uniquely smallest valuation. If w=vK​(α)>0, the constant term has valuation one, all the other nonleading terms have valuation strictly greater than one, and the leading term has valuation nw. Cancellation forces nw=1. The value group of K(α) is (1/e)Z, so n∣e. Since e≤[K(α):K]≤n, equality holds throughout. Thus the polynomial is irreducible and the extension is totally ramified.
Conversely, let L/K be totally ramified of degree n, and choose a uniformizer πL​. Its extended vK​ value is 1/n. The same denominator argument gives [K(πL​):K]≥n, so K(πL​)=L. Every conjugate has valuation 1/n, by the unique extension of an absolute value to a finite extension of a complete field. The nonleading coefficients of its minimal polynomial are elementary symmetric expressions in these conjugates, so their valuations are positive, hence at least one because the coefficients lie in K. The constant coefficient is their product and has valuation exactly one. Its minimal polynomial is an Eisenstein polynomial, and L=K(πL​)​.

One form of Krasner's lemma is: if K is a complete non-Archimedean field, α is separable over K, and β is algebraic with
∣β−α∣<minα′=α​∣α−α′∣,
(16)
where α′ ranges over the other K-conjugates, then K(α)⊆K(β). If α∈K, the conclusion is immediate.
To prove the nontrivial case, let σ be an automorphism of an algebraic closure fixing K(β). The unique extension of an absolute value to a finite extension implies that σ preserves the extended absolute value. Therefore
∣σ(α)−α∣≤max(∣σ(α)−β∣,∣β−α∣)=∣β−α∣.
(17)
This is strictly less than the distance to every other conjugate, so σ(α)=α. As α is separable over K(β), being fixed by all these automorphisms puts it in K(β). This proves K(α)⊆K(β)​. The strict inequality is what prevents switching to another conjugate.

We count extensions up to K-isomorphism. An extension of degree n has a maximal unramified extension E/K inside it, with [E:K]=f∣n, and L/E is totally ramified of degree e=n/f. The unramified extension of each degree is unique, so there are only finitely many possibilities for E.
Fix E and e. The coefficient space of monic degree-e Eisenstein polynomials is compact: its nonconstant nonleading coefficients lie in the compact maximal ideal, and its constant coefficient lies in the compact set mE​∖mE2​. Each such polynomial F has a root α generating a degree-e extension. For sufficiently close coefficients of a polynomial G, the numbers G(α) and G′(α)−F′(α) are arbitrarily small, while F′(α)=0 because the characteristic is zero. The strong form of Hensel lemma, applied in E(α), supplies a root β of G as close to α as desired. Choose the neighborhood so that the Krasner's lemma inequality holds. Then E(α)⊆E(β), and both degrees are e, so the extensions are equal. Compactness gives a finite cover by such neighborhoods, proving finiteness of degree-n extensions. If extensions are realized as subfields of a fixed algebraic closure rather than counted up to isomorphism, each has only finitely many embeddings, so that count is finite too.

Herbrand quotient and local norm indices. Let G=⟨σ⟩ have order n. For an additive G-module M, put D=σ−1 and N=1+σ+⋯+σn−1. The relevant Tate cohomology of a cyclic group groups are
H0(G,M)=MG/NM,H−1(G,M)=kerN/DM.
(19)
If both groups are finite, their size ratio is the Herbrand quotient
hG​(M)=#H−1(G,M)#H0(G,M)​.
(20)
Multiplicative modules use products for N and D(m)=σ(m)/m. A finite module has quotient one: #kerN=#M/#NM, while #DM=#M/#MG, and the two cohomology orders are equal. The six-term periodic cohomology sequence shows h(B)=h(A)h(C) for a short exact sequence 0→A→B→C→0 when these groups are finite. Thus the quotient is unchanged by finite-index changes of lattices. For the trivial G-module Z, the zeroth group is Z/nZ and the negative first group is zero, so hG​(Z)=n.
Now let L/K be a cyclic extension of p-adic fields of degree n. For a sufficiently deep principal unit subgroup ULr​=1+mLr​, the p-adic logarithm is a G-equivariant isomorphism with the additive p-adic lattice mLr​; it suffices to take r>vL​(p)/(p−1). The normal basis theorem makes L a regular K[G]-module. Consequently mLr​, as a Zp​[G]-lattice, is commensurable with a direct sum of [K:Qp​] copies of Zp​[G]. These regular lattices have zero Tate groups: its invariants are the multiples of the sum of the basis elements, every such element is a norm, and vectors with coefficient sum zero are images of D. Commensurability and the finite-module calculation give hG​(ULr​)=1. Since UL​/ULr​ is finite, hG​(UL​)=1. These comparisons also establish finiteness of the Tate groups concerned.
The valuation exact sequence 1→UL​→L×→Z→0 now gives hG​(L×)=n. Hilbert theorem 90 makes H−1(G,L×) trivial. One can prove the cyclic statement directly: for a of norm one set A0​=1, Ai​=aσ(a)⋯σi−1(a) and choose c for which b=∑i​Ai​σi(c)=0. Such a c exists by linear independence of distinct field automorphisms. Then σ(b)=b/a, so a=b/σ(b) is a coboundary. Therefore the local cyclic norm index is
[K×:NL/K​L×]=[L:K]=n.​
(21)
This is the central use of the Herbrand quotient: it calculates a norm index without first constructing the local reciprocity map.
Let e,f be the ramification index and residue degree. With normalized integer valuations, vK​(Nx)=fvL​(x). A norm is a unit exactly when its preimage is a unit, and the norm valuations fill fZ. Hence there is an exact sequence
1→OK×​/NOL×​→K×/NL×→Z/fZ→0.
(22)
The unit norm index is [OK×​:NOL×​]=e​. In an unramified extension all units are norms and the obstruction is the valuation modulo n; in a totally ramified cyclic extension the entire index comes from units. The norm subgroup is open: on deep principal units, logarithm carries the norm to the field trace, and the trace of a full p-adic lattice contains a sufficiently deep p-adic lattice in K.
Hilbert norm residue symbol and the local-to-global principle. More generally, for a local field F containing μm​, fix the local reciprocity map recF​ with uniformizers acting as arithmetic Frobenius on unramified extensions. The Hilbert norm residue symbol is
(a,b)F,m​=a1/mrecF​(b)(a1/m)​∈μm​.
(23)
This is independent of the chosen root, is bilinear, and has value one exactly when b is a norm from F(a1/m). These are standard consequences of Local Artin reciprocity. The case m=2, for which the values are signs, is the one directly governing quadratic forms. The quadratic Hilbert symbol at a place v is defined for a,b∈Kv×​ by
(a,b)v​={1,−1,​b∈NKv​(a​)/Kv​​(Kv​(a​)×),otherwise,​
(24)
with value one for all b when a is a square. Equivalently it is one precisely when z2=ax2+by2 has a nonzero solution over Kv​. For nonsquare a, a solution has y=0 and gives b=(z/y)2−a(x/y)2; the converse follows from the same norm identity. When a is square the conic is already isotropic. Symmetry follows from this conic criterion. The local cyclic norm index gives a norm subgroup of index two; its sign character is multiplicative in b, and symmetry gives multiplicativity in a. Thus the symbol is a nondegenerate bilinear pairing on the square-class group of a field, since every nonsquare a gives a nontrivial norm character. Also (a,−a)v​=1, because N(a​)=−a, and (a,a)v​=(a,−1)v​.
For an odd-residue-characteristic p-adic field with residue size q, write a=πru, b=πsw, and let χ be the quadratic character of the residue units. Then
(a,b)=(−1)rs(q−1)/2χ(u)sχ(w)r.​
(25)
The unramified quadratic extension has every unit as a norm and only even norm valuations; in a ramified quadratic extension the norm of a unit has square residue. The preceding unit norm index is two, so the square-residue condition in the ramified case is also sufficient. These facts, together with N(π​)=−π, determine the formula on the generators u,π of the square-class group. At a real place the symbol is negative exactly when both arguments are negative, and at a complex place it is always one. For Q2​, with u,w odd units, the dyadic formula is
(a,b)2​=(−1)2u−1​2w−1​+r8w2−1​+s8u2−1​.
(26)
Only residue classes modulo eight and the parities of r,s enter; other dyadic fields retain the norm definition.
The Hilbert reciprocity law states ∏v​(a,b)v​=1 for a,b∈K×. Only finitely many factors can be nontrivial: outside the places above two, the Archimedean places, and the finite places where a or b is not a unit, the odd-residue formula gives one. For K=Q this product formula is a formulation of quadratic reciprocity, including its supplementary laws. It forces local norm obstructions to occur with compatible parity. It is a necessary compatibility law, not by itself a substitute for the following local-to-global theorem.
The Hasse-Minkowski theorem states that a nondegenerate quadratic form over a number field has a nonzero isotropic vector if and only if it does over every completion. Equivalently, two nondegenerate quadratic forms are isometric globally if and only if they are isometric at every place. The forward directions are immediate; the reverse directions are the substantive global theorem. Over non-Archimedean completions, local isometry classes are determined by dimension, determinant square class and the Hasse invariant of a quadratic form
ϵv​(⟨a1​,…,am​⟩)=∏i<j​(ai​,aj​)v​.
(27)
At real places one uses signature, and at complex places dimension suffices. Reciprocity gives ∏v​ϵv​(q)=1 for a globally diagonalized form. These invariants make the theorem practically usable: local square classes and norm characters replace an unrestricted search for rational solutions. In particular every quadratic form of dimension at least five over a p-adic field is isotropic; for such a form over a number field the only isotropy obstructions are definite signatures at real places.
As a concrete application, take nonsquare a∈K×. Then b is a global norm from K(a​) if and only if (a,b)v​=1 at every place. The local conditions make the ternary form ⟨1,−a,−b⟩ isotropic everywhere. Hasse-Minkowski theorem gives a global solution of z2−ax2=by2; since a is nonsquare, y cannot be zero, and division by y gives the global norm. For example −1 fails to be a norm from Q(i) already at the real place. Thus the quadratic Hilbert symbol detects local norm solvability, while the Hasse-Minkowski theorem turns solvability at all places into a global quadratic solution. The reciprocity and local-to-global theorems in this essay are stated as standard results; the norm interpretation, bilinearity and application are derived above.
