Write Ph=Eh(X) and Pn​h=n−1∑i​h(Xi​) for the expectation and empirical measure. A sufficient bracketing of a function class condition is that, for every ε>0, finitely many brackets [lj​,uj​] cover H, with measurable integrable endpoints and P(uj​−lj​)<ε. The uniform strong law from finite L1 bracketing then gives
h∈Hsup​∣Pn​h−Ph∣⟶0almost surely.​
(1)
Here the observations are independent and identically distributed. For an uncountable class, one either assumes a measurable supremum, for example through a pointwise separable function class, or formulates the conclusion as a pathwise bound on a common probability-one event.
For the parameterized class, the Heine-Borel theorem makes Θ compact. Put M(x)=supθ∈Θ​∣q(θ,x)∣. A countable dense subset of Θ gives the same supremum because of continuity, so M is measurable. Define the modulus
wδ​(x)=supθ,θ′∈Θ∣θ−θ′∣≤δ​​∣q(θ,x)−q(θ′,x)∣.
(2)
The supremum is measurable by taking a countable dense subset of the compact set of admissible pairs. For each x, uniform continuity on Θ gives wδ​(x)→0. Also wδ​≤2M, and PM<∞. Thus the dominated convergence theorem gives Pwδ​→0.
Choose a finite net of radius δ θ1​,…,θN​ in Θ. The lower and upper envelopes of q(θ,⋅) over each closed ball Θ∩B(θj​,δ) are measurable integrable function brackets; the same countable-dense-set argument applies within each such compact ball. Their widths are at most w2δ​. They cover the whole class, so its bracketing of a function class condition follows. Moreover, dominated convergence shows that θ↦Pq(θ,⋅) is continuous. Both empirical and population functions therefore have a supremum over a common countable dense parameter set. Applying the uniform strong law from finite L1 bracketing proves uniform almost-sure convergence over the entire compact parameter set, rather than merely convergence at each fixed parameter.
For the exponential family,
logf(θ,x)=θx−K(θ)+logf0​(x),supθ​∣logf(θ,X)∣≤A∣X∣+B+∣logf0​(X)∣,
(3)
where A=supΘ​∣θ∣ and B=supΘ​∣K(θ)∣. Thus a weak sufficient condition is bounded K on Θ, f0​(X)>0 almost surely, and integrability of ∣X∣+∣logf0​(X)∣ under the sampling law. No positive lower bound on f0​ over the whole real line is required. Zeros off the sampling support are harmless: choose arbitrary finite versions of the log-densities on that common null set when applying the function-class theorem.
If the sampling law is f(θ0​,⋅), one convenient assumption is that Θ is a compact subset of the interior of the finite domain of the cumulant function of an exponential family, and
∫eθ0​xf0​(x)∣logf0​(x)∣dx<∞.
(4)
On that interior, K is continuous, hence bounded on Θ. Finiteness of K at θ0​±a for some a>0 implies Eθ0​​ea∣X∣<∞, hence Eθ0​​∣X∣<∞. The displayed integral gives the remaining integrability. If the observations come from an arbitrary unrelated law, assumptions on K and f0​ alone cannot control that law's tails; the sampling-law integrability must then be stated explicitly.

At an interior statistical parameter θ, local asymptotic normality means that there are random vectors Δn,θ​ and a symmetric nonnegative Fisher information matrix Iθ​ such that, for each fixed h∈Rp,
log∏i=1n​f(θ,Yi​)∏i=1n​f(θ+h/n​,Yi​)​=hTΔn,θ​−21​hTIθ​h+oPθn​​(1),Δn,θ​d​Np​(0,Iθ​).
(5)
A common stronger definition requires this expansion also for hn​→h. The fixed-h version suffices here. A regular identifiable model usually has positive definite Fisher information; nonsingularity is unnecessary for the contiguity conclusion.
Here are sufficient differentiability conditions for a proof by Taylor's theorem. Suppose the local probability density functions have common support, ℓϑ​(y)=logf(ϑ,y) is twice continuously differentiable near θ, differentiation can pass through the normalization integral twice, the score function sθ​=∇ℓθ​ has finite second moment, and the local Hessian matrix is bounded in operator norm by an integrable envelope of a function class. These hypotheses imply
Eθ​sθ​=0,Iθ​=Eθ​[sθ​sθT​]=−Eθ​∇2ℓθ​.
(6)
The first identity differentiates ∫fθ​=1 once; the second uses ∇2fθ​=fθ​(∇2ℓθ​+sθ​sθT​) and differentiates twice. The central limit theorem gives Δn,θ​=n−1/2∑i​sθ​(Yi​)d​Np​(0,Iθ​). Taylor's integral remainder gives
∑i​(ℓθ+h/n​​(Yi​)−ℓθ​(Yi​))=hTΔn,θ​+hT[n1​∑i​∫01​(1−t)∇2ℓθ+th/n​​(Yi​)dt]h.
(7)
The weak law of large numbers and the continuity and integrable-envelope hypotheses make the bracket converge in probability to −Iθ​/2. Indeed, the expectation of the supremum of the Hessian matrix difference over a shrinking neighborhood tends to zero by dominated convergence, and Markov inequality controls its empirical average. This proves local asymptotic normality and explains the role of both derivatives.
Write Qn​◃Pn​ for contiguity of probability measures: for every measurable sequence An​, Pn​(An​)→0 implies Qn​(An​)→0. Mutual contiguity requires this in both directions. A useful form of Le Cam's first lemma is the following: if the Radon-Nikodym derivative Ln​ of the absolutely continuous part of Qn​ relative to Pn​ converges in distribution under Pn​ to L, with L>0 almost surely and EL=1, then the two sequences are mutually contiguous. This includes the usual case Ln​=dQn​/dPn​. The mean-one condition ensures uniform integrability of Ln​ and vanishing mass of any singular part; positivity ensures the reverse implication.
Take Pn​=Pθn​ and Qn​=Pθ+h/n​n​. Their likelihood ratio on the support of Pn​ satisfies, by local asymptotic normality and the continuous mapping theorem,
Ln​d​L=exp(W−v/2),W∼N(0,v),v=hTIθ​h.
(8)
This limit is positive and EL=e−v/2ev/2=1, including v=0. The conditions of Le Cam's first lemma therefore hold, giving mutual contiguity of the product sampling laws. The products, rather than the one-observation laws, are the relevant sequences.
For any ε>0, let An​={∥θn​−θ∥>ε}. Consistency under Pθn​ makes Pθn​(An​)→0, and contiguity gives Qn​(An​)→0. Thus the estimator remains consistent for θ under each fixed local alternative. Since ∥θ+h/n​−θ∥→0, it is also consistent for the moving parameter in the sense that ∥θn​−(θ+h/n​)∥→0 in probability under Qn​.

Use the usual probability-kernel convention: K≥0, ∫K=1, and K∈L2, in addition to symmetry and compact support. These conditions are needed for the stated finite bound; symmetry and compact support by themselves do not impose normalization or nonnegativity. Interpret the differentiability assumption in the usual Sobolev space sense f∈H2(R), or assume classical regularity sufficient for the integral Taylor remainder. The kernel density estimator is
f​h​(x)=nh1​∑i=1n​K(hx−Xi​​),Kh​(x)=h−1K(x/h),Ef​h​=Kh​∗f.
(9)
The bias-variance decomposition of mean squared error, followed by Tonelli theorem, yields
E∥f​h​−f∥22​=∫Var(f​h​(x))dx+∥Kh​∗f−f∥22​.
(10)
Independence and a change of variables give the exact integrated variance of a kernel density estimator
∫Var(f​h​(x))dx=n1​(h∥K∥22​​−∥Kh​∗f∥22​)≤nh∥K∥22​​.
(11)
All changes of integration order are justified either by nonnegativity or by the displayed finite integrals.
For the bias of a kernel density estimator, symmetry gives ∫uK(u)du=0. Taylor's integral remainder, with translations understood in L2, gives
f(x−hu)−f(x)+huf′(x)=h2u2∫01​(1−t)f′′(x−thu)dt.
(12)
The Minkowski inequality and translation invariance of the L2 norm imply
∥f(⋅−hu)−f+huf′∥2​≤2h2u2​∥f′′∥2​,∥Kh​∗f−f∥2​≤2h2​μ2​(K)∥f′′∥2​,

where μ2​(K)=∫u2K(u)du. Consequently we obtain the slightly stronger conclusion
E∥f​h​−f∥22​≤nh∥K∥22​​+41​h4μ2​(K)2∥f′′∥22​.​

Since 1/4≤1/3, this proves the requested bound. For signed kernels, the same argument has ∫u2∣K(u)∣du in place of μ2​(K); the printed bound is not generally justified merely by signed-kernel symmetry. For example, let Ua​=(2a)−11[−a,a]​ and K=(4/3)U1​−(1/3)U2​. This symmetric compactly supported signed kernel has integral one and μ2​(K)=0, but its fourth moment is nonzero. For a normal density its convolution has nonzero bias at any fixed positive bandwidth. Sending n→∞ would therefore contradict a bound containing only the integrated variance term.
Writing the requested bound as A/(nh)+Bh4, differentiation gives h∗​=(A/(4Bn))1/5 when B>0. Thus h≍n−1/5 gives mean integrated squared error of order n−4/5. With the sharper constant one instead has h∗​=(∥K∥22​/(nμ2​(K)2∥f′′∥22​))1/5; the rate is identical.
A direct heuristic for J2​(f)=∥f′′∥22​ is to choose a twice differentiable pilot kernel L, estimate f′′ by the second derivative kernel density estimator
f​b′′​(x)=nb31​∑i​L′′(bx−Xi​​),
(15)
and integrate its square. Its integrated variance contains a diagonal term ∥L′′∥22​/(nb5), which should not be mistaken for the target. A useful corrected U-statistic removes that diagonal:
J2,b​=n(n−1)b51​∑i=j​A(bXi​−Xj​​),A(v)=∫L′′(t)L′′(t+v)dt.
(16)
Its expectation is ∥Lb​∗f′′∥22​. Thus smoothing bias of an estimator, rather than the diagonal contribution, remains. For a smooth compactly supported probability pilot kernel, the same variance calculation gives
E∥f​b′′​−f′′∥22​≤nb5∥L′′∥22​​+∥Lb​∗f′′−f′′∥22​.
(17)
The second term tends to zero because convolution with a shrinking probability kernel approximates every L2 function. Thus b→0 and nb5→∞ make the raw squared pilot estimate consistent for the quadratic density derivative functional. If Qb​=∫(f​b′′​)2, then
J2,b​=n−1n​(Qb​−nb5∥L′′∥22​​),
(18)
so the diagonal-corrected U-statistic is consistent under the same choice. Consistency already uses the assumed f′′∈L2; the additional third-derivative hypothesis is relevant to more ambitious rate heuristics.
Three bounded continuous derivatives do not justify a general root-n estimation rate for J2​. For a smooth perturbation a with compact support, the directional derivative is
dtd​J2​(f+ta)​t=0​=2∫f′′a′′=2∫f(4)a,
(19)
where the last expression requires a fourth derivative in the appropriate weak sense. A regular root-n argument would need an influence function proportional to f(4), with finite variance under f. Three derivatives do not supply that condition.
For a more quantitative heuristic, impose the additional assumption f∈Hβ, use a smooth Fourier cutoff of width 1/b, and estimate the quadratic functional by its off-diagonal U-statistic. The tail bias of an estimator is O(b2(β−2)) because the Plancherel theorem weights this functional by frequency to the fourth power. The degenerate part of its standard deviation is O(n−1b−9/2): the squared L2 norm of the quadratic kernel scales as b−9. At β=3, these two terms balance at b≍n−2/13 and have size n−4/13, already slower than n−1/2; any additional first-order variance cannot improve that balance. To make both terms O(n−1/2) requires b2(β−2)≲n−1/2 and b−9/2≲n1/2, which are compatible only when β≥17/4. This is an illustrative smoothness calculation under extra Sobolev and moment assumptions, not a rate theorem asserted solely from bounded third derivatives. Particular much smoother probability density functions may of course permit root-n estimation.

There is a genuine qualification: the first printed inequality is valid as a useful general assertion for 0<η<1, but is false for arbitrary η>0. Also the displayed likelihood ratio presupposes Pf1​​≪Pf0​​. We first prove the intended bound and then give a counterexample to the unrestricted quantifier.
For any estimator T, set A={d(T,f0​)<rn​}. By the triangle inequality, d(T,f1​)≥rn​ on A, while d(T,f0​)≥rn​ on Ac. Hence
maxj=0,1​rn−1​Efj​​d(T,fj​)≥21​{Pf0​​(Ac)+Pf1​​(A)}.
(20)
Let C={∣Z−1∣≤η} with 0<η<1. On C, Z≥1−η. Therefore
Pf0​​(Ac)+Pf1​​(A)≥(1−η){Pf0​​(Ac)+Pf0​​(A∩C)}≥(1−η)Pf0​​(C)≥(1−η)(1−ηEf0​​∣Z−1∣​).
(21)
The last step is Markov inequality. Taking the supremum over the model and then the infimum over estimators proves the intended metric two-point risk bound. At η=1 the right side is zero. For η>1 a negative right side is also harmless, but a positive right side need not be a lower bound.
Here is an explicit counterexample with positive probability density functions and n=3. Let A0​=[0,1/2), δ=1/100, and take the model consisting of
f0​=2(1−δ)1A0​​+2δ1A0c​​,f1​=2δ1A0​​+2(1−δ)1A0c​​.

Use the L2 norm metric, for which D=d(f0​,f1​)=2(1−2δ), and take rn​=D/2 for every n. The majority-half decision selects f0​ if at least two observations lie in A0​. In fixed-design nonparametric regression, the covariates xi​ are deterministic and
Yi​=m(xi​)+εi​,Eεi​=0.
(26)
For the usual finite-variance model assume independent errors with Var(εi​)≤σ2. In random-design nonparametric regression, the pairs (Xi​,Yi​) are independent observations and m(x)=E[Yi​∣Xi​=x]; equivalently Yi​=m(Xi​)+εi​ with conditional mean-zero errors. Conditional variances are generally assumed bounded for risk bounds.
For a nonnegative regression kernel K and a smoothing bandwidth h>0, the Nadaraya–Watson estimator is
mNW​(x)=∑i​K((xi​−x)/h)∑i​K((xi​−x)/h)Yi​​,
(27)
when the denominator is positive; replace xi​ by Xi​ for random-design nonparametric regression. The local polynomial regression estimator of degree ℓ minimizes the weighted least squares criterion
∑i​K(ti​)(Yi​−∑j=0ℓ​βj​j!tij​​)2,ti​=hxi​−x​,
(28)
and reports β​0​. If the weighted Gram matrix is invertible, this minimizer is unique. The Nadaraya-Watson estimator is exactly local polynomial regression of degree zero. Higher degrees reproduce nonconstant polynomials and reduce boundary bias of an estimator.
The requested error bound needs ℓ≥s−1 and mean-zero independent errors of uniformly bounded variance, along with the matrix condition in the hint. The degree requirement is missing from the printed question. To see the problem, take ℓ=0, K=1[−1,1]​, m(t)=t, zero errors, x=0, and h=n−1/4. This regression function has two bounded derivatives, but the local constant estimate is the average of i/n for 1≤i≤N=⌊nh⌋:
m(0)−m(0)=2nN+1​∼2h​.
(29)
The scalar matrix B=N/(nh) is bounded away from zero for every n. Nevertheless (nh)−1/2+h2=n−3/8+n−1/2=o(h), so the printed conclusion with s=2 fails. A bounded-variance error hypothesis is also essential; otherwise an expectation bound need not even be finite.
Under the qualified hypotheses, use U(t)=(1,t,…,tℓ/ℓ!)T and
Bn​=nh1​∑i​U(ti​)U(ti​)TK(ti​),Wni​(x)=nh1​U(0)TBn−1​U(ti​)K(ti​).
(30)
This symmetric Gram matrix has inverse operator norm bounded by the reciprocal of its uniform positive eigenvalue lower bound. If K is supported in [−R,R], only ∣xi​−x∣≤Rh contribute. The equally spaced design and nh≥1 imply that there are at most 2Rnh+1≤Cnh such points. The bounded kernel and bounded U on this interval therefore yield
maxi​∣Wni​(x)∣≤nhC​,∑i​∣Wni​(x)∣≤C,∑i​Wni​(x)2≤nhC​.

For the stochastic part, variance additivity for independent random variables and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality give
E∣∑i​Wni​(x)εi​∣≤(∑i​Wni​(x)2Varεi​)1/2≤Cσ(nh)−1/2.

For the deterministic part, let Qx​ be the Taylor polynomial of m at x of degree s−1. The polynomial reproduction property of local polynomial regression gives ∑i​Wni​(x)Qx​(xi​)=Qx​(x)=m(x). By Taylor's theorem, ∣m(xi​)−Qx​(xi​)∣≤C∣xi​−x∣s≤Chs on the kernel window. Thus

A map Φ:SD​→R is Hadamard differentiable at s0​ if there is a continuous linear functional Φ˙s0​​:S→R such that, whenever tj​→0 with tj​=0, aj​→a in S, and s0​+tj​aj​∈SD​,
tj​Φ(s0​+tj​aj​)−Φ(s0​)​⟶Φ˙s0​​(a).
(35)
For tangential Hadamard differentiability, the limit directions a are restricted to a specified tangent subspace and the derivative is continuous linear on that subspace. The domain condition is indispensable.
Assume Xn​∈SD​ almost surely and use the usual measurable, separably supported setting for norm-valued convergence in distribution, with the limit in the tangent space. The functional delta method gives
rn​(Φ(Xn​)−Φ(s0​))d​Φ˙s0​​(X).​
(36)
To justify this by Skorokhod representation theorem, a sufficient precise version is: for weakly convergent random elements in a separable complete metric space, one can construct copies of the whole sequence and its limit on one probability space which converge almost surely in the metric. More generally the separable-limit-support version of the representation theorem can be used. Apply it to Zn​=rn​(Xn​−s0​), obtaining Zn​→X almost surely. The variables s0​+rn−1​Zn​ have the same laws as Xn​ and lie in SD​ on a common probability-one event. The definition of Hadamard differentiability, with tn​=1/rn​, gives convergence almost surely of the transformed quotients to Φ˙s0​​(X), and hence the claimed convergence in distribution. If X is a Gaussian measure, the limit is Gaussian because the derivative is linear. Without membership of Xn​ in SD​, the expression Φ(Xn​) in the question is not defined.
The final function space is Cb​(R) with supremum norm; it is not separable as a whole. This does not affect the deterministic differentiability proof below. Any statistical application of the preceding representation argument must use its separability or tightness hypotheses, rather than assuming that every normed space automatically satisfies them.
Fix admissible F,G and set T(F,G)=∫0u​G(x)F′(x)dx. The candidate derivative, defined even for continuous directions a,b without derivatives, is
T˙F,G​(a,b)=∫0u​b(x)F′(x)dx+G(u)a(u)−G(0)a(0)−∫0u​G′(x)a(x)dx.​
(37)
This follows formally from the product rule and integration by parts. We now establish the uniform-direction limit required by Hadamard differentiability.
Let Fj​=F+tj​aj​ and Gj​=G+tj​bj​ belong to the stated domain, with aj​→a, bj​→b in supremum norm. The domain gives ∫∣Fj′​∣≤1 and ∫∣F′∣≤1, while Fj​→F uniformly. We first claim that, for every continuous b on [0,u],
∫0u​b(x)Fj′​(x)dx⟶∫0u​b(x)F′(x)dx.
(38)
For a continuously differentiable test function v, integration by parts gives convergence directly from uniform convergence of Fj​: both boundary terms and ∫v′Fj​ converge. By the Weierstrass approximation theorem, approximate b uniformly on [0,u] by a polynomial v. The difference between the two integrals introduced by this approximation is at most 2∥b−v∥∞​, by the uniform bounded variation bounds. Letting the approximation error decrease proves the claim. This is weak convergence of bounded-variation integrators.
Now decompose the quotient in the order that retains the bounded-variation bound:
tj​T(Fj​,Gj​)−T(F,G)​=∫0u​bj​Fj′​dx+∫0u​Gaj′​dx.
(39)
The first term differs from ∫bFj′​ by at most ∥bj​−b∥∞​, so it tends to ∫bF′. For the second term, integration by parts yields
∫0u​Gaj′​=G(u)aj​(u)−G(0)aj​(0)−∫0u​G′aj​,
(40)
which tends to the corresponding expression with a. Hence the quotient tends to T˙F,G​(a,b) for every admissible perturbation sequence. Finally,
∣T˙F,G​(a,b)∣≤∥b∥∞​+(∣G(u)∣+∣G(0)∣+∫0u​∣G′∣)∥a∥∞​.
(41)
This proves that the derivative is a continuous linear functional on the product space. Therefore T is Hadamard differentiable at every point of the stated domain. The bounded-variation restriction is what controls the apparently dangerous interaction between uniformly small perturbations and possibly large perturbation derivatives; no convergence of aj′​ or bj′​ has been assumed.
