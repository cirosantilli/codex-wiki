Fix the Riemann curvature tensor convention
R(X,Y)Z=∇X​∇Y​Z−∇Y​∇X​Z−∇[X,Y]​Z.
(1)
With this convention the round sphere has positive sectional curvature. Write R(X,Y,Z,W)=g(R(X,Y)Z,W). We use skew-symmetry in the first and last pairs, pair interchange R(X,Y,Z,W)=R(Z,W,X,Y), and the first Bianchi identity. These are the standard curvature symmetries of the Levi-Civita connection.
For an orthonormal basis e1​,…,en​ of Tp​M, the Ricci curvature is the trace
Ricp​(X,Y)=∑i​g(R(ei​,X)Y,ei​).
(2)
For a plane spanned by linearly independent vectors U,V, its sectional curvature is
K(U,V)=g(U,U)g(V,V)−g(U,V)2g(R(U,V)V,U)​.
(3)
Both definitions are independent of the chosen basis. The first is the trace of v↦R(v,X)Y; the second is unchanged by replacing U,V with another basis of their plane, because numerator and denominator both scale by the square of the change-of-basis determinant. For U=ei​,V=ej​, i=j, the denominator is one. The terms with i=j vanish, so
Scalp​=j∑​Ric(ej​,ej​)=i=j∑​K(ei​,ej​)=2i<j∑​K(ei​,ej​)​.
(4)
The ordered-pair sum counts every unoriented coordinate plane twice.
For AX​(v)=R(X,v)X, pair interchange immediately gives
g(AX​v,w)=R(X,v,X,w)=R(X,w,X,v)=g(v,AX​w).
(5)
Thus AX​ is self-adjoint, including when X=0. The usual Jacobi curvature operator v↦R(v,X)X is its negative and is self-adjoint as well. Keeping these two signs distinct will also fix the Jacobi field equation below.
The Bonnet-Myers theorem states that a connected, complete n-dimensional Riemannian manifold, n≥2, with Ric≥(n−1)κg for a constant κ>0 has diameter at most π/κ​ and is compact. Completeness is required; a pointwise positive Ricci tensor without a uniform positive lower bound would not suffice for this stated conclusion.
A left-invariant metric on a Lie group is a Riemannian metric for which every left translation La​ is an isometry, equivalently La∗​g=g. It is determined by an inner product on the Lie algebra g=Te​G. If the metric is also right-invariant, this inner product is invariant under the adjoint action; differentiating yields
⟨[X,Y],Z⟩+⟨Y,[X,Z]⟩=0.
(6)
Use left-invariant vector fields and the permitted formula ∇X​Y=21​[X,Y]. The Jacobi identity gives
R(X,Y)Z=41​([X,[Y,Z]]−[Y,[X,Z]])−21​[[X,Y],Z]=−41​[[X,Y],Z].
(7)
Adjoint skew-symmetry then implies
g(R(X,Y)Y,X)=41​∥[X,Y]∥2,Ric(X,X)=41​∑i​∥[X,ei​]∥2.
(8)
This is the Ricci curvature of a bi-invariant Riemannian metric. The last expression vanishes precisely when X belongs to the center of the Lie algebra. If that center is zero, it is positive for every nonzero X. Its minimum λ on the unit sphere in g is therefore positive. Left invariance transfers this bound to every point: Ric≥λg.
Work in the identity component G0, since π1​(G,e)=π1​(G0,e). It is compact and hence complete. Give its universal cover G0 the lifted metric. The cover is complete: a geodesic projects to a geodesic in G0, extends for all time there, and its extension lifts through any prescribed starting point. The local isometry also preserves Ricci curvature, so the same uniform bound holds on the cover. Applying Bonnet-Myers with κ=λ/(n−1) makes G0 compact. The fiber above e is closed and discrete, and hence finite in this compact space; its cardinality equals ∣π1​(G0,e)∣. Consequently π1​(G,e) is finite. This is the finite fundamental group from a uniform positive Ricci bound. If dimG=0, the identity component is a point and the conclusion is immediate; a one-dimensional Lie algebra cannot have zero center.

For an affinely parametrized geodesic γ, put T=γ˙​ and Dt​=∇T​. A Jacobi field is a smooth vector field J along γ satisfying
Dt2​J+R(J,T)T=0.
(9)
This is a linear second-order equation, so J is uniquely determined by J(0) and Dt​J(0). The defining sign uses the curvature convention in the preceding solution.
To prove the realization of Jacobi fields by geodesic variations, write p=γ(0), V=T(0), A=J(0) and B=Dt​J(0). Choose a small smooth curve p(s) with p(0)=p and p′(0)=A, for instance p(s)=expp​(sA). Let Ps​:Tp​M→Tp(s)​M be parallel transport along this curve and set V(s)=Ps​(V+sB). Then V(0)=V and ∇s​V(s)∣s=0​=B. Define
f(t,s)=expp(s)​(tV(s)).
(10)
For sufficiently small ε>0, this is defined and smooth for every (t,s)∈[0,1]×(−ε,ε). No completeness assumption is needed: smooth dependence for the geodesic ordinary differential equation gives an open set of initial data whose solutions exist throughout the compact interval [0,1] around the given solution. The given geodesic also extends slightly beyond its endpoints by local existence.
Every t-curve of f is a geodesic and f(t,0)=γ(t). If J=∂s​f∣s=0​, torsion-freeness gives ∇s​∂t​f=∇t​∂s​f. Since ∇t​∂t​f=0, commuting covariant derivatives yields
0=∇s​∇t​∂t​f=∇t2​∂s​f+R(∂s​f,∂t​f)∂t​f.
(11)
Thus J satisfies the Jacobi equation. Moreover J(0)=A and Dt​J(0)=B. Uniqueness of the linear equation proves ∂s​f(t,0)=J(t).
If J(0)=0, keep the initial point fixed and choose initial velocities V+sB. Differentiating f(t,s)=expp​(tV+stB) gives the general formula for Jacobi fields vanishing at their initial point:
J(t)=(dexpp​)tV​(tB),B=Dt​J(0)∈Tp​M​.
(12)
Here TtV​(Tp​M) is identified with Tp​M. Every choice of B gives a Jacobi field with zero initial value, and uniqueness shows that these are all such fields. The factor t is part of the formula.
Points γ(0) and γ(L), with L>0, are conjugate along this geodesic if there is a nonzero Jacobi field with J(0)=J(L)=0. Equivalently, (dexpp​)LV​ has a nontrivial kernel: for a field with zero initial value, J is nonzero exactly when B=0. This definition refers to the particular geodesic segment, not merely to the two endpoints.
Now suppose the sectional curvatures along the segment are nonpositive. Let h(t)=∣J(t)∣2. Metric compatibility and the Jacobi equation give
h′′(t)=2∣Dt​J∣2−2g(R(J,T)T,J).
(13)
When J,T are independent, the curvature term is K(J,T)(∣J∣2∣T∣2−g(J,T)2)≤0; it is zero when they are dependent. Therefore h′′≥0. If J vanishes at both endpoints, convexity gives h(t)≤(1−t/L)h(0)+(t/L)h(L)=0, whereas h(t)≥0 by definition. Hence h=0 and J=0. There are no conjugate points along the segment. This proves that nonpositive sectional curvature excludes conjugate points; it does not require the manifold to be complete or simply connected.

The Riemannian distance on a connected manifold is
d(p,q)=infc​Lg​(c),Lg​(c)=∫ab​g(c˙,c˙)​dt,
(14)
where the infimum is over piecewise smooth curves joining p to q. A connected manifold is path connected, so this infimum is finite. The metric is positive definite and induces the manifold topology; locally this follows from comparison with the Euclidean metric in a coordinate chart.
Geodesic completeness means that every geodesic with prescribed initial point and velocity extends to all parameter values in R. The Hopf-Rinow theorem says that, for a connected finite-dimensional Riemannian manifold without boundary, this is equivalent to completeness of the distance metric and equivalent to compactness of every closed bounded set. It is also equivalent to the exponential map at one, and hence every, point being defined on the whole tangent space. Under these equivalent conditions, any two points are joined by a distance-minimizing geodesic.
A homogeneous Riemannian manifold has an isometry group acting transitively on its points. Fix o∈M. Local compactness and the local metric-topology comparison give an r>0 such that B(o,r) is compact: choose a relatively compact coordinate neighborhood of o, then a small closed metric ball contained in it. Homogeneity makes B(p,r) isometric to this fixed compact ball for every p∈M.
Let (pj​) be a Cauchy sequence. Its tail lies inside B(pN​,r) for some N. Compactness gives a convergent subsequence, and the Cauchy property forces the whole sequence to converge to the same limit. Thus the distance metric is complete. Hopf-Rinow now gives every homogeneous Riemannian manifold is geodesically complete. This is the completeness of homogeneous Riemannian manifolds; the compact ball used here is local and does not presuppose global completeness.
A two-point homogeneous Riemannian manifold has isometries acting transitively on ordered pairs at each fixed distance. We prove the unit tangent transitivity characterizes two-point homogeneity equivalence. First suppose two-point homogeneity holds. Applying it to pairs with identical endpoints gives ordinary homogeneity. Given p,q and unit tangent vectors u,v, choose a>0 sufficiently small that au and av lie in normal neighbourhoods at p and q. The Gauss lemma and local minimizing property give
d(p,expp​(au))=a=d(q,expq​(av)).
(15)
There is therefore an isometry f taking these ordered pairs to one another. Isometries preserve the Levi-Civita connection and geodesics, so
f(expp​(au))=expq​(adfp​u)=expq​(av).
(16)
Both vectors on the right lie in the injectivity neighborhood of expq​, because dfp​ preserves length. It follows that dfp​u=v. Thus isometries act transitively on the unit tangent bundle.
Conversely, suppose the stated transitivity on unit tangent vectors holds. In positive dimension it implies point homogeneity, and hence completeness by the preceding argument. Consider two ordered pairs at the same distance a>0. Hopf-Rinow supplies unit-speed minimizing geodesics γ1​,γ2​:[0,a]→M joining their respective endpoints. Choose an isometry taking γ1​(0) to γ2​(0) and γ˙​1​(0) to γ˙​2​(0). Then f∘γ1​ and γ2​ solve the same geodesic initial-value problem, so they agree throughout [0,a]. In particular f takes both endpoints of the first pair to those of the second. For a=0, point homogeneity suffices. If the connected manifold has dimension zero, it is a single point and both properties are immediate. Hence two-point homogeneity is equivalent to transitivity on unit tangent vectors. Completeness is the step allowing the local initial-direction condition to control pairs at arbitrary distance.

The Riemannian volume form of an oriented n-dimensional Riemannian manifold is the unique positive n-form taking value one on every positively oriented orthonormal frame. In positive coordinates,
ωg​=det(gij​)​dx1∧⋯∧dxn.
(17)
The metric induces an inner product on exterior powers of the cotangent bundle, with wedges of distinct orthonormal covectors forming an orthonormal basis. The Hodge star is the pointwise linear map ∗:ΛpT∗M→Λn−pT∗M specified by
α∧∗β=⟨α,β⟩g​ωg​.
(18)
It is an isometry and satisfies ∗∗α=(−1)p(n−p)α. With the positive-sign convention, the Hodge Laplacian on differential forms is
Δ=dδ+δd,δ∣Ωp​=(−1)n(p−1)+1∗d∗.
(19)
Here d is the exterior derivative and δ its formal adjoint; δ on functions is zero. On functions this is the Laplace-Beltrami operator
Δf=−div(gradf)=−detg​1​∂i​(detg​gij∂j​f).
(20)
The convention divgrad gives the negative of this operator. All subsequent Laplacians use the nonnegative convention above.
On a compact oriented manifold without boundary, the Hodge decomposition theorem gives an orthogonal decomposition of smooth real forms,
Ωp(M)=Hp(M)⊕dΩp−1(M)⊕δΩp+1(M),Hp=kerΔ.
(21)
The three component forms are uniquely determined, although the potentials in the last two summands need not be unique. The space Hp is finite-dimensional, and each de Rham cohomology class has a unique harmonic representative. Integration by parts gives ⟨Δα,α⟩L2​=∥dα∥L22​+∥δα∥L22​, so harmonic forms are exactly the closed and coclosed forms. Set Ωk=0 outside 0≤k≤n. Compactness without boundary is a hypothesis of this version of the theorem; orientation alone does not supply it.
A connection on TM induces a dual connection on T∗M and then connections on its tensor and exterior powers by the Leibniz rule. Explicitly, for a p-form α,
(∇X​α)(Y1​,…,Yp​)=X(α(Y1​,…,Yp​))−∑j=1p​α(Y1​,…,∇X​Yj​,…,Yp​).
(22)
To prove the parallelism of the Riemannian volume form, use a local positively oriented orthonormal frame Ei​, its dual coframe θi, and write ∇X​Ej​=∑i​aij​(X)Ei​. Metric compatibility makes the matrix a(X) skew-symmetric. Since ωg​=θ1∧⋯∧θn, differentiating the wedge yields
∇X​ωg​=−∑i​aii​(X)ωg​=0.
(23)
Off-diagonal terms vanish because they repeat a coframe factor. Thus ∇ωg​=0; equivalently parallel transport preserves both the metric and the continuously chosen orientation.
Now let M be compact, oriented and without boundary. For representing functionals on harmonic forms by wedge pairing, choose an L2-orthonormal basis h1​,…,hb​ of Hp and define
hf​=∑a=1b​f(ha​)ha​,ψ0​=∗hf​.
(24)
If φ=∑a​ca​ha​, linearity gives
f(φ)=⟨φ,hf​⟩L2​=∫M​φ∧ψ0​​.
(25)
This also works when b=0, using the empty sum. Since the Hodge star commutes with the Hodge Laplacian, ψ0​ is harmonic of degree n−p.
For the full ambiguity of harmonic wedge-pairing representatives, write another representative as ψ0​+χ. Because the star is an isometry,
∫M​φ∧χ=⟨∗φ,χ⟩L2​.
(26)
The forms ∗φ range over all of Hn−p. Hence χ gives zero functional exactly when its harmonic projection is zero. Hodge decomposition proves that the complete answer is
ψ=∗hf​+dα+δβ,α∈Ωn−p−1(M),β∈Ωn−p+1(M)​.
(27)
Both summands in this ambiguity are permitted; it is not only an exact-form ambiguity. The exact and coexact component forms are orthogonal to every harmonic form, so every displayed choice works. Conversely a nonzero harmonic component would pair nontrivially with some ∗φ, and therefore cannot occur. The harmonic representative ∗hf​ is unique; the arbitrary smooth representative generally is not. The formula also covers the endpoint degrees and any exceptional case where the ambiguity space is zero.
For a concrete coexact ambiguity, take the torus (R/2πZ)2 with metric dx2+dy2. The form χ=δ(sinydx∧dy)=cosydx annihilates every harmonic one-form by orthogonality, yet dχ=sinydx∧dy is nonzero. Thus an annihilating representative need not even be closed, let alone exact.

The divergence of a Riemannian vector field X is the trace of the covariant derivative map Y↦∇Y​X:
divX=∑i​g(∇ei​​X,ei​)=detg​1​∂i​(detg​Xi).
(28)
It is characterized by LX​ωg​=(divX)ωg​ in the oriented case. The metric-dual vector field Xθ​=θ♯ is defined by g(Xθ​,Y)=θ(Y) for every Y, using the musical isomorphism.
On a compact oriented manifold without boundary, the permitted exactness assertion and Stokes theorem imply ∫M​divYωg​=0 for every smooth vector field Y. For a smooth function a, the product rule gives
div(aXθ​)=Xθ​(a)+adivXθ​.
(29)
Since ⟨da,θ⟩=Xθ​(a), the formal-adjoint identity yields
∫M​aδθωg​=∫M​⟨da,θ⟩ωg​=−∫M​adivXθ​ωg​.
(30)
This holds for all a; taking a=δθ+divXθ​ proves the pointwise formula
δθ=−div(θ♯)​.
(31)
It agrees with δθ=−∗d∗θ, since ∗θ=ιXθ​​ωg​ and dιXθ​​ωg​=LXθ​​ωg​.
The Bochner formula for one-forms, with the positive Hodge Laplacian, is
Δθ=∇∗∇θ+Ric♯θ​.
(32)
Here Δ=dδ+δd acts on one-forms. The covariant derivative ∇θ is a section of T∗M⊗T∗M, and its formal-adjoint composition, the rough Laplacian, is
∇∗∇θ=−∑i​(∇ei​​∇ei​​θ−∇∇ei​​ei​​θ).
(33)
This expression is independent of the local orthonormal frame. The Ricci term is the zero-order bundle endomorphism specified by
(Ric♯θ)(Y)=Ric(θ♯,Y),(Δθ)i​=−∇j∇j​θi​+Rici​jθj​.
(34)
Thus the identity separates the differential energy of the form from the curvature contribution; the sign of the Ricci term matches positive sectional curvature on the sphere.
Let θ be a harmonic one-form on a compact connected manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature. Pair the formula with θ and integrate:
0=∫M​⟨Δθ,θ⟩dvolg​=∫M​(∣∇θ∣2+Ric(Xθ​,Xθ​))dvolg​.
(35)
Both integrands are nonnegative and continuous, so each vanishes everywhere. In particular ∇θ=0, proving that harmonic one-forms are parallel under nonnegative Ricci curvature. This integration uses the Riemannian volume density and formal adjoints, so orientation is not necessary for the final conclusion.
Fix p∈M. The evaluation map
evp​:H1(M)⟶Tp∗​M,θ⟼θp​
(36)
is injective: a parallel form with zero value at p stays zero under parallel transport along every path, and connectedness makes every point reachable from p. Therefore
dimH1(M)≤dimTp∗​M=dimM​.
(37)
This is the dimension bound for harmonic one-forms under nonnegative Ricci curvature. The bound is sharp: on a flat n-torus the n constant coordinate one-forms are parallel and harmonic. If Ricci curvature is positive definite at even one point, the same integral and parallelism argument force every harmonic one-form to vanish.
