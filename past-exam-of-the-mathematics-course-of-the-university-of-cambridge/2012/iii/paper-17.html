An integral curve of a vector field X is a smooth curve γ:I→M satisfying γ˙​(t)=Xγ(t)​ for every t in its interval of definition. Passing through p at time zero means γ(0)=p. The tangent-vector equality is intrinsic; in a coordinate chart x:U→Rn it becomes
dtd​xi(γ(t))=Xi(x(γ(t))).
(1)
Smoothness makes the coordinate right side locally Lipschitz continuous. The Picard-Lindelöf theorem supplies a locally unique solution through each coordinate initial value.
In fact, two integral curves of X with the same initial point agree throughout their common time interval containing zero. To pass from local to global uniqueness, the set of coincidence times is nonempty and closed by continuity and the Hausdorff property of the smooth manifold. At a coincidence time, put both curves in one coordinate chart; local ordinary differential equation uniqueness makes them agree on a neighborhood of that time. Thus the coincidence set is open as well as closed in the connected common interval, so it is the whole interval. This argument applies around any prescribed time, not only zero.
By joining all extensions that agree on overlaps, one obtains the unique maximal integral curve of a vector field through p. Its maximal interval need not be all of R: completeness is an additional property of the vector field.

The local flow of X is the smooth map Φ:D→M, where D⊂R×M is the maximal open domain of initial-point/time pairs, whose curve t↦Φ(t,q)=ϕt​(q) is the maximal integral curve of a vector field through q, with ϕ0​(q)=q. Smooth dependence in the ordinary differential equation theorem gives joint smoothness and openness of D. Each time slice is a diffeomorphism between its domain and image, with inverse ϕ−t​.
Here is the group law, including its domain qualification. Fix s,q where the relevant trajectories exist. Both curves
t⟼ϕt+s​(q),t⟼ϕt​(ϕs​(q))
(2)
solve the same autonomous ordinary differential equation and have value ϕs​(q) at t=0. The uniqueness from the first part therefore gives
ϕt+s​(q)=ϕt​(ϕs​(q))​
(3)
on their common interval. These are local identities; neither the vector field nor its local flow has been assumed complete.
For the commuting local flows ϕt​ and ψs​, differentiate ϕt​(ψs​(q))=ψs​(ϕt​(q)) with respect to s at zero. The chain rule gives
(Dq​ϕt​)Yq​=Yϕt​(q)​.​
(4)
Thus the pushforward of a vector field Y by the X flow equals Y. Interchanging the two fields gives the analogous identity for X under ψs​.
Now let Dq​=span(Xq​,Yq​). Pointwise linear independence makes this a rank-two smooth distribution. The identity just proved implies [X,Y]=0: in coordinates, differentiating Dq​ϕt​Yq​=Yϕt​(q)​ at t=0 gives DX(q)Yq​=DY(q)Xq​, precisely the vanishing Lie bracket of vector fields. The bracket of two local sections is also in D, since
[fX,gY]=fg[X,Y]+fX(g)Y−gY(f)X
(5)
and the analogous terms for [fX,gX] and [fY,gY] remain in the same span. Therefore D is an involutive distribution.
The Frobenius theorem says that a smooth constant-rank involutive distribution has local coordinates (u1,…,un) in which it is spanned by ∂u1​,∂u2​, and has unique maximal connected integral manifolds through its points. These are the leaves of a regular foliation. Let S be the maximal leaf through p. The global confinement to an immersed leaf gives the answer: S is a two-dimensional immersed integral manifold and every smooth D-tangent curve starting at p stays in S.
For the curve assertion, in each Frobenius chart the transverse coordinate functions u3,…,un have zero derivative along such a curve. Hence its segment in that chart stays in one plaque. On any compact subinterval of the curve's parameter interval, a finite chain of overlapping such segments connects the initial plaque to the last; all belong to the same maximal leaf. Exhausting the parameter interval proves the assertion for every time.
The two commuting local flows also give the leaf parametrization directly near p:
F(s,t)=ϕs​(ψt​(p)).
(6)
Its partial derivatives are ∂s​F=XF​ and ∂t​F=YF​, by the pushforward identity. Its differential has rank two. The constant rank theorem makes a sufficiently small image an embedded local plaque with tangent plane D. This is a concrete local construction of the integral manifold.
There is a global distinction in the word “submanifold”. If it is required to mean an embedded submanifold, the assertion for all times is not valid in general. On the three-dimensional torus R3/Z3, take
X=∂x​+α∂z​,Y=∂y​,α∈/Q.
(7)
The fields are independent and commute, and their dense immersed cylinder in a three-dimensional torus through zero is
S={(s,t,αs)modZ3:s,t∈R}.
(8)
It is an injectively immersed cylinder R×(R/Z) and is dense in the torus. Indeed, fixing xmod1 and varying s by integers makes αsmod1 dense by an irrational rotation of the circle; t is unrestricted. Every point of this leaf is reached by a tangent curve r↦(rs,rt,αrs)modZ3. An embedded surface containing it would, in a submanifold chart near one of its points, be a closed coordinate plane containing a dense subset of the ambient open chart, an impossibility. Thus the global conclusion uses an immersed leaf; the embedded conclusion is local, for curve segments remaining in a suitable chart.

The space of smooth differential forms of degree p is
Ωp(M)=Γ(ΛpT∗M).​
(9)
Thus a form assigns smoothly to each point an alternating p-linear function on its tangent space. Here Ω0(M)=C∞(M), and forms of degree greater than dimM vanish. The wedge product of differential forms gives the graded exterior algebra of forms.
The exterior derivative is an R-linear map of degree one satisfying the following axioms: df(X)=X(f) for functions; for a p-form α,
d(α∧β)=dα∧β+(−1)pα∧dβ;
(10)
and d2=0. The sign is the graded Leibniz rule.
To construct it, write in a coordinate chart
α=∑i1​<⋯<ip​​ai1​…ip​​dxi1​∧⋯∧dxip​,dα=∑i1​<⋯<ip​​dai1​…ip​​∧dxi1​∧⋯∧dxip​.
(11)
This formula is linear, agrees with differentiation on functions and obeys the graded Leibniz rule. Its square vanishes because symmetric second partial derivatives are contracted against antisymmetric wedge products.
The construction is independent of the chart. For coordinates yj=yj(x), the chain rule gives dyj=∑i​(∂i​yj)dxi, and the coordinate formula gives d(dyj)=0 by equality of mixed partial derivatives. Consequently, expressing a form as ∑I​bI​dyI and applying the x-coordinate formula gives exactly ∑I​dbI​∧dyI, the y-coordinate formula. The local constructions agree on overlaps and therefore define a global exterior derivative.
For uniqueness, first derive locality from the axioms. If α vanishes near p, choose a smooth bump function χ supported in that neighborhood and equal to one near p. Then χα=0, and
0=d(χα)=dχ∧α+χdα
(12)
implies (dα)p​=0. Thus the value depends only on the local germ of α. Local coordinate functions can be extended using bump functions, so the same axioms apply to their germs. Since d(dxi)=d2xi=0, the graded Leibniz rule forces precisely the coordinate formula above. The exterior derivative exists and is unique, as expressed by uniqueness of the exterior derivative from its axioms.

Write the differential one-form as ω=∑i​ωi​dxi, and the vector fields as X=Xi∂i​, Y=Yi∂i​. The coordinate definition of the exterior derivative gives
dω(X,Y)=∑i,j​(∂i​ωj​−∂j​ωi​)XiYj.
(13)
Expand the right side of the invariant formula:
X(ω(Y))−Y(ω(X))=​i,j∑​(∂i​ωj​)XiYj−i,j∑​(∂i​ωj​)YiXj+i,j∑​ωj​(Xi∂i​Yj−Yi∂i​Xj).​
(14)
The last line is ω([X,Y]), by the coordinate formula for the Lie bracket of vector fields. Subtract it and interchange the two indices in the second sum. The remaining terms are exactly dω(X,Y). Therefore
dω(X,Y)=X(ω(Y))−Y(ω(X))−ω([X,Y]).​
(15)
This is the exterior derivative of a one-form evaluated on vector fields; it is valid in any frame, not just a commuting coordinate frame.

Define the pointwise representation of one-forms by vector-field functionals by
θ(ω)(X)=ω(X).​
(16)
It is R-linear, its value is a smooth function, and Xp​=0 implies θ(ω)(X)(p)=0. Moreover
θ(gω)=gθ(ω),
(17)
so it is a map of C∞(M)-modules. This is the properly typed scalar-multiplication property: the same symbol for a form and its associated functional is understood through θ.
To construct its inverse, let α∈W and v∈Tp​M. Choose a global smooth vector field X with Xp​=v; a local coordinate field multiplied by a smooth bump function supplies such an extension. Set
ωp​(v)=α(X)(p).
(18)
This is well-defined: if Xp​=Yp​, then X−Y vanishes at p, so α(X−Y)(p)=0. Real linearity of α makes ωp​ a covector. In fact the pointwise vanishing assumption also forces
α(fX)(p)=f(p)α(X)(p),
(19)
because (f−f(p))X vanishes at p. Thus α(fX)=fα(X) for every smooth f, even though only real linearity was initially imposed.
It remains to prove smoothness, rather than assume continuity of an abstract linear map. Near a fixed point, choose global fields E1​,…,En​ equal to the coordinate basis on a smaller neighborhood, using a bump function equal to one there. On that neighborhood,
ω=∑i​α(Ei​)dxi.
(20)
All coefficients are smooth by the definition of W, so ω is a smooth differential one-form. The construction holds near every point, hence gives ω∈Ω1(M) with α(X)=ω(X) globally.
Every tangent vector can be realized by a global field, so θ(ω)=0 forces ω=0. The inverse just constructed gives surjectivity. The evaluation map is a natural C∞(M)-module isomorphism Ω1(M)≅W. No auxiliary metric or connection was chosen.

Let ω=θ−1(α). The preceding invariant formula for the exterior derivative identifies the expression in the question with
X(α(Y))−Y(α(X))−α([X,Y])=dω(X,Y).
(21)
Its vanishing for every pair of global vector fields is equivalent to dω=0: global fields with arbitrary prescribed tangent values are available by bump-function extension. Thus it is exactly the condition that ω be a closed differential one-form.
By definition, the first de Rham cohomology is closed one-forms modulo exact differential forms. The hypothesis HdR1​(M)=0 makes every closed one-form exact. Hence dω=0 gives a globally smooth function g with ω=dg, and
α(X)=dg(X)=X(g)for every X.​
(22)
Conversely, if α(X)=X(g), then
X(α(Y))−Y(α(X))−α([X,Y])=X(Yg)−Y(Xg)−[X,Y]g=0
(23)
by the definition of the Lie bracket of vector fields. Equivalently, d(dg)=0. The bracket compatibility condition is necessary and, when HdR1​(M)=0, sufficient for a global potential. The potential is unique up to a constant on each connected component, since the difference of two potentials has zero differential.

The Lie algebra of a Lie group G is the vector space g=Te​G equipped with the bracket transferred from left-invariant vector fields. For a∈G, write La​(g)=ag. A field is left-invariant when (Dg​La​)Xg​=Xag​ for all a,g. Given v∈Te​G, define
Xv​(g)=(De​Lg​)v.
(24)
This is smooth and left-invariant, because La​Lg​=Lag​ and the chain rule applies. Conversely a left-invariant field is determined by its value at e, through this formula. Thus
v⟼Xv​​
(25)
is a vector-space isomorphism, whose inverse is evaluation at the identity.
The Lie bracket of vector fields is preserved by diffeomorphisms: on functions this follows by transporting the commutator of derivations. Therefore the bracket of two left-invariant fields is again left-invariant. Define [v,w]=[Xv​,Xw​]e​. Bilinearity, antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity follow from the same properties of commutators of derivations. This supplies the asserted Lie algebra structure.
For the general linear group GLn​(R), invertible matrices form an open subset of Mn​(R), so TI​GLn​(R)=Mn​(R). The left-invariant field determined by A is XA​(g)=gA. Its ambient derivative is Dg​XA​(H)=HA. Therefore differentiating left-invariant matrix fields gives
[XA​,XB​](g)=Dg​XB​(gA)−Dg​XA​(gB)=g(AB−BA).
(26)
Hence
gln​(R)=Mn​(R),[A,B]=AB−BA.​
(27)
This is the general linear Lie algebra, with the ordinary matrix commutator; right invariance with the same identification would give the opposite sign.
Now let F:G→H be a smooth Lie group homomorphism. It sends the identity to the identity and induces the linear map
F∗​=De​F:Te​G→Te​H.
(28)
Since F∘Lg​=LF(g)​∘F, differentiation gives
(Dg​F)Xv​(g)=XF∗​v​(F(g)).
(29)
These fields are F-related; no injectivity or surjectivity of F is needed. For every smooth function f on H,
Xv​(f∘F)=(XF∗​v​f)∘F.
(30)
Apply this first with w and then with v, and subtract the reversed order. The result is
[Xv​,Xw​](f∘F)=([XF∗​v​,XF∗​w​]f)∘F.
(31)
At e, smooth functions detect tangent vectors, so
F∗​[v,w]=[F∗​v,F∗​w].​
(32)
This proves that the differential of a Lie group homomorphism preserves Lie brackets, and hence that F∗​ is a Lie algebra homomorphism.
For the exponential identity, let ηv​ be the integral curve of Xv​ through e. Left invariance and uniqueness show ηv​(t+s)=ηv​(t)ηv​(s) for small times: translating the curve through e gives the curve through ηv​(s). Repeating this identity extends the curve to all real times. This proves completeness of left-invariant vector fields without assuming that an arbitrary smooth field is complete. Rescaling the parameter also gives ηv​(t)=ηtv​(1). By the definition of the Exponential map of a Lie group, expG​(v)=ηv​(1).
The field-related identity shows that F(ηv​(t)) is an integral curve of XF∗​v​ starting at eH​. By uniqueness it equals ηF∗​v​(t) for all times. At time one,
F(expG​v)=expH​(F∗​v).​
(33)
This is the naturality of the Lie group exponential.
Apply this to the determinant homomorphism GLn​(R)→R×. The derivative of the determinant at I is
DI​det(A)=trA,
(34)
since the permutation formula gives det(I+tA)=1+ttrA+O(t2). The left-invariant field on R× with initial tangent c is y↦cy; its curve through one solves y′=cy, hence is ect. The matrix exponential determinant identity therefore follows from naturality:
det(expA)=etrA.​
(35)
This argument uses only the flow definition of the exponential and the scalar exponential, not an unproved matrix formula.
To identify it explicitly with the usual matrix exponential, the series E(t)=∑k≥0​tkAk/k! converges in operator norm, uniformly with its differentiated series on bounded intervals. Termwise differentiation gives E′=EA and E(0)=I. The series commutes with A, and differentiating E(t)E(−t) gives zero, so E(t)E(−t)=I. Thus E(t) stays in GLn​(R) and, by uniqueness, is the integral curve of XA​ through I. The matrix series is exactly the flow-defined exponential, justifying either notation in the determinant identity.

A connection on a vector bundle E→M is an R-linear map
∇:Γ(E)⟶Ω1(M;E)
(36)
satisfying ∇(fs)=df⊗s+f∇s. Equivalently its directional version is real-bilinear and satisfies
∇fX​s=f∇X​s,∇X​(fs)=X(f)s+f∇X​s.
(37)
For a complex bundle it is complex-linear in the section variable and has the same Leibniz property.
Every smooth vector bundle over an ordinary Hausdorff, second-countable smooth manifold has such a connection. Choose trivializing open sets Ua​, local frames ea,i​ and a subordinate locally finite partition of unity ρa​. In each frame define the flat local connection
∇(a)(∑i​fi​ea,i​)=∑i​dfi​⊗ea,i​.
(38)
Then set ∇s=∑a​ρa​∇(a)(s∣Ua​​), extending each weighted term by zero outside Ua​. The supports lie inside the corresponding trivializing sets, making the extensions smooth, and local finiteness makes the sum smooth. Since ∑a​ρa​=1, its Leibniz rule is
∇(fs)=∑a​ρa​(df⊗s+f∇(a)s)=df⊗s+f∇s.
(39)
This is the construction of a vector bundle connection by a partition of unity.
The difference A=∇′−∇ of two connections is C∞(M)-linear in both its vector-field argument and its section argument. The derivative terms cancel, so AX​s depends only on Xp​,sp​ at each point. Smooth local coefficients therefore identify it with
A∈Ω1(M;EndE)=Γ(T∗M⊗EndE).
(40)
Conversely, any such A added to a connection preserves its axioms. Thus connections form an affine space modeled on Ω1(M;EndE). Choosing a base connection ∇0 gives the noncanonical bijection A↦∇0+A; there is no distinguished zero connection and hence no canonical vector-space identification. This is the affine space of vector-bundle connections.
For a connection on TM, the torsion tensor is the alternating operation τ(X,Y)=∇X​Y−∇Y​X−[X,Y]. The connection and bracket Leibniz rules give
τ(fX,Y)​=f∇X​Y−(Y(f)X+f∇Y​X)−(f[X,Y]−Y(f)X)=fτ(X,Y).​
(41)
Alternation then gives τ(X,fY)=fτ(X,Y) as well. Hence it is tensorial, of type one contravariant and two covariant indices:
τ∈Γ(TM⊗Λ2T∗M).​
(42)
It is equivalently a TM-valued two-form, a torsion form.
For the local frame ei​ with dual coframe ϕj​, define
ωij​(X)=ϕj​(∇X​ei​).
(43)
The connection is C∞-linear in X, so these are smooth differential one-forms. Expansion in the frame gives ∇X​ei​=∑j​ωij​(X)ej​, and uniqueness follows by applying each ϕj​. Here the first index is the input frame vector and the second the output coefficient; this fixes the connection one-form convention.
Expand Y=∑i​ϕi​(Y)ei​ and apply the connection Leibniz rule:
ϕj​(∇X​Y)=X(ϕj​(Y))+∑i​ϕi​(Y)ωij​(X).
(44)
Subtract the corresponding expression with X,Y exchanged, and then subtract ϕj​([X,Y]). By the exterior derivative of a one-form evaluated on vector fields,
τj​(X,Y)​=dϕj​(X,Y)+i∑​(ϕi​(Y)ωij​(X)−ϕi​(X)ωij​(Y))=(dϕj​−i∑​ϕi​∧ωij​)(X,Y).​
(45)
Since all pairs of tangent vectors can be tested, equality of the two-forms follows:
dϕj​=i∑​ϕi​∧ωij​+τj​.​
(46)
This is the Cartan first structure equation with input-first indices. Its plus sign is correct with the stated index order and wedge order; the common output-first convention writes the same identity with a minus sign and reversed wedge factors.

A Riemannian metric gives a smooth fibrewise isomorphism g♭:TM→T∗M, defined by g♭(v)=g(v,⋅). Its inverse is smooth because the inverse of a smoothly varying invertible matrix is smooth. Define the Riemannian gradient by
Vσ​=(g♭)−1(dσ)=gradg​σ.​
(47)
It is a smooth vector field satisfying g(Vσ​,Y)=dσ(Y)=Y(σ). Nondegeneracy also gives uniqueness. In coordinates its components are Vσi​=∑j​gij∂j​σ.
Multiplication of g by the globally defined smooth positive function e2σ gives a smooth symmetric two-tensor g​. For any nonzero tangent vector v,
g​(v,v)=e2σg(v,v)>0.
(48)
Thus g​=e2σg is a Riemannian metric. This conformal rescaling of a Riemannian metric changes lengths but preserves angles.

The Levi-Civita connection is the unique connection on a vector bundle TM that is torsion-free and metric-compatible. These conditions are
∇X​Y−∇Y​X=[X,Y],X(g(Y,Z))=g(∇X​Y,Z)+g(Y,∇X​Z).
(49)
Existence and uniqueness follow from the Koszul formula; equivalently, these are the defining properties that characterize the connection.
Define the correction tensor
A(X,Y)=X(σ)Y+Y(σ)X−g(X,Y)Vσ​
(50)
and set DX​Y=∇X​Y+A(X,Y). All terms of A are C∞(M)-linear in both X and Y, so D is a connection. The symmetry A(X,Y)=A(Y,X) preserves vanishing of the torsion tensor.
To check compatibility with g​, compute
X(g​(Y,Z))=e2σ(2X(σ)g(Y,Z)+g(∇X​Y,Z)+g(Y,∇X​Z)).
(51)
On the other hand,
g(A(X,Y),Z)g(Y,A(X,Z))​=X(σ)g(Y,Z)+Y(σ)g(X,Z)−g(X,Y)Z(σ),=X(σ)g(Y,Z)+Z(σ)g(X,Y)−g(X,Z)Y(σ).​
(52)
The cross terms cancel, so their sum is 2X(σ)g(Y,Z). Multiplying by e2σ proves X(g​(Y,Z))=g​(DX​Y,Z)+g​(Y,DX​Z). Hence D is torsion-free and compatible with g​, so uniqueness of the Levi-Civita connection gives
∇X​Y=∇X​Y+X(σ)Y+Y(σ)X−g(X,Y)gradg​σ.​
(53)
This is the Levi-Civita connection under conformal rescaling.
On a nonempty compact manifold without boundary, σ attains a maximum at some p. The derivative of a smooth function vanishes at an interior extremum, so (dσ)p​=0 and (Vσ​)p​=0. The correction tensor therefore vanishes at p, giving
(∇X​Y)p​=(∇X​Y)p​for every X,Y.​
(54)
This is equality for all vector fields at the same point, not an assertion that either connection vanishes there. More generally, the two connections agree at any critical point of σ. Conversely, if their correction vanishes for all X,Y at a point of positive dimension, evaluating g(A(v,v),v)=g(v,v)dσ(v) for every v shows that the point is critical. This is the critical-point criterion for equal conformal connections.
The boundaryless hypothesis is the usual convention for “manifold” here. If manifolds with boundary were included, compactness alone would not suffice: on [0,1] with g=dx2 and σ=x, one has ∇∂x​​∂x​=∂x​ everywhere while ∇∂x​​∂x​=0. An extremum at the boundary need not be a critical point.
