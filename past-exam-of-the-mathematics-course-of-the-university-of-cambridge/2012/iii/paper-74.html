For the optical trap, use the trap-frame coordinate y=x−vT​t and the force-induced speed u(y)=F(y)/ζ. Then y˙​=u(y)−vT​. The particle first enters the support at y=XR​ and exits at y=−XL​. Thus the actual support width is w=XR​+XL​. The printed difference XR​−XL​ is inconsistent with those endpoints and would give zero width for the symmetric example; below use the geometrically correct w=2X0​.
A sufficient escape condition for a translating trap is
vT​>ysup​u(y).​
(1)
For a bounded continuous force this makes the relative velocity strictly negative and bounded away from zero, ensuring finite passage. For an ordinary smooth well, a root of u(y)=vT​ met from the incident side instead prevents passage: the trajectory approaches a trap-frame equilibrium. At the limiting maximum-speed equality, the passage time can diverge. Under the strict condition, passage through a translating optical trap gives
Δt=∫−XL​XR​​vT​−u(y)dy​,Δx=∫−XL​XR​​vT​−u(y)u(y)​dy=vT​Δt−w.​
(2)
The time is the support-crossing time; gaps where the force vanishes inside that interval are included.
There is an essential qualification to the direction claim. Compact support of F alone does not imply positive displacement. For example, F=−F0​<0 throughout the support gives Δx=−w(F0​/ζ)/(vT​+F0​/ζ)<0 and satisfies the strict passing condition. A smooth negative bump is also a counterexample. The intended optical trap is a localized conservative well with F=−U′ and equal outside potential values. This additional assumption gives ∫udy=0. Now
vT​−uu​=vT​u​+vT​(vT​−u)u2​,Δx=∫−XL​XR​​vT​[vT​−u(y)]u(y)2​dy≥0.​
(3)
It is strictly positive for a nonzero force on a set of positive measure. This is forward displacement from a translating localized potential. During motion in the trap direction, the relative passage is slower, so the positive-force side acts longer; opposite motion speeds passage through the negative-force side. A complementary energy argument makes the sign transparent:
dtdU​=−ζx˙2+vT​ζx˙,Uexit​=Uentry​⟹vT​Δx=∫x˙2dt≥0.
(4)
The moving well must supply the viscous energy loss.
For the high-speed passage expansion of a localized trap, require ∥u∥∞​/vT​≪1. Expanding the denominator uniformly gives
Δx=vT​1​∫udy+vT2​1​∫u2dy+O(vT−3​).
(5)
Therefore the localized conservative well has
Δx∼ζ2vT2​1​∫F(y)2dy,Δt=vT​w​+ζ2vT3​1​∫F(y)2dy+O(vT−4​).​
(6)
For a symmetric well the force is odd, the cubic moment vanishes, and the displacement remainder improves to O(vT−4​). For an arbitrary compact force without equal outside potential levels, its signed area instead gives the leading vT−1​ displacement.
For circular motion, let C=2πR and use the local arclength model, with nonoverlapping support w<C. In each encounter the relative position traverses w, while the particle advances Δx. Outside the support the particle is stationary and the trap must traverse the remaining relative arclength C−w. Hence the time between kicks and the repeated kicks from a circular optical trap response are
Tkick​=Δt+vT​C−w​,fp​=C[Δt+(C−w)/vT​]Δx​=fT​C−w+vT​ΔtΔx​=fT​C+ΔxΔx​.​
(7)
The last equality uses the single-passage identity. Equivalently, kicks arrive at the relative lap frequency fT​−fp​.
The precise small-correction condition for the large-speed approximation for circular-trap kicks is Δx/C≪1. A sufficient high-speed regime for a localized well is vT​≫∥F∥∞​/ζ, with w<C fixed: writing ϵ=∥u∥∞​/vT​<1, the positive-displacement integral bounds Δx/C≤(w/C)ϵ2/(1−ϵ). Thus
fp​≃2πRΔx​fT​.​
(8)
The locally straight force profile on a real circular track also presumes a trap width small relative to the radius; R≫a supplies the particle-size part of that approximation.
For the triangular optical-trap response, put vc​=F/ζ=Cfc​, β=vT​/vc​ and α=w/C=X0​/(πR). In the passing regime β>1, the two constant-force halves give
Δt=vT​+vc​X0​​+vT​−vc​X0​​=vT2​−vc2​wvT​​,Δx=vT2​−vc2​wvc2​​=β2−1w​.
(9)
Substitution into the circular formula yields
fc​fp​​=β2−1+ααβ​(β>1).​
(10)
For 0<β≤1, the particle remains locked to the trap and fp​/fc​=β. At the ideal triangular cusp this is understood as sticking, or as the limit of a rounded well; it is not a finite passing kick. The passing branch tends continuously to one at β↓1, while at large β it behaves as α/β. The zero-speed limiting response is zero. These branches apply to separated kicks, 0<α<1.

For the elastic filament, apply a variation η(x). Two integration by parts operations give
δE=A∫0L​h′′η′′dx=A∫0L​h(4)ηdx+A[h′′η′−h(3)η]0L​.
(11)
At a free endpoint both η and η′ are arbitrary. The free-end bending boundary conditions and the unloaded Euler-Lagrange equation are therefore
h(4)=0(0<x<L),h′′=h(3)=0at x=0,L.​
(12)
Here Ah′′ is the bending moment and −Ah(3) the transverse shear-force convention. The equilibrium shapes are affine, reflecting free translation and tilt.
On L2(0,L) define K=A∂x4​ on H4 functions obeying those endpoints. The self-adjoint endpoint conditions for filament bending annul the boundary form
⟨u,Kv⟩−⟨Ku,v⟩=A[uv(3)−u′v′′+u′′v′−u(3)v]0L​.
(13)
This proves symmetry. To establish actual self-adjointness, the adjoint-domain boundary form must vanish for every free-end u. The endpoint values u,u′ can be varied independently, forcing v′′=v(3)=0 at both ends. The regular fourth-order expression then gives the same H4 domain for the adjoint. Thus the domains coincide and K is self-adjoint. Also ⟨h,Kh⟩=A∫∣h′′∣2dx≥0.

A positive-eigenvalue normal mode satisfies KWn​=Akn4​Wn​. Solving the constant-coefficient equation gives the sine, cosine, hyperbolic sine and hyperbolic cosine family. To avoid confusing modal coefficients with the filament bending modulus, write those coefficients as c1​,c2​,c3​,c4​.
The free conditions at zero require c3​=c1​ and c4​=c2​. With q=kL, the remaining conditions are
(coshq−cosqsinhq+sinq​sinhq−sinqcoshq−cosq​)(c2​c1​​)=0.
(14)
Its determinant is 2(1−cosqcoshq). Therefore the free-free bending spectrum is
cosqn​coshqn​=1,kn​=qn​/L(n≥1).​
(15)
A convenient corresponding eigenfunction is
Wn​(x)=Nn​[cos(kn​x)+cosh(kn​x)−γn​(sin(kn​x)+sinh(kn​x))],γn​=sinhqn​−sinqn​coshqn​−cosqn​​,
(16)
where Nn​ gives unit L2 norm. The stable characteristic equation for free-free bending modes is cosq=sechq. Intersections or numerical bracketing give
q1​≃4.730040745,q2​≃7.853204624,q3​≃10.995607838,q4​≃14.137165491,q5​≃17.278759657,….​

Choose real normalized eigenfunctions, including the two zero-energy filament modes. The expansion is
h=atr​Wtr​+atilt​Wtilt​+∑n≥1​an​Wn​.
(20)
Integration by parts with the natural endpoints gives ∫Wm′′​Wn′′​dx=kn4​δmn​. Hence
E=2A​∑n≥1​kn4​an2​.
(21)
For each positive mode, the equipartition theorem gives
⟨an2​⟩=Akn4​kB​T​.​
(22)
Distinct modal amplitudes are independent centered Gaussian random variables in the canonical bending ensemble.
The rigid translation and tilt amplitudes have no energy cost. Their integrals in the canonical ensemble are unbounded, so a completely free filament has no normalizable equilibrium height distribution and no finite height variance determined by this energy alone. This is a physical qualification of the requested variance, not a reason to omit the bending fluctuations.
Fixing translation and tilt, for example by imposing ∫hdx=∫(x−L/2)hdx=0, leaves the rigid-motion-projected thermal covariance of a free filament:
C(x,y)=AkB​T​n≥1∑​kn4​Wn​(x)Wn​(y)​,Varh(x)=AkB​T​n≥1∑​kn4​Wn​(x)2​.​
(23)
For unnormalized modes, divide each summand by ∫Wn2​dx. Any prescribed independent variance of the two rigid amplitudes must be added separately; equipartition theorem does not assign it.
The free-filament variance with fixed translation and tilt also has a closed form. Integrate a white noise curvature field twice, and subtract its affine least-squares projection. With u=x/L, t=s/L, the dimensionless kernel is

Adding the two homogeneous reaction rates gives a−u, so the unique positive Brusselator equilibrium is
u∗​=a,v∗​=b/a.​
(29)
The reaction Jacobian matrix at that state is
J=(b−1−b​a2−a2​),τ=trJ=b−1−a2,Δ=detJ=a2.
(30)
A real two-dimensional linear system has strict asymptotic stability exactly when τ<0 and Δ>0: the eigenvalues solve λ2−τλ+Δ=0. Real eigenvalues then have positive product and negative sum; a complex conjugate pair has real part τ/2. Conversely, stable eigenvalues require those signs.
Since a>0, the Brusselator is homogeneously stable precisely for
b<1+a2.​
(31)
At bH​=1+a2, the linear eigenvalues are ±ia, so strict decay is lost through a homogeneous Hopf bifurcation threshold. For larger b the equilibrium is unstable. The source's matrix label A is here written J to distinguish it from the filament modulus in the preceding question.

For linear stability analysis of a general stable two-species reaction–diffusion system, take Fourier modes proportional to eλt+ikx. Their matrix is Jk​=J−k2diag(Du​,Dv​). Put s=k2, τ=trJ, Δ=detJ and B=Dv​a11​+Du​a22​. Then
τk​=τ−(Du​+Dv​)s,Δk​=Δ−Bs+Du​Dv​s2.
(32)
The full system is stable precisely when τk​<0 and Δk​>0 for every allowed mode. If the homogeneous state is stable, the trace already decreases with s; only the determinant can change sign. If B≤0, its minimum for s≥0 is at zero. If B>0, it is at s∗​=B/(2Du​Dv​). Thus the two-species Turing criterion for instability on a continuum of wavenumbers is
τ<0,Δ>0,B>0,B2>4Du​Dv​Δ.​
(33)
Strict stability has B<2Du​Dv​Δ​ together with the homogeneous conditions. Equality at positive B marks a stationary neutral mode. At that first onset,
kc2​=2Du​Dv​B​=Du​Dv​Δ​​,kc​=(Du​Dv​Δ​)1/4.​
(34)
Beyond threshold, the growing band lies between the positive roots s±​=[B±B2−4Du​Dv​Δ​]/(2Du​Dv​), as long as the homogeneous trace stays negative.
For the Brusselator, B=Dv​(b−1)−Du​a2. Write r=Du​/Dv​. The stationary determinant threshold gives the Turing threshold of the Brusselator in dimensional diffusivities:
bT​=(1+ar​)2,kc2​=Du​Dv​​a​,kc​=(Du​Dv​)1/4a​​.​
(35)
For this to be a genuine diffusion-driven loss of stability, it must occur before the homogeneous Hopf bifurcation threshold. The Brusselator Turing-before-Hopf diffusivity condition is
bT​<1+a2⟺Du​Dv​​>(a1+a2​+1​)2.​
(36)
Indeed a2r+2ar​<a2 is equivalent to r​<(1+a2​−1)/a. With this strict inequality, bT​<b<bH​ contains a spatially unstable but homogeneously stable interval. If the ratio condition fails, the first linear instability as b increases is homogeneous at bH​, rather than a Turing instability; equality gives simultaneous neutral homogeneous and finite-wavenumber modes. Equal diffusivities cannot produce a Turing instability.
These continuum formulas assume the onset wavenumber is available. On a finite domain with a specified discrete mode set, the discrete-mode Turing threshold for the Brusselator is
bTdiscrete​=k=0 allowedmin​[1+a2Dv​Du​​+Du​k2+Dv​k2a2​],​
(37)
provided this minimum is below bH​. The question supplies no boundary geometry, so the continuous-mode onset above is the usual answer; this last expression states how a finite-size restriction changes it.
