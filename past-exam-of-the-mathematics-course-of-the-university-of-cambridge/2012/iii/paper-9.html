Let a=kn/(2k+1), u=(k+1)n/(2k+1) and b=u/k. A graph cut with no crossing edges is obtained by assigning whole graph components to its two sides. Among all such assignments, maximize the smaller side's size S≤n/2, and write T=n−S for the other side.
Suppose S<a. Then T>u=kb. If a graph component on the larger side had size 0<w<n−2S, moving it to the smaller side would make both S+w and T−w greater than S. That contradicts maximality. Hence every graph component on the larger side has at least n−2S vertices. Since each has at most b vertices and their total exceeds kb, there must be at least k+1 of them. Consequently
n−S=T≥(k+1)(n−2S),S≥2k+1kn​=a,
(10)
a contradiction. Thus a≤S≤T≤u, proving the required empty cut with both sides at most (k+1)n/(2k+1). This balanced component cut argument works for arbitrary positive component weights as well; it does not require divisibility of n.

A breadth-first exploration of a binomial random graph is dominated by a Galton-Watson process whose offspring counts are independent random variables with law Bin(n,λ/n). If its total progeny T is at least j, the first j−1 offspring counts sum to at least j−1. Their moment-generating function is bounded by that of a Poisson distribution of mean λ(j−1). For the binomial branching process, the offspring probability generating function is f(s)=(1−p+ps)n. The Galton-Watson extinction fixed point gives 1−ρ=f(1−ρ)=(1−pρ)n.
The printed finite-binomial upper bound is false without an additional asymptotic qualification. For n=2 and p=(1+ε)/2, solving the fixed-point equation exactly gives
ρ=(1+ε)24ε​.

For instance ε=1/20 gives ρ=80/441>1/10=2ε. More generally, at every fixed n>1 the Taylor expansion gives ρ=2nε/(n−1)+On​(ε2), again contradicting that upper bound for sufficiently small positive ε.
Here is the corrected binomial branching survival correction. The Poisson branching process of mean 1+ε has branching survival probability r satisfying −log(1−r)=(1+ε)r. Expanding the logarithm gives
ε=∑j≥1​j+1rj​,2r​≤ε≤2(1−r)r​.

Use the standard Achlioptas process with two independent uniform candidate edges per step and a rule choosing one. The component-band vertex count N[k,Dk)​ counts vertices, not the number of graph components. Integer time rounding changes the arguments below by O(1) steps.
First, at tk​ the eventual graph component of order at least δn is assembled from graph components already present at tk​. Contract those initial graph components. During s=tc​−tk​ steps, at most s new edges are added, so the eventual connected contracted graph contains at most s+1 initial graph components. Initial graph components of order less than k can therefore contribute at most
(s+1)(k−1)≤δn/2
(31)
vertices, for sufficiently large n and fixed k, since s=δn/(2k)+O(1). For k=1 their contribution is zero. Consequently N≥k​(Gtk​​)≥δn/2.
We now use forced merging of large components. If at a time m the graph components of order at least K contain at least an vertices, then after at most A(a)n/K+O(1) steps there is a graph component of order at least an/3, except on an event of probability exponentially small in n/K. One explicit choice is A(a)=81(log2+2)/a4. The proof is a union bound over set partitions of the at most n/K initial large graph components: if all final graph components were small, a balanced component cut would split their initial vertices into sets of size at least an/3, and both candidate edges cross that fixed cut with probability at least a4/81 in every step. The rule cannot avoid such a forced crossing. The failure bound is e−2n/K, and a union bound makes the estimate simultaneous over all starting times at most 3n, for each fixed K.

The persistence of unsampled graph components prevents the mass from each size band from disappearing entirely before tc​. More precisely, a band [k,Dk) containing at least δn/4 vertices retains at least βn vertices in that same band after at most n/k steps, with high probability, for a fixed β>0 depending only on δ,D. To see why, each initial graph component in that band has a fixed positive probability that none of the offered edge endpoints touches it during the interval. Such a graph component remains unchanged regardless of the selection rule. The McDiarmid inequality concentrates the total mass of these untouched graph components. The reusable lemma supplies the full uniform-in-starting-time argument, including the version that samples absent edges.
Choose finitely many disjoint bands [Dj,Dj+1), j=0,…,J, with (J+1)β>1. Part (i) provides at least δn/4 vertices in band j at its time tDj​. Each interval from that time to tc​ has length at most n/Dj, so persistence leaves at least βn vertices in every one of these disjoint bands at the common time tc​. A union bound over this fixed finite number of bands makes all the conclusions simultaneous. Their total exceeds n, a contradiction. No fixed two-choice Achlioptas rule satisfies the explosive percolation hypothesis.
This continuity of fixed-choice percolation is the fixed-choice obstruction proved by Riordan and Warnke; their original research paper also treats a broader class of rules. The argument here does not apply when the number of offered choices grows with n.
