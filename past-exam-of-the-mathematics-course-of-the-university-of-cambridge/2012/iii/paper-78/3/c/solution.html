The zero-mean fluctuation is W, not the refractive index n: ⟨n⟩=1. Also the full scattering potential has a nonzero mean and is not Gaussian:
V=2μk02​W+μ2k02​W2,V=μ2k02​.
(36)
The usual Gaussian closure requires W to be a jointly Gaussian random field, not merely to have a Gaussian distribution at each point. We first give the intended leading weak-fluctuation result with the linearized potential V1​=2μk02​W, then account for the quadratic term.
Keep the definitions q,B from the preceding solution and let χ1​=B∫D​V1​(y)e−iq⋅ydy. Because the incident field has unit modulus,
I=⟨eχ1​+χ1∗​⟩,h(y)=Be−iq⋅y+B∗eiq⋅y=2Re(Be−iq⋅y).
(37)
The exponent is a real Gaussian linear functional Z=∫D​hV1​. If CW​(y−z)=⟨W(y)W(z)⟩, the linearized potential has autocorrelation function of a random field C1​(s)=4μ2k04​CW​(s). Complete the square in the one-dimensional Gaussian density of Z: ⟨eZ⟩=exp(⟨Z⟩+VarZ/2). Here its mean is zero, so
Ilin​=exp{21​∫D​∫D​h(y)h(z)C1​(y−z)dydz}.​
(38)
This is Gaussian intensity in the first Rytov approximation. In a more explicit complex notation, put
J1​=∫D​∫D​C1​(y−z)e−iq⋅(y−z)dydz,J2​=∫D​∫D​C1​(y−z)e−iq⋅(y+z)dydz.
(39)
Then
Ilin​=exp{∣B∣2J1​+Re(B2J2​)}.​
(40)
The second term is the pseudo-covariance of a complex random variable contribution. It does not vanish for an arbitrary finite real medium. The real-kernel expression shows that the combined exponent is nonnegative, even though its two complex-form contributions need not be separately nonnegative.
For the full potential, Gaussian moment pairing theorem gives its centered covariance function
CV​(s)=Cov(V(y),V(y+s))=4μ2k04​CW​(s)+2μ4k04​CW​(s)2.
(41)
Its uncentered autocorrelation is RV​(s)=CV​(s)+μ4k04​. The first Rytov approximation is linear in V, but its intensity is an exponential average; applying a Gaussian formula exactly to this quadratic potential would be incorrect. A consistent expansion through order μ2 is
logI=2μ2k02​Re[B∫D​e−iq⋅ydy]+21​∫D​∫D​h(y)h(z)C1​(y−z)dydz+O(μ4).​
(42)
The first term is the coherent mean-potential correction. Odd powers vanish by the symmetry of the joint Gaussian field. The variance term can equally use CV​=RV​−V2 through this order. Thus the mean shift must not be discarded while retaining the order-μ2 fluctuation intensity.
An exact expression within the far-field, first-Rytov approximation model is also available when the full n2 is retained. Assume the restriction of W to D is a Gaussian element of the real Hilbert space L2(D), with covariance operator C of kernel CW​(y−z). Let H denote multiplication by h, ℓh​=2μk02​h, Q=μ2k02​C1/2HC1/2 and bh​=C1/2ℓh​. The Gaussian quadratic exponential moment is
I=det(I−2Q)−1/2exp[21​⟨bh​,(I−2Q)−1bh​⟩].​
(43)
Here the determinant is the Fredholm determinant; require I−2Q strictly positive for finiteness. Diagonalize the self-adjoint trace-class operator Q and complete the square in each Gaussian coordinate to derive the formula. On a bounded D, unit pointwise variance makes trC=∣D∣, so the covariance is trace class under the stated measurable L2 hypothesis. Small enough μ satisfies the positivity condition. Expanding the determinant and inverse recovers the preceding mean and covariance terms. For small μ this is also determined by the potential autocorrelation: since CW​∈[−1,1],
CW​(s)=μ2−1+1+CV​(s)/(2k04​)​​,CV​=RV​−μ4k04​,
(44)
using the branch near zero. This makes explicit the extra joint-Gaussian assumption behind an exact correlation-based answer.
Finally, Gaussian marginals alone are insufficient. Take X∼N(0,1) and independent random signs Sj​. The stationary sequence Wj​=Sj​X has Gaussian marginals and covariance ⟨Wi​Wj​⟩=δij​, just like independent standard Gaussian variables. However ⟨et(W1​+W2​)⟩=(1+e2t2)/2, whereas for the independent Gaussian pair it is et2. The different intensities show why the joint hypothesis is needed. The mean-zero hint is interpreted for W, and the linearized Gaussian formula is not claimed to be exact for V=k02​(n2−1).
