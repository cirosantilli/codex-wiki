Screening applies the eligibility criteria for a clinical trial before treatment allocation. It identifies the population to which the comparison pertains and excludes individuals for whom the intervention or measurements are unsuitable. Recording potentially eligible individuals also helps describe recruitment and assess external validity. It should precede randomization so eligibility is not decided using knowledge of the allocated treatment.

Obtain informed consent so that participation in the clinical trial is voluntary and based on an understanding of its procedures, potential benefits and risks, alternatives and withdrawal arrangements. Consent before allocation prevents willingness to participate from being selected according to the known treatment assignment.

Measure baseline covariates before the intervention can change them. These measurements describe the recruited population, permit assessment of covariate balance, and provide prognostic predictors that can improve statistical efficiency. In particular, including prognostic baseline covariates in a linear regression reduces unexplained outcome variation without conditioning on a treatment consequence.

Randomization makes assignment independent of the participants' pre-existing potential outcomes, giving an unbiased comparison of allocation groups under the usual trial assumptions. It therefore protects against baseline confounding, including from unmeasured factors, and supplies an assignment distribution for inference. Allocation concealment protects this step from recruitment decisions based on the upcoming assignment. It does not guarantee exact balance in every realized sample.

Use a baseline-adjusted linear regression for an intention-to-treat analysis:
Yi​=α+βZi​+γTxi​+εi​,εi​∼indN(0,σ2).
(1)
Condition on the observed baseline covariates and assignment. The parameter of primary interest is β, the mean effect of assignment to the experimental intervention, under this constant-effect conditional mean model. Independent normal distributions with common variance give the usual Gaussian likelihood and regression inference. Prognostic baseline covariates improve precision by explaining outcome variability.
Do not include the actual intervention-use variable Di​ as an ordinary adjustment variable for this estimand: it is measured after assignment and can mediate the effect being estimated. Post-randomization adjustment changes a treatment estimand; the resulting coefficient on Zi​ would generally represent a different comparison. Participants remain in their assigned groups even when adherence differs.

Within the displayed sequential response selection model, θ=0 makes every attempt's nonresponse probability independent of Y, and hence gives the outcome-independence needed for complete-case analysis. The estimate θ=0.38 suggests that larger outcomes are associated with greater odds of an unsuccessful attempt. Per one-unit outcome increase, the fitted odds ratio is e0.38≈1.46, with the reported interval transformed to (e−0.06,e0.82)≈(0.94,2.27).
The confidence interval for θ includes zero, so there is no rejection of outcome-independent missingness at the five-percent level within this model. This does not establish that the missing at random assumption is true: the interval also allows appreciable outcome dependence, and inference depends on the chosen selection model. The intercepts describe response levels, not independence from the outcome. Nor can the respondents' mean and standard deviation establish the missing outcomes' distribution. There are 300−211=89 unobserved outcomes, about 29.7%, so conclusions about the full trial depend materially on the missingness assumptions.

A fixed-effect meta-analysis models the studies as estimating a common underlying treatment effect. In a generic inverse-variance version, independent log odds ratios yi​ have approximate distributions N(δ,vi​) and are pooled with weights 1/vi​. The supplied forest plot instead has the Mantel–Haenszel pooled odds ratio weights: for event/nonevent cells ai​,bi​,ci​,di​ and total ni​, the normalized weights are proportional to bi​ci​/ni​. They reproduce the printed percentages and yield a pooled odds ratio about 2.338. This is still a common-effect analysis; the two weighting methods should not be silently identified.
A random-effects meta-analysis allows different true effects: yi​∣δi​∼N(δi​,vi​) with δi​∼N(μ,τ2). The between-study heterogeneity variance τ2 adds to sampling variance, giving marginal variance vi​+τ2. Its pooled estimate targets the mean of the study-effect distribution rather than one identical effect. Uncertainty about heterogeneity must also be considered.

c OurBigBook.com
The I-squared statistic of 6.6% is a small estimate of excess variation on the heterogeneity scale. It does not mean that 6.6% of studies are different, nor that this percentage of patients responds differently. The Cochran's Q statistic compares the observed variation with that expected from the studies' sampling errors; its approximate null distribution has five degrees of freedom here. A heterogeneity-test p-value of 0.374 provides no rejection of the common-effect hypothesis.
The individual confidence intervals in the forest plot overlap substantially, although some point estimates are larger than others. Taken together, there is little evidence of between-study heterogeneity in these six studies. Neither the nonsignificant test nor the small I-squared statistic proves equal effects: with only six studies, detecting moderate heterogeneity can be difficult. A study being individually significant while another is not is also not itself a test of their difference.

Use a subgroup analysis in meta-analysis comparing studies before and after 2000, with a direct test of the between-subgroup effect difference. Alternatively use meta-regression with year, or the before/after indicator, as a study-level predictor. Compare the effect estimates themselves: significant and nonsignificant results need not differ significantly.
Two reasons for caution are that the proposed contrast was selected after examining the results, creating a multiple testing issue, and that only six studies supply an observational comparison of study characteristics. Year can be confounded with dose, participant populations, follow-up or design, so an apparent year effect need not be caused by time itself. The small number of studies also makes these comparisons imprecise. These would be exploratory findings rather than confirmation of an effect-modification hypothesis.

Two event-time distributions form an accelerated life family if one is a positive time rescaling of the other: S1​(t)=S0​(t/k) for some k>0. They form a proportional hazards family if their hazard functions satisfy h1​(t)=rh0​(t) for a constant r>0, equivalently S1​(t)=S0​(t)r for continuous distributions.
First take the standard positive-scale convention c>0. Write SU​(u)=FU​(u)=P(U>u), retaining the question's survivor notation rather than interpreting FU​ as a cumulative distribution. Since Tz​=ea+bzUc,
Sz​(t)=SU​(t1/ce−(a+bz)/c),t>0.​
(8)
It follows that Sz​(t)=S0​(te−bz), proving the accelerated life family property with time multiplier ebz. In fact the scaling identity Tz​=debzT0​ holds for any fixed real c.
The given density integrates to P(X≤x)=1−e−ex, so U=eX has the unit exponential distribution. Its survivor is e−u. Consequently
Sz​(t)=exp[−e−(a+bz)/ct1/c],hz​(t)=c1​e−(a+bz)/ct1/c−1,
(9)
and
hz​(t)/h0​(t)=e−bz/c.​
(10)
These are the Weibull accelerated-life and proportional-hazards families, with common shape 1/c>0.
The positive-scale assumption is necessary for this last conclusion and is not explicit in the printed description of the constants. If c<0, the transformation reverses the inequality, giving Sz​(t)=1−SU​(r−) with r=t1/ce−(a+bz)/c; for continuous U this is 1−SU​(r). For the given density these are scaled Fréchet distributions, generally not proportional hazards. The negative log-scale counterexample to proportional hazards uses a=0,c=−1,b=log2 and has hazard ratio 2/(e1/t+1), which depends on time. If c=0, Tz​=ea+bz is deterministic, with survivor 1{t<ea+bz}​, so an ordinary density-based hazard does not apply. These cases preserve the accelerated-life identity but do not prove the asserted proportional-hazards conclusion.

At each event time within a stratum, let rA​,rB​ be the group risk sets and let d be the total number of observed failures. Under the null of equal hazard functions, the expected A count is drA​/(rA​+rB​). The log-rank statistic adds observed minus expected A counts over event times; the stratified log-rank statistic first makes these comparisons within each patient stratum, then adds across patients. Let V be the sum of the corresponding null conditional variances. The standardized statistic U/V​ has an approximate standard normal distribution under the null, and its square has an approximate one-degree-of-freedom chi-squared distribution. Compare it with the appropriate quantile for a two-sided test, under the usual independent censoring and exchangeability assumptions.

No: with one pair per stratum, the statistic uses failure order but discards the size of the time difference. The paired log-rank reduction to a sign test assigns the same contribution when A fails just before B as when A fails much earlier. This deliberately avoids a model for the failure-time distances, but it does not extract every kind of evidence in the recorded times. An accelerated failure time model, for example, could use log-time differences in uncensored pairs under additional distributional assumptions.

A survivor function is S(t)=P(T>t). For a nonnegative event time it is nonincreasing and right-continuous, with values between zero and one; a proper finite event time has S(t)→0 as t→∞. Here w(0)=1, w(t)>0, w(t)→0, and w′(t)=−(e−t+6e−2t)/4<0 for t≥0. Extending it by one for t<0 supplies a valid continuous survivor function.
The density is −w′, so the hazard function is
h(t)=e−t+3e−2te−t+6e−2t​=1+1+3e−t3e−t​,h′(t)=−(1+3e−t)23e−t​<0.​
(13)
In particular h(0)=7/4 and t→∞lim​h(t)=1​. The survivor is a mixture of rate-one and rate-two exponential distributions with weights 1/4 and 3/4. Among long-term survivors the rate-one component dominates, explaining the limiting hazard. More generally the hazard derivative for a mixture of exponential distributions is minus the variance of the component rate among survivors; the decreasing hazard is selection, not an individual component's changing hazard.

A frailty model uses an unobserved nonnegative random hazard multiplier to represent variation in individual risk. In a proportional frailty model, conditional on U=u the cumulative hazard function is uH0​(t), giving survivor e−uH0​(t). Averaging over the frailty random variable by the law of total probability yields
S(t)=∫0∞​e−uH0​(t)g(u)du=g​(H0​(t)),​
(14)
the Laplace transform of the frailty distribution evaluated at the baseline integrated hazard. This also applies to atoms by integration against the probability measure; the printed Dirac expression is not an ordinary continuous density.
In the given two-atom case, the Dirac delta function notation assigns probabilities 1/4 and 3/4 to U=4/7 and U=8/7. These have mean one, fixing the frailty scale relative to the baseline hazard. With H0​(t)=7t/4, the two conditional integrated hazards are t and 2t. Therefore
S(t)=41​e−t+43​e−2t=w(t).​
(15)
The frailty distribution among survivors shifts toward the smaller multiplier 4/7 over time, reproducing the declining population hazard and limit one without changing either individual's constant conditional hazard.
