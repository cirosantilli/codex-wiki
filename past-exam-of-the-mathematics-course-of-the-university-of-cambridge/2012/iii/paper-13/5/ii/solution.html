An elliptic curve works over every algebraically closed field, including one algebraic over a finite field. Give it explicitly in P2 by
E:{y2z=x3−xz2,y2z+yz2=x3,​chark=2,chark=2.​
(42)
In the first case the only point at infinity is O=[0:1:0], where the derivative with respect to z is nonzero. In the affine chart z=1, a singular point of an algebraic variety would have y=0, x3−x=0, and 3x2−1=0. The possible roots x=0,1,−1 cannot satisfy the last equation in characteristic different from 2: at 0 it is −1, and at ±1 it is 2. In the second case the three homogeneous partial derivatives are x2,z2,y2, up to harmless signs, and cannot vanish simultaneously at a projective point. Thus both cubics are smooth varieties. A reducible plane cubic would have intersecting positive-degree components by Bézout theorem, and be singular at an intersection; therefore these smooth cubics are irreducible varieties. The genus-degree formula gives g=(3−1)(3−2)/2=1. With the marked point O they are elliptic curves.
For a smooth algebraic curve, its divisor class group is its Picard group. Its degree-zero subgroup is
Pic0(E)={[D]:degD=0}.
(43)
The map P↦[P−O] gives an isomorphism of abelian groups
E(k)≅Pic0(E).
(44)
To recall why it is a bijection, Riemann-Roch theorem in geometric genus one says every degree-one divisor class has exactly one independent section: a canonical divisor has degree zero, so the dual degree-−1 term has no section. Thus the class contains one effective divisor on an algebraic curve of degree one, namely a point P, and that point is unique. Translating by −O gives the claimed bijection; this is precisely the chord-and-tangent group law on the elliptic curve.
Choose a prime integer ℓ different from chark. The multiplication-by-n morphism [ℓ]:E→E has derivative ℓ times the identity at O, so it is nonconstant. A nonconstant morphism of algebraic varieties between smooth projective curves is surjective: its image is closed by proper morphism and cannot have algebraic dimension zero. Since k is an algebraically closed field, this makes [ℓ] surjective on k-points. Therefore
E(k)=ℓE(k).
(45)
Yet E(k) is infinite. For each x∈k, the affine equation has a solution for y in the algebraically closed field; since k is infinite, this gives infinitely many points.
By the Fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups, a finitely generated abelian group A satisfying A=ℓA has no free part and is a finite torsion group of order prime to ℓ. The infinite group E(k) cannot have this property while being finitely generated. Thus Pic0(E) is not finitely generated. A subgroup of a finitely generated abelian group is finitely generated, so
Cl(E) is not a finitely generated abelian group.​
(46)
This proof uses divisibility and infinitude, rather than uncountability, so it remains valid over countable algebraically closed fields.
