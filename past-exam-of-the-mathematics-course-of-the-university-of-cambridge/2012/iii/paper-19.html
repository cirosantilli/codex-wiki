Choose a generator [Sn] of Hn​(Sn;Z)≅Z. The mapping degree of a continuous map is the integer determined by
f∗​[Sn]=(degf)[Sn].​
(1)
For n≥1 this is ordinary top-dimensional homology; using reduced homology also gives a convention for n=0. Domain and target use the same orientation, so reversing the chosen generator in both leaves the integer unchanged. Homotopy invariance of homology makes the mapping degree invariant under homotopy.
We interpret the manifold criterion for a suspension in the category of nonempty topological manifolds without boundary, and “sphere” as homeomorphic to the standard sphere. Let X have dimension d. If X≅Sd, an explicit homeomorphism from its suspension to Sd+1 is
[x,t]⟼(sin(πt)x,cos(πt)).
(2)
It is a continuous map that is a bijection: the two collapsed ends become the poles, and every other point determines t and x uniquely. The compact-to-Hausdorff continuous bijection theorem makes it a homeomorphism.
Conversely suppose SX is a topological manifold. Its open part X×(0,1) shows its dimension is d+1. At a suspension vertex v there is an open cone neighbourhood U on X. It is contractible, while U∖{v} admits a deformation retraction onto X. The long exact sequence in relative homology and excision give the local homology from a link calculation
Hi​(SX,SX∖{v};Z)≅Hi−1​(X;Z).
(3)
A point of a (d+1)-dimensional topological manifold without boundary has local homology Z in degree d+1 and zero in all other degrees. Consequently
Hj​(X;Z)≅{Z,0,​j=d,j=d.​
(4)
Thus X is an integral homology sphere. This conclusion alone does not identify its homeomorphism type.
If d≥2, it is also simply connected. Choose a cone neighbourhood U of the vertex and an open coordinate ball B around the vertex inside U. By compactness of X, sufficiently short entire cone neighbourhoods fit inside any neighbourhood of v: the inverse image of that neighbourhood contains X×{0}, and a finite subcover supplies one uniform collar length. Choose such a shorter cone V⊂B. The inclusions of punctured neighbourhoods factor as
V∖{v}⟶B∖{v}⟶U∖{v}.
(5)
The composite induces an isomorphism of fundamental groups, since both cone punctures are products of X with intervals and their inclusion is a homotopy equivalence. The middle punctured ball has the homotopy type of Sd and is simply connected for d≥2. Hence the composite is also zero on the fundamental group, forcing π1​(X)=0. This uses the nested neighbourhoods, not an unsupported claim that an arbitrary punctured neighbourhood is a sphere.
The Hurewicz theorem, applied successively to the vanishing lower homology groups, now gives πj​(X)=0 for j<d and πd​(X)≅Hd​(X)≅Z. A representative of its generator is a continuous map Sd→X inducing an isomorphism in every integral homology degree. Topological manifolds have the homotopy type of CW complexes, so the homological Whitehead theorem makes it a homotopy equivalence. We have proved that X is a homotopy sphere.
At this point the topological classification theorem is essential: the topological generalized Poincare theorem says that a closed homotopy sphere is homeomorphic to the standard sphere. Applying it completes the converse for d≥2. For d=1, the local calculation makes X a connected compact one-dimensional manifold without boundary, hence a circle. For d=0, a compact zero-dimensional manifold is a finite set, and H0​(X)≅Z forces exactly two points. Thus it is S0. Altogether,
SX is a topological manifold without boundary⟺X≅Sd.​
(6)
The last classification step is not a consequence of homology alone, and we make no assertion of diffeomorphism with a prescribed smooth structure. If the word manifold allows boundary on both sides, the printed assertion needs this correction: the suspension of the interval is a closed disk. For instance [x,t]↦((2x−1)(1−∣2t−1∣),2t−1) identifies it with the filled diamond ∣u∣+∣v∣≤1, although the interval is not a sphere.
To construct sphere maps of arbitrary integer degree, first use hm​:S1→S1, hm​(z)=zm for any integer m, including the constant map when m=0. Lifting its argument to the real line gives θ↦mθ, so the fundamental one-cycle is sent to m times itself. Therefore deghm​=m.
Two cone neighbourhoods cover a suspension; their intersection admits a deformation retraction onto the original space. The reduced Mayer–Vietoris theorem gives a natural suspension isomorphism
Hq+1​(SX)≅Hq​(X).
(7)
Naturality means that the suspension of a continuous map induces the same integer multiplier. This proves degree under suspension. Using the standard identification S(Sk)≅Sk+1, set
fn,m​=Sn−1hm​:Sn→Sn,degfn,m​=m(n≥1).​
(8)
Thus every integer occurs in every positive sphere dimension, with negative degrees handled just as well as positive ones.
If “every n” includes zero, that final printed assertion is false. The generator of H0​(S0) is the difference of its two points. A map of a two-point set is the identity, the transposition, or one of two constant maps, giving precisely
degf∈{−1,0,1}on S0.​
(9)
These degrees of maps of the zero-sphere are the explicit exception to the arbitrary-degree construction.

Use integral homology throughout. The singular chain group Ck​(X) is free on the continuous maps from the standard k-simplex to X. Since the boundary operator preserves chains lying in A, these form a chain subcomplex. The relative chain complex and its relative homology are
Ck​(X,A)=Ck​(X)/Ck​(A),Hk​(X,A)=ker(∂:Ck​(X,A)→Ck−1​(X,A))/im(∂:Ck+1​(X,A)→Ck​(X,A)).​
(10)
The short exact sequence of chain complexes gives the long exact sequence in relative homology.
A sufficient hypothesis for the quotient comparison is a nonempty good pair: A is closed and has an open neighbourhood U which admits a deformation retraction onto A while fixing A throughout. A CW pair is another standard sufficient setting. These hypotheses hold for a simple closed curve in the surface here, using its annular collar neighbourhood. The induced quotient map q:X→Y=X/A collapses A to a point a.
Here is the proof of the collapsing a pair theorem. Put V=U/A⊂Y. The deformation retraction makes H∗​(U,A)=0, so the long exact sequence of the triple gives H∗​(X,A)≅H∗​(X,U). It also contracts V onto a, giving H∗​(Y,{a})≅H∗​(Y,V). Because A is closed inside the open neighbourhood U, excision gives
H∗​(X,U)≅H∗​(X∖A,U∖A).
(11)
Similarly, excision of the closed point a in the open set V gives
H∗​(Y,V)≅H∗​(Y∖{a},V∖{a}).
(12)
The quotient map restricts to a homeomorphism between the two punctured pairs, so these are the same groups and the identifications commute with q∗​. Finally H∗​(Y,{a})≅H∗​(Y), including degree zero. Thus
q∗​:Hk​(X,A)∼​Hk​(X/A).​
(13)
The neighbourhood condition is part of the result; the quotient assertion is not made for arbitrary bad pairs.
For the genus-two closed orientable surface with its chosen orientation, H0​(X)=Z, H1​(X)=Z4, H2​(X)=Z, with higher groups zero. The curve has H0​(A)=H1​(A)=Z. Its relevant long exact sequence in relative homology is
0⟶Z⟶H2​(X,A)⟶Zi∗​​Z4⟶H1​(X,A)⟶Z∼​Z⟶0.
(14)
The final map is an isomorphism because both spaces are connected. Consequently H1​(X,A)=cokeri∗​ and H0​(X,A)=0; all relative groups above degree two vanish. The quotient topological space is connected, so its unreduced H0​ is Z.
In the separating case, the curve is the oriented boundary of one of the two subsurfaces. Its homology class in X is therefore zero, so i∗​=0. The exact sequence becomes
0→Z→H2​(X,A)→Z→0,H1​(X,A)=Z4.
(15)
The first sequence splits since its quotient is a free abelian group. Hence the homology after collapsing a separating surface curve is
Hk​(X/A;Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z4,Z2,0,​k=0,k=1,k=2,k≥3.​​
(16)
Geometrically, collapsing the boundary of each once-punctured torus fills its puncture with a cone on the circle, which is a disk. The quotient is a wedge sum of two tori at the collapsed point. Their two independent fundamental classes explain the extra second-homology generator.
In the nonseparating case, join the two new boundary components of the cut surface by an arc. Upon regluing, this supplies a closed curve meeting A once transversely. The signed intersection pairing on an oriented surface therefore provides an integer homomorphism H1​(X)→Z taking [A] to ±1. Thus [A] is a nonzero primitive homology class, and i∗​:Z→Z4 is an injection onto a direct summand. Its kernel is zero and its cokernel is Z3. The same exact sequence gives H2​(X,A)≅H2​(X)≅Z. Therefore the homology after collapsing a nonseparating surface curve is
Hk​(X/A;Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z3,0,​k=0,2,k=1,k≥3.​​
(17)
Another description starts from the genus-one surface with two boundary circles. Collapse those two circles separately to obtain a closed torus with two marked points, then identify the two points. Identifying two distinct points in a connected CW complex adds a loop up to homotopy, so this quotient has the homotopy type of T2∨S1. The preceding relative homology calculation proves the groups without requiring that homotopy description.

For a CW complex X, let Xk be its k-skeleton. The cellular chain complex is
Ckcell​(X)=Hk​(Xk,Xk−1;Z)≅⨁k-cells e​Z[e].
(18)
The isomorphism uses excision and Hk​(Dk,Sk−1)≅Z, after choosing an orientation for each cell. In degree zero, take X−1=∅ and the free group on the vertices. The differential is the composite
Hk​(Xk,Xk−1)∂​Hk−1​(Xk−1)⟶Hk−1​(Xk−1,Xk−2).
(19)
The long exact sequences of skeleton pairs imply dk−1​dk​=0. The cellular boundary formula describes its coefficient at a (k−1)-cell as the mapping degree of the attaching sphere after all other (k−1)-cells are collapsed. In degree one this is the signed difference of the two endpoints. The cellular homology theorem identifies the homology of this complex with singular homology: relative homology of successive skeletons is concentrated in their cell dimension, and the exact skeleton sequences leave precisely these kernels modulo images. This is the construction, rather than merely a count of cells.
The filtration CP0⊂CP1⊂CP2 gives one cell in each of dimensions zero, two and four, since CPk∖CPk−1≅Ck. There are no odd-dimensional cells, so every cellular differential vanishes. Thus
Hk​(CP2;Z)≅{Z,0,​k=0,2,4,otherwise.​
(20)
The complex projective line has one zero-cell and one two-cell. Its product with itself has one zero-cell, two two-cells and one four-cell. Its cellular chain complex again has zero differentials, giving
Hk​(CP1×CP1;Z)≅⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z2,0,​k=0,4,k=2,otherwise.​
(21)
A homeomorphism induces isomorphisms on homology groups. The second groups have different ranks, and therefore
CP2≅CP1×CP1.​
(22)
Now orient the Complex projective plane by its complex coordinates. The inclusion of its two-skeleton CP1 identifies the fundamental class of a projective line with a generator h of H2​(CP2;Z)≅Z: the cellular generator has no incoming three-cell boundary and no outgoing boundary.
Take the projective lines L0​={z2​=0} and L1​={z1​=0}. A path of unitary coordinate transformations takes one to the other, so their oriented homology classes are both h. They meet in the single point [1:0:0]. In its affine chart, let w=z1​/z0​ and v=z2​/z0​. Their tangent spaces are the complex w-axis and complex v-axis, so the intersection is a transverse intersection. The ordered real bases
(∂Rew​,∂Imw​),(∂Rev​,∂Imv​)
(23)
concatenate to the complex orientation of the ambient four-manifold. Thus the local sign is +1, and
h⋅h=[L0​]⋅[L1​]=1.​
(24)
This computes the intersection form using distinct representatives of the same class, avoiding an ill-defined attempt to count a line intersecting itself as a set. Bilinearity now gives
λ(ah,bh)=ab,[λ]h​=(1),det[λ]=1.
(25)
Equivalently, the map H2​→Hom(H2​,Z) sending a to λ(a,−) is an isomorphism. This is exactly a unimodular intersection pairing, and proves projective lines generate a unimodular intersection form. Reversing the ambient orientation would change the matrix to (−1) and would still be unimodular.

The multiplicative norm first rules out zero divisors: if xy=0 and x=0, then 0=∣x∣∣y∣ forces y=0. If the dimension is zero the claimed bound already holds, so suppose it is positive.
It is worth checking that the printed description of a ring with an underlying real vector space really permits differentiation of multiplication. This follows from the norm identity even if compatibility with real scalars is not separately stated. For fixed x, the map Lx​(y)=xy is additive by distributivity, and
∣Lx​(y)−Lx​(z)∣=∣x(y−z)∣=∣x∣∣y−z∣.
(26)
It is therefore continuous. Additivity gives rational linearity. Approximating a real number by rationals and using continuity proves Lx​(ty)=tLx​(y) for every real t. The same argument holds in the other variable. This is automatic real bilinearity from a multiplicative norm, so multiplication is a bilinear map between finite-dimensional vector spaces, and hence is smooth.
Let S={x∈A:∣x∣=1} be the unit sphere of the given positive-definite bilinear form. It is a copy of Sn−1. The squaring map
Q:S→S,Q(x)=x2,
(27)
is well defined because ∣x2∣=∣x∣2=1, and is smooth. Commutativity gives its derivative
DQx​(v)=xv+vx=2xv.
(28)
For x∈S and v=0, the norm identity gives ∣DQx​(v)∣=2∣v∣, so the derivative is injective, including on the tangent space Tx​S=x⊥. It takes tangent vectors into Tx2​S since Q maps the sphere to itself. Alternatively, polarization of ∣xy∣2=∣x∣2∣y∣2 gives ⟨xy,xz⟩=∣x∣2⟨y,z⟩, so ⟨x2,2xv⟩=2⟨x,v⟩=0 directly.
The only identifications under squaring are antipodal:
Q(x)=Q(y) ⟹ (x−y)(x+y)=0 ⟹ y=x or y=−x.
(29)
The factorization uses commutativity, and the last implication uses the absence of zero divisors. Conversely Q(−x)=Q(x). It follows that Q induces an injective smooth map
q:RPn−1=S/(x∼−x)⟶Sn−1.
(30)
The antipodal quotient map is a local diffeomorphism, so the injectivity of DQ implies injectivity of Dq. Since Real projective space is compact, the stated compact injective immersion is an embedding theorem makes q a closed embedding.
For n≥2 its source and target have the same dimension n−1. The inverse function theorem makes its image open as well as closed in the connected sphere Sn−1. It is nonempty, so the image is the whole sphere; consequently q is a diffeomorphism. But for n≥3 this is impossible:
π1​(RPn−1)≅Z/2,π1​(Sn−1)=0.
(31)
Indeed, the antipodal two-sheeted covering map Sn−1→RPn−1 has simply connected total space in this range, and is its universal cover, with deck group Z/2. A sphere of dimension at least two is simply connected. These fundamental groups cannot be those of homeomorphic spaces.
We have obtained the dimension bound for a commutative Euclidean normed algebra:
dimR​A≤2.​
(32)
The real numbers and complex numbers with their usual products and Euclidean norms attain dimensions one and two. In dimension two, the induced map RP1→S1 is entirely compatible with the argument: the circle squaring map identifies exactly the two antipodal preimages. We neither assume a multiplicative identity nor use a classification of real division algebras; the squaring argument proves the bound from the supplied hypotheses.
