Write u=ux​ and B=Bz​. In this geometry the Lorentz force density is purely longitudinal and equals −∂x​(B2/2μ0​)ex​. The ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation becomes Bt​+∂x​(uB)=0. Together with the continuity equation and adiabatic pressure evolution, the four primitive-variable equations are
ρt​+uρx​+ρux​=0,pt​+upx​+γpux​=0,ut​+uux​+ρ−1px​+μ0​ρB​Bx​=0,Bt​+uBx​+Bux​=0.
(1)
They have the required quasilinear form Ut​+AUx​=0 with
A=​u000​0u1/ρ0​ργpuB​00B/(μ0​ρ)u​​.
(2)
Here the notation ux​ in derivatives means ∂x​u, whereas u denotes the longitudinal velocity. Spatial dependence is one-dimensional; time dependence is allowed in the wave problem.
Put cf2​=γp/ρ+B2/(μ0​ρ), and assume ρ>0 and cf​>0. Expansion of the characteristic polynomial gives
det(A−λI)=(u−λ)2[(u−λ)2−cf2​],λ0​=u twice,λ±​=u±cf​.​
(3)
Two independent eigenvectors for λ0​ can be chosen as (1,0,0,0)T and (0,−B/μ0​,0,1)T. For the two propagating branches, useful eigenvectors are
r±​=(1,ργp​,ρ±cf​​,ρB​)T.
(4)
The repeated branch advects an entropy disturbance and a disturbance with unchanged total gas-plus-magnetic pressure. The ± branches are perpendicular fast magnetosonic waves, with speed cf​ relative to the fluid.
On a uniform background, a Fourier amplitude Uei(kx−ωt) obeys (kA−ωI)U=0. Thus the eigenvalues are the phase velocities ω/k for k=0, and the four dispersion branches are ω=ku twice and ω=k(u±cf​). The reduced geometry retains neither an independent transverse velocity nor an additional propagating Alfvén wave.
To construct each finite-amplitude simple wave in magnetohydrodynamics, trace a state-space curve with tangent r±​, using ρ as its parameter. Integrating the pressure and magnetic components gives
p=Kργ,B=bρ,dρdu​=±ρcf​(ρ)​,cf2​=γKργ−1+μ0​b2ρ​.
(5)
The constants K and b are the entropy and transverse magnetic flux freezing invariants along this state curve. Hence
u(ρ)=u∗​±∫ρ∗​ρ​rcf​(r)​dr.
(6)
Since AUρ​=λ±​Uρ​, substituting this curve into the original system reduces it to ρt​+λ±​(ρ)ρx​=0. By the chain rule,
∂t​λ±​+λ±​∂x​λ±​=0.​
(7)
Thus the characteristic speed itself satisfies the Inviscid Burgers equation. For initial speed L(a), its smooth solution is λ±​=L(a) along x=a+tL(a), until the characteristic curves intersect. This derivation supplies genuine state curves for simple waves, not merely a linear dispersion relation.

Let u=ur​<0 for accretion. The steady spherical continuity equation and radial Euler equations for an inviscid fluid give
drd​(r2ρu)=0,udrdu​=−ρ1​drdp​−drdΦ​,M˙=−4πr2ρu>0.
(8)
For a barotropic fluid, dp/dr=cs2​dρ/dr. Dividing continuity equation by r2ρu gives ρ′/ρ=−2/r−u′/u. Eliminating this derivative yields
uu2−cs2​​drdu​=r2cs2​​−Φ′(r).​
(9)
At a smooth transonic critical point, the coefficient of du/dr vanishes. A finite derivative then requires the right side to vanish simultaneously:
uc​=−cs,c​,Φ′(rc​)=rc​2cs,c2​​.​
(10)
These are the sonic and regularity conditions. Otherwise the derivative is singular and the proposed smooth transonic passage fails. The local slope must also be a real root compatible with the desired branch. For example, defining C=ρd(cs2​)/dρ at the point and differentiating both sides gives
(2+cs2​C​)(uc′​)2+rc​uc​4C​uc′​+rc2​4C+2cs2​​+Φ′′(rc​)=0.
(11)
This explains why simultaneous vanishing is a necessary regularity condition, rather than an automatic proof that every potential admits a critical crossing.
Define the barotropic enthalpy function by dh=dp/ρ. The momentum equation integrates to the Bernoulli equation
21​u2+h(ρ)+Φ=B.​
(12)
Changing the reference mass density in h changes only the constant B. For an isothermal closure this logarithmic function is a barotropic barotropic pressure potential; it should not be confused with the constant thermodynamic specific enthalpy of an ideal gas held at fixed temperature.
For isothermal transonic accretion in the Paczyński-Wiita potential, assume M>0 and cs​>0, and define rB​=GM/(2cs2​) and β=16cs2​/c2. The critical equation is
(rc​−rG​)2=rB​rc​,
(13)
whose two roots are rG​+21​rB​(1±1+β​). Only the plus root lies outside rG​. Therefore
rc​=rG​+4cs2​GM​(1+1+β​)=4cs2​GM​(1+β/2+1+β​)>rG​.​
(14)
For this isothermal case C=0, and the slope relation reduces to
(uc′​)2=(rc​−rG​)3GM​−rc2​cs2​​=rc2​(rc​−rG​)cs2​(rc​+rG​)​>0.
(15)
The positive slope is the inward-accretion solution connecting a small inward speed at large radius to the supersonic inward branch. Thus the exterior critical point is a genuine nondegenerate transonic point.
Choose the reference mass density as ρ0​, so h=cs2​log(ρ/ρ0​) and B=0 from the conditions at infinity. At uc​=−cs​,
ρc​=ρ0​exp[cs2​(rc​−rG​)GM​−21​]=ρ0​exp[1+1+β​4​−21​].
(16)
Substitution into M˙=4πrc2​ρc​cs​ gives
M˙=4cs3​πρ0​(GM)2​(1+β/2+1+β​)2exp[1+1+β​4​−21​].​
(17)
This is the rate selected by the smooth transonic accretion solution. Arbitrary static or subsonic formal solutions are not assigned this rate merely by specifying conditions at infinity. As rG​/rB​→0, the result tends to the isothermal Bondi accretion rate πe3/2ρ0​(GM)2/cs3​, a useful normalization check.

The flux representation gives BR​=−ψz​/R and Bz​=ψR​/R. Therefore ∇⋅B=0 and B⋅∇ψ=0. With magnetohydrodynamic mass loading k=k(ψ),
∇⋅(ρu)=∇⋅(kB)+∇⋅(ρveϕ​)=k∇⋅B+k′(ψ)B⋅∇ψ+R−1∂ϕ​(ρv)=0.
(18)
Thus the steady continuity equation is satisfied.
The cross product in the ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation simplifies to
u×B=veϕ​×B=Rv​∇ψ,∇×(u×B)=∇(v/R)×∇ψ=Reϕ​B⋅∇(v/R).
(19)
Hence steady induction requires B⋅∇(v/R)=0. On a regular connected poloidal flux surface, this integrates locally to
Rv​=ω(ψ).​
(20)
This is the field-line angular velocity of an axisymmetric wind. It need not equal the gas angular velocity when Bϕ​=0, because uϕ​=Rω+kBϕ​/ρ. The regular-surface qualification matters: if ψ is constant and the field is purely toroidal, induction alone cannot turn an arbitrary v(R,z)/R into a function of ψ.
Put F=(∇×B)×B and E=∣u∣2/2+Φ. Dot the pressure-free momentum equation with u. Since B⋅F=0, axisymmetry gives
ρkB​⋅∇E=μ0​ρRω​Fϕ​.
(21)
The azimuthal momentum equation in cylindrical coordinates similarly gives
uR​∂R​(Ruϕ​)+uz​∂z​(Ruϕ​)=μ0​ρR​Fϕ​,orρkB​⋅∇(Ruϕ​)=μ0​ρR​Fϕ​.
(22)
Subtracting ω times the second relation from the first, and using B⋅∇ω=0, proves that E−ωRuϕ​ is constant along a flowing regular poloidal flux surface. For nonzero mass loading its corotating energy invariant of an axisymmetric magnetic wind is
21​(uR2​+uz2​+(uϕ​−Rω)2)+Φ−21​R2ω2=ϵJ​(ψ).​
(23)
This is the invariant labeled ϵ in the question. It is a corotating mechanical energy, distinct from the total magnetohydrodynamic Bernoulli invariant, which includes electromagnetic energy flux. In standard notation it equals total specific energy minus ω times the total specific angular-momentum invariant.
Near the footpoint, negligible kBϕ​/ρ gives uϕ​≃Rω, while the poloidal velocity is parallel to the poloidal magnetic field. The gas approximately corotates and slides along a rotating field line as a bead on a wire, with effective potential
Φeff​=Φ−21​ω2R2.
(24)
A decreasing effective potential converts corotating potential energy into poloidal kinetic energy. This is the mechanism of magnetocentrifugal acceleration; local launching does not alone guarantee global escape.
For an attractive point mass, take Φ=−GM/(R2+z2)1/2 and ω2=GM/R03​. Parameterize the outward straight line by R=R0​+ssinα, z=scosα. The first derivative of Φeff​ vanishes at s=0 because the footpoint is in circular radial balance. The meridional Hessian there is ω2diag(−3,1), so
ds2d2Φeff​​​0​=ω2(cos2α−3sin2α)=ω2(1−4sin2α).
(25)
Thus the thirty-degree magnetocentrifugal launching criterion in its strict negative-curvature form is
α>π/6,0≤α≤π/2.​
(26)
Above this angle the equilibrium is unstable to an outward displacement; below it the near-footpoint potential initially rises. The source's proportionality constant must be negative to describe attraction.
The marginal straight-line launch at thirty degrees has zero quadratic curvature. For precisely the straight line and point-mass potential here, let x=s/R0​ and a=sinα. Direct expansion gives
GM/R0​Φeff​(s)−Φeff​(0)​=(21​−2a2)x2+(25​a3−23​a)x3+O(x4).
(27)
At α=π/6, this is −7x3/16+O(x4), so the outward direction is downhill at cubic order. Consequently the strict inequality is the nondegenerate quadratic criterion; it should not be interpreted as excluding every one-sided nonlinear marginal launch at equality. An exactly resting bead remains an equilibrium until displaced.

In the inertial frame the steady convective acceleration is −RΩ2eR​. For a barotropic fluid, ∇p/ρ=∇h, where h is the barotropic enthalpy function. The equilibrium momentum equation therefore gives
∇(h+Φ−21​Ω2R2)=0,H=h+Φ−21​Ω2R2=constant​
(28)
on each connected fluid region. Uniform rotation is essential: it makes the centrifugal acceleration derivable from the centrifugal potential −Ω2R2/2.
The Cowling approximation sets the gravitational-potential perturbation to zero while retaining background gravity. The barotropic pressure force linearizes as δ(∇p/ρ)=∇δh, with δh=p′/ρ=cs2​ρ′/ρ. Denote its mode amplitude by W. The advective time derivative on a scalar mode is ∂t​+Ω∂ϕ​, so the stated positive-frequency exponential produces iσ with σ=ω+mΩ. Linearizing the radial centrifugal term produces −2Ωvϕ​, while linearizing the azimuthal convective term produces 2ΩvR​. Thus
iσvR​−2Ωvϕ​=−WR​,iσvϕ​+2ΩvR​=−imW/R,iσvz​=−Wz​.
(29)
These are the pressure-gradient and Coriolis acceleration terms in the corotating-frame form. Independently, linearizing continuity equation gives
iσcs2​ρW​=−R1​∂R​(RρvR​)−Rimρvϕ​​−∂z​(ρvz​).​
(30)
The equilibrium mass density is axisymmetric, so no additional azimuthal background-density derivative appears.
For σ=0 and D=4Ω2−σ2=0, invert the horizontal momentum system:
vR​=−Di​(σWR​+R2mΩW​),vϕ​=D1​(2ΩWR​+RmσW​),vz​=σi​Wz​.
(31)
Inserting these into the continuity equation, dividing by iσ and multiplying by D gives
Dcs2​ρW​=R1​∂R​(RρWR​)−R2m2ρW​−σ2D​∂z​(ρWz​)+Rσ2mΩ​ρR​W.
(32)
The two terms proportional to ρWR​ cancel; this cancellation leaves precisely the background-density derivative in the last term. Since −D/σ2=1−4Ω2/σ2, this is exactly
Dcs2​ρW​=R1​∂R​(RρWR​)−R2m2ρW​+(1−σ24Ω2​)∂z​(ρWz​)+Rσ2mΩ​ρR​W.​
(33)
The singular frequency cases require the original velocity equations rather than this inversion.
In the low-frequency anelastic approximation for a rotating barotropic star, neglect the left side. Spherical background mass density satisfies ρR​/R=ρz​/z, understood by its smooth limiting form on the equatorial plane. For W=zRm,
R1​∂R​(RρWR​)−R2m2ρW​=mzRm−1ρR​,∂z​(ρWz​)=Rmρz​.
(34)
The remaining equation reduces to
Rmρz​[m+1+σ2mΩ​−σ24Ω2​]=0.
(35)
Writing q=2Ω/σ, the bracket factors as (m+1−q)(q+1). The regular desired branch is therefore
σ=m+12Ω​,W=zRm.​
(36)
The other algebraic factor corresponds to σ=−2Ω, where the eliminated horizontal system is singular; it is not classified by this pressure-equation inversion. A constant-density background makes the bulk factor identically zero but does not invalidate the displayed solution.
For the regular branch, an explicit velocity check is particularly informative. Up to a common mode normalization,
(vR​,vϕ​,vz​)=2Ωm+1​(−izRm−1, zRm−1, iRm).
(37)
It satisfies RvR​+zvz​=0 and ∇⋅v=0. Thus ∇⋅(ρv)=0 for every spherical mass density profile, directly verifying the anelastic continuity condition. The motion is tangential to spherical shells and is the sectoral inertial mode of a slowly rotating barotropic star. For single-valued azimuthal modes, m is a positive integer. With the source's exponential convention,
ω=σ−mΩ=−m+1(m−1)(m+2)​Ω.
(38)
For m≥2 the pattern is retrograde relative to the star but prograde in the inertial frame. This is a leading slow-rotation anelastic mode, not an exact solution of the compressible equations whose left-hand term was discarded.
