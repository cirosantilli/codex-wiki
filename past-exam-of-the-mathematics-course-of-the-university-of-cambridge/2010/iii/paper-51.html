a OurBigBook.com
Quantum teleportation transfers an arbitrary unknown qubit state to a separated receiver while using local operations and classical communication, rather than sending that input qubit. Alice and Bob first share one maximally entangled state of two qubits, such as a Bell pair; a shared spin singlet state is equivalent after a known local unitary operation.
Alice performs a joint Bell-basis measurement on the input qubit and her half of the pair. There are four possible outcomes. Bob's conditional qubit is the input state transformed by a known Pauli operator determined by that outcome. Alice sends the outcome as two classical bits, and Bob applies the inverse Pauli operator. Thus one shared Bell pair and two classical bits, together with local measurement and correction, transmit one unknown qubit exactly. The entangled resource is consumed.
The procedure does not produce a classical description of the unknown amplitudes and does not leave a second copy at Alice, so it respects the no-cloning theorem. Before receiving the classical outcome, Bob's reduced density matrix is I/2, independent of the input; this respects the no-communication theorem. The next part proves the full identity-channel action, including inputs entangled with another system.

Label an orthonormal basis of the n-dimensional Hilbert space by j=0,…,n−1, with indices modulo n. Let
ω=e2πi/n,X∣j⟩=∣j+1⟩,Z∣j⟩=ωj∣j⟩.
(1)
These are the shift and phase Heisenberg-Weyl operators. Alice holds the input S and one half A of the shared maximally entangled state
∣Φn​⟩AB​=n​1​∑j=0n−1​∣j⟩A​∣j⟩B​.
(2)
She measures SA in the generalized Bell basis
∣Bpq​⟩SA​=n​1​∑j=0n−1​ωpj∣j⟩S​∣j+q⟩A​,p,q=0,…,n−1.
(3)
Its inner products are
⟨Bpq​∣Bp′q′​⟩=δqq′​n1​∑j=0n−1​ω(p′−p)j=δpp′​δqq′​.
(4)
There are n2 orthonormal vectors in an n2-dimensional space, so this is a complete projective measurement.
For an unknown normalized state ∣u⟩=∑j​uj​∣j⟩, its contraction with measurement outcome (p,q) gives Bob's unnormalized state
SA​⟨Bpq​∣(∣u⟩S​∣Φn​⟩AB​)​=n1​j∑​uj​ω−pj∣j+q⟩B​=n1​XqZ−p∣u⟩B​.​
(5)
Each outcome has probability 1/n2, independently of the input. Alice communicates the two n-valued labels, and Bob applies the ordered inverse
Upq​=ZpX−q,Upq​(n1​XqZ−p∣u⟩)=n1​∣u⟩.​
(6)
The order matters because shift and phase operators do not generally commute. Normalizing the branch recovers the unknown state exactly, and all outcomes are corrected, so success is deterministic. The quantum resource is one maximally entangled pair of local dimension n. The classical resource is an n2-valued message, with information content 2log2​n bits; ⌈log2​(n2)⌉ ordinary bits suffice in fixed-length binary encoding.
This is faithful qudit teleportation even for an input entangled with a reference R. Expand a joint pure state as ∑j​∣j⟩S​∣rj​⟩R​, without requiring the reference vectors to be orthogonal. The same contraction and correction act on each system basis vector, producing n−1∑j​∣j⟩B​∣rj​⟩R​. Thus the whole joint state, not just a reduced state, is preserved. Linearity extends the conclusion to mixed inputs. This is teleportation as an identity channel on a reference.

Yes: deterministic entanglement swapping gives the required singlet. Denote Bob's two local qubits by B1​,B2​. Known local unitary operations first convert the two given spin singlet states into ∣Φ+⟩AB1​​ and ∣Φ+⟩B2​C​. For example applying ZX to the second qubit of a singlet gives ∣Φ+⟩, where X,Z are Pauli operators.
Use the binary-labelled Bell states
∣Bpq​⟩=(I⊗XqZp)∣Φ+⟩,p,q∈{0,1}.
(7)
They are Φ+,Φ−,Ψ+,Ψ− for labels (0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1). Reordering registers to group Bob's qubits, direct expansion gives
∣Φ+⟩AB1​​∣Φ+⟩B2​C​=21​∑p,q=01​∣Bpq​⟩B1​B2​​∣Bpq​⟩AC​.
(8)
All four basis vectors are real in this convention; otherwise the paired expansion must account for complex conjugation. Bob performs the Bell-basis measurement on B1​B2​. This joint operation is local to him, so it is allowed by LOCC. Each outcome occurs with probability 1/4 and leaves AC in the corresponding Bell state.
Bob sends (p,q) to Charlie. Charlie applies ZpX−q to obtain ∣Φ+⟩AC​, and then XZ to obtain
∣Ψ−⟩AC​=2​∣01⟩−∣10⟩​.​
(9)
The correction works for every outcome, so no postselection or successful-outcome assumption is needed. This is entanglement swapping, equivalently teleportation of B1​ to C while preserving its entanglement with A. If Bob's measurement result is ignored, the average AC state is I4​/4; the protocol therefore does not supply entanglement usable without the classical outcome or violate the no-communication theorem.

Introduce the orthonormal local vectors ∣+⟩=(∣1⟩+∣2⟩)/2​ and ∣−⟩=(∣1⟩−∣2⟩)/2​. Reexpressing the second state's factors in this basis gives its Schmidt decomposition
∣ϕ2​⟩=3​1​∣+⟩A​∣+⟩B​+32​​∣−⟩A​∣−⟩B​.
(10)
The squared Schmidt coefficients are therefore 1/3,2/3, whereas those of the first state are 1/2,1/2.
To see directly why these distinguish the states, a local unitary operation UA​⊗UB​ changes the reduced density matrix to
ρA′​=TrB​[(UA​⊗UB​)ρAB​(UA†​⊗UB†​)]=UA​ρA​UA†​.
(11)
This is local-unitary invariance of reduced-state spectra: the unitary on the traced subsystem cancels, and conjugation on A leaves its eigenvalues unchanged. Here the reductions are
ρA(1)​=2I​,ρA(2)​=31​∣+⟩⟨+∣+32​∣−⟩⟨−∣=(1/2−1/6​−1/61/2​).
(12)
Their spectra differ. Hence no pair of local unitary operations carries the first state to the second. More generally, normalized bipartite pure states are locally unitarily equivalent exactly when their squared Schmidt coefficients agree, including multiplicities; sufficiency follows by mapping one pair of Schmidt bases to the other.

If the preparation label i is sampled with probability pi​ but is not supplied to the experimenter, the quantum state ensemble is represented by
ρ=i=1∑n​pi​∣ψi​⟩⟨ψi​∣,pi​≥0,i∑​pi​=1.​
(13)
For normalized states, this density operator is Hermitian, positive semidefinite and has trace one. The states in an ensemble need not be orthogonal or distinct. For any measurement in quantum mechanics effect E, averaging the Born rule over the preparation label gives
P(E)=∑i​pi​⟨ψi​∣E∣ψi​⟩=Tr(Eρ).
(14)
Thus the density operator summarizes all measurements performed on the prepared system alone. For an ensemble of mixed states ρi​, the same reasoning gives ρ=∑i​pi​ρi​.

Yes: distinct density operators have a projective measurement with different outcome probabilities. Let ρ,σ be the two density operators and let D=ρ−σ. It is a nonzero Hermitian operator with trace zero. Its nonzero eigenvalues cannot all have the same sign, since their sum is zero. Let P+​ be the orthogonal projection onto its positive-eigenvalue subspace. Then the two-outcome projective measurement {P+​,I−P+​} satisfies
Pρ​(+)−Pσ​(+)=Tr[P+​(ρ−σ)]=∑λj​(D)>0​λj​(D)>0.
(15)
The outcome therefore carries statistical information about which quantum ensemble was used.
For example, with equal prior probabilities, guess ρ after + and σ after the other outcome. The success probability is
Psucc​=21​Tr(P+​ρ)+21​Tr[(I−P+​)σ]=21​+21​Tr(P+​D).
(16)
Since the positive and negative eigenvalue sums of D have equal absolute value, Tr(P+​D)=∥D∥1​/2. Consequently
Psucc​=21​+41​∥ρ−σ∥1​>21​.​
(17)
This is the equal-prior measurement in the Holevo–Helstrom theorem, expressed using the trace distance. The conclusion is some information, not necessarily perfect one-shot discrimination: nonorthogonal density operators generally cannot be distinguished with certainty.

d OurBigBook.com
No measurement on the supplied system alone distinguishes ensembles with the same density operator. A completely general measurement in quantum mechanics has POVM effects Er​≥0 with ∑r​Er​=I. The outcome probability for a quantum ensemble is
P(r)=∑i​pi​⟨ψi​∣Er​∣ψi​⟩=Tr(Er​ρ).
(18)
If both ensembles have the same ρ, every outcome probability is identical. This proves the claim for all positive operator-valued measures, and includes projective measurements as a special case. An quantum ancilla in a fixed independent state does not help: the input density operators remain identical after tensoring with that ancilla, and the same argument applies to any joint measurement.
The premise concerns access only to the prepared state. An accessible preparation label or a correlated purification of a density operator is additional information outside that premise and can distinguish global preparations. Without such side information, different ensemble decompositions do not define distinguishable states.

Put s=sin2α and t=sin2β. Then 0<s<t<1/2, and the ordered squared Schmidt coefficients of the source and target are (1−s,s) and (1−t,t).
Nielsen's pure-state conversion theorem states that a deterministic exact conversion between bipartite pure states by LOCC exists precisely when the source's vector of squared Schmidt coefficients is majorized by the target's vector, with zero padding if needed. In the convention x≺y, majorization requires ∑j=1k​xj↓​≤∑j=1k​yj↓​ for every k, and equality of total sums. Here the first inequality would require 1−s≤1−t, but s<t gives the reverse strict inequality. Thus exact conversion with certainty is impossible.

An upper bound follows directly from the Schmidt-tail entanglement monotone
E2​(χ)=1−λmax​(ρAχ​),
(19)
where ρAχ​ is the reduced density matrix of a bipartite pure state. On these two-qubit states, this is the smaller squared Schmidt coefficient, so E2​(ψ)=s and E2​(ϕ)=t.
Here is the average-monotonicity argument needed for all LOCC strategies. The largest eigenvalue is convex: for any unit vector v, ⟨v∣∑r​pr​ρr​∣v⟩≤∑r​pr​λmax​(ρr​), and maximizing the left side gives the claim. Hence 1−λmax​ is concave. If Alice makes a local measurement, Bob's conditional reductions average to his original reduction. Concavity therefore gives ∑r​pr​E2​(χr​)≤E2​(χ). If Bob measures, use Alice's reductions instead. The two reductions of a pure state have the same nonzero eigenvalues by the Schmidt decomposition. Refining all measurement outcomes into individual Kraus operators keeps conditional states pure; iterating the inequality covers adaptive rounds of classical communication. The same bound persists for limits of such protocols.
If success produces the target with probability p, its contribution to the final average is pt. Failure branches have nonnegative E2​. Thus
pt≤s,pmax​≤ts​=sin2βsin2α​.​
(20)
This is a bound on arbitrary local protocols, not merely on a particular filtering measurement.

Alice makes a two-outcome local measurement in quantum mechanics with Kraus operators
Ks​=(tanα/tanβ0​01​),Kf​=(1−tan2α/tan2β​0​00​).
(21)
Since 0<tanα/tanβ<1, these are valid and satisfy Ks†​Ks​+Kf†​Kf​=I. Alice sends Bob the success or failure outcome. The successful unnormalized branch is
(Ks​⊗I)∣ψ⟩​=sinβsinαcosβ​∣00⟩+sinα∣11⟩=sinβsinα​∣ϕ⟩.​
(22)
Therefore the normalized success state is exactly the target and
psucc​=sin2βsin2α​=ts​=pmax​.​
(23)
Bob needs no quantum correction in this known Schmidt basis. The failure branch is proportional to ∣00⟩, a product state, and has probability 1−s/t. The successful branch saturates the previous bound: psucc​E2​(ϕ)=E2​(ψ) and failure contributes zero. This establishes optimal stochastic conversion of a two-qubit pure state.

Use the usual asymptotic convention: the output approaches the desired m(n)-copy pure target in trace distance, and any recorded failure probability tends to zero. Define the binary entropy
h2​(x)=−xlog2​x−(1−x)log2​(1−x).
(24)
The entanglement entropies per source and target are Eψ​=h2​(s) and Eϕ​=h2​(t). The asymptotic pure-state entanglement conversion rate is
Rmax​=h2​(sin2β)h2​(sin2α)​.​
(25)
Both achievability and the upper bound matter here.
For entanglement concentration, expand n sources in their correlated computational basis. Every pair of strings of Hamming weight k has the same amplitude (1−s)(n−k)/2sk/2. Alice measures only this weight, without resolving the individual string. Bob's string has the same weight automatically. Conditional on k, the normalized state is maximally entangled on Dk​=(kn​) correlated strings. Its outcome probability is (kn​)sk(1−s)n−k, a binomial distribution. By the weak law of large numbers, k/n→s in probability; Stirling's formula then gives n−1log2​Dk​→h2​(s). A uniform Schmidt vector on Dk​ entries is majorized by a uniform vector on 2⌊log2​Dk​⌋ entries padded with zeros. Thus Nielsen's pure-state conversion theorem converts the conditional state into that many Bell pairs exactly. For any fixed δ>0, one obtains at least n(Eψ​−δ) Bell pairs with probability tending to one.
For entanglement dilution, consider the Schmidt strings of m targets with probabilities qx​. The quantum typical subspace retains strings satisfying
2−m(Eϕ​+δ)≤qx​≤2−m(Eϕ​−δ).
(26)
The retained probability Qm​ tends to one by the weak law applied to the independent variables −log2​qxj​​. The number of retained strings is at most 2m(Eϕ​+δ), because their probabilities sum to at most one. Normalize the target after truncation to those strings. Its squared overlap with the full target is Qm​→1. A uniform Schmidt vector of dimension 2L, with L=⌈m(Eϕ​+δ)⌉, is majorized by the truncated target's Schmidt vector padded with zeros: the sum of its largest k entries is at least k/2L. Hence L Bell pairs prepare the truncated target exactly by LOCC. Its trace distance from the true target is 1−Qm​​→0. Combining concentration and dilution achieves any rate R<Eψ​/Eϕ​. Choosing margins tending sufficiently slowly to zero attains the ratio as a limiting rate.
For the converse, average monotonicity of pure-state entanglement entropy follows from Concavity of Von Neumann entropy: a measurement by one party leaves the other party's reduction unchanged on average, so its average conditional entropy cannot increase. Refine local measurement records, including local discarded systems, to obtain pure output branches {pr​,∣χr​⟩} on the m output qubits of each party. Iterating the concavity argument gives
∑r​pr​E(χr​)≤nEψ​.
(27)
Let ∣Φm​⟩=∣ϕ⟩⊗m and let the squared-overlap error of the average output ρout​ be ε=1−⟨Φm​∣ρout​∣Φm​⟩→0. Define dr​=1−∣⟨Φm​∣χr​⟩∣2​, the pure-state trace distance. Concavity of the square root gives ∑r​pr​dr​≤ε​. Partial trace cannot increase trace distance, so the reduced states are also within dr​. The continuity bound for quantum conditional entropy, with trivial conditioning and local dimension 2m, implies
∣E(χr​)−mEϕ​∣≤2mdr​+g(dr​),g(d)=(1+d)h2​(1+dd​)≤2.
(28)
Consequently
nEψ​≥mEϕ​−2mε​−2,
(29)
which excludes a limiting rate exceeding Eψ​/Eϕ​.
The vanishing-error convention is essential. If exact deterministic conversion were required for every finite block, majorization would also require the largest squared Schmidt coefficient to satisfy (1−s)n≤(1−t)m, hence
nm​≤−log(1−t)−log(1−s)​.
(30)
This can be strictly smaller than the entropy ratio: at s=0.1,t=0.2, the two bounds are approximately 0.4722 and 0.6496. Thus the boxed rate answers the standard asymptotic task, rather than imposing unmentioned exact zero-error conditions on every finite block.

Applying the optimal single-copy filter independently gives a random number Mn​ of exact successful targets with binomial distribution
Mn​∼Bin(n,s/t),EMn​=nts​.
(31)
The weak law of large numbers gives Mn​/n→s/t in probability. In contrast, collective entanglement concentration and entanglement dilution yield asymptotically nh2​(s)/h2​(t) targets with vanishing output error. The improvement is strict, not just nonnegative. Differentiating binary entropy gives
dxd​(xh2​(x)​)=x2log2​(1−x)​<0(0<x<1).
(32)
Since s<t, it follows that
h2​(t)h2​(s)​>ts​,m(n)∼nh2​(t)h2​(s)​>EMn​.​
(33)
This is collective advantage over independent two-qubit filtering. Independent filtering discards the entanglement in product-state failure branches; the collective reversible asymptotic procedure avoids that extensive loss. The separately filtered targets are exact conditional on success, whereas the collective statement uses the vanishing-error convention from the previous part. Here the counted outputs are target states ∣ϕ⟩; that is also the notation in the original printed question.

Diagonalize the density operator in the Hadamard gate basis ∣±⟩=(∣0⟩±∣1⟩)/2​. Its eigenvalues are 2/3,1/3, so
ρA​=32​∣+⟩⟨+∣+31​∣−⟩⟨−∣.
(34)
Choose orthonormal records on a second qubit to construct the first purification of a density operator:
∣Ω1​⟩=32​​∣+⟩A​∣0⟩B​+31​​∣−⟩A​∣1⟩B​.​
(35)
It is normalized. In its outer product the cross terms have partial trace zero because ⟨0∣1⟩B​=0, while the diagonal terms give precisely ρA​.
A different purification follows from unitary freedom of purification. Apply the Hadamard gate to B:
∣Ω2​⟩=(I⊗H)∣Ω1​⟩=32​​∣+⟩A​∣+⟩B​+31​​∣−⟩A​∣−⟩B​.​
(36)
The same partial-trace calculation uses ⟨+∣−⟩B​=0 and gives ρA​. The two global states are distinct, not related by a global phase, although their reduced density operators agree. Explicitly the second is
∣Ω2​⟩=a(∣00⟩+∣11⟩)+b(∣01⟩+∣10⟩),a=23​2​+1​,b=23​2​−1​.
(37)
Its reduction has diagonal entries a2+b2=1/2 and off-diagonal entries 2ab=1/6, checking all the required matrix entries.

Use the first copy to determine computational-basis parity and the second to determine phase parity. On the first pair, Alice and Bob each measure the Pauli operator Z and communicate their signs zA​,zB​. On the second, each measures X in the ∣+⟩,∣−⟩ basis and communicates the signs xA​,xB​.
Every Bell state is an eigenstate of both Z⊗Z and X⊗X. Direct application to its two basis terms gives the table
stateΦ+Φ−Ψ+Ψ−​zA​zB​+1+1−1−1​xA​xB​+1−1+1−1​​
(38)
For example, Z⊗Z acts positively on ∣00⟩,∣11⟩ and negatively on ∣01⟩,∣10⟩; X⊗X exchanges the two terms in each pair, detecting their relative sign. Thus individual outcomes are random, but the products in the table are certain. The Bell parity and phase observables label the state uniquely.
The two communicated parities identify all four states with probability one. Each measurement is local and all remaining operations are classical, so the protocol is LOCC discrimination of four Bell states using two copies. Alice can simply send her two signs to Bob, who combines them with his own. Measuring Z on the first copy destroys its phase information, but the second identical copy still supplies it; this is why the two-copy assumption enables this protocol.

Charlie measures his qubit in the X basis, ∣±⟩=(∣0⟩±∣1⟩)/2​. Rewriting the GHZ state in that basis gives
∣GHZ⟩ABC​=2​1​(∣Φ+⟩AB​∣+⟩C​+∣Φ−⟩AB​∣−⟩C​).
(39)
Each outcome occurs with probability 1/2. Charlie records c=0 for + and c=1 for − and sends that bit to Bob. Alice and Bob's conditional Bell state is (I⊗Zc)∣Φ+⟩. Bob applies XZc, with Zc applied first. Then
(I⊗XZc)(I⊗Zc)∣Φ+⟩=(I⊗X)∣Φ+⟩=∣Ψ+⟩AB​=2​∣01⟩+∣10⟩​.​
(40)
Thus every measurement branch gives the requested state after a known local correction: success is deterministic. All quantum operations act on only one party, and the only nonlocal resource used during the protocol is classical communication. This is localizing a Bell pair from a GHZ state by LOCC.
Ignoring Charlie's outcome instead gives Alice and Bob the average state 21​(∣Φ+⟩⟨Φ+∣+∣Φ−⟩⟨Φ−∣)=21​(∣00⟩⟨00∣+∣11⟩⟨11∣), which is separable. The classical record supplies the correction needed to use the conditional entanglement; no communication-free preparation of a known entangled state is being claimed.
