For each prime number p, reduction of the mapping cone is the mapping cone of the reduced chain map. Part (a), whose proof works over any coefficient ring, therefore gives
Hi​(M⊗Fp​)=0for every i,p.
(18)
Every Mi​ is a finitely generated free abelian group. Multiplication by p consequently gives a short exact sequence of chain complexes
0⟶Mp​M⟶M⊗Fp​⟶0.
(19)
The neighboring terms Hi+1​(M⊗Fp​) and Hi​(M⊗Fp​) in its long exact sequence in homology both vanish. Thus multiplication by p on Hi​(M) is an isomorphism for every prime number p.
The group Hi​(M) is a finitely generated abelian group, since it is a quotient of a subgroup of Mi​. Write it, using the Fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups, as
Hi​(M)≅Zbi​⊕Ti​,
(20)
with Ti​ finite. If bi​>0, multiplication by any prime number is not surjective. If Ti​=0, choose a prime number dividing its order; multiplication by that prime is not injective. Both contradict the preceding isomorphism. Hence Hi​(M)=0 for every i.
Applying part (a) integrally now proves the stronger natural conclusion
f∗​:Hi​(C;Z) ≅ ​Hi​(D;Z)for every i.​
(21)
This is prime coefficient detection of quasi-isomorphisms. The finite-generation hypothesis is used precisely when excluding nonzero groups on which every prime acts invertibly.

Hj​(Y;Z)=⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z4,Z6,0,​j=0,8,j=2,6,j=4,otherwise.​​
(22)
Let ui​∈H2(Y;Z) be the pullback of the positively normalized cohomology class from factor i. The cohomology ring is
H∗(Y;Z)=Z[u1​,u2​,u3​,u4​]/(u12​,u22​,u32​,u42​),∣ui​∣=2,
(23)
with ⟨u1​u2​u3​u4​,[Y]⟩=1. Its generators commute by graded commutativity of the cup product.
Fix a point in each sphere. For each pair i<j, let Sij​ be the four-dimensional submanifold in which factors i,j vary and the remaining factors equal their fixed points. Orient Sij​ by the ordered factors i,j. The six homology classes
[S12​], [S13​], [S14​], [S23​], [S24​], [S34​]
(24)
give a coordinate sphere intersection basis by the homology cross product description in the Künneth theorem. Moving a fixed point in one of the other two factors produces a disjoint homologous representative of each Sij​. Hence every basis element has zero self-intersection number.
More precisely, the intersection pairing is
[Sij​]⋅[Skl​]={1,0,​{i,j}⊔{k,l}={1,2,3,4},otherwise.​
(25)
For complementary pairs the representatives intersect transversely at one point. The sign is positive because swapping two-dimensional factors contributes (−1)2⋅2=1. For pairs with a common index, a factor fixed in both representatives can be displaced to make them disjoint. In the paired order (12,34),(13,24),(14,23), the intersection form is three copies of (01​10​).
For the diagonal map δ:X→Y, write a,b∈H2(X;Z) for the two sphere generators. Then
δ∗u1​=δ∗u3​=a,δ∗u2​=δ∗u4​=b,a2=b2=0,⟨ab,[X]⟩=1.

Work with coefficients F2​. For n≥1, the mod-two cohomology ring of real projective space and the Künneth theorem give
H∗(RP2n;F2​)=F2​[z]/(z2n+1),H∗(RPn×RPn;F2​)=F2​[x,y]/(xn+1,yn+1),
(29)
where all three generators have degree one. Any induced map on cohomology has f∗z=αx+βy, with α,β∈F2​. By naturality of the cup product, the only possible term in top degree is
f∗(z2n)=(αx+βy)2n=(n2n​)αnβnxnyn.
(30)
All other terms vanish because their exponent of x or y exceeds n. But
(n2n​)=2(n−12n−1​)
(31)
is even for every n≥1. Thus the displayed top-degree induced map on cohomology is zero for every f.
Both top-degree homology groups are one-dimensional over F2​, and their dual cohomology groups pair with them nondegenerately. Naturality of this evaluation makes f∗​ dual to f∗, so f∗​ is also zero and cannot be an isomorphism. This is the mod-two degree obstruction for equal projective factors. Consequently
There is no such map for any positive n.​
(32)
If zero is included among the allowed values, RP0 is a point and the unique map is an isomorphism on H0​. In that convention, the only value is n=0.

Use the standard orientations on the three spheres and the complex orientation on Complex projective space. Let h∈H2(CP3;Z) satisfy ⟨h3,[CP3]⟩=1. The cohomology ring of complex projective space is Z[h]/(h4). The Künneth theorem gives
H∗((S2)3;Z)=Z[a,b,c]/(a2,b2,c2),∣a∣=∣b∣=∣c∣=2,⟨abc,[(S2)3]⟩=1.
(33)
Write the induced map on cohomology as f∗h=pa+qb+rc with integers p,q,r. Naturality and graded commutativity of the cup product give
f∗(h3)=(pa+qb+rc)3=6pqrabc.
(34)
The mapping degree is therefore d=6pqr. This is the factorial degree obstruction for products of two-spheres. Every positive value is a positive multiple of six, so the least possible positive degree is at least 6.
To attain it, identify each oriented sphere with CP1. Send three projective linear forms to the coefficients of their product:
F:(CP1)3⟶CP3,([a1​:b1​],[a2​:b2​],[a3​:b3​])⟼[c0​:c1​:c2​:c3​],
(35)
where
∏i=13​(ai​z+bi​w)=c0​z3+c1​z2w+c2​zw2+c3​w3.
(36)
No product of nonzero linear forms is the zero polynomial. Rescaling any factor rescales all coefficients by the same nonzero scalar. The map is therefore well-defined and is a holomorphic map on the products of projective charts.
Choose a homogeneous cubic with three distinct roots, all in an affine chart. Its preimages are exactly the 3!=6 orderings of its three projective linear factors. In root coordinates, the derivative of the elementary-symmetric coefficient map has determinant equal, up to sign, to the Vandermonde determinant, the product of the three pairwise root differences. This is nonzero for distinct roots, so all six preimages are regular. A nonsingular complex-linear derivative preserves the underlying real orientation, since its real determinant is the squared absolute value of its complex determinant. Each preimage consequently contributes +1 to the mapping degree. Hence
degF=6,dmin​=6.​
(37)
This polynomial multiplication map to complex projective space provides the requested example without assuming that a prescribed cohomology homomorphism is realizable by a continuous map.

Let D+n​ and D−n​ be the two closed hemispheres of the sphere. Use γ as the clutching function between the trivial bundles on these hemispheres. The clutching construction defines
Vγ​=((D+n​×Rk)⊔(D−n​×Rk))/∼,(u,v)+​∼(u,γ(u)v)−​,u∈Sn−1.
(38)
The projection to Sn is locally a vector bundle. In a collar of the equator, extend the transition function by keeping it constant in the normal coordinate; this gives explicit vector bundle trivializations across the seam. Since the transition matrices lie in the special orthogonal group, the standard fiber orientations and Euclidean inner products agree on overlaps. Thus this is an oriented rank-k vector bundle with a fiber metric.
For k≥2, choose a base point u0​ on the equator. A constant change of the negative hemisphere frame replaces γ by γ(u0​)−1γ, so we may suppose γ(u0​)=I. With ek​ the last standard unit vector, define the section obstruction for a clutched vector bundle
fγ​=[u⟼γ(u)ek​]∈πn−1​(Sk−1);r=n−1, s=k−1.​
(39)
The crucial point is that a nowhere-zero section can be normalized using the fiber metric. It is then represented by continuous maps σ±​:D±n​→Sk−1 satisfying
σ−​(u)=γ(u)σ+​(u)
(40)
on the equator.
If fγ​=0, the extension-null-homotopy criterion for a sphere extends u↦γ(u)ek​ to D−n​. Take this extension as σ−​ and the constant map ek​ as σ+​. They glue to a nowhere-zero section.
Conversely, suppose such a section exists. Restricting a contraction of D+n​ shows that σ+​∣Sn−1​ is homotopic to a constant. The target sphere is path connected, so the constant can be moved to ek​. Multiplying this homotopy pointwise by γ(u) gives a homotopy from γσ+​ to γek​. The first of these maps equals σ−​∣Sn−1​ and extends over D−n​, hence is null-homotopic. So γek​ is null-homotopic too. A freely null-homotopic based sphere map represents zero in its homotopy group; any change of base point sends the zero class to zero. We have proved both directions:
fγ​=0⟺Vγ​ has a nowhere-zero section.​
(41)
For n=1, the same argument reads as the extension criterion for a two-point boundary and uses the trivial set π0​(Sk−1) when k≥2. For k=1, the special orthogonal group SO(1) is trivial, so the vector bundle is already a trivial vector bundle; the corresponding constant map to S0 has zero obstruction. These cases require no higher homotopy group calculation.
If k>n, then n−1<k−1 and the permitted vanishing of lower homotopy groups of a sphere makes fγ​=0. Normalize the resulting nowhere-zero section to a unit section σ, and set
Vx′​={v∈(Vγ​)x​:⟨v,σ(x)⟩=0}.
(42)
Local orthonormal frames show that these orthogonal complements form a rank-(k−1) vector bundle. The explicit vector bundle isomorphism
V′⊕(Sn×R)⟶Vγ​,(x,v,t)⟼v+tσ(x)
(43)
has inverse w↦(w−⟨w,σ⟩σ,⟨w,σ⟩). Orient V′ so that a positive frame followed by σ is positive in Vγ​. This is splitting a trivial line from a nowhere-zero section and proves the oriented splitting
Vγ​≅V′⊕R(k>n).​
(44)
Finally consider the special orthogonal sphere fibration
SO(s)⟶SO(s+1)p​Ss,p(A)=Aes+1​.
(45)
The fiber over es+1​ consists exactly of diag(B,1) with B∈SO(s). Near any unit vector, the Gram-Schmidt process continuously completes it to an oriented orthonormal frame, giving local sections of p and local product fiber bundle charts. In particular p has the homotopy lifting property.
Let α:Sr→SO(s+1) be based and suppose r<s. The composite pα is a based null-homotopic map by the given vanishing of πr​(Ss). Lift a based null-homotopy of pα, beginning at α, to a homotopy G:Sr×[0,1]→SO(s+1). The lift might initially move its base point u0​, but G(u0​,t) always fixes es+1​. Right multiplication by G(u0​,t)−1 corrects this: the homotopy
G(u,t)=G(u,t)G(u0​,t)−1
(46)
still covers the same sphere homotopy, remains based, and starts at α. At its endpoint every matrix fixes es+1​, so G(−,1) lies in the included SO(s). Thus every homotopy class in the target is represented by one from SO(s):
πr​(SO(s))⟶πr​(SO(s+1)) is surjective for r<s.​
(47)
This proves surjectivity of special orthogonal stabilization below the sphere dimension. For r=0, this is the corresponding assertion about path components; the positive-rank special orthogonal groups are connected. The lifting argument also explains the zero term πr​(Ss) in the long exact sequence of homotopy groups of a fibration.
