Use the optimization Lagrangian with the sensitivity sign convention
Lb​(x,λ)=f(x)−λT(h(x)−b),λ∈Rm.​
(1)
For equality constraints the Lagrange multipliers have unrestricted signs. The Lagrangian sufficiency theorem says that if h(x∗)=b and, for some λ∗, x∗ globally minimizes Lb​(⋅,λ∗) over X, then x∗ globally minimizes f subject to the constraint. Indeed every feasible x satisfies
f(x)=Lb​(x,λ∗)≥Lb​(x∗,λ∗)=f(x∗).
(2)
This proves the theorem without a convexity or differentiability assumption. Merely finding a stationary point of the optimization Lagrangian would not establish its required global minimum.
Let ϕ(u)=inf{f(x):x∈X,h(x)=u}, with value +∞ when the feasible set is empty, and suppose ϕ(b) is finite. The problem has the Strong Lagrangian property if a finite multiplier λ satisfies
x∈Xinf​Lb​(x,λ)=ϕ(b).​
(3)
This includes attainment of the dual bound by a multiplier. It does not by itself assert attainment of the primal infimum. For every multiplier, restricting the infimum to feasible x gives infX​Lb​(x,λ)≤ϕ(b), the relevant weak duality. A non-vertical supporting hyperplane of a value function is a plane
r=ϕ(b)+λT(u−b)
(4)
with ϕ(u)≥ϕ(b)+λT(u−b) for every u. Its vertical coefficient is nonzero and has been normalized to one; the epigraph lies above it. The two directions of the equivalence are proved in parts (a) and (b).
For the sampling allocation put A=∑i​ai​vi​​. Finite variance requires every xi​>0; interpret the objective at a zero coordinate as +∞. An optimum uses all the budget, because scaling every positive xi​ up would otherwise reduce the objective while remaining feasible. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives the global bound
A2=(∑i​vi​/xi​​ai​xi​​)2≤(∑i​xi​vi​​)(∑i​ai​xi​)≤b∑i​xi​vi​​.
(5)
Equality requires vi​/xi​ to be proportional to ai​xi​. The budget then determines the unique allocation:
xi∗​(b)=Ab​ai​vi​​​,ϕ(b)=bA2​.​
(6)
This is the optimal cost-constrained stratified sampling allocation.
For an inequality ∑i​ai​xi​≤b, our sign convention uses λ≤0, so Lb​=f−λ(∑i​ai​xi​−b) is a lower bound on f at feasible points. Its coordinate stationary equations are −vi​/xi2​−λai​=0, and the displayed allocation gives
λ∗(b)=−b2A2​.​
(7)
The function vi​/xi​+(−λ∗)ai​xi​ has a strict global minimum at xi∗​: its second derivative is 2vi​/xi3​>0 and its value diverges at both endpoints of (0,∞). Thus the multiplier also supplies the Lagrangian sufficiency theorem certificate, with the tight inequality giving complementary slackness.
Finally, for b+δb>0,

(8)
Hence the first-order change in minimal variance is λ∗δb​. Increased resources decrease the variance. With the alternative convention L=f+μ(∑i​ai​xi​−b), the multiplier is μ=−λ∗>0 and the sensitivity is −μδb.

If the Strong Lagrangian property holds with multiplier λ, its infimum identity implies, for every x∈X,
f(x)−λT(h(x)−b)≥ϕ(b).

For a finite directed flow network with source s, sink t and nonnegative finite flow network edge capacities ce​, the max-flow min-cut theorem states
f feasiblemax​∣f∣=S:s∈S, t∈/Smin​c(S,Sc).​
(27)
Here feasible arc flows satisfy 0≤fe​≤ce​ and flow conservation at every vertex except s,t. Their strength of a flow is the net outflow at s. A cut of a flow network has cut capacity equal to the sum of capacities on arcs directed from S to Sc.
For any feasible flow and any such cut of a flow network, summing flow conservation over S cancels internal arcs and gives
∣f∣=∑u∈S,v∈/S​fuv​−∑u∈/S,v∈S​fuv​≤∑u∈S,v∈/S​cuv​=c(S,Sc).
(28)
Thus every flow value is bounded by every cut capacity.
A maximum flow exists: the feasible arc-flow vectors form a nonempty closed subset of the finite-dimensional compact box ∏e​[0,ce​], and flow value is continuous. Choose one, f∗. Its residual network has a forward arc of capacity ce​−fe∗​ and a reverse arc of capacity fe∗​ for each original arc. If a residual s–t augmenting path existed, its smallest positive residual capacity would permit increasing forward flows and decreasing reverse flows along the path. This preserves all capacity bounds and internal flow conservation while strictly increasing ∣f∗∣, a contradiction.
Let S be the vertices reachable from s through positive residual arcs. Then t∈/S. Every original arc leaving S is saturated, since otherwise its endpoint would also be reachable. Every original arc entering S carries zero flow, since a positive flow would create a residual reverse arc leaving S. Therefore
∣f∗∣=∑u∈S,v∈/S​cuv​=c(S,Sc).
(29)
Together with the universal upper bound, this proves both optimality claims and the theorem. This existence argument works for real capacities; it does not assume that arbitrary choices in the Ford-Fulkerson algorithm terminate. For integer capacities, positive augmentations are integral and increase the flow value by at least one, so the algorithm does terminate.

tk​∈{0,1},j=1∑Mi​​Lxij​​(t)≥1(1≤i≤N),​
(42)
with objective zero. Every feasible binary vector gives a satisfying assignment of the Boolean satisfiability problem, and conversely. An empty clause gives 0≥1 and hence infeasibility.
For maximum satisfiability, introduce binary variables qi​ marking clauses selected as satisfied and solve
maxi=1∑N​qi​,tk​,qi​∈{0,1},qi​≤j=1∑Mi​​Lxij​​(t)(1≤i≤N).​
(43)
A false clause forces qi​=0, while a true clause permits qi​=1. At an optimum every permitted qi​ is one, since increasing it improves the objective without affecting another constraint. Therefore the objective is exactly the maximum number of simultaneously true clauses. Repeated literals cause no difficulty, and a clause containing both signs of a variable is always true.
For the literal-frequency greedy approximation for MAX-SAT, keep only clauses not already satisfied and remove literals made false. Count occurrence in clauses, rather than multiplicity within a clause. At a step let a be the number of current clauses containing the chosen literal z, and b the number containing its opposite z. Since z has maximum clause frequency among all remaining literals, b≤a.
Setting z true permanently satisfies exactly the a clauses containing it. Some other clauses may become empty and are then permanently lost. Every newly empty clause previously contained z, so their number d satisfies
d≤b≤a.
(44)
A clause containing both signs is satisfied and cannot be one of the lost clauses. Repeat this accounting at every step. Satisfied clauses are removed, so no success is counted twice; each lost nonempty original clause becomes empty at exactly one step. If G is the total number satisfied and D the number of initially nonempty clauses lost, summing the inequalities gives D≤G. Every initially nonempty clause eventually falls into one of these two classes. Writing their number as N+​, we get
G+D=N+​,G≥N+​/2.
(45)
Even an optimal assignment can satisfy at most N+​ clauses. Consequently
G≥21​N+​≥21​OPT.​
(46)
Thus Greedy is a 1/2-approximation in the convention stated in the question. The proof also covers initial empty clauses by excluding them from N+​; they are unsatisfiable for every algorithm.

A coalitional game with transferable utility has a finite player set N and a characteristic function of a coalitional game v:2N→R, normalized by v(∅)=0. The value v(S) is the total payoff the coalition S can secure and redistribute among its members.
An imputation is an efficient, individually rational payoff vector:
i∈N∑​xi​=v(N),xi​≥v({i})(i∈N).​
(47)
The core of a cooperative game consists of imputations satisfying every coalition constraint:
C(v)={x:i∈N∑​xi​=v(N),i∈S∑​xi​≥v(S) for every S⊆N}.​
(48)
A coalition assigned less than its characteristic value can leave and make every member better off, by sharing the positive surplus. Conversely a coalition with no such surplus cannot improve every member's payoff simultaneously. This explains the stability represented by the core of a cooperative game.

(r−1n−1​)q≥(rn​),nrq​≥1.

The payoff e(x,z) is the expected fitness of an individual using strategy x against a population using strategy z. The resident x∗ is initially common; the mutant y is introduced at small frequency ϵ. Convexity of the strategy set makes their population mixture admissible. If residents have higher expected fitness in every sufficiently small invasion by a fixed mutant, selection works against that mutant: this is an evolutionarily stable strategy. The allowed upper bound on ϵ may depend on y.
For expected payoffs of mixed strategies, e(x,z) is affine in the population strategy z; in a finite matrix game it is xTAz. This property is the basis of the two-condition criterion for evolutionary stability. For fixed y=x∗, put
Ay​=e(x∗,x∗)−e(y,x∗),By​=e(x∗,y)−e(y,y).
(55)
Affineness gives the exact invasion difference
e(x∗,(1−ϵ)x∗+ϵy)−e(y,(1−ϵ)x∗+ϵy)=(1−ϵ)Ay​+ϵBy​.
(56)
If this is positive for all sufficiently small ϵ>0, taking ϵ↓0 gives Ay​≥0. If Ay​=0, the difference is ϵBy​, so necessarily By​>0. Conversely, if Ay​>0, it remains positive for sufficiently small ϵ: for example take ϵ<Ay​/(Ay​+∣By​∣). If Ay​=0 and By​>0, it is positive for every ϵ>0. Applying this argument separately to every y proves
x∗ is ESS⟺∀y=x∗,Ay​≥0 and (Ay​=0⇒By​>0).​
(57)
Thus an evolutionarily stable strategy is a symmetric Nash equilibrium with an additional condition against tied mutants. For a general nonlinear interaction function on a convex set, affineness would be an additional necessary hypothesis for this particular equivalence; here it follows from the mixed-strategy expected-payoff interpretation. For example, on X=[0,1] take e(x,z)=xz(x/2−z) and resident x∗=0. For every y>0, Ay​=0 and By​=y3/2>0, but at population ϵy the mutant earns ϵy3(1/2−ϵ)>0 while the resident earns zero. Thus the two conditions alone would not suffice for an arbitrary nonlinear e; the expected-payoff assumption is used explicitly.

We use this formula in each case. In the usual interpretation, resource value V and damage cost D are positive. The parts below also specify the boundary or negative-value alternatives because positivity is not written explicitly in the PDF.

If the equal parameters are negative, pure Dove is instead an evolutionarily stable strategy: for a mutant p>0, e(0,0)−e(p,0)=−pV/2>0. If V=D=0, every payoff is zero and no resident has a strict advantage over any mutant. Thus the full equal-parameter answer is Hawk for V=D>0, Dove for V=D<0, and no ESS for V=D=0.

In the usual positive-parameter case 0<V<D, set p∗=V/D∈(0,1). Every mutant ties the resident against the resident population, because V−Dp∗=0. Against a mutant population, however,
e(p∗,p)−e(p,p)=2(p∗−p)(V−Dp)​=2D​(p∗−p)2>0(p=p∗).
(63)
The second condition of the two-condition criterion for evolutionary stability holds, so
x∗=(DV​,1−DV​) is an ESS for 0<V<D.​
(64)
Indeed its advantage at invasion frequency ϵ is exactly ϵD(p∗−p)2/2. The interior frequency balances the two pure-strategy payoffs in the Hawk-Dove game.
For completeness, if V<D but V<0, pure Dove is an evolutionarily stable strategy by the strict inequality e(0,0)−e(p,0)=−pV/2>0 for every mutant p>0. If V=0<D, that difference is zero, but e(0,p)−e(p,p)=Dp2/2>0, so Dove again satisfies the second condition. Thus for the remaining allowed values x∗=(0,1) when V≤0<D or V<D≤0​; equivalently, Dove works whenever V<D and V≤0.
