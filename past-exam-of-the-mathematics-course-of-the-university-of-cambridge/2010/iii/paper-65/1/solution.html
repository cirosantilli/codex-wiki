Take the boundary to be horizontal, put z=0 on it, and let z=h(x,t) be the interface. Write Δρ=ρ−ρ2​>0. Under the long-thin hydrostatic approximation, the deep ambient fluid has pressure p2​=p∗​−ρ2​gz to leading order, with negligible horizontal pressure gradient. Matching pressure at the interface gives, inside the dense porous gravity current,
p=p∗​−ρgz+Δρgh(x,t),px​=Δρghx​.
(1)
The Darcy velocity is superficial volume flux per total area, not pore speed. The horizontal Darcy law and depth-integrated flux therefore give
uD​=−μkΔρg​hx​,q=∫0h​uD​dz=−μkΔρg​hhx​.
(2)
The porosity ϕ multiplies storage, so mass conservation is ϕht​+qx​=0. Consequently
ht​=λ(hhx​)x​,λ=ϕμkΔρg​.​
(3)
The deep-ambient and long-thin assumptions are what permit neglecting the ambient return-flow pressure variation, vertical inertia, and nonhydrostatic pressure corrections.
For a localized finite release, first use the reflecting half-line x≥0, so hx​(0,t)=0. Let S=V/ϕ be the conserved cross-sectional area of the current; V here is actual pore-fluid volume per unit cross-flow distance. A similarity solution of the porous medium equation has thickness proportional to t−α and extent proportional to tβ. Conservation gives α=β, and balancing ht​ against (hhx​)x​ gives α+2β=1. Thus α=β=1/3. Put
L∗​=(λSt)1/3,h=L∗​S​F(ξ),ξ=L∗​x​.
(4)
Substitution gives −(F+ξF′)/3=(FF′)′. Zero flux at ξ=0 allows one integration: FF′=−ξF/3. In the positive-current region, F′=−ξ/3, so F=(ξf2​−ξ2)/6. The normalization ∫0ξf​​Fdξ=1 gives ξf3​=9. The one-sided constant-volume porous gravity current is therefore
h(x,t)=6λt[L(t)2−x2]+​​,L(t)=(ϕ9λVt​)1/3.​
(5)
Here [a]+​=max(a,0). Its Darcy flux vanishes at the moving front because h=0 there, and its physical volume is ϕ∫0L​hdx=V. This is the exact concentrated-release Barenblatt solution of the depth equation, physically used after the short initial adjustment to a long-thin current; an arbitrary finite initial patch requires its initial profile as well as its volume, although the same profile describes the usual late spreading regime. If a line release spreads symmetrically in both directions and V denotes the total volume, use the same parabola on −L<x<L with L3=9λVt/(2ϕ). If instead volume has already been divided by porosity, replace V/ϕ by that geometric volume.
Drainage at superficial rate ϕΓh changes mass conservation to ϕht​+qx​=−ϕΓh, so
ht​=λ(hhx​)x​−Γh.​
(6)
For constant Γ>0, use the exponential drainage transform for porous-medium diffusion
h=e−ΓtH(x,τ),τ(t)=Γ1−e−Γt​.
(7)
Indeed, ht​+Γh=e−Γtτ′Hτ​ and (hhx​)x​=e−2Γt(HHx​)x​. Choosing τ′=e−Γt leaves Hτ​=λ(HHx​)x​. Substituting the undrained Barenblatt solution gives
h(x,t)=e−Γt6λτ(t)[Ld​(t)2−x2]+​​,Ld​(t)=(ϕ9λVτ(t)​)1/3.​
(8)
Use the factor 1/2 in Ld3​ for a total-volume symmetric release, as above. For general initial data the same transform is exact, but H is the corresponding undrained solution rather than automatically a source parabola.
The remaining physical volume is V(t)=Ve−Γt. Early times have τ∼t, recovering undrained spreading. Late times have τ→1/Γ, so the front approaches a finite runout while the current drains exponentially:
Ld​⟶(ϕΓ9λV​)1/3,V(t)=Ve−Γt.​
(9)
The front slows because drainage reduces the hydrostatic driving head. The finite transformed time also explains why drainage does not guarantee that an arbitrary initial profile reaches its undrained universal large-time shape.
