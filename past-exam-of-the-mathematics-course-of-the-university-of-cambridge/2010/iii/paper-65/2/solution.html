Write θ=T−Te​ and use the Boussinesq approximation for air, ρe​−ρ≃ρθ/Te​. A uniformly heated, well-mixed room has the buoyancy pressure difference Δpb​=ρgHθ/Te​ between its openings. Let Co​ denote the combined hydraulic coefficient of the two equal openings, so the effective opening area for pressure-driven ventilation gives
V=Co​ATe​gHθ​​.
(10)
For example, if each orifice has conventional discharge coefficient Cd​ in V=Cd​A2Δp/ρ​, the two pressure losses add and Co​=Cd​. The question's coefficient is the prefactor in the final heat-load law; define c=(2Co2​)1/3. This explicitly states the numerical loss-coefficient convention. Steady heat balance is Q=ρCp​Vθ. Eliminating θ gives
V0​=cA2/3(2ρCp​Te​gHQ​)1/3,θ0​=ρCp​V0​Q​.​
(11)
Here Q is total heating power and Cp​ is specific heat capacity at constant pressure. Writing c=Cd​ without this conversion would incorrectly change the power of the discharge coefficient.
After the reduction, cooler newly heated air occupies the bottom and displaces the old warmer air upward. Use the ideal two-layer displacement ventilation model: each layer is well mixed, the upper layer remains at θ0​, walls have negligible heat capacity, and the sharp interface is advected without exchange between the layers. Let the lower layer have depth h and excess temperature θ. Integrating the hydrostatic buoyancy over the full height gives
Δpb​=Te​ρg​{hθ+(H−h)θ0​},V=Co​A[Te​g​{hθ+(H−h)θ0​}]1/2.​
(12)
The room's excess thermal energy is ET​=ρCp​Ar​[hθ+(H−h)θ0​]. The floor supplies λQ, incoming air has zero excess temperature, and the ceiling removes ρCp​Vθ0​. Thus the required heat balance is
ρCp​Ar​dtd​[h(θ−θ0​)]=−ρCp​Vθ0​+λQ.​
(13)
This moving-interface energy term must include the loss of old upper-layer volume; balancing only the lower-layer temperature would miss it.
For a genuine reduction assume 0<λ<1. Set
s=Ar​HV0​t​,η=Hh​,x=η(1−θ0​θ​).
(14)
The integrated buoyancy factor is Hθ0​(1−x), so V/V0​=1−x​. Since Q=ρCp​V0​θ0​, the thermal balance becomes the ventilation after a heating reduction equation
dsdx​=1−x​−λ,x(0)=0.​
(15)
The material interface also moves with the upward displacement velocity, Ar​h˙=V. Therefore ηs​=1−x​, and subtracting the two equations gives
η=x+λs,ηθ0​θ​=λs.​
(16)
The second relation is also the lower-layer energy balance: its total excess energy increases at rate λQ. It does not assume its temperature stays constant. Since xs​>0 until x=1−λ2, the solution remains 0≤x<1−λ2, and V/V0​≥λ. Hence ηs​≥λ, so the lower layer reaches η=1 in finite time sH​≤1/λ. Also ηs​≤1, so sH​≥1.
An explicit implicit solution is useful. Put y=1−x​, which decreases from 1 toward λ. Separation gives
s=2(1−y)+2λlogy−λ1−λ​.
(17)
Filling occurs when 1=1−yH2​+λsH​, so λsH​=yH2​ and θH​/θ0​=yH2​. These relations determine the exit time and the continuous temperature at that time. For complete switch-off λ=0, the limiting solution is y=1−s/2, x=η=s−s2/4 for 0≤s≤2, so the cold lower layer fills the room at sH​=2 even though its final ventilation rate is zero.
After the interface exits, the whole room is well mixed. For z=θ/θ0​, the natural ventilation rate is V=V0​z​, and the well-mixed ventilation temperature balance becomes
dsdz​=λ−z3/2,z(sH​)=yH2​.​
(18)
Equivalently, ρCp​Ar​Hθ˙=λQ−ρCp​Co​AgH/Te​​θ3/2. The heat-removal term is strictly increasing in θ≥0, so every nonnegative solution tends to the unique stable equilibrium
T∞​=Te​+λ2/3ΔT0​,V∞​=λ1/3V0​.​
(19)
For λ=0 the final temperature is Te​. The unchanged-load case λ=1 has no distinct new temperature layer and is outside the reduction scenario.
