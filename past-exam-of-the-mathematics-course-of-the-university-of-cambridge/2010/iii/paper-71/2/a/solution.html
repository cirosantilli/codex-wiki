Take U0​>0, k=2π/L>0, and represent the stationary topographic internal gravity wave by phase kx+mz. Its laboratory angular frequency is zero, and its intrinsic frequency is ω=−kU0​. The internal gravity wave dispersion relation gives
m2=U02​N02​​−k2.
(8)
The radiation condition selects m>0, since this makes the vertical group velocity upward. The lower kinematic boundary condition is w=U0​hx​, so a corresponding linear wave field is
w=kU0​h0​cos(kx+mz),ζ=h0​sin(kx+mz),
(9)
where ζ is the vertical fluid displacement. The constant-phase lines of an internal gravity wave slope down to the right, with dz/dx=−k/m.
Let K2=k2+m2. In the frame fixed to the hills the phase pattern is stationary: its phase velocity is zero, while the group velocity is
cg​=K2U0​​(k2,km).​
(10)
Energy travels up and downwind, with ray slope dz/dx=m/k. In coordinates x′=x−U0​t moving with the wind, the phase is kx′+mz+kU0​t. Thus
cp′​=−K2kU0​​(k,m),cg′​=K2U0​​(−m2,km).​
(11)
The intrinsic phase velocity is upstream and downward; the intrinsic group velocity is upstream and upward, perpendicular to the wave vector. Adding (U0​,0) to the group velocity recovers the laboratory energy ray.
Vertically propagating waves require kU0​<N0​, equivalently L>2πU0​/N0​​. At equality the vertical group velocity vanishes; above the mountain-wave cutoff the bounded response is an evanescent wave. The linearization requires small terrain slope kh0​≪1 and small wave-induced displacement gradients ∣m∣h0​≪1, often summarized by N0​h0​/U0​≪1. The model also neglects rotation, viscosity and compressibility; its scales must permit those approximations.
