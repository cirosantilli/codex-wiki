The gravitational variable and the fields held fixed in a variation must be specified. For bosonic matter a second-order metric tensor formulation is sufficient. For spinorial matter a vierbein and a spin connection are needed to define the local Lorentz representation and its covariant derivative. “Second order” refers to a connection already expressed through derivatives of the vierbein; “first order” treats that connection as an independent variable.
For a metric tensor, a scalar field and an Abelian gauge field, consider the Einstein-Hilbert action with matter,
S=∫d4xe[2κ2R−2Λ​−21​gμν∂μ​ϕ∂ν​ϕ−V(ϕ)−41​Fμν​Fμν],F=dA.
(41)
Here e=−g​ and the connection in R is Levi-Civita. The metric tensor variation uses δe=−egμν​δgμν/2 and
δR=Rμν​δgμν+gμνδRμν​,δRμν​=∇ρ​δΓρμν​−∇ν​δΓρμρ​.
(42)
Metric compatibility makes the last two terms a divergence. Thus
δS=21​∫e[κ−2(Gμν​+Λgμν​)−Tμν​]δgμν+matter variations+boundary,Tμν​=−e2​δgμνδSmatter​​.
(43)
Stationarity for arbitrary compactly supported variations gives
Gμν​+Λgμν​=κ2Tμν​.​
(44)
Explicitly,
Tμνϕ​=∂μ​ϕ∂ν​ϕ−gμν​[21​(∂ϕ)2+V],TμνA​=Fμρ​Fν​ρ−41​gμν​F2.
(45)
Scalar field and gauge-potential variations give ∇2ϕ−V′=0 and ∇μ​Fμν=0. Their equations imply stress-energy conservation, consistently with the contracted Bianchi identity. If the boundary metric tensor is fixed instead of using compactly supported variations, add the Gibbons–Hawking–York boundary term, κ−2∫∂M​ε∂M​∣h∣​K, with the outward-normal orientation. Fixing the metric tensor alone does not otherwise remove the normal derivatives of its variation.
For spinorial matter, write gμν​=eμ​aeν​bηab​ and ∇μ​χ=∂μ​χ+ωμab​γabχ/4. In the second-order theory set ω=ω(e) before varying. A symmetrized Dirac action, in the same no-i, mostly-plus convention as the earlier solutions, is
Sχ​=∫e[−21​(χˉ​γμ∇μ​χ−(∇μ​χˉ​)γμχ)−mχˉ​χ].
(46)
Varying χˉ​ and integrating by parts gives (∇+m)χ=0; varying χ gives the adjoint equation. The spinor components held fixed in a gravitational variation are components in the Lorentz frame. Both γμ=ea​μγa and ω(e) vary with the vierbein. The former gives the canonical stress, and integrating the spin-connection variation supplies its spin-current improvement. On the Dirac equations the resulting symmetric stress tensor is
Tμνχ​=41​[χˉ​γμ​∇ν​χ+χˉ​γν​∇μ​χ],χˉ​γμ​∇ν​χ=χˉ​γμ​∇ν​χ−(∇ν​χˉ​)γμ​χ.
(47)
The off-shell expression also contains gμν​Lχ​/e; this vanishes on the matter equations for the displayed symmetrized action. Treating the coordinate gamma matrices as fixed while varying the metric tensor would miss the displayed stress. Local Lorentz invariance removes the independent antisymmetric vierbein equation, leaving the symmetric Einstein field equations with this matter source.
In a first-order vierbein formulation, ea and an antisymmetric Lorentz connection ωab are independent. Define
Ta=dea+ωab​∧eb,Rab=dωab+ωac​∧ωcb,
(48)
and use R(e,ω)=ea​μeb​νRμν​ab. The curvature form of a connection variation is δRab=Dω​δωab. For the Einstein-Hilbert term, integration by parts in form notation gives a connection equation proportional to
εabcd​Tc∧ed=0
(49)
when matter has no connection dependence. For an invertible vierbein this forces Ta=0, so ω=ω(e). The vierbein equation then becomes the second-order Einstein field equations. This applies to the scalar and Maxwell examples: use F=dA, which is independent of ω and remains gauge invariant even if torsion is present.
A Dirac field changes the connection equation. Directly varying its two covariant derivatives gives
δω​Lχ​=−8e​χˉ​{γμ,γab}χδωμab​=−4e​χˉ​γμabχδωμab​.
(50)
Thus its spin density sources totally antisymmetric torsion. This is Einstein-Cartan theory, and the connection equation is algebraic rather than a propagation equation for an extra field. To see both its solution and its effect, write
ωμab​=ω(e)μab​+Kμab​,Kabc​=ea​μKμbc​,Babc=χˉ​γabcχ.
(51)
For the totally antisymmetric component sourced by this field, the Ricci scalar is R(e,ω)=R(e)−Kabc​Kabc up to a divergence. The other irreducible contorsion components have no spinor source and their connection equations set them to zero. The auxiliary part of the Lagrangian is therefore
LK​=−2κ2e​Kabc​Kabc−4e​Kabc​Babc.
(52)
Its algebraic variation gives the algebraic torsion of a Dirac field,
Kabc​=−4κ2​Babc​,Tabc​=−2Kabc​=2κ2​Babc​.​
(53)
Here the first index of Kabc​ labels the derivative, and the torsion convention is Tabc​=Kbac​−Kcab​; specifying this convention fixes the sign. Eliminating K leaves the second-order effective action with contact interaction
Lcontact​=32eκ2​(χˉ​γabcχ)(χˉ​γabc​χ).​
(54)
It is a local axial-current four-fermion interaction. Hence first-order gravity with Dirac spin density is equivalent to torsion-free second-order gravity with this additional interaction. It is not equivalent to simply declaring the spin connection torsion-free and dropping the contact term. In the effective formulation the Einstein field equations have the stress tensor of the full effective matter action. Before eliminating the connection, the antisymmetric stress and its spin current are related by the local Lorentz Noether identity.
Finally, the 1.5-order formalism is a convenient way to vary this effective action. Let ω(e,χ) solve the algebraic connection equation and let S2​(e,χ)=S1​(e,ω(e,χ),χ). The chain rule gives
δS2​=δeδS1​​​ω​δe+δχδS1​​​ω​δχ+δχˉ​δS1​​​ω​δχˉ​+0δωδS1​​​ω​​​δω.
(55)
Thus vary as in first order, then insert the solved connection. One does not need to calculate the complicated induced connection variation. This procedure is justified by its connection equation, not by discarding that variation arbitrarily. In supergravity the gravitino sources a contorsion quadratic in fermions; substituting it generates the four-fermion terms, while the 1.5-order method simplifies the local supersymmetry cancellation through the lower orders considered above.
