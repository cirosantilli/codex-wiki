One convenient version of Watson's lemma is the following. Suppose G(t) is integrable away from zero and has, as t↓0, an asymptotic expansion G(t)∼∑j≥0​cj​tβ+j−1, where β>0. On a finite interval, or on an infinite interval with suitable exponential growth control,
∫0T​e−λtG(t)dt∼∑j≥0​λβ+jcj​Γ(β+j)​,λ→+∞.
(3)
To prove a finite truncation, split the integral at a fixed small δ>0. The part away from zero is exponentially small. On (0,δ) subtract the first N local terms and bound the remainder by Ctβ+N−1; its integral is O(λ−β−N). Extending each polynomial integral to infinity changes it only exponentially, and the substitution u=λt produces the Gamma integral. A little-o local remainder gives the corresponding little-o asymptotic remainder. This is a proof by localization and remainder bounds, rather than merely formal integration of a series.
For the monotone-phase integral, assume the derivatives needed below exist and are bounded near a, and the rest of the integral is controlled. Put t=ϕ(a)−ϕ(x). Since ϕ′(a)<0, this is a regular endpoint coordinate and
f(λ)=eλϕ(a)∫0ϕ(a)−ϕ(b)​e−λtG(t)dt,G(t)=−ϕ′(x(t))g(x(t))​.
(4)
Differentiating with dx/dt=−1/ϕ′ gives G(0)=−g(a)/ϕ′(a) and G′(0)=g′(a)/ϕ′(a)2−g(a)ϕ′′(a)/ϕ′(a)3. Watson's lemma therefore gives the two-term monotone-endpoint Laplace expansion
f(λ)∼eλϕ(a)[−λϕ′(a)g(a)​+λ21​(ϕ′(a)2g′(a)​−ϕ′(a)3g(a)ϕ′′(a)​)].​
(5)
An O(eλϕ(a)λ−3) remainder follows, for example, from a sufficiently smooth transformed amplitude with bounded second derivative near zero. The displayed second coefficient can vanish for special amplitudes without changing the expansion.
The unheaded transition request concerns a cubic endpoint-to-saddle transition. Near x=0 its exponent has the expansion
−x+αsinx=−(1−α)x−6αx3​+O(x5).
(6)
At the cubic endpoint x has size λ−1/3. Requiring the linear term to contribute on the same scale gives 1−α=O(λ−2/3). Thus the distinguished limit and its uniform leading integral are
p=32​,q=31​,h(ν)=∫0∞​e−νt−t3/6dt,f(λ,1−νλ−2/3)∼λ−1/3h(ν).​
(7)
For fixed ν, the substitution x=λ−1/3t makes the omitted exponent terms O(λ−2/3) on bounded t intervals. Cubic decay controls the remaining tail, while the original interval away from zero is exponentially negligible. The estimate is uniform for ν in compact sets. In the printed range α≤1, ν≥0; the same transition integral also makes sense for negative fixed ν.
At ν=0, the Gamma integral gives h(0)=61/3Γ(1/3)/3, recovering the cubic endpoint regime. For ν→+∞, scale t=u/ν and expand e−u3/(6ν3) to obtain h(ν)=ν−1−ν−4+O(ν−7). Its leading term gives λ−1/3/ν=1/[λ(1−α)], recovering the ordinary endpoint regime in their overlap. Both matching limits are therefore checked explicitly.

(S0′​)2+k2=0,2S0′​S1′​+S0′′​=0.

With K=k2​>0, they give S0′​=±iK and S1​=−21​logK+constant. Thus the leading WKB approximation for a slowly varying oscillator is
y(x)∼K(ϵx)−1/2[C+​ei∫xK(ϵs)ds+C−​e−i∫xK(ϵs)ds].​
(11)
The amplitude is fixed by the transport equation, not just by replacing the constant wave number in a sinusoid. The local validity condition is that variation of K over a wavelength is small, for example ∣dK/dx∣/K2≪1.
If k2=−κ2<0, the same calculation gives the evanescent form
y(x)∼κ(ϵx)−1/2[C+​e∫xκ(ϵs)ds+C−​e−∫xκ(ϵs)ds].​
(12)
At a classical turning point where k2 vanishes, these amplitudes diverge and the separated WKB descriptions cease to be uniform. For a simple zero, an Airy turning-point connection formula replaces them in an O(ϵ2/3) interval in X; it connects oscillation to exponential behavior with the usual quarter-phase shift. A higher-order zero needs a different local canonical problem, so the Airy statement assumes a simple turning point.
For a smooth positive weight r on the closed interval, let T=∫0π​r(x)​dx. The local wave number in the Sturm-Liouville problem is λ​r​, and the condition at zero selects a sine. Imposing the condition at π gives the WKB quantization condition

Use normal modes eikx+st, so s=−iω in the convention eikx−iωt. For a translation-invariant, well-posed linear initial value problem on the whole line, localized data are propagated by a causal Green function. The Fourier transform in space and Laplace transform in time give an inverse integral whose denominator is the dispersion relation D(k,s). The initial temporal contour is a Bromwich line to the right of every real-wave-number temporal singularity.
The Briggs-Bers technique lowers that temporal contour while deforming the spatial contour to avoid its poles. A spatial pinch point occurs when two spatial branches originating on opposite sides of the original Fourier contour collide and prevent further deformation. Algebraically a simple candidate satisfies D=Dk​=0, with Ds​=0 and Dkk​=0; these equations alone do not establish the pinch topology. A same-side collision is a false spatial saddle in quartic dispersion. The opposite-side requirement is also explicit in the primary numerical study of pinch-point detection.
The rightmost contributing pinch determines the fixed-position exponential growth of the impulse response, ordinarily with a t−1/2 saddle point prefactor. A positive rate is absolute wave-packet instability. If real-wave-number normal modes grow but the impulse response decays at each fixed position, it is convective wave-packet instability; growth can then occur along moving rays. If no temporal normal mode grows, the system is stable, with equality thresholds treated as marginal rather than exponential decay. Along a ray x=vt, apply the same argument to s(k)+ikv, whose saddle point condition is s′(k)+iv=0. Thus the laboratory frame is part of the absolute/convective definition.
For the quartic problem the temporal dispersion relation is
s(k)=a1​k2−a2​k4−a3​,D(k,s)=s+a3​−a1​k2+a2​k4.
(16)
Write a=Rea1​, b=Rea2​, c=Rea3​. For b>0 the causal Fourier kernel is absolutely convergent for positive time and the finite maximum temporal growth rate is
σT​=k∈Rsup​Res(k)=−c+4bmax(a,0)2​.​
(17)
The usual initial-value framework fails if b<0, or if b=0 and a>0, because arbitrarily large real wave numbers grow arbitrarily fast: no finite Bromwich line can start to their right. This is a high-frequency ill-posedness obstruction, not an absolute instability criterion. If b=0 and a≤0, the growth bound is finite, but the dispersive limiting case needs oscillatory Fourier inversion or a limiting-absorption construction when there is no quadratic damping. It must not be justified by the absolutely convergent quartic-diffusion argument. The following explicit calculation first assumes b>0 and a2​=0.
The double-root candidates are
k0​=0,s0​=−a3​;k±2​=2a2​a1​​,s±​=4a2​a12​​−a3​.
(18)
Rather than assume all are physical, compute the causal resolvent at the impulse location. Put S=s+a3​, and for large Res factor
S−a1​k2+a2​k4=a2​(k2+κ12​)(k2+κ22​),Reκj​>0.
(19)
Then κ1​κ2​=S/a2​​ and (κ1​+κ2​)2=−a1​/a2​+2S/a2​​, with sheets selected from that large-s region. Partial fraction decomposition and ∫R​(k2+κ2)−1dk=π/κ give the quartic impulse-response Laplace resolvent
G(0,s)=2π1​∫R​S−a1​k2+a2​k4dk​=2S​2a2​​S​−a1​​1​.​
(20)
All roots in this expression are continued from the causal region, not selected afresh at a candidate collision. The two possible singularities are now transparent. S=0 is a square-root singularity when a1​=0. The extra singularity requires S​=a1​/(2a2​​). Taking a2​​ with positive real part and putting z=a1​/a2​​, this lies on the physical S​ sheet precisely when Rez>0. If that real part is zero the candidate is on a cut boundary and has nonpositive real S, so it cannot lie to the right of S=0. For Rez<0, the apparently growing candidate may be on the wrong sheet altogether.
Therefore the physical-sheet growth rate of a quartic impulse response is
σA​=−c+M,M={max{0,Re(a12​/(4a2​))},0,​Re(a1​/a2​​)>0,Re(a1​/a2​​)≤0.​​
(21)
These are resolvent branch singularities on the causal continuation, so the formula implements the pinch test instead of merely listing formal saddle point rates. For a1​=0, the zero saddle point is fourth-order and the resolvent is proportional to S−3/4; its impulse response is proportional to t−1/4e−a3​t and the same exponential-rate formula applies. At nondegenerate candidates the prefactor is t−1/2.
The classification in this diffusive case is now explicit: σT​<0 gives stability; σA​>0 gives absolute instability; σT​>0 with σA​<0 gives convective instability. When σT​>0 and σA​=0, the nondegenerate fixed-position response still decays algebraically, at the marginal absolute/convective threshold. Other equality cases require the stated saddle point prefactors. Temporal instability supplies a growing ray: at a real maximum of Res(k), choose v=−Ims′(k) so that the moving-ray saddle point is stationary there.
Three examples show why complex coefficients and sheets matter. For (a1​,a2​,a3​)=(2,1,1/2), σT​=σA​=1/2, so the instability is absolute. For (1+2i,1,1/10), σT​=3/20>0 but σA​=−1/10, so it is convective even though there is no explicit first-derivative advection term: dispersion carries packets in opposite directions. For (−2,1,1/2), both physical rates are −1/2. The formal candidates k=±i have s=1/2 but fail the physical-sheet condition, so this temporally stable example is not absolutely unstable.
On the admissible boundary b=0, a≤0, a2​=0, the oscillatory or damped Fourier construction has σT​=−c. The surviving zero-wave-number stationary response has the same exponential rate −c, with a quadratic or quartic algebraic factor; damping/dispersion therefore does not generate a separate convective-growth range there. If a2​=0, the problem is second order and its diffusion/dispersive kernel must be used instead of the quartic formula. Application to a finite interval or nonlocalized data also changes the spatial inversion problem, so the whole-line localized-data hypothesis is essential.

Let U be real and twice continuously differentiable, take a nonzero real wave number, and write c=cr​+ici​. Exponential instability requires kci​>0, in particular ci​=0, so U−c has no zero on the channel. Divide the Rayleigh equation for inviscid shear flow by U−c, multiply by ψ∗ and integrate. The wall conditions remove the boundary term, giving
I+∫y1​y2​​U−cU′′​∣ψ∣2dy=0,I=∫y1​y2​​(∣ψ′∣2+k2∣ψ∣2)dy>0.
(25)
Its imaginary part is
ci​∫y1​y2​​(U−cr​)2+ci2​U′′∣ψ∣2​dy=0.
(26)
The weight is nonnegative and positive wherever the nontrivial eigenfunction does not vanish. A nontrivial solution of this regular second-order equation cannot vanish on an interval. If U′′ had one strict sign without changing sign, the integral could not vanish. If U′′ were identically zero, the real identity would instead give the contradiction I=0. Thus U′′ must take both signs, and continuity gives an interior zero. This proves Rayleigh's inflection-point theorem: an unstable profile must have an inflection point, in fact a sign change of U′′ rather than merely an isolated zero without sign change.
