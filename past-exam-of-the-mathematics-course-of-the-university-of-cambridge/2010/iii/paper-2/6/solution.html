We first prove the simple Artinian case of the Artin–Wedderburn theorem. This part does not need finite dimension over k. Choose a minimal nonzero left ideal L, using the descending chain condition. Simplicity gives LA=A. Also L2=0: otherwise LAL=(LA)L=L would be zero, since each lal′ lies in L2. Choose x∈L for which right multiplication rx​:L→L is nonzero. By simplicity of L, it is an automorphism. Choose e∈L with ex=x. Then (e2−e)x=0, and injectivity of rx​ gives e2=e. Thus L=Ae.
Right multiplication by e splits the left regular module as A=Ae⊕A(1−e). Repeat within the remaining left ideal: if it contains a minimal left ideal Af generated by an idempotent, the map v↦vf splits off Af. Each nonzero remaining complement is a strictly smaller left ideal, so the Artinian condition terminates the process. Therefore the regular module is a finite direct sum of minimal left ideals.
All these simple summands are isomorphic. For primitive idempotents e,f, simplicity gives AeA=A, so fAe=0; otherwise fAeA=fA would be zero. A nonzero element of fAe defines a nonzero homomorphism Af→Ae, which is an isomorphism between simple modules. Thus A≅L⊕n as a left module. Put Δ=EndA​(L), a division algebra by the Schur lemma. Right multiplication gives
Aop≅EndA​(A)≅Mn​(Δ).
(46)
Taking opposites, with matrix transposition reversing products, proves
A≅Mn​(D),D=Δop a division algebra.​
(47)
The construction is k-linear and embeds k into the center of D.
A central simple algebra is a finite-dimensional simple k-algebra whose center is exactly k. The Brauer group consists of such algebras modulo matrix stabilization: A,B represent the same class if Mr​(A)≅Ms​(B) for some positive integers r,s. Its operation and inverse are
[A][B]=[A⊗k​B],1=[k],[A]−1=[Aop].​
(48)
The tensor product is central simple, and A⊗Aop≅Endk​(A) by (a⊗b)(x)=axb, explaining the inverse. Both facts can be checked after a splitting field of a central simple algebra extension, where they are matrix-algebra identities, and descended by faithful scalar extension. Every class has a unique central division-algebra representative, by the regular-module description above.
To prove infinitude over Q explicitly, put E=Q(i) and, for a=0, let
Aa​=(−1,a)Q​=E⊕Ej,j2=a,jz=zj.
(49)
This is a quaternion algebra. It is central simple: the center is Q, and after adjoining i its generators have the faithful matrix model
i⟼diag(i,−i),j⟼(01​a0​).
(50)
These generate M2​(E); consequently a nonzero proper two-sided ideal over Q is impossible. The quaternion conjugation gives
N(z+wj)=NE/Q​(z)−aNE/Q​(w).
(51)
A nonzero element has an inverse exactly when this norm is nonzero. Thus Aa​ is a division algebra when a is not an E/Q norm; if a is a norm it has a nonzero zero divisor and is M2​(Q) by the classification just proved.
For a prime p≡3(mod4), every nonzero rational sum x2+y2 has even p-adic valuation. Divide out the minimum valuation of x,y; cancellation modulo p is impossible because −1 is not a square modulo p. Therefore p is not an E/Q norm, and Ap​ is a nontrivial division-algebra Brauer class. This is the quaternion norm obstruction at primes congruent to three modulo four.
We also prove the product rule needed to distinguish classes. In Aa​⊗Ab​, write the generators as i1​,j1​,i2​,j2​. The commuting subalgebras generated respectively by
i1​, j1​j2​andi1​i2​, j2​
(52)
are Aab​ and the split quaternion algebra (1,b)Q​≅M2​(Q). The latter has the matrix model diag(1,−1) and (01​b0​). Together the two subalgebras generate the whole tensor product, since they recover i2​ and j1​. Dimensions are sixteen on both sides, giving
Aa​⊗Ab​≅Aab​⊗M2​(Q),[Aa​][Ab​]=[Aab​].​
(53)
Quaternion conjugation identifies Aa​ with its opposite, so these classes have order at most two. For distinct primes p,q≡3(mod4), the parameter pq has odd valuation at p, so Apq​ is a division algebra. Thus [Ap​]=[Aq​]. There are infinitely many such primes: if their list were p1​,…,pm​, the integer 4p1​⋯pm​−1 would have a prime factor congruent to three modulo four outside that list. We conclude
Br(Q) is infinite.​
(54)
Indeed the same valuation argument for any nonempty finite product gives independent quaternion classes over rational numbers.
For R, we prove the required division-algebra classification. Let D be finite-dimensional and central over R. If D=R, choose a nonreal element; its irreducible real minimal polynomial is quadratic, so translating and rescaling it gives i∈D with i2=−1. The centralizer D+​ of i is a finite-dimensional division algebra over C=R(i), with that field central. Every element has a split minimal polynomial over C, hence belongs to C, so D+​=C.
Conjugation by i is an involution, giving D=D+​⊕D−​, where D−​={x:ix=−xi}. If D−​=0, then D=C, contrary to its center being R. Choose j=0 in D−​. For any x∈D−​, xj−1 commutes with i, so D−​=Cj. Also j2∈C and commutes with j, forcing j2∈R. It cannot be positive, since (j−j2​)(j+j2​)=0, or zero, since D is a division algebra. Rescale to j2=−1. Consequently
D=R⊕Ri⊕Rj⊕Rij≅H.
(55)
Conversely the positive definite quaternion norm makes H a division algebra. The two possible central division representatives are therefore R and H. They are not equivalent, and conjugation identifies H with its opposite, so
Br(R)≅Z/2,generated by [H].​
(56)
This uses the finite-dimensional real division-algebra classification directly, not as an unexplained theorem citation.
Finally let ks​ be a separable closure and Γ=Gal(ks​/k). The cohomological description is
Br(k)≅Hcont2​(Γ,ks×​)=L/k finite Galoislim​​H2(Gal(L/k),L×).​
(57)
Here ks×​ has the discrete topology and its natural Galois action. For a finite Galois splitting field L, identify A⊗k​L with a matrix algebra. Its descent action is implemented by matrices uσ​, unique up to scalars, so
uσ​σ(uτ​)uστ−1​=c(σ,τ)−1∈L×.
(58)
The inverse convention here agrees with the crossed-product convention below. The semilinear operators uσ​σ commute with the descended algebra A; their crossed product has class [A]−1 by the commuting-factor decomposition of the full endomorphism algebra. Inverting its scalar cocycle therefore gives the class [A]. Associativity gives the two-cocycle identity, and changing the representatives changes c by a coboundary. Conversely a two-cocycle defines the crossed-product algebra of a Galois extension, with uσ​a=σ(a)uσ​ and uσ​uτ​=c(σ,τ)uστ​. It is central simple and split by L. Galois descent identifies coboundaries with split matrix-algebra classes and gives H2(Gal(L/k),L×)≅ker(Br(k)→Br(L)). Every central simple algebra has a finite separable, hence finite Galois, splitting field, yielding the displayed limit.
