An idempotent e gives a projective module Ae, since A=Ae⊕A(1−e). It is primitive when it cannot be written as a sum of two nonzero orthogonal idempotents. This is equivalent to indecomposability of Ae: a projection onto a direct summand is an idempotent of EndA​(Ae)≅(eAe)op, and conversely such an idempotent splits Ae. Starting with 1, split any nonprimitive summand repeatedly. The process terminates because each splitting increases the number of nonzero vector-space summands in a finite-dimensional algebra. Thus
1=e1​+⋯+em​,A​A=ν=1⨁m​Aeν​,​
(9)
with orthogonal primitive idempotents and indecomposable projective modules. Each summand is generated by its idempotent.
Let D=Homk​(−,k). A Frobenius algebra is a finite-dimensional algebra with A≅D(A) as left modules over A, where (aϕ)(x)=ϕ(xa). Equivalently there is a linear functional λ:A→k for which (x,y)↦λ(xy) is nondegenerate. A symmetric algebra satisfies the stronger bimodule isomorphism A≅D(A), with (aϕb)(x)=ϕ(bxa); equivalently the functional can be chosen with λ(xy)=λ(yx). This is the associative-algebra meaning of symmetric, rather than the symmetric algebra of a vector space.

A primitive idempotent remains primitive modulo the nilpotent radical: idempotents lift through that radical, so a decomposition in the quotient would lift to one in eAe. In the semisimple algebra A/J, the left simple Ae/JAe and the dual of the right simple eA/eAJ belong to the same matrix block. There is exactly one left simple isomorphism class in that block, even over a nonalgebraically closed field. Hence the head-socle identity for a symmetric algebra is

In characteristic two, the central idempotents are b0​=1+r+r2 and b1​=1−b0​. The first block has basis b0​,b0​s and is kC2​≅k[u]/(u2). For the second, choose a primitive cube root ω and use

The two r-eigenlines are interchanged by s, proving irreducibility. Polynomial interpolation in r gives diagonal matrix units, and multiplying by s gives the off-diagonal units. Thus the four-dimensional block maps onto M2​(k) and is isomorphic to it:
kS3​≅k[u]/(u2)⊕M2​(k)(chark=2).​
(15)
There are two simple modules, the trivial one-dimensional module T and the two-dimensional module V; the sign module equals T. Their projective covers are PT​=k[u]/(u2), with layers T,T, and PV​=V. The regular module is PT​⊕V⊕2, and the Cartan matrix of a group algebra is diag(2,1). Every module is a sum of T,PT​,V: the first block is classified by Jordan chains for a nilpotent linear map of lengths one and two, while the matrix block is semisimple. Thus there are exactly three indecomposable isomorphism classes. This describes the 2-modular blocks of S3.

Thus A=P+​⊕P−​ and its Cartan matrix of a group algebra is
(21​12​).​

The elements e−​ve+​ and e+​ve−​ give one arrow in each direction between the two simples. All paths of length three vanish, and the six paths of lengths zero, one and two are linearly independent. Hence A is the path algebra of that two-vertex oriented cycle modulo the ideal of paths of length three. Its connected quiver also proves there is a single block of S3 in characteristic three.
Any module is a Z/2-graded vector space given by the s-eigenspaces, with v interchanging the grades and v3=0. Constructing Jordan chains with homogeneous bases in the kernel and image filtrations and homogeneous lifts decomposes it into graded chains. Each chain has length 1,2 or 3 and is determined by its top sign. It is a uniserial module and hence indecomposable. Consequently there are exactly six indecomposables, the length-ℓ quotients P±​/JℓP±​, ℓ=1,2,3.
