Write A=kG and J=J(A), its Jacobson radical. First suppose the characteristic is p>0. We prove the counting statement through the cocenter of a finite-dimensional algebra
C(A)=A/[A,A],
(1)
where [A,A] is the linear span of commutators, not an ideal. In a group algebra, this span is exactly the span of differences of conjugate group elements: gh−hg is such a difference, and xgx−1−g=[x,gx−1]. Consequently C(A) has one basis vector for each conjugacy class.
There is a well-defined Frobenius map on an algebra cocenter
F:C(A)⟶C(A),[a]⟼[ap].
(2)
Here F is additive and F(c[a])=cpF([a]). To prove additivity, expand (a+b)p into words of length p. Cyclic rotations of a word are equal modulo commutators. Every nonconstant word has a rotation orbit of length p, so its orbit contributes zero; only ap,bp remain. Also
[(xy−yx)p]=[(xy)p]−[(yx)p]=0,
(3)
because the two products are cyclic rotations. Additivity then shows that the whole commutator subspace maps to zero, proving well-definedness on the quotient.
Let s be the number of simple modules up to isomorphism. The semisimple algebra A/J is ∏ν=1s​Mdν​​(k): over an algebraically closed field, a finite-dimensional division algebra is the field itself, since the minimal polynomial of any element splits into linear factors and a division algebra has no zero divisors. In a matrix algebra, the commutator subspace is precisely the traceless matrices. Off-diagonal matrix units are commutators, and Eii​−Ejj​=[Eij​,Eji​]; these span that subspace in every characteristic. Thus
C(A/J)≅ks.
(4)
The map induced by F on each coordinate is z↦zp, since tr(Bp)=(trB)p. This is bijective because k is a perfect field.
The natural surjection C(A)↠C(A/J) has kernel (J+[A,A])/[A,A]. Every kernel class has a representative in J. Since J is nilpotent, FN kills that kernel for sufficiently large N. On the quotient it remains bijective, so its image has dimension exactly s. On the conjugacy-class basis, however,
FN([g])=[gpN].
(5)
For large N, every gpN is a p-regular element; moreover the pN-power map permutes the p-regular conjugacy classes, because an inverse exponent can be chosen modulo the prime-to-p part of the exponent of G. The stable image therefore has precisely those conjugacy classes as its basis. Comparing dimensions proves
#{simple kG-modules}=#{p-regular conjugacy classes of G}.​
(6)
This argument uses dimensions of semilinear images: a bijective scalar Frobenius map preserves dimension, so the usual rank count applies.
In characteristic zero every group element is relevant. Averaging a linear projection onto a submodule over G makes that projection G-equivariant, proving Maschke's theorem and semisimplicity of kG. The same matrix-algebra cocenter calculation now gives s=dimC(kG), the total number of conjugacy classes.
For the second request, M is a relative projective module for H when every G-equivariant epimorphism onto M which splits after restriction to H already splits over G. Equivalently M is a direct summand of kG⊗kH​M, the induced representation of the restriction of a representation to H. We can verify the defining splitting property directly: if q:N↠M has an H-linear section u, choose representatives T of the left cosets G/H and set
v(m)=[G:H]1​∑t∈T​tu(t−1m).
(7)
The sum is independent of representatives by H-linearity. Reindexing gt=t′h proves G-linearity, while qv(m)=[G:H]−1∑t​m=m. Hence every M is relatively H-projective when the index is invertible in k.
For completeness the canonical induced-module epimorphism ε(g⊗m)=gm has the explicit G-linear section
σ(m)=[G:H]1​t∈T∑​t⊗t−1m,εσ=idM​.​
(8)
It exhibits the promised direct summand and is the projectivity detected on a subgroup of invertible index construction.
