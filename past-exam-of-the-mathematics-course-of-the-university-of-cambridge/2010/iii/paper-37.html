The least-squares estimator minimizes (Y−Xb)T(Y−Xb). Differentiation gives XTXβ^​=XTY, and the full column rank of X makes XTX positive definite. Hence
β^​=(XTX)−1XTY.​
(1)
The fitted values are Y^=Xβ^​=HY, where the hat matrix is H=X(XTX)−1XT. The regression residuals are ε^=Y−Y^=(I−H)Y. Since H is the orthogonal projection matrix onto the column space of X, (I−H)X=0 and
ε^∼Nn​(0,σ2(I−H)).​
(2)
This is a singular multivariate normal distribution supported on the (n−p)-dimensional orthogonal complement of the column space: its components are generally correlated. The equality Y^=Xβ^​ puts the fitted vector in that column space, while XTε^=0 expresses fitted-residual orthogonality. No residual component lies in an explanatory direction that could improve the least-squares fit.
For the calibration model, write Ri​ for a reading and ai​ for the known amount. The first fit assumes Ri​=α+βai​+εi​ with independent N(0,σ2) errors. Its residual-versus-fitted plot has systematic changes in group means and an increasing within-group spread. These suggest both a nonlinear mean relationship and heteroscedasticity, rather than the random scatter expected from a satisfactory normal linear model.
The Box–Cox transformation plot is a profile log-likelihood for the power parameter λ. Its maximum is near 0.94; the horizontal line gives an approximate 95% likelihood-ratio confidence interval, which excludes the untransformed value 1. Since affine rescaling of the response does not change the fitted mean space, fitting the raw power Ri0.94​ is equivalent to using (Ri0.94​−1)/0.94 with an intercept. The second model is
Ri0.94​=α+βai​+εi​,εi​∼iidN(0,σ2).
(3)
Its residual spread is more even, but observation 17 has a conspicuously negative residual near −9.95. This motivates checking for an outlier or recording error. The third fit omits that observation; omission should be justified by that investigation, since choosing exclusions from residuals can affect inference. Some group pattern still remains, so the transformation alone does not certify a perfect model.
At an amount of 3 nanograms, the third fit predicts the transformed mean
m^=−3.62509+30.87295(3)=88.99376.
(4)
Simply applying the inverse power gives m^1/0.94=118.5187. The requested expected reading needs bias correction after an inverse transformation, since E[g(Z)]=g(E[Z]) for nonlinear g. With q=1/0.94 and s=2.832, a second-order Taylor expansion gives
E[R∣a=3]​≃m^q+21​q(q−1)m^q−2s2=118.5227.​
(5)
Here the correction uses the residual variance, rather than the variance of the estimated fitted mean. The power-transformed Gaussian model is an approximation for positive readings; the fitted mean is over 31 residual standard deviations above zero, so the negative tail has negligible effect on this local approximation. The ordinary inverse-transformed prediction is therefore essentially 118.52 at the displayed precision.

The data do not cross every age with every gender: only age 21 has both genders, while age 30 has only women and age 40 only men. Thus a gender effect separate from age can be estimated under an additive model, but age-by-gender interactions cannot all be estimated or checked. There is only one observation in each recorded combination of age, gender, policy, and points, so there is no within-cell replication for separating pure error from lack of fit. No sampling method or additional risk characteristics are supplied, limiting comparisons between policyholder categories.
There is also a source discrepancy: the printed table gives 268 for one comprehensive premium, whereas the R data vector gives 368 at the corresponding position. The supplied regression output is treated as referring to the R data. This should be resolved against the original data before substantive use.

(6)

The reduced model combines the 0-, 3-, and 6-point categories. Relative to the full model, the null hypothesis is η3​=η6​=0, leaving the 9-point contrast unrestricted. These are two linear restrictions on an otherwise unchanged normal linear model.
For nested normal models, the reduction in residual sum of squares divided by σ2 has a chi-squared distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of restrictions. It is independent of the full model's residual variance estimate. Hence the F-test statistic is
F=19512/24(22323−19512)/2​=1.728782∼F2,24​under H0​.
(8)
It is below the supplied 5% critical value 3.402826, so do not reject equal effects for 0, 3, and 6 points. There is insufficient evidence that separating those categories improves this additive model; this does not prove that their true premiums are identical.

For separate binary observations, the saturated model fits each outcome exactly and has maximized log-likelihood zero. With p^​i​=pi​(β^​), the residual deviance is therefore
D=−2ℓ(β^​)=−2∑i​[yi​logitp^​i​+log(1−p^​i​)].
(17)
Use the identity in part (b) to replace yi​ by p^​i​ in its first term:
D=−2i∑​[p^​i​logitp^​i​+log(1−p^​i​)]=−2i∑​[p^​i​logp^​i​+(1−p^​i​)log(1−p^​i​)].​
(18)
Thus the deviance is twice the sum of the fitted binary information entropies. A deviance goodness-of-fit test against χn−p2​ is unreliable for individual ungrouped binary observations: there is only one trial per saturated-model parameter, so the usual large-cell-count approximation fails. Deviance differences between fixed-dimensional nested logistic regression models can still support a likelihood-ratio test, subject to its usual regularity assumptions.
The three tree models are respectively logitpi​=β0​, logitpi​=β0​+β1​log2​Ti​, and logitpi​=β0​+β1​log2​Ti​+β2​Si​, with independent Bernoulli random variables conditional on the covariates. Testing the additional severity effect gives
2{ℓ(β​full​)−ℓ(β​reduced​)}=655.24−563.90=91.34.​
(19)
Under H0​:β2​=0, its reference distribution is asymptotically χ12​. The p-value is approximately 1.21×10−21, giving overwhelming evidence that severity improves the fitted model. Holding severity fixed, doubling T increases log2​T by one and multiplies the odds by
e2.2164≃9.17.​
(20)
This is an odds ratio, not a multiplication of the event probability itself.

Let Yit​ be the accident count at location i in period t∈{0,1}, and eit​ its exposure in years, where t=1 denotes after installation. The Poisson exposure model is
Yit​∼indPoisson(eit​eα+βt),logE[Yit​]=logeit​+α+βt.
(22)
The offset logeit​ has its coefficient fixed at one. The model assumes constant rates within each period, a common before rate across locations, a common treatment rate ratio, independent counts, and Poisson variance equal to the mean. The incidence rate ratio is r=eβ, so
r^=e−0.69901=0.497077.​
(23)
An approximate 95% Wald confidence interval transforms the interval for β:
[e−0.69901−1.96(0.27466),e−0.69901+1.96(0.27466)]=[0.290,0.852].​

The second Poisson exposure model allows each location its own baseline accident rate:
Yit​∼indPoisson(eit​eα+λi​+βt),λ1​=0.
(25)
The last equality is the corner-point constraint. The common after-to-before incidence rate ratio remains eβ. This model is appropriate because the sites can have different background risks; treating those differences as unexplained Poisson variation made the first model fit poorly, with deviance 50.863 on 14 residual degrees of freedom.
Adding seven location effects to the first fitted model reduces the deviance by 50.863−16.275=34.588, which is large relative to χ72​. The supplied sequential analysis of deviance reports a slightly different location contribution because it enters locations before the period indicator. The full model is much better but its deviance 16.275 on 7 degrees of freedom still gives an approximate goodness-of-fit p-value 0.0227. That suggests residual lack of fit or overdispersion, although sparse accident counts make the asymptotic deviance goodness-of-fit test approximate.

Let I∈{1,2} denote eventual death or recovery, respectively; T is the time from admission to that terminal event, and θ=Pr(I=1). A patient still hospitalized at collection has right censoring: only T>c is known, where c is the elapsed time since admission. Both eventual outcomes are then unobserved. A recorded death or discharge is an observed terminal event in this competing risks model; discharge is not censoring of an otherwise observable later in-hospital death. Assume the observation deadline, conditional on relevant admission information, is noninformative for (T,I).

This is an EM algorithm for a censored lognormal competing-risks mixture. Write Yi​=logTi​ and suppose Yi​∣Ii​=j∼N(μj​,σj2​), with mixing weights π1​=θ, π2​=1−θ. The missing data are Ii​ and Yi​ for censored patients; neither is missing for recorded deaths or recoveries. The complete-data log-likelihood, apart from terms independent of the parameters, is
ℓc​=∑i​∑j=12​1{Ii​=j}​[logπj​−logσj​−2σj2​(Yi​−μj​)2​].
(30)
The lognormal Jacobian −logTi​ is also parameter-independent and therefore does not affect the maximization.
At iteration k, set zij​=(logci​−μj(k)​)/σj(k)​ for each censored patient and let Φ(z)=1−Φ(z). Bayes' rule gives the E-step class weights
wij​=∑ℓ=12​πℓ(k)​Φ(ziℓ​)πj(k)​Φ(zij​)​.​
(31)
Given that class, Yi​ has a truncated normal distribution above logci​. With the Inverse Mills ratio ψ(z)=φ(z)/Φ(z), compute both moments needed for the E-step:
mij​vij​sij​​=μj(k)​+σj(k)​ψ(zij​),=(σj(k)​)2[1−ψ(zij​)(ψ(zij​)−zij​)],=mij2​+vij​.​
(32)
Thus E[1{Ii​=j}​∣data]=wij​, E[1{Ii​=j}​Yi​∣data]=wij​mij​, and the corresponding second moment is wij​sij​. For an observed outcome ji​ at time ti​, use wij​=1{j=ji​}​, mij​=logti​, and sij​=(logti​)2. These specify the full conditional expectation of ℓc​.
Put Wj​=∑i​wij​ and let n be the total number of patients. Maximizing that expected complete-data log-likelihood gives the M-step
θ(k+1)μj(k+1)​(σj2​)(k+1)​=W1​/n,=Wj​∑i​wij​mij​​,=Wj​∑i​wij​sij​​−(μj(k+1)​)2.​​
(33)
All moments on the right are evaluated at the old parameters. Repeat the E-step and M-step until the observed log-likelihood stabilizes, using positive initial variances and mixing weights. The expectation-maximization algorithm increases that likelihood at each exact iteration, but need not find its global maximum; multiple starts help distinguish local optima. The resulting estimates give the case fatality probability θ^ and
F(t∣I=j)=Φ(σ^j​logt−μ^​j​​),t>0.​
(34)
These are conditional event-time distributions, distinct from the cumulative incidence functions θ^F(t∣I=1) and (1−θ^)F(t∣I=2).
