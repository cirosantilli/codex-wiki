Fix the orientation of the Berezin integral by defining its measure as the linear functional that extracts the coefficient of ∏i=1N​(θi​θˉi​); all monomials of lower degree integrate to zero. In particular ∫D(θˉ,θ)∏i​(θi​θˉi​)=1. This specifies the sign convention in the notation dNθˉdNθ.
The Grassmann change-of-variables formula gives
dNθ′=det(U)−1dNθ,dNθˉ′=det(U∗)−1dNθˉ.
(7)
For a unitary matrix, detUdetU∗=1, so the combined measure is invariant. Diagonalize the Hermitian matrix B by a unitary change of variables. If its eigenvalues are bi​, the integrand factors as ∏i​(1−bi​θˉi​θi​)=∏i​(1+bi​θi​θˉi​). Extracting its top coefficient proves the Grassmann Gaussian integral
∫dNθˉdNθe−θˉi​Bij​θj​=i∏​bi​=detB.​
(8)
The determinant identity also holds without Hermiticity by direct expansion; Hermiticity makes the diagonalization proof immediate.
Let C(x)=∑k​xk​C(k). Integrating out the Grassmann variables first gives the exact polynomial expression
I(g)=∫RN​dNxe−xTAx/2det[B−gC(x)].
(9)
The requested factorization requires B to be invertible, an assumption omitted from the question. Under it, use the formal perturbative matrix logarithm near g=0:
det[B−gC(x)]=detBexp{Trlog[I−gB−1C(x)]}.
(10)
Consequently
Veff​(x)=−Trlog[I−gB−1C(x)]=m≥1∑​mgm​Tr[B−1C(x)]m.​
(11)
Expanding C(x) gives exactly the displayed coefficients Vk1​…km​(m)​=(m−1)!Tr[B−1C(k1​)⋯B−1C(km​)], and hence I(g)=detB∫dNxe−xTAx/2−Veff​(x). The logarithmic representation is a formal series, not a globally convergent logarithm for every x: the determinant can have zeros. The original determinant integral remains well-defined for singular B, but I(0)=0 and the requested normalized expansion then cannot be formed.
There is a second convention to specify. The xi​ are real, so a complex Hermitian A gives xTAx=xTAR​x, where AR​=ReA=(A+AT)/2 is real symmetric positive definite. Its Gaussian covariance matrix is AR−1​. It equals A−1 only if A is real symmetric. Thus
I(0)=detB(2π)N/2(detAR​)−1/2,I(0)I(g)​=Ex∼N(0,AR−1​)​[e−Veff​(x)].
(12)
The effective Feynman rules are: a bosonic contraction joins indices k,ℓ with (AR−1​)kℓ​; an m-leg vertex carries −gmSymVk1​…km​(m)​; sum all closed vacuum diagrams with their symmetry factors, including disconnected diagrams. The symmetrization is necessary because the xk​ commute: the trace tensor is cyclically invariant but need not be fully symmetric. The minus sign comes from expanding e−Veff​. Taking the logarithm of the normalized integral instead keeps only connected vacuum diagrams.
Equivalently, retain the fermions: an oriented contraction is (B−1)ij​=⟨θi​θˉj​⟩, the mixed vertex has weight +gCij(k)​, and every closed fermion loop gives a minus sign. The same bosonic covariance joins its x legs. Expanding these contractions recovers the trace vertices above. This is bosonic trace vertices from a finite fermionic determinant.

Use ℏ=c=1 and normalize the interaction of quartic scalar field theory as λϕ4/4!. For a renormalized n-point connected correlation function, adopt ϕB​=Zϕ1/2​ϕR​ and γϕ​=21​μdlogZϕ​/dμ. Independence of bare quantities from the renormalization scale gives the Callan-Symanzik equation
[μ∂μ​+β(λ)∂λ​+γm2​(λ)m2∂m2​+nγϕ​(λ)]GR(n)​=0,
(13)
where β=μdλ/dμ and γm2​=μdlogm2/dμ, with bare parameters fixed. A convention using proper vertices reverses the field-normalization term, so the choice of Green's function must be stated.
Solve it by characteristics: set μ(s)=μ0​es, dλ(s)/ds=β(λ(s)), and dlogm2(s)/ds=γm2​(λ(s)). At fixed external momenta,
GR(n)​(μ(s),λ(s),m2(s))=GR(n)​(μ0​,λ0​,m02​)exp[−n∫0s​γϕ​(λ(v))dv].
(14)
This is the characteristic solution of the multiplicative Callan-Symanzik equation. If the field anomalous dimension vanishes, the function is constant along the running-parameter characteristics. At a massless fixed point with constant anomalous dimension, the normalization factor is a power of μ/μ0​. Choosing the scale close to a characteristic external momentum avoids large perturbative logarithms.
For the supplied minimal subtraction scheme relation in dimensional regularization with d=4−ϵ, let a=3/(16π2) and write λB​=μϵ[λ+aλ2/ϵ+O(λ3)]. Differentiating at fixed bare coupling gives
0=ϵ(λ+ϵaλ2​)+β(λ)(1+ϵ2aλ​)+O(λ3).
(15)
A consistent expansion yields β=−ϵλ+aλ2+O(λ3). The −ϵλ term must be retained while it multiplies the pole; setting ϵ=0 prematurely loses the finite result. In four dimensions the one-loop quartic scalar beta function is
β(λ)=16π23λ2​+O(λ3).​
(16)
The one-loop two-point diagram is a momentum-independent tadpole diagram, so there is no field wavefunction renormalization at this order and γϕ​=O(λ2). Differentiating the supplied bare-mass relation in the same way gives γm2​=λ/(16π2)+O(λ2).
Integrating dλ/dlogμ=aλ2 gives the running coupling
λ(μ)=1−16π23λ(μ0​)​log(μ/μ0​)λ(μ0​)​.​
(17)
For positive coupling it grows toward the ultraviolet and decreases toward the infrared: the theory is not asymptotically free. Its one-loop Landau pole lies at μL​=μ0​exp[16π2/(3λ(μ0​))]. Perturbation theory fails before reaching that pole, so the pole in this approximation alone does not prove the existence of an exact physical divergence. The corresponding one-loop running mass satisfies
m2(μ)=m2(μ0​)[λ(μ0​)λ(μ)​]1/3.​
(18)
It follows from dlogm2/dλ=1/(3λ). The mass-independent subtraction scheme keeps these beta functions independent of the mass; physical low-energy matching can still require threshold treatment. Running parameters resum the leading logarithms within the regime of small coupling.
