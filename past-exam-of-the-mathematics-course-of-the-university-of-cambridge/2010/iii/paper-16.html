A locally ringed space is a topological space X equipped with a sheaf of commutative rings OX​ whose stalk OX,x​ at every point is a local ring. A morphism of locally ringed spaces (f,f#):(Y,OY​)→(X,OX​) consists of a continuous map f:Y→X and a morphism of sheaves of rings f#:OX​→f∗​OY​, with every induced stalk map OX,f(y)​→OY,y​ a local homomorphism: the inverse image of the target maximal ideal is the source maximal ideal. Here f∗​OY​ is the direct image sheaf, with sections OY​(f−1U) on U.
Let ϕ:A→B be a ring homomorphism. Define the map between the spectra of rings by
f(q)=ϕ−1(q).
(1)
The inverse image is a prime ideal, and f−1(D(a))=D(ϕ(a)). Thus f is a continuous map, because the principal open subsets form a basis. On these principal open subsets, define the structure sheaf map using the localizations
Aa​⟶Bϕ(a)​,arb​⟼ϕ(a)rϕ(b)​.
(2)
These maps commute with restrictions and consequently give a morphism of sheaves. At q, putting p=ϕ−1q, the stalk map is Ap​→Bq​. An element b/s belongs to its inverse image of qBq​ exactly when b∈p, so this is a local homomorphism. We have constructed a morphism of locally ringed spaces.
Conversely, take a morphism of locally ringed spaces f:SpecB→SpecA. Its map on global sections gives a ring homomorphism
ϕ:A=Γ(X,OX​)⟶Γ(Y,OY​)=B.
(3)
Locality of the stalk map implies, for every a∈A,
a∈f(q)⟺a/1∈mf(q)​⟺ϕ(a)/1∈qBq​⟺ϕ(a)∈q.
(4)
Hence the point map must be q↦ϕ−1q. On D(a), its structure sheaf map must send b/1 to ϕ(b)/1 and the inverse of a to the inverse of ϕ(a). Thus it is precisely the localization map constructed above. Agreement on this basis proves agreement of the morphisms of sheaves. In particular, the inducing ring homomorphism exists and is unique.
If ϕ is surjective, write B=A/I. The correspondence between prime ideals of A/I and prime ideals of A containing I identifies SpecB homeomorphically with the closed subset V(I): the principal open subsets correspond on both sides. Moreover, the stalk maps are the surjections Ap​→(A/I)p/I​, so OX​→f∗​OY​ is surjective.
For the converse, it is important not to assume that global sections preserve an arbitrary surjection of sheaves. Here the affine module-sheaf equivalence supplies the extra information: on every D(a),
(f∗​OY​)(D(a))=Bϕ(a)​=B⊗A​Aa​.
(5)
Thus f∗​OY​ is the quasi-coherent sheaf associated with the A-module B, and the cokernel sheaf of OX​→f∗​OY​ is associated with the module C=coker(A→B), since localization of a module is exact. Surjectivity of the morphism of sheaves gives Cp​=0 for every prime ideal p. This implies C=0: if 0=c∈C, its proper annihilator lies in a maximal ideal m, and then c/1=0 in Cm​. Therefore A→B is surjective. We have proved the closed immersion criterion for affine schemes:
ϕ surjective ⟺ f is a homeomorphism onto a closed subset and OX​↠f∗​OY​.​
(6)

A proper morphism of schemes is a separated morphism of finite type which is a universally closed morphism: after every base change, the resulting map of underlying topological spaces takes closed subsets to closed subsets.
The valuative criterion for properness, in the Noetherian setting used here, says that a finite type morphism f:X→Y is a proper morphism if and only if the following existence and uniqueness condition holds. For every valuation ring V, its fraction field K, and compatible morphisms of schemes a:SpecK→X and b:SpecV→Y with f∘a=b∣SpecK​, there is exactly one morphism of schemes h:SpecV→X such that h∣SpecK​=a and f∘h=b. All valuation rings are allowed; no discreteness assumption is needed in this formulation. The following arguments also use that each of the three defining conditions for a proper morphism is preserved by base change.

A finite morphism is a finite type morphism. It is also a separated morphism: on an affine scheme chart SpecB→SpecA, its diagonal morphism is represented by the surjective multiplication ring homomorphism B⊗A​B→B, and is therefore a closed immersion by Question 1.
We prove the lifting condition in the valuative criterion for properness. Choose an affine open subset of Y containing the image of the closed point of SpecV. Every point of SpecV is a generalization of its closed point, so the whole map factors through this affine open subset. Its inverse image under the finite morphism is SpecB, with B a finitely generated A-module. Every b∈B is integral over A: apply the determinant trick to multiplication by b on a finite set of module generators to obtain a monic polynomial which annihilates all generators and hence annihilates 1.
In the given valuative square, we have compatible ring homomorphisms A→V and B→K. The image in K of each b∈B satisfies a monic polynomial over V. To see directly why it belongs to V, suppose x∈K∖V. The defining property of a valuation ring gives x−1∈V, and it lies in the maximal ideal since it cannot be a unit. A monic equation for x, multiplied by x−n, would then express 1 as a sum of elements in that maximal ideal, a contradiction. This proves that valuation rings are integrally closed and that B→K factors through B→V. The factorization is unique because V↪K is injective. Thus the lift exists uniquely, proving
finite morphisms are proper.​

The composite of two finite type morphisms is of finite type: on suitable affine open subsets, take the algebra generators of the first algebra together with the images of the algebra generators of the second. To check separatedness, factor the diagonal morphism of the composite as
XΔf​​X×Y​X⟶X×Z​X.
(8)

Write f as the ring homomorphism A→B. By the permitted assumption, B is a finitely generated A-algebra. We will prove that every element of B is integral over A, including any nilpotent contribution. If B=0, there is nothing to prove.
We use the integral closure as an intersection of valuation rings in its general form: if an integral domain R lies in a field K, then its integral closure inside K is the intersection of all valuation rings of K containing R. Here K need not be the fraction field of R. One direction follows from valuation rings are integrally closed. For the other, if 0=x∈K is not integral, the ideal x−1R[x−1] is proper: an expression for 1 in that ideal, multiplied by a sufficiently large power of x, would be a monic equation for x. Choose a maximal ideal containing this ideal, and apply the valuation domination lemma to the corresponding local ring inside K. The resulting valuation ring V contains R and has x−1 in its maximal ideal, so x∈/V. This proves the claimed intersection criterion. The valuation domination lemma is obtained by choosing, using Zorn's lemma, a maximal local overring dominating the given local ring; the usual alternative between adjoining z and adjoining z−1 forces this maximal overring to be a valuation ring.
The Noetherian ring B has finitely many minimal prime ideals q1​,…,qr​. Set
Di​=B/qi​,Ki​=Frac(Di​),Ri​=im(A→Di​).
(15)
Take any valuation ring V of Ki​ containing Ri​. The maps B→Di​→Ki​ and A→Ri​→V form a square for the valuative criterion for properness. Its lift gives B→V, whose composite with V↪Ki​ is the original map. Consequently the image Di​ is contained in V. The intersection criterion proves that every element of Di​ is integral over Ri​.

Use the local-presentation definition of a quasi-coherent sheaf: locally it admits a presentation O(I)→O(J)→F→0 by direct sums of copies of the structure sheaf, with no finiteness requirement on the index sets. If M is an A-module, choose a free module presentation A(I)→A(J)→M→0. Exactness of localization of a module gives the corresponding local presentation of M. Hence a sheaf which is associated with a module on every affine open subset is a quasi-coherent sheaf.
For the converse, begin with a quasi-coherent sheaf and a local presentation on an affine open subset V=SpecR. The free sheaves have global sections R(I) and R(J): a section has locally finite support, and quasi-compactness of V makes its support finite globally. Part (i) identifies the presentation map with an R-module homomorphism. Exactness of localization then identifies its cokernel sheaf with MV​ for the module cokernel MV​. Thus there is an affine open cover on which F is associated with modules.
It remains to show this on an arbitrary affine open subset U=SpecA, rather than only the selected cover. Around each point of U, choose an affine neighborhood contained in U with such a local presentation. Choose a finite cover U=⋃i=1r​D(si​) refining these neighborhoods. On each D(si​) the restriction is associated with a module. Its restriction to D(si​sj​) is therefore the corresponding localization, and the same is true after intersecting with D(a). Put M=Γ(U,F). The sheaf gluing axiom gives
M=ker(∏i​Γ(D(si​),F)⟶∏i,j​Γ(D(si​sj​),F)),

Recall that a flasque sheaf has surjective restriction maps on every inclusion of open subsets. We first prove the flasque-kernel section-lifting lemma: in an exact sequence 0→A→B→C→0 of sheaves, with A a flasque sheaf, every section of C on any open subset V lifts to a section of B on V.
Fix such a section c. Order its lifts on open subsets W⊆V by extension. A chain has an upper bound by the sheaf gluing axiom, so Zorn's lemma gives a maximal lift bW​. If W=V, choose a point outside W and a neighborhood T⊆V on which c has a local lift bT​. On W∩T, the difference bT​−bW​ is a section of A. Extend it to T by flasqueness and subtract it from bT​. The corrected lift now agrees with bW​ and glues on W∪T, contradicting maximality. Thus W=V, proving the lemma.
It follows also that if both A and B are flasque sheaves, then C is a flasque sheaf. Given a section of C on U⊆V, first lift it to B on U, extend that lift to V, and project to C.
Next, an injective sheaf of modules I is a flasque sheaf. For U⊆V, let jU​ and jV​ denote the open inclusions into X. Extension by zero gives a monomorphism jU!​OU​→jV!​OV​: on every stalk it is either 0→OX,x​, the identity, or 0→0. By the adjunction for extension by zero,
HomOX​​(jU!​OU​,I)=Γ(U,I).
(31)
Injectivity therefore makes Γ(V,I)→Γ(U,I) surjective. This proves injective module sheaves are flasque.
Take an injective resolution of sheaves of F. Write C0=F and, successively,
0⟶Cr⟶Ir⟶Cr+1⟶0.
(32)
The initial C0 is a flasque sheaf; each Ir is a flasque sheaf; and the quotient argument proves inductively that all Cr are flasque sheaves. The section-lifting lemma makes each displayed sequence exact after global sections. Consequently the complex of global sections of the injective resolution of sheaves has zero positive cohomology. Since that complex defines sheaf cohomology,
Hp(X,F)=0(p>0).​

If X is an affine scheme, each reduced irreducible component is a closed subscheme, hence an affine scheme. We prove the converse by explicit affine gluing along closed subschemes.
First suppose X is the scheme-theoretic union of two closed subschemes which are affine schemes Y and Z: their defining ideal sheaves have zero intersection. Their scheme-theoretic intersection W is a closed subscheme of both, hence an affine scheme. Write
Y=SpecA,Z=SpecB,W=SpecC.
(35)
The two maps A→C and B→C are surjective. Form the fiber product of rings
R=A×C​B={(a,b):a∣W​=b∣W​}.
(36)
Both projections are surjective. Let I=ker(R→A) and J=ker(R→B). Then
I∩J=IJ=0,R/I=A,R/J=B,R/(I+J)=C.
(37)
For the last equality, the common image map R→C is surjective, and its kernel of a ring homomorphism consists exactly of pairs whose two coordinates map to zero. Therefore SpecR has closed pieces isomorphic to Y and Z, with intersection W. They cover it because each prime ideal contains I or J, as IJ=0.
A finite closed cover determines the topology: a subset is closed if and only if its intersections with both closed pieces are closed. The identifications of Y,Z,W therefore give a homeomorphism between X and SpecR. To check the structure sheaf, on X the elementary ideal-intersection sequence gives
0⟶OX​⟶i∗​OY​⊕j∗​OZ​(a,b)↦a∣W​−b∣W​​k∗​OW​⟶0.
(38)
It is exact on stalks, by IY​∩IZ​=0. The same sequence holds on SpecR, using I∩J=0. Under the homeomorphism, the two middle sheaves and their restriction maps identify, so their kernels identify as sheaves of rings. This identifies the structure sheaves, giving an isomorphism of schemes X≃SpecR. The intersection W need not be reduced.
Now a Noetherian scheme has finitely many irreducible components Y1​,…,Yr​. Induct on r. For r=1, the reduced scheme is its single reduced irreducible component, so is an affine scheme. For r>1, let Y=Y1​ and let Z be the reduced union of the remaining irreducible components. Its components are the corresponding Yi​, so Z is an affine scheme by induction. The defining ideal sheaves of Y and Z have zero intersection: their intersection is the ideal sheaf of the reduced union of all components, which is zero because X is reduced. The two-piece argument proves that X is an affine scheme. The empty scheme is Spec0 and causes no exception. Hence
X affine ⟺ every reduced irreducible component of X is affine.​
(39)

If ∣N∣=2, there is one copy of k in each of degrees 1 and 2 and the map between them is an isomorphism. These two cases are acyclic over every field, including characteristic two. H1(P,OP​(m))=0for every m,
(44)
and that H0(P,OP​)=k, H0(P,OP​(−d))=0, and H2(P,OP​)=0. Moreover the Laurent-monomial description of top cohomology on projective space gives a basis of H2(P,OP​(−d)) consisting of
t0−b0​​t1−b1​​t2−b2​​,bi​≥1,b0​+b1​+b2​=d.
(45)
For d≥3, subtracting one from each bi​ counts these as (2d−1​). For d=1,2 there are no such triples, and the formula (d−1)(d−2)/2 is also zero.
Use the long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology of the initial sequence, and cohomology under a closed immersion to identify the terms for i∗​OX​. The vanishings just proved give
H0(X,OX​)≃k,H1(X,OX​)≃H2(P,OP​(−d)).
(46)
Therefore
dimk​H0(X,OX​)=1,dimk​H1(X,OX​)=2(d−1)(d−2)​.​
(47)
This proves the cohomology of a projective plane hypersurface without imposing smoothness, irreducibility or reducedness. In particular, the second number is an arithmetic genus, not a claim about the genus of a normalization.

Take the affine scheme X=Ak1​, its function field K=k(t), and the constant sheaf K with value K. By Question 4(ii), K is a flasque sheaf. At the two distinct closed rational points p0​=0 and p1​=1, let S0​,S1​ be skyscraper sheaves with value K: on an open subset V, Si​(V)=K if pi​∈V and is zero otherwise. Both are flasque sheaves.
There is a natural morphism of sheaves
ρ:K⟶S0​⊕S1​,
(48)
which sends a constant rational function to the same element in every summand present on the open subset. At pi​, its stalk map is the identity K→K, and at any other point the target stalk is zero. Thus ρ is a surjection of sheaves, although it is not surjective on global sections. Put F=kerρ.
This can even be an example of sheaves of OX​-modules: let regular functions act on each copy of K by multiplication after embedding them in K. At the closed points this action is multiplication by a rational function, not evaluation at the point. With these actions, ρ is OX​-linear.
The exact sequence 0→F→K→S0​⊕S1​→0, together with flasque acyclicity, gives
0⟶H0(X,F)⟶Ka↦(a,a)​K2⟶H1(X,F)⟶0.
(49)
Hence H0(X,F)=0 and H1(X,F)=K2/ΔK≃K=0. In contrast, use the one-member affine open cover U={X}. Its alternating Čech cochain complex has no positive-degree terms, so Hˇ1(U,F)=0. We have the explicit discrepancy
Hˇ1({X},F)=0=H1(X,F)≃k(t).​
