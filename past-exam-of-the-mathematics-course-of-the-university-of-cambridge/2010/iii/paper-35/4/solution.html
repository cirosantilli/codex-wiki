Let tk​∈{0,1} encode the truth value of Xk​. For a Boolean literal ℓ, write Lℓ​(t)=tk​ if ℓ=Xk​ and Lℓ​(t)=1−tk​ if ℓ=Xk​. A clause is true precisely when the sum of its literal values is at least one. Thus the integer programming formulation of satisfiability is the feasibility problem
tk​∈{0,1},j=1∑Mi​​Lxij​​(t)≥1(1≤i≤N),​

with objective zero. Every feasible binary vector gives a satisfying assignment of the Boolean satisfiability problem, and conversely. An empty clause gives 0≥1 and hence infeasibility.
For maximum satisfiability, introduce binary variables qi​ marking clauses selected as satisfied and solve
maxi=1∑N​qi​,tk​,qi​∈{0,1},qi​≤j=1∑Mi​​Lxij​​(t)(1≤i≤N).​
(43)
A false clause forces qi​=0, while a true clause permits qi​=1. At an optimum every permitted qi​ is one, since increasing it improves the objective without affecting another constraint. Therefore the objective is exactly the maximum number of simultaneously true clauses. Repeated literals cause no difficulty, and a clause containing both signs of a variable is always true.
For the literal-frequency greedy approximation for MAX-SAT, keep only clauses not already satisfied and remove literals made false. Count occurrence in clauses, rather than multiplicity within a clause. At a step let a be the number of current clauses containing the chosen literal z, and b the number containing its opposite z. Since z has maximum clause frequency among all remaining literals, b≤a.
Setting z true permanently satisfies exactly the a clauses containing it. Some other clauses may become empty and are then permanently lost. Every newly empty clause previously contained z, so their number d satisfies
d≤b≤a.
(44)
A clause containing both signs is satisfied and cannot be one of the lost clauses. Repeat this accounting at every step. Satisfied clauses are removed, so no success is counted twice; each lost nonempty original clause becomes empty at exactly one step. If G is the total number satisfied and D the number of initially nonempty clauses lost, summing the inequalities gives D≤G. Every initially nonempty clause eventually falls into one of these two classes. Writing their number as N+​, we get
G+D=N+​,G≥N+​/2.
(45)
Even an optimal assignment can satisfy at most N+​ clauses. Consequently
G≥21​N+​≥21​OPT.​
(46)
Thus Greedy is a 1/2-approximation in the convention stated in the question. The proof also covers initial empty clauses by excluding them from N+​; they are unsatisfiable for every algorithm.
