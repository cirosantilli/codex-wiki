Write ϵ=1−ρs​/ρ>0 and Δv=1/ρs​−1/ρ=ϵ/ρs​. When the ice sphere grows by volume 4πa2a˙dt, it consumes liquid volume (ρs​/ρ)4πa2a˙dt. The difference is expelled from the fixed cavity. Thus mass conservation fixes the outward Darcy flux in the surrounding porous medium:
4πr2ur​=4πϵa2a˙,ur​=r2ϵa2a˙​(r≥R).
(1)
The Darcy law ur​=−(Π/μ)∂r​p and p(∞)=pm​ imply
p(r)−pm​=Πrμϵa2a˙​.
(2)
Matching at the cavity wall, whose internal pressure is uniform, gives
p−pm​=Πμ​(1−ρρs​​)Ra2a˙​.​
(3)
The solid and liquid pressures are equal when the Gibbs--Thomson relation is neglected; nevertheless the coexistence temperature changes with their common pressure because their specific volumes differ. Along coexistence, equality of chemical potentials gives
−(sl​−ss​)dT+(1/ρ−1/ρs​)dp=0,sl​−ss​=L/Tm​.
(4)
Hence the common-pressure melting-point slope is dTm​/dp=−Tm​Δv/L. To first order in the pressure change,
Ti​=Tm​−γp​(p−pm​),γp​=LTm​Δv​>0.
(5)
The Tm​ multiplying this coefficient is absolute temperature, approximately 273.15K for water; a temperature difference may equally be measured in kelvin or degrees Celsius. Substituting the numerical value 0 from the Celsius scale into a thermodynamic denominator would be incorrect.
In the quasi-stationary thermal conduction approximation, neglect advective heat transport and use the stated effective conductivity k outside the sphere. The radial Laplace equation gives the bounded solid temperature and the liquid/porous-region temperature:
Ts​(r)=Ti​(0≤r<a),Tl​(r)=T∞​+(Ti​−T∞​)ra​(r>a).​
(6)
This treats the exterior as a uniform conducting medium, as required by the printed reduction; distinct cavity and porous-medium conductivities would give an additional thermal resistance. There is no solid-side heat flux in the bounded quasi-stationary solution. The Stefan condition therefore becomes
ρs​La˙=−k∂r​Tl​(a)=ak(Ti​−T∞​)​.
(7)
Eliminate Ti​ and p to obtain
(a+KRa2​)a˙=ρs​LkΔT​,ΔT=Tm​−T∞​,K=(Δv)2kTm​μL2Π​.
(8)
For x=a/R, t=t0​τ and t0​=R2ρs​L/(kΔT), this is
(x+Kx2​)dτdx​=1.​
(9)
The pressure follows either from the Darcy flux or the Stefan condition:
p−pm​=p∗x+Kx​,p∗=γp​ΔT​=ρs​LTm​ΔT​(1−ρρs​​)−1.​
(10)
In particular Ti​−T∞​=ΔTK/(x+K): rising pressure progressively reduces the available undercooling. This is spherical freezing inside a porous cavity, whose fixed hydraulic outlet radius R distinguishes it from freezing directly through a porous matrix.
For the idealized zero-radius start, integration gives
τ=2x2​+3Kx3​,0≤x≤1.​
(11)
A finite seed replaces the right side by its difference from the initial value. The reduction ends when the sphere fills the cavity.
For K≫1, hydraulic drainage is easy and the pressure feedback is small throughout the cavity. The leading expressions are
p(x)=pm​+Kp∗​x+O(K−2p∗),x(t)≃t0​2t​​,a(t)≃ρs​L2kΔT​t​.​
(12)
This is ordinary conduction-controlled spherical solidification, with a completion time asymptotic to t0​/2.
For K≪1, distinguish the initial crossover from the pressure-dominated regime. At x≪K, equivalently τ≪K2, the same square-root growth applies and p−pm​≃p∗x/K. At K≪x≤1, equivalently K2≪τ≲1/K,
x(t)≃(t0​3Kt​)1/3,p(x)=pm​+p∗[1−xK​+O(x2K2​)].​
(13)
The interface approaches T∞​, the pressure approaches the finite coexistence value pm​+p∗, and growth becomes hydraulically limited. The leading completion time is t0​/(3K). The cube-root approximation is not valid at the instant of nucleation, where the initial square-root regime must be retained.

Use the one-sided thermal conduction model encoded by the requested dispersion relation: neglect solid-side perturbation heat flux, take equal phase densities, and let the normal point from solid into liquid. Write the isotropic linear kinetic undercooling law using the solidification attachment mobility M>0:
U=M(Tm​−γT​K−Ti​),Ti​=Tm​−MU​−γT​K,γT​=ρLTm​γ​.​
(14)
Here γ is solid-liquid surface energy, K is the sum of principal curvatures, and Tm​ is absolute temperature. Positive curvature convex into liquid lowers the equilibrium freezing temperature through the Gibbs--Thomson relation. The planar base state has K=0.
Set ΔT=Tm​−T∞​>0 and define
U0​=MΔT,ℓ=κ/U0​,t0​=κ/U02​,θ=ΔTT−T∞​​,S=cp​ΔTL​,Γ=ΔTℓγT​​.
(15)
The paper's S is the latent-to-sensible heat ratio, the reciprocal of the common Stefan number convention. Below, velocities, lengths and growth rates are dimensionless in these scales. In the travelling coordinate ξ=X−Vt, the liquid heat equation gives θ0′′​+Vθ0′​=0, while the bounded solid steady field is constant. Matching a decaying liquid profile to the Stefan condition SV=−θ0′​(0) gives
θl​(ξ)=Se−Vξ(ξ>0),θs​=S,Ti​=T∞​+cp​L​.​
(16)
The kinetic undercooling condition is θi​=1−V. Thus
V=1−S>0,U=M(Tm​−T∞​−cp​L​)=cp​ML​(S−1−1).​
(17)
The speed is constant for S<1, determined by S and the attachment coefficient in the specified scales. Thermal diffusivity sets the exponential decay length κ/U, not the selected speed. Including solid-side perturbation conduction would change the stability equation below.
For linear stability analysis, corrugate the interface to ξ=ζeσt+iαy and write the liquid perturbation as A1​e−pξeσt+iαy. The liquid heat equation requires
p2−Vp=σ+α2,p=2V+V2+4(σ+α2)​​.​
(18)
For this graph the linearized curvature is α2ζeσt+iαy. Evaluate the temperature on the displaced interface: its perturbation is A1​+θ0′​(0)ζ=A1​−SVζ. The kinetic undercooling law therefore gives
A1​=(SV−σ−Γα2)ζ.
(19)
Likewise the derivative evaluated at the displaced surface is θ0′​(0)+θ0′′​(0)ζ−pA1​, with θ0′′​(0)=SV2. The Stefan condition at perturbation order reads
Sσζ=pA1​−SV2ζ.
(20)
Elimination proves the one-sided kinetic solidification dispersion:
σ(1+pS​)=SV−Γα2−pSV2​.​
(21)
The destabilizing term comes from a protrusion encountering colder liquid; capillarity and kinetic relaxation oppose it.
At S=0, V=1 and the base liquid has no thermal contrast generated by latent heat. The interface obeys σ=−Γα2​: positive surface energy damps all nonzero modes, while Γ=0 is neutral at this order. Here the thermal perturbation amplitude vanishes, so the auxiliary decay exponent is immaterial to that pure kinetic relaxation. At S=1, V=0 and the formal limit gives
σ(1+σ+α2​1​)=−Γα2.
(22)
For Γ>0 it has a negative real branch −α2<σ<0 and no growing branch; at Γ=0 the interface branch is neutral. Strictly, V=0 is a singular endpoint of the travelling profile: no finite-width stationary exponential connects θi​=1 to the far-field value zero. These stability statements describe the limit of the travelling-front model.
For 0<S<1, assume σ=O(α2) at small wavenumber. Expand
p=V+Vσ+α2​+O(α4),pV2​=V−Vσ+α2​+O(α4).
(23)
Substituting into the dispersion relation cancels Sσ/V on both sides and gives
σ=(VS​−Γ)α2+O(α4).​
(24)
At the capillary stability threshold for a kinetic freezing front Γ=S/V, continuing the same expansion gives σ=−2Sα4/V3+O(α6): the nonzero long waves are damped even at threshold.
For large wavenumber, multiply the dispersion relation by p and eliminate σ using the thermal equation. The exact algebraic form is
p3+(S−V)p2+[(Γ−1)α2−2SV]p−Sα2+SV2=0,σ=p2−Vp−α2.
(25)
It prevents the uncontrolled replacement p≃α when σ itself is of order α2. In the usual small-capillarity range the leading results are
Γ=0:σ=SV−αSV​+O(α−2),​
(26)
0<Γ<1:σ=−Γα2+1−Γ​SΓ​α+O(1).​
(27)
For the second result, set p=1−Γ​α+d+O(α−1) in the cubic; its quadratic-order balance gives 2d−V=SΓ/(1−Γ). Thus positive capillarity stabilizes sufficiently short waves. If also Γ≪1 and ∣σ∣≪α2, the useful reduced approximation is
σ≃1+S/αSV−Γα2−SV2/α​;
(28)
its finite terms should not be treated as a uniform expansion for arbitrary fixed Γ.
For completeness, large dimensionless capillarity has different balances. At Γ=1, p∼S1/3α2/3 and σ=−α2+S2/3α4/3+o(α4/3). For 1<Γ<1+2S/V, p→p∗​≡S/(Γ−1)>V/2, so σ=−α2+p∗2​−Vp∗​+O(α−2). At Γ=1+2S/V, the real branch reaches p=V/2: for S<1/3 its growth rate is −α2−V2/4+O(α−4), and for S=1/3 that endpoint is exact. For S>1/3 at this boundary, or for Γ>1+2S/V, the selected real-p normal mode ceases to exist at sufficiently large wavenumber; the full diffusion problem includes a thermal continuous spectrum and cannot be assigned a real isolated interface growth rate by this ansatz there. Indeed, writing the bulk thermal perturbation as θ1​=e−Vξ/2u reduces its heat equation to ut​=uξξ​−(α2+V2/4)u, whose half-line diffusion modes have decay rates −α2−V2/4−k2 for k≥0. The thermal branch endpoint is p=V/2. Loss of a damped isolated interface mode is not a morphological instability.
The approximate instability condition is already clear from the long-wave coefficient: Γ<S/V, equivalently S>Γ/(1+Γ). In fact it is exact for growing modes of the present one-sided model. At σ=0, let p0​=[V+V2+4α2​]/2, so α2=p0​(p0​−V). The right side of the dispersion relation becomes
α2(p02​SV​−Γ).
(29)
For real σ≥0, the residual F=σ(1+S/p)−SV+Γα2+SV2/p is strictly increasing, since
dσdF​=1+p2(2p−V)S(p2+α2−V2)​≥1.
(30)
Therefore a unique positive root exists precisely when Γ<SV/p02​. For 0<Γ<S/V, the unstable band is
0<α<αc​,αc2​=pc​(pc​−V),pc​=SV/Γ​.​
(31)
There are no unstable oscillatory roots hidden by this real-root test. Indeed, if p=a+ib and Reσ≥0, the thermal equation implies a≥V. For b=0, the imaginary parts of the two equations would require 2a−V=S(V−Γα2)/∣p+S∣2≤SV<V, contradicting 2a−V≥V. Thus growing modes have real p and real σ.
The sketch shows zero capillarity approaching the positive plateau SV, weak capillarity giving a positive band followed by short-wave damping, and threshold/stronger capillarity giving damping at every nonzero wavenumber. The zero-wavenumber mode is a translation. As S→1 with fixed positive Γ, both the unstable bandwidth and the positive growth rates shrink to zero; the small-wavenumber expansion is not uniform in that limit.

Use a one-sided top-hat plume model with Boussinesq approximation and neglect the solid/liquid density difference in the interfacial volume source. Define the positive ablation speed U=−a˙, volume flux per unit wall span Q=bw, and kinematic momentum flux J=bw2. Let ambient entrainment per height be E=εw, with entrainment coefficient ε, and take ρ=ρ∞​[1+βC​(C−C∞​)] for the salinity contribution to density. Conservation of volume and vertical momentum, with negligible wall stress and negligible vertical momentum of entering fluid, gives
dzd(bw)​=εw+U=εw−a˙,dzd(bw2)​=gβC​(C∞​−C)b.​
(32)
Only the ocean-facing edge entrains. Using two edges would change the entrainment coefficient in these formulas.
To derive the heat balance, choose liquid specific enthalpy hl​=cp​T+L and solid specific enthalpy hs​=cp​Ts​. A horizontal slab receives ambient volume Edz with enthalpy h∞​=cp​T∞​+L, and solid mass-equivalent volume Udz with enthalpy hs​. Thus
dzd​[Q(cp​T+L)]=E(cp​T∞​+L)+Ucp​Ts​.
(33)
Subtract (cp​T∞​+L)Q′ and divide by cp​:
dzd​[bw(T−T∞​)]=a˙[cp​L​+T∞​−Ts​].​
(34)
Similarly, the incoming salt flux is EC∞​+UCs​, so (QC)′=EC∞​+UCs​. Subtracting C∞​Q′ proves
dzd​[bw(C−C∞​)]=a˙(C∞​−Cs​).​
(35)
Both anomalies are negative for fresh, cold melt entering the ocean. These balances account for the heat needed to melt the entering ice, rather than treating it as already-liquid water at Ts​.
Now use L/cp​≫T∞​−Ts​, the large latent-to-sensible heat ratio convention of the paper. Put ΔC=C∞​−Cs​>0. The stated small plume-deficit limits allow us to replace plume T,C by their ambient values in the diffusive interfacial fluxes. The Stefan condition and interfacial salt balance are then
cp​L​U=δκ​(T∞​−Ti​),U(Ci​−Cs​)=δD​(C∞​−Ci​).
(36)
For the compositional Péclet number P≡Pec​=Uδ/D, solve the second equation and impose the liquidus:
Ci​=Cs​+1+PΔC​,Ti​=Tm​−1+PΓΔC​.​
(37)
Substitution in the heat balance gives the diffusive glacier-ablation interface law
cp​κLD​P=T∞​−Tm​+1+PΓ(C∞​−Cs​)​,a˙=−δD​P.​
(38)
Here Γ is the liquidus slope, unrelated to the surface-energy parameter of the previous question. If AT​=LD/(cp​κ), Θ=T∞​−Tm​ and BT​=ΓΔC, the physical root is
P=2AT​Θ−AT​+(AT​+Θ)2+4AT​BT​​​.​
(39)
It is positive when Θ+BT​>0, exactly the condition that the ocean is warmer than its own liquidus. Since δ and ambient properties are fixed, U is constant with height in this approximation.
For the melting-driven saline wall plume, measure height Z=z−z0​ from a virtual origin. The integrated anomaly balances are
Q(T∞​−T)=Ucp​L​Z,Q(C∞​−C)=UΔCZ.
(40)
In the specified small-deficit regime the meltwater volume is negligible compared with entrainment. Indeed, the solution below gives (C∞​−C)/ΔC=4U/(3εw), so a small compositional plume deficit implies U≪εw. Thus retain Q′=εw for the leading power laws, although U remains essential in the salt and heat sources. Try b=BZ, w=WZq. The salt deficit is proportional to Z−q, and the momentum balance has powers Z2q=Z1−q, hence q=1/3. The volume balance gives (4/3)BW=εW, and the momentum coefficient gives (5/3)BW2=gβC​UΔC/W. Therefore
b=43​εZ,w=WZ1/3,W=(5ε4gβC​UΔC​)1/3,​
(41)
C∞​−C=3εW4UΔC​Z−1/3,T∞​−T=3cp​εW4UL​Z−1/3.​
(42)
These are entrainment-dominated power laws, not an exact solution with the U term retained in the volume equation. They fail close to the virtual origin, where the formal temperature and salinity deficits diverge. Their buoyancy source is distributed melting along the wall, unlike a line plume with a constant buoyancy flux from a localized source.
The profiles rise from Ti​,Ci​ across the laminar boundary layer to the almost-ambient plume values T,C, then approach T∞​,C∞​ across the entraining edge. The sketch uses the linear-gradient approximation implicit in the prescribed diffusive fluxes; it is not an exact normal advection–diffusion profile at large P.
For P≪1,
Ci​=C∞​−ΔCP+O(P2),Ti​=TL​(C∞​)+BT​P+O(P2),P≃AT​+BT​Θ+BT​​.​
(43)
The last formula retains both first-order terms and is appropriate near the no-ablation threshold. Salt diffusion keeps the interface saline, and freezing-point depression allows ice dissolution in saline water even when T∞​<Tm​. Latent heat is still needed, but the thermodynamic permission to ablate comes from the depressed liquidus; small P does not mean a negligible energy cost.
For P≫1, Ci​−Cs​∼ΔC/P and Ti​∼Tm​−BT​/P. Rapid production of fresh melt dilutes the interface, so its temperature approaches the pure-ice melting point. In the usual thermally driven regime Θ>0 and Θ≫BT​/P,
P≃Θ/AT​,U≃Lδcp​κ​(T∞​−Tm​).​
(44)
This is predominantly thermal melting. More generally the retained large-P balance is AT​P≃Θ+BT​/P. For example, Θ=0 gives P∼BT​/AT​​, which can be large while the driving term is still saline freezing-point depression. Thus the melting/dissolution interpretation must also consider the ocean's temperature relative to Tm​, not the compositional Péclet number alone.

Take G,V,D,m,C0​>0 and TE​<0, with the liquidus referenced to the pure-water melting point. The solid-liquid segregation coefficient kD​=0 means that newly formed ice contains no salt. A steady sharp front producing only pure ice cannot remove the incoming solute flux. More explicitly, in the liquid the steady solute flux is JC​=−VC−DCz​=−VC0​, whereas in pure solid it is zero. Interfacial salt conservation would require VC0​=0, a contradiction. An equilibrium mushy layer avoids this by carrying concentrated pore liquid down to the eutectic region, where a salt-bearing solid assemblage can form.
Use equal densities and common downward translation velocity −V. Write q=1−ϕ for the liquid fraction; ϕ here is the solid fraction. Local liquidus equilibrium in the mush requires
Cm​(z)=−mTE​+Gz​.
(45)
Above a stationary roof h0​, the liquid advection-diffusion equation is −VCz​=DCzz​, with solution
Cl​(z)=C0​+(Ci​−C0​)e−V(z−h0​)/D(z≥h0​).
(46)
The marginal equilibrium at a mush-liquid boundary condition is −mCl′​(h0​)=G, hence Ci​=C0​+GD/(mV). Matching the temperature and liquidus at the roof gives
h0​=G−mC0​−TE​​−VD​.​
(47)
A positive equilibrium mush requires (−mC0​−TE​)/G>D/V; otherwise this positive-thickness construction does not apply.
Including solute diffusion in the liquid pore space, the steady solute flux in the mush is
JC​=−VqC−DqCz​=−VC0​.
(48)
Thus q[C+(D/V)Cz​]=C0​. Inserting Cz​=−G/m and the value of h0​ yields
q=C−GD/(mV)C0​​=mC0​/G+h0​−zmC0​/G​,ϕ(z)=mC0​/G+h0​−zh0​−z​.​
(49)
This is the diffusive steadily pulled equilibrium mush. Omitting the diffusive contribution would give a different solid-fraction distribution.
The imposed temperature and local liquidus coincide throughout the mush. In the liquid put y=V(z−h0​)/D≥0. Their difference is
T(z)−TL​(Cl​(z))=VGD​(y−1+e−y)≥0,
(50)
with equality and matching first derivatives at the roof. The inequality follows because the derivative of y−1+e−y is 1−e−y≥0. Thus the liquid is not constitutionally supercooled. In the phase diagram, the pore-liquid trajectory follows the liquidus from the eutectic composition CE​=−TE​/m to (Ci​,Ti​), then curves into the liquid region and approaches C=C0​ as height increases. Pure-ice crystals have composition zero; the trajectory denotes the liquid composition, not the bulk mush composition.
After pulling stops, the imposed temperature keeps the pore-liquid composition fixed wherever the material remains mush. Solute conservation becomes
∂t​[(1−ϕ)C]=D∂z​[(1−ϕ)Cz​],C=−mTE​+Gz​.
(51)
Because Czz​=0, this reduces to
(z+GTE​​)ϕt​=Dϕz​.​
(52)
The method of characteristics uses dz/dt=−D/(z+TE​/G) and dϕ/dt=0. Hence
dtd​[z2+G2TE​​z+2Dt]=0,ϕ=f(η),η=z2+G2TE​​z+2Dt.​
(53)
These are the solute-diffusion characteristics in a fixed-temperature mush; their validity is local to the coexisting solid–liquid region.
For an explicit initial-data solution, define H=−TE​/G, c=mC0​/G and d=D/V, so h0​=H−c−d. A characteristic through (z,t) has initial foot
zi​=H−(H−z)2+2Dt​.
(54)
While that foot lies in the original mush and no phase boundary has removed the point,
ϕ(z,t)=1−(H−z)2+2Dt​−dc​.​
(55)
This recovers the initial distribution and shows that interior ice fraction increases as salt diffuses upward from colder, more concentrated pore liquid.
If one simply transports the initial zero-solid-fraction level, f(η(h0​,0))=0 implies the printed formal law
(h+GTE​​)2=(h0​+GTE​​)2−2Dt.​
(56)
On the physical concentration branch h<H, this is h(t)=H−(H−h0​)2−2Dt​, so h˙=D/(H−h)>0. It starts moving above h0​ immediately.
This formal continuation cannot be the physical mush–liquid roof of a column sealed to salt by the membrane. The no-flux constraint in a fixed-temperature mush is JC​=−DqCz​=0 at the membrane. At a smooth zero-fraction equilibrium roof, however, q=1 and Cz​=−G/m, giving JC​=DG/m=0. Moreover the formal roof has left the membrane-bounded domain. The printed interface law is incompatible with a salt-impermeable membrane when interpreted as the physical roof. Its derivation above is a characteristic extrapolation; it does not enforce the stated boundary condition. If “impermeable” were intended to block bulk flow but allow salt diffusion, a different boundary condition would need to be specified.
The closed-column physical sketch is consequently different. Salt transported upward accumulates below the membrane and produces a liquid brine cap; the cap composition need not lie on the liquidus at every height. Its lower boundary can have a finite jump in solid fraction, so it need not follow the initial zero-fraction characteristic. Near the cold solid base, zero salt supply from below requires the liquid fraction to vanish, and a pure-ice region develops. In the surviving mush, the characteristic formula increases ϕ relative to its initial value. An intermediate profile therefore has pure ice at the bottom, a remaining mush with elevated solid fraction, and liquid at the top. The plotted intermediate roof is schematic, not claimed to obey the inconsistent formal law.
One can characterize the final state quantitatively if the solid base also admits no salt diffusion, as in the usual pure-solid/no-solid-diffusion closure. In a stationary column, solute flux is constant and is zero at the sealed roof. A finite-width equilibrium mush would have JC​=DGq/m, forcing q=0 everywhere in that region; it therefore cannot remain mush. The final state is pure ice for 0<z<hf​ and a uniform-composition liquid cap for hf​<z<h0​. At their boundary,
Cf​=mG​(H−hf​),ϕf​(z)={1,0,​0<z<hf​,hf​<z<h0​.​​
(57)
The cap is liquid because T(z)>−mCf​=T(hf​) above its base. The fixed external thermal field can supply or remove the heat involved; this is a solute-budget determination rather than an isolated enthalpy budget.
The final salt budget of a sealed mush column determines hf​. Initially
N=∫0h0​​(1−ϕ0​)Cm​dz=C0​[h0​+dlog(1+ch0​​)],
(58)
since (1−ϕ0​)Cm​=C0​[1+d/(c+h0​−z)]. Conservation requires Cf​(h0​−hf​)=N. Writing the final cap depth ℓf​=h0​−hf​, the equation and its unique positive root are
ℓf​(ℓf​+c+d)=c[h0​+dlog(1+ch0​​)],
(59)
ℓf​=2(c+d)2+4c[h0​+dlog(1+h0​/c)]​−(c+d)​,hf​=h0​−ℓf​.​
(60)
The initial salt integral is less than CE​h0​, so 0<ℓf​<h0​ and 0<hf​<h0​; the resulting Cf​<CE​ remains on the ice–brine branch above the eutectic. If the basal eutectic region is instead allowed to exchange solute with the column, this salt budget must include that flux and the numerical final height is not fixed by the printed data alone. No universal physical h(t) follows from the interior characteristic equation without the cap and basal boundary conditions.
