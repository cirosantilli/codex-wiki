We first prove the algebraic extension step needed for Hahn-Banach separation theorem. Suppose p is a finite sublinear functional on a real vector space and g is a linear functional on a vector subspace W, with g≤p there. For v∈/W, choose a real number c between
supw∈W​{g(w)−p(w−v)}andinfw∈W​{p(w+v)−g(w)}.
(1)
This interval is nonempty: for any w,z∈W,
g(w)+g(z)=g(w+z)≤p(w+z)≤p(w−v)+p(z+v).
(2)
Taking w=0 or z=0 also bounds the endpoints by finite numbers. Define g​(w+tv)=g(w)+tc. For t>0, the upper bound for c, applied to w/t, proves g​(w+tv)≤p(w+tv). For t<0, writing t=−s and using the lower bound at w/s proves the same inequality. The case t=0 is the original domination. Thus a dominated linear functional extends over one further direction. Order all dominated extensions by extension of their domains. A chain has its union as an upper bound, so Zorn's lemma supplies a maximal extension. The one-direction argument shows its domain is the whole vector space. This proves the required algebraic Hahn-Banach theorem, rather than assuming the separation result.
Choose a∈A, and put C=A−a. This is a radially open convex set containing zero. It is absorbing: along each line through zero, sufficiently small multiples of any vector lie in C. Its Minkowski functional
p(x)=inf{t>0:x∈tC}
(3)
is finite and nonnegative. Positive homogeneity follows by rescaling t. If x∈sC and y∈tC, convexity gives x+y∈(s+t)C, proving subadditivity by taking infima. Moreover,
C={x:p(x)<1}.
(4)
Indeed, p(x)<1 gives x∈sC for some s<1, hence x∈C by convexity and 0∈C. Conversely, radial openness at x∈C permits (1+ϵ)x∈C for some ϵ>0, giving p(x)<1.
Since A∩U=∅, a∈/U and u−a∈/C for every u∈U. Define a linear functional on U+Ra by
g(u+ta)=−t.
(5)
It is well defined because a∈/U. For t<0, write s=−t; then p(u+ta)=sp(u/s−a)≥s=g(u+ta). For t≥0, domination follows from g(u+ta)≤0≤p(u+ta). The extension step therefore gives L:V→R with L≤p, L∣U​=0 and L(a)=−1. If x∈A, then
L(x)+1=L(x−a)≤p(x−a)<1,
(6)
so L(x)<0. Consequently
H=kerL⊇U,H∩A=∅.​
(7)
Because L(a)=0, this kernel of a linear map has codimension one and is a hyperplane. This establishes the geometric Hahn-Banach theorem for radially open sets without assuming a norm or a topology on V.
For the convex function, use its strict epigraph in V×R. It is nonempty and convex, and does not contain (0,0). It is also radially open: on every affine line, a finite convex function is continuous. To justify this last fact, convexity orders secant slopes; on a smaller interval the slopes are bounded above and below by slopes to two fixed exterior endpoints, giving a local Lipschitz bound. Thus t↦f(x+ty)−β−ts is continuous, and a strict negative value persists near t=0.
Apply the separation theorem with the zero subspace. A nonzero separating linear functional has form L(x,β)=g(x)+cβ. Its values on the convex epigraph cannot have both signs, since a line segment would then meet its kernel. Choose the sign so that L>0 on the epigraph. At x=0 and β>0 this gives c>0. Letting β decrease to f(x) yields g(x)+cf(x)≥0. Therefore
l(x)=−g(x)/c≤f(x)for every x∈V.​
(8)
The resulting linear functional need not be nonzero: for example, the zero functional is the only linear minorant of f(x)=x2 on R.
Now suppose the norm and continuity hypotheses hold. Domination at x and −x gives
−f(−x)≤l(x)≤f(x).
(9)
If xn​∈kerl and xn​→x in norm, apply this inequality to x−xn​. Since l(x−xn​)=l(x) and both bounding values tend to f(0)=0, we obtain l(x)=0. The kernel of a linear map is therefore closed.
Finally, a linear functional with closed kernel on a normed vector space is continuous. If l=0, this is immediate. Otherwise choose v with l(v)=1 and put M=kerl. Closedness gives d=dist(v,M)>0. For l(x)=0, x/l(x)−v∈M, so d≤∥x∥/∣l(x)∣. The same resulting bound holds when l(x)=0:
∣l(x)∣≤d−1∥x∥.​
(10)
This proves continuity and explains exactly how closedness of the kernel is used.

We construct a normalized positive translation-invariant functional on C(G) and then represent it by a measure. The main step is a finite matching in a graph argument, for which we establish all the covering-net properties.
Let V be an open neighbourhood of the identity in the compact topological group G. The sets xV, x∈G, form an open cover. Compactness supplies a finite set F with G=FV. Among all such finite sets choose one of smallest cardinality, denoted m(V). This is a minimal left covering net of a compact group. If g∈G, then G=gFV, and gF has the same cardinality. Hence gF is also minimal for the same V.
Let F,F′ be any two minimal left covering nets of a compact group for V. They both have cardinality m(V). Form a bipartite graph by joining x∈F to y∈F′ whenever xV∩yV=∅. For a subset S⊆F, let N(S) be its set of neighbours. Every point of SV lies in some yV with y∈F′; that y is a neighbour of a member of S. Therefore
SV⊆N(S)V,
(19)
and (F∖S)∪N(S) is still a left covering set. By minimality,
m(V)≤∣(F∖S)∪N(S)∣≤m(V)−∣S∣+∣N(S)∣.
(20)
Thus ∣N(S)∣≥∣S∣. Hall's marriage theorem gives a bijection π:F→F′ with xV∩π(x)V=∅ for every x. If xv=π(x)v′ at an intersection point, then
x−1π(x)=vv′−1∈VV−1.​

This proves the matching minimal covering nets of a compact group property, including the estimate needed below.

The estimate holds for every g∈G; no commutativity of G is being assumed.
Direct the open identity neighbourhoods by reverse inclusion and choose a minimal left covering net of a compact group FV​ for each. The values IFV​​(f) lie in the closed complex disk of radius ∥f∥∞​. The Tychonoff theorem makes the product of these disks over all f∈C(G) compact. Consequently the net of vectors (IFV​​(f))f∈C(G)​ has a convergent subnet. Call its coordinatewise limit I(f). Since each finite average is linear, positive on nonnegative real functions, and equal to one at the constant function one, the limit satisfies
I(αf+βh)=αI(f)+βI(h),I(f)≥0 (f≥0),I(1)=1,

Given an identity neighbourhood W, continuity of multiplication and inversion supplies a sufficiently small V with VV−1⊆W. The translation estimate consequently tends to zero along the subnet. For every f∈C(G) and every g∈G,
I(f∘Lg​)=I(f).​
(26)

A uniform algebra on a compact Hausdorff space K is a closed complex subalgebra A⊆C(K) containing the constants and separating points of K, with the supremum norm. It is therefore a commutative unital Banach algebra. Its character space of an algebra ΦA​, also called its carrier space, consists of nonzero multiplicative linear functionals, equipped with the Gelfand topology.
For x∈K, evaluation gives the character of an algebra δx​(f)=f(x). The map δ:K→ΦA​ is continuous: composing it with the coordinate φ↦φ(f) gives the continuous function f for every f∈A. It is injective because A separates points. The Gelfand topology is Hausdorff because distinct algebra characters differ on some element of A. A continuous injection from compact K into a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism onto its image: closed subsets of K have compact, hence closed, images. Its image is also compact and closed. Thus
δ:K⟶j(K)={δx​:x∈K}⊆ΦA​​
(28)
is the asserted homeomorphism. A natural uniform algebra is one for which this image is all of ΦA​.

We prove the forward direction of the finite-generator criterion for a natural uniform algebra. Suppose that A is a natural uniform algebra, and let f1​,…,fn​ have no common zero on K. The ideal
J=f1​A+⋯+fn​A
(29)
is all of A. Otherwise it is contained in a maximal ideal M, whose associated character of an algebra vanishes on all the fj​. Since every algebra character is an evaluation character under naturality, this would give a common zero, a contradiction. Therefore 1∈J, meaning that
j=1∑n​fj​gj​=1for suitable gj​∈A.​
(30)
Here are the Banach-algebra details behind the algebra character associated with M. A proper ideal cannot have dense closure: if it contained an element within distance less than one of 1, the Neumann series would make that element invertible, forcing the ideal to contain 1. Thus the closure of a proper ideal is proper. A maximal ideal, obtained from Zorn's lemma, is therefore closed. Its quotient is a complex unital Banach algebra in which every nonzero element is invertible. The Gelfand-Mazur theorem identifies this quotient with C, so the quotient map is the required algebra character. No closedness of the originally generated ideal J is needed.
