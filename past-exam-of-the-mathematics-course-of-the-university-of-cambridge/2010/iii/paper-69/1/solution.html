The solar constant measures the incident power per area normal to the sunlight. A spherical Earth of radius R intercepts sunlight over the disc πR2, but radiates over its surface 4πR2. For uniform surface albedo a in the global energy-budget approximation, absorbed power is (1−a)SπR2. The exceptional 2007 Arctic sea ice decline is best understood as a weather event acting on an already vulnerable ice cover, with several reinforcing pathways. Warmer air increases downward long-wave heating, lengthens the melt season and delays autumn freezing. Changes in snowfall and the development of melt ponds also change the surface energy balance. Air temperature alone, however, does not identify the source of the observed bottom melt: heat must reach the underside through the ocean.
Warmer Atlantic-origin water supplies a larger subsurface ocean heat content after entering through Fram Strait and the Barents Sea. Its influence on sea ice depends on vertical exchange, because a cold, relatively fresh halocline normally separates it from the ice. Erosion of that halocline, through altered freshwater distributions, wind-driven mixing or changes in circulation, weakens stable density stratification and can increase upward ocean heat flux. A warmer Atlantic layer without this exchange need not produce immediate extra bottom melt. Conversely, warmer Pacific inflow through Bering Strait introduces heat into the shallower western Arctic; advection through the Chukchi Sea provides another route to ice retreat and ocean heat storage.
The Arctic Oscillation changes winds, ice motion and export. Positive phases during earlier years helped remove old, thick ice through Fram Strait and left a younger, thinner pack. Summer 2007 also had a pronounced pressure contrast between the Beaufort region and the Eurasian Arctic, associated with the Arctic dipole anomaly. Its winds promoted ice displacement and warm-air transport. The summer circulation should not be described merely as a particular Arctic Oscillation index: the spatial pattern and the preconditioning from earlier years both matter.
Long-term thinning reduces the latent heat of fusion needed to remove the remaining ice. Replacement of multi-year sea ice by first-year sea ice therefore makes a given summer heat input more effective, while mobile thin ice is more readily redistributed or exported. These dynamical changes can expose open water early enough for substantial solar absorption. Water and melt ponds have lower surface albedo than snow-covered ice, so absorbed short-wave energy rises, increasing upper-ocean heat storage and bottom melt. This ice-ocean albedo feedback also delays refreezing. In the Beaufort Sea in 2007, enhanced solar heating of the newly exposed upper ocean was an especially important explanation of extreme bottom melt. Thus thinning and wind-driven opening can initiate retreat, while solar heating amplifies it; changes in Atlantic or Pacific heat supply can further reinforce the process where circulation permits.
A further positive climate feedback involves terrestrial carbon. Loss of the insulating ice cover permits greater autumn and winter transfer of stored ocean heat to the atmosphere. The resulting regional warming can increase thaw of coastal and inland permafrost. Microbial decomposition of previously frozen organic material then releases carbon dioxide, and oxygen-poor wet environments also release methane. These greenhouse gases strengthen the greenhouse effect, increasing global warming and further thaw. The permafrost carbon feedback provides the causal chain from summer ice retreat to an additional global influence. An Arctic methane feedback involving subsea sources is also possible, but the amount actually reaching the atmosphere depends on oxidation and transport; an abrupt, wholesale release is not a necessary consequence of summer ice loss.
