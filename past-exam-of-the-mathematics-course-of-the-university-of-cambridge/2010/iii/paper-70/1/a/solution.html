Past exam of the mathematics course of the University of Cambridge / 2010 / iii / Paper 70 / 1 / a / Solution

 ... Past exam of the mathematics course of the University of Cambridge 2010 iii Paper 70 1 a
OurBigBook.com Words: 264
Use a Fourier transform parallel to the boundary. Splitting its traces into right- and left-supported parts gives Half-range Fourier transforms F+​ and F−​, analytic respectively above and below a common inversion contour. A small positive imaginary part of the wavenumber implements the limiting absorption principle and separates the incident poles and outgoing branch points. Solve the transformed differential equation normal to the boundary using its outgoing or decaying solution. The boundary conditions then yield a Wiener-Hopf equation of the form
K(α)F+​(α)=F−​(α)+H(α).
(1)
Factor its Wiener-Hopf kernel as K=K+​K−​, with each factor analytic and nonzero in its designated half-plane. Divide by K−​ and split H/K−​=H+​+H−​, allocating poles according to the incident-wave prescription. Then
K+​F+​−H+​=F−​/K−​+H−​.
(2)
The two sides continue to a common entire function. Their growth at infinity, inherited from the field's edge behavior, determines that function, usually a constant or a polynomial; in a decaying case it is zero. Solve for the unknown transforms and apply Fourier inversion, preserving the outgoing contour prescription.
The principal analytic results used are as follows. The Paley–Wiener theorem and its half-line Laplace-transform version relate support on a half-line to holomorphy in a half-plane, with growth or square-integrability bounds. The Cauchy integral formula and Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem provide additive splitting: a Cauchy integral of a sufficiently decaying jump has upper and lower boundary values with that prescribed difference. Applying the same construction to a continuous logarithm of a nonvanishing scalar Wiener-Hopf kernel gives Wiener-Hopf factorization when its winding number is zero; a nonzero index requires an explicit index factor. The identity theorem gives analytic continuation across the common strip. Finally Liouville's theorem makes a bounded entire function constant; its polynomial-growth extension bounds the possible degree. The physical edge and radiation conditions select the remaining constants. Zeros, branch cuts, nonzero index and insufficient decay must be accounted for rather than discarded during splitting.
