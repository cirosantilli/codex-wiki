For a finite group, define the Frattini subgroup by
Φ(G)=⋂M maximal in G​M;
(3)
the intersection is G when there are no maximal subgroups, which for a finite group happens only for G=1. A second definition is the set of non-generators: an element x such that ⟨S,x⟩=G always implies ⟨S⟩=G.
If x∈Φ(G) and H=⟨S⟩<G, put H in a maximal subgroup M. Then x∈M, so ⟨S,x⟩≤M<G. Conversely, if x∈/Φ(G), some maximal subgroup M omits it; maximality gives ⟨M,x⟩=G, although M=G. This proves equivalence.
Every group automorphism permutes maximal subgroups, so Φ(G) is a characteristic subgroup, in particular a normal subgroup. Also
HΦ(G)=G⟹H=G:
(4)
a proper H would lie in a maximal subgroup containing both factors.
Set F=Φ(G) and take a Sylow subgroup P of F. For g∈G, normality of F makes Pg a Sylow subgroup of F. Conjugating inside F therefore gives the Frattini argument factorization G=FNG​(P). The preceding generating property forces NG​(P)=G. Thus every Sylow subgroup of F is normal, and part (i) shows that F is a nilpotent group.
Now suppose G is a finite p-group. The normalizer condition makes every maximal subgroup M normal: M<NG​(M) and maximality force NG​(M)=G. The quotient G/M has no proper nontrivial subgroup, hence is cyclic of prime order; its p-power order makes that prime p. Consequently every such M contains
D=[G,G]Gp,
(5)
where the two factors are the commutator subgroup and the subgroup generated by pth powers. Thus D≤Φ(G).
The quotient G/D is an elementary abelian group, hence a vector space over Fp​. If x∈/D, extend the nonzero vector xD to a basis and choose a linear functional nonzero on it. Its kernel pulls back to a subgroup of index p, a maximal subgroup omitting x. Therefore x∈/Φ(G), proving
Φ(G) is characteristic and nilpotent,Φ(G)=[G,G]Gp for finite p-groups.​
(6)
