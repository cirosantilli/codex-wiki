The defining rule for a derivation of a Lie algebra is linear in the operator, so Der(g) is a vector subspace of the general linear Lie algebra gl(g). Its Lie bracket is the operator commutator. If D,E are derivations of a Lie algebra, expand both compositions:
DE[x,y]ED[x,y]​=[DEx,y]+[Ex,Dy]+[Dx,Ey]+[x,DEy],=[EDx,y]+[Dx,Ey]+[Ex,Dy]+[x,EDy].​
(1)
Subtracting cancels the mixed terms and gives
[D,E][x,y]=[[D,E]x,y]+[x,[D,E]y].
(2)
Thus [D,E] is again a derivation of a Lie algebra, proving that the derivation Lie algebra is a Lie subalgebra of gl(g). This calculation works in every characteristic of a field.
The Jacobi identity states [x,[y,z]]=[[x,y],z]+[y,[x,z]]. It first shows that adx​ is a derivation of a Lie algebra, and then gives
[adx​,ady​](z)=[x,[y,z]]−[y,[x,z]]=[[x,y],z].
(3)
The map x↦adx​ is linear, so this identity proves it is a Lie algebra homomorphism.
Finally, for any derivation of a Lie algebra D,
[D,adx​](y)=D[x,y]−[x,Dy]=[Dx,y].
(4)
Consequently
[D,adx​]=adDx​,[Der(g),adg]⊆adg.​
(5)
This proves that the inner derivations of a Lie algebra form an ideal of a Lie algebra in Der(g).

Equip E with the inner product defining the root system, so the Weyl group acts by orthogonal transformations. As usual, the root system spans its ambient space E. Let U⊆E be an invariant subspace for the reflection representation of a Weyl group. Its orthogonal complement is also invariant.
For a root α, the Weyl reflection satisfies
sα​(u)=u−(α,α)2(u,α)​α.
(6)
If (u,α)=0 for every u∈U, then α∈U⊥. Otherwise choose u∈U with (u,α)=0. Both u and sα​(u) belong to U, and their difference is a nonzero multiple of α, so α∈U. Hence
R=(R∩U)⊔(R∩U⊥).
(7)
The two sets are mutually orthogonal and individually closed under their root reflections. If both were nonempty, this would decompose R into two orthogonal root systems, contradicting that it is an irreducible root system. All roots therefore lie in one of the two subspaces. Because they span E, either U=E or U⊥=E, the latter giving U=0. Thus the reflection representation is an irreducible representation.

ei​⊗(ej​∧ek​),1≤i≤n,j<k.

weight2εa​+εb​εa​+εb​+εc​​indicesa=ba<b<c​weight-space dimension13​​​

No two different listed coefficient patterns restrict to the same functional on the traceless diagonal Cartan subalgebra: their difference would have to be a multiple of (1,…,1), but its coefficient sum is zero. All unlisted weight spaces are zero. As a check,
n(n−1)+3(3n​)=2n2(n−1)​=dim(V⊗Λ2V).

Let V=C4 with its nondegenerate alternating bilinear form. Its inverse-form bivector Ω∈Λ2V is invariant under the symplectic Lie algebra, and Ω∧Ω=0. Choose vol=Ω∧Ω/2. The exterior product defines a symmetric bilinear form on Λ2V by
ξ∧η=B(ξ,η)vol.
(19)
It is symmetric because two-forms commute under the wedge product. It is nondegenerate because each basis wedge ei​∧ej​ pairs with its complementary basis wedge. Since B(Ω,Ω)=2, the five-dimensional orthogonal complement
W=Ω⊥=Λ02​V
(20)
also has a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form. This is the primitive exterior square, equivalently the kernel of symplectic contraction of an exterior square.
Every element of sp4​ fixes Ω and preserves volume, since its trace is zero. The induced exterior-power Lie algebra representation consequently preserves W and satisfies B(Aξ,η)+B(ξ,Aη)=0. Thus it gives a Lie algebra homomorphism
φ:sp4​⟶so(W,B).
(21)
We check injectivity explicitly. If A acts as zero on W, it also kills Ω, and therefore acts as zero on all of Λ2V=W⊕CΩ. For three distinct indices i,j,k, the coefficient of ek​∧ej​ in A(ei​∧ej​) is Aki​, so all off-diagonal entries of A vanish. Its action on ei​∧ej​ is then (Aii​+Ajj​)ei​∧ej​. All these sums vanish, which in characteristic zero forces every diagonal entry to vanish. Hence A=0.
Finally,
dimsp4​=4+3+3=10=25⋅4​=dimso(W,B).
(22)
The injective Lie algebra homomorphism is therefore surjective. Over C, every nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form admits a basis in which its matrix is the identity, so this constructs the exceptional isomorphism between sp4 and so5:
sp4​(C)≅so5​(C).​
(23)

Identify the diagonal Cartan subalgebra with Cn through its coordinates (h1​,…,hn​), and identify real roots with coordinate vectors using the invariant Euclidean inner product. A Weyl reflection is sα​(h)=h−2(h,α)α/(α,α). It does not change when α is replaced by −α. For the Cn root system, its explicit actions are
root, up to sign2εi​εi​−εj​εi​+εj​​changed coordinateshi​↦−hi​(hi​,hj​)↦(hj​,hi​)(hi​,hj​)↦(−hj​,−hi​)​​​

Every coordinate not displayed stays fixed. Thus each generating Weyl reflection is a signed permutation, so the Weyl group is contained in the signed symmetric group. Conversely, the first row supplies each independent sign change and the second supplies every coordinate transposition; these generate the entire signed symmetric group. Suppose that H is not a discrete subgroup. The identity is then an accumulation point of nonidentity subgroup elements: translate any failure of isolation to the identity. Choose hj​∈H∖{e} with hj​→e, and use a local exponential chart to write hj​=expXj​, where Xj​=0 and Xj​→0 in the real Lie algebra g.
Choose a norm on this finite-dimensional vector space. By compactness of its unit sphere, pass to a subsequence with Xj​/∥Xj​∥→X, where ∥X∥=1. For an arbitrary real t, choose an integer mj​ nearest to t/∥Xj​∥. Then mj​∥Xj​∥→t and
hjmj​​=exp(mj​Xj​)⟶exp(tX).
(26)
Every left-hand element belongs to H, including for negative mj​. Closedness gives exp(tX)∈H. Since t was arbitrary and X=0, the limit directions of a closed subgroup provide a nontrivial one-parameter subgroup in H. Its derivative at zero is X, so it cannot be the constant subgroup.

Define
h={X∈g:exp(tX)∈H for every t∈R}.
(27)
It contains zero, and X∈h implies aX∈h for every real scalar a, by replacing t with at.
For X,Y∈h, we prove closure under addition using the Lie product formula in a Lie group. In the local exponential chart, let Z(s)=log(exp(sX)exp(sY)). Smooth multiplication has differential (U,V)↦U+V at the identity, so Taylor's theorem gives
Z(s)=s(X+Y)+O(s2).
(28)
For each fixed real t and all sufficiently large positive integers m,
[exp(tX/m)exp(tY/m)]m=exp(mZ(t/m))⟶exp(t(X+Y)).
(29)
Every term belongs to H, and H is closed, proving X+Y∈h. Thus h is a real vector subspace. It is also closed: if Xj​→X with Xj​∈h, continuity of the Exponential map of a Lie group gives exp(tX)∈H for each t.
For the Lie bracket, use the Adjoint representation of a Lie group. Conjugating a one-parameter subgroup gives
exp(sX)exp(tY)exp(−sX)=exp(tAdexp(sX)​Y)∈H.
(30)
Consequently Adexp(sX)​Y∈h. For s=0, linearity puts (Adexp(sX)​Y−Y)/s in h. Its limit as s→0 is adX​Y=[X,Y]. Closedness of h therefore gives [X,Y]∈h, proving that the Lie algebra of a closed subgroup is a Lie subalgebra of g.

Choose a real vector subspace m complementary to h, so g=m⊕h. The smooth map
F:m×h⟶G,F(U,V)=expUexpV
(31)
has differential (U,V)↦U+V at (0,0), an isomorphism. The inverse function theorem makes F a diffeomorphism from a sufficiently small product neighborhood onto an open neighborhood of the identity in G.
There is a neighborhood of zero in m in which expU∈H implies U=0. Otherwise choose nonzero Uj​∈m tending to zero with expUj​∈H. A normalized subsequence tends to a unit vector U∈m. The limit directions of a closed subgroup from part (a) give U∈h, contradicting m∩h=0. Shrink the product neighborhood so that this exclusion holds throughout its first factor.
If F(U,V)∈H, then expV∈H by the definition of h, and hence expU=F(U,V)exp(−V)∈H. The exclusion forces U=0. Conversely, every F(0,V)=expV belongs to H. Thus the local product coordinates for a closed subgroup identify H near the identity exactly with the smooth coordinate slice U=0.
Left translation by each element of H gives the same description around that element, so H is an embedded submanifold of G in its subspace topology. The multiplication and inversion maps of G restrict to smooth maps on this embedded submanifold, making H a Lie group and an embedded Lie subgroup. Its tangent space at the identity is h, because dexp0​ is the identity on this coordinate slice. It remains closed by hypothesis. This proves the closed-subgroup theorem, with no prior smoothness assumption on H.
