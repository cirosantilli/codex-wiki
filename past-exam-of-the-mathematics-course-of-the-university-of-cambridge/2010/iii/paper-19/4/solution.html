Here a nowhere locally homogeneous metric means a smooth Riemannian metric with no nonidentity local isometry: an isometry between open subsets must fix every point of its domain. This is the strong local-rigidity meaning of “bumpy” used in this question, not the different convention about nondegenerate closed geodesics. Sunada's local isometry lemma states that, on a closed smooth manifold of dimension at least two, such Riemannian metrics form a residual subset of the space of smooth Riemannian metrics and are dense in its C∞ topology. In dimension one this conclusion is false: arclength coordinates give local translations for every Riemannian metric.
For maps Mm→Nn, the jet bundle of maps Jk(M,N) records the source point, the target value and all derivatives through order k. There are (km+k​) monomials of total degree at most k in m variables. Hence
dimJk(M,N)=m+n(km+k​).​
(41)
Over (x,y)∈M×N, the value is already fixed, so the fibre has dimension
n((km+k​)−1).​
(42)
In local coordinates it is the space of Taylor series coefficients of orders one through k, represented by a direct sum of symmetric powers, ⨁r=1k​Symr(Tx∗​M)⊗Ty​N. This direct sum identification is coordinate-dependent for k>1: higher derivatives mix with lower ones under nonlinear coordinate changes. Intrinsically, the truncation to order k−1 is an affine bundle modeled on Symk(Tx∗​M)⊗Ty​N. For k=1 the fibre is canonically Hom(Tx​M,Ty​N).
For the density assertion as printed, an isometry Ui​→Uj​ is a bijective Riemannian isometry. A localized volume perturbation suffices; it is not necessary to prove the stronger generic local-rigidity lemma afresh. We take the coordinate-ball basis without repetitions, and each Ui​ as the interior of its smooth closed-ball closure. Distinct such domains have a nonempty open difference in at least one direction. Indeed, if both were contained in the other's closure, their regular-open property U=intU would make them equal.
Fix any Riemannian metric g. If the two domains have unequal volumes, they cannot be isometric, so g already lies in the desired complement. Otherwise, after interchanging the two indices if necessary, choose a nonnegative nonzero smooth bump function ψ supported in Ui​∖Uj​, and set
gϵ​=e2ϵψg.
(43)
These are positive-definite smooth Riemannian metrics tending to g in the C∞ topology as ϵ→0. Their Riemannian volume forms satisfy dVgϵ​​=emϵψdVg​. The Riemannian metric and volume on Uj​ are unchanged, whereas
dϵd​volgϵ​​(Ui​)​ϵ=0​=m∫Ui​​ψdVg​>0.
(44)
For every sufficiently small ϵ>0, the volumes differ. Thus no isometry of the two closed domains exists, proving
CSij​ is dense in the space of smooth metrics.​
(45)
The volume difference is continuous in the smooth topology, so the unequal-volume Riemannian metrics even form an open dense subset of this complement. The regular-open assumption is also needed to justify this literal closed-domain assertion. As written, requiring only that each closure be a closed ball permits two distinct basis members with the same closure. For example in Rm, adjoin U=B(0,1) and V=B(0,1)∖{0} to a countable basis of a topology of coordinate balls. Both are open and both closures are the same closed ball. The identity is an isometry of those closures for every Riemannian metric, so the indicated complement is empty. Thus the conclusion needs distinct closures, as in the usual basis of genuine coordinate balls used above. The no-repetitions convention is necessary as well: if the indexed basis repeats a domain, its identity map is always an isometry and the asserted complement for those two indices is empty. This volume argument concerns isometries onto the specified whole domains; the stronger assertion excluding arbitrary local isometries is the separate Sunada lemma stated above.
