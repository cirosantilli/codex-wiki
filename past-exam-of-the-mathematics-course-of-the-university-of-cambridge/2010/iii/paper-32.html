Put G=XTX and H=XG−1XT. The rank of a matrix assumption makes G invertible, and H is the orthogonal projection matrix onto the column space of the design matrix. The Gaussian likelihood has log-likelihood
ℓ(b,v)=−2n​log(2πv)−2v∥Y−Xb∥2​,v>0.
(1)
Minimizing the squared Euclidean norm gives the ordinary least squares normal equations Gβ​=XTY. Maximizing over v then gives the maximum-likelihood estimators
β​=G−1XTY,σ2=nRSS​,RSS=YT(I−H)Y.​
(2)
The divisor for the maximum-likelihood estimator of the variance is n, whereas the unbiased residual variance estimate is s2=RSS/(n−p). The exceptional event RSS=0 has probability zero under the Gaussian linear model with σ2>0; on that event the likelihood is unbounded as v↓0.
For the joint sampling distribution, write β​−β=G−1XTϵ and Y−Xβ​=(I−H)ϵ. Their cross-covariance matrix vanishes because XT(I−H)=0. These two vectors are jointly multivariate normal, so they are independent random variables. The residual orthogonal projection has rank n−p, hence the standard chi-squared distribution result gives
β​∼Np​(β,σ2G−1),σ2nσ2​∼χn−p2​,β​ and σ2 independent.​
(3)
Thus these two displayed marginal laws, with their independence, specify the full joint distribution.
The quadratic form
σ2(β​−β)TG(β​−β)​
(4)
has a chi-squared distribution with p degrees of freedom and is independent of RSS/σ2. Their ratio therefore supplies the exact F-distribution pivot
T(β)=RSS/(n−p)(β​−β)TG(β​−β)/p​∼Fp,n−p​.
(5)
If f1−α​ is the (1−α) quantile of that F-distribution, inversion gives the normal linear-model confidence ellipsoid
C1−α​(Y)={b∈Rp:(β​−b)TG(β​−b)≤ps2f1−α​}.​
(6)
For every β and every σ2>0, the probability that this confidence set contains β is exactly 1−α. To test the null hypothesis, reject precisely when β0​ lies outside this confidence ellipsoid, equivalently when T(β0​)>f1−α​. Under the null hypothesis the rejection probability is exactly α, independent of the unknown variance.
For the numerical calculation, the second column of the design matrix sums to zero and its sum of squares is 60. Direct calculation from the observations gives
G=(90​060​),XTY=(030​),YTY=22.
(7)
Consequently the ordinary least squares estimate, residual sum of squares and unbiased residual variance are
β​=(01/2​),RSS=22−(XTY)TG−1(XTY)=22−15=7,s2=7/7=1.
(8)
The maximum-likelihood estimator of the variance is instead 7/9. At the proposed null value the F-distribution statistic is
T(0)=7/715/2​=7.5>4.74.​
(9)
Therefore reject the null hypothesis at the 5% level. The relevant critical value has 2 numerator and 7 residual degrees of freedom.

One sufficient maximum likelihood estimation statistical consistency theorem is the following. Let the observations be independent random variables with a common density fθ​ relative to a fixed measure, let Θ be a compact metric parameter set containing the true value θ0​, and suppose that the expected log-likelihood
m(θ)=Eθ0​​logfθ​(Y1​)
(10)
is finite, continuous, and uniquely maximized at θ0​. Assume also the uniform law of large numbers
supθ∈Θ​​n1​∑i=1n​logfθ​(Yi​)−m(θ)​P​0,
(11)
and assume that a measurable global maximum-likelihood estimator exists. Then every such maximum-likelihood estimator converges in probability to the true parameter. A useful sufficient condition for this uniform law of large numbers is almost-sure continuity of θ↦logfθ​(Y1​) on the compact parameter set and an integrable envelope supθ​∣logfθ​(Y1​)∣≤M(Y1​), Eθ0​​M(Y1​)<∞. Under these conditions, identifiability supplies uniqueness through the Kullback-Leibler divergence identity
m(θ0​)−m(θ)=DKL​(fθ0​​∥fθ​)≥0,
(12)
with equality only at θ=θ0​.
To see the statistical consistency conclusion, fix ε>0. Compactness and the unique maximum give a strictly positive separation gap
δε​=m(θ0​)−supd(θ,θ0​)≥ε​m(θ)>0
(13)
whenever the set outside the neighbourhood is nonempty. If Δn​ denotes the supremum discrepancy in the uniform law of large numbers, maximization of the empirical log-likelihood yields
0≤m(θ0​)−m(θn​)≤2Δn​.
(14)
Hence the probability of d(θn​,θ0​)≥ε is at most P(2Δn​≥δε​), which tends to zero. This is the argmin consistency under uniform convergence in probability argument applied to the negative log-likelihood. These are sufficient conditions, not necessary ones; parameter-dependent support can require a direct argument, and independent observations need not have a common distribution in other models.

The likelihood function for the shifted exponential distribution is
Ln​(θ)=exp(nθ−∑i=1n​Yi​)1{θ≤Y(1)​},Y(1)​=mini​Yi​.
(15)
It increases strictly up to the smallest observation and is zero beyond it. Thus shifted exponential maximum likelihood gives
θn​=Y(1)​.​
(16)
For t≥0, independence and the survival function of the exponential distribution give the exact order statistic law
Pθ​(θn​−θ>t)=∏i=1n​Pθ​(Yi​−θ>t)=e−nt.
(17)
In particular, θn​≥θ almost surely and Pθ​(∣θn​−θ∣>ε)=e−nε→0. This proves statistical consistency. The exact endpoint limit for a shifted exponential distribution is stronger than an asymptotic approximation:
n(θn​−θ)∼Exp(1) for every n,n(θn​−θ)d​Exp(1).​
(18)
The limit is one-sided and nonnormal; the moving support endpoint explains why a regular square-root-n asymptotic normality of a maximum likelihood estimator theorem does not apply here.

Set Sn​=∑i=1n​Yi​ and an​=∑i=1n​2−i=1−2−n. For the Poisson distribution observations, factors independent of θ can be removed from the likelihood function, leaving
Ln​(θ)∝θSn​e−an​θ,ℓn​(θ)=Sn​logθ−an​θ+constant.
(19)
When Sn​>0, the log-likelihood derivative is Sn​/θ−an​ and its second derivative is negative. The unique positive maximum-likelihood estimator is therefore
θn​=1−2−nSn​​(Sn​>0).​
(20)
There is a genuine boundary qualification: if Sn​=0, the likelihood decreases strictly on θ>0, so no maximum is attained in the stated open parameter space. On the closure θ≥0, its maximizer is 0. Defining the usual extended maximum-likelihood estimator by the same displayed formula for every Sn​ allows its statistical consistency to be examined; the all-zero event cannot be ignored because its probability does not tend to zero.
Indeed, sums of independent Poisson distribution variables give Sn​∼Poi(θan​), so
Pθ​(∣θn​−θ∣>θ/2)≥Pθ​(Sn​=0)=e−θan​⟶e−θ>0.
(21)
Thus the extended maximum-likelihood estimator is inconsistent. More precisely, Sn​ increases to S∞​=∑i≥1​Yi​, and ES∞​=θ<∞ shows that this total count is finite almost surely. Its distribution is Poisson distribution with mean θ, by taking limits of the finite-sum laws. Hence
θn​⟶S∞​∼Poi(θ)almost surely.
(22)
This finite-exposure Poisson inconsistency leaves a nondegenerate random limit even with infinitely many observations. Correspondingly, the total Fisher information is an​/θ→1/θ, rather than diverging. The common-distribution hypothesis in the introductory maximum likelihood estimation theorem is absent here.

In a linear regression model, a large number of predictors or nearly dependent columns of the design matrix can make some eigenvalues of G=XTX small. The ordinary least squares estimate has covariance matrix σ2G−1, so these directions have large variance, even though the estimator is unbiased. Ridge regression reduces this variance by shrinking the estimate towards zero, at the cost of introducing bias. This bias-variance tradeoff can improve the mean squared error when the signal is not too large in the shrunken directions.
For the usual preprocessing with a regression intercept, separate the constant column from the predictors and leave the intercept unpenalized. Subtract the sample mean from the response and from each nonconstant predictor, then divide each centered predictor column by its positive scale, for example its Euclidean norm. Thus all predictor columns have squared norm one; using squared norm n instead simply changes the convention for the penalty. Centering is performed on the slope columns, not on the constant column. A full-rank design containing an intercept retains full rank on its centered slope columns. A model without an intercept should retain its specified mean structure unless an intercept is deliberately introduced.
Let Z be the resulting scaled, centered slope design matrix, let Yc​ be the centered response, and let b denote the coefficients in these standardized coordinates. The ridge regression problem is
minb∈Rq​{∥Yc​−Zb∥2+λ∥b∥2},λ>0.
(23)
Here q is the number of slopes; it is one less than the original number of columns when a constant column was present. The gradient of this objective is 2(ZTZ+λI)b−2ZTYc​. Its Hessian matrix is a positive-definite matrix, giving the unique closed-form ridge regression estimator
bλR​=(ZTZ+λI)−1ZTYc​.​
(24)
The regression intercept in the original coordinates is recovered as a=Y−∑j​Xj​bj​/sj​, and the original slopes are β​j​=bj​/sj​, where sj​ is the scale used for column j. This is the unpenalized intercept in ridge regression convention. For a fixed full-rank coefficient design written simply as X, with response Y and coefficient β, the same formula reads
β​λR​=(XTX+λI)−1XTY.​
(25)
The risk comparison below first uses the fixed coefficient coordinates in which this quadratic penalty is imposed, and then addresses reversal of the scaling.
Assume at least one penalized coefficient, zero-mean errors with covariance matrix σ2I, and σ2>0. No normal distribution assumption is needed for this mean squared error calculation. Put G=XTX and Aλ​=(G+λI)−1. The covariance and bias of a ridge regression estimator follow directly from its linear expression:
Eβ​λR​−β=−λAλ​β,Cov(β​λR​)=σ2Aλ​GAλ​.
(26)
For the centered slope model, the response errors have covariance matrix σ2C, where C=I−11T/n. Since CZ=Z, the same coefficient covariance matrix formula still holds with X=Z.
By the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices, write G=Qdiag(d1​,…,dq​)QT, with Q orthogonal and every dj​>0, and put γ=QTβ. The bias-variance decomposition of mean squared error now gives
R(λ)=E∥β​λR​−β∥2=∑j=1q​(dj​+λ)2λ2γj2​+σ2dj​​,R(0)=σ2∑j=1q​dj​1​.
(27)
The expression at λ=0 is the ordinary least squares mean squared error. Its right derivative is
R′(0)=−2σ2∑j=1q​dj2​1​<0.
(28)
Therefore, for every fixed coefficient vector, all sufficiently small positive penalties strictly improve mean squared error.
An explicit sufficient choice follows by subtracting the ordinary least squares risk term by term:
R(λ)−R(0)=∑j=1q​(dj​+λ)2λ(λγj2​−2σ2−λσ2/dj​)​.
(29)
Since γj2​≤∥β∥2, every summand is negative whenever
λ>0,λ∥β∥2<2σ2.​
(30)
When β=0, every positive penalty works. This is a pointwise result: the permitted penalty may depend on the true coefficient vector. A fixed positive penalty cannot improve risk for all arbitrarily large signals, because its squared bias grows quadratically with the signal. The same distinction appears in uniform directional risk improvement by ridge regression and unbounded directional risk of fixed ridge shrinkage. In centered coordinates the unpenalized intercept contributes the same σ2/n to both coefficient risks, so it does not affect the improvement. Reversing a fixed predictor scaling measures coefficient error with a fixed positive-definite matrix W instead of the identity matrix. For this weighted loss the squared bias still has derivative zero at λ=0, while the variance term has derivative
−2σ2tr(WG−2)<0.
(31)
The inequality follows because tr(WG−2)=tr(G−1WG−1)>0. Thus sufficiently small penalties also improve the original-coordinate mean squared error, though the explicit sufficient bound above was for the unweighted coordinates. Recovering an uncentered intercept adds a fixed nonnegative quadratic form to the slope loss, and its sample-mean error is uncorrelated with the centered slope errors, so the same weighted argument applies. A model with only an unpenalized intercept gives identical estimators. If the noise variance were zero, the strict improvement assertion would fail; positive noise variance is essential.

Let R be the total number of rejected null hypotheses, and let V count rejected null hypotheses that are in fact true. The false discovery proportion and false discovery rate are
FDP=R∨1V​,FDR=E[R∨1V​].​
(32)
Thus the false discovery proportion is zero when there are no rejections, and the false discovery rate averages the realized proportion of erroneous rejections.
For the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, order the p-values as P(1)​≤⋯≤P(m)​ and define
R=max({k∈{1,…,m}:P(k)​≤αk/m}∪{0}).
(33)
Reject every hypothesis whose p-value satisfies Pi​≤αR/m when R>0, and reject none otherwise. There are exactly R such hypotheses: if a further p-value also met the threshold, its larger rank would satisfy its own, still larger, threshold, contradicting maximality. Under the stated independence and uniform true-null p-values, this procedure has false discovery rate αm0​/m≤α.
For the first modified Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, remove P1​, order the remaining m−1 p-values as Q(1)​,…,Q(m−1)​, and use the shifted critical values α(j+1)/m. Define its rejection count by
R−1​=max({j∈{1,…,m−1}:Q(j)​≤α(j+1)/m}∪{0}).
(34)
Equivalently, insert a zero in place of P1​ and run the ordinary Benjamini-Hochberg procedure on m values; its total rejection count is R(1)=R−1​+1. For r=1,…,m, the Benjamini-Hochberg leave-one-out identity in event form is
{P1​≤αr/m, R=r}={P1​≤αr/m, R−1​=r−1}.​
(35)
To prove it, suppose first that the left event holds. Then P1​ is among the rejected values. Lowering it to zero leaves all ordered p-values at ranks greater than r unchanged, so no new rank can meet its threshold. Rank r still meets its threshold, giving R(1)=r. Conversely, if R(1)=r and P1​≤αr/m, the r−1 rejected remaining values and P1​ all meet the original rank-r threshold. Hence the original count is at least r. Lowering a p-value cannot decrease the rejection count, so the original count is also at most R(1)=r. This proves equality of the events, including possible ties.
For the second modified Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, remove both P1​,P2​, order the remaining p-values as T(j)​, and use shifted critical values α(j+2)/m. Its count is
R−12​=max({j∈{1,…,m−2}:T(j)​≤α(j+2)/m}∪{0}).
(36)
Replacing P1​,P2​ by zeros in the full procedure gives a total of R(12)=R−12​+2 rejections. The same argument gives the Benjamini-Hochberg leave-two-out identity, for r=2,…,m:
{P1​≤αr/m, P2​≤αr/m, R=r}={P1​≤αr/m, P2​≤αr/m, R−12​=r−2}.​
(37)
In the forward direction both replaced values already lie among the first r, so ordered ranks greater than r remain unchanged. In the reverse direction reinserting the two values below the rank-r threshold still leaves at least r qualifying values, while monotonicity under lowering bounds the original count above by r. For r=1, the event of two p-values meeting the displayed threshold while only one is rejected is empty. For m=1 only the first modification is needed.
To calculate the second moment, write Ii​=1{i rejected} for a true null. Expanding the square gives
V2=∑i=1m0​​Ii​+∑1≤i,j≤m0​i=j​​Ii​Ij​.
(38)
For a true-null index i, let R−i​ denote the first modified count after removing Pi​. It is a function only of the remaining p-values, and so is independent of the uniform variable Pi​. The first event identity therefore yields
E(R∨1)2Ii​​=∑r=1m​r21​P(Pi​≤αr/m, R−i​=r−1)=mα​∑r=1m​rP(R−i​=r−1)​=mα​ER−i​+11​.
(39)
For distinct true-null indices i,j, the two independent uniform p-values are independent of the second modified count R−ij​. The second event identity gives
E(R∨1)2Ii​Ij​​=∑r=2m​r21​(mαr​)2P(R−ij​=r−2)=m2α2​.
(40)
The last sum is one because R−ij​ always takes a value in {0,…,m−2}. The ordered pairs in the expansion of V2 number m0​(m0​−1), with no factor of one half.
The true-null p-values are identically distributed and independent, so permutations among their indices leave the law of the full collection unchanged. Thus every R−i​ with i≤m0​ has the same distribution as R−1​, even though the false-null p-values need not be identically distributed. Combining the diagonal and off-diagonal contributions proves the second moment of the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery proportion:
E(FDP2)=mαm0​​E(A)+m2α2m0​(m0​−1)​,A=R−1​+11​.​
(41)
If the first modified count is instead defined to include the inserted zero, the same answer is A=1/R(1). When m0​=0, both sides are zero; when m0​=1, there are no off-diagonal terms. Finally, repeating the first calculation with denominator R∨1 rather than its square gives E[Ii​/(R∨1)]=α/m, which verifies the claimed exact false discovery rate.
