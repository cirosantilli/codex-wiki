Write the clock states modulo M. The detailed balance equations are pm​λm​=νpm−1​. The product condition makes these equations consistent around the circle, so the stationary distribution is
pm​=∑k=0M−1​wk​wm​​,w0​=1,wm​=j=1∏m​λj​ν​.​
(1)
Indeed, the rate of the time reversal of a continuous-time Markov chain along a clockwise edge is pm+1​λm+1​/pm​=ν, while its anticlockwise rate is pm−1​ν/pm​=λm​. Thus the clock is a reversible Markov chain.
The reversed clockwise transitions can be driven by a single independent rate-ν Poisson process, since their rate does not depend on the clock state. They correspond to the original anticlockwise transitions. Consequently the anticlockwise event times form a stationary Poisson process of rate ν. Its events before a given time are independent of the clock state at that time, which is the relevant quasireversibility property. If clockwise and anticlockwise transitions have the same destination, their direction is retained as an event mark when reversing the process.

Let (m,n) denote the clock position and the queue population, including any customer in service. By the memoryless property of the exponential distribution, the nonzero transition rates are
(m,n)⟶⎩⎨⎧​(m+1,n)(m−1,n+1)(m,n−1)​at rate ν,at rate λm​,at rate μ(n>0).​
(2)
Set ρ=ν/μ<1. We claim that
π(m,n)=pm​(1−ρ)ρn,n≥0,​
(3)
where pm​ is the clock's stationary distribution from part (a). To check global balance for a continuous-time Markov chain, divide the incoming probability flux by π(m,n). The clockwise contribution is νpm−1​/pm​=λm​. The arrival contribution, present only for n>0, is λm+1​(pm+1​/pm​)ρ−1=μ. The service contribution is μρ=ν. Their sum is the outgoing rate ν+λm​+μ1{n>0}​.
The proposed probability distribution is normalized, so it is the equilibrium law. In particular, the clock position and queue population are independent random variables at any fixed equilibrium time, and the population has the geometric distribution of a stable M/M/1 queue. Independence of these two random variables does not assert independence of the two entire processes.

A multiclass queue must specify its service discipline as well as its arrival and service rates. Here is an ordered-queue model that includes the usual class-independent exponential single-server queue. Let class-r arrivals be independent Poisson processes of rates αr​, and let a class-r service requirement have the exponential distribution of rate μr​. A state is a finite word x=(c1​,…,cn​) of customer classes.
Choose service fractions γi​(n)≥0 with ∑i=1n​γi​(n)=1, and insertion probabilities δi​(n)≥0 with ∑i=1n​δi​(n)=1. The customer at position i departs at rate μci​​γi​(n); a class-r arrival is inserted at position i at rate αr​δi​(n+1). All rates leading to the same word are added. For class-dependent μr​, impose the symmetric service discipline δi​(n)=γi​(n). If all μr​=μ, arbitrary class-independent γ,δ are permitted, including first come first served with insertion at the tail and service at the head.
Put ρr​=αr​/μr​ and ρ=∑r​ρr​. For ρ<1, the equilibrium word law is
π(c1​,…,cn​)=(1−ρ)i=1∏n​ρci​​.​
(7)
For the symmetric service discipline, insertion and deletion satisfy detailed balance for a continuous-time Markov chain because
π(x)αr​γi​(n+1)=π(insi,r​x)μr​γi​(n+1).
(8)
For the common-service-rate case, use time reversal of a continuous-time Markov chain instead: the reversed queue inserts with probabilities γ and serves with fractions δ. A forward insertion gives a reverse deletion rate μδi​, and a forward deletion gives a reverse insertion rate αr​γi​. Forward and reverse total rates both equal ∑r​αr​+μ1{n>0}​, proving global balance for a continuous-time Markov chain.
Summing the weights of all words of length n gives (1−ρ)ρn, so the law is normalized. If nr​ counts class-r customers and n=∑r​nr​, the multinomial coefficient counting class orderings gives
π((nr​)r​)=(1−ρ)n!r∏​nr​!ρrnr​​​.​
(9)
For processor sharing, γi​(n)=δi​(n)=1/n, the class-count vector itself is a Markov chain, with arrival rate αr​ and departure rate μr​nr​/n. Under first come first served, the ordered word is generally necessary. Class-dependent service rates combined with first come first served do not in general have the displayed simple equilibrium law; the discipline assumption matters.

Index the queue-class pairs by a=(j,r). Let νa​ be the external Poisson process arrival rate, let pab​ be the routing probability after class a completes service, and put pa0​=1−∑b​pab​ for the exit probability. An open network has a transient routing matrix P, equivalently spectral radius less than one. The effective throughputs solve the traffic equations for a multiclass queueing network:
λa​=νa​+b∑​λb​pba​,λ=(I−PT)−1ν.​
(10)
At each queue use the ordered multiclass single-server queue model of part (a), with parameters μjr​,γji​,δji​. An external class-a customer is inserted at position i with rate νa​δji​(Nj​+1). A customer of class a in position i completes at rate μa​γji​(Nj​): it exits with probability pa0​ or changes to class b and is inserted at its destination with probability pab​δkℓ​(Nk′​+1). Here Nk′​ is the destination population after the origin deletion, so this also defines routes returning to the same queue. At each node, either service rates are common to its classes or insertion and service fractions form a symmetric service discipline.
Let ρj​=∑r​λjr​/μjr​<1. The product-form stationary distribution of a multiclass queueing network is
π((xj​)j​)=j∏​(1−ρj​)i=1∏Nj​​μj,cji​​λj,cji​​​.​
(11)
To prove it by time reversal of a continuous-time Markov chain, interchange each node's insertion and service fractions, and use
νa∗​=λa​pa0​,pba∗​=λb​λa​pab​​,pb0∗​=λb​νb​​.
(12)
Pairs with λb​=0 can be deleted. The traffic equations for a multiclass queueing network ensure ∑a​pba∗​+pb0∗​=1. The ratio of the product weights for an insertion is λa​/μa​; for a deletion it is its reciprocal. Consequently every routing, entrance, and exit transition satisfies π(x)q(x,x′)=π(x′)q∗(x′,x). The symmetric or common-rate hypothesis makes each node's total service rate identical forward and backward. Summing the traffic equations for a multiclass queueing network also gives ∑a​νa∗​=∑a​νa​. Thus the total transition rates match, proving global balance for a continuous-time Markov chain; each factor is normalized by part (a).
For the class counts the equilibrium law is
π((njr​)jr​)=j∏​(1−ρj​)Nj​!r∏​njr​!(λjr​/μjr​)njr​​,Nj​=r∑​njr​.​
(13)
Under processor sharing these counts have explicit transition rates νa​ for n→n+ea​, μa​na​/Nj​pa0​ for n→n−ea​, and μa​na​/Nj​pab​ for n→n−ea​+eb​. Under a closed network, remove external entrances and exits and normalize the same product weights on the conserved customer populations, using a positive solution of the homogeneous traffic equations for a multiclass queueing network. That finite-state normalization does not require the open-network conditions ρj​<1.

Start from the Erlang loss formula and reverse the order in its denominator:
E(Nν,NC)1​=∑j=0NC​(NC−j)!(Nν)j(NC)!​=∑j=0NC​∏ℓ=0j−1​NνNC−ℓ​.
(14)
For each fixed j, the product tends to (C/ν)j. If ν>C, it is bounded by this same summable geometric series. Extend the summand by zero for j>NC and apply the dominated convergence theorem with counting measure to obtain
E(Nν,NC)1​⟶∑j=0∞​(C/ν)j=1−C/ν1​.
(15)
If ν≤C, retain the first K+1 summands. Their limiting sum is at least K+1, so the reciprocal tends to infinity as K can be arbitrarily large. This includes ν=C without a separate normal approximation. Therefore
E(Nν,NC)⟶max{0,1−C/ν}.​
(16)
Here C and N are positive integers, as appropriate for circuit counts; taking ⌊NC⌋ gives the same limit for a positive real capacity scale. This is the proportional scaling limit of the Erlang loss formula.

For the link-route incidence matrix A, the reduced-load approximation and Erlang loss formula give
Bj​=E(aj​,Cj​),aj​=r∑​Ajr​νr​k=j∏​(1−Bk​)Akr​.​
(17)
Introduce yj​=−log(1−Bj​) and accepted route flows xr​=νr​e−(ATy)r​. Multiplying the reduced load by the link's own acceptance probability gives
aj​(1−Bj​)=∑r​Ajr​xr​=U(yj​,Cj​).
(18)
These are precisely the first-order optimality conditions for the convex potential for the Erlang fixed point, since
∂yj​∂F​=−∑r​Ajr​νr​e−(ATy)r​+U(yj​,Cj​).
(19)
Each exponential term is a convex function, and the integral of the strictly increasing function U(⋅,Cj​) is a strictly convex function. Hence F is a strictly convex function. Moreover U(z,Cj​)→Cj​>0 as z→∞, by the saturation of the carried load of an Erlang loss resource. Its integrals tend to infinity at least linearly, so F is a coercive function on the nonnegative orthant and has a unique minimizer.
At yj​=0, U(0,Cj​)=0. If link j is used by any positive-rate route, then ∂j​F<0, excluding a boundary minimizer there. An unused link instead minimizes at yj​=0 and has zero derivative. Thus the minimizer satisfies the displayed flow equations for every link. Conversely, those equations imply ∇F=0 and therefore minimize the convex potential for the Erlang fixed point. Finally U(y,C)=a[1−E(a,C)] with y=−log(1−E(a,C)) converts the flow equations back to Bj​=E(aj​,Cj​). The Erlang fixed point approximation has exactly one solution under fixed routing.

The alternative routing term feeds additional traffic back into the link. We can prove the existence of three solutions rather than relying on an iteration that happens to find different answers. Choose νN​=20N, CN​=21N, and define the continuous residual
GN​(B)=E(20N[1+2B(1−B)],21N)−B.
(20)
By the proportional scaling limit of the Erlang loss formula, at each fixed B,
GN​(B)⟶G∞​(B)=max{0,1−1+2B(1−B)21/20​}−B.
(21)
At B=1/100, the multiplier is 1.0198<21/20, so G∞​(1/100)=−1/100. At B=1/8 it is 39/32, giving
G∞​(1/8)=1−6556​−81​=5207​>0.
(22)
Also GN​(0)>0 and GN​(1)<0 for every N, since a finite-capacity Erlang loss formula gives a blocking probability strictly between zero and one. For all sufficiently large N, the signs at 0,1/100,1/8,1 therefore alternate. Applying the intermediate value theorem in the three disjoint intervening intervals proves at least three distinct fixed points. This supplies a fluid scaling proof of multiple Erlang fixed points.
This multiplicity concerns the Erlang fixed point approximation. It does not imply several equilibrium laws for an exact finite irreducible Markov chain, which has a unique stationary distribution.

The objective approximates the logarithm of the product-form stationary distribution of a loss network. To make the approximation precise, multiply arrival rates and capacities by N and write x=n/N. The Stirling formula gives, uniformly on the feasible compact set,
log∏r​nr​!(Nνr​)nr​​=NHν​(n/N)+O(logN),Hν​(x)=∑r​[xr​logνr​+xr​−xr​logxr​],
(23)
with 0log0=0. The feasible set is K={x≥0:Ax≤C}. Positive capacities and a nonempty route for each class make K compact with a strictly positive feasible point. Since Hν​ is a strictly concave function, its maximizer x∗ is unique; its infinite inward derivative at xr​=0 makes every component positive.
The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions give nonnegative link Lagrange multipliers zj​ satisfying
xr∗​=νr​e−(ATz)r​,zj​(Cj​−(Ax∗)j​)=0.​
(24)
For any neighborhood of x∗, continuity and uniqueness give a strictly positive objective gap outside it. There are only polynomially many feasible lattice states, while their weights differ exponentially in N. The feasible point ⌊Nx∗⌋ has objective tending to Hν​(x∗). It follows that the stationary law concentrates at x∗ on the scale n/N; in particular,
Nn​⟶x∗ in probability,E[n]=Nx∗+o(N).​
(25)
Thus NETWORK predicts the dominant occupancy and mean occupancy in a large loss network. For unscaled inputs it gives a continuous approximation to the modal integer state.
It also gives an exact useful change of variables. Substituting νr​=xr∗​e(ATz)r​ into the equilibrium weights yields
πN​(n)∝1{An≤NC}​ezT(An−NC)∏r​Pr{Poisson(Nxr∗​)=nr​}.

This is Poisson exponential tilting for a loss network: independent Poisson random variables centered at the predicted occupancies, restricted to the feasible region and penalized for slack in positively priced resources. It explains why an unconstrained Gaussian approximation should not automatically be applied at a saturated capacity boundary.

Let x∗ maximize SYSTEM. Under the usual positive-capacity, nonempty-route assumptions, the feasible set is compact, contains a positive point, and has a Slater condition point. Strict concavity makes the optimum unique. The condition Ur′​(0)=∞ forces xr∗​>0: moving from any optimum with a zero component slightly toward a positive feasible point gives an infinite positive directional derivative in that component and only finite losses in the positive components.
By the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions, there are link Lagrange multipliers pj∗​≥0 with
Ur′​(xr∗​)=(ATp∗)r​,pj∗​(Cj​−(Ax∗)j​)=0.
(27)
Set the route congestion price yr∗​=(ATp∗)r​=Ur′​(xr∗​) and choose
νr∗​=xr∗​eyr∗​.​
(28)
Then xr∗​=νr∗​e−yr∗​, as required. For USER with fixed yr∗​, the substitution x=νr​e−yr∗​ is a bijection of the nonnegative half-line, and its objective becomes Ur​(x)−yr∗​x. This is a strictly concave function whose derivative vanishes at xr∗​, so its unique maximizer is xr∗​ and the corresponding USER optimizer is νr∗​.
For NETWORK at ν∗, its objective derivative at x∗ is
logxr∗​νr∗​​=yr∗​=(ATp∗)r​.
(29)

The triple (x∗,y∗,ν∗) solves all three problems simultaneously. This is a user-network decomposition of concave utility maximization.
