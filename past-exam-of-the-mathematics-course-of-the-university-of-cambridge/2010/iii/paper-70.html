Use a Fourier transform parallel to the boundary. Splitting its traces into right- and left-supported parts gives Half-range Fourier transforms F+​ and F−​, analytic respectively above and below a common inversion contour. A small positive imaginary part of the wavenumber implements the limiting absorption principle and separates the incident poles and outgoing branch points. Solve the transformed differential equation normal to the boundary using its outgoing or decaying solution. The boundary conditions then yield a Wiener-Hopf equation of the form
K(α)F+​(α)=F−​(α)+H(α).
(1)
Factor its Wiener-Hopf kernel as K=K+​K−​, with each factor analytic and nonzero in its designated half-plane. Divide by K−​ and split H/K−​=H+​+H−​, allocating poles according to the incident-wave prescription. Then
K+​F+​−H+​=F−​/K−​+H−​.
(2)
The two sides continue to a common entire function. Their growth at infinity, inherited from the field's edge behavior, determines that function, usually a constant or a polynomial; in a decaying case it is zero. Solve for the unknown transforms and apply Fourier inversion, preserving the outgoing contour prescription.
The principal analytic results used are as follows. The Paley–Wiener theorem and its half-line Laplace-transform version relate support on a half-line to holomorphy in a half-plane, with growth or square-integrability bounds. The Cauchy integral formula and Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem provide additive splitting: a Cauchy integral of a sufficiently decaying jump has upper and lower boundary values with that prescribed difference. Applying the same construction to a continuous logarithm of a nonvanishing scalar Wiener-Hopf kernel gives Wiener-Hopf factorization when its winding number is zero; a nonzero index requires an explicit index factor. The identity theorem gives analytic continuation across the common strip. Finally Liouville's theorem makes a bounded entire function constant; its polynomial-growth extension bounds the possible degree. The physical edge and radiation conditions select the remaining constants. Zeros, branch cuts, nonzero index and insufficient decay must be accounted for rather than discarded during splitting.

Work above the plane, with e−iωt time dependence and 0<θ<π away from grazing incidence. Put a=kcosθ and b=ksinθ. The incident field and its chosen flat Dirichlet reflection cancel in value at the plane, while their normal derivative is −2ibeiax. Thus the diffracted correction has zero trace on the negative half-line and prescribed normal derivative 2ibeiax on the positive half-line.
There is an incompatibility in the stated edge data: a normal derivative that is identically zero on the Neumann half-line cannot diverge as that half-line approaches the junction. The inverse-square-root normal-derivative singularity belongs to the Dirichlet side, x→0−; on x>0 the total normal derivative remains zero. The following solution satisfies the mixed conditions and bounded-field edge behavior, with that corrected side.
Take the transform and its inverse as
f​(α)=∫R​f(x)eiαxdx,f(x)=2π1​∫C​f​(α)e−iαxdα.
(3)
Let U+​ be the transform of the diffracted boundary value, and V−​ the transform of its unknown normal derivative on the negative half-line. The outgoing transformed field is U+​(α)eiβ(α)y, where
β=k2−α2​=β+​β−​,β+​=k+α​,β−​=k−α​.
(4)
Continue k into the upper half-plane. Choose branches so that β(0)=k, outgoing propagating waves have positive vertical wavenumber, and evanescent waves decay. The branch point −k lies below C and belongs to β+​; k lies above C and belongs to β−​. Since ∫0∞​ei(α+a)xdx=i/(α+a), the boundary transform equation is
iβ+​U+​=β−​V−​​−(α+a)β−​2b​.
(5)
The forcing splits by subtracting its numerator at the incident pole:
H+​=−β−​(−a)(α+a)2b​,H−​=−α+a2b​(β−​(α)1​−β−​(−a)1​).
(6)
The pole in H−​ is removable; H−​ is analytic below C, while H+​ is analytic above it. Hence iβ+​U+​−H+​=V−​/β−​+H−​ is entire. The bounded mixed-boundary edge solution has U+​=O(α−3/2) and V−​=O(α−1/2), making both sides decay at infinity. These orders can also be obtained locally from the leading wedge solution r1/2cos(φ/2), which is Neumann at φ=0 and Dirichlet at φ=π. Liouville's theorem sets the entire remainder to zero. Therefore
U+​(α)=k+a​(α+a)k+α​2ib​.​
(7)
The solution of mixed Dirichlet-Neumann half-plane diffraction is consequently
ϕd​(x,y)=πk+a​ib​∫C​(α+a)k+α​e−iαx+ik2−α2​y​dα,ϕt​=eiax−iby−eiax+iby+ϕd​.​
(8)
For k=k0​+iε, take C from left to right inside −ε<Imα<ε and above the incident pole −a. Cuts from −k run into the lower half-plane and cuts from k into the upper half-plane, without crossing C. For example choose Imα=c with max(−ε,−εcosθ)<c<ε. Then let ε↓0, keeping the contour above −a and −k and below k. Any subsequent deformation for asymptotic evaluation must preserve these bypasses or include the residues of crossed poles. This prescription fixes the reflected-wave contributions and the radiation condition; a principal-value integral alone would not do so.
As a boundary check, iβU+​=−2bβ−​/[k+a​(α+a)]. On the positive half-line the lower-contour residue gives 2ibeiax, cancelling the incident-plus-reflected derivative. The boundary trace vanishes on the negative half-line by upper-half-plane analyticity. Finally U+​=O(α−3/2) gives a square-root boundary value and hence a bounded field at the junction; the singular derivative occurs on its Dirichlet side.

2ikEx​+Exx​+Δ⊥​E+k2(n2−1)E=0.

mx​=Dm+ia⟨WE⟩,m(0)=E0​,m=⟨E⟩.​

A square of ideal white noise is not defined by the covariance in part (b); that limit uses the linear random-potential model and its stated drift.

Translation invariance in y, together with a y-independent incident field, permits a y-independent solution. In homogeneous space Maxwell's equations then separate into two scalar sectors.
For a TE wave, set E=y​Ey​(x,z). Its component satisfies (∂x2​+∂z2​+k2)Ey​=0. The y direction is tangent to the conducting surface, so vanishing tangential electric field imposes Ey​(x,h(x))=0. Therefore TE scattering is exactly the two-dimensional Dirichlet scalar problem, with Ey​ replacing the acoustic scalar. Its first-order boundary expansion and angular-spectrum formula are those above.
For a TM wave, set H=y​Hy​(x,z). Maxwell's equation ∇×H=−iωϵE gives
E=ωϵi​(−∂z​Hy​,0,∂x​Hy​).
(35)
A surface tangent is (1,0,h′), so vanishing tangential electric field gives
(∂z​−h′∂x​)Hy​(x,h(x))=0,equivalently ∂n​Hy​=0.​
(36)
Thus TM also reduces to two dimensions, but to a Neumann problem, not the Dirichlet problem in part (a). This is scalar polarization reduction at a conducting corrugation.
For example the flat TM reflection has positive sign, H[0]=Ai​eipi​x(e−iqi​z+eiqi​z). Its linearized rough forcing is
∂z​Hs[1]​(x,0)=2Ai​eipi​x(qi2​h+ipi​h′).
(37)
In the transform domain this becomes 2Ai​(k2−pi​ξ)h(ξ−pi​), and propagation divides by iβ(ξ) rather than prescribing the electric Dirichlet trace. The explicit slope term and different reflection sign show why simply reusing the TE boundary formula for TM would be incorrect.

In first Born order the unknown potential enters linearly. Let Mi​ multiply by the known incident field, G be the outgoing Green integral over the known support D, and R restrict the resulting field to the measurement set. With y the measured scattered data,
y=AV,A=RGMi​.​

n(r)=1+V(r)/k2​,​

The adjoint operators, orthonormal singular vectors and squared-norm normal equation for a linear inverse problem conditions require Hilbert spaces. For arbitrary normed spaces the Banach-space adjoint takes values in the dual spaces and need not produce this formula. Interpret the given singular value system in Hilbert spaces, with uj​ in the unknown space and vj​ in the data space, as in the printed identities. Positive singular values tending to zero provide the compact ill-posed case. A general bounded linear operator need not have this property; the identity operator is a well-conditioned counterexample. Components in the kernel must be fixed by a minimum-norm convention, and the unregularized inverse is defined only for data satisfying the corresponding range condition.

(A∗A+αI)−1A∗=A∗(αI+AA∗)−1.
(51)
It follows by multiplying (C+αI)A∗=A∗(B+αI) on either side by the appropriate resolvent. A useful exact inverse identity is
(αI+B)−1=α−1I−α−2A(I+α−1C)−1A∗.
(52)
Expanding (I+α−1C)−1 to two terms gives the printed approximation, namely
Rα,2​=α−1I−α−2B+α−3B2.
(53)
This is the truncated Tikhonov resolvent. Multiplication gives (αI+B)Rα,2​=I+α−3B3, so the expression is not an exact inverse. For example A=1 and α=1 give the approximate value 1 instead of the exact 1/2. Its exact remainder is
Rα,2​−(αI+B)−1=α−3B3(αI+B)−1,∥Rα,2​−(αI+B)−1∥≤α4∥B∥3​.
(54)
In the regime ∥C∥/α<1 it is a controlled Neumann expansion. Using it to reconstruct gives
xα,2​=(α−1I−α−2C+α−3C2)A∗y.
(55)
For each fixed α>0 this is a bounded polynomial in bounded operators, with
∥xα,2​(yδ)−xα,2​(y)∥≤(α∥A∥​+α2∥A∥3​+α3∥A∥5​)∥yδ−y∥.
(56)
Thus the supplied approximation also has continuous data dependence in its domain of use. It must not be extrapolated to α↓0 with A fixed: its gain on any nonzero singular mode diverges, so it would not define a convergent regularization family there. The exact Tikhonov filter supplies the well-posed regularized problem; the truncated formula is only a controlled computational approximation.
