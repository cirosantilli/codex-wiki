In the variational convention needed here, a positive definite symmetric operator on a real Hilbert space satisfies
⟨Lu,v⟩=⟨u,Lv⟩,⟨Lv,v⟩>0(v=0).
(9)
For a bounded operator on the whole space, symmetry is self-adjointness. On a complex Hilbert space, the corresponding convention is Hermitian positivity; the quadratic expression is real. Strict positivity should be distinguished from uniformly positive definite symmetric operator coercivity, which requires a fixed γ>0 such that ⟨Lv,v⟩≥γ∥v∥2.
Symmetry matters in a real space. If “positive definite” means only the inequality for the quadratic form, the next variational assertion is false: L=(11​−11​) has ⟨Lv,v⟩=∥v∥2>0, but its skew part disappears from that form. Its Euler-Lagrange equation is v=f, not Lv=f. Thus the usual symmetric/Hermitian convention, or an explicit symmetry assumption, is required.

Interpret the differential expression as Lu=(pu′′)′′+qu, with a suitable differential-operator domain and the stated clamped boundary conditions. For sufficiently smooth u,v in that domain, twice integrating by parts gives
⟨Lu,v⟩=[(pu′′)′v−pu′′v′]−11​+∫−11​(pu′′v′′+quv)dx=∫−11​(pu′′v′′+quv)dx.
(13)
The boundary term vanishes because v=v′=0 at both ends. For real coefficients this form is symmetric/Hermitian. In particular
⟨Lu,u⟩=∫−11​(p∣u′′∣2+q∣u∣2)dx>0(u=0).​
(14)
Indeed nonnegativity follows from p>0 and q≥0. If the integral were zero, u′′=0 almost everywhere in the interval, so u would be affine. Its endpoint values then force u=0. This proves strict positivity on the intended clamped domain and gives the weak formulation of a clamped fourth-order equation.
There is a genuine domain issue in the literal wording. The closure of clamped smooth functions in the stated L2 norm is all of L2(−1,1), since it contains the dense set Cc∞​(−1,1). Boundary traces are not retained by that closure, and a fourth-order expression does not define an operator on every element of L2. For example p=(1−x2)2, q=0 satisfy the given sign conditions, but u=1(−1/2,1/2)​ belongs to this closure and makes (pu′′)′′ a distribution rather than an L2 function. Even a general twice-smooth function need not have the four derivatives required by the classical expression.
A rigorous classical repair is to take smooth enough coefficients, for example p∈C2([−1,1]), q∈C([−1,1]), and use a clamped domain such as H4∩H02​ in L2, with the appropriate operator realization. A weak repair uses the clamped second-order Sobolev space H02​ and the form just displayed, with hypotheses ensuring its boundedness and closure; uniform positivity p≥p0​>0 makes it coercive there. The integration-by-parts and strict-positivity proof does not need that stronger uniform bound, but existence and completion statements must not be inferred from the original L2 closure alone. The printed L[f] with an expression in u is also a harmless variable-name mismatch; the calculation consistently uses Lu.

The collocation order theorem identifies the classical order with the order of its interpolatory quadrature. If ∑i​bi​g(ci​) integrates every polynomial of degree at most p−1 exactly, but fails for some polynomial of degree p, then the collocation method has order p for sufficiently smooth differential equations and the local implicit stage branch. With s nodes, s≤p≤2s.
Equivalently, put πs​(τ)=∏i​(τ−ci​). If its first k moments vanish,
∫01​τjπs​(τ)dτ=0(0≤j<k),
(24)
and the next does not, then p=s+k. Polynomial division explains the quadrature part: any polynomial through degree s+k−1 is a remainder of degree below s plus πs​ times a polynomial of degree below k. The remainder is integrated exactly by interpolation, and the remaining integral vanishes. Conversely a nonzero next moment is an explicit failed quadrature test. The differential-equation order assertion is the collocation theorem being stated here. In particular Gauss collocation methods achieve 2s, Radau IIA methods achieve 2s−1, and endpoint-including Lobatto IIIA methods achieve 2s−2 for s≥2.

A-stability removes the scalar linear decay restriction on the step size; it does not replace accuracy, nonlinear solvability or a suitable system norm. Its importance is clearest for a stiff differential equation, where rapidly decaying modes coexist with slowly varying quantities of interest. On the Dahlquist test equation y′=λy, the exact solution decays for Reλ<0. A stable time integrator should not turn that decay into numerical growth.
For a one-step method write yn+1​=R(z)yn​, where z=hλ and R is its stability function. Its linear stability domain consists of parameters with ∣R(z)∣≤1 and a well-defined update. The method is A-stable when this domain contains the closed left half-plane. A consistent order-p one-step method has R(z)=ez+O(zp+1) near zero, but that local agreement alone says nothing about stability at large negative z.
For a Runge-Kutta method with stage matrix A, weights b and 1=(1,…,1)T, eliminating the stages on the test equation gives
R(z)=1+zbT(I−zA)−11.
(40)
An explicit finite-stage method has a polynomial R. A nonconstant polynomial is unbounded along the negative real axis, so no consistent explicit Runge-Kutta method is A-stable. The explicit Euler method, with R(z)=1+z, is stable in the disk ∣1+z∣≤1 and on the negative real axis only for −2≤z≤0. A stiff mode with a large negative eigenvalue therefore forces an unnecessarily small step even after its exact contribution has almost disappeared.
The Backward Euler method has R(z)=1/(1−z). For Rez≤0, ∣1−z∣2=1−2Rez+∣z∣2≥1, so it is A-stable. The trapezoidal rule has
R(z)=1−z/21+z/2​,∣1−z/2∣2−∣1+z/2∣2=−2Rez≥0,
(41)
and is also A-stable. The implicit midpoint method has the same scalar stability function, although its nonlinear update differs from the trapezoidal rule. The first method has order one and the latter two order two. Their implicit solves trade greater work per step for freedom from the scalar stiff stability restriction.
Damping of very stiff modes separates A-stability from L-stability. For a one-step method, the latter additionally requires R(z)→0 as z tends to infinity in the left half-plane. Backward Euler method satisfies this. The trapezoidal and midpoint rules instead have R(z)→−1 along the negative real axis: a very stiff mode can alternate in sign with little damping even though its amplitude remains bounded. L-stability is often preferable for diffusion and rapid relaxation, while retaining oscillatory modes can be desirable in conservative dynamics.
For a linear multistep method, write
∑j=0k​αj​yn+j​=h∑j=0k​βj​fn+j​,ρ(ζ)=∑j​αj​ζj,σ(ζ)=∑j​βj​ζj.
(42)
Its test-equation amplification roots satisfy ρ(ζ)−zσ(ζ)=0. Absolute stability requires every root to lie in the unit disk or on its boundary with all unit-modulus roots simple; all parasitic modes matter. At z=0 this is the zero-stability root condition. Consistency requires ρ(1)=0 and ρ′(1)=σ(1), and the Dahlquist equivalence theorem combines consistency and zero-stability to characterize convergence. Thus a claim of stability obtained by canceling a common factor is not a proof about the original recurrence and its arbitrary consistent starting data.
Explicit linear multistep methods cannot be A-stable either. If βk​=0 and αk​=0, some lower coefficient of ρ−zσ becomes unbounded as z→−∞, while the leading coefficient stays fixed. If all roots remained in the unit disk, every coefficient-to-leading-coefficient ratio, an elementary symmetric function of those roots, would remain bounded. This contradiction forces an unstable root.
An important implicit example is BDF2 method, whose characteristic equation is (3−2z)ζ2−4ζ+1=0. A unit root ζ=eiθ gives
z(θ)=21​(3−4e−iθ+e−2iθ),Rez(θ)=(1−cosθ)2≥0.
(43)
The leading coefficient vanishes only at z=3/2, outside the left half-plane. Near a small negative z, both roots are inside the disk; no unit-circle crossing can occur in the connected open left half-plane. On the imaginary axis the boundary equality can occur only at z=0, where the roots are 1,1/3. Hence BDF2 is A-stable and its two roots tend to zero for very large negative z, giving strong stiff-mode damping. This is a complete boundary-locus argument, not just a boundary plot.
The Second Dahlquist barrier says that a convergent A-stable ordinary linear multistep method has order at most two. This theorem does not limit implicit Runge-Kutta methods or multiderivative multistep methods. In particular the A-stable third-order two-step multiderivative method in question 1 uses y′′ and satisfies different order conditions. Among collocation Runge-Kutta methods, the standard family results give Gauss collocation methods order 2s with diagonal [s/s] Padé approximants to ez as stability functions, and Radau IIA methods order 2s−1 with subdiagonal [s−1/s] approximants. Both families are A-stable; the Gauss functions have a nonzero limit at infinity, whereas Radau IIA is L-stable. The corrected two-stage right-Radau method, for example, has
R(z)=1−2z/3+z2/61+z/3​,
(44)
which matches the exponential through cubic order and tends to zero at infinity. Its poles 2±i2​ lie in the right half-plane. For z=−r+iy, r≥0, direct subtraction gives
∣1−2z/3+z2/6∣2−∣1+z/3∣2=36r(r+6)(r2+2r+12)+2r(r+4)y2+y4​≥0,
(45)
proving its A-stability directly.
Sectorial A-alpha stability relaxes the requirement to a sector around the negative real axis, useful when the spectrum of a stiff problem remains in that sector; higher-order backward differentiation formulas illustrate the usefulness of this weaker requirement. For a normal linear system y′=Ly, unitary diagonalization reduces the numerical update to its scalar factors R(hλj​). For a nonnormal system, a scalar eigenvalue test alone does not give a uniform bound in the chosen norm. Nonlinear stability is different again: B-stability asks for contractivity when Re⟨f(u)−f(v),u−v⟩≤0, and algebraic stability of a Runge-Kutta method is a standard sufficient criterion. Thus A-stability addresses a precise and valuable linear test, while step selection still has to meet accuracy requirements and the actual implicit equations must be solved on the relevant branch.

Eigenvalue analysis proves mesh-uniform stability when it is accompanied by control of eigenvectors or an energy norm; the signs of eigenvalues alone are insufficient. For an evolution equation, the method of lines first produces uh′​=Lh​uh​. A time integrator then gives an amplification matrix Sh,δ​, for example Sh,δ​=R(δLh​) for a Runge-Kutta method. The useful stability requirement on a fixed physical time interval is
supnδ≤T​∥Sh,δn​∥h​≤CT​,
(46)
where CT​ is independent of the refining spatial and temporal meshes. The semidiscrete analogue is ∥etLh​∥h​≤CT​ for 0≤t≤T. These are bounds on propagation of initial errors and residuals, not merely statements that each finite matrix has a bounded solution over its own finite time interval.
If Lh​ is a normal matrix in the chosen discrete inner product, it has a unitary eigenbasis. Then
∥etLh​∥h​=maxj​etReλj​,∥R(δLh​)n∥h​=maxj​∣R(δλj​)∣n.
(47)
For dissipative problems it is sufficient that every δλj​ lie in the integrator's linear stability domain. A diagonalizable family also suffices if its eigenvector matrices have uniformly bounded condition numbers: the same maximum is multiplied by ∥Vh​∥∥Vh−1​∥. Without that uniform bound the argument does not establish mesh stability. For finite-time estimates, an amplification modulus 1+O(δ) can also be acceptable; strict contraction at every step is a stronger property.
For a constant-coefficient stencil on the full lattice or a periodic grid, von Neumann stability analysis provides the eigenvectors explicitly. A Fourier mode diagonalizes each translation-invariant spatial difference, giving a scalar symbol λh​(θ) or a small matrix symbol for a system. The discrete Fourier transform and Parseval identity turn a uniform modal bound into an actual discrete L2 norm bound. The two-dimensional stencil in question 3 has Reλh​=−(1−cosη)2/h≤0, so its continuous-time amplification is contractive; a later time integrator must still be checked. With boundaries one must analyze the boundary rows as well: a whole-line Fourier symbol cannot detect an unstable boundary closure.
For the heat equation ut​=κuxx​ on a unit interval with zero Dirichlet data, the centred second difference has a sine eigenbasis and eigenvalues
λj​=−h24κ​sin22Njπ​,h=1/N,1≤j<N.
(48)
Explicit Euler method multiplies mode j by 1+δλj​. Requiring all factors to have modulus at most one yields κδ/h2≤1/[2sin2((N−1)π/(2N))] on a given grid, and the sharp grid-independent sufficient limit is
κδ/h2≤21​in one dimension,κδ/h2≤41​on the standard equal-mesh two-dimensional grid.​
(49)
These parabolic time-step restrictions express the growth of the largest-magnitude diffusion eigenvalue like h−2. Backward Euler method instead has factor (1−δλj​)−1 and the Crank-Nicolson method factor (1+δλj​/2)/(1−δλj​/2), both bounded by one for every δ≥0. Their unconditional stability follows from A-stability, with much stronger damping of the shortest scales for backward Euler.
For ut​=−aux​, a>0, explicit time stepping with a backward spatial difference gives
S(θ)=1−ν+νe−iθ,ν=aδ/h,∣S(θ)∣2=1−2ν(1−ν)(1−cosθ).
(50)
Thus the upwind finite difference scheme is contractive exactly for 0≤ν≤1, a Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition matching the direction and speed of information propagation. A centred spatial difference has purely imaginary eigenvalues, and forward Euler gives ∣S∣2=1+ν2sin2θ>1. Under a fixed positive hyperbolic Courant number, high-frequency data grow by a fixed factor in each of O(1/h) steps, proving instability as the mesh is refined. This is not a claim that no exceptionally small coupled step can ever give a finite-time bound: if δ=O(h2), the excess per step is only O(δ), but the usual hyperbolic scaling is unstable. Higher-order explicit integrators can possess nontrivial imaginary-axis stability intervals and are then suitable for skew-adjoint advection or the energy-form first-order wave equation.
Variable coefficients usually prevent Fourier diagonalization, but may preserve a self-adjoint spatial operator. The positive-face diffusion discretization in question 5 is an example. Its discrete energy identity makes A+B symmetric negative definite, so a unitary eigenbasis still exists even without a closed eigenvalue formula. More generally, a dissipative operator Lh​ in a discrete energy inner product obeys
dtd​∥uh​∥h2​=2Re⟨Lh​uh​,uh​⟩h​≤0,
(51)
which directly proves a uniform semigroup estimate. The dissipative Cayley-transform contraction also follows without normality: for α≥0,
∥(I+αLh​)v∥h2​−∥(I−αLh​)v∥h2​=4αRe⟨Lh​v,v⟩h​≤0.
(52)
Taking v=(I−αLh​)−1u proves contraction of the Crank–Nicolson factor. The contractivity of split Crank-Nicolson diffusion then follows from multiplying two contraction bounds; simultaneous eigenvectors of the two directional matrices are unnecessary.
The negative spectra do not imply uniform semidiscrete stability example shows what can go wrong. Let
Lh​=(−10​h−1−1​),etLh​=e−t(10​t/h1​).
(53)
Both eigenvalues are −1 for every mesh, but applying the propagator to the second unit vector at t=1 gives norm at least 1/(eh). The evolution is therefore not mesh-uniformly stable. For a discrete matrix, a unit-modulus eigenvalue with a nontrivial Jordan block gives powers growing polynomially in the step number. Even strictly interior eigenvalues can allow large transient growth when eigenvectors are badly conditioned. A pseudospectrum or numerical range of an operator, and especially a direct energy estimate, can reveal behavior missed by eigenvalues.
A resolvent estimate provides another way to see the needed uniformity. If ∥Shn​∥≤C for every n, then for ∣z∣>1,
(zI−Sh​)−1=∑n=0∞​zn+1Shn​​,∥(zI−Sh​)−1∥≤∣z∣−1C​.
(54)
This shows directly that a uniform power estimate imposes more than the location of the spectrum. Conversely, resolvent-to-power estimates in varying dimension must themselves be uniform; a bound with constants depending on the number of grid points is not enough.
For a linear multistep method, each spatial eigenvalue gives an amplification polynomial, and all roots must satisfy the root condition for a multistep method, including simplicity of any unit-modulus root. Merely following the root approximating eδλ misses parasitic modes. The temporal and spatial analyses must therefore be combined with the appropriate zero-stability and uniform modal bounds.
Finally stability connects to convergence. If the exact grid samples have normalized residual τn and the error obeys en+1=Sh​en+δτn, iteration gives
∥en∥h​≤CT​(∥e0∥h​+δ∑j<n​∥τj∥h​).
(55)
A residual tending uniformly to zero then gives convergence on [0,T]. For a well-posed linear initial-value problem and a consistent linear finite-difference approximation, this is the forward implication of the Lax equivalence theorem. The practical conclusion is to analyze the full operator including boundaries, select a time method whose stability region contains the relevant scaled spectrum, and verify that eigenvectors or an energy estimate make the resulting bounds uniform as the grid is refined.
