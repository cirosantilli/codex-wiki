The density operators are Hermitian, so their squared Hilbert-Schmidt distance is 2V=Tr(ρ2)+Tr(ρd2​)−2Tr(ρρd​). Both purity of a density operator values are constant under their respective unitary time evolution: cyclicity gives Tr(ρ[H,ρ])=0 for every Hermitian H. Consequently
V˙=−Tr(ρ˙​ρd​)−Tr(ρρ˙​d​)=−fTr(ρd​[−iH1​,ρ]).
(1)
The terms containing H0​ cancel because Tr([−iH0​,ρ]ρd​)=−Tr(ρ[−iH0​,ρd​]). The quantity F=Tr(ρd​[−iH1​,ρ]) is real: [−iH1​,ρ] is Hermitian, and the trace of the product of two Hermitian matrices equals its conjugate. The Lyapunov quantum control choice f=F therefore gives V˙=−f2≤0​. For t2​≥t1​, integration yields V(t2​)=V(t1​)−∫t1​t2​​f(t)2dt≤V(t1​). Since the distance is 2V​, it is monotonically nonincreasing. The decrease need not be strict when f=0.

Neither monotonic decrease alone nor equality of the initial spectra guarantees convergence. A nonnegative Lyapunov function can approach a positive limiting value, and the set where V˙=0 can contain trajectories other than the target. Different initial spectra already obstruct exact tracking: unitary time evolution preserves the eigenvalues of each density operator, while two matrices approaching one another in norm must have the same limiting eigenvalues.
Even the spectral obstruction can be absent while the Lyapunov quantum control stalls. Take a qubit with H0​=Z/2, H1​=X, ρd​(0)=∣0⟩⟨0∣ and ρ(0)=∣1⟩⟨1∣. Both density operators commute with H0​, and the commutator of X with ρ has zero diagonal, so f=Tr(ρd​[−iX,ρ])=0. Both states remain constant. They have the identical spectrum (1,0), but ∥ρ(t)−ρd​(t)∥HS​=2​ for all t​. Moreover iZ,iX generate the full special unitary Lie algebra su(2), so the failure here is a limitation of this particular feedback law, not a lack of available state-transfer controls.

The Lyapunov quantum control law requires an exact instantaneous expectation value. Cyclicity writes it as
f(t)=Tr(ρ(t)A(t)),A(t)=i[H1​,ρd​(t)],
(2)
where A(t) is a known Hermitian observable. A measurement on one system produces a random outcome, not its exact ensemble expectation, and changes the system state. Repeated measurements for state estimation or continuous weak measurements introduce noise and backaction absent from the assumed closed-system equation. Thus the law cannot be implemented unchanged as exact measurement-based quantum feedback while retaining the dynamics and guarantee in part (a). A conditional-state feedback scheme must include the measurement evolution and rederive its stability properties.
If the initial density operator and Hamiltonian model are known, we can instead solve the deterministic equations in a computer, calculate f(t) along the simulated trajectory, and apply that precomputed waveform in the laboratory as open-loop control. Ensemble measurements can help calibrate such a waveform, but they do not supply nondisturbing instantaneous feedback from an individual evolving system.

A quantum optimal control problem specifies a terminal task, admissible fields and resource costs. Pure-state transfer can minimize 1−∣⟨ψd​∣ψ(T)⟩∣2; mixed-state transfer on an accessible spectral orbit can minimize ∥ρ(T)−ρd​∥HS2​/2; preparing an observable value can minimize −Tr(Oρ(T)). A gate task can minimize 1−∣Tr(Ud†​U(T))∣2/n2 for an n-dimensional Hilbert space, which is insensitive to an overall phase. These different terminal functions encode different tasks even when the dynamics are the same. Fluence penalties, amplitude bounds, bandwidth limits, or the total duration can be added to express experimental requirements.
For example, minimize
J[f]=Φ(ρ(T))+21​∑m​λm​∫0T​fm​(t)2dt,λm​>0,
(3)
subject to ρ˙​=Lf​(ρ) and a specified ρ(0). For a closed system, Lf​(ρ)=−i[H0​+∑m​fm​Hm​,ρ]; for an open system add the known Lindblad equation dissipator. Introducing a Hermitian costate Λ and taking the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product gives the forward and backward equations and functional gradient
ρ˙​=Lf​(ρ),Λ˙=−Lf†​(Λ),Λ(T)=∇ρ​Φ(ρ(T)),
(4)
δfm​(t)δJ​=λm​fm​(t)+Tr(Λ(t)∂fm​∂Lf​​(ρ(t)))​.
(5)
For Hamiltonian controls the second term is Tr(Λ[−iHm​,ρ]). The adjoint is defined by the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product; in particular, the closed-system costate equation is Λ˙=−i[H,Λ], integrated backward from its terminal condition. An unconstrained interior optimum has zero gradient; amplitude constraints replace this by the corresponding constrained first-order conditions.
Numerically, represent the fields by piecewise constant amplitudes fm,j​ on slices, or by a finite basis fm​(t)=∑j​amj​bj​(t) such as splines or Fourier modes. For piecewise constant closed-system dynamics, Uj​=e−iHj​Δt and U(T)=UK​⋯U1​. Forward products and backward costates provide all slice derivatives in one pair of propagations. In gradient ascent pulse engineering, update these amplitudes to improve the chosen objective, with projection onto amplitude bounds if needed. The exact slice derivative is the derivative of the matrix exponential
∂fm,j​∂Uj​​=−i∫0Δt​e−iHj​(Δt−τ)Hm​e−iHj​τdτ.
(6)
Replacing it by −iΔtHm​Uj​ is only a short-slice approximation when Hj​ and Hm​ do not commute. Other gradient descent or nonlinear optimization methods can optimize the same finite parameter set; direct-adjoint looping avoids a separate dynamical simulation for each control coefficient.
A pulse is accepted after propagation with the actual constrained waveform confirms its terminal error and resource use. Sampling model uncertainties in the objective can favor robust pulses. Nonconvexity means a numerical stationary point need not be a global optimum, so initial guesses, convergence tolerances and a sufficiently resolved time grid matter.

Spectral pulse shaping implements a calculated optical control by modifying its complex frequency spectrum. If the available input pulse has spectrum Ein​(ω), program a calibrated mask M(ω)=a(ω)eiφ(ω) so that
Eout​(ω)=M(ω)Ein​(ω),Eout​(t)=2π1​∫Eout​(ω)e−iωtdω​.
(7)
The temporal waveform follows from Fourier inversion. For a real applied field, its spectrum has conjugate symmetry, so the mask must satisfy M(−ω)=M(ω)∗. Spectral phase controls interference between frequencies and hence their arrival times, chirp and temporal subpulses; spectral attenuation changes their weights. The optimized field envelope is translated into this complex mask, with compensation for known phase shifts and losses in the optical system.
A typical 4f pulse shaper contains a first diffraction grating, a focusing lens, a mask or spatial light modulator in the common focal plane, a second lens and a second grating. The first grating separates the frequencies by angle. The first lens converts those angles into different transverse positions at the spectral Fourier plane. The pixels of the spatial light modulator modify the amplitude and phase of the corresponding frequency bins. The second lens and grating reverse this spatial dispersion and recombine the components into one shaped output beam. In the ideal symmetric layout the four consecutive propagation distances are f,f,f,f, giving the name 4f.
A phase-only spatial light modulator needs suitable polarization optics or another modulation stage for independent amplitude shaping. A passive mask has 0≤a(ω)≤1 and cannot create frequencies absent from the input; finite bandwidth and finite pixel resolution limit temporal features and the useful shaping window. These constraints must be included when converting a mathematical optimum into a laboratory pulse. The programmed mask is an open-loop control, although repeated measurements of the output pulse or system performance can be used to calibrate or optimize it experimentally.

A steady state is a time-independent density operator satisfying L(ρ∗​)=0, equivalently a constant physical Bloch vector s∗​ satisfying As∗​=−c​. Let K=N2−1. By the rank criterion for a linear system, this equation has a solution exactly when
rankA=rank[A∣c]​.
(13)
When it is consistent, every algebraic solution has the form s0​+v with v∈kerA, so the solution affine space has dimension K−rankA by the rank-nullity theorem. Physical steady states are its intersection with the positive density operator body. The algebraic equilibrium is unique exactly when rankA=K, in which case s∗​=−A−1c​.
An arbitrary affine differential equation need not have an equilibrium: A=0, c=0 is inconsistent. For the finite-dimensional, time-independent Lindblad equation under discussion, however, a physical steady state always exists. Indeed, the averages ρˉ​T​=T−1∫0T​ρ(t)dt remain positive with trace one. Compactness supplies a convergent subsequence as T→∞, and
L(ρˉ​T​)=Tρ(T)−ρ(0)​⟶0.
(14)
The limit is a physical steady state, proving that Finite-dimensional Lindbladians have stationary states and that the rank consistency condition is automatically satisfied for this physical generator.
In this setting, full rank also characterizes uniqueness among physical steady states. To justify the converse when A is singular, a nonzero v∈kerA gives a fixed traceless Hermitian operator X=∑k​vk​σk​. Write its positive-negative decomposition X=X+​−X−​. Time-average the evolutions of the two positive operators along a common convergent subsequence; the limits Y±​ are positive stationary operators and satisfy X=Y+​−Y−​. Their traces are equal because TrX=0. If there were only one stationary density operator, both Y±​ would be this matrix times the same trace, forcing X=0, a contradiction. Hence unique physical steady state⟺rankA=N2−1​.

A steady state is attractive here if every physical initial density operator approaches it. Subtracting its Affine Bloch equation from the trajectory equation gives δs˙=Aδs, so δs(t)=eAtδs(0). The necessary and sufficient condition is
Reλ<0for every eigenvalue λ of A​.
(15)
Such an A is a Hurwitz stable matrix. To prove sufficiency without assuming diagonalizability, put it in Jordan normal form. Each Jordan block contributes eλt∑j=0q−1​tjNj/j!, and the negative real part makes the exponential dominate all polynomial factors. For necessity, an eigenmode with positive real part grows; an eigenmode with zero real part remains constant or oscillates, so it does not tend to zero. A nontrivial Jordan block on the imaginary axis also prevents decay.
The physical Bloch vector body has nonempty interior, so differences of physical initial vectors from s∗​ span the full real coordinate space. Thus convergence for every physical initial state also forces eAt→0 on that space. This rules out hiding a nondecaying eigenmode outside the physical set. The strict spectral condition entails uniqueness and global asymptotic stability of the steady state.

For reachability in control theory, a state x1​ is reachable from x0​ if an admissible input and a permitted duration drive the dynamical system from x0​ to x1​. Controllability requires this for every initial-target pair in the specified state space. Restrictions on duration or field amplitudes change the reachable set, so the admissible controls are part of the definition.
For a closed quantum system, U˙=−iH[f(t)]U, U(0)=I, and states evolve as ψ(t)=U(t)ψ(0) or ρ(t)=U(t)ρ(0)U(t)†. This gives three notions of quantum controllability. Unitary operator controllability requires every desired Ud​∈U(N) to be reachable from the identity; when a global phase is immaterial, one instead asks for every special unitary operation. Density operator controllability requires every pair of density operators with the same spectrum to be connected. The spectral qualification is necessary because unitary time evolution cannot change eigenvalues; the natural state space is a unitary orbit of a density operator. Pure-state controllability requires any normalized initial state to be transferred to any target ray, U(T)ψ0​=eiχψd​.
Thus operator controllability⇒density operator controllability⇒pure-state controllability​, with the operator implication also valid if global phase is discarded. The converse implications need not hold. A pure-state task concerns one vector, whereas an operator task must act correctly on every input vector at once.

Assume the usual real Hamiltonian controls with freely chosen finite duration, rather than a fixed-time or amplitude-restricted task. Define the Dynamical Lie algebra
g=LieR​{iH0​,iH1​,…,iHM​}⊆u(N),
(19)
the real linear span closed under all nested commutators. Replacing each generator by −iHm​ gives the same algebra. The associated connected reachable group acts on propagators, on each unitary orbit of a density operator, and on pure states. For N≥2, the necessary and sufficient conditions are as follows.
For exact unitary operator controllability, g=u(N)​. For operator control up to global phase, the possibilities are su(N) or u(N). If every Hamiltonian is traceless, detU(T)=exp[−i∫0T​TrH(t)dt]=1, so the central phase direction is absent and exact access is to SU(N). The full special unitary Lie algebra has dimension N2−1 and the Lie algebra of the unitary group has dimension N2.
For density operator controllability on every spectral orbit,
g=su(N) or u(N)​.
(20)
Scalar phases do not affect conjugation. If only a particular density spectrum is being considered, a smaller group can suffice: for distinct eigenvalue multiplicities nj​ and centralizer cρ​={X∈u(N):[X,ρ]=0}, the orbit criterion is dimg−dim(g∩cρ​)=N2−∑j​nj2​. A maximally mixed state has a one-point orbit and needs no controls. This restricted-orbit condition is weaker than controllability for every density spectrum.
For pure-state controllability, up to a fixed unitary change of basis,
g=su(N), u(N), sp(N/2), or sp(N/2)⊕RiI​,
(21)
where the last two alternatives occur only for even N. Here sp(n) is the standard compact symplectic Lie algebra acting on C2n, not the real noncompact symplectic algebra. These are precisely the unitary matrix algebras whose connected groups act transitively on normalized complex vectors, and also give the ray version of controllability. For N=2, sp(1)=su(2). For even N≥4, the symplectic cases show why pure-state control can be weaker than density control: dimsp(n)=n(2n+1) is smaller than the nondegenerate density-orbit dimension N2−N, and its scalar center acts trivially on that orbit. For N=1, the physical density and pure-state spaces are single points, while exact operator controllability still requires the phase algebra u(1).

For the physical generator x(t)=−i(A+f(t)C), differentiation gives dtd​(UTJU)=UT(xTJ+Jx)U=0, and therefore U(t)TJU(t)=J​. This is an orthogonal dynamical symmetry in quantum control: a preserved symmetric bilinear form. It need not give eigenspaces of a commuting operator; indeed J does not commute with A or C.
Set D=diag(1,i,1). Then DTJD=I, so the transformed generators x′=D†xD satisfy x′T+x′=0 as well as x′†+x′=0. They are consequently real antisymmetric, lying in so(3). The original generators already supply the independent directions p=iC=i(E12​+E21​) and q=i(A−C)=i(E23​+E32​), and their commutator is [p,q]=E31​−E13​. These three independent directions exhaust the three-dimensional algebra. Thus
D†gD=so(3),dimg=3​.
(28)
Here Emn​ denotes a matrix unit. The reachable connected group is a unitary conjugate of SO(3), a proper subgroup of SU(3), so there is no unitary operator controllability.
Pure-state control also fails. For every reachable state ψ′=Uψ, the quantity ψ′TJψ′=ψTJψ is invariant, and its absolute value is unchanged even by an overall state phase. The normalized states e1​ and (e1​+e2​)/2​ have respectively ∣ψTJψ∣=1 and 0, so no allowed control connects their rays. Therefore neither pure-state nor density operator controllability holds​ for this reduced three-level system, despite the absence of a simple excitation-sector obstruction within the sector.
