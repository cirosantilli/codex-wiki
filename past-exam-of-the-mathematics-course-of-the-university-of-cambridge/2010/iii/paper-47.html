For the usual canonical scalar field theory, subtract the vacuum energy so that the potential U is nonnegative, and write the static energy in d space dimensions as
E[ϕ]=T+W,T=21​∫Rd​∣∇ϕ∣2,W=∫Rd​U(ϕ).
(1)
Assume finite energy and sufficient decay to justify the scaling variation, with a fixed vacuum at infinity. Under Derrick scaling ϕλ​(x)=ϕ(λx),
E[ϕλ​]=λ2−dT+λ−dW.
(2)
A static solution must be stationary under this admissible variation. Thus the Derrick virial identity is
(2−d)T−dW=0.
(3)
For d≥3 both terms are nonpositive, and the coefficient of T is strictly negative. Therefore T=W=0: the field is a constant vacuum. This proves the Derrick theorem under its hypotheses. Negative potentials, additional derivative terms, gauge fields or time dependence can invalidate this particular obstruction; it is not a theorem about arbitrary energy functionals.
A non-topological soliton can instead be supported by a conserved ordinary Noether charge. For a complex scalar field with global U(1) invariance, take ϕ=eiωtf(x) and let I=∫f2. In the normalization with kinetic density ∣∂ϕ∣2/2, its charge is Q=ωI. At fixed charge, eliminating ω gives
EQ​[f]=21​∫∣∇f∣2+∫U(f)+2IQ2​.
(4)
The charge-constrained scalar-field scaling now reads
EQ​[fλ​]=λ2−dT+λ−dW+λd2IQ2​.
(5)
The positive scaling power of the charge term can balance the others even for d≥3. Such a Q-ball is time-dependent although its energy density is stationary, and the fixed-charge variational problem is different from the static one.
Suitable potentials permit this mechanism in any spatial dimension. For example, suppose U(f)>0 for f=0 and the ratio below attains a strictly positive minimum:
0<ω02​:=minf>0​f22U(f)​<U′′(0)=m2
(6)
Such a potential can favor a large region of nearly constant nonzero amplitude. At its preferred amplitude, the bulk terms minimize at I≈∣Q∣/ω0​, with energy about ω0​∣Q∣. A wall has surface energy of order ∣Q∣(d−1)/d, lower order than the bulk term for every fixed d≥1. For large charge the energy can therefore be below the free-particle threshold m∣Q∣. This explains how a localized fixed-charge solution may exist and resist dispersal. Existence and stability still require a suitable potential and charge range; not every complex-field theory or every member of a rotating family is a constrained minimum.
For the particular one-dimensional profile, substituting the rotating field into the equation gives
f′′=(1−ω2)f−f3.
(7)
Multiplying by f′ shows that
dxd​[21​(f′)2−21​(1−ω2)f2+41​f4]=0.
(8)
Both f and f′ vanish at infinity, so the integration constant is zero. Hence
(f′)2=(1−ω2)f2−21​f4.​
(9)
Here U(f)=f2/2−f4/4. This potential is not globally bounded below; the existence of the displayed localized solutions alone does not prove their nonlinear stability.
Use metric signature (+,−) and set
γ=(1−v2)−1/2,ξ=γ(x−x0​−vt),τ=γ(t−v(x−x0​)).
(10)
The Lorentz boost of a Q-ball gives
ϕv​(t,x)=eiωτf(ξ),∣v∣<1.​
(11)
The peak follows x=x0​+vt. Because the equation is Lorentz invariant and the field is a scalar, this is again a solution.
The canonical stress-energy tensor gives the energy and signed physical momentum at fixed time:
E=∫R​[21​∣ϕt​∣2+21​∣ϕx​∣2+21​∣ϕ∣2−41​∣ϕ∣4]dx,P=−∫R​Re(ϕt​​ϕx​)dx.
(12)
The minus sign agrees with the scalar-field momentum flux in the chosen signature. For the boosted field,
∣ϕt​∣2=γ2[ω2f2+v2(f′)2],∣ϕx​∣2=γ2[(f′)2+v2ω2f2],
(13)
and
−Re(ϕt​​ϕx​)=γ2v[(f′)2+ω2f2].
(14)
The first integral gives U(f)=21​[(f′)2+ω2f2]. Changing variable to ξ, so dx=dξ/γ, yields
E=2γγ2(1+v2)+1​∫R​[(f′)2+ω2f2]dξ=γM,P=γvM.
(15)
Thus the rest-energy normalization and the relativistic energy-momentum relation are
M=∫R​[(f′)2+ω2f2]dx,E2−P2=M2.​
(16)

Choose orientation ϵ1234​=1 and the anti-Hermitian basis in the question. The Pauli matrix commutator identity gives [ea​,eb​]=ϵabc​ec​. Define the gauge curvature and adjoint covariant derivative by
Fμν​=∂μ​Aν​−∂ν​Aμ​+[Aμ​,Aν​],Dμ​X=∂μ​X+[Aμ​,X].
(17)
For a compactly supported variation aμ​=δAμ​,
δFμν​=Dμ​aν​−Dν​aμ​.
(18)
Antisymmetry and the invariant Lie-algebra inner product therefore give
δV=4∫R4​⟨Fμν​,Dμ​aν​⟩=−4∫R4​⟨Dμ​Fμν​,aν​⟩.
(19)
Covariant integration by parts is valid because
⟨X,[A,Y]⟩=−⟨[A,X],Y⟩. Thus the Euler-Lagrange field equations are
Dμ​Fμν​=0,∂μ​Fμνa​+ϵabc​Aμb​Fμνc​=0.​
(20)
The Euclidean Hodge star operator on two-forms is
(∗F)μν​=21​ϵμνκλ​Fκλ​,∗2=1.
(21)
The Anti-self-dual Yang-Mills equations mean ∗F=−F​.
The gauge-theory Bianchi identity is
Dμ​Fνρ​+Dν​Fρμ​+Dρ​Fμν​=0.​
(22)
To prove it, act on the defining representation with ∇μ​=∂μ​+Aμ​. Its commutator is multiplication by Fμν​. The operator Jacobi identity for ∇μ​,∇ν​,∇ρ​ becomes multiplication by the displayed cyclic sum, which must vanish. Equivalently, substitution of F=dA+A∧A into DA​F cancels the ordinary derivatives, leaving the Lie-algebra Jacobi identity. Contracting the cyclic identity with the alternating tensor gives Dμ​(∗F)μν​=0. If ∗F=−F, this is exactly Dμ​Fμν​=0, proving the first-order-to-second-order implication without assuming the field equations.
Let
J=∫R4​ϵμνκλ​Fμνa​Fκλa​d4x=2∫R4​Fμνa​(∗F)μνa​d4x.
(23)
All squared component sums below include both orders of each antisymmetric pair, as does the printed energy. Since the Hodge star preserves this norm,
V=21​∫R4​∣F+∗F∣2d4x−2J​=21​∫R4​∣F−∗F∣2d4x+2J​.​
(24)
Consequently V≥∣J∣/2. An anti-self-dual field has J=−2V≤0 and saturates the first bound. Among all finite-energy potentials with the same value of J, it therefore has the absolute minimum −J/2. For positive J, the saturating sign is self-duality instead; there cannot be a nonzero anti-self-dual solution in that sector. This is the Yang-Mills instanton Bogomolny bound in the paper's normalization.
For the topological meaning, regard F=21​Fμνa​ea​dxμ∧dxν as a matrix-valued two-form. Since tr(ea​eb​)=−δab​/2,
tr(F∧F)=−81​ϵμνκλ​Fμνa​Fκλa​d4x.
(25)
In the Second Chern number convention used here,
k=8π21​∫R4​tr(F∧F)=−64π2J​.​
(26)
With the standard instanton boundary condition, the connection approaches a pure gauge at infinity and extends to a bundle over the compactified four-sphere. This makes k an integer, the instanton number; reversing the orientation or using the opposite charge convention reverses its sign. The square-completion argument itself does not require quantization or a chosen framing.
One can see the boundary interpretation directly. The Chern-Simons 3-form obeys
dtr(A∧dA+32​A∧A∧A)=tr(F∧F).
(27)
For an asymptotic potential A=−dgg−1, the flatness identity dA=−A∧A gives
k=24π21​∫S∞3​​tr[(dgg−1)∧3].
(28)
This is the winding number of g:S∞3​→SU(2)≅S3, with the corresponding orientation convention. Smooth variations preserving that boundary class cannot change it. Thus the integral in the question labels the topological sector of the Yang-Mills instanton, and in an anti-self-dual sector the energy is V=32π2k​ with k≥0 in our convention.

Put h=∣Φ∣2, Di​=∂i​−iAi​, ji​=Im(ΦˉDi​Φ), and W(h)=h(1−h)2/4. Because the inverse-density Abelian Higgs model contains B2/h, its finite-energy admissible class matters at zeros of the Higgs field. First derive the formal equations on the open set where h>0; on the first-order solutions below the potentially singular quotients extend across the zeros.
For variations ψ=δΦ and ai​=δAi​,
δh=2Re(Φˉψ),δB=∂1​a2​−∂2​a1​,δ(Di​Φ)=Di​ψ−iai​Φ.
(29)
Covariant integration by parts in the scalar term, and ordinary integration by parts in the magnetic term, give
δV=∫Re[(−Di​Di​Φ−h2B2​Φ+W′(h)Φ)​ψ]d2x+∫[ϵij​∂j​(B/h)−ji​]ai​d2x,
(30)
where ϵ12​=1. Here
W′(h)=41​(1−h)(1−3h).
(31)
Thus the Euler-Lagrange field equations are
Di​Di​Φ+[h2B2​−41​(1−h)(1−3h)]Φ=0,​
(32)
∂2​(B/h)=j1​,−∂1​(B/h)=j2​.​
(33)
Equivalently the gauge equation is ∂j​(Fji​/h)+ji​=0. The current sign follows from the explicit choice D=∂−iA.
For the Bogomolny bound for the inverse-density Abelian Higgs model, compute
∂1​j2​−∂2​j1​=2Im(D1​Φ​D2​Φ)−Bh,
(34)
using [D1​,D2​]Φ=−iBΦ. Expanding the first square gives
∣(D1​+iD2​)Φ∣2=∣DΦ∣2−2Im(D1​Φ​D2​Φ),
(35)
so
∣DΦ∣2=∣(D1​+iD2​)Φ∣2+Bh+∂1​j2​−∂2​j1​.
(36)
Also,
hB2​+4h​(1−h)2=h[B−21​h(1−h)]2​+B(1−h).
(37)
The current boundary integral vanishes by the assumed rapid decay. Combining the identities therefore gives
V=21​∫R2​[∣(D1​+iD2​)Φ∣2+h[B−21​h(1−h)]2​]d2x+πN.​
(38)
For fixed positive flux the minimum is Vmin​=πN​, attained when the Bogomolny equations are
(D1​+iD2​)Φ=0,B=21​h(1−h).​
(39)
The quotient at a zero is understood through the finite-energy limit, not by dividing a literal nonzero numerator by zero.
These equations imply the second-order equations directly. Applying D1​−iD2​ to the first gives (Di​Di​+B)Φ=0. On the second equation,
−h2B2​+W′(h)=−41​(1−h)2+41​(1−h)(1−3h)=−21​h(1−h)=−B,
(40)
which is exactly the scalar equation. The first equation also implies j1​=−21​∂2​h and j2​=21​∂1​h. Since B/h=(1−h)/2, the gauge equations follow too. All these coefficients are regular across zeros for smooth first-order fields.
For the scalar reduction, write Φ=eu/2+iχ away from its zeros. Separating the real and imaginary parts of the first Bogomolny equation gives
A1​=∂1​χ+21​∂2​u,A2​=∂2​χ−21​∂1​u.
(41)
Thus, with the oriented planar Hodge star ∗dx1=dx2, ∗dx2=−dx1,
A=dχ−21​∗du,B=−21​Δu
(42)
away from the zeros. A zero pj​ of multiplicity nj​ has u=2nj​log∣x−pj​∣+smooth and phase winding 2πnj​. The multiplicities are positive: locally the first-order equation is a covariant Cauchy–Riemann equation, and a nonvanishing integrating factor turns its solutions into holomorphic functions. Distributionally,
B=2π∑j​nj​δpj​​−21​Δu.
(43)
Consequently the logarithmic equation for inverse-density Abelian Higgs vortices is
Δu+eu(1−eu)=4πj∑​nj​δpj​​,u(x)→0 as ∣x∣→∞,j∑​nj​=N.​
(44)
The delta terms are essential for multi-vortex reconstruction; omitting them would lose the specified zeros and flux.
Here is an explicit regular reconstruction, which also removes all artificial singularities. Given distinct locations pj​ and integer multiplicities, put
u0​(x)=∑j​nj​log1+∣x−pj​∣2∣x−pj​∣2​,g(x)=4∑j​(1+∣x−pj​∣2)2nj​​.
(45)
Since Δu0​=4π∑j​nj​δpj​​−g, solving the smooth nonlinear elliptic problem for v=u−u0​ amounts to
Δv=g−eu0​+v(1−eu0​+v),v→0.​
(46)
Let z=x1+ix2 and let zj​ represent pj​. A smooth solution with the topological decay reconstructs
Φ(z)=ev(z)/2j∏​(1+∣z−zj​∣2​z−zj​​)nj​,​
(47)
A=21​j∑​nj​∗dlog(1+∣x−pj​∣2)−21​∗dv.​
(48)
These formulas are smooth at every prescribed zero and agree with the phase formula off the zeros. Taking a curl and using the equation for v verifies B=eu(1−eu)/2; substitution verifies the other first-order equation. At infinity the phase winds N times, producing flux 2πN. Thus multi-vortex construction reduces to solving one scalar elliptic boundary-value problem for specified positions and multiplicities, followed by these explicit formulas.
For the topological branch, u≤0: a positive interior maximum would have Δu≤0, whereas the equation there gives Δu=eu(eu−1)>0. At infinity, the linearized scalar equation is Δu−u=0. Comparison barriers and elliptic estimates give the decaying tail for regular topological solutions, and hence the required decay of B,DΦ and 1−h. The nonlinear elliptic problem determines v; the reconstruction formulas do not constitute an arbitrary superposition of single-vortex profiles.

For a gauge-field configuration, spatial symmetry means symmetry up to gauge transformation: the pullback of the fields by a spatial isometry lies in their original gauge orbit. In the anti-Hermitian convention,
Ag=gAg−1−dgg−1,Φg=gΦg−1.
(49)
The potential and charged fields need not be strictly fixed in a particular gauge; their gauge-invariant observables must be fixed.
For example, the Abelian potential A=Bxdy describes a uniform magnetic field. Translating x by a adds Bady=d(Bay), a gauge change, although the potential itself changes. For an axially symmetric vortex Φ=f(r)eiNθ, A=a(r)dθ, rotation through β multiplies Φ by eiNβ; a constant U(1) transformation compensates. These illustrate why literal invariance of the representative is too strong.
For the 't Hooft-Polyakov monopole, a spatial rotation R∈SO(3) has an SU(2) lift gR​ with adjoint action R. The two lifts differ by a central sign and act identically on adjoint fields. The hedgehog ansatz for a monopole satisfies
Φa(Rx)=Rab​Φb(x),(R∗A)ia​(x)=Rji​Aja​(Rx)=Rab​Aib​(x).
(50)
The second identity follows from invariance of ϵiaj​ under simultaneous rotations of all three indices. Thus the pulled-back fields are obtained by the constant gauge transformation gR​; applying gR−1​ restores the representative. In particular ∣Φ∣2 and the energy density are radial even though Φa=f(r)xa/r is not literally invariant under rotating space alone.
For the field equations, use the invariant inner product with [ea​,eb​]=ϵabc​ec​. Since Bia​Bia​=21​Fija​Fija​, a variation aj​=δAj​, ψ=δΦ gives
δVλ​=2∫R3​⟨−Di​Fij​+[Φ,Dj​Φ],aj​⟩−2⟨Di​Di​Φ+λΦ,ψ⟩d3x.
(51)
Here the scalar kinetic contribution to the gauge variation uses
⟨Dj​Φ,[aj​,Φ]⟩=⟨[Φ,Dj​Φ],aj​⟩. The Euler-Lagrange field equations for the energy actually printed are
Di​Fij​=[Φ,Dj​Φ],Di​Di​Φ+λΦ=0.​
(52)
The PDF has λ(1−∣Φ∣2), without a square. This is not the usual nonnegative monopole potential with vacuum ∣Φ∣=1. If the usual λ(1−∣Φ∣2)2 were intended, only the scalar equation would change to
Di​Di​Φ+2λ(1−∣Φ∣2)Φ=0.
(53)
The final requested proof at λ=0 is identical for either interpretation; we do not silently alter the printed energy.
To derive the covariant derivative of the radial fields, first differentiate the Higgs profile:
∂k​Φa=rf​δak​+r1​(rf​)′xaxk.
(54)
The commutator term is
[Ak​,Φ]a=ϵabc​ϵkbj​xjxcrαf​=rαf​(r2δak​−xaxk).
(55)
Adding the two terms gives exactly
Dk​Φa=(rf​+rαf)δak​+r1​[(rf​)′−αf]xaxk.​
(56)
The radial eigenvalue of the supplied magnetic tensor is 2α+r2α2, while that of this covariant derivative is f′. Its transverse coefficients are respectively rα′+2α and f/r+rαf. Consequently B=−DΦ reduces to
f′=−2α−r2α2,rα′+2α=−rf​−rαf.​
(57)
Equivalently, the hedgehog monopole equations in radial profile variables with K=1+r2α are
K′=−fK,f′=r21−K2​.​
(58)
These are two equations rather than an overdetermined system: radial and transverse projections span the two invariant tensor structures. For a regular monopole in this hedgehog sector, smoothness at the origin gives f(0)=0, K(0)=1, while the normalized vacuum conditions give f→1, K→0 at infinity; solving the ODEs is not needed here.
Finally, prove that the Bogomolny monopole equations imply Yang-Mills-Higgs equations, without restricting to the radial ansatz. The three-dimensional gauge-theory Bianchi identity is Di​Bi​=0. If Bi​=−Di​Φ, then
Di​Di​Φ=−Di​Bi​=0,
(59)
which is the scalar equation at λ=0. For the gauge equation,
Di​Fij​​=−ϵijk​Di​Dk​Φ=−21​ϵijk​[Fik​,Φ]=[Bj​,Φ]=[Φ,Dj​Φ].​
(60)
In the second line antisymmetry eliminates the symmetric derivative part; the curvature commutator is [Di​,Dk​]Φ=[Fik​,Φ]. The next equality uses
ϵijk​ϵikℓ​=−2δjℓ​ and Fik​=ϵikℓ​Bℓ​. These are exactly both field equations at λ=0, so every smooth Bogomolny solution satisfies them, not merely those of the hedgehog form.
For finite-energy monopoles, the associated energy argument is also transparent:
V0​=∫∣B+DΦ∣2d3x−2∫S∞2​​⟨Φ,Bi​⟩dSi​.
(61)
The gauge-theory Bianchi identity converts ⟨Bi​,Di​Φ⟩ into this boundary term. At fixed asymptotic magnetic charge, B=−DΦ saturates the Bogomolny bound. This complements the local differential proof above and explains the energy-minimizing role of the first-order equations.
