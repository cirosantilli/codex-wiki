Put B=EI2​, the filament bending modulus in the easier bending direction; I2​ is the relevant second moment of area. Take filament tension as positive in extension. A cut at height z supports the weight of the material above it, so its axial force is compressive:
T(z)=−λg(L−z)​,T(L)=0.
(1)
The small transverse displacement induces a transverse tension force density (TXz​)z​, while bending produces −BXzzzz​. Their balance gives
BXzzzz​−(TXz​)z​=0​.
(2)
The same result follows from elastic energy and gravitational energy. To quadratic order, local inextensibility shortens the height of the material point labelled z by 21​∫0z​Xz​(s)2ds. Integrating its weight and interchanging the integrals gives the second-order potential energy
E2​[X]=21​∫0L​[BXzz2​−λg(L−z)Xz2​]dz=21​∫0L​[BXzz2​+TXz2​]dz.
(3)
Two integration by parts steps in its first variation give
δE2​=∫0L​[BXzzzz​−(TXz​)z​]δXdz+[BXzz​δXz​+(TXz​−BXzzz​)δX]0L​.
(4)
The clamped boundary conditions fix displacement and slope at the base, and the unconstrained tip must have zero bending moment and transverse end force. Thus the complete four conditions are
X(0)=Xz​(0)=0,Xzz​(L)=0,BXzzz​(L)−T(L)Xz​(L)=0​.
(5)
Since the filament tension vanishes at the tip, the last condition is Xzzz​(L)=0. Integrate the ordinary differential equation once: BXzzz​−TXz​ is constant, and its tip value makes that constant zero. For the slope u=Xz​, it follows that
Buzz​+λg(L−z)u=0,u(0)=0,uz​(L)=0​.
(6)
The other tip condition uzz​(L)=0 already follows from this equation for a regular solution; it is not a third independent condition on the second-order slope problem. Finally reconstruct X(z)=∫0z​u(s)ds to impose the base position.
Set x=L−z, α=λg/B, and η=(2/3)α​x3/2. Then dη/dx=3η/(2x) and d2η/dx2=3η/(4x2), so uxx​+αxu=0 becomes
η2uηη​+31​ηuη​+η2u=0.
(7)
Substituting u=η1/3F(η) gives
η2F′′+ηF′+(η2−91​)F=0.
(8)
This is the Bessel differential equation of order 1/3, whose two independent Bessel functions of the first kind give
u=η1/3[aJ−1/3​(η)+bJ1/3​(η)]​.
(9)
The tip is x=η=0. To decide which branch is admissible, retain both leading behaviours, not just the fact that u stays finite:
η1/3J−1/3​(η)=Γ(2/3)21/3​[1−83​η2+O(η4)],η1/3J1/3​(η)=21/3Γ(4/3)η2/3​[1+O(η2)].
(10)
Since η2∝x3 and η2/3∝x, the first branch has zero tip derivative, while the second has a nonzero tip derivative. The moment-free condition therefore sets b=0, and it does not require the tip slope itself to vanish. The base condition now requires
J−1/3​(32​EI2​λgL3​​)=0.
(11)
Let j−1/3,1​ be the first positive zero. The Bessel threshold for self-buckling of a vertical rod is
EI2​λgLc3​​=49​j−1/3,12​≃7.83735,Lc​≃1.98635(λgEI2​​)1/3​.
(12)
To identify this as the first loss of stability, write the quadratic energy in terms of the slope as 21​[B∫uz2​dz−λg∫(L−z)u2dz]. On functions with u(0)=0, its critical coefficient is the minimum Rayleigh quotient ∫uz2​dz/∫(L−z)u2dz; variation supplies the moment-free condition at the tip and the same Sturm-Liouville problem. The first zero gives the lowest eigenvalue, so the straight state is stable below this load, neutral at it, and unstable above it, with the easier I2​ direction buckling first.

Introduce a constant filament bending modulus B, which sets the mechanical force scale. The filament has intrinsic curvature of an elastic filament κ0​(s), and its actual signed curvature in a small-slope Monge representation is κ≃ζxx​. To quadratic order one may identify material arc length s with x in κ0​, while
L−L=21​∫0L​ζx2​dx+O(ζx4​).
(13)
At fixed positive extension force, the mechanical potential is bending energy minus FL. Replacing L by L−(L−L) leaves, up to a force-dependent constant,
H[ζ]=21​∫0L​[B(ζxx​−κ0​)2+Fζx2​]dx​.
(14)
Its first variation is
δH=∫0L​[B(ζxx​−κ0​)xx​−Fζxx​]δζdx+[B(ζxx​−κ0​)δζx​+{Fζx​−B(ζxxx​−κ0,x​)}δζ]0L​.
(15)
The higher-order Euler-Lagrange equation is therefore
Bζxxxx​−Fζxx​=Bκ0,xx​​.
(16)
For a Fourier transform relation, take a periodic bulk segment, or a long-filament bulk approximation with negligible endpoint contributions; these are necessary boundary conditions because the question specifies no filament end conditions. Using f(x)=∫f​(q)eiqxdq/(2π), every nonzero wavenumber obeys
(Bq4+Fq2)ζ​(q)=−Bq2κ0​(q),ζ​(q)=−F+Bq2Bκ0​(q)​​.
(17)
A zero displacement mode is an arbitrary translation. In a periodic representation a constant intrinsic-curvature mode contributes a constant bending cost but no bulk slope, so it does not contribute to the following deficit. The Parseval identity gives
L−L=2B2​∫2πdq​(F+Bq2)2q2∣κ0​(q)∣2​​.
(18)
For clarity about finite normalization, on a periodic interval of length L define κn​=L−1∫0L​κ0​(x)e−iqn​xdx, qn​=2πn/L. Then the equivalent exact quadratic-order formula is
L−L=2LB2​∑n=0​(F+Bqn2​)2qn2​∣κn​∣2​.
(19)
This is force-extension of an intrinsically curved filament, determined by its particular fixed shape rather than by a thermal orientation entropy.
For an ensemble with quenched intrinsic curvature, first minimize each realization and then average the last expression, replacing ∣κn​∣2 by its ensemble mean. For stationary bulk disorder, let Sκ​(q) be the power spectrum, normalized so that its correlation is ⟨κ0​(x)κ0​(x+r)⟩=∫Sκ​(q)eiqrdq/(2π). The measurable mean deficit per unit projected length is
L⟨L−L⟩​=2B2​∫2πdq​(F+Bq2)2q2Sκ​(q)​​.
(20)
If the curvature is smooth enough that the second spectral moment is finite, one can take the large-force limit under this integral, obtaining the smooth-curvature high-force extension law
⟨L−L⟩∼2F2B2​∫0L​⟨(κ0,x​)2⟩dx​.
(21)
For a stationary ensemble the integral is L⟨(κ0,x​)2⟩. Equivalently, the bulk slope is ζx​≃−(B/F)κ0,x​; a plot of the mean deficit against F−2 measures the mean squared intrinsic-curvature gradient, if B is known. The F−2 law is a smooth-curvature bulk result, not a universal law for every random filament. For example, ideal white intrinsic-curvature disorder with Sκ​(q)=S0​ has an infinite second spectral moment; retaining the full integral instead gives ⟨L−L⟩/L=S0​B​/(8F​) in that ideal continuum model.
Endpoint conditions also matter. If each end is moment-free and has no transverse applied force, the natural conditions from the variation are
ζxx​=κ0​,B(ζxxx​−κ0,x​)−Fζx​=0
(22)
at each end. These can produce a free-end curvature boundary layer of width ℓ=B/F​. For a constant intrinsic filament curvature κ, integration of the higher-order Euler-Lagrange equation with zero transverse force gives B(ζx​)xx​−Fζx​=0 and hence
ζx​=κℓcosh[L/(2ℓ)]sinh[(x−L/2)/ℓ]​,L−L=2κ2ℓ3​[tanha−asech2a],a=2ℓL​.
(23)
The last formula follows by integrating sinh2[(x−L/2)/ℓ], and tends to κ2ℓ3/2∝F−3/2, despite the vanishing bulk curvature gradient. Thus a finite free-ended measurement must include or suppress endpoint bending before interpreting a bulk F−2 fit.
For the freely jointed chain, rigid links have independent orientations, so the restoring force is entropic. Let T now denote absolute temperature, and β=1/(kB​T) with Boltzmann constant kB​. In the usual three-dimensional chain, a segment at polar angle θ has force energy −Fbcosθ. Its angular canonical partition function and the full chain partition function are
Z1​=2π∫−11​eξμdμ=4πξsinhξ​,ZN​=Z1N​,ξ=βFb.
(24)
Differentiating logZN​ with respect to βF yields the exact mean projected displacement
⟨L⟩=Nb(cothξ−ξ1​)=NbL(ξ)​.
(25)
Here L is the Langevin function; this is force-extension of a three-dimensional freely jointed chain, in the fixed-force canonical ensemble. At high extension, cothξ=1+2e−2ξ+⋯, so
Nb−⟨L⟩∼FNkB​T​,F∼Nb−⟨L⟩NkB​T​​.
(26)
Thus the smooth quenched-curvature bulk deficit falls as F−2, whereas the three-dimensional freely jointed chain deficit falls as F−1 and depends explicitly on temperature. The distinction is mechanical straightening of a fixed intrinsic shape versus orientational entropy; thermal bending fluctuations of a stiff filament have not been included in the first calculation.
If the comparison chain is also restricted to two dimensions, it is a planar freely jointed chain rather than the three-dimensional model. In this case,
Z1​=∫02π​eξcosθdθ=2πI0​(ξ),Nb⟨L⟩​=I0​(ξ)I1​(ξ)​​.
(27)
The last identity follows directly by differentiating the integral defining I0​; I0​ and I1​ are Modified Bessel functions of the first kind. At large ξ the angular weight is proportional to e−ξθ2/2, giving ⟨cosθ⟩≃1−⟨θ2⟩/2=1−1/(2ξ). Consequently Nb−⟨L⟩∼NkB​T/(2F): the same inverse-force exponent, with half the three-dimensional coefficient. Both versions assume inextensible links and forces below the regime where the links themselves stretch.

First find the positive spatially homogeneous equilibrium. Setting the reactions to zero gives v=u2 and u/v=b, and hence the unique positive equilibrium is
u∗​=b1​,v∗​=b21​​.
(28)
The zero pair is not an admissible positive state and is singular in the u2/v term. For homogeneous deviations, the reaction Jacobian matrix at this equilibrium is
J=(2u∗​/v∗​−b2u∗​​−u∗2​/v∗2​−1​)=(b2/b​−b2−1​).
(29)
Thus trJ=b−1, detJ=b, and its eigenvalues are
σ±​=2b−1±b2−6b+1​​​.
(30)
For 0<b<1, both real parts are negative: a real pair has positive product and negative sum, while a complex pair has real part (b−1)/2. The reaction equilibrium is therefore asymptotically stable, with no growing oscillatory mode. This does not exclude damped oscillations. Indeed, for 3−22​<b<1 the eigenvalues are complex; for example, b=1/2 gives −1/4±i7​/4, a stable focus with oscillatory transients. Thus a literal exclusion of every oscillatory solution is false; the intended exclusion of sustained or growing oscillations is the meaningful statement.
One can strengthen this to exclude nonlinear periodic oscillations in the positive quadrant without relying only on the local linear stability analysis. For the reactions f=u2/v−bu, g=u2−v, use the Dulac function H=u−2. Then
∂u​(Hf)+∂v​(Hg)=∂u​(v1​−ub​)+∂v​(1−u2v​)=u2b−1​<0.
(31)
Were there a periodic orbit wholly in the positive quadrant, the flux of H(f,g) through it would vanish, since this vector is tangent to the orbit; Green's theorem would equate that flux to a strictly negative interior integral. This contradiction proves there is no sustained positive periodic oscillation when 0<b<1, the positive-quadrant Dulac multiplier for quadratic activation argument. It leaves the damped transients entirely consistent with the conclusion.
Now introduce a perturbation (δu,δv)=aeσt+ikx in the quadratic activator-inhibitor model. The Fourier mode of squared wavenumber q=k2 evolves under
Jq​=J−q(10​0d​)=(b−q2/b​−b2−1−dq​).
(32)
Its matrix trace and determinant are
τ(q)=b−1−(1+d)q,D(q)=dq2+(1−db)q+b.
(33)
A Turing instability must start with reaction stability, requiring 0<b<1. Then τ(q)<0 for every q≥0, so a mode is unstable exactly when D(q)<0: its two real eigenvalues have opposite signs. This also proves the diffusion-driven onset is stationary rather than oscillatory.
The upward-opening quadratic D(q) has its minimum at qm​=(db−1)/(2d). To have a negative value at a physically allowed positive squared wavenumber, one needs
db>1,D(qm​)=b−4d(db−1)2​<0.
(34)
Writing r=db, the second inequality is r2−6r+1>0, with roots 3±22​. The first inequality discards the lower branch, giving
0<b<1,db>3+22​​.
(35)
These are the Turing threshold of the quadratic activator-inhibitor model conditions. Equivalently, in the (b,d) plane the instability region lies strictly above d=(3+22​)/b and strictly to the left of b=1, with b>0. The boundary diverges as b→0+ and approaches 3+22​ as b→1−.
At equality, the determinant has a double root and one growth rate is zero, while the other remains negative. Substituting db=3+22​ into its minimum location gives
kc2​=qc​=2ddb−1​=d1+2​​​.
(36)
Beyond onset, the unstable band is
q−​<k2<q+​,q±​=2ddb−1±(db−1)2−4db​​​.
(37)
The stated parameter region assumes continuously available wavenumbers, as on an infinite domain. With specified finite-domain boundary conditions, an allowed nonzero spatial eigenmode must additionally lie in this band; homogeneous stability alone does not ensure that a finite box admits the critical wavelength.
