Eigenvalue analysis proves mesh-uniform stability when it is accompanied by control of eigenvectors or an energy norm; the signs of eigenvalues alone are insufficient. For an evolution equation, the method of lines first produces uh′​=Lh​uh​. A time integrator then gives an amplification matrix Sh,δ​, for example Sh,δ​=R(δLh​) for a Runge-Kutta method. The useful stability requirement on a fixed physical time interval is
supnδ≤T​∥Sh,δn​∥h​≤CT​,
(46)
where CT​ is independent of the refining spatial and temporal meshes. The semidiscrete analogue is ∥etLh​∥h​≤CT​ for 0≤t≤T. These are bounds on propagation of initial errors and residuals, not merely statements that each finite matrix has a bounded solution over its own finite time interval.
If Lh​ is a normal matrix in the chosen discrete inner product, it has a unitary eigenbasis. Then
∥etLh​∥h​=maxj​etReλj​,∥R(δLh​)n∥h​=maxj​∣R(δλj​)∣n.
(47)
For dissipative problems it is sufficient that every δλj​ lie in the integrator's linear stability domain. A diagonalizable family also suffices if its eigenvector matrices have uniformly bounded condition numbers: the same maximum is multiplied by ∥Vh​∥∥Vh−1​∥. Without that uniform bound the argument does not establish mesh stability. For finite-time estimates, an amplification modulus 1+O(δ) can also be acceptable; strict contraction at every step is a stronger property.
For a constant-coefficient stencil on the full lattice or a periodic grid, von Neumann stability analysis provides the eigenvectors explicitly. A Fourier mode diagonalizes each translation-invariant spatial difference, giving a scalar symbol λh​(θ) or a small matrix symbol for a system. The discrete Fourier transform and Parseval identity turn a uniform modal bound into an actual discrete L2 norm bound. The two-dimensional stencil in question 3 has Reλh​=−(1−cosη)2/h≤0, so its continuous-time amplification is contractive; a later time integrator must still be checked. With boundaries one must analyze the boundary rows as well: a whole-line Fourier symbol cannot detect an unstable boundary closure.
For the heat equation ut​=κuxx​ on a unit interval with zero Dirichlet data, the centred second difference has a sine eigenbasis and eigenvalues
λj​=−h24κ​sin22Njπ​,h=1/N,1≤j<N.
(48)
Explicit Euler method multiplies mode j by 1+δλj​. Requiring all factors to have modulus at most one yields κδ/h2≤1/[2sin2((N−1)π/(2N))] on a given grid, and the sharp grid-independent sufficient limit is
κδ/h2≤21​in one dimension,κδ/h2≤41​on the standard equal-mesh two-dimensional grid.​
(49)
These parabolic time-step restrictions express the growth of the largest-magnitude diffusion eigenvalue like h−2. Backward Euler method instead has factor (1−δλj​)−1 and the Crank-Nicolson method factor (1+δλj​/2)/(1−δλj​/2), both bounded by one for every δ≥0. Their unconditional stability follows from A-stability, with much stronger damping of the shortest scales for backward Euler.
For ut​=−aux​, a>0, explicit time stepping with a backward spatial difference gives
S(θ)=1−ν+νe−iθ,ν=aδ/h,∣S(θ)∣2=1−2ν(1−ν)(1−cosθ).
(50)
Thus the upwind finite difference scheme is contractive exactly for 0≤ν≤1, a Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition matching the direction and speed of information propagation. A centred spatial difference has purely imaginary eigenvalues, and forward Euler gives ∣S∣2=1+ν2sin2θ>1. Under a fixed positive hyperbolic Courant number, high-frequency data grow by a fixed factor in each of O(1/h) steps, proving instability as the mesh is refined. This is not a claim that no exceptionally small coupled step can ever give a finite-time bound: if δ=O(h2), the excess per step is only O(δ), but the usual hyperbolic scaling is unstable. Higher-order explicit integrators can possess nontrivial imaginary-axis stability intervals and are then suitable for skew-adjoint advection or the energy-form first-order wave equation.
Variable coefficients usually prevent Fourier diagonalization, but may preserve a self-adjoint spatial operator. The positive-face diffusion discretization in question 5 is an example. Its discrete energy identity makes A+B symmetric negative definite, so a unitary eigenbasis still exists even without a closed eigenvalue formula. More generally, a dissipative operator Lh​ in a discrete energy inner product obeys
dtd​∥uh​∥h2​=2Re⟨Lh​uh​,uh​⟩h​≤0,
(51)
which directly proves a uniform semigroup estimate. The dissipative Cayley-transform contraction also follows without normality: for α≥0,
∥(I+αLh​)v∥h2​−∥(I−αLh​)v∥h2​=4αRe⟨Lh​v,v⟩h​≤0.
(52)
Taking v=(I−αLh​)−1u proves contraction of the Crank–Nicolson factor. The contractivity of split Crank-Nicolson diffusion then follows from multiplying two contraction bounds; simultaneous eigenvectors of the two directional matrices are unnecessary.
The negative spectra do not imply uniform semidiscrete stability example shows what can go wrong. Let
Lh​=(−10​h−1−1​),etLh​=e−t(10​t/h1​).
(53)
Both eigenvalues are −1 for every mesh, but applying the propagator to the second unit vector at t=1 gives norm at least 1/(eh). The evolution is therefore not mesh-uniformly stable. For a discrete matrix, a unit-modulus eigenvalue with a nontrivial Jordan block gives powers growing polynomially in the step number. Even strictly interior eigenvalues can allow large transient growth when eigenvectors are badly conditioned. A pseudospectrum or numerical range of an operator, and especially a direct energy estimate, can reveal behavior missed by eigenvalues.
A resolvent estimate provides another way to see the needed uniformity. If ∥Shn​∥≤C for every n, then for ∣z∣>1,
(zI−Sh​)−1=∑n=0∞​zn+1Shn​​,∥(zI−Sh​)−1∥≤∣z∣−1C​.
(54)
This shows directly that a uniform power estimate imposes more than the location of the spectrum. Conversely, resolvent-to-power estimates in varying dimension must themselves be uniform; a bound with constants depending on the number of grid points is not enough.
For a linear multistep method, each spatial eigenvalue gives an amplification polynomial, and all roots must satisfy the root condition for a multistep method, including simplicity of any unit-modulus root. Merely following the root approximating eδλ misses parasitic modes. The temporal and spatial analyses must therefore be combined with the appropriate zero-stability and uniform modal bounds.
Finally stability connects to convergence. If the exact grid samples have normalized residual τn and the error obeys en+1=Sh​en+δτn, iteration gives
∥en∥h​≤CT​(∥e0∥h​+δ∑j<n​∥τj∥h​).
(55)
A residual tending uniformly to zero then gives convergence on [0,T]. For a well-posed linear initial-value problem and a consistent linear finite-difference approximation, this is the forward implication of the Lax equivalence theorem. The practical conclusion is to analyze the full operator including boundaries, select a time method whose stability region contains the relevant scaled spectrum, and verify that eigenvectors or an energy estimate make the resulting bounds uniform as the grid is refined.
