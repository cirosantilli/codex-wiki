The Bernstein polynomial is
Bn​(f,x)=∑j=0n​f(j/n)(jn​)xj(1−x)n−j.
(1)
It has degree at most n, since cancellation can lower the degree. For 0≤x≤1, the Bernstein basis weights are nonnegative and their sum is (x+(1−x))n=1 by the binomial theorem. Consequently
∣Bn​(f,x)∣≤∑j=0n​∣f(j/n)∣(jn​)xj(1−x)n−j≤∥f∥∞​.
(2)
Thus the Bernstein polynomial defines a positive linear operator on continuous functions satisfying ∥Bn​f∥∞​≤∥f∥∞​. Since Bn​1=1, its operator norm in the supremum norm is exactly one.

The Weierstrass approximation theorem states that the polynomials are a dense subspace of C[0,1] in the supremum norm. By linearity and (c), Bn​p→p uniformly for every polynomial p. Given f∈C[0,1] and ε>0, choose a polynomial p with ∥f−p∥∞​<ε. The operator norm estimate in (a) gives
∥Bn​f−f∥∞​≤∥Bn​(f−p)∥∞​+∥Bn​p−p∥∞​+∥p−f∥∞​≤2ε+∥Bn​p−p∥∞​.
(7)
First let n→∞, then ε↓0. Hence ∥Bn​f−f∥∞​→0 for every continuous f. This is an instance of extension of approximation operators from a dense subspace.

For later use, extend the finite knot sequence to a strictly increasing sequence in both directions. The full order-one sum is one. When the Cox-de Boor recurrence is summed, the two coefficients of each fixed lower-order B-spline add to one. Induction therefore gives a full partition of unity. Removing terms proves the subpartition of unity for B-splines, ∑i=1n​Ni,k​(t)≤1. For repeated knots, the same statement follows by a limit of strictly increasing knot sequences, with the usual convention that a recurrence term with zero denominator is zero; the bound holds on each open knot interval and for the standard one-sided endpoint values. The terminology “L∞​-normalized” refers to this standard basis normalization; it does not claim that each individual B-spline has supremum norm exactly one.

For the usual spline interpolation problem the sites are distinct and ordered; the Schoenberg–Whitney theorem then makes Ax​ invertible under the stated support positivity. More generally, the following argument applies whenever the interpolation map in the question is well defined and Ax​ is invertible.
Factor the spline interpolation operator as Px​=BAx−1​R, where
Rf=(f(x1​),…,f(xn​)),Bc=∑j=1n​cj​Nj​.
(30)
Sampling has operator norm at most one from the supremum norm to ℓ∞​. Nonnegativity and the subpartition of unity for B-splines give
∣Bc(t)∣≤∥c∥ℓ∞​​∑j​Nj​(t)≤∥c∥ℓ∞​​.
(31)
Consequently ∥Px​∥≤∥Ax−1​∥ℓ∞​​.
For the reverse bound, select a row r of Ax−1​ with maximal absolute row sum, and choose yj​=sgn((Ax−1​)rj​), assigning any value of modulus at most one when the entry is zero. Then ∥y∥ℓ∞​​=1 and
∥Ax−1​y∥ℓ∞​​=∑j​∣(Ax−1​)rj​∣=∥Ax−1​∥ℓ∞​​.
(32)
At distinct sites there is a continuous piecewise linear function f with f(xj​)=yj​ and ∥f∥∞​=1: interpolate linearly between the ordered sites and extend constantly to the endpoints. The given uniform-norm stability of a B-spline basis therefore implies
∥Px​f∥∞​=∥BAx−1​y∥∞​≥dk​1​∥Ax−1​y∥ℓ∞​​.
(33)
Thus the B-spline interpolation operator norm satisfies
dk​1​∥Ax−1​∥ℓ∞​​≤∥Px​∥≤∥Ax−1​∥ℓ∞​​.​
(34)
Positivity Ni​(xi​)>0 alone, without the usual distinct-site convention, is insufficient to define an interpolation operator. For example, take order-two B-splines with knots 0,1,2,3 and x1​=x2​=3/2. Both diagonal values are 1/2, but the B-spline collocation matrix has identical rows (1/2,1/2) and is singular. The displayed proof uses the well-defined interpolation map posited in the question; ordered distinct sites supply its standard existence guarantee.

An orthonormal multiresolution analysis consists of closed subspaces Vj​⊂L2​(R), j∈Z, with Vj​⊂Vj+1​, ⋃j​Vj​​=L2​(R), ⋂j​Vj​={0}, and the dilation equivalence h∈Vj​⟺h(2⋅)∈Vj+1​. A scaling function ϕ has integer translates forming an orthonormal basis of V0​. Hence
ϕj,k​(x)=2j/2ϕ(2jx−k),k∈Z,
(38)
is an orthonormal basis of Vj​. Set Wj​=Vj+1​⊖Vj​, the orthogonal complement of Vj​ inside Vj+1​. A function ψ whose integer translates form an orthonormal basis of W0​ produces a complete orthonormal wavelet system {2j/2ψ(2j⋅−k):j,k∈Z}: different Wj​ are orthogonal, their sum contains every difference Vb​⊖Va​, and density together with the zero intersection exhausts L2​(R).
Here is the filter construction that guarantees such a wavelet. Nesting puts ϕ in V1​, so expansion in its orthonormal basis gives the scaling refinement equation ϕ=∑n​an​ϕ(2⋅−n), with a∈ℓ2​. Define the MRA low-pass filter and its complementary quadrature mirror filter by
m(t)=21​∑n​an​e−int,q(t)=−e−itm(t+π)​.
(39)
Splitting the orthonormal translates and Fourier periodization identity at 2t, as in part (b), gives ∣m(t)∣2+∣m(t+π)∣2=1 almost everywhere. Consequently the rows of
(m(t)−e−itm(t+π)​​m(t+π)e−itm(t)​​)
(40)
have length one and are orthogonal; the matrix is unitary. Put bn​=(−1)na1−n​​ and ψ=∑n​bn​ϕ(2⋅−n), so q(t)=21​∑n​bn​e−int.
To see completeness as well as orthogonality, identify V1​ with its fine-scale coefficient sequences and take their Fourier series. Group the frequencies t and t+π over a half-period. The two rows of the displayed matrix are the normalized coarse and complementary channels; their unitarity is exactly an onto isometry from these two channels to the fine-scale coefficient space. Translates of ϕ and ψ correspond to the Fourier basis in the respective channels. They therefore jointly form an orthonormal basis of V1​. The orthogonal complement channel gives the wavelet completion of a multiresolution filter, namely the integer translates of ψ span W0​. First use the usual real-valued, measurable interpretation of the transition data. The complementary squares then imply ∣f∣≤1, and the compact support gives f∈L2​. Consider the periodization on [−π,π]. For ∣t∣<2π/3, only f(t)=1 contributes. For 2π/3≤t≤π, only f(t) and f(t−2π) can contribute, and their squares sum to one. For −π≤t≤−2π/3, apply the complementary identity at t+2π to obtain f(t)2+f(t+2π)2=1. Therefore the orthonormal translates and Fourier periodization identity holds almost everywhere.
Define a periodic MRA low-pass filter by prescribing it on [−π,π]:
m(t)={f(2t),0,​∣t∣<2π/3,2π/3≤∣t∣≤π,​m(t+2π)=m(t).
(45)
In the central band, f(t)=1, so f(2t)=m(t)f(t). In the rest of this fundamental interval, both m(t) and f(2t) vanish. If π<∣t∣<4π/3, reducing t modulo 2π puts it in an outer band where m=0, and again f(2t)=0. For ∣t∣≥4π/3, both f(t) and f(2t) vanish. Endpoint choices affect only a null set.
