Work in the frame in which the distant fluid is stationary, and take the pressure at infinity as zero. In the Unscaled Papkovich–Neuber representation, choose the harmonic potentials
Φ=−4r3a​V,χ=4a3​r3V⋅x​.
(1)
Both are harmonic away from the sphere's centre: 1/r is harmonic there and V⋅x/r3=−V⋅∇(1/r). Substitution into u=∇(x⋅Φ+χ)−2Φ, p=2μ∇⋅Φ, gives the translating sphere in Stokes flow:
u=[4r3a​(I+nn)+4r3a3​(I−3nn)]V,p=2r23μa​V⋅n.​
(2)
Here n=x/r. At r=a the coefficients of I sum to one and those of nn sum to zero, so the no-slip boundary condition is satisfied. The field decays at infinity. The Papkovich–Neuber representation enforces the Stokes equation and incompressibility, and Uniqueness of Stokes flow identifies the physical solution.
Differentiate radially at fixed direction n:
∂r​u∣a​=[−4a3​(I+nn)−4a3​(I−3nn)]V=2a3​(nn−I)V.​
(3)
On the surface, multiplying the specified Newtonian fluid stress tensor by the outward normal from the solid gives
σn=2a3μ​[(V⋅n)n−V−n(V⋅n)]=−2a3μ​V.
(4)
Thus the surface traction in translating-sphere Stokes flow is uniform and the fluid's force on the sphere is
Ffluid​=∫r=a​σndS=−6πμaV.​
(5)
This is the Stokes drag law; an external force of the opposite sign maintains the motion.

Let F denote the applied force, and take the normal nS​ outward from the solid. At leading order the Stokes drag law and the torque on a rotating sphere in Stokes flow give
U0​=6πμaF​,Ω0​=0.​
(6)
The exact no-slip boundary condition and the exact resultant-load conditions are
u(x)=U+Ω×x(x∈S),u→0(r→∞),∫S​σnS​dS=−F,∫S​x×(σnS​)dS=0.​
(7)
The stress condition specifies the total force and torque, not a pointwise prescribed traction.
For the boundary perturbation of a nearly spherical particle, evaluate the no-slip condition at x=(a+εf)n. Taylor expansion gives
u0​(an)+ε[u1​(an)+f∂r​u0​(an)]=U0​+ε[U1​+Ω1​×(an)]+O(ε2).
(8)
There is no fΩ0​×n term because Ω0​=0. Therefore
u1​=U1​+Ω1​×x−f∂r​u0​(r=a).​
(9)
To expand the integral conditions correctly, use surface independence of Stokes force and torque integrals. The Stokes equation implies ∇⋅σ=0, and symmetry of the stress tensor implies that the angular-momentum flux is divergence-free as well. Evaluate the exact force and torque on a fixed sphere of radius R enclosing the perturbed particle. There are then no geometric terms: the O(ε) integrals of σ1​ on that fixed sphere vanish because the external force and torque have no O(ε) corrections. Each perturbation field solves the homogeneous exterior Stokes equations, so the divergence theorem transfers those integrals back to r=a. Hence
∫r=a​σ1​ndS=0,∫r=a​x×(σ1​n)dS=0.​
(10)
If the integrals are expanded directly on the displaced surface, terms involving the displaced evaluation of σ0​, the changed normal and the changed area do appear individually. Their sum vanishes by the same surface-independence identity. Thus their absence in the final equations is a cancellation, not permission to omit geometric terms independently.

First estimate transverse relaxation without assuming a thin cross-section. With both depth and width of order h0​, a transverse surface-height variation of order h0​ gives a pressure gradient of order ρg. The Stokes equation then gives transverse horizontal and vertical velocities of order gh02​/ν. Moving the surface through distance h0​ takes
τy​∼gh0​ν​.​
(17)
For longitudinal variations over L≫h0​, the hydrostatic pressure gradient is of order ρgh0​/L, so the longitudinal velocity is u∼gh03​/(νL). Incompressibility gives a vertical velocity w∼h0​u/L, hence
τx​∼wh0​​∼gh03​νL2​,τx​τy​​∼L2h02​​≪1.​
(18)
Thus cross-channel variations relax rapidly compared with the longitudinal evolution. These are viscous estimates, requiring inertial relaxation to be negligible; the slow-flow regime in particular excludes a large transverse inertial response. After that initial relaxation the free surface is nearly horizontal across the channel, so its height is h0​(x,t) and its local vertical depth is h0​−∣y∣cotα.
Retain the full cross-sectional geometry for a fixed 0<α<π/2; no small-angle approximation is used. With ∣h0x​∣≪1, the leading hydrostatic pressure is p=pa​+ρg(h0​−z). Axial viscous derivatives are small relative to cross-sectional ones. The axial Stokes equation is therefore
ν(uyy​+uzz​)=gh0x​.
(19)
Introduce Y=y/h0​, Z=z/h0​, and the triangular domain
Dα​={(Y,Z):∣Y∣cotα<Z<1}.
(20)
Let the dimensionless cross-sectional Poisson equation be
ϕYY​+ϕZZ​=−1in Dα​,ϕ=0(Z=∣Y∣cotα),ϕZ​=0(Z=1).
(21)
The two lower sloping sides impose the no-slip boundary condition; the upper horizontal side imposes the stress-free boundary condition. Symmetry would additionally give ϕY​=0 at Y=0 if only half the domain were used. The full domain needs no extra midline boundary condition. Define Q(α)=∫Dα​​ϕdYdZ. Then
u=−νg​h02​h0x​ϕ(Y,Z),q=∫udydz=−νgQ(α)​h04​h0x​.

The cross-sectional area is A=h02​tanα. Conservation of mass, At​+qx​=0, gives the V-shaped channel gravity current equation
tanα(h02​)t​=νgQ(α)​(h04​h0x​)x​.​
(23)
In particular, neither replacing this triangular Poisson problem by a vertically parabolic film nor taking an extreme angle is necessary.
Put C=gQ(α)/(νtanα). For a symmetric fixed-volume similarity solution, write h0​=t−bf(xt−d). Volume conservation gives d=2b, and balancing (h02​)t​=C(h04​h0x​)x​ gives 2b+1=5b+2d. Hence b=1/7, d=2/7. Integrating the similarity equation once, with zero flux at the centre, yields
Ch04​h0x​=−7t2x​h02​.
(24)
Integrating again and imposing h0​=0 at x=±X(t) gives
h0​(x,t)=[7Ct3​(X2−x2)]1/3(∣x∣<X),h0​=0(∣x∣≥X).​

The constant-volume similarity in a V-shaped channel has a compact support. If H(t)=h0​(0,t), its volume is
V=tanαH2XI,I=∫−11​(1−s2)2/3ds,H3=7Ct3X2​.
(26)
Eliminating H therefore gives
xN​(t)=X(t)=(ItanαV​)3/7(3νtanα7gQ(α)t​)2/7.​
(27)
This describes the large-time spreading from a localized release; the point-release profile is singular at t=0, and the idealized front has a narrow region where its divergent slope violates the long-wave approximation. Neither feature changes the leading bulk similarity law.
At a fixed nonzero x∗, the depth is initially zero until X(t)=∣x∗∣. After arrival it rises and then falls. Because X2∝t4/7, differentiation of h0​(x∗,t)3=3(X2−x∗2)/(7Ct) gives
dtd​h03​=7Ct23​(x∗2−73​X2).
(28)
Thus its unique maximum occurs at X2=7x∗2/3; thereafter the depth tends to zero as t−1/7. If ta​ is the arrival time, tmax​/ta​=(7/3)7/4. At the singular release point x∗=0, the point-source similarity profile instead decreases from the start; a finite initial release regularizes its early behaviour.
The right-hand sketch shows the triangular bulk at half its previous maximum depth, with thin draining films on the previously wetted portions of both walls. These residual wall films in a receding gravity current are not captured by a perfectly dry-wall triangular section. They drain rapidly compared with bulk longitudinal spreading. For an order-one angle, a wall film of thickness b and wetted length ℓ has speed O(gb2/ν) by falling film flow; its drainage time gives b∼(νℓ/(gt))1/2. At a fixed nonzero station the previous maximum wetted height is finite, so film area decays as t−1/2, faster than the bulk area h02​∼t−2/7. In stations whose arrival and peak times are of order the observation time, the typical residual film fraction is O(τy​/t​)→0. Therefore the films modify the detailed receding cross-section but do not hold a leading-order fraction of the volume or invalidate the large-time nose law. Microscopic contact-line physics is beyond this ideal viscous model.

Let the longitudinal variation scale be ℓ≫h. The stress-free boundary condition gives μ(uz​+wx​)=0 at each face. Incompressibility implies w=O(hu/ℓ), so wx​ is smaller than a putative uz​=O(u/h) by O(h2/ℓ2). The leading Stokes equation and the two shear-free conditions therefore make u independent of z; its thickness-dependent correction is smaller-order. Symmetry gives w=0 at z=0, and incompressibility integrates to
w=−zux​.​
(29)
Vertical momentum makes the leading normal stress independent of z. With the normal directed from liquid into gas, the upper-face curvature is −hxx​/2 to leading order. The Young–Laplace equation gives
σzz​=−pa​+2γ​hxx​.​
(30)
The Newtonian fluid stress tensor also gives σzz​=−p+2μwz​=−p−2μux​. Hence p=pa​−γhxx​/2−2μux​ and
σxx​=−pa​+2γ​hxx​+4μux​.​
(31)
The factor four is the planar extension factor: both the longitudinal viscous stress and the pressure adjustment from transverse contraction contribute.
For force balance on the illustrated slice, the vertical cuts give (hσxx​)x​δx. Pressure on the sloping broad faces gives pa​hx​δx horizontally. Each of the two interfaces exerts a tangential surface tension pull at each cut, with horizontal component γcosθ, where tanθ=hx​/2. Thus the horizontal force balance is the derivative of the effective tension
T=h(σxx​+pa​)+2γcosθ=4μhux​+2γ​hhxx​+2γ−4γ​hx2​
(32)
to the retained small-slope order. The ambient-pressure contributions cancel in this excess tension. Differentiating cancels the two hx​hxx​ terms, leaving the planar viscous-sheet stretching equations
(4μhux​)x​+2γ​hhxxx​=0.​
(33)
The upper-face kinematic condition is w(x,h/2,t)=(ht​+uhx​)/2. Combining it with w=−zux​ gives the second equation,
ht​+(hu)x​=0,​
(34)
which is conservation of mass per unit transverse width.
For the two bubbles, let pg​ be the common gas pressure and pe​ the surrounding liquid pressure away from the sheet. A cylindrical bubble has one nonzero principal curvature, so the Young–Laplace equation gives pg​−pe​≃γ/a. In the nearly flat sheet the normal stress is −pg​, giving liquid pressure pfilm​=pg​−2μux​. The extensional correction is small compared with γ/a because the transition scale will satisfy ah0​​/L≪1. Thus pfilm​≃pg​>pe​, and this capillary pressure difference drives liquid out through both ends.
For a spatially uniform flat sheet, hx​=hxxx​=0, so the longitudinal equation gives uxx​=0. Reflection symmetry at x=0 selects
u(x,t)=LU(t)​x.​
(35)
Here U is the outward speed at the positive end. The mass equation then gives ht​=−Uh/L, independent of x, so initially uniform thickness remains uniform. Writing it as h0​(t),
h˙0​=−LU(t)​h0​.​
(36)
The end transitions determine U, as derived in the following parts.

In the short end transition, the full thickness changes by O(h0​) while the curvature of each interface changes to O(1/a). Since each interface has height h/2, the curvature scale is O(h0​/δ2), with order-one factors irrelevant to this estimate. Therefore

This is the geometric scale of the capillary drainage of a film between two bubbles. The assumed regime additionally requires h0​≪a and ah0​​≪L, so the transition is long compared with its thickness and short compared with the flat sheet length.

The conservation of mass equation is ht​+(uh)x​=0. The flat region changes on time scale L/U, whereas a transition has advection time δ/U. Take p as excess pressure relative to the fluid far from the gap, and let h˙0​<0 for approach. The lower surface of the sphere is h0​+a−a2−r2​ above the plane. In the dominant region r=O(ah0​​)≪a, this is the parabolic lubrication gap
h(r,t)=h0​+2ar2​+O(r4/a3).
(47)
Its slope is O(h0​/a​)≪1, justifying lubrication theory. Both rigid surfaces have zero radial velocity, so the no-slip boundary condition and radial Stokes equation give the radial volume flux per unit circumference
q=−12μh3​pr​.
(48)
Axisymmetric conservation of mass gives ht​+r−1(rq)r​=0. Symmetry at r=0 therefore yields q=−rh˙0​/2, hence pr​=6μh˙0​r/h3. Matching to p→0 outside the pressure-bearing region gives
p=−3μah˙0​(h0​+2ar2​)−2.​
(49)
Extending the parabolic profile to infinity in this integral is a leading-order matching device; the main pressure and force come from r=O(ah0​​).
The radial scale ah​ balances the two contributions to the gap. The time scale h/v is the time for a typical gap change, and the lubrication pressure scale μav/h2 follows either from the formula above or from p∼μvℓ2/h3 with ℓ2=ah. The elastic surface's upward displacement is −ηp, so the actual gap is increased by +ηp. Thus the dimensionless gap in weakly compliant sphere-plane squeeze flow is
H=H0​+2R2​+εP,ε=h3ημav​.​
(50)
A dot now means differentiation with respect to T. The dimensionless Reynolds lubrication equation is
12HT​=R1​∂R​(RH3PR​).
(51)
Even without fluid inertia, the elastic surface has a pressure-dependent normal velocity, so HT​ includes εPT​; omitting it would wrongly discard the acceleration term below.
Write d=H0​+R2/2 and abbreviate H=H0​, v=H˙0​, b=H¨0​ during the calculation. The rigid term is

Set ξ=R2/(2H), so d=H(1+ξ). Dividing and changing variables yields
P1ξ​=H3ξ(1+ξ)3k​+H4ξ(1+ξ)418b​−H5ξ(1+ξ)518v2​+H5(1+ξ)654v2​.

The first three terms are derivatives of I3​,I4​,I5​, respectively. Integrating from infinity to enforce P1​→0 gives

Hence weak compliance initially increases the sedimentation speed in the regime where the perturbation is valid. The squeeze pressure indents the coating away from the sphere, widens the flow passage and lowers resistance. This is a load-controlled conclusion on the slow sedimenting branch, not a claim about an arbitrary externally imposed acceleration at startup.
The load-controlled breakdown of weak squeeze-flow compliance occurs when the relative correction O(εA/H02​) becomes order one:
H0∗​∼εA​,H0∗​∼ε​ for A=O(1).​

Thus breakdown means that elastic deflection is comparable with the nominal undeformed gap. The regular expansion about a rigid plane then ceases to be uniform and the coupled elastic-lubrication problem must be retained. It does not by itself imply contact, fluid inertia or failure of the thin-gap geometry.
