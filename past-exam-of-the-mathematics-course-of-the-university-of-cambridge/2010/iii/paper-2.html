Write A=kG and J=J(A), its Jacobson radical. First suppose the characteristic is p>0. We prove the counting statement through the cocenter of a finite-dimensional algebra
C(A)=A/[A,A],
(1)
where [A,A] is the linear span of commutators, not an ideal. In a group algebra, this span is exactly the span of differences of conjugate group elements: gh−hg is such a difference, and xgx−1−g=[x,gx−1]. Consequently C(A) has one basis vector for each conjugacy class.
There is a well-defined Frobenius map on an algebra cocenter
F:C(A)⟶C(A),[a]⟼[ap].
(2)
Here F is additive and F(c[a])=cpF([a]). To prove additivity, expand (a+b)p into words of length p. Cyclic rotations of a word are equal modulo commutators. Every nonconstant word has a rotation orbit of length p, so its orbit contributes zero; only ap,bp remain. Also
[(xy−yx)p]=[(xy)p]−[(yx)p]=0,
(3)
because the two products are cyclic rotations. Additivity then shows that the whole commutator subspace maps to zero, proving well-definedness on the quotient.
Let s be the number of simple modules up to isomorphism. The semisimple algebra A/J is ∏ν=1s​Mdν​​(k): over an algebraically closed field, a finite-dimensional division algebra is the field itself, since the minimal polynomial of any element splits into linear factors and a division algebra has no zero divisors. In a matrix algebra, the commutator subspace is precisely the traceless matrices. Off-diagonal matrix units are commutators, and Eii​−Ejj​=[Eij​,Eji​]; these span that subspace in every characteristic. Thus
C(A/J)≅ks.
(4)
The map induced by F on each coordinate is z↦zp, since tr(Bp)=(trB)p. This is bijective because k is a perfect field.
The natural surjection C(A)↠C(A/J) has kernel (J+[A,A])/[A,A]. Every kernel class has a representative in J. Since J is nilpotent, FN kills that kernel for sufficiently large N. On the quotient it remains bijective, so its image has dimension exactly s. On the conjugacy-class basis, however,
FN([g])=[gpN].
(5)
For large N, every gpN is a p-regular element; moreover the pN-power map permutes the p-regular conjugacy classes, because an inverse exponent can be chosen modulo the prime-to-p part of the exponent of G. The stable image therefore has precisely those conjugacy classes as its basis. Comparing dimensions proves
#{simple kG-modules}=#{p-regular conjugacy classes of G}.​
(6)
This argument uses dimensions of semilinear images: a bijective scalar Frobenius map preserves dimension, so the usual rank count applies.
In characteristic zero every group element is relevant. Averaging a linear projection onto a submodule over G makes that projection G-equivariant, proving Maschke's theorem and semisimplicity of kG. The same matrix-algebra cocenter calculation now gives s=dimC(kG), the total number of conjugacy classes.
For the second request, M is a relative projective module for H when every G-equivariant epimorphism onto M which splits after restriction to H already splits over G. Equivalently M is a direct summand of kG⊗kH​M, the induced representation of the restriction of a representation to H. We can verify the defining splitting property directly: if q:N↠M has an H-linear section u, choose representatives T of the left cosets G/H and set
v(m)=[G:H]1​∑t∈T​tu(t−1m).
(7)
The sum is independent of representatives by H-linearity. Reindexing gt=t′h proves G-linearity, while qv(m)=[G:H]−1∑t​m=m. Hence every M is relatively H-projective when the index is invertible in k.
For completeness the canonical induced-module epimorphism ε(g⊗m)=gm has the explicit G-linear section
σ(m)=[G:H]1​t∈T∑​t⊗t−1m,εσ=idM​.​
(8)
It exhibits the promised direct summand and is the projectivity detected on a subgroup of invertible index construction.

An idempotent e gives a projective module Ae, since A=Ae⊕A(1−e). It is primitive when it cannot be written as a sum of two nonzero orthogonal idempotents. This is equivalent to indecomposability of Ae: a projection onto a direct summand is an idempotent of EndA​(Ae)≅(eAe)op, and conversely such an idempotent splits Ae. Starting with 1, split any nonprimitive summand repeatedly. The process terminates because each splitting increases the number of nonzero vector-space summands in a finite-dimensional algebra. Thus
1=e1​+⋯+em​,A​A=ν=1⨁m​Aeν​,​
(9)
with orthogonal primitive idempotents and indecomposable projective modules. Each summand is generated by its idempotent.
Let D=Homk​(−,k). A Frobenius algebra is a finite-dimensional algebra with A≅D(A) as left modules over A, where (aϕ)(x)=ϕ(xa). Equivalently there is a linear functional λ:A→k for which (x,y)↦λ(xy) is nondegenerate. A symmetric algebra satisfies the stronger bimodule isomorphism A≅D(A), with (aϕb)(x)=ϕ(bxa); equivalently the functional can be chosen with λ(xy)=λ(yx). This is the associative-algebra meaning of symmetric, rather than the symmetric algebra of a vector space.

SocM=S⊆M simple∑​S,RadM=N⊊M maximal⋂​N.​

Now let P=Ae for a primitive idempotent, allowing the field k to be nonsplit. Symmetry identifies Ae with D(eA) through the pairing
eA×Ae⟶k,(u,v)⟼λ(uv).

The right-module quotient eA/eAJ is simple. A primitive idempotent remains primitive modulo the nilpotent radical: idempotents lift through that radical, so a decomposition in the quotient would lift to one in eAe. In the semisimple algebra A/J, the left simple Ae/JAe and the dual of the right simple eA/eAJ belong to the same matrix block. SocP≅P/RadP.​

For the modular examples put r=(123), s=(12), so r3=s2=1 and srs=r−1. The coefficient-of-identity functional on the group algebra is symmetric and nondegenerate, so the identity just proved applies.

kS3​≅k[u]/(u2)⊕M2​(k)(chark=2).​

In characteristic three put u=r−1. The ideal I=uA satisfies I3=0, and A/I≅kC2​≅k⊕k, so J(A)=I. The only simples are the trivial and sign modules S+​,S−​. Put e±​=(1±s)/2. The projectives P±​=Ae±​ have dimension three. A convenient radical generator is v=r−r−1:
v3=0,svs=−v,v=−u−u2,v2=u2.
(16)
The basis e±​,ve±​,v2e±​ gives the radical layers
P+​: S+​,S−​,S+​;P−​: S−​,S+​,S−​.​
(17)

(21​12​).​
(18)
The elements e−​ve+​ and e+​ve−​ give one arrow in each direction between the two simples. All paths of length three vanish, and the six paths of lengths zero, one and two are linearly independent. Hence A is the path algebra of that two-vertex oriented cycle modulo the ideal of paths of length three. Its connected quiver also proves there is a single block of S3 in characteristic three.
Any module is a Z/2-graded vector space given by the s-eigenspaces, with v interchanging the grades and v3=0. Constructing Jordan chains with homogeneous bases in the kernel and image filtrations and homogeneous lifts decomposes it into graded chains. Each chain has length 1,2 or 3 and is determined by its top sign. It is a uniserial module and hence indecomposable. Consequently there are exactly six indecomposables, the length-ℓ quotients P±​/JℓP±​, ℓ=1,2,3.

For a finite-dimensional algebra over an algebraically closed field, basic algebra means A/J(A)≅kn, so its simple modules are one-dimensional and occur once in the semisimple quotient of the regular module. A hereditary ring has every submodule of a projective module projective; equivalently its global dimension is at most one.
Choose representatives S1​,…,Sn​ of the simple modules. Our Ext quiver convention gives one vertex i for Si​ and
#{i→j}=dimk​ExtA1​(Si​,Sj​).
(19)
With primitive orthogonal idempotents ei​, put Pi​=Aei​ and J=J(A). Applying HomA​(−,Sj​) to 0→JPi​→Pi​→Si​→0 gives
ExtA1​(Si​,Sj​)≅HomA​(JPi​/J2Pi​,Sj​).
(20)
The restriction map from Hom(Pi​,Sj​) is zero since every map to a simple kills JPi​, and Pi​ is projective. Thus the arrow multiplicity is dimej​(J/J2)ei​. This specifies the orientation rather than silently replacing the quiver by its opposite.
Because A is hereditary, JPi​ is projective. Its semisimple top has aij​ copies of Sj​, where aij​ is the arrow multiplicity. Its projective cover therefore supplies an epimorphism
⨁j​Pjaij​​↠JPi​.
(21)
This splits by projectivity of JPi​. Its kernel is a summand contained in the radical of the source; projection onto that summand and Nakayama lemma force the kernel to be zero. Hence
JPi​≅j⨁​Pjaij​​,dimPi​=1+j∑​aij​dimPj​.​
(22)
Every arrow i→j forces dimPi​>dimPj​, so Q has no directed cycles.
Choose lifts in ej​Jei​ of bases of the arrow spaces. Send the vertices and arrows of the path algebra to these idempotents and lifts, multiplying paths in the order βα for consecutive arrows α:i→j, β:j→l. This defines an algebra homomorphism kQ→A. It is surjective: vertices span A/J, arrows span J/J2, and products show that paths of length r span Jr/Jr+1. Nilpotence of J then gives all of A.
Let Ni​ be the number of directed paths starting at i, including the length-zero path. Acyclicity gives the same recursion Ni​=1+∑j​aij​Nj​, with value one at a sink. Induction from sinks gives Ni​=dimPi​. Therefore
dimkQ=∑i​Ni​=∑i​dimPi​=dimA.
(23)
The surjective homomorphism is an isomorphism. This proves the basic hereditary algebra path-algebra theorem:
A≅kQ.​
(24)
For each connected component C of the underlying unoriented graph, eC​=∑i∈C​ei​ is a central idempotent. Paths never connect distinct components, so kQ=⨁C​eC​kQ as a direct sum of two-sided ideals. To prove each is a block, let z be any central idempotent. Modulo the arrow ideal it has coordinates ci​∈{0,1}. Commuting z with an arrow i→j and reducing modulo the square of that ideal gives ci​=cj​. Thus these coordinates are constant on each component. If they are zero on a component, the restriction of z there is a nilpotent idempotent, hence zero; if they are one, apply that argument to 1−z. Consequently
blocks of A ⟷ connected components of the underlying graph of Q.​

The finite representation type of a quiver condition means there are only finitely many isomorphism classes of indecomposable finite-dimensional kQ-modules. Equivalently, every finite-dimensional module is a direct sum of members of a finite list of indecomposables.
For the E6​ case, we give the reflection argument underlying the Gabriel theorem, rather than using its conclusion as the proof. Since the underlying graph is a tree, every orientation is acyclic. At a sink i, write
h:⨁j→i​Vj​⟶Vi​
(26)
for the sum of the incoming arrow maps. If an indecomposable V is not the simple Si​, then h is surjective: a nonzero complementary subspace to its image at the sink would be a direct summand supported only at i. The Bernstein–Gelfand–Ponomarev reflection functor reverses the arrows and replaces Vi​ by kerh, with reversed maps the component projections. The source reflection replaces that kernel by the cokernel of its inclusion into ⨁Vj​, recovering Vi​. The inclusion is injective, so the reflected representation has no direct summand Si​ at the new source. If it decomposed, applying the inverse would decompose V. Thus reflection preserves indecomposability except that it annihilates Si​.
On a dimension vector of a quiver representation, it acts by the integral simple reflection
(si​d)i​=−di​+∑j∼i​dj​,(si​d)j​=dj​(j=i).
(27)
Let B=2I−Adj(E6​). These reflections preserve the positive-definite quadratic form dTBd. Positivity can be checked without a classification theorem: label the graph by the chain 1−2−3−4−5 and attach vertex 6 to 3. The leading principal minors are 2,3,4,5,6,3, all positive.
Choose a topological ordering so arrows point from smaller to larger indices. Reflect successively at sinks 6,5,…,1. This restores the orientation after one full cycle. The transformation of dimension vectors is the Coxeter element C=s1​s2​⋯s6​. If U has diagonal entries one and entry −1 above the diagonal at an arrow, direct multiplication of the reflection matrices gives
C=−U−1UT,I−C=U−1(U+UT)=U−1B.
(28)
Thus C has no fixed vector. It is an integral isometry of a positive definite form, so it has finite order h: the image of each lattice basis vector lies in a finite set of vectors of the same norm, giving only finitely many possible isometries. Since Ch=I and I−C is invertible,
I+C+⋯+Ch−1=0.
(29)
If an indecomposable survived all h reflection cycles, its dimension vectors d,Cd,…,Ch−1d would all be nonzero and componentwise nonnegative, contradicting their zero sum. Therefore it reaches a simple at one of the finitely many reflection steps and is then annihilated. Applying the inverse reflections uniquely reconstructs it from that simple. There are at most 6h such stopping positions, proving
kQ3​ has finite representation type for every orientation of E6​.​
(30)
This supplies the requested finite-type proof sketch.

Take the Kronecker quiver, with two arrows α,β:1→2. There are no paths of length two, so its path algebra has basis e1​,e2​,α,β and dimension four.

For a left ZG-module, group cohomology is
Hn(G,M)=ExtZGn​(Z,M),​
(33)
with Z the trivial module. Equivalently take a projective resolution P∙​→Z and the cohomology of HomZG​(P∙​,M). This also describes the right-derived functors of the invariant-module functor M↦MG.
For an explicit model, the homogeneous bar free resolution has Pn​=Z[Gn+1], with diagonal G-action and boundary the alternating sum of coordinate deletions. It is free over ZG: each orbit has a unique representative with first coordinate 1. The augmented complex is exact because inserting 1 as a first coordinate supplies a contracting homotopy of underlying abelian groups; its lack of G-equivariance does not affect exactness. The associated inhomogeneous group cochains are functions f:Gn→M, with
(df)(g1​,…,gn+1​)=g1​f(g2​,…,gn+1​)+∑j=1n​(−1)jf(g1​,…,gj​gj+1​,…,gn+1​)+(−1)n+1f(g1​,…,gn​).
(34)
For group cochains of degree zero, (dm)(g)=gm−m. Adjacent deletions cancel in pairs, giving d2=0 and hence Hn=kerd/imd.
In degree zero this gives
H0(G,M)=MG={m:gm=m for all g}.​
(35)
It is also HomZG​(Z,M), since a map is determined by the invariant image of 1.
In degree one the cocycle condition is f(gh)=f(g)+gf(h), so
H1(G,M)={crossed homomorphisms G→M}/{g↦gm−m}.​
(36)
These are crossed homomorphisms modulo principal crossed homomorphisms. For trivial action they reduce to ordinary group homomorphisms, so H1(G,M)=Hom(Gab​,M).
There are two further useful descriptions. If I=ker(ZG→Z) is the augmentation ideal, a crossed homomorphism gives an equivariant map I→M by g−1↦f(g), because gh−1=(g−1)+g(h−1). Applying Hom(−,M) to 0→I→ZG→Z→0 therefore gives
H1(G,M)≅HomZG​(I,M)/resHomZG​(ZG,M).
(37)
Also a section of M⋊G→G has the form g↦(f(g),g) and is a group homomorphism exactly when f is a crossed homomorphism. Conjugation of sections by an element of M changes f by a principal crossed homomorphism. Thus H1 classifies the M-conjugacy classes of such sections, or equivalently of complements to M in the semidirect product.
In degree two, a normalized two-cocycle c:G×G→M satisfies
gc(h,l)−c(gh,l)+c(g,hl)−c(g,h)=0,c(1,g)=c(g,1)=0.
(38)
Define multiplication on M×G by
(m,g)(n,h)=(m+gn+c(g,h),gh).
(39)
The displayed cocycle identity is precisely associativity; normalization gives the identity and inverses exist. This produces a group extension 1→M→Ec​→G→1 with the prescribed action on M.
Conversely a set-theoretic section s:G→E, s(1)=1, gives c(g,h)=s(g)s(h)s(gh)−1 in the additive kernel of a group homomorphism. Associativity gives the cocycle condition. Replacing s(g) by b(g)s(g) changes c by
(db)(g,h)=gb(h)−b(gh)+b(g).
(40)
The resulting extensions are equivalent through a change of coordinates in M×G. Conversely any extension equivalence preserving both kernel of a group homomorphism and quotient yields such a change of section. Hence
H2(G,M) ⟷ equivalence classes of extensions of G by M with the specified action.​
(41)
The zero class corresponds exactly to a split extension. With trivial action these are central group extensions; addition of classes corresponds to the Baer sum. This proves the second group cohomology classifies group extensions interpretation.
For G=Z2 and trivial coefficients Z, put R=Z[x±1,y±1]. The Koszul resolution for a rank-two free abelian group is
0⟶Rd2​​R2d1​​Rx,y↦1​Z⟶0,
(42)
where d1​(a,b)=(x−1)a+(y−1)b and d2​(c)=(−(y−1)c,(x−1)c). To check exactness at R2, reduce d1​(a,b)=0 modulo x−1. The augmentation kernel is generated by x−1,y−1, proving the other exactness statement.
Applying HomR​(−,Z) makes both differentials zero, since x,y act trivially. Thus
H0(Z2,Z)=Z,H1(Z2,Z)=Z2,H2(Z2,Z)=Z,Hn=0 (n>2).​
(43)
Degree-one classes assign arbitrary integers to the two generators. Geometrically these are also the cohomology groups of the torus, a classifying space for Z2; its cohomology ring is the exterior algebra on the two degree-one classes.

(a,b,m)(c,d,l)=(a+c,b+d,m+l+nad).
(44)
The bilinear term is a two-cocycle. With generators x=(1,0,0), y=(0,1,0) and central z=(0,0,1),

The case n=0 is Z3; n=1 is the integer Heisenberg group. This realizes the integral central extensions of a rank-two free abelian group concretely.

We first prove the simple Artinian case of the Artin–Wedderburn theorem. This part does not need finite dimension over k. Choose a minimal nonzero left ideal L, using the descending chain condition. Simplicity gives LA=A. Also L2=0: otherwise LAL=(LA)L=L would be zero, since each lal′ lies in L2. Choose x∈L for which right multiplication rx​:L→L is nonzero. By simplicity of L, it is an automorphism. Choose e∈L with ex=x. Then (e2−e)x=0, and injectivity of rx​ gives e2=e. Thus L=Ae.
Right multiplication by e splits the left regular module as A=Ae⊕A(1−e). Repeat within the remaining left ideal: if it contains a minimal left ideal Af generated by an idempotent, the map v↦vf splits off Af. Each nonzero remaining complement is a strictly smaller left ideal, so the Artinian condition terminates the process. Therefore the regular module is a finite direct sum of minimal left ideals.
All these simple summands are isomorphic. For primitive idempotents e,f, simplicity gives AeA=A, so fAe=0; otherwise fAeA=fA would be zero. A nonzero element of fAe defines a nonzero homomorphism Af→Ae, which is an isomorphism between simple modules. Thus A≅L⊕n as a left module. Put Δ=EndA​(L), a division algebra by the Schur lemma. Right multiplication gives
Aop≅EndA​(A)≅Mn​(Δ).

A≅Mn​(D),D=Δop a division algebra.​

[A][B]=[A⊗k​B],1=[k],[A]−1=[Aop].​

Every class has a unique central division-algebra representative, by the regular-module description above.

(52)
are Aab​ and the split quaternion algebra (1,b)Q​≅M2​(Q). The latter has the matrix model diag(1,−1) and (01​b0​). Together the two subalgebras generate the whole tensor product, since they recover i2​ and j1​. Dimensions are sixteen on both sides, giving
Aa​⊗Ab​≅Aab​⊗M2​(Q),[Aa​][Ab​]=[Aab​].​
(53)
Quaternion conjugation identifies Aa​ with its opposite, so these classes have order at most two. For distinct primes p,q≡3(mod4), the parameter pq has odd valuation at p, so Apq​ is a division algebra. Thus [Ap​]=[Aq​]. There are infinitely many such primes: if their list were p1​,…,pm​, the integer 4p1​⋯pm​−1 would have a prime factor congruent to three modulo four outside that list. We conclude
Br(Q) is infinite.​
(54)
Indeed the same valuation argument for any nonempty finite product gives independent quaternion classes over rational numbers.
For R, we prove the required division-algebra classification. Let D be finite-dimensional and central over R. If D=R, choose a nonreal element; its irreducible real minimal polynomial is quadratic, so translating and rescaling it gives i∈D with i2=−1. The centralizer D+​ of i is a finite-dimensional division algebra over C=R(i), with that field central. Every element has a split minimal polynomial over C, hence belongs to C, so D+​=C.
Conjugation by i is an involution, giving D=D+​⊕D−​, where D−​={x:ix=−xi}. If D−​=0, then D=C, contrary to its center being R. Choose j=0 in D−​. For any x∈D−​, xj−1 commutes with i, so D−​=Cj. Also j2∈C and commutes with j, forcing j2∈R. It cannot be positive, since (j−j2​)(j+j2​)=0, or zero, since D is a division algebra. Rescale to j2=−1. Consequently
D=R⊕Ri⊕Rj⊕Rij≅H.
(55)
Conversely the positive definite quaternion norm makes H a division algebra. The two possible central division representatives are therefore R and H. They are not equivalent, and conjugation identifies H with its opposite, so
Br(R)≅Z/2,generated by [H].​
(56)
This uses the finite-dimensional real division-algebra classification directly, not as an unexplained theorem citation.
Finally let ks​ be a separable closure and Γ=Gal(ks​/k). The cohomological description is
Br(k)≅Hcont2​(Γ,ks×​)=L/k finite Galoislim​​H2(Gal(L/k),L×).​
(57)
Here ks×​ has the discrete topology and its natural Galois action. For a finite Galois splitting field L, identify A⊗k​L with a matrix algebra. Its descent action is implemented by matrices uσ​, unique up to scalars, so
uσ​σ(uτ​)uστ−1​=c(σ,τ)−1∈L×.
(58)
The inverse convention here agrees with the crossed-product convention below. The semilinear operators uσ​σ commute with the descended algebra A; their crossed product has class [A]−1 by the commuting-factor decomposition of the full endomorphism algebra. Inverting its scalar cocycle therefore gives the class [A]. Associativity gives the two-cocycle identity, and changing the representatives changes c by a coboundary. Conversely a two-cocycle defines the crossed-product algebra of a Galois extension, with uσ​a=σ(a)uσ​ and uσ​uτ​=c(σ,τ)uστ​. It is central simple and split by L. Galois descent identifies coboundaries with split matrix-algebra classes and gives H2(Gal(L/k),L×)≅ker(Br(k)→Br(L)). Every central simple algebra has a finite separable, hence finite Galois, splitting field, yielding the displayed limit.
