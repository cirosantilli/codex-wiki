For a circular orbit the origin of mean anomaly is arbitrary because there is no distinguished pericentre. Choose M=0 at the ascending node, so M is also the argument of latitude. If a different periapsis convention is used, replace M below by M+ω. The orbital-frame rotation from inclination and node gives
r=Rz​(Ω)Rx​(I)​acosMasinM0​​,xyz​=a(cosΩcosM−sinΩcosIsinM),=a(sinΩcosM+cosΩcosIsinM),=asinIsinM.​​
(23)
In particular the crossing at M=0 has increasing z for prograde n>0 and 0<I<π, confirming the ascending node convention.
Put ϵ=a/apl​ and c=cosψ=r⋅r⋆​. The binomial series expansion of the direct disturbing function is
∣r⋆​−r∣1​=apl​1​(1−2ϵc+ϵ2)−1/2=apl​1​[1+ϵc+2ϵ2​(3c2−1)+O(ϵ3)].
(24)
The indirect disturbing function subtracts GM⋆​ac/apl2​, cancelling the dipole term. The constant GM⋆​/apl​ has no spatial derivative and does not perturb the particle. After omitting that constant, the leading stellar quadrupole disturbing function is
R⋆​=apl3​GM⋆​a2​23cos2ψ−1​+O(apl4​GM⋆​a3​).​
(25)
Thus the PDF's equality is understood modulo a dynamically irrelevant additive constant and higher multipoles.
Define ℓ=M+Ω and let ℓ⋆​ be the star's orbital longitude as seen from the planet. Its orbital plane has Ipl​,Ωpl​. The vector from planet to star is the negative of the planet's heliocentric vector: if ℓpl​ describes that latter vector, use ℓ⋆​=ℓpl​+π. This sign matters in cosψ but drops out of cos2ψ. Expanding the two rotated unit vectors gives
cosψ=​cos(ℓ−ℓ⋆​)−2I2​sin(ℓ−Ω)sin(ℓ⋆​−Ω)−2Ipl2​​sin(ℓ⋆​−Ωpl​)sin(ℓ−Ωpl​)+IIpl​sin(ℓ−Ω)sin(ℓ⋆​−Ωpl​)+O((∣I∣+∣Ipl​∣)4).​​
(26)
For example, the particle's horizontal correction is −(I2/2)sin(ℓ−Ω)(−sinΩ,cosΩ), while its vertical component is Isin(ℓ−Ω); their dot products with the corresponding stellar components produce these terms. No cubic terms occur because simultaneous reversal of both small orbital inclinations leaves the dot product unchanged.
The two mean longitudes are uniform for the circular Kepler orbits, so orbit averaging is uniform in both ℓ and ℓ⋆​. Set c0​=cos(ℓ−ℓ⋆​) and c2​=cosψ−c0​ through quadratic order. The needed averages follow by integrating sine and cosine products over [0,2π]2:
⟨c02​⟩=21​,⟨c0​sin(ℓ−Ω)sin(ℓ⋆​−Ω)⟩=41​,
(27)
⟨c0​sin(ℓ−Ω)sin(ℓ⋆​−Ωpl​)⟩=41​cos(Ωpl​−Ω).
(28)
For the last identity, expand c0​=cosℓcosℓ⋆​+sinℓsinℓ⋆​ and factor the two integrations, each using ⟨sin2ℓ⟩=⟨cos2ℓ⟩=1/2. Squaring c0​+c2​ now yields
⟨cos2ψ⟩=21​−41​(I2+Ipl2​)+21​IIpl​cos(Ωpl​−Ω)+O(I4),
(29)
where the error includes all fourth-order combinations of the two orbital inclinations. Therefore
⟨23cos2ψ−1​⟩=41​+43​IIpl​cos(Ωpl​−Ω)−83​(I2+Ipl2​)+O(I4).​
(30)
Multiplying this by GM⋆​a2/apl3​ gives the requested secular disturbing function. Equivalently, the exact angular factor for two circular rings is (3cos2Im​−1)/8, where Im​ is their mutual inclination; its small-angle expansion gives the same result. This is the double-averaged circular tidal potential.
For aligned equatorial and planetary orbital planes, set Ipl​=0. The secular stellar quadrupole disturbing function then has derivative ∂I​R⋆​=−3GM⋆​a2I/(4apl3​). The supplied Lagrange planetary equations for nodal precession gives
Ω˙⋆​=−4napl3​3GM⋆​​.​
(31)
The planetary quadrupole coefficient J2​>0 contributes ∂I​RJ2​​=−3GMpl​J2​Rpl2​I/(2a3), hence
Ω˙J2​​=−2na53GMpl​J2​Rpl2​​=−23​nJ2​(aRpl​​)2.​
(32)
Both are retrograde nodal precession rates. Equating them cancels n and gives the Laplace radius
aL​=[2J2​M⋆​Mpl​​Rpl2​apl3​]1/5.​
(33)
The planetary quadrupole dominates inside aL​ and the stellar tide outside it. These are small-inclination rates for planet-bound Kepler orbits in the hierarchical regime a≪apl​. At exactly I=0, the node itself is undefined; the rates are the limiting precession frequencies of an infinitesimal tilt.
