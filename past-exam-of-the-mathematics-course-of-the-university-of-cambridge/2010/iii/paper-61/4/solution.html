Assume the physical coefficients Qa​,Qb​ are positive. Differentiate the catastrophic disruption threshold:
dDdQD∗​​=−aQa​D−a−1+bQb​Db−1=D−a−1[−aQa​+bQb​Da+b].
(34)
The bracket is strictly increasing from a negative value to positive infinity, so there is one critical point and it is the global minimum. In fact QD∗​ tends to infinity at both endpoints D→0 and D→∞. The strength-gravity disruption transition is therefore
Dw​=(bQb​aQa​​)1/(a+b).​
(35)
At this diameter, bQb​Dwb​=aQa​Dw−a​, so Qw∗​=[(a+b)/b]Qa​Dw−a​. This minimizes the full catastrophic disruption threshold; it need not be where its two contributions are equal.
For equal-density spherical planetesimals, the specific impact energy per unit target mass is 21​(Dim​/Dw​)3vrel2​. Setting this to Qw∗​ gives the catastrophic projectile diameter
Dwc​=Dw​(vrel2​2Qw∗​​)1/3=[bvrel2​2(a+b)Qa​​]1/3(bQb​aQa​​)(3−a)/[3(a+b)].​
(36)
Here vrel​ is the impact speed, the threshold is defined per target mass, and energy-coupling changes are not included. If projectile and target densities differ, multiply the diameter by (ρtarget​/ρprojectile​)1/3. For a=1/2, b=3/2,
Dw​=(3Qb​Qa​​)1/2,Qw∗​=34​Qa​(3Qb​Qa​​)−1/4,
(37)
so collecting the powers gives
Dwc​=23−3/4Qa3/4​Qb−5/12​vrel−2/3​.​
(38)
The result presumes that this projectile diameter is available in the population.
For the dust, n(D)dD is the total number in a diameter interval, not a number per unit volume. The mass normalization of a power-law size distribution with dust mass M/2 gives
2M​=∫Dmin​Dmax​​6πρD3​KD−αdD=6(4−α)πρK​(Dmax4−α​−Dmin4−α​),
(39)
K=πρ(Dmax4−α​−Dmin4−α​)3M(4−α)​≃πρDmax4−α​3M(4−α)​.​
(40)
The approximation uses (Dmin​/Dmax​)4−α≪1. Merely having a small diameter ratio does not guarantee this accuracy if α approaches four, so retain the exact denominator in that case.
With γ>0, the stated Poynting–Robertson drag law integrates directly:
dtd​r2=−D2γ​,r2(D,t)=r02​−D2γt​,tp​(D)=2γD(r02​−rp2​)​.​
(41)
Thus dust arrives in order of increasing diameter. This is burst transport under inverse-size radial drag, assuming an instantaneous release at r0​ and no intervening destruction or changes of diameter.
Dust that reaches the planet's orbital radius has several possible fates. It can pass without a close encounter and continue spiralling towards the star; undergo resonant trapping of dust in an exterior mean-motion resonance, where resonant torque temporarily balances the drag; be scattered onto a different stellar Kepler orbit or ejected; or collide with the planet and be accreted. Resonant trapping of dust can increase orbital eccentricity until escape or an encounter becomes possible. Temporary planet-bound capture can become permanent if drag or another dissipative process removes sufficient energy. Arrival at a radial circle alone does not guarantee a collision with the planet at the correct longitude.
A more massive planet has a larger Hill sphere, stronger resonant perturbations and stronger planetary scattering. Capture into a mean-motion resonance is favoured when drift across it is slow compared with its libration timescale. Since ∣r˙∣∝D−1 here, larger grains drift more slowly and are generally easier to trap; small fast-drifting grains more readily cross a resonance. Grain radiation pressure can also move resonance locations, though the prescribed nearly circular drift model omits that additional change. For direct impact, a larger physical radius increases the geometric cross-section, and gravitational focusing gives, in the two-body encounter approximation,
σacc​=πRpl2​(1+v∞2​vesc2​​),vesc2​=Rpl​2GMpl​​.
(42)
A compact massive planet can nevertheless scatter or eject grains rather than hit them: its encounter region can be much larger than its physical radius. Slower dust migration permits more repeated encounter opportunities. These competing effects mean neither planet mass nor grain diameter alone specifies an accretion probability.
For the idealized complete-accretion calculation, put T=tp​(Dmax​) and η=Dmin​/Dmax​. At time t, the arriving diameter is Dt​=Dmax​t/T and dDt​/dt=Dmax​/T. Hence the dust-burst accretion pulse follows from the arriving mass per diameter:
M˙p​=6πρK​Dt3−α​TDmax​​=2T[1−η4−α](4−α)M​(Tt​)3−α,ηT≤t≤T.
(43)
Dropping the small lower-cutoff normalization correction gives the requested expression
M˙p​=(2−2α​)tp​(Dmax​)M​[tp​(Dmax​)t​]3−α,tp​(Dmin​)≤t≤tp​(Dmax​).​
(44)
The rate is zero before the smallest grains arrive and after the largest arrive. Integrating the exact expression over its support returns M/2, as required by mass conservation. The approximate expression has that integral to the same lower-cutoff accuracy. This pulse assumes no resonant delay or scattering loss before accretion.
For the surface density of a size-sorted dust burst, take 0<r<r0​ and write
Hr​=r02​−r2,Tr​=tr​(Dmax​)=2γDmax​Hr​​,y=Tr​t​,Dr​=Dmax​y=Hr​2γt​.
(45)
At fixed t>0, the size-radius map is monotone and dDr​/dr=2rDr​/Hr​. Define Σ(r,t) as the azimuthally averaged surface density, so the mass in an annulus is 2πrΣdr. Transforming the power-law size distribution into radial mass gives
2πrΣ(r,t)=6πρK​Dr3−α​drdDr​​=3πρK​Hr​rDr4−α​​.
(46)
It follows that
Σ(r,t)=2π(r02​−r2)[1−η4−α](4−α)M​[tr​(Dmax​)t​]4−α,η≤tr​(Dmax​)t​≤1.​
(47)
The requested leading approximation omits 1−η4−α from the denominator. The surface density is zero where the inferred Dr​ lies outside the original size interval. Equivalently, one can obtain it from the instantaneous inward mass flux divided by 2πr∣r˙(Dr​)∣. At t=0 the idealized initial distribution is a delta-function ring at r0​; the formula above describes the subsequent radial spread. No uniform initial azimuthal distribution is required for this azimuthal average, but the local density of a clump would require its angular evolution. If the complete-absorption planet assumption is retained, this expression holds exterior to rp​ and Σ=0 interior to the planet.
