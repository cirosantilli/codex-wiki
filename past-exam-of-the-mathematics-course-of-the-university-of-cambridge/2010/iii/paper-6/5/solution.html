For an element a of a complex unital Banach algebra, its spectrum of an element is
σA​(a)={λ∈C:λ1−a is not invertible in A}.​
(40)
The nonzero spectra of products in opposite orders agree:
σ(ab)∖{0}=σ(ba)∖{0}.​
(41)
For λ=0, if R=(λ1−ab)−1 exists, direct multiplication on both sides verifies
(λ1−ba)−1=λ−1(1+bRa).
(42)
Interchanging a,b proves the converse. Zero may differ. On the Hilbert space ℓ2(N0​), let S be the unilateral shift operator Sen​=en+1​ and take a=S∗, b=S. Then ab=S∗S=I, whereas ba=SS∗=I−P0​, where P0​ is projection onto the first coordinate. Hence
σ(ab)={1},σ(ba)={0,1}.​
(43)
Both values for the second operator occur on its two nonzero invariant summands; every other value has an inverse obtained separately on those summands.
We use the spectral definition of a Positive element of a C-star algebra: h is positive when h=h∗ and σ(h)⊆[0,∞). The continuous functional calculus, supplied by the Commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem for the algebra generated by a Normal element of a C-star algebra and its adjoint, sends F∈C(σ(h)) to F(h). It preserves products, sums, conjugation and the identity, is isometric, and satisfies σ(F(h))=F(σ(h)). For a self-adjoint element the spectrum is real, so real-valued functions produce self-adjoint elements.
For example, F(t)=t​ gives the positive square root in a C-star algebra of a Positive element of a C-star algebra. It satisfies (h1/2)2=h. Its uniqueness follows from the composition rule: if d≥0 and d2=h, then the continuous functional calculus in the algebra generated by d gives d2​=d. For positive invertible h, the functions t−1 and t−1/2 give positive inverses and inverse square roots. If 0≤h and r≥∥h∥, the function r−t gives r1−h≥0; the same calculus gives
∥r1−h∥=maxt∈σ(h)​∣r−t∣≤r.
(44)
These transfer elementary scalar inequalities for functions of one element to the algebra. They do not justify applying a scalar inequality to arbitrary noncommuting elements.
To prove closure under addition without assuming an order theorem, let a,b≥0, set α=∥a∥, β=∥b∥ and s=α+β. If s=0, both elements are zero. Otherwise,
∥s1−(a+b)∥≤∥α1−a∥+∥β1−b∥≤s.
(45)
The element a+b is self-adjoint, so any λ∈σ(a+b) is real and satisfies ∣s−λ∣≤s. Thus λ≥0, proving
a,b≥0⟹a+b≥0.​
(46)
We will also use that the positive cone is proper: if h≥0 and −h≥0, then σ(h)⊆{0}, and the norm formula for self-adjoint elements gives h=0.
We next prove a∗a≥0 without presupposing that positivity is defined by such products. Put h=a∗a, which is self-adjoint, and form its negative part by the continuous functional calculus:
h−​=F(h),F(t)=max{−t,0}.
(47)
This is positive. Let c=ah−1/2​. Since h−​ commutes with h,
c∗c=h−1/2​hh−1/2​=−h−2​≤0.
(48)
The nonzero product-spectrum identity proved above gives σ(cc∗)∖{0}=σ(c∗c)∖{0}. As cc∗ is self-adjoint, it too has spectrum in (−∞,0], so cc∗≤0.
Write c=u+iv with u=u∗ and v=v∗. The continuous functional calculus makes u2 and v2 positive, and the addition result gives
c∗c+cc∗=2(u2+v2)≥0.
(49)
But both summands on the left are nonpositive, so their sum is also nonpositive. Properness of the cone forces c∗c+cc∗=0. Now c∗c=−cc∗ is positive as well as nonpositive, so c∗c=0. The C-star identity gives c=0, whence h−2​=0 and h−​=0. Thus h has no negative spectral part:
a∗a≥0for every a∈A.​
(50)
This proves the positivity of adjoint products from spectral positivity and the equivalence with the usual definition by elements of the form d∗d: the reverse direction is the factorization h=(h1/2)∗h1/2 for h≥0.
Finally, if a≥0 and c∈A, write
c∗ac=(a1/2c)∗(a1/2c).
(51)
The preceding result therefore proves
c∗ac≥0.​
(52)
In particular, congruence preserves positivity in a C-star algebra, and a≤b implies c∗ac≤c∗bc. These facts will justify the noncommutative order manipulations in the numbered parts.
