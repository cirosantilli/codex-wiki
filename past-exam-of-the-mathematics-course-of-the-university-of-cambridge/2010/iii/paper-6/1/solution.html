We first prove the algebraic extension step needed for Hahn-Banach separation theorem. Suppose p is a finite sublinear functional on a real vector space and g is a linear functional on a vector subspace W, with g≤p there. For v∈/W, choose a real number c between
supw∈W​{g(w)−p(w−v)}andinfw∈W​{p(w+v)−g(w)}.
(1)
This interval is nonempty: for any w,z∈W,
g(w)+g(z)=g(w+z)≤p(w+z)≤p(w−v)+p(z+v).
(2)
Taking w=0 or z=0 also bounds the endpoints by finite numbers. Define g​(w+tv)=g(w)+tc. For t>0, the upper bound for c, applied to w/t, proves g​(w+tv)≤p(w+tv). For t<0, writing t=−s and using the lower bound at w/s proves the same inequality. The case t=0 is the original domination. Thus a dominated linear functional extends over one further direction. Order all dominated extensions by extension of their domains. A chain has its union as an upper bound, so Zorn's lemma supplies a maximal extension. The one-direction argument shows its domain is the whole vector space. This proves the required algebraic Hahn-Banach theorem, rather than assuming the separation result.
Choose a∈A, and put C=A−a. This is a radially open convex set containing zero. It is absorbing: along each line through zero, sufficiently small multiples of any vector lie in C. Its Minkowski functional
p(x)=inf{t>0:x∈tC}
(3)
is finite and nonnegative. Positive homogeneity follows by rescaling t. If x∈sC and y∈tC, convexity gives x+y∈(s+t)C, proving subadditivity by taking infima. Moreover,
C={x:p(x)<1}.
(4)
Indeed, p(x)<1 gives x∈sC for some s<1, hence x∈C by convexity and 0∈C. Conversely, radial openness at x∈C permits (1+ϵ)x∈C for some ϵ>0, giving p(x)<1.
Since A∩U=∅, a∈/U and u−a∈/C for every u∈U. Define a linear functional on U+Ra by
g(u+ta)=−t.
(5)
It is well defined because a∈/U. For t<0, write s=−t; then p(u+ta)=sp(u/s−a)≥s=g(u+ta). For t≥0, domination follows from g(u+ta)≤0≤p(u+ta). The extension step therefore gives L:V→R with L≤p, L∣U​=0 and L(a)=−1. If x∈A, then
L(x)+1=L(x−a)≤p(x−a)<1,
(6)
so L(x)<0. Consequently
H=kerL⊇U,H∩A=∅.​
(7)
Because L(a)=0, this kernel of a linear map has codimension one and is a hyperplane. This establishes the geometric Hahn-Banach theorem for radially open sets without assuming a norm or a topology on V.
For the convex function, use its strict epigraph in V×R. It is nonempty and convex, and does not contain (0,0). It is also radially open: on every affine line, a finite convex function is continuous. To justify this last fact, convexity orders secant slopes; on a smaller interval the slopes are bounded above and below by slopes to two fixed exterior endpoints, giving a local Lipschitz bound. Thus t↦f(x+ty)−β−ts is continuous, and a strict negative value persists near t=0.
Apply the separation theorem with the zero subspace. A nonzero separating linear functional has form L(x,β)=g(x)+cβ. Its values on the convex epigraph cannot have both signs, since a line segment would then meet its kernel. Choose the sign so that L>0 on the epigraph. At x=0 and β>0 this gives c>0. Letting β decrease to f(x) yields g(x)+cf(x)≥0. Therefore
l(x)=−g(x)/c≤f(x)for every x∈V.​
(8)
The resulting linear functional need not be nonzero: for example, the zero functional is the only linear minorant of f(x)=x2 on R.
Now suppose the norm and continuity hypotheses hold. Domination at x and −x gives
−f(−x)≤l(x)≤f(x).
(9)
If xn​∈kerl and xn​→x in norm, apply this inequality to x−xn​. Since l(x−xn​)=l(x) and both bounding values tend to f(0)=0, we obtain l(x)=0. The kernel of a linear map is therefore closed.
Finally, a linear functional with closed kernel on a normed vector space is continuous. If l=0, this is immediate. Otherwise choose v with l(v)=1 and put M=kerl. Closedness gives d=dist(v,M)>0. For l(x)=0, x/l(x)−v∈M, so d≤∥x∥/∣l(x)∣. The same resulting bound holds when l(x)=0:
∣l(x)∣≤d−1∥x∥.​
(10)
This proves continuity and explains exactly how closedness of the kernel is used.
