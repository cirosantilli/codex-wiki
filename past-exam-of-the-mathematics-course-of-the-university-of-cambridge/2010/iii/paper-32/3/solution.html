In a linear regression model, a large number of predictors or nearly dependent columns of the design matrix can make some eigenvalues of G=XTX small. The ordinary least squares estimate has covariance matrix σ2G−1, so these directions have large variance, even though the estimator is unbiased. Ridge regression reduces this variance by shrinking the estimate towards zero, at the cost of introducing bias. This bias-variance tradeoff can improve the mean squared error when the signal is not too large in the shrunken directions.
For the usual preprocessing with a regression intercept, separate the constant column from the predictors and leave the intercept unpenalized. Subtract the sample mean from the response and from each nonconstant predictor, then divide each centered predictor column by its positive scale, for example its Euclidean norm. Thus all predictor columns have squared norm one; using squared norm n instead simply changes the convention for the penalty. Centering is performed on the slope columns, not on the constant column. A full-rank design containing an intercept retains full rank on its centered slope columns. A model without an intercept should retain its specified mean structure unless an intercept is deliberately introduced.
Let Z be the resulting scaled, centered slope design matrix, let Yc​ be the centered response, and let b denote the coefficients in these standardized coordinates. The ridge regression problem is
minb∈Rq​{∥Yc​−Zb∥2+λ∥b∥2},λ>0.
(23)
Here q is the number of slopes; it is one less than the original number of columns when a constant column was present. The gradient of this objective is 2(ZTZ+λI)b−2ZTYc​. Its Hessian matrix is a positive-definite matrix, giving the unique closed-form ridge regression estimator
bλR​=(ZTZ+λI)−1ZTYc​.​
(24)
The regression intercept in the original coordinates is recovered as a=Y−∑j​Xj​bj​/sj​, and the original slopes are β​j​=bj​/sj​, where sj​ is the scale used for column j. This is the unpenalized intercept in ridge regression convention. For a fixed full-rank coefficient design written simply as X, with response Y and coefficient β, the same formula reads
β​λR​=(XTX+λI)−1XTY.​
(25)
The risk comparison below first uses the fixed coefficient coordinates in which this quadratic penalty is imposed, and then addresses reversal of the scaling.
Assume at least one penalized coefficient, zero-mean errors with covariance matrix σ2I, and σ2>0. No normal distribution assumption is needed for this mean squared error calculation. Put G=XTX and Aλ​=(G+λI)−1. The covariance and bias of a ridge regression estimator follow directly from its linear expression:
Eβ​λR​−β=−λAλ​β,Cov(β​λR​)=σ2Aλ​GAλ​.
(26)
For the centered slope model, the response errors have covariance matrix σ2C, where C=I−11T/n. Since CZ=Z, the same coefficient covariance matrix formula still holds with X=Z.
By the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices, write G=Qdiag(d1​,…,dq​)QT, with Q orthogonal and every dj​>0, and put γ=QTβ. The bias-variance decomposition of mean squared error now gives
R(λ)=E∥β​λR​−β∥2=∑j=1q​(dj​+λ)2λ2γj2​+σ2dj​​,R(0)=σ2∑j=1q​dj​1​.
(27)
The expression at λ=0 is the ordinary least squares mean squared error. Its right derivative is
R′(0)=−2σ2∑j=1q​dj2​1​<0.
(28)
Therefore, for every fixed coefficient vector, all sufficiently small positive penalties strictly improve mean squared error.
An explicit sufficient choice follows by subtracting the ordinary least squares risk term by term:
R(λ)−R(0)=∑j=1q​(dj​+λ)2λ(λγj2​−2σ2−λσ2/dj​)​.
(29)
Since γj2​≤∥β∥2, every summand is negative whenever
λ>0,λ∥β∥2<2σ2.​
(30)
When β=0, every positive penalty works. This is a pointwise result: the permitted penalty may depend on the true coefficient vector. A fixed positive penalty cannot improve risk for all arbitrarily large signals, because its squared bias grows quadratically with the signal. The same distinction appears in uniform directional risk improvement by ridge regression and unbounded directional risk of fixed ridge shrinkage. In centered coordinates the unpenalized intercept contributes the same σ2/n to both coefficient risks, so it does not affect the improvement. Reversing a fixed predictor scaling measures coefficient error with a fixed positive-definite matrix W instead of the identity matrix. For this weighted loss the squared bias still has derivative zero at λ=0, while the variance term has derivative
−2σ2tr(WG−2)<0.
(31)
The inequality follows because tr(WG−2)=tr(G−1WG−1)>0. Thus sufficiently small penalties also improve the original-coordinate mean squared error, though the explicit sufficient bound above was for the unweighted coordinates. Recovering an uncentered intercept adds a fixed nonnegative quadratic form to the slope loss, and its sample-mean error is uncorrelated with the centered slope errors, so the same weighted argument applies. A model with only an unpenalized intercept gives identical estimators. If the noise variance were zero, the strict improvement assertion would fail; positive noise variance is essential.
