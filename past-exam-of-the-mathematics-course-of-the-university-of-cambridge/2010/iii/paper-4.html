A nilpotent group has a finite central series
1=Z0​≤Z1​≤⋯≤Zc​=G,[G,Zj+1​]≤Zj​.
(1)
Equivalently the upper central series, defined by Zj+1​/Zj​=Z(G/Zj​), reaches G.
We first prove the normalizer condition for nilpotent groups. If H<G, choose the least j with Zj​⊆H. Then Zj−1​≤H, and an element z∈Zj​∖H satisfies [z,H]≤Zj−1​≤H. Thus z normalizes H, giving H<NG​(H).
Let P be a Sylow subgroup of a finite nilpotent G, and write N=NG​(P). If N<G, the normalizer condition gives some g∈NG​(N)∖N. The subgroups P and Pg are both Sylow subgroups of N, so Sylow theorem supplies n∈N with Pgn=P. But then gn∈NG​(P)=N, forcing g∈N, a contradiction. Therefore every Sylow subgroup is a normal subgroup of G.
For normal Sylow subgroups P,Q at different primes, [P,Q]≤P∩Q=1. They commute, have coprime orders, and their product has order ∣G∣. Multiplication consequently gives an isomorphism from their external direct product of groups onto G.
Conversely, a nontrivial finite p-group has nontrivial center of a group: its class equation has every noncentral conjugacy-class size divisible by p, so ∣Z(G)∣ is a positive multiple of p. Induction on order, applied to G/Z(G), gives a central series for G. Finite direct products of these central series give a central series for the product, padding shorter series by the full factor. Thus the finite nilpotent group decomposition is
G nilpotent ⟺ G≅p∣∣G∣∏​Pp​.​
(2)

For a finite group, define the Frattini subgroup by
Φ(G)=⋂M maximal in G​M;
(3)
the intersection is G when there are no maximal subgroups, which for a finite group happens only for G=1. A second definition is the set of non-generators: an element x such that ⟨S,x⟩=G always implies ⟨S⟩=G.
If x∈Φ(G) and H=⟨S⟩<G, put H in a maximal subgroup M. Then x∈M, so ⟨S,x⟩≤M<G. Conversely, if x∈/Φ(G), some maximal subgroup M omits it; maximality gives ⟨M,x⟩=G, although M=G. This proves equivalence.
Every group automorphism permutes maximal subgroups, so Φ(G) is a characteristic subgroup, in particular a normal subgroup. Also
HΦ(G)=G⟹H=G:
(4)
a proper H would lie in a maximal subgroup containing both factors.
Set F=Φ(G) and take a Sylow subgroup P of F. For g∈G, normality of F makes Pg a Sylow subgroup of F. Conjugating inside F therefore gives the Frattini argument factorization G=FNG​(P). The preceding generating property forces NG​(P)=G. Thus every Sylow subgroup of F is normal, and part (i) shows that F is a nilpotent group.
Now suppose G is a finite p-group. The normalizer condition makes every maximal subgroup M normal: M<NG​(M) and maximality force NG​(M)=G. The quotient G/M has no proper nontrivial subgroup, hence is cyclic of prime order; its p-power order makes that prime p. Consequently every such M contains
D=[G,G]Gp,
(5)
where the two factors are the commutator subgroup and the subgroup generated by pth powers. Thus D≤Φ(G).
The quotient G/D is an elementary abelian group, hence a vector space over Fp​. If x∈/D, extend the nonzero vector xD to a basis and choose a linear functional nonzero on it. Its kernel pulls back to a subgroup of index p, a maximal subgroup omitting x. Therefore x∈/Φ(G), proving
Φ(G) is characteristic and nilpotent,Φ(G)=[G,G]Gp for finite p-groups.​
(6)
The two factors in the last formula are multiplied; the superscripted expression in the TeX aid is not the formula printed in the PDF.

For a subset S of a finite p-group, its images span the Frattini quotient precisely when ⟨S⟩Φ(G)=G. By the generating property proved in part (ii), this is equivalent to ⟨S⟩=G.
If S is a minimal generating set of a group, its images must be an irredundant spanning set in the vector space G/Φ(G). A linear dependence would express one image using the others; those other images would still span, so the other elements would still generate G, a contradiction. Thus the images form a basis. Conversely lifts of a basis generate G, and deleting a lift destroys spanning and hence generation.
This proves the Burnside basis theorem, including equality of sizes for every inclusion-minimal generating set of a group:
∣S∣=dimFp​​G/Φ(G)=logp​∣G:Φ(G)∣.​
(7)
Inclusion-minimal generating sets of a group need not have equal sizes in arbitrary finite groups; the elementary-abelian Frattini quotient is the essential p-group feature.

We prove the primitive three-cycle criterion through supports. First, suppose a subgroup contains AU​, the alternating group on a set U of at least three letters, and a three-cycle with support E meeting U.
If E adds one letter, it has two letters in U. The group AU​ is transitive on unordered pairs of U: for three letters its cyclic action permutes the three pairs, and for at least four letters one can adjust the parity of a permutation sending one pair to another while preserving its target pair. Conjugating the new cycle, and using inverses, supplies every three-cycle on two old letters and the new letter. Together with AU​, these generate AU∪E​.
If E adds two letters, write its cycle as τ=(abc) with a∈U. Choose a′∈U∖{a} and conjugate within AU​ to obtain τ′=(a′bc). Then
τ(τ′)−1=(aba′).
(11)
The preceding case first adds b; applying it again to τ adds c. This proves connected triple supports generate an alternating group, by successively merging the intersecting triples of a connected support hypergraph. We used that three-cycles generate alternating groups: pairs of transpositions with a common letter are three-cycles, while a pair of disjoint transpositions is a product of two three-cycles.
Now take all conjugates in G of the given three-cycle. Their support hypergraph is G-invariant, and its connected components form a block system. Transitivity makes their union the whole set. There is an edge, so a component has more than one vertex. A primitive group action forces a single component. The preceding merging argument proves
An​≤G.​
(12)
For the first family of counterexamples, let
G=PGL2​(p)↷P1(Fp​),p>3.
(13)
Its elements are Möbius transformations x↦(ax+b)/(cx+d), modulo nonzero scalar matrices. Any ordered triple of distinct projective points is the image of (∞,0,1) under exactly one such transformation: representatives of the first two target lines form a basis, and scaling the two columns sends their sum to the third target line. Thus the action is sharply three-transitive, hence two-transitive and primitive. Its degree is p+1 and order p(p2−1). It is divisible by p, but
∣PGL2​(p)∣∣Ap+1​∣​=2(p−2)!​>1,

so it cannot contain Ap+1​.
For the second example take
p=7,G=PGL2​(8),n=9,∣G∣=504.​
(15)
The same projective-line construction over F8​ is sharply three-transitive. Its order is divisible by 7 and is less than ∣A9​∣=181440, so it does not contain A9​. For a concrete field one can use F8​=F2​[u]/(u3+u+1).

An element fixing every t must have a=b by evaluating at the identity, and then a−1ta=t for every t. Its kernel is therefore {(z,z):z∈Z(T)}, which is trivial by the center of a group hypothesis.
The identity stabilizer subgroup is Δ={(a,a):a∈T}. For every subgroup H containing Δ, set
N={b:(1,b)∈H}.
(16)
Conjugation by diagonal elements makes N normal in T. Multiplying (a,b)∈H by (a,a)−1 shows (1,a−1b)∈H, so
H={(a,b):a−1b∈N}.
(17)
Conversely each normal subgroup N gives such an H. Thus the subgroups strictly between Δ and T×T correspond exactly to proper nontrivial normal subgroups of T.
For a transitive action, blocks containing a chosen point correspond to subgroups containing its stabilizer subgroup: a block is the orbit of that point under its setwise stabilizer subgroup, and conversely the orbit under an intermediate subgroup gives a block. Therefore the diagonal action on a centerless group is primitive exactly when T is simple. A nontrivial centerless simple group cannot be abelian, because an abelian group's center of a group is the whole group. Hence
The action is faithful and transitive, and primitive iff T is nonabelian simple.​
(18)
This uses the usual nontrivial degree convention. If one allows a one-point action to count as primitive, T=1 is the additional degenerate exception.

n=60=p+1,G=A5​×A5​,∣G∣=3600,59∤3600.​

Work in the finite-group setting. If M is maximal in a nonabelian simple G, then M=1: otherwise every nonidentity element would generate G, forcing a cyclic group of prime order. Choose a minimal normal subgroup N=1 of M. Every characteristic subgroup of N is normal in M, so minimality makes N a characteristically simple group. Its normalizer contains M. By maximality it is M or G, and the latter would make N a nontrivial normal subgroup of the simple group G, impossible because N≤M<G. This proves the maximal subgroup normalizer criterion:
M=NG​(N) for some characteristically simple N.​
(20)
For A5​, stabilizer subgroups have order 12. Stabilizer subgroups of a two-element subset have order ∣(S2​×S3​)∩A5​∣=6, and are isomorphic to S3​; for example ⟨(123),(12)(45)⟩. The normalizer of a Sylow 5-subgroup has order 10: its normalizer in S5​ has order 5⋅4, and exactly half its permutations are even, since a multiplier of order four on F5​ is an odd four-cycle. This gives a dihedral group of order 10.
To prove completeness, an intransitive subgroup either fixes a point, or its orbits on five letters have sizes two and three. It therefore lies in one of the first two types. A transitive proper subgroup H has order divisible by 5. Its number of Sylow 5-subgroups is one or six, since A5​ has six and the count is 1 modulo 5. A count of six would make 30∣∣H∣, forcing an index-two subgroup and contradicting Simplicity of the alternating group A5. Therefore H normalizes its unique Sylow 5-subgroup and lies in the order-ten normalizer.
Stabilizer subgroups are maximal because their index is prime. The order-ten group is transitive and cannot have a larger proper transitive overgroup by the preceding argument. The order-six group has no fixed point, cannot lie in a stabilizer subgroup, and cannot lie in the order-ten type because 3 does not divide 10. Thus all three types are maximal. A5​ is transitive on points and on unordered pairs, while Sylow conjugacy handles the normalizers. Consequently
A5​: three maximal-subgroup classes, of orders 6, 10, 12.​
(21)
Now let G=GL3​(2). Choosing independent columns gives ∣G∣=7⋅6⋅4=168. Its simplicity follows from the elementary projective-transvection proof in part 4(i), which does not use this subgroup classification.
A stabilizer subgroup and a plane stabilizer subgroup each have index seven and order 24. To construct the remaining type, identify F23​ with F8​ and take the Singer cycle P of multiplications by nonzero field elements, of order seven. Any linear map commuting with a primitive multiplication commutes with all of F8​, so its centralizer is precisely P. A normalizer sends a primitive element a to another root of its minimum polynomial, namely a,a2,a4. The Frobenius map x↦x2 realizes those three choices. Hence
NG​(P)=P⋊C3​,∣NG​(P)∣=21,n7​(G)=8.
(22)
If 7∣∣H∣ for a proper H<G, its Sylow count is one or eight. Eight would make 56∣∣H∣, hence ∣H∣=56. Its index-three coset action would inject the simple group G into S3​: the kernel is normal and cannot be all of G. This is impossible. Thus H normalizes a unique Sylow 7-subgroup and lies in the order-21 normalizer.
If 7∤∣H∣, its order divides 24. In its action on seven nonzero vectors, an odd orbit has size one or three. A one-point orbit gives a fixed point. If a three-point orbit is collinear, it is the set of nonzero vectors of an invariant plane. If its three vectors are independent, their nonzero sum is fixed by H. Thus H lies in a point or plane stabilizer subgroup.
The index-seven stabilizer subgroups are maximal, and any proper overgroup of the order-21 normalizer would again have a unique Sylow 7-subgroup and normalize that same subgroup, so the normalizer is maximal too. The point and plane stabilizer subgroups are not conjugate: a stabilizer subgroup fixes a point, whereas a plane stabilizer subgroup is transitive on its three internal points and its four external points, so fixes no point. Each type is one conjugacy class. We have proved maximal subgroups of GL3 over F2:
GL3​(2): three classes, of orders 21, 24, 24.​

The Fano plane has as points the seven nonzero vectors of F23​, because every one-dimensional vector subspace has just one nonzero vector. Its lines are the seven two-dimensional vector subspaces. Every line has three points, every point lies on three lines, two points determine the line {u,v,u+v}, and two lines meet in one point.
The two order-24 classes are therefore stabilizer subgroups of points and of lines. Each is isomorphic to S4​: fixing a vector gives the block group F22​⋊GL2​(2), the full affine group on four points; inverse transpose gives the dual line stabilizer subgroup. Their distinct fixed-point behavior in part (i) distinguishes the two conjugacy classes.
For a point p and line L, Gp​∩GL​ stabilizes the ordered pair (p,L). There are 7⋅3=21 incident pairs and 7⋅4=28 nonincident pairs. A change of basis takes any pair to a fixed model of its type, so each type is a single orbit. Orbit-stabilizer theorem gives the Fano point-line stabilizer intersection:
∣Gp​∩GL​∣={8,6,​p∈L,p∈/L.​​
(24)
Both occur: take L=⟨e1​,e2​⟩ and choose p=⟨e1​⟩ or p=⟨e3​⟩.
For the extension, identify vectors and covectors by the standard pairing. The inverse-transpose automorphism exchanges a point with its orthogonal plane and exchanges the two order-24 classes. Thus it is outer. The Fano plane duality group G=G⋊⟨τ⟩ has order 336 and acts on points and lines, preserving incidence while allowing their types to be swapped.
The stabilizer subgroup of an unordered point-line pair has twice the corresponding ordered-pair stabilizer subgroup: some element outside G swaps the pair, since duality produces a pair of the same incidence type and G is transitive on such pairs. Hence these stabilizer subgroups have orders 16 and 12. Also every outside conjugate of a Sylow 7-subgroup can be moved back by an element of G, giving
∣NG​(P)∣=2∣NG​(P)∣=42.
(25)
We prove these exhaust the maximal subgroups other than G. Let K be such a maximal subgroup and H=K∩G. Since K is not contained in G, H is normal in K and ∣K:H∣=2. If 7∣∣H∣, part (i) gives a unique Sylow 7-subgroup P of H. It is characteristic in H, hence normalized by K. Therefore maximality forces K=NG​(P).
Suppose 7∤∣H∣ and H=1. The classification argument in part (i) says H fixes a point or a line. Because H is normal in K, an element outside G swaps its fixed-point and fixed-line sets, so it fixes at least one of each. Write
d=dimVH,d∗=dim(V∗)H.
(26)
Fixed lines are kernels of fixed nonzero covectors, since the only nonzero scalar is one. Duality gives 2d−1=2d∗−1, hence d=d∗∈{1,2}.
If d=1, there is a unique fixed point and unique fixed line; K preserves their unordered pair. If d=2, put W=VH and A=((V∗)H)⊥. For every h∈H, ker(h−I) contains the plane W and im(h−I) lies in the line A. Over F2​ there is only one nonzero linear map V/W→A, so H has order two. Invertibility forces A⊆W: for h=I+vf one needs f(v)=0. Thus A and W are its unique image point and kernel line. These are canonically swapped by duality, so K again preserves their unordered incident pair.
If H=1, then K has order two, generated by an involutory duality u. For any point p, the pair {p,u(p)} is preserved by u. Hence K lies in an order-12 or order-16 pair stabilizer subgroup and is not maximal. We have exhausted all cases.
The pair stabilizer subgroups are themselves maximal: a proper maximal overgroup is one of the just-listed types, and divisibility excludes orders 12 inside 16 or 42, and 16 inside 12 or 42. The order-42 normalizer is maximal by the unique-Sylow argument. Incidence types give one conjugacy class each; Sylow conjugacy gives one normalizer class. Thus, apart from the normal index-two subgroup G,
G: three maximal-subgroup classes, of orders 12, 16, 42.​

Let F=Fq​. The general linear group over a finite field consists of all invertible linear maps Fn→Fn, and the special linear group over a finite field is its determinant-one subgroup. Counting the ordered bases gives
∣GLn​(q)∣=∏i=0n−1​(qn−qi)=qn(n−1)/2∏j=1n​(qj−1).
(28)
The determinant map is onto F×, since diag(t,1,…,1) has determinant t. Its kernel is SLn​(q), so
∣SLn​(q)∣=q−1∣GLn​(q)∣​.​
(29)
For n>1, commuting with all the elementary transvection matrices I+tEij​ forces a matrix to be scalar. Therefore the center of a group of SLn​(q) consists of λI with λn=1. The multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic, so this center of a group has size d=gcd(n,q−1). The projective special linear group over a finite field consequently has order
∣PSLn​(q)∣=gcd(n,q−1)qn(n−1)/2∏j=2n​(qj−1)​.
(30)
Here is a proof of simplicity which isolates all the required ingredients. First we prove the Iwasawa simplicity lemma in the form needed. Suppose a group H acts faithfully and primitively, is a perfect group, and has an abelian subgroup U normal in a stabilizer subgroup Hα​, whose conjugates generate H. For a nontrivial normal subgroup N, its orbits form a block system. Faithfulness prevents all these orbits from being singletons, so primitivity makes N transitive. Thus H=NHα​. Modulo N, each conjugate of U has exactly the same image as U, because we may write its conjugating element as nh with n∈N and h∈Hα​. The quotient H/N is generated by this one abelian image and hence is abelian. Since H is a perfect group, H/N=1. Thus every nontrivial normal subgroup is H.
Apply this to the action on projective points, that is, one-dimensional vector subspaces of Fn. Any ordered pair of distinct lines can be sent to any other by a linear map obtained by extending representatives to bases. Multiplying one target basis vector by a suitable nonzero scalar adjusts the determinant to one without changing either target line. Thus SLn​(q) is two-transitive on these points. A linear map fixing every line is scalar: it is diagonal on a fixed basis, and fixing the lines spanned by ei​+ej​ makes all diagonal entries equal. The projective quotient therefore acts faithfully, two-transitively, and primitively.
For the line ⟨e1​⟩, use
U={I+e1​f:f∈(Fn)∗, f(e1​)=0}.
(31)
These matrices multiply by addition of f, so U is abelian. Conjugating by a matrix preserving ⟨e1​⟩ preserves U, so it is normal in that stabilizer subgroup. Its conjugates include every elementary transvection matrix I+tEij​.
These elementary transvection matrices generate SLn​(q) by row elimination. For completeness, row addition is elementary, signed row interchange is elementary, and compensating diagonal scalings are elementary because, in a two-dimensional coordinate block,
w(t)=(10​t1​)(1−t−1​01​)(10​t1​)=(0−t−1​t0​),w(t)w(−1)=diag(t,t−1).
(32)
Elimination using these operations reduces a determinant-one matrix to the identity.
It remains to show that these groups are perfect groups. In the following matrix identities use [x,y]=xyx−1y−1; the alternative group commutator convention generates the same commutator subgroup. If n≥3, distinct indices give the group commutator identity
[I+aEij​,I+bEjk​]=I+abEik​.
(33)
Every elementary generator is therefore a group commutator. For n=2 and q>3, choose λ∈F× with λ2=1, and let D=diag(λ,λ−1). Then
[D,I+tE12​]=I+(λ2−1)tE12​.
(34)
As t varies this supplies all upper elementary transvection matrices, and conjugation supplies the lower ones. Thus SLn​(q), and its projective quotient, are perfect in the asserted cases. The preceding simplicity argument now applies.
The two exceptions are genuine: the projective actions identify PSL2​(2) with S3​ and PSL2​(3) with A4​, of orders 6 and 12 respectively. The first contains its normal subgroup of order 3; the second contains the normal Klein four group. We have proved
PSLn​(q) is simple for n>1,(n,q)∈/{(2,2),(2,3)}.​

Sp2m​(q)={g∈GL2m​(q):gTJg=J},J=(0−Im​​Im​0​).

Expanding B(Tx,Ty) shows that the two cross terms cancel and the last term vanishes because B(v,v)=0. Thus these maps preserve B; their inverses are Tv,−c​.
We need both generation and primitivity to use the argument from part (i). For nonzero vectors v,w with B(v,w)=0, the symplectic transvection with direction w−v and parameter 1/B(v,w) sends v to w. When B(v,w)=0, choose z nonorthogonal to both and use two such steps. Hence symplectic transvections act transitively on nonzero vectors.
They generate the whole symplectic group over a finite field. Indeed first use them to make a given symplectic transformation fix e1​. Its image f′ of f1​ still satisfies B(e1​,f′)=1. A symplectic transvection with direction f1​−f′ fixes e1​ and sends f′ to f1​ if the pairing is nonzero. A nontrivial proper block through the fixed point would be a union of these stabilizer subgroup orbits, hence would have size either h or q2m−1+1. A block size divides N. But N−qh=1, so h>1 cannot divide N, and N/2<q2m−1+1<N, excluding the other size. This proves primitivity. For m=1 the projective action is the two-transitive action of PSL2​(q).
Here is a direct check of perfectness of finite symplectic groups, including the small fields where the diagonal group commutator needs replacement. If q>3, every symplectic transvection lies in an embedded SL2​(q) on a hyperbolic plane containing its direction, and that subgroup is perfect by part (i). Since the symplectic transvections generate, the whole symplectic group over a finite field is perfect.
For q=3 and m≥2, all Tv,1​ are conjugate, so their common image z generates the abelianization and satisfies 3z=0. Choose independent perpendicular vectors e,f. The four commuting symplectic transvections with directions e,f,e+f,e−f and parameter one multiply to the identity: the sum of their rank-one matrices is zero in characteristic 3. Thus also 4z=0, giving z=0.
For q=2 and m≥3, use the seven nonzero vectors of a three-dimensional isotropic subspace of a symplectic vector space. Their symplectic transvections commute, and their product is the identity. To see this explicitly, write a vector in three isotropic basis coordinates: in the sum of the outer products vvT each diagonal coefficient occurs four times and each off-diagonal coefficient twice, all zero in characteristic 2. The common abelianization image z therefore satisfies 2z=7z=0. Again the group is perfect.
The simplicity argument from part (i) now applies to the projective symplectic group over a finite field. The remaining exceptional group besides Sp2​(2) and PSp2​(3) is Sp4​(2). It is not simple: it is isomorphic to S6​. One can verify this identification without assuming it. On F24​ set
Q0​(x1​,x2​,y1​,y2​)=x1​y1​+x2​y2​,Qa​(v)=Q0​(v)+B(a,v).
(40)

The symplectic group over a finite field permutes these six forms. Differences of their parameter vectors span F24​: writing ei​,fi​ for the symplectic basis, the six parameters are
e1​+f1​, e1​+f1​+e2​, e1​+f1​+f2​, e2​+f2​, e2​+f2​+e1​, e2​+f2​+f1​.

The Grassmann graph is connected, and its distance can be determined directly. Put C=A∩B, r=dimC, and s=k−r. Choose vectors so that
A=C⊕⟨a1​,…,as​⟩,B=C⊕⟨b1​,…,bs​⟩.
(47)
The vectors of a basis of C, followed by all the ai​ and bi​, are independent: a relation would identify a vector in the two chosen complements modulo C, contradicting A∩B=C. For 0≤j≤s define
Aj​=C⊕⟨b1​,…,bj​,aj+1​,…,as​⟩.
(48)
Each successive pair has an intersection of dimension k−1. Thus A0​=A and As​=B are joined by a path of length s.
For the lower bound, if two vertices U,V are adjacent, their common vector subspace D has codimension one in each. Both dim(U∩B) and dim(V∩B) lie between dim(D∩B) and dim(D∩B)+1. Thus one edge can change the dimension of the intersection with B by at most one. It must change from r to k along a path from A to B, requiring at least k−r edges. Therefore
d(A,B)=k−dim(A∩B).​
(49)
If two ordered pairs have equal distance, their intersections have equal dimension. Construct adapted bases for each pair as above and extend both to bases of the ambient vector space. The invertible linear map between them sends the first ordered pair to the second. It preserves intersection dimensions, so it is a graph automorphism. This proves that the Grassmann graph is distance-transitive, with the required simultaneous images. Its diameter is k, because 2k≤n permits two disjoint k-dimensional vector subspaces.

A finite two-transitive group action is transitive on ordered pairs of distinct points. Equivalently, the action is transitive and a point stabilizer subgroup is transitive on the remaining points. Its rank of a transitive permutation group is two, and the orbit-stabilizer theorem gives n(n−1)∣∣G∣ for degree n. Such an action is primitive: a block containing a point and another point must, by the transitivity of the stabilizer subgroup, contain every point.
These groups illustrate two quite different sources of symmetry. The structural theorem for finite two-transitive groups says that they have either a regular elementary-abelian normal subgroup, giving the affine case, or a nonabelian simple normal subgroup, giving the almost simple case. The full classification of the second case depends on the classification of finite simple groups; the useful point here is to describe the constructions, actions, and representative families rather than treat two-transitivity as an abstract property of a group without a specified action.
In the affine case let V be a finite vector space over Fp​ and let H≤GL(V) act transitively on V∖{0}. The affine two-transitive group
G=V⋊H,(v,h):x↦v+hx,
(50)
is transitive by translations, and the stabilizer subgroup of zero is exactly H, so it is two-transitive. Conversely, if a two-transitive group has an abelian minimal normal subgroup V, normal-subgroup orbits and primitivity make V transitive. An abelian transitive faithful permutation subgroup is regular: an element fixing one point commutes with every element moving that point and hence fixes them all. Minimal normality makes V elementary abelian, since its prime-primary subgroups and its subgroup of pth powers are characteristic. Conjugation by the stabilizer subgroup acts faithfully as linear automorphisms of V, and regularity gives G=V⋊G0​. This recovers precisely the construction above.
Many examples follow at once. The group AGLd​(q) acts on the qd vectors of Fqd​; GLd​(q) sends any nonzero vector to any other by a basis map. For d≥2, the subgroup ASLd​(q) also gives a two-transitive action, since the determinant of a basis map can be adjusted on a second basis vector while retaining the prescribed first vector. The affine group with linear part Sp2m​(q) gives another family: the nonzero-vector transitivity was proved using symplectic transvections in part 4(ii). Here the additive group of Fqd​, for q=pf, is an elementary abelian p-group of rank fd.
In particular AGL1​(q) consists of x↦ax+b with a=0. Given distinct x,y and distinct u,v, the equations ax+b=u, ay+b=v have the unique solution a=(v−u)/(y−x), b=u−ax. Thus this action is sharply two-transitive, of degree q and order q(q−1). Adding field automorphisms gives AΓL1​(q), still two-transitive, with elements x↦axpi+b.
An almost simple group lies between a nonabelian simple group T and its automorphism group. One must still specify an action: a group can have several permutation representations with different degrees or different transitivity properties. The natural actions of Sn​ for n≥2 and An​ for n≥4 are the most familiar examples of a two-transitive group action. For n≥5, the simple group An​, a normal subgroup, places these groups in the almost simple case; the smaller two-transitive examples belong to the affine case. The symmetric group sends any ordered distinct pair to any other; for the alternating group one can adjust an odd map by swapping two letters outside the target pair. Thus both are two-transitive in the indicated ranges.
The projective linear examples are just as concrete. The groups PSLn​(q) and PGLn​(q) act two-transitively on
∣Pn−1(Fq​)∣=q−1qn−1​
(51)
points, by the basis and determinant adjustment argument of part 4(i). Their field-automorphism extensions are also two-transitive. On the projective line, PGL2​(q) is sharply three-transitive: three distinct points determine a unique Möbius transformation. Its degree is q+1 and its order is q(q2−1). For even q, PSL2​(q)=PGL2​(q); for odd q the special projective group has half that order and remains two-transitive, but cannot be three-transitive. In projective dimension at least two, triples of distinct points may be collinear or noncollinear, so the natural action is not three-transitive.
The projective special unitary group over a finite field PSU3​(q), for q≥3, acts on the isotropic points of a space with a nondegenerate Hermitian form of dimension three over Fq2​. Its degree and order are
q3+1,gcd(3,q+1)q3(q3+1)(q2−1)​.

The corresponding isometry sends one ordered pair to another, and a norm-one scalar on the orthogonal one-dimensional complement adjusts its determinant without moving the two lines. Thus the special projective unitary action is two-transitive.

The symplectic group over a finite field in characteristic two has a particularly illuminating pair of two-transitive actions which are not their projective-vector actions. For m≥3, Sp2m​(2) acts on the two classes of quadratic forms whose polar form is the given alternating bilinear form, distinguished by the Arf invariant of a quadratic form. Their degrees are

The stabilizer subgroup of Q, an orthogonal group over a finite field, is transitive on its nonzero isotropic vectors: extend such a vector to a hyperbolic pair and map adapted quadratic-form bases. It is therefore transitive on the other forms of the same type. This proves two-transitivity of both actions. In particular Sp6​(2) has actions of degrees 28 and 36. The organizing distinction is affine versus almost simple; the action, its degree, and its stabilizer subgroup are essential data. The explicitly verified affine, symmetric, alternating, projective, unitary, and symplectic actions already give many infinite families, while the exceptional families show why no single matrix construction covers every example.
