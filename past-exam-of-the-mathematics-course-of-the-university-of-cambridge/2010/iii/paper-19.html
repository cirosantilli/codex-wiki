We use Δ=div∇, whose spectrum on a compact manifold is nonpositive; write P=−Δ for the positive Laplace-Beltrami operator. This convention will also make the heat operator in Question 3 equal to ∂t​−Δ.

dt2d2​f(γi​(t))​t=0​=Hessg​f(ei​,ei​).

Δf(p)=i=1∑d​dt2d2​f(γi​(t))​t=0​.​

The positive-sign convention P puts a minus sign in front of this sum.

dt2d2​F(γi​(t))​0​=D2Fp​(ei​,ei​)−DFp​(p).

We prove the harmonic decomposition of homogeneous polynomials that both counts these eigenfunctions and establishes completeness. For an ambient harmonic homogeneous polynomial Hm​ of degree m and j≥1, the spherical coordinates formula gives
Δ(r2jHm​)=2j(2m+2j+d−1)r2j−2Hm​.

For d≥1 its coefficient is nonzero. Inductively assume homogeneous polynomials of degree ℓ−2 have been decomposed into sums of r2jHm​. Apply this decomposition to ΔQ for Q∈Pℓ​, and lift each summand by multiplying by r2 and dividing by the displayed nonzero coefficient. The resulting R∈r2Pℓ−2​ satisfies ΔR=ΔQ, so Q−R∈Hℓ​. The same coefficient formula shows that no nonzero element of r2Pℓ−2​ can be harmonic. Starting at degrees zero and one therefore proves the direct decomposition
Pℓ​=Hℓ​⊕r2Pℓ−2​.
(8)
Since dimPℓ​=(dℓ+d​), the multiplicity is
mℓ​=(dℓ+d​)−(dℓ+d−2​),​
(9)
where the second term is zero for ℓ<2.
On r=1, iterating the decomposition writes every polynomial restriction as a finite sum of spherical harmonics. The Stone-Weierstrass theorem makes these polynomial restrictions dense in C(Sd), since coordinate functions separate points and constants are included; they are consequently dense in L2(Sd). Integration by parts makes P symmetric on smooth functions and gives orthogonality between distinct eigenvalues. Normalizing within each finite-dimensional eigenspace gives a complete orthonormal eigenbasis Yℓa​. To check the full operator spectrum, let cℓa​=⟨f,Yℓa​⟩. For smooth f, integration by parts gives the coefficients of Pf as λℓ​cℓa​. Therefore finite harmonic partial sums approximate both f and Pf in L2, hence approximate in the graph norm. Conversely every coefficient sequence with ∑ℓ,a​λℓ2​∣cℓa​∣2<∞ is the graph norm limit of its finite harmonic sums. Thus the closure of P has exactly this operator domain and is the real diagonal self-adjoint operator with entries λℓ​=ℓ(ℓ+d−1). If z is not one of these entries, the inverse has entries (λℓ​−z)−1 and is bounded because λℓ​→∞. This proves that there are no missing spectral values. The spectrum of the Laplacian on a sphere is
σ(P)={ℓ(ℓ+d−1):ℓ=0,1,2,…},​
(10)
with the above multiplicities; for Δ, negate every eigenvalue. For example S1 has eigenvalues ℓ2, with multiplicities one at zero and two for ℓ≥1, and S2 has multiplicity 2ℓ+1. The zero-dimensional sphere consists of two isolated points and has only the zero eigenvalue, with multiplicity two.

Fix a bounded tile in Euclidean space Ω with labelled flat boundary faces. For each face label s, encode an assembly of n copies by an involutive real symmetric signed permutation matrix Ms​: a paired interface contributes a two-by-two swap, an unglued face with a Neumann boundary condition contributes +1 on the diagonal, and an unglued face with a Dirichlet boundary condition contributes −1. To prove it, restrict an eigenfunction to the tile copies and identify each copy with Ω. Along a face, let a be its vector of boundary values and b its vector of outward normal derivatives. At a glued interface the values agree and the outward derivatives are opposite. At an unglued face with a Neumann boundary condition the derivative vanishes, and at a face with a Dirichlet boundary condition the value vanishes. All three cases are encoded by
Ms​a=a,Ms​b=−b.
(12)
The intertwining identities give Ns​(Ca)=Ca and Ns​(Cb)=−Cb, so the transplanted function satisfies the second assembly's interface and boundary conditions. On each tile its Laplace-Beltrami operator is the same constant linear combination of the original Laplacians, hence it has the same eigenvalue. Across an interface, matching values and opposite normal derivatives make it a weak solution; elliptic regularity, or the allowed reflection principle, makes it a genuine smooth solution there. At corners the operator is understood by its finite-energy, Friedrichs extension; the same matching argument applies to its quadratic form domain. Applying C−1 reverses the construction. Thus dimensions of all eigenspaces agree, including multiplicities, and the two specified boundary-value spectra coincide.
The requested pair exists with explicitly specified mixed boundary conditions and polygonal boundary. Take four copies Q0​,Q1​,Q2​,Q3​ of an octagonal regular polygon in Euclidean space. We now verify the actual topology using the topology of disk tiles glued along disjoint boundary arcs. Because the gluing arcs in each tile are disjoint, the resulting surface with boundary is a thickening of its gluing graph, with one vertex for each closed disc tile and one edge for each paired arc. One can construct this homotopy equivalence by taking a small central closed disc in each tile and narrow strips to its paired arcs. What remains is a collar neighbourhood of the boundary, which retracts onto this connected system of closed discs and strips. Shrinking each central closed disc and each strip then gives the gluing graph. Equivalently, begin with a spanning tree of the gluing graph: its closed disc tiles joined along boundary arcs form one closed disc, and every remaining pair of boundary arcs adds one band. A heat parametrix is an approximate kernel with the same delta initial limit and an error under L that is sufficiently regular, or flat, at t=0 to be corrected by time convolution. The local coefficient construction requires no compactness. The global uniformly bounded convolution argument just given uses a closed compact manifold, the usual spectral-geometry setting. For noncompact or incomplete Riemannian manifolds one must specify the heat realization and justify global convergence separately; a canonical choice is the minimal kernel obtained as the increasing limit of Dirichlet kernels on a smooth relatively compact exhaustion. This gives the minimal heat semigroup rather than asserting an unqualified uniqueness statement at infinity or at a missing boundary.

Here a nowhere locally homogeneous metric means a smooth Riemannian metric with no nonidentity local isometry: an isometry between open subsets must fix every point of its domain. This is the strong local-rigidity meaning of “bumpy” used in this question, not the different convention about nondegenerate closed geodesics. Sunada's local isometry lemma states that, on a closed smooth manifold of dimension at least two, such Riemannian metrics form a residual subset of the space of smooth Riemannian metrics and are dense in its C∞ topology. In dimension one this conclusion is false: arclength coordinates give local translations for every Riemannian metric.
For maps Mm→Nn, the jet bundle of maps Jk(M,N) records the source point, the target value and all derivatives through order k. There are (km+k​) monomials of total degree at most k in m variables. Hence
dimJk(M,N)=m+n(km+k​).​
(41)
Over (x,y)∈M×N, the value is already fixed, so the fibre has dimension
n((km+k​)−1).​
(42)
In local coordinates it is the space of Taylor series coefficients of orders one through k, represented by a direct sum of symmetric powers, ⨁r=1k​Symr(Tx∗​M)⊗Ty​N. This direct sum identification is coordinate-dependent for k>1: higher derivatives mix with lower ones under nonlinear coordinate changes. Intrinsically, the truncation to order k−1 is an affine bundle modeled on Symk(Tx∗​M)⊗Ty​N. For k=1 the fibre is canonically Hom(Tx​M,Ty​N).
For the density assertion as printed, an isometry Ui​→Uj​ is a bijective Riemannian isometry. A localized volume perturbation suffices; it is not necessary to prove the stronger generic local-rigidity lemma afresh. We take the coordinate-ball basis without repetitions, and each Ui​ as the interior of its smooth closed-ball closure. Distinct such domains have a nonempty open difference in at least one direction. Indeed, if both were contained in the other's closure, their regular-open property U=intU would make them equal.
Fix any Riemannian metric g. If the two domains have unequal volumes, they cannot be isometric, so g already lies in the desired complement. Otherwise, after interchanging the two indices if necessary, choose a nonnegative nonzero smooth bump function ψ supported in Ui​∖Uj​, and set
gϵ​=e2ϵψg.
(43)
These are positive-definite smooth Riemannian metrics tending to g in the C∞ topology as ϵ→0. Their Riemannian volume forms satisfy dVgϵ​​=emϵψdVg​. The Riemannian metric and volume on Uj​ are unchanged, whereas
dϵd​volgϵ​​(Ui​)​ϵ=0​=m∫Ui​​ψdVg​>0.
(44)
For every sufficiently small ϵ>0, the volumes differ. Thus no isometry of the two closed domains exists, proving
CSij​ is dense in the space of smooth metrics.​
(45)
The volume difference is continuous in the smooth topology, so the unequal-volume Riemannian metrics even form an open dense subset of this complement. The regular-open assumption is also needed to justify this literal closed-domain assertion. As written, requiring only that each closure be a closed ball permits two distinct basis members with the same closure. For example in Rm, adjoin U=B(0,1) and V=B(0,1)∖{0} to a countable basis of a topology of coordinate balls. Both are open and both closures are the same closed ball. The identity is an isometry of those closures for every Riemannian metric, so the indicated complement is empty. Thus the conclusion needs distinct closures, as in the usual basis of genuine coordinate balls used above. The no-repetitions convention is necessary as well: if the indexed basis repeats a domain, its identity map is always an isometry and the asserted complement for those two indices is empty. This volume argument concerns isometries onto the specified whole domains; the stronger assertion excluding arbitrary local isometries is the separate Sunada lemma stated above.

Sunada's theorem states that if a finite group T acts isometrically on a closed Riemannian manifold X, and two Gassmann equivalent subgroups H1​,H2​ act freely, then H1​\X and H2​\X are isospectral for the function Laplace-Beltrami operator. Gassmann equivalence means ∣H1​∩C∣=∣H2​∩C∣ for every conjugacy class C of T. For each Laplace-Beltrami operator eigenspace Eλ​, its invariant subspace has dimension
dimEλHi​​=∣Hi​∣1​∑h∈Hi​​tr(h∣Eλ​).
(46)
The character of a representation is a class function, so the two multiplicities agree. A necessary condition for nonisometry is that the subgroups are not conjugate in T: conjugation by an ambient deck transformation would induce an isometry. This is not sufficient when the common covering has additional geometric symmetries.
Use the oriented hyperbolic triangle group
Γ=⟨a,b:a3=b3=(ab)6=1⟩
(47)
and the epimorphism ρ:Γ→T given by a↦s, b↦d. Its base orbifold is the doubled hyperbolic triangle with angles π/3,π/3,π/6, having signature (0;3,3,6). Let K=kerρ and Ki​=ρ−1(Hi​).
The groups Ki​ are torsion free. Indeed, torsion elements in an oriented triangle group are conjugate to nontrivial powers of its three vertex rotations. Such powers map either to elements of order three, which cannot lie in a subgroup of order 96/12=8, or to the order-two cube z=(sd)3. The latter is central and absent from both Hi​, so it is absent from every conjugate as well. Thus the degree-twelve orbifold covers
Ni​=Ki​\H2
(48)
are smooth, closed, connected orientable surfaces. The common cover X=K\H2 is smooth too; Hi​ act freely on it. Orbifold Euler characteristic gives
χ(Ni​)=12(31​+31​+61​−1)=−2,genus(Ni​)=2.​
(49)
For example, this formula follows by triangulating the base and weighting a vertex of order r by 1/r before multiplying by the cover degree. Equivalently, each doubled triangle has hyperbolic area π/3, so Ni​ has area 4π and Gauss-Bonnet theorem gives the same genus.
There is one extra issue in obtaining the same hyperbolic limit on a single marked surface: the two limiting hyperbolic covers must themselves be isometric. Nonconjugacy and the displayed element orders alone do not prove this. An explicit realization of the supplied example resolves the issue. The group seed is recorded in the original genus-two construction; the following coset actions and reflection identities give a direct verification. Label the twelve sheets so that the two right-coset actions of s,d are
s1​d1​s2​d2​​=(1 3 6)(2 11 7)(4 8 9)(5 10 12),=(1 12 8)(2 3 7)(4 5 10)(6 9 11),=(1 6 12)(2 8 11)(3 7 10)(4 5 9),=(1 6 10)(2 8 9)(3 5 11)(4 7 12).​
(50)
These are concrete permutation data for an order-96 realization with the required two subgroups. The permutation
P=(1 3 10 2)(4 9)(5 8)(6 7)(11 12)
(51)
satisfies, by direct substitution,
Ps1​=s2−1​P,Pd1​=d2−1​P.
(52)
Reflection of the doubled triangle reverses both vertex rotations. Apply this reflection on every fundamental domain and relabel sheets by P. The two identities ensure that identified edges go to identified edges, so this descends to a hyperbolic isometry F0​:N1​→N2​. Fix N=N1​, let g be its hyperbolic metric, and use F0​ to mark N2​ by the same smooth surface. Thus both pulled-back limiting Riemannian metrics are exactly g. The explicit reversed-action check is what makes the common limit justified, rather than treating it as a consequence of Sunada's theorem.
Choose smooth orbifold Riemannian metrics hk​ on the base converging to its hyperbolic metric h0​, with no nonidentity local isometry on the regular part. The density assertion in Sunada's local isometry lemma applies in orbifold charts as well: use equivariant perturbations at the finitely many cone points and ordinary local perturbations elsewhere. Smoothness here is orbifold smoothness, so the lifted Riemannian metrics on X and the two smooth covers are ordinary smooth Riemannian metrics. Such choices can be made in successively smaller smooth neighbourhoods of h0​; cone orders and the topological coverings stay fixed.
For area separation of convergent Sunada families, adjust each hk​ by a constant positive factor so that
area(hk​)=area(h0​)(1+2−k).
(53)
Explicitly, multiply the initially chosen Riemannian metric by the ratio of the desired area to its actual area. In dimension two this ratio is exactly the area multiplier. The ratios tend to one, so smooth convergence persists, and constant scaling preserves the absence of local isometries. Lift these adjusted Riemannian metrics to Ni​ and pull them back by the fixed markings to obtain gik​, for i=1,2. The three required properties follow below.

For each fixed k, the two quotient Riemannian metrics are not isometric. If an isometry F:N1​→N2​ existed, choose a small open set avoiding the finite preimages of cone points and their inverse images under F. Composing F with the local inverse of the first base projection and the second base projection gives a local isometry of the base Riemannian metric hk​. Local rigidity forces this map to be the identity. Hence p2​F=p1​ on a dense open set, and by continuity everywhere.
The alleged isometry would therefore be an isomorphism of the two orbifold covers over the same base. Covering-space classification makes K1​,K2​ conjugate in Γ, and applying ρ makes H1​,H2​ conjugate in T, contrary to the supplied hypothesis. Thus g1k​,g2k​ are nonisometric.
For k=ℓ, the surface areas are different:
area(N,gik​)=4π(1+2−k).​
(54)
Area is preserved by isometries, so no surface from the kth pair is isometric to a surface from the ℓth pair. Together these arguments prove the full pairwise nonisometry assertion.
