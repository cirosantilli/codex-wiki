The relevant Banach-Alaoglu theorem says that for 1<p<∞ the closed bounded balls of Lp(Rn) are compact in the weak topology. In particular, every bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence whose limit has norm no larger than the common bound. Applying the preceding duality of Lp spaces also identifies this as weak-star compactness of Lp=(Lq)∗; its weak and weak-star test spaces are both Lq.
Here is a direct proof of both compactness and the subsequence assertion. Let ∥fj​∥p​≤M and choose a countable dense set {gm​} in the separable Lp space Lq. Each scalar sequence ∫fj​gm​ is bounded. Successive subsequence selection followed by the diagonal subsequence argument gives fjk​​ for which these pairings converge for every m. For arbitrary g∈Lq,
​∫(fjk​​−fjl​​)g​≤2M∥g−gm​∥q​+​∫(fjk​​−fjl​​)gm​​.
(4)
First approximate g by gm​ and then take k,l large. This proves convergence of every pairing, and the limit Λ(g)=limk​∫fjk​​g is linear with ∣Λ(g)∣≤M∥g∥q​. Apply the already proved duality of Lp spaces with q in place of p: Λ(g)=∫fg for an f∈Lp with ∥f∥p​≤M. Thus
fjk​​⇀f in Lp,∥f∥p​≤M.​
(5)
To obtain compactness rather than only a sequential statement, on the radius-M ball define
d(f,h)=∑m=1∞​2−m1+∣∫(f−h)gm​∣∣∫(f−h)gm​∣​.
(6)
Density and Hölder's inequality show that this is a metric space distance and that its topology is exactly the weak topology on this ball: every remaining test pairing is uniformly approximated there by a dense-set pairing. The subsequence argument proves sequential compactness for this metric. A sequentially compact metric space is compact: otherwise either an infinite separated sequence contradicts total boundedness or a Cauchy sequence without a limit contradicts sequential compactness; completeness and total boundedness give compactness by successive finite coverings. This completes the proof. The same argument is the Sequential Banach-Alaoglu theorem for a separable predual.

The assertion that fails is weak compactness of the L1 unit ball; the abstract Banach-Alaoglu theorem for dual balls never fails. Consider the concentration prevents weak compactness in L1 example
fj​(x)=jn1[0,1/j]n​(x),fj​≥0,∥fj​∥1​=1.​
(7)
Suppose a subsequence converged weakly in L1 to f. For each m, all sufficiently late supports lie inside B1/m​(0). Testing against every bounded measurable function supported outside that ball shows that f=0 almost everywhere there. Taking the countable union over m proves f=0 almost everywhere on Rn. But testing against the bounded constant function one gives ∫f=limj​∫fj​=1, a contradiction.
The same argument applies to every subnet whose indices tend to infinity, so the sequence has no weak cluster point in the ball and the ball is not weakly compact. It converges against smooth compactly supported tests to a point mass, which is not an L1 density. Thus boundedness in L1 does not imply the weak compactness proved in part (b); concentration is the obstruction.

As usual, the bounded set is assumed Lebesgue measurable and an Lq exponent is positive; the conventional Banach-space assertion concerns 1≤q<2∗=2n/(n−2). No boundary regularity of Ω is needed. Choose a smooth compactly supported cutoff η equal to one on a ball containing Ω. Weak convergence and the Uniform boundedness principle give a common H1 bound for fj​, hence also for gj​=ηfj​. These functions have a common compact support.
We prove their local L2 compactness directly. Translation and the fundamental theorem along line segments give, by density from smooth functions,
∥gj​(⋅−h)−gj​∥2​≤∣h∣∥∇gj​∥2​.
(19)
For a compactly supported mollifier ρε​, integration of this estimate gives
∥gj​∗ρε​−gj​∥2​≤Cε∥∇gj​∥2​≤C′ε.
(20)
At fixed ε, Hölder's inequality bounds ∥gj​∗ρε​∥∞​ and ∥∇(gj​∗ρε​)∥∞​ uniformly by the L2 norm of the corresponding smoothing kernels. Their supports lie in one fixed compact set, so the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem makes this smoothed family precompact in L2. The uniform O(ε) approximation makes the original family totally bounded in L2: choose a finite net for the smoothed family and add the approximation error. Completeness gives relative compactness.
Every strongly convergent subsequence of gj​ has limit ηf, since multiplication by η preserves weak L2 convergence. Consequently the whole sequence converges strongly: otherwise a subsequence with distance at least a fixed positive number would have a further convergent subsequence and contradict that unique limit. In particular ∥χΩ​(fj​−f)∥2​→0.
The critical Sobolev inequality supplies a uniform bound in L2∗. For clarity, this estimate follows from part 2(a), not from compactness at the critical exponent. For a compactly supported smooth v, integrating by parts against the vector field (x−y)/∣x−y∣n gives
∣v(x)∣≤Cn​∫∣x−y∣1−n∣∇v(y)∣dy.
(21)
Its distributional divergence is the sphere-area multiple of a point mass; applying the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality with α=1,p=2 yields ∥v∥2∗​≤C∥∇v∥2​. Density extends this to H1(Rn).
For 2<q<2∗ choose 0<θ<1 with 1/q=θ/2+(1−θ)/2∗. Hölder's inequality gives
∥χΩ​(fj​−f)∥q​≤∥χΩ​(fj​−f)∥2θ​∥χΩ​(fj​−f)∥2∗1−θ​⟶0.
(22)
For 1≤q<2, use ∥v∥Lq(Ω)​≤∣Ω∣1/q−1/2∥v∥L2(Ω)​. The same integral estimate also works for 0<q<1 if Lq is understood with its usual quasi-norm. Thus χΩ​fj​→χΩ​f strongly for every positive q<2∗. We have never asserted that multiplying by the possibly nonsmooth indicator preserves H1.

For a bounded connected domain with the interior cone property, and 1≤p<n, the mean-zero form of the Poincaré inequality is
∥v−vΩ​∥Lp(Ω)​≤CΩ,p​∥∇v∥Lp(Ω)​,vΩ​=∣Ω∣1​∫Ω​v.​
(23)
No zero boundary trace is imposed. Subtracting the mean is essential because nonzero constants have zero gradient.
Suppose the estimate failed. Subtract means and normalize to obtain vj​∈W1,p(Ω) with
∫Ω​vj​=0,∥vj​∥p​=1,∥∇vj​∥p​⟶0.
(24)
This sequence is bounded in the Sobolev space W1,p. The allowed Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorem on cone domains provides a subsequence converging strongly in Lp(Ω) to v. Its mean is zero and its norm is one. For any compactly supported smooth test function ϕ,
∫v∂i​ϕ=limj​∫vj​∂i​ϕ=−limj​∫(∂i​vj​)ϕ=0.
(25)
Thus every weak derivative of v vanishes. To show this implies constancy, mollify on any ball compactly contained in Ω. The resulting smooth function has zero gradient and is constant on the smaller ball. Passing to its Lp limit makes v constant almost everywhere on that ball. Overlapping balls have the same constant, and connectedness connects any two points by a chain of such balls. Hence v is constant almost everywhere throughout Ω. Its zero mean makes it zero, contradicting norm one. This proves the stated Poincaré inequality. The frequently used stronger Sobolev-Poincare formulation also follows: with p∗=np/(n−p), the cone-domain Sobolev embedding theorem gives ∥w∥p∗​≤CS​(∥w∥p​+∥∇w∥p​). Insert w=v−vΩ​ and the estimate just proved to obtain
∥v−vΩ​∥Lp∗(Ω)​≤CS​(1+CΩ,p​)∥∇v∥Lp(Ω)​.​
(26)
Thus both the same-exponent and critical-exponent mean-zero versions are covered. Connectedness cannot be dropped without subtracting a separate mean on every component. The usual meaning of the stated range is 1≤p<n; W1,p with p<1 is not part of this Sobolev-space formulation.

The real part paired with ϕ is

For a real locally integrable function, the definitions are distributional:
f subharmonicf superharmonicf harmonic​⟺∫Ω​fΔϕ≥0(0≤ϕ∈Cc∞​(Ω)),⟺∫Ω​fΔϕ≤0(0≤ϕ∈Cc∞​(Ω)),⟺∫Ω​fΔϕ=0(ϕ∈Cc∞​(Ω)).​​
(36)
Thus the Laplacian is respectively a positive distribution, a negative distribution, or zero. A superharmonic function is the negative of a subharmonic function; being both is equivalent to being harmonic. For a C2 function these conditions are Δf≥0, Δf≤0, and Δf=0 pointwise.
An Lloc1​ equivalence class does not itself specify point values. For the maximum principle use the canonical representative of a subharmonic distribution
f​(x)=limr↓0​∣Br​∣1​∫Br​(x)​f.
(37)
These normalized integrals are ball averages. To justify this construction, first mollify f: its smooth local regularizations satisfy Δfε​≥0. For any smooth subharmonic function, the spherical mean mx​(r) obeys
mx′​(r)=∣Sn−1∣rn−11​∫Br​(x)​Δf≥0.
(38)
This follows by differentiating the spherical integral and applying the divergence theorem. Its ball mean also increases with radius: its derivative is n/r times the spherical mean minus the ball mean. Local L1 convergence of the regularizations passes the monotonicity of the ball means to f. The decreasing-radius limit therefore exists, possibly as minus infinity at an exceptional point, and equals f almost everywhere by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem. It is upper semicontinuous as the local infimum of continuous ball averages and satisfies
f​(x)≤∣Br​∣1​∫Br​(x)​f​.
(39)
For a superharmonic function use the corresponding lower semicontinuous representative and reverse the inequality.
For a distributionally harmonic function, the smooth regularizations are harmonic and have constant spherical means. A radial mollifier ρδ​ of integral one therefore reproduces them by averaging these means. At fixed interior radius δ, pass to the local L1 limit to obtain f=f∗ρδ​ almost everywhere on a smaller region. The right side is smooth and has zero distributional Laplacian, giving the smooth harmonic representative. This proves the relevant Weyl lemma. Changing a value at a single point can spoil a pointwise maximum principle, so the representative convention is essential.

The strong maximum principle for subharmonic functions is: on a connected open domain, the canonical upper semicontinuous representative of a subharmonic function cannot attain a finite global maximum at an interior point unless it is constant. A local maximum instead forces constancy on an appropriate neighbourhood; it alone does not assert constancy on the whole domain unless that value is a global maximum.
Suppose u≤M on Ω and u(x0​)=M. For every sufficiently small ball centred at x0​, the subharmonic mean inequality gives
M=u(x0​)≤∣Br​∣1​∫Br​(x0​)​u≤M.
(40)
The nonnegative function M−u has integral zero, so u=M almost everywhere on that ball. Its canonical representative is then M at every point of the ball, by taking still smaller local averages.
The maximum set E={x:u(x)=M} is consequently open by the same argument at each of its points. It is closed relative to Ω, because upper semicontinuity makes its complement {u<M} open. It is nonempty, and connectedness gives E=Ω. Therefore u≡M. This proves the principle, including its representative and connectedness hypotheses. Arbitrary representatives do not satisfy it: the function equal to zero almost everywhere but assigned value one at the origin has zero distributional Laplacian and an artificial pointwise maximum.

The local Harnack inequality for harmonic functions states that if u≥0 is harmonic on a neighbourhood of B4r​(a)​, then
Br​(a)sup​u≤3nBr​(a)inf​u.​
(41)
More generally, for any compact K in a connected open domain Ω, there is C=C(K,Ω,n) such that supK​u≤CinfK​u for every nonnegative harmonic u on Ω. Positivity is essential; the assertion is not for arbitrary sign-changing functions.
First prove the mean value property for harmonic functions. For a smooth harmonic function the spherical mean has derivative
mx′​(t)=∣Sn−1∣tn−11​∫Bt​(x)​Δu=0,
(42)
so it equals u(x); integration in the radius gives the ball mean property. The preceding Weyl lemma supplies smoothness if harmonicity was initially distributional.
For x,z∈Br​(a) the inclusion Br​(x)⊂B3r​(z)⊂B4r​(a) and nonnegativity give
u(x)=∣Br​∣1​∫Br​(x)​u≤∣Br​∣1​∫B3r​(z)​u=3nu(z).
(43)
Taking the supremum over x and the infimum over z proves the local estimate directly. To obtain the compact-set form, connect centres of a finite ball cover of K to a fixed interior point by paths in Ω. The compact union of those paths stays a positive distance from the complement of Ω. Subdivide the paths into finitely many steps small enough that the local estimate applies at consecutive points. Multiply the finitely many comparison constants, first from one point of K to the reference point and then to any other point of K. This gives a constant independent of u. In particular a nonnegative harmonic function is either everywhere positive or identically zero on each connected component.

Fix a compact K⊂Ω and choose δ>0 such that a closed 3δ-neighbourhood of K is compactly contained in Ω. The hypothesis gives a common bound M on that neighbourhood. Choose a smooth radial mollifier ρδ​ supported in Bδ​(0) with integral one. The mean value property for harmonic functions, integrated against its radial weights, gives
uj​(x)=∫ρδ​(x−y)uj​(y)dy
(44)
in a neighbourhood of K. Differentiating this fixed-radius convolution gives the uniform interior estimate
∣∇uj​(x)∣≤M∥∇ρδ​∥1​≤Cρ​M/δ.
(45)
Thus the sequence is equicontinuous there; taking its pointwise limit gives the same local modulus of continuity for u. A finite small-ball cover of K now proves uniform convergence directly: choose the cover so both uj​ and u vary by less than ε inside each ball, then use convergence at its finitely many centres to make ∣uj​−u∣<ε there. The triangle inequality gives supK​∣uj​−u∣<3ε for large j.
Finally, pass to the limit in the reproducing convolution identity on smaller compact sets. It yields u=u∗ρδ​, so u is smooth locally. Also, for each compactly supported smooth test function ϕ, uniform convergence on its support gives
∫Ω​uΔϕ=limj​∫Ω​uj​Δϕ=0.
(46)
Hence its smooth Laplacian is zero. We have proved uj​→u uniformly on every compact subset and u is harmonic. Using the continuous representatives in the pointwise hypothesis makes this conclusion pointwise, rather than only almost everywhere. This is compact convergence of locally bounded harmonic functions.
