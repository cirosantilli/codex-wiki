The general adjoint functor theorem says that, for a functor U:A→B between locally small categories, with A a complete category, U has a left adjoint if and only if it preserves small limits and satisfies the solution-set condition. The solution-set condition means that for each B∈B there is a set of arrows bi​:B→UAi​ such that every b:B→UA factors as U(a)bi​ for some i and some a:Ai​→A.
Here is the smallness argument behind the theorem. A complete category that is locally small and has a weakly initial set (Wi​) has an initial object. Form the product in a category W=∏i​Wi​. It is weakly initial, since a projection W→Wi​ followed by a chosen arrow Wi​→X gives an arrow W→X. Take the simultaneous equalizer e:E→W of all endomorphisms of W with 1W​; the family is a set by local smallness. The object E is also weakly initial. For parallel arrows a,b:E⇉X, take their equalizer j:Y→E and a map t:W→Y. Since ejt is an endomorphism of W, the defining property of e gives ejte=e. Cancellation of the monomorphism e gives jte=1E​. Thus the monomorphism j has a right inverse and is an isomorphism, so a=b. Existence of arrows out of E was already established, proving the initial-object lemma for complete categories with a weakly initial set.
If U preserves small categorical limits, the comma category (B↓U) is complete: take the limiting object in A, and use preservation to assemble the arrows from B. It is locally small, and the solution-set condition provides a weakly initial set. Its initial object is an arrow ηB​:B→ULB with a natural bijection
A(LB,A)≅B(B,UA).
(1)
For a morphism v:B→B′, define Lv by U(Lv)ηB​=ηB′​v. Uniqueness proves functoriality and naturality, giving a left adjoint L⊣U. Conversely, a right adjoint preserves categorical limits, because applying the adjunction converts a limiting cone over a diagram into the corresponding limiting cone of hom-sets. Its adjunction unit at B is a singleton solution set. This proves both directions of the general adjoint functor theorem.
Apply it to the inclusion J:KHaus↪Top, where KHaus is the full subcategory of compact Hausdorff spaces. A small product in a category of compact Hausdorff spaces is again compact Hausdorff by the Tychonoff theorem. An equalizer of two continuous functions into a Hausdorff space is closed, hence compact Hausdorff. Products and equalizers construct all small categorical limits, and the inclusion preserves these constructions. Both categories are locally small.
Fix a topological space X and a continuous function f:X→K into a compact Hausdorff space. The closure Kf​=f(X)​ is compact Hausdorff and has a dense subset of cardinality at most ∣X∣. The permitted cardinal estimate gives
∣Kf​∣≤22∣X∣.
(2)
For X=∅, use Kf​=∅. Choose a set of representatives of all compact Hausdorff topologies on underlying sets of cardinality at most this bound, and all continuous functions from X to those representatives. Every f factors through one of them, using the inclusion Kf​↪K. This is the solution-set condition. Consequently the inclusion of compact Hausdorff spaces has a left adjoint, the compact Hausdorff reflection:
KHaus(RX,K)≅Top(X,JK).​
(3)
The universal arrow X→RX has dense image: factoring it through its closed image and applying uniqueness supplies an inverse to that image inclusion. It need not be an embedding when X is not sufficiently separated; the assertion is a compact Hausdorff reflection, rather than a claim that every topological space embeds in a compact Hausdorff space.
For completeness, the alternative Special adjoint functor theorem can be proved from the same initial-object argument. Its limit form assumes that A is complete, locally small and well-powered, with a small cogenerating family (Qi​)i∈I​; then U:A→B, with B locally small, has a left adjoint precisely when it preserves small limits. To prove sufficiency, start with b:B→UA and intersect all subobjects M↪A through which b factors after applying U. Well-poweredness makes this a small intersection of subobjects, and preservation of limits gives a smallest supporting object (M,bM​). For maps s,t:M⇉Qi​, equality U(s)bM​=U(t)bM​ makes their equalizer another supporting subobject. Minimality forces that equalizer to be invertible, so s=t. Therefore
A(M,Qi​)↪B(B,UQi​),s⟼U(s)bM​
(4)
is an injective function. The evaluation embedding into cogenerator products embeds M in ∏i​QiSi​​, where Si​ is the realized subset of the fixed set B(B,UQi​). There are only a set of choices of these subsets, only a set of subobjects of each resulting product, and only a set of arrows from B to each U-image. They give a solution set, so the already proved general adjoint functor theorem applies. Necessity is again preservation of limits by a right adjoint. For KHaus, monomorphisms are embeddings onto closed subspaces, and [0,1] is a coseparator because continuous functions to it separate points. Thus the Special adjoint functor theorem gives the same reflection directly.

The Yoneda embedding is an exact representation of a category, but does not by itself reduce the subject to the study of arbitrary functor categories. There are both a size issue and a structure issue.
Let A be a locally small category and set yA=A(−,A). For a categorical presheaf P:Aop→Set, a natural transformation θ:yA→P is determined by x=θA​(1A​). Indeed, naturality along f:B→A forces
θB​(f)=P(f)(x).
(5)
Conversely this formula defines a natural transformation for each x∈P(A), since functoriality gives naturality along every g:C→B. Taking P=yA′ gives
Nat(yA,yA′)≅A(A,A′).​
(6)
Thus the Yoneda embedding is full and faithful, and A is equivalent to its full subcategory of representable presheaves. The indexing category in the usual version is Aop, not necessarily a small category chosen independently of A.
Local smallness makes the values A(B,A) sets; it does not make the collection of objects a set. For a large indexing category, the collection of natural transformations between arbitrary set-valued functors can itself be a proper class. For example, take the discrete category on the class of ordinals and the constant two-element functor. Its endomorphisms include an independently chosen function {0,1}→{0,1} at each ordinal. A small functor category cannot encode this collection without changing universes. The Yoneda lemma remains meaningful when the source is representable because the displayed bijection identifies its natural transformations with a set. It does not make the entire large functor category locally small.
Even for a small category, the representable image is usually a very special part of its presheaf category. If A is the terminal category, its presheaf category is Set, but its representable image consists only of a singleton. The presheaf category has all small categorical limits and colimits, whereas an arbitrary full subcategory need have almost none. The Yoneda embedding preserves existing limits because representable functors preserve limits. It generally does not preserve colimits. For example, the coproduct of two singleton sets in category of finite sets is a two-element set, but evaluating the proposed presheaf coproduct at a two-element set gives
∣(y1⨿y1)(2)∣=2,∣y(1⨿1)(2)∣=4.
(7)
The pointwise coproduct therefore leaves the representable image. Identifying which presheaves are representable, and which ambient constructions return to the original category, is precisely substantive category theory.
The representation is nonetheless powerful. The Yoneda lemma replaces a morphism by all its probes, converts a universal property into a representation of a functor, and makes the presheaf category a natural place to add formal colimits. A small presheaf category is the free free cocompletion under small colimits: every presheaf is a colimit of representables indexed by its category of elements. But this is a controlled enlargement of the category, not an identification of its original objects with all functors. The stronger conclusion in the proposed assertion therefore does not follow from the full-faithfulness statement.

A balanced category is one in which every morphism that is both a monomorphism and an epimorphism is an isomorphism. A strong monomorphism m:A→B is a monomorphism right orthogonal to all epimorphisms: whenever e:X→Y is epic and a:X→A, b:Y→B satisfy ma=be, there is a unique d:Y→A with de=a and md=b. A regular monomorphism is an equalizer of some parallel pair of morphisms. Its equalizer property supplies the indicated lift after epic cancellation, so every regular monomorphism is strong.
If every monomorphism is strong and m:A→B is also epic, apply strongness to the square whose left and right sides are both m, with horizontal arrows 1A​ and 1B​. The lift d:B→A satisfies dm=1A​ and md=1B​. Thus m is invertible and the category is balanced.
Conversely, suppose the category is balanced and has pullbacks in a category. Given the lifting square above, form P=A×B​Y with projections p:P→Y, q:P→A. The pair (a,e) gives h:X→P with ph=e, qh=a. The pullback of the monomorphism m is monic, so p is monic. It is also epic: rp=sp implies re=se, hence r=s because e is epic. Balancedness makes p invertible. Now d=qp−1 satisfies both required equations, and monicity of m proves uniqueness. Hence in a balanced category with pullbacks, every monomorphism is strong.
For the finite example, choose a strong monomorphism m:A→B that is not regular. It is not an isomorphism, since an isomorphism is the equalizer of an identical pair. It is not epic either, since an epic strong monomorphism is invertible by the first argument. Consequently there are distinct arrows r,s:B⇉D with rm=sm. Since m is not the equalizer of this pair, there is a map h:X→B with rh=sh that does not factor through m. Monicity rules out failure of uniqueness, so failure of existence is the only possible failure of its equalizer property.
Define C0​ to have four formally distinct objects a,b,x,d and exactly the following six nonidentity arrows:
m0​:a→b,h0​:x→b,r0​,s0​:b⇉d,k0​:a→d,l0​:x→d.
(8)
Their only nontrivial composites are
r0​m0​=s0​m0​=k0​,r0​h0​=s0​h0​=l0​.
(9)
There are no longer composable strings of nonidentity arrows, so these rules define an associative category. Sending a,b,x,d to A,B,X,D, respectively, and the six arrows to m,h,r,s,rm,rh, defines a functor. It is a faithful functor: every hom-set has at most one arrow except C0​(b,d)={r0​,s0​}, and their images are distinct. Faithfulness does not require the four image objects to be distinct.
The map m0​ is monic, by direct inspection of arrows into a. Neither m0​ nor h0​ is epic, since the distinct pair r0​,s0​ agrees after either. Every other nonidentity arrow has target d and is epic, because there is only one arrow out of d. A lifting square against m0​ whose left side is one of these epimorphisms would require an arrow d→b, which does not exist. Squares whose left side is an identity have the evident unique lift. Thus m0​ is a strong monomorphism.
It is not a regular monomorphism. The only distinct parallel pair out of b is r0​,s0​, and h0​ equalizes it but has no factorization through m0​, since there is no arrow x→a. An identical pair has 1b​ as its equalizer and cannot have the noninvertible m0​ as its equalizer. Therefore
C0​ has four objects, six nonidentity arrows, and a strong nonregular monomorphism.​
(10)

Let Un​:Cn+1​→Cn​ forget the last partial unary operation. For an object A of the nested partial unary operation category Cn​, set
Sn​(A)={x∈A:αn​(x) is defined and αn​(x)=x}.
(15)
The nesting rule implies that every earlier operation is defined and fixes such an x. To construct a free extension of nested partial unary operations, adjoin a disjoint infinite chain for each x∈Sn​(A):
Ln​A=A⨿{(x,k):x∈Sn​(A), k∈N}.
(16)
Keep the old operations on A. On each new chain put α1​(x,k)=(x,k+1); every old operation αi​ with 2≤i≤n is undefined there, because the first operation has no fixed points there. Define the new operation only on Sn​(A), by αn+1​(x)=(x,0). These are exactly the fixed points of the old top operation, so the required domain rule is satisfied. The inclusion A↪Un​Ln​A preserves all old defined operations.
For a morphism f:A→Un​B, preservation of the old operations makes f(x)∈Sn​(B) whenever x∈Sn​(A). Any extension to a morphism f​:Ln​A→B must have
f​(x,k)=β1k​(βn+1​(f(x))),f​∣A​=f.
(17)
The operation βn+1​ is defined at f(x), and β1​ is total, so this formula exists. It preserves the new operation and the chain operation; there are no other defined operations on new points to check. It therefore gives the unique extension. Naturality and functoriality follow from uniqueness. Thus
Cn+1​(Ln​A,B)≅Cn​(A,Un​B),Ln​⊣Un​.​
(18)
Notice the useful extra fact: the new top operation has no fixed points, since every value (x,0) is new and every input x is old. Hence freely adding any further operations adds no more points; those operations have empty domains.

Put C0​=Set. The initial free construction L0​:Set→C1​ sends A to A×N with α1​(a,k)=(a,k+1). A function f:A→UB extends uniquely by (a,k)↦β1k​(f(a)). Its induced monad is T0​A=A×N, with unit a↦(a,0) and multiplication (a,k,l)↦(a,k+l). An algebra for a monad is determined by the action of 1∈N, so its Eilenberg-Moore category is C1​. Thus this first free–forgetful adjunction is a monadic adjunction without needing the given assumption for n=0.
The monadic length counts successive Eilenberg-Moore comparison functor steps until the comparison becomes an equivalence. Fix 0≤m<n. For m≥1, the free construction in the preceding part adds the (m+1)st operation with no fixed points. For m=0, the free first operation also has no fixed points. Consequently the left adjoint Fn,m​ to the composite forgetful functor Un,m​:Cn​→Cm​ is obtained by the one-step free construction, followed by empty higher operations. In particular, its underlying object of Cm​, unit and multiplication are exactly those of the one-step adjunction:
Un,m​Fn,m​=Um​Lm​=Tm​
(19)
as monads, not merely as object functions. The multiplication agrees because both counits evaluate the same first new operation and the same chains; no later operation has a value on a free object.
By the assumed one-step monadic adjunction property, or the explicit argument above when m=0, the Eilenberg-Moore category CmTm​​ is equivalent to Cm+1​. Under this equivalence the comparison Cn​→CmTm​​ is precisely Un,m+1​. Indeed, its algebra action evaluates each newly adjoined chain using α1​, and evaluates the first new value using αm+1​; it retains that operation and ignores all higher ones. The comparison has a left adjoint by the same explicit free extension. Thus the whole monadic tower for nested partial unary operations is
Set=C0​,C1​,…,Cn​,
(20)
with the remaining comparison at stage m equal to Un,m​, and stage n the identity.
It does not terminate earlier. If m<n, take the same underlying two-element set in two objects of Cn​, making every operation up to m the identity. In the first object make all remaining operations identities; in the second make αm+1​ exchange the two elements, and leave all subsequent operations undefined. Both satisfy the nesting rule. The identity function is a morphism after forgetting to Cm​, but is not a morphism between the original objects. Thus Un,m​ is not a full functor, and cannot be an equivalence. Therefore
monadic length(Cn​→Set)=n.​
(21)

Let F:C→D be a final functor, and let λc​:GFc→V be a cocone under a diagram GF. For d∈D, choose an object (c,a:d→Fc) of the nonempty comma category (d↓F) and define
λˉd​=λc​G(a):Gd→V.
(22)
If t:(c,a)→(c′,a′) is a morphism in that comma category, then Fta=a′, so the cocone equation gives λc​G(a)=λc′​G(Ft)G(a)=λc′​G(a′). Equality also holds along a reversed arrow, and hence along any zigzag. Connectedness makes the definition independent of the chosen object. For v:d→d′, use (c,a:d′→Fc) and (c,av:d→Fc) to obtain λˉd​=λˉd′​Gv. Thus λˉ is a cocone under G. Taking (c,1Fc​) shows that it extends λ.
Every extension must satisfy λˉd​=λc​G(a) by its cocone equation, proving uniqueness. The construction commutes with postcomposition by a morphism V→V′. Hence restriction gives a natural bijection between the sets of cocones of G and GF with any fixed vertex. A universal cocone for GF therefore extends to a universal cocone for G. In particular,
colimD​G≅colimC​GF,​
(23)
whenever the latter exists, and existence of all colimits of shape C implies existence of all colimits of shape D.
For the factorization of an arbitrary functor H:C→E, let
P(e)=π0​(e↓H),
(24)
the set of connected components of a category, where components are defined by finite zigzags. Precomposition by v:e→e′ gives a functor (e′↓H)→(e↓H) and therefore a map P(v):P(e′)→P(e). Identity and composition laws make P a categorical presheaf. This is a set-valued construction when the relevant comma categories are small or have only a set of components, as in the usual small-category convention for this factorization.
Take D to be the category of elements of this presheaf. Its objects are pairs (e,S) with S∈P(e); a morphism (e,S)→(e′,S′) is an arrow v:e→e′ satisfying P(v)(S′)=S. Define G:D→E by forgetting S. Given v:e→e′ and an object (e′,S′), the unique lift into that object has source (e,P(v)(S′)) and underlying arrow v. This proves that G is a discrete fibration.
Define F(c)=(Hc,[(c,1Hc​)]). For t:c→c′, take F(t)=Ht. The arrow t in (Hc↓H) joins (c,1Hc​) to (c′,Ht), so the required component equation holds. It follows that F is a functor and GF=H.
Finally, an object of ((e,S)↓F) is a pair (c,v:e→Hc) whose component in (e↓H) is S. Its morphisms are exactly the morphisms of that comma category between objects in S. Consequently ((e,S)↓F) is precisely the full connected component S, and is nonempty and connected. Thus F is final, and every such functor factors as a final functor followed by a discrete fibration.

A regular category has finite limits, coequalizers of kernel pairs, and regular epimorphisms stable under pullback. Equivalently, every morphism factors as a regular epimorphism followed by a monomorphism, and these factorizations are stable under pullback. A regular functor preserves finite limits and regular epimorphisms, hence also preserves these image factorizations.
For this question, the capital regular category convention is the one in which every well-supported object is a well-pointed object in a category. Here an object A is well-supported if A→1 is a regular epimorphism. It is well-pointed if a subobject m:B↪A through which every point 1→A factors must be the whole object. This convention is documented in Definitions 1.1–1.3. It imposes no such point-detection requirement on objects whose support is a proper subobject of 1. In particular, it should not be replaced here by the stronger assertion that A(1,−) reflects every isomorphism.
First prove two facts about a capital regular category A. Every well-supported object A has a point. Otherwise A×A is well-supported and has no points, so its diagonal A↪A×A contains all points and is invertible. That makes A a subterminal object; the regular epimorphism A→1 is then also monic and hence invertible, contradicting the absence of a point. If e:X→Y is a regular epimorphism and y:1→Y is a point, its pullback in a category is a well-supported object over 1, so it has a point lifting y. Thus the representable functor
Γ=A(1,−):A→Set
(25)
preserves regular epimorphisms; it already preserves all existing limits. Hence Γ is a regular functor. Second, if ΓX is a singleton, its unique point x:1→X is a monomorphism, X is well-supported, and x contains all points of X. Well-pointedness makes x invertible. Thus Γ reflects terminal objects, even though it need not reflect arbitrary isomorphisms.
Now let C be a small category that is a regular category. For each object B, its slice category C/B is small and regular: finite limits, images and the stability of regular epimorphisms are computed using the corresponding constructions in C. By the permitted capitalization result choose a conservative functor that is regular,
JB​:C/B→AB​,
(26)
with AB​ a small capital regular category. The base-change functor B∗:C→C/B, given by X↦(X×B→B), preserves finite limits and regular epimorphisms. So does
RB​=ΓB​JB​B∗:C→Set.
(27)
Taking these as the coordinates defines a regular functor
R:C→SetobC.
(28)
The exponent is a set of indices, so this is a power of the Category of sets, with finite limits and regular epimorphisms computed coordinatewise.
To prove reflection of isomorphisms, suppose R(f) is invertible for f:A→B. In C/B, the object (A,f) is the pullback of B∗f:(A×B→B)→(B×B→B) along the point given by the diagonal B→B×B. Applying ΓB​JB​ shows that ΓB​JB​(A,f) is the fibre of the bijection RB​(f) over this diagonal point, and is a singleton. The preceding lemma gives JB​(A,f)≅1 through its canonical arrow to 1. Since JB​ reflects isomorphisms, (A,f)→(B,1B​) is invertible. Its underlying morphism is f. Therefore
R:C→SetobC is regular and reflects isomorphisms.​
(29)
In the small-category setting of this representation result, the elementary condition for replacing the power by one copy of Set is:
Every object is either well-supported or a strict initial object.​
(30)
This is almost total support for a regular category. “Strict initial” means initial and every arrow into it is invertible; the condition allows the case in which all objects are well-supported and no initial object exists.
For necessity, let V:C→Set be regular and conservative. If VA=∅, factor A→1 through its support of an object in a regular category S↪1. Preservation of images gives VS=1. Conservativity makes S→1 invertible, so A is well-supported. If VA=∅, then for every X the projection A×X→A is sent to the bijection ∅→∅, hence is invertible. Its inverse followed by the other projection supplies an arrow A→X. For any two such arrows, their equalizer is sent to a bijection onto VA, hence is invertible, proving uniqueness. Thus A is initial. If X→A exists, then VX→∅ exists, so VX=∅ and that arrow is sent to a bijection. Conservativity makes it invertible. Hence A is strict initial.
For sufficiency, choose the permitted conservative regular functor J:C→A into a small capital regular category, and put V=ΓJ. This is regular. A well-supported object of C is sent to a well-supported object of A, which has a point. A non-well-supported object is strict initial by the condition, and is consequently a proper subterminal object. The functor J preserves subterminality and reflects the noninvertibility of its arrow to 1; its image is a proper subterminal object and has no points. Thus VA is empty exactly on the non-well-supported objects.
Suppose Vf is a bijection for f:A→B. If both sets are empty, A and B are strict initial objects and f is invertible. Otherwise A,B are well-supported. Factor Jf as a regular epimorphism followed by a monomorphism m:I↪JB. Surjectivity of Vf makes every point of JB factor through m; capitality of A makes m invertible. Hence Jf is a regular epimorphism. Its kernel pair P=JA×JB​JA is well-supported, because P→JA and JA→1 are regular epimorphisms. Injectivity of Vf implies that every point of P has equal coordinates, and therefore factors through the diagonal JA↪P. Since P is well-pointed, this diagonal is invertible. Thus Jf is also monic, hence an isomorphism, and conservativity of J makes f invertible. This proves sufficiency with actual point and image arguments, rather than asserting that global sections of every capital regular category is conservative.
