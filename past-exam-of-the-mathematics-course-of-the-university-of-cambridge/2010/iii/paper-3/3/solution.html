An extension A⊆B is an integral extension when every b∈B is an integral element over A, meaning that
bn+an−1​bn−1+⋯+a0​=0(ai​∈A)
(12)
for some monic polynomial. The Krull dimension of a ring is the supremum of the lengths r of strict chains p0​⊊⋯⊊pr​ of prime ideals; the number counted is the number of inclusions.
We prove the prime-chain facts needed for integral extensions preserve Krull dimension. Taking quotients preserves an integral equation. Localizing both rings by the same multiplicative subset of A also preserves integrality: for b/s, divide the equation for b by sn, so its new coefficients are ai​/sn−i. Localizing just B at all elements outside one of its primes would not be the same operation.
Two elementary field observations are useful. If a field C is integral over a subdomain R, then R is a field, by the inverse argument given in Question 2. Conversely, if an integral domain C is integral over a field R, every nonzero c∈C has a monic equation with nonzero constant term: cancel a factor of c from an equation if necessary. Dividing by c and by that constant term expresses c−1 as a polynomial in c. Thus C is a field. In particular, the contraction of a maximal ideal under an integral extension is maximal.
For the Lying-over theorem, fix p∈SpecA and localize by S=A∖p. The nonzero domain S−1B is integral over the local ring Ap​. Choose a maximal ideal n of S−1B. Its contraction is maximal in Ap​, so it is the unique maximal ideal pAp​. The prime ideal correspondence for localization now pulls n back to a prime ideal q of B with q∩A=p.
For the going-up theorem, suppose p⊆p′ and q∩A=p. The injection A/p↪B/q is still integral. Apply the just-proved Lying-over theorem to p′/p. Pulling the resulting prime back gives q′⊇q with q′∩A=p′.
For the incomparability theorem for integral extensions, suppose q⊆q′ have the same contraction p. Write R=A/p and C=B/q. The latter is a domain integral over R. Localizing both by R∖{0} makes S−1C integral over the fraction field of R, so S−1C is a field. The prime q′/q is disjoint from S and therefore localizes to a proper prime, which must be zero in this field. Since C is a domain and all denominators are nonzero, an element of C whose localization is zero was already zero. Hence q′/q=0, proving q′=q.
A strict prime chain in B therefore contracts to a strict chain in A, giving dimB≤dimA. Conversely, start over the bottom of any finite prime chain in A using Lying-over theorem, and successively use going-up theorem to lift the entire chain. Distinct contractions ensure that the lifted inclusions are strict, giving the reverse inequality. Taking suprema, including when arbitrarily long chains exist, proves
dimB=dimA.​
(13)
For an algebraic extension K/Q, its ring O of algebraic integers is an integral extension of Z. The primes of Z are (0) and the maximal ideals (p), so the longest strict chains have length one; for example (0)⊊(2). The preceding argument yields
dimO=dimZ=1.​
(14)
This Krull dimension of rings of algebraic integers argument also covers infinite algebraic extensions. It uses integrality, not an assumption that K is a number field or that O is Noetherian.
