Two absolute values on a field are equivalent absolute values when they induce the same topology. For nontrivial absolute values on a field this is equivalent to
∣x∣2​=∣x∣1c​(x∈K)
(1)
for a fixed real number c>0. The trivial absolute value is equivalent only to itself.
Restriction of a Non-Archimedean absolute value plainly preserves the ultrametric inequality. Conversely, if the restriction is non-Archimedean, every integer, interpreted in either field, has absolute value on a field at most one. In particular every binomial coefficient has absolute value on a field at most one. The triangle inequality applied to the binomial expansion gives, for x,y∈L and every positive integer N,
∣x+y∣N≤∑j=0N​​(jN​)xjyN−j​≤(N+1)max(∣x∣,∣y∣)N.
(2)
Raise both sides to the power 1/N and let N→∞. Since (N+1)1/N→1, this yields
∣x+y∣≤max(∣x∣,∣y∣).​
(3)
This is the bounded-integer criterion for a non-Archimedean absolute value. The proof works for arbitrary field extensions, not just algebraic ones, and in positive characteristic as well.

First include the trivial absolute value if that convention is allowed. For a nontrivial Non-Archimedean absolute value on Q, every integer has absolute value on a field at most one, and some nonzero integer has absolute value on a field less than one: otherwise every nonzero rational number, being a quotient of two integers, would have absolute value on a field one. Factoring that integer shows that some prime number p has ∣p∣<1.
There cannot be two such prime numbers. If ∣p∣<1 and ∣q∣<1 for distinct prime numbers, choose integers a,b with ap+bq=1. Then the ultrametric inequality gives
1=∣1∣≤max(∣a∣∣p∣,∣b∣∣q∣)<1.
(4)
Every other prime number therefore has absolute value on a field one. Factoring numerator and denominator of a rational number gives
∣x∣=∣p∣vp​(x)=∣x∣pc​,c=logp−log∣p∣​>0.
(5)
Thus the nontrivial equivalence classes are precisely the p-adic absolute values, one for each prime number.
Now let F be a number field, with ring of integers of a number field A. If the restriction of an absolute value on a field to Q is trivial, a monic polynomial over Q satisfied by x∈F forces ∣x∣≤1: if ∣x∣>1, its leading term would strictly dominate the other terms. Applying the same argument to x−1 forces ∣x∣=1 for x=0. Thus a nontrivial absolute value on a field defined on F has a nontrivial restriction, associated with some prime number p.
Every a∈A has ∣a∣≤1, by applying the same leading-term argument to its monic integral equation. Consequently
p={a∈A:∣a∣<1}
(6)
is a proper prime ideal: it is an ideal by the ultrametric inequality, and ∣ab∣<1 with ∣a∣,∣b∣≤1 implies that at least one factor has absolute value on a field less than one. It is nonzero because it contains p.
The localization Ap​ lies in the valuation ring of the absolute value on a field: a denominator outside p has absolute value on a field one. Since A is a Dedekind domain, Ap​ is a discrete valuation ring. Choose its uniformizer π. Each x∈F× has a unique expression
x=πmu,m=vp​(x),u∈Ap×​.
(7)
Both u and u−1 lie in the valuation ring, so ∣u∣=1. Also π belongs to its maximal ideal, hence ∣π∣<1. Therefore ∣x∣=∣π∣vp​(x).
Conversely each nonzero prime ideal of A gives the Non-Archimedean absolute value ∣x∣=cvp​(x) for any 0<c<1, using the discrete valuation of Ap​. All choices of c are equivalent, and different prime ideals are distinguished by the elements of A having absolute value on a field less than one. We have proved Non-Archimedean absolute values on a number field:
{nontrivial equivalence classes on F}⟷{nonzero prime ideals of OF​}.​
(8)
The trivial class is additional when trivial absolute values on a field are admitted.

An extending absolute value on a field is non-Archimedean by Question 1(a). We prove finite-dimensional non-Archimedean norm equivalence over a complete field, using completeness rather than local compactness.
Fix a basis e1​,…,ed​ of a finite-dimensional vector space over K, and let ∥⋅∥ be a norm homogeneous for the given absolute value on a field and satisfying the ultrametric inequality. Write ∥∑ai​ei​∥0​=maxi​∣ai​∣. The upper bound
∥v∥≤C∥v∥0​,C=maxi​∥ei​∥,
(9)
is immediate. For the lower bound use induction on d. The one-dimensional case is homogeneity. For the induction step, the span W of e1​,…,ed−1​ is complete for its restricted norm by the induction comparison with the coordinate norm and completeness of K. A complete vector subspace of a metric space is closed, so
δ=infw∈W​∥ed​−w∥>0.
(10)
For v=w+aed​ with a=0, scaling by a gives ∥v∥≥∣a∣δ; the same coefficient bound is trivial when a=0. Also
∥w∥≤max(∥v∥,∣a∣∥ed​∥)≤max(1,δ−1∥ed​∥)∥v∥.
(11)
The induction bound controls every coordinate of w by a constant times ∥w∥. Together with the bound on a, this proves ∥v∥0​≤C′∥v∥.
Two extending absolute values on a field on L are norms of this kind on its finite-dimensional vector space over K. Hence there is B>0 with ∣x∣2​≤B∣x∣1​ for every x∈L. Apply this to xN and use multiplicativity:
∣x∣2​≤B1/N∣x∣1​.
(12)
Letting N→∞ gives ∣x∣2​≤∣x∣1​. Interchanging the two norms gives equality. Thus
There is at most one extending absolute value.​
(13)
The proof covers the trivial base absolute value on a field too. It proves uniqueness, without assuming an existence theorem or compactness of the coordinate sphere {v:∥v∥0​=1}.

The intended statement uses a nontrivial Non-Archimedean absolute value. Under that convention choose t with 0<∣t∣<1. A compact neighborhood C of zero contains an open ball of some radius r>0. For sufficiently large N, tNOK​ is contained in that ball. The valuation ring is closed, so tNOK​ is a closed subset of C and is compact. Scaling shows that OK​ itself is compact.
Its maximal ideal m={x:∣x∣<1} is open. The residue field k=OK​/m is therefore discrete and compact, hence finite: the open cover by its singleton sets has a finite subcover. Since m has finite index, its complement is a finite union of open cosets. Thus m is also closed and compact.
The continuous absolute value on a field attains its maximum on m. Nontriviality supplies a nonzero element there, so this maximum is ρ=∣π∣ for some π=0, with 0<ρ<1. If x∈m, then ∣x/π∣≤1, so m=πOK​.
For arbitrary x=0, choose the integer n for which ρn+1<∣x∣≤ρn. Then ρ<∣x/πn∣≤1. This element cannot lie in m, by maximality of ρ, so its absolute value on a field is one. Therefore ∣x∣=ρn. This proves discrete valuation from nontrivial local compactness:
∣K×∣=ρZ,k finite.​
(23)
The nontrivial valuation in the local compactness criterion is essential. If the trivial absolute value is admitted, the displayed source claim is false: take Q with that absolute value on a field. It is complete and discrete, hence locally compact, but its valuation ring and residue field are both Q, which is infinite. The intended nontrivial case has been proved above.

A local field is nondiscrete and locally compact. In the non-Archimedean case its valuation ring is compact by the scaling argument in part (b). Any Cauchy sequence has a tail in a translate of a compact ball, so has a convergent subsequence; the Cauchy property forces the whole sequence to converge. Thus the field is complete. Part (b) gives a discrete valuation, a uniformizer π, and a finite residue field k=Fq​, q=pf.
We construct the coefficient field rather than presuming it exists. The polynomial Tq−T has derivative −1 in characteristic p. Every residue element therefore has a unique polynomial root lift by Hensel's lemma. The set S of these q lifts is a field: qth powers preserve addition and multiplication, so sums and products remain polynomial root lifts; a nonzero lift has inverse aq−2. Reduction identifies S with Fq​. This is the finite coefficient field in positive-characteristic local fields.
For x∈OK​, choose a0​∈S with the same residue, write x=a0​+πx1​, and repeat with x1​∈OK​. Completeness gives
x=∑j≥0​aj​πj,aj​∈S.
(24)
The first nonzero coefficient determines the valuation, so the expansion is unique. Multiplying an arbitrary field element by a suitable power of π reduces it to this case. Consequently substitution T↦π gives a valued-field isomorphism
K≅Fpf​((T)),f≥1.​
(25)
Addition and multiplication agree with those of Laurent series by convergence, and a nonzero series has a nonzero leading coefficient, establishing injectivity as well as surjectivity.
Conversely, Fq​((T)) is complete for its T-adic absolute value on a field. Its valuation ring Fq​[[T]] is compact: fixing finitely many coefficients gives the finite partitions into balls, and the successive coefficient choices realize the inverse limit of the finite rings Fq​[T]/(Tn). Equivalently it is the product of countably many finite discrete coefficient sets. Thus it is a non-Archimedean local field. Distinct q give nonisomorphic valued fields because their residue fields have different sizes. Replacing ∣T∣ by any number in (0,1) gives an equivalent absolute value on a field.

Let L/K be a finite Galois extension of non-Archimedean local fields, with Galois group G, normalized valuation vL​, integer ring OL​, and residue characteristic p. The lower ramification numbering is
G−1​=G,Gi​={σ∈G:vL​(σa−a)≥i+1 for every a∈OL​},i≥0.
(37)
These higher ramification groups measure successively finer agreement with the identity. The group G0​ is the inertia group, the kernel of the action on the residue field. Each Gi​ is normal in G: conjugation permutes integral elements and preserves their valuations. The groups eventually become trivial, since each nonidentity automorphism moves some integral element and its displacement has finite valuation.
Let K0​=LG0​, the maximal unramified subextension of a local field extension. Then G/G0​ is the Galois group of the residue extension, cyclic and generated by a Frobenius automorphism. For a uniformizer π of L, the uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups gives
Gi​={σ∈G0​:vL​(σπ−π)≥i+1}.
(38)
To see why one uniformizer suffices, the extension L/K0​ is totally ramified and its integer ring is OK0​​[π]. Elements of G0​ fix its coefficients. Differences of powers of π are divisible by σπ−π, so the valuation bound for π implies it for every integral element. The converse follows by testing π itself.
The tame inertia quotient is seen explicitly from
G0​⟶kL×​,σ⟼σπ/π​.
(39)
It is a homomorphism because inertia acts trivially on the residue field, and its kernel is G1​. Thus G0​/G1​ is cyclic of order not divisible by p. For i≥1, the homomorphism
Gi​⟶(kL​,+),σ⟼πi+1σπ−π​​
(40)
has kernel Gi+1​. Indeed composing two automorphisms adds the leading displacements, since σπ/π≡1 and inertia fixes residue coefficients. Hence each Gi​/Gi+1​ is an elementary abelian p-group. Eventual triviality makes G1​ a p-group; the prime-to-p quotient makes it the unique Sylow subgroup of G0​. This is the wild inertia group. Tame ramification means G1​=1.
The filtration also measures the different exponent. For the totally ramified extension over K0​, the minimal polynomial of an algebraic element g of π has polynomial roots σπ, σ∈G0​. The derivative formula gives
vL​(g′(π))=∑σ∈G0​∖{1}​vL​(π−σπ).
(41)
For a fixed σ, its displacement valuation is the number of indices i≥0 for which it belongs to Gi​. Interchanging the finite sums therefore gives different exponent from ramification groups:
δ(L/K)=i≥0∑​(∣Gi​∣−1).​
(42)
The unramified part contributes zero. More generally different in a tower follows by taking products of bases of a module dual for the trace pairing, and gives
δ(L/K)=δ(L/E)+e(L/E)δ(E/K).
(43)
Thus a tame extension has different exponent e−1, while nontrivial wild groups contribute additional positive terms.
The lower numbering restricts to subgroups: if H=Gal(L/E), its groups are Hi​=H∩Gi​, directly from the definition using the same top-field valuation. Quotients need a different indexing. For real s≥0 put Gs​=G⌈s⌉​ and define the Herbrand function
φ(s)=∫0s​[G0​:Gt​]dt​,ψ=φ−1,Gu=Gψ(u)​.
(44)
This upper ramification numbering stretches or compresses intervals according to the inertia indices. The quotient theorem says that for a normal subgroup H, upper ramification groups commute with quotients:
(G/H)u=GuH/H.
(45)
In particular the same upper index has a meaning compatible with passing to a smaller Galois extension, which lower indices generally lack.
For a tame totally ramified extension the picture is simple: G0​=G, all Gi​ with i≥1 are trivial, and the different exponent is ∣G∣−1. The extension Qp​(ζp​) for odd p illustrates this, giving exponent p−2.
A mixed-characteristic wild family is the lower ramification filtration of a prime-power cyclotomic extension. Take L=Qp​(ζpr​) with odd p and π=ζpr​−1. If σa​(ζ)=ζa and a=1 modulo pr, then
vL​(σa​π−π)=vL​(ζa−1−1)=pvp​(a−1).
(46)
The last equality follows because ζa−1 has order pr−vp​(a−1) and its uniformizer valuation scales by the ramification index in that cyclotomic tower. The lower breaks are 0,p−1,p2−1,…,pr−1−1. Integrating the successive indices (p−1),p(p−1),… gives upper breaks 0,1,…,r−1.
In equal characteristic there is an equally explicit wild example. Start with K=Fq​((t)) and an Artin–Schreier extension
yp−y=t−m,m>0,p∤m.
(47)
There is no polynomial root in K: a negative-valuation element has v(xp−x)=pv(x), while the right side has valuation −m. Adjoining a polynomial root splits the polynomial, whose polynomial roots are y+c for c∈Fp​, so the extension is cyclic of degree p. The valuation equation forces total ramification, vL​(t)=p and vL​(y)=−m. Choose 1≤b≤p−1 with −bm≡1(modp) and set a=(1+bm)/p. Then π=tayb is a uniformizer. For a nonidentity automorphism y↦y+c,
πσπ​=(1+c/y)b,vL​(σπ−π)=1+m;
(48)
the first binomial coefficient b is nonzero modulo p, and all subsequent terms have strictly higher valuation. Therefore
G0​=⋯=Gm​=Cp​,Gm+1​=1,δ=(m+1)(p−1).
(49)
This is the ramification break of an Artin–Schreier pole. It shows concretely that wild breaks, unlike tame inertia, can occur arbitrarily far out in the filtration.
The lower groups measure integral displacements, the upper groups make quotient comparison possible, and the sizes of the groups determine the different.

The polynomial is Eisenstein at every prime number p, so a polynomial root α generates a totally ramified cubic field F/Qp​. The splitting-field Galois group is a transitive subgroup of S3​, hence C3​ or S3​. Its polynomial discriminant is
Δ=−p2(4p+27).
(50)
The product of the three polynomial root differences is a square root of Δ, transformed by the sign of the polynomial root permutation. Thus the group is C3​ exactly when Δ is a square, and otherwise it is S3​. In the latter case the splitting field is
L=FQp​(Δ​).
(51)
Indeed adjoining one polynomial root leaves a quadratic factor, whose polynomial discriminant is Δ/f′(α)2, so the square root of Δ supplies its remaining polynomial roots.
Suppose first that p>3. The unit −(4p+27) reduces to −27, and Hensel's lemma makes it a square exactly when −3 is a square modulo p. Quadratic reciprocity gives
(p−3​)={1,−1,​p≡1(mod3),p≡2(mod3).​
(52)
If p≡1(mod3), the field generated by α is already the cyclic splitting field, totally ramified of degree three. If p≡2(mod3), the nonsquare unit gives an unramified quadratic field E=Qp​(Δ​). Since F and E have coprime degrees, [FE:Qp​]=6. Its residue degree is at least two and its ramification index at least three; their product is six, so they are exactly two and three. Thus its inertia group is the normal C3​ inside S3​.
In both cases the ramification index is three and is not divisible by p. The wild inertia group is trivial, so all positive lower groups are trivial. As a different check, f′(α)=3α2+p has valuation two in F when p>3, agreeing with tame different exponent 3−1.
For p=3 the polynomial discriminant is −351=−33⋅13, which is nonsquare. Hence G=S3​. The quadratic subfield can be written E=Q3​(β) with β2=−39. It is ramified of degree two, by the Eisenstein polynomial T2+39. Together with the totally ramified cubic field this forces e(L/Q3​) to be divisible by both two and three. Since [L:Q3​]=6, the splitting field is totally ramified. In its normalized valuation,
vL​(3)=6,vL​(α)=2,vL​(β)=3,π=β/α has vL​(π)=1.
(53)
All differences between distinct polynomial roots have the same valuation, since S3​ permutes the polynomial root pairs and preserves the absolute value on a field. Also
f′(α)=3(α2+1)=(α−α2​)(α−α3​)
(54)
has valuation six, so each polynomial root difference has valuation three. A nonidentity three-cycle fixes β, and therefore
vL​(σπ−π)=vL​(ασαβ(α−σα)​)=3+3−2−2=2.
(55)
The transposition fixing α negates β, so sends π to −π and has displacement valuation vL​(2π)=1. The other transpositions have the same value by conjugacy. The uniformizer criterion now determines every group:
pp>3, p≡1(mod3)p>3, p≡2(mod3)p=3​GC3​S3​S3​​e336​f121​(G0​,G1​,G2​,…)(C3​,1,1,…)(C3​,1,1,…)(S3​,C3​,1,1,…)​​

For the wild case the different exponent is 5+2=7. Independently, the cubic subfield has different exponent vF​(3(α2+1))=3, and the quadratic extension L/F is tame with exponent one. The tower formula gives 1+2⋅3=7, confirming the filtration. Its positive upper break is 1/2, because [G0​:G1​]=2. This completes ramification of the splitting field of X3 plus pX plus p for every odd prime number.
