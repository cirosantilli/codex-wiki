Write λ=∑i​λi​, and suppose first that λ>0. Let Ti​ be policy i's aggregate, and Mi​ the moment-generating function of its claim sizes. The Poisson distribution count has probability generating function Gi​(z)=exp(λi​(z−1)), so the random-sum transform identity and independence of the policies give
MT​(u)=∏i​exp(λi​(Mi​(u)−1))=exp[λ(∑i​λλi​​Mi​(u)−1)].
(3)
The expression in the weighted sum is the moment-generating function of a mixture distribution with claim cumulative distribution function
F∗​(x)=∑i​λλi​​Fi​(x).
(4)
Thus the aggregate has a compound Poisson distribution with intensity λ and severity law F∗​. This identification remains valid without positive exponential moments, using the same calculation for Laplace transforms of nonnegative random variables and their uniqueness. It is the Poisson superposition of insurance portfolios.
For common unit-rate exponential distribution severities, the merged count K is Poisson distributed with mean λ. Because each severity is strictly positive, T=0 exactly when K=0, giving
a=e−λ.​
(5)
For k≥1, the sum of k unit-rate exponential distribution claims has Erlang distribution density xk−1e−x/(k−1)!. This density follows by induction: convolving the k-claim density with e−x gives e−x∫0x​yk−1dy/(k−1)!=e−xxk/k!. Conditional on a positive aggregate, the count weights are
P(K=k∣K>0)=1−e−λe−λλk/k!​=(eλ−1)k!λk​.
(6)
Therefore the hurdle decomposition of a positive random sum has positive-component probability density function
f​T​(x)=k=1∑∞​(eλ−1)k!λk​(k−1)!xk−1e−x​,x>0.​
(7)
Each density integrates to one and the weights sum to one. Hence FT​(x)=a+(1−a)FT​(x) for x≥0, where FT​ is the conditional cumulative distribution function given T>0. If λ=0, the aggregate is identically zero, and the positive component is undefined; the requested a∈(0,1) presupposes a positive total intensity.

Let Ct​ be the cumulative claim amount and Ut​=u+ct−Ct​ the surplus in the classical risk model. The supplied adjustment coefficient satisfies λ(MX​(R)−1)=cR. The Compound Poisson process has independent increments, and the random-sum transform identity gives
E[eR(Ct​−Cs​)]=exp((t−s)λ(MX​(R)−1)).
(23)
It follows that Zt​=exp(R(Ct​−ct)) is a nonnegative martingale with Z0​=1: conditional on the past, the multiplicative increment has expected value one. This is the exponential surplus martingale.
Let τ=inf{t≥0:Ut​<0} be the ruin stopping time. Surplus decreases only at claim arrivals, so on {τ≤t} the deficit at ruin gives Cτ​−cτ>u and Zτ​>eRu. The optional stopping theorem at the bounded stopping time τ∧t yields
1=EZτ∧t​≥E[Zτ​1{τ≤t}​]≥eRuP(τ≤t).
(24)
As t increases without bound, the events {τ≤t} increase to eventual ruin. Hence the ultimate ruin probability satisfies the Lundberg inequality
ψ(u)=P(τ<∞)≤e−Ru,u≥0.​
(25)
The proof uses only bounded-time stopping; it does not presume that the martingale can be stopped with equality of expected values at an unbounded ruin time.

The severity is an equal-weight mixture distribution of rate-2 and rate-2/3 exponential distributions. Its expected value is
μ=21​21​+21​23​=1.​
(28)
The moment-generating function, finite exactly for r<2/3, is
MX​(r)=2−r1​+2−3r1​,MX​(r)−1=(2−r)(2−3r)r(4−3r)​.
(29)
Canceling the nonzero r in the adjustment coefficient equation and multiplying by the positive denominator on 0<r<2/3 gives
4−3r=(1+θ)(4−8r+3r2).
(30)
Thus the required polynomial is
P(r)=3(1+θ)r2−(8θ+5)r+4θ.
(31)
Since P(0)=4θ>0, P(2/3)=−2<0, and the leading coefficient is positive, one root lies in (0,2/3) and the other exceeds 2/3. Only the smaller root is in the moment-generating function domain. Consequently
Rmix​=6(1+θ)8θ+5−16θ2+32θ+25​​=8θ+5+16θ2+32θ+25​8θ​.​
(32)
The rationalized expression avoids subtraction of nearly equal numbers when θ is small.

The PDF gives an inverse-gamma distribution with shape k and scale θ, and a gamma distribution prior with shape α and rate λ. Write μ(θ)=θ/(k−1). The supplied conditional moments, together with Eθ=α/λ and Eθ2=α(α+1)/λ2, give the three structural quantities of the Bühlmann model:
mvs2​=Eμ(θ)=λ(k−1)α​,=Var(μ(θ))=λ2(k−1)2α​,=E[Var(Xi​∣θ)]=λ2(k−1)2(k−2)α(α+1)​.​
(34)
Here v is the variance of hypothetical means and s2 the expected process variance. The law of total covariance and conditional independence of different years imply
EXi​=m,Cov(μ(θ),Xi​)=v,Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=v+s21{i=j}​.
(35)
All these quantities are finite because k>2.
For any coefficients ci​, minimizing the mean squared error over the intercept first gives c0​=m(1−∑i​ci​). Let Y=μ(θ)−m and Wi​=Xi​−m. Expanding the remaining mean squared error gives
E[(Y−∑i​ci​Wi​)2]=v−2v∑i​ci​+s2∑i​ci2​+v(∑i​ci​)2.
(36)
Its normal equations are s2ci​+v∑j​cj​=v for every i. Since s2>0, subtracting any two equations shows that all ci​ agree. The quadratic part is strictly positive for every nonzero coefficient change, so the solution is the unique global minimizer. Solving gives
ci​=s2+nvv​=n(k−2)+α+1k−2​(1≤i≤n),c0​=λ(k−1)[n(k−2)+α+1]α(α+1)​.​
(37)
A credibility estimate combines individual experience with the collective expected value, using an experience weight Z∈[0,1]. In this equal-exposure setting, the Bühlmann credibility premium is
μ​lin​=(1−Z)m+ZX,Z=s2+nvnv​=n(k−2)+α+1n(k−2)​.​
(38)
Thus the computed coefficients give exactly a credibility estimate, with a fixed credibility factor determined by known parameters and the number of observed years. This is Bühlmann credibility for inverse-gamma observations.
For the final Bayesian calculation, the likelihood function in θ is proportional to θnkexp(−θ∑i​xi−1​). Multiplication by the gamma distribution prior gives the Gamma prior for an inverse-gamma scale update
θ∣x1​,…,xn​∼Gamma(α+nk, rate λ+∑i​xi−1​).
(39)
Under quadratic loss, the conditional risk of an estimate d is Var(μ(θ)∣x)+(d−E[μ(θ)∣x])2. Its minimizer is therefore the posterior mean:
μ​Bayes​=(k−1)(λ+∑i​xi−1​)α+nk​.​
(40)
This is not a fixed-weight arithmetic-mean credibility estimate. For n=2, the observations (1,3) and (2,2) have the same sample mean, but reciprocal sums 4/3 and 1, hence different posterior means. For any n>2, append the same positive observations to these two samples. For n=1, the estimate is Cx/(1+λx), with C=(α+k)/(k−1); its second derivative is −2Cλ/(1+λx)3=0, so it is not affine either. Symmetry in the observations would force equal slopes in any affine representation, so these examples also exclude a general fixed-coefficient linear representation.
One can express the posterior mean as a broader data-dependent blend. With A=∑i​xi−1​ and harmonic mean H=n/A, it is
μ​Bayes​=(1−ZB​)m+ZB​k−1kH​,ZB​=λ+AA​.
(41)
This weights a harmonic-mean-based experience estimate, and the weight itself depends on the observations. It is not the credibility estimate based on X and the fixed Bühlmann credibility factor derived above.

The ratio of expected process variance to variance of hypothetical means is K=s2/v=(α+1)/(k−2), and the credibility factor is Z=n/(n+K). Holding α,λ fixed, an increase in k decreases K. Hence the credibility factor increases, approaching one as k tends to infinity. Extending the formula to real k>2 makes the sign explicit:
∂k∂Z​=[n(k−2)+α+1]2n(α+1)​>0.
(42)
For the integer values in the model this establishes the same strict monotonicity. The collective claim expected value m=α/[λ(k−1)] also changes, but the conclusion concerns the relative weight of experience: conditional noise declines faster than the variation of the risk means.
