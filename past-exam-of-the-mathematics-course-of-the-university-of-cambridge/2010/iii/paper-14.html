For a nonsingular smooth flow generated by F on a closed manifold, the Anosov flow condition is a continuous Dϕt​-invariant splitting
TN=Es⊕RF⊕Eu
(1)
and constants C,λ>0 such that, for all t≥0,
∥Dϕt​vs​∥≤Ce−λt∥vs​∥,∥Dϕ−t​vu​∥≤Ce−λt∥vu​∥​.
(2)
The norm can come from any auxiliary Riemannian metric; compactness makes the resulting definitions equivalent. The centre direction is the flow direction, with F nowhere zero.
One construction is a suspension flow. The hyperbolic toral automorphism of T2 defined by
A=(21​11​)
(3)
has eigenvalues λ±​=(3±5​)/2, with 0<λ−​<1<λ+​. Form the closed three-dimensional mapping torus (T2×[0,1])/((x,1)∼(Ax,0)) and move in the second coordinate at unit speed. Each crossing of the section applies A. The two one-dimensional eigenspaces give invariant transverse bundles, and after k crossings their derivatives multiply by λ−k​ and λ+k​. The difference between k and elapsed time is bounded, so any smooth Riemannian metric gives the required uniform exponential estimates. The vertical direction supplies the centre line.
A different construction is the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle of a closed oriented hyperbolic surface. This bundle is a closed three-manifold. In curvature −1, a normal Jacobi field along a unit-speed geodesic satisfies j′′−j=0. The initial data (j,j′)=(a,−a) give j(t)=ae−t, while (a,a) give j(t)=aet. The linearized geodesic flow transports these initial data as (j(t),j′(t)), so they give one stable and one unstable direction with precisely those exponential rates. The remaining tangent direction is the geodesic vector field. Thus this also constructs an Anosov flow, using geometric rather than return-map hyperbolicity.

Put ρ=1/f and denote the flow generated by fF by ψt​. It follows the same oriented orbits as ϕs​, with the clock determined implicitly by

For v∈Es(x) define

(5)
The integrand is bounded by a constant times e−λr∥v∥, since dρ is bounded and v is stable. The integral converges uniformly on bounded bundle vectors, so it is continuous and linear in v. Consider the graph
Es(x)={v+λs​(x,v)F(x):v∈Es(x)}.
(6)

For a fixed old time s, set
Is​(x,v)=∫0s​dρϕr​x​(Dϕr​v)dr.
(7)
Differentiation gives Dx​T(v)=Is​(x,v) and Dx​T(F)=ρ(ϕs​x)−ρ(x). Differentiating the implicit clock at fixed t, then differentiating ϕs(t,x)​(x), therefore yields for w=v+aF(x)
Dψt​(w)=Dϕs​v+ρ(ϕs​x)aρ(x)−Is​(x,v)​F(ϕs​x).
(8)
For a=λs​(x,v) this becomes
Dψt​(w)=Dϕs​v+ρ(ϕs​x)1​∫s∞​dρϕr​x​(Dϕr​v)dr F(ϕs​x).

λu​(x,v)=−ρ(x)1​∫−∞0​dρϕr​x​(Dϕr​v)dr,v∈Eu(x).
(11)
The same differentiation formula gives the tail −ρ(ϕs​x)−1∫−∞s​dρ(Dϕr​v)dr for s≤0, proving invariance and exponential contraction in negative new time.
Finally, these two graphs and RF=R(fF) form a direct splitting. Projecting onto TN/RF identifies the graphs with the old complementary stable and unstable subspaces, so they intersect neither each other nor the centre. We have established the continuous invariant splitting and both uniform estimates, and therefore the flow of fF is Anosov.

The Livsic theorem for a topologically transitive Anosov flow says that a Hölder function h:N→R satisfies the periodic-orbit obstruction
∫0T​h(ϕt​x)dt=0whenever ϕT​x=x
(12)
if and only if there is a Hölder function u satisfying the flow coboundary identity
u(ϕt​x)−u(x)=∫0t​h(ϕs​x)ds​.
(13)
The solution is differentiable along orbits and Fu=h in that sense. For a smooth flow and smooth h, the smooth regularity version gives u∈C∞(N), so Fu=h holds smoothly. The solution is unique up to an additive constant: the difference of two solutions is invariant and must be constant on a dense orbit and then everywhere by continuity. Necessity of the periodic obstruction follows immediately by taking t=T in the displayed identity.

The geodesic flow of a closed negatively curved surface is a topologically transitive Anosov flow. In particular, its Jacobi-field hyperbolicity and the usual negative-curvature transitivity theorem allow the smooth Livsic theorem to be applied. Set b(x,v)=βx​(v,v). The given closed-geodesic integrals are exactly its periodic-orbit obstructions on SM. Therefore
Xu=b,u∈C∞(SM)​.
(14)
We make the differential operators and signs explicit. On the unit tangent bundle, let V generate positive fibre rotation, X be the geodesic vector field and H=[V,X]. For the canonical coframe of a surface unit tangent bundle, write
α(ξ)=⟨v,dπξ⟩,θ(ξ)=⟨iv,dπξ⟩,
(15)
and let ω be the Levi-Civita connection form with ω(V)=1. This uses θ for a one-form, keeping β for the given tensor. The dual frame is (X,H,V), and the structure equations are
dα=ω∧θ,dθ=α∧ω,dω=−Kα∧θ.
(16)
These follow by writing the two horizontal forms as a rotated local orthonormal coframe and ω=dϑ+ω0​, with ω0​ its connection 1-form. Equivalently, differentiating the sine and cosine components of X twice in the fibre angle gives
[V,X]=H,[V,H]=−X.
(17)
The curvature equation also gives [X,H]=KV. The Liouville volume of a surface geodesic flow will be taken positive in the base/positive-fibre orientation, namely μ=α∧θ∧ω.
In an oriented orthonormal frame at x, put v=(cosϑ,sinϑ). The symmetric tensor evaluation is
b(x,v)=2β11​+β22​​+2β11​−β22​​cos(2ϑ)+β12​sin(2ϑ).
(18)
Since V=∂ϑ​, this proves (V3+4V)b=0.
The Lie bracket of vector fields identities give an operator identity that supplies the required equation. First,
XV2=V2X−2HV+X.

VXφ=−2Hφ​.

A simple Riemannian manifold has strictly convex boundary and a unique geodesic between each pair of points, smoothly dependent on its endpoints; there are no conjugate points and no trapped geodesics. Let ν denote the outward unit normal. For a boundary unit vector with ⟨v,ν⟩<0, follow its unit-speed geodesic γx,v​ until the next boundary intersection at time τ(x,v)>0. Its geodesic scattering relation is
α(x,v)=(γx,v​(τ),γ˙​x,v​(τ))​.
(28)
Thus it maps incoming boundary unit vectors to outgoing ones, recording both exit point and direction.
If the scattering relation is defined on the full boundary unit bundle, extend it on outgoing vectors by following the same oriented geodesic backwards to its previous boundary intersection, and fix tangential vectors. This extension is an involution, because the second application follows the same segment back to its starting point with the same velocity. The exit time is not a component of the scattering relation itself; together with it one obtains lens data. For a simple Riemannian metric, τ is the boundary distance between the endpoints of the segment.

First the common boundary distance function determines the induced tangential Riemannian metric. For a boundary curve c(t) with c(0)=x and c′(0)=w∈Tx​∂M, the local first-order distance expansion gives
limt→0​∣t∣dgi​​(x,c(t))​=∣w∣gi​​.
(29)
For clarity, the upper bound follows from the length of the boundary curve. In a small coordinate neighbourhood the Riemannian metric is arbitrarily close to its value at x, so the length of any sufficiently short joining path is bounded below by that constant-metric displacement norm with an arbitrarily small relative error. A minimizing short path cannot leave that neighbourhood. These two bounds prove the limit. Equality of the boundary distance functions then gives ∣w∣g1​​=∣w∣g2​​. The polarization identity yields
g1​∣T∂M​=g2​∣T∂M​=:h.
(30)
This alone fixes no choice of normal coordinates, so a boundary-fixing gauge is needed to compare the full tangent-space Riemannian metrics.
Let ni​ be the inward unit normals and let
Ci​(x,r)=expxgi​​(rni​(x))
(31)
be sufficiently small boundary normal coordinates. Their pullbacks have the form Ci∗​gi​=dr2+hi​(r), with hi​(0)=h. On a collar define ψ=C2​∘C1−1​. It fixes the boundary, has Dψ(w)=w for tangential w, and sends n1​ to n2​. For tangent-space vectors w+an1​ and z+bn1​ at the boundary, therefore,
(ψ∗g2​)(w+an1​,z+bn1​)=h(w,z)+ab=g1​(w+an1​,z+bn1​).
(32)
This proves the desired full Riemannian metric equality locally at the boundary.
We also justify extending this local identification to a global diffeomorphism. Interpolate the positive Riemannian metrics by gs​=(1−s)g1​+sg2​, for 0≤s≤1. Let Cs​ be their inward unit-normal exponential collars. Compactness of the boundary and parameter interval gives a common small collar width for all s. The intermediate Riemannian metrics need not be simple; only these local collars are used.
On the image of Cs​, define the time-dependent vector field
Ys​(Cs​(x,r))=χ(r)∂s​Cs​(x,r),
(33)
where χ is one near zero and vanishes before the outer edge of the collar; extend Ys​ by zero elsewhere. This is a smooth vector field on M, vanishing at the boundary because Cs​(x,0)=x. Its time-dependent flow Ψs​ is a global diffeomorphism fixing the boundary. For every sufficiently small fixed r, the curve s↦Cs​(x,r) solves its equation, since χ(r)=1. Uniqueness gives Ψs​(C0​(x,r))=Cs​(x,r). Consequently Ψ1​ agrees near the boundary with C2​C1−1​. Taking ψ=Ψ1​ proves
ψ∣∂M​=id,ψ∗g2​=g1​ on every Tx​M (x∈∂M)​.
(34)

Full equality of the Riemannian metrics at the boundary identifies their boundary unit bundles, outward normals and induced boundary Riemannian metrics. We show directly that the common boundary distance function determines both endpoint directions.
For distinct boundary points x,y, let γ be the unique joining unit-speed geodesic, with initial velocity v and final velocity w. The first variation of its length gives, for a∈Tx​∂M and b∈Ty​∂M,
dx​dg​(x,y)(a)=−⟨v,a⟩g​,dy​dg​(x,y)(b)=⟨w,b⟩g​.
(35)
Indeed, integration by parts in the length variation leaves just the endpoint terms, because the interior term contains ∇γ˙​​γ˙​=0. The Riemannian metric is simple, so the off-diagonal endpoint dependence is smooth and these derivatives are valid.
Thus the tangential components are vT​=−gradx∂M​dg​(x,y) and wT​=grady∂M​dg​(x,y). Unit speed and the incoming/outgoing signs supply the missing normal components:
v=vT​−1−∣vT​∣2​νx​,w=wT​+1−∣wT​∣2​νy​.
(36)
All quantities on the right use only boundary distance and the common boundary Riemannian metric. Every strictly incoming vector for g1​ has some exit point y; the geodesic joining the same pair for g2​ has exactly the same reconstructed initial and final vectors. Hence both geodesic scattering relations give the same exit for that input. Tangential vectors are fixed, and the outgoing extensions agree as inverses. Therefore
αg1​​=αg2​​​.
(37)
In fact their exit times also agree, since each is dg​(x,y).

A coorientation gives a globally nonvanishing C2 one-form ζ with E=kerζ. For tangent fields A,B in the integrable distribution,
dζ(A,B)=−ζ([A,B])=0,
(38)
since integrability makes their bracket tangent to E. Thus ζ∧dζ=0. Choose a transverse field Y with ζ(Y)=1. The C1 one-form η=−ιY​dζ then satisfies dζ=η∧ζ, as is checked on E and Y.
The Godbillon-Vey invariant in the unnormalized convention is
gv(E)=∫N​η∧dη​.
(39)
The three-form is continuous at the specified regularity, so its integral is defined. We prove independence of both choices by producing exact differences with C1 primitives, allowing ordinary Stokes theorem.
First, for a fixed ζ, another admissible auxiliary form has the form η′=η+aζ. From d2ζ=0 and dζ=η∧ζ, one gets dη∧ζ=0. Expanding the two three-forms, using this relation and ζ∧dζ=0, gives
η′∧dη′−η∧dη​=η∧da∧ζ=−da∧dζ=−d(adζ).​
(40)
Its integral on the closed manifold vanishes.
Next any other defining form is ζ′=hζ with h nowhere zero. Let g=log∣h∣. Then
dζ′=(η+dg)∧ζ′,
(41)
so one may use η0′​=η+dg. Its three-form differs by
η0′​∧dη0′​−η∧dη=dg∧dη=d(−dg∧η).
(42)
Again the difference integrates to zero; any subsequent auxiliary choice is handled by the first calculation. This includes reversing the chosen coorientation, since h can be negative. Thus the invariant, and the corresponding de Rham cohomology class, depends only on the oriented manifold and the foliation defined by E. Reversing the orientation of N, unlike reversing the coorientation of E, changes the sign of its numerical value.

Use the canonical coframe of a surface unit tangent bundle (α,θ,ω) and its dual (X,H,V). In constant curvature −1, the decaying normal Jacobi fields have data (j,j′)=(a,−a), so the stable line is generated by H−V. The weak stable bundle is therefore
Ews=span{X,H−V}.
(43)
It is annihilated by the nonvanishing form ζ=θ+ω. The structure equations now give
dθ=α∧ω,dω=α∧θ,dζ=α∧ζ.
(44)
Thus the auxiliary form in the Godbillon-Vey invariant can be taken as η=α. In the natural orientation given by the oriented base and positive fibre rotation, put μ=α∧θ∧ω. Then
η∧dη=α∧ω∧θ=−μ.
(45)
This sign is important: in these conventions the contact three-form is the negative of the positive Liouville volume of a surface geodesic flow.
Integration around each circle fibre gives ∫Sx​M​ω=2π, while α∧θ=π∗Ωa​. Hence ∫SM​μ=2πArea(M). The Gauss-Bonnet theorem and K=−1 give −Area(M)=2πχ(M). Consequently
gv(Ews)=−2πArea(M)=4π2χ(M)​.
(46)
For genus g≥2, this is −8π2(g−1) in the stated orientation. The opposite orientation of SM reverses the sign.

We use the following precise degree-zero/one kernel of the magnetic X-ray transform in degrees zero and one theorem, as permitted here: for a smooth Anosov magnetic flow on a closed oriented surface, if
Fh=q(x)+ξx​(v),h∈C∞(SM),
(52)
with q a smooth base function and ξ a smooth one-form, then q=0 and ξ is exact. Exact one-forms give the converse, because F(w∘π)=dw(v). No assumption of pointwise negative Gaussian curvature is needed for this theorem. We now derive the contact obstruction from it rather than assume the desired rigidity conclusion.
Use the usual connected-surface convention. Since ∫M​fΩa​=0, the top-degree de Rham cohomology class of fΩa​ vanishes: integration identifies HdR2​(M) with R. Choose a smooth one-form η on M satisfying dη=fΩa​, and set
σ=α−π∗η.
(53)
The structure equations and the previous volume calculation give
dσ=dα−fπ∗Ωa​=−ιF​μ,σ(F)=1−ηx​(v).
(54)
This primitive exists because of the zero-flux hypothesis, and need not itself be contact.
A useful elementary fact is that every smooth invariant function of an Anosov flow is constant on a connected manifold. If Fh=0, then dhx​(vs​)=dhϕt​x​(Dϕt​vs​), and boundedness of dh with exponential contraction makes this zero as t→∞. Negative time does the same for an unstable vector. Also dh(F)=0. The Anosov splitting spans the tangent space, so dh=0. This avoids any extra ergodicity assumption.
Suppose a smooth contact form τ is preserved. Invariance and contraction imply τ(vs​)=τ(Dϕt​vs​)=0 in the limit; negative time gives the same on unstable vectors. Thus τ annihilates both transverse bundles. Its value τ(F) cannot vanish anywhere, since otherwise τ would be zero at that point, contradicting the contact condition. It is a smooth invariant function and therefore a nonzero constant. Divide τ by that constant to arrange τ(F)=1.
The Cartan formula for the Lie derivative now gives ιF​dτ=0. In dimension three, all two-forms annihilating F are scalar multiples of ιF​μ, so
dτ=kιF​μ.
(55)
The scalar k is smooth and nowhere zero by the contact condition. Both sides without k are flow-invariant; hence Fk=0 and the preceding Anosov argument makes k constant. It follows that
ζ:=τ+kσ
(56)
is a closed differential one-form.
We require the Liouville mean of such a closed differential form, and derive it explicitly. Let Rs​ be fibre rotation and average ζ over 0≤s≤2π. Since dζ=0, the Cartan formula for the Lie derivative gives
Rs∗​ζ−ζ=d(∫0s​Rr∗​(ζ(V))dr).
(57)
Consequently its rotational average differs from ζ by an exact form. For a closed rotationally invariant one-form ζ​, the identity d(ζ​(V))=LV​ζ​−ιV​dζ​=0 makes its vertical value a constant a. Subtracting aω leaves a rotationally invariant horizontal form, which descends to a base form ξ. We have therefore proved the averaging closed one-forms on a surface unit tangent bundle decomposition
ζ=π∗ξ+aω+dh,dξ=aKΩa​.
(58)
The last relation follows from closedness and dω=−Kπ∗Ωa​.
Evaluation at F gives ζ(F)=ξx​(v)+af+Fh. The average of the linear expression ξx​(v) around each unit circle is zero. The second term has total integral 2πa∫M​fΩa​=0. Finally ∫SM​Fhdμ=0 by volume preservation and Stokes theorem. Thus
∫SM​ζ(F)dμ=0
(59)
for every closed differential one-form ζ in this zero-flux system. Apply this to τ+kσ: the directional average of ηx​(v) also vanishes, so its integral is (1+k)Volμ​(SM). Positivity of the volume forces k=−1. Hence dτ=dσ and ζ=τ−σ is closed.
Using its proved decomposition and τ(F)=1, we obtain
1=1−ηx​(v)+ξx​(v)+af+Fh,Fh=(η−ξ)x​(v)−af​.
(60)
The stated magnetic transform kernel theorem gives af=0 and exactness of η−ξ, hence dη=dξ. If a=0, then dξ=0 and fΩa​=dη=0, so f=0. If a=0, the equation af=0 already gives f=0. Necessity is proved.
Conversely, if f=0, then F=X. The canonical form α is contact because α∧dα=−μ never vanishes, and
LX​α=ιX​dα+d(α(X))=0+d1=0.
(61)
Thus the flow preserves α, proving
ϕt​ preserves a smooth contact form⟺f≡0​.
(62)
If disconnected surfaces are admitted, the flux condition must hold on each component for the exactness step; a single cancelling total flux does not imply componentwise exactness. The result above uses the standard connected interpretation of a surface.
