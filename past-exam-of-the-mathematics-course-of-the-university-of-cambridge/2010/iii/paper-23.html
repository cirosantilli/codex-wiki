Fix a separable closure ks. The Tate module and rational Tate module are
Tℓ​(E)=lim​n​E[ℓn](ks),Vℓ​(E)=Tℓ​(E)⊗Zℓ​​Qℓ​,
(1)
where the transition maps are multiplication by ℓ. Since ℓ is different from the characteristic, E[ℓn](ks)≃(Z/ℓn)2 and these transition maps are surjective. Choosing compatible bases gives Tℓ​(E)≃Zℓ2​, and Tℓ​(E)/ℓnTℓ​(E)≃E[ℓn]. Both spaces carry the natural continuous action of the absolute Galois group of k and of the base-defined endomorphism ring of an elliptic curve.
For (i), choose a nonrational a∈R. Such an element exists because the intersection of the given two-dimensional rational subalgebra with the integral endomorphism ring contains a full lattice. The characteristic equation of an elliptic endomorphism is
a2−tr(a)a+deg(a)=0.
(2)
Since a∈K is nonrational, its minimal polynomial over Q has degree two. Comparing the two monic equations gives tr(a)=a+aˉ and deg(a)=aaˉ. The same is the characteristic polynomial on the rational Tate module: the Weil pairing gives determinant deg(a), and applying it to 1−a gives trace 1+deg(a)−deg(1−a). Thus on Vℓ​(E) the characteristic polynomial is
(X−a)(X−aˉ).
(3)
If Kℓ​ is a quadratic field over Qℓ​, a unital Kℓ​-action on this two-dimensional Qℓ​-space makes it a one-dimensional Kℓ​-space. If Kℓ​≃Qℓ​×Qℓ​, the two roots above are distinct in Qℓ​ and their eigenspaces each have dimension one. The two idempotent factors therefore have equal rank one, so the module is again free of rank one. This also proves faithfulness in the split case. Hence
Vℓ​(E)≃Kℓ​as Kℓ​-modules.​
(4)
For (ii), first prove an integral divisibility statement. If a∈R maps Tℓ​(E) into ℓnTℓ​(E), then its induced action on E[ℓn] is zero. The kernel of an isogeny of [ℓn] is therefore contained in the kernel of a. The quotient property of this isogeny of elliptic curves gives a unique endomorphism b with
a=b∘[ℓn]=[ℓn]∘b.
(5)
This factorization is defined over k: applying any descent automorphism preserves it, and uniqueness fixes b. Inside the rational endomorphism ring, b=a/ℓn belongs to K, so b∈R. Consequently
{a∈R:aTℓ​(E)⊆ℓnTℓ​(E)}=ℓnR.
(6)
Here the prime-to-characteristic torsion is étale, so vanishing on its geometric points is vanishing on the entire kernel scheme.
Every element of Rℓ​ preserves the Tate module by continuity. Conversely suppose ϕ∈Kℓ​ preserves it. Choose n≥0 with ℓnϕ∈Rℓ​, and approximate it by a∈R modulo ℓnRℓ​. Write ℓnϕ=a+ℓnc with c∈Rℓ​. Then aTℓ​(E)⊆ℓnTℓ​(E), so the divisibility statement gives a/ℓn∈R. It follows that ϕ=a/ℓn+c∈Rℓ​. Thus the CM Tate module stabilizer is
{ϕ∈Kℓ​:ϕTℓ​(E)⊆Tℓ​(E)}=Rℓ​.​
(7)
This does not presume freeness of the integral Tate module over a nonmaximal order.
For (iii), every element of R is defined over k, so the Galois representation commutes with R and therefore with Kℓ​. By (i), its action on Vℓ​(E) is multiplication by an element of Kℓ×​. The action and its inverse preserve Tℓ​(E); by (ii), this scalar and its inverse belong to Rℓ​. Hence
ρℓ​(Gk​)⊆Rℓ×​,so the image is abelian.​

The coefficient ring I is complete for its p-adic norm, with uniformizer p and residue field Fp​. Its residue field is infinite, so I is not compact. The measure construction uses completeness, not compactness of the coefficient ring.
An I-valued p-adic measure on G is a bounded I-linear functional
μ:C(G,I)⟶I,
(9)
on the continuous functions with the uniform norm. Equivalently it assigns a value μ(C)∈I to each clopen subset, finitely additively on disjoint unions. Integration of a locally constant function is defined by
∫G​∑j​aj​1Cj​​dμ=∑j​aj​μ(Cj​).
(10)
Refining finite clopen partitions shows that this is independent of the expression. Since every μ(Cj​)∈I, the nonarchimedean triangle inequality gives ∣∫fdμ∣p​≤∥f∥∞​.
Locally constant functions are uniformly dense in C(G,I). Indeed, for every n, continuity and compactness of G supply a finite clopen partition on which f is constant modulo pnI; choose one value of f on each part. These approximations converge uniformly, and completeness of I extends integration uniquely. Conversely, a bounded functional gives μ(C)=μ(1C​) and finite additivity. This proves the equivalence of the two definitions. Ordinary countable additivity of real measures is not part of the definition.
Define the Iwasawa algebra by
I[[G]]=lim​U​I[G/U]=lim​U,n​(I/pnI)[G/U],
(11)
where U runs over open subgroups. Since G is an abelian profinite group, every such quotient is a finite abelian group. The transition map for V⊆U sends a basis element [gV] to [gU], so sums coefficients over the fibers.
A p-adic measure defines an element of this inverse limit by
μU​=∑gU∈G/U​μ(gU)[gU].
(12)
Finite additivity is precisely compatibility under the transition maps. Conversely, compatible coefficients define values on every coset and hence on every clopen subset: any clopen subset is a union of cosets of one open subgroup, by compactness and a finite common refinement. Compatibility makes the resulting value independent of that subgroup. The preceding integration argument then constructs the p-adic measure.
Finally, multiplication is convolution of p-adic measures. On a finite quotient its coefficients are
(μ∗ν)(gU)=∑aU∈G/U​μ(aU)ν(a−1gU).
(13)
This is exactly the coefficient of [gU] in μU​νU​. Equivalently,
∫G​hd(μ∗ν)=∫G​∫G​h(xy)dμ(x)dν(y),
(14)
first for locally constant h, then by uniform approximation. The Dirac measure at the identity corresponds to the identity element of the group algebra. We have proved a bijection respecting addition, scalar multiplication and multiplication:
I[[G]]≃Meas(G,I).​
(15)

The map
[i](x,y)=(−x,iy)
(16)
is an automorphism of the elliptic curve over K, fixes its identity point, and squares to [−1]. For the invariant differential on an elliptic curve ω=dx/(2y), it satisfies [i]∗ω=iω. Thus Z[i]⊆EndK​(E), with the indicated embedding of K.
Under analytic complex uniformization of an elliptic curve E(C)≃C/L, this automorphism acts as multiplication by i, so L is stable under Z[i]. It is a rank-one torsion-free module over the Gaussian integers. Since that ring is a principal ideal domain, L=ΩZ[i]. Every analytic endomorphism is multiplication by an a∈C with aL⊆L, which here says exactly a∈Z[i]. Therefore the geometric endomorphism ring of an elliptic curve is Z[i], and all its elements are generated over Z by the displayed K-defined automorphism. Thus EndK​(E)=Z[i].
If an endomorphism is defined over Q, its pullback on the rational differential ω has scalar in Q. The differential action is injective in characteristic zero, because a nonzero isogeny of elliptic curves is separable and has nonzero differential. Its scalar must therefore lie in Q∩Z[i]=Z. Conversely integer multiplication is defined over Q. Hence
EndQ​(E)=Z,EndQ(i)​(E)=Z[i].​
(17)
Put λ=1+i. The valuations of the differences of the three nonidentity fourth roots of unity from 1 are
vλ​(−1−1)=2,vλ​(i−1)=1,vλ​(−i−1)=1.
(18)
For instance 2=−iλ2, and the other two differences are associates of λ. None is divisible by λ3. The group (Z[i]/λ3)× has 8−4=4 elements, so the four units map bijectively to it. Every ideal prime to λ consequently has a unique primary Gaussian integer generator.
The Grössencharacter of the square-lattice elliptic curve is explicitly
ψE​(a)=α,(α)=a,α≡1(modλ3),f=λ3.​
(19)
For integral ideals prime to 2, the generator is an odd Gaussian integer; extend multiplicatively to fractional ideals prime to 2. Products of primary generators are primary, so this is a Hecke character with ideal infinity type (1,0): on a principal ideal with generator a≡1(modλ3), its value is a. Its conductor divides λ3, and cannot divide λ2, since −1≡1(modλ2) would force ψE​((−1))=−1, whereas the unit ideal has value 1. Thus the displayed conductor is exact.
We next verify directly that this character gives the Frobenius values of this particular curve, fixing the unit ambiguity. The point
P=(i,1−i)∈E(K)
(20)
satisfies, by the chord-and-tangent addition law,
[λ]P=(1,0),[λ]2P=(0,0),[λ]3P=O.
(21)
For the first addition, [i]P=(−i,1+i) and the chord has slope −1. Since P is killed by λ3 but not by λ2, it generates the eight-element Z[i]-module E[λ3]≃Z[i]/λ3. Thus all this torsion is rational over K.
The model has discriminant 64, so it has good reduction at every γ=(λ). First suppose γ lies over a split rational prime p. Then its residue field is Fp​, p≥5, and π=ψE​(γ) has norm p. Reduction of [π] still has degree p and has zero differential, since its differential scalar π belongs to γ. It is therefore purely inseparable of degree p. Factoring out the Frobenius isogeny gives
[π]​=u∘Frobp​
(22)
for an automorphism u of the reduction. In characteristic at least five, the automorphisms of this model fixing O are the four Gaussian units: comparing coefficients in (x,y)↦(v2x,v3y) gives v4=1. Prime-to-p torsion reduces injectively. Both [π] and Frobp​ fix the reduction of E[λ3], the former by the primary congruence and the latter by its rationality over the residue field. Thus u fixes this entire module. The unit congruences proved above force u=1.
For an inert rational prime p≡3(mod4), we have γ=(p), Nγ=p2, and its primary generator is π=−p, because 4∣p+1 implies λ3∣(−p−1). To determine Frobenius without excluding p=3, write
ap​=−∑x∈Fp​​(px3−x​).
(23)
Replacing x by −x changes each nonzero quadratic-character value to its negative, because −1 is a nonsquare. Hence ap​=0. The Frobenius characteristic equation Frobp2​−[ap​]Frobp​+[p]=0 gives Frobp2​=[−p]. This proves the asserted Frobenius value at inert primes as well, and identifies the displayed Hecke character with that of E.
Finally, the formal parameter t=−x/y is defined over the local ring at γ. Since the reduced endomorphism is the q-power Frobenius isogeny, with q=Nγ, its pullback sends t to (−x/y)q=tq. Passing to the completion at the identity gives the equality of formal series
[π](t)≡tNγ(modγ).​

The construction must first specify the field of definition of the complex multiplication. Let E/F be a CM elliptic curve and let K be its geometric CM field, embedded by its action on an invariant differential. In characteristic zero this action is injective. Consequently every geometric endomorphism is defined over F′=FK: a Galois automorphism fixing FK fixes its differential scalar and therefore fixes the endomorphism itself. Initially assume F already contains K; we return to the other case below.
Choose a complex embedding and an analytic complex uniformization of an elliptic curve
E(C)≃C/L,L=Ωa,
(25)
where a is a lattice in K, stable under the CM order R. It need not be the maximal order or a principal ideal. Its torsion is described by the finite adelic quotients of this lattice.
The input from the CM reciprocity theorem is precise: if s is an idele of F, n=NF/K​s, and σ=ArtF​(s), then σ takes the CM torus to the torus with lattice nf−1​L; on torsion the comparison is induced by multiplication by nf−1​. This reciprocity statement comes from the ideal action on CM lattices: an ideal b gives the isogeny to b−1L, composition multiplies ideals, and adding torsion level structure removes automorphism ambiguity. The resulting action on algebraic CM points is the ray-class Artin action. This is the deep CM input, not an assertion that an arbitrary abelian Galois representation automatically comes from an algebraic Hecke character.
Because σ fixes F, the algebraic curve Eσ is E. Comparing the reciprocity uniformization with the original one therefore gives multiplication by a scalar
a(sf​)∈K×,a(sf​)nf−1​L=L.
(26)
The scalar belongs to K: a complex scalar carrying one lattice contained in K to another is the quotient of two nonzero lattice elements. Its torsion action is
ρℓ​(ArtF​(sf​))=a(sf​)(NF/K​sf​)ℓ−1​in (K⊗Qℓ​)×.
(27)
The uniqueness of the torsion-compatible comparison is important: two possible scalars would induce the same action on all torsion, hence their difference would annihilate torsion of arbitrarily large order and be zero. Multiplying the torsion-action formulas for sf​ and tf​ now proves a(sf​tf​)=a(sf​)a(tf​).
For b∈F×, Artin reciprocity sends its principal idele to the identity. The archimedean factors are complex and connected, so its finite part also has trivial Artin image. The preceding formula therefore gives
a(bf​)=NF/K​(b).
(28)
It follows that
ψE​(s)=(NF/K​s)∞​a(sf​)​​
(29)
is trivial on principal ideles and defines a character of the idèle class group of F. This establishes the multiplicativity and principal-idele condition, rather than merely prescribing Frobenius values and hoping that they extend.
We must still prove continuity and a finite conductor. For a finite place v and a prime ℓ different from its residue characteristic, an idele supported on the units at v has trivial ℓ-component of its norm. Thus its action on the Tate module is exactly multiplication by a(u). At a place of good reduction, inertia acts trivially, so a(u)=1. At a bad place, a CM curve has potentially good reduction, and inertia on the prime-to-residue-characteristic Tate module has finite image. Hence a is trivial on an open subgroup of the local units there. There are only finitely many bad places. Together these facts give an open finite-congruence kernel on the unit ideles and prove continuity. The archimedean norm factor is already continuous. The potentially-good-reduction and inertia facts are the reduction-theoretic inputs in Serre and Tate's treatment of CM abelian varieties.
Consequently ψE​ is a Hecke character. In ideal notation, for b≡1(modf),
ψE​((b))=NF/K​(b),
(30)
so its infinity type of a Hecke character is the type determined by the chosen CM embedding, with the inverse archimedean norm in the idele convention. Nonmaximal orders merely contribute additional finite-level conditions; the proof retains their lattice and does not replace it by the maximal-order lattice without justification.
At a good prime v, choose a uniformizer idele and a prime ℓ different from the residue characteristic. Its norm has trivial ℓ-component, so arithmetic Frobenius acts on the Tate module by ψE​(v). Specialization of prime-to-residue-characteristic torsion identifies this with the reduced Frobenius isogeny. Thus
1−av​T+NvT2=(1−ψE​(v)T)(1−ψE​(v)​T).
(31)
The corresponding Hasse-Weil L-function factors as L(E/F,s)=L(ψE​,s)L(ψˉ​E​,s), including the local factors determined by inertia. This explains why the Hecke character encodes both the torsion representations and the Euler factors.
If F does not contain K, apply the construction over FK. The two conjugate CM characters are exchanged by the nontrivial automorphism of FK/F, and the two-dimensional Galois representation over F is their induced representation. In that case the natural description is L(E/F,s)=L(ψE​/FK,s) by induction, rather than a pair of one-dimensional characters over F. Question 3 supplies a concrete example: Q does not define [i], whereas Q(i) does. This completes the existence argument for an arbitrary CM curve over a number field, with its field of definition correctly accounted for.

A one-variable main conjecture for CM elliptic curves compares a torsion arithmetic Iwasawa module with a measure interpolating special Hecke L-function values. The classical bounded-measure formulation uses a prime of good reduction which splits in the CM field, hence gives ordinary reduction. We first describe the one-prime division-tower formulation, including the analytic construction, and then explain its cyclotomic Selmer counterpart and the change at supersingular primes.
For a precise basic setting, take E/K with complex multiplication by OK​, K of class number one, and a split prime p>3 of good reduction, prime to the Hecke conductor f. Write pOK​=ppˉ​, choose the p-adic embedding corresponding to p, and let π=ψE​(p). Its formal group of an elliptic curve has height one, with [π](t)≡tp(modp). The one-prime division tower
F∞​=K(E[p∞]),G=Gal(F∞​/K)=Δ×Γ
(32)
has Δ of order prime to p and Γ≃Zp​. Its CM character κ:G→Zp×​ describes the action on the prime-primary Tate module. Let F=F∞Γ​ and Λ=I[[Γ]]≃I[[T]]. We extend coefficients to the complete unramified ring I to include character values and a CM period.
Let X be the Galois group over F∞​ of the maximal abelian pro-p extension unramified outside primes above p. Let A be the analogous everywhere-unramified group, equivalently the norm inverse limit of the p-parts of the ideal class groups. Write U for the norm limit of principal local units at p, E for the closures of global units in U, and C for the closures of elliptic units. Roots of unity and finite initial-layer errors are retained when formulating integral statements; after passage to a finite-character component they do not change a characteristic ideal.
The class-field unit sequence, obtained by taking norm limits in global Artin reciprocity, gives
0⟶E/C⟶U/C⟶X⟶A⟶0.
(33)
In this ordinary one-prime tower, the unit and global-unit terms have the same Iwasawa rank, and the displayed quotients and X,A are torsion on their character components. The equality
charΛ​(E/C)χ​=charΛ​Aχ​
(34)
is the global-units/class-groups form of the CM main theorem. Multiplicativity of characteristic ideals in the sequence then gives
charΛ​Xχ​=charΛ​(U/C)χ​.
(35)
The analytic construction must identify the latter ideal with a genuinely interpolating p-adic L-function; it is not enough to rename an arbitrary characteristic power series as an L-function.
Here is that construction. For an auxiliary ideal a prime to 6pf, a suitably normalized rational elliptic function has divisor a multiple of
Na(O)−∑P∈E[a]​(P).
(36)
Translate by a generator of the conductor torsion and take the product over its ray-class orbit. This produces a rational function fa​(t) defined over K which is a unit series at the identity. Evaluating it on compatible pn-division points gives elliptic units. The reason for the norm relations is geometric: multiplication by π groups the translated zeros and poles into fibers, so the product over E[p] has the divisor of fa​([π]t). The normalization fixes the constant, giving the local identity
∏Q∈E[p]​fa​(t+E​Q)=fa​([π]t).
(37)
This is also the norm relation of the Coleman power series for a CM formal group interpolating the resulting local units.
An integral logarithmic correction makes the passage from those units to a bounded measure explicit. In the chosen split completion Kp​=Qp​, put
Lf​(t)=logf(t)−p1​logf([π]t)=p1​logf([π]t)f(t)p​.
(38)
Since [π]t≡tp(modp) and the coefficients of f reduce to Fp​, the ratio is in 1+pZp​[[t]]. For p>2, its logarithm divided by p is integral. Constants are treated using the usual p-adic logarithm on units. This proves the integrality needed for the Frobenius-corrected logarithm; applying an uncorrected logarithm alone would not prove boundedness.
Choose an isomorphism η:Gm​→E over I, and normalize the CM period by
logE​(η(T))=Ωp​log(1+T).
(39)
Then Af​(T)=Lf​(η(T))∈I[[T]] is the Amice transform of a unique I-valued p-adic measure on Zp​. The norm relation above gives
∑ζp=1​Af​(ζ(1+T)−1)=0:
(40)
the first logarithmic sum is logf([π]t) and the second is the same sum, because [π]Q=0 and there are p points. On the measure side the sum selects pZp​ and multiplies by p, so this identity says that the measure is supported on Zp×​. Transport it to G by the CM character and project to the desired component.
The moments can now be calculated rather than postulated. With D=(1+T)d/dT,
∫xkdμf​(x)=DkAf​(0)=Ωpk​(1−pπk​)dzkdk​logf(expE​z)​z=0​(k≥1).
(41)
The factor πk appears because [π] is multiplication by π in the additive formal-logarithm coordinate. The remaining derivative is a Coates–Wiles homomorphism. The complex product for the elliptic function makes it a sum of shifted inverse powers of lattice elements. Grouping these lattice elements by ray-class ideals identifies that sum with (k−1)!Lf​(ψˉ​Ek​,k)/Ω∞k​, multiplied by the auxiliary factor Na−ψE​(a)k. Here Lf​ omits primes dividing the original conductor, including when the conductor of the power character becomes smaller.
Normalize the rational functions and matching complex and p-adic periods consistently, and remove the auxiliary factors by the distribution relations. They are evaluations of Na−[a] in the Iwasawa algebra. On each finite-character component an auxiliary ideal can be chosen making this element a unit: its residues at p and pˉ​ can be prescribed independently by the Chinese remainder theorem. The resulting integral measure μE​ satisfies, on its branch and with k in the corresponding residue class,
Ωp−k​∫G​κ(g)kdμE​(g)=(k−1)!(1−pψE​(p)k​)Ω∞k​Lf​(ψˉ​Ek​,k)​.​
(42)
The exact normalization of periods matters for this equality; multiplication by an integral unit does not affect the ideal in the main conjecture. Finite-order twists are obtained by inserting a character in the ray-class sums, with its local Gauss sum. The lattice calculation proves algebraicity of the normalized complex values, while the integral series construction proves their p-adic interpolation. These are different steps.
The Coleman measure map identifies Uχ​ with a rank-one Iwasawa algebra lattice, up to finite errors, and takes Cχ​ to the ideal generated by the corresponding power series Lχ​. The conjecture therefore has the concrete form
charI[[Γ]]​Xχ​=(Lχ​).​
(43)
Its proof uses the Euler system of elliptic units. Adjoining auxiliary ray moduli supplies norm relations with factors 1−Frobq−1​. Derivative operators in the auxiliary cyclic groups produce Kolyvagin classes; their controlled valuations relate a new auxiliary prime to its ideal class. Chebotarev density theorem permits the required Frobenius choices. Successively choosing these primes bounds the class-group elementary divisors by the elliptic-unit index, giving the difficult characteristic-ideal divisibility. The opposite comparison comes from the class-number/unit-index formula and passage through the tower. Their equality, combined with the exact sequence above and the logarithmic computation, is the proof mechanism. The ordinary split-prime setting of this formulation is specified in Rubin's one-variable main-conjecture paper.
For completeness, the familiar cyclotomic formulation concerns a different tower. If E/Q has complex multiplication and good ordinary reduction at p, put Γcyc​=Gal(Q∞​/Q) and
XE​=Homcont​(Selp∞​(E/Q∞​),Qp​/Zp​).
(44)
For a finite layer L, its Selmer group of an elliptic curve is
ker(H1(GS​(L),E[p∞])⟶∏v∈S​im(E(Lv​)⊗Qp​/Zp​)H1(Lv​,E[p∞])​),
(45)
where S contains the bad primes and primes above p; at infinite level take the direct limit. Thus it imposes the local Kummer conditions and sits between rational points tensored with Qp​/Zp​ and the p-primary Tate–Shafarevich group. Its dual XE​ is a torsion Iwasawa module, and the cyclotomic main conjecture is
charZp​[[Γcyc​]]​XE​=(Lp​(E)).​
(46)
With compatible reciprocity conventions, the ordinary function has the finite-character interpolation
χ(Lp​(E))=α−nτ(χ)ΩE+​L(E,χˉ​,1)​(condχ=pn>1),Lp​(E)(1)=(1−α−1)2ΩE+​L(E,1)​,
(47)
where α is the p-adic unit root of X2−ap​X+p and τ(χ)=∑amodpn​χ(a)e2πia/pn; characters of this cyclotomic Zp​-extension are even. The two-variable CM measure specializes to this line after the CM-character twist. Kummer theory, class-field descriptions of the prime-primary local conditions and control of finite-layer errors identify its algebraic counterpart with the displayed Selmer dual. The one-prime division tower is not itself the cyclotomic tower, so this requires the twist and specialization argument, not just changing its name.
Finally, at a good inert prime the curve has supersingular reduction. The ordinary unit root is absent, the unmodified cyclotomic Selmer dual is not torsion, and the ordinary bounded-function argument does not apply. For CM curves over Q and odd supersingular p, signed local trace conditions and signed functions give instead charXE±​=(Lp±​(E)), as proved in Pollack and Rubin's supersingular main-conjecture theorem. Thus the ordinary hypotheses and the analytic local correction are essential parts of the essay's main-conjecture statement.
