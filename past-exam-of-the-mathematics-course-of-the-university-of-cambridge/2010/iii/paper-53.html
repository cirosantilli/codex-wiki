Set c=1 and let H=a˙/a denote the proper-time Hubble parameter. The Friedmann equation is the expansion constraint: density drives expansion, while spatial curvature and the cosmological constant contribute separately. The Friedmann acceleration equation shows that the active gravitational density is ρ+3P; positive pressure gravitates, while positive vacuum energy accelerates expansion.
Subtracting the constraint from the acceleration equation gives H˙=−4πG(ρ+P)+k/a2. Differentiating the constraint gives 2HH˙=(8πG/3)ρ˙​+2kH/a2. Eliminate H˙ to obtain
ρ˙​+3H(ρ+P)=0​.
(1)
This cosmological continuity equation is local energy conservation. For a comoving volume V∝a3, it is d(ρV)=−PdV, the adiabatic first law: expansion does work against pressure.
Define the deceleration parameter q=−aa¨/a˙2, the critical density ρc​=3H2/(8πG), and the cosmological density parameters Ωm​=ρm​/ρc​ and ΩΛ​=Λ/(3H2). The dust-plus-vacuum acceleration equation then gives q=Ωm​/2−ΩΛ​. Dust conservation gives ρm​∝a−3, while ρΛ​=Λ/(8πG) is constant. At the acceleration transition, ρm​=2ρΛ​, so
1+ztr​=(0.32(0.7)​)1/3,ztr​≃0.671​.
(2)
For spatial flatness and a0​=1, the constraint today sets ΩΛ0​=1−Ωm0​. Thus H2=H02​[Ωm0​a−3+1−Ωm0​]. Put M=Ωm0​, L=1−M>0 and y=a3/2. Then y˙​=(3H0​/2)M+Ly2​. Integrating from y(0)=0 gives arsinh(yL/M​)=(3H0​/2)L​t, hence the flat dust-and-vacuum expansion

For small t, a≃[(3/2)H0​M​t]2/3, the matter-dominated law. At late times, a≃[M/(4L)]1/3eH0​L​t, approaching de Sitter spacetime. Negative spatial curvature adds a positive a−2 contribution to H2. This is subdominant to dust's a−3 at small a and to the constant vacuum term at large a, so it does not change either leading limit.
The model describes the transition from matter domination to the accelerated late-time expansion indicated by cosmological distance observations, with nearly flat geometry and vacuum-like dark energy. Its formal t→0 dust limit is not the actual earliest universe: radiation, neglected here, dominates then and gives a∝t1/2.

For an expanding universe, cosmic inflation requires a¨>0. Including any vacuum contribution in the total density and pressure, the acceleration equation gives ρ+3P<0​, or w=P/ρ<−1/3 for positive density. Equivalently the comoving Hubble radius (aH)−1=a˙−1 decreases. Since ∣Ωtotal​−1∣=∣k∣/(aH)2, prolonged acceleration suppresses curvature and addresses the flatness problem. A sufficiently long inflationary phase also stretches a region that was initially causally connected to encompass the observed region, addressing the horizon problem. The condition on acceleration must last long enough; an arbitrarily short interval is insufficient.
For a homogeneous canonical scalar field Φ with potential V(Φ),
ρΦ​=21​Φ˙2+V,PΦ​=21​Φ˙2−V,ρΦ​+3PΦ​=2(Φ˙2−V).
(4)

At temperatures above a few MeV, weak interactions such as n+νe​↔p+e− and n+e+↔p+νˉe​ maintain neutron-proton chemical equilibrium. Negligible lepton chemical potentials give the neutron-to-proton ratio req​=nn​/np​=exp[−Δ/(kB​T)], where Δ=(mn​−mp​)c2≃1.293MeV. At high temperature this ratio is nearly one.
During radiation domination, the interaction time grows as T−5, faster than the expansion time T−2. Their crossing therefore produces cosmological weak freeze-out. If Θ=T/(1010K), equating the displayed order-of-magnitude times gives Θf−5​∼g∗−1/2​Θf−2​, so
Tf​∼1010g∗1/6​ K,Tf​∝(Gg∗​)1/6Gweak−2/3​​.

After freeze-out, free neutrons decay while protons increase correspondingly. Define the neutron baryon fraction Xn​=nn​/(nn​+np​). In the instantaneous-freeze-out estimate,
Xn,f​=1+rf​rf​​,rf​=e−Δ/(kB​Tf​),Xn​(t)=Xn,f​e−(t−tf​)/τn​,

Nuclear burning initially waits at the deuterium bottleneck: although deuterium is bound by about 2.2MeV, the many photons per baryon can still photodissociate it. Roughly, ηb−1​e−BD​/(kB​TN​)∼1 sets the bottleneck temperature, with refinements from reaction rates and phase space. Around kB​TN​∼0.1MeV, deuterium survives, reactions through deuterium, tritium and helium-3 rapidly build tightly bound helium-4, and nearly all surviving neutrons are captured.
Thus nHe​≃nn​/2 and the primordial helium mass fraction is

The printed numerical time coefficients are schematic, not a precision prediction of this quarter fraction: their literal crossing with g∗​=10.75 gives kB​Tf​≃1.28MeV. Keeping physical weak-rate coefficients and the gradual freeze-out instead gives an effective freeze-out around 0.7–0.8MeV, followed by decay over a few minutes. The abundance formulas above explain the standard quarter fraction and its parameter dependence without treating the rounded time formulas as exact nuclear-rate data.

Reducing the gravitational constant decreases H at fixed temperature. The helium sensitivity to the expansion rate has two effects: weak interconversion persists to a lower freeze-out temperature, leaving fewer neutrons, and the universe takes longer to reach the deuterium-burning temperature, allowing more free-neutron decay after freeze-out. A thermally populated extra light neutrino species is relativistic during nucleosynthesis; its 0.001eV mass is negligible at MeV temperatures. One neutrino-antineutrino species adds (7/8)×2=7/4 to g∗​. Thus the expansion rate increases by 12.5/10.75​ and the freeze-out temperature by (12.5/10.75)1/6. The helium yield is primarily neutron-limited, so small changes in cosmological baryon density have only a weak effect compared with their effect on residual deuterium. Increasing the baryon-to-photon ratio makes deuterium destruction less effective relative to its formation and releases the deuterium bottleneck at a slightly higher temperature. f OurBigBook.com
At nucleosynthesis temperatures, 50GeV particles are nonrelativistic. Their equilibrium abundance is negligible because of Boltzmann suppression, so they do not behave as an extra relativistic species contributing its full degree count to g∗​. A stable thermal weakly interacting massive particle generally freezes out much earlier; a surviving relic population with the usual dark-matter abundance remains negligible compared with radiation during nucleosynthesis. In that standard relic scenario the helium abundance is essentially unchanged.
The mass and weak coupling alone do not specify the relic density. If an unusually abundant stable population contributes appreciably to the expansion density, it raises H at fixed radiation temperature and increases helium by the same freeze-out and survival mechanism as part (g). Decays or energetic annihilation products would require their own specified history. Thus a universal numerical change cannot be inferred from the mass alone.

Inflation produces the primordial comoving curvature perturbation while modes exit the Hubble radius. Its adiabatic super-Hubble conservation allows that initial amplitude to be carried through the end of inflation and subsequent changes of the background equation of state without solving each mode's full evolution at every intervening time. After Hubble re-entry, cosmological transfer functions relate it to the cosmic microwave background temperature and polarization fluctuations and to the matter power spectrum. The conserved curvature perturbation connects inflationary initial conditions to later cosmological observables. This bridge uses the adiabatic conditions stated in part (v); entropy perturbations can supply an additional evolving source.
