An integral curve of a vector field X through p is a smooth curve c:I→M, on an interval containing zero, satisfying c(0)=p and c˙(t)=Xc(t)​. The local flow is the smoothly varying family of these integral curves of a vector field:
ϕ:D⟶M,ϕt​(p)=cp​(t),ϕ0​(p)=p,∂t​ϕt​(p)=Xϕt​(p)​.
(1)
Here D is an open neighborhood of {0}×M; for the maximal local flow, its fibre over p is the maximal existence interval of cp​. Local existence, uniqueness and smooth dependence for a smooth ordinary differential equation give this family. Uniqueness also gives ϕt+s​(p)=ϕt​(ϕs​(p)) whenever the relevant curves and compositions are defined, and ϕ−t​=ϕt−1​ on their common domains.
Reparametrizing the integral curves of a vector field gives
dtd​ϕ2t​(p)=2Xϕ2t​(p)​.
(2)
Thus the flow of 2X is ϕ​t​=ϕ2t​​, with domain {(t,p):(2t,p)∈D}.

A smooth rank-r vector bundle over a smooth manifold M consists of a smooth total space E, a smooth projection π:E→M, and an r-dimensional real vector space structure on each fibre Ep​=π−1(p). Each point has a neighborhood U with a vector bundle trivialization
τU​:π−1(U)∼​U×Rr
(13)
that is a diffeomorphism, commutes with projection to U, and is a linear isomorphism on every fibre. The overlap maps have the form (p,v)↦(p,h(p)v) with h:U∩V→GLr​(R) smooth. For a complex vector bundle, replace R by C and require complex-linear fibre maps.
A connection on a vector bundle is an R-linear map
∇:Γ(E)⟶Ω1(M;E),∇(fs)=df⊗s+f∇s,
(14)
where Γ(E) is the module of smooth sections, Ω1(M;E) consists of vector-bundle-valued differential forms of degree one, and f is a smooth function. Equivalently, evaluating on a vector field X gives operators ∇X​ with ∇fX​s=f∇X​s and ∇X​(fs)=X(f)s+f∇X​s. A connection on a vector bundle is local: its value at a point depends on the germ of the section of a vector bundle, so the same definition applies to local sections.

Realize the pullback vector bundle as
ϕ∗E={(p,v)∈M′×E:ϕ(p)=π(v)},π′(p,v)=p.
(15)
Its fibre over p is canonically Eϕ(p)​, with the same vector space operations. If a vector bundle trivialization over U⊂M is τU​(v)=(π(v),v), give the pullback vector bundle the local trivialization
TU​:(π′)−1(ϕ−1U)⟶ϕ−1U×Rr,TU​(p,v)=(p,v).
(16)
Declare these maps to be local homeomorphisms and use coordinate charts on M′ to obtain a smooth atlas on the total space. On overlaps the smooth transition maps are (p,w)↦(p,h(ϕ(p))w), hence smooth and fibrewise linear. They satisfy the same cocycle identities as the original transition maps. This gives a well-defined smooth vector bundle, with smooth projection and rank r. It also gives the subspace topology inherited from M′×E: inside each product ϕ−1(U)×π−1(U) the defining condition is the graph of ϕ in the base coordinates. Thus the total space is Hausdorff and second countable, as required for a smooth manifold.
For a smooth section of a vector bundle s over U, its pulled-back section is more precisely p↦(p,s(ϕ(p))). If s has component column u:U→Rr in the original vector bundle trivialization, its component column in TU​ is u∘ϕ, which is smooth. The pullback is a smooth rank-r vector bundle, and every local section pulls back smoothly.

The expression dϕ(X) is in general a vector field along a map, rather than a vector field on M. Its intended meaning in the required formula is pointwise evaluation of the vector-bundle-valued differential form ∇s:
(∇X′​(ϕ∗s))p​=(∇s)ϕ(p)​(dϕp​Xp​),
(17)
using the canonical identification (ϕ∗E)p​=Eϕ(p)​. This interpretation works for every smooth map, including a constant map or a map with self-intersections.
Choose a frame of a vector bundle over U, written as a row e=(e1​,…,er​). Define its connection one-form Ω by
∇(eu)=e(du+Ωu)
(18)
for component columns u. The pulled-back frame eϕ=(ϕ∗e1​,…,ϕ∗er​) is a frame of a vector bundle over ϕ−1U. For any component column v of smooth functions on that open set, define
∇′(eϕv)=eϕ(dv+(ϕ∗Ω)v).​

Ω=h−1Ωh+h−1dh.

The curvature form of a connection is the endomorphism-valued differential two-form defined by
R∇(X,Y)s=∇X​∇Y​s−∇Y​∇X​s−∇[X,Y]​s.
(22)
It is alternating in X,Y. To see its tensoriality, replacing X by fX produces an extra −Y(f)∇X​s from the second term and an extra +Y(f)∇X​s from [fX,Y]=f[X,Y]−Y(f)X, so these cancel. Alternation gives linearity over smooth functions in Y too. Replacing s by fs produces the additional coefficient (X(Yf)−Y(Xf)−[X,Y]f)s=0; all terms involving one derivative of s also cancel. Thus R∇ belongs to Ω2(M;EndE).
In the coefficient-column convention of part (b), expanding the definition gives
R∇(X,Y)(eu)=e(X(Ω(Y))−Y(Ω(X))−Ω([X,Y])+Ω(X)Ω(Y)−Ω(Y)Ω(X))u.
(23)
The first three terms are dΩ(X,Y), and the last two are (Ω∧Ω)(X,Y). This proves the Cartan curvature matrix equation F=dΩ+Ω∧Ω, with matrix multiplication combined with the wedge product of differential forms.
The pullback connection has matrix Ω′=ϕ∗Ω. Since the pullback of a differential form commutes with the exterior derivative and the wedge product of differential forms, its curvature form of a connection has matrix
F′=d(ϕ∗Ω)+(ϕ∗Ω)∧(ϕ∗Ω)=ϕ∗(dΩ+Ω∧Ω)=ϕ∗F.
(24)
Changes of frame of a vector bundle conjugate both sides by the same pulled-back transition matrix, so this identity is intrinsic. In fibre notation the answer is
Rp∇′​(u,v)=Rϕ(p)∇​(dϕp​u,dϕp​v),u,v∈Tp​M′.​
(25)
Both sides act on the same fibre Eϕ(p)​. No claim that dϕ preserves Lie brackets of vector fields for an arbitrary map is needed.

An oriented atlas is a smooth atlas whose smooth transition maps have positive Jacobian determinants at every point of their domains. Such an atlas supplies an orientation of a vector space on every tangent space, and these choices agree on overlaps.
Let ω be a nowhere-vanishing smooth top-degree differential form. In a coordinate chart write
ω=f(x)dx1∧⋯∧dxn.
(26)
The coefficient f is smooth and nowhere zero. Restrict the coordinate chart to connected neighborhoods; the intermediate value theorem makes the sign of f constant on each. If f is negative, replacing x1 by −x1 changes the sign of the coordinate volume form. Thus for n≥1 we can choose a covering of coordinate charts in each of which the coefficient is positive.
On an overlap of two such coordinate charts, the wedge product of differential forms transforms by
dx1∧⋯∧dxn=det(∂y∂x​)dy1∧⋯∧dyn.
(27)
Consequently fy​=fx​det(∂x/∂y). Both coefficients are positive, so the Jacobian determinant is positive, as is that of the inverse transition. A nowhere-vanishing top form therefore determines an oriented atlas. The zero-dimensional case has only zero-dimensional transitions, whose determinant is one, and is automatically orientable. Connectedness of M is not needed for the positive-dimensional construction; only the local sign choice matters.

The integral identity requires compactness of M, compact support of X, or suitable conditions eliminating flux at infinity. The printed question does not state such a hypothesis. We prove the Riemannian divergence theorem for a compact manifold, and more generally for a compactly supported smooth vector field on an oriented Riemannian manifold with boundary.
Write j:∂M↪M for the inclusion. Give ∂M the outward-normal-first boundary orientation, and let N be the outward unit normal vector. The Riemannian volume form of the induced Riemannian metric is
ωg​​=j∗(ιN​ωg​).
(31)
Indeed, if (e1​,…,en−1​) is a positively oriented orthonormal basis tangent to the boundary, (N,e1​,…,en−1​) is a positively oriented orthonormal basis in M. Both sides therefore evaluate to one on the boundary basis.
At a boundary point decompose X=g(X,N)N+X⊤, where X⊤ is tangent to ∂M. Evaluating on n−1 tangent vectors, the term with X⊤ vanishes: all n arguments of the alternating volume form then lie in the (n−1)-dimensional boundary tangent space. Thus the interior product of a differential form satisfies
j∗(ιX​ωg​)=g(X,N)ωg​​.
(32)
The general Stokes theorem for an oriented manifold with boundary states ∫M​dη=∫∂M​j∗η for any smooth differential form η of degree n−1 with compact support; compactness of M makes the support condition automatic. Applying it to η=ιX​ωg​ and using the defining equation for the divergence of a Riemannian vector field proves
∫M​(divX)ωg​=∫∂M​g(X,N)ωg​​.​
(33)
For a concrete failure without the extra hypothesis, take M=[0,∞) with Riemannian metric dx2 and X=(arctanx)∂x​. The divergence of a Riemannian vector field is divX=(1+x2)−1, so
∫M​divXdx=2π​,∫∂M​g(X,N)ωg​​=0,
(34)
because X vanishes at the only boundary point. Both integrals are finite. The missing π/2 is the limiting flux at infinity, so mere integrability of the divergence cannot repair the unrestricted assertion.

A subset Z of an m-dimensional smooth manifold M is an r-dimensional embedded submanifold if, for each p∈Z, some coordinate chart x:U→V⊂Rm containing p satisfies
x(U∩Z)=V∩(Rr×{0}m−r).
(35)
The induced topology on Z is the subspace topology, and the first r coordinates restricted to Z give its smooth atlas. To see their smooth transition maps agree, restrict the ambient smooth transitions to the coordinate planes; their inverses restrict in the same way. The inclusion then has injective differential of a smooth map and is a homeomorphism onto its image. Conversely this is the local coordinate description of a smooth smooth embedding. An embedded submanifold need not be a closed subset of M.

We use the following existence and uniqueness statement for parallel transport: for a smooth affine connection, a smooth curve γ:[0,1]→M, an initial time t0​ and a vector v∈Tγ(t0​)​M, there is a unique smooth vector field along a map V on the whole curve satisfying Dt​V=0 and V(t0​)=v. In a frame of a vector bundle this is the linear ordinary differential equation V˙=−Γ(γ˙​)V. Its smooth coefficient matrix is bounded on every compact time subinterval in a trivializing neighborhood, and the linear existence theorem gives a solution throughout that subinterval. A finite subdivision of [0,1] into such neighborhoods and uniqueness patch the solutions. No completeness hypothesis on M is needed.
For arbitrary fields U,V along a curve, the definition of the induced covariant derivative of the metric tensor gives
dtd​g(U,V)=(∇γ˙​​g)(U,V)+g(Dt​U,V)+g(U,Dt​V).
(45)
This can also be checked by differentiating gij​(γ(t))Ui(t)Vj(t) and substituting the formula in part (a). If ∇ is a metric connection and V is parallel, it follows that d(g(V,V))/dt=0. Positivity of the Riemannian metric makes ∣V∣=g(V,V)​ constant. Thus parallel transport preserves lengths.
Conversely assume every parallel field along every smooth curve has constant length. Fix p∈M and arbitrary x,v∈Tp​M. Choose a curve through p with velocity x at an interior time t0​. Explicitly, in a coordinate chart z centered at p, take γ(t)=z−1(h(t)dzp​(x)), where h(t0​)=0, h′(t0​)=1, and h has sufficiently small amplitude and support near t0​. A smooth bump function produces such h, so the curve is defined on all of [0,1] and remains inside the chart. By the stated parallel transport theorem there is a parallel field with V(t0​)=v. Evaluating the derivative of its squared length gives
0=dtd​g(V,V)​t0​​=(∇x​g)p​(v,v).
(46)
For fixed x, the bilinear form Bx​(v,w)=(∇x​g)p​(v,w) is symmetric. Its diagonal values vanish, so polarization identity gives
2Bx​(v,w)=Bx​(v+w,v+w)−Bx​(v,v)−Bx​(w,w)=0.
(47)
Since p,x,v,w were arbitrary, ∇g=0. A connection is metric-compatible if and only if all parallel fields have constant length. Polarization also shows that its parallel transport preserves all inner products, not just lengths.

First verify the difference of affine connections is a tensor. Linearity over smooth functions in X follows from the affine connection axioms. In Y, the two derivative terms cancel:
A(X,fY)=∇X​(fY)−∇X′​(fY)=fA(X,Y).
(48)
Thus A is a smooth (1,2)-tensor field, and Ap​(u,v) depends only on the tangent vectors u,v at p. For any smooth curve, its two covariant accelerations satisfy
Dt​γ˙​−Dt′​γ˙​=A(γ˙​,γ˙​).
(49)
If A(X,Y)=−A(Y,X), its diagonal values vanish. The two geodesic equations are therefore identical, and the connections have the same parametrized geodesics.
For necessity, the required local geodesic existence theorem is this: for every smooth affine connection, p∈M and v∈Tp​M, a unique geodesic exists on some interval about zero with γ(0)=p and γ˙​(0)=v. Indeed the geodesic equation is the smooth first-order ordinary differential equation on position and velocity
x˙k=vk,v˙k=−Γkij​(x)vivj,
(50)
so local existence and uniqueness apply. If the two connections have the same parametrized geodesics, apply the acceleration identity to this geodesic at zero to obtain Ap​(v,v)=0 for every p,v. Expanding Ap​(u+v,u+v)=0 gives Ap​(u,v)+Ap​(v,u)=0. Therefore
same parametrized geodesics⟺A(X,Y)=−A(Y,X).​
(51)
This is the criterion that parametrized geodesics determine the symmetric part of an affine connection. It concerns equality with the same affine parameters. Agreement merely of unparametrized images is weaker: adding α(X)Y+α(Y)X to a connection, for a smooth one-form α, changes acceleration by a multiple of the velocity, which can be absorbed by reparametrization. Such a difference need not be antisymmetric.
Finally, since ∇X′​Y=∇X​Y−A(X,Y), expand the derivative of the metric tensor:
(∇X′​g)(Y,Z)=X(g(Y,Z))−g(∇X′​Y,Z)−g(Y,∇X′​Z)=(∇X​g)(Y,Z)+g(A(X,Y),Z)+g(Y,A(X,Z)).
(52)
Under the assumed metric compatibility of ∇, the first term is zero. Consequently
∇′g=0⟺g(A(X,Y),Z)=−g(Y,A(X,Z))for all X,Y,Z.​
(53)
Equivalently, for each fixed X, the endomorphism A(X,⋅) is skew-adjoint for the Riemannian metric. This metric criterion does not require the two connections to have the same geodesics.
