Compose permutations from right to left. Tracking the images gives 1↦2↦1, 3↦4↦3, and 5↦5, so
(123)(234)=(12)(34).​
(4)
Two transpositions give sign of a permutation +1, proving that this element belongs to the alternating group A5​.
Its conjugacy class consists of all double transpositions. Conjugation merely relabels the letters, so no other cycle type can occur. Conversely, choose a permutation h∈S5​ relabelling the two pairs into any desired two pairs. If h is odd, replace it by h(12): the transposition (12) centralizes (12)(34), so this replacement is even and gives the same conjugate. Thus every double transposition is reached by conjugation inside A5​.
The complete list of fifteen elements, grouped by their fixed letter, is
​(12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23),(12)(35), (13)(25), (15)(23),(12)(45), (14)(25), (15)(24),(13)(45), (14)(35), (15)(34),(23)(45), (24)(35), (25)(34).​
(5)
There are five choices of fixed letter and three pairings of the other four letters, confirming the conjugacy class size 15.

For a finite group action, the orbit-stabilizer theorem states
∣G∣=∣Gx∣∣Gx​∣,Gx​={g∈G:gx=x}.
(15)
The bijection gGx​↦gx explains the formula by identifying cosets with the group orbit.
The cube rotation group acts transitively on its six faces. A rotation fixing one face fixes its center and its outward normal, so its axis is the line joining that center to the opposite face's center. The allowed rotations are the four multiples of π/2 about this axis. Thus the stabilizer subgroup is C4​, and
∣G∣=6⋅4=24.​
(16)
The face-pair orbits of cube rotations are the ordered equal-face pairs, opposite-face pairs, and adjacent-face pairs. These relations are preserved by every cube rotation. The first two families are transitive because the face action is transitive and the opposite face is uniquely determined. For the third, first send the first face to a prescribed face, then use its four quarter-turns to send the neighboring second face to any chosen neighbor. Thus there are exactly three orbits, of sizes
6,6,24.​
(17)
Their sizes sum to 36=∣X×X∣.

A subgroup N≤G is a normal subgroup if gNg−1=N for every g∈G.
Place the cube with its opposite-face axes along the coordinate axes. Consider
N={I, diag(1,−1,−1), diag(−1,1,−1), diag(−1,−1,1)}.
(18)
The three nonidentity elements are the half-turns about those axes. Multiplying two different ones gives the third, and each squares to the identity, so this is a Klein four-group of order four.
Every cube rotation permutes the three pairs of opposite faces. The associated group homomorphism G→S3​ has exactly N as its kernel: fixing all three face-axis pairs allows only independent sign changes of the three coordinate directions, with determinant one. Hence the kernel of the cube face-axis action is a normal subgroup, and
N⊴G,∣N∣=4.​
(19)

The finite-group Lagrange theorem states that ∣H∣ divides ∣G∣ for every subgroup H≤G, since the disjoint left cosets all have size ∣H∣. In particular, the order of an element divides the group order.
If ∣G∣=p and g=1, the cyclic subgroup ⟨g⟩ has order greater than one and dividing p, so it has order p. Therefore every group of prime order is cyclic.
Let G now be abelian of order p2. If an element has order p2, it generates G, giving G≅Cp2​. Otherwise every nonidentity element has order p. Choose a=1 and b∈/⟨a⟩, which is possible because ∣⟨a⟩∣=p<p2. Commutativity makes
Φ:Cp​×Cp​→G,(i,j)↦aibj
(20)
a group homomorphism. If aibj=1 and j=0(modp), choose t with jt≡1(modp). Then b=(bj)t∈⟨a⟩, a contradiction. Hence j=0 and then i=0. The homomorphism is injective and both groups have order p2, so it is an isomorphism. This proves the abelian group of prime-square order classification:
G≅Cp2​orCp​×Cp​.​
(21)
Here D12​ denotes the dihedral group of order twelve, as specified in the paper: the hexagon's rotation through π/3 has order six. The possible cycle types in A4​ are the identity, three-cycles and double transpositions, of orders one, three and two. It has no element of order six. A group isomorphism preserves element orders, so
D12​≅A4​.​
(22)

By transitivity, choose h∈G with hy=x. If gy=y, then
(hgh−1)x=hgy=hy=x.
(23)
Thus hgh−1∈B. This proves the conjugation assertion directly from the transitive group action.
For the complex special linear group in dimension two, the Möbius transformation of a matrix g acts by z↦(az+b)/(cz+d), with ad−bc=1. It fixes infinity exactly when c=0, so its stabilizer subgroup is
B={(a0​ba−1​):a∈C×, b∈C}.​
(24)
We distinguish the cases to avoid dividing by a vanishing coefficient.
If c=0, infinity is not fixed, and the finite fixed points solve
cz2+(d−a)z−b=0.
(25)
Neither root is the pole −d/c: substituting that value gives (ad−bc)/c=1/c=0. The fixed-point set is therefore
{2ca−d+(a+d)2−4​​, 2ca−d−(a+d)2−4​​},​
(26)
with the two entries coinciding when the discriminant is zero. The discriminant identity uses (a−d)2+4bc=(a+d)2−4.
If c=0 and a=d, the fixed points are {∞,b/(d−a)}. If c=0, a=d and b=0, only infinity is fixed. If c=0, a=d and b=0, the determinant condition gives g=±I and every point is fixed. Both central matrices induce the identity Möbius transformation.
Every case has a fixed point, because a quadratic over the complex numbers has a root. The action is transitive: any finite z0​ is sent to infinity by
h=(01​−1−z0​​)∈SL2​(C),
(27)
and infinity already needs no change. Applying the first argument to a fixed point proves every element of SL2​(C) is conjugate to an element of B.

For each g∈G, conjugation sends a subgroup H to gHg−1, a subgroup of the same order and hence still proper. Identity conjugation fixes every subgroup, and
g1​(g2​Hg2−1​)g1−1​=(g1​g2​)H(g1​g2​)−1.
(28)
These verify the group action laws.
The stabilizer subgroup of B for this conjugation action is its normalizer NG​(B). Since B≤NG​(B), the orbit-stabilizer theorem gives the number of distinct conjugates as
m=[G:NG​(B)]≤[G:B].​
(29)
All these conjugate subgroups contain the identity, so counting that element only once gives
​⋃g∈G​gBg−1​≤1+m(∣B∣−1)≤1+[G:B](∣B∣−1)=∣G∣−[G:B]+1<∣G∣.
(30)
The last inequality uses the properness of B, so [G:B]>1. There is therefore an element outside this union, which is not conjugate to any element of B. This proves that conjugates of a proper subgroup do not cover a finite group, including the case B={1}.

A Cartesian second-rank tensor has components in every orthonormal Cartesian frame that transform according to
Aij′​=Qip​Qjq​Apq​,A′=QAQT,
(31)
when vector components transform as x′=Qx for an orthogonal matrix Q. Repeated indices are summed. If A=B in one frame, their transformed difference is Q(A−B)QT=0, so their equality holds in every such frame, at the same physical point.
Now impose the scalar contraction hypothesis on C. For every tensor A, invariance in the two frames says
Cij′​Qip​Qjq​Apq​=Cpq​Apq​.
(32)
At a fixed point, choose the components of A to be each matrix unit in turn; each is an admissible tensor once its components in other frames are transformed. Thus every coefficient of Apq​ agrees, yielding QTC′Q=C, or
C′=QCQT.​
(33)
Therefore C is necessarily a Cartesian second-rank tensor. This is the scalar contraction test for a Cartesian tensor. Testing all tensors, rather than only symmetric tensors, is essential to the proof.

For a bounded volume Ω with piecewise smooth boundary and a continuously differentiable vector field on a neighborhood of its closure, the divergence theorem states
∮∂Ω​F⋅ndS=∫Ω​∇⋅FdV,
(45)
where n is the outward unit normal.
The boundary here consists of a paraboloid cap over the unit disk, a cylindrical wall of height one, and the bottom disk. Their joined surface encloses
Ω={(x,y,z):x2+y2≤1, 0≤z≤3−2(x2+y2)}.
(46)
The cap meets the wall at r=1,z=1, and the wall meets the base at r=1,z=0. The sketches show each boundary piece and their closed union.
Differentiate the field components to find the divergence:
∇⋅F=(y+6x5)+(−y+8y7)+1=1+6x5+8y7.
(47)

For a spherically symmetric potential, the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity becomes
r21​drd​(r2ϕ′(r))=4πGρ(r).
(49)

For the dyadic shells, multiply their constant mass density by their spherical volumes. The nth shell contributes
Mn​​=34π​ρ0​2n−1(23−3n−2−3n)=314πρ0​​4−n.​
