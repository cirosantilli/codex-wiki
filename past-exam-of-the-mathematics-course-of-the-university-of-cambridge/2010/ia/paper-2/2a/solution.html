The first-order chain rule, with x,y regarded as functions of u,v, is
gu​=xu​fx​+yu​fy​,gv​=xv​fx​+yv​fy​.
(8)
Apply the product rule when differentiating the first identity in v, and apply the chain rule to fx​,fy​. Equality of mixed partial derivatives for a C2 function gives
guv​=xu​xv​fxx​+(xu​yv​+xv​yu​)fxy​+yu​yv​fyy​+xuv​fx​+yuv​fy​.
(9)
Thus H=xuv​, K=yuv​​. These terms record the second derivatives of the coordinate transformation, not of f.
For the given transformation,
xu​=v,xv​=u,yu​=v1​,yv​=−v2u​,xuv​=1,yuv​=−v21​.
(10)
The mixed-derivative coefficient is zero, while xu​xv​=x, yu​yv​=−y2/x, and 1/v2=y/x. The required partial differential equation is therefore exactly guv​=0. Integrating first in u and then in v on a coordinate rectangle gives
g(u,v)=A(u)+B(v),​
(11)
where A,B are arbitrary twice continuously differentiable functions. The Jacobian determinant of the transformation is −2u/v, so this calculation uses patches with u,v=0. On a positive-quadrant patch we can take u=xy​ and v=x/y​, obtaining
f(x,y)=A(xy​)+B(x/y​).​
(12)
Changing branches merely redefines the arbitrary functions.
For completeness, the original equation also makes sense in quadrants with xy<0, where that particular real square-root transformation is unavailable. Multiplying the equation by x gives
(x∂x​)2f−(y∂y​)2f=0.
(13)
On any quadrant with xy=0, put a=log∣x∣, b=log∣y∣; then set ξ=a+b, η=a−b. The equation becomes 4fξη​=0. Thus an equivalent general local answer on every such quadrant is
f(x,y)=Φ(log∣xy∣)+Ψ(log∣x/y∣).​
(14)
Here Φ,Ψ are arbitrary C2 functions on the relevant intervals. If a solution is required across y=0, its quadrant expressions must extend with matching derivatives; on that line the equation itself imposes xfxx​(x,0)+fx​(x,0)=0, so its trace is a0​log∣x∣+b0​ on each interval with x=0. The displayed coordinate transformations do not assert invertibility at y=0.
