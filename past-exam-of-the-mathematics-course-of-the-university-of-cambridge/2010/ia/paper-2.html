Substitution of yn​=rn gives the characteristic equation of a linear recurrence
r3−3r+2=(r−1)2(r+2)=0.
(1)
The repeated characteristic root of a linear recurrence 1 supplies the solutions 1 and n, while the simple root −2 supplies (−2)n. They are linearly independent: their values at three consecutive indices have nonzero determinant. A third-order linear recurrence relation is uniquely determined by three consecutive initial values, so these three solutions span its solution space. The general solution is
yn​=A+Bn+C(−2)n.​
(2)

At a critical point p of a twice continuously differentiable function, the linear term in the Taylor expansion vanishes:
f(p+h)=f(p)+21​hTHh+o(∥h∥2).
(52)
The real symmetric Hessian matrix has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. If all its eigenvalues are positive, its quadratic term is bounded below by a positive multiple of ∥h∥2, giving a strict local minimum. If all are negative, it gives a strict local maximum. If positive and negative eigenvalues both occur, directions along their eigenvectors give values on both sides of f(p), so the point is a saddle point of a scalar function. The non-singularity assumption excludes zero eigenvalues; with them the quadratic test may be inconclusive and higher-order terms are needed.

The future levels are independent of the observed maximum. Given Yn​=z, the first k−1 future levels must be at most z, and the kth must exceed z. Each non-overflow has probability F(z) and an overflow has probability 1−F(z). Therefore
P(τ=k∣Yn​=z)=F(z)k−1[1−F(z)],k≥1.​
(68)
This is a geometric distribution of parameter 1−F(z) when F(z)<1. Since Yn​ has a continuous distribution, conditioning on an exact value is understood via a regular conditional distribution, rather than division by P(Yn​=z)=0. The formula specifies its usual version. If F(z)=1 there is no possible overflow at that threshold, but such thresholds have probability zero for the random observed maximum.
