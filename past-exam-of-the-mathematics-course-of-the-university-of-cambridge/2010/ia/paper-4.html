The Fermat little theorem states that if p is a prime number, then ap≡a(modp) for every integer a. Equivalently, for p∤a, ap−1≡1(modp).
To prove it when p∤a, multiplication by a permutes the nonzero residues 1,…,p−1. Indeed, ai≡aj(modp) implies p∣a(i−j), and the Euclid lemma implies i=j in this range. Multiplying all these residues gives
ap−1(p−1)!≡(p−1)!(modp).
(27)
None of the factors of (p−1)! is divisible by p, so the factorial has a modular inverse. Cancelling it proves ap−1≡1, hence ap≡a. When p∣a, the latter identity has both sides zero, completing the proof.
For an odd prime number p=5, the numbers 10,p are coprime. The Fermat little theorem gives 10p−1≡1(modp), and consequently
p∣10j(p−1)−1for every j≥1.​

A set is countable if it admits an injective function into N, equivalently if it is finite or can be listed in a sequence. The empty set is included; an infinite countable set has a bijection with N.
Every rational number has a representation p/q with p∈Z and q≥1. For each k≥1, there are only finitely many such pairs with ∣p∣+q=k. List these finite levels successively, ordering each level by p, and discard repeated fractions when they first recur. Every rational number appears in some finite level, proving Q is countable​.
For the uncountability proof, suppose all numbers in (0,1) could be listed as r1​,r2​,…. Choose each decimal expansion not to end in an infinite string of nines, and denote its jth digit by dij​. Set bi​=1 when dii​=1, and bi​=2 otherwise. Then the decimal
s=0.b1​b2​b3​⋯
(36)
defines a number in (0,1) with a unique such expansion, since its digits are only 1 and 2. It differs from ri​ at digit i for every i, contradicting the enumeration. This Cantor diagonal argument proves R is uncountable​ because its subset (0,1) already is.
For the union of two countable sets C,D, take injective functions ιC​:C→N and ιD​:D→N. The map
ι(x)={2ιC​(x),2ιD​(x)+1,​x∈C,x∈D∖C​
(37)
is injective: it is injective within each branch, and their images have different parity. Thus C∪D is countable​, regardless of overlap.
The property of A says that both A and its complement are dense subsets of the real line. Both possible cardinalities occur. To establish the needed density directly, given x∈R and ε>0, choose an integer q>1/ε. Then ⌊qx⌋/q is rational and differs from x by less than 1/q<ε. For an irrational approximation, choose a rational r within ε/2 of x and a positive integer q large enough that 2​/q<ε/2. The number r+2​/q is irrational and lies within ε of x; irrationality follows from that of 2​, since otherwise subtracting r and multiplying by q would make 2​ rational. To recall the latter fact, a reduced fraction p/q=2​ would satisfy p2=2q2, forcing p even and then q even, contrary to coprimality. Therefore
A=Q is a countable example,A=R∖Q is an uncountable example.​
(38)
The second set is uncountable because, if it were countable, its union with Q would make R countable. The same two density arguments give the required approximations from A and from its complement in either example.
Finally, the condition on B makes every one of its points an isolated point, so B is a discrete subset of the real line. Enumerate all rational intervals (pj​,qj​) with pj​<qj​; enumerate pairs of indices of the rational list by increasing sum and keep those whose endpoints have the required order. For each b∈B, its isolating neighbourhood contains a rational interval (pj​,qj​) containing b and no other point of B. Assign to b the least such index j. Two distinct points cannot receive the same index, because that interval would then contain two members of B. This gives an injective function B→N and proves that discrete subsets of the real line are countable:
B is countable.​
(39)
Using the least index makes the assignment explicit and requires no arbitrary selection of an uncountable family of intervals.
