Define the change-of-basis matrices by expressing each new basis vector in the corresponding old basis:
bi′​=∑k=1n​Bki​bk​,cj′​=∑ℓ=1m​Cℓj​cℓ​.
(1)
Thus B is n×n, C is m×m, and both are invertible matrices: a linear combination of their columns is zero exactly when the same combination of the new basis vectors is zero. For any vector v, its coordinate columns therefore satisfy [v]b​=B[v]b′​; likewise [w]c​=C[w]c′​.
The matrix representation of a linear map gives
C[Φ(v)]c′​=[Φ(v)]c​=A[v]b​=AB[v]b′​.
(2)
Multiplying by the matrix inverse of C and comparing with [Φ(v)]c′​=A′[v]b′​ for every v proves
A′=C−1AB.​
(3)
The domain change of basis acts on the right, while the codomain change of basis acts on the left through its inverse.

A power series has radius of convergence R when it converges absolutely for every ∣z∣<R and diverges for every ∣z∣>R. Its behaviour on ∣z∣=R is not specified by this definition. When R=0, it converges at the centre and diverges at every nonzero point; an infinite radius of convergence means absolute convergence at every finite z.
Suppose, towards a contradiction, that ∣an​zn∣≤M for all n at some ∣z∣>R. Choose a complex number w with R<∣w∣<∣z∣. Then
∣an​wn∣=∣an​zn∣(∣z∣∣w∣​)n≤Mqn,q=∣z∣∣w∣​<1.
(10)

∣z∣>R ⟹ (∣an​zn∣)n≥0​ is unbounded.​

One precise version of the Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder is as follows. Let n≥0, let f and its derivatives through order n be continuous on the closed interval between distinct points a and x, and suppose f(n+1) exists throughout its interior. Then some ξ strictly between a and x satisfies
f(x)=k=0∑n​k!f(k)(a)​(x−a)k+(n+1)!f(n+1)(ξ)​(x−a)n+1.​
(56)
Here the endpoint derivatives are understood as restrictions of derivatives defined near the interval, or as their continuous one-sided extensions. No continuity of f(n+1) is required. For x=a the equality is immediate with zero remainder.
To prove the formula, write the Taylor polynomial as Pn​(t)=∑k=0n​f(k)(a)(t−a)k/k! and define
K=(x−a)n+1f(x)−Pn​(x)​,h(t)=f(t)−Pn​(t)−K(t−a)n+1.
(57)
Then h(a)=h(x)=0, and h(j)(a)=0 for j=1,…,n. The Rolle theorem gives a zero ξ1​ of h′ strictly between a and x. If n≥1, apply the Rolle theorem to h′ between a and ξ1​, using h′(a)=0, to get a zero ξ2​ of h′′. Repeating produces a point ξn+1​ strictly between a and x with h(n+1)(ξn+1​)=0. Each application is justified by continuity on the relevant closed interval and differentiability in its interior; the same construction works when x<a.
Since Pn​ has degree at most n,
0=h(n+1)(ξn+1​)=f(n+1)(ξn+1​)−(n+1)!K.
(58)
Substituting this value of K into its definition proves the displayed Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder.
