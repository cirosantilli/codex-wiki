A power series has radius of convergence R when it converges absolutely for every ∣z∣<R and diverges for every ∣z∣>R. Its behaviour on ∣z∣=R is not specified by this definition. When R=0, it converges at the centre and diverges at every nonzero point; an infinite radius of convergence means absolute convergence at every finite z.
Suppose, towards a contradiction, that ∣an​zn∣≤M for all n at some ∣z∣>R. Choose a complex number w with R<∣w∣<∣z∣. Then
∣an​wn∣=∣an​zn∣(∣z∣∣w∣​)n≤Mqn,q=∣z∣∣w∣​<1.

The comparison test for series with the convergent geometric series ∑Mqn yields absolute convergence at w, contradicting the definition of R. Thus terms of a power series are unbounded outside its convergence disc:
∣z∣>R ⟹ (∣an​zn∣)n≥0​ is unbounded.​
(11)
For the given power series, the ratio test gives, when z=0,
∣z∣n/n3∣z∣n+1/(n+1)3​=∣z∣(n+1n​)3⟶∣z∣.

Hence it converges absolutely for ∣z∣<1 and its terms fail to tend to zero for ∣z∣>1 (the ratios are eventually larger than some number greater than 1). Therefore R=1​. In fact, on ∣z∣=1 it also converges absolutely by comparison with the convergent p-series ∑n−3.
