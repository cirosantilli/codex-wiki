Let p first be an arbitrary degree-n polynomial with leading coefficient an​=0. Choose R so large that on ∣z∣=R,
∣p(z)−an​zn∣<∣an​∣Rn.
(49)
The homotopy an​zn+s[p(z)−an​zn], 0≤s≤1, never meets zero on that circle. Hence the image curve p(Reiθ) has winding number n, the same as an​Rneinθ. The argument principle counts precisely n zeros inside, with multiplicity, since there are no poles. Choose R large enough that the same leading-term domination holds for every ∣z∣≥R; the lower-term ratios decrease with radius, so this also excludes all exterior zeros. This proves the fundamental theorem of algebra with its multiplicity count. A degree-zero nonzero polynomial has no zeros.
Now use the specified bound for the monic quartic. Compare Γ with the closed curve
γ0​(t)={R4e4πit,R4,​0≤t≤1/2,1/2≤t≤1.​
(50)
Its first piece travels once counterclockwise around a circle, and its second stays at the endpoint, so its winding number is one. On the first piece, ∣Γ−γ0​∣<R4. On the second, both p(R) and p(iR) differ from R4 by less than R4; every convex combination of those errors also has modulus less than R4. Thus the quoted winding number stability result gives
n(Γ,0)=1.​
(51)
For real coefficients define q(y)=y4−by2+d. It has no real zeros and positive leading coefficient, so q(y)>0 for every real y, including d=q(0)>0. Therefore
p(iy)=q(y)+i(cy−ay3)
(52)
lies strictly in the right half-plane for every real y. Also p(x)>0 for x≥0: there are no zeros there, and the polynomial is positive for sufficiently large positive x. These facts exclude roots on the positive real and imaginary axes.
Consider the positively oriented quarter-disk boundary: first the arc from R to iR, then the imaginary axis to zero, then the real axis back to R. Under p, its last two pieces form a path from p(iR) to p(R) entirely within the right half-plane. The chord closing Γ also lies there. Since this half-plane is convex and excludes zero, deforming one closing path into the other changes no winding number. Thus the full image of the quarter-disk boundary has winding number one. By the argument principle there is exactly one root inside that quarter-disk, counted with multiplicity.
Finally, all polynomial roots lie in ∣z∣<R under the given strict coefficient bound: for ∣z∣≥R, the sum of lower terms divided by ∣z∣4 is smaller than one. Hence
exactly one zero lies in the open first quadrant.​
(53)
Its multiplicity is one. This is the quarter-sector winding test for a real quartic; it accounts for the axis exclusions and does not replace the sector boundary by a chord without justifying the deformation.
