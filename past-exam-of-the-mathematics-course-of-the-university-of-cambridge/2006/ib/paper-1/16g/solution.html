Take zero electric potential at infinity and put K=(4πε0​)−1. By spherical symmetry and Gauss's law, the electric field is radial, with magnitude determined by the total enclosed electric charge. Thus, away from the idealized shell surfaces,
E(r)=Kq⎩⎨⎧​0,er​/r2,−er​/r2,2er​/r2,​0≤r<a,a<r<b,b<r<c,r>c.​​
(49)
Integrating Er​=−dΦ/dr from infinity, or adding the three spherical-shell potentials, gives
Φ(r)=Kq⎩⎨⎧​a−1−2b−1+3c−1,r−1−2b−1+3c−1,−r−1+3c−1,2r−1,​r≤a,a≤r≤b,b≤r≤c,r≥c.​​
(50)
The expressions agree at each interface, so the potential is continuous everywhere, including the origin. The electric field has different interior and exterior limits at each infinitely thin charged surface, with jump equal to surface charge density divided by ε0​. An ideal sheet has no single classical field value on the sheet itself; the displayed adjacent limits specify the field there. In conducting material of nonzero thickness the internal field is zero.
The electrostatic energy is the integral of ε0​∣E∣2/2. There is no contribution from r<a, and spherical integration gives
U=8πε0​q2​[∫ab​r2dr​+∫bc​r2dr​+4∫c∞​r2dr​]=8πε0​q2​(a1​+c3​).​
(51)
The dependence on b cancels because the magnitude of enclosed electric charge is ∣q∣ on both sides of the middle shell, even though the field direction changes.
