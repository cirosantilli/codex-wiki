Sylvester's law of inertia says that any real quadratic form has coordinates in which it is a sum of p positive squares minus m negative squares, with z unused coordinates, and that the numbers (p,m,z) are invariant under every invertible linear change of coordinates. Its rank is p+m; its signature of a quadratic form is commonly p−m, with (p,m) also called its signature pair.
The symmetric matrix here is J−I, where J=11T and 1=(1,…,1)T. On the line spanned by 1, J−I has eigenvalue n−1. If ∑i​xi​=0, then Jx=0, so that the orthogonal complement has eigenvalue −1 with multiplicity n−1. Thus the off-diagonal all-ones quadratic form has
rankq=n,(p,m,z)=(1,n−1,0),σ=2−n(n>1).​
(1)
For n=1 the form vanishes, so rank and scalar signature are both zero, with inertia (0,0,1). Including this case is necessary because the distinguished eigenvalue then becomes zero.

A Euclidean domain is a commutative integral domain R with a function δ:R∖{0}→Z≥0​ such that, for a,b∈R with b=0, there are q,r∈R satisfying a=qb+r, where either r=0 or δ(r)<δ(b). One may also impose the usual monotonicity δ(a)≤δ(ab) for nonzero a,b; the example below has it.
The integers form a Euclidean domain that is not a field. Use δ(a)=∣a∣. Integer division by ∣b∣ supplies a remainder with 0≤r<∣b∣, and changing the sign of the quotient handles negative b. The integers are an integral domain, while 2 has no multiplicative inverse in them. This verifies both the Euclidean property and the failure to be a field.

The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain applies because every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain. In invariant-factor form, every finitely generated module M over such a domain R has a decomposition
M≅Rr⊕R/(d1​)⊕⋯⊕R/(ds​),d1​∣d2​∣⋯∣ds​,​
