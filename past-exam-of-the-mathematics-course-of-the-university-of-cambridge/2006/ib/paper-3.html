Take the integral domain R=Z[−5​], embedded in C. It exhibits failure of unique factorization in the ring of integers generated by square root of minus five:
6=2⋅3=(1+−5​)(1−−5​).​
(1)
Here is why these are genuinely distinct factorizations into irreducible elements. The multiplicative algebraic norm N(a+b−5​)=a2+5b2 is a nonnegative integer and equals one only for ±1, which are therefore the only units. Neither two nor three is a value of N: if b=0 the value is at least five, and if b=0 it is a square. A factorization of 2 into nonunits would require norms two and two; one of 3 would require norms three and three; one of 1±−5​ would require norms two and three. All are impossible. Thus all four factors are irreducible. The factors of norms four and nine cannot be associate elements of those of norm six, and 1+−5​ and 1−−5​ are not related by a sign. Therefore R is an integral domain which is not a unique factorization domain.

If an open set contains an even integer, invariance under every even translation makes it contain all even integers. Similarly, containing an odd integer makes it contain all odd integers. Conversely each parity class is invariant under those translations. Thus the proposed open sets are exactly
∅,2Z,2Z+1,Z.​
(9)
They form a partition topology. Both the empty and whole sets are included, and any union or finite intersection is again a union of parity classes. Hence this is a topological space. It is not the discrete topology: no singleton is open.

Use the usual commutative unital-ring convention for Noetherian rings. A ring is Noetherian exactly when every ideal is finitely generated, equivalently when ascending chains of ideals stabilize. Indeed, the union of an ascending chain is an ideal; finitely many generators all lie in a single chain member. Conversely, if an ideal cannot be finitely generated, adjoining a new element at each stage constructs a strictly ascending chain.
First assume R[X] is Noetherian. Evaluation at zero gives a surjective ring homomorphism R[X]→R with kernel (X). A quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian: lift an ideal to the source, take finitely many generators there, and map those generators down. Thus R≅R[X]/(X) is Noetherian.
For the converse, assume R is Noetherian and take any ideal I⊆R[X]. For d≥0, let Jd​ be the set of coefficients of Xd in elements of I of degree at most d. It is an ideal of R, since sums and scalar multiples of such polynomials stay in I. Multiplication by X shows Jd​⊆Jd+1​. The ascending chain stabilizes, say at N.
For each 0≤d≤N, choose finitely many generators ad,j​ of Jd​ and corresponding fd,j​∈I of degree at most d having coefficient ad,j​ at Xd. Discard zero generators. We claim these finitely many polynomials generate I. If a nonzero f∈I has degree m≤N, express its leading coefficient as ∑j​rj​am,j​ and subtract ∑j​rj​fm,j​. The degree decreases. If m>N, use Jm​=JN​, express the leading coefficient using the aN,j​, and subtract
∑j​rj​Xm−NfN,j​.
(38)
Again the leading term cancels and the degree decreases. Induction on degree, including degree zero, expresses every f in the claimed ideal. Therefore every ideal of R[X] is finitely generated, proving the Hilbert basis theorem and
R[X] is Noetherian⟺R is Noetherian.​
(39)
The argument is the bounded-degree coefficient proof of Hilbert basis theorem; it never divides by a leading coefficient and therefore does not require R to be an integral domain. With the analogous left-ideal convention the same proof applies to left-Noetherian rings with a central indeterminate.

In a zero-sum game, an entry aij​ is the net amount received by the row player when the players choose actions i,j; the column player receives its negative. A mixed strategy p has nonnegative entries summing to one. Against column strategy q, expected row payoff is pTAq. An optimal row strategy maximizes its guaranteed payoff minq​pTAq; an optimal column strategy minimizes maxp​pTAq.
The betting payoff must subtract the player's own stake: a winner receives both stakes but has already paid its own. Its net gain is the opponent's stake, and a loser loses its own. Ties give zero. Thus the row-payoff matrix is
