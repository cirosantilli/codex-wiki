1H OurBigBook.com
Let u1​,…,um​ and v1​,…,vn​ be two finite bases of the vector space V. We prove the underlying exchange argument rather than assuming that dimension is already well-defined.
More generally, suppose u1​,…,um​ are linearly independent and v1​,…,vn​ form a spanning set. Starting with the spanning list of v's, replace one of its members by u1​. Indeed, express u1​ as a linear combination of that list; at least one coefficient is nonzero, so we can solve for its corresponding vj​ and retain a spanning list. Inductively, suppose the spanning list now consists of u1​,…,ur​ and n−r of the original v's. Express ur+1​ in that list. Some coefficient of a remaining v must be nonzero, since otherwise ur+1​ would lie in the span of the preceding u's, contradicting linear independence. Solve for that v and replace it by ur+1​.
If m>n, after n replacements the spanning list consists of u1​,…,un​, forcing un+1​ to lie in their span, again a contradiction. Thus every finite linearly independent list has at most as many members as every finite spanning set. This proves the Steinitz exchange lemma in the form required here. Applying it to the two bases in both orders gives m≤n and n≤m, hence m=n. The zero vector space has only the empty basis, which also fits the argument.

We first justify the maximum in the proposed supremum norm. If a null sequence x is nonzero, choose an index j with ∣xj​∣>0. All sufficiently late terms have absolute value smaller than ∣xj​∣/2, since xi​→0. Hence the largest absolute value occurs among a finite initial set of terms, and is finite. For the zero sequence the maximum is zero.
The norm axioms now follow directly. The maximum is nonnegative and vanishes precisely when every coordinate vanishes. For a scalar a, ∥ax∥∞​=∣a∣∥x∥∞​. For two null sequences, coordinatewise addition remains a null sequence and
∣xi​+yi​∣≤∣xi​∣+∣yi​∣≤∥x∥∞​+∥y∥∞​.
(2)
Taking the maximum proves the triangle inequality, so this really is a norm on V.
Let en​ have a single 1 in position n. If m=n, then ∥em​−en​∥∞​=1. A convergent sequence in a normed vector space must be a Cauchy sequence, by the triangle inequality. These distances violate the Cauchy condition with, for example, ε=1/2. Thus (en​) does not converge in this norm, even though each fixed coordinate converges to zero.

The identity theorem can be expressed as the principle of isolated zeros: a holomorphic function on a connected open set is either identically zero or has isolated zeros. Locally this follows from its Taylor series: at a zero of a function which is not locally zero, factor the first nonzero power, f(z)=(z−z0​)mh(z) with h(z0​)=0. Connectedness then rules out a nonempty open region of zeros unless the whole function vanishes.
Set g(z)=f(zˉ)​. This is holomorphic on the punctured plane: reflecting and conjugating a local power series produces another power series. The absence of an essential singularity means that the singularity at zero is removable or a pole. Thus for some integer m≥0, zmf(z) extends holomorphically across zero; the same is true of zmg(z). Their difference
H(z)=zm(f(z)−g(z))
(3)
therefore extends to a holomorphic function on all of C. At every 1/n, real-valuedness gives H(1/n)=0. These zeros accumulate at zero, which is now an interior point of the domain of H. The identity theorem forces H≡0. For z=0 we may divide by zm, yielding
f(z)=f(zˉ)​.​
(4)
The conjugation outside f is essential. Also, applying the identity theorem directly on the punctured plane would be invalid: the accumulation point zero does not belong to that domain.

An orthogonal transformation preserves the real inner product. If W is an invariant subspace, its orthogonal complement is invariant too: αW=W in finite dimensions, and for v∈W⊥, w∈W, we have ⟨αv,w⟩=⟨v,α−1w⟩=0.
Complexify the space and the transformation. There is a complex eigenvalue λ, and preservation of the Hermitian norm gives ∣λ∣=1. If λ is real, it is 1 or −1, and the real kernel of α−λI contains a nonzero vector. Its span is an invariant line on which α acts as +1 or −1.
If λ is nonreal, write an eigenvector as u+iv. Its real and imaginary parts are linearly independent: otherwise a nonzero real vector would have the nonreal eigenvalue. The real plane W=span(u,v) is invariant, because comparison of real and imaginary parts in α(u+iv)=λ(u+iv) gives real combinations of u,v. The restriction has determinant λλˉ=1. In an oriented orthonormal basis of W, it is therefore a rotation
Rθ​=(cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​).
(27)
There is no invariant real line in this plane: such a line would supply a real eigenvalue, whereas its two eigenvalues are nonreal.
Apply the argument repeatedly to the invariant orthogonal complement. Thus the smallest mutually orthogonal invariant blocks are lines with action ±1, and planes with a genuine rotation. Planes with angle 0 or π can be decomposed further into lines.
In dimension three a suitably chosen orthonormal basis therefore gives either
diag(ϵ1​,ϵ2​,ϵ3​),ϵj​∈{1,−1},​
(28)
or
​ϵ00​0cosθsinθ​0−sinθcosθ​​,ϵ∈{1,−1},0<θ<π.​
(29)
Changing the orientation of the plane reverses the angle, so this angle range loses no cases. These exhaust rotations, reflections and their possible compositions in three dimensions.

In the Poincare disc model, hyperbolic lines are diameters and circular arcs meeting the boundary circle orthogonally. In the upper half-plane model, they are vertical lines and semicircles with centres on the real axis. The metric is conformal, so hyperbolic perpendicularity agrees with Euclidean perpendicularity.
Use an isometry of the hyperbolic plane to send an arbitrary line to the positive imaginary axis. For P=x+iy with x=0, a point on that axis is Q=iv with v>0. The hyperbolic distance formula gives
coshρ(P,iv)=1+2yvx2+(y−v)2​=2yvx2+y2+v2​.
(33)
Put R=x2+y2​. The last expression is (v+R2/v)/(2y), uniquely minimized at v=R, since v+R2/v≥2R with equality only there. Hence the unique closest point is Q0​=iR. The semicircle ∣z∣=R passes through P and Q0​ and meets the imaginary axis at a right angle. Conversely a semicircle centred at a real number b can meet that axis orthogonally only if b=0; a vertical line cannot furnish such a crossing. Thus the perpendicular is unique. Isometries transfer both uniqueness and minimization back to the original line.
For P on the specified second semicircle, a−r≤x≤a+r and R=∣P∣≤a+r. Its perpendicular to the axis is the semicircle just found, with radius R. Moreover
coshd(P,L1​)=yR​,tanhd(P,L1​)=Rx​.

T(x)−s=2x(x−s)2​≥0.

The nearest-point assertion requires F=∅. If F=∅, there is no possible point q, so the literal assertion for every closed subset has that exception.
For a nonempty closed subset F of a compact metric space, F is itself compact. The function q↦d(p,q) is continuous, since ∣d(p,q)−d(p,q′)∣≤d(q,q′) by the triangle inequality. The extreme value theorem therefore gives a minimizer q∈F, with
