Put S=∑i​Xi​, and let z.95​ be the exact standard-normal 95th percentile. Under parameter θ, S=nθ+n​Z with Z standard normal. This distribution can be justified directly: the joint density of the centered observations is proportional to exp[−∑i​(Xi​−θ)2/2]. An orthogonal change of coordinates with first row (1,…,1)/n​ has unit absolute Jacobian and preserves the sum of squares. Its density factors into n one-dimensional standard-normal densities, making the first coordinate Z=(S−nθ)/n​ standard normal.
Consider the test ϕ∗​=1{S>n​z.95​}​. Its rejection probability is
Prθ​(ϕ∗​=1)=1−Φ(z.95​−n​θ),
(46)
increasing in θ. Therefore its maximum rejection probability over the null θ≤0 is achieved at zero and equals α=1/20. This proves it has the required test size.
To prove the uniformly most powerful test property, rather than quote the Neyman-Pearson lemma, fix any θ1​>0. The likelihood ratio of the full sample against θ=0 is
p0​(x)pθ1​​(x)​=exp(θ1​S−2nθ12​​),
(47)
strictly increasing in S. Set K=exp(θ1​n​z.95​−nθ12​/2). For any competing possibly randomized test 0≤ϕ≤1 with composite-null size at most α, we have E0​ϕ≤α=E0​ϕ∗​. Pointwise,
(ϕ∗​−ϕ)(pθ1​​−Kp0​)≥0,
(48)
because both factors are nonnegative above the threshold and both are nonpositive below it. Integrating gives
Eθ1​​ϕ∗​−Eθ1​​ϕ≥K(E0​ϕ∗​−E0​ϕ)≥0.
(49)
This comparison proves the likelihood-ratio optimality result needed here in full. Since the same critical region works for every θ1​>0, it proves the uniformly most powerful test property over the entire alternative.
For n=9, the exact critical region and its quoted-table approximation are
i=1∑9​Xi​>3z.95​,equivalently X>z.95​/3≈0.55.​
(50)
Using the supplied rounded percentile 1.65 gives ∑Xi​>4.95. The exact cutoff uses z.95​≈1.644854 and hence X>0.548285; the rounded cutoff is the intended numerical approximation, not a literally exact size-0.05 threshold. The Gaussian distribution is continuous, so equality at the cutoff has probability zero and no randomization there is needed.
