Use the one-sided Laplace transform F(s)=∫0∞​e−stf(t)dt. Since f=0 on [0,1), substitution gives L[f(t+1)](s)=esF(s). Thus
F(s)=s(es−1/2)1​=s(1−e−s/2)2e−s​.
(17)
Its poles are s=0 and sn​=−log2+2πin, with residues of estF(s) respectively two and 2esn​t/sn​. For t>1 close the Bromwich inversion formula to the left, using horizontal heights avoiding those poles. The left-side integrand decays like est/s, and on the right-side horizontal ends like es(t−1)/s, so the added integrals vanish with the usual symmetric limiting contour. The residue theorem gives
f(t)=2+21−tn=−∞∑∞​2πin−log2e2πint​(t≥1),​
(18)
where the series is summed symmetrically and at jumps has the midpoint value.
For t<1 the vanishing can be checked directly in the inversion integral, rather than inferred from the recurrence. On any vertical line ℜs=c>0 expand the denominator geometrically, uniformly there:
F(s)=2∑j=0∞​2−jse−(j+1)s​.
(19)
Each inversion term is 21−j(2πi)−1∫c−i∞c+i∞​es(t−j−1)ds/s. If t<1, close this contour to the right: the exponential decays, and the pole at zero is outside, so every term is zero. Equivalently the geometric expansion bounds the full closing contour by the same decaying factors. Thus f(t)=0 for t<1.
The same integral gives f(t)=2∑j≥0​2−jH(t−j−1) elsewhere. For m<t<m+1 with integer m≥1, this is 4(1−2−m), which directly verifies the difference equation. Neither a Laplace transform nor a Fourier series determines values at isolated jumps; the printed series fixes the standard convention H(0)=1/2, including f(1)=1.
