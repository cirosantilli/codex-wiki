Uniqueness in the defining characterization gives ℘(−z)=℘(z): the reflected function has the same poles, periodicity and prescribed principal part. Hence ℘′ is odd and periodic. At each nonzero half-period ω∈{1/2,τ/2,(1+τ)/2}, −ω≡ω(modΛ), so ℘′(ω)=−℘′(ω)=0.
An elliptic function has equally many zeros and poles, counted with multiplicities, in a fundamental parallelogram. This standard property applies to ℘′, whose sole pole modulo the lattice has order three. The three distinct nonzero half-periods already supply three zeros, so these are all its zeros and each is simple. Thus
{z:℘′(z)=0}=ω∈{1/2,τ/2,(1+τ)/2}⋃​(ω+Λ).​

Evenness and the prescribed expansion give ℘(z)=z−2+a2​z2+a4​z4+O(z6). There is no constant term because the defining difference tends to zero, and only even powers occur. Consequently
℘′(z)2−4℘(z)3=−20a2​z−2−28a4​+O(z2).
(62)
Adding 20a2​℘ cancels the last pole. The resulting elliptic function is holomorphic everywhere and therefore constant, by boundedness on a fundamental parallelogram and Liouville theorem. Its value at zero is −28a4​. Thus
℘′2=4℘3−20a2​℘−28a4​=Q(℘).
(63)
At the three half-periods Q(℘(ω))=0. Their values are distinct: if two such points had the same value e, then ℘−e would have a zero of multiplicity at least two at each, because its derivative vanishes there. That gives at least four zeros, contrary to its sole double pole and the zero-pole counting property. Hence the cubic has three distinct roots and leading coefficient four, proving
Q(w)=4(w−℘(1/2))(w−℘(τ/2))(w−℘((1+τ)/2)).​
(64)
All properties specific to ℘ used here were derived from its defining characterization; only the general elliptic zero-pole count and holomorphic constancy were used as standard facts.
