A subset is nowhere dense when its closure has empty interior. It is of first category, or meagre, when it is a countable union of nowhere-dense subsets; otherwise it is of second category.
The Baire category theorem says that in a complete metric space every countable intersection of open dense subsets is dense. To prove it, start inside an arbitrary nonempty open set and choose a closed ball of positive radius there and in the first dense open set. Inductively choose a closed ball inside the interior of the preceding ball and the next dense open set, with radius less than 2−n. The centres are Cauchy. Completeness gives a limit lying in all the nested closed balls, hence in all the dense open sets and the original open set. Thus a nonempty complete metric space is not meagre in itself.
For p<r≤∞, put Em​={x∈ℓp:∥x∥p​≤m}, viewed inside ℓr. Necessarily p<∞. Each Em​ is closed: convergence in the ℓr norm gives coordinatewise convergence, and every finite partial sum of ∑∣xi​∣p is bounded by mp in the limit. Taking the supremum of partial sums proves the assertion.
It has empty interior. Given a point in it and any small ℓr radius, perturb a block of N coordinates by entries of modulus δN−1/r, choosing signs to avoid cancelling the existing coordinates. The ℓr distance is δ, while the resulting ℓp norm is at least δN1/p−1/r, exceeding m for large N. For r=∞, use entries of modulus δ on that block. Hence each Em​ is nowhere dense, and
