For 0≤r<1, summing two geometric series gives the Poisson kernel on the circle:
1+∑n≥1​rn(eint+e−int)=1−reit1​+1−re−it1​−1=1−2rcost+r21−r2​=Pr​(t).

This Fourier series converges absolutely and uniformly for each fixed r<1. Consequently we can integrate it term by term in the convolution; the substitution u=t−s gives
(f∗Pr​)(t)=n∈Z∑​f​(n)r∣n∣eint.​
(3)
The resulting series is itself absolutely and uniformly convergent, since ∣f​(n)∣≤∥f∥∞​ and ∑n​r∣n∣<∞.
The Poisson kernel on the circle is nonnegative and has normalized integral one, as its constant Fourier coefficient is one. Its mass concentrates near zero. Indeed, for r≥1/2 and δ≤∣s∣≤π,
0≤Pr​(s)=(1−r)2+2r(1−coss)1−r2​≤1−cosδ1−r2​.
(4)
Hence its normalized integral outside (−δ,δ) tends to zero as r↑1. Given η>0, uniform continuity of f gives a δ>0 such that ∣f(t−s)−f(t)∣<η whenever the circular distance of s from zero is less than δ. Splitting the convolution error into this arc and its complement yields
supt​∣(f∗Pr​)(t)−f(t)∣≤η+2∥f∥∞​2π1​∫δ≤∣s∣≤π​Pr​(s)ds.

The second term tends to zero, and η is arbitrary. Thus the Abel sums converge uniformly to f, or ∥f∗Pr​−f∥∞​→0​. This is uniform Poisson summability of continuous circle functions: positivity, unit mass and concentration supply the required approximate identity argument.

Use the given integer u satisfying u2≡−1(modp). The Euclidean lattice under consideration has basis (1,u),(0,p), because every vector in it is uniquely (a,ua+bp) with a,b∈Z. Its fundamental parallelogram therefore has area
​det(1u​0p​)​=p.
(6)
We use the following two-dimensional form of the Minkowski convex body theorem: a convex, centrally symmetric measurable subset of R2 of area strictly greater than four times the covolume of a full-rank Euclidean lattice contains a nonzero vector of that lattice. Apply it to the closed disk of squared radius 3p/2. Its area is 3πp/2>4p, so it contains a nonzero lattice vector (a,b) satisfying
0<a2+b2≤23p​<2p.
(7)
On the other hand the defining modular congruence and u2≡−1(modp) imply
a2+b2≡a2+(ua)2=(1+u2)a2≡0(modp).
(8)
There is exactly one positive multiple of p strictly below 2p, so p=a2+b2​. Neither coordinate can be zero, since a prime is not the square of an integer. This congruence lattice proof of the prime sum of two squares proves the required case of the sum of two squares theorem without assuming any conclusion at the critical area 4p.

Write SN​f(t)=∑∣n∣≤N​f​(n)eint for the symmetric Fourier partial sum, with normalized Fourier coefficients on T=R/(2πZ). We will prove the stronger conclusion
N→∞limsup​∣SN​f(t)∣=∞for every t∈E.​
(9)
If E is empty, take f=0. Otherwise the proof of the Kahane-Katznelson divergence theorem has three steps: a bounded trigonometric polynomial whose partial sum is large on short arcs, a finite-arc batching of an arbitrary null set, and a uniformly convergent sequence of separated frequency blocks. In particular, no assumption that E is closed or compact is needed.
First consider a nonempty finite family of arcs with centers θν​, half-lengths 0<dν​≤1, and D=∑ν​dν​. On the closed unit disk define
Φ(z)=∑ν​Ddν​​1+dν​−ze−iθν​1+dν​​.
(10)
Each summand has positive real part there, and Φ(0)=1. Thus the principal complex logarithm A=logΦ is holomorphic on a neighborhood of the closed disk, A(0)=0, and ∣ImA∣<π/2. To bound its real part on an arc, put h=t−θν​ with ∣h∣≤dν​ and q=1+dν​−cosh. Then
dν​≤q≤23​dν​,∣sinh∣≤dν​,Re1+dν​−eih1+dν​​=q2+sin2h(1+dν​)q​≥13dν​4​>4dν​1​.
(11)
After multiplication by the weight dν​/D, this contribution is at least 1/(4D); all the other contributions have positive real part. Throughout the arc union we therefore have
ReΦ(eit)≥4D1​,ReA(eit)=log∣Φ(eit)∣≥log4D1​.
(12)
This is the positive-real logarithmic amplifier for short arcs. Since A is holomorphic beyond the closed unit disk, its Taylor series converges uniformly there. Choose a polynomial Q(z)=∑ν=1d​aν​zν with sup∣z∣≤1​∣Q(z)−A(z)∣<1. There is no constant term because A(0)=0. Set C=π/2+1 and

where the integer M>d may be chosen arbitrarily large. We have ∥P∥∞​≤1, and its frequencies lie in [M−d,M+d]. At the cut N=M, the positive-frequency half of Q is excluded and the negative-frequency half is included. Hence

on the arc union. In particular, for any K>0, a total half-length D≤41​e−1−2CK gives ∣SM​P∣≥K there. This proves the needed compact-set Fourier amplification lemma, with the additional freedom to place its entire frequency block above any prescribed frequency.
Next we construct the null-set limsup cover by finite arc families. Set
aj​=2−j,Kj​=2j(j+2),Dj​=41​e−1−2CKj​(j≥1).
(15)
Since E has Lebesgue measure zero, for every row ℓ≥1 choose a countable arc cover {Iℓ,m​:m≥1} of E with positive half-lengths dℓ,m​ and ∑m​dℓ,m​<2−ℓD1​. We can take closed arcs by slightly enlarging an open cover; their half-lengths are less than one. The whole two-index array has total half-length less than D1​. Absolute convergence of this sum lets us choose strictly increasing integers Nj​ such that the sum outside the finite square 1≤ℓ,m≤Nj​ is less than Dj+1​. Put N0​=0 and let Fj​ contain those arcs for which
Nj−1​<max(ℓ,m)≤Nj​.
(16)
Each family is finite. Its total half-length is less than Dj​: for j=1 use the total sum, and for j>1 use the tail outside the preceding square. Moreover, every t∈E belongs to arcs with unbounded row index, so belongs to the unions of infinitely many of the families Fj​. This batching is essential: a dense null set need not have a finite cover of small total length, whereas the infinitely-often finite covers above suffice.
Apply the amplification construction to Fj​ with height Kj​, obtaining Pj​ with ∥Pj​∥∞​≤1 and ∣SMj​​Pj​(t)∣≥Kj​ on that family's union. Choose the modulation integers successively so that the frequency intervals [Mj​−dj​,Mj​+dj​] are positive and each lies strictly above the preceding one. Empty families may be represented by Pj​=0 and an unused frequency interval. Define the frequency-separated Fourier block series
f(t)=∑j=1∞​aj​Pj​(t).
(17)
The bound ∑j​aj​∥Pj​∥∞​≤1 gives absolute uniform convergence, so f is continuous. Uniform convergence also justifies computing every Fourier coefficient term by term. At frequency Mj​, all earlier blocks have been included in full and all later blocks are absent:
SMj​​f(t)=∑i<j​ai​Pi​(t)+aj​SMj​​Pj​(t).

∣SMj​​f(t)∣≥aj​Kj​−∑i<j​ai​≥j+1.
(19)
Every t∈E lies in infinitely many such unions, and Mj​→∞. Therefore the boxed unboundedness holds at every point of E, completing the proof for arbitrary null sets.

Use the angular-frequency Fourier transform convention
f​(λ)=∫R​f(x)e−iλxdx.
(20)
A precise sufficient interpretation of the regularity assumption is f=0, f∈H1(R) in the first-order Sobolev space and xf∈L2(R); in particular, nonzero Schwartz functions are admissible. We use the Plancherel theorem, in the normalization ∥f​∥22​=2π∥f∥22​, together with its derivative identity f′​(λ)=iλf​(λ). Both identities hold in L2 for f∈H1, by extending their identities for Schwartz functions. Thus the frequency factor is
∫∣f​(λ)∣2dλ∫λ2∣f​(λ)∣2dλ​=∥f∥22​∥f′∥22​​.
(21)
For a Schwartz function, integration by parts gives ∥f∥22​=−2Re∫xf′(x)f(x)​dx. This remains true under the stated assumptions: insert a smooth cutoff χR​(x)=χ(x/R) into the derivative of x∣f∣2, where χ is one near zero and compactly supported. Integration gives
∫(χR​+xχR′​)∣f∣2dx=−2Re∫χR​xf′f​dx.
(22)
The cutoff-derivative term tends to zero, since xχR′​ is bounded independently of R and supported in a tail. The other terms converge by dominated convergence and the integrability of ∣f′∣∣xf∣, furnished by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Hence
∥f∥22​=−2Re⟨f′,xf⟩≤2∣⟨f′,xf⟩∣≤2∥f′∥2​∥xf∥2​.
(23)
After squaring and using the Plancherel theorem, we obtain the uncentred Fourier uncertainty principle:
∫∣f(x)∣2dx∫x2∣f(x)∣2dx​∫∣f​(λ)∣2dλ∫λ2∣f​(λ)∣2dλ​≥41​.​
(24)
If equality holds, both displayed inequalities must be equalities. The equality condition in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives f′=cxf almost everywhere for a constant c∈C; the real-part inequality and the identity for ∥f∥22​ then force c=−a with a>0 real. The weak derivative equation has the solution f(x)=Ce−ax2/2: locally multiply by eax2/2 to obtain a function whose weak derivative is zero, hence a constant. Conversely, for this Gaussian function the normalized second moments are 1/(2a) in position and a/2 in angular frequency, whose product is 1/4. Therefore
Equality holds exactly for f(x)=Ce−ax2/2,a>0, C∈C∖{0}.​
(25)
These are raw second moments, rather than variances about their respective means. Consequently translations and nonzero frequency modulations of the Gaussian function are not equality cases here; the equality case of the Heisenberg uncertainty relation for centred variances has those additional freedoms.

Use f​(λ)=∫f(t)e−iλtdt and interpret the regularity assumption to include f∈L2(R) with the continuous representative furnished by Fourier inversion. Write F=f​. The bandwidth assumption implies F∈L1 as well as L2, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on its finite support, so
f(t)=2π1​∫−ππ​F(λ)eitλdλ
(26)
is continuous and defined at every t. The integer samples are precisely the Fourier coefficients of F with the sign convention appropriate to the basis e−inλ:
f(n)=2π1​∫−ππ​F(λ)einλdλ.
(27)
The completeness and Parseval identity for the exponential orthonormal basis on [−π,π] give F=∑n∈Z​f(n)e−inλ in L2 and
∑n∈Z​∣f(n)∣2=2π1​∥F∥22​=∥f∥22​.
(28)
The last equality is the Plancherel theorem. Apply the inverse integral to the finite symmetric Fourier partial sums. Since
2π1​∫−ππ​ei(t−n)λdλ=π(t−n)sinπ(t−n)​,
(29)
we obtain the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem in this normalization:
f(t)=n∈Z∑​f(n)π(t−n)sinπ(t−n)​(t∈R),​
(30)
where the quotient is one at t=n. This is a sampling expansion by periodic Fourier projection, not merely an almost-everywhere inversion. Indeed, the inverse integral of an error G∈L2[−π,π] is bounded, uniformly in t, by ∥G∥2​/2π​. Hence the symmetric finite sampling sums converge uniformly on all of R. Moreover, the Parseval identity applied to eitλ gives
∑n∈Z​​π(t−n)sinπ(t−n)​​2=1.
(31)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality therefore makes the sampling series absolutely convergent at every t, with its absolute tail bounded uniformly by (∑∣n∣>N​∣f(n)∣2)1/2. Thus the integer samples recover the function everywhere.
For the nonuniqueness beyond this bandwidth, fix 0<ϵ0​<min(ϵ,π) and a nonzero smooth function Φ compactly supported in (−ϵ0​,ϵ0​). Let h be its inverse Fourier transform and put
g(t)=sin(πt)h(t),g​(λ)=2iΦ(λ−π)−Φ(λ+π)​.
(32)
The function h, and hence g, is a Schwartz function. The two shifted spectral supports are disjoint, so g​=0, but its support is contained strictly inside (−π−ϵ,π+ϵ). Every integer sample of g is zero. Therefore g and the zero function have identical samples but are distinct, providing a strictly supercritical sampling alias counterexample for every positive bandwidth enlargement.

For the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma, a half-period shift of the oscillation gives a direct proof. For a nonzero integer r, set h=π/r. Periodicity and a change of variable show that the Fourier coefficient of t↦f(t+h) at r is eirhf​(r)=−f​(r). Thus
2f​(r)=2π1​∫−ππ​(f(t)−f(t+h))e−irtdt,∣f​(r)∣≤21​supt​∣f(t)−f(t+π/r)∣.
(33)
The right side tends to zero as ∣r∣→∞ by uniform continuity on the circle. Consequently f​(r)→0 as ∣r∣→∞​.
There is nevertheless no prescribed rate shared by all continuous functions. Choose increasing positive integers nj​ with k(nj​)≥j2j, which is possible since k(r)→∞. Define
g(t)=∑j=1∞​2−jeinj​t.
(34)
The series converges absolutely and uniformly, so g is continuous. Termwise integration is justified by uniform convergence, and the orthogonality of distinct exponential modes gives g​(nj​)=2−j. Hence
k(nj​)∣g​(nj​)∣≥j,r→∞limsup​k(r)∣g​(r)∣=∞.​
(35)
This arbitrarily slow Fourier coefficient decay is compatible with the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma: for this particular function all Fourier coefficients still tend to zero, but along the selected subsequence they beat the proposed decay scale by an unbounded factor.

Put L=2π and represent the circle by [0,L). The full orthonormal basis of Haar wavelets includes the constant H∗​=L−1/2 and, for every dyadic interval I of length ℓ=L2−j, the normalized detail

The constant basis function is necessary: if it were omitted, even f≡1 could not satisfy the asserted convergence. We use the full conventional Haar wavelet system in the threshold sum.
Let AJ​f be the function equal to the mean of f on each level-J dyadic interval.
