A strongly continuous semigroup on the complex Banach space E is a family Tt​∈L(E) with T0​=I, Ts+t​=Ts​Tt​ and ∥Tt​f−f∥→0 as t↓0 for every f∈E. Its semigroup generator is the linear operator
D(Z)={f∈E:limh↓0​hTh​f−f​ exists in E},Zf=limh↓0​hTh​f−f​.
(1)
For f∈D(Z), boundedness of Tt​ and the semigroup identity give
hTh​Tt​f−Tt​f​=Tt​hTh​f−f​⟶Tt​Zf.
(2)
Thus semigroup commutation with its unbounded generator means
Tt​D(Z)⊆D(Z),ZTt​f=Tt​Zf(f∈D(Z)).​
(3)
There is no assertion that Z is defined on every vector. The orbit is differentiable on this domain, with dtd​Tt​f=Tt​Zf.
A contraction semigroup additionally satisfies ∥Tt​∥≤1 for every t≥0. For Reλ>0, define the Bochner integral
Lλ​f=∫0∞​e−λtTt​fdt.
(4)
Strong continuity gives a continuous integrand, and its norm is bounded by e−tReλ∥f∥, so the integral exists and
∥Lλ​∥≤Reλ1​.
(5)
Changing variables in Th​Lλ​f yields
Th​Lλ​f=eλh(Lλ​f−∫0h​e−λsTs​fds).
(6)
After subtracting Lλ​f and dividing by h, the limit is λLλ​f−f. Consequently
Lλ​(E)⊆D(Z),(λI−Z)Lλ​f=f.​
(7)
For f∈D(Z), integrate the derivative of e−λtTt​f over [0,∞). Its limit at infinity is zero by the contraction bound, and its value at zero is f. Therefore
Lλ​(λI−Z)f=−∫0∞​dtd​(e−λtTt​f)dt=f.
(8)
Thus this is also the Laplace-transform formula for a semigroup resolvent, with Lλ​=(λI−Z)−1.
To prove that Z is a closed operator, suppose fn​∈D(Z), fn​→f and Zfn​→g. Fix one λ with positive real part. The identity just proved and boundedness of Lλ​ give
f=limn​Lλ​(λfn​−Zfn​)=Lλ​(λf−g).
(9)
Its range lies in D(Z), and applying λI−Z shows Zf=g. Thus the graph is closed. Linearity follows directly from linearity of the defining difference quotient, so Z is a closed linear operator.
The closedness conclusion also holds without the contraction assumption. For a general strongly continuous semigroup, the Uniform boundedness principle gives a uniform operator bound on each compact time interval. Integrating the generator-orbit identity gives Tt​fn​−fn​=∫0t​Ts​Zfn​ds. Under fn​→f and Zfn​→g, pass to the limit to obtain Tt​f−f=∫0t​Ts​gds. Divide by t and let t↓0; strong continuity makes the right side converge to g, so f∈D(Z) and Zf=g.

A linear operator Z on a Banach space is called a dissipative operator when
∥(λI−Z)f∥≥λ∥f∥(λ>0, f∈D(Z)).
(10)
On a Hilbert space, using the inner product linear in its first argument,
∥(λI−Z)f∥2=λ2∥f∥2−2λRe⟨Zf,f⟩+∥Zf∥2.
(11)
If Re⟨Zf,f⟩≤0, this proves the defining inequality. Conversely that inequality implies 2λRe⟨Zf,f⟩≤∥Zf∥2 for every λ>0; letting λ→∞ gives
Z is dissipative⟺Re⟨Zf,f⟩≤0 for all f∈D(Z).​
(12)
For the next spectral assertion, dissipativity must be retained as a hypothesis. Let Z be closed, densely defined and dissipative. For α>0, the dissipative bound makes αI−Z injective. Its range is closed: if (αI−Z)fn​→h, the bound makes fn​ Cauchy, and then Zfn​=αfn​−(αI−Z)fn​ converges. Closedness produces a preimage of h. If the range is all of H, the same bound gives a bounded inverse, so α is in the resolvent set. If the closed range is proper, there is a nonzero vector in its orthogonal complement, and the definition of the adjoint of a densely defined operator gives
Ran(αI−Z)⊥=ker(αI−Z∗).
(13)
This proves that the positive spectrum of a dissipative operator is adjoint point spectrum:
α∈σ(Z)⟺α is an eigenvalue of Z∗(α>0).​
(14)
Without dissipativity the PDF's assertion is false. For example, multiplication by x on L2(0,2) is bounded, closed and densely defined. The value 1 is spectral: normalized functions supported closer and closer to x=1 make ∥(Z−I)f∥ tend to zero. But Z∗=Z has no eigenvector for eigenvalue 1, since a square-integrable function supported on the singleton {1} is zero.
For the given maximal-domain operator Q, finite-support sequences belong to D(Q), so the domain is dense. Coordinatewise limits also show it is closed: if f(j)→f and Qf(j)→h in ℓ2, each defining coordinate equation passes to the limit, giving Qf=h∈ℓ2 and hence f∈D(Q).
Put gn​=2nfn​ and h=Qf. For n≥1,
gn​−gn−1​=−2−nhn​.
(15)
Since ∑n≥1​2−n∣hn​∣<∞ by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, gn​ has a finite limit ℓf​. Its increments are square summable as well. Direct summation gives
Ren=0∑N​(Qf)n​fn​​​=−n=0∑N​∣gn​∣2+n=1∑N​Re(gn−1​gn​​)=−21​∣g0​∣2−21​∣gN​∣2−21​n=1∑N​∣gn​−gn−1​∣2.​
(16)
The original inner-product series converges because Qf,f∈ℓ2. Taking N→∞ therefore gives
Re⟨Qf,f⟩=−21​(∣g0​∣2+∣ℓf​∣2+n=1∑∞​∣gn​−gn−1​∣2)≤0.​
(17)
Thus Q is dissipative, including its full maximal domain; the limiting boundary term has not been discarded.
To decide generation, solve (αI−Q)f=h for arbitrary h∈ℓ2 and fixed α>0. The first coordinate gives g0​=f0​=h0​/(α+1), and the rest give
gn​=1+α4−ngn−1​+2−nhn​​(n≥1).
(18)
Because all denominators are at least one,
∣gn​∣≤∣g0​∣+∑k=1n​2−k∣hk​∣≤∣g0​∣+3​1​(∑k≥1​∣hk​∣2)1/2.
(19)
Hence gn​ is bounded, so fn​=2−ngn​ belongs to ℓ2. The coordinate equations give Qf=αf−h∈ℓ2, proving f∈D(Q) and surjectivity of αI−Q. Together with density, closedness and dissipativity, the Lumer-Phillips theorem proves the contraction generator from an exponentially weighted difference operator conclusion:
Q generates a strongly continuous contraction semigroup on ℓ2.​
(20)
The domain is crucial. The formal transposed matrix suggests a positive-eigenvalue solution
un​=2−nu0​∏k=0n−1​(1+α4−k),
(21)
which is square summable because the products converge. It is not a true adjoint eigenvector: the admissible test vector fn​=2−n has Qf=(−1,0,0,…), so ⟨Qf,u⟩=−u0​​, while α⟨f,u⟩=αu0​​∑n​4−n∏k<n​(1+α4−k). These cannot agree for α>0 unless u0​=0. This exemplifies how boundary terms exclude formal adjoint eigenvectors, and explains why a calculation using only finite-support test vectors would give the wrong generation answer.

For the Volterra integration operator, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
∣Jf(x)∣2=​∫0x​f(t)dt​2≤x∫0x​∣f(t)∣2dt.
(22)
Integrating and reversing the order yields
∥Jf∥22​≤∫01​21−t2​∣f(t)∣2dt≤21​∥f∥22​.
(23)
Thus J is bounded, with ∥J∥≤1/2​. Since f∈L2(0,1)⊆L1(0,1), Jf is absolutely continuous and (Jf)′=f almost everywhere. If Jf=0 as an L2 element, its continuous representative is zero everywhere, so its derivative is zero almost everywhere. Hence J is one-to-one.
Its range is exactly {u∈H1(0,1):u(0)=0}, and Au=iu′. This contains the smooth compactly supported functions in (0,1), so A is a densely defined operator. Closedness can also be seen directly: if un​→u and Aun​→v in L2, then un​=J(−iAun​)→J(−iv), giving u=J(−iv)∈D(A) and Au=v.
For every λ∈C,
(λI−A)J=λJ−iI.
(24)
If λ=0, the allowed fact σ(J)={0} makes J−(i/λ)I invertible. At λ=0, λJ−iI=−iI is invertible too. Thus the bounded operator
Rλ​=J(λJ−iI)−1
(25)
has range in D(A) and is a two-sided inverse of λI−A. Equivalently, solving the initial-value differential equation gives
Rλ​h(x)=i∫0x​e−iλ(x−s)h(s)ds.
(26)
This is bounded for every fixed λ on the finite interval. Therefore Volterra inverse differentiation has empty spectrum:
σ(A)=∅.​
(27)
The familiar nonempty-spectrum theorem for bounded operators does not apply to this unbounded operator.
The subspace H0​ is the kernel of the bounded functional f↦Jf(1)=∫01​f, so it is closed. Its image under J is
D(A0​)={u∈H1(0,1):u(0)=u(1)=0}=H01​(0,1),
(28)
the zero-trace Sobolev space. If un​∈D(A0​), un​→u and A0​un​→v, then −iA0​un​∈H0​ converges to −iv∈H0​, and u=J(−iv)∈D(A0​). Thus A0​ is closed. It is densely defined because it also contains the smooth compactly supported functions.
For u,v∈D(A0​), integration by parts and their zero endpoint traces give
⟨A0​u,v⟩=i∫01​u′v=i[uv]01​−i∫01​uv′=⟨u,A0​v⟩.
(29)
Hence A0​ is closed and symmetric.
For any v∈C1([0,1]), the same formula gives ⟨A0​u,v⟩=⟨u,iv′⟩, with no endpoint term because u vanishes there. Consequently v∈D(A0∗​) and A0∗​v=iv′, proving the requested inclusion. In fact, testing against compactly supported smooth u shows that every element of D(A0∗​) has weak derivative in L2; conversely the integration-by-parts formula applies to every v∈H1(0,1). Thus
D(A0∗​)=H1(0,1),A0∗​v=iv′,
(30)
with no boundary restrictions.
For any λ∈C, the nonzero function vλ​(x)=e−iλx belongs to this domain and satisfies ivλ′​=λvλ​. Conversely this first-order equation has only scalar multiples of that exponential. Therefore
σp​(A0∗​)=C,ker(A0∗​−λI)=span{e−iλx}.​
(31)
For any λ, the adjoint eigenfunction with eigenvalue λ annihilates Ran(λI−A0​), so this range is not all of H. Explicitly the resolvent equation with zero initial value has the solution Rλ​h above, and its endpoint value is zero exactly when
∫01​eiλsh(s)ds=0.
(32)
Thus the range is a proper closed hyperplane for every λ. This proves that the two-endpoint symmetric derivative has whole complex spectrum:
σ(A0​)=C.​
(33)
There are no eigenvalues of A0​ itself: the eigenfunction equation together with u(0)=0 forces the exponential coefficient to vanish. The operator is symmetric but not self-adjoint, since its adjoint has a strictly larger domain; symmetry alone does not force a real spectrum.

Use the joint energy convention
Eμ​(f,g)=lims↓0​s1​⟨f−Ps​f,g⟩L2(μ)​,Eμ​(f)=Eμ​(f,f).
(34)
On the generator domain it is −⟨Lf,g⟩. The symmetric Feller semigroup has symmetric two-point measure νs​(dx,dy)=μ(dx)Ps​(x,dy), with both marginals equal to μ. Expanding the product of increments gives the finite-time identity
Eμ,s​(f,g)=2s1​∫(f(x)−f(y))(g(x)−g(y))dνs​(x,y)
(35)
for real functions. This supplies a proof that works for a general symmetric Markov kernel, without assuming a diffusion chain rule.
For a≥b≥0, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
(aq/2−bq/2)2​=4q2​(∫ba​rq/2−1dr)2≤4q2​(a−b)∫ba​rq−2dr=4(q−1)q2​(aq−1−bq−1)(a−b).​
(36)
Interchanging a,b gives the same inequality in the other order. Apply it with a=f(x),b=f(y), integrate against νs​, and take s↓0. This proves the power inequality for symmetric Markov energies
Eμ​(fq/2)≤4(q−1)q2​Eμ​(fq−1,f).​
(37)
Initially take bounded nonnegative functions in the form domain. On their bounded range, the power maps are Lipschitz; their finite-time squared-increment energies are therefore bounded by a constant times that of f. This puts the powers in the form domain and makes both joint energy limits well defined. Truncation gives the corresponding extended-energy interpretation when needed. In particular the proof applies to the bounded positive functions used below.
Normalize the logarithmic Sobolev inequality as
Entμ​(g2)≤cLS​Eμ​(g),Entμ​(h)=∫hloghdμ−(∫hdμ)log(∫hdμ).
(38)
Start with a bounded f bounded away from zero, and put u(t)=Pt​f, q=q(t) and F(t)=∫uqdμ. Positivity and preservation of constants keep u within the same positive bounds. Symmetry realizes Pt​ as a self-adjoint contraction semigroup on L2(μ), so for t>0 its orbit lies in the generator domain and ∂t​u=Lu; this justifies the following differentiation away from zero.
Differentiate both the evolving function and exponent:
F′=q∫uq−1Ludμ+q′∫uqlogudμ.
(39)
Since log∥u∥q​=q−1logF, it follows that
dtd​log∥u∥q​=−FEμ​(uq−1,u)​+q2Fq′​Entμ​(uq).
(40)
Apply the logarithmic Sobolev inequality to g=uq/2, and then the joint energy inequality:
Entμ​(uq)≤cLS​Eμ​(uq/2)≤4(q−1)cLS​q2​Eμ​(uq−1,u).
(41)
Therefore
dtd​log∥u∥q​≤(4(q−1)cLS​q′​−1)FEμ​(uq−1,u)​.
(42)
The specified exponent satisfies q(0)=2 and q′=4(q−1)/cLS​. Thus the right side is zero and the evolving norm is nonincreasing. Integrating from a positive time δ and letting δ↓0 gives
∥Pt​f∥q(t)​≤∥f∥2​.
(43)
Here the initial limit follows from strong L2 continuity, boundedness of f and Pδ​f, and q(δ)→2.
For a bounded nonnegative f, apply the estimate to f+ϵ and let ϵ↓0. For arbitrary f∈L2(μ), truncate ∣f∣ to bounded nonnegative functions. Their images converge in L2 to Pt​∣f∣; an almost-everywhere convergent subsequence and Fatou lemma give the same Lq(t) estimate for that limit. Positivity of the Markov kernel gives ∣Pt​f∣≤Pt​∣f∣, also for complex f, so
Pt​f∈L1+e4t/cLS​(μ),∥Pt​f∥1+e4t/cLS​​≤∥f∥2​.​
(44)
This proves that the logarithmic Sobolev inequality implies hypercontractivity, with the numerical exponent matched to the stated joint-energy normalization.

Write dγ1​(x)=(2π)−1/2e−x2/2dx. On the dense polynomial algebra in L2(γ1​), the Gaussian creation and annihilation operators are
a−f=f′,a+f=xf−f′.​
(45)
Gaussian integration by parts gives ⟨a−f,g⟩=⟨f,a+g⟩. Their closed realizations are adjoints. More explicitly, for the orthonormal basis hn​=Hen​/n!​ of normalized Probabilists' Hermite polynomials,
a−hn​=n​hn−1​,a+hn​=n+1​hn+1​.
(46)
Thus the closed annihilation operator has domain {∑cn​hn​:∑n∣cn​∣2<∞}, and the closed creation operator has domain {∑cn​hn​:∑(n+1)∣cn​∣2<∞}. These describe the same set of L2 functions, the Gaussian Sobolev space, though the two operators differ. Directly on polynomials, [a−,a+]=I, and
Lf=−a+a−f=f′′−xf′.​
(47)
Use the standard carré du champ operator convention Γ(f,g)=21​(L(fg)−fLg−gLf). The product rule for this semigroup generator gives
L(fg)−fLg−gLf=2f′g′,
(48)
so the requested squared-gradient and joint energy formulas, for real functions in the standard algebra, are
Γ(f,g)=f′g′,Eγ1​​(f,g)=−∫fLgdγ1​=∫f′g′dγ1​.​
(49)
For complex functions the joint energy is sesquilinear, with g′​ in the second factor. If “squared gradient” is defined without the factor 1/2 in the product-rule expression, its value is instead 2f′g′ and the joint energy is one half of its integral; the joint energy and logarithmic Sobolev constant used here remain as displayed.
For the Gaussian logarithmic Sobolev inequality, first take smooth bounded f with bounded first and second derivatives, bounded away from zero. The Mehler formula for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup is
Pt​f(x)=∫R​f(e−tx+1−e−2t​y)dγ1​(y).
(50)
It preserves γ1​ and differentiating in x gives the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck gradient commutation identity
(Pt​f)′=e−tPt​(f′).
(51)
Put ut​=Pt​f. Differentiate its entropy functional. Since ∫ut​dγ1​=∫fdγ1​ is constant, and Gaussian integration by parts applies,
dtd​Entγ1​​(ut​)=∫(1+logut​)Lut​dγ1​=−∫ut​∣ut′​∣2​dγ1​.
(52)
For such data, Mehler's formula and dominated convergence give Pt​f(x)→∫fdγ1​ as t→∞. The uniform positive upper and lower bounds justify integrating this limit inside the entropy, whose limit is zero. Hence
Entγ1​​(f)=∫0∞​∫Pt​f∣(Pt​f)′∣2​dγ1​dt.
(53)
Apply the allowed weighted Cauchy-Schwarz inequality with g=f′:
Pt​f∣(Pt​f)′∣2​=e−2tPt​f(Pt​f′)2​≤e−2tPt​(f∣f′∣2​).
(54)
After integration, invariance of γ1​ gives
∫Pt​f∣(Pt​f)′∣2​dγ1​≤e−2t∫f∣f′∣2​dγ1​.
(55)
Integrating in t yields
Entγ1​​(f)≤21​∫f∣f′∣2​dγ1​.
(56)
For a real smooth compactly supported g, take f=g2+ϵ. Then
f∣f′∣2​=g2+ϵ4g2∣g′∣2​≤4∣g′∣2.
(57)
Letting ϵ↓0 proves
Entγ1​​(g2)≤2∫∣g′∣2dγ1​=2Eγ1​​(g).​

Smooth approximation and cutoff in the Gaussian Sobolev norm extend this to the full form domain. The entropy is lower semicontinuous under the resulting L2 convergence: the square densities converge in L1, their masses converge, and, along an almost-everywhere convergent subsequence, Fatou's lemma applies to hlogh after adding the bound 1/e.
