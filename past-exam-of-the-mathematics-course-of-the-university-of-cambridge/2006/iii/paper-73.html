A local stellar relaxation time depends on the number density n of scatterers. Calling N a total number, as the PDF does, leaves the displayed expression dimensionally wrong: σ3/(G2m2N) has units of time per volume. The intended local expression must use n, or N/V for a homogeneous sample of volume V. We shall also make the normalization of the stellar relaxation time explicit, since an unspecified representative one-dimensional velocity dispersion does not fix an exact numerical coefficient.
In a small-angle gravitational encounter, approximate the relative trajectory by a straight line of speed u and impact parameter b. The perpendicular acceleration of one star due to the other is Gmb/(b2+u2t2)3/2. Its impulse is therefore
∣Δv⊥​∣=Gmb∫−∞∞​(b2+u2t2)3/2dt​=bu2Gm​.
(1)
A star encounters 2πbdbnu scatterers per unit time in this interval of impact parameters. Independent deflections have random directions, so their variances add as in a random walk. Hence the accumulated squared velocity change has rate
Dv​≡dtd⟨∣Δv∣2⟩​=∫bmin​bmax​​(bu2Gm​)22πbnudb=u8πG2m2n​lnΛ,Λ=bmin​bmax​​.
(2)
The Coulomb logarithm in stellar dynamics arises because each logarithmic interval of impact parameter contributes equally. The lower cutoff is approximately the strong-deflection scale, bmin​≃G(2m)/u2 for equal masses, or the physical collision scale if larger. The upper cutoff is a scale on which the local density and straight-line encounter approximation cease to apply, such as the local disk thickness. Thus Λ is dimensionless; it is not a number density or an arbitrary dimensional argument of a logarithm.
To display the normalization issue, use the representative relative speed u=2​σ and define T(κ) by Dv​T=κσ2. This gives
T(κ)=4π2​κ​nG2m2lnΛσ3​.
(3)
Choosing κ=3/4 recovers precisely the printed coefficient after repairing N to n:
TRprinted convention​=16π2​3​nG2m2lnΛσ3​.​
(4)
This is an explicitly defined convention, not a uniquely derivable normalization from the supplied data. If instead relaxation means a mean-square change equal to the full initial random-speed variance, 3σ2, the same encounter approximation gives TR​=4TRprinted convention​. Averaging the encounter speed distribution instead of using one representative u changes the coefficient again. The robust result is the scaling TR​∝σ3/(nG2m2lnΛ).
For a transparent solar-neighbourhood estimate, take n=0.1pc−3, m=M⊙​, σ=30kms−1 and bmax​=300pc. With G=4.3009×10−3pcM⊙−1​(kms−1)2, one obtains bmin​=4.78×10−6pc, lnΛ=17.96, and
TRprinted convention​≃3.35×1013yr,TRfull variance​≃1.34×1014yr.
(5)
These illustrative parameters give an order 1013--1014 year stellar relaxation time, much longer than a galactic lifetime of order 1010 years. The solar neighbourhood is consequently a collisionless stellar system with respect to encounters between individual stars. Scattering by massive clouds and collective structure is a different process; the estimate does not assert that every mechanism of disk heating is negligible.

Assume a stationary, spherical, nonrotating collisionless stellar system, a constant mass-to-light ratio Υ, and vanishing boundary stresses at infinity. Let λ be the luminosity density, so ρ=Υλ, and set γ=GΥ. Distinguish spatial radius r from projected radius R. The enclosed luminosity and radial force are

Here σt2​ is the velocity dispersion of either one of the two tangential components; thus β=1−σt2​/σr2​. If a combined tangential variance is used instead, its contribution must be divided by two in this definition.
To derive the Jeans equation, start with the steady Collisionless Boltzmann equation vj​∂j​f−(∂j​Φ)∂vj​​f=0. Multiply by vi​ and integrate over all velocities. Integration by parts in velocity, with f decaying fast enough to eliminate boundary terms, gives
∂j​(λvi​vj​​)=−λ∂i​Φ.
(18)
The same equation holds with luminosity weighting because the tracer's luminosity is conserved along its orbit. Spherical symmetry makes the local stellar velocity moment tensor diagonal, with entries q=λσr2​, qt​=λσt2​, qt​. Its radial divergence is q′+(2q−2qt​)/r: the second term accounts for the changing directions of the radial and tangential unit vectors. Therefore
drd(λσr2​)​+r2β​λσr2​=−r2γλL(r)​.​

This is the Spherical Jeans equation; it follows from a stellar velocity moment rather than requiring a particular form of the galactic distribution function.

In an axisymmetric cylindrical coordinate system, the steady Collisionless Boltzmann equation is
vR​∂R∂f​+vz​∂z∂f​+(Rvϕ2​​−ΦR​)∂vR​∂f​−RvR​vϕ​​∂vϕ​∂f​−Φz​∂vz​∂f​=0.
(42)
Let ν=∫fd3v and A=ν−1∫Afd3v. Multiply by vR​ and integrate over velocities. The spatial terms give ∂R​(νvR2​​)+∂z​(νvR​vz​​). Integration by parts in vR​ supplies νΦR​−νvϕ2​​/R, and the vϕ​ derivative supplies νvR2​​/R. The vz​ force term vanishes because vR​ does not depend on vz​. This assumes sufficient decay of the galactic distribution function to discard all velocity boundary terms. The radial Jeans equation is consequently
∂R​(νvR2​​)+∂z​(νvR​vz​​)+Rν​(vR2​​−vϕ2​​)+νΦR​=0.
(43)
Reflection symmetry about z=0 makes ν even and vR​vz​​ odd. At the midplane the latter vanishes, and ν−1∂z​(νvR​vz​​)=∂z​vR​vz​​. Multiplying by R/ν proves
νR​∂R​(νvR2​​)+R∂z​vR​vz​​+vR2​​−vϕ2​​+RΦR​=0(z=0).​
(44)
All quadratic velocities here are population moments, not individual stellar velocities. In particular, symmetry does not make the midplane tilt contribution to asymmetric drift disappear: an odd mixed moment can have a nonzero derivative at zero.
For application to the Milky Way, assume negligible mean radial and vertical flows, and define the circular speed by vc2​=RΦR​(R,0). Write vR2​​=σR2​ and vϕ2​​=vϕ2​+σϕ2​. Rearranging gives the exact stress-support relation
vc2​−vϕ2​=σR2​[−dlnRdln(νσR2​)​−1+σR2​σϕ2​​]−R∂z​vR​vz​​​0​.​
(45)
Thus the stellar asymmetric drift va​=vc​−vϕ​ is the right-hand side divided by vc​+vϕ​. For va​≪vc​, this denominator is approximately 2vc​. If ν∝e−R/hν​ and σR2​∝e−R/hσ​ locally, the bracket becomes R/hν​+R/hσ​−1+σϕ2​/σR2​, making the density and dispersion-gradient contributions explicit.
A radially declining random-motion stress supplies some of the force needed to support a galactic disk, so its stars need less ordered azimuthal motion than a population on circular orbits. Hotter, often older, stellar populations therefore generally rotate more slowly than cold populations in the same gravitational potential; this is the observed stellar asymmetric drift. It is a stress-support effect, not a frictional slowing of each orbit. For a velocity ellipsoid aligned approximately with spherical coordinates, vR​vz​​≃(σR2​−σz2​)z/R, and the midplane tilt term contributes −(σR2​−σz2​) rather than zero.
Local measurements of mean rotation and all components of the velocity dispersion, combined with tracer-density and dispersion gradients, can therefore constrain vc​ and the radial Newtonian gravitational field. A hotter sample's mean rotation must not be identified directly with the circular speed. The tracer number density need not be the gravitating mass density. Nonaxisymmetric streaming, a nonstationary disk, poorly measured radial gradients, or a neglected tilt term can bias this inference; these are assumptions of the derivation rather than extra free corrections to the circular force.

First consider a genuinely one-dimensional, time-independent collisionless stellar system with Hamiltonian Ez​=vz2​/2+ψ(z). Choose ψ(0)=0. For bound vertical oscillations, a phase-mixed population has equal occupations of the upward and downward branches of each orbit. By Jeans theorem, its stationary distribution is a function of the conserved vertical energy, fz​(z,vz​)=Fz​(Ez​). Equivalently, the one-dimensional Collisionless Boltzmann equation vz​∂z​fz​−ψ′(z)∂vz​​fz​=0 makes fz​ constant along the energy curves. Branch-dependent incoming and outgoing populations or time-dependent waves would not satisfy this assumption.
Integrate both signs of vz​ and change variable to E=vz2​/2+ψ:
ν(ψ)=2∫0∞​Fz​(ψ+2vz2​​)dvz​=2​∫ψ∞​E−ψ​Fz​(E)​dE.
(46)
The density is thus an Abel transform of the vertical-energy distribution. To invert it without simply quoting an inversion rule, apply a second integral and exchange its order:
J(E)=∫E∞​ψ−E​ν(ψ)​dψ=2​∫E∞​Fz​(t)dt∫Et​(t−ψ)(ψ−E)​dψ​=π2​∫E∞​Fz​(t)dt.
(47)
The inner integral is π, as follows by setting ψ=E+(t−E)sin2θ. Writing J(E)=∫0∞​ν(E+s)s−1/2ds before differentiating avoids an artificial infinite endpoint term. Under sufficient differentiability and decay,
J′(E)=∫E∞​ψ−E​dν/dψ​dψ=−π2​Fz​(E).
(48)
The vertical-energy Abel inversion of a stellar distribution is therefore
Fz​(Ez​)=π1​∫Ez​∞​2(ψ−Ez​)​−dν/dψ​dψ.​
(49)
This proves the normalization, including the factor from the two velocity signs. The additive origin of ψ merely changes the origin of energy.
For this expression to define a physical phase-space distribution function, ν must be expressible as a single-valued function of ψ, the integrals and differentiated integral must converge, and the resulting Fz​ must be nonnegative. Reflection symmetry and a potential increasing with ∣z∣ provide the usual single-valued relation on either side of the disk. A sufficiently smooth decreasing ν(ψ) is a sufficient condition for nonnegativity; it is not asserted to be necessary for every positive energy distribution. If there is a finite escape energy, replace the infinite limits by that energy and impose the appropriate cutoff and boundary conditions. The displayed infinite-limit formula presumes either a confining potential or a distribution extended by zero above its allowed energy, with no omitted boundary population.
A three-dimensional galactic disk does not automatically meet these hypotheses. Exact separation Φ(R,z)=Φ∥​(R)+ψ(z) makes Ez​ an integral of motion. If the full galactic distribution function factorizes into an in-plane factor and a vertical factor Fz​(Ez​), integrating the in-plane velocities gives the required one-dimensional distribution after its normalization is absorbed into ν. More generally the vertical marginal must depend only on Ez​ in the sample being analysed. Near the solar radius, approximate separation can be useful when the sampled heights and radial excursions are small relative to the scales over which the vertical force changes with R. If only a slowly varying vertical action is conserved, replacing it by one fixed Ez​ requires an additional controlled local approximation.
The population must be sufficiently stationary and phase mixed, with negligible mean vertical flow, and the data must measure a well-defined tracer population with a known selection function. Radial coupling and the tilted velocity ellipsoid must be negligible or modelled. In the full vertical Jeans equation, that coupling is R−1∂R​(RνvR​vz​​); it cannot generally be discarded merely because a mixed moment vanishes at the midplane. The tracer number density ν is not the mass density producing the potential.
To infer the local mass, measure the tracer density as a function of height and its vertical velocity distribution near the midplane. With ψ(0)=0, the latter directly samples Fz​(vz2​/2). Insert that measured distribution into the forward integral for ν(ψ), and match it to the observed ν(z) to infer ψ(z). Equivalently, for trial potentials compute dν/dψ=(dν/dz)/(dψ/dz) away from the plane, apply the inversion, and require agreement with the measured velocities. Density data alone do not determine both an unknown potential and an unknown energy distribution.
Once the vertical force is known, use the full axisymmetric Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity, rather than identifying every vertical derivative with density in isolation:
4πGρtot​(R,z)=∂z2​Φ+R1​∂R​(R∂R​Φ).
(50)
At the midplane, vc2​(R)=R∂R​Φ(R,0), so
ρtot​(R0​,0)=4πG1​[ψ′′(0)+R0​1​dRdvc2​​​R0​​].​
(51)
In terms of the circular-orbit Oort constants, A=21​(vc​/R−dvc​/dR) and B=−21​(vc​/R+dvc​/dR), the radial term equals 2(B2−A2). It vanishes for a locally flat rotation curve, not for an arbitrary rotating disk. An integrated version gives the dynamical surface density from vertical stellar motions:
2πGΣtot​(<∣z∣)=ψ′(z)+∫0z​R0​1​∂R​(R∂R​Φ)​R0​,z′​dz′(z>0).
(52)
Here the actual vertical acceleration is −ψ′(z). The sign of the radial correction follows directly from the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity.
For an illustrative isothermal tracer with constant vertical velocity dispersion s, take
Fz​(E)=2π​sν0​​e−E/s2.
(53)
The velocity integral gives ν(z)=ν0​e−ψ(z)/s2, hence ψ(z)=−s2ln[ν(z)/ν0​]. For a locally flat rotation curve this yields
ρtot​(R0​,0)=−4πGs2​dz2d2lnν​​0​.
(54)
This example shows explicitly how a measured tracer scale and velocity scale constrain the gravitating density; using the tracer's own density as the mass source would instead impose an unjustified self-gravitating model.
The inferred density includes luminous and faint stars, gas and dark matter. Comparing it with an independently estimated baryonic census tests for extra gravitating mass. An excess need not be a dark disk: a dark matter halo also contributes to the local force. Conversely, agreement with the baryonic density locally does not exclude a halo at other radii or heights. Vertical disequilibrium, radial mixing, errors in selection or tracer populations, and neglected velocity tilt can mimic a mass discrepancy, so the one-dimensional equilibrium assumptions must be tested when interpreting such an inference.
