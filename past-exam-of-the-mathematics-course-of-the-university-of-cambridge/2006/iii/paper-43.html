In the classical risk model, the insurer's surplus is
U(t)=u+ct−C(t),C(t)=∑j=1N(t)​Xj​,
(13)
where u≥0 is initial capital, N(t) is a Poisson process of rate λ>0, and the claim sizes are positive independent and identically distributed random variables with expected value μ, independent of the claim-arrival process. Premium flows in at constant rate c. A positive relative safety loading θ means
c=(1+θ)λμ,θ>0,​
(14)
so premium income exceeds the expected claim cost per unit time. The ruin stopping time is τ=inf{t≥0:U(t)<0}, with τ=∞ if ruin never occurs, and the ultimate ruin probability is ψ(u)=P(τ<∞).
Assume the stated positive adjustment coefficient R exists and that the claim moment-generating function is finite at R. The Lundberg inequality is
ψ(u)≤e−Ru,u≥0.​
(15)
Here is a proof, including the stopping argument. By conditioning on the Poisson distribution of N(t), the random-sum transform identity gives
EerC(t)=exp(λt(M(r)−1)).
(16)
The adjustment coefficient equation is λ(M(R)−1)=cR. Consequently the exponential surplus martingale
Zt​=exp(R(C(t)−ct)),Z0​=1,
(17)
has expected value one at every finite time. More strongly, the claim process has independent increments, so for s≤t,
E[Zt​∣Fs​]​=Zs​Eexp(R(C(t)−C(s)−c(t−s)))=Zs​exp((t−s)(λ(M(R)−1)−cR))=Zs​.​
(18)
Thus Z is a nonnegative continuous-time martingale for the filtration generated by arrivals and claim sizes. For a fixed finite horizon T, the optional stopping theorem at the bounded stopping time τ∧T gives EZτ∧T​=1. This bounded use is legitimate: the stopped values belong to the closed martingale on [0,T] given by conditional expectations of the integrable variable ZT​.
On {τ≤T}, the surplus is negative, and therefore C(τ)−cτ=u−U(τ)>u. By nonnegativity on the complementary event,
1=EZτ∧T​≥E[Zτ​1{τ≤T}​]≥eRuP(τ≤T).
(19)
The ruin events increase to {τ<∞} as T increases, so continuity of probability gives the claimed Lundberg inequality. No expectation identity at the unbounded stopping time τ is needed. Positive loading by itself does not guarantee a positive adjustment coefficient for every claim law; the existence assumption is used here.
For the exponential distribution of claim sizes, M(r)=1/(1−μr) for r<1/μ. Substitution in the adjustment coefficient equation gives
1−μrμr​=(1+θ)μr.
(20)
Discard the zero root and divide by μr. The unique positive solution is
R=(1+θ)μθ​​,
(21)
which lies strictly inside the finite-transform domain.
For deterministic claim sizes, the claim moment-generating function is eμr. Define h(r)=eμr−1−(1+θ)μr. It satisfies h(0)=0, h′(0)=−θμ<0, h′′(r)=μ2eμr>0, and h(r)→∞ as r→∞. Thus its strict convexity gives exactly one positive zero Rμ​, with h(r)<0 for 0<r<Rμ​.
Put x=μR=θ/(1+θ)∈(0,1). Since −log(1−x)>x, we have ex<1/(1−x). Hence
h(R)=eμR−1−(1+θ)μR<1−μR1​−1−(1+θ)μR=0.
(22)
It follows that
R<Rμ​,e−Rμ​u<e−Ru(u>0).​
(23)
Thus the deterministic-claim Lundberg inequality provides a smaller upper bound at positive capital; at zero capital both bounds equal one. This is a special case of deterministic claims maximize the adjustment coefficient at fixed mean and loading: fixing the mean and loading, variability lowers the exponential decay coefficient relative to deterministic claims. The ordering of these upper bounds alone is not a proof that the actual ultimate ruin probabilities are ordered.

Let M=∑i=1n​mi​ be the total exposure, and define the prior distribution expected value, prior variance and average binomial noise by
η=Eθ,v=Var(θ),w=E[θ(1−θ)].
(24)
Since the prior distribution has a probability density function on (0,1), v>0 and w>0. All relevant moments exist because the parameter and observations are bounded. The binomial distribution gives
E[Xi​∣θ]=θ,Var(Xi​∣θ)=mi​θ(1−θ)​.
(25)
The law of total expectation, law of total variance and law of total covariance consequently yield
EXi​=η,Var(Xi​)=v+mi​w​,Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=v (i=j),Cov(θ,Xi​)=v.
(26)
In particular, conditional independence of the annual observations does not imply unconditional independence: they share the uncertain parameter.
To derive the optimal credibility estimate among affine functions of the observations, put A=∑i​ai​ and write Xi​=θ+εi​. The conditional errors have zero conditional expectation, conditional variance θ(1−θ)/mi​, and zero pairwise conditional covariances. They are also uncorrelated with every integrable function of θ. Therefore the joint mean squared error is
L(a0​,a1​,…,an​)​=E[((1−A)θ−a0​−i∑​ai​εi​)2]=((1−A)η−a0​)2+v(1−A)2+wi∑​mi​ai2​​.​
(27)
For fixed slopes, the first term is uniquely minimized by a0​=(1−A)η. For fixed sum A, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
A2=(∑i​mi​​ai​​mi​​)2≤M∑i​mi​ai2​​,
(28)
with equality exactly when ai​=Ami​/M. Thus the remaining optimization is the strictly convex quadratic
v(1−A)2+Mw​A2.
(29)
Its derivative vanishes at A=Mv/(Mv+w). Consequently the Bühlmann–Straub credibility estimate is
θ=ZM∑i​mi​Xi​​+(1−Z)η,Z=Mv+wMv​=M+w/vM​.​
(30)
The individual coefficients are ai​=Zmi​/M and a0​=(1−Z)η. The strict quadratic minimizations establish global optimality and uniqueness, not just necessary equations. Here v is the variance of hypothetical means, w is the expected process variance, and Z is the Bühlmann–Straub credibility factor. More exposure gives more weight to the observed claim fraction. Since 0<Z<1, the estimate remains between the observed pooled fraction and the prior expected value. If one separately permits a degenerate prior, v=0 leads to the constant estimate η, interpreted as Z=0.
For the uniform distribution prior on (0,1),
η=21​,v=121​,w=∫01​θ(1−θ)dθ=61​.
(31)
With two years, put M=m1​+m2​ and s=Y1​+Y2​=m1​X1​+m2​X2​. Then Z=M/(M+2) and
θ=M+2s+1​=m1​+m2​+2m1​X1​+m2​X2​+1​.​
(32)
To compare with the Bayes estimator under squared error loss, use conditional independence to multiply the two binomial likelihood functions. Terms independent of θ cancel on normalization, and the uniform prior makes the posterior density proportional to θs(1−θ)M−s. Hence Beta-binomial conjugacy gives
θ∣(Y1​,Y2​)∼Beta(s+1,M−s+1),E[θ∣Y1​,Y2​]=M+2s+1​.
(33)
Finally, for an arbitrary reported value d, conditional quadratic loss decomposes as
E[(θ−d)2∣Y1​,Y2​]=Var(θ∣Y1​,Y2​)+(d−E[θ∣Y1​,Y2​])2.
(34)
The unique minimum is the posterior mean. Therefore the affine credibility estimate and the exact Bayesian estimate coincide in this case. The agreement illustrates exact beta-binomial credibility with unequal exposures; for a general prior distribution, minimizing over affine functions of the observations need not recover the unrestricted posterior mean.
