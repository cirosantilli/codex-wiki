A linear operator Z on a Banach space is called a dissipative operator when
∥(λI−Z)f∥≥λ∥f∥(λ>0, f∈D(Z)).
(10)
On a Hilbert space, using the inner product linear in its first argument,
∥(λI−Z)f∥2=λ2∥f∥2−2λRe⟨Zf,f⟩+∥Zf∥2.
(11)
If Re⟨Zf,f⟩≤0, this proves the defining inequality. Conversely that inequality implies 2λRe⟨Zf,f⟩≤∥Zf∥2 for every λ>0; letting λ→∞ gives
Z is dissipative⟺Re⟨Zf,f⟩≤0 for all f∈D(Z).​
(12)
For the next spectral assertion, dissipativity must be retained as a hypothesis. Let Z be closed, densely defined and dissipative. For α>0, the dissipative bound makes αI−Z injective. Its range is closed: if (αI−Z)fn​→h, the bound makes fn​ Cauchy, and then Zfn​=αfn​−(αI−Z)fn​ converges. Closedness produces a preimage of h. If the range is all of H, the same bound gives a bounded inverse, so α is in the resolvent set. If the closed range is proper, there is a nonzero vector in its orthogonal complement, and the definition of the adjoint of a densely defined operator gives
Ran(αI−Z)⊥=ker(αI−Z∗).
(13)
This proves that the positive spectrum of a dissipative operator is adjoint point spectrum:
α∈σ(Z)⟺α is an eigenvalue of Z∗(α>0).​
(14)
Without dissipativity the PDF's assertion is false. For example, multiplication by x on L2(0,2) is bounded, closed and densely defined. The value 1 is spectral: normalized functions supported closer and closer to x=1 make ∥(Z−I)f∥ tend to zero. But Z∗=Z has no eigenvector for eigenvalue 1, since a square-integrable function supported on the singleton {1} is zero.
For the given maximal-domain operator Q, finite-support sequences belong to D(Q), so the domain is dense. Coordinatewise limits also show it is closed: if f(j)→f and Qf(j)→h in ℓ2, each defining coordinate equation passes to the limit, giving Qf=h∈ℓ2 and hence f∈D(Q).
Put gn​=2nfn​ and h=Qf. For n≥1,
gn​−gn−1​=−2−nhn​.
(15)
Since ∑n≥1​2−n∣hn​∣<∞ by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, gn​ has a finite limit ℓf​. Its increments are square summable as well. Direct summation gives
Ren=0∑N​(Qf)n​fn​​​=−n=0∑N​∣gn​∣2+n=1∑N​Re(gn−1​gn​​)=−21​∣g0​∣2−21​∣gN​∣2−21​n=1∑N​∣gn​−gn−1​∣2.​
(16)
The original inner-product series converges because Qf,f∈ℓ2. Taking N→∞ therefore gives
Re⟨Qf,f⟩=−21​(∣g0​∣2+∣ℓf​∣2+n=1∑∞​∣gn​−gn−1​∣2)≤0.​
(17)
Thus Q is dissipative, including its full maximal domain; the limiting boundary term has not been discarded.
To decide generation, solve (αI−Q)f=h for arbitrary h∈ℓ2 and fixed α>0. The first coordinate gives g0​=f0​=h0​/(α+1), and the rest give
gn​=1+α4−ngn−1​+2−nhn​​(n≥1).
(18)
Because all denominators are at least one,
∣gn​∣≤∣g0​∣+∑k=1n​2−k∣hk​∣≤∣g0​∣+3​1​(∑k≥1​∣hk​∣2)1/2.
(19)
Hence gn​ is bounded, so fn​=2−ngn​ belongs to ℓ2. The coordinate equations give Qf=αf−h∈ℓ2, proving f∈D(Q) and surjectivity of αI−Q. Together with density, closedness and dissipativity, the Lumer-Phillips theorem proves the contraction generator from an exponentially weighted difference operator conclusion:
Q generates a strongly continuous contraction semigroup on ℓ2.​
(20)
The domain is crucial. The formal transposed matrix suggests a positive-eigenvalue solution
un​=2−nu0​∏k=0n−1​(1+α4−k),
(21)
which is square summable because the products converge. It is not a true adjoint eigenvector: the admissible test vector fn​=2−n has Qf=(−1,0,0,…), so ⟨Qf,u⟩=−u0​​, while α⟨f,u⟩=αu0​​∑n​4−n∏k<n​(1+α4−k). These cannot agree for α>0 unless u0​=0. This exemplifies how boundary terms exclude formal adjoint eigenvectors, and explains why a calculation using only finite-support test vectors would give the wrong generation answer.
