Let X be a connected compact Riemann surface of geometric genus g, equivalently a smooth projective curve over C, and put V=H0(X,KX​). Choose a symplectic basis a1​,…,ag​,b1​,…,bg​ of H1​(X,Z) with ai​⋅bj​=δij​. For a closed differential form α, write Ai​(α)=∫ai​​α and Bi​(α)=∫bi​​α. The underlying bilinear identity is
∫X​α∧β=∑i=1g​(Ai​(α)Bi​(β)−Bi​(α)Ai​(β)).
(1)
It holds for any two smooth closed one-forms. In particular, the Riemann bilinear relations for holomorphic differential forms are
∑i​(Ai​(ω)Bi​(η)−Bi​(ω)Ai​(η))=0,i∑i​(Ai​(ω)Bi​(ω)​−Bi​(ω)Ai​(ω)​)>0
(2)
for ω,η∈V and ω=0 in the inequality. The inequality fixes the orientation and the sign convention.
To prove the identity, cut X along the symplectic basis to a fundamental polygon P. On P, a closed one-form α has a primitive F. The Stokes theorem gives
∫P​α∧β=∫∂P​Fβ.
(3)
Opposite copies of each cut have opposite orientations, while their values of F differ by the corresponding period of α. Pairing those edges therefore leaves Ai​(α)∫bi​​β−Bi​(α)∫ai​​β for the ith handle. Summing proves the identity. The same calculation can be made with a small disk about the polygon's vertices removed; its extra boundary terms tend to zero, so the vertices cause no additional term.
For holomorphic differential forms ω,η, their wedge product of differential forms vanishes: locally both are multiples of dz. This proves the first relation. If ω=f(z)dz and z=x+iy, then
iω∧ω=2∣f(z)∣2dx∧dy.
(4)
A nonzero holomorphic differential form is nonzero on an open set, so the integral is strictly positive. Applying the identity to ω,ω proves the second relation.
These relations first prove that the map V→Cg taking a-periods is injective: if every Ai​(ω) vanishes, the positive integral just computed would vanish. Since dimV=g by the Riemann-Roch theorem, it is an isomorphism. Consequently there is a unique basis ω1​,…,ωg​ with ∫aj​​ωi​=δij​. Set τij​=∫bj​​ωi​. The first relation gives τij​=τji​, and the second gives, for any nonzero column c,
i∫X​(∑i​ci​ωi​)∧(∑i​ci​ωi​)​=2ct(Imτ)c>0.
(5)
Thus the normalized period matrix of a complex torus is symmetric and has positive-definite imaginary part:
τt=τ,Y=Imτ>0.​
(6)
The integration map sends a cycle γ to the functional ω↦∫γ​ω in V∗. In the normalized basis its image is the period lattice
Λ=Zg+τZg.
(7)
Indeed, the real-linear map (x,y)↦x+τy from R2g to Cg is an isomorphism: its imaginary part is Yy, and Y is invertible. Its image of Z2g is therefore discrete and has compact quotient. This proves that
Jac(X)=V∗/Λ≅Cg/(Zg+τZg)​
(8)
is a well-defined g-dimensional complex torus, rather than merely a quotient by an arbitrary subgroup.
The relations also provide its canonical principal polarization of a complex torus. With the convention that a Hermitian form is conjugate-linear in its first argument, put H(z,w)=z,tY−1w and E=ImH. Symmetry of τ gives
E(m+τn,m′+τn′)=mtn′−ntm′,E(v,iv)=H(v,v)>0.
(9)
In particular E is integral and unimodular on Λ, and it is compatible with the complex structure. These are exactly the positivity and integrality conditions for a Riemann form on a complex torus; they make the complex torus a Jacobian variety with a principal polarization of a complex torus. If instead one uses a first-argument-linear Hermitian form, the same alternating form is −ImH. Changing the symplectic basis changes the coordinates but not this polarized quotient. For g=0, V=0 and the Jacobian variety is a point.

Fix p0​∈X on a connected compact Riemann surface, and construct its Jacobian variety by periods as in the first solution. For a divisor on an algebraic curve E=∑p​np​p of degree zero, define
AJ(E)=[ω⟼∑p​np​∫p0​p​ω]∈Jac(X).
(10)
Changing the paths adds a period lattice element; changing p0​ adds a common integral multiplied by ∑np​=0. The Abel theorem for divisors says
AJ(E)=0⟺E=div(f) for a nonzero meromorphic function f.​
(11)
Equivalently, effective divisors on an algebraic curve of the same degree have the same Abelian sum exactly when they are equivalent under linear equivalence of divisors.
We need a meromorphic version of the Riemann bilinear relations. Let η be a differential of the third kind, with residues np​ at its simple poles, and let ω be a holomorphic differential form. Choose representatives of the cycles and paths avoiding the poles in the same cut polygon, and let F(p)=∫p0​p​ω there. Then
∑i​(Ai​(ω)Bi​(η)−Bi​(ω)Ai​(η))=2πi∑p​np​F(p).
(12)
Here the equality is an equality of the chosen complex numbers, before taking any quotient. To prove it, remove small disks about the poles. Since d(Fη)=ω∧η=0 on the remaining polygon, its boundary integral is zero. Pairing opposite outer edges gives the left side, just as in the first solution. The clockwise boundary about p contributes −2πinp​F(p) by the residue theorem. Moving these terms to the other side proves the formula and its sign.
Such an η exists whenever ∑np​=0. Here is the required existence argument. Let S be the reduced support of E, and consider
0⟶KX​⟶KX​(S)res​⨁p∈S​Cp​⟶0.
(13)
Locally the last map takes cdz/z to c. By Serre duality, the obstruction in H1(KX​)≅C is dual to restriction of constant functions, so it is the sum of the residues. Thus the kernel of this sum is exactly the image of global meromorphic differentials. Alternatively, the Riemann-Roch theorem gives h0(KX​(S))−h0(KX​)=∣S∣−1, and the residue theorem identifies that image with the same codimension-one subspace. Subtracting a linear combination of the normalized holomorphic differential forms makes Ai​(η)=0 for every i.
Suppose now that AJ(E)=0. Let ui​=∑p​np​∫p0​p​ωi​ with the paths used above. There are integer columns m,n such that u=m+τn. The normalized meromorphic bilinear formula gives Bi​(η)=2πiui​. Therefore
η′=η−2πi∑j​nj​ωj​
(14)
has a-periods −2πini​ and b-periods 2πimi​. Its integrals around the poles are also in 2πiZ. These cycles generate the homology of the punctured surface, so
f(q)=exp(∫q∗​q​η′)
(15)
is a single-valued, nowhere-zero holomorphic function away from the support of E. Near p, write η′=np​dz/z+h(z)dz. Then f=znp​exp(H(z)) times a nonzero constant, with H′=h. It extends meromorphically with order exactly np​. Hence div(f)=E.
Conversely, if E=div(f), take η=df/f. It has residues np​ and every period lies in 2πiZ: continuation of a local logarithm of f changes it by an integral multiple of 2πi. Write Ai​(η)=2πiri​ and Bi​(η)=2πisi​. Applying the meromorphic bilinear formula to ωi​ gives
ui​=si​−∑j​τij​rj​.
(16)
Thus u∈Λ and AJ(E)=0, completing both directions without assuming the conclusion as a property of the Jacobian variety.
For completeness, the effective degree-d version lives on the symmetric product of a curve X(d)=Xd/Sd​. The sum of integrals on Xd is invariant under the finite symmetric group and descends to the Abelian sum map ud​. The quotient is locally described by elementary symmetric coordinates, so repeated points are included. The proof above identifies its fibre over ud​(D) with
∣D∣=PH0(X,OX​(D)).
(17)
Explicitly, a nonzero global section gives its effective zero divisor, and two sections give the same divisor precisely when their quotient is constant. These maps are holomorphic in local symmetric coordinates. More intrinsically, a family of effective divisors on an algebraic curve in this fixed class determines a line of sections of OX​(D), after locally trivializing any line bundle pulled back from the parameter space. Conversely such a line of sections gives its family of zero divisors on an algebraic curve. These operations are inverse and unaffected by that trivialization, proving the identification with the projective space in families as well as on points.
Finally this description identifies the analytic Jacobian variety with Pic0(X). Every degree-zero line bundle has a meromorphic section: after twisting by a sufficiently large effective divisor, the Riemann-Roch theorem supplies a nonzero global section. Its divisor represents the original line bundle. The correspondence to the period quotient is well-defined and injective by the theorem. It is surjective because one may choose g distinct points with independent evaluations of holomorphic differential forms; the derivative of the sum of their Abel integrals is then an isomorphism. Hence the subgroup generated by point differences contains an open subset of the connected complex torus, and must be the whole group. Local integration coordinates, or the exponential sequence for OX​, give the same holomorphic identification. This justifies the subsequent use of degree-d line bundles as points of a translate of the Jacobian variety.

Let X be a smooth projective curve over C, let D=∑p​mp​p be an effective divisor on an algebraic curve of degree d, and identify a translate of the Jacobian variety with Picd(X). The precise derivative theorem is
TD​X(d)=H0(X,OD​(D)),dud​∣D​=δD​,​
(18)
where δD​ is the connecting homomorphism of
0⟶OX​⟶OX​(D)⟶OD​(D)⟶0.
(19)
The target is Tud​(D)​Jac(X)=H1(X,OX​)=H0(X,KX​)∗ under Serre duality. Its dual is restriction of holomorphic differential forms to the length-d closed subscheme D:
(dud​∣D​)∗:H0(KX​)⟶H0(KX​∣D​).
(20)
In particular, this includes derivatives of the local coefficients at repeated points, rather than simply evaluating once for each point of the support.
Here is a local proof of all these identifications. Near a point of multiplicity m, take a coordinate z vanishing at that point. Nearby effective divisors are represented by monic polynomials of degree m. A first-order deformation has equation
zm−εv(z)=0,v(z)=v0​+v1​z+⋯+vm−1​zm−1,ε2=0.
(21)
These coefficients are the elementary symmetric coordinates on the symmetric product of a curve; they give m independent tangent directions even at a repeated point. Dividing the variation of the equation by the original equation identifies the tangent direction with the principal part v(z)/zm. Doing this at each point gives H0(OD​(D)), a space of dimension d.
To see the derivative of the line bundle directly, use the local frame 1/(zm−εv) for OX​(Dε​) and the frame 1 outside the small disks. Relative to the frame 1/zm at ε=0, its transition multiplier changes by
zm−εvzm​=1+εzmv​.
(22)
First-order changes of line bundle transition functions have the form 1+εcij​; changing frames adds a Čech coboundary. Thus the tangent space to the Picard group is H1(OX​), and the derivative is exactly the Čech cocycle of these principal parts, namely δD​.
This agrees with differentiating the Abel integrals, including the sign. If ω=f(z)dz and F′=f, the sum of F at the roots of zm−εv has derivative
resz=0​(zmv(z)​ω).
(23)
For example, expand F=∑k≥1​fk−1​zk/k. The first-order Newton identities give ∂ε​∑zik​=kvm−k​ for 1≤k≤m, and zero for k>m, yielding exactly the residue above. Equivalently the same formula follows by differentiating the contour integral for the sum over roots. Serre duality pairs the principal part class with ω by the sum of these residues. Locally this pairing is perfect between the principal parts z−1,…,z−m and the jets 1,z,…,zm−1 times dz. This proves the asserted dual restriction map.
The long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology now gives the kernel and rank without any reduced-support assumption:
ker(dud​∣D​)=H0(OX​(D))/C,rank(dud​∣D​)=d+1−h0(OX​(D)).​
(24)
By duality,
im(dud​∣D​)=H0(KX​(−D))⊥.​
(25)
This is the geometric form of the derivative of the Abelian sum map. For distinct points, the image is spanned by the evaluation vectors of the canonical map; at repeated points, the corresponding osculating directions must also be included. The Riemann-Roch theorem confirms the rank as g−h0(KX​(−D)). When h0(D)=1, the Abelian sum map is an immersion at D. For d=g−1 and h0(D)=1, its image is a hyperplane annihilated by the unique nonzero holomorphic differential form vanishing along D, which will be the tangent hyperplane to the theta divisor.

Assume X is a connected compact Riemann surface of geometric genus g≥1. Work first in Picg−1(X), translating back to the Jacobian variety by tensoring with OX​(−(g−1)p0​) when desired. Put
Θ=Wg−1​={L:degL=g−1, h0(X,L)>0}.
(26)
This is the image of the Abelian sum map from X(g−1). The Riemann singularity theorem asserts that this image is a reduced Cartier divisor and that
multL​Θ=h0(X,L).​
(27)
Here multiplicity means the order of a local defining equation in the maximal ideal of the smooth ambient Jacobian variety. Consequently the singular points are precisely the classes with at least two independent sections. We will prove the multiplicity equality, including the nonvanishing of its proposed leading term.
Choose an effective divisor E of degree n≥g, and a local holomorphic family L of degree-g−1 line bundles about L on X×U, where U is a small neighborhood in Picg−1(X). Such a family is obtained by varying the Čech cocycles of a line bundle. Since degL′(E)>2g−2 for all L′∈U, Serre duality gives H1(L′(E))=0; the Riemann-Roch theorem gives h0(L′(E))=n. The cohomology and base change for line bundles on a curve then identifies
A=π∗​L(E),B=π∗​(L(E)∣E​)
(28)
as vector bundles of rank n. Evaluation defines a matrix M:A→B. For each L′, its exact sequence is
0⟶H0(L′)⟶AL′​M(L′)​BL′​⟶H1(L′)⟶0.
(29)
Thus f=detM vanishes exactly on Θ.
The function f is not identically zero. To exhibit a degree-g−1 line bundle with no section, choose g distinct points p1​,…,pg​ giving independent evaluation functionals on H0(KX​). Such points can be chosen successively because a nonzero holomorphic differential form cannot vanish everywhere. For F=∑pi​, the Riemann-Roch theorem gives h0(OX​(F))=1. Choose q outside F. The unique section of OX​(F) does not vanish at q, so OX​(F−q) has no section. Therefore the evaluation determinant genuinely cuts out a hypersurface. Its support is irreducible, since it is the image of the irreducible symmetric product of a curve X(g−1).
Fix now L∈Θ, and put r=h0(L)=h1(L), the equality following from the Riemann-Roch theorem. The matrix M(L) has an invertible (n−r) by (n−r) block. After invertible holomorphic row and column operations, its local form is
(In−r​0​0N(x)​),N(0)=0,
(30)
where N is r by r. More explicitly, eliminate the invertible block by its Schur complement; the discarded determinant is a holomorphic unit. Hence f is a unit times detN. Every entry of N lies in the maximal ideal, so its order is at least r.
The linear term of N is the cup-product map
N1​(ξ):H0(L)⟶H1(L),s⟼ξ⌣s,ξ∈H1(OX​).
(31)
To verify this, deform the transition functions as gij​(1+εξij​) and try to lift a section s to s+εs′. Its first-order gluing equation has right side ξij​sj​, whose Čech cohomology class is exactly the obstruction to the lift. The kernel-to-cokernel derivative of the evaluation matrix gives that same obstruction. Under Serre duality, if s1​,…,sr​ and t1​,…,tr​ are bases of H0(L) and H0(KX​⊗L−1), then
(N1​(ξ))ji​=⟨ξ,si​tj​⟩.
(32)
We must show that this matrix is invertible for some ξ; otherwise the lower bound on multiplicity would not be an equality.
For each of these two r-dimensional section spaces, there are r distinct points with independent evaluations. Choose them successively, using a section in the remaining kernel to find the next point. The set of such r-tuples is a nonempty open subset of Xr. The two open sets intersect because Xr is irreducible, and we may avoid their diagonals. Choose q1​,…,qr​ in that intersection and trivialize L and KX​ there. Both matrices
Ski​=si​(qk​),Tkj​=tj​(qk​)
(33)
are invertible. Let ξ∈H0(KX​)∗=H1(OX​) be the sum of the evaluation functionals at these points, with any nonzero weights ck​. Then
N1​(ξ)=Ttdiag(c1​,…,cr​)S
(34)
is invertible. It follows that the degree-r polynomial detN1​ is nonzero, so ordL​f=r. This proves the needed invertible cup-product direction for a line bundle on a curve by an explicit construction.
We still need to identify this determinant hypersurface with the reduced image, rather than a multiple of it. Choose g−1 distinct points imposing independent conditions on H0(KX​). For their sum D, the Riemann-Roch theorem gives h0(D)=h0(KX​−D)=1. At its class, the calculation above gives order one. The determinant hypersurface has irreducible support, so its sole divisor on an algebraic curve coefficient is one everywhere. A local hypersurface in a smooth space with these divisor on an algebraic curve coefficients is reduced. Thus it is exactly the reduced image Wg−1​, and the multiplicity formula holds for that image itself.
The argument gives the useful additional description
the tangent cone at L is defined by det(ξ⌣−)=0.​
(35)
For r=1, its nonzero linear equation is ⟨ξ,st⟩=0. This agrees with the derivative of the Abelian sum map: if div(s)=D, then st is the unique holomorphic differential form vanishing along D. For r≥2, the local equation has no linear term and the point is singular. When g=1, W0​ is the single trivial line bundle in Pic0(X), with multiplicity one; the argument includes this case using the empty effective divisor. In genus zero there is no Wg−1​ of effective divisors on an algebraic curve, so that hypothesis is necessary.

The Torelli theorem concerns a connected smooth projective curve over C and its canonical principal polarization of a complex torus. Its conclusion is
(Jac(X),[ΘX​])≅(Jac(Y),[ΘY​])⟹X≅Y.​
(36)
The polarization of a complex torus is part of the hypothesis. We will reconstruct the curve intrinsically from the Jacobian variety and the theta divisor, using the Gauss map of a theta divisor, and prove the reconstruction in both the nonhyperelliptic and hyperelliptic curve cases. The needed biduality and ordinary-tangency facts about projective dual varieties are the general projective-duality results permitted here.
First explain why the polarized data determine the theta divisor up to translation. On J=Jac(X), take integral one-form classes αi​,βi​ dual to the symplectic cycle basis. Pullback by the degree-one Abelian sum map identifies them with the corresponding classes on X; the Riemann bilinear relations give
∫X​αi​∧βj​=δij​,∫X​αi​∧αj​=∫X​βi​∧βj​=0.
(37)
On Xg−1, the sum map pulls each one-form back to the sum of its pullbacks from the factors. It has generic degree (g−1)! onto Wg−1​: the Abel theorem for divisors and the existence of classes with h0=1 give a unique effective unordered representative, and ordering gives the factorial. Integrating a wedge of 2g−2 basis one-forms over the product, only terms assigning a matching pair αi​,βi​ to each factor survive. Dividing by (g−1)! gives integral one when precisely one matching pair is omitted, and zero for all other basis wedges. Hence the Poincaré dual class of Wg−1​ is
c1​(OJ​(Θ))=∑i=1g​αi​∧βi​.
(38)
This is exactly the integral form of the canonical principal polarization of a complex torus constructed by periods.
Its associated line bundle has one-dimensional section space. One can check this without any theta multiplicity assumption: in normalized period coordinates a representative has sections satisfying
F(z+m)=F(z),F(z+τn)=e−πintτn−2πintzF(z).
(39)
Expand the first periodicity in a Fourier series F(z)=∑k∈Zg​ck​e2πiktz. The second periodicity forces ck​=c0​eπiktτk. Positive definiteness of Imτ makes this series normally convergent, so the section space has dimension exactly one. Other line bundles giving the same polarization of a complex torus differ by a degree-zero line bundle. The map x↦Tx∗​L⊗L−1 is an isomorphism from J to its dual abelian variety for a principal polarization of a complex torus: on lattices it is the unimodular alternating form. Thus all these line bundles are translates, and so are their unique zero divisors. In particular the given polarized data recover the reduced divisor on an algebraic curve Wg−1​ up to translation. Translation does not change tangent hyperplanes after identifying tangent spaces with T0​J.
Put V=T0​J=H0(KX​)∗ and P=P(V∗). At a smooth class L=OX​(D)∈Θ, the Riemann singularity theorem says h0(D)=1. The derivative of the Abelian sum map identifies the translated tangent hyperplane with H0(KX​(−D))⊥. Thus the intrinsic Gauss map of a theta divisor is
Γ:Θsm​⟶P,Γ(L)=[ω],H0(KX​(−D))=Cω.
(40)
In particular
D≤div(ω).
(41)
We will see that this map is dominant and generically finite. Its function-field extension therefore determines an intrinsic finite cover: normalize P in C(Θ), obtaining Z→P. Normalization in a finite extension is finite here. Let B⊂P be its reduced codimension-one branch locus. Using this finite cover avoids counting omitted points of the rational Gauss map of a theta divisor as ramification. Everything in this construction is determined by the polarized Jacobian variety.
Suppose first that g≥3 and X is nonhyperelliptic. Its canonical map embeds it as a nondegenerate curve C⊂P(V). For clarity, this follows directly from the Riemann-Roch theorem: h0(p)=1 on a positive-genus curve, so KX​ has no base point. If a length-two effective divisor E had h0(E)≥2, a nonconstant meromorphic function with poles bounded by E would give a map of degree at most two to P1. Degree one forces genus zero, and degree two is the hyperelliptic curve case. Hence h0(E)=1, and the Riemann-Roch theorem gives h0(KX​(−E))=g−2. The canonical sections therefore separate every pair of points and every tangent direction, proving the embedding.
Let U⊂P be the open set of hyperplanes transverse to C. A hyperplane in U cuts out n=2g−2 distinct points. These points span the hyperplane: if a second independent canonical section vanished at all of them, its zero divisor would have to equal the same degree-2g−2 divisor, and the ratio of the two sections would be a constant. Consequently some g−1 of the intersection points are independent. For the divisor D formed by such a subset, h0(KX​(−D))=1 and h0(D)=1.
We need to know that every (g−1)-subset of a general hyperplane section has this property. Here is the monodromy argument, with its essential steps. The incidence space choosing one intersection point is a projective bundle over C before restricting to U, so it is irreducible. The incidence space choosing two distinct ordered intersection points is likewise a projective bundle over C×C minus its diagonal, with fibre Pg−3; it too is irreducible. Restricting to transverse sections preserves irreducibility and hence connectedness of these covers. Thus the monodromy action of a covering space on the n intersection points is two-transitive. A general tangent hyperplane has just one contact of order two, by characteristic-zero projective biduality theorem; locally its intersection equation is z2=t together with the other simple points. A small loop in t exchanges exactly those two points. Two-transitivity conjugates this transposition to every transposition, so the monodromy is the full symmetric group Sn​.
It follows that the finite étale cover of U choosing a (g−1)-subset is connected and irreducible. The condition of dependent evaluations is closed on that cover. It is proper because we have exhibited an independent subset; its image under the finite map is therefore a proper closed subset of U. Outside that image every subset is independent. By the Abel theorem for divisors, distinct such subsets have distinct Abelian sums: h0(D)=1 means there is only one effective representative of its class. Thus the subset cover is birational to Θ with its Gauss map of a theta divisor, and
degΓ=(g−12g−2​).
(42)
More precisely it is exactly the restriction of Z→P over U, since this étale cover is normal and has the same function field.
There can consequently be no branch component over U. Its complement is the irreducible hypersurface C∗ of tangent hyperplanes, the projective dual variety of C. Around a general point of C∗, the transposition of two roots acts nontrivially on (g−1)-subsets: choose a subset containing exactly one of those two roots. Hence the finite cover is ramified there. This proves the exact identity
B=C∗.​
(43)
The quoted biduality theorem now recovers C=(C∗)∗=B∗ as an embedded curve, and the canonical map identifies X with C. This proves reconstruction in the nonhyperelliptic case, including special curves rather than only a general curve in moduli.
Now suppose that X is a hyperelliptic curve of genus g≥3. Let π:X→P1 be its degree-two map. The Riemann-Hurwitz formula gives 2g+2 branch points b1​,…,b2g+2​. Choose an affine coordinate with no branch at infinity and write
y2=∏i=12g+2​(x−bi​).
(44)
The forms dx/y,xdx/y,…,xg−1dx/y are holomorphic and independent; their orders at the two points at infinity show there are exactly g of them. Thus the canonical map factors as π followed by the degree-g−1 rational normal curve embedding
ν:P1⟶P(V),x⟼[1:x:⋯:xg−1].
(45)
Write R=ν(P1).
A general hyperplane section of R consists of g−1 distinct points x1​,…,xg−1​ avoiding the branch points. The corresponding canonical divisor on X consists of the g−1 pairs π−1(xj​). Choosing one lift from each pair gives an effective divisor D with h0(D)=1: the evaluation matrix of the canonical forms has Vandermonde rank g−1. If instead a degree-g−1 sub-divisor contains an entire pair, it has at least two sections, because that pair is a fibre of π and moves in a pencil. Every choice not taking exactly one point from each pair contains an entire pair. Therefore the generic smooth theta points over the hyperplane are exactly the independent choices of one lift in each pair, and
degΓ=2g−1.
(46)
The cover of these choices is again the restriction of Z to the open set of transverse sections of R avoiding the bi​: the identification on a dense open follows from the Abel theorem for divisors, and the choice cover is finite étale and normal. Its components would correspond to components of the function field of the irreducible theta divisor, so it is connected.
There are precisely two types of codimension-one branching. If a simple root xj​ crosses a branch point bi​, its two lifts exchange, giving nontrivial monodromy on the choices. This yields the hyperplane
Hi​={H∈P:ν(bi​)∈H}.
(47)
If two roots collide away from the bi​, a local transverse parameter gives roots x0​±t​. Trivialize the two sheets of π near x0​. Choices selecting different sheets for the two roots are exchanged when the roots exchange; hence this also causes branching. Its locus is R∗, the hypersurface of tangent hyperplanes to R. Away from these two loci all roots and all their lifts vary locally without branching, so there are no other branch components. Consequently
B=R∗ ∪ i=1⋃2g+2​Hi​.​
(48)
For g≥3, R∗ is irreducible and nonlinear. Indeed, hyperplane sections of the degree-g−1 rational normal curve are binary forms of degree g−1, and the repeated-root hypersurface is their irreducible discriminant of degree 2g−4≥2. Thus B intrinsically separates into its single nonlinear component R∗ and its 2g+2 linear components. Biduality recovers R, while dualizing Hi​ recovers the point ν(bi​) on R.
These data recover the double cover uniquely. Identify R with P1 and move infinity away from the recovered branch set. Its function field is
C(x)(∏i​(x−bi​)​).
(49)
Any other quadratic extension with the same simple branch points has a defining rational function whose ratio to this product has even valuation everywhere. On P1 such a rational function is a constant times a square, and every nonzero complex constant is a square. The quadratic extensions are therefore isomorphic; their smooth projective models are isomorphic. This proves the hyperelliptic reconstruction. The branch locus also distinguishes the two cases: the nonhyperelliptic locus has one irreducible branch hypersurface, whereas the hyperelliptic locus has the additional 2g+2 linear components.
Finally handle the small genera. In genus two, the theta divisor is W1​, the image of X itself. The Abelian sum map on X is injective because a principal divisor p−q with p=q would give a degree-one map to P1. Its derivative is nonzero since the canonical bundle has no base point, so this compact injective immersion identifies X with W1​. In genus one the same argument identifies X with its Jacobian variety after choosing an origin. In genus zero the Riemann-Roch theorem supplies a function with one simple pole, giving X≅P1. These cases complete the proof: the canonically polarized Jacobian determines the curve in every genus.
