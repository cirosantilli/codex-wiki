Extend q by zero to the negative half-line. Its ordinary Fourier transform is then exactly the Half-range Fourier transform q​(k). The Fourier inversion theorem gives the real-line term for x>0; no endpoint convention at x=0 is needed.
It remains to show that each extra contour contributes zero, independently of its coefficient. For Imk<0, the integral defining q​ is holomorphic. With α=e2πi/3, multiplication by α2 rotates the sector E into −2π/3≤arg(α2k)≤−π/3, and multiplication by α rotates D into −2π/3≤arg(αk)≤−π/3. Thus the two rotated transforms are holomorphic on their respective upper sectors.
Orient the contours as in the PDF: ∂E runs from infinity on the π/3 ray into zero and then out along the positive real axis; ∂D runs from negative real infinity into zero and then out along the 2π/3 ray. Both have their sector on their left. Smooth decay and integration by parts give q​(k)=O(1/k) in closed lower-half-plane sectors. The factor eikx decays in the upper half-plane for x>0. Close each sector by a large arc; the arc integral vanishes by Jordan lemma, including its short portion near the real axis. The Cauchy integral theorem therefore gives the rotated null contours for half-range Fourier inversion:
∫∂E​eikxq​(α2k)dk=0,∫∂D​eikxq​(αk)dk=0(x>0).​
(1)
Adding arbitrary constant multiples of these two zero integrals to ordinary Fourier inversion proves the asserted inversion formula. The constants c1​,c2​ are arbitrary in this part; selecting them later is what removes an unknown boundary trace.

Define the initial Half-range Fourier transform Q(k)=∫0∞​e−ikxq0​(x)dx, and, for each available boundary trace, its finite-time spectral boundary transform
Fj​(k,t)=∫0t​eik3sfj​(s)ds.
(2)
Temporarily let fj​=∂xj​q(0,t) also denote the missing trace. Three integrations by parts in x give
∫0∞​e−ikxqxxx​dx=−f2​−ikf1​+k2f0​−ik3q​.
(3)
Consequently the backward-sign Airy half-line global relation is
∂t​q​+ik3q​=k2f0​−ikf1​−f2​,eik3tq​(k,t)=Q(k)+k2F0​(k,t)−ikF1​(k,t)−F2​(k,t).
(4)
The spatial transform is holomorphic below the real axis, while each finite-time Fj​ is an entire function. Moreover, Fj​(αk,t)=Fj​(α2k,t)=Fj​(k,t) because α3=1.
Suppose the missing derivative has order j, and put r=2−j. Its contribution to the right-hand side is aj​krFj​, where a0​=1, a1​=−i, a2​=−1. Apply the inversion from the preceding part at time t and select
c1​=−α−2r,c2​=−α−r.
(5)
The rotated coefficients of that missing trace now equal minus its real-line coefficient. Its entire contribution is therefore
2πaj​​(∫R​−∫∂E​−∫∂D​)eikx−ik3tkrFj​(k,t)dk.
(6)
The real-axis pieces cancel. What remains is the boundary of the middle upper sector, outward on argk=π/3 and inward on argk=2π/3. In this sector Imk3≤0, and
e−ik3tFj​(k,t)=∫0t​e−ik3(t−s)fj​(s)ds
(7)
is bounded by ∫0t​∣fj​(s)∣ds. The factor eikx decays exponentially throughout the closing arc, overcoming kr. There are no poles. Thus the Cauchy integral theorem proves the cubic-dispersion elimination of one missing boundary trace and removes every occurrence of the unknown fj​.
For a compact explicit answer, use only the two prescribed traces to form S(k,t)=Q(k)+H(k,t). The three choices are
prescribed tracesf0​,f1​f0​,f2​f1​,f2​​H(k,t)k2F0​−ikF1​k2F0​−F2​−ikF1​−F2​​c1​−1−α−α2​c2​−1−α2−α​​
(8)
For each row the solution is
q(x,t)=2π1​∫R​eikx−ik3tS(k,t)dk​+2πc1​​∫∂E​eikx−ik3tS(α2k,t)dk+2πc2​​∫∂D​eikx−ik3tS(αk,t)dk.​​
(9)
All quantities in this formula are transforms of the given data. On the contour rays k3 is real, so the time exponential is oscillatory; the integrals use the usual limiting interpretation of Fourier inversion. The decay estimate used for elimination belongs to the middle sector, not to D or E, where e−ik3(t−s) can grow.
At t=0, Fj​=0 and the preceding inversion recovers q0​. The spectral exponential satisfies the Airy equation with the required minus sign. Differentiating the finite-time amplitudes produces only derivatives of the Dirac delta supported at x=0 and zero rotated closed-contour contributions, so the interior equation also holds. Finally, two solutions with the same data have Q=H=0 for their difference, so this representation gives zero: each of the three prescribed pairs determines the solution uniquely in the smooth decaying class.

Use the Wirtinger derivative f=qz​=(qx​−iqy​)/2. Since q satisfies the Laplace equation, ∂zˉ​f=Δq/4=0, so f is holomorphic. The differential relation is understood as a one-form identity:
d(μe−ikz)=e−ikzf(z)dz.
(10)
The differential dz is needed on its right-hand side. Because this form is closed, its integral is independent of path within the semistrip.
Three spectral primitives are obtained by integrating from the two finite corners and from the infinite end:
μ0​=eikz∫0z​e−ikwf(w)dw,μl​=eikz∫ilz​e−ikwf(w)dw,μ∞​=−eikz∫z∞+iImz​e−ikwf(w)dw.
(11)
The last integral is taken horizontally and defines the infinite-end primitive for Imk<0. Their differences are eikz times the three boundary spectral functions. Using counterclockwise boundary orientation, define
ρL​(k)=∫il0​e−ikwf(w)dw,ρB​(k)=∫0∞​e−ikxf(x)dx,ρT​(k)=−ekl∫0∞​e−ikxf(x+il)dx.
(12)
Then μ0​−μ∞​=eikzρB​, μ∞​−μl​=eikzρT​, and μl​−μ0​=eikzρL​. Their sum gives the global relation ρL​+ρB​+ρT​=0 for Imk≤0.
To express these spectra in boundary data, write g0​(x)=q(x,0), gl​(x)=q(x,l), h(y)=q(0,y), n0​(x)=qy​(x,0), nl​(x)=qy​(x,l), and v(y)=qx​(0,y). Direct substitution into f gives
ρB​(k)ρT​(k)ρL​(k)​=21​∫0∞​e−ikx[g0′​(x)−in0​(x)]dx,=−2ekl​∫0∞​e−ikx[gl′​(x)−inl​(x)]dx,=−21​∫0l​eky[h′(y)+iv(y)]dy.​
(13)
These are transforms of tangential derivatives of Dirichlet boundary data and the corresponding Neumann boundary data. The outward derivatives are −n0​ at the bottom, nl​ at the top, and −v at the left.
The boundary spectral representation of a holomorphic function on a semistrip is
qz​(z)=2π1​[∫0i∞​eikzρL​(k)dk+∫0∞​eikzρB​(k)dk+∫0−∞​eikzρT​(k)dk].​
(14)
All three k-rays are oriented outward from zero. To verify the spectral inversion, use
∫0eiθ∞​eik(z−w)dk=z−wi​
(15)
whenever the exponential decays. The positive real ray works for the bottom, the negative real ray for the top, and the positive imaginary ray for the left. Substitution of the boundary spectra therefore turns the boxed formula into (2πi)−1∫∂Ω​f(w)/(w−z)dw=f(z) by the Cauchy integral formula. This also fixes all orientation signs.

Let G0​(k),Gl​(k) denote the Half-range Fourier transforms of g0​,gl​, and let N0​(k),Nl​(k) denote those of the upward derivatives n0​,nl​. Also set
H(k)=∫0l​ekyh(y)dy,W(k)=∫0l​ekyv(y)dy.
(16)
Integrate the tangential derivatives in the three spectral functions by parts. For example, ∫0∞​e−ikxg0′​=−h(0)+ikG0​, and ∫0l​ekyh′=eklh(l)−h(0)−kH. All corner values cancel in ρL​+ρB​+ρT​=0. The result is the semistrip Laplace spectral global relation
eklNl​(k)−N0​(k)+k[G0​(k)−eklGl​(k)]−W(k)−ikH(k)=0.
(17)
For κ>0, use the sine transforms
Gjs​(κ)=∫0∞​sin(κx)gj​(x)dx,Sj​(κ)=∫0∞​sin(κx)nj​(x)dx.
(18)
Reality of q makes W(κ) and W(−κ) real, so taking imaginary parts removes the unknown left-side normal derivative. At k=κ and k=−κ the two consequences of the global relation are
eκlSl​−S0​e−κlSl​−S0​​=κ[eκlGls​−G0s​−H(κ)],=κ[G0s​−e−κlGls​−H(−κ)].​
(19)
Subtracting these equations and then substituting back gives the semistrip Dirichlet-to-Neumann sine transforms entirely in terms of the prescribed Dirichlet boundary data:
S0​(κ)=sinh(κl)κ​[−cosh(κl)G0s​(κ)+Gls​(κ)+∫0l​sinh(κ(l−y))h(y)dy],​
(20)
Sl​(κ)=sinh(κl)κ​[−G0s​(κ)+cosh(κl)Gls​(κ)−∫0l​sinh(κy)h(y)dy].​
(21)
These are the requested transforms of qy​ itself. The bottom outward Neumann boundary data have the opposite sign. The apparent division at κ=0 is handled by a continuous limit whenever the corresponding moments exist; there is no singularity for any κ>0.

Let sj​=sinβj​, cj​=cosβj​. Inverting the two orthogonal derivative systems gives, on the vertical axis,
qx​(0,y)=−s1​h1​(y)−c1​j1​(y),qy​(0,y)=c1​h1​(y)−s1​j1​(y),
(22)
and, on the horizontal axis,
qx​(x,0)=c2​h2​(x)+s2​j2​(x),qy​(x,0)=−s2​h2​(x)+c2​j2​(x).
(23)
Using the Wirtinger derivative qz​=(qx​−iqy​)/2, these become
qz​(iy)=−2e−iβ1​​[j1​(y)+ih1​(y)],qz​(x)=2eiβ2​​[h2​(x)−ij2​(x)].
(24)
Define the appropriate boundary transforms by
H1​(k)H2​(k)​=∫0∞​ekyh1​(y)dy,=∫0∞​e−ikxh2​(x)dx,​J1​(k)J2​(k)​=∫0∞​ekyj1​(y)dy,=∫0∞​e−ikxj2​(x)dx.​
(25)
The first pair is holomorphic for Rek<0; the second pair consists of Half-range Fourier transforms, holomorphic for Imk<0. Parametrizing the vertical boundary by z=iy gives dz=idy, which must be retained. Substitution yields the quarter-plane oblique boundary spectral functions:
q​1​(k)=2e−iβ1​​[H1​(k)−iJ1​(k)],q​2​(k)=−2eiβ2​​[H2​(k)−iJ2​(k)].​
(26)
Their signs follow from the stated clockwise boundary orientations: upward on the left and inward on the bottom.

Since q is harmonic, qz​ is holomorphic and the one-form e−ikzqz​dz is closed. Closing the physical quadrant at infinity gives the global relation
q​1​(k)+q​2​(k)=0(Rek<0, Imk<0).
(27)
In this third spectral quadrant the exponential decays on both boundary axes. Set γ=β1​+β2​. The relation becomes
H1​(k)−iJ1​(k)=eiγ[H2​(k)−iJ2​(k)].
(28)
All four boundary functions are real. Consequently their transforms obey
H1​(kˉ)​=H1​(k),H2​(−kˉ)​=H2​(k),
(29)
and the same identities for J1​,J2​. Complex conjugation of the global relation therefore gives its upper-left consequence
H1​(k)+iJ1​(k)=e−iγ[H2​(−k)+iJ2​(−k)](Rek<0, Imk>0).
(30)
Introduce just one unknown function,
Φ(k)=H2​(−k)−iJ2​(−k)=∫0∞​eikx[h2​(x)−ij2​(x)]dx,Imk>0.​
(31)
It is holomorphic in the upper half-plane. Under the transform-admissible decay assumptions, ∣Φ(k)∣≤∫0∞​(∣h2​∣+∣j2​∣)dx, so it is bounded there.
For k on the positive real ray, use both consequences of the global relation at −k. Adding them removes J1​(−k) and gives
H2​(k)+iJ2​(k)=2eiγH1​(−k)−e2iγΦ(k).
(32)
For k on the positive imaginary ray, use the upper-left relation and H2​(−k)+iJ2​(−k)=2H2​(−k)−Φ(k). The resulting single-function spectral elimination for a rational-angle quadrant is
q​2​(k)q​1​(k)​=−eiβ2​[H2​(k)−eiγH1​(−k)]−2eiβ2​+2iγ​Φ(k),k>0,=e−iβ1​[H1​(k)−e−iγH2​(−k)]+2e−iβ1​−iγ​Φ(k),k∈iR+​.​​
(33)
Boundary limits from the appropriate transform domains are understood. Every remaining unknown boundary derivative is contained in the one bounded upper-half-plane function Φ.

First resolve the orientation sign in quadrant harmonic spectral inversion. The printed spectral definitions traverse the physical boundary clockwise, whereas both reconstruction rays run outward from zero. Integrating first in k gives the kernel i/(z−w). The resulting clockwise Cauchy integral is the negative of the desired holomorphic function. Thus, with exactly the spectral definitions used above, the consistent reconstruction is
qz​(z)=−2π1​[∫0∞​eikzq​2​(k)dk+∫0i∞​eikzq​1​(k)dk].
(34)
The printed plus sign would reconstruct −qz​. For a concrete nonzero example, q(z)=−2Re[(1+i)/(z+1+i)] is real, harmonic, smooth on the closed quadrant and decays along both axes. With β1​=β2​=0, it has h1​(0)=h2​(0)=1 and qz​=(1+i)/(z+1+i)2. The printed orientation returns the negative of this derivative, so the issue persists even under the stated corner condition.
Let a=21​e−iβ1​−iγ. In the expressions from the preceding part, the coefficient of Φ on the imaginary ray is a, and the coefficient on the real ray is −ae4iγ. For each of the three allowed sums, e4iγ=1. The unknown part is therefore proportional to
∫0i∞​eikzΦ(k)dk−∫0∞​eikzΦ(k)dk=0.
(35)
Indeed, close the first spectral quadrant. For z=x+iy with x,y>0, ∣eikz∣=e−(xImk+yRek), so the large-arc contribution vanishes for bounded Φ. The Cauchy integral theorem proves the equality and eliminates the single unknown function.
Substituting only the known pieces leaves
qz​(z)=2π1​[eiβ2​∫0∞​eikzH2​(k)dk−eiβ2​+iγ∫0∞​eikzH1​(−k)dk−e−iβ1​∫0i∞​eikzH1​(k)dk+e−iβ1​−iγ∫0i∞​eikzH2​(−k)dk].
(36)
This already determines the answer from h1​,h2​ alone. Evaluating the elementary ray kernels gives the more direct rational-angle oblique derivative problem on a quadrant formula
qz​(z)=2πi​[​eiβ2​∫0∞​z−xh2​(x)​dx+e−iβ1​−iγ∫0∞​z+xh2​(x)​dx−e−iβ1​∫0∞​z−iyh1​(y)​dy−eiβ2​+iγ∫0∞​z+iyh1​(y)​dy].​​
(37)
No denominator vanishes for an interior point of the first quadrant.
For explicit formulas in the three cases, define
I2​(z)=∫0∞​z2−x2h2​(x)​dx,I1​(z)=∫0∞​z2+y2h1​(y)​dy,
(38)
K2​(z)=∫0∞​z2−x2xh2​(x)​dx,K1​(z)=∫0∞​z2+y2yh1​(y)​dy.
(39)
Combining the paired fractions and using β2​=γ−β1​ yields
qz​(z)=πe−iβ1​​⎩⎨⎧​iz[I2​(z)−I1​(z)],K1​(z)−K2​(z),−iz[I2​(z)+I1​(z)],​γ=0,γ=π/2,γ=π.​​
(40)
There is also a necessary corner compatibility for collinear oblique derivative data. When γ=π/2, the two prescribed derivative directions are opposites, so a continuous corner gradient requires h2​(0)=−h1​(0). Together with the stipulated equality, this forces h1​(0)=h2​(0)=0 in that case. Existence was assumed, so admissible data must satisfy this additional consequence. Smoothness at the corner and decay at infinity also exclude the homogeneous corner ambiguity in a quadrant Laplace problem; no extra singular or growing term is permitted in the boxed reconstruction.
