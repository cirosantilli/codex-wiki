For G=U(n) take the diagonal maximal torus
T={diag(z1​,…,zn​):∣zi​∣=1}.
(1)
The spectral theorem for unitary matrices says that every element is conjugate to an element of T. A diagonal element with pairwise distinct eigenvalues has centralizer exactly T. Its conjugates therefore determine its eigenvalues up to permutation, and the normalizer quotient is W=NG​(T)/T≅Sn​. The character lattice of a torus T is Zn, with eλ(z)=z1λ1​​⋯znλn​​. Conjugation on the matrix unit Eij​ has weight ei​−ej​, so these are the roots. Choose ei​−ej​ positive for i<j; then
ρ=21​(n−1,n−3,…,1−n).
(2)
Dominant integral weights are precisely the integer tuples λ1​≥⋯≥λn​. Negative entries are allowed: they correspond to determinant twists, so polynomial representations alone do not exhaust the irreducible representations of U(n).
Normalize Haar measure on G and T to have mass one, and put
Δ(z)=det(zin−j​)i,j=1n​=∏i<j​(zi​−zj​).
(3)
The Weyl integration formula for U(n) for a continuous class function is
∫U(n)​f(g)dg=n!1​∫[0,2π)n​f(diag(eiθ1​,…,eiθn​))i<j∏​∣eiθi​−eiθj​∣2i=1∏n​2πdθi​​.​
(4)
For a general continuous function, replace f(t) in the torus integral by ∫G/T​f(gtg−1)d(gT), using the normalized invariant quotient measure.
To prove the formula, consider the conjugation map q:G/T×T→G, q(gT,t)=gtg−1. On regular torus elements its fibres have exactly n! points: the possible orderings of the distinct eigenvalues. Equip the group with an invariant inner product on its Lie algebra and split u(n)=t⊕t⊥. After translating the derivative back to the identity, its torus directions contribute the identity, while its transverse directions contribute Adt−1​−I. Each pair i<j supplies a real two-dimensional off-diagonal plane. On that plane Adt​ is multiplication by zi​/zj​, regarded as a plane rotation, so the absolute real determinant of the transverse derivative is
J(t)=∏i<j​∣zi​/zj​−1∣2=∣Δ(z)∣2.
(5)
The change-of-variables theorem, divided by the covering multiplicity, now gives the desired weighted integral up to the constant relating the normalized invariant measures. The nonregular locus has measure zero: repeated eigenvalues are the zero-set of the nonzero discriminant, and the torus coincidence sets likewise have measure zero. Thus its omission causes no change to the integral. To determine the normalization, expand Δ as a sum of n! distinct torus characters. Fourier orthogonality gives ∫T​∣Δ∣2dt=n!. Applying the formula to f=1 fixes the coefficient to 1/n!. This proves both the class-function and conjugacy-averaged forms.
We can now derive the character formula from integration, rather than merely quote it. Use the basic highest-weight theorem: a finite-dimensional irreducible representation has a unique highest weight λ with a one-dimensional highest-weight space; all its other weights are λ minus a nonnegative sum of positive roots. Its character is Weyl invariant. Also, character orthogonality for compact groups gives ∫G​∣χλ​∣2=1. This orthogonality follows by averaging the representation on End(Vλ​): the integral projects onto intertwining operators, whose dimension is one by the Schur lemma.
Let δ=(n−1,n−2,…,0) and, for a strictly decreasing integer tuple η, put Aη​(z)=det(ziηj​​). The finite Laurent polynomial Δχλ​ is alternating. Every alternating Laurent polynomial is a linear combination of the Aη​: collect its monomials by permutation orbits, and observe that an orbit with repeated exponents has zero coefficient by antisymmetry. Different Aη​ are orthogonal on T and each has squared norm n!.
The coefficient of zλ+δ in Δχλ​ is exactly one. Indeed, a contribution from the term zwδ would require a weight λ+δ−wδ in Vλ​. For w=1, the vector δ−wδ is a nonzero nonnegative combination of the positive simple roots, so that weight lies above λ and is impossible. The identity permutation supplies the highest-weight coefficient one. Consequently
Δχλ​=Aλ+δ​+∑η=λ+δ​cη​Aη​.
(6)
Apply the integration formula to ∣χλ​∣2. Torus orthogonality gives
1=n!1​∫T​∣Δχλ​∣2dt=1+∑η=λ+δ​∣cη​∣2.
(7)
All the other coefficients vanish, proving
χλ​(z)=det(zin−j​)det(ziλj​+n−j​)​.​
(8)
The quotient extends across repeated eigenvalues because its product with the denominator is the already defined continuous character. Equivalently, alternant divisibility makes it a Laurent polynomial invariant under permutations; multiply by a determinant power if negative exponents must first be removed.
Finally, δ−ρ=2n−1​(1,…,1) is fixed by the Weyl group, so its common factor cancels in the quotient. Since Aρ​=eρ∏α>0​(1−e−α), the determinant expression is exactly
χλ​=∏α>0​(1−e−α)∑w∈W​ε(w)ew(λ+ρ)−ρ​.
(9)
Using δ in the derivation avoids treating a possibly half-integral ρ as an actual character of the torus. At the identity, taking the leading Vandermonde term of the two determinants gives the accompanying dimension formula
dimVλ​=∏i<j​j−iλi​−λj​+j−i​.
(10)

There are two necessary qualifications to the literal formulation. The whole group G is itself a closed normal subgroup, so the claim about containment in the centre must concern proper closed normal subgroups. Also, the homomorphism conclusion needs a nontrivial target H: for example, SU(2)→{1} has surjective differential onto the zero Lie algebra, but its group kernel is all of SU(2) and no quotient by a finite subgroup is a one-point group. We prove the precise assertions, including these exceptional cases.
Write g=Lie(G). The centre z(g) is invariant under the Adjoint representation, so irreducibility makes it either zero or all of g. In the latter case g is Abelian. For connected G, the adjoint action is then trivial: the group is generated by exponentials, and AdexpX​=eadX=I. A nonzero trivial representation is irreducible only in dimension one. A connected compact one-dimensional Lie group is a circle, so G≅U(1).
For every other G, the Lie algebra of its centre is zero. The centre is a closed compact Lie subgroup; a zero-dimensional compact Lie group is finite. Let N be a closed normal subgroup. The closed-subgroup theorem identifies n=Lie(N) as an adjoint-invariant subspace of g, hence n=0 or n=g. If the latter holds, N contains an identity neighbourhood and is open, so connectedness forces N=G. If N is proper, it therefore has zero Lie algebra and is finite. For any a∈N, the continuous map g↦gag−1 takes connected G into the finite set N, so it is constant and equal to a. Thus
G≅U(1)⟹Z(G) is finite;N⊲G,N closed and proper⟹N⊆Z(G).​
(11)
Proper closed subgroups of the circle are finite as well, and of course central.
Now let dΦ be surjective. The submersion theorem makes Φ(G) contain an identity neighbourhood in H, so it is an open subgroup. Since H is connected, Φ(G)=H. Its group kernel F is closed and normal. If H is nontrivial, F is proper and the preceding argument gives a finite central group kernel; this also holds for G≅U(1), whose proper closed subgroups are finite. The Lie-group first isomorphism theorem gives
H≅G/F,F⊆Z(G) finite,H={1}.​
(12)
If H is trivial, the correct statement is instead H=G/G.
For the Spin groups, use the real Clifford algebra with v2=−∥v∥2. The group Spin(m) consists of products of an even number of unit vectors inside its invertible even part. Conjugation on the embedded Rm defines the standard double covering Spin(m)→SO(m) with group kernel {±1}. The Clifford relations show that a unit-vector reflection, with the usual twisted conjugation, is a hyperplane reflection; the even products give orientation-preserving transformations. The Cartan–Dieudonné theorem states that every orthogonal transformation is a product of reflections, proving surjectivity. For m≥3 this connected covering group is simply connected; Spin(2) is a circle.
Here is an explicit exterior-square construction of the requested six-dimensional map. Put E=C4 with its standard Hermitian form and volume e1​∧e2​∧e3​∧e4​. On Λ2E, write eij​=ei​∧ej​ and define an antilinear map J by
Je12​=e34​,Je13​=−e24​,Je14​=e23​,Je34​=e12​,Je24​=−e13​,Je23​=e14​.
(13)
It satisfies J2=1. Intrinsically, if u∧v=B(u,v)vol and the Hermitian inner product h is linear in its first variable, then B(u,Jv)=h(u,v). Because SU(4) preserves both B and h, it commutes with J. This is the real structure of the SU4 exterior square.
The fixed space W has the following real orthonormal basis, with all vectors divided by 2​:
e12​+e34​,i(e12​−e34​),e13​−e24​,i(e13​+e24​),e14​+e23​,i(e14​−e23​).
(14)
Thus W is six-dimensional over R, its complexification is Λ2E, and the exterior-square action restricts to an orthogonal representation on W. Its determinant is one because SU(4) is connected. We obtain R:SU(4)→SO(W)≅SO(6).
To compute the group kernel, diagonalize g∈SU(4) with eigenvalues z1​,…,z4​. If R(g)=I, its complexified exterior-square action is the identity, hence zi​zj​=1 for every i<j. Comparing pairs shows that all zi​ are equal, and their common value has square one. A unitary matrix with that single eigenvalue is scalar, so kerR={±I4​}. Conversely, those two scalar matrices plainly act trivially on the exterior square.
The differential of R is injective because its group kernel is finite. Both Lie algebras have dimension 15, so the differential is an isomorphism. Its image is consequently open in connected SO(6), hence all of SO(6). Therefore
SU(4)/{±I4​}≅SO(6).​
(15)
This also realizes SU(4) as Spin(6), since SU(4) is simply connected, by uniqueness of the connected simply connected covering group.

For v=(v1​,v2​,v3​) define the skew-symmetric matrix
v=​0v3​−v2​​−v3​0v1​​v2​−v1​0​​.
(16)
Then vx=v×x. This is a linear isomorphism R3→so(3), with inverse ϕ(ξ)=(ξ32​,ξ13​,ξ21​). Since rotations preserve the cross product,
RvR−1=Rv(R∈SO(3)).
(17)
Thus ϕ intertwines the Adjoint representation with the standard representation. The vector triple-product identity gives, for every x,
[v,w]x=v×(w×x)−w×(v×x)=(v×w)×x.
(18)
Consequently [v,w]=v×w​ and the chosen normalization satisfies
ϕ([ξ,η])=ϕ(ξ)×ϕ(η).​
(19)
No undetermined scale remains in this explicit matrix convention.
Identify SU(2) with the unit quaternions using
q=z+wj⟼(z−w​wz​),∣z∣2+∣w∣2=1.
(20)
The relations jz=zj and j2=−1 verify multiplication, and every matrix in SU(2) has this form. If v is imaginary, then v=−v. Quaternionic conjugation reverses product order and q−1=q​, so
qvq−1​=qvq−1=−qvq−1.
(21)
Thus conjugation preserves ImH≅R3. Multiplicativity of the quaternion norm shows that this action preserves the inner product. The unit quaternions form the connected three-sphere, so its determinant, equal to one at the identity, is always one. We obtain a homomorphism C:SU(2)→SO(3).
Its group kernel consists of unit quaternions commuting with every imaginary quaternion. Commuting with both i and j forces a quaternion to be real, so the group kernel is exactly {±1}. To prove surjectivity explicitly, let u be a unit imaginary quaternion and put q=cos(θ/2)+usin(θ/2). For imaginary u,v, multiplication satisfies uv=−u⋅v+u×v. Expanding qvq−1 therefore gives
qvq−1=vcosθ+(u×v)sinθ+u(u⋅v)(1−cosθ).
(22)
This is the Rodrigues rotation formula about axis u. Every element of SO(3) has such an axis-angle description: an odd-dimensional orthogonal matrix of determinant one has an eigenvector of eigenvalue one, and its action on the perpendicular plane is a plane rotation. Hence C is onto, and
SU(2)/{±I2​}≅SO(3).​
(23)
This is the quaternionic form of the Adjoint double cover from SU(2) to SO(3). Its differential sends an imaginary quaternion u to 2u, because [u,v]=2u×v, consistent with the bracket normalization used above.

Put V=Cn. We use complete reducibility of compact-group representations, and the highest-weight classification: the multiplicity of Vλ​ in a completely reducible representation is the dimension of its weight-λ subspace killed by all positive root vectors. Each irreducible summand contributes just its one-dimensional highest-weight line to that subspace.
Symmetrizing and antisymmetrizing the three tensor factors give invariant subspaces Sym3V and Λ3V of V⊗3. The vectors e1⊗3​ and e1​∧e2​∧e3​ are highest-weight vectors of weights (3,0,…,0) and (1,1,1,0,…,0) respectively. The unitary version of the Weyl dimension formula is
dimVλ​=∏i<j​j−iλi​−λj​+j−i​.
(24)
It gives
dimV(3)​=∏j=2n​j−1j+2​=(3n+2​),dimV(1,1,1)​=∏i=13​4−in−i+1​=(3n​).
(25)
These are exactly the dimensions of the third symmetric power and exterior power, so each is irreducible and occurs once in those subspaces.
The weight (2,1,0,…,0) in V⊗3 has basis
a=e1​⊗e1​⊗e2​,b=e1​⊗e2​⊗e1​,c=e2​⊗e1​⊗e1​.
(26)
The only positive root vector that acts nontrivially on this weight space is E12​. Acting on the tensor product as the sum of its actions on the three factors, it sends each of a,b,c to e1⊗3​. Consequently the highest-weight vectors in this space are precisely
Aa+Bb+CcwithA+B+C=0.
(27)
This is a two-dimensional space, so V(2,1)​ occurs exactly twice. Its dimension is
dimV(2,1)​=2∏j=3n​j−2j+1​=3n(n2−1)​.
(28)
The dimensions of the summands already found add to
(3n+2​)+(3n​)+23n(n2−1)​=n3.
(29)
Thus no other irreducible summand can remain. We have proved the full decomposition
V⊗3≅V(3,0,…)​⊕V(1,1,1,0,…)​⊕V(2,1,0,…)⊕2​.​
(30)
There are four summands counted with multiplicity and three distinct isomorphism types.
For n=2, the exterior cube vanishes. The third symmetric power still has highest weight (3,0) and dimension 4, while the same two-dimensional highest-vector calculation gives two copies of the representation of weight (2,1) and dimension 2. It is det⊗V, since a determinant twist shifts the standard highest weight (1,0) to (2,1). Hence
(C2)⊗3≅Sym3C2⊕(det⊗C2)⊕2,8=4+2+2.​
(31)

Choose the maximal torus of SO(2n) consisting of rotations in n orthogonal planes, and write zi​=eiθi​. In the complexified standard representation E=C2n the torus eigenvalues are z1​,z1−1​,…,zn​,zn−1​, with weights ei​,−ei​. For n≥2 the complexified Lie algebra has the Dn root system {±ei​±ej​:i<j}. The root-space decomposition theorem splits this Lie algebra into its Cartan subalgebra and one-dimensional root spaces. The Cartan subalgebra contributes the zero weight with multiplicity n. Therefore its character is
χad​(z)=n+i<j∑​(zi​zj​+zj​zi​​+zi​zj​​+zi​zj​1​).​
(32)
Take positive roots ei​−ej​,ei​+ej​ for i<j, so ρ=(n−1,n−2,…,0). For n>2 the root θ=e1​+e2​ is a highest weight: its root vector is killed by every positive root vector, since θ+α is never a root for positive α. To verify irreducibility without relying on an unstated simplicity theorem, apply complete reducibility and compare dimensions. For the Dn root system the Weyl dimension formula reads
dimVλ​=∏i<j​ρi2​−ρj2​(λi​+ρi​)2−(λj​+ρj​)2​.
(33)
For λ=(1,1,0,…,0) only pairs involving the first two indices change. With s=0,…,n−3, cancellation gives
dimVθ​=2n−32n−1​∏s=0n−3​(n−2)2−s2n2−s2​=2n−32n−1​(2n(n−1)​)(n−12(2n−3)​)=n(2n−1).
(34)
This is exactly dimso2n​(C), so the irreducible summand containing the highest-weight vector exhausts the Adjoint representation. Thus for n>2 the complexified Adjoint representation is irreducible, with highest weight e1​+e2​.
For n=2, the roots split into the two independent systems {±(e1​+e2​)} and {±(e1​−e2​)}. The Adjoint representation splits into two irreducibles of dimension three, with respective highest weights (1,1) and (1,−1). Equivalently these are the self-dual and anti-self-dual exterior two-forms in four dimensions. Their characters are
1+z1​z2​+(z1​z2​)−1,1+z1​/z2​+z2​/z1​.
(35)
They add to the displayed adjoint character. This agrees with the Chiral decomposition of the complexified so4 Lie algebra.
For a direct character comparison, put s(z)=∑i​(zi​+zi−1​). The eigenvalue formula for an exterior square gives
χΛ2E​(z)=21​(s(z)2−∑i​(zi2​+zi−2​))=n+∑i<j​(zi​zj​+zi​/zj​+zj​/zi​+(zi​zj​)−1).
(36)
This is precisely χad​. More intrinsically, let B be the nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form preserved by SO(2n) and define
Ψ(u∧v)(x)=B(v,x)u−B(u,x)v.
(37)
This endomorphism is skew with respect to B, so Ψ maps Λ2E to so(E,B). In an orthonormal basis the bivectors ei​∧ej​ map to a basis of skew skew-symmetric matrices, proving that it is an isomorphism. Since B is invariant,
Ψ(gu∧gv)=gΨ(u∧v)g−1.
(38)
Thus the exterior square and the complexified Adjoint representation are naturally isomorphic, explaining the character equality without a weight calculation.
The symmetric square is not irreducible. The inverse metric tensor Ω is a nonzero invariant vector, and contraction with B splits off its trivial line:
Sym2E=CΩ⊕Sym02​E.
(39)
The contraction sends Ω to 2n, so this is indeed a direct sum. A highest-weight vector f1​⊗f1​, where f1​ has torus weight e1​, lies in the traceless part because B(f1​,f1​)=0, and has highest weight 2e1​. The same dimension formula gives, for n≥2,
dimV2e1​​=∏s=0n−2​(n−1)2−s2(n+1)2−s2​=(2n(n+1)​)(n2(2n−1)​)=(n+1)(2n−1).
(40)
This equals dimSym2E−1=n(2n+1)−1. Complete reducibility therefore proves that the traceless part is irreducible, including the dimension-nine case for SO(4). In particular,
Sym2(C2n)≅1⊕V2e1​​(n≥2),so it is reducible.​
(41)
If the circle case n=1 is included, the adjoint character is 1 and the symmetric square instead has three one-dimensional weights 2,0,−2.

The printed identity has a sign error. With the defined Kostant partition function and the exponent e−ν, the cancelling factors must be 1−e−α. This is already visible for SU(2) with positive root α: the formal series ∑k≥0​e−kα multiplied by 1−eα is −eα, not 1.
For the corrected formula, a partition of ν is a tuple of nonnegative integers (kα​)α>0​ satisfying ν=∑α>0​kα​α. The order of summands is not counted. Multiplying the formal geometric series yields
∏α>0​(∑k≥0​e−kα)=∑ν​p(ν)e−ν.
(42)
This product is coefficientwise meaningful. Choose a linear functional strictly positive on every positive root. For a fixed ν it bounds each kα​, so only finitely many tuples contribute. We are working in the completion supported on the negative positive-root cone, not claiming convergence of an ordinary Fourier series on the torus.
Each geometric series cancels its factor 1−e−α, proving
(ν∑​p(ν)e−ν)α>0∏​(1−e−α)=1.​
(43)
Equivalently, reversing all the exponential signs gives (∑ν​p(ν)eν)∏α>0​(1−eα)=1 in the opposite completion. More generally, if N is the number of positive roots, the expression literally printed in the paper equals
(−1)Ne2ρ,
(44)
since ∏α>0​(1−eα)=(−1)Ne2ρ∏α>0​(1−e−α). Thus the correction is substantive, not merely a choice of Fourier convention.

Use the corrected generating series of the Kostant partition function and the permitted Weyl character formula:
χλ​=(∑w∈W​ε(w)ew(λ+ρ)−ρ)(∑ν​p(ν)e−ν).
(45)
For a fixed w, the coefficient of eμ arises exactly when
w(λ+ρ)−ρ−ν=μ,that is,ν=w(λ+ρ)−(μ+ρ).
(46)
There are only finitely many Weyl-group terms, and the partition function vanishes outside the cone of nonnegative root combinations. Coefficient extraction is therefore legitimate in the formal completion used in the preceding part. Summing the signed contributions proves
mλ​(μ)=w∈W∑​ε(w)p(w(λ+ρ)−(μ+ρ)).​
(47)
This proves the Kostant multiplicity formula for every compact connected Lie group, not just the unitary groups. In the presence of a central torus, roots have zero central component, so the partition function automatically gives zero for any incompatible central weight. Also ρ need not itself be an integral weight for the given global group: wρ−ρ always belongs to the root lattice, so every argument in the formula is nevertheless integral whenever λ and μ are integral group weights.
As a normalization check, for SU(2) write a highest weight as the nonnegative integer ℓ, with positive root 2 and ρ=1. The two Weyl-group terms give
mℓ​(μ)=p(ℓ−μ)−p(−ℓ−μ−2).
(48)
Since p(a)=1 precisely when a is even and nonnegative, this is one for μ=ℓ,ℓ−2,…,−ℓ and zero otherwise, including the cancellation below the lowest weight. This recovers the full familiar weight string.
