Put L=L1​∩L2​, and let j:L↪M be inclusion. The transverse intersection theorem makes L a smooth submanifold without boundary, with
Tx​L=Tx​L1​∩Tx​L2​,codimR​L=codimR​L1​+codimR​L2​.
(1)
It is compact because it is a closed subset of either compact input submanifold.
Write νr​=TM∣Lr​​/TLr​. On L, consider the bundle map
TM∣L​⟶ν1​∣L​⊕ν2​∣L​,v⟼(vmodTL1​, vmodTL2​).
(2)
Its kernel is TL, and it is onto because TL1​+TL2​=TM at every intersection point. Thus the normal bundle of a transverse intersection has the actual bundle isomorphism
ν(j)≅ν1​∣L​⊕ν2​∣L​.
(3)
Transfer the direct-sum complex structure J1​⊕J2​ through this isomorphism. This supplies a complex structure on the normal bundle itself, not merely after stabilization.
Geometrically, multiply the two classes by forming their external product in M×M and pulling it back along the diagonal Δ:M→M×M. The transversality hypothesis is precisely what makes the inverse image of L1​×L2​ under Δ the submanifold L. The normal bundle of this inverse image is the direct sum just calculated. Multiplication of the corresponding Thom classes agrees with the Thom class of this direct sum, so the complex cobordism product represented by transverse intersections is
[L1​,i1​,ν1​]∗[L2​,i2​,ν2​]=[L1​∩L2​, j, ν1​∣L​⊕ν2​∣L​].​
(4)
If the inputs have complex normal ranks a,b, the product has degree 2(a+b). If they are disjoint, the representative is empty and the product is zero.

Regard Matn​(C) as a real vector space of dimension 2n2, and let Hermn​(C) be the real vector space of Hermitian matrices, of dimension n2. The smooth Gram map
F(A)=A∗A
(5)
has F−1(I)=U(n) and differential DFA​(K)=A∗K+K∗A. At a unitary A, any Hermitian matrix H is obtained by taking K=AH/2. Indeed A∗K=H/2 and K∗A=H/2. Hence I is a regular value, and the regular level set theorem proves the unitary group as a regular level set result:
U(n) is a smooth manifold of real dimension n2.​
(6)
Similarly let G(A)=ATA from Matn​(R) to the symmetric real matrices, whose dimension is n(n+1)/2. At an orthogonal A its differential is DGA​(K)=ATK+KTA, again onto by choosing K=AH/2 for a symmetric H. Therefore the orthogonal group as a regular level set has
dimR​O(n)=n2−2n(n+1)​=2n(n−1)​.​
(7)
Matrix multiplication is smooth on both groups, and inversion is conjugate transpose or transpose, respectively, so these manifold structures also make them Lie groups. Both groups are closed and bounded in their finite-dimensional ambient matrix spaces, hence compact.
Realification identifies a unitary complex matrix with an orthogonal transformation of R2n. This is a smooth injective group homomorphism, with closed image, so U(n) is the stated Lie subgroup of O(2n). The assumed maximal-rank quotient map has fibers diffeomorphic to U(n). Consequently the compact homogeneous space, also known as the space of orthogonal complex structures, has dimension
d=dimO(2n)−dimU(n)=n(2n−1)−n2=n(n−1).
(8)
The original PDF's sphere is Sn2, with a square on the exponent; its dimension exceeds d by n.
For completeness, maps to a sphere above the dimension of a compact smooth manifold are null-homotopic. Let X be a compact smooth manifold of dimension d<q, with q≥1, and let f:X→Sq⊂Rq+1 be continuous. Choose a finite open cover on which f is uniformly close to its value at a chosen point of that open set, and take a smooth partition of unity subordinate to the cover. The weighted sum of those chosen sphere values is a smooth map g0​:X→Rq+1 uniformly within ϵ<1 of f. It never vanishes. Normalize it to g=g0​/∥g0​∥. Normalizing (1−s)f+sg0​ gives a homotopy from f to g, since that vector remains within ϵ of the unit vector f.
Every value of g is critical because its differential has rank at most d<q. The Sard theorem makes its image have measure zero in Sq, so it misses some point p. The punctured sphere Sq∖{p} is homeomorphic to Rq and contractible. Thus g, and hence f, is null-homotopic. This reasoning does not require X to be connected, and all constant maps are homotopic because Sq is path connected.
Apply it with X=O(2n)/U(n) and q=n2, for positive integer n. We obtain
[O(2n)/U(n),Sn2]={[constant map]}.​
(9)
The notation means homotopy classes of maps; no homotopy equivalence between the two spaces is asserted.

Let ξ have real rank r, choose a bundle metric, and write D(ξ),S(ξ) for its disk and sphere bundles. In a multiplicative generalized cohomology theory, a Thom class in a generalized cohomology theory is a class
uh​(ξ)∈hr(D(ξ),S(ξ))
(10)
whose restriction to each fiber pair (Dr,Sr−1) is the suspension of the coefficient unit 1∈h0(pt) under the chosen oriented identification. Equivalently, it is a fiberwise generator over the coefficient ring compatible with the h-orientation. Cup product with this class gives the Thom isomorphism theorem
hk(X)⟶hk+r(D(ξ),S(ξ)),a⟼π∗a⌣uh​(ξ),
(11)
where X is the base.
Let z:X→D(ξ) be the zero section and let :hr(D(ξ),S(ξ))→hr(D(ξ)) forget relative supports. The Euler class in a generalized cohomology theory is
eh​(ξ)=z∗(uh​(ξ))∈hr(X).​
(12)
Equivalently, identify relative cohomology with reduced cohomology of the Thom space and pull its Thom class back along the zero section followed by the quotient map.
Suppose s is a nowhere-zero section. Normalize it to s(x)=s(x)/∥s(x)∥∈S(ξ)x​. The maps
Ht​(x)=ts(x),0≤t≤1,
(13)
give a homotopy within D(ξ) from z to the sphere-valued section s. The image (uh​(ξ)) restricts to zero on S(ξ), by the exact sequence of the pair. Homotopy invariance therefore yields
z∗(uh​(ξ))=s∗(uh​(ξ))=0.
(14)
Thus a nowhere-zero section annihilates generalized Euler classes:
eh​(ξ)=0.​
(15)
The argument uses the actual nowhere-zero section and is valid for any chosen Thom orientation in the multiplicative theory.

For a complex bundle E, write ct​(E)=∑j≥0​cjMU​(E)tj, with c0​(E)=1 and cj​(E)=0 for j>rankC​E. The Whitney sum formula for Chern classes in complex cobordism is
ct​(η⊕ξ)=ct​(η)ct​(ξ),ck​(η⊕ξ)=a+b=k∑​ca​(η)cb​(ξ).​
(16)
Products are in the complex-cobordism cohomology ring; these classes have even degrees 2j. The splitting principle for complex vector bundles explains the formula: on a splitting space the Chern classes are elementary symmetric functions of the first Chern classes of the line summands, and joining the two lists multiplies their total Chern polynomials.
Use the line convention for the projective bundle:
CP(η)={(x,ℓ):x∈X, ℓ⊂ηx​ is a complex line}.
(17)
Its relative tautological line bundle is
η(1)={((x,ℓ),v):v∈ℓ}⟶CP(η).
(18)
It is a complex rank-one subbundle of p∗η. Choose a Hermitian metric and let E=η(1)⊥, of rank n−1, so p∗η=η(1)⊕E.
Throughout, x=c1MU​(η(1)), the tautological line's class, as specified before the final equation. Put ak​=p∗ckMU​(η) and bk​=ckMU​(E), with a0​=b0​=1. Since ct​(η(1))=1+xt, Whitney multiplication gives
∑k=0n​ak​tk=(1+xt)∑k=0n−1​bk​tk.
(19)
Comparison of coefficients gives ak​=bk​+xbk−1​, hence recursively bk​=ak​−xbk−1​. Therefore the Chern classes of a tautological-line complement are
ckMU​(η(1)⊥)=j=0∑k​(−1)jxjp∗ck−jMU​(η)(0≤k≤n−1),​
(20)
and the classes for k≥n vanish by the rank.
At degree n the same recursion has bn​=0, giving
0=an​−xan−1​+x2an−2​−⋯+(−1)nxn.
(21)
Multiplying by (−1)n proves the projective bundle relation in complex cobordism
xn−p∗c1MU​(η)xn−1+p∗c2MU​(η)xn−2−⋯+(−1)np∗cnMU​(η)=0.​
(22)
These alternating signs come from the inverse formal power series (1+xt)−1. They do not require identifying the First Chern class of a dual line with −x, which is generally incorrect in complex cobordism.
The final sentence's designation x=c1​(η) must mean the earlier c1​(η(1)). If instead one substitutes p∗c1​(η) literally, the claimed relation is false even in ordinary cohomology. For example, take η=L⊕L over CP2, with a=c1​(L) a generator. Its projective bundle is CP2×CP1, and p∗a2=0. With the incorrect substitution x=2p∗a, the rank-two polynomial evaluates to
(2p∗a)2−(2p∗a)(2p∗a)+p∗a2=p∗a2=0.
(23)
The tautological-line interpretation above is the one consistent with the bundle decomposition and the stated relation.

For a real or complex vector bundle E, choose a fiber metric. Its Thom space is the pointed quotient
Th(E)=D(E)/S(E),
(24)
collapsing the sphere bundle to one basepoint. When the base is compact, this is the one-point compactification of the total space: fiberwise radial compression identifies the total space with the interior of D(E), and all directions approaching its boundary become the single point at infinity.
Decompose Cn+m+2=V⊕W with V=Cn+1, W=Cm+1, so the subspace being collapsed is P(V)=CPn. Every line outside it has a representative (v,w) with w=0. Its projection to W gives the line ℓ=Cw∈P(W)=CPm, and there is a unique complex-linear map A:ℓ→V such that A(w)=v. The line in V⊕W is precisely the graph of A. This construction is unaffected by replacing (v,w) with (λv,λw).
Let γ=η1​ be the tautological line over P(W). The graph construction and its inverse are continuous, indeed smooth in the usual affine charts, and identify
CPn+m+1∖CPn≅Tot(HomC​(γ,V))=Tot((γ∗)⊕(n+1)).
(25)
A Hermitian metric gives a complex-linear bundle isomorphism γˉ​≅γ∗. With the Hermitian form linear in its first argument, it sends vˉ to the functional w↦⟨w,v⟩. Linearity in the conjugate vector is exactly what makes this a complex, rather than merely real, isomorphism. Thus the bundle is (ηˉ​1​)⊕(n+1), with the conjugation bars present in the original PDF.
Collapsing the closed subspace CPn in compact Hausdorff space CPn+m+1 gives the one-point compactification of its open complement: neighborhoods of the collapsed point have compact complements, and conversely every compact subset of the complement is closed in the ambient space. The graph homeomorphism therefore extends over the added point. This proves the projective-space quotient as a Thom space of conjugate tautological lines:
CPn+m+1/CPn≅Th((ηˉ​1​)⊕(n+1)⟶CPm).​
(26)
The graph coordinates also determine the normal complex structures requested in the second part. The copy P(W) corresponds to the zero section of the graph bundle, whose normal bundle is that bundle itself. For a coordinate embedding CPk⊂CPn, use W=Ck+1 and its complementary coordinate space V=Cn−k. Then the normal bundle of a linear complex-projective embedding is
νk​=HomC​(γk​,Cn−k)≅(γk∗​)⊕(n−k)≅γˉ​k⊕(n−k)​.​
(27)
The complex structure is the linear structure in these graph coordinates. Equivalently, applying the preceding homeomorphism with its parameters n−k−1 and k identifies the Thom space of this normal bundle with CPn/CPn−k−1, after reordering coordinates. Define νm​ in the identical way. The Hermitian-metric identification with the conjugate line does not change the complex normal orientation.
The original coordinate embeddings may be nested, so first move one into transverse position. Keep CPk=P(A) for the first k+1 coordinates, and move CPm to P(B) for the last m+1 coordinates. A unitary coordinate permutation accomplishes this. Every unitary matrix can be joined to the identity by diagonalizing it and varying its eigenvalue phases continuously, so this move is an ambient isotopy. Its trace gives a cobordism carrying the transported normal complex structure, and hence leaves the input cobordism class unchanged.
If k+m≥n, then
A+B=Cn+1,dimC​(A∩B)=k+m−n+1.
(28)
Their projectivizations are transverse and meet in CPr, where r=k+m−n. The direct-sum normal structure from question 1 restricts to
νk​∣CPr​⊕νm​∣CPr​≅γˉ​r⊕((n−k)+(n−m))​=γˉ​r⊕(n−r)​≅νr​.
(29)
The quotient map defining the transverse normal-bundle isomorphism is complex-linear here, so it agrees with the standard normal complex structure and contributes no orientation sign. If k+m<n, the first and last coordinate subspaces have no nonzero vector in common, so their projectivizations are disjoint.
Consequently the product of linear projective subspaces in complex cobordism is
[CPk,νk​]∗[CPm,νm​]={[CPk+m−n,νk+m−n​],0,​k+m≥n,k+m<n.​​
(30)
The product degree is 2(2n−k−m), equal to the real codimension of the stated intersection when it exists. In the equality case k+m=n, the representative is one point with its complex-oriented normal space.
