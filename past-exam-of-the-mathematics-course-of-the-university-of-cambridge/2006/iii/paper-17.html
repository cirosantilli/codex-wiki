Work in the classical setting of varieties over an algebraically closed field k. A prevariety is separated when its diagonal morphism ΔX​:X→X×k​X is a closed immersion; for prevarieties the diagonal is already an immersion, so this is equivalent to its image being closed. A complete variety has the following closed-projection property: for every variety Y, projection X×k​Y→Y maps closed subsets to closed subsets. This is universal closedness; since a variety is separated and of finite type, it is equivalent to the structural map being proper.
First consider projective space. In two sets of homogeneous coordinates its diagonal is defined by
Xi​Yj​−Xj​Yi​=0(0≤i,j≤n).
(1)
These equations hold exactly when the two nonzero coordinate vectors are proportional. They are homogeneous in each coordinate set, so they define a closed subset of Pn×Pn. On a common affine chart, the diagonal map is the usual closed immersion defined by equality of the affine coordinates. Thus projective space is separated. If X↪Pn is a projective embedding, its diagonal is the intersection of that closed diagonal with X×X. Consequently every projective variety is separated.
Here is an algebraic proof of completeness, rather than an appeal to projective compactness in an analytic topology. It suffices to prove closedness of projection from projective space over an affine chart Y, with coordinate ring A=k[Y]. A closed subset Z⊆Pn×Y is cut out by a homogeneous ideal I⊆A[T0​,…,Tn​]. Put B=A[T0​,…,Tn​]/I and write Bd​ for its degree-d part. Each Bd​ is a finitely generated A-module, because it is a quotient of the free module on the degree-d monomials.
Suppose the fibre over y∈Y is empty. The specialized homogeneous equations then have no nonzero common zero. By the Hilbert Nullstellensatz, some power of each Ti​ belongs to the specialized ideal. If those powers are Tiri​​, then every monomial of degree d=1+∑i​(ri​−1) belongs to that ideal. Hence
Bd​⊗A​k(y)=0.
(2)
The Nakayama lemma gives (Bd​)my​​=0. Since Bd​ is finitely generated, there is a∈A∖my​ with (Bd​)a​=0: choose generators and multiply finitely many elements annihilating their localizations. On the principal open subset D(a), all graded pieces of degree at least d vanish too, since the quotient is generated in degree one. Every fibre over D(a) is therefore empty: a projective point would have a nonzero coordinate whose degree-d power could not vanish. Thus the complement of the projection of Z is open, proving the required closed-projection property.
An affine open cover of an arbitrary Y establishes the same result there. Finally, a closed subset of X×Y is closed in Pn×Y when X is a projective variety, so its image is closed by the preceding argument. Therefore
Every projective variety is separated and complete.​
(3)
Over a non-algebraically-closed field, the corresponding statement concerns schemes or geometric points. Projection of rational points alone need not be closed, so it must not replace the completeness definition.

Form the presheaf whose value on U⊆X is
G(U)⊗OX​(U)​H(U),
(4)
with restrictions induced from those of the two sheaves of modules and the structure sheaf. Its sheafification is the tensor product of sheaves G⊗OX​​H. Sheafification is needed because taking a tensor product on each open set need not itself satisfy the gluing axiom. The resulting sheaf represents bilinear sheaf morphisms, and localization gives the stalk formula
(G⊗OX​​H)P​≅GP​⊗OX,P​​HP​​.
(5)
Indeed, sheafification preserves stalks, and filtered direct limits commute with the module tensor product.

ϕ∗H=OY​⊗ϕ−1OX​​ϕ−1H​.

(10)

The affine module sheaf construction makes the remaining statements explicit. For A=k[V] and an A-module M, define
M(D(f))=Mf​
(11)
on the basis of principal open subsets. Restrictions are the natural localization maps. Equivalently, a section on an arbitrary open set is a family sP​∈MmP​​ which near each point is represented by a single fraction m/fr, with f nonvanishing throughout that neighbourhood. This locally representable family construction gives a sheaf; the usual localization gluing verifies its quasi-coherence. Its stalk is MmP​​ and
Γ(V,M)=M​.
(12)
This last identification is precisely the standard module-localization gluing on the affine basis, not an assertion that arbitrary sheaves are determined by their global sections.
Now write A=k[X], B=k[Y] and α=ϕ#:A→B. Let G=M, H=N for A-modules M,N, and let F=L for a B-module L. The affine versions of the three constructions are
G⊗OX​​H≅M⊗A​N​,ϕ∗​F≅A​L​,ϕ∗H≅B⊗A​N​​.
(13)
Here A​L denotes restriction of scalars along α, whereas the last associated sheaf lives on Y. To verify the tensor-product assertion, localize at every prime: both stalks are Mm​⊗Am​​Nm​. For the direct image, ϕ−1D(a)=D(α(a)), and
Γ(D(a),ϕ∗​L)=Lα(a)​=(A​L)a​.
(14)
These equalities identify the sheaves on a basis and respect restrictions. For the pullback of a sheaf of modules, its stalk at Q is BmQ​​⊗A​N, exactly the stalk of B⊗A​N​. The local tensor maps are compatible, so these stalk identifications come from an actual sheaf isomorphism. Thus all three constructions are quasi-coherent in the stated affine setting.
Finally construct the natural projection formula for sheaves map by sending a local tensor m⊗b to b(1⊗m) in the pulled-back module, followed by direct image. In the affine description, the target ϕ∗​ϕ∗M is the sheaf associated with the A-module A​(B⊗A​M), while M⊗OX​​ϕ∗​OY​ corresponds to M⊗A​B. The maps
m⊗b⟼b⊗m,b⊗m⟼m⊗b
(15)
are inverse A-linear maps. Localizing them commutes with every restriction, proving
ϕ∗​ϕ∗M≅M⊗OX​​ϕ∗​OY​​.
(16)
No flatness assumption on A→B or finiteness assumption on M is needed.

For a sheaf of abelian groups F on a topological space X, put
C(F)(U)=∏P∈U​FP​.
(17)
These arbitrary families of germs form a sheaf, and restrictions are projections. Extending a family by zero proves that C(F) is flasque. Sending a section to all its germs gives an injective sheaf map F→C(F), since a section with every germ zero vanishes locally and hence globally.
Set Q−1=F, Ij=C(Qj−1) and Qj=coker(Qj−1→Ij), using the sheaf cokernel. Compose Ij→Qj→Ij+1 to obtain the Godement resolution
0⟶F⟶I0⟶I1⟶⋯.
(18)
It is exact by construction, with flasque terms. Define sheaf cohomology by the cohomology of its global-section complex:
Hj(X,F)=Hj(Γ(X,I∙)),H0(X,F)=Γ(X,F).
(19)
The resolution principle for sheaf cohomology identifies this with the computation using any flasque resolution.
The elementary input for acyclicity is the flasque-kernel section-lifting lemma: in a short exact sequence with flasque kernel, sections of the quotient lift over every open subset. Consequently a quotient of two flasque sheaves is flasque, because a quotient section lifts and that lift extends. If F itself is flasque, induction through 0→Qj−1→Ij→Qj→0 makes every Qj flasque. The same lifting lemma makes these sequences exact on global sections. Hence the global-section complex has no positive-degree cohomology, proving
Hj(X,F)=0(j>0) for flasque F​.
(20)
This deduction uses the lifting lemma, not the desired cohomological vanishing as an assumption.
For the rational-section construction, add two representative sections after restricting to the intersection of their dense domains. Multiply by a representative rational function in the same way. Finite intersections of dense open sets are dense, and further restricting both representatives does not change their resulting class. The module axioms hold on their common domain. Thus rational sections form a module over Rat(X); irreducibility is not needed for this module statement. When X is irreducible, Rat(X) is the function field K=k(X).
Suppose now that F is locally free and X irreducible. A germ at P is represented on a nonempty open neighbourhood, which is dense, so it defines a rational section. Two representatives of the germ agree on a neighbourhood of P and therefore give the same class. If the class is zero, trivialize F on a neighbourhood of P. Its coefficient functions vanish on a dense open subset; regular functions on a reduced irreducible variety with this property vanish identically. The germ is therefore zero. This proves the stalk inclusion into rational sections of a locally free sheaf:
FP​↪Rat(F)​.
(21)
Local freeness matters: a nonzero torsion germ can vanish on a dense open set.
On an irreducible algebraic curve, every proper closed subset is finite, and every open subset is quasi-compact because the Zariski space is Noetherian. Let R=Rat(F). The constant sheaf R(F) has sections R on every nonempty open set: such opens are irreducible and hence connected. Define
P(F)(U)=⨁P∈U​R/FP​.
(22)
Restriction drops the components outside the smaller open set. For a compatible family over an open cover of U, take a finite subcover by quasi-compactness. The union of the finite supports of these finitely many sections is finite. Their agreeing components therefore glue to a unique finite-support tuple on U. This verifies the sheaf gluing axiom, including arbitrary covers, rather than merely asserting that a presheaf direct sum is always a sheaf.
A rational section is regular on some dense open set, whose complement on a curve is finite. Its classes [s]P​∈R/FP​ therefore have finite support, defining a sheaf map R(F)→P(F). At P, the first sheaf has stalk R. The second has stalk R/FP​: any finite tuple can be restricted to a neighbourhood omitting all its other support points, and its P-component is unaffected by such restriction. The resulting sequence on stalks is
0→FP​→R→R/FP​→0.
(23)
Exactness of sheaves can be tested on stalks, so this proves the rational principal-parts resolution on an algebraic curve
0→F→R(F)→P(F)→0​.
(24)
Both right-hand sheaves are flasque: restrictions for the constant rational-section sheaf are identities between nonempty opens, and restrictions for the rational principal-parts sheaf are projections, with extension by zero. Consequently
H1(X,F)≅coker(R→⨁P∈X​R/FP​),Hj(X,F)=0(j≥2).
(25)
Surjectivity on stalks has not been confused with surjectivity on global sections.
For an explicit failure of global surjectivity take X=P1 and F=O(−2). Use coordinate z=X1​/X0​ and frame e0​=X0−2​ on the finite chart. At infinity use w=1/z and e∞​=X1−2​=z−2e0​. Prescribe the rational principal part of z−1e0​ at zero and zero at every other point. If a rational section r(z)e0​ realized this tuple, then r−z−1 would be regular at zero and r would be regular at every other finite point. Thus r=z−1+p(z) with p∈k[z]. Regularity at infinity would require
z2r(z)=w−1+w−2p(w−1)
(26)
to be regular at w=0. Its w−1 term cannot cancel with any term of the polynomial contribution, whose powers are at most −2. This is impossible. Hence the global rational-principal-part map need not be surjective.
For an affine curve it is always surjective, and here is a direct construction. Write X=SpecA, K=FracA and F=M. By the affine module sheaf construction, R=M⊗A​K=:MK​ and FP​=MmP​​. Choose representatives vP​∈MK​ for a prescribed finite tuple, and choose one nonzero a∈A with avP​∈M for all of them. The zero set T=V(a) is finite. The ring A/(a) is Artinian, since it is Noetherian of dimension zero, and its Artinian decomposition into local factors gives
M/aM≅⨁P∈T​(M/aM)mP​​.
(27)
Prescribe the class of avP​ in the indicated component for each requested point in T, and zero in the other components. A requested point outside T already has zero principal part because a is a unit there. Lift the tuple to w∈M. Then s=w/a∈MK​ satisfies s−vP​∈MmP​​ at every requested point in T, and is regular at every other point of T. Outside T it is regular because a is invertible. It therefore realizes exactly the prescribed tuple. This proves affine principal-parts interpolation for a locally free sheaf and gives
R⟶P∈X⨁​R/FP​ is surjective when X is affine;H1(X,F)=0​.
(28)
No nonsingularity assumption was used; the construction applies to singular irreducible affine curves as well.

On the standard affine charts Ui​={Xi​=0}, use the formal frame ei​=Xim​ and glue rank-one free sheaves by
ej​=(Xj​/Xi​)mei​.
(29)
The transition functions are regular units on overlaps and satisfy the cocycle identity. This constructs the twisting sheaf on projective space O(m) as an invertible sheaf for every integer m, including negative m.
On a nonempty U, a section has local expressions hi​ei​, where hi​∈O(U∩Ui​). Pulling to the punctured affine cone gives hi​(X/Xi​)Xim​. Their agreement on overlaps makes them one homogeneous rational function r of degree m, regular on π−1U. To make its polynomial representation explicit, write one nonzero local coefficient as p(y)/q(y) in the affine coordinates. Homogenize p,q to degrees a,b; the resulting rational expression is
r=Xim−a+b​qhomphom​.
(30)
If the exponent of Xi​ is negative, move its power to the denominator. Cancel common factors; the greatest common divisor of homogeneous polynomials can be chosen homogeneous. Thus r=F/G with F,G coprime homogeneous polynomials, G=0, and degF−degG=m.
Conversely, for such a homogeneous rational function regular on π−1U, its coefficient r/Xim​ is regular on U∩Ui​: restrict to the slice Xi​=1. These coefficients obey the displayed transition rule and hence define a section. This proves the homogeneous rational sections of a twisting sheaf description in both directions, for arbitrary open U rather than only a standard chart.
Now lift a regular degree-zero rational function f=P/Q to the punctured cone. The quotient rule gives
∂Xi​∂f​=Q2Q∂i​P−P∂i​Q​.
(31)
This is a rational function homogeneous of degree −1. It is independent of the chosen representation because differentiation is a derivation of the rational function field. It is regular wherever f is regular: a derivation of a polynomial ring extends to each localization by the quotient rule, and regular functions are locally such fractions. The preceding homogeneous-section description therefore proves
∂i​f∈Γ(U,O(−1))​.
(32)
Zeros of a particular displayed denominator do not invalidate this argument; regularity is a property of the rational function, which can have another local representation.
The map f↦(∂0​f,…,∂n​f) is a k-linear sheaf derivation with values in O(−1)⊕(n+1). The universal property of Kähler differentials consequently defines
D:ΩPn1​⟶O(−1)⊕(n+1),df⟼(∂i​f)i​.
(33)
Multiplication by the coordinate section Xi​∈Γ(Pn,O(1)) defines the other map
σ:O(−1)⊕(n+1)⟶O,(gi​)i​⟼∑i​Xi​gi​.
(34)
For a degree-d homogeneous polynomial, differentiating each monomial gives ∑i​Xi​∂i​P=dP. Applying this to the equal-degree numerator and denominator gives ∑i​Xi​∂i​f=0 for degree-zero f, so σD=0. This identity is valid in every characteristic.
To prove all the exactness assertions, fix Ui​ and put yj​=Xj​/Xi​ for j=i. The Kähler differential sheaf is free there on dyj​, because a derivation of the polynomial ring is determined freely by its values on the coordinates. Trivialize each O(−1) with frame Xi−1​. In this frame,
D(dyj​)=ej​−yj​ei​,σ((b0​,…,bn​))=bi​+∑j=i​yj​bj​,
(35)
where ej​ now denotes the jth coordinate vector of the direct sum. The map σ is surjective, since its ith coefficient is one. Its kernel consists exactly of tuples with bi​=−∑j=i​yj​bj​, so the n displayed vectors ej​−yj​ei​ form a free basis of that kernel. The map D sends the differential basis bijectively to this kernel basis and is therefore injective. Exactness on these charts proves the cotangent Euler sequence in homogeneous coordinates
0→ΩPn1​D​O(−1)⊕(n+1)σ​O→0​.
(36)
The proof uses no division by an integer and hence no characteristic-zero assumption.
For n≥1, the cohomology of twisting sheaves on projective space gives
hq(Pn,O(m))=⎩⎨⎧​(nm+n​),(n−m−1​),0,​q=0, m≥0,q=n, m≤−n−1,otherwise.​
(37)
In particular, H0(O)=k, Hq>0(O)=0, and Hq(O(−1))=0 for every q. The long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology of the displayed Euler sequence begins
0→H0(Ω1)→0→k→H1(Ω1)→0,
(38)
and gives zero in the remaining degrees. Thus
dimk​Hq(Pn,ΩPn1​)={1,0,​q=1,q=1,​(0≤q≤n, n≥1)​.
(39)
The nonzero group is generated by the connecting image of the section 1 of O. If n=0 is admitted, P0 is a point and its cotangent sheaf is zero, so the sole requested group H0 is zero.
