Take St​ to be the ex-dividend stock price: the date-t output has already been paid when trading occurs. Receiving θt​dt​ units of fruit and selling θt​−θt+1​ shares gives the discrete dividend budget equation
Ct​=θt​dt​+(θt​−θt+1​)St​,Ct​+St​θt+1​=(St​+dt​)θt​.​
(1)
The entering holding θt​ is chosen with date-(t−1) information, while Ct​ and the next holding θt+1​ may use date-t information. The output is the consumption good and price numéraire; its stochastic law is exogenous to the individual's price-taking choice.
For the interior differentiable case, attach an adapted Lagrange multiplier Λt​ to each budget constraint. On a finite horizon, with the terminal holding fixed or its continuation value included, the optimization Lagrangian is
L=E∑t​{βtU(Ct​)+Λt​[(St​+dt​)θt​−St​θt+1​−Ct​]}.
(2)
An adapted variation in Ct​ gives Λt​=βtU′(Ct​). An arbitrary date-t measurable variation in θt+1​ affects two adjacent terms. Its coefficient must have zero conditional mean, so
Λt​St​=E[Λt+1​(St+1​+dt+1​)∣Ft​],Λt​=βtU′(Ct​).​
(3)
These are the Euler equations of marginal utility pricing in a dividend economy. If the utility function is merely concave, a supporting slope replaces U′; binding consumption or trading restrictions give the corresponding inequalities rather than unconstrained equalities.
With known initial information and Λ0​>0, normalize ζt​=Λt​/Λ0​. This is a state-price density for the traded asset. In particular,
St​=Et​[ζt​ζt+1​​(St+1​+dt+1​)],ζt​ζt+1​​=βU′(Ct​)U′(Ct+1​)​.
(4)
The ratio is the stochastic discount factor: payment in a state with greater marginal utility has a higher marginal value. For a priced contingent payoff H at t+1, its price is Et​[(ζt+1​/ζt​)H]; pricing arbitrary claims uniquely also requires market completeness. The deflated cumulative gains ζt​St​+∑s=1t​ζs​ds​ form a martingale whenever the displayed terms are integrable.
Iterating the Euler equation gives
ζt​St​=Et​[∑s=t+1N​ζs​ds​+ζN​SN​].
(5)
Under the dividend-price transversality condition and the needed integrability, this becomes the fundamental price
St​=ζt​1​Et​s>t∑​ζs​ds​.​
(6)
Thus an infinite-horizon price is not automatically just a dividend sum: its terminal term must vanish. Telescoping the individual budget constraints similarly gives
E∑t=0N​ζt​Ct​=ζ0​θ0​(S0​+d0​)−E[ζN​SN​θN+1​].
(7)
A no-Ponzi lower restriction on this final term gives the consumption budget inequality; its vanishing gives equality. Together with the supporting-line inequality for a concave utility function, the Euler equations and these terminal conditions supply the usual sufficiency argument for an admissible candidate. In an incomplete market, each optimal agent's marginal utility can supply a different state-price density pricing the same traded tree.

For a competitive equilibrium with one productive asset, the primitives are the probability law and information filtration of total output d, each agent's utility function and discount factor, the initial ownership shares θ0j​ summing to one, and the permitted consumption and trading strategies. Prices are not primitives of the equilibrium problem. The derived objects are the ex-dividend price S, each agent's consumption Cj and holdings θj, and their marginal-utility pricing processes. At a common price, every agent solves the individual problem, and resource and share clearing require
∑j=1J​Ctj​=dt​,∑j=1J​θtj​=1.
(8)
The individual discrete dividend budget equations, optimality Euler equations and terminal conditions from part (i), together with these clearing equations, define equilibrium even when the market is incomplete. Summing the individual budget constraints is consistent with total fruit consumption.
For the given constant absolute risk aversion utility, Uj′​(c)=e−γj​c. Hence each interior optimum satisfies
St​βjt​e−γj​Ctj​=Et​[βjt+1​e−γj​Ct+1j​(St+1​+dt+1​)].​
(9)
The same S must satisfy all these equations, but their conditional expectation identities do not by themselves identify the different agents' pricing densities pointwise.
To illustrate explicit exponential-utility risk sharing, first consider a complete or otherwise implementable interior allocation. With positive constant welfare weights aj​, a pointwise resource multiplier νt​ satisfies
aj​βjt​e−γj​Ctj​=νt​.
(10)
Write
Γ=(∑j​γj−1​)−1,logβˉ​=Γ∑j​γj−1​logβj​,logA=Γ∑j​γj−1​logaj​.
(11)
Solving the marginal equations and summing the consumptions gives
νt​=Aβˉ​te−Γdt​,Ctj​=γj​Γ​dt​+γj​t​(logβj​−logβˉ​)+γj​1​(logaj​−logA).​
(12)
The output risk is shared in proportion to risk tolerance γj−1​; differences in patience produce the time-dependent transfer term. The common normalized state-price density is ζt​=νt​/ν0​. The weights, up to a common scale, are determined by the initial wealth budgets
E∑t≥0​ζt​Ctj​=θ0j​(S0​+d0​),ζ0​=1,
(13)
and, for a fundamental price,
St​=νt​1​Et​∑s>t​νs​ds​=eΓdt​Et​∑s>t​βˉ​s−te−Γds​ds​.
(14)
If consumption is required to be nonnegative and the interior formula produces a negative allocation, replace it by
Ctj​=max{0,γj​logaj​+tlogβj​−logνt​​},∑j​Ctj​=dt​.
(15)
For dt​>0 this determines the resource multiplier uniquely. At zero total output all constrained consumptions are zero and the corresponding marginal inequalities apply.
A single traded tree need not span the contingent transfers in the general risk-sharing formula. A genuine equilibrium example within the literal one-tree market is obtained by taking all βj​=β and initial shares θ0j​=Γ/γj​. Then
θtj​=Γ/γj​,Ctj​=(Γ/γj​)dt​,ζt​=βte−Γ(dt​−d0​).​
(16)
Every agent simply keeps the initial shares, all budget constraints and clearing conditions hold, and their discounted marginal utilities coincide. The fundamental price above satisfies every Euler equation. For example, nonnegative bounded output ensures the required price and utility integrability and the terminal terms vanish; the supporting-line argument from part (i) then verifies individual optimality. For heterogeneous discount factors or arbitrary initial ownership, the displayed coupled Euler-budget-clearing system remains the correct one-tree formulation; using the unconstrained Pareto allocation as a traded equilibrium requires an additional financing check.

Use θt​ for the dollar amount invested in the stock; the number of shares is θt​/St​. The bank account contains wt​−θt​ dollars. With no intermediate consumption, the self-financing portfolio equation is
dwt​=rt​(wt​−θt​)dt+θt​St​dSt​​=[rt​wt​+θt​(μt​−rt​)]dt+θt​σt​dWt​.​
(17)
If instead θ denotes share holdings, replace each dollar θt​ here by St​θt​. The equation says that trading only reallocates existing portfolio wealth; gains come from cash interest and stock returns.
For the standard unconstrained complete-diffusion formulation, assume the completed natural Brownian filtration, initial wealth w0​>0, and nonnegative admissible wealth. Define the market price of risk κt​=(μt​−rt​)/σt​. The boundedness assumptions make κ bounded. The state-price density normalized at zero is
ζt​=exp(−∫0t​rs​ds−∫0t​κs​dWs​−21​∫0t​κs2​ds),dζt​=−ζt​(rt​dt+κt​dWt​).​
(18)
Its money-market-adjusted process Zt​=e∫0t​rs​dsζt​ is a true martingale by the Novikov condition. Under dQ=ZT​dP, the Girsanov theorem gives WtQ​=Wt​+∫0t​κs​ds as a Brownian motion, and the discounted stock has zero drift.
The Itô product rule applied to the wealth equation gives
d(ζt​wt​)=ζt​(σt​θt​−κt​wt​)dWt​.
(19)
For nonnegative admissible wealth this is a nonnegative local martingale, hence a supermartingale. Therefore every affordable terminal payoff X=wT​ satisfies the state-price budget constraint E[ζT​X]≤w0​. Nonzero volatility and the Brownian martingale representation theorem allow replication of nonnegative claims with finite state-price cost.
For a differentiable strictly concave utility function with the usual conditions ensuring an interior integrable optimizer, put I=(U′)−1, the inverse marginal utility. Pointwise maximization of U(x)−yζT​x gives
wT∗​=I(yζT​),E[ζT​I(yζT​)]=w0​.​
(20)
The second equation determines the positive constant y. To prove optimality, use the concave supporting-tangent inequality:
U(X)−U(wT∗​)≤U′(wT∗​)(X−wT∗​)=yζT​(X−wT∗​).
(21)
Taking expectations and using the budget bound proves that the candidate dominates every admissible affordable payoff, whenever the utility expectations are well defined. This is the complete-market terminal utility optimizer.
The actual wealth and dollar portfolio are as explicit as general adapted coefficients permit. Let
Nt​=E[ζT​I(yζT​)∣Ft​],dNt​=ht​dWt​.
(22)
The Brownian martingale representation theorem, with localization if only L1 integrability is initially known, gives h. The dollar portfolio from a deflated wealth martingale is
wt∗​=ζt​Nt​​,θt∗​=σt​ht​/ζt​+κt​wt∗​​.​
(23)
The bank holds wt∗​−θt∗​ dollars. Equivalently, with Bt​=e∫0t​rs​ds, wt∗​=Bt​EQ[wT∗​/BT​∣Ft​]. These formulas need no unjustified differentiability of a value function with random coefficients.
The printed assumption that U is only increasing and concave does not guarantee that the interior formula exists, or even that the optimal value is finite. In general replace the inverse formula by an affordable selection
X∗(ω)∈argmaxx∈D​{U(x)−yζT​(ω)x},
(24)
where D is the wealth domain; boundary choices or supporting slopes handle nondifferentiability. Existence, finite cost and replication still have to hold. A concrete obstruction is the unbounded linear terminal-wealth utility: take U(x)=x, r=0, μ=σ=1, and the Brownian filtration. Then ζT​=e−WT​−T/2. For An​={ζT​<1/n}, the bounded nonnegative claim
Xn​=E[ζT​1An​​]w0​1An​​​
(25)
is replicable, costs w0​, and has EXn​>nw0​. Thus there is no finite optimal expected utility under the literal utility hypotheses alone. Also, bounded coefficients do not ensure market completeness in an arbitrarily enlarged filtration containing untraded randomness; the full replication formula uses the Brownian market information assumption stated above.

Assume, as in the Bayesian model, that the unknown drift α is independent of the driving Brownian motion, with known positive prior precision τ0​. Recover the observed signal from the positive stock price:
Yt​=σ1​log(St​/S0​)+2σ​t=αt+Wt​.
(26)
For a fixed time t, the centered observed path Ys​−(s/t)Yt​ is a Brownian bridge independent of (α,Yt​): it is independent of the prior and has zero covariance with Wt​ in their joint Gaussian law. Thus the whole observed history has the same posterior information about α as its endpoint. This proves Brownian endpoint sufficiency for a constant drift rather than assuming it.
The likelihood of that endpoint, as a function of α, is proportional to exp(αYt​−α2t/2). Multiplying by the normal distribution prior and completing the square gives the Gaussian Brownian drift filter
α∣FtS​∼N(αt​,τt−1​),τt​=τ0​+t,αt​=τ0​+tτ0​α0​+Yt​​.​
(27)
Define the observed innovation process Wt​=Yt​−∫0t​αs​ds. It is a continuous observed-filtration martingale: for s<u, independence of future noise and the tower property of conditional expectation give E[α−αu​∣FsS​]=0, so the conditional mean of its increment vanishes. Its quadratic variation is t, since subtracting the finite-variation drift from Y does not change quadratic variation. The Lévy characterization of Brownian motion makes W an observed-filtration Brownian motion. Differentiating the posterior mean gives
dαt​=τt−1​(dYt​−αt​dt)=τt−1​dWt​.​
(28)
Conversely, the last equation determines α from W with deterministic coefficients and known initial value, and Y=W+∫αdt. Thus the observed and innovation filtrations coincide, supplying the natural Brownian information needed for replication. Consequently the observed stock dynamics are dSt​/St​=σdWt​+σαt​dt. Its observed market price of risk is κt​=αt​−r/σ, and its normalized state-price density solves
dζt​=−ζt​(rdt+κt​dWt​),ζt​=exp(−rt−∫0t​κs​dWs​−21​∫0t​κs2​ds).
(29)
The posterior mean is unbounded, so bounded-coefficient Novikov condition reasoning from Question 2 is not available automatically. A direct finite-horizon argument supplies true pricing. Relative to driftless Wiener measure for Y, the Bayesian mixture has observation density
Lt​(Yt​)=τ0​+tτ0​​​exp(2(τ0​+t)(τ0​α0​+Yt​)2​−2τ0​α02​​).
(30)
A law with observation drift k=r/σ instead has density ekYt​−k2t/2. Therefore
Dt​=Lt​(Yt​)ekYt​−k2t/2​,ζt​=e−rtDt​.​
(31)
This is the finite-horizon pricing density for Gaussian drift learning. The ratio changes the physical observed law into a genuine probability law, hence has mean one. Indeed, differentiation of the explicit likelihood, using dYt​=dWt​+αt​dt, gives
dlogLt​=αt​dYt​−21​αt2​dt,dlogDt​=(k−αt​)dWt​−21​(αt​−k)2dt.
(32)
The Itô formula therefore gives dDt​=−Dt​κt​dWt​, agreeing with the stochastic exponential above. Under this pricing law the stock drift is σk=r.
For logarithmic utility, terminal marginal utility is 1/wT​. The state-price budget constraint and the marginal condition imply wT∗​=1/(yζT​) and 1/y=w0​. Since ζT​wT∗​=w0​ is constant, its conditional-expectation pricing process is constant as well. Thus the entire optimal wealth process is
wt∗​=ζt​w0​​.​
(33)
Indeed, for any other nonnegative admissible terminal wealth X, the elementary inequality log(X/wT∗​)≤X/wT∗​−1 gives
Elog(X/wT∗​)≤E[ζT​X/w0​]−1≤0.
(34)
This directly verifies optimality. Expected candidate log wealth is finite because Eαt2​=α02​+τ0−1​−(τ0​+t)−1 and its time integral is finite on every fixed horizon. Applying the Itô formula to 1/ζ gives
wt∗​dwt∗​​=(r+κt2​)dt+κt​dWt​.
(35)
The observed self-financing portfolio with risky dollar fraction πt​ has diffusion coefficient πt​σ. Matching the coefficients proves the log-optimal investment with Gaussian drift learning rule
πt∗​=σκt​​=σ2σαt​−r​,θt∗​=πt∗​wt∗​.​
(36)
The remaining dollars are held in the bank account; the fraction may be negative or exceed one because the question imposes neither short-sale nor borrowing restrictions.

Use column vectors for dollar holdings θt​ and put b=μ−r1, h=σ−1b and V=σσT. The normalized state-price density is
ζt​=exp(−rt−hTWt​−21​∣h∣2t),dζt​=−ζt​(rdt+hTdWt​).​
(37)
The vector h is the market price of risk. The density ertζt​ defines an equivalent risk-neutral measure, under which WtQ​=Wt​+ht is Brownian motion and every stock has drift r. A terminal claim H with finite state-price cost is priced at ζt−1​E[ζT​H∣Ft​]. Nonsingularity of σ and the natural Brownian market information make this a complete market; in particular the optimal nonnegative payoff below can be replicated.
Let q=n−11 and v=σTq. Apply the Itô formula to each log price, whose return variance is Vii​:
dlogSti​=∑j​σij​dWtj​+(μi​−21​Vii​)dt.
(38)
Summing and dividing by n proves the geometric stock index formula
log(Jt​/J0​)=n1​[1TσWt​+(1Tμ−21​trV)t].​
(39)
Thus Jt​=J0​exp(vTWt​+aJ​t), where aJ​=qTμ−tr(V)/(2n). The factor 1/n applies to the whole bracket. The geometric average need not itself be a self-financing portfolio; its instantaneous drift is aJ​+∣v∣2/2.
If θti​ is the dollar amount invested in stock i, the bank account holds wt​−1Tθt​. The self-financing portfolio equation is
dwt​=[rwt​+θtT​b]dt+θtT​σdWt​.​
(40)
Equivalently, πt​=θt​/wt​ gives dwt​/wt​=(r+πtT​b)dt+πtT​σdWt​. For share-holding notation replace θti​ by Sti​ times the number of shares.
Static expected utility maximization uses the terminal budget E[ζT​wT​]≤w0​. Since JT​ is an exogenous positive benchmark, differentiating U(x/JT​) in x gives JT−1​U′(x/JT​). An interior Lagrange multiplier λ>0 for the budget therefore gives
JT​1​U′(wT∗​/JT​)=λζT​.
(41)
For CRRA utility, write p=1/R. Since U′(y)=y−R, solving this marginal equation gives
wT∗​=λ−pζT−p​JT1−p​.​
(42)
Every positive lognormal moment is finite here, so the budget determines the constant uniquely. Put
KT​=E[(ζT​JT​)1−p].
(43)
Then
λ−p=w0​/KT​,λ=(KT​/w0​)R.​
(44)
This is also a sufficiency condition: concavity gives U(X/JT​)−U(wT∗​/JT​)≤λζT​(X−wT∗​), and taking expectations and using the budget inequality proves global optimality.
To find the whole optimal process, set c=aJ​−r−∣h∣2/2 and
A=(1−p)c+21​(1−p)2∣v−h∣2.
(45)
Since ζt​Jt​=J0​exp((v−h)TWt​+ct), the moment-generating function of a normal distribution gives
KT​=J01−p​eAT,E[(ζT​JT​)1−p∣Ft​]=(ζt​Jt​)1−peA(T−t).
(46)
Pricing the terminal payoff yields the explicit benchmark-relative power-utility portfolio wealth
wt∗​=λ−pζt−p​Jt1−p​eA(T−t)=w0​ζt−p​(Jt​/J0​)1−pe−At.​
(47)
Its proportional diffusion coefficient is ℓ=ph+(1−p)v. Since the wealth diffusion coefficient for dollar fractions is σTπ, replication requires
σTπ∗=ph+(1−p)σTq.
(48)
Using σ−Th=V−1b gives the requested fixed proportions:
π∗=R−1V−1(μ−r1)+(1−R−1)n1​1,θt∗​=wt∗​π∗.​
(49)
The drift matches the self-financing portfolio equation as well. Indeed, ℓ=h+(1−p)(v−h), so the drift of logw∗ from the explicit formula is
r+21​∣h∣2−21​(1−p)2∣v−h∣2=r+ℓTh−21​∣ℓ∣2=r+(π∗)Tb−21​(π∗)TVπ∗.
(50)
Consequently an equivalent form of the solution is
wt∗​=w0​exp([r+(π∗)Tb−21​(π∗)TVπ∗]t+(σTπ∗)TWt​).​
(51)
The bank account fraction is 1−1Tπ∗, with negative values representing borrowing. The first stock term is ordinary power-utility risk exposure, while the second hedges the random benchmark. At the logarithmic limit R=1 the benchmark term disappears, consistently with log(wT​/JT​)=logwT​−logJT​.
