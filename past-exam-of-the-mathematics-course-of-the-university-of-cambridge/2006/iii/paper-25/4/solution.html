The axiom of determinacy, abbreviated AD, asserts that every length-ω infinite game of perfect information in which the two players alternately choose natural numbers is determined. For a payoff set A⊆ωω, player I wins exactly when the completed play belongs to A. A strategy specifies the next move at every finite position where its player is to move; determination means that one player has a winning strategy.
Finite games are determined by backward induction, choosing the least move leading to a guaranteed win when several are available. In ZF one can also prove open determinacy, hence determinacy for closed payoffs. Here is the full open-payoff argument. Let S be the finite positions s with [s]⊆A, so reaching S ensures a win for I. Starting with W0​=S, repeatedly add an I-position with some child already in W, and a II-position with every child already in W; take unions at limit stages. This increasing process stabilizes because the set of finite positions is a set: continuing strict enlargement through its Hartogs number would choose distinct new positions, contradicting that bound. Assign to each admitted position its least entry stage.
At an I-position in the final W, choose the least child of smaller entry stage. At a II-position in W∖S, every child has smaller entry stage. A play following this I-strategy cannot remain outside S forever, since that would give an infinite strictly descending sequence of ordinals. It reaches S and wins. Outside W, every child of an I-position remains outside W, and a II-position has at least one child outside W. Player II chooses the least such child. The play never reaches S, so it cannot belong to the open set A. The initial position is either inside or outside W, proving determination. For a closed payoff apply the same argument to the open complementary payoff for the other player, with the appropriate player assigned to each level. None of these strategies needs a choice principle because moves are natural numbers and least available moves are definable.
To refute simultaneous AD and the axiom of choice, assume choice and well-order all strategies of either player in length c=2ℵ0​. There are exactly c strategies for each player, and any fixed strategy has exactly c compatible plays, since the opponent's successive moves are arbitrary. At stage α<c, choose two fresh plays: xα​ compatible with I's αth strategy, and yα​ compatible with II's αth strategy. Fewer than c plays have previously been excluded, so this is possible even if c is singular. Put A={yα​:α<c}. No xα​ ever enters A. I's αth strategy loses on xα​, and II's αth strategy loses on yα​. Thus this game has no winning strategy for either player, and
AD ⟹ ¬AC.​
