Statistical homogeneity makes the two-point correlation depend on the displacement u=r−r′. Substituting the Fourier transforms and changing variables from (r,r′) to (u,r′) gives
E[Bi​(k)Bj​(k′)]​=∫d3ud3r′Cij​(u)e−ik⋅ue−i(k+k′)⋅r′=Cij​(k)∫d3r′e−i(k+k′)⋅r′.​
(1)
The last integral is a Dirac delta function, so
E[Bi​(k)Bj​(k′)]=(2π)3δ(k+k′)Cij​(k).​
(2)
The plus sign occurs because neither Fourier transform has undergone complex conjugation. For a real magnetic field, the version with Bj​(k′)∗ instead has δ(k−k′). Homogeneity determines dependence on the displacement vector; dependence only on its norm needs statistical isotropy as well.

Statistical isotropy and statistical reflection symmetry allow the spectral tensor to contain only δij​ and ki​kj​:
Cij​(k)=A(k)δij​+D(k)ki​kj​.
(3)
The solenoidal magnetic-field constraint is essential here. Taking its Fourier transform gives ki​Bi​=0, hence ki​Cij​=0 and A+Dk2=0. Thus the spectral tensor is a scalar multiple of the transverse projector of a vector field. Its trace is 2A, so the prescribed normalization gives
Cij​(k)=H(k)(δij​−k2ki​kj​​),k=0.​
(4)
This is the solenoidal isotropic spectral tensor. Without the solenoidal condition, isotropy and reflection symmetry would leave two scalar functions. Without reflection symmetry, an antisymmetric term proportional to iϵijm​km​ could additionally encode magnetic helicity.

Write the transverse displacement as s and take the common line-of-sight interval to be [0,L]. The rotation measure is a line integral of the magnetic field, so its correlation is
CRM​(s)=a02​ne2​∫0L​dz∫0L​dz′Czz​(s,z−z′).
(5)
Substitute the Fourier inversion of the solenoidal isotropic spectral tensor. Keeping the finite observation length gives the exact windowed expression
CRM​(s)=a02​ne2​∫(2π)3d3k​H(k)(1−k2kz2​​)eik⊥​⋅s​∫0L​eikz​zdz​2.
(6)
The allowed extension of the longitudinal displacement integral to the whole real line is the long-path projection of a magnetic correlation. It applies when L is much larger than the correlation length: equivalently, the squared window becomes 2πLδ(kz​) inside the integral. The Dirac delta function sets kz​=0, leaving
CRM​(s)≃a02​ne2​L∫(2π)2d2k⊥​​H(k⊥​)eik⊥​⋅s.​
(7)
Statistical isotropy makes this a function of s=∣s∣. The displayed two-dimensional relation is the intended long-path approximation; the preceding windowed formula accounts for finite-length edge effects.

Put a=c/(4πen) and retain terms linear in δB. The electron magnetohydrodynamics induction equation becomes
∂t​δB=−aB0​∂z​(∇×δB).
(11)
Here the curl of (∇×δB)×B0​z^ reduces to B0​∂z​∇×δB, since the background magnetic field is constant and the divergence of a curl vanishes. For a plane wave δB=bei(k⋅r−ωt), the linear equation and the solenoidal constraint are
ωb=iaB0​k∥​k×b,k⋅b=0.
(12)
On the transverse plane, the operator ik× has eigenvalues ±k by the helicity decomposition of a transverse Fourier mode. Equivalently, squaring the equation and using the vector triple product gives ω2=a2B02​k∥2​k2. Since aB0​=vA​di​, where vA​ is the Alfvén speed and di​ the ion skin depth,
ω±​(k)=±vA​di​k∥​k.​
(13)
The two branches have opposite circular polarizations. The specified electron-only induction model describes a whistler wave; the paper calls these branches kinetic Alfvén waves. The following cascade calculation uses the specified model and its dispersion relation, without adding the pressure response needed for the usual kinetic Alfvén interpretation.

The strain is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. Multiplying its equation by the integrating factor et/τ and using the zero initial condition gives
σ(t)=∫0t​e−(t−s)/τf(s)ds=κ​∫0t​e−(t−s)/τdWs​.
(21)
The second expression is a stochastic integral against Brownian motion. A deterministic linear functional of Gaussian white noise is Gaussian, and its expected value is zero. For t≥t′, the noise covariance gives
E[σ(t)σ(t′)]​=κ∫0t′​e−(t−s)/τe−(t′−s)/τds=2κτ​[e−(t−t′)/τ−e−(t+t′)/τ].​
(22)
For t′≫τ, the initial-condition term is negligible. The stationary autocorrelation is therefore
E[σ(t)σ(t′)]≃2κτ​e−∣t−t′∣/τ.​
(23)
Its normalized exponential decay has correlation time τ. The zero-start Ornstein-Uhlenbeck covariance also shows how stationarity is approached.

Differentiate the product of Dirac delta functions defining P, regarding B and σ as independent density arguments. The random continuity equation is
∂t​P=−∂B​(σBP)+τ1​∂σ​(σP)−∂σ​(fP).
(24)
For 0<s<t, the causal functional derivatives follow from the explicit strain solution and B(t)=B0​exp[∫0t​σ(u)du]:
δf(s)δσ(t)​=e−(t−s)/τ,δf(s)δB(t)​=B(t)τ[1−e−(t−s)/τ].
(25)
Consequently,
δf(s)δP(t)​=−e−(t−s)/τ∂σ​P−τ[1−e−(t−s)/τ]∂B​(B(t)P).
(26)
In the Furutsu–Novikov formula, the covariance κδ(t−s) samples the upper endpoint of the causal time integral. Symmetric regularization of the white noise gives half the mass there. The B response vanishes as s↑t, while the strain response tends to one, so E[f(t)P(t)]=−(κ/2)∂σ​P. Averaging the continuity equation yields the Fokker-Planck equation
∂t​P=−∂B​(σBP)+τ1​∂σ​(σP)+2κ​∂σ2​P.​
(27)
There is no direct diffusion term in B: its equation contains the time integral of colored noise, whereas the strain equation receives Gaussian white noise. The same result follows from the Itô diffusion with drift (σB,−σ/τ) and diffusion vector (0,κ​).

Multiply the Fokker-Planck equation by Bn and integrate over B>0 and all σ, assuming the required boundary terms vanish. Integration by parts gives
dtd​E[Bn]=nE[σBn].
(28)
The mixed moment is not determined by E[Bn]: the current strain and the accumulated magnetic field are correlated. Zero mean strain does not imply E[σBn]=0, so this is an unclosed moment equation.
Keep the strain dependence by defining Pn​(σ,t)=∫0∞​BnP(B,σ,t)dB. The magnetic drift term satisfies
−∫0∞​Bn∂B​(σBP)dB=nσPn​.
(29)
The strain derivatives commute with the B integral. Hence
∂t​Pn​=2κ​∂σ2​Pn​+τ1​∂σ​(σPn​)+nσPn​.​
(30)
This weighted density obeys a tilted Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Fokker-Planck equation. Its integral over σ is the desired moment, while retaining σ makes the evolution closed.

2κ​p′′+τ1​(σp)′+nσp=γp.

Set p=ψexp[−σ2/(2κτ)]. Differentiation eliminates the first derivative of ψ, giving the tilted Ornstein-Uhlenbeck oscillator transformation
2κ​ψ′′+[2τ1​−2κτ2σ2​+nσ]ψ=γψ.

Complete the square and introduce the dimensionless variable x=(σ−κnτ2)/κτ​. The equation becomes
dx2d2ψ​+[1+κn2τ3−2γτ−x2]ψ=0.

Comparing with the quantum harmonic oscillator gives 2E=1+κn2τ3−2γτ. Its energies Em​=m+1/2 therefore correspond to γm​=κn2τ2/2−m/τ. The dominant nonnegative separated mode is the ground state, which is nodeless. Consequently

A general transient can contain sign-changing excited eigenfunctions in its expansion while the total weighted density remains nonnegative.

Assume statistically steady, three-dimensional turbulence at large Reynolds number, with local statistical homogeneity and statistical isotropy at scales small compared with the forcing scale L. The Kolmogorov 1941 theory additionally assumes a local energy cascade, constant mean energy flux per unit mass ϵ, and negligible viscous dissipation within the inertial range. Its dimensional form neglects corrections from internal intermittency.
At separation l, let δul​ be a typical velocity increment. The eddy turnover time is τl​∼l/δul​. Constant transfer rate then gives ϵ∼δul2​/τl​∼δul3​/l, hence
δul​∼(ϵl)1/3,E(k)=CK​ϵ2/3k−5/3.​
(36)
Here E(k) is the one-dimensional turbulent energy spectrum, obtained from δul2​∼kE(k) with k∼l−1. The scaling holds only for
η≪l≪L,L−1≪k≪η−1,η=(ν3/ϵ)1/4.​
(37)
The Kolmogorov length scale follows by setting the scale-dependent Reynolds number δul​l/ν to one. The associated Kolmogorov microscales give τη​=(ν/ϵ)1/2. At larger scales the forcing matters; at smaller scales kinematic viscosity matters.

Take a positive initial separation 0<l0​≪η. In the dissipation range, the velocity field is smooth, so the difference of the two Lagrangian trajectories obeys the linearized separation equation l˙≃(∇u)l. Chaotic stretching gives a positive Lyapunov exponent of order τη−1​ and a typical separation
l(t)∼l0​eλt,t1​∼λ−1log(η/l0​).
(38)
This needs a nonzero initial separation: exactly coincident particles remain coincident in a smooth flow.
Once l lies in the inertial range, the Kolmogorov 1941 theory gives a relative speed of order (ϵl)1/3. A scale-local typical-separation estimate dl/dt∼(ϵl)1/3 integrates to
l(t)2/3∼η2/3+Cϵ1/3(t−t1​).
(39)
After loss of the entry-scale memory, the statistical Richardson pair dispersion law is E[l2]∼gR​ϵ(t−t1​)3. Thus the characteristic separation grows as (t−t1​)3/2 while η≪l≪L. This is a statistical scaling closure, rather than a deterministic equation for every pair.
For l≫L, the particle velocities become approximately independent. If their Lagrangian velocity autocorrelations have a finite Lagrangian integral time TL​∼L/U, the diffusive large-scale pair dispersion has effective relative diffusivity of order UL. After a further velocity-decorrelation transient,
E[l2(t)]∼L2+CD​UL(t−t2​),lrms​∝(t−t2​)1/2.
(40)
This is the long-time mechanism of Taylor turbulent dispersion applied to the difference of two decorrelated trajectories. The root mean square separation eventually loses memory of the L2 term.
Reaching l=L at the end of the inertial range takes
t2​−t1​∼ϵ−1/3(L2/3−η2/3)∼(ϵL2​)1/3∼UL​.​
(41)
Order-one coefficients depend on the dispersion closure. The initial exponential stage can take a long time if l0​ is extremely small, but the inertial-range stage is of order one outer eddy turnover time.
The three-regime turbulent pair-separation model in the sketch matches the regimes continuously for illustration. Its coefficients are schematic; the universal claims here are the scaling powers under the stated stretching, locality and decorrelation assumptions.
