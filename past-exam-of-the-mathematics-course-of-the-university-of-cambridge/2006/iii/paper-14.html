Expose the vertices in order, revealing at step i every edge from vertex i to an earlier vertex. With the resulting filtration Fi​, put
Mi​=E[χ(G)∣Fi​],M0​=μ,Mn​=χ(G).

This is the vertex exposure for chromatic number martingale. Its increments Di​=Mi​−Mi−1​ have conditional mean zero and satisfy ∣Di​∣≤1, by the permitted Lipschitz condition.

We use the random alteration method, controlling large hypergraph independent sets and then removing the few forbidden overlaps. Fix k=2005, let n tend to infinity, and independently include each three-element subset of an n-vertex set as an hypergraph edge with probability p=n−7/4. Call the resulting three-uniform hypergraph H0​, and let s=⌈n/(2k)⌉.
For any prescribed s-vertex set, the probability that it is a hypergraph independent set is (1−p)(3s​). Hence the union bound gives
P(H0​ has an independent set of size s)≤(sn​)(1−p)(3s​)≤exp(nlog2−p(3s​))⟶0,
(8)
because p(3s​) has order n5/4, with a positive constant depending only on k.
Let Y count unordered pairs of distinct hypergraph edges meeting in two vertices. Each potential pair consists of its common pair and two distinct additional vertices, so
EY=(2n​)(2n−2​)p2=O(n1/2).
(9)
The Markov inequality implies P(Y≥n/2)=O(n−1/2). We may therefore fix one realization for which Y<n/2 and no s-vertex hypergraph independent set exists. For each offending hypergraph edge pair choose one vertex from its union, and let D be the set of all chosen vertices. Delete D, together with all incident hypergraph edges, and call the induced hypergraph H. Then ∣D∣≤Y<n/2, so N=∣V(H)∣>n/2. The next two parts verify both desired properties of this same H.

If two distinct hypergraph edges survived in H and shared two vertices, they were an offending pair in H0​. The deletion set D contains a chosen vertex from their union, so at least one of those hypergraph edges could not have survived. This contradiction proves that H is a linear hypergraph. Deleting vertices leaves every surviving hypergraph edge of size three, so the construction meets the uniformity requirement as well as the chromatic-number requirement.

Colour every vertex independently red or blue, each with probability 1/2. A fixed r-element hypergraph edge is monochromatic with probability 21−r. If there are m<2r−1 hypergraph edges, the expected number of monochromatic hypergraph edges is m21−r<1. Equivalently, the union bound says that the probability of even one monochromatic hypergraph edge is less than one. A proper two-colouring therefore exists.
For the converse, suppose r≥2, put N=2r2, and sample m independent uniformly random r-subsets of an N-vertex set. Initially this is a multihypergraph. For a fixed two-colouring with a red vertices, a sampled hypergraph edge is monochromatic with probability
q(a)=(rN​)(ra​)+(rN−a​)​.
(11)
The numerator is minimized by a=N/2=r2. To see this discretely, use (ra+1​)−(ra​)=(r−1a​): the successive difference of the numerator is (r−1a​)−(r−1N−a−1​), negative up to the middle and positive afterwards. Binomial coefficients with an upper index below r are understood as zero.
For this balanced colouring,
q(a)≥2(r2r2​)(rr2​)​=21−r∏j=0r−1​1−j/(2r2)1−j/r2​≥21−r∏j=0r−1​(1−j/r2).

Let Y be the number of isolated vertices and set λ=e−c. For fixed k≥1, the falling factorial (Y)k​ counts ordered k-tuples of distinct isolated vertices. A specified tuple is isolated exactly when all edges incident to its vertices are absent. There are k(n−k)+(2k​) such edges, so
E(Y)k​=(n)k​(1−p)k(n−k)+(2k​).
(16)
Since np=logn+c and np2=o(1), taking logarithms gives
logE(Y)k​=klogn−k(logn+c)+o(1)=−kc+o(1).
(17)
Thus every fixed factorial moment tends to λk. We use the standard factorial-moment criterion for Poisson convergence: nonnegative integer-valued random variables whose factorial moments converge, for every fixed order, to λk converge in distribution to Poisson(λ). Consequently Y has that limiting distribution, and its mass at each fixed integer converges. In particular,
P(Y≥1)⟶1−e−e−c.​
(18)
This establishes the limiting probability of at least one isolated vertex, rather than merely a bound based on its expectation.

A connected graph with n>1 has no isolated vertices. Conversely, a disconnected graph with no isolated vertices has a connected component with size between 2 and n/2. We must show that this latter event has probability tending to zero.
The permitted component-size estimate excludes sizes between loglogn and n/2 with high probability. For the remaining small sizes, let Ck​ count components with k vertices. A connected graph on a fixed k-vertex set contains a spanning tree. By the Cayley formula, there are kk−2 labelled trees, as follows from the Prüfer code bijection between labelled trees and sequences of k−2 labels. A union bound over the trees, together with the absence of every edge to the complementary vertex set, gives
ECk​≤(kn​)kk−2pk−1(1−p)k(n−k).
(20)
Put a=np=logn+c and L=⌈loglogn⌉. Using (kn​)≤(en/k)k and 1−p≤e−p, uniformly for 2≤k≤L we obtain
ECk​≤ak2n​(ne1−ca​)kepk2.
(21)
Here pL2→0. Therefore, with ρn​=e1−ca/n→0,
∑k=2L​ECk​≤a2n​∑k=2∞​ρnk​=O(a/n)⟶0.
(22)
The Markov inequality excludes all these small components with high probability. Together with the permitted estimate, this proves that the difference between the probability of connectivity and the probability of no isolated vertices tends to zero. Hence
P(G is connected)⟶e−e−c.​

The general, or asymmetric Lovász local lemma, is as follows. Let E1​,…,Em​ be events, with a dependency graph of events having neighbour sets N(i). Each Ei​ must be independent of the sigma-algebra generated by all the events indexed by its nonneighbours. If numbers 0≤xi​<1 satisfy
P(Ei​)≤xi​∏j∈N(i)​(1−xj​),
(29)
then
P(⋂i=1m​Eic​)≥∏i=1m​(1−xi​)>0.
(30)
For completeness, the key inductive bound is P(Ei​∣⋂j∈S​Ejc​)≤xi​ whenever i∈/S. Split S into neighbours S1​ of i and nonneighbours S2​. If S1​ is empty, the dependency condition gives the bound directly. Otherwise,
P(Ei​∣⋂j∈S​Ejc​)≤P(⋂j∈S1​​Ejc​∣⋂j∈S2​​Ejc​)P(Ei​∣⋂j∈S2​​Ejc​)​≤∏j∈S1​​(1−xj​)P(Ei​)​≤xi​.
(31)
The denominator bound follows by exposing the exclusions in S1​ one at a time and using the induction hypothesis with strictly fewer conditioning events. The same induction ensures that all conditioning events have positive probability. Finally multiply the successive conditional probabilities of avoiding E1​,…,Em​ to obtain the stated lower bound.
For the symmetric form, suppose each event has probability at most q and the dependency graph has maximum degree D≥1. Choose xi​=1/(D+1). Then
xi​∏j∈N(i)​(1−xj​)≥D+11​(D+1D​)D≥e(D+1)1​.
(32)
The final inequality follows from (1+1/D)D≤e. Thus eq(D+1)≤1 suffices for positive probability that no bad event occurs. If D=0, the dependency condition gives joint independence and the same criterion implies q<1, so the conclusion follows directly.
Apply this to a fair independent two-colouring of the hypergraph vertices, with EW​ the event that hypergraph edge W is monochromatic. Its probability is q=21−r. Events on disjoint hypergraph edges use disjoint independent variables, giving a dependency graph by joining intersecting hypergraph edges. Each hypergraph edge meets at most
D≤∑v∈W​(dH​(v)−1)≤r(Δ−1)
(33)
other hypergraph edges; repeated counting only enlarges this upper bound. The slightly looser bound D+1≤r(Δ+1) is enough for the printed condition. Indeed,
Δ<2re2r​−1⟹e,21−r(D+1)≤e,21−rr(Δ+1)<1.
(34)
The symmetric Lovász local lemma therefore gives a proper two-colouring. A hypergraph with no hypergraph edges is trivially colourable and needs no dependency calculation.

We prove both inequalities, including the BK inequality, for the inhomogeneous product measure in the question. Identify a configuration with its set of coordinates equal to one. An up-set is then an increasing event. For an increasing event A, a set S of open coordinates is a witness if the configuration with precisely S open belongs to A: upward closure ensures that those coordinates alone force A, whatever the remaining coordinates do. The event A□B means that there are disjoint open witnesses S,T for A,B respectively.
First prove the Harris' inequality by induction on the number of coordinates. For the last coordinate, of parameter p, write A0​⊆A1​ and B0​⊆B1​ for the two sections in the remaining coordinates, and put ai​=P(Ai​), bi​=P(Bi​). Induction applied to each section gives
P(A∩B)≥(1−p)a0​b0​+pa1​b1​.
(40)
Subtract the product of the marginal probabilities from the right-hand side:
(1−p)a0​b0​+pa1​b1​−((1−p)a0​+pa1​)((1−p)b0​+pb1​)=p(1−p)(a1​−a0​)(b1​−b0​)≥0.
(41)
The base case of zero coordinates is immediate. Thus
P(A∩B)≥P(A)P(B).​
(42)
For disjoint occurrence, first assume every coordinate parameter is at most 1/2, and again use induction. Put cij​=P(Ai​□Bj​) on the remaining coordinates. The zero-section of A□B is exactly A0​□B0​. The one-section is exactly
(A1​□B0​)∪(A0​□B1​).
(43)
Indeed, the last open coordinate can belong to at most one of two disjoint witnesses. Assigning it to the A witness leaves a witness for A1​ and a witness for B0​; assigning it to the B witness gives the other event. If neither witness uses it, both descriptions still hold. Conversely, witnesses for either displayed event lift to disjoint witnesses by adding the last coordinate only to the appropriate one.
The intersection of the two displayed events contains A0​□B0​, because the sections are increasing. Therefore
P(A□B)≤(1−p)c00​+p(c10​+c01​−c00​)=(1−2p)c00​+pc10​+pc01​.
(44)
The coefficient 1−2p is nonnegative. Applying the induction hypothesis to each term gives
P(A□B)≤(1−2p)a0​b0​+pa1​b0​+pa0​b1​.
(45)
The product of the marginal probabilities is exactly this last expression plus
p2(a1​−a0​)(b1​−b0​)≥0.
(46)
This proves the desired inequality whenever every parameter is at most 1/2.
To remove that restriction, suppose first pi​<1 for every coordinate. Replace coordinate i by the logical disjunction of mi​ independent Bernoulli bits, each with parameter
qi​=1−(1−pi​)1/mi​.
(47)
Choose mi​ large enough that qi​≤1/2. Different blocks are independent, and their OR values have exactly the original distribution. Let A,B be the inverse images of A,B under this block-OR map; they are increasing events. If the OR configuration has disjoint witnesses S,T, choose one open bit from each block indexed by S or T. The bits chosen for S force A, those for T force B, and the two bit sets are disjoint. Thus the inverse image of A□B is contained in A□B. The small-parameter result proves
P(A□B)≤P(A□B)≤P(A)P(B)=P(A)P(B).
(48)
Parameters equal to one follow by a limit: probabilities of any fixed event on a finite cube are polynomials in the coordinate parameters, hence continuous. Zero parameters already cause no difficulty. We have obtained
P(A□B)≤P(A)P(B).​
(49)
For the graph application, take coordinates to be the edges of the complete graph. Let A be the increasing event that x is connected by a graph path to U, and B the corresponding event for W. Their probabilities are α,β. A simple graph path from U to W through x splits at x into two graph paths with disjoint edge sets. Those sets witness A and B, so this graph path event is contained in A□B. The BK inequality gives
P(a U–W path through x)≤αβ.​
(50)
On the other hand, if both A and B occur, concatenate an x-to-U graph path and an x-to-W graph path to obtain a walk from U to W. Removing loops yields a simple graph path, although it need not pass through x. Thus A∩B is contained in the unrestricted connection event, and the Harris' inequality gives
P(a U–W path)≥P(A∩B)≥αβ.​
(51)
The distinction between a simple graph path through x and an unrestricted connection is essential: a walk may revisit an edge, so a through-x walk would not in general provide disjoint witnesses.
