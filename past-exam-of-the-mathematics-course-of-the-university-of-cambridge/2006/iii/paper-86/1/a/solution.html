OurBigBook.com Words: 235
Extend q by zero to the negative half-line. Its ordinary Fourier transform is then exactly the Half-range Fourier transform q​(k). The Fourier inversion theorem gives the real-line term for x>0; no endpoint convention at x=0 is needed.
It remains to show that each extra contour contributes zero, independently of its coefficient. For Imk<0, the integral defining q​ is holomorphic. With α=e2πi/3, multiplication by α2 rotates the sector E into −2π/3≤arg(α2k)≤−π/3, and multiplication by α rotates D into −2π/3≤arg(αk)≤−π/3. Thus the two rotated transforms are holomorphic on their respective upper sectors.
Orient the contours as in the PDF: ∂E runs from infinity on the π/3 ray into zero and then out along the positive real axis; ∂D runs from negative real infinity into zero and then out along the 2π/3 ray. Both have their sector on their left. Smooth decay and integration by parts give q​(k)=O(1/k) in closed lower-half-plane sectors. The factor eikx decays in the upper half-plane for x>0. Close each sector by a large arc; the arc integral vanishes by Jordan lemma, including its short portion near the real axis. The Cauchy integral theorem therefore gives the rotated null contours for half-range Fourier inversion:
∫∂E​eikxq​(α2k)dk=0,∫∂D​eikxq​(αk)dk=0(x>0).​
(1)
Adding arbitrary constant multiples of these two zero integrals to ordinary Fourier inversion proves the asserted inversion formula. The constants c1​,c2​ are arbitrary in this part; selecting them later is what removes an unknown boundary trace.
