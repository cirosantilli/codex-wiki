Suppose first that M is a Noetherian module. An ascending chain of submodules of N is also a chain in M, so it stabilizes. A chain in the quotient module M/N lifts to a chain in M, so it also stabilizes. Thus both N and M/N are Noetherian modules.
Conversely, let M1​⊆M2​⊆⋯ be a chain of submodules of M. If N and M/N are Noetherian modules, both Mi​∩N and (Mi​+N)/N stabilize, say for i≥i0​. For x∈Mi+1​, equality of the images gives y∈Mi​ with x−y∈N. Then x−y∈Mi+1​∩N=Mi​∩N, and hence x∈Mi​. This proves the extension direction of Noetherian modules in a short exact sequence.
The left regular module R is Noetherian because R is a left Noetherian ring. Applying the extension result inductively makes each finite direct sum Rs Noetherian. Every finitely generated module on the left is a quotient module of such a direct sum, so it is Noetherian.
A poly-(cyclic or finite) group has a finite subnormal series
1=G0​◃G1​◃⋯◃Gℓ​=G
(1)
whose factors are cyclic groups or finite groups. We prove that the group ring R[Gi​] is a left Noetherian ring by induction. Put A=R[Gi−1​]. A finite factor makes R[Gi​] a finite free left A-module, using a coset transversal. Its left ideals are A-submodules, so finite-module extension preserves Noetherianity handles this case.
If the factor is infinite cyclic, choose a lift t of a generator and let σ(a)=tat−1. The group ring is the skew Laurent polynomial ring A[t,t−1;σ]. Here is the required left-sided skew Hilbert basis theorem. For a left ideal L⊆A[t;σ], define
In​={σ−n(an​):∑j=0n​aj​tj∈L}.
(2)
Each In​ is a left ideal of A. Left multiplication by t gives In​⊆In+1​. The ascending chain condition makes these ideals constant for n≥n0​; choose finitely many generators for each In​, 0≤n≤n0​, and lift them to polynomials fnj​∈L.
If f∈L has degree d≥n0​ and leading coefficient ad​, write
σ−d(ad​)=∑j​rj​σ−n0​(an0​j​).
(3)
Subtracting ∑j​σd(rj​)td−n0​fn0​j​ cancels the leading coefficient. For d<n0​, use the lifts for Id​ instead. Induction on polynomial degree shows that the finitely many fnj​ generate L. Thus the skew polynomial ring of an automorphism is a left Noetherian ring.
Finally, for a left ideal K of A[t,t−1;σ], its intersection with A[t;σ] has finitely many generators. Every f∈K satisfies tmf∈A[t;σ] for some m≥0, so those same generators generate K after multiplication by t−m. This completes the induction and proves that R[G] is left Noetherian, the left-sided form of group rings of poly-(cyclic or finite) groups are Noetherian.

The Jacobson radical is J(R)=⋂L​L, where L runs over the maximal right ideals. Every proper right ideal I lies in such an L: the union of a chain of proper right ideals containing I is a right ideal and remains proper because it omits 1. Thus Zorn lemma applies.
The right-module form of Nakayama lemma says that a finitely generated module M with MJ=M is zero. More generally, if N is a submodule and M=N+MJ, then M=N. To prove the first statement, suppose M=0 and take a generating set m1​,…,ms​ of minimal size. Since MJ=M, there are ai​∈J with
ms​=∑i=1s​mi​ai​,ms​(1−as​)=∑i<s​mi​ai​.
(4)
The unit criterion for the Jacobson radical makes 1−as​ a unit. Hence ms​ is generated by the other mi​, a contradiction. Apply this result to the quotient module M/N to obtain the general form.
For an injective module endomorphism f of an Artinian module M, the descending chain condition gives
fn(M)=fn+1(M)
(5)
for some n. Given x∈M, write fn(x)=fn+1(y); injectivity of fn gives x=f(y). Thus f is surjective, as asserted by Artinian modules are co-Hopfian.
Now R is a semilocal ring and V=V/VJ is a finitely generated module over the right Artinian ring R=R/J. A finite direct sum of copies of R, and hence its quotient module V, is an Artinian module. The module endomorphism α induces α on V, and the given inverse-image condition says precisely that kerα=0. The preceding argument makes α surjective, so
V=α(V)+VJ.
(6)
The quotient module W=V/α(V) is finitely generated and satisfies WJ=W. Applying Nakayama lemma yields W=0. Therefore α is surjective, by surjectivity from injectivity modulo the Jacobson radical.

An essential right ideal intersects every nonzero right ideal nontrivially. A regular element of a ring is a two-sided non-zero-divisor: both cr=0 and rc=0 imply r=0. Writing S for the set of these elements, the classical right ring of quotients is a ring Q containing R, with every s∈S invertible and every element of Q expressible as as−1.
Goldie theorem states that a semiprime ring has a semisimple Artinian classical right ring of quotients if and only if it is a right Goldie ring: it satisfies the ascending chain condition on right annihilators and has finite uniform dimension. In particular, every semiprime ring that is a right Noetherian ring satisfies the theorem. We prove the requested case directly using the supplied assumption about essential right ideals.
First let c∈S. If a nonzero right ideal B had B∩cR=0, the sum
B+cB+c2B+⋯
(7)
would be direct. Indeed, a finite relation b0​+cb1​+⋯+cmbm​=0 gives b0​∈B∩cR, hence b0​=0; cancellation of c repeats the argument. Each cjB is a nonzero right ideal, so the partial sums form a strictly ascending chain. This contradicts right Noetherianity. Thus a regular principal right ideal in a right Noetherian ring is essential, and every right ideal containing c is essential.
For r∈R and c∈S, put
E={x∈R:rx∈cR}.
(8)
This is an essential right ideal. To check this, take a nonzero right ideal B. If rB=0, then B⊆E. Otherwise rB is a nonzero right ideal, so it meets cR nontrivially; lifting an element of that intersection gives a nonzero element of B∩E. By the supplied assumption, E contains some d∈S, giving
rd=cbfor some b∈R.
(9)
This is the right Ore condition. The set S is multiplicatively closed, and the denominator cancellation condition holds because its elements are non-zero-divisors. Therefore Ore theorem constructs Q and embeds R in it.
For every right ideal J of Q,
J=(J∩R)Q.
(10)
Indeed, if x=as−1∈J, then a=xs∈J∩R. Contracting an ascending chain of right ideals of Q to R therefore proves that Q is a right Noetherian ring.
Suppose J is an essential right ideal of Q. For a nonzero right ideal B of R, choose 0=x∈J∩BQ. The right Ore condition gives a common right denominator for a finite expression of x in BQ, so there is s∈S with
0=xs∈B∩(J∩R).
(11)
Thus J∩R is an essential right ideal of R. It contains a regular element of a ring, which becomes a unit in Q, and consequently J=Q.
Finally, for any right ideal A of Q, choose by Zorn lemma a right ideal B maximal subject to A∩B=0. Then A⊕B is essential: a nonzero right ideal disjoint from A+B would enlarge B while keeping it disjoint from A. Hence A⊕B=Q. Every submodule of the right regular module has a complement, making it a semisimple module. Since it is also Noetherian, it is a finite direct sum of simple modules. It is therefore Artinian, and Q is a semisimple Artinian ring. This is semisimplicity of a classical quotient from regular elements in essential ideals.

A filtration of a ring is an exhaustive increasing family of additive subgroups Fi​R, indexed by the integers, with 1∈F0​R and Fi​RFj​R⊆Fi+j​R. A compatible filtration of a module on a right module satisfies Fi​MFj​R⊆Fi+j​M. The associated graded module has components Fi​M/Fi−1​M, and its action by the associated graded ring is
(m+Fi−1​M)(r+Fj−1​R)=mr+Fi+j−1​M.
(12)
Changing either representative changes the product only by an element of Fi+j−1​M, so the action is well-defined.
The subspace filtration on N is Fi​N=N∩Fi​M. The quotient filtration is Fi​(M/N)=(Fi​M+N)/N. Inclusion induces an injective map grN→grM, and the quotient induces a surjective map to gr(M/N). If m∈Fi​M maps to zero in its degree-i quotient, write m=n+m′ with n∈N and m′∈Fi−1​M. Then n∈N∩Fi​M, so the kernel is exactly the image of grN. We have the short exact sequence
0⟶grN⟶grM⟶gr(M/N)⟶0​,
(13)
which is exactness of associated graded modules for induced filtrations.
For the negative case, Fi​R=R for i≥0. Completeness means that R→lim​q​R/F−q​R is an isomorphism; in particular the filtration of a ring is separated. If x∈F−1​R and r∈R, then (xr)q∈F−q​R. The geometric series
∑q=0∞​(xr)q
(14)
converges in the complete negative filtration and is a two-sided inverse of 1−xr, by the finite geometric identity and continuity of multiplication. The unit criterion for the Jacobson radical now gives
F−1​R⊆J(R)​.
(15)
Let I be any right ideal. The associated graded module grI for its subspace filtration is a homogeneous ideal on the right in grR. If grR is a right Noetherian ring, choose homogeneous generators that lift to x1​,…,xs​∈I, with degrees d1​,…,ds​≤0.
For x∈I∩Fd​R, cancel its degree-d symbol by a sum ∑i​xi​ai,0​ with ai,0​∈Fd−di​​R. The remainder belongs to I∩Fd−1​R. Repeat the cancellation in each succeeding degree. After q steps,
x−∑i​xi​∑j=0q−1​ai,j​∈Fd−q​R,ai,j​∈Fd−j−di​​R.
(16)
For every i, the coefficient series converges to some ai​∈R by completeness. Passing to the limit gives x=∑i​xi​ai​. Thus these finitely many xi​ generate the actual right ideal I; no assumption that I is closed is needed. Since every right ideal is finitely generated, R is right Noetherian, by complete negative filtered-graded transfer of Noetherianity.

The prime radical of a noncommutative ring is N(R)=⋂P​P, with P ranging over all prime ideals of a noncommutative ring. Here primeness means that for two-sided ideals A,B, AB⊆P implies A⊆P or B⊆P. A minimal prime over an ideal I is a prime ideal containing I with no strictly smaller prime ideal containing I.
The ascending chain condition on left ideals also applies to two-sided ideals. We first prove by Noetherian induction that every proper I contains a finite product of prime ideals, each containing I. If there were counterexamples, choose a maximal one. It is not prime, so there are strictly larger two-sided ideals A,B with AB⊆I. Neither is a counterexample; products of prime ideals contained in A and in B then concatenate to a product contained in I, a contradiction.
Write P1​⋯Pt​⊆I⊆Pj​. Every prime ideal P over I contains at least one Pj​, by primeness. The inclusion-minimal members of the finite list Pj​ are therefore precisely the minimal primes over an ideal I: any smaller prime over I would contain a member of that same list. Consequently there are finitely many. Replacing each factor Pj​ by a minimal prime contained in it gives
Q1​Q2​⋯Qt​⊆I
(17)
with each Qj​ minimal over I. Repeated factors may be necessary; in k[ε]/(εm) the sole minimal prime (ε) requires its mth power to reach zero.
Apply this to I=0. Since N(R)⊆Qj​ for every j,
N(R)t⊆Q1​⋯Qt​=0​.
(18)
Thus the prime radical is a nilpotent ideal, as in finite minimal primes and nilpotent prime radical.
Now assume R is commutative. A prime ideal P is an associated prime of a module M when P=AnnR​(m) for some 0=m∈M. Every nonzero module over R has an associated prime: the ascending chain condition gives a maximal annihilator of a nonzero element, and maximal annihilator of a module element is prime.
Since the given M is a Noetherian module, repeatedly choosing such a cyclic submodule in the next quotient yields a finite prime filtration
0=M0​⊊M1​⊊⋯⊊Ms​=M,Mi​/Mi−1​≅R/Pi​.
(19)
The process terminates by the ascending chain condition. In a short exact sequence 0→U→M→W→0, one has Ass(M)⊆Ass(U)∪Ass(W). Indeed, if P=Ann(m) and Rm∩U=0, the image of m in W still has annihilator P. Otherwise choose 0=am∈U; then a∈/P, and primeness gives Ann(am)=P. As Ass(R/Pi​)={Pi​}, induction along the prime filtration proves
AssR​(M)⊆{P1​,…,Ps​}​.
(20)
In particular there are only finitely many associated primes of a module.
Finally let P be a minimal prime ideal of R. Its localization at a prime ideal RP​ has exactly one prime, its maximal ideal PRP​. The preceding nilpotence result makes this maximal ideal nilpotent. Choose 0=y∈RP​ annihilated by PRP​, for example a nonzero element in its last nonzero power, or 1 if PRP​=0. Since elements outside the maximal ideal are units, AnnRP​​(y)=PRP​.
Write y=r/s. The element r/1 is nonzero and also has annihilator PRP​. Generate P by p1​,…,pℓ​. For each i, choose ui​∈/P with ui​pi​r=0, and put u=∏i​ui​. Then m=ur remains nonzero after localization, while Pm=0. If am=0, localization forces a/1∈PRP​, and hence a∈P. Therefore AnnR​(m)=P. This proves that minimal primes are associated primes.

Fix a finite-dimensional nonnegative filtration of a ring giving R its almost commutative algebra structure, and choose a good filtration of a module on M. When grR is a standard graded algebra, the Hilbert-Serre theorem makes the cumulative Hilbert function hM​(j)=dimk​Fj​M eventually a polynomial. For M=0, define the dimension of a filtered module and multiplicity of a filtered module by
d(M)=deghM​,m(M)=d(M)![jd(M)]hM​(j)​.
(21)
Use d(0)=−∞ and m(0)=0. With positive weighted generators instead of a standard grading, the cumulative Hilbert function is eventually a quasipolynomial with a common positive leading coefficient across residue classes. Equivalently, hM​(j)=cjd+O(jd−1), and the definitions are d(M)=d and m(M)=d!c. The common leading coefficient follows from monotonicity: interlacing consecutive residue classes forces their leading coefficients to agree. For a standard grading, multiplicity is a positive integer, since an integer-valued polynomial has an integral expansion in the binomial polynomials; weighted normalization can instead give a positive rational number.
Two good filtrations of a module for this fixed algebra filtration bound each other after fixed index shifts. The corresponding Hilbert functions therefore satisfy hF​(j−a)≤hG​(j)≤hF​(j+b) for fixed a,b. Comparing their leading growth shows that the degree and leading coefficient agree. Thus d(M) and m(M) are independent of the module filtration; the fixed algebra filtration is part of the multiplicity convention.
For a submodule N, use its subspace filtration and the quotient filtration on M/N. These are good because grR is Noetherian. The short exact sequence of filtered pieces gives
hM​(j)=hN​(j)+hM/N​(j).
(22)
Nonzero leading coefficients are positive, so
d(M)=max{d(N),d(M/N)}​.
(23)
If d(N)=d(M/N), those leading coefficients add and
m(M)=m(N)+m(M/N)​.
(24)
This is dimension and multiplicity in a filtered exact sequence.
For the Weyl algebra An​(k) in characteristic zero, Bernstein inequality for Weyl algebra modules asserts d(M)≥n for every nonzero finitely generated module M. Write its generators as xi​,Di​, with [Di​,xj​]=δij​ and all other generator commutators zero. The ordered monomials xαDβ give the Bernstein filtration
Aj​=spank​{xαDβ:∣α∣+∣β∣≤j},dimk​Aj​=(2nj+2n​).
(25)
Take a finite-dimensional generating subspace V=0 of M and put Mj​=Aj​V.
To prove faithful finite-step action of a Weyl algebra, consider 0=a∈Aj​ and choose a nonzero coefficient cαβ​ of a monomial of maximal total degree d≤j. Applying ∏i​(adDi​)αi​(adxi​)βi​ to a gives
λ=cαβ​α!β!(−1)∣β∣=0.
(26)
Indeed, these commutators differentiate the ordered coefficients: lower-degree monomials vanish, and another degree-d monomial can survive only if all its exponents dominate (α,β), which forces equality. This is scalar extraction by iterated Weyl commutators.
Expanding these iterated commutators writes λ=∑ℓ​uℓ​avℓ​ with uℓ​,vℓ​∈Aj​. If a annihilated Mj​, every summand would annihilate V, contrary to λV=0. Therefore the action map is injective:
Aj​↪Homk​(Mj​,M2j​).
(27)
Taking dimensions and comparing polynomial degrees gives
(2nj+2n​)≤hM​(j)hM​(2j),2n≤2d(M),
(28)
which proves Bernstein inequality for Weyl algebra modules. The polynomial representation k[x1​,…,xn​] has dimension n, so the bound is sharp.
The associated graded ring of the Weyl algebra is a polynomial ring, so ascending filtered-graded transfer of Noetherianity makes An​(k) a left Noetherian ring. Thus every submodule of a finitely generated module is finitely generated. If d(M)=n, every nonzero subquotient has dimension at most n by the exact-sequence formula, and at least n by Bernstein inequality for Weyl algebra modules. Every such subquotient therefore has dimension n and positive integral multiplicity.
In a strictly descending chain M=M0​⊋M1​⊋⋯, each nonzero quotient Mi​/Mi+1​ consumes at least one unit of multiplicity, by dimension and multiplicity in a filtered exact sequence. Hence there are at most m(M) strict steps. This proves the descending chain condition, so M is Artinian. In fact this holonomic Weyl algebra module has module length at most m(M), as in multiplicity bounds the length of a holonomic Weyl module.
