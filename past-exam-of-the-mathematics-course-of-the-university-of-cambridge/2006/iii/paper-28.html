Write ϑ(x)=∑p≤x​logp and ψ(x)=∑n≤x​Λ(n) for the two Chebyshev functions, and π(x) for the prime-counting function. If m<p≤2m, the prime p divides the central binomial coefficient (m2m​). Consequently
ϑ(2m)−ϑ(m)≤log(m2m​)≤2mlog2.
(1)
Sum this inequality over dyadic integers m=1,2,4,…,2k−1. It gives ϑ(2k)≤2k+1log2. Monotonicity, with 2k−1<x≤2k, gives ϑ(x)=O(x) for real x≥2. This is the Chebyshev estimate from central binomial coefficients; no information about the distribution of primes beyond unique factorization has been used.
Split the primes at x​. For p>x​, logp>21​logx, so
π(x)≤x​+logx2ϑ(x)​.
(2)
Since x​=O(x/logx),
π(x)=O(logxx​).​
(3)
We also need ψ(x)=O(x). The contributions of higher prime powers satisfy
ψ(x)=∑k≥1​ϑ(x1/k)=ϑ(x)+O(x​logx)=O(x),
(4)
because only k≤logx/log2 contribute, and ϑ(x1/k)≪x​ for k≥2.
The Von Mangoldt divisor identity is logn=∑d∣n​Λ(d), which follows by factoring n into prime powers. Summing it for n≤M, with M a positive integer, gives
log(M!)=∑d≤M​Λ(d)⌊dM​⌋=M∑d≤M​dΛ(d)​+O(ψ(M)).
(5)
The bound already proved makes the last error O(M). Integral comparison for ∑n≤M​logn, or the Stirling formula, gives log(M!)=MlogM−M+O(logM). Therefore
∑d≤M​dΛ(d)​=logM+O(1).

The contribution from higher prime powers is uniformly bounded:
∑p​∑k≥2​pklogp​=∑p​p(p−1)logp​≤∑n≥2​n(n−1)logn​<∞.
(7)
Subtract it, and replace M by ⌊x⌋, to obtain the Mertens first theorem
A(x):=p≤x∑​plogp​=logx+O(1).​
(8)
Now fix δ>0. Apply partial summation to A(t) and f(t)=(logt)−1−δ. With the lower endpoint interpreted as 2−, so that the prime 2 is included, this gives
∑p≤x​p(logp)δ1​=(logx)1+δA(x)​+(1+δ)∫2x​t(logt)2+δA(t)​dt.
(9)
The boundary term tends to zero, and the integral converges, since A(t)≪logt and ∫2∞​dt/(t(logt)1+δ)<∞. Thus the series converges for every positive δ. Keeping the main term of A(t) also gives the useful logarithmically weighted reciprocal-prime tail
p>x∑​p(logp)δ1​=δ(logx)δ1​+Oδ​((logx)1+δ1​).​
(10)
Indeed, subtract the finite partial sum from the limiting integral: the boundary contributes −(logx)−δ and the main integral contributes (1+δ)(logx)−δ/δ.

For σ>1, the absolutely convergent logarithm of the Euler product for the Riemann zeta function is
logζ(s)=∑p​∑k≥1​kp−ks​.
(11)
The elementary identity 3+4cosv+cos2v=2(1+cosv)2≥0 therefore gives

ζ(s)=s−1s​−s∫1∞​us+1{u}​du(Res>0),

whose integral is holomorphic there.
Suppose t=0 and ζ has a zero of order m≥1 at 1+it. As σ↓1, the real factor is O((σ−1)−3), the middle factor is O((σ−1)4m), and the factor at 1+2it is bounded. Their product would tend to zero, contradicting its lower bound one. Hence
ζ(1+it)=0(t=0).​

At t=0 there is a pole, not a zero; the statement about the line does not assert a finite value for ζ(1). This proves the three-four-one product proof of zeta boundary nonvanishing.
To relate the Second Chebyshev function to the logarithmic derivative, differentiate the Euler product in its half-plane of absolute convergence:
−ζ(s)ζ′(s)​=∑n≥1​nsΛ(n)​.

Since ψ(u)=∑n≤u​Λ(n), its first integral of the Chebyshev function is
Ψ1​(x)=∫0x​ψ(u)du=∑n≤x​(x−n)Λ(n).

For any fixed c>1, its Mellin inversion formula is
Ψ1​(x)=2πi1​∫c−i∞c+i∞​−ζ(s)ζ′(s)​s(s+1)xs+1​ds.​

For justification, the elementary contour kernel is 2πi1​∫(c)​ys/(s(s+1))ds=1−y−1 for y>1 and zero for 0<y≤1. Close left or right and take the residues at zero and minus one; at y=1 the continuous value is zero. Absolute convergence on Res=c allows termwise integration of the Dirichlet series, and multiplying this kernel by x gives (x−n)+​.
Here is how the relation yields the asymptotic. Move the contour to the left, using the functional equation of the Riemann zeta function to control the left-hand side. The residue at s=1 is x2/2, and a Nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function ρ contributes −xρ+1/(ρ(ρ+1)), multiplied by its multiplicity. The kernel poles at zero and minus one contribute −xlog(2π) and ζ′(−1)/ζ(−1); a trivial zero of the Riemann zeta function −2k contributes −x1−2k/(2k(2k−1)). Thus the smoothed explicit formula for the Chebyshev function is
Ψ1​(x)=2x2​−∑ρ​ρ(ρ+1)xρ+1​−xlog(2π)+ζ(−1)ζ′(−1)​−∑k≥1​2k(2k−1)x1−2k​.
(18)
For the contour argument, use heights avoiding the zero ordinates and then let those heights increase. The local zero bound from the Riemann–von Mangoldt formula, together with the Local partial-fraction expansion of the Riemann zeta logarithmic derivative, permits heights with logarithmic-derivative bound O(log2T) in each fixed vertical strip. The horizontal integrals then vanish because the denominator is of size T2. After that, send the left edge through negative odd integers to minus infinity; the functional equation of the Riemann zeta function bounds the logarithmic derivative there by a logarithm, and xs+1 makes the left integral vanish for fixed x>1. This explains why this smoothing allows a convergent contour calculation without a quantitative zero-free region.
In fact the Riemann–von Mangoldt formula, stated in the next solution, gives N(T)=O(TlogT) and hence
∑ρ​∣ρ(ρ+1)∣1​<∞.
(19)
The Nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function satisfy 0<Reρ<1, using the Euler product, the functional equation of the Riemann zeta function, and the nonvanishing just proved. For each such zero, xρ−1→0 as x→∞, whereas ∣xρ−1∣≤1 for x≥1. The dominated convergence theorem therefore makes the absolutely convergent zero sum, divided by x2, tend to zero. All the other displayed terms are o(x2). We obtain
∫0x​ψ(u)du∼2x2​.​
(20)
It remains to unsmooth; differentiating an asymptotic without justification would not suffice. Monotonicity of ψ gives, for fixed 0<h<1,
hxΨ1​(x)−Ψ1​((1−h)x)​≤ψ(x)≤hxΨ1​((1+h)x)−Ψ1​(x)​.
(21)
Divide by x and use the integral asymptotic. The lower and upper limits lie between 1−h/2 and 1+h/2. Letting h↓0 proves ψ(x)∼x. Higher prime powers contribute O(x​logx), as in the first solution, so ϑ(x)∼x. Finally partial summation gives
π(x)=logxϑ(x)​+∫2x​t(logt)2ϑ(t)​dt.
(22)
The integral is O(x/(logx)2), by ϑ(t)=O(t) and splitting at x​. Hence
π(x)∼logxx​,​
(23)
which is the Prime number theorem.

Use the meromorphic completion
Z(s)=π−s/2Γ(s/2)ζ(s).
(24)
The functional equation of the Riemann zeta function is
Z(s)=Z(1−s).​
(25)
Its entire version is ξ(s)=21​s(s−1)Z(s) with ξ(s)=ξ(1−s). The completion Z itself has poles at zero and one; it should not be confused with the entire Riemann xi function.
We prove the equation by a theta integral. Set
Θ(t)=∑n∈Z​e−πn2t(t>0).
(26)
The Gaussian Fourier transform, with kernel e−2πixy, is
∫R​e−πtx2e−2πixydx=t−1/2e−πy2/t.
(27)
For example, differentiation in y and integration by parts give the differential equation I′(y)=−(2πy/t)I(y); the Gaussian integral gives I(0)=t−1/2. Periodize the Gaussian by summing e−πt(x+n)2 over integers n. Its kth Fourier coefficient is the displayed transform at k. Both the periodized function and its Fourier series converge absolutely, so evaluation at x=0 gives the Poisson summation formula in this case:
Θ(t)=t−1/2Θ(1/t).
(28)
For Res>1, integration term by term in the Gamma function integral gives
Z(s)=21​∫0∞​(Θ(t)−1)ts/2tdt​.
(29)
Indeed, the two terms for n and −n cancel the factor 1/2, and substitution v=πn2t gives π−s/2Γ(s/2)n−s. Split at one, and use the transformation above in the integral from zero to one. The elementary contribution is
21​∫01​(t−1/2−1)ts/2tdt​=s−11​−s1​.
(30)
Substitution t=1/u in the remaining term therefore gives
Z(s)=s−11​−s1​+21​∫1∞​(Θ(t)−1)(ts/2+t(1−s)/2)tdt​.​
(31)
The theta tail decays exponentially. The integral is consequently an entire function of s, with uniform convergence and termwise differentiation on compact subsets. The formula continues Z meromorphically to the whole plane. Both its rational part and its integral are unchanged by s↦1−s, proving the equation and the entire xi formulation. Using the reflection and duplication identities of the Gamma function, one may equivalently write
ζ(s)=2sπs−1sin(πs/2)Γ(1−s)ζ(1−s),​
(32)
understood as an identity of meromorphic functions, including points where individual factors need continuation.
Now let N(T) count the Nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function ρ=β+iγ with 0<γ≤T, including multiplicity. The Riemann–von Mangoldt formula is
N(T)=2πT​log2πT​−2πT​+O(log(T+2)).​
(33)
For T not a zero ordinate, the more precise usual form is
N(T)=2πT​log2πT​−2πT​+87​+S(T)+O(T−1),S(T)=π1​argζ(21​+iT)=O(logT).
(34)
Here the argument is continued from ζ(2)>0 along the path 2→2+iT→21​+iT. The first formula remains valid at zero ordinates with the stated endpoint convention. Only its leading asymptotic is needed below.
List the positive ordinates as γ1​≤γ2​≤⋯, with multiplicity, consistent with this counting function. Put bn​=2πn/logn. For every fixed ε∈(0,1),
log((1±ε)bn​)=logn−loglogn+Oε​(1),N((1±ε)bn​)∼(1±ε)n.
(35)
For all sufficiently large n, the lower count is less than n and the upper count is greater than n. Consequently (1−ε)bn​<γn​≤(1+ε)bn​. Letting ε↓0 proves the asymptotic inversion of the zeta zero count
γn​∼logn2πn​.​
(36)
This argument works even when several zeros have the same ordinate; “increasing” is interpreted in the standard nondecreasing, multiplicity-counted sense. A count listing only distinct ordinates is a different object and is not determined by this formula alone.
In this notation the Riemann hypothesis says that every Nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function has real part one-half:
ρ=21​+iγfor every nontrivial zero.​
(37)
It concerns their horizontal location, not merely the growth of the ordinates, and does not assert that the zeros are simple. The functional equation and complex conjugation make the zero set symmetric about both the real axis and the critical line; the hypothesis puts all the Nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function on that line.

The Selberg upper-bound sieve replaces a difficult coprimality indicator by a nonnegative square whose coefficients can be optimized. Let P(z)=∏p≤z​p, and suppose a finite sequence has divisibility counts
Ad​=Xg(d)+rd​(d∣P(z)),
(38)
where g is multiplicative on squarefree integers and 0<g(p)<1. Choose real Selberg sieve weights λ1​=1 supported on d∣P(z) with d≤R. If an entry has no prime factor in P(z), its only weighted divisor is 1, so its indicator is bounded above by the square of the divisor sum. Summing gives
S≤∑d,e≤R​λd​λe​A[d,e]​=XQ(λ)+O(∑d,e≤R​∣λd​λe​r[d,e]​∣),
(39)
where Q(λ)=∑d,e​λd​λe​g([d,e]). This is an upper bound regardless of the signs of the weights.
Here is the optimization. On the permitted squarefree integers put
h(r)=∏p∣r​g(p)1−g(p)​,k(r)=h(r)−1,Yr​=∑r∣d​λd​g(d).
(40)
The identity
g([d,e])=g(d)g(e)∑r∣(d,e)​h(r)
(41)
can be checked one prime at a time: a prime in both d and e gives the factor 1+h(p)=1/g(p). Thus
Q(λ)=∑r​h(r)Yr2​.
(42)
Finite Möbius inversion gives λd​g(d)=∑d∣r​μ(r/d)Yr​, and in particular the condition λ1​=1 becomes ∑r​μ(r)Yr​=1. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality now yields
Q(λ)≥J(R,z)1​,J(R,z)=∑r≤Rr∣P(z)​​k(r).
(43)
Equality is attained by Yr​=μ(r)k(r)/J. Inverting again gives the explicit optimizing weights
λd​=μ(d)∏p∣d​(1−g(p))−1J(R,z)v≤R/dv∣P(z)(v,d)=1​∑​k(v)​.
(44)
They satisfy ∣λd​∣≤1. To see this, multiply out ∏p∣d​(1−g(p))−1=∑e∣d​k(e). The numerator becomes the sum of k(ev) over distinct permitted integers ev≤R with e∣d and (v,d)=1, a subset of the terms in J. We therefore have a main term X/J and a controllable remainder. This proves the basic Selberg sieve diagonalization and the useful bound on the weights, rather than merely asserting an optimized density.
Apply it to the polynomial F(n)=n(n+2) for 1≤n≤N. If ω(d) is the number of roots of F modulo squarefree d, the Chinese remainder theorem gives
ω(2)=1,ω(p)=2 (p>2),Ad​=dω(d)​N+O(ω(d)).
(45)
Use g(d)=ω(d)/d, R=z=N1/8, and sieve the indices for which F(n) has no prime factor at most z. The local weights are
k(2)=1,k(p)=p−22​(p>2).
(46)
We claim J(z,z)≫(logz)2. The following truncated-product argument supplies the necessary lower bound. Put w=z1/8, and consider all squarefree integers on the primes at most w, with total weight
W=∏p≤w​(1+k(p))=∏p≤w​(1−g(p))−1.
(47)
Give such an integer d probability k(d)/W. Prime inclusion is independent with probability g(p), so
E[logd]=∑p≤w​g(p)logp=2logw+O(1)=41​logz+O(1),
(48)
by the Mertens first theorem, absorbing the prime 2 separately. For sufficiently large z, this is at most 21​logz. The Markov inequality shows that at least half of W lies on d≤z, all of which are terms in J(z,z).
Moreover, partial summation applied to the Mertens first theorem gives
∑p≤w​p1​=loglogw+B+O(1/logw).
(49)
Indeed it is A(w)/logw+∫2w​A(t)/(t(logt)2)dt, and the contribution of A(t)−logt=O(1) has a finite limiting constant with tail O(1/logw). Expanding the logarithm of W now gives
logW=2∑3≤p≤w​p1​+O(1)=2loglogw+O(1).
(50)
The error is bounded since ∑p​p−2<∞. Consequently W≍(logw)2≍(logz)2, proving the claim. The exponent two reflects the two forbidden classes at almost every prime.
For the remainder use ∣λd​∣≤1 and the simple bound ω([d,e])≤[d,e]≤de. Hence
∑d,e≤z​∣λd​λe​r[d,e]​∣≪∑d,e≤z​de≪z4.
(51)
The number of surviving indices is therefore
S≪(logz)2N​+z4.
(52)
Every pair of primes p,p+2 with p>z survives, since both its prime factors exceed z. The exceptional pairs with p≤z number at most z. With z=N1/8 we obtain

This is the twin-prime upper bound from a quadratic sieve. The method constructs a majorant and bounds the sifted set; it does not give a positive lower bound for twin primes.

Fix integers q≥1 and a with (a,q)=1. We prove the Dirichlet theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions by showing that the prime Dirichlet sum in this residue class diverges as its real exponent decreases to one.
First justify the required Dirichlet L-function facts. If χ is a nonprincipal Dirichlet character modulo q, its sum over a full period is zero: choose a unit b with χ(b)=1, and multiplication by b permutes the residue classes, forcing the sum to equal χ(b) times itself. Therefore the partial sums Cχ​(u)=∑n≤u​χ(n) are bounded by q. Partial summation gives
L(s,χ)=s∫1∞​Cχ​(u)u−s−1du(Res>0),
(54)
which supplies holomorphic continuation near one, with locally uniform convergence. For the principal Dirichlet character χ0​,
L(s,χ0​)=ζ(s)∏p∣q​(1−p−s)
(55)
has a simple pole at one, with residue φ(q)/q.
The assumption in the question gives nonvanishing at one for real nonprincipal characters. We must also prove it for nonreal characters. For a nonreal χ, its square χ2 is nonprincipal. For real σ>1, logarithms of the Euler products give
ζ(σ)3∣L(σ,χ)∣4∣L(σ,χ2)∣≥1.
(56)
For a prime not dividing q, its logarithmic coefficient is 3+4Reχ(p)k+Reχ(p)2k=2(1+cosθ)2≥0, where χ(p)k=eiθ. Primes dividing q contribute only the positive zeta term. If L(1,χ) vanished to order m≥1, the product would be O((σ−1)4m−3) and tend to zero: the squared-character factor is bounded because χ2 is nonprincipal. This is a contradiction. Hence
L(1,χ)=0for every nonprincipal χ.
(57)
This step uses the given real-character hypothesis and the Euler product positivity for L-function nonvanishing for all remaining characters.
In σ>1 choose the logarithm furnished by the absolutely convergent Euler product. Its prime-power expansion gives
logL(σ,χ)=∑p​pσχ(p)​+O(1).
(58)
The error from powers k≥2 is uniformly bounded as σ↓1, by ∑p​∑k≥2​1/(kpkσ)<∞. For nonprincipal χ, nonvanishing and holomorphic continuation at one provide a bounded logarithm in a neighborhood of one. The Euler-product branch differs from that branch by a constant integral multiple of 2πi on the final real interval, so it too is bounded. Thus
∑p​pσχ(p)​=Oq​(1)(χ=χ0​).
(59)
For the principal character, its simple pole instead gives
∑p∤q​pσ1​=logσ−11​+Oq​(1).
(60)
The Orthogonality of Dirichlet characters gives, for primes not dividing q,
1p≡a(modq)​=φ(q)1​∑χmodq​χ(a)​χ(p).
(61)
Multiply by p−σ and sum. Absolute convergence allows the interchange for σ>1, so
p≡a(modq)∑​pσ1​=φ(q)1​logσ−11​+Oq​(1).​
(62)
The right side tends to infinity. If there were only finitely many primes in the progression, the left side would have a finite limit at one. This contradiction proves infinitely many primes occur in every reduced residue class. For q=1 or 2 the character argument simply has no nonprincipal terms and gives the same conclusion.
For the final deduction the modulus q is fixed. Let A(t)=π(t;q,a). The given consequence of the Siegel–Walfisz theorem is A(t)∼t/(φ(q)logt). Partial summation, including the prime 2 through the lower endpoint 2− when appropriate, gives
∑p≤xp≡a(modq)​​p1​=xA(x)​+∫2x​t2A(t)​dt.
(63)
The boundary is Oq​(1/logx). Write A(t)=t(1+ε(t))/(φ(q)logt) with ε(t)→0. The main integral is (loglogx−loglog2)/φ(q). The error is o(loglogx): given η>0, choose T such that ∣ε(t)∣≤η for t≥T. Its integral from 2 to T is a fixed constant, and its absolute value from T to x is at most ηloglogx/φ(q) plus a fixed constant. Divide by loglogx and then let η↓0. We obtain the reciprocal primes in a fixed arithmetic progression asymptotic
p≤xp≡a(modq)​∑​p1​∼φ(q)1​loglogx.​
(64)
The argument uses the supplied prime-counting asymptotic in this last part; the preceding infinitude proof did not assume the Prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions.
