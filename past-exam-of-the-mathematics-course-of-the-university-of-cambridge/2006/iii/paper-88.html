For a period lattice Λ, define the Weierstrass elliptic function by
℘(z)=z21​+∑ω∈Λ∖{0}​((z−ω)21​−ω21​).
(1)
The subtraction is essential: the separate inverse-square lattice sum need not converge. Let r be the distance to the nearest nonzero lattice point. For ∣z∣<r, the power series identity (1−w)−2=∑m≥0​(m+1)wm gives
(z−ω)21​−ω21​=∑m≥1​(m+1)ωm+2zm​.
(2)
The stipulated convergence allows the sums to be interchanged on smaller disks. Pairing ω with −ω cancels odd powers. In terms of the lattice Eisenstein series, this proves the Laurent coefficients of the Weierstrass elliptic function:
℘(z)=z−2+k≥2∑​(2k−1)G2k​z2k−2,G2k​=ω=0∑​ω−2k.​
(3)
For completeness, the function is even and is a periodic function: differentiating gives ℘′(z)=−2∑ω∈Λ​(z−ω)−3, which is invariant under translation by a lattice generator. Thus ℘(z+ω)−℘(z) is constant. For a primitive generator, evaluating at z=−ω/2 and using evenness makes this constant zero. Two generators establish lattice periodicity. Hence any polynomial in ℘,℘′ is an elliptic function.
Set c1​=3G4​ and c2​=5G6​. The first terms are ℘=z−2+c1​z2+c2​z4+O(z6). Squaring its derivative and cubing the function give
℘′2=4z−6−8c1​z−2−16c2​+O(z2),4℘3=4z−6+12c1​z−2+12c2​+O(z2).
(4)
Consequently H=℘′2−4℘3+20c1​℘+28c2​ has no pole at zero and has value zero there. Lattice periodicity removes the poles at every lattice point; there are no others. Thus H is an entire function and an elliptic function, bounded on a closed fundamental parallelogram and therefore throughout the plane. The Liouville theorem makes it constant, and its value at zero makes it zero. This proves the Weierstrass elliptic differential equation
℘′2=4℘3−g2​℘−g3​,g2​=60G4​,g3​=140G6​.​
(5)
Differentiating and cancelling ℘′ wherever it is nonzero gives ℘′′=6℘2−g2​/2; the identity extends across its isolated zeros by holomorphy. Write ℘=z−2+∑n≥1​cn​z2n. For n≥3, compare coefficients of z2n−2:
(2n(2n−1)−12)cn​=6∑r=1n−2​cr​cn−1−r​,cn​=(2n+3)(n−2)3​r=1∑n−2​cr​cn−1−r​.​
(6)
The denominator is positive and rational, and c1​=3G4​,c2​=5G6​. Induction therefore gives a polynomial with positive rational coefficients on every monomial that occurs. Since G2k​=ck−1​/(2k−1), the same holds for every k>3. This is the positive polynomial recurrence for lattice Eisenstein sums; for example
G8​=73​G42​,G10​=115​G4​G6​,G12​=14318​G43​+14325​G62​.​
(7)
Positive coefficients do not assert that the complex values of the lattice sums themselves are positive.

Use the Hecke-normalized rational slash operator from the PDF, writing f[α]k​ for its right action. Put AD​=diag(D,1). If γ=(ac​bd​)∈Γ0​(N), then
AD​γAD−1​=(ac/D​Dbd​)∈Γ0​(M).
(8)
Indeed N/D is an integer divisible by M, so c/D is an integer divisible by M. The ordinary weight transformation of the original cusp form therefore gives
f(Dγτ)=(cτ+d)kf(Dτ).
(9)
Thus g(τ)=f(Dτ) has the correct transformation for the Gamma 0 congruence subgroup at level N, and it is holomorphic in the complex upper half-plane.
It remains to check every modular cusp, not just infinity. Let ρ∈SL2​(Z) carry infinity to that cusp, and choose σ∈SL2​(Z) with σ∞=AD​ρ∞. The matrix B=σ−1AD​ρ is upper triangular over the rationals, with positive ratio of diagonal entries. Therefore, up to a nonzero constant weight factor,
g[ρ]k​=(f[σ]k​)[B]k​.
(10)
The original cusp expansion of f[σ]k​ has only positive exponents. The change τ↦rτ+s induced by B, with r>0, preserves exponential decay as Imτ→∞. The transformed function also has a positive integral period because g is modular for a finite-index subgroup. Its bounded periodic holomorphic expansion consequently has no negative exponents and its limiting constant term is zero. This proves cusp holomorphy under rational slash operators and establishes the degeneracy map for cusp forms:
f(Dτ)∈Sk​(Γ0​(N)).​
(11)

The modular discriminant has the convergent product
Δ(τ)=q∏n≥1​(1−qn)24,q=e2πiτ,
(12)
is a weight-twelve level-one cusp form, and has order one at infinity. Applying the degeneracy map for cusp forms with M=1,N=D=p gives Δ(pτ)∈S12​(Γ0​(p))​. The original form Δ is also a cusp form on this subgroup.
There are precisely two modular cusps for prime level, represented by infinity and zero. To see this directly, write a rational cusp as a/c in lowest terms. If p∣c, completing (a,c)T to a determinant-one matrix gives a member of Γ0​(p) taking infinity there. If p∤c, the equation Ac−aC=1 has a solution with p∣C: adjust a Bézout solution modulo p, using the invertibility of c. The matrix (AC​ac​) then lies in Γ0​(p) and takes zero there. The two classes are distinct because their denominator divisibility is preserved by the group.
At infinity the cusp width is one, so q is the local parameter. The products start with q and qp, giving orders one and p. At zero use S=(01​−10​). Since SThS−1=(1−h​01​), the cusp width is p and the parameter is q0​=e2πiτ/p. The weight-twelve inversion law of the modular discriminant gives

∞0​Δ(τ)1p​Δ(pτ)p1​​.​

The dimension assumption supplies a nonzero h∈S2​(Γ0​(11)). The projective index of the Gamma 0 congruence subgroup is twelve: reduction modulo eleven identifies its cosets with the twelve points of P1(F11​). There are two modular cusps, by the preceding classification. There are no effective elliptic fixed points. An order-two fixed point would require a root of x2+1 modulo eleven, which does not exist; an order-three fixed point would require a root of x2+x+1, equivalent to a nontrivial cube root of unity in a group of order ten, which also does not exist.
The regular-cusp valence formula on a torsion-free modular curve therefore gives
∑P∈X0​(11)​ordP​h=122⋅12​=2.
(15)
All orders are nonnegative and both cusp orders are at least one. Thus h has order exactly one at both modular cusps and has no zeros in the half-plane. One way to justify the degree count here is the genus formula for a modular curve: the same index, cusp and elliptic data give genus one. The invariant differential h(τ)dτ has cusp order ordh−1, because dτ is a nonzero constant times dqc​/qc​. Its total canonical degree 2g−2=0 therefore makes the sum of the two cusp-adjusted orders and the interior orders zero, giving precisely the count above.
Let H(τ)=Δ(τ)Δ(11τ). By the product formula it has no zeros in the half-plane; by the cusp-order table it has order twelve at each cusp. Its weight is twenty-four. Therefore H/h12 is a weight-zero modular function, with neither zeros nor poles anywhere on the compactified modular curve. It is constant by the maximum modulus principle on a compact surface. Normalize h so its leading Fourier coefficient at infinity is one. Since H=q12+O(q13), that constant becomes one:
h12=Δ(τ)Δ(11τ),h(τ)=qn≥1∏​(1−qn)2(1−q11n)2.​
(16)
This fixes the holomorphic twelfth root by its leading term and proves the weight-two discriminant root at level eleven is a nonzero member, hence a generator, of the given space. Taking an arbitrary pointwise twelfth root without this argument would not establish modularity: it could carry an unwanted character.

Put AN​=(0N​−10​). All calculations here use the Hecke-normalized rational slash operator of the PDF. Its automorphy factor identity gives (f[α]k​)[β]k​=f[αβ]k​. For γ=(ac​bd​)∈Γ1​(N),
AN​γAN−1​=(d−Nb​−c/Na​)∈Γ1​(N).
(17)
This proves that AN​ normalizes the Gamma 1 congruence subgroup. Right-action associativity shows f[AN​]k​ has the same transformation law as f. It is holomorphic in the complex upper half-plane; at each modular cusp, the rational change of variable preserves cusp decay by the upper-triangular factorization used in 2(i). Consequently WN​ maps the cusp form space to itself.
For a scalar matrix −NI, the printed slash normalization gives
f[−NI]k​=(−1)kNk−2f.
(18)
Since AN2​=−NI, the two phase and determinant factors cancel:
WN2​f=i2kN2−kf[−NI]k​=f.​
(19)
Explicitly, the Fricke involution in the question is
WN​f(τ)=ikN−k/2τ−kf(−Nτ1​).
(20)
Its phase is essential for the involution and the functional-equation sign.
Suppose WN​f=εf. Define F(y)=f(iy/N​) for y>0. Substitution at τ=iy/N​ gives
F(1/y)=εykF(y).​
(21)
At infinity F decays exponentially because f is a cusp form. This identity gives F(y)=O(y−ke−c/y) near zero for some c>0. Thus its Mellin transform converges for every complex s, locally uniformly with all s derivatives.
Initially, in a right half-plane where the Dirichlet series converges absolutely, termwise integration and the gamma function integral give
∫0∞​F(y)ys−1dy=Ns/2(2π)−sΓ(s)∑n≥1​nsan​​=ΛN​(f,s).
(22)
Such a half-plane exists without any eigenform hypothesis. Indeed the invariant quantity (Imτ)k/2∣f(τ)∣ is bounded on the modular quotient, by cusp decay and compactness away from the cusps. Fourier coefficient extraction on the line Imτ=1/n gives an​=O(nk/2), so Res>1+k/2 suffices for the interchange.
Split the integral at one and change y to 1/y in its lower part. The phase-normalized Fricke functional equation becomes transparent:
ΛN​(f,s)=∫1∞​F(y)(ys−1+εyk−s−1)dy.​
(23)
The integral defines an entire function: exponential decay dominates every power and every logarithmic derivative uniformly on compact s sets. Replacing s by k−s and using ε2=1 now proves
ΛN​(f,k−s)=εΛN​(f,s).​
(24)
This gives the full analytic continuation of the completed L-function of a cusp form, not merely a formal identity in its initial convergence region.

Write A,B for the two given congruence subgroups, to distinguish them from the notation Γ1​(N). Their double coset has a finite decomposition
AαB=∐j=1r​Aαj​.
(34)
Finiteness follows because B∩α−1Aα has finite index in B. To check this without assuming integrality of α, clear its denominators: a sufficiently deep principal congruence subgroup lies both in B and, after conjugation by α, in A. Representatives for (B∩α−1Aα)\B give the displayed decomposition.
Using the Hecke-normalized rational slash operator from the PDF, define the double-coset operator on modular forms by
[AαB]f=j=1∑r​f[αj​]k​.​
(35)
Replacing αj​ by γαj​ for γ∈A changes no term because f[γ]k​=f. Right multiplication by β∈B permutes these left cosets, so ([AαB]f)[β]k​=[AαB]f. Each summand is holomorphic in the complex upper half-plane. At any rational modular cusp, factor αj​ρ=σC with σ∈SL2​(Z) and C upper triangular of positive slope, as in 2(i). The cusp expansion of f[σ]k​ has no negative terms, and substitution by C preserves boundedness. The sum is periodic in a cusp coordinate for B, so it has a removable singularity there. This proves
[AαB]f∈Mk​(B).​

Now set Γ=Γ1​(N). For an integer d prime to N, choose σd​∈Γ0​(N) with lower-right entry congruent to d modulo N. Such a lift exists by choosing a with ad≡1(modN) and using (aN​(ad−1)/Nd​). The subgroup Γ is normal in Γ0​(N), and its quotient is (Z/NZ)× through the lower-right entry. Define the diamond operator by
⟨d⟩f=f[σd​]k​.​
(37)
Different lifts differ by a member of Γ, so this is well defined. The diamond operators satisfy ⟨d⟩⟨e⟩=⟨de⟩.
For a prime p∤N, put Ap​=diag(1,p) and define the Hecke operator
Tp​=[ΓAp​Γ].​
(38)
Choose a,b∈Z with ap−bN=1 and set σp​=(aN​bp​). A complete set of left-coset representatives is
Aj​=(10​jp​)(0≤j<p),Bp​=σp​(p0​01​).

Here Aj​=Ap​Tj and Bp​=Ap​(apN​b1​), with both right factors in Γ. To prove completeness, H=Γ∩Ap−1​ΓAp​ consists of the members of Γ whose upper-right entry is divisible by p. Reduction of Γ modulo p is onto SL2​(Fp​): use the Chinese remainder theorem to impose identity modulo N and any chosen determinant-one matrix modulo p, then lift to SL2​(Z) by elementary matrices. Reduction of H is the lower triangular subgroup. Its left cosets correspond to the p+1 lines of top rows in Fp2​.
