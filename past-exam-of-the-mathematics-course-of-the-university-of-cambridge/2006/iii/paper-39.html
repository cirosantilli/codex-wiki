Use a strictly positive bank account as numéraire, and write all prices and payoffs in discounted units. There are d risky assets with deterministic initial price vector S0​, terminal price vector S1​∈L2(P), and gains Y=S1​−S0​. A self-financing portfolio with initial capital x and constant risky holdings θ has discounted terminal wealth x+θTY. The initial amount in the bank account is x−θTS0​, so x denotes total initial capital, not just the cash holding. Assume the usual absence of arbitrage, and hence existence of an equivalent martingale measure; wherever a general probability space is used below, explicitly assume that such a measure exists and makes the asset payoffs integrable.
The attainable claims are precisely the linear subspace
A=span{1,Y1​,…,Yd​}⊂L2(P).
(1)
The finite-dimensional subspace is closed, so every square-integrable contingent claim H has a unique orthogonal projection onto A. This is its optimal one-period least-squares hedging payoff. Holdings are unique after redundant assets have been removed. Put
m=EP​Y,C=CovP​(Y),cH​=CovP​(Y,H),
(2)
and suppose the remaining covariance matrix C is invertible. Expanding the squared error gives
EP​(H−x−θTY)2=(EP​H−x−θTm)2+VarP​(H)−2θTcH​+θTCθ.
(3)
First minimize over x, then complete the square in θ. Thus
θ∗​=C−1cH​,x∗​=EP​H−mTC−1cH​.​
(4)
The residual L=H−x∗​−θ∗T​Y satisfies
EP​L=0,EP​[YL]=0,minx,θ​EP​(H−x−θTY)2=VarP​(H)−cHT​C−1cH​.
(5)
Indeed, every other squared error equals this minimum plus
(x−x∗​+(θ−θ∗​)Tm)2+(θ−θ∗​)TC(θ−θ∗​).
(6)
This proves optimality, and also shows that zero error is equivalent to attainability of a European contingent claim. If initial capital x is prescribed instead, the normal equations for linear least squares give E[YYT]θ=E[Y(H−x)]; one must not use the free-capital formula without this adjustment.
A dominated martingale measure is a probability measure Q≪P such that EQ​Y=0. Its Radon-Nikodym derivative Z satisfies Z≥0, EP​Z=1, and EP​[ZY]=0. An equivalent martingale measure additionally requires Z>0 almost surely. Every integrable attainable claim H=x+θTY then has price x=EQ​H, independent of which such measure is chosen. For an unattainable contingent claim, different dominated martingale measures can give different prices; least-squares approximation chooses a projection criterion rather than exact replication.
Write Y=m+M, where M=Y−m is the centered martingale part of the one-period gains. The minimal martingale measure in a one-period market changes the means of the traded gains to zero while preserving all square-integrable mean-zero directions L orthogonal to M. Its candidate density is
Z∗​=1−mTC−1(Y−m).​
(7)
Direct computation gives EP​Z∗​=1 and EP​[Z∗​Y]=m−CC−1m=0. If EP​L=0 and EP​[ML]=0, then EP​[Z∗​L]=0, as required. Conversely, an L2 density with this preservation property is orthogonal to the orthogonal complement of span{1,M1​,…,Md​}, so it lies in that span. Its normalization and martingale constraints force exactly Z∗​. Moreover,
x∗​=EP​[Z∗​H].
(8)
Every other square-integrable signed pricing density has the form Z∗​+D, with D orthogonal to 1 and Y, hence to Z∗​. Therefore
EP​[(Z∗​+D)2]=EP​Z∗2​+EP​D2,
(9)
so the minimal density is also the minimum-norm one-period pricing weight. This coincidence concerns one period; it is not an unrestricted assertion about dynamic hedging models.
Positivity needs checking. If Z∗​>0 it defines an equivalent martingale measure; if merely Z∗​≥0, it defines a dominated martingale measure. Otherwise it defines only a signed martingale measure. For example, take S0​=2 and Y=−1,1,3 with physical probabilities 1/100,89/100,10/100. Then m=59/50, C=1019/2500, and Z∗​=−4350/1019 in the third state. There is nonetheless an equivalent martingale measure assigning probabilities 3/5,3/10,1/10, whose mean gain is zero. Thus absence of arbitrage does not guarantee positivity of the least-squares density, and a least-squares initial cost need not be an arbitrage-free price for an unattainable payoff.
Finally prove completeness and uniqueness of dominated martingale measures. Suppose first that every bounded contingent claim is attainable. For any event A, replicate 1A​=xA​+θAT​Y. Under any dominated martingale measure, Q(A)=xA​. Thus all such measures agree on every event. Existence follows from our no-arbitrage assumption, giving uniqueness.
Conversely, fix an equivalent martingale measure Q0​, and let k=dimA. If the probability space admits k+1 disjoint events A1​,…,Ak+1​ of positive probability, choose a basis a1​,…,ak​ of A, including 1. The k×(k+1) matrix with entries EQ0​​[ai​1Aj​​] has a nonzero null vector (cj​). Consequently the bounded, nonzero random variable
h=∑j=1k+1​cj​1Aj​​
(10)
satisfies EQ0​​h=0 and EQ0​​[hY]=0. For 0<∣ε∣<1/∥h∥∞​, define
dQ0​dQε​​=1+εh.
(11)
These are distinct equivalent martingale measures, because their densities are positive and their gain moments remain zero. Thus uniqueness rules out such a partition. A probability space with no k+1 disjoint positive-probability events consists, modulo null sets, of at most k atoms: repeatedly split any non-atomic positive event to obtain a larger finite partition. On N atoms the payoff space has dimension N, while A has dimension k≤N. Hence N=k and A is the whole payoff space. Every contingent claim is attainable. This proves
complete, arbitrage-free one-period market⟺unique dominated martingale measure.​
(12)
Here completeness means all bounded claims, or all square-integrable claims in the L2 formulation. The existence/no-arbitrage hypothesis matters: on a one-state space, cash and a stock with a sure positive discounted gain span every payoff, but no dominated martingale measure can make that gain have zero mean.

Let the one-period riskless gross return be R>0, with 0<d<R<u, and let the physical up probability be π∈(0,1). The binomial market has risk-neutral probability
q=u−dR−d​∈(0,1),qu+(1−q)d=R.
(13)
Under its equivalent martingale measure Q, successive multipliers Jk​ are independent, taking u,d with probabilities q,1−q. The risk-neutral pricing function has the extension
gr​(s)=R−(n−r)EQ​[f(s∏k=r+1n​Jk​)],s>0.
(14)
For every fixed positive multiplier A, the function s↦f(As) is convex when f is convex. Taking a positive weighted sum preserves convexity. More explicitly, for 0≤λ≤1,
gr​(λs+(1−λ)t)≤λgr​(s)+(1−λ)gr​(t).
(15)
Thus the pricing extension is convex, and so are its values on the attainable stock-price grid.
The replicating portfolio in a binomial market holds
Δr​(s)=(u−d)sgr+1​(us)−gr+1​(ds)​
(16)
shares over the next interval. If r+1<n, put A=Δr+1​(us) and D=Δr+1​(ds). These are the secant slopes of gr+2​ on [uds,u2s] and [d2s,uds], respectively. For a convex function, the slope of a secant on a later adjacent interval is no smaller than that on the earlier interval: apply the defining convexity inequality to the middle point uds, then rearrange. Hence A≥D. Using backward option pricing twice gives
gr+1​(us)−gr+1​(ds)Δr​(s)​=R−1[q{gr+2​(u2s)−gr+2​(uds)}+(1−q){gr+2​(uds)−gr+2​(d2s)}],=Rqu​A+R(1−q)d​D.​
(17)
The two coefficients are positive and sum to one. Therefore convex-payoff delta monotonicity in a binomial market yields
Δr+1​(us)≥Δr​(s)≥Δr+1​(ds).​
(18)
In particular an up move cannot reduce the stock holding. “Increases” here means nondecreases: an affine payoff gives equality, so strict increase cannot be claimed for every convex payoff.
For the expected utility maximization problem, let P be the physical measure and Z=dQ/dP on terminal paths. If a path has j up moves, its binomial-market probability density is
Zj​=(πq​)j(1−π1−q​)n−j.
(19)
Every terminal payoff is replicable in this complete market. Buying a positive payoff H with initial wealth w0​ therefore imposes the state-price budget constraint EQ​H=Rnw0​. Since the utility function is increasing, the full budget is used. Its derivative and second derivative are
v′(x)=x−(γ−1)/γ,v′′(x)=−γγ−1​x−(2γ−1)/γ<0.
(20)
Put a=γ/(γ−1). The first-order condition v′(H∗​)=λZ for a positive multiplier λ gives H∗​=CZ−a. The budget determines C uniquely. Independence of the successive moves gives
D:=EQ​Z−a=[q(πq​)−a+(1−q)(1−π1−q​)−a]n.
(21)
Thus the optimal claim, as a function of the number of up moves, is
H∗​(j)=DRnw0​​(qπ​)aj(1−q1−π​)a(n−j).​
(22)
Because Sn​=S0​ujdn−j uniquely determines j, this is also a terminal-stock-price payoff, attainable by backward option pricing and claim replication. It rewards states relatively more probable under the investor's physical measure than under the pricing measure. If π=q, the optimum is the riskless payoff Rnw0​.
To prove global optimality rather than merely assert a stationary point, use the tangent inequality for the strictly concave function v:
v(H)≤v(H∗​)+v′(H∗​)(H−H∗​)=v(H∗​)+λZ(H−H∗​).
(23)
Taking physical expectations makes the last term zero for any fully financed competing claim, and nonpositive for any claim costing less. Therefore EP​v(H)≤EP​v(H∗​), with equality only when H=H∗​ almost surely. Finiteness causes no difficulty on the finite binomial tree, and H∗​>0 in every terminal state.

Subtract the boundary slope from the Brownian motion: Xt​=Bt​−bt. Then Ta,b​ is the first-passage time of X to the fixed level a. The zero-drift Brownian reflection principle gives
P(Ta​≤t)=2{1−Φ(a/t​)},h0​(t)=2πt3​a​e−a2/(2t).
(24)
Here Φ is the standard normal distribution function. To change the drift to −b, use the Girsanov theorem on each finite horizon, with density Lt​=exp(−bBt​−b2t/2). On {Ta​≤t}, conditional expectation at the stopped time gives E[Lt​∣FTa​​]=LTa​​, because the density is a true martingale. Since BTa​​=a, the drifted Brownian first-passage density is consequently
hb​(s)=e−ab−b2s/2h0​(s)=2πs3​a​exp(−2s(a+bs)2​),s>0.
(25)
There may be additional mass at infinite hitting time; the displayed density only describes finite passage. With e−θ∞=0, apply the given zero-drift Brownian first-passage Laplace transform:
E[e−θTa,b​]​=e−ab∫0∞​e−(θ+b2/2)sh0​(s)ds=exp[−a(b+b2+2θ​)]​,θ>0.​
(26)
The zero-discount limit is 1 for b≤0 and e−2ab for b>0, so the hitting probability is less than one precisely when the line moves away with positive slope.
For a direct verification of the linear-boundary Brownian first-passage distribution, define
Fb​(t)=e−2abΦ(t​bt−a​)+Φ(−t​a+bt​).
(27)
Both terms tend to zero as t↓0. Let ϕ be the standard normal density. The identity
e−2abϕ(bt​−t​a​)=ϕ(bt​+t​a​)

Fb′​(t)=ϕ(bt​+t​a​)[2t​b​+2t3/2a​−2t​b​+2t3/2a​]=hb​(t).
(29)

P(Ta,b​≤t)=e−2abΦ(t​bt−a​)+1−Φ(t​a+bt​).​
(30)
This proof applies to every real b, including the defective distribution when b>0.
For the barrier digital put, use the dividend-free Black-Scholes model with constant interest rate ρ and spot volatility σ>0. Under the risk-neutral measure,
St​=S0​exp((ρ−σ2/2)t+σWtQ​).
(31)
Set T=t0​ and
a=σlog(c/S0​)​>0,b=σσ2/2−ρ​.
(32)
Reaching the barrier c is equivalent to WtQ​ reaching a+bt. The contingent claim pays one only on the survival event {Ta,b​>T}. Risk-neutral pricing therefore gives
V0​=e−ρT[Φ(T​a+bT​)−e−2abΦ(T​bT−a​)].​
(33)
This is a path-survival price, not the price of a terminal digital put option. Continuity of the stock paths and the absence of an atom in the finite-horizon maximum imply that alternative touching conventions change no price. As c→∞, the value tends to e−ρT; as c↓S0​, it tends to zero. Both checks agree with the survival interpretation.

Take a non-dividend-paying stock with physical dynamics dSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​ and continuous-time bank account Bt​=eρt. Write T=t0​ and assume the candidate claim price p(x,t) is C2,1 before maturity, with enough growth control for the hedge to be admissible. The terminal condition is p(x,T)=f(x).
The Itô formula gives the claim's price change
dp(St​,t)=(pt​+μSt​px​+21​σ2St2​pxx​)dt+σSt​px​dWt​.
(34)
A self-financing portfolio worth p with Δ shares has bank-account value p−ΔSt​. Its gain is
ΔdSt​+ρ(p−ΔSt​)dt={μΔSt​+ρ(p−ΔSt​)}dt+σΔSt​dWt​.
(35)
To replicate the claim, the Brownian coefficients must agree, forcing the delta hedge Δ=px​. Equating the remaining drift coefficients cancels the physical drift μ and yields
pt​+ρxpx​+21​σ2x2pxx​−ρp=0,p(x,T)=f(x).​
(36)
The cancellation explains why the Black-Scholes equation contains the interest rate, not the physical stock drift. Conversely, if an admissible solution of this equation is given, holdings px​ in the stock and (p−xpx​)/Bt​ in the bank account have value p and gains exactly dp. They are therefore a self-financing portfolio replicating the terminal payoff. Absence of arbitrage forces the claim to have this price. Merely decomposing the current value into arbitrary holdings would not justify self-financing; the gain identity and delta hedge are essential.
Now suppose the claim holder receives the cash rate k(St​,t) before T. The price p is the ex-dividend value, so its total gain is dp+kdt. The replicating portfolio pays the same amounts out of its wealth, with no external injections. Thus its gain identity is
dp+k(St​,t)dt=ΔdSt​+ρ(p−ΔSt​)dt.
(37)
Again the Brownian coefficients force Δ=px​. Comparing drifts now gives the Black-Scholes equation with claim dividends:
pt​+ρxpx​+21​σ2x2pxx​−ρp+k(x,t)=0,p(x,T)=f(x).​
(38)
In particular the cash-flow source has a positive sign in the left-hand side. It is not an underlying-stock dividend yield and does not replace the drift ρx by a dividend-adjusted drift.
For another justification, under the risk-neutral measure Q, discount the claim's cum-dividend gain. The Itô formula shows that
d(e−ρtp(St​,t)+∫0t​e−ρuk(Su​,u)du)=e−ρtσSt​px​dWtQ​
(39)
exactly when the displayed equation holds. Subject to the usual integrability making this a true martingale, conditional expectation at maturity gives
p(x,t)=EQ​[e−ρ(T−t)f(ST​)+∫tT​e−ρ(u−t)k(Su​,u)du​St​=x].
(40)
Positive interim payments therefore add positive value, which also checks the source sign. For instance, a constant payment rate k0​ and zero terminal payoff have price k0​(1−e−ρ(T−t))/ρ, interpreted as k0​(T−t) at ρ=0; substitution satisfies pt​−ρp+k0​=0.

Let T=t0​, τ=T−t, x=St​>0, and σ>0. Use a dividend-free Black-Scholes model with constant interest rate ρ. The terminal payoff is assumed independent of the parameters being varied. In addition to twice differentiability, impose enough integrability to differentiate the pricing formula; for example, f,f′,f′′ of polynomial growth suffice. Twice differentiability alone is not enough: the smooth payoff f(x)=ex2 has infinite expectation under every nondegenerate lognormal distribution.
Under the risk-neutral measure Q, future stock values conditional on St​=x have the form
ST​=xM,M=exp((ρ−σ2/2)τ+στ​Z),Z∼N(0,1).
(41)
Claim replication, or equivalently the martingale property of discounted value in the complete market, gives
p(x,t)=e−ρτE[f(xM)]=2π​e−ρτ​∫R​f(xe(ρ−σ2/2)τ+στ​z)e−z2/2dz.​
(42)
The replicating strategy holds the option delta px​ shares and bank-account value β=p−xpx​. No physical drift parameter appears: the Girsanov theorem replaces the physical drift by ρ under the pricing measure, and the replication argument cancels it directly. The risk-neutral pricing operator is linear in the payoff and preserves pointwise payoff inequalities.
Here is a verification of the Black-Scholes equation using the integral itself. Differentiation in spot gives
px​=e−ρτE[Mf′(xM)],pxx​=e−ρτE[M2f′′(xM)].
(43)
Differentiating with respect to τ gives
pτ​=−ρp+e−ρτE[ST​f′(ST​)(ρ−2σ2​+2τ​σZ​)].
(44)
For G(Z)=ST​f′(ST​), the Gaussian integration by parts identity E[ZG]=E[G′] yields
E[ZST​f′(ST​)]=στ​E[ST​f′(ST​)+ST2​f′′(ST​)].
(45)
Substitution cancels the −σ2/2 term and proves
pτ​=−ρp+ρxpx​+21​σ2x2pxx​.
(46)
Since pt​=−pτ​, this is precisely the Black-Scholes equation. As τ↓0, M→1 and growth control permits passage to the limit, giving p(x,T)=f(x). The delta hedge then supplies the admissible replicating gains under the same regularity assumptions.
The Black-Scholes parameter sensitivities describe the dependence on the model parameters without assuming a particular payoff. If f is increasing, then px​≥0. If f is convex, then pxx​≥0, so its price is convex in spot. The option gamma pxx​ measures the response of the option delta to spot changes.
For the interest rate, the distribution of the future stock also changes with ρ, so looking only at discounting gives the wrong general answer. Differentiation gives the option rho
pρ​=−τp+τe−ρτE[ST​f′(ST​)]=τ(xpx​−p)=−τβ.​
(47)
Thus a short bank-account position gives positive rate sensitivity, while a positive bank-account position gives negative rate sensitivity. For a cash payoff the result is negative; for the payoff f(x)=x it is zero.
For spot volatility, differentiate the lognormal multiplier and then use Gaussian integration by parts:
pσ​​=e−ρτE[ST​f′(ST​)(τ​Z−στ)]=στe−ρτE[ST2​f′′(ST​)]=στx2pxx​​.​
(48)
Consequently a convex payoff has nonnegative option vega. This monotonicity can also be understood by a mean-preserving increase of dispersion in the discounted stock. Strict positivity requires nonzero curvature seen with positive probability; an affine payoff has zero option vega.
Time enters through the remaining maturity τ. Holding spot fixed, the Black-Scholes equation gives
pt​=ρβ−21​σ2x2pxx​,pT​=−pt​.​
(49)
Under the conventional assumption ρ≥0, a convex payoff and a short bond position β<0 imply pt​≤0. The inequality is strict if ρ>0, or if pxx​>0 with positive volatility. Thus the price at fixed stock value is nonincreasing in calendar time, and nondecreasing in maturity, under these hypotheses. It is not a claim that the realized stochastic price path decreases: that path still has Brownian exposure σSt​px​dWtQ​.
The printed conclusion needs the nonnegative-rate qualification; it is false for arbitrary negative rates. For K>0, the twice-differentiable convex payoff f(x)=x−K has
p=x−Ke−ρτ,β=−Ke−ρτ<0,pt​=−ρKe−ρτ>0if ρ<0.
(50)
This is an exact counterexample, not a failure of the pricing formula. Also, with ρ=0 and an affine payoff, “decreasing” can only mean nonincreasing. Without payoff monotonicity or the specified bond-position sign, neither the spot nor rate nor time sensitivity has a universal sign. Any additional payoff parameter, such as a strike, is differentiated through f; pointwise payoff ordering, rather than a generic parameter rule, determines its price effect.

An interest rate model must describe a whole term structure of interest rates, not just one future scalar rate. Let P(t,T) be the time-t price of a unit zero-coupon bond maturing at T. For a sufficiently regular maturity curve, define the instantaneous forward rate and short rate by
f(t,T)=−∂T​logP(t,T),rt​=f(t,t).
(51)
Since P(t,t)=1, integration gives
P(t,T)=exp(−∫tT​f(t,u)du),Bt​=exp(∫0t​rs​ds).
(52)
Here B is the continuous-time bank account. Thus modelling the two-parameter field f(t,T) determines both the bonds and the discounting account. A Gaussian random field means that every finite collection of its values is jointly Gaussian; merely making each individual forward rate normally distributed is insufficient.
A useful arbitrage-free construction works under a money-market risk-neutral measure Q. Take independent Brownian motions Wj, deterministic coefficient sequences σj​(t,T), and a deterministic initial forward curve f(0,T). With the coefficients viewed as vectors in a separable Hilbert space H, write
df(t,T)=α(t,T)dt+⟨σ(t,T),dWt​⟩H​,0≤t≤T.
(53)
For infinitely many factors, the last term means the L2 limit of ∑j​σj​(t,T)dWtj​, not an assumption that the infinite vector of Brownian motions is itself H-valued. Require ∫0t​∥σ(s,T)∥H2​ds<∞ and the maturity/time regularity needed for stochastic integration and exchange of integrals. Deterministic drift and volatility make this a Gaussian forward-rate field.
The field covariance is determined by
ct​(T,U)=⟨σ(t,T),σ(t,U)⟩H​,Cov(f(t,T),f(s,U))=∫0min(t,s)​cv​(T,U)dv.
(54)
For every finite set of maturities, (ct​(Ti​,Tj​)) must be positive semidefinite. A one-factor model has rank-one instantaneous covariance, while additional or infinitely many factors permit richer correlations along the yield curve. Equivalently one can drive the curve with a maturity-correlated Brownian field Zt​(T) satisfying d⟨Z(T),Z(U)⟩t​=c(T,U)dt. A Brownian sheet gives the example
Cov(Zt​(T),Zs​(U))=min(t,s)min(T,U).
(55)
Multiplication by deterministic maturity-dependent amplitudes, or deterministic increasing changes of the two sheet coordinates, gives further Gaussian covariance structures. A covariance specification alone is not sufficient: the forward drift must also obey absence of arbitrage.
To derive that drift restriction, put
Σ(t,T)=∫tT​σ(t,u)du.
(56)
Differentiate the moving lower endpoint in logP(t,T)=−∫tT​f(t,u)du to obtain
dlogP(t,T)=(rt​−∫tT​α(t,u)du)dt−⟨Σ(t,T),dWt​⟩H​.
(57)
The Itô formula therefore gives
P(t,T)dP(t,T)​=(rt​−∫tT​α(t,u)du+21​∥Σ(t,T)∥H2​)dt−⟨Σ(t,T),dWt​⟩H​.
(58)
Discounted bond prices must be martingales under Q. Their drift vanishes precisely when ∫tT​α(t,u)du=21​∥Σ(t,T)∥H2​. Differentiating in maturity gives the Heath-Jarrow-Morton model restriction
α(t,T)=⟨σ(t,T),Σ(t,T)⟩H​=∫tT​ct​(T,u)du.​
(59)
For scalar amplitudes v(t,T) multiplying a field with covariance kernel c(T,U), this becomes α(t,T)=v(t,T)∫tT​v(t,u)c(T,u)du. The extra maturity correlation factor cannot in general be dropped.
The complete Gaussian specification is consequently
f(t,T)=f(0,T)+∫0t​∫sT​cs​(T,u)duds+∫0t​⟨σ(s,T),dWs​⟩H​.
(60)
Its mean and covariance follow immediately from deterministic integration and the Itô isometry. The initial yield curve is an input, so fitting initial bond prices requires choosing f(0,T) rather than changing a few scalar parameters. With the displayed drift, the discounted bond obeys
P(t,T)/Bt​d(P(t,T)/Bt​)​=−⟨Σ(t,T),dWt​⟩H​.
(61)
On finite horizons, deterministic square-integrable bond volatility gives an expectation-one stochastic exponential, hence a true discounted bond price martingale. This supplies an arbitrage-free pricing model for admissible portfolios in any finite collection of these bonds. Under a physical measure, a market price of risk vector changes the drift: if dWtQ​=dWtP​+λt​dt, then the physical forward drift is α(t,T)+⟨σ(t,T),λt​⟩H​. Historical mean changes must therefore not be inserted in place of the risk-neutral drift restriction. Gaussianity under both measures additionally requires suitable deterministic drift changes; state-dependent risk premiums need not preserve Gaussianity under the physical measure.
There is also a useful general consistency test for a proposed Gaussian field beyond this independent-increment construction. Define
LtT​=∫0t​f(s,s)ds+∫tT​f(t,u)du,Bt−1​P(t,T)=e−LtT​.
(62)
In the filtration generated by a Gaussian field, conditional distributions of such linear functionals are Gaussian. For s≤t≤T, the martingale requirement is therefore exactly
EQ​[LtT​∣Fs​]−21​VarQ​(LtT​∣Fs​)=LsT​.​
(63)
This follows by taking the conditional exponential moment and equating it to e−LsT​. It constrains both the deterministic mean and the covariance-induced conditional mean. Matching only unconditional discounted bond expectations does not suffice. For the Brownian-field construction, the Heath-Jarrow-Morton model restriction realizes this consistency condition through local drift cancellation.
Gaussian structure makes risk-neutral pricing particularly tractable. As a finite-factor example, take the Vasicek model under Q:
drt​=a(b−rt​)dt+ηdWtQ​,a>0.
(64)
Solving this Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process gives, for u≥t,
ru​=b+(rt​−b)e−a(u−t)+η∫tu​e−a(u−v)dWvQ​.
(65)
Hence, with τ=T−t and B(τ)=(1−e−aτ)/a, the conditional Gaussian integral I=∫tT​ru​du has
E[I∣Ft​]=bτ+(rt​−b)B(τ),Var(I∣Ft​)=a2η2​[τ−2B(τ)+2a1−e−2aτ​].
(66)
The exponential moment identity for a normal variable now gives the zero-coupon bond price P(t,T)=EQ​[e−I∣Ft​]=A(τ)e−B(τ)rt​, where
logA(τ)=(b−2a2η2​)(B(τ)−τ)−4aη2​B(τ)2.
(67)
This outlines the derivation of the familiar exponential-affine form without assuming a deterministic future discount rate. More general forward-field models need not reduce to a finite-dimensional Markov short-rate process.
For another application, a call at time T on a unit maturity-U bond, with t<T<U and strike K>0, is priced under the T-forward measure, using P(t,T) as numéraire. The bond-price ratio Fs​=P(s,U)/P(s,T) is a martingale under that measure and has deterministic volatility −(Σ(s,U)−Σ(s,T)). It is consequently lognormal, with integrated log-variance
V=∫tT​∥Σ(s,U)−Σ(s,T)∥H2​ds.
(68)
Integrating the lognormal payoff gives the Gaussian bond-option formula
Ct​=P(t,U)Φ(d1​)−KP(t,T)Φ(d2​),d1​=V​log(P(t,U)/(KP(t,T)))+V/2​,d2​=d1​−V​.​
(69)
At V=0 use (P(t,U)−KP(t,T))+. The same idea prices other suitable Gaussian-rate derivatives through forward measures and normal exponential moments.
The attractions are exact initial-curve fitting, explicit covariance control across maturities, and tractable bond and option prices. The limitations are also structural: Gaussian short rates and instantaneous forward rates generally can be negative; finite-factor specifications restrict maturity correlations; and deterministic volatilities cannot reproduce arbitrary stochastic changes in rate dispersion. An infinite-factor model allows richer covariance but typically leaves risks that cannot be spanned by finitely many traded bonds. Thus Gaussianity is a useful modelling choice, not by itself a guarantee of positivity, market completeness, or absence of arbitrage.
