First scale the stated unit-ball approximation. For every nonzero residual r∈F, apply the hypothesis to r/∥r∥ and multiply the resulting vector by ∥r∥. This gives v∈E with
∥v∥≤R∥r∥,∥Tv−r∥≤k∥r∥.
(1)
For zero residual use v=0. Start with r0​=y, choose vj​ by this rule for rj​, and set rj+1​=rj​−Tvj​. Inductively,
∥rj​∥≤kj∥y∥,∥vj​∥≤Rkj∥y∥.
(2)
The Banach space E therefore contains the sum x=∑j≥0​vj​, with
∥x∥≤1−kR​∥y∥.​
(3)
The partial sums satisfy T∑j=0n−1​vj​=y−rn​. Boundedness of T gives convergence of the left side to Tx, while rn​→0 gives Tx=y. Thus T is surjective with the stated lifting bound. This geometric correction for approximate surjectivity used completeness of E, not any unproved completeness of F.
Put C=R/(1−k). Given a Cauchy sequence (yn​) in F, choose a subsequence (ynj​​) such that ∥ynj+1​​−ynj​​∥≤2−j for j≥1. Lift each difference to uj​∈E with Tuj​=ynj+1​​−ynj​​ and ∥uj​∥≤C2−j. Lift yn1​​ to u0​. The sum u=u0​+∑j≥1​uj​ exists in E, and its image is the limit of the subsequence. A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges to the same limit: use the triangle inequality between an arbitrary late term, a later subsequence term, and the limit. This proves F is complete, the completeness forced by uniformly bounded lifting principle.
The open mapping theorem states that a bounded surjective linear operator between Banach spaces maps open sets to open sets. In particular a bounded linear bijection between Banach spaces has a bounded inverse. To deduce the closed graph theorem, let S:E1​→F1​ be an everywhere-defined linear map between Banach spaces with closed graph. Its graph is a closed subspace of the Banach product E1​×F1​, with norm ∥(x,y)∥=∥x∥+∥y∥, so it is itself Banach. The projection
π1​:graphS→E1​,(x,Sx)↦x
(4)
is a bounded linear bijection. The open mapping theorem makes its inverse bounded. Composing that inverse with the bounded second projection proves that S is bounded. This is the required closed graph conclusion, rather than a continuity assumption on S.
Finally consider the identity map I:(C(X),∥⋅∥)→(C(X),∥⋅∥∞​). If fn​→f in the new norm and fn​→g uniformly, each assumed continuous point evaluation functional gives fn​(x)→f(x), while uniform convergence gives fn​(x)→g(x). Hence f=g. Since both spaces are metric, this sequential argument proves the graph is closed. Both norms are complete, so the closed graph theorem makes I bounded. Its inverse is bounded by the open mapping theorem. Consequently there are constants c,C′>0 such that
c∥f∥∞​≤∥f∥≤C′∥f∥∞​(f∈C(X)).​
(5)
This proves Banach norm rigidity from continuous point evaluations. Pointwise continuity alone would not give a common bound on all evaluations; completeness supplies that through the closed graph argument.

A point x of a convex set K is an extreme point if x=(1−t)a+tb with a,b∈K and 0<t<1 forces a=b=x. It is enough to test midpoints: a nontrivial interior point of a segment is the midpoint of a smaller nontrivial segment.
Here is a proof of the Krein-Milman theorem. The empty-set case is immediate, so take K=∅. A face of a convex set is a convex subset F⊆K such that an interior convex combination lying in F has both endpoints in F. Consider nonempty compact faces. They include K. Every inclusion chain has nonempty intersection, because its closed subsets of the compact set K have the finite intersection property. The intersection remains a compact face. The Zorn lemma, ordered by reverse inclusion, therefore supplies a minimal nonempty compact face F.
If F contains two distinct points, the Hausdorff locally convex hypotheses and the allowed separation theorem supply a continuous real linear functional ℓ taking different values there. Its maximizer set in F is nonempty and compact. It is convex, and if a convex combination attains the maximum, both endpoint values attain it too; hence it is a face of F. A face of a face is a face of K, by applying the defining endpoint property twice. This maximizer face is proper because ℓ is nonconstant, contradicting minimality. Thus F is a singleton, whose point is extreme in K. The same minimal compact face argument inside any nonempty compact face shows that each such face contains an extreme point of K.
Let H=conv(extK). Since K is compact, hence closed in the Hausdorff space, and convex, H⊆K. It is nonempty, closed and convex. If p∈K∖H, the allowed strict separation theorem supplies a continuous linear functional with
ℓ(p)>suph∈H​ℓ(h).
(6)
The maximizer face of ℓ on K contains an extreme point e by the preceding argument. Then e∈H but ℓ(e)=maxK​ℓ≥ℓ(p)>supH​ℓ, a contradiction. Therefore
K=conv(extK).​
(7)
All closures refer to the given locally convex topology, and compactness is what justified both maxima and chain intersections.
For the real l-infinity sequence space, if ∣xj​∣<1 for any coordinate, choose 0<ϵ≤1−∣xj​∣. The two distinct sequences x±ϵej​ belong to the closed unit ball and have midpoint x, so x is not extreme. Conversely, if every xj​ is one or minus one, writing x=(1−t)a+tb for unit-ball sequences forces aj​=bj​=xj​ at every coordinate, because an endpoint of [−1,1] cannot be a nontrivial convex average of its points. Thus
extBℓ∞​={−1,1}N.​
(8)
For the space of sequences converging to zero, every unit-ball sequence has some coordinate with ∣xj​∣<1, since its coordinates tend to zero. The same opposite single-coordinate perturbations still belong to c0​, giving
extBc0​​=∅.​
(9)
These are extreme points of real sequence-space unit balls. There is no conflict with Krein-Milman: the norm-closed unit ball of c0​ is not compact in the norm topology, as the coordinate vectors have mutual distance one.

Work with a nonzero complex unital Banach algebra, so 1=0. The spectrum of an element is
σA​(x)={λ∈C:λ1−x is not invertible in A}.
(10)
For ∣λ∣>∥x∥, the Neumann series
(λ1−x)−1=∑n=0∞​λn+1xn​
(11)
converges in norm and is a two-sided inverse, by multiplying its partial sums and letting the remainder tend to zero. Thus the spectrum lies in ∣λ∣≤∥x∥. The assumed openness of the invertible group makes its complement closed, so the spectrum is compact.
To prove nonemptiness, suppose every λ has an inverse R(λ)=(λ1−x)−1. Locally,
R(λ+h)=R(λ)(1+hR(λ))−1=∑j≥0​(−h)jR(λ)j+1,
(12)
so the resolvent is holomorphic. At infinity its Neumann series gives ∥R(λ)∥=O(∣λ∣−1). For any bounded linear functional ℓ∈A∗, the scalar entire function ℓ(R(λ)) is bounded: it is bounded on compact disks by continuity, and tends to zero outside them. The Liouville theorem makes it constant, and its limit makes that constant zero. The Hahn-Banach theorem separates points of the normed space A, so R(λ)=0, contradicting (λ1−x)R(λ)=1. Hence the spectrum is nonempty and compact.
On the algebra of entire functions, set
∥f∥D​=∣z∣≤1sup​∣f(z)∣.​
(13)
It is finite, homogeneous and satisfies the triangle inequality and submultiplicativity. If it vanishes, the identity theorem makes f identically zero, so it is an algebra norm. However, no algebra norm on the entire-function algebra can be complete. The coordinate function Z(z)=z satisfies σ(Z)=C algebraically: Z−λ vanishes at z=λ and has no entire multiplicative inverse. A complete algebra norm would make this a Banach algebra, contradicting the proved boundedness of its spectrum. This is the entire function algebra admits no complete algebra norm obstruction; it applies to every proposed complete algebra norm, not only the displayed one.
For all continuous functions on C, choose continuous cutoffs
hn​(z)=⎩⎨⎧​1,2(1−∣z−3n∣),0,​∣z−3n∣≤1/2,1/2<∣z−3n∣<1,∣z−3n∣≥1.​
(14)
Their supports are disjoint and escape every compact set. Thus f(z)=∑n≥1​nhn​(z) is a locally finite sum and is continuous. Let gn​(z)=max(0,1−4∣z−3n∣). This nonzero continuous function is supported where hn​=1 and all the other cutoffs vanish. Hence fgn​=ngn​. Any algebra norm would imply
n∥gn​∥=∥fgn​∥≤∥f∥∥gn​∥,
(15)
so n≤∥f∥ for every positive integer, impossible. Therefore the algebra of all continuous functions on C admits no algebra norm, even an incomplete one. This proves the continuous functions on the complex plane admit no algebra norm obstruction directly.

Write K=σA​(x) and Rx​(ζ)=(ζ1−x)−1. Suppose first that a unital complex-algebra homomorphism sends Z to x. If λ∈/U, the holomorphic function 1/(Z−λ) belongs to O(U) and is the multiplicative inverse of Z−λ. Its image is an inverse for x−λ1. Thus existence forces K⊂U, even before continuity is used.
For the converse, assume K⊂U. Choose a bounded finite union of polygonal regions V with K⊂V, V⊂U, and boundary avoiding K. Orient its boundary cycle Γ positively around V, including negative orientations on holes. Its winding number is one near K and zero outside U. Define
Θx​(f)=2πi1​∫Γ​f(ζ)Rx​(ζ)dζ.​
(26)
The integral exists in A because the integrand is continuous on finitely many compact contour pieces. The Cauchy theorem shows it is unchanged by replacing Γ with another such spectral cycle: the difference has zero winding around the possible singularities in K. Banach-valued Cauchy statements follow by applying bounded linear functionals. We use the scalar Cauchy integral formula in its cycle form: a cycle with index one at a point and surrounding only a holomorphy region integrates f(ζ)/(ζ−z) to 2πif(z).
Linearity is immediate and, for a fixed cycle,
∥Θx​(f)∥≤2πlengthΓ​maxΓ​∥Rx​(ζ)∥supΓ​∣f∣.
(27)
This is a bound by one compact-uniform seminorm, proving continuity for local uniform convergence. To obtain Θx​(1)=1, deform the resolvent integral, which is holomorphic on C∖K, to a large circle and integrate its Neumann series. Similarly ζRx​(ζ)=1+xRx​(ζ) gives Θx​(Z)=x.
For multiplicativity choose an outer spectral cycle for f and an inner one for g, nested in U so the inner cycle lies inside the outer region. The resolvent identity is
Rx​(ζ)Rx​(η)=ζ−ηRx​(η)−Rx​(ζ)​.
(28)
Substitute it into the double integral for Θx​(f)Θx​(g). In the term containing Rx​(η), first integrate f(ζ)/(ζ−η) around the outer cycle, obtaining f(η). In the term containing Rx​(ζ), first integrate g(η)/(ζ−η) around the inner cycle, obtaining zero because ζ lies outside it. Interchange is valid for the continuous integrands on disjoint compact cycles. Hence
Θx​(f)Θx​(g)=Θx​(fg).
(29)
This proves the required continuous unital homomorphism, the holomorphic functional calculus.
For uniqueness use the following version of the allowed Runge theorem: on any open U⊆C, rational functions with all finite poles outside U approximate each holomorphic function locally uniformly; poles at infinity are allowed, giving polynomials. A unital homomorphism sending Z to x is forced on polynomials and on every inverse (Z−λ)−1, λ∈/U, and hence on these rational functions. Continuity then forces its value on their limits. Thus Θx​ is unique. This argument works for disconnected U as well and does not assume polynomials alone are dense. It proves continuity and uniqueness of holomorphic functional calculus.
We will use the character description of spectra, with a short justification. A character of an algebra φ is a nonzero complex multiplicative linear functional; it satisfies φ(1)=1. Applying it to an inverse shows φ(a)∈σA​(a), so ∣φ(a)∣≤∥a∥ and the character is automatically continuous. Conversely, for noninvertible a−λ1, its principal ideal is proper because A is commutative. Put it in a maximal ideal M. The closure of M is proper: an ideal containing an element sufficiently close to one contains an invertible element and thus the identity. Maximality makes M closed. The quotient is a complex Banach division algebra. By the nonempty-spectrum result of Question 3, each quotient element differs from some scalar by a noninvertible element, which in a division algebra must be zero. Thus the quotient is C and its quotient map is a character taking a to λ. This proves
σA​(a)={φ(a):φ a character of A}.
(30)
It is the spectrum equals character values in a commutative Banach algebra statement and does not assume semisimplicity.
For any character, continuity permits passing it inside the contour integral. Since φ(Rx​(ζ))=(ζ−φ(x))−1 and φ(x)∈K, the scalar Cauchy formula gives
φ(Θx​(f))=2πi1​∫Γ​ζ−φ(x)f(ζ)​dζ=f(φ(x)).​
(31)
Apply the character description first to Θx​(f) and then to x to obtain the holomorphic spectral mapping theorem
σA​(Θx​(f))=f(σA​(x)).​
(32)
On Π+​, use the analytic logarithm with argument in (−π/2,π/2) and set s(z)=exp(21​Logz). It satisfies s(z)2=z and takes its values in Π+​. The principal square root in a commutative Banach algebra is
y=Θx​(s),y2=x,σA​(y)⊂Π+​.​
(33)
For uniqueness let v be another root with spectrum in Π+​. For each character, φ(y) and φ(v) are scalar roots of φ(x) with positive real part, so both equal s(φ(x)). Hence every character takes the nonzero value 2s(φ(x)) at y+v. The character description implies y+v is invertible. Commutativity gives (y−v)(y+v)=y2−v2=0, and multiplying by the inverse yields y=v. Equal character values alone would not imply equality in a possibly nonsemisimple algebra; the invertible sum is the essential extra step.

First the C-star identity and submultiplicativity give ∥a∥2=∥a∗a∥≤∥a∗∥∥a∥, hence ∥a∥≤∥a∗∥ when a=0. Applying this to a∗ gives equality, so the involution is isometric and continuous. Also ∥1∥=∥1∥2 and 1=0, yielding ∥1∥=1.
For a Normal element of a C-star algebra x, put b=x∗x, which is a self-adjoint C-star element. Normality gives (x∗)2x2=b2, and the C-star identity applied to x2 and to b gives
∥x2∥2=∥(x∗)2x2∥=∥b2∥=∥b∥2=∥x∥4.
(34)
Thus ∥x2∥=∥x∥2. Every power of x is normal because x commutes with x∗, so iteration yields
∥x2j∥=∥x∥2j(j≥0).
(35)
The spectral radius formula has a limit along all positive integers, and along this subsequence its root norms are exactly ∥x∥. Therefore
r(x)=∥x∥.​
(36)
This proves spectral radius norm equality for normal elements directly from the defining norm identity, rather than assuming a spectral representation in advance.

For the unheaded representation request, retain the question's standing unital hypothesis. Let K be the character space of an algebra of A, with its weak-star or Gelfand topology. The standard commutative Banach-algebra results allowed here give a nonempty compact Hausdorff character space and σA​(a)={φ(a):φ∈K}. In particular, character compactness follows from the Banach-Alaoglu theorem: the equations φ(1)=1 and φ(ab)=φ(a)φ(b) define a weak-star closed subset of the dual unit ball.
The Gelfand transform is the unital homomorphism
Γ:A→C(K),Γ(a)(φ)=φ(a).
(40)
Every element of the commutative C-star algebra is normal. Part i and the character spectrum formula therefore give
∥Γ(a)∥∞​=maxφ∈K​∣φ(a)∣=r(a)=∥a∥.
(41)
Thus Γ is an isometry, hence injective, and its range is closed because A is complete. Part ii gives Γ(a∗)=Γ(a)​. The range contains constants and separates points of K, since two distinct characters differ on some element of A. The complex Stone-Weierstrass theorem states that a unital, conjugation-closed subalgebra of C(K) separating points of a compact Hausdorff space is uniformly dense. Its application here, together with closedness of the range, yields
Γ:A⟶≅​C(K) is an isometric ∗-isomorphism.​
(42)
This proves the Commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem from the preceding parts and the stated approximation theorem.
The word “every” in the printed deduction must be read under the initial unital hypothesis. If nonunital commutative C-star algebras are included, the compact-space statement is false: the nonzero algebra c0​ has no multiplicative identity, whereas C(K) for nonempty compact K does. The general nonunital version is C0​(K) on a locally compact Hausdorff space, with vanishing at infinity. This qualification preserves the proved unital result.
For the final real spectrum criterion for a normal C-star element, the ambient algebra may be noncommutative. Let B be the norm-closed unital subalgebra generated by x and x∗. Normality makes these two generators commute. The involution preserves their polynomial algebra and is continuous, so B is a commutative C-star subalgebra.
We first show that x has the same spectrum in B as in A, without assuming spectral permanence. Put Ω=C∖σA​(x). A compact subset of the real axis has connected complement: upper and lower half-plane paths join around either end of a bounded interval containing it, and each omitted real point can be joined locally to the upper half-plane. Define
V={λ∈Ω:(λ1−x)−1∈B}.
(43)
This set is nonempty because the Neumann series puts the inverse in B for ∣λ∣>∥x∥. It is relatively open: from an inverse in B, the local inverse formula
R(λ+h)=R(λ)∑n≥0​[−hR(λ)]n
(44)
stays in B for small h. It is relatively closed by continuity of the ambient resolvent and norm-closedness of B. Connectedness gives V=Ω. Conversely an inverse in B is an inverse in A, so
σB​(x)=σA​(x)⊂R.
(45)
This is the connected-resolvent case of spectrum in a closed unital subalgebra.
Apply the just-proved commutative C-star representation to B. For every character ψ of B, ψ(x) lies in this real spectrum. Part ii makes ψ(x∗)=ψ(x)​=ψ(x). The Gelfand transform of B is injective by its isometry, so
x=x∗.​
(46)
For a nonunital ambient C-star algebra, perform the same argument in its standard C-star unitization, using the usual unitization spectrum.
Normality cannot be dropped. In M2​(C), take
N=(00​10​).
(47)
Since N2=0, its spectrum is {0}: for λ=0, (λI−N)−1=λ−1I+λ−2N, while N is not invertible. Yet N=N∗ and
NN∗=(10​00​)=(00​01​)=N∗N.
(48)
Thus a real spectrum alone does not force self-adjointness; normality is essential.
