There are two necessary qualifications to the literal formulation. The whole group G is itself a closed normal subgroup, so the claim about containment in the centre must concern proper closed normal subgroups. Also, the homomorphism conclusion needs a nontrivial target H: for example, SU(2)→{1} has surjective differential onto the zero Lie algebra, but its group kernel is all of SU(2) and no quotient by a finite subgroup is a one-point group. We prove the precise assertions, including these exceptional cases.
Write g=Lie(G). The centre z(g) is invariant under the Adjoint representation, so irreducibility makes it either zero or all of g. In the latter case g is Abelian. For connected G, the adjoint action is then trivial: the group is generated by exponentials, and AdexpX​=eadX=I. A nonzero trivial representation is irreducible only in dimension one. A connected compact one-dimensional Lie group is a circle, so G≅U(1).
For every other G, the Lie algebra of its centre is zero. The centre is a closed compact Lie subgroup; a zero-dimensional compact Lie group is finite. Let N be a closed normal subgroup. The closed-subgroup theorem identifies n=Lie(N) as an adjoint-invariant subspace of g, hence n=0 or n=g. If the latter holds, N contains an identity neighbourhood and is open, so connectedness forces N=G. If N is proper, it therefore has zero Lie algebra and is finite. For any a∈N, the continuous map g↦gag−1 takes connected G into the finite set N, so it is constant and equal to a. Thus
G≅U(1)⟹Z(G) is finite;N⊲G,N closed and proper⟹N⊆Z(G).​
(11)
Proper closed subgroups of the circle are finite as well, and of course central.
Now let dΦ be surjective. The submersion theorem makes Φ(G) contain an identity neighbourhood in H, so it is an open subgroup. Since H is connected, Φ(G)=H. Its group kernel F is closed and normal. If H is nontrivial, F is proper and the preceding argument gives a finite central group kernel; this also holds for G≅U(1), whose proper closed subgroups are finite. The Lie-group first isomorphism theorem gives
H≅G/F,F⊆Z(G) finite,H={1}.​
(12)
If H is trivial, the correct statement is instead H=G/G.
For the Spin groups, use the real Clifford algebra with v2=−∥v∥2. The group Spin(m) consists of products of an even number of unit vectors inside its invertible even part. Conjugation on the embedded Rm defines the standard double covering Spin(m)→SO(m) with group kernel {±1}. The Clifford relations show that a unit-vector reflection, with the usual twisted conjugation, is a hyperplane reflection; the even products give orientation-preserving transformations. The Cartan–Dieudonné theorem states that every orthogonal transformation is a product of reflections, proving surjectivity. For m≥3 this connected covering group is simply connected; Spin(2) is a circle.
Here is an explicit exterior-square construction of the requested six-dimensional map. Put E=C4 with its standard Hermitian form and volume e1​∧e2​∧e3​∧e4​. On Λ2E, write eij​=ei​∧ej​ and define an antilinear map J by
Je12​=e34​,Je13​=−e24​,Je14​=e23​,Je34​=e12​,Je24​=−e13​,Je23​=e14​.
(13)
It satisfies J2=1. Intrinsically, if u∧v=B(u,v)vol and the Hermitian inner product h is linear in its first variable, then B(u,Jv)=h(u,v). Because SU(4) preserves both B and h, it commutes with J. This is the real structure of the SU4 exterior square.
The fixed space W has the following real orthonormal basis, with all vectors divided by 2​:
e12​+e34​,i(e12​−e34​),e13​−e24​,i(e13​+e24​),e14​+e23​,i(e14​−e23​).
(14)
Thus W is six-dimensional over R, its complexification is Λ2E, and the exterior-square action restricts to an orthogonal representation on W. Its determinant is one because SU(4) is connected. We obtain R:SU(4)→SO(W)≅SO(6).
To compute the group kernel, diagonalize g∈SU(4) with eigenvalues z1​,…,z4​. If R(g)=I, its complexified exterior-square action is the identity, hence zi​zj​=1 for every i<j. Comparing pairs shows that all zi​ are equal, and their common value has square one. A unitary matrix with that single eigenvalue is scalar, so kerR={±I4​}. Conversely, those two scalar matrices plainly act trivially on the exterior square.
The differential of R is injective because its group kernel is finite. Both Lie algebras have dimension 15, so the differential is an isomorphism. Its image is consequently open in connected SO(6), hence all of SO(6). Therefore
SU(4)/{±I4​}≅SO(6).​
(15)
This also realizes SU(4) as Spin(6), since SU(4) is simply connected, by uniqueness of the connected simply connected covering group.
