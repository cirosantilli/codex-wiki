Use the Levi-Civita connection and the Riemann curvature tensor convention
R(X,Y)Z=∇X​∇Y​Z−∇Y​∇X​Z−∇[X,Y]​Z,KM​(T,E)=⟨R(E,T)T,E⟩
(1)
for orthonormal T,E. Thus the round sphere has positive sectional curvature. Work on a fixed compact parameter interval [a,b] inside the given open interval; this keeps all differentiations and endpoint evaluations well-defined. The base geodesic is regular and has constant speed, so a constant rescaling of t makes it unit-speed. Put T=∂t​ϕ, J=∂s​ϕ, Dt​=∇T​, Ds​=∇J​ and evaluate at s=0. The torsion-free connection gives Ds​T=Dt​J because the two parameter derivatives commute. The geodesic equation is Dt​T=0.
Differentiating the length of a curve once and twice, using metric compatibility, gives
L′(0)=∫ab​⟨Dt​J,T⟩dt=[⟨J,T⟩]ab​,
(2)
and
L′′(0)=∫ab​(∣Dt​J∣2−⟨Dt​J,T⟩2+⟨Ds​Dt​J,T⟩)dt.
(3)
Let A=Ds​J. The curvature commutator gives Ds​Dt​J=Dt​A+R(J,T)J, and the skew-adjoint curvature symmetry gives ⟨R(J,T)J,T⟩=−⟨R(J,T)T,J⟩. Integration by parts therefore proves the moving-endpoint second variation of Riemannian arc length:
L′′(0)=[⟨A,T⟩]ab​+∫ab​(∣Dt​J∣2−⟨Dt​J,T⟩2−⟨R(J,T)T,J⟩)dt.​
(4)
Equivalently, with W=J−⟨J,T⟩T, the integral is I(W,W), the Riemannian index form. The endpoints of this geodesic variation generally move, so the displayed boundary term must be retained. For a base speed c instead of one, the boundary and integral have an overall factor 1/c, and the subtracted square inside the integral is ⟨Dt​J,T⟩2/c2. Returning to the original parameter, every ruling has constant speed, so L[a,b]​(s)=(b−a)∣∂t​ϕ(0,s)∣ and the full open-interval length is 2δ∣∂t​ϕ(0,s)∣. Consequently
ds2d2​L(γs​)​s=0​=b−a2δ​L[a,b]′′​(0).
(5)
The preceding curvature formula, with the base-speed factor 1/c when necessary, therefore evaluates the original full length derivative using any compact subinterval. No extension to the missing endpoints is required.
Differentiating Dt​T=0 in s yields the Jacobi field equation
Dt2​J+R(J,T)T=0.
(6)
The same ruling curves are geodesics of the surface with its induced metric, because the tangential part of their ambient acceleration is zero. Write its second fundamental form as II and its induced Levi-Civita connection as DN. In particular II(T,T)=0. The scalar f=⟨J,T⟩ satisfies f′′=0, by the Jacobi field equation, so W=J−fT is a Jacobi field both intrinsically and in the ambient manifold. In a regular surface chart h=∣W∣>0, and E=W/h is the perpendicular unit tangent. Along a two-dimensional geodesic, DtN​E=0: its derivative is orthogonal to both E and the parallel field T. The intrinsic Jacobi field equation consequently gives
h′′=−KN​h.
(7)
For the ambient derivative, the Gauss formula gives Dt​W=h′E+hII(T,E). Applying the same norm differentiation used above to h=∣W∣ gives
hh′′=∣Dt​W∣2−(h′)2−⟨R(W,T)T,W⟩=h2(∥II(T,E)∥2−KM​(T,E)).
(8)
Comparing these identities proves the curvature comparison for a geodesically ruled surface:
KN​=KM​(T,E)−∥II(T,E)∥2≤KM​(Tp​N).​
(9)
This is also the Gauss equation in a curved ambient manifold with II(T,T)=0. It applies locally to each regular patch of the surface.
For strict inequality, take the Euclidean embedded submanifold parametrized by ϕ(t,s)=(t,s,ts). Each t-curve is a straight-line geodesic. Its first fundamental form has coefficients E0​=1+s2, F0​=st, G0​=1+t2; the normal (−s,−t,1)/1+t2+s2​ gives second fundamental form coefficients e=0, f0​=1/1+t2+s2​, g0​=0. Hence
KN​(t,s)=−(1+t2+s2)21​<0=KR3​.​
(10)
Thus equality can fail everywhere, although every ruling is an ambient geodesic.
The length differentiation requires a nonconstant base ruling; a regular parametrized surface patch guarantees this. If “image of a smooth map” is read literally without a rank assumption, a degenerate member can fail even to have a first length derivative. For example, in the flat cylinder R×S1, the map ϕ(t,s)=(st,eis) for ∣t∣<δ, ∣s∣<4π has geodesic rulings and a two-dimensional open image: every nonzero-s point has full-rank parametrization, and the sole zero-s image point also has a full-rank preimage at s=2π,t=0. Yet L(γs​)=2δ∣s∣ is not differentiable at zero. The variation formula therefore applies to nonconstant members, as usual. The curvature inequality still holds on the whole image: regular values are dense by Sard theorem, and both curvatures extend continuously to the remaining points.
