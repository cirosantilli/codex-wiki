We prove a weighted version of the Szemerédi regularity lemma, which is sufficient for the grid application. For disjoint nonempty vertex sets U,W in a graph, write d(U,W)=e(U,W)/(∣U∣∣W∣) for their edge density of a bipartite graph. Call the pair ε-regular if every U′⊆U, W′⊆W with ∣U′∣≥ε∣U∣ and ∣W′∣≥ε∣W∣ satisfies ∣d(U′,W′)−d(U,W)∣≤ε.
The weighted Szemerédi regularity lemma states that, for every 0<ε<1, there are integers M,v0​ such that every graph on v≥v0​ vertices has a set partition into at most M nonempty cells V1​,…,Vk​, each of size at most εv, for which
∑i=j(Vi​,Vj​) not ε-regular​​∣Vi​∣∣Vj​∣≤εv2.
(13)
The sum is ordered. The cells need not have equal sizes; their weights replace the usual unweighted count of bad pairs.
To prove it, start with k0​=⌈2/ε⌉ nearly equal cells, taking v≥k0​. Each has size at most v/k0​+1≤εv. Define the regularity energy
q(P)=v21​∑U,W∈P​∣U∣∣W∣d(U,W)2.
(14)
Here even diagonal rectangles use the mean adjacency indicator, counting ordered pairs of vertices. Thus 0≤q≤1. Under a refinement, the old rectangle density is the weighted mean of the new ones, and the refinement variance identity for regularity energy gives
∑a,b​∣Ua​∣∣Wb​∣d(Ua​,Wb​)2−∣U∣∣W∣d(U,W)2=∑a,b​∣Ua​∣∣Wb​∣(d(Ua​,Wb​)−d(U,W))2.
(15)
In particular refinement cannot decrease energy.
If the irregular-pair weight exceeds εv2, choose witness subsets U′⊆U, W′⊆W for every irregular unordered pair, and split each cell simultaneously by all its witnesses. There are at most k−1 such subsets in any one cell, so the new number of cells is at most k2k. In an irregular rectangle, its witness has relative area at least ε2 and density discrepancy greater than ε. Since the witness rectangle is now a union of refined rectangles, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to their weighted density discrepancies bounds the variance contribution from below by
∣U′∣∣W′∣∣d(U′,W′)−d(U,W)∣2>ε4∣U∣∣W∣.
(16)
Summing over both orientations of all irregular pairs raises q by more than ε5. Since q≤1, after at most ⌈ε−5⌉ rounds the required weight bound must hold. The finite recurrence k↦k2k, starting at k0​, supplies M(ε); all cells remain small because we only refine. This proves the stated weighted Szemerédi regularity lemma.
We next derive the triangle removal lemma from this version. First, three disjoint classes U,W,Z whose pairs are ε-regular pairs of density at least d have many triangles if ε≤d/2 and ε≤1/4. Fewer than ε∣U∣ vertices of U have fewer than (d−ε)∣W∣ neighbours in W: otherwise that set of vertices, paired with all of W, would violate regularity. The same holds for Z. For each of the at least (1−2ε)∣U∣ remaining vertices, both neighbour sets have relative size at least d−ε≥ε. Regularity of (W,Z) then supplies at least (d−ε) times the product of those two neighbour-set sizes edges. Thus the regular triangle counting lemma, also for unequal class sizes, gives
#triangles across U,W,Z≥(1−2ε)(d−ε)3∣U∣∣W∣∣Z∣≥16d3​∣U∣∣W∣∣Z∣.
(17)
For a desired deletion tolerance 0<η<1, take ε=η/100, d=η/4, and a weighted regularity partition with at most M=M(ε) cells. Put τ=ε/M. Delete edges inside cells, between irregular pairs, between regular pairs of density less than d, and incident to cells smaller than τv. The first two deletions each cost at most εv2/2. The sparse-pair deletion costs at most dv2/2. For the last deletion, small-cell deletion in weighted regularity removes at most Mτv=εv vertices and at most εv2 incident edges. The total is bounded by
(2ε+d/2)v2<ηv2.
(18)
A triangle surviving these deletions would lie in three distinct cells, all of size at least τv, with all their pairs regular and of density at least d. The original graph would then have at least d3τ3v3/16 triangles. Therefore, with
κ=32d3τ3​>0,
(19)
a graph on sufficiently many vertices with at most κv3 triangles can be made triangle-free by deleting fewer than ηv2 edges. This establishes the triangle removal lemma with every parameter depending only on η.
Now apply a tripartite graph encoding of a grid. Take separate vertex classes X=[N], Y=[N], Z={2,…,2N}. For each grid point (x,y) in the given set, put in the three edges
xy,x(x+y),y(x+y),
(20)
where their endpoints belong respectively to X×Y, X×Z, and Y×Z. Each point supplies a triangle. These ∣A∣ triangles are edge-disjoint triangles, since any one of the three edge types uniquely determines the point that supplied it.
Conversely, any triangle with vertices x∈X, y∈Y, z∈Z forces the three points
(x,y),(x,z−x),(z−y,y)
(21)
to belong to A. Put d=z−x−y. For d=0 these form a corner in an integer grid; for d=0 they all arise from the same grid point. Thus if there is no corner, the graph has exactly ∣A∣≤N2 triangles, yet destroying all triangles requires at least ∣A∣≥δN2 edge deletions.
We may suppose 0<δ≤1, since larger densities make the request vacuous. Use the triangle removal lemma with η=δ/32. Our graph has v=4N−1 vertices, so, for sufficiently large N, its bound N2 on the number of triangles is at most κv3. The lemma would delete fewer than
ηv2<32δ​(4N)2=2δ​N2
(22)
edges to destroy all triangles. This contradicts the lower bound δN2 from the edge-disjoint family. Hence every sufficiently large positive-density square grid contains a nondegenerate corner, proving the requested corners theorem and in particular the existence of a suitable N.
