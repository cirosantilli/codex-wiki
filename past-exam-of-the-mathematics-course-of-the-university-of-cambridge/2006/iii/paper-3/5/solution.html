Let K=kerθ. The subgroup correspondence for a surjective group homomorphism sends a subgroup B≤H to θ−1(B) and a subgroup A≤G containing K to θ(A). Inverse images are subgroups containing K, and images are subgroups. Surjectivity gives θ(θ−1(B))=B. Conversely, if K≤A and θ(g)∈θ(A), choose a∈A with θ(g)=θ(a). Then ga−1∈K≤A, so g∈A. Thus θ−1(θ(A))=A. These two assignments are inverse bijections.
For C=θ−1(B), the map of left coset sets
G/C⟶H/B,gC⟼θ(g)B
(34)
is well defined and onto. If θ(g1​)B=θ(g2​)B, then θ(g2−1​g1​)∈B, so g2−1​g1​∈C and g1​C=g2​C. Hence it is injective, proving
[G:θ−1(B)]=[H:B].​
(35)
No normality of B is required; these are coset sets, not necessarily quotient groups.
The abelianization is G=G/[G,G], where the commutator subgroup is generated by the commutators [x,y]=x−1y−1xy. Conjugating a commutator gives another commutator, so this subgroup is normal. In the quotient all commutators are trivial, and therefore the quotient is abelian. If N⊲G and G/N is abelian, every commutator lies in N, so [G,G]≤N. Hence
π:G→G/N,g[G,G]↦gN
(36)
is well defined and satisfies π=π∘q, where q:G→G is the natural map. It is uniquely determined because q is onto. This is the universal factorization property of abelianization.
Now write Si​ for the unique index-i subgroup of S. Since conjugation preserves index, as shown in part (ii), gSi​g−1=Si​ for every g∈S. Thus every Si​ is normal. For each prime p the quotient S/Sp​ has order p and is cyclic, so there is a surjection S→Cp​. Being an abelian quotient, it factors through S.
The abelianization is finitely generated because S is. By the Fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups,
S≅Zr⊕T,
(37)
where T is finite. If r=0, choose a prime p>∣T∣. The finite group T could not surject onto the order-p group Cp​, contradicting the preceding surjections. Hence r≥1, and projection from a free coordinate supplies
S↠Z.​
(38)
Every subgroup of finite index in S has an inverse image of the same index in S, so there cannot be two different subgroups of any given finite index in S. If r≥2, reduction modulo a prime p in the first and second free coordinates gives two distinct surjective maps to Cp​ with different kernels: the first basis vector lies in one kernel but not the other. Both kernels have index p, a contradiction. Therefore r=1.
If T=1, choose a prime p dividing its order. The decomposition of a finite abelian group into cyclic factors gives a surjection χ:T→Cp​. In Z⊕T, the maps
(n,t)⟼nmodp,(n,t)⟼χ(t)
(39)
have distinct index-p kernels, again distinguished by (1,0). This is another contradiction, so T=1. We have proved uniqueness of finite-index subgroups forces infinite cyclic abelianization:
S≅Z.​
(40)
A residually finite group is one in which every nonidentity element has nonidentity image under some homomorphism to a finite group. Equivalently, the intersection of all finite-index normal subgroups is trivial: a finite separating map has such a kernel, and conversely quotienting by such a normal subgroup gives a finite separating map.
Choose the surjection σ:S→S≅Z. For each positive integer i, the subgroup σ−1(iZ) has index i and hence equals Si​. Its kernel [S,S] is therefore contained in every finite-index subgroup. Indeed,
⋂i≥1​Si​=σ−1(⋂i≥1​iZ)=kerσ=[S,S].
(41)
If S is residually finite, this intersection is trivial. Thus [S,S]=1 and σ is an isomorphism. The residually finite group with one subgroup of each finite index is infinite cyclic conclusion is
S≅Z.​
(42)
Residual finiteness is used exactly to eliminate the commutator subgroup that all finite quotients would otherwise fail to detect.
