Write Dt​=∂t​+u⋅∇ for the material derivative. The resistive induction equation, together with the solenoidal conditions on both fields, is
Dt​B=(B⋅∇)u+ηΔB,∇⋅B=∇⋅u=0.
(1)
Since Dt​x=u, the product rule gives
Dt​P=x⋅Dt​B+B⋅u,B⋅∇Q=x⋅[(B⋅∇)u]+B⋅u.
(2)
Also ΔP=x⋅ΔB+2∇⋅B=x⋅ΔB. Substitution proves the radial magnetic induction scalar equation:
∂t​P+u⋅∇P=B⋅∇Q+ηΔP(r<a).​
(3)
In the insulating exterior the quasistatic magnetic field is current-free, so ∇×B=0 and ∇⋅B=0. Thus ΔB=0 and ΔP=0 for r>a, with decay at infinity for an isolated field. This is an instantaneous exterior Laplace equation, rather than a diffusion equation with the interior value of η.
Use the usual nonmagnetic-interface assumptions: equal magnetic permeability on both sides and no surface current. The electromagnetic boundary conditions then make Br​ and the tangential magnetic field continuous. Hence P=rBr​ is continuous. In spherical coordinates, the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint reads
∂r​P=−Br​−divS2​Btan​,
(4)
where the last term is the angular divergence on the unit sphere. Its right-hand side is also continuous, giving the insulating boundary condition for the radial magnetic scalar:
[P]r=a​=0,[∂r​P]r=a​=0.​
(5)
Equivalently, the decaying exterior degree-l spherical harmonic is proportional to r−(l+1), so its boundary amplitude satisfies ∂r​Plm​(a)=−(l+1)Plm​(a)/a.

Set N=∫V​P2dV, D=∫R3​∣∇P∣2dV, M=∫R3​∣B∣2dV, and q=maxV​∣Q∣. The confined flow satisfies the impermeability condition u⋅n=0, so Q=0 at r=a. Multiplying the radial magnetic induction scalar equation by P and integrating, the advection term vanishes by the divergence theorem:
∫V​Pu⋅∇PdV=21​∫∂V​P2u⋅ndS=0.
(6)
The same integration by parts, using ∇⋅B=0, gives
∫V​PB⋅∇QdV=∫∂V​PQB⋅ndS−∫V​QB⋅∇PdV=−∫V​QB⋅∇PdV.
(7)
For diffusion, Green's first identity in the sphere gives ∫V​PΔP=−∫V​∣∇P∣2+∫∂V​P∂r​P. In the exterior, ΔP=0 and the normal on its inner boundary is −r^, so
∫R3∖V​∣∇P∣2dV=−∫∂V​P∂r​PdS.
(8)
The infinity term vanishes for the isolated decaying field. The insulating boundary condition for the radial magnetic scalar permits matching the two traces, and therefore the exterior contribution is essential: it turns the diffusive term into −ηD. Altogether,
21​N′=−∫V​QB⋅∇PdV−ηD≤q∫R3​∣B⋅∇P∣dV−ηD.
(9)
Applying the pointwise Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and then its integral version,
∫R3​∣B⋅∇P∣≤∫R3​∣B∣∣∇P∣≤MD​,21​N′≤qMD​−ηD.
(10)
Thus a nonzero field with stationary or growing N must satisfy the radial-flow dynamo energy bound:
(Vmax​∣Q∣)2≥η2∫R3​∣B∣2dV∫R3​∣∇P∣2dV​.​
(11)
The temporal qualification matters. For general time-dependent dynamo action, nondecay does not make N′≥0 at every instant; the boxed bound applies at nondecreasing instants, to a marginal or growing mode, or in an appropriate long-time sense. A uniform strict violation, qM/D​≤η−ε with ε>0, gives N′≤−2εD. The whole-space Sobolev inequality and Holder inequality give N≤∣V∣2/3∥P∥62​≤Ca​D, forcing exponential decay of N. Alternatively, for a bounded statistically steady field, time averaging the energy balance and applying Cauchy-Schwarz once more gives q∗2​≥η2⟨D⟩/⟨M⟩, where q∗​=supt​q(t). Sustained dynamo action cannot have a uniform negative dissipation gap.

There is a minor operator misprint to resolve first. Interpreting (x⋅∇)2 as composition, the Cartesian formula for the angular Laplacian is
L2=−ΔS2​=−r2Δ+(x⋅∇)2+x⋅∇.
(12)
The last term is missing from the printed Cartesian expression. For example, its truncated expression maps x1​ to x1​, whereas a degree-one spherical harmonic must have angular eigenvalue two. In what follows, use the stated angular spectrum l(l+1) and the supplied poloidal-energy identity; these are consistent with the corrected operator.
The divergence theorem and the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint imply zero magnetic flux through every sphere. Consequently P=rBr​ has zero angular mean and no l=0 component. Expand in orthonormal spherical harmonics:
P(r,Ω)=∑l≥1​∑m=−ll​plm​(r)Ylm​(Ω),L−2P=∑l≥1,m​l(l+1)plm​(r)​Ylm​(Ω).
(13)
By angular orthogonality, with
Dlm​=∫0∞​[r2∣plm′​(r)∣2+l(l+1)∣plm​(r)∣2]dr≥0,
(14)
the gradient norm and the supplied poloidal-toroidal decomposition identity become
D=∑l≥1,m​Dlm​,MP​:=∫R3​∣BP​∣2dV=∑l≥1,m​l(l+1)Dlm​​.
(15)
Since l(l+1)≥2, this proves the poloidal magnetic energy bound by the radial scalar gradient:
MP​≤21​D.​
(16)
Equality holds when the radial scalar contains only degree-one angular components.
Define the poloidal and total magnetic energies by EP​=MP​/(2μ0​) and Etotal​=M/(2μ0​). Combining the radial-flow dynamo energy bound with D≥2MP​ gives the necessary condition
(ηmaxV​∣u⋅x∣​)2≥2Etotal​EP​​.​
(17)
It has the same temporal interpretation as the bound in the preceding part; statistically steady versions use the corresponding time-averaged energies. It quantifies why dynamo action with a substantial poloidal fraction requires radial motion, while a purely tangential flow has Q=0.

Let ⟨⋅⟩ denote the spatial average. Assume periodicity, or an equivalent homogeneous averaging limit in which total derivatives average to zero and integration by parts is valid. Fix the induced field to have zero mean. Expand the solution of the resistive induction equation in small magnetic Reynolds number:
b=Rm​b1​+Rm2​b2​+⋯,Δb1​=−(B⋅∇)u,Δb2​=−∇×(u×b1​).
(18)
The uniform test magnetic field commutes with differentiation. Since Δu=−u, the first equation has solution b1​=(B⋅∇)u.
The quadratic second-order forcing need not be monochromatic, so one must not simply replace its inverse Laplacian by multiplication by −1. Instead use the self-adjointness of the Laplacian under the average:
⟨u×b2​⟩=−⟨Δu×b2​⟩=−⟨u×Δb2​⟩=⟨u×∇×(u×b1​)⟩.
(19)
This proves the monochromatic small-Reynolds-number mean electromotive force expansion
E=Rm​⟨u×(B⋅∇)u⟩+Rm2​⟨u×∇×[u×(B⋅∇)u]⟩+O(Rm3​).​
(20)
For the symmetry of the first-order monochromatic alpha tensor, write
αij(1)​=ϵikl​⟨uk​∂j​ul​⟩.
(21)
Its antisymmetric part is determined by contraction with the Levi-Civita symbol. The contraction identity gives
ϵpij​αij(1)​=⟨uj​∂j​up​−up​∂j​uj​⟩.
(22)
The second term vanishes by incompressibility; the first is ⟨∂j​(uj​up​)⟩=0. Since every three-dimensional antisymmetric second-rank tensor is equivalent to its contracted axial vector, α(1) is symmetric. No Fourier series or Fourier transform is required.
To derive the next identity, set C=u×(B⋅∇)u. Expanding the curl and cross product in components,
(u×∇×C)i​=uj​∂i​Cj​−uj​∂j​Ci​.
(23)
The average of the second term is zero: integration by parts makes it −⟨Ci​∂j​uj​⟩. Integrating the first term by parts gives
Ei(2)​=−⟨(∂i​uj​)[u×(B⋅∇)u]j​⟩.​
(24)
Extracting the coefficient of Bj​ now yields
αij(2)​=−⟨∂i​u⋅(u×∂j​u)⟩=⟨u⋅(∂i​u×∂j​u)⟩.
(25)
Interchanging i and j reverses the cross product, proving the antisymmetry of the second-order monochromatic alpha tensor:
αij(1)​=αji(1)​,αij(2)​=−αji(2)​.​
(26)
The second coefficient of the alpha tensor can therefore contribute turbulent magnetic pumping, while its symmetric part vanishes at this order.

Let ρ0​ be the reference mass density, ν the kinematic viscosity, κ the thermal diffusivity, η the magnetic diffusivity, and αT​ the coefficient of thermal expansion. The conductive state of Rayleigh-Bénard convection is Tb​=T0​+ΔT(1−z/d). For the Boussinesq approximation, density variation is retained only in buoyancy. Linearizing about this state and the imposed magnetic field B0​y^​ gives
∂t​u∂t​θ∂t​b​=−ρ0​1​∇p∗​+μ0​ρ0​B0​​∂y​b+gαT​θz^+νΔu,=dΔT​w+κΔθ,=B0​∂y​u+ηΔb,∇⋅u=∇⋅b=0.​
(27)
Here w=uz​ and p∗​=p′+B0​by​/μ0​ is the perturbation of magnetohydrodynamic total pressure. The gradient part of the Lorentz force has been absorbed into p∗​; the remaining term is magnetic tension. The sign in the temperature equation follows from dTb​/dz=−ΔT/d.
Use the thermal-diffusion scaling of a convection layer: length d, time d2/κ, velocity κ/d, temperature perturbation ΔT, field perturbation B0​, and total-pressure perturbation ρ0​νκ/d2. Dropping dimensionless-variable decorations gives
σ−1∂t​u∂t​θ∇⋅u​=−∇p+ζQ∂y​b+Rθz^+Δu,=w+Δθ,∂t​b=∂y​u+ζΔb,=∇⋅b=0,​
(28)
with
R=νκgαT​ΔTd3​,Q=μ0​ρ0​νηB02​d2​,σ=κν​,ζ=κη​.​
(29)
These are respectively the Rayleigh number, Chandrasekhar number, Prandtl number, and magnetic-to-thermal diffusivity ratio. In particular, the magnetic coefficient is ζQ, not Q alone, in these thermal-time units. At z=0,1, the boundary conditions are θ=w=bz​=0, ∂z​ux​=∂z​uy​=0, and ∂z​bx​=∂z​by​=0.

Put ah2​=k2+m2>0 and Λ=ah2​+π2. The plane-layer poloidal-toroidal decomposition supplies the assumed poloidal fields as
u=∇×∇×(ϕz^),b=∇×∇×(χz^).
(30)
Since w=−Δh​ϕ and bz​=−Δh​χ, potentials proportional to est+ikx+imysinπz have the required vertical components. Their horizontal components are proportional to cosπz, satisfying the stress-free boundary conditions and the horizontal-field boundary conditions. The double-curl representation automatically enforces incompressibility and the solenoidal magnetic-field constraint.
Let W,Θ,C be the amplitudes of w,θ,bz​. The temperature and resistive induction equations immediately give
(s+Λ)Θ=W,(s+ζΛ)C=imW.
(31)
Eliminate the total-pressure perturbation by applying Δ to the vertical momentum equation and subtracting ∂z​ times its divergence. The resulting vertical equation is
Λ(s/σ+Λ)W=Rah2​Θ+ζQimΛC.
(32)
In particular, imC=−m2W/(s+ζΛ) shows explicitly that magnetic tension opposes the displacement.
Taking the determinant of these three amplitude equations gives the horizontal-field magnetoconvection dispersion relation:
Λ(s+Λ)[(s+σΛ)(s+ζΛ)+σζQm2]−σRah2​(s+ζΛ)=0.​
(33)
This cubic polynomial includes all roots of the assumed poloidal coupled system; using its determinant form avoids discarding possible roots by dividing by s+Λ or s+ζΛ. As a check, Qm2=0 factors off magnetic diffusion and leaves the standard stress-free Rayleigh-Bénard convection growth equation.

Set s=0 in the horizontal-field magnetoconvection dispersion relation. With positive σ,ζ and nonzero ah​, cancellation gives the stationary neutral curve
R=ah2​Λ3​+ah2​Qm2Λ​,Λ=ah2​+π2.
(34)
At fixed ah2​, the Chandrasekhar number contributes a nonnegative penalty proportional to m2. It can be eliminated by choosing m=0. It remains to minimize f(x)=(x+π2)3/x for x=k2>0:
f′(x)=x2(x+π2)2(2x−π2)​.
(35)
The function diverges at both endpoints and has its unique minimum at x=π2/2. Thus the magnetic-field-aligned rolls evade steady magnetic inhibition:
mc​=0,∣kc​∣=2​π​,Rc​=427π4​,​
(36)
independently of Q. If higher vertical sine modes are included, replacing π by nπ gives minimum 27n4π4/4, so the displayed first vertical mode also gives the smallest steady threshold.
Physically, these convection rolls have axes along the imposed magnetic field. Their perturbations are independent of y, so ∂y​u=0 in the resistive induction equation and no growing magnetic perturbation is generated. The flow does not bend the field; consequently magnetic tension cannot inhibit these rolls, however large the field strength. This conclusion concerns the requested steady disturbances.

For disturbances varying only along the imposed magnetic field, set k=0 and x=m2>0. The steady horizontal-field magnetoconvection dispersion relation becomes
R=m2(m2+π2)3​+Q(m2+π2).​
(37)
Expand it in powers of x:
R(x)=π2Q+(Q+3π2)x+x2+3π4+xπ6​.
(38)
Its derivative and second derivative are
R′(x)=Q+3π2+2x−x2π6​,R′′(x)=2+x32π6​>0.
(39)
Because R diverges as x→0 and x→∞, the unique minimum is determined by
x2(Q+3π2+2x)=π6.
(40)
For large Chandrasekhar number, this equation first gives x∼π3Q−1/2 and hence the requested strong-field steady magnetoconvection varying along the field selection law:
mc4​∼Qπ6​.​

x=π3Q−1/2[1−2Q3π2​+O(Q−3/2)].

Write cT2​=RT0​ for the squared isothermal sound speed, distinguishing the gas constant R from a Rayleigh number, and put H=cT2​/g. A uniform magnetic field has no bulk Lorentz force, so hydrostatic equilibrium and the ideal gas equation give
dzdpi​​=−ρi​g,pi​=cT2​ρi​,pi​(z)=pi​(0)e−z/H.
(44)
At the interface, it is magnetohydrodynamic total pressure, not gas pressure alone, that is continuous:
p1​(0)+2μ0​B12​​=p2​(0)+2μ0​B22​​.
(45)
Define ΔB​=B12​−B22​>0. The magnetic pressure discontinuity in an isothermal atmosphere therefore produces
ρ1​(z)ρ2​(z)​=ρe−z/H,=(ρ+2μ0​cT2​ΔB​​)e−z/H,​p1​(z)p2​(z)​=ρcT2​e−z/H(z<0),=(ρcT2​+2μ0​ΔB​​)e−z/H(z>0).​​
(46)
In particular, the upper gas is denser at the interface. Both halves have the same scale height H, despite their different pressure normalizations.
For the disturbance calculation, write ρ−​=ρ, ρ+​=ρ+δρ, where δρ=ΔB​/(2μ0​cT2​), and Σρ​=ρ−​+ρ+​. Work to leading order in the prescribed short-vertical-scale approximation: the bulk motion is locally incompressible potential flow, while the isothermal equation determines the equilibrium density jump. Its scale separation and small compressive correction will be checked at the maximizing mode.
Let the interface displacement be hest+ikx+imy and K=k2+m2​>0. Using displacement rather than velocity potentials, write ξi​=∇χi​ and ui​=sξi​. Incompressibility and irrotational flow give Δχi​=0. Decay away from the interface and continuity of its normal displacement fix
χ1​=Kh​eKz,χ2​=−Kh​e−Kz,
(47)
with the common horizontal and temporal exponential understood. Both satisfy ∂z​χi​(0)=h. Thus the disturbance has vertical extent of order K−1.
In ideal magnetohydrodynamics, magnetic flux freezing gives bi​=(Bi​⋅∇)ξi​ for these locally incompressible displacements. If πi​=pi′​+Bi​⋅bi​/μ0​ is the total-pressure perturbation, linear momentum is
ρi​s2ξi​=−∇πi​+μ0​1​(Bi​⋅∇)bi​.
(48)
Since (Bi​⋅∇)2=−(kh​⋅Bi​)2 on the mode, integration in each half-space yields
πi​=−[ρi​s2+μ0​(kh​⋅Bi​)2​]χi​,kh​=(k,m,0).
(49)
At the displaced interface, continuity of Lagrangian magnetohydrodynamic total pressure requires π1​−ρ−​gh=π2​−ρ+​gh. Inserting the two potentials gives the orthogonal-field magnetic Rayleigh-Taylor dispersion relation:
s2=Σρ​gδρk2+m2​−(B22​k2+B12​m2)/μ0​​.​
(50)
The first term is the destabilizing Rayleigh-Taylor instability of the heavier upper gas; the second is stabilizing magnetic tension. A negative right-hand side gives an oscillatory mode rather than growth.
For fixed K, the magnetic penalty is
B22​k2+B12​m2=B22​K2+ΔB​m2.
(51)
It is minimized by m=0: the wavevector is along the weaker upper magnetic field and perpendicular to the stronger lower field. If B2​=0, define A=gδρ/Σρ​ and C=B22​/(μ0​Σρ​). The growth law for this orientation is s2=AK−CK2, a concave quadratic with maximum at K∗​=A/(2C). Hence
m∗​=0,∣k∗​∣=K∗​=4B22​HΔB​​,smax​=2∣B2​∣Σρ​​gδρμ0​​​=4cT2​∣B2​∣μ0​Σρ​​gΔB​​.​
(52)
For this orientation the unstable interval is 0<K<2K∗​; the maximum lies halfway along it.
The localization condition for the fastest orthogonal-field interface mode follows directly from the vertical decay length:
ℓ∗​=K∗​1​=ΔB​4B22​​H,4B22​≪B12​−B22​.​
(53)
This is the condition for ℓ∗​≪H on both sides. It also controls the locally incompressible approximation, since
(cT​K∗​smax​​)2=μ0​Σρ​cT2​B22​​=2μ0​ρcT2​+ΔB​/2B22​​≤ΔB​2B22​​≪1.
(54)
Acoustic adjustment is then fast compared with growth, so bulk density perturbations are higher-order corrections. If this localization condition fails, the constant-background approximation cannot be used to identify the physical fastest mode.
Finally, the printed assumptions allow B2​=0. In that case m=0 eliminates magnetic tension on both sides and gives s2=gδρ∣k∣/Σρ​. There is no finite maximum growth rate or maximizing wavenumber in the ideal sharp-interface model: growth is unbounded as ∣k∣→∞. The finite maximum and localization formula above therefore require 0<∣B2​∣<∣B1​∣.
In the growth curves, θ is the angle of the horizontal wavevector from the x-axis. The illustrated ratio B12​/B22​=101 gives K∗​H=25, and shows how even a small rotation toward the stronger magnetic field lowers the maximum growth.
