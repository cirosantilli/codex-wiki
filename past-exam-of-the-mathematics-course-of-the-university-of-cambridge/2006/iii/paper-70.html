Write v=ux​ and D=∂t​+v∂x​. The Euler equations and conservation of mass give Dρ=−ρvx​ and ρDu=−px​ex​. The internal-energy density of the ideal gas is e=p/(γ−1). The adiabatic pressure equation therefore gives
∂t​e+∂x​(ve)=−p∂x​v.
(1)
Multiply the momentum equation by u and use continuity to obtain the kinetic-energy balance
∂t​(ρu2/2)+∂x​(vρu2/2)=−v∂x​p.
(2)
Adding the two equations moves ∂x​(pv) into the flux. Thus the entire system is ∂t​Q+∂x​F=0, where
Q=​ρρvρuy​ρuz​E​​,F=​ρvρv2+pρvuy​ρvuz​v(E+p)​​,E=21​ρ(v2+uy2​+uz2​)+γ−1p​.​
(3)
This derives the conservative energy flux of a polytropic ideal gas. In particular one-dimensional spatial dependence does not remove the transverse velocities from the total energy. The energy flux can equally be written ρv(u2/2+w) with specific enthalpy w=γp/[(γ−1)ρ].

Integrate any conservation equation from part (a) through a vanishingly thin control volume moving with shock speed s. Its singular terms require [F]=s[Q]. The stationary shock has s=0, so all five fluxes are continuous. Substitution gives the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas:
[ρv]=0,[ρv2+p]=0,[ρvuy​]=[ρvuz​]=0,[ρv(u2/2+w)]=0.​
(4)
Let the common mass flux be j=ρ1​v1​=ρ2​v2​>0. Dividing the transverse conditions by j proves uy2​=uy1​ and uz2​=uz1​. Dividing the energy condition by j shows that u2/2+w is unchanged. Thus tangential kinetic energies cancel when solving for the normal shock compression. No global equality of the entropy parameter p/ργ across the shock has been assumed: physical dissipative shocks increase that parameter.

Let U=∣u1​∣ and q=(γ−1)/(γ+1), the strong-shock limit of the normal velocity ratio. In the plane containing the upstream direction and the shock normal, take u1​=UeX​ and n=cosβeX​+sinβeY​. The normal velocity is multiplied by q while the tangential velocity is unchanged, hence
u2​=u1​−(1−q)(u1​⋅n)n,uX2​=U[1−(1−q)cos2β],uY2​=−U(1−q)cosβsinβ.
(11)
Eliminating β gives the strong-shock polar for a perfect gas:
uY22​=(U−uX2​)(uX2​−qU).​
(12)
Equivalently this is the circle
(uX2​−γ+1γU​)2+uY22​=(γ+1U​)2.
(13)
Both signs of the perpendicular component are possible by changing shock orientation. The endpoints uX2​=qU,U lie on the upstream axis. An original sketch for γ=5/3 illustrates the locus and a tangent ray:
The deflection is the angle of the ray from the origin to the downstream velocity, rather than the shock-normal angle β. Its maximum occurs where this ray is tangent to the circle. The circle's center is at distance γU/(γ+1) and its radius is U/(γ+1), so the tangent right triangle proves
δmax​=arcsin(1/γ).​
(14)
This is the maximum deflection through a strong perfect-gas shock; for γ=5/3 it is about 36.87 degrees.

a OurBigBook.com
Axisymmetry makes all cylindrical components independent of ϕ. The divergence-free condition is therefore ∂R​(RBR​)+∂z​(RBz​)=0. On a simply connected meridional patch introduce a streamfunction ψ so that
BR​=−R∂z​ψ​,Bz​=R∂R​ψ​.​
(15)
These definitions satisfy the solenoidal condition identically and represent any such poloidal field locally. Since ∇ϕ=eϕ​/R, they give Bp​=∇ψ×∇ϕ. The toroidal field is independent of this constraint, yielding the full representation B=Bp​+Bϕ​eϕ​. Global use of a single flux function assumes the corresponding meridional domain has no obstructing flux topology; a regular field extends to the axis by the usual limiting construction.
The axisymmetric magnetic flux function has a direct physical normalization. The flux through an annulus in a plane of constant z is ∫2πRBz​dR=2πΔψ. Thus ψ is poloidal magnetic flux divided by 2π, up to an arbitrary additive constant. Also B⋅∇ψ=0, including the toroidal part, so its level surfaces are axisymmetric magnetic flux surfaces. Field lines remain on those surfaces even when they wind azimuthally.

Put I=RBϕ​ and define Δ∗​ψ=ψRR​−R−1ψR​+ψzz​. Direct cylindrical differentiation gives
(∇×B)R​=−Iz​/R,(∇×B)z​=IR​/R,(∇×B)ϕ​=−Δ∗​ψ/R.
(16)
The azimuthal component of the Lorentz force is proportional to Iz​ψR​−IR​ψz​. A force-free magnetic field therefore has parallel meridional gradients of I and ψ, implying I=f(ψ) on connected regular flux surfaces. Consequently
Bϕ​=f(ψ)/R.​
(17)
With this dependence the poloidal part of ∇×B is f′(ψ)Bp​. Its full cross product with B is
(∇×B)×B=−R2Δ∗​ψ+f(ψ)f′(ψ)​∇ψ.
(18)
Vanishing force therefore requires Δ∗​ψ+ff′=0 wherever the flux gradient is nonzero, extending by regularity through ordinary isolated nulls. Finally, evaluating the three-dimensional cylindrical divergence gives R2∇⋅(R−2∇ψ)=Δ∗​ψ, so
R2∇⋅(R−2∇ψ)+fdψdf​=0.​
(19)
This derives the Grad-Shafranov equation for a force-free magnetic field. The function f is set by boundary/current information; it is not an additional fixed constant. Its single-valued global form presumes the usual connected flux-surface labeling. The equation also makes ∇×B=f′(ψ)B, explicitly showing that current is parallel to the magnetic field.

For fixed volume define EB​=∫V​B2/(2μ0​)dV and J=∇×B/μ0​. Differentiate and use the ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation. The vector identity
B⋅∇×(u×B)=∇⋅[(u×B)×B]+(u×B)⋅∇×B
(20)
then gives the general balance
dtdEB​​=μ0​1​∮S​[(u⋅B)B−B2u]⋅dS−∫V​u⋅(J×B)dV.
(21)
The volume term is the work done by the magnetic force on the fluid, with the opposite sign for magnetic energy. Under the force-free assumption of part (b), it vanishes, leaving the requested boundary expression. Without that assumption, or another reason for zero net Lorentz work, a boundary-only balance is not generally valid.
For an axisymmetric boundary its normal has no azimuthal component. The differential rotation is tangential, so u⋅dS=0, and u⋅B=RΩBϕ​=Ωf(ψ). Therefore E˙B​=μ0−1​∮S​Ωf(ψ)B⋅dS. A thin axisymmetric tube between neighboring flux labels carries flux 2πdψ. Its exit endpoint contributes +2πΩout​fdψ and its entry endpoint contributes −2πΩin​fdψ. Pair the endpoints, counting each once, to obtain
dtdEB​​=μ0​2π​∫f(ψ)ΔΩ(ψ)dψ,ΔΩ=Ωout​−Ωin​.​
(22)
The endpoint convention fixes the sign. Include each connected tube segment through V once; a field line with no boundary crossing contributes nothing. This is magnetic-energy injection by differential boundary rotation. A common angular velocity at both ends produces no such injection.

Let v=−ur​>0, c=vs​ and c0​=vs0​. Steady smooth adiabatic flow has p=Kργ with the entropy parameter fixed by infinity, and
M˙=4πr2ρv,2v2​+γ−1c2​−rGM​=γ−1c02​​,c2=γKργ−1.
(23)
The second expression follows by integrating the radial momentum equation with dp/ρ=d(c2/(γ−1)) and imposing the outer rest condition. Differentiate continuity to give ρ′/ρ=−2/r−v′/v, and substitute into momentum. This yields the Bondi accretion wind equation
(v−vc2​)v′=r2c2​−r2GM​.
(24)
At a smooth positive-radius sonic point, v=c=cs​ and both sides' vanishing factors require GM/rs​=2cs2​. Evaluate the Bernoulli relation there:
2(γ−1)5−3γ​cs2​=γ−1c02​​,cs2​=5−3γ2c02​​,rs​=45−3γ​c02​GM​.
(25)
Both the squared sound speed and sonic radius must be positive and finite. Hence
1<γ<5/3.​
(26)
For γ=5/3 no finite-radius regular sonic crossing is possible; the critical solution discussed next approaches sonic speed only at the central limit.

At γ=5/3, the sound-speed relation becomes ρ/ρ0​=(c/c0​)3. With M=v/c, mass conservation gives c4=M˙c03​/(4πρ0​r2M). Thus
c2r=CM−1/2,C=(4πρ0​M˙c03​​)1/2.
(27)
Multiply v2/2+3c2/2=3c02​/2+GM/r by r and substitute. This proves
C(21​M3/2+23​M−1/2)=23​c02​r+GM.​
(28)
For the curves, define x=rc02​/(GM), λ=M˙c03​/(4πG2M2ρ0​) and h(M)=M3/2/2+3M−1/2/2. Then
x=32​[λ​h(M)−1],h′=43​(M1/2−M−3/2).
(29)
The function decreases up to Mach one, increases thereafter, and has minimum h(1)=2. The original sketch uses rates relative to M˙max​, that is η=4λ:
For 0<λ<1/4, the minimum of the formal radius curve lies at negative radius. Its physical parts are separate subsonic and supersonic branches extending to r=0. Only the subsonic branch satisfies the specified outer state: as r→∞, it has c→c0​, ρ→ρ0​ and v∼M˙/(4πρ0​r2)→0. The supersonic branch instead has ρ→0, c→0 and v2→3c02​, so it is incompatible with that boundary condition.
At λ=1/4, the two branches meet at (r,M)=(0,1). The outer-rest branch remains subsonic at every positive radius and approaches Mach one at the centre. For λ>1/4, they meet at a positive minimum radius rmin​=(2GM/(3c02​))(2λ​−1), and no real solution exists below it. Such a curve cannot describe a global smooth inflow to a point mass.
Thus globally defined positive-rate solutions satisfying the given outer condition are the subsonic branches with 0<λ≤1/4. These constitute monatomic Bondi accretion. The outer condition alone does not select a unique member; the conventional maximal Bondi solution is the limiting sonic one, while a specific inner accretor condition is additional physical information. Zero rate is the separate hydrostatic limit, not a positive accretion flow.

Let ξ be the fluid Lagrangian displacement, so δu=∂t​ξ about the static background. A displaced material volume changes by fractional amount ∇⋅ξ. Mass conservation therefore gives the fluid Lagrangian perturbation of density Δρ=−ρ∇⋅ξ. Converting to the fluid Eulerian perturbation by Δ=δ+ξ⋅∇ gives
δρ=−ξ⋅∇ρ−ρ∇⋅ξ.
(31)
Adiabatic conservation of p/ργ gives Δp/p=γΔρ/ρ, and consequently δp=−ξ⋅∇p−γp∇⋅ξ.
Integrating the linearized ideal induction equation for a perturbation generated by the displacement gives δB=∇×(ξ×B). Since the equilibrium field is solenoidal, expansion yields
δB=−ξ⋅∇B+B⋅∇ξ−B(∇⋅ξ).
(32)
This is the displacement form of magnetic flux freezing. It also preserves ∇⋅δB=0.
Rewrite the magnetic force as −∇(B2/(2μ0​))+μ0−1​B⋅∇B. Linearizing the full momentum equation, subtracting static force balance, and setting δΠ=δp+B⋅δB/μ0​ gives
ρ∂t2​ξ=−∇δΠ−gδρez​+μ0​1​(δB⋅∇B+B⋅∇δB).​
(33)
Together these are the linear displacement equations for a magnetized atmosphere. The density multiplying acceleration is the equilibrium value, because a perturbation of that factor would multiply a first-order acceleration and contribute only at second order.

Use complex Fourier amplitudes and let primes denote z derivatives. Put C=B2/μ0​, T=γp+C and d=ikx​ξx​+iky​ξy​+ξz′​. Static balance gives (p+B2/(2μ0​))′=−ρg. The perturbations from part (a) then imply
δρ=−ρ′ξz​−ρd,δB=iky​Bξ−(B′ξz​+Bd)ey​,δΠ=ρgξz​−Td+Ciky​ξy​.
(34)
For the last identity, combine δp with BδBy​/μ0​ and use static balance; this is where the background magnetic-pressure gradient cancels.
Multiply the Fourier momentum equation by −ξ∗ and integrate from a to b. Integrating the pressure-gradient term by parts gives [ξz∗​δΠ]ab​−∫d∗δΠdz. The gravity term is ∫[−gρ′∣ξz​∣2−ρgξz∗​d]dz. The magnetic terms reduce to
∫ab​[Cky2​∣ξ∣2+Ciky​ξy∗​d]dz.
(35)
Indeed the term from δBz​B′ey​ cancels exactly the B′ term arising from iky​BδB. This establishes, before completing the square,
ω2∫ab​ρ∣ξ∣2dz=[ξz∗​δΠ]ab​+∫ab​[−d∗δΠ−A∗d+Cky2​∣ξ∣2−gρ′∣ξz​∣2]dz,A=ρgξz​+Ciky​ξy​.
(36)
Now δΠ=A−Td, so
−d∗δΠ−A∗d=T∣d∣2−2Re(d∗A)=T∣δΠ∣2−∣A∣2​.
(37)
Thus the magnetohydrodynamic energy principle has the requested quadratic form
ω2∫ab​ρ∣ξ∣2dz=[ξz∗​δΠ]ab​+∫ab​[T∣δΠ∣2​−T∣ρgξz​+Ciky​ξy​∣2​+Cky2​∣ξ∣2−gρ′∣ξz​∣2]dz.​

For fixed smooth ξy​,ξz​, the only appearances of ξx​ are in δΠ=A−Td and the nonnegative tension contribution Cky2​∣ξx​∣2. Positivity of T makes the pressure square nonnegative too. Impose d=A/T by choosing
ξx​=ikx​1​[Tρgξz​+Ciky​ξy​​−iky​ξy​−ξz′​].​
(39)
Then δΠ=0 exactly and ξx​=O(kx−1​) as ∣kx​∣→∞, removing both nonnegative contributions in the limit. Since the remaining terms do not involve ξx​, no other choice can lower their limiting value. This is the short transverse wavelength limit for magnetic buoyancy.
Take the chosen vertical and along-field displacements compactly supported in the interior when constructing instability trials; the resulting ξx​ also vanishes near the boundaries, satisfying the surface condition. The argument constructs an infimum, not necessarily a minimizing finite-wavelength eigenfunction. Whenever the limiting energy is strictly negative, sufficiently large finite ∣kx​∣ already gives negative energy and hence the instability asserted by the supplied variational criterion.

[−Tρ2g2​−gρ′]∣ξz​∣2=ρg[−dzdlnρ​−Tρg​]∣ξz​∣2.

Set P=γp, so T=P+C, and for ky​=0 put q=iky​ξy​. This variable can be chosen freely. The minimized transverse-displacement energy from part (c) has density
C∣q∣2−P+C∣ρgξz​+Cq∣2​+(Cky2​−gρ′)∣ξz​∣2.
(42)
Complete the square in q to obtain
P+CCP​​q−Pρg​ξz​​2+[Cky2​−gρ′−Pρ2g2​]∣ξz​∣2.​
(43)
This identity also applies at a zero of B, where C=0 and the first square contributes nothing. If −(lnρ)′≥ρg/P everywhere, every term is nonnegative, including the pressure and transverse-tension terms removed earlier. Thus no nonzero along-field wavenumber gives an unstable trial.
For the converse, suppose the strict reverse inequality holds at an interior point. On a sufficiently small closed interval around it, −gρ′−ρ2g2/P is uniformly negative and C is bounded. Choose a sufficiently small but nonzero ∣ky​∣ so that addition of Cky2​ keeps it negative there. Choose a smooth supported ξz​ in that interval and set q=ρgξz​/P, making the first square vanish. This determines a finite ξy​=q/(iky​). Finally choose ξx​ by part (c) with sufficiently large finite ∣kx​∣; its remaining tension energy can be made smaller than the strict negative margin. All displacements vanish near the boundaries. The full energy is therefore negative.
We have proved the long-wavelength undular magnetic buoyancy criterion:
instability for some ky​=0⟺−dzdlnρ​<γpρg​ somewhere.​
(44)
Under the assumed absence of interchange instability, the new unstable range is ρg/(γp+C)≤−(lnρ)′<ρg/(γp). Along-field motion allows material to drain and removes magnetic pressure from the limiting buoyancy stiffness, while tension at nonzero ky​ remains stabilizing. The statement is about existence of an allowed nonzero wavenumber; a fixed prescribed ky​ retains the term Cky2​, and a finite imposed periodic length could exclude the required long wavelengths.
