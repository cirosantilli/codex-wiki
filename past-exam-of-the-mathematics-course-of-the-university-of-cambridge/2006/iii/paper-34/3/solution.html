Use finite sets of links J, source-sink pairs S, and admissible routes R, with at least one route for each pair. Route r carries aggregate flow xr​. The link-route incidence matrix has Ajr​=1 if route r uses link j and zero otherwise; repeated traversals, if allowed, are counted with their multiplicity. The source-sink route incidence matrix has Hsr​=1 if r serves pair s. Thus y=Ax gives link throughputs and f=Hx gives source-sink demands. Write Rs​ for the routes serving s and
Lr​(x)=∑j​Ajr​Dj​(yj​),λs​(x)=minr∈Rs​​Lr​(x).
(18)
A Wardrop equilibrium routes positive flow only on minimum-delay routes:
xr​>0⟹Lr​(x)=λs​(x)(r∈Rs​),Lr​(x)≥λs​(x) for every r∈Rs​.
(19)
This is a nonatomic equilibrium: an individual infinitesimal user's route change does not alter the aggregate link delays.
For fixed nonnegative demand f, the feasible route-flow set {x≥0:Hx=f} is a nonempty product of finite-dimensional simplexes and hence a compact set. The Beckmann potential
V(y)=∑j∈J​∫0yj​​Dj​(u)du
(20)
is continuous, so it attains a minimum over this feasible set. Each primitive is a strictly convex function, since its derivative Dj​ is continuous and strictly increasing. Its route-flow derivative is exactly the corresponding route delay:
∂xr​∂​V(Ax)=∑j​Ajr​Dj​((Ax)j​)=Lr​(x).
(21)
If a minimizer had positive flow on r and a lower-delay alternative q∈Rs​, transferring a small amount from r to q would give directional derivative Lq​−Lr​<0, contradicting minimality. Hence every minimizer is a Wardrop equilibrium.
Conversely, let x be a Wardrop equilibrium and z any feasible competitor. Convexity gives
V(Az)−V(Ax)≥∑r​Lr​(x)(zr​−xr​).
(22)
For each s, subtract λs​ inside this sum. The demand constraint makes ∑r∈Rs​​λs​(zr​−xr​)=0, and the remaining terms are nonnegative: used routes have Lr​−λs​=0, while unused routes have xr​=0, zr​≥0 and Lr​−λs​≥0. Thus V(Az)≥V(Ax). We have proved both existence and the optimization characterization.
Strict convexity in the link vector makes y unique: two different minimizing link vectors would have a feasible midpoint of strictly smaller potential. Thus link delays and the minimum route delays at the Wardrop equilibrium are also unique. The route vector need not be unique, because different route decompositions may have the same Ax and Hx. For example, two successive stages with two parallel links at each stage have four routes. With unit demand and link delay D(u)=u, the vectors
(1/4+t,1/4−t,1/4−t,1/4+t),∣t∣≤1/4,
(23)
all give throughput 1/2 on each of the four links and delay one on every route. This exhibits route-flow nonuniqueness at a Wardrop equilibrium. The equilibrium link throughputs are unique; the individual route flows need not be.
For elastic demand, make the usual physical assumptions explicit: delays are nonnegative on [0,∞), every route is nonempty, and Bs​:[0,∞)→[0,∞) is finite, continuous and strictly decreasing. Put
Ms​=Bs​(0),ℓs​=limλ→∞​Bs​(λ),Ps​(v)=Bs−1​(v)(ℓs​<v≤Ms​).
(24)
The limit exists by monotonicity; it need not be zero. The inverse demand function Ps​ is continuous, strictly decreasing, satisfies Ps​(Ms​)=0, and tends to infinity as v↓ℓs​.
Choose any reference bs​∈(ℓs​,Ms​) and use elastic-demand utility with an interior reference:
G(f)=s∑​Gs​(fs​),Gs​(v)=∫bs​v​Ps​(u)du.​
(25)
Each utility is strictly concave, with derivative Ps​. A reference inside the range avoids a potentially divergent integral from zero. For instance, B(λ)=(1+λ)−1/2 has inverse v−2−1, whose integral from zero is infinite, although the displayed reference-point primitive is finite at every interior demand.
Give Gs​ its limiting value at ℓs​, which may be −∞, and extend it by −∞ outside [ℓs​,Ms​]. This encodes the physical demand range in the objective. After substituting y=Ax, f=Hx, minimize
V(Ax)−G(Hx),x≥0.
(26)
Its effective feasible set is contained in {x≥0:ℓs​≤(Hx)s​≤Ms​}, a nonempty compact set. The objective is lower semicontinuous, including a possible value +∞ at a lower endpoint, and is finite when all demands equal their interior reference values. It therefore has a finite-valued minimizer.
The boundary exclusion for elastic Wardrop demand shows that every minimizing demand is interior. If fs​=ℓs​ and the utility there is finite, adding a small flow h on any route serving s costs at most a bounded constant times h, while the utility gain is at least hPs​(ℓs​+h), whose ratio to h tends to infinity. This decreases the objective. If that endpoint utility is −∞, a finite minimizer cannot occur there in the first place. At fs​=Ms​>0, choose a used route. Its delay is strictly positive, since its nonempty links have positive throughput and strictly increasing nonnegative delays. Removing a small amount of its flow saves a positive first-order cost, whereas the utility loss is o(h) because Ps​(Ms​)=0. This also decreases the objective. Hence ℓs​<fs​<Ms​ for every pair.
The interior-demand route derivatives are now
∂xr​∂​{V(Ax)−G(Hx)}=Lr​(x)−Ps​(fs​),r∈Rs​.
(27)
At a minimizer they are zero on positive route flows and nonnegative on zero route flows, by increasing or decreasing a single route flow. Since fs​>0, some route is used, so
λs​=Ps​(fs​),fs​=Bs​(λs​).​
(28)
These are precisely the elastic-demand Wardrop equilibrium conditions. Conversely, those conditions give the same nonnegative directional derivative for every competitor; convexity then proves global minimality. The equivalent constrained form is the requested minimization of V(y)−G(f) with Hx=f, Ax=y, x≥0. Strict convexity determines both y and f uniquely, while their route decomposition can still be nonunique.
