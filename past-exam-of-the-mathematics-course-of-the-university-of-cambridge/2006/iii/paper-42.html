The empirical distribution function gives each observation mass 1/n:
Fn​(x)=n1​∑i=1n​1{Xi​≤x}.
(1)
The Glivenko-Cantelli theorem asserts the uniform, almost sure convergence
x∈Rsup​∣Fn​(x)−F(x)∣⟶0almost surely.​
(2)
No continuity of the distribution function is required. Here is a proof that also handles its jumps. Let Ui​ be independent uniform random variables on (0,1), and write Xi​=F−1(Ui​), where F−1(u)=inf{x:F(x)≥u}. The generalized inverse property gives 1{Xi​≤x}=1{Ui​≤F(x)}, so, if Gn​ is the empirical distribution function of the Ui​, then Fn​(x)=Gn​(F(x)).
For a fixed positive integer m, the strong law of large numbers applied at each of the finitely many grid points j/m shows that Δn,m​=max0≤j≤m​∣Gn​(j/m)−j/m∣→0 almost surely. If j/m≤t≤(j+1)/m, monotonicity sandwiches Gn​(t) between its endpoint values, whence
sup0≤t≤1​∣Gn​(t)−t∣≤Δn,m​+m1​.
(3)
Take the probability-one intersection of these events over all m. On that event the limit superior is at most 1/m for every m, hence is zero. Therefore supx​∣Gn​(F(x))−F(x)∣→0. The coupled sequence has the same joint law as any independent sample from F, which proves the asserted almost sure conclusion for the original sample.
For 0<p<1, the sample quantile is
Fn−1​(p)=inf{x:Fn​(x)≥p}=X(⌈np⌉)​,
(4)
where X(j)​ denotes the jth order statistic, with repeated observations retained. Suppose the population median m=F−1(1/2) is unique and F is continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of m, with probability density function f(m)>0. Then
n​(Fn−1​(1/2)−m) d​ N(0,4f(m)21​).​
(5)
To see the variance directly, fix t and count observations below m+t/n​. This count has a binomial distribution with success probability qn​=1/2+f(m)t/n​+o(n−1/2). The event that the sample median is below this point is that the count is at least ⌈n/2⌉. The central limit theorem for this binomial triangular array gives a limiting probability Φ(2f(m)t), exactly the displayed normal distribution. Centering is necessary for stating the limiting distribution; subtracting the deterministic population median has no effect on the asymptotic variance requested in the comparisons.

Suppose first that F∈D(G). For fixed k, the distribution functions Hn​=Fkn have the two nondegenerate limits
Hn​(an​x+bn​)⟶G(x)k,Hn​(akn​x+bkn​)⟶G(x).

A natural exponential family of order p has an observation vector Y∈Rp and probability density function, relative to a fixed reference measure ν,
fθ​(y)=h(y)exp{θTy−κ(θ)},κ(θ)=log∫h(y)eθTydν(y).
(25)
Its natural parameter space is N={θ∈Rp:κ(θ)<∞}. A full natural exponential family allows every θ∈N. A regular natural exponential family has an open parameter space; fullness and regularity together mean that N itself is open. A minimal exponential family additionally requires that no nonzero linear combination of the components of Y be constant almost surely. This is the condition that the genuinely needed number of canonical sufficient statistics is p.
Hölder's inequality shows that N is convex: for 0<t<1, its normalizing integral at tθ+(1−t)η is at most the product of the respective integrals to powers t and 1−t. Taking logarithms also proves convexity of the cumulant function κ. In the minimal case its Hessian matrix is positive definite on the interior, as the covariance calculation below establishes. Keeping fullness, regularity and minimality separate matters when discussing existence or uniqueness of a maximum-likelihood estimator.

A general exponential family of order p has the representation
f(x;α)=h(x)exp{η(α)TT(x)−κ(η(α))},T(x)=(T1​(x),…,Tp​(x))T.
(28)
The canonical sufficient statistics Tj​ are independent of the parameter, the support is fixed, and the cumulant function normalizes the probability density function. Minimality rules out a redundant affine relation among the sufficient statistics. If α ranges over an open subset of Rq, q<p, and η is a smooth embedding with derivative of rank q, its image is a q-dimensional parameter surface in the p-dimensional natural parameter space: this is a (p,q) curved exponential family. In a genuinely curved example the surface is nonaffine.
Take the normal distribution N(μ,μ2) with μ>0. Its log density expands as
logf(y;μ)=−21​log(2πμ2)−21​+μy​−2μ2y2​.
(29)
It is a (2,1) curved exponential family with sufficient statistics (y,y2) and natural parameters η1​=1/μ, η2​=−1/(2μ2). The ambient full normal distribution family has η1​∈R, η2​<0 and
κ(η)=−4η2​η12​​+21​log−η2​π​.
(30)
The curve is η2​=−η12​/2 with η1​>0, and its derivative with respect to μ has rank one. Thus the mean and variance are constrained by Var(Y)=(EY)2; the two canonical parameters cannot vary independently. Concavity of an ambient canonical log likelihood need not persist along a curved parameter surface.

For independent observations in a natural exponential family, the log likelihood is
ℓn​(θ)=n{θTYˉ−κ(θ)}+constant,Un​(θ)=n{Yˉ−∇κ(θ)},jn​(θ)=n∇2κ(θ).
(31)
In a minimal exponential family the Observed Fisher information matrix is positive definite, so the log likelihood is strictly concave. Any finite solution of the likelihood equation ∇κ(θ)=Yˉ is therefore the unique global maximum likelihood estimator. Existence is a separate issue: the observed sufficient-statistic mean must belong to the image of the mean parameter map ∇κ.
For a full regular minimal natural exponential family this image is the interior of the convex support of an exponential family, C=conv(supp(hν))​. The necessity follows from a supporting hyperplane argument. If a finite parameter had its mean on a boundary face, some nonzero v would satisfy vTY≤b almost surely and E(vTY)=b. Equality of the expectation forces vTY=b almost surely. Exponential tilting preserves the base-measure null sets, so this contradicts minimality.
For sufficiency, let Yˉ be an interior point of C. Choose finitely many points y1​,…,ym​ of the support whose convex hull contains a ball about Yˉ, say of radius 2ε. Such points exist because Yˉ is interior to the closed convex hull; one may approximate the vertices of a small simplex surrounding it by finite convex combinations of support points. Take support neighbourhoods of radius at most ε with positive finite base-measure masses aj​. These masses can be taken finite by restricting to bounded neighbourhoods and using finiteness of the normalizing integral at any fixed interior parameter. For every vector θ, at least one j has θT(yj​−Yˉ)≥2ε∥θ∥. Integrating over that neighbourhood yields
κ(θ)−θTYˉ≥ε∥θ∥+minj​logaj​.
(32)
Thus the negative log likelihood grows to infinity as ∥θ∥→∞. At a finite boundary point of the full open natural parameter space, the normalizing integral is infinite, since otherwise that point would itself belong to the full domain. Fatou's lemma makes κ(θ) diverge on approach to such a boundary. A minimizing sequence for κ(θ)−θTYˉ therefore stays in a bounded subset away from the boundary; it has an interior limit where the minimum is attained. Its gradient vanishes, giving the required likelihood equation. Strict convexity gives uniqueness.
Hence, under these full, regular and minimal conventions,
a finite unique MLE exists ⟺ Yˉ∈intC.​
(33)
This is maximum likelihood existence in a full regular natural exponential family. Regularity alone does not make every sample admissible: in a Bernoulli distribution an all-zero or all-one sample has its sample mean on the boundary of C=[0,1], and the canonical maximum is approached only as θ→−∞ or +∞. The usual probability parameter then has a boundary maximum in an enlarged model, but no finite canonical maximizer. A regular restriction of the natural parameter space requires membership in its actual mean-map image, rather than merely in intC. Without minimality, affine redundancies may prevent uniqueness of the canonical parameter even when the fitted probability distribution is unique.

The printed local assumptions alone do not guarantee localization. A useful localization condition for Laplace's method is that g(y)≥g(y​)+δε​ outside each sufficiently small fixed neighbourhood of y​, for some δε​>0, and that ∫ab​e−n∗​g(y)dy<∞ for some n∗​>0.
