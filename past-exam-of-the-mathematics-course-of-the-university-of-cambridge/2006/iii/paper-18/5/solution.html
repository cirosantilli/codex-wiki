The Torelli theorem concerns a connected smooth projective curve over C and its canonical principal polarization of a complex torus. Its conclusion is
(Jac(X),[ΘX​])≅(Jac(Y),[ΘY​])⟹X≅Y.​
(36)
The polarization of a complex torus is part of the hypothesis. We will reconstruct the curve intrinsically from the Jacobian variety and the theta divisor, using the Gauss map of a theta divisor, and prove the reconstruction in both the nonhyperelliptic and hyperelliptic curve cases. The needed biduality and ordinary-tangency facts about projective dual varieties are the general projective-duality results permitted here.
First explain why the polarized data determine the theta divisor up to translation. On J=Jac(X), take integral one-form classes αi​,βi​ dual to the symplectic cycle basis. Pullback by the degree-one Abelian sum map identifies them with the corresponding classes on X; the Riemann bilinear relations give
∫X​αi​∧βj​=δij​,∫X​αi​∧αj​=∫X​βi​∧βj​=0.
(37)
On Xg−1, the sum map pulls each one-form back to the sum of its pullbacks from the factors. It has generic degree (g−1)! onto Wg−1​: the Abel theorem for divisors and the existence of classes with h0=1 give a unique effective unordered representative, and ordering gives the factorial. Integrating a wedge of 2g−2 basis one-forms over the product, only terms assigning a matching pair αi​,βi​ to each factor survive. Dividing by (g−1)! gives integral one when precisely one matching pair is omitted, and zero for all other basis wedges. Hence the Poincaré dual class of Wg−1​ is
c1​(OJ​(Θ))=∑i=1g​αi​∧βi​.
(38)
This is exactly the integral form of the canonical principal polarization of a complex torus constructed by periods.
Its associated line bundle has one-dimensional section space. One can check this without any theta multiplicity assumption: in normalized period coordinates a representative has sections satisfying
F(z+m)=F(z),F(z+τn)=e−πintτn−2πintzF(z).
(39)
Expand the first periodicity in a Fourier series F(z)=∑k∈Zg​ck​e2πiktz. The second periodicity forces ck​=c0​eπiktτk. Positive definiteness of Imτ makes this series normally convergent, so the section space has dimension exactly one. Other line bundles giving the same polarization of a complex torus differ by a degree-zero line bundle. The map x↦Tx∗​L⊗L−1 is an isomorphism from J to its dual abelian variety for a principal polarization of a complex torus: on lattices it is the unimodular alternating form. Thus all these line bundles are translates, and so are their unique zero divisors. In particular the given polarized data recover the reduced divisor on an algebraic curve Wg−1​ up to translation. Translation does not change tangent hyperplanes after identifying tangent spaces with T0​J.
Put V=T0​J=H0(KX​)∗ and P=P(V∗). At a smooth class L=OX​(D)∈Θ, the Riemann singularity theorem says h0(D)=1. The derivative of the Abelian sum map identifies the translated tangent hyperplane with H0(KX​(−D))⊥. Thus the intrinsic Gauss map of a theta divisor is
Γ:Θsm​⟶P,Γ(L)=[ω],H0(KX​(−D))=Cω.
(40)
In particular
D≤div(ω).
(41)
We will see that this map is dominant and generically finite. Its function-field extension therefore determines an intrinsic finite cover: normalize P in C(Θ), obtaining Z→P. Normalization in a finite extension is finite here. Let B⊂P be its reduced codimension-one branch locus. Using this finite cover avoids counting omitted points of the rational Gauss map of a theta divisor as ramification. Everything in this construction is determined by the polarized Jacobian variety.
Suppose first that g≥3 and X is nonhyperelliptic. Its canonical map embeds it as a nondegenerate curve C⊂P(V). For clarity, this follows directly from the Riemann-Roch theorem: h0(p)=1 on a positive-genus curve, so KX​ has no base point. If a length-two effective divisor E had h0(E)≥2, a nonconstant meromorphic function with poles bounded by E would give a map of degree at most two to P1. Degree one forces genus zero, and degree two is the hyperelliptic curve case. Hence h0(E)=1, and the Riemann-Roch theorem gives h0(KX​(−E))=g−2. The canonical sections therefore separate every pair of points and every tangent direction, proving the embedding.
Let U⊂P be the open set of hyperplanes transverse to C. A hyperplane in U cuts out n=2g−2 distinct points. These points span the hyperplane: if a second independent canonical section vanished at all of them, its zero divisor would have to equal the same degree-2g−2 divisor, and the ratio of the two sections would be a constant. Consequently some g−1 of the intersection points are independent. For the divisor D formed by such a subset, h0(KX​(−D))=1 and h0(D)=1.
We need to know that every (g−1)-subset of a general hyperplane section has this property. Here is the monodromy argument, with its essential steps. The incidence space choosing one intersection point is a projective bundle over C before restricting to U, so it is irreducible. The incidence space choosing two distinct ordered intersection points is likewise a projective bundle over C×C minus its diagonal, with fibre Pg−3; it too is irreducible. Restricting to transverse sections preserves irreducibility and hence connectedness of these covers. Thus the monodromy action of a covering space on the n intersection points is two-transitive. A general tangent hyperplane has just one contact of order two, by characteristic-zero projective biduality theorem; locally its intersection equation is z2=t together with the other simple points. A small loop in t exchanges exactly those two points. Two-transitivity conjugates this transposition to every transposition, so the monodromy is the full symmetric group Sn​.
It follows that the finite étale cover of U choosing a (g−1)-subset is connected and irreducible. The condition of dependent evaluations is closed on that cover. It is proper because we have exhibited an independent subset; its image under the finite map is therefore a proper closed subset of U. Outside that image every subset is independent. By the Abel theorem for divisors, distinct such subsets have distinct Abelian sums: h0(D)=1 means there is only one effective representative of its class. Thus the subset cover is birational to Θ with its Gauss map of a theta divisor, and
degΓ=(g−12g−2​).
(42)
More precisely it is exactly the restriction of Z→P over U, since this étale cover is normal and has the same function field.
There can consequently be no branch component over U. Its complement is the irreducible hypersurface C∗ of tangent hyperplanes, the projective dual variety of C. Around a general point of C∗, the transposition of two roots acts nontrivially on (g−1)-subsets: choose a subset containing exactly one of those two roots. Hence the finite cover is ramified there. This proves the exact identity
B=C∗.​
(43)
The quoted biduality theorem now recovers C=(C∗)∗=B∗ as an embedded curve, and the canonical map identifies X with C. This proves reconstruction in the nonhyperelliptic case, including special curves rather than only a general curve in moduli.
Now suppose that X is a hyperelliptic curve of genus g≥3. Let π:X→P1 be its degree-two map. The Riemann-Hurwitz formula gives 2g+2 branch points b1​,…,b2g+2​. Choose an affine coordinate with no branch at infinity and write
y2=∏i=12g+2​(x−bi​).
(44)
The forms dx/y,xdx/y,…,xg−1dx/y are holomorphic and independent; their orders at the two points at infinity show there are exactly g of them. Thus the canonical map factors as π followed by the degree-g−1 rational normal curve embedding
ν:P1⟶P(V),x⟼[1:x:⋯:xg−1].
(45)
Write R=ν(P1).
A general hyperplane section of R consists of g−1 distinct points x1​,…,xg−1​ avoiding the branch points. The corresponding canonical divisor on X consists of the g−1 pairs π−1(xj​). Choosing one lift from each pair gives an effective divisor D with h0(D)=1: the evaluation matrix of the canonical forms has Vandermonde rank g−1. If instead a degree-g−1 sub-divisor contains an entire pair, it has at least two sections, because that pair is a fibre of π and moves in a pencil. Every choice not taking exactly one point from each pair contains an entire pair. Therefore the generic smooth theta points over the hyperplane are exactly the independent choices of one lift in each pair, and
degΓ=2g−1.
(46)
The cover of these choices is again the restriction of Z to the open set of transverse sections of R avoiding the bi​: the identification on a dense open follows from the Abel theorem for divisors, and the choice cover is finite étale and normal. Its components would correspond to components of the function field of the irreducible theta divisor, so it is connected.
There are precisely two types of codimension-one branching. If a simple root xj​ crosses a branch point bi​, its two lifts exchange, giving nontrivial monodromy on the choices. This yields the hyperplane
Hi​={H∈P:ν(bi​)∈H}.
(47)
If two roots collide away from the bi​, a local transverse parameter gives roots x0​±t​. Trivialize the two sheets of π near x0​. Choices selecting different sheets for the two roots are exchanged when the roots exchange; hence this also causes branching. Its locus is R∗, the hypersurface of tangent hyperplanes to R. Away from these two loci all roots and all their lifts vary locally without branching, so there are no other branch components. Consequently
B=R∗ ∪ i=1⋃2g+2​Hi​.​
(48)
For g≥3, R∗ is irreducible and nonlinear. Indeed, hyperplane sections of the degree-g−1 rational normal curve are binary forms of degree g−1, and the repeated-root hypersurface is their irreducible discriminant of degree 2g−4≥2. Thus B intrinsically separates into its single nonlinear component R∗ and its 2g+2 linear components. Biduality recovers R, while dualizing Hi​ recovers the point ν(bi​) on R.
These data recover the double cover uniquely. Identify R with P1 and move infinity away from the recovered branch set. Its function field is
C(x)(∏i​(x−bi​)​).
(49)
Any other quadratic extension with the same simple branch points has a defining rational function whose ratio to this product has even valuation everywhere. On P1 such a rational function is a constant times a square, and every nonzero complex constant is a square. The quadratic extensions are therefore isomorphic; their smooth projective models are isomorphic. This proves the hyperelliptic reconstruction. The branch locus also distinguishes the two cases: the nonhyperelliptic locus has one irreducible branch hypersurface, whereas the hyperelliptic locus has the additional 2g+2 linear components.
Finally handle the small genera. In genus two, the theta divisor is W1​, the image of X itself. The Abelian sum map on X is injective because a principal divisor p−q with p=q would give a degree-one map to P1. Its derivative is nonzero since the canonical bundle has no base point, so this compact injective immersion identifies X with W1​. In genus one the same argument identifies X with its Jacobian variety after choosing an origin. In genus zero the Riemann-Roch theorem supplies a function with one simple pole, giving X≅P1. These cases complete the proof: the canonically polarized Jacobian determines the curve in every genus.
