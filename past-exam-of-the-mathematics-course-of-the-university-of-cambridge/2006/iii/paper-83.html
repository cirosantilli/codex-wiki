Let K denote isotropic permeability of a porous medium, μ the fluid dynamic viscosity, ρ0​ the reference fluid mass density, α>0 its volumetric thermal-expansion coefficient and g gravitational acceleration. The Boussinesq approximation uses ρ=ρ0​[1−α(T−T∗​)] only in the gravitational force. With upward coordinate z, hydraulic pressure P and Darcy flux q, the equations are
q=−μK​∇P+Bθez​,∇⋅q=0,B=μρ0​gαK​,
(1)
where θ is the perturbation to the conductive temperature. To keep porous heat-capacity conventions explicit, let Ce​ be effective heat capacity per bulk volume, Cf​=ρ0​cf​ the liquid volumetric heat capacity, ke​ effective thermal conductivity, σ=Ce​/Cf​, and κ=ke​/Cf​. The thermal equation is σTt​+q⋅∇T=κ∇2T. If the usual unit-capacity convention is adopted, σ=1 and κ is the thermal diffusivity. Thresholds below do not depend on σ; it only changes temporal rates.
For a conductive state with upward temperature decrease Γ=−Tz​>0, linearization gives
σθt​−Γw=κ∇2θ,∇2w=B∇⊥2​θ,
(2)
where w=qz​. The second equation follows by taking the divergence of Darcy law to eliminate P and then differentiating its vertical component. It is important that the Laplacian on its right is horizontal.
In the horizontal layer, both boundary planes prescribe the temperature and are impermeable, so θ=w=0 at z=0,h. Use a normal mode proportional to est+ik⋅xsin(nπz/h), with horizontal wavenumber k=∣k∣, n≥1. Writing b=nπ/h, the momentum relation gives w=Bk2θ/(k2+b2), and the thermal relation gives
σs=k2+b2BΓk2​−κ(k2+b2).
(3)
A neutral mode therefore requires BΓ/κ=(k2+b2)2/k2. Since k2+2b2+b4/k2≥4b2, the smallest value is 4π2/h2, attained at n=1 and k=π/h. This derives the onset of convection in a horizontal Darcy layer rather than simply importing its critical number. Thus
Tm,crit​=T0​+ρ0​gαKh4π2μκ​.​
(4)
Every small disturbance decays for Tm​<Tm,crit​; at equality the critical roll is neutral. Strict decay has this threshold as a supremum, not an attained maximum. The first roll wavelength is 2h, compatible with the very large horizontal extent. Cooling from above gives the opposite, stable buoyancy feedback.
For the tall square column, define a as its side length and H≫a as its height. The insulated vertical walls impose ∂n​θ=0, while impermeability imposes zero normal Darcy flux. Away from the remote ends, a nearly height-independent mode has hydrostatic pressure adjustment and w=Bθ, with zero cross-sectional mean. The transverse temperature modes are
θ∝cos(mπx/a)cos(nπy/a),k⊥2​=a2π2(m2+n2)​,
(5)
where m,n≥0 but (m,n)=(0,0) is excluded: a closed column cannot support a net vertical discharge. Its horizontally uniform density perturbation is balanced by pressure, rather than driving overturning. The rate of a nonconstant mode is
σs=BΓ−κk⊥2​.
(6)
The smallest allowed eigenvalue is π2/a2, so the insulated square-column Darcy convection onset is
Γcrit​=ρ0​gαKa2π2μκ​.​
(7)
Again strict decay requires Γ<Γcrit​. This is the leading long-column result. End conditions that maintain the basic gradient require the vertical velocity to turn around near the ends and give small finite-height corrections; their exact coefficient cannot be inferred from insulation of the vertical walls alone. For a separated vertical mode of small wavenumber kz​, the neutral expression is BΓ/κ=(k⊥2​+kz2​)2/k⊥2​, which tends to the displayed value as H/a→∞.
Just above threshold, warm fluid rises along one side and colder fluid sinks along the opposite side, with horizontal return flow at the top and bottom. The two lowest modes, proportional to cos(πx/a) and cos(πy/a), have the same threshold. A square therefore admits either orientation or a superposition, including diagonal overturning. Linear theory predicts the weak columnar circulation and its degeneracy; it does not select a unique nonlinear planform.

Use constant porosity ϕ, isotropic rock permeability of a porous medium K, common liquid dynamic viscosity μ and gravitational acceleration g. Treat the two liquids as separated by a sharp interface, with negligible capillary pressure, molecular mixing, ambient-current resistance and inertia. At distances beyond the initial source scale the current is shallow relative to its horizontal length, so its pressure is hydrostatic. The initial release is symmetric about x=0 and has actual liquid volume V per unit length of source. Consequently its geometric thickness integral is V/ϕ, not V.
Below the interface the excess pressure relative to the ambient hydrostatic field is p′=Δρg(h−z), so Darcy law gives horizontal discharge qx​=−KΔρghx​/μ. The vertically integrated discharge is Qx​=−KΔρghhx​/μ. Take the specified downward leakage v=γh to mean Darcy flux per bulk horizontal area. Mass conservation then gives
ϕht​+∂x​Qx​=−γh,ht​=β(hhx​)x​−λh,β=μϕKΔρg​,λ=ϕγ​.​
(8)
If v is instead defined as interstitial seepage speed, the actual leakage discharge is ϕv and the corresponding coefficient is λ=γ. Stating the velocity convention removes that otherwise ambiguous porosity factor. For a shale of thickness ds​ and permeability Ks​, a simple hydrostatic leakage model has γ=Ks​Δρg/(μds​), if the reservoir below it has the same ambient pressure reference and no capillary entry threshold.
Let R(t) be the half-width of the support; the full end-to-end length will be L(t)=2R(t). Appropriate conditions are symmetry hx​(0,t)=0, h(±R,t)=0, no flux beyond the support, and the moving-front condition R˙=−βhx​(R−,t). Initially ϕh(x,t)dx converges weakly to Vδ0​, expressing the concentrated line release. The equations describe the spreading porous gravity current, not the singular immediate release itself.
With λ=0, conserved volume and balance of ht​ against β(hhx​)x​ give width proportional to t1/3 and height proportional to t−1/3. Write h=t−1/3f(η), η=x/t1/3. Substitution gives
−31​(f+ηf′)=β(ff′)′.
(9)
Integrating from the symmetry axis gives βff′=−ηf/3. Inside the support f>0, so f′=−η/(3β) and f=A−η2/(6β). Thus the compact Barenblatt solution is
h(x,t)=6βt[R(t)2−x2]+​​.​
(10)
The positive-part notation makes h=0 outside the current. Volume fixes its remaining constant:
ϕV​=∫−RR​hdx=9βt2R3​,R(t)=(2ϕ9βVt​)1/3,L(t)=2(2ϕ9βVt​)1/3.​
(11)
If “length” denotes the distance from the source to one front, it is R rather than L; both conventions are given explicitly. Differentiating gives R˙=R/(3t)=−βhx​(R−,t), so the front condition holds. Using V/2 on each reflecting half-line gives the same normalization.
For positive leakage define actual retained volume V(t)=ϕ∫hdx. Since the edge thickness and boundary flux vanish, integration of the equation yields
dtdV​=−λV,V(t)=Ve−λt.​
(12)
Put h=e−λtH(x,τ), where τ=(1−e−λt)/λ. Because τ˙=e−λt, substitution cancels the leakage term and leaves Hτ​=β(HHx​)x​. This derives the exponential drainage transform for porous-medium diffusion. Its conserved geometric volume is still V/ϕ, so
h(x,t)=e−λt6βτ(t)[R(t)2−x2]+​​,R(t)=[2ϕλ9βV​(1−e−λt)]1/3.​
(13)
The full length is again L(t)=2R(t). In particular,
R∞​=(2ϕλ9βV​)1/3,L∞​=2R∞​.​
(14)
At early times τ∼t, recovering impermeable-bed spreading. At late times, drainage reduces the current thickness and therefore its hydrostatic driving pressure; the effective spreading time saturates at 1/λ. The current approaches a finite horizontal reach while its retained volume and thickness vanish exponentially. This does not mean a positive-volume pool remains at a finite final depth. For small positive λ the length scale diverges as λ−1/3, consistently with unbounded spreading when leakage is absent.

Model the post-eruption flow as an axisymmetric, shallow, hydrostatic, turbulently mixed layer on a horizontal bed. Neglect entrainment, erosion, heat exchange, rotation and large-scale viscous drag. Particles are dilute, with density ρp​, uniform-in-depth particle volume fraction c(r,t) and constant downward settling speed ws​>0 relative to the carrier gas. Deposited particles are not resuspended. Their volume is small enough that settling does not significantly change the bulk carrier volume. Let ρa​ be ambient air density and ρg​ carrier-gas density at its local temperature, and use a leading Boussinesq approximation for the mixture motion. Define
g′=bp​c+bT​,bp​=ρa​g(ρp​−ρa​)​,bT​=ρa​g(ρg​−ρa​)​<0.
(15)
The hot gas reduces the density excess; the ground-hugging current model requires g′>0. If density contrasts are too large for Boussinesq dynamics, density-weighted balances would be needed instead. The equations below are a stated shallow-layer model, not a description of the initial explosive vent jet.
For layer height h and radial depth-averaged speed u, the appropriate shallow-water balances are
ht​+r1​(rhu)r​=0,
(16)
(hu)t​+r1​(rhu2)r​+21​(g′h2)r​=0,
(17)
(hc)t​+r1​(rhuc)r​=−ws​c.
(18)
The hydrostatic force gives the nonconservative momentum equation ut​+uur​=−g′hr​−(h/2)gr′​. In particular a spatially varying particle fraction must not be treated as constant reduced gravity when differentiating pressure. With no heat exchange or entrainment, a carrier-temperature anomaly Θ is advected:
(hΘ)t​+r1​(rhuΘ)r​=0.
(19)
At this order bT​ is proportional to −Θ. At the bed the carrier has no normal penetration and particles have absorbing deposition flux ws​c; at the top the pressure equals ambient pressure. Axis regularity requires u(0,t)=0 and smooth scalar fields, with no further source after the initial pulse. The initial height, particle inventory and temperature anomaly specify the released volume and ash content. Outside the front r=R(t), the current layer is absent. At a resolved advancing head, use the material-front condition and the gravity-current front condition R˙=Fgf′​hf​​, where F is an order-one front Froude number and hf​,gf′​ refer to the fluid feeding the head. The front closure replaces unresolved head dynamics; simply prescribing both a zero-depth mathematical edge and a finite-depth box-head speed would be inconsistent.
To obtain a gravity-current box model, assume horizontal as well as vertical mixing, uniform h(t),c(t),Θ(t) in a disk of radius R(t), and a negligible-volume head whose feeding depth is the box depth. The conserved bulk volume V gives
πR2h=V,h=R2A​,A=πV​.
(20)
A bulk velocity u=(R˙/R)r satisfies interior continuity for this box. Rather than impose a uniform pressure on the full shallow-water equations and thereby lose the head force, replace their momentum dynamics by the front closure. Total suspended particle volume is Vc, so bed deposition gives
c˙=−hws​​c,R˙=F(bp​c+bT​)h​,Θ˙=0.​
(21)
These ordinary differential equations, together with initial R0​,c0​,V,Θ0​, are the axisymmetric settling box model. The maintained negative thermal term may eventually cancel particle buoyancy and cause lift-off; beyond g′=0 this horizontal gravity-current model does not apply.
For the requested particle-only calculation set bT​=0 and define the initial reduced gravity g0′​=bp​c0​. The equations reduce to
R˙=Fbp​A​Rc​​,c˙=−Aws​R2​c.
(22)
Eliminating time gives dc​/dR=−ws​R3/[2Fbp​​A3/2]. Integrating from the release state yields
c0​c​​=1−8Fg0′​​A3/2ws​(R4−R04​)​.
(23)
Thus the monodisperse circular ash runout has limiting radius
R∞​=[R04​+ws​8Fg0′​​​(πV​)3/2]1/4.​
(24)
The maximum outward distance beyond the initial release edge is R∞​−R0​. When the vent scale is negligible, set R0​=0 in this late-time source idealization. The resulting radius scales as V3/8(g0′​)1/8ws−1/4​; it is not obtained by conserving reduced gravity after particles settle out.
To determine the time, put s=R2/R∞2​, s0​=R02​/R∞2​ and τ=4A/(ws​R∞2​). The concentration relation becomes c/c0​​=(1−s2)/(1−s02​), and the front equation reduces to
s˙=τ1​(1−s2),s(t)=tanh(τt​+artanhs0​).​
(25)
Consequently
t(R)=τ[artanh(R∞2​R2​)−artanhs0​],t(R∞​)=∞.​
(26)
The furthest reach is a limiting radius, approached asymptotically rather than attained at a finite stopping time. As the residual particle concentration vanishes, the front slows exponentially. A finite observational threshold for remaining ash would give a finite practical stopping time, but no such threshold is part of these balances.
The local ash deposit density is most naturally its mass per horizontal area, Σ(r). Once the front has arrived at r, its local deposition flux is ρp​ws​c(t). For r<R∞​, define sa​=max(s0​,r2/R∞2​); the arrival time is zero inside the initial footprint and otherwise ta​=τ[artanhsa​−artanhs0​]. Since
c(t)=(1−s02​)2c0​​sech4(τt​+artanhs0​),
(27)
we can integrate the flux exactly. Using ∫sech4ξdξ=tanhξ−(tanh3ξ)/3 gives the circular ash deposit profile
Σ(r)=(1−s02​)2ρp​ws​c0​τ​(32​−sa​+3sa3​​),r<R∞​;Σ(r)=0(r≥R∞​).​
(28)
It is constant under the initial disk, then decreases to zero at the limiting edge. Particle solid volume per area is Σ/ρp​; deposit thickness is Σ/ρd​ if a bulk deposit density ρd​ including voids is supplied. The problem does not determine that packing density.
For a negligible initial radius this simplifies to
Σ(r)=ρp​ws​c0​τ[32​−(R∞​r​)2+31​(R∞​r​)6].​
(29)
Its integral 2π∫0R∞​​rΣ(r)dr=ρp​c0​V confirms that the complete initial ash inventory is deposited. The finite-R0​ formula gives the same budget including its central flat portion.
The diagram uses the negligible-vent-radius idealization and time normalized by τ. It illustrates both the asymptotic approach to R∞​ and the smoothly vanishing outer deposit. All these predictions depend on the mixing and constant-Froude head closure; they do not claim to resolve the initial blast or the ultimate inertia-dominated motion of almost particle-free gas.

Let the two populations have settling speeds w1​≫w2​>0 and particle volume fractions c1​,c2​. In the same mixed box their scalar equations are c˙j​=−wj​cj​/h, while the common reduced gravity is g′=b1​c1​+b2​c2​ after neglecting thermal buoyancy. Both populations are carried by the same radius and depth:
R˙=F(b1​c1​+b2​c2​)h​,h=V/(πR2).
(30)
The coarse ash deposits rapidly near the source; the fine ash remains aloft longer, sustains the current's buoyancy and is carried farther. The composition of the deposit therefore fines outward. Since the remaining fine ash drives the flow transporting both classes, the runout is not obtained by superposing two independent monodisperse currents.
Define the settling exposure for a particle size distribution J=∫0t​ds/h(s). Then cj​=cj0​e−wj​J and
R∞4​−R04​=4F(V/π)3/2∫0∞​b1​c10​e−w1​J+b2​c20​e−w2​J​dJ.
(31)
The integral is finite when both settling speeds are positive. Each deposited mass is Σj​(r)=ρpj​wj​∫ta​(r)∞​cj​(t)dt; their sum is the total deposit, with a coarse proximal component and a finer distal tail. If the actual hot-gas density deficit is retained, early loss of coarse ash can make g′ vanish and loft the remaining fine-rich cloud, shortening its ground-hugging reach relative to this particle-only calculation.

For a continuous size distribution, let c0​(w)dw be initial particle volume fraction in a settling-speed interval. The settling exposure for a particle size distribution gives
c(w,t)=c0​(w)e−wJ(t),g′(J)=∫0∞​b(w)c0​(w)e−wJdw.
(32)
The spectrum continually shifts toward slower-settling particles. The deposit becomes progressively finer outward, with a smooth range of depositional distances instead of two distinct size components. Size-resolved deposition is ρp​(w)w∫ta​(r)∞​c0​(w)e−wJ(t)dt per speed interval.
There is no universal monodisperse runout formula with an arbitrarily chosen mean settling speed. The box relation is
R4−R04​=4F(V/π)3/2∫0J​g′(s)​ds.
(33)
A spectrum bounded away from zero settling speed has a finite limiting radius, but an arbitrarily slow-settling tail can extend the reach substantially or remove a finite runout bound. For example, if b(w)c0​(w)∼Cwp near w=0, p>−1, its Laplace transform behaves as g′(J)∼CΓ(p+1)J−(p+1). The runout integral converges only for p>1. A positive population with exactly zero settling speed retains buoyancy indefinitely and spreads without a finite radius limit in this no-entrainment model. Thus the fine tail of the actual distribution, and possible lofting when thermal buoyancy is restored, control the distal outcome.

Let z>0 measure height from the lower edge and y>0 distance from one face of the vertical plate. Use the steady Boussinesq approximation, homogeneous isotropic permeability of a porous medium K, reference fluid mass density ρ0​, dynamic viscosity μ, thermal expansion coefficient α, effective thermal conductivity ke​ and liquid volumetric heat capacity Cf​=ρ0​cf​. Define B=ρ0​gαK/μ and κ=ke​/Cf​, consistently with the thermal convention used above. Write θ=T−Ta​, where Ta​ is the uniform ambient temperature.
Interpret F as uniform wall heat-flux density: a plate segment of height dz inputs heat Fdz per unit horizontal span. The cumulative heat input up to height z is therefore Fz. If F instead denotes the sum of flux densities into two identically heated faces, replace it by F/2 in each face formula before summing. A specification only of total power per span, without its distribution in height, would not determine this uniformly heated-wall problem. We calculate one heated face; identical heating on both faces doubles the integrated fluxes.
Away from the leading edge, suppose the boundary layer is slender, δ(z)≪z, and porous inertia and longitudinal heat conduction are negligible. Lateral pressure adjustment leaves the vertical Darcy velocity proportional to buoyancy:
w=Bθ,vy​+wz​=0,vθy​+wθz​=κθyy​.
(34)
At the plate, v=0 and −ke​θy​=F; in the far field w,θ→0. Darcy law permits tangential discharge at an impermeable plate, so imposing a further no-slip condition w=0 would be inappropriate in this leading porous model.
Balance θ∼Fδ/ke​, w∼Bθ and wθ/z∼κθ/δ2 to obtain
δ(z)=(BFκke​z​)1/3,θ∼ke​Fδ​∝z1/3,w∝z1/3.​
(35)
Thus the warmed layer thickens and accelerates upward. Define a streamfunction by w=ψy​, v=−ψz​ and set
η=δy​,ψ=δκz​f(η),w=δ2κz​f′(η),θ=ke​Fδ​f′(η).
(36)
The last two expressions agree because δ3=κke​z/(BF). Differentiation gives v=−(κ/3δ)(2f−ηf′). Substituting in the thermal equation leaves the single nonlinear equation for the uniformly heated vertical plate in a porous medium:
f′′′+32​ff′′−31​(f′)2=0,f(0)=0,f′′(0)=−1,f′(∞)=0.​
(37)
The first condition is impermeability; the second fixes the wall heat flux; the third fixes ambient temperature and vertical velocity. Seek the positive-temperature branch with f′≥0 and finite f(∞). Decay also gives f′′(∞)=0. The far-field transverse inflow need not vanish: v∞​=−(2κ/3δ)f(∞) supplies the increasing discharge.
An approximate solution can be obtained without a numerical shooting calculation. Integrate the differential equation over 0<η<∞. The boundary conditions and integration by parts give
0=[f′′]0∞​+32​[ff′]0∞​−∫0∞​(f′)2dη=1−∫0∞​(f′)2dη.
(38)
Thus the exact thermal budget is ∫0∞​(f′)2dη=1. Assume an exponential thermal shape, f=C(1−e−η/d). The wall derivative requires C/d2=1, and the integral condition requires C2/(2d)=1. Hence d=21/3, C=22/3 and the integral exponential profile for porous wall convection is
f(η)≃22/3(1−e−η/21/3),θ(y,z)≃ke​21/3Fδ​e−y/(21/3δ).​
(39)
It satisfies all boundary conditions, has positive decaying temperature, and matches the exact integrated heat budget. It is an integral approximation, not an exact pointwise solution of the differential equation; its mass-flux coefficient should be read accordingly.
Define the volume discharge, kinematic Darcy momentum flux and buoyancy flux per horizontal span by
Q(z)=∫0∞​wdy,M(z)=∫0∞​w2dy,B(z)=gα∫0∞​wθdy.
(40)
With these standard discharge conventions the mass flux is m˙=ρ0​Q. Their similarity expressions are
Q=δκz​f(∞),M=δ3κ2z2​∫0∞​(f′)2dη,B=Bgα​M.
(41)
The integral identity evaluates two of them exactly:
M(z)=ke​BκFz​=Cf​BFz​,B(z)=ke​gακFz​=Cf​gαFz​.​
(42)
Equivalently Cf​∫wθdy=Fz: all the heat supplied below height z is carried upward through that section. Although these fluxes are exact within the boundary-layer equations, porous drag means that the momentum flux itself is not conserved with height.
The approximate mass flux is
m˙(z)≃22/3ρ0​δ(z)κz​=22/3ρ0​κ2/3(ke​BF​)1/3z2/3.​
(43)
For clarity, if “momentum flux” means actual pore-fluid momentum rather than the commonly quoted kinematic Darcy diagnostic, constant porosity ϕ and pore speed w/ϕ give P˙=(ρ0​/ϕ)M. The mass flux remains ρ0​Q because w already denotes discharge per bulk area. Stating both prevents an implicit porosity error. Validity also requires αθ≪1 and a sufficiently slender, low-inertia porous boundary layer; the approximation is not the immediate leading-edge conduction field.
