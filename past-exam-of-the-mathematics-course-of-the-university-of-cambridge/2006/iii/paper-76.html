Let F be an invertible, orientation-preserving deformation gradient, with J=detF>0. To prove Nanson's formula, take two reference surface tangent vectors a,b. Their current images are Fa,Fb, and their oriented area vectors are proportional to a×b and (Fa)×(Fb). For every vector c, the scalar triple product gives
(Fc)⋅[(Fa)×(Fb)]=Jc⋅(a×b).
(1)
Thus FT[(Fa)×(Fb)]=J(a×b), and
dS=JF−TdS0​​.
(2)
This proof also identifies the area transformation as the cofactor of F, rather than a transformation by F itself.
Use the paper's material-index-first nominal stress tensor PIi​. Equality of force on the same material surface in its two configurations gives PIi​dS0I​=σji​dSj​. Substitute the area transformation to obtain
PIi​=J(F−1)Ij​σji​​,τji​=Jσji​=FjI​PIi​​.
(3)
Here τ is the Kirchhoff stress tensor. With symmetric Cauchy stress tensor, the matrix forms are P=JF−1σ and τ=FP. This P is the transpose of the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor; keeping the index convention prevents a spurious transpose later.
For the velocity gradient L=F˙F−1, let D=(L+LT)/2 be the rate-of-strain tensor. Then the reference-volume mechanical power is
PIi​F˙iI​=τji​(F−1)Ij​F˙iI​=τji​Lij​=τji​Dij​​.
(4)
The last step uses stress symmetry, as supplied by angular-momentum balance in an ordinary continuum without couple stresses: a symmetric tensor has zero contraction with the skew part of L.
By work-conjugate stress and strain, a pair (T,E) must satisfy T:E˙=τ:D for every deformation rate, with both sides measured per reference volume. For the Green-Lagrange strain tensor, set C=FTF and calculate
E˙(2)=21​(F˙TF+FTF˙)=FTDF.
(5)
Cyclically rearranging the contraction shows T(2):E˙(2)=(FT(2)FT):D. Hence the symmetric conjugate is
T(2)=F−1τF−T​,
(6)
the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor.
The inverse right Cauchy-Green strain is E(−2)=(I−C−1)/2, because C−1=F−1F−T. Differentiating CC−1=I gives
E˙(−2)=21​C−1C˙C−1=F−1DF−T.
(7)
Consequently T(−2):E˙(−2)=(F−TT(−2)F−1):D, and
T(−2)=FTτF​.
(8)
The two stress measures have a geometric interpretation in the convected coordinate net. Its basis vectors are gI​=FeI​ and its reciprocal basis is gI=F−TeI​. The entries of T(2) are the contravariant components in τ=TIJ(2)​gI​⊗gJ​, whereas TIJ(−2)​=gI​⋅τgJ​ are the covariant components, equivalently τ=TIJ(−2)​gI⊗gJ. They describe the same spatial Kirchhoff stress tensor in a deforming, generally nonorthonormal coordinate net.

(10)

Apply the divergence theorem and use the reference momentum conservation equation ρ0​v˙i​=PIi,I​+ρ0​gi​. In the total-energy equation, the kinetic derivative cancels the body-force work and the vi​PIi,I​ term. Since vi,I​=F˙iI​, the remaining local Lagrangian internal energy balance and Lagrangian entropy inequality are
ρ0​u˙=ρ0​r−qI,I0​+PIi​F˙iI​​,ρ0​η˙​≥θρ0​r​−(θqI0​​),I​​.
(11)
They hold pointwise because B0​ was arbitrary. In particular,
ρ0​θη˙​≥ρ0​r−qI,I0​+θqI0​θ,I​​.
(12)
The specific Helmholtz free energy is ψ=u−θη. Substitution yields its balance
ρ0​(ψ˙​+ηθ˙+θη˙​)=ρ0​r−qI,I0​+PIi​F˙iI​,

and elimination of the heat supply yields the Clausius-Duhem inequality
PIi​F˙iI​−ρ0​(ψ˙​+ηθ˙)−θqI0​θ,I​​≥0.

For ψ=ψ(F,θ,ξr​), expand the derivative to obtain

PIi​=ρ0​ψ,FiI​​,η=−ψ,θ​​,ρ0​fr​ξ˙​r​−θqI0​θ,I​​≥0​.

Internal-variable evolution and heat conduction must satisfy this remaining inequality. Substituting the constitutive relations back into the energy balance gives
ρ0​θη˙​=ρ0​r−qI,I0​+ρ0​fr​ξ˙​r​​.
(17)
The last printed equation is missing a factor ρ0​ on its internal-variable term. The PDF defines fr​=−ψ,ξr​​ with ψ per unit mass, so the expression above is the dimensionally consistent consequence. Equivalently define the volumetric thermodynamic force conjugate to an internal variable f​r​=−ρ0​ψ,ξr​​; the term is then f​r​ξ˙​r​ with no extra density. The two conventions must not be mixed.
For thermoelasticity with a temperature-dependent constraint, admissible rates obey
ϕ,FiI​​F˙iI​=h′(θ)θ˙.
(18)
The reversible coefficient functional need now vanish only on this tangent hyperplane. Hence it can be a scalar multiple q of the constraint normal:
PIi​−ρ0​ψ,FiI​​=qϕ,FiI​​,−ρ0​(ψ,θ​+η)=−qh′(θ).
(19)
Therefore
PIi​=ρ0​ψ,FiI​​+qϕ,FiI​​,η=−ψ,θ​+ρ0​q​h′(θ)​.
(20)
The reaction terms cancel on admissible rates, leaving the same internal-variable/conduction inequality and entropy heating equation. The multiplier q is determined by equilibrium and boundary conditions rather than by the free energy alone.
For linear thermoelasticity with prescribed thermal volume, write F=I+H, Hij​=ui,j​, and ϑ=θ−θ0​. Hold the internal variables fixed in this reversible linearization, and evaluate all derivatives below at (I,θ0​) and zero reaction. The stress-free reference state has ψ,F​=0. Since ∂detF/∂F=I there, first-order nominal and Cauchy stress tensors agree. Their constitutive laws become
σji​=Cjilk​uk,l​+βji​ϑ+qδji​​,ρ0​(η−η0​)=Ce​ϑ−βji​ui,j​+qh′(θ0​)​,
(21)
where equality of mixed free-energy derivatives gives
Cjilk​=ρ0​ψ,Fij​Fkl​​,βji​=ρ0​ψ,Fij​θ​,Ce​=−ρ0​ψ,θθ​​.
(22)
The linearized constraint is ui,i​=h′(θ0​)ϑ. Thus volume is thermally prescribed; strict temperature-independent incompressibility corresponds to h′=0. The coefficient Ce​ is an entropy slope; the corresponding volumetric heat capacity at fixed deformation is θ0​Ce​.
Together with ρ0​u¨i​=σji,j​+ρ0​gi​, the first-order thermal balance is θ0​(Ce​ϑ˙−βji​u˙i,j​+h′(θ0​)q˙​)=ρ0​r−qI,I0​. A heat-flux constitutive law completes the evolution problem. The paired signs of the coupling tensor follow from the same free energy, rather than from separately postulated mechanical and thermal equations.

For fixed nominal boundary tractions and dead body forces, use the total potential
Π[x]=∫Ω​W(F)dX−∫Ω​ρ0​gi​xi​dX−∫∂ΩT​​Ti​xi​dA0​.

The dead-load terms are linear in the deformation map. Let x(ε) be a constraint-preserving perturbation path through equilibrium, with H=δF and K=δ2F. Differentiating ϕ(F)=0 twice gives ϕ,F​:H=0 and ϕ,F​:K=−ϕ,FF​[H,H]. Equilibrium has nominal stress tensor PIi​=W,FiI​​+qϕ,FiI​​. Its virtual-work relation, applied to δ2x, gives the otherwise remaining first-derivative contribution Π′[δ2x]=−∫qϕ,F​:KdX. Therefore the constrained second variation is
Q[H]=∫Ω​(W,FiI​FjJ​​+qϕ,FiI​FjJ​​)HiI​HjJ​dX​.
(32)
For a time-dependent linearized perturbation y, the incremental reaction stress contains δqϕ,F​, but its power vanishes because ϕ,F​:∇y˙​=0. Multiply the incremental momentum equation by y˙​, integrate by parts, and use zero incremental dead-load traction or the prescribed displacement condition. One obtains
dtd​{21​∫Ω​ρ0​∣y˙​∣2dX+21​Q[∇y]}=0.
(33)
Positive Q is therefore the standard constrained second-variation stability in elasticity criterion: perturbations cannot grow in this quadratic energy norm. A statement in a specified displacement norm also uses the usual coercivity and boundary conditions removing uncontrolled rigid-motion modes; translations with δF=0 are not tested by the displayed condition. This is a small-perturbation energy argument, not a claim of global nonlinear stability.
For the cube use incompressibility in the form ϕ=detF−1. A neo-Hookean solid has W,F​=μF, and hence the spatial-index-first transpose of nominal stress is μF+qF−T. On the diagonal family, equality to TI gives
T=μλ+qλ−1=μλ−1/2+qλ1/2.
(34)
The first relation immediately gives q=Tλ−μλ2​. Eliminating q and factoring yields
(1−λ−3/2)[μ(λ+λ−1/2)−T]=0.
(35)
Thus λ=1orT/μ=λ+λ−1/2​.
Let t=T/μ and h(λ)=λ+λ−1/2. Its derivative h′=1−21​λ−3/2 vanishes only at λc​=2−2/3, where h′′>0 and hc​=32−2/3. Also h→∞ at both ends of (0,∞). Therefore h(λ)=t has two distinct positive roots for t>hc​, one repeated root at hc​, and none below. There is an exception to the printed assertion that both roots differ from one: at t=2, one root is exactly λ=1. Indeed, with x=λ​, the equation becomes (x−1)(x2+x−1)=0, so the only other positive stretch is λ=(3−5​)/2.
Evaluate the stability form directly on the prescribed uniform perturbation
H=δλdiag(1,−21​λ−3/2,−21​λ−3/2).
(36)
It satisfies tr(F−1H)=0. The determinant Hessian identity is
(detF),FF​[H,H]=J{[tr(F−1H)]2−tr[(F−1H)2]}=−23​λ−2(δλ)2.
(37)
Meanwhile W,FF​[H,H]=μ(1+21​λ−3)(δλ)2. For the unit reference cube,
Q=[μ(1+21​λ−3)−2λ23q​](δλ)2.
(38)
On the trivial branch, q=T−μ and
Q=23​μ(2−t)(δλ)2​.
(39)
Thus the undeformed state is stable in this perturbation family for t<2, unstable for t>2, and quadratically neutral at t=2.
On a nontrivial branch, substitute t=h(λ) and put z=λ−3/2:
Q=2μ​(z−1)(z−2)(δλ)2​.
(40)
The smaller root always has λ<λc​, hence z>2, and is stable. For hc​<t<2, the larger root lies between λc​ and one, so 1<z<2 and it is unstable; the trivial branch is the second stable equilibrium. For t>2, the larger root lies above one and has 0<z<1, so it is stable; the trivial branch is now unstable. Whenever there are three distinct equilibria in this family, precisely two are stable and one unstable against the specified uniform perturbations. This does not establish stability to every nonuniform or symmetry-breaking perturbation.
The homogeneous tensile bifurcation of a neo-Hookean cube has a nontrivial-branch fold at t=32−2/3 and a meeting with the undeformed branch at t=2​. The latter is the bifurcation from the undeformed state. Stating both values distinguishes this branch intersection from the earlier creation of the two nontrivial solutions.

In infinitesimal elasticity and plasticity in continuum mechanics, decompose the symmetric strain as e=ee+ep. For incompressible isotropic elasticity, σ′=2Gee, with G the shear modulus and both strains traceless. The associated flow rule for the Von Mises yield criterion has e˙p=Λσ′ after absorbing a positive factor into Λ≥0. Plane strain has e33​=0, so
0=e˙33e​+e˙33p​=2Gσ˙33′​​+Λσ33′​.
(53)
If σ33′​=0 initially, it remains zero by this homogeneous scalar differential equation. Then e33e​=0 and e˙33p​=0, so e33p​=0 is consistent with zero initial plastic component. This proves consistency, not a claim that arbitrary prestressed plane-strain histories must have zero separate components. The out-of-plane shear components can consistently be zero by the same argument.
Write p=−trσ/3, so σ33​=−p. The proposed in-plane parameterization has
σ′=k​sin2ϕ−cos2ϕ0​−cos2ϕ−sin2ϕ0​000​​.
(54)
Its squared contraction is 2k2(sin22ϕ+cos22ϕ)=2k2, proving the yield condition identically. Its principal deviatoric stresses are k,−k,0. Define the two slip line families by tangent unit vectors
a=(cosϕ,sinϕ),b=(−sinϕ,cosϕ).
(55)
Thus α lines have tangent angle ϕ and β lines have tangent angle ϕ+π/2. Their normal extensions from the associated plastic strain rate vanish, while their shear stresses have maximum magnitude k; they are not the principal stress directions.
In the absence of body force, differentiate the stress parameterization in the two equilibrium equations. One obtains
p,1​=2k(cos2ϕϕ,1​+sin2ϕϕ,2​),p,2​=2k(sin2ϕϕ,1​−cos2ϕϕ,2​).
(56)
The symmetric matrix multiplying 2k∇ϕ has eigenvectors a,b with eigenvalues +1,−1. Taking its contraction with these vectors proves
a⋅∇(p−2kϕ)=0,b⋅∇(p+2kϕ)=0.
(57)
Hence the Hencky stress relations are
p−2kϕ=constant on each α line,p+2kϕ=constant on each β line​.
(58)
Constants can differ between lines, and these signs depend on the specified negative shear-stress convention.
Place the notch tip at the origin, with the opening towards negative x1​ and tensile loading in the transverse direction. For a usual reentrant notch, 0≤γ≤π/2. The upper face has tangent angle ϑf​=π−γ. Choose its tangent t=(cosϑf​,sinϑf​) directed away from the tip and outward material normal n=(−sinϑf​,cosϑf​) pointing into the notch. Direct rotation of the stress gives the normal and tangential traction components
tn​=n⋅σn=−p−ksin(2ϕ−2ϑf​),tt​=t⋅σn=−kcos(2ϕ−2ϑf​)​.
(59)
Take the notch faces traction-free, as intended by the notch problem. Then the cosine vanishes. Select the tensile branch sin(2ϕ−2ϑf​)=1, for which the face-tangential normal stress is 2k. Thus the upper face has p=−k, ϕf​=π/4−γ(modπ)​. Choosing the opposite branch would describe compression rather than the tensile field.
Construct the centred slip-line fan at a traction-free V-notch to connect this surface state to the forward symmetry axis. Near the upper face there is a constant-stress region. It joins a fan bounded by rays
ϑ1​=π/4,ϑ2​=3π/4−γ.
(60)
Inside the fan choose β lines radial and α lines circular about the tip, so their angle parameter is ϕ=ϑ−π/2. Along a circular α line the first invariant gives
p(ϑ)=−k+2k[ϕ(ϑ)−ϕf​]=−k+2k[ϑ−(3π/4−γ)].
(61)
The second invariant is automatically constant along each radial β line because p,ϕ are independent of radius. Thus this is an actual equilibrium fan, not just an assumed transport of a surface value. At ϑ2​ it matches the free-face constant state. At ϑ1​ it matches a forward constant-stress region with
ϕ=−π/4,p=−k(1+π−2γ).
(62)
Reflect the upper field across the symmetry axis to obtain the lower field. The forward region has zero shear, respects this symmetry, and contains the positive x1​ axis. There sin2ϕ=−1, and hence
σ22​=−p+k=k(2+π−2γ)​.
(63)
This value holds in the fully plastic notch-tip region described by the field. It is not a uniform stress assertion for the entire axis of an arbitrary finite specimen, including any elastic or far-field region.
