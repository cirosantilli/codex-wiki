Use βT​=1/(kB​T) for inverse temperature, to distinguish it from the order-parameter critical exponent β. In a translation-invariant pure thermodynamic phase, the connected two-point correlation function is
G(r)=⟨σ0​σr​⟩−⟨σ0​⟩⟨σr​⟩=⟨σ0​σr​⟩−M2.
(15)
For finite correlation length, at ∣r∣≫ξ it decays exponentially, possibly with an algebraic prefactor; ξ−1 is the asymptotic exponential decay rate. Below an ordered transition the pure phase is selected by taking the thermodynamic limit before h→0+ or h→0−. A zero-field symmetric mixture of the two ordered phases has a nondecaying connected term and does not describe the usual pure-phase magnetic susceptibility.
Let F=−kB​TlogZ denote extensive physical free energy and S=∑r​σr​. Since the field contributes −hS to the statistical Hamiltonian, differentiation gives
M=−N1​∂h∂F​=N⟨S⟩​,χ=∂h∂M​=−N1​∂h2∂2F​=NβT​​Var(S).
(16)
Expanding the variance and using translation invariance proves the correlation-function susceptibility sum rule:
χ=βT​r∑​G(r).​
(17)
The sum includes r=0. If the source is instead defined as the dimensionless quantity βT​h, the corresponding derivative omits the displayed inverse-temperature factor.
A real-space renormalization group step of length factor b>1 uses a local blocking kernel Bb​(σ′,σ)≥0 satisfying ∑σ′​Bb​(σ′,σ)=1. For example, a deterministic majority-spin rule assigns one coarse Ising spin to each block; tied blocks can be resolved with equal probabilities. Define the blocked statistical Hamiltonian by
e−βT​[H(u′,σ′)+N′C′]=∑σ​Bb​(σ′,σ)e−βT​[H(u,σ)+NC],N′=b−DN.
(18)
The spacing becomes a′=ba. All generated interactions, including multispin terms and the field-independent constant, must be retained for an exact transformation. Summing over σ′ uses normalization to prove Z(u′,C′,N′)=Z(u,C,N). Iteration gives Np​=b−pDN and Z(up​,Cp​,Np​)=Z(u0​,C0​,N).
The coarse probability law is the exact marginal law of these block variables. Indeed, for any coarse observable A, its expected value is the microscopic expected value of ∑σ′​Bb​(σ′,σ)A(σ′). A blocking rule that retains the long-wavelength order-parameter mode, with source and observable transformations tracked, therefore retains long-distance response and correlation functions. Their field normalization may change, while their physical length scales and critical singularities do not. Partition-function equality alone would not justify this assertion for an arbitrary kernel that discards all slow variables. Truncating the generated operator family is likewise an approximation.
For the free-energy recursion use dimensionless free energy per site,
F(u,C)=−N1​logZ(u,C,N)=f(u)+βT​C,
(19)
in the thermodynamic limit. Exact partition-function invariance gives F(u0​,C0​)=b−pDF(up​,Cp​). If one step sends C to C′=bDC+c(u), the coefficient c(u) records the identity-operator contribution from eliminated fluctuations and normalization. Consequently
f(u)=b−Df(u′)+g(u),g(u)=βT​b−Dc(u)=βT​(b−DC′−C).
(20)
Repeated substitution derives the additive free-energy recursion under blocking:
f(u0​)=b−pDf(up​)+j=0∑p−1​b−jDg(uj​).​
(21)
In physical energy units, divide this convention by βT​. The additive term is essential for absolute free energies even when it does not affect the leading critical powers.
A renormalization-group fixed point satisfies Rb​(u∗​)=u∗​. Linearize in scaling coordinates vi​ so that vi′​=byi​vi​+O(v2). A relevant operator has yi​>0, an irrelevant operator has yi​<0, and a marginal operator has yi​=0 with behaviour decided by nonlinear terms. Thus the eigenvalues of the discrete linear map are byi​; the sign criterion applies to the generator exponents yi​, not to the discrete multipliers. The critical surface is the stable manifold on which every relevant scaling field has been tuned away. Irrelevant coordinates flow to the renormalization-group fixed point along it. A repulsive renormalization-group trajectory leaves the renormalization-group fixed point along an unstable relevant direction, ultimately entering a noncritical phase or another scaling regime.
To separate the singular free-energy density from its regular background, suppose the eliminated-mode contribution g is analytic near the renormalization-group fixed point. Look for an analytic background a(u) satisfying a(u)=b−Da(Rb​u)+g(u). Then Fs​=f−a satisfies Fs​(u)=b−DFs​(Rb​u). In linear thermal and field coordinates, an analytic monomial gmn​tmhn has background coefficient
amn​=1−bmλt​+nλh​−Dgmn​​,
(22)
provided the denominator is nonzero. This explicitly explains when analytic subtraction of an inhomogeneous renormalization recursion works. The printed inhomogeneous equation may describe the free-energy function before this subtraction, or a singular part defined only up to analytic terms. The inhomogeneous sum need not be small compared with the full free energy: an analytic constant can dominate a vanishing singular contribution. It can be omitted in deriving leading singular powers after regular subtraction, provided no renormalization-group free-energy resonance, marginal logarithm or dangerously irrelevant coupling obstructs the homogeneous critical asymptotics. At a renormalization-group free-energy resonance mλt​+nλh​=D, additive logarithms can remain, so simply dropping the term would miss them.
Suppose there are exactly two relevant scaling fields, thermal t and magnetic h, with positive exponents λt​,λh​, and the remaining fields approach a regular limit. Physical reduced temperature and magnetic field agree with these coordinates to leading order, up to nonuniversal metric factors. The homogeneous scaling hypothesis for critical phenomena is
Fs​(t,h)=ℓ−DFs​(ℓλt​t,ℓλh​h),ℓ>0,
(23)
as an asymptotic scaling equation. Choose ℓ=∣t∣−1/λt​ to make the thermal argument +1 or −1. Defining f±​(z)=Fs​(±1,z) gives
Fs​(t,h)=∣t∣D/λt​f±​(∣t∣λh​/λt​h​).​
(24)
The two functions describe respectively t>0 and t<0: approaching the transition from disordered and ordered sides need not give the same function. In particular the ordered zero-field function has one-sided derivatives associated with spontaneous magnetization. Metric factors multiply this formula in different microscopic normalizations.
The correlation length in Bravais lattice units obeys ξ(t,h)=ℓξ(ℓλt​t,ℓλh​h). At h=0, the same choice of ℓ gives ν=1/λt​. Two thermal derivatives of Fs​(t,0) give 2−α=D/λt​. One and two field derivatives give, respectively, β=(D−λh​)/λt​ and γ=(2λh​−D)/λt​, assuming the relevant branch amplitudes are nonzero. At t=0, choose ℓ=∣h∣−1/λh​; then M∝sgn(h)∣h∣(D−λh​)/λh​, so δ=λh​/(D−λh​). Hence
ν=λt​1​,α=2−λt​D​,β=λt​D−λh​​,γ=λt​2λh​−D​,δ=D−λh​λh​​.​
(25)
Simple algebra now gives the Rushbrooke scaling relation α+2β+γ=2 and the hyperscaling relation α=2−Dν. Also βδ=λh​/λt​=β+γ, giving the Widom scaling relation. These statements refer to leading singular contributions with the homogeneous two-field scaling assumptions. Above the upper critical dimension, the quartic coupling is dangerously irrelevant because it is still needed to stabilize the ordered phase; ordinary mean-field critical exponents then satisfy Rushbrooke and Widom but generally not hyperscaling. Marginal logarithms also require qualifications at the upper critical dimension.
For the connected-correlation scaling form G(r)=∣r∣−(D−2+η)fG​(∣r∣/ξ), the function fG​(x) tends to a finite nonzero value as x→0 and decays exponentially at large x, up to powers of x, in a massive short-range phase. The scaling ansatz alone does not fix that prefactor. If the usual massive Gaussian field correlation tail G(r)∝r−(D−1)/2e−r/ξ applies, matching the powers gives fG​(x)∼Bx(D−3)/2+ηe−x, with a correlation-length-dependent amplitude in G fixed by matching the critical scaling form.
In physical coordinates the lattice sum has site-density factor a−D. Its long-distance singular part therefore gives
χs​≃βT​a−DΩD−1​∫a∞​r1−ηfG​(r/ξ)dr=βT​a−DΩD−1​ξ2−η∫a/ξ∞​x1−ηfG​(x)dx,
(26)
where ΩD−1​ is the area of the unit sphere in D dimensions. For η<2, finite fG​(0), an integrable exponential tail and a nonzero integral, the last factor tends to a finite nonzero constant as ξ→∞. Microscopic distances contribute a regular background. Thus χs​∝ξ2−η∝∣t∣−(2−η)ν, proving the Fisher scaling relation
γ=(2−η)ν.​
(27)
Equivalently the field has scaling dimension (D−2+η)/2, so its conjugate field has λh​=(D+2−η)/2, which gives the same identity from the exponent formulas above.
