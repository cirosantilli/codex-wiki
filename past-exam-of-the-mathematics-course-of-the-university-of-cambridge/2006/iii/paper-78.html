Use the Newtonian fluid stress tensor σ=−pI+2μe, where e=(∇u+∇uT)/2. The Stokes equations for a point force on the fluid are ∇⋅u=0 and ∇⋅σ+Fδ(x)=0. Put r=∣x∣. Away from the origin, a harmonic vector potential proportional to F/r in the Unscaled Papkovich–Neuber representation gives the rotationally covariant decaying solution. Specifically, take Φ=−F/(8πμr) and χ=0. Differentiation yields
ui​=Jij​Fj​,Jij​(x)=8πμ1​(rδij​​+r3xi​xj​​),p=4πr3F⋅x​.​
(1)
This satisfies the homogeneous Stokes equations for r>0. Its stress is
σij​=Kkij​Fk​,Kkij​(x)=−4πr53xk​xi​xj​​.​
(2)
To verify the point-force normalization, on a sphere r=ε the traction is σn=−3(F⋅n)n/(4πε2). Since ∫S2​ni​nj​dΩ=4πδij​/3, its surface integral is −F. Thus integrating momentum balance over the small ball gives exactly the applied force F, fixing the normalization of the Stokeslet. Its radial velocity is (F⋅n)/(4πμr), so
∫∣x∣=r​u⋅ndS=4πμr​F⋅∫S2​ndΩ=0.​
(3)
There is no net volume or mass source at the singularity. The kernels have the parity J(−x)=J(x) and K(−x)=−K(x).
For two Stokes flows with the same viscosity and body forces f(1),f(2), the Lorentz reciprocal theorem including body forces is
∫∂V​(u(1)⋅σ(2)n−u(2)⋅σ(1)n)dS=∫V​(u(2)⋅f(1)−u(1)⋅f(2))dV.​
(4)
Indeed, apply the divergence theorem to ui(1)​σij(2)​−ui(2)​σij(1)​. The cross-gradient terms cancel: incompressibility removes each pressure contraction and the remaining terms are both 2μe(1):e(2). The two stress divergences give the body-force terms shown.
Choose flow 1 to be the desired body-force-free field and flow 2 to be a Stokeslet with arbitrary force vector q at y. For y∈V, the body-force term is −q⋅u(y). Rearrange the reciprocal identity and use the parity of J,K; equality for every q gives
ui​(y)=∫∂V​Jij​(y−x)σjk​(x)nk​dS+∫∂V​uj​(x)Kijk​(y−x)nk​dS.​
(5)
For y outside the volume there is no point force in V, so the same two integrals sum to zero. To obtain the smooth boundary value, exclude a small hemisphere about y, use continuity of u to replace it by u(y) on that hemisphere, and take the radius to zero. A full small sphere contributes u(y); a hemisphere contributes half of it. Equivalently, the local solid-angle factor is 2π/(4π). The result is the boundary integral representation of Stokes flow with right side 21​u(y) on a smooth boundary, and with the double layer interpreted by its boundary principal value. A nonsmooth corner would have a different solid-angle coefficient.

Let n point out of the drop, and use − for the interior and + for the exterior. The interfacial velocity is continuous. Define κ=∇s​⋅n; for a sphere of radius a it is 2/a. With constant surface tension, the interfacial stress balance with variable surface tension reduces to
(σ+−σ−)n=γκn.
(6)
Use the exterior-viscosity kernels to define S[t]=∫J(y−x)t(x)dSx​ and D[u]=∫u(x)K(y−x)n(x)dSx​. The interior viscosity is λμ, so its velocity kernel is J/λ, while its stress kernel K is unchanged. The interior boundary equation is
2λ​u=S[σ−n]+λD[u].
(7)
For the decaying exterior flow, the inner boundary's fluid-domain normal is −n, so
21​u=−S[σ+n]−D[u].
(8)
Add these equations and use the traction jump. The required capillary boundary integral equation for a viscous drop is
21+λ​u(y)=−γ∫∂V​J(y−x)κ(x)n(x)dSx​+(λ−1)∫∂V​u(x)K(y−x)n(x)dSx​.​
(9)
The formula uses the drop-outward normal consistently in both integrals. If λ=1, the double-layer term cancels. A spherical drop of constant curvature has zero velocity: a constant Laplace-pressure jump is balanced without motion. A prescribed ambient flow would add its known far-boundary contribution; none is present for the surface-tension-driven flow here.

The fluid is outside the body, so its normal on the inner boundary is −n′. Let t=σn′ be traction on the body's surface from the fluid. For an exterior point and a flow vanishing at infinity, the boundary representation reads
ui​(y)=−∫Jij​(y−x)tj​(x)dSx​−∫uj​(x)Kijk​(y−x)nk′​(x)dSx​.
(10)
For r=∣y∣≫a, expand the single layer through its first moment and the double layer through its zeroth moment:
ui​=Jij​(y)Fj​+∂α​Jiβ​(y)Σαβ​−Kijk​(y)Ujk​+O(r−3),F=−∫tdS.​
(11)
The next single-layer and double-layer terms are both O(r−3) at fixed body size and boundary data. Differentiating J gives
∂α​Jiβ​=8πμ1​[r3−δiβ​yα​+δiα​yβ​+δαβ​yi​​−r53yi​yα​yβ​​].
(12)
Hence the dipole terms combine into
8πμ1​[r3(Σ−ΣT)y+TrΣy​−r53(y⋅My)y​],M=Σ−2μU.
(13)
The antisymmetric part obeys (Σ−ΣT)y=G×y for Gj​=−ϵjαβ​Σαβ​. For the symmetric trace-free tensor S, y⋅My=y⋅Sy+(TrM)r2/3. Also TrΣ−TrM=2μTrU=2μQ. Substitution gives
u(y)=J(y)F+8πμr3G×y​+4πr3Qy​−8πμr53(y⋅Sy)y​+O(r−3).​
(14)
This derives the Stokes far-field multipoles of a deforming body. F is the force exerted by the body on the fluid, the negative of the integrated fluid traction. G is its torque on the fluid: G=−∫x×tdS. Their kernels are the Stokeslet and rotlet. Q=∫u⋅n′dS is the rate of increase of the body's volume, by the Reynolds transport theorem. It gives a radial source flow with volume flux Q. A volume-preserving body has Q=0. The trace-free tensor S gives the stresslet, which can remain nonzero for a force-free, torque-free swimmer.

For body-force-free Stokes fields with the same viscosity,
∫∂V​u(1)⋅σ(2)ndS=∫∂V​u(2)⋅σ(1)ndS.​
(15)
To prove this Lorentz reciprocal theorem, integrate the divergence of the difference of the two cross-work fluxes. Both stress divergences are zero; incompressibility and symmetry of the Newtonian stress reduce each cross-gradient contraction to 2μe(1):e(2). These are equal, so the divergence integrates to zero. The argument applies to an exterior domain by first cutting it off and taking the outer radius to infinity; the decaying velocity and stress make the outer boundary contribution vanish.
Define the generalized force as force and torque exerted by the rigid body on the fluid. With a resting far field and no-slip boundary conditions, u=U+Ω×x on the body, and linearity gives
(GF​)=R(ΩU​).
(16)
For two such motions the reciprocal theorem becomes
U(1)⋅F(2)+Ω(1)⋅G(2)=U(2)⋅F(1)+Ω(2)⋅G(1).
(17)
Since the six-component velocities can be chosen independently, R=RT​. The ordinary work identity gives
U⋅F+Ω⋅G=2μ∫Vfluid​​e:edV>0​
(18)
for every nonzero rigid-body motion. If the integral were zero, e=0 would make the connected exterior flow an infinitesimal rigid motion. Its decay at infinity requires that motion to be zero, which is incompatible with a nonzero no-slip body velocity. Thus R is positive definite. Fluid-on-body forces have the opposite sign; their map is −R, not a positive-definite resistance matrix.
Write sϕ​=sinϕ and cϕ​=cosϕ. The helix has ∣dX/dθ∣=b1+tan2ϕ​=bsecϕ, so
ds=bsecϕdθ,L=Nπbsecϕ.​
(19)
Its unit tangent is X′=cϕ​eθ​+sϕ​ez​, where eθ​=(−sinθ,cosθ,0). The given slender-body force density is the force per arc length exerted on the fluid. For pure axial translation, V=Uez​, it gives
fz​=CU(1−sϕ2​/2),fθ​=−CUsϕ​cϕ​/2.
(20)
For pure rotation, V=bΩeθ​, it gives
fz​=−CbΩsϕ​cϕ​/2,fθ​=CbΩ(1−cϕ2​/2).
(21)
The axial torque per unit arc length is bfθ​. Integrating along the wire gives the axial resistance matrix of a slender helix
(Gz​Fz​​)=(AB​BD​)(ΩU​),​
(22)
A=2CL​(1+cϕ2​),B=−2CLb​sϕ​cϕ​,D=2CLb2​(1+sϕ2​).​
(23)
Thus translation gives (Fz​,Gz​)=(AU,BU), and rotation gives (BΩ,DΩ). These are the requested axial components; the axial swimmer approximation does not require proving statements about the other components. The equality of the two off-diagonal coefficients checks reciprocity, and
AD−B2=2C2L2b2​>0.
(24)
For the organism, retain the specified entrainment approximation: the head moves with the local large-scale fluid translation, and its angular velocity Ω−ω differs from the local fluid rotation Ω by −ω. Put D0​=8πμa3. Its torque on the fluid is therefore −D0​ω, and its force is neglected. Neutral buoyancy and absence of external torque give
AU+BΩ=0,BU+DΩ=D0​ω.
(25)
Solving,
U=−AD−B2BD0​ω​=CLbD0​ωsϕ​cϕ​​=bLωa3∣logϵ∣sin2ϕ​​.
(26)
This is the entrained-head approximation for a helical microswimmer, which differs from treating the head as an isolated sphere in otherwise stationary fluid. The flagellum's angular speed is also Ω=D0​ω(1+cϕ2​)/(CLb2).
At fixed b,L,a,ω,ϵ, the speed is maximal at ϕ=π/4 and tends to zero at either limiting pitch. At fixed other parameters it decreases as 1/L: the longer flagellum has more resistance at the fixed torque supplied in this model. Holding the number of turns fixed instead would make L change with pitch, so that is a different comparison.
The motor exerts equal and opposite torques of magnitude D0​ω on flagellum and head, whose relative angular speed is ω. Therefore its rate of working is
P=D0​ω2=8πμa3ω2.​
(27)
Equivalently, the work delivered to the fluid is Gflag​Ω+Ghead​(Ω−ω)=D0​ωΩ−D0​ω(Ω−ω). Translation contributes no net work because the total force vanishes. This explains the independence from U and Ω, without assigning an isolated-fluid drag law to the entrained head.

The material derivative follows surfactant carried by the moving interface. The term −C∇s​⋅us​ is dilution or concentration by tangential area expansion or contraction. The term −C(u⋅n)∇s​⋅n is the additional area change produced by normal motion of a curved interface. The term Ds​∇s2​C represents surface diffusion, while −k(C−C0​) relaxes the concentration towards a reservoir value through exchange. All these terms refer to concentration per surface area, as in surfactant transport with exchange relaxation.
In the steady bubble frame, the spherical interface has u⋅n=0. Write C=C0​+C′ with uniform C0​ and regard both u and C′ as first order in the rising speed. Advection of C′ and its product with surface dilation are second order. Hence the linear steady equation is
Ds​∇s2​C′−kC′=C0​∇s​⋅us​.​
(28)
Spherical symmetry and linearity leave only the tangential projection of the imposed translation vector: us​=AIs​U. An azimuthal term n×U would change sign under reflection and is excluded for this non-chiral rising bubble. The degree-one forcing does not excite other spherical-harmonic degrees in the linear problem.
On r=a, differentiating n=x/r gives
(∇s​n)ij​=aδij​−ni​nj​​=a(Is​)ij​​,∇s​⋅n=a2​.​
(29)
Each component of n is a degree-one spherical harmonic, so
∇s2​n=−a22n​.​
(30)
One may also obtain the coefficient from n⋅n=1: its surface Laplacian gives n⋅∇s2​n=−∣∇s​n∣2=−2/a2, while rotational symmetry makes the vector Laplacian parallel to n. Expanding Is​U=U−n(U⋅n) then yields
∇s​⋅(Is​U)=−a2​U⋅n.​
(31)
The candidate C′=BU⋅n therefore solves the linear transport equation if
−(k+a22Ds​​)B=−a2AC0​​,B=ka2+2Ds​2AC0​a​.​
(32)
Its spherical mean is zero. This is the dipolar surfactant distribution. Assuming ka2+2Ds​>0, its consistency condition is
C0​max∣C′∣​=ka2+2Ds​2∣A∣∣U∣a​≪1.​
(33)
Diffusion or exchange must restore uniform concentration faster than the interfacial flow redistributes it: this holds with a small surface Péclet number, fast exchange, or sufficiently immobilized surface velocity. These same conditions justify neglecting the perturbation's advection. Small capillary number and sufficiently small buoyancy-induced shape distortion are separate requirements for a spherical interface.
Take + to mean the outer liquid, − the bubble interior and n outward from the bubble. The general interfacial stress balance with variable surface tension is
(σ+−σ−)n=γκn−∇s​γ,κ=∇s​⋅n.​
(34)
For γ=γ0​−γ1​C′, its tangential part is −∇s​γ=γ1​BIs​U/a. Thus the requested form is
[Is​σn]−+​=a6μAλ​Is​U,λ=3μ(ka2+2Ds​)γ1​C0​a​.​
(35)
This positive parameter measures the strength of the restoring Marangoni stress relative to viscous stress, including the relaxation of concentration by diffusion and exchange.
The far velocity −U is produced by the constant harmonic vector potential Φ=U. The two independent decaying degree-one modes are the force-carrying vector potential αaU/r and the scalar dipole χ=βa3U⋅∇(1/r). They are harmonic outside the bubble and have precisely the symmetry of uniform translation. The Unscaled Papkovich–Neuber representation gives, at arbitrary radius,
u=−(1+rαa​+r3βa3​)U+(−rαa​+r33βa3​)(U⋅n)n,p=−r22μαa​U⋅n.
(36)
The velocity at r=a agrees with the printed expression. However, the original PDF's traction formula has a factor-of-two error. Direct differentiation with σ=−pI+μ(∇u+∇uT) gives
σn=a6μ​{βU+(α−3β)(U⋅n)n},​
(37)
not the printed prefactor 12μ/a. This is traction of translating-sphere Papkovich–Neuber potentials. The α term follows immediately from the Stokeslet stress, and the scalar-potential part gives 6μβ[U−3(U⋅n)n]/a; neither contribution contains the extra factor two.
With this consistent traction, no penetration requires β−α=1/2, tangential velocity requires A=−(1+α+β), and tangential stress balance requires β=Aλ. Solving,
A=−2(1+2λ)1​,β=−2(1+2λ)λ​,α=−2(1+2λ)1+3λ​.​
(38)
For comparison, taking the printed 12μ/a literally would instead give
Aprinted​=−2(1+λ)1​,βprinted​=−4(1+λ)λ​,αprinted​=−4(1+λ)2+3λ​.
(39)
Those solve the supplied algebraic pair but not the Newtonian stress calculated from the supplied potentials. The factor-six result is the physically consistent translating surfactant-coated bubble solution.
As λ→0, A→−1/2 and β→0: the clean bubble has a mobile interface and zero tangential traction. As λ→∞, A→0, α→−3/4 and β→−1/4: the interface becomes immobilized and the exterior flow approaches that past a no-slip rigid sphere. The limiting tangential traction is −3μIs​U/(2a), a useful check on the correct normalization.
The normal dynamic traction is
n⋅σn=a6μ​(α−2β)U⋅n=−a(1+2λ)3μ(1+λ)​U⋅n,
(40)
apart from an arbitrary constant pressure. In the normal stress balance on a translating bubble, its degree-one part is balanced by the buoyant hydrostatic pressure difference and by the variation −2γ1​C′/a of normal capillary stress. A uniform internal gas pressure balances the constant Laplace-pressure term but cannot by itself balance this angular variation. If U is upwards and Δρ is liquid minus bubble mass density, the degree-one balance can be written
n⋅σdyn​n+Δρganz​=−a2γ1​B​Unz​.
(41)
It fixes U=Δρga2(1+2λ)/[3μ(1+3λ)], tending to the clean-bubble and rigid-sphere rise speeds. A degree-one radial displacement translates a sphere rather than changing its curvature, so a supposed dipolar shape distortion cannot replace this force balance.

Let the tangential velocity scale be U, gap scale H and along-gap length L≫H. The tangential Stokes equation balances P/L with μU/H2, while wall shear is τ∼μU/H. Therefore
P∼H2μUL​,Pτ​∼LH​≪1.​
(42)
This is lubrication pressure dominates shear stress; pressure force projections may nevertheless be small in a particular direction or may give no torque on a spherical surface.
Write the signed centre displacement along the downward axis as d=αΔ. A point an on the inner sphere has distance ∣an+dez​∣=a+dcosθ+O(Δ2/a) from the outer centre. The leading normal gap to radius a+Δ is consequently
h(θ)=Δ−dcosθ=Δ(1−αcosθ).​
(43)
The local-curvature corrections are smaller by Δ/a. This approximation also follows by solving the outer-sphere intersection along the ray n from the inner centre and expanding its positive root.

ht​+asinθ1​∂θ​(sinθqθ​)=0.

pθ​=−Δ3(1−αcosθ)36μa2Vsinθ​,p=αΔ3(1−αcosθ)23μa2V​+p∗​​.

The apparent singularity at α=0 is a constant-pressure gauge term: subtract 3μa2V/(αΔ3) before taking the limit. The remaining pressure tends to 6μa2Vcosθ/Δ3.
To leading lubrication order the force is the pressure integral. Its downward component on the inner sphere is
Fz​=−2πa2∫0π​pcosθsinθdθ=−α3Δ36πμa4V​∫−αα​(1−t)2t​dt.

Let ϵ=1−α≪1, and use local distance ρ=aθ from the closest pole. The gap is
h≃Δ(ϵ+2θ2​)=h0​+2Rρ2​,h0​=ϵΔ,R≃Δa2​.
(51)
Horizontal rotation has nonzero wall speed Vs​∼∣Ω′∣a at this pole. In the broad region with h=O(Δ), shear is O(μ∣Ω′∣a/Δ), the area is O(a2) and the lever arm is O(a). Its torque is therefore O(μ∣Ω′∣a4/Δ). The smallest gap patch has radius O(aϵ​) and area O(a2ϵ); its shear is O(μ∣Ω′∣a/(ϵΔ)). It gives the same bounded torque scale.
These are the two estimates requested in the paper. They omit an intermediate annulus, so the printed uniform O(μ∣Ω′∣a4/Δ) assertion is not correct as ϵ→0. For aϵ​≪ρ≪a, h∼Δρ2/a2. An annular shear-torque contribution scales as
dG∼ahμVs​​2πρdρ∼Δμ∣Ω′∣a4​ρdρ​.
(52)
Integrating between the inner and outer scales produces log(1/ϵ).
A viscous dissipation lower bound makes this conclusion independent of possible signs of local tractions. A fluid velocity changing by Vs​ between two no-slip walls dissipates at least μVs2​/h per unit area, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the cross-gap coordinate. The lubrication velocity profile gives more explicitly
∫0h​μ∣∂z​u∣2dz=hμ∣vs​∣2​+12μh3∣∇p∣2​,
(53)
with no cross term. The second term is nonnegative. Thus the annulus entails a logarithmic divergence in total power and hence in ∣G′∣, since the centre is held fixed and all boundary work is G′Ω′.
The horizontal rotation in a nearly touching spherical shell calculation also gives the corrected force scale. Choose local x along vs​ and put Vs​=aΩ′ with its signed value. At leading order the stationary-gap Reynolds lubrication equation is
∇⋅(h3∇p)=6μVs​∂x​h.
(54)
For h=h0​+(x2+y2)/(2R) it has the particular pressure
p=−56μVs​​h2x​,
(55)
which satisfies the equation by direct differentiation. The pressure itself gives no torque about the centre of an exactly spherical inner body, because its normal is radial. It does contribute to tangential shear through the pressure gradient.
For any fixed local outer cutoff, the logarithmically divergent integrals are
I1​=∫hdA​∼2πRlog(1/ϵ),I2​=∫h2x2​dA∼2πR2log(1/ϵ).
(56)
The resisting tangential traction is μVs​/h+(h/2)px​. Integration by parts gives torque magnitude aμ∣Vs​∣(I1​+3I2​/(5R)) to logarithmic order. Consequently, at leading order in Δ/a,
∣G′∣∼516π​Δμ∣Ω′∣a4​logϵ1​.​
(57)
The strict corrected order is Θ((μ∣Ω′∣a4/Δ)log(1/ϵ)); the paper's scale is the bounded contribution of each of its two nominated regions, not the full asymptotic answer.
For the horizontal force, pressure dominates shear by a factor a/Δ. Its horizontal projection on the inner sphere has normal component nx​≃x/a, giving ∣Fp​∣∼(6μ∣Vs​∣/(5a))I2​. Hence the required holding-force magnitude is
∣F′∣∼512π​Δ2μ∣Ω′∣a4​logϵ1​.​
(58)
The broad region and smallest patch each supply O(μ∣Ω′∣a4/Δ2); the intermediate annulus again supplies the extra logarithm. The shear force is smaller by Δ/a. These expressions assume the completely filled lubrication film of the problem.
The vertical holding force is zero. The geometry is invariant under a half-turn about the vertical axis. That operation reverses the horizontal angular velocity but leaves a vertical force component unchanged. Linearity would reverse that force component too, forcing it to vanish. Equivalently, the induced pressure has the first azimuthal harmonic, whose vertical-force integral over azimuth is zero. The horizontal holding force is the negative of the fluid's horizontal force on the body.
