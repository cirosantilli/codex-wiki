Fermat's little theorem states that, for a prime number p and any integer a,
ap≡a(modp).
(30)
If p∣a, both sides vanish modulo p. Otherwise multiplication by a permutes the nonzero residue classes modulo p: it is injective because ai≡aj implies i≡j by cancellation. Taking the products of these residues gives
ap−1(p−1)!≡(p−1)!(modp).
(31)
The factorial is coprime to p, so cancellation proves ap−1≡1(modp) and hence the stated version. This proves Fermat's little theorem in both cases.
Let n be a Carmichael number. If a prime square p2 divided n, choose a=p. Since n is an odd composite integer, n>1, and pn−p≡−p≡0(modp2). This contradicts the required modular congruence modulo n. Therefore every Carmichael number is squarefree.
For each prime divisor p of n, choose the allowed primitive root g modulo p. The Carmichael identity applied to the positive integer g gives gn≡g(modp), and cancellation gives gn−1≡1(modp). By its defining order property,
p−1∣n−1.
(32)
Conversely, suppose a composite integer n is squarefree and has this divisibility for every prime divisor. For an arbitrary integer a, modulo a prime divisor p there are two cases. If p∣a, then an≡a≡0. Otherwise Fermat's little theorem and p−1∣n−1 give an−1≡1, so again an≡a. Since the prime divisors are distinct and pairwise coprime, their product n divides an−a. This is the proof of Korselt criterion:
n is Carmichael ⟺ n is composite and squarefree, and p−1∣n−1 for all p∣n.​
(33)
If n=pq for distinct odd primes with p<q, the condition at q would require q−1∣pq−1. Reducing pq−1 modulo q−1 gives p−1, so q−1∣p−1. But 0<p−1<q−1, which is impossible. Thus a product of two distinct odd primes is not Carmichael.
For n=pqr, squarefreeness is automatic. Since p≡1(modp−1),
pqr−1≡qr−1(modp−1).
(34)
Apply the analogous reductions at q and r to obtain
pqr is Carmichael ⟺ p−1∣qr−1,q−1∣pr−1,r−1∣pq−1.​
(35)
Finally 1729=7⋅13⋅19, with distinct odd prime factors, and
13⋅19−1=246=6⋅41,7⋅19−1=132=12⋅11,7⋅13−1=90=18⋅5.
(36)
All three divisibilities hold, so 1729 is a Carmichael number.
