The logistic equation has the constant solutions x=0 and x=1. For a nonconstant solution, separation of variables gives
∫(x1​+1−x1​)dx=t+C,log​1−xx​​=t+C.
(1)
Solving and imposing the initial value yields
x(t)=1−x0​+x0​etx0​et​.​
(2)
This expression also includes the two equilibria when x0​=0 or 1. For other initial values it is taken on the maximal interval containing t=0 on which its denominator does not vanish.
The phase portrait follows from the sign of x(1−x): arrows point left for x<0, right for 0<x<1, and left for x>1. Thus zero is an unstable equilibrium point and one is asymptotically stable. The requested sketch is shown below.
If 0<x0​<1, the denominator is positive for every real t. The solution stays between the equilibria and increases monotonically, with
t→−∞lim​x(t)=0,t→+∞lim​x(t)=1.​
(3)
More precisely x∼[x0​/(1−x0​)]et in the remote past and 1−x∼[(1−x0​)/x0​]e−t in the remote future. Outside that interval there is a finite-time pole: for x0​<0 it occurs in the future, and for x0​>1 in the past, at t∗​=log[(x0​−1)/x0​]. This does not affect the globally defined trajectories requested between zero and one.

Seek a power series y=∑j≥0​aj​xj. The initial values give a0​=0 and a1​=1. Matching the coefficient of xj for j≥1 gives j(j+1)aj+1​+aj​=0. Induction yields aj​=(−1)j−1/[(j−1)!j!], hence

The first recursive approximations are y1​=x−x2/2, y2​=x−x2/2+x3/12 and y3​=x−x2/2+x3/12−x4/144. The general formula is

yn​=x−∫0x​(x−s)syn−1​(s)​ds.
(26)
Each monomial integrates by ∫0x​(x−s)skds=xk+2/[(k+1)(k+2)], producing precisely the next truncated series and proving the induction. Condition on the current generation size. By independence of offspring families, E[sZn+1​∣Zn​=k]=G(s)k. Taking the expected value gives the probability generating function recursion
Gn+1​(s)=Gn​(G(s)),G0​(s)=s.
(27)
Induction therefore proves the branching-process generating-function iteration
Gn​(s)=G∘n(s),​
(28)
where the right side means n-fold composition, not an ordinary power.
Let m=G′(1) and σ2=G′′(1)+m−m2 be the offspring mean and variance, assuming a finite second moment. Differentiating the generating-function recursion at one gives EZn​=mn and, with an​=Gn′′​(1),
an+1​=m2an​+G′′(1)mn.
(29)
Since Var(Zn​)=an​+mn−m2n, this implies
vn+1​=m2vn​+σ2mn,v0​=0.
(30)
Iteration and summation of the geometric progression give the mean and variance of a Galton-Watson generation:
Var(Zn​)=⎩⎨⎧​σ2mn−1m−1mn−1​,nσ2,​m>0, m=1, n≥1,m=1.​​
(31)
For m=0 the offspring count is identically zero, so every later generation has zero variance. The zeroth generation always has zero variance. Finiteness of the needed moments is an assumption for this finite-valued variance formula, not a consequence of merely having a probability generating function. With finite positive mean but infinite offspring variance, all positive-generation variances are infinite.
For the total count, make the generation convention explicit. If the first n generations include the ancestor, put Tn​=Z0​+⋯+Zn−1​ and Hn​(s)=E[sTn​], with T0​=0. Given k children of the ancestor, each starts an independent depth-n descendant tree with generating function Hn​. The ancestor contributes an additional factor s, so
Hn+1​(s)=sG(Hn​(s)),H0​(s)=1,H1​(s)=s.​
(32)
This is the finite-horizon total progeny generating function. If instead “first n generations” means descendants Z1​+⋯+Zn​, excluding Z0​, write Hn​ for that convention. Conditioning on the children now gives Hn+1​(s)=G(sHn​(s)), with H0​=1 and H1​=G. The two conventions encode the same tree but different counts.

Independence gives the joint probability density function fX,Y​(x,y)=4e−(x2+y2)/π on x,y>0. The inverse transformation is
x=1+v2u​​,y=v1+v2u​​,u,v>0.

Its Jacobian matrix has absolute determinant 1/[2(1+v2)]. One way to see the determinant is to use polar coordinates: u=r2, v=tanθ, with 0<θ<π/2. Then dxdy=rdrdθ=dudv/[2(1+v2)]. The change-of-variables formula for probability densities gives
fU,V​(u,v)=π2​1+v2e−u​,u,v>0,​

fU​(u)=e−u  (u>0),fV​(v)=π(1+v2)2​  (v>0).​
(35)
These are respectively a unit-rate exponential distribution and a unit-scale half-Cauchy distribution. Their product equals the joint probability density function, so U and V are independent. This radial-ratio independence of two half-normal variables results from the radial symmetry of the positive-quadrant joint probability density function.

a=P(matching endpoints)=513​=171​.​
