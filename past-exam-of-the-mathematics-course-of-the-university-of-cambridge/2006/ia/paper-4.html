A prime number is an integer p>1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and p. Every integer greater than one has a prime factorization; in particular it has a prime divisor.
To prove infinitely many primes congruent to minus one modulo six, suppose a finite list p1​,…,pn​ contains all such primes, and put P=p1​⋯pn​; the empty product is one. The integer N=6P−1>1 is coprime to 6, so each prime factor is congruent to 1 or −1 modulo 6. If all its prime factors, counted with multiplicity, were congruent to 1, their product would also be congruent to 1. But N≡−1(mod6). Hence some prime divisor q satisfies q≡−1(mod6). None of the listed primes divides N, since N≡−1(modpi​). This contradicts completeness of the list.
For infinitely many primes congruent to one modulo six, suppose instead that p1​,…,pn​ lists every prime congruent to 1 modulo 6, and use
N=(2P)2+3.
(1)
It is odd. Since each listed prime is coprime to 3, P2≡1(mod3), so N≡1(mod3) and 3 does not divide it. Any prime divisor q is therefore greater than 3 and satisfies the modular congruence
(2P)2≡−3(modq).
(2)
The allowed quadratic-congruence result gives q≡1(mod6). Yet N≡3(modpi​), and pi​>3, so no listed prime divides N. Again there is a new prime in the required residue class. Thus both residue classes 6k−1 and 6k+1 contain infinitely many primes.

Let m be the car's mass and let v(t) be its forward speed. Initially the driven contact surfaces slip backward relative to the ground because v<rΩ. Thus kinetic friction on the car acts forward. On the horizontal surface the total normal force is mg, so Newton's second law gives mv˙=μmg. While slipping persists,
v(t)=μgt.
(10)
The contact slip speed is rΩ−v(t). Its first zero is the onset of rolling without slipping, hence
T=μgrΩ​.​
(11)
The engine maintains the prescribed wheel speed, so wheel spin-down is not part of this calculation.
On a slope of angle α>0, directed uphill, the normal force is mgcosα and gravity contributes −mgsinα along the slope. Therefore the uphill acceleration while slipping is g(μcosα−sinα). If it is positive, the onset of rolling without slipping occurs at
Tα​=g(μcosα−sinα)rΩ​>T.​
(12)
The inequality follows because μcosα−sinα<μ. If μcosα≤sinα, the car does not accelerate uphill to rΩ, so there is no finite catch-up time in this sliding-friction model. The finite-time conclusion presumes positive traction sufficient to climb the slope.

An equivalence relation on A is a binary relation that is reflexive, symmetric and transitive: xRx for every x; xRy⇒yRx; and xRy, yRz⇒xRz. Write T=R∘S, using the stated order for composition of relations. Composition is associative because both possible bracketings mean that there is a two-step sequence of intermediate witnesses. Reflexivity shows
R⊆T,S⊆T,
(18)
and also that T is reflexive. Transitivity and reflexivity of the original relations give R∘R=R, S∘S=S. Since both original relations are symmetric,
T−1=(R∘S)−1=S∘R.
(19)
The four parts below show that each condition is equivalent to the commuting equivalence relations condition R∘S=S∘R.
For the integer example, put d=gcd(m,n). Membership of (x,z) in the composite means that some y satisfies y−x=ma, z−y=nb for integers a,b. Consequently z−x=ma+nb is divisible by d. Conversely, if z−x is divisible by d, the Bezout identity expresses it as ma+nb; setting y=x+ma provides the required witness. Thus
R∘S={(x,z)∈Z2:x≡z(modd)}.​
(20)
Reversing the roles of m,n gives exactly the same modular congruence relation, hence R∘S=S∘R. This verifies all four conditions and identifies their common smallest equivalence relation explicitly.

Fermat's little theorem states that, for a prime number p and any integer a,
ap≡a(modp).
(30)
If p∣a, both sides vanish modulo p. Otherwise multiplication by a permutes the nonzero residue classes modulo p: it is injective because ai≡aj implies i≡j by cancellation. Taking the products of these residues gives
ap−1(p−1)!≡(p−1)!(modp).
(31)
The factorial is coprime to p, so cancellation proves ap−1≡1(modp) and hence the stated version. This proves Fermat's little theorem in both cases.
Let n be a Carmichael number. If a prime square p2 divided n, choose a=p. Since n is an odd composite integer, n>1, and pn−p≡−p≡0(modp2). This contradicts the required modular congruence modulo n. Therefore every Carmichael number is squarefree.
For each prime divisor p of n, choose the allowed primitive root g modulo p. The Carmichael identity applied to the positive integer g gives gn≡g(modp), and cancellation gives gn−1≡1(modp). By its defining order property,
p−1∣n−1.
(32)
Conversely, suppose a composite integer n is squarefree and has this divisibility for every prime divisor. For an arbitrary integer a, modulo a prime divisor p there are two cases. If p∣a, then an≡a≡0. Otherwise Fermat's little theorem and p−1∣n−1 give an−1≡1, so again an≡a. Since the prime divisors are distinct and pairwise coprime, their product n divides an−a. This is the proof of Korselt criterion:
n is Carmichael ⟺ n is composite and squarefree, and p−1∣n−1 for all p∣n.​
(33)
If n=pq for distinct odd primes with p<q, the condition at q would require q−1∣pq−1. Reducing pq−1 modulo q−1 gives p−1, so q−1∣p−1. But 0<p−1<q−1, which is impossible. Thus a product of two distinct odd primes is not Carmichael.
For n=pqr, squarefreeness is automatic. Since p≡1(modp−1),
pqr−1≡qr−1(modp−1).
(34)
Apply the analogous reductions at q and r to obtain
pqr is Carmichael ⟺ p−1∣qr−1,q−1∣pr−1,r−1∣pq−1.​
(35)
Finally 1729=7⋅13⋅19, with distinct odd prime factors, and
13⋅19−1=246=6⋅41,7⋅19−1=132=12⋅11,7⋅13−1=90=18⋅5.
(36)
All three divisibilities hold, so 1729 is a Carmichael number.

A countable set is one admitting an injection into N; this includes finite sets and the empty set. An infinite countable set can equivalently be listed in a sequence indexed by N.
For a countable union of countable sets A=⋃i≥1​Ai​, choose an injection ei​:Ai​→N for each nonempty Ai​. Assign x∈A the pair (i,ei​(x)), where i is the least index with x∈Ai​. This is an injection into N2. The pairs are countable by diagonal enumeration; explicitly, with natural numbers starting at one, the Cantor pairing function
(i,j)⟼2(i+j−2)(i+j−1)​+j
(37)
maps them bijectively to N. Composing the two maps proves that the union is countable. Empty members contribute no elements.
To prove uncountability of the power set P(N), suppose all subsets could be listed as A1​,A2​,…. Define
D={n∈N:n∈/An​}.
(38)
For every n, D differs from An​ on whether it contains n. Thus D is missing from the supposedly exhaustive list. This Cantor diagonal argument proves P(N) is uncountable.
For increasing functions in the question's non-strict sense, encode every subset A⊆N by
fA​(n)=n+∑k=1n​1A​(k),fA​(0)=0
(39)
where the value at zero is only a convenient auxiliary convention. Then
fA​(n)−fA​(n−1)=1+1A​(n).
(40)
Each fA​ is even strictly increasing, hence is a nondecreasing function as required, and its successive differences recover A uniquely. This is an injection from the uncountable power set into the requested collection. Therefore the increasing functions N→N form an uncountable set.
For a decreasing function in the question's sense, f is a nonincreasing function with positive integer values. Each strict decrease lowers its value by at least one, so there can be at most f(1)−1 strict decreases. There is consequently a final drop, after which the function is constant. Every such function is determined by a finite initial list whose final value repeats forever. The set of all these lists is contained in
⋃N=1∞​NN,
(41)
a countable union of countable sets, hence countable by the result just proved. Thus the decreasing functions N→N form a countable set. These contrasting cardinalities of monotone natural-number functions come from the absence of an upper bound for increasing values and the finite number of drops available to decreasing natural-number values. If the natural numbers include zero, the same proof uses the bound f(1) instead.

Together they specify the initial position and velocity. If V=0, the planar position stays fixed and the angle ϕ is immaterial.
For Bz​(t)=βt, the inner integral gives θ(t)=−at2+ϕ, with a=qβ/(2m)>0. Under the specified initial data,
x(t)=∫0t​cos(as2)ds,y(t)=−∫0t​sin(as2)ds,z(t)=0.
(71)
Rescale u=a​s. The given Fresnel integrals then give
x(∞)=a​1​8π​​=4qβπm​​,y(∞)=−4qβπm​​.
(72)
Hence the limiting position is
r(∞)=4qβπm​​(1,−1,0).​
(73)
The negative y coordinate follows from clockwise rotation of planar velocity for positive charge and positive Bz​. The position converges through cancellation of increasingly rapid oscillations; its convergence does not mean the constant planar speed tends to zero.
