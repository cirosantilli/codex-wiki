For the centralizer of a subset, the identity commutes with every h∈A. If g,k commute with each member of A, then for every such h,
(gk)h=g(kh)=g(hk)=(gh)k=h(gk).
(15)
Also gh=hg implies g−1h=hg−1 after multiplication by g−1 on both sides. Thus identity, products and inverses lie in C(A), proving that it is a subgroup.
For the conjugation action, identity acts trivially, and
ρ(g1​,ρ(g2​,h))=g1​g2​hg2−1​g1−1​=ρ(g1​g2​,h).
(16)
These are the defining laws of a left group action. Its orbits of a group action are the conjugacy classes. The stabilizer of h is exactly its centralizer, since ghg−1=h if and only if gh=hg. The orbit-stabilizer theorem therefore gives
∣Oi​∣=[G:C({hi​})]=∣C({hi​})∣∣G∣​.
(17)
A class has one element exactly when that element commutes with all of G, so the singleton classes are precisely the elements of C(G), the centre of a group. Partitioning G into all its conjugacy classes now proves the class equation
∣G∣=∣C(G)∣+∣Oi​∣>1∑​∣C({hi​})∣∣G∣​​.
(18)
For ∣G∣=pr, Lagrange's theorem for finite groups makes every class size a power of p. A nonsingleton class has size at least p and is therefore divisible by p. Reducing the class equation modulo p yields ∣C(G)∣≡0(modp), because r>0. The identity lies in C(G), so its order is positive and divisible by p. Consequently
∣C(G)∣≥p>1​.
(19)
This proves the nontrivial center of a finite p-group directly from the conjugation classes.

A group homomorphism sends identity to identity. For θ(g),θ(h) in its image,
θ(g)θ(h)−1=θ(gh−1),
(20)
which is still in the image; hence the image of a group homomorphism is a subgroup of H. Let N=kerθ. It is a subgroup by the same identity, and for k∈N and g∈G,
θ(gkg−1)=θ(g)θ(k)θ(g)−1=e.
(21)
Thus gNg−1⊆N, and replacing g by g−1 gives equality, proving that N is a normal subgroup.
Define a map from the quotient group by
Φ:G/N⟶θ(G),Φ(gN)=θ(g).
(22)
If gN=hN, then h−1g∈N and θ(g)=θ(h), so the map is well defined. Conversely equality of the images implies h−1g∈N, giving injectivity. It is onto by the definition of image, and Φ((gN)(hN))=θ(gh)=θ(g)θ(h). This proves the first isomorphism theorem for groups in this case:
G/kerθ≅θ(G)​.
(23)
An orthogonal matrix Q has QTQ=I, so (detQ)2=1. The determinant is therefore a group homomorphism O(3)→{1,−1}, with image both signs because diag(−1,1,1) has determinant −1. Its kernel is the special orthogonal group SO(3). Hence SO(3) is normal in O(3), and
O(3)/SO(3)≅{1,−1}≅C2​​.
(24)
For the last request, the determinant-twist projection in odd dimension gives an explicit example:
π:O(3)⟶SO(3),π(Q)=(detQ)Q​.
(25)
Indeed detπ(Q)=(detQ)4=1, and scalar factors commute, so π(QR)=π(Q)π(R). If π(Q)=I, then Q=(detQ)I, making Q=I or Q=−I. Both lie in the kernel, which is therefore exactly {I,−I}, of order two. The map also fixes every member of SO(3), so it is onto.

Since σ is a group homomorphism, σ(0)=I, σ(x+y)=σ(x)σ(y), and σ(−x)=σ(x)−1. The product is closed on R×R2. Its two associative bracketings give
[(x,v)∗(y,w)]∗(z,u)(x,v)∗[(y,w)∗(z,u)]​=(x+y+z,v+σ(x)w+σ(x+y)u),=(x+y+z,v+σ(x)w+σ(x)σ(y)u),​
(26)
and coincide by the homomorphism property. The identity element is (0,0), and the two-sided inverse is
(x,v)−1=(−x,−σ(−x)v)​.
(27)
For example, multiplying on the right gives v−σ(x)σ(−x)v=0, and multiplying on the left gives −σ(−x)v+σ(−x)v=0. Thus this law defines a group, specifically a semidirect product.
If it is an abelian group, comparing (x,0)∗(0,w) and (0,w)∗(x,0) gives σ(x)w=w for every w. Hence σ(x)=I for every x. Conversely this trivial action makes the product ordinary vector addition. Therefore the unique action giving a commutative group is
σ(x)=Ifor all x​.
(28)
For the final condition, compare (0,ae1​)∗(x,w) and (x,w)∗(0,ae1​). They are (x,ae1​+w) and (x,w+σ(x)ae1​) respectively. They coincide for every a,x,w exactly when σ(x)e1​=e1​ for every x. The first column of σ(x) is then (1,0)T; since it lies in the special linear group, its determinant is one and its other diagonal entry is also one. Thus
σ(x)=(10​r(x)1​).
(29)
Matrix multiplication gives r(x+y)=r(x)+r(y). Conversely any such additive function gives a homomorphism into SL(2,R) fixing e1​ and therefore makes these translations central. This proves the full characterization of the central translations in a linear semidirect product:
r:(R,+)⟶(R,+) may be any homomorphism​.
(30)
No continuity or measurability is assumed, so it would be unjustified to restrict the answer to r(x)=cx.

Let T(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d), with ad−bc=0. If c=0, then d=0 and
T(z)=da​z+db​,
(31)
a composition of a nonzero scaling and a translation. If c=0, rearrange the fraction as
T(z)=ca​+c2bc−ad​z+d/c1​.
(32)
Thus every Möbius transformation is a composition, in order, of translation by d/c, reciprocal inversion, nonzero scaling by (bc−ad)/c2, and translation by a/c. All formulas act on the Riemann sphere, including zero and infinity. The scaling parameter must be nonzero for that factor to be a Möbius transformation.
For three distinct finite points, define the cross-ratio normalization
Fz​(ζ)=(ζ−z3​)(z2​−z1​)(ζ−z1​)(z2​−z3​)​.
(33)
It sends z1​,z2​,z3​ to 0,1,∞ respectively. If z3​=∞, use (ζ−z1​)/(z2​−z1​); if z1​=∞, use (z2​−z3​)/(ζ−z3​); if z2​=∞, use (ζ−z1​)/(ζ−z3​). These are the corresponding limits of the same formula and cover all possible triples. Form the analogous normalization Fw​ of the target triple. Then

J=αE+γn(n⋅E).

Choose the plane through the origin perpendicular to n:
Π={E:n⋅E=0}​.
