Since σ is a group homomorphism, σ(0)=I, σ(x+y)=σ(x)σ(y), and σ(−x)=σ(x)−1. The product is closed on R×R2. Its two associative bracketings give
[(x,v)∗(y,w)]∗(z,u)(x,v)∗[(y,w)∗(z,u)]​=(x+y+z,v+σ(x)w+σ(x+y)u),=(x+y+z,v+σ(x)w+σ(x)σ(y)u),​
(26)
and coincide by the homomorphism property. The identity element is (0,0), and the two-sided inverse is
(x,v)−1=(−x,−σ(−x)v)​.
(27)
For example, multiplying on the right gives v−σ(x)σ(−x)v=0, and multiplying on the left gives −σ(−x)v+σ(−x)v=0. Thus this law defines a group, specifically a semidirect product.
If it is an abelian group, comparing (x,0)∗(0,w) and (0,w)∗(x,0) gives σ(x)w=w for every w. Hence σ(x)=I for every x. Conversely this trivial action makes the product ordinary vector addition. Therefore the unique action giving a commutative group is
σ(x)=Ifor all x​.
(28)
For the final condition, compare (0,ae1​)∗(x,w) and (x,w)∗(0,ae1​). They are (x,ae1​+w) and (x,w+σ(x)ae1​) respectively. They coincide for every a,x,w exactly when σ(x)e1​=e1​ for every x. The first column of σ(x) is then (1,0)T; since it lies in the special linear group, its determinant is one and its other diagonal entry is also one. Thus
σ(x)=(10​r(x)1​).
(29)
Matrix multiplication gives r(x+y)=r(x)+r(y). Conversely any such additive function gives a homomorphism into SL(2,R) fixing e1​ and therefore makes these translations central. This proves the full characterization of the central translations in a linear semidirect product:
r:(R,+)⟶(R,+) may be any homomorphism​.
(30)
No continuity or measurability is assumed, so it would be unjustified to restrict the answer to r(x)=cx.
