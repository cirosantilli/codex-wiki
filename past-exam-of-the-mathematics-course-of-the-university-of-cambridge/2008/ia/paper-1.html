Taking the conjugate transpose of U=A+iB and using that both parts are Hermitian matrices gives U†=A−iB. Addition and subtraction recover the Hermitian parts of a unitary matrix:
A=2U+U†​,B=2iU−U†​.​
(5)

The ratio test states that, for a series ∑an​ with nonzero terms eventually, if ∣an+1​/an​∣→L<1, the series converges absolutely; if L>1, including L=∞, it diverges because its terms do not tend to zero. At L=1 the test is inconclusive: the harmonic series diverges while ∑n−2 converges.
For ∣x∣<1, the ratio test applied to xn/n gives absolute convergence when x=0, and at x=0 every such term vanishes. Thus
∑n=1N​nxn−1​=∑n=1N​nxn​−∑n=1N​n1​⟶−∞.
(9)
At x=1 all terms are zero, so the series converges to zero. At x=−1, the even terms vanish and the odd terms are −2/n, whose partial sums tend to −∞. Finally, if ∣x∣>1, then ∣xn−1∣/n≥(∣x∣n−1)/n→∞, so the term test for divergence applies. Consequently
The series converges exactly for x=1, and its sum there is 0.​

Fix x∈(0,1). For any sufficiently small nonzero h, the additivity of the Riemann integral gives
hF(x+h)−F(x)​−f(x)=h1​∫xx+h​[f(t)−f(x)]dt.
(11)
Taking absolute values, for either sign of h,
​hF(x+h)−F(x)​−f(x)​≤sup∣t−x∣≤∣h∣​∣f(t)−f(x)∣.
(12)
Because f is continuous at x, the right side tends to zero. Thus the fundamental theorem of calculus here follows directly from the difference quotient:
F′(x)=f(x)(0<x<1).​
(13)
Only continuity at the point under consideration was used.
Without continuity, the assertion is false. Take the Riemann integrable step function f(t)=0 for t<1/2 and f(t)=1 for t≥1/2. Its primitive is F(x)=0 for x≤1/2 and F(x)=x−1/2 for x≥1/2. The left and right difference quotients at 1/2 are respectively zero and one, so the primitive need not be differentiable at every interior point. A function with this single jump is Riemann integrable; changing its value at the jump does not change the integral or the counterexample.

The intermediate value theorem states that, if f is a continuous function on a closed interval [a,b], every value between f(a) and f(b) equals f(c) for some c∈[a,b]. If the value is strictly between the endpoint values, c may be chosen in (a,b).
Here is a proof from the supremum property of the real numbers. Suppose f(a)<y<f(b); the reverse inequality is reduced to this case by replacing f,y with −f,−y. Let
E={t∈[a,b]:f(t)≤y},c=supE.
(51)
The set is nonempty because it contains a and is bounded above by b. Continuity at a supplies points of E to the right of a, while continuity at b excludes a neighborhood of b from E. Hence a<c<b. By the definition of supremum, there are tn​∈E with tn​→c. Continuity gives f(c)=limf(tn​)≤y. If f(c)<y, continuity would give a point just to the right of c still satisfying f(t)<y, contradicting that c is an upper bound for E. Thus f(c)=y. Values equal to either endpoint are attained at that endpoint, completing the proof.

Comparison with a convergent geometric series proves absolute convergence at z.

(66)
The set contains zero, since at w=0 only the constant term remains. If ∣z∣<R, the definition of supremum gives a point of convergence w with ∣w∣>∣z∣. The lemma proves absolute convergence at z. The proof leaves behavior on ∣z∣=R open; it need not be uniform around that circle. The two examples illustrate opposite possibilities.
