For a permutation σ∈Sn​, let Pσ​ be its permutation matrix, defined by Pσ​ej​=eσ(j)​. Define the signature of a permutation by ϵ(σ)=detPσ​. A permutation matrix is orthogonal, so (detPσ​)2=1 and its permutation sign lies in {1,−1}. A transposition exchanges two columns of the identity and therefore has determinant −1. Every permutation is a product of transpositions, by decomposing each permutation cycle as
(a1​a2​…,ar​)=(a1​ar​)(a1​ar−1​)⋯(a1​a2​).
(1)
Hence ϵ(σ)=(−1)m for any expression as m transpositions; the determinant definition proves that this parity of a permutation is independent of the expression. Equivalently the permutation sign is positive for an even permutation and negative for an odd permutation.
Composition is taken rightmost first. Since Pστ​ej​=eσ(τ(j))​=Pσ​Pτ​ej​, we have Pστ​=Pσ​Pτ​. Multiplicativity of the determinant therefore proves the sign homomorphism identity
ϵ(στ)=ϵ(σ)ϵ(τ).​
(2)
The alternating group consists of the even permutations:
An​={σ∈Sn​:ϵ(σ)=1}=kerϵ.​
(3)
It contains the identity. If σ,τ∈An​, their product has permutation sign 1, and ϵ(σ−1)=ϵ(σ)−1=1, so their inverses also belong to An​. This proves it is a subgroup. Finally, for every g∈Sn​ and a∈An​,
ϵ(gag−1)=ϵ(g)ϵ(a)ϵ(g)−1=1.
(4)
Thus An​ is a normal subgroup of Sn​. This also exhibits directly why a kernel of a group homomorphism is normal.

For a constant orthogonal Cartesian coordinate change xi′​=Rij​xj​, a Cartesian second-rank tensor has components satisfying
Tij′​(x′)=Rik​Rjl​Tkl​(x).​
(13)
Both indices transform by the coordinate-change matrix; summation over repeated indices is understood by the Einstein summation convention. A vector would have the one-index rule Ui′​=Rik​Uk​.

For a normal subgroup H⊴G, define multiplication of left cosets by
(gH)(kH)=(gk)H.​
(15)
To check that this is well-defined, replace g,k by gh1​,kh2​ with h1​,h2​∈H. Their product is gh1​kh2​=gk(k−1h1​k)h2​, and normality puts (k−1h1​k)h2​ in H. Thus the product coset is independent of the representatives. Associativity follows from multiplication in G, the identity is H, and the inverse of gH is g−1H. These operations form the quotient group G/H.
For the unheaded finite-index conclusion, let G act on the n left cosets of its proper subgroup H by g⋅(xH)=gxH. This is well-defined even if H is not normal: replacing x by xh leaves gxH unchanged. The maps are bijections with inverses supplied by g−1, and their compositions give a group homomorphism φ:G→Sn​. Its kernel of a group homomorphism N is normal, by the calculation in part (ii).
The action is not trivial, because some g∈/H sends the coset H to gH=H; hence N=G. If N were trivial, φ would be injective, implying ∣G∣≤∣Sn​∣=n!. Under the given strict inequality this is impossible. Thus N is a nontrivial proper normal subgroup, and
∣G∣>n! ⟹ G is not simple.​
(16)
More precisely, N=⋂x∈G​xHx−1 is the subgroup core: g fixes every coset if and only if x−1gx∈H for every x. This identifies the normal subgroup produced by the coset action.

For any permutation τ, conjugating a permutation cycle relabels its entries:
τ(a1​a2​…,ar​)τ−1=(τ(a1​)τ(a2​)…,τ(ar​)).
(19)
Indeed, the conjugate sends τ(aj​) to τ(aj+1​) and fixes the other relabeled entries exactly as the original permutation cycle does. Therefore conjugation preserves the multiset of permutation cycle lengths. Conversely, if two permutations have the same cycle type, pair their permutation cycles of each length and let τ carry the jth entry of each first permutation cycle to the jth entry of its partner. Include one-cycles for fixed points. This defines a bijection of all labels, and the displayed relabeling identity makes the permutations conjugate. Thus
σ∼Sn​​ρ⟺σ,ρ have the same cycle type.​
(20)
For n≥2, the alternating conjugacy class splitting criterion says that an even permutation has the same class in An​ and Sn​ precisely when its type is not a collection of pairwise distinct odd lengths. Equivalently, there is an even-length permutation cycle or a repeated permutation cycle length, counting each fixed point as a length-one permutation cycle. Distinct odd lengths instead give two alternating-group classes. For n=1, A1​=S1​ and the sole class is the same; the index-two splitting criterion is not applicable.
Squaring an odd-length permutation cycle keeps one permutation cycle of that length, since advancing by two visits all of its entries. Squaring an even-length permutation cycle splits it into two permutation cycles, each of half that length. Thus any even-length permutation cycle strictly increases the total permutation cycle count on squaring, while odd permutation cycles leave that count unchanged. The cycle type can agree with its square exactly when every length is odd. This proves permutation conjugate to its square:
σ∼Sn​​σ2⟺every cycle length is odd⟺ord(σ) is odd.​
(21)
The final equivalence follows because the order of a group element that is a permutation is the least common multiple of its permutation cycle lengths.
The possible even types on five labels are the identity, a three-cycle, a product of two disjoint transpositions and a five-cycle. The identity and the double transpositions are their own inverses. For a three-cycle (abc) with unused labels d,e, the even permutation (bc)(de) conjugates it to its inverse. For a five-cycle (abcde), the even permutation (be)(cd) reverses its order. Therefore every element of A5​ is conjugate to its inverse within A5​. Inverting a five-cycle uses two transpositions, in agreement with inversion of an odd cycle in an alternating group.
A small counterexample elsewhere is
n=3,σ=(123).​
(22)
The alternating group A3​={e,(123),(132)} is cyclic and therefore abelian. Every conjugacy class in an abelian group is a singleton, while σ=σ−1. Thus these two elements are not conjugate in A3​.

Let a finite group G act on a set X. For x∈X, define its stabilizer subgroup Gx​={g:g⋅x=x} and its group orbit Gx={g⋅x:g∈G}. The map
gGx​⟼g⋅x
(29)
is well-defined, because multiplying g on the right by an element fixing x does not change its image. It is onto by the definition of the orbit. If g⋅x=h⋅x, then h−1g∈Gx​, so gGx​=hGx​; hence it is one-to-one. Every coset has ∣Gx​∣ elements and the cosets partition G. This proves the orbit-stabilizer theorem:
∣G∣=∣Gx∣∣Gx​∣.​
(30)
For a group element x, its cyclic subgroup H=⟨x⟩ has ord(x) elements. Apply the theorem to the action of G on its left cosets G/H, whose stabilizer at H is H. It follows that
ord(x)=∣H∣ divides ∣G∣.​
(31)
This is the needed case of Lagrange's theorem, obtained directly from the action.
To prove Cauchy's theorem for finite groups, let p be a prime divisor of ∣G∣ and consider
X={(g1​,…,gp​)∈Gp:g1​⋯gp​=e}.
(32)
The first p−1 entries are arbitrary and determine the last, so ∣X∣=∣G∣p−1 is divisible by p. Cyclically rotate the entries. This preserves the product constraint even if G is nonabelian, since
g2​⋯gp​g1​=g1−1​(g1​⋯gp​)g1​=e.
(33)
Thus a cyclic group of order p acts on X. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, its orbits have size one or p. Its fixed tuples are exactly (g,…,g) with gp=e. If m is their number, counting the remaining size-p orbits gives m≡∣X∣≡0(modp). The identity provides one fixed tuple, so m≥p and a nonidentity g with gp=e exists. The order of this g divides p: division of p by its order and minimality of that order prove this directly. Since p is prime and g=e, the order is exactly p. This is the cyclic-tuple proof of Cauchy theorem.
Now suppose every nonidentity element has order two. Then every element equals its inverse, and for any a,b∈G,
ab=(ab)−1=b−1a−1=ba.
(34)
Thus group of exponent two is abelian applies. Starting from the identity subgroup, choose an element outside the subgroup already generated. Because the group is abelian and that element squares to the identity, adjoining it doubles the subgroup: it is the disjoint union of the old subgroup and its translate by the new element. Repeating until the finite group is exhausted shows ∣G∣=2r. Conversely the direct product of groups C2r​ has order 2r and every nonidentity element has order two. Therefore
n=2r(r=0,1,2,…).​
(35)
The case r=0 is the trivial group and satisfies the condition vacuously. These finite examples are elementary abelian groups of exponent two.
An infinite example is the group of all binary sequences, with coordinatewise addition modulo two:
G=j=1∏∞​Z/2Z.​
(36)
Every sequence added to itself is zero, so every nonzero sequence has order exactly two. The sequences having a single 1 in coordinate j are all distinct, proving that the group is infinite.
