The intermediate value theorem states that, if f is a continuous function on a closed interval [a,b], every value between f(a) and f(b) equals f(c) for some c∈[a,b]. If the value is strictly between the endpoint values, c may be chosen in (a,b).
Here is a proof from the supremum property of the real numbers. Suppose f(a)<y<f(b); the reverse inequality is reduced to this case by replacing f,y with −f,−y. Let
E={t∈[a,b]:f(t)≤y},c=supE.
(51)
The set is nonempty because it contains a and is bounded above by b. Continuity at a supplies points of E to the right of a, while continuity at b excludes a neighborhood of b from E. Hence a<c<b. By the definition of supremum, there are tn​∈E with tn​→c. Continuity gives f(c)=limf(tn​)≤y. If f(c)<y, continuity would give a point just to the right of c still satisfying f(t)<y, contradicting that c is an upper bound for E. Thus f(c)=y. Values equal to either endpoint are attained at that endpoint, completing the proof.
