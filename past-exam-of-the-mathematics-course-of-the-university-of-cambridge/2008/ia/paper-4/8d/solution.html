The key telescoping series identity is
ck​=(q+k)!(q+k−1)q!​=(q+k−1)!q!​−(q+k)!q!​.
(25)
For the requested mathematical induction, when n=1 the proposed sum is q/(q+1)=1−q!/(q+1)!. If the identity holds for n, adding cn+1​=q!/(q+n)!−q!/(q+n+1)! cancels its last term and yields 1−q!/(q+n+1)!. Thus it holds for every n≥1. Since q!/(q+n)!→0 for fixed positive integer q, the infinite series has sum 1​.
For the second series, the integral digit condition is 0≤an​≤n−1, and in particular a1​=0. Its partial sums are increasing and satisfy
0≤∑n=1N​n!an​​≤∑n=1N​n!n−1​=1−N!1​≤1.
(26)
Thus the bounded monotone sequence theorem proves convergence to a real number S∈[0,1]. The same telescoping calculation gives, for every N≥1,
∑n>N​n!n−1​=N!1​,RN​:=N!(S−∑n=1N​n!an​​)∈[0,1].
(27)
If positive digits occur infinitely often, then every tail contains a positive term, so RN​>0. If an​≤n−2 occurs infinitely often, then every tail contains a digit smaller than its maximum. Indeed,
1−RN​=N!∑n>N​n!n−1−an​​>0.
(28)
Consequently the two conditions together give 0<RN​<1 for every N. Were S=A/B a rational number, with positive integer B, choose N≥B. Because B∣N!, the number N!S is an integer, as is ∑n=1N​an​N!/n!. Their difference RN​ would be an integer strictly between zero and one, a contradiction.
Conversely, if positive digits occur only finitely often, then S is a finite sum of rational numbers. If digits below their maximum occur only finitely often, then an​=n−1 for every n>N for some N, giving
S=∑n=1N​n!an​​+N!1​∈Q.
(29)
These are exactly the two ways the conjunction of infinitude conditions can fail. Hence the irrationality criterion for factorial series is proved in both directions: S is irrational exactly when both kinds of digit occur infinitely often.
