Measure temperature relative to ambient by θi​=Ti​−T∞​. Newton's law of cooling models ordinary cooling by θi′​=−aθi​, since heat loss is proportional to the excess temperature. The additional negative forcing represents the cooling from the milk, with the temperature drop or rate normalized to one in the chosen units.
The Dirac delta function has unit integral and is supported at the instant of addition. It therefore imposes a sudden unit drop for cup 1. The difference H(t−1)−H(t−2) of Heaviside step functions is one during the addition interval and zero outside it, giving a unit cooling rate for cup 2. The second temperature is continuous; only its derivative changes at the switching times. The value assigned to a Heaviside step function exactly at its jump does not change the integrated solution. This is the impulse versus finite-duration cooling comparison.

For this quartic potential with fourfold symmetry, the first derivatives factor:
fx​=2x(1−x2−by2),fy​=2y(1−y2−bx2).
(36)
The critical points are the origin, the four axis points (±1,0),(0,±1), and four points with both coordinates nonzero. At the latter, x2+by2=y2+bx2=1. Subtracting and using b=1 gives x2=y2=1/(1+b). Thus the complete list is
(0,0),(±1,0),(0,±1),(±1+b​1​,±1+b​1​),​
(37)
where the two signs in the final group are chosen separately.
The Hessian is
D2f=(2−6x2−2by2−4bxy​−4bxy2−6y2−2bx2​).
(38)
At the origin it is 2I, so there is a strict local minimum with value zero. At an axis point its eigenvalues are −4 and 2(1−b), giving a saddle point of a scalar function if b<1 and a strict local maximum if b>1. All four axis values are 1/2.
At a diagonal point the Hessian has eigenvalues
−4,−1+b4(1−b)​.
(39)
Hence these four points are strict local maxima for b<1 and saddle points of a scalar function for b>1. Their value is 1/(1+b). None of these critical points is degenerate when b=1.
To describe the level curves precisely, use polar coordinates and write
f=r2−21​q(θ)r4,q(θ)=1+2b−1​sin2(2θ)>0.
(40)
For a level f=c, the possible radii satisfy
r2=q(θ)1±1−2q(θ)c​​,​
(41)
retaining only real, nonnegative values. This formula, together with reflection in each axis and interchange of the coordinates, determines the level curves. The radial maximum in a direction is 1/(2q), attained at r2=1/q. It also proves that the local maxima identified above are global: the largest directional value is 1/(1+b) on the diagonals if b<1, and 1/2 on the axes if b>1.
For negative c only the plus sign gives a positive radius, producing one outer closed level curve. At c=0 there is the isolated origin and the outer curve r2=2/q. For 0<c<cs​, where cs​ is the saddle value, both radial branches exist in every direction, giving an inner loop around the origin and an outer loop. At c=cs​ they meet at the four saddle points of a scalar function. Above the saddle value, four separate loops surround the four maxima; they shrink to those points at the maximum value. There are no level curves above it.
For 0<b<1, the saddle value is cs​=1/2 and the four high-level loops lie along the diagonals. For b>1, cs​=1/(1+b) and the four high-level loops lie along the coordinate axes. The sketches show representative values in both regimes; the highlighted level curve is the saddle level.

Let G(s)=E[sξ] be the offspring probability generating function, and let Gn​(s)=E[sZn​], for 0≤s≤1. Given Zn​=k, the next generation is a sum of k independent offspring counts. Multiplication of their probability generating functions gives
E[sZn+1​∣Zn​=k]=G(s)k.
(43)
Taking conditional expectation and then averaging proves
Gn+1​(s)=Gn​(G(s)),G0​(s)=s.​
(44)
Thus Gn​ is the n-fold composition of G with itself. Induction makes this precise: it is true for n=0, and composing once more gives the next generation. In particular Gn​∘G=G∘Gn​, since both are the same iterated function. This is the generating-function recursion for a Galton-Watson process.
Write m=EZ1​=G′(1−), initially assuming it is finite. The law of total expectation gives
E[Zn+1​∣Zn​]=mZn​,EZn+1​=mEZn​.
(45)
Starting at one therefore yields EZn​=mn​, with the value at n=0 understood to be one even if m=0. Equivalently, differentiating the composition at one gives the same recurrence. If the offspring expectation is infinite, every positive generation has infinite expectation: a positive chance of at least one parent remains at each finite generation, and conditional on any positive parent count the next expected count is infinite.
For a Poisson branching process, G(s)=eλ(s−1). Extinction is absorbing because an empty generation has no parents, so extinction by generation n is precisely Zn​=0. Hence xn​=Gn​(0). Using the composition in the order G∘Gn​ gives
xn+1​=eλ(xn​−1),x0​=0.​
(46)
For example x1​=e−λ is the chance of no offspring in the first generation. The recurrence includes the degenerate case λ=0, where extinction occurs in the first generation with certainty.

Put q=1−p and track the difference Sn​ between the numbers of wins. It is a biased random walk with increments +1 and −1, stopped on first reaching 3 or −3.
To finish at +3 on game five, there must be four wins for A and one for B, with the final game won by A. The unique loss for A can be in position one, two or three. If it were in position four or five, the first three games would already have stopped the contest. Each of the three allowed paths remains strictly inside the barriers until the last step and has probability p4q. By interchanging players, there are also three paths ending at −3, each with probability pq4. Therefore
P(N=5)=3p4q+3pq4=3pq(p3+q3).​
(47)
For a fair game this is 3/16. Counting all five-game sequences with final win difference three would overcount paths on which the contest ended earlier.
