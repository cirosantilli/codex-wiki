1E OurBigBook.com
A linear map preserves linear combinations: ψ(av+bw)=aψ(v)+bψ(w) for all vectors and real scalars. The rank-nullity theorem gives dimV=dimkerψ+dimimψ. For an endomorphism of the same finite-dimensional vector space, surjectivity means rank dimV, equivalent to zero kernel and hence injectivity.
If ψϕ=I, then every v=ψ(ϕv) lies in the image, so ψ is surjective and consequently injective. Now ψ(ϕψv)=ψv; injectivity implies ϕψv=v for every v. Thus the right inverse is also a left inverse: ϕψ=I​. This is the one-sided inverses of finite-dimensional endomorphisms property.
Apply it to the linear maps on Rn represented by A and B: AB=I implies BA=I​. Equal finite dimensions are essential to the argument.

2G OurBigBook.com
A complex number is an algebraic number over Q when it is a root of a nonzero rational polynomial. Its minimal polynomial of an algebraic element is the unique monic polynomial in Q[X] of smallest degree annihilating it; polynomial division shows that it divides every rational polynomial annihilating the element, and it is irreducible.
Consider the surjective evaluation ring homomorphism evα​:Z[X]→Z[α]. Certainly (f)⊆kerevα​. Because the nonconstant f is irreducible over Z, its content is a unit: otherwise its content and its nonconstant primitive part give a factorization into nonunits. By the Gauss lemma for polynomials, f is irreducible in Q[X] and is a scalar multiple of the minimal polynomial.
If g∈Z[X] vanishes at α, then f divides g in Q[X]. Primitive divisibility in Gauss lemma for polynomials implies division in Z[X] as well. Explicitly, write the rational quotient as (a/b)h with h primitive integral and gcd(a,b)=1; the primitive product fh shows that integrality of (a/b)fh forces b=1. Thus the kernel is precisely (f), and the first isomorphism theorem for rings yields
Z[X]/(f)≅Z[α],[g]↦g(α).​
(1)
No monicity of f is needed; this is an isomorphism of rings, not an assertion that either ring is a field.

Use three compactness facts: a closed subset of a compact space is compact; a continuous image of a compact space is compact; and a compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed. For a closed C⊆X, these facts show that f(C) is closed in Y. Therefore the bijection f is a closed map, and (f−1)−1(C)=f(C) proves continuity of its inverse. Thus f is a homeomorphism. This proves the compact-to-Hausdorff continuous bijection theorem without requiring X itself to be Hausdorff.
Let q:[0,1]→[0,1]/∼ be the quotient map and h(t)=(cos2πt,sin2πt). The map h is continuous and identifies precisely the equal parameters and the endpoint pair. Hence it induces a bijection hˉ from the quotient space to the unit circle, with h=hˉ∘q; the definition of the quotient topology makes hˉ continuous. The quotient is compact as a continuous image of [0,1], while the circle is Hausdorff as a subspace of R2. Applying the preceding theorem gives
[0,1]/∼ ≅S1.​

We prove the needed Hilbert basis theorem step rather than assuming it. Suppose A is a commutative Noetherian ring and I⊆A[X] is an ideal. The set J of leading coefficients of elements of I, together with zero, is an ideal of A: align degrees with powers of X before addition, and use scalar multiplication. Choose finitely many generators of J, represented by polynomials f1​,…,fs​∈I, and let d be the maximum of their degrees. If J=0 then I=0 and there is nothing to prove.
For each 0≤j<d, let Jj​ consist of the coefficients of Xj of elements of I of degree at most j. This is also an ideal of A; choose finitely many generating coefficients and representing polynomials gj,l​∈I of degree at most j. The finite collection of all fi​ and gj,l​ generates I: for any h∈I of degree m≥d, express its leading coefficient using those of fi​ and subtract the matching combination of Xm−degfi​fi​ to lower degree. When the degree becomes j<d, use the representatives of Jj​ to lower it again. Induction on degree eventually leaves zero.
A field is Noetherian because its only ideals are zero and the whole field. Repeatedly applying the proved step gives every ideal of F[X1​,…,Xn​]​ finitely generated.
The coefficient restriction in the second ring says precisely
R=F+XYF[X,Y].
(20)
It contains one and is closed under addition, additive inverses and multiplication: (a+XYp)(b+XYq)=ab+XY(aq+bp+XYpq). To show it is not Noetherian, consider the ideals in R
Id​=(XY,XY2,…,XYd)R​,d≥1.
(21)
They form an ascending chain. In any ∑j=1d​rj​XYj with rj​∈R, the coefficient of X1 is a polynomial in Y of degree at most d, since the nonconstant part of rj​ introduces at least X2. It cannot equal XYd+1; hence Id​⊊Id+1​. If the union ideal were finitely generated, all its generators would lie in one Id​, forcing the chain to stabilize. This contradiction proves R is not Noetherian​, the constant-plus-ideal non-Noetherian subring example.

Work on the unit sphere with geodesic edges. A spherical lune of angle α has area ∫0α​∫0π​sinθdθdφ=2α. The three great circles through the sides of a spherical triangle partition the sphere into four antipodal pairs of triangles. If the original triangle has area A and the other pair representatives have areas B,C,D, then A+B+C+D=2π. The three lunes at its vertices have areas A+B,A+C,A+D, so
2(α+β+γ)=3A+B+C+D=2A+2π.
(22)
This proves the spherical excess formula A=α+β+γ−π​. Triangulate a convex spherical k-gon from one vertex into k−2 triangles; summing areas and vertex angles gives
Ak​=j=1∑k​αj​−(k−2)π.​
(23)
For a decomposition of the whole sphere, angles around every vertex sum to 2π and each edge belongs to two faces. Summing the polygon area formula over all faces therefore gives

(24)
