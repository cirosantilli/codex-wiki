For n≥1, an eigenvalue is a number λ∈C for which there is a nonzero vector v satisfying Av=λv. Equivalently, ker(A−λI)={0}, or det(A−λI)=0. The characteristic polynomial det(tI−A) has degree n and leading coefficient one. By the fundamental theorem of algebra it has a complex root λ. At that root A−λI is a singular matrix, so it has a nonzero kernel vector, proving that A has an eigenvalue.

The Lagrangian sufficiency theorem for an equality constraint states that if a feasible x∗​ and a multiplier λ∗​ exist such that x∗​ globally maximizes L(x,λ∗​)=f(x)−λ∗​⋅(g(x)−b) over the underlying domain, then x∗​ globally maximizes f over g(x)=b. Indeed L=f at every feasible point, so global maximality of L directly compares the feasible objective values. The underlying domain can already impose xi​≥0.
For 0<p<1, choose λ=pd1−p. Each function tp−λt on t≥0 is a strictly concave function and attains its unique maximum at t=1/d, since ptp−1=λ there. The sum Lagrangian is therefore globally maximized when every xi​=1/d, and this point is feasible. For p=1 the objective is identically one on the feasible set. For p>1, each feasible xi​ lies in [0,1], so xip​≤xi​, with equality only at 0 or 1; equality in the total is attained exactly at a simplex vertex. Consequently
maxi=1∑d​xip​={d1−p,1,​0<p<1,p≥1.​​
(15)
For 0<p<1 the unique maximizer is (1/d,…,1/d); for p=1 every feasible point is a maximizer; for p>1 the maximizers are exactly the d vertices with one coordinate one and all others zero. When d=1 these descriptions coincide.

The row rank of A is the dimension of the span of its rows in Rn; its column rank is the dimension of the span of its columns in Rm. Elementary row operations replace rows by invertible linear combinations, so preserve their span. They also preserve every column relation: Ac=0 if and only if EAc=0 for an invertible elementary matrix E. Reduce A to row echelon form R, with r nonzero rows. These rows are independent because their leading entries occur in distinct, successively later columns. The r pivot columns are independent by their triangular pivot submatrix, while every column lies in the r-dimensional coordinate space supported on the nonzero rows. Thus both ranks of R, and hence both ranks of A, equal r. Row rank equals column rank.
Elementary column operations replace columns by invertible linear combinations and preserve their span. Equivalently, right multiplication by an invertible elementary matrix F preserves row relations, since cTAF=0 if and only if cTA=0. Therefore either elementary row operations or elementary column operations preserve the matrix rank, and any finite sequence gives rank(A′)=rank(A)​.
For the block matrices, put M=(PQQ​0QR​). The explicit invertible multiplications
(I0​−PI​)M(I0​−RI​)=(0Q​−PQR0​)
(16)
follow by first subtracting P times the lower block row from the upper one and then subtracting the first block column times R from the second. Changing the sign of the upper block row gives N=(0Q​PQR0​). These operations preserve matrix rank, proving that the two specified block matrices have equal rank. Their off-diagonal form gives rank(N)=rank(Q)+rank(PQR), since the two image components occupy independent coordinate blocks.
Choose r1​=rank(PQ) independent columns of PQ and r2​=rank(QR) independent columns of QR. Select the corresponding r1​ columns from the first block column of M and r2​ from the second. In a linear relation among these selected columns, the upper coordinate block forces every coefficient of the first selection to vanish; the lower block then forces every coefficient of the second to vanish. Consequently rank(M)≥r1​+r2​. Combining this with the equality of block ranks proves the Frobenius rank inequality:
rank(PQ)+rank(QR)≤rank(Q)+rank(PQR).​
(17)

ii OurBigBook.com
Write G=Rot(D) for the rotational symmetry group of a regular dodecahedron. Centre the solid at the origin. A rotation in three dimensions is determined by the image of a chosen face and one of its vertices, so there are at most 12⋅5=60 rotations. All these choices occur. The outward unit normal to a face and the direction from its centre to the chosen vertex determine an oriented orthonormal frame. The proper rotation mapping one such frame to another maps the whole regular pentagonal face. At each edge, the common dihedral angle and convexity then determine the adjacent face uniquely; propagating across the connected face-adjacency graph shows that the rotation maps the entire solid to itself. Hence ∣G∣=60, and the action is transitive on faces, vertices and edges.
Its rotations can be counted geometrically. There are six axes joining opposite face centres, each allowing four nonidentity rotations of order five; ten axes joining opposite vertices, each allowing two of order three; and fifteen axes joining opposite edge midpoints, each allowing a half-turn. The orbit-stabilizer theorem also verifies these possibilities: vertex stabilizers have order 60/20=3 and edge stabilizers have order 60/30=2, and their nonidentity elements rotate about the corresponding vertex or edge-midpoint axis. Together with the identity this accounts for all 1+24+20+15=60 elements. In particular there are no elements of order four.
We now determine the conjugacy classes. The group is transitive on edges, so all fifteen edge half-turns are conjugate. For a rotation whose angle is neither zero nor π, a commuting rotation must preserve its oriented axis: conjugation carries the axis and its rotation angle to their images, and reversing the axis would reverse a non-half-turn angle. Thus its centralizer consists of rotations about the same axis. For a face rotation this centralizer has order five, and for a vertex rotation it has order three. The orbit-stabilizer theorem for conjugation therefore gives class sizes 60/5=12 and 60/3=20. The twenty vertex rotations form one class, and the twenty-four face rotations form two classes. The class sizes are 1,15,20,12,12.
A normal subgroup is a union of these classes and contains the identity. Its possible orders are
1,13,16,21,25,28,33,36,40,45,48,60.
(19)
By Lagrange's theorem its order must divide 60. Only 1 and 60 in this list do so. Therefore G is simple of order 60​.

For each involution t∈G, its conjugacy class has size fifteen, so its centralizer CG​(t) has order four. A group of order four is a cyclic group or a Klein four-group; the cyclic possibility would contain an element of order four, excluded by the geometric enumeration. Hence each CG​(t) is a Klein four-group containing three involutions.
If u is any of the three involutions in CG​(t), the latter group is an abelian group and hence is contained in CG​(u). Both centralizers have order four, so they are equal. It follows that the fifteen involutions partition into five triples, with five distinct centralizers. Conjugation by G permutes these five subgroups and gives a group homomorphism θ:G→S5​. This action is nontrivial: otherwise each of these order-four subgroups would be a normal subgroup, contrary to simplicity. The kernel of θ is normal; since G is simple and the action nontrivial, its kernel is trivial. Thus θ is injective.
The sign of θ(g) defines a homomorphism G→{±1}. If it were nontrivial, simplicity would make it injective, impossible for a group of order sixty mapping into one of order two. Hence every image permutation is even, and θ(G)⊆A5​. Both groups have order sixty, so they are equal. This constructs the requested group isomorphism:
Rot(D)≅A5​.​

The contraction mapping theorem states that a contraction mapping T:X→X on a nonempty complete metric space has a unique fixed point. Here contraction means that d(Tx,Ty)≤qd(x,y) for all x,y, with one constant 0≤q<1. Choose x0​∈X and define xn+1​=Txn​. Successive distances satisfy d(xn+1​,xn​)≤qnd(x1​,x0​), so for every m≥1,
d(xn+m​,xn​)≤∑j=nn+m−1​qjd(x1​,x0​)≤1−qqn​d(x1​,x0​).
(21)
Thus (xn​) is a Cauchy sequence. Completeness gives xn​→x∗​. Since a contraction mapping is a continuous function, Tx∗​=limTxn​=limxn+1​=x∗​. If y∗​ were another fixed point, d(x∗​,y∗​)=d(Tx∗​,Ty∗​)≤qd(x∗​,y∗​), forcing distance zero. This proves both existence and uniqueness.
Now suppose T=fk is a contraction mapping, where k is a positive integer. Let y be its unique fixed point. Since f commutes with its own iterate, T(fy)=f(Ty)=fy. Thus fy is also a fixed point of T, so fy=y. Any fixed point of f is a fixed point of T, and must equal y. A contractive iterate therefore gives a unique fixed point of f, without assuming continuity of f itself.
The space X=C([0,1],R) is complete for the uniform norm. Indeed, a uniformly Cauchy sequence converges pointwise by completeness of R; passing to the limit in its uniform Cauchy bound shows uniform convergence, and the uniform limit of continuous functions is a continuous function. The operator F maps this space to itself: s↦ϕ(h(s),s) is a continuous function, and its integral plus the continuous g is a continuous function.
For n=0 the desired bound is the definition of ∥h−k∥∞​. Suppose it holds for n. The Lipschitz condition and the fact that the same g cancels give
∣Fn+1(h)(t)−Fn+1(k)(t)∣​≤∫0t​M∣Fn(h)(s)−Fn(k)(s)∣ds≤n!Mn+1​∥h−k∥∞​∫0t​snds=(n+1)!Mn+1tn+1​∥h−k∥∞​.​
(22)
This proves the estimate by induction. Taking the supremum over 0≤t≤1, the Lipschitz constant of Fn is at most Mn/n!. These constants tend to zero, since the ratio of successive terms is M/(n+1). Some iterate is consequently a contraction. The preceding result gives a unique fixed point of F, which is exactly the unique continuous solution of the integral equation on [0,1]​. No assumption M<1 is needed.

A topology on X is a family T of subsets containing ∅ and X, closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. Its members are the open sets. A Hausdorff space separates each pair of distinct points by disjoint open neighbourhoods; a compact space is one for which every open cover has a finite subcover.

A Hausdorff compact topology. The empty set omits zero and Z has finite complement. An arbitrary union omitting zero belongs to the family. If a union contains zero, one of its constituent sets contains zero and is cofinite, so the union is also cofinite. A finite intersection either omits zero or is an intersection of finitely many cofinite sets and is therefore cofinite. This proves all topology axioms.
For distinct nonzero integers a,b, the disjoint open singletons {a},{b} separate them. To separate 0 and n=0, use the disjoint open sets Z∖{n} and {n}. Thus this is a Hausdorff space. Every open cover has a member containing zero, which leaves only finitely many integers uncovered; choose one member for each of those points. This gives a finite subcover and proves compactness. The space is the one-point compactification of the discrete space Z∖{0}, with zero as the added point.

A Hausdorff, noncompact topology. The condition says precisely that each point of U lies in a residue class n+kZ contained in U. These classes form a basis: the intersection of two classes containing a common point contains the class through that point with modulus equal to the least common multiple of their moduli. Thus arbitrary unions obey the condition, and so do finite intersections. Also ∅ and Z obey it. This is the profinite topology on Z.

19H (Markov Chains)

Solution

Words: 310
19H OurBigBook.com
Let L be the last vertex visited for the first time, and assume N≥2. Every vertex is eventually painted with probability one: from any position, N−1 consecutive clockwise steps visit the entire cycle, and the probability of such a block is 2−(N−1). Thus the probability that coverage has not occurred after m successive blocks is at most (1−2−(N−1))m, which tends to zero.
Fix j=0 and cut the cycle at j. Represent it as the path with positions 0,1,…,N, where the two endpoints both represent j and the original starting vertex is at position N−j. Up to the first visit to j, the walk is a simple symmetric random walk on this path, killed at either endpoint. The event L=j is precisely that it visits both 1 and N−1 before being killed: travelling between these two positions visits all intermediate vertices, while visiting all non-j vertices necessarily includes these two.
For N≥3, first wait until the walk reaches one of 1,N−1. This happens almost surely before it can reach an absorbing endpoint. If it reaches 1 first, it must next reach N−1 before zero. The gambler's ruin probability is 1/(N−1): the hitting probability h(i) on 0≤i≤N−1 satisfies
h(0)=0,h(N−1)=1,h(i)=21​[h(i−1)+h(i+1)],
(59)
so its successive differences are constant and h(i)=i/(N−1). Starting at 1 gives the stated probability. If the first endpoint reached is N−1, reflection of the path gives the same probability of reaching 1 before N. The Strong Markov property at the first such hit therefore gives P(L=j)=1/(N−1), independently of which endpoint was hit first and of the start position.
Consequently the last visited vertex of a simple random walk on a cycle has distribution
P(L=0)=0,P(L=j)=N−11​(1≤j≤N−1).​
(60)
For N=2 the same formula follows directly since the only other post must be last. For N=1, painting the initial post already completes the task, so there is no subsequent last unpainted post.
