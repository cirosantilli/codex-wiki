For ℓ∈V∗ define (gℓ)(v)=ℓ(g−1v). The representing matrix is the transpose of the inverse matrix on V, so the dual character is
β(g)=α(g−1)=α(g)​.​
(46)
The last equality follows by averaging a Hermitian inner product to make the finite-group group representation unitary. Complex finite-group representations are isomorphic exactly when their characters agree. Hence V is self-dual exactly when its character is real-valued.
The invariant tensor identification (V⊗V)G≃HomG​(V∗,V) and Schur lemma show that, for irreducible V, the trivial multiplicity is zero unless V∗≃V, and one in that case. Dually, a self-duality isomorphism gives a nonzero invariant bilinear form B on V. Its radical is an invariant subspace, so irreducibility makes B nondegenerate. The space of invariant bilinear forms is one dimensional. Since the transpose BT is another such form, BT=cB; transposing again gives c2=1. Therefore B is symmetric or alternating: in the negative-transpose case, B(v,v)=−B(v,v) forces B(v,v)=0. Both types cannot coexist, since proportional nonzero forms cannot have both transpose signs.
In odd dimension an alternating matrix has determinant zero: detB=det(−BT)=(−1)ndetB=−detB. Thus only the symmetric case is possible. Over C a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form has a basis with matrix I: diagonalize it by congruence and rescale each nonzero diagonal entry. In that basis invariance says ρ(g)Tρ(g)=I. Consequently
ρ(G) is conjugate to a subgroup of O(n,C)(n odd, irreducible self-dual).​
