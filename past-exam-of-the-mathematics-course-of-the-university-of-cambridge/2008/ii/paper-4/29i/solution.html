Use the maximizing form of the Pontryagin maximum principle. For a cost-minimization problem there are a nonnegative multiplier λ0​ and a costate ψ, not both identically zero, with Hamiltonian H=ψb−λ0​c. The optimal control maximizes H, the costate obeys ψ˙​=−Hx​, and free terminal time at the fixed terminal state zero gives H(τ)=λ0​C′(τ). There is no fixed terminal costate value since the terminal state is fixed. In the normal case scale λ0​=1.
For the energy problem b=−u, c=up(t), C=−R, so ψ is constant and the normal Hamiltonian is u(p∗​−p(t)), where p∗​=−ψ. Maximization gives full power for p(t)<p∗​ and zero power for p(t)>p∗​; either value, or a fractional value, is allowed on a price tie. On the final active arc, the free-time condition gives
p∗​+R′(τ∗​)=p(τ∗​).​
(107)
This is the threshold energy scheduling with a completion reward rule. In this example the normal case is justified: an abnormal constant costate would require either no processing or processing at all times, and free-time transversality would then force that nonzero costate to vanish.
For p∗​=1/4, processing starts at t=1/2. Energy 1/2 is completed at τ=1, before the price rises again. Its cost and reward are
∫1/21​(t−1)2dt=1/24,R(1)=1/2,J=−11/24.​
(108)
Any threshold carrying completion into day two has τ≥2 and reward at most 1/3. Since energy cost is nonnegative, J≥−1/3>−11/24, so it is suboptimal.
For a global check, even any completion time τ≥5/4 has reward at most 4/9<11/24 and cannot beat the displayed candidate. Also τ≥1/2 since power is bounded by one. At a fixed τ∈[1/2,5/4], the cheapest half-unit of processing consists of the last half-unit interval [τ−1/2,τ]: every earlier point has a price at least as large as every selected point. Exchanging any earlier powered interval with a later unpowered cheaper one proves this rearrangement claim, also for fractional controls.
Write h=3/2−τ∈[1/4,1]. This minimal cost minus reward is
J(h)=h2/2−h/4+1/24−5/2−h1​,
(109)
with
J′(h)=h−1/4−(5/2−h)21​,J′′(h)=1−(5/2−h)32​>0.
(110)
It has its unique minimum at h=1/2, where J′=0. The selected interval is then [1/2,1] and its threshold is h2. Consequently
p∗​=1/4 is globally optimal.​
(111)
The terminal condition also checks: 1/4+R′(1)=1/4−1/4=p(1)=0.
