A divisor on a compact connected Riemann surface is a finite formal sum D=∑p​np​p with integer coefficients; its degree is ∑p​np​. A canonical divisor is the divisor of zeros and poles of a nonzero meromorphic differential. Two divisors are linearly equivalent when their difference is the divisor of a nonzero meromorphic function. Such principal divisors have degree zero, but equal degree does not generally imply linear equivalence of divisors.
For example take distinct points p,q on a genus-one surface. If p−q were principal, its function would define a degree-one meromorphic map to the sphere, since it has just one simple pole. A degree-one map between compact Riemann surfaces is a biholomorphism, contradicting their different genera. Thus these degree-one divisors are not equivalent.
Define L(D)={f meromorphic:(f)+D≥0}∪{0} and ℓ(D)=dimC​L(D). The Riemann-Roch theorem states
ℓ(D)−ℓ(K−D)=degD+1−g.
(47)
Since all holomorphic functions on the compact surface are constant, ℓ(0)=1. Setting D=0 gives ℓ(K)=g. Multiplication by a fixed meromorphic differential identifies L(K) with holomorphic differentials, so their dimension is g. Setting D=K then gives
dimH0(S,Ω1)=g,degK=2g−2.​
(48)
For g=2, choose a nonzero holomorphic differential, whose effective canonical divisor has degree two. Since ℓ(K)=2, L(K) contains a nonconstant meromorphic function. Its pole divisor is bounded by that effective K, proving existence of a nonconstant function with total pole multiplicity at most two.
