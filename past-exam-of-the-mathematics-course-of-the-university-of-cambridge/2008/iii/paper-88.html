For p2+q2>1, m=ip2+q2−1​ and the Weyl plane-wave representation contains evanescent waves. Their amplitudes decay exponentially with the distance from the scattering region. Excluding them removes high transverse spatial frequencies, including subwavelength information. Near-field measurements can in principle detect them, but back-propagating their decay exponentially amplifies measurement errors.
For propagating data, the Fourier transfer satisfies ∣ks​−ki​∣≤2k. One illumination still samples only the Ewald sphere, so this frequency bound does not imply access to every mode inside that ball. The lack of a volumetric spectrum is the separate nonuniqueness described in the previous part.
The Born approximation requires the incident field to remain a good approximation inside the scatterer. A useful sufficient smallness estimate for a weak, extended inclusion is small accumulated phase kD∣n−1∣≪1, alongside weak reflection and multiple scattering. Small pointwise contrast alone can fail for a thick inclusion or a resonance. The scalar Helmholtz and background-medium assumptions, and the extra linearization of n2−1, also belong to the model's validity conditions.
Numerically, a finite aperture windows the measured fields and limits angular and Fourier resolution. Discrete sampling must satisfy the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem for the retained transverse bandwidth; spacings Δx,Δy≲π/Kmax​ avoid its simplest aliasing. Finite discrete Fourier transforms also introduce periodic continuation, truncation, interpolation and window errors. Noise, unresolved evanescent modes and near-grazing modes with m≃0 limit reliable recovery. Refining the grid cannot by itself fill the missing three-dimensional Fourier data.

A Sobolev norm penalty can instead express a smoothness preference. Reality, known support and n=1−V/k>0 can be imposed as constraints. For the unconstrained complex Hilbert-space problem with the displayed quadratic penalty, write A=(A+​,A−​) and d=(d+​,d−​). Its Tikhonov normal equation is
(A∗A+αI)Vα​=A∗d,Vα​=(A∗A+αI)−1A∗d.
(54)
Tikhonov regularization selects a stable preferred estimate; it does not establish that the original two-plane data uniquely determine the true contrast.
