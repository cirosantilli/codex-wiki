Put κ=(μ−r)/σ, assuming σ>0, and let
ξt​=exp(−rt−κWt​−21​κ2t).
(1)
This state-price density prices the bank account and the risky asset. The Itô formula gives
d(ξt​wt​)+ξt​ct​dt=ξt​(σθt​−κwt​)dWt​.
(2)
Nonnegative wealth and consumption make the deflated cumulative gains a nonnegative local martingale, hence a supermartingale. Every admissible consumption stream therefore satisfies the state-price budget constraint E∫0∞​ξt​ct​dt≤w0​.
Write I=(U′)−1 for the inverse marginal utility. The Inada conditions make I decreasing from infinity to zero. A positive Lagrange multiplier y for the budget gives the pointwise condition e−ρtU′(ct​)=yξt​. Thus, whenever a finite optimum exists and a multiplier exhausts the budget,
ct∗​=I(yeρtξt​),E∫0∞​ξt​I(yeρtξt​)dt=w0​.​
(3)
The concave supporting-tangent inequality, integrated against the budget, proves optimality. These curvature and endpoint assumptions alone do not ensure that the infinite-horizon value is finite; part (b) gives the exact additional condition for power utility.
The optimal wealth is the conditional price of remaining consumption:
wt∗​=ξt−1​E[∫t∞​ξs​cs∗​ds​Ft​].
(4)
It is nonnegative. Apply the Martingale representation theorem to the integrable total discounted consumption claim:
Mt​=E[∫0∞​ξs​cs∗​ds​Ft​]=w0​+∫0t​ϕs​dWs​.
(5)
Comparing Mt​=ξt​wt∗​+∫0t​ξs​cs∗​ds with the deflated wealth equation yields
θt∗​=σ1​(ξt​ϕt​​+κwt∗​).​
(6)
This constructs the portfolio without assuming extra smoothness of the value function. If the stationary value V is twice differentiable with V′′<0, optimization of its Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation instead gives θ∗(w)=−(μ−r)V′(w)/(σ2V′′(w)) and c∗(w)=I(V′(w)).

For constant relative risk aversion utility, I(z)=z−1/R. Define the Merton consumption constant
γM​=Rρ−(1−R)(r+κ2/(2R))​.
(7)
The Gaussian exponential moment gives
E[ξt​(yeρtξt​)−1/R]=y−1/Re−γM​t.
(8)
When γM​>0, the budget is w0​=y−1/R/γM​. Conditional pricing then gives ct∗​=γM​wt∗​, and the Merton consumption-investment problem has
V(w)=1−RγM−R​w1−R​,ct∗​=γM​wt∗​,θt∗​=Rσ2μ−r​wt∗​.​
(9)
The resulting wealth is a geometric Brownian motion, so positive initial wealth remains positive. Substitution in the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation verifies the first-order conditions and the value; the budget argument in part (a) supplies global optimality.
The problem has a finite value precisely when γM​>0. For 0<R<1 and γM​≤0, the supremum is +∞. Indeed, use the displayed risky fraction and consumption ct​=kwt​, k>0. Its expected utility is
1−Rw1−Rk1−R​∫0∞​e−[RγM​+(1−R)k]tdt.
(10)
For γM​<0, sufficiently small k already gives infinite utility. For γM​=0, the finite values tend to infinity as k↓0.
For R>1 and γM​≤0, every admissible stream has utility −∞. To see this, put q=(R−1)/R and apply Holder inequality on [0,T]×Ω:
∫0T​e−γM​tdt=E∫0T​(e−ρtct1−R​)1/R(ξt​ct​)(R−1)/Rdt≤(E∫0T​e−ρtct1−R​dt)1/Rw0(R−1)/R​.
(11)
The left side diverges as T→∞, forcing the positive utility-cost integral to diverge. Thus there is no finite-value optimization problem in either ill-posed case.

In a price-taking equilibrium, market clearing fixes aggregate consumption at the dividend δt​, so cti​=pti​δt​ and ∑i​pti​=1. Each agent takes this aggregate benchmark as given when varying their own consumption. Let ξt​ be a common state-price density and yi​>0 their budget multipliers. Marginal pricing gives
e−ρi​tδt​Ui′​(pti​)​=yi​ξt​.
(12)
Write Ii​=(Ui′​)−1 and bt​=ξt​δt​. Then
pti​=Ii​(yi​eρi​tbt​),∑i​Ii​(yi​eρi​tbt​)=1.
(13)
For fixed t, the second left side is continuous and strictly decreasing from infinity to zero, so it determines a unique positive number b(t). Every coefficient in that equation is deterministic. Consequently
pti​=Ii​(yi​eρi​tb(t)) is deterministic,ξt​=δt​b(t)​.​
(14)
This is the relative-consumption share equilibrium. The absolute consumption streams remain random through δt​.
Assuming finite fundamental prices, the ex-dividend price of the productive asset follows from conditional pricing:
St​=ξt−1​Et​∫t∞​ξs​δs​ds=b(t)δt​​∫t∞​b(s)ds.​
(15)
The initial state-price budget constraints determine the multipliers up to a common normalization:
∫0∞​b(t)Ii​(yi​eρi​tb(t))dt=π0i​∫0∞​b(t)dt,i=1,…,J.
(16)
Together with the scalar market-clearing equation these characterize the equilibrium. A common scaling of all yi​ is offset by the reciprocal scaling of b. At least one redundant budget equation can be removed, since the initial fractions sum to one.
For completeness, define B(t)=∫t∞​b(s)ds and Bi​(t)=∫t∞​b(s)psi​ds. The consumption-financing wealth is wti​=δt​Bi​(t)/b(t). Its stochastic exposure and that of St​ are both proportional to δt​. Thus holding Bi​(t)/B(t) units of the productive asset, with no bank-account balance, finances this wealth and consumption. Indeed, the holding's deterministic changes and dividend consumption balance because Bi′​=−bpi and B′=−b.
If all ρi​=ρ and Ui​=U, market clearing gives b(t)=ke−ρt for a constant k>0, and pti​=I(yi​k) is constant. The budget equations then imply
pti​=π0i​,cti​=π0i​δt​,St​=δt​/ρ.​
(17)
For differentiable b, the general equilibrium has short rate rt​=μ−σ2−b′(t)/b(t) and risky-asset total expected return rt​+σ2; its volatility is σ. These follow directly from ξt​=b(t)/δt​ and the Itô formula. In the identical-agent case, r=μ−σ2+ρ.

Use κ=(μ−r)/σ and γM​ from Question 1, and take the finite-value case γM​>0. After retirement the value is VM​(w)=γM−R​w1−R/(1−R). Working for an infinitesimal extra interval while using the retired portfolio and consumption produces a gain εVM′​(w)−λ. Its zero is
wmyopic​=γM−1​(ε/λ)1/R.
(18)
This proves the instantaneous indifference level appearing in the question. For the stated irreversible optimal stopping problem it is generally a lower bound on the retirement boundary, rather than the boundary itself: retaining the opportunity to keep working has value. The following explicit wealth-variable Legendre dual calculation exhibits the correction. In particular, imposing continuity of V′′ at retirement would discard that option value; smooth fit requires continuity of V′.
Assume the usual borrowing-against-income solvency condition: w>−ε/r while working, with nonnegative wealth after retirement, and take r>0, ρ>0, κ=0. The question does not specify a tighter borrowing constraint. Let J(z)=supw​{V(w)−zw}, so w=−J′(z) and V=J−zJ′. The working Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation becomes the linear Cauchy-Euler equation
21​κ2z2J′′+(ρ−r)zJ′−ρJ+1−RR​z1−1/R+εz−λ=0.
(19)
The retired dual is JM​(z)=Rz1−1/R/[(1−R)γM​]. Let m<0 be the negative root of
21​κ2m(m−1)+(ρ−r)m−ρ=0.
(20)
The other root exceeds one. Its growing term is excluded by the solvency asymptotic as z→∞. Therefore the working dual has the form
J(z)=JM​(z)+rε​z−ρλ​+Dzm.
(21)
Retirement corresponds to z≤z∗​, where J=JM​. Value matching and smooth fit give
rεz∗​​−ρλ​+Dz∗m​=0,rε​+Dmz∗m−1​=0.
(22)
Consequently the retirement boundary with an income option is
z∗​=ερλr​m−1m​,D=−rmε​z∗1−m​>0,w=γM−1​z∗−1/R​.​
(23)
Using the root equation, z∗​/(λ/ε)=1+κ2m/(2ρ), which lies strictly between zero and one. Thus w>wmyopic​ for a nonzero risk premium. For example, r=ρ=ε=λ=κ=1 and R=2 give m=−1, γM​=9/8, and
w=982​​,wmyopic​=98​.
(24)
These parameters give an explicit counterexample to identifying the printed level with the irreversible retirement boundary.
For −ε/r<w<w, find the unique z>z∗​ from
w=γM−1​z−1/R−rε​−Dmzm−1.
(25)
This right side decreases strictly from w to −ε/r, since J′′>0. Recover the value and controls by
V(w)=J(z)+zw,c∗(w)=z−1/R,θ∗(w)=σ2μ−r​zJ′′(z).​
(26)
For w≥w, retire immediately and use the Merton controls. Along the working strategy, zt​ satisfies dzt​=(ρ−r)zt​dt−κzt​dWt​, and the optimal retirement time is its first hitting time of z∗​, equivalently wealth's first hitting time of w.
To check the optimal stopping inequalities, write h=J−JM​. It satisfies h(z∗​)=h′(z∗​)=0 and h′′(z)=Dm(m−1)zm−2>0 for z>z∗​. Hence continuation has positive option value there. On z≤z∗​ the stopping candidate has working residual εz−λ≤0. The matched dual is continuously differentiable, so the Itô formula introduces no boundary local-time term. Localization, the state-price budget, and the appropriate infinite-horizon transversality justify verification.
If nonnegative financial wealth is imposed during employment as an additional constraint, the working dual also has an upper boundary, and that extra constraint must be included in the free-boundary problem. It cannot be recovered by assuming the printed myopic equality.

Assume the regime is observed and σi​=σ(i)>0. With μi​=μ(i), the wealth dynamics are
dwt​=[rwt​+(μξt​​−r)θt​−ct​]dt+σξt​​θt​dWt​.​
(27)
Let Vi​(w) be the value function conditional on the continuous-time Markov chain being in state i. Its generator adds ∑j​qij​Vj​(w) to the diffusion generator. The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation is
0=supc>0,θ​{U(c)+(rw+(μi​−r)θ−c)Vi′​+21​σi2​θ2Vi′′​}+∑j​qij​Vj​−ρVi​.
(28)
For Vi′​>0, Vi′′​<0, the maximizing controls are c=I(Vi′​) and θ=−(μi​−r)Vi′​/(σi2​Vi′′​). Put κi​=(μi​−r)/σi​. The optimized equation is
0=U(Vi′​)+rwVi′​−2Vi′′​κi2​(Vi′​)2​+∑j​qij​Vj​−ρVi​.
(29)
Here U is the utility conjugate.
Choose the normalization U(c)=c1−R/(1−R); an additive utility constant shifts every value by that constant divided by ρ. Homogeneity gives Vi​(w)=Ai​w1−R/(1−R) with Ai​>0. The regime-switching Merton equations reduce the differential system to one nonlinear algebraic equation per regime:
[ρ−(1−R)(r+2Rκi2​​)]Ai​−j∑​qij​Aj​−RAi1−1/R​=0.​
(30)
The candidate feedback controls are
ci∗​(w)=Ai−1/R​w,θi∗​(w)=Rσi2​μi​−r​w.​
(31)
Changing the regime changes consumption's wealth coefficient, while the risky fraction uses the current regime's risk premium and volatility.
Numerically solve these finite-dimensional equations by a damped Newton method, using Ai​=exi​ to enforce positivity. For di​=ρ−(1−R)(r+κi2​/(2R)), the Jacobian in the A variables is
∂Aj​∂Fi​​=di​1{i=j}​−qij​−(R−1)Ai−1/R​1{i=j}​.
(32)
Multiply its jth column by Aj​ in the logarithmic variables. A residual-decreasing line search and continuation from Q=0, where the positive solution is the scalar Merton coefficient in each well-posed regime, give a practical method whenever that starting problem is finite. More generally use a positive initialization for the coupled system, and check residuals, positivity and conditioning rather than accepting an arbitrary algebraic root.
Verification requires constructing admissible feedback controls, proving the resulting wealth stays nonnegative, and establishing the stochastic-integral and transversality bounds needed at infinite horizon. Apply the Itô formula for semimartingales with jumps to e−ρtVξt​​(wt​), stopping before wealth leaves a compact interval. The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation makes accumulated utility plus discounted candidate value a local supermartingale for every admissible policy and a local martingale under the feedback. Justify removal of localization and let the terminal discounted value vanish under the candidate. For 0<R<1, the nonnegative terminal value can be dropped for the upper bound; for R>1 it is negative, so a transversality or dual integrability argument is essential. This establishes the global value bound and attainment, rather than just stationarity of the algebraic equations. The boundary is Vi​(0)=0 for R<1 and the limiting value −∞ for R>1.
