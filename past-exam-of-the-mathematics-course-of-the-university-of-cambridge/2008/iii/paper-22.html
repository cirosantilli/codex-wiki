The complex structure on a complex manifold splits its complexified cotangent bundle into the +i and −i eigenspaces, locally spanned by dzj and dzˉj. A differential form of type (p, q) is a smooth section of ⋀p(T1,0X)∗⊗⋀q(T0,1X)∗, so in holomorphic coordinates it has the expression
η=∑∣I∣=p,∣J∣=q​ηI,J​dzI∧dzˉJ.
(1)
Here the increasing multi-indices avoid duplicating alternating factors. The exterior derivative has just two type components: the conjugate Dolbeault operator ∂ and the Dolbeault operator ∂ˉ. On such a local expression they are
∂η=∑I,J,k​∂zk∂ηI,J​​dzk∧dzI∧dzˉJ,∂ˉη=∑I,J,k​∂zˉk∂ηI,J​​dzˉk∧dzI∧dzˉJ.
(2)
Holomorphic changes of coordinates preserve these types, so the definitions are intrinsic. Comparing type components of d2=0 gives ∂2=∂ˉ2=0 and ∂∂ˉ+∂ˉ∂=0.
A real (p, p)-form satisfies ηˉ​=η, where complex conjugation acts on both coefficients and differential factors. Conjugation exchanges the two Dolbeault operators, so
∂η=∂ˉη​.
(3)
Thus ∂ˉη=0 forces ∂η=0 as well. Conversely, if dη=0, its distinct (p+1,p) and (p,p+1) components both vanish. Therefore
dη=0⟺∂ˉη=0for real η of type (p,p).​
(4)
The assertion remains valid in top degree, when the indicated type components are zero automatically.
For a holomorphic function f, ∂ˉf=0 and ∂fˉ​=0. Writing its real part as u=(f+fˉ​)/2 gives
∂ˉ∂u=21​∂ˉ∂f+21​∂ˉ∂fˉ​=−21​∂∂ˉf+0=0.
(5)
Hence the real part of a holomorphic function is a pluriharmonic function.
For the local exactness assertion, work on a smaller polydisc whose closure lies in the original one. The allowed one-variable integral is the Cauchy-Green operator Tj​ in coordinate zj​. Applied coefficientwise, it satisfies ∂zˉj​​Tj​a=a on a smaller disc and commutes with differentiation in all other coordinates; the smooth parameter dependence follows by applying the integral to a smooth compactly supported extension in the integration variable. This extension can be made with a cutoff equal to one on the smaller disc.
Begin with a Dolbeault operator closed form φ and decompose it uniquely as φ=dzˉ1∧α+β, where neither α nor β contains dzˉ1. Set ψ1​=T1​α. The dzˉ1 component of ∂ˉψ1​ is exactly dzˉ1∧α, so
φ1​=φ−∂ˉψ1​
(6)
has no dzˉ1 factor. It is still ∂ˉ-closed. The dzˉ1 component of ∂ˉφ1​=0 now shows that every coefficient of φ1​ is holomorphic in z1​.
Apply the same construction in z2​ to remove its antiholomorphic differential factor. The operator T2​ preserves holomorphicity in z1​, since it commutes with ∂zˉ1​​. Consequently the second subtraction introduces no previously removed factor. Continue through all n coordinates, shrinking the polydisc when necessary. The final remainder has positive antiholomorphic degree but contains none of the dzˉj, and hence is zero. This coordinate elimination for local Dolbeault primitives constructs
ψ=ψ1​+⋯+ψn​,∂ˉψ=φ∣U0​​.​
(7)
If q>n, the original form is already zero, so one can take ψ=0.
Finally let φ be a smooth (0,1)-form on the complex projective line. Choose 0<r<R and the two coordinate neighborhoods U={∣z∣<R} and V={∣z∣>r}∪{∞}, the latter expressed in w=1/z. They cover the sphere. The one-variable construction, integrating over slightly larger closed discs in each coordinate chart, gives smooth functions a on U and b on V with ∂ˉa=φ∣U​ and ∂ˉb=φ∣V​.
Their difference h=a−b is a holomorphic function on the annulus r<∣z∣<R. Its Laurent series splits as
h(z)=h+​(z)+h−​(z),h+​=∑m≥0​cm​zm,h−​=∑m<0​cm​zm.
(8)
The first series extends holomorphically throughout U, while the second extends throughout V, including infinity, where it has value zero. Thus a−h+​ and b+h−​ agree on the overlap and glue to a global smooth function F. Both corrections are holomorphic, so
∂ˉF=φ,H∂ˉ0,1​(P1)=0.​
(9)
This Laurent gluing of Dolbeault primitives on the projective line uses no Hodge theorem.

A holomorphic line bundle L→X is a rank-one holomorphic vector bundle: it has local trivializations L∣Ui​​≅Ui​×C whose fiberwise linear transition functions of a vector bundle are nowhere-zero holomorphic functions. Equivalently, choose local holomorphic frames ei​ and write ej​=gij​ei​ on overlaps, with gij​∈O∗(Ui​∩Uj​) and the cocycle relation on triple overlaps. A holomorphic section over U has coefficients si​ holomorphic on U∩Ui​ in s=si​ei​; consistency means si​=gij​sj​.
The dual bundle has fiber Lx∗​=HomC​(Lx​,C). Its dual frames ei∗​ are characterized by ei∗​(ei​)=1, and the frame relation becomes ej∗​=gij−1​ei∗​. Reciprocals of nowhere-zero holomorphic functions are holomorphic, and their cocycle relations persist. This defines the holomorphic dual vector bundle structure on L∗, with holomorphic evaluation pairing L∗⊗L→OX​.
Assume now that X is compact and connected, and let s and t be nonzero global holomorphic sections of L and L∗, respectively. The function t(s) is a global holomorphic function. The maximum modulus principle, applied in local complex coordinate lines near a maximum and then propagated on the connected manifold, makes it constant. Moreover it is not identically zero: the zero locus of a nonzero holomorphic section has empty interior by the identity theorem, so the two nonvanishing loci are dense open sets and intersect. Thus t(s)=c=0 everywhere.
In particular, s never vanishes. The map (x,a)↦as(x) is a holomorphic bundle isomorphism X×C→L; in a local frame its inverse divides by the nowhere-zero holomorphic coefficient of s. Conversely, a holomorphic trivialization supplies the constant unit sections of both L and L∗. We have proved the two-section criterion for holomorphic triviality:
L is holomorphically trivial⟺H0(X,L)=0 and H0(X,L∗)=0.​

Write points of Complex projective space as lines [Z]⊂Cn+1. The complex tautological line bundle is
O(−1)={([Z],v):v∈CZ}⊂Pn×Cn+1,
(11)
with fiber that very line. Its dual is the hyperplane line bundle O(1). On the affine chart Ui​={Zi​=0}, the vector ei​([Z])=Z/Zi​ is a nowhere-zero holomorphic frame of O(−1). On Ui​∩Uj​,
ej​=Zj​Zi​​ei​,ej∗​=Zi​Zj​​ei∗​.
(12)
Both ratios are nowhere-zero holomorphic functions of the affine coordinates. They exhibit the holomorphic transition functions of a vector bundle for the two bundles explicitly.
Choose a nonzero linear functional ℓ:Cn+1→C whose zero hyperplane is H. At [Z], its restriction to CZ is an element of that line's dual and therefore defines a holomorphic section sℓ​ of O(1). In the dual frame above its coefficient is
sℓ​=Zi​ℓ(Z)​ei∗​on Ui​.
(13)
This expression is holomorphic even along H, and its zero locus is exactly H. Consequently sℓ​∣Pn∖H​ is the requested nowhere-zero section; its global holomorphic extension vanishes precisely on H. The extension need not, and does not, stay nowhere zero on the hyperplane.

On a complex manifold, a divisor on a complex manifold is a locally finite formal sum D=∑Y​mY​Y, where Y ranges over irreducible complex analytic hypersurfaces and mY​∈Z. The local algebra needed here is that the local ring OX,x​, identified in coordinates with convergent power-series germs, is a regular local ring and a unique factorization domain. In particular, each height-one prime is generated by one irreducible germ, and a nonzero meromorphic germ is a quotient of holomorphic germs. Reduced local equations of the same hypersurface differ by a holomorphic unit. These facts include local branches when a globally irreducible hypersurface has several branches at a singular point.
The order of a meromorphic function along a hypersurface is the exponent of its reduced local equation in the quotient's factorization. Equivalently, at a generic smooth point of Y, write f=tmu, with t a transverse local equation of Y and u a holomorphic unit; then ordY​(f)=m. Changing a reduced equation multiplies it by a unit and leaves m unchanged. These local orders agree on overlaps and are constant along the generic smooth locus of an irreducible hypersurface. A nonzero global meromorphic function therefore defines its principal divisor on a complex manifold
div(f)=∑Y​ordY​(f)Y.
(14)
Positive coefficients record zeros and negative coefficients record poles.
A local defining function fi​ for D on Ui​ is a nonzero meromorphic function whose orders there equal the coefficients of D. It can be constructed as the product of local reduced hypersurface equations to the prescribed integer powers. On an overlap, fi​/fj​ has no zero or pole along any hypersurface. The unique factorization domain property then makes it a nowhere-zero holomorphic function, after choosing representatives of the germs. The holomorphic line bundle associated to a divisor is obtained by gluing holomorphic frames using
ei​=fi​fj​​ej​.
(15)
These ratios satisfy the cocycle condition, and the expressions fi​ei​ agree on overlaps. They form the canonical meromorphic section of a holomorphic line bundle with divisor D.
If D=div(f) globally, fi​/f is a holomorphic unit on Ui​, and (fi​/f)ei​ is a global nowhere-zero holomorphic section. Hence [D] is holomorphically trivial. Conversely, if [D] has a nowhere-zero global holomorphic frame e, write ei​=ai​e with ai​ holomorphic units. The coefficients fi​ai​ of the canonical meromorphic section of a holomorphic line bundle agree on overlaps and give a nonzero global meromorphic function f. Since multiplication by a unit does not change orders, div(f)=D. Therefore
[D]≅OX​⟺D is principal.​
(16)
For a smooth hypersurface Y⊂X, the adjunction formula is
KY​≅(KX​⊗[Y])∣Y​.​
(17)
Indeed, the holomorphic conormal sequence has first term NY/X∗​≅[−Y]∣Y​; taking its determinant yields KX​∣Y​≅NY/X∗​⊗KY​, which gives the formula. Here the canonical bundle is the top exterior power of the holomorphic cotangent bundle.
Apply this first to W⊂P4. Its degree-d1​ equation is a section of O(d1​), so [W]≅O(d1​). Since KP4​≅O(−5), we obtain KW​≅OW​(d1​−5). The restricted second equation cuts out S in W and has associated bundle OW​(d2​). A second application of adjunction formula gives the canonical bundle of a regular projective complete intersection
KS​≅OS​(d1​+d2​−5).
(18)
It remains to prove that a nonzero exponent cannot yield a trivial holomorphic line bundle on S. Choose a connected component S0​ and a hyperplane meeting but not containing it. Such a hyperplane exists: a positive-dimensional compact projective submanifold cannot lie entirely in an affine chart, since all its affine coordinate functions would be global holomorphic functions and hence constant. A hyperplane through one point and not another consequently meets S0​ properly. Its equation gives a holomorphic section of OS0​​(1) that has both a zero and a nonzero value. For m>0, the mth power has the same two properties. Were OS0​​(m) trivial, this section would be represented by a global holomorphic function, contradicting its constancy on compact connected S0​. For m<0, triviality would imply triviality of its positive dual power. This proves nontrivial hyperplane powers on a compact projective submanifold and hence
d1​+d2​=5,(d1​,d2​)=(1,4),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1).​
(19)
The degrees are positive because the two equations define a hypersurface and a surface of the specified dimensions.
The calculation uses “non-singular” in the usual smooth complete-intersection sense: the equations define reduced smooth divisors, with independent differentials along S. If only their reduced zero sets are required to be smooth, polynomial multiplicities cannot determine the canonical bundle. For example, Q1​=Z0m​ and Q2​=Z14​+Z24​+Z34​+Z44​ have the same smooth quartic surface in the hyperplane Z0​=0 for every m≥1. That surface has trivial canonical bundle, while the displayed polynomial degree sum is m+4. Thus unreduced equations must not be substituted into the adjunction formula for the reduced surface.

Let g be the Riemannian metric underlying the Hermitian metric and J its compatible complex structure. The fundamental form of a Hermitian manifold is ω(u,v)=g(Ju,v). It is a real alternating form of type (1,1). At a point choose an orthonormal real coframe x1,y1,…,xn,yn adapted to J, with xj+iyj of type (1,0). Then
ω=∑j=1n​xj∧yj,dVg​=x1∧y1∧⋯∧xn∧yn=n!ωn​.​
(20)
The orientation here is the complex orientation. In coordinates for which g=∑j​((dxj)2+(dyj)2), the same convention reads ω=(i/2)∑j​dzj∧dzˉj.
Use the complex Hodge star operator, the complex-linear extension of the real Hodge star operator. With the pointwise Hermitian inner product linear in its first variable, its defining identity is
α∧∗βˉ​=⟨α,β⟩g​dVg​
(21)
for forms of the same total degree. It maps type (p,q) to (n−q,n−p) and satisfies ∗2=(−1)p+q in real dimension 2n. It commutes with complex conjugation. These conventions distinguish it from the conjugate-linear Hodge star α↦∗αˉ.
Write a top holomorphic-type form as η=aζ1∧⋯∧ζn, where ζj=xj+iyj. On each oriented two-plane, ∗xj=yj and ∗yj=−xj, so the two-dimensional star of ζj is −iζj. In taking the star of their product, regrouping complementary one-form factors across the n two-planes contributes (−1)n(n−1)/2. Thus the Hodge star on top holomorphic forms is
∗η=(−1)n(n−1)/2(−i)nη,∗η=(−1)n(n+1)/2inη.​
(22)
This is pointwise and requires neither holomorphicity of the coefficient a nor closedness of ω.
To compute the formal adjoint, take compactly supported forms α of degree k−1 and β of degree k. The Stokes theorem gives
∫X​dα∧∗βˉ​=(−1)k∫X​α∧d(∗βˉ​).
(23)
Since the degree of d(∗βˉ​) is 2n−k+1, the identity for ∗2 makes the right side equal to ∫X​α∧∗(−∗d∗β)​. Consequently the formal adjoint of d in even real dimension is d∗=−∗d∗. Splitting by type, −∗∂∗ lowers antiholomorphic degree, while −∗∂ˉ∗ lowers holomorphic degree. Orthogonality of distinct types therefore yields
∂ˉ∗=−∗∂∗,∂∗=−∗∂ˉ∗.​
(24)
The argument applies locally with compact support, and globally without support restrictions on a compact manifold without boundary.
The Dolbeault Laplacian is Δ∂ˉ​=∂ˉ∂ˉ∗+∂ˉ∗∂ˉ. A form is ∂ˉ-harmonic when it lies in its kernel. On a compact Hermitian manifold, the adjoint identities imply
⟨Δ∂ˉ​α,α⟩L2​=∥∂ˉα∥L22​+∥∂ˉ∗α∥L22​.
(25)
Thus harmonicity is equivalent to both first-order equations vanishing. The Dolbeault Hodge decomposition on a compact Hermitian manifold states that the harmonic space H∂ˉp,q​ is finite-dimensional and that smooth forms have the orthogonal direct-sum decomposition
Ωp,q(X)=H∂ˉp,q​⊕∂ˉΩp,q−1(X)⊕∂ˉ∗Ωp,q+1(X).
(26)
To deduce the identification with Dolbeault cohomology, let ∂ˉα=0 and decompose α=h+∂ˉu+∂ˉ∗v. The closedness of α makes it orthogonal to the entire image of ∂ˉ∗. Taking its inner product with ∂ˉ∗v and using the orthogonality of the decomposition gives ∥∂ˉ∗v∥2=0. Hence α=h+∂ˉu, so each cohomology class has a harmonic representative. If a harmonic form equals ∂ˉu, its squared norm is ⟨∂ˉ∗h,u⟩=0, so the representative is unique. We have proved
H∂ˉp,q​⟶H∂ˉp,q​(X),h⟼[h], is an isomorphism.​
(27)
For the complementary types, define Cα=∗αˉ. It maps (p,q) to (n−p,n−q) and is conjugate-linear. If α is harmonic, then ∂ˉα=0 and ∂(∗α)=0 by the adjoint formula. Conjugating the latter equation gives ∂ˉ(∗αˉ)=0. Also, with k=p+q,
∂ˉ∗(Cα)=−∗∂∗(∗αˉ)=−(−1)k∗∂αˉ=0,
(28)
since ∂αˉ=∂ˉα. Therefore C carries harmonic forms to harmonic forms, and C2=(−1)k makes it a bijection. This proves complementary Dolbeault harmonic types on a Hermitian manifold:
dimC​H∂ˉp,q​=dimC​H∂ˉn−p,n−q​.​
(29)
The metric supplies a natural conjugate-linear isomorphism. If a complex-linear isomorphism is desired, choose a basis hj​ of the first space and send it to the basis Chj​ of the second, extending linearly. This last choice is not canonical; no Kähler assumption is needed for either the dimension equality or the harmonic bijection.

For each of the operators D=d,∂,∂ˉ,dc, define its nonnegative Laplacian by ΔD​=DD∗+D∗D using the L2 formal adjoint. In particular, Δ=dd∗+d∗d, Δ∂​=∂∂∗+∂∗∂, Δ∂ˉ​=∂ˉ∂ˉ∗+∂ˉ∗∂ˉ, and Δc=dc(dc)∗+(dc)∗dc. Use the d c operator convention
dc=i(∂ˉ−∂),ddc=2i∂∂ˉ.
(30)
This normalization, without an extra factor of one-half, is the one for which the requested equality with Δc holds.
The Lefschetz operator of a Kähler manifold is Lα=ω∧α, and the adjoint Lefschetz operator is its pointwise metric adjoint Λ=L∗. It lowers type by (1,1) and is a real operator because ω and the metric are real. Write P=∂ and B=∂ˉ, use [A,D]=AD−DA, and use {A,D}=AD+DA for the anticommutators of the odd-degree operators. The allowed relation among the Kähler identities and its complex conjugate give
[Λ,P]=iB∗,[Λ,B]=−iP∗,B∗=−i[Λ,P],P∗=i[Λ,B].
(31)
These identities alone suffice for the calculation. Since P2=B2=0,
{P,B∗}=−i{P,[Λ,P]}=−i(PΛP−P2Λ+ΛP2−PΛP)=0.
(32)
Replacing P by B in the same calculation gives {B,P∗}=0. Thus both mixed terms vanish when expanding the Hodge Laplacian of d=P+B:
Δ=ΔP​+ΔB​.
(33)
The anticommutation PB=−BP now proves equality of the two summands. Indeed,
ΔB​ΔP​​=−i{B,[Λ,P]}=−i(BΛP+PBΛ+ΛPB−PΛB),=i{P,[Λ,B]}=i(PΛB−PBΛ−ΛPB−BΛP),​
(34)
which are the same expression. Finally, (dc)∗=i(P∗−B∗), so its Laplacian expands as
Δc=ΔB​+ΔP​−{B,P∗}−{P,B∗}=ΔB​+ΔP​.
(35)
This proves the Kähler Laplacian identity, with its real-operator version included:
Δ=2Δ∂ˉ​=2Δ∂​=Δc.​
(36)
The action of the almost complex structure on forms is Jα(v1​,…,vk​)=α(Jv1​,…,Jvk​). It multiplies a form of type (p,q) by ip−q. Since Δ=2Δ∂ˉ​ preserves each bidegree, it commutes with this action of J. Thus Δ(Jα)=J(Δα); the invertibility of J gives
Δα=0⟺Δ(Jα)=0.​
(37)
This argument applies to arbitrary sums of types, not only to pure-type forms.
The ddbar lemma for a compact Kähler manifold states that a d-closed form of pure type is ∂∂ˉ-exact if it is d-exact, ∂-exact, or ∂ˉ-exact. Conversely, a ∂∂ˉ-exact form is d-closed and exact for all three operators. In particular a d-exact, d-closed (p,q)-form equals ∂∂ˉβ for a form β of type (p−1,q−1).
The difference γ=ω−ω of the two Kähler forms is a real (1,1)-form, is d-closed, and is d-exact by equality of their de Rham cohomology classes. The ddbar lemma therefore gives a smooth complex-valued function h with γ=∂∂ˉh. Reality and anticommutation imply
γ=γˉ​=∂ˉ∂hˉ=−∂∂ˉhˉ.
(38)
Set f=(h−hˉ)/(2i), which is real. It follows directly that i∂∂ˉf=γ, giving the global potential for cohomologous Kähler forms
ω=ω+i∂∂ˉf,f∈C∞(X,R).​
(39)
If f1​,f2​ are two real potentials, u=f1​−f2​ satisfies ∂∂ˉu=0, so it is a pluriharmonic function. The allowed relation among the Kähler identities gives ∂ˉ∗∂ˉu=−i[Λ,∂]∂ˉu=−iΛ∂∂ˉu=0, because Λ∂ˉu=0 by degree. The Kähler Laplacian identity then gives Δu=2∂ˉ∗∂ˉu=0. Integration by parts on compact X gives
0=⟨Δu,u⟩L2​=∥du∥L22​,
(40)
so u is constant on each connected component. Consequently the potential is unique up to one additive constant when X is connected; on a disconnected X, one constant per component is the exact conclusion. Without the usual connectedness convention the printed uniqueness assertion needs this qualification: distinct constants on the components of a disjoint union leave the same pair of Kähler forms unchanged.
