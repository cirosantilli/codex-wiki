Write q=n−p. In this normal linear model, the ordinary least squares criterion is ∥Y−Xb∥2. Differentiating with respect to b gives the normal equations for linear least squares, XTXb=XTY. Full column rank of the design matrix makes XTX invertible, and completing the square proves that the unique minimum is
β​=(XTX)−1XTY.​
(1)
Since β​=β+(XTX)−1XTϵ, its sampling distribution is the multivariate normal distribution
β​∼Np​(β,σ2(XTX)−1).
(2)
Let H=X(XTX)−1XT be the hat matrix. It is symmetric and idempotent with rank p, and HX=X. The regression residual vector is (I−H)Y=(I−H)ϵ, so
RSS=∥Y−Xβ​∥2=YT(I−H)Y,s2=n−pRSS​.​

The residual projection and fitted projection have zero cross-covariance, hence are independent because they are jointly normal. In particular, s2 is an unbiased estimator of σ2 and is independent of β​. The maximum-likelihood estimator of σ2 instead divides by n and is not unbiased.

Ordinary coefficient inference after choosing a model does not account for the selection itself.

A Poisson distribution has probability mass e−μμy/y!=exp{yθ−eθ−log(y!)}, where θ=logμ. It is an exponential family with b(θ)=eθ, mean μ and variance μ. The responses are independent, their linear predictors are xiT​β, and the log link relates these predictors to their means. These are precisely the components of a generalized linear model; the log link is its canonical link function and the dispersion is one.
The log-likelihood and score function are
ℓ(β)=∑i​{yi​xiT​β−exiT​β−log(yi​!)},U(β)=∑i​xi​{yi​−exiT​β}.
(17)
Thus a finite maximum-likelihood estimator satisfies
i∑​xi​(yi​−μ​i​)=0,μ​i​=exiT​β​.​
(18)
The Hessian is −∑i​μi​xi​xiT​; with a full-rank design matrix it is negative definite at finite parameter values, so a finite solution is the unique maximum. A finite maximum need not exist for every possible response/design combination; the displayed equations describe it when it does exist. If the first component of every xi​ is one, the first score equation gives ∑i​yi​=∑i​μ​i​.
The saturated model fits each mean as yi​, with the limiting mean zero allowed for a zero count. Subtracting the fitted log-likelihood from the saturated log-likelihood gives the Poisson deviance
D=2i∑​[yi​logμ​i​yi​​−(yi​−μ​i​)],​
(19)
where 0log(0/μ​i​)=0. With an intercept, the sum of the second terms is zero, but retaining them is necessary without that score equation.
Take low pH as a reference level. For group j, the model with interaction has
Yi​∼Poisson(μi​),logμi​=α+γj​+(β+δj​)bi​,γlow​=δlow​=0,
(20)
where bi​ is biomass. The additive model sets every δj​ to zero. The interaction model estimates six parameters and the additive model four. In the additive model the log-mean curves are parallel straight lines, while the mean curves are positive exponentials with fixed ratios eγj​−γk​. In the interaction model the log-mean slopes differ, and the mean ratio is eγj​−γk​+(δj​−δk​)b. The following sketches illustrate common versus factor-specific slopes in Poisson regression; their coefficients are illustrative, not estimates from the data.
The additive model is nested in the interaction model with two restrictions. The likelihood-ratio test therefore uses
Dadd​−Dint​=99.2−83.2=16.0 ≈H0​ χ22​,
(21)
whose p-value is e−8=0.0003355. There is strong evidence that the biomass slope on the log-mean scale depends on pH. The interaction-model residual deviance 83.2 is close to its 90−6=84 residual degrees of freedom, giving no obvious indication of overdispersion from this comparison alone. The supplied deviances contain no slope estimates: they do not establish which pH group has more species, whether each slope is positive or negative, or whether any actual curves cross. Those conclusions require the fitted coefficients and their uncertainty.

They are not 15 arbitrary precision weights: the conditional variance of the proportion is πgj​(1−πgj​)/Ngj​. The logit link models log odds, and the two sets of factor coefficients use corner-point constraints.
There are nine parameters: one intercept, seven age contrasts and one city contrast. Hence the residual degrees of freedom are 15−9=6, not the total population minus nine. The null model has only an intercept and 14 residual degrees of freedom. Adding age uses seven more parameters and reduces the binomial deviance from 2330.46 to 232.28, a drop of 2098.19. Adding city then uses one parameter and reduces it by 227.12 to 5.15. These are sequential likelihood-ratio tests, asymptotically compared with chi-squared distributions on seven and one degrees of freedom. Both provide extremely strong evidence of an effect; the printed age p-value 0.00 is rounding, not an exactly zero probability. Because the table is incomplete, sequential main-effect sums need not be invariant to the order of fitting.

For rare events, Binomial(N,π) is approximately Poisson(Nπ). The binomial variance is Nπ(1−π), close to the Poisson variance Nπ, and log{π/(1−π)} is close to logπ. Even the largest observed proportion here is below 0.009. Thus the binomial log-odds model and Poisson log-rate model should give similar fitted counts and tests, as their very similar deviances show.
Both analyses indicate strong age and adjusted city associations, with no apparent large residual lack of fit. The binomial model is natural when each person can contribute at most one case during a fixed period and the denominator counts people at risk. A Poisson count model is natural for event counts with an appropriate exposure or person-time denominator; population size is an approximation to such exposure if the observation periods are comparable. An odds ratio and a rate ratio are different parameters despite being close in this rare-event setting.
The models both assume a common city effect across ages. An age-by-city interaction can be checked by comparing with the saturated model for the 15 observed cells, using the six additional degrees of freedom; the small final deviances offer no substantial evidence against the additive fits in that comparison. This cannot recover the missing city-by-age cell or eliminate unmeasured confounding. Nor is choosing the lower of 5.1509 and 5.21 a valid model-selection rule: these deviances are measured against saturated models for different response distributions.

Survival data describe the time T from a specified origin to an event, together with covariates and information about how observation begins and ends. Some event times are observed exactly; others are subject to censoring or excluded by truncation. Here the time origin is the opening of a business and the event is its closure. An observed record contains an entry age, an exit age, an event indicator and the business characteristics. The time origin and observation mechanism are part of the data specification.

With right censoring, an event has not occurred by the last observation time C, so the data establish only T>C. One records Y=min(T,C) and δ=1{T≤C}​, distinguishing an observed event from a censored exit. A business still operating at the follow-up deadline is administratively right-censored. Its observed age is a lower bound on its lifetime, not its eventual closure age. Standard survival analysis requires suitable independent censoring, possibly conditional on the fitted covariates.

With left truncation, a subject is observed only if it survives to its entry time E: subjects with T≤E are absent from the sample. A business already operating when observation begins has this delayed-entry mechanism. It joins a risk set only after its recorded entry age. Left truncation excludes earlier failures entirely; left censoring instead includes subjects whose event is known to have happened before a specified time. These mechanisms must not be conflated.

Statistician B uses the more appropriate analysis. Its start–stop representation includes left truncation as well as right censoring. Both entry and exit are ages since opening, so a business belongs to the risk set at age t only when ageatentry<t≤obsage. The event indicator distinguishes closure from a censored end of observation. The counting-process representation does not mean that multiple closures per business have been observed.

Before reporting results, check the size coding discrepancy, the precise sampling dates and the survival records. The stated January 2000–December 2005 span is six calendar years, despite being called five years. Check nonnegative ages, entry no later than exit, correctly recorded closure indicators and the same opening-based clock throughout. Establish whether inclusion and censoring are plausibly independent of lifetime given the covariates, and whether different opening cohorts or calendar environments can reasonably share the fitted lifetime model. Simply including delayed entry does not cure informative selection, omitted confounders or dependence between businesses.
Assess proportional hazards using Schoenfeld residuals against time and a proportional hazards assumption test, supplemented by groupwise log-minus-log survival plots. Investigate influential observations with coefficient-deletion diagnostics and deviance residuals, and inspect overall fit using Cox–Snell residuals. Consider a city-by-size interaction or time-varying effects if supported by the available events, rather than relying on the displayed three main effects. Finally, report the uncertainty and declining late risk sets—only six remain at the final listed event—and distinguish a conditional association from a causal explanation or a prediction for all future businesses.
