The concern is that the rapid-decay assumptions need not be preserved by the dynamics. In a constant-density incompressible flow, the divergence of the Navier-Stokes equation gives the pressure Poisson equation for incompressible flow, with π=p/ρ:
∇2π=−∂i​∂j​(ui​uj​).
(16)

π(r)=4π1​∫ui​(y)uj​(y)∂yi​​∂yj​​∣r−y∣1​d3y.
(17)
At large separation the differentiated kernel has leading form (3ni​nj​−δij​)/r3. Thus an anisotropic stress moment of an individual eddy produces a quadrupolar fluid pressure of order r−3, even if the velocity source is spatially localized. Correlating that fluid pressure with local ux2​ allows ⟨ux2​p′⟩=O(r−3). Isotropy may cancel a mean multipole but does not automatically cancel such a mixed fourth-order statistic.
The triple-correlation evolution contains a pressure-gradient term of the form −⟨ux2​∂x′​π′⟩, so these long-range pressure correlations in turbulence can generate a longitudinal triple-correlation tail K∼ar−4. With L=0, part (ii) then gives I˙=8πu3a, which need not vanish. This is the substance of the objection to deducing exact conservation from initially short velocity correlations. Batchelor and Proudman's original long-range calculation concerned anisotropic turbulence; extending it to isotropic decay requires the appropriate higher-order correlations and allows cancellations. A permitted tail is not a proof that its coefficient stays appreciable in fully developed turbulence.
This possibility appealed to workers constructing heuristic turbulence closures. A closure that generated a changing low-wavenumber k4 coefficient and a decay exponent different from 10/7 could then be viewed as representing a pressure-mediated effect, rather than as automatically violating a universal invariant. The closure's far-field coefficient still needs physical validation; closure-generated nonconservation does not itself prove the true dynamics nonconserving.
For evidence available before this exam, the 2006 simulations of Ishida, Davidson and Kaneda found that I approached an approximately constant value after a transient when the box was much larger than the integral scale of turbulence and the Reynolds number was large. The energy decay was correspondingly close to u2∼t−10/7. The observed conclusion was approximate late-time conservation in that regime, indicating weak net long-range triple-correlation transfer, rather than exact conservation for every initial condition, box size or Reynolds number.

The three questions concern different statistics. Taylor turbulent dispersion follows the absolute displacement of one marked fluid element from its release position, or the spreading of the centre of a cloud. Its input is the velocity autocorrelation along a Lagrangian trajectory. It relates short-time ballistic motion to long-time effective diffusion. This is not the shear-flow mechanism ordinarily called Taylor dispersion.
Richardson pair dispersion instead follows the separation of two fluid elements, or the growth of a cloud relative to its moving centre. Common sweeping by an eddy much larger than the cloud contributes to absolute displacement but largely cancels from relative displacement. The relative diffusivity depends on separation; the classical inertial-range prediction is a t3 mean-square separation regime after memory of the release separation is lost.
Obukhov-Corrsin theory, also called Kolmogorov-Obukhov-Corrsin theory, concerns spatial fluctuations of a passive scalar, their transfer across scales and their destruction by molecular diffusion. Its objects are scalar structure functions and scalar spectra, with an inertial-convective prediction controlled by scalar-variance flux and the turbulent energy supply. Thus Taylor describes one-particle transport, Richardson describes relative spreading, and Obukhov-Corrsin describes scale-by-scale scalar mixing. Following a cloud's location is not the same as predicting its internal concentration fluctuations.

The first similarity hypothesis of Kolmogorov 1941 theory states that at sufficiently large Reynolds number, the small-scale statistics in the equilibrium range are locally universal and determined by the mean turbulent kinetic energy dissipation rate ϵ and kinematic viscosity ν, together with the separation or wavenumber being measured. Large-scale forcing details are excluded only at scales well below the integral scale of turbulence.

The intermittency parameter μ must be supplied by dissipation statistics; it is not determined by dimensional analysis. The formula preserves ζ3​=1. Its extrapolation to arbitrarily high orders is not justified: the quadratic correction eventually predicts pathological exponents and is one limitation of the lognormal model, distinct from the refined hypothesis itself.

Separate a smooth large-scale velocity UL​ from a remaining smaller-scale component wL​. If the large-scale variation length is much greater than r, their longitudinal increment has the form
Δv=rA+Δw+O(r2),A=∂L​UL​.
(53)
For a positive integer p, the leading expansion of its structure function is
⟨(Δv)p⟩=∑j=0p​(jp​)rj⟨Aj(Δw)p−j⟩+higher Taylor terms.
(54)
Thus even a velocity component at scales much larger than r enters through its gradients and through mixed moments with the finer field. It is not generally legitimate to discard those mixed moments: large-scale strain can organize and modulate the finer motions. For example, at fourth order the expansion contains
6r2⟨A2(Δw)2⟩+r4⟨A4⟩,
(55)
in addition to the small-scale fourth moment and the generally present odd mixed terms. Even if the components were independent and symmetric, the displayed mixed second-moment term would survive. This explicitly exhibits large-scale contributions to structure functions beyond second order.
The implication for K62 refined similarity is that an observed structure function need not measure a purely scale-local velocity fluctuation. Dividing it by (ϵr​r)1/3 may still leave dependence on outer-scale gradients, correlations or finite-range cutoffs. The universal conditional coefficients βp​ and the identification of a measured exponent with the lognormal intermittency model therefore require additional locality and scale-separation tests. Finite-range mixed contributions can imitate or obscure anomalous scaling. This is an objection to treating the 1962 dimensional argument as an unconditional law for measured increments; it is not, by itself, a proof that an asymptotic refined similarity regime cannot exist.
