The strong maximum principle for harmonic functions states that a C2 harmonic function on a connected domain which attains its supremum at an interior point is constant. Applying it to −u gives the corresponding assertion for an attained infimum.
Let M=u(x0​)=supΩ​u. The ball mean value property for harmonic functions gives, for every sufficiently small ball about x0​,
∫Br​(x0​)​(M−u)dx=0.
(5)
The integrand is continuous and nonnegative. If it were positive at any point, it would be bounded below by a positive number on a smaller ball, contradicting the zero integral. Therefore u=M on the whole ball. The set A={x∈Ω:u(x)=M} is relatively closed by continuity and relatively open by this same argument at each of its points. It is nonempty. Connected space forces A=Ω, proving the principle. If the open set is disconnected, constancy is asserted on the relevant connected component.

First suppose u is a classical harmonic function. At a local maximum of u−v, its Hessian matrix is negative semidefinite, so 0=Δu(x0​)≤Δv(x0​). At a local minimum the Hessian inequality reverses and gives Δv(x0​)≤0. Thus both test conditions hold.
Conversely, suppose only that u is continuous and satisfies the two conditions. We prove the stronger conclusion that these conditions themselves imply u∈C∞ and Δu=0. Use the standard Dirichlet existence theorem on a ball: every continuous function on its boundary has a harmonic extension h∈C∞(BR​(a))∩C(BR​(a)​) with exactly those boundary values. This follows, for example, from the Poisson integral. Apply it to u on an arbitrary relatively compact ball, obtaining a harmonic replacement h.
If u−h is positive somewhere, choose ε>0 small enough that u−h−ε(R2−∣x−a∣2) is still positive there. It vanishes on the boundary and therefore has a positive maximum at an interior point. The upper test
v=h+ε(R2−∣x−a∣2)
(6)
has Δv=−2nε<0, contradicting the maximum test condition. Similarly, if u−h is negative somewhere, v=h−ε(R2−∣x−a∣2) yields an interior negative minimum of u−v for small ε, but Δv=2nε>0, contradicting the minimum condition. Hence u=h throughout the ball.
If the quantifier requires v∈C2(Ω) rather than a local test, multiply the displayed smooth v by a smooth cutoff supported within the ball and equal to one near the touching point, extending by zero outside. The resulting global test has the same germ and Laplacian there, so the argument remains valid. Since every point lies in such a ball, u is a smooth harmonic function throughout Ω​. This is the continuous viscosity harmonic functions are classical characterization.

Choose an upper ellipticity bound Λ<∞ for the symmetric coefficient matrix, possible because its entries are bounded. Then
λ∥Du∥22​≤Q(u,u)≤Λ∥Du∥22​.
(8)
The Poincaré inequality on a bounded open set for zero-boundary functions states ∥u∥2​≤CP​∥Du∥2​. Consequently
1+CP2​λ​∥u∥W1,22​≤Q(u,u)≤Λ∥u∥W1,22​.
(9)
The symmetric positive bilinear form Q is therefore an inner product, and its square-root norm is equivalent to the usual Sobolev norm. Its triangle inequality follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for that inner product. completeness of W01,2​ in its original norm makes it a Hilbert space in the Q norm as well.
The linear functional ℓ(v)=−∫fv is bounded, since
∣ℓ(v)∣≤∥f∥2​∥v∥2​≤CP​λ−1/2∥f∥2​Q(v,v)1/2.
(10)
The Hilbert-space Riesz representation theorem states that every bounded linear functional is the inner product with a unique element. Applied in the Q inner product it gives a unique u with Q(u,v)=ℓ(v) for all v, which is precisely the weak-solution identity. Alternatively uniqueness follows directly by testing the difference of two solutions with itself: Q(u1​−u2​,u1​−u2​)=0. Thus the zero-boundary problem has exactly one weak solution​.

The required functional, with the sign appropriate to a positive divergence operator, is

First, multiplication by w maps W01,2​ into L2 by the Hölder and Sobolev estimates just given. Thus a weak solution u of the perturbed equation has v=Δu=f+wu∈L2. Uniqueness for the unperturbed Dirichlet problem gives u=Tv. Its equation becomes
(I−K)v=f.​
(19)
Conversely, if this equation has a solution v∈L2, then u=Tv belongs to W01,2​ and satisfies Δu=v=f+wTv=f+wu weakly. These constructions are inverse to one another.
The Fredholm alternative for a compact operator states that, on a Banach space, I−K for compact K is bijective if and only if its kernel is zero; in that case its inverse is bounded. Apply it on L2. Kernels correspond under T: a nonzero v with v=wTv gives a nonzero u=Tv, since ΔTv=v; a nonzero homogeneous solution u gives v=wu, which cannot be zero because a zero-boundary harmonic weak solution is zero. Therefore triviality of the homogeneous PDE kernel is equivalent to triviality of ker(I−K) and hence to unique solvability for every f∈L2. The reverse implication also follows immediately by taking f=0. No sign assumption on w is required.

With the convention Lu≥0 for a classical subsolution, the weak maximum principle for elliptic operators is
Ωsup​u≤∂Ωmax​u,u∈C2(Ω)∩C(Ω).​
(20)
Here L is uniformly elliptic with bounded drift, has no zeroth-order term, and the domain is bounded. Equivalently, Lu≤0 gives infΩ​u≥min∂Ω​u.
One can see why measurable coefficients suffice for the pointwise classical statement by adding εeκx1​. Choose κ large enough that λκ>∥b1​∥∞​; then L(eκx1​)=eκx1​(κ2a11​+κb1​)>0. The perturbed subsolution cannot attain a maximum inside: its gradient would be zero and its Hessian negative semidefinite, forcing its elliptic second-order term to be nonpositive. Its maximum is therefore on the boundary. Letting ε↓0 proves the stated estimate. Only the symmetric part of a acts on the Hessian, and ellipticity gives its positive definiteness.

Recall that gi​∈Lloc1​(Ω) is the weak derivative Di​u if u∈Lloc1​(Ω) and ∫uDi​ϕ=−∫gi​ϕ for all ϕ∈Cc∞​(Ω). A global weak derivative restricts to every open neighborhood simply by taking test functions supported there.
Conversely, suppose local weak derivatives exist near each point. local integrability of u follows by a finite cover of each compact subset. On overlapping neighborhoods the two candidates for Di​u have identical integrals against every smooth compactly supported test function. The fundamental lemma for locally integrable functions therefore makes them equal almost everywhere. Choose a countable local cover, possible in Euclidean space; the countably many exceptional overlap sets can all be discarded. The derivatives consequently patch to measurable gi​∈Lloc1​(Ω).
For a test function ϕ, take a smooth partition of unity (χj​) subordinate to the local cover and locally finite on its support. Only finitely many terms occur there. Because ∑j​χj​=1 near the support,
∫Ω​uDi​ϕ=∑j​∫Ω​uDi​(χj​ϕ)=−∑j​∫Ω​gi​χj​ϕ=−∫Ω​gi​ϕ.
(25)
The derivatives of the partition sum to zero, explaining the first equality. Thus the patched functions are the global weak derivatives. This proves both directions of locality of weak differentiability.

∫uχε​Di​ϕ+∫uϕDi​χε​=−∫gi​ϕχε​.

For n=1, take u(x)=0 for x<0 and u(x)=1 for x>0 on (−1,1), with any value at zero. It is bounded and C1 off zero, with classical derivative zero there. But
