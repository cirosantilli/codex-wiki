Take a nonzero complex unital Banach algebra, with identity 1. The spectrum of an element is
σA​(a)={λ∈C:λ1−a is not invertible in A}.​
(18)
The standard Banach algebra spectrum theorem states that it is a nonempty compact subset of C, contained in the disk ∣λ∣≤∥a∥. Conversely, every nonempty compact set K⊆C occurs: in the Banach algebra C(K), the coordinate function a(z)=z has Banach algebra spectrum exactly K, since λ−a has a continuous reciprocal precisely when λ∈/K. Thus the possible Banach algebra spectra are exactly the nonempty compact subsets of the complex plane.
For a nonconstant polynomial p, factor p(z)−λ=c∏j=1n​(z−zj​) over C. Substitution gives
p(a)−λ1=c∏j=1n​(a−zj​1).
(19)
These factors commute. A finite commuting product is invertible exactly when every factor is invertible: if P=∏j​xj​ is invertible, its inverse commutes with each xj​, and xj−1​=(∏k=j​xk​)P−1 is a two-sided inverse. Hence λ belongs to the Banach algebra spectrum of p(a) exactly when some root zj​ belongs to σA​(a), which is equivalent to λ∈p(σA​(a)). Constant polynomials give the same result directly, since σA​(c1)={c}. This proves the polynomial spectral mapping theorem:
σA​(p(a))=p(σA​(a)).​
(20)
For a closed unital Banach subalgebra B⊆A, use the common identity 1A​∈B. An inverse in B is an inverse in A, so σA​(b)⊆σB​(b)​. Let λ∈∂σB​(b). Choose λn​∈/σB​(b) tending to λ. Then λn​∈/σA​(b), and (λn​1−b)−1∈B. If λ were outside σA​(b), continuity of inversion in A would give
(λn​1−b)−1⟶(λ1−b)−1∈B,
(21)
where membership follows from closedness. This would make λ∈/σB​(b), contrary to the closed Banach algebra spectrum having its boundary inside it. Therefore λ∈σA​(b) and is approached by points outside that Banach algebra spectrum, proving
∂σB​(b)⊆∂σA​(b).​
(22)
Continuity of inversion here follows, for example, by writing a nearby element as an invertible element times 1+ a norm-small perturbation and using the Neumann series. This is spectrum in a closed unital subalgebra.
To retain the original Banach algebra spectrum in a commutative subalgebra, take the resolvent-generated commutative algebra
C=alg(1,a,(λ1−a)−1:λ∈/σA​(a))​∥⋅∥.
(23)
Every generator commutes with a and with every other generator: commuting invertible elements have commuting inverses, and their inverses commute with all elements commuting with them. commutativity persists on the algebra they generate and on its norm closure. For every λ∈/σA​(a), the required inverse is in C by construction, so σC​(a)⊆σA​(a). The inclusion for unital subalgebras gives the reverse containment. Hence σC​(a)=σA​(a)​.
Finally, the Banach subalgebra generated by one element is D={p(a):p a polynomial}​, and it is commutative. Put K=σD​(a) and suppose its complement has a bounded connected component U. The boundary of U lies in K. For λ∈U, the maximum modulus principle on a bounded domain applied to any polynomial gives
∣p(λ)∣≤maxz∈∂U​∣p(z)∣≤maxz∈K​∣p(z)∣=rD​(p(a))≤∥p(a)∥.
(24)
Here rD​ is the spectral radius, and the equality uses the already proved polynomial spectral mapping theorem. Define χ(p(a))=p(λ). The displayed bound makes this well defined, even when two polynomials represent the same algebra element, and bounded on the dense polynomial algebra. Its continuous extension to D remains linear and multiplicative, with χ(1)=1 and χ(a)=λ, so it is a character of an algebra. If λ1−a were invertible in D, applying χ to its inverse equation would give 0=1. Thus λ∈K, contradicting λ∈U.
There are therefore no bounded complementary components. A compact subset of the plane has exactly one unbounded complementary component: everything outside a sufficiently large disk is connected, and every unbounded component meets it. Consequently C∖σD​(a) is connected.​
