A proper filter on a set X is a nonempty family F⊆P(X) that excludes ∅, is closed under finite intersections, and is upward closed under inclusion. In particular X∈F. In a topological space, convergence of a filter F→x means that every neighbourhood of x belongs to F. An ultrafilter is a proper filter maximal under inclusion; equivalently, it contains exactly one of A and X∖A for every subset A⊆X.
To justify the equivalence, if A∈/U and every member of U met A, adjoining A would generate a larger proper filter. Maximality therefore supplies F∈U with F∩A=∅, and upward closure gives X∖A∈U. Both complementary sets cannot belong to a proper filter. Conversely, a filter with this dichotomy cannot acquire any new set without also containing its complement, which would give an empty intersection.
The ultrafilter characterization of compact Hausdorff spaces, stated without proof, is: a topological space is compact and Hausdorff exactly when every ultrafilter on it converges to a unique point.
For a finite set partition X=A1​∪⋯∪An​, if no cell belonged to U, the ultrafilter dichotomy would put all X∖Aj​ in U. Their finite intersection is empty, a contradiction. Thus some Aj​∈U, and at most one cell can belong because distinct cells are disjoint. The converse is: a proper filter on a set meeting every finite partition by containing one cell is an ultrafilter. Apply the partition hypothesis to X=A∪(X∖A) to obtain the dichotomy, then the maximality argument above. Empty cells can be omitted, and the cases A=∅ or A=X are automatic.
For a function f:X→Y, the image of a filter is
f∗​F={B⊆Y:f−1(B)∈F}.
(1)
Since preimages preserve inclusions and finite intersections, this is a proper filter on a set Y. Equivalently, it is the filter generated by f(F) for F∈F: if f−1(B)∈F, then its image is contained in B, while f(F)⊆B implies F⊆f−1(B). For an ultrafilter U, the identity f−1(Y∖B)=X∖f−1(B) shows that f∗​U contains exactly one of B,Y∖B. Therefore the pushforward filter of an ultrafilter is an ultrafilter.
Now let x∈ℓ∞, the l-infinity sequence space, and put ax​(n)=n−1∑j=1n​xj​. All these Cesaro means lie in the closed disk Kx​={z:∣z∣≤∥x∥∞​}, a compact Hausdorff space. The pushforward filter ax∗​U contains Kx​; restricting it to that disk and applying the ultrafilter theorem gives a unique limit
l(x)=n→Ulim​ax​(n).​
(2)
For real sequences the disk can be replaced by a closed interval. This is the ultrafilter construction of a Banach limit.
The identities aαx+βy​(n)=αax​(n)+βay​(n) give linearity of l. Explicitly, the two sets on which ∣ax​(n)−l(x)∣<δ and ∣ay​(n)−l(y)∣<δ both belong to U, hence so does their intersection. On that intersection the difference from αl(x)+βl(y) is at most (∣α∣+∣β∣)δ, proving l(αx+βy)=αl(x)+βl(y). If xn​≥0, its means lie in [0,∥x∥∞​], so l(x)≥0. Thus l is a positive linear functional. The bound ∣l(x)∣≤∥x∥∞​ gives ∥l∥≤1, while the constant-one sequence satisfies l(1)=1. Hence ∥l∥=1​.
Because U contains no finite sets, its dichotomy puts every cofinite set in U. If xn​→L ordinarily, the Cesaro theorem for convergent sequences follows directly by splitting the sum: for a fixed N with ∣xj​−L∣<ε for j>N,
∣ax​(n)−L∣≤n1​∑j=1N​∣xj​−L∣+ε(n>N).
(3)
The finite head tends to zero, so ax​(n)→L ordinarily. Every sufficiently long tail belongs to U, making its ultralimit also L. Thus xn​→L⟹l(x)=L​.
Finally, for the left shift on bounded sequences S,
aSx​(n)−ax​(n)=nxn+1​−x1​​,∣aSx​(n)−ax​(n)∣≤n2∥x∥∞​​→0.
(4)
Taking ultralimits along the same cofinite-containing ultrafilter gives l(Sx)=l(x)​. This establishes every defining property of a Banach limit.

We construct the density and singular part, rather than assuming the desired Lebesgue decomposition theorem. Put ρ=μ+ν, a finite dominating measure, and use the real Hilbert space L2(ρ). The linear functional
T(g)=∫Ω​gdν
(5)
is well defined on its equivalence classes, since a ρ-null set is ν-null. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and ν≤ρ give
∣T(g)∣≤ν(Ω)1/2(∫g2dν)1/2≤ν(Ω)1/2∥g∥L2(ρ)​.
(6)
By the Riesz representation theorem, there is h∈L2(ρ) with T(g)=∫ghdρ. Indicators of all measurable sets belong to this Hilbert space, so
ν(A)=∫A​hdρ,μ(A)=∫A​(1−h)dρ(A∈Σ).
(7)
The nonnegativity of these two measures forces 0≤h≤1 almost everywhere. For instance, if ρ({h≤−1/n})>0, its ν-measure would be negative; the analogous sets {h≥1+1/n} contradict positivity of μ. Their countable union excludes every violation. Modify h on its measurable ρ-null exceptional set so that these inequalities hold everywhere. This is the Hilbert-space construction of dominated measure densities.
Define
B={h=1},f={h/(1−h),0,​h<1,h=1.​
(8)
Then B∈Σ, f is a nonnegative measurable function, and μ(B)=∫B​(1−h)dρ=0. The identity for integration against μ follows first for indicators, then for nonnegative simple functions and finally for all nonnegative functions by the monotone convergence theorem. Applying it to f1A​ gives
∫A​fdμ=∫A∖B​hdρ=ν(A∖B).
(9)
In particular ∫Ω​fdμ=ν(Ω∖B)<∞, so f∈L1(μ). Additivity on the disjoint measurable sets A∖B and A∩B now gives
ν(A)=∫A​fdμ+ν(A∩B),μ(B)=0.​
(10)
This is the Lebesgue decomposition from a sum-measure density: the first measure has a density, and the second is concentrated on a μ-null set, so is mutually singular with μ.
The absolute continuity of measures condition ν≪μ means that μ(A)=0 implies ν(A)=0 for every A∈Σ. If this holds, then ν(B)=0, eliminating the singular part above. Conversely, a measure of the form ν(A)=∫A​fdμ vanishes on every μ-null set. Therefore
ν≪μ⟺ν(A)=∫A​fdμ for some nonnegative f∈L1(μ).​
(11)
Thus absolute continuity is exactly the absence of the singular part in this construction.

Give C(G), the space of continuous functions on a compact space, its uniform norm. The left regular action is lg​f(x)=f(g−1x). The group law gives lg​lh​=lgh​ and le​=I, and each translation is a linear isometry because x↦g−1x is a bijection of G.
For fixed f∈C(G), the map (g,x)↦f(g−1x) is continuous. Compactness of the x-space makes this dependence uniform: for a given g0​ and ε>0, continuity gives a neighbourhood of g0​ and a neighbourhood of each x on which the difference from f(g0−1​x) is less than ε; a finite cover of G permits one common neighbourhood of g0​. Hence ∥lg​f−lg0​​f∥∞​→0. The estimate
∥lg​f−lg0​​f0​∥∞​≤∥f−f0​∥∞​+∥lg​f0​−lg0​​f0​∥∞​
(12)
then proves the joint continuity of the action on G×C(G).
For a left action on the continuous dual space, use the contragredient convention
Tg​ϕ=ϕ∘lg−1​.
(13)
It satisfies Tg​Th​=Tgh​ and preserves the dual unit ball B′. If the printed prime denotes the literal transpose lg∗​ϕ=ϕ∘lg​, these maps instead satisfy lg∗​lh∗​=lhg∗​ and form a right action; replacing g by g−1 gives the left action above. Their invariant functionals are the same.
For gi​→g and ϕi​→ϕ in the weak-star topology on B′, every f∈C(G) satisfies
∣(Tgi​​ϕi​)(f)−(Tg​ϕ)(f)∣≤∥lgi−1​​f−lg−1​f∥∞​+∣(ϕi​−ϕ)(lg−1​f)∣⟶0.
(14)
The first term uses strong continuity of translation and ∥ϕi​∥≤1; the second uses weak-star convergence at one fixed function. This proves the requested joint weak-star topology continuity.
To obtain Haar measure, consider the set K of positive linear functionals ϕ on C(G) with ϕ(1)=1. Such functionals have norm one: for complex f, choose a phase making ϕ(f) real and nonnegative and use Re(eitf)≤∥f∥∞​; this gives ∣ϕ(f)∣≤∥f∥∞​, with equality on the constant-one function. Thus K⊆B′. Evaluation at the identity belongs to K, and positivity and normalization are weak-star closed conditions. The Banach-Alaoglu theorem makes K a nonempty compact convex set. Each Tg​ is a continuous affine self-map of it.
Here is the fixed-point step, including why no commutativity assumption on G is needed. For any finite list g1​,…,gn​, the Schauder-Tychonoff fixed-point theorem gives a fixed point ϕ∈K of n−1∑j​Tgj​​. Let H be the closed subgroup they generate and, for real f∈C(G), set h(x)=ϕ(lx​f) on H. It is continuous and satisfies
h(x)=n1​∑j=1n​h(gj−1​x).
(15)
At a maximum point x0​, every summand must have the same maximum value. Iteration gives that value at all inverse-generator words times x0​. The closure of their positive-word monoid is a closed subsemigroup of a compact group, hence is the entire generated subgroup H. Density and continuity make h constant on H. Therefore ϕ is fixed by each of Tg1​​,…,Tgn​​, first on real functions and then on complex functions by linearity. The closed fixed-point sets consequently have the finite intersection property in K, so compactness gives a common fixed functional for every g∈G.
The Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem converts this positive functional into a regular Borel probability measure μl​ with ϕ(f)=∫fdμl​. Its invariance gives left translation invariance of the measure. It has full support: if a nonempty open set had measure zero, a finite collection of its left translates would cover the compact group and force μl​(G)=0, contradicting normalization. Thus it is a normalized left Haar measure. The right translation action gives a normalized right Haar measure μr​ by the same argument.
For uniqueness and equality, take any f∈C(G) and apply the Fubini theorem to
I=∫G​∫G​f(yx)dμl​(x)dμr​(y).
(16)
For fixed y, left invariance of μl​ makes the inner integral ∫fdμl​. Reversing the order, for fixed x, right invariance of μr​ makes the inner integral ∫fdμr​. Since both total masses are one,
∫fdμl​=I=∫fdμr​.
(17)
Uniqueness in the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem gives μl​=μr​​. Comparing any normalized left Haar measure to this fixed right Haar measure proves uniqueness as well. The conclusion is uniqueness of normalized Haar measure. Without normalization, the printed uniqueness claim is false: even on the one-point group, δe​ and 2δe​ are distinct Haar measures. In general the result is uniqueness up to a positive scalar, and left and right probability normalizations coincide.

Take a nonzero complex unital Banach algebra, with identity 1. The spectrum of an element is
σA​(a)={λ∈C:λ1−a is not invertible in A}.​
(18)
The standard Banach algebra spectrum theorem states that it is a nonempty compact subset of C, contained in the disk ∣λ∣≤∥a∥. Conversely, every nonempty compact set K⊆C occurs: in the Banach algebra C(K), the coordinate function a(z)=z has Banach algebra spectrum exactly K, since λ−a has a continuous reciprocal precisely when λ∈/K. Thus the possible Banach algebra spectra are exactly the nonempty compact subsets of the complex plane.
For a nonconstant polynomial p, factor p(z)−λ=c∏j=1n​(z−zj​) over C. Substitution gives
p(a)−λ1=c∏j=1n​(a−zj​1).
(19)
These factors commute. A finite commuting product is invertible exactly when every factor is invertible: if P=∏j​xj​ is invertible, its inverse commutes with each xj​, and xj−1​=(∏k=j​xk​)P−1 is a two-sided inverse. Hence λ belongs to the Banach algebra spectrum of p(a) exactly when some root zj​ belongs to σA​(a), which is equivalent to λ∈p(σA​(a)). Constant polynomials give the same result directly, since σA​(c1)={c}. This proves the polynomial spectral mapping theorem:
σA​(p(a))=p(σA​(a)).​
(20)
For a closed unital Banach subalgebra B⊆A, use the common identity 1A​∈B. An inverse in B is an inverse in A, so σA​(b)⊆σB​(b)​. Let λ∈∂σB​(b). Choose λn​∈/σB​(b) tending to λ. Then λn​∈/σA​(b), and (λn​1−b)−1∈B. If λ were outside σA​(b), continuity of inversion in A would give
(λn​1−b)−1⟶(λ1−b)−1∈B,
(21)
where membership follows from closedness. This would make λ∈/σB​(b), contrary to the closed Banach algebra spectrum having its boundary inside it. Therefore λ∈σA​(b) and is approached by points outside that Banach algebra spectrum, proving
∂σB​(b)⊆∂σA​(b).​
(22)
Continuity of inversion here follows, for example, by writing a nearby element as an invertible element times 1+ a norm-small perturbation and using the Neumann series. This is spectrum in a closed unital subalgebra.
To retain the original Banach algebra spectrum in a commutative subalgebra, take the resolvent-generated commutative algebra
C=alg(1,a,(λ1−a)−1:λ∈/σA​(a))​∥⋅∥.
(23)
Every generator commutes with a and with every other generator: commuting invertible elements have commuting inverses, and their inverses commute with all elements commuting with them. commutativity persists on the algebra they generate and on its norm closure. For every λ∈/σA​(a), the required inverse is in C by construction, so σC​(a)⊆σA​(a). The inclusion for unital subalgebras gives the reverse containment. Hence σC​(a)=σA​(a)​.
Finally, the Banach subalgebra generated by one element is D={p(a):p a polynomial}​, and it is commutative. Put K=σD​(a) and suppose its complement has a bounded connected component U. The boundary of U lies in K. For λ∈U, the maximum modulus principle on a bounded domain applied to any polynomial gives
∣p(λ)∣≤maxz∈∂U​∣p(z)∣≤maxz∈K​∣p(z)∣=rD​(p(a))≤∥p(a)∥.
(24)
Here rD​ is the spectral radius, and the equality uses the already proved polynomial spectral mapping theorem. Define χ(p(a))=p(λ). The displayed bound makes this well defined, even when two polynomials represent the same algebra element, and bounded on the dense polynomial algebra. Its continuous extension to D remains linear and multiplicative, with χ(1)=1 and χ(a)=λ, so it is a character of an algebra. If λ1−a were invertible in D, applying χ to its inverse equation would give 0=1. Thus λ∈K, contradicting λ∈U.
There are therefore no bounded complementary components. A compact subset of the plane has exactly one unbounded complementary component: everything outside a sufficiently large disk is connected, and every unbounded component meets it. Consequently C∖σD​(a) is connected.​

For λ=0, suppose R=(λ1−ba)−1 exists. The identities (λ1−ab)a=a(λ1−ba) and b(λ1−ab)=(λ1−ba)b show, by multiplication on either side, that
(λ1−ab)−1=λ−1(1+aRb).​
(25)
For example, multiplying on the left gives 1−λ−1ab+λ−1a(λ1−ba)Rb=1, and the right product gives the same cancellation. Thus invertibility of λ1−ba implies invertibility of λ1−ab. Interchanging a,b proves the converse. The nonzero spectra of products in opposite orders are therefore equal:
σA​(ab)∖{0}=σA​(ba)∖{0}.​
(26)
In particular the requested inclusion follows. The exclusion of zero matters: for the unilateral shift operator S, S∗S=1 has Banach algebra spectrum {1}, whereas SS∗=1−P0​ has Banach algebra spectrum {0,1}.
Use the spectral definition of a Positive element of a C-star algebra: h is positive if h=h∗ and σA​(h)⊆[0,∞). We use the standard continuous functional calculus for a Hermitian element of a C-star algebra, including its real Banach algebra spectrum and the equality of its norm with its spectral radius. To prove a∗a positive without assuming this as part of the definition, first establish two elementary cone facts.
If p,q are positive and s=∥p∥+∥q∥, the continuous functional calculus gives ∥∥p∥1−p∥≤∥p∥ and the corresponding bound for q, hence
∥s1−p−q∥≤s.
(27)
Since p+q is self-adjoint, every spectral value t is real and satisfies ∣s−t∣≤s, so t≥0. Thus the positive cone of a C-star algebra is closed under addition. It is proper: if both h and −h are positive, their Banach algebra spectra force σ(h)⊆{0}, and the self-adjoint norm equality gives h=0. It is also norm closed: if pn​≥0 converges to p, choose r≥supn​∥pn​∥; then ∥r1−pn​∥≤r passes to the limit and forces every spectral value of p to be nonnegative.
Now put h=a∗a, and form its negative part d=max(−h,0) by continuous functional calculus. Let c=ad1/2. Because d is a function of h, the scalar identity tmax(−t,0)=−max(−t,0)2 gives

This has nonpositive Banach algebra spectrum. The previously proved equality of nonzero product Banach algebra spectra shows that cc∗ also has nonpositive Banach algebra spectrum; it is self-adjoint, so both c∗c and cc∗ are negative elements. Write c=u+iv, where u=(c+c∗)/2 and v=(c−c∗)/(2i) are self-adjoint. Direct multiplication gives
c∗c+cc∗=2(u2+v2).
(29)
By the spectral mapping theorem, the squares u2,v2 are positive, so their sum is positive by the cone fact above. But the same sum is negative, since both its original summands are negative. Properness forces c∗c+cc∗=0. Then c∗c=−cc∗ is both negative and positive, so c∗c=0, whence d2=0. The C-star identity applied to the self-adjoint d gives ∥d∥2=∥d2∥=0. Thus d=0, proving

This establishes positivity of adjoint products from spectral positivity. It also proves positivity under congruence: for p≥0 and any z, write p=r2 using its functional-calculus square root; then z∗pz=(rz)∗(rz)≥0.
