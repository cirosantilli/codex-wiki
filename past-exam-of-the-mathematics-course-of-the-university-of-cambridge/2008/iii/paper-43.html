Let P(z)=∑n≥0​pn​zn, F(z)=∑j≥1​fj​zj, and G(z)=∑k≥0​gk​zk be the count, claim-size and aggregate probability generating functions. Independence in the aggregate claims model gives
G(z)=E(F(z)N)=P(F(z)).
(1)
The Panjer claim-count class recurrence can be written npn​=(an+b)pn−1​. Multiply by zn−1 and sum to obtain
P′(z)=a(zP′(z)+P(z))+bP(z),(1−az)P′(z)=(a+b)P(z).
(2)
Using the chain rule in G=P∘F then yields
(1−aF(z))G′(z)=(a+b)F′(z)G(z).
(3)
The coefficient of zk−1 on the left is kgk​−a∑j=1k​fj​(k−j)gk−j​, where the j=k term is zero. On the right it is (a+b)∑j=1k​jfj​gk−j​. Rearranging gives the Panjer recursion
g0​=p0​,gk​=j=1∑k​(a+kbj​)fj​gk−j​,k≥1.​
(4)
The initial value is p0​ because all claim sizes are positive: zero total loss occurs exactly when N=0. Only already computed aggregate probabilities occur on the right. The count normalization determines p0​ from a,b as well: with w0​=1 and wn​=∏j=1n​(a+b/j), we have pn​=p0​wn​ and p0​=(∑n≥0​wn​)−1. The assumed valid count law makes this sum finite.
Finally p0​ must be positive. If it were zero, the recurrence would make every pn​ zero, contradicting normalization. At n=1 the recurrence therefore gives
a+b=p0​p1​​≥0.​
(5)
This proves the sign constraint without assuming any particular count family. The three parts below also provide explicit initial probabilities for the aggregate recursion.

Under quota share reinsurance, the direct insurer retains a fixed fraction α of each claim and the reinsurer pays the other fraction. Thus their annual claim totals are αS and (1−α)S. Under excess of loss reinsurance with retention d, the direct insurer pays min(Xj​,d) on each individual claim and the reinsurer pays (Xj​−d)+​. Under stop loss reinsurance with annual retention D, the direct insurer pays min(S,D) and the reinsurer pays (S−D)+​. The last two contracts differ because one caps each claim and the other caps the aggregate.
Conditioning on the annual Poisson distribution count gives the compound Poisson distribution transform:
MS​(t)=∑n≥0​e−λn!λn​MX​(t)n=exp{λ(MX​(t)−1)}.​
(10)
This equality is understood on the domain where the moment-generating function is finite. By the expected value premium principle, annual premium income is c=(1+θ)λμ. Without reinsurance, final capital is W0​=u+c−S, and therefore
MW0​​(t)=exp{t(u+(1+θ)λμ)+λ(MX​(−t)−1)}.​
(11)
For the quota-share contract, the reinsurer's expected claim cost is (1−α)λμ, so its annual premium is
cR​=(1+θR​)(1−α)λμ.​
(12)
The direct insurer keeps its original premium income, pays this reinsurance premium and retains only αS. Thus
W=u+c−cR​−αS=u+λμ{θ−θR​+(1+θR​)α}−αS,
(13)
and
MW​(t)=exp[t(u+λμ{θ−θR​+(1+θR​)α})+λ{MX​(−αt)−1}].​
(14)
The reinsurer's loading is applied to the ceded expected loss, not to the direct insurer's original premium.
Maximizing expected exponential utility E[−e−βW] is equivalent to minimizing the exponent H(α)=logE[e−βW]. For exponential distribution claim sizes of mean μ,
MX​(r)=1−μr1​,r<1/μ,
(15)
so on the finite-utility domain βμα<1,
H(α)=−β[u+λμ{θ−θR​+(1+θR​)α}]+λ(1−βμα1​−1).
(16)
For a nontrivial positive arrival rate, differentiation gives
H′(α)=λβμ[(1−βμα)−2−(1+θR​)],H′′(α)=(1−βμα)32λ(βμ)2​>0.
(17)
The unique unconstrained stationary retention is therefore
α∗​=βμ1−(1+θR​)−1/2​.​
(18)
It is positive and lies below 1/(βμ). If α∗​<1, it is the unique optimal retention in the printed range 0<α<1. If α∗​≥1, expected utility increases throughout that open interval and its supremum is approached as α↑1; there is no attained maximum in the strictly open range. Allowing the usual no-reinsurance endpoint gives the compact formula
αopt​=min{1,α∗​}when 0≤α≤1.​
(19)
Retentions with βμα≥1 have infinite exponential loss moment and expected utility −∞, so they cannot displace the finite stationary solution. The stationary point and full-retention endpoint just described stay within the finite-transform domain whenever they are selected.
For the requested comparisons,
∂θR​∂α∗​​=2βμ(1+θR​)3/21​>0.
(20)
Higher reinsurer loading increases optimal retention, up to full retention: reinsurance is more expensive. The direct insurer's loading θ does not affect the optimizer. It adds only the constant −βλμθ to H, reflecting the translation property of constant absolute risk aversion. These observations give the quota-share retention under exponential utility rule; u and the positive arrival rate also drop out of the stationary equation. In the degenerate case λ=0, no claims or premiums occur, W=u, and every retained fraction has the same expected utility.

In the classical risk model, write the surplus as
U(t)=u+ct−∑j=1N(t)​Xj​,c=(1+θ)λμ,
(21)
where N(t) is the claim-arrival Poisson process. The ruin time is τ=inf{t≥0:U(t)<0}. The ultimate ruin probability is
ψ(u)=P(τ<∞).​
(22)
The positive root R in the question is the adjustment coefficient. The Lundberg inequality and the Cramér–Lundberg approximation are, respectively,
ψ(u)≤e−Ru,​ψ(u)∼Ce−Ru(u→∞),C=MX′​(R)−(1+θ)μθμ​.​
(23)
The interior adjustment coefficient ruin prefactor is finite here because R lies strictly inside the finite domain of the moment-generating function; the claim-size density supplies the nonarithmetic condition for the asymptotic. Strict convexity makes its denominator positive. The approximation is an asymptotic statement, while the inequality is a bound at every nonnegative initial capital.
The inequality can also be seen directly from the exponential surplus martingale e−RU(t). Its expectation factor per unit time is zero in the exponent because λ(MX​(R)−1)−cR=0. Apply the optional stopping theorem at τ∧T, a bounded time. On {τ≤T}, e−RU(τ)>1, so P(τ≤T)≤e−Ru. Increasing T proves the bound without stopping at an unbounded time.
For any nonnegative claim size, the Tonelli theorem applied to eRX−1=∫0X​ReRxdx gives the exponential tail identity
MX​(R)−1=R∫0∞​eRx(1−FX​(x))dx.
(24)
Combining it with the adjustment equation yields
μ1​∫0∞​eRx(1−FX​(x))dx=1+θ.​
(25)
The density fY​(x)=(1−FX​(x))/μ integrates to one by the tail integral formula for moments. Thus Y has the equilibrium claim-size distribution, and the preceding formula says MY​(R)=1+θ.
Under the given NBUE condition, the stochastic order comparison is 1−FY​(x)≤1−FX​(x) at every x≥0. Apply the same exponential tail identity to both variables to obtain
EeRY=1+R∫0∞​eRx(1−FY​(x))dx≤1+R∫0∞​eRx(1−FX​(x))dx=EeRX.
(26)
Consequently
1+θ=MY​(R)≤MX​(R)=1+(1+θ)μR,
(27)
and rearrangement proves the adjustment-coefficient lower bound for NBUE claims:
R≥(1+θ)μθ​.​
(28)
This uses the specified distributional comparison, not an unwarranted assumption that an arbitrary equilibrium claim law is always smaller than its original law.
For the final example, fX​(x)=xe−x is the shape-two, rate-one gamma distribution, with μ=2, survival function FX​(x)=(1+x)e−x, and moment-generating function MX​(r)=(1−r)−2 for r<1. With θ=1/10, the positive adjustment root satisfies

A credibility estimate combines a risk's own observed experience with a collective or prior mean, in the form Zμ​experience​+(1−Z)μcollective​. The credibility factor Z∈[0,1] is the weight given to that risk's experience. Bayesian credibility starts with a prior distribution for a latent risk parameter, updates it using observations, and estimates the conditional risk mean by its Bayesian posterior expected value under squared-error loss. In conjugate models this posterior mean can have exactly the displayed credibility form.
For the unequal policy exposures, define
M=∑i=1k​mi​,s=∑i=1k​xi​.
(33)
Conditional independence of the binomial distribution observations gives the likelihood, up to a factor not depending on θ,
L(θ)∝∏i=1k​θxi​(1−θ)mi​−xi​=θs(1−θ)M−s.
(34)
Multiplication by the Beta distribution prior therefore gives Beta-binomial conjugacy:
θ∣x1​,…,xk​∼Beta(α+s,β+M−s).​
(35)
Both posterior parameters are positive, since 0≤s≤M and the prior parameters are positive.
The conditional target is μ(θ)=mk+1​θ. To verify the quadratic-loss rule directly, for any proposed estimate d and observed data D,
E[(d−μ(θ))2∣D]=(d−E[μ(θ)∣D])2+Var(μ(θ)∣D).
(36)
The variance term does not depend on d, so the unique Bayes estimator under squared error loss is the posterior mean. Applying the beta mean formula gives
μ​k+1​=mk+1​α+β+Mα+s​.​
(37)
It is also the posterior expected future claim count, by the tower property of conditional expectation.
Let p0​=α/(α+β) be the collective prior claim probability and p​=s/M the observed claims per policy. Then
α+β+Mα+s​=M+α+βM​Ms​+M+α+βα+β​α+βα​.
(38)
Therefore the exact beta-binomial credibility with unequal exposures is
μ​k+1​=Z(mk+1​Ms​)+(1−Z)(mk+1​α+βα​),Z=M+α+βM​.​
(39)
The individual experience must be weighted by exposure: s/M is the policy-weighted frequency, not the unweighted average of xi​/mi​ when the mi​ differ. The factor multiplies next-year expected counts after scaling both component probabilities by mk+1​.

Write A=α+β and keep p0​=α/A fixed. Then α=p0​A, β=(1−p0​)A, while the fixed observed years and exposures leave M unchanged. The credibility factor is
Z=M+AM​,dAdZ​=−(M+A)2M​<0.
(41)
As prior concentration A increases without bound,
Z⟶0,μ​k+1​⟶mk+1​p0​.​
(42)
The beta prior variance is p0​(1−p0​)/(A+1), which tends to zero while its mean stays fixed. Increasing A therefore makes the prior increasingly concentrated and reduces the influence of the same finite data. This explains the limiting Bayesian credibility estimate, rather than merely treating α,β as two larger arbitrary parameters.
