Index city by g∈{0,1} and age group by j, with the youngest group the reference level. For each of the 15 observed cells, the grouped-binomial logistic regression assumes
Cgj​∼Binomial(Ngj​,πgj​),log1−πgj​πgj​​=α+aj​+γg,a15–24​=0,
(22)
with independent cell counts. The response supplied to R is Cgj​/Ngj​, and the weights specify the binomial numbers of trials Ngj​. They are not 15 arbitrary precision weights: the conditional variance of the proportion is πgj​(1−πgj​)/Ngj​. The logit link models log odds, and the two sets of factor coefficients use corner-point constraints.
There are nine parameters: one intercept, seven age contrasts and one city contrast. Hence the residual degrees of freedom are 15−9=6, not the total population minus nine. The null model has only an intercept and 14 residual degrees of freedom. Adding age uses seven more parameters and reduces the binomial deviance from 2330.46 to 232.28, a drop of 2098.19. Adding city then uses one parameter and reduces it by 227.12 to 5.15. These are sequential likelihood-ratio tests, asymptotically compared with chi-squared distributions on seven and one degrees of freedom. Both provide extremely strong evidence of an effect; the printed age p-value 0.00 is rounding, not an exactly zero probability. Because the table is incomplete, sequential main-effect sums need not be invariant to the order of fitting.
The intercept estimate −11.69364 is the log odds for the youngest Minneapolis–Saint Paul group. It implies baseline odds e−11.69364 and a fitted probability about 8.35×10−6. Its standard error is 0.44923; its reported Wald test tests log odds zero, or probability 1/2, rather than a scientifically interesting comparison of groups.
The age coefficients are log odds ratios relative to the youngest group within the same city. Their exponentials are approximately 13.86, 46.82, 99.03, 162.23, 284.38, 497.07 and 484.57 as age increases through the seven other groups. All these comparisons have large positive Wald statistics and small p-values. Thus the fitted probabilities are much higher in the older groups than in the youngest group. The slightly smaller coefficient in the oldest group than in the preceding group does not itself prove a real decline: that claim needs a contrast and its standard error, including the covariance of the two estimates.
The city coefficient 0.85492 is the log odds ratio for Fort Worth relative to Minneapolis–Saint Paul, adjusted for age. Its standard error 0.05969 gives z=14.322, so the null city effect is strongly rejected by the Wald test, consistent with the sequential likelihood-ratio test. The fitted constant-over-age city odds ratio and an approximate 95% confidence interval are
OR=e0.85492=2.351,CI95%​=e0.85492±1.96(0.05969)≈(2.09,2.64).​
(23)
This is an odds ratio, not generally a probability ratio. The probabilities here are sufficiently small that the two ratios are close. The absence of an age-by-city interaction is an assumption of the fitted additive log-odds model.
The final binomial deviance is 5.1509 on six degrees of freedom; its approximate upper-tail p-value is 0.525. It gives no indication of substantial lack of fit against the saturated observed-cell model. The deviance residuals range from −1.2830 to 1.0820, with median zero and no conspicuously large value in the five-number summary. Neither statement proves that every model assumption holds. In particular, the small case counts motivate checking the accuracy of the chi-squared asymptotic approximation, for example by a fitted-model parametric bootstrap.
The dispersion is fixed at one by the binomial distribution; it was not estimated as a free scale parameter. Four Fisher scoring iterations describe the numerical optimization, not four additional parameters. Residual and cellwise observed-versus-fitted comparisons would help assess adequacy and potential overdispersion.
