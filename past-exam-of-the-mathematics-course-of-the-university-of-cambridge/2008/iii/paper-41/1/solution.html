Write q=n−p. In this normal linear model, the ordinary least squares criterion is ∥Y−Xb∥2. Differentiating with respect to b gives the normal equations for linear least squares, XTXb=XTY. Full column rank of the design matrix makes XTX invertible, and completing the square proves that the unique minimum is
β​=(XTX)−1XTY.​
(1)
Since β​=β+(XTX)−1XTϵ, its sampling distribution is the multivariate normal distribution
β​∼Np​(β,σ2(XTX)−1).
(2)
Let H=X(XTX)−1XT be the hat matrix. It is symmetric and idempotent with rank p, and HX=X. The regression residual vector is (I−H)Y=(I−H)ϵ, so
RSS=∥Y−Xβ​∥2=YT(I−H)Y,s2=n−pRSS​.​
(3)
An orthogonal projection of a Gaussian vector onto the residual space gives RSS/σ2∼χq2​. The residual projection and fitted projection have zero cross-covariance, hence are independent because they are jointly normal. In particular, s2 is an unbiased estimator of σ2 and is independent of β​. The maximum-likelihood estimator of σ2 instead divides by n and is not unbiased.
For the null hypothesis β1​=0, put v11​=((XTX)−1)11​. Under that null hypothesis, β​1​/(σv11​​) is standard normal and independent of qs2/σ2. Therefore the Student t-test uses
T=sv11​​β​1​​∼tq​.
(4)
A two-sided level-α test rejects when ∣T∣>tq,1−α/2​. The denominator is the estimated standard error of the regression coefficient.
The fitted linear regression includes an intercept and three covariates:
NO2i​=β0​+βw​windi​+βt​maxtempi​+βs​insoli​+ϵi​,ϵi​∼iidN(0,σ2).
(5)
Thus n=25, p=4 and the residual degrees of freedom are 21. The fitted conditional mean is
NO2=3.784916−0.527410wind+0.124991maxtemp−0.005259insol.
(6)
Each slope measures a conditional association with the response while the other two covariates are held fixed. Increasing wind speed by one mile per hour corresponds to a fitted decrease of 0.527410 in the concentration units used. Its standard error is 0.224904, giving t=−2.345 and a two-sided p-value of 0.0289: evidence of a negative conditional association at the 5% level. The temperature slope is 0.124991 per degree Fahrenheit, with standard error 0.075173, t=1.663 and p-value 0.1112. The insolation slope is −0.005259 per langley per day, with standard error 0.006637, t=−0.792 and p-value 0.4370. Neither of these last two conditional effects is significant at 5%; that does not establish that either true slope is zero. These are associations, not a demonstration of causal effects.
The intercept estimates the mean at zero values of all three covariates. Its value 3.784916 has a large standard error 7.979647, giving t=0.474 and p-value 0.6402. The zero-covariate combination may be outside the observed range, so this test may have little practical relevance. Every coefficient test uses a Student t-distribution with 21 degrees of freedom.
The reported residual standard deviation s=1.844 measures unexplained variation in concentration units; the subsequent table gives RSS=71.428, consistent with s=71.428/21​ after rounding. The five-number residual summary describes the spread, from −2.3052 to 3.4033, with median −0.4990. An intercept forces the residual sum to zero, not the residual median. These five numbers alone cannot establish normal errors, constant variance or independence; residual-versus-fitted and quantile-quantile plots would be more informative, and successive days also warrant checking serial dependence. The coefficient of determination R2=0.6533 means that 65.33% of the sample response sum of squares about its mean is explained by this fit. Its adjusted version is
R2=1−TSS/24RSS/21​=0.6037,
(7)
which accounts for the number of estimated regression coefficients.
The stepwise selection by the Akaike information criterion searches between the intercept-only model and the specified model with all three main effects. For these Gaussian fits, the printed Akaike information criterion is, up to a constant common to the candidates,
AIC=25log(RSS/25)+2p.
(8)
The full model has p=4 and AIC 34.246. Removing insolation increases the residual sum of squares by 2.136 but decreases AIC to 32.982, so that removal is chosen. Removing temperature or wind instead would give AIC 35.337 or 38.060 and is worse. In the next step the candidate models include deletions and restoration of insolation. Keeping wind and temperature has the smallest AIC: removing temperature gives 33.389, restoring insolation gives 34.246, and removing wind gives 37.960. Hence the search stops, giving
NO2=4.4368−0.5734wind+0.1040maxtemp.​
(9)
The accompanying F-tests compare a larger model with a one-parameter reduction: F=ΔRSS/s2, where s2 is estimated from the larger model. For example, removing insolation gives 2.136/(71.428/21)=0.628, equal to the corresponding squared test statistic. Deletions from the second-stage model use 73.564/22 in the denominator, whereas restoring insolation uses the full-model denominator. These tests describe the comparisons; they do not determine the AIC choice. In particular, temperature is retained despite its deletion-test p-value 0.15016. AIC balances fit and complexity, and a stepwise optimum need not be a global optimum over a richer model class. Ordinary coefficient inference after choosing a model does not account for the selection itself.
For positive responses, the Box–Cox transformation is Tλ​(y)=(yλ−1)/λ for λ=0, and T0​(y)=logy. Its profile likelihood refits the linear regression and variance at each λ and includes the transformation Jacobian. The graph peaks at roughly λ=0.3–0.4. The approximate 95% likelihood-ratio confidence interval contains values a little below zero through approximately one, using the cutoff ℓmax​−χ1,0.952​/2. Thus a logarithmic transformation (λ=0) and a square-root transformation (λ=1/2) are plausible, while leaving the response on its original scale (λ=1) is at or very near the upper confidence boundary. The broad profile supports considering a mild power transformation; it does not determine a sharply estimated power. The raster does not justify more precise endpoints or a decisive assertion about which side of the boundary λ=1 lies.
