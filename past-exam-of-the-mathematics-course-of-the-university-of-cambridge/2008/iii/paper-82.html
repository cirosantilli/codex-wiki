The limiting polynomial has a double root at x=−1, so two roots require a Puiseux series. For positive ϵ, put δ=ϵ​ and x=−1+aδ+bδ2+⋯. Substitution and dominant balance give
0=(a2−1)δ2+(2ab+3a)δ3+O(δ4).
(1)
Thus a=±1 and b=−3/2. The two finite roots have the three-term asymptotic expansions
x±​=−1±ϵ1/2−23​ϵ+O(ϵ3/2).​
(2)
This is square-root splitting of a double polynomial root. For negative ϵ, the same formulas apply with a chosen complex square root and describe a complex conjugate pair.
The third root is a divergent algebraic root in a singular perturbation. Set x=X/ϵ: the equation for X is X3+X2+2ϵX+ϵ2=0. Its nonzero limiting root is X=−1. Substituting X=−1+Aϵ+Bϵ2+⋯ yields A=2 and B=3, hence
xL​=−ϵ−1+2+3ϵ+O(ϵ2).​
(3)
As an independent check, Vieta formulas give the sum of roots as −1/ϵ: the two finite-root expansions sum to −2−3ϵ+O(ϵ2), giving the same large-root expansion.

The method of multiple scales applies when a small perturbation acts cumulatively over a long time, as in weakly perturbed harmonic oscillators or slowly modulated waves. A regular asymptotic expansion can contain secular terms, such as ϵtsin(ωt), which cease to be small on the time scale of interest. Introduce independent fast and slow times, for example t and T=ϵt, and allow the leading amplitude and phase to depend on T. The solvability condition in the method of multiple scales removes resonant forcing at each order and determines that slow evolution. For the Mathieu equation, these amplitude equations reveal parametric resonance and its instability intervals.

y=y0​(t,T)+ϵy1​(t,T)+⋯,y0​=a(T)cos(t/2)+b(T)sin(t/2).

The error is uniform on bounded intervals of T, for bounded initial data. When λ is imaginary this expression uses ordinary sine and cosine; at λ=0 its continuous limit is I+TM.
The primary instability tongue of a weak Mathieu oscillator has a positive slow eigenvalue precisely when
−21​<k<21​,growth rate=ϵ41​−k2​+O(ϵ2).​

These are the leading edges, rather than an exact finite-ϵ interval. Away from the first parametric resonance, one forcing interaction changes a harmonic frequency by one and produces no resonant coupling of the two conjugate oscillations. Two interactions can couple frequency +1 to frequency −1, so the next parametric resonance is near ω=1 and its effect first appears at order ϵ2. This gives p=2 and the appropriate slow time T=ϵ2t.
Write ω=1+κϵ2 and y0​=a(T)cost+b(T)sint. The first correction satisfies (∂t2​+1)y1​=−costy0​, whose bounded particular solution is
y1​=−2a​+6a​cos2t+6b​sin2t.
(17)
At second order,
(∂t2​+1)y2​=−2∂t​∂T​y0​−2κy0​−costy1​.
(18)
The resonant part of −costy1​ is (5a/12)cost−(b/12)sint. Applying the solvability condition in the method of multiple scales gives
aT​=(κ+1/24)b,bT​=(5/24−κ)a.​
(19)
The squared slow eigenvalue is
λ2=(κ+1/24)(5/24−κ)=641​−(κ−1/12)2.
(20)
The second instability tongue of a weak Mathieu oscillator therefore has the leading interval
−241​<κ<245​,1−24ϵ2​+o(ϵ2)<ω<1+245ϵ2​+o(ϵ2).​
(21)
Here the two little-o terms describe the respective exact edge locations; the strict inequality in κ is the leading interior criterion. In particular ω=1 is unstable, with physical growth rate ϵ25​/24+o(ϵ2). The general leading solution for bounded T again has y0​=a(T)cost+b(T)sint, where the matrix exponential of the displayed two-by-two amplitude system evolves arbitrary a(0),b(0). Adding ϵy1​ identifies the first nonresonant harmonic correction. The leading expression alone has error O(ϵ) on t=O(ϵ−2).
For comparison, at fixed ω=1/2,1, write y0​=A(T)eiωt+A(T)e−iωt. The first forced harmonics have coefficients A/[2(2ω+1)] and A/[2(1−2ω)]. Their return to the original harmonic at the next order gives
AT​=4ω(1−4ω2)i​A.
(22)
It is a phase correction, not amplitude growth. Consequently, away from the first two resonance neighborhoods, a general leading solution valid for bounded ϵ2t is
y=Ccos(Ωt)+Dsin(Ωt)+O(ϵ),Ω=ω+4ω(1−4ω2)ϵ2​.
(23)
Narrower higher-order instability intervals are invisible on this time scale. The detuned resonant calculation above is needed near ω=1 because both the phase shift and the conjugate-mode coupling then matter.

Floquet theory suggests a sequence of instability tongues near
ω=2m​,m=1,2,3,…,tongue width O(ϵm).​
(24)
At these limiting frequencies the unperturbed Floquet multipliers e±2πiω coincide. Each multiplication by cost changes a Fourier frequency by one, so m forcing interactions are needed to couple +m/2 and −m/2. This explains both the sequence of parametric resonances and the successive powers of ϵ in their widths.
There is no implication that the exact half-integers themselves lie in every finite-ϵ growing interval. The tongue centers generally shift by O(ϵ2), which exceeds the width when m>2. It is the narrow intervals near the shifted centers that contain exponentially growing solutions. Frequencies staying a fixed distance from every half-integer are stable for sufficiently small ϵ.

Consider a nonreal phase velocity c, so U−c never vanishes. Substitute ϕ=(U−c)ψ into the Rayleigh equation for inviscid shear flow. Differentiating cancels the two terms involving U′′, leaving
(U−c)ψ′′+2U′ψ′−k2(U−c)ψ=0,[(U−c)2ψ′]′−k2(U−c)2ψ=0.​
(35)
For k>0, the wall condition on normal velocity gives ϕ=0, hence ψ=0, at both walls. Multiply by ψ​ and use integration by parts to obtain the weighted identity for Howard's semicircle theorem:
∫−y0​y0​​(U−c)2Wdy=0,W=∣ψ′∣2+k2∣ψ∣2≥0.
(36)
For a nonzero normal mode, N=∫Wdy>0. The imaginary and real parts of this identity give, respectively,
−2ci​∫(U−cr​)Wdy=0,∫[(U−cr​)2−ci2​]Wdy=0.
(37)
Since ci​=0, these imply
∫UWdy=cr​N,∫U2Wdy=(cr2​+ci2​)N.​
(38)
The first equation says that cr​ is a positive weighted mean of U, and consequently Umin​≤cr​≤Umax​. The division by ci​ is essential: the weighted-mean assertion concerns nonreal modes. A neutral mode may have a critical level where U=c and the substitution for ψ is singular.
Pointwise, (U−Umax​)(U−Umin​)≤0. Integrating against the nonnegative weight W and substituting both identities yields
[cr2​+ci2​−(Umax​+Umin​)cr​+Umax​Umin​]N≤0.
(39)

(cr​−2Umax​+Umin​​)2+ci2​≤(2Umax​−Umin​​)2.​
(40)
Thus all nonreal phase velocities lie in the closed disk with the real interval [Umin​,Umax​] as diameter. For the convention eik(x−ct) and k>0, exponentially growing normal modes occupy its upper semicircle, while decaying modes occupy its lower semicircle.

Away from the velocity-profile corners, U′′=0 and an unstable normal mode satisfies ϕ′′−k2ϕ=0. On the upper half of the jet write
ϕ=Acoshky+Bsinhky(0<y<1),ϕ=De−k(y−1)(y>1).
(41)
The exterior solution is selected by decay at infinity. From the given velocity components, the perturbation pressure is
p=−(U−c)ϕ′+U′ϕ.
(42)
Continuity of normal velocity and pressure therefore gives continuity of ϕ and pressure matching at a piecewise-linear shear interface:
[(U−c)ϕ′−U′ϕ]=0,(U−c)[ϕ′]=[U′]ϕ.
(43)
At y=1 the slope of U jumps from −1 to zero. Using ϕ′(1+)=−kϕ(1) yields
c[ϕ′(1−)+kϕ(1)]=ϕ(1),ckek(A+B)=Acoshk+Bsinhk.
(44)
The lower interface then follows by parity.
For the even sinuous mode of a planar jet, the derivative can jump at the center: being an even function does not require the one-sided derivative to vanish. The slope jump at y=0 is −2, whereas [ϕ′]=2ϕ′(0+). Thus
(1−c)ϕ′(0+)+ϕ(0)=0,A+k(1−c)B=0.
(45)
Substitute A=−k(1−c)B into the upper-interface relation. With q=e−2k, division by ek and multiplication by 2k give
2kc(1−k+kc)+k(1−c)(1+q)−(1−q)=0.
(46)
Collecting powers of the phase velocity gives the required dispersion relation:
2k2c2+k(1−2k−e−2k)c−[1−k−(1+k)e−2k]=0.​
(47)
These equations can also be treated as a homogeneous two-by-two system in A,B, avoiding division by any potentially zero amplitude or phase-velocity factor.
For the odd varicose mode of a planar jet, ϕ(0)=0, so A=0; the central pressure matching at a piecewise-linear shear interface is automatic. The upper-interface equation becomes ckek=sinhk, and therefore
2kc−(1−e−2k)=0,c=2k1−e−2k​.​
(48)
This phase velocity is real for every k>0, so the discrete varicose mode of a planar jet is neutral and there is no exponentially unstable odd mode.
To determine the unstable even modes, factor the reduced discriminant of the quadratic:
D(k)​=(2k−3)2−2(2k+5)e−2k+e−4k=(2k−3−e−2k−4e−k)(2k−3−e−2k+4e−k).​

The second factor vanishes at zero and has derivative 2(1−e−k)2>0 for k>0, so it is positive there. The first factor starts at −8, is strictly increasing, and tends to infinity. It therefore has one positive zero kc​. The even phase velocities are
c±​=4k2k+e−2k−1±D(k)​​.
(50)

The sinuous branch is exponentially unstable exactly for 0<k<kc​,2kc​−3−e−2kc​−4e−kc​=0,kc​≃1.832744.​
(51)
For 0<k<kc​, one member of the complex conjugate pair grows at rate kci​=−D(k)​/4 and the other decays. At k=kc​ the speeds merge and there is no exponential growth; for k>kc​ both speeds are real. This is inviscid instability of a triangular jet. The unstable sinuous mode of a planar jet also satisfies Howard's semicircle theorem with Umin​=0 and Umax​=1: its speed lies in the upper half-disk centered at 1/2 with radius 1/2.
