A one-period arbitrage is a portfolio of initial-information-measurable holdings (hB​,hS​) with value Vt​=hB​Bt​+hS​St​ such that
V0​=0,V1​≥0 almost surely,P(V1​>0)>0.​
(1)
The holdings are fixed during the period, so this is a self-financing strategy. A portfolio costing a negative amount with nonnegative terminal value also gives an arbitrage: invest its initial surplus in the strictly positive bank account to obtain a zero-cost portfolio with a strict gain. The same definition applies when further traded assets are added.

An equivalent martingale measure is a probability measure Q equivalent to the objective measure P, meaning the two have exactly the same null events, under which the discounted stock St​=St​/Bt​ is a martingale. In this one-period model the condition is
EQ​[S1​/B1​∣F0​]=S0​/B0​,Q∼P.​
(2)
The discounted bank account is identically one. Integrability of the discounted stock is part of the martingale condition. In an augmented market the discounted price of every additional asset must satisfy the same condition.

Suppose Q is an equivalent martingale measure and a zero-cost self-financing strategy has holdings (hB​,hS​). Its discounted terminal value has conditional expectation
EQ​[V1​/B1​∣F0​]=hB​+hS​EQ​[S1​/B1​∣F0​]=hB​+hS​S0​/B0​=V0​/B0​=0.
(3)
If it were an arbitrage, V1​/B1​ would be nonnegative because B1​>0. Equivalence implies that it is strictly positive on an event of positive Q-probability. A nonnegative random variable with zero conditional expectation must be zero almost surely, a contradiction. Thus an equivalent martingale measure excludes arbitrage​. The calculation uses the usual integrable one-period strategies, and also works conditionally for finite initial-information-measurable holdings.

The European call option pays one in the state S1​=5 and zero in the other two states. Short one call, buy one third of a stock, and borrow one half unit of cash. Writing the option quote as c=1/2, the holdings and initial value are
(hB​,hS​,hC​)=(−1/2,1/3,−1),V0​=−1/2+1−c=0.​
(4)
The terminal value is −1/2+S1​/3−(S1​−4)+. At stock prices 5,3,2 it is respectively
(1/6, 1/2, 1/6).​
(5)
It is strictly positive in every state, so this portfolio is an arbitrage.

Let c be the initial call price. Write the risk-neutral probabilities of stock prices 5,3,2 as q5​,q3​,q2​. The stock's martingale equation and the normalization give q2​=2q5​ and q3​=1−3q5​. The call pays only in the first state, so its martingale equation is q5​=c. Thus
(q5​,q3​,q2​)=(c,1−3c,2c).
(6)
All three objective probabilities are positive. This defines an equivalent martingale measure precisely when 0<c<1/3, and part (iii) proves that every such price excludes arbitrage.
To prove necessity without appealing to an existence theorem, consider the other prices explicitly. If c≤0, buy one call and hold −c units of cash. Its initial value is zero and terminal value is (S1​−4)+−c, nonnegative and positive with positive probability. If c≥1/3, take holdings (hB​,hS​,hC​)=(c−1,1/3,−1). The initial value is c−1+1−c=0 and terminal values at 5,3,2 are (c−1/3,c,c−1/3). They are nonnegative and the middle-state value is strictly positive. This includes the endpoint c=1/3. Consequently
the arbitrage-free call prices are exactly c∈(0,1/3).​
(7)

A complete market is one in which every contingent claim in the pricing class can be replicated: for each finite maturity T and terminal FT​-measurable payoff H, there are an initial value and an admissible self-financing strategy whose terminal value is H almost surely. It is enough for the complete market characterization to include all claims with bounded discounted payoffs. Thus every such payoff is attainable by trading the existing assets, rather than needing an additional independent security.

Suppose Q1​,Q2​ are equivalent martingale measures. Fix a finite maturity T and an event E∈FT​. Because the market is a complete market, replicate the claim H=BT​1E​, whose discounted payoff is bounded. For a self-financing strategy, the discounted value satisfies
Vt+1​−Vt​=htS​(St+1​−St​),S=S/B.
(8)
Under either pricing measure its conditional mean increment is zero, since htS​ is known at time t and the discounted stock is a martingale. In the standard admissible discrete-time pricing class these finite-horizon values are integrable; a constant lower bound on discounted wealth also gives integrability successively from the conditional increment formula. Therefore replication and taking expectations give
B0​V0​​=EQj​​[H/BT​]=Qj​(E),j=1,2.
(9)
The replicating portfolio and its initial cost are the same for both measures, so Q1​(E)=Q2​(E). Since E was arbitrary, the measures agree on FT​, for every finite T, and hence on the sigma-algebra generated by the market filtration. Thus the equivalent martingale measure is unique​. This is the measure version of complete markets have unique state-price densities.

Let Q be the unique equivalent martingale measure. The risk-neutral pricing formula is
C(T,K)=B0​EQ​[BT​(ST​−K)+​]=B0​EQ​[(MT​−K/BT​)+],Mt​=St​/Bt​.
(10)
For the usual nonnegative strike K and maturities u≥t, the monotonic bank account gives K/Bu​≤K/Bt​. Therefore
EQ​[(Mu​−K/Bu​)+∣Ft​]​≥EQ​[(Mu​−K/Bt​)+∣Ft​]≥(EQ​[Mu​∣Ft​]−K/Bt​)+=(Mt​−K/Bt​)+.​
(11)
The second inequality follows directly since the conditional expectation of a positive part is at least both zero and the conditional expectation of its argument; it is also conditional Jensen's inequality. Taking expectations proves C(u,K)≥C(t,K), the maturity monotonicity of calls with nonnegative strikes.
If K1​≤K2​, then (ST​−K1​)+≥(ST​−K2​)+ pointwise; positive discounting and expectation prove monotonicity of a European call price in strike. For 0≤a≤1, the pointwise inequality
(ST​−aK1​−(1−a)K2​)+≤a(ST​−K1​)++(1−a)(ST​−K2​)+
(12)
gives convexity of a European call price in strike after the same operations. Hence
C(T,K) is nondecreasing in T, nonincreasing and convex in K.​
(13)
The maturity assertion uses K≥0; it need not hold for a negative strike. For example, in the deterministic complete market St​=Bt​=ert with r>0, a negative-strike call has price 1−Ke−rT, which decreases with T. The monotonicity assertions are non-strict, as equality can occur.

Relative to a filtration (Ft​), a supermartingale is an adapted process Ut​ with E∣Ut​∣<∞ for every t and
E[Ut+1​∣Ft​]≤Ut​almost surely for every t.​

For a stopping time τ, Ut∧τ​ is adapted: split according to {τ=j} for j<t and {τ≥t}, all events belonging to Ft​. It is integrable because ∣Ut∧τ​∣≤∑j=0t​∣Uj​∣. Moreover,
U(t+1)∧τ​−Ut∧τ​=1{τ>t}​(Ut+1​−Ut​).
(15)
The indicator is Ft​-measurable, so conditioning and the supermartingale property give
E[U(t+1)∧τ​−Ut∧τ​∣Ft​]=1{τ>t}​(E[Ut+1​∣Ft​]−Ut​)≤0.
(16)
Thus (Ut∧τ​)t​ is a supermartingale​. This proves stopping preserves supermartingales in discrete time without assuming τ itself bounded or integrable.

Use the tree's natural filtration. The stopping times take values in the process horizon {0,1,2}. Construct a dominating supermartingale Y by backward comparison, the finite-horizon Snell envelope. At time two put Y2​=ξ2​. At time one the continuation values are
E[ξ2​∣ξ1​=11]=32​13+31​10=12,E[ξ2​∣ξ1​=7]=21​8+21​4=6.

Y0​=max{8,EY1​}=max{8,52​12+53​7}=9.
(18)

(19)
The reward process itself need not be a supermartingale; the constructed majorant is what proves the bound.

Use the usual Black-Scholes model with σ>0 and the filtration generated by W. Set
λ=σμ−r​,Zt​=exp(−λWt​−21​λ2t).​
(22)
For u>t, independence and the Gaussian distribution exponential moment formula give EP​[Zu​/Zt​∣Ft​]=1. Thus Z is a strictly positive martingale of mean one, defining an equivalent measure Q on every finite horizon by dQ/dP∣Ft​​=Zt​.
To verify the measure change directly, put Wt​=Wt​+λt. For any real a and h=u−t,
EQ​[eia(Wu​−Wt​)∣Ft​]​=EP​[(Zu​/Zt​)eia(Wu​−Wt​+λh)∣Ft​]=eiaλh−λ2h/2exp(21​(−λ+ia)2h)=e−a2h/2.​
(23)
The conditional characteristic function is that of N(0,h) and does not depend on past information. Therefore W has independent increments with Gaussian distribution and continuous paths, and is a Brownian motion under Q. Substituting dW=dW−λdt gives dSt​=St​(rdt+σdWt​). Hence St​/Bt​ is a geometric Brownian motion that is a martingale and Q is an equivalent martingale measure.
The form is also forced. By the Brownian martingale representation theorem, any density process that is a martingale has dZt​=at​dWt​. The change of measure identity makes Zt​St​/Bt​ a local martingale under P. Its drift, calculated by Itô formula, is (St​/Bt​)[Zt​(μ−r)+σat​]dt, which must vanish. Thus at​=−λZt​. Solving dZt​=−λZt​dWt​ with Z0​=1 gives exactly the displayed stochastic exponential. Equivalence here is on each finite horizon, as usual for Black-Scholes pricing.

Let Φ denote the standard normal distribution cumulative distribution function, and ϕ its density. Put τ=T−t. For K>0 and t<T, define
d1​=στ​log(St​/K)+(r+σ2/2)τ​,d2​=d1​−στ​.

ST​=St​exp((r−σ2/2)τ+στ​Z),Z∼N(0,1).

Thus Q(ST​<K∣Ft​)=Φ(−d2​). The identity eazϕ(z)=ea2/2ϕ(z−a) for the standard Gaussian distribution probability density function gives
EQ​[ST​1{ST​≥K}​∣Ft​]=St​erτΦ(d1​).

ξt​=V(t,St​)=St​Φ(d1​)+Ke−r(T−t)Φ(−d2​),ξT​=max(K,ST​).​

Let ϕ denote the standard Gaussian distribution probability density function. Directly from the definitions, Sϕ(d1​)=Ke−rτϕ(d2​). Differentiating the price in part (ii), the terms involving derivatives of d1​,d2​ cancel, yielding VS​=Φ(d1​). Therefore, in units of the two traded assets, the replicating strategy is
htS​=Φ(d1​),htB​=Bt​Ke−r(T−t)Φ(−d2​)​(t<T).​

VSS​=Sστ​ϕ(d1​)​,Vt​=−2τ​Sσϕ(d1​)​+rKe−rτΦ(−d2​).

Let Q be the given risk-neutral measure, and write Bt​=B0​ert. Take V to be classical on t<T and continuous at its prescribed terminal value. By Itô formula and the pricing equation for a local volatility model,
dV(t,St​)​=[Vt​+rSt​VS​+21​σ(St​)2St2​VSS​]dt+σ(St​)St​VS​dWt​=rV(t,St​)dt+σ(St​)St​VS​dWt​,​
(31)
so
d(Bt​ξt​​)=Bt​σ(St​)St​VS​(t,St​)​dWt​.
(32)
The discounted added asset is a local martingale, as is St​/Bt​, while Bt​/Bt​=1. Consequently Q is an equivalent local martingale measure for all three assets.
Here is the arbitrage argument, including the usual admissible trading strategy condition. If X is the discounted wealth of a self-financing strategy, it is a stochastic integral against the discounted asset prices, plus its initial value, and hence a local martingale. Assume Xt​≥−c for a fixed c, as required for an admissible trading strategy. For a localizing sequence τn​, the stopped process Xτn​ is a martingale. Apply the conditional Fatou lemma to Xt∧τn​​+c≥0: for s≤t, continuity and the stopped martingale identity give EQ​[Xt​+c∣Fs​]≤Xs​+c. Thus X is a supermartingale. An arbitrage would have X0​=0, XT​≥0 and positive probability of XT​>0. Equivalence of P,Q makes that probability positive under Q, contradicting EQ​[XT​]≤0. Therefore the augmented market has no admissible arbitrage.
Nonnegativity of V gives the needed price process, but does not by itself make its discounted local martingale a true martingale. If V is bounded, the latter follows by bounded localization, and its price is also Bt​EQ​[g(ST​)/BT​∣Ft​]. The arbitrage proof above applies to the stated nonnegative classical solution without assuming that additional bound.

The diffusion coefficient becomes Sσ(S)=S​, so the stock is a driftless square-root diffusion, with zero taken as an absorbing boundary. Substitution of V(t,S)=eA(t)S+B(t) into the pricing equation for a local volatility model gives
A′(t)S+B′(t)+21​A(t)2S=0.
(35)
Equating the constant and linear terms and imposing the terminal payoff gives
A′+21​A2=0,B′=0,A(1)=1,B(1)=0.
(36)
Thus 1/A(t)=(1+t)/2 and B(t)=0, and the price is
ξt​=V(t,St​)=exp(1+t2St​​),0≤t≤1.​
(37)
This is the exponential claim in a driftless square-root model. At the absorbing boundary its value is 1, matching the payoff at zero.
The payoff in this part is unbounded, unlike the payoff assumed in part (i), so it is useful to verify that the proposed price is a finite conditional expectation. For h>0, starting from stock value s, define
F(v,x)=exp(−1+u(h−v)/2ux​),0≤v≤h,u≥0.
(38)
Then Fv​+21​xFxx​=0, F(h,x)=e−ux, and 0≤F≤1. By Itô formula, F(v,Sv​) is a bounded local martingale, hence a true martingale. It follows that
Es​[e−uSh​]=exp(−1+uh/2su​).
(39)
This is also the Laplace transform of a sum of N independent variables with exponential distribution of rate 2/h, where N has Poisson distribution of mean 2s/h and is independent of the summands: its transform is exp[(2s/h)((2/h)/(2/h+u)−1)]. Uniqueness of the Laplace transform therefore gives the compound Poisson transition law of a driftless square-root diffusion. The exponential distribution jump has exponential moment E[eY]=(2/h)/(2/h−1) whenever h<2. Summing over the Poisson distribution yields
Es​[eSh​]=exp[h2s​(2/h−12/h​−1)]=exp(1−h/2s​).
(40)
For the remaining horizon h=1−t≤1, this is finite and, by the Markov property, is precisely EQ​[eS1​∣Ft​]=e2St​/(1+t). Thus the displayed process is indeed the risk-neutral pricing value and a true martingale. The replicating strategy from part (ii) has htS​=[2/(1+t)]V(t,St​) and htB​=[1−2St​/(1+t)]V(t,St​)/Bt​.

Let Q denote the given unique equivalent martingale measure. A zero-coupon bond paying one at T has risk-neutral pricing value
Pt​(T)=EQ​[exp(−∫tT​rs​ds)​Ft​].
(41)
Write h=T−t. Split the integrated short rate into its known and future parts:
∫tT​rs​ds=∫tT​g(s)ds+σhWt​+σ∫tT​(Ws​−Wt​)ds.
(42)
By integrating the future Brownian motion increments in the opposite order, the last integral equals ∫tT​(T−u)dWu​. It is independent of Ft​ and is a Gaussian random variable of mean zero and variance
∫tT​(T−u)2du=3h3​.
(43)
The Gaussian exponential moment formula E[eaZ]=ea2Var(Z)/2 for centered Z therefore gives the bond price in this Gaussian short-rate model with a deterministic shift:
Pt​(T)=exp[−∫tT​g(s)ds−σ(T−t)Wt​+6σ2(T−t)3​].​
(44)
The instantaneous forward rate is the negative maturity derivative of the logarithm of the bond price. Consequently
ft​(T)=g(T)+σWt​−2σ2​(T−t)2.​
(45)
In particular, Pt​(t)=1 and ft​(t)=g(t)+σWt​=rt​, as required. The derivative formula is pointwise for continuous g and almost everywhere for locally integrable g.
